A <metric space> $(X,d)$ is a <complete metric space> if every <Cauchy sequence> in $X$ converges to a point of $X$. Explicitly, the hypothesis is
$$
\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall m,n\geq N:\ d(x_m,x_n)<\varepsilon,
$$
and the conclusion is that some $x\in X$ satisfies $d(x_n,x)\to0$.

It is a <sequentially compact space> if every <sequence> in $X$ has a <convergent subsequence> whose <limit of a sequence> belongs to $X$.

It is a <connected space> if there are no disjoint nonempty <open subsets> $U,V$ of $X$ with $X=U\cup V$. Here openness refers to the <metric topology> of $X$.

It is a <path-connected space> if, for every $x,y\in X$, there is a <continuous map> $\gamma:[0,1]\to X$ with $\gamma(0)=x$ and $\gamma(1)=y$. Such a map is a <path>. Every <path-connected space> is a <connected space>: a separation would pull back along a <path> joining its two sides to a separation of the <interval> $[0,1]$.

The answers, in the order <completeness>, <sequential compactness>, <connectedness>, and <path connectedness>, are
$$
\boxed{\text{yes, no, yes, yes}.}
$$
Define $F(0)=0$ and $F(x)=\sqrt{x}\sin(1/x)$ for $x>0$. The estimate $|F(x)|\leq\sqrt{x}$ proves <continuity> at zero by the <squeeze theorem>; elsewhere it follows from the usual <continuous functions>. Thus $X$ is the <graph of a function> $F$ on the <closed set> $[0,\infty)$.

To check closedness directly, if $(x_n,F(x_n))\to(x,y)$ in the <Euclidean plane>, then $x\geq0$ and <continuity> gives $y=F(x)$. Therefore $X$ is a <closed subset> of the <complete metric space> $\mathbb R^2$, hence is a <complete metric space>. The <sequence> $(n,F(n))$ has no <convergent subsequence>, since its first <Cartesian coordinates> tends to infinity, so <sequential compactness> fails.

For any two points $(u,F(u)),(v,F(v))$, the map
$$
\gamma(s)=\bigl((1-s)u+sv,F((1-s)u+sv)\bigr),\qquad 0\leq s\leq1,
$$
is a <continuous path> in $X$ joining them. Thus $X$ is <path-connected>, and consequently <connected>. The factor $\sqrt{x}$ is crucial: the oscillations shrink to the single point $(0,0)$.

Every <singleton> is an <open ball> of radius $1$, so the <metric topology> is the <discrete topology>. For example, $\{1\}$ and $\mathbb N\setminus\{1\}$ form a separation into two nonempty <open sets>, so $X$ is not <connected>. It is consequently not <path-connected> either.

The answers are
$$

Write $M_n(f,g)=\sup_{0\leq t\leq n}|f(t)-g(t)|$. The given <metric> describes <locally uniform convergence>: $d(f_j,f)\to0$ if and only if $M_n(f_j,f)\to0$ for every fixed $n$. Indeed,
$$
2^{-n}\min\{M_n(f_j,f),1\}\leq d(f_j,f),
$$
so convergence in $d$ forces $M_n\to0$. Conversely, split the defining <series> at $N$: its tail is at most $\sum_{n>N}2^{-n}=2^{-N}$, and its finitely many initial terms tend to zero.

If $(f_j)$ is a <Cauchy sequence> in $d$, the same single-term estimate makes it a <Cauchy sequence> in the <supremum norm> on every $[0,n]$. The <completeness of the continuous-map space in the uniform metric> gives a <continuous function> $F_n$ on $[0,n]$ with $f_j\to F_n$ uniformly there. Uniqueness of the <limit of a sequence> gives $F_{n+1}|_{[0,n]}=F_n$, so these <functions> define a single <continuous function> $f$ on $[0,\infty)$. The preceding finite-head and tail argument proves $d(f_j,f)\to0$. Thus the space is <complete>.

The constant <functions> $f_j(t)=j$ have $d(f_j,f_k)=1$ whenever $j\ne k$. They have no <convergent subsequence>, so the space is not <sequentially compact>.

For fixed $f,g$, set $\gamma(s)=(1-s)f+sg$. On $[0,n]$,
$$
M_n(\gamma(s),\gamma(s'))=|s-s'|M_n(f,g)\longrightarrow0
$$
as $s'\to s$. The equivalence with <locally uniform convergence> proves that $\gamma$ is a <continuous path> for $d$. Hence the space is <path-connected> and <connected>.

The <inverse tangent> $T(x)=\arctan x$ is a <homeomorphism> from the <real line> with its <Euclidean metric> onto $I=(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$ with its <subspace topology>: $T$ and its inverse $\tan$ are <continuous>. $$
\boxed{U\text{ is }d\text{-open}\iff T(U)\text{ is open in }I\iff U\text{ is }\rho\text{-open}.}
$$
For a direct <open ball> interpretation, $B_\rho(x,r)=T^{-1}((T(x)-r,T(x)+r)\cap I)$ is $d$-open by <continuity> of $T$. Conversely, <continuity> of $\tan$ at $T(x)$ supplies a sufficiently small $\rho$-ball inside any prescribed $d$-ball about $x$. Thus both <metrics> generate exactly the same <open sets>.

A <norm> on a real <vector space> $X$ is a map $\|\cdot\|:X\to[0,\infty)$ such that, for $x,y\in X$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb R$,
$$
\|x\|=0\iff x=0,\qquad \|\lambda x\|=|\lambda|\|x\|,\qquad \|x+y\|\leq\|x\|+\|y\|.
$$
The last property is the <triangle inequality>; the middle property is <absolute homogeneity of a norm>. The induced <metric> is
$$
\boxed{d(x,y)=\|x-y\|.}
$$
Positivity and separation follow from the <norm> axioms, symmetry from <absolute homogeneity of a norm> with $\lambda=-1$, and the <triangle inequality> from $x-z=(x-y)+(y-z)$.

First verify that $V_0$ is a <vector space>. Sums and scalar multiples preserve <continuity> and the condition at zero. For every <partition of an interval> $D$, the <triangle inequality> and <absolute homogeneity of a norm> of the <absolute value> imply
$$
V(f+g,D)\leq V(f,D)+V(g,D),\qquad V(\lambda f,D)=|\lambda|V(f,D).
$$
Taking <suprema> yields
$$
V(f+g)\leq V(f)+V(g),\qquad V(\lambda f)=|\lambda|V(f).
$$
For $\lambda=0$ the second equality follows directly from $V(0)=0$. In particular, <finite variation> is preserved, so $V_0$ is closed under these operations.

Nonnegativity is immediate. For any $t\in[0,1]$, use the <partition of an interval> $(0,t,1)$, omitting repeated endpoints if necessary, to get
$$
|f(t)|=|f(t)-f(0)|\leq V(f).
$$
Thus $V(f)=0$ forces $f=0$, and conversely $f=0$ has zero <total variation of a function>. These observations prove that $\|f\|=V(f)$ is a <norm> on $V_0$.

Applying the same bound to $f_n-f$ gives
$$
\boxed{\|f_n-f\|_\infty\leq V(f_n-f)=\|f_n-f\|.}
$$
Therefore convergence in the <anchored variation norm> implies <uniform convergence> on $[0,1]$. The anchoring condition $f(0)=0$ removes the nonzero constant <functions> which otherwise have zero <total variation of a function>.

We first show that a <continuous injection> $j:S^1\to S^1$ must be <surjective>. Otherwise its <image> misses a point $q$. <Stereographic projection> identifies $S^1\setminus\{q\}$ with $\mathbb R$, giving a <continuous injection> $u:S^1\to\mathbb R$. The <extreme value theorem> supplies distinct points $p_-,p_+$ where $u$ attains its minimum $m$ and maximum $M$, with $m<M$ because $u$ is <injective>.

The two <circular arcs> from $p_-$ to $p_+$ each contain a point at which $u=(m+M)/2$, by the <intermediate value theorem>. Those points are distinct because they lie in the disjoint interiors of the two arcs. This contradicts <injectivity>, proving the claim about $j$.

Now suppose $F:S^2\to S^1$ were a <continuous injection>. Its restriction to the equatorial <circle> $E=\{(x,y,0):x^2+y^2=1\}$ would be a <continuous injection> from $S^1$ to itself, hence $F(E)=S^1$. The north pole $N=(0,0,1)$ is outside $E$, but $F(N)$ must equal $F(e)$ for some $e\in E$. This contradicts <injectivity>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{there is no continuous injection }S^2\longrightarrow S^1.}
$$

Assume condition (1). For fixed $a$, the <supremum>
$$
M_a(r)=\sup_{x\in B(a,r)}f(x)
$$
is nondecreasing as $r$ increases. Therefore its limit as $r\downarrow0$ exists in the extended <real numbers> and equals $\inf_{r>0}M_a(r)$. Because $a\in B(a,r)$, it is at least $f(a)$. Condition (1) says precisely that this limit is $f(a)$.

It follows that for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $r>0$ with $M_a(r)<f(a)+\varepsilon$. Fix $C\in\mathbb R$ and $a$ with $f(a)<C$. Taking $\varepsilon=C-f(a)$ produces an <open ball> on which $f(x)<C$. Thus $\{x:f(x)<C\}$ is an <open set>, and its <complement>
$$
\boxed{\{x:f(x)\geq C\}\text{ is a closed set}.}
$$
This proves (1) implies (2). The property being characterized is <upper semicontinuity>.

The zeros of the <hyperbolic sine> are exactly $z=k\pi i$, $k\in\mathbb Z$, since $\sinh z=0$ is equivalent to $e^{2z}=1$. Each zero is simple because $\cosh(k\pi i)=(-1)^k\ne0$. The quotient is therefore <holomorphic> precisely on

\boxed{\mathbb C\setminus\{k\pi i:k\in\mathbb Z\}.}

At zero, the <Taylor expansion> $\sinh z=z+O(z^3)$ gives $z/\sinh^2z=1/z+O(z)$, so zero is a <simple pole>. At $k\pi i\ne0$, the denominator has a double zero and the numerator is nonzero, giving a <pole> of order two. Thus none of the omitted points admits a <holomorphic extension>.

Let $F(z)=\overline{\sinh z}$. More generally, for a <holomorphic function> $h$, the increment expansion at $z_0$ gives
$$
\frac{\overline{h(z_0+w)}-\overline{h(z_0)}}w=\overline{h'(z_0)}\frac{\overline w}w+o(1).

Along real increments $w$ the first factor $\overline w/w$ is $1$, while along purely imaginary increments it is $-1$. Thus <complex differentiability> of $\overline h$ requires $h'(z_0)=0$. Conversely, if $h'(z_0)=0$, the displayed expression tends to zero for arbitrary complex increments, so the <complex derivative> exists and equals zero.

For $h=\sinh$, this condition is $\cosh z_0=0$, equivalent to $e^{2z_0}=-1$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{F\text{ is complex differentiable exactly at }z_0=(k+\tfrac12)\pi i,\quad F'(z_0)=0.}
$$
These points form a <discrete subset> of the <complex plane> and contain no nonempty <open set>. Every <neighbourhood> of each one contains points where $F$ is not <complex differentiable>. Therefore $F$ is <holomorphic> on no nonempty <open set>, even though it has <complex derivatives> at the specified points.

The <identity theorem> states that if two <holomorphic functions> on a <connected> <open set> agree at a <sequence> of distinct points with an <accumulation point> inside that set, then they agree everywhere on it. Equivalently, the zeros of a <holomorphic function> which is not identically zero cannot accumulate inside its <domain>.

Because $f,g$ never vanish, their <logarithmic derivatives> are <holomorphic>. The <holomorphic function>
$$
H=\frac{f'}f-\frac{g'}g
$$
vanishes at every $a_n$. Since the distinct $a_n$ converge to $a\in U$, the <identity theorem> gives $H\equiv0$. Applying the <quotient rule> now gives
$$
\left(\frac fg\right)'=\frac{f'g-fg'}{g^2}=\frac fg\left(\frac{f'}f-\frac{g'}g\right)=0.
$$
A <holomorphic function> with zero <derivative> on a <domain> is constant. One can see this locally from its <Taylor series>, then propagate the constant using <connectedness>. Hence $f/g=c$ on $U$, with $c\ne0$, and
$$
\boxed{f=cg\text{ on }U.}
$$
The conclusion can fail if the accumulation occurs outside $U$. Take the <punctured complex plane> $U=\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$ and
$$
f(z)=\exp(\cos(1/z)),\qquad g(z)=1,\qquad a_n=\frac1{n\pi}\quad(n\geq1).
$$
Both <functions> are nowhere vanishing and <holomorphic> on $U$, and $a_n\to0\notin U$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}=\frac{\sin(1/z)}{z^2},\qquad \frac{g'(z)}{g(z)}=0,
$$
so these <logarithmic derivatives> agree at every $a_n$. However, $f$ is not constant: for example $f(1/\pi)=e^{-1}$ and $f(1/(2\pi))=e$. Therefore no constant $c$ gives $f=cg$.

$$
|c_n|\leq M_rr^{-n}\quad(n\geq0),\qquad |c_{-m}|\leq M_rr^m\quad(m\geq1).
$$
The first bound ensures convergence uniformly on every closed <disk> $|z|\leq r'<r$; letting $r$ range up to $S$ covers $D_S$. The second gives convergence uniformly on $|z|\geq r''>r$; letting $r$ range down to $R$ covers $A_{R,\infty}$. Locally uniform convergence of these <power series>, in $z$ and $1/z$ respectively, proves the claimed <holomorphy>. On the original <annulus> their sum is $f$.

The second estimate also proves $h(z)\to0$ as $|z|\to\infty$: for any fixed $r\in(R,S)$ and $|z|>r$,
$$
|h(z)|\leq M_r\sum_{m=1}^\infty(r/|z|)^m=\frac{M_rr}{|z|-r}\longrightarrow0.
$$

For uniqueness under this condition, suppose $f=g_1+h_1=g_2+h_2$. Define $F=g_1-g_2$ on $D_S$ and $F=h_2-h_1$ on $A_{R,\infty}$. The two definitions agree on the overlap, and their <domains> cover $\mathbb C$, so they define an <entire function>. Moreover, $F(z)\to0$ at infinity. It is bounded outside a large <disk> by this limit, and inside by <continuity> and the <extreme value theorem>. The <Liouville theorem> makes $F$ constant, and its limit makes that constant zero. Hence $g_1=g_2$ and $h_1=h_2$. Without the limiting condition, adding a constant to $g$ and subtracting it from $h$ would preserve the decomposition.
