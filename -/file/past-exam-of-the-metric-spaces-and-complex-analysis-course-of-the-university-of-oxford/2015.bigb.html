== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Heine-Borel theorem> says that a <subset> $K$ of finite-dimensional <Euclidean space> is <compact> exactly when it is both <closed> and <bounded>:
$$
\boxed{K\subseteq\mathbb R^n\text{ compact}\iff K\text{ closed and bounded}.}
$$
Here <compactness> refers to the <subspace topology> induced by the usual <Euclidean metric>, and <closedness> and <boundedness> are measured in the ambient <Euclidean space>. This characterization does not hold for arbitrary <metric spaces>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <empty set> is <bounded>. Otherwise choose $p$ in the <compact metric space> $X$. The <open balls> $B(p,n)$, $n=1,2,\ldots$, form an <open cover>. A <finite subcover> exists by <compactness>; because the balls are nested, the largest one already covers $X$. Hence $X\subseteq B(p,N)$ for some $N$.

For $x,y\in X$, the <triangle inequality> gives $d(x,y)\leq d(x,p)+d(p,y)<2N$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{diam}X\leq2N<\infty,\quad\text{so }X\text{ is bounded}.}
$$
The proof uses only <compactness> and the <metric>, not a coordinate system.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <sequence> $n\in[0,\infty)$ is <unbounded>. Every <compact metric space> is <bounded>, by part (b), so **this subspace is not <compact>**.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Take an <irrational number> $a\in(0,1)$. The <density of the rational numbers> provides a <sequence> in $\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]$ converging to $a$, which is outside that <subset>. Thus the <subset> is not <closed> in $\mathbb R$. By the <Heine-Borel theorem>, **it is not <compact>**, despite being <bounded>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <unit circle> is the inverse image of the <closed set> $\{1\}$ under the <continuous map> $(x,y)\mapsto x^2+y^2$, so it is <closed>. It is also <bounded>, since every point has <Euclidean norm> one. The <Heine-Borel theorem> gives **<compactness>**.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

The open <unit disk> is <bounded> but not <closed>: $(1-1/n,0)$ lies in it for $n\geq2$ and converges to the omitted point $(1,0)$. By the <Heine-Borel theorem>, **it is not <compact>**.

==== v
{scope}

===== Solution

The points $(n,0)$ belong to the indicated <subset> for every positive integer $n$. They form an <unbounded> <sequence>, so this <metric space> is not <bounded>. Therefore **it is not <compact>**. Shrinking the vertical width does not bound its horizontal extent.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

By the <Heine-Borel theorem>, a noncompact <subset> $X\subseteq\mathbb R$ is either <unbounded> or not <closed>. If $X$ is <unbounded>, the restriction $f(x)=x$ is a <continuous function> with <unbounded> range.

Otherwise $X$ is <bounded> and not <closed>. Choose $a\in\overline X\setminus X$, and define
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\frac1{|x-a|},\qquad x\in X.}
$$
Its denominator is nonzero on $X$, so it is <continuous>. Since $a$ is in the <closure>, for every $n$ there is $x_n\in X$ with $0<|x_n-a|<1/n$. Hence $f(x_n)>n$, proving that this <continuous function> is <unbounded>. These two cases establish <unbounded continuous witnesses to noncompactness of a real subspace>.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

We can arrange that **neither the <supremum> nor the <infimum> is attained**. By part (d), choose a <continuous function> $f$ on $X$ with <unbounded> range, and put $u=|f|$. Choose $x_n\in X$ so that $t_n=u(x_n)$ increases strictly to infinity. Construct a <continuous function> $h:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ with
$$
h(t_n)=(-1)^n n:
$$
keep it constant up to $t_1$ and interpolate linearly on each $[t_n,t_{n+1}]$. Since $t_n\to\infty$, the joining points have no finite accumulation, so this defines a <continuous function> everywhere on its domain.

Now set
$$
\boxed{g(x)=\frac{h(u(x))}{\sqrt{1+h(u(x))^2}}.}
$$
By <function composition>, $g$ is <continuous>, and $-1<g(x)<1$ for every $x$. Along the even and odd chosen points,
$$
g(x_{2n})\longrightarrow1,\qquad g(x_{2n+1})\longrightarrow-1.
$$
Thus $\sup_Xg=1$ and $\inf_Xg=-1$, and neither is a value of $g$. This proves the <bounded continuous witness with neither extremum attained> construction, including for <subsets> that are <bounded> but not <closed>.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

A <metric space> is <connected> when it has no <separation of a topological space>: it cannot be written as the union of two disjoint nonempty <open subsets>. Equivalently, the only <subsets> that are both <open> and <closed> are the <empty set> and the whole space.
$$
\boxed{X\text{ connected}\iff\text{no two disjoint nonempty open sets cover }X.}
$$
The <open sets> here are those of the <metric topology>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

A <metric space> is <path-connected> if every pair $x,y$ can be joined by a <path>: a <continuous map> $\gamma:[0,1]\to X$ with $\gamma(0)=x$ and $\gamma(1)=y$.
$$
\boxed{\forall x,y\in X\ \exists\gamma:[0,1]\to X\text{ continuous joining }x\text{ to }y.}
$$
The <path> must remain in $X$, including at its endpoints.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Suppose a <path-connected space> had a <separation> $X=U\cup V$. Choose $u\in U$, $v\in V$, and a <path> $\gamma$ from $u$ to $v$. The <inverse images> $\gamma^{-1}(U)$ and $\gamma^{-1}(V)$ are disjoint <open sets> in the <subspace topology> of $[0,1]$, cover it, and are nonempty because they contain its opposite endpoints. This contradicts the <connectedness> of an <interval>.

Therefore **every <path-connected space> is <connected>**. Notice that it is the <subspace topology> on $[0,1]$ that is used in the inverse-image argument.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The two <open balls> are disjoint: a common point would have distances to their centres summing to less than two, contradicting the <triangle inequality>. Both are nonempty and relatively <open>, and they cover the union. They therefore give a <separation>, so the union is **neither <connected> nor <path-connected>**.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Each <closed ball> is a <convex set> and contains the origin. Join any point to zero by a <line segment> inside its ball, then join zero to the other point. Concatenating the two segments gives a <path> in the union. It is therefore **<path-connected> and <connected>**.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Only the right-hand ball is <closed>, but the union is still **<path-connected> and <connected>**. Its right-hand <closed ball> contains zero, and every point $z$ of the left-hand <open ball> can be joined to zero by $\gamma(t)=(1-t)z$.

To check the endpoint carefully, write $a=(-1,0)$. For $0\leq t<1$, <convexity> and the <triangle inequality> give
$$
\lVert(1-t)z-a\rVert
\leq(1-t)\lVert z-a\rVert+t\lVert a\rVert<1.
$$
At $t=1$, the <path> reaches zero in the other, closed ball. Points in that ball also join zero by <line segments>, completing the construction.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

Each vertical <line segment> over a rational coordinate meets the horizontal <line> at height one. A point on such a segment can move vertically to the top, then horizontally along that <line>; points already at height one use only the horizontal motion. Concatenating these motions joins any two points.

Thus the <rational comb with a deleted attachment point> construction, before deletion, is **<path-connected> and hence <connected>**. The infinitely many vertical pieces cause no difficulty because each individual <path> uses only finitely many segments.

==== v
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $D$ be this union of rational-coordinate horizontal and vertical <lines>. A point $(x,y)\in D$ has either rational $x$, in which case it moves vertically to $(x,0)$ then horizontally to $(0,0)$, or rational $y$, in which case it moves horizontally to $(0,y)$ then vertically to $(0,0)$. Every segment remains on a <line> with a rational fixed coordinate.

Thus **$D$ is <path-connected> and <connected>**. It is not necessary for both coordinates to be rational.

==== vi
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $C'=C\setminus\{(1,1)\}$ and $V=\{1\}\times[0,1)$. Let $L$ and $R$ be the portions of $C'$ with first coordinate respectively less than and greater than one. Each is <path-connected>: move along a rational tooth to its corresponding open half of the top <line>, then horizontally there. By the <density of the rational numbers>, every point of $V$ is a <limit point> of both $L$ and $R$.

Use the elementary fact that adjoining any collection of <limit points> to a <connected set> preserves <connectedness>. Indeed, in a proposed <separation>, the connected subset lies on one side; relative openness would prevent any point on the other side from being a <limit point> of it. Therefore $L\cup V$ and $R\cup V$ are <connected>. They intersect in $V$, so their union $C'$ is <connected>.

There is, however, no <path> from $(1,0)$ to $(0,1)$. If $\gamma(t)=(x(t),y(t))$ were one, let $t_0$ be the first time at which $y(t_0)=1$. Such a first time exists by <continuity> and <compactness> of $[0,1]$, and $t_0>0$. For $0\leq t<t_0$, membership in the comb forces $x(t)\in\mathbb Q$. The <continuous image of a connected space> is <connected>, and a <connected subset> of $\mathbb Q$ is a <singleton>: an irrational number between any two distinct rational values would separate it. Hence $x(t)=1$ for $t<t_0$. By <continuity>, $\gamma(t_0)=(1,1)$, which was deleted.
$$
\boxed{C'\text{ is connected but not path-connected}.}
$$

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Define $g(p)=f(p)-f(-p)$ on the <sphere>. It is <continuous> and satisfies $g(-p)=-g(p)$. If $g(p)=0$ at a chosen point, we are done. Otherwise choose a <unit vector> $q$ perpendicular to $p$ and follow the half <great circle>
$$
\gamma(t)=\cos(\pi t)p+\sin(\pi t)q,\qquad0\leq t\leq1.
$$
The <Pythagorean theorem> shows that $\gamma(t)$ lies on the <sphere>, and its endpoints are $p,-p$. The <continuous function> $g\circ\gamma$ has opposite signs at the endpoints. The <Intermediate value theorem> therefore gives a zero $t_*$, so
$$
\boxed{f(\gamma(t_*))=f(-\gamma(t_*))}.
$$
This is a scalar equal-value assertion for an <antipodal pair>; it uses only a <path> and the <Intermediate value theorem>.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

One standard form of the <Cauchy integral formula> is this: if $f$ is <holomorphic> on an <open set> containing a <contour> that is a <simple closed curve> $\Gamma$ and its interior, and $\Gamma$ has <positive orientation of a contour>, then for every point $w$ inside it,
$$
\boxed{f(w)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\Gamma\frac{f(z)}{z-w}\,dz.}
$$
The hypothesis includes the contour itself; <singularities> on the contour are excluded. In particular, the formula applies on a <circle> whose closed <disk> is contained in the domain of <holomorphy>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $S=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}|a_j|$ and choose $R>\max(1,2S/|a_n|)$. For $|z|>R$, the <triangle inequality> gives
$$
\left|\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_jz^j\right|\leq S|z|^{n-1}<\frac{|a_n|}{2}|z|^n.
$$
The <reverse triangle inequality> therefore yields
$$
\boxed{|P(z)|>\frac{|a_n|}{2}|z|^n\qquad(|z|>R).}
$$
If $P$ had no <root>, the <function> $h=1/P$ would be an <entire function>. The bound just proved makes $h(z)\to0$ as $|z|\to\infty$. On the remaining closed <disk>, <continuity> and the <extreme value theorem> make $h$ <bounded>. Thus $h$ is a bounded <entire function>, so <Liouville's theorem> makes it constant. Its limit at infinity forces that constant to be zero, impossible for a reciprocal.

Consequently **$P$ has a <root> in $\mathbb C$**. This proves the existence part of the <fundamental theorem of algebra>. The invoked <Liouville theorem> follows from the <Cauchy integral formula>: the <Cauchy estimate> $|h'(w)|\leq M/r$ on arbitrarily large <circles> gives $h'=0$ when $h$ is bounded by $M$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

List the <roots> with their <multiplicities>. By the <factor theorem>, $Q(z)=a\prod_{k=1}^n(z-\alpha_k)$ with $a\ne0$. The <product rule>, followed by division where $Q(z)\ne0$, gives its <logarithmic derivative>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{Q'(z)}{Q(z)}=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac1{z-\alpha_k}.}
$$
Let $Q'(\beta)=0$. If $Q(\beta)=0$ too, then $\beta$ is already one of the <roots>, so either desired location conclusion follows immediately. Otherwise the displayed sum vanishes at $\beta$. If all <roots> have positive <imaginary parts> but $\operatorname{Im}\beta\leq0$, then
$$
\operatorname{Im}\frac1{\beta-\alpha_k}
=\frac{\operatorname{Im}\alpha_k-\operatorname{Im}\beta}{|\beta-\alpha_k|^2}>0
$$
for every $k$. Their sum cannot be zero. Thus **$\operatorname{Im}\beta>0$**.

For the <unit disk> conclusion, the same sum gives an especially transparent <convex combination>. Taking <complex conjugates> and setting $w_k=|\beta-\alpha_k|^{-2}>0$ gives
$$
0=\sum_k w_k(\beta-\alpha_k),\qquad
\boxed{\beta=\frac{\sum_k w_k\alpha_k}{\sum_k w_k}.}
$$
Therefore, if every $|\alpha_k|<1$,
$$
|\beta|\leq\frac{\sum_k w_k|\alpha_k|}{\sum_k w_k}<1.
$$
The strict inequality follows because this is a finite weighted average of numbers strictly below one. This also proves the <Gauss-Lucas theorem>: every <critical point> of a nonconstant <polynomial> lies in the <convex hull> of its <roots>. The common-root case, handled before division, is essential when <multiplicities> occur.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Choose $0<\rho<r$ and traverse the <circle> $|z|=\rho$ counterclockwise, so it has <positive orientation of a contour>. The <Cauchy coefficient formula> gives, for every <nonnegative integer> $n$,
$$
\boxed{c_n=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=\rho}\frac{f(z)}{z^{n+1}}\,dz,\qquad 0<\rho<r.}
$$
The smaller <radius> matters: the <holomorphic function> is only known on $|z|<r$, so its values on $|z|=r$ need not exist. To see directly why this <contour integral> selects $c_n$, the <power series> converges uniformly on $|z|=\rho$, allowing <uniform convergence and contour integration> to justify integrating term by term. The <contour integral> of $z^{k-n-1}$ is zero for $k\ne n$ and $2\pi i$ for $k=n$. Thus all other terms disappear.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the <generalized binomial theorem> with exponent $-1/2$, choosing the <analytic branch of a square root> that equals $1$ at $z=0$. For $|z|<1$,
$$
(1-z)^{-1/2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom{-1/2}{n}(-z)^n.
$$
The signs cancel in each <binomial coefficient>. For $n\geq1$,
$$
(-1)^n\binom{-1/2}{n}=\frac{1\cdot3\cdots(2n-1)}{2^n n!}=\frac{(2n)!}{2^{2n}(n!)^2}=\frac1{4^n}\binom{2n}{n}.
$$
The same expression equals $1$ for $n=0$, using the empty-product convention. Consequently the <power series> is
$$
\boxed{\frac1{\sqrt{1-z}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom{2n}{n}\left(\frac z4\right)^n,\qquad |z|<1.}
$$
Its coefficients are the <normalized central binomial coefficients>. As a convergence check, the ratio of successive absolute terms tends to $|z|$, so the <ratio test> gives absolute convergence inside the <unit disc>. Applying the <Cauchy coefficient formula> to the <holomorphic function> $(1-z)^{-1/2}$ now immediately yields
$$
\boxed{\int_{\gamma(0,r)}\frac{dz}{z^{n+1}\sqrt{1-z}}=\frac{2\pi i}{4^n}\binom{2n}{n},\qquad 0<r<1,\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
This value is independent of the chosen <radius> because the <contour integral> extracts the same coefficient on every such <circle>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For $z\in\mathbb C\setminus[1,\infty)$, the number $w=1-z$ avoids the nonpositive <real axis>. Define the <branch of the complex logarithm> by
$$
\boxed{L(1-z)=\log|1-z|+i\operatorname{Arg}(1-z),\qquad -\pi<\operatorname{Arg}(1-z)<\pi.}
$$
Here the <principal argument> is selected as a <continuous map> on that slit <domain>, so this is a <holomorphic function>, with <derivative> $-1/(1-z)$ and $L(1)=0$. For example, locally writing $w=\rho e^{i\phi}$ gives $d(\log\rho+i\phi)=dw/w$, verifying the <complex derivative>. The normalization also makes this <branch of the complex logarithm> unique: any two continuous choices differ by a constant integer multiple of $2\pi i$ on the <connected> slit <domain>, and their values at $w=1$ agree.

To evaluate the corresponding <analytic branch of a square root> on the <unit circle>, factor
$$
1-e^{i\theta}=e^{i\theta/2}\bigl(e^{-i\theta/2}-e^{i\theta/2}\bigr)=2\sin(\theta/2)e^{i(\theta/2-\pi/2)}.
$$
For $0<\theta<2\pi$, we have $\sin(\theta/2)>0$ and $-\pi/2<\theta/2-\pi/2<\pi/2$. Thus this factorization already uses the selected <principal argument>, with no hidden change by $2\pi$. It follows that
$$
L(1-e^{i\theta})=\log\bigl(2\sin(\theta/2)\bigr)+i(\theta/2-\pi/2).
$$
Halving this expression and applying the <exponential function> gives
$$
\sqrt{1-e^{i\theta}}=\sqrt{2\sin(\theta/2)}\,e^{i\theta/4-i\pi/4}.
$$
Since $\sqrt2e^{-i\pi/4}=1-i$, the desired branch value is
$$
\boxed{\sqrt{1-e^{i\theta}}=(1-i)\sqrt{\sin(\theta/2)}\,e^{i\theta/4},\qquad 0<\theta<2\pi.}
$$
For instance, $\theta=\pi$ gives $\sqrt2>0$, a quick check that the sign agrees with the normalization.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $b_n=4^{-n}\binom{2n}{n}$. The given limiting identity and the <Cauchy coefficient formula> from part (a) give the boundary <contour integral>
$$
J_n:=\int_{\gamma(0,1)}\frac{dz}{z^{n+1}\sqrt{1-z}}=2\pi i b_n.
$$
The <contour> passes through $z=1$, so its value here is an <improper integral> using the one-sided branch values. Parametrize its open arc by $z=e^{i\theta}$, $0<\theta<2\pi$. Substitution of the <analytic branch of a square root> calculated in part (b) gives
$$
J_n=\frac{i}{1-i}\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{e^{-i(n+1/4)\theta}}{\sqrt{\sin(\theta/2)}}\,d\theta.
$$
The factor $e^{-in\theta}$ comes from cancelling $e^{i\theta}$ in $dz$ against $z^{n+1}$; the extra $e^{-i\theta/4}$ comes from the chosen branch. Keeping both phases prevents a change in the half-integer frequency.

Now put $t=\theta/2$. Then
$$
J_n=\frac{2i}{1-i}\int_0^\pi\frac{e^{-i(2n+1/2)t}}{\sqrt{\sin t}}\,dt=(i-1)\int_0^\pi\frac{e^{-i(2n+1/2)t}}{\sqrt{\sin t}}\,dt.
$$
Dividing by $i-1$ gives
$$
\int_0^\pi\frac{e^{-i(2n+1/2)t}}{\sqrt{\sin t}}\,dt=\frac{2\pi i b_n}{i-1}=\pi b_n(1-i).
$$
Taking the <real part> therefore proves
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\pi\frac{\cos\bigl((2n+\tfrac12)t\bigr)}{\sqrt{\sin t}}\,dt=\frac{\pi}{4^n}\binom{2n}{n},\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
All these <improper integrals> converge absolutely: the numerators are bounded, while $(\sin t)^{-1/2}$ is of order $t^{-1/2}$ at zero and $(\pi-t)^{-1/2}$ at $\pi$. Hence the substitutions and passage to the <real part> are legitimate. The <imaginary part> also shows that the corresponding sine <integral> has the same positive value, which checks the phase calculation.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

One standard form of <Cauchy's residue theorem> is the following. Let $\Gamma$ be a <positively oriented contour> that is a <simple closed curve>, and let $f$ be a <holomorphic function> on an <open set> containing $\Gamma$ and its interior except for finitely many interior <isolated singularities> $z_1,\ldots,z_m$. There are no <singularities> on $\Gamma$. Then
$$
\boxed{\int_\Gamma f(z)\,dz=2\pi i\sum_{j=1}^m\operatorname{Res}(f,z_j).}
$$
The <residue> $\operatorname{Res}(f,z_j)$ is the coefficient of $(z-z_j)^{-1}$ in the <Laurent series> at $z_j$. Each interior <isolated singularity> contributes once because the <winding number> of this <positively oriented contour> about each such point is one. Reversing the <orientation of a contour> reverses the sign. This form of <Cauchy's residue theorem> applies to the rectangle below.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Start with the <exponential function> definition of the <hyperbolic sine> and use <Euler's formula>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sinh(x+iy)&=\frac{e^xe^{iy}-e^{-x}e^{-iy}}2\\
&=\frac{e^x(\cos y+i\sin y)-e^{-x}(\cos y-i\sin y)}2\\
&=\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}2\cos y+i\frac{e^x+e^{-x}}2\sin y.
\end{aligned}
$$
Identifying the <hyperbolic sine> and <hyperbolic cosine> in the final line gives
$$
\boxed{\sinh(x+iy)=\sinh x\cos y+i\cosh x\sin y.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The two terms in the preceding formula are the <real part> and <imaginary part>. Their squared sum is the squared <complex modulus>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
|\sinh(x+iy)|^2&=\sinh^2x\cos^2y+\cosh^2x\sin^2y\\
&=\sinh^2x(\cos^2y+\sin^2y)+\sin^2y\\
&=\sinh^2x+\sin^2y.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $\cosh^2x-\sinh^2x=1$ follows directly from the <exponential function> definitions. The <hyperbolic sine modulus identity> therefore yields
$$
\boxed{|\sinh(x+iy)|=\sqrt{\sinh^2x+\sin^2y}\geq|\sinh x|.}
$$
Equality holds exactly when $\sin y=0$, that is, when $y$ is an integer multiple of $\pi$. The lower bound is uniform in $y$, which will control whole vertical sides of the <contour> rather than just their endpoints.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The equation requires $\sinh z=\pm i\cosh a$. Write $z=x+iy$, with real $x,y$. Comparing <real parts> and <imaginary parts> using part (b)(i) gives
$$
\sinh x\cos y=0,\qquad \cosh x\sin y=\pm\cosh a.
$$
If $x=0$, the second equality would require $|\sin y|=\cosh a>1$, impossible because $a>0$. Thus $\sinh x\ne0$, forcing $\cos y=0$ and
$$
y=\frac\pi2+k\pi,\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.
$$
Now $|\sin y|=1$, so $\cosh x=\cosh a$. The <hyperbolic cosine> is even and is a <strictly increasing function> on $[0,\infty)$, hence $x=\pm a$. Conversely, substituting these values gives $\sinh(x+iy)=i(-1)^k\cosh a$, so every value found does satisfy the original equation. Therefore the complete set is
$$
\boxed{z=\pm a+i\left(\frac\pi2+k\pi\right),\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $D(z)=\sinh^2z+\cosh^2a$ and $S=\sinh(2a)>0$. Traverse the rectangle $-R\to R\to R+\pi i\to-R+\pi i\to-R$ with <positive orientation of a contour>, taking $R>a$. Part (i) shows that the only enclosed <poles> of $f(z)=z/D(z)$ are
$$
z_+=a+\frac{\pi i}2,\qquad z_-=-a+\frac{\pi i}2.
$$
They are <simple poles> because
$$
D'(z)=2\sinh z\cosh z=\sinh(2z),\qquad D'(z_+)=-S,\quad D'(z_-)=S.
$$
The <residue formula for a quotient at a simple denominator zero> gives
$$
\operatorname{Res}(f,z_+)=-\frac{a+\pi i/2}{S},\qquad \operatorname{Res}(f,z_-)=\frac{-a+\pi i/2}{S}.
$$
Their imaginary terms cancel and their <residues> sum to $-2a/S$. Thus <Cauchy's residue theorem> gives
$$
\int_{\Gamma_R}f(z)\,dz=-\frac{4\pi ia}{S}.
$$

{description=The top edge is traversed from right to left. Its reversed <orientation of a contour> and the imaginary shift in the numerator produce $-i\pi$ times the desired real <integral>.}

The choice of numerator $z$ makes the horizontal sides useful. The <hyperbolic sine> satisfies $\sinh(z+\pi i)=-\sinh z$, so $D(x+\pi i)=D(x)$. Consequently the lower and upper <contour integrals>, with their actual orientations, add to
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-R}^{R}\frac{x}{D(x)}\,dx+\int_R^{-R}\frac{x+\pi i}{D(x)}\,dx
&=-\pi i\int_{-R}^{R}\frac{dx}{D(x)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
It remains to justify discarding both vertical sides. On either side $z=\pm R+iy$, $0\leq y\leq\pi$, the <reverse triangle inequality> and part (b)(ii) imply
$$
|D(z)|\geq|\sinh z|^2-\cosh^2a\geq\sinh^2R-\cosh^2a.
$$
This lower bound is positive for sufficiently large $R$. Also $|z|\leq R+\pi$ and each vertical side has length $\pi$. The <estimation lemma> bounds the absolute value of the sum $V_R$ of the vertical contributions by
$$
|V_R|\leq\frac{2\pi(R+\pi)}{\sinh^2R-\cosh^2a}\longrightarrow0.
$$
The real <improper integral> converges absolutely, since its integrand is continuous on the <real axis> and decays like $4e^{-2|x|}$ at infinity. Combining all four sides and taking $R\to\infty$ gives
$$
-\pi i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{\sinh^2x+\cosh^2a}=-\frac{4\pi ia}{\sinh(2a)}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{\sinh^2x+\cosh^2a}=\frac{4a}{\sinh(2a)},\qquad a>0.}
$$
An independent real-variable check uses the <hyperbolic tangent> substitution $u=\tanh x$: the <integral> becomes $\int_{-1}^{1}du/(\cosh^2a-u^2\sinh^2a)$. Its <antiderivative> uses the <inverse hyperbolic tangent>, giving $2\operatorname{arctanh}(\tanh a)/(\sinh a\cosh a)=4a/\sinh(2a)$. As $a\downarrow0$, this tends to $2$, agreeing with $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\operatorname{sech}^2x\,dx=2$ and checking the factor of two.

== 6
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use $z\overline z=|z|^2$ and $\overline Bz+B\overline z=2\operatorname{Re}(\overline Bz)$. When $A\ne0$, <completing the square> gives
$$
A|z|^2+\overline Bz+B\overline z+C=A\left|z+\frac BA\right|^2+C-\frac{|B|^2}{A}.
$$
Thus, with $\Delta=|B|^2-AC$,
$$
\boxed{\left|z+\frac BA\right|^2=\frac{\Delta}{A^2}\quad(A\ne0).}
$$
If $\Delta>0$, this is a <circle> with centre $-B/A$ and <radius> $\sqrt\Delta/|A|$. If $\Delta=0$, it is the single point $-B/A$, sometimes called a degenerate <circle> of <radius> zero. If $\Delta<0$, it is the <empty set>, because a squared <complex modulus> cannot be negative.

When $A=0$ and $B\ne0$, write $B=u+iv$ and $z=x+iy$. The remaining equation is
$$
\boxed{ux+vy=-\frac C2,\qquad (u,v)\ne(0,0),}
$$
which is a <straight line> with <normal vector> $(u,v)$. It passes through zero exactly when $C=0$. Finally, when $A=B=0$, the equation has no solutions if $C\ne0$ and every <complex number> is a solution if $C=0$.

The complete <classification of a real complex-circle equation> therefore includes two degeneracies in addition to ordinary <circles>, <straight lines> and the <empty set>:
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{condition}&\text{locus}\\\hline
A\ne0,\ \Delta>0&\text{circle}\ (-B/A,\sqrt\Delta/|A|)\\
A\ne0,\ \Delta=0&\{-B/A\}\\
A\ne0,\ \Delta<0&\varnothing\\
A=0,\ B\ne0&\text{line }2\operatorname{Re}(\overline Bz)+C=0\\
A=B=0,\ C\ne0&\varnothing\\
A=B=C=0&\mathbb C
\end{array}}
$$
For the three advertised alternatives to be exhaustive, one must exclude the identically zero equation and either exclude or explicitly allow <radius>-zero <circles>. This is a qualification of the unrestricted coefficients, not a reason to discard the genuine <circle> and <straight line> cases used next.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $w=1/z$, so $z=1/w$ and $w\ne0$. Substitute into the equation in part (a) and multiply by $|w|^2$:
$$
\frac{A}{|w|^2}+\frac{\overline B}{w}+\frac B{\overline w}+C=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\boxed{C|w|^2+Bw+\overline B\overline w+A=0.}
$$
This is the <reciprocal transformation of a generalized circle>. In the original coefficient convention the new triple is $(C,\overline B,A)$: the linear coefficient is conjugated, which reflects the distinction between $1/z$ and <inversion in a circle> $1/\overline z$.

**A <circle> through zero.** Here $A\ne0$, $C=0$, and nondegeneracy forces $|B|^2>0$. Its image satisfies
$$
2\operatorname{Re}(Bw)+A=0.
$$
This is a <straight line>, and $w=0$ does not satisfy it because $A\ne0$. Conversely every point of this <straight line> has a nonzero reciprocal on the original <circle>. Thus the original <circle> with zero omitted maps onto the full finite <straight line>.

**A <circle> missing zero.** Now $A\ne0$, $C\ne0$ and $\Delta=|B|^2-AC>0$. Applying part (a) to the transformed coefficients gives centre $-\overline B/C$ and <radius> $\sqrt\Delta/|C|$, so the image is again a genuine <circle>. It misses zero, because the transformed equation has nonzero constant $A$. The inverse $z=1/w$ proves that the whole of this <circle> is attained.

**A <straight line> through zero.** Here $A=C=0$ and $B\ne0$. The transformed equation is $2\operatorname{Re}(Bw)=0$, another <straight line> through zero. Precisely, the finite map takes the punctured original <straight line> onto the punctured image <straight line>, since neither the domain nor the image of $1/z$ contains zero.

In the <Riemann sphere> convention $0\leftrightarrow\infty$, all three become exact statements about whole <generalized circles>:
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\text{circle through }0&\longmapsto\text{line missing }0,\\
\text{circle missing }0&\longmapsto\text{circle missing }0,\\
\text{line through }0&\longmapsto\text{line through }0.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Finite <straight lines> acquire their point at infinity in this convention. Stating it avoids accidentally adding $w=0$ merely because it satisfies a multiplied equation.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

**The three contacts in the strip.** Work with the external-contact arrangement drawn in the original diagram: both <disks> lie between the two <parallel lines> and touch opposite boundaries. A <rigid motion> puts those boundaries at $x=0$ and $x=d$, with $d>0$, and makes $P=(0,0)$. Write $R=(d,h)$, and let the positive <radii> be $r_1,r_2$. Because a <tangent to a circle> is perpendicular to the corresponding <radius>, the centres are
$$
O_1=(r_1,0),\qquad O_2=(d-r_2,h).
$$
At the <external tangency>, the two radial segments form the segment $O_1O_2$, with $|O_1Q|=r_1$ and $|QO_2|=r_2$. Hence the internal division formula gives
$$
Q=\frac{r_2O_1+r_1O_2}{r_1+r_2}=\left(\frac{r_1d}{r_1+r_2},\frac{r_1h}{r_1+r_2}\right)=\frac{r_1}{r_1+r_2}R.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{Q=P+\frac{r_1}{r_1+r_2}(R-P),\qquad P,Q,R\text{ are collinear}.}
$$
The coefficient is strictly between zero and one, so $Q$ lies on the actual <line segment> $PR$. This proves <collinearity for tangent circles between parallel lines>. We will also use the fact that $PQ$ and $QR$ are <chords> of the respective <circles>; the entire <line segment> $PR$ therefore lies in the union of their closed <disks>.

**The four contacts.** Label the successive contact points $T_{12},T_{23},T_{34},T_{41}$, with $T_{jk}$ belonging to $C_j$ and $C_k$. The original diagram has four positive-<radius> <circles> with disjoint interiors, successive <external tangencies>, and distinct contact points. Set $T=T_{41}$ and apply the reciprocal <Möbius transformation>
$$
w=\frac1{z-T}.
$$
By part (b), the two <circles> $C_1,C_4$ through $T$ become <straight lines> $\ell_1,\ell_4$. These <straight lines> cannot meet at a finite point, since their inverse images would give a second intersection of $C_1,C_4$. They are therefore distinct <parallel lines>. The other two <circles> avoid $T$, so their images $D_2,D_3$ are ordinary <circles> missing zero.

The three remaining contacts retain their tangencies. Indeed, away from $T$ the map and its inverse are <holomorphic functions> with nonzero <derivatives>, hence are local <conformal maps>. In particular they preserve a shared <tangent line>. Equivalently, bijectivity preserves the unique intersection of two <generalized circles>, which characterizes their tangency here.

The finite closed <disks> bounded by $D_2,D_3$ lie between $\ell_1,\ell_4$, have <external tangency> to each other, and touch opposite boundaries at the images of $T_{12}$ and $T_{34}$. To track the sides as well as the boundaries, a closed <disk> avoiding $T$ maps to the bounded closed <disk> of its image <circle>, while each <disk> with $T$ on its boundary maps to a closed <half-plane>. The two latter <half-planes> have disjoint interiors, so their complement is the strip containing zero. Disjointness of the original interiors puts $D_2,D_3$ inside that strip. Neither image closed <disk> contains zero, since the reciprocal <Möbius transformation> sends only infinity to zero.

The first part now makes the three finite transformed contact points <collinear>, on a <straight line> $\ell$. It is essential that $\ell$ misses zero. If it passed through zero, its segment between $\ell_1$ and $\ell_4$ would contain zero, since zero lies strictly within the strip. But the first part shows that this whole <line segment> is covered by the two image closed <disks>, neither of which contains zero. This contradiction proves $0\notin\ell$.

The inverse image of a <straight line> missing zero is a genuine <circle> through $T$, by the same coefficient transformation in part (b), or by applying the involution $u\mapsto1/u$ in reverse and translating back. This <circle> contains $T_{12},T_{23},T_{34}$, while $T$ is the inverse image of the point at infinity on $\ell$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{T_{12},\ T_{23},\ T_{34},\ T_{41}\text{ lie on one circle}.}
$$
This is the <four contact points of a circle chain> result; the four contacts form a <cyclic quadrilateral>.

{title=The <reciprocal transformation of a generalized circle> turns the chain into two <tangent circles> between <parallel lines>}
{description=An original exact construction illustrates the arrangement. The orange <circle> through all four contacts becomes the orange <straight line> through the three finite transformed contacts. The hollow point at zero lies between the <parallel lines> and outside the two image <disks>.}

The pictured arrangement matters. Arbitrary mixtures of <internal tangency> and <external tangency> can instead give four distinct <collinear> contacts: take four <circles> whose diameter endpoints on the <real axis> are respectively $[0,3]$, $[0,1]$, $[1,2]$, and $[2,3]$. Their successive contacts are $0,1,2,3$, so no ordinary <circle> contains all four. The proof above establishes the requested genuine-<circle> conclusion for the external-contact arrangement actually drawn, rather than silently treating every <generalized circle> as an ordinary <circle>.
