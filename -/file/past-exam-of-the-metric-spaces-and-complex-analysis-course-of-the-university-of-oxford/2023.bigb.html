A <metric space> $(X,d)$ is a <bounded set> if there is a finite constant $M\geq0$ such that
$$
\boxed{d(x,y)\leq M\quad\text{for every }x,y\in X}.
$$
For nonempty $X$, this is equivalent to containment in an <open ball> of finite radius: the <triangle inequality> converts a radius bound into a diameter bound, and conversely a diameter bound gives a ball of any slightly larger radius about a chosen point.

It is a <complete metric space> if every <Cauchy sequence> in $X$ has a <limit of a sequence> belonging to $X$. Explicitly, the hypothesis on $(x_n)$ is
$$
\forall\epsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall m,n\geq N:\ d(x_m,x_n)<\epsilon,
$$
and <completeness> requires an $x\in X$ with $d(x_n,x)\to0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{every Cauchy sequence converges in }X}.
$$
Boundedness controls distances; <completeness> ensures that these limiting points remain in the space.

A <subset> $F\subseteq X$ is a <closed set> in $X$ when its <complement> is an <open set> in the <metric topology>. Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{x\in X\setminus F\ \Longrightarrow\ \exists r>0:\ B(x,r)\cap F=\varnothing}.
$$
The <open ball> is taken inside the ambient <metric space> $X$; closedness always depends on that ambient space.

First suppose $F$ is a <closed set>. If $x\notin F$, the definition in part (ii) gives an <open ball> $B(x,r)$ disjoint from $F$. Thus every $y\in F$ has $d(x,y)\geq r$, and the <distance to a set> satisfies $\operatorname{dist}(x,F)\geq r>0$. Its contrapositive is the required implication.

Conversely, suppose that zero <distance to a set> forces membership. For $x\notin F$ we have $\operatorname{dist}(x,F)>0$. Choose $0<r<\operatorname{dist}(x,F)$. Then $B(x,r)\cap F=\varnothing$, proving $X\setminus F$ is an <open set>. Hence
$$
\boxed{F\text{ is closed}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \forall x\in X,\quad\operatorname{dist}(x,F)=0\Rightarrow x\in F}.
$$
For $F=\varnothing$, use the extended-real convention $\inf\varnothing=+\infty$: both assertions hold, with the implication vacuous. Part (b) only uses nonempty sets, so all its <distances to a set> are ordinary finite real numbers.

Write $h(A,B)=\sup_{a\in A}\operatorname{dist}(a,B)$, so that $\rho(A,B)=\max\{h(A,B),h(B,A)\}$ is the <Hausdorff distance>. Since $X$ is a <bounded set>, there is a finite $M$ with $d(x,y)\leq M$. Nonemptiness of $A,B$ therefore gives $0\leq h(A,B)\leq M$, so $\rho$ is finite. Its symmetry and nonnegativity are immediate, as is $\rho(A,A)=0$.

If $\rho(A,B)=0$, then every $a\in A$ has zero <distance to a set> $B$. Since $B$ is a <closed set>, part (a)(iii) gives $A\subseteq B$. Interchanging the sets gives $B\subseteq A$. Thus $\rho(A,B)=0$ implies $A=B$. Closedness is essential here: without it a set and its <closure> have <Hausdorff distance> zero.

For the <triangle inequality>, fix $a\in A$ and $\epsilon>0$. The <approximation property of an infimum> supplies $b\in B$ with $d(a,b)<\operatorname{dist}(a,B)+\epsilon$, and then $c\in C$ with $d(b,c)<\operatorname{dist}(b,C)+\epsilon$. Consequently,
$$
\operatorname{dist}(a,C)\leq d(a,c)<\operatorname{dist}(a,B)+h(B,C)+2\epsilon.
$$
Let $\epsilon\downarrow0$ and take the <supremum> over $a$ to obtain $h(A,C)\leq h(A,B)+h(B,C)$. Applying this inequality in the reverse direction as well yields
$$
\boxed{\rho(A,C)\leq\rho(A,B)+\rho(B,C)}.
$$
All <metric> axioms follow. Boundedness was used to ensure finiteness, and closedness to distinguish different sets; neither was needed for the <triangle inequality>.

The <intersection> $G$ is a <closed set> because arbitrary <intersections> of <closed sets> are closed. Its nonemptiness needs a construction: bounded closed sets need not be <compact sets> in this setting.

Fix $n$, $x_n\in F_n$, and $\eta>0$. Recursively choose $x_{k+1}\in F_{k+1}$, for $k\geq n$, using the <approximation property of an infimum> so that
$$
d(x_k,x_{k+1})<2^{-k}+\eta\,2^{n-k-1}.
$$
This is possible because $x_k\in F_k$ and $\operatorname{dist}(x_k,F_{k+1})\leq\rho(F_k,F_{k+1})\leq2^{-k}$. The two bounds form convergent <geometric series>, so the <triangle inequality> shows $(x_k)_{k\geq n}$ is a <Cauchy sequence>. By <completeness> it converges to some $x\in X$.

For each fixed $j$, all sufficiently late $x_k$ lie in $F_j$, by nesting. Closedness of $F_j$ gives $x\in F_j$, and hence $x\in G$. In particular, starting with any $x_1\in F_1$ proves $G\ne\varnothing$. Moreover,
$$
d(x_n,x)\leq\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\bigl(2^{-k}+\eta\,2^{n-k-1}\bigr)=2^{1-n}+\eta.
$$
For each starting point $x_n$, let $\eta\downarrow0$ in this bound on its <distance to a set> $G$. This does not assert that a nearest point exists. It proves $\operatorname{dist}(x_n,G)\leq2^{1-n}$. Since $G\subseteq F_n$, the other directed <Hausdorff distance> is zero, and
$$
\boxed{G\in\mathcal F(X),\qquad\rho(F_n,G)\leq2^{1-n}\longrightarrow0}.
$$
This is the <summably nested Hausdorff intersection theorem>; the summable movement replaces any need for <compactness>.

Let $(F_n)$ be a <Cauchy sequence> for the <Hausdorff distance>, and put
$$
\delta_n=\sup_{p,q\geq n}\rho(F_p,F_q),\qquad A_n=\overline{\bigcup_{m\geq n}F_m}.
$$
The <bounded set> hypothesis makes $\delta_n$ finite, and the <Cauchy sequence> property gives $\delta_n\to0$. Each $A_n$ is nonempty and a <closed set>, with $A_{n+1}\subseteq A_n$.

We need estimates that survive taking the <closure>. For every nonempty $F$, the <distance to a set> is <Lipschitz continuous> with constant one: the <triangle inequality> gives $\operatorname{dist}(x,F)\leq d(x,y)+\operatorname{dist}(y,F)$, and interchanging $x,y$ proves
$$
|\operatorname{dist}(x,F)-\operatorname{dist}(y,F)|\leq d(x,y).
$$
For every $m\geq n$, points of $F_m$ have distance at most $\delta_n$ from $F_n$. The same holds throughout their union and its <closure>, by this <continuity>. Since $F_n\subseteq A_n$,
$$
\rho(F_n,A_n)\leq\delta_n.
$$
More generally, if $m\geq n$, every point in $\bigcup_{k\geq n}F_k$ is within $\delta_n$ of $F_m\subseteq A_m$. Passing to its <closure> again, and using $A_m\subseteq A_n$, gives
$$
\rho(A_n,A_m)\leq\delta_n.
$$
Choose strictly increasing indices $n_j$ with $\delta_{n_j}\leq2^{-j}$. Then $(A_{n_j})$ satisfies part (ii), so it converges in <Hausdorff distance> to the nonempty <closed set> $G=\bigcap_j A_{n_j}=\bigcap_n A_n$, with $\rho(A_{n_j},G)\leq2^{1-j}$.

For $n\geq n_j$, the <triangle inequality> gives the explicit full-sequence bound
$$
\rho(F_n,G)\leq\rho(F_n,F_{n_j})+\rho(F_{n_j},A_{n_j})+\rho(A_{n_j},G)
\leq2\delta_{n_j}+2^{1-j}\leq2^{2-j}.
$$
It tends to zero as $j\to\infty$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{X\text{ complete}\ \Longrightarrow\ (\mathcal F(X),\rho)\text{ complete}}.
$$
This proves <completeness of the Hausdorff hyperspace> using the proposed tail closures, rather than assuming their diameters shrink.

A <metric space> $X$ is a <compact space> if every <open cover> $X=\bigcup_{\alpha\in I}U_\alpha$, with all $U_\alpha$ open in $X$, has a <finite subcover>: finitely many indices $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_r$ cover $X$.

It is a <sequentially compact space> if every <sequence> $(x_n)$ in $X$ has a <convergent subsequence> $(x_{n_k})$ whose <limit of a sequence> belongs to $X$. In formulas,
$$
\boxed{\forall(x_n)\subseteq X\ \exists x\in X,\ n_1<n_2<\cdots:\ d(x_{n_k},x)\to0}.
$$
A <finite subcover> is the defining test for <compactness>; a <convergent subsequence> is the defining test for <sequential compactness>.

Let $(x_n)$ be any <sequence> in the <compact metric space> $X$. There must be an $x\in X$ such that every <open ball> about $x$ contains $x_n$ for infinitely many indices $n$. Otherwise, for each $x\in X$ choose a ball $B(x,r_x)$ containing only finitely many terms, counted by indices. These balls are an <open cover>. A <finite subcover> would imply that only finitely many indices occur anywhere in $X$, a contradiction.

Now choose successively $n_k>n_{k-1}$ with $x_{n_k}\in B(x,1/k)$; infinitely many available indices make this possible. Then $d(x_{n_k},x)<1/k\to0$, so $(x_{n_k})$ is a <convergent subsequence>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{compact metric space}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{sequentially compact space}}.
$$
The proof counts indices rather than distinct points, so it also handles a value repeated infinitely often.

$$
\boxed{d(e^{(n)},e^{(k)})=1}.
$$
Indeed, the difference is $1$ at coordinate $n$, $-1$ at coordinate $k$, and zero elsewhere. No subsequence is a <Cauchy sequence>, since arbitrarily late distinct terms remain distance one apart. Every <convergent sequence> is a <Cauchy sequence>, so no <convergent subsequence> exists. Therefore $\boxed{X\text{ is not sequentially compact}}$.

Yes. Let $(x^{(k)})_{k\geq1}$ be any <sequence> in $A$, where $x^{(k)}=(x_m^{(k)})_{m\geq1}$. At coordinate $m$, the values lie in the <compact interval> $[-1/m,1/m]$. By part (a)(ii) we can successively extract a <convergent subsequence> for coordinate one, a further one for coordinate two, and so on. Taking the diagonal indices gives a single subsequence $(x^{(k_j)})$ for which
$$
x_m^{(k_j)}\longrightarrow x_m\quad\text{for every fixed }m.
$$
The <closed interval> bound passes to the limit, so $|x_m|\leq1/m$. Hence $x_m\to0$ and $x=(x_m)\in A$.

Coordinate convergence alone would not imply <supremum norm> convergence. Here the common tail bound supplies the missing estimate. Given $\epsilon>0$, choose $M$ with $2/(M+1)<\epsilon$. For $m>M$,
$$
|x_m^{(k_j)}-x_m|\leq2/m\leq2/(M+1)<\epsilon.
$$
For the finitely many $m\leq M$, coordinate convergence makes all differences less than $\epsilon$ once $j$ is large. Therefore $d(x^{(k_j)},x)<\epsilon$, and
$$
\boxed{A\text{ is sequentially compact in the supremum norm metric}}.
$$
This is an instance of <compactness from uniformly vanishing sequence tails>. The common $1/m$ bound is exactly what the coordinate vectors in part (i) lack.

By the permitted <sequential compactness> of the <product metric> space $X\times X$, the sequence of pairs $(\phi^{(k)}x,\phi^{(k)}y)$ has a <convergent subsequence>, and hence a <Cauchy subsequence>. Choose two of its indices $r<s$ sufficiently late that
$$
d(\phi^{(r)}x,\phi^{(s)}x)+d(\phi^{(r)}y,\phi^{(s)}y)<\epsilon.
$$
The distance-increasing assumption iterates: $d(\phi^{(r)}u,\phi^{(r)}v)\geq d(u,v)$. With $n=s-r\geq1$, use $u=x,v=\phi^{(n)}x$, and then $u=y,v=\phi^{(n)}y$, to obtain
$$
d(x,\phi^{(n)}x)+d(y,\phi^{(n)}y)\leq d(\phi^{(r)}x,\phi^{(s)}x)+d(\phi^{(r)}y,\phi^{(s)}y)<\epsilon.
$$
Both summands are nonnegative, so
$$
\boxed{d(x,\phi^{(n)}x)<\epsilon,\qquad d(y,\phi^{(n)}y)<\epsilon}.
$$
The use of paired iterates ensures that the same positive return time works for both points.

Choose $n\geq1$ as in part (i). Since distances between iterates are nondecreasing, the <triangle inequality> gives
$$
d(\phi x,\phi y)\leq d(\phi^{(n)}x,\phi^{(n)}y)
\leq d(x,y)+d(x,\phi^{(n)}x)+d(y,\phi^{(n)}y)<d(x,y)+2\epsilon.
$$
Letting $\epsilon\downarrow0$ and combining with the given reverse bound proves that $\phi$ is an <isometry>:
$$
\boxed{d(\phi x,\phi y)=d(x,y)\quad(x,y\in X)}.
$$
For surjectivity, fix $x\in X$ and use part (i) with $y=x$ and $\epsilon=1/k$ to choose $n_k\geq1$ satisfying $d(x,\phi^{(n_k)}x)<1/k$. Put $z_k=\phi^{(n_k-1)}x$, interpreting $\phi^{(0)}$ as the identity. By <sequential compactness>, a <convergent subsequence> satisfies $z_{k_j}\to z\in X$. The assumed <continuity> yields
$$
\phi z=\lim_j\phi z_{k_j}=\lim_j\phi^{(n_{k_j})}x=x.
$$
Thus every $x$ has a preimage, and $\boxed{\phi\text{ is surjective}}$. If $X$ is empty, both conclusions hold vacuously. This proves the <distance-increasing self-map theorem for sequentially compact metric spaces>.

At every $z=x+iy\in U$, the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> are
$$
\boxed{u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x}.
$$
To derive them from <complex differentiability>, approach $z$ along real increments $h$. The <complex derivative> gives
$$
f'(z)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(z+h)-f(z)}h=u_x+iv_x.
$$
Approaching along imaginary increments $ih$ instead gives
$$
f'(z)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(z+ih)-f(z)}{ih}=v_y-iu_y.
$$
These limits exist because $f$ is <holomorphic>. Equating their real and imaginary parts proves the equations, together with $f'=u_x+iv_x=v_y-iu_y$.

A sufficient condition is that $U$ is a <connected space>. Suppose, towards a contradiction, that $|f|$ is the same constant $R$ throughout $U$. If $R=0$, then $f=0$, contradicting $f(a)\ne f(b)$.

If $R>0$, differentiate $u^2+v^2=R^2$ in $x$ and $y$, and use the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> from part (a). They give
$$
uu_x+vv_x=0,\qquad vu_x-uv_x=0.
$$
The coefficient <matrix> for $(u_x,v_x)$ has <determinant> $-(u^2+v^2)=-R^2\ne0$. Hence $u_x=v_x=0$, so $f'=0$ everywhere. A <holomorphic function> with zero <complex derivative> is locally constant: within a small disc, differentiate along a straight segment. Local constancy and <connectedness> imply constancy on $U$, again a contradiction.

Consequently,
$$
\boxed{U\text{ connected and }f(a)\ne f(b)\ \Longrightarrow\ \exists c,d\in U:\ |f(c)|\ne|f(d)|}.
$$
This is the <constant-modulus holomorphic function> principle. The extra hypothesis matters: on two disjoint <open discs>, define $f=1$ on one and $f=-1$ on the other. It is <holomorphic> and nonconstant, yet $|f|=1$ everywhere.

The <identity theorem> states: if $V\subseteq\mathbb C$ is a nonempty <connected> <open set> and $p,q$ are <holomorphic functions> on $V$, and their equality set has a <limit point> in $V$, then
$$
\boxed{p=q\quad\text{throughout }V}.
$$
The interior location of the <limit point> and <connectedness> are essential hypotheses.

For the proof put $h=p-q$. At any $z_0\in V$, its convergent <Taylor series> either has every coefficient zero, making $h$ zero on a neighbourhood, or has a first nonzero coefficient, of degree $m\geq0$. In the latter case,
$$
h(z)=(z-z_0)^m k(z),\qquad k(z_0)\ne0,
$$
where $k$ is <holomorphic> near $z_0$. By <continuity>, $k$ is nonzero on a smaller disc. Thus any zero at $z_0$ is isolated unless $h$ vanishes on a neighbourhood. An accumulation of distinct zeros at the assumed interior <limit point> therefore forces the neighbourhood alternative.

Let $E$ be the set of points of $V$ with a neighbourhood on which $h=0$. We have just proved $E\ne\varnothing$, and $E$ is an <open set>. It is also a <closed set> relative to $V$: a point of its <closure> outside $E$ would be approached by distinct points of $E$, hence by zeros, and the same <Taylor series> argument would place it in $E$. Since $V$ is <connected>, $E=V$. Thus $h=0$ on $V$, proving the theorem.

Define $F(z)=\overline{f(\overline z)}$. To apply the <identity theorem>, we first verify that $F$ is <holomorphic>; mere <complex conjugation> of the values of $f$ would not suffice. For $h\ne0$,
$$
\frac{F(z+h)-F(z)}h
=\overline{\frac{f(\overline z+\overline h)-f(\overline z)}{\overline h}}
\longrightarrow\overline{f'(\overline z)}.
$$
Thus $F$ is an <entire function>, just as $f$ is. At each positive integer $n$, the point $1/n$ is real and $f(1/n)$ is real, so $F(1/n)=f(1/n)$. These distinct points accumulate at $0\in\mathbb C$. The <identity theorem> on the <connected> <complex plane> gives
$$
\boxed{f(z)=\overline{f(\overline z)}\quad(z\in\mathbb C)}.
$$
This is <conjugate reflection of a holomorphic function>, with the reality data forcing equality to its reflection.

A function has an <isolated singularity> at $a$ if, for some $r>0$, it is defined and <holomorphic> on the punctured disc $0<|z-a|<r$, while a <holomorphic> extension through $a$ has not been assumed. In particular, its domain omits $a$, or its assigned value there fails to give such an extension. The key local condition is
$$
\boxed{f\text{ holomorphic on }B(a,r)\setminus\{a\}}.
$$
The convention includes <removable singularities>, even when a suitable extension would eliminate the missing point.

An <isolated singularity> at $a$ is a <removable singularity> when there is a <holomorphic function> $F$ on a full disc $B(a,r)$ agreeing with $f$ away from $a$. Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{\lim_{z\to a}f(z)=L\in\mathbb C,\qquad F(a)=L}.
$$
The <Riemann removable singularity theorem> also characterizes this by boundedness of $f$ on a sufficiently small punctured disc. A preassigned incorrect value at $a$ is replaced by $L$.

A <holomorphic function> has a zero of order $n$ at $a$ when it is <holomorphic> on a neighbourhood of $a$ and factors as
$$
\boxed{f(z)=(z-a)^n h(z),\qquad h\text{ holomorphic},\quad h(a)\ne0}.
$$
Equivalently, $f(a)=f'(a)=\cdots=f^{(n-1)}(a)=0$ and $f^{(n)}(a)\ne0$. The <order of a zero of a holomorphic function> is the degree of the first nonzero term of its <Taylor series>.

A function has a <pole> of order $n$ at $a$ when, on a punctured neighbourhood,
$$
\boxed{f(z)=\frac{h(z)}{(z-a)^n},\qquad h\text{ holomorphic near }a,\quad h(a)\ne0}.

We prove the <Casorati-Weierstrass theorem> by contradiction. Fix an allowed $\rho$. If the stated image were not a <dense set> in $\mathbb C$, there would be a disc $B(w,\epsilon)$ disjoint from it. Hence
$$
|f(z)-w|\geq\epsilon\qquad(0<|z-a|<\rho).
$$
The <holomorphic function> $h(z)=1/(f(z)-w)$ is bounded by $1/\epsilon$ on that punctured disc. By the permitted <Riemann removable singularity theorem>, it extends to a <holomorphic function> $H$ on the full disc.

If $H(a)\ne0$, then $w+1/H$ is <holomorphic> near $a$ and agrees with $f$ away from $a$. This makes $a$ a <removable singularity> of $f$.

If $H(a)=0$, then $H$ is not identically zero, since $h$ is nowhere zero away from $a$. Its <Taylor series> therefore has a finite first nonzero coefficient: otherwise the <identity theorem> would make it identically zero. Write $H(z)=(z-a)^m K(z)$ with $m\geq1$ and $K(a)\ne0$. Then
$$
f(z)=w+\frac{(z-a)^{-m}}{K(z)}
$$
has a <pole> of order $m$ at $a$, since multiplying by $(z-a)^m$ gives the nonzero limit $1/K(a)$.

Both cases contradict an <essential singularity>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\overline{f(B(a,\rho)\setminus\{a\})}=\mathbb C\quad\text{for every allowed }\rho>0}.
$$

Use the local factorization for a <pole> of order $n$:
$$
f(z)=(z-a)^{-n}h(z),\qquad h(a)\ne0.

Shrink the disc so that the <holomorphic function> $h$ has no zeros there. Differentiating and dividing gives the <logarithmic derivative>

This is the pole case of the <integer residue of a logarithmic derivative>.

Let $F=e^g$. It is <holomorphic> and nowhere zero on a punctured neighbourhood of $a$, and the <chain rule> gives
$$
\frac{F'}F=g'.
$$
If $F$ had a <pole> of order $n\geq1$, part (c) would imply $\operatorname{Res}_a(g')=-n$. But the derivative of a <holomorphic function> on a punctured disc has <residue> zero. Indeed, write its <Laurent series>
$$
g(z)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}c_k(z-a)^k,\qquad g'(z)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}k c_k(z-a)^{k-1}.
$$
Termwise differentiation is valid on every smaller closed annulus. The only possible $(z-a)^{-1}$ term comes from $k=0$ and has coefficient zero. Thus
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Res}_a(g')=0\ne-n},
$$
a contradiction. Therefore $\boxed{e^g\text{ cannot have a pole at }a}$. This proves <an exponential has no isolated pole>, including when $g$ has an <essential singularity>; no choice of a <holomorphic logarithm> on the entire punctured disc was assumed.

where $k$ is <holomorphic>. In a small disc $k$ stays nonzero, so $q=g/k$ is <holomorphic> there. Expanding $q$ to first order,
$$
\frac{g(z)}{h(z)}=\frac{q(z)}{z-a}=\frac{q(a)}{z-a}+\text{a function holomorphic near }a.
$$
The <residue> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Res}_a\left(\frac gh\right)=q(a)=\frac{g(a)}{h'(a)}}.
$$
If $g(a)=0$, the quotient has a <removable singularity> rather than a <simple pole>, and the same formula correctly gives zero. This is the <residue formula for a quotient at a simple denominator zero>.

Apply the <residue theorem> to $F(z)=1/(1+z^n)$ on the positively oriented sector contour consisting of the segment $0\to R$, the arc $Re^{it}$ for $0\leq t\leq2\theta$, and the segment $R\omega\to0$, with $R>1$. No <pole> lies on the boundary. Exactly one root of $z^n=-1$ is inside this sector, namely $\zeta$; it is a <simple pole>. Part (a) gives
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{\zeta}F=\frac1{n\zeta^{n-1}}=-\frac\zeta n.
$$
On the returning ray, put $z=\omega x$ with $x$ decreasing from $R$ to zero. Since $\omega^n=1$, the two ray contributions are
$$
\int_0^R\frac{dx}{1+x^n}+\int_R^0\frac{\omega\,dx}{1+x^n}=(1-\omega)I_R,
\qquad I_R=\int_0^R\frac{dx}{1+x^n}.
$$
On the arc, $|1+z^n|\geq R^n-1$, so the <estimation lemma> bounds its <contour integral> by
$$
\frac{2\pi R}{n(R^n-1)}\longrightarrow0.
$$
The <residue theorem> and the convergence already established now give
$$
(1-e^{2i\theta})I=-\frac{2\pi i}{n}e^{i\theta}.
$$
Using $1-e^{2i\theta}=-2ie^{i\theta}\sin\theta$, we conclude
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^n}=\frac\pi{n\sin(\pi/n)}\qquad(n\geq2)}.
$$
The sector also works for $n=2$, when it is the upper semicircle. Its angle is chosen so that $z^n$ has the same value on the two rays.

A <conformal map> at a point $z_0$ is a map that preserves oriented angles between regular differentiable curves through $z_0$. In the differential formulation, it is real differentiable at $z_0$ and its derivative is an orientation-preserving similarity: a rotation followed by multiplication of lengths by a positive constant. The map is conformal on $U$ when it has this property at every point:
$$
\boxed{D f(z_0)=\lambda R_\alpha,\qquad\lambda>0}.
$$
Here $R_\alpha$ is a planar rotation. Conformality is a local property and does not itself require global injectivity. We use the oriented convention standard in <complex analysis>; a reflection reverses oriented angles.

At $z_0$, <complex differentiability> gives
$$
f(z_0+h)=f(z_0)+f'(z_0)h+o(|h|).
$$
Writing $f'(z_0)=\lambda e^{i\alpha}$, the assumption $f'(z_0)\ne0$ gives $\lambda>0$. Its real derivative is therefore the similarity $h\mapsto\lambda e^{i\alpha}h$, a rotation through $\alpha$ followed by scaling by $\lambda$.

More explicitly, for two differentiable curves $\gamma_1,\gamma_2$ with $\gamma_j(0)=z_0$ and nonzero tangent vectors, the <chain rule> gives
$$
(f\circ\gamma_j)'(0)=f'(z_0)\gamma_j'(0),\qquad
\frac{(f\circ\gamma_2)'(0)}{(f\circ\gamma_1)'(0)}=\frac{\gamma_2'(0)}{\gamma_1'(0)}.
$$
Taking the <complex argument> of this ratio shows that the oriented angle is preserved, while nonvanishing ensures both image tangents remain regular. Thus
$$
\boxed{f\text{ holomorphic and }f'\ne0\text{ on }U\ \Longrightarrow\ f\text{ conformal on }U}.
$$

$$
\boxed{F(z)=\left(\frac z{1-z}\right)^2,\qquad |z|<1}.
