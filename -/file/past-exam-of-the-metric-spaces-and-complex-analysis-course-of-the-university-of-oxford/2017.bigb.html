== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

A <sequence> $(x_n)$ in a <metric space> $(X,d)$ is a <Cauchy sequence> if
$$
\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall m,n\geq N:\quad d(x_m,x_n)<\varepsilon.
$$
The index $N$ may depend on $\varepsilon$, but must work for every pair of sufficiently late terms. No candidate limit is part of this definition.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <sequence> converges to $x\in X$ if
$$
\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall n\geq N:\quad d(x_n,x)<\varepsilon.
$$
Equivalently $d(x_n,x)\to0$. The limit must belong to the specified <metric space>, not just to a larger space containing it.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

A <complete metric space> is one in which **every <Cauchy sequence> converges to a point of that space**. For an incomplete example, take $(0,1)$ with the usual <Euclidean metric> and $x_n=1/(n+1)$. It is <Cauchy> because it converges to zero in $\mathbb R$. It cannot converge inside $(0,1)$: any such convergence would also hold in $\mathbb R$, contradicting uniqueness of the limit, since zero is excluded. Thus the ambient metric and the underlying set both matter to <completeness>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $\phi(t)=t/(1+t)$. Every summand is nonnegative and at most $2^{-i}$, so the series defining $d$ converges and $d\leq1$. Symmetry follows from each $d_i$. If $d(x,y)=0$, every nonnegative summand is zero, hence every $d_i(x_i,y_i)=0$ and $x_i=y_i$. The converse is immediate.

For the <triangle inequality>, first observe that for $a,b\geq0$,
$$
\phi(a+b)=\frac a{1+a+b}+\frac b{1+a+b}\leq\frac a{1+a}+\frac b{1+b}=\phi(a)+\phi(b).
$$
Monotonicity of $\phi$ and the triangle inequality for $d_i$ therefore give
$$
\phi(d_i(x_i,z_i))\leq\phi(d_i(x_i,y_i))+\phi(d_i(y_i,z_i)).
$$
Multiply by $2^{-i}$ and sum the convergent nonnegative series. This proves the required triangle inequality for $d$, so **$d$ is a <metric>**. The construction combines a <bounded metric transform> with a <countable product metric>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $(x^{(n)})$ be <Cauchy> for $d$, and fix a coordinate $i$. Every nonnegative summand is bounded by the whole sum:
$$
2^{-i}\phi\!\left(d_i(x_i^{(m)},x_i^{(n)})\right)\leq d(x^{(m)},x^{(n)}).
$$
Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ such that the right-hand side is less than $2^{-i}\varepsilon/(1+\varepsilon)$ for all $m,n\geq N$. Then strict monotonicity of $\phi$ implies $d_i(x_i^{(m)},x_i^{(n)})<\varepsilon$. Hence **every coordinate sequence is <Cauchy> in its original metric $d_i$**, with the index bound allowed to depend on the fixed coordinate.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

By part (ii) and completeness of each factor, choose $x_i\in X_i$ with $x_i^{(n)}\to x_i$. Let $x=(x_i)$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ such that $\sum_{i>N}2^{-i}=2^{-N}<\varepsilon/2$. Coordinate convergence gives a single $n_0$ for which $d_i(x_i^{(n)},x_i)<\varepsilon/2$ for every $i\leq N$ and $n\geq n_0$. Since $\phi(t)\leq t$,
$$
d(x^{(n)},x)\leq\sum_{i\leq N}2^{-i}d_i(x_i^{(n)},x_i)+\sum_{i>N}2^{-i}<\varepsilon.
$$
Thus $x^{(n)}\to x$ in the product metric, proving **the product is complete**. The finite head uses coordinate convergence; the summable tail is controlled uniformly, regardless of how large the remaining original coordinate distances are. This is <completeness of a countable product metric>.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

A <topological space> is <connected> if it cannot be expressed as a union of two disjoint nonempty sets open in that space. Suppose $f(X)$ had such a separation $U\cup V$. Here $U,V$ are open in the <subspace topology> of $f(X)$. Continuity makes $f^{-1}(U)$ and $f^{-1}(V)$ open in $X$. They are disjoint, nonempty because $U,V$ contain image points, and cover $X$. This contradicts connectedness. Hence **a <continuous image of a connected space> is connected**.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

A space is <path-connected> if for every pair $x_0,x_1$ it contains a <continuous path> $\gamma:[0,1]\to X$ with $\gamma(0)=x_0$ and $\gamma(1)=x_1$. For any $y_0,y_1\in f(X)$ choose preimages $x_0,x_1$. A path between those preimages maps to $f\circ\gamma$, a continuous path from $y_0$ to $y_1$ staying in $f(X)$. Thus **a <continuous image of a path-connected space> is <path-connected>**. Surjectivity onto the ambient codomain $Y$ is not needed; the conclusion concerns $f(X)$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Suppose a <path-connected> space had a separation $X=U\cup V$ as above. Choose $u\in U$, $v\in V$, and a continuous path $\gamma$ joining them. The nonempty disjoint sets $\gamma^{-1}(U)$ and $\gamma^{-1}(V)$ would separate $[0,1]$, contradicting <connectedness of an interval>. Hence **<path-connectedness implies connectedness>**. Equivalently, the connected path image from part (i) cannot meet both sides of a separation.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Expanding the <characteristic polynomial> gives
$$
\det(A-\lambda I)=(z-\lambda)^2-x^2-y^2=\lambda^2-2z\lambda+z^2-x^2-y^2.
$$
Therefore **$a=-2z$ and $b=z^2-x^2-y^2$**. Both are <polynomial functions> of the three real coordinates, so they are <continuous functions>, including on the open subset $X$ of <invertible matrices>.

On $X$, $b=\det A\ne0$. Its image contains positive values, for example $b(I)=1$, and negative ones, for example $b(\operatorname{diag}(1,-1))=-1$. If $X$ were connected, its continuous real image would be an interval containing both signs and hence zero. That is impossible. Thus **$X$ is not connected**. More precisely its determinant image is $\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$: take $x=y=0$ for a positive value and $z=y=0$ for a negative one.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

On $X^+$, $z^2>x^2+y^2$, so $z\ne0$ and therefore $a=-2z\ne0$. Yet $a(I)=-2$ and $a(-I)=2$. The same <continuous image of a connected space> argument shows that **$X^+$ is not connected**.

Put $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. The required three disjoint nonempty open sets are
$$
\boxed{X_1=\{z>r\},\qquad X_2=\{z<-r\},\qquad X_3=\{|z|<r\}.}
$$
Their union is precisely $X$, the complement of the double cone $z^2=x^2+y^2$. The <eigenvalues> are $z\pm r$, so $X_1$ consists of <positive-definite matrices> above the upper sheet, $X_2$ of <negative-definite matrices> below the lower sheet, and $X_3$ of <indefinite matrices> outside the double cone. Examples are $I,-I,\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$ respectively.

The two definite cones are <convex sets>. The indefinite region is <path-connected>: shrink $z$ continuously to zero while keeping $r>0$, then change the nonzero vector $(x,y)$ within the <punctured plane>. Thus these sets are also exactly the three <connected components>. This is the <determinant cone for real symmetric two-by-two matrices>.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For a piecewise continuously differentiable <contour> $\gamma:[a,b]\to U$, define its <contour integral> by
$$
\boxed{\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz=\int_a^b f(\gamma(t))\gamma'(t)\,dt,}
$$
adding the integrals over the finitely many smooth pieces if necessary. Continuity of $f$ along the contour makes each integrand integrable. The derivative supplies both the direction and the complex line element; replacing it by its modulus would give a different integral with respect to arc length.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $\phi(t)=re^{it}$, $\phi'(t)=ire^{it}$ and $|\phi'(t)|=r$. The <triangle inequality for an integral> applied to the parametrized <contour integral> gives
$$
\boxed{\left|\int_\phi f(z)\,dz\right|=\left|\int_0^{2\pi}f(re^{it})ire^{it}\,dt\right|\leq r\int_0^{2\pi}|f(re^{it})|\,dt.}
$$
This is the circular-contour instance of the <estimation lemma>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the continuous argument $0<\arg z<2\pi$ on the positive-axis slit plane and define
$$
\boxed{L(z)=\log|z|+i\arg z.}
$$
This <analytic logarithm on the positive-axis slit plane> is holomorphic, satisfies $e^{L(z)}=z$ and $L'(z)=1/z$, and has $L(-1)=i\pi$. That value fixes the branch uniquely on the connected domain. For positive real $x$, the two boundary limits are
$$
L(x+i0)=\log x,\qquad L(x-i0)=\log x+2\pi i.
$$
The jump, rather than a pole of the integrand, is what the keyhole argument exploits.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <Cauchy integral theorem> for a <star-shaped domain> says: if $U$ is open and star-shaped and $F$ is holomorphic on $U$, then **$\int_\gamma F(z)\,dz=0$ for every closed contour $\gamma$ in $U$**. Equivalently, $F$ has a <complex antiderivative> on $U$. Star-shaped means that there is one point of $U$ from which every straight segment to another point of $U$ remains in $U$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Apply the <Cauchy integral theorem> to $F(z)=f(z)(L(z)-i\pi)$ on the slit plane, which is star-shaped about $-1$. For fixed $\varepsilon>0$, first let $\delta\to0$. The upper bank is traversed from $\varepsilon$ to one and the lower bank from one to $\varepsilon$, so their limiting sum is
$$
\int_\varepsilon^1f(x)(\log x-i\pi)\,dx+\int_1^\varepsilon f(x)(\log x+i\pi)\,dx=-2\pi i\int_\varepsilon^1f(x)\,dx.
$$
The outer arc tends to the positively oriented unit-circle integral, interpreting the logarithm by its one-sided boundary values at the cut. On the inner arc, $|L-i\pi|\leq|\log\varepsilon|+\pi$ and $f$ is bounded near zero. Its integral is therefore $O(\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|)$ and tends to zero. The sum of all four contour pieces is zero. Letting $\varepsilon\to0$ gives
$$
\boxed{\int_0^1f(x)\,dx=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\phi f(z)(L(z)-i\pi)\,dz.}
$$
The integral around the full circle is a piecewise integral of the branch's boundary values; $L$ is not a single holomorphic function across the cut-crossing point. This is the <centred logarithmic keyhole identity>.

{description=The upper and lower banks have opposite traversal directions and logarithmic boundary values differing by $2\pi i$. The small circle is traversed clockwise. The slit width is illustrative; its limit is taken before the inner radius tends to zero.}

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

Along $z=e^{i\theta}$ with $0<\theta<2\pi$, the chosen branch has $L(z)-i\pi=i(\theta-\pi)$. Use part (iii) and the circular estimate in part (a):
$$
\left|\int_0^1f(x)\,dx\right|\leq\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|f(e^{i\theta})|\,|\theta-\pi|\,d\theta\leq\boxed{\frac12\int_0^{2\pi}|f(e^{i\theta})|\,d\theta.}
$$
The second step uses $|\theta-\pi|\leq\pi$. The single branch-cut point does not affect the integral. Keeping that weight gives the sharper intermediate bound.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

On the real interval, $h(x)$ is real and $H(x)=h(x)^2\geq0$. Expanding and integrating the finite sum yields
$$
\sum_{r,s=1}^n\frac{a_ra_s}{r+s+1}=\int_0^1h(x)^2\,dx.
$$
Apply part (b)(iv) to the entire function $H=h^2$. Since $|H(e^{i\theta})|=|h(e^{i\theta})|^2$, <orthogonality of complex exponentials> gives
$$
\int_0^{2\pi}|h(e^{i\theta})|^2\,d\theta=\sum_{r,s}a_ra_s\int_0^{2\pi}e^{i(r-s)\theta}\,d\theta=2\pi\sum_{r=1}^n a_r^2.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\left|\sum_{r,s=1}^n\frac{a_ra_s}{r+s+1}\right|\leq\pi\sum_{r=1}^n a_r^2.}
$$
The quadratic form is actually nonnegative, so its absolute value can be dropped. This <shifted Hilbert quadratic-form bound> comes from combining the logarithmic contour estimate with a finite <Parseval identity>.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <identity theorem> says that two holomorphic functions on a connected domain which agree on a set with an accumulation point inside that domain agree everywhere there.

**No such <entire function> exists.** On the <subsequence> $1/(2m)\to0$ indexed by <even numbers>, the proposed function equals $\sin z$. The <identity theorem> would force $f(z)=\sin z$ throughout $\mathbb C$. At the points indexed by <odd numbers> it is instead required to equal $-\sin z$, a contradiction since $\sin(1/n)>0$ for positive $n$. The accumulation point zero lies inside the assumed domain of holomorphy, which is essential to this argument.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

**Yes:** take the <unnormalized sinc function> with its removable value at zero,
$$
\boxed{g(z)=\begin{cases}\sin z/z,&z\ne0,\\1,&z=0.\end{cases}}
$$
Its <power series> is $\sum_{m=0}^\infty(-1)^mz^{2m}/(2m+1)!$, with infinite <radius of convergence>, so it is an <entire function>. At $z=1/n$ it gives $n\sin(1/n)$ as required. The <identity theorem> also proves this entire solution is unique.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

**No such <entire function> exists.** If it did, the entire function $h(z)^2-z$ would vanish at the distinct points $(n/(n+1))^2$, whose <accumulation point> is one. The <identity theorem> would give $h(z)^2=z$ on all of $\mathbb C$.

At zero this forces $h(0)=0$. Differentiating the identity gives $2h(z)h'(z)=1$, impossible at zero. Equivalently, a squared holomorphic function has an even-order zero, whereas $z$ has a simple zero. This is the obstruction expressed by <no entire square root of the identity function>; a square-root branch near one cannot extend holomorphically through zero.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

<Morera's theorem> states that a continuous function on a domain is holomorphic if its contour integral around every triangle whose filled-in interior lies in the domain is zero.

The <uniform limit> $f$ is a <continuous function>, by the <uniform limit theorem>. For any such triangle $T$, the <estimation lemma> gives
$$
\left|\int_{\partial T}(f-f_n)(z)\,dz\right|\leq\operatorname{length}(\partial T)\sup_{z\in\partial T}|f(z)-f_n(z)|\longrightarrow0.
$$
Each integral of $f_n$ vanishes by the <Cauchy integral theorem>. Hence the integral of $f$ vanishes too, and <Morera's theorem> proves **$f$ is holomorphic on $D$**. This is the basic <locally uniform convergence of holomorphic functions> argument; the given uniform convergence on all of $D$ is more than needed.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $|z_0|<1$ and $k\geq0$, the <Cauchy integral formula> for derivatives is
$$
\boxed{f^{(k)}(z_0)=\frac{k!}{2\pi i}\int_\gamma\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^{k+1}}\,dz.}
$$
The formula applies because the closed unit disc lies inside the domain of holomorphy. For $k=0$ this is the ordinary value formula, with $0!=1$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Fix $0<r<1$ and $k\geq1$. On $|z|=1$ and $|z_0|<r$, the <reverse triangle inequality> gives $|z-z_0|\geq1-r$. Subtract the two <Cauchy integral formula> expressions and apply the <estimation lemma>, using circle length $2\pi$:
$$
\sup_{|z_0|<r}|f_n^{(k)}(z_0)-f^{(k)}(z_0)|\leq\frac{k!}{(1-r)^{k+1}}\sup_{|z|=1}|f_n(z)-f(z)|\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus **every fixed-order derivative converges uniformly on each smaller disc**. The right-hand factor tends to zero by the assumed uniform convergence on $D$. Keeping a positive gap $1-r$ from the integration contour is the essential feature of this <Cauchy derivative convergence bound>. If $r\leq0$, the specified open disc is empty and the claim is vacuous.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

Take **$g_n(z)=z^n/n$ and $g(z)=0$**, for $n\geq1$. These functions are entire and
$$
\sup_{|z|\leq1}|g_n(z)|=1/n\longrightarrow0,
$$
so convergence is uniform on the closed unit disc. But $g_n'(z)=z^{n-1}$, giving
$$
\boxed{\sup_{|z|\leq1}|g_n'(z)-g'(z)|=1}
$$
for every $n$. In particular $g_n'(1)=1$ never approaches zero. On every strictly smaller disc the <derivative> does converge uniformly, consistent with part (iii). This is <uniform holomorphic convergence without boundary derivative convergence>.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

<Laurent's theorem> states that a function holomorphic on $R<|z-a|<S$ has a unique expansion
$$
f(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_n(z-a)^n,
$$
converging uniformly on every compact subannulus. Its coefficients are
$$
\boxed{c_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta-a|=\rho}\frac{f(\zeta)}{(\zeta-a)^{n+1}}\,d\zeta,\qquad R<\rho<S,}
$$
with counterclockwise orientation. The value is independent of the chosen intermediate radius. The nonnegative powers form a power series convergent inside the outer radius, and the negative powers form the <principal part of a Laurent series>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <residue> $\operatorname{res}(f;a)$ is **the coefficient $c_{-1}$ of $(z-a)^{-1}$** in the <Laurent series> at the isolated singularity. Equivalently it is $(2\pi i)^{-1}$ times the integral around a sufficiently small counterclockwise circle enclosing $a$.

The <residue theorem> states that if $f$ is holomorphic on a neighborhood of a positively oriented simple closed contour and its interior except for finitely many isolated singularities $a_j$ inside, then
$$
\boxed{\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz=2\pi i\sum_j\operatorname{res}(f;a_j).}
$$
No singularity may lie on the integration contour. A more general contour weights each residue by its winding number.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For $z=x+iy$, $e^{iz}=e^{-y}e^{ix}$, so $|e^{iz}|=e^{-y}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{|e^{iz}|\leq1\iff\operatorname{Im}z\geq0,}
$$
the closed <upper half-plane>. Equality holds on the real axis.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <Taylor expansions> give
$$
e^{iaz}\sin(bz)=(1+iaz+O(z^2))(bz+O(z^3))=bz+iabz^2+O(z^3),
$$
and hence $f(z)=b/z+iab+O(z)$. Since $b>0$, zero is **a <simple pole> with <residue> $b$**. It is not a <removable singularity>, despite the vanishing numerator, because the denominator has a zero of order two.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the upper half-disc of radius $R$, with a clockwise semicircular indentation of radius $\varepsilon$ above zero. The integrand is <holomorphic> inside that indented contour, so its total integral is zero. On the large arc,
$$
f(z)=\frac{e^{i(a+b)z}-e^{i(a-b)z}}{2iz^2},\qquad |f(z)|\leq R^{-2},
$$
because $a+b>0$, $a-b>0$ and $\operatorname{Im}z\geq0$. Its integral has modulus at most $\pi/R$ and vanishes as $R\to\infty$.

On the small clockwise arc, $f(z)=b/z+O(1)$, so the integral tends to $-i\pi b$. Therefore the remaining real-axis integral satisfies
$$
\operatorname{PV}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{iax}\sin(bx)}{x^2}\,dx=i\pi b.
$$
The <imaginary part> is <even> and is an ordinary <absolutely convergent integral>: near zero its integrand tends to $ab$, and at infinity it is $O(x^{-2})$. Taking imaginary parts gives
$$
2\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin(ax)\sin(bx)}{x^2}\,dx=\pi b,\qquad\boxed{\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin(ax)\sin(bx)}{x^2}\,dx=\frac{\pi b}{2}.}
$$
The complex integral needs the <Cauchy principal value> because its <real part> has a $b/x$ <singularity>; the requested sine-product integral does not. This <sine-product integral with inverse-square weight> is an application of the <upper-half-plane indentation rule>.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For a <holomorphic map>, <conformality> means preservation of oriented angles locally; it holds at every point of $G$ exactly when $f'(z)\ne0$ there. A <Möbius transformation> is $M(z)=(az+b)/(cz+d)$ with $ad-bc\ne0$, extended to the <Riemann sphere> at its pole and at infinity.

For three distinct finite points define
$$
T_z(z)=\frac{(z-z_1)(z_2-z_3)}{(z-z_3)(z_2-z_1)},
$$
which sends $z_1,z_2,z_3$ to $0,1,\infty$. If one point is infinite, take the corresponding limit: for $z_3=\infty$ use $(z-z_1)/(z_2-z_1)$; for $z_1=\infty$ use $(z_2-z_3)/(z-z_3)$; for $z_2=\infty$ use $(z-z_1)/(z-z_3)$. Construct $T_w$ similarly. Then **$M=T_w^{-1}\circ T_z$** has the required three images.

For uniqueness, normalize any two proposed maps by these same transformations. Composing one normalized map with the inverse of the other fixes $0,1,\infty$. A Möbius map fixing infinity has $c=0$, one fixing zero then has $b=0$, and fixing one forces $a=d$ up to the irrelevant common nonzero scalar. It is the identity. This proves <sharp three-transitivity of Möbius transformations>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Treat a straight line together with infinity as a <generalized circle> on the <Riemann sphere>. Its finite points have a real equation
$$
A|z|^2+Bz+\overline B\,\overline z+C=0,\qquad A,C\in\mathbb R.
$$
Translations and nonzero complex scalings preserve circles and lines. Under $z=1/w$, multiplying the equation by $|w|^2$ gives
$$
C|w|^2+\overline B w+B\overline w+A=0,
$$
again the equation of a circle or line. Nondegeneracy is preserved because the reciprocal map is a bijection of the sphere. The zero and infinite points are interpreted there, rather than discarded when multiplying denominators.

Every Möbius map with $c\ne0$ factors as
$$
\frac{az+b}{cz+d}=\frac ac+\frac{bc-ad}{c^2}\frac1{z+d/c};
$$
if $c=0$ it is already affine. Thus <Möbius translation-scaling-inversion factorization> and the <reciprocal transformation of a generalized circle> prove **every generalized circle maps to another generalized circle**. A circle becomes a line exactly when its image contains infinity, that is, when the original circle contains the map's pole.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The first region is the lens around zero bounded by the two circular arcs through $-1$ and $1$. Writing $z=x+iy$, its inequalities combine to $x^2+y^2+2|y|<1$. The second region is the open first quadrant, and the third is the open horizontal strip between imaginary parts zero and $\pi/2$.

Set $t=(1+z)/(1-z)=u+iv$. The inverse is $z=(t-1)/(t+1)$, and
$$
1-|z|^2=\frac{4u}{|t+1|^2},\qquad\operatorname{Im}z=\frac{2v}{|t+1|^2}.
$$
Hence the lens condition is exactly $u>|v|$. Multiplying by $1+i$ gives real part $u-v$ and imaginary part $u+v$, both positive exactly under that condition. Therefore
$$
\boxed{M(z)=(1+i)\frac{1+z}{1-z}}
$$
is a <Möbius transformation> mapping the lens bijectively to the first quadrant. Its pole at one lies on the boundary, and its inverse is $z=(w-(1+i))/(w+(1+i))$, well-defined throughout the quadrant. The derivative $2(1+i)/(1-z)^2$ never vanishes in the lens. This is a <lens-to-quadrant Möbius map>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

On the first quadrant choose the holomorphic logarithm with $0<\arg w<\pi/2$. It maps $w=\rho e^{i\theta}$ to $\log\rho+i\theta$, so its image is precisely the strip $0<\operatorname{Im}\zeta<\pi/2$. Its inverse there is the <exponential function>; both derivatives are nonzero. Composing with part (i) gives
$$
\boxed{F(z)=\operatorname{Log}\!\left((1+i)\frac{1+z}{1-z}\right).}
$$
This is a bijective <conformal map> from the lens onto the strip, with inverse
$$
z=\frac{e^\zeta-(1+i)}{e^\zeta+(1+i)}.
$$
The logarithm's branch is fixed by its argument range on the quadrant. Using a different branch would shift the strip vertically by an integer multiple of $2\pi$. This is the <lens-to-strip logarithmic conformal map>.
