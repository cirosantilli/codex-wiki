==== i

===== Solution

A <homeomorphism> $f:M\to N$ is a <bijection> for which both $f$ and $f^{-1}$ are <continuous maps>, using the respective <metric topologies>.
$$
\boxed{f\text{ homeomorphism}\iff f\text{ bijective and }f,f^{-1}\text{ continuous}.}
$$
<Continuity> of a <bijection> alone is insufficient: <continuity> of the inverse is a separate requirement.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <closure> $\overline A$ is the smallest <closed subset> of $M$ containing $A$, equivalently the intersection of all such closed subsets. In a <metric space> it is characterized by
$$
\boxed{x\in\overline A\iff B(x,r)\cap A\ne\varnothing\text{ for every }r>0.}
$$
If some ball misses $A$, its centre lies outside a closed superset of $A$; conversely the complement of any closed superset is an open neighbourhood disjoint from $A$. This verifies the equivalence of the two descriptions.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

**False.** Removing a point from the <real line> leaves two disjoint nonempty open rays, so the remainder is disconnected. Removing a point from the <Euclidean plane> leaves a <path-connected space>: join any two remaining points by a <line segment> if it avoids the removed point, or by two segments through a point not on either of the two lines through the removed point and the chosen endpoints.

A proposed <homeomorphism> would restrict to a <homeomorphism> between these punctured spaces and preserve <connectedness>, which is impossible. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbb R^2\not\cong\mathbb R.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

**False:** the target need not equal the image. Take the one-point <compact space> $M=\{0\}$, $N=\mathbb R$, and the <continuous map> $f(0)=0$. The target is not <compact>, for example because it is unbounded.

The valid theorem is that a <continuous image of a compact space> is <compact>. Thus $f(M)$ is <compact>; <compactness> of $N$ follows if <surjectivity> is added.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

**True.** For $u,v\in f(M)$ choose preimages $x,y\in M$. A <path> $\gamma:[0,1]\to M$ joining $x$ to $y$ exists because $M$ is <path-connected>. The <composition of continuous functions> $f\circ\gamma$ is a <path> in $f(M)$ from $u$ to $v$.
$$
\boxed{M\text{ path-connected}\Longrightarrow f(M)\text{ path-connected}.}
$$
<Surjectivity> onto a larger target is unnecessary because the conclusion concerns the image itself.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

**True.** For any <continuous map>, $f(\overline A)\subseteq\overline{f(A)}$: every open neighbourhood of $f(x)$ pulls back to an open neighbourhood of $x\in\overline A$, and therefore meets $A$ in its preimage.

Apply the same inclusion to the <continuous> inverse of the <homeomorphism>:
$$
f^{-1}(\overline{f(A)})\subseteq\overline{f^{-1}(f(A))}=\overline A.
$$
Applying $f$ gives the reverse inclusion, proving
$$
\boxed{\overline{f(A)}=f(\overline A).}
$$
The closures are taken in their respective ambient spaces.

==== v
{scope}

===== Solution

**True.** For every finite partial sum,
$$
\sum_{j=0}^N|x_j|^2\leq\left(\sum_{j=0}^N|x_j|\right)^2\leq\|x\|_1^2.
$$
The nondecreasing partial sums on the left are bounded, so the square series converges. Hence $\ell^1\subseteq\ell^2$ and
$$
\boxed{\|x\|_2\leq\|x\|_1.}
$$
Applying this <norm> inequality to $x-y$ proves $\|f(x)-f(y)\|_2\leq\|x-y\|_1$. Thus the inclusion is a <Lipschitz continuous> map, in particular <continuous>, and it is well-defined on every element of <l1 sequence space>.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Nonnegativity and symmetry follow from the corresponding <metric> axioms in the two factors. The maximum is zero exactly when both coordinate distances are zero, hence exactly when the ordered pairs are equal.

For the <triangle inequality>, put $p=(m_1,n_1)$, $q=(m_2,n_2)$ and $r=(m_3,n_3)$. Then
$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta(p,r)&\leq\max\{d_M(m_1,m_2)+d_M(m_2,m_3),\ d_N(n_1,n_2)+d_N(n_2,n_3)\}\\
&\leq\max\{d_M(m_1,m_2),d_N(n_1,n_2)\}+\max\{d_M(m_2,m_3),d_N(n_2,n_3)\}\\
&=\delta(p,q)+\delta(q,r).
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore **the maximum formula defines a <metric>**, the <maximum product metric>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Each coordinate distance is at most the maximum, so convergence of the pairs implies convergence of both coordinates. Conversely, for any $\epsilon>0$, coordinate convergence gives indices $N_M,N_N$ after which the respective distances are below $\epsilon$. Beyond their maximum, both are below $\epsilon$, and so is the <maximum product metric>.
$$
\boxed{(x_n,y_n)\to(x,y)\iff x_n\to x\text{ and }y_n\to y.}
$$
The same distance comparison will also handle <Cauchy sequences>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $(x_n,y_n)$ be a <Cauchy sequence> in the product. Since each coordinate distance is bounded by $\delta$, both $(x_n)$ and $(y_n)$ are Cauchy in their respective factors. By <completeness> they have limits $x\in M$, $y\in N$. Part (ii) then gives convergence of the pairs to $(x,y)$.

Thus **the product of two <complete metric spaces> is complete under the <maximum product metric>**. This checks every <Cauchy sequence>, rather than assuming beforehand that it has a coordinate limit.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For coordinate differences $a,b$, the elementary inequalities
$$
\max(|a|,|b|)\leq\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\leq\sqrt2\max(|a|,|b|)
$$
give
$$
\boxed{\delta(p,q)\leq d_2(p,q)\leq\sqrt2\,\delta(p,q).}
$$
The identity map between the two versions of the square is bijective, and both it and its inverse are <Lipschitz continuous> maps by these inequalities. It is therefore a <homeomorphism>. In fact the same comparison works on all of $\mathbb R^2$; restriction to the square causes no difficulty.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Take $M=\mathbb R$ and the constant <contraction mapping> $f(x)=0$. For $p=(0,0)$, $q=(0,1)$,
$$
\delta(p,q)=1,\qquad F(p)=(0,0),\quad F(q)=(1,0),\qquad\delta(F(p),F(q))=1.
$$
No Lipschitz constant less than one can work, so **$F$ need not be a contraction**. More generally, differing only in the second input coordinate already prevents a strict contraction whenever $M$ has two points.

If $f$ has contraction constant $0\leq c<1$, two iterations give
$$
F^2(m,m')=(f(m),f(m')),
$$
and consequently
$$
\boxed{\delta(F^2(p),F^2(q))\leq c\,\delta(p,q).}
$$
Thus **$F^2$ is a <contraction mapping>**. The <swap lift of a contraction> shows why an iterate can contract even when one step does not.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

At $z=x+iy$, compute the <complex derivative> first along real increments and then along imaginary increments. <Holomorphy> gives
$$
f'(z)=u_x+iv_x=\frac{u_y+iv_y}{i}=v_y-iu_y.
$$
Equating real and imaginary parts proves the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>:
$$
\boxed{u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x.}
$$
The partial derivatives exist because these directional difference quotients are limits of the <holomorphic> <derivative>. This argument proves the necessary equations; it does not claim that their pointwise existence alone is sufficient for <holomorphy>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the <principal complex logarithm>:
$$
\boxed{L(z)=\log|z|+i\operatorname{Arg}z,\qquad-\pi<\operatorname{Arg}z<\pi.}
$$
The excluded nonpositive real ray allows a <continuous> choice of argument, and $L(1)=0$. For $z=x+iy$, its <real part> is $u=\tfrac12\log(x^2+y^2)$. Differentiating the chosen local argument gives
$$
u_x=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2},\quad u_y=\frac{y}{x^2+y^2},\quad
v_x=-\frac{y}{x^2+y^2},\quad v_y=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}.
$$
These <continuous> partial derivatives satisfy the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>, proving <holomorphy> on the slit domain. Its <derivative> is
$$
\boxed{L'(z)=u_x+iv_x=\frac{x-iy}{x^2+y^2}=\frac1z.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

A <holomorphic primitive> on the slit domain is $P(z)=zL(z)-z$, since the <product rule> and $L'=1/z$ give $P'=L$. The <line segment> stays in that domain. The <fundamental theorem for line integrals> therefore gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{[1,i]}L(z)\,dz&=P(i)-P(1)\\
&=\left(i\frac{\pi i}{2}-i\right)-(-1).
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\int_{[1,i]}L(z)\,dz=1-\frac\pi2-i.}
$$
The value $L(i)=\pi i/2$ is fixed by the chosen branch.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

In the <upper half-plane>, the argument lies strictly between zero and $\pi$, while $\log|z|$ can be any real number. Conversely, if $w=s+it$ with $0<t<\pi$, then $z=e^w$ is in the <upper half-plane> and $L(z)=w$.
$$
\boxed{L(H)=\{w\in\mathbb C:0<\operatorname{Im}w<\pi\}.}
$$
Thus the <principal complex logarithm> is a <conformal bijection> from the <upper half-plane> onto that horizontal strip, with inverse the <exponential function> restricted to the strip.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $\alpha=a+ib$ with $b>0$. Then
$$
\boxed{f_\alpha(z)=a+bz.}
$$
It preserves the <upper half-plane>, has inverse $(z-a)/b$, and sends $i$ to $\alpha$. Its nonzero <derivative> $b$ verifies that this <bijection> is conformal.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The map $h(z)=e^{\pi z/2}$ is a <conformal bijection> from the width-two strip to the <upper half-plane>, and $h(i)=i$. Its inverse there is $h^{-1}(w)=2L(w)/\pi$.

Write $e^{\pi\beta/2}=A+iB$, where $B>0$. Conjugate the preceding affine half-plane map by $h$:
$$
\boxed{g_\beta(z)=\frac2\pi L\left(A+B e^{\pi z/2}\right).}
$$
Every argument of $L$ lies in the <upper half-plane>. The formula is therefore a conformal <bijection> of the strip onto itself, and
$$
g_\beta(i)=\frac2\pi L(A+iB)=\frac2\pi L(e^{\pi\beta/2})=\beta.
$$
The final equality uses $0<\operatorname{Im}(\pi\beta/2)<\pi$, so no multiple of $2\pi i$ is lost. This is a <strip automorphism with a prescribed image of its midpoint>.

=== a

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

<Morera's theorem> says that a <continuous> complex-valued <function> $f$ on an <open set> $U\subseteq\mathbb C$ is <holomorphic> if
$$
\boxed{\int_{\partial T}f(z)\,dz=0}
$$
for every triangle $T$ whose closed interior is contained in $U$. Triangle boundaries are traversed with positive orientation. The <continuity> hypothesis matters: the zero-integral condition is combined with <continuity>, not used in isolation.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For the usual piecewise smooth complex <path> $\gamma$, let $\ell(\gamma)$ denote its finite length. The <estimation lemma> gives
$$
\left|\int_\gamma f\,dz-\int_\gamma f_n\,dz\right|
\leq\ell(\gamma)\sup_{z\in\gamma}|f(z)-f_n(z)|.
$$
By <uniform convergence>, the supremum tends to zero, so
$$
\boxed{\int_\gamma f\,dz=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_\gamma f_n\,dz.}
$$
The uniform limit is <continuous> on the <path>, so its integral exists. This is <uniform convergence and contour integration>; the <path> length is fixed while the functions vary.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let the common domain be an <open set> $U$. The <uniform limit theorem> makes the limit $f$ <continuous>. For every triangle whose closed interior lies in $U$, <Cauchy's integral theorem> gives $\int_{\partial T}f_n=0$. Part (ii) passes this identity to the limit, giving $\int_{\partial T}f=0$. <Morera's theorem> now proves that **$f$ is <holomorphic>**.

The same reasoning works for <locally uniform convergence>, since each triangle boundary is <compact>. No passage of derivatives through a limit has been assumed.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Fix any bounded disk avoiding the integers, and choose $R$ with $|z|\leq R$ on it. For sufficiently large $|n|$, say $|n|\geq2R$ and $|n|\geq1$,
$$
|n^2-z^2|\geq n^2-|z|^2\geq\frac34 n^2,
\qquad
\left|\frac1{n^2-z^2}\right|\leq\frac4{3n^2}.
$$
The convergent numerical series $\sum_{n\ne0}n^{-2}$ and the <Weierstrass M-test> give absolute and <uniform convergence> of the tails. The finitely many remaining summands are <holomorphic> away from their integer poles. Thus the partial sums converge locally uniformly on $\mathbb C\setminus\mathbb Z$.

By part (a)(iii), **the sum is a <holomorphic function> on $\mathbb C\setminus\mathbb Z$**. The term $n=0$ is $-1/z^2$ and is harmless on this domain; it must not be omitted.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For fixed $z\notin\mathbb Z$, integrate
$$
F(w)=\frac{\pi\cot(\pi w)}{w^2-z^2}
$$
counterclockwise around the specified square of half-side $R_N=N+1/2$, choosing $N>|z|$. The integer poles inside it are $-N,\ldots,N$, and their residues are $1/(n^2-z^2)$ because $\pi\cot(\pi w)$ has <residue> one at each integer.

The two other possible poles are $w=z$ and $w=-z$. Their residues are each $\pi\cot(\pi z)/(2z)$, hence sum to $\pi\cot(\pi z)/z$. This formula still applies when the numerator vanishes and a possible <pole> is removable. The <residue theorem> gives
$$
\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma_N}F(w)\,dw
=\sum_{n=-N}^N\frac1{n^2-z^2}+\frac{\pi\cot(\pi z)}z.
$$
On the square, $|w|\geq R_N$, its perimeter is $8R_N$, and the supplied cotangent bound gives
$$
\left|\int_{\Gamma_N}F(w)\,dw\right|
\leq\frac{8CR_N}{R_N^2-|z|^2}\longrightarrow0.
$$
The series converges absolutely by part (b), so its symmetric partial sums have the same limit as the full sum. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2-z^2}=-\frac{\pi\cot(\pi z)}z=-\frac\pi{z\tan(\pi z)},\qquad z\notin\mathbb Z.}
$$
At half-integers the cotangent form makes the value zero explicit; the reciprocal-tangent notation is understood by its <continuous> extension there. The <cotangent contour summation> rests on the <contour> estimate, not only on formally matching poles.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Write the <Laurent series> about $a$ as $\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}c_k(z-a)^k$. The isolated singularity is:

* <removable singularity> if every negative-index coefficient is zero;
* a <pole> of order $m\geq1$ if $c_{-m}\ne0$ and $c_k=0$ for $k<-m$;
* an <essential singularity> if infinitely many negative-index coefficients are nonzero.

These cases exhaust the possible principal parts. The <residue> is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{res}(f;a)=c_{-1}=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|z-a|=\rho}f(z)\,dz,\quad0<\rho<r.}
$$
For the first example, $\sin z=z+O(z^3)$ gives
$$
\frac1{\sin^2z}=\frac1{z^2}+\frac13+O(z^2).
$$
Thus zero is a **double <pole>**, with <residue> zero. For the other example,
$$
1-\cos z=\frac{z^2}2+O(z^4),\qquad1-e^z=-z+O(z^2),
$$
so the ratio is $-z/2+O(z^2)$. Its singularity is **removable**, with extended value zero and <residue> zero. The numerator's zero has higher order than the denominator's, so there is no <pole>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

A <simple zero> means $h(a)=0$ and $h'(a)\ne0$. The local factorization is $h(z)=(z-a)h_1(z)$, where $h_1$ is <holomorphic> and $h_1(a)=h'(a)\ne0$. Thus
$$
\frac{g(z)}{h(z)}=\frac{g(z)/h_1(z)}{z-a}.
$$
The numerator is <holomorphic> near $a$, so its constant Taylor coefficient is the coefficient of $(z-a)^{-1}$ in the quotient. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{res}\left(\frac gh;a\right)=\frac{g(a)}{h'(a)}.}
$$
If $g(a)=0$, the possible <pole> cancels and the <residue> is zero; the formula does not require $g/h$ actually to have a <pole>. This is the <residue formula for a quotient at a simple denominator zero>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Consider the counterclockwise <unit circle> and set
$$
I=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=1}z^{n-1}e^{-xz/2}e^{x/(2z)}\,dz.
$$
The parametrization $z=e^{i\theta}$ gives
$$
I=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}e^{i(n\theta-x\sin\theta)}\,d\theta.
$$
The <imaginary part> is zero: replacing $\theta$ by $2\pi-\theta$ conjugates the integrand, since $n$ is an integer and $x$ is real. The <real part> is precisely the defining Bessel integral, so $I=J_n(x)$.

Both exponential series converge absolutely and uniformly on the <circle>. Their product may therefore be integrated term by term:
$$
z^{n-1}e^{-xz/2}e^{x/(2z)}
=\sum_{j,k\geq0}\frac{(-1)^j(x/2)^{j+k}}{j!k!}z^{n-1+j-k}.
$$
Uniform <absolute convergence> can also be checked by summing the absolute coefficient bounds, which gives $e^{|x|}$. The <residue> is obtained exactly when $n-1+j-k=-1$, or $k=n+j$. The <Cauchy coefficient formula> consequently yields
$$
\boxed{J_n(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^j}{j!(n+j)!}\left(\frac x2\right)^{n+2j},\qquad n\geq0.}
$$
This proves the <Bessel function of the first kind> series directly from its integral, including the sign and <factorial> normalization.

=== a

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The line through the north <pole> and $p=(a,b,c)$ is
$$
(0,0,1)+t(a,b,c-1).
$$
Its intersection with the equatorial complex plane has third coordinate zero, giving $t=1/(1-c)$ when $p$ is not the north <pole>. The first two coordinates are therefore $a/(1-c)$ and $b/(1-c)$, hence
$$
\boxed{\pi(a,b,c)=\frac{a+bi}{1-c}.}
$$
The north <pole> maps to the point at infinity. This is <stereographic projection> onto the <Riemann sphere>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $z=x+iy$ and $r^2=|z|^2$, solving the <sphere> and projection equations gives
$$
\pi^{-1}(z)=\left(\frac{2x}{1+r^2},\frac{2y}{1+r^2},\frac{r^2-1}{r^2+1}\right).
$$
For $z\ne0$, the reciprocal has real and imaginary parts $x/r^2$ and $-y/r^2$, and squared modulus $1/r^2$. Substituting these into the inverse formula produces
$$
\left(\frac{2x}{1+r^2},-\frac{2y}{1+r^2},-\frac{r^2-1}{r^2+1}\right).
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\pi^{-1}\circ(z\mapsto1/z)\circ\pi:(a,b,c)\longmapsto(a,-b,-c).}
$$
The reciprocal exchanges zero and infinity, corresponding to exchanging the south and north poles, so the formula holds there too. On the <sphere> this is a <rotation> through $\pi$ about the first coordinate axis, not the antipodal map.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

When all three points are finite, use
$$
\boxed{g(z)=\frac{(z-z_1)(z_2-z_3)}{(z-z_3)(z_2-z_1)}.}
$$
The distinctness assumptions ensure that it is a nonconstant <Möbius transformation>, and direct substitution gives the three required values. If one of the specified points is infinity, the limiting formulas are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
z_3=\infty&(z-z_1)/(z_2-z_1)\\
z_1=\infty&(z_2-z_3)/(z-z_3)\\
z_2=\infty&(z-z_1)/(z-z_3).
\end{array}
$$
These cover every case because the points are distinct.

For uniqueness, if $g$ and $\widetilde g$ have the same three images, the Möbius map $\widetilde g\circ g^{-1}$ fixes $0,1,\infty$. Fixing zero and infinity makes it $z\mapsto cz$ with $c\ne0$; fixing one gives $c=1$. Therefore **the normalized map is unique**. This is the <sharp three-transitivity of Möbius transformations>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $G$ be the subgroup preserving the three-point set. Its action gives a <group homomorphism> $\Phi:G\to S_3$. If $\Phi(h)$ is the identity permutation, then $h$ fixes all three points, so part (i) forces $h$ to be the identity Möbius map. Thus the <kernel> is trivial and $\Phi$ is <injective>.

For any permutation $\sigma$, part (i) gives a normalization $g_\sigma$ sending the ordered triple $(z_{\sigma(1)},z_{\sigma(2)},z_{\sigma(3)})$ to $(0,1,\infty)$. If $g$ normalizes the original triple, then $h_\sigma=g_\sigma^{-1}\circ g$ sends $z_j$ to $z_{\sigma(j)}$. Hence every permutation is attained, so $\Phi$ is <surjective>.
$$
\boxed{G\cong S_3.}
$$
This is the setwise stabilizer; its six maps need not fix each point individually.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Choose a Möbius map $T$ with $T(z_1)=0$ and $T(z_2)=\infty$, for example by normalizing the ordered triple $(z_1,z_3,z_2)$. Conjugation $h\mapsto T\circ h\circ T^{-1}$ takes the requested subgroup to the subgroup fixing zero and infinity individually.

Such a transformation must be $z\mapsto cz$ with $c\in\mathbb C^*$: the zero condition removes the constant numerator term and the infinity condition removes the nonconstant denominator term. Conversely every such dilation is Möbius and fixes the two points. Composition multiplies its nonzero parameter.
$$
\boxed{\{h:h(z_1)=z_1,\ h(z_2)=z_2\}\cong(\mathbb C^*,\times).}
$$
This identifies the <Möbius pointwise stabilizer of two points>. Allowing an interchange of the two fixed points would give a larger group, but those interchanges are excluded by the stated equalities.
