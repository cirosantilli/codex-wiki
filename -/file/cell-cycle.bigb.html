= Cell cycle
{wiki}

The <cell cycle> is the sequence of events by which a <biological cell> prepares for and completes division, including <DNA replication> and distribution of <chromosomes>. In <Saccharomyces cerevisiae>, changing bud appearance provides an accessible readout; see the Burgess, Powers and Mell account and Hartwell, Culotti and Reid's primary study cited there.

For a typical cycling <diploid> <eukaryotic> <biological cell>, the order is <G1 phase>, <S phase>, <G2 phase>, then <M phase>. <DNA replication> doubles nuclear <DNA> amount during <S phase>; <mitosis> distributes the copies, and <cytokinesis> separates the daughters. The useful distinction is between copying a library and allocating its copies: <DNA replication> changes how much material exists, whereas division changes which <biological cell> contains it.

\Image[cell-cycle-phases-and-dna-content.png]
{title=The <cell cycle>: copying nuclear <DNA> once, then distributing it to two daughters}
{description=Original schematic for a typical cycling <diploid> <eukaryotic> <biological cell>. Each box states nuclear <DNA> content and <chromosome> count; <M phase> is summarized by its completed daughters. Arrow lengths do not represent phase durations. The <G0 phase> branch shows reversible withdrawal where re-entry is possible, rather than claiming that all nondividing <biological cells> can return.}

<Gene expression> continues to supply <RNA> and <proteins> as the <biological cell> grows; it is different from duplicating the <genome>. In the <yeast cross-species complementation experiment>, restored growth requires successful repeated <cell cycles>, but growth alone does not locate a defect within a phase. Bud appearance in <Saccharomyces cerevisiae> provides another readout, while a <flow cytometric DNA-content assay> asks how much nuclear <DNA> is present and <DNA pulse labeling> asks whether new <DNA> was synthesized during an exposure.

= Mitosis
{parent=M phase}
{wiki}

<Mitosis> allocates copied <chromosomes> to daughter <cell nuclei>. The <spindle apparatus> aligns <sister chromatids> before separation in <anaphase>.

The copying has already occurred during <S phase>, before <mitosis>. Nuclear <chromosome segregation> and <cytokinesis>, the partition of the <cytoplasm>, are distinct processes: two daughter <cell nuclei> can form inside one undivided <biological cell>.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others/The Cell Cycle>[Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others; §10.2, Interphase and The Mitotic Phase; Figure 10.5: replication precedes nuclear division, which can occur without cytokinesis]{ref}

The original cartoon follows a hypothetical <diploid> <animal> <biological cell> with $2n=4$. Its two <homologous chromosome> pairs are $A,a$ and $B,b$; upper- and lower-case letters identify homologues, not dominant and recessive <alleles>. In <prophase> and <metaphase>, each replicated <chromosome> contains two <sister chromatids>: four replicated <chromosomes> contain eight <chromatids>, rather than eight replicated <chromosomes>. The X shape depicts two associated copies, not the shape of every <chromosome> throughout the <cell cycle>.

= Anaphase
{parent=Mitosis}
{wiki}

In mitotic <anaphase>, <sister chromatids> separate into daughter <chromosomes> moving toward opposite poles.

Release of <cohesin>-mediated association permits the sister <centromere> regions to separate. Each former <chromatid> is then counted as an independent daughter <chromosome>. This changes the counting convention without making additional <DNA> copies. For the $2n=4$ example, the whole undivided <biological cell> transiently contains eight daughter <chromosomes>, four moving toward each pole. Movement toward poles and separation of the poles both contribute to partition in the illustrated <animal> setting.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others/The Cell Cycle>[Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others; §10.2, Karyokinesis, Figure 10.6 and Anaphase: release of cohesion, daughter chromosomes and spindle elongation]{ref}

= Mitotic daughter chromosome accounting
{parent=Mitosis}
{title2=$2n=4$}

Count the same hypothetical <diploid> <animal> <biological cell> in three ways: <chromosomes>, double-stranded <DNA> <molecules>, and nuclear <DNA> amount. Assume one continuous double-stranded <DNA> <molecule> per unreplicated <chromosome> or replicated <chromatid>, complete <S phase>, and no losses or <mutations>. Exclude <mitochondrion> <DNA>. Here $n=2$ is the size of one <haploid> set, and $C$ is its unreplicated nuclear <DNA> amount, as defined in <cell-cycle DNA content>. A double-stranded <DNA> <molecule> counts as one, despite containing two strands.

\Image[mitosis-daughter-chromosome-dna-accounting.png]
{title=Four replicated <chromosomes>, eight <DNA> <molecules>, then four of each per daughter}
{description=Original accounting diagram for $2n=4$, counting nuclear <DNA> only. Each colored stroke represents one double-stranded <DNA> <molecule>; the small bridge in a replicated pair indicates association, not a new <DNA> <molecule>. The eight separate strokes in the whole <anaphase> <biological cell> are already partitioning into two groups. The final row states counts per daughter, not their sum.}

The allocation can be checked copy by copy. <DNA replication> turns $A$ into $A_1,A_2$, $a$ into $a_1,a_2$, and similarly for $B,b$. One daughter receives $A_1,a_1,B_1,b_1$ and the other $A_2,a_2,B_2,b_2$. Each therefore inherits the same four <chromosome> identities and the same <gene> and <allele> content as the unreplicated parent, under the stated assumptions. Across both daughters there are $4+4=8$ <DNA> <molecules> and $2C+2C=4C$: partition conserves the material present after <S phase>.

The transient whole-cell count of eight does not mean that each daughter's <ploidy> has doubled. Each completed daughter is <diploid>, containing both homologues of each <homologous chromosome> pair. This differs from separating <homologous chromosomes> during <meiosis I>. Faithful <mitosis> preserves a nuclear <genome> through growth and renewal; equal inherited <genes> need not imply equal <gene expression> or equal <cell fate>. <Mutations> or <chromosome segregation> errors can break the ideal inheritance pattern.

= Kinetochore ablation experiment
{parent=Spindle checkpoint}

In 1995, <Conly L. Rieder>, <Richard W. Cole>, <Alexey Khodjakov> and <Greenfield Sluder> tested whether an unattached <kinetochore> supplies the inhibitory signal delaying <anaphase>. Living PtK1 <biological cells> on glass coverslips were mounted in Rose chambers at $35$–$37\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$. An inverted microscope with a $60\times$ objective recorded <differential interference contrast microscopy> video. A pulsed $532\,\mathrm{nm}$ <laser> focused through that objective destroyed selected <chromosome> regions. The readout was <chromosome> movement and <anaphase> onset before and after surgery.<The checkpoint delaying anaphase in response to chromosome monoorientation is mediated by an inhibitory signal produced by unattached kinetochores by Conly L. Rieder and others>[pp. 941–942, Abstract and Materials and Methods: Cell Culture; Laser Microsurgery and Video-enhanced Light Microscopy]{ref}

Destroying the last unattached <kinetochore> permitted <anaphase> without that <chromosome> aligning. Cutting <chromosome> arms served as a damage control: progression still awaited attachment of the last naturally one-pole-attached <chromosome>. Correlative <electron microscopy> checked the absence of the targeted <kinetochore>. Together these results located an inhibitory activity in or near the unattached <kinetochore>, rather than establishing a requirement for alignment itself.<The checkpoint delaying anaphase in response to chromosome monoorientation is mediated by an inhibitory signal produced by unattached kinetochores by Conly L. Rieder and others>[pp. 943–947, arm-cutting control, Table I, Figures 1, 5 and 6; destruction of the last unattached kinetochore and correlative electron microscopy]{ref}

This intervention localized control; it did not identify every signaling <molecule> or guarantee faithful inheritance after damage. The cultured PtK1 preparation differs from the hypothetical $2n=4$ cartoon. Neither the cartoon nor this experiment establishes identical control in every <species>.

= Cytokinesis
{parent=M phase}
{wiki}

<Cytokinesis> partitions the <cytoplasm>. Without <cytokinesis>, two <cell nuclei> can share one <biological cell>. <Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others/The Cell Cycle>[Biology 2e, §10.2, The Mitotic Phase, Figure 10.6 and Cytokinesis]{ref}

<Cytokinesis> makes physically separate daughter <biological cells> by partitioning their <cytoplasm> and completing their <cell membrane> boundaries. <Mitosis> divides nuclear material. These processes can overlap during <M phase>, but completion of one does not imply completion of the other.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others/The Cell Cycle>[Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others; §10.2, The Mitotic Phase and Cytokinesis: nuclear division versus physical daughter-cell separation]{ref}

For an original compartment-counting example, consider a hypothetical <diploid> <biological cell> after two daughter <cell nuclei> have formed. Let $n=5$ be the number of <chromosomes> in one <haploid> set, and let $C$ be that set's unreplicated nuclear <DNA> amount, as in <cell-cycle DNA content>. Assume faithful <mitosis>, no nuclear <DNA replication> during the partition, and no loss of nuclear <DNA>; exclude <mitochondrion> <DNA>.

[

\Tr[\Td[Nuclear <DNA> across all descendants]\Td[$4C$]\Td[$4C$]]

The check is to follow the two existing <cell nuclei>: initially one boundary encloses both; finally each daughter boundary encloses one. The total remains two <cell nuclei>, twenty <chromosomes> and $2C+2C=4C$ of nuclear <DNA>. Thus the amount per <biological cell> halves because the counting boundary changes, without any new <DNA replication> or change of nuclear <ploidy>. Doubling the number of <biological cells> need not double their combined material.

Coordinating <chromosome segregation> with <cytoplasm> partition lets descendants inherit nuclear information inside separate working compartments. Equal nuclear <DNA> allocations do not require equal daughter sizes or equal allocations of every <organelle>. The geometry of <cytokinesis> connects an internal allocation to the creation of two independently bounded <biological cells>.

= Toroidal sand dollar egg experiment
{parent=Cleavage furrow}

In 1961, <Raymond Rappaport> gently removed membrane and jelly from <sand dollar> <ova> with a constricted pipette. After <fertilization>, he used an inverted <optical microscope> and rack-and-pinion manipulator to press a needle-mounted glass sphere through an egg onto the chamber floor, producing a <torus>-shaped <biological cell>. During the next division, daughters separated from its free ends; an additional <cleavage furrow> crossed between <mitotic asters> of different <spindle apparatuses>. Control eggs supplied the timing comparison. This region lacked intervening <chromosomes> or a previous nearby <spindle apparatus>. This supported <mitotic aster>-associated positioning, without identifying the signal or establishing universal necessity.<Experiments concerning the cleavage stimulus in sand dollar eggs by Raymond Rappaport>[pp. 81–82, introductory experiment summary, Results §1 and Figure 1 legend: horseshoe, second division, control comparison and furrowing between separate mitotic figures]{ref}

= Cell-cycle DNA content
{parent=Cell cycle}
{title2=$(2n,2C)\to(2n,4C)\to2\,(2n,2C)$}

Let $n$ count <chromosomes> in one complete set, and let $C$ denote the nuclear <DNA> amount in one unreplicated <haploid> set of this <organism>. <Ploidy> counts sets, whereas $C$ units count material; $C$ is not a universal mass shared by all <species>. Here we count a paired replicated <chromosome> as one until its <sister chromatids> separate. A copied <diploid> <genome> is therefore still <diploid> through <S phase>: $2n$ persists even as $2C$ becomes $4C$.

An original bookkeeping example uses a hypothetical <diploid> <biological cell> with $n=3$. Name its three <homologous chromosome> pairs $A,a$, $B,b$ and $D,d$. Assume ordinary nuclear <DNA replication>, no <chromosome> loss, and one continuous double-stranded <DNA> <molecule> per unreplicated <chromosome> or replicated <chromatid>. Count nuclear material only, excluding <mitochondrion> <DNA>.

\Table
[
\Tr[\Th[Stage and counting compartment]\Th[<Chromosomes>]\Th[Double-stranded <DNA> <molecules>]\Th[Nuclear <DNA> amount]]
\Tr[\Td[One <G1 phase> <biological cell>]\Td[6 unreplicated]\Td[6]\Td[$2C$]]
\Tr[\Td[One <G2 phase> <biological cell>]\Td[6 replicated]\Td[12 in 6 pairs of <sister chromatids>]\Td[$4C$]]
\Tr[\Td[Whole undivided <anaphase> <biological cell>]\Td[12 separated daughter <chromosomes>]\Td[12]\Td[$4C$ total]]
\Tr[\Td[Each completed daughter <cell nucleus>]\Td[6 unreplicated]\Td[6]\Td[$2C$ each]]
]

For example, $A$ becomes the paired copies $A_1,A_2$ during <S phase>. They remain one replicated <chromosome> until separation. Each completed daughter receives one $A$ copy and one $a$ copy, and similarly for the other two pairs. Across both daughters, $2\times6=12$ double-stranded <DNA> <molecules> and $2(2C)=4C$: division allocates the copied material without another round of <DNA replication>.

The whole undivided <anaphase> <biological cell> transiently contains $4n$ separately counted daughter <chromosomes>. This counting change does not establish a four-set <ploidy> in a completed daughter <genome>: each pole receives $2n$, and each completed daughter <cell nucleus> is <diploid>. Always state whether a number refers to one <cell nucleus>, one pole, or the entire undivided <biological cell>. Compare <meiotic DNA content>: after <meiosis I>, a <haploid> <cell nucleus> can contain $2C$, so amount alone cannot determine <ploidy>.

= Cell cycle analysis
{parent=Cell cycle}
{wiki}

<Cell cycle analysis> assigns evidence to stages of the <cell cycle>. A <flow cytometric DNA-content assay> measures accumulated nuclear <DNA>, whereas <DNA pulse labeling> detects synthesis over an exposure window. Agreement between these readouts is useful because an amount and a synthesis event answer different questions.

= DNA pulse labeling
{c}
{parent=Cell cycle analysis}

<DNA pulse labeling> exposes <biological cells> to a detectable precursor for a bounded interval. After removal, later sampling can follow the labeled material and descendants. A short pulse asks about synthesis during that interval; overnight labeling instead accumulates evidence across many hours. Detectable synthesis does not by itself prove completion of a <cell cycle>, and <DNA repair> can also incorporate precursors.

<EdU labeling> uses a modified <nucleoside>, 5-ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine, incorporated into newly synthesized <DNA>, then chemically couples it to a fluorescent reporter.

A 2008 experiment injected adult mice with $100\,\mathrm{\mu g}$ of this label and inspected intestinal sections 96 hours later. Fixed sections on slides received $10\,\mathrm{\mu M}$ Alexa568-azide for 10 minutes. Imaging with a <fluorescence microscope> combined the reporter image with a Hoechst <DNA> counterstain and a transmitted-light view. Labeled <cell nuclei> marked descendants of <biological cells> synthesizing <DNA> during exposure, rather than identifying current <S phase> at collection. <A chemical method for fast and sensitive detection of DNA synthesis in vivo by Adrian Salic and Timothy J. Mitchison>[A chemical method for fast and sensitive detection of DNA synthesis in vivo, Figure 4 legend: mouse pulse, section preparation and imaging]{ref}

The same study's cultured-cell controls compared overnight $10\,\mathrm{\mu M}$ label, no label, and label with $10\,\mathrm{mM}$ <hydroxyurea>. No-label samples had little reporter background; <hydroxyurea> strongly suppressed incorporation. The camera detected residual signal with a 3-second exposure versus 40 milliseconds under otherwise identical settings, demonstrating why acquisition settings matter. These controls support synthesis-dependent labeling, without proving that every labeled <biological cell> completes division. <A chemical method for fast and sensitive detection of DNA synthesis in vivo by Adrian Salic and Timothy J. Mitchison>[Figure 2 legend, A–M: NIH 3T3 controls, fluorescence and differential interference contrast imaging, exposure-time comparison]{ref}

= Spindle apparatus
{parent=Cell cycle}

The <spindle apparatus> distributes <chromosomes> during division of a <cell nucleus>.

= Spindle pole body
{parent=Spindle apparatus}

The microtubule-organizing structure at a yeast spindle pole. Its location relative to mother and bud contributes to spatial regulation of mitotic exit.

= Central spindle
{parent=Spindle apparatus}

The overlapping spindle microtubule array assembled between segregating <chromosome> masses during <anaphase>, contributing to cytokinetic organisation.

= Mitotic aster
{parent=Spindle apparatus}

A <mitotic aster> is a radial array of <microtubules> around a pole of the <spindle apparatus>.
