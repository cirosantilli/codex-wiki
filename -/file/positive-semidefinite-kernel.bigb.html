= Positive-semidefinite kernel

= Positive-definite kernel
{synonym}

A symmetric function $k$ is a positive-semidefinite kernel when every finite Gram matrix $(k(x_i,x_j))$ is positive semidefinite.

= Mercer kernel
{parent=Positive-semidefinite kernel}
{c}
{title2=$k(x,z)$}

In the compact-domain spectral setting, a Mercer kernel is a continuous symmetric <positive-semidefinite kernel> on a compact space equipped with a finite full-support measure. <Mercer's theorem> yields nonnegative eigenvalues and an expansion $k(x,z)=\sum_j\lambda_j e_j(x)e_j(z)$, giving features $(\sqrt{\lambda_j}e_j(x))_j$ in $\ell^2$. The finite-Gram positivity condition on arbitrary domains is more general than these spectral hypotheses; all such kernels still have a <reproducing-kernel Hilbert space> realization.

= Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for positive-semidefinite kernels
{parent=Positive-semidefinite kernel}
{c}
{title2=$|k(x,y)|^2\leq k(x,x)k(y,y)$}

A real <positive-semidefinite kernel> satisfies $|k(x,y)|^2\leq k(x,x)k(y,y)$. This follows from positivity of its two-point <kernel matrix>. If a diagonal value is zero, the corresponding off-diagonal values must also vanish.

= Kernel matrix
{title2=$K$}
{parent=Positive-semidefinite kernel}
{wiki}

For points $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, the kernel matrix has entries $K_{ij}=k(x_i,x_j)$ and is positive semidefinite.

= Feature map
{parent=Positive-semidefinite kernel}

= Feature function
{synonym}

A feature map sends $x$ to a vector $\phi(x)$ in a vector space with an <inner product> so that $k(x,y)=\langle\phi(x),\phi(y)\rangle$.

= Canonical feature space of a positive-semidefinite kernel
{parent=Feature map}
{title2=$\phi(x)=[e_x]$}

On the finite formal span of symbols $e_x$, define $B(e_x,e_y)=k(x,y)$ and extend bilinearly. For a <positive-semidefinite kernel>, the null vectors of this <positive semidefinite bilinear form> form its <radical of a bilinear form>. Quotienting by that <linear subspace> gives an <inner-product space> and <feature map> $x\mapsto[e_x]$ with $\langle[e_x],[e_y]\rangle=k(x,y)$. This construction works for degenerate and zero kernels.

= Random feature map
{parent=Feature map}

A random feature map is a random function $\widehat\phi:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R^q$ whose expected inner product represents a kernel:
$$
k(x,y)=\mathbb E\langle\widehat\phi(x),\widehat\phi(y)\rangle.
$$

= Random Fourier feature
{c}
{synonym}

Random Fourier features represent a translation-invariant <positive-semidefinite kernel> by sampling frequencies from its spectral distribution and using sine and cosine features. They replace an implicit infinite-dimensional <feature map> by a finite random approximation.

= Gaussian kernel
{title2=$k(x,y)=e^{-\lVert x-y\rVert^2/(2\sigma^2)}$}
{c}
{parent=Positive-semidefinite kernel}

The Gaussian kernel is a translation-invariant <positive-semidefinite kernel> whose canonical <feature map> takes values in an <infinite-dimensional vector space>.

= Linear kernel
{title2=$k(x,y)=x^Ty$}
{parent=Positive-semidefinite kernel}

The linear kernel is the ordinary <inner product> on a real vector space.

= Polynomial kernel
{parent=Positive-semidefinite kernel}
{wiki}
{title2=$k(x,z)=(x^Tz+c)^r$}

For $x,z\in\mathbb R^d$, a polynomial kernel is the <positive-semidefinite kernel> $k(x,z)=(x^Tz+c)^r$, where $r$ is a positive integer and $c\geq0$. The case $c=0$ uses <monomials> of total degree $r$; $c>0$ also includes lower degrees. The <feature map> weights matter: listing each distinct <monomial> once with coefficient one generally gives a different <inner product>.<Gaussian Processes for Machine Learning by Carl Edward Rasmussen and Christopher K. I. Williams>[Section 4.2.2, pp. 89–90, equations (4.23)–(4.24): polynomial kernels and weighted monomial features]{ref}

For the homogeneous quadratic case in two dimensions, take the scaled <quadratic feature map>
$$
\phi(x_1,x_2)=\begin{pmatrix}x_1^2\\\sqrt2 x_1x_2\\x_2^2\end{pmatrix},\qquad k(x,z)=(x^Tz)^2.
$$
The <inner-product> identity is explicit:
$$
\phi(x)^T\phi(z)=x_1^2z_1^2+2x_1x_2z_1z_2+x_2^2z_2^2=(x_1z_1+x_2z_2)^2=k(x,z).
$$
The factor $\sqrt2$ accounts for the two equal cross terms in the square. Thus an <inner product> of three derived coordinates can be obtained from an <inner product> of the two input coordinates followed by squaring. The <kernel trick> uses this equality inside an algorithm; the <feature map> explains which nonlinear <functions> the algorithm can represent.

= Closure property of positive-semidefinite kernels
{parent=Positive-semidefinite kernel}

Nonnegative scalar multiples, sums, pointwise products, pointwise limits, and pullbacks of positive-semidefinite kernels are positive semidefinite whenever the displayed expressions are defined.

= Integral closure of positive-semidefinite kernels
{parent=Closure property of positive-semidefinite kernels}

Suppose $k_\tau$ is a <positive-semidefinite kernel> for every $\tau$ and its diagonal entries are integrable. Positivity of each $2\times2$ <kernel matrix> gives
$$
|k_\tau(x,y)|\leq\sqrt{k_\tau(x,x)k_\tau(y,y)}.
$$
The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> makes every entry integrable, and integrating each nonnegative finite quadratic form shows that $\int k_\tau\,d\tau$ is again a positive-semidefinite kernel.

= Product of positive-semidefinite kernels
{parent=Closure property of positive-semidefinite kernels}

The pointwise product of two <positive-semidefinite kernels> is positive semidefinite because its finite <kernel matrix> is the entrywise product of two positive-semidefinite matrices, to which the <Schur product theorem> applies.

= Laplace kernel
{title2=$k(x,y)=e^{-\lambda\|x-y\|_1}$}
{c}
{parent=Positive-semidefinite kernel}

The Laplace kernel on $\mathbb R^d$ is a translation-invariant <positive-semidefinite kernel>. Independent standard <Cauchy random variables> give its <random Fourier feature> representation through their characteristic function $e^{-|t|}$.

= Reproducing kernel Hilbert space
{c}
{parent=Positive-semidefinite kernel}
{wiki}

A reproducing-kernel Hilbert space is a Hilbert space of functions in which point evaluation is continuous and satisfies $f(x)=\langle f,k(x,\cdot)\rangle$.

= Sum of reproducing-kernel Hilbert spaces
{parent=Reproducing kernel Hilbert space}
{title2=$\mathcal H_{\sum k_j}$}

For <positive-semidefinite kernels> $k_j$ with <reproducing-kernel Hilbert spaces> $\mathcal H_j$, the <reproducing-kernel Hilbert space> of $k=\sum_jk_j$ consists of sums $f=\sum_jf_j$, with
$$
\|f\|_{\mathcal H_k}^2=\min_{\sum_jf_j=f}\sum_j\|f_j\|_{\mathcal H_j}^2.
$$
The minimum is attained uniquely. Indeed, in the <Hilbert space> direct sum $E=\bigoplus_j\mathcal H_j$, the tuples summing to zero form a closed subspace $N$: they are the intersection, over inputs $x$, of the kernels of the continuous maps $(f_j)\mapsto\sum_jf_j(x)$. Every affine fibre has a unique representative in $N^\perp$ by <orthogonal decomposition by a closed subspace>. The <vector> $(k_j(\cdot,x))_j$ lies in $N^\perp$ and represents evaluation in the quotient; its evaluation at $y$ is $\sum_jk_j(y,x)$, identifying the <positive-semidefinite kernel> with $k$.

= Common representer coefficients for a sum of kernels
{parent=Sum of reproducing-kernel Hilbert spaces}

For a real-valued <loss function> depending only on evaluations at $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, and a positive squared-norm penalty, any minimizing tuple in a <sum of reproducing-kernel Hilbert spaces> has the form
$$
\widehat f_j=\sum_{i=1}^n\widehat\alpha_i k_j(\cdot,x_i)
$$
with the same coefficient <vector> for every component. The tuple must be the unique minimum-norm decomposition of its sum. Apply the <representer theorem> to that sum and note that the displayed tuple has total squared <norm> $\sum_j\widehat\alpha^TK_j\widehat\alpha=\widehat\alpha^T(\sum_jK_j)\widehat\alpha$, exactly the <norm> of the sum. This argument is conditional on existence of a <minimizer>; arbitrary <loss functions> need not attain their infimum.

= Reproducing property
{parent=Reproducing kernel Hilbert space}

The reproducing property is $f(x)=\langle f,k(x,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal H}$; it turns point evaluation into an inner product with a kernel section.

= Representer theorem
{parent=Reproducing kernel Hilbert space}
{wiki}

The representer theorem reduces regularized optimization over a reproducing-kernel Hilbert space to the span of kernel sections at the observed points.

= Kernel method
{parent=Reproducing kernel Hilbert space}
{wiki}

A kernel method represents data through a <positive-semidefinite kernel>, allowing algorithms to use inner products in an implicit <feature map>.

= Kernel ridge regression
{parent=Kernel method}

Kernel ridge regression minimizes empirical squared error plus the squared norm in a <reproducing-kernel Hilbert space>. The <representer theorem> reduces it to <ridge regression> with the <kernel matrix> as its feature Gram matrix.

= Prediction-risk bound for kernel ridge regression
{parent=Kernel ridge regression}

For the unscaled objective $\sum_i(Y_i-f(x_i))^2+\lambda\|f\|_{\mathcal H}^2$, the <kernel-ridge hat matrix> is $H=K(K+\lambda I)^{-1}$. Its prediction variance is $\sigma^2\operatorname{tr}(H^2)/n$. For eigenvalues $d_i\geq0$, use $d_i^2/(d_i+\lambda)^2\leq\min\{d_i/(4\lambda),1\}$ and $\lambda^2d_i/(d_i+\lambda)^2\leq\lambda/4$ to bound the variance and the squared bias. The latter also uses the <reproducing property> and <Bessel inequality>, and does not require invertibility of $K$.

= Kernel-ridge hat matrix
{title2=$H_\lambda$}
{parent=Kernel ridge regression}

For kernel matrix $K$ and objective normalized as $n^{-1}\lVert Y-f\rVert_2^2+\lambda\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal H}^2$, the fitted values are $H_\lambda Y$, where
$$
H_\lambda=K(K+n\lambda I)^{-1}.
$$

= Moore-Aronszajn theorem
{c}
{parent=Reproducing kernel Hilbert space}

Every positive-semidefinite kernel $k$ has a Hilbert-space feature map $\phi$ satisfying $k(x,y)=\langle\phi(x),\phi(y)\rangle$, and determines a unique reproducing-kernel Hilbert space.

= Kernel covariance operator
{title2=$\Sigma$}
{parent=Reproducing kernel Hilbert space}

For feature vectors $\phi(x_i)$, the empirical uncentered kernel covariance operator is
$$
\Sigma v=\frac1n\sum_i\phi(x_i)\langle\phi(x_i),v\rangle.
$$

= Kernel trick
{parent=Reproducing kernel Hilbert space}
{wiki}

The kernel trick evaluates <inner products> of <feature-map> outputs through $k(x,z)=\langle\phi(x),\phi(z)\rangle$ without explicitly constructing those <vectors>. An algorithm expressed entirely through these <inner products> can therefore operate on nonlinear derived coordinates using only evaluations of a <positive-semidefinite kernel> on the original inputs. A <classifier> linear in the derived coordinates can have a nonlinear boundary in the original coordinates.

The <polynomial kernel> gives a finite, fully visible example: $k(x,z)=(x^Tz)^2$ corresponds to $\phi(x)=(x_1^2,\sqrt2 x_1x_2,x_2^2)^T$. The <XOR separation by a product feature> below makes the interaction coordinate $x_1x_2$ useful, while <dual prediction with a kernel> shows how to predict without calculating it explicitly.

An arbitrary score called a similarity need not be a <positive-semidefinite kernel>. Symmetry alone, even with nonnegative entries, is insufficient: the symmetric <matrix>
$$
S=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}
$$
has $a^TSa=-2$ for $a=(1,-1)^T$, so it cannot be a <Gram matrix> of real <inner products>. A <positive-semidefinite kernel> requires a <positive semidefinite matrix> for every finite choice of inputs, not just the observed <training data>.<Gaussian Processes for Machine Learning by Carl Edward Rasmussen and Christopher K. I. Williams>[Section 4.1, p. 80: Gram matrices, quadratic-form definition of positive semidefiniteness, and nonnegative eigenvalues]{ref} The existing <Moore-Aronszajn theorem> connects this requirement to a <reproducing-kernel Hilbert space>; this example needs only finite <linear algebra>.

= XOR separation by a product feature
{c}
{parent=Kernel trick}
{title2=$y=-x_1x_2$}

Give the four corners of the square the <exclusive or> labels: $+1$ when the input signs differ and $-1$ when they agree. In the following order,
$$
p_1=(1,1),\quad p_2=(1,-1),\quad p_3=(-1,1),\quad p_4=(-1,-1),\qquad y=(-1,1,1,-1)^T.
$$
No <affine function> $g(x)=a_1x_1+a_2x_2+b$ has the required strictly positive scores at $p_2,p_3$ and strictly negative scores at $p_1,p_4$: both pairs have midpoint zero, so $g(p_2)+g(p_3)=2b=g(p_1)+g(p_4)$ would have to be both positive and negative. This also rules out perfect classification by a single threshold with a consistent tie rule.

Under the <polynomial kernel>'s <quadratic feature map>, the two classes become two distinct <vectors>:
$$
\phi(p_1)=\phi(p_4)=(1,\sqrt2,1)^T,\qquad \phi(p_2)=\phi(p_3)=(1,-\sqrt2,1)^T.
$$
The <hyperplane> $\phi_2=0$ separates them. With $w=(0,-1/\sqrt2,0)^T$ and $b=0$, the score and <classifier> are
$$
f(x)=w^T\phi(x)=-x_1x_2,\qquad h(x)=\operatorname{sgn}f(x).
$$
All four signed scores $y_i f(p_i)$ equal one. In the input plane the boundary is $x_1x_2=0$, the union of the coordinate axes; in the derived coordinates it is a single <hyperplane>. The <feature map> identifies opposite corners, which is harmless for these labels because opposite corners belong to the same class. A fixed <feature map> would not separate labels that disagree at identical mapped <vectors>.

= Dual prediction with a kernel
{parent=Kernel trick}
{title2=$f(z)=\sum_i\beta_i k(p_i,z)+b$}

If the weight <vector> lies in the <linear span> of the training <feature-map> outputs, write $w=\sum_i\beta_i\phi(p_i)$. Then
$$
f(z)=w^T\phi(z)+b=\sum_i\beta_i k(p_i,z)+b.
$$
This is a dual representation: coefficients weight training examples rather than feature coordinates. They need not be positive, and need not be unique when the <kernel matrix> is singular. Some <kernel methods> instead write $\beta_i=\alpha_i y_i$ with $\alpha_i\geq0$ under their own optimization constraints.<The kernel trick by Ryan Giordano>[Kernelizing classification with support vector machines: displayed prediction as a sum of label-weighted kernel evaluations]{ref} The formula is an algebraic conversion for a weight <vector> in this <linear span>, not a claim about every possible weight <vector>. The <representer theorem> gives a broader reason this form appears in regularized <kernel methods>.

For the <XOR separation by a product feature>, let $\Phi$ have rows $\phi(p_i)^T$. The <Gram matrix> of those rows, equivalently the <kernel matrix>, is
$$
\Phi=\begin{pmatrix}1&\sqrt2&1\\1&-\sqrt2&1\\1&-\sqrt2&1\\1&\sqrt2&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
K=\Phi\Phi^T=\bigl((p_i^Tp_j)^2\bigr)_{ij}=\begin{pmatrix}4&0&0&4\\0&4&4&0\\0&4&4&0\\4&0&0&4\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <eigenvalues> are $8,8,0,0$, so it is a <positive semidefinite matrix> of <rank> two, not an invertible <matrix>. More generally, for any real coefficient <vector> $a$ and any finite set of inputs,
$$
a^TKa=\left\|\sum_i a_i\phi(p_i)\right\|_2^2\geq0.
$$
This proves the <positive-semidefinite kernel> condition for the displayed <polynomial kernel> on all of $\mathbb R^2$, rather than checking only this square.

Here $Ky=8y$, so the explicit choice $\beta=y/8$ gives $K\beta=y$ and $\Phi^T\beta=w$. Consequently
$$
f(z)=\frac{-k(p_1,z)+k(p_2,z)+k(p_3,z)-k(p_4,z)}8=-z_1z_2.
$$
For the new input $z=(1,1/2)$, the four <polynomial kernel> evaluations are $(9/4,1/4,1/4,9/4)^T$. Their weighted sum is $-1/2$, and the <sign function> predicts $-1$. No explicit <feature map> is needed for this calculation. These coefficients were constructed to reproduce the chosen separator; no fitting algorithm or <generalization> guarantee is being asserted.

= Computational tradeoffs of the kernel trick
{parent=Kernel trick}

For $d$ input coordinates, the homogeneous quadratic <polynomial kernel> has a weighted <quadratic feature map> with $D=d(d+1)/2$ distinct <monomials>. Computing an <inner product> of already stored mapped <vectors> costs $O(D)$ arithmetic operations, whereas $(x^Tz)^2$ costs $O(d)$ using the input <vectors>. Here $O$ denotes <big O notation>. Higher fixed degrees can make the dimension gap much larger.

Sample count still matters. With $n$ training examples, materializing a dense <kernel matrix> stores $n^2$ numbers and requires $n(n+1)/2$ distinct evaluations using symmetry. A dense direct solve for <kernel ridge regression> typically takes $O(n^3)$ operations after constructing the <kernel matrix>, while a dual prediction with $m$ nonzero coefficients uses $m$ kernel evaluations. Explicit finite <feature maps> can be preferable when $D$ is small relative to $n$, and a stored primal weight <vector> can make prediction depend on $D$ rather than on $n$. <The kernel trick by Ryan Giordano> (sections Ridge regression and The polynomial kernel) explains the change from feature-coordinate equations to training-example equations; neither representation is universally faster.

The <kernel trick> saves explicit feature construction when the <positive-semidefinite kernel> is economical to evaluate; it does not remove the cost of optimization, choose a useful <positive-semidefinite kernel>, or establish <generalization>. <Random Fourier features> illustrate a different tradeoff: replacing the exact <Gaussian kernel> with a finite approximate <feature map> can reduce dependence on the sample count while introducing approximation error.

= Reproducing-kernel Hilbert space of a stationary Gaussian process
{c}
{parent=Reproducing kernel Hilbert space}

If a stationary <Gaussian process> has covariance spectral density $\widehat K$, its reproducing-kernel Hilbert space has norm
$$
\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal H_K}^2
=c\int\frac{|\widehat f(u)|^2}{\widehat K(u)}\,du
$$
on the functions for which this integral is finite.
