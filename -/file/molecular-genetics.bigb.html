= DNA library

A collection of <DNA> molecules representing different sequences. For <directed evolution>, accessible diversity, sequence frequencies and linkage to the measured phenotype matter more than nominal molecule count.

= Library coverage
{parent=DNA library}

The fraction or distribution of intended variants actually constructed or sampled, with those two meanings distinguished. Unequal variant frequencies and repeated clones reduce effective exploration.

= Mutagenesis
{parent=Molecular genetics}

Deliberate or naturally induced generation of sequence changes. In experimental <protein> evolution the method determines the distribution of variants available for testing.

= Mutation load
{parent=Mutagenesis}

The number or distribution of sequence changes per variant in a mutagenized <DNA library>[library]. Higher load explores more distant combinations while commonly reducing functional recovery.

= Saturation mutagenesis
{parent=Mutagenesis}

Diversification of specified sequence positions across a defined set of residues. It focuses screening but can exclude important effects at unchosen sites.

= Retrotransposon insertion
{parent=Molecular genetics}

Insertion of a copied mobile element through an RNA intermediate. Orthologous insertion patterns can inform phylogenetic relationships, with lineage sorting and insertion-site properties considered.

= Ultraconserved element
{parent=Molecular genetics}

A genomic region showing exceptionally strong sequence conservation across the compared lineages. Conserved cores can anchor sequence capture, while associated variable regions contribute phylogenetic information.

= Transcriptomics

= Genetic perturbation

A controlled change to a genetic system used to test a functional hypothesis. Its interpretation depends on specificity, timing and controls for secondary effects.

= Protein engineering
{parent=Genetic engineering}

Modification or design of a <protein> to change its properties. Rational design, library construction and <directed evolution> offer complementary ways to test sequence–function relationships.

= Computational enzyme design
{parent=Protein engineering}

Design of a protein catalyst using computational models of structure and chemistry. Experimental screening and <directed evolution> test and improve the proposed catalytic arrangement.

= Off-target effect

An unintended effect outside the nominated molecular target. Independent reagents, verified target changes and functional rescue help distinguish target-specific consequences from these effects.

= Gene knockout
{parent=Genetic engineering}

A genetic intervention eliminating functional output of a gene. A targeted insertion or deletion is not by itself proof of knockout: remaining alleles, reading frame and functional protein must be checked.

Guide-directed reduction of <gene expression> using nuclease-inactive DNA targeting. In mammalian cells, <dCas9> fused to a repressor can reduce transcription without requiring a DNA break.

= Protospacer adjacent motif

A short target-adjacent <DNA> motif recognized by a CRISPR-associated nuclease. The motif requirement depends on the nuclease; the commonly used <Cas9> from <Streptococcus pyogenes> recognizes NGG.

= Guide RNA
{parent=CRISPR genome editing}

An <RNA> molecule whose complementary sequence directs a targeting complex to a nucleic-acid sequence. In <CRISPR genome editing>, guide complementarity and a compatible <protospacer adjacent motif> jointly constrain targeting.

= Optogenetics
{parent=Genetic engineering}
{wiki}

<Optogenetics> uses genetically introduced light-responsive components to perturb or monitor biological processes with spatial and temporal control. Light-only, construct-only and expression-matched controls help distinguish the intended response from phototoxicity.

= Site-directed mutagenesis
{parent=Molecular genetics}

<site-directed mutagenesis> deliberately changes a specified sequence position. Functional interpretation requires distinguishing a local mechanistic effect from altered abundance, localization or <protein folding>.

= De novo gene birth
{parent=Molecular genetics}

Origin of a new coding <gene> from ancestrally <non-coding DNA>, rather than divergence from a previously coding copy. Failure to detect a distant relative is insufficient evidence: comparative genomic context and evidence of ancestral coding status matter.

= Neofunctionalization
{parent=Molecular genetics}

Acquisition of a new function by a <gene> copy, classically following <gene duplication>. The other copy can retain an ancestral function while changes accumulate in the retained duplicate.

= Subfunctionalization
{parent=Molecular genetics}

Partitioning of ancestral functions or expression domains between duplicated <genes>. Each copy can retain a subset of deployments that a single ancestral copy performed.

= Gene duplication
{parent=Molecular genetics}

Creation of an additional inherited copy of a <gene>. Copies can be lost, retain shared functions, undergo <subfunctionalization>, or undergo <neofunctionalization>; duplication alone does not guarantee a useful novelty.

= Whole-genome duplication
{parent=Gene duplication}

Duplication of the full genomic complement. <gene loss>, <subfunctionalization> and <neofunctionalization> can obscure the original duplicated organization.

= Two-round vertebrate genome duplication hypothesis
{parent=Whole-genome duplication}

The hypothesis that two early large-scale duplication rounds contributed to the multiple paralogous genomic regions of jawed <vertebrates>. <Hox clusters> are one informative set of loci; a single family does not by itself establish <genome>-wide duplication.

= Tandem gene duplication
{parent=Gene duplication}

Creation of adjacent copies of a <gene> or genomic segment. Subsequent divergence can produce a local <gene> family while preserving aspects of ancestral order.

= Tandem duplication

= Birth-and-death evolution

Gene-family evolution through duplication and retention of some copies and loss or pseudogenization of others.

= Paralogy

= Paralogue

= Plasmid
{parent=Molecular genetics}
{wiki}

A <plasmid> is an extrachromosomal <DNA> <replicon>: genetic material maintained by <DNA replication> separately from the host's main <chromosomes>. Separate copying does not mean independence of the host's <enzymes> or resources. Many <plasmids> are circular, but naturally occurring linear <plasmids> also exist.<Conservation of plasmid maintenance functions between linear and circular plasmids in Borrelia burgdorferi by Philip E. Stewart and others>[Stewart, Chaconas and Rosa (2003), abstract opening sentence: natural linear and circular plasmids]{ref}

Engineered <plasmids> can carry <genes> for <genetic transformation> and <functional complementation>. Typical laboratory <plasmids> combine an <origin of replication>, a <selectable marker> and a <plasmid insert>. These are engineering roles, not requirements that every natural <plasmid> carry an experimentally chosen <selectable marker> or <plasmid insert>.<Molecular Biology Reference by Addgene>[Plasmid Elements, Figure 2 and table: origin, antibiotic-resistance gene, insert and promoter; description applies to engineered vectors]{ref}

The useful separation is between keeping a <DNA> fragment, finding its carriers and testing its function. <Molecular cloning> uses propagation to recover more copies for study. <Plasmid restriction mapping> tests a proposed arrangement, while <DNA sequencing> examines its letters. A successful <genetic transformation> or a <biological colony> showing <antibiotic resistance> answers neither question completely. The <yeast cross-species complementation experiment> goes a step further by separating <plasmid> selection from a test of <functional complementation>.

= Expression vector
{parent=Plasmid}

An <expression vector> supplies regulatory and maintenance sequences for producing a chosen <protein> or RNA in a host. The coding insert must have a compatible <promoter (genetics)>, initiation signals and reading frame; successful plasmid propagation does not establish successful expression.

= Plasmid feature map
{parent=Plasmid}

A <plasmid feature map> records the positions of specified <DNA> features. Construct an original circular example of total length $L=6000$ <base pairs>. Coordinates run clockwise from a chosen zero, and $0$ and $6000$ label the same boundary. Positions here denote boundaries between <base pairs>, so the interval from 1000 to 2500 contains $2500-1000=1500$ <base pairs>. This convention avoids counting both endpoints as additional material.

The <origin of replication> is assigned to 200–700, the <selectable marker> to 3200–4000 and the <plasmid insert> to 1000–2500. Other positions are unspecified <DNA>, not empty gaps. Their lengths account for the entire circle:
$$
500+800+1500+3200=6000.
$$
The first three terms are the marked regions; the last is all remaining material. Choosing a coordinate zero does not add a physical break. Rotating every coordinate by the same amount changes the drawing's labels without changing any distance around the <plasmid>.

A <gene promoter> must function in the recipient for the intended <gene expression>; copying a <plasmid> alone does not establish <gene expression>.<Molecular Biology Reference by Addgene>[Plasmid Elements, Promoter Region row: recipient-dependent transcriptional machinery]{ref} The map therefore supports three separate questions: can the carrier persist, can carriers be recovered, and can the selected <DNA> perform the proposed task? The <galactose induction> comparison illustrates the third question in an actual <yeast> experiment.

= Plasmid insert
{parent=Plasmid}

A <plasmid insert> is the <DNA> fragment added to a carrier <plasmid> during construction. In the <plasmid feature map>, its stipulated boundaries are 1000 and 2500, giving 1500 <base pairs>. The label identifies a construction role; it does not establish <gene expression> or <functional complementation>.

= Plasmid restriction mapping
{parent=Plasmid}

<Plasmid restriction mapping> compares fragment lengths after a <restriction digest> with the pattern predicted by specified cut positions. Use the original 6000-<base-pair> <plasmid feature map>. Abstract <restriction enzyme> A cuts once, at 1000. Abstract <restriction enzyme> B cuts twice, at 2500 and 4500. Assume complete cutting of identical circular copies at exactly those sites, with no loss of <DNA>. These assumptions define a length-accounting exercise; the labels A and B do not stand for particular commercial <enzymes>.

|| Complete <restriction digest>
|| Cut coordinates
|| Linear fragment lengths in <base pairs>

| A
| 1000
| 6000

| B
| 2500, 4500
| 2000, 4000

| A and B
| 1000, 2500, 4500
| 1500, 2000, 2500

For the combined <restriction digest>, successive clockwise distances are
$$
2500-1000=1500,\qquad 4500-2500=2000,
$$
and the final fragment crosses the chosen zero:
$$
(6000-4500)+1000=2500.
$$
Their sum is $1500+2000+2500=6000$. The single A cut opens the circle into one full-length linear fragment. It does not produce pieces of lengths 1000 and 5000, because coordinate zero was never an end.

For contrast, stipulate a different starting substrate: a linear 6000-<base-pair> <DNA> fragment with physical ends at 0 and 6000 and the same three internal cut coordinates. Its complete <restriction digest> gives
$$
1000,\quad1500,\quad2000,\quad1500
$$
<base pairs>, summing again to 6000. There are four fragments, including two of equal length. Even an ideal length-resolving comparison would show only three distinct sizes if both 1500-<base-pair> fragments occupy the same <gel band>. Fragment number and visible <gel band> number need not agree.

The general counting argument is short. For $k\geq1$ distinct complete cuts of a circle, each fragment runs from one cut to the next, so there are $k$ fragments. For a linear substrate with $k$ distinct internal cuts, its two pre-existing ends give $k+1$ intervals. Summing successive distances counts every original <base pair> once in either case. The rule assumes internal double-strand cuts and does not treat an uncut circle as a zero-length product.

In practice, compare released linear fragments with an appropriate <DNA ladder> using <agarose gel electrophoresis>. The original uncut circular <DNA> need not migrate like a linear fragment of equal length; the existing <agarose gel electrophoresis> discussion explains that shape-dependent limitation. Partial cutting also changes the expected pattern. In this example, a circular copy cut at 1000 and 2500 but not 4500 yields lengths 1500 and 4500. A 4500-<base-pair> product would therefore be compatible with that missed cut rather than uniquely identifying a different construction.

Matching lengths constrain an arrangement without determining every <nucleotide sequence>. For example, any change of letters preserving all three cut positions leaves this predicted pattern unchanged. Even the order of fragments can be ambiguous when only their unordered lengths are observed. <DNA sequencing> across both joins and through the <plasmid insert> tests additional claims that this length model cannot decide.

= 1973 plasmid construction experiment
{parent=Plasmid}

In his account of the 1973 work, <Stanley Norman Cohen> describes purified R6-5 <plasmid> <DNA> cut with <EcoRI>, reassociated and joined by <DNA ligase>. The mixture entered <calcium chloride>-treated <Escherichia coli>; plates containing <antibiotics> selected <biological colonies> showing <antibiotic resistance>. The recovered pSC102 carried three R6-5 fragments and <kanamycin> <antibiotic resistance>. The team then joined material from pSC102 to the carrier pSC101 providing <tetracycline> <antibiotic resistance>, recovering pSC105 with both forms of <antibiotic resistance>.<DNA cloning: A personal view after 40 years by Stanley Norman Cohen>[Cohen (2013), The Hawaii Meeting and the Initial DNA Cloning Experiments, Figure 2 and Figure 3 caption; pSC102, pSC101 and pSC105 identities]{ref}

Stained <agarose gel electrophoresis>, gradient <centrifugation> and <electron microscope> comparisons characterized recovered <plasmids>. Parental and mixture lanes in Figure 3 supplied fragment comparisons; pSC101 alone gave one <EcoRI> fragment, while pSC105 combined its carrier fragment with the resistance-bearing fragment. This supported joined, propagating genetic material beyond selection alone. The comparisons did not determine every <nucleotide sequence> or establish a universal construction efficiency.<DNA cloning: A personal view after 40 years by Stanley Norman Cohen>[Cohen (2013), paragraphs describing the two laboratories' analyses and Figure 3, top and bottom: parental, mixed and recovered DNA comparisons]{ref}

The interesting inference combines two kinds of evidence: a recoverable <phenotype> identifies descendants worth examining, and the extracted <DNA> tests a structural explanation. The fragment comparisons are molecular <scientific controls>; they answer a different objection from a control for background growth. The hypothetical diagrams here illustrate that logic without reproducing the historical fragment lengths or <biological colony> counts.

= Genetic transformation
{parent=Molecular genetics}

<Genetic transformation> introduces <DNA> into recipient <biological cells>. It can change their <genotype> and sometimes their <phenotype>; the <yeast cross-species complementation experiment> separates detecting introduction of a <plasmid> from detecting its functional effect.

= Plasmid selection and confirmation
{parent=Genetic transformation}

<Plasmid selection and confirmation> separate recovering candidates from establishing what they contain. In an original idealized comparison, stipulate a susceptible recipient and a <selectable marker> that permits growth under the chosen condition. The intended 6000-<base-pair> construction and an empty carrier retaining that marker can both yield <biological colonies>. If removal of the 1500-<base-pair> <plasmid insert> followed by <DNA ligation> joins its two boundaries without losing other material, the empty carrier is $6000-1500=4500$ <base pairs>. Selection for their shared <selectable marker> cannot distinguish these candidates.

Use separate <scientific controls> for separate uncertainties. A recipient-only comparison tests background growth under selection. A known intact <plasmid> tests whether recipient preparation, <genetic transformation> and recovery can yield selected carriers. A carrier-only construction comparison tests recovery in the absence of added <plasmid insert>. Growth on supporting <cell culture medium> tests viability under that supporting condition. These are analytical comparisons for the constructed example, not a list claimed to have been performed in the <1973 plasmid construction experiment>.

Recover candidate <DNA>, compare a complete <restriction digest> with the stipulated pattern, and inspect relevant joins by <DNA sequencing>. Each step narrows the claim: selection supports a growth <phenotype>; length comparison supports size and some structural features; <DNA sequencing> tests the letters sampled. Even a confirmed <nucleotide sequence> does not alone show the intended biological function. = Replica plating
{parent=Molecular genetics}
{wiki}

<Replica plating> transfers samples from corresponding <biological colonies> or patches to several growth conditions while preserving their spatial arrangement. A master plate makes it possible to relate growth or failure at one location to the same source population on another plate.

The master preserves the source <biological colonies>; each replica tests a sample of their <biological cells>. A fabric carrier takes one spatial impression from the master and deposits samples onto fresh plates. Different <cell culture medium> formulations can then reveal different growth <phenotypes> at corresponding sites. The carrier copies locations, not a guaranteed number of <biological cells>. <Replica plating spatial correspondence> therefore lets an investigator connect a readout to a recoverable source without treating every empty replica site as a missing master <biological colony>.

= Replica plating spatial correspondence
{parent=Replica plating}

<Replica plating spatial correspondence> is the preserved association between a master site and its sampled sites on replicas. Label a master site by an <ordered pair> $(r,c)$, specifying its row and column. Keeping the same orientation and viewing convention makes that label refer to the same source on every plate. The <biological colonies> need not have identical outlines, because the transferred samples can contain different numbers of <biological cells>.

A simple orientation check shows why a circular outline is insufficient. In a constructed four-by-four layout, a half-turn sends $(r,c)$ to $(5-r,5-c)$. Thus B2, or $(2,2)$, would be confused with C3, or $(3,3)$. Applying the half-turn twice returns the original <ordered pair>, but applying it once generally selects the wrong source. An asymmetric orientation mark fixes that ambiguity. Two contact transfers also preserve the handedness when all plates are viewed from the same side: any reflection in going from plate to fabric is undone in going from fabric to plate. A fabric-side view must not be silently substituted for a plate-side view.

= Lederberg replica plating experiment
{c}
{parent=Replica plating}

<Joshua Lederberg> and <Esther Lederberg> reported <replica plating> in 1952. They <autoclave>-sterilized velveteen and secured it pile-up on a wood or cork cylinder with a metal hoop. Inverted <agar> plates touched the fabric lightly. Pencil marks or pin indentations fixed orientation. Complete-medium and minimal-medium replicas compared growth of <biological colonies>.<Replica plating and indirect selection of bacterial mutants by Joshua Lederberg and Esther Lederberg>[pp. 399–402, Methods: Replica plating; Figure 1 caption; Results: plate orientation]{ref}

<Escherichia coli> W-1 grew on plain <agar>; serial T-1 <bacteriophage> replicas showed resistant <biological colonies> at matching sites. Corresponding unexposed master regions seeded repeated broth-and-plain-<agar> enrichment, yielding resistant isolates. Stray-<bacteriophage> checks were negative; resistance persisted without challenge. This supported preexisting heritable resistance. The authors noted variable transfer and moisture-dependent resolution.<Replica plating and indirect selection of bacterial mutants by Joshua Lederberg and Esther Lederberg>[pp. 401–403: transfer variability, Clonal occurrence of phage resistant mutants, Indirect selection and stray-phage checks; p. 404, Discussion]{ref}

The <Luria-Delbrück experiment> uses variation between independently grown <cell cultures> to investigate the timing of resistant <mutations>. The <Lederberg replica plating experiment> adds a spatial route to a recoverable source: the tested samples identify an unexposed lineage. These are complementary arguments about <mutation> and <natural selection>. Growth readouts establish a tested <phenotype>; identifying the responsible <nucleotide sequence> requires other evidence, such as <DNA sequencing>.

= Replica plating controls
{parent=Replica plating}

<Replica plating controls> distinguish a growth <phenotype> from failure of the copying process. A replica on a <cell culture medium> that supports the starting <biological colonies> is a touch-transfer <scientific control>: growth at a site demonstrates that viable <biological cells> reached that control site. If a source is visible on the master but absent on both control and restrictive replicas, the restrictive readout is unresolved. If it grows on the control but fails on the restrictive replica, the difference is a candidate condition-dependent <phenotype>.

The touch-transfer <scientific control> still cannot guarantee transfer to every other replica. A known growing comparison on the restrictive <cell culture medium>, repeat sampling, and retesting a recovered source address different possible failures. Likewise, an orientation mark checks which source a site represents; it cannot check whether any <biological cells> arrived. Gentle, aligned contact helps preserve the map. In Investigations in Molecular Cell Biology by <Clare M. O’Connor>, section 12.6: Exercise 2 - Replica plating and complementation, the instructions align master and carrier marks and call for light, even contact.<Investigations in Molecular Cell Biology by Clare M. O’Connor/12.6: Exercise 2 - Replica plating and complementation>[Investigations in Molecular Cell Biology by Clare M. O’Connor; Introductory light-touch instruction and steps 1–2: orientation marks, aligned inversion and gentle even contact]{ref}

For <indirect selection of bacterial mutants>, keep the recovery route on the unexposed master. Returning a carrier that has touched a challenged replica to that master would introduce a possible exposure route. The original experiment's stray-<bacteriophage> check addresses contamination of the retained lineage, while the orientation and touch-transfer <scientific controls> address spatial identity and successful sampling. These questions must be checked separately.

= Indirect selection of bacterial mutants
{parent=Replica plating}

<Indirect selection of bacterial mutants> recovers a source using a related sample's tested <phenotype>, rather than directly challenging the retained lineage. A positive example uses a surviving replica <biological colony> to locate its unexposed source. A negative example uses failure to grow in one condition to locate a source that does grow elsewhere. The second is particularly useful for finding <auxotrophy>: dependence on an externally supplied nutrient.

Construct a four-by-four teaching layout with rows A–D and columns 1–4. The master has 13 <biological colonies>; A4, D1 and D4 are empty. A growth-supporting control replica grows at every occupied master site except B2 and C3. A replica lacking an assumed nutrient X grows at those same control sites except A2 and C1. All other requirements are stipulated equal. These are invented readouts, not observations from the <Lederberg replica plating experiment>.

Let $M$, $C$ and $S$ be the <finite sets> of sites with growth on the master, control and X-omission replica respectively. In this construction $S\subseteq C\subseteq M$, with <cardinalities>
$$
|M|=13,\qquad |C|=11,\qquad |S|=9.
$$
The <set difference>
$$
C\setminus S=\{\mathrm{A2},\mathrm{C1}\}
$$
contains the two condition-dependent candidates. Pick from A2 and C1 on the master, then compare fresh samples of each recovered source with and without X. By contrast,
$$
M\setminus C=\{\mathrm{B2},\mathrm{C3}\}

is unresolved sampling or viability in this first comparison. Neither site should be counted as an X-requirement candidate. The three sites outside $M$ have no master source to recover at all. Being outside $S$ alone supplies only the second condition. Among the seven empty X-omission sites, two are candidates, two are unresolved and three have no master source. = DNA sequencing
{c}
{parent=Molecular genetics}
{wiki}

<DNA sequencing> determines the order of <nucleotide> bases in <DNA>. Sequence data can test molecular rearrangements, as in the <functional plastid-to-nucleus gene transfer experiment>, or support <phylogenetics> after a suitable <sequence alignment>.

An ordered <nucleotide sequence> answers a different question from fragment size. The eight-<nucleotide> strands ACGTTAGC and AACCGGTT have equal lengths and identical counts of <adenine>, <cytosine>, <guanine> and <thymine>, yet different orders. A length-compatible <gel band> therefore cannot identify either strand by itself. <Sanger sequencing> makes length informative by deliberately coupling each stopping position to the identity of its last added <nucleotide>.

That coupling is the beautiful experimental idea: convert an invisible order into a collection of ordered endpoints. <Sanger ladder reading> recovers the newly synthesized strand; <base pairing> and the <reverse complement> then connect that readout to the original template. The distinction also matters when checking a <mutation>: a reliable local <nucleotide sequence> supports a particular molecular change, while its effect on a <phenotype> requires additional evidence.

= Dideoxy sequencing
{synonym}

<Sanger sequencing> determines a <DNA> <nucleotide sequence> by extending a common <sequencing primer> and occasionally incorporating a <dideoxynucleoside triphosphate>. A normal <deoxynucleoside triphosphate> already has <hydrogen> in place of a $2'$ <hydroxy group>; it retains the $3'$ <hydroxy group>. A <dideoxynucleoside triphosphate> lacks that $3'$ <hydroxy group> too. It can join the growing strand because the preceding <nucleotide> supplies the reacting $3'$ <hydroxy group>. Once incorporated, its own $3'$ end cannot form the next <phosphodiester bond>. Thus the blocked step is the next addition, not attachment of the terminator itself.<Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others/Isolating, Cloning, and Sequencing DNA>[Alberts and others, Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition (2002), Isolating, Cloning, and Sequencing DNA, Figure 8-36 and accompanying dideoxy-method explanation]{ref}

In the historical four-reaction format, each tube receives copies of the same template, the same <sequencing primer>, a <DNA polymerase> and all four ordinary <deoxynucleoside triphosphates>. The tubes differ in their terminating substrate: ddATP, ddCTP, ddGTP or ddTTP. In the A reaction, ordinary dATP can allow copying to continue past one A position, whereas incorporation of ddATP stops another copy there. Competition produces a family of endpoints, rather than every strand stopping at the first A. Adjusting substrate proportions makes later endpoints detectable. Historical <denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis> suppressed strand folding and resolved these adjacent lengths; radioactive labels were detected by <autoradiography>. = Sanger sequencing controls
{c}
{parent=Sanger sequencing}

= PCR amplification
{c}
{synonym}

The <polymerase chain reaction> amplifies a selected <DNA> region using repeated copying steps. Amplified material can be examined by <DNA sequencing>; appropriate template controls help distinguish a new rearrangement from material already present in the starting preparation.

The essential trick is to choose two <PCR primers> on opposite <DNA> strands with their extendible $3'$ ends facing into the same interval. A <DNA polymerase> extends each <PCR primer> $5'\to3'$; the resulting <DNA> can itself become a template. The <amplicon> is therefore selected by two opposed <PCR primers>, rather than by the <DNA polymerase> recognizing a named <gene>. This makes <PCR amplification> useful for inspecting one interval within a much larger <genome>. Here PCR means <polymerase chain reaction>; the statistical abbreviation <PCR> means <principal component regression>.<Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) by Addgene>[Background Information: two primers, template-directed copying and product verification]{ref}

The right <PCR primer>, written in its own $5'$-to-$3'$ direction, is $5'\text{-}\mathrm{GCTG}\text{-}3'$: the <reverse complement> of CAGC. It binds the upper strand and extends leftward in the drawing. With flanks outside this interval, the bounded <amplicon> contains $4+6+4=14$ <base pairs>, including both <PCR primer> sequences. = Amplicon
{parent=Polymerase chain reaction}
{wiki}

An <amplicon> is a <nucleic acid> product of amplification. In ordinary <PCR amplification> without added primer tails, its boundaries eventually correspond to the outer ends of the two <PCR primer> binding sites. First-cycle extensions on long starting templates can run beyond the opposite site; exact-length single strands appear in cycle 2 and exact-length duplex products in cycle 3 under complete extension. This explains why the bounded product becomes dominant even though the initial extensions need not have its length.

= PCR thermal cycle
{c}
{parent=Polymerase chain reaction}

A <PCR thermal cycle> separates three jobs. During <PCR denaturation>, heating separates the paired <DNA> strands without intentionally cutting their <phosphodiester bonds>. During <PCR annealing>, cooling permits <base pairing> between <PCR primers> and templates. During <PCR extension>, the chosen interval supports <DNA polymerase> synthesis from the paired $3'$ ends. Each new <nucleotide> is supplied as a <deoxynucleoside triphosphate>; its incorporation lengthens the strand. The <DNA polymerase> is a <catalyst>, while these <nucleotides> are consumed substrates.<Principles of PCR by Thermo Fisher Scientific>[Principles of PCR, accompanying description: denaturation, annealing, primer-dependent extension and dNTP incorporation]{ref}

<Taq polymerase> withstands repeated high-<temperature> strand separation better than a heat-sensitive <DNA polymerase>. Heat resistance does not mean constant activity at every stage: the separation <temperature> serves the <DNA>, whereas the extension <temperature> serves the copying <enzyme>. As one institutional example, Addgene's Basic PCR Program specifies $94^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ separation, $55^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ primer binding and $72^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ extension. = PCR denaturation
{c}
{parent=PCR thermal cycle}

<PCR denaturation> is the high-<temperature> stage that separates a <DNA> duplex into single-stranded templates for the next copying step. It disrupts the pairing and stacking arrangement of <nucleobases>; it does not require cutting the <phosphodiester bonds> along either strand. The distinction matters: breaking the backbone would damage the ordered <nucleotide sequence> that is meant to serve as a template.

For an original eight-<base pair> duplex, complete <PCR denaturation> exposes two eight-<nucleotide> strands. It leaves sixteen <nucleotides> altogether: separation has made the templates accessible without yet making more <DNA>. <PCR extension>, rather than heating alone, adds the new material. Already copied duplexes also need to separate at the beginning of subsequent cycles.

Separation is a condition-dependent process. Template composition and the <buffer solution> affect the required <temperature> and duration; heating longer is not automatically better because prolonged heating can reduce <DNA polymerase> activity. Incomplete separation leaves fewer accessible templates even when the <PCR primers> themselves are well chosen.<PCR Cycling Parameters—Six Key Considerations for Success by Thermo Fisher Scientific>[Template DNA denaturation assessment, paragraphs 1 and 3–6; DNA denaturation considerations, paragraph 1]{ref}

{c}
{parent=PCR thermal cycle}

= PCR extension
{c}
{parent=PCR thermal cycle}

<PCR extension> is template-directed construction of <DNA> from an annealed <PCR primer> by a <DNA polymerase>. Incoming <deoxynucleoside triphosphates> supply the new <nucleotides>. Each addition lengthens the growing strand at its $3'$ end, so the product grows $5'\to3'$ while the template is read $3'\to5'$. For a complete worked copying step, start with template $3'\text{-}\mathrm{TCAGGGAC}\text{-}5'$ and paired <PCR primer> $5'\text{-}\mathrm{AGTC}\text{-}3'$. The next four template letters are GGAC, encountered in the template's $3'$-to-$5'$ direction. The <base pairing> rule therefore selects CCTG in order, giving
$$
5'\text{-}\underbrace{\mathrm{AGTC}}_{\text{primer}}\underbrace{\mathrm{CCTG}}_{\text{extension}}\text{-}3'.
$$
The <PCR primer> supplies four <nucleotides>, and <PCR extension> adds four more; this copying event consumes four <deoxynucleoside triphosphates>. On the oppositely oriented template $5'\text{-}\mathrm{AGTCCCTG}\text{-}3'$, the other <PCR primer> is $5'\text{-}\mathrm{CAGG}\text{-}3'$. It pairs at the right and extends leftward. The direction argument is short: the <PCR primer> and its template are <antiparallel nucleic acid strands>; extension takes place at the <PCR primer>'s $3'$ end; hence forward progress along the new strand is backward progress along the template. = PCR efficiency
{c}
{parent=Polymerase chain reaction}
{title2=$E_n$}

For a stipulated population of amplifiable copies, let $N_n$ be the amount after cycle $n$ and define <PCR efficiency> by the fractional new yield
$$
N_{n+1}=N_n(1+E_n),\qquad 0\leq E_n\leq1.
$$
Here $E_n=1$ means one additional copy per existing copy, hence ideal doubling. If every cycle has the same <PCR efficiency> $E$, the <exponential growth> model gives $N_n=N_0(1+E)^n$. Starting with $N_0=100$ copies, twenty ideal cycles give $104\,857\,600$ copies; the stipulated value $E=0.8$ gives about $12\,748\,236$. An efficiency of 80 percent therefore means multiplication by 1.8 each cycle, not retention of 80 percent of the final ideal yield.

In the ideal strand-counting example, all duplex-equivalent material doubles, but exact-length <amplicons> lag initially. Starting from one longer duplex and retaining its two original strands, cycle $n\geq1$ contains $2(2^n-n-1)$ exact-length strands out of $2^{n+1}$ total strands. Thus their fraction is $1-(n+1)/2^n$: zero after cycle 1, one quarter after cycle 2 and one half after cycle 3. This counts strands rather than assuming every strand immediately finds an exact-length partner. The generic yield model above ignores this early distinction.

= DNA replication
{c}
{parent=Molecular genetics}
{wiki}

<DNA replication> copies a double-stranded <DNA> <molecule> into two daughter double-stranded <DNA> <molecules>. In <semiconservative replication>, each daughter retains one parental strand and receives one newly synthesized complementary strand. <DNA polymerases> build the new strands from incoming <nucleotides>; they do not make <RNA> or <proteins> in this copying step. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26850/[Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition (2002): DNA replication mechanisms].

For a five-<base pair> example, separate $5'\text{-}\mathrm{ATGCC}\text{-}3'$ from its partner $3'\text{-}\mathrm{TACGG}\text{-}5'$. <Base pairing> specifies a new TACGG partner for the old ATGCC strand and a new ATGCC partner for the old TACGG strand. With error-free copying, both daughter <DNA> <molecules> have the original <nucleotide sequence>, but different physical strand histories. One initial duplex contains ten <nucleotides>; one complete round produces two duplexes containing twenty <nucleotides>, ten retained and ten newly incorporated.

\Image[dna-replication-semi-conservative.png]
{title=<Semiconservative replication>: follow two original <DNA> strands through two complete copying rounds}
{description=Blue O1 and purple O2 are the original strands; green strands were made in round 1 and orange strands in round 2. Each arrow connects a parent duplex to its daughters. After round 2, two of four duplexes retain an original strand, and the other two retain a round-1 strand. All eight strands have the appropriate complementary <nucleotide sequences>; colors identify strand age, not different <nucleobases>. The flattened duplexes show ancestry and orientation, not a replication fork or molecular shape.}

This is useful for inheritance: both daughter <biological cells> can receive a copy of the <DNA> record. The elegant feature is that <base pairing> lets each strand specify its missing partner. Direction matters too: a replicating <DNA polymerase> reads its template $3'\to5'$ and extends the new strand at its $3'$ end, so synthesis proceeds $5'\to3'$. These are opposite directions on <antiparallel nucleic acid strands>, not two alternative directions of synthesis. The enzyme needs a pre-existing paired strand end to extend; the diagram omits the machinery that starts and coordinates copying. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26850/[Alberts et al.: template-directed synthesis and the requirement for a primer].

The strand accounting predicts observable differences between copying mechanisms. In 1958, Meselson and Stahl grew <Escherichia coli> with the heavier <nitrogen> <isotope> $^{15}\mathrm{N}$, then supplied $^{14}\mathrm{N}$ and measured extracted <DNA> by density-gradient centrifugation. After one generation their samples contained intermediate-density <DNA>; after two, intermediate-density and light <DNA> occurred in equal amounts. This supported <semiconservative replication> in the tested <Escherichia coli> culture. It was an <isotope> assay of sampled <DNA>, not simultaneous testing of every kind of <DNA> or direct colored-strand photography. See <The replication of DNA in Escherichia coli by Matthew Meselson and Franklin W. Stahl>.

= Semiconservative replication
{parent=DNA replication}
{wiki}

In <semiconservative replication>, each daughter duplex retains one strand of its immediate parent. Under complete copying without strand loss or exchange, one starting duplex gives $2^n$ duplexes after $n$ rounds, but still only two original strands. For $n\geq1$, the fraction of duplexes containing an original strand is $2/2^n=2^{1-n}$: all after one round, half after two, a quarter after three. The fraction of all strands that are original is instead $2^{-n}$. A strand made in round 1 is parental when round 2 begins.

= Gene expression
{parent=Molecular genetics}
{wiki}

<Gene expression> uses a <gene>'s information to produce functional <RNA> or a <protein>. <Transcription> makes <RNA> from a <DNA> template; for a <protein>-coding <gene>, <biological translation> then uses <messenger RNA> to specify an <amino acid> chain. Many <genes> instead produce functional <RNA>, including <transfer RNA> and <ribosomal RNA>, without a translated <protein> product. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26887/[Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition (2002): from DNA to RNA].

Follow the chosen <coding strand> $5'\text{-}\mathrm{ATGGCTTAA}\text{-}3'$: <transcription> of its complementary template gives $5'\text{-}\mathrm{AUGGCUUAA}\text{-}3'$ in <messenger RNA>. In the stated <reading frame>, its three <codons> are AUG, GCU and UAA: <methionine>, <alanine> and a <stop codon>. Thus nine <nucleotides> specify two <amino acid> residues followed by termination, not three residues. This is an illustrative coding segment embedded in suitable expression and initiation context, not a claim that an isolated nine-<nucleotide> <RNA> is sufficient to initiate <biological translation>.

= Transcription
{synonym}

<Transcription> is synthesis of <RNA> using one <DNA> strand as a template by an <RNA polymerase>. During elongation the <RNA polymerase> reads that template $3'\to5'$ while extending <RNA> $5'\to3'$. Only the selected region is transcribed; other regions need not be active, and the template strand can differ between <genes>. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26887/[Alberts et al.: RNA polymerase and selection of transcribed DNA regions].

For the displayed example, align these three <nucleotide sequences> in the same left-to-right positions:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{coding DNA:}\quad&5'\text{-}\mathrm{ATGGCTTAA}\text{-}3',\\
\text{template DNA:}\quad&3'\text{-}\mathrm{TACCGAATT}\text{-}5',\\
\text{RNA product:}\quad&5'\text{-}\mathrm{AUGGCUUAA}\text{-}3'.
\end{aligned}
$$
The first template triplet TAC selects AUG: T pairs with A, A with U, and C with G. The next CGA selects GCU, and ATT selects UAA. The <coding strand> therefore matches the <RNA> lettering except for T versus U, whereas the template is complementary. Reading that template in its own $5'\to3'$ direction gives $5'\text{-}\mathrm{TTAAGCCAT}\text{-}3'$; orientation cannot be discarded. The <gene expression> diagram keeps all three rows aligned. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/15-2-prokaryotic-transcription[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: template and coding strands in transcription].

This correspondence makes <nucleotide sequences> interpretable, but replacing T by U is valid only after identifying the <coding strand>. Moreover, <RNA> may be processed after <transcription>, so the initial product and mature <messenger RNA> need not have identical uninterrupted <nucleotide sequences>. <Transcription> also makes functional <RNA> that will not undergo <biological translation>. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26887/[Alberts et al.: RNA processing and RNA as the final gene product].

= Biological translation
{synonym}

<Biological translation> uses a <ribosome> to read <messenger RNA> <codons> $5'\to3'$ and assemble the corresponding <amino acid> chain. <Transfer RNA> adaptors match <codons> through <base pairing> and carry the <amino acids>; the <genetic code> supplies the correspondence. The chain grows from the <N-terminus> toward the <C-terminus>, by adding each new residue at the <C-terminus>. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/15-5-ribosomes-and-protein-synthesis[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: ribosomes, transfer RNA and protein synthesis].

In the <gene expression> diagram, read $5'\text{-}\mathrm{AUG\ GCU\ UAA}\text{-}3'$ in the chosen <reading frame>. Under the standard <genetic code>, AUG specifies <methionine> and GCU specifies <alanine>; UAA is a <stop codon>. The result before any subsequent processing is the two-residue chain N–<methionine>–<alanine>–C, followed by termination. The <stop codon> contributes zero <amino acids>. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/15-1-the-genetic-code[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: the standard genetic code]. This example assumes appropriate initiation at the displayed AUG in a suitable context in a <eukaryote>; the diagram displays the coding segment, not a complete independently translatable <messenger RNA>.

Initiation establishes the starting position and <reading frame>, using a <ribosome>, an initiating <transfer RNA> and additional factors. At a standard <stop codon>, a release factor promotes release of the chain rather than delivery of another <amino acid> by <transfer RNA>. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/15-5-ribosomes-and-protein-synthesis[Clark, Douglas and Choi: initiation and termination of translation]. A short two-residue example illustrates decoding, not the size or folding of a typical functional <protein>.

The useful connection is between two different alphabets: <nucleotides> specify the order of <amino acids> through the <genetic code>. <Base pairing> helps select <transfer RNA>, while the <ribosome> catalyzes joining the delivered <amino acids>. The <messenger RNA> remains a template rather than being consumed as the chain's building material. Separating template, adaptor and product makes clear why <transcription> and <biological translation> are distinct mechanisms.

= Genetic code
{parent=Translation (biology)}
{wiki}

The <genetic code> assigns <RNA> <codons> to <amino acids> or termination signals. Four possible <nucleobases> at each of three positions give $4^3=64$ <codons>. In the standard <genetic code>, 61 specify <amino acids> and three are <stop codons>; several <codons> can specify the same <amino acid>. Variants exist, so state which <genetic code> is being used. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/15-1-the-genetic-code[Clark, Douglas and Choi: genetic code assignments and variants].

= Codon
{parent=Genetic code}

A <codon> is a three-<nucleotide> unit read in a specified <reading frame> during <biological translation>. For example, GCU specifies <alanine> in the standard <genetic code>. Its letters are read $5'\to3'$.

For an original example, choose the <messenger RNA> segment $5'\text{-}\mathrm{AUG\ GCU\ UUC\ UGA}\text{-}3'$ and stipulate initiation at the displayed AUG in a suitable <eukaryotic> context. Under the standard <genetic code>, its first three <codons> specify <methionine>, <alanine> and <phenylalanine>; the fourth is a <stop codon>. The resulting <peptide> before processing is N–<methionine>–<alanine>–<phenylalanine>–C. Twelve <nucleotides> therefore specify three <amino acid residues> and termination. The displayed segment omits the surrounding initiation signals; its <nucleotide sequence> alone is not a complete expression system.

A <codon> belongs to an oriented <nucleotide sequence>: GCU and UCG are different ordered triplets. Four possible <nucleobases>, <adenine>, <cytosine>, <guanine> and <uracil>, allow $4\times4\times4=64$ triplets because each position can be chosen independently when enumerating possible sequences. This is a counting argument, not a claim that <nucleobases> occur independently in real <RNA>. Sixty-one triplets are sense <codons>, meaning <amino acid> assignments, and UAA, UAG and UGA are <stop codons> in the standard <genetic code>.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[§15.1, The Genetic Code, Figure 15.4 and following paragraph: triplets and standard assignments]{ref}

A <DNA library>[library] <codon> with any base at the first two positions and G or T at the third. Its 32 <codons> represent the 20 standard <amino acids> unequally and include one <stop codon>.

= Stop codon
{parent=Codon}
{wiki}

In the standard <genetic code>, UAA, UAG and UGA are <stop codons>: they signal termination of <biological translation> rather than an <amino acid> assignment. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/15-1-the-genetic-code[Clark, Douglas and Choi: stop codons].

A <stop codon> supplies no additional <amino acid residue>. In the constructed AUG GCU UUC UGA example, replacing UGA by UAA preserves termination after the same three <amino acid residues>. Replacing UUC by UAA instead terminates after <methionine> and <alanine>. These are consequences of the stated <genetic code>, not measurements of expression efficiency.

At a standard <stop codon>, a <release factor> promotes release of the <polypeptide> from the <ribosome>. Ordinary delivery of an <amino acid> by <transfer RNA> does not supply a “stop <amino acid>”.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[§15.5, Translation, Elongation, and Termination, paragraph beginning “Termination of translation”: stop codons and release factors]{ref}

= Release factor
{parent=Stop codon}

A <release factor> is a <protein> involved in terminating <biological translation> and releasing the completed <polypeptide> at a <stop codon>.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[§15.5, Translation, Elongation, and Termination, paragraph beginning “Termination of translation”]{ref}

= Reading frame
{parent=Genetic code}
{wiki}

A <reading frame> specifies how consecutive <nucleotides> are grouped into <codons>. Starting at position 1 of AUGGCUUAA gives AUG / GCU / UAA; starting at position 2 gives UGG / CUU with AA left over. The letters are unchanged but the groups differ. A possible grouping alone does not establish an actual <biological translation> initiation site.

For the twelve-<nucleotide> example, number positions from the $5'$ end. The three possible forward <reading frames> start at positions 1, 2 and 3:
$$
\begin{aligned}
1:&\quad\mathrm{AUG\;GCU\;UUC\;UGA},\\
2:&\quad\mathrm{UGG\;CUU\;UCU}\quad(\mathrm{GA}),\\
3:&\quad\mathrm{GGC\;UUU\;CUG}\quad(\mathrm{A}).
\end{aligned}
$$
Parentheses enclose incomplete trailing groups. The second and third rows do not assert initiation of <biological translation> in a <biological cell>. A chosen start fixes which positions belong together; the <ribosome> then advances through consecutive <codons> rather than searching each three-letter window independently.

= ORF

{c}

A sequence interpreted in a particular <codon> frame without an internal in-frame stop, with the boundary convention stated. Prediction of an <open reading frame> alone does not demonstrate <biological translation> or a stable functional product.

= Small open reading frame
{parent=Open reading frame}

A relatively short potential coding region under an explicitly chosen length convention. Evidence of <biological translation> and evidence for a stable functional peptide are different.

= Ribosomal frameshifting
{parent=Reading frame}

A change of reading frame during <biological translation>. It need not change the underlying <DNA> sequence, unlike a <frameshift mutation>. It can be programmed by a recoding signal or occur as a translation error.

= Programmed ribosomal frameshifting
{parent=Reading frame}

<Programmed ribosomal frameshifting> changes the reading frame during translation at a specific recoding signal. A slippery sequence and a downstream RNA structure can bias the event, allowing one transcript to produce different polypeptides in controlled proportions.

= Frameshift mutation
{parent=Reading frame}
{wiki}

A <frameshift mutation> changes downstream grouping into <codons> through insertion or deletion of a number of <nucleotides> not divisible by three. In the constructed <messenger RNA> example, insert C immediately after AUG while retaining the same initiation position: $\mathrm{AUG\;CGC\;UUU\;CUG}\;(\mathrm{A})$. The first <codon> is preserved, but the original downstream groups GCU, UUC and UGA disappear. The displayed fragment now has no complete standard <stop codon>; what happens later depends on its continuation.

The short proof is positional: every downstream <nucleotide> moves by $k$ positions after an insertion of length $k$. Boundaries recur every three positions, so they retain the original phase exactly when $k$ is divisible by three. An insertion of three <nucleotides> can still change the <peptide>, but it does not shift the downstream <reading frame>. This distinction helps interpret <genetic variants> without predicting a particular <phenotype> from grouping alone.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[§15.1, The Genetic Code, Figure 15.5: nucleotide deletion and shifted grouping]{ref}

= Degeneracy of the genetic code
{parent=Genetic code}

<Degeneracy of the genetic code> means that distinct <codons> can specify the same <amino acid>. For example, UUU and UUC both specify <phenylalanine> in the standard <genetic code>.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[§15.1, Figure 15.4 and paragraph defining degeneracy]{ref} In the original AUG GCU UUC UGA example, changing UUC to UUU preserves the predicted <amino acid> order. This illustrates redundancy of assignment, not equality of every possible effect on <gene expression>.

<Degeneracy of the genetic code> differs from ambiguity: multiple inputs share one output, whereas an ambiguous assignment would give a single <codon> more than one meaning in the same specified decoding context. The standard <genetic code> table has one listed assignment per triplet. Alternative <genetic codes>, including those used in some <mitochondria>, and engineered decoding contexts must be specified separately; the standard table is not a universal statement about all <organelles> or synthetic systems.

= Nirenberg and Matthaei experiment
{c}
{parent=Genetic code}
{wiki}

In 1961, <Marshall Warren Nirenberg> and <J. Heinrich Matthaei> ground washed <Escherichia coli> and used <centrifugation> to prepare a <cell-free extract>. Reactions at $35^\circ\mathrm{C}$ combined <ribosomes>, soluble components, <ATP> regeneration and <phenylalanine> carrying a $^{14}\mathrm{C}$ <isotope> label, with or without <polyuridylic acid>, an <RNA> containing only U. After incubation, <trichloroacetic acid> <precipitation (chemistry)>, washing and counting <radioactive decay> detected label in insoluble material. Added <polyuridylic acid> strongly increased incorporation; removing <ribosomes> or <ATP> regeneration suppressed it. Product characterization supported a <phenylalanine> <polypeptide>.<The dependence of cell-free protein synthesis in E. coli upon naturally occurring or synthetic polyribonucleotides by Marshall Warren Nirenberg and J. Heinrich Matthaei>[pp. 1589–1590, Methods and Materials; p. 1592, Table 1 assay; pp. 1595–1600, Tables 6–9 and Figure 6]{ref}

= Auxotrophy
{parent=Molecular genetics}

<Auxotrophy> is a <phenotype> in which growth requires an externally supplied substance that a comparison <biological strain> can make for itself. Growth on supplemented <cell culture medium> formulations can reveal the requirement without identifying the responsible <gene>.
