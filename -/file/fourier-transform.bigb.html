= Fourier transformation
{c}
{synonym}

For $f\in L^1(\mathbb R^n)$, the angular-frequency Fourier transform is
$$
\widehat f(\xi)
=\int_{\mathbb R^n}f(x)e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\,dx.
$$

= Half-line Fourier transform
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$Y_+(k)=\int_0^\infty y(x)e^{ikx}\,dx,\quad Y_-(k)=\int_{-\infty}^0y(x)e^{ikx}\,dx$}

A <half-line Fourier transform> separates the contributions supported on the positive and negative real axes. With the displayed sign convention, a bound $y(x)=O(e^{-\gamma_+x})$ as $x\to+\infty$ gives a <holomorphic> $Y_+$ in $\operatorname{Im}k>-\gamma_+$; a bound $y(x)=O(e^{-\gamma_-|x|})$ as $x\to-\infty$ gives a <holomorphic> $Y_-$ in $\operatorname{Im}k<\gamma_-$. The estimates hold uniformly on compact subsets of these half-planes and remain integrable after multiplication by any fixed power of $x$, which justifies complex differentiation under the <integral>. The overlap strip is the natural domain for a <Wiener-Hopf equation>. <Holomorphic> continuation of a transform beyond its convergence region is a separate question from convergence of the defining <integral>.

= Uniqueness of the Fourier transform on integrable functions
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$\widehat f=\widehat g\Longrightarrow f=g\text{ almost everywhere}$}

For <integrable functions> on the real line, equality of their <Fourier transforms> implies equality almost everywhere. To prove it, subtract and assume $\widehat h=0$. Convolve $h$ with a <Gaussian heat kernel> $q_t$. The <convolution theorem> gives $\widehat{h*q_t}=0$, which is integrable, so the <Fourier inversion theorem> gives $h*q_t=0$ almost everywhere. Finally $q_t$ is an <approximate identity>: $\|h*q_t-h\|_1\leq\int q_t(y)\|h(\cdot-y)-h\|_1dy\to0$. This follows from <translation continuity in Lp> with exponent one and the vanishing Gaussian tails. Hence $h=0$ in $L^1$. If the two original functions are continuous, equality almost everywhere strengthens to equality everywhere, since a nonzero continuous difference has magnitude bounded below by a positive constant on some interval.

= Fourier transform of a two-sided exponential
{parent=Fourier transform}
{c}
{title2=$\mathcal F(e^{-a|x|})(s)=2a/(a^2+s^2)$}

For $a>0$, use the <Fourier transform> convention $\widehat f(s)=\int_{\mathbb R}f(x)e^{-isx}\,dx$. Splitting the integral at zero gives $1/(a+is)+1/(a-is)=2a/(a^2+s^2)$. By the <Fourier inversion theorem>, the dual identity is
$$
\mathcal F\!\left(\frac1{a^2+x^2}\right)(s)=\frac\pi a e^{-a|s|}.
$$
This pair connects an <exponential function> with the kernel of a <Cauchy distribution> and produces the <Poisson kernel for the upper half-plane> by inversion of $e^{-y|s|}$.

= Frequency domain
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$\widehat f(\omega)$}

A time-dependent response can be represented by its <Fourier transform> in <angular frequency>. With $\widehat f(\omega)=\int f(t)e^{i\omega t}dt$, a <time derivative> transforms to $-i\omega\widehat f$ when boundary terms vanish. A time-translation-invariant response convolution becomes multiplication by a <frequency>-dependent response coefficient. This is why a dispersive <electrical conductivity> is naturally specified in the frequency domain. The inverse-transform modes use $e^{-i\omega t}$.

= Fourier transform of an inverse power of one plus x squared
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$\mathcal F[(1+x^2)^{-\alpha/2}]=h_\alpha$}

= Gaussian mixture formula for an inverse-power Fourier transform
{c}
{synonym}

For $\alpha>0$, put $a=\alpha/2$. The <regular distribution> of $(1+x^2)^{-a}$ is a <tempered distribution>, since the <function> is bounded. Its <Fourier transform> is the <integrable function>
$$
h_\alpha(\xi)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{\Gamma(a)}\int_0^\infty t^{a-3/2}e^{-t-\xi^2/(4t)}\,dt\qquad(\xi\ne0),
$$
with arbitrary finite value at zero when the displayed <integral> diverges there. To prove the identity, the <gamma function> gives $(1+x^2)^{-a}=\Gamma(a)^{-1}\int_0^\infty t^{a-1}e^{-t}e^{-tx^2}\,dt$. Pair this with a <Schwartz function>, use the <Fourier transform of a Gaussian>, and interchange <integrals> by <Fubini's theorem>. The absolute bound for the transformed pairing follows from <Tonelli's theorem>:
$$
\|h_\alpha\|_1=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{\Gamma(a)}\int_0^\infty t^{a-3/2}e^{-t}\,2\sqrt{\pi t}\,dt=2\pi.
$$
The positive kernel and <dominated convergence> give positivity and continuity away from zero. It has a continuous representative at zero exactly when $\alpha>1$, in which case $h_\alpha(0)=\sqrt\pi\Gamma((\alpha-1)/2)/\Gamma(\alpha/2)$. For $\alpha\leq1$, <Fatou's lemma> gives $h_\alpha(\xi)\to\infty$ as $\xi\to0$, so no change on a set of zero <Lebesgue measure> can give continuity.

= Inverse Fourier transform
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$\mathcal F^{-1}g(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}g(k)e^{ikx}\,dk$}

With the <Fourier transform> convention $\widehat f(k)=\int_{\mathbb R}f(x)e^{-ikx}\,dx$, the inverse Fourier transform is the displayed <linear operator>. The integral is defined directly for an <integrable function> $g$. The <Fourier inversion theorem> ensures $\mathcal F^{-1}\widehat f=f$ under its hypotheses, in particular for <Schwartz functions>, and for integrable $f$ and $\widehat f$ at continuity points of $f$. The operation also extends to <tempered distributions>; the normalization and sign depend on the chosen <Fourier transform> convention.

= Fourier transform of a polynomial multiple
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$\mathcal F[x^nf](k)=i^n\partial_k^n\widehat f(k)$}

With <Fourier transform> convention $\widehat f(k)=\int f(x)e^{-ikx}dx$, differentiation under the <integral> gives $\partial_k^n\widehat f=\int(-ix)^nf(x)e^{-ikx}dx$, proving the displayed identity. For example it applies when all $x^jf(x)$ for $0\leq j\leq n$ are absolutely integrable, or in the <tempered distribution> sense. It is the dual counterpart of the <Fourier transform of a derivative>.

= Duality property of the Fourier transform
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$\mathcal F^2f(\omega)=2\pi f(-\omega)$}

For the <Fourier transform> convention $\mathcal Ff(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb R}f(t)e^{-i\omega t}\,dt$, <Fourier inversion> at $-\omega$ gives $f(-\omega)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{\mathbb R}(\mathcal Ff)(t)e^{-i\omega t}\,dt$. Thus transforming twice reflects the argument and multiplies by $2\pi$. The identity holds directly on the <Schwartz space>, and extends to the <L2 space> and to <tempered distributions>. It lets an already known transform pair be used in reverse without another integration.

= Fourier transform of a truncated cosine
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$\widehat f(k)=\sin[(k-1)a]/(k-1)+\sin[(k+1)a]/(k+1)$}

For $f(x)=\cos x$ on $[-a,a]$ and zero outside, the angular-frequency <Fourier transform> follows by writing the cosine as two <complex exponentials>. Combining fractions gives $[2k\cos a\sin(ak)-2\sin a\cos(ak)]/(k^2-1)$. The apparent singularities at $k=\pm1$ are removable, with value $a+\sin(2a)/2$. If $\cos a\ne0$, jumps at the endpoints give an oscillatory $O(|k|^{-1})$ envelope. If $\cos a=0$, the function is continuous at the endpoints but its first <derivative> jumps, giving an $O(|k|^{-2})$ envelope. This is an explicit instance of <Fourier decay from a derivative jump>.

= Fourier transform of the hyperbolic secant
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-ikx}\operatorname{sech}x\,dx=\pi\operatorname{sech}(\pi k/2)$}

For real $k$, integrate $e^{-ikz}/\cosh z$ around a rectangle of height $\pi$. Its top-edge value is $-e^{\pi k}$ times the bottom-edge value, and the only enclosed <simple pole> is $i\pi/2$, with <residue> $e^{\pi k/2}/i$. The vertical-edge integrals vanish as their real parts tend to positive and negative infinity. The <residue theorem> gives $(1+e^{\pi k})\widetilde f(k)=2\pi e^{\pi k/2}$, proving the stated <Fourier transform> identity. This is an example of a function reproducing its shape under a <Fourier transform>, up to rescaling.

= Hermitian symmetry
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$F(-u)=\overline{F(u)}$}

If $f$ is a <real-valued function> and an <integrable function>, its <Fourier transform> has <Hermitian symmetry>: $F(-u)=\overline{F(u)}$. The value at negative <frequency> is the <complex conjugate> of the value at positive <frequency>.

= Window function
{parent=Fourier transform}
{wiki}
{title2=$w_T(t)=\mathbf1_{[0,T]}(t)$}

A window function is a <function> that vanishes outside a chosen finite <interval>; multiplying an input by it selects and weights that part of the history before a <Fourier transform>. The rectangular window $w_T$ is the <indicator function> of $[0,T]$. Applied to the weighted <causal extension by zero> of an input, it produces the finite-history <integral> $\int_0^T e^{-st}f(t)\,dt$ used to approximate a <Laplace transform>.

= Spectral leakage
{parent=Window function}

Multiplying a measured <signal> by a finite-duration <window function> convolves its <Fourier transform> with the transform of the window. A spectrally sharp component therefore contributes to nearby frequencies rather than remaining a single ideal line. Smooth tapering can reduce distant sidelobes at the cost of widening the main spectral feature. This finite-record effect is distinct from <aliasing>, which superposes copies of the spectrum because of time sampling.

= Uncentred Fourier uncertainty principle
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$\|xf\|_2\|f'\|_2\geq\|f\|_2^2/2$}

For nonzero $f$ with $f,xf,f'\in L^2(\mathbb R)$, integration by parts gives $\|f\|_2^2=-2\operatorname{Re}\langle f',xf\rangle$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> and <Plancherel theorem> yield the moment product bound $1/4$ with the angular-frequency Fourier convention. Equality occurs exactly for $f(x)=C e^{-ax^2/2}$ with $a>0$ real and $C\ne0$. Translation, modulation or a quadratic phase increase one of these uncentred moments; the equality family is narrower than that for centred variances.

= Fourier transform on a locally compact abelian group
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$\widehat f(\chi)=\int_G f(x)\overline{\chi(x)}\,dm_G(x)$}

For $f\in L^1(G)$ the displayed integral defines a bounded continuous function on the <Pontryagin dual group>. It takes <convolution> to pointwise multiplication and left translation $T_x$ to multiplication by $\overline{\chi(x)}$. The transform depends on the scale of <Haar measure> on $G$; the compatible <dual Haar measure> scales reciprocally.

= Fourier inversion on a locally compact abelian group
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform on a locally compact abelian group}
{title2=$f(x)=\int_{\widehat G}\widehat f(\chi)\chi(x)\,dm_{\widehat G}(\chi)$}

For the <dual Haar measure>, the formula first follows for continuous compactly supported convolution squares from the <Bochner theorem>. More generally, if $f\in L^1(G)$ and $\widehat f\in L^1(\widehat G)$, the right-hand side is a continuous representative of $f$; equality holds everywhere if $f$ is continuous, and otherwise at every point where the chosen representative is continuous. Convolution with normalized small-support positive convolution squares proves the extension: the spatial convolutions approach $f$ in $L^1$, while their transforms approach $\widehat f$ in $L^1$ by <equicontinuity of compact families of characters> and an integrable-tail split.

= Fourier decay from a derivative jump
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$\widehat f(k)\supset\frac{[f^{(j)}]_a e^{-ika}}{(ik)^{j+1}}$}

For a piecewise smooth decaying function, a jump in its $j$th derivative at $a$ contributes the displayed algebraic term to its high-frequency <Fourier transform>. Distributionally, $D^{j+1}f$ contains $[f^{(j)}]_a\delta(x-a)$, and <integration by parts> or the derivative-transform identity supplies the factor $(ik)^{-j-1}$. Adequate integrability of the subsequent derivatives controls the remainder. Smooth localized polynomial terms give no algebraic cusp contribution.

= Fourier transform of an algebraic cusp
{parent=Fourier decay from a derivative jump}
{title2=$\mathcal F(|x|^p)=2\Gamma(p+1)\cos[\pi(p+1)/2]\,|k|^{-p-1}$}

For $p>-1$ this formula is interpreted away from $k=0$ as a <tempered distribution> or by exponential regularization. It also supplies local high-frequency cusp terms after multiplying by a smooth cutoff. Even nonnegative integer $p$ give smooth polynomials and only contact terms at $k=0$, so their localized forms have no algebraic tail. For example, the cusp expansion of $(1+|x|^3)^{-1}$ gives $-12|k|^{-4}+2\,9!|k|^{-10}+O(|k|^{-16})$.

= Modulation property of the Fourier transform
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$\widehat{e^{ia\cdot x}\,d\nu}(\xi)=\widehat\nu(\xi-a)$}

= Fourier modulation and translation identity
{c}
{synonym}

Multiplication by a unit-modulus linear phase shifts the <Fourier transform>. Substituting $e^{ia\cdot x}$ into the defining integral gives the displayed identity. For a surface measure this permits independent translations of cap transforms without changing their input <norms> or angular supports.

= Fourier transform of a finite measure
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$\widehat\mu(\xi)=\int e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\,d\mu(x)$}

The angular-frequency <Fourier transform> of a <finite measure>, or a complex measure of finite total variation, is defined by the displayed integral. It is bounded by total variation and continuous by <dominated convergence>. An integrable density recovers the ordinary <Fourier transform> of a function. A measure supported on a curved surface instead leads to a <Fourier extension operator>.

= Fourier coefficient of a measure on the circle
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform of a finite measure}
{title2=$\widehat\mu(n)=\int e^{-inx}\,d\mu(x)$}

For a finite <complex measure> on the additive <circle group> $\mathbb R/(2\pi\mathbb Z)$, its Fourier coefficients are integrals against the <group characters> $e^{-inx}$, $n\in\mathbb Z$. They obey $|\widehat\mu(n)|\leq\|\mu\|$, where the norm is the <total variation norm of a measure>. A <probability measure> has zeroth coefficient one. The normalized <Haar measure> $m(dx)=dx/(2\pi)$ has all nonzero-frequency coefficients zero. A bounded family of finite <Radon measures> converges in the <weak-star topology> exactly when its coefficients converge to those of the proposed limit: extend linear combinations to all continuous tests using the density of <trigonometric polynomials> and the common variation bound.

= Complex spectral Fourier transform
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$Q(k)=\int_{\mathbb C}e^{\bar kz-k\bar z}q(z)dA(z)$}

A complex-coordinate parametrization of the two-dimensional <Fourier transform>. With $z=x+iy$, $k=k_1+ik_2$, and $(\xi_1,\xi_2)=(2k_2,-2k_1)$, its exponent is $-i\xi\cdot(x,y)$. Since $d\xi=4dA(k)$, its inverse is $q(z)=\pi^{-2}\int e^{k\bar z-\bar kz}Q(k)dA(k)$. The spectral equation $(\partial_{\bar z}-k)F=q$ gives $\partial_{\bar k}F=-e^{k\bar z-\bar kz}Q(k)/\pi$, linking <Fourier inversion> to a <spectral dbar equation>.

= Bandlimited function
{parent=Fourier transform}

A <bandlimited function> has <Fourier transform> supported in a bounded frequency interval. An integrable bandlimited function has a bounded continuous representative when its <Fourier transform> is integrable, and that representative extends analytically using the compact-frequency integral. Scaling arguments scales its frequency support inversely, while multiplying two such functions adds their support intervals through convolution.

= Half-range Fourier transform
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$F^\pm(k)=\int_{\mathbb R_\pm}e^{ikx}f(x)dx$}

With the $e^{ikx}$ convention, a right-supported decaying function has a transform analytic in the upper half-plane and a left-supported one in the lower half-plane. Incident modes continued with small imaginary frequency can have poles outside the initial convergence strip; these poles must be tracked separately from outgoing scattered waves.

= Rotated null contours for half-range Fourier inversion
{parent=Half-range Fourier transform}
{title2=$\int_{\partial E}e^{ikx}Q(\alpha^2k)\,dk=\int_{\partial D}e^{ikx}Q(\alpha k)\,dk=0$}

For $Q(k)=\int_0^\infty e^{-ikx}q(x)\,dx$, the <half-range Fourier transform> is <holomorphic> in the lower half-plane. Let $\alpha=e^{2\pi i/3}$, $E=\{0<\arg k<\pi/3\}$ and $D=\{2\pi/3<\arg k<\pi\}$, with their boundaries oriented counterclockwise. The rotated arguments $\alpha^2k$ on $E$ and $\alpha k$ on $D$ remain in the lower half-plane. For $x>0$ and transform-admissible smooth decay, the <Cauchy integral theorem> and <Jordan lemma> give the displayed null integrals. Arbitrary multiples can therefore be added to <Fourier inversion> without changing the recovered function on the positive half-line.

= Half-line drift null contour identity
{parent=Half-range Fourier transform}
{title2=$\int_L e^{ikx}\widehat q(i\alpha-k)\,dk=0$}

For $\alpha>0$, let $L$ be the upper branch $\operatorname{Im}k=(\alpha+\sqrt{\alpha^2+4(\operatorname{Re}k)^2})/2$, oriented left to right. Above $L$, $i\alpha-k$ lies in the lower half-plane, where a decaying half-line <Fourier transform> is analytic. For $x>0$, closing upward proves that the displayed integral vanishes. An additional multiplier analytic above $L$ with at most polynomial growth is allowed, using exponential decay and, if needed, regularized contours. This supplies a null term in a <Fokas method> inversion formula without changing the function in the interior.

= Fourier transform isomorphism of the Schwartz space
{parent=Fourier transform}
{c}
{title2=$\mathcal F:\mathcal S\longrightarrow\mathcal S$}

The <Fourier transform> with negative exponential and inverse factor $(2\pi)^{-n}$ is a continuous isomorphism of the <Schwartz space>. Weighted frequency derivatives are transforms of derivatives of weighted input functions, so their suprema are controlled by finitely many Schwartz <seminorms>. <Fourier inversion> gives $\mathcal F^2=(2\pi)^n\mathcal R$, where $\mathcal Rf(x)=f(-x)$. Transposition gives a continuous isomorphism on <tempered distributions> as well.

= Fourier bounds for Schwartz functions
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform isomorphism of the Schwartz space}
{title2=$p_{m,n}(\widehat\phi)\leq C_{m,n}\sum_{a,b\leq N_{m,n}}p_{a,b}(\phi)$}

= Fourier bounds on the Schwartz space
{c}
{synonym}

For the angular-frequency <Fourier transform> and <Schwartz seminorms> $p_{m,n}(\phi)=\sup_x|x^m\phi^{(n)}(x)|$, every output <seminorm> is bounded by finitely many input <seminorms>. To prove this, <differentiation under the integral sign> and <integration by parts> give
$$
\xi^m\partial_\xi^n\widehat\phi=i^{-m}\mathcal F\!\left[\partial_x^m((-ix)^n\phi)\right].
$$
The <generalized Leibniz rule>, $\|\widehat g\|_\infty\leq\|g\|_1$, and $\|x^a\phi^{(b)}\|_1\leq\pi(p_{a,b}+p_{a+2,b})$ imply
$$
p_{m,n}(\widehat\phi)\leq\pi\sum_{j=0}^{\min(m,n)}\binom mj\frac{n!}{(n-j)!}
\bigl(p_{n-j,m-j}(\phi)+p_{n-j+2,m-j}(\phi)\bigr).
$$
This proves continuity of the <Fourier transform> on the <Schwartz space> and rapid decay of every transformed <derivative>.

= Fourier cosine transform
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$C(k)=\int_0^\infty f(x)\cos(kx)dx$}

= Cosine transform
{synonym}

For sufficiently regular decaying data, this half-line transform has inverse $f(x)=(2/\pi)\int_0^\infty C(k)\cos(kx)dk$. To derive it, extend $f$ evenly to the real line. Its <Fourier transform> with negative exponential is $2C(k)$, and its odd sine contribution vanishes. The <Fourier inversion theorem> then gives the stated factor $2/\pi$. If the forward transform is instead defined as $\widehat f_c=2C$, the inverse factor is $1/\pi$.

With boundary terms at infinity vanishing, two <integrations by parts> give the cosine transform of $f''$ as $-k^2C(k)-f'(0)$. Thus a homogeneous <Neumann boundary condition> makes it a multiplier $-k^2C(k)$, paralleling the <Fourier sine transform> with a homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary condition>.

= Fourier sine transform
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform}

= Sine transform
{synonym}

The Fourier sine transform is a half-line transform adapted to a homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary condition>. With the displayed normalization, inversion is $f(x)=(2/\pi)\int_0^\infty\sin(kx)S(k)dk$. It is equivalent to the <Fourier transform> of an odd extension. If $f(0)=0$ and boundary terms vanish, two <integrations by parts> give the transform of $f''$ as $-k^2S(k)$.

= Fourier sine inversion
{c}
{parent=Fourier sine transform}
{title2=$f(x)=\frac2\pi\int_0^\infty\sin(kx)S(k)dk$}

For sufficiently regular integrable functions on a half-line, <Fourier sine transform> inversion recovers the odd extension at continuity points. A zero endpoint value is required when demanding continuity at the endpoint.

= Sinc function
{parent=Fourier transform}
{wiki}
{title2=$\operatorname{sinc}(x)=\sin x/x$}

The <unnormalized sinc function> is $\operatorname{sinc}(x)=\sin x/x$, extended by value one at zero. The <normalized sinc function> is $\sin(\pi x)/(\pi x)$, with the same removable value. These arise, with convention-dependent scaling, as inverse <Fourier transforms> of frequency-interval <indicator functions>, so they describe ideal <low-pass filters> and the <Shannon scaling function>.

= Normalized sinc function
{parent=Sinc function}
{title2=$\operatorname{sinc}_{\pi}(z)=\sin(\pi z)/(\pi z),\quad\operatorname{sinc}_{\pi}(0)=1$}

= Unnormalized sinc function
{parent=Sinc function}
{title2=$\operatorname{sinc}(z)=\sum_{m\geq0}\frac{(-1)^mz^{2m}}{(2m+1)!}$}

The <sinc function> $\sin z/z$ for $z\ne0$, extended by value one at zero. The displayed <power series> has infinite <radius of convergence>, so the extension is <entire>. Its removable value follows from the first coefficient or from the sine limit. This convention differs by the argument scaling $z\mapsto\pi z$ from the normalized sinc convention.

= Sinc fourth-power integral
{parent=Sinc function}
{title2=$\displaystyle\int_0^\infty(\sin x/x)^4dx=\pi/3$}

The <triangular function> $f(x)=(1-|x|)$ on $|x|<1$, zero elsewhere, has <Fourier transform> $\widehat f(k)=[\sin(k/2)/(k/2)]^2$ in the angular-frequency convention. Its squared <L2 norm> is $2/3$. <Parseval's identity> therefore gives
$$
\frac23=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\sin(k/2)}{k/2}\right)^4dk=\frac2\pi\int_0^\infty\left(\frac{\sin u}u\right)^4du.
$$
This proves the displayed value. The removable value at zero is one, and the fourth-power decay bounds the tail. The <sinc-squared integral> follows similarly from a rectangular function rather than a triangle.

= Fourier space
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$\mathbf k$}

Fourier space labels a field by the frequency or wavevector variables of its <Fourier transform>, rather than by its original coordinates. Spatial gradients act by multiplication by $i\mathbf k$, and the negative <Laplacian> acts by $k^2$.

= Moment differentiation of the Fourier transform
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$\widehat f^{(k)}(\xi)=\int(-ix)^kf(x)e^{-ix\xi}\,dx$}

If $f\in L^1(\mathbb R)$ and $x^kf\in L^1(\mathbb R)$ for $0\le k\le r$, then its <Fourier transform> is $r$ times <continuously differentiable> and the displayed formula holds. The <dominated convergence theorem> permits each <derivative> under the <integral>. Thus $r+1$ <vanishing moments> give a zero <Taylor polynomial> through degree $r$ at the origin. Unweighted $L^1$ membership only guarantees a <continuous function> as the <Fourier transform>.

= Fourier transform has proper dense image from L1 to C0
{parent=Fourier transform}
{c}

The <Riemann-Lebesgue lemma> and <Fourier inversion> give a bounded injective map $L^1(\mathbb R)\to C_0(\mathbb R)$ with dense image. It is not onto: inverse transforms of $\chi_{[-n,n]}*\chi_{[-1,1]}$ have unbounded $L^1$ norms while the targets have <uniform norm> $2$, contradicting the bounded inverse that the <open mapping theorem> would give.

= Isotropic transverse covariance spectrum
{parent=Hankel transform}
{title2=$C(\rho)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^\infty\Phi(\kappa)J_0(\kappa\rho)\kappa\,d\kappa$}

For the <Fourier transform> convention $C(\mathbf s)=\int\Phi(\boldsymbol\kappa)e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf s}d^2\kappa/(2\pi)^2$, angular integration of an isotropic <power spectrum> gives the displayed <Hankel transform>. A purely radial Hankel transform without the prefactor corresponds to defining its spectrum as $\Phi/(2\pi)$.

= Fourier-Hankel normalization for an isotropic spectrum
{parent=Hankel transform}
{c}
{title2=$\widehat f(\nu)=2\pi\mathcal H_0f(\nu)$}

For the Cartesian convention $\widehat f(\mathbf q)=\int_{\mathbb R^2}f(\mathbf r)e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}d\mathbf r$, an isotropic function has $\widehat f(\nu)=2\pi\int_0^\infty f(r)J_0(\nu r)r\,dr$. Its inverse is $f(r)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_0^\infty\widehat f(\nu)J_0(\nu r)\nu\,d\nu$. The self-reciprocal <Hankel transform> instead uses $F_H=\widehat f/(2\pi)$. In particular $f(0)=\int F_H(\nu)\nu\,d\nu$. Mixing these spectral conventions changes attenuation coefficients by $2\pi$.

= Wigner distribution
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$W[u,v](\mathbf z,\mathbf p)$}

= Wigner transform
{c}
{synonym}

The <Wigner distribution> of two fields is the <Fourier transform> of a field's two-point product in its separation variable:
$$
W[u,v](\mathbf z,\mathbf p)=(2\pi)^{-d}\int e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf s}u(\mathbf z+\mathbf s/2)\overline{v(\mathbf z-\mathbf s/2)}\,d\mathbf s.
$$
Its inverse reconstructs $u(\mathbf z)\overline{v(\mathbf z')}$. For vector fields, specify whether the product is an outer product or a scalar contraction; the latter can encode an average over receiver channels.

= Optical Wigner function
{parent=Wigner distribution}
{title2=$W(x,y)$}

For $X_1=x$, $X_2=y$ with $[X_1,X_2]=i/2$, normalize the phase-space function by $\int W(x,y)\,dx\,dy=1$. A <coherent state> of amplitude $\alpha$ has $W=2e^{-2|x+iy-\alpha|^2}/\pi$. Quadrature marginals are physical probability distributions, but the full function can be negative. Cross terms of an <optical cat state> give interference fringes, even when low-order moments approach those of a mixture.

= Free Schrodinger Wigner transport
{parent=Wigner distribution}
{c}
{title2=$w_t+\xi\cdot\nabla_xw=0$}

For $i\Psi_t=-\Delta\Psi/2$, differentiate the two-point product $\Psi(x+y/2)\overline\Psi(x-y/2)$. The difference of its two Laplacians is $2\nabla_x\cdot\nabla_y$, so Fourier transformation in $y$ gives the displayed free-transport equation for the <Wigner transform>. Its solution is $w(x,\xi,t)=w(x-t\xi,\xi,0)$.

= Receiver-averaged Wigner distribution
{parent=Wigner distribution}
{title2=$\mathcal W_A$}

For receiver-dependent <Green functions> $g_{\boldsymbol\zeta}(\mathbf z)$ and <aperture> weight $a(\boldsymbol\zeta)$, define $\mathcal W_A(\mathbf Z,\mathbf p)=\int a(\boldsymbol\zeta)W[g_{\boldsymbol\zeta},g_{\boldsymbol\zeta}](\mathbf Z,\mathbf p)\,d\boldsymbol\zeta$. Its inverse separation <Fourier transform> is the kernel of the <time reversal operator> on the source plane.

= Japanese bracket
{c}
{title2=$\langle\xi\rangle=(1+|\xi|^2)^{1/2}$}
{parent=Fourier transform}

The Japanese bracket is a smooth positive frequency weight, comparable to $1+|\xi|$. It defines <Sobolev space> norms and the growth estimates of a <symbol class> without singularities at zero frequency.

= Peetre weight inequality
{c}
{parent=Japanese bracket}
{title2=$\langle\xi\rangle^s\le C_s\langle\eta\rangle^s\langle\xi-\eta\rangle^{|s|}$}

= Peetre's inequality
{c}
{synonym}

For real $s$ and vectors $\xi,\eta$, the displayed bound follows from $\langle a+b\rangle\le\sqrt2\langle a\rangle\langle b\rangle$, applied in the opposite direction when $s<0$. One can take $C_s=2^{|s|/2}$. Combined with <Young's convolution inequality>, it proves <Sobolev multiplication by a smooth cutoff> for all real orders: the rapidly decreasing transform of the cutoff absorbs the extra weight. The negative-order case is particularly useful when a <distribution> is not initially a function.

= Fourier shift theorem
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform}

For the angular-frequency convention,
$$
\mathcal F[e^{iax}f(x)](k)=\widehat f(k-a),
\qquad
\mathcal F[f(x-a)](k)=e^{-iak}\widehat f(k).
$$

= Fresnel integral
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform}
{wiki}

The Fresnel integral is an oscillatory Gaussian integral. In the angular-frequency convention, its distributional form is
$$
\int_{\mathbb R}e^{i\omega x^2/2-i\lambda x}\,dx
=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{|\omega|}}
e^{i\pi\operatorname{sgn}(\omega)/4}
e^{-i\lambda^2/(2\omega)}
$$
for nonzero real $\omega$.

= Optical normalization of Fresnel integrals
{parent=Fresnel integral}
{title2=$C(w)+iS(w)=\int_0^w e^{i\pi u^2/2}\,du$}

For the optical convention of <Fresnel integrals>, $C(w)=\int_0^w\cos(\pi u^2/2)du$ and $S(w)=\int_0^w\sin(\pi u^2/2)du$. Both functions are odd and tend to $1/2$ as $w\to+\infty$. If the unscaled convention uses phase $t^2$, multiply its argument by $\sqrt{\pi/2}$ and its value by $\sqrt{2/\pi}$ to obtain this convention. An aperture factor with phase $\pi x^2/(\lambda R)$ uses $u=x\sqrt{2/(\lambda R)}$. Stating the convention prevents missing factors of $\pi$ or $\sqrt2$ in <diffraction> calculations.

= Finite Fresnel integrals
{c}
{parent=Fresnel integral}
{title2=$C(\lambda),S(\lambda)$}

In the unscaled convention, the finite <Fresnel integrals> are $C(\lambda)=\int_0^\lambda\cos(t^2)dt$ and $S(\lambda)=\int_0^\lambda\sin(t^2)dt$. Their <Taylor series> follow by termwise integration. At infinity both tend to $\sqrt{\pi/8}$, and their oscillatory tails have <asymptotic expansions> obtained by <integration by parts>. This convention differs from the convention with phase $\pi t^2/2$.

= Fresnel tail expansion
{c}
{parent=Finite Fresnel integrals}
{title2=$C=A+P\sin\lambda^2-Q\cos\lambda^2$}

For $A=\sqrt{\pi/8}$, define real tail functions by $Q+iP=e^{-i\lambda^2}\int_\lambda^\infty e^{it^2}dt$. Then $C=A+P\sin\lambda^2-Q\cos\lambda^2$ and $S=A-P\cos\lambda^2-Q\sin\lambda^2$. Repeated <integration by parts> gives
$$
P\sim\sum_{m\ge0}\frac{(-1)^m(4m-1)!!}{2^{2m+1}\lambda^{4m+1}},\qquad Q\sim\sum_{m\ge0}\frac{(-1)^m(4m+1)!!}{2^{2m+2}\lambda^{4m+3}},
$$
where $(-1)!!=1$. These are <asymptotic expansions>, rather than convergent infinite sums at a fixed argument.

= Regulated complex Fresnel integral
{parent=Fresnel integral}
{title2=$\int_{\mathbb C}e^{(i\lambda-\alpha)|z|^2}d^2z=\pi/(\alpha-i\lambda)$}

For $\alpha>0$, polar coordinates reduce the integral to $2\pi\int_0^\infty r e^{-(\alpha-i\lambda)r^2}dr$. The result is $\pi/(\alpha-i\lambda)$. Its damped limit for real $\lambda>0$ is $i\pi/\lambda$, which fixes the sign of the oscillatory complex <Gaussian integral>. The <Fresnel integral> convention can be compared with https://dlmf.nist.gov/7.2[NIST definitions].

= Oscillatory Gaussian integral with negative phase
{parent=Fresnel integral}

For $t>0$, contour rotation of the <Gaussian integral> gives
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-itu^2}du
=e^{-i\pi/4}\sqrt{\frac\pi t}.
$$

= Additive character
{parent=Fourier transform}
{wiki}

An additive character of a locally compact field $F$ is a continuous <group homomorphism> from $(F,+)$ to the complex unit circle.

= Character annihilator
{parent=Additive character}
{title2=$H^\perp$}

For a nontrivial <additive character> $\psi$ on a <local field>, the character annihilator of an additive subgroup $H$ is $H^\perp=\{y:\psi(xy)=1\text{ for all }x\in H\}$. If $\mathcal O_F^\perp=\pi^c\mathcal O_F$, then $(\pi^n\mathcal O_F)^\perp=\pi^{c-n}\mathcal O_F$. This is a character pairing construction, distinct from the <annihilator> of a module.

= Fourier transform over a local field
{title2=$\widehat f(y)=\int_Ff(x)\psi(xy)\,dx$}
{parent=Fourier transform}

For a <local field> $F$, an <additive character> $\psi$, and a <Haar measure> $dx$, the Fourier transform is
$$
\widehat f(y)=\int_Ff(x)\psi(xy)\,dx.
$$
It maps the <Schwartz-Bruhat space> to itself.

= Fourier transform of a ball indicator over a local field
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform over a local field}
{title2=$\widehat{\mathbf1_{a+H}}(y)=\operatorname{vol}(H)\psi(ay)\mathbf1_{H^\perp}(y)$}

For a compact open additive subgroup $H$ of a <local field>, translation and <additive character> orthogonality give the displayed transform of a coset indicator. With the <self-dual Haar measure>, transforming again gives $\mathbf1_{a+H}(-x)$. Every <Schwartz-Bruhat function> is a finite linear combination of these indicators.

= Self-dual Haar measure
{parent=Fourier transform over a local field}
{title2=$\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal O_F)=q^{c/2}$}

Relative to a nontrivial <additive character>, the self-dual additive <Haar measure> makes the twice-applied <Fourier transform over a local field> equal to reflection. If the <annihilator of the valuation ring> is $\pi^c\mathcal O_F$ and its <residue field> has $q$ elements, the normalization is $\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal O_F)=q^{c/2}$. Volumes of a compact open subgroup and its <character annihilator> then multiply to one.

= Annihilator of the valuation ring
{title2=$\mathcal O_F^\perp$}
{parent=Fourier transform over a local field}

For an <additive character> $\psi$ of a non-Archimedean <local field> $F$,
$$
\mathcal O_F^\perp
=\{y\in F:\psi(xy)=1\text{ for every }x\in\mathcal O_F\}.
$$
For the character induced from the standard character of $\mathbb Q_p$, this annihilator is the <inverse different>.

= Fourier multiplier
{title2=$m(\xi)$}
{parent=Fourier transform}

A Fourier multiplier is a function $m$ on frequency space used to define an operator by multiplication after taking a <Fourier transform>.

= Everywhere-defined Fourier multipliers on continuous periodic functions are bounded
{parent=Fourier multiplier}

Suppose every continuous periodic function $f$ has a unique continuous periodic $Tf$ with <Fourier coefficients> $\widehat{Tf}(n)=\alpha_n\widehat f(n)$. Uniqueness first makes $T$ linear. If $f_j\to f$ and $Tf_j\to g$ uniformly, continuity of each coefficient functional gives $\widehat g(n)=\alpha_n\widehat f(n)$ for every fixed $n$, hence $g=Tf$. Thus $T$ has a closed graph and the <closed graph theorem> makes it bounded. Applying it to normalized exponentials gives $|\alpha_n|\leq\|T\|$.

For $e_n(t)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{int}$, the bounded functional $\phi(f)=\sqrt{2\pi}(Tf)(0)$ satisfies $\phi(e_n)=\alpha_n$. The normalization factor follows from $e_n(0)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}$.

= Half-plane Fourier projection
{parent=Fourier multiplier}
{title2=$\widehat{P_vg}(\xi)=\mathbf1_{\{\xi\cdot v<0\}}\widehat g(\xi)$}

The half-plane Fourier projection is $(I-iH_v)/2$, where $H_v$ is the <directional Hilbert transform>. A disk of radius $R$, modulated to put a boundary point at the origin, has symbol $\mathbf1_{\{|\xi+Rv|\le R\}}$, which converges almost everywhere to this half-plane symbol. The <Plancherel theorem> makes this an $L^2$ multiplier limit.

= Nonintegrability of a radial triangular Fourier cutoff in three dimensions
{parent=Fourier multiplier}
{title2=$\mathcal F^{-1}[(1-|\xi|)_+]\notin L^1(\mathbb R^3)$}

The inverse <Fourier transform> of $(1-|\xi|)_+$ in three dimensions, with $a=2\pi|x|$, is $-4\pi\sin a/a^3+8\pi(1-\cos a)/a^4$. Its leading oscillatory $|x|^{-3}$ tail is not absolutely integrable against the radial <volume> element $4\pi|x|^2d|x|$. Multiplying by a Gaussian <Fourier transform> retains a nonzero boundary-<derivative> tail, so the radial cutoff need not map even <Schwartz functions> into $L^1$. A <tensor product> of one-dimensional triangular cutoffs is a valid <approximate identity>, and must not be confused with this Euclidean radial cutoff.

= Fourier multiplier operator
{parent=Fourier multiplier}
{wiki}

A Fourier multiplier operator $T_m$ is defined by $\widehat{T_mf}(\xi)=m(\xi)\widehat f(\xi)$. On $L^2$, a bounded symbol gives a bounded operator by the <Plancherel theorem>, while an unbounded symbol defines a <closed linear operator> on the functions for which $m\widehat f$ remains square-integrable.

= Fourier transform of a function with reciprocal tails
{parent=Fourier transform}

Suppose $w$ is locally integrable and
$$
w(x)=\frac{c_+}{x}+O(x^{-2})\quad(x\to+\infty),
\qquad
w(x)=\frac{c_-}{x}+O(x^{-2})\quad(x\to-\infty).
$$
Then $\widehat w(\lambda)+(c_+-c_-)\log|\lambda|$ has finite limits at zero from both sides. With the convention $\widehat w(\lambda)=\int e^{-i\lambda x}w(x)dx$, the positive-frequency limit minus the negative-frequency limit is
$$
-i\pi(c_++c_-).
$$
Subtracting suitable reflected copies of $x^{-1}\mathbf1_{(1,\infty)}(x)$ leaves an $L^1$ function, whose Fourier transform is continuous; the jump then follows from the <Dirichlet integral>.

= Scaling property of the Fourier transform
{parent=Fourier transform}

If $f_\lambda(x)=f(\lambda x)$ on $\mathbb R^n$ with $\lambda>0$, then
$$
\widehat {f_\lambda}(\xi)
=\lambda^{-n}\widehat f(\xi/\lambda),
$$
and consequently
$$
\|f_\lambda\|_p=\lambda^{-n/p}\|f\|_p,
\qquad
\|\widehat {f_\lambda}\|_q
=\lambda^{-n(1-1/q)}\|\widehat f\|_q.
$$

= Scaling necessity for an Lp to Lq Fourier bound
{parent=Scaling property of the Fourier transform}

If a nontrivial uniform estimate
$$
\|\widehat f\|_q\leq C\|f\|_p
$$
holds for all integrable $L^p$ functions on $\mathbb R^n$, dilation by every $\lambda>0$ forces
$$
1-\frac1q=\frac1p.
$$
Thus $q$ must be the <conjugate exponents>[conjugate exponent] of $p$.

= Fourier transform of inverse distance in three dimensions
{title2=$\mathcal F(|x|^{-1})$}
{parent=Fourier transform}

As a <tempered distribution> on $\mathbb R^3$,
$$
\mathcal F(|x|^{-1})(\xi)=C|\xi|^{-2}
$$
for a nonzero convention-dependent constant $C$. One proof writes
$$
|x|^{-1}=C_1\int_0^\infty t^{-1/2}e^{-t|x|^2}\,dt
$$
and applies the <Fourier transform of a Gaussian> before substituting $u=|\xi|^2/(4t)$.

= Hilbert transform
{parent=Fourier transform}
{c}
{wiki}

With one common sign convention,
$$
Hf(y)=\frac1\pi\operatorname{PV}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{f(x)}{y-x}\,dx.
$$
Reversing the denominator reverses the sign.

= Finite Hilbert transform
{parent=Hilbert transform}
{title2=$H_{(0,1)}f(x)=\dfrac1\pi\operatorname{PV}\int_0^1\dfrac{f(t)}{t-x}\,dt$}

The finite Hilbert transform is a <singular integral operator> whose integration is restricted to a finite interval. The displayed convention uses the denominator $t-x$; reversing that denominator reverses the sign. The <Cauchy principal value> deletes equal intervals on the two sides of the interior point $x$. Endpoint behavior matters: the integrable <function> $1/\sqrt{x(1-x)}$ belongs to the null space for this interval. This nontrivial null space is one reason that an inversion formula needs a normalization or a stated endpoint class.

= Finite Hilbert transform inversion
{parent=Finite Hilbert transform}
{title2=$f(x)=-\dfrac{i}{\pi\rho(x)}\operatorname{PV}\int_0^1\dfrac{\rho(t)G(t)}{t-x}\,dt,\quad\rho(x)=\sqrt{x(1-x)}$}

For $G\in C^1([0,1])$, the displayed expression solves $Tf=G$, where $T=H_{(0,1)}/i$ and $H_{(0,1)}$ is the <finite Hilbert transform> with denominator $t-x$. To derive it, put $S(z)=\sqrt{z(z-1)}\sim z$ at <infinity>, take $h$ to be the <Cauchy transform> of $i\rho G$, and set $w=h/S$. The <Sokhotski–Plemelj formula> gives $w_++w_-=G$ and the displayed jump $f=w_+-w_-$. The endpoint behavior is at most inverse <square root>, and $w=O(z^{-2})$ at <infinity>. <Cauchy reconstruction from a jump across a finite interval> then gives $w=C[f]$ and hence $Tf=G$. Adding any constant multiple of $1/\rho$ gives another solution in the integrable endpoint class.

Multiplying an auxiliary sign-changing factor by a growing analytic factor can invalidate this reconstruction. For example, using $S$ instead of $1/S$ with $G=1$ produces the constant <function> $w=1/2$ and zero jump. The <boundary> sum alone is not enough: the entire remainder in reconstructing $w$ from its jump must also be controlled.

= Hilbert transform of the sinc function
{c}
{parent=Hilbert transform}
{title2=$\mathcal H(\sin t/t)=(1-\cos t)/t$}

With convention $\mathcal Hf(t)=\pi^{-1}\operatorname{PV}\int f(\tau)/(t-\tau)\,d\tau$, the analytic function $F(z)=(e^{iz}-1)/(iz)$ has removable singularity at zero and decays on large upper semicircles. Its boundary real part is $\sin t/t$ and imaginary part is $(1-\cos t)/t$. The upper-half-plane contour indented above $t$ gives $\operatorname{PV}\int F(\tau)/(\tau-t)\,d\tau=i\pi F(t)$. Taking imaginary parts after reversing the denominator proves the displayed transform, with value zero at $t=0$ by continuous extension.

= Directional Hilbert transform
{parent=Hilbert transform}
{title2=$H_vg(x)=\pi^{-1}\operatorname{pv}\int_{\mathbb R}g(x-sv)\,ds/s$}

For a unit vector $v$, the directional <Hilbert transform> integrates along lines parallel to $v$. Its <Fourier multiplier> is $-i\operatorname{sgn}(\xi\cdot v)$ under the usual transform convention. Applied to a rectangle whose long axis is $v$, it reduces to the one-dimensional logarithmic tail of an interval <indicator function>, separately on each transverse coordinate.

= Large-distance tail of the Hilbert transform
{parent=Hilbert transform}
{title2=$\mathcal Hf(x)=\widehat f(0)/(\pi x)+o(|x|^{-1})$}

For a <Schwartz function> $f$, the <Hilbert transform> satisfies $\mathcal Hf(x)=(\int f)/(\pi x)+o(|x|^{-1})$ at both infinities. Splitting the inverse <Fourier transform> at the sign multiplier's jump and integrating each half-line by parts extracts the zero-frequency value $\widehat f(0)$; the remainder vanishes by the <Riemann–Lebesgue lemma>. A nonzero zeroth moment therefore obstructs Schwartz decay.

= Hilbert-transform Fourier multiplier
{parent=Hilbert transform}
{c}
{title2=$\widehat{\mathcal Hf}=-i\operatorname{sgn}(\xi)\widehat f$}

For $\mathcal Hf(x)=\pi^{-1}\operatorname{pv}\int f(y)/(x-y)\,dy$ and $\widehat f(\xi)=\int e^{-ix\xi}f(x)dx$, the <Fourier transform> multiplier is $-i\operatorname{sgn}\xi$. The <Heaviside function> transform and the double-transform reflection identity give the sign. <Plancherel theorem> proves the $L^2$ isometry, and multiplication by $i\xi$ proves commutation with differentiation. The map need not preserve the <Schwartz space>.

= Fourier transform of an interval indicator
{parent=Fourier transform}

For $f=\mathbf1_{[-a,a]}$ and the convention $\widehat f(\xi)=\int f(x)e^{-ix\xi}\,dx$,
$$
\widehat f(\xi)=\frac{2\sin(a\xi)}{\xi},
$$
with its continuous value $2a$ at zero.

= Sinc-squared integral
{parent=Fourier transform}
{wiki}

With the standard Fourier convention, <Plancherel theorem> applied to $\mathbf1_{[-1,1]}$ gives
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\sin x}{x}\right)^2dx=\pi,
\qquad
\int_0^\infty\left(\frac{\sin x}{x}\right)^2dx=\frac\pi2.
$$
