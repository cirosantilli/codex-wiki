= Homogeneous Poisson process
{c}
{synonym}

A <homogeneous Poisson process> models arrivals at a constant rate $\lambda>0$. Its <counting process> $N_t=N(t)$ counts arrivals in $(0,t]$, with $N_0=0$. Its paths are <right-continuous> <nondecreasing functions>, take nonnegative <integer> values, and have unit jumps <almost surely>. The defining <Poisson distribution> of each increment is
$$
\mathbb P(N_{s+t}-N_s=k)=e^{-\lambda t}\frac{(\lambda t)^k}{k!},\qquad s\geq0,\quad t>0,\quad k=0,1,2,\ldots.
$$
It has <stationary increments>: the <probability distribution> above depends on the duration $t$, not the starting time $s$. It also has <independent increments>: counts in any finite collection of disjoint time intervals are <independent random variables>. Together these conditions specify the joint count laws, not merely the <Poisson distribution> at a single time.<Discrete Stochastic Processes (draft of 2nd edition, 31 January 2011) by Robert Gallager/Poisson Processes (Chapter 2)>[pp. 78-79: counting-process definition and its equivalence to exponential interarrival times]{ref}

The rate has units of arrivals per unit time; $\lambda t$ is dimensionless. The <Poisson distribution> gives
$$
\mathbb E[N_{s+t}-N_s]=\operatorname{Var}(N_{s+t}-N_s)=\lambda t.
$$
For a toy rate $\lambda=2$ per minute and a duration of $1.5$ minutes, the <expected value> and <variance> of the count are both $3$. The <probability> of no arrival is $e^{-3}\approx0.049787$, and the <probability> of exactly three is $3^3e^{-3}/3!=\tfrac92e^{-3}\approx0.224042$. In two consecutive one-minute intervals, the <probability> of exactly two arrivals in the first and exactly one in the second is $(2e^{-2})(2e^{-2})=4e^{-4}\approx0.073263$, using <independent increments>. These are exact model calculations with rounded decimals, not measured frequencies.

\Image[poisson-arrival-events-counting-path.png]{title=Synthetic arrival times and their right-continuous counting path, compared with the exact mean at two arrivals per minute}

The diagram's nine hand-chosen arrivals illustrate a path; the line $\lambda t$ is an <expected value>, not a prescribed trajectory. Constant rate does not imply evenly spaced arrivals. <Poisson interarrival times> connect the count description to waiting, while <Poisson thinning> explains how independently routing arrivals produces separate streams. This makes the <Poisson process> useful as a simple arrival model for a <queue>, provided constant rate and <independent increments> are plausible. For a deterministic changing rate, use an <inhomogeneous Poisson process>; bunching or history-dependent arrivals need additional assumptions. Conditional on $N_t=n$, the arrival times are the <order statistics> of $n$ <independent> <uniform distributions> on $[0,t]$, as developed in <Poisson process conditional arrival times>.

= Waiting times of a Poisson process
{c}
{synonym}

Let $T_n=\inf\{t\geq0:N_t\geq n\}$ be the $n$th arrival time, and set $T_0=0$. In a <homogeneous Poisson process> of rate $\lambda>0$, the gaps $W_n=T_n-T_{n-1}$ are <independent and identically distributed random variables> with an <exponential distribution>:
$$
\mathbb P(W_n>w)=e^{-\lambda w},\qquad f_{W_n}(w)=\lambda e^{-\lambda w},\qquad w\geq0;
\qquad \mathbb E[W_n]=\frac1\lambda,\quad \operatorname{Var}(W_n)=\frac1{\lambda^2}.
$$
Here $\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)$ uses the rate convention. Under the scale convention $\theta=1/\lambda$, the same <probability density function> is $\theta^{-1}e^{-w/\theta}$; a simulation function accepting a scale must receive $1/\lambda$. Conversely, summing <independent> rate-$\lambda$ <exponential random variables> to form $T_n$ and counting those sums constructs a <homogeneous Poisson process>.<Lectures on the Poisson Process by Günter Last and others>[Theorem 7.2, printed p. 59: interval theorem characterizing independent exponential gaps]{ref}

The first-gap formula follows immediately from $\{W_1>w\}=\{N_w=0\}$ and the <Poisson distribution>. Independence of all successive gaps is the additional interval-theorem assertion; deterministic-time <independent increments> alone should not simply be substituted at random arrival times without justification. The <memorylessness of the exponential distribution> gives
$$
\mathbb P(W_n>a+w\mid W_n>a)=e^{-\lambda w},\qquad a,w\geq0.
$$
At the toy rate of two per minute, the <mean> gap is half a minute, the <median> is $(\log2)/2\approx0.346574$ minutes, and the <probability> of waiting longer than half a minute is $e^{-1}\approx0.367879$. Here $\log$ is the <natural logarithm>. Already having waited a minute without an arrival leaves this same <conditional probability> of waiting another half minute. The <exponential distribution> describes a model with no aging of the remaining wait; a scheduled arrival with a fixed gap has no such property.

= Poisson observation scheme and rate likelihood
{parent=Poisson process}
{title2=$\widehat\lambda=N_H/H\quad\text{or}\quad n/T_n$}

A <Poisson process> observed for a fixed duration $H$ has likelihood proportional to $\lambda^{N_H}e^{-\lambda H}$. Complete independent <exponential distribution> gaps observed until a fixed event count $n$ have likelihood $\lambda^ne^{-\lambda T_n}$. Both give count divided by exposure, but the exposure is random in the second design. Under fixed exposure the total count is sufficient: conditional on it, the event locations are independent of the rate, so exact timestamps add no rate information to interval counts. Gap estimates and fixed-horizon estimates should not be compared without distinguishing their stopping rules and any final censored gap.

= Strong law for Poisson arrival times
{parent=Poisson process}
{title2=$Y_n/n\to1/\lambda$}

For a homogeneous <Poisson process> of rate $\lambda>0$, $N(t)/t\to\lambda$ almost surely: unit-time increments are independent Poisson variables and the <strong law of large numbers> applies, then monotonicity fills the intervals between integers. At its $n$th point $Y_n$, simplicity gives $N(Y_n)=n$. Consequently $Y_n/n\to1/\lambda$. Rate and mean interarrival time are reciprocals.

= Poisson exponential martingale
{parent=Poisson process}
{c}
{title2=$M_t^\theta=\exp(\theta N_t-t(e^\theta-1))$}

For a unit-rate <Poisson process> and real $\theta$, the displayed process is a nonnegative <martingale> with initial value one. Independent increments give $\mathbb E e^{\theta(N_t-N_s)}=\exp((t-s)(e^\theta-1))$, proving its conditional mean property. The <Ville inequality> turns this exponential moment into a maximal tail bound.

= Poisson maximal concentration bound
{parent=Poisson process}
{c}
{title2=$\Pr(\sup_{t\leq L}|N(t)-t|\geq L\eta)\leq2e^{-Lh(\eta)}$}

For a unit-rate <Poisson process>, $h(\eta)=(1+\eta)\log(1+\eta)-\eta$ gives the displayed maximal deviation bound for $L,\eta>0$. <Poisson exponential martingales> and the <Ville inequality> control upper and lower deviations; the upper-tail rate $h$ also bounds the lower-tail probability. Since $h(0)=0$ and $h'(\eta)=\log(1+\eta)>0$, the rate is strictly positive for every positive deviation level.

= Every second Poisson arrival has underdispersed counts
{c}
{parent=Poisson process}
{title2=$M(t)=\lfloor N(t)/2\rfloor$}

For a rate-$\lambda$ <Poisson process>, keeping every second arrival gives a renewal process with independent interarrival times equal to sums of two exponential waiting times. Put $x=\lambda t$. Its count mean and variance are $(2x+e^{-2x}-1)/4$ and $(4x-8xe^{-2x}-e^{-4x}+1)/16$. The variance is strictly smaller than the mean for $x>0$: sixteen times their difference has derivative $4[-1+4xe^{-2x}+e^{-4x}]<0$ and is zero at zero, because $\sinh(2x)>2x$. Therefore this process is not a <Cox process>.

= Doubly-stochastic Poisson process
{synonym}

A counting process which, conditional on a random nonnegative locally integrable intensity $\Lambda$, is an <inhomogeneous Poisson process>. For $L_t=\int_0^t\Lambda(s)\,ds$, conditioning gives $\mathbb EN(t)=\mathbb EL_t$ and $\operatorname{Var}N(t)=\mathbb EL_t+\operatorname{Var}L_t$. Thus finite-variance counts are never underdispersed. Randomness of the intensity may induce dependence between increments which are independent conditionally.

= Poisson offspring clusters directed by their parent process
{parent=Cox process}
{c}
{title2=$\Lambda_\Pi(A)=\mu\sum_{z\in\Pi}\kappa(A-z)$}

When each parent has a Poisson number of independently displaced offspring, the conditional offspring process has the displayed directing measure. For disjoint target sets, <multinomial distribution> splitting of a parent's Poisson count gives joint <probability generating function> $\exp(\mu\sum_j\kappa(A_j-z)(s_j-1))$. Multiply over <independent> parents to obtain <independent> Poisson counts conditional on the parents. Compactly supported displacement ensures local finiteness for a locally finite parent process.

= Random directing measure of a Cox process
{parent=Cox process}
{title2=$N\mid\Lambda\sim\operatorname{PRM}(\Lambda)$}

A <Cox process> can be directed by a random locally <finite measure>, which need not have a density. Conditional counts in disjoint sets are <independent> Poisson variables with that measure's masses. Uniform circular offspring displacements give a directing measure supported on random circles; its unconditional mean can still be a multiple of planar <Lebesgue measure>. A random intensity function is the special case in which the directing measure is absolutely continuous.

= Inhomogeneous Poisson process
{c}
{parent=Poisson process}
{wiki}

An inhomogeneous Poisson process with locally integrable intensity $\lambda(t)\geq0$ has independent increments and
$$
N_t-N_s\sim\operatorname{Poisson}\left(\int_s^t\lambda(u)\,du\right)
$$
for $0\leq s<t$. Its cumulative intensity is $\Lambda(t)=\int_0^t\lambda(u)\,du$.

= Time change of an inhomogeneous Poisson process
{parent=Inhomogeneous Poisson process}

If $\lambda>0$ and $g=\Lambda^{-1}$, then $M_t=N_{g(t)}$ is a rate-one <Poisson process>, because
$$
\Lambda(g(t))-\Lambda(g(s))=t-s.
$$
Conversely, $N_t=M_{\Lambda(t)}$, so $N_t$ has a Poisson distribution of mean $\Lambda(t)$.

= Symmetric Poisson difference process
{parent=Poisson process}

The difference of two independent rate-$\lambda$ <Poisson processes> is a continuous-time simple symmetric random walk with total jump rate $2\lambda$ and variance $2\lambda t$ at time $t$.

= Poisson thinning
{c}
{parent=Poisson process}

<Poisson thinning> splits a <Poisson process> by independently marking each arrival as retained or rejected. Let the original <homogeneous Poisson process> have rate $\lambda>0$, and let $p(s)\in[0,1]$ be a deterministic <measurable function> of time. Attach <independent> <uniform distributions> $U_1,U_2,\ldots$ on $(0,1)$, independent of the entire original <Poisson process>. At arrival time $T_i$, retain the arrival if $U_i\leq p(T_i)$. Given all arrival times, these decisions are <independent> and have the specified retention <probabilities>. Define the two <counting processes> using an <indicator function>:
$$
R_t=\sum_{i:T_i\leq t}\mathbf1_{\{U_i\leq p(T_i)\}},\qquad D_t=N_t-R_t.
$$
The <Poisson thinning theorem> gives <independent> retained and rejected <inhomogeneous Poisson processes>. Their rates are $\lambda p(s)$ and $\lambda(1-p(s))$, respectively, in the sense of the <intensity function of a point process>. In particular, for $0\leq a<b$,
$$
R_b-R_a\sim\operatorname{Poisson}\!\left(\lambda\int_a^b p(s)\,ds\right),\qquad D_b-D_a\sim\operatorname{Poisson}\!\left(\lambda\int_a^b(1-p(s))\,ds\right).
$$
The <independence> is of the two whole processes: every finite collection of retained counts is <independent> of every finite collection of rejected counts. It is not merely zero <covariance> for one interval. For constant $p$, the streams are <homogeneous Poisson processes> of rates $\lambda p$ and $\lambda(1-p)$; the endpoints $p=0,1$ include the identically zero stream.<Lectures on the Poisson Process by Günter Last and others>[Definition 5.7, Proposition 5.5, Theorem 5.8 and Corollary 5.9, printed pp. 42-44: location-dependent independent marking, intensities, and independence of the split processes]{ref}

For an exact toy calculation, take $\lambda=2$ per minute and retain with $p(s)=1/4$ on $(0,2]$ and $p(s)=3/4$ on $(2,4]$. In the first two minutes, the retained and rejected <expected values> are $2\cdot2\cdot1/4=1$ and $2\cdot2\cdot3/4=3$. In the next two minutes they are $3$ and $1$. Over all four minutes, $R_4,D_4$ are <independent> <Poisson distributions> with <mean> $4$ each, and their sum $N_4$ has <mean> $8$. Thus
$$
\mathbb P(R_4=0,D_4=0)=e^{-4}e^{-4}=e^{-8},\qquad \mathbb P(R_4=2,D_4=3)=e^{-8}\frac{4^2}{2!}\frac{4^3}{3!}=\frac{256}{3}e^{-8}.
$$
These <probabilities> and <expected values> describe the model, not empirical measurements. In the diagram, the original nine synthetic arrivals split into five retained and four rejected arrivals; those realized counts need not equal their <expected values>.

\Image[poisson-independent-thinning-expected-counts.png]{title=Independent marks split a synthetic Poisson arrival path, with exact expected counts for two time-dependent retention probabilities}

The mechanism explains why mutually exclusive labels can yield <independent> counts: randomness of the original total offsets the dependence from allocating a fixed total. For constant $p$ and an interval with original <mean> $m$, the conditional retained count has a <binomial distribution> given $N=n$. The joint <probability generating function> is
$$
\mathbb E[z^Rw^D]=\mathbb E[(pz+(1-p)w)^N]=\exp\{m(pz+(1-p)w-1)\}=e^{mp(z-1)}e^{m(1-p)(w-1)},\qquad 0\leq z,w\leq1.
$$
This factorization proves the two <independent> <Poisson distributions>; disjoint original intervals give <independent> pairs. For varying $p(s)$, the <independent marking theorem for Poisson point processes> gives the same conclusion with the two integrated intensities. The <joint red and green Poisson count formula> gives the corresponding direct calculation with <probability mass functions>.

Independence here is unconditional. After conditioning on $N=n$, the identity $D=n-R$ gives <covariance> $\operatorname{Cov}(R,D\mid N=n)=-np(1-p)$ for constant $p$. Thus the retained and rejected counts are dependent for $n>0$ and $0<p<1$, even though their unconditional <covariance> is zero. <Poisson thinning> can model independent routing of arrivals, or independent detection of arrivals with a known efficiency schedule; using only a retention fraction without checking the marking mechanism can give a wrong count law.

= Dependence in Poisson thinning
{c}
{parent=Poisson thinning}

Independent marking is the hypothesis that guarantees the <Poisson thinning theorem>. Equal marginal retention <probabilities> do not guarantee it. Consider one <Bernoulli random variable> $B$ with success <probability> $p\in(0,1)$, independent of a <Poisson process>, and retain every arrival when $B=1$ and none when $B=0$. Each arrival is retained with <probability> $p$, but the marks share the same coin. In an interval whose original count has <Poisson distribution> of <mean> $m>0$, the retained count is $R=BN$ and the rejected count is $D=(1-B)N$, so
$$
\mathbb E[R]=pm,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(R)=pm+p(1-p)m^2,\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(R,D)=-p(1-p)m^2.
$$
The extra <variance> shows that $R$ is not <Poisson distributed>; the negative <covariance> proves dependence. Its no-arrival <probability> is $1-p+pe^{-m}$, rather than $e^{-pm}$. For the toy values $p=1/2,m=2$, both rules have retained <mean> $1$, but the common-coin rule has <variance> $2$ and no-arrival <probability> $(1+e^{-2})/2\approx0.567668$, compared with <variance> $1$ and no-arrival <probability> $e^{-1}\approx0.367879$ for independent marking. These formulas follow by conditioning on $B$ and using the <law of total variance>.

An arrival-dependent or adversarial retention rule can change the law just as strongly. Keeping every second arrival gives $R_t=\lfloor N_t/2\rfloor$. Its successive retained gaps are sums of two <independent> <exponential random variables>, hence have the <Erlang distribution> of shape two and rate $\lambda$, not an <exponential distribution>. The resulting <renewal process> and its count <variance> are analyzed in <every second Poisson arrival has underdispersed counts>, and the two dependent streams in <alternating split of a Poisson process>. As an even more restrictive rule, keeping only the first arrival ever gives $R_t=\mathbf1_{\{N_t\geq1\}}$, a <Bernoulli distribution> at each $t>0$, so it cannot have a positive-mean <Poisson distribution>. A thinning rule must specify how decisions depend on arrivals and on one another; the independent-marking conclusion cannot be applied automatically to arbitrary deletion.

= Poisson characterization by independent binomial splitting
{c}
{parent=Poisson thinning}

Let $N$ be a nonnegative integer-valued <random variable> with finite <expected value> $\mu$, and independently label each of its items with either of two equally likely labels. If the two resulting counts are <independent random variables>, then $N$ has a <Poisson distribution>. Indeed, its <probability generating function> obeys $G(s)=G((1+s)/2)^2$. With $H(t)=G(1-t)$, iteration gives $H(t)=H(t/2^k)^{2^k}$. Finite <expected value> gives $H(h)=1-\mu h+o(h)$, hence $H(t)=e^{-\mu t}$ and $G(s)=e^{\mu(s-1)}$. Conversely, <Poisson thinning> produces independent <Poisson distributions> for the two counts.

= Categorical thinning of a Poisson claim count
{parent=Poisson thinning}
{title2=$N_i\text{ independent},\quad N_i\sim\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda p_i)$}

Assign each of $N\sim\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda)$ claims with <independent> category labels to one of finitely many categories with <probabilities> $p_i$. Conditional counts have a <multinomial distribution> with <probability generating function> $(\sum_i p_i z_i)^N$. Averaging over $N$ gives $\exp(\lambda(\sum_i p_i z_i-1))=\prod_i\exp(\lambda p_i(z_i-1))$. This proves <independent> category counts and supports discrete-severity <compound Poisson distribution> representations.

= Poisson onset-bin independence
{parent=Poisson thinning}
{c}

Mark each member of an independent Poisson infection cohort by its symptom-onset interval, using independent delays. <Poisson thinning> gives independent onset-bin counts, and sums over independent cohorts remain independent Poisson counts. Fixed cohort totals instead induce multinomial dependence. The marking assumptions are essential.

= Binomial thinning conditional on a total
{parent=Poisson thinning}

Conditioned on a total of $n$ Poisson points, the number falling in a region of relative intensity $p$ is $\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p)$.

= Infinite-server queue transient distribution
{parent=Poisson thinning}

For an <M-M-infinity queue> with empty initial state, Poisson arrivals of rate $\lambda$ and exponential lifetimes of rate $\mu$ produce a Poisson occupancy of mean $\lambda(1-e^{-\mu t})/\mu$ at time $t$.

= Kolmogorov forward equation for a Poisson process
{parent=Poisson process}
{c}

The state probabilities satisfy $p_0'=-\lambda p_0$ and $p_n'=\lambda p_{n-1}-\lambda p_n$, yielding the Poisson law of mean $\lambda t$.

= Poisson process conditional arrival times
{parent=Poisson process}
{c}

Conditioned on $N_t=n$, the arrival times are distributed as the order statistics of $n$ independent uniform points on $[0,t]$. Their conditional joint density on
$$
0<s_1<\cdots<s_n<t
$$
is $n!/t^n$, obtained by dividing the Poisson-process density $\lambda^ne^{-\lambda t}$ by $\mathbb P(N_t=n)$.

= Number of one Poisson type before another
{parent=Poisson process}

For independent Poisson processes of rates $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$, the number $K$ of type-two arrivals before the first type-one arrival satisfies
$$
\mathbb P(K=k)
=\left(\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}\right)^k
\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}.
$$
This follows by marking the superposed process by its arrival type.

= Exponential formula for a marked Poisson sum
{parent=Poisson process}

Let $J_i$ be the points of a rate-$\lambda$ Poisson process and let independent identically distributed marks $X_i$ be independent of the process. Then
$$
\mathbb E\exp\left(\theta\sum_{i=1}^{N_t}g(J_i,X_i)\right)
=\exp\left\{\lambda\int_0^t
\left(\mathbb E e^{\theta g(s,X_1)}-1\right)ds\right\}.
$$
Condition on $N_t$, use uniform order statistics, and sum the resulting exponential series.

= Age of a Poisson process
{parent=Poisson process}

With $J_0=0$, the age $A(t)=t-J_{N_t}$ of a rate-$\lambda$ Poisson process has mean
$$
\mathbb EA(t)=\frac{1-e^{-\lambda t}}{\lambda}.
$$
Given $N_t=n$, the last arrival is the maximum of $n$ uniform points and $\mathbb E[A(t)\mid N_t=n]=t/(n+1)$, including $n=0$.
