= Quantum ideal-gas statistics

= Quantum statistics
{synonym}

An ideal quantum gas has mean occupation
$$[\exp((E-\mu)/(k_BT))\mp1]^{-1},$$
with one sign for bosons and the other for fermions.

= Heat capacity for a two-dimensional linear fermion dispersion
{parent=Quantum ideal-gas statistics}
{title2=$C_+=9g\zeta(3)Ak_B^3T^2/(4\pi\hbar^2v^2)$}

For $g$ copies of a positive-energy branch $E=\hbar v|\mathbf k|$ in <area> $A$, the <density of states> is $D(E)=gAE/(2\pi\hbar^2v^2)$. At zero <chemical potential>, the thermal <energy> is $3g\zeta(3)A(k_BT)^3/(4\pi\hbar^2v^2)$, giving the displayed fixed-zero-chemical-potential <heat capacity>. If a symmetric filled negative-energy band also supplies holes, its excitation <energy> contributes an equal amount. Counting cones, <spin> species and holes explicitly is necessary before assigning an absolute numerical coefficient.

= Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics
{parent=Quantum ideal-gas statistics}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$f(E)\simeq e^{(\mu-E)/(k_BT)}$}

Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics describes a dilute classical gas whose single-particle occupation factors have the displayed exponential form. It is the low-occupation <classical limit of Bose--Einstein and Fermi--Dirac statistics>. Integrating this factor over momentum gives the <nonrelativistic Maxwell--Boltzmann number density> when the particle energy is $mc^2+p^2/(2m)$.

= Nonrelativistic Maxwell--Boltzmann number density
{parent=Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics}
{title2=$n=g_s(2\pi mk_BT/h^2)^{3/2}e^{(\mu-mc^2)/(k_BT)}$}

Integrating <Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics> over momentum for <nonrelativistic particles> of mass $m$, spin-state count $g_s$, and energy $mc^2+p^2/(2m)$ gives
$$
n=g_s\left(\frac{2\pi mk_BT}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}
e^{(\mu-mc^2)/(k_BT)}.
$$

= Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution
{parent=Quantum ideal-gas statistics}
{c}
{wiki=Maxwell–Boltzmann_distribution}
{title2=$s=\sqrt{k_BT/m}$}

The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution describes the random <velocities> and <speeds> of particles in a classical <ideal gas> at <thermal equilibrium>. Individual particles keep changing <velocity> through <collisions>, while the equilibrium <probability distribution> stays fixed. For one species of particle <mass> $m$ at positive <temperature> $T$, the characteristic scale is
$$
s=\sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{m}},
$$
where $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant>. A signed <velocity> component follows a <normal distribution>; the nonnegative magnitude follows the <Maxwell speed distribution>. Their peaks differ because a <speed> measurement groups together all directions of motion.

Use the gas rest frame, three spatial dimensions, and <nonrelativistic particles>, with $k_BT\ll mc^2$ where $c$ is the <speed of light>. Assume a dilute gas whose interactions between encounters can be neglected in the <ideal gas> approximation and whose translational motion is classical. A sufficient condition for negligible quantum exchange effects is $n\lambda^3\ll1$, where $n$ is the <number density> and $\lambda$ the <thermal de Broglie wavelength>. Diluteness alone does not guarantee <thermal equilibrium>; the gas must have equilibrated. These are the assumptions behind the classical <Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution>. https://farside.ph.utexas.edu/teaching/sm1/lectures/node72.html[Fitzpatrick, The Maxwell distribution]{ref} https://farside.ph.utexas.edu/teaching/sm1/lectures/node82.html[Fitzpatrick, Quantum statistics in the classical limit]{ref}

The connection to <Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics> is the <Boltzmann factor> for translational <kinetic energy>, $E_{\rm kin}=m|\mathbf v|^2/2$. Normalizing $e^{-E_{\rm kin}/(k_BT)}$ over <velocity> gives a probability density; an occupation factor $e^{(\mu-E)/(k_BT)}$ instead specifies a mean occupation, with <chemical potential> $\mu$. Their normalizations serve different purposes. https://farside.ph.utexas.edu/teaching/sm1/lectures/node81.html[Fitzpatrick, Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics]{ref}

This connects microscopic motion to measurements: a <gas thermometer> uses the <ideal gas law>, <collisions> and the <mean free path> govern transport, and measurements of <spectral lines> use <thermal Doppler broadening> to infer <temperature>. Each probes a different aspect of the same thermal motion.

In 1860, <James Clerk Maxwell> presented the component and <speed> laws in Part I, Proposition IV of *Illustrations of the Dynamical Theory of Gases*, pp. 22–23. His argument combined <independence> of the three <velocity> components with <isotropy>, and he explicitly compared the component law with the <normal distribution> of observational errors. Independence is an assumption of that argument; perpendicular coordinate axes alone do not establish it. https://sites.pitt.edu/~jdnorton/teaching/2559_Therm_Stat_Mech/handouts/Maxwell_1860_annot.pdf#page=5[Maxwell (1860), original article scan with annotations]{ref}

= Wall-impact speed distribution
{parent=Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution}

The <speed distribution> of atoms striking a wall is weighted by the normal component of <velocity>, relative to the distribution of atoms present in the bulk. For an isotropic classical monatomic gas, the impact speed density is proportional to $v^3e^{-mv^2/(2k_BT)}$, and the mean impact <kinetic energy> is $2k_BT$, rather than $3k_BT/2$.

{title2=$f(\mathbf v)$}
{c}
{parent=Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution}

= Maxwell velocity distribution
{c}
{synonym}

In the gas rest frame, the <Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution> is the <probability density function> per unit volume in <velocity> space:
$$
f(\mathbf v)=
\left(\frac{m}{2\pi k_BT}\right)^{3/2}
\exp\left(-\frac{m|\mathbf v|^2}{2k_BT}\right)
=\frac{e^{-|\mathbf v|^2/(2s^2)}}{(2\pi s^2)^{3/2}},
\qquad \int_{\mathbb R^3}f(\mathbf v)\,d^3\mathbf v=1.
$$
Thus $f(\mathbf v)\,d^3\mathbf v$ gives the <probability> of a small three-dimensional <velocity> region. For a gas with bulk <mean velocity> $\mathbf V$, replace $\mathbf v$ by $\mathbf v-\mathbf V$; the <Maxwell speed distribution> then describes the magnitude relative to that bulk motion.

For any one Cartesian <velocity> component $v_x$, integrating out the other two gives
$$
q(v_x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}s}e^{-v_x^2/(2s^2)},
\qquad \langle v_x\rangle=0,
\qquad \langle v_x^2\rangle=s^2=\frac{k_BT}{m}.
$$
The factorization $f(\mathbf v)=q(v_x)q(v_y)q(v_z)$ establishes <independence>: all three components are centered <normal random variables>, with <variance> $s^2$. Both the component density $q$ and the vector density $f$ have their largest values at zero. This concerns densities for small equal-sized regions; any single exact <velocity> still has <probability> zero. https://farside.ph.utexas.edu/teaching/sm1/lectures/node72.html[Fitzpatrick, component distribution and factorization]{ref}

The <standard deviation> $s$ measures thermal motion even though the <mean velocity> is zero. Positive and negative components cancel in the <mean>, but their squares add. This is why <thermal Doppler broadening> measures a nonzero line width in a gas with no bulk motion.

= Maxwell-Boltzmann speed distribution
{c}
{synonym}

The Maxwell speed distribution is the <probability density function> for the magnitude $v=|\mathbf v|$. Unlike a signed <velocity> component, <speed> cannot be negative:
$$
g(v)=4\pi v^2
\left(\frac{m}{2\pi k_BT}\right)^{3/2}
e^{-mv^2/(2k_BT)}
=\sqrt{\frac2\pi}\frac{v^2}{s^3}e^{-v^2/(2s^2)},
\qquad v\geq0.
$$
For $0\leq a<b$, the <probability> of a <speed> in $[a,b]$ is $\int_a^b g(v)\,dv$, and $\int_0^\infty g(v)\,dv=1$. The density has units of inverse <speed>, so its height alone is not a <probability>. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/2-4-distribution-of-molecular-speeds[OpenStax, University Physics Volume 2, §2.4]{ref}

All <velocities> with magnitudes from $v$ to $v+dv$ occupy a thin <spherical shell> of volume $4\pi v^2\,dv$ in <velocity> space. The <isotropy> of the <Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution> makes its density constant over each shell, giving $g(v)=4\pi v^2 f(v\mathbf e_x)$ for any fixed unit direction $\mathbf e_x$. A larger radius offers more directions and more volume. The increasing shell factor initially wins over the decreasing <Boltzmann factor>, so $g(0)=0$ and the <most probable speed> is positive. This is the geometric reason that the peaks of the vector and <speed> densities disagree.

With the <dimensionless quantity> $u=v/s$, every such gas has the same rescaled <speed> density:
$$
g(v)\,dv=h(u)\,du,
\qquad h(u)=\sqrt{\frac2\pi}u^2e^{-u^2/2},\quad u\geq0.
$$
For comparison, $w=v_x/s$ has the standard <normal distribution> density $\phi(w)=e^{-w^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ on the whole real line. The <Maxwell speed distribution> is neither the positive half of this curve nor the distribution of $|v_x|$: it combines three components.

\Image[maxwell-velocity-versus-speed.png]
{title=One signed velocity component peaks at zero, while speed peaks above zero because of spherical shells}

The first two <moments> of the <Maxwell speed distribution> give the <mean> and <root mean square>:
$$
\langle v\rangle=\sqrt{\frac8\pi}\,s,\qquad
\langle v^2\rangle=3s^2,\qquad
v_{\rm rms}=\sqrt{\langle v^2\rangle}=\sqrt3\,s.
$$
Consequently the <mean> translational <kinetic energy> is $m\langle v^2\rangle/2=3k_BT/2$, as required by the <equipartition theorem>. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/2-2-pressure-temperature-and-rms-speed[OpenStax, University Physics Volume 2, §2.2]{ref} The <mean> <speed> weights fast particles more than the density peak does, and the <root mean square> weights them more strongly still.

Changing <temperature> or particle <mass> only changes $s\propto\sqrt{T/m}$. At fixed <mass>, four times the <temperature> doubles every characteristic <speed>; at fixed <temperature>, four times the <mass> halves them. A stretched density becomes lower so that its area stays one. For two species at the same <temperature>, equal <mean> translational <kinetic energy> therefore means different typical <speeds>.

= Most probable speed in the Maxwell distribution
{title2=$v_{\rm mp}=\sqrt{2k_BT/m}$}
{parent=Maxwell speed distribution}

= Most probable speed
{synonym}

The most probable speed is the <mode>, or density maximum, of the <Maxwell speed distribution>:
$$
v_{\rm mp}=\sqrt{\frac{2k_BT}{m}}=\sqrt2\,s.
$$
For small equal-width <speed> bins, the bin near $v_{\rm mp}$ contains the largest expected fraction of particles. This does not mean that any exact <speed> has positive <probability>, or that every particle has this <speed>. The three common summaries obey
$$
\frac{v_{\rm mp}}s=\sqrt2\approx1.414,
\qquad \frac{\langle v\rangle}s=\sqrt{\frac8\pi}\approx1.596,
\qquad \frac{v_{\rm rms}}s=\sqrt3\approx1.732,
$$
so $v_{\rm mp}<\langle v\rangle<v_{\rm rms}$. The first describes a density peak, the second the <mean> <speed>, and the third the <root mean square> associated with <kinetic energy>. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/2-4-distribution-of-molecular-speeds[OpenStax, characteristic molecular speeds]{ref}

For a concrete scale $s=300\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, these are approximately $424$, $479$, and $520\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The most probable <velocity> vector and each most probable component remain zero in the gas rest frame. A zero vector labels one small region of <velocity> space, whereas a positive <speed> labels an entire <spherical shell>.

A short check uses the <derivative> of the <logarithm> of the <Maxwell speed distribution> for $v>0$:
$$
\frac{d}{dv}\log g(v)=\frac2v-\frac{v}{s^2}.
$$
It is positive below $\sqrt2\,s$ and negative above it, locating the unique peak. The factor $v^2$ from the <spherical shell> supplies the term $2/v$; omitting it would incorrectly move the <speed> peak to zero.

= Doppler broadening
{parent=Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution}
{wiki}

Doppler broadening is the spread of an observed spectral line caused by the distribution of emitter velocities along the line of sight.

= Doppler resolution of a Zeeman pattern
{parent=Doppler broadening}
{title2=$B_{\rm res}\sim3h\Delta\nu_D/(2\mu_B)$}

A Maxwellian emitter has <Gaussian> Doppler <frequency> FWHM $\Delta\nu_D/\nu=\sqrt{8k_BT\ln2/(mc^2)}$. Equating this width to the minimum adjacent weak-field sodium component separation $2\mu_BB/(3h)$ gives the title resolution scale. A <Gaussian> blend has no unique resolution threshold: disappearance of individual components and disappearance of the whole broadened multiplet are different criteria. Instrumental resolution, pressure broadening, selected <polarization> and line strengths must be specified for a sharper prediction.

= Thermal Doppler broadening
{parent=Doppler broadening}

If nonrelativistic emitters of mass $m$ have rest-frame angular frequency $\omega_0$, then the one-dimensional <Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution> produces a Gaussian line with standard deviation
$$
\sigma_\omega=\frac{\omega_0}{c}\sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{m}}.
$$
Measuring the line width therefore determines the gas temperature when nonthermal broadening is negligible or separately modeled.

= Thermal cutoff of Lyman-alpha line widths
{parent=Thermal Doppler broadening}
{title2=$b_{\rm th}^2=2k_BT/m_{\rm H}$}

A purely thermal hydrogen <absorption line> has velocity-space optical depth proportional to $\exp[-(v-v_0)^2/b^2]$ with the displayed width. Additional independent Gaussian broadening contributes positive terms to $b^2$. Fitting the lower envelope of width against neutral-hydrogen <column density>, with numerical calibration for density and pressure smoothing, estimates the <temperature-density relation of photoionized intergalactic gas>. A single broad line only bounds the temperature from above when its nonthermal width is unknown.

= Density of states
{title2=$g(E)$}
{parent=Quantum ideal-gas statistics}
{wiki}

The density of states $g(E)$ is defined so that $g(E)\,dE$ counts one-particle states with energies in an interval of width $dE$. A sum over closely spaced states then becomes an integral weighted by $g(E)$.

= Free-electron state counting in different dimensions
{parent=Density of states}
{title2=$g(E_F)=dn/(2E_F)$}

Two spin states per <wavevector> and <parabolic dispersion> give $n=2v_dk_F^d/(2\pi)^d$, where $v_d$ is the unit-ball volume. Thus $E_F=\hbar^2[na_d]^{2/d}/(2m)$ with $a_1=\pi/2$, $a_2=2\pi$, $a_3=3\pi^2$. Differentiating the cumulative count yields $g(E_F)=dn/(2E_F)$, normalized per length, area or volume as appropriate.

= Anisotropic ideal-gas density of states
{parent=Density of states}
{title2=$N(\varepsilon,\gamma)=\sqrt\gamma\,N(\varepsilon,1)$}

For the continuum dispersion $\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}=\hbar^2(k_x^2+k_y^2+k_z^2/\gamma)/(2m)$ with $\gamma>0$, substituting $q_z=k_z/\sqrt\gamma$ makes the energy isotropic and gives Jacobian $\sqrt\gamma$. Every momentum-state counting integral therefore acquires that factor. Per internal state and unit volume, $N=\sqrt\gamma(2m/\hbar^2)^{3/2}\sqrt\varepsilon/(4\pi^2)$. The result assumes an unrestricted three-dimensional continuum; increasing this mass anisotropy is not the same operation as imposing a two-dimensional Brillouin zone and a finite interlayer band width.

= Single-polarization two-dimensional photon density of states
{parent=Density of states}
{title2=$g(\omega)/V=\omega/(2\pi c^2d)$}

For an area $A$ with periodic in-plane <boundary conditions>, one polarization contributes $A\,2\pi k\,dk/(2\pi)^2$ <normal modes> of the <electromagnetic field>. For linear <dispersion relation> $\omega=ck$ and effective physical volume $Ad$, this gives the <density of states> in the title. No extra factor two for polarization is allowed. Opposite in-plane directions are already counted in the full circle of <wavevectors>.

= Semiclassical density of states in a central potential
{parent=Density of states}

For a spinless nonrelativistic particle in a central potential, $g(\epsilon)=(2m)^{3/2}(4\pi^2\hbar^3)^{-1}\int_{U<\epsilon}\sqrt{\epsilon-U}\,d^3r$. Spherical symmetry reduces this to a radial integral over every allowed shell. It is a leading phase-space approximation.

= Spinless cubic-box wave-vector counting
{parent=Density of states}

Periodic boundaries on a cube give one spinless state per $(2\pi/L)^3$ in wave-vector space. Counting the full spherical shell gives $d\mathcal N/V=k^2dk/(2\pi^2)$. Hard-wall counting in the positive octant gives the same leading <volume> term.

= Phonon density of states
{parent=Density of states}
{title2=$g(\omega)=dN/d\omega$}

The number of lattice-vibration modes per unit <angular frequency>. For a three-dimensional isotropic monatomic <crystal> with three linear acoustic branches of a common speed $v_s$, wavevector-shell counting gives $g(\omega)=3V\omega^2/(2\pi^2v_s^3)$ below a suitable cutoff. Different branch speeds replace $3v_s^{-3}$ by $v_L^{-3}+2v_T^{-3}$.

= Density of states of a monatomic harmonic chain
{parent=Phonon density of states}
{title2=$g(\omega)=2N/[\pi\sqrt{\omega_m^2-\omega^2}]$}

For the nearest-neighbour <monatomic-chain dispersion relation>, $\omega_m=2\sqrt{\alpha/M}$ and $d\omega/dq=(a/2)\sqrt{\omega_m^2-\omega^2}$ on the positive half-zone. The folded wave-number count gives the displayed <density of states>, including both propagation directions. Its upper-edge <Van Hove singularity> is integrable: $\int_0^{\omega_m}g(\omega)d\omega=N$. A finite chain has discrete modes; the continuous density describes its large-size limit.

= One-dimensional acoustic phonon heat capacity
{parent=Phonon density of states}
{title2=$C/\ell=\pi k_B^2T/(3\hbar v)$}

A single acoustic branch $\omega=v|k|$ has thermal energy per length $U_T/\ell=\int dk\,\hbar v|k|/[2\pi(e^{\beta\hbar v|k|}-1)]=\pi(k_BT)^2/(6\hbar v)$. Differentiating gives the displayed linear <heat capacity>. The regime requires <temperatures> below the microscopic <phonon> cutoff but above the finite-size level spacing, with the translational <zero mode> treated separately.

= Van Hove singularity
{parent=Density of states}
{c}
{wiki}

A nonanalyticity of the <density of states> associated with a stationary point of a band <dispersion relation>. Its form depends on dimension and on whether the stationary point is an extremum or a saddle.

= Logarithmic two-dimensional saddle joint density of states
{parent=Van Hove singularity}
{title2=$J(E)\sim g_s m^*/(2\pi^2\hbar^2)\ln(E_\Lambda/|E-E_s|)$}

A single two-dimensional quadratic saddle with $A=\hbar^2/(2m^*)$ contributes this leading <joint density of states> when both contour branches are included and $g_s$ counts independent spin channels. The contour integral is proportional to $\int_{-\Lambda}^{\Lambda}dy/\sqrt{y^2+\delta/A}=2\operatorname{arsinh}(\Lambda\sqrt{A/\delta})$ above the saddle. Its asymptote gives the logarithm with $E_\Lambda$ of order $A\Lambda^2$. The cutoff-dependent constant is not universal. This is a density per area per energy; changing to a density per frequency requires a Jacobian. <Optical Properties of Solids by Mildred S. Dresselhaus>[Chapter4, especiallyTable4.1: two-dimensional saddle critical point]{ref}

= Spin and branch counting in saddle-point optical spectra
{parent=Logarithmic two-dimensional saddle joint density of states}

A spin factor and disconnected contour branches count different independent contributions. For the transition energy $E_s+\hbar^2(k_x^2-k_y^2)/(2m^*)$, one branch contributes $g_s m^*I/(4\pi^2\hbar^2)$, with $I=\int dk_y/\sqrt{k_y^2+\delta/A}$. The full two-branch saddle doubles this. A formula for one branch with spin degeneracy two can therefore equal a full-contour formula with spin degeneracy one, but those are distinct counting conventions. An extra valley or inequivalent saddle requires its own multiplicity.

= One-dimensional van Hove singularity
{parent=Van Hove singularity}

For a one-dimensional subband $E=E_0+\alpha k^2$ with $\alpha>0$, the <density of states> varies as $(E-E_0)^{-1/2}$ just above its edge. Optical peaks also depend on the <joint density of states>, selection rules and <excitons>.

= Density of occupied states
{parent=Density of states}
{title2=$Z(E)=D(E)f(E)$}

The density of available one-particle states multiplied by their mean occupation. For equilibrium electrons, $Z(E)=D(E)f(E)$ with the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> $f$. The integral of $Z$ counts electrons; the integral of $D$ counts available states.

= Positive-energy quasiparticle density from a folded spectrum
{parent=Density of states}
{title2=$D_+(E)=\sum_{\xi:E(\xi)=E}g(\xi)|d\xi/dE|$}

A many-to-one change of energy variable requires summing every inverse branch. For $E(\xi)=\sqrt{\xi^2+\Delta^2}$ and constant normal <density of states> $g_F$ on both sides of zero, there are two roots for $E>\Delta$, giving $D_+(E)=2g_FE/\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}$. A single branch instead gives $g_FE/\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}$. The distinction persists when $\Delta\to0$: folding $\xi$ into $|\xi|$ doubles a two-sided constant density on the positive axis. Spin degeneracy is a separate normalization choice; it must be applied consistently to both normal and transformed densities. The <BCS electronic density of states> has the single-factor expression because it is spectrally weighted rather than an unweighted branch count.

= Joint density of states
{parent=Density of states}
{title2=$J(\Omega)=\sum_{v,c}\int\frac{d^dk}{(2\pi)^d}\,\delta(E_c(\mathbf k)-E_v(\mathbf k)-\Omega)$}

For filled valence and empty conduction <energy bands>, the joint <density of states> counts pairs of states at a prescribed interband energy difference. In a direct optical transition the photon momentum is usually negligible compared with a <Brillouin zone> dimension, so initial and final states have nearly the same <crystal momentum>. The absorption spectrum weights this count by transition <matrix elements>; its onset and structure can constrain the <band gap> and <electronic band structure>. Indirect transitions additionally involve <phonons> to supply crystal momentum.

= Three-dimensional parabolic interband joint density of states
{parent=Joint density of states}
{title2=$g_j(E)=\frac{(2m_r/\hbar^2)^{3/2}}{2\pi^2}\sqrt{E-E_g}$}

For isotropic direct-gap parabolic bands, $m_r^{-1}=m_e^{*-1}+m_h^{*-1}$ and transition <energy> is $E_g+\hbar^2k^2/(2m_r)$. Differentiating the two-spin state count $k^3/(3\pi^2)$ per volume gives the displayed density for $E>E_g$, and zero below. Density per <angular frequency> is $\hbar g_j(\hbar\omega)$, not the same dimensioned quantity.

= Saddle-point optical transition contour
{parent=Joint density of states}
{title2=$k_x^2-k_y^2=(\hbar\omega-E_s)/A$}

For a local transition-energy model $E_c-E_v=E_s+A(k_x^2-k_y^2)$ with $A>0$, positive detuning has two hyperbola branches opening along x. Negative detuning opens along y; the zero-detuning limiting contour is a pair of intersecting lines. The coarea integrand on one positive-detuning branch reduces to $dk_y/[2A\sqrt{k_y^2+\delta/A}]$. The gradient vanishes at the saddle, so this is a limiting description as nonzero detuning approaches zero.

= Square-zone cutoff of a saddle contour
{parent=Saddle-point optical transition contour}
{title2=$|k_y|\leq\sqrt{\Lambda^2-\delta/A}$}

For positive detuning inside a strict square region $|k_x|,|k_y|\leq\Lambda$, substituting $k_x^2=k_y^2+\delta/A$ bounds $k_y$ by the displayed value, provided $0<\delta/A<\Lambda^2$. Integrating to $\Lambda$ instead extends the contour slightly outside that square. Either prescription has the same leading logarithmic singularity near zero detuning; it does not give the same exact finite-detuning result.

= Dimensional interband threshold shapes
{parent=Joint density of states}
{title2=$\rho_{eh}(E)\propto(E-E_{\rm th})^{d/2-1}$}

Parabolic electron-hole continua with $d$ unconfined directions have cumulative state count proportional to $(E-E_{\rm th})^{d/2}$. Differentiating gives the threshold joint density proportional to $(E-E_{\rm th})^{d/2-1}$: a square root for bulk, a step for a well, and an inverse square root for a wire. A fully confined dot instead has discrete lines. Optical matrix elements, occupations, <excitons> and broadening determine the actual absorption beyond this counting argument.

= Level-set representation of the joint density of states
{parent=Joint density of states}

For a smooth two-dimensional transition <energy> $D(\mathbf k)$ and a regular level $D=E$, the coarea identity gives $\int d^2k\,\delta(D(\mathbf k)-E)=\int_{D=E}dl/|\nabla D|$. Critical points require a limiting or distributional treatment; the apparent small contour at a parabolic edge is compensated by its small <energy> gradient.

= Two-dimensional joint density of states
{parent=Joint density of states}
{title2=$J_{2D}$}

For two isotropic parabolic subbands, the <joint density of states> per area and per independent channel is $\mu/(2\pi\hbar^2)\,\Theta(\hbar\omega-E_{\rm edge})$. The constant is a two-dimensional phase-space effect. Degeneracy and optical <matrix elements> determine the actual step weights.

= One-dimensional free-electron density of states
{parent=Density of states}
{title2=$g_1(E)=L\sqrt{2m}/(\pi\hbar\sqrt E)$}

For a length $L$ and parabolic free-electron <dispersion relation> $E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$, the displayed total <density of states> includes both signs of $k$ and both <spin> states. Per spin it is half as large. Its $E^{-1/2}$ dependence follows by transforming the constant signed-$k$ density $L/(2\pi)$ to <energy> and including both momenta. The continuum singularity at the band bottom is integrable; a finite specimen still has discrete levels.

= Local density of states
{parent=Density of states}
{title2=$\rho(\mathbf r,E)$}

The <local density of states> resolves the <density of states> by <position> as well as <energy>. With a consistent counting of <spin> states, its <integral> over the specimen's <volume> gives the total <density of states>, $g(E)=\int\rho(\mathbf r,E)\,d^3\mathbf r$. It describes the spatial distribution of available states, rather than their occupation.

= Spin-summed density of states
{parent=Density of states}
{title2=$\nu=2\nu_s$}

For a spin-degenerate band, the total <density of states> includes both spin species and is twice the <single-spin density of states>. Per lattice site, $\nu(\epsilon)=2N^{-1}\sum_k\delta(\epsilon-\epsilon_k)$. Using a separate explicit spin sum then requires a compensating factor one half when converting momentum sums. The normalization matters in the <Stoner criterion>.

= Single-spin density of states
{parent=Density of states}
{title2=$\nu_s=\nu_{\mathrm{tot}}/2$}

The <single-spin density of states> counts momentum states for one spin species. For degenerate up and down species, the spin-summed <density of states> is twice this value. Specify separately whether the density is per unit volume or extensive. In the <BCS gap equation>, the momentum sum labels opposite-spin pairs once and therefore uses the single-spin density per volume when the interaction has normalization $V/\mathcal V$.

= Fermi distribution
{c}
{synonym}

The <Fermi-Dirac distribution> follows from <Fermi-Dirac statistics> and is $n(E)=[e^{\beta(E-\mu)}+1]^{-1}$.

The mean occupation of a fermionic one-particle state of energy $E$ is
$$
f(E)=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(E-\mu)}+1},
\qquad \beta=(k_BT)^{-1}.
$$
At zero temperature this becomes the step function $\mathbf 1_{E<E_F}$.

= Fermi window
{parent=Fermi-Dirac distribution}
{title2=$w(\epsilon)=-\partial_\epsilon n_F=[4k_BT]^{-1}\operatorname{sech}^2[(\epsilon-\mu)/(2k_BT)]$}

The <Fermi window> measures sensitivity of occupation to <energy> near the <chemical potential>. It is positive, even about $\mu$, has width of order $k_BT$, and integrates to one over the full <energy> line. Its centred <variance> is $\pi^2(k_BT)^2/3$. A band restricted to nonnegative <energy> contains weight $n_F(0,\mu)$ instead of exactly one; extending to the full line is exponentially accurate when $\mu\gg k_BT$. At zero <temperature> the window tends to a <Dirac delta function> at the <chemical potential>.

= Thermal damping of a sinusoidal density of states
{parent=Fermi window}
{title2=$R_T=X/\sinh X,\quad X=2\pi^2k_BT/\Delta$}

A <density of states> $g(\epsilon)=g_0[1+\alpha\cos(2\pi\epsilon/\Delta)]$ has zero-temperature oscillatory <grand potential> $g_0\alpha\Delta^2\cos(2\pi\mu/\Delta)/(4\pi^2)$. Its Fermi-window <convolution> multiplies that <oscillation> by $R_T$ when the lower band edge is far below the <chemical potential>. The <Fourier transform> of the even window removes its <sine> component and gives the title factor. At high $X$, $R_T\sim2Xe^{-X}$; at small $X$, $R_T=1-X^2/6+O(X^4)$. The corresponding thermal change relative to zero <temperature> has <amplitude> proportional to $1-R_T$, rather than $R_T$.

= Oscillatory thermal-change amplitude ratio
{parent=Thermal damping of a sinusoidal density of states}
{title2=$\mathcal R=6|\alpha|(1-X/\sinh X)/X^2$}

For the sinusoidal density-of-states model, divide the oscillatory <amplitude> of $\Phi(T,\mu)-\Phi(0,\mu)$ by its smooth magnitude $g_0\pi^2(k_BT)^2/6$. This gives the title ratio. Its limit as $T\to0$ is $|\alpha|$, although both changes vanish at exactly zero <temperature>. The expansion is $|\alpha|[1-7X^2/60+O(X^4)]$, and its large-$X$ envelope is $6|\alpha|/X^2$. A fixed <chemical potential> adds the actual <cosine> <phase>, which can make the oscillatory contribution zero even when the envelope is nonzero. Exponential suppression of the full oscillatory potential therefore must not be confused with this algebraic thermal-change ratio.

= Boltzmann approximation
{c}
{parent=Fermi-Dirac distribution}
{title2=$f(E)\simeq e^{-(E-\mu)/(kT)}$}

When $E-\mu\gg kT$, the unit term in the denominator of the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> is negligible. The resulting exponentially small occupation is the <Boltzmann approximation>. It justifies the usual nondegenerate <semiconductor> carrier formulas; it fails near strongly occupied band states.

= Sommerfeld expansion
{c}
{parent=Fermi-Dirac distribution}
{wiki}
{title2=$\int_0^\infty f(E)p_F(E)\,dE=\int_0^\mu f(E)\,dE+\frac{\pi^2}{6}(k_BT)^2f'(\mu)+\cdots$}

The <Sommerfeld expansion> approximates integrals weighted by the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> when $k_BT\ll\mu$ and $f$ is smooth near $\mu$. Applying it to the number integral at fixed particle number gives $\mu-E_F=-(\pi^2/6)(k_BT)^2g'(E_F)/g(E_F)+\cdots$. The sign depends on the slope of the <density of states>. For an unbounded parabolic band, $g(E)\propto E^{d/2-1}$, yielding opposite quadratic shifts in one and three dimensions. In two dimensions the leading algebraic shift vanishes, but the finite band-bottom cutoff still gives an exponentially small negative shift.

= Fixed-chemical-potential Sommerfeld density increase
{parent=Sommerfeld expansion}

For a three-dimensional parabolic band and fixed positive $\mu-E_c$, the <Sommerfeld expansion> gives $n(T)=n(0)[1+\pi^2(k_BT/(\mu-E_c))^2/8+O(T^4)]$. This increase differs from the usual fixed-density calculation, in which the <chemical potential> decreases with <temperature>. The exact density is proportional to $T^{3/2}F_{1/2}[(\mu-E_c)/(k_BT)]$.

= Monotonic density at fixed chemical potential in a square-root density of states
{parent=Fixed-chemical-potential Sommerfeld density increase}

The <temperature> derivative of the Fermi occupation has opposite signs below and above $\mu$. Pairing energies $\mu\pm x$ leaves a positive contribution because the <density of states> increases with energy. The unpaired higher-energy tail is also positive. Hence the density increases at fixed $\mu$ for an unbounded three-dimensional parabolic band.

= Electron-positron thermal pair abundance
{parent=Fermi-Dirac distribution}

Thermal electron-positron pairs in <chemical equilibrium> with <photons> have opposite <chemical potentials> when the one-particle <energy> includes rest <energy>. For negligible degeneracy and $k_BT\ll m_ec^2$, the zero-chemical-potential density per charge species is $n_0=2[m_ek_BT/(2\pi\hbar^2)]^{3/2}e^{-m_ec^2/(k_BT)}$. In that nondegenerate limit, <charge neutrality> and the classical equilibrium product $n_-n_+=n_0^2$ give $n_+=(\sqrt{n_b^2+4n_0^2}-n_b)/2$, with net <electron> density $n_b$. Pairs are important when $n_0\gtrsim n_b$. At arbitrary <temperature> the zero-chemical-potential marker uses the integral $n_0=(\pi^2\hbar^3)^{-1}\int_0^\infty p^2[\exp(\sqrt{m_e^2c^4+p^2c^2}/k_BT)+1]^{-1}dp$. Its ultrarelativistic limit is $3\zeta(3)(k_BT/\hbar c)^3/(2\pi^2)$. A nonzero net <chemical potential> shifts the exact abundance boundary; the zero-potential curve is an approximate crossover marker.

= Fermion-to-boson thermal integral ratio
{parent=Fermi-Dirac distribution}

For $s>0$,
$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{x^s\,dx}{e^x+1}
=\left(1-2^{-s}\right)
\int_0^\infty\frac{x^s\,dx}{e^x-1}.
$$
This follows from $(e^x+1)^{-1}=(e^x-1)^{-1}-2(e^{2x}-1)^{-1}$. In three spatial dimensions it gives the fermion-to-boson ratios $3/4$ for ultrarelativistic particle number and $7/8$ for energy density.

= Low-temperature heat capacity of a three-dimensional Fermi gas

For a three-dimensional <ideal gas> with quadratic dispersion and fixed particle number, the total <density of states> at the <Fermi energy> is $g(E_F)=3N/(2E_F)$. The <Sommerfeld coefficient> then gives $C_V=(\pi^2/2)Nk_B(T/T_F)$ to leading order, where $T_F=E_F/k_B$. The fixed-number <energy> correction is $\Delta U=(\pi^2/4)N(k_BT)^2/E_F$. Only states near the <Fermi surface> change occupation, explaining the small active fraction.

= Nuclear Fermi gas model

A model with separate filled <proton> and <neutron> <momentum> seas in a <nuclear mean field>. = Two-dimensional electron gas

An <electron gas> confined in one spatial direction, with motion available in the other two. Its <electron> number per unit <area> is $n_s=N/A$. The ideal <Fermi gas> approximation neglects interactions between <electrons>; an actual <two-dimensional electron gas> can have important interactions. Confinement alone does not require a <magnetic field> or <Landau levels>.

= Boltzmann carrier density of a two-dimensional parabolic band

{c}

State counting with total degeneracy $g$ gives a constant two-dimensional <density of states> $gm/(2\pi\hbar^2)$ above a parabolic <conduction band> edge. Integrating the Boltzmann occupation yields the displayed areal <carrier density>. For one valley and two spin states the coefficient is $mk_BT/(\pi\hbar^2)$. Applying the same count to <holes> gives $np=N_cN_v e^{-E_g/(k_BT)}$ and the intrinsic density $n_i=\sqrt{N_cN_v}e^{-E_g/(2k_BT)}\propto T e^{-E_g/(2k_BT)}$, distinct from the three-dimensional $T^{3/2}$ prefactor.

= Top-gate density tuning
{parent=Two-dimensional electron gas}

A gate separated by <dielectric> thickness $d$ has geometric <capacitance> per area $C/A=\epsilon/d$ in the parallel-plate approximation. = Transverse magnetic focusing

{title2=$B_j=2j\hbar k_F/(eL)$}

A <magnetic field> curves ballistic carrier trajectories between an injector and a collector. For openings separated along a specular edge by $L$, peaks occur near $L=2jr_c$ with $r_c=\hbar k_F/(e|B|)$, giving $|B_j|=2j\hbar k_F/(eL)$. Field polarity and carrier sign select the connection direction. <Electron phase coherence> can produce fine structure but is not required for the basic semiclassical focusing geometry.

= Ballistic focusing mobility criterion
{parent=Transverse magnetic focusing}

In a parabolic band, transport mobility and <mean free path> obey $\ell=\mu\hbar k_F/e$. The rough condition $\ell\gtrsim L$ gives $\mu\gtrsim eL/(\hbar k_F)$, whereas surviving a first semicircle uses path length $\pi L/2$. Clear peaks usually need a larger margin and a specular boundary, so this is an observability scale rather than a universal sharp threshold.

= Quantum point contact
{parent=Two-dimensional electron gas}

A short electrostatically defined constriction connecting two <electron> reservoirs. Transverse confinement produces transport modes, and sufficiently narrow contacts can collimate carriers for electron-focusing experiments. Its quantum conductance depends on mode transmission; an opening described only as narrower than a micrometre is not automatically in a single-mode limit.

= Parallel-field splitting of quantum-point-contact steps
{parent=Quantum point contact}

A parallel field lifts spin degeneracy and separates threshold energies. The step size may change from $2e^2/h$ to $e^2/h$ when the two spin branches are resolved. Finite layer thickness can additionally alter orbital confinement.

= Nonlinear conductance of a quantum point contact
{parent=Quantum point contact}
{title2=$dI/dV=(e^2/h)\sum_n[T_n(\mu_S)+T_n(\mu_D)]$}

At zero <temperature>, a rigid spin-degenerate transmission spectrum with symmetric source/drain bias has $\mu_{S,D}=E_F\pm eV/2$ and the displayed differential conductance. Half-integer plateaus in units $2e^2/h$ occur when the two bias-window edges sample different channel counts. The bias convention and electrostatic response matter.

= Bias spectroscopy of one-dimensional subbands
{parent=Nonlinear conductance of a quantum point contact}
{title2=$E_{n+1}-E_n=e|V|$}

In a rigid spectrum, source and drain threshold lines in a bias-versus-gate map satisfy $E_n(V_g)=\mu_S$ or $\mu_D$. Where adjacent thresholds align with opposite edges of the bias window, their spacing equals the window width $e|V|$. For symmetric bias and <spin degeneracy>, neighboring channel counts at those edges give a differential <electrical conductance> halfway between integer $2e^2/h$ plateaus. The dc ratio $I/V$ is a different observable; barrier motion and heating can modify the interpretation.

= Voltage partition in a ballistic constriction
{parent=Nonlinear conductance of a quantum point contact}

A channel supporting <ballistic electron transport> carries incident populations set by different source and drain <chemical potentials>, rather than an everywhere-equilibrated local distribution with an Ohmic <voltage> gradient. Its electrostatic barrier responds self-consistently to contacts, gates and <electrostatic screening>. A symmetric rigid-barrier model places reservoir energies at $E_F\pm eV/2$ relative to the barrier. Asymmetric partition or bias-dependent transmission changes the weights in the differential <electrical conductance>, and must be included when extracting an <energy> scale.

= Half-integer channel plateaus from a symmetric bias window
{parent=Nonlinear conductance of a quantum point contact}
{title2=$G_d=(e^2/h)\sum_n[T_n(\mu_S)+T_n(\mu_D)]$}

For rigid spin-degenerate channel transmissions and $\mu_{S,D}=E_F\pm eV/2$, differentiating $I=(2e/h)\sum_n\int_{\mu_D}^{\mu_S}T_n(E)dE$ gives $G_d=(e^2/h)\sum_n[T_n(\mu_S)+T_n(\mu_D)]$. If the two edges sample neighboring integer channel counts, $G_d$ is a half-integer multiple of $2e^2/h$. Explicit bias dependence of the potential adds terms; the apparent fraction does not imply fractional carriers.

= Saddle-point model of a quantum point contact
{parent=Quantum point contact}
{title2=$U=U_0-m^*\omega_x^2x^2/2+m^*\omega_y^2y^2/2$}

A locally separable potential with an unstable longitudinal maximum and stable transverse confinement. Mode $n$ sees barrier top $E_n=U_0+\hbar\omega_y(n+1/2)$ and transmission $T_n(E)=[1+\exp(-2\pi(E-E_n)/(\hbar\omega_x))]^{-1}$. It is a single-particle smooth-barrier model; interactions and bias-induced reshaping require additions.

= Adiabatic electron collimation

Slow widening of a constriction approximately conserves the transverse mode action, reducing transverse momentum as the width $W$ increases. A lowering band bottom also increases longitudinal kinetic energy. Together they can narrow the angular distribution of an emitted electron beam. An abrupt narrow aperture alone can instead diffract strongly.

= Collinear quantum point contacts

An injector and collector aligned across an open <two-dimensional electron gas>. A direction-sensitive signal between them probes <ballistic electron transport>. Contact resistance and diffuse current backgrounds must be separated from the direct beam contribution.

= Split gate

Two surface electrodes whose depletion regions define a narrow conducting opening in an underlying <two-dimensional electron gas>. Gate voltage changes the transverse confinement and channel transmission without physically etching the conducting channel to its electrical width.

= Degenerate electron gas
{parent=Fermi gas}
{title2=$k_BT\ll E_F$}

An electron <Fermi gas> whose occupation is governed mainly by the <Pauli exclusion principle>, with thermal energy small compared with its kinetic <Fermi energy>. At zero temperature its momentum states are filled up to $p_F$. In the ultrarelativistic limit $E/V=3ncp_F/4\propto n^{4/3}$, with $n=4\pi g_sp_F^3/(3h^3)$. This supplies the pressure supporting a <white dwarf> until the relativistic mass limit is approached.

= Finite-temperature electron equation of state
{parent=Fermi gas}
{title2=$P_e(\rho,T)$}

Neglecting interactions and thermal pairs, the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> $f(p)=[\exp((\varepsilon(p)-\mu_{\rm kin})/(k_BT))+1]^{-1}$ with kinetic energy $\varepsilon(p)=\sqrt{m_e^2c^4+p^2c^2}-m_ec^2$ determines the <electron> contribution to a stellar <equation of state>. The kinetic <chemical potential> is fixed by the electron <number density>, not an independently chosen pressure fraction:
$$
n_e=\frac1{\pi^2\hbar^3}\int_0^\infty p^2f(p)\,dp,\qquad
P_e=\frac1{3\pi^2\hbar^3}\int_0^\infty\frac{p^4c^2}{\sqrt{m_e^2c^4+p^2c^2}}f(p)\,dp.
$$
The electron kinetic <internal energy> per volume is $(\pi^2\hbar^3)^{-1}\int_0^\infty p^2\varepsilon(p)f(p)\,dp$. In the nondegenerate limit $P_e=n_ek_BT$; at zero <temperature> the integrals become the <equation of state of a cold electron gas>. These are limits of one component and must not be added together as independent electron pressures. When <electron-positron thermal pair abundance> matters, both charge species must be included, with charge neutrality fixing their net density.

= Adiabatic scaling of a Fermi gas with power-law density of states
{parent=Fermi gas}

For <density of states> $AV\epsilon^a$, $a>-1$, the <grand canonical ensemble> has $\log\mathcal Z=VT^{a+1}f(\mu/T)$. <Strict monotonicity of Fermi gas entropy per particle> makes $\mu/T$ constant at fixed entropy and particle number. Therefore $VT^{a+1}$ and $PV^{(a+2)/(a+1)}$ are constant along a reversible <adiabatic process>.

= Strict monotonicity of Fermi gas entropy per particle
{parent=Fermi gas}

For <density of states> proportional to $\epsilon^a$, $a>-1$, and positive temperature, set $q=\mu/(kT)$, $n(x)=(1+e^{x-q})^{-1}$, $b=a+1$ and $M_j=\int_0^\infty x^{a+j}n(1-n)\,dx$. Differentiation and <integration by parts> give
$$
\frac d{dq}\frac S{kN}=b\left(1-\frac{M_0M_2}{M_1^2}\right)<0.
$$
The inequality is strict <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>, since $x$ is not constant under this positive weight. Thus fixed entropy per particle forces fixed $\mu/T$. This is the injectivity step behind the adiabatic scaling of an ideal <Fermi gas>.

= Fermi energy
{title2=$E_F$}
{c}
{parent=Fermi gas}
{wiki}

The Fermi energy is the highest occupied one-particle <energy> in the <ground state> of a noninteracting <Fermi gas> at zero <temperature>, measured here relative to the lowest one-particle <energy>. The <Pauli exclusion principle> allows only one <fermion> in each complete one-particle <quantum state>, including its <spin>, so adding more particles fills progressively higher <energy eigenstates>. A cold gas can therefore have substantial <kinetic energy> even when its <temperature> is zero. In the <thermodynamic limit>, with the same <energy> reference, $E_F=\mu(T=0)$ where $\mu$ is the <chemical potential>.<Statistical Physics by David Tong/3 Quantum Gases>[Statistical Physics by David Tong; §3.6.2, equation (3.130), zero-temperature filling]{ref}

For a uniform, three-dimensional gas of noninteracting <electrons>, with <spin one-half>, equal populations of the two <spin> states, no applied <magnetic field>, and <nonrelativistic particles> of <mass> $m=m_e$,
$$
n=\frac NV,\qquad
p_F=\hbar(3\pi^2n)^{1/3},\qquad
E_F=\frac{p_F^2}{2m}=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(3\pi^2n)^{2/3},\qquad
T_F=\frac{E_F}{k_B}.
$$
Here $n$ is the total <number density>, $V$ the <volume>, $p_F$ the <Fermi momentum>, $\hbar=h/(2\pi)$ the reduced <Planck constant>, and $k_B$ the <Boltzmann constant>. The <electron Fermi temperature> $T_F$ expresses the same <energy> scale in <kelvin>; it is not the actual gas <temperature>. Strong degeneracy of a <degenerate electron gas> means $T\ll T_F$. The <momentum> formula counts states and survives changing the <energy-momentum relation>, whereas $E_F=p_F^2/(2m)$ requires $p_F\ll mc$.<Degenerate Electron Gases by Richard Fitzpatrick>[Richard Fitzpatrick, Degenerate Electron Gases, equations (506)–(509), two spin states and density dependence]{ref}

The reference matters: the displayed $E_F$ excludes rest <energy> and sets the zero at $p=0$. Including rest <energy> shifts both the one-particle <energy> and the zero-temperature <chemical potential> by $mc^2$, leaving occupations unchanged. More generally, adding a constant $C$ to every one-particle <energy> also adds $C$ to $\mu$; the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> depends only on their difference. In a finite <quantum system> the <chemical potential> at zero <temperature> can lie between the last occupied and first empty <energy eigenstates>; its equality with the filling edge is a continuum statement.

At positive <temperature>, $E_F$ remains the zero-temperature scale associated with the specified <number density>. The <Fermi level> $\mu(T)$ must instead satisfy the particle-number condition
$$
N=\int_0^\infty\frac{g(E)}{\exp[(E-\mu(T))/(k_BT)]+1}\,dE,
$$
where $g(E)$ is the extensive <density of states>, including both <spin> states. Smearing the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> while arbitrarily holding $\mu=E_F$ generally changes $N$. This distinction matters when interpreting <electron heat capacity> and any experiment that infers <temperature> from an occupation profile.<Statistical Physics by David Tong/3 Quantum Gases>[Statistical Physics by David Tong; §§3.6.1–3.6.4, equation (3.127) fixes particle number, and finite-temperature chemical potential is determined separately]{ref}

= Fermi temperature
{c}
{parent=Fermi energy}
{title2=$T_F=E_F/k_B$}

The Fermi temperature expresses a <Fermi energy> as a <temperature> scale, $T_F=E_F/k_B$, where $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant>. In the convention $E_F>0$ measured from the relevant band minimum, $T\ll T_F$ is the regime of a strongly degenerate <Fermi gas>. For an interacting <Fermi liquid>, an effective Fermi scale can be introduced through its quasiparticle dispersion; its convention should be specified.

= Fermi energy of a model electron gas
{parent=Fermi energy}
{c}

Choose $n=10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$ for a uniform noninteracting <electron> model with two equally populated <spin> states. This is an original worked example, not a measured <number density> assigned to an actual material. Using $m_e\simeq9.10938\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$, $\hbar\simeq1.05457\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$, and $k_B=1.380649\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$ gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
k_F&=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}\simeq6.6651\times10^9\,\mathrm{m^{-1}},\\
p_F&=\hbar k_F\simeq7.0288\times10^{-25}\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}},\\
E_F&=\frac{p_F^2}{2m_e}\simeq2.7117\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J\simeq1.6925\,\mathrm{eV},\\
T_F&=\frac{E_F}{k_B}\simeq1.9641\times10^4\,\mathrm K.
\end{aligned}
$$
The conversion uses $1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.602176634\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J$: an <electronvolt> is an <energy> unit, while a <joule> is its <SI> counterpart. The dimensions provide a quick check: $n^{1/3}$ is inverse <length>, $\hbar n^{1/3}$ has units of <momentum>, $p_F^2/m$ has units of <energy>, and $E_F/k_B$ has units of <temperature>. The ratio $p_F/(m_ec)\simeq0.002574$ verifies the <nonrelativistic limit> for this model, where $c$ is the <speed of light>.

Increasing this chosen <number density> eightfold doubles the <Fermi momentum> and quadruples the <Fermi energy>, giving approximately $6.7701\,\mathrm{eV}$. The exponent $2/3$ has a geometric origin: the number of occupied states grows as the cube of a <momentum> radius, but <kinetic energy> of <nonrelativistic particles> grows as its square. This makes <Fermi energy> a useful bridge between a count of particles and a mechanical <energy> scale.

= Electron Fermi temperature
{parent=Fermi energy}
{title2=$T_F$}

The kinetic <electron Fermi temperature> is $T_F=[\sqrt{m_e^2c^4+p_F^2c^2}-m_ec^2]/k_B$, where the <Fermi momentum> $p_F=\hbar(3\pi^2n_e)^{1/3}$ counts the net <electrons>. Strong degeneracy requires $T\ll T_F$. With <mean molecular weight per electron> $\mu_e$ and $n_e=\rho/(\mu_em_u)$, $T_F=(m_ec^2/k_B)[\sqrt{1+(\rho/\rho_*)^{2/3}}-1]$. Its nonrelativistic and ultrarelativistic limits scale as $\rho^{2/3}$ and $\rho^{1/3}$, respectively.

= Fermi momentum
{title2=$p_F$}
{c}
{parent=Fermi energy}

Fermi momentum is the magnitude of the <momentum> at the boundary of filled one-particle states in an isotropic zero-temperature <Fermi gas>. For the uniform three-dimensional two-component <electron> model, a short state count gives
$$
N\simeq 2\,\frac{V}{(2\pi\hbar)^3}\,\frac{4\pi p_F^3}{3},\qquad
n=\frac{p_F^3}{3\pi^2\hbar^3},\qquad
p_F=\hbar(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}.
$$
To see the factors, impose <periodic boundary conditions> on a cubic box of side $L$, so $V=L^3$ and each <momentum> component is $p_i=2\pi\hbar j_i/L$ for an <integer> $j_i$. Each allowed <momentum> point occupies a cell of <volume> $(2\pi\hbar/L)^3$ in <momentum space>. Divide the <sphere> <volume> $4\pi p_F^3/3$ by this cell <volume> and multiply by two for the two <spin> states. Replacing the discrete count with a <volume> is the large-box approximation; surface and shell effects are neglected.<Statistical Physics by David Tong/3 Quantum Gases>[Statistical Physics by David Tong; §3.1, periodic wavevectors and momentum-space integration measure; §3.6.1, spin degeneracy]{ref}

The factor two is not automatic for every <Fermi gas>. If only one internal state is populated, its <number density> $n_\sigma$ instead satisfies $p_{F,\sigma}=\hbar(6\pi^2n_\sigma)^{1/3}$. For two equally populated states, $n_\sigma=n/2$ recovers the displayed result. A <Fermi surface> in a crystal can have a more complicated shape, so a single spherical <Fermi momentum> is a property of this isotropic model.

The <group velocity> of an excitation evaluated on the <Fermi surface>. For an isotropic <dispersion relation> $\epsilon(p)$, $v_F=(d\epsilon/dp)_{p_F}$, where $p_F$ is the <Fermi momentum>.

= Fermi wavevector

The Fermi wavevector is the radius of the occupied <sphere> in <wavevector> coordinates for an isotropic <Fermi gas>. = Fermi wavelength
{parent=Fermi wavevector}

= Fermi sea
{c}
{parent=Fermi energy}
{wiki}

The Fermi sea is the zero-temperature many-<fermion> <quantum state> with every one-particle state below the <Fermi energy> occupied and every state above it empty. For a uniform isotropic <Fermi gas>, the occupied region in <momentum space> is a solid <sphere>; its boundary is the <Fermi surface>. These are <momenta>, not positions: filling the <sphere> does not put the particles in a ball in ordinary space. Opposite <momentum> contributions cancel, so the gas at rest has zero total <momentum> even though its total <kinetic energy> is positive.

The filled states are forced upward in <energy> by the <Pauli exclusion principle>. This is different from the <zero-point energy> of one particle confined by a boundary. At fixed <number density>, the <Fermi energy> remains positive as the box grows to the <thermodynamic limit>, while the lowest box confinement <energy> tends to zero. Low-<energy> excitations move particles from occupied states near the <Fermi surface> into nearby empty states; deep states have no nearby available destination. This explains why only a small fraction of <electrons> contribute to the low-<temperature> <electron heat capacity>.<Statistical Physics by David Tong/3 Quantum Gases>[Statistical Physics by David Tong; §§3.6.2–3.6.3, filled sea and thermally active surface shell]{ref}

= Three-dimensional free-electron density of states
{parent=Fermi sea}
{title2=$g(E)=\frac{V}{2\pi^2}(2m_e/\hbar^2)^{3/2}\sqrt E$}

For the uniform noninteracting <electron> model with two <spin> states and <kinetic energy> of <nonrelativistic particles> $E=p^2/(2m_e)$, count all available one-particle states up to an <energy> $E\geq0$:
$$
\mathcal N(E)=\frac{V}{3\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m_eE}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2},\qquad
g(E)=\frac{d\mathcal N}{dE}=\frac{V}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m_e}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt E.
$$
This extensive <density of states> has units of inverse <energy>; $g(E)/V$ is the <density of states> per unit <volume>. Availability and occupation are different: $g(E)$ is positive above $E_F$, but at zero <temperature> those states are empty. The occupied <energy> distribution is $g(E)f(E)$, with the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> reduced to $f(E)=1$ below $E_F$ and $f(E)=0$ above it. The convention at the exact boundary does not affect a continuum <integral>.

\Image[fermi-sea-density-of-states-occupation.png]
{title=At zero temperature occupation cuts off at the Fermi energy although the density of available states keeps growing}

With the <dimensionless quantity> $x=E/E_F$, the occupied count per $dx$, divided by the total particle number, is $3\sqrt x/2$ for $0<x<1$. Its area is one. Weighting this by $E$ gives the <internal energy> and its <mean> per particle:
$$
N=\int_0^{E_F}g(E)\,dE,\qquad
U=\int_0^{E_F}Eg(E)\,dE=\frac35NE_F,\qquad
\frac UN=\frac35E_F.
$$
Thus the highest occupied <energy> and the <mean> <energy> differ. For the <Fermi energy of a model electron gas>, $U/N\simeq1.0155\,\mathrm{eV}$. At fixed $N$, $E_F\propto V^{-2/3}$, so the <derivative> $P=-(\partial U/\partial V)_N$ gives the <electron degeneracy pressure> $P=2nE_F/5$. The same state-counting geometry therefore predicts a nonzero <pressure> at zero <temperature>.

Sequential <absorption imaging> recorded both trapped clouds on a camera. Fits to axial profiles used the <boson> cloud as a <thermometer>. At common <temperature>, the nearly equal magnetic trapping potentials provided a comparison: the clouds had similar sizes near $T/T_F=1$, but the <fermion> cloud remained broader as cooling reached $T/T_F=0.25$. At zero temperature,

= Electron degeneracy pressure

Electron degeneracy pressure supports dense objects when electrons fill momentum states up to a Fermi surface. In the nonrelativistic limit, a fixed-mass self-gravitating object has the characteristic degenerate scaling $R\propto M^{-1/3}$.

= Ideal Fermi gas bulk modulus
{parent=Electron degeneracy pressure}
{title2=$B=\frac23nE_F$}

For a nonrelativistic three-dimensional ideal electron gas at zero temperature, the pressure is $p=2nE_F/5$ and the electronic <bulk modulus> is $B=2nE_F/3$. This is the electronic model contribution, not automatically the total bulk modulus of a real crystal.

= Degeneracy-pressure bulk modulus
{parent=Electron degeneracy pressure}
{title2=$K=(5/3)P=(2/3)nE_F$}

For a nonrelativistic zero-temperature <free electron model> with fixed particle number, <electron degeneracy pressure> obeys $P\propto(N/V)^{5/3}$. Consequently its <bulk modulus> is $K=-V(\partial P/\partial V)_N=5P/3=2nE_F/3$. At <temperatures> small compared with the <electron Fermi temperature>, the leading <isothermal compressibility> is $1/K$. This electronic contribution alone omits ionic binding and interaction corrections in a real <metal>.

= Nonrelativistic electron-degeneracy pressure in a hydrogen-helium mixture
{parent=Electron degeneracy pressure}
{title2=$P_e=K\rho^{5/3}$}

For completely degenerate, nonrelativistic electrons in fully ionized hydrogen and helium with hydrogen mass fraction $X$, $n_e=(1+X)\rho/(2m_p)$. Filling the spin-degenerate momentum states to $p_F$ gives $n_e=8\pi p_F^3/(3h^3)$ and $P_e=(1/3)\int_0^{p_F}(p^2/m_e)(8\pi p^2/h^3)dp=8\pi p_F^5/(15m_eh^3)$. Eliminating $p_F$ gives $P_e=K\rho^{5/3}$, where
$$
K=\left(\frac3{2\pi}\right)^{2/3}\frac{h^2(1+X)^{5/3}}{40m_em_p^{5/3}}.
$$
It is a <polytropic index> $3/2$ relation. Complete degeneracy suppresses thermal dependence, while the nonrelativistic assumption requires $p_F\ll m_ec$.

= Electron relativistic density threshold
{parent=Electron degeneracy pressure}
{title2=$\rho_*$}

The condition $p_F=m_ec$ gives $\rho_*=[\mu_em_u/(3\pi^2)](m_ec/\hbar)^3\simeq9.74\times10^5\mu_e\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$. Degenerate <electrons> are nonrelativistic for $\rho\ll\rho_*$ and ultrarelativistic for $\rho\gg\rho_*$. This density threshold differs from thermal relativity in a <nondegenerate gas>.

= Equation of state of a cold electron gas
{parent=Electron degeneracy pressure}

For noninteracting electrons at zero temperature, define $x=p_F/(m_ec)$ with <Fermi momentum> $p_F=\hbar(3\pi^2n_e)^{1/3}$. The isotropic momentum flux gives <electron degeneracy pressure>
$$
P=\frac1{3\pi^2\hbar^3}\int_0^{p_F}\frac{p^4c^2}{\sqrt{m_e^2c^4+p^2c^2}}\,dp
=\frac{m_e^4c^5}{24\pi^2\hbar^3}
\left[x(2x^2-3)\sqrt{1+x^2}+3\operatorname{arsinh}x\right].
$$
Its nonrelativistic and ultrarelativistic limits are
$$
P\sim\frac{\hbar^2(3\pi^2)^{2/3}}{5m_e}n_e^{5/3},\qquad
P\sim\frac{\hbar c(3\pi^2)^{1/3}}4n_e^{4/3},
$$
respectively. In a fully ionized stellar gas, $n_e=\rho/(\mu_em_u)$, using <mean molecular weight per electron> $\mu_e$. This neglects finite-temperature effects, interactions, and changes in composition.

= Pauli paramagnetism
{c}
{parent=Fermi gas}
{wiki}

Pauli paramagnetism is the weak positive magnetic response produced when a magnetic field shifts the two spin Fermi surfaces of a degenerate electron gas. At zero temperature, if $g(E_F)$ includes both spin states, its total magnetic moment and susceptibility are
$$
M=\mu_B^2B\,g(E_F),
\qquad
\chi=\mu_B^2g(E_F).
$$

= Fixed-number low-temperature Pauli susceptibility
{parent=Pauli paramagnetism}
{title2=$\chi/(2\mu_B^2)=g_F+\frac{\pi^2}{6}(g_F''-g_F'^2/g_F)(k_BT)^2+O(T^4)$}

For a smooth single-spin <density of states> $g$ near a positive <Fermi energy> and fixed total <particle number>, the <Sommerfeld expansion> gives $\mu=\epsilon_F-(\pi^2/6)(g_F'/g_F)(k_BT)^2+O(T^4)$. The <spin> contribution to the total-moment <magnetic susceptibility>, defined by differentiating with respect to $B$, is $2\mu_B^2\int g(E)[-n'(E)]dE$. Expanding about $\mu$ and including the shift of <chemical potential> gives the displayed formula. The $g_F''$ term is thermal broadening; the $-g_F'^2/g_F$ term enforces fixed <particle number>. A band edge or singular <density of states> within $k_BT$ of $\epsilon_F$ requires a separate analysis. Orbital magnetic response is excluded.

= Pauli moment of a massless electron gas
{c}
{parent=Pauli paramagnetism}
{title2=$\mathcal M=3N\mu^2B/(cp_F)+O(B^3)$}

For noninteracting spin-half particles with dispersion $E=cp$, the two spin populations at common chemical potential $\varepsilon$ are $V(\varepsilon\pm\mu B)^3/(6\pi^2\hbar^3c^3)$. Fixed total number changes $\varepsilon$ only at quadratic order in $B$. Their population difference is therefore $3N\mu B/(cp_F)$ to first order, giving the displayed magnetic moment. Only the specified spin-energy splitting is included, not orbital magnetic effects.

= Two-dimensional free-electron density of states
{parent=Fermi gas}

For nonrelativistic electrons in area $A$, with spin degeneracy $g_s=2$ and energy $E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$, the density of one-particle states is independent of energy:
$$
g(E)=\frac{Am}{\pi\hbar^2}.
$$

= Chemical potential of a two-dimensional ideal Fermi gas
{parent=Two-dimensional free-electron density of states}
{title2=$\mu=\epsilon_F+k_BT\ln(1-e^{-\epsilon_F/(k_BT)})$}

For a constant total <density of states> $D$ above a zero-energy band bottom, integrating the Fermi-Dirac occupation gives $N=Dk_BT\ln(1+e^{\mu/(k_BT)})$. With $\epsilon_F=N/D$, inversion gives the displayed <chemical potential>. It approaches $\epsilon_F$ from below with an exponentially small correction at low <temperature>, rather than the usual three-dimensional quadratic correction. This formula assumes an ideal parabolic band extending to arbitrarily high <energy>.

= Spin normalization in two-dimensional free-electron state counting
{parent=Two-dimensional free-electron density of states}
{title2=$g(\epsilon)=g_sAm/(2\pi\hbar^2)$}

Each allowed <wavevector> occupies area $(2\pi)^2/A$ under <periodic boundary conditions>, for each independent spin state. For <parabolic dispersion>, the number below <energy> $\epsilon$ is $g_sAm\epsilon/(2\pi\hbar^2)$. Thus the often-quoted coefficient $Am/(2\pi\hbar^2)$ is per spin. Unpolarized <electrons> have $g_s=2$; the same convention must be used in particle counting and the <Fermi energy>. A linear Dirac dispersion has a different <energy> dependence.

= Mean energy of a two-dimensional Fermi gas
{parent=Two-dimensional free-electron density of states}
{title2=$U/N=E_F/2$}

A quadratic-dispersion <Fermi gas> in two dimensions has constant <density of states> $g$. At zero <temperature>, $N=gE_F$ and $U=gE_F^2/2$. Hence the mean <energy> is half the <Fermi energy>, independent of a common spin-degeneracy factor.

= Low-temperature particle-number cancellation for constant density of states
{parent=Two-dimensional free-electron density of states}

When the density of states is constant and the chemical potential is fixed at $E_F$, the thermal gain above $E_F$ cancels the thermal depletion below it after the energy range is extended to the whole real line. The finite lower edge leaves only an exponentially small correction of order $e^{-\beta E_F}$.

= Quadratic low-temperature energy correction for constant density of states
{parent=Low-temperature particle-number cancellation for constant density of states}

For constant density of states, weighting the difference between the Fermi-Dirac distribution and the zero-temperature step by $E-E_F$ produces a convergent integral that scales as $\beta^{-2}$. Hence the leading low-temperature energy correction is proportional to $T^2$.

= Linear low-temperature heat capacity of a two-dimensional Fermi gas
{parent=Quadratic low-temperature energy correction for constant density of states}

For a two-dimensional <Fermi gas> of $N$ spin-one-half particles with <Fermi energy> $E_F$, the constant <density of states> makes the low-temperature <internal energy> correction and <heat capacity at constant area>
$$
U(T)-U(0)=\frac{\pi^2N}{6E_F}(k_BT)^2+o(T^2),
\qquad
C_A=\frac{\pi^2Nk_B^2}{3E_F}T+o(T).
$$
Thus its low-temperature heat capacity obeys a <power law> with exponent one.

= Degeneracy pressure of a two-dimensional Fermi gas
{parent=Fermi gas}

At zero temperature, a two-dimensional free Fermi gas satisfies
$$
N=gE_F,
\qquad
E_{\rm tot}=\frac12NE_F,
\qquad
p=-\left(\frac{\partial E_{\rm tot}}{\partial A}\right)_N
=\frac{NE_F}{2A}.
$$

When $E-\mu\gg k_BT$, the additive $\mp1$ is negligible and both quantum distributions reduce to the Maxwell--Boltzmann factor $e^{-(E-\mu)/(k_BT)}$.

= Bose statistics
{c}
{synonym}

<Bose-Einstein statistics> describes identical noninteracting <bosons> in equilibrium. Any occupation number is allowed in a one-particle state; the resulting <Bose-Einstein distribution> is $n(E)=[e^{\beta(E-\mu)}-1]^{-1}$.

= Bose gas
{c}
{parent=Bose-Einstein statistics}

A gas whose indistinguishable constituents are <bosons>. The ideal model follows <Bose-Einstein statistics>, while interactions modify its excitations and thermodynamics. Macroscopic ground-state occupation can occur under suitable dimension and density conditions, but it does not alone determine a superfluid response.

= Bose-Einstein distribution
{parent=Bose-Einstein statistics}
{c}

The mean occupation of a bosonic one-particle state of energy $E$ is
$$
n(E)=\frac1{e^{\beta(E-\mu)}-1},
\qquad \beta=(k_BT)^{-1}.
$$

= Low-temperature energy of gapless bosonic excitations
{parent=Bose-Einstein distribution}
{title2=$\epsilon(k)\propto k^s\ \Longrightarrow\ U-U_0\propto T^{1+d/s}$}

In $d$ dimensions, isotropic modes with dispersion $\epsilon=A k^s$ have <density of states> proportional to $\epsilon^{d/s-1}$. Their equilibrium number is not conserved, so the excitation <chemical potential> is zero. Integrating $\epsilon D(\epsilon)/(e^{\epsilon/(k_BT)}-1)$ and rescaling by $k_BT$ gives the displayed energy power and <heat capacity> proportional to $T^{d/s}$. In three dimensions, linear acoustic <phonons> give $T^4$ energy and $T^3$ heat capacity; quadratic gapless ferromagnetic <magnons> give $T^{5/2}$ energy and $T^{3/2}$ heat capacity. A gap changes the asymptotically low-temperature law to activated behaviour. The mode approximation applies below the relevant band cutoff and when interactions between excitations are negligible.

= Quadratic-boson heat-capacity coefficient
{parent=Low-temperature energy of gapless bosonic excitations}
{title2=$C/V=5\Gamma(5/2)\zeta(5/2)k_B^{5/2}T^{3/2}/(8\pi^2\hbar^3\alpha^{3/2})$}

One isotropic three-dimensional bosonic branch with dispersion $\epsilon=\alpha p^2$ and zero <chemical potential> has <density of states> per <volume> $\sqrt\epsilon/(4\pi^2\hbar^3\alpha^{3/2})$. Integrating its Bose-Einstein <energy> and differentiating <temperature> gives the title coefficient. Identical independent branches multiply it by their number. Thus a measured $T^{3/2}$ coefficient fixes branch multiplicity divided by $\alpha^{3/2}$, not a dispersion coefficient independent of that counting convention. A gap or low-dimensional spectrum changes the asymptotic law.

= Mean photon energy
{parent=Bose-Einstein distribution}
{title2=$\langle E_\gamma\rangle=\frac{\pi^4}{30\zeta(3)}T\simeq2.701T$}

The number and energy integrals of a zero-chemical-potential <photon> gas give $n=2\zeta(3)T^3/\pi^2$ and $\rho=\pi^2T^4/15$ in <natural units>. Their ratio is the mean energy per <photon>. It is useful for converting a relic number-density ratio into an energy-density ratio.

= Bose-Einstein condensation
{c}
{parent=Bose-Einstein distribution}
{wiki}

Bose-Einstein condensation is the macroscopic occupation of the lowest one-particle state by bosons below a critical temperature.

= Two-dimensional harmonic-trap Bose condensation
{parent=Bose-Einstein condensation}
{title2=$k_BT_c=\hbar\omega\sqrt{6N}/\pi$}

For an ideal <Bose gas> in a two-dimensional isotropic harmonic trap, the leading <density of states> measured above its <ground state> is $D(E)=E/(\hbar\omega)^2$. At saturation of the <Bose-Einstein distribution>, $N_{\mathrm{ex}}=(k_BT/(\hbar\omega))^2\int_0^\infty u/(e^u-1)\,du=(\pi^2/6)(k_BT/(\hbar\omega))^2$. The finite excited-state capacity gives the displayed <critical temperature> and <condensate fraction> $N_0/N=1-(T/T_c)^2$ below it. The appropriate trapped-gas <thermodynamic limit> fixes $N\omega^2$ as $N\to\infty$ and $\omega\to0$; a finite trap exhibits <finite-size rounding>. The <number density> is inhomogeneous, and the physical <chemical potential> approaches the oscillator <ground-state> <energy>, not an unshifted zero by convention.

= Bose condensation temperature from a fermionic density scale
{c}
{parent=Bose-Einstein condensation}

Compare uniform three-dimensional <ideal gases> at the same <number density> n, with <fermion> <mass> $m_F$, <spin degeneracy> $g_F$, <boson> <mass> $m_B$ and a single <boson> <spin> component. The <Fermi temperature> has $k_BT_F=\hbar^2(6\pi^2n/g_F)^{2/3}/(2m_F)$, while Bose saturation gives $k_BT_B=2\pi\hbar^2[n/\zeta(3/2)]^{2/3}/m_B$. Eliminating <density> yields
$$
\frac{T_B}{T_F}=\frac{4\pi}{(6\pi^2/g_F)^{2/3}\zeta(3/2)^{2/3}}\frac{m_F}{m_B}.
$$
The degeneracy is essential; using a spinless Fermi sea changes the numerical estimate. This is an ideal-gas comparison and does not remove interaction effects in a dense liquid.

= Bose-Einstein condensation temperature for an anisotropic effective mass
{parent=Bose-Einstein condensation}
{c}
{title2=$k_BT_c=2\pi\hbar^2[n/(\sqrt\gamma\zeta(3/2))]^{2/3}/m$}

For a one-component ideal Bose gas with masses $(m,m,\gamma m)$, insert the <anisotropic ideal-gas density of states> in the excited-state number integral at chemical potential zero. The substitution $x=\varepsilon/(k_BT)$ gives $n=\sqrt\gamma(mk_BT/(2\pi\hbar^2))^{3/2}\zeta(3/2)$, which yields the displayed transition temperature. Thus at fixed density and the two lighter masses, $T_c\propto\gamma^{-1/3}$. This continuum ideal-gas result does not supply a finite-temperature transition for decoupled two-dimensional layers.

= Random-loss cooling of a Bose-Einstein condensate
{parent=Bose-Einstein condensation}
{title2=$T\propto N^{2/5}$}

For a partially condensed equilibrium ideal <Bose gas> in a fixed three-dimensional box, the <energy> scales as $T^{5/2}$. Unbiased random particle removal keeps mean <energy> per particle fixed, so re-equilibration gives $T\propto N^{2/5}$ until condensation disappears. Removal of an excited particle can be followed by partial replenishment from the zero-energy condensate. This differs from selective <evaporative cooling of atoms>, which preferentially removes high-energy atoms and reduces mean <energy> per particle.

= Saturation particle number for Bose-Einstein condensation
{parent=Bose-Einstein condensation}
{title2=$N_c(T)=\int_0^\infty g(\epsilon)/(e^{\epsilon/(k_BT)}-1)\,d\epsilon$}

With the ground-state <energy> set to zero, the <chemical potential> of an ideal <Bose gas> cannot exceed zero. The limiting population of excited states as it approaches zero is the saturation <particle number>, provided the integral converges. Additional particles occupy the ground state macroscopically. For a three-dimensional uniform box, $N_c=\zeta(3/2)V/\lambda^3$ with <thermal wavelength> $\lambda=h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}$.

= Field-induced triplon condensation
{parent=Bose-Einstein condensation}
{title2=$\eta(H_c-H_g)=\nu n$}

A <magnetic field> can close the lowest gap of a dilute bosonic triplet branch. In a shifted-parabola model with actual <chemical potential> zero, $\epsilon_k=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)+\nu n-\eta(H-H_g)$ and repulsion $\nu>0$ prevent indefinite <density> growth. The normal minimum reaches zero when $\eta(H_c-H_g)=\nu n$. At that point the finite thermal excited population saturates and macroscopic ground-state occupation can develop.

= Condensate density in a shifted-parabola triplon model
{parent=Field-induced triplon condensation}
{title2=$n_0=(\eta/\nu)[H-H_c(T)]$}

If the phenomenological single-density shift is continued into the condensed <phase> and the thermal population remains saturated at $n_T(T)$, the zero minimum requires $\nu[n_0+n_T]=\eta(H-H_g)$. At fixed <temperature>, $n_0=(\eta/\nu)[H-H_c(T)]$ above onset and zero below it. This prefactor belongs to that closure; a microscopic interaction model can distinguish normal and condensed self-energies. Continuing the normal <parabola> into negative energies without changing the state is not a consistent Bose distribution.

= Triplon condensation field scaling
{parent=Field-induced triplon condensation}
{title2=$H_c-H_g\propto T^{3/2}$}

For a three-dimensional quadratic branch, the critical thermal <density> is $n_T=\zeta(3/2)[mk_BT/(2\pi\hbar^2)]^{3/2}$. Inserting this into the mean-field gap-closing condition gives $H_c(T)-H_g=(\nu/\eta)n_T\propto T^{3/2}$. This is a dilute low-temperature model; finite bandwidth, anisotropy and changes in interactions can alter its applicability outside that regime.

= Homogeneous ideal Bose gas condensation
{parent=Bose-Einstein condensation}
{title2=$T_c=2\pi\hbar^2[n/\zeta(3/2)]^{2/3}/(mk_B)$}

For a uniform three-dimensional ideal gas of spinless <bosons> with energy $p^2/(2m)$ and ground energy set to zero, the excited number density is $n_{\rm ex}=\lambda_T^{-3}\operatorname{Li}_{3/2}(z)$, with <quantum gas fugacity> $z=e^{\mu/(k_BT)}\leq1$. Its finite maximum $\zeta(3/2)/\lambda_T^3$ gives the displayed <critical temperature>. Below it, particle conservation gives <condensate fraction> $N_0/N=1-(T/T_c)^{3/2}$. The excited-state <internal energy> is $U=(3/2)Vk_BT\lambda_T^{-3}\operatorname{Li}_{5/2}(z)$, with $z\to1$ below the transition. The exponent differs from that of a harmonic trap. Condensation is not by itself a demonstration of <superfluidity>; the ideal quadratic dispersion has vanishing Landau critical speed.

= Ideal Bose-gas heat capacity below condensation
{parent=Homogeneous ideal Bose gas condensation}
{title2=$C_V=\frac{15}{4}Nk_B\frac{\zeta(5/2)}{\zeta(3/2)}(T/T_c)^{3/2}$}

For quadratic three-dimensional dispersion below condensation, the excited-state thermal <energy> is proportional to $T^{5/2}$. Differentiating at fixed particle number and volume gives the displayed <heat capacity>, with limiting value approximately $1.926Nk_B$ at $T_c^-$. The macroscopic ground occupation carries no excitation <energy> in the shifted reference. The exponent differs from the linear-phonon result of an interacting gas.

= Finite-box ground-energy convention in Bose condensation
{parent=Homogeneous ideal Bose gas condensation}
{title2=$\mu\to E_0^-$}

A hard-wall cube has positive one-particle ground <energy> $E_0=3\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)$. Condensation corresponds to the absolute <chemical potential> approaching $E_0$, not zero. Shifting the <energy> reference makes the limiting <chemical potential> zero, while the exact shifted excited-state <density of states> also shifts. Keeping its bulk square-root form is a large-system approximation valid when $k_BT\gg E_0$. A periodic cube instead has zero ground <energy>.

= Particle saturation in a homogeneous Bose gas
{parent=Homogeneous ideal Bose gas condensation}
{title2=$N_c(T)=V\zeta(3/2)/\lambda_T^3$}

At fixed <temperature>, the continuum excited population of a three-dimensional ideal <Bose gas> reaches a finite maximum as the <chemical potential> approaches the ground <energy>. Adding particles beyond that maximum creates macroscopic ground occupation. Thus $N'=\min(N,N_c)$ and $N_0=\max(0,N-N_c)$ to leading thermodynamic-limit order. Finite systems retain nonmacroscopic ground occupation below the nominal threshold.

= Dilute-gas Bose-Einstein condensation
{parent=Bose-Einstein condensation}

In a dilute gas, <Bose-Einstein condensation> makes a single <one-particle state> hold a substantial fraction of the <bosons>. This is a population effect: cooling reduces the number that the excited <energy eigenstates> can accommodate at <thermal equilibrium>. The surplus accumulates in a <Bose-Einstein condensate>. For an <ideal gas> the occupied mode is the trap <ground state>; interactions can deform that mode, as described by the <Gross–Pitaevskii equation>. Dilute does not mean classical: a small <number density> can still give overlapping <thermal de Broglie wavelengths> at sufficiently low <temperature>.

= Bose condensation in a three-dimensional harmonic trap
{c}
{parent=Dilute-gas Bose-Einstein condensation}
{title2=$k_BT_c=\hbar\bar\omega[N/\zeta(3)]^{1/3}$}

Consider $N$ noninteracting <bosons> in one internal <quantum state>, at <thermal equilibrium> in the three-dimensional <harmonic oscillator> potential
$$
V(\mathbf r)=\frac m2(\omega_x^2x^2+\omega_y^2y^2+\omega_z^2z^2),\qquad
\bar\omega=(\omega_x\omega_y\omega_z)^{1/3}.
$$
Here $m$ is the <mass>, the positive $\omega_i$ are <angular frequencies>, and $\bar\omega$ is their <geometric mean>. Measure the one-particle <energy> $\epsilon$ and <chemical potential> $\mu$ from the <ground-state energy>. The <quantum harmonic oscillator> levels then have $\epsilon=\hbar(\omega_x n_x+\omega_y n_y+\omega_z n_z)$ with nonnegative integer $n_i$ and reduced <Planck constant> $\hbar=h/(2\pi)$.

When $\epsilon\gg\hbar\omega_i$, counting <lattice points> can be approximated by the volume of a <tetrahedron> in the positive octant of $(n_x,n_y,n_z)$:
$$
\mathcal N(\epsilon)\simeq\frac{\epsilon^3}{6\hbar^3\omega_x\omega_y\omega_z},\qquad
g(\epsilon)=\frac{d\mathcal N}{d\epsilon}\simeq\frac{\epsilon^2}{2(\hbar\bar\omega)^3}.
$$
This is a <density of states>, with units of inverse <energy>. The factor $1/6$ is the unit tetrahedron volume; differentiating produces the factor $1/2$. Treat the single <ground state> separately: its macroscopic population would be lost by replacing every level with a smooth <density of states>.

For $k_BT\gg\hbar\omega_i$, integrate the <Bose-Einstein distribution> over excited <energy eigenstates>. As $\mu$ approaches zero from below, their maximum population is
$$
N_{\rm ex}^{\rm sat}(T)=\int_0^\infty\frac{g(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon}{e^{\epsilon/(k_BT)}-1}
=\zeta(3)\left(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar\bar\omega}\right)^3.
$$
Here $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant> and $\zeta$ the <Riemann zeta function>. A short proof uses $1/(e^x-1)=\sum_{\ell\ge1}e^{-\ell x}$ and $\int_0^\infty x^2e^{-\ell x}dx=2/\ell^3$. The nonnegative terms permit exchanging <summation> and <integration> by <Tonelli's theorem>, giving $\int_0^\infty x^2/(e^x-1)dx=2\zeta(3)$. Near zero the integrand is proportional to $x$, so its integral converges. That finite excited-state capacity is the mechanism of <Bose-Einstein condensation>.

Set $N_{\rm ex}^{\rm sat}(T_c)=N$. Particle conservation gives, to this continuum accuracy,
$$
k_BT_c=\hbar\bar\omega\left(\frac{N}{\zeta(3)}\right)^{1/3},\qquad
\frac{N_0}{N}=1-\left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^3\quad(0\le T\le T_c).
$$
Above $T_c$, the macroscopic <condensate fraction> is zero and the <chemical potential> adjusts below zero. This cubic power belongs to a three-dimensional harmonic trap; it is not the homogeneous three-dimensional <ideal gas> exponent. For $N=10^5$, $\bar\omega/(2\pi)=100\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and $\zeta(3)\simeq1.202056903$, the model gives $T_c\simeq209.5\,\mathrm{nK}$. At $T=0.8T_c\simeq167.6\,\mathrm{nK}$, $N_0/N=0.488$, or approximately $48\,800$ condensed <bosons>. These are chosen parameters and calculated values.

Finite $N$ gives a smooth crossover rather than a sharp <phase transition>. The exact <ideal gas> sum over excited <quantum harmonic oscillator> levels differs from the continuum integral, especially when $k_BT$ is not large relative to every $\hbar\omega_i$. A sharp trapped-gas limit takes $N\to\infty$, $\bar\omega\to0$ with $N\bar\omega^3$ fixed and fixed frequency ratios. Interactions also alter the <critical temperature>, <condensate fraction> and release profile; a dilute gas can have appreciable interaction <energy> compared with the trap level spacing. The cubic law is a useful baseline, not a fit guaranteed for either 1995 experiment.

= Condensation, matter-wave coherence and superfluidity
{parent=Dilute-gas Bose-Einstein condensation}

<Bose-Einstein condensation> concerns occupation of a <one-particle state>. <Matter-wave coherence> concerns field correlations between positions or times. <Superfluidity> concerns a flow response, including suppression of dissipation under appropriate conditions. A density peak diagnoses neither a relative <quantum phase> between two separately prepared clouds nor a critical flow speed. These observables require different experiments.

For example, in a single-mode fixed-number <Fock state>, $\langle\hat a\rangle=0$ but $\langle\hat a^\dagger\hat a\rangle=N$. Writing $\hat\psi(\mathbf r)=\phi(\mathbf r)\hat a$ for that occupied mode gives
$$
\langle\hat\psi^\dagger(\mathbf r)\hat\psi(\mathbf r')\rangle
=N\phi^*(\mathbf r)\phi(\mathbf r').
$$
Thus vanishing mean field does not imply vanishing first-order <matter-wave coherence>. For two independent fixed-number modes, $\langle\hat a^\dagger\hat b\rangle=0$: there is no ensemble mean interference term establishing a fixed relative <quantum phase>. Interpreting fringes in an individual detection record requires considering correlations and the measurement; assigning a pre-existing definite relative <quantum phase> is unnecessary.

<Wolfgang Ketterle>'s lecture describes a separate coherence test: two condensates expanded into one another and produced fringes. Selectively removing one condensate made the fringes disappear on alternating preparation cycles, testing against diffraction from the confinement. This compared two-cloud and one-cloud signals; it did not measure dissipationless flow.<Nobel lecture: When atoms behave as waves: Bose-Einstein condensation and the atom laser by Wolfgang Ketterle>[pp. 1143–1145, Fig. 15, condensate-elimination control]{ref}

= Bose condensation with power-law dispersion
{c}
{parent=Bose-Einstein condensation}

For a homogeneous ideal gas in $d$ dimensions with $A,\alpha>0$, the <density of states> scales as $\epsilon^{d/\alpha-1}$. At limiting chemical potential zero, the <Bose-Einstein distribution> is proportional to $1/\epsilon$ near zero energy. The excited-particle integral is finite exactly when $d/\alpha>1$. Thus positive-temperature condensation at fixed nonzero density is possible precisely for $0<\alpha<d$. = Bose-Einstein condensate
{c}
{parent=Bose-Einstein condensation}

A Bose-Einstein condensate is a state with macroscopic occupation of a one-particle mode. A phase-selected mean-field description can use $\langle\psi\rangle\ne0$, while a fixed-number finite-volume state need not have a nonzero field expectation. <Bose-Einstein condensation> and <superfluidity> are related but distinct notions; long-wavelength phase fluctuations can invalidate a uniform condensate saddle in low dimensions.

= Two-species uniform boson number state
{parent=Bose-Einstein condensate}
{title2=$|G\rangle=(A_0^\dagger)^{n_A}(B_0^\dagger)^{n_B}|0\rangle/\sqrt{n_A!n_B!}$}

Two independent <boson> species occupy uniform single-particle modes on $L$ sites, with fixed <occupation numbers> $n_A,n_B$. This differs from a <fixed-number two-mode condensate> whose particles all occupy one superposition of the two modes. The latter follows from two independent <binomial distributions> with success probability $1/L$. = Fixed-number two-mode condensate
{parent=Bose-Einstein condensate}

All $N$ <bosons> occupy the same normalized one-particle superposition with $|C|^2+|S|^2=1$. = Phase derivative of a two-mode condensate energy
{parent=Fixed-number two-mode condensate}
{title2=$\partial_\chi E=i\langle[H,n_R]\rangle=-i\langle[H,n_L]\rangle$}

For $|\chi\rangle=(c a_L^\dagger+e^{i\chi}s a_R^\dagger)^N|0\rangle/\sqrt{N!}$ with real $c,s$ and $c^2+s^2=1$, one has $|\chi\rangle=e^{i\chi n_R}|0_{\rm phase}\rangle$. = Condensate density
{parent=Bose-Einstein condensate}
{title2=$n_0=N_0/V$}

For a homogeneous gas, the condensate density is the macroscopic occupation $N_0$ of its condensate mode divided by volume. More generally its spatial density is $N_0|\varphi_0(x)|^2$ for a normalized condensate orbital. The mode and occupation are defined by a macroscopic eigenvalue of the one-body density matrix, so a finite fixed-number state need not have a nonzero field expectation. Condensate density is distinct from the superfluid density defined by response to a phase twist; interactions can deplete the former even when the latter equals the total density at zero temperature in a uniform Galilean-invariant gas.

Its <angular momentum> per atom is $n\hbar$, while the flow contribution to its total <chemical potential> is $\hbar^2n^2/(2mR^2)$.

= Finite-size condensate chemical potential
{parent=Bose-Einstein condensate}

For a noninteracting <ground state> of <energy> zero, $N_0=(e^{-\beta\mu}-1)^{-1}$ gives $\mu=-k_BT\ln(1+1/N_0)$. Thus a finite condensate has a slightly negative <chemical potential>; exact zero pinning is a macroscopic limiting statement.

= Condensate depletion
{parent=Bose-Einstein condensate}
{title2=$n-n_0$}

Occupation outside the macroscopically occupied condensate mode, caused by interactions or thermal excitations. A weak homogeneous three-dimensional Bose gas has zero-temperature depletion fraction $8\sqrt{na_s^3}/(3\sqrt\pi)$ in the leading dilute-gas approximation. Depletion measures single-particle mode occupation, not directly the <superfluid fraction>.

= Two-component Bose-Einstein condensate
{parent=Bose-Einstein condensate}
{title2=$\psi_1,\psi_2$}

Two separately conserved bosonic species have two condensate fields and independent <chemical potentials>. = Bogoliubov theory
{c}
{parent=Weakly interacting Bose gas}

Expand an interacting Bose field about a macroscopically occupied condensate and retain terms quadratic in its fluctuations. A <Bogoliubov transformation> diagonalizes the stable quadratic Hamiltonian. For contact repulsion this gives the <Bogoliubov quasiparticle dispersion> $E_k=\sqrt{\epsilon_k(\epsilon_k+2g_Bn_0)}$, a linear sound mode at small <momentum> and particle-like behavior at large <momentum>. Mixing creation and <annihilation operators> also produces <quantum depletion of a Bose condensate>. The approximation requires controlled fluctuations; it does not give an exact strongly interacting liquid or the full fluctuation-dominated critical region.

= Fixed-mean-number Bogoliubov variational energy
{parent=Bogoliubov theory}
{title2=$\mathcal E=\frac{gN}{2}+\sum_{k\ne0}[(\epsilon_k+g)\sinh^2\theta_k-g\sinh\theta_k\cosh\theta_k]$}

For a weakly depleted homogeneous <Bose gas> with repulsive <contact interaction>, set $g=uN/V$ and $\epsilon_k=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$. A zero-mode <coherent state> times a <two-mode squeezed vacuum> for each opposite-<momentum> pair has mean <particle number> $N=N_0+\sum_{k\ne0}\sinh^2\theta_k$. Substitution of $N_0=N-\sum\sinh^2\theta_k$ into the leading condensate <interaction energy>, followed by a consistent quadratic truncation, gives the displayed variational <energy>. Omitting this substitution loses the subtraction embodied in the <number-conserving Bogoliubov quadratic Hamiltonian>.

For each mode set $A_k=\epsilon_k+g$ and $E_k=\sqrt{A_k^2-g^2}$. If $A_k>|g|$, choose $\tanh(2\theta_k^*)=g/A_k$. The mode contribution equals $\tfrac12E_k\cosh[2(\theta_k-\theta_k^*)]-A_k/2$, proving a unique minimum $(E_k-A_k)/2$. At fixed <ultraviolet cutoff> this lowers the <energy> relative to the uncorrelated condensate. The approximation requires small <quantum depletion of a Bose condensate>; an unregulated three-dimensional <ground-state energy> also requires <renormalization of a three-dimensional dilute-gas contact coupling>.

= Condensate fluctuation power counting

Expand a weakly depleted <Bose gas> around its macroscopically occupied mode. = Quadratic condensate fluctuation Lagrangian

= Grand-canonical Hamiltonian of a Bose field
{parent=Weakly interacting Bose gas}
{title2=$K=H-\mu N$}

For a repulsive contact-interacting <Bose-Einstein condensate>, the classical <Hamiltonian density> generating evolution in a frame rotating at the <chemical potential> is $\mathcal K=\hbar^2|\nabla\psi|^2/(2M)-\mu|\psi|^2+g|\psi|^4/2$. {parent=Weakly interacting Bose gas}
{title2=$\psi=\sqrt\rho e^{i\phi}$}

after removing the periodic total density <derivative>. The potential has positive-density minimum $\rho_0=\mu/g$ for $\mu>0$. Multiply the equation by $\psi^*$ and subtract its <complex conjugate> multiplied by $\psi$ to obtain this identity; real interaction and potential terms cancel. An optical phonon is a quantum of vibration on an <optical phonon branch>, involving relative motion of atoms within a primitive cell. Its zone-center frequency can remain nonzero. Some such modes couple strongly to light when the relative displacement changes electric polarization; the name does not imply that every optical phonon is optically active. Their finite energies produce thresholds for electron emission and thermally suppressed absorption at low temperature.

= Reststrahlen effect
{parent=Optical phonon}

= Reststrahlen band
{synonym}

In a polar insulating crystal, a band with negative real <permittivity> can occur between the transverse and longitudinal optical resonances. A lossless bulk transverse wave is evanescent there, producing high normal-incidence <reflectance>. Damping rounds the edges and permits absorption. The reflection minimum beyond the band can occur where the refractive index approaches one; it is not exactly the zero of permittivity.

= Rattling mode
{parent=Optical phonon}
{title2=$C_E=gR\,x^2e^x/(e^x-1)^2,\quad x=\hbar\Omega/(k_BT)$}

A rattling mode is a relatively soft, often localized vibration of a guest atom within a more rigid surrounding cage. An approximately harmonic narrow-frequency contribution can be modeled as an <Einstein solid> term with frequency $\Omega$ and degeneracy $g$, giving the heat capacity in the title. It freezes out exponentially at low temperature and approaches $gR$ per mole at high temperature, so it can bend a plot of $C/T$ versus $T^2$ away from its pure electronic-plus-acoustic straight line.

Calorimetry of <Ba4Na2Ge25> shows an additional low-energy contribution fitted by an Einstein energy $\hbar\Omega/k_B\simeq32\ \mathrm K$, assigned to a barium guest site.<Superconductivity in the Filled Cage Compounds Ba6Ge25 and Ba4Na2Ge25 by Friedrich Malte Grosche and others>[Page 247003-3, Figure 3]{ref} This is a measured-material example; the narrow-mode description remains an approximation to a crystal's full vibrational spectrum.

= Acoustic phonon
{parent=Phonon}
{title2=$\omega_{\boldsymbol q}\to0\text{ as }q\to0$}

An acoustic phonon is a quantum of vibration on an <acoustic phonon branch>. At long wavelengths the atoms in each primitive cell move together and translational invariance makes the frequency approach zero. A three-dimensional crystal has three acoustic branches, corresponding to longitudinal and transverse polarizations. A linear small-wavevector dispersion has slope equal to the appropriate <sound speed>.

= Electron-phonon interaction
{parent=Phonon}

The <electron-phonon interaction> couples electronic states to the atomic displacements represented by <phonons>. A lattice vibration changes the potential seen by an <electron>; its quantized displacement permits phonon absorption and emission. This interaction affects <electrical resistance>, electronic <thermal conductivity>, lattice dynamics and pairing in conventional superconductors.

= Phonon-mediated electron attraction
{parent=Electron-phonon interaction}
{title2=$V_{\rm eff}$}

For <electron> <energy> transfer $\Delta$ and <phonon> <energy> $\Omega_q=\hbar\omega_q$, the symmetrized pair amplitude is $V_{\rm eff}=2|M_q|^2\Omega_q/(\Delta^2-\Omega_q^2)$. It is attractive for $|\Delta|<\Omega_q$. This interaction is retarded, and screened Coulomb repulsion must also be considered.

= Fröhlich Hamiltonian
{parent=Electron-phonon interaction}
{c}

A second-quantized electron-phonon Hamiltonian with free <electrons>, harmonic <phonons> and terms linear in <phonon> displacement, $M_qc_{k+q,\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{k,\sigma}(b_q+b_{-q}^{\dagger})$. The name is used for both general linear electron-phonon models and the more specific long-range coupling to polar optical modes; the intended dispersion and <matrix element> must be stated.

= Fröhlich canonical transformation
{parent=Fröhlich Hamiltonian}
{c}

An <anti-Hermitian> generator $S$ is chosen so $[S,H_0]=-H_{e\text{-}ph}$. Expanding $e^SH e^{-S}$ leaves $H_0+[S,H_{e\text{-}ph}]/2$ to second order. Eliminating virtual <phonons> produces an effective electron-electron interaction, plus <energy> shifts. Resonant denominators and higher orders limit this perturbative elimination.

= Kohn anomaly
{c}
{parent=Electron-phonon interaction}
{wiki}

A feature in phonon dispersion caused by a nonanalytic wavevector dependence of electronic screening. In a quasi-one-dimensional conductor, enhanced response near $2k_F$ can soften a lattice mode and signal an approaching <Peierls transition>.

= Peierls transition
{c}
{parent=Electron-phonon interaction}
{wiki}

A <Peierls transition>, named for <Rudolf Peierls>, is a <metal-insulator transition> caused by a <Peierls distortion> of an initially metallic chain.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology, fifth edition (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry/Peierls transition>[Entry P04468: electronic transition caused by a structural distortion and opening a gap at the Fermi energy]{ref} A modulation at $2k_F$ mixes states near opposite <Fermi points> and can open a <band gap> at the <Fermi energy>. At half filling with one <atomic orbital> per <chemical atom>, this modulation doubles the original lattice period and alternates strong and weak bonds. In a weak-coupling zero-temperature description, the <Peierls energy gain> is proportional to $-(\delta^2/t)\ln(t/|\delta|)$ whereas the elastic cost is proportional to $\delta^2$, where $t>0$ is the undistorted hopping strength and $\delta$ its small alternating change. The logarithm permits a distortion at arbitrarily weak coupling in the ideal model. Finite <temperature>, finite size, interchain coupling and <thermal fluctuations> matter for a real transition; strictly one-dimensional reasoning alone does not establish finite-temperature long-range order.

= Inelastic neutron scattering of TTF-TCNQ
{parent=Peierls transition}

The experiment follows phonon peaks as both momentum and energy transfer are varied. In <inelastic neutron scattering>, $\mathbf q=\mathbf k_i-\mathbf k_f$ locates the lattice modulation and $\hbar\omega=E_i-E_f$ measures the mode energy, so a softened mode can be distinguished from static diffraction alone. Measurements on deuterated <TTF-TCNQ> between $60$ and $300\,\mathrm K$ found a growing <Kohn anomaly> near $2k_F$ below about $150\,\mathrm K$ as the $54\,\mathrm K$ transition was approached.<Phonon dispersion and Kohn anomaly in TTF-TCNQ by Gen Shirane and others>[Abstract: measurement method, temperature interval and wavevector-dependent anomaly]{ref}

= Peierls distortion
{parent=Peierls transition}
{c}

A <Peierls distortion> modulates the atomic positions and <chemical bonds> of a one-dimensional chain with a partly filled <energy band>. In the half-filled example, alternating short and long bonds double the <primitive unit cell>. The distortion is the structural change; the associated <Peierls transition> is the change in electronic conduction.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology, fifth edition (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry/Peierls distortion>[Entry PT07442: bond alternation caused by distortion of a partially occupied one-dimensional structure]{ref}

= Dimerized tight-binding chain
{parent=Peierls distortion}
{title2=$E_\pm(k)=\pm2\sqrt{t^2\cos^2(ka)+\delta^2\sin^2(ka)}$}

For spin-degenerate <electrons> at atomic spacing $a$, alternate the hopping amplitudes $-(t+\delta)$ and $-(t-\delta)$, with $t>0$ and $|\delta|<t$. The doubled <primitive unit cell> gives an off-diagonal <Bloch Hamiltonian> entry $-(t+\delta)-(t-\delta)e^{-2ika}$. Its two <eigenvalues> are the displayed <energy bands>, for $|k|\leq\pi/(2a)$. The <band gap> at the edge of the <Brillouin zone> is $4|\delta|$. At half filling there are two <electrons> per doubled <primitive unit cell>, so the lower band is full and the upper empty. This explicit model realizes the period-doubling form of a <Peierls distortion>.

= Logarithmic energy gain from a Peierls distortion
{parent=Dimerized tight-binding chain}
{title2=$\Delta\mathcal E=-\frac{4\delta^2}{\pi t}\log\frac{t}{|\delta|}+O(\delta^2/t)$}

= Peierls energy gain
{synonym}
{c}

Fill the lower <energy band> of the <dimerized tight-binding chain> with both <spin> states. Take zero on-site <energy> and measure <ground-state energy> per doubled <primitive unit cell>. <State counting in a Bloch band> and $q=ka$ give
$$
\mathcal E(\delta)=-\frac8\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}\sqrt{t^2\cos^2q+\delta^2\sin^2q}\,dq,\qquad \mathcal E(0)=-\frac{8t}\pi.
$$
Equivalently, for $|\delta|\leq t$, $\mathcal E(\delta)=-(8t/\pi)E(\sqrt{1-\delta^2/t^2})$, where $E(k)$ is the <complete elliptic integral of the second kind> in the modulus convention.

For fixed $t>0$ and $|\delta|/t\to0$, the change is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E(\delta)-\mathcal E(0)=-\frac{4\delta^2}{\pi t}\log\frac{t}{|\delta|}+O(\delta^2/t).}
$$
The <logarithm> comes from states near the edge of the <Brillouin zone>. Put $u=\pi/2-q$. To leading order the square root there is $\sqrt{t^2u^2+\delta^2}$. For a fixed small cutoff $\eta>0$, direct integration gives
$$
\int_0^\eta\left[\sqrt{t^2u^2+\delta^2}-tu\right]du=\frac{\delta^2}{2t}\log\frac{t}{|\delta|}+O(\delta^2/t).
$$
The rest of the band, and the higher-order corrections near its edge, contribute only $O(\delta^2/t)$; multiplying by $-8/\pi$ gives the displayed <asymptotic expansion>. Here the remainder uses <Big O notation>. The negative logarithmic term exceeds any fixed quadratic elastic cost $K\delta^2$, $K>0$, for sufficiently small nonzero $\delta$. This explains the zero-<temperature> instability to a <Peierls distortion> in the ideal chain model.

= Electron-phonon scattering
{parent=Electron-phonon interaction}

In <electron-phonon scattering>, an <electron> changes its energy by $\pm\hbar\omega_{\mathbf q}$ while its <crystal momentum> changes by a phonon wavevector modulo a <reciprocal lattice> vector. Charge-transport relaxation weights a scattering angle $\theta$ by $1-\cos\theta$. Small-angle events can therefore relax heat efficiently while relaxing electrical current weakly. At temperatures above the phonon excitation scale, scattering becomes approximately elastic in the electron thermal shell; residual elastic disorder often dominates at very low temperature.

= Electron-acoustic recoil length
{parent=Electron-phonon scattering}
{title2=$\lambda=\hbar/(2m_ec_s)$}

For free-electron recoil and a linear acoustic branch, $E_e(q)=\hbar^2q^2/(2m_e)$ and $E_{\rm ph}(q)=\hbar c_sq$. Their ratio is $q\lambda$, so equality occurs at $q=1/\lambda$. This is a momentum/localization crossover scale, formally half a sound-speed analogue of the <reduced Compton wavelength>. It does not replace the speed of light in electron relativity or determine a <mean free path>. A Fermi-surface electron emitting a phonon has fractional radial loss $\delta\simeq q/(2\lambda k_F^2)$ when the transferred energy is small.

= Electron-phonon Umklapp threshold
{parent=Electron-phonon scattering}

For a spherical <Fermi surface> of radius $k_F$ lying inside a zone with shortest <reciprocal lattice> vector magnitude $G>2k_F$, a translated neighbouring Fermi sphere is separated from it by $q_U=G-2k_F$. An electron-phonon <Umklapp scattering> process satisfying $\mathbf k+\mathbf q=\mathbf k'+\mathbf G$ therefore needs a phonon wavevector of at least $q_U$. For an approximately linear acoustic dispersion its activation scale is
$$
T_U\sim\frac{\hbar v_s(G-2k_F)}{k_B}.
$$
At $T\ll T_U$ these phonons are sparsely occupied; once they become thermally available, momentum-relaxing scattering can grow rapidly. Normal small-angle scattering and residual disorder need not vanish below this scale, so it is a crossover estimate rather than a sharp universal conductivity threshold.

= Small-angle electron-phonon transport at low temperature
{parent=Electron-phonon scattering}

For acoustic modes $\omega_q\simeq c_s q$, thermally active wavevectors have $q_T\sim k_BT/(\hbar c_s)$. When $q_T\ll k_F$, a nearly elastic event on a spherical <Fermi surface> has $1-\cos\theta=q^2/(2k_F^2)$, suppressing momentum relaxation. In the clean three-dimensional acoustic model, the thermal relaxation rate scales as $T^3$ while the electrical transport rate gains two extra powers and scales as $T^5$. With electronic <heat capacity> proportional to $T$, this gives $K_e\propto T^{-2}$ and electrical resistivity proportional to $T^5$ in the phonon-limited regime. Residual elastic scattering cuts off these behaviours as temperature falls further.https://home.itp.ac.ru/~grigorev/LectureSlides/MetalsLecture2.pdf[Low-temperature electron-phonon scattering: thermal lifetime versus transport lifetime]{ref}

= Phonon scattering
{parent=Phonon}

<Phonon scattering> changes the occupation or propagation of lattice-vibration <normal modes> through interactions with boundaries, defects or other <phonons>. Anharmonic terms permit phonon-phonon scattering. Different processes need not relax heat flow equally: normal processes conserve total <crystal momentum> within the first <Brillouin zone>, whereas <Umklapp scattering> changes its representative by a nonzero <reciprocal lattice> vector.

= Normal phonon collisions and heat-current relaxation
{parent=Phonon scattering}

Normal phonon collisions conserve the crystal momentum of the distribution, whereas <Umklapp scattering> transfers momentum by a reciprocal vector. A normal collision can redistribute heat-carrying modes without relaxing a drifting distribution in the same manner as a momentum-relaxing event. Boundaries, impurities and Umklapp processes can then set the resistance. Counting all collision rates as interchangeable inverse transport times can miss collective phonon flow and nontrivial current weighting.

= Phonon mean free path
{parent=Phonon scattering}
{title2=$\ell=v_g\tau$}

The <phonon mean free path> is the typical distance traveled by a <phonon> between the relevant scattering events. For heat transport, the relevant relaxation time must describe relaxation of heat current, not merely count all collisions. An isotropic kinetic estimate is $\kappa\simeq c_Vv_s\ell/3$, where $c_V$ is <heat capacity> per unit <volume>. Boundary-limited $\ell$ and the low-temperature <Debye model> give $\kappa\propto T^3$.

= Umklapp scattering
{c}
{parent=Phonon scattering}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mathbf q_1+\mathbf q_2-\mathbf q_3=\mathbf G\neq0$}

= Umklapp process
{c}
{synonym}

An <Umklapp process> conserves reduced <crystal momentum> modulo a nonzero <reciprocal lattice> vector. It can occur in electron-phonon scattering as well as phonon-phonon scattering; the lattice takes up the reciprocal-lattice momentum.

In a three-phonon <Umklapp scattering> process, the first-zone <wavevectors> obey the displayed equation for a nonzero <reciprocal lattice> vector $\mathbf G$, together with <conservation of energy>. The <crystal momentum> transferred to the lattice allows these processes to relax a phonon heat current. They are suppressed at sufficiently low <temperature> and often determine the approximate $1/T$ high-temperature <thermal conductivity> of a crystalline <electrical insulator>.

= Debye–Waller factor
{c}
{parent=Phonon}
{wiki}

The Debye–Waller factor suppresses coherent scattering because atoms fluctuate about their equilibrium positions. For Gaussian displacement $u$, the amplitude factor is $\langle e^{iqu}\rangle=\exp[-q^2\langle u^2\rangle/2]$ and the intensity factor is its squared modulus. It multiplies the lattice interference factor, so exact extrema of the full finite-lattice intensity need not coincide with the constructive-interference centers when this envelope varies appreciably.

= Periodic displacements in a harmonic chain
{parent=Phonon}

For a chain with lattice spacing $b$ and $N$ atoms, write $x_s=sb+u_s$. The periodic normal-mode condition is $u_N=u_0$, equivalently $x_N=x_0+Nb$ for unwrapped positions. The literal position condition $x_N=x_0$ is incompatible with the equilibrium $x_s=sb$ in that coordinate system. Fourier modes $k_r=2\pi r/(Nb)$ give $\omega_r=2\lambda|\sin(k_rb/2)|$ for spring coefficient $m\lambda^2$. The zero mode describes free center-of-mass translation and must be fixed or handled separately when defining an oscillator vacuum.

= Phonon dispersion relation
{parent=Phonon}

A phonon dispersion relation gives its angular frequency $\omega$ as a function of its <wavevector> $k$. For an isotropic power law, $\omega=C|k|^\alpha$.

= Long-range harmonic-chain dispersion
{parent=Phonon dispersion relation}
{title2=$\omega^2(k)=4M^{-1}\sum_{n>0}C_n\sin^2(nka/2)$}

For a translation-invariant monatomic chain with symmetric pair force constants, $M\ddot u_p=\sum_{n>0}C_n(u_{p+n}+u_{p-n}-2u_p)$. Substituting a traveling <normal mode> gives the displayed even, reciprocal-periodic <phonon dispersion relation>. If $\sum n^2C_n$ converges and the chain is stable, its long-wavelength speed is $a\sqrt{\sum n^2C_n/M}$. A truly long-range interaction need not have a finite second moment or a finite linear acoustic slope.

= Next-nearest-neighbour monatomic-chain frequency maximum
{parent=Long-range harmonic-chain dispersion}
{title2=$\omega_{\max}/[2\sqrt{C_1/M}]=(1+4\alpha)/(4\sqrt\alpha)$}

Let $\alpha=C_2/C_1>0$ and $x=\sin^2(ka/2)$. The squared frequency in units $4C_1/M$ is $(1+4\alpha)x-4\alpha x^2$. For $\alpha\le1/4$ its maximum on $0\le x\le1$ is at the zone edge and equals one. For $\alpha>1/4$ it moves inside the zone to $x=(1+4\alpha)/(8\alpha)$, with the displayed frequency ratio. The small-wavevector speed instead increases by $\sqrt{1+4\alpha}$; maximizing the frequency and finding the sound speed are different operations.

= Phonon branch
{parent=Phonon dispersion relation}
{title2=$\omega_s(\mathbf k)$}

A continuous family of <normal modes> labelled by the <wavevector> and a branch index $s$. For $r$ <chemical atoms> per three-dimensional <primitive unit cell>, the <dynamical matrix> has $3r$ <eigenvalues>, giving $3r$ branches: three <acoustic phonon branches> and $3r-3$ <optical phonon branches> for a mechanically stable ordinary <crystal structure>.

= Extended-zone representation of a phonon dispersion
{parent=Phonon dispersion relation}

The <crystal momentum> of a <phonon> is defined modulo a <reciprocal lattice vector>. Extending its <dispersion relation> periodically beyond the <first Brillouin zone> labels equivalent excitations by different extended <wavevectors>. A scattering transfer $Q=G+q$ can be outside that zone while its reduced <phonon> <wavevector> $q$ is inside. The measured <neutron> recoil depends on the actual transfer $Q$, not on the reduced representative alone.

= Diatomic-chain dispersion relation with equal springs
{parent=Phonon dispersion relation}

For alternating <masses> $m,M$ at spacing $d$, equal nearest-neighbour <spring constants> $K$, and cell length $2d$, a two-component <normal mode> gives
$$
\omega_\pm^2=K\left(\frac1m+\frac1M\right)\pm K\sqrt{\left(\frac1m+\frac1M\right)^2-\frac4{mM}\sin^2(kd)},\qquad |k|\leq\frac\pi{2d}.
$$
The minus sign is the <acoustic phonon branch> and the plus sign is the <optical phonon branch>. For $M>m$ the edge frequencies are $\sqrt{2K/M}$ and $\sqrt{2K/m}$, leaving a gap. At $k=0$ they are zero and $\sqrt{2K(1/m+1/M)}$.

= Diatomic-chain zone-edge polarizations
{parent=Diatomic-chain dispersion relation with equal springs}
{title2=$\omega_{\rm ac}^2=2\alpha/M,\quad\omega_{\rm op}^2=2\alpha/m$}

In an alternating-mass chain with nearest-neighbour spacing $a$ and cell length $2a$, the inter-sublattice Bloch coupling is proportional to $\cos qa$. At the first-zone edge $q=\pi/(2a)$ it vanishes. For $M>m$, the acoustic edge mode moves only the heavy sublattice and the optical edge mode only the light one. Their <frequency> difference is $\sqrt{2\alpha/m}-\sqrt{2\alpha/M}$. At long <wavelength> the acoustic displacements are in phase, whereas the optical mode has zero centre-of-mass displacement.

= Long-wavelength inference of diatomic-chain parameters
{parent=Diatomic-chain dispersion relation with equal springs}

For alternating <masses> $m_A,m_B$, nearest-neighbour spacing $a$ and equal <spring constants> $\alpha$, the zone-centre optical <angular frequency> and acoustic <group velocity> obey
$$
\omega_{\rm op}^2(0)=2\alpha\left(\frac1{m_A}+\frac1{m_B}\right),\qquad
v_s^2=\frac{2\alpha a^2}{m_A+m_B}.
$$
Therefore $\alpha=m_Am_B\omega_{\rm op}^2(0)/[2(m_A+m_B)]$ and $a=v_s\sqrt{(m_A+m_B)/(2\alpha)}$. These infer effective model parameters from a measured intercept and initial slope of a <phonon dispersion relation>. Fitting a real polar crystal to a nearest-neighbour scalar model does not guarantee that every measured point or its structural spacing is reproduced exactly.

= Monatomic-chain dispersion relation
{parent=Phonon dispersion relation}

For equal <masses> $m$ at spacing $d$ joined by nearest-neighbour <springs> of <spring constant> $K$, <Newton's second law> gives $m\ddot u_j=K(u_{j+1}+u_{j-1}-2u_j)$. A <normal mode> $u_j=u_0e^{i(kjd-\omega t)}$ gives
$$
\omega(k)=2\sqrt{K/m}\,|\sin(kd/2)|,\qquad |k|\leq\pi/d.
$$
This single <acoustic phonon branch> is linear at small <wavevector> and has zero <group velocity> at the first <Brillouin zone> edge.

= Lennard-Jones chain sound speed
{parent=Monatomic-chain dispersion relation}
{c}
{title2=$v=\sqrt{72\epsilon/m}$}

A nearest-neighbour chain with the <Lennard-Jones potential> $V(x)=4\epsilon[(\sigma/x)^{12}-(\sigma/x)^6]$ has equilibrium spacing $a=2^{1/6}\sigma$ and <spring constant> $K=V''(a)=72\epsilon/a^2$. The <monatomic-chain dispersion relation> gives <speed of sound> $v=a\sqrt{K/m}=\sqrt{72\epsilon/m}$ and maximum <angular frequency> $2v/a$. The cancellation of $\sigma$ from the sound speed holds in this chain model; it does not remove the spacing from the cutoff frequency.

= Viscously damped monatomic-chain phonon
{parent=Monatomic-chain dispersion relation}
{title2=$\omega_\pm=-i\Gamma/(2m)\pm\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\Gamma^2/(4m^2)}$}

With real wave number and time convention $e^{-i\omega t}$, an on-site friction force $-\Gamma\dot u_j$ shifts the frequencies as displayed. Underdamped oscillations have amplitude envelope $e^{-\Gamma t/(2m)}$, while overdamped modes have two nonoscillatory rates. At small wave number a stable acoustic chain has slow decay $\omega\simeq-imc_s^2k^2/\Gamma$. Exactly uniform displacement remains a zero-restoring-force mode, so a constant displacement is not forced to vanish.

= Folded wave-number counting for a monatomic chain
{parent=Monatomic-chain dispersion relation}
{title2=$g_{|q|}=Na/\pi$}

A periodic chain of $N$ sites and length $Na$ has allowed signed <wave numbers> separated by $2\pi/(Na)$. The continuum state density on the signed axis is $Na/(2\pi)$. Combining opposite propagation directions onto $0<|q|<\pi/a$ doubles it to $Na/\pi$. This counts the same $N$ modes, not two independent <acoustic phonon branches>.

= Acoustic phonon branch
{parent=Phonon dispersion relation}

An acoustic phonon branch has $\omega(k)\to0$ as $k\to0$. Its long-wavelength <group velocity> is the <speed of sound>.

= Optical phonon branch
{parent=Phonon dispersion relation}

An optical phonon branch has a nonzero frequency as $k\to0$, corresponding to relative motion of degrees of freedom within one primitive cell.

= Alternating-spring chain dispersion relation
{parent=Phonon dispersion relation}

For equal masses $m$ separated by $a$ and alternating spring constants $\lambda$ and $\alpha\lambda$, the primitive-cell length is $2a$ and the two branches are
$$
\omega_\pm^2(k)=\frac{\lambda}{m}
\left[1+\alpha\pm\sqrt{(1-\alpha)^2+4\alpha\cos^2(ka)}\right].
$$
The minus and plus signs give the <acoustic phonon branch> and <optical phonon branch>, respectively.

= Strong-bond limit of the alternating-spring chain
{parent=Alternating-spring chain dispersion relation}

Take equal masses $m$, atomic spacing $a$, and alternating springs $K\gg\kappa>0$. The <primitive unit cell> contains a strong-bonded pair, so its length is $2a$ and the <Brillouin zone> ends at $\pm\pi/(2a)$. Expanding the exact <alternating-spring chain dispersion relation> gives
$$
\omega_-^2(q)\simeq\frac{2\kappa}{m}\sin^2(qa),\qquad
\omega_+^2(q)\simeq\frac{2K}{m}+\frac{2\kappa}{m}\cos^2(qa).
$$
The low-frequency <acoustic phonon branch> moves almost rigid dimers of mass $2m$ coupled by weak springs; its <speed of sound> tends to $a\sqrt{2\kappa/m}$. The high-frequency <optical phonon branch> describes internal dimer stretch. At the zone edge the exact frequencies are $\sqrt{2\kappa/m}$ and $\sqrt{2K/m}$.

= Density of states for a two-dimensional power-law phonon dispersion
{parent=Phonon dispersion relation}

For two polarizations in area $A$ and $\omega=C|k|^\alpha$, counting <wavevector> states in a disk gives
$$
g(\omega)=\frac{A}{\pi\alpha C^{2/\alpha}}
\omega^{2/\alpha-1}.
$$

= Debye model
{c}
{parent=Phonon}
{wiki}

The Debye model replaces the crystal's vibrational spectrum by a continuum of phonon modes up to a cutoff chosen to preserve the total number of vibrational degrees of freedom.

= Low-temperature thermal phonon number
{parent=Debye model}
{title2=$N_{\mathrm{ph}}\sim18\zeta(3)N(T/\Theta_D)^3$}

Integrating the <Bose-Einstein distribution> against the three-dimensional <Debye model> <phonon density of states> gives $N_{\mathrm{ph}}=9N(T/\Theta_D)^3\int_0^{\Theta_D/T}x^2/(e^x-1)\,dx$. For $T\ll\Theta_D$, expand $(e^x-1)^{-1}=\sum_{j\geq1}e^{-jx}$ and integrate termwise: the limiting integral is $2\sum_{j\geq1}j^{-3}=2\zeta(3)$. This counts thermal quanta, not available modes or half quanta of <zero-point energy>.

= Debye sound speed
{parent=Debye model}
{c}
{title2=$3/v_D^3=1/v_L^3+2/v_T^3$}

The effective isotropic acoustic speed used to replace separate branches in the <Debye model>. With atomic number density $n$, counting three branches gives $k_D=(6\pi^2n)^{1/3}$ and $\omega_D=v_Dk_D=k_B\theta_D/\hbar$. Matching the low-frequency <density of states> for one longitudinal and two transverse branches gives the displayed inverse-cube average. A <Debye temperature> alone does not determine both individual <sound speeds>.

= Mode count in the Debye model
{parent=Debye model}
{title2=$\beta=\frac{12\pi^4}{5}\frac{N_mk_B}{\Theta_D^3},\quad k_D^3=6\pi^2N_m/V$}

Suppose a three-dimensional <Debye model> is normalized to $3N_m$ vibrational modes in volume $V$. Its low-temperature heat-capacity coefficient is the one displayed, and its spherical wavevector cutoff obeys $k_D^3=6\pi^2N_m/V$. The Debye temperature is $\Theta_D=\hbar v_Dk_D/k_B$. At fixed measured $\beta$ and volume, changing the counting convention by $N_m\mapsto cN_m$ raises both $\Theta_D$ and $k_D$ by $c^{1/3}$; the inferred average <sound speed> is unchanged.

For a mole of formula units containing $r$ atoms each, a conventional full atomic-mode count uses $N_m=rN_A$ and tends to lattice heat capacity $3rR$ at high temperature. A reduced convention using $N_m=N_A$ instead has three effective modes per formula unit. These are different continuum normalizations, not a claim that the real crystal has only three modes per formula unit. A real multi-atom primitive cell has three acoustic branches and additional optical branches; a Debye sphere preserving the full atomic count is an effective representation of that spectrum, rather than its literal first Brillouin zone.

= High-temperature phonon number in the Debye model
{parent=Debye model}
{title2=$N_{\mathrm{ph}}\sim(9/2)N T/\Theta_D$}

For three-dimensional <Debye model> mode density $D(\omega)=9N\omega^2/\omega_D^3$, the <Bose-Einstein distribution> becomes $n_\omega\simeq k_BT/(\hbar\omega)$ when $T\gg\Theta_D$. Integrating the occupations gives $N_{\mathrm{ph}}\simeq(9/2)NT/\Theta_D$. The number of <phonon> quanta is not the number $3N$ of vibrational modes and need not be bounded by it. If a stipulated <electron-phonon scattering> model makes resistivity proportional to this <phonon> number with a temperature-independent coefficient, the high-temperature <electrical resistivity> is proportional to $T$.

= Debye heat capacity
{c}
{parent=Debye model}
{title2=$C_V=9Nk_B(T/\Theta_D)^3\int_0^{\Theta_D/T}x^4e^x/(e^x-1)^2\,dx$}

The three-dimensional <Debye model> normalizes its <phonon> <density of states> to $3N$ modes: $g(\omega)=9N\omega^2/\omega_D^3$ below the <Debye frequency> $\omega_D$. The thermal <energy> is $U=\int_0^{\omega_D}g(\omega)\hbar\omega/[e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1]d\omega$. Differentiate at fixed <volume> and substitute $x=\hbar\omega/(k_BT)$ to obtain the displayed <heat capacity at constant volume>.

At $T\ll\Theta_D$, integration by parts gives $\int_0^\infty x^4e^x/(e^x-1)^2dx=4\int_0^\infty x^3/(e^x-1)dx=4\pi^4/15$. Therefore $C_V\sim(12\pi^4/5)Nk_B(T/\Theta_D)^3$. At $T\gg\Theta_D$, each mode approaches the <equipartition theorem> value $k_B$ of <heat capacity>, so $C_V\to3Nk_B$, the <Dulong-Petit law>. Fixed harmonic <zero-point energy> does not contribute to either limit.

= Electronic and lattice heat-capacity crossover in a monovalent metal
{parent=Debye heat capacity}
{title2=$T_*^2=15k_B\theta_D^3/(8\pi^4E_F)$}

For a monovalent three-dimensional <free electron gas>, the spin-summed molar <density of states> is $g(E_F)=3N_A/(2E_F)$. Combining the shell estimate $C_{el}=3k_B^2g(E_F)T$ with $C_{la}=(12\pi^4/5)R(T/\theta_D)^3$ gives the displayed nonzero crossover. The controlled <Sommerfeld expansion> replaces $3$ by $\pi^2/3$ and multiplies $T_*^2$ by $\pi^2/9$. A <Fermi energy> alone does not determine the DOS in an unspecified band structure.

= Surface-electron and bulk-phonon heat-capacity crossover
{parent=Debye heat capacity}
{title2=$C_{\mathrm{surf}}/C_{\mathrm{bulk}}\propto A/(NT)$}

A neutral two-dimensional linear electronic spectrum has <heat capacity> proportional to $AT^2$, while three-dimensional acoustic <phonons> have <Debye heat capacity> proportional to $NT^3/\Theta_D^3$. Thus their ratio scales as $Ak_B^2\Theta_D^3/(\hbar^2v^2NT)$. At fixed <crystal> composition and shape, $A/N\propto1/L$ for linear size $L$, favoring the electronic surface contribution in smaller or colder specimens. The continuum laws require thermally populated wavelengths small compared with sample size; their formal low-temperature crossover need not lie inside that regime.

= Debye wavevector
{c}
{parent=Debye model}
{title2=$q_D$}

The spherical cutoff in the three-dimensional <Debye model> preserves three vibrational modes per atom. For atom density $n_a$, $q_D=(6\pi^2n_a)^{1/3}$ and $\omega_D=c_s q_D$ in a single-speed approximation. A monovalent free-electron metal has $k_F=(3\pi^2n_a)^{1/3}$, giving $q_D=2^{1/3}k_F$. This cutoff is a mode-counting approximation, not an exact spherical boundary of the crystal's <Brillouin zone>.

= Debye frequency
{title2=$\omega_D$}
{c}
{parent=Debye model}

The Debye frequency is the upper frequency cutoff fixed by equating the continuum mode count to the crystal's number of vibrational degrees of freedom.

= Debye temperature
{title2=$T_D$}
{c}
{parent=Debye frequency}

The Debye temperature is $T_D=\hbar\omega_D/k_B$.

= Two-dimensional power-law Debye model
{parent=Debye model}

For two polarizations, $n$ atoms in area $A$, and $\omega=C|k|^\alpha$, mode counting gives
$$
\omega_D=C\left(\frac{4\pi n}{A}\right)^{\alpha/2}.
$$
At low temperature its energy scales as $T^{1+2/\alpha}$ and its constant-volume heat capacity as $T^{2/\alpha}$.
