The electron-optical column contains an <electron gun>, an accelerating supply, a <condenser lens> system, an objective aperture, an <electron objective lens>, scan coils and alignment/stigmation controls. The <electron gun> may use <thermionic emission> or <field emission>. The <condenser lens> system sets the incident probe current and source demagnification; the <electron objective lens> focuses the probe on the specimen. A movable specimen stage controls position, tilt and working distance. The column and specimen chamber need a <vacuum> system, appropriate pumps and specimen-transfer hardware. Detectors collect <secondary electrons>, <backscattered electrons> or characteristic <X-rays>; amplification and synchronized raster acquisition assign a measured signal to each image pixel. The first two signals provide different imaging information, while characteristic <X-rays> allow elemental analysis. **A source, probe-forming optics, raster scan, specimen stage, detectors, acquisition electronics and <vacuum> system form the essential chain.**<Scanning Electron Microscope A To Z by JEOL>[printed pp.3–6, construction of the column, detectors and vacuum system]{ref}

The image annotation gives probe <electric current> $I=43\,\mathrm{pA}=4.3\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm A$ and working distance $L=2.4\,\mathrm{mm}$. Interpret $d_o$ as the probe diameter and $\alpha$ as its convergence semiangle, in radians. To estimate that angle from the available dimensions, approximate the effective aperture plane as a distance $L$ from the specimen. With aperture radius $r_a=25\,\mathrm{\mu m}$,
$$
\alpha\simeq\frac{r_a}{L}=1.0417\times10^{-2},\qquad d_o=\sqrt{\frac{4I}{\pi^2\alpha^2\beta}}=\boxed{4.01\,\mathrm{nm}}.
$$
The <electron beam brightness> $\beta$ measures <electric current> per probe area per <solid angle>; the supplied convention corresponds to a circular probe and a paraxial cone of <solid angle> approximately $\pi\alpha^2$. The aperture diameter must be halved, and the annotated current is in picoamperes. Treating the full aperture angle as $\alpha$ would introduce a factor-of-two error in $d_o$.

This is a conditional optical estimate: the working distance is not necessarily the actual electron-optical distance from the effective aperture to the focused probe. The answer assumes that approximation, the quoted <electron beam brightness> at the operating <voltage>, and negligible <optical aberrations>. The 30 kV annotation gives a much shorter <de Broglie wavelength> than 4 nm, so a nanometre-scale probe is plausible. Actual <electron microscope resolution> also depends on <diffraction>, scattering in the specimen, signal generation and collection, <signal-to-noise ratio>, drift and specimen charging. A 4 nm probe does not establish that every measured edge width is 4 nm.

The SE annotation identifies a <secondary electron> image. The specimen has an open network of ridges, thin walls and irregular cavities, with lateral features on the scale of tens to hundreds of nanometres when compared with the 500 nm bar. Exposed edges can emit <secondary electrons> from more than one nearby surface; inclined faces also have a larger near-surface escape fraction than a face perpendicular to the beam. These mechanisms make protruding rims conspicuous. Recesses can be dark because emitted <secondary electrons> are intercepted or collected less efficiently. A through-lens detector changes the collection geometry, so a simple one-sided illumination analogy is incomplete.<Scanning Electron Microscope A To Z by JEOL>[printed pp.10–14, secondary-electron inclination, edge effects and detector collection]{ref}

**An irregular porous relief with bright rims and darker recesses is consistent with the image.** The cross-section below is an original illustrative geometry, rather than a reconstruction of a particular image line. A single <secondary electron> intensity map does not supply calibrated heights, prove that every dark patch is a through-hole, or distinguish charging from all material contrast. Repeat with specimen tilt, altered detector collection or reduced accelerating <voltage>, and compare <backscattered electron> or elemental signals before assigning a compositional cause.

Model the <AFM cantilever> as a driven <damped harmonic oscillator>. If the drive stays at the free-space <resonance> and the <spring constant> $k$ and effective <mass> $m$ are unchanged, its steady amplitude is
$$
A=\frac{F_d}{\sqrt{(k-m\omega^2)^2+(\gamma\omega)^2}},\qquad A(\omega_0)=\frac{F_d}{(\gamma_0+\gamma_{\rm ts})\omega_0}.
$$
Here $\gamma_0$ is intrinsic damping and $\gamma_{\rm ts}$ the added dissipative interaction with the surface. At large average separation there is no tapping, so $\gamma_{\rm ts}\simeq0$ and $A=A_{\rm free}$. As the lower turning point reaches the surface, intermittent contacts dissipate <energy> and the amplitude decreases. With further approach the oscillation becomes strongly constrained, eventually approaching a continuously contacting, nearly quenched response. **The damping-only sketch has a free-amplitude plateau followed by a descending amplitude on approach.** The rounded transition in the sketch is illustrative; <adhesion> and actual contact mechanics determine its shape. The deliberately omitted force-gradient-induced <resonance> shift can make real approach curves more complicated.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

An uncorrected amplitude image has a useful vertical range only across the transition between free oscillation and strongly suppressed oscillation. If the free motion is a few tens of nanometres peak-to-peak, its one-sided amplitude is half that excursion. The relevant vertical height range is therefore **of the order of the oscillation excursion, only tens of nanometres**, rather than micrometres. It is not a sharp universal limit equal to the quoted peak-to-peak value: the chosen mean gap, contact law and usable sensitivity determine the actual range. A sufficiently high feature can quench the motion or produce destructive contact; a sufficiently deep depression leaves the probe oscillating freely with essentially no height sensitivity. This argument limits height tracking, not the lateral width of a feature. Lateral resolution instead depends on <AFM tip convolution>, sampling and the probe-surface interaction.

= Solution

In <tapping-mode atomic force microscopy>, demodulate the cantilever displacement to obtain its amplitude and compare it with a setpoint below $A_{\rm free}$. A <closed-loop control> system drives a <piezoelectric scanner> in $z$ to restore the setpoint as the raster crosses raised or depressed regions. The interaction remains in its sensitive amplitude range while the scanner follows heights much larger than the oscillation amplitude. **The principal height image is the calibrated feedback $z$ displacement at each lateral pixel.** The amplitude-error and phase channels are supplementary signals, rather than interchangeable height measurements. Maximum height is now bounded by scanner travel, clearance and stable tracking. Steep features require adequate feedback bandwidth and sufficiently slow scanning; trace/retrace disagreement tests tracking, while a changed <AFM probe> tests geometric broadening. Surface-dependent dissipation can move the setpoint height even on a geometrically flat specimen.

A <sacrificial layer> temporarily supports the intended moving structure and determines its clearance above the substrate. One workable <surface micromachining> sequence deposits <silicon dioxide> on <silicon>, opens anchor windows in that oxide, deposits a structural <polysilicon> film and patterns the beam and release openings. The structural film contacts the substrate at the anchors. Selective removal of the oxide beneath the beam leaves the beam suspended while retaining its attachment. An access opening or exposed perimeter is essential; an etchant cannot instantly reach sealed material.

The release needs substantial lateral removal, so an <isotropic etch> with high <etch selectivity> is useful. Hydrofluoric-acid oxide release is compatible with a suitable <polysilicon> structure; a directional <reactive-ion etching> step alone would mainly clear exposed vertical paths and leave material beneath the beam. Choose the structural material, anchor design, sacrificial material and etchant together, and allow for transport-limited undercut and structural residual stress. Wet release followed by ordinary evaporation can pull a compliant structure onto the substrate through <capillary force> and cause <stiction>. <critical point drying> or a compatible <vapor-phase release etch> avoids the receding <liquid> interface. Neither method removes the need to check residues, selectivity and anchor survival. **Pattern the support and structure first, then selectively undercut the support while preserving the anchors.**<Fabricating MEMS and Nanotechnology by MEMS and Nanotechnology Exchange>[Surface Micromachining: oxide/polysilicon/HF example and capillary stiction]{ref}

**Oxygen-plasma ashing**, preferably a remote or downstream arrangement when low damage matters, removes organic <photoresist> chemically. Feed <oxygen> through a plasma source energized by radiofrequency or microwave <power>. Electron-impact dissociation generates reactive <oxygen> species; neutral radicals reach the wafer in a separate downstream region. Keep the wafer off a strongly biased driven <electrode> so energetic <ion> bombardment is much smaller than in directional <reactive-ion etching>. A barrel asher with shielded wafers is another chemical-stripping implementation.
$$
\mathrm{O_2}+e^-\longrightarrow2\mathrm O+e^-,\qquad\mathrm C_x\mathrm H_y+(2x+y/2)\mathrm O\longrightarrow x\mathrm{CO_2}+(y/2)\mathrm{H_2O}.
$$
These equations represent radical production and an ideal complete-oxidation balance; actual exhaust can also contain CO and fragments. Pumping removes volatile products. Directional shape fidelity is unnecessary for blanket stripping, while access around surface relief and high chemical removal selectivity are useful. Monitor endpoint and wafer <temperature>, and verify removal with a witness surface. <oxygen> can oxidize exposed materials, charging can still damage sensitive structures, and inorganic or metal-containing residues may require a compatible separate clean. **Ashing removes organic contamination; it is not a universal residue-removal step.**<Physics of Microfabrication: Front End Processing, Lecture 20 by Massachusetts Institute of Technology>[lecture transcript, discussion of barrel and parallel-plate plasma ashing, and comparison with RIE]{ref}

The solid <electrolyte> conducts oxide <ions>. Equilibrating <oxygen> at the two interfaces couples one $\mathrm{O_2}$ molecule to four <electrons>. Taking the positive measured <electromotive force> as the air-reference potential relative to the Ni/NiO side, the <Nernst equation> is
$$
E=\frac{RT}{4F}\ln\frac{p_{\rm air}}{p_{\rm eq}},\qquad \frac{p_{\rm eq}}{p^\circ}=\frac{p_{\rm air}}{p^\circ}\exp\left(-\frac{4FE}{RT}\right).
$$
Assume air at one bar with $p_{\rm air}/p^\circ=0.21$, pure solid phases of unit <thermodynamic activity>, and $p^\circ=1\,\mathrm{bar}$. For the reaction normalized to one mole of <oxygen>,
$$
2\mathrm{Ni}(s)+\mathrm{O_2}(g)\longrightarrow2\mathrm{NiO}(s),\qquad \Delta_rG^\circ=RT\ln\frac{p_{\rm eq}}{p^\circ}=RT\ln0.21-4FE.
$$
The negative sign is physically consistent with a strongly reducing Ni/NiO equilibrium relative to air. Using the supplied $R$ and $F$ gives
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|r}
T\ (\mathrm K)&p_{\rm eq}\ (\mathrm{bar})&\Delta_rG^\circ\ (\mathrm{kJ\,mol_{O_2}^{-1}})\\\hline
900&6.92\times10^{-19}&-312.88\\
1000&3.95\times10^{-16}&-294.87\\
1100&7.75\times10^{-14}&-276.09
\end{array}
$$
**The tabulated <free energies> are per mole of $\mathrm{O_2}$, not per mole of NiO.** Values normalized to one mole of NiO would be half as large. A reliable <oxygen concentration cell> measurement requires reversible interfaces, calibrated uniform <temperature>, a known reference <oxygen> <pressure> and predominantly ionic rather than electronic conduction through <yttria-stabilized zirconia>. Air is not pure <oxygen>: dropping the $RT\ln0.21$ term changes the inferred <entropy>.

A <thermoelectric generator> maintains hot and cold junctions of two materials with different <Seebeck coefficients>. Mobile charge carriers redistribute in the <temperature gradient>, producing an open-circuit <voltage> of magnitude $|V|=|S_p-S_n|\Delta T$ for constant coefficients. A load permits <electric current>; pairs of p-type and n-type legs are commonly connected electrically in series and thermally in parallel. Maintaining the output requires continuous <heat transfer> from the hot reservoir and rejection of <heat> to the cold reservoir. The <thermoelectric figure of merit> $ZT=S^2\sigma T/\kappa$ expresses the favorable combination of large <Seebeck coefficient>, large <electrical conductivity> and small <thermal conductivity>. Contact losses, Joule heating and parasitic <heat> leaks reduce practical efficiency.

A <pyroelectric generator> instead exploits a change of spontaneous <electric polarization> with <temperature>. If the relevant component of the <pyroelectric coefficient> is $p=dP_s/dT$, an <electrode> of area $A$ receives a short-circuit <electric current> approximately $I=pA\,dT/dt$. A heating stroke and a cooling stroke produce opposite charge changes. Rectification or a synchronized electrical cycle can transfer <energy> to storage. **A spatial <temperature> difference drives the thermoelectric device; a temporal <temperature> change drives the pyroelectric device.** Uniform, constant <temperature> gives no continuing current from <pyroelectricity> even if the material is hot. Reported nanowire generators deliberately use changing <temperature>, rather than infer continuous generation from a static hot specimen.<Pyroelectric nanogenerators for harvesting thermoelectric energy by Ya Yang and others>[Nano Letters 12 (2012), pp.2833–2838, transient-temperature operating principle]{ref}

Both approaches can be compact, silent and free of macroscopic moving parts. The <thermoelectric generator> gives continuous output under a sustained gradient, but needs two effective thermal contacts and sufficient <heat> flow; very small $\Delta T$ gives a small <voltage> and thermal contacts can consume much of the available gradient. Thin films exhibiting <pyroelectricity> can respond to intermittent heating without a permanently maintained hot/cold separation across their thickness, but require repeated thermal cycling, low electrical leakage and suitable charge extraction. Their thermal <time constant> limits useful cycling <frequency>, and sensible heating/cooling can dominate the <heat> budget. A steady hot pipe with an available cold sink favors a <thermoelectric generator>; periodic radiative heating can favor a <pyroelectric generator>. Neither converts equilibrium thermal motion at a single fixed <temperature> into sustained useful <work>.

In <transmission electron microscopy>, the <electron objective lens> forms an intermediate real-space image and intermediate/projector lenses enlarge it onto a screen or camera. The total optical <magnification> is the product of the relevant stage magnifications. The user changes imaging-lens excitations to select <magnification>, while separately controlling illumination and focus. Display resizing introduces an additional enlargement, so the recorded pixel calibration matters.

In <scanning transmission electron microscopy>, a focused probe is rastered across the specimen and a detector signal is assigned to each probe position. The displayed <magnification> is display width divided by scanned specimen width. The user increases <magnification> by reducing raster-deflection amplitude, independently of choosing a smaller incident probe with the probe-forming lenses. **TEM changes image-forming lens magnification; STEM changes the raster field of view.** A smaller field or more pixels does not create finer physical resolution than the probe, specimen scattering and detection permit. A calibrated real-space scale is more transferable than the apparent size on a monitor.<Scanning Electron Microscope A To Z by JEOL>[printed p.7, raster-width/display-width magnification; this geometric relation also applies to scanned transmitted-electron imaging]{ref}

Choose cyclic reciprocal units, so the incident and elastically scattered wavevectors have magnitude $1/\lambda$. Place the <reciprocal lattice> origin $O$ at the end of the incident vector from the sphere centre $C$. A reciprocal vector $\mathbf g$ is excited when its endpoint $G$ lies on the <Ewald sphere>, because
$$
\mathbf k_s=\mathbf k_0+\mathbf g,\qquad |\mathbf k_s|=|\mathbf k_0|=1/\lambda.
$$
A specimen tilt rotates the <reciprocal lattice> relative to this construction. The very short <electron> <de Broglie wavelength> makes the sphere nearly flat across several ordinary reciprocal-lattice spacings. In a thin foil, finite thickness $t$ broadens reciprocal points into <reciprocal lattice rods> approximately along the foil normal, with characteristic longitudinal extent $1/t$. A rod may intersect the sphere even when its ideal point is slightly off it. Consequently several reflections can appear simultaneously near a <zone axis>, and thinning changes the excitation tolerance. A small particle has finite-size broadening in other directions too. This geometrical excitation condition does not guarantee a particular spot intensity: <crystal basis structure factors>, specimen thickness and <dynamical diffraction> still matter.

Indexed <electron diffraction> ring positions from a polycrystalline deposit identify candidate phases and give <lattice parameters> after camera-length calibration. Spot patterns from a small selected area establish <zone axis> directions, epitaxial orientation relationships or <crystal twinning>. Nanobeam mapping can reveal grain orientation changes, while systematic spot shifts can measure relative <strain> if lens distortions and scan drift are controlled. Spot or ring broadening can reflect finite coherent size, but also <strain> and instrumental broadening, so it is not an unqualified particle-diameter measurement. Use a calibration standard and an empty support region, vary dose to check <electron beam damage>, and combine spacing information with elemental analysis when chemically distinct phases have similar <lattice parameters>.

The resolved periodic contrast comes principally from <electron phase contrast>: transmitted and diffracted <electron> waves interfere after passing through the image-forming optics. The resulting intensity depends on specimen thickness, orientation, defocus and the <contrast transfer function>; bright dots cannot automatically be labelled gold-atom positions without image simulation. The surrounding nonperiodic background is consistent with an amorphous support or contamination, but its chemistry is not determined by contrast alone.

The particle contains differently oriented fringe regions, and the <Fourier transform> has several opposing spot pairs with an approximately pentagonal/tenfold composite appearance. Together with the faceted outline, this is **consistent with a multiply twinned <gold> particle, plausibly a decahedral arrangement**, rather than one perfect cubic lattice with fivefold rotational symmetry. Opposite Fourier spots occur because the recorded image intensity is real; counting ten spots is not itself proof of a tenfold crystal. Overlapping domains, contrast-transfer effects and a slightly off-axis view can complicate the pattern. A tilt series or spatially windowed transforms across individual domains would test the twinning interpretation. The stability of such particles involves a competition among surface, twin-boundary and elastic strain energies.<Stability of Multiply-Twinned Particles by Shozo Ino>[J. Phys. Soc. For cubic <lattice planes>, $d_{hkl}=a/\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}$. The <face-centred cubic selection rule> permits indices that are all odd or all even. Thus
$$
d_{111}=0.2336\,\mathrm{nm},\qquad d_{200}=0.2023\,\mathrm{nm},\qquad d_{220}=0.1430\,\mathrm{nm}.
$$
The first two spacings exceed the quoted 0.19 nm real-space resolution, whereas $d_{220}$ does not. This restriction is for the resolved image and its transform; a <diffraction> instrument need not have the same cutoff.

A natural candidate is a beam near a **local $\langle110\rangle$ <zone axis>**. For example, the $[110]$ <zone law> $h+k=0$ includes four $\{111\}$ reflections and two $\{200\}$ reflections, all within that real-space cutoff. Their reciprocal-space <length> ratio is $g_{200}/g_{111}=2/\sqrt3=1.1547$. The angle between $(1\bar11)$ and $(1\bar1\bar1)$ reciprocal vectors is $\cos^{-1}(1/3)=70.53^\circ$. In contrast, a $[001]$ view gives a fourfold arrangement of the resolvable $\{200\}$ family, while the lowest ordinary $[111]$ zone reflections are $\{220\}$ and would not be resolved at the stated cutoff.

In a five-domain decahedral interpretation, neighboring tetrahedral fcc regions meet on $\{111\}$ twin planes around a common near-$\langle110\rangle$ axis. Five unstrained wedges supply $5\times70.53^\circ=352.64^\circ$; elastic distortion accommodates the remaining $7.36^\circ$. Their rotated <diffraction> contributions need not resemble a single six-spot $[110]$ pattern. **Near-$\langle110\rangle$ fcc domains in a <multiply twinned particle> are a defensible orientation proposal, not a unique full indexing.** The printed transform has no reciprocal-space scale or indexed spot list, so it cannot establish every domain rotation or exclude an overlapping-particle contribution. Calibrated spacings and transforms from individual regions would settle those distinctions. The five-domain geometry is independently observed in atomic-resolution gold-particle measurements.<Study of Nanoparticles at UTSA: One Year of Using the First JEM-ARM200F Installed in USA by Alvaro Mayoral and others>[printed p.2, Fig.1 and accompanying discussion of decahedral gold and angular deficit]{ref}

Finite <numerical aperture> and <diffraction> remove high <spatial frequencies> from a mask image. Neighboring features modify the aerial intensity, and the <photoresist> threshold turns this blurred intensity into biased edges, shortened lines and rounded corners. <optical proximity correction> deliberately predistorts the mask: line-end extensions and small serifs can compensate for local loss of exposure, while other edges are moved back where exposure spreads too far. The correction is calibrated for illumination, resist and focus; it does not restore arbitrarily high lost <spatial frequencies>.

<double patterning> separates a dense target pattern into two sparser patterns, each printable within the single-exposure process window. Two exposures with an intervening pattern-fixing or transfer step combine their features on the wafer. For regular lines, alternating line assignment can halve the final pitch while each exposure retains a larger pitch. **<optical proximity correction> corrects local image bias; <double patterning> divides the density burden between exposures.** Overlay error and the minimum printable isolated feature still constrain the result. Simply exposing the same unmodified resist twice without an appropriate process separation does not give the same independent thresholding.

Treat the letter-shaped regions as openings to be etched, using positive-tone resist; the clear mask areas therefore correspond to those openings. Label the two L features $L_1,L_2$ from left to right. An alternating assignment is **mask A: H, $L_1$, O; mask B: E, $L_2$**. Each full letter remains on one mask, avoiding unnecessary stitch boundaries across its stems. Nearest-neighbor interletter gaps now occur between different exposures, rather than having to be resolved within one exposure. Verify that each individual letter's internal spaces are still printable; without dimensions, this partition is a process proposal rather than proof of a particular numerical minimum linewidth.

A robust sequence is <litho-etch-litho-etch double patterning> into a durable <hard mask>. Deposit an oxide <hard mask> on <silicon>; expose and develop mask A and transfer those openings through the <hard mask>. Strip resist, apply fresh resist, align and expose mask B, then transfer its openings into the same <hard mask>, the second resist covering mask-A openings during that second oxide transfer. Remove resist and perform one final deep <silicon> etch through the union of the openings. An inductively coupled <deep reactive-ion etching> process with alternating fluorine <etching> and sidewall <passivation> gives much more vertical, high-aspect-ratio features than an <isotropic etch>. Mask thickness and <etch selectivity> must survive the required depth. Control sidewall scalloping, aspect-ratio-dependent etch rate, charging, microloading and the combined overlay/etch-bias budget.

In a sufficiently supersaturated <vapor>, making <liquid> lowers bulk <Gibbs free energy>, but making a small droplet creates a costly liquid-vapor interface. A <La Mer diagram> plots the <concentration> of growth species against time. Label the equilibrium <solubility> $C_s$ and a higher effective <nucleation> threshold $C_n$. Generate growth species until the <concentration> briefly exceeds $C_n$; a short <burst nucleation> interval then creates seeds. The <Young–Laplace equation> is $p_l-p_v=\gamma(1/R_1+1/R_2)$ with signed principal curvatures positive for a convex <liquid> droplet. A <wetting> bridge between plates has a strongly concave meridional curvature. Let $P_{\rm sat}$ denote the flat-interface <saturation pressure> at the same <temperature>, $P_V$ the surrounding equilibrium <vapor pressure>, and $\overline V_l$ the incompressible <liquid> <molar volume>. Equating <liquid> and ideal-vapor <chemical potentials> to their common reference at $P_{\rm sat}$ gives
$$
\overline V_l(p_l-P_{\rm sat})=RT\ln\frac{P_V}{P_{\rm sat}}.
$$
Thus the <pressure> contribution to the attractive normal <force> on a wetted circular area is
$$
F_p=\pi a^2(P_V-p_l)=\pi a^2\left[\frac{RT}{\overline V_l}\ln\frac{P_{\rm sat}}{P_V}+P_V-P_{\rm sat}\right].
$$
For <capillary pressures> large compared with the vapor-pressure difference, and a bridge <wetting> essentially the plate area,
$$
\boxed{F\simeq\frac{\pi a^2RT}{\overline V_l}\ln\frac{P_{\rm sat}}{P_V}}.
$$
Here $F$ is the magnitude of the external separating <force> balancing attraction, and $P_V<P_{\rm sat}$. A finite <contact angle> adds a contact-line <force> $2\pi a\gamma\sin\theta$ at each plate's rim, counted once in the <force> on that plate, while the exact <pressure> also retains both curvatures. The rim term is normally smaller than the narrow-gap <pressure> term by order $d/a$ for appreciable <wetting>; it cannot be neglected uniformly near $\theta=90^\circ$. For complete <wetting> the leading equivalent expression is $F\simeq2\pi a^2\gamma/d$. The corresponding equilibrium humidity is $P_V/P_{\rm sat}\simeq\exp[-2\gamma\overline V_l/(RTd)]$.

These are quasistatic, equilibrium expressions for an existing bridge, neglecting viscous suction, contact-angle hysteresis, disjoining <forces> and deformation of the plates. The wetted radius must replace $a$ if the <liquid> does not reach the plate edge. During actual separation a bridge can shrink, depin or break, so this local <force> balance is not a promise of a constant <force> for arbitrary travel.

A material exhibiting <piezoelectricity> converts cyclic mechanical stress into <electric charge>; its mechanical and electrical boundary conditions jointly set the extractable <energy>. <piezoelectric ceramics> such as <lead zirconate titanate> are stiff and often have large charge coefficients and electromechanical coupling, but are brittle and can be difficult to match to large-strain flexible motion. <piezoelectric polymers> such as <polyvinylidene fluoride> are compliant, light and compatible with flexible large-area films. Their lower charge coefficient is partly offset in voltage-sensitive applications by lower <permittivity>: the <voltage> coefficient is $g=d/\epsilon$.

**Ceramics often favor strong charge generation and coupling; polymers favor flexibility and strain accommodation.** The larger open-circuit <voltage> of a low-capacitance film does not itself imply more useful <power>. For a simple fixed-stress comparison the electrical <energy> scale involves $d^2/\epsilon$, while coupling also depends on mechanical compliance. At fixed imposed strain, different stiffnesses change the available mechanical <energy>. <resonance> tuning, fatigue, mechanical mounting, rectifier losses and load matching can reverse a simplistic material ranking; compare complete devices under the same actual vibration source.
