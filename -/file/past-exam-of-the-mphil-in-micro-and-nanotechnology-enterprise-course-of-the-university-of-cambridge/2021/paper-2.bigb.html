Rolling <graphene> into a <single-walled carbon nanotube> quantizes the circumferential <wave vector>, leaving a set of one-dimensional longitudinal subbands. At each subband edge the <group velocity> tends to zero, producing a <Van Hove singularity> in the <density of states>. For a simple gapped Dirac subband, the contribution scales as $|E|/\sqrt{E^2-\Delta_j^2}$ outside its edge. At low <temperature> the valence states below the <Fermi level> are occupied and the conduction states above it are empty. A semiconducting tube has a gap; an ideal metallic tube has gapless channels as well as higher-subband singularities.

For light polarized along the tube, the strongest allowed interband transitions connect corresponding subbands while approximately conserving axial <wave vector> and circumferential <angular momentum>. The large <joint density of states> makes distinct absorption resonances, conventionally labelled $E_{11},E_{22},\ldots$. Strong <electron>–hole interactions produce bound <excitons>, so the observed optical resonances differ from the noninteracting band separations. Defects, doping, bundling and <dielectric screening> change peak energies and <oscillator strengths>. The original schematic below depicts the subband-edge structure, not measured spectra.

With the <graphene> lattice-vector length $a=0.246\ \mathrm{nm}$, the <carbon nanotube chiral vector> is $\mathbf C_h=n\mathbf a_1+m\mathbf a_2$. The vectors meet at $60^\circ$, giving
$$
C=a\sqrt{n^2+nm+m^2},\qquad d=\frac C\pi,\qquad
\theta=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\sqrt3\,m}{2n+m}\right).
$$
Here $C$ is circumference, $d$ diameter, and the <carbon nanotube chiral angle> $\theta$ is measured from the zigzag direction. <Nanotube zone folding> gives a nominal metallic tube when $n-m\equiv0\pmod3$ and a semiconducting tube otherwise. Evaluating all the supplied indices gives the following derived results:
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrr|c}
(n,m)&d\ (\mathrm{nm})&C\ (\mathrm{nm})&\theta\ ({}^\circ)&\text{type}\\\hline
(5,4)&0.6116&1.9213&26.33&\mathrm S\\
(6,4)&0.6826&2.1446&23.41&\mathrm S\\
(7,3)&0.6960&2.1865&17.00&\mathrm S\\
(6,5)&0.7470&2.3467&27.00&\mathrm S\\
(8,3)&0.7712&2.4228&15.30&\mathrm S\\
(10,0)&0.7830&2.4600&0.00&\mathrm S\\
(9,2)&0.7947&2.4966&9.83&\mathrm S\\
(7,5)&0.8175&2.5683&24.50&\mathrm S\\
(8,4)&0.8287&2.6034&19.11&\mathrm S\\
(7,6)&0.8824&2.7723&27.46&\mathrm S\\
(11,1)&0.9030&2.8370&4.31&\mathrm S
\end{array}
$$
**All eleven listed tubes are semiconducting in the zone-folding classification.** Curvature can open small gaps in some nominally metallic non-armchair tubes, but none of these indices falls in that nominal metallic class.

Diameter gives a useful inverse-size trend in transition energies, and the full chiral indices constrain the <electronic band structure> much more strongly. They do not by themselves give the perceived solution colour through a simple geometrical rule: one needs the visible <absorption spectrum>, transition strengths and widths, <exciton>/environment shifts, <concentration>, optical path and illumination. The observed transmitted colour is complementary to the absorbed spectrum, not a direct label for the <band gap>. Structure-assigned excitation–emission measurements provide an experimental connection between chiral structure and optical transitions.<Structure-assigned optical spectra of single-walled carbon nanotubes by Sergei M. Bachilo and others>[Science 298 (2002), pp. 2361–2366, structure assignment of semiconducting-tube optical transitions]{ref}

One application is a **semiconducting nanotube transistor array**. Removing metallic tubes prevents conducting bridges that would bypass gate control; a narrow chirality distribution also narrows the spread of <band gaps> and switching behaviour. High <charge carrier mobility> and thin flexible channels are attractive, but <contact resistance>, tube alignment, residual metallic contamination, <dielectric> traps and the cost/yield of sorting still limit uniform large-area circuits.

A second is a **wavelength-selective near-infrared <photodetector> or emitter**. A selected chirality concentrates absorption and emission around its characteristic <nanotube optical transitions>, avoiding the broad mixed-tube response. Optical selectivity can be tailored by choosing the species. Limitations include low absolute emission yield, dark <exciton> states, contact-related recombination, environmental spectral shifts and quenching by bundles or metallic contaminants. Optical and electrical characterization after device processing is needed; the sorting label alone does not guarantee device performance.

Prepare size-characterized gold-particle ensembles, heat them on a calibrated in-situ <electron diffraction> stage, and follow the loss of sharp fcc <diffraction> features and appearance of liquid-like diffuse scattering. Measure the <particle size distribution> rather than treating a polydisperse ensemble as one diameter. Temperature-dependent weakening of a <diffraction> peak alone is insufficient: thermal vibrations, particle motion, coalescence, evaporation and beam damage can also reduce intensity. Low-dose repeats, heating/cooling runs, size checks before and after heating, a bulk-like reference and support-dependent controls help distinguish <melting> from those effects. <Electron diffraction> measurements on <gold> particles down to about 2 nm are a classic demonstration.<Size effect on the melting temperature of gold particles by Ph. Buffat and J-P. Borel>[Physical Review A 13 (1976), pp. 2287–2298, abstract: scanning diffraction and size-distribution analysis]{ref}

The physical origin is the large interface contribution to <free energy> at small radius. A curved solid–liquid interface raises the solid's <thermodynamic equilibrium> <chemical potential> relative to the flat-interface case, so coexistence occurs below the bulk <melting> <temperature>. Reduced coordination and surface disorder provide a microscopic interpretation, but the quantitative continuum relation is a <free energy> balance, not simply a claim that every surface atom has a universal lower <melting> point.

Let $g_l-g_s$ be the liquid-minus-solid <free energy> density at the same <temperature>. Near the bulk coexistence <temperature> $T_m$, linearization gives
$$
g_l-g_s\simeq\Delta s_V(T_m-T)
=\frac{\Delta H_V}{T_m}\Delta T.
$$
For a sphere, an interface displacement changes area and volume with $dA/dV=2/r$. Equality of the bulk driving force and the capillary cost at a curved solid–liquid interface therefore gives $g_l-g_s=2\gamma/r^*$. Equivalently, differentiating $4\pi r^2\gamma-(4\pi/3)r^3(g_l-g_s)$ gives the same stationary radius. Combining the expressions yields
$$
\boxed{\Delta T=\frac{2\gamma T_m}{r^*\Delta H_V}.}
$$
This form of the <Gibbs-Thomson effect> assumes isotropic interfacial energy, a spherical interface, bulk-like <latent heat> per volume and a near-coexistence <free energy> expansion. More detailed free-particle models must distinguish solid–vapor, liquid–vapor and solid–liquid energies.

The map shows **<Coulomb blockade> with discrete single-electron charging**: low-current diamonds separate charge-stable regions from finite-bias transport windows. A small conducting island or <quantum dot> must be tunnel-coupled to source and drain, with a gate shifting its <electrochemical potential>. Charging prevents sequential transport until an island addition/removal energy enters the <bias voltage> window.

The tunnel resistances should substantially exceed the <resistance quantum> $h/e^2\simeq25.8\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$, preventing charge fluctuations from washing out integer island occupancy, while still permitting a measurable current. <Temperature> and tunnel broadening must be small compared with the addition-energy scale. Here the smaller diamonds imply $e^2/C_\Sigma\simeq2.5\ \mathrm{meV}$, so $k_BT\ll2.5\ \mathrm{meV}$, or $T\ll29\ \mathrm K$; resolving the inferred 1 meV orbital structure requires the stricter scale $T\ll12\ \mathrm K$. Stray <capacitance> and unintended multiple islands can complicate this simple interpretation.

Assume the usual one-lead-biased measurement, $V_s=V_{sd}$ with drain grounded and the gate referred to that ground; the printed map does not separately specify the wiring. This assumption is needed to assign individual source/drain capacitances. In this <Coulomb diamond capacitance extraction>, the smaller zero-bias gate intervals are 5 mV wide. In the <constant interaction model> with no orbital gap between those additions,
$$
C_g=\frac e{\Delta V_g}=\frac{1.602\times10^{-19}}{5\times10^{-3}}
=32.04\ \mathrm{aF}.
$$
An upper diamond tip lies at gate 123.75 mV between the zero-bias vertices 120 and 125 mV and at bias 2.50 mV. Its positive and negative edge slopes are therefore $s_+=2.50/3.75=2/3$ and $s_-=-2.50/1.25=-2$. Reservoir alignment gives
$$
s_-=-\frac{C_g}{C_s},\qquad
s_+=\frac{C_g}{C_\Sigma-C_s}=\frac{C_g}{C_g+C_d}.
$$
The <gate capacitance> is not negligible in the second denominator. Consequently
$$
\boxed{C_s\simeq16.0\ \mathrm{aF},\quad C_d\simeq16.0\ \mathrm{aF},\quad
C_g\simeq32.0\ \mathrm{aF},\quad C_\Sigma\simeq64.1\ \mathrm{aF}.}
$$
The consistency check is $e^2/C_\Sigma=2.50\ \mathrm{meV}$, matching the smaller tip's bias energy. The conventional electrostatic <charging energy> $E_C=e^2/(2C_\Sigma)$ is 1.25 meV; it is half the <charging energy> contribution to the <addition energy>. A different applied-bias convention changes the slope formulas, so the convention must accompany the extraction.

The <addition energy> has both charging and orbital terms,
$$
E_{\rm add}(N)=\frac{e^2}{C_\Sigma}+\epsilon_{N+1}-\epsilon_N.
$$
The larger diamonds indicate <quantum dot shell closure> after **2, 6 and 12 <electrons>**. A <two-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator> confinement, $V(x,y)=m^*\omega_0^2(x^2+y^2)/2$, explains that sequence: shell $q=n_x+n_y$ has $q+1$ orbital states and capacity $2(q+1)$ after <spin degeneracy>. The cumulative capacities are $(q+1)(q+2)=2,6,12,20,\ldots$. Within a degenerate shell, the extra orbital term vanishes to this approximation; crossing a shell boundary costs an extra $\hbar\omega_0$.

The <gate capacitance> lever arm is $\alpha_g=C_g/C_\Sigma=1/2$. Large and small gate widths differ by $7-5=2\ \mathrm{mV}$, so **$\hbar\omega_0\simeq e\alpha_g(2\ \mathrm{mV})=1.0\ \mathrm{meV}$**. Equivalently the tip energies differ by $3.5-2.5=1.0\ \mathrm{meV}$. Confinement anisotropy, exchange and disorder would split the ideal degeneracies; the diagram supports approximate shell symmetry rather than proving a perfectly parabolic potential.

For an isolated easy-axis <ferromagnet> undergoing coherent reversal, the ideal low-temperature loop is approximately rectangular, switching between $\pm M_s$ at $\pm H_c$. It has remanent <Magnetization> and a large response near switching. An ideal compensated <antiferromagnet> has essentially no <remanence> and a small, approximately reversible linear net <Magnetization> $M=\chi_{\rm AF}H$ at fields far below its exchange/spin-flop scales. Strong antiferromagnetic exchange makes substantial canting costly, so its low-field <magnetic susceptibility> is small relative to the <ferromagnet>'s unsaturated switching response.

This comparison does not say the <ferromagnet> has a larger differential slope at every field: on its saturated branches $dM/dH$ can also be small. Nor does it rule out spin-flop transitions at much larger fields, outside the sketched range. The original plot uses arbitrary normalized scales.

Heat the bilayer into a window **$T_N<T<T_c$**, apply an in-plane <magnetic field> sufficient to align the <ferromagnet>, and cool through the <antiferromagnet>'s ordering/blocking range while retaining the field. The <ferromagnet> supplies a preferred interfacial spin direction as the <antiferromagnet> orders. Sufficient antiferromagnetic anisotropy then pins part of the interfacial spin configuration, producing <unidirectional magnetic anisotropy> rather than merely <uniaxial magnetic anisotropy> in the <ferromagnet>.

Measure a full major <hysteresis> loop after removing the cooling field, and compare with the isolated <ferromagnet>, a zero-field-cooled bilayer and the result of reversing the cooling-field direction. Those controls separate a true loop-centre shift from a changed coercive width, minor-loop artefacts or trapped applied field. The effect requires appropriate interfacial exchange and stable pinning; an ideally compensated or freely rotating interface need not produce a large bias. Exchange anisotropy in coupled ferromagnetic/antiferromagnetic material was demonstrated in the classic experiment below.<New Magnetic Anisotropy by W. H. Meiklejohn and C. P. Write the descending and ascending switching fields as $H_-$ and $H_+$. The <exchange bias> loop centre and <magnetic coercivity> half-width are
$$
\boxed{H_E=\frac{H_++H_-}{2},\qquad H_c=\frac{H_+-H_-}{2}.}
$$
With <exchange bias>, a field-cooled bilayer has $H_E\ne0$, often a shift opposite to the cooling field for the conventional negative-bias case shown in the diagram. The sign is not universal; it depends on interfacial coupling and preparation. A simple rigid-pinning estimate is $H_E\sim-J_{\rm int}/(\mu_0M_st_F)$.

Cooling farther below the blocking range usually strengthens antiferromagnetic anisotropy and suppresses thermal relaxation, so the magnitude of the shift and often the coercive width grow. Close to or above the blocking <temperature> the shift disappears; the blocking <temperature> can be below $T_N$. Repeated cycling can rearrange interfacial spins and reduce the bias, and the two reversal branches can acquire different shapes. Thus increasing low-temperature asymmetry is a common tendency, not a guaranteed monotonic law for every interface.

For this <self-assembly> into a <colloidal crystal>, four useful elements are **building blocks, interactions, environment and reversible dynamics**. The building blocks here are nearly monodisperse <polymer> spheres; their shape and size distribution constrain the accessible packing. Their interactions include <excluded volume>, <Van der Waals forces> and electrostatic or <steric stabilization>, which set the <free energy> landscape. The aqueous environment supplies <temperature>, <viscosity>, <ionic strength>, confinement and <concentration> controls. Finally, <Brownian motion> and rotation must let particles encounter one another and rearrange or detach from incorrect contacts. Attractions that bind every collision irreversibly favour an amorphous aggregate rather than an annealed <colloidal crystal>. These four categories include both thermodynamic selection and the transport/kinetic access needed to realize it.

A useful microscopic screening estimate compares <thermal energy> with the buoyant gravitational <potential energy> over one particle radius. Write $\Delta\rho=50\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and $m_b=(4\pi/3)a^3\Delta\rho$. The <sedimentation length> is $\ell_g=k_BT/(m_bg)$, and the <gravitational Peclet number> is
$$
\mathrm{Pe}_g=\frac{m_bga}{k_BT}
=\frac{4\pi\Delta\rho ga^4}{3k_BT}.
$$
Using $T=298\ \mathrm K$ and requiring $\mathrm{Pe}_g\lesssim1$ gives
$$
\boxed{a\lesssim\left(\frac{3k_BT}{4\pi\Delta\rho g}\right)^{1/4}
=1.19\ \mathrm{\mu m},\qquad d\lesssim2.4\ \mathrm{\mu m}.}
$$
Equivalently, combining <Stokes law> speed $v_s=2\Delta\rho ga^2/(9\eta)$ and <diffusion coefficient> $D=k_BT/(6\pi\eta a)$ gives $v_sa/D=\mathrm{Pe}_g$. <Buoyancy>, not the full <polystyrene> density, is essential to the estimate. Taking a diameter rather than radius as the comparison length changes the numerical cutoff by an order-one factor.

There is **no universal largest particle that stays uniformly suspended indefinitely**. If uniformity over a vessel height $H$ is required, use $\ell_g\gtrsim H$, not merely $\ell_g\gtrsim a$. For example $H=1\ \mathrm{cm}$ gives a much smaller radius, about 58 nm. Observation time, density matching, convection, particle <polydispersity> and shape also matter. Independent of <sedimentation>, electrostatic/steric barriers must prevent aggregation; low <ionic strength>, suitable surface chemistry and clean <solvent> can be as important as size.

Increase <particle volume fraction> slowly by controlled evaporation or gentle <concentration> until ordering becomes favourable, while preserving sufficient colloidal repulsion to prevent irreversible sticking. Hard-sphere-like monodisperse suspensions can crystallize by packing <entropy>; weak, reversible attractions can also favour ordered assembly if they allow defect correction. A controlled drying front or patterned surface may guide the orientation, and gentle <sedimentation> can create a <concentration> gradient that crosses the ordering range.

The practical requirement is **slow structural change relative to <Brownian motion>**, with uniform <temperature>, well-characterized <ionic strength> and a narrow size distribution. An abrupt salt addition or rapid drying can remove the repulsive barrier and trap aggregates instead of growing a <crystal>. Scattering or microscopy should distinguish crystalline peaks/order from merely increased turbidity.

<Flocculation> indicates that the attraction/kinetic balance has changed: <electrolyte> may screen a double-layer barrier; <pH> may reduce surface charge; multivalent ions can promote aggregation; added <polymer> can bridge particles or cause <depletion force>; <poor solvent> conditions can collapse stabilizing layers. Rapid evaporation concentrates both particles and salts and can force contacts faster than they anneal. Aggregates then sediment much faster because their effective buoyant size has grown.

Check <ionic strength>, <pH>, surface charge and the <particle size distribution> before and during assembly. Dialyse or reduce salt where appropriate, restore surface charge, add a nonbridging <steric stabilization> with a compatible <solvent>, and slow the <concentration> step. Compare untreated and deliberately salt-screened controls. Restoring repulsion after irreversible aggregation may not redisperse the particles, so prevention is preferable to assuming all settled material can be recovered.

Three suitable examples are **<cubes> bounded mainly by $\{100\}$ <facets>, <octahedra> bounded by $\{111\}$ <facets>, and <truncated octahedra> bounded by both $\{111\}$ and $\{100\}$ <facets>**. These are crystallographically distinct shapes rather than three arbitrary rounded particle outlines.

At fixed volume the <thermodynamic equilibrium> shape minimizes $G_{\rm surf}=\sum_i\gamma_iA_i$, with an edge/corner correction when particles are very small. In the <Wulff construction>, <facet> support-plane distances scale as their interfacial free energies. <Silver>'s clean low-index energies, selective <adsorption> of <ligands> or ions, <solvent> <chemical potential> and possible support contact alter those energies. <Crystal twinning> and elastic <strain> can add further costs or stabilize multiply twinned forms. A face with slow attachment kinetics can also survive during growth even when that morphology is not the <thermodynamic equilibrium> Wulff shape. <Homogeneous nucleation> describes where nuclei form; it does not guarantee an <thermodynamic equilibrium> final shape.

In a <polyol process>, a hot polyol such as <ethylene glycol> acts as <solvent> and reductant for a <silver> precursor. <Nucleation> creates seeds; controlled <reduction> supplies atoms for their subsequent growth, while <polyvinylpyrrolidone> <adsorption> and seed <crystal twinning> favour anisotropic growth. For the common <silver nanowire> route, a fivefold-twinned seed can develop into a pentagonal wire with predominantly $\{100\}$ side faces and growth at less-passivated ends. Cross-sectional microscopy and <diffraction> of microtomed wires provide evidence for that twinned structure rather than an inference from a long outline alone.<Polyol Synthesis of Uniform Silver Nanowires: A Plausible Growth Mechanism and the Supporting Evidence by Yugang Sun and others>[Nano Letters 3 (2003), pp. 955–960, pentagonal cross sections and fivefold twinning]{ref}

The <aspect ratio> depends on seed population and twin survival, relative end/side growth rates, precursor feed and <reduction> rate, <PVP> coverage, <temperature>, trace halides, dissolved oxygen and growth time. High <aspect ratio> requires a suitable population of twinned seeds, suppression of competing particle <nucleation> and continued atom supply while sidewall thickening is restrained. These variables must be controlled together; adding more capping <polymer> alone is not a universal recipe. Advantages are solution processing, substantial yield and tunable dimensions without an etched hard template. Disadvantages include sensitive batch reproducibility, particle by-products, residual insulating <ligand>, purification, oxidation/tarnish and <nanowire junction resistance> in later films. Compare seed/capping/halide controls using microscopy and size distributions, not only solution colour.

A transparent metallic network must span the patterned electrode with many redundant current paths while obscuring little area. For random rods, number density times squared wire length is a useful <percolation> measure; greater <aspect ratio> generally reaches a connected network at lower coverage. Conductivity also depends on intrinsic metal resistivity, wire cross section, orientation, length distribution, wire–wire <contact resistance> and the number of junctions along each spanning path. Insulating capping layers can make <nanowire junction resistance> dominate even when the individual wires are excellent conductors.

Near the connectivity threshold, small density changes or wire breaks produce large resistance changes. For a narrow stripe, finite-size effects and orientation matter: a macroscopic sheet-average <percolation> result does not ensure a reliable 100 µm-wide path. Optical performance depends on coverage, metal extinction, wire diameter, aggregation, <optical haze>, substrate transmission and any binder. Increasing wire density lowers resistance but usually lowers transmission. <Welding>, pressing or suitable embedding can improve contacts with less additional optical coverage than simply adding metal.<Self-limited plasmonic welding of silver nanowire junctions by Erik C. Garnett and others>[Nature Materials 11 (2012), pp. 241–249, junction-localized welding mechanism]{ref}

Three credible architectures are **a welded <silver nanowire> network; a thin <silver nanowire> network with a <conducting polymer> overcoat; and a nanocrystalline <silver> grid formed from <nanoparticle ink>**. <Silver> provides low intrinsic resistivity. A sparse long-wire network can be both flexible and highly transmitting if its junctions are well connected. A thin, optically weak <conducting polymer> overcoat can bridge junctions and smooth the surface, provided its added absorption and moisture sensitivity are controlled. A fine grid offers a more directly designed optical/electrical trade-off and does not rely on operating near random <percolation>.

For a quantitative grid feasibility screen, take pitch $p=5\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, line width $w=100\ \mathrm{nm}$ and metal thickness $t=200\ \mathrm{nm}$. An opaque-line square-grid estimate gives $T_{\rm grid}\simeq(1-w/p)^2=0.9604$. With a transmitting <polymer> substrate of 0.95, the combined estimate is 0.912. The <sheet resistance> is approximately $R_\square=\rho_{\rm eff}p/(wt)$. Even an effective <silver> resistivity four times $1.6\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\mathrm m$ gives **$R_\square\simeq16\ \Omega$**, with a coating thinner than 400 nm. There are about 20 mesh periods across the stripe, making the finite-width design more credible than a pitch comparable with its whole width.

These are feasible material/process choices, not guaranteed specifications for every film carrying those names. A <two-dimensional electron gas> is confined into discrete subbands perpendicular to an interface while retaining nearly free motion in its plane. It is not a literal zero-thickness <electron> sheet. In a <GaAs>/<AlGaAs> <semiconductor heterostructure>, the <band offset> and self-consistent <band bending> confine <electrons> on the <GaAs> side. Donors in <AlGaAs> can supply <electrons> while an undoped spacer separates them from the ionized dopants, reducing impurity scattering. This modulation-doping arrangement yields a high-mobility channel, with the lowest confinement subband occupied when the confinement spacing exceeds the Fermi and thermal scales. Gates tune the density or locally deplete the channel into constrictions. Low-temperature Hall and longitudinal measurements test the resulting sheet density and transport quality.

For a parabolic <two-dimensional electron gas> with one valley and two spin states, the <density of states> per area is $g_{2D}=m^*/(\pi\hbar^2)$. Integrating from the subband edge to the zero-temperature <Fermi energy> gives $n_s=g_{2D}E_F$, hence

\boxed{E_F=\frac{\pi\hbar^2n_s}{m^*}=1.716\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm J

Here $m^*=0.067(9.109\times10^{-31})=6.103\times10^{-32}\ \mathrm{kg}$, and the energy is measured from the occupied subband bottom. Equivalently $k_F=\sqrt{2\pi n_s}=1.3722\times10^8\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$. Therefore

This is **<transverse magnetic focusing>**. The depleted gates form reflecting boundaries and two constrictions. A narrow <quantum point contact> collimates carriers into cyclotron trajectories with <cyclotron radius> $r_c=m^*v_F/(e|B|)=\hbar k_F/(e|B|)$. When an integer number of semicircular skipping arcs links the two openings, the collector couples particularly strongly to the injector population. A floating voltage contact then changes its <electrochemical potential> to satisfy zero net current, producing a peak in the measured nonlocal voltage. Sweeping the field changes the arc diameter, moving the focused population into or away from the collector.

The connection condition is $L=2jr_c$ for integer $j\ge1$, with the higher orders requiring specular reflections. The effect needs <ballistic electron transport>, sufficiently narrow openings and a smooth boundary. <Electron phase coherence> can add interference structure, but the basic geometrical focusing condition is semiclassical.<Coherent electron focusing with quantum point contacts in a two-dimensional electron gas by H. van Houten and others>[Physical Review B 39 (1989), pp. 8556–8575, focusing geometry and peak structure]{ref}

There is a polarity qualification in the printed geometry. The <electron> charge is negative: for a field out of the page, an <electron> launched upward through the left opening bends left, not toward the right opening. A direct left-opening-to-right-opening focusing path in the upper region therefore requires the field into the page; the printed outward field supports the reverse connection. Reversing conventional current reverses the occupation perturbation and voltage sign, but does not reverse the curvature of an individual <electron>. Thus the standard injector-to-collector explanation describes the focusing mechanism, while the asserted signed voltage increase requires a consistent field/lead convention. The calculated results below are peak-field magnitudes; taking outward field positive gives negative fields for direct left-to-right electron injection.

<Brownian motion> is the irregular thermal displacement produced by fluctuating molecular forces from the surrounding liquid. In the overdamped regime its long-time displacement is diffusive, with <mean squared displacement> proportional to time. It brings nanoscale building blocks into contact and lets them sample orientations and escape weak incorrect contacts. Interparticle forces bias this exploration toward lower-free-energy structures; <thermal motion> alone does not select a particular <crystal>. Strong irreversible attraction, high <viscosity> or glassy arrest can stop the defect correction needed for successful <self-assembly>.

= ii
{parent=22}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For a no-slip spherical particle in a <Newtonian fluid> at low <Reynolds number>, <Stokes law> gives $\zeta=6\pi\eta a$. The <fluctuation-dissipation theorem> then gives the <Stokes–Einstein relation> $D=k_BT/\zeta$. Using the conventional <radial Brownian displacement> in three dimensions,
$$
\boxed{d_{\rm rms}=\sqrt{\langle|\Delta\mathbf r|^2\rangle}
=\sqrt{6Dt}=\sqrt{\frac{k_BTt}{\pi\eta a}}.}
$$
Here $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant>, $T$ absolute <temperature>, $\eta$ <dynamic viscosity>, $a$ particle radius and $t$ elapsed time. Larger thermal forcing raises <diffusion>; larger viscous drag reduces it. The formula requires times long relative to momentum relaxation and neglects walls, interactions and external drift.

The word “average” needs a statistic: the vector mean is zero, while the mean radial displacement for Gaussian three-dimensional <diffusion> is $\langle|\Delta\mathbf r|\rangle=4\sqrt{Dt/\pi}$, not $\sqrt{6Dt}$. Both scale as $t^{1/2}$. Neither is the total length of a microscopic random path.

In the imposed <free-draining polymer friction> model, the drag coefficients of the $N$ <monomers> add, giving $\zeta_1=N(6\pi\eta a)$ and $D_1=k_BT/(6\pi\eta aN)$. Taking the same three-dimensional centre-of-mass RMS convention as part (ii),
$$
\boxed{d_1=\sqrt{6D_1t}=\sqrt{\frac{k_BTt}{\pi\eta aN}}.}
$$
The <polymer> is a one-dimensional connected contour, but that alone does not restrict its <centre of mass> to one spatial axis. If the intended coordinate is instead literal one-dimensional curvilinear motion along an entanglement tube, its RMS contour displacement is $\sqrt{2D_1t}=\sqrt{k_BTt/(3\pi\eta aN)}$. That is a different coordinate and should not be compared with a three-dimensional displacement without stating the conversion/model.

Additive local drag is the free-draining <Rouse model> approximation. It neglects long-range solvent-mediated <hydrodynamic interactions> and is not a complete theory of an entangled chain. In a tube/<reptation> description, curvilinear <diffusion> can scale as $N^{-1}$ while long-time spatial centre-of-mass <diffusion> scales as $N^{-2}$ after the chain renews its tube. Conversely a dilute unentangled coil can exhibit <Zimm model> hydrodynamic coupling. <Monomer> motion, centre-of-mass motion and contour sliding therefore cannot all be identified with the same displacement law.

At the theta condition, effective <excluded volume> approximately cancel and a freely jointed <ideal chain> has $\langle R^2\rangle=Nb^2$. With the stipulated segment length $b=2a$,
$$
\boxed{R_{\rm rms,\theta}=2a\sqrt N.}

In a <good solvent> repulsive <excluded volume> swells the coil, giving $R\sim bN^\nu$ with $\nu\simeq0.588$ in three dimensions, or the <Flory approximation> $\nu=3/5$. The <Flory interaction parameter> $\chi_{AB}$ measures the effective <free energy> penalty for unlike A–B contacts relative to like contacts, and $N$ is the total number of statistical segments per chain. Their product is the segregation strength: contact-energy preference competes with the <conformational entropy> cost of separating covalently joined blocks. $N$ does not grow during <solvent> evaporation. For an approximately nonselective <solvent>, increasing <polymer> <concentration> instead increases the effective importance of A–B contacts, so a roughly fixed-composition path moves from weak toward stronger effective segregation.

The diagram's labels are **DIS: disordered; S: <block-copolymer sphere phase>; H: <hexagonal cylinder block-copolymer phase>; G: <double gyroid>; L: <lamellar microphase>**. At $f_A=0.15$, A is the minority component and B forms the continuous matrix. Reading the supplied diagram vertically at this composition gives disorder initially, a sphere region when segregation first becomes sufficient, and a cylinder region if sufficiently strong segregation is reached within the displayed range. The thin gyroid and central lamellar regions are not the <thermodynamic equilibrium> path at this composition. The dry endpoint cannot be fixed numerically without the final $\chi_{AB}N$ value. The cited theoretical calculation determines <thermodynamic equilibrium> phase stability, not a universal evaporation trajectory.<Stability of the Gyroid Phase in Diblock Copolymers at Strong Segregation by Eric W. Cochran and others>[Macromolecules 39 (2006), pp. 2449–2451, equilibrium diblock phase stability]{ref}

Crucially, at least one block is glassy at room <temperature>. <Solvent> can initially plasticize it, but <solvent> loss can raise its effective glass-transition <temperature> above the processing <temperature>. Chain relaxation then becomes too slow to equilibrate. Even slow evaporation can therefore freeze disorder, spheres, cylinders or another transient morphology before the <thermodynamic equilibrium> dry state is reached. <Solvent> <concentration>, <annealing> time and segmental mobility must be followed alongside the <phase diagram>.

One explanation is **<block-copolymer kinetic trapping>**.
