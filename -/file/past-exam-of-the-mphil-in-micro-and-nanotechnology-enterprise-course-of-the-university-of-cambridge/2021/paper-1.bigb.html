**The recorded profile has approximately 30 nm deep, flat-bottomed depressions with broadened, sloping edges.** The full-height regions are the inorganic surface; the lower plateaux are the infiltrated <perovskite structure>. With adequate <feedback control>, <imaging trace and retrace> should agree when plotted against the same laboratory position.

The tip has a finite opening angle. Its flank contacts a pore rim before its apex can follow an ideal vertical wall, so <tip convolution> makes a narrow depression appear narrower and its sidewall less steep. For a flank angle $\alpha$ measured from the vertical, the horizontal distance needed to descend a height $h$ is $h\tan\alpha$. Along the <cantilever>, the nominal front and back values give
$$
\boxed{30\tan25^\circ\simeq14.0\ \mathrm{nm},\qquad
30\tan15^\circ\simeq8.0\ \mathrm{nm}.}
$$
Thus the two edges can have different apparent slopes. Across the <cantilever>, the nominal side angle instead gives about $12.4$ nm on each side. The scan orientation relative to the <cantilever> must therefore be specified before choosing the relevant pair of slopes.

Even a 100 nm wide pore leaves ample room for the tip apex to reach a flat bottom: in the along-cantilever idealization its bottom plateau is about $100-14-8=78$ nm wide. A 200 nm pore gives about 178 nm. The tip height is far greater than the 30 nm step, so it does not prevent access. An unreported finite apex radius adds edge rounding; its size cannot be inferred from the listed flank angles.

Reducing the <feedback gain> increases the distance traveled before the vertical actuator responds to a change in height. A simple illustrative response is
$$
\tau\frac{dz_m}{dt}+z_m=z_g[x(t)],
$$
where $z_g$ is the accessible, tip-convolved profile and $z_m$ is the recorded actuator height. At scan speed $v$, the characteristic spatial lag is **$v\tau$**.

On a left-to-right trace, descent into a pore is late: the tip initially remains too high and may lose contact. At the opposite rim it also rises late, increasing the contact force before the actuator catches up. The lower plateau may be shortened or not reached, making the depression appear too shallow. The whole response is displaced towards larger $x$.

On the right-to-left retrace the same temporal lag produces the opposite displacement in laboratory coordinates. Descent is now delayed at the right-hand edge and ascent at the left-hand edge. **<imaging trace and retrace> separate most strongly near height changes, with opposite spatial shifts and rounded transitions.** The central low-gain curves in the figure show this behavior on common $x$ axes. Reduced gain alone does not require oscillatory ringing; the first-order lag already explains the principal difference.

<friction force microscopy> records <cantilever> torsion caused by lateral tip–sample force. Choose the detector sign so that <friction> on a flat region contributes positively during a left-to-right trace. On a common laboratory-position axis, write the two signals schematically as
$$
S_+(x)=B+G(x)+F_f(x),\qquad
S_-(x)=B+G(x)-F_f(x).
$$
Here $B$ is an offset and $G$ represents a slope-related lateral-force or detector cross-talk contribution. A reversed electronic convention reverses all plotted signs without changing the physical conclusions.

At the same controlled <normal contact force> $N$, the flat-region contribution is approximately $F_f=\mu N$. **The lower <perovskite structure> plateaux give a larger trace–retrace separation than the upper inorganic surface**, because their <coefficient of kinetic friction> is larger. Genuine <friction> reverses sign with scan direction, so a larger <friction> magnitude means a higher positive trace and a more negative retrace in this convention.

Sloped edges produce additional peaks or shoulders. The normal reaction to a slope has a lateral component, and the finite tip flank can contact the rim, so edge contrast is not a direct map of the local <friction> coefficient. With the two scans registered and load control maintained,
$$
\frac{S_+-S_-}{2}\simeq F_f,\qquad
\frac{S_++S_-}{2}\simeq B+G.
$$
The half-difference therefore emphasizes <friction> while the half-sum diagnoses common geometrical contributions. Real <contact mechanics> and imperfect registration can prevent exact cancellation. The bottom panel of the figure shows these two kinds of contrast separately.

**Deposit a dilute dispersion onto a clean, electron-transparent <TEM> support and let the <ethanol> evaporate.** A practical sequence is to redisperse gently, dilute enough to reduce overlap, place a small drop on a holey or lacey support grid, wick away excess liquid and dry thoroughly. Choose support chemistry compatible with wetting by <ethanol>.

Look for flakes spanning support holes, where no support film obscures the light-element lattice. Adjacent supported regions help locate and stabilize the flakes. Dilution reduces accidental multilayer stacks, while a clean grid and clean <solvent> reduce hydrocarbon background. Gentle agitation may be useful; prolonged aggressive sonication can itself change the defects one intends to measure.

Drying prevents a volatile liquid from entering the microscope <vacuum>. Additional cleaning must be chosen cautiously because heating or energetic <plasma> treatment can alter <oxygen> groups and defects in <reduced graphene oxide>. Record a low-dose survey before atomic-resolution imaging, and distinguish pre-existing defects from changes induced during acquisition.

**Use low-voltage, preferably aberration-corrected <HRTEM>, with about 60–80 kV as a defensible operating range.** An approximately 80 kV starting point balances useful lattice resolution against <carbon> displacement damage; use the lower end if the instrument still resolves the lattice and defect stability is the priority.

A higher accelerating <voltage> reduces the <electron> <wavelength>, but can remove <carbon> atoms by <knock-on displacement>. Measurements on pristine single-layer <graphene> show a strong increase in displacement probability above the vicinity of 80 kV, so simply selecting a conventional 200–300 kV setting is a poor way to preserve the original lattice.<Accurate Measurement of Electron Beam Induced Displacement Cross Sections for Single-Layer Graphene by Jannik C. Meyer and others>[Beam-energy dependence of displacement cross sections, Physical Review Letters 108, 196102]{ref}

<reduced graphene oxide> is already defective and chemically modified. Its vulnerable sites need not share the pristine-graphene threshold, and <ionization>, contamination and chemical etching remain possible at low <voltage>. Minimize dose, spread the beam while aligning and compare successive short exposures. **The <voltage> choice suppresses one damage mechanism; it does not guarantee damage-free imaging.**

**Atomic-resolution contrast is primarily <electron phase contrast>.** <elastic scattering> by the specimen potential changes the phase of the transmitted <electron> wave. <interference> between transmitted and diffracted components, modified by the objective lens, converts that phase change into intensity variations. Appropriate <defocus>, sufficiently small <optical aberrations>, good <spatial optical coherence> and transfer of the <graphene> <spatial frequencies> are needed. At exact focus an ideal weak phase object need not give a strong visible lattice.

Mass–thickness or amplitude contrast can also distinguish thicker overlap regions, folds, contamination and support film, especially when scattered <electrons> are excluded by an aperture. It is comparatively weak for a clean monolayer of low-$Z$ <carbon>. <diffraction contrast> becomes important in crystalline, multilayer or differently oriented regions when Bragg-scattered beams are selected or excluded. Relative rotation of layers can additionally produce a <moiré pattern>.

A dark region alone is not a reliable layer count: <electron phase contrast> can change sign with <defocus>, and contamination also changes intensity. Use fold-edge fringes, <diffraction> information and calibrated image simulations or thickness-sensitive measurements together. Defect identification should likewise compare a structural model with the actual imaging conditions, rather than reading every dark lattice site as a missing atom.

**Aberration-corrected <scanning transmission electron microscopy> with annular dark-field imaging and spatially resolved <EELS>** can locate the Si atoms and test their chemical environment. <silicon> scatters much more strongly to high angles than <carbon>, so an isolated Si impurity can stand out in a thin <graphene> lattice. Neighboring <carbon> positions reveal whether a site is, for example, three- or four-coordinated in projection.

Acquire a spectrum image at the same time. A <silicon> core-loss edge identifies the element, while its <energy-loss near-edge structure> contains information about the unoccupied states and bonding. This combination has experimentally distinguished individual Si coordination environments in <graphene>.<Direct Determination of the Chemical Bonding of Individual Impurities in Graphene by Wu Zhou and others>[Figures 1–2, atomic-resolution imaging and Si-edge spectra]{ref}

Advantages are atom-scale spatial localization, simultaneous structural and chemical evidence, and stronger chemical identification than brightness alone. Limitations include low counts at one atom, background subtraction, finite <energy> resolution, drift and the high dose needed for a useful spectrum. The beam can move or damage an impurity, and a two-dimensional projection does not by itself determine its out-of-plane geometry. Compare spectra and images with plausible structural calculations rather than assigning coordination solely from one bright pixel.

**The resist is positive tone**, because the written regions are the regions removed during development. A suitable example is <poly(methyl methacrylate)>, conventionally <PMMA>, used in its ordinary positive-tone electron-beam exposure regime. = Solution

The deposited <energy> is a blurred, nonlocal version of the commanded write pattern. Forward scattering spreads <energy> within the resist; <backscattered electrons> from the substrate contribute a wider background; <secondary electrons> and the finite incident probe add further spreading. Development then removes regions whose accumulated dose exceeds the effective clearing requirement.

Line ends and isolated short rectangles receive less supporting exposure from neighboring written regions. Their ends can recede and round, and isolated features may shrink to narrow openings. Dense features receive more overlapping scattered dose. Thus **one global dose cannot generally reproduce both isolated and dense shapes faithfully**.

Use <electron beam proximity correction>: calibrate a scattering point-spread model for the actual resist, thickness, substrate and beam <energy>, then adjust local dwell time or beam <electric current>. Increase or reshape the write dose at underexposed isolated features and line ends, while compensating for the excess background in dense regions. Geometric prebias, such as modest line-end extension, can complement dose correction. A thinner resist and an appropriate higher beam <energy> may reduce forward-scattering blur, but the backscatter distribution must still be included. Correction must be based on the developed result, rather than assuming the incident beam diameter is the only resolution limit.

**A transmissive mask is placed in contact with the resist, or a small distance above it for proximity printing.** Chromium-patterned glass or quartz is a typical mask. Illumination is normally collimated <ultraviolet> light, with refractive illumination optics used to prepare the beam.

There is no projection objective imaging a reduced mask pattern onto the resist: the mask shadows the resist approximately one-to-one. The finite gap in proximity printing introduces <diffraction> and limits edge sharpness; contact printing reduces that gap at the cost of mask wear and contamination risk.

**Use multilayer reflective optics and a reflective patterned mask.** Near the usual <extreme ultraviolet> <wavelength> of 13.5 nm, ordinary lenses and thick transmissive masks absorb too strongly. Reading the straight-line extrapolation in the printed <photoresist contrast> curve gives approximately $D_0=20\ \mathrm{mJ\,cm^{-2}}$ at unit remaining thickness and $D_c=100\ \mathrm{mJ\,cm^{-2}}$ at clearing. On the logarithmic dose axis,
$$
\boxed{\gamma=\frac1{\log_{10}(D_c/D_0)}
=\frac1{\log_{10}5}\simeq1.43.}
$$
The critical image modulation follows by requiring one exposure time to give $D_{\max}\geq D_c$ in a bright region and $D_{\min}\leq D_0$ in a dark region:
$$
\frac{I_{\max}}{I_{\min}}\geq\frac{D_c}{D_0}.
$$
For unit object modulation, this means
$$
\boxed{M_c=\frac{D_c-D_0}{D_c+D_0}=\frac{100-20}{100+20}\simeq0.667.}
$$
The doses are graphical estimates, so extra significant figures would not represent greater measurement accuracy.

For a spatially coherent, on-axis source, the coherent spatial-frequency cutoff is
$$
f_c=\frac{\mathrm{NA}}\lambda.
$$
A line-space grating must place at least its first diffracted orders within the pupil. Its limiting pitch is therefore $\lambda/\mathrm{NA}$, and its equal line/space half-pitch is
$$
\boxed{a_{\min}=\frac{\lambda}{2\,\mathrm{NA}}
=\frac{193}{2(1.35)}\ \mathrm{nm}\simeq71.5\ \mathrm{nm}.}
$$
The corresponding full pitch is **143 nm**. Explicitly distinguishing pitch from half-pitch avoids a factor-of-two ambiguity in the phrase “feature size.” This is an ideal optical cutoff estimate; finite resist contrast and process requirements can make the usable feature size larger.

Decreasing the source diameter increases <spatial optical coherence> and moves the illumination towards the coherent on-axis limit. Here the grating frequency is only
$$
f=1/(800\ \mathrm{nm}),\qquad
\frac f{f_c}=\frac{193}{1.35(800)}\simeq0.179.
$$
The zero and first orders are comfortably within the pupil for on-axis illumination. Reducing an extended source can prevent some off-axis source points from losing <diffraction> orders at the pupil edge, and can therefore **increase the grating modulation, or leave it approximately unchanged if the relevant orders were already fully passed**. It need not give a universal numerical increase: higher orders, source shape, <defocus> and <optical aberrations> matter for a binary grating.

The resist's $\gamma$ and critical modulation $M_c$ in part (i) are material/process properties and **do not change merely because the source becomes smaller**. What may change is part (ii)'s comparison between the actual image modulation and $M_c$. If the improved image exceeds about 0.667, an exposure window opens; the source information alone does not establish a new numerical value.

The important change is **preorganization of the chloride-binding <amide> groups**. In the hydroxylated host, intramolecular <hydrogen bonds> from the phenolic OH groups to the <amide> <carbonyl> <oxygens> restrict rotation and favor a geometry in which the <amide> NH donors converge on the anion-binding cavity. <chloride> can therefore accept two favorable <hydrogen bonds> with a smaller conformational penalty than in the unsubstituted host. The hydroxyl groups need not be counted as two additional freely available <chloride> donors: their role includes organizing the existing donors.

The <association constant> increases by $5230/195=26.8$. At $298\,\mathrm K$, the corresponding difference in standard <Gibbs free energy> is
$$
\Delta\Delta G^\circ=-RT\ln(5230/195)\simeq\boxed{-8.15\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}}.
$$
This is a modest molecular free-energy advantage amplified into a substantial change in the <equilibrium constant>. <solvent> competition and changes in <solvation> also contribute to measured binding; the structures chiefly suggest the preorganization explanation.

The <molecule> combines three chemically different binding regions.

* **<sodium>: the oxygen-rich <calixarene>/<polyether> region**, including the <ether> <oxygens> and suitably oriented <ester> <carbonyl> <oxygens>. <sodium> is a hard <Lewis acid> and favors multidentate coordination by hard <oxygen> donors. The scaffold holds several donors near one another and can reduce the conformational cost of binding.
* **<nitrate>: the two <urea> groups.** Their NH groups donate <hydrogen bonds> to <nitrate> <oxygens>. Several NH donors can cooperate around an <anion>; the <carbonyl> <oxygen> of a <urea> is an acceptor and is not the proposed nitrate-binding donor.
* **<silver>: the terminal bipyridyl nitrogen donors.** The <pyridine> lone pairs coordinate the softer <silver> <ion>. The actual coordination may involve donor sites on more than one arm or more than one receptor, depending on geometry and <concentration>.

These assignments identify the preferred local sites, rather than guaranteeing three independent binding constants: occupancy can alter <conformation>, electrostatics and the affinity of neighboring sites.

Each <imidazole> already has one nitrogen substituted. Its remaining pyridine-like nitrogen attacks a benzylic <carbon> bearing chlorine in an $S_\mathrm N2$ reaction, expelling <chloride> and producing an <imidazolium> center. Repetition at the other end closes the ring. The expected product is therefore **a dicationic bis-imidazolium <macrocycle>**, in which two meta-xylylene bridges connect the two <imidazolium> rings. Both nitrogens of each <imidazolium> are alkylated; the aryl rings remain intact.

A compact way to specify its connectivity is to traverse the ring:
$$
\mathrm{m\!\!-!C_6H_4-CH_2-N(imidazolium)-CH_2-
 m\!\!-!C_6H_4-CH_2-N(imidazolium)-CH_2-}\quad\text{back to the first aryl group}.
$$
Here each indicated <imidazolium> connects its two substituents through its two different ring nitrogen atoms, rather than through a single nitrogen.

<chloride> acts as an **<anion> template**: association with the positively charged intermediates and the inward-facing <imidazolium> CH donors brings the reacting ends together, favoring cyclization over intermolecular oligomerization. Tetrabutylammonium is a spectator counterion, not a covalent part of the product. The two substitutions produce two <chloride> counterions for the dication; template <chloride> can also occupy the cavity, with the remaining <ions> in solution. One design is an **<anthracene>–methylene–<aza-15-crown-5> sensor**, with the <tertiary amine> of the aza-crown linked through $\mathrm{CH_2}$ to <anthracene>. The crown supplies the sodium-binding <oxygen>/nitrogen cavity, the <amine> supplies an <electron donor> and <anthracene> supplies the <fluorophore>. A sufficiently rigid sodium-sized <cryptand> could improve discrimination further.

Without a bound <cation>, <photoinduced electron transfer> from the <amine> can quench excited <anthracene>. <sodium> coordination lowers the electron-donating ability of that lone pair, suppressing this quenching route and increasing <fluorescence>. The methylene spacer allows electronic communication by <electron> transfer while limiting direct conjugation between the binding site and <fluorophore>.

A cavity appropriate to <sodium> is preferable to an 18-crown-6-like potassium-sized cavity. **Selectivity must be tested, not inferred perfectly from cavity size.** Potassium binding, including possible sandwich complexes of flexible crowns, can still compete. At the intended <concentrations> the design should satisfy $K_{\mathrm{Na}}[\mathrm{Na^+}]\gg K_{\mathrm K}[\mathrm{K^+}]$ and should give a <fluorescence> change on <sodium> binding. Rigidity, cavity size and suppression of competing stoichiometries are useful design controls. <fluorescence> calibration in the actual potassium-containing <solvent> establishes the usable detection range.

The species that oxidizes <nickel> is **<gold>(III) <ion>**, rather than metallic <gold>. The <half-reactions> are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Ni}&\longrightarrow\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}+2e^-,\\
\mathrm{Au^{3+}}+3e^-&\longrightarrow\mathrm{Au}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Multiplying the first by three and the second by two gives
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{3Ni+2Au^{3+}\longrightarrow3Ni^{2+}+2Au}}.
$$
Standard <electrode potentials> are not multiplied by the balancing factors. Thus $E^\circ_{\rm cell}=1.50-(-0.25)=1.75\,\mathrm V$ and six <moles> of <electrons> are transferred per <mole> of this balanced reaction:
$$
\boxed{\Delta G^\circ=-6FE^\circ_{\rm cell}\simeq-1.01\times10^6\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}}.
$$
The negative sign predicts a thermodynamically favorable <galvanic replacement> under standard conditions.

Clean and degrease the <nickel> surface, remove its inhibiting <oxide> by an appropriate brief surface treatment, rinse, and immerse it in an aqueous bath containing reducible <gold>(III) species. Control <concentration>, <temperature> and immersion time, then rinse after the desired deposition. A solution of a <gold> complex is practical, but its actual <reduction> potential must be evaluated for that complex and bath: it need not equal the supplied free-ion standard potential.

At anodic sites <nickel> dissolves as $\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}$, releasing <electrons> into the conducting substrate. Those <electrons> reduce <gold> <ions> at cathodic sites, nucleating and growing metallic <gold>. The <nickel> itself is the reducing agent, so **no external electrical supply or separate reducing reagent is required**. The coupled <electron> balance is three <nickel> atoms dissolved for two <gold> atoms deposited for the stated <oxidation states>.

The process can slow or stop when a sufficiently continuous <gold> coating prevents the <electrolyte> from reaching <nickel>. This is not an absolute thickness rule: pores, cracks or exposed edges preserve <nickel> dissolution and allow continued deposition, sometimes with undercutting. <nickel> <passivation>, depletion of <gold> <ions> near the surface, <diffusion> through a porous layer, bath speciation and competing reactions also affect the rate. Dense <gold> coverage and porous <gold> coverage therefore have very different limiting behavior.

Use a shaped <nickel> particle or <nanowire> as both **sacrificial template and reducing agent**. <gold> grows where <nickel> is consumed, allowing gold-coated particles, porous shells and, with suitable removal of the remaining <nickel>, hollow <gold> <nanostructures>. The original template controls the overall shape; the balance of dissolution, deposition and <diffusion> controls wall thickness and <porosity>.

A hollow product is not guaranteed simply by writing the reaction: deposition may seal a solid <nickel> core, whereas defects can maintain access to the interior and permit further replacement. Controlled composition and transport can give a progression from core–shell to porous or hollow structures. This is useful for high-surface-area <electrodes>, catalytic surfaces and plasmonic structures, while avoiding an electrical lead to every individual <nanoparticle>.

Three standard approaches are **piezoelectric, electromagnetic and <electrostatic energy harvesting>**.

* A piezoelectric microcantilever or <nanowire> develops <electric polarization> when strained. An inertial mass, bending substrate or vibration bends the element; <electrodes> and a rectifier collect charge. <PZT>, ZnO and <PVDF> illustrate different material choices. This approach needs no external bias for transduction and can give appreciable <voltage> in a compact element. Its <capacitance> gives high source impedance and relatively small <electric current>; brittle materials, fatigue, depolarization and a narrow mechanical <resonance> can limit performance.
* Electromagnetic harvesting uses relative motion of a magnet and coil to change <magnetic flux>, producing $\mathcal E=-N\,d\Phi/dt$. A micromachined coil and movable magnet can implement it at the microscale. It can offer low source impedance and useful <electric current>, but the small flux and coil area make <voltage> and <power> increasingly difficult to maintain on miniaturization; coil resistance and fabrication of magnets also matter. A practical microscale device does not imply efficient scaling all the way to individual <nanowires>.
* Electrostatic harvesting uses a variable <capacitor>, for example <MEMS> comb fingers or moving plates. Mechanical motion changes <capacitance>; an initial charge, electrical bias or <electret> allows this change to do electrical work. Small gaps favor strong coupling and compatibility with microfabrication. Limitations include the need for charge or bias, parasitic <capacitance>, leakage, pull-in, stiction and <dielectric breakdown>.

In all three cases the output is constrained by the available mechanical input and damping. Nanoscale dimensions alone do not supply an <energy> advantage.

A <thermocouple> uses **two materials with different <seebeck coefficients>**. With their junctions at different <temperatures>, the net <open-circuit voltage> is
$$
V_{\rm oc}=\int_{T_c}^{T_h}(\alpha_p-\alpha_n)\,dT
\simeq(\alpha_p-\alpha_n)\Delta T.
$$
Using p-type and n-type legs makes the two contributions add. Many pairs can be connected electrically in series to add <voltage> while remaining thermally in parallel between the same hot and cold plates. A single homogeneous wire does not provide this same complete-circuit <voltage> when its return lead has the same <thermopower>.

The reciprocal <Peltier effect> transports heat when <electric current> passes through a junction. The Kelvin relation $\Pi=\alpha T$ connects the heat carried per unit charge to the <Seebeck coefficient>. The same combination of large <thermopower>, high <electrical conductivity> and low <thermal conductivity> gives a favorable dimensionless figure of merit
$$
ZT=\frac{\alpha^2\sigma T}{\kappa}.
$$
Thus materials useful for a generator are also useful for a cooler when <electric current> is driven in the appropriate direction. <Joule heating> and thermal leakage oppose cooling; device geometry, contact resistance and operating <temperature> must still be appropriate. Reversibility of the thermoelectric coupling does not make either device lossless.

A pyroelectric material has a spontaneous <electric polarization> that changes with <temperature>. <electrodes> collect the resulting change in compensating charge. For a uniform element,
$$
\Delta Q=A p\Delta T,\qquad i=A p\frac{dT}{dt}.
$$
Consequently **<temperature> variation in time**, rather than a steady <temperature> difference alone, drives a <pyroelectric generator>. A cyclic thermal input and a suitable electrical circuit are needed for repeated <energy> extraction.

The <primary pyroelectric effect> is the change of <electric polarization> at fixed <strain>. The <secondary pyroelectric effect> arises because <thermal expansion> <strains> a piezoelectric crystal and thereby changes its <electric polarization>. In component notation its contribution is
$$
p_i^{\rm secondary}=\sum_j e_{ij}\alpha_j,\qquad
p_i^{\rm free}=p_i^{\rm clamped}+p_i^{\rm secondary},
$$

$$
The dark curve has $I_{\rm ph}=0$; illumination shifts it downward. At short circuit $I\simeq-I_{\rm ph}$; at open circuit $I=0$ and $V=V_{\rm oc}$. Between them the illuminated cell lies in **quadrant IV** and supplies <power> $P_{\rm out}=-VI>0$. The <maximum power point> maximizes the area of the rectangle with sides $V$ and $-I$:
$$
\boxed{\frac{d(-VI)}{dV}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad I+V\frac{dI}{dV}=0}.
$$
It is neither the open-circuit point nor the short-circuit point, both of which supply zero <power>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

At fixed spectrum and <temperature>, photocurrent is approximately proportional to <irradiance>, so the magnitude of <short-circuit current> increases nearly linearly. In the ideal model,
$$
V_{\rm oc}=n\frac{k_BT}{q}\ln\!\left(1+\frac{I_{\rm ph}}{I_0}\right),
$$
so <open-circuit voltage> rises only logarithmically with <irradiance>. More incident optical <power> generally raises the maximum electrical <power>, within the device's thermal and resistive limits.

Increasing <temperature> strongly increases the dark saturation <electric current>. In <silicon> this normally reduces <open-circuit voltage> and maximum <power>, despite a small increase in <short-circuit current> associated with the <temperature> dependence of absorption and <band gap>. The thermal-voltage factor alone is insufficient to predict the sign; the <temperature> dependence of $I_0$ matters.

**<maximum power point tracking> adjusts the load seen by the cell** as <irradiance> and <temperature> change. A controlled DC–DC converter can transform the external load so that operation stays near the changing maximum of $-VI$, instead of at the generally suboptimal intersection with a fixed load. It matches operating conditions and cannot generate additional <energy> beyond the photovoltaic output.

The illuminated area and incident <power> are
$$
A=(0.04)^2=1.6\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^2},\qquad
P_{\rm in}=87A=0.1392\,\mathrm W.
$$
A $412\,\mathrm{nm}$ <photon> has <energy> $hc/\lambda=4.821\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J=3.01\,\mathrm{eV}$, above the <silicon> <band gap>. With one collected <electron> per <photon>,
$$
I_{\rm ph}=q\frac{P_{\rm in}\lambda}{hc}=0.0462562\,\mathrm A.
$$
Use the ideal-diode approximation with **ideality factor $n=1$**, negligible shunt leakage and the same dark <diode> parameters under illumination. At $300\,\mathrm K$, $V_T=k_BT/q=0.025852\,\mathrm V$. The measured dark point then fixes
$$
I_0=\frac{10^{-4}}{\exp(0.466/V_T)-1}=1.4844\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm A.
$$
At open circuit the forward <diode> <electric current> balances the photocurrent:
$$
\boxed{V_{\rm oc}=V_T\ln\!\left(1+\frac{I_{\rm ph}}{I_0}\right)=0.62465\,\mathrm V\simeq0.625\,\mathrm V}.
$$
This is below the $1.1\,\mathrm{eV}$ <band gap> expressed per elementary charge. Excess <photon> <energy> does not translate directly into terminal <voltage>; much of it thermalizes.

The paper does not explicitly specify an ideality factor. If it is not taken as unity, one dark operating point cannot determine both $I_0$ and $n$. The corresponding family is
$$
V_{\rm oc}(n)=nV_T\ln\!\left[1+\frac{I_{\rm ph}}{10^{-4}}\left(\exp\!\left(\frac{0.466}{nV_T}\right)-1\right)\right].
$$
Thus $0.625\,\mathrm V$ is a stated-model estimate, not a parameter-free inference from that one measurement.
