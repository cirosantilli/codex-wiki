A <single-walled carbon nanotube> confines the <electron> around its circumference while allowing motion along its axis. <Nanotube zone folding> therefore turns the <graphene> bands into one-dimensional subbands. A <semiconducting carbon nanotube> has no allowed circumferential wave vector through a <Dirac valley>; a metallic tube has a band crossing at the <Fermi energy> in the ideal model. Each massive subband edge gives a <one-dimensional van Hove singularity> in the <density of states>, and corresponding peaks appear in the <joint density of states>.

For <light> polarized along the tube, the main <electric dipole selection rule> conserves circumferential angular momentum: transitions connect matching valence and conduction subbands. These finite-energy transitions are conventionally $S_{11},S_{22},\ldots$ in a <semiconducting carbon nanotube> and $M_{11},M_{22},\ldots$ in a metallic tube. A metallic band crossing does not make its first strong interband peak have zero energy. Perpendicular polarization admits different angular-momentum channels, often much weaker because of depolarization. <Excitons>, dielectric screening and trigonal warping modify the simple band-edge transition energies.

\Image[paper-2-nanotube-bands.png]
{title=Nanotube optical transitions and two example roll-up vectors}
{description=Original band and lattice schematics. Arrows indicate matching-subband transitions; the metallic crossing is separate from its finite-energy interband resonance. Lattice points represent graphene unit cells rather than individual atoms.}
{source=paper-2-nanotube-bands.py}

Experimentally, measure absorption versus photon energy; <Raman spectroscopy> becomes resonantly enhanced when the excitation matches an optical transition. A <photoluminescence excitation spectrum> of dispersed semiconducting tubes correlates excitation near $S_{22}$ with emission near $S_{11}$ after relaxation. Metallic tubes generally lack comparable bright emission because nonradiative relaxation is efficient. Combining optical energies with radial-breathing-mode <Raman spectroscopy> or <electron diffraction> assigns tube diameter and roll-up indices; a <Kataura plot> organizes those assignments.<Structure-assigned optical spectra of single-walled carbon nanotubes by Sergei M. Bachilo and others>[Excitation-emission spectroscopy and assignments to nanotube structures]{ref}

Let $\mathbf a_1,\mathbf a_2$ be equal-length primitive translation vectors of the <graphene> lattice, separated by $60^\circ$. The <carbon nanotube chiral vector>
$$
\mathbf C_h=n\mathbf a_1+m\mathbf a_2
$$
identifies the two points joined when the sheet is rolled: $|\mathbf C_h|$ is the circumference, not the tube axis. The right-hand panel of the preceding original diagram shows $\mathbf C_h=6\mathbf a_1+6\mathbf a_2$ and $\mathbf C_h=7\mathbf a_1$.

The periodic boundary condition is $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf C_h=2\pi\ell$, with $\ell$ an integer. Applying it at either <Dirac valley> gives the <nanotube zone folding> criterion
$$
n-m\equiv0\pmod3
$$
for a metallic tube. Thus $(6,6)$ is an armchair metallic example, whereas $(7,0)$ is a zigzag semiconducting example. Other choices with $n-m\not\equiv0\pmod3$ also give a <semiconducting carbon nanotube>.

This criterion is the ideal flat-sheet approximation. Curvature can open a small gap in nominally metallic non-armchair tubes; ideal armchair tubes remain gapless in that approximation. The property is determined by the wrapping vector, not simply by calling a tube “chiral” or by its diameter alone.

The important distinctions are changes of ring topology, missing or added atoms, and changes of local bonding:

* A <Stone-Wales defect> rotates one carbon-carbon bond by approximately $90^\circ$, converting four hexagons into a $5$-$7$-$7$-$5$ ring arrangement without adding or removing atoms. It changes strain and electronic scattering.
* A <carbon nanotube vacancy defect> removes an atom. Reconstruction commonly gives a pentagon and a nine-membered ring, with an undercoordinated site; a reconstructed divacancy can give a $5$-$8$-$5$ arrangement. Larger vacancies make holes or broken walls.
* An adjacent pentagon-heptagon pair acts as a <dislocation> in the hexagonal network. It can connect regions with different wrapping indices, producing a bend or an electronic junction. Isolated nonhexagonal rings impose curvature: pentagons favor positive curvature and heptagons negative curvature.
* An <interstitial defect>, an adsorbed atom, or chemical functionalization can distort the network and locally change $sp^2$ bonding toward $sp^3$. These defects can scatter carriers and create chemically reactive sites.

\Image[paper-2-carbon-defects.png]
{title=Carbon-ring inventories and additional imperfections in multiwall nanotubes}
{description=Original topological sketches: polygon labels identify ring sizes, not a relaxed atom-by-atom geometry. The wall sketch illustrates interwall bridges and an interrupted outer shell.}
{source=paper-2-carbon-defects.py}

Closed end caps require nonhexagonal rings, typically six pentagons per hemispherical cap; these are part of the cap topology rather than necessarily damage. Kinks, buckling, branches and incomplete closure are larger-scale structural imperfections.

A <multi-walled carbon nanotube> can contain any of these defects in individual shells, plus interrupted or terminated shells, extra incomplete graphitic layers, irregular interwall spacing, and covalent interwall bridges formed by displaced atoms. Such bridges change intershell coupling and sliding. Different shells having different wrapping indices is normal and is not, by itself, a defect.

Two layers need not have the same electronic structure everywhere. Different rotational alignments create domains of <twisted bilayer graphene>; AB-stacked regions instead have the usual coupled low-energy bilayer bands. In a twisted domain, displaced <Dirac cones> hybridize where they meet, producing avoided crossings and saddle points in the two-dimensional <dispersion relation>. The resulting <van Hove singularities> enhance optical transitions at energies that depend on the twist angle. Different domains therefore absorb different parts of the visible spectrum and can show different colours despite having the same layer count.<Optical absorption in twisted bilayer graphene by Pilkyung Moon and Mikito Koshino>[Angle-dependent band hybridization and optical absorption resonances]{ref}

\Image[paper-2-bilayer-bands.png]
{title=Bilayer stacking changes the bands and optical absorption}
{description=Original illustrative bands: AB-stacked bands, a local cut through avoided crossings in a twisted domain, and schematic angle-dependent absorption peaks. The middle panel is a local cut, not the entire two-dimensional band structure.}
{source=paper-2-bilayer-bands.py}

On a silicon/silicon-dioxide substrate, reflected beams from the film stack also interfere. The wavelength-dependent optical response of the <bilayer graphene> changes this reflected contrast, making the domain differences visible. The observation is consistent with stacking domains; colour alone does not uniquely prove a twist angle. Variations in oxide thickness, trapped contamination, folds or local doping can also change contrast and should be checked independently, for example by <Raman spectroscopy>.

The <gas impingement flux> onto a surface follows from the normal component of the <Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution>:
$$
J=\frac{P}{\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}}.
$$
Here $P=0.1\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and $m=18(1.66\times10^{-27})=2.99\times10^{-26}\,\mathrm{kg}$, giving
$$
J\simeq3.60\times10^{21}\,\mathrm{m^{-2}s^{-1}}.
$$
Assume a conventional <monolayer> density $N_s=10^{19}\,\mathrm{m^{-2}}$ and unit <sticking coefficient>. The incident-monolayer time is
$$
t_{\mathrm{ML}}=\frac{N_s}{J}\simeq2.8\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm s.
$$
Thus **a few milliseconds** is the usual contamination-time estimate at this pressure.

The assumed molecular footprint matters: neither $N_s$ nor the <sticking coefficient> is specified. More generally $t_{\mathrm{ML}}=N_s/(sJ)$ if the sticking probability is $s$ and <desorption> is negligible. Water can desorb rapidly from clean <graphene> at room temperature, so the incident-monolayer estimate does not establish that a stable, complete water layer actually forms in that time. Residues and defects can increase local adsorption.

An <atomic layer deposition> recipe depends on the surface chemistry during the first cycles. A silicon wafer normally carries native oxide with reactive hydroxyl groups. A pristine <graphene> basal plane has no comparable dense population of dangling bonds or hydroxyl groups, so precursor adsorption and initial nucleation are much less uniform.

Consequently the nominal number of cycles calibrated to give $2\,\mathrm{nm}$ on silicon can instead give isolated alumina islands, preferential decoration of edges, grain boundaries and defects, and uncovered graphene between islands. Adsorbed transfer residues may provide additional, irregular nucleation sites. Once alumina has nucleated, later cycles grow on the alumina normally; the problem is delayed, spatially uneven nucleation. The resulting local island height can exceed the film's area-averaged equivalent thickness.<Atomic layer deposition of metal oxides on pristine and functionalized graphene by Xinran Wang and others>[Pristine graphene nucleation at edges and defects, and uniform growth after noncovalent functionalization]{ref}

Two suitable improvements are:

* Introduce a dense, controlled nucleation layer, for example a noncovalently adsorbed functional layer carrying reactive groups or a very thin oxidized metal seed. This supplies precursor-binding sites while limiting damage to the conducting carbon network.
* Optimize initial exposure rather than reuse the silicon recipe unchanged: use longer precursor residence/soak times and suitably reduced initial temperature to increase adsorption and nucleation, then continue normal cycles after closure. Direct sub-$2\,\mathrm{nm}$ continuous growth with extended residence times has been demonstrated.<Parameter space of atomic layer deposition of ultrathin oxides on graphene by Adrianus I. Aria and others>[Pulse/residence-time control of initial nucleation and continuous ultrathin films]{ref}

Ozone or a mild plasma is another possible activation route, but it must be controlled because creating covalent defects can degrade the electrical properties that motivate using <graphene>.

Take a two-dimensional conducting sheet in the $xy$ plane with <magnetic flux density> $\mathbf B=B\hat{\mathbf z}$ and <electric current density> along $x$. Moving carriers feel the transverse <Lorentz force> $q\mathbf v_d\times\mathbf B$. They accumulate at opposite edges, producing a transverse <electric field> until its force balances the magnetic deflection:
$$
q(\mathbf E_H+\mathbf v_d\times\mathbf B)=0.
$$
For the transverse-voltage convention $E_y/j_x$, this gives $E_y=v_xB$ and
$$
\frac{E_y}{j_x}=\frac{B}{nq}.
$$
The <Hall effect> therefore measures carrier sign as well as density in the single-carrier approximation: electrons give the opposite transverse-voltage sign from positive carriers. Some tensor conventions call $E_y/j_x$ $\rho_{yx}$ rather than $\rho_{xy}$; the physical sign is fixed by the stated lead and field directions.

The <Hall voltage> is the integral of this transverse <electric field> across the sample width. In a three-dimensional slab of thickness $t$, its magnitude is $|V_H|=IB/(n|q|t)$; for a sheet, use the sheet number density $n_s$ and omit $t$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

In the classical single-carrier model,
$$
|\rho_{xy}|=\frac{|B|}{n_s e},\qquad
\left|\frac{d\rho_{xy}}{dB}\right|=\frac1{n_se}
$$
for a two-dimensional sheet. The curve is a straight line through the origin, with its sign determined by the carrier charge and the voltage convention. The sheet <Hall coefficient> has units $\mathrm{m^2/C}$, consistent with the numerical slope used later.

The classical transverse response is not quantized. With carrier density and scattering parameters fixed, reversing $B$ reverses the transverse signal. Several carrier populations can produce a nonlinear classical curve, so straight-line behaviour here uses the stated single-population approximation rather than being a property of every conductor.

The <integer quantum Hall effect> requires a conducting <two-dimensional electron gas> with well-resolved <Landau levels>. In the simple parabolic-band model their orbital spacing is
$$
\hbar\omega_c=\frac{\hbar eB}{m^*}.
$$
Low <temperature> and sufficiently low disorder broadening are needed so that $k_BT$ and the relevant level width $\Gamma$ are small compared with this spacing. A high-mobility sample with $\omega_c\tau\gg1$ is therefore useful.

Some disorder has a constructive role: localized states between extended Landau-level states allow the <Fermi energy> to move through a range of carrier populations while the transport response stays on a plateau. The longitudinal response is then very small and the transverse response is quantized. Whether odd or only even integer plateaux are resolved also depends on whether spin splitting is resolved.

= Solution

On an integer plateau the sheet transverse resistivity has magnitude
$$
|\rho_{xy}|=\frac{h}{\nu e^2},
$$
where the integer $\nu$ counts occupied resolved <quantum Hall edge states>. Increasing $B$ at fixed sheet density reduces the <Landau filling factor> $\nu_{\mathrm{fill}}=n_sh/(eB)$. The observed trace is therefore a staircase toward larger $|\rho_{xy}|$, with transitions between plateaux rather than the classical straight line. In the plateau regions $\rho_{xx}\simeq0$; dissipative peaks occur at transitions.

\Image[paper-2-hall-response.png]
{title=Classical Hall line and illustrative integer quantum Hall plateaux}
{description=Original schematic responses, with the classical slope fixed by the later calculation. Plateau widths and dissipative peak shapes are illustrative rather than fitted to an experimental trace.}
{source=paper-2-hall-response.py}

It is not correct to demand an exactly integer value of $n_sh/(eB)$ everywhere along a finite-width plateau. Localization accommodates changes in total population without changing the number of extended current-carrying channels. If spin remains unresolved, the usual spin-degenerate sequence contains even integers rather than every integer.

The <giant magnetoresistance> mechanism is spin-dependent conduction through the two ferromagnets. Exchange splitting gives majority and minority $d$ bands different <density of states> values at the <Fermi energy>. Scattering into those bands, together with interface scattering, produces different resistances for the two spin channels. The relevant quantity is the spin-dependent transport/scattering rate, not simply a count of all available states.

For parallel layer <magnetizations>, one global spin channel encounters relatively weak scattering in both Co and NiFe, providing a low-resistance path. For antiparallel <magnetizations>, each global spin channel encounters one more strongly scattering ferromagnet, so both paths are comparatively resistive. The channels conduct in parallel while each crosses the layers in series. This is <current-perpendicular-to-plane magnetoresistance>, because the current crosses the interfaces.<Nobel Lecture: Origin, development, and future of spintronics by Albert Fert>[Spin-dependent conduction and the distinction between CPP and CIP transport]{ref}

In the simplest <two-current resistor model>, with equal magnetic layers having channel resistances $a,b$ and spacer resistance $r$ per channel,
$$
R_P=\frac{(2a+r)(2b+r)}{2(a+b+r)},\qquad
R_{AP}=\frac{a+b+r}{2},\qquad
R_{AP}-R_P=\frac{(a-b)^2}{2(a+b+r)}.
$$
Real Co/NiFe layers need not have equal parameters, but the same-sign spin asymmetries normally give $R_{AP}>R_P$. Their different <magnetic coercivities> allow the two configurations to be selected separately.

= Solution

Assume NiFe is the softer layer, with $0<H_{c,\mathrm{NiFe}}<H_{c,\mathrm{Co}}$, and that interlayer coupling is weak enough to permit separate reversal. Starting at large negative $H$, both <magnetizations> point in the same direction and $R=R_P$. During an increasing-field sweep:

* At $H=H_{c,\mathrm{NiFe}}$, NiFe reverses while Co does not: the state is antiparallel and the resistance rises to $R_{AP}$.
* At $H=H_{c,\mathrm{Co}}$, Co also reverses: the state becomes parallel and the resistance returns to $R_P$.

On decreasing $H$ from positive saturation, the high-resistance window instead lies between $-H_{c,\mathrm{Co}}$ and $-H_{c,\mathrm{NiFe}}$. Thus the two sweep directions give displaced rectangular high-resistance windows, not a single reversible resistance peak centered on zero.

\Image[paper-2-spin-valve.png]

{source=paper-2-spin-valve.py}

If Co were the softer layer, the sequence of layer labels would be exchanged. A continuous spacer and sufficiently weak coupling are additional assumptions: negligible surface roughness alone does not guarantee independent switching at every Cu thickness.

= Solution

Specify the <magnetoresistance ratio> as $(R_{AP}-R_P)/R_P$. Increasing a continuous Cu spacer adds spin-independent series resistance. Even when $d$ remains below the <spin diffusion length>, this dilutes the ratio; spin information need not be destroyed to reduce the percentage signal. In the illustrative <two-current resistor model>,
$$
\frac{R_{AP}-R_P}{R_P}
=\frac{(a-b)^2}{(2a+r)(2b+r)},\qquad r\propto d,
$$
which decreases as $d$ increases. The absolute difference also decreases in this model. At a fixed bias current, the measured voltage contrast is $\Delta V=I(R_{AP}-R_P)$.

For $d$ comparable to or greater than the <spin diffusion length>, additional <spin relaxation> further suppresses the contrast. At the other extreme an ultrathin or discontinuous Cu layer can introduce direct magnetic coupling or pinholes, preventing a fully antiparallel state. Thickness-dependent interlayer coupling can therefore make the very-thin-spacer trend nonmonotonic; a universal exponential law does not follow from the supplied assumptions.

Roughness can produce a distribution of local switching fields and nonuniform reversal. The abrupt edges in the ideal <pseudo spin valve> loops then become rounded or sloping, the antiparallel plateau becomes less flat, and overlapping layer-reversal distributions can narrow or partly eliminate the clear high-resistance window. The middle panel of the original diagram illustrates only this broadening mechanism.

Correlated roughness on the two interfaces can also introduce <orange-peel coupling>, a magnetostatic coupling that favors parallel alignment. Such coupling shifts switching fields and can suppress independent reversal. Sufficiently severe roughness or pinholes can have a similar qualitative effect by directly coupling the ferromagnets.

Changes in amplitude require care. Some spin-dependent interface scattering contributes to <giant magnetoresistance>, so a small increase in roughness is not guaranteed to lower the signal monotonically. Intermixing, spin-flip scattering and an incompletely antiparallel state generally reduce useful contrast. The detailed width, displacement and height of $R(H)$ therefore depend on how the interfaces become rough, rather than on a single roughness number.

= Solution
{parent=v}

Two practical approaches retaining Co and NiFe are:

* Improve the spin-selective interfaces: control deposition and intermixing, avoid oxidation or magnetic dead layers, and reduce pinholes and spin-flip defects. The aim is large majority/minority transport asymmetry with low spin-memory loss, rather than merely the smallest total resistance.
* Optimize the Cu thickness and electrical stack: use the thinnest continuous spacer that still allows independent layer reversal and remains well below its <spin diffusion length>; reduce extraneous contact or lead resistance. This preserves a full antiparallel state and limits spin-independent dilution of the measured <magnetoresistance ratio>.

Increasing magnetic-layer thickness toward the useful spin-dependent-scattering scale can be another optimization, but growth beyond the relevant spin diffusion scale eventually gives diminishing returns. Merely increasing the bias current increases the readout voltage and possible heating; it does not, by itself, increase the intrinsic <giant magnetoresistance> ratio.

The <Flory interaction parameter> $\chi$ measures the free-energy penalty of unlike block contacts relative to like contacts, in units of $k_BT$. The <degree of polymerization> $N$ counts the reference segments per chain, using the segment-volume convention associated with $\chi$. Their product, the <block copolymer segregation strength> $\chi N$, compares the tendency to avoid unlike contacts with the entropy cost of organizing and stretching connected chains.

At small $\chi N$, the <diblock copolymer> is disordered. Increasing $\chi N$ can cause <microphase separation>: the blocks form distinct domains, but their covalent connection prevents complete macroscopic separation. Domain interfaces cost energy while chain stretching and lost configurations cost <entropy>; their competition selects a finite domain scale.

A common temperature law is $\chi=A/T+B$, so increasing temperature often decreases segregation when $A>0$. This is not a universal sign law for every polymer pair. For an intact, fixed chain, $N$ does not change just because temperature or solvent concentration changes.

Adding a nonselective good solvent usually dilutes unlike-block contacts and reduces an effective segregation strength; it does not chemically shorten the chain. A selective solvent additionally changes domain swelling and solvent-block interactions. One needs a ternary-mixture description, or a clearly defined effective $\chi$, rather than treating the melt's $\chi$ as a universal solvent-concentration function.<Polyisoprene-polystyrene diblock copolymer phase diagram near the order-disorder transition by Ashish K. Khandpur and others>[Definition of the segregation and composition variables and experimental phase behaviour]{ref}

For the dry <diblock copolymer>, the <block volume fraction>
$$
f_{PI}=\frac{V_{PI}}{V_{PI}+V_{PS}}
$$
is the volume assigned to the <polyisoprene> block divided by total polymer volume, not its mass fraction or the volume fraction of polymer in the whole solution. It controls domain curvature and topology. Similar block volumes favor alternating lamellae; a smaller minority block generally favors curved interfaces, cylinders and eventually isolated spherical domains.

The chemical block lengths are fixed. Temperature changes their volumes only through thermal expansion, so $f_{PI}$ is often approximated as temperature independent; unequal expansion coefficients can produce a small change.

Solvent requires a distinction between dry composition and swollen domain composition. The dry $f_{PI}$ remains fixed. If solvent volumes $V_{s,PI}$ and $V_{s,PS}$ partition into the two domains, an effective swollen fraction is
$$
f_{PI}^{\mathrm{eff}}=
\frac{V_{PI}+V_{s,PI}}
{V_{PI}+V_{PS}+V_{s,PI}+V_{s,PS}}.
$$
A <polystyrene>-selective solvent therefore lowers $f_{PI}^{\mathrm{eff}}$, whereas a polyisoprene-selective solvent raises it. Equal proportional swelling leaves this effective ratio unchanged.

The starting point is in the lamellar region. Preferential swelling of <polystyrene> makes <polyisoprene> the increasingly small minority domain, so the relevant effective composition moves left from $f_{PI}^{\mathrm{eff}}=0.45$.

If segregation stayed near $\chi N=35$, following the horizontal path in the supplied diagram would give **lamellae → hexagonally perforated layers → hexagonally arranged PI cylinders → PI spheres**, before sufficient asymmetry reaches the disordered region. Solvent also changes effective segregation, so the actual trajectory need not be horizontal. If it passes closer to the order-disorder boundary, it can enter the narrow <double gyroid> region instead of, or after, the <hexagonally perforated layer phase>. The robust prediction is the change toward minority-PI curved structures, not a guarantee that every named phase occurs. A solvent partition coefficient and a concentration-dependent interaction model would be needed to locate the actual path quantitatively; applying a dry-melt diagram to a swollen material is an effective approximation.

Refraction changes the propagation direction, and hence the <momentum>, of transmitted <light>. The particle receives the opposite momentum change by <conservation of momentum>. For a ray carrying power $P_r$, a useful force scale is
$$
\mathbf F_r\sim\frac{n_mP_r}{c}
(\hat{\mathbf s}_{\mathrm{in}}-\hat{\mathbf s}_{\mathrm{out}}),
$$
with corrections for reflected rays and their momentum fluxes. Here $n_m$ is the surrounding refractive index; this is a ray-force scaling rather than a complete electromagnetic stress calculation.

For fixed geometry and refractive indices, $P_r=I\,\delta A$, so the force contribution is proportional to the local intensity $I$. Refraction toward the normal on entry and away from the normal on exit must both be included: a force inferred from only the first interface can have the wrong direction.

The specified size is larger than the wavelength, so a <geometrical optics> treatment is appropriate despite the generic “nanoparticle” label. A dipole/Rayleigh-particle formula would use the opposite size limit.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Because the sphere lies below the optical axis, a ray passing through its upper half samples a brighter part of the <Gaussian beam> than a comparable ray through its lower half. For a particle with higher <refractive index> than its surroundings, the stronger upper ray is deflected downward overall and transfers an upward reaction to the particle. The weaker lower ray gives a smaller downward reaction. Their transverse sum is therefore upward, toward the brighter axis.

\Image[paper-2-optical-forces.png]
{title=Unequal refracted rays give a transverse restoring force}
{description=Original local ray tracing near a beam waist, with chosen refractive indices. The sphere is below the optical axis. The stronger ray contributes the larger upward reaction; the diagram isolates transverse restoration rather than reconstructing the examination figure.}
{source=paper-2-optical-forces.py}

If the displacement reverses, the force reverses, so the central position is transversely stable. Summing all the rays gives an <optical gradient force> toward the intensity maximum. There is also a forward <optical scattering force>. A tightly focused beam can have an axial restoring component that balances it, generally at a small displacement downstream of the ideal focus.<Observation of a single-beam gradient force optical trap for dielectric particles by Arthur Ashkin and others>[Single-beam trapping by a sufficiently strong intensity gradient]{ref}

Three-dimensional stability requires the restoring axial component to exceed the forward force over part of the trajectory. A Gaussian transverse intensity profile alone does not prove that condition; insufficient focusing can yield transverse confinement while the particle still moves downstream.

<Homeotropic anchoring> means that the <nematic director> is locally normal to the particle surface, hence radial on a sphere. Far away, the uniform <nematic liquid crystal> has a fixed director $\mathbf n_\infty$, with $\mathbf n$ and $-\mathbf n$ physically equivalent.

One plausible configuration is a quadrupolar <Saturn-ring defect>: the director is radial near the particle, bends into $\mathbf n_\infty$ far away, and has a disclination loop just outside the particle's equator, in the plane perpendicular to $\mathbf n_\infty$. A meridional section cuts the loop at two points.

Absorption converts part of the incident optical power into heat. Increasing laser power can therefore raise the local <temperature>, potentially driving a thermotropic <nematic liquid crystal> through its nematic-isotropic transition. The associated loss or change of orientational order, <refractive index> and anchoring can alter the trapping force or destabilize the trap.

Even below that transition, thermal gradients can induce convection, thermophoretic drift and changes in viscosity or Brownian fluctuations; sufficiently strong heating can damage the particle or host. Thus increasing laser power need not simply strengthen an otherwise unchanged <optical trap>.

<Nucleation> is the formation of a sufficiently large embryo of the new metallic phase. Producing metal atoms from dissolved precursor can create <supersaturation>; aggregation lowers bulk free energy but initially creates costly metal-medium interface. In the simplest spherical <classical nucleation theory>,
$$
\Delta G_{\mathrm{hom}}(r)=4\pi r^2\gamma-\frac{4\pi}{3}r^3\Delta g_v,\qquad
r_c=\frac{2\gamma}{\Delta g_v},
$$
where $\gamma$ is the metal-medium interfacial free energy and $\Delta g_v>0$ is the bulk driving force per volume. Clusters below the critical size tend to redissolve; larger ones can grow.

In <homogeneous nucleation>, the critical cluster forms in the solution without a pre-existing interface: the initial products are free particles. In <heterogeneous nucleation>, a substrate, vessel wall or suspended seed replaces part of that costly new interface. For a spherical cap on a planar substrate,
$$
\Delta G_{\mathrm{het}}^\ddagger=
\frac{(2+\cos\theta)(1-\cos\theta)^2}{4}
\Delta G_{\mathrm{hom}}^\ddagger.
$$
A wetting substrate therefore lowers the barrier; this assumes the same bulk driving force and isotropic interfacial energies.

\Image[paper-2-nucleation.png]
{title=Free and supported particles arise through different nucleation histories}
{description=Original sequential schematics: metal precursor is dispersed in the liquid, a critical embryo forms, and the stable nucleus grows. The lower sequence represents cap nucleation on a fixed substrate.}
{source=paper-2-nucleation.py}

A final supported particle need not have nucleated heterogeneously: a free particle can nucleate in solution and later adsorb on the support. Conversely, a particle nucleated heterogeneously on a suspended seed can remain freely dispersed as a composite particle. Nucleation location and final support status are different classifications.

Microwave absorption heats the medium and possibly the support, changing reduction, diffusion and supersaturation. Preferential local heating can change the relative rates of the two routes. Microwave irradiation alone does not remove the thermodynamic nucleation barrier or demonstrate a special nonthermal mechanism.

The thermodynamic questions can be organized from precursor chemistry to the final supported shape:

* **Can the precursor produce metal?** Evaluate the reaction <Gibbs free energy>, including reduction, ligand exchange, precursor and reductant activities, solvation and byproducts. A nominal metal concentration is not the metal-atom chemical potential.
* **What is the driving force for condensation?** Compare the chemical potential of available metal species with that of the condensed phase. The resulting supersaturation supplies the bulk free-energy gain and competes with the surface cost of a critical nucleus.
* **Where does it wet and nucleate?** Let $\gamma_{sm}$, $\gamma_{pm}$ and $\gamma_{sp}$ denote support-medium, particle-medium and support-particle interfacial free energies. For an isotropic cap, the contact angle through the particle obeys $\gamma_{sm}=\gamma_{sp}+\gamma_{pm}\cos\theta$. This wetting balance determines the extent of substrate contact and the heterogeneous barrier.
* **Which crystal faces survive?** At fixed particle volume, minimize the sum of orientation-dependent surface and interface energies, plus any important strain or defect energy. For a free crystal, the <Wulff construction> gives facet distances proportional to their surface free energies. A <Winterbottom construction> incorporates the support interface and truncates the corresponding equilibrium shape.

Adsorbed ligands and solvent can change different facet energies by different amounts, so the relevant energies belong to the actual chemical environment. Small particles have large surface-to-volume ratios and are particularly sensitive to these terms.

This sequence identifies thermodynamic factors rather than guaranteeing that an equilibrium shape is reached. Facet-specific attachment rates, precursor delivery and limited annealing can preserve a kinetically selected shape even when another shape has lower free energy.

Write the reaction using the species actually supplied by solution. For either halide, a bulk contribution per converted formula unit is
$$
\Delta\mu_X=
\mu_{\mathrm{MAPbX_3}}-\mu_{\mathrm{PbX_2}}
-\mu_{\mathrm{MA^+,sol}}-\mu_{\mathrm{X^-,sol}}.
$$
The stated room-temperature feasibility means that the overall reaction free energy is negative under those conditions; it does not say that each microscopic step is downhill. Equal nominal $50\,\mathrm{mM}$ concentrations also do not make iodide and bromide activities or solvation free energies identical.

At the nanoscale the balance additionally contains changing interfaces:
$$
\Delta G_X=N_c\Delta\mu_X+
\sum_j\gamma_{j,\mathrm{product}}A_{j,\mathrm{product}}
-\sum_j\gamma_{j,\mathrm{precursor}}A_{j,\mathrm{precursor}}
+\Delta G_{\mathrm{strain}}+\Delta G_{\mathrm{defect}}.
$$
Here $N_c$ is the number of converted formula units, and $j$ includes solvent-facing surfaces and contact with the titanium-dioxide support. Surface terms scale approximately as size squared whereas bulk terms scale as size cubed. Particle size, wetting, expansion during conversion and new grain interfaces can therefore shift both the conversion driving force and the nucleation barrier.

Microscopically, conversion requires desolvation, uptake of the organic cation and halide, redistribution through the precursor, and reorganization of the lead-halide framework. A study using these conversion conditions found energetically costly dispersed-ion intercalation for both compounds, with a much larger calculated penalty for MABr in PbBr$_2$ than for MAI in PbI$_2$. The iodide's layered framework accommodates the cation more favorably; the bromide's undulated framework gives poorer coordination and greater local distortion. Stabilization on forming intermediate compounds and the final perovskite can nevertheless make the complete reaction favorable. The calculations are approximate energetic comparisons, not measured finite-temperature activation free energies.<Kinetics and energetics of metal halide perovskite conversion reactions at the nanoscale by Neha Arora and others>[Fig. 4 and accompanying discussion: solvation reference, intercalation and conversion intermediates]{ref}

Thus both reactions can be spontaneous yet proceed at very different rates: the same study observed faster MAPbI$_3$ formation. One must distinguish net reaction free energy from intermediate penalties and diffusion barriers. Spontaneity alone supplies no quantitative ranking of the complete iodide and bromide reaction free energies.

In catalytic <chemical vapor deposition>, a metal nanoparticle activates a carbon-containing feedstock and supplies mobile carbon to the growing rim. Carbon can diffuse over the particle surface and, depending on the catalyst state, through its bulk. Once a curved carbon cap nucleates, continued incorporation extends the tube. Strong catalyst-support adhesion favors base growth; weaker adhesion can allow the particle to lift with the tube tip. A liquid catalyst is not universally required.

Particle size constrains tube diameter, but semiconducting character requires a particular wrapping family: $n-m\not\equiv0\pmod3$. Merely making particles smaller does not select that congruence class. Routes toward predominantly <semiconducting carbon nanotubes> include controlling catalyst composition, exposed facets and size distribution to bias cap/rim nucleation and growth, or adjusting carbon chemical potential and growth/etching conditions so that metallic tubes are less likely to survive.

These are mechanistic strategies rather than a universal recipe. Experiments with catalyst conditioning found that high semiconducting selectivity depended sensitively on oxidizing/reducing gas conditions and came with a strong yield penalty, motivating a catalyst-deactivation model.<A phenomenological model for selective growth of semiconducting single-walled carbon nanotubes based on catalyst deactivation by Shunsuke Sakurai and others>[Catalyst conditioning, gas composition and selectivity-yield tradeoff]{ref}

The useful distinction is between selecting a structure at nucleation, selecting its growth rate, and selectively terminating or removing unwanted tubes. Each can enrich the semiconducting fraction, but they do not establish the same growth mechanism.

The pattern is characteristic of a <single-electron transistor>: a small conducting island is connected to source and drain by two <tunnel junctions>, and to a gate through a capacitance. Inside a <Coulomb diamond>, changing the island's integer charge costs more energy than the leads can supply, so sequential tunnelling is blocked. Outside it, an island electrochemical potential lies in the transport window and current can flow.

For clear <Coulomb blockade>, the island charging scale must exceed thermal broadening, and the tunnel junctions must be sufficiently resistive to preserve well-defined charge states:
$$
E_C=\frac{e^2}{2C_\Sigma}\gg k_BT,\qquad
R_s,R_d\gg\frac{h}{e^2},
\qquad C_\Sigma=C_s+C_d+C_g
$$
in the simplest circuit. Stray capacitances should be included in $C_\Sigma$ if appreciable. The gate changes electrostatic energy but should not provide a conducting leakage path. Lifetime broadening and cotunnelling should be small enough not to wash out the blocked regions.

Inside the rectangle the effective-mass <Schrödinger equation> is
$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m^*}\left(\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2\right)\psi=E\psi,
$$
with $\psi=0$ at all four walls because the confining potential is infinite outside. Separate $\psi(x,y)=X(x)Y(y)$. Each factor obeys a one-dimensional <particle in a box> equation. For $x$,
$$
X''+k_x^2X=0,\qquad
X(0)=X(L_x)=0,
$$
so $X$ is proportional to $\sin(n_x\pi x/L_x)$ with $n_x=1,2,\ldots$; the zero and negative integers give no new physical states. The same argument applies in $y$.

The normalized spatial states and their energies are
$$
\psi_{n_x,n_y}(x,y)=\frac2{\sqrt{L_xL_y}}
\sin\left(\frac{n_x\pi x}{L_x}\right)
\sin\left(\frac{n_y\pi y}{L_y}\right),
$$
$$
E_{n_x,n_y}=E_x+E_y
=\frac{h^2}{8m^*}
\left(\frac{n_x^2}{L_x^2}+\frac{n_y^2}{L_y^2}\right).
$$
The product form and energy sum follow from separability. Each spatial orbital can accommodate two opposite-spin electrons under the stated spin degeneracy. These are single-particle confinement energies; the electrostatic charging contribution must still be added when interpreting transport addition energies.

At terminal speed the downward buoyant weight balances the <Stokes drag>:
$$
m_bg=\zeta v_s,\qquad
v_s=\frac{2a^2(\rho_p-\rho_f)g}{9\eta}.
$$
Positive $\rho_p-\rho_f$ gives downward settling; a lighter particle would rise. With $a=10^{-7}\,\mathrm m$, $\rho_p-\rho_f=200\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, $\eta=10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$ and $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$,
$$
v_s\simeq4.36\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},
$$
about $4.4\,\mathrm{nm\,s^{-1}}$, or $0.38\,\mathrm{mm}$ per day.

The very small Reynolds number makes inertial corrections to <Stokes flow> negligible for an isolated particle. On short particle-scale distances, <Brownian motion> is much faster than this systematic drift. Nevertheless the nonzero settling speed can produce concentration gradients over sufficiently long times and large sample heights; Brownian motion does not cancel gravity.

For ideal <hard spheres>, potential energy is zero for nonoverlapping configurations and infinite for overlaps. At fixed temperature and volume, the accessible fluid and crystal configurations therefore have no energetic preference of the ordinary bonding kind: the phase with larger <entropy> has lower <Helmholtz free energy>.

Ordering particle centers costs some configurational freedom, but at a high <packing fraction> it can give each particle more usable cage/free volume than a disordered crowded arrangement. The total number of allowed vibrational and positional configurations can then be greater in a crystal despite its visible long-range order. This is <entropy-driven hard-sphere crystallization>.

The crystal need not be at close packing. At the stated volume fraction near $0.55$, substantial free space remains for thermal motion. Close packing near $0.74$ is the geometric upper bound for equal spheres, not the fraction at which the crystal first becomes thermodynamically favorable. Treating crystallization as an inevitable decrease in total entropy would confuse visible positional order with the full phase-space count.

Let $A$ be projected area. Close-packed circular footprints cover $\phi A$ and the exposed flat substrate covers $(1-\phi)A$. A hemisphere's curved area is twice its circular footprint, so the actual wetted solid area is
$$
A_{\mathrm{wet}}=2\phi A+(1-\phi)A=(1+\phi)A.
$$
The <Wenzel roughness factor> is therefore $r=A_{\mathrm{wet}}/A=1+\phi=1.9069$, not $\phi$ or $2$.

Using the <Wenzel wetting> relation,
$$
\cos\theta^*=1.9069\cos68^\circ\simeq0.7143,
\qquad \theta^*\simeq44.4^\circ.
$$
The nanostructure makes this surface **more wettable**, because planar PMMA already has $\theta<90^\circ$. Under complete Wenzel wetting, roughening amplifies the underlying hydrophilic character; it does not turn this material hydrophobic.

The calculation assumes identical chemistry on spheres and substrate, complete liquid penetration, ideal hemispheres, a macroscopic contact angle and negligible pinning/line-tension effects. Actual dried particles may have different contact geometry, deformation, contamination or trapped air. A hydrophobic coating with planar angle above $90^\circ$ would make roughness increase the angle instead. Designing a texture that retains air in a <Cassie–Baxter state> is another route, but its stability against liquid penetration must be checked.

Define $\gamma_{SV}$, $\gamma_{SL}$ and $\gamma_{LV}$ as the solid-vapor, solid-liquid and liquid-vapor interfacial free energies per actual area. For the corresponding planar material, <Young's contact-angle equation> is
$$
\gamma_{SV}-\gamma_{SL}=\gamma_{LV}\cos\theta.
$$
Consider advancing the macroscopic liquid footprint over projected area $A$, while retaining the specified wetting only to each sphere's equator. The newly wetted upper hemispheres have actual area $2\phi A$. The gaps occupy projected area $(1-\phi)A$, across which a liquid-vapor interface remains instead of solid-liquid contact.

The microscopic free-energy change, apart from the outer macroscopic droplet surface, is
$$
\frac{\Delta F}{A}
=2\phi(\gamma_{SL}-\gamma_{SV})
+(1-\phi)\gamma_{LV}.
$$
Equating this with $-\gamma_{LV}\cos\theta^*$, the macroscopic wetting contribution, yields
$$
\boxed{\cos\theta^*=2\phi\cos\theta-(1-\phi)
=\phi(2\cos\theta+1)-1.}
$$
The factor $2$ comes from the curved hemisphere area; the negative gap term is the trapped liquid-vapor interface. This combines Wenzel roughness on the wetted solid fraction with Cassie-type air gaps. It differs from both homogeneous Wenzel wetting and a Cassie formula that treats the solid contacts as flat.

For $\phi=0.9069$ and $\theta=68^\circ$, this gives $\cos\theta^*\simeq0.5864$ and $\theta^*\simeq54.1^\circ$. The hybrid state is less wettable than the fully penetrated hemispherical model, but still not hydrophobic. The result is conditional on the assumed equatorial meniscus and retained air: pinning or metastability may be required, and allowing further penetration can change the equilibrium geometry.
