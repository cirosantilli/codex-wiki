= Nonparametric statistics
{wiki}

Nonparametric statistics uses models whose effective dimension can grow with the sample size instead of fixing a finite-dimensional parametric family.

= Rank test
{parent=Nonparametric statistics}

A rank test is a <hypothesis test> whose statistic is a function of the <ranks of observations>, possibly together with fixed sample labels. Under independent observations from a common continuous <probability distribution>, exchangeable rank allocations give its exact null law without specifying that distribution. The <Wilcoxon rank sum test> is an example. Invariance under common increasing transformations follows because ranks do not change.

= Wilcoxon signed-rank test
{c}
{parent=Nonparametric statistics}
{wiki}
{title2=$W_+=\sum_{D_i>0}\operatorname{rank}(|D_i|)$}

For independent paired observations, form differences $D_i=U_i-V_i$. Assume the differences are identically distributed, continuous and symmetric about a location $\delta$. Under the zero-location <null hypothesis>, the signs are independent fair signs conditional on their absolute values. Rank the absolute differences and add ranks with positive signs. A negative-location alternative rejects for small $W_+$. Its exact conditional distribution is that of a sum of an independently selected subset of ranks, each of the $2^n$ subsets equally likely. Thus its mean is $n(n+1)/4$ and its variance is $n(n+1)(2n+1)/24$. Symmetry is essential: a zero <median> alone does not justify this signed-rank null law. Zero differences are omitted in the usual version, and tied absolute differences receive average ranks.

= Walsh average
{c}
{parent=Wilcoxon signed-rank test}
{title2=$(X_i+X_j)/2$}

The Walsh averages of a sample are $(X_i+X_j)/2$ for $1\le i\le j\le n$, including the diagonal pairs. If the absolute observations are distinct and nonzero, order them as $|X_{(1)}|<\cdots<|X_{(n)}|$. Among the $r$ pairs whose larger absolute observation is $X_{(r)}$, every sum has the sign of $X_{(r)}$. Hence the number of positive Walsh averages is exactly $\sum_r r\mathbf1_{X_{(r)}>0}$, the positive <Wilcoxon signed-rank statistic>. This proves equivalence of the corresponding tests; the Walsh averages themselves are generally dependent.

= Wilcoxon signed-rank statistic
{parent=Wilcoxon signed-rank test}
{c}
{title2=$W_+=\sum_{X_i>0}\operatorname{rank}(|X_i|)$}

For a continuous sample, the statistic also equals $\sum_i\mathbf1_{\{X_i>0\}}+\sum_{i<j}\mathbf1_{\{X_i+X_j>0\}}$. In each pair the sign of the sum is the sign of the observation with larger absolute value, proving the identity by counting its smaller-rank partners. Under a continuous symmetric null, the pair kernel has projection $F(x)-1/2$ and projection <variance> $1/12$. The <U-statistic central limit theorem> yields <asymptotic normality> of $W_+$, with <variance> asymptotic to $n^3/12$.

= Wilcoxon rank sum test
{c}
{synonym}

For two independent samples of sizes $m,n$, rank their pooled observations and let $W_X$ be the sum of the ranks of the first sample. With continuous observations, the <null hypothesis> of a common distribution makes every allocation of $m$ ranks to that sample equally probable. A one-sided alternative that the first sample is stochastically smaller rejects for small $W_X$, equivalently small $U_X$. In a common-shape location model this tests a negative location difference. Without the common-shape assumption it is not simply a test of <medians>. The shifted statistic $U_X$ counts cross-sample pairs for which the first observation exceeds the second. Ties use average ranks and a conditional <permutation test> or a tie-adjusted <normal approximation>.

= Normal approximation for a rank sum
{parent=Mann–Whitney U test}
{title2=$\mathbb EW_X=m(N+1)/2,\quad\operatorname{var}W_X=mn(N+1)/12$}

With $N=m+n$ and no ties, sampling $m$ ranks without replacement gives the displayed mean and variance. For observed rank sum below its mean, a continuity-corrected lower-tail statistic is $Z=[W_X-m(N+1)/2+1/2]/\sqrt{mn(N+1)/12}$. Ties of sizes $t_g$ replace the variance by $mn[(N+1)-\sum_g(t_g^3-t_g)/(N(N-1))]/12$. These formulas concern the raw rank sum; the <Mann–Whitney U test> statistic subtracts $m(m+1)/2$ from both the observation and its mean.

= Exact null distribution of a rank sum
{parent=Mann–Whitney U test}
{title2=$\Pr(W_X=w)=\binom{m+n}{m}^{-1}[z^mt^w]\prod_{k=1}^{m+n}(1+zt^k)$}

Under the continuous common-distribution null of the <Wilcoxon rank sum test>, pooled labels are exchangeable. The product records every choice of ranks: choosing $zt^k$ assigns rank $k$ to the first sample. Its indicated coefficient counts subsets of size $m$ and rank sum $w$. Divide by $\binom{m+n}{m}$ and sum the lower tail for the one-sided <P-value>. Equivalently the counts satisfy $a_{k,h,w}=a_{k-1,h,w}+a_{k-1,h-1,w-k}$ with $a_{0,0,0}=1$, allowing an exact integer computation.

= Exact rank-sum tail one above the minimum
{parent=Exact null distribution of a rank sum}
{title2=$\Pr\{W\le m(m+1)/2+1\}=2/\binom{m+n}{m}$}

For two independent continuous samples of positive sizes $m,n$ under a common-distribution null, each $m$-subset of ranks $1,\ldots,m+n$ is equally likely. The smallest rank sum is $m(m+1)/2$, attained only by $\{1,\ldots,m\}$. The only subset with rank sum one larger is $\{1,\ldots,m-1,m+1\}$. Hence the exact lower tail at that threshold is $2/\binom{m+n}{m}$. This follows directly by observing that any other change increases the sum by at least two.

= Fourier deconvolution
{parent=Nonparametric statistics}
{title2=$\varphi_X=\varphi_Y/\varphi_\epsilon$}

Independent additive noise $Y=X+\epsilon$ multiplies <characteristic functions>: $\varphi_Y=\varphi_X\varphi_\epsilon$. When the noise <characteristic function> is nonzero, division recovers $\varphi_X$. A smoothing kernel limits the frequencies used, controlling the amplification of sampling error where $|\varphi_\epsilon|$ is small.

= Empirical likelihood
{parent=Nonparametric statistics}
{wiki}

Empirical likelihood places probability masses on observed or data-supported values and maximizes their product subject to normalization and any estimating constraints.

= Empirical likelihood with mixed censoring
{parent=Empirical likelihood}

For independent event times with <survivor function> $S(t)=\mathbb P(T>t)$, an exact event at $t$ contributes mass $S(t-)-S(t)$, a right-censored observation at $c$ contributes $S(c)$, and an interval-censored observation $l<T\leq u$ contributes $S(l)-S(u)$. Multiplying these factors gives the event-time likelihood when the observation mechanism is noninformative. Maximizing over nonincreasing right-continuous survivor functions gives a <nonparametric maximum-likelihood estimator> rather than requiring a parametric density.

= Likelihood support reduction under censoring
{parent=Empirical likelihood with mixed censoring}
{title2=$L(w)=\prod_i\left(\sum_j A_{ij}w_j\right)$}

Partition the time axis into cells on which membership in each observed exact-event or <censoring> set is constant. Let $w_j$ be a cell's probability mass and $A_{ij}$ indicate whether cell $j$ is permitted by observation $i$. The <empirical likelihood> has the displayed form, with nonnegative masses summing to one. Columns of $A$ that agree can be combined. If column $j$ is componentwise dominated by another column $k$, moving all mass from $j$ to $k$ cannot reduce any factor, so an unconstrained <nonparametric maximum-likelihood estimator> can omit column $j$. The remaining <log-likelihood> is a <concave function> of the masses. Extra constraints can prevent a domination move; for a fixed <survivor function> value, split cells at the constrained time and retain the associated linear mass constraint.

= Monotonicity bound for a mixed-censoring likelihood
{parent=Empirical likelihood with mixed censoring}
{title2=$L(S)\leq(1-u)(u-v)^2uv$}

Suppose three exact event times $t_1<t_2<t_3$ coexist with a right-censored time $b$ and an interval $(a,c]$, ordered as $t_1<a<b<t_2<c<t_3$. Set $u=S(a)$ and $v=S(c)$. Monotonicity bounds the three event masses by $1-u$, $u-v$ and $v$, while $S(b)\leq u$. Thus the mixed-censoring likelihood is bounded by the displayed expression. Equality is attained by putting all mass at $t_1,t_2,t_3$. The reduced log-likelihood is strictly concave for $0<v<u<1$, and its stationary equations yield $u=4/5$, $v=4/15$. The fitted masses are $1/5$, $8/15$, $4/15$.

= Nonparametric maximum-likelihood estimator
{parent=Nonparametric statistics}

A nonparametric maximum-likelihood estimator maximizes a likelihood over a nonparametric family of distributions or functions rather than over a fixed finite-dimensional parameter space.

= Density estimation
{parent=Nonparametric statistics}
{wiki}

Density estimation constructs an estimate of an unknown <probability density function> from sampled observations.

= Haar density estimator
{c}
{parent=Density estimation}
{title2=$\widehat f_j(x)=\frac{2^j}{n}\sum_i\mathbf1_{I_j(x)}(X_i)$}

A Haar density estimator estimates the <Haar scaling function> coefficients by sample averages. It is exactly the <histogram> with cells of width $2^{-j}$. Its expectation is the <Haar projection> of the density. If the density has bounded derivative $L$ and is bounded by $B$, its pointwise expected absolute error is at most $L2^{-j}+\sqrt{B2^j/n}$: the first term is the <Haar projection error for a Lipschitz function>, and the second follows from the <variance> of a cell count and the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. Choosing $2^j\asymp n^{1/3}$ gives error $O(n^{-1/3})$ while preserving nonnegativity and integral one.

= Quadratic density derivative functional
{parent=Density estimation}
{title2=$J_r(f)=\int|f^{(r)}|^2$}

The squared <L2 norm> of a <probability density function> <derivative> measures roughness and appears in optimal smoothing constants. For $r=2$, its <directional derivative> along a smooth compactly supported perturbation $a$ is $2\int f^{\prime\prime}a^{\prime\prime}$. When <integration by parts> is valid twice, this equals $2\int f^{(4)}a$. Thus regular root-$n$ estimation requires substantially more regularity than merely estimating a smooth <probability density function>.

= Diagonal correction for a quadratic density derivative estimate
{parent=Quadratic density derivative functional}
{title2=$\widehat J_{2,b}=\frac1{n(n-1)b^5}\sum_{i\ne j}A((X_i-X_j)/b)$}

For $A(v)=\int L^{\prime\prime}(t)L^{\prime\prime}(t+v)\,dt$, this <U-statistic> has <expectation> $\|L_b*f^{\prime\prime}\|_2^2$. It removes the diagonal term $\|L^{\prime\prime}\|_2^2/(nb^5)$ from the integral of a squared <second derivative kernel density estimator>, but leaves smoothing <bias of an estimator>.

= Nearest neighbour density estimation
{parent=Density estimation}

= Nearest neighbour density estimator
{synonym}

In one dimension, a <nearest neighbour density estimator> is $\widehat f_{(k)}(x)=k/[2(n+1)\rho_{(k)}(x)]$. It uses the <nearest neighbour distance> to select an interval containing a fixed number of observations. The factor $n+1$ corresponds to the exact <expected value> of the associated <uniform order statistic>.

= Kernel for density estimation
{parent=Density estimation}

= Kernels for density estimation
{synonym}

A kernel for density estimation is an integrable function $K$ with unit integral, usually nonnegative and centered, whose rescaling $K_h(u)=h^{-1}K(u/h)$ averages observations over bandwidth $h$.

= Triweight kernel
{parent=Kernel for density estimation}
{title2=$K(x)=\frac{35}{32}(1-x^2)^3\mathbf1_{|x|<1}$}

The triweight kernel is a compactly supported symmetric probability density with variance $1/9$. Its value and first two derivatives vanish at the support endpoints. Its integrated squared second derivative is $35$, and it minimizes that curvature among twice continuously differentiable densities with zero mean and the same variance, by the <triweight density minimizes integrated squared curvature> inequality.

= Triweight density minimizes integrated squared curvature
{parent=Triweight kernel}
{title2=$R(h'')\geq R(f_0'')$}

Let $f_0$ be the <triweight kernel> and h a twice continuously differentiable density with matching zero mean and variance. Put $e=h-f_0$ and $c=35/32$. Two integrations by parts over its support give $\int e''f_0''=48c[h(1)+h(-1)]+c\int_{|x|>1}(360x^2-72)h(x)dx\geq0$, using the zero mass and second moment of e. Therefore $R(h'')=R(f_0'')+R(e'')+2\int e''f_0''\geq R(f_0'')$. The endpoint terms matter because the third derivative jumps there.

= Gaussian density kernel
{c}
{parent=Kernel for density estimation}
{title2=$K_N(u)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-u^2/2}$}

The Gaussian density kernel is the standard <normal probability density> used as a unit-integral <kernel for density estimation>. Its second moment is one and $R(K_N)=1/(2\sqrt\pi)$. Its <canonical kernel> has scale $(2\sqrt\pi)^{-1/5}$. It is a function of one smoothing displacement, distinct from the two-argument <Gaussian kernel> used as a <positive-semidefinite kernel>.

= Epanechnikov kernel
{c}
{parent=Kernel for density estimation}
{title2=$K_E(u)=\tfrac34(1-u^2)\mathbf1_{\{|u|\leq1\}}$}

The Epanechnikov <kernel for density estimation> is a nonnegative symmetric truncated parabola with integral one, $\mu_2(K_E)=1/5$, and $R(K_E)=3/5$. It has the smallest optimized <asymptotic mean integrated squared error> among nonnegative second-order density kernels, up to rescaling. Its <canonical kernel> has scale $15^{1/5}$ and support $[-15^{1/5},15^{1/5}]$.

= Optimality of the Epanechnikov kernel
{parent=Epanechnikov kernel}

Fix the second moment $v>0$. Put $B=\sqrt{5v}$ and $E(u)=3(B^2-u^2)_+/(4B^3)$. For every nonnegative unit-integral <kernel for density estimation> $K$ with the same second moment and finite $R(K)$, the mass and second-moment constraints give $\int(B^2-u^2)(K-E)=0$. Outside $[-B,B]$, $E=0$ and $(B^2-u^2)K\leq0$, so $\int E(K-E)\geq0$. Expanding the square gives $R(K)-R(E)=\int(K-E)^2+2\int E(K-E)\geq0$, with equality only when $K=E$ almost everywhere. Since $R(K)^2\mu_2(K)$ is scale invariant, rescaled <Epanechnikov kernels> minimize the optimized <asymptotic mean integrated squared error> over all such second moments. This conclusion concerns nonnegative second-order kernels; signed higher-order kernels obey a different bias expansion.

= Flat-top kernel for density estimation
{parent=Kernel for density estimation}
{title2=$\widehat K(t)=1\text{ near }0$}

Choose a real even smooth compactly supported <Fourier transform> $\eta$ equal to one near zero and let $K$ be its inverse <Fourier transform>. Repeated integration by parts makes $K$ a <Schwartz function>. It is real, integrable and square-integrable, with unit integral. <Moment differentiation of the Fourier transform> gives $\int x^jK(x)dx=i^j\eta^{(j)}(0)=0$ for every positive integer $j$. Such a <kernel for density estimation> therefore satisfies arbitrary finite-order moment cancellation, and necessarily takes negative values. If the transform plateau contains the frequency support of a <bandlimited function> $f$, the <convolution theorem> proves $K*f=f$.

= Parametric-rate kernel estimation of a bandlimited density
{parent=Flat-top kernel for density estimation}
{title2=$\sup_x\mathbb E|f_n^K(x,1)-f(x)|=O(n^{-1/2})$}

Let a bounded <probability density function> have <Fourier transform> supported in the plateau of a <flat-top kernel for density estimation>. At fixed <smoothing bandwidth> one, the <convolution theorem> gives $K*f=f$, so the <kernel density estimator> is pointwise unbiased. Its <variance> is at most $\|f\|_\infty\|K\|_2^2/n$, and the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> proves the displayed rate. An explicit example is $f(x)=(1-\cos x)/(\pi x^2)$, with value $1/(2\pi)$ at zero and transform $(1-|t|)_+$. Direct inverse Fourier integration gives the formula. The function is nonnegative, integrable and has integral one because its transform at zero is one. Compact frequency support also gives bounded derivatives of every order.

= Kernel of order ell
{title2=$\ell$}
{parent=Kernel for density estimation}

= Kernels of order ell
{synonym}

A kernel is of order $\ell$ when its moments of orders $1,\ldots,\ell-1$ vanish and its absolute moment of order $\ell$ is finite.

= Legendre polynomial kernel construction
{c}
{parent=Kernel of order ell}
{title2=$K_\ell(u)=\mathbf1_{[-1,1]}(u)\sum_{m<\ell}\phi_m(0)\phi_m(u)$}

For normalized <Legendre polynomials>, this <kernel for density estimation> reproduces evaluation at zero on every <polynomial> of degree below $\ell$: expand $p$ in the <orthonormal basis> to obtain $\int K_\ell p=p(0)$. Taking monomials proves integral one and zero moments of orders $1,\ldots,\ell-1$; <compact support> gives finite absolute moments. If exact order requires a nonzero moment of order $\ell$, add $(\phi_\ell(0)+1)\phi_\ell\mathbf1_{[-1,1]}$. Lower moments remain unchanged. Writing $a_\ell=\langle u^\ell,\phi_\ell\rangle\ne0$ and evaluating the full expansion of $u^\ell$ at zero shows that the new moment is $a_\ell$. A useful three-derivative example is $K_3=(9-15u^2)\mathbf1_{[-1,1]}/8$, whose first two moments vanish and whose squared <L2 norm> is $9/8$.

= Kernel density estimator
{synonym}

Given <independent and identically distributed random variables> $X_1,\ldots,X_n$, the kernel density estimate is
$$
\widehat f_{n,h,K}(x)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nK_h(x-X_i).
$$

= Exact mean integrated squared error of a kernel density estimator
{parent=Kernel density estimation}
{title2=$\operatorname{MISE}=R(K_h*f-f)+n^{-1}[R(K_h)-R(K_h*f)]$}

For independent observations with common density f, the <kernel density estimator> has mean $K_h*f$. Its pointwise variance is $n^{-1}[(K_h^2*f)-(K_h*f)^2]$. Integrate the variance and add integrated squared bias to get the displayed exact <mean integrated squared error>, assuming the integrals are finite. Since $R(K_h)=R(K)/h$, this also separates the smoothing bias from the stochastic variance.

= Pointwise optimal kernel bandwidth
{parent=Kernel density estimation}
{title2=$h_{\rm AMSE}(x)=\left(\frac{R(K)f(x)}{n\mu_2(K)^2f''(x)^2}\right)^{1/5}$}

For a second-order <kernel density estimator>, if $f(x)>0$ and $f''(x)\ne0$, the leading pointwise <asymptotic mean squared error> is $R(K)f(x)/(nh)+\mu_2(K)^2f''(x)^2h^4/4$. Differentiation gives the displayed interior optimum. At a zero of $f$, the leading variance term vanishes and this two-term approximation has no positive interior minimizer; at a zero of $f''$, a higher-order bias expansion is needed.

= Normal-density local-to-global bandwidth ratio
{parent=Pointwise optimal kernel bandwidth}
{title2=$\inf_x h_{\rm AMSE}(x)/h_{\rm AMISE}=(9e^5/8192)^{1/10}$}

For the <standard normal density>, $f''(x)=(x^2-1)f(x)$ and $R(f'')=3/(8\sqrt\pi)$. The fifth power of the bandwidth ratio is $3\sqrt2\,e^{x^2/2}/[8(x^2-1)^2]$. Its global minima occur at $x=\pm\sqrt5$; $x=0$ is only the minimum within the interval between the two inflection points. The ratio diverges as $x$ approaches either inflection point.

= Canonical kernel for density estimation
{parent=Kernel density estimation}
{title2=$K_c(u)=c^{-1}K(u/c),\quad c=[R(K)/\mu_2(K)^2]^{1/5}$}

= Canonical kernel
{synonym}

For a nonnegative symmetric unit-integral <kernel for density estimation> with $0<\mu_2(K)=\int u^2K(u)\,du<\infty$ and $R(K)=\int K^2<\infty$, the canonical scaling satisfies $R(K_c)=\mu_2(K_c)^2$. Since $R(K_c)=R(K)/c$ and $\mu_2(K_c)=c^2\mu_2(K)$, the unique scale is $c=[R(K)/\mu_2(K)^2]^{1/5}$. Consequently its <asymptotic mean integrated squared error> is $C(K)[(nh)^{-1}+h^4R(f^{\prime\prime})/4]$, where $C(K)=R(K)^{4/5}\mu_2(K)^{2/5}$ is invariant under kernel rescaling. The leading optimal <smoothing bandwidth> becomes $[nR(f^{\prime\prime})]^{-1/5}$, independent of the kernel shape. Unit second moment is a different normalization and does not have this separation property.

= Pointwise central limit theorem for a kernel density estimator
{parent=Kernel density estimation}
{title2=$\sqrt{nh}(\widehat f_{n,h}(x)-f(x))\xrightarrow{d}N(0,f(x)\|K\|_2^2)$}

Let the <kernel for density estimation> have integral one, be bounded and have compact support. For a <probability density function> with bounded derivative, assume $h\to0$, $nh\to\infty$ and $nh^3\to0$. The <bias of a kernel density estimator> is $O(h)$, negligible on the $\sqrt{nh}$ scale. For $Y_{n,i}=K((x-X_i)/h)$, $h^{-1}\operatorname{Var}Y_{n,i}\to f(x)\|K\|_2^2$. The centered summands divided by $\sqrt{nh}$ are uniformly small, so the <Lindeberg-Feller central limit theorem> applies. The condition $nh^3\to0$ alone is insufficient: with $h=n^{-2}$ and a compactly supported kernel, with probability tending to one no observation enters the kernel window, producing a degenerate limit at a point with positive density.

= Third-order pointwise kernel error bound
{parent=Kernel density estimation}
{title2=$\mathbb E|f_n^K(x,h)-f(x)|\leq C_1h^3+C_2(nh)^{-1/2}$}

Suppose a <probability density function> and its third <derivative> are bounded. A square-integrable unit-integral <kernel for density estimation> with first two moments zero and $\mu_3=\int|u|^3|K(u)|du<\infty$ has <bias of a kernel density estimator> at most $\|f^{(3)}\|_\infty\mu_3h^3/6$, by <Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder> and moment cancellation. Independence gives <variance> at most $\|f\|_\infty\|K\|_2^2/(nh)$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> bounds the expected centred absolute deviation by the square root of this variance. Adding the bias proves the inequality uniformly in $x$. The choice $h=n^{-1/7}$ gives expected absolute error $O(n^{-3/7})$.

= Integrated variance of a kernel density estimator
{parent=Kernel density estimation}
{title2=$\mathbb E\|\widehat f_{n,h}-K_h*f\|_2^2$}

For an $L^2$ kernel and an independent sample from a density $f$, the centred <kernel density estimator> has
$$
\mathbb E\|\widehat f_{n,h}-K_h*f\|_2^2=\frac1n\left(\frac{\|K\|_2^2}{h}-\|K_h*f\|_2^2\right).
$$
Independence removes cross terms and <Tonelli theorem> integrates the pointwise variance. <Young's convolution inequality> ensures $K_h*f\in L^2$ even without $f\in L^2$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> yields the expected-norm bound $\mathbb E\|\widehat f_{n,h}-K_h*f\|_2\le\|K\|_2/\sqrt{nh}$.

= Gaussian autoregressive kernel variance correction
{c}
{parent=Integrated variance of a kernel density estimator}
{title2=$g_h(j)=\sqrt\pi[(1+h^2-\rho^j)^{-1/2}-(1+h^2)^{-1/2}]$}

For a stationary <autoregressive process of order one> with unit-variance <normal distribution> marginals and fixed $0<\rho<1$, the <Gaussian kernel> covariance correction at lag $j$ has the displayed form. The difference of two observations has variance $2(1-\rho^j)$, and their <characteristic functions> reduce the Fourier integral to the difference of two <Gaussian integrals>. The <mean value theorem> gives $0\leq g_h(j)\leq\sqrt\pi\rho^j/[2(1-\rho)^{3/2}]$, uniformly over $h>0$. Thus the integrated variance differs from its independent-sample counterpart by $O(n^{-1})$. The marginal bias is unchanged, so this dependence does not alter the leading optimal bandwidth or the $n^{-4/5}$ <mean integrated squared error> for a second-order density kernel.

= Pointwise minimax rate for Hölder density estimation
{parent=Kernel density estimation}

Over a one-dimensional Hölder density class of smoothness $\beta$, the optimized pointwise mean-squared error has order $n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+1)}$. A kernel of sufficiently high order balances squared bias $h^{2\beta}$ against variance $(nh)^{-1}$.

= Bias of a kernel density estimator
{parent=Kernel density estimation}

For a symmetric bandwidth-$h$ averaging kernel and a density with bounded first derivative, the interior pointwise bias is at most a constant times $h\|f'\|_\infty$. Near a support boundary, an uncorrected symmetric kernel can instead have nonvanishing bias.

= Integrated second-order kernel bias bound
{parent=Bias of a kernel density estimator}
{title2=$\|K_h*f-f\|_2\le h^2\mu_2(K)\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_2/2$}

For a nonnegative symmetric unit-integral <kernel for density estimation> and $f\in H^2(\mathbb R)$, the integral <Taylor remainder> and <Minkowski inequality> give $\|K_h*f-f\|_2\le h^2\mu_2(K)\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_2/2$. Translation preserves the <L2 norm>. For a signed kernel the same proof requires the absolute moment $\int u^2|K(u)|\,du$.

= Integrated squared bias from a density jump
{parent=Bias of a kernel density estimator}
{title2=$\int\operatorname{Bias}^2\asymp h$}

An uncorrected nonnegative symmetric <kernel density estimator> can leak an order-one amount across a jump in a <probability density function>. If its transition region has width proportional to the <smoothing bandwidth> $h$, the <integrated mean squared error> includes squared <bias of an estimator> of order $h$, rather than the order $h^4$ familiar for twice-smooth interior densities. For the unit-width uniform window and the <exponential distribution> of rate one, the integrated squared <bias of an estimator> is $h/12+o(h)$.

= Box-kernel bias of a piecewise constant density
{parent=Integrated squared bias from a density jump}
{title2=$\|K_h*f-f\|_2^2=\frac h{12}\sum_r\Delta_r^2$}

For a density with finitely many jumps $\Delta_r$ at separated points and the <unit-width box kernel>, take $h$ smaller than all consecutive jump spacings. The smoothing bias is supported in disjoint length-$h$ neighbourhoods of the jumps. At each jump its two sides form triangles of height $|\Delta_r|/2$, and exact integration gives
$$
\|K_h*f-f\|_2^2=\frac h{12}\sum_r\Delta_r^2.
$$
Combined with the <integrated variance of a kernel density estimator>, this gives expected $L^2$ error $O(n^{-1/4})$ at $h\asymp n^{-1/2}$.

= Second-order pointwise bias bound for kernel density estimation
{parent=Bias of a kernel density estimator}
{title2=$|\mathbb E\widehat f_h(x)-f(x)|\leq h^2\mu_2(K)\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_\infty/2$}

Let a nonnegative <kernel for density estimation> $K$ have integral one, zero first moment and finite second moment $\mu_2(K)$. If a <probability density function> $f$ has bounded <second derivative>, its <kernel density estimator> has pointwise <bias> at most $h^2\mu_2(K)\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_\infty/2$. Apply the <Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder> to $f(x-hu)$ and integrate: the first-order term vanishes and the remainder is bounded by $h^2u^2\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_\infty/2$. For $K=\mathbb1_{[-1/2,1/2]}$, $\mu_2(K)=1/12$.

= Lepski bandwidth selection method
{c}
{parent=Kernel density estimation}

Lepski's method compares estimators across an ordered bandwidth grid. It chooses the largest bandwidth whose confidence interval has nonempty intersection with every interval at a smaller bandwidth, thereby adapting to unknown smoothness while controlling excessive undersmoothing.

= Derivative kernel density estimator
{title2=$\widehat f'_{n,h,K}$}
{parent=Kernel density estimation}

Differentiating a smooth kernel estimate gives
$$
\widehat f'_{n,h,K}(x)=\frac1{nh^2}\sum_{i=1}^nK'\!\left(\frac{x-X_i}{h}\right).
$$
Its integrated variance is at most $\lVert K'\rVert_2^2/(nh^3)$.

= Second derivative kernel density estimator
{parent=Derivative kernel density estimator}
{title2=$\widehat f_b^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac1{nb^3}\sum_iL^{\prime\prime}((x-X_i)/b)$}

Differentiating a twice differentiable <kernel density estimator> twice estimates $f^{\prime\prime}$. Its <integrated variance of a kernel density estimator> analogue is at most $\|L^{\prime\prime}\|_2^2/(nb^5)$. This explains why <derivative> estimation requires more smoothing than ordinary <density estimation>.

= Integrated squared density curvature
{parent=Second derivative kernel density estimator}
{title2=$R(f'')=\int(f''(x))^2dx$}

The integrated squared second derivative measures a smooth density's curvature in L2. It supplies the leading integrated squared bias constant of a second-order <kernel density estimator>. For nonzero second moment $\mu_2(K)$, the <asymptotic mean integrated squared error> has bias term $h^4\mu_2(K)^2R(f'')/4$, so greater curvature calls for a smaller <smoothing bandwidth>.

= Scale-invariant density curvature
{parent=Integrated squared density curvature}
{title2=$D(f)=\sigma(f)^5R(f'')$}

For $f_a(x)=af(ax)$ with a positive, differentiation and change of variables give $R(f_a'')=a^5R(f'')$, while its standard deviation is $\sigma(f)/a$. Their product D is therefore invariant under scale changes, and translation also leaves it unchanged. Raw integrated squared curvature can be arbitrarily small under dilation even though this dimensionless shape measure is unchanged.

= Nikolsky class
{c}
{title2=$\mathcal N(\beta,L)$}
{parent=Derivative kernel density estimator}

The $L^2$ Nikolsky class $\mathcal N(\beta,L)$ consists of functions with $r=\lfloor\beta\rfloor$ square-integrable derivatives whose highest derivative obeys the translation bound
$$
\lVert g^{(r)}(\mathord\cdot+t)-g^{(r)}\rVert_2
\leq L|t|^{\beta-r},
$$
with the corresponding finite-difference definition at integer smoothness.

= MISE rate for derivative kernel density estimation
{title2=$n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+3)}$}
{c}
{parent=Derivative kernel density estimator}

If $f'\in\mathcal N(\beta,L)$ and the kernel has sufficient order, the mean integrated squared error of $\widehat f'$ is bounded by
$$
\frac{C_1}{nh^3}+C_2h^{2\beta}.
$$
Balancing the terms gives $h\asymp n^{-1/(2\beta+3)}$ and rate $n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+3)}$.

= Nonparametric regression
{parent=Nonparametric statistics}
{wiki}

Nonparametric regression estimates a regression function without restricting it to a fixed finite-dimensional family.

= Random-design nonparametric regression
{parent=Nonparametric regression}
{title2=$Y_i=m(X_i)+\varepsilon_i,\quad\mathbb E[\varepsilon_i\mid X_i]=0$}

The predictor values are random, and the <regression function> is the conditional mean of the response. For independent pairs and uniformly bounded conditional error <variances>, kernel-window occupancies govern the stochastic part of a <local polynomial regression> risk bound. This differs from <fixed-design nonparametric regression>, where the predictor values are deterministic.

= Smoothing parameter
{parent=Nonparametric regression}
{title2=$\lambda$}

A smoothing parameter controls the amount of averaging or regularization in a fitted function. Increasing the penalty multiplier $\lambda$ in a <cubic smoothing spline> discourages curvature and generally reduces <effective degrees of freedom>. Increasing a <smoothing bandwidth> enlarges local averaging neighbourhoods. Software may use a monotone transformed parameter such as `spar`, so its numerical value should not be confused with the raw penalty multiplier.

= Smoothing spline
{parent=Nonparametric regression}
{title2=$\sum_i(Y_i-g(x_i))^2+\lambda\int_a^b(g^{(m)})^2\,dx$}
{wiki}

A penalized function estimate balancing a residual loss against an integrated <derivative> penalty. For <squared-error loss> and the <second derivative roughness penalty>, the estimate is a <cubic smoothing spline>. Higher <derivative> orders give other spline degrees.

= Quadratic smoothing spline for interval averages
{parent=Smoothing spline}
{title2=$\sum_i(Y_i-L_i g)^2+\lambda\int_a^b(g')^2$}

Let $L_i g$ be the average of $g$ on successive knot intervals. Minimizing the displayed loss over $C^1[a,b]$ gives a <quadratic spline> with constant tails outside the first and last knots. To prove the reduction, choose the unique constant-tail quadratic spline $q$ with the same interval averages as a candidate $g$, and put $v=g-q$. Since $q''$ is constant on every knot interval, $q'$ is continuous and vanishes on both tails, <integration by parts> gives $\int q'v'=-\sum_iq''|_{I_i}\int_{I_i}v=0$. Consequently the data loss is unchanged and $\int(g')^2=\int(q')^2+\int(v')^2$. The penalty decreases strictly unless $v$ is constant; its zero interval averages then force $v=0$. The finite-dimensional quadratic optimization on this spline space also gives a unique minimizer because the interval-average map is injective.

= Cubic smoothing spline
{parent=Smoothing spline}
{title2=$\widehat g_\lambda=N(I+\lambda\Gamma)^{-1}\mathbf Y$}

= Natural cubic smoothing spline
{synonym}

For $\lambda>0$ and at least two distinct design points, the unique $C^2[a,b]$ minimizer of squared residuals plus $\lambda J(g)$ is $N(I+\lambda\Gamma)^{-1}\mathbf Y$. The <minimum roughness property of the natural cubic spline interpolant> reduces the optimization to the value vector. The <matrix> $I+\lambda\Gamma$ is a <positive-definite matrix>, so the normal equations have a unique solution; the interpolation equality case gives uniqueness as a function. Linear tails are part of the natural boundary convention.

= Roughness-matrix formula for a natural cubic smoothing spline
{parent=Cubic smoothing spline}
{title2=$A_\lambda=(I+\lambda K)^{-1}$}

For distinct ordered observations $x_i$, let $s_u$ be their <natural cubic spline interpolant> with values $u_i$. Its roughness is a <quadratic form>, $\int(s_u'')^2=u^TKu$, where $K$ is positive semidefinite and its nullspace consists of vectors sampled from affine functions. Minimizing <penalized least squares> $\|y-u\|^2+\lambda u^TKu$ gives $\widehat u=(I+\lambda K)^{-1}y$. Thus the <smoothing matrix> is $A_\lambda=(I+\lambda K)^{-1}$ and its <effective degrees of freedom> are $\operatorname{tr}(A_\lambda)$. As $\lambda\downarrow0$ the fit approaches interpolation and the trace approaches $n$; as $\lambda\to\infty$ it approaches the least-squares affine line and the trace approaches $2$.

= Fixed-design nonparametric regression
{parent=Nonparametric regression}

The <fixed-design nonparametric regression> model observes $Y_i=m(x_i)+\sigma\epsilon_i$ at deterministic design points $x_i$. For <independent and identically distributed random variables> $\epsilon_i$ with the standard <normal distribution>, comparing two mean functions has <Kullback-Leibler divergence> $\sum_i(m_1(x_i)-m_0(x_i))^2/(2\sigma^2)$.

= Wavelet regression estimator
{parent=Fixed-design nonparametric regression}
{title2=$\widehat m_J=P_J\widetilde Y_n$}

At design sites $i/n$, let $\widetilde Y_n$ be the step function taking response $Y_i$ on $((i-1)/n,i/n]$. Project it onto an <interval-adapted wavelet basis> through resolution $J$. Each coefficient is $\widehat c_\lambda=\sum_iY_i\int_{I_i}b_\lambda(t)dt$. Its <expectation> is the corresponding coefficient of the step function of sampled regression means. For a <Haar wavelet> basis, dyadic sample size and resolution at most the sampling scale, this is exactly $n^{-1}\sum_iY_i b_\lambda(i/n)$ with right-closed pointwise versions. Independent errors of <variance> $\sigma^2$ give coefficient variance at most $\sigma^2/n$: <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> on each sampling cell yields $\sum_i(\int_{I_i}b_\lambda)^2\leq\|b_\lambda\|_2^2/n$.

= Wavelet coefficient thresholding
{parent=Wavelet regression estimator}
{title2=$\widehat b\mapsto\widehat b\mathbf1_{\{|\widehat b|>\tau\}}$}

Hard thresholding keeps detail coefficients whose magnitude exceeds a prescribed threshold and sets the rest to zero, while retaining the coarse approximation coefficients. <Soft thresholding> instead uses $\operatorname{sgn}(\widehat b)(|\widehat b|-\tau)_+$. The <simultaneous wavelet coefficient noise bound> motivates thresholds of order $\sigma\sqrt{\log n/n}$ in a normalized Gaussian regression model with at most order $n$ details. This is a nonlinear selection rule: coefficients can be removed individually rather than discarding an entire finest resolution.

= Simultaneous wavelet coefficient noise bound
{parent=Wavelet coefficient thresholding}
{title2=$\tau_\lambda=\sigma q_\lambda\sqrt{2\log(2N/\delta)}$}

For coefficient weights $w_{\lambda i}$ and independent mean-zero <sub-Gaussian random variables> of parameter $\sigma$, put $q_\lambda^2=\sum_iw_{\lambda i}^2$. Multiplying moment-generating-function bounds shows that the weighted noise has parameter $\sigma q_\lambda$. The exponential Markov inequality, optimized in its parameter, gives tail bound $2\exp(-t^2/(2\sigma^2q_\lambda^2))$. At the displayed thresholds, a <union bound> over $N$ coefficients gives simultaneous control with <probability> at least $1-\delta$. No coefficient independence is required. Integrated wavelet weights satisfy $q_\lambda\leq n^{-1/2}$. Finite variance alone only supports the weaker Chebyshev choice $\tau_\lambda=\sigma q_\lambda\sqrt{N/\delta}$.

= Window occupancy for an equally spaced regression design
{parent=Fixed-design nonparametric regression}
{title2=$N_x\ge\frac n4\min(h,1)$}

Let $x_i=i/n$, $1\le i\le n$, and let $N_x$ count sites within distance $h/2$ of $x\in[0,1]$. If $nh\ge2$, a nearest site lies in the closed window, so $N_x\ge1$. A clipped window has length at least $\min(h,1)/2$, and a grid count gives $N_x\ge n\min(h,1)/2-1$. Combining these two bounds yields $N_x\ge n\min(h,1)/4$. For $0<h\le2$, the stronger clipped-length bound $h/2$ directly gives $N_x\ge\lfloor nh/2\rfloor\ge nh/4$. These estimates include both endpoints of the domain.

= Pointwise minimax rate for Lipschitz regression
{parent=Fixed-design nonparametric regression}

At an interior target point in regular <fixed-design nonparametric regression>, mean functions with <Lipschitz bound> $L$ have pointwise <minimax risk> of order $(L\sigma^2/n)^{2/3}$ for fixed positive $L,\sigma$ as $n\to\infty$. A <smoothing bandwidth> of order $(\sigma^2/(nL^2))^{1/3}$ balances squared <bias of an estimator> and <variance>; the <two-point lower bound with a triangular bump> gives the matching lower rate.

= Linear estimator in nonparametric regression
{parent=Nonparametric regression}

A linear estimator at $x_0$ has the form $\widehat m(x_0)=\sum_iW_i(x_0)Y_i$, where the weights depend on the design and tuning parameters but not on the observed responses.

= Smoothing matrix
{title2=$S$}
{parent=Linear estimator in nonparametric regression}

= Influence matrix
{synonym}

A smoothing matrix maps an observation vector linearly to fitted values, $\widehat Y=SY$. Its diagonal entries quantify each observation's direct contribution to its own fitted value.

= Kernel regression
{parent=Nonparametric regression}
{wiki}

<Kernel regression> estimates a regression function using localized kernel weights rather than a fixed finite-dimensional functional form. The <Nadaraya–Watson estimator> is the local-constant version; <local polynomial regression> improves its local approximation by fitting a polynomial.

= Local polynomial regression
{parent=Nonparametric regression}
{wiki}

= Local polynomial estimator
{synonym}

Local polynomial regression fits a low-degree polynomial by <weighted least squares> near each target point.

= Mean absolute error bound for local polynomial regression
{parent=Local polynomial regression}
{title2=$\mathbb E|\widehat m(x)-m(x)|\le C((nh)^{-1/2}+h^s)$}

For equally spaced <fixed-design nonparametric regression>, independent mean-zero uniformly finite-variance errors, a bounded compactly supported kernel, and a uniformly invertible local <Gram matrix>, degree $\ell\ge s-1$ gives the displayed bound for $s$ bounded <derivatives>. Kernel weights have absolute sum $O(1)$ and squared sum $O((nh)^{-1})$. <Polynomial reproduction> removes a degree-$s-1$ <Taylor polynomial>. Without the degree requirement the general <bias of an estimator> is only $O(h^{\min(s,\ell+1)})$.

= Local linear regression
{parent=Local polynomial regression}
{title2=$\widehat f(x)=\widehat a_x$}

Local linear regression fits $a+b(X_i-x)$ to responses near each target $x$ by <weighted least squares>, then returns the fitted intercept. With weights $w_i=K((X_i-x)/h)$, put $S_k=\sum_iw_i(X_i-x)^k$ and $T_k=\sum_iw_i(X_i-x)^kY_i$. If $S_0S_2-S_1^2>0$, then $\widehat f(x)=(S_2T_0-S_1T_1)/(S_0S_2-S_1^2)$. Its <polynomial reproduction property of local polynomial regression> reproduces affine functions, reducing boundary bias relative to a local constant fit.

= Kernel for nonparametric regression
{parent=Local polynomial regression}

= Regression kernel
{synonym}

A nonnegative <regression kernel> supplies weights $K((x_i-x)/h)$ in a local <weighted least squares> fit. Unlike a <probability density function>, its integral need not equal one, because rescaling every weight does not change the fitted coefficients.

= Smoothing bandwidth
{parent=Kernel for nonparametric regression}
{title2=$h$}

A <smoothing bandwidth> determines the spatial scale over which a <regression kernel> averages data. Larger values reduce <variance> and may increase the <bias of an estimator>.

= Plug-in bandwidth selection
{parent=Smoothing bandwidth}
{title2=$\widehat h=[R(K)/(\mu_2(K)^2n\widehat R(f''))]^{1/5}$}

Estimate the unknown curvature in the AMISE minimizer and substitute it into that formula. A consistent positive curvature estimate makes the selected bandwidth ratio-consistent. Pilot smoothing is needed because differentiating an undersmoothed density estimator magnifies variance. A normal-reference pilot can initialize an estimate of a higher derivative functional; the final density-curvature estimate is computed from the data rather than assuming the density is normal.

= Fourth-derivative pilot estimate of density curvature
{parent=Plug-in bandwidth selection}
{title2=$\widehat R_g^{(2)}=n^{-2}\sum_{i,j}K_g^{(4)}(X_i-X_j)$}

Twice integrating by parts gives $R(f'')=\int f f^{(4)}$. Estimate this by averaging a fourth-derivative <kernel density estimator> at the observations. Its expectation contains diagonal term $K^{(4)}(0)/(ng^5)$ and leading smoothing bias $-g^2\mu_2(K)R(f''')/2$. Balancing them yields pilot scale $g\asymp R(f''')^{-1/7}n^{-1/7}$. With suitable derivatives and tails, the estimation error is bounded by $O_p(g^2+(ng^5)^{-1}+n^{-1/2}+n^{-1}g^{-9/2})$, giving an $O_p(n^{-2/7})$ relative-bandwidth bound after substitution. More carefully calibrated bias cancellation can improve that bound.

= Uniform smoothing kernel
{parent=Kernel for nonparametric regression}
{title2=$K(u)=\tfrac12\mathbf1_{\{|u|\leq1\}}$}

= Uniform kernel
{synonym}

The <uniform smoothing kernel> assigns equal weight inside the window $[x-h,x+h]$ and zero weight outside. Its <Nadaraya–Watson estimator> is the average of observations in that window.

= Unit-width box kernel
{parent=Uniform smoothing kernel}
{title2=$K(u)=\mathbf1_{[-1/2,1/2]}(u)$}

The unit-width centred box kernel is $K(u)=\mathbf1_{[-1/2,1/2]}(u)$. It has integral one and squared $L^2$ norm one. Its rescaling $K_h(u)=h^{-1}K(u/h)$ averages a function on intervals of length $h$. It is obtained from the <uniform smoothing kernel> with support $[-1,1]$ by rescaling, and is useful in both <kernel density estimation> and <local polynomial regression>.

= Local polynomial Gram matrix
{title2=$B_n(x_0)$}
{parent=Local polynomial regression}

The local polynomial Gram matrix is the weighted matrix of inner products of the local monomial basis. Its invertibility makes the local fit unique.

= Effective kernel weight
{title2=$W_i(x_0)$}
{parent=Local polynomial regression}

Effective kernel weights are the coefficients $W_i(x_0)$ for which a local polynomial estimate can be written $\widehat m(x_0)=\sum_iW_i(x_0)Y_i$.

= Polynomial reproduction property of local polynomial regression
{parent=Local polynomial regression}

= Polynomial reproduction
{synonym}

Local polynomial weights of degree $p$ reproduce every <multivariate polynomial> of total degree at most $p$: applying the estimator to its design values returns its value at the target point.

= Local polynomial derivative estimator
{parent=Local polynomial regression}

If a degree-$p$ local polynomial is fitted in the scaled coordinate $(x_i-x)/h$, its coefficient of that coordinate divided by $h$ estimates the first derivative. Its effective weights differentiate every polynomial of degree at most $p$ exactly at the target.

= Nadaraya–Watson estimator
{c}
{parent=Local polynomial regression}

= Local constant estimator
{synonym}

This local-constant estimator is one particular <kernel regression> method.

The Nadaraya–Watson estimator is the degree-zero local polynomial estimate
$$
\widehat m(x)=
\frac{\sum_iK((x_i-x)/h)Y_i}
{\sum_iK((x_i-x)/h)}.
$$
At a support boundary it generally has first-order bias because it reproduces constants but not linear functions.

= Interior absolute-error rate of the Nadaraya-Watson estimator
{parent=Nadaraya–Watson estimator}
{title2=$\mathbb E|\widehat m_h(x)-m(x)|=O(h^2+(nh)^{-1/2})$}

For the unit-width symmetric box <kernel for density estimation>, suppose the design density is positive and differentiable at $x$, the regression function is twice differentiable there, and the conditional variance and design density are bounded near $x$. Write $G_n=(nh)^{-1}\sum_i(Y_i-m(x))\mathbf1_{\{|X_i-x|\le h/2\}}$. Symmetry cancels its linear Taylor term, giving $\mathbb EG_n=O(h^2)$; <independence> gives $\operatorname{Var}G_n=O((nh)^{-1})$. The <absolute error bound for a ratio estimator with a controlled denominator> gives the displayed rate if the small-denominator contribution is negligible. The balance $h\asymp n^{-1/5}$ gives $O(n^{-2/5})$. Interior design positivity matters: support boundaries can prevent the linear cancellation.

= Boundary bias of local constant regression
{parent=Nadaraya–Watson estimator}
{title2=$\operatorname{Bias}(\widehat m(0))\asymp h$}

A local constant average reproduces constants but need not reproduce a linear <regression function> near an endpoint. For $x_i=i/n$, $K=\mathbf1_{[-1,1]}$, $m(x)=x$, and $nh\to\infty$, its deterministic estimate at zero is $(\lfloor nh\rfloor+1)/(2n)\sim h/2$. A local linear fit removes this first-order boundary <bias of an estimator>.

= Second-order boundary expansion for local constant regression
{parent=Boundary bias of local constant regression}
{title2=$\operatorname{Bias}(\widehat m_h(\alpha h;0))=h\beta_1m'(\alpha h)+\tfrac12h^2\beta_2m''(\alpha h)+o(h^2)$}

For fixed $\alpha\in[0,1)$, design $x_i=i/n$, $m\in C^2[0,1]$, a nonzero nonnegative symmetric piecewise $C^1$ <regression kernel> supported on $[-1,1]$, and $h\to0$, $nh^2\to\infty$, write $\mu_{r,\alpha}=\int_{-\alpha}^1u^rK(u)\,du$ and $\beta_r=\mu_{r,\alpha}/\mu_{0,\alpha}$. On the rescaled grid of spacing $1/(nh)$, the <Riemann sum> error for $u^rK(u)$ is $O((nh)^{-1})$, by <bounded variation>. The normalized first and second local moments are therefore $h\beta_1+O(n^{-1})$ and $h^2\beta_2+O(h/n)$. A uniform second-order <Taylor expansion> gives the displayed <bias of an estimator>, since $n^{-1}=o(h^2)$. Equivalently it is $h\beta_1m'(0)+h^2(\alpha\beta_1+\beta_2/2)m''(0)+o(h^2)$. The first-order term explains the generic boundary bias of a <local constant estimator>.

= Mean absolute error of local constant regression
{parent=Nadaraya–Watson estimator}
{title2=$\mathbb E|\widehat m(x)-m(x)|\le2\sigma/\sqrt{n\min(h,1)}+Lh/2$}

For an equally spaced design with independent errors of variance at most $\sigma^2$ and a regression function with <Lipschitz constant> $L$, the <unit-width box kernel> estimator averages the $N_x$ observations in the window. Its bias is at most $Lh/2$, and its variance is at most $\sigma^2/N_x$. The <window occupancy for an equally spaced regression design> gives, whenever $nh\ge2$,
$$
\mathbb E|\widehat m(x)-m(x)|\le\frac{2\sigma}{\sqrt{n\min(h,1)}}+\frac{Lh}{2}.
$$
In the usual range $h\le1$, this is $O(\sigma/\sqrt{nh}+Lh)$ uniformly up to the boundary. For arbitrarily large bandwidth, the stochastic error cannot continue to decrease as $1/\sqrt{nh}$: once all observations are included the estimator is simply their mean.

= Interior first-order bias cancellation for local constant regression
{parent=Nadaraya–Watson estimator}
{title2=$\operatorname{Bias}=o(h)$}

At an interior point where the <regression function> is differentiable, a symmetric compactly supported <regression kernel> makes the first-order <bias of an estimator> vanish asymptotically. In regular <fixed-design nonparametric regression> with $h\to0$ and $nh\to\infty$, the <local constant estimator> then has squared <bias of an estimator> $o(h^2)$ and <variance> $R(K)/(nh)+o((nh)^{-1})$ for a unit-integral <regression kernel>. The <differentiability> remainder is pointwise and need not hold uniformly over a function class.

= Isotonic regression
{parent=Nonparametric regression}

Isotonic regression estimates a sequence or function subject to an order constraint such as $\theta_1\leq\cdots\leq\theta_n$.

= Minimax rate for isotonic sequence estimation

For bounded monotone sequences observed with independent regular location noise, the normalized squared-error minimax risk is of order $n^{-2/3}$.
