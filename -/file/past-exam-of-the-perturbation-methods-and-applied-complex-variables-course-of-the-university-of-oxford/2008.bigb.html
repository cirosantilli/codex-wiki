= Solution

For each fixed $n$, the next term must be negligible relative to its predecessor:
$$
\boxed{a_{n+1}(\epsilon)=o(a_n(\epsilon))
\quad(\epsilon\to0).}
$$
The ratios are understood where their denominators are nonzero near the limiting point, or equivalently through the <little o notation> definition. For example $1,\epsilon,\epsilon^2,\ldots$ form an <asymptotic sequence>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

For every fixed truncation index $N$, the remainder must be smaller than the last retained term:
$$
\boxed{f(\epsilon)-\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_n(\epsilon)
=o(a_N(\epsilon))\quad(\epsilon\to0).}
$$
This is an <asymptotic expansion> relative to the <sequence> in part (i). The number of retained terms is fixed before taking the limit. The associated <series> need not converge for any nonzero parameter; its role is to give successively smaller truncation errors. If $a_n$ is an <asymptotic sequence>, the same condition can be stated as a remainder $O(a_{N+1})$ when the next expansion condition is available, but the displayed <little o notation> formulation is the defining one.

Take $g(x)=x$ and $f(x)=x+1$. Then $f/g=1+1/x\to1$, so $f\sim g$. However,
$$
\frac{e^{f(x)}}{e^{g(x)}}=e
$$
for every $x$, and this ratio does not approach one. Thus **<asymptotic equivalence> need not survive exponentiation**. A small relative error between large exponents can leave a nonvanishing additive error. This is the distinction in <asymptotic comparison of exponential functions>.

The ratio can be evaluated directly:
$$
\frac{e^{f(x)}}{e^{g(x)}}=\exp(f(x)-g(x)).
$$
The assumption $|f-g|\to0$ gives $f-g\to0$, so <continuity> of the <exponential function> gives
$$
\boxed{e^{f(x)}\sim e^{g(x)}.}
$$
The additional premise $f\sim g$ is not needed for this implication. The useful requirement is additive control of the exponents, as explained by <asymptotic comparison of exponential functions>.

First take the usual positive-parameter limit $\epsilon\downarrow0$. For real $x$, the equation implies $|x^2-1|\leq\epsilon$, so every root lies near $1$ or $-1$. Near the positive root,
$$
\tanh(x/\epsilon)=1+O(e^{-2x/\epsilon}),
$$
and hence $x^2=1-\epsilon$ up to an exponentially small correction. Near the negative root, the corresponding limit is minus one, giving $x^2=1+\epsilon$ to all algebraic orders. Expanding the square roots gives
$$
\boxed{x_+=1-\frac{\epsilon}{2}+O(\epsilon^2),
\qquad x_-=-1-\frac{\epsilon}{2}+O(\epsilon^2).}
$$
There is one root in each neighborhood: the <derivative> $2x+\operatorname{sech}^2(x/\epsilon)$ has the sign of $x$ there for sufficiently small positive $\epsilon$. The bound above excludes further real roots.

If the source's unqualified $\epsilon\to0$ is read as a two-sided limit, use the exact identity
$$
\epsilon\tanh(x/\epsilon)
=|\epsilon|\tanh(x/|\epsilon|).
$$
The two-sided versions therefore replace $\epsilon$ by $|\epsilon|$ in both displayed first corrections. The two one-sided algebraic expansions otherwise have the same reasoning.

The <function> $e^{-x^2}/x$ is strictly decreasing from infinity to zero on $x>0$, so there is a unique positive root, which tends to infinity. Let $L=\log(1/\epsilon)$. Taking <logarithms> gives
$$
x^2+\log x=L.
$$
First $x^2\sim L$. Put $x=\sqrt L+d$ with $d=o(\sqrt L)$, expand, and cancel $L$:
$$
2\sqrt L\,d+d^2+\tfrac12\log L
+\log(1+d/\sqrt L)=0.
$$
The leading balance gives $d=-(\log L)/(4\sqrt L)$. Substitution leaves a residual $O((\log L)^2/L)$; the <derivative> of $x^2+\log x$ is of order $\sqrt L$, so the root error is $O((\log L)^2/L^{3/2})$. Thus
$$
\boxed{x=\sqrt L-\frac{\log L}{4\sqrt L}
+O\!\left(\frac{(\log L)^2}{L^{3/2}}\right).}
$$
The second term is logarithmic rather than a constant multiple of $L^{-1/2}$. This is <logarithmic inversion of a Gaussian divided by its argument>.

Write $y=e^{S/\delta}$ and divide the equation by $y$:
$$
\frac{\epsilon}{\delta^2}(S')^2+
\frac{\epsilon}{\delta}S''+
\frac1\delta S'+x=0.
$$
Balancing the first and third terms gives $\delta=\epsilon$. With $S=S_0+\epsilon S_1+\cdots$, the terms of order $\epsilon^{-1}$ and one give
$$
S_0'(S_0'+1)=0,\qquad
(2S_0'+1)S_1'+S_0''+x=0.
$$
For the slow branch $S_0'=0$, choose $S_0=0$ and obtain $S_1=-x^2/2$. For the fast branch $S_0'=-1$, choose $S_0=-x$ and obtain $S_1=x^2/2$. Additive integration constants enter the two independent amplitudes. The two-term <WKB approximation> is consequently
$$
y(x)\approx A e^{-x^2/2}+B e^{-x/\epsilon+x^2/2}.
$$
Imposing the <boundary conditions> on these two approximating modes yields
$$
\boxed{
y(x)\approx
\frac{e^{-x^2/2}-e^{-x/\epsilon+x^2/2}}
{e^{-1/2}-e^{-1/\epsilon+1/2}}.}
$$
This uses the first two exponent <functions> $S_0,S_1$ for both branches. The next transport equations, if desired to identify the algebraic error, give $S_2=x-x^3/3$ for the slow mode and $S_2=x+x^3/3$ for the fast mode. Thus each displayed mode has relative amplitude correction $1+O(\epsilon)$ uniformly on the fixed interval; no <turning point> occurs because the two leading exponent roots remain distinct.

Dropping the small highest-derivative term gives $y_o'+xy_o=0$. The <outer solution> satisfying the right endpoint condition is
$$
y_o(x)=e^{(1-x^2)/2}.
$$
It fails the left endpoint condition, so a <boundary layer> is needed at $x=0$. If $X=x/\epsilon^\gamma$, diffusion and first <derivative> balance when $\epsilon^{1-2\gamma}=\epsilon^{-\gamma}$, giving $\gamma=1$.

With $y(x)=Y(X)$, the exact <inner expansion> after multiplication by $\epsilon$ is
$$
Y_{XX}+Y_X+\epsilon^2XY=0.
$$
Its leading solution is $Y=A+B e^{-X}$. The zero boundary value gives $B=-A$, and <asymptotic matching condition> as $X\to\infty$, $x\to0$ fixes $A=e^{1/2}$. Thus
$$
Y(X)=e^{1/2}(1-e^{-X}).
$$
The common <overlap region> value is $e^{1/2}$. Add the outer and inner approximations and subtract that <overlap region>:
$$
\boxed{y_c(x)=e^{(1-x^2)/2}-e^{1/2-x/\epsilon}.}
$$
The decaying inner <exponential function> can accommodate the left condition, whereas a right-end inner coordinate would give a mode that grows into the interval. There is no right <boundary layer>. The <composite asymptotic expansion> satisfies the left condition exactly and the right one up to the exponentially small term $e^{1/2-1/\epsilon}$; the remaining approximation error is algebraic and vanishes as $\epsilon\downarrow0$.

Let $b=\pi/4$ and introduce
$$
J(x)=\int_0^b e^{ixt^2}\tan^2t\,dt,\qquad I(x)=\operatorname{Re}J(x).
$$
Deform the <contour> upward to the <steepest descent contour> $t=e^{i\pi/4}r$ from zero and the endpoint path $t=(b^2+is)^{1/2}$ from $b$. More precisely the original integral is the first path traversed forward minus the endpoint path traversed forward. Connect the truncated paths where $\operatorname{Im}(t^2)=R$ and let $R\to\infty$. That connector has exponentially small factor $e^{-xR}$, and no <pole> of $\tan t$ is crossed: all its <poles> are real and outside the original segment.

On the first path $\tan^2t=t^2+O(t^4)$, so its leading contribution is
$$
J_0(x)=e^{3i\pi/4}\int_0^\infty r^2e^{-xr^2}\,dr
+O(x^{-5/2})
=e^{3i\pi/4}\frac{\sqrt\pi}{4x^{3/2}}
+O(x^{-5/2}).
$$
The last equality follows by $u=xr^2$, $\Gamma(3/2)=\sqrt\pi/2$.

On the endpoint path $dt=i\,ds/(2\sqrt{b^2+is})$, and $\tan^2b=1$. Its negative orientation gives the leading endpoint term
$$
J_b(x)=-i e^{ixb^2}\int_0^\infty
e^{-xs}\frac{\tan^2\sqrt{b^2+is}}{2\sqrt{b^2+is}}\,ds
=-\frac{i e^{ixb^2}}{2bx}+O(x^{-2}).
$$
Taking real parts and inserting $b=\pi/4$ gives
$$
\boxed{I(x)=\frac2{\pi x}\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi^2x}{16}\right)
-\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{8x^{3/2}}+O(x^{-2}).}
$$
The oscillatory first coefficient can vanish at particular values of $x$; the stated remainder still holds as an additive <asymptotic expansion>.

The only <nondegenerate stationary point of a phase> of the phase $t^2$ is the endpoint zero. Its usual leading <stationary phase method> term is proportional to the amplitude there, but $\tan^2(0)=0$. Thus the nominal $x^{-1/2}$ stationary contribution vanishes.

Expand one order further in the local amplitude: $\tan^2t=t^2+O(t^4)$. The rescaling $t=u/\sqrt x$ then gives a first nonzero stationary contribution of order $x^{-3/2}$, with coefficient $-\sqrt{2\pi}/8$ in the real integral, as in part (i). Meanwhile the upper endpoint is nonstationary and has nonzero amplitude; its <integration by parts> contribution is $2\sin(\pi^2x/16)/(\pi x)$, of order $x^{-1}$.

Consequently **the nonstationary endpoint dominates the first nonzero saddle contribution**. A <stationary phase method> formula retaining only the usual leading saddle term would miss the answer. The full <stationary phase method>, with the vanishing-amplitude correction and the endpoint term, agrees with <method of steepest descent>. This is <quadratic stationary endpoint with quadratically vanishing amplitude>.

Treat $t,T$ as independent variables, with $T=\epsilon^2t$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\frac d{dt}=\partial_t+\epsilon^2\partial_T,\qquad
\frac{d^2}{dt^2}=\partial_t^2+
2\epsilon^2\partial_t\partial_T+\epsilon^4\partial_T^2.

Therefore the <method of multiple scales> is
$$
\boxed{y_{tt}+y+\epsilon\cos t\,y+
\epsilon^2(2y_{tT}+a_2y)+\epsilon^4y_{TT}
+O(\epsilon^4)y=0.}
$$
The unspecified $O(\epsilon^4)$ term includes the higher correction in $a$. Only the displayed terms through order $\epsilon^2$ are needed to determine the leading slow-amplitude system.

At order one, $(\partial_t^2+1)y_0=0$, so
$$
y_0=A(T)\cos t+B(T)\sin t.

At order $\epsilon$,
$$
(\partial_t^2+1)y_1
=-\cos t\,y_0
=-\frac A2-\frac A2\cos2t-\frac B2\sin2t.

The operator sends a constant to itself and sends $\cos2t,\sin2t$ to minus three times themselves. Hence
$$
\boxed{y_1=-\frac A2+\frac A6\cos2t+\frac B6\sin2t.}

An additional homogeneous $\cos t,\sin t$ contribution can be absorbed into the definitions of $A,B$. There is no <resonance> forcing at this order; the first amplitude evolution therefore occurs on the chosen slower scale $T=\epsilon^2t$.

The order-$\epsilon^2$ equation is
$$
(\partial_t^2+1)y_2=-2y_{0,tT}-a_2y_0-\cos t\,y_1.
$$
Using $\cos t\cos2t=(\cos3t+\cos t)/2$ and $\cos t\sin2t=(\sin3t+\sin t)/2$, its <resonance> coefficients are
$$
\cos t:\ -2B_T+(5/12-a_2)A,\qquad
\sin t:\ 2A_T-(a_2+1/12)B.
$$
<secular term> are removed by setting these to zero:
$$
A_T=\tfrac12(a_2+1/12)B,\qquad
B_T=\tfrac12(5/12-a_2)A.
$$
The <eigenvalues> of this two-by-two system satisfy
$$
\lambda^2=\frac14(a_2+1/12)(5/12-a_2).
$$
They are real with opposite signs for $-1/12<a_2<5/12$. Generic initial amplitudes then contain an exponentially growing slow-time component. At either leading endpoint the <matrix> is nonzero <nilpotent matrix>, so suitable initial amplitudes give linear slow-time growth; higher-order detuning decides the precise corrected boundary. Outside this interval the leading <eigenvalues> are purely imaginary and the slow amplitudes remain oscillatory.

Substitution into $a=1+a_2\epsilon^2+\cdots$ gives the requested leading instability interval
$$
\boxed{1-\frac{\epsilon^2}{12}+O(\epsilon^3)
\leq a\leq1+\frac{5\epsilon^2}{12}+O(\epsilon^3).}
$$
“Can become unbounded” permits special initial amplitudes without growth. The calculation identifies the <second instability tongue of a weak Mathieu oscillator> and its <slow amplitudes for the second Mathieu instability tongue>; it is not an assertion that every solution grows throughout the closed interval.

Near a <prevertex of a Schwarz-Christoffel map> $X_j$, a <conformal map> to an interior angle $(1-\beta_j)\pi$ has local form
$$
f(Z)-f(X_j)\sim c_j(Z-X_j)^{1-\beta_j}.
$$
Its <derivative> therefore has the factor $(Z-X_j)^{-\beta_j}$. On each real interval between <prevertex>, the argument of the product of these factors is constant, so its integral traces a straight polygon side. Passing a <prevertex of a Schwarz-Christoffel map> changes the argument by the prescribed exterior angle $\beta_j\pi$. Thus
$$
\boxed{f'(Z)=C\prod_{j=1}^n(Z-X_j)^{-\beta_j}}
$$
has exactly the required straight sides and corner angles, with the branches chosen in the <upper half-plane>. Equivalently $f''/f'$ has simple <poles> $-\beta_j/(Z-X_j)$; removing them leaves the constant normalization determined by behavior at infinity. The additive integration constant translates the polygon and $C$ rotates and scales it. This is the <Schwarz-Christoffel formula>.

The automorphisms of the <upper half-plane> are real <Möbius transformations> with positive determinant, modulo a common nonzero scalar. They have three real degrees of freedom, so **three <prevertex of a Schwarz-Christoffel map> positions may be assigned independently**. The remaining positions are constrained by the side-length ratios.

If $X_n=\infty$, omit its finite factor:
$$
\boxed{f'(Z)=C\prod_{j<n}(Z-X_j)^{-\beta_j}.}
$$
Since $\sum_j\beta_j=2$, the <derivative> behaves like $Z^{\beta_n-2}$ at infinity; in the coordinate $U=1/Z$, this is exactly the required corner exponent there. Thus the missing angle is encoded by behavior at infinity.

The finite corners have interior angle $3\pi/2$, hence exterior-angle parameters $-1/2$. The channel end at $Z=0$ is logarithmic. Their <Schwarz-Christoffel mapping> is consequently
$$
f'(Z)=C\frac{\sqrt{Z^2-1}}{Z}.
$$
Choose $R(Z)=\sqrt{Z^2-1}$ analytic in the <upper half-plane> with $R(Z)\sim Z$ at upper infinity. In particular $R(iy)=i\sqrt{1+y^2}$ for $y>0$. A direct differentiation gives
$$
\frac d{dZ}\left(R+\tan^{-1}\frac1R\right)
=\frac ZR-\frac1{ZR}=\frac RZ.
$$
Hence $f=A+C H$ with $H=R+\tan^{-1}(1/R)$.

The <inverse trigonometric functions> here is the branch obtained by <analytic continuation> from $Z>1$ through the <upper half-plane>, not an independent <Cauchy principal value> evaluation at each point. Its endpoint values give $H(1)=\pi/2$, $H(-1)=-\pi/2$. Imposing $f(1)=1$ and $f(-1)=-1$ therefore gives $A=0$, $C=2/\pi$:
$$
\boxed{z=\frac2\pi\left(\sqrt{Z^2-1}
+\tan^{-1}(Z^2-1)^{-1/2}\right).}
$$
Along the positive imaginary axis, $H$ is purely imaginary and tends to $-i\infty$ as $Z\to0$; at upper infinity $H\sim Z$. The real intervals $(0,1)$ and $(-1,0)$ trace the channel walls $x=1$ and $x=-1$ downward, while the outer intervals trace the horizontal boundary. This verifies the stated end assignments as well as the constants. The map is the <Schwarz-Christoffel map of a half-plane joined to a strip>.

A uniform horizontal <velocity potential> in the upper $Z$-plane is a real constant times $Z$. Since $dz/dZ=(2/\pi)R/Z$, choose
$$
\boxed{w(z)=\frac2\pi Z(z)}
$$
up to an additive constant. Its <complex velocity> is
$$
\frac{dw}{dz}=\frac ZR.
$$
As $Z\to\infty$ this tends to one, giving physical <velocity> $(1,0)$. As $Z\to0$, $R\to i$ and $Z/R\to0$, giving zero <velocity> far down the channel. The <imaginary part> of $w$ is constant on the real $Z$-axis, so the physical boundary is a <streamline> and the impermeability condition holds. The usual <complex velocity> convention is $dw/dz=u-iv$.

Orient $\Gamma$ and label its left side plus and its right side minus. Assume the density is <Hölder continuity> near the point in question, or has sufficient regularity for the boundary limits below. The source does not explicitly state this regularity assumption. Parametrize locally by oriented arclength $\gamma(s)$, with $\gamma(0)=t$. Define the <Cauchy principal value> by removing equal arclengths on the two sides:
$$
\operatorname{PV}\int_\Gamma\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-t}\,d\zeta
:=\lim_{\eta\downarrow0}
\int_{\Gamma\setminus\gamma((-\eta,\eta))}
\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-t}\,d\zeta.
$$
Subtract $f(t)$ from the numerator near $t$. The <Hölder continuity> remainder is locally bounded in absolute value by a constant times $|s|^{\nu-1}$ for some $\nu>0$, so its integral has the same limit from the two sides. The constant part contributes half the <residue>, with sign determined by the side of the small <semicircle>. This proves the <Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem>
$$
\boxed{w_\pm(t)=\pm\frac{f(t)}2+
\frac1{2\pi i}\operatorname{PV}\int_\Gamma
\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-t}\,d\zeta.}
$$
In particular $f=w_+-w_-$ and the <Cauchy principal value> term with coefficient $1/(\pi i)$ equals $w_++w_-$. Substituting these two identities into the <singular integral equation> gives
$$
\boxed{(a+b)w_++(b-a)w_-=c.}
$$

Choose a canonical homogeneous solution $\widetilde w$ whose boundary values do not vanish, and account for any permitted zeros or <poles> when forming a quotient. The assumptions $a\pm b\neq0$ alone do not ensure that an arbitrary homogeneous solution has this property. Its jump relation is
$$
(a+b)\widetilde w_+=(a-b)\widetilde w_-.
$$
Dividing the inhomogeneous relation by $(a+b)\widetilde w_+$ therefore gives
$$
\left(\frac w{\widetilde w}\right)_+
-\left(\frac w{\widetilde w}\right)_-
=\rho,\qquad
\rho=\frac{c}{(a+b)\widetilde w_+}.
$$
The <Cauchy integral> solves this additive jump. More generally every solution in the chosen analytic class has
$$
w(z)=\widetilde w(z)
\left[\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\Gamma
\frac{\rho(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}\,d\zeta+H(z)\right],
$$
where $H$ has no jump across $\Gamma$ and obeys the applicable growth, endpoint and divisor conditions. Setting $H=0$ gives exactly the intermediate formula printed in the source.

Let $C\rho=(2\pi i)^{-1}\operatorname{PV}\int_\Gamma\rho(\zeta)/(\zeta-t)d\zeta$. Applying <Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem> to the displayed solution and subtracting boundary values yields
$$
f=(\widetilde w_+-\widetilde w_-)C\rho
+\tfrac12(\widetilde w_++\widetilde w_-)\rho
+(\widetilde w_+-\widetilde w_-)H.
$$
Use
$$
\widetilde w_+-\widetilde w_-
=-\frac{2b\widetilde w_+}{a-b},
\qquad
\widetilde w_++\widetilde w_-
=\frac{2a\widetilde w_+}{a-b}.
$$
The normalized particular solution is consequently
$$
f_{\rm p}(t)=
-\frac{b(t)\widetilde w_+(t)}{a(t)-b(t)}
\frac1{\pi i}\operatorname{PV}\int_\Gamma
\frac{c(\zeta)}{(a(\zeta)+b(\zeta))\widetilde w_+(\zeta)}
\frac{d\zeta}{\zeta-t}
+\frac{a(t)c(t)}{a(t)^2-b(t)^2},
$$
and the full solution is
$$
\boxed{f=f_{\rm p}-\frac{2b\widetilde w_+}{a-b}H.}
$$
Thus the printed expression is a **particular solution; homogeneous terms need separate normalization**. To see why “general” cannot hold without qualification, take the positively oriented <unit circle>, $a=(1+z)/2$, $b=(1-z)/2$, and $c=0$. Then $a+b=1$ and $a-b=z$ are nonzero on the <contour>. The analytic pair $\widetilde w_+=1$, $\widetilde w_-=1/z$ satisfies the homogeneous jump and decays on the exterior side. Its nonzero density $f=1-1/z$ has these very Cauchy boundary values and solves the original homogeneous equation, whereas the source formula with $c=0$ gives zero. This is <homogeneous freedom in a scalar singular integral equation>; endpoint and index conditions determine which jump-free additions are permitted.

First state the normalization needed for the displayed logarithmic factorization: choose a continuous <holomorphic function> branch $L=\log F$ in the strip that tends to zero at both horizontal ends and has sufficient decay for its <Cauchy integrals>. In particular the index of the kernel along a horizontal line must be zero.

Choose $\alpha<c_+<c_-<\beta$ and let $\Gamma_\pm=\{\xi+ic_\pm:\xi\in\mathbb R\}$, both oriented from left to right. Put
$$
C_\pm L(z)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma_\pm}
\frac{L(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}\,d\zeta.
$$
The first is analytic above the lower line and the second below the upper line. On the region between the lines, closing a rectangle gives $C_+L-C_-L=L$: the lower horizontal side is traversed forward and the upper side backward. Hence
$$
F_\pm=e^{C_\pm L},\qquad \frac{F_+}{F_-}=F.
$$
The equation $Fw_+=w_-$ becomes $F_+w_+=F_-w_-$ on the <overlap region>. With the canonical bounded factors normalized at infinity, these two products glue to a bounded <entire function>. Part (a) gives
$$
\boxed{F_+w_+=F_-w_-=C.}
$$
This is <Wiener-Hopf factorization> with the quotient convention used in this question.

The source hypotheses require a qualification: nonvanishing in the strip and $F\to1$ do not by themselves force index zero or a <logarithm> tending to zero at both ends. For example
$$
F(z)=\frac{z-i}{z+i}
$$
is analytic and nonzero for $|\operatorname{Im}z|<1/2$, tends to one at both ends, but winds once around zero along the real line. A <logarithm> continued along that line changes by $2\pi i$, so it cannot have the stated normalization at both ends. One must first separate the index factor, or allow appropriately subtracted factors and a correspondingly different common-function growth class. The displayed constant conclusion follows under the canonical zero-index assumptions above; it is not an unconditional consequence of the two printed properties of $F$. This is the issue measured by the <index of a Wiener-Hopf kernel>.

Use the <Fourier transform> convention $\overline U(k,y)=\int_{\mathbb R}u(x,y)e^{ikx}dx$, initially in a class where the <integral transforms> and subsequent limits are defined. Transforming <Laplace's equation> gives $\overline U_{yy}-k^2\overline U=0$ on real $k$. Decay for $y\to\infty$ selects
$$
\overline U(k,y)=\overline U(k,0)e^{-|k|y}.
$$
The boundary trace is the sum of its negative-half-line unknown and the given positive-half-line datum. The normal <derivative> is zero on the negative side. Therefore
$$
\boxed{-|k|(\bar f+H_+)=\bar g,\qquad
H_+(k)=\Gamma(3/4)e^{3i\pi/8}k^{-3/4}.}
$$
For the $e^{ikx}$ convention, negative support gives a <integral transform> analytic for $\operatorname{Im}k<0$, and positive support gives one analytic for $\operatorname{Im}k>0$, subject to the appropriate endpoint interpretation. The normal trace in this particular problem requires a finite-part interpretation, detailed below.

For the <Wiener-Hopf method>, regularize the real-axis kernel by
$$
|k|_\epsilon=a_+(k)a_-(k),\qquad
a_+(k)=(k+i\epsilon)^{1/2},\quad
a_-(k)=(k-i\epsilon)^{1/2}.
$$
Take the <branch cut> of $a_+$ vertically downward from $-i\epsilon$ and that of $a_-$ vertically upward from $i\epsilon$. Their product on the <real axis> is the positive square root $\sqrt{k^2+\epsilon^2}$. The branch of $H_+$ has its cut downward from zero. Dividing the regularized equation by $a_+$ gives
$$
a_-\bar f+\frac{\bar g}{a_+}=-P,\qquad
P=a_-H_+.
$$
The forcing $P$ is analytic in $0<\operatorname{Im}k<\epsilon$, between its two cuts. Decompose $P=P_+-P_-$ by <Cauchy integrals> on two horizontal lines within that strip. Separation gives
$$
a_-\bar f-P_-=-\frac{\bar g}{a_+}-P_+=A,
$$
when the endpoint growth class makes the common entire remainder bounded. For $\operatorname{Im}k<0$ one can take the minus <Cauchy integral> on $\operatorname{Im}\widehat k=\delta$, with $0<\delta<\epsilon$, obtaining
$$
\bar f_\epsilon(k)=\frac{A_\epsilon}{(k-i\epsilon)^{1/2}}
+\frac{\Gamma(3/4)e^{3i\pi/8}}{2\pi i(k-i\epsilon)^{1/2}}
\int_{-\infty+i\delta}^{\infty+i\delta}
\frac{(\widehat k-i\epsilon)^{1/2}\widehat k^{-3/4}}
{\widehat k-k}\,d\widehat k.
$$
The integrand is $O(|\widehat k|^{-5/4})$ at infinity, so the <Cauchy integral> converges there. In the limit choose $\delta=\delta(\epsilon)$, for example $\delta=\epsilon/2$; a fixed positive $\delta$ cannot remain between zero and $\epsilon$. With $\sqrt k$ denoting the limiting lower-analytic branch and with the prescribed limiting interpretation,
$$
\boxed{\bar f(k)=\frac A{\sqrt k}
+\frac{\Gamma(3/4)e^{3i\pi/8}}{2\pi i\sqrt k}
\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow0}
\int_{-\infty+i\delta(\epsilon)}^{\infty+i\delta(\epsilon)}
\frac{(\widehat k-i\epsilon)^{1/2}\widehat k^{-3/4}}
{\widehat k-k}\,d\widehat k.}
$$
The numerator square root has a cut going upward from $i\epsilon$; the power $\widehat k^{-3/4}$ has a cut going downward from zero. The <contour> passes between them. The <pole> $\widehat k=k$ is below this <contour> when $k$ is in the lower <integral transform> domain. These branch choices are essential, rather than independent principal powers of the factors.

There is an important mathematical limitation in the source's ordinary integral definition of $\bar g$. An explicit <harmonic function> particular solution in <polar coordinates> $0<\theta<\pi$ is
$$
u_{\rm p}(r,\theta)=r^{-1/4}
\bigl(\cos(\theta/4)+\sin(\theta/4)\bigr).
$$
Each separated polar power is <harmonic function>; at $\theta=0$ it has the required <Dirichlet boundary condition> value, and at $\theta=\pi$ its angular <derivative> vanishes, giving the required zero normal <derivative>. It also decays at infinity. Its unknown boundary traces are
$$
f_{\rm p}(x)=\sqrt2\,|x|^{-1/4}\quad(x<0),\qquad
g_{\rm p}(x)=\frac14x^{-5/4}\quad(x>0).
$$
The last <function> is not locally integrable at zero. Therefore the printed ordinary integral $\int_0^\infty g(x)e^{ikx}dx$ diverges, even for $\operatorname{Im}k>0$. The <integral transform> calculation is valid for this solution only after specifying a <Hadamard finite-part integral>, or equivalently a <distribution (mathematical analysis)> boundary trace.

For the canonical homogeneous extension of this power, the two transformed traces are
$$
\bar f_{\rm p}(k)=\sqrt2\,\Gamma(3/4)(ik)^{-3/4},\qquad
\bar g_{\rm p}(k)=-\Gamma(3/4)(-ik)^{1/4}.
$$
The first is analytic below and the second above. The second formula follows from $\Gamma(3/4)=(-1/4)\Gamma(-1/4)$ and the finite-part continuation of the <Laplace transform>. Substituting their real-axis boundary values directly verifies the boxed <integral transform> relation for both signs of $k$, so the qualification can be checked without relying only on formal factorization.

There is also homogeneous corner freedom:
$$
u_{\rm h}=C r^{-1/2}\sin(\theta/2)
$$
satisfies zero <Dirichlet boundary condition> data on the positive axis, zero <Neumann boundary condition> data on the negative axis and decay at infinity. Its negative trace has <integral transform> $C\Gamma(1/2)(ik)^{-1/2}$, corresponding to the term $A/\sqrt k$. Higher singular modes $r^{-(n+1/2)}\sin((n+1/2)\theta)$ would also satisfy the homogeneous <boundary conditions> for integers $n\geq1$, but are excluded if the negative trace is required to be locally integrable. Thus the one-constant formula belongs to a specified corner-growth class. The original <boundary conditions> alone, without that class, do not assert uniqueness. These facts form the <finite-part normal trace for mixed quarter-power boundary data>.
