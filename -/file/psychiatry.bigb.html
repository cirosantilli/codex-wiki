= Psychiatry

The medical specialty concerned with mental disorders and their assessment, mechanisms and treatment.

= Postpartum psychosis
{parent=Psychiatry}

A severe psychiatric presentation following childbirth that requires urgent specialist assessment; historical “puerperal insanity” encompassed more than one modern syndrome.

= Computational psychiatry
{parent=Psychiatry}

The use of <mathematical models> to investigate processes contributing to <mental disorder> and its <treatment>. Model fitting, <parameter recovery> and experimental intervention have different evidential roles; an estimated parameter is not automatically a diagnosis or a biological mechanism.

= National Center for PTSD
{parent=Psychiatry}
{c}

A United States public clinical-research and education center whose official diagnostic explainer distinguishes qualifying <exposure> from the remaining requirements for a <PTSD> diagnosis.

= Mental health
{parent=Psychiatry}

Psychological functioning and well-being considered across relevant domains and contexts. Absence of a particular diagnosis is informative but does not exhaust positive functioning, and success in one domain need not establish equal adaptation in another.

= Mental disorder
{parent=Psychiatry}

= Psychological disorder
{synonym}

A clinically significant disturbance in psychological functioning associated with relevant distress or impairment. Diagnostic categories specify patterns for assessment and research; sharing a diagnosis does not establish identical causes or an identical treatment need.

= Depressive disorder
{parent=Mental disorder}

= Neurodevelopmental disorder
{parent=Mental disorder}

A diagnostic grouping involving developmental differences in functioning. A label does not specify one neural lesion or a uniform cognitive profile.

= Autism
{parent=Neurodevelopmental disorder}

= Autism spectrum disorder
{synonym}

= Autism spectrum condition
{synonym}

A heterogeneous neurodevelopmental condition involving characteristic differences in <social communication> and restricted or repetitive patterns of behaviour or interests. Sensory differences and varying support needs matter; an <autism>[autistic] individual’s <empathy>, <language> or mental health cannot be inferred from the label alone.

= Social motivation theory of autism
{parent=Autism}

A developmental proposal linking differences in social orienting, reward or engagement to later social experience. Reduced observed participation cannot by itself distinguish reduced interest from sensory overload, <anxiety> or exclusion.

= Weak central coherence theory
{parent=Autism}

A proposal emphasizing differences in integrating information into wider context or in local/global processing style. It must make specific predictions rather than redescribe every difficulty as lack of context.

= Broken mirror hypothesis of autism
{parent=Autism}

A proposal that dysfunction of action observation–execution matching explains <autism>[autistic] social differences. Its broad form requires direct evidence connecting the mechanism to the relevant behaviour, not merely atypical activation or an <imitation> score.

= Hoarding disorder
{parent=Mental disorder}

A <clinical syndrome> characterized by persistent difficulty discarding possessions and accumulation that compromises use of living space, with relevant distress or impairment. Differential assessment asks whether the appearance is better accounted for by another condition or context; excessive acquisition and insight can vary.

= Post-traumatic stress disorder
{parent=Mental disorder}

= PTSD
{c}
{synonym}

A disorder defined by a qualifying traumatic <exposure> together with the required subsequent symptom, duration and impairment criteria. The <exposure> requirement is a diagnostic condition, not a claim that <exposure> alone is sufficient or that every psychological disorder has the same prerequisite.<PTSD and DSM-5 by National Center for PTSD>[DSM-5 Criteria for PTSD, Criterion A and full-syndrome requirement]{ref}

= Dual representation theory
{parent=Post-traumatic stress disorder}

The original 1996 account distinguishes verbally accessible <Memory in animals>[memories] from representations accessed automatically through situational cues. It aims to explain differences between deliberate narrative retrieval and sensory reliving. This functional distinction does not by itself prove two anatomically discrete <Memory in animals>[memory] stores.<A dual representation theory of posttraumatic stress disorder by Chris R. Brewin and others>[Psychological Review 103, 670–686; original verbally/situationally accessible memory distinction]{ref}

= Sense of current threat
{parent=Post-traumatic stress disorder}

The experience that danger is present now, even when a reminder refers to a past event. The cognitive model of <Post-traumatic stress disorder>[PTSD] links this to <Cognitive appraisal>[appraisals] and trauma-memory properties rather than simply the historical severity of the event.<A cognitive model of posttraumatic stress disorder by Anke Ehlers and David M. Clark>[Behaviour Research and Therapy 38, 319–345; current threat, trauma-memory properties and maintaining strategies]{ref}

= Reexperiencing
{parent=Post-traumatic stress disorder}

= Re-experiencing
{synonym}

Involuntary recurrence of aspects of a traumatic experience, potentially including sensory impressions and a sense of current occurrence. It should be distinguished from deliberate recollection, <rumination> and a future-oriented image.

= Trauma reminder
{parent=Reexperiencing}

A cue associated with a traumatic experience that can prompt <emotion> or recollection. Its relation may be perceptual, situational or semantic, and need not be consciously recognised at the moment of response.

= Flashback
{parent=Reexperiencing}

A form of reliving in which a past event or aspect of it is experienced with a sense of occurring in the present. A bodily <conditioned response> alone does not identify this phenomenology.

= Externalizing disorder
{parent=Mental disorder}

A grouping emphasizing outwardly directed behavioral difficulties. Diagnostic and dimensional outcomes should be specified rather than treated as interchangeable measures of a single disorder.

= Oppositional defiant disorder
{parent=Externalizing disorder}

= Conduct disorder
{parent=Externalizing disorder}

= Internalizing disorder
{parent=Mental disorder}

A grouping of disorders involving inwardly experienced distress, including several <anxiety> and depressive conditions. It is not one single diagnosis, and an intervention effect on an aggregate measure need not hold for every component.

= Antisocial personality disorder
{parent=Mental disorder}

= Substance use disorder
{parent=Mental disorder}

= Bipolar disorder
{parent=Mental disorder}

A grouping of disorders involving manic or hypomanic episodes and, commonly, depressive episodes. Shared <genetic liability> with another diagnosis does not make the diagnoses interchangeable or establish an identical clinical course.

= Manic episode
{parent=Bipolar disorder}

= Mania
{synonym}

A clinical syndrome involving marked mood and activity change. It must be distinguished from <delirium> and substance- or illness-related behavioural disturbance.

= Schizophrenia
{parent=Mental disorder}

A psychiatric disorder involving characteristic disturbances of perception, belief, thought and functioning. Genetic evidence often concerns diagnostic liability rather than a single symptom mechanism or the exact timing of onset.

= Obsessive-compulsive disorder
{parent=Mental disorder}

= OCD
{c}
{synonym}

A disorder involving <obsessions>, <compulsions> or both with clinically significant distress, time cost or impairment. <Compulsions> may be overt or mental. Unwanted thoughts alone are insufficient to establish the disorder; their appraisal and associated responses matter.

= Family accommodation
{parent=Obsessive-compulsive disorder}

Changes by relatives or carers that facilitate avoidance or rituals, such as supplying repeated reassurance or completing checks. Addressing accommodation collaboratively can support treatment without blaming the family for the origin of the disorder.

= Inflated responsibility
{parent=Obsessive-compulsive disorder}

An excessive appraisal of personal responsibility for causing or preventing harm. It can make ordinary uncertainty feel unacceptable and motivate repeated checking or neutralization.<Obsessional-compulsive problems: A cognitive-behavioural analysis by Paul Salkovskis>[1985, responsibility appraisals in the proposed model]{ref}

= Compulsion
{parent=Obsessive-compulsive disorder}

A repetitive behavior or mental act performed according to rules or in response to an <obsession>, commonly to reduce distress or prevent a feared outcome. Covert neutralization can serve the same maintaining function as a visible ritual.

= Mental neutralization
{parent=Compulsion}

A covert mental act intended to cancel a threat, remove a thought's significance or prevent a feared outcome. <Mental neutralization> can preserve a <compulsion> cycle even when visible rituals have decreased.

= Obsession
{parent=Obsessive-compulsive disorder}

A recurrent intrusive thought, image or urge experienced as unwanted, often associated with distress and attempts to suppress or neutralize it. An intrusive thought's content is not by itself a measure of intention or a substitute for assessment.

= Anxiety disorder
{parent=Mental disorder}

A diagnostic grouping characterized by persistent or excessive fear or <anxiety> and associated impairment. Specific disorders differ in their feared outcomes and behavioral patterns, so a shared maintaining process is a hypothesis to assess rather than a universal explanation.

= Separation anxiety disorder
{parent=Anxiety disorder}

= Generalized anxiety disorder
{parent=Anxiety disorder}

= Specific phobia
{parent=Anxiety disorder}

Clinically significant <fear> and avoidance linked to a specified object or situation. Evidence from one phobia and age range does not establish the optimal <Exposure therapy>[exposure] format for every <anxiety disorder>.

= Social anxiety disorder
{parent=Anxiety disorder}

= Social phobia
{synonym}

Persistent fear of social scrutiny or negative evaluation with relevant distress or impairment. <Self-focused attention> and <safety behaviors> can maintain threatening self-impressions while reducing access to corrective information.

= Negative self-image
{parent=Social anxiety disorder}

An unfavorable mental representation of oneself that can be treated as if it were accurate evidence of how others perceive the person. Comparing an explicit prediction with external feedback can test that inference.

= Negative self-imagery
{parent=Social anxiety disorder}

An internally generated representation of how one appears, often experienced as an observer's view. Its vividness or emotional force does not establish that it matches an independent observer's impression.

= Anticipatory processing
{parent=Social anxiety disorder}

Thinking ahead about a feared encounter. It can become anxiety-maintaining when selective predictions and rehearsal preserve threat expectations instead of preparing flexible engagement.

= Post-event processing
{parent=Social anxiety disorder}

Repetitive review of a social encounter, potentially privileging threatening interpretations and internal impressions over external evidence. Its occurrence and its causal contribution should be assessed separately.

= Clark–Wells model of social anxiety disorder
{parent=Social anxiety disorder}
{c}

= Clark–Wells model
{synonym}
{c}

A cognitive account in which feared evaluation promotes <self-focused attention> and an internally generated threatening impression of the self. <Safety behaviors> can distort an interaction and prevent disconfirmation; anticipatory and <post-event processing> can maintain the threat. Developed by <David M. Clark> and <Adrian Wells>, the account generates separate experimental questions about <attention>, behaviour and interpretation. <Adolescent> applicability requires developmentally valid measures, rather than assuming an adult <self-report> vocabulary.<Understanding Social Anxiety Disorder in Adolescents and Improving Treatment Outcomes: Applying the Cognitive Model of Clark and Wells (1995) by Eleanor Leigh and David M. Clark>[Clinical Child and Family Psychology Review 21, 388–414; cognitive model and developmental qualifications]{ref}

= Adolescent conversation safety-behavior experiment
{parent=Clark–Wells model of social anxiety disorder}

Leigh and colleagues compared counterbalanced conversations under combined internal-focus/<safety-behavior> instructions versus external-focus/safety-reduction instructions. Self, partner and independent ratings in a symptom-selected sample provided experimental evidence for the combined manipulation. Because two processes changed together, this experiment does not by itself identify their separate <causal effects> or establish treatment efficacy in diagnosed patients.<Self-focused attention and safety behaviours maintain social anxiety in adolescents: An experimental study by Eleanor Leigh and others>[PLOS ONE 16, e0247703; counterbalanced conversations, 57 participants, partner and independent ratings]{ref}

= Panic disorder
{parent=Anxiety disorder}

A disorder involving recurrent unexpected panic attacks and persistent concern or behavioral change related to them. Cognitive accounts examine how interpretations of bodily sensations contribute to recurrent fear; the diagnosis does not establish that every bodily symptom is psychologically caused.

= Panic attack
{parent=Panic disorder}

An abrupt episode of intense <fear> or discomfort with associated symptoms. A <panic attack> is an event; <panic disorder> additionally involves a diagnostic pattern of recurrent unexpected attacks and persistent concern or behavioural change.

= Clark cognitive model of panic
{parent=Panic disorder}
{c}

<David M. Clark>'s account proposes that catastrophic interpretations of bodily sensations increase <fear> and <Physiological arousal>[arousal], intensifying sensations available for further threatening interpretation. <Safety behaviors> and avoidance can prevent disconfirmation. The theory distinguishes the presence of a sensation from the meaning assigned to it; a test should manipulate interpretation while measuring physiological input and subsequent <fear>.<A cognitive approach to panic by David M. Clark>[Behaviour Research and Therapy 24, 461–470; original model of catastrophic misinterpretation]{ref}

= Agoraphobia
{parent=Anxiety disorder}

Marked fear and avoidance of situations where escape or obtaining help is perceived as difficult. It can occur with or without <panic disorder>; a specific feared consequence and its maintaining responses should be assessed.

= Major depressive disorder
{parent=Psychiatry}

= MDD
{c}
{synonym}

A clinically assessed syndrome involving persistent depressed mood and/or <anhedonia>, with associated cognitive, motivational, sleep and bodily symptoms and functional impairment. Similar symptom profiles need not have one biological cause.

= Monoamine hypothesis of depression
{parent=Major depressive disorder}

A family of proposals relating depression to reduced or dysregulated <monoamine> signalling. A simple universal deficiency account is stronger than the claim that a monoaminergic manipulation can alter symptoms. Drug-target engagement, therapeutic mediation and original disease cause are separate causal claims.

= Acute tryptophan depletion
{parent=Monoamine hypothesis of depression}

An experimental manipulation intended to reduce the availability of the <serotonin> precursor <tryptophan>. Effects in a previously treated remitted sample test dependence of that state on precursor availability; they do not directly measure a universal baseline serotonin deficit.

= Anhedonia
{parent=Major depressive disorder}

Reduced interest or pleasure. Anticipation, experienced pleasure, learning from outcomes and willingness to exert effort are distinguishable components, so one reward task does not measure them all.
