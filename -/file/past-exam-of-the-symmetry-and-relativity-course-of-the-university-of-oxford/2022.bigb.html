== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Write the <Lorentz transformation> as $x'^\mu=\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}x^\nu$. Acting on a <scalar field>, the <chain rule> gives the <four-gradient>
$$
\partial'_\mu=\frac{\partial x^\nu}{\partial x'^\mu}\partial_\nu
=(\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_{\mu}\partial_\nu.
$$
For the specified <Lorentz boost>, the inverse is obtained by changing $\beta$ to $-\beta$, so
$$
\partial'_0=\gamma(\partial_0+\beta\partial_1),\qquad
\partial'_1=\gamma(\partial_1+\beta\partial_0),\qquad
\partial'_2=\partial_2,\quad\partial'_3=\partial_3.
$$
These are precisely the <covector> component rules for the lowered coordinates. Raising the index with the <Minkowski metric> $\eta^{\mu\nu}=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1,1)$ reverses the time component, yielding
$$
\boxed{\partial_\mu=(c^{-1}\partial_t,\partial_x,\partial_y,\partial_z),\qquad
\partial^\mu=(-c^{-1}\partial_t,\partial_x,\partial_y,\partial_z).}
$$
Indeed $\partial'^0=\gamma(\partial^0-\beta\partial^1)$ and $\partial'^1=\gamma(\partial^1-\beta\partial^0)$, the contravariant <four-vector> rules. Thus the index on a <partial derivative> with respect to an upper coordinate is lower.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <four-gradient> rules from <1/a> give
$$
\partial'_\mu\partial'^\mu
=(\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_{\mu}\partial_\nu
\Lambda^\mu{}_{\rho}\partial^\rho
=\delta^\nu{}_{\rho}\partial_\nu\partial^\rho
=\partial_\nu\partial^\nu.
$$
The <Lorentz transformation> coefficients are constant, so the <partial derivatives> do not differentiate them. This proves the <Lorentz invariance> of the <d'Alembert operator>:
$$
\boxed{\Box=\partial_\mu\partial^\mu=\nabla^2-c^{-2}\partial_t^2.}
$$
For example, the two longitudinal terms transform as $-\gamma^2(\partial_0+\beta\partial_1)^2+\gamma^2(\partial_1+\beta\partial_0)^2=-\partial_0^2+\partial_1^2$, using $\gamma^2(1-\beta^2)=1$ and commuting <partial derivatives>. The transverse terms are unchanged. Applied to a <scalar field>, $\Box$ therefore gives a <Lorentz scalar>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Take the <divergence> of the <Ampère-Maxwell equation> and use that the <divergence of a curl is zero>:
$$
0=\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)
=\mu_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)
=\mu_0(\nabla\cdot\mathbf J+\partial_t\rho).
$$
The last step uses <Gauss's law>. Thus the <charge continuity equation> is exactly
$$
\boxed{J^\mu=(c\rho,\mathbf J),\qquad \partial_\mu J^\mu=\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0.}
$$
For a direct justification that this <four-current> is a <four-vector>, take one locally comoving population of <electric charges>, with rest <charge density> $\rho_0$ and <four-velocity> $U^\mu=\gamma(c,\mathbf v)$. Invariance of <electric charge> and <length contraction> give $\rho=\gamma\rho_0$ and $\mathbf J=\rho\mathbf v$, hence $J^\mu=\rho_0U^\mu$. Summing populations preserves the <four-vector> rule. In particular, $c\rho'=\gamma(c\rho-\beta J_x)$ and $J'_x=\gamma(J_x-\beta c\rho)$. The <Lorentz invariance> of its <four-divergence> then explains why <charge conservation> holds in every <inertial frame>. A single zero-divergence condition alone would not determine a field's transformation law; the physical <charge density> and <current density> provide the additional content.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Lorenz gauge condition> is $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=-c^{-2}\partial_t\phi$. Substitute the <electric potential> and <magnetic vector potential> into <Gauss's law>:
$$
-\nabla^2\phi-\partial_t(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)=\rho/\epsilon_0,
\qquad
\Box\phi=-\rho/\epsilon_0.
$$
Next the <Ampère-Maxwell equation>, the <curl of the curl identity>, and the <Lorenz gauge condition> give
$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)-\nabla^2\mathbf A
&=\mu_0\mathbf J-c^{-2}\nabla\partial_t\phi-c^{-2}\partial_t^2\mathbf A,\\
\Box\mathbf A&=-\mu_0\mathbf J.
\end{aligned}
$$
The <gradient> terms cancel. Since $\mu_0\epsilon_0c^2=1$, the time component obeys $\Box(\phi/c)=-\mu_0c\rho$, so together
$$
\boxed{\partial^\nu\partial_\nu A^a=-\mu_0J^a,\qquad A^\mu=(\phi/c,\mathbf A).}
$$
The <four-potential> can be chosen to transform as a <four-vector>. To make the argument rigorous, choose the <retarded potential> with no freely added homogeneous solution. If $\Box G_{\mathrm{ret}}(x)=\delta^{(4)}(x)$, this choice is
$$
A^\mu(x)=-\mu_0\int G_{\mathrm{ret}}(x-y)J^\mu(y)\,d^4y.
$$
The <retarded Green function> is a <Lorentz scalar> under <proper orthochronous Lorentz transformations>, and $d^4y$ is invariant. The <four-vector> rule for $J^\mu$ therefore proves $A'^\mu(x')=\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}A^\nu(x)$. More generally, transform any homogeneous initial data by this same rule. Both the <wave equation> and the <Lorenz gauge condition> are then preserved.

**Qualification.** The <wave equation> by itself cannot prove the transformation rule for every independently chosen <gauge potential>: the <d'Alembert operator> has a nontrivial <kernel of a linear map>. For a concrete counterexample, zero sources and zero fields admit $A^\mu=0$ in one <inertial frame> and $A'^\mu=(0,C,0,0)$ in another. Both satisfy the <wave equation> and the <Lorenz gauge condition>, but the latter is not the <Lorentz transformation> of zero. It is a <residual Lorenz gauge symmetry>, generated by $\chi=Cx'$. Thus the usual assertion is a covariant choice of <four-potential>, with a consistent <gauge transformation> and homogeneous data, rather than a uniqueness result from the displayed equation.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $s=k^\mu x_\mu$. The <Lorenz gauge condition> on the <plane electromagnetic wave> gives
$$
k_\mu\epsilon^\mu=0.
$$
The vacuum <wave equation> gives $k_\mu k^\mu=0$ for a nonzero amplitude; equivalently $k^0=\omega/c$ and $|\mathbf k|=\omega/c$. Use the <gauge transformation> $A'^\mu=A^\mu+\partial^\mu\chi$ with
$$
\chi=-\lambda\cos s,\qquad \epsilon'^\mu=\epsilon^\mu+\lambda k^\mu.
$$
Because $\Box\chi=0$, this is a <residual Lorenz gauge symmetry> and $k_\mu\epsilon'^\mu=0$. For a propagating <plane electromagnetic wave>, $k^0\ne0$, so take $\lambda=-\epsilon^0/k^0$. Then
$$
\boxed{\epsilon'^0=0,\qquad \mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon'=0.}
$$
The second equality follows from $-k^0\epsilon'^0+\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon'=0$. The <electric field> and <magnetic field> are unchanged by this <gauge transformation>. Thus <Lorenz gauge> permits a representative in <radiation gauge>; <Lorenz gauge> alone does not require the spatial amplitude of every representative to be transverse.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use $c=1$ and define the total <four-momentum> $P^\mu=(E,\mathbf P)=\sum_i(E_i,\mathbf p_i)$. For an <inertial frame> moving with <velocity> $\mathbf V$ relative to the laboratory, the <Lorentz boost> gives
$$
P'_{\parallel}=\gamma_V(P_{\parallel}-VE),\qquad \mathbf P'_{\perp}=\mathbf P_{\perp}.
$$
Choose $\mathbf V$ parallel to $\mathbf P$ and require zero total <three-momentum>. Then
$$
\boxed{\mathbf V_{\mathrm{CM/lab}}=\frac{\mathbf P}{E}=\frac{\sum_i\mathbf p_i}{\sum_iE_i}.}
$$
This requires $E>|\mathbf P|$, so that the total <four-momentum> is timelike. Its <invariant mass> is $\sqrt{E^2-|\mathbf P|^2}$, the total <relativistic energy> in the <center-of-momentum frame>. If all particles are exactly parallel <photons>, the total <four-momentum> is null and no finite-<velocity> <center-of-momentum frame> exists.

**Sign qualification.** The minus sign printed in the source describes the laboratory's <velocity> in the <center-of-momentum frame>, or the parameter of an active <Lorentz boost> with a plus-sign spatial rule. It is not the <velocity> of the <center-of-momentum frame> relative to the laboratory under question 1's passive convention. For example, a single particle with $(E,P_x)=(5,3)$ has its rest frame moving at $V_x=3/5$. The printed $V_x=-3/5$ would give $P'_x=(5/4)(3+3)=15/2$, which is not zero. This explicitly resolves the sign rather than assigning a rest frame moving away from the particle.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

By <four-momentum conservation>, $p_\pi=p_1+p_2$. With the $(-,+,+,+)$ <Minkowski metric>, the <neutral pion> has $p_\pi^2=-M_\pi^2$, while each <photon> has $p_i^2=0$ and $|\mathbf p_i|=E_i$. Taking the <Minkowski norm> gives
$$
-M_\pi^2=2p_1\cdot p_2
=2(-E_1E_2+\mathbf p_1\cdot\mathbf p_2)
=-2E_1E_2(1-\cos A).
$$
The half-angle identity therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\sin^2(A/2)=\frac{M_\pi^2}{4E_1E_2}.}
$$
This is a measurement of the <diphoton invariant mass> using two <photon> <energies> and their opening angle, independent of the <neutral pion>'s laboratory <velocity>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

In the <center-of-momentum frame>, <four-momentum conservation> gives two opposite <photon> momenta, each of <energy> $M_\pi/2$. Let $z=\cos\theta_*$, with the axis along the <Lorentz boost> from that frame to the laboratory. Reversing the axis specified in the source changes $z$ to $-z$ and has no effect on the following product. The <Lorentz transformation> of <four-momentum> gives
$$
E_1=\frac{\gamma M_\pi}{2}(1+\beta z),\qquad
E_2=\frac{\gamma M_\pi}{2}(1-\beta z).
$$
Writing $s=\sin(A/2)$, <2/b> now implies
$$
s^2=\frac1{\gamma^2(1-\beta^2z^2)},\qquad
|z|=\frac{\sqrt{\gamma^2s^2-1}}{\beta\gamma s}.
$$
The <isotropy> assumption is $dN=a\,|dz|$ on $-1\le z\le1$. Each interior opening angle has **two** inverse branches, $z$ and $-z$, both of which must enter the <change of variables for probability densities>. The positive branch has <derivative>
$$
\left|\frac{dz}{dA}\right|
=\frac{\cos(A/2)}{2\beta\gamma\sin^2(A/2)\sqrt{\gamma^2\sin^2(A/2)-1}}.
$$
Consequently the <diphoton opening-angle distribution> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{dN}{dA}=\frac a\gamma\frac{\cos(A/2)}{\beta\sin^2(A/2)\sqrt{\gamma^2\sin^2(A/2)-1}},\qquad f(\gamma)=\gamma^{-1}.}
$$
Its <support> is $2\arcsin(1/\gamma)<A<\pi$ for $0<\beta<1$, and its integral is $2a$, the integral of the original angular <probability density> before normalization. Thus the normalized <probability density> is $(2a)^{-1}dN/dA$. Omitting one inverse branch would incorrectly halve the total count.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

From <2/c>, $\sin(A/2)\ge1/\gamma$, with equality when $z=0$: the <photons> are emitted perpendicular to the <Lorentz boost> axis in the <center-of-momentum frame>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{A_{\min}=2\arcsin(\gamma^{-1}),\qquad A_{\mathrm{peak}}=A_{\min}.}
$$
At the minimum, the map $z\mapsto A$ is stationary: writing $A=A_{\min}+\delta$, its leading behavior is $\delta=\beta z^2/\gamma+O(z^4)$. A nonzero interval of uniformly distributed $z$ is therefore compressed near $A_{\min}$, producing an integrable <inverse-square-root singularity> in the opening-angle <probability density>. No <differentiation> of that <probability density> is needed. For finite $\gamma>1$, the <probability density> vanishes at $A=\pi$. For $\gamma=1$ the <center-of-momentum frame> is the laboratory and every event has $A=\pi$: the <probability distribution> becomes a <Dirac delta distribution>, rather than the singular $\beta=0$ substitution in <2/c>. At large $\gamma$, $A_{\min}\sim2/\gamma$ in radians.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For a massive particle, the <proper time> is determined by the <Minkowski interval> along its <worldline>:
$$
-c^2d\tau^2=-c^2dt^2+d\mathbf x\cdot d\mathbf x
=-c^2dt^2(1-|\mathbf u|^2/c^2).
$$
Choose future-directed time increments. With the <Lorentz factor> $\gamma_u=(1-|\mathbf u|^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$,
$$
\boxed{d\tau=dt/\gamma_u,\qquad U^\alpha=\frac{dx^\alpha}{d\tau}=\gamma_u(c,\mathbf u).}
$$
We use $U^\alpha$ for the source's $u^\alpha$ to distinguish <four-velocity> from <velocity> $\mathbf u$. Since <proper time> is invariant and $dx^\alpha$ is a <four-vector>, $U^\alpha$ is a <four-vector>, with $U_\alpha U^\alpha=-c^2$. The rest-frame result $U^\alpha=(c,\mathbf0)$ follows immediately.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Lorentz boost> from A to B gives
$$
dt'=\gamma_v(dt-v\,dx/c^2),\quad dx'=\gamma_v(dx-v\,dt),\quad dy'=dy,\quad dz'=dz.
$$
Divide each spatial displacement by $dt'=\gamma_vdt(1-vu_x/c^2)$. The <velocity-addition formula> is
$$
\boxed{w_x=\frac{u_x-v}{1-vu_x/c^2},\qquad
w_y=\frac{u_y}{\gamma_v(1-vu_x/c^2)},\qquad
w_z=\frac{u_z}{\gamma_v(1-vu_x/c^2)}.}
$$
The same result follows by transforming the <four-velocity> and using $w_i=cU'^i/U'^0$. The transverse <velocities> change because the transformed time increment depends on $dx$, even though the transverse spatial coordinates are unchanged.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For $v=|\mathbf v|>0$, decompose the <velocity> as $\mathbf u=\mathbf u_\parallel+\mathbf u_\perp$, parallel and perpendicular to $\mathbf v$. A rotation-free <Lorentz boost> gives the <velocity-addition formula>
$$
\mathbf w_\parallel=\frac{\mathbf u_\parallel-\mathbf v}{D},\qquad
\mathbf w_\perp=\frac{\mathbf u_\perp}{\gamma_vD},\qquad
D=1-\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u/c^2.
$$
These two components are orthogonal, so
$$
\begin{aligned}
D^2|\mathbf w|^2
&=|\mathbf u_\parallel-\mathbf v|^2+(1-v^2/c^2)|\mathbf u_\perp|^2\\
&=|\mathbf u-\mathbf v|^2-\frac{v^2|\mathbf u_\perp|^2}{c^2}\\
&=|\mathbf u-\mathbf v|^2-\frac{|\mathbf v\times\mathbf u|^2}{c^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The last equality is <Lagrange's identity> for the <cross product>. For physical <subluminal speeds>, $D>0$, giving
$$
\boxed{|\mathbf w|=\frac{\sqrt{(\mathbf v-\mathbf u)\cdot(\mathbf v-\mathbf u)-|\mathbf v\times\mathbf u|^2/c^2}}{1-\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u/c^2}.}
$$
The $\mathbf v=0$ case follows directly or by continuity. An independent form useful for checks is $1-|\mathbf w|^2/c^2=(1-u^2/c^2)(1-v^2/c^2)/D^2$, obtained from the transformed <four-velocity> normalization.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $\mathbf v_1=(c/2,0,0)$ and $\mathbf v_2=(c/(2\sqrt2),c/(2\sqrt2),0)$ in A. Each has <Lorentz factor> $g=2/\sqrt3$. The relative <Lorentz factor> of $S_2$ in the rest frame of $S_1$ follows from their <four-velocity> <tensor contraction>:
$$
\Gamma=-\frac{U_1\cdot U_2}{c^2}
=g^2\left(1-\frac{\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_2}{c^2}\right)
=\frac{8-\sqrt2}{6}.
$$
Let $b=\sqrt{1-\Gamma^{-2}}$ be the relative <speed> divided by $c$. In the rest frame of $S_1$, take $\theta$ between the <photon>'s propagation direction and $S_2$'s <velocity>. The observed <angular frequency> is the invariant $\omega=-k\cdot U$. Applying it to the emitter, whose rest <angular frequency> is $\omega_0$, gives $\omega_0=\Gamma\omega_1(1-b\cos\theta)$. Since $\omega_1=\omega_0$,
$$
\boxed{\cos\theta=\frac{1-\Gamma^{-1}}{b}
=\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma-1}{\Gamma+1}}
=\sqrt{\frac{2-\sqrt2}{14-\sqrt2}},\qquad \theta\simeq77.54^\circ.}
$$
This is a <relativistic Doppler effect> calculation using both emitters' and receivers' rest clocks; the time delay of reception introduces no extra factor for constant <velocities>.

For the direction in A, write the <wave four-vector> as $k^\mu=(\omega_A/c)(1,\mathbf n)$, where $\mathbf n$ is a <unit vector>. Equality of the emitted and received <angular frequencies> gives $k\cdot(U_1-U_2)=0$. Since the two laboratory <Lorentz factors> are equal, their time components cancel:
$$
\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf v_1-\mathbf v_2)=0,
\qquad n_y=(\sqrt2-1)n_x.
$$
Thus the complete answer in three spatial dimensions is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf n=\cos\psi\,\big(\cos(\pi/8),\sin(\pi/8),0\big)+\sin\psi\,(0,0,1),\qquad 0\le\psi<2\pi.}
$$
If propagation is additionally assumed to lie in the stars' motion plane, this reduces to either direction along the internal <angle bisector>, $22.5^\circ$ or $202.5^\circ$ from $+x$. Reflection exchanging the stars' laboratory <velocities> explains the <angle bisector> by symmetry. **The source does not specify coplanar propagation or the stars' positions**, so it does not fix the sign or exclude an out-of-plane ray. For example $\mathbf n=(0,0,1)$ gives $\omega_1=\omega_2=g\omega_A$ and satisfies the same no-shift condition. Initial positions can be chosen to make such a ray connect emission and reception. All these possibilities give the same rest-frame $\theta$ above.

== 4
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For constant laboratory <force> $\mathbf F$ and initial rest, <integration> gives <three-momentum> $\mathbf p=\mathbf Ft$. The <energy–momentum relation> gives $E=\sqrt{m^2c^4+c^2|\mathbf F|^2t^2}$, and $\mathbf v=c^2\mathbf p/E$, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbf v(t)=\frac{\mathbf Ft/m}{\sqrt{1+|\mathbf F|^2t^2/(m^2c^2)}}.}
$$
For $\mathbf F\ne0$ and $t\ge0$, its <speed> increases monotonically, is strictly below the <speed of light> at every finite time, and obeys $\lim_{t\to\infty}|\mathbf v(t)|=c$. Thus $c$ is the limiting supremum, rather than a <speed> attained at a finite time. The degenerate case $\mathbf F=0$ leaves the particle at rest and does not have this limit.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

With constant <rest mass>, $\mathbf p=m\gamma\mathbf v$ and the <chain rule> gives $\dot\gamma=\gamma^3(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a)/c^2$. Hence the laboratory <relativistic force> is
$$
\mathbf F=m\gamma\mathbf a+\frac{m\gamma^3}{c^2}(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a)\mathbf v.
$$
Use the <vector triple product identity> $\mathbf v\times(\mathbf v\times\mathbf a)=\mathbf v(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a)-v^2\mathbf a$, and $\gamma^3(1-v^2/c^2)=\gamma$, to obtain
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F=m\gamma^3\left(\mathbf a+\frac{\mathbf v\times(\mathbf v\times\mathbf a)}{c^2}\right).}
$$
The <relativistic energy> is $E=m\gamma c^2$, whose <time derivative> is $m\gamma^3\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a$. Taking the <dot product> of the first expression for $\mathbf F$ with $\mathbf v$ gives the same quantity, because $\gamma+\gamma^3v^2/c^2=\gamma^3$. Thus $dE/dt=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v$. Since $d/d\tau=\gamma\,d/dt$, the <four-force> is
$$
\boxed{F^\alpha=\frac{dp^\alpha}{d\tau}=\left(\frac{\gamma\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v}{c},\gamma\mathbf F\right).}
$$
Its <tensor contraction> with the <four-velocity> vanishes: $F^\alpha U_\alpha=-\gamma^2\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v+\gamma^2\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v=0$. This <orthogonality> also follows by differentiating $p^\alpha p_\alpha=-m^2c^2$. Resolving the laboratory <acceleration> relative to $\mathbf v$ gives $\mathbf F_\parallel=m\gamma^3\mathbf a_\parallel$ and $\mathbf F_\perp=m\gamma\mathbf a_\perp$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

**Metric-sign qualification.** With this paper's $(-,+,+,+)$ <metric signature>, <4/b> gives
$$
F^\alpha F_\alpha=\gamma^2\left(|\mathbf F|^2-\frac{(\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v)^2}{c^2}\right)\ge0.
$$
The minus sign in the printed <Larmor formula> would therefore make a positive radiative loss negative. A concrete counterexample is an instant of rest with nonzero <acceleration>: $F^\alpha=(0,m\mathbf a)$, so the printed expression is $-q^2a^2/(6\pi\epsilon_0c^3)$. That sign is appropriate for the opposite <metric signature>. With the convention already fixed in question 1, positive emitted <power> instead is
$$
P_{\mathrm{rad}}=\frac{q^2}{6\pi\epsilon_0m^2c^3}F^\alpha F_\alpha.
$$
Alternatively the printed negative quantity can be interpreted as the particle's signed radiative <energy> change, rather than the positive loss magnitude.

For fixed <rest mass>, resolve the <acceleration> parallel and perpendicular to the <velocity>, as in <4/b>. This gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{F^\alpha F_\alpha}{m^2}
&=\gamma^2\left(\gamma^6a_\parallel^2+\gamma^2a_\perp^2-\gamma^6\frac{v^2}{c^2}a_\parallel^2\right)\\
&=\gamma^6a_\parallel^2+\gamma^4a_\perp^2.
\end{aligned}
$$
Writing $C_q=q^2/(6\pi\epsilon_0c^3)$, the <Liénard radiated-power formula> becomes $P_{\mathrm{rad}}=C_q(\gamma^6a_\parallel^2+\gamma^4a_\perp^2)$. For uniform <circular motion>, the <acceleration> is perpendicular to the <velocity>; for straight-line motion it is parallel. Comparing the same particle at equal <relativistic energy> and equal laboratory <acceleration> magnitude $a$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{P_{\mathrm{circular}}=C_q\gamma^4a^2,\qquad
P_{\mathrm{linear}}=C_q\gamma^6a^2,\qquad
\frac{P_{\mathrm{circular}}}{P_{\mathrm{linear}}}=\gamma^{-2}.}
$$
This particular comparison makes the linear loss larger. The familiar larger loss from bending applies to a different comparison: at equal applied laboratory <force> magnitude $F$, $a_\perp=F/(m\gamma)$ and $a_\parallel=F/(m\gamma^3)$, giving $P_{\mathrm{circular}}=C_q\gamma^2F^2/m^2$ and $P_{\mathrm{linear}}=C_qF^2/m^2$. Stating which quantity is held fixed is essential.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Both the <muon> and <electron> have the same <electric charge> magnitude. At equal kinetic <energy> $K$ per particle, their <Lorentz factors> are $\gamma=1+K/(mc^2)$, so the heavier <muon> has the smaller <Lorentz factor>. At equal total <relativistic energy> the same ordering follows from $\gamma=E/(mc^2)$, provided that <energy> is large enough to create either particle.

**If the equal-<acceleration> comparison of <4/c> is retained**, $P_{\mathrm{circular}}=C_qa^2\gamma^4\le C_qa^2\gamma^6=P_{\mathrm{linear}}$. Thus the four-way instantaneous comparison selects
$$
\boxed{\text{a muon in a circular accelerator, at fixed energy and coordinate acceleration}.}
$$
For positive $K$, its smaller <Lorentz factor> reduces the <power> relative to an <electron>, and transverse <acceleration> reduces the <power> relative to longitudinal <acceleration> of the same magnitude. This conclusion follows from the constraint actually stated in <4/c>.

**For the customary engineering comparison at equal applied <force>**, the result changes. From <4/c>,
$$
P_{\mathrm{linear}}=\frac{q^2F^2}{6\pi\epsilon_0m^2c^3},\qquad
P_{\mathrm{circular}}=\gamma^2P_{\mathrm{linear}}.
$$
Under that comparison the choice is
$$
\boxed{\text{a muon in a linear accelerator, at fixed energy and applied force}.}
$$
Its longitudinal radiative <power> is smaller than an <electron>'s by $(m_e/m_\mu)^2$, while avoiding bending removes the extra $\gamma^2$ factor. At a fixed bend radius $R$, the circular loss is $C_q\gamma^4v^4/R^2$, giving the familiar $m^{-4}$ suppression at fixed ultrarelativistic <energy> and $R$.

**An <energy> budget alone does not specify a unique optimization.** The <force>, <acceleration>, bend radius, acceleration time, and length are otherwise unrestricted. For example, in a <linear accelerator> a constant small <force> $F$ gives radiative <power> proportional to $F^2$, and reaching a specified final <three-momentum> takes time proportional to $F^{-1}$; the total radiated <energy> therefore tends to zero as $F\to0$. One cannot infer a unique accelerator choice without an additional constraint. The standard practical preference is the second boxed answer, with its stated comparison. <Muon decay> introduces a separate lifetime constraint if slow acceleration is contemplated, but it was not part of the specified radiative-loss calculation.
