== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For a massive particle on a timelike <world line>, <proper time> satisfies $d\tau=dt\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}=dt/\gamma$. Its <four-velocity> and <four-acceleration> are $U=dX/d\tau$ and $A=dU/d\tau$. Differentiating $U=\gamma(c,\mathbf v)$ gives
$$
\boxed{A^\mu=\left(\gamma^4\frac{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a}{c},\quad
\gamma^2\mathbf a+\gamma^4\frac{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a}{c^2}\mathbf v\right).}
$$
The invariant <Minkowski inner products> are
$$
\boxed{U^2=c^2,\qquad P^2=m^2c^2,\qquad P=mU.}
$$
For the explicitly requested contraction,
$$
U\cdot A=\gamma^5\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a-\gamma^3\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a
-\gamma^5\frac{v^2}{c^2}\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a
=\gamma^3\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a\,[\gamma^2(1-v^2/c^2)-1]=0.
$$
Thus <four-acceleration> is orthogonal to <four-velocity>. Equivalently this follows by differentiating the constant <Lorentz scalar> $U^2=c^2$ with respect to <proper time>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The event separation is timelike precisely when
$$
\boxed{(D-B)^2=c^2(t_d-t_b)^2-|\mathbf x_d-\mathbf x_b|^2>0.}
$$
A simultaneous pair in an inertial frame would instead have $(D-B)^2=-|\Delta\mathbf x'|^2\le0$. Since a <Lorentz transformation> preserves this <interval>, no such simultaneous frame exists. A timelike pair can be brought to the same spatial position: the required <velocity> is $\mathbf u=\Delta\mathbf x/\Delta t$, whose magnitude is less than $c$.

The separate condition $D\cdot B=0$ says that the two event-position <four-vectors>, measured from the chosen spacetime origin, are orthogonal in the <Minkowski inner product>. It is invariant under <Lorentz transformations> about that origin but not under arbitrary <translations>. It is not a general criterion for their causal separation.

For a concrete example, put $B=(cT,0,0,0)$ and $D=(0,R,0,0)$. Their inner product vanishes, but their separation has squared interval $c^2T^2-R^2$, which can be positive, zero or negative. If $B$ is timelike, a rest frame for $B$ makes $D^0=0$; this means that $D$ and the chosen origin are simultaneous in that frame, not that $D$ and $B$ are simultaneous.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The <proper acceleration> is $\alpha=\sqrt{-A^2}$, the magnitude of the instantaneous rest-frame three-acceleration. A <pure force> is a <four-force> preserving <rest mass>, or equivalently satisfying $F\cdot U=0$. Indeed $F=d(mU)/d\tau$ gives $F\cdot U=c^2dm/d\tau$.

For the right-hand branch of the hyperbola, use $c=1$, $x>0$ and $x=\sqrt{L^2+t^2}$, taking $L>0$. Direct differentiation gives
$$
v=\frac{dx}{dt}=\frac t x,\qquad
|v|=\sqrt{1-\frac{L^2}{x^2}},\qquad
\gamma=\frac xL,\qquad
 a=\frac{dv}{dt}=\frac{L^2}{x^3}.
$$
The sign of $v$ follows the sign of $t$, whereas the coordinate acceleration on this branch is positive. Consequently
$$
\boxed{a\gamma^3=\frac1L=\alpha.}
$$
The proper-time parameterization $t=L\sinh(\tau/L)$, $x=L\cosh(\tau/L)$ independently gives constant <proper acceleration>. Restoring dimensions, a hyperbola $x^2-c^2t^2=L^2$ has $\alpha=c^2/L$. Constant proper acceleration does not mean that all inertial-frame components of the <four-force> are constant.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $f=|\mathbf f|$ and $\hat{\mathbf f}=\mathbf f/f$. A constant laboratory three-force means $d\mathbf p/dt=\mathbf f$, hence $\mathbf p=\mathbf f t$ from rest. Using $E^2=m^2c^4+p^2c^2$ gives
$$
\boxed{\gamma(t)=\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{ft}{mc}\right)^2},\qquad
\boldsymbol\beta(t)=\frac{ft/(mc)}{\sqrt{1+[ft/(mc)]^2}}\,\hat{\mathbf f}.}
$$
The dimensionless time $s=ft/(mc)$ makes the shapes transparent: $\gamma=\sqrt{1+s^2}$ starts at one with zero slope and becomes asymptotically linear; $\beta=s/\sqrt{1+s^2}$ starts linearly and approaches one without reaching it at finite time. The <acceleration> decreases as $f/(m\gamma^3)$.

Integrating the <velocity> gives the distance along the force,
$$
\ell(t)=\frac{mc^2}{f}[\gamma(t)-1].
$$
Thus a constant three-force produces <hyperbolic motion> with constant <proper acceleration> $f/m$, although its velocity is never Newtonian at large time. The following original curves supply the requested sketches.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The magnitude of the electric force is $f=e\mathcal E$, directed so that the electron gains <energy>. The work across the gap is $e\mathcal E L=50\,\mathrm{MeV}$. With the source precision $mc^2=0.511\,\mathrm{MeV}$,
$$
\boxed{\gamma=1+\frac{50}{0.511}=98.84736,\qquad
\beta=\sqrt{1-\gamma^{-2}}=0.999948826.}
$$
Reducing the field by 20 percent gives kinetic <energy> $40\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and
$$
\gamma_{80\%}=79.27789,\qquad\beta_{80\%}=0.999920442,
\qquad\boxed{\Delta\beta=-2.83837\times10^{-5}.}
$$
A large fractional energy change therefore produces only a small speed change, since the original electron is already highly relativistic.

For the transit time, combine the distance formula from <1/d/solution> with $\gamma^2-1=[ft/(mc)]^2$:
$$
\boxed{t=\frac{mc}{e\mathcal E}\sqrt{\gamma^2-1}
=\frac Lc\sqrt{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}}
=3.36956\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm s.}
$$
This is slightly longer than $L/c$, since the electron starts from rest. For the reduced field it becomes $3.37798\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm s$.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For a <Lorentz boost> of speed $v$ along $x$, the <velocity-addition formula> formulas are
$$
u'_x=\frac{u_x-v}{1-u_xv/c^2},\qquad
u'_y=\frac{u_y}{\gamma_v(1-u_xv/c^2)}.
$$
Substitute $u_x=u\cos\theta$, $u_y=u\sin\theta$ and take the ratio. The common time-transformation denominator cancels, yielding
$$
\boxed{\tan\theta'=\frac{u\sin\theta}{\gamma_v(u\cos\theta-v)}.}
$$
To specify the direction rather than just its tangent, use $\theta'=\operatorname{atan2}(u\sin\theta/\gamma_v,u\cos\theta-v)$. This keeps the correct quadrant when the boost reverses the sign of the longitudinal <velocity>. For physical subluminal speeds the canceled denominator is positive. At $u_x=v$ and nonzero $u_y$, the transformed direction is perpendicular to the boost; if the particle is exactly comoving, its transformed velocity vanishes and the angle is undefined.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The initial <momentum> magnitude satisfies $p^2/(m^2c^2)=\gamma_i^2-1=288$. Equality of longitudinal and transverse magnitudes therefore gives
$$
p_{z,i}=p_\perp=12mc.
$$
An electric field along $z$ changes $p_z$, while the magnetic <Lorentz force> only rotates the transverse momentum. Its magnitude $p_\perp$ remains fixed. At the longitudinal turning point $p_z=0$, hence
$$
\boxed{\gamma_f=\sqrt{1+\frac{p_\perp^2}{m^2c^2}}=\sqrt{145}=12.04159.}
$$
For an electron initially moving toward positive $z$, the decelerating field points toward positive $z$ because its charge is $-e$. The electric work over $\Delta z=1\,\mathrm m$ gives
$$
-eE_z\Delta z=(\gamma_f-17)mc^2,
\qquad
\boxed{E_z=2.53375\,\mathrm{MV\,m^{-1}}.}
$$
The radius is determined by transverse momentum rather than total <energy>:
$$
\boxed{R(z)=\frac{p_\perp}{e|B|}=\frac{12mc}{e|B|},}
$$
so the requested radius plot is horizontal. The cyclotron angular frequency changes as $e|B|/(\gamma m)$ and the helix pitch shrinks to zero. These statements neglect radiation, as stipulated.

=== c
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

Each <photon> has energy $\varepsilon=\hbar\omega$. Their total <four-momentum> is
$$
P=\frac\varepsilon c(2,1+\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0).
$$
Its squared invariant is $P^2=2\varepsilon^2(1-\cos\theta)/c^2$. Therefore the system's <rest energy> is
$$
\boxed{Mc^2=\sqrt{E_{\rm tot}^2-c^2|\mathbf p_{\rm tot}|^2}
=2\hbar\omega\,\left|\sin\frac\theta2\right|.}
$$
Two individually massless <particles> can thus have positive total <invariant mass>. Back-to-back photons give rest energy $2\hbar\omega$, whereas exactly parallel photons have zero invariant mass and no massive-system rest frame.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

The <center-of-momentum frame> moves with velocity $\mathbf V=c^2\mathbf p_{\rm tot}/E_{\rm tot}$. Using the total <four-momentum> in <2/c/a/solution> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf V=\frac c2(1+\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0),\qquad
\frac{|\mathbf V|}{c}=\left|\cos\frac\theta2\right|.}
$$
For $0<\theta\le\pi$, its direction bisects the two photon directions, and its speed decreases from the limiting value $c$ to zero. At $\theta=\pi$, the lab already is the <center-of-momentum frame>. At $\theta=0$, the formal speed is $c$ and no inertial rest frame exists; this endpoint is a limit, not an allowable boost.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $\varepsilon=\hbar\omega$ be the incident laser-photon energy and $\gamma=W/(mc^2)$. The maximum in the usual soft-laser geometry occurs for a head-on encounter followed by a forward outgoing photon along the electron beam. Boost into the initial electron rest frame: the incident energy becomes $\varepsilon_*=\gamma(1+\beta)\varepsilon$. Backscattering there gives the <Compton scattering> recoil relation $\varepsilon_{*,s}=\varepsilon_*/[1+2\varepsilon_*/(mc^2)]$. Boosting this outgoing photon forward to the lab gives
$$
\boxed{\varepsilon_{\max}=\frac{\gamma^2(1+\beta)^2\varepsilon}{1+2\gamma(1+\beta)\varepsilon/(mc^2)}.}
$$
The familiar $4\gamma^2\varepsilon$ limit additionally requires $\gamma\gg1$ and $\varepsilon_*\ll mc^2$. The denominator is the recoil correction, not an optional factor at arbitrary incident energy.

For completeness, if the incidence direction is prescribed rather than optimized, let its unit vector be $\mathbf n$ and the outgoing direction $\mathbf n_s$. Squaring the residual electron four-momentum on its mass shell gives the more general kinematic relation
$$
\varepsilon_s=\frac{\varepsilon(W-c\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf n)}{W-c\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf n_s+\varepsilon(1-\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf n_s)}.
$$
Thus an unspecified crossing angle must not silently be identified with head-on incidence; the boxed result is the maximum for the stated head-on soft-photon arrangement.

Numerically $W=2\,\mathrm{GeV}$ gives $\gamma=3913.89$, and a $1\,\mathrm{cm}$ photon has energy $1.23984\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{eV}$. The maximum scattered energy is $7.5970\,\mathrm{keV}$, giving
$$
\boxed{\lambda_{\rm scattered}=1.6320\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm m=0.16320\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
The rest-frame incident photon is only about one electronvolt, so the small recoil correction is consistent with the amplification estimate.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Three useful observational phenomena are <relativistic Doppler effect> in spectral lines from approaching and receding ejecta, <relativistic beaming> through jet brightness asymmetry, and <apparent superluminal motion> inferred from successive radio images of moving knots. The first compares spectral frequencies with rest-frame lines; the second compares the two sides after accounting for intrinsic emission and absorption; the third tests the light-travel-time relation $\beta_{\rm app}=\beta\sin\vartheta/(1-\beta\cos\vartheta)$. NRAO's time sequence of Galactic microquasar ejecta gives a concrete example of the third measurement.<Microquasars in Our Own Galaxy by National Radio Astronomy Observatory>[VLA time-series observations of GRS 1915+105 and explanation of apparent superluminal separation by subluminal jets directed toward the observer]{ref}

These are tests of relativistic radiation kinematics when source geometry and environmental effects are controlled, rather than claims that any one redshift or brightness contrast by itself uniquely proves <special relativity>.

For the frequency transformation, a photon has <wave four-vector> $K=(\omega/c,\mathbf k)$ with $|\mathbf k|=\omega/c$ in vacuum. The temporal component of a <Lorentz boost> is
$$
\frac{\omega'}c=\gamma_v\left(\frac\omega c-\frac{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf k}{c}\right),
\qquad
\boxed{\omega'=\gamma_v\omega\left(1-\frac vc\cos\theta\right).}
$$
This is the <relativistic Doppler effect>: frequency changes both because of relative motion along the ray and because clocks in relative motion are time dilated. The angle here is measured in S. A frame chasing the photon redshifts it, $\omega'/\omega=\sqrt{(1-\beta)/(1+\beta)}$; a head-on frame blueshifts it. Transverse angles defined in different frames must not be interchanged.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <phase velocity> is the speed of a constant-phase surface, $v_{\rm ph}=\omega/k$. The <group velocity> is the envelope speed of a narrow packet, $v_{\rm gr}=d\omega/dk$, requiring a local <dispersion relation> rather than only a single monochromatic wave. In an isotropic transparent medium with $k=n(\omega)\omega/c$,
$$
\boxed{v_{\rm ph}=\frac c{n(\omega)},\qquad
v_{\rm gr}=\frac c{n(\omega)+\omega\,dn/d\omega}.}
$$
For a vacuum wave, $n\equiv1$ over the packet bandwidth, so both speeds are $c$ and $\boxed{v_{\rm ph}v_{\rm gr}=c^2}$. No <superluminal signalling> is implied by a phase speed in a dispersive material.

Strictly, the value $n(\omega_0)=1$ at one frequency alone does not prove the product identity. For example $n(\omega)=1+q(\omega-\omega_0)/\omega_0$ gives $v_{\rm ph}(\omega_0)=c$ but $v_{\rm gr}(\omega_0)=c/(1+q)$. The vacuum interpretation, or an equivalent nondispersive assumption, is necessary.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Keep the unequal masses: equal and opposite velocities are not equal and opposite momenta. Write the rest energies $r=m_ec^2$, $R=M_pc^2$, $S=r+R$, and let the incoming Lorentz factor of each particle be $\gamma$. Then
$$
E_{\rm tot}=\gamma S,\qquad cp_z=\gamma\beta(r-R),\qquad
E_{\rm tot}^2-c^2p_z^2=(R-r)^2+4rR\gamma^2.
$$
The transverse photon has energy $\varepsilon=hc/\lambda$ and no $z$ momentum. If the final composite has rest energy $Q$, squaring its four-momentum gives the exact constraint
$$
\boxed{Q^2=(R-r)^2+4rR\gamma^2-2\varepsilon\gamma S.}
$$
The mixed term contains no longitudinal momentum contribution because the photon is transverse. For a specified final rest energy, the physical positive root is
$$
\gamma=\frac{\varepsilon S+\sqrt{\varepsilon^2S^2+4rR[Q^2-(R-r)^2]}}{4rR},
$$
provided it is real and at least one.

The source does not specify the composite rest mass or its binding energy. With the usual approximation that the lowest final rest energy is $Q=S$, the minimum incoming energy occurs at that lowest mass and is
$$
\boxed{E_{{\rm tot},\min}=S\left[\chi+\sqrt{1+\chi^2}\right],\qquad
\chi=\frac{\varepsilon S}{4rR}=\frac{hc}{4\mu c^2\lambda},\quad
\mu=\frac{m_eM_p}{m_e+M_p}.}
$$
If a hydrogen bound state is intended at precision sensitive to its binding, replace $Q$ by its actual rest energy in the preceding equation; the mass cannot be inferred exactly from the phrase “single particle.” Higher composite excitation raises the required incoming energy on this branch. Setting initial total momentum to zero would give a different answer and contradict the stipulated equal-speed electron and proton.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For a forward wave, the <Faraday's law of induction> fixes the relative amplitudes and signs:
$$
\mathbf B_0=\frac1\omega\mathbf k\times\mathbf E_0,
\qquad B_0=-\frac{k}{\omega}E_0=-\frac ncE_0.
$$
Thus the two scalar amplitudes in the source cannot both be positive when the stated propagation direction and axes are used. The supplied product magnitude fixes only an overall amplitude in the chosen field units.

One convenient potential representative is
$$
\boxed{\varphi=0,\qquad
\mathbf A=-\hat{\mathbf y}\frac{E_0}{\omega}\sin(\omega t-kz),\qquad
K^\mu=(\omega/c,0,0,n\omega/c).}
$$
Indeed $-\partial_t\mathbf A$ gives the electric field and $\nabla\times\mathbf A$ gives the signed magnetic field above. Moreover $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A+c^{-2}\partial_t\varphi=0$, the <Lorenz gauge condition>. The potentials are not unique because residual <gauge transformations> may preserve this condition.

Since $n>1$, the electromagnetic <electromagnetic field invariants> satisfy $\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B=0$ and $B^2-E^2/c^2=(n^2-1)E^2/c^2>0$ away from field nodes. A boost along positive $z$ with speed $u=c/n$ gives
$$
E'_y=\gamma_u(E_y+uB_x)=0,\qquad
B'_x=-\frac{E_0}{c}\sqrt{n^2-1}\cos(\omega't'-k'z').
$$
Here $\omega'=\gamma_u(\omega-uk)=0$ and $k'=(\omega/c)\sqrt{n^2-1}$. The wave becomes a static spatially varying magnetic pattern in this frame. This is possible because its medium wave four-vector is spacelike, $K^2=(1-n^2)\omega^2/c^2<0$; it is not a vacuum-photon rest frame. The medium moves in the new frame, so its original rest-frame constitutive relation must not be reused there unchanged.

No inertial frame makes the magnetic field vanish throughout a nontrivial wave: that would turn the positive invariant $B^2-E^2/c^2$ into a nonpositive one. Both fields vanish at isolated nodes already, which is distinct from removing a field everywhere.

== 4
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Take $A^\mu=(\varphi/c,\mathbf A)$, $\partial_\mu=(c^{-1}\partial_t,\nabla)$ and $J^\mu=(c\rho,\mathbf j)$. Then
$$
\mathbf E=-\nabla\varphi-\partial_t\mathbf A,\qquad
\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A,
\qquad F^{0i}=-E_i/c,\qquad F^{ij}=-\epsilon^{ijk}B_k.
$$
Expanding the cyclic derivative of $F^{ab}=\partial^aA^b-\partial^bA^a$ produces six second derivatives, which cancel in pairs because partial derivatives commute. Hence
$$
\boxed{\partial^cF^{ab}+\partial^aF^{bc}+\partial^bF^{ca}=0.}
$$
This <Bianchi identity> contains $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B$. The inhomogeneous <Maxwell equations> are
$$
\boxed{\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=\mu_0J^\nu,\qquad
\partial^\mu F_{\mu\nu}=\mu_0J_\nu.}
$$
The temporal component is $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0$, since $\epsilon_0\mu_0c^2=1$; the spatial components give $\nabla\times\mathbf B-c^{-2}\partial_t\mathbf E=\mu_0\mathbf j$. The source suppresses the coupling factor, as in units with $\mu_0=1$ or a rescaled current.

For the physical <Lorentz force density> on matter, the consistent tensor formula is
$$
\boxed{f^\mu=F^{\mu\nu}J_\nu=
\left(\frac{\mathbf j\cdot\mathbf E}{c},\rho\mathbf E+\mathbf j\times\mathbf B\right),\qquad
f_\mu=F_{\mu\nu}J^\nu.}
$$
Its temporal component is power transferred to matter per unit volume divided by $c$. The printed expression $J^\nu F_{\nu\mu}$ has the **opposite sign** for the stated definition of $F$ in this convention. A stationary positive charge in a positive $x$ electric field is a concrete check: the physical upper spatial force is $+\rho E_x$, whereas the printed expression, raised to an upper index, gives $-\rho E_x$. It can represent the opposite momentum transfer to the electromagnetic field, but must not be identified with the force on the charge without changing a convention.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

In the massive particle's rest frame, $P=(m_0c,\mathbf0)$ and the physical <Pauli-Lubanski pseudovector> is $W=(0,m_0c\,\mathbf s_{\rm rest})$, up to a common overall sign. Their <Minkowski inner product> is therefore zero. Since it is a <Lorentz scalar>,
$$
\boxed{W\cdot P=0,\qquad U_W\cdot P=0}
$$
in every inertial frame, for any nonzero scalar normalization of $W$. This proves the requested orthogonality without a boosted component calculation.

The printed assertion that its particular normalization is always a unit vector needs qualification. For the displayed components $W=(\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf p,E\mathbf s/c)$,
$$
W^2=-m_0^2c^2|\mathbf s|^2-|\mathbf s\times\mathbf p|^2.
$$
Thus dividing by $m_0c|\mathbf s|$ is unit spacelike only when $\mathbf s\times\mathbf p=0$, or when $\mathbf s$ in that denominator is explicitly replaced by the proper rest-spin magnitude with correctly boosted components of $W$. As a counterexample to the literal displayed normalization, choose $\mathbf s\perp\mathbf p$ with nonzero momentum: it gives $U_W^2=-\gamma^2$, not $-1$. Orthogonality remains true, but it does not establish normalization.

=== c
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

An isolated free <electron> in vacuum cannot spontaneously emit a nonzero real photon. If $P=P_f+Q$, where $Q$ is the emitted photon four-momentum, $P^2=P_f^2=m^2c^2$ and $Q^2=0$, conservation would require $P_f\cdot Q=0$. A future-pointing timelike <four-vector> has strictly positive inner product with every nonzero future-pointing null <four-vector>, so this is impossible.

The same conclusion follows from the classical <Cherenkov radiation> condition $v>c/n$: in vacuum $n=1$, while the electron with $\gamma=100$ has $\beta=\sqrt{1-10^{-4}}<1$. Hence there is no vacuum emission angle for the specified free particle. Acceleration by an external field or scattering from another body would change the physical problem and can permit radiation.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

In a transparent uniform medium, constructive phase interference along the trajectory requires $\omega-\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v=0$. Since $k=n\omega/c$, the <Cherenkov cone angle> obeys
$$
\boxed{\cos\theta_C=\frac1{n\beta}.}
$$
Here $n\beta=2\sqrt{1-10^{-4}}>1$, so emission is allowed and
$$
\boxed{\theta_C=\arccos\left[\frac1{2\sqrt{1-10^{-4}}}\right]
=59.99835^\circ.}
$$
The <electron> is not an isolated vacuum particle: it interacts with the dielectric polarization of the medium. Here the angle describes the <photon> direction relative to the trajectory; the conical <wavefront> surface has the complementary angle.

This angle is the classical or negligible-recoil result. At finite photon energy, energy-momentum bookkeeping in a nondispersive medium gives a recoil correction proportional to $\hbar\omega/E$; no photon frequency is supplied for such a correction. In a dispersive medium use $n(\omega)$ separately for each transparent frequency band.

=== d
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Model the wire as an infinite straight line along $z$. Let $\lambda_0$ be its rest-frame line charge density and $\lambda$ its laboratory line charge density. Length contraction gives $\lambda=\gamma_v\lambda_0$. If the symbol $\rho$ in the source is intended as volume charge density, replace these line densities by their integrals over the cross section; an unspecified wire radius does not determine an interior field.

In the comoving frame the wire is static, so <Gauss's law> and cylindrical symmetry give the exterior fields
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E'(r)=\frac{\lambda_0}{2\pi\epsilon_0r}\hat{\mathbf r},\qquad
\mathbf B'=\mathbf0.}
$$
If the source's $\rho$ means laboratory line density, this electric field is $\rho/(2\pi\epsilon_0\gamma_vr)$; if it means proper line density, it is $\rho/(2\pi\epsilon_0r)$. Stating the density's reference frame is necessary for an unambiguous factor of $\gamma_v$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Boost the comoving electric field perpendicular to itself along the wire. The transverse <electric field> gains a factor $\gamma_v$, while a circulating <magnetic field> appears. Equivalently apply <Gauss's law> and <Ampère's law> with laboratory line charge $\lambda$ and current $I=\lambda v$:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(r)=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0r}\hat{\mathbf r}
=\gamma_v\mathbf E',\qquad
\mathbf B(r)=\frac{\mu_0\lambda v}{2\pi r}\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}
=\frac1{c^2}\mathbf v\times\mathbf E.}
$$
These are exterior fields of the ideal straight wire. For laboratory line density $\rho$, substitute $\lambda=\rho$; for proper line density $\rho$, substitute $\lambda=\gamma_v\rho$. The radial distance is transverse to the boost and unchanged between the two frames.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The final unnumbered force question has this inferred third subpart. For either wire's exterior field,
$$
\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B=0,\qquad
\boxed{E^2-c^2B^2=E^2(1-v^2/c^2)>0.}
$$
This <electromagnetic field invariants> is positive in every inertial frame. A purely magnetic nonzero field would instead have $E'^2-c^2B'^2<0$. Thus no physical boost makes the interaction purely magnetic. There is a comoving frame with a purely electric interaction, which is consistent with the invariant's sign.

The convected charge current gives the same conclusion. Its effective line-current invariant is $\lambda^2-I^2/c^2=\lambda_0^2>0$, so no inertial frame can neutralize the charge while leaving only a current. In particular for a longitudinal boost of speed $u$, $\lambda'=\gamma_u\lambda(1-uv/c^2)$ cannot vanish when $|u|,|v|<c$.

For two identical wires separated by $d$, the laboratory transverse force per unit length is
$$
\boxed{\frac FL=\lambda E-IB
=\frac{\lambda^2}{2\pi\epsilon_0d}(1-v^2/c^2)
=\frac{\lambda_0^2}{2\pi\epsilon_0d}>0.}
$$
Electric repulsion exceeds the magnetic attraction. This assumes each moving wire has uncompensated convected charge; a neutral wire carrying conduction current is a different charge-current system and has a different invariant classification.
