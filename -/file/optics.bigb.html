= Optics
{wiki}

Optics studies <light>, its propagation, and instruments for forming images and measuring <optical spectra>.

The central question is how <light> carries information from a source to an observer. A useful description must explain both where the <light> goes and how its distribution changes. In <geometrical optics>, <light rays> locate paths and images; in <physical optics>, an <electromagnetic wave> has a <wave amplitude> and a <wave phase>, so overlapping contributions can produce <wave interference> and <diffraction>. These descriptions address different levels of detail within the same subject. \a[https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-1-the-propagation-of-light][OpenStax: The propagation of light]{ref}.

An image is especially interesting because a single object point emits <light> in many directions. An <optical lens> redirects part of that spreading bundle toward a corresponding image point; repeating this construction over the object preserves spatial information. Simply gathering more <light> is insufficient: <light rays> from distinct object points must arrive at distinct locations. This is why an <optical camera> needs both an <optical lens> and a correctly positioned receiving surface. The <thin lens> example below makes that correspondence quantitative. \a[https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/2-3-images-formed-by-refraction][OpenStax: Images formed by refraction]{ref}.

= Lambertian reflectance
{c}
{parent=Optics}
{wiki}

Lambertian reflectance is the ideal diffuse-reflection model with direction-independent outgoing radiance, the frequency-integrated <specific intensity>. A surface with hemispherical reflectance $\rho$ receiving irradiance $F$ has outgoing radiance $\rho F/\pi$. The observed radiant intensity still acquires the projected-area cosine factor described by <Lambert's cosine law>.

= Lambert's cosine law
{c}
{parent=Optics}
{wiki}
{title2=$I(\theta)=I(0)\cos\theta$}

For an ideal diffuse emitting or reflecting planar surface, the radiant intensity in a direction at angle $\theta$ to its normal is proportional to $\cos\theta$. The factor comes from projected area. <Lambertian reflectance> instead describes the corresponding direction-independent radiance; intensity and radiance are distinct quantities.

= Optical lattice
{parent=Optics}
{title2=$V(x)=sE_R\sin^2(\pi x/d)$}
{wiki}

An optical lattice is a periodic <optical trap> produced by interfering light fields. Its <periodic potential> gives atomic <Bloch states> and <energy bands>. For the displayed one-dimensional lattice, the <recoil energy> is $E_R=\hbar^2\pi^2/(2md^2)$ and $s$ measures the depth in recoil units.

= Condensate compressibility in an optical lattice
{parent=Optical lattice}
{title2=$\kappa^{-1}=n(\partial\mu/\partial n)_s$}

Here $n$ is average <number density> and $\mu$ the ground-state <chemical potential>. This commonly used condensate response has units of inverse energy; thermodynamic <isothermal compressibility> is instead $\kappa/n$. In a weakly interacting repulsive condensate, the normalized one-cell ground state $f_s$, with average $|f_s|^2=1$, gives $\mu=E_0(s)+Un I(s)+\cdots$, $I(s)=d^{-1}\int_0^d|f_s|^4dx$. Localization raises $I$ and reduces $\kappa$ at fixed $n,U,d$. In deep harmonic wells $I\propto s^{1/4}$, hence $\kappa\propto s^{-1/4}$. This is a ground-band, repulsive-superfluid statement with the held variables specified, not a theorem for arbitrary signs of the interaction or arbitrary excited branches.

= Recoil energy
{parent=Optical lattice}
{title2=$E_R=p_R^2/(2m)$}

Recoil energy is the <kinetic energy> gained by an initially stationary atom absorbing a photon of momentum $p_R$. In a standing-wave <optical lattice> of spacing $d$, the photon wavenumber is $\pi/d$, giving $E_R=\hbar^2\pi^2/(2md^2)$.

= Refraction
{parent=Optics}
{wiki}

Refraction changes the direction of a transmitted <wave> when it crosses between media with different <phase velocities>. For <light> at a planar interface, <Snell's law> relates the angles measured from the <normal vector> through the <refractive indices>. At normal incidence the direction remains unchanged even though the <wavelength> and <phase velocity> can change. The worked example under <geometrical optics> distinguishes transmission from <total internal reflection>.

= Image formation
{parent=Optics}

An optical system forms an image when the <light rays> from each object point converge to, or appear to originate from, a corresponding image point. A <thin lens> gives a calculable example; <real images> and <virtual images> distinguish actual intersection from intersection of backward ray extensions.

= Real image
{parent=Image formation}
{wiki}

A real image is formed where outgoing <light rays> actually converge. A screen at the image position can collect the focused pattern. The <thin lens> example with object distance $0.3\,\mathrm m$ and focal length $0.1\,\mathrm m$ places a real image $0.15\,\mathrm m$ beyond the lens.

= Virtual image
{parent=Image formation}
{wiki}

A virtual image is located where backward extensions of outgoing <light rays> meet. The rays do not actually pass through that image position. A plane mirror and a converging <thin lens> used with the object inside its focal length provide examples.

= Transverse magnification
{parent=Magnification}
{title2=$m=h_i/h_o$}

Transverse magnification is the ratio of signed image height to signed object height. Under the real-object positive-distance convention of the <thin lens> article, $m=-v/u$. A negative value indicates an inverted image; $|m|$ gives its size relative to the object. This differs from angular <magnification>, which compares apparent viewing angles.

= Specular reflection
{parent=Optics}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mathbf r=\mathbf i-2(\mathbf i\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf n$}

At a smooth mirror with unit <normal vector> $n$, an incoming unit direction $i$ has outgoing direction $i-2(i\cdot n)n$. Thus the incident and reflected rays make equal angles with the normal.

More precisely, <specular reflection> redirects each incident <light ray> into the direction prescribed by the <law of reflection>, rather than spreading it over many outgoing directions. The incident <light ray>, reflected <light ray>, and local <normal vector> lie in one plane. The surface must be sufficiently smooth on the scale of the <wavelength> for this model to describe its reflected beam; a visibly flat surface can still scatter <light> if its microscopic roughness is appreciable. \a[https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-2-the-law-of-reflection][OpenStax: The law of reflection]{ref}.

The displayed formula uses <unit vectors> $\mathbf i,\mathbf n,\mathbf r$ and the <dot product>. It preserves the component parallel to the mirror and reverses the <normal component>. For a mirror along $y=0$, take $\mathbf n=(0,1)$ and $\mathbf i=(3/5,-4/5)$. Then $\mathbf i\cdot\mathbf n=-4/5$ and $\mathbf r=(3/5,4/5)$: both directions have <vector norm> one, and both make an <angle> $\arccos(4/5)\approx36.87^\circ$ with the relevant normal direction. Replacing $\mathbf n$ by $-\mathbf n$ leaves the result unchanged.

This same local rule explains a flat mirror's <virtual image>. For a source at $(0,a)$ above $y=0$, backward extensions of reflected <light rays> intersect at $(0,-a)$. The <light> remains above the mirror; its apparent origin lies below it. A curved mirror changes its <normal vector> from point to point and can instead make reflected <light rays> converge. The geometry of image formation therefore starts with one simple direction rule. \a[https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/2-1-images-formed-by-plane-mirrors][OpenStax: Images formed by plane mirrors]{ref}.

= Reflection curve on a surface
{parent=Specular reflection}

For a <parametric surface> $P(u,v)$, source <parametric curve> $Q(t)$ and eye $E$, put $a=(Q-P)/\lVert Q-P\rVert$ and $b=(E-P)/\lVert E-P\rVert$. The visible reflection locus satisfies $(a+b)\cdot P_u=(a+b)\cdot P_v=0$, with the source and eye on the reflecting side. Rank two of the resulting <Jacobian matrix> gives a locally one-dimensional locus in $(u,v,t)$ by the <implicit function theorem>.

= Spatial light modulator

{wiki}

= SLM
{c}
{synonym}

A <spatial light modulator> applies an electronically controlled spatially varying optical phase, amplitude, or polarization change. In a <4f pulse shaper>, frequency maps to transverse position, allowing the pixels to implement a calibrated spectral mask. A phase-only modulator generally needs additional polarization optics or another modulation stage for independent amplitude control.

= Spectral pulse shaping
{parent=Optics}
{title2=$\widetilde E_{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)=M(\omega)\widetilde E_{\mathrm{in}}(\omega)$}

<Spectral pulse shaping> changes an optical pulse by applying a complex amplitude-and-phase mask $M(\omega)$ to its frequency components. The output in time is the inverse <Fourier transform> of the masked spectrum. A passive device cannot amplify unavailable spectral components; finite bandwidth and spectral resolution constrain the realizable waveforms and time window.

= 4f pulse shaper
{parent=Spectral pulse shaping}
{title2=Grating–lens–mask–lens–grating}

A <4f pulse shaper> uses a <diffraction grating> to disperse an input pulse, a lens to focus its frequencies at different positions in a shared Fourier plane, a spectral mask or <spatial light modulator> there to modify them, and a second lens and grating to recombine them. In the symmetric idealization the distances are $f,f,f,f$, with the lenses separated by $2f$. Shaping the spatially resolved spectrum implements <spectral pulse shaping> rather than a direct time-domain mask.

= Optical tweezers
{parent=Optics}

= Optical trap
{synonym}

An <optical trap> uses the forces from a focused light field to confine or manipulate a small particle. In an overdamped one-dimensional model, a translating localized potential exerts $F(x-v_Tt)$ and balances <linear drag>.

= Optical momentum efficiency
{parent=Optical tweezers}
{title2=$F=QP/c$}

An optical momentum efficiency $Q$ expresses the net axial momentum transferred to a bead relative to incident power $P$ divided by vacuum light speed. It absorbs medium-index factors and the incident/outgoing ray geometry. Refractive-index contrast alone does not determine $Q$ for a transparent bead illuminated by a focused beam. The estimate $Q\sim m-1$ is a weak-contrast scale, not a universal force formula. An effective reflector with power reflectance $\mathcal R$ instead gives $Q\simeq2\mathcal R$ in the simple vacuum-momentum convention.

= Passage through a translating optical trap
{parent=Optical tweezers}
{title2=$\Delta t,\Delta x$}

For a <force> profile supported on an interval of width $w$, set $u=F/\zeta$. If a trap of speed $v$ overtakes an overdamped particle with $v-u>0$, then $\Delta t=\int dy/(v-u)$ and $\Delta x=v\Delta t-w$. These expressions follow by changing to the trap-frame coordinate, whose <velocity> is $u-v$. Smooth zeroes of $v-u$ can prevent finite passage.

= High-speed passage expansion of a localized trap
{parent=Passage through a translating optical trap}
{title2=$\Delta x=\sum_{m\geq1}v_T^{-m}\int(F/\zeta)^m dy$}

If the maximum force-induced speed is smaller in magnitude than $v_T$, expand the passage denominator as a uniformly convergent <geometric series>. For a localized conservative well with equal outside potential levels, the first force moment vanishes and displacement starts at order $v_T^{-2}$. An odd force has no cubic moment, giving the next correction at order $v_T^{-4}$. Compact force support alone does not guarantee a vanishing first moment or positive displacement.

= Sufficient escape condition for a translating trap
{parent=Passage through a translating optical trap}
{title2=$v_T>\sup(F/\zeta)$}

In the trap frame, a particle under <linear drag> has relative speed $F(y)/\zeta-v_T$. A uniform strictly negative relative speed guarantees finite passage through a <compact support>. For a bounded continuous force, $v_T>\sup F/\zeta$ supplies that condition. For ordinary smooth forces, an encountered zero of the relative speed is an equilibrium or a passage bottleneck, not a finite crossing. Negative forces do not impede forward trap passage.

= Repeated kicks from a circular optical trap
{parent=Passage through a translating optical trap}
{title2=$f_p=f_T\Delta x/(L+\Delta x)$}

For a passing <optical trap> on a circle of circumference $L$ with nonoverlapping support, a particle that is stationary between encounters receives displacement $\Delta x$ in each encounter. The trap revolution <frequency> $f_T$ and particle revolution <frequency> obey $Lf_p=\Delta x(f_T-f_p)$. This corrects the naive independent-kick estimate when the particle motion changes the encounter rate.

= Large-speed approximation for circular-trap kicks
{parent=Repeated kicks from a circular optical trap}
{title2=$f_p\simeq f_T\Delta x/(2\pi R)$}

The exact separated-kick response is $f_p=f_T\Delta x/(C+\Delta x)$ for circumference $C=2\pi R$. Its relative correction to the independent-kick estimate is controlled by $\Delta x/C$. For a localized well with force-induced speed bounded by $\epsilon v_T$, $\Delta x/C\leq(w/C)\epsilon^2/(1-\epsilon)$. Thus high speed is sufficient when the support width is below one circumference. The local straight-force approximation also requires a narrow trap on the curved track.

= Triangular optical-trap response
{parent=Repeated kicks from a circular optical trap}
{title2=$f_p/f_c$}

A triangular localized <potential energy> has forces $+F$ behind and $-F$ ahead of the trap centre. With $v_c=F/\zeta=Lf_c$, $\alpha=2X_0/L$ and $\beta=f_T/f_c$, its circular response is $f_p/f_c=\alpha\beta/(\beta^2-1+\alpha)$ for $\beta>1$. A trapped particle has $f_p/f_c=\beta$ for $0\le\beta\le1$, interpreting the discontinuous <force> at the cusp by a sticking or rounded-well limit.

= Forward displacement from a translating localized potential
{parent=Passage through a translating optical trap}
{title2=$v\Delta x=\int\dot x^2dt$}

For a translating <potential energy> profile with equal values at the two ends and <linear drag> <force> $-\zeta\dot x$, a finite passing trajectory satisfies $v\Delta x=\int\dot x^2dt\ge0$. Equivalently, since $\int F\,dy=0$, $\Delta x=\int u^2/[v(v-u)]\,dy$. <Compact support> of the <force> alone is insufficient. At high speed the displacement is $v^{-2}\int(F/\zeta)^2dy+O(v^{-3})$.

= Coronagraph
{parent=Optics}
{wiki}

A coronagraph suppresses the light of a bright central object to improve detection of nearby faint sources. In <exoplanet direct imaging>, its inner working angle and residual stellar leakage constrain accessible separations and contrasts.

= Scattering of light
{parent=Optics}

Scattering of light redirects radiation through interaction with particles or spatial variations in a medium. It can remove light from a beam without true absorption, or add light from other directions. Wavelength and particle-size dependence make <Rayleigh scattering> and aerosol scattering useful probes of planetary atmospheres.

= Aperture stop
{parent=Aperture}

An <aperture stop> is the physical opening that limits the angular cone of rays from an on-axis object point. Its images through preceding and following optics are the entrance and exit <optical pupils>; it differs from a field stop, which limits the extent of the imaged field.

= Optical pupil
{parent=Aperture stop}

An <optical pupil> is an image of the <aperture stop>. The <entrance pupil> is its image viewed through optics before the stop; the exit <optical pupil> is its image through the optics after it. <optical pupil> reimaging maps the telescope aperture onto devices such as a <deformable mirror>.

= Light
{parent=Optics}
{wiki}

Light is <electromagnetic radiation> in the visible range; <optics> also studies neighboring <infrared> and <ultraviolet> radiation.

= Optical pulse
{parent=Light}
{title2=$\tau$}

An optical pulse is a finite-duration envelope of <light>. A nearly monochromatic pulse has many carrier oscillations within its duration $\tau$: in units with light speed one, its <wavelength> obeys $\lambda\ll\tau$. A slowly varying propagation-time map stretches the pulse duration by its derivative and divides the observed <frequency> by the same factor.

= Gaussian beam with one transverse coordinate
{parent=Gaussian beam}
{c}
{title2=$E=q^{-1/2}e^{-Az^2/q}$}

For the free <parabolic wave equation> $E_x=iE_{zz}/(2k)$, input $E(0,z)=e^{-Az^2}$ with $\operatorname{Re}A>0$ propagates as
$$
 E(x,z)=q(x)^{-1/2}e^{-Az^2/q(x)},\qquad q(x)=1+2iAx/k.
$$
The branch is continuous from $q(0)^{-1/2}=1$. <One-dimensional transverse Fresnel propagation> or two <Gaussian integrals> prove the formula. With $A=D^{-2}+ik/(2F)$, the squared envelope is $|q|^{-1}\exp[-2z^2/(D^2|q|^2)]$. The amplitude power is $-1/2$ because there is only one transverse coordinate. A two-transverse-coordinate <Gaussian beam> instead has power $-1$ when both coordinates share the same $q$.

= Wavefront
{parent=Physical optics}
{wiki}

A wavefront is a surface of constant <phase function>. A <plane wave> has planar wavefronts; variations in <refractive index> distort them.

= Optical path length
{parent=Wavefront}
{wiki}
{title2=$\int n\,ds$}

The optical path length along a ray is the integral of its <refractive index> over geometric length. A difference $h$ in optical path length produces a phase difference $2\pi h/\lambda$, where $\lambda$ is the vacuum <wavelength>.

= Wavefront error
{parent=Wavefront}
{title2=$h$}

A wavefront error is the difference in <optical path length> from a reference wavefront, often an ideal plane or sphere. Its <root mean square> should specify the illuminated pupil and which modes, such as constant phase or image displacement, have been removed.

= Modal amplitude norm versus wavefront RMS
{parent=Wavefront error}

For a real <wavefront error> $h=\sum_k a_k s_k$, its squared spatial <root mean square> is $\langle h^2\rangle=\sum_{k,l}a_ka_l\langle s_ks_l\rangle$. Orthogonal unit-peak <sine> modes have mean square $1/2$, so $h_{\rm rms}^2=\frac12\sum_k a_k^2$, whereas the coefficient norm is $\sqrt{\sum_k a_k^2}$. On a finite <optical pupil> <orthogonality> needs checking; independent uniform random <wave phases> give the same $1/2$ diagonal after <wave phase> averaging. This normalization matters when a <speckle contrast of a sinusoidal wavefront error> is specified by peak <wave amplitude>.

= Interference
{disambiguate=wave propagation}
{parent=Physical optics}
{wiki}

= Wave interference
{synonym}

Wave interference occurs when amplitudes add coherently. Equal phases give <constructive interference>; opposite phases can cancel.

= Which-path information
{parent=Interference (wave propagation)}

If the two arms have internal marker states $|a\rangle,|b\rangle$, an interference cross term is multiplied by $\langle a|b\rangle$. Orthogonal markers remove interference from polarization-insensitive detection even if nobody reads the markers. Coherently making the two markers identical erases that distinguishing information and restores the cross term.

Interferometric visibility measures the contrast of a scanned interference fringe. = Diffraction grating
{parent=Physical optics}
{wiki}

A diffraction grating is a periodic optical structure that directs different <wavelengths> into different angles through <wave interference>.

= Diffraction order
{parent=Diffraction grating}
{title2=$m$}

A diffraction order labels a <constructive interference> maximum by the integer number of <wavelengths> in the path difference between adjacent grating elements.

= Grating equation
{parent=Diffraction grating}
{title2=$m\lambda=d(\sin\alpha+\sin\beta)$}

For a reflection <diffraction grating> with both ray directions measured on the same side of its normal, adjacent grooves have path difference $d(\sin\alpha+\sin\beta)$. <Constructive interference> requires this to be an integer multiple $m\lambda$. Signed-angle conventions can turn the sum into a difference; the physical path difference is unchanged.

= Grating dispersion
{parent=Grating equation}
{title2=$d\beta/d\lambda=m/(d\cos\beta)$}

At fixed incidence angle, differentiating the <grating equation> gives $d\beta/d\lambda=m/(d\cos\beta)$. A camera with <focal length> $f$ therefore has local focal-plane dispersion $q=fm/(d\cos\beta)$ near its optical axis. With coordinate $X=f\tan(\beta-\beta_0)$, the full derivative is $q=f\sec^2(\beta-\beta_0)m/(d\cos\beta)$.

= Anamorphic magnification of a grating
{parent=Grating equation}
{title2=$|d\beta/d\alpha|=\cos\alpha/\cos\beta$}

At fixed <wavelength>, the <grating equation> gives $d\beta/d\alpha=-\cos\alpha/\cos\beta$. Thus an incident angular width is magnified by $\cos\alpha/\cos\beta$ in the dispersion direction. Including the ratio of camera and <collimator> <focal lengths> gives the projected slit width. The https://www.eso.org/sci/libraries/historicaldocuments/Operating_Manuals/Operating_Manual_No.9_A1b.pdf[ESO B&C operating manual, Appendix A] explicitly includes this anamorphic factor.

= Slit-limited resolving power of a grating
{parent=Anamorphic magnification of a grating}
{title2=$R=m\lambda f_{\rm coll}/(xd\cos\alpha)$}

A slit of width $x$ at the focus of a <collimator> subtends $x/f_{\rm coll}$. Its projected width is $x f_{\rm cam}\cos\alpha/(f_{\rm coll}\cos\beta)$. Dividing this width by the <grating dispersion> gives $\Delta\lambda=xd\cos\alpha/(mf_{\rm coll})$. Thus the <spectral resolving power> is $R=m\lambda f_{\rm coll}/(xd\cos\alpha)$. Omitting the anamorphic factor gives a denominator $\cos\beta$ instead; these agree in the <Littrow configuration>.

= Littrow configuration
{c}
{parent=Diffraction grating}

In the Littrow configuration the selected diffracted ray retraces the incident ray, so $\alpha=\beta$ and the <grating equation> becomes $m\lambda=2d\sin\alpha$.

= Echelle grating
{parent=Diffraction grating}
{wiki}

An echelle grating works in high <diffraction orders>. A separate <cross-disperser> separates their overlapping <optical spectra> on a two-dimensional detector.

= Free spectral range
{parent=Physical optics}
{wiki}
{title2=$\Delta\lambda_{\rm FSR}$}

The free spectral range is the wavelength or frequency spacing between adjacent orders or resonances of an optical instrument. It identifies when different orders can overlap.

= Free spectral range of an echelle grating
{parent=Free spectral range}

At fixed incident and central outgoing angles, the <grating equation> gives $m\lambda_m=K=d(\sin\alpha+\sin\beta_0)$. Adjacent central wavelengths are separated by
$$
\lambda_m-\lambda_{m+1}=\frac K{m(m+1)}\sim\frac{\lambda_m}m.
$$
This is the usual adjacent-order <free spectral range>. A detector width of approximately $q\Delta\lambda_{\rm FSR}$, where $q$ is the <grating dispersion>, covers one such interval.

= Adjacent-order spacing versus nearest-axis echelle interval
{parent=Free spectral range of an echelle grating}
{title2=$S_{\rm centres}=K/[m(m+1)]$}

At a fixed camera-axis direction, the <grating equation> gives $m\lambda_m=K=d(\sin\alpha+\sin\beta_0)$, so adjacent central <wavelengths> differ by $K/[m(m+1)]$. This differs from the exact <wavelength> cell where an order is nearest the <optical camera> axis. For $\beta_0=0$ and $x=f\tan\beta$, equal distances of orders $m$ and $m+1$ mean opposite <diffraction> <angles>; adding their <diffraction grating> equations gives $\lambda=K/(m+1/2)$. The full order-$m$ cell is bounded by $K/(m+1/2)$ and $K/(m-1/2)$, hence has width $K/(m^2-1/4)$ for $m\ge2$ and accessible neighbouring orders. Both widths approach $K/m^2$ at high order.

= Point spread function
{parent=Physical optics}
{wiki}
{title2=$\operatorname{PSF}$}

The point spread function is the image of an unresolved point source. It describes blurring from <diffraction>, <optical aberrations>, and <astronomical seeing>.

= Diffraction-limited system
{parent=Point spread function}
{wiki}

A diffraction-limited system has imaging performance set primarily by <diffraction> rather than by <optical aberrations>.

= Diffraction-limited resolution
{parent=Diffraction-limited system}
{title2=$\theta\sim\lambda/D$}

For a circular illuminated aperture of diameter $D$, the characteristic angular <diffraction> scale is $\lambda/D$. The first zero of the ideal intensity pattern lies at approximately $1.22\lambda/D$.

= Spatial resolution element
{parent=Diffraction-limited resolution}
{title2=$\Delta\vartheta\sim\lambda/D$}

A <spatial resolution element> is one independently resolved angular or spatial scale under a specified resolution criterion. For an ideal telescope of <diameter> $D$, its angular scale is of order $\lambda/D$ and its angular area of order $(\lambda/D)^2$. A <photodetector> <detector pixel> or <spaxel> can be smaller than a resolution element without adding independent spatial information.

= Strehl ratio
{c}
{parent=Point spread function}
{wiki}
{title2=$S=I_{\rm peak}/I_{\rm ideal,peak}$}

The Strehl ratio compares the peak of an observed <point spread function> with the ideal <diffraction-limited system> peak for the same pupil and total flux. It measures how strongly <wavefront errors> redistribute light away from the central peak.

= Maréchal approximation
{c}
{parent=Strehl ratio}
{title2=$S\simeq e^{-\sigma_\phi^2}$}

For small residual phase errors, $S\simeq e^{-\sigma_\phi^2}$, where $\sigma_\phi=2\pi h_{\rm rms}/\lambda$. The variance is taken after removing irrelevant constant phase. This approximation to the <Strehl ratio> is most useful near the <diffraction limit of a telescope>.

= Speckle pattern
{parent=Physical optics}
{wiki}

A speckle pattern is a granular intensity pattern from coherent <wave interference>. In stellar imaging, residual <wavefront errors> produce speckles around the central <point spread function>.

= Speckle contrast of a sinusoidal wavefront error
{parent=Speckle pattern}
{title2=$C=(\pi h_0/\lambda)^2$}

For a small sinusoidal <optical path length> error $h_0\cos(2\pi x/\Lambda)$, expand its phase factor to first order. The two exponential harmonics each have field amplitude $i\pi h_0/\lambda$ relative to the unperturbed field, so each speckle has contrast $C=(\pi h_0/\lambda)^2$. Its angular displacement is $\lambda/\Lambda$. Here $h_0$ is optical path error, not physical mirror displacement; a reflecting mirror introduces twice its surface displacement in the near-normal limit.

= Geometrical optics
{parent=Optics}
{wiki}

= Geometric optics
{synonym}

Geometrical optics describes propagation by rays and <Snell's law>, neglecting <diffraction> on scales much larger than the <wavelength>.

A <light ray> is a path in an approximation, not a physical thread of zero width. In a transparent, homogeneous, isotropic medium it is straight and perpendicular to a <wavefront>; boundaries redirect it by the <law of reflection> or <Snell's law>. The approximation is useful when the <wavelength> is small compared with relevant apertures, surface curvature scales, and distances over which the <refractive index> changes. For an illustrative <wavelength> of $500\,\mathrm{nm}$ and a $10\,\mathrm{mm}$ aperture, their ratio is $5\times10^{-5}$. That small ratio supports <ray tracing> for image location, but does not eliminate <diffraction> when calculating the fine structure of a focused spot. At a <wave caustic>, <geometrical optics> can fail despite large optical elements. \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/2-71-optics-spring-2014/d558a4fabb2cc2eee8f864d3928b31f6_MIT2_71S14_lec2_notes.pdf][Nick Fang, MIT: Geometrical optics, pages 1-3]{ref}.

The mechanism of <refraction> is a change in <phase velocity>. For transparent isotropic media at the chosen <frequency>, the <refractive index> is
$$
n=\frac{c}{v_{\mathrm{phase}}},\qquad n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2,
$$
where $c$ is the vacuum <speed of light> and the <angles> are measured from the interface <normal vector>. At a stationary planar interface the <frequency> and tangential <wavevector> match across the boundary. Since the <wavenumber> is $k=n\omega/c$, that matching gives <Snell's law>. The transmitted <wavelength> changes while the <frequency> stays fixed. Here $n$ uses <phase velocity>, which need not equal <group velocity>. \a[https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-3-refraction][OpenStax: Refraction]{ref}. \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-974-fundamentals-of-photonics-quantum-electronics-spring-2006/ce6075363fa304e0a61078f7048e96f9_maxwell_eq_iso.pdf][MIT: Classical electromagnetism and optics, phase and group velocities]{ref}.

Choose model air with $n_1=1$ and model glass with $n_2=1.5$, without claiming these are exact values for every sample or <wavelength>. At $\theta_1=45^\circ$, <Snell's law> gives
$$
\theta_2=\arcsin\!\left(\frac{\sin45^\circ}{1.5}\right)\approx28.1255^\circ.
$$
The transmitted <light ray> bends toward the <normal vector>; its <phase velocity> is $c/1.5$, and its <wavelength> is two thirds of the air value. By <reversibility of an optical ray>, incidence from glass at $28.1255^\circ$ returns the outgoing air <angle> $45^\circ$.

Incidence from glass at $45^\circ$ is a different experiment. It would require $\sin\theta_2=1.5\sin45^\circ\approx1.06066$, so there is no real transmitted <angle>. The <critical angle for total internal reflection> is
$$
\theta_c=\arcsin(1/1.5)\approx41.8103^\circ.
$$
Above this <angle>, an ideal lossless glass-air boundary produces <total internal reflection>. The <physical optics> description still permits an <evanescent wave> in the air, although no propagating transmitted <light ray> crosses the boundary. \a[https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-4-total-internal-reflection][OpenStax: Total internal reflection]{ref}. \a[https://web.mit.edu/2.710/Fall06/2.710-wk7-b-ho.pdf][MIT optics: Total internal reflection and evanescent waves, slide 40]{ref}.

{title=Refraction into glass and total internal reflection on incidence from glass; all angles are measured from the normal}

The usefulness of <geometrical optics> is that complicated instruments can be assembled from these local rules. Applying <Snell's law> at successive curved surfaces changes a spreading bundle into a converging one. The <Fermat principle> unifies the construction through stationary <optical path length>; stationarity does not require a global minimum. \a[https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_26.html][Feynman Lectures: The principle of least time, section 26-5]{ref}. Fang's MIT notes explicitly connect this principle to <specular reflection>, <refraction>, and <thin lens> imaging, providing a route from individual paths to complete instruments. \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/2-71-optics-spring-2014/d558a4fabb2cc2eee8f864d3928b31f6_MIT2_71S14_lec2_notes.pdf][Nick Fang, MIT: Geometrical optics]{ref}.

= Wave caustic
{parent=Geometrical optics}

= Caustic
{synonym}

A <wave caustic> is a locus where the projection of a family of rays or wavefronts becomes singular. Several ray paths may coalesce there, and the geometrical approximation can predict divergent intensity. Finite-wavelength <diffraction> replaces that ideal divergence by a resolved wave pattern. The construction applies to acoustic as well as optical waves.

= Light ray
{parent=Geometrical optics}

A path of light propagation in the <geometrical optics> approximation. In a smoothly varying propagation speed, <Fermat principle> makes its travel time stationary.

= Circular light ray in a linear speed profile
{parent=Light ray}
{title2=$(x-x_0)^2+(y+c_0)^2=R^2$}

For speed $c(y)=y+c_0>0$, <Fermat principle> and the <Beltrami identity> yield circular <light rays> whose centres lie on $y=-c_0$. The ray between $(\pm a,0)$ in the upper half-plane is $y=\sqrt{a^2+c_0^2-x^2}-c_0$.

= Eikonal equation for a variable-speed wave
{parent=Geometrical optics}
{title2=$\omega^2=c^2|\mathbf k|^2$}

For the <wave equation> $\varphi_{tt}-\nabla\cdot(c^2\nabla\varphi)=0$ with slow variables and phase $e^{i\theta/\varepsilon}$, define $\omega=-\theta_T$ and $\mathbf k=\nabla\theta$. The leading balance is $\omega^2=c^2|\mathbf k|^2$. Its positive-frequency <dispersion relation> is $\Omega=c|\mathbf k|$, and the <group velocity> is $c\mathbf k/|\mathbf k|$.

= Wave-action transport for a variable-speed wave
{parent=Eikonal equation for a variable-speed wave}
{title2=$(\omega A^2)_T+\nabla\cdot(c^2\mathbf kA^2)=0$}

The first-order imaginary balance of the <wave equation> in <geometrical optics> gives $\partial_T(\omega A^2)+\nabla\cdot(c^2\mathbf k A^2)=0$ for a real leading <amplitude>. The action density $\omega A^2$ travels with the <group velocity> $c^2\mathbf k/\omega$. For a complex <amplitude> replace $A^2$ by $|A|^2$.

= Stationary wave amplitude in a stratified wavespeed
{parent=Wave-action transport for a variable-speed wave}
{title2=$A=\mu/(c\sqrt\ell)$}

For an individual monochromatic component in $c=c(Y)$, conserved $\omega,k$ give $\ell=\sqrt{\omega^2/c^2-k^2}$. With amplitude independent of $X,T$, <wave-action transport for a variable-speed wave> conserves $c^2\ell A^2$ on a positive-$\ell$ branch, hence $A=\mu/(c\sqrt\ell)$. The divergence at $\ell=0$ signals failure of the outer <WKB approximation>.

= Phase compatibility in geometrical optics
{parent=Eikonal equation for a variable-speed wave}
{title2=$k_Y=\ell_X,\quad\mathbf k_T=-\nabla\omega$}

A smooth phase gives $\partial_{X_i}k_j=\partial_{X_j}k_i$ and $\mathbf k_T=-\nabla\omega$. These mixed-<derivative> identities allow the spatially differentiated <dispersion relation> to be rewritten as <Hamiltonian ray equations for a local dispersion relation>. Away from phase singularities they express the curl-free character of a local wavevector.

= Lagrange optical invariant
{c}
{parent=Geometrical optics}
{title2=$H=n(y_1u_2-y_2u_1)$}

For paraxial rays, the <Lagrange optical invariant> is $H=n(y_1u_2-y_2u_1)$. Lossless paraxial ray transfer preserves this quantity: in coordinates $(y,nu)$, free propagation and a <thin lens> act by <matrices> with <determinant> one, which preserve the oriented area of two ray vectors. Consequently a full angular <optical slit> width $\theta$ at an <optical pupil> <diameter> $D$ satisfies $D\theta=A_{\rm cam}p/f_{\rm cam}$ for its image width $p$ and <optical camera> <optical pupil> width $A_{\rm cam}$ in the same meridional plane.

= Etendue
{parent=Geometrical optics}
{title2=$G=n^2A\Omega$}

<Etendue> measures the area and angular extent of a <light> bundle: $G=\iint n^2\cos u\,dA\,d\Omega$, approximately $n^2A\Omega$ for a narrow bundle. Ideal lossless specular <geometrical optics> conserves it, which constrains how <optical instruments> reformat a field.

= Optical axis
{parent=Geometrical optics}
{wiki}

An optical axis is the reference line through the centres of rotational symmetry of an optical system. A folded system can have successive axis segments related by reflection.

= Law of reflection
{parent=Geometrical optics}

The incident ray, reflected ray and surface normal lie in one plane; incidence and reflection angles measured from the normal are equal. For a unit incident direction $\mathbf d$ and unit normal $\mathbf n$, reflection gives $\mathbf d-2(\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf n$.

= Reversibility of an optical ray
{parent=Geometrical optics}

In a stationary reciprocal optical medium, reversing the direction of a ray produces a ray along the same path. <Snell's law> is unchanged when its two media and ray directions are interchanged.

= Thin lens
{parent=Lens (optics)}
{wiki}
{title2=$1/u+1/v=1/f$}

A thin lens is modeled with negligible thickness relative to its object and image distances. For real conjugate distances $u,v>0$, it obeys $1/u+1/v=1/f$, with <focal length> $f$.

The <thin lens> model collapses an <optical lens>'s two refracting surfaces into one plane. Besides negligible thickness, the familiar equation assumes the same surrounding <refractive index> on both sides and the <paraxial approximation>: <light rays> remain near the <optical axis> and make small <angles> with it. Under these assumptions, a converging <thin lens> directs parallel <light rays> to its rear focus. Its curved surfaces have different local <normal vectors>, so <Snell's law> gives different direction changes at different heights. An <optical lens> therefore redirects an entire bundle systematically, rather than bending every <light ray> by the same <angle>. \a[https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_27.html][Feynman Lectures: Refraction at curved surfaces and lenses]{ref}.

Use the real-object positive-distance convention. Put the <thin lens> at $x=0$ with incident <light> traveling from left to right. An object at $x=-u$ has $u>0$. An image at $x=v$ has $v>0$ for a <real image>, where outgoing <light rays> actually intersect, and $v<0$ for a <virtual image>, where their backward extensions intersect. A converging <thin lens> has $f>0$; a diverging <thin lens> has $f<0$. Object and image heights $h_o,h_i$ are signed relative to the <optical axis>. The <transverse magnification> is
$$
\frac1u+\frac1v=\frac1f,\qquad m=\frac{h_i}{h_o}=-\frac vu.
$$
Thus $m<0$ means inverted, $m>0$ means upright, and $|m|$ measures the size ratio. In particular, $u$ is a positive distance even though the object's coordinate is negative. \a[https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/2-4-thin-lenses][OpenStax: Thin-lens equation, sign conventions, and magnification]{ref}.

For $f=0.10\,\mathrm m$ and $u=0.30\,\mathrm m$, direct substitution gives
$$
\frac1v=10-\frac{10}{3}=\frac{20}{3}\,\mathrm m^{-1},\qquad v=0.15\,\mathrm m,\qquad m=-\frac{0.15}{0.30}=-0.5.
$$
The <real image> is $15\,\mathrm{cm}$ beyond the <thin lens>, inverted and half as tall as the object. A point $5\,\mathrm{mm}$ above the <optical axis> maps to a point $2.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ below it. A receiving screen at that image plane collects the focused pattern.

To visualize this result, trace one <light ray> from the object's top parallel to the <optical axis>; it exits toward the rear focus. A second passes through the centre of the ideal <thin lens> without changing direction. A third follows a line through the front focus before reaching the <thin lens> and emerges parallel to the <optical axis>. Their common intersection locates the image of that one object point. The construction can then be repeated for every object point. \a[https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/2-4-thin-lenses][OpenStax: Ray-tracing rules]{ref}.

\Image[lens-image-formation.png]
{title=One converging thin lens: a real inverted image, no finite image at the focus, and an upright virtual image inside the focus}

At $u=f$, the equation gives $1/v=0$: no finite image plane exists. Outgoing <light rays> from each object point are parallel to one another, with different object points producing different directions. For $0<u<f$, they diverge instead. With $f=0.10\,\mathrm m$ and $u=0.05\,\mathrm m$,
$$
\frac1v=10-20=-10\,\mathrm m^{-1},\qquad v=-0.10\,\mathrm m,\qquad m=2.
$$
The <virtual image> is upright, twice as tall, and apparently $10\,\mathrm{cm}$ before the <thin lens>. A screen placed there cannot collect that image from the outgoing bundle, but an observer on the outgoing side can see it. This is the basis of using a converging <optical lens> as a magnifier. The three cases explain why changing object distance can change both image location and image character. \a[https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-6-image-formation-by-lenses][OpenStax College Physics: Focal placement and virtual images]{ref}.

Finally, ideal <ray tracing> predicts points, whereas a finite aperture produces a <diffraction-limited resolution>. Outside the <paraxial approximation>, <optical aberrations> also spoil the common intersection. The <thin lens> equation is valuable because it isolates the leading geometry before those corrections are included. \a[https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_27.html][Feynman Lectures: Aberrations and resolving power, sections 27-6 and 27-7]{ref}.

= Bessel lens displacement method
{c}
{parent=Thin lens}

For fixed object-screen separation $L>4f$, a converging <thin lens> focuses at two positions separated by $d=\sqrt{L^2-4Lf}$. Thus $f=(L^2-d^2)/(4L)$. The method finds <focal length> without needing to locate a thin lens's optical center precisely.

= Lensmaker's equation
{parent=Thin lens}

For a thin <optical lens> in air, surface radii $R_a,R_b$ and <refractive index> $n$ give $1/f=(n-1)(1/R_a-1/R_b)$. The radii carry the usual signed curvature convention.

= Minimum deviation

Astronomical seeing is the atmospheric blurring of a stellar image, conventionally expressed as the angular full width at half maximum of its long-exposure <point spread function>, usually in arcseconds. = Fried parameter
{c}

High-contrast imaging seeks faint objects near a bright source. Residual <point spread functions> and <speckle patterns> can dominate <photon shot noise>; <angular differential imaging> and <simultaneous spectral differential imaging> use source motion or spectral diversity to distinguish them.

A reflecting telescope forms an image using curved mirrors. Its primary and secondary shapes, spacing and aperture stop determine its <optical aberrations>, access to the focus, and obstruction.

= Gregorian telescope
{c}
{parent=Reflecting telescope}
{wiki}

A Gregorian telescope has a concave parabolic primary and a concave ellipsoidal secondary beyond the primary focus. Light crosses the intermediate focus, reaches the secondary, and returns through the primary to the final focus. The ellipsoid’s two foci are the intermediate and final focus.

= Cassegrain reflector
{c}
{parent=Reflecting telescope}
{wiki}

A Cassegrain arrangement places a secondary before the primary focus and returns light through a hole in the primary to a focus behind it. In the classical design the primary is parabolic and the secondary convex hyperbolic. The compact arrangement has a long effective <focal length>; its classical implementation retains off-axis <coma>.

= Ritchey–Chrétien telescope
{c}
{parent=Cassegrain reflector}

= Ritchey–Chrétien reflector
{c}
{synonym}

A Ritchey–Chrétien telescope has hyperbolic primary and secondary mirrors chosen to cancel third-order <spherical aberration> and <coma>. It retains <astigmatism> and <field curvature>; wide-field instruments may require additional correction.

= Classical Cassegrain reflector
{parent=Cassegrain reflector}

A classical Cassegrain reflector uses a concave parabolic primary and a convex hyperbolic secondary before the prime focus. The secondary returns the converging beam through a hole in the primary. It corrects on-axis <spherical aberration> but retains off-axis <coma>.

= Newtonian telescope
{c}
{parent=Reflecting telescope}
{wiki}

= Newtonian reflector
{c}
{synonym}

A Newtonian telescope uses a concave parabolic primary and a flat diagonal secondary to send the converging beam to a side focus. The primary avoids on-axis <spherical aberration>, but has off-axis <coma>.

= Diffraction limit of a telescope
{parent=Optical telescope}

The angular <diffraction-limited resolution> of a telescope scales as $\lambda/D$, with <wavelength> $\lambda$ and illuminated diameter $D$. Larger diameter resolves smaller angular structure, while longer wavelength makes atmospheric phase correction easier.

= Collimator
{parent=Optical instrument}
{wiki}

A collimator turns light from a source near its focus into a nearly parallel beam. A slit of width $x$ produces an angular width approximately $x/f$, where $f$ is the <focal length>.

= Spectrometer
{parent=Optical instrument}
{wiki}

= Fourier transform spectroscopy
{c}
{parent=Spectrometer}
{wiki}

Fourier transform spectroscopy measures an interferogram as a function of <optical path length> difference. Its <Fourier transform> recovers the spectrum. An imaging implementation obtains an interferogram at every detector pixel, but a scanned cube is not simultaneous at all wavelengths.

= Spectrograph
{parent=Spectrometer}

A spectrograph records the <optical spectrum> dispersed by an instrument. Its <spectral resolving power> describes which nearby wavelengths it separates.

= Spectral resolution element
{parent=Spectrograph}

= SRE
{c}
{synonym}

A <spectral resolution element> is a spectral interval at the adopted instrumental resolution. At <spectral resolving power> $R$, its width is $\Delta\lambda=\lambda/R$. The number of <photodetector> <detector pixels> sampling it is distinct from $R$.

= Linear spectral dispersion
{parent=Spectrograph}
{title2=$q=dx/d\lambda$}

<Linear spectral dispersion> is the derivative of the <photodetector> coordinate in the dispersion direction with respect to <wavelength>. Near the <optical axis>, $q=f_{\rm cam}\,d\beta/d\lambda$.

= Integral field spectrograph
{parent=Spectrograph}

An integral field spectrograph records a spectrum for each of many spatial elements across a two-dimensional field, producing a <spectral data cube>. Field reformatters include <lenslet arrays>, <optical fibers> and <image slicers>.

= Integral-field spectrograph volume scaling
{parent=Integral field spectrograph}

With fixed <spectral resolving power>, <wavelength> interval, <diffraction grating> geometry, <photodetector> sampling and <collimator> <focal ratio>, a beam <diameter> scales as the <spaxel> width $\theta$, but the <optical camera> <focal length> stays fixed. A simple on-axis beam-envelope model therefore gives <collimator> and disperser volumes proportional to $\theta^3$ and <optical camera> volume proportional to $\theta^2$. With a fixed number of <spaxels> per <photodetector>, replication multiplies these volumes by $M\propto\theta^{-2}$. A fixed <photodetector> field adds transverse clearances and changes the small-$\theta$ limit; these exponents are not universal mechanical-volume laws.

= Spaxel
{parent=Integral field spectrograph}

A <spaxel> is one spatial sampling element in a <spectral data cube>. For a square field of angular side $\beta$ sampled at angular side $\theta$, the number of <spaxels> is $(\beta/\theta)^2$, before edge rounding.

= Image slicer
{parent=Integral field spectrograph}

An image slicer divides a two-dimensional image into narrow strips and optically rearranges them into a long pseudo-slit. A <spectrograph> then disperses each strip while preserving its position along the slit.

= Spectral data cube
{parent=Integral field spectrograph}
{title2=$I(x,y,\lambda)$}

A spectral data cube stores intensity $I(x,y,\lambda)$ with two spatial coordinates and one spectral coordinate. The third coordinate is <wavelength>, not an additional spatial dimension.

= Spectral resolving power
{parent=Spectrograph}
{title2=$R=\lambda/\Delta\lambda$}

Spectral resolving power is $R=\lambda/\Delta\lambda$, where $\Delta\lambda$ is the wavelength separation just resolved under the specified instrumental and source conditions.

= Cross-dispersed echelle spectrograph
{parent=Spectrograph}

A cross-dispersed echelle spectrograph uses an <echelle grating> for strong dispersion and a <cross-disperser> in the perpendicular direction to separate overlapping <diffraction orders>. It records many orders simultaneously in an echellogram.

= Cross-disperser
{parent=Cross-dispersed echelle spectrograph}

A cross-disperser is a second dispersing element, often an <optical prism> or <diffraction grating>, oriented to separate <optical spectra> perpendicular to their principal dispersion direction.

= Echellogram
{parent=Cross-dispersed echelle spectrograph}

An echellogram is the two-dimensional detector pattern of separated <diffraction orders> from a <cross-dispersed echelle spectrograph>.

= Multi-object spectrograph
{parent=Spectrograph}

A multi-object spectrograph records <optical spectra> of many selected objects simultaneously. The main designs are <multi-slit spectrographs> and <fiber-fed spectrographs>. The https://www.eso.org/sci/facilities/paranal/instruments/multiobject.html[ESO instrumentation overview] describes both arrangements.

= Multi-slit spectrograph
{parent=Multi-object spectrograph}

A multi-slit spectrograph puts separate slits at target positions in the focal plane. Each slit transmits the target plus nearby sky; dispersion places its spatially resolved <optical spectrum> on the detector.

= Fiber-fed spectrograph
{parent=Multi-object spectrograph}

A fiber-fed spectrograph places <optical fibers> on selected targets and reformats their output into a common entrance slit. Dedicated sky fibers sample <sky brightness>. Fiber coupling, transmission, and output beam spreading affect throughput and resolution.

= Optical fiber
{parent=Optical instrument}
{wiki}

= Optical fibre
{synonym}

= Focal-ratio degradation
{parent=Optical fiber}

Focal-ratio degradation is the broadening of the output angular cone of an <optical fiber> relative to its input cone. It can make a spectrograph collimator lose transmitted light or require larger optics. Stress, bends, and mode mixing in the fiber can contribute.

= Adaptive optics
{parent=Optical instrument}
{wiki}

Adaptive optics measures changing <wavefront errors> and corrects them using a <deformable mirror>. A <wavefront sensor> supplies measurements to a fast feedback controller; a <guide star> provides the reference. It improves <astronomical seeing> toward <diffraction-limited resolution>.

= Deformable mirror
{parent=Adaptive optics}
{wiki}

A deformable mirror changes its optical surface using an array of actuators. For reflection near normal incidence, surface displacement changes <optical path length> by twice that displacement.

= Deformable-mirror Fourier-mode counting
{parent=Deformable mirror}

For actuator pitch $D/N$, full-cycle <spatial frequency> components are limited to $N/2$ by sampling. Integer lattice counting in a circular <frequency> disk gives approximately $\pi N^2/4$ signed points; conjugate pairing halves the number of pairs, and two real <wave phase> quadratures per pair restore approximately $\pi N^2/4$ real coefficients. Counting only positive-quadrant <frequency> representatives gives $\pi N^2/16$, but it omits other orientations or quadratures unless a restricted convention is specified. Area approximations have boundary corrections and do not give exact integer ranks. A square sampling cutoff and a circular illuminated actuator footprint are distinct geometric restrictions.

= Deformable-mirror control radius
{parent=Deformable mirror}
{title2=$\theta_{\max}=N\lambda/(2D)$}

With $N$ actuators across diameter $D$, the projected pitch is approximately $D/N$. The <Nyquist spatial frequency> is $N/(2D)$, so the largest correctable angular speckle displacement along an actuator row is $N\lambda/(2D)$. A square actuator array gives a square ideal spatial-frequency region. See the https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0510597[Bordé–Traub wavefront-control derivation].

= Wavefront sensor
{parent=Adaptive optics}
{wiki}

A wavefront sensor measures information from which a <wavefront error> can be reconstructed. For example, a <Shack–Hartmann wavefront sensor> measures local wavefront slopes using lenslets.

= Guide star
{disambiguate=adaptive optics}
{parent=Adaptive optics}
{wiki}

= Guide star
{synonym}

A guide star provides photons for a <wavefront sensor>. Its brightness and angular separation from the target limit <adaptive optics> sky coverage.

= Laser guide star
{parent=Guide star (adaptive optics)}
{wiki}

A laser guide star is an artificial atmospheric beacon for <adaptive optics>. It improves sky coverage but samples a finite-distance cone rather than the full incoming stellar beam. Conventional systems still require a natural reference for absolute image motion.

= Adaptive-optics sky coverage
{parent=Adaptive optics}

Adaptive-optics sky coverage is the fraction of targets for which a sufficiently bright nearby <guide star> allows useful correction. <Anisoplanatism> limits the offset, and photon statistics limit the sensing speed. A <laser guide star> improves coverage but does not remove all low-order and finite-distance limitations.

= Photodetector
{parent=Optical instrument}
{wiki}

= Detector bias frame
{parent=Photodetector}

= Bias image
{synonym}

A <photodetector> bias frame is an exposure of effectively zero integration <time> used to estimate the electronic offset and <read noise>. For independent identical bias reads, the <variance> of their difference is twice the single-read <variance>; subtracting an estimated mean bias removes the offset but adds the uncertainty of that estimate.

= Flat-field correction
{parent=Photodetector}
{wiki}

= Flatfield correction
{synonym}

<Flat-field correction> compensates stable multiplicative variations in <photodetector> response using calibrated illumination. Identically illuminated exposure ratios cancel the stable response in their mean, but their <photon shot noise> remains signal dependent.

= Analogue-to-digital unit
{parent=Photodetector}

= ADU
{c}
{synonym}

= Data number
{synonym}

An analogue-to-digital unit is a unit of a digitized <photodetector> output after electronic conversion. Its relation to collected <electrons> is set by the <detector conversion gain>; an additive electronic offset must be removed before interpreting a count as a <light> signal.

= Detector conversion gain
{parent=Photodetector}
{title2=$g$}

= Detector gain
{synonym}

<Detector conversion gain> is the number of collected <electrons> per <analogue-to-digital unit>. With independent <Poisson distribution> photoelectron counts, the shot-noise <variance> in count units is the mean count divided by the gain. Detected-photon and <electron> conventions agree only when each detected <photon> contributes one <electron>.

= Gain estimation from flat-field ratios
{parent=Detector conversion gain}

Let independent exposure sums $A,B$ have mean $N$ <ADU> at each <detector pixel>, with pure <Poisson distribution> noise and fixed gain $g$. The <delta method> gives $A/B\simeq1+(A-N)/N-(B-N)/N$, so $\operatorname{Var}(A/B)\simeq2/(gN)$ and $g\simeq2/(NE^2)$. For nonuniform <detector pixel> means $N_i$, an unweighted spatial ratio <variance> is instead $(2/g)\langle1/N_i\rangle$. This high-count approximation requires linear unsaturated data, bias subtraction and negligible <read noise>.

= Bias of pooled flat-field ratio gain
{parent=Gain estimation from flat-field ratios}
{title2=$g_{\rm naive}/g=1/(\langle N\rangle\langle1/N\rangle)$}

For stable positive <detector pixel> means $N_i$ and uniform <detector conversion gain> $g$, high-count independent <Poisson distribution> noise gives $\operatorname{Var}(A_i/B_i)\simeq2/(gN_i)$. Thus substituting a global mean in the equal-signal formula yields $g_{\rm naive}=g/[\langle N_i\rangle\langle1/N_i\rangle]\le g$, by the <Jensen inequality>. Equality holds for uniform means. The <flat-field correction> pattern cancels from the ratio mean but not from the signal-dependent noise. Local signal bins, or a <variance> fit after normalization of differences by the square root of their local mean, avoid this leading bias.

= Optical detector pixel
{parent=Photodetector}

= Detector pixel
{synonym}

An optical <photodetector> <detector pixel> is a spatially sampled photosensitive element. Counts from distinct <detector pixels> have independent <photon shot noise> in the ideal model, but charge transfer, electrical coupling and resampling can produce <covariance>.

= Photodiode
{parent=Photodetector}
{wiki}

A photodiode is a <semiconductor> diode that converts absorbed photons into electrical carriers. A reverse-biased detector separates the carriers and collects charge; its <quantum efficiency> and <dark current> determine sensitivity.

= Dark current
{disambiguate=physics}
{parent=Photodetector}
{wiki}

= Dark current
{synonym}

Dark current is charge produced in a detector without illumination, often by thermally excited carriers. Cooling reduces it, although cooling requirements also depend on the wavelength range and thermal background.

= Correlated double sampling
{parent=Photodetector}
{wiki}

Correlated double sampling differences a signal reading and its associated reset or baseline reading. It suppresses correlated offsets and reset noise, while the noise of the two measurements must still be propagated.

= Hybrid infrared detector
{parent=Photodetector}

A hybrid infrared detector bonds a photosensitive <semiconductor> array to a separate silicon readout circuit. Each pixel has its own electrical connection; charge need not be transferred across the array as in a <charge-coupled device>.

= Detector reference pixel
{parent=Hybrid infrared detector}

A detector reference pixel has no illuminated photosensitive connection and samples readout offsets. Reference-pixel subtraction can suppress common electronic drifts but does not measure the sky background or all pixel-dependent errors.

= Nondestructive detector readout
{parent=Hybrid infrared detector}

A nondestructive read samples a pixel’s accumulated signal without resetting it. Repeated samples permit <Fowler sampling> and <up-the-ramp sampling>, and can help identify saturation and cosmic-ray charge steps.

= Up-the-ramp sampling
{parent=Nondestructive detector readout}

Up-the-ramp sampling measures accumulated charge repeatedly through an exposure and estimates flux from its slope. Cosmic-ray steps, saturation and nonlinearity must be identified or modelled.

= Fowler sampling
{c}
{parent=Nondestructive detector readout}

Fowler sampling averages several nondestructive reads near the start and end of an integration and differences the two averages. For independent equal read noise, averaging lowers the read-noise variance; correlated noise limits the improvement.

= H2RG detector
{c}
{parent=Hybrid infrared detector}

= HAWAII-2RG
{c}
{synonym}

The HAWAII-2RG architecture has a $2048\times2048$ grid with an 18-micrometre pitch. For infrared work its readout circuit is normally bonded to a <mercury cadmium telluride> absorber. Reference pixels track electronic offsets and an independently addressed guide window can be read alongside the science array.

= Background subtraction
{parent=Photodetector}

Background subtraction estimates a source contribution by removing a sky or detector-background measurement. An independent background estimate adds <photon shot noise>; a mismatched background adds <bias of an estimator>.

= Systematic-error signal-to-noise ceiling
{parent=Background subtraction}
{title2=$Z_\infty$}

Let $X\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(Q+B)$ and $Y\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(fB)$ be independent. Then $X-Y$ has source-estimation bias $(1-f)B$ and variance $Q+(1+f)B$. Its accuracy ratio $Q/\sqrt{\mathbb E[(X-Y-Q)^2]}$ tends to $R_Q/(|1-f|R_B)$ when $Q=R_QT$, $B=R_BT$ and $T$ grows. This uses <mean squared error>, not variance alone, and assumes a fixed uncorrected mismatch.

= Read noise
{parent=Photodetector}

Read noise is uncertainty added by measuring detector charge or voltage. It is often expressed as an equivalent number of input electrons per pixel per read.

= Photon shot noise
{parent=Photodetector}

Independent photon arrivals have a <Poisson distribution>: a mean count $\mu$ has variance $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sqrt\mu$. Subtraction of independent counting measurements adds their variances.

= Quantum efficiency
{parent=Photodetector}
{title2=$\eta$}
{wiki}

Quantum efficiency is the mean fraction of incident photons producing collected signal carriers. Noise-equivalent quantum efficiency can be lower when amplification adds fluctuations; this does not imply a corresponding loss of actual photon absorption.

= Charge-coupled device
{parent=Photodetector}
{wiki}

= CCD
{c}
{synonym}

A charge-coupled device stores photoelectrons in potential wells and transfers the charge through clocked stages to an output amplifier. The transfer differs from independently addressed pixels in a <hybrid infrared detector>.

= Electron-multiplying CCD
{parent=Charge-coupled device}
{title2=$g$}

= EMCCD
{c}
{synonym}

An electron-multiplying CCD adds a high-field multiplication register before the output amplifier. <Impact ionization> generates a large mean gain, reducing input-referred <read noise> but adding <multiplication excess noise>.

= Multiplication excess noise
{parent=Electron-multiplying CCD}
{title2=$F$}

Random multiplication increases the noise relative to deterministic gain. If the output from one input electron has mean $g$ and variance $g^2$, a <Poisson distribution> of input electrons with mean $\mu$ produces output variance $2g^2\mu$, by the <law of total variance>. The high-gain analog-mode excess-noise factor is $\sqrt2$.

= Impact ionization
{parent=Electron-multiplying CCD}
{wiki}

An energetic charge carrier creates an additional electron–hole pair. In a multiplication register this is stochastic, so the output charge fluctuates even when the input electron number is fixed.

= Signal-to-noise ratio in photon counting
{parent=Photodetector}

For source counts $S$ and independent background counts $B$ with <Poisson distributions>, the ideal counting <standard deviation> is $\sqrt{S+B}$, so the signal-to-noise ratio is $S/\sqrt{S+B}$. Detector noise and uncertainty in the estimated background add further variance. A larger sky aperture raises $B$, while better throughput raises $S$; both affect the faintness limit of a <spectrograph>.
