= Wave equation
{wiki}
{title2=$u_{tt}=c^2\Delta u$}

The wave equation is a second-order <linear partial differential equation> describing <waves> with <finite propagation speed>. In one spatial dimension its homogeneous, constant-coefficient form is
$$
u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx},\qquad c>0.
$$
Here $x$ is position, $t$ is time, $u(x,t)$ is the disturbance, and subscripts denote <partial derivatives>. For a <wave equation on a string>, $u$ is transverse displacement; in <linear acoustics>, it can be a pressure perturbation. In several spatial dimensions, $u_{xx}$ becomes the <Laplacian> $\Delta u$. The same mathematical structure connects vibrating strings and <acoustic waves>, although their physical assumptions and <wave speeds> differ. \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/2-062j-wave-propagation-spring-2017/2f9f71f7f386b36c9c42f48c03aecfe6_MIT2_062J_S17_Chap1.pdf][MIT, Wave Propagation, Chapter 1]{ref}

For the whole-line <Cauchy problem>, prescribe both displacement $u(x,0)=f(x)$ and <velocity> $u_t(x,0)=g(x)$. A <classical solution> has continuous <partial derivatives> through order two; $f\in C^2(\mathbb R)$ and $g\in C^1(\mathbb R)$ suffice for <D'Alembert's formula> to produce one. A finite string additionally needs <boundary conditions>. These statements concern the unforced model with constant $c$; <damping>, spatially varying coefficients and nonlinear terms change the solution formula.

A <travelling wave> $F(x-ct)$ carries its entire profile to the right at <wave speed> $c$; $G(x+ct)$ travels left. Their sum is again a solution by the <superposition principle>. A <standing wave> instead has a fixed <mode shape> whose size and sign oscillate: in $\sin(kx)\cos(ckt)$, each <node of a standing wave> stays stationary. The distinction is especially clear in the pulse and string diagrams below. For this ideal model, the <dispersion relation> is $\omega^2=c^2k^2$: each propagation branch is <nondispersive>, so a localized profile translates without spreading. This makes the equation a useful reference against which to understand <wave dispersion> and <damping>.

The appealing combination is local interaction with global predictability: neighbouring displacements determine <acceleration> through $u_{xx}$, yet <D'Alembert's formula> explicitly identifies exactly which initial locations can influence a later observation. <Finite propagation speed> turns that observation into a <domain of dependence>, rather than requiring knowledge of the entire initial string.

= Regular time-harmonic spherical wave
{parent=Wave equation}
{title2=$u(r,t)=A\sin(\omega t)\,\sin(\omega r/c)/(\omega r/c)$}

For a <spherically symmetric function>, $v=ru$ reduces the radial <wave equation> to $v_{tt}=c^2v_{rr}$. A time-harmonic separated solution has radial numerator $C\sin(kr)+D\cos(kr)$, with $k=\omega/c$. Finiteness at the origin excludes $D$, and the prescribed finite origin value fixes $C$. This is a standing combination of incoming and outgoing waves. A purely outgoing point-source wave instead has a $1/r$ singularity and must be specified by its source strength, not by a finite value at the origin.

= Retarded acoustic Green function
{parent=Wave equation}
{title2=$G_R(r,t)=\delta(t-r/c_0)/(4\pi c_0^2r)$}

This is the causal three-dimensional fundamental solution of $\partial_t^2-c_0^2\nabla^2$. It concentrates on the outgoing sound cone and gives the retarded convolution of acoustic sources.

= All-time boundedness of a wave equation with a reaction term
{parent=Wave equation}
{title2=$\alpha<\lambda_1$}

For $u_{tt}=\Delta u+\alpha u$ with a homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary condition>, let $\lambda_1>0$ be the first <Dirichlet Laplacian eigenvalue>. On a bounded one-dimensional interval, finite-energy displacement remains bounded for all time and all initial data exactly when $\alpha<\lambda_1$. The conserved energy $\tfrac12(\|u_t\|_2^2+\|\nabla u\|_2^2-\alpha\|u\|_2^2)$ controls the gradient and, by the <Poincare inequality> and one-dimensional integration, the maximum <norm>. At equality a mode can grow linearly; above it a mode can grow exponentially. Bounded oscillations need not have a long-time limit.

= Vector field method for wave equations
{parent=Wave equation}

The vector field method commutes a <wave equation> with spacetime symmetry generators, estimates the resulting <commuted wave energies>, and converts weighted <L2 norms> to pointwise decay using a <Klainerman-Sobolev inequality>. The <commutation vector fields for the wave equation> include translations, rotations, <Lorentz boost vector fields> and the <scaling vector field>. It is useful for <almost global existence for wave equations> and for small-data global existence under the <classical null condition for wave equations>.

= Commuted wave energy
{parent=Vector field method for wave equations}
{title2=$A_m(t)=\sum_{|I|\leq m}\|\partial Z^Iu(t)\|_2$}

A commuted wave energy controls derivatives of a solution after applying the <commutation vector fields for the wave equation>. A convenient equivalent norm is $A_m(t)=\sum_{|I|\leq m}\|\partial Z^Iu(t)\|_2$. The <wave energy estimate> controls its growth through commuted sources, while the <Klainerman-Sobolev inequality> gives pointwise decay for low-order derivatives.

= Klainerman-Sobolev inequality
{parent=Vector field method for wave equations}
{c}
{title2=$|u|\lesssim\frac{\sum_{|I|\leq2}\|Z^Iu\|_2}{(1+t+r)(1+|t-r|)^{1/2}}$}

In three spatial dimensions, with $Z$ the eleven translations, spatial rotations, boosts and scaling <commutation vector fields for the wave equation>, a sufficiently decaying <smooth function> satisfies $|u(t,x)|\leq C(1+t+|x|)^{-1}(1+|t-|x||)^{-1/2}\sum_{|I|\leq2}\|Z^Iu(t)\|_2$. This weighted <Sobolev inequality> converts control of <commuted wave energy> into decay. It holds independently of any <wave equation> satisfied by $u$.

= Commutation vector field for the wave equation
{parent=Vector field method for wave equations}

= Commutation vector fields for the wave equation
{synonym}

A first-order <vector field> $Z$ is a commutation field for the flat <wave equation> when $[\Box,Z]$ is zero or a controlled multiple of $\Box$. Translations, spatial rotations and <Lorentz boost vector fields> commute with $\Box$; the <scaling vector field> satisfies $[\Box,S]=2\Box$. Repeated commutation preserves the differential order of a <semilinear wave equation>.

= Scaling vector field
{parent=Commutation vector field for the wave equation}
{title2=$S=t\partial_t+x\cdot\nabla$}

The <vector field> $S=t\partial_t+x\cdot\nabla$ generates simultaneous spacetime dilation. For the flat <d'Alembert operator>, $[\Box,S]=2\Box$. Consequently it commutes with the homogeneous <wave equation> at the level of its solution set, although its operator <commutator> is nonzero.

= Lorentz boost vector field
{parent=Commutation vector field for the wave equation}
{c}
{title2=$L_i=t\partial_i+x_i\partial_t$}

For <Minkowski spacetime> with unit light speed, $L_i=t\partial_i+x_i\partial_t$ generates a <Lorentz boost>. It commutes with the <d'Alembert operator> and controls derivatives transverse to time slices in the <vector field method for wave equations>.

= Spatial rotation vector field
{parent=Commutation vector field for the wave equation}
{title2=$\Omega_{ij}=x_i\partial_j-x_j\partial_i$}

The <vector field> $\Omega_{ij}=x_i\partial_j-x_j\partial_i$ is an infinitesimal spatial rotation. It commutes with the flat <d'Alembert operator> and supplies angular derivatives in the <Klainerman-Sobolev inequality>.

= Spacetime translation vector field
{parent=Commutation vector field for the wave equation}
{title2=$\partial_\mu$}

The <vector fields> $\partial_t$ and $\partial_i$ generate time and space translations and commute with the flat <d'Alembert operator>. They form the unweighted part of the <commutation vector fields for the wave equation>.

= Wave energy
{parent=Wave equation}
{title2=$E_{\mathrm{lin}}=\tfrac12\int(|u_t|^2+|\nabla u|^2)\,dx$}

For a real scalar <wave equation>, the kinetic and gradient <energy> is $E_{\mathrm{lin}}(t)=\frac12\int(|u_t|^2+|\nabla u|^2)\,dx$. A potential term is added for a <semilinear wave equation> $u_{tt}-\Delta u+V^{\prime}(u)=0$, giving the conserved <wave energy> $E=E_{\mathrm{lin}}+\int V(u)\,dx$.

= Reflected-step solution of the wave equation
{parent=Wave equation}
{title2=$y(x,t)$}

For $y_{tt}=c^2y_{xx}$ on $0<x<L$, initially at rest with zero displacement, apply $y(0,t)=a$ for $t>0$ and keep $y(L,t)=0$. A <geometric series> in the <Laplace transform> gives
$$
y(x,t)=a\sum_{m\geq0}\left[H\!\left(t-\frac{2mL+x}{c}\right)-H\!\left(t-\frac{(2m+2)L-x}{c}\right)\right].
$$
Here $H$ is the <Heaviside step function>, $L,c>0$, and only finitely many terms contribute at any finite time. The second step in each pair is a sign-reversed reflection from the fixed endpoint. The formula solves the <wave equation> classically away from the fronts and distributionally across them; the midpoint is a square wave of period $2L/c$.

= Wavespeed
{synonym}

The wave speed $c$ is the propagation speed in a wave equation such as $u_{tt}=c^2\nabla^2u$.

= Slowness
{parent=Wave speed}
{title2=$s=1/v$}

<Slowness> is the reciprocal of <phase velocity>, measured in time per distance. A <plane wave> with <wavevector> $\mathbf k$ and <angular frequency> $\omega$ has vector <slowness> $\mathbf p=\mathbf k/\omega$. Its direction is the <wavefront> normal, which need not be the <group velocity> direction in an <anisotropic> medium.

= Kirchhoff formula
{c}
{parent=Wave equation}

In three spatial dimensions, the solution with initial position $u_0$ and velocity $u_1$ is
$$
u(t,x)=\partial_t\bigl(tM_tu_0(x)\bigr)+tM_tu_1(x),
$$
where $M_t$ is the spherical mean.

= Method of descent for the wave equation
{parent=Kirchhoff formula}

Extend lower-dimensional data independently of an extra coordinate and apply the higher-dimensional <wave equation> solution. Projecting a three-dimensional sphere to the planar disk yields the <kernel> $[2\pi\sqrt{t^2-r^2}]^{-1}$ for the two-dimensional solution with zero initial displacement. This <method of descent for the wave equation> explains why three-dimensional wave propagation can depend on a sphere while two-dimensional propagation depends on its interior.

= Strong Huygens principle
{c}
{parent=Kirchhoff formula}

The strong Huygens principle says that the three-dimensional wave solution at $(t,x)$ depends only on initial data on the sphere $|y-x|=|t|$. A compactly supported disturbance therefore leaves no tail in the interior of its light cone.

= Finite propagation speed
{parent=Wave equation}

Finite propagation speed means that data outside the backward light cone of a spacetime point cannot affect the solution at that point.

= Weak Huygens principle
{parent=Finite propagation speed}

The <weak Huygens principle> is the domain-of-dependence statement that initial data outside a backward light cone cannot influence its tip. It permits an interior tail and is weaker than the <strong Huygens principle>. For $u_{tt}-\Delta u+V(x)u=0$ with $V\ge0$, integrating the local <energy> over shrinking balls proves this statement: the boundary contribution is $u_t\partial_\nu u-\tfrac12(u_t^2+|\nabla u|^2+Vu^2)\le0$.

= Shrinking cone energy argument
{parent=Finite propagation speed}

For the homogeneous speed-one <wave equation>, integrate its <wave energy> density over $B(x_*,T-t)$. The moving boundary contributes $-e$, while the usual flux contributes $u_t\partial_nu$. Their sum is $-\frac12(u_t-\partial_nu)^2-\frac12|\nabla_{\mathrm{tan}}u|^2\leq0$. Zero <Cauchy data> in the initial ball therefore force zero solution in its backward <light cone>.

= Characteristic diamond for speed one minus y squared
{title2=$|x|+|\operatorname{artanh}y|<S$}
{parent=Finite propagation speed}

For <Cauchy data> prescribed on $x=0$ over $|y|<a<1$, put $S=\operatorname{artanh}a$. The maximal region determined solely by those data is
$$
\left\{(x,y):|x|+|\operatorname{artanh}y|<S\right\}.
$$
In the <travel-time coordinate for a one-dimensional variable-speed wave equation> $(x,s)$ this is the ordinary characteristic diamond $|x|+|s|<S$, as follows from <finite propagation speed>.

= Radiation field
{parent=Wave equation}

A radiation field is the rescaled limit of a wave along null infinity. For a three-dimensional wave, the outgoing field is the limit of $ru(t,r\omega)$ with $t-r$ fixed, while the incoming field fixes $t+r$.

= Scattering map
{parent=Radiation field}

A scattering map sends incoming asymptotic data to outgoing asymptotic data for the same solution.

The one-dimensional wave equation has left-moving and right-moving travelling-wave solutions. Their superposition is described by the <D'Alembert formula> and remains valid distributionally for <locally integrable functions>[locally integrable profiles].

= Semilinear wave equation
{parent=Wave equation}
{wiki}

A semilinear wave equation has the form
$$
u_{tt}-\Delta u=F(u,\nabla u),
$$
so its highest-order part is linear while the forcing may depend nonlinearly on the solution and its lower derivatives.

= Wave map
{parent=Semilinear wave equation}
{title2=$\Box\phi=\phi(|\phi_t|^2-|\nabla\phi|^2)$}

A wave map is a <harmonic map> with Lorentzian domain: a critical point of the action obtained by contracting the pullback target metric with the domain <Lorentzian metric>. For target <sphere> $S^2$ in Euclidean space and $\Box=-\partial_t^2+\Delta$, its extrinsic equation is $\Box\phi=\phi(|\phi_t|^2-|\nabla\phi|^2)$, subject to $|\phi|=1$. The derivative contractions are <null forms for wave equations>. <Wave map Cauchy data> must include a tangent initial velocity.

= Small data global regularity for wave maps
{parent=Wave map}

Smooth compatible data sufficiently small in high weighted <Sobolev norms> relative to a constant map produce global smooth <wave maps> in three and four spatial dimensions. In four dimensions, derivative decay $(1+t)^{-3/2}$ is time-integrable and closes <commuted wave energy> estimates. In three dimensions the weaker $(1+t)^{-1}$ decay requires the cancellation of <null forms for wave equations>, exploited by the <vector field method for wave equations>. These classical localized-data statements do not assert global regularity from small supercritical energy alone.

= Global regularity for one-dimensional wave maps
{parent=Wave map}

For smooth localized compatible <wave map Cauchy data> and compact Riemannian target, one-dimensional <wave maps> stay smooth globally. In null coordinates, the equation is $D_v\phi_u=D_u\phi_v=0$. Compatibility of the target metric with the <covariant derivative> makes $|\phi_u|^2$ independent of $v$ and $|\phi_v|^2$ independent of $u$. These transported derivative bounds and higher <energy estimates> prevent finite-time breakdown.

= Stationary wave map
{parent=Wave map}
{title2=$\Delta Q+|\nabla Q|^2Q=0$}

A time-independent <wave map> is a <harmonic map> of its spatial domain into the target. For target $S^2$, its equation is $\Delta Q+|\nabla Q|^2Q=0$. In two dimensions inverse <stereographic projection> gives a smooth nonconstant example of finite <wave map energy> $4\pi$.

= Wave map energy
{parent=Wave map}
{title2=$E=\tfrac12\int(|\phi_t|^2+|\nabla\phi|^2)\,dx$}

The conserved <energy> of a <wave map> from Minkowski spacetime to a Riemannian target is $E=\frac12\int(|\phi_t|^2+|\nabla\phi|^2)\,dx$, with norms measured in the target metric. Contraction of the <wave map> equation with the velocity and <integration by parts> gives conservation.

= Wave map energy and criticality
{parent=Wave map energy}
{title2=$E(\phi_\lambda)=\lambda^{n-2}E(\phi)$}

Under $\phi_\lambda(t,x)=\phi(t/\lambda,x/\lambda)$, the conserved <wave map energy> scales as $\lambda^{n-2}E$. Energy is subcritical for $n=1$, critical for $n=2$, and supercritical for $n\geq3$. The critical derivative index of the <homogeneous Sobolev space> is $s_c=n/2$. Small energy alone is not an appropriate general small-data regularity hypothesis in supercritical dimensions.

= Wave map Cauchy data
{parent=Wave map}

Initial position $\phi_0$ for a <wave map> takes values in the target, and initial velocity $\phi_1$ belongs to its <tangent space> at $\phi_0(x)$. For $S^2$ this means $|\phi_0|=1$ and $\phi_0\cdot\phi_1=0$. Localized data agree with a constant map and zero velocity outside a compact set; the map itself need not be a compactly supported ambient function.

= Null form for wave equations
{parent=Semilinear wave equation}
{title2=$Q_0(f,g)=-f_tg_t+\nabla f\cdot\nabla g$}

= Null forms for wave equations
{synonym}

A quadratic derivative expression is a null form when its symbol vanishes on parallel <null covectors>. The Lorentz contraction $Q_0(f,g)=-f_tg_t+\nabla f\cdot\nabla g$ is the basic example. Its cancellation suppresses interactions of derivatives pointing along the same light ray. It is the algebraic structure behind the <classical null condition for wave equations>.

= Classical null condition for wave equations
{parent=Null form for wave equations}
{title2=$A^{\alpha\beta}\ell_\alpha\ell_\beta=0\quad\text{for null }\ell$}

For a quadratic derivative source $A^{\alpha\beta}\partial_\alpha u\partial_\beta u$, the classical null condition is $A^{\alpha\beta}\ell_\alpha\ell_\beta=0$ whenever $-\ell_0^2+|\ell_{\mathrm{sp}}|^2=0$. It removes the leading interaction of parallel lightlike derivatives. In three dimensions this structure gives global smooth solutions for sufficiently small, localized data through the <vector field method for wave equations>. This PDE condition is distinct from the <null condition> asserting that a single vector is lightlike.

= Focusing semilinear wave equation
{parent=Semilinear wave equation}
{title2=$u_{tt}-\Delta u=u|u|^{p-1}$}

The focusing power <wave equation> is $u_{tt}-\Delta u=u|u|^{p-1}$. Its conserved <energy> has the negative potential term $-|u|^{p+1}/(p+1)$ and is not positive definite. <Localized ordinary differential equation blowup for a wave equation> produces smooth compactly supported data with finite maximal lifespan for suitable focusing powers.

= Localized ordinary differential equation blowup for a wave equation
{parent=Focusing semilinear wave equation}

A spatially constant blowup profile for a <wave equation> solves an <ordinary differential equation>. Choose smooth compactly supported initial data which agree with the profile on a ball larger than its blowup time times the <wave speed>. <Finite propagation speed> preserves agreement in an interior <light cone>, forcing finite-time breakdown of the localized solution. For $u_{tt}-\Delta u=u^3$, the profile $u(t)=\sqrt2/(1-t)$ is one example.

= Smooth continuation criterion for semilinear wave equations
{parent=Semilinear wave equation}

For a smooth <semilinear wave equation> whose nonlinearity is a smooth function of the solution and its first derivatives, local <Sobolev space> theory gives a continuation criterion: a finite maximal existence time requires divergence of a sufficiently high solution norm. A bounded base norm $H^{s+1}\times H^s$, with $s>n/2$, allows restarting local existence. Higher regularity persists by differentiated <energy estimates>. Precise norms can be weakened for particular nonlinearities.

= Almost global existence for wave equations
{parent=Semilinear wave equation}
{title2=$T_\varepsilon\gtrsim\exp(c/\varepsilon)$}

Almost global existence means a lifespan which grows exponentially in the inverse size of small initial data, typically $T_\varepsilon\geq\exp(c/\varepsilon)-1$ for derivative-quadratic <semilinear wave equations> in three dimensions. <Commuted wave energy> and the <Klainerman-Sobolev inequality> lead to a logarithmic accumulation $\varepsilon\log(1+T)$ in a <bootstrap argument>. This implies existence up to every fixed inverse power $\varepsilon^{-N}$ when the data are small enough depending on $N$.

= Defocusing semilinear wave equation
{title2=$u_{tt}-\Delta u+u|u|^{p-1}=0$}
{parent=Semilinear wave equation}

The defocusing power-type wave equation has the conserved nonnegative energy
$$
E(u)=\int\left(\frac{|u_t|^2+|\nabla u|^2}{2}+\frac{|u|^{p+1}}{p+1}\right)dx.
$$

= H2 bound for the defocusing cubic wave equation
{parent=Defocusing semilinear wave equation}
{c}
{title2=$\|u(t)\|_{\dot H^2}+\|u_t(t)\|_{\dot H^1}\leq CDe^{CEt}$}

For $u_{tt}-\Delta u+u^3=0$ in three spatial dimensions, conserved positive <wave energy> $E$ controls $\|\nabla u\|_2$. The <Sobolev inequality> gives $\|u^2\nabla u\|_2\lesssim\|\nabla u\|_2^2\|D^2u\|_2\lesssim E\|D^2u\|_2$. Differentiated <wave energy estimates> and the <Gronwall inequality> then give $\|u(t)\|_{\dot H^2}+\|u_t(t)\|_{\dot H^1}\leq C D e^{C E t}$, where $D$ bounds the corresponding initial <Sobolev norms>.

= Morawetz identity for the defocusing wave equation
{c}
{parent=Defocusing semilinear wave equation}

Multiplying a defocusing wave equation by $\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla u+(\Delta\psi)u/2$ and integrating by parts gives a weighted spacetime identity. For a radial solution and radial weight, its coercive terms are
$$
\psi''|\nabla u|^2-\frac14(\Delta^2\psi)u^2
+\frac{p-1}{2(p+1)}(\Delta\psi)|u|^{p+1}.
$$

= Distributional bilaplacian of the radial coordinate in three dimensions
{title2=$-\Delta^2|x|=8\pi\delta_0$}
{parent=Morawetz identity for the defocusing wave equation}

In three dimensions, $\Delta|x|=2/|x|$ away from the origin and $-\Delta(1/|x|)=4\pi\delta_0$. Hence $-\Delta^2|x|=8\pi\delta_0$ as a distribution.

= Morawetz estimate for the defocusing wave equation
{c}
{parent=Morawetz identity for the defocusing wave equation}

For a finite-energy defocusing power-wave solution in three dimensions,
$$
\int_0^\infty\int_{\mathbb R^3}\frac{|u(t,x)|^{p+1}}{|x|}\,dx\,dt
\lesssim E(u(0)).
$$
Hardy's inequality bounds the Morawetz action by the conserved energy.

= Radial reduction of the three-dimensional wave equation
{parent=Defocusing semilinear wave equation}

For a radial function $u(t,r)$ in three dimensions, setting $w=ru$ removes the radial first derivative because $r\Delta u=w_{rr}$. The defocusing power equation becomes
$$
w_{tt}-w_{rr}+r^{1-p}|w|^{p-1}w=0
$$
on the half-line.

= Outgoing shell source estimate for a radial wave
{parent=Radial reduction of the three-dimensional wave equation}

In three dimensions set $w=ru$ for a <radial function> satisfying $\Box u=F$. If the source has <support> in $s\leq r\leq s+1$ and $|F(s,r)|\leq(1+s)^{-2}$, the <Duhamel principle> bounds its contribution to $w(t,t+a)$ by $\frac12\log(1+t)$ for $1/2\leq a\leq1$. Its backward characteristic interval meets the source in a set of length at most one, and $r|F|\leq(1+s)^{-1}$. Division by $r\simeq1+t$ gives $|u|\lesssim\log(2+t)/(1+t)$ for compactly supported initial data.

= Outgoing-energy identity for a radial defocusing wave
{parent=Radial reduction of the three-dimensional wave equation}

For $q=w_t+w_r$ and a radial weight $\psi$,
$$
\frac d{dt}\int_0^\infty\psi\left(\frac12q^2+\frac{|w|^{p+1}}{(p+1)r^{p-1}}\right)dr
=-\frac12\int_0^\infty\psi'q^2dr
+\frac1{p+1}\int_0^\infty\frac{|w|^{p+1}}{r^{p-1}}
\left(\psi'-(p-1)\frac\psi r\right)dr.
$$

= Local weak solution by contraction for a semilinear wave equation
{parent=Semilinear wave equation}

Suppose the inhomogeneous linear wave equation has an energy estimate in a Banach solution space $X_T$, and its nonlinearity obeys
$$
\|F(w)\|_{L^2((0,T)\times U)}\leq C\sqrt T\,\|w\|_{X_T}^2,
$$
together with the corresponding locally Lipschitz estimate. For a sufficiently large closed ball and sufficiently small $T$, the map that solves the linear equation with source $F(w)$ is a contraction. The <contraction mapping theorem> then gives a local <weak solution>.

= Elastic wave
{parent=Wave equation}

An elastic wave propagates a disturbance through an elastic solid. Small displacements in <linear elasticity> satisfy a vector <wave equation> governed by <mass density> and the two <Lamé parameters>.

= Characteristic crossing in a boundary-generated elastic simple wave
{parent=Elastic wave}
{title2=$t_{\rm sh}=\inf_{\dot c_b>0}(s+c_b/\dot c_b)$}

In a right-going <simple wave>, the state emitted at boundary time $s$ travels along $X=c_b(s)(t-s)$. The mapping from emission time to position first becomes singular when $\dot c_b(t-s)-c_b=0$, giving the displayed shock time and position $X=c_b^2/\dot c_b$. A smooth increase of <traction> produces steepening only if it increases the propagation speed. This describes the first gradient singularity; continuation as a <shock wave> needs admissible jump conditions.

= Elastic slowness surface
{parent=Elastic wave}
{title2=$\det(c_{ijkl}p_jp_l-\rho\delta_{ik})=0$}

= Slowness surface
{synonym}

For homogeneous stable <linear elasticity>, the three positive <eigenvalues> of the <acoustic tensor> give three <phase velocities> for each <wavefront> normal. Plotting the corresponding three vectors $\mathbf p=\mathbf n/v$ gives the three sheets of the elastic <slowness surface>. Sheets can meet at degeneracies. The <elastic-wave energy flux> is normal to each regular sheet: differentiating its <eigenvalue> equation along a sheet tangent $d\mathbf p$ gives $c_{ijkl}a_i a_k p_l\,dp_j=0$.

= Vertical slowness sextic of an anisotropic solid
{parent=Elastic slowness surface}
{title2=$\det[Tq^2+(R+R^{\mathsf T})q+Q-\rho I]=0$}

Fix horizontal <slowness> $p_\alpha$ and define $T_{ip}=c_{i3p3}$, $R_{ip}=c_{i3p\alpha}p_\alpha$ and $Q_{ip}=c_{i\alpha p\beta}p_\alpha p_\beta$. Under <strong ellipticity in elasticity>, $T$ is positive definite, so the vertical-slowness determinant has degree six with real coefficients. Real roots are intersections of a vertical line with the <elastic slowness surface>; complex-conjugate pairs give <evanescent waves>.

= Elastic wave surface
{parent=Elastic slowness surface}
{title2=$\mathbf x=t\nabla_{\mathbf k}\omega$}

= Wave surface
{synonym}

The unit-time elastic <wave surface> is the locus of <group velocities> on the three branches of a homogeneous <elastic wave>. At time $t$, its scaled sheets describe the propagating <wavefronts> from an impulsive point source when that source excites the corresponding modes. Homogeneity of the <dispersion relation> gives $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf g=1$, so each regular sheet is the envelope of planes $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x=1$. It is generally different from the radial reciprocal of the <elastic slowness surface>.

= Slowness curvature and wave-surface cusps
{parent=Elastic wave surface}
{title2=$\mathbf g\prime=(v+v\prime\prime)\mathbf t$}

In a symmetry-plane cross-section, write $\mathbf p=r(\theta)\mathbf n(\theta)$, $v=1/r$, and $\mathbf t=\mathbf n\prime$. The <elastic wave surface> is $\mathbf g=v\mathbf n+v\prime\mathbf t$. Its derivative vanishes when $v+v\prime\prime=(r^2+2r\prime^2-r r\prime\prime)/r^3=0$, exactly an <inflection point> of the regular <slowness surface>. At a simple zero the second and third derivatives of $\mathbf g$ are linearly independent, giving an ordinary <cusp>. Smooth nonconvex <slowness surface> sheets can therefore generate cusped <wavefronts>.

= Elastic energy velocity
{parent=Elastic slowness surface}

= Elastodynamic Green tensor
{parent=Elastic wave}

= Far-field P and S radiation from a point force

= Elastodynamic surface-jump representation

= Energy uniqueness for traction-driven elasticity

= Seismic impedance
{parent=Elastic wave}

For a one-dimensional shear <elastic wave>, the <seismic impedance> is the magnitude of the ratio of <stress> to particle <velocity> in a progressive wave. With the positive coordinate aligned with <elastic wave> propagation and tension-positive <stress>, $\sigma=-Zv$ for the forward wave and $\sigma=Zv$ for the backward wave. = Seismic layer transfer matrix

At normal incidence, a uniform lossless <elastic wave> layer of thickness $h_j$, speed $c_j$ and <seismic impedance> $q_j=\rho_jc_j$ has <wave phase> thickness $\delta_j=\omega h_j/c_j$. With phasors proportional to $e^{-i\omega t}$, particle <velocity> $V$ and compressive <traction> $P=-T$, the displayed <matrix> maps $(V,P)$ from the top to the bottom. It has <determinant> one. Multiplying these <matrices> in physical order accounts for all coherent multiple reflections. Continuity of $V,P$ connects adjacent layers without another propagation factor.

= Periodic seismic multilayer stop band
{parent=Seismic layer transfer matrix}
{title2=$\cos(Kd)=\cos\delta_1\cos\delta_2-\tfrac12(q_1/q_2+q_2/q_1)\sin\delta_1\sin\delta_2$}

The two-layer cell <seismic layer transfer matrix> has reciprocal <eigenvalues> because its <determinant> is one. Writing them as $e^{\pm iKd}$ gives the displayed half-trace relation. At a real <frequency>, an absolute half-trace exceeding one forces an exponentially growing/decaying pair instead of propagating cell modes. These characteristic <wave amplitudes> split a one-dimensional <elastic wave> into forward and backward components. Their instantaneous net <elastic-wave energy flux> is $\phi_+^2-\phi_-^2$. = Zero-frequency scattering through an elastic layer

= Love wave
{c}
{parent=Elastic wave}

A Love wave is a horizontally polarized shear wave trapped in a slower elastic surface layer above a faster elastic half-space. Zero shear traction at the free surface, displacement continuity and traction continuity at the interface produce its dispersion relation. A trapped mode has phase speed between the two shear speeds and decays exponentially into the half-space.

= Anisotropic Love-wave dispersion
{parent=Love wave}
{title2=$\tan(qh)=\mu\kappa/(\rho'\beta_z'^2q)$}

For an aligned anisotropic surface layer, $q^2=(\omega^2-\beta_x'^2k^2)/\beta_z'^2$, while the isotropic substrate has $\kappa^2=k^2-\omega^2/\beta^2$. Zero top <traction> and welded displacement/traction continuity give the displayed <dispersion relation>. The horizontal speed sets the limiting <phase velocity>; the vertical speed sets rigid-base cutoff frequencies. The effective contrast for the high-contrast transition is $\rho'\beta_x'\beta_z'/(\rho\beta^2)$.

= Rigid-base approximation to Love waves
{parent=Love wave}
{title2=$\omega_n=\beta'(n+\tfrac12)\pi/h$}

A free top and rigid base impose transverse <wavenumbers> $(n+1/2)\pi/h$ on <guided SH modes between rigid and free planes>. Their <phase velocity> and <group velocity> are $c=\beta'/\sqrt{1-\omega_n^2/\omega^2}$ and $U=\beta'\sqrt{1-\omega_n^2/\omega^2}$ for $\omega>\omega_n$. A real <Love wave> replaces the rigid base by an evanescent elastic load; the approximation becomes reliable sufficiently above the corresponding strong-dispersion transition, not at the actual mode cutoff.

= Group-velocity minimum of a high-contrast Love wave
{parent=Rigid-base approximation to Love waves}
{title2=$\omega_{\min,n}\sim\beta'(n+\tfrac12)\pi/h$}

With $\epsilon=\beta'/\beta\ll1$, $\eta=\mu'/\mu=O(\epsilon^2)$ and a fixed mode index, put $y=qh$, $W=\omega h/\beta'$ and $K=kh$. The exact <Love wave> branch has $W=y\sqrt{1+\eta^2\tan^2y}/\sqrt{1-\epsilon^2}$ and $K=y\sqrt{\epsilon^2+\eta^2\tan^2y}/\sqrt{1-\epsilon^2}$. Its minimum <group velocity> occurs near $y=(n+1/2)\pi$, with transition width $W-(n+1/2)\pi$ of order $(\eta^2(n+1/2)\pi)^{1/3}$. Well above that width, the <rigid-base approximation to Love waves> captures both velocities. Density ratios and the mode index must be controlled in this estimate.

= Love-wave cutoff frequencies
{c}
{parent=Love wave}
{title2=$|\omega_n|=n\pi/[h\sqrt{\beta_0^{-2}-\beta^{-2}}]$}

For a slow uniform layer over a faster half-space, the nth <Love wave> branch reaches the half-space shear <wave speed> at the displayed threshold. Its half-space decay exponent then vanishes, so the threshold is a limiting nonlocalized state, not a trapped finite-energy mode. The fundamental has zero threshold and exists at every positive <frequency>; overtones require <frequency> strictly above their positive threshold. At high <frequency> each fixed branch approaches the layer shear <wave speed> from above.

= Love-wave interface reflection phase
{c}
{parent=Love wave}
{title2=$R_h=\frac{\mu_0q-i\mu p}{\mu_0q+i\mu p}$}

For a slower surface layer supporting propagating <SH-waves> with vertical <wavenumber> $q>0$ above an <evanescent> half-space with decay exponent $p>0$, continuity of <displacement field> and <traction> gives the displayed <reflection coefficient> for a downward incident <displacement field> wave. Its modulus is one and its <wave phase> is $-2\arctan(\mu p/(\mu_0q))$. A traction-free top reflects <displacement field> with coefficient $+1$. The round-trip condition $R_he^{2iqh}=1$ then gives $\tan(qh)=\mu p/(\mu_0q)$, equivalent to direct <Love wave> mode matching.

= Strict cutoff of the second Love-wave mode

{title2=$kh>\pi/\sqrt{c_s^2/\bar c_s^2-1}$}

For a slow elastic layer above a faster half-space, put $q=\sqrt{c^2/\bar c_s^2-1}$ and $q_{\max}=\sqrt{c_s^2/\bar c_s^2-1}$. At equality $c=c_s$ and the half-space decay exponent vanishes, so that endpoint is a cutoff, not a trapped mode satisfying decay at minus infinity.

= Rayleigh wave
{parent=Elastic wave}
{c}

A <Rayleigh wave> propagates along a stress-free boundary of an isotropic elastic half-space and decays into its interior. It combines an evanescent longitudinal wave with an evanescent vertically polarized shear wave. If its speed is $0<c<c_S<c_P$, the decay factors are $\alpha=\sqrt{1-c^2/c_P^2}$ and $\beta=\sqrt{1-c^2/c_S^2}$. Vanishing shear and normal traction gives the displayed determinant condition.

\qquad

\quad

$$

= P-wavefront discontinuity transport
{c}
{parent=P-wave}
{title2=$\nabla\cdot[\rho\alpha(a^{[k]})^2\mathbf n]=0$}

Let $t=T(\mathbf x)$ be a smooth <wavefront> and suppose the first jump in time <derivatives> of <displacement field> is $[\partial_t^k\mathbf u]=a^{[k]}\mathbf n$, with $k\geq2$. Substitution of $\mathbf u=a^{[k]}\mathbf n(t-T)_+^k/k!+\cdots$ into the elastic equation first gives the <eikonal equation> $|\nabla T|=1/\alpha$ and longitudinal polarization $\mathbf n=\alpha\nabla T$. Coefficients and <wavefront> must be sufficiently differentiable for this classical transport equation; its local form fails at a <caustic>.

= Transmitted jump at a collimating solid-fluid interface

= Reflection of a P-wave from a rigid plane
{parent=P-wave}
{title2=$A=(ab-\kappa^2)/(ab+\kappa^2)$}

where $\mu>0$ is <linear mass density>, meaning mass per unit length of the straight reference string. The approximation assumes small slope $|y_x|\ll1$, transverse motion, negligible bending stiffness, negligible <damping>, and no distributed external force. Constant <tension> and uniform <linear mass density> make $c$ constant. Some texts use $\rho$ for this same <linear mass density>, giving $c=\sqrt{T/\rho}$; here $\rho$ must have units $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}}$, rather than units of volume <mass density>. \a[https://web.stanford.edu/class/math220a/handouts/waveequation1.pdf][Stanford Math 220A, Section 5.1]{ref}

The units provide a useful check: $T/\mu$ has units $(\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}})/(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}})=\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}$. For $T=100\,\mathrm N$ and $\mu=0.010\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}}$, the <wave speed> is $100\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, so a pulse crosses a one-metre string in $0.010\,\mathrm s$. Quadrupling <tension> doubles <wave speed>; quadrupling <linear mass density> halves it. These square-root laws explain how material and loading set the time scale of the vibration.

For a length-$L$ string with fixed endpoints, the homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions> are $y(0,t)=y(L,t)=0$. Its nonzero <normal modes> have
$$
\phi_n(x)=\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\qquad
\omega_n=\frac{cn\pi}{L},\qquad
\nu_n=\frac{\omega_n}{2\pi}=\frac{cn}{2L},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.
$$
Thus $cn\pi/L$ is an <angular frequency>, in radians per second, whereas $cn/(2L)$ is the <frequency> in hertz. The <wavelength> is $2L/n$, and each <node of a standing wave> lies at $x=jL/n$ for some $j=0,\ldots,n$. For the numerical string above with $L=1\,\mathrm m$, the first three <frequencies> are $50$, $100$ and $150\,\mathrm{Hz}$, with <angular frequencies> $100\pi$, $200\pi$ and $300\pi\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. \a[https://farside.ph.utexas.edu/teaching/315/Waveshtml/node28.html][Richard Fitzpatrick, Normal Modes of Uniform String]{ref}

\Image[string-standing-modes.png]
{title=First three fixed-end string modes, showing opposite extrema and stationary nodes}

Each <normal mode> is a <standing wave>, but it also has a <travelling wave> description:
$$
\sin(kx)\cos(ckt)=\frac12\sin(k(x-ct))+\frac12\sin(k(x+ct)).
$$
At a fixed endpoint an incident displacement pulse reflects with reversed sign, allowing incident and reflected displacements to cancel at the endpoint. <Periodic reflection for a fixed-end string> implements both endpoints through odd, $2L$-periodic extensions of the initial data. This is the bridge between moving pulses and the stationary <Fourier sine basis>. \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/2-062j-wave-propagation-spring-2017/9e6f7f49eea8381d72965b469e1a3fdf_MIT2_062J_S17_Chap2.pdf][MIT, Wave Propagation, Section 2.4]{ref}

A short derivation comes from <Newton's second law>. The transverse force on a small segment is approximately $T[y_x(x+\delta x,t)-y_x(x,t)]$, and its mass is $\mu\delta x$. Dividing the force balance by $\delta x$ and taking the limit gives $\mu y_{tt}=Ty_{xx}$. <Separation of variables> then gives $\phi''+k^2\phi=0$; the fixed-end <boundary conditions> select $k=n\pi/L$, while the temporal equation is a <harmonic oscillator equation> with $\omega=ck$.

= Impulsively struck fixed-end string
{parent=Wave equation on a string}

For $0<\xi<l$, the <wave equation> $y_{tt}=c^2y_{xx}$ with zero endpoint displacement, zero initial displacement and initial velocity $\delta(x-\xi)$ has the <distributional weak solution>
$$
y(x,t)=\frac{2}{\pi c}\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\sin(n\pi\xi/l)\sin(n\pi x/l)\sin(n\pi ct/l)}{n}.
$$
Expand the <Dirac delta> in the <Fourier sine basis> and solve each modal <ordinary differential equation>. Equivalently take the odd, $2l$-periodic extension $v$ of the initial velocity and use $y=(2c)^{-1}\int_{x-ct}^{x+ct}v(s)\,ds$. The image impulses enforce the fixed endpoints. At jump fronts the <Fourier series> selects the midpoint of the two one-sided values.

= Modal energy of a localized velocity impulse on a string
{parent=Wave equation on a string}
{title2=$E_n/E=8L\sin^2(n\pi l/L)\sin^2(n\pi a/(2L))/(a\pi^2n^2)$}

A fixed-end string of length $L$ initially straight receives velocity $v$ on an interval of width $a$ centred at $l$, and zero elsewhere. Its sine-mode velocity coefficient is $g_n=4v\sin(n\pi l/L)\sin(n\pi a/(2L))/(n\pi)$. For linear density $\rho$, orthogonality gives mode energy $E_n=\rho Lg_n^2/4$ and total energy $E=\rho av^2/2$. Their ratio is the displayed formula. Zeros in either sine factor suppress a <normal mode> completely.

= Periodic reflection for a fixed-end string
{parent=Wave equation on a string}

Zero values at both endpoints of a length-$L$ string allow the <D'Alembert formula> to be written with opposite travelling waves and a $2L$-periodic profile. = Velocity-impulse Green function for a damped string
{parent=Linearly damped string}
{title2=$G(x,a,t)=\frac2l\sum_{n\geq1}\sin\frac{n\pi x}l\sin\frac{n\pi a}l\,e^{-kt}\frac{\sin(\omega_nt)}{\omega_n}$}

For $y_{tt}+2ky_t=c^2y_{xx}$ with fixed endpoints, initial displacement zero and initial velocity a <Dirac delta> at $a\in(0,l)$, the <Fourier sine series> coefficient of that velocity is $2\sin(n\pi a/l)/l$. Each coefficient evolves by $T_n''+2kT_n'+(n\pi c/l)^2T_n=0$. Here $\omega_n^2=(n\pi c/l)^2-k^2$. At <critical damping>, replace $\sin(\omega_nt)/\omega_n$ by $t$; for an overdamped mode replace it by $\sinh(\sqrt{k^2-(n\pi c/l)^2}\,t)/\sqrt{k^2-(n\pi c/l)^2}$. The initial conditions hold in the sense of <distributions>.

= Separated solution of the damped string equation
{parent=Linearly damped string}

For initial sine coefficient $a_n$ and zero initial velocity, put $\omega_n^2=n^2\pi^2-1/4$. The modal amplitude is

T_n(t)=a_ne^{-t/2}
\left(\cos(\omega_nt)+\frac{\sin(\omega_nt)}{2\omega_n}\right).
$$

integration by parts and the damped wave equation give

= Low-frequency decomposition of the wave propagator
{parent=Fourier transform method for the wave equation}
{title2=$\widehat E(\xi,t)=\sin(ct|\xi|)/(c|\xi|)$}

For zero initial displacement and unit delta initial velocity, the spatial <Fourier transform> of the <wave equation> gives the displayed multiplier. A compact <cutoff function> around zero frequency produces an ordinary smooth function after inversion. The remaining sine splits into two <oscillatory integrals> with phases $x\cdot\xi\pm ct|\xi|$ and symbols proportional to $(1-\chi)/|\xi|$ of order $-1$. = Entire wave cosine multiplier
{parent=Fourier transform method for the wave equation}
{title2=$C_t(z)=\cos(ct\sqrt{z\cdot z})$}

For real wave speed $c$ and $t\ge0$, define the complex-frequency cosine through the entire series $C_t(z)=\sum_{j\ge0}(-1)^j(ct)^{2j}(z\cdot z)^j/(2j)!$. The expression is independent of the square-root branch even though the square root itself need not be entire. Writing $z=a+ib$ gives $|\operatorname{Im}\sqrt{z\cdot z}|\le|b|$, and hence $|C_t(z)|\le e^{|c|t|\operatorname{Im}z|}$. The <Paley–Wiener–Schwartz theorem> therefore makes its inverse <Fourier transform> a <distribution> supported in the radius-$|c|t$ ball. Multiplication by $C_t$ evolves initial displacement with zero initial velocity in the <wave equation>, giving <finite propagation speed> for compactly supported initial <distributions>.

= D'Alembert's formula
{parent=Wave equation}

{wiki}
{title2=$u(x,t)=\tfrac12[f(x-ct)+f(x+ct)]+\tfrac1{2c}\int_{x-ct}^{x+ct}g(s)\,ds$}

= D'Alembert formula
{c}
{synonym}

For constant <wave speed> $c>0$, the whole-line <Cauchy problem>
$$
u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx},\qquad u(x,0)=f(x),\qquad u_t(x,0)=g(x)
$$
has the unique <classical solution>, for $f\in C^2(\mathbb R)$ and $g\in C^1(\mathbb R)$,
$$
u(x,t)=\frac{f(x-ct)+f(x+ct)}2+\frac1{2c}\int_{x-ct}^{x+ct}g(s)\,ds.
$$
The first term evolves displacement released from rest; the second evolves initial <velocity>. The factor $1/(2c)$ converts an integral of <velocity> over distance into displacement. On a finite string the formula applies before the backward <characteristic curves> reach an endpoint, or globally after an extension that enforces the <boundary conditions>. \a[https://web.stanford.edu/class/math220a/handouts/waveequation1.pdf][Stanford Math 220A, Sections 5.2–5.4]{ref}

For a completely smooth example, choose a <bump function> with <compact support>:
$$
b(z)=\begin{cases}\exp\!\left(1-\frac1{1-z^2}\right),&|z|<1,\\0,&|z|\geq1,\end{cases}
\qquad f(x)=A b(x/a),\qquad g(x)=0,
$$
where $a>0$ sets the half-width and $A$ sets the initial peak. Then
$$
u(x,t)=\frac A2 b\!\left(\frac{x-ct}{a}\right)+\frac A2 b\!\left(\frac{x+ct}{a}\right).
$$
At $t=0$ the two copies coincide, recovering peak $A$. At $t=a/(2c)$, $u(0,t)=A e^{-1/3}\simeq0.716531A$. At $t=3a/(2c)$ the pulses are separated, centred at $x=\pm3a/2$, each of width $2a$ and peak $A/2$. Zero initial <velocity> forces equal left and right contributions; a purely right-moving initial profile instead requires $g=-cf'$. Thus “released from rest” and “launched to the right” describe different <Cauchy data>.

\Image[wave-pulse-propagation.png]
{title=A smooth pulse released from rest splits in two, while backward characteristics select a causal interval}

The <domain of dependence> of $(x_*,t_*)$, for $t_*\geq0$, is contained in $[x_*-ct_*,x_*+ct_*]$ on the initial line. More precisely, displacement contributes only through the two endpoints, while initial <velocity> contributes throughout the interval. With $c=1$, $x_*=0.5$ and $t_*=1.5$, that interval is $[-1,2]$. Changing either initial datum outside it cannot change $u(0.5,1.5)$. If both initial data vanish outside $[-a,a]$, the solution vanishes outside $[-a-ct,a+ct]$: this is <finite propagation speed>. The expanding interval bounds possible influence; it need not be filled by nonzero displacement, as the separated bumps demonstrate.

Historically, d'Alembert studied the vibrating-string problem in “Recherches sur la courbe que forme une corde tendue mise en vibration”, in the Berlin Academy's volume for the year 1747, pages 214–219. The memoir introduces the differential equation and integrates it through two arbitrary one-variable functions. The original geometric problem makes the formula's enduring appeal clear: arbitrary initial shapes can be handled without first decomposing them into <normal modes>. \a[https://webusers.imj-prg.fr/~david.aubin/cours/Textes/Dalembert-HAB-1747-cordes-vibrantes.pdf][D'Alembert, vibrating-string memoir, Academy year 1747]{ref}

For a brief derivation, use <characteristic coordinates> $\xi=x-ct$ and $\eta=x+ct$. The <chain rule> gives $u_{tt}-c^2u_{xx}=-4c^2u_{\xi\eta}$, so $u=F(\xi)+G(\eta)$. The initial conditions yield $F+G=f$ and $-cF'+cG'=g$. Integrating these relations gives the displayed formula; the arbitrary integration constants cancel in their sum.

= Rectangular pulse splitting under the wave equation
{parent=D'Alembert's formula}
{title2=$u=[u_0(x+ct)+u_0(x-ct)]/2$}

A rectangular initial displacement with zero initial velocity splits into two equal half-height copies moving in opposite directions. The <D'Alembert formula> reads $u(x,t)=[u_0(x+ct)+u_0(x-ct)]/2$. Overlap adds the heights; separated copies each retain the original width. For discontinuous fronts the formula defines a <weak solution> of the <wave equation>.

= D'Alembert formula with initial velocity
{parent=D'Alembert's formula}
{c}
{title2=$u(x,t)=\tfrac1{2c}\int_{x-ct}^{x+ct}g(s)\,ds$}

For the <wave equation> $u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}$ with zero initial displacement and initial <velocity> $g$, <D'Alembert's formula> becomes
$$
u(x,t)=\frac1{2c}\int_{x-ct}^{x+ct}g(s)\,ds.
$$
For $c=1$ this is the original speed-one form $u(t,x)=\tfrac12\int_{x-t}^{x+t}f(s)\,ds$ when the initial <velocity> is named $f$. The integral records the total initial kick inside the <domain of dependence>.

For a computed example, set $g(x)=V$ on $[-a,a]$ and zero elsewhere, with $V>0$. This discontinuous datum gives a <weak solution>:
$$
u(x,t)=\frac{V}{2c}\,\operatorname{length}\bigl([x-ct,x+ct]\cap[-a,a]\bigr),\qquad t\geq0.
$$
At the centre, $u(0,t)=V\min(t,a/c)$. For $a=1\,\mathrm m$, $c=2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and $V=0.4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, this is $0.1\,\mathrm m$ at $t=0.25\,\mathrm s$ and $0.2\,\mathrm m$ at $t=1\,\mathrm s$. Once $ct>a$, a central plateau $u=Va/c$ occupies $|x|\leq ct-a$. In contrast, <central zero region for odd compactly supported initial velocity> follows when the full integral of $g$ vanishes. <Finite propagation speed> permits an interior tail; it does not imply the <strong Huygens principle>.

= Central zero region for odd compactly supported initial velocity
{parent=D'Alembert formula with initial velocity}

If $u(x,0)=0$, $u_t(x,0)=g(x)$, and the odd function $g$ is supported on $[-a,a]$, then
$$
u(x,t)=0
\qquad\text{whenever }t>a\text{ and }|x|\leq t-a.
$$
The interval $[x-t,x+t]$ then contains the entire support, whose integral vanishes by oddness.

= Modulational instability

{wiki}

Modulational instability is growth of long-wavelength <perturbations> of a uniform or periodic wave due to coupling between its modulation and nonlinear dynamics. In the <focusing nonlinear Schrodinger equation>, the amplitude and phase perturbations can have imaginary modulation frequencies even though the unperturbed wave is an exact solution.

= Benjamin-Feir instability
{c}
{parent=Modulational instability}
{title2=$1+c_1c_3<0$}

For the <complex Ginzburg–Landau equation> $A_t=A+(1+ic_1)A_{xx}-(1+ic_3)|A|^2A$, the uniform oscillation has a neutral <phase> mode whose <growth rate> is $\lambda=-(1+c_1c_3)k^2+O(k^4)$. Negative <phase> diffusion therefore causes a long-wavelength <modulational instability>. The <Benjamin-Feir stability condition> is the complementary positive-diffusion condition. = Focusing nonlinear Schrodinger modulation dispersion

Their two-by-two <dispersion relation> gives the displayed equation. The <modulational instability> band is $0<K^2<2\mu/\chi$, with maximum <growth rate> $\mu$ at $K^2=\mu/\chi$.

= Briggs-Bers criterion
{c}
{parent=Spatiotemporal wave-packet stability}
{wiki=Briggs–Bers_criterion}

= Briggs-Bers technique
{c}
{synonym}

A criterion for <absolute wave-packet instability> based on analytic continuation of the spatial roots of a <dispersion relation>. Start the temporal inversion contour above the spectrum, then lower it while deforming the spatial contour. A <spatial pinch point> occurs when branches originating in opposite spatial half-planes obstruct that deformation. A double root $D=D_k=0$ is only an algebraic candidate. The opposite-half-plane condition is stated in the primary study https://doi.org/10.1017/jfm.2016.195, section 4.2.

= Bounded temporal growth condition for cubic dispersion
{parent=Briggs-Bers criterion}
{title2=$\operatorname{Im}\beta=0,\quad\operatorname{Re}\alpha\leq0$}

For $\omega(k)=i\alpha k^2-\beta k^3-i\gamma$, real-wavenumber growth is $\operatorname{Re}\alpha\,k^2-\operatorname{Im}\beta\,k^3-\operatorname{Re}\gamma$. A nonzero cubic coefficient is unbounded above on one side; after it vanishes, a positive quadratic coefficient is unbounded above on both sides. The displayed conditions are therefore necessary and sufficient for a finite temporal upper bound, which is then $-\operatorname{Re}\gamma$. The bound permits the initial causal temporal contour required by the <Briggs-Bers criterion>.

= Quartic impulse-response Laplace resolvent
{parent=Briggs-Bers criterion}
{title2=$\widehat G(0,s)=[2\sqrt{s+a_3}\sqrt{2\sqrt{a_2}\sqrt{s+a_3}-a_1}]^{-1}$}

For the whole-line equation $A_t+a_1A_{xx}+a_2A_{xxxx}+a_3A=0$ with $\operatorname{Re}a_2>0$, the <dispersion relation> is $s=a_1k^2-a_2k^4-a_3$. The temporal <Laplace transform> of the impulse response at zero is the real-k integral of $[s+a_3-a_1k^2+a_2k^4]^{-1}/(2\pi)$. Factor the denominator as $a_2(k^2+\kappa_1^2)(k^2+\kappa_2^2)$ with $\operatorname{Re}\kappa_j>0$ for large $\operatorname{Re}s$. Partial fractions give the displayed resolvent. Square-root sheets are chosen by continuation from this causal large-s region; choosing roots independently can introduce false <spatial pinch points>.

= Physical-sheet growth rate of a quartic impulse response
{parent=Quartic impulse-response Laplace resolvent}
{title2=$\sigma_{\rm abs}=-\operatorname{Re}a_3+\max\{0,\operatorname{Re}(a_1^2/(4a_2))\text{ on the physical sheet}\}$}

Put $z=a_1/\sqrt{a_2}$ using the root with positive real part. The additional resolvent singularity at $s+a_3=z^2/4$ lies on the physical square-root sheet only when $\operatorname{Re}z>0$; otherwise the apparent saddle is on the other sheet. The singularity at $s=-a_3$ remains, including a quartic degeneracy when $a_1=0$. The rightmost physical singularity determines the exponential fixed-position growth rate. Compare it with the temporal maximum $-\operatorname{Re}a_3+\max(\operatorname{Re}a_1,0)^2/(4\operatorname{Re}a_2)$ to distinguish <absolute wave-packet instability> from <convective wave-packet instability>. Equality cases require their algebraic prefactors.

= Spatial pinch point
{parent=Briggs-Bers criterion}
{title2=$D(k_*,\omega_*)=D_k(k_*,\omega_*)=0$}

A collision of spatial branches which pinches the spatial inversion contour as the temporal contour is lowered from above all singularities. The branches must originate on opposite sides of the spatial contour. A collision of two upper-half-plane branches or two lower-half-plane branches can solve the double-root equations without causing <absolute wave-packet instability>.

= False complex saddle in dissipative cubic dispersion
{parent=Spatial pinch point}
{title2=$\alpha=-1,\quad\beta=1,\quad\gamma=1/10$}

For these constants, every real-wavenumber <growth rate> is $-k^2-1/10<0$. The real-contour impulse integral decays with envelope $e^{-t/10}t^{-1/2}$ at fixed position. Yet the stationary candidate $k=-2i/3$ has $\operatorname{Im}\omega=4/27-1/10>0$. It cannot be a contributing <spatial pinch point>; otherwise it would contradict the real-contour growth bound. This explicit example shows why solving the algebraic saddle equations is insufficient for <absolute wave-packet instability>.

= False spatial saddle in quartic dispersion
{parent=Spatial pinch point}
{title2=$\sigma(k)=-k^4-2k^2-1/2$}

The real dispersion above is temporally stable for every real $k$, but its analytic continuation has stationary points at $k=\pm i$ with $\omega=i/2$. Writing $\omega=i\Omega$ gives $k^2=-1\pm\sqrt{1/2-\Omega}$; as $\Omega$ decreases to $1/2$, both branches at $+i$ approach from the upper half-plane and both at $-i$ from the lower. These are not <spatial pinch points>. This provides a concrete counterexample to treating growing algebraic double roots as sufficient for <absolute wave-packet instability>.

= Convective wave-packet instability
{parent=Spatiotemporal wave-packet stability}

A localized disturbance grows along some moving rays but decays at its original fixed location. This wave-packet meaning differs from buoyancy-driven stellar convection and is therefore given a distinct canonical title. Changing the observation frame can change the absolute/convective classification. The <Briggs-Bers criterion> uses the laboratory-frame impulse response to distinguish this case from <absolute wave-packet instability>.

= Absolute wave-packet instability
{parent=Spatiotemporal wave-packet stability}

A localized perturbation has positive asymptotic exponential growth at a fixed observation point in the chosen reference frame. The relevant <spatial pinch point> of the <Briggs-Bers criterion> has positive imaginary <frequency>. Merely finding a growing temporal mode or a formal complex saddle is not sufficient; the saddle must contribute to the localized impulse response.

= Finite maximum temporal growth rate
{parent=Spatiotemporal wave-packet stability}
{title2=$\sup_{k\in\mathbb R}\operatorname{Im}\omega(k)<\infty$}

With normal modes $e^{ikx-i\omega t}$, a finite upper bound on $\operatorname{Im}\omega$ allows the inverse temporal <Laplace transform> contour to start above all temporal singularities. An unbounded growth rate at high real <wavenumber> prevents the standard causal <Green function> construction used in the <Briggs-Bers criterion>. For $\operatorname{Im}\omega=\alpha k^2-\beta k^4-\gamma$ with nonzero real $\beta$, the bound holds exactly when $\beta>0$.

= Wave dispersion

= Dispersive
{synonym}

Wave dispersion means that <phase velocity> depends on <wavenumber> or, equivalently, <wavelength>. <Wave packets> generally travel with <group velocity>, which need not equal <phase velocity>. The <linear rotating shallow-water dispersion relation> $\omega^2=f^2+c^2k^2$ has faster long-wave phase motion but slower long-<wave packet> motion when $f\ne0$.

= Nondispersive wave

= Nondispersive
{synonym}

A nondispersive wave has constant <phase velocity> on a propagation branch. For $\omega=ck$, its <group velocity> is also $c$. A <frequency> relation with a nonzero offset, such as $\omega=ck+b$, has constant <group velocity> but a <phase velocity> $c+b/k$ depending on <wavenumber>; constant <group velocity> alone is therefore not sufficient for nondispersion.

= Ray tracing
{parent=Dispersion relation}

Ray tracing follows the paths along which a slowly modulated wave packet propagates. For a local dispersion relation $\omega=\Omega(\mathbf k;\mathbf x,t)$, the ray velocity is the <group velocity> $\nabla_{\mathbf k}\Omega$.

= Travel time
{parent=Ray tracing}
{title2=$T=\int_\gamma ds/v$}

A wave's travel time along a <elastic ray> is the integral of local <slowness> against <arc length>. Its variation with source and receiver position describes the arrival branches, including transmitted and reflected paths. Different paths can have different travel times between the same endpoints.

= Elastic ray
{parent=Ray tracing}
{title2=$d\mathbf x/ds=\mathbf n,\quad dt/ds=1/c$}

In a smooth isotropic medium, an <elastic ray> is the path followed by a local <wavefront> normal, with the appropriate P or S <wave speed> $c$. Arclength $s$ gives the displayed trajectory and travel-time equations. Its neighboring ray paths determine geometrical spreading, while the <eikonal equation> determines the normals. A <caustic> occurs where the neighboring paths focus and the elementary ray <wave amplitude> description ceases to be uniform.

= Spherical elastic ray invariant
{parent=Elastic ray}
{title2=$p=r\sin i/\alpha(r)$}

For an <isotropic> radial <wave speed> $\alpha(r)$, the <elastic ray> equations imply conservation of $\mathbf x\times\mathbf y$, where $\mathbf y$ is the <slowness> vector. Indeed, its derivative is $\alpha\mathbf y\times\mathbf y+\mathbf x\times\nabla(1/\alpha)=0$. Thus each nonradial <elastic ray> lies in a fixed plane through the centre and $p=|\mathbf x\times\mathbf y|$ is constant. The angle $i$ is measured from the radial direction; tangential <slowness> continuity preserves $p$ across spherical interfaces.

= Herglotz–Wiechert inversion
{c}
{parent=Spherical elastic ray invariant}
{title2=$r(\mu)=a\exp[-\pi^{-1}\int_\mu^{\eta_a}\Delta(p)(p^2-\mu^2)^{-1/2}\,dp]$}

For a spherically symmetric speed with strictly increasing $\eta(r)=r/v(r)$, a turning ray of parameter $p$ has $\Delta(p)=2p\int_p^{\eta_a}(d\log r/d\eta)(\eta^2-p^2)^{-1/2}\,d\eta$. Integrate this against $(p^2-\mu^2)^{-1/2}$ and interchange the triangular integration region. The inner kernel integral is $\pi/2$, giving the displayed radius and then $v(r(\mu))=r(\mu)/\mu$. Complete turning-ray branch data are required.

= Hidden turning-ray zone from nonmonotone spherical slowness
{parent=Herglotz–Wiechert inversion}

Where $\eta=r/v$ decreases outwards, it increases as a surface ray travels inward, so no surface ray turns inside that interval. Penetrating rays may traverse it and turn deeper. The radius is then not a single-valued function of $\eta$, and angular ray data constrain combined branch contributions rather than the single function inverted by <Herglotz–Wiechert inversion>. Additional structural or observational information is needed to locate the hidden profile.

= Core-reflected travel time
{parent=Spherical elastic ray invariant}
{title2=$T=2\sqrt{R^2+a^2-2Ra\cos(\Delta/2)}/c$}

In a constant-speed spherical shell with outer radius $R$ and inner radius $a$, a symmetric single reflection from the inner boundary has the displayed <travel time>. The boundary lies on the angular bisector of source and receiver. The branch exists only for $0\leq\Delta\leq2\arccos(a/R)$, since the incident line must remain in the shell. At the endpoint it joins the mantle-only chord branch with the same slope $a/c$. Continuing the formula beyond that endpoint would make the line intersect the inner boundary before its supposed reflection point.

= Hyperbolic interface collimation of P-waves
{parent=Elastic ray}
{title2=$r/\alpha-Z(R)/\alpha_f=z_0/\alpha-z_0/\alpha_f$}

A solid-to-fluid interface can turn spherical source <P-waves> into parallel vertical rays if its height $Z(R)$ satisfies the displayed travel-time identity, with $r=\sqrt{R^2+Z^2}$. The positive branch through $Z(0)=z_0$ is a hyperboloid when $\alpha>\alpha_f$. Differentiating the travel-time identity enforces <Snell law for elastic and acoustic waves>. Travel time above the interface is then $z_0/\alpha+(z-z_0)/\alpha_f$, independent of cylindrical radius. The leading <wavefront> is planar although its <wave amplitude> need not be uniform across that plane.

= Hamiltonian ray equations for a local dispersion relation
{c}
{parent=Ray tracing}
{title2=$\dot{\mathbf X}=\Omega_{\mathbf k},\quad\dot{\mathbf k}=-\Omega_{\mathbf X}$}

A local <dispersion relation> $\omega=\Omega(\mathbf k;\mathbf X,T)$ yields
$$
\frac{d\mathbf X}{dT}=\nabla_{\mathbf k}\Omega,\quad\frac{d\mathbf k}{dT}=-\nabla_{\mathbf X}\Omega\big|_{\mathbf k},\quad\frac{d\omega}{dT}=\Omega_T\big|_{\mathbf k}.
$$
The spatial and temporal derivatives on the right hold the <wavevector> fixed. Translation symmetries conserve the corresponding wavevector components, while time independence conserves frequency.

= Simple turning point of a variable-speed wave
{parent=Hamiltonian ray equations for a local dispersion relation}
{title2=$Q(Y_s)=0,\quad Q\prime(Y_s)\ne0$}

A simple turning point for a stratified <wavespeed> occurs at $c(Y_s)=\omega/k$, where $Q=\omega^2/c^2-k^2$ has a nonzero derivative. If $c_Y(Y_s)>0$, write $q=-Q'(Y_s)>0$; then $\ell\sim[q(Y_s-Y)]^{1/2}$ below it. The vertical <group velocity> vanishes and the <stationary wave amplitude in a stratified wavespeed> diverges as $(Y_s-Y)^{-1/4}$.

= Airy scaling at a variable-speed wave turning point
{c}
{parent=Simple turning point of a variable-speed wave}
{title2=$\delta=(\varepsilon^2/q)^{1/3}$}

In $\varepsilon^2\Psi''+2\varepsilon^2(c'/c)\Psi'+Q(Y)\Psi=0$, with $Q\sim-q(Y-Y_s)$ and $q>0$, set $Y-Y_s=(\varepsilon^2/q)^{1/3}\eta$. The leading equation is $\Psi_{\eta\eta}-\eta\Psi=0$, the <Airy ordinary differential equation>. The first derivative and nonlinear coefficient corrections are smaller by $O(\varepsilon^{2/3})$.

= Decaying Airy continuation and ray reflection
{parent=Airy scaling at a variable-speed wave turning point}
{title2=$\Psi=B\operatorname{Ai}(\eta)$}

The local inner solution is a combination of <Airy functions> $\operatorname{Ai},\operatorname{Bi}$. Decay in the forbidden region removes $\operatorname{Bi}$. The remaining <Airy turning-point connection formula> is oscillatory on the allowed side and represents an incident/reflected pair, not a single upward branch. Without the forbidden-side condition the local differential equation alone leaves both coefficients free.

= Turning-point enhancement of wave amplitude
{parent=Airy scaling at a variable-speed wave turning point}
{title2=$\varphi=O(\varepsilon^{-1/6})$}

A simple ray turning point has outer <amplitude> proportional to $|Y-Y_s|^{-1/4}$. At the <Airy scaling at a variable-speed wave turning point>, $|Y-Y_s|=O(\varepsilon^{2/3})$, this gives an inner wavefield of size $O(\varepsilon^{-1/6})$ when the incident action normalization is order one. The enhancement is finite for nonzero $\varepsilon$ and is resolved by the <Airy function>.

= Stationary square-root-dispersion wake
{parent=Ray tracing}

For the zero-frequency dispersion relation
$$
\Omega=\alpha|\mathbf k|^{1/2}-Vk_1,
$$
write $\mathbf k=\kappa(\cos\phi,\sin\phi)$. Then
$$
\kappa=\frac{\alpha^2}{V^2\cos^2\phi},
\qquad
\tan\psi=-\frac{\tan\phi}{1+2\tan^2\phi},
$$
where $\psi$ is the direction of the <group velocity>. Maximizing the angular slope gives a downstream wake wedge of semi-angle
$$
\tan^{-1}(2^{-3/2}).
$$

= Growth rate
{title2=$\operatorname{Re}\sigma$}
{parent=Dispersion relation}

= Temporal growth rate
{synonym}

For a normal mode proportional to $e^{\sigma t}$, the real part $\operatorname{Re}\sigma$ is its exponential growth rate. A positive growth rate signals linear instability.

= Phase velocity and group velocity
{parent=Dispersion relation}

For a dispersion relation $\omega(k)$, the phase velocity is $c_p=\omega/k$ and the group velocity is $c_g=d\omega/dk$. For the Klein-Gordon relation at $k>0$,
$$
c_p=c\frac{\sqrt{k^2+A^2}}{k}>c,
\qquad
c_g=c\frac{k}{\sqrt{k^2+A^2}}<c,
\qquad
c_pc_g=c^2.
$$

= Phase-group orthogonality for degree-zero dispersion
{parent=Phase velocity and group velocity}
{title2=$\mathbf c_p\cdot\mathbf c_g=0$}

If a differentiable <dispersion relation> $\omega(\mathbf k)$ is homogeneous of degree zero at nonzero $\mathbf k$, the <Euler homogeneous function theorem> gives $\mathbf k\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf k}\omega=0$. Therefore <phase velocity> $\omega\mathbf k/k^2$ is orthogonal to <group velocity> $\nabla_{\mathbf k}\omega$. The result applies exactly to limiting <inertial waves>; finite compressibility can alter the dispersion.

= Phase velocity
{parent=Phase velocity and group velocity}
{wiki}

Phase velocity is the velocity of a surface of constant phase. For a one-dimensional monochromatic wave it is $c_p=\omega/k$.

= Horizontal phase velocity
{parent=Phase velocity}
{title2=$c_x=\omega/k$}

For phase $kx+mz-\omega t$ with $k\ne0$, hold $z$ fixed: a contour of constant <wave phase> advances in $x$ at $\omega/k$. This is the speed at which a tilted <wavefront> crosses a horizontal line. It differs from the horizontal component of the vector <phase velocity>, $\omega k/(k^2+m^2)$, which is defined along the <wavevector>.

= Phase speed
{parent=Phase velocity}
{title2=$|\omega|/|\mathbf k|$}

The magnitude of the <phase velocity> of a real-frequency plane <Fourier mode> is $|\omega|/|\mathbf k|$. In an anisotropic medium it depends on the direction of the <wave vector> and need not equal the <group velocity> magnitude.

= Group velocity
{parent=Phase velocity and group velocity}
{wiki}

Group velocity is the gradient of angular frequency with respect to wavevector, $\mathbf c_g=\nabla_{\mathbf k}\omega$. It gives the leading propagation velocity of a narrow wave packet.

= Crest and trough
{parent=Phase velocity and group velocity}
{wiki}

The <wave crest> and <wave trough> are respectively the highest and lowest displacements in a <wave>.

= Wave crest
{parent=Crest and trough}

A wave crest is a <local maximum> of the wave's displacement. In a monochromatic travelling <wave> it lies at a fixed phase and propagates at the <phase velocity>.

= Wave trough
{parent=Crest and trough}

A wave trough is a <local minimum> of the wave's displacement.

= Wave packet
{parent=Phase velocity and group velocity}
{wiki}

A wave packet is a localized superposition of nearby wavenumbers. Its envelope propagates at the <phase velocity and group velocity>[group velocity] to leading order.

= Ninth-order dispersive advection equation
{parent=Phase velocity and group velocity}

For
$$
\phi_t+U\phi_x+\frac19\phi_{xxxxxxxxx}=0,
$$
the dispersion relation is $\omega=Uk+k^9/9$, so $c_p=U+k^8/9$ and $c_g=U+k^8$.

= Klein-Gordon equation
{parent=Wave equation}
{c}
{wiki=Klein–Gordon_equation}

The one-dimensional Klein-Gordon equation
$$
\phi_{tt}-c^2\phi_{xx}+A^2c^2\phi=0
$$
has positive-frequency dispersion relation $\omega(k)=c\sqrt{k^2+A^2}$.

= Exponential initial-velocity tail for the Klein-Gordon equation
{parent=Klein-Gordon equation}
{title2=$u(x,t)=te^{-|x|}\quad(|x|>t)$}

For $u_{tt}=u_{xx}-u$ with $u(x,0)=0$ and $u_t(x,0)=e^{-|x|}$, the solution outside $|x|\leq t$ is exactly $te^{-|x|}$. On the positive half-line, $te^{-x}$ satisfies both the equation and initial data; the <finite propagation speed> limits dependence at $x>t$ to positive initial points. Uniqueness on this <domain of dependence> proves the formula there, and reflection gives the negative half-line. The exterior tail is nonzero because the initial velocity is not compactly supported.

= Stationary-phase asymptotic of a Klein-Gordon wave along a subluminal ray
{parent=Klein-Gordon equation}

For zero initial velocity and Fourier amplitude $a(k)$, observation along $x=Vt$ with $0\leq V<c$ selects the two stationary wavenumbers $\pm k_0$, where
$$
k_0=\frac{AV}{\sqrt{c^2-V^2}}.
$$
Writing $s=\sqrt{c^2-V^2}$, the leading oscillation has angular frequency $As$ and amplitude proportional to $t^{-1/2}$.

= Upward zero crossings of an oscillatory stationary-phase tail
{parent=Stationary-phase asymptotic of a Klein-Gordon wave along a subluminal ray}

If the leading asymptotic is $Ct^{-1/2}\cos(\Omega t+\gamma)$ with $C>0$, its upward zero crossings satisfy
$$
\Omega t+\gamma=\frac{3\pi}{2}+2\pi n
$$
to leading order.
