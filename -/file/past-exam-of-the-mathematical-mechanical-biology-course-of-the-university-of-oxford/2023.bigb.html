Solutions to all three questions and all sixteen terminal parts of the <mathematical mechanical biology course of the University of Oxford> examination, sat on 7 June 2023. Source: https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/request_for_mathematics_examinat/response/3129786/attach/3/FOI%20202507%20740%20Data.pdf#page=335[original released PDF, inclusive bundle pages 335–338; paper pages 1–4]. In Question 1(b), <bending strain of a rod> is measured per reference <arc length>, exactly as in the printed kinematic identities. In Question 3(b), <elastic incompressibility> constrains the elastic part of the <deformation gradient>; growth can still increase the total volume.

= a

= i
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}
{c}

The <material frame of a rod> obeys the <director kinematics of a rod>
$$
\mathbf d_i'=\mathbf u\times\mathbf d_i.
$$
In the stress-free <reference configuration>, the <constitutive equation> gives $u_1=0$, $u_2=\hat u$, and $u_3=0$, since all three stiffnesses are positive. Thus $\mathbf u=\hat u\mathbf d_2$. Its <cross product> with $\mathbf d_2$ vanishes, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbf d_2'=\hat u\mathbf d_2\times\mathbf d_2=\mathbf0.}
$$
Consequently this director is a fixed vector in space, even though the other two directors rotate along the <elastic rod>.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

The right-handed <material frame of a rod> has $\mathbf d_2\times\mathbf d_1=-\mathbf d_3$ and $\mathbf d_2\times\mathbf d_3=\mathbf d_1$. Hence the <director kinematics of a rod> reduce to
$$
\mathbf d_1'=-\hat u\mathbf d_3,\qquad
\mathbf d_3'=\hat u\mathbf d_1,\qquad \mathbf d_2=\mathbf e_2.
$$
Solving these constant-coefficient <ordinary differential equations> with the given initial frame gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\mathbf d_1(S)&=\cos(\hat u S)\mathbf e_1-\sin(\hat u S)\mathbf e_3,\\
\mathbf d_3(S)&=\sin(\hat u S)\mathbf e_1+\cos(\hat u S)\mathbf e_3.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The <inextensible rod> has $\mathbf r'=\mathbf d_3$. An <integration> using $\mathbf r(0)=\mathbf0$ yields, for $\hat u\ne0$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf r(S)=\frac{1-\cos(\hat u S)}{\hat u}\mathbf e_1
+\frac{\sin(\hat u S)}{\hat u}\mathbf e_3.}
$$
Indeed,
$$
\left|\mathbf r(S)-\frac{\mathbf e_1}{\hat u}\right|^2=\frac1{\hat u^2},
$$
so the centreline is a <circular arc> of radius $1/|\hat u|$ in the plane perpendicular to $\mathbf e_2$. The continuous $\hat u\to0$ limit is $\mathbf d_1=\mathbf e_1$, $\mathbf d_3=\mathbf e_3$, and $\mathbf r=S\mathbf e_3$.

= iii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

The <second moment of area> about an axis weights each element of <cross-sectional area> by its squared perpendicular distance from that axis. The <ellipse> extends farther along $\mathbf d_2$ than along $\mathbf d_1$, so its material lies, on balance, farther from the $\mathbf d_1$ axis. Therefore
$$
\boxed{I_1>I_2.}
$$
With zero <intrinsic curvature of an elastic filament>, the respective <bending moments> required by the <constitutive equation> are $EI_1\kappa\mathbf d_1$ and $EI_2\kappa\mathbf d_2$. Thus their magnitudes satisfy
$$
\boxed{|m_{\mathbf d_1}|=EI_1|\kappa|>EI_2|\kappa|=|m_{\mathbf d_2}|}
$$
for $\kappa\ne0$: bending about the minor axis is harder. Here $E$ is <Young's modulus>, and $EI_i$ is the corresponding <bending stiffness>.

= iv
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}
{c}

Zero applied end <force> and the <force balance> $\mathbf n'=\mathbf0$ imply $\mathbf n(S)=\mathbf0$ everywhere. The <moment balance> then reduces to $\mathbf m'=\mathbf0$.

Choose an untwisted straight configuration: all three directors are constant, $\mathbf r'=\mathbf d_3$, and $\mathbf u=\mathbf0$. The <constitutive equation> gives
$$
\mathbf m=-EI_2\hat u\mathbf d_2,
$$
which is constant in space and therefore satisfies the <moment balance>. The required end <bending moment> is consequently
$$
\boxed{m_2=-EI_2\hat u.}
$$
Its sign opposes the positive <intrinsic curvature of an elastic filament>. If the initial position and frame from part (ii) are retained, this solution is simply $\mathbf r(S)=S\mathbf e_3$ with $\mathbf d_i=\mathbf e_i$; the clamp supplies the opposite reaction <moment> at the other end.

= Solution
{parent=i}
{c}

Because the two <elastic rods> each have thickness $a$, their centrelines are separated by $a$ along the common director $\mathbf d_1$. The bonded geometry is
$$
\mathbf r^+(S^+)=\mathbf r(S)+a\mathbf d_1(S),\qquad S^+=S.
$$
Differentiate with respect to reference <arc length>. The first rod is an <inextensible rod>, and the printed <director kinematics of a rod> give
$$
\frac{d\mathbf r^+}{dS^+}
=\mathbf d_3+a(-u\mathbf d_3)=(1-au)\mathbf d_3.
$$
For the physically admissible orientation-preserving configuration $1-au>0$, the rods have the same unit tangent $\mathbf d_3^+=\mathbf d_3$ and the same planar <material frame of a rod>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\alpha^+=1-au.}
$$
In particular, the second rod is shortened on the inside of a bend with $u>0$.

The <bending strain of a rod> is defined here by differentiation with respect to reference <arc length>. Since the reference labels match,
$$
\frac{d\mathbf d_3^+}{dS^+}=\frac{d\mathbf d_3}{dS}=u\mathbf d_1
=u^+\mathbf d_1^+,
\qquad\boxed{u^+=u,\quad\beta=1.}
$$
The distinction from spatial <curvature> matters: the second centreline has
$$
\kappa^+=\left|\frac{d\mathbf d_3^+}{ds^+}\right|
=\frac{|u|}{1-au}.
$$
Thus $u/(1-au)$ is its signed spatial <curvature>, rather than the $u^+$ appearing in the given <elastic energy>.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

The first term is the <bending energy> of rod 1, whose preferred <bending strain of a rod> is zero. The second is the <bending energy> of rod 2 relative to its preferred <intrinsic curvature of an elastic filament> $\hat u$. The third is its <stretching energy>, with stretching stiffness $kB_2$ and stress-free <stretch ratio> $\alpha^+=1$. Each term is an energy per unit reference <arc length> before <integration>.

Substitute $u^+=u$ and $\alpha^+-1=-au$. The resulting <quadratic function> of $u$ has density
$$
q(u)=\frac12(B_1+B_2+ka^2B_2)u^2-B_2\hat u\,u+\frac12B_2\hat u^2.
$$
There are no derivatives of $u$ or imposed end constraints in this <minimum potential energy principle in elasticity>. Since
$$
q'(u)=(B_1+B_2+ka^2B_2)u-B_2\hat u,\qquad
q''(u)=B_1+B_2+ka^2B_2>0,
$$
the unique pointwise minimizer is also the constant global minimizer:
$$
\boxed{u_* =\frac{B_2\hat u}{B_1+B_2+ka^2B_2}
=\frac{\hat u}{B_1/B_2+1+ka^2}.}
$$
For example, <completing the square> gives
$$
\mathcal E[u]-\mathcal E[u_*]
=\frac{B_1+B_2+ka^2B_2}{2}\int_0^L(u-u_*)^2\,dS\ge0,
$$
which also proves uniqueness, up to equality <almost everywhere>, without merely assuming a uniform <curvature>.

With $k$, $a$ and $\hat u$ fixed, the two limits are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
B_1/B_2\to\infty &: \quad u_*\to0,\\
B_1/B_2\to0 &: \quad u_*\to\frac{\hat u}{1+ka^2}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
In the first limit, rod 1 dominates the <bending energy> and suppresses the bend. In the second, rod 1 offers negligible bending resistance, but following rod 2's preferred <bending strain of a rod> still shortens rod 2 through the bonded geometry. The competition between its <bending energy> and <stretching energy> leaves $u_*<\hat u$ when $\hat u>0$ and $ka^2>0$. Only when that stretching penalty also vanishes does $u_*\to\hat u$.

These formulae describe an admissible bonded <elastic rod> provided $1-au_*>0$. If parameters instead force $au_*\ge1$, the unconstrained quadratic minimizer is outside the kinematic regime of the model; the given energy does not itself enforce positive <stretch ratio>.

= a

= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

Use the <cylindrical coordinate system> basis $\mathbf e_r,\mathbf e_\varphi,\mathbf e_z$. The meridional unit <tangent vector> and outward unit <normal vector> consistent with the given geometry are
$$
\mathbf e_s=\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_r-\sin\theta\,\mathbf e_z,
\qquad
\mathbf n=\sin\theta\,\mathbf e_r+\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_z.
$$
An <surface of revolution> has $\theta$ independent of $\varphi$. Consequently
$$
\partial_s\mathbf e_s=-\theta_s\mathbf n,\qquad
\partial_\varphi\mathbf e_\varphi=-\mathbf e_r,
\qquad
\mathbf e_r=\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_s+\sin\theta\,\mathbf n.
$$
Apply the <product rule> to the vector <force balance>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_s(rt_s\mathbf e_s)
&=(r_s t_s+r\partial_s t_s)\mathbf e_s-rt_s\theta_s\mathbf n,\\
\partial_\varphi(t_\varphi\mathbf e_\varphi)
&=(\partial_\varphi t_\varphi)\mathbf e_\varphi
-t_\varphi\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_s-t_\varphi\sin\theta\,\mathbf n.
\end{aligned}
$$
Equating the three independent components to zero, and using $r_s=\cos\theta$, gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf e_s &: \quad r\partial_s t_s+(t_s-t_\varphi)\cos\theta=0,\\
\mathbf e_\varphi &: \quad \partial_\varphi t_\varphi=0,\\
\mathbf n &: \quad rP-rt_s\theta_s-t_\varphi\sin\theta=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus, away from the rotation axis where $r>0$, the scalar <mechanical equilibrium> equations are
$$
\boxed{P=t_s\theta_s+t_\varphi\frac{\sin\theta}{r},\qquad
\partial_s t_s=\frac{\cos\theta}{r}(t_\varphi-t_s),\qquad
\partial_\varphi t_\varphi=0.}
$$
The two geometric factors $\theta_s$ and $\sin\theta/r$ are the signed meridional and azimuthal <principal curvatures> in this convention. The normal equation is the <Young–Laplace equation> generalized to unequal principal <membrane tensions>.

= i

= Solution
{parent=i}
{c}

Take $s=\sigma$ up to an irrelevant additive constant, $r=a$ constant, $z=z_0-\sigma$, and $\theta=\pi/2$. These satisfy the geometric relations and give the <principal stretches>
$$
\lambda_s=1,\qquad\lambda_\varphi=\frac a{a_0}.
$$
The <constitutive equations> therefore produce constant <membrane tensions>
$$
t_s=f_s(1,a/a_0),\qquad t_\varphi=f_\varphi(1,a/a_0).
$$
The tangential <mechanical equilibrium> equations hold automatically, while the normal equation reduces to
$$
\boxed{Pa=f_\varphi\left(1,\frac a{a_0}\right).}
$$
Every positive root of this algebraic equation provides a cylindrical solution with no axial <stretch ratio> change. There is no further constraint on $t_s$ in the interior equations when end <boundary conditions> are discarded. In particular, a closed-end pressure balance would impose additional axial loading data that are absent here. Existence of a positive root for an arbitrary specified $P$ depends on the given <constitutive equation>.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Here a prime denotes $d/d\sigma$. It is useful to retain terms through $O(\epsilon^2)$ in the normal <mechanical equilibrium> equation, even though only first-order shape corrections are sought. The undeformed <elastic membrane> is tensionless, so a first-order change of <curvature> contributes to normal force only at second order.

The geometric equations written in reference <arc length> are
$$
r'=s'\cos\theta,\qquad z'=-s'\sin\theta.
$$
Their first-order <Taylor expansions> give
$$
\epsilon r_1'=-\epsilon\theta_1+O(\epsilon^2),\qquad
-1+\epsilon z_1'=-1-\epsilon s_1'+O(\epsilon^2),
$$
and hence
$$
\theta_1=-r_1',\qquad s_1'=-z_1'.
$$
The <principal stretches> and the first-order <constitutive equation> are
$$
\lambda_s=1+\epsilon s_1'+O(\epsilon^2),\qquad
\lambda_\varphi=1+\epsilon\frac{r_1}{a_0}+O(\epsilon^2),
\qquad
\boxed{t_1=-Az_1'+\frac B{a_0}r_1.}
$$
Because $t_s=t_\varphi=t$ exactly, the tangential <mechanical equilibrium> equation gives $\partial_s t=0$. Thus both $t_1$ and $t_2$ are spatial constants: $t_1'=t_2'=0$.

Expand the geometric factors in the normal balance:
$$
\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial s}=\epsilon\theta_1'+O(\epsilon^2),\qquad
\frac{\sin\theta}{r}=\frac1{a_0}-\epsilon\frac{r_1}{a_0^2}+O(\epsilon^2).
$$
Consequently
$$
\epsilon p=\epsilon\frac{t_1}{a_0}
+\epsilon^2\left(t_1\theta_1'+\frac{t_2}{a_0}-\frac{t_1r_1}{a_0^2}\right)
+O(\epsilon^3).
$$
Matching the first-order coefficient gives the requested relation
$$
\boxed{p=\frac{t_1}{a_0},\qquad t_1=a_0p.}
$$
Since the prescribed <pressure> has no second-order term, and $\theta_1'=-r_1''$, its second-order coefficient gives
$$
-t_1r_1''+\frac{t_2}{a_0}-\frac{t_1r_1}{a_0^2}=0.
$$
A closed <boundary value problem> for the three requested unknowns is therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
p a_0^2 r_1''+p r_1&=t_2,\\
A z_1'&=\frac B{a_0}r_1-a_0p,\\
t_2'&=0,
\end{aligned}\qquad
r_1(0)=r_1(L)=0,\quad z_1(0)=z_1(L)=0.}
$$
The first equation has order two, the second order one, and the last order one, so the four <boundary conditions> match the total order. The unknown constant $t_2$ is selected by the axial endpoint constraint, rather than by an additional specified <traction>.

For $p\ne0$, an equivalent formulation eliminates $z_1$ and displays that constraint as an <integral>:
$$
r_1''+\frac{r_1}{a_0^2}=\frac{t_2}{p a_0^2},\qquad
r_1(0)=r_1(L)=0,\qquad
\int_0^L r_1\,d\sigma=\frac{a_0^2pL}{B}.
$$
After solving this system, recover $z_1$ by <integration> from $z_1(0)=0$. No second derivatives of the unspecified function $f$ are required: they enter the next-order shape corrections through the second-order <constitutive equation>, whereas the displayed normal balance already determines the leading shape. The case $p=0$ has the undeformed solution $r_1=z_1=t_2=0$.

= i
{parent=a}

= Solution
{parent=i}
{c}

Before contact with both walls, the <elastic rod> can grow freely, so its elastic <stretch ratio> is $\alpha=1$ and its length is its stress-free grown length,
$$
\ell(t)=L_0\gamma(t)=L_0(1+ct).
$$
The first contact occurs when $\ell(t_c)=L_1$. For the intended growth case $c>0$,
$$
\boxed{t_c=\frac{L_1/L_0-1}{c}=\frac{L_1-L_0}{cL_0}.}
$$
The condition $L_1>L_0$ ensures $t_c>0$. If $c=0$, contact never occurs; negative $c$ describes shrinkage rather than the stated continuing growth.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Write $\lambda=L_1/L_0>1$. The one-dimensional <multiplicative decomposition of the deformation gradient> reads $\ell/L_0=\alpha\gamma$. Before contact, $\alpha=1$; afterwards, the imposed fixed length gives
$$
\alpha(t)=\frac{\lambda}{1+ct},\qquad t\ge t_c.
$$
Substituting in the linear <constitutive equation> yields
$$
\boxed{n(t)=\begin{cases}
0,&0\le t\le t_c,\\
E_s\left(\dfrac{\lambda}{1+ct}-1\right),&t>t_c.
\end{cases}}
$$
Here negative <axial stress> means compression. The two branches agree at $t_c$ because $1+ct_c=\lambda$. At late times,
$$
\alpha\to0,\qquad n\to-E_s.
$$
Thus the law permits arbitrarily severe elastic shortening at a finite compressive <stress>. It is a small-<strain> approximation, appropriate near $\alpha=1$, and is not a credible material law as $\alpha\to0$. The one-dimensional constraint rules out <buckling> in this particular model; the finite limiting <stress> is a defect of extrapolating the linear <constitutive equation>, even with that constraint retained.

= iii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

The kinematic relation is unchanged: $\alpha=1$ before contact and $\alpha=\lambda/(1+ct)$ afterwards. Therefore the nonlinear <constitutive equation> gives
$$
\boxed{n(t)=\begin{cases}
0,&0\le t\le t_c,\\
E_s\left[\dfrac{\lambda^2}{(1+ct)^2}-\dfrac{1+ct}{\lambda}\right],&t>t_c.
\end{cases}}
$$
The first term in the post-contact expression is $O(t^{-2})$. Hence its <asymptotic expansion> is
$$
n(t)=-\frac{E_sc}{\lambda}t-\frac{E_s}{\lambda}+O(t^{-2}),\qquad
\boxed{n(t)\sim-\frac{E_scL_0}{L_1}t\quad(t\to\infty).}
$$
In this model the compressive <axial stress> diverges in magnitude, because the $-\alpha^{-1}$ term penalizes vanishing elastic <stretch ratio> without bound.

= Solution
{parent=i}
{c}

In <morphoelasticity>, the <deformation gradient> factors as $\mathbf F=\mathbf A\mathbf G$, where $\mathbf G$ accounts for local stress-free growth and $\mathbf A$ accounts for the subsequent elastic accommodation. <Elastic incompressibility> means that the latter preserves volume:
$$
\det\mathbf A=1.
$$
It does not forbid volume increase by growth: here $\det\mathbf F=\det\mathbf G=\gamma$.

For the homogeneous diagonal deformation, the two unloaded transverse directions are equivalent. Taking the transversely symmetric branch implicit in the paper's hint gives $\alpha_1=\alpha_2>0$. Set $\alpha_3=\alpha>0$. The <determinant> constraint is then
$$
\alpha_1^2\alpha=1,
$$
so
$$
\boxed{\mathbf A=\operatorname{diag}\left(\alpha^{-1/2},\alpha^{-1/2},\alpha\right).}
$$
Equality of the transverse stretches uses that symmetry assumption; <elastic incompressibility> alone would only give $\alpha_1\alpha_2\alpha_3=1$. The total <deformation gradient> and the actual axial length are
$$
\mathbf F=\operatorname{diag}\left(\alpha^{-1/2},\alpha^{-1/2},\alpha\gamma\right),
\qquad \ell=\alpha\gamma,
$$
because the initial <cuboid> is a unit cube.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Write $W_i=\partial W/\partial\alpha_i$, evaluated at $(\alpha^{-1/2},\alpha^{-1/2},\alpha)$. Applying the <chain rule> to the reduced <strain energy density> gives
$$
\hat W'(\alpha)
=-\frac12\alpha^{-3/2}W_1-\frac12\alpha^{-3/2}W_2+W_3.
$$
Multiply by $\alpha$ and use $\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\alpha^{-1/2}$:
$$
\alpha\hat W'(\alpha)
=\alpha_3W_3-\frac12\alpha_1W_1-\frac12\alpha_2W_2.
$$
The transverse symmetry specified in the hint has $W_1=W_2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\alpha\hat W'(\alpha)=\alpha_3W_3-\alpha_1W_1.}
$$
The lateral faces have zero <traction>, so their diagonal <Cauchy stress tensor> components satisfy $t_1=t_2=0$. The <hydrostatic pressure> enforcing <elastic incompressibility> is therefore $p=\alpha_1W_1=\alpha_2W_2$, and the axial component simplifies to
$$
\boxed{t_3=\alpha_3W_3-p=\alpha\hat W'(\alpha).}
$$
This elimination of the <Lagrange multiplier> $p$ is what makes the reduced <strain energy density> useful for the spring balance.

= iii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

The end displacement relative to the initial unit axial length is
$$
\delta=\ell-1=\alpha\gamma-1.
$$
By <Hooke's law>, the total resisting <force> supplied by the spring is $F_3=-K\delta$. To convert this resultant into the axial <Cauchy stress tensor>, divide by the current end <cross-sectional area>. The transverse total stretches are both $\alpha^{-1/2}$, so that area is
$$
\mathcal A=\alpha^{-1/2}\alpha^{-1/2}=\alpha^{-1}.
$$
Uniform <traction> over that face consequently gives
$$
t_3=\frac{F_3}{\mathcal A}=-K\alpha(\alpha\gamma-1).
$$
Equate this to the expression from part (ii):
$$
\boxed{-K\alpha(\alpha\gamma-1)=\alpha\hat W'(\alpha).}
$$
The extra factor $\alpha$ on the left is essential: the spring law specifies a total <force>, whereas the material <constitutive equation> specifies <force> per current area. The reference end area is one, so the same spring resultant can also be read as a nominal end <traction>.

= iv
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}
{c}

For the <neo-Hookean solid>, the axial <Cauchy stress tensor> under the unloaded lateral <boundary conditions> is
$$
t_3=\mu(\alpha^2-\alpha^{-1}).
$$
With $K,\mu>0$, the spring balance is equivalent, after multiplication by $\alpha>0$, to the <cubic equation>
$$
\boxed{(K\gamma+\mu)\alpha^3-K\alpha^2-\mu=0.}
$$
For a fixed $\gamma\ge1$, write its left side as $h(\alpha)$. It obeys $h(0)=-\mu<0$ and tends to $+\infty$ as $\alpha\to\infty$. Moreover
$$
h'(\alpha)=\alpha\big[3(K\gamma+\mu)\alpha-2K\big].
$$
Thus $h$ first decreases from a negative value and then increases strictly, crossing zero exactly once on $\alpha>0$. For $\gamma>1$,
$$
h(1)=K(\gamma-1)>0,\qquad
h(1/\gamma)=\mu(\gamma^{-3}-1)<0,
$$
so the unique equilibrium has $1/\gamma<\alpha<1$. The tissue is elastically compressed, while its actual length $\alpha\gamma$ exceeds one and compresses the spring.

To determine the large-growth limit without presuming a power law, solve for $\gamma$:
$$
\gamma=\frac1\alpha+\frac\mu K(\alpha^{-3}-1).
$$
This is a strictly decreasing function of $\alpha>0$, since its <derivative> is
$$
-\alpha^{-2}-\frac{3\mu}{K}\alpha^{-4}<0.
$$
It tends to $+\infty$ only as $\alpha\to0^+$. Consequently unlimited growth forces $\alpha\to0^+$, and
$$
\boxed{t_3=\mu(\alpha^2-\alpha^{-1})\to-\infty.}
$$
More precisely, the <dominant balance> is $K\gamma\alpha^3\sim\mu$, giving
$$
\boxed{\alpha\sim\left(\frac\mu{K\gamma}\right)^{1/3},\qquad
t_3\sim-\mu^{2/3}(K\gamma)^{1/3}.}
$$
The surrounding spring softens the divergence relative to rigid confinement but does not bound the compressive <stress>. Both axial extension and lateral expansion help accommodate growth:
$$
\ell=\alpha\gamma\sim\left(\frac\mu K\right)^{1/3}\gamma^{2/3},\qquad
\mathcal A=\alpha^{-1}\sim\left(\frac K\mu\right)^{1/3}\gamma^{1/3},
$$
whose product is the total volume $\gamma$, as required by <elastic incompressibility> and the imposed <growth tensor>.
