= Measure theory
{wiki}

Measure theory studies <measures>, which assign sizes to <measurable sets>, and the <Lebesgue integral>, which accumulates values weighted by those sizes. The same framework describes <Lebesgue measure> on the <real numbers>, <counting measure> on discrete collections, and <probability measures> on possible outcomes. The choice of <measure> determines what counts as large; the choice of <sigma-algebra> determines what can be measured.

A striking example separates size from proximity: the <rational numbers> in $[0,1]$ form a <dense set> with <Lebesgue measure> zero, although the whole interval has <Lebesgue measure> one. This distinction makes <almost everywhere> statements useful: exceptional <null sets> can be ignored in a <Lebesgue integral> even when they meet every open subinterval. The <monotone convergence theorem> and <dominated convergence theorem> then give precise conditions for passing a <limit> through an <integral>.

A historical starting point is Lebesgue's 1902 thesis, https://www.ime.usp.br/~glaucio/mat5798/textos/aula1.pdf#page=2[Intégrale, Longueur, Aire (original title page reproduced in USP lecture notes)]{ref}. Its title connects <integration> with the geometric task of assigning sizes.

= Convergence in measure
{parent=Measure theory}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mu(|f_n-f|>\varepsilon)\to0\quad(\varepsilon>0)$}

A sequence of real <measurable functions> converges in measure to $f$ when the displayed condition holds for every positive threshold. It controls the measure of the exceptional set at each index, rather than requiring eventual closeness on each sample path. On a probability space it is exactly <convergence in probability>. On a finite measure space, almost-everywhere convergence implies convergence in measure by continuity of measure on the decreasing tail events. The converse need not hold for the full sequence, but convergence in measure always admits an almost-everywhere convergent subsequence.

= Completeness of convergence in measure on a finite measure space
{parent=Convergence in measure}
{title2=$d(f,g)=\int\min\{1,|f-g|\}\,d\mu,\qquad\mu(E)<\infty$}

Modulo equality almost everywhere, the displayed metric induces <convergence in measure> and is complete. A sequence Cauchy in measure has a strictly increasing subsequence $f_{n_k}$ with $\mu(|f_{n_{k+1}}-f_{n_k}|>2^{-k})<2^{-k}$. Subadditivity makes the limsup of these exceptional sets null, so the subsequence has summable pointwise successive differences almost everywhere. It therefore has a finite measurable limit. Almost-everywhere convergence implies convergence in measure for finite measure, and the triangle inequality plus the original Cauchy property extends convergence to the full sequence.

For the metric equivalence, $d(f_n,f)\leq\varepsilon\mu(E)+\mu(|f_n-f|>\varepsilon)$ for $0<\varepsilon<1$, while $\mu(|f_n-f|>\varepsilon)\leq d(f_n,f)/\min\{1,\varepsilon\}$. The same estimates compare the two Cauchy properties. Define the subsequence limit as zero where no finite limit exists; that exceptional set is measurable by the countable Cauchy criterion, so completeness of the underlying measure is unnecessary.

= Non-measurable set
{parent=Measure theory}
{title2=$A\notin\Sigma$}

A <subset> $A$ of a <measurable space> $(X,\Sigma)$ is non-measurable when $A\notin\Sigma$. On the <real line>, unless another <sigma-algebra> is specified, this commonly means that $A$ is not a <Lebesgue measurable set>. By <completeness of Lebesgue measure>, such a set cannot be contained in a <Lebesgue null set>.

For an example, use the <axiom of choice> to select one representative from each equivalence class of $[0,1]$ under $x\sim y$ if $x-y\in\mathbb Q$, and call the resulting set $V$. The translates $V+q$, for $q\in\mathbb Q\cap[-1,1]$, are pairwise disjoint, their union contains $[0,1]$, and their union is contained in $[-1,2]$. If $V$ were a <Lebesgue measurable set>, translation invariance and countable additivity would make that union have measure zero when $m(V)=0$ and infinite measure when $m(V)>0$. Both conclusions contradict these inclusions. Thus $V$ is non-measurable.

{parent=Measure theory}

= Wiener ball selection lemma
{c}
{synonym}

A finite collection of Euclidean open balls has a disjoint subcollection whose concentric triples cover the original union. Greedily retain a largest remaining ball and discard all intersecting ones. Each discarded ball has radius no greater than the retained one and lies in its triple. <Lebesgue measure> consequently bounds the original union by $3^d$ times the sum of the retained volumes. This covering lemma is distinct from inverse-closedness results for the <Wiener algebra>.

= Disjoint ball decomposition modulo null sets
{parent=Wiener covering lemma}

Every open set of finite <Lebesgue measure> in Euclidean space is covered, up to a <null set>, by countably many pairwise disjoint open balls contained in it. At each stage cover a compact subset of the open remainder, choose a packing with the <Wiener covering lemma>, and remove its closed balls. A fixed positive fraction of the remaining measure is removed each time, while sphere boundaries are null.

= Geometric measure theory
{parent=Measure theory}
{wiki}

Geometric measure theory uses <measures> and <analysis> to study geometric objects that need not be smooth. Its measuring rules can distinguish a <curve> from its surrounding plane or capture the fine structure of a <fractal>. <Hausdorff measure> supplies size adapted to an exponent, while <Hausdorff dimension> identifies the exponent at which that size changes character.

The <Cantor set> is an elementary starting point: it has zero <Lebesgue measure> but positive <Hausdorff dimension>. In <Hausdorff dimension of the Cantor set>, explicit small <covers> give an upper bound and a distributed <probability measure> supplies the matching lower bound. This combination of geometric coverings and analytic control is useful even before more advanced tools for irregular surfaces are introduced.

= Coarea formula
{parent=Geometric measure theory}
{wiki}
{title2=$\int g|\nabla\Phi|\,dx=\int_{\mathbb R}\int_{\Phi^{-1}(s)}g\,dS\,ds$}

For a scalar smooth map $\Phi:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$, integration can be decomposed into its level surfaces with the <gradient> weight displayed in the title. Near a regular level, using $\Phi$ as the normal coordinate gives the volume element $dx=dS\,ds/|\nabla\Phi|$, which explains the formula by <integration by substitution>. In particular, $\int W(x)\delta(\Phi(x))\,dx=\int_{\Phi=0}W/|\nabla\Phi|\,dS$ when the zero level is regular and the integrals are well-defined. General versions allow <Lipschitz functions> and maps into higher-dimensional spaces.

= Hausdorff measure
{c}
{parent=Geometric measure theory}
{title2=$\mathcal H^s$}
{wiki}

Hausdorff measure measures the geometric size of a <set> using <covers> by increasingly small pieces. The exponent $s$ chooses the measuring scale: $s=1$ is length-like, $s=2$ is area-like, and noninteger exponents can measure <fractals>. Unlike a count of occupied grid cells, the construction allows pieces of different sizes and considers every admissible <cover>.

For $s>0$ and a <subset> $E$ of a <metric space>, use the diameter-cost convention
$$
\mathcal H_\delta^s(E)=\inf\left\{
\sum_{j=1}^\infty(\operatorname{diam}U_j)^s:
E\subseteq\bigcup_jU_j,\quad\operatorname{diam}U_j\leq\delta
\right\},\qquad
\mathcal H^s(E)=\lim_{\delta\downarrow0}\mathcal H_\delta^s(E).
$$
The first quantity is <scale-restricted Hausdorff content>. Shrinking $\delta$ removes options from the <infimum>, so these contents increase to a <limit>, possibly infinite. The resulting <outer measure> restricts to a <measure> on the <Borel sets>. For $s=0$, counting each nonempty covering piece with cost one gives <counting measure>.

A different common convention multiplies these quantities by $c_s=\pi^{s/2}/(2^s\Gamma(1+s/2))$, using the <Gamma function>. At integer $s$, that normalization agrees with ordinary Euclidean $s$-dimensional volume. Here $c_1=1$, so both conventions make $\mathcal H^1$ agree with <arc length> on <smooth curves> in <Euclidean space>. Positive normalization constants never change <Hausdorff dimension>. A definition must specify this constant when numerical values of <Hausdorff measure> matter.

For a line segment of <length> $L$, splitting it into $n$ equal pieces gives the <cover> cost
$$
n(L/n)^s=L^s n^{1-s}.
$$
It tends to zero for $s>1$, stays at $L$ for $s=1$, and grows for $s<1$. The matching lower bound comes from the fact that any interval <cover> must have total <length> at least $L$. The exponent one is therefore the transition between a measuring rule too coarse to see the segment and one that assigns it infinite size. For a <Cantor set>, the transition is instead at $\log2/\log3$, as shown in <Hausdorff dimension of the Cantor set>.

This makes <Hausdorff measure> useful when ordinary <Lebesgue measure> sees too little: a <curve> in the plane has zero planar area but can have positive <arc length>, and the <Cantor set> has zero line <length> but positive geometric size at its own dimension. It also separates local covering estimates from global cardinality: a <countable> dense <set> has zero <Hausdorff dimension>, despite meeting every open region of its closure.

An original source for the construction is Felix Hausdorff's https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01457179[Dimension und äußeres Maß, Mathematische Annalen 79, pp. 157–179]{ref}. The modern covering and <Hausdorff dimension> framework is developed in https://www.math.stonybrook.edu/~bishop/fractalbook.pdf[Bishop and Peres, Fractals in Probability and Analysis, §1.2].

= Scale-restricted Hausdorff content
{parent=Hausdorff measure}
{title2=$\mathcal H_\delta^s(F)$}

For $\delta>0$, <scale-restricted Hausdorff content> is the <infimum> of $\sum_j(\operatorname{diam}U_j)^s$ over <countable> <covers> of $F$ by <sets> of <diameter> at most $\delta$, using the convention in <Hausdorff measure>. It increases as $\delta$ decreases; its <limit> is <Hausdorff measure>. Unrestricted Hausdorff content imposes no <diameter> bound and can behave differently.

Any explicit <cover> supplies an upper bound on <scale-restricted Hausdorff content>, rather than its exact value. The level-$n$ intervals of the <Cantor set>, for example, give
$$
\mathcal H_{3^{-n}}^s(C)\leq2^n3^{-ns}.
$$
If this right-hand side tends to zero, then $\mathcal H^s(C)=0$. If it grows, that alone proves no positive lower bound: a different <cover> might be cheaper. The <mass distribution principle> addresses precisely this gap by bounding the cost of every sufficiently small <cover>.

= Hausdorff dimension
{c}
{parent=Hausdorff measure}
{wiki}
{title2=$\dim_HE$}

The Hausdorff dimension is the critical exponent at which <Hausdorff measure> changes from infinite to zero:
$$
\dim_HE=\inf\{s\geq0:\mathcal H^s(E)=0\}
=\sup\{s\geq0:\mathcal H^s(E)=\infty\},
$$
with the standard endpoint convention for an empty supremum. This measures fine-scale geometric size rather than the number of coordinates used to describe the <set>. A line segment has <Hausdorff dimension> one; a square has dimension two; the middle-third <Cantor set> has dimension $\log2/\log3\approx0.63093$.

Why can a dimension be fractional? At a scale reduced by a factor of three, the <Cantor set> needs twice as many construction intervals. The balance $2(1/3)^d=1$ gives $d=\log2/\log3$, between the growth rates of finitely many points and a whole interval. Making this argument rigorous requires a <cover> upper bound and the <mass distribution principle> lower bound, as in <Hausdorff dimension of the Cantor set>.

At the critical exponent itself, <Hausdorff measure> can be zero, finite and positive, or infinite; dimension does not specify that endpoint value. For example, <finite sets> and <countably infinite sets> both have dimension zero, although their <counting measures> differ. Dimension is also different from a finite-resolution estimate: <box-counting dimension> is another asymptotic definition, and fitting a scaling slope over a handful of observed scales does not establish either dimension.

The metric matters. Multiplying every distance by a fixed positive constant multiplies $\mathcal H^s$ by that constant to the power $s$ and preserves <Hausdorff dimension>. A <Lipschitz map> cannot increase it, because mapped <cover> <diameters> increase by at most a fixed factor. A <bi-Lipschitz map> therefore preserves it by applying this fact in both directions. These properties make <Hausdorff dimension> useful for comparing geometric models while controlling how their measurements change.

= Mass distribution principle
{parent=Hausdorff dimension}
{title2=$\mu(B(x,r))\leq Cr^s\ \Longrightarrow\ \dim_HE\geq s$}

The mass distribution principle converts a diffuse <measure> into a lower bound on <Hausdorff dimension>. Let $s>0$ and let a <Borel probability measure> $\mu$ give a <Borel set> $E$ full mass. If, for some $C,r_0>0$, every <ball> centered in $E$ satisfies
$$
\mu(B(x,r))\leq Cr^s\qquad(0<r<r_0),
$$
then $\mathcal H^s(E)\geq1/C>0$ in the diameter-cost convention, and consequently $\dim_HE\geq s$.

The idea is to keep unit mass from being crowded into cheap covering pieces. For a piece $U$ of positive <diameter> $r$ meeting $E$, choose $x\in U\cap E$. A ball of radius $r+\varepsilon$ contains $U$; letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ gives $\mu^*(U)\leq Cr^s$, where $\mu^*$ is the associated <outer measure>. Zero-diameter pieces have zero mass by the same estimate. For any sufficiently small <cover>,
$$
1=\mu(E)\leq\sum_j\mu^*(U_j)\leq C\sum_j(\operatorname{diam}U_j)^s.
$$
Thus every cover costs at least $1/C$. This is the lower-bound counterpart to the explicit covers in <scale-restricted Hausdorff content>. The <Cantor distribution> supplies the needed mass in <Hausdorff dimension of the Cantor set>. https://www.math.stonybrook.edu/~bishop/fractalbook.pdf[Bishop and Peres, §1.2: the mass distribution principle]{ref}.

= Hausdorff dimension of the Cantor set
{c}
{parent=Hausdorff dimension}
{title2=$\dim_HC=\log2/\log3$}

The middle-third <Cantor set> has <Hausdorff dimension>
$$
d=\frac{\log2}{\log3}\approx0.63093.
$$
Its <Lebesgue measure> is zero, yet $0<\mathcal H^d(C)<\infty$. These assertions measure different aspects of size: interval <length> is too coarse to distinguish this set from a <null set>, while the critical fractional measuring rule captures its remaining structure.

At level $n$, the construction <covers> $C$ by $2^n$ intervals of <diameter> $3^{-n}$. Their total $s$-cost is
$$
2^n(3^{-n})^s=(2\,3^{-s})^n.
$$
For $s>d$ it tends to zero, proving the upper dimension bound. At $s=d$ it equals one, giving $\mathcal H^d(C)\leq1$. For $s<d$ this particular <cover>'s cost grows, but that alone would not prove a lower bound on <Hausdorff measure>.

\Image[hausdorff-cover-cost-threshold.png]
{title=Cantor cover costs change trend at the critical exponent, and distributed mass supplies the lower bound}

For the matching lower bound, use the <Cantor distribution>, which assigns mass $2^{-n}$ to each level-$n$ <Cantor cylinder>. An interval of length $r$ with $3^{-(n+1)}<r\leq3^{-n}$ meets at most two level-$n$ construction intervals, since their gaps are at least $3^{-n}$. Its mass is therefore at most
$$
2\,2^{-n}=2(3^{-n})^d<2\,3^d r^d=4r^d.
$$
Every covering <set> of <diameter> $r$ in the line lies in an interval of length $r$, so any small <cover> must have total $d$-cost at least $1/4$. This is the <mass distribution principle> in its interval form: $1/4\leq\mathcal H^d(C)\leq1$, sufficient to prove the claimed dimension. The constants are convenient bounds, rather than an assertion of the exact critical <Hausdorff measure>. In the diagram, the right panel is a finite-stage approximation to the <Cantor function>, showing how probability is shared while the middle third receives none.

The reusable pattern is to obtain an upper bound by constructing <covers> and a lower bound by distributing mass. The same strategy underlies <Cartesian powers of the Cantor set> and the dimension formula for a <finite similarity iterated function system> satisfying the <open set condition>.

= Pressure upper bound for Hausdorff dimension
{parent=Hausdorff dimension}
{title2=$P(\gamma_*)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \dim_H F\leq\gamma_*$}

Suppose shrinking interval covers of $F$ have length-power sums with logarithmic growth rate $P(\gamma)$, and $P$ is strictly decreasing with zero $\gamma_*$. For every $\gamma>\gamma_*$ those sums tend to zero exponentially. The definition of <Hausdorff measure> then gives $\mathcal H^\gamma(F)=0$, hence the displayed upper bound. A matching lower bound requires additional information and does not follow from this covering argument alone.

= Hausdorff dimension of a connected set
{c}
{parent=Hausdorff dimension}

A <connected> subset $C$ of <Euclidean space> containing distinct points $a,b$ has <Hausdorff dimension> at least one. The <Lipschitz> map $x\mapsto|x-a|$ maps $C$ onto a set containing $[0,|b-a|]$, since <continuous images of connected spaces> are <connected>. A <Lipschitz map> cannot increase <Hausdorff dimension>, whereas an interval has <Hausdorff dimension> one. Consequently any set of <Hausdorff dimension> less than one is <totally disconnected>.

= Vector measure
{parent=Measure theory}
{title2=$\mu=(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_n)$}

A finite-dimensional <vector measure> has scalar <measures> as components and a <total variation norm of a measure> defined using their joint vector <norm>. Finite variation controls every pairing with a bounded continuous vector test. <Derivatives> in the <bounded-variation space> are examples.

= Vector Radon measure
{parent=Vector measure}
{title2=$\mathcal M(\Omega;\mathbb R^n)$}

A vector <Radon measure> has <Radon measures> as components. The vector form of the <Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem> identifies finite such <measures> with bounded functionals on $C_0(\Omega;\mathbb R^n)$.

= Polar decomposition of a vector measure
{parent=Vector Radon measure}
{title2=$\mu=\sigma|\mu|,\quad|\sigma|=1\quad|\mu|\text{-a.e.}$}

Each component of a finite <vector measure> is absolutely continuous with respect to its variation <measure>. The <Radon-Nikodym theorem> gives a vector density $\sigma$. Taking the <variation measure> of this representation gives $|\mu|=|\sigma||\mu|$, hence the unit-length property. For a BV <derivative> this separates the magnitude of variation from its local direction.

= Standard Borel probability space
{parent=Measure theory}
{title2=$(X,\mathcal B,\mu)$}

A probability space whose measurable space is isomorphic to a Borel subset of a complete separable metric space with its Borel sigma-algebra. This supplies a <countable generating algebra> and permits the <disintegration theorem for a probability measure>. Completions can be handled modulo null sets; conditional kernels are chosen on the underlying Borel model.

= Measurable space
{parent=Measure theory}
{title2=$(\Omega,\mathcal F)$}
{wiki}

A measurable space is a set equipped with a <sigma-algebra> of measurable subsets. A <measure space> additionally specifies a <measure> on that sigma-algebra.

= Standard Borel space
{parent=Measurable space}
{wiki}

A <standard Borel space> is a <measurable space> isomorphic to a <Borel set> of a <Polish space>, with its Borel sigma-algebra. This measurable regularity permits the usual constructions of random samples and random counting measures, without requiring a particular compatible topology.

= Null set
{parent=Measure theory}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mu(N)=0$}

A <measurable set> $N$ is a null set for a <measure> $\mu$ when $\mu(N)=0$. A property holds <almost everywhere> when its exceptions lie in a null set. With a <complete measure>, every <subset> of a null set is measurable and is itself a null set.

= Lebesgue null set
{c}
{parent=Null set}
{title2=$\lambda(E)=0$}

A Lebesgue null set has zero <Lebesgue measure>. Since <Lebesgue measure> is complete, every <subset> of one is also measurable and null. The <Cantor set> is an <uncountable> example with positive <Hausdorff dimension>; the <rational numbers> form a <countable>, <dense> example with <Hausdorff dimension> zero. Being null does not imply being empty or having finitely many points.

= Borel null set
{parent=Null set}

A <Borel set> of <Lebesgue measure> zero. Every Lebesgue-null <set> is contained in a Borel <null set>, by outer regularity; consequently testing all <Borel null sets> suffices in the definition of a <Sierpiński set>.

= Random measure
{parent=Measure theory}
{wiki}

A random measure assigns a <measure> $\mu_\omega$ to each outcome so that $\omega\mapsto\mu_\omega(A)$ is a <random variable> for every measurable $A$. A <finite-variation process> gives pathwise signed random measures through <Lebesgue-Stieltjes integration>.

= Measurable partition
{parent=Measure theory}
{title2=$\xi$}

A countable measurable partition is a countable family of pairwise disjoint measurable sets whose union has full measure. Its members are its atoms; null atoms can be discarded in statements made <almost everywhere>.

= Partition atom
{parent=Measurable partition}
{title2=$A\in\xi$}

A partition atom is a member of a <measurable partition>. For a finite or countable partition, each cell is an <atom of a sigma-algebra> generated by that partition, whose measurable sets are unions of cells. A partition atom need not be an <atom of a measure>: an interval cell with positive <Lebesgue measure> can be split into smaller positive-measure sets that lie outside the generated partition sigma-algebra.

= Entropy of a countable measurable partition
{parent=Measurable partition}
{title2=$H_\mu(\xi)$}

For a countable <measurable partition>, its entropy is $H_\mu(\xi)=-\sum_{A\in\xi}\mu(A)\log\mu(A)$, with $0\log0=0$. This extends <entropy of a finite measurable partition> and is allowed to be infinite.

= Conditional information function
{parent=Entropy of a countable measurable partition}
{title2=$I_\mu(\xi\mid\mathcal G)$}

For a <measurable partition> $\xi$ and a <sigma-algebra> $\mathcal G$, the conditional information function is
$$
I_\mu(\xi\mid\mathcal G)
=-\sum_{A\in\xi}\mathbf1_A\log\mathbb E_\mu[\mathbf1_A\mid\mathcal G].
$$
The <conditional expectation> in this formula is positive <almost everywhere> on $A$. Without conditioning, the information is $I_\mu(\xi)(x)=-\log\mu(\xi(x))$.

= Maximal inequality for conditional information functions
{parent=Conditional information function}

For increasing <sigma-algebras> $\mathcal G_n$, put $f_n=I_\mu(\xi\mid\mathcal G_n)$ and $F=\sup_n f_n$. For each atom $A\in\xi$ and $t\geq0$,
$$
\mu(A\cap\{F>t\})\leq e^{-t}.
$$
Together with the bound by $\mu(A)$ and the <tail integral formula for moments>, this yields $\int F\,d\mu\leq H_\mu(\xi)+1$. The inequality follows by stopping the <conditional-expectation martingale> $\mathbb E[\mathbf1_A\mid\mathcal G_n]$ the first time it falls below $e^{-t}$.

= Conditional entropy of a countable measurable partition
{parent=Conditional information function}
{title2=$H_\mu(\xi\mid\mathcal G)$}

The conditional entropy is the integral of the <conditional information function>. Its chain rule expresses the entropy of a <join of measurable partitions> as unconditional entropy plus conditional entropy. <Conditioning reduces entropy>, also for countable partitions of finite entropy.

= Join of measurable partitions
{parent=Measurable partition}
{title2=$\xi\vee\eta$}

The join is the common refinement of two <measurable partitions>, with atoms given by their nonempty intersections. For a <measure-preserving transformation>, the block partition is $\xi_0^{n-1}=\bigvee_{j=0}^{n-1}T^{-j}\xi$.

= Field of sets

= Boolean algebra of sets
{synonym}

A Boolean algebra of subsets contains the empty set and is closed under finite unions and complements. It is consequently closed under finite intersections, differences, and symmetric differences.

= Semiring of sets
{parent=Measure theory}

A semiring of sets contains the empty set, is closed under intersections, and has the property that the difference of two members is a finite disjoint union of members. Predictable rectangles form a semiring generating the predictable sigma-algebra.

= Monotone class
{parent=Measure theory}

A monotone class of sets is closed under increasing countable unions and decreasing countable intersections.

= Monotone class theorem
{c}
{parent=Monotone class}
{wiki}

The monotone class theorem says that the smallest monotone class containing an algebra of sets equals the sigma-algebra generated by that algebra. It extends identities proved on a generating algebra to the full sigma-algebra.

= Functional monotone-class theorem
{parent=Monotone class theorem}

A vector space of bounded functions containing constants and indicators of a generating pi-system, and closed under uniformly bounded monotone pointwise limits, contains all bounded functions measurable for the generated sigma-algebra. This function version extends identities established on elementary integrands to every bounded measurable integrand.

= Measurable function
{parent=Measure theory}
{wiki}

A function $f:(X,\mathcal A)\to(Y,\mathcal B)$ between measurable spaces is measurable when $f^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal A$ for every $B\in\mathcal B$.

= Lebesgue measurable function
{parent=Measurable function}
{c}

A <Lebesgue measurable function> $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ has a <Lebesgue measurable set> as the <preimage> of every open <subset> of $\mathbb R$. Equivalently, use every <Borel set> in the target: the sets whose preimages are measurable form a <sigma-algebra> containing all open sets. The source carries the <Lebesgue sigma-algebra> and the target its <Borel sigma-algebra>. A <Borel measurable function> on $\mathbb R$ instead requires the preimages to be Borel in the source as well. Restricting the source to a <Lebesgue measurable set> uses its induced sigma-algebra.

= Doob-Dynkin lemma
{parent=Measurable function}
{c}
{title2=$Z\text{ is }\sigma(X)\text{-measurable}\Longrightarrow Z=g(X)$}

For real <random variables> $X,Z$, if $Z$ is <measurable> with respect to the <sigma-algebra generated by a random variable> $X$, there is a <Borel measurable function> $g:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ with $Z=g(X)$. To prove the bounded case, approximate $Z$ pointwise by finite-valued <simple functions> $Z_m$. Each level <set> is $X^{-1}(B)$ for a <Borel set> $B$, so each approximation factors as $Z_m=g_m(X)$ with $g_m$ a <Borel measurable function>. Apply <scalar clipping> to $g_m$ with a common bounded interval and use its <limit superior> to obtain $g$. For unbounded finite $Z$, apply the bounded case to the one-to-one bounded <continuous function> $t\mapsto\arctan t$, then invert on $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$ and assign arbitrary values elsewhere. Exact <measurability> gives exact factorization; assumptions after adjoining <null sets> give an <almost surely> equal version.

= Essential range
{parent=Measurable function}
{wiki}
{title2=$\operatorname{essran}_\mu m$}

The essential range of a real or complex <measurable function> $m$ consists of the values $z$ whose every <neighborhood> receives inputs of positive <measure>:
$$
\operatorname{essran}_\mu m=\{z:\mu(\{x:|m(x)-z|<\varepsilon\})>0\text{ for every }\varepsilon>0\}.
$$
It depends on the <measure> as well as the <function>. This definition concerns values arbitrarily near $z$, not necessarily inputs at which $m=z$ exactly. It therefore retains meaningful limiting values while ignoring values occurring only on <null sets>. https://www.mat.univie.ac.at/~gerald/ftp/book-schroe/schroe2.pdf[Teschl, Mathematical Methods in Quantum Mechanics, §2.4, equation (2.77): essential range]{ref}.

For $m(x)=x$ on $(0,1)$ with <Lebesgue measure>, **the ordinary <image of a function> is $(0,1)$, but the <essential range> is $[0,1]$**. Neither endpoint is attained, yet inputs with $|m(x)|<\varepsilon$ or $|m(x)-1|<\varepsilon$ have positive <measure> for every $\varepsilon>0$. Every interior value also belongs to the <essential range>, although its exact <fiber> is a <singleton> of zero <measure>. Positive <measure> of a fiber is sufficient, but is not necessary, for a value to belong to the <essential range>.

The two-sided example $g$ from <essential infimum and essential supremum> illustrates what disappears. It follows $g(x)=x$ on $[0,1]$, except $g(1/4)=-3$ and $g(3/4)=4$. Its ordinary <image of a function> is $([0,1]\setminus\{1/4,3/4\})\cup\{-3,4\}$, whereas its <essential range> remains $[0,1]$. The missing ordinary values $1/4$ and $3/4$ still have arbitrarily close neighbors with positive <measure>; the exceptional values $-3$ and $4$ can each be isolated by a sufficiently small <neighborhood> whose <preimage> is a <null set>.

= Conditional expectation
{title2=$\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G]$}
{parent=Measure theory}
{wiki}

= Conditional expected value
{synonym}

= Conditional mean
{synonym}

The conditional expectation is the average of a <random variable> that can be predicted using specified information. It is itself a <random variable>: the average appropriate to one observed group may differ from that appropriate to another group. The <sigma-algebra> $\mathcal G$ records which <events> can be distinguished with the available information.

For a real <integrable random variable> $X$ on a <probability space> $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb P)$ and a sub-<sigma-algebra> $\mathcal G$, the conditional expectation $m=\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G]$ is the <almost surely> unique <integrable random variable> that is <measurable> with respect to $\mathcal G$ and satisfies
$$
\int_A m\,d\mathbb P=\int_A X\,d\mathbb P\qquad\text{for every }A\in\mathcal G.
$$
Its average therefore agrees with that of $X$ on every distinguishable <event>. The integral characterization handles discrete observations, continuous observations, and information that is not given by a single numerical value.<18.175 Lecture 16: Conditional expectation, random walks, martingales by Scott Sheffield>[PDF pp. 4–8]{ref}

If the information tells us which member of a finite <measurable partition> $A_1,\ldots,A_k$ contains the outcome, then on every member of positive <probability>,
$$
m=\frac{\mathbb E[X\mathbf1_{A_i}]}{\mathbb P(A_i)}\quad\text{on }A_i.
$$
Here $\mathbf1_{A_i}$ is an <indicator function>. Zero-probability members do not determine a unique value, which is why uniqueness is only <almost surely>. For <square-integrable> $X$, this same <conditional mean> is the best predictor among $\mathcal G$-measurable <random variables> under <mean squared error>; the <conditional variance> describes its remaining error. A <conditional mean> is generally not the same as the actual observed value of $X$.<Probability: Theory and Examples (fifth edition) by Rick Durrett/4.1 Conditional Expectation>[Theorem 4.1.15, p. 213]{ref}

= Conditional dominated convergence theorem
{parent=Conditional expectation}
{title2=$\mathbb E(X_n\mid\mathcal G)\to\mathbb E(X\mid\mathcal G)$}

If $X_n\to X$ almost surely and $|X_n|\leq H$ for integrable $H$, <conditional expectations> converge almost surely and in $L^1$. <conditional Fatou lemma> applied to $H+X_n$ and $H-X_n$ bounds the conditional liminf and limsup by the same limit. For $L^1$ convergence, the contraction estimate $\mathbb E|\mathbb E(X_n-X\mid\mathcal G)|\leq\mathbb E|X_n-X|$ and <dominated convergence theorem> finish the proof. This also permits taking an unbounded measurable factor out of a <conditional expectation> by clipping it, provided the original product is integrable.

= First-crossing maximal inequality for refining partitions
{parent=Conditional expectation}
{title2=$\mu(\{\sup_k\operatorname{avg}_{M_k(x)}|f|>t\})\leq\|f\|_1/t$}

Let a <probability space> have countable measurable partitions of positive-<measure> cells, each partition refining its predecessor. An exceeding point has a first level where its cell average of $|f|$ exceeds $t$. The cells at those first crossings are disjoint: intersecting cells at different levels are nested, and an earlier exceeding cell prevents any of its descendants from being a first crossing. They cover exactly the maximal superlevel set. Summing their strict average inequalities gives the displayed <weak type (1,1)> estimate with constant one. The same proof works on a general <measure> space when every relevant cell has positive finite <measure>.

= Differentiation along refining metric partitions
{parent=First-crossing maximal inequality for refining partitions}
{title2=$\operatorname{avg}_{M_k(x)}|f(y)-f(x)|\longrightarrow0\quad\text{almost everywhere}$}

Assume in addition that the <probability space> is a <metric space>, continuous integrable functions are dense in $L^1$, and the diameters of its partition cells tend to zero at each point. For a continuous $g$, the oscillation average tends to zero because every point in $M_k(x)$ is within its <diameter> of $x$. For $h=f-g$, the upper limit of the oscillation of $f$ is bounded by the partition maximal function of $h$ plus $|h(x)|$. The <first-crossing maximal inequality for refining partitions> and <Chebyshev's inequality> bound the <measure> where this exceeds $2\varepsilon$ by $2\|h\|_1/\varepsilon$. Approximation proves the displayed <almost everywhere> assertion. Refinement supplies the maximal estimate; shrinking <diameter> supplies the approximation argument. Without shrinking <diameter>, a constant partition generally converges only to its cell averages.

= Conditional expectation under a martingale density
{parent=Conditional expectation}
{title2=$\mathbb E_Q[Y\mid\mathcal F_s]=\mathbb E_P[YZ_T\mid\mathcal F_s]/Z_s$}

Let $Z_s>0$ be a <martingale> with $Z_0=1$ and let $dQ=Z_T\,dP$ on a finite horizon. Its restriction to $\mathcal F_s$ has <Radon-Nikodym derivative> $Z_s$. If $Y$ is Q-integrable and $U=\mathbb E_Q[Y\mid\mathcal F_s]$, then for every $A\in\mathcal F_s$,
$$
\mathbb E_P[\mathbf1_AZ_sU]=\mathbb E_Q[\mathbf1_AU]
=\mathbb E_Q[\mathbf1_AY]=\mathbb E_P[\mathbf1_AYZ_T].
$$
Also $Z_sU$ is P-integrable because $\mathbb E_P[Z_s|U|]=\mathbb E_Q|U|<\infty$. The defining uniqueness of <conditional expectation> gives the displayed formula. In particular, if $N_sZ_s$ is a P-martingale and $N_s$ is Q-integrable, conditioning first at an intermediate time proves $N_s$ is a Q-martingale.

= Mutual conditional expectation rigidity in L2
{parent=Conditional expectation}
{title2=$\mathbb E[A\mid\mathcal H]=B,\ \mathbb E[B\mid\mathcal G]=A\Longrightarrow A=B\text{ a.s.}$}

Let $A,B$ belong to <L2 space>, and suppose $B=\mathbb E[A\mid\mathcal H]$ and $A=\mathbb E[B\mid\mathcal G]$ <almost surely>, for two possibly nonnested <sigma-algebras>. Then $A=B$ <almost surely>. No <independence> or inclusion between the <sigma-algebras> is required.

The identities supply $\mathcal H$-<measurable> and $\mathcal G$-<measurable> versions of $B$ and $A$. The <measurable-factor rule for conditional expectation> and <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> give
$$
\mathbb E[AB]=\mathbb E[B^2]=\mathbb E[A^2].
$$
Expanding the square shows $\mathbb E[(A-B)^2]=0$, and hence the required <almost sure equality>. Equivalently, each <conditional expectation> is an <orthogonal projection> in <L2 space>, and the two projection identities force equality in the <equality case for conditional second moments>.

Equality after applying a <function> need not identify its original input. For instance, on four equally likely outcomes let $X$ take $-2,-1,1,2$ and set $Y=-X$. Their squares are the same nonconstant <random variable>, even though $X$ and $Y$ differ everywhere. Injectivity of the <function> would rule out this loss of information.

= Conditional monotone convergence theorem
{parent=Conditional expectation}
{title2=$Y_n\uparrow Y\Longrightarrow\mathbb E[Y_n\mid\mathcal G]\uparrow\mathbb E[Y\mid\mathcal G]$}

For nonnegative <random variables> $Y_n\uparrow Y$, the corresponding nonnegative <conditional expectations> increase <almost surely> to $\mathbb E[Y\mid\mathcal G]$, allowing the extended value $+\infty$. When $Y$ is an <integrable random variable>, all <conditional expectations> can be taken in their ordinary finite sense.

To prove this integrable case, choose versions simultaneously ordered outside a single <null set>, and put $V=\lim_n\mathbb E[Y_n\mid\mathcal G]$. This limit is $\mathcal G$-<measurable>. For every $A\in\mathcal G$, the ordinary <monotone convergence theorem> twice gives
$$
\mathbb E[\mathbf1_AV]
=\lim_n\mathbb E[\mathbf1_A\mathbb E[Y_n\mid\mathcal G]]
=\lim_n\mathbb E[\mathbf1_AY_n]
=\mathbb E[\mathbf1_AY].
$$
In particular $\mathbb EV=\mathbb EY<\infty$, and this is exactly the defining property of <conditional expectation>. For a general nonnegative $Y$, define its extended <conditional expectation> by increasing bounded truncations; the same nonnegative <integral> argument proves the statement.

= Measurable-factor rule for conditional expectation
{parent=Conditional expectation}
{title2=$\mathbb E[HY\mid\mathcal G]=H\mathbb E[Y\mid\mathcal G]$}

Let $Y$ be an <integrable random variable>, let $H$ be a finite $\mathcal G$-<measurable> <random variable>, and suppose $HY$ is an <integrable random variable>. Then a known factor can be taken outside the <conditional expectation>:
$$
\mathbb E[HY\mid\mathcal G]=H\mathbb E[Y\mid\mathcal G]\quad\text{a.s.}
$$
For a bounded $H$, first verify the defining <integral> identity when $H$ is the <indicator function> of a <measurable set> in $\mathcal G$, extend by <linearity> to <simple functions>, and then use bounded approximation and the <dominated convergence theorem>. For nonnegative $H,Y$, truncating $H$ and using the <conditional monotone convergence theorem> proves the identity with extended nonnegative <conditional expectations>. In particular,
$$
\mathbb E\bigl[|H|\mathbb E[|Y|\mid\mathcal G]\bigr]=\mathbb E|HY|<\infty.
$$
For general $H,Y$ under the stated <integrability> assumptions, the <conditional Jensen inequality> bounds $|H\mathbb E[Y\mid\mathcal G]|$ by that <integrable random variable>. Apply the bounded-factor result to truncations of $H$, then use the <dominated convergence theorem> on the products and the <L1 contraction of conditional expectation>. This proves the identity without assuming $H$ itself is an <integrable random variable>.

The product-integrability condition is essential to the ordinary finite <conditional expectation> statement. An unbounded factor cannot be moved through an <expected value> simply because its partner is an <integrable random variable>.<Probability: Theory and Examples (fifth edition) by Rick Durrett>[§4.1.2, Theorem 4.1.14, printed pp. 212–213]{ref}

= Conditional mean of a bounded success time
{parent=Conditional expectation}
{title2=$\mathbb E[K\mid K\leq m]=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^m jh_j\prod_{i<j}(1-h_i)}{1-\prod_{j=1}^m(1-h_j)}$}

Let $h_j$ be the probability of first success on attempt $j$, conditional on reaching that attempt. Then the <chain rule for probabilities> gives $P(K=j)=h_j\prod_{i<j}(1-h_i)$; no assumption of independent attempts is needed when these are the specified conditional probabilities. The chance of success by attempt $m$ is $1-\prod_{j=1}^m(1-h_j)$. Divide the truncated first moment by that probability to obtain the displayed <conditional expectation>, provided its denominator is positive. This is different from counting all unsuccessful cases as though they succeeded on the last permitted attempt.

= Conditional expectation preserving a distribution
{parent=Conditional expectation}
{title2=$\mathcal L(\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G])=\mathcal L(X)\Longrightarrow X=\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G]\text{ a.s.}$}

For a real <integrable random variable> $X$, a <conditional expectation> $Y=\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G]$ with the same <probability distribution> as $X$ must equal $X$ <almost surely>. No second moment is needed. For any real $c$, the nonnegative <random variable> $D_c=|X-c|-\operatorname{sign}(Y-c)(X-c)$ has <expected value> zero, by conditioning and equality of the two absolute moments. Thus $Y>c$ implies $X\geq c$, $Y<c$ implies $X\leq c$, and $Y=c$ implies $X=c$, outside a null set. Using a <countable> family of rational $c$ separates any unequal real values and proves the claim. Equality of the probabilities of $X=c$ and $Y=c$ also gives equality of their zero sets and hence equality of the two sign functions for every fixed $c$.

= Dyadic conditional averages recover integrable functions
{parent=Conditional expectation}
{title2=$f_n\to f\text{ almost everywhere and in }L^1$}

For an integrable <function> on $[0,1]$, average $f$ on each <dyadic interval> of length $2^{-n}$. The resulting <step function> $f_n$ converges to $f$ <almost everywhere> and in the <L1 norm>. If $U$ has the <uniform distribution> on $[0,1]$, then $f_n(U)=\mathbb E[f(U)\mid\sigma(b_n(U))]$, where $b_n$ rounds down to a dyadic endpoint. Refinement makes these <conditional expectations> a <uniformly integrable> <martingale>. The <uniformly integrable martingale convergence theorem> gives almost-sure and <convergence in L1> to a limit $Y$.

Since $b_n(U)\to U$, the generated limiting <sigma-algebra> contains $U$ up to null sets. For every <event> $A$ in any finite-stage <sigma-algebra>, convergence in the <L1 norm> gives $\mathbb E[Y\mathbf1_A]=\mathbb E[f(U)\mathbf1_A]$. The <pi-lambda theorem> extends this equality to the limiting <sigma-algebra>, identifying $Y=f(U)$. The <uniform distribution> of $U$ translates the two convergence conclusions into the assertions for $f_n$. Choosing a Borel representative of $f$ and assigning arbitrary values at the endpoint one makes all random variables well defined without changing either conclusion.

= Symmetrization as conditional expectation
{parent=Conditional expectation}
{title2=$\mathbb E[Z\mid\mathcal I]=\frac1{|\Gamma|}\sum_{g\in\Gamma}Z\circ T_g$}

If a finite group $\Gamma$ acts by measure-preserving maps on a <probability space>, let $\mathcal I$ be its invariant <sigma-algebra>. For an integrable <random variable> $Z$, the displayed group average is $\mathcal I$-measurable. For $A\in\mathcal I$, changing variables under each group element gives $\int_A Z\circ T_g\,d\mathbb P=\int_A Z\,d\mathbb P$. The average therefore satisfies the defining integral identity for <conditional expectation>. For permutations of $N$ exchangeable coordinates, averaging a product attached to $d$ distinct indices yields the sum over all ordered distinct $d$-tuples divided by the <falling factorial> $(N)_d=N!/(N-d)!$.

= Sampling with and without replacement comparison for bounded products
{parent=Symmetrization as conditional expectation}
{title2=$|U_N-V_N|\leq1-(N)_d/N^d\leq\binom d2/N$}

For arrays of values in $[0,1]$, let $U_N$ average a product of $d$ entries over all ordered index tuples from $N$ coordinates, and let $V_N$ average it over the tuples with distinct indices. Decompose the first average as $U_N=\alpha V_N+(1-\alpha)W_N$, where $\alpha=(N)_d/N^d$ and the repeated-index average $W_N$ lies in $[0,1]$. This gives $|U_N-V_N|\leq1-\alpha$. For uniformly sampled indices, a <union bound> over the $\binom d2$ pairs bounds the collision probability by $\binom d2/N$. This deterministic estimate is the bridge between empirical-product limits and the <De Finetti theorem>.

= Conditional expectation from finite-measure densities

For nonnegative <integrable> $f$, the finite <measure> $A\mapsto\int_Af\,d\mu$ restricted to a sub-<sigma-algebra> $\Sigma_0$ is <absolutely continuous with respect to> the restricted base <measure>. Its <Radon-Nikodym derivative> is $\Sigma_0$-measurable and has the required integral identities. = Equality case for conditional second moments

= Uniform conditional L2 norm
{parent=Conditional L2 norm}
{title2=$\|f\|_{2,\infty\mid Y}=\mathrm{ess\,sup}_y\|f\|_y$}

The essential supremum over the base of the <conditional L2 norm>. It may be infinite for a globally square-integrable function. For probability fibers, $\|f\|_2\leq\|f\|_{2,\infty\mid Y}$; the uniform norm controls all fibers outside a null set rather than only their average.

= Conditional Jensen inequality
{parent=Conditional expectation}

For an integrable <random variable> $X$, a sub-<sigma-algebra> $\mathcal G$, and a convex function $\phi$ with suitable integrability, $\phi(\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G])\le\mathbb E[\phi(X)\mid\mathcal G]$ almost surely. One proof writes $\phi$ as a supremum of supporting affine functions and applies monotonicity and <linearity> of <conditional expectation> to each. This proves the convex-loss form of the <Rao-Blackwell theorem>.

= L1 contraction of conditional expectation
{c}
{parent=Conditional expectation}
{title2=$\|\mathbb E[Y\mid\mathcal G]\|_1\leq\|Y\|_1$}

For an <integrable random variable> $Y$, the <conditional expectation> satisfies $|\mathbb E[Y\mid\mathcal G]|\leq\mathbb E[|Y|\mid\mathcal G]$ <almost surely>. Taking <expected values> gives the <L1 contraction of conditional expectation>. Applying it to $Y_n-Y$ shows that <convergence in L1> is preserved by <conditional expectation> with respect to any fixed <sigma-algebra>.

= Conditional expectation of a summand given future partial sums
{parent=Conditional expectation}

For <independent and identically distributed random variables> $X_j$ that are <integrable random variables>, with $S_n=\sum_{j=1}^nX_j$, symmetry gives $\mathbb E[X_1\mid S_n,S_{n+1},\ldots]=S_n/n$. The decreasing <sigma-algebras> generated by these future sums permit application of the <reverse martingale convergence theorem>.

= Law of total expectation
{title2=$\mathbb E[\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G]]=\mathbb E[X]$}
{parent=Conditional expectation}
{wiki}

= Tower property of conditional expectation
{synonym}

= Law of iterated expectation
{synonym}

= Tower property
{synonym}

The law of total expectation makes averaging in stages reliable, provided the outer stage uses the actual <probabilities> of the groups. For an <integrable random variable> $X$ and a sub-<sigma-algebra> $\mathcal G$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G]]=\mathbb E[X].}
$$
Replacing each outcome by its group's <conditional mean> preserves the overall <expected value>. In the definition of <conditional expectation>, take the <event> $A=\Omega$ to obtain this identity.<Probability: Theory and Examples (fifth edition) by Rick Durrett/4.1 Conditional Expectation>[Equation (4.1.5), p. 212]{ref}

For a complete finite example, choose a group $Y=A$ with <probability> $1/4$ and $Y=B$ with <probability> $3/4$. Within group $A$, $X$ is $0$ or $4$ with equal <conditional probabilities>; within group $B$, it is $6$ or $10$ with equal <conditional probabilities>. Thus the <joint probability distribution> puts probabilities $1/8,1/8,3/8,3/8$ on $(A,0),(A,4),(B,6),(B,10)$. The <conditional means> are two and eight. Consequently,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E X=\frac14\cdot2+\frac34\cdot8=\frac{13}{2}.}
$$
Direct averaging of the four values gives the same $0/8+4/8+18/8+30/8=13/2$. Taking the unweighted average of the two group means instead gives five, which describes a different choice of group <probabilities>. This is why the outer weighting matters.

The formula is useful when conditioning on a first choice makes the remaining experiment simple. It can replace a large outcome enumeration by smaller calculations within groups. In a <mixture distribution>, it combines component <means> using mixture weights; in a <random sum of independent claims>, it separates the number of summands from their individual <expected values> under the stated <independence> assumptions. The same averaging step appears in the <law of total variance>.

= Expectation closure error for a nonlinear recurrence
{parent=Law of total expectation}

A bound $\mathbb E[B_{j+1}\mid B_j]\leq f(B_j)$ implies $\mathbb E B_{j+1}\leq\mathbb E f(B_j)$. It does not by itself imply $\mathbb E B_{j+1}\leq f(\mathbb E B_j)$. For $f(b)=b^2/n$, <Jensen's inequality> goes in the opposite direction. The missing contribution is the <variance>: $\mathbb E[B_j^2]=(\mathbb E B_j)^2+\operatorname{Var}(B_j)$.

= Nested conditioning keeps the coarser information
{parent=Law of total expectation}
{title2=$\mathbb E[\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G]\mid\mathcal H]=\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal H]\quad(\mathcal H\subseteq\mathcal G)$}

For <integrable random variable> $X$ and nested sub-<sigma-algebras> $\mathcal H\subseteq\mathcal G$, the <tower property> says
$$
\mathbb E[\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G]\mid\mathcal H]
=\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal H].
$$
The finer-information <conditional mean> can be averaged within each coarser group, recovering the coarser-information <conditional mean>. Conversely, conditioning an already $\mathcal H$-measurable <conditional mean> on $\mathcal G$ leaves it unchanged. Both orders therefore recover $\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal H]$ when the information is nested.<Probability: Theory and Examples (fifth edition) by Rick Durrett/4.1 Conditional Expectation>[Theorem 4.1.13, p. 212]{ref}

For the finite example in <law of total expectation>, exact knowledge of $X$ gives the finer mean $X$ itself. Averaging within the groups $A,B$ recovers two and eight. Forgetting the group then gives $13/2$. The nesting condition is essential; <iterated conditional expectation over nonnested sigma-algebras> describes why arbitrary successive conditioning cannot simply be replaced by conditioning on an intersection.

= Independent sigma-algebras have trivial intersection
{parent=Conditional expectation}

If sub-sigma-algebras $\mathcal G$ and $\mathcal H$ are independent, every event in $\mathcal G\cap\mathcal H$ is independent of itself and consequently has probability zero or one. Thus
$$
\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G\cap\mathcal H]=\mathbb E[X]
$$
almost surely for every integrable random variable $X$.

= Iterated conditional expectation over nonnested sigma-algebras
{parent=Conditional expectation}

In general,
$$
\mathbb E[\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G]\mid\mathcal H]
\ne\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G\cap\mathcal H].
$$
Equality does hold when either sigma-algebra contains the other or when they are independent.

= Conditional Fatou lemma
{c}
{parent=Conditional expectation}

For nonnegative random variables $X_n$ and a sigma-algebra $\mathcal G$,
$$
\mathbb E[\liminf_nX_n\mid\mathcal G]
\leq\liminf_n\mathbb E[X_n\mid\mathcal G]
$$
almost surely. It follows from the ordinary <Fatou lemma> after testing both sides on every event in $\mathcal G$.

= Fatou's lemma
{c}
{parent=Measure theory}
{wiki}

= Fatou lemma
{c}
{synonym}

For nonnegative measurable functions,
$$
\int\liminf_nf_n\,d\mu\leq\liminf_n\int f_n\,d\mu.
$$

= Reverse Fatou lemma
{parent=Fatou's lemma}
{c}
{title2=$\limsup_n\mathbb E Y_n\leq\mathbb E\limsup_nY_n$}

If $Y_n$ are <random variables> bounded above by an <integrable function> $Z$, with all relevant expectations well defined, <Fatou's lemma> applied to the nonnegative variables $Z-Y_n$ gives $\limsup_n\mathbb E Y_n\leq\mathbb E\limsup_nY_n$. In particular, bounded <indicator random variables> that tend almost surely to zero have probabilities tending to zero.

= Almost-everywhere limit under a uniform L1 bound
{parent=Fatou's lemma}
{title2=$\int|g|\leq\liminf_n\int|g_n|\leq\sup_n\|g_n\|_1$}

Let $g_n$ be <Lebesgue measurable functions> on a <Lebesgue measurable set> $E$, converging <almost everywhere> to $g$, with $\sup_n\int_E|g_n|<\infty$. The limit has a measurable representative: use $\limsup_ng_n$ off the exceptional <null set> and zero on it. The <completeness of Lebesgue measure> makes any alternative values on that null set measurable as well. <Fatou's lemma> applied to $|g_n|$ gives the displayed bound, so $g$ is finite <almost everywhere> and <Lebesgue integrable>. A uniform <L1 norm> bound alone does not imply convergence in <L1 space>; the <strict inequality in Fatou lemma> supplies a shrinking-spike example.

= Fatou lemma for series
{c}
{parent=Fatou's lemma}

For nonnegative numbers $a_{n,k}$, <Fatou lemma> applied to counting measure gives
$$
\sum_k\liminf_{n\to\infty}a_{n,k}
\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_ka_{n,k}.
$$

= Proof of Fatou lemma
{parent=Fatou's lemma}

Set
$$
g_n=\inf_{k\geq n}f_k.
$$
Then $0\leq g_n\uparrow\liminf_k f_k$. The <monotone convergence theorem> and $g_n\leq f_k$ for every $k\geq n$ give
$$
\int\liminf_kf_k\,d\mu
=\lim_n\int g_n\,d\mu
\leq\lim_n\inf_{k\geq n}\int f_k\,d\mu
=\liminf_k\int f_k\,d\mu.
$$

= Strict inequality in Fatou lemma
{parent=Fatou's lemma}

On $([0,1],\lambda)$, the functions
$$
f_n=n\mathbf1_{(0,1/n)}
$$
converge pointwise to zero but satisfy $\int f_n\,d\lambda=1$. Hence the two sides of <Fatou lemma> can be zero and one.

= Outer measure
{parent=Measure theory}
{wiki}

An outer measure $\mu^*$ is defined on every subset, vanishes on the empty set, is monotone, and is countably subadditive.

= Metric outer measure
{parent=Outer measure}
{title2=$d(A,B)>0\Longrightarrow\mu^*(A\cup B)=\mu^*(A)+\mu^*(B)$}

An <outer measure> on a <metric space> is metric when positively separated sets satisfy the displayed equality. Every <Borel set> is then measurable under <Caratheodory measurability criterion>. <Lebesgue outer measure> is metric: refine every box of a covering into finitely many boxes of diameter smaller than the separation. Each refined box meets at most one of the two sets, splitting the cover without changing its total volume. Taking infima proves the additive lower bound; outer subadditivity gives equality.

= Regular outer measure
{parent=Outer measure}
{title2=$\forall A\ \exists G\supseteq A:\quad G\text{ measurable},\quad\mu^*(G)=\mu^*(A)$}

An <outer measure> is regular in this sense when every <set> admits a measurable hull of equal <outer measure>. This is different from merely specifying inner or outer approximation of already <measurable sets> by compact or open sets. <Lebesgue outer measure> has a hull that is a <Borel set>: for finite <outer measure> choose open covers of total volumes within $1/j$ of its value and intersect their unions. <Monotonicity> gives equality of the hull's <outer measure> with that of the original <set>. For infinite <outer measure> the whole Euclidean space is a hull. This regularity is useful for turning numerical equality of inner and outer sizes into <measurability>.

= Maximal additivity algebra of a regular outer measure
{parent=Regular outer measure}

For a <regular outer measure>, its <Caratheodory measurability criterion> defines a maximal <algebra of sets> on which that <outer measure> is finitely additive. Indeed, suppose an additive algebra contains all <measurable sets> and also $A$. Given $E$ of finite <outer measure>, take a measurable hull $H\supseteq E$ of equal mass. <Finite additivity> splits $H$ by $A$, and <monotonicity> gives $\mu^*(E)\geq\mu^*(E\cap A)+\mu^*(E\setminus A)$. Outer subadditivity gives equality. For infinite mass that equality already follows from outer subadditivity. Thus $A$ was measurable. No <sigma-finite measure> assumption is necessary.

= Measurability criterion inside a finite measurable hull
{parent=Regular outer measure}
{title2=$A\subseteq M,\quad\mu^*(M)<\infty:\quad A\text{ measurable}\iff\mu^*(A)+\mu^*(M\setminus A)=\mu^*(M)$}

For a <regular outer measure> and measurable $M$, the forward implication is <countable additivity>. For the reverse, choose a measurable hull of $A$ and intersect it with $M$ to obtain $A\subseteq B\subseteq M$ and $\mu(B)=\mu^*(A)$. <Measurability> of $B$ splits $M\setminus A$ into $M\setminus B$ and $B\setminus A$, so
$$
\mu^*(M\setminus A)=\mu(M\setminus B)+\mu^*(B\setminus A).
$$
The assumed equality and finiteness force $\mu^*(B\setminus A)=0$. Every outer-<measure>-null <set> is measurable by <Caratheodory measurability criterion>, so $A=B\setminus(B\setminus A)$ is measurable. Finiteness prevents invalid subtraction of infinite masses.

= Carathéodory's criterion
{c}
{parent=Outer measure}
{wiki}

= Caratheodory measurability criterion
{c}
{synonym}

= Caratheodory criterion
{c}
{synonym}

A set $E$ is measurable for an <outer measure> $\mu^*$ when every set $A$ is split additively by $E$:
$$
\mu^*(A)=\mu^*(A\cap E)+\mu^*(A\setminus E).
$$
The measurable sets form a <sigma-algebra>, and the restriction of $\mu^*$ to it is a complete measure.

= Lebesgue outer measure
{title2=$m^*$}
{parent=Outer measure}
{c}

The resulting <Lebesgue measure> is the restriction of this outer measure to the sets satisfying the measurability criterion.

Lebesgue outer measure on $\mathbb R$ is
$$
m^*(E)=\inf\left\{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|I_n|:E\subseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}I_n\right\},
$$
where the infimum is over countable interval covers. The <Caratheodory measurability criterion> selects the Lebesgue-measurable sets.

= Translation invariance of Lebesgue outer measure
{parent=Lebesgue outer measure}
{title2=$m^*(A+a)=m^*(A)$}

Translating every <interval> of a countable cover of $A\subseteq\mathbb R$ by $a$ produces a cover of $A+a$ with the same lengths. Taking the <infimum> of cover lengths gives $m^*(A+a)\leq m^*(A)$. Translating back by $-a$ gives the reverse inequality. This proves <translation invariance> for <Lebesgue outer measure> on every <subset>, without first assuming the set is <Lebesgue measurable>.

= Monotonicity of Lebesgue outer measure
{parent=Lebesgue outer measure}
{title2=$A\subseteq B\Longrightarrow m^*(A)\leq m^*(B)$}

If $A\subseteq B$, every countable <interval> cover of $B$ is also a cover of $A$. Taking the <infimum> of the lengths over the larger family of covers can only decrease the result. Thus <Lebesgue outer measure> is monotone on all <subsets>, with no <measurability> assumption.

= Countable subadditivity of Lebesgue outer measure
{parent=Lebesgue outer measure}
{title2=$m^*(\bigcup_n A_n)\leq\sum_n m^*(A_n)$}

If the sum of <Lebesgue outer measures> on the right is infinite, the inequality is automatic. Otherwise, fix $\varepsilon>0$ and cover each $A_n$ by a countable family of <intervals> whose total length is less than $m^*(A_n)+\varepsilon2^{-n}$. These covers exist by the definition of <infimum>. The combined countable family covers $\bigcup_nA_n$ and has total length at most $\sum_nm^*(A_n)+\varepsilon$. Take the infimum over covers of the union, then let $\varepsilon$ tend to zero. No <measurability> assumption on the $A_n$ is needed.

= High-density interval for outer measure
{parent=Lebesgue outer measure}
{title2=$m^*(E\cap I)>\lambda|I|,\quad0<\lambda<1$}

= Outer-measure high-density interval
{synonym}

For any $E\subseteq\mathbb R$ with positive <Lebesgue outer measure> and any $0<\lambda<1$, some bounded nonempty open <interval> $I$ satisfies the displayed inequality. The set $E$ need not be a <Lebesgue measurable set>.

To prove this, first choose a bounded portion $F=E\cap[-N,N]$ with $0<a=m^*(F)<\infty$, using <countable subadditivity>. Choose a countable open-interval cover $(I_j)$ of $F$ with $\sum_j|I_j|<a/\lambda$. If $m^*(E\cap I_j)\leq\lambda|I_j|$ for every $j$, then
$$
a\leq\sum_jm^*(F\cap I_j)\leq\lambda\sum_j|I_j|<a,
$$
a contradiction. For measurable $E$ this gives the <high-density interval in a positive-measure subset of the real line> without requiring the full <Lebesgue density theorem>.

= Step function
{parent=Measure theory}
{wiki}

A step function is constant on each member of a finite or countable measurable partition of its domain. Indicator functions of measurable sets are basic examples.

= Uniform step approximation of a continuous function on a compact interval
{parent=Step function}

A <continuous function> on a <compact interval> is <uniformly continuous> by the <Heine-Cantor theorem>. Choose a <partition of an interval> whose widths lie below the input tolerance for a desired output error $\varepsilon$. On each half-open partition subinterval, use the value of the <function> at its left endpoint, and assign the rightmost endpoint its own value. This defines a <step function> whose error is strictly below $\varepsilon$ at every input. Finitely assigned partition-point values are allowed and do not alter the <Riemann integral>.

= Lebesgue integral
{parent=Measure theory}
{c}
{title2=$\int_X f\,d\mu$}
{wiki}

For a nonnegative <measurable function> $f:X\to[0,\infty]$ on a <measure space>, define
$$
\int_X f\,d\mu=\sup\left\{\int_X s\,d\mu: s\text{ is a nonnegative simple function},\ 0\leq s\leq f\right\}.
$$
The <supremum> may be infinite. A <simple function> assigns finitely many heights to <measurable sets>, so its <integral> is height times <measure>, summed over those pieces. This definition groups points by their values rather than requiring a partition into short intervals. See https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-102-introduction-to-functional-analysis-spring-2021/615c26170233747c081d6dab928f7d91_MIT18_102s21_lec11.pdf[MIT lecture 11, Definition 108].

A <real-valued function> is <Lebesgue integrable> when $\int_X|f|\,d\mu<\infty$. Its <integral> is $\int_X f_+\,d\mu-\int_X f_-\,d\mu$, where $f_+=\max(f,0)$ is its <positive part> and $f_-=\max(-f,0)$. This finiteness requirement avoids subtracting two infinities. For <functions> taking values in the <complex numbers>, integrate the <real part> and <imaginary part> separately.

Consider the <Dirichlet function> $f=\mathbf1_{\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]}$ on $[0,1]$. It is a <simple function>, and
$$
\int_{[0,1]}f\,d\lambda=\lambda(\mathbb Q\cap[0,1])=0.
$$
Its <Riemann integral> does not exist. For every <partition of an interval> $0=x_0<\cdots<x_n=1$, each nondegenerate $[x_{i-1},x_i]$ contains both <rational numbers> and <irrational numbers>, by their respective <density of the rational numbers> and <density of the irrational numbers>. Thus its <infimum> there is $0$ and its <supremum> is $1$. The <lower Darboux sum> is $\sum_i0(x_i-x_{i-1})=0$, whereas the <upper Darboux sum> is $\sum_i1(x_i-x_{i-1})=1$. Refinement never closes this gap. A finite scatterplot cannot represent this behavior: the assertion concerns every subinterval and every <partition of an interval>.

Another view builds the <Lebesgue integral> from horizontal layers. For any nonnegative <measurable function>, the <layer cake representation> states
$$
\int_X f\,d\mu=\int_0^\infty\mu\bigl(\{x\in X:f(x)>t\}\bigr)\,dt.
$$
Both sides may be infinite. The right side integrates the <measure> of a <superlevel set> against <Lebesgue measure> in the height variable $t$. See https://www.math.cmu.edu/~gautam/sj/teaching/2022-23/720-measure/pdfs/measure-twocolumn.pdf[Gautam Iyer's measure theory notes, Theorem 9.5].

For $f(x)=x^2$ on $[0,1]$ with <Lebesgue measure>, the <superlevel set> at $0\leq t<1$ is $(\sqrt t,1]$, of <Lebesgue measure> $1-\sqrt t$; for $t\geq1$ it is empty. Consequently
$$
\int_0^1 x^2\,d\lambda(x)=\int_0^1(1-\sqrt t)\,dt
=\left[t-\frac23t^{3/2}\right]_0^1=\frac13.
$$
At height $t=1/4$, the layer extends from $x=1/2$ to $x=1$, a width of $1/2$. The picture shows why the same area can be accumulated vertically or horizontally. In a <probability space>, the identical formula expresses the <expected value> of a nonnegative <random variable> through its exceedance <probabilities>.

\Image[lebesgue-level-sets.png]
{title=Integrating $x^2$ by horizontal layers and their widths}

A proof outline avoids extra assumptions on the <measure space>. For a nonnegative <simple function> $s=\sum_ja_j\mathbf1_{A_j}$ with disjoint <measurable sets> $A_j$, the <superlevel set> has <measure> $\sum_{a_j>t}\mu(A_j)$. Integrating this finite sum gives $\sum_ja_j\mu(A_j)=\int s\,d\mu$. Next use <approximation by nonnegative simple functions> $s_n\uparrow f$. For every $t$, the <superlevel sets> $\{s_n>t\}$ increase to $\{f>t\}$. <Continuity from below of a measure> and the <monotone convergence theorem> on the height variable give the formula for $f$.

= Integral average
{parent=Lebesgue integral}

The <integral average> of an integrable function over a measurable set $E$ of finite positive measure is $\langle v\rangle_E=|E|^{-1}\int_Ev$. Normalization removes the volume factor when comparing estimates on balls of different radii.

= Volume average
{parent=Integral average}
{title2=$\langle f\rangle_V=V^{-1}\int_V f\,d^3x$}

A <volume average> is the normalized <integral average> of a field over a three-dimensional region of finite positive <volume>. For a fixed region, differentiating in time commutes with this average under the usual regularity conditions. The <divergence theorem> converts the average of a divergence into its net boundary flux divided by volume.

= Simple function
{parent=Lebesgue integral}
{title2=$s=\sum_{j=1}^m a_j\mathbf1_{A_j}$}
{wiki}

A simple function is a <measurable function> whose <image> is a <finite set>. Its value sets $A_j=\{x:s(x)=a_j\}$ form a finite <measurable partition>. For nonnegative $a_j$, its <Lebesgue integral> is $\sum_ja_j\mu(A_j)$, with the convention $0\cdot\infty=0$. The pieces need not be intervals, or even <connected> <sets>. See https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-102-introduction-to-functional-analysis-spring-2021/ebbbc59aa40252dff3d4ec766c057451_MIT18_102s21_lec10.pdf[MIT lecture 10, Definitions 100 and 106].

For example, on $[0,1]$ let $s$ be $2$ on $[0,1/4)$, $1$ on $[1/4,3/4)$, and $0$ on $[3/4,1]$. Its <Lebesgue integral> is $2(1/4)+1(1/2)=1$. Its <superlevel sets> have <Lebesgue measure> $3/4$ for $0\leq t<1$, $1/4$ for $1\leq t<2$, and $0$ thereafter. The <layer cake representation> gives the same answer: $(3/4)(1)+(1/4)(1)=1$. These two calculations are the building blocks of the general <Lebesgue integral>.

= Sigma-algebra
{parent=Measure theory}
{title2=$\mathcal F$}
{wiki}

A sigma-algebra on a <set> $\Omega$ is a collection $\mathcal F$ of <subsets> such that $\Omega\in\mathcal F$, $A\in\mathcal F$ implies $\Omega\setminus A\in\mathcal F$, and $A_n\in\mathcal F$ for all $n$ implies $\bigcup_nA_n\in\mathcal F$. Thus it contains the <empty set> and is closed under countable <intersections> as well. This is precisely the closure needed to apply <countable additivity> without leaving the domain of a <measure>.

For $\Omega=\{a,b,c\}$,
$$
\mathcal F=\{\varnothing,\{a\},\{b,c\},\Omega\}
$$
is a <sigma-algebra>. It describes observations that distinguish $a$ from the pair $\{b,c\}$, but cannot distinguish $b$ from $c$. The <power set> provides a finer <sigma-algebra> with all eight <subsets>. Both are valid; choosing a <sigma-algebra> records which questions the model can answer.

On the <real numbers>, the <Borel sigma-algebra> contains all <open sets> and everything generated from them by these operations. The <Lebesgue measurable sets> form a larger <sigma-algebra>, including all <subsets> of <Borel null sets>. Closure under <countable unions> accommodates constructions involving an entire <sequence> of observations or approximations.

= Sigma-algebra generated by a collection
{parent=Sigma-algebra}
{title2=$\sigma(\mathcal I)=\bigcap_{\mathcal A\supseteq\mathcal I,\ \mathcal A\text{ a sigma-algebra}}\mathcal A$}

The <sigma-algebra generated by a collection> is the smallest <sigma-algebra> containing all sets in that collection. The displayed intersection is over a nonempty family, since the power set is one candidate. An intersection of sigma-algebras contains the whole space and is closed under complements and countable unions, proving it is a sigma-algebra and establishing minimality.

For example, the half-lines $(-\infty,x]$ generate the <Borel sigma-algebra> on the real line. They are Borel, and conversely $(-\infty,b)=\bigcup_n(-\infty,b-1/n]$ and $(a,b)=(-\infty,b)\setminus(-\infty,a]$ recover every open interval. Preimages of these half-lines similarly generate the <sigma-algebra generated by a random variable>.

= Trace sigma-algebra
{parent=Sigma-algebra}
{title2=$\mathcal F|_E=\{E\cap A:A\in\mathcal F\}$}

For a <measurable space> $(\Omega,\mathcal F)$ and any <subset> $E\subseteq\Omega$, the <trace sigma-algebra> is the displayed family of <subsets> of $E$. It contains $E=E\cap\Omega$, is closed under relative <complements> since $E\setminus(E\cap A)=E\cap(\Omega\setminus A)$, and is closed under <countable unions>. If $E\in\mathcal F$, it equals $\{B\in\mathcal F:B\subseteq E\}$. Without that measurability assumption, the traces need not be <measurable sets> in the original <sigma-algebra>.

= Sub-sigma-algebra
{parent=Sigma-algebra}
{title2=$\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal F$}

A <sigma-algebra> $\mathcal G$ on the same underlying <set> as $\mathcal F$, with $\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal F$. In <probability theory> it represents information retained from the original <probability space> and is a possible conditioning collection for a <conditional expectation>.

= Sigma-algebra generated by a random variable
{parent=Sigma-algebra}
{title2=$\sigma(X)=\{X^{-1}(B):B\in\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\}$}

For a real <random variable> $X$ on a <probability space>, $\sigma(X)$ is the smallest <sigma-algebra> making $X$ a <measurable function> into the <Borel sigma-algebra> on $\mathbb R$. The collection of all <inverse images> of <Borel sets> is already a <sigma-algebra>, since <inverse images> preserve <complements> and <countable unions>; this proves the displayed formula without completing any <measure>.

= Sigma-algebra of a finite-valued random vector
{parent=Sigma-algebra generated by a random variable}
{title2=$|\sigma(V)|=2^{|V(\Omega)|}$}

Let a <random vector> $V$ have a finite actual range of $q$ values. Its nonempty fibres partition the <sample space>, and every <event> in $\sigma(V)$ is a <union> of those fibres. Thus each fibre is an <atom of a sigma-algebra> and $\sigma(V)$ has exactly $2^q$ elements. This assertion concerns the actual range; additional values attained only on <null sets> must still be counted when working with an uncompleted <sigma-algebra> literally.

To verify the claim, the fibres belong to $\sigma(V)$ as preimages of singleton <Borel sets>. Their <unions> form a finite <sigma-algebra> making $V$ <measurable>, so minimality of the generated <sigma-algebra> gives the reverse inclusion. Distinct subcollections have distinct <unions> because the fibres are nonempty and disjoint.

Consequently a finite history of finite-valued observations generates a finite <natural filtration> at each fixed time. It may be strictly smaller than the <filtration> of its driving continuous observations: a single <uniform random variable> already generates infinitely many distinct <events> $\{U\leq t\}$ with different <probabilities>.

= An antisymmetric kernel with unequal iterated integrals
{parent=Fubini's theorem}
{title2=$f(x,y)=\frac{x-y}{(x+y)^3}$}

The function $(x-y)/(x+y)^3$ on the <unit square> is not <Lebesgue integrable>. On $0<y<x/2$, its absolute value is at least $4/(27x^2)$, whose <area> <integral> diverges logarithmically. Its two signed <iterated integrals> nevertheless exist, ignoring null boundary sections, and equal $1/2$ and $-1/2$. This illustrates why <absolute integrability> is required for <Fubini's theorem>.

= Finite iterated integrals without joint integrability
{parent=Fubini's theorem}
{title2=$f(x,y)=xy/(x^2+y^2)^3$}

Define the displayed <function> away from the origin and set $f(0,0)=0$. Every section in either coordinate is integrable on $[-1,1]$ and is an <odd function>, so both orders of <iterated integrals> on $[-1,1]^2$ are zero. However, on the annulus $\varepsilon<\sqrt{x^2+y^2}<1$, <polar integration> gives
$$
\int|f|=\int_\varepsilon^1r^{-3}\,dr\int_0^{2\pi}|\cos\theta\sin\theta|\,d\theta=\varepsilon^{-2}-1.
$$
Thus the <function> is not <Lebesgue integrable> jointly. Reflection interchanges its <positive part> and <negative part>, whose <integrals> are both infinite. Existence and even agreement of finite <iterated integrals> do not establish the <absolute integrability> hypothesis of <Fubini's theorem>.

= Weighted dilation kernel
{parent=Fubini's theorem}
{title2=$G(x,y)=a(x)f(b(x)y)$}

Let $f$ be a <Borel measurable function> in <L1 space> on $\mathbb R$, and let $a,b$ be measurable real functions of $x$ with $b(x)>0$ and $W=\int_{\mathbb R}|a(x)|/b(x)\,dx<\infty$. The joint integrand $G(x,y)=a(x)f(b(x)y)$ is measurable, and <Tonelli's theorem> followed by <Lebesgue substitution on an interval> gives
$$
\|G\|_{L^1(\mathbb R^2)}=W\|f\|_{L^1(\mathbb R)}.
$$
For each fixed $x$, substitute $z=b(x)y$ in the inner $y$ integral. The absolute Jacobian is $1/b(x)$, leaving $|a(x)|\|f\|_1/b(x)$. Integrating in $x$ proves the formula and <absolute integrability>. <Fubini's theorem> then permits both orders of signed integration, giving
$$
\iint_{\mathbb R^2}G(x,y)\,dx\,dy=
\left(\int_{\mathbb R}\frac{a(x)}{b(x)}\,dx\right)
\left(\int_{\mathbb R}f(y)\,dy\right).
$$
The one-dimensional sections are integrable for <almost every> value of the other variable; the theorem does not assert integrability of every section.

= Almost-everywhere measurability of Lebesgue sections
{c}
{synonym}

For a planar <Lebesgue measurable set> $B$, its vertical section $B_x$ is a one-dimensional <Lebesgue measurable set> for $x$ <almost everywhere>. Write $B\mathbin\triangle C\subseteq Z$ with $C$ a planar <Borel set> and $Z$ a Borel <Lebesgue null set>. Every section $C_x$ and $Z_x$ is Borel; <Tonelli's theorem> gives $\int m(Z_x)\,dx=m_2(Z)=0$. Thus $m(Z_x)=0$ for $x$ <almost everywhere>, and $B_x\mathbin\triangle C_x\subseteq Z_x$ proves the assertion by <completeness of Lebesgue measure>. No finite-measure condition on $B$ is needed.

Exceptional sections need not be measurable: $\{0\}\times A$ is a planar <Lebesgue null set> for every $A\subseteq\mathbb R$, even when $A$ is a <non-measurable set>. Conversely, if both $A_1$ and $A_2$ are non-measurable, $A_1\times A_2$ cannot be measurable. Otherwise every $x\in A_1$ would have to lie in the null exceptional set, making $A_1$ measurable by <completeness of Lebesgue measure>.

= Frullani integral
{c}
{parent=Fubini's theorem}
{wiki}
{title2=$\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-ax}-e^{-bx}}x\,dx=\log(b/a)$}

For $0<a<b$, the nonnegative function $e^{-xy}\mathbf1_{\{x>0,\ a\le y\le b\}}$ is measurable and has integral $\int_a^bdy/y=\log(b/a)$. <Tonelli theorem> first proves integrability without assuming it; <Fubini theorem> then permits the order of integration to be exchanged. Integrating in $y$ gives the stated <Frullani integral>. The cancellation between the exponentials is essential at $x=0$.

= Hyperbolic-secant Frullani integral
{parent=Frullani integral}
{title2=$\int_0^\infty[\operatorname{sech}x-\operatorname{sech}(\alpha x)]\,dx/x=\ln\alpha$}

For $\alpha>1$, write $[\operatorname{sech}x-\operatorname{sech}(\alpha x)]/x=\int_1^\alpha\operatorname{sech}(xy)\tanh(xy)dy$. The integrand is nonnegative, so <Tonelli's theorem> permits reversal. Substitution $u=xy$ makes the inner $x$ integral $1/y$ because $\int_0^\infty\operatorname{sech}u\tanh u\,du=1$. Integrating $dy/y$ proves the formula. The original difference is $O(x^2)$ at zero, making division by $x$ integrable. For $0<\alpha<1$, substitution and reversal of the two terms give the same $\ln\alpha$ result; at $\alpha=1$ both sides vanish.

= Lebesgue measure
{parent=Sigma-finite uniqueness theorem for measures}
{c}
{title2=$\lambda$}
{wiki}

Lebesgue measure on the <real numbers> extends interval length: $\lambda([a,b])=b-a$ for $a\leq b$. Its restriction to the <Borel sigma-algebra> is the unique translation-invariant <sigma-finite measure> on that <sigma-algebra> normalized by $\lambda((0,1])=1$. The full <Lebesgue measure> is the <completion of a measure> of this restriction; its domain is the <Lebesgue measurable sets>. Translation invariance follows by comparing a translated <Borel measure> on half-open intervals and applying the <uniqueness theorem for measures>. For the construction and interval properties, see https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-102-introduction-to-functional-analysis-spring-2021/1bf275c0667d3e903e4de22b9fb4481b_MIT18_102s21_lec8.pdf[MIT lecture 8].

Every <singleton> has <Lebesgue measure> zero: $\{x\}\subseteq[x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon]$ gives $0\leq\lambda(\{x\})\leq2\varepsilon$ for every $\varepsilon>0$. Enumerating the <countable set> $\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]$ and applying <countable additivity> yields
$$
\lambda(\mathbb Q\cap[0,1])=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda(\{q_n\})=0,
\qquad\lambda([0,1])=1.
$$
Thus a <dense set> can be a <null set>, and a <union> of <null sets> indexed by an <uncountable set> can have positive <Lebesgue measure>. The distinction explains both the <Dirichlet function> example and why <almost everywhere> is weaker than everywhere.

= Lebesgue inner measure
{parent=Lebesgue measure}
{c}
{title2=$m_*(E)=\sup\{m(K):K\subseteq E,\ K\text{ compact}\}$}

The <Lebesgue inner measure> approximates a subset of the real line from within by <compact sets>, while <Lebesgue outer measure> approximates it from outside by countable interval covers. For a finite closed interval $I$ containing $E$,
$$
m_*(E)=m(I)-m^*(I\setminus E).
$$
Indeed, every compact $K\subseteq E$ gives $m^*(I\setminus E)\leq m(I)-m(K)$. Conversely, an open cover of $I\setminus E$ with total length arbitrarily close to its <outer measure> has compact complement in $I$ lying inside $E$, giving the reverse inequality in the limit.

For $m^*(E)<\infty$, equality of inner and outer measures holds precisely when $E$ is <Lebesgue measurable>. For an unbounded set, measurability is equivalently checked on all bounded intersections $E\cap[-n,n]$. Equality $m_*(E)=m^*(E)=\infty$ alone is insufficient: adjoining a bounded nonmeasurable subset to a disjoint half-line makes both quantities infinite without making the union measurable.

= Translation invariance of Lebesgue measure
{parent=Lebesgue measure}
{title2=$m(E+t)=m(E)$}

Translating a <Lebesgue measurable set> by a real number preserves measurability and its <Lebesgue measure>. <Translation invariance of Lebesgue outer measure> and <Carathéodory's criterion> give the measurability assertion and equality. Applying the result to <indicator functions>, then <simple functions>, and finally increasing simple approximations gives $\int_{\mathbb R}u(x-t)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb R}u(x)\,dx$ for every nonnegative <Lebesgue measurable function>. For a <Lebesgue integrable function>, apply the identity to its <positive part> and <negative part>.

= Essential closure
{parent=Lebesgue measure}
{title2=$\operatorname{esscl}_\Omega A$}

For a <Lebesgue measurable set> $A$ in an <open set> $\Omega\subseteq\mathbb R^n$, its essential closure consists of the points whose every sufficiently small ball has positive <Lebesgue measure> intersection with $A$. Equivalently,
$$
\operatorname{esscl}_\Omega A=\operatorname{supp}\mathbf1_A,
$$
where the right side is the <support of a distribution>. To see the equivalence, a ball on which $A$ is null gives zero pairings with all <test functions>. If $A$ has positive measure in a ball, cover that ball by smaller compactly contained balls and choose a nonnegative <cutoff function> positive on one meeting $A$ in positive measure; its pairing is positive. Thus essential closure is relatively closed and unchanged by modifying $A$ on a <Lebesgue null set>. For an <open set> $A$ it equals its relative closure.

= Null-set limsup cover by finite arc families
{parent=Lebesgue measure}
{title2=$E\subset\limsup_j F_j,\qquad\sum_{I\in F_j}|I|<\epsilon_j$}

For any circle null set and any positive sequence $\epsilon_j$, take infinitely many countable covers with summable total lengths. Regard their arcs as a two-index array. Choose increasing finite-square cutoffs so the length sum outside each square is smaller than the next desired bound. The finite square annuli then give finite arc families with the stated small lengths. Every point belongs to some arc in every row, hence to infinitely many annular families. This handles dense and nonclosed null sets without claiming that they have finite small covers.

= Compact batching of a small open set
{parent=Lebesgue measure}

An open subset of the circle can be covered by countably many closed subarcs with disjoint interiors. Grouping a fixed enumeration into finite batches gives compact sets covering every point of the open set. By taking each finite batch long enough, the remaining total measure can be forced below any prescribed positive sequence tending to zero. This controls the measure of subsequent batches without assuming that a prescribed null subset is itself a countable union of compact null sets.

= Inner regularity of Lebesgue measure
{parent=Lebesgue measure}

The <Lebesgue measure> of a measurable set is the supremum of the measures of its compact subsets. In particular, an open set of finite positive measure contains a compact subset with at least half its measure. Together with a finite subcover, this turns a finite <Wiener covering lemma> estimate into an estimate for arbitrary open superlevel sets.

= Divisibility of Lebesgue measure
{parent=Lebesgue measure}
{title2=$\lambda(B)=b$}

If $E\subseteq[0,1]$ is a <Lebesgue measurable set> and $0\leq b\leq\lambda(E)$, some measurable <subset> $B\subseteq E$ has <Lebesgue measure> $b$. The function $F(t)=\lambda(E\cap[0,t])$ has <Lipschitz continuity> with constant $1$, starts at $0$ and ends at $\lambda(E)$; the <intermediate value theorem> proves the assertion. Applying the assertion successively to remaining portions of $E$ gives pairwise disjoint measurable pieces with any finite list of nonnegative measures whose sum is at most $\lambda(E)$. This is a concrete consequence of <non-atomic measure> structure, which fails for general <measures> containing an <atom of a measure>.

= Cumulative measure of a finite-measure set
{parent=Divisibility of Lebesgue measure}
{title2=$H_E(t)=m(E\cap(-\infty,t])$}

For a <Lebesgue measurable set> $E\subseteq\mathbb R$ of finite <measure>, the displayed <function> satisfies $|H_E(t)-H_E(s)|\leq|t-s|$. Indeed, for $s<t$, <finite additivity> and <monotonicity of a measure> give $H_E(t)-H_E(s)=m(E\cap(s,t])\leq t-s$. Thus $H_E$ is a nondecreasing <Lipschitz function> and is <uniformly continuous> even if $E$ is unbounded.

<Continuity from above of a measure> gives $H_E(t)\to0$ as $t\to-\infty$, while <continuity from below of a measure> gives $H_E(t)\to m(E)$ as $t\to\infty$. Every strictly intermediate value is attained by the <intermediate value theorem>. The corresponding half-line cut of $E$ realizes any prescribed <measure> between zero and $m(E)$; the endpoints are realized by the <empty set> and $E$ itself.

= Completeness of Lebesgue measure
{parent=Lebesgue measure}

Every <subset> $B$ of a <Lebesgue null set> is a <Lebesgue measurable set> and is itself a <null set>. In fact the same conclusion follows directly from $m^*(B)=0$, without assuming measurability in advance. For any $A\subseteq\mathbb R$, <monotonicity of Lebesgue outer measure> gives $m^*(A\cap B)=0$ and $m^*(A\setminus B)\leq m^*(A)$. Therefore
$$
m^*(A\cap B)+m^*(A\setminus B)\leq m^*(A).
$$
The reverse inequality follows from <countable subadditivity of Lebesgue outer measure>, applied to the union $A=(A\cap B)\cup(A\setminus B)$. This is <Carathéodory's criterion>, proving measurability. If $B$ is a subset of an outer-null $N$, its outer measure is zero by the same monotonicity. Thus the restriction of outer measure to measurable sets is a <complete measure>.

= Mean-square integral

An ordinary time integral of <random variables> taken in the <L2 space> of the underlying <probability space>. = Strongly measurable function

A function into a <Banach space> is strongly measurable if, outside a null set, it is a pointwise norm limit of measurable simple functions. = Essential supremum
{title2=$\operatorname{ess\,sup}_\mu f$}
{parent=Measure theory}

= Essential suprema
{synonym}

The <essential supremum> is the upper bound for a <measurable function> after disregarding <null sets> for a specified <measure> $\mu$. Its definition is
$$
\operatorname{ess\,sup}_\mu f=\inf\{M\in\mathbb R:\mu(\{x:f(x)>M\})=0\},
$$
with values allowed in the <extended real number line>. A <supremum> controls every value; an <essential supremum> controls values <almost everywhere>. It therefore depends on the chosen <measure>, as well as on the <function>. https://www.math.ucdavis.edu/~hunter/measure_theory/measure_notes_ch7.pdf[Hunter, Measure Theory, Definition 7.3: essential supremum]{ref}.

For a worked example, use <Lebesgue measure> on $[0,1]$ and put
$$
f(x)=\begin{cases}10,&x=1/2,\\x,&x\ne1/2.\end{cases}
$$
Its <supremum> is ten, attained at $1/2$. But **its <essential supremum> is one**: $f\leq1$ outside the <singleton> $\{1/2\}$, which is a <null set>. Every candidate $M<1$ fails, because the <interval> $(\max(0,M),1)$ has positive <Lebesgue measure> and includes values larger than $M$; changing one point cannot remove that interval. In particular, $\mu(\{f>0.8\})=0.2$, while $\mu(\{f>1\})=0$.

\Image[essential-bounds-ignore-null-sets.png]
{title=A <supremum> sees an isolated exceptional value; an <essential supremum> depends on positive <measure>}
{description=Left: $f(1/2)=10$, with the ordinary value $1/2$ excluded at that input. Right: a zoom shows that values above $0.8$ still occur on an <interval> of <Lebesgue measure> $0.2$. The isolated value at ten lies outside the zoom. Thus the <essential supremum> is one, despite the pointwise <supremum> being ten.}

Why is this useful? <Lebesgue integrals> ignore changes on <null sets>, and <Lp spaces> identify <functions> that agree <almost everywhere>. The <L-infinity norm> uses $\operatorname{ess\,sup}|f|$ so it respects that identification. For this nonnegative example the <supremum norm> is ten and the <L-infinity norm> is one; replacing $f(1/2)$ by $1/2$ changes the former while leaving the latter unchanged. https://www.math.ucdavis.edu/~hunter/measure_theory/measure_notes_ch7.pdf[Hunter, Measure Theory, Definition 7.4: the L-infinity norm]{ref}.

The <measure> qualification is essential. For $\mu=\delta_{1/2}$, the <Dirac measure> concentrated at the exceptional input, that <singleton> has <measure> one and this <function> has <essential supremum> ten. A set ignored by one <measure> need not be ignored by another. The lower counterpart is the <essential infimum>, with $\operatorname{ess\,inf}f=-\operatorname{ess\,sup}(-f)$.

The space $L^p$ consists of measurable functions with finite $p$-norm, identified when they agree almost everywhere. This quotient is necessary because the integral seminorm vanishes on functions supported on null sets.

= L1 Kolmogorov-Riesz compactness criterion
{c}
{parent=Lp space}
{title2=$S\Subset L^1(\mathbb R)\iff S\text{ bounded, uniformly tight and translation continuous}$}

A subset of $L^1(\mathbb R)$ is relatively <norm> compact exactly when it is bounded, its absolute-integral tails tend to zero uniformly, and $\sup_{f\in S}\|f(\cdot+h)-f\|_1\to0$ as $h\to0$. Necessity follows by taking a finite <norm> net and using the tail decay and <translation continuity in Lp> of its finitely many centers; the error contributes at most twice the net radius. For sufficiency, convolve with a triangular unit-integral kernel of width $\delta$. Translation continuity gives uniform approximation in $L^1$, while boundedness gives a common supremum bound and a common Lipschitz constant for the smoothed family. The original tail condition controls its tails outside a slightly enlarged interval. On that interval <Arzelà-Ascoli theorem> gives a finite uniform net. Extend its centers by zero outside the interval to obtain a finite $L^1$ net, and combine the smoothing and tail errors. Completeness of $L^1$ turns <total boundedness> into <relative compactness>.

= Density of continuous functions in Lp on an interval
{parent=Lp space}
{title2=$\overline{C([a,b])}^{\|\cdot\|_p}=L^p((a,b)),\quad1\leq p<\infty$}

For a finite interval $(a,b)$ with <Lebesgue measure>, restrictions of <continuous functions> on $[a,b]$ form a <dense subspace> of the <Lp space> $L^p((a,b))$ for $1\leq p<\infty$. One proof first approximates by <simple functions>. The <regularity of Lebesgue measure> approximates each of their <measurable sets> in measure by a finite union of intervals. An interval's <indicator function> is approximated by <continuous functions> taking values in $[0,1]$ and differing from that <indicator function> only in endpoint strips of total length tending to zero. Their <Lp norm> error is at most the $1/p$ power of that length. The <triangle inequality> completes the approximation of each <simple function>.

= Typewriter sequence
{parent=Lp space}
{title2=$f_{2^k+j}=\mathbf1_{[j2^{-k},(j+1)2^{-k})}$}

= Dyadic typewriter sequence
{synonym}

On $(0,1)$, for $k\geq0$ and $0\leq j<2^k$, the displayed <indicator functions> form a sequence that converges to zero in every finite <Lp norm> while failing to converge pointwise anywhere. Indeed, for $1\leq p<\infty$,
$$
\|f_{2^k+j}\|_p^p=2^{-k}.
$$
Every point belongs to exactly one interval in each dyadic block, and for $k\geq1$ it misses at least one other interval in that block. Thus every point sees infinitely many ones and infinitely many zeros. Half-open intervals specify the behaviour at dyadic endpoints. This separates <norm convergence> in an <Lp space> from <pointwise convergence> and convergence <almost everywhere>.

= Unbounded typewriter sequence with vanishing L1 norm
{parent=Typewriter sequence}
{title2=$\|f_n\|_1\to0,\quad\limsup_nf_n(x)=\infty$}

For each $m\geq1$, enumerate the $m^2$ half-open intervals $[j/m^2,(j+1)/m^2)$ partitioning $[0,1)$ and use the functions $m$ times their indicator functions. Concatenate these finite blocks in increasing $m$. Every function in block $m$ has <L1 norm> $1/m$, which tends to zero. Every $x\in(0,1)$ is in one interval of every block, so the values at that point have a subsequence increasing to infinity. Thus small integral norm does not bound the pointwise upper limit, even when every function is nonnegative. In contrast, <Fatou's lemma> prevents a strictly positive lower limit everywhere on an interval when the norms tend to zero.

= L1 space
{c}

= Summable L1 norms imply almost-everywhere absolute convergence
{parent=L1 space}
{title2=$\sum_n\|a_n\|_1<\infty\Longrightarrow\sum_n|a_n(x)|<\infty\text{ a.e.}$}

Suppose measurable $a_n$ on a <Lebesgue measurable set> $E$ satisfy $\sum_n\int_E|a_n|<\infty$. The nonnegative partial sums $S_N=\sum_{n\leq N}|a_n|$ increase to a measurable extended-valued $S$. The <monotone convergence theorem> gives $\int_ES=\sum_n\int_E|a_n|<\infty$, forcing $S<\infty$ <almost everywhere>. Each $a_n$ is finite almost everywhere too; discard the union of these countably many <null sets> when defining signed partial sums. The <series> $\sum_na_n$ then converges absolutely outside a null set.

= Integration of a series with summable L1 norms
{parent=Summable L1 norms imply almost-everywhere absolute convergence}
{title2=$\int\sum_n a_n=\sum_n\int a_n$}

Suppose $\sum_n\|a_n\|_1<\infty$. By <summable L1 norms imply almost-everywhere absolute convergence>, let $A=\sum_na_n$ where the series converges absolutely, and choose zero on the exceptional <null set>. Then $A$ is a <Lebesgue measurable function> and $|A|\leq S=\sum_n|a_n|$ almost everywhere. Since $S$ is <Lebesgue integrable>, so is $A$. The signed partial sums are bounded in absolute value by $S$, so the <dominated convergence theorem> proves termwise integration. It also gives the quantitative <L1 norm> estimate
$$
\left\|A-\sum_{n=1}^Na_n\right\|_1\leq\sum_{n>N}\|a_n\|_1\longrightarrow0.
$$
The numerical series of integrals is absolutely convergent because $|\int a_n|\leq\int|a_n|$.

For example, on $(0,1)$ take $a_n(x)=(-1)^n n\mathbf1_{(0,2^{-n})}(x)$. Its <L1 norm> is $n2^{-n}$, and these norms sum to two. Every positive $x$ lies in only finitely many supports, so the series is pointwise finite, although its terms are not uniformly small. Termwise integration gives $\int_0^1\sum_na_n=\sum_n(-1)^nn2^{-n}=-2/9$, by differentiating the <geometric series>. This illustrates why a small <L1 norm> can permit tall terms on short intervals.

= Sum of Lp spaces
{parent=Lp space}
{title2=$L^{p_0}+L^{p_1}$}

The sum consists of <measurable functions> $f=g+h$ with $g\in L^{p_0}$ and $h\in L^{p_1}$, with <norm> $\inf_{f=g+h}(\|g\|_{p_0}+\|h\|_{p_1})$. It is a <Banach space>. One construction identifies it with the quotient of $L^{p_0}\oplus_1L^{p_1}$ by the closed kernel of the addition map. Bounded endpoint operators are therefore bounded on the corresponding sum spaces.

= Threshold decomposition between Lp spaces
{parent=Sum of Lp spaces}
{title2=$g=f\mathbf1_{\{|f|>a\}},\quad h=f\mathbf1_{\{|f|\leq a\}}$}

If $p_0<p<p_1$, the displayed split of an $L^p$ function gives $\|g\|_{p_0}\leq a^{1-p/p_0}\|f\|_p^{p/p_0}$ and $\|h\|_{p_1}\leq a^{1-p/p_1}\|f\|_p^{p/p_1}$. Weighted multiplication with $1/p=(1-\theta)/p_0+\theta/p_1$ cancels the threshold, yielding $\|g\|_{p_0}^{1-\theta}\|h\|_{p_1}^{\theta}\leq\|f\|_p$. Choosing $a=\|f\|_p$ also gives the continuous inclusion $\|f\|_{L^{p_0}+L^{p_1}}\leq2\|f\|_p$. The analogous split works if the larger endpoint is infinity.

= Sine sequence is not Cauchy in the integral norm
{parent=Lp space}
{title2=$f_n(x)=\sin(nx)$}

On $[0,1]$, the <continuous functions> $f_n(x)=\sin(nx)$ do not form a <Cauchy sequence> in the <Lp space> with $p=1$. Put $d_n=f_n-f_{2n}$. The identity $\int_0^1d_n^2\,dx=1-\sin(2n)/(4n)-\sin(4n)/(8n)-\sin n/n+\sin(3n)/(3n)$ tends to $1$. Since $|d_n|\leq2$, one has $\int_0^1|d_n|\,dx\geq\frac12\int_0^1d_n^2\,dx$, which stays bounded away from zero. Thus arbitrarily late pairs are separated in the integral <norm>; in particular the sequence cannot converge in that <norm>.

= Translation continuity in Lp on a locally compact group
{parent=Lp space}

For left <Haar measure>, $T_xf(t)=f(x^{-1}t)$ is an isometry on $L^p(G)$, $1\leq p<\infty$. For compactly supported continuous functions, uniform continuity on a slightly enlarged compact support proves norm continuity. Approximation by those functions and the triangle inequality extend it to all $L^p$. The continuous image of a compact set of translation parameters is consequently compact in $L^p$.

= Derivative of a power of the Lp norm
{parent=Lp space}
{title2=$\frac{d}{dt}\|u+tv\|_p^p\big|_{t=0}=p\operatorname{Re}\int|u|^{p-2}\overline u\,v\,d\mu$}

For $1<p<\infty$ and $u,v$ in an <Lp space>, the derivative with respect to the real scalar $t$ has the displayed form; the integrand is zero where $u=0$. The pointwise <chain rule> gives the derivative, while the <Holder inequality> makes $(|u|+|v|)^{p-1}|v|$ integrable and permits the <dominated convergence theorem>. = Closest point theorem for a closed convex subset of Lp
{parent=Lp space}
{title2=$h=\operatorname*{argmin}_{g\in K}\|g-f\|_p$}

For $2\le p<\infty$, a nonempty <closed> <convex set> $K$ in an <Lp space> has exactly one nearest point to every $f$. A <minimizing sequence> $g_j$ is a <Cauchy sequence>: the <Clarkson inequality> bounds $\|g_j-g_k\|_p^p$ by $2^{p-1}(\|g_j-f\|_p^p+\|g_k-f\|_p^p)-2^pd^p$, where $d$ is the infimum of the distances. <Completeness of Lp spaces> and the <closed set> property give a limit in $K$. Applying the same inequality to two minimizers proves uniqueness. Nonemptiness is essential.

= Completeness of Lp spaces
{parent=Lp space}
{title2=$1\leq p<\infty\ \Longrightarrow\ L^p\text{ is Banach}$}

From an <Lp norm> Cauchy sequence choose a subsequence with successive difference norms at most $2^{-j}$. <Minkowski inequality> and <monotone convergence theorem> make the sum of the absolute differences an $L^p$ function, hence finite almost everywhere. The telescoping series defines an $L^p$ limit; the geometric bound on its tail proves norm convergence of the subsequence, and the original Cauchy property proves convergence of the whole sequence. No sigma-finiteness assumption is needed.

= Weak Lq space
{parent=Lp space}
{title2=$L^{q,\infty}$}

For $1<q<\infty$, the weak Lq space consists of measurable functions for which $A_q(f)=\sup_{t>0}t|\{|f|>t\}|^{1/q}$ is finite. This distribution-function expression is a <quasi-norm>. The equivalent norm $N_q(f)=\sup_{0<|E|<\infty}|E|^{-1/q'}\int_E|f|$ satisfies $A_q\leq N_q\leq q'A_q$, with $q'=q/(q-1)$. The <layer cake representation> bounds the integral by $\int_0^\infty\min(|E|,(A_q/t)^q)dt=q'A_q|E|^{1/q'}$. The lower bound uses finite-measure subsets of a superlevel set.

= Integral norm for weak Lq
{parent=Weak Lq space}
{title2=$N_q(f)=\sup_E|E|^{-1/q'}\int_E|f|$}

The integral expression for the <weak Lq space> is homogeneous, positive definite up to equality almost everywhere, and obeys the <triangle inequality> by integrating $|f+g|\leq|f|+|g|$ over each set. Its exponent is $-1/q'$, not $-1/q$. The sharp comparison constant with the distribution-function quasi-norm is $q'$; the power function $x^{-1/q}$ on $(0,\infty)$ attains that ratio on initial intervals.

= Mixed Lebesgue norm

A <mixed Lebesgue norm> first takes the <Lp norm> in one variable and then the norm in another. = Locally square-integrable function

= Translation continuity in Lp
{parent=Lp space}
{title2=$\|f(\cdot+h)-f\|_p\to0\quad(h\to0)$}

For $1\leq p<\infty$ and $f\in L^p(\mathbb R^d)$, translations converge to $f$ in the <Lp space> <norm>. First prove this for continuous compactly supported <functions>, using uniform continuity and a common bounded support. Their density in <Lp space>, together with the norm-preserving translation map and the <triangle inequality>, proves the assertion for arbitrary $f$. = Translation continuity in L1 on the circle
{parent=Lp space}

For $f\in L^1(\mathbb T,m)$ with $m$ normalized <Lebesgue measure>, translation on the <circle group> is continuous in the <L1 norm>: $\|f(\cdot+t)-f\|_1\to0$ as $t\to0$ modulo one. = Lp norms converge to the supremum norm
{c}
{parent=Lp space}

On a measure space of finite measure, a bounded measurable function satisfies the displayed limit, where the right side is the <essential supremum> of its absolute value. = Moment ratios converge to the essential supremum

= Logarithmic reweighting under dominated convergence
{parent=Dominated convergence theorem}
{title2=$\left\|\dfrac{\log(n+x)}n u(x)\right\|_1\leq\dfrac{\log n\,\|u\|_1+\|\log(1+x)u\|_1}{n}$}

The resulting nonnegative <martingale> has constant <expectation> $\mu([0,1))$: a parent interval's mass is the sum of its children's masses. Its almost-sure limit is integrable but the <martingale> need not be <uniformly integrable>. The limit recovers the density of the absolutely continuous component in the <Lebesgue decomposition theorem>.

= Martingale construction of Lebesgue decomposition
{parent=Dyadic density martingale}

The <conditional Fatou lemma> gives $X_n\geq\mathbb E[X_\infty\mid\mathcal F_n]$ for the <dyadic density martingale>. Integration first on dyadic sets, then extension by the <monotone class theorem>, makes $\nu=\mu-X_\infty\lambda$ a nonnegative <measure>. Its finite-level density is $Y_n=X_n-\mathbb E[X_\infty\mid\mathcal F_n]$, which tends to zero almost everywhere for <Lebesgue measure>. The <density level sets separate a singular measure> argument shows that this zero-limit set has $\nu$-measure zero. Thus $\nu$ and $\lambda$ are <mutually singular measures>, obtaining the decomposition without assuming it to identify the <martingale> limit.

= Lebesgue decomposition from a sum-measure density
{c}
{parent=Lebesgue decomposition theorem}
{title2=$B=\{h=1\},\quad f=h/(1-h)\text{ on }B^c$}

Use the <Hilbert-space construction of dominated measure densities> with $\rho=\mu+\nu$. The set $B=\{h=1\}$ is a <null set> for $\mu$. Put $f=0$ on $B$ and $f=h/(1-h)$ elsewhere. Then $\int f\,d\mu=\nu(B^c)<\infty$ and $\nu(A)=\int_Af\,d\mu+\nu(A\cap B)$. This is the <Lebesgue decomposition> into a density part and a part concentrated on a $\mu$-<null set>.

= Dominating mixture measure
{parent=Absolute continuity of measures}

A countable family of probability measures is dominated by any mixture $\sum_nw_n\nu_n$ with every $w_n>0$ and $\sum_nw_n=1$.

= Lebesgue-Stieltjes measure
{title2=$\mu_F$}
{parent=Measure theory}
{c}

An increasing right-continuous function $F:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ determines a measure by
$$
\mu_F((a,b])=F(b)-F(a).
$$
The <Radon-Nikodym theorem> and <Lebesgue differentiation theorem> identify the density of the absolutely continuous part of this measure with $F'$ almost everywhere.

= Lp inclusion on a finite measure space
{parent=Measure theory}

On a finite measure space, $L^q\subseteq L^p$ for $0<p<q\leq\infty$. For $1\leq p<q<\infty$, <Holder inequality>[Hölder's inequality] gives
$$
\lVert f\rVert_p
\leq\mu(X)^{1/p-1/q}\lVert f\rVert_q,
$$
with the same formula interpreted as $\lVert f\rVert_p\leq\mu(X)^{1/p}\lVert f\rVert_\infty$ when $q=\infty$.

= Almost-everywhere convergent subsequence from Lp convergence
{parent=Lp inclusion on a finite measure space}
{c}

If $f_n\to f$ in $L^p$ for $1\leq p<\infty$, choose a subsequence with $\lVert f_{n_k}-f\rVert_p^p\leq2^{-k}$. Tonelli's theorem makes $\sum_k|f_{n_k}-f|^p$ finite almost everywhere, so $f_{n_k}\to f$ almost everywhere. For $p=\infty$, norm convergence itself gives almost-everywhere convergence after removing one common null set.

= Lq inclusion implies finite measure on a Euclidean Borel subset
{parent=Lp inclusion on a finite measure space}
{c}

Let $X\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ be Borel. If $L^q(X)\subseteq L^p(X)$ for $1\leq p<q\leq\infty$, the <closed graph theorem> makes the inclusion continuous. Applying its norm bound to indicators of $X\cap B(0,R)$ shows that their measures are uniformly bounded, hence $\mu(X)<\infty$.

= Power singularity integrability
{parent=Lp inclusion on a finite measure space}

On $(0,1)$, $x^{-a}$ is integrable exactly when $a<1$, providing sharp counterexamples between finite-measure $L^p$ spaces.
