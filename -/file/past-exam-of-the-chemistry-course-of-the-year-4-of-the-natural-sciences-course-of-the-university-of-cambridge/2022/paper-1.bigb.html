**An aromatic–aliphatic <polyamide> and methanol are formed.** Let $\mathrm{Ar}=p\text{-}\mathrm{C_6H_4-C_6H_4}\text{-}p$. The <polymer repeat unit> is
$$
\boxed{[\mathrm{-NH(CH_2)_3NH-C(O)-Ar-C(O)-}]_n.}
$$
Each <amide> linkage comes from <nucleophilic acyl substitution>: an <amine> attacks an <ester> <carbonyl>, the <tetrahedral intermediate> expels <methoxide>, and <proton transfer> produces <methanol>. There are approximately $2n$ <molecules> of <methanol> for $n$ complete repeat units in a long chain; the precise finite-chain count depends on its end groups. Removal of <methanol> drives this <step-growth polymerization>.

**Syndiotactic <polypropylene>** is the expected principal product:

The bridged cyclopentadienyl–fluorenyl <metallocene> has approximately $C_s$ site <symmetry>. Activation by <methylaluminoxane> gives a cationic zirconium–alkyl <polymerization-active site>. **Predominantly head-to-tail poly(3-hexylthiophene)** is formed, with thiophene rings connected through their 2 and 5 positions. The hexyl groups occupy equivalent 3 positions along the <conjugated polymer>.

Selective iodine–magnesium exchange first generates an organomagnesium <monomer> while retaining the carbon–bromine bond. Nickel-catalyzed <Kumada coupling> then joins the organomagnesium end of one ring to the brominated end of another. Transfer of the nickel center to the new chain end permits <catalyst-transfer polymerization> and favors head-to-tail <regioregularity>. Organic side products of the activation include isopropyl iodide; magnesium halides accompany the coupling. The repeat unit itself contains neither iodine nor bromine.

In (i), any pair of compatible functional groups can react: <monomers>, oligomers and long chains all participate. For an equimolar, strictly bifunctional mixture without <cyclization in step-growth polymerization>, the <Carothers equation> gives
$$
\boxed{\overline X_n=\frac{1}{1-p}.}
$$
Here $p$ is functional-group conversion and $\overline X_n$ counts the original <monomer> <molecules>. Large <degree of polymerization> therefore requires nearly complete conversion.

In (ii), growth occurs only at the metal-bound chain end. If initiation is rapid and <chain transfer in chain polymerization> is negligible, the number of chains is fixed and
$$
\overline X_n\simeq \frac{[M]_0}{[I]_0}p.
$$
This is the linear conversion dependence of <living polymerization>. Actual zirconocene systems can undergo chain transfer, so linear growth is a limiting model, not a consequence of <coordination polymerization> alone.

In (iv), a newly initiated chain rapidly attains a large size and then undergoes <radical termination>. Further conversion mostly creates additional chains. Thus high <degree of polymerization> can occur at low conversion, without the systematic linear increase of an ideal living system. <temperature>, initiator depletion and <monomer> depletion can change that size during the run.

<Gel permeation chromatography> separates by solution <hydrodynamic volume>, rather than <molar mass> alone. At equal <molar mass>, a more extended chain accesses fewer pores and elutes earlier. **The relatively rigid, conjugated poly(3-hexylthiophene) is the natural first-eluting candidate**, compared with the more flexible chains, provided all samples are molecularly dissolved.

This is a structural prediction, not an unconditional ordering: <solvent quality>, <polymer aggregation> and column interactions affect retention, and the hydrogen-bonding <polyamide> may not dissolve adequately in tetrahydrofuran. An insoluble sample cannot be assigned an ordinary solution <gel permeation chromatography> elution time.

A differential <refractive index detector> can detect all four <polymers> when their refractive-index contrasts are nonzero. <Light scattering> with an appropriate concentration detector can additionally provide <molar mass>. A single ultraviolet detector is unsuitable as the universal detector because <polypropylene> lacks the strong chromophores present in the aromatic and conjugated products.

At high <temperature>, $\chi T$ tends towards the nearly constant <Curie constant> of weakly correlated $S=\tfrac12$ centers. On cooling, $\chi T$ falls because neighboring <spins> increasingly correlate antiparallel. The broad maximum in $\chi$ marks the crossover from the high-temperature Curie-like regime to substantial <short-range order>.

At still lower <temperature>, the <Magnetic susceptibility> becomes small because the antiferromagnetic ladder favors a singlet-like ground state and has few thermally accessible magnetic excitations. An even-leg <spin ladder> can have a <spin gap>, which reinforces this suppression. **A broad <Magnetic susceptibility> maximum and falling $\chi T$ support short-range antiferromagnetic correlations; they do not establish a three-dimensional Néel transition.** Such a transition would require independent evidence, rather than identifying the broad maximum itself as the ordering <temperature>.

Formal ionic counting gives La(III), N(−III), and therefore Re(VI), with a $5d^1$ configuration. An isolated localized $d^1$ center would carry a <saturation magnetic moment> of order $1\mu_{\rm B}$, much larger than the value quoted. It is not a $d^0$ ion made diamagnetic simply by an octahedral <ligand field>.

The spatially extended Re $5d$ orbitals overlap strongly with N $2p$ orbitals. Re–N $\sigma$ interactions raise the $e_g$-derived antibonding states, while $\pi$ interactions mix the $t_{2g}$ states with the nitrogen network. Repetition through corner-sharing <octahedra> turns these combinations into broad <energy bands>. The nominal $d$ <electron> occupies a delocalized, partially filled band rather than an isolated magnetic orbital.

Only <electrons> close to the <Fermi energy> change their occupations appreciably in an applied <magnetic field>. Consequently a <Pauli paramagnetism> has a small, nonzero field-induced <magnetization>:
$$
M\simeq\mu_{\rm B}^2 g(E_F)B
$$
when $g$ is the density of states including both <spin> directions, expressed per formula unit and per unit energy. This band model explains **a very small moment without requiring the absence of <electrons> in the Re-derived band**. The reported material has metallic transport and a Pauli-like response.<Preparation of bulk-phase nitride perovskite LaReN3 and topotactic reduction to LaNiO2-type LaReN2 by Simon D. Kloß and others>[Magnetic and transport measurements, Figure 4]{ref}

The description “saturated” in the question should not be interpreted as complete alignment of one localized <spin> per Re: a nearly linear metallic response over a laboratory field range need not be a true fully spin-polarized saturation moment.

For a homogeneous <polymer solution>, examine the curvature of the mixing <Helmholtz free energy> with respect to composition. A composition fluctuation into equal volumes at $\phi\pm\delta\phi$ changes the average free energy by
$$
\frac{f(\phi+\delta\phi)+f(\phi-\delta\phi)}2-f(\phi)
=\frac12 f''(\phi)(\delta\phi)^2+O((\delta\phi)^4).
$$
Thus $f''>0$ locally resists fluctuations and $f''<0$ amplifies them, leading to <spinodal decomposition>. **The <spinodal> curve is the locus $f''=0$**, separating locally stable or metastable homogeneous compositions from locally unstable ones. The coexistence <binodal> instead follows a common tangent to the free energy. Between the <binodal> and <spinodal>, separation needs <nucleation>.

In <Flory–Huggins solution theory>, $\phi$ is the <volume fraction>, $N$ is the number of equal-sized lattice segments per chain, $T$ is <temperature>, $k_{\rm B}$ is the <Boltzmann constant>, and $\chi$ is the dimensionless <Flory interaction parameter>. The stated $f$ is the mixing <Helmholtz free energy> per lattice site. The first two terms describe mixing <entropy>; the last describes the contact-energy contribution.

At fixed $T$ and composition-independent $\chi$,
$$
\frac{f'}{k_{\rm B}T}=\frac{\ln\phi+1}{N}-\ln(1-\phi)-1+\chi(1-2\phi),
\qquad
\frac{f''}{k_{\rm B}T}=\frac1{N\phi}+\frac1{1-\phi}-2\chi.
$$
Hence the <spinodal> is
$$
\boxed{\chi_s(\phi)=\frac12\left[\frac1{N\phi}+\frac1{1-\phi}\right],\quad0<\phi<1.}
$$
The homogeneous state is locally unstable when $\chi>\chi_s(\phi)$. The minimum gives the <critical point of a Flory–Huggins polymer solution>:
$$
\boxed{\phi_c=\frac1{1+\sqrt N},\qquad\chi_c=\frac12(1+N^{-1/2})^2.}
$$
If $\chi$ depends on <temperature>, the equation $\chi(T)=\chi_s(\phi)$ converts this result into a <temperature>–composition boundary.

In the <Asakura–Oosawa model>, nonadsorbing <polymers> are treated as ideal, mutually penetrable particles, but their centers cannot enter a layer around a hard <colloid>. The <polymer> <osmotic pressure> is $\Pi=\rho k_{\rm B}T$ in the ideal dilute limit, with the appropriate number density in the space dimension considered.

For fixed <polymer> number, reducing the inaccessible volume increases the available volume and lowers the <polymer> <Helmholtz free energy>. Equivalently, at fixed <polymer> <chemical potential>, the <polymer> contribution to the effective colloid potential is
$$
\boxed{U_{\rm dep}=\Pi\,\Delta V_{\rm excluded}=-\Pi V_{\rm overlap}.}
$$
When the excluded layers overlap, their union occupies less space than two separate layers. More <polymer> configurations become available, so the resulting <depletion force> attracts the colloids. It is an <entropy>-driven effective interaction, even though each microscopic colloid–<polymer> interaction is purely repulsive.

The unrotated squares have a gap $d>a$, so their facing exclusion layers do not overlap. Rotation can bring them into contact even though their centers remain fixed. The strongest <depletion force> comes from **parallel faces in contact over a long shared length**, rather than a corner-to-corner contact.

Let $r=\ell+d$. Rotate both squares through the same angle $\theta$ relative to their original orientation. Their center displacement has a face-normal component $r\cos\theta$ and a tangential component $r\sin\theta$. Face contact requires
$$
\boxed{\cos\theta_* =\frac\ell r,\qquad
\theta_*=\pm\arccos\frac\ell{\ell+d}\simeq\pm\sqrt{\frac{2d}{\ell}}.}
$$
The contacting faces overlap for length
$$
L=\ell-r|\sin\theta_*|=\ell-\sqrt{r^2-\ell^2}\simeq\ell-\sqrt{2\ell d}.
$$
These two mirror-related orientations, together with equivalent square rotations, maximize the leading overlap area when $a<d\ll\ell$. A small <polymer> gap sacrifices overlap width without a compensating leading increase in contact length.

This is an **<Ireland–Claisen rearrangement>**. Number the <ester> <carbonyl> carbon $C_0$, its enolizable carbon $C_\alpha$, and the three allylic ring carbons $C_1-C_2=C_3$, starting at the carbon bearing the <ester> oxygen.

<lithium hexamethyldisilazide> removes an $\alpha$ <proton> and <trimethylsilyl chloride> traps the oxygen of the resulting <ester enolate>, giving a <silyl ketene acetal>. Heating permits a six-electron, \[3,3\]-<sigmatropic rearrangement> through the array
$$
C_\alpha=C_0-O-C_1-C_2=C_3.
$$
The electron-pair movements are $\pi(C_\alpha=C_0)\to C_0=O$, $\sigma(O-C_1)\to\pi(C_1=C_2)$, and $\pi(C_2=C_3)\to\sigma(C_3-C_\alpha)$. Acidic work-up cleaves the silyl <ester> to the <carboxylic acid>. The benzyl ether remains a protecting group; it is not the migrating bond.

The <suprafacial reaction> transfers the starting allylic <stereochemistry> to $C_3$, where the new side chain is delivered from the original oxygen face. In the product's printed orientation, **the new C$_3$–C$_\alpha$ bond is behind the ring and the C$_3$ methyl group is toward the viewer**. At that ring star, the <Cahn-Ingold-Prelog priority rules> orders the groups as side chain, alkenyl ring direction, saturated ring direction, methyl, giving **$R$**.

The second star is $C_\alpha$. Its priorities are carboxyl, substituted ring carbon, $\mathrm{CH_2CH_2OBn}$, H. A chair-like transition state normally places the bulky side chain pseudoequatorial. However, **its absolute configuration also depends on the E/Z geometry of the trapped <silyl ketene acetal>**: opposite geometries give opposite configurations at this star under the same suprafacial ring transfer. The printed conditions do not state solvent or the ketene-acetal geometry. Thus the secure assignment is the ring-star $R$ configuration and conditional $\alpha$-star <stereochemistry>, rather than an unqualified absolute assignment to both stars. Drawing the assumed ketene-acetal geometry and its chair is necessary before selecting one $\alpha$ epimer.

**A is the kinetic, less-hindered-side <silyl enol ether>.** Its double bond lies away from the methyl substituent. Forming B requires removal of a <proton> from the more hindered $\alpha$ position adjacent to the methyl-bearing carbon.

The base strengths alone do not determine the ratios. <trimethylsilyl triflate> is strongly electrophilic: oxygen silylation activates the <ketone> towards deprotonation, and irreversible trapping of the resulting <silyl enol ether> can make the product ratio reflect competing deprotonation rates. DBU is the strongest and comparatively accessible base, and distinguishes the two sites least strongly, giving the smallest A:B ratio. Triethylamine gives greater preference for the less-hindered site. The more sterically hindered diisopropylethylamine discriminates most strongly and gives the largest proportion of A. function trapezoid(f, a, b, n):
    dx = (b-a)/n
    total = (f(a)+f(b))/2
    for j in 1,...,n-1:
        total += f(a+j*dx)
    return dx*total

function midpoint(f, a, b, n):
    dx = (b-a)/n
    total = 0
    for j in 0,...,n-1:
        total += f(a+(j+1/2)*dx)

A weighting function must first be normalized to a <probability density>. The constant choice on $[a,b]$ means
$$
q(x)=\frac1{b-a},\qquad
I=\mathbb E_q\left[\frac{f(X)}{q(X)}\right]=(b-a)\mathbb E_q[f(X)].
$$
Equivalently, transform $x=a+(b-a)z$ and use the already normalized $g(z)=1$ on $[0,1]$. Independent samples give the unbiased <Monte Carlo integration> estimator
$$
\boxed{\widehat I_N=\frac{b-a}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N f(a+(b-a)U_j),\quad U_j\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1).}
$$

``
function uniform_monte_carlo(f, a, b, N):
    require N >= 2
    total = 0
    total_squared = 0
    for j in 1,...,N:
        u = independent_uniform_0_1()
        value = f(a+(b-a)*u)
        total += value
        total_squared += value*value
    average = total/N
    sample_variance = (total_squared-N*average*average)/(N-1)
    integral = (b-a)*average
    standard_error = abs(b-a)*sqrt(sample_variance/N)
    return integral, standard_error
``

For a general positive normalized importance density $q$, replace each sample contribution by $f(X_j)/q(X_j)$ with $X_j\sim q$. Here the uniform choice is the special case of <importance sampling> that assigns equal weight to every region of equal length.

The <diffusion coefficient> relates particle flux to concentration gradient through <Fick's first law>, $\mathbf j=-D\nabla c$. For unbiased isotropic motion in $d$ dimensions it can equivalently be defined by the long-time <mean squared displacement>:
$$
\boxed{D=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\langle|\mathbf r(t)-\mathbf r(0)|^2\rangle}{2dt}.}
$$
An uncorrelated <random walk> with jump length $a$ and total jump rate $\Gamma$ has $\langle r^2\rangle=\Gamma a^2t$, hence $D=\Gamma a^2/(2d)$; correlations introduce a multiplicative correlation factor. The factor $d$ counts the directions sharing the total displacement. It does not mean that the same material's single-coordinate diffusivity changes merely by choosing to observe fewer coordinates: $\langle x^2\rangle=2Dt$ in every Cartesian direction of the same isotropic system.

At $500$ K, silver iodide is in its fast-ion-conducting $\alpha$ phase. For an order-of-magnitude estimate, take a jump length $a\sim3$ Å and an effective successful jump rate $\Gamma\sim10^{11}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Then
$$
\boxed{D\sim\frac{(3\times10^{-10})^2(10^{11})}{6}
\sim1.5\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
Thus **$D$ of order $10^{-9}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$** is a reasonable estimate for this <superionic conductor>. The length and rate are explicitly assumed material scales; the question supplies neither a measured conductivity nor jump data, so an exact numerical coefficient cannot be deduced from <temperature> alone. The estimate is not presented as an experimentally measured value.

A one-dimensional <probability density> has units $L^{-1}$, while $D$ has units $L^2T^{-1}$. With no other length scale, <dimensional analysis> requires
$$
\boxed{n(x,t)=\frac1{\sqrt{Dt}}F\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{Dt}}\right).}
$$
Normalization gives $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(\xi)\,d\xi=1$ and unbiased <diffusion> gives an even $F$. **Dimensional arguments alone do not specify $F$.**

For ordinary Fickian <diffusion>, impose $\partial_t n=D\partial_x^2 n$ with $n(x,0)=\delta(x)$. Substitution of the similarity form yields
$$
F''+\tfrac12(\xi F)'=0.
$$
A decaying symmetric solution obeys $F'=-\xi F/2$, so $F=C e^{-\xi^2/4}$. Normalization fixes $C=1/\sqrt{4\pi}$:
$$
\boxed{n(x,t)=\frac1{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}
\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{4Dt}\right),\qquad t>0.}
$$
This <heat kernel> integrates to one, has <variance> $2Dt$, and tends to the initial <Dirac delta distribution> as $t\downarrow0$.

A <SMILES string> encodes a molecular <graph> as a sequence of atom and bond symbols, with parentheses for branches, matching digits for ring closures and additional symbols for charges and <stereochemistry>. For example, ``CCO`` specifies ethanol. A <molecule> may have several valid SMILES traversals, so the raw character sequence is not itself a unique physical descriptor.

Two numerical encodings are:

* A <molecular fingerprint>: a fixed-length vector indicating local substructures, for example hashed circular neighborhoods around atoms. Each coordinate is a bit or a count. The same atom–bond graph should produce the same fingerprint regardless of its SMILES traversal.
* A <molecular descriptor> vector: real-valued quantities such as <molar mass>, atom counts, formal charge, <hydrogen bond donor> and <hydrogen bond acceptor> counts, or topological indices. Standardize these coordinates using the training set before fitting a scale-sensitive model.

These are chemical-structure representations. No biological mechanism is needed to encode the <molecules> or treat the supplied target as a binary label.

Include an intercept $w_0$ and let $\mathbf z$ be the chosen numerical feature vector. <Logistic regression> uses
$$
\boxed{h(\mathbf z;\mathbf w)=\sigma(w_0+\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\mathbf z),
\qquad\sigma(u)=\frac1{1+e^{-u}}.}
$$
The <logistic function> maps every real score into $(0,1)$, allowing a <Bernoulli distribution> model for the label. Equivalently, the <log odds> are linear in the features:
$$
\log\frac h{1-h}=w_0+\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\mathbf z.
$$
This gives a smooth differentiable <probability> model with a linear decision boundary at any fixed <probability> threshold. The sigmoid output is a fitted conditional <probability>, not a categorical prediction until a threshold is chosen.

For conditionally independent binary labels, the <likelihood> is
$$
L(\mathbf w)=\prod_{i=1}^{m} h_i^{y_i}(1-h_i)^{1-y_i},\qquad
h_i=h(\mathbf z_i;\mathbf w).
$$
Maximizing it is equivalent to minimizing its negative <log likelihood>, the mean <binary cross-entropy>:
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L(\mathbf w)=-\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}
[y_i\log h_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-h_i)].}
$$
It is the loss implied by a <Bernoulli distribution> for each target, penalizes confident errors strongly and is convex in the linear-logistic parameters. For numerical stability, each contribution can be evaluated as $\log(1+e^{u_i})-y_i u_i$ using a stable softplus implementation.

The 10:90 class imbalance does not invalidate this <likelihood>. Class weighting or resampling may be useful if errors have unequal costs, but changes the training objective and potentially the interpretation of fitted <probabilities>. Optional <ridge regression> adds $\lambda\|\mathbf w\|^2/2$, usually excluding the intercept, to control overly large weights or complete separation.
