Only one bromine is replaced by <hydrogen>. Writing the unchanged protected chain as $R=\mathrm{THPOCH_2CH(Me)}$, the product is **$R\mathrm{CH=C(Me)CH=CHBr}$**, retaining the original internal <alkene> geometry and forming predominantly the **$Z$ terminal bromoalkene**: bromine and the <carbon> chain lie on the same side of that new stereodefined double bond. The formula is consequently $\mathrm{C_{13}H_{21}BrO_2}$.

Phosphine dissociation exposes Pd(0), which undergoes preferential <oxidative addition> into the less hindered C–Br bond, the bromine trans to the <carbon> chain in the gem-dibromoalkene. <hydride> transfer from <tributyltin hydride> replaces the <palladium> bromide ligand, producing tributyltin bromide and an alkenyl–Pd–H intermediate. <reductive elimination> joins the alkenyl <carbon> and <hydride>, regenerating Pd(0). Retention of the alkenyl geometry through this sequence puts the surviving bromine cis to the chain. The conjugated <carbon> skeleton and <protecting group> remain intact; this is not addition of tin <hydride> across either <alkene>. The stereoselective monohydrogenolysis is established for this reagent combination.<A general and convenient synthetic method of geometrically pure (Z)-1-bromo-1-alkenes by Junichi Uenishi and others>[Tetrahedron Letters 37, pp. 6759–6762: palladium/tributyltin-hydride monoreduction]{ref}

The first equivalent of <titanocene methylidene> methylenates the <ester>: the <carbonyl> <oxygen> and <titanium> form an <oxatitanacyclobutane>, whose fragmentation gives **$\mathrm{PhC(OMe)=CH_2}$** and a <titanium>–<oxygen> product. The second equivalent adds across this electron-rich double bond. The initial adduct is a <titanacyclobutane> with cyclic connectivity $\mathrm{Cp_2Ti-CH_2-C(Ph)(OMe)-CH_2-}$, closing from the last methylene to <titanium>. The <ester>'s phenyl-substituted <carbon> lies opposite Ti; the two new methylenes originate from the two methylidene equivalents.

Beta-methoxy elimination opens that metallacycle to **B, $\mathrm{Cp_2Ti(OMe)[CH_2C(Ph)=CH_2]}$**, the <allyltitanium reagent>. The C–OMe bond breaks, OMe transfers to Ti, one Ti–C bond becomes the new C=C bond, and the other Ti–C bond remains as the allylic <nucleophile>. The cyclic adduct is therefore a precursor to the opened reagent B; treating B as merely the one-equivalent <enol ether> loses the second reagent <carbon>.

The <aldehyde> <oxygen> coordinates to <titanium>. Allylic <carbon> transfer to its <carbonyl> <carbon>, accompanied by movement of the C=O pi <electrons> to <oxygen>, gives the <titanium> <alkoxide> $\mathrm{PhCH_2CH_2CH(O{-}Ti)CH_2C(Ph)=CH_2}$. Acidic aqueous workup cleaves O–Ti and protonates <oxygen>, giving **$\mathrm{PhCH_2CH_2CH(OH)CH_2C(Ph)=CH_2}$**. A six-membered allyl-transfer arrangement is a convenient mechanistic model. No enantioselectivity is imposed by these achiral reagents.

<Schwartz reagent> adds syn across the terminal <alkene>, placing <zirconium> at its terminal <carbon> and <hydrogen> at the substituted <carbon>. The organozirconium intermediate is **$\mathrm{PhCH(OMe)CH(Me)CH_2ZrCp_2Cl}$**. The two faces of the achiral starting <alkene> give enantiomeric organometallic intermediates.

Boron trifluoride coordinates the <ether> <oxygen>, making the benzylic C–OMe bond susceptible to cleavage. The C–Zr bond then transfers its <carbon> terminus intramolecularly to the benzylic <carbon> as the activated methoxy group departs, closing a cyclopropane. This can be represented as a concerted, strongly assisted displacement rather than requiring a freely equilibrating long-lived benzylic <carbocation>. <hydrozirconation> fixes the relative disposition of the new C–H and C–Zr bonds, and the preferred closure conformation places the bulky phenyl and methyl groups on opposite faces. The result is **racemic trans-1-methyl-2-phenylcyclopropane**, with much less steric crowding than the cis alternative. The ring contains the original two <alkene> <carbons> and the original benzylic <carbon>; no carbene <carbon> is added.

Let $\mathrm{Ar}=2,6$-dimethylphenyl and $\mathrm{Ar'}=2$-methoxyphenyl. After conversion of the Pd(OAc)$_2$ precatalyst into an active phosphine–Pd(0) species, <oxidative addition> of $\mathrm{Ar'Cl}$ gives $\mathrm{L_nPd^{II}(Ar')Cl}$. Carbonate-assisted replacement of chloride by the <alcohol>'s <alkoxide> produces $\mathrm{L_nPd^{II}(Ar')[OC(Me)_2Ar]}$.

In the key <beta-carbon elimination>, the <alkoxide> <oxygen> forms a C=O bond while the bond between its carbinol <carbon> and $\mathrm{Ar}$ cleaves, transferring $\mathrm{Ar}$ to <palladium>. This gives **<acetone> and a diarylpalladium(II) complex**, $\mathrm{L_nPd^{II}(Ar')(Ar)}$. <reductive elimination> creates the new biaryl bond and regenerates Pd(0). Thus the <tertiary alcohol> functions as an aryl donor by <carbon>–<carbon> bond cleavage, rather than being incorporated as a carbinol substituent. The overall organic products are **the cross-coupled biaryl and <acetone>**. The carbinol <carbon> has no <hydrogen>, so ordinary <beta-hydride elimination> from this <alkoxide> cannot explain the observed aryl transfer. The bulky electron-rich phosphine helps access the aryl-chloride <oxidative addition> and the sterically congested biaryl-forming elimination.

Both free primary allylic alcohols undergo <Sharpless asymmetric epoxidation>. With slightly more than two equivalents of <hydroperoxide>, the expected fully oxidized product is **the diepoxide, with both original C=C bonds converted to <epoxides> and both <primary alcohols> retained**. The two central pre-existing <stereocentres> and their <ether> substituents are retained.

The stereochemical construction is unambiguous by applying the L-(+)-tartrate mnemonic separately at each end: orient that <alkene> horizontally with its CH$_2$OH group at lower right, and deliver <oxygen> from the back face. In the source's original in-plane orientation this gives **the left <epoxide> <oxygen> behind the plane and the right <epoxide> <oxygen> in front**. The methyl and hydroxymethyl groups retain their original relative arrangement about each former double bond. One cannot simply draw both <oxygens> on the same face of the original extended chain: the two allylic-alcohol ends have opposite drawing orientations. <titanium> binding to each free hydroxyl directs <oxygen> transfer; the pre-existing <stereocentres> can create matched/mismatched rate differences, but the stated reagent control predicts the <stereochemistry> of both epoxidations. <acetone> first forms a <proline> <enamine> by reversible iminium formation and alpha-deprotonation. The <enamine> attacks the aromatic <aldehyde> in a hydrogen-bonded arrangement involving the catalyst's carboxylic acid. <hydrolysis> of the resulting iminium releases the beta-hydroxyketone and regenerates <proline>. The usual L-proline arrangement gives the **$R$ <secondary alcohol>**.

The sterically crowded aryl–<amide> axis is selected at the same bond-forming step: rapid rotation in the <aldehyde> permits the catalyst to react preferentially with the conformer that puts the smaller carbonyl-oxygen side of the <amide> towards the approaching aldol transition structure and the bulky diisopropylamino side away. A chair-like <enamine>–<aldehyde> model minimizes the interaction of the <amide> with the catalyst-side axial <hydrogen>; reversing the <amide> orientation creates the less favourable diastereomeric <transition state>. Replacing planar CHO by tetrahedral CH(OH)CH$_2$COMe then raises the rotation barrier and traps the axis. This is a **<dynamic kinetic resolution>**, not resolution of two configurationally stable starting atropisomers.

To specify the predicted major axis without relying on a flat bond-line formula, look from the aromatic ipso <carbon> towards the <amide> <carbonyl> <carbon>. Put the higher-priority aryl path, the trimethylsilyl-bearing ortho side, at nine o'clock. The preferred model puts the <carbonyl> <oxygen> at approximately six o'clock and the diisopropylamino group at twelve o'clock. This is **$S_a$** using the near-to-far high-priority convention; <oxygen> outranks <nitrogen> at the far end. Coupled with the <alcohol> assignment, the model predicts **$(R,S_a)$**. The drawing defines the configuration even if an alternative viewing direction is used. The dynamic-resolution mechanism is established by the primary aldol study; the product-axis assignment here is a stereochemical-model prediction for the printed substrate.<Dynamic kinetic resolution of atropisomeric amides by Vincent Chan and others>[Organic Letters 6, pp. 2051–2053: simultaneous alcohol and amide-axis control]{ref}

An unsaturated Rh(I)–<BINAP> complex undergoes <oxidative addition> of the aldehydic C–H bond, giving a Rh(III) **acyl <hydride>**. Coordination of the tethered <alkene> brings it into the <chiral> coordination sphere. <hydride> migration to the internal, tert-butyl-substituted <alkene> <carbon> places Rh on the terminal methylene. The resulting acyl–alkyl complex has the connectivity required for a five-membered ring. <reductive elimination> between its acyl and alkyl <carbons> closes the C–C bond, releases the cyclopentanone and regenerates Rh(I).

The <aldehyde> <carbon> becomes the <ketone> <carbonyl> <carbon>; its <hydrogen> ultimately reaches the alkene-derived internal <carbon>. The original tert-butyl substituent remains attached to the internal <alkene> <carbon>, which becomes the beta <stereocentre> of the cyclopentanone. <alkene> coordination and insertion favour the <chiral> pocket arrangement with the bulky tert-butyl group in the less crowded region of the <BINAP> complex. For the stated (S)-BINAP system the observed product is **the $R$ beta-tert-butylcyclopentanone**, equivalently the product with tert-butyl on the solid-wedge face in the supplied ring orientation. Its priorities are tert-butyl, the shorter carbonyl-side ring path, the other ring path, and <hydrogen>. The ligand's S label alone is not a general rule that every product <stereocentre> must be S; the substrate orientation in the catalyst must also be specified. No decarbonylation is needed in the productive <hydroacylation> cycle.

One valid asymmetric route uses **N-Boc-(R)-proline as a <chiral> starting material**. Convert its acid to the <Weinreb amide> with N,O-dimethylhydroxylamine and a coupling reagent such as EDC, then add phenylmagnesium bromide at low temperature. <chelation> of the magnesium tetrahedral intermediate prevents a second addition; aqueous workup releases the phenyl <ketone>. Reduce it with <sodium borohydride>, separate the resulting <alcohol> <diastereomers>, and retain the one with **$R$ configuration at both pyrrolidine C2 and the benzylic carbinol**. This is an asymmetric synthesis through a pre-existing enantiopure centre, even though the final <reduction> need not be perfectly diastereoselective. If the other protected <alcohol> is isolated, Mitsunobu esterification with a carboxylic acid followed by <ester> <hydrolysis> inverts just the carbinol centre. Keeping <nitrogen> Boc-protected during that inversion avoids neighbouring-amine participation. Mild Boc removal followed by reductive benzylation with benzaldehyde and <sodium triacetoxyborohydride> gives **D, the $(R,R)$ amino <alcohol>**. Use conditions that avoid epimerizing the amino <ketone>.

For ring expansion, activate D's <alcohol> with methanesulfonyl chloride and a <tertiary amine> base. Its own tertiary <nitrogen> attacks the benzylic <carbon> from the back, displacing mesylate with inversion and making a fused <aziridinium ion>. Acetate, supplied as a soluble acetate salt, attacks the **original pyrrolidine C2** from the opposite face and breaks the old C2–N bond. The new N–benzylic-C bond is retained, incorporating that side-chain <carbon> into the six-membered ring. Inversion at the two successively attacked <carbons> gives **the cis acetate/phenyl piperidine E** in the displayed orientation. Attack at the benzylic <carbon> instead would simply open the three-membered <ion> back to a five-membered-ring derivative; specifying the attacked <carbon> is the crucial regiochemical step.

The <binding energies> are referred to the common <Fermi level>. Immediately outside the <copper> surface, <conservation of energy> gives
$$
K_s=h\nu-E_B-\phi_s=515.0-399.2-4.0=\boxed{111.8\ \mathrm{eV}}.
$$
This is the emitted <photoelectron> energy at the sample. On reaching the electrically connected, unbiased analyser, its energy is instead $K_a=h\nu-E_B-\phi_a=\boxed{111.4\ \mathrm{eV}}$. The 0.4 eV difference is the contact-potential correction, not an additional binding-energy shift. Distinguishing these two reference positions is essential for the retarding-voltage calculation.

The hemispherical component needs 100.0 eV <electrons>, whereas <electrons> arrive at its entrance optics with 111.4 eV. The optics must therefore remove **11.4 eV**, requiring a **retarding-potential magnitude of 11.4 V**. For a negative <electron>, moving to a more negative electrostatic potential increases potential energy, so the retarding region is 11.4 V negative relative to the analyser entrance. Reporting 11.8 V as the additional instrument retardation would count the contact correction twice.

Define the retard ratio using the <kinetic energy> at the analyser entrance before the deliberate retardation: $R=K_a/E_p$. Initially $R=111.4/100.0=1.114$. With the original excitation, a <carbon> <photoelectron> arrives with $K_{a,C}=515.0-284.8-4.4=225.8\ \mathrm{eV}$. Thus

The large resonance at the smallest field-to-normal angle identifies an unoccupied orbital whose transition dipole is mainly normal to the surface. An N 1s initial state is approximately spherical; the dipole <matrix> element for a $1s\to\pi^*$ transition is directed along the normal to the <adsorbate>'s aromatic plane. Consequently **the <adsorbate> rings lie predominantly parallel to the <copper> surface**. For perfectly flat molecules the resonance strength scales as $|\mathbf E\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}|^2\propto\cos^2\theta$, which decreases strongly from $17^\circ$ to $67^\circ$ as observed.

For azimuthally disordered molecular normals tilted by $\alpha$ from the surface normal, averaging gives $I(\theta)\propto\cos^2\theta\cos^2\alpha+\tfrac12\sin^2\theta\sin^2\alpha$. Strong dichroism therefore implies a large orientational order parameter, but the plotted qualitative curves alone do not determine a unique tilt angle or distribution. Background subtraction, incident <polarization> and intensity normalization would be needed for such a fit. This experiment identifies the orientation of the pi system; it does not by itself identify a particular <nitrogen>–<copper> bonding topology.

A step elastically reflects a surface-state wave. Interference between $e^{ikx}$ and $re^{-ikx}$ produces a local density oscillation at $2k$, so the measured spatial period is $\lambda_{\rm osc}=\pi/k$, **not** $2\pi/k$. Thus $k_1=\pi/20=0.15708\ \mathring{\mathrm A}^{-1}$ and $k_2=\pi/15=0.20944\ \mathring{\mathrm A}^{-1}$. Write $E=E_0+A k^2$ with $A=\hbar^2/(2m^*)$. The two points give
$$
A=\frac{0.50}{(\pi/15)^2-(\pi/20)^2}=26.054\ \mathrm{eV}\,\mathring{\mathrm A}^2,\qquad
\boxed{m^*=0.1462m_e}.
$$
Here $\hbar^2/(2m_e)=3.80998\ \mathrm{eV}\,\mathring{\mathrm A}^2$. The fitted minimum is $E_0=1.00-A(\pi/20)^2=\boxed{0.35714\ \mathrm{eV}}$ above the <Fermi level>. This assumes the bias measures sample excitation energy, <parabolic dispersion> over the measured interval, and no wave-vector-dependent phase contribution that changes the fitted period.

An Au(111) Shockley state requires the near-surface band structure and projected bulk gap of a sufficiently thick <gold> region. In an ultrathin film, its <wavefunction> reaches the <platinum> interface, where hybridization and leakage into <platinum> states can shift, broaden or destroy the bound <surface state>. When $|eV|$ reaches a vibrational quantum, a tunnelling <electron> can lose energy by exciting that vibration. Opening an inelastic channel changes $dI/dV$ at both voltage polarities. If the conductance increases in each direction, $d^2I/dV^2$ has a positive feature at positive threshold and a negative feature at the corresponding negative threshold. Approximate antisymmetry follows from approximate symmetry of the conductance; unequal tip and sample electronic structures can make amplitudes unequal.

The three energy scales are **8, 48 and 375 meV**, corresponding to about **65, 387 and 3025 cm$^{-1}$**. A plausible assignment is a low-energy frustrated lateral translation or rotation, an <adsorbate>–metal stretch or hindered molecular mode at 48 meV, and a C–H stretching vibration at 375 meV. The terminal <carbon> of methyl isocyanide supplies a plausible <adsorption> contact, but exact mode assignment requires calculated <normal modes> or independent vibrational measurements. In particular the 48 meV band should not be labelled a C–N stretching vibration simply because the molecule contains that bond: its <frequency> is far too low for the usual internal stretching mode.

The <gyromagnetic ratio> is the signed proportionality coefficient $\boldsymbol\mu=\gamma\mathbf I$ between a <nuclear magnetic moment> and its <spin angular momentum>. With the angular-frequency convention, $\omega_0=|\gamma|B_0$, and $\gamma$ is expressed in rad s$^{-1}$ T$^{-1}$.

= b
{parent=42}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For a positive <gyromagnetic ratio> the <magnetic moment> is parallel to the <spin angular momentum>; for a negative ratio it is antiparallel. The <Hamiltonian> $\hat H_Z=-\gamma B_0\hat I_z$ gives $E_m=-\gamma\hbar B_0m$. Thus **$m=+1/2$ is lower for the proton, whereas $m=-1/2$ is lower for nitrogen-15**. Their <spin> precession senses are opposite for a common positive field convention; measurable <absorption> <frequencies> and energy separations remain positive.

{parent=42}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The energy separation is $\Delta E=\hbar|\gamma|B_0$. At 10 T this gives
$$
\boxed{\Delta E_H=2.816\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm J=1.757\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{eV}},\qquad
\boxed{\Delta E_N=2.847\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm J=1.777\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{eV}}.
$$
At 300 K, $k_BT=4.142\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm J=0.02585\ \mathrm{eV}$, so the splitting-to-thermal-energy ratios are $6.80\times10^{-5}$ and $6.87\times10^{-6}$. The two <spin> levels therefore have nearly equal populations. Their equilibrium population <polarizations> are approximately half these ratios, **$3.40\times10^{-5}$ and $3.44\times10^{-6}$**, explaining weak thermal <NMR> sensitivity despite well-defined resonant transition energies.

$$
C_{fs}^{\rm plateau}=\frac1{2\pi\hbar}\int_{\sqrt{2mV_0}}^\infty\frac p m e^{-\beta p^2/(2m)}dp
=\boxed{\frac{e^{-\beta V_0}}{2\pi\hbar\beta}}.
$$
The exact rate is again unchanged; the transmission correction to the reactant-side transition-state estimate is $e^{-\beta V_0}$. The return time depends on surface placement and velocity, so the plateau means a timescale-separated limiting flux, not the claim that every trajectory has one identical finite recrossing time.

For $K$ mutually exclusive classes, the output layer should provide $K$ nonnegative <probabilities> summing to one, with labels aligned consistently to output coordinates. A softmax activation on <logits> $a_k$ provides

Compose <affine maps> with nonlinear hidden <activation functions>, for example $h(x)=W_2\operatorname{ReLU}(W_1x+b_1)+b_2$. Several hidden units can respond to different linear combinations of input features; later units combine their responses to represent curved or piecewise-linear decision boundaries and interactions. A product interaction can be understood from $xy=[(x+y)^2-(x-y)^2]/4$: units approximating nonlinear functions of $x+y$ and $x-y$ can represent effects that no single additive linear fit captures.

**Nonlinearity in hidden layers is essential:** with only identity activations, repeated <affine maps> collapse to one <affine map> regardless of depth. Width and depth determine available representations; <regularization>, validation and suitable optimization are needed to learn useful rather than overfitted interactions. A nonlinear output activation alone applied to one linear score does not give an unrestricted model of <feature interactions>.

A <multilayer perceptron> usually connects every input unit of a layer to every output unit. For $n$ inputs and $m$ outputs its dense affine layer has **$nm+m$ parameters**, including biases. A convolutional layer instead applies small local filters with <weight sharing> across positions. A two-dimensional filter of size $k_h\times k_w$, with $C_{\rm in}$ input channels and $C_{\rm out}$ output channels, has **$k_hk_wC_{\rm in}C_{\rm out}+C_{\rm out}$ parameters**. The count does not increase with spatial height and width, although the activation storage and arithmetic do.

Local <receptive fields> and repeated layers capture increasingly extended patterns. <translation equivariance> follows from shared filters under compatible boundary conventions; pooling or global aggregation can help build invariance but a convolution alone is not generally translation-invariant. Flattening a large convolutional output into a dense head can reintroduce many parameters. For an unordered descriptor vector, a convolutional locality assumption must be justified rather than imposed merely because convolutional layers have fewer weights.
