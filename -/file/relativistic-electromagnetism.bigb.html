= Relativistic electromagnetism
{wiki}

Relativistic electromagnetism combines electric and magnetic fields into Lorentz tensors.

= Boosted uniform line charge
{parent=Relativistic electromagnetism}
{title2=$\lambda=\gamma\lambda_0,\quad I=\lambda u$}

An infinite uniform line with proper charge per unit <length> $\lambda_0$ moving axially at <speed> $u$ has Lorentz-contracted <density> $\lambda=\gamma\lambda_0$ and current $I=\lambda u$. Transverse distance $r$ is unchanged by the boost. The rest <electric field> $\lambda_0\widehat{\mathbf r}/(2\pi\epsilon_0r)$ transforms into $\mathbf E=\lambda\widehat{\mathbf r}/(2\pi\epsilon_0r)$ and $\mathbf B=\mathbf u\times\mathbf E/c^2$. This equals the <magnetic field> $\mu_0I\widehat{\boldsymbol\varphi}/(2\pi r)$ from <Ampère's law>. The formulas concern a uniform stationary current pattern, not an accelerating finite filament's radiation.

= Neutral current filament in a boosted frame
{parent=Boosted uniform line charge}
{title2=$\lambda'=-\gamma uI/c^2,\quad I'=\gamma I$}

For an ideal infinite filament with zero <line charge> <density> and current $I$ in a laboratory frame, the line-integrated <four-current> is $(0,I)$ in temporal/axial components. An axial boost of <speed> $u$ gives the title <density> and current. The laboratory <magnetic field> $\mu_0I\widehat{\boldsymbol\varphi}/(2\pi r)$ therefore becomes both an <electric field> from $\lambda'$ and a <magnetic field> from $I'$. <Charge neutrality> is frame-dependent for a current-carrying system. A model built from oppositely charged moving and resting populations makes this result explicit; the charge of each fixed material particle set remains invariant.

= Polarization-magnetization tensor
{parent=Relativistic electromagnetism}
{title2=$M^{\mu\nu}$}

An <antisymmetric tensor> combines the <electric polarization> and <magnetization> of a medium. For <Minkowski metric> $(-,+,+,+)$ and units with light speed one, choose $M^{i0}=P_i$ and $M^{ij}=\epsilon_{ijk}M_k$, together with $F_{i0}=E_i$ and $F_{ij}=\epsilon_{ijk}B_k$. Then the <Lorentz scalar> interaction is $M^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}/2=\mathbf P\cdot\mathbf E+\mathbf M\cdot\mathbf B$. The <bound charge and bound current> have four-current $j_b^\mu=\partial_\nu M^{\mu\nu}$, giving $\rho_b=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P$ and $\mathbf J_b=\partial_t\mathbf P+\nabla\times\mathbf M$. This current is identically conserved because partial derivatives commute and $M$ is antisymmetric.

= Relativistic charged-particle action
{parent=Relativistic electromagnetism}

For metric signature $(-,+,+,+)$, a particle of mass $m$ and charge $q$ coupled to a four-potential has the reparametrization-invariant action
$$
S=-mc\int\sqrt{-\dot x^2}\,d\lambda
+q\int A_\mu(x)\dot x^\mu\,d\lambda.
$$

= Electromagnetic minimal coupling
{parent=Relativistic charged-particle action}

Electromagnetic minimal coupling replaces the free kinetic momentum in an action or Hamiltonian by a combination involving the electromagnetic potential. For $L=-m\sqrt{\dot x^a\dot x_a}-qA_a\dot x^a$, the canonical covector is $P_a=-m\dot x_a/\sqrt{\dot x^2}-qA_a$, so $P_a+qA_a$ recovers the potential-independent covector. Under $A_a\mapsto A_a+\partial_a\chi$, the action changes by an endpoint term. The fixed-endpoint equations are the <relativistic Lorentz force> equation and are gauge invariant.

= Gauge invariance of the charged-particle worldline action
{parent=Relativistic charged-particle action}

Under $A_\mu\mapsto A_\mu+\partial_\mu\chi$, the interaction changes by
$$
q\int\frac{d\chi(x(\lambda))}{d\lambda}\,d\lambda
=q\,[\chi]_{\rm endpoints}.
$$
Fixed-endpoint equations of motion are therefore gauge invariant.

= Lorentz-force equation from the worldline action
{parent=Relativistic charged-particle action}
{c}

Varying the <relativistic charged-particle action> with fixed endpoints gives
$$
m\frac{du_\mu}{d\tau}=qF_{\mu\nu}u^\nu,
$$
or the equivalent raised-index equation.

= Electromagnetic four-potential
{parent=Relativistic electromagnetism}

= Four-potential
{synonym}

The four-potential combines the <electric potential> and <magnetic vector potential> as $A^\mu=(\phi/c,\mathbf A)$. The electromagnetic four-potential $A_\mu$ transforms under
$$
A_\mu\mapsto A_\mu+\partial_\mu\chi,
$$
while the antisymmetric field tensor $F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu A_\nu-\partial_\nu A_\mu$ is unchanged.

= Electromagnetic multipole expansion
{parent=Electromagnetic four-potential}

An electromagnetic multipole expansion expresses the potentials or fields of a localized source as successively smaller monopole, dipole, quadrupole, and higher moments in the ratio of source size to observation distance.

= Retarded potential
{parent=Electromagnetic four-potential}
{wiki}

= Retarded electromagnetic potential
{synonym}

A retarded potential evaluates each source at the event on the source's past light cone. This accounts for propagation at the speed of light and enforces <causality>.

= Retarded vector potential
{parent=Retarded potential}
{title2=$\mathbf A(\mathbf r,t)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\mathbf J(\mathbf r',t-|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|/c)}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}\,d^3r'$}

The causal <magnetic vector potential> generated by a <current density> in the <Lorenz gauge>, with no additional incoming homogeneous field. Each source contribution is evaluated at its <retarded time>. The integral is a convolution with the retarded wave-equation <Green function>; its validity includes the stated boundary and source conditions.

= Switched infinite-wire retarded potential
{parent=Retarded vector potential}

For a spatially uniform line current $I\Theta(t)$, the causal potential outside the line is $A_z=(\mu_0I/2\pi)\operatorname{arcosh}(ct/r)$ for $ct>r$, and zero before arrival. The finite causal integration length makes it finite at finite time, unlike an absolute magnetostatic potential for an infinite wire.

= Radial energy flow after switching a line current
{parent=Switched infinite-wire retarded potential}

The induced <electric field> opposes the increasing axial current, while the <magnetic field> circles it. Their <Poynting vector> points radially outward and transports energy into the growing magnetic-field region.

= Abrupt line-current wavefront singularity
{parent=Switched infinite-wire retarded potential}

The ideal fields after instantaneous switching behave as $E_z,H_\phi\propto(t-r/c)^{-1/2}$ near arrival, and the radial <Poynting vector> as $(t-r/c)^{-1}$. A finite switching rise time smooths the ideal front; the singular solution does not represent a finite-energy real pulse.

= Retarded time
{title2=$t_{\rm ret}$}
{parent=Retarded potential}
{wiki}

For a source point $y(s)$ and field event $(x,t)$, the retarded time solves
$$
t_{\rm ret}+\frac{|x-y(t_{\rm ret})|}{c}=t.
$$

= Uniqueness of retarded time for a subluminal source
{parent=Retarded time}

For $R(s)=|x-y(s)|$ and $|v|<c$, the function $F(s)=s+R(s)/c$ has
$$
F'(s)=1-\widehat R\cdot v/c>0.
$$
It is strictly increasing, so the retarded-time equation has at most one solution.

= Liénard–Wiechert potential
{c}
{parent=Retarded potential}
{wiki}

The potentials of a point charge $q$ are
$$
\phi=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac1{R-\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf R/c},
\qquad
\mathbf A=\frac{\mathbf v}{c^2}\phi,
$$
with every source quantity evaluated at the <retarded time>.

= Liénard-Wiechert field
{c}
{parent=Liénard–Wiechert potential}

The <Liénard-Wiechert field> is the <electric field> and <magnetic field> obtained from the retarded potentials of a moving point charge. In the nonrelativistic radiation zone, the electric radiation field is $\mathbf E_{\rm rad}=q\widehat{\mathbf n}\times(\widehat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf a)/(4\pi\epsilon_0c^2R)$ at <retarded time>. Its outgoing <Poynting vector> integrates to the <Larmor formula>. Coulomb and induction fields must also be retained when a near-field calculation is required.

= Electric field of a uniformly moving point charge
{parent=Liénard-Wiechert field}

Let $\mathbf R$ join the instantaneous position of charge q to the field point, with constant $\boldsymbol\beta=\mathbf v/c$. Transforming its rest-frame Coulomb field gives
$$
\mathbf E=\frac{q(1-\beta^2)\mathbf R}{4\pi\epsilon_0[R^2-|\boldsymbol\beta\times\mathbf R|^2]^{3/2}},\qquad
\mathbf B=\mathbf v\times\mathbf E/c^2.
$$
The <electric field> is radial from the current position, reduced along the <velocity> and enhanced transversely. This present-position form is compatible with the causal retarded solution specifically because motion is uniform. There is no acceleration-dependent radiation field, although the moving charge has a <magnetic field> and transports electromagnetic <energy>.

= Lorenz gauge
{c}
{synonym}

The Lorenz gauge condition is
$$
\frac1{c^2}\partial_t\phi+\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0,
$$
or $\partial_\mu A^\mu=0$ in covariant notation.

= Residual Lorenz gauge symmetry
{parent=Lorenz gauge condition}
{title2=$\Box\lambda=0$}

An Abelian <gauge transformation> $A_a\mapsto A_a+\partial_a\lambda$ changes $\partial\cdot A$ by $\Box\lambda$. Consequently <gauge fixing> by a quadratic function of $\partial\cdot A$ retains transformations satisfying $\Box\lambda=0$, subject to the same <boundary conditions>. Eliminating those residual transformations is part of specifying an inverse with a particular <Green function> prescription.

= Electromagnetic field tensor
{title2=$F^{\mu\nu}$}
{parent=Electromagnetic four-potential}
{wiki}

The electromagnetic field tensor is
$$
F^{\mu\nu}=\partial^\mu A^\nu-\partial^\nu A^\mu.
$$
It packages the electric and magnetic fields into an antisymmetric rank-two Lorentz tensor and transforms as
$$
F'^{\mu\nu}(x')
=\Lambda^\mu{}_\rho\Lambda^\nu{}_\sigma F^{\rho\sigma}(x).
$$

= Index conventions for the electromagnetic field tensor
{parent=Electromagnetic field tensor}

Choose coordinates $x^\mu=(ct,\mathbf x)$ and <Minkowski metric> $\eta=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$. With the convention $F^{0i}=-E_i/c$ and $F^{ij}=-\epsilon_{ijk}B_k$, lowering both <tensor> indices gives $F_{0i}=+E_i/c$ while leaving $F_{ij}=F^{ij}$. An array with all upper indices cannot be used unchanged as the corresponding array with all lower indices. The propagation <Lorentz four-vector> $n^\mu=(1,\mathbf s)$ for unit spatial direction $\mathbf s$ similarly has $n_\mu=(1,-\mathbf s)$. These signs matter in contractions that encode <Maxwell equations>.

= Dual electromagnetic field tensor
{parent=Electromagnetic field tensor}
{title2=$\widetilde F^{\mu\nu}$}

The dual electromagnetic field tensor is obtained by applying the <Hodge star operator> to the <electromagnetic field tensor>. For metric signature $(+---)$ and $\epsilon^{0123}=+1$, define $\widetilde F^{\mu\nu}=\tfrac12\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}F_{\rho\sigma}$. With $F^{0i}=-E_i/c$ and $F^{ij}=-\epsilon_{ijk}B_k$, it has components $\widetilde F^{0i}=-B_i$ and $\widetilde F^{ij}=\epsilon_{ijk}E_k/c$. The homogeneous <Maxwell equations> become $\partial_\mu\widetilde F^{\mu\nu}=0$.

= Normalization of the electromagnetic dual tensor
{parent=Dual electromagnetic field tensor}
{title2=$\widetilde F^{ab}=\tfrac12\epsilon^{abcd}F_{cd}$}

In oriented Minkowski coordinates with $(+---)$ signature and $\epsilon^{0123}=+1$, the conventional dual is $\widetilde F^{ab}=\tfrac12\epsilon^{abcd}F_{cd}$. For $F_{0i}=E_i$ and $F_{ij}=-\epsilon_{ijk}B_k$ in $c=1$ units, $\widetilde F^{ab}F_{ab}=-4\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B$. A definition without the half produces twice this dual and contraction $-8\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B$. The factor accounts for summing both orders of each antisymmetric index pair.

= Maxwell Lagrangian
{c}
{parent=Electromagnetic field tensor}
{title2=$\mathcal L=-\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$}

The source-free electromagnetic <Lagrangian density> is quadratic in the <electromagnetic field tensor>. Its <Euler-Lagrange field equation> is $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=0$. Antisymmetry of $F$ makes the density invariant under <gauge transformations>, and contraction of its indices makes it a <Lorentz scalar>.

= Maxwell action
{c}
{parent=Maxwell Lagrangian}
{title2=$S_E=\tfrac14\int F_{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}\,d^4x$}

The source-free <Maxwell Lagrangian> gives $S=-\tfrac14\int F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}\,d^4x$ in mostly-minus Lorentzian signature. Its <Euclidean action> is the displayed positive quadratic functional of the <electromagnetic field tensor>. Variation, with boundary terms vanishing, yields $\partial_\mu F_{\mu\nu}=0$.

= First-order Maxwell action
{parent=Maxwell action}
{title2=$S=\frac1{16\pi}\int\sqrt{-g}\,[F^{ab}F_{ab}-2F^{ab}(\nabla_aA_b-\nabla_bA_a)]$}

Vary the antisymmetric auxiliary <electromagnetic field tensor> and the <electromagnetic four-potential> independently. The first variation sets $F_{ab}=\nabla_aA_b-\nabla_bA_a$ algebraically, and variation of the potential gives $\nabla_bF^{ab}=0$. Substitution of the algebraic equation reduces the first-order action to the usual negative quadratic <Maxwell action>. Metric variation gives the same stress tensor after eliminating the auxiliary field, because the chain-rule term multiplying its variational equation then vanishes.

= Electromagnetic Bianchi identity
{c}
{parent=Electromagnetic field tensor}

Because the <electromagnetic field tensor> is the <exterior derivative> $F=dA$ of a potential, $dF=d^2A=0$. For a torsion-free connection this is
$$
\nabla_aF_{bc}+\nabla_bF_{ca}+\nabla_cF_{ab}=0,
$$
which is the covariant form of the two homogeneous <Maxwell equations>.

= Lorentz force law
{c}
{synonym}

The <Lorentz force> is the <force> exerted by an <electric field> $\mathbf E$ and a <magnetic field> $\mathbf B$ on a body with <electric charge> $q$ and <velocity> $\mathbf v$. In <SI> units its form in nonrelativistic <classical mechanics>, using <mass> $m$ and <Newton's second law>, is
$$
\mathbf F=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B),\qquad
m\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt}=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B).
$$
Both fields are evaluated at the body's <position> at the current <time>. The <electric field> contributes $q\mathbf E$ even at rest, whereas the magnetic <force> depends on <velocity> through a <cross product>. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/5-4-electric-field[OpenStax, electric force and field]{ref}; https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/11-2-magnetic-fields-and-lines[OpenStax, magnetic force on moving charge]{ref}.

For a concrete <charged particle in a uniform magnetic field>, use <nondimensionalization> to stipulate $m=q=B=1$, <magnetic field> $\mathbf B=(0,0,1)$, zero <electric field>, initial <position> $(0,0,0)$, and initial <velocity> $(1,0,0)$. These are rescaled quantities, not an assertion that the <mass>, <electric charge>, and <magnetic field> have the same physical units. At the start, $(1,0,0)\times(0,0,1)=(0,-1,0)$, so the magnetic <acceleration> points toward $-y$. The resulting <circular motion> is
$$
\mathbf r(t)=(\sin t,\cos t-1,0),\qquad
\mathbf v(t)=(\cos t,-\sin t,0),\qquad
\mathbf a(t)=(-\sin t,-\cos t,0).
$$
At $t=\pi/2$ the <position> is $(1,-1,0)$ and the <velocity> is $(0,-1,0)$; at $t=\pi$ the <position> is $(0,-2,0)$ and the <velocity> is $(-1,0,0)$. Thus the <circle> has center $(0,-1,0)$ and <radius> one. Viewed from $+z$ toward the $xy$ <plane>, the motion is clockwise. Changing only the <electric charge> to $q=-1$ gives <position> $(\sin t,1-\cos t,0)$ and reverses the bending to counterclockwise.

Adding initial <velocity> $v_z=0.5$ leaves the transverse <circular motion> unchanged and gives
$$
\mathbf r(t)=(\sin t,\cos t-1,0.5t),\qquad
\mathbf v(t)=(\cos t,-\sin t,0.5).
$$
This is a <helix> of transverse <radius> one, <cyclotron frequency> $\omega_c=1$, and transverse <period> $T=2\pi$; the advance along $z$ in one turn is $\pi$. The parallel component of <velocity> stays constant because the magnetic <force> has no parallel component: there is no magnetic <acceleration> along the <magnetic field>. If the initial <velocity> were entirely parallel to the <magnetic field>, the magnetic <force> would vanish and the path would be straight. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/11-3-motion-of-a-charged-particle-in-a-magnetic-field[OpenStax, circular and helical motion]{ref}.

More generally, with zero <electric field>, no other <forces>, and a uniform constant <magnetic field> of magnitude $B>0$, decompose <velocity> into perpendicular and parallel components. For nonzero <electric charge> $q$, the perpendicular <speed> $v_\perp$ determines the <gyroradius>, while the <cyclotron frequency> is an <angular frequency>:
$$
r=\frac{mv_\perp}{|q|B},\qquad
\omega_c=\frac{|q|B}{m},\qquad
T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_c}=\frac{2\pi m}{|q|B}.
$$
These are nonrelativistic formulas; see the <relativistic cyclotron orbit> for the correction. The beauty is that increasing perpendicular <speed> increases the <radius> in exactly the proportion needed to leave the <period> unchanged. Conversely, known <magnetic field>, perpendicular <speed>, and measured <radius> determine the magnitude of the <electric charge> divided by <mass>. This makes the same geometry useful for comparing bodies with different <electric charge> and <mass>.

Two short checks explain the result. First, the <cross product> gives $\mathbf v\times\mathbf B=(-\sin t,-\cos t,0)$ for both stipulated positive-<electric charge> paths, exactly their <acceleration>. Second, the transverse magnetic <force> supplies the <centripetal acceleration>, so $mv_\perp^2/r=|q|v_\perp B$; for $v_\perp>0$ this gives the <gyroradius>, and $\omega_c=v_\perp/r$ gives the <cyclotron frequency>.

<Magnetic forces do no work>: the instantaneous <power> of the magnetic part of the <Lorentz force> vanishes because a <cross product> is perpendicular to its first argument. The <work-energy theorem> therefore gives
$$
\frac{dK}{dt}=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v
=q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v
+q\mathbf v\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)
=q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v,
\qquad K=\tfrac12m|\mathbf v|^2.
$$
The <dot product> $\mathbf v\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)$ is zero at every instant, rather than only after a full turn. With zero <electric field> and no other <forces>, the <kinetic energy> and <speed> remain constant; the <electric field> can increase or decrease <kinetic energy> according to the sign of $q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v$. In the two stipulated examples the constant <kinetic energy> is respectively $K=1/2$ and $K=5/8$ in rescaled units. The extra parallel <velocity> raises <kinetic energy> without changing the <gyroradius> or the <cyclotron frequency>.

For an electric example in rescaled units, stipulate $m=q=1$, zero <magnetic field>, constant <electric field> $\mathbf E=(2,0,0)$, no other <forces>, and rest at $t=0$. The <Lorentz force> gives <acceleration> $(2,0,0)$ and <velocity> $(2t,0,0)$, hence <kinetic energy> $K=2t^2$ and <power> $dK/dt=4t=q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v$. At $t=1$, the <kinetic energy> has risen from zero to two: the <electric field> has done positive <work>.

The existing <electromagnetic field tensor> formulation expresses the same interaction covariantly. For <four-momentum> $p^\mu$ and <four-velocity> $u^\mu$, using <proper time> $\tau$, the <Lorentz force law> is
$$
\frac{dp^\mu}{d\tau}=qF^{\mu\nu}u_\nu.
$$
Its spatial and temporal components are
$$
\frac{d\mathbf p}{dt}=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B),
\qquad
\frac{d\mathcal E}{dt}=q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v.
$$

= Magnetic force
{parent=Lorentz force}
{title2=$\mathbf F_B=q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$}

The magnetic contribution to the <Lorentz force> on a <point charge>. It is perpendicular to that charge's instantaneous <velocity>, so its direct work on the charge is zero. Summing contributions in a conductor leads to the <magnetic force on a current-carrying wire>; mechanical work in moving circuits must also account for the electric fields and constraints that maintain the current.

= Force on a current loop in a bilinear magnetic field
{parent=Lorentz force}
{title2=$\mathbf F=I\alpha a^3(\hat{\mathbf x}+\hat{\mathbf y})/2$}

For a square spanning $0\leq x,y\leq a$, with current counterclockwise as seen from positive $z$, an applied field $B_z=\alpha xy$ vanishes on two edges. Integrating $I\,d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf B$ on the other two gives the displayed force. Its direction reverses with the current. The formula concerns the loop, not unspecified supply leads, and cannot be replaced by a small-loop approximation without checking the field variation.

= Cyclotron radius
{parent=Lorentz force}
{title2=$r_c$}

The radius of a charged particle orbit perpendicular to a uniform <magnetic field>. In a parabolic band $r_c=m^*v_\perp/(|q|B)$, or $r_c=\hbar k_F/(e|B|)$ for a Fermi-level <electron>. Charge sign sets the curvature direction, while the radius uses magnitudes.

= Magnetic rigidity
{parent=Lorentz force}
{title2=$B r=p_\perp/|q|$}
{wiki}

<Magnetic rigidity> measures the resistance of a <charged particle>'s <momentum> to bending by a <magnetic field>. The <relativistic cyclotron orbit> gives $p_\perp=|q|Br$, where $r$ is the transverse <gyroradius>. For motion perpendicular to a uniform <magnetic field>, $p_\perp=p$ is the whole <momentum>. A measured <magnetic rigidity> fixes <momentum> per magnitude of <electric charge>; it does not by itself fix <mass>, <kinetic energy>, or the sign of the <electric charge>. This distinction makes the <Anderson positron track experiment> an especially revealing application of the <Lorentz force>.

= Anderson positron track experiment
{c}
{parent=Magnetic rigidity}

The <Anderson positron track experiment> identified a light positively <charged particle>, now called the <positron>, by combining magnetic bending, loss of <kinetic energy> in <lead>, and <particle range>. Each observation answered a different question: bending constrained <momentum>, the <lead> supplied a direction of travel, and <particle range> tested the proposed <mass>.

<Carl David Anderson>'s https://journals.aps.org/pr/pdf/10.1103/PhysRev.43.491[The Positive Electron] (1933), pp. 491–493, describes the 1932 discovery in a vertical <cloud chamber> with a $15{,}000\,\mathrm{gauss}$ <magnetic field>. A $6\,\mathrm{mm}$ <lead> plate separated two photographed track segments. The effective viewing depth was $1\,\mathrm{cm}$ along the <magnetic field>, with $14\,\mathrm{cm}$ transverse diameter: this restricted out-of-plane motion. The photographic readout supplied <curvature> on both sides. Comparisons with <electron> tracks under the same conditions constrained <ionization>; specific <ionization> was not directly measured for these events. He tested accidental alignment of unrelated tracks and rejected a reversed <electron> interpretation requiring an increase of <kinetic energy> in <lead>. Assuming unit <electric charge>, the <mass> bound was about twenty <electron> masses, rather than an exact <mass> determination. The paper credits <Robert Andrews Millikan> with the design and <Seth Henry Neddermeyer> with assistance.<The Positive Electron by Carl David Anderson>[pp. 491–493, Fig. 1 and charge/mass discussion; p. 494, acknowledgment]{ref}

Preparation used rapid piston expansion and a <water>–<alcohol> mixture in the <cloud chamber>; cooling permitted droplets to form along <ionization> trails. A camera photographed these droplets. Initially, expansions occurred at random, so many photographs contained no tracks. Later counter triggering improved the useful yield.<Biographical Memoirs: Volume 73/Carl David Anderson by William Hayward Pickering>[Biographical Memoirs: Volume 73; William H. Pickering, Carl David Anderson, pp. 27–28: preparation, photographs and timing]{ref}

In https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/anderson-lecture.pdf[The Production and Properties of Positrons], his 1936 lecture, <Carl David Anderson> describes the chamber inside an <electromagnet> and explains why the <lead> plate was decisive: after losing <kinetic energy>, a particle bent more sharply. He describes a <proton> hypothesis predicting only $5\,\mathrm{mm}$ of <particle range>, against an observed path longer than $50\,\mathrm{mm}$. These are historical values from the lecture, not the stipulated example below. The point was the incompatibility of <particle range> with the <momentum> inferred from <curvature>, rather than positive <electric charge> alone.<Nobel Lectures, Physics 1922–1941/The Production and Properties of Positrons by Carl David Anderson>[Nobel Lectures, Physics 1922–1941; pp. 365–366, electromagnet, lead-plate direction test and proton range comparison]{ref}

= Charge-sign ambiguity of a curved track
{parent=Anderson positron track experiment}

A photograph records a path but supplies no arrow of <time>. The <Lorentz force> depends on $q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$: reversing both <electric charge> and direction of travel leaves the spatial bending compatible with the same path. Thus a particular arc can represent a <positron> travelling one way or an <electron> travelling the other. Knowing the <magnetic field>'s polarity is necessary but insufficient. <Gravity> does not justify assuming that every <cosmic ray> track travels downward.

The <lead> plate adds a physical ordering. For a single particle crossing a passive absorber without an accelerating interaction, the outgoing <momentum> is smaller, so the outgoing <gyroradius> is smaller in the same <magnetic field>. The less curved segment comes first. Once this direction is assigned, the <cross product> in the <Lorentz force> determines the sign of <electric charge>. For the convention below, $\mathbf B=B\widehat{\mathbf z}$ points out of the page; at upward <velocity> $\mathbf v=v\widehat{\mathbf y}$, $\mathbf v\times\mathbf B=vB\widehat{\mathbf x}$. Bending to the right therefore means positive <electric charge>.

= Proton exclusion by magnetic rigidity and range
{parent=Anderson positron track experiment}
{title2=$K=\sqrt{p^2c^2+m^2c^4}-mc^2$}

For a concrete independent example, stipulate perpendicular motion, $|q|=e$ with $e$ the <elementary charge>, uniform <magnetic field> $B=1.00\,\mathrm T$, incoming <gyroradius> $r_{\rm in}=0.100\,\mathrm m$ and outgoing <gyroradius> $r_{\rm out}=0.0500\,\mathrm m$. These are invented teaching inputs. Converting <SI> <momentum> to <electronvolt> units gives
$$
p[\mathrm{MeV}/c]=299.792458\,B[\mathrm T]r[\mathrm m]
\quad (|q|=e),
$$
so $p_{\rm in}=29.9792458\,\mathrm{MeV}/c$ and $p_{\rm out}=14.9896229\,\mathrm{MeV}/c$. Both a <positron> and a <proton> with these <momenta> trace the same ideal arcs. Their <kinetic energies> differ because the <relativistic energy-momentum relation> gives
$$
K=\sqrt{(pc)^2+(mc^2)^2}-mc^2
=\frac{(pc)^2}{\sqrt{(pc)^2+(mc^2)^2}+mc^2}.
$$
The second expression avoids subtracting nearly equal quantities when $pc\ll mc^2$. For this calculation stipulate rounded <rest energies> $m_ec^2=0.511\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and $m_pc^2=938.272\,\mathrm{MeV}$. The <positron> interpretation gives $K_{\rm in}\simeq29.473\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and $K_{\rm out}\simeq14.487\,\mathrm{MeV}$; the <proton> interpretation gives only $0.479\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and $0.120\,\mathrm{MeV}$. The corresponding proposed losses of <kinetic energy> are about $14.985\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and $0.359\,\mathrm{MeV}$.

= Charge-to-mass ratio
{parent=Lorentz force}
{title2=$q/m$}

The <charge-to-mass ratio> is a body's <electric charge> $q$ divided by its <mass> $m$. Its <SI> unit is the <coulomb> per kilogram, $\mathrm{C\,kg^{-1}}$. In a prescribed <electric field> and <magnetic field>, nonrelativistic <acceleration> depends on $q/m$ through the <Lorentz force>. Two bodies with the same initial <position>, <velocity>, and <charge-to-mass ratio> therefore have the same trajectory in this ideal model. For an <electron>, write $q=-e$ with $e>0$ the <elementary charge>: $q/m=-e/m$, whereas the positive number $e/m$ is the magnitude of the <charge-to-mass ratio>.

= Velocity selector
{parent=Lorentz force}
{title2=$v_0=E/B_s$}

A <velocity selector> uses perpendicular <electric field> and <magnetic field> to transmit a narrow range of <speeds> along a chosen beam direction. Assume uniform static <electric field> $\mathbf E=E\widehat{\mathbf y}$ and <magnetic field> $\mathbf B_s=B_s\widehat{\mathbf z}$, with $E,B_s>0$, and an incident <velocity> $\mathbf v=v\widehat{\mathbf x}$. The <cross product> gives $\mathbf v\times\mathbf B_s=-vB_s\widehat{\mathbf y}$, so the transverse <Lorentz force> is
$$
F_y=q(E-vB_s),\qquad F_y=0\ \Longrightarrow\ v_0=\frac{E}{B_s}.
$$
For an <electron>, the electric <force> points downward and the magnetic <force> upward. A faster <electron> initially bends upward; a slower one initially bends downward. At the selected <speed>, both <forces> cancel along the whole straight trajectory. The cancellation is independent of <mass> and nonzero <electric charge>; it selects <velocity>, not a particular <charge-to-mass ratio>. A component of <velocity> parallel to the <magnetic field> is not constrained by the cancellation, so the incident beam must also have its direction restricted.

= Electron charge-to-mass experiment
{synonym}

The <Thomson charge-to-mass experiment> infers the magnitude of an <electron>'s <charge-to-mass ratio> by combining electric and magnetic deflection of <cathode rays>. The central idea is to determine <speed> separately from magnetic curvature: a known <speed> turns the observed <gyroradius> into a measurement of $e/m$.

In 1897, <J. J. Thomson> used two grounded slits in an evacuated glass tube, then <aluminium> plates $5\,\mathrm{cm}$ long, $2\,\mathrm{cm}$ wide and $1.5\,\mathrm{cm}$ apart. A <battery> supplied their <electric potential difference>. Deflected <cathode rays> produced a <phosphorescence> patch on the glass; an external scale and movable luminous pointer recorded its position in darkness. Greater evacuation reduced gas <electrical conductivity> that screened the <electric field>; reversing plate polarity reversed deflection. External coils supplied the <magnetic field>, calibrated by induction against larger coils. He adjusted equal electric and magnetic deflections. <Cathode Rays by Joseph John Thomson>[Cathode Rays (1897), Fig. 2; second m/e method, apparatus and calibration]{ref}

His comparison included air, <hydrogen>, <carbon dioxide>, and <aluminium> versus <platinum> <cathodes>, supporting a common carrier. He explicitly noted uncorrected magnetic bending outside the plates. His tables give $m/e$, the reciprocal of the magnitude used here. The inferred ratio alone could not separate <electric charge> from <mass>. <Cathode Rays by Joseph John Thomson>[Cathode Rays (1897), second m/e method: table, corrections and interpretation]{ref}

To connect this readout with the <Lorentz force>, derive a simple nonrelativistic model in modern <SI> notation. Let the uniform deflecting region have <length> $\ell$, followed by a field-free drift of <length> $L$. Assume a narrow <speed> distribution, small deflection <angles>, negligible collisions, and matching electric and magnetic field profiles. For an <electron> with <speed> $v$, the electric <acceleration> has magnitude $eE/m$, the transit <time> is approximately $\ell/v$, and the subsequent drift adds exit transverse <velocity> times $L/v$. Thus the displacement magnitudes measured separately with only <electric field> or only <magnetic field> are
$$
|y_E|=\frac{eE}{mv^2}\ell\left(L+\frac{\ell}{2}\right),\qquad
|y_B|\simeq\frac{eB}{mv}\ell\left(L+\frac{\ell}{2}\right).
$$
The electric expression follows from constant transverse <acceleration>; the magnetic expression uses its small-deflection approximation. Equal displacement magnitudes then give
$$
v\simeq\frac{E}{B},\qquad
\frac{e}{m}\simeq\frac{E|y_E|}{B^2\ell(L+\ell/2)}.
$$
This is an ideal derivation of the comparison principle, not a numerical reanalysis of the historical tube. Different exterior <magnetic field> profiles invalidate cancellation of the common geometrical factor. The comparison determines $e/m$ through deflection and the known apparatus dimensions; it does not require a downstream semicircle.

For a second, deliberately different teaching geometry, combine a <velocity selector> with a separate region containing only a uniform <magnetic field> $B_a$ perpendicular to the selected <velocity>. Here $B_s$ belongs to the <velocity selector>, while $B_a$ belongs to the analyzer: they need not be equal. The magnetic <Lorentz force> supplies the <centripetal acceleration>, so
$$
\frac{mv^2}{r}=evB_a,\qquad
\frac{e}{m}=\frac{v}{B_a r}=\frac{E}{B_s B_a r}.
$$
If the analyzer turns the beam through a semicircle before it hits a screen in the entry plane, the separation $D$ between entrance and impact is the diameter: $r=D/2$. For a shorter observed arc, the screen displacement is not generally a <radius>; fit the appropriate <circle> geometry. This two-stage <velocity selector> and curvature apparatus is an original ideal teaching model, not a claim that Thomson's Fig. 2 used a separate selector and semicircular analyzer.

The <SI> units provide an independent check: $(\mathrm{V/m})/\mathrm T=\mathrm{m/s}$ and $(\mathrm{m/s})/(\mathrm{T\,m})=\mathrm{C/kg}$, because $\mathrm T=\mathrm{kg/(C\,s)}$. The <charge-to-mass ratio> of the <electron> is negative; this calculation gives its magnitude. The bending direction gives the sign of the <electric charge> once the <magnetic field> direction is known. Scaling both $e$ and $m$ by the same positive factor preserves every displayed trajectory, so an independent <electric charge> measurement is required to determine <mass> separately.

The curvature formula assumes nonrelativistic <momentum> $p=mv$. At higher <speeds>, the <relativistic cyclotron orbit> gives $p=\gamma mv=eB_a r$, hence $e/m=\gamma v/(B_a r)$, where $\gamma$ is the <Lorentz factor>; the ideal straight-through selection $v=E/B_s$ still follows from vanishing <Lorentz force>. The stipulated <speed> is about $0.0067$ times the <speed of light>, making $\gamma-1$ about $2.2\times10^{-5}$. For this example the nonrelativistic approximation is adequate, but no massive particle can be selected at $E/B_s\geq c$. Finite slit widths, collisions with residual <gas>, uncertain <magnetic field> calibration, and fields outside the ideal boundaries limit a real inference; repeat at several selected <speeds> and analyzer strengths to test consistency of $e/m$ rather than trusting one spot position.

= Lenard window

The <Lenard cathode-ray window experiment> lets <cathode rays> leave a <gas-discharge tube> through a thin <aluminium> foil while preserving the tube's <vacuum>. This separates production from propagation.

<Philipp Lenard>'s lecture, delivered on 28 May 1906, describes sealing a small hole in a metal plate with <aluminium> foil. Luminous phosphor grains first rested on the window, then glowed above it in air. His improved tube separated production and observation chambers; coated paper served as a <fluorescent screen>. An aperture revealed spreading beyond a narrow straight beam. Evacuating the observation chamber reduced spreading and attenuation without interrupting production. Interposed layers and stepped foil stacks gave shadow comparisons; approximate scaling with mass had exceptions.<On Cathode Rays by Philipp Lenard>[On cathode rays, title page p. 105; pp. 108–114, Figs. 3–8: foil window, phosphor readout, aperture, layer comparisons and evacuated observation chamber]{ref}

The experimental advantage is unusually clean: reducing the <gas> content in the observation chamber tests whether <gas> is needed to carry the <cathode rays>, while retaining the conditions needed to generate them. The window is a boundary that transmits some <electrons> while confining the source environment. A spot outside establishes a transmitted effect; the aperture and changed observation environment provide additional ways to distinguish propagation from the source's behavior. This logic is useful whenever a complicated source would otherwise be changed by the very intervention intended to test the emitted beam.

Several inferences require care. A darker <fluorescent screen> can mean fewer arriving <electrons>, less deposited <energy> per <electron>, or redistribution by <electron scattering> beyond the observed area. A visible endpoint depends on the screen's sensitivity and background. It is not an intrinsic <electron> range independent of initial <kinetic energy>, <gas> <density>, and observation geometry. For a modern comparison, measure a reference at fixed source conditions, use <background subtraction>, and compare the whole luminous patch as well as a small central region. A wider collection area tests redistribution; changing source-to-screen separation also changes collection geometry. These are proposed controls for interpreting a readout, not additional historical observations attributed to Lenard.

Nor is <fluorescence> a unique signature of <cathode rays>: <X-rays> can also excite it. Lenard notes that a later tube could transmit both, and that slow <cathode rays> failed to pass its window.<On Cathode Rays by Philipp Lenard>[On cathode rays, pp. 114–115 and 120–121: X-rays in the later tube; limited transmitted velocity range]{ref} In modern terms, <cathode rays> are <electrons> carrying negative <electric charge>, whereas <X-rays> are <photons> with zero <electric charge>. Magnetic bending of the incident <electrons>, interpreted through the <Lorentz force>, supplies a discrimination that brightness alone cannot. Passage through foil alone determines neither the sign of <electric charge> nor its magnitude, and it does not measure <charge-to-mass ratio>. The separate <Thomson charge-to-mass experiment> explains that inference. Even a measured <charge-to-mass ratio> needs an independent <electric charge> measurement to determine <mass>.

= Cathode-ray attenuation with finite detector acceptance
{parent=Lenard cathode-ray window experiment}
{title2=$U(x)=e^{-(a+b)x},\quad W(x)=e^{-ax}$}

An original toy model separates two ways to lose a central <cathode-ray> signal. Let $x$ be forward depth into a uniform <gas>. Normalize the entering <electron> population to one. Write $U(x)$ for the fraction still in a narrow forward channel, and $D(x)$ for the fraction diverted into a wider collection channel. Assume constant removal rate $a$ per unit depth in both channels, and constant transfer rate $b$ from $U$ to $D$, with no return. Removal means that an <electron> stops or is trapped before reaching the collecting plane; it does not mean that its <electric charge> disappears. The wide ideal collector accepts all surviving members of both channels. This is a stipulated two-population <differential equation> model, not a general law of <electron> transport:
$$
\frac{dU}{dx}=-(a+b)U,\qquad
\frac{dD}{dx}=bU-aD,\qquad
U(0)=1,\quad D(0)=0.
$$
The first equation gives <exponential decay>. Adding the equations gives $W'= -aW$ for $W=U+D$, hence
$$
U(x)=e^{-(a+b)x},\qquad
W(x)=e^{-ax},\qquad
D(x)=e^{-ax}\left(1-e^{-bx}\right).
$$
These formulas also give a conservation check: $U+D+(1-W)=1$. The fraction $1-W$ has been removed before the plane, while $D$ remains present but misses the narrow collector. An exponential fit to the narrow signal therefore measures $a+b$, not the removal rate $a$ by itself. This is an <identifiability> issue: one declining signal cannot distinguish the two unknown rates.

For a worked example, choose $a=0.15\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ and $b=0.35\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. These are invented teaching inputs, not measured coefficients for air or Lenard's apparatus. At $x=2.00\,\mathrm{cm}$,
$$
U=e^{-1}\simeq0.3679,\qquad
W=e^{-0.3}\simeq0.7408,\qquad
D\simeq0.3729,\qquad
1-W\simeq0.2592.
$$
Thus about $63.2\%$ misses the narrow collector, although only about $25.9\%$ has been removed in this model. Treating the whole narrow-signal deficit as stopping would confuse redistribution with removal.

Suppose a calibrated readout is proportional to arriving <electron> number with the same response for either channel. Choose an empty-path signal $R_0=1020$ arbitrary units, of which $B=20$ is background; the two collection geometries have the same calibrated reference. The calculated readings at $2\,\mathrm{cm}$ are $R_{\rm n}=20+1000e^{-1}\simeq387.88$ and $R_{\rm w}=20+1000e^{-0.3}\simeq760.82$. After <background subtraction>, define $T_j=(R_j-B)/(R_0-B)$ for each geometry. Then the original <inverse problem> has the solution
$$
a=-\frac{\ln T_{\rm w}}{x}=0.15\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}},\qquad
b=-\frac{\ln T_{\rm n}}{x}-a=0.35\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}.
$$
A single narrow reading would instead allow every nonnegative pair with $a+b=0.50\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. Using the raw ratios without subtracting $B$ gives an erroneous narrow rate of about $0.4834\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. In a real <fluorescent screen>, the assumed response must be tested: deposited <energy> and conversion to <light> need not remain constant as <electrons> slow. Repeating the comparison at several depths tests the constant slopes, although straight slopes alone do not establish the microscopic mechanism.

Actual <electrons> change <kinetic energy> and direction. <Electron stopping power> $S(K)=-dK/ds>0$ describes mean <kinetic energy> loss per travelled <path length> $s$; collisional losses include <ionization>, while radiative losses include <bremsstrahlung>. The <continuous slowing-down approximation range> integrates reciprocal total <electron stopping power>, neglecting fluctuations. It approximates travelled <path length>, not forward penetration depth.<Stopping-Power and Range Tables for Electrons, Protons, and Helium Ions by M. J. Berger and others/Appendix: Significance of Calculated Quantities>[Stopping-Power and Range Tables for Electrons, Protons, and Helium Ions by M. J. Berger and others; Appendix: Significance of Calculated Quantities, collision/radiative/total stopping power, CSDA range, projected range and detour factor]{ref}
$$
R_{\mathrm{CSDA}}(K_0)=\int_0^{K_0}\frac{dK}{S(K)}.
$$
As a separate original limiting example, stipulate a straight path, constant $S=2\,\mathrm{keV\,cm^{-1}}$, and entering <kinetic energy> $K_0=6\,\mathrm{keV}$. Then $K(s)=6\,\mathrm{keV}-Ss$ until stopping, and $R_{\mathrm{CSDA}}=3\,\mathrm{cm}$. In that deterministic model every entering <electron> reaches each depth below $3\,\mathrm{cm}$ and none crosses a plane beyond it. There is no exponential population tail. Real fluctuations and <electron scattering> make the situation more complicated, but the example already proves that continuous <energy> loss does not imply constant-rate removal.

The window also changes the entering population: source <kinetic energy> before the foil need not equal $K_0$ just outside it. For an <electron> initially at rest, an ideal static accelerating <electric potential difference> $V>0$ supplies electrical <work> $eV$, where $e$ is the <elementary charge>. If losses before and within the foil total $\Delta K\geq0$, a transmitted <electron> enters the observation chamber with $K_0=eV-\Delta K\leq eV$. A screen's brightness determines neither $V$ nor $\Delta K$. These formulas specify a modern ideal <work-energy theorem> argument; no numerical historical <voltage> or foil thickness is inferred from the diagram.

= Hall effect
{c}
{parent=Lorentz force}
{wiki}

The <Hall effect> is a transverse <electric potential difference> produced when an <electric current> flows through a material in a transverse <magnetic field>. The contact orientation fixes its measured sign. The <Drude conductivity and Hall experiment> gives an explicit convention and the limitations of inferring a <number density> from it.

= Hall angle
{c}
{parent=Hall effect}
{title2=$\tan\theta_H=E_y/E_x$}

In an isotropic single-carrier <Drude model>, let the <electric current density> point along $x$, with zero transverse current and <magnetic field> along $z$. The <Hall coefficient> is $R_H=1/(nq)$ and the longitudinal <electrical conductivity> is $\sigma=n|q|\mu$, where the <charge carrier mobility> $\mu$ is positive. Consequently $E_y/E_x=R_H\sigma B=\operatorname{sgn}(q)\mu B$. This signed Hall angle convention describes the angle of the electric field relative to the current; a convention measuring current relative to field has the opposite sign.

= Hall voltage
{c}
{parent=Hall effect}
{title2=$V_H$}

A transverse <Hall voltage> develops when a longitudinal current flows through a sample in a perpendicular <magnetic field>. With $R_H=E_y/(j_xB_z)$, a uniform slab of thickness $t$ along the magnetic field has a signed transverse voltage magnitude set by $R_HIB_z/t$, with the final sign determined by the chosen voltage leads.

{c}

The <Hall coefficient> relates a transverse <electric field> to longitudinal <current density> and transverse <magnetic field>, with oriented axes as in the displayed formula. Its <SI> unit is $\mathrm{m^3/C}$. For one isotropic <Drude model> species it is $1/(nq)$; multiple <charge carriers> need not follow that formula.

= Free-electron count from a Hall coefficient
{parent=Hall coefficient}
{title2=$z=M/(e|R_H|\rho_mN_A)$}

In a one-carrier <Drude model>, the <Hall coefficient> is $R_H=1/(nq)$. Combining its inferred <electron density> with atomic density $\rho_mN_A/M$ gives the displayed number of mobile <electrons> per atom. The <molar mass> and <mass density> must be supplied independently. Multiple bands, anisotropic carrier motion and Hall factors limit this simple interpretation; field reversal helps distinguish the transverse response from voltage offsets.

= Hall scattering factor
{c}
{parent=Hall coefficient}
{title2=$r_H$}

For one positive carrier population, define $r_H$ by $R_H=r_H/(ep)$ with $e>0$. Different statistical averages of energy-dependent scattering affect <hall conductivity> and longitudinal <electrical conductivity>, so $r_H$ need not be one. The measured <Hall mobility> is $\mu_H=\sigma R_H=r_H\mu_{\rm drift}$. Multiple <charge carriers> require a more general model even before such a correction is made.

= Hall carrier count versus valence
{c}
{parent=Hall coefficient}

In a one-carrier isotropic <Drude model>, $R_H=1/(nq)$ and an inferred <carrier density> is $n_H=1/(e|R_H|)$. Dividing by the atomic <number density> gives an effective Hall count per atom. A noninteger result need not mean a literal fractional count of <valence electrons>: multiple <energy bands>, anisotropic <Fermi surfaces> and different <charge carrier mobilities> can change the <Hall coefficient>. In a two-carrier isotropic approximation,
$$
R_H=\frac{p\mu_h^2-n\mu_e^2}{e(p\mu_h+n\mu_e)^2}.
$$
Its sign identifies the dominant Hall response rather than necessarily the most numerous <charge carrier>.

= Two-carrier Hall coefficient
{parent=Hall coefficient}
{title2=$R_H=\dfrac{p\mu_h^2-n\mu_e^2}{e(p\mu_h+n\mu_e)^2}$}

For isotropic <electrons> and <electron holes> with positive mobilities, adding their weak-field <electrical conductivity> tensors and imposing zero transverse total current gives
$$
R_H=\frac{p\mu_h^2-n\mu_e^2}{e(p\mu_h+n\mu_e)^2},\qquad
\sigma=e(n\mu_e+p\mu_h).
$$
The <Hall coefficient> changes sign at $p\mu_h^2=n\mu_e^2$. Carrier abundance alone does not fix the sign; mobilities enter quadratically. The formula assumes $\mu_e B\ll1$, $\mu_h B\ll1$, independent semiclassical carriers, and no anomalous Hall contribution.

The <quantum Hall effect> connects an electrical measurement to a quantum counting rule: a <two-dimensional electron gas> in a perpendicular <magnetic field> can exhibit plateaus of transverse <electrical resistance>. The integer case supplies a resistance scale built from the <Planck constant> and <elementary charge>, rather than the sample's dimensions. The <fractional quantum Hall effect> involves additional many-<electron> physics.

= von Klitzing-Dorda-Pepper quantum Hall experiment
{c}
{parent=Integer quantum Hall effect}

<Klaus von Klitzing>, <G. Dorda> and <M. Pepper> reported a silicon <MOSFET> measurement in 1980. Its gate controlled an <semiconductor inversion layer>; transverse contacts measured the Hall voltage and separate longitudinal contacts measured dissipation. = Quantum Hall conductivity tensor inversion
{parent=Integer quantum Hall effect}
{title2=$\boldsymbol\rho_s=\boldsymbol\sigma_s^{-1}$}

Choose conventional <surface current density> along $+x$, <magnetic field> along $+z$, and negative <electron> charge. An isotropic in-plane response has the following <sheet conductivity> and <sheet resistivity> <tensors> for $s\geq0$, $k>0$:
$$
\mathbf K=\frac{e^2}{h}\begin{pmatrix}s&-k\\k&s\end{pmatrix}\mathbf E,
\qquad
\mathbf E=R_K\frac1{s^2+k^2}\begin{pmatrix}s&k\\-k&s\end{pmatrix}\mathbf K.
$$
These are <matrix inverses>, rather than componentwise reciprocals. The <sheet conductivity> has units <siemens>, the <sheet resistivity> <ohms>. With $K_y=0$, the sign is $E_y/K_x<0$, agreeing with the negative <Hall coefficient> of a single <electron> species. Contact polarity can reverse the measured voltage sign, so quote the Hall magnitude. For a homogeneous strip of width $W$ and longitudinal probe separation $L$, $I=WK_x$ gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{|V_H|}{I}=R_K\frac{k}{s^2+k^2},\qquad
\frac{V_{xx}}{I}=R_K\frac{L}{W}\frac{s}{s^2+k^2}.}
$$
At $s=0$, $k=N$, these reduce to $R_K/N$ and zero. A nonzero transverse <sheet resistivity> creates no local <Joule heating>: the antisymmetric contribution cancels in $\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf K=(e^2/h)s|\mathbf E|^2$.

For an original nonideal example, choose $k=2$, $s=0.01$ and $L/W=2$. Then $V_{xx}/I\simeq129.061\,\Omega$, while the Hall magnitude is below $R_K/2$ by the relative amount $s^2/(k^2+s^2)\simeq25.0$ parts per million. An unwanted admixture $0.02V_{xx}$ in the Hall readout would instead introduce approximately $2.58\,\Omega$. The chosen calculation shows why a small intrinsic tensor correction does not by itself guarantee an accurate contact measurement.

= Laughlin wavefunction
{c}
{parent=Fractional quantum Hall effect}
{title2=$\Psi_m=\mathcal N\prod_{i<j}(z_i-z_j)^m e^{-\sum_i|z_i|^2/(4\ell_B^2)}$}
{wiki}

<Robert Laughlin>'s 1983 <variational method> uses pairwise zeros to suppress close <electron> encounters within the <lowest Landau level>. For aligned <spin>, odd $m$ gives <fermionic exchange symmetry>.<Anomalous Quantum Hall Effect: An Incompressible Quantum Fluid with Fractionally Charged Excitations by Robert Laughlin>[pp. 1395–1396, Eqs. (5)–(7)]{ref}

Here $\mathcal N$ enforces <wavefunction normalization>, $\ell_B$ is the <magnetic length>, and <complex coordinates> $z_j=x_j-iy_j$ describe <electrons> in a <magnetic field> $B\widehat{\mathbf z}$, $B>0$, with the <symmetric gauge>. Reversing the orientation conjugates the <polynomial> without changing <probability densities>. The <Gaussian function> confines the <wavefunction>.

The exchange check is especially simple for two <electrons>. Writing $w=z_1-z_2$, exchange sends $w\mapsto-w$, so $w^m\mapsto(-1)^mw^m$. An aligned two-<electron> <spin> state is symmetric, requiring the spatial <wavefunction> to change sign. Hence $m=1,3,5,\ldots$ works, whereas $m=2$ would give the wrong sign. At a close encounter, the <probability density> vanishes as $|w|^{2m}$: $m=3$ imposes a sixth-order zero, stronger than the quadratic zero at $m=1$. This is the local geometric mechanism for avoiding a large repulsive <Coulomb potential energy>.

A useful large-$N$ counting outline follows directly from the <polynomial>. Each <complex coordinate> has maximum degree $m(N-1)$. A <lowest Landau level> orbital proportional to $z^k e^{-|z|^2/(4\ell_B^2)}$ has its areal <probability density> maximum at $r^2=2k\ell_B^2$, by differentiating its logarithm. This suggests droplet <area> $A\simeq2\pi mN\ell_B^2$, neglecting the boundary correction. Dividing $N/A$ by the <degeneracy of a Landau level> per <area>, $1/(2\pi\ell_B^2)$, gives <filling factor> $\nu\simeq1/m$. This counting is a motivation for the fraction, not an independent proof that the <wavefunction> minimizes the interacting <Hamiltonian>.

= Two-electron Laughlin relative probability
{parent=Laughlin wavefunction}
{title2=$p_m(u)=\frac{u^{2m+1}e^{-u^2/4}}{2^{2m+1}m!}$}

Consider the original two-<electron> example obtained by taking $N=2$ in the <Laughlin wavefunction>, with no additional <polynomial> for the <centre of mass>. Introduce $Z=(z_1+z_2)/2$, relative <complex coordinate> $w=z_1-z_2$, separation $r=|w|$, and dimensionless separation $u=r/\ell_B$. Since $|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2=2|Z|^2+|w|^2/2$, the <probability density> factorizes:
$$
|\Psi_m|^2\propto e^{-|Z|^2/\ell_B^2}\,r^{2m}e^{-r^2/(4\ell_B^2)}.
$$
Integrating out $Z$ leaves an isotropic relative <probability density>. Define $D_m(u)$ per unit dimensionless <area> and $p_m(u)$ per unit dimensionless separation:
$$
D_m(u)=\frac{u^{2m}e^{-u^2/4}}{\pi4^{m+1}m!},
\qquad p_m(u)=2\pi uD_m(u),
\qquad \int_0^\infty p_m(u)\,du=1.
$$
The physical areal <probability density> is $D_m(r/\ell_B)/\ell_B^2$. The probability of a separation between $r$ and $r+dr$ is $p_m(r/\ell_B)\,dr/\ell_B$. The extra $u$ comes from the <Jacobian determinant> in <polar coordinates>: a thin annulus has <area> $2\pi r\,dr$. Omitting that factor changes the question being answered.

Both <wavefunction normalization> and the peak shift can be checked exactly. The substitution $t=u^2/4$ gives the <Gamma function> integral

For $m>0$, logarithmic differentiation gives

Consequently the areal maximum lies at $u=2\sqrt m$, while the most probable separation lies farther out, at $u=\sqrt{4m+2}$. = Magnetic force on a current-carrying wire
{parent=Lorentz force}

A wire carrying current $I$ in a magnetic field experiences
$$
d\mathbf F=I\,d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf B.
$$
This follows by summing the magnetic part of the <Lorentz force> over the moving charge carriers.

= Electrostatic-frame derivation of parallel-wire forces
{parent=Magnetic force on a current-carrying wire}

Consider lab-neutral parallel wires with stationary positive line density $+\lambda$ and electron line density $-\lambda$. Let wire 1's electrons move at $v_1$ along the wire and wire 2's at $v_2$. The conventional currents are $I_j=-\lambda v_j$. In the rest frame of wire 2's electrons, wire 1's transformed total <linear charge density> is $\lambda_1'=\gamma_2[0-v_2I_1/c^2]=\gamma_2\lambda v_1v_2/c^2$. Its radial <electric field> is $\lambda_1'/(2\pi\epsilon_0d)$.

A wire-2 electron at rest there feels only an electric force. Transforming its transverse force back to the lab divides it by $\gamma_2$, and multiplying by the lab electron count per length $\lambda/e$ gives
$$
f_\perp/\ell=-\frac{\lambda^2v_1v_2}{2\pi\epsilon_0c^2d}=-\frac{\mu_0I_1I_2}{2\pi d},
$$
with positive radial direction away from wire 1. The lab-stationary positive charges feel zero net electric force because wire 1 is neutral in their frame. Equal current directions attract; opposite directions repel. Transforming force on each carrier and then counting carriers in the specified frame avoids an incorrect extra length-contraction factor in force per unit length.

= Relativistic cyclotron orbit
{title2=$\omega_\tau=qB/m$}
{parent=Lorentz force}

In a constant <magnetic field> $B\widehat z$ with no <electric field>, a charged relativistic particle moves in a circle in the perpendicular plane. Its angular frequency with respect to <proper time> is
$$
\omega_\tau=\frac{qB}{m},
$$
whereas its angular frequency in an inertial coordinate time is $\omega_t=qB/(\gamma m)$. The orbit radius at speed $v$ is
$$
R=\frac{\gamma mv}{|q|B}.
$$

= Charged-particle action over one relativistic cyclotron orbit
{parent=Relativistic cyclotron orbit}

For $qB>0$, choose $A_y=Bx$ and parametrize one clockwise orbit by $x=R\cos(\omega_\tau\tau)$ and $y=-R\sin(\omega_\tau\tau)$. Over $\Delta\tau=2\pi/\omega_\tau$, the <relativistic charged-particle action> is
$$
S=-mc^2\Delta\tau+q\oint A\mathbin\cdot dx
=-\frac{\pi mc^2}{\omega_\tau}(1+\gamma^2).
$$
The same formula with $|\omega_\tau|$ applies for either orbit orientation.

= Lorentz force density
{title2=$\mathbf f_{\rm L}$}
{parent=Lorentz force}

The Lorentz force per unit volume is $\mathbf f_{\rm L}=\rho_e\mathbf E+\mathbf j\times\mathbf B/c$ in Gaussian units.

= E-cross-B drift
{title2=$\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/B^2$}
{c}
{parent=Lorentz force}
{wiki}

In uniform perpendicular electric and magnetic fields, a charged particle's cyclotron orbit has guiding-centre velocity
$$
\mathbf v_D=\frac{\mathbf E\times\mathbf B}{B^2},
$$
independent of its charge and mass.

= Relativistic electromagnetic work-energy theorem
{parent=Lorentz force}

For $\mathcal E=\gamma mc^2$, the temporal component of the covariant Lorentz-force equation gives
$$
\frac{d\mathcal E}{dt}=q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v.
$$
The magnetic field does no work because $\mathbf v\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)=0$.

= Coordinate acceleration of a relativistic charged particle
{parent=Lorentz force}

Combining $d(\gamma m\mathbf v)/dt=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)$ with $mc^2\,d\gamma/dt=q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v$ gives
$$
\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt}
=\frac q{m\gamma}
\left[
\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B
-\frac{\mathbf v(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf E)}{c^2}
\right].
$$

= Electromagnetic field invariants
{title2=$F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu},\,F_{\mu\nu}\widetilde F^{\mu\nu}$}
{parent=Electromagnetic field tensor}
{wiki}

For signature $(-,+,+,+)$ and a consistent orientation,
$$
F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}
=2\left(B^2-\frac{E^2}{c^2}\right),
\qquad
F_{\mu\nu}\widetilde F^{\mu\nu}
=-\frac4c\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B.
$$
Overall signs depend on tensor and orientation conventions, while their vanishing does not.

= Null electromagnetic field
{parent=Electromagnetic field invariants}
{wiki}

A null field has both electromagnetic invariants zero:
$$
\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B=0,
\qquad
E^2=c^2B^2.
$$
For a nonzero constant null field, the mixed tensor is nilpotent of index three,
$$
F^\mu{}_\rho F^\rho{}_\sigma F^\sigma{}_\nu=0.
$$

= Electromagnetic stress-energy tensor
{parent=Electromagnetic field tensor}
{wiki=Electromagnetic_stress–energy_tensor}

For metric signature $(-,+,+,+)$,
$$
T^{\mu\nu}=\frac1{\mu_0}
\left(F^{\mu\rho}F^\nu{}_{\rho}
-\frac14\eta^{\mu\nu}F^{\rho\sigma}F_{\rho\sigma}\right).
$$
In particular, $T^{00}=\tfrac12(\epsilon_0E^2+B^2/\mu_0)$.

= Mixed electromagnetic energy-flux signs
{parent=Electromagnetic stress-energy tensor}
{title2=$T^0{}_i=-S_i\quad(c=1)$}

With $(+---)$ signature and $c=1$, $T^0{}_0=(E^2+B^2)/(2\mu_0)$ while $T^0{}_i=-(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)_i/\mu_0$. Raising the second index gives $T^{0i}=S_i$, where $\mathbf S$ is the <Poynting vector>, and symmetry gives the physical energy-flux components $T^{i0}$. The negative mixed component is a metric-sign effect, not reversed <energy> transport. In SI coordinates $x^0=ct$, $T^{i0}=S_i/c$.

= Electromagnetic stress-energy divergence identity
{parent=Electromagnetic stress-energy tensor}
{title2=$\nabla_bT^{ab}=F^a{}_c\nabla_bF^{cb}/(4\pi)$}

For signature $(+---)$ in Gaussian electromagnetic units, $T^{ab}=(4\pi)^{-1}[-F^a{}_cF^{bc}+g^{ab}F_{cd}F^{cd}/4]$. The <electromagnetic Bianchi identity> gives $F^{bc}\nabla_bF^a{}_c=\nabla^a(F_{cd}F^{cd})/4$, so differentiating the stress tensor proves the displayed identity. Stress conservation implies the source-free <Maxwell equations> if the matrix $F^a{}_c$ is invertible; otherwise it only forces the field divergence into that matrix's kernel. Conversely source-free Maxwell equations always imply stress conservation.

= Gauge-compensated time translation in electromagnetism
{parent=Electromagnetic stress-energy tensor}
{title2=$\delta A_\nu=\alpha F_{0\nu}$}

With signature $(+,-,-,-)$ and $\mathcal L=-F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}/4$, combine a time translation $\alpha\partial_0A_\nu$ with the <gauge transformation> of parameter $-\alpha A_0$. Its <Noether current> is $J^\mu=-F^{\mu\nu}F_{0\nu}-\delta^\mu_0\mathcal L$, the gauge-invariant energy current. The density is $(|\mathbf E|^2+|\mathbf B|^2)/2$. On the source-free field equations it differs from the canonical translation current by $\partial_\nu(A_0F^{\mu\nu})$, an antisymmetric superpotential divergence, so total energies agree when the boundary term vanishes.

= Null energy condition for the electromagnetic field
{parent=Electromagnetic stress-energy tensor}

For every null vector $k$, put $q^\rho=k_\mu F^{\mu\rho}$. Antisymmetry gives $q\cdot k=0$, so $q$ has nonnegative norm and
$$
T^{\mu\nu}k_\mu k_\nu=\frac1{\mu_0}q^\rho q_\rho\geq0.
$$

= Lorentz transformation of electromagnetic fields
{parent=Relativistic electromagnetism}
{c}
{wiki}

Lorentz boosts mix transverse electric and magnetic field components.

= Longitudinal solenoid field under an axial boost
{parent=Lorentz transformation of electromagnetic fields}

An ideal long <solenoid> has $B=\mu_0nI$, with axial turn <density> n. Under an axial boost, turn <density> becomes $n'=\gamma n$. The transverse circulation path is unchanged while a turn's charge-circulation rate, measured relative to its moving material, becomes $I'=I/\gamma$. Thus $n'I'=nI$ and the axial <magnetic field> remains unchanged. Equivalently, the ideal transverse current-sheet <density> is invariant under this boost. End effects are excluded.

= Longitudinal capacitor field under a normal boost
{parent=Lorentz transformation of electromagnetic fields}

For ideal oppositely charged parallel plates, the normal <electric field> is $\sigma/\epsilon_0$. A boost along that normal leaves each transverse plate area unchanged. Invariance of total charge therefore preserves <surface charge density> and the normal <electric field>. The plate separation contracts, but the large-plate field does not depend on that separation. Including longitudinal contraction in a <voltage> argument while holding <voltage> fixed would impose an unsupported extra assumption.

= Relativistic charge density of counterstreaming beams
{parent=Lorentz transformation of electromagnetic fields}
{title2=$\lambda'=-\gamma vI/c^2$}

Two equal opposite line charge densities moving in opposite directions have zero net line charge but a nonzero total current $I$. Under a longitudinal <Lorentz boost>, the charge-current four-vector gives $\lambda'=\gamma(\lambda-vI/c^2)=-\gamma vI/c^2$. Equivalently, for lab densities $n$ and speeds $\pm u$, their separate boosted number densities are $\gamma n(1\mp vu/c^2)$. Their imbalance creates the boosted radial <electric field>, consistently with <Lorentz transformation of electromagnetic fields> and <Gauss's law>.

= Electromagnetic jump conditions across a normally moving plane
{parent=Lorentz transformation of electromagnetic fields}

If a plane is stationary in one frame and an observer moves normally to it with velocity $\mathbf v$, then
$$
[B'_n]=0,
\qquad
[\mathbf E'_t]=\mathbf v\mathbin\times[\mathbf B'_t].
$$
The second relation accounts for the magnetic-field discontinuity moving through the observer's frame.
