= Solution
{parent=a}

For $K=\mathbb Q(\zeta)$, every <Galois automorphism> sends the primitive root $\zeta$ to another primitive $m$th <root of unity>, uniquely $\zeta^a$ for $a\in(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z)^\times$. The <cyclotomic character of a finite cyclotomic extension> is
$$
\boxed{\chi_m:\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb Q)\longrightarrow(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z)^\times,\qquad\sigma(\zeta)=\zeta^{\chi_m(\sigma)}}.
$$
If $\chi_m(\sigma)=a$ and $\chi_m(\tau)=b$, then $(\sigma\tau)(\zeta)=\sigma(\zeta^b)=\zeta^{ab}$, proving the <group homomorphism> property. Its <kernel> is trivial: an automorphism fixing $\zeta$ fixes the field it generates. Thus the character is <injective>. Here its domain is the finite cyclotomic <Galois group>; the corresponding map from an <absolute Galois group> need not be injective.

The element $\zeta$ satisfies $T^2-\eta T+1=0$, so $[\mathbb Q(\zeta):\mathbb Q(\eta)]\leq2$. Since $\eta=\zeta+\overline\zeta$ is real, $\mathbb Q(\eta)\subseteq\mathbb R$. A primitive root of order $m\geq3$ is nonreal: the only real <roots of unity> are $\pm1$, of orders at most two. Hence $\zeta\notin\mathbb Q(\eta)$ and
$$
\boxed{[\mathbb Q(\zeta):\mathbb Q(\eta)]=2}.
$$
Equivalently, nontrivial <complex conjugation> fixes precisely the <maximal real subfield of a cyclotomic field> in this degree-two extension.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Irreducibility of the <cyclotomic polynomial> gives $[K:\mathbb Q]=\varphi(15)=8$. The extension is <Galois>, since $K$ is the <splitting field> of $T^{15}-1$ in characteristic zero. The injection in part (a) is consequently an isomorphism, and the <Chinese remainder theorem> gives
$$
\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb Q)\cong(\mathbb Z/15\mathbb Z)^\times\cong C_2\times C_4.
$$
The <fundamental theorem of Galois theory> identifies proper intermediate fields strictly above $\mathbb Q$ with proper nontrivial <subgroups>. Such subgroups have order two or four. The two-torsion subgroup is $C_2\times C_2$, with three nonidentity elements and hence three order-two subgroups. There are also three order-four subgroups: they are the kernels of the three nonzero <group homomorphisms> $C_2\times C_4\to C_2$. Distinct such maps have distinct kernels because the target has only one nonidentity element. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{three quadratic subfields and three quartic subfields, six in total}.}
$$

= ii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Put $\omega=\zeta^5$, $\tau=\zeta^3$, $a=1+2\omega$, and $b=1+2(\tau+\tau^{-1})$. The primitive cubic and quintic root identities give $a^2=-3$, $b^2=5$. These elements therefore provide the following six generators:
* $\mathbb Q(a)=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})$, degree two.
* $\mathbb Q(b)=\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)$, degree two.
* $\mathbb Q(ab)=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-15})$, degree two.
* $\mathbb Q(\tau)$, degree four.
* $\mathbb Q(a+b)=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3},\sqrt5)$, degree four.
* $\mathbb Q(\zeta+\zeta^{-1})$, degree four.

The three quadratic fields are distinct because $-3,5,-15$ are distinct rational square classes. To justify the fifth entry, the two quadratic fields $\mathbb Q(a)$ and $\mathbb Q(b)$ are distinct, so their <compositum> has degree four. Moreover $a+b\ne0$ and
$$
(a+b)(b-a)=8,\qquad a=\frac{a+b-8/(a+b)}2,\qquad b=\frac{a+b+8/(a+b)}2.
$$
Thus their sum alone generates the compositum.

The field $\mathbb Q(\tau)$ has degree four by the quintic <cyclotomic polynomial>. Its <Galois group> is $C_4$ and its unique quadratic subfield is $\mathbb Q(b)$, so it cannot contain $a$. It is therefore different from $\mathbb Q(a,b)$. The final field has degree four by part (b) and is real; the preceding two quartic fields contain nonreal $\tau$ and $a$, respectively. Thus all three quartics are distinct, and the count in part (i) proves the list exhaustive. This gives the <subfields of the fifteenth cyclotomic field> without relying on a choice of square-root signs.

The <Galois group> acts <transitively> on the $n$ roots because $f$ is <irreducible> and <separable>. The <orbit-stabilizer theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{|G|=n\,|\operatorname{Stab}_G(\alpha_1)|},
$$
so $n$ divides $|G|$. Equivalently, the <tower law> gives $[K:F]=[K:F(\alpha_1)]n$, while the finite <Galois extension> has group order $[K:F]$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For the root-discriminant convention in the requested identity, define
$$
\Delta_f=\prod_{i<j}(\alpha_i-\alpha_j)^2.
$$
The <Vandermonde determinant> is $\det V(\alpha)=\prod_{i<j}(\alpha_j-\alpha_i)$. Squaring removes the signs, proving
$$
\boxed{\Delta_f=(\det V(\alpha))^2}.
$$
It is nonzero because the roots are distinct. It lies in $F$, since its root expression is fixed by every <Galois automorphism> and $K^G=F$.

If $f$ is not monic and has leading coefficient $c$, the usual <polynomial discriminant> is $c^{2n-2}\prod_{i<j}(\alpha_i-\alpha_j)^2$. Thus the printed Vandermonde equality uses the discriminant of the monic normalization $f/c$. The extra factor is itself a nonzero square in $F$, so it does not alter the square-class criterion in the next part.

= Solution
{parent=c}

First assume $\operatorname{char}F\ne2$. Set $d=\det V(\alpha)\ne0$. If $\Delta_f=b^2$ with $b\in F$, then $(d-b)(d+b)=0$ in the <field> $K$, so $d=\pm b\in F$. For an automorphism inducing the root permutation $\pi$,
$$
\sigma(d)=\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)d.
$$
Since $\sigma$ fixes $d$ and $1\ne-1$ in $F$, every induced permutation has positive sign. The root action is faithful because the roots generate $K$, giving
$$
\boxed{G\hookrightarrow A_n\qquad(\operatorname{char}F\ne2)}.
$$
This is the <discriminant criterion for an alternating Galois group>.

**As printed for arbitrary fields, the assertion is false in characteristic two.** Over $\mathbb F_2$, the polynomial $T^2+T+1$ is irreducible and separable, with roots $\alpha,\alpha+1$ in $\mathbb F_4$. Their difference is one, so the discriminant is the square $1$. Nevertheless its <Galois group> is $C_2$, whereas $A_2$ is trivial. In characteristic two the permutation signs become indistinguishable as field elements; <square discriminants in characteristic two> therefore cannot detect the alternating subgroup in this way.

Denote the three stated pair sums by $r_1,r_2,r_3$. They are pairwise distinct: for example,
$$
r_1-r_2=(\alpha_1-\alpha_4)(\alpha_2-\alpha_3)\ne0,
$$
and the other differences factor similarly. Every <Galois automorphism> fixes each $r_i\in F$, and hence fixes each of the three corresponding partitions of four roots into two pairs. The kernel of the permutation action $S_4\to S_3$ on these partitions is
$$
V_4=\{1,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}.
$$
Thus $G$ embeds in the <Klein four-group>. By part (a), $4$ divides $|G|$, and the containing group has order four. Consequently
$$
\boxed{G\cong V_4}.
$$
This <split resolvent criterion for a Klein four Galois group> uses the <cubic resolvent of a quartic>. The pair-sum differences remain nonzero in characteristic two, so this proof does not need the missing characteristic assumption from part (c).

= Solution

A nonconstant <polynomial> is <separable> when it has distinct roots in a <splitting field>, equivalently when it is coprime to its <formal derivative>. For such $f$, <Bézout's identity for polynomials> gives $a,b\in F[T]$ with $af+bf'=1$. This identity remains valid over any extension $E/F$. A repeated root $\gamma\in E$ would satisfy $f(\gamma)=f'(\gamma)=0$, giving the contradiction $1=0$. Thus there are no repeated roots in any field extension. The argument in fact applies to every separable polynomial, whether irreducible or not.

= b
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For nonconstant irreducible $f$, its <greatest common divisor> with $f'$ is either $1$ or $f$. Since $\deg f'<\deg f$ when $f'\ne0$, inseparability is equivalent to $f'=0$. In characteristic $p$,
$$
f'(T)=\sum_jj a_jT^{j-1}=0
$$
exactly when $a_j=0$ for every exponent $j$ not divisible by $p$. Thus
$$
\boxed{f\text{ separable}\iff f'\ne0\iff f\notin F[T^p]}.
$$
No assumption that the coefficient field is perfect is required: vanishing derivative concerns the exponents, not whether the coefficients have $p$th roots in $F$.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Let $E=F(\alpha^p)\subseteq F(\alpha)$. If $\alpha$ is a <separable algebraic element> over $F$, its minimal polynomial over $E$ divides the original separable <minimal polynomial>. It also divides $T^p-\alpha^p=(T-\alpha)^p$, which has only one distinct root in any extension. Being separable with only one distinct root, that minimal polynomial has degree one. Hence $\alpha\in E$ and $F(\alpha)=F(\alpha^p)$.

Conversely, if the degree-$n$ minimal polynomial $m$ over $F$ is inseparable, part (b) writes it as $m(T)=g(T^p)$ with $\deg g=n/p$. Then $g(\alpha^p)=0$, so
$$
[F(\alpha^p):F]\leq n/p<n=[F(\alpha):F].
$$
The fields cannot be equal. This proves the equivalence
$$
\boxed{\alpha\text{ separable over }F\iff F(\alpha)=F(\alpha^p)},
$$
the <separability criterion using a pth power>.

= Solution

Put $\gamma=\alpha+\beta$, $E=F(\gamma)$ and $E_0=F(\gamma^p)$. By the preceding <separability criterion using a pth power>, it suffices to prove $E=E_0$.

Since $\alpha$ is separable, $\alpha\in F(\alpha^p)$. But $\alpha^p=\gamma^p-\beta^p\in E_0(\beta)$, so $\alpha\in E_0(\beta)$. Consequently
$$
M:=F(\alpha,\beta)=E(\beta)=E_0(\beta).
$$
Let $h$ and $m$ be the monic <minimal polynomials> of $\beta$ over $E_0$ and $E$, respectively. Both divide its separable minimal polynomial over $F$, so both are separable. Also $m\mid h$ in $E[T]$.

Every $c\in E=F(\gamma)$ has $c^p\in F(\gamma^p)=E_0$. Hence $m(T)^p$ has coefficients in $E_0$ and vanishes at $\beta$, forcing $h\mid m^p$ in $E_0[T]$. Since $h$ remains a <square-free polynomial> over $E$, this divisibility implies $h\mid m$ in $E[T]$. Together with $m\mid h$, monicity gives $h=m$. Therefore
$$
[M:E_0]=\deg h=\deg m=[M:E].
$$
The <tower law> makes $[E:E_0]=1$. Part (c) now proves that $\gamma=\alpha+\beta$ is separable over $F$. This establishes <closure of separable algebraic elements under addition> without presupposing that closure as a field theorem.
