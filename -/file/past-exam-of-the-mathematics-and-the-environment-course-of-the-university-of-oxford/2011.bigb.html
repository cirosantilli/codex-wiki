= Solution
{parent=1}

**Balances and meanings.** The first equation is <conservation of energy> per unit area. Here $c$ is effective areal <heat capacity>, $Q$ is the <solar constant>, and $Q/4$ accounts for the ratio of the Earth's intercepting disc area to its total surface area. The <albedo> $a$ reflects a fraction of that input. The outgoing <thermal radiation> is the <Stefan–Boltzmann law> modified by $\gamma(p)$, an effective transmission factor for the <greenhouse effect>; it normally decreases as atmospheric <carbon dioxide> increases.

The <albedo> relaxes towards its <temperature>-dependent equilibrium on the <ice>-adjustment time $t_i$. The atmospheric mass inventory per unit <pressure> is $M_{\mathrm{CO}_2}A/(M_ag)$, using the <molar mass> ratio of <carbon dioxide> to air and hydrostatic atmospheric mass. Its sources and sinks are volcanic input $v$, removal by <chemical weathering> over land area $A_L$ at rate $W$, and the atmosphere-to-ocean exchange $h(p-p_s)$. The ocean equation is a molar <mass conservation> balance: $\rho_{\mathrm{H_2O}}V_{\mathrm{oc}}$ is ocean <water> mass, division by $M_{\mathrm{CO}_2}$ converts the incoming carbon-dioxide mass to moles, and $bC$ removes dissolved carbon by burial/export. With $C$ in moles per kilogram, $b$ has units of kilograms of <water>-equivalent per year.

**Times and reduction.** Linearize the <temperature> equation at fixed $a,p$ about <radiative equilibrium>. Its <exponential relaxation time> is
$$
\boxed{t_T=\frac{c}{4\sigma\gamma(p)T^3}=\frac{cT}{Q(1-a)}=3.00\times10^6\ \mathrm{s}=0.0952\ \mathrm y.}
$$
The equality uses $\sigma\gamma T^4=Q(1-a)/4$. A stock-over-flux thermal time, sometimes used as a scale, is four times this, $0.381\ \mathrm y$; both describe a rapid thermal response relative to the other processes.

For <pressure> in atmospheres, write $I=M_{\mathrm{CO}_2}A\,10^5/(M_ag)$ for the atmospheric carbon-dioxide mass per atmosphere. The factor $10^5$ is essential because $h$ is given per atmosphere. The <pressure> and slow ocean <exponential relaxation times> are
$$
\boxed{t_p=\frac{I}{h}=108.8\ \mathrm y,\qquad t_C=\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{H_2O}}V_{\mathrm{oc}}}{b}=1.667\times10^5\ \mathrm y.}
$$
Thus $t_T\ll t_p\ll t_i=10^4\ \mathrm y<t_C$. The leading <quasi-steady approximation> gives
$$
T=\Theta(a,p)=\left[\frac{Q(1-a)}{4\sigma\gamma(p)}\right]^{1/4},\qquad h(p-p_s)=v-A_LW.
$$
Substitution in the two remaining equations yields
$$
\boxed{t_i\dot a=B(a,p)-a,\quad B(a,p)=a_{\mathrm{eq}}(\Theta(a,p)),\qquad t_C\dot C=C_0-C,\quad C_0=\frac{v}{M_{\mathrm{CO}_2}b}.}
$$
**The coefficient of $\dot a$ remains $t_i$; the printed $t_T$ in the reduced equation is a typographical error.** Eliminating a fast <temperature> variable does not replace the <ice>-response time. The ocean time here is the slow time after atmospheric balance cancels the exchange flux; holding <pressure> fixed after imposing a carbon-dependent $p_s$ would describe a different conditional relaxation.

The supplied volcanic input gives
$$
\boxed{C_0=8.21\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}},}
$$
about $41.1\%$ of the stated present value $2.0\times10^{-3}$; that value is about $2.43C_0$.

**Carbon-dioxide levels.** Since $A_LW=3.0\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{kg\,y^{-1}}=v$, the atmospheric balance with $p_s=C/K$ gives
$$
\boxed{p_0=\frac{2.0\times10^{-3}}{7.1}=2.817\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{atm}\approx282\ \mathrm{ppm}.}
$$
This uses the specified present ocean <concentration>, as requested, rather than replacing it by the model's smaller volcanic-only equilibrium.

After sustained additional input $v_i$, $C$ barely changes during the atmospheric adjustment. At this stage
$$
p(t)\simeq\frac{C(0)}K+\frac{v_i}{h}(1-e^{-t/t_p}),\qquad\boxed{p_{\mathrm{fast}}\simeq4.187\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{atm}\approx419\ \mathrm{ppm}.}
$$
This is an intermediate balance, approached over several centuries. On the slower ocean time,
$$
C(t)\simeq C_\infty+[C(0)-C_\infty]e^{-t/t_C},\qquad C_\infty=\frac{v+v_i}{M_{\mathrm{CO}_2}b}=2.820\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}},
$$
with <pressure> following $p\simeq C/K+v_i/h$. Since $C(0)<C_\infty$, <pressure> continues to rise slowly. Its eventual level in this stipulated constant-parameter model is
$$
\boxed{p_\infty=\frac{C_\infty}{K}+\frac{v_i}{h}=4.109\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{atm}\approx4109\ \mathrm{ppm}.}
$$
The <two-reservoir carbon adjustment> therefore has a rapid rise towards the frozen-ocean balance followed by a much longer increase. The figure solves the coupled linear <pressure>–ocean equations, retaining their small inventory correction to the reduced <time scales>.

**Sliding and cavities.** <Regelation> is melting under increased <pressure> and refreezing when the <pressure> is released. For <ice> near its melting point, the <Clapeyron equation> gives a lower melting <temperature> at greater <pressure>. Against an obstacle, <ice> melts on the high-<pressure> upstream face, the <water> passes around it, and it refreezes on the lower-<pressure> downstream face. Transport of the released <latent heat> back towards the melting face sustains the process. Together with viscous deformation around obstacles, this permits <basal sliding>; <regelation> is especially effective for small obstacles, for which heat conduction distances are short.

<Subglacial cavitation> occurs where the contact <pressure> predicted behind an obstacle falls below the <fluid pressure>[water pressure]: the <ice> separates from the bed and a <water>-filled cavity opens. The <effective pressure in subglacial drainage> is
$$
\boxed{N=p_i-p_w,}
$$
the <ice> overburden <pressure> minus <fluid pressure>[water pressure]. Smaller $N$ facilitates larger cavities and reduces the resisting <ice>–bed contact. The exponent $n$ comes from <Glen's flow law>, with <strain rate> proportional to the $n$th power of <deviatoric stress>, commonly $n\simeq3$. Its homogeneity makes $N^n$ the natural <velocity> scale, yielding $\tau/N=f(u/N^n)$ after including the fixed bed and <ice>-rheology scales.

Without cavities, <material creep> gives a drag law with $f(x)\propto x^{1/n}$. Cavities lower the resistance relative to that extrapolation. On a bounded-slope bed the drag is bounded in proportion to $N$; depending on bed geometry, the curve may level off or pass through a maximum and weaken as contact diminishes. The illustrated weakening curve is a qualitative possibility, not a universal formula for all beds.

**Channels versus distributed drainage.** In <Röthlisberger channel drainage>, frictional dissipation of flowing <water> supplies heat to melt the channel walls, while <material creep> of <ice> closes them under effective <pressure>. An area balance has the schematic form
$$
\dot S_{\mathrm{ch}}=\frac{Q\Psi}{\rho_i L_f}-\mathcal A N^nS_{\mathrm{ch}},
$$
where $\Psi$ is hydraulic-potential drop per length, $L_f$ is <latent heat> per mass, and the positive constant $\mathcal A$ incorporates <ice> <material creep> and geometry. <Water>-flow resistance relates the flux to channel size and potential gradient. Balancing melting against closure produces the stated increasing relation $N_R(Q)$.

A channel gaining flux can maintain lower <fluid pressure>[water pressure], since larger $N$ means smaller $p_w$. It draws still more <water> from neighboring routes, melts wider, and causes competing small conduits to lose flux and close. This <positive feedback> concentrates flow into an arterial network.

A <linked cavity drainage system> consists of <water>-filled lee-side cavities connected by narrow passages. Sliding over bed obstacles opens cavities; <ice> <material creep> tends to close them. The given decreasing relation $N_K(Q)$ means that carrying more <water> requires higher <fluid pressure>[water pressure]. A route gaining flux then has a higher <pressure> and diverts <water> towards neighboring routes. This negative redistribution feedback supports many connected routes and a distributed system.

**Coexistence instability.** At a coexistence point let $N_R(Q_R)=N_K(Q_K)$ and hold $Q=Q_R+Q_K$ fixed. A loss $\Delta Q_R=-\varepsilon$ from the channel gives $\Delta Q_K=\varepsilon$. The changes in the two equilibrium effective pressures are
$$
\Delta N_R=-N_R'(Q_R)\varepsilon,\qquad\Delta N_K=N_K'(Q_K)\varepsilon=-|N_K'(Q_K)|\varepsilon.
$$
Consequently
$$
N_R(Q_R-\varepsilon)-N_K(Q_K+\varepsilon)\simeq-\bigl[N_R'(Q_R)-|N_K'(Q_K)|\bigr]\varepsilon.
$$
If the bracket is positive, the cavity system's common effective <pressure> exceeds what the reduced-flux channel can support against <material creep> closure. The channel shrinks, loses further flux, and closes down. Thus the <channel-cavity drainage instability> condition is
$$
\boxed{N_R'(Q_R)>|N_K'(Q_K)|.}
$$

= Solution

These are the one-dimensional <shallow water equations> with bed slope and <friction>. The coordinate $s$ measures distance downstream, $t$ is time, $A(s,t)$ is the wetted cross-sectional area, $u(s,t)$ is section-averaged downstream <velocity>, and $h(s,t)$ is <water> <water depth> above the bed. Thus $Au$ is <water> volume flux and the first equation expresses <mass conservation>.

In the <momentum> equation, $u_t+uu_s$ is the material acceleration, $g$ is gravitational acceleration, and $S$ is the positive downward bed slope, so $gS$ drives the flow. The wetted perimeter is $l$ and $f$ is a dimensionless <friction> coefficient in the stated convention. The frictional deceleration is $flu^2/A$ for downstream flow, while $-gh_s$ is the <hydrostatic pressure> contribution from surface-height variation relative to the sloping bed. The formula $u^2$ assumes the chosen positive-flow direction; a bidirectional <friction> model uses $u|u|$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let $B$ be the constant canal width, large compared with <water depth>. Then $A\simeq Bh$ and $l\simeq B$, so <friction> is $fu^2/h$. Choose a reference uniform <water depth> $H$, normal-flow speed $U$ satisfying $fU^2/H=gS$, and scales
$$
\ell=H/S,\qquad t_0=\ell/U,\qquad h=H\widehat h,\quad u=U\widehat u,\quad s=\ell\widehat s,\quad t=t_0\widehat t.
$$
The <continuity equation> becomes $\widehat h_{\widehat t}+(\widehat h\widehat u)_{\widehat s}=0$. Dividing <momentum> by $gS$ and dropping hats gives
$$
\boxed{h_t+(hu)_s=0,\qquad F^2(u_t+uu_s)=1-\frac{u^2}{h}-h_s,\qquad F=\frac{U}{\sqrt{gH}}=\sqrt{\frac Sf}.}
$$
The <Froude number> compares <velocity> to long gravity-<wave speed>. At the reference state, $F<1$ is <subcritical flow>, $F=1$ critical flow, and $F>1$ <supercritical flow>. In dimensional variables the <wave> characteristics travel at $u\pm\sqrt{gh}$: one can propagate upstream in subcritical flow, while both move downstream in supercritical flow. Away from the reference state the local <Froude number> is $F|u|/\sqrt h$.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For the <travelling wave> $\xi=s+ct$, continuity gives $(ch+hu)'=0$. The upstream state fixes the constant as $1+c$, hence
$$
\boxed{u=-c+\frac{1+c}{h},\qquad u'=-\frac{1+c}{h^2}h'.}
$$
Substituting in <momentum> yields
$$
-\frac{F^2(1+c)^2}{h^3}h'=1-\frac{(1+c-ch)^2}{h^3}-h',
$$
so
$$
\boxed{h'=\frac{h^3-(1+c-ch)^2}{h^3-(1+c)^2F^2}.}
$$
A constant downstream state $H=h_+>1$ requires $H^3=(1+c-cH)^2$. The positive square-root choice would give $c<0$, so the upstream-moving <wave> uses $1+c-cH=-H^{3/2}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{c=\frac{H^{3/2}+1}{H-1},\qquad u(H)=-\sqrt H.}
$$
The downstream flow reverses. Where the numerator is positive in $1<h<H$, a smooth increasing profile requires a positive denominator. Its minimum occurs at $h=1$, giving
$$
1-(1+c)^2F^2>0,\qquad\boxed{F<\frac1{1+c}=\frac{H-1}{H+H^{3/2}}.}
$$
Equality does not give the prescribed asymptotic upstream state: cancellation of the two simple zeros at $h=1$ leaves a nonzero slope there. For the strict inequality, separation of variables constructs the monotone profile whenever the numerator remains positive between its endpoint zeros.

**There is an additional restriction hidden in the printed positivity hint.** Put $r=\sqrt h$ and $R=\sqrt H$. Since $c=R+1/(R-1)$, the potentially vanishing factor of the numerator is
$$
h^{3/2}+1+c-ch=(r+1)(r-R)\left(r-\frac{R}{R-1}\right).
$$
The other factor, $h^{3/2}-(1+c-ch)$, is positive for $h>1$. Therefore the asserted positivity throughout $1<h<H$ holds precisely for $1<R\leq2$, that is $1<H\leq4$. For $H>4$, the zero at $h=[R/(R-1)]^2$ intervenes before $H$, preventing the requested monotone connection. For example $H=9$, $c=3.5$, $h=4$ gives $h^3-(1+c-ch)^2=-26.25$. Thus the <smooth upstream tidal-wave admissibility> conditions are
$$
\boxed{1<h_+\leq4,\qquad F<\frac{h_+-1}{h_++h_+^{3/2}}.}
$$
At $h_+=4$ the downstream zero is double, giving an algebraic approach; below four the endpoint approaches are exponential. The displayed <Froude number> bound is the requested necessary condition, with the source's missing amplitude qualification made explicit.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Write $e=E'(1)>0$, $q_1=q'(1)>0$ and introduce perturbations $(H,U,C,S)e^{\lambda t+ikx}$ to <water depth>, <velocity>, <concentration> and bed height. Take the leading <quasi-steady approximation> $\epsilon=0$ for the fast <water> and suspension response, retaining the small <friction> parameter $\delta$. For $k\ne0$, the linearized equations are
$$
\begin{aligned}
&H+U=0,\\
&[ik(F^2-1)+3\delta]U=-ikS,\\
&(\gamma+ik)C=\gamma eU,\\
&\lambda S+ikq_1U=\beta(C-eU).
\end{aligned}
$$
Here surface height is $H+S$, and <linearization> of $1-u^2/h$ gives $H-2U=-3U$. Eliminating $H,C,U$ gives the <dispersion relation>
$$
\boxed{\lambda(k)=-\frac{k^2\left(q_1+\dfrac{\beta e}{\gamma+ik}\right)}{3\delta+ik(F^2-1)}.}
$$
In particular, putting $B=F^2-1\ne0$ and expanding at large $|k|$,
$$
\boxed{\lambda(k)=i\frac{q_1}{B}k+\frac{\beta e}{B}-\frac{3\delta q_1}{B^2}+O(k^{-1}).}
$$
Thus the temporal growth rate tends to a finite constant, while the <phase velocity> is
$$
\boxed{v_{\mathrm{ph}}=-\frac{\operatorname{Im}\lambda}{k}\longrightarrow-\frac{q_1}{F^2-1}.}
$$
For <supercritical flow> $F>1$, the waves propagate upstream. They grow if $\beta e(F^2-1)>3\delta q_1$; at leading order in small $\delta$, this holds for fixed positive $\beta,e$ away from critical flow. They are <antidunes>, upstream-migrating bedforms coupled to the free surface. Indeed $H=-U$ and $S\simeq-(F^2-1)U$, so surface height $H+S\simeq-F^2U$ is in phase with the bed perturbation in this regime.

For clarity, neglecting $\delta$ completely gives the exact leading-order expressions
$$
\operatorname{Re}\lambda=\frac{\beta e}{F^2-1}\frac{k^2}{\gamma^2+k^2},\qquad v_{\mathrm{ph}}=-\frac1{F^2-1}\left(q_1+\frac{\beta e\gamma}{\gamma^2+k^2}\right).
$$
These also identify the missing qualification in the source: **the unstable upstream branch requires $F>1$.** For $F<1$ this leading model gives decay and downstream propagation. The near-critical case $F^2-1\simeq0$ needs a different balance; it is not covered by the expansion. Small nonzero $\epsilon$ perturbs the slow bedform branch and adds fast <water> modes, so the stated result concerns the leading slow morphodynamic branch. This is the <high-wavenumber antidune dispersion> calculation.
