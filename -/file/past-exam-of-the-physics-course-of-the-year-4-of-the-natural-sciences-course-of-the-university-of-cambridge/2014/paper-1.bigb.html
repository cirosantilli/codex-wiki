The solar <flux> at <Earth> is $\sigma T_\odot^4R_\odot^2/L^2$. A spherical <Earth> intercepts sunlight over its cross section but radiates over its whole surface, giving
$$
\pi R_E^2\sigma T_\odot^4\frac{R_\odot^2}{L^2}=4\pi R_E^2\sigma T_E^4,
\qquad
\boxed{T_E=T_\odot\sqrt{\frac{R_\odot}{2L}}=275\,\mathrm K.}
$$
This is **below the observed mean surface <temperature>**. Atmospheric infrared absorption and emission produce a <greenhouse effect> that raises the surface <temperature> relative to the effective radiating <temperature>. Including reflected sunlight alone would lower the estimate further; <albedo> does not explain a warmer surface.<Climate and Earth's Energy Budget by NASA Earth Observatory>[Sections The Natural Greenhouse Effect and Effect on Surface Temperature]{ref}

Let $a$ be the cube's side and $z$ a small upward displacement. Its displaced water <volume> decreases by $a^2z$, so the change in <buoyancy> is $-\rho_wga^2z$. With cube <mass> $m=0.9\rho_wa^3$,
$$
m\ddot z=-\rho_wga^2z,\qquad
\boxed{\omega^2=\frac{g}{0.9a},\qquad T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.9a}{g}}=0.426\,\mathrm s.}
$$
The displacement must be small enough that the upper face remains above water and the bottom remains submerged. This <buoyancy> estimate neglects fluid <added mass>, <damping> and melting.

For the <photopion threshold on a photon background>, write $M=m_pc^2$ and $m=m_\pi c^2$ in <energy> units. For a head-on <photon> of <energy> $\epsilon$ and a highly relativistic <proton> of total <energy> $E$, the invariant squared centre-of-momentum <energy> is approximately $M^2+4E\epsilon$. At threshold the final <proton> and <pion> are at rest relative to one another, so
$$
M^2+4E_{\rm th}\epsilon=(M+m)^2,
\qquad
\boxed{E_{\rm th}=\frac{m(2M+m)}{4\epsilon}.}
$$
Using a typical CMB <photon energy> $\epsilon\simeq2.70k_BT=6.28\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{eV}$ gives
$$
\boxed{E_{\rm th}\simeq1.1\times10^{20}\,\mathrm{eV}.}
$$
Thus photopion losses suppress the arrival of <protons> of order $10^{20}\,\mathrm{eV}$ from sufficiently distant sources. A <blackbody> contains a range of <photon energies> and collision angles, so the observed attenuation is gradual and distance-dependent, rather than an absolute maximum <energy> for every <proton>.

$$
\sigma_S^2=(\cos2\theta\,\sigma_{S_0})^2+
(2S_0\sin2\theta\,\sigma_\theta)^2,
\qquad
\boxed{S=106.1\pm6.5.}
$$

Our prediction differs from them by $0.19$ and $1.07$ combined <standard deviations> respectively. **All three values are statistically compatible.** If the input errors are strict bounds instead, the first-order bound on the calculated value is about $\pm9.0$; consistency remains possible, but a Gaussian significance test then has no stated justification.

<Zero-point energy> is the <ground-state energy> above the classical minimum, remaining even at zero <temperature>. For a <harmonic oscillator> it is $\hbar\omega/2$; simultaneous vanishing position and <momentum> fluctuations is forbidden by the uncertainty principle.

A concrete test is the Casimir interaction: changing conducting boundaries changes electromagnetic ground-state <modes> and produces a measurable <force>. Ideal parallel plates have pressure
$$
\boxed{P_C=-\frac{\pi^2\hbar c}{240a^4}.}
$$
A torsion-pendulum experiment measured the attraction of closely spaced conducting surfaces, testing this boundary-dependent interaction.<Demonstration of the Casimir Force in the 0.6 to 6 micrometre Range by S. K. Lamoreaux>[Physical Review Letters 78, 5 (1997), primary abstract: electromechanical torsion-pendulum measurement; later erratum noted in bibliography]{ref} Such measurements test **changes in <ground-state energy>**, not an arbitrary absolute additive vacuum <energy>; real-material and thermal corrections must be included when comparing precision data.

For <nonequivalent p-s and p-p term counting>, the closed $3s^2$ shell has $L=S=0$. For $3p4s$, the open orbitals give $L=1$ and $S=0,1$, hence
$$
\boxed{3p4s:\quad{}^1P_1,\quad{}^3P_{0,1,2}.}
$$
<Hund's rules> predict the order $^3P_0<{}^3P_1<{}^3P_2<{}^1P_1$.

For $3p4p$, the electrons are in **nonequivalent orbitals**. All $L=0,1,2$ and $S=0,1$ combinations are allowed:
$$
\boxed{3p4p:\quad{}^1S_0,\ {}^1P_1,\ {}^1D_2,\
{}^3S_1,\ {}^3P_{0,1,2},\ {}^3D_{1,2,3}.}
$$
Applying maximum <spin> first, maximum <orbital angular momentum> next, and smallest $J$ first for these less-than-half-filled open subshells gives the qualitative ordering
$$
\boxed{{}^3D_1<{}^3D_2<{}^3D_3<{}^3P_0<{}^3P_1<{}^3P_2
<{}^3S_1<{}^1D_2<{}^1P_1<{}^1S_0.}
$$
The state counts are $12$ for $3p4s$ and $36$ for $3p4p$. Imposing the equivalent-$p^2$ restrictions would incorrectly discard allowed terms. These are Hund-rule predictions; exact interleavings in a real excited configuration require the actual Coulomb and spin-orbit matrix elements.

For an isolated system with equally probable accessible microstates, <Boltzmann entropy> is $S=k_B\ln\Omega$. The second law states that an isolated system's total <entropy> cannot decrease; it remains constant in a reversible process and increases in an irreversible one.

The insulated mixture exchanges no heat with its surroundings. At constant pressure, with only pressure-volume work, its <enthalpy> is conserved. Constant and equal <specific heat capacities> therefore give $T_f=(T_1+T_2)/2$. Calculate each <entropy> change along a hypothetical reversible <temperature> path:
$$
\Delta S=Mc_P\ln\frac{T_f}{T_1}+Mc_P\ln\frac{T_f}{T_2}
=\boxed{2Mc_P\ln\left[\frac{T_1+T_2}{2\sqrt{T_1T_2}}\right].}
$$
The surroundings gain no <entropy> through heat exchange; ideal mechanical work carries no <entropy>. The <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality> gives **$\Delta S\ge0$**, with equality only for $T_1=T_2$. Mixing unequal <temperatures> is irreversible even though total <energy> is conserved.

Perturbation theory expands known <eigenvalues> and <eigenfunctions> about a solvable Hamiltonian. Small corrections require appropriate energy-gap and matrix-element scales; degeneracies must first be diagonalized within their subspace. The <variational method> instead minimizes a normalized trial state's <energy> expectation. It gives an upper bound on the ground <energy> without requiring a small perturbation; excited-state bounds need orthogonality constraints or an appropriate symmetry subspace.

Normalize the unperturbed oscillator states:
$$
|\psi_0|^2=\frac\alpha{\sqrt\pi}e^{-\alpha^2x^2},\qquad
|\psi_1|^2=\frac{2\alpha^3}{\sqrt\pi}x^2e^{-\alpha^2x^2}.
$$
For the <Gaussian perturbation of a quantum harmonic oscillator>, the first-order shifts are its expectation values:
$$
\boxed{\Delta E_0=\frac{V_0\alpha}{\sqrt{\alpha^2+\beta^2}},\qquad
\Delta E_1=\frac{V_0\alpha^3}{(\alpha^2+\beta^2)^{3/2}}.}
$$
For $\beta\gg\alpha$, they scale as $V_0\alpha/\beta$ and $V_0(\alpha/\beta)^3$ respectively: a narrow central perturbation couples weakly to the odd state's central <node>. For $\beta\ll\alpha$, both approach $V_0$ because the perturbation is nearly constant over the wavefunctions. The leading corrections are $V_0[1-\beta^2/(2\alpha^2)]$ and $V_0[1-3\beta^2/(2\alpha^2)]$.

For an improved variational estimate, replace the fixed width $\alpha$ by an adjustable positive width $s$ in each even/odd Gaussian. Their <energy> functionals are
$$
E_{\rm even}(s)=\frac{\hbar^2s^2}{4m}+\frac{m\omega^2}{4s^2}
+\frac{V_0s}{\sqrt{s^2+\beta^2}},
$$
$$
E_{\rm odd}(s)=\frac{3\hbar^2s^2}{4m}+\frac{3m\omega^2}{4s^2}
+\frac{V_0s^3}{(s^2+\beta^2)^{3/2}}.
$$
Minimize each separately. At $s=\alpha$ they recover the unperturbed <energy> plus the first-order shift, so optimization cannot raise those trial estimates. For this even one-dimensional potential the lowest odd state is the first <excited state>, making its odd-sector minimum an upper bound on $E_1$. Enlarging each parity-preserving trial basis can improve the estimates further.

For noninteracting particles, a many-particle configuration has $E=\sum_jn_j\epsilon_j$ and $N=\sum_jn_j$. Summing independently over allowed occupations therefore factorizes the grand <partition function>:
$$
\Theta=\prod_j\Theta_j,\qquad
\boxed{\Phi=-k_BT\sum_j\ln\Theta_j.}
$$
At fixed $T,V$, differentiating the original state sum gives $\partial_\mu\ln\Theta=\beta\langle N\rangle$ and $\partial_\mu^2\ln\Theta=\beta^2\operatorname{Var}N$, so
$$
\boxed{\langle N\rangle=-\partial_\mu\Phi,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}N=-k_BT\partial_\mu^2\Phi
=k_BT\partial_\mu\langle N\rangle.}
$$
For one level, write $q=e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}$. <Fermions> allow $n=0,1$, giving $\Theta_F=1+q$ and $\bar n=q/(1+q)$. <Bosons> allow all nonnegative integers, giving $\Theta_B=1/(1-q)$ and $\bar n=q/(1-q)$, with $q<1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\bar n_F=\frac1{e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}+1},\qquad
\bar n_B=\frac1{e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}-1}.}
$$
The <grand-canonical single-level occupation variance> follows from the same derivative identity applied to one level:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}n=k_BT\partial_\mu\bar n
=\begin{cases}\bar n(1-\bar n),&\text{fermions},\\
\bar n(1+\bar n),&\text{bosons}.\end{cases}}
$$
In the high-temperature nondegenerate regime, $\bar n\ll1$ and both approach classical Poisson-like fluctuations, $\operatorname{Var}n\simeq\bar n$. At low <temperature>, nearly filled or empty <fermion> levels have strongly suppressed fluctuations; only the Fermi-surface window changes occupation. Highly occupied low-energy <boson> levels can instead have $\operatorname{Var}n\sim\bar n^2$, showing bosonic enhancement. This last statement depends on occupation and reservoir <chemical potential>, not <temperature> alone; grand-canonical condensate fluctuations must not be confused with a fixed-total-number ensemble.

= Solution
{parent=c}

For <proper-time acceleration by reflected laser light>, the spacecraft receives <momentum> $(hf+hf_r)/c=2hf/[c(1+\beta)]$ per incident <photon>. Combining with the impact rate gives
$$
F=\frac{2Rhf}{c}\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta},\qquad
\frac{d(\gamma\beta)}{dt}=\frac1{\tau_0}\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta},\qquad
\tau_0=\frac{Mc^2}{2Rhf}.
$$
Because $dt/d\tau=\gamma$ and $d(\gamma\beta)/d\beta=\gamma^3$,
$$
\gamma^2\frac{d\beta}{d\tau}=\frac1{\tau_0}\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{d\beta}{d\tau}=\frac{(1-\beta)^2}{\tau_0}.
$$
Integrating from rest gives $1/(1-\beta)-1=\tau/\tau_0$, hence
$$
\boxed{\beta(\tau)=\frac{\tau}{\tau+\tau_0}.}
$$
The mirror's <rest mass> is assumed constant and the laser illuminates it continuously; <diffraction> losses, incomplete interception and microscopic recoil are neglected.

The <method of images> replaces conductors by fictitious charges outside the physical solution domain. Choose images so the real-plus-image potential obeys the specified conductor boundary values. The electrostatic uniqueness theorem then identifies that potential in the physical domain; fields inside the replaced conductor are not physical predictions.

Put the cylinder axis at the origin, real line at $(2a,0)$ and image at $(a/2,0)$. Let $r_1,r_2$ be distances from these lines. The exterior potential can be written
$$
\boxed{\phi=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\ln\frac{2r_2}{r_1}.}
$$
On the surface, $r_1^2=a^2(5-4\cos\theta)$ and $r_2^2=r_1^2/4$, so $\phi=0$ everywhere as required. The additive constant is essential for a grounded cylinder; the two-dimensional potential need not also be zero at infinity.

The physical line feels the image field at separation $3a/2$. Its <force> per unit length is attractive:
$$
\boxed{\frac{\mathbf F}{\mathcal L}=-\frac{\lambda^2}{3\pi\epsilon_0a}\,\hat{\mathbf x}.}
$$
Measure $\theta$ from the radius toward the real line, as in the original diagram. The radial field just outside the surface is
$$
E_r=\frac\lambda{2\pi\epsilon_0}
\left[\frac{a-2a\cos\theta}{a^2(5-4\cos\theta)}
-\frac{a-(a/2)\cos\theta}{(a^2/4)(5-4\cos\theta)}\right].
$$
Since the interior field is zero,
$$
\boxed{\sigma(\theta)=\epsilon_0E_r=-\frac{3\lambda}{2\pi a(5-4\cos\theta)}.}
$$
Integrating $a\sigma(\theta)$ around the circumference gives total induced charge $-\lambda$ per unit length.

A <normal mode> is a motion in which all generalized coordinates oscillate at one <frequency> with fixed <amplitude> ratios. Small <oscillations> about <stable equilibrium> in a time-independent conservative system give $\mathsf M\ddot{\boldsymbol\theta}+\mathsf K\boldsymbol\theta=0$, with positive <mass matrix> and symmetric <stiffness matrix>, allowing a generalized normal-mode eigenproblem.

For the <three-to-one mass double-pendulum modes>, the angles are absolute displacements from vertical, as printed. To quadratic order,
$$
K=\frac{m\ell^2}{2}(4\dot\theta_1^2+2\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2+\dot\theta_2^2),\qquad
U=\frac{mg\ell}{2}(4\theta_1^2+\theta_2^2).
$$
For $\boldsymbol\theta=\mathbf A\cos\omega t$ and $q=\omega^2\ell/g$, the determinant is
$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}4-4q&-q\\-q&1-q\end{pmatrix}
=3q^2-8q+4=(3q-2)(q-2).
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\omega_-^2=\frac{2g}{3\ell},\quad(\theta_1,\theta_2)\propto(1,2);
\qquad\omega_+^2=\frac{2g}{\ell},\quad(\theta_1,\theta_2)\propto(1,-2).}
$$
The low <mode> has the strings leaning together, with the lower angle twice as large; the high <mode> has opposite angular signs. Both reverse together after half their respective periods.

For the specified release, decompose $(\alpha,\alpha)=(3\alpha/4)(1,2)+(\alpha/4)(1,-2)$. Therefore
$$
\theta_1=\frac\alpha4(3\cos\omega_-t+\cos\omega_+t),\qquad
\theta_2=\frac\alpha2(3\cos\omega_-t-\cos\omega_+t),
$$
and the lower <mass>'s horizontal displacement is
$$
\boxed{x_m\simeq\ell(\theta_1+\theta_2)
=\frac{\alpha\ell}{4}(9\cos\omega_-t-\cos\omega_+t),\qquad
\sup|x_m|=\frac52|\alpha|\ell.}
$$
This is the <supremum of two incommensurate harmonic modes>, conventionally described as the maximum-amplitude estimate. Strictly, $\omega_+/\omega_-=\sqrt3$ is irrational, so the opposite cosine extrema are approached arbitrarily closely but never coincide at a finite time. The quoted value is a <supremum> of the ideal linearized motion.

<Fresnel diffraction> retains quadratic aperture-dependent propagation <phases> and generally depends on both aperture and screen distance. <Fraunhofer diffraction> neglects those <phases> and gives the <Fourier transform> of the aperture in angle space, either sufficiently far away or in a suitable lens's focal plane.

The calculations below use the **Fraunhofer, <paraxial approximation>**. For an overall aperture scale $D\sim1000\lambda$, free propagation requires $L\gg D^2/\lambda\sim10^6\lambda$. The printed $L\gg1000\lambda$ alone only makes the separation small relative to distance; it is not by itself the full far-field condition.

A slit of width $w$ has its first zeros at $\sin\theta=\pm\lambda/w$. Thus the full central-envelope widths are
$$
\boxed{W_1\simeq\frac{2L\lambda}{w_1}=\frac L{10},\qquad

Treat the transparent plate as a <phase plate> at normal incidence. Its extra optical path is $(n-1)50\lambda$. An unchanged <interference> pattern requires an integer number of full <phase> cycles:
$$
50(n-1)=m\in\mathbb Z,\qquad n=1+\frac m{50}.

Here $\mathbf H$ is <magnetic field intensity>, $\mathbf J$ free <electric current> <density>, and $\mathbf D$ electric displacement. In vacuum, $\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf H$ and $\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E$. Integrating the equation gives <Ampère's circuital law> with <displacement current>; in a <steady state> the displacement term vanishes.

Take $z$ upward and define $V=\phi_{\rm upper}-\phi_{\rm lower}=V_0\sin\omega t$. Neglect fringing and use the low-frequency thin-capacitor approximation. Then
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E=E_z(t)\hat{\mathbf z},\qquad
E_z=-\frac{V_0}{L}\sin\omega t,\qquad E_r=E_\phi=0.}
$$
Inside the gap, a circular Ampère loop encloses only <displacement current>:
$$
2\pi rH_\phi=\epsilon_0\pi r^2\dot E_z,
\qquad
\boxed{\mathbf H=\frac{\epsilon_0r}{2}\dot E_z\,\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}
=-\frac{\epsilon_0r\omega V_0}{2L}\cos\omega t\,\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}.}
$$
Because $\hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}=-\hat{\mathbf r}$, the <Poynting vector> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf S=-\frac{\epsilon_0r}{2}E_z\dot E_z\,\hat{\mathbf r}.}
$$
It points inward while the electrical <energy> is increasing and outward while it decreases. The net <power> entering the curved side is
$$
-2\pi aL S_r(a)=\epsilon_0\pi a^2L E_z\dot E_z
=\frac d{dt}\left(\frac{\epsilon_0\pi a^2L}{2}E_z^2\right)
=\dot U_E.
$$
This is the leading quasistatic <energy> balance. The time-averaged field energies are
$$
\overline U_E=\frac{\epsilon_0\pi a^2V_0^2}{4L},\qquad
\overline U_B=\frac{\mu_0\epsilon_0^2\omega^2\pi a^4V_0^2}{32L},
\qquad
\boxed{\frac{\overline U_B}{\overline U_E}=\frac18\left(\frac{\omega a}{c}\right)^2.}
$$
For the <axisymmetric high-frequency capacitor field> when $\omega a/c$ is not small, induction and radial propagation prevent a spatially uniform $E_z$. In an ideal axially uniform axisymmetric interior model, Faraday's and Ampère's equations give
$$
\frac1r\frac d{dr}\left(r\frac{dE_z}{dr}\right)+\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}E_z=0.
$$
The solution regular at the axis and normalized to the central <voltage> is approximately
$$
\boxed{E_z(r,t)=-\frac{V_0}{L}J_0(\omega r/c)\sin\omega t.}
$$
At $\omega=\pi c/a$, the radial profile is $J_0(\pi r/a)$: its first zero is at $r\simeq0.765a$, and the edge has opposite <phase>, $J_0(\pi)\simeq-0.304$. This is an illustrative thin-disc interior profile; the exact driven high-frequency field also depends on the feed and edge/radiation <boundary conditions>. Continuing the uniform quasistatic field would be inconsistent.
