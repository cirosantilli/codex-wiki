= Quantum optics
{wiki}

<Quantum optics> studies <light> using <quantum states> of the <electromagnetic field> and their interaction with matter. <Photon statistics> asks how detections fluctuate; the <quantum theory of optical coherence> relates those detections to field correlations. A <coherent state>, <thermal light>, and a <Fock state> can have the same mean <photon> number yet different pair counts. This distinction makes <photon counting> useful for testing sources intended to emit one <photon> at a time.

= Field-squared quadrature
{parent=Quantum optics}
{title2=$K_1=(a^{\dagger2}+a^2)/2,\quad K_2=(a^{\dagger2}-a^2)/(2i)$}

Hermitian combinations of pair creation and <annihilation operators>. With $K_3=(N+1/2)/2$, their <commutators> are $[K_1,K_2]=-4iK_3$, $[K_2,K_3]=iK_1$ and $[K_3,K_1]=iK_2$. A <coherent state> has both <variances> $\langle N\rangle+1/2$, saturating the corresponding uncertainty product.

= Amplitude-squared squeezing
{parent=Field-squared quadrature}
{title2=$(\Delta K_j)^2<\langle N\rangle+1/2$}

A <field-squared quadrature> <variance> below the equal-variance coherent-state reference in this normalization. Its conjugate <variance> must remain compatible with the state-dependent <commutator> bound. This is not the same observable as ordinary field-amplitude squeezing or a reduction of mean intensity.

= Nonclassical state of light
{parent=Quantum optics}

A state of light is nonclassical when it is not a <classical mixture of coherent states>. The <Glauber-Sudarshan representation> states this criterion in phase space. Examples include nonvacuum <number states> and <quadrature squeezing> states. A coherent state is on the classical boundary despite its vacuum fluctuations; <thermal light> is a classical mixture despite its non-Poisson fluctuations. None of these <nonclassicality witnesses> is a necessary condition for nonclassicality.

= Nonclassicality witnesses
{parent=Nonclassical state of light}

Sufficient single-mode witnesses include a <Fano factor> $\operatorname{Var}n/\langle n\rangle<1$, an <optical quadrature> variance below $1/4$ for $[X,P]=i/2$, or <photon antibunching> with $g^{(2)}(0)<1$. Classical mixtures obey $\operatorname{Var}n\ge\langle n\rangle$, and $g^{(2)}(0)=\mathbb E(|\alpha|^4)/\mathbb E(|\alpha|^2)^2\ge1$ by nonnegative variance. The tests are sufficient rather than necessary and exclude the vacuum case when dividing by zero mean occupation.

= Photon subtraction
{parent=Quantum optics}
{title2=$|\psi_-\rangle=a|\psi\rangle/\sqrt{\langle N\rangle}$}

Ideal conditional application of an <annihilation operator>, defined only for positive initial mean <photon number>. Number-dependent weighting means that the conditional mean need not fall. Vacuum input gives the zero vector and therefore no successful conditional state.

= Conditional photon-number bias
{parent=Photon subtraction}
{title2=$\bar n_-=\mu-1+\operatorname{Var}N/\mu$}

A subtraction event weights initial occupation $n$ by $n$ before removing one <photon>. Thus its conditional output mean is $\langle N(N-1)\rangle/\mu$, giving the displayed identity for $\mu>0$. If the initial <variance> exceeds its mean, this biased selection can offset the removed <photon>. The unconditional ensemble includes unsuccessful events and has a different <energy> balance.

= Photon addition
{parent=Quantum optics}
{title2=$|\psi_+\rangle=a^\dagger|\psi\rangle/\sqrt{\langle N\rangle+1}$}

Ideal conditional application of a <creation operator>, followed by normalization. It reweights the input number distribution as well as raising each occupied number by one. A heralded physical implementation has a success <probability>; the algebraic <creation operator> alone is not a trace-preserving deterministic <quantum operation>.

= Schrödinger cat state
{c}
{parent=Quantum optics}

= Optical cat state
{synonym}

A coherent <superposition> of distinct optical coherent amplitudes, for example $N(|\alpha\rangle+e^{i\phi}|-\alpha\rangle)$. It differs from the corresponding incoherent mixture through cross terms in observables and its phase-space interference. For large $|\alpha|$, branch distinguishability is large while ordinary <quadrature squeezing> can become exponentially small.

= Loss of coherence in an optical cat state
{parent=Schrödinger cat state}
{title2=$s_{\rm env}=e^{-2(1-\eta)|\alpha|^2}$}

A pure-loss channel of transmissivity $\eta$ maps the two branches to system amplitudes $\pm\sqrt\eta\alpha$ and environment amplitudes $\pm\sqrt{1-\eta}\alpha$. Tracing over the environment multiplies their cross terms by its branch overlap, the factor displayed. Large cat states can therefore lose interference rapidly even while their mean <intensities> change only moderately.

= Phase-dependent cat-state quadrature variance
{parent=Schrödinger cat state}
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}X_2=\frac14-\frac{\alpha^2s(\cos\phi+s)}{(1+s\cos\phi)^2}$}

For real $\alpha$, put $s=e^{-2\alpha^2}$ in $N(|\alpha\rangle+e^{i\phi}|-\alpha\rangle)$. Its mean second quadrature is $-\alpha s\sin\phi/(1+s\cos\phi)$, which is generally nonzero. Subtracting its square from the second moment gives the displayed <variance> and squeezing precisely when $\cos\phi>-s$, for nonzero finite amplitude. A useful counterexample to confusing moment and <variance> is $\phi=\pi/2$.

= Odd coherent-state superposition
{parent=Schrödinger cat state}

= Odd cat state
{synonym}

The difference $N_-(|\alpha\rangle-|-\alpha\rangle)$ contains only odd <photon numbers> and tends to a one-photon state as $\alpha\to0$. Exactly at zero amplitude the unnormalized difference vanishes; the normalized limiting state is obtained only by taking a limit.

= Even coherent-state superposition
{parent=Schrödinger cat state}

= Even cat state
{synonym}

The normalized sum $N_+(|\alpha\rangle+|-\alpha\rangle)$ contains only even <photon numbers>, since the two coherent-state expansions cancel every odd term. It tends to vacuum at small amplitude. With real $\alpha$, its second quadrature can be squeezed below the vacuum <variance>.

= Optical homodyne detection
{parent=Quantum optics}

Interference of a signal with a coherent reference at the same optical carrier frequency to measure phase-sensitive field information. The reference fixes an <optical quadrature>. The inferred noise depends on reference strength, detector efficiency and the detection arrangement.

= Local oscillator
{parent=Optical homodyne detection}

A coherent reference field that selects the measured <optical quadrature> through its relative phase. Mode matching and phase <stability> determine how faithfully the desired signal <optical quadrature> is sampled.

= Optical homodyne tomography
{parent=Optical homodyne detection}

= Balanced homodyne detection
{parent=Optical homodyne detection}

Mix a signal mode $a$ and an independent coherent reference mode $b$ on a balanced <beam splitter>, detect both outputs, and subtract the currents. For $c=(a+ib)/\sqrt2$ and $d=(ia+b)/\sqrt2$, the difference is $I=c^\dagger c-d^\dagger d=i(a^\dagger b-ab^\dagger)$. A reference $\beta=|\beta|e^{i\phi}$ gives mean $2|\beta|\langle X_\vartheta\rangle$ with $\vartheta=\phi+\pi/2$. Other beam-splitter conventions shift this phase calibration. The exact <finite-reference balanced homodyne variance> quantifies the strong-reference approximation.

Matched subtraction cancels the shared total-power term. It therefore suppresses common reference intensity noise, while imbalance and phase fluctuations reduce this advantage. Repeated samples measure a distribution, not just its mean; scanning reference phase supports <optical homodyne tomography>.

= Finite-reference balanced homodyne variance
{parent=Balanced homodyne detection}
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}I=4|\beta|^2\operatorname{Var}X_\vartheta+\langle a^\dagger a\rangle$}

For the convention of <balanced homodyne detection>, assume independent signal and coherent reference states and ideal detectors. Write $b=\beta+\delta b$, with $\delta b$ in vacuum. The current difference splits into $2|\beta|X_\vartheta$ and $i(a^\dagger\delta b-a\delta b^\dagger)$. Cross terms have zero expectation, and the second term has variance $\langle a^\dagger a\rangle$. Consequently the exact variance is $4|\beta|^2\operatorname{Var}X_\vartheta+\langle a^\dagger a\rangle$. Division by $4|\beta|^2$ approaches the signal <optical quadrature> variance when the photon-number correction is negligible.

= Optical quadrature
{parent=Quantum optics}
{title2=$X_\vartheta=(ae^{-i\vartheta}+a^\dagger e^{i\vartheta})/2$}

A phase-selected Hermitian field observable. With <annihilation operator> $a$ and $[a,a^\dagger]=1$, define $X_1=(a+a^\dagger)/2$ and $X_2=(a-a^\dagger)/(2i)$. Then $[X_1,X_2]=i/2$ and the <Fock vacuum> has variance $1/4$ in either quadrature. This dimensionless convention gives $X_\vartheta=X_1\cos\vartheta+X_2\sin\vartheta$. <Balanced homodyne detection> selects $\vartheta$ through the reference <wave phase>.

= Coherent-state electric-field variance
{parent=Optical quadrature}
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}E=E_0^2$}

For one optical mode, $E=E_0(a+a^\dagger)$ and $[a,a^\dagger]=1$. A <coherent state> obeys $a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle$, so <normal ordering> gives $\langle E^2\rangle=E_0^2[(\alpha+\alpha^*)^2+1]$. Subtracting the squared mean leaves $E_0^2$, independent of the coherent displacement. The extra one is the <canonical commutation relation>, not noise from a random coherent amplitude. A rotated <optical quadrature> has the same coherent-state variance with the same amplitude normalization.

= Quadrature noise ellipse
{parent=Optical quadrature}

For a single-mode Gaussian state with no quadrature covariance, the covariance contour has semiaxes $\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}X}$ and $\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}P}$. A <coherent state> has both equal to $1/2$ in the convention $[X,P]=i/2$. A pure <squeezed vacuum state> has semiaxes $e^{-r}/2$ and $e^r/2$; the uncertainty area remains fixed while one noise width decreases. An ellipse of means alone does not specify a general non-Gaussian state.<Quantum Optical Communication lecture 6 by Jeffrey H. Shapiro>[Slides9–12, physical PDF pp.5–6: minimum-uncertainty and squeezed states]{ref}

= Quadrature squeezing
{parent=Optical quadrature}
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}X_\vartheta<1/4$}

With $X_\vartheta=(ae^{-i\vartheta}+a^\dagger e^{i\vartheta})/2$, the coherent-state and vacuum <variances> are $1/4$. A state is squeezed in the chosen quadrature when its <variance> about its own mean is smaller. A second moment about zero is not the <variance> when that mean is nonzero. <balanced homodyne detection> measures a phase-selected quadrature distribution.

= Optical squeezing degradation by loss
{parent=Quadrature squeezing}
{title2=$V_{\rm out}=\eta V_{\rm in}+(1-\eta)/4$}

Mixing a mode with independent vacuum on a <beam splitter> of <intensity> transmission $\eta$ gives the displayed <optical quadrature> <variance> when vacuum <variance> is $1/4$. It approaches vacuum noise as transmission falls. Phase noise instead mixes squeezed and anti-squeezed axes, and cannot in general be represented by the same efficiency alone.

= Quadrature squeezing of a vacuum–one-photon superposition
{parent=Optical quadrature}
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}X_1=\tfrac14-\tfrac12\epsilon^2+\epsilon^4$}

For real $\epsilon$ with $|\epsilon|\leq1$, let $|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{1-\epsilon^2}|0\rangle+\epsilon|1\rangle$. The <Fock state> matrix elements give $\langle a\rangle=\epsilon\sqrt{1-\epsilon^2}$, $\langle a^2\rangle=0$ and $\langle a^\dagger a\rangle=\epsilon^2$. Therefore
$$
\operatorname{Var}X_1=\frac14-\frac12\epsilon^2+\epsilon^4,\qquad \operatorname{Var}X_2=\frac14+\frac12\epsilon^2.
$$
Only the first of these <optical quadratures> is squeezed for this real superposition, with minimum variance $3/16$ at $\epsilon^2=1/4$. The second is never below vacuum noise. This non-Gaussian superposition is distinct from an <optical squeezed vacuum>.

= Quantum theory of optical coherence
{parent=Quantum optics}

Fix a detected position and <polarization of waves>. Write the <electric field> operator as $\widehat E(t)=\widehat E^{(+)}(t)+\widehat E^{(-)}(t)$, where the positive-frequency part contains <annihilation operators> and $\widehat E^{(-)}=(\widehat E^{(+)})^\dagger$. An ideal absorbing <photodetector> responds to
$$
G^{(1)}(t,t')=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\rho\widehat E^{(-)}(t)\widehat E^{(+)}(t')\right],
$$
with <density matrix> $\rho$. The diagonal gives the single-count rate up to the detector coupling. Multiple absorptions require higher, <normal-ordered> field products. <Roy J. Glauber> used these correlations to develop the quantum account of <optical coherence>.<Coherent and Incoherent States of the Radiation Field by Roy Jay Glauber>[1963, §II, equations (2.1)–(2.4), pp. 2766–2767; Universidade Estadual de Campinas mirror]{ref}

The ordering follows from a detection amplitude. If absorption takes an initial <pure state> $|i\rangle$ to an unobserved final state $|f\rangle$, its field factor is $\langle f|\widehat E^{(+)}|i\rangle$. Squaring its <complex modulus> and summing over a complete <orthonormal basis> of final states gives $\langle i|\widehat E^{(-)}\widehat E^{(+)}|i\rangle$. Averaging over initial preparations replaces this expression by a <trace>. Thus a detector that absorbs cannot count the <Fock vacuum>, although the vacuum has <quantum fluctuations>.

For one selected <normal mode>, $\widehat E^{(+)}(t)=\mathcal E u(t)a$. All <Fock states> with nonzero occupation, <coherent states>, and <thermal light> in this same mode have
$$
\frac{G^{(1)}(t,t')}{\sqrt{G^{(1)}(t,t)G^{(1)}(t',t')}}
=\frac{u^*(t)u(t')}{|u(t)||u(t')|}
$$
where both denominators are nonzero. Its magnitude is one even when the <expected value> of the field vanishes. Perfect <first-order optical coherence> therefore does not identify a <coherent state>. Pair detection supplies additional information.

= Second-order optical coherence
{parent=Quantum theory of optical coherence}
{title2=$g^{(2)}(\tau)$}

For a source whose counting correlations are stationary, define the dimensionless correlation
$$
g^{(2)}(\tau)=
\frac{\left\langle\widehat E^{(-)}(t)\widehat E^{(-)}(t+\tau)\widehat E^{(+)}(t+\tau)\widehat E^{(+)}(t)\right\rangle}
{\left\langle\widehat E^{(-)}(t)\widehat E^{(+)}(t)\right\rangle
\left\langle\widehat E^{(-)}(t+\tau)\widehat E^{(+)}(t+\tau)\right\rangle}.
$$
Assume finite moments and nonzero denominators. The numerator is in <normal ordering>: two detections remove two <photons>. The denominator is the product of the two single-count factors, so independent counts have reference value one. This is a diagonal restriction of the four-point second-order field correlation, rather than its complete definition.<One Hundred Years of Light Quanta by Roy Jay Glauber>[2005 individual Nobel lecture, pp. 87–89, equations (18)–(22); Liceo Galilei Verona mirror]{ref}

For one selected mode with <number operator> $N=a^\dagger a$ and $\mu=\langle N\rangle>0$, evaluate both detections at the same time. The <canonical commutation relation> $aa^\dagger=a^\dagger a+1$ implies $N^2=(a^\dagger)^2a^2+N$, hence
$$
g^{(2)}(0)=\frac{\langle N(N-1)\rangle}{\mu^2}
=1+\frac{\operatorname{Var}N-\mu}{\mu^2}.
$$
The <factorial moment> counts ordered pairs of distinct <photons>. Using $\langle N^2\rangle$ would include pairing each <photon> with itself and would give the wrong coincidence prediction. At $\mu=0$ this normalized expression is undefined, rather than zero.

= Photon statistics
{parent=Quantum optics}
{title2=$p_n=\Pr(N=n)$}

<Photon statistics> is the <probability distribution> of the <number operator> in specified field modes, or the distribution of registered counts in a specified gate. Those distributions agree only for an appropriate ideal measurement. Mode selection, losses, and <detector dead time> matter. For a one-mode <density matrix>, $p_n=\langle n|\rho|n\rangle$ and
$$
\mu=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}np_n,\qquad
\langle N(N-1)\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n(n-1)p_n.
$$
These two sums already distinguish preparations with the same <expected value>.

= Number squeezing
{parent=Photon statistics}
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}N<\langle N\rangle$}

Sub-Poissonian number <variance> in a specified mode or detection gate. It concerns <photon counting>, not an <optical quadrature>. An <optical squeezed vacuum> has <quadrature squeezing> but fails this number-variance condition.

= Normalization of photon-added and photon-subtracted states
{parent=Photon statistics}

Raw vectors $a^\dagger|\psi\rangle$ and $a|\psi\rangle$ have squared norms $\mu+1$ and $\mu$. Matrix elements of the <number operator> in these vectors must be divided by their squared norms before calling them means. For an <optical squeezed vacuum> with mean $s$, the raw moments are $(3s+1)(s+1)$ and $s(3s+1)$, while both normalized means equal $3s+1$ when subtraction is possible.

= Coherent-state photon statistics
{parent=Photon statistics}
{title2=$p_n=e^{-\mu}\mu^n/n!,\quad g^{(2)}(0)=1$}

For a <coherent state>, write $a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle$ and $|\alpha\rangle=\sum_n c_n|n\rangle$ in <number states>. The <annihilation operator> gives $c_{n+1}\sqrt{n+1}=\alpha c_n$, so $c_n=c_0\alpha^n/\sqrt{n!}$. Normalization yields $|c_0|^2=e^{-|\alpha|^2}$. Thus <photon statistics> follows a <Poisson distribution> with $\mu=|\alpha|^2$, and the <normal ordering> of every moment gives $\langle(a^\dagger)^ka^k\rangle=\mu^k$. This factorization is the defining virtue of <coherent states> for optical calculations.<Coherent and Incoherent States of the Radiation Field by Roy Jay Glauber>[1963, §III, equations (3.1)–(3.7): coherent-state expansion; Universidade Estadual de Campinas mirror]{ref}

As an original comparison, choose $\mu=2$. Then $p_0=e^{-2}$, $p_1=p_2=2e^{-2}$, $\operatorname{Var}N=2$, and $\langle N(N-1)\rangle=4$. A two-<photon> <Fock state> also has mean two but has $\operatorname{Var}N=0$ and pair moment two. Consequently
$$
g^{(2)}_{\rm coherent}(0)=1,\qquad
g^{(2)}_{|n\rangle}(0)=1-\frac1n\quad(n\geq1).
$$
In particular, $|1\rangle$ cannot supply a pair. Nevertheless, <photon statistics> alone does not determine a <quantum state>: averaging $|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|$ over a uniform <quantum phase> removes its off-diagonal entries while retaining exactly the same $p_n$ and these equal-time moments.

= Thermal light
{parent=Photon statistics}

For a single mode in <thermal equilibrium>, its <quantum harmonic oscillator> energy ladder gives a <geometric distribution>
$$
p_n=(1-q)q^n,\qquad q=e^{-\hbar\omega/(k_{\rm B}T)},\qquad
\mu=\frac{q}{1-q},\qquad 0<q<1.
$$
Its <probability generating function> is $F(z)=(1-q)/(1-qz)$. Differentiating twice gives $F''(1)=2q^2/(1-q)^2=2\mu^2$, hence $g^{(2)}(0)=2$. At the chosen mean $\mu=2$, $q=2/3$, $p_0=1/3$, $p_1=2/9$, $p_2=4/27$, and $\operatorname{Var}N=6$. Rare high occupations increase the pair count even though the mean matches the other preparations. The geometric weights also arise from a Gaussian mixture of <coherent states>.<One Hundred Years of Light Quanta by Roy Jay Glauber>[2005 individual Nobel lecture, pp. 91–92, equations (31)–(38)]{ref}

Single-mode selection is essential to this factor of two. If $M$ independent equally populated thermal modes are collected together, let $N=\sum_{j=1}^M N_j$ with $\langle N_j\rangle=\mu/M$. Adding their <variances> gives $\operatorname{Var}N=\mu+\mu^2/M$, hence $g^{(2)}(0)=1+1/M$. Averaging over many unresolved modes can make thermal pair counts approach the independent-count reference.

= Photon bunching
{parent=Photon statistics}

<Photon bunching> is enhanced near-coincident detection relative to an appropriate independent-count reference. It need not require nonclassical <light>. In a classical intensity model with nonnegative instantaneous <light intensity> $I$, ideal detection with a conditional <Poisson distribution> gives
$$
g^{(2)}(0)=\frac{\langle I^2\rangle}{\langle I\rangle^2}
=1+\frac{\operatorname{Var}I}{\langle I\rangle^2}\geq1.
$$
A fluctuating intensity therefore bunches counts. For a stationary classical intensity with finite second moment, the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> further gives $\langle I(t)I(t+\tau)\rangle\leq\langle I^2\rangle$. A zero-delay correlation smaller than one at a later delay cannot be explained by this model.

= Photon antibunching
{parent=Photon statistics}
{wiki}

<Photon antibunching> means suppression of close pairs compared with pairs at some later delay: $g^{(2)}(0)<g^{(2)}(\tau)$ for some $\tau$. If correlations decay to the independent-count reference, $g^{(2)}(0)<1$ is a sufficient signature. It violates the classical intensity bounds in <photon bunching>. For the specified single-mode number measurement, <second-order optical coherence> also shows that $g^{(2)}(0)<1$ is equivalent to $\operatorname{Var}N<\mu$. This variance criterion concerns that mode or gate; it must not be applied unchanged to arbitrarily long integrated counting intervals.

= Photon counting
{parent=Quantum optics}

<Photon counting> registers discrete detector events and their times. A <beam splitter> sends the selected <light> to two <photodetectors>; a <coincidence circuit> or time-recording system compares the outputs. With a vacuum input at the unused <optical port>, splitter fractions $R,T$ and independent <quantum efficiencies> $\eta_1,\eta_2$, a selected input <photon> reaches the two recorded channels with probabilities $p_1=R\eta_1$ and $p_2=T\eta_2$. Conditional on input occupation $N$, each pair of distinct <photons> contributes $p_1p_2$ to the cross-channel pair moment. Therefore
$$
\langle K_1\rangle=p_1\mu,\qquad
\langle K_2\rangle=p_2\mu,\qquad
\langle K_1K_2\rangle=p_1p_2\langle N(N-1)\rangle.
$$
Their normalized ratio recovers $g^{(2)}(0)$. This assumes number-resolving counts, or sufficiently dilute gates for threshold detectors to approximate them. One <photon> cannot create both channel counts in this ideal model.

= Coincidence counting
{parent=Photon counting}

Count detections in two channels within a specified time window to estimate pair correlations. Delayed windows or independent singles rates estimate accidental coincidences. Finite efficiency, timing jitter, dark counts and multiple pairs must be included before interpreting a coincidence fringe as a property of the prepared state.

= Finite-bin photon correlations
{parent=Photon counting}
{title2=$\widehat g^{(2)}_j=C_j/(r_1r_2T_j\Delta\tau)$}

For stationary continuous detection, let $C_j$ count all cross-channel pairs with $t_2-t_1$ in a bin of full width $\Delta\tau$, and let $r_1,r_2$ be observed single-count rates. Let $T_j$ be their joint live observation time at that delay, approximately the common duration $T_{\rm obs}$ when delays are short. Independent streams have expected pair count $r_1r_2T_j\Delta\tau$, giving the displayed estimator. It estimates a bin average of <second-order optical coherence>, further blurred by detector timing response. For a finite uninterrupted run the exact overlap weighting is $T_{\rm obs}-|\tau|$ inside the delay integral, rather than a constant at long delays.

For an original numerical example, choose $r_1=2000\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $r_2=3000\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $T_j=100\,\mathrm s$, and $\Delta\tau=10\,\mathrm{ns}$. The independent reference is six pairs per bin. Chosen counts $C_j=3,6,12$ give estimates $0.5,1,2$; they are teaching inputs with large counting fluctuations, not historical measurements. For pulsed sources, compare properly normalized same-pulse and separate-pulse coincidences instead of assuming this continuous-source baseline.

<Detector dead time> suppresses nearby same-detector counts and can manufacture an apparent dip in a single-detector record. Two independent detector channels avoid that particular same-channel exclusion, but shared electronics can still reject pairs. Timing spread and wide bins wash out narrow features. A device recording only the first stop after each start measures a waiting-time distribution; interpreting it as all-pair correlations requires a sufficiently low stop probability over the relevant delay range.

= Photon correlation loss and background model
{parent=Photon counting}
{title2=$g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}=1+\rho_1\rho_2(g^{(2)}_{\rm sig}-1)$}

Independent loss alone leaves normalized <factorial moments> invariant: if every input <photon> is retained with <probability> $\eta$, <binomial thinning conditional on a total> gives $\langle K\rangle=\eta\mu$ and $\langle K(K-1)\rangle=\eta^2\langle N(N-1)\rangle$. Both powers cancel from $g^{(2)}(0)$. Fewer coincidences still mean poorer statistical precision.

Now let each channel contain a signal rate $s_i$ and an independent <Poisson process> background rate $b_i$, independent also of the signal. Put $\rho_i=s_i/(s_i+b_i)$. The pair density is $s_1s_2g^{(2)}_{\rm sig}+s_1b_2+b_1s_2+b_1b_2$. Dividing by $(s_1+b_1)(s_2+b_2)$ proves the displayed formula, for the same bin and timing response. For chosen equal signal fractions $\rho_1=\rho_2=4/5$, ideal signal values $0,1,2$ become $9/25,1,41/25$. Background pulls both dips and peaks toward one.

This calculation assumes additive, mutually independent background counts. Scattered coherent <light> that interferes with the signal, correlated electronic noise, or recovery effects require a different model. Correcting a dip without independently estimating the backgrounds cannot establish <photon antibunching>.

= Hanbury Brown and Twiss effect
{parent=Quantum optics}
{c}
{wiki}

<Robert Hanbury Brown> and <Richard Q. Twiss> sought <angular sizes> through correlations of detected <light intensities>. Their method multiplies detector outputs after detection, whereas a <Michelson interferometer> combines fields before detection.<Correlation between Photons in two Coherent Beams of Light by Robert Hanbury Brown and Richard Quentin Twiss>[1956, Nature 177, pp. 27–29, accessible opening paragraphs]{ref}

A monochromatic discharge source illuminated a half-silvered <beam splitter> and two <photodetectors>. The averaged current product showed an excess near coincidence. Moving one detector introduced a delay that removed it, supplying a control. Slow response weakened the peak. This <photon bunching> is compatible with fluctuating classical <light intensity>.<One Hundred Years of Light Quanta by Roy Jay Glauber>[2005 individual Nobel lecture, pp. 86–88, figures 3–4]{ref}

= Stellar intensity interferometry
{parent=Hanbury Brown and Twiss effect}

<Stellar intensity interferometry> measures a <star>'s <angular diameter> by comparing fluctuations of detected <light intensity> at separated collectors. Each collector has its own <photodetector>; an <analog multiplier> or a digital correlator compares the electrical outputs. Changing the <interferometric baseline> changes the sampled <spatial optical coherence>. An unresolved <star> produces correlated fluctuations; a resolved extended source supplies different mixtures of emission directions, reducing their correlation.

This makes a useful telescope out of a surprising observable: the fluctuations discarded by an ordinary average-brightness measurement. Optical beams need not travel to a common combiner. A relative <wave phase> perturbation $E_2\mapsto e^{i\phi}E_2$ leaves $|E_2|^2$ unchanged, so pure phase errors do not directly change the local <light intensity>. Electronic delay alignment remains necessary; intensity changes from <atmospheric scintillation>, collection losses, and instrumental noise still require controls.

Let $\mathbf b$ join the collectors and let $\mathbf s_0$ be the unit <line of sight> toward the <star>. The relevant <interferometric baseline> is its projection onto the <plane of the sky>:
$$
\mathbf b_\perp=\mathbf b-(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf s_0)\mathbf s_0,
\qquad B=|\mathbf b_\perp|.
$$
The reference arrival-time difference has magnitude $|\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf s_0|/c$, with <speed of light> $c$. Correct that delay, with a sign fixed by channel order, before averaging the residual correlation at $\tau=0$. The physical collector separation equals $B$ only when it is perpendicular to $\mathbf s_0$; tracking a <star> can change the projection even for fixed collectors.

= Sirius intensity interferometry experiment
{parent=Stellar intensity interferometry}
{c}

In their 1956 <Sirius> report, <Robert Hanbury Brown> and <Richard Q. Twiss> used two 1.56-m searchlight reflectors feeding <photomultipliers>. The <photocurrent> fluctuations passed through 5–45-MHz amplifiers; an adjustable delay aligned arrival times. A linear mixer multiplied the outputs; an integrating motor accumulated their product. A second motor monitored its root-mean-square fluctuation to control gain changes. Separate lamps illuminated capped <photomultipliers> before and after observations to test electronic coupling and drift; blank-sky runs tested correlated <sky background>. They fitted a uniformly bright disk and an unknown zero-baseline scale to relative correlations at different <interferometric baselines>. Broadband response and possible baseline-dependent <systematic errors> limited the diameter inference.<A Test of a New Type of Stellar Interferometer on Sirius by Robert Hanbury Brown and Richard Quentin Twiss>[pp. 1046–1048: reflectors, delay/correlator, lamp and sky controls, relative-baseline disk fit]{ref}

This astronomical test extends the preceding laboratory <Hanbury Brown and Twiss effect> to two independently collected samples of a <star>. The historical motor reading was an instrument-specific correlation statistic. It should not be identified numerically with the normalized $g^{(2)}$ below without the corresponding <measurement calibration>.

= Siegert relation
{parent=Stellar intensity interferometry}
{c}
{title2=$g^{(2)}_{12}(\tau)=1+|g^{(1)}_{12}(\tau)|^2$}

For one selected <polarization of waves>, write the detected complex fields as $E_1(t),E_2(t+\tau)$ and their <light intensities> as $I_j=|E_j|^2$, absorbing a common conversion constant. With stationary <ensemble averages> and nonzero $\bar I_j=\langle I_j\rangle$, define
$$
g^{(1)}_{12}(B,\tau)=\frac{\langle E_1^*(t)E_2(t+\tau)\rangle}{\sqrt{\bar I_1\bar I_2}},
\qquad
g^{(2)}_{12}(B,\tau)=\frac{\langle I_1(t)I_2(t+\tau)\rangle}{\bar I_1\bar I_2}.
$$
Here <second-order optical coherence> concerns distinct detector channels, with the corresponding <normal ordering> in the quantum formulation. Independent detection has $g^{(2)}=1$. Subtracting the means gives the normalized <covariance>
$$
\frac{\langle\delta I_1(t)\delta I_2(t+\tau)\rangle}{\bar I_1\bar I_2}=g^{(2)}_{12}(B,\tau)-1.
$$
Thus multiplying raw <photocurrents> contains a large mean-product contribution; multiplying their fluctuations isolates the small excess. Independent detector <shot noise> does not supply a mean cross-channel excess, although it increases <measurement uncertainty>.

The displayed <Siegert relation> requires a zero-mean, jointly circular complex <Gaussian random field>, the usual chaotic <thermal light> model. Circular means invariance under a common arbitrary complex phase rotation; in particular $\langle E_iE_j\rangle=0$. Apply the <Gaussian moment pairing theorem> to the real and imaginary parts:
$$
\langle E_1^*E_1E_2^*E_2\rangle
=\langle|E_1|^2\rangle\langle|E_2|^2\rangle
+|\langle E_1^*E_2\rangle|^2.
$$
The remaining pairing vanishes by circularity. Division proves the <Siegert relation>. Gaussianity is an assumption about fourth moments, not a consequence of merely measuring <first-order optical coherence>; a <coherent state> or <photon antibunching> cannot be interpreted by this thermal formula.

For equal-intensity beams combined in a common optical mode with matched <wave polarizations>, <interferometric visibility> is $V=|g^{(1)}_{12}|$. Consequently ideal thermal <stellar intensity interferometry> measures $g^{(2)}-1=V^2$, while the full $g^{(2)}$ includes the independent-count reference one. Unpolarized <thermal light> with two independent equally populated <wave polarizations> has half this excess: adding their <covariances> doubles the numerator while multiplying total means quadruples the denominator. Finite detector response further suppresses the peak and must be calibrated.<Spatial intensity interferometry on three bright stars by William Guerin and others>[Guerin and others, Spatial intensity interferometry on three bright stars, arXiv:1805.06653v2 (13 August 2018), §§2.1–2.3: normalized correlation, thermal contrast and temporal-response calibration]{ref}

= Uniform-disk stellar visibility
{parent=Stellar intensity interferometry}
{title2=$\gamma(B)=2J_1(x)/x,\quad x=\pi B\theta/\lambda$}

Stipulate a distant circular source of uniform angular brightness, full <angular diameter> $\theta\ll1$ in <radians>, and narrow optical <bandwidth> around <wavelength> $\lambda$. Distinct source patches emit independently. Assume each collector is small enough not to resolve the disk individually. For a sky offset $\boldsymbol\alpha$ from the disk centre, the baseline-dependent <phase difference> is $2\pi\mathbf b_\perp\cdot\boldsymbol\alpha/\lambda$, up to channel-sign convention. Summing independent patch contributions makes the equal-delay <first-order optical coherence> the normalized <Fourier transform> of angular brightness:
$$
\gamma(B)=\frac{1}{\pi a^2}\int_{|\boldsymbol\alpha|\leq a}
 e^{i2\pi\mathbf b_\perp\cdot\boldsymbol\alpha/\lambda}\,d^2\boldsymbol\alpha,
\qquad a=\frac\theta2.
$$
Choose the horizontal coordinate along $\mathbf b_\perp$, put $k=2\pi B/\lambda$, and integrate along vertical chords of the disk:
$$
\gamma(B)=\frac{2}{\pi a^2}\int_{-a}^{a}\sqrt{a^2-u^2}\,e^{iku}\,du
=\frac{2J_1(ka)}{ka}=\frac{2J_1(x)}x,
\qquad x=\frac{\pi B\theta}{\lambda}.
$$
The imaginary part cancels between opposite chords. Here $J_1$ is the order-one <Bessel function of the first kind>. One way to obtain the last equality is to integrate the exponential's <power series> term by term on the finite disk. Equivalently the result is specified by the convergent series
$$
J_1(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^m(x/2)^{2m+1}}{m!(m+1)!},
\qquad \gamma(B)=1-\frac{x^2}{8}+\frac{x^4}{192}+O(x^6).
$$
At $B=0$ take the continuous limit $\gamma=1$; $V^2=|\gamma|^2$ starts as $1-x^2/4+O(x^4)$. This is why very short <interferometric baselines> give little leverage on <angular diameter>. Larger disks lose <spatial optical coherence> at shorter baselines.

The first positive zero of $J_1$ is $x_1\approx3.831705970$, so
$$
B_0=\frac{x_1\lambda}{\pi\theta}\approx\frac{1.21967\lambda}{\theta},
\qquad \theta\approx\frac{1.21967\lambda}{B_0}.
$$
This is a full diameter convention. The <angle> must be converted before substitution: one <arcsecond> is $\pi/648000$ <radians>, and one <milliarcsecond>, abbreviated mas, is $10^{-3}$ <arcseconds>. For the stipulated $\theta=5\,\mathrm{mas}$ and $\lambda=500\,\mathrm{nm}$, $\theta=2.4240684\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{rad}$ and $B_0\approx25.1575\,\mathrm m$. At $B=10\,\mathrm m$, the model gives $x\approx1.52309$ and $V^2\approx0.542783$.

Beyond $B_0$, $\gamma$ changes sign but $V^2$ stays nonnegative and develops weak further lobes. A single measured squared visibility can therefore match several <angular diameters>; sampling several <interferometric baselines> constrains the fit. <Limb darkening>, spectral averaging, or resolved collector apertures change the uniform-disk prediction. A uniform-disk diameter is a parameter of that brightness model; converting it to a physical <stellar radius> also requires distance and an appropriate brightness profile.

= Stellar angular diameter from a visibility envelope
{parent=Uniform-disk stellar visibility}
{title2=$\alpha=1.21967\lambda/Y_0$}

For a uniform circular stellar disc, the first zero of $J_1$ at $x=3.831706\ldots$ gives $Y_0=1.21967\lambda/\alpha$ when $\alpha$ is full <angular diameter>. In contrast a one-dimensional top-hat angular profile of full width $\alpha$ has a sinc envelope with $Y_0=\lambda/\alpha$. Diameter estimates therefore require a brightness-profile convention. A binary additionally multiplies the envelope by its separation-dependent <interference> factor.

= Finite-response stellar intensity correlations
{parent=Stellar intensity interferometry}
{title2=$g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(B,0)-1=p\rho_1\rho_2\beta|\gamma(B)|^2$}

An exact teaching model makes the peak suppression explicit. Assume the narrowband field correlation separates as $g^{(1)}_{12}(B,u)=\gamma(B)g_t^{(1)}(u)$ after delay compensation. Average each channel's <light intensity> over the same rectangular gate of duration $T$. The two time integrals have triangular relative-delay weight
$$
W_T(u)=\begin{cases}(T-|u|)/T^2,&|u|\leq T,\\0,&|u|>T,\end{cases}
\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}W_T(u)\,du=1.
$$
Integrating the <Siegert relation> against this weight gives
$$
\beta=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}W_T(u)|g_t^{(1)}(u)|^2\,du,
\qquad 0\leq\beta\leq1.
$$
The factor $p$ is one for a selected <polarization of waves> and $1/2$ for two independent equally populated polarizations. The signal fraction $\rho_j=s_j/(s_j+b_j)$ uses mean stellar signal $s_j$ and independent additive background $b_j$ in each detector. The <photon correlation loss and background model> then supplies $\rho_1\rho_2$. This assumes no correlated <sky background> or electronic coupling.

For the original model $|g_t^{(1)}(u)|^2=e^{-|u|/\tau_0}$, elementary <integration> gives
$$
\beta=\frac{2\tau_0}{T}-2\left(\frac{\tau_0}{T}\right)^2(1-e^{-T/\tau_0}).
$$
The area-based <optical coherence time> of this intensity envelope is $\tau_c=\int|g_t^{(1)}(u)|^2du=2\tau_0$. For $T\gg\tau_0$, $\beta\approx\tau_c/T$; for $T\ll\tau_0$, $\beta\to1$. With the chosen $\tau_0=1\,\mathrm{ps}$, $T=50\,\mathrm{ps}$, $p=1$, and $\rho_1=\rho_2=0.8$, $\beta\approx0.0392$ and $g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(0,0)=1.025088$. At $B=10\,\mathrm m$ the stipulated disk gives approximately $1.013617$. A nearly unit measured $g^{(2)}$ can therefore conceal a substantial $V^2$.

This rectangular-gate calculation specifies a counting model. An analog instrument's amplifier <bandwidth> and impulse response instead weight the fluctuations it actually passes; the Sirius amplifiers were bandpass devices. Both require measuring the response and calibrating the zero-baseline excess. When $p$, $\rho_j$, and the temporal response are unchanged, the ratio of excesses recovers $V^2$:
$$
\frac{g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(B,0)-1}{g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(0,0)-1}=|\gamma(B)|^2.
$$
A sufficiently unresolved calibration source can supply the denominator; dividing the full $g^{(2)}$ values would give the wrong result. Narrower optical <bandwidth> lengthens <optical coherence time>, whereas wider usable electronic <bandwidth> can resolve faster fluctuations; these are distinct bandwidths. Background changes, unequal spectral responses, drift, or averaging over changing <interferometric baselines> can invalidate a common calibration. Resolving a disk means establishing a reproducible decline of calibrated correlation, supported by those controls.

= Resonance fluorescence antibunching experiment
{parent=Quantum optics}

In 1977, <H. Jeff Kimble>, <Mario Dagenais>, and <Leonard Mandel> excited a dilute <sodium> atomic beam with a resonant <dye laser>. Apertures restricted the observed atoms; a <microscope objective> collected <fluorescence> perpendicular to both beams. A <beam splitter> sent the collected <light> to two <photomultipliers>. Shaped pulses drove the start and stop inputs of a time-to-digital converter; its delay histogram rose away from zero delay, indicating <photon antibunching>. Turning the atomic beam off measured scattered-laser backgrounds. More than one contributing atom and scattered <light> limited the comparison with an ideal isolated <two-level system>; the paper explicitly treated corrections.<Photon Antibunching in Resonance Fluorescence by Harry Jeffrey Kimble and others>[1977, p. 693, figure 2, equation (6), apparatus and atomic-beam-off/on control; MIT mirror]{ref}

A simple reason for suppressed immediate re-emission is visible in an ideal <two-level system>. After <spontaneous emission>, the atom is in $|g\rangle$. Its emission operator $\sigma_-=|g\rangle\langle e|$ obeys $\sigma_-^2=0$, so two instantaneous emissions are impossible. Excitation must rebuild the excited-state population before another emission. This argument explains the zero-delay idealization; the actual <photon counting> record additionally depends on collection, background, timing, and the number of contributing atoms.
