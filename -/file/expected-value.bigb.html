= Expected value
{title2=$\mathbb E[X]$}
{wiki}

= Expectation
{synonym}

= Mean
{synonym}

= Ensemble average
{synonym}

An <expected value> is the <probability>-weighted <arithmetic mean> of a <real-valued random variable>. On a <probability space> $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb P)$, its general definition is the <Lebesgue integral>
$$
\mathbb E[X]=\int_\Omega X\,d\mathbb P=\int_{\mathbb R}x\,d\mu_X(x),
$$
where $\mu_X$ is the <probability distribution> of $X$. For an <integrable random variable>, this is a finite <real number>. With a <probability mass function> $p_X$, use $\sum_x xp_X(x)$; with a <probability density function> $f_X$, use $\int_{\mathbb R}xf_X(x)\,dx$. These are two ways of computing the same weighted average, rather than different definitions for different experiments.

For example, suppose $X$ takes the values $-1,2,8$ with <probabilities> $1/2,3/10,1/5$. Then
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X]=(-1)\frac12+2\frac3{10}+8\frac15=\frac{17}{10}=1.7.}
$$
The most likely outcome is $-1$, and $1.7$ is not a possible outcome at all. The <expected value> summarizes all outcomes and their weights; it need not predict the result of one trial. Geometrically, placing <masses> equal to the <probability masses> at the outcome positions gives a <center of mass> at $1.7$, since $\sum_xp_X(x)(x-1.7)=0$. This balance-point interpretation also explains the connection to a <weighted arithmetic mean>. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-05-introduction-to-probability-and-statistics-spring-2022/mit18_05_s22_class04-prep-b.pdf[Orloff and Bloom, expected value: probability weights and center of mass]{ref}.

For the dimensionless <random variable> $Y$ with <probability density function> $f_Y(y)=2y$ on $[0,1]$, the <expected value> is $\mathbb E[Y]=\int_0^1y(2y)\,dy=2/3$. Integrating the <probability density function> itself gives one; to compute the <expected value>, multiply by the outcome first. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-05-introduction-to-probability-and-statistics-spring-2022/mit18_05_s22_class06-prep-a.pdf[Orloff and Bloom, expectation for continuous random variables]{ref}.

{description=The left panel's dashed line marks $1.7$, even though the only possible outcomes are $-1,2,8$. In the right panel, the shaded <area> under $y f_Y(y)=2y^2$ is $2/3$; the <area> under the dashed <probability density function> is one. Both examples use dimensionless outcomes.}

Why is this useful? <Linearity of expectation> lets us compute an average total by adding average contributions, even for <dependent random variables>. And for <independent and identically distributed> <integrable random variables>, the <strong law of large numbers> says their <sample mean> approaches their common <expected value> <almost surely>. The theorem supplies the link to repeated observations; the weighted-average definition itself does not promise that any particular finite run will be close to the <expected value>.

There is a useful historical connection to valuing uncertain rewards. In his 1657 *De ratiociniis in ludo aleae*, <Christiaan Huygens> assigns the value $(a+b)/2$ to equally likely rewards $a,b$, and $(pa+qb)/(p+q)$ when they have $p,q$ equally likely chances. These are the finite weighted-average formulas for <expected value>. His opening discussion asks how to divide the stakes when a game stops before completion, connecting <probability> to a concrete question about an unfinished contest. https://math.dartmouth.edu/~doyle/docs/huygens/huygens.pdf[Huygens, De ratiociniis in ludo aleae, 1657; reprint of the 1714 English translation, introduction and Propositions I–III]{ref}.

= Expected value rule
{synonym}

The <law of the unconscious statistician> computes the <expected value> of a <measurable function> of a <random variable> directly from the original <probability distribution>. If $g(X)$ is an <integrable random variable>, then
$$
\mathbb E[g(X)]=\int_{\mathbb R}g(x)\,d\mu_X(x).
$$
Use $\sum_xg(x)p_X(x)$ for a <probability mass function>, or $\int_{\mathbb R}g(x)f_X(x)\,dx$ for a <probability density function>. There is no need to derive the <probability distribution> of $g(X)$ first. The identity is the integration rule for a <pushforward measure>, and also holds with an infinite value when $g$ is nonnegative. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-6-012-introduction-to-probability-spring-2018/pages/part-i-the-fundamentals/[Tsitsiklis, Introduction to Probability, Lecture 5: the expected value rule]{ref}.

For $X$ taking $-1,2,8$ with <probabilities> $1/2,3/10,1/5$, choosing $g(x)=x^2$ gives $\mathbb E[X^2]=\tfrac12+4\tfrac3{10}+64\tfrac15=14.5$. Its <variance> is therefore $14.5-1.7^2=11.61$. Squaring the <expected value> would instead give $2.89$, because squaring does not commute with averaging.

= Outer expectation
{parent=Expected value}
{title2=$\mathbb E^*Z=\inf\{\mathbb EY:Y\geq Z,\ Y\text{ measurable}\}$}

For a bounded possibly nonmeasurable real function $Z$ on a <probability space>, its outer expectation is the infimum of <expected values> of measurable majorants. Its inner expectation is $\mathbb E_*Z=-\mathbb E^*(-Z)$. They agree with the usual <expected value> for measurable $Z$. This permits function-space <convergence in distribution> for an <empirical distribution process> whose paths are not Borel-measurable in a nonseparable norm topology: both expectations of each bounded continuous test functional must approach its ordinary expectation under the tight measurable limiting law.

= Expectation of a product of independent random variables
{parent=Expected value}
{title2=$\mathbb E\prod_iX_i=\prod_i\mathbb E X_i$}

For independent integrable variables, $\mathbb E\prod_iX_i=\prod_i\mathbb E X_i$. <Tonelli theorem> first gives $\mathbb E\prod_i|X_i|=\prod_i\mathbb E|X_i|<\infty$; then <Fubini's theorem> factors the signed joint integral.

= Mean-zero function
{parent=Expected value}
{title2=$\mathbb E f=0$}

A mean-zero function has <expectation> zero with respect to the specified <probability measure>. On a <finite set> with uniform measure this means $\sum_xf(x)=0$. A <balanced subset indicator> is an example. In <Fourier analysis on a finite group>, this is equivalent to vanishing of the component at the <trivial representation>.

= Integrable random variable
{parent=Expected value}
{title2=$\mathbb E|X|<\infty$}

A real or complex <random variable> $X$ is integrable when $\mathbb E|X|<\infty$. This is <integrability> with respect to its <probability measure>, and ensures that its <expected value> is finite. A bounded <random variable> is always an <integrable random variable>; so is the example taking $-1,2,8$, for which $\mathbb E|X|=2.7$.

For a real <random variable>, put $X_+=\max(X,0)$ and $X_-=\max(-X,0)$, its <positive part> and <negative part>. Each has a nonnegative <Lebesgue integral>, possibly infinite. The expression $\mathbb E[X]=\mathbb E[X_+]-\mathbb E[X_-]$ is defined when at least one of these two <expected values> is finite; if both are infinite, the <expected value> is undefined. <Integrability> makes both finite. A nonnegative <random variable> can have <expected value> $+\infty$, even though it is not an <integrable random variable>.

The standard <Cauchy distribution> is a useful warning. With its <probability density function> $f(x)=1/[\pi(1+x^2)]$, the positive contribution is $\int_0^Rxf(x)\,dx=\log(1+R^2)/(2\pi)$, which tends to infinity; the negative contribution has the same magnitude. Integrating symmetrically over $[-R,R]$ gives zero for each $R$, but this cancellation does not define an <expected value>. The <Cauchy sample mean has an unchanged distribution>, showing why the <integrability> hypothesis in the <strong law of large numbers> matters.

For an <integrable random variable>, <conditional expectation> preserves <integrability>, and <linearity of expectation> can safely combine finite averages without forming $\infty-\infty$.

= Integrability threshold for exponential powers of a Gaussian
{parent=Integrable random variable}
{title2=$\mathbb E[Ze^{\alpha Z^{2m}}]$}

Let $Z$ have <standard normal distribution>, let $m\ge1$ be an integer and let $\alpha\in\mathbb R$. A finite <expected value> of $Ze^{\alpha Z^{2m}}$ requires absolute <integrability>, which reduces to finiteness of
$$
\int_0^\infty z\exp(\alpha z^{2m}-z^2/2)\,dz.
$$
For $m=1$ it is finite exactly when $\alpha<1/2$. For every $m\ge2$ it is finite exactly when $\alpha\le0$: the higher power dominates the negative quadratic when $\alpha>0$, and for $\alpha\le0$ the <integrand> is bounded by $ze^{-z^2/2}$. In the finite cases the signed <expected value> is zero by <symmetry>. Otherwise each signed part has infinite <integral>; a formal symmetric cancellation is only a <Cauchy principal value>.

= Moment

The $n$th raw moment of a random variable $X$ is $\mathbb E[X^n]$ when this expectation exists.

The $r$th raw moment of a <random variable> $X$ is $\mathbb E[X^r]$, when it exists. A <central moment> instead uses $\mathbb E[(X-\mathbb E[X])^r]$.

The <fourth moment> is $\mathbb E[X^4]$. Its finiteness is equivalent to square integrability of $X^2$. For centered variables, dividing it by the squared <variance> gives <kurtosis>.

= Indicator random variable
{parent=Expected value}
{title2=$\mathbf1_A$}

An <indicator random variable> is the <indicator function> of an <event> $A$ in a <probability space>: $\mathbf1_A$ is one when $A$ occurs and zero otherwise. It has a <Bernoulli distribution> with parameter $p=\mathbb P(A)$, so $\mathbb E[\mathbf1_A]=p$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf1_A)=p(1-p)$. Even an <event> described without numbers becomes a numerical <random variable> in this way.

For a finite collection of <events>, their <indicator random variables> add up to the number that occur. <Linearity of expectation> therefore turns an average count into a sum of <probabilities>, without requiring <independence>. The <fixed point count of a uniform random permutation> provides an example where the indicators are <dependent random variables>.

= Tail-sum formula
{parent=Expected value}
{title2=$\mathbb E[Y]=\int_0^\infty\mathbb P(Y>t)\,dt$}

The <tail-sum formula> expresses an <expected value> as the <area> under a <survival function>. For every nonnegative <random variable> $Y$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[Y]=\int_0^\infty\mathbb P(Y>t)\,dt.}
$$
This <Lebesgue integral> and the <expected value> may both be $+\infty$; a <probability density function> is unnecessary. For a <random variable> $N$ taking values in the <nonnegative integers>, the corresponding <sum> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[N]=\sum_{k=1}^\infty\mathbb P(N\geq k)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\mathbb P(N>k).}
$$
The <events> $\{N>k\}$ and $\{N\geq k+1\}$ coincide because $N$ takes <integer> values. Thus the strict <sum> starts at zero, while the inclusive <sum> starts at one.<Random: Probability, Mathematical Statistics, Stochastic Processes by Kyle Siegrist>[Expected Value, §2 Additional Properties, Right Distribution Function: nonnegative tail integral and integer-valued tail sums]{ref}

For an original example, let the <probability mass function> of $N$ be
$$
\mathbb P(N=0)=\frac15,\qquad\mathbb P(N=1)=\frac3{10},\qquad\mathbb P(N=3)=\frac12.
$$
Its <expected value> computed directly is $0(1/5)+1(3/10)+3(1/2)=9/5=1.8$. The strict <tail probabilities> at thresholds $0,1,2,3$ are $4/5,1/2,1/2,0$: their <sum> is again $1.8$. There is no <probability mass> at two, yet the <event> $\{N>2\}$ still has <probability> $1/2$. The <survival function> has heights $4/5,1/2,1/2$ on the successive <intervals> $[0,1),[1,2),[2,3)$; each <interval> has length one, so its <integral> is the same <sum>.

\Image[tail-sum-mass-and-survival.png]
{title=The same expected value from probability masses and survival columns}
{description=The exact constructed <probability mass function> assigns $1/5,3/10,1/2$ to $0,1,3$. The $3/10$ contribution appears in one survival column, while the $1/2$ contribution appears in three. Filled endpoints give the strict <survival function> at its jumps; open endpoints show the preceding height. The shaded <area> is $3/10+3(1/2)=1.8$.}

The indexing matters numerically. Starting the inclusive <sum> at zero adds $\mathbb P(N\geq0)=1$, giving $2.8$ for this example. Starting the strict <sum> at one omits $\mathbb P(N>0)=4/5$, giving $1$. For a general nonnegative <random variable>, replacing $>$ with $\geq$ inside the <integral> is harmless: the difference is $\mathbb P(Y=t)$, nonzero at only a <countable set> of thresholds. For each positive <integer> $m$, at most $m$ thresholds can have <probability mass> at least $1/m$; their <countable union> contains all thresholds with positive <probability mass> and has <Lebesgue measure> zero. This reasoning concerns an <integral>, not equality of the two <survival function> conventions at every <point>.

For a continuous original example, take the <uniform distribution> on the <interval> $[0,4]$. Its <survival function> is $S(t)=1-t/4$ for $0\leq t\leq4$ and zero for $t>4$. The <area> is a <triangle> of base four and height one:
$$
\mathbb E[Y]=\int_0^4\left(1-\frac t4\right)dt=2.
$$
Directly integrating against the <probability density function> gives the same <expected value>, $\int_0^4 y(1/4)\,dy=2$. The <integral> of the <probability density function> alone is one; it is the <survival function> whose <area> is the <expected value>.

\Image[tail-sum-uniform-survival-area.png]
{title=A uniform density has total probability one and survival area two}
{description=Exact curves for the <uniform distribution> on $[0,4]$. The <probability density function> has <integral> one, while the <survival function> has <integral> two. Splitting the survival <area> at $t=2$ gives $1.5$ before the cap and $0.5$ beyond it, the <expected values> of $\min(Y,2)$ and the <positive part> $(Y-2)_+$, respectively.}

The proof counts layers of each outcome. For a realized value $y\geq0$, the <indicator function> $\mathbf1_{\{y>t\}}$ equals one over an <interval> of length $y$, so
$$
Y=\int_0^\infty\mathbf1_{\{Y>t\}}\,dt,\qquad
\mathbb E[Y]=\int_0^\infty\mathbb E[\mathbf1_{\{Y>t\}}]dt=\int_0^\infty\mathbb P(Y>t)dt.
$$
<Tonelli theorem> permits the interchange because the <indicator function> is nonnegative; no finite <expected value> is assumed. Similarly, an outcome $N=n$ contributes one <indicator random variable> for each of the $n$ <events> $\{N\geq k\}$ that occur:
$$
N=\sum_{k=1}^\infty\mathbf1_{\{N\geq k\}},\qquad
\mathbb E[N]=\sum_{k=1}^\infty\mathbb E[\mathbf1_{\{N\geq k\}}]=\sum_{k=1}^\infty\mathbb P(N\geq k).
$$
Again <Tonelli theorem> applies to the nonnegative <sum>, or equivalently the <monotone convergence theorem> applies to its <partial sums>. This exchanges counting layers within each outcome with averaging across outcomes. The existing <integer-valued hypothesis in the tail-sum formula> explains what changes when the values are not <integers>; the <tail integral formula for moments> weights the layers to obtain higher <moments>.

For an <integrable random variable> $X$ that can have either sign, apply the nonnegative formula separately to its <positive part> $X_+=\max(X,0)$ and <negative part> $X_-=\max(-X,0)$:
$$
\mathbb E[X]=\int_0^\infty\mathbb P(X>t)dt-\int_0^\infty\mathbb P(X<-t)dt.
$$
<Integrability> makes both <integrals> finite. More generally the difference defines an extended <expected value> when at least one is finite; if both are infinite, subtraction does not define an <expected value>.<Random: Probability, Mathematical Statistics, Stochastic Processes by Kyle Siegrist>[Expected Value, §2 Additional Properties, A General Definition: positive and negative parts and the condition on their expectations]{ref}

<Tail probabilities> are often easier to measure or bound than a whole <probability density function>. For a device's <survival time>, the <survival function> records the <probability> of still operating at each threshold; its <integral> gives the mean operating time. A finite observation window naturally gives the <expected value of a capped nonnegative random variable>. For a mathematical bound, $\mathbb P(Y>t)\leq e^{-t/2}$ for all $t\geq0$ implies $\mathbb E[Y]\leq\int_0^\infty e^{-t/2}dt=2$. The <tail-sum formula> turns control of a whole family of <events> into control of an average.

= Integer-valued hypothesis in the tail-sum formula
{parent=Tail-sum formula}
{title2=$\sum_{m\ge0}\mathbb P(X>m)=\mathbb E\lceil X\rceil\quad(X\geq0)$}

For a nonnegative integer-valued variable, $X=\sum_{m\ge0}\mathbf1_{\{X>m\}}$ and the <tail-sum formula> gives $\mathbb E X=\sum_{m\ge0}\mathbb P(X>m)$. For arbitrary positive real values, that integer-indexed sum equals $\mathbb E\lceil X\rceil$ instead. Two independent constants equal to $3/2$ demonstrate that the product of such tail sums need not equal the tail sum of their product: the two sides are four and three.

= Expected value of a capped nonnegative random variable
{parent=Tail-sum formula}
{title2=$\mathbb E[\min(Y,a)]=\int_0^a\mathbb P(Y>t)dt$}

For a nonnegative <random variable> $Y$ and a fixed cap $a\geq0$, the <tail-sum formula> gives
$$
\mathbb E[\min(Y,a)]=\int_0^a\mathbb P(Y>t)dt,\qquad
\mathbb E[(Y-a)_+]=\int_a^\infty\mathbb P(Y>t)dt.
$$
Below the cap, the <events> $\{\min(Y,a)>t\}$ and $\{Y>t\}$ coincide; at and above the cap, the former <event> is empty. For the second identity, the <positive part> $(Y-a)_+$ exceeds $t\geq0$ precisely when $Y>a+t$. Thus the two <integrals> divide the <area> under the <survival function>, just as $Y=\min(Y,a)+(Y-a)_+$ divides each outcome. The capped <expected value> is always finite, while the excess <expected value> may be infinite.

For the <uniform distribution> on the <interval> $[0,4]$ and cap $a=2$, the capped <expected value> is $\int_0^2(1-t/4)dt=3/2$ and the excess <expected value> is $\int_2^4(1-t/4)dt=1/2$. Directly averaging the capped values also gives $\int_0^2y(1/4)dy+2\mathbb P(Y>2)=1/2+1=3/2$. Capping therefore measures a different average from the uncapped <expected value> two, even when both are finite.

= Linearity of expectation
{parent=Expected value}
{title2=$\mathbb E[aX+bY]=a\mathbb E[X]+b\mathbb E[Y]$}

For <integrable random variables> $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ on the same <probability space> and fixed <real numbers> $a_1,\ldots,a_n$, <linearity of expectation> states
$$
\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^n a_iX_i\right]=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\mathbb E[X_i].
$$
The <random variables> can be <dependent random variables>. The formula follows by the <linearity of integration>; a finite <linear combination> of <integrable random variables> remains <integrable>. For example, if $X$ takes $-1,2,8$ with <probabilities> $1/2,3/10,1/5$, then $X$ and $2X$ are <dependent random variables>, yet $\mathbb E[X+2X]=3\mathbb E[X]=5.1$.

An especially useful application counts how many <events> occur. Put $I_i=\mathbf1_{A_i}$, an <indicator random variable>, and $N=\sum_iI_i$. Since $\mathbb E[I_i]=\mathbb P(A_i)$, we get $\mathbb E[N]=\sum_i\mathbb P(A_i)$. There is no need to compute the whole <probability distribution> of $N$, or to assume <independence>. For a <uniform random permutation> of $n\geq1$ objects, each object stays fixed with <probability> $1/n$, so the <fixed point count of a uniform random permutation> has <expected value> one for every $n$. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-042j-mathematics-for-computer-science-spring-2015/mit6_042js15_textbook.pdf[Lehman, Leighton and Meyer, Mathematics for Computer Science, §18.5: linearity and sums of indicators]{ref}.

<Linearity of expectation> concerns <addition> and multiplication by fixed <scalars>. Products instead satisfy $\mathbb E[XY]=\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y]$ under the hypotheses of <expectation of a product of independent random variables>. For the example above, $\mathbb E[X^2]=14.5$, whereas $(\mathbb E[X])^2=2.89$. Applying a nonlinear <function> before averaging can therefore change the answer substantially; use the <law of the unconscious statistician> to compute that average.

= Expected number of observed categories
{parent=Linearity of expectation}
{title2=$\mathbb E K_n=\sum_{j=1}^m(1-(1-p_j)^n)$}

In $n$ independent trials with category probabilities $p_1,\ldots,p_m$, let $K_n$ count the distinct categories observed. Write it as the <sum> of <indicator random variables> for the categories' appearances. Category $j$ is absent with <probability> $(1-p_j)^n$, so <linearity of expectation> gives the displayed formula. The appearance indicators need not be independent. For equally likely categories the expression becomes $m(1-(1-1/m)^n)$; uneven probabilities can substantially delay observation of rare categories.

= Expectation of an absolutely summable random series
{parent=Linearity of expectation}
{title2=$E[\sum_iX_i]=\sum_iE[X_i]\quad\text{if }\sum_iE|X_i|<\infty$}

If <random variables> $X_i$ satisfy $\sum_iE|X_i|<\infty$, then $\sum_iX_i$ converges absolutely <almost surely>, is integrable, and its <expected value> equals $\sum_iE[X_i]$. Indeed, the <monotone convergence theorem> gives $E[\sum_i|X_i|]=\sum_iE|X_i|<\infty$, so that nonnegative sum is finite <almost surely>. It dominates every finite partial sum in absolute value; the <dominated convergence theorem> then justifies passing the <limit> through the <expected value>. No <independence> is required.

For example, if $|X_i|\le Mq^i$ for a fixed $M$ and $0<q<1$, the <geometric series> bound proves the hypothesis directly. This is useful when repeated observations are multiplied by geometrically shrinking distances or weights.

= Variance of a sample mean
{parent=Expected value}
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}(\overline X)=\sigma^2/n$}

Let $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ be an <IID sample> of real-valued <random variables> with finite <expected value> $\mu$ and finite <variance> $\sigma^2$, where $n\geq1$. Its <sample mean> $\overline X=n^{-1}\sum_iX_i$ satisfies
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[\overline X]=\mu,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(\overline X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}.}
$$
<Linearity of expectation> gives the first identity. The <variance of a sum> and vanishing cross <covariances> give $\operatorname{Var}(\sum_iX_i)=n\sigma^2$; scaling by $1/n$ scales the <variance> by $1/n^2$. <Pairwise independent random variables> with the same <expected value> and <variance> suffice for these identities, even without full <independence>. Grinstead and Snell state the corresponding finite-variance independent-trials result.<Introduction to Probability by Charles M. Grinstead and J. Laurie Snell>[§6.2, Theorems 6.7–6.9, printed pp. 259–260: scaling variance and sums of independent trials]{ref}

The <standard deviation> of the <sample mean> is $\sigma/\sqrt n$. Four times as many observations halve this scale; they do not divide it by four. In the fair <Bernoulli distribution> example, $\sigma=1/2$, so sample sizes $4$ and $64$ give standard deviations $1/4$ and $1/16$, respectively. The <common-coin averaging> example shows why merely repeating observations with a common marginal law does not justify this calculation.

<Chebyshev's inequality> turns the <variance> identity into a probability bound: for $\varepsilon>0$,
$$
\mathbb P(|\overline X-\mu|\geq\varepsilon)\leq\frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}.
$$
This proves <convergence in probability> of the <sample mean> to $\mu$ in this finite-variance setting, a form of the <weak law of large numbers>. It is an upper bound, so values above one convey no improvement over the universal <probability> bound one. <Cauchy sample mean stability> lies outside these hypotheses.

For a nonnegative <random variable>, $Y=\int_0^\infty\mathbf1_{\{Y>t\}}dt$ pointwise, including when $Y=\infty$. <Tonelli's theorem> permits taking <expectation> under the integral and proves the formula in the extended nonnegative reals. = Second moment bound from a clipped Gaussian tail
{parent=Tail integral formula for moments}
{title2=$\mathbb E Z^2\leq(1+\log A)/b$}

If $\mathbb P(|Z|\geq t)\leq A e^{-bt^2}$ for $A\geq1$ and $b>0$, then $\mathbb E Z^2\leq(1+\log A)/b$. Indeed the <tail integral formula for moments> gives $\mathbb E Z^2\leq\int_0^\infty\min(1,Ae^{-bu})\,du$; split at $u=(\log A)/b$. Clipping at one improves the constant compared with integrating the exponential bound alone.
