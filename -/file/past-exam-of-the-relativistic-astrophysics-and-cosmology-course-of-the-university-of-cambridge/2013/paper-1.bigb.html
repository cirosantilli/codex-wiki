= Solution
{parent=1}

For a blob in a <relativistic jet>, take two emission events separated by time $\Delta t_e$ in the galaxy frame. The blob moves transversely by $u\Delta t_e\sin\theta$, while its second <photon> has $u\Delta t_e\cos\theta$ less distance to travel to the distant observer. Therefore the arrival interval is $\Delta t_{\rm obs}=\Delta t_e(1-\beta\cos\theta)$ and
$$
\boxed{\beta_{\rm app}\equiv\frac{u_t}{c}=\frac{\beta\sin\theta}{1-\beta\cos\theta}}.
$$
Differentiating with respect to viewing angle gives $d\beta_{\rm app}/d\theta=\beta(\cos\theta-\beta)/(1-\beta\cos\theta)^2$. The maximum occurs at $\cos\theta=\beta$ and equals $\beta\gamma$. Hence <apparent superluminal motion> is possible precisely when $\beta\gamma>1$, or **$\beta>1/\sqrt2$**. This is an arrival-time effect; the material and every local signal still travel slower than <light>.

\Image[paper-1-jet-arrival.png]
{title=Original arrival-time geometry and apparent transverse speeds for subluminal jet velocities}

For $\gamma\gg1$, $1-\beta\simeq1/(2\gamma^2)$ and $1-\beta\cos\theta\simeq(\gamma^{-2}+\theta^2)/2$. Thus the <Doppler factor> is
$$
D\simeq\frac{2\gamma}{1+\gamma^2\theta^2}\longrightarrow\boxed{2\gamma}\quad(\theta\ll\gamma^{-1}).
$$
The <Lorentz-invariant specific intensity> gives $I_\nu/\nu^3=I'_{\nu'}/\nu'^3$. Since $\nu=D\nu'$, a <power-law spectrum> with $I'_{\nu'}\propto\nu'^{-\alpha}$ gives $I_\nu(\nu)=D^3I'_{\nu'}(\nu/D)\propto D^{3+\alpha}$. This is <Doppler boosting of a moving blob>: the <spectral flux density> of an unresolved, intrinsically isotropic blob has the same boost. Comparing identical approaching and receding blobs, with no differential absorption,
$$
\frac{S_1}{S_2}=\left(\frac{D_1}{D_2}\right)^{3+\alpha}
=\boxed{\left(\frac{1+\beta\cos\theta}{1-\beta\cos\theta}\right)^4}\quad(\alpha=1).
$$
A spatially steady continuous jet can have a different boost exponent because the emitting material contributing at one arrival time differs; it must not silently replace the specified blob model.

Using the stipulated peak of the <photon-photon pair production> <cross section>, not the exact head-on threshold, gives
$$
\epsilon_{20}=\frac{2(m_ec^2)^2}{\epsilon_1}
=\frac{2(5.11\times10^5)^2}{5\times10^{11}}\,\mathrm{eV}
=\boxed{1.04\,\mathrm{eV}}.
$$
The relevant <photons> are near <infrared>. Interpret the quoted $F_2$ as target-band <energy> flux, or <energy> flux per logarithmic <frequency> interval, consistent with its units $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$; it is not a <spectral flux density> per hertz. For isotropic emission $L_2=4\pi d^2F_2$. In a stationary source of radius $R$, the order-of-magnitude <photon> <energy density> is $L_2/(4\pi R^2c)$, so
$$
n\sim\frac{F_2d^2}{R^2c\epsilon_{20}},\qquad
\tau\sim\frac{F_2d^2\sigma_T}{5Rc\epsilon_{20}}.
$$
The <luminosity variability size bound> gives $R\lesssim c\Delta t$. Taking the largest allowed radius gives the smallest stationary-source <optical depth> in this estimate:
$$
\boxed{\tau_0\sim\frac{F_2d^2\sigma_T}{5\epsilon_{20}c^2\Delta t}}.
$$
This assumes the <gamma rays> and target <photons> occupy the same region, with an approximately isotropic target field and an order-unity geometric escape factor. A smaller region makes the <gamma-ray compactness problem> worse.

Here $d=3\times10^{25}\,\mathrm m$, $R\sim3\times10^{11}\,\mathrm m$, and $\epsilon_{20}=1.67\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\tau_0\simeq3.2\times10^7\gg1}.
$$
Such a stationary co-spatial source would absorb almost all of its high-energy <photons>.

Let primes denote the blob <rest frame> and continue to ignore <cosmological redshift>. For fixed observed gamma-ray <energy>, $\epsilon'_1=\epsilon_1/D$. The co-moving target <energy> at the assumed cross-section peak is therefore $\epsilon'_2=D\epsilon_{20}$. The <internal pair-production target energy in a relativistic blob> acquires a second <Doppler shift> when this same target population is observed along our <line of sight>:
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_{2,\rm obs}=D\epsilon'_2=D^2\epsilon_{20}}.
$$
It would be incorrect to transform only the <gamma ray> while treating the internal target <radiation> as a stationary external field. The target <photons> responsible for collisions span directions in the blob frame; the observed <energy> here labels the corresponding part of the internally emitted <radiation spectrum>.

<relativistic beaming> both increases the allowed rest-frame source radius, $R'\lesssim Dc\Delta t$, and lowers the inferred rest-frame <luminosity>, $L'_{\log}\sim L_{{\rm iso},\log}/D^4$. The <beamed gamma-ray optical depth> then follows from $\epsilon'_2=D\epsilon_{20}$:
$$
\tau'(D)\sim\tau_0D^{-6}
\frac{F_{\log}(D^2\epsilon_{20})}{F_{\log}(\epsilon_{20})}.
$$
For an unbroken <power-law spectrum> $F_\nu\propto\nu^{-\alpha}$, $F_{\log}=\nu F_\nu\propto\nu^{1-\alpha}$, so $\tau'\propto D^{-(4+2\alpha)}$. In particular, $\alpha=1$ gives the stated $D^{-6}$. Spectral breaks, an external target field or non-coincident emission regions change this inference. Under the requested scaling, transparency requires
$$
D\gtrsim\tau_0^{1/6}\simeq17.8,\qquad
\boxed{\gamma_{\min}\simeq8.9}.
$$
This minimum uses the most favourable nearly axial view, $D\simeq2\gamma$. The exact axial relation gives $\gamma_{\min}=(D_{\min}+D_{\min}^{-1})/2\simeq8.94$; a less favourable viewing angle requires a larger <Lorentz factor>. The numerical bound is conditional on the stipulated opacity scaling and internal-source geometry.

= Solution

The <Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker metric> describes homogeneous, isotropic spatial slices. Here $t$ is <cosmic time>, equal to the <proper time> of a <comoving observer>; $R(t)$ is the length-valued <scale factor>; and $\chi,\theta,\phi$ are dimensionless comoving radial, polar and azimuthal coordinates. The <speed of light> is $c$, and
$$
S(\chi)=\begin{cases}\sin\chi&k=+1,\\\chi&k=0,\\\sinh\chi&k=-1,\end{cases}
$$
for positive, zero and negative <cosmological spatial curvature>. The convention used makes $ds^2$ a squared time interval, with signature $(+---)$; multiplying the metric by $c^2$ gives the more usual squared-length convention. At fixed $t$, radial proper length is $R(t)d\chi$, while a sphere has area $4\pi R^2(t)S^2(\chi)$.

<luminosity distance> is defined by $F=L/(4\pi d_L^2)$ for bolometric <radiative flux> $F$ and intrinsic <luminosity> $L$. Radial null propagation gives $d\chi=\pm c\,dt/R(t)$. Applying the same path integral to two successive wave crests gives $\Delta t_0/R_0=\Delta t_e/R_e$, hence $1+z=R_0/R_e$. Each <photon> loses an <energy> factor $1+z$, and their arrival rate loses another. For transparent propagation and isotropic emission,
$$
F=\frac{L}{4\pi R_0^2S^2(\chi)(1+z)^2},\qquad
\boxed{d_L=R_0S(\chi)(1+z)}.
$$
The two <redshift> factors have distinct physical origins; geometrical dilution alone is insufficient.

With $H=\dot R/R$, differentiating $1+z=R_0/R$ yields $dt/dz=-[(1+z)H(z)]^{-1}$. Therefore the radial <comoving coordinate> is
$$
\chi=\int_{t_e}^{t_0}\frac{c\,dt}{R(t)}
=\boxed{\frac c{R_0}\int_0^z\frac{dz'}{H(z')}}.
$$
This is independent of any particular matter model; the <transverse comoving distance> is $R_0S(\chi)$, which equals $R_0\chi$ only for flat space.

For <pressureless matter>, the <continuity equation> $\dot\rho_m+3H\rho_m=0$ integrates to $\rho_m=\rho_{m0}(R_0/R)^3=\rho_{m0}(1+z)^3$. The <cosmological constant> is kept separately from this matter density. In the flat <Friedmann equation>, define
$$
\Omega_{\Lambda0}=\frac{\Lambda}{3H_0^2},\qquad
\Omega_{m0}=\frac{8\pi G\rho_{m0}}{3H_0^2}=1-\Omega_{\Lambda0}.
$$
The <flat dust-vacuum Hubble parameter> is therefore
$$
\boxed{H(z)=H_0\sqrt{(1-\Omega_{\Lambda0})(1+z)^3+\Omega_{\Lambda0}}}.
$$
In the equation's convention $\rho_m$ is mass-equivalent <energy density> and $\Lambda$ has units $\mathrm{s^{-2}}$; the geometrical <cosmological constant> convention instead writes $c^2\Lambda_{\rm geom}$. The model contains dust and <vacuum energy> with negligible <radiation> over the relevant late epochs: “matter-dominated” here does not require matter to exceed <vacuum energy> today.

The <cosmic microwave background> supplies a standard ruler through its acoustic scale: the angular positions of the <Cosmic microwave background acoustic peaks> constrain <cosmological spatial curvature>, while their relative heights constrain matter and <baryon> densities. Together with independent distance information, these favour a nearly flat <Lambda-CDM model> containing substantial <cold dark matter> and <vacuum energy>. The <cosmic microwave background> alone has a geometrical degeneracy between parameters affecting distance to last scattering; its analysis also includes early <radiation>, which the late-time formula above omits. The nine-year <WMAP> results were already available as a December 2012 preprint.<Nine-Year Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe (WMAP) Observations: Final Maps and Results by C. L. Bennett and others>[Version 1 abstract: combined-data curvature constraint and consistency with the six-parameter Lambda-CDM model]{ref}

A standardized <Type Ia supernova> at appreciable <redshift> is fainter, hence farther in <luminosity distance>, than in a matter-only <Einstein-de Sitter universe> with the same local expansion scale. Fitting the supernova <Hubble diagram> supports late acceleration; in flat dust plus vacuum, $q_0=\Omega_{m0}/2-\Omega_{\Lambda0}$ is negative for $\Omega_{\Lambda0}>1/3$. Light-curve and colour corrections, extinction, calibration, selection bias and possible population evolution must be controlled. The supernova evidence and <cosmic microwave background> constraints are complementary; together they favour this model without proving that every alternative dark-energy model is impossible.<Observational Evidence from Supernovae for an Accelerating Universe and a Cosmological Constant by Adam Riess and others>[Abstract and cosmological fits of the supernova distance-redshift relation]{ref}<Measurements of Omega and Lambda from 42 High-Redshift Supernovae by Saul Perlmutter and others>[Abstract and joint matter-density/cosmological-constant fits]{ref}

For the final comparison, assume $z>0$, a common $H_0$ and $0<\Omega_{\Lambda0}<1$ in model (a). Then
$$
H_c=H_0,\qquad H_a=H_0\sqrt{(1-\Omega_{\Lambda0})(1+z)^3+\Omega_{\Lambda0}},\qquad
H_b=H_0(1+z)^{3/2}.
$$
Thus $H_c<H_a<H_b$ and the integrands obey $1/H_b<1/H_a<1/H_c$. Because all three models are flat, compare the normalization-independent distance $R_0\chi=c\int dz'/H(z')$, rather than separately normalized coordinates. The <luminosity-distance ordering of flat dust-vacuum universes> is $d_{L,b}<d_{L,a}<d_{L,c}$, hence
$$
\boxed{F_b>F_a>F_c}.
$$
Model (b) gives the largest received flux, model (c) the smallest. Equalities replace the strict ordering at the endpoint values of the vacuum fraction; all have the same leading local <Hubble law> as $z\to0$.

In a close <binary star>, the rotating effective gravitational potential defines two <Roche lobes> meeting at the <inner Lagrange point>. Stellar expansion or orbital shrinkage can bring the donor into <Roche-lobe overflow>. Gas then flows through the <inner Lagrange point>; its <angular momentum> can form an <accretion disk> around the companion. <wind mass transfer in a binary star> is also possible without Roche-lobe filling. For a circular, synchronized binary, the <Eggleton Roche-lobe radius approximation> is
$$
\frac{R_L}{a}\simeq\frac{0.49q^{2/3}}{0.6q^{2/3}+\ln(1+q^{1/3})},\qquad q=\frac{M_d}{M_a}.
$$
This describes the lobe geometry, not the donor's dynamical response.<Approximations to the radii of Roche lobes by Peter P. Eggleton>[Printed p. 368, equation 2]{ref}

<conservative binary mass transfer> has fixed total <mass> and <orbital angular momentum>, neglecting stellar spin. Using $J=M_dM_a\sqrt{Ga/(M_d+M_a)}$ gives
$$
\frac{\dot a}{a}=-2\dot M_d\left(\frac1{M_d}-\frac1{M_a}\right).
$$
Since $\dot M_d<0$, transfer from the more massive donor initially shrinks the orbit; after <binary mass ratio> reversal it expands. Stability depends on both stars and their changing lobes. Let $\zeta_*=d\ln R_d/d\ln M_d$ and $\zeta_L=d\ln R_L/d\ln M_d$. A small <mass> loss reduces the relative overfill if **$\zeta_*>\zeta_L$**. Use the adiabatic donor response for dynamical stability, and its thermal response for slower adjustment; an equilibrium mass-radius law alone cannot decide both.

Stable transfer can proceed on nuclear or thermal timescales, while dynamically unstable overflow may create a <common envelope>, orbital spiral-in and either envelope ejection or a merger. Escaping gas, <gravitational radiation> and <magnetic braking> make evolution nonconservative by changing <orbital angular momentum>. <accretion> onto a <compact stellar object> releases <power> of order $GM_a\dot M/R_a$, producing bright <X-ray binaries>; radiation-driven outflows can limit retained <mass>. Eclipses, <radial velocities> and disk emission constrain the geometry and <masses>, but inclination, irradiation and assumptions about <mass> or angular-momentum loss affect the inference. **The essential feedback is the donor's radius relative to its evolving <Roche lobe>.**

A <galaxy cluster> deflects background <light> through its projected gravitational field. In the <thin-lens approximation for gravitational lensing>, $\boldsymbol\beta=\boldsymbol\theta-\boldsymbol\alpha(\boldsymbol\theta)$ maps image to source angle. Its <surface mass density> enters through
$$
\kappa=\frac{\Sigma}{\Sigma_{\rm crit}},\qquad
\Sigma_{\rm crit}=\frac{c^2D_s}{4\pi GD_lD_{ls}},\qquad
\mu=\frac1{(1-\kappa)^2-|\gamma_s|^2}.
$$
Here $\mu$ is signed <lensing magnification>; observed brightness uses $|\mu|$. The $D$'s are <angular diameter distances> to lens, source and between them; $\gamma_s$ denotes <lensing shear>, not a <Lorentz factor>. <strong gravitational lensing> creates arcs and multiple images near critical curves. For an axisymmetric lens, a tangential <Einstein ring> satisfies mean enclosed convergence one, giving
$$
\boxed{M_{\rm proj}(<D_l\theta_E)=\pi(D_l\theta_E)^2\Sigma_{\rm crit}}.
$$
This is projected enclosed <mass>, not a general three-dimensional <mass> profile inferred from a single arc.

<weak gravitational lensing> uses coherent distortions of many background galaxies. Their intrinsic orientations average approximately to zero, allowing an estimate of <reduced shear> $g=\gamma_s/(1-\kappa)$ and a projected <mass> map. The method traces all gravitating material without assuming <hydrostatic equilibrium>, which is valuable in merging clusters. Accurate work requires correction of the <point spread function>, calibrated shape measurement, source <redshifts>, foreground-member rejection and selection effects. Intrinsic shape noise, unrelated line-of-sight structures and lens-model degeneracies remain. The <mass-sheet degeneracy> leaves <reduced shear> unchanged under $\kappa\mapsto\lambda\kappa+(1-\lambda)$ and $\gamma_s\mapsto\lambda\gamma_s$, so absolute <mass> normalization needs additional information.

In the <Bullet Cluster>, <weak gravitational lensing> places the main projected <mass> peaks near the collisionless galaxies, displaced from the X-ray-emitting gas that contains most observed <baryon> <mass>. Comparing the lensing, optical and <X-ray> maps supports a dominant weakly collisional dark component under the usual gravity model; lensing alone does not identify its particle species. **Cluster lensing measures projected gravitational <mass> independently of gas <temperature> or dynamical equilibrium.**<A direct empirical proof of the existence of dark matter by Douglas Clowe and others>[Abstract and spatial comparison of weak-lensing mass, galaxies and X-ray gas]{ref}

= Solution

<cosmic inflation> is an expanding epoch with $\ddot a>0$, equivalently a shrinking <comoving Hubble radius> $(aH)^{-1}=1/\dot a$. In <general relativity> it requires $\rho+3P/c^2<0$ for the total effective fluid. A <scalar field> whose <potential energy> dominates its <kinetic energy> has $P\simeq-\rho c^2$ and can produce approximately exponential expansion. <slow-roll inflation> is one realization; rapid expansion alone without acceleration does not have the required effect.

The <horizon problem> is that widely separated regions of the <cosmic microwave background> have nearly the same <temperature> despite lying outside one another's causal domains in a purely decelerating hot <Big Bang> extrapolation. Sufficient inflation stretches a previously small causally connected region across our observable universe; relevant perturbation <wavelengths> can begin inside the <Hubble radius>, leave it during inflation and re-enter later. This does not require local signals to outrun <light>. Inflation supplies a possible earlier causal history, not a proof that every possible initial state becomes homogeneous.

For the <flatness problem>, the <Friedmann equation> gives $\Omega_{\rm tot}-1=kc^2/(aH)^2$ when $a$ is length-valued. Since $aH$ grows during inflation,
$$
\boxed{|\Omega_{\rm tot}-1|\ \hbox{decreases during inflation}}.
$$
Pre-existing unwanted massive relics such as <magnetic monopoles> are also diluted: conserved <number density> falls as $a^{-3}$. Enough inflation, followed by <reheating> that does not recreate the relic problem, can make their abundance negligible. These were central motivations of the original proposal, whose first phase-transition implementation also faced a graceful-exit problem.<Inflationary universe: A possible solution to the horizon and flatness problems by Alan H. Guth>[Abstract: horizon, flatness, monopole suppression and limitations of the original scenario]{ref}

<quantum fluctuations> of the inflating field and metric supply seeds for <linear cosmological density perturbations>. Simple single-field slow-roll models predict a dominant <adiabatic mode> with nearly Gaussian, nearly scale-invariant <scalar cosmological perturbations>, consistent with the <cosmic microwave background>; <tensor cosmological perturbations> are another possible test. These predictions are model-dependent, and consistency is not unique proof of inflation. <reheating> must connect the accelerated phase to the hot expanding universe. **Inflation addresses causal reach, small <cosmological spatial curvature> and unwanted relic abundance, while providing a mechanism for primordial structure.**

= Solution

<accretion> onto a massive <black hole> releases <luminosity> $L\simeq\eta\dot M c^2$. Even a small coupling of this <energy> to surrounding gas can matter because a galactic binding <energy> is only of order $M_{\rm gas}\sigma^2$, with stellar <velocity> dispersion $\sigma\ll c$. <active-galactic-nucleus feedback> means that the <energy> or <momentum> changes the gas supply, cooling or star formation, potentially regulating further <accretion>. Large <energy> availability alone does not establish efficient coupling.

In a radiatively efficient bright nucleus, <radiation pressure> and winds can push on dusty or ionized gas. Outflows can remove fuel or shock-heat it; the outcome depends on cooling, covering factor, clumping and whether the flow escapes. In a mechanically dominated state, <relativistic jets> inflate radio cavities in a hot halo or <galaxy cluster>. Their expansion, shocks, <sound waves> and subsequent motions can distribute <energy> and suppress a <cooling flow>. The two descriptions identify useful channels rather than a universal sharp division between all nuclei.

An approximately pressure-balanced cavity containing a relativistic fluid has <enthalpy>
$$
E_{\rm cav}=\frac{\Gamma}{\Gamma-1}pV\simeq4pV\quad(\Gamma=4/3),\qquad
P_{\rm cav}\sim\frac{4pV}{t_{\rm age}}.
$$
<X-ray> <pressure> estimates, cavity volume and buoyant or sound-crossing ages permit comparison with radiative cooling losses. Radio images check the jet association; <X-ray> fronts and ripples help locate transferred <energy>. Deep Chandra observations of Perseus reveal such structures and motivate mechanical heating of the cooling atmosphere.<A deep Chandra observation of the Perseus cluster: shocks and ripples by A. C. Fabian and others>[Abstract and discussion of radio-bubble-associated fronts, ripples and heating]{ref}

Projection, nonrelativistic cavity contents, <pressure> gradients, uncertain ages and intermittent duty cycles affect the <power> estimate. Injected cavity <energy> need not all dissipate where cooling occurs. A successful negative feedback loop requires that falling gas increases <accretion>, heating then reduces cooling or inflow, and the delay does not destroy regulation. This can explain reduced cooling and the shortage of excessively massive star-forming galaxies; correlations between black-hole <mass> and bulge properties are supporting context rather than standalone proof of causation. **The key comparison is coupled heating versus local cooling and binding, including where and when <energy> is deposited.**

The <age of the universe> is a model-dependent elapsed <cosmic time>, not simply the inverse present <Hubble constant>. For a model with a hot <Big Bang> origin,
$$
\boxed{t_0=\int_0^\infty\frac{dz}{(1+z)H(z)}}.
$$
An <Einstein-de Sitter universe> gives $t_0=2/(3H_0)$. The <age of a flat matter-Lambda universe>, neglecting early <radiation>, is
$$
t_0=\frac{2}{3H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda0}}}
\operatorname{arsinh}\sqrt{\frac{\Omega_{\Lambda0}}{\Omega_{m0}}},\qquad
\Omega_{m0}=1-\Omega_{\Lambda0}.
$$
This follows by integrating $dt=da/(aH)$ and substituting $x=\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda0}/\Omega_{m0}}\,(a/a_0)^{3/2}$. It tends to $2/(3H_0)$ as the vacuum fraction tends to zero. Pure <de Sitter spacetime> instead has constant $H$ and no finite dust <Big Bang> age obtained from this integral. <cosmic microwave background> ages therefore depend on the assumed expansion model and fitted parameters; the <WMAP> nine-year combined-data <Lambda-CDM model> inference was approximately 13.8 billion years, not a direct clock reading.<Nine-Year Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe (WMAP) Observations: Final Maps and Results by C. L. Bennett and others>[Version 1 abstract, inferred cosmic age]{ref}

Old <globular clusters> provide an independent lower bound. Their <main-sequence turnoff> identifies stars just exhausting core hydrogen; the nuclear fuel divided by <luminosity> sets a mass-dependent lifetime, so a fitted <colour-magnitude diagram> constrains the common <stellar age>. Distance, extinction, <metallicity>, helium abundance, diffusion and stellar-model physics introduce uncertainties. The elapsed time before cluster formation must be added when comparing with the cosmic age.<New Globular Cluster Age Estimates and Constraints on the Cosmic Equation of State and The Matter Density of the Universe by Lawrence M. Krauss and Brian Chaboyer>[Abstract and joint stellar-age/cosmological interpretation]{ref}

<white-dwarf cooling ages> offer another clock: faint remnants have lost more stored thermal <energy>, but progenitor lifetimes must be added and <crystallization>, envelope properties and sample completeness affect the inference. <nucleocosmochronology> uses long-lived radioactive abundance ratios, with $Q/Q_0=e^{-(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)t}$ for two radioactive species, or a stable reference with $\lambda_2=0$. Uncertain initial production ratios and subsequent enrichment limit precision. **Every reliably dated object must be younger than the universe by at least its formation delay**; consistency among distinct clocks tests both cosmology and their astrophysical calibrations.
