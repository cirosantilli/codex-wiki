= Solution
{parent=1}

**<photon> orbit and the Newtonian limit.** Write $r_g=GM/c^2$ and $e=E/(mc^2)$. For a <timelike circular Schwarzschild geodesic>,
$$
e(r)=\frac{1-2r_g/r}{\sqrt{1-3r_g/r}}.
$$
The denominator tends to zero at $r=3r_g$, so the <energy> per unit <rest mass> diverges as a massive circular orbit approaches that <radius>. The corresponding local <orbital speed> measured by a static observer satisfies $v_{\rm loc}^2/c^2=r_g/(r-2r_g)$ and tends to one. This suggests a null circular orbit, but one must not simply insert $m=0$ into a massive-particle formula. For a <photon>, the radial <effective potential> is proportional to $(1-2r_g/r)/r^2$; its <derivative> vanishes at $r=3r_g$ and its second <derivative> there is negative. Thus **the <photon sphere> is at $3GM/c^2$, and its circular null orbits are unstable**.

At $r\gg r_g$, expansion gives
$$
e=1-\frac{r_g}{2r}+O\left(\frac{r_g^2}{r^2}\right),\qquad
\boxed{E=mc^2-\frac{GMm}{2r}+\cdots}.
$$
The second term is the total Newtonian circular-orbit <energy>, kinetic plus potential; the relativistic <energy> also includes <rest energy>. Here $E$ is the conserved <energy> referred to a stationary observer at infinity, not the local <kinetic energy>.

**The stable inner edge.** At fixed <angular momentum>, write $\ell=L/(mc)$. The timelike radial <geodesic> equation can be written
$$
\left(\frac{1}{c}\frac{dr}{d\tau}\right)^2+V_{\rm eff}(r)=e^2,\qquad
V_{\rm eff}=\left(1-\frac{2r_g}{r}\right)\left(1+\frac{\ell^2}{r^2}\right).
$$
The circular condition $V_{\rm eff}'=0$ gives $\ell^2=r_g r^2/(r-3r_g)$. Substituting into the second <derivative> taken at fixed $\ell$ gives
$$
V_{\rm eff}''=\frac{2r_g(r-6r_g)}{r^3(r-3r_g)}.
$$
Timelike circular orbits exist for $r>3r_g$; they are stable against radial perturbations for $r>6r_g$ and unstable for $3r_g<r<6r_g$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{r_{\rm ISCO}=6r_g,\qquad e_{\rm ISCO}=\sqrt{\frac89}=0.942809.}
$$
The boundary orbit is marginally stable. Differentiating $e(r)$ also gives a minimum there, consistently with this fixed-angular-momentum stability calculation.

**<accretion> efficiency and <black-hole spin>.** Matter supplied slowly from far away initially brings <rest energy> $dm_0c^2$. A radiatively efficient, zero-inner-torque <thin disk> releases the <binding energy> down to the <innermost stable circular orbit>; the remaining <energy> $e_{\rm ISCO}dm_0c^2$ is carried into the hole during the plunge. Thus
$$
\boxed{\eta=1-e_{\rm ISCO}=1-\sqrt{\frac89}=0.05719}.
$$
Here $\eta$ denotes the efficiency called $\epsilon$ in this question. The ideal relation assumes negligible additional dissipation during plunge and neglects <photon> capture and <advection>. A hot radiatively inefficient flow need not have this efficiency.

For a <Kerr black hole>, an equatorial prograde disk has a smaller <innermost stable circular orbit> and greater binding-energy efficiency than the Schwarzschild value; a retrograde disk has the opposite trend. The formal extremal prograde limits are $r_{\rm ISCO}\to r_g$ and $\eta\to1-1/\sqrt3\simeq0.423$. These are ideal limiting values, not a universal realized efficiency. <photon> capture and the <accretion> geometry affect the achievable <black-hole spin> and escaping <radiation>.

**<radiation> <force> and the limiting <luminosity>.** For isotropic <luminosity> $L$, the outward <radiation> <momentum flux> at <radius> $r$ is $L/(4\pi r^2c)$. In fully ionized <hydrogen>, <Thomson scattering> acts mainly on the <electron>, while electrostatic coupling makes the <electron> and <proton> move together. Neglecting the <electron> <mass>, one <electron>–<proton> pair experiences
$$
F_{\rm rad}=\frac{\sigma_T L}{4\pi r^2c},\qquad
F_{\rm grav}=\frac{GMm_p}{r^2}.
$$
Equating them yields
$$
\boxed{L_{\rm Edd}=\frac{4\pi GMm_pc}{\sigma_T}}.
$$
This <Eddington luminosity> uses <electron-scattering opacity>, spherical or isotropic <force> balance and negligible relativistic corrections at the force-comparison <radius>. Composition, other <opacity>, anisotropy and <photon> trapping can change the relevant limit.

**Exponential growth and doubling.** Distinguish rest-mass supply $\dot m_0$ from black-hole <mass> growth $\dot M$:
$$
L=\eta\dot m_0c^2,\qquad
\dot M=(1-\eta)\dot m_0.
$$
At the Eddington limit,
$$
\dot M=\frac{1-\eta}{\eta}\frac{M}{t_E},\qquad
t_E=\frac{\sigma_Tc}{4\pi Gm_p},\qquad
M(t)=M_i\exp\left(\frac{t-t_i}{t_S}\right),
\quad
t_S=\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}t_E.
$$
Using the printed scattering cross section gives $t_E\simeq4.50\times10^8\ \mathrm{yr}$. The <Salpeter time> $t_S$ is an e-folding time; the <Eddington-limited doubling time> is
$$
\boxed{t_2=\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}\frac{\sigma_Tc}{4\pi Gm_p}\log2
\simeq1.89\times10^7\ \mathrm{yr}}
$$
for the Schwarzschild thin-disk efficiency. If one approximates $\dot M\simeq\dot m_0$, the factor $1/(1-\eta)$ is dropped and the estimate is about $1.79\times10^7\ \mathrm{yr}$. The retained-energy version is the mass-conserving expression.

**The efficiency bound.** A growth factor $10^8$ requires $N=\log(10^8)=18.4207$ e-folds. Eddington-limited <accretion> is the fastest growth allowed by the adopted <luminosity> bound at fixed efficiency, so growth within a time $\Delta t$ requires
$$
N\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}t_E\leq\Delta t,\qquad
\boxed{\eta\leq\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta t+N t_E}\simeq0.108}.
$$
This <efficiency upper bound from Eddington-limited growth> is an **upper bound**: higher efficiency radiates more of each supplied <rest mass>, and the <luminosity> ceiling therefore allows less <mass> to enter per unit time. A duty cycle below unity or sub-Eddington <luminosity> tightens it. The approximate convention $\dot M=\dot m_0$ would instead give $\eta\lesssim0.121$; both figures refer to the same effect, with different treatment of the radiated <mass> deficit. Mergers, super-Eddington growth or a different seed history would change the premises.

Coherent prograde <accretion> <spins> up the hole, moves the inner edge inward and increases $\eta$. For fixed $L/L_{\rm Edd}$ the mass-growth e-folding time then grows, so the logarithmic growth curve becomes less steep; one cannot simply retain the initial Schwarzschild efficiency throughout. Episodic misaligned <accretion> can have a different <black-hole spin> history. In general,
$$
\log\frac{M_f}{M_i}
=\frac1{t_E}\int_{t_i}^{t_f} f_{\rm Edd}(t)\frac{1-\eta(t)}{\eta(t)}\,dt.
$$

**<energy> available to affect the <galaxy>.** Let $M_g$ denote <galaxy> <mass>, keeping it distinct from black-hole <mass>. At constant efficiency, negligible seed <mass> and no merger contribution, the emitted <energy> in building $M_{\rm BH}=0.002M_g$ is
$$
E_{\rm rad}=\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}M_{\rm BH}c^2,\qquad
\boxed{\frac{E_{\rm rad}}{E_{\rm bind}}\sim
0.002\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}\left(\frac{c}{v}\right)^2}.
$$
For $v=300\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and the nonspinning efficiency this is **approximately $121$**. The common small-efficiency approximation gives $2000\eta\simeq114$, and a fiducial $\eta=0.1$ would give about $222$ with retained <mass> included. These differences do not alter the conclusion: the available <accretion> <energy> substantially exceeds the stated galactic binding-energy estimate.

Only a coupling fraction of order one percent would then be energetically sufficient to affect the gas strongly. <radiation>, winds or jets can heat gas, drive outflows and suppress further cooling and <star formation>, providing <active-galactic-nucleus feedback> and possible self-regulation of black-hole growth. <energy> availability alone does not prove that the whole <galaxy> is expelled: most <radiation> can escape, jets can be collimated, heated gas can cool, and collisionless <stars> couple differently from gas.

**Definitions and <pressure> convention.** $R(t)$ is the <scale factor>, $t$ <cosmic time> and dots <time derivatives>. $G$ is the <gravitational constant>, $c$ the <speed of light>, $\rho$ the mass-equivalent <density> of the material fluid and $k=+1,0,-1$ the spatial-curvature sign in this normalization. $\Lambda$ here has units of inverse time squared, rather than inverse length squared. Comparison of the acceleration equation with $\ddot R/R=\Lambda/3-4\pi G(\rho+3P/c^2)/3$ fixes
$$
\boxed{\epsilon=\frac{3P}{\rho c^2}=3w}.
$$
This $\epsilon$ is a <pressure> parameter, unrelated to Question 1's radiative efficiency. <pressureless matter> has $P=0$ and $\epsilon=0$. When solving the generalized model below, $\epsilon$ is assumed constant.

**Evidence for a positive vacuum component.** Standardized <Type Ia supernovae> measure the luminosity-distance–<redshift> relation. Their corrected apparent magnitudes at cosmological distances imply greater distances than a decelerating matter-dominated model of comparable parameters, favoring late accelerated expansion. Light-curve standardization, <interstellar extinction>, selection bias and possible population evolution must be controlled rather than treating every supernova as an identical candle.<Observational Evidence from Supernovae for an Accelerating Universe and a Cosmological Constant by Adam Riess and others>[Abstract and distance–redshift analysis]{ref}<Measurements of Omega and Lambda from 42 High-Redshift Supernovae by Saul Perlmutter and others>[Abstract and cosmological likelihood analysis]{ref}

The <cosmic microwave background> and <baryon acoustic oscillation> measurements constrain geometry and matter <density> in combination. Near spatial flatness together with a subcritical matter <density> leaves room for an additional smooth component; cosmic ages and structure growth supply consistency tests. A positive <cosmological constant> fits these observations within the standard framework. They do not establish that every allowed dark-energy theory must be a strictly constant vacuum <energy>; the inference depends on the cosmological and gravitational model.

**<pressureless matter> evolution.** Set $k=0$ and $\epsilon=0$. Since $\dot H=\ddot R/R-H^2$, eliminating $\rho$ gives
$$
\dot H=-\frac32\left(H^2-\frac{\Lambda}{3}\right),\qquad
\boxed{\frac23\dot H+H^2=\frac{\Lambda}{3}}.
$$
For $H=b\coth(at)$, $\dot H=-ab\,\mathrm{csch}^2(at)$ and $\coth^2x=1+\mathrm{csch}^2x$. Therefore
$$
b^2+\left(b^2-\frac23ab\right)\mathrm{csch}^2(at)=\frac{\Lambda}{3}.
$$
On the expanding Big-Bang branch with positive $\Lambda$, choose
$$
\boxed{b=\sqrt{\frac{\Lambda}{3}},\qquad
a=\frac32b=\frac{\sqrt{3\Lambda}}2,\qquad
H(t)=\sqrt{\frac{\Lambda}{3}}
\coth\left(\frac{\sqrt{3\Lambda}}2t\right)}.
$$
The origin $t=0$ is the <Big Bang>; an arbitrary time translation would shift this origin. Integrating gives $R\propto\sinh^{2/3}(at)$. At early times $H\sim2/(3t)$, while at late times $H\to\sqrt{\Lambda/3}$.

**Age and the <density> fractions.** Dividing the flat constraint by $H^2$, with $\rho_{\rm crit}=3H^2/(8\pi G)$, gives
$$
\boxed{\Omega_m+\Omega_\Lambda=1,\qquad
\Omega_\Lambda=\frac{\Lambda}{3H^2}}.
$$
The solution implies $\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda}=\tanh(at)$, so
$$
\boxed{t_0=\frac{2}{3H_0\sqrt{1-\Omega_{m0}}}
\operatorname{artanh}\sqrt{1-\Omega_{m0}}}.
$$
Here $H_0=2.3331\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, using the printed megaparsec conversion. Taking one year as $365.25$ days gives
$$
\boxed{t_0\simeq1.309\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{yr}=13.09\ \mathrm{Gyr}.}
$$
For the same $H_0$, an <Einstein-de Sitter universe> has
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm EdS}=\frac{2}{3H_0}\simeq9.05\ \mathrm{Gyr}.}
$$
A cosmic model must be older than its oldest objects, with additional time allowed for their formation. Old stellar populations are dated through stellar-evolution fitting, particularly the <globular cluster> <main-sequence turnoff>, with uncertainties from distances, composition and input stellar physics. A pre-examination <globular-cluster age constraint on cosmology> obtained a $95\%$ lower cosmic-age limit of $11\ \mathrm{Gyr}$ after allowing for formation time. This conflicts with the $9.05\ \mathrm{Gyr}$ model but is compatible with the $13.09\ \mathrm{Gyr}$ calculation.<New Globular Cluster Age Estimates and Constraints on the Cosmic Equation of State and The Matter Density of the Universe by Lawrence M. Krauss and Brian Chaboyer>[Abstract and stellar-age/formation-time analysis]{ref} Individual <stellar age> estimates are not exact clocks, and the numerical $13.09\ \mathrm{Gyr}$ is the result for the specified $H_0$ and matter fraction, not a claim to be a contemporary best-fit age.

**Constant nonzero <pressure> parameter.** Eliminating $\rho$ before setting the <pressure> to zero instead gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot H
&=\frac{\Lambda}{3}-H^2-\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}
\left(H^2-\frac{\Lambda}{3}\right)\\
&=-\frac{3+\epsilon}{2}
\left(H^2-\frac{\Lambda}{3}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\frac{2}{3+\epsilon}\dot H+H^2-\frac{\Lambda}{3}=0}.
$$
For constant $\epsilon>-3$, the positive-density expanding Big-Bang branch is
$$
\boxed{H(t)=H_\Lambda\coth\left(\frac{3+\epsilon}{2}H_\Lambda t\right),
\qquad H_\Lambda=\sqrt{\frac{\Lambda}{3}}}.
$$
Equivalently use $t'=(3+\epsilon)t/3$ in the <pressureless matter> solution. The <scale factor> obeys $R\propto\sinh^{2/(3+\epsilon)}[(3+\epsilon)H_\Lambda t/2]$, and the early limit is $H\simeq2/[(3+\epsilon)t]$. The <age of a flat barotropic matter-vacuum universe> with the same present <density> fractions and $H_0$ is
$$
t_0(\epsilon)=\frac{2\,\operatorname{artanh}\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda0}}}
{(3+\epsilon)H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda0}}}
=\frac{3}{3+\epsilon}t_0(0).
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{t_0(0.2)\simeq12.28\ \mathrm{Gyr}.}
$$
The small positive <pressure> makes the early universe expand with a smaller age at the same late expansion parameters.

**The acceleration transition.** Normalize the present <scale factor> to one. Constant $\epsilon$ and the continuity equation give $\rho=\rho_0(1+z)^{3+\epsilon}$. Dividing the acceleration equation by $H_0^2$ gives
$$
\frac{\ddot R}{R H_0^2}
=\Omega_{\Lambda0}-\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}
\Omega_{m0}(1+z)^{3+\epsilon}.
$$
At the <barotropic matter-vacuum acceleration transition> its right-hand side vanishes. For the parameters here, $1+\epsilon>0$, so the past transition is
$$
\boxed{z_{\rm tr}=
\left[\frac{2(1-\Omega_{m0})}{(1+\epsilon)\Omega_{m0}}\right]^{1/(3+\epsilon)}-1
\simeq0.529}.
$$
At greater <redshift> the material term dominates and expansion decelerates; at smaller <redshift> vacuum <energy> dominates and expansion accelerates. The <pressure> factor $1+\epsilon$ belongs in the numerator of the gravitating material term; omitting it would confuse this model with <pressureless matter>.

Three tracers give complementary estimates of a cluster's gravitating <mass>. First, <galaxy> <redshifts> measure a line-of-sight <velocity dispersion> $\sigma_{\rm los}$. For a roughly stationary bound cluster, the <virial theorem> gives a <cluster velocity-dispersion mass estimate>
$$
M(<r)\sim\frac{3\sigma_{\rm los}^2r}{G},
$$
with a coefficient depending on the <density> profile and orbital anisotropy. The <galaxy> <luminosity> and plausible stellar <mass-to-light ratio> supply too little <mass> to bind typical rich clusters. Foreground interlopers, substructure, velocity anisotropy and a recent merger can bias the dynamical estimate; a large dispersion is not by itself proof of equilibrium.

Second, diffuse X-ray emission measures a hot intracluster <plasma>, not merely unresolved starlight. Spectral continuum and lines constrain its <temperature>, while surface-brightness profiles constrain its <density> after <line-of-sight projection> and emissivity modeling. If the gas has constant composition and is approximately spherical and in <hydrostatic equilibrium>,
$$
\frac{dP_g}{dr}=-\rho_g\frac{GM(<r)}{r^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{M(<r)=-\frac{k_BT(r)r}{G\mu m_p}
\left(\frac{d\log\rho_g}{d\log r}+\frac{d\log T}{d\log r}\right)}.
$$
This <cluster hydrostatic mass estimator> uses $\mu m_p$ as the mean <mass> per pressure-supporting particle. The inferred potential requires substantially more <mass> than the <stars> plus measured gas, although the gas contains much of the visible baryonic <mass>. Turbulence, bulk motion, <temperature> structure and departures from <hydrostatic equilibrium> are important <systematic errors>.

Third, strong-lensing arcs and multiple images, together with coherent weak-lensing shear of background <galaxies>, measure projected gravitational deflection. In <general relativity>,
$$
\kappa=\frac{\Sigma}{\Sigma_{\rm crit}},\qquad
\Sigma_{\rm crit}=\frac{c^2}{4\pi G}\frac{D_s}{D_lD_{ls}}.
$$
This <cluster gravitational-lensing mass estimate> does not require the cluster <galaxies> or gas to be in dynamical equilibrium. It does require source-distance information, a lens model and control of point-spread-function-induced shapes, <line-of-sight projection>, unrelated line-of-sight structure and the <mass-sheet degeneracy>.

**The convergence of dynamical, hydrostatic and lensing <masses>, exceeding the baryonic inventory, is the principal evidence.** The Bullet Cluster adds a spatial test: collisional X-ray gas lags behind the <galaxies> during the merger, while the inferred lensing concentrations lie nearer the collisionless <galaxy> component. An additional weakly collisional gravitating <mass> accounts naturally for that separation.<A direct empirical proof of the existence of dark matter by Douglas Clowe and others>[L109–L113, optical/X-ray/lensing comparison]{ref} The conclusion assumes a gravitational framework and does not identify the microscopic dark-matter particle; the different tracer systematics should be evaluated rather than treating three <mass> estimates as completely assumption free.

<cosmic inflation> is an early interval of accelerated expansion, $\ddot R>0$. For expansion this means a shrinking <comoving Hubble radius>, $d[(RH)^{-1}]/dt<0$. A nearly constant positive <scalar potential> can supply it: in $c=1$ units,
$$
\rho_\phi=\frac{\dot\phi^2}{2}+V(\phi),\qquad
P_\phi=\frac{\dot\phi^2}{2}-V(\phi).
$$
Potential domination gives $\rho_\phi+3P_\phi<0$ and approximately exponential expansion. Sustaining the interval requires sufficiently slow evolution, not just setting the initial field velocity to zero on a steep potential.

The <horizon problem> is the near equality of conditions in widely separated microwave-background regions that could not equilibrate in the standard decelerating hot <Big Bang> history. <cosmic inflation> permits the currently observed patch to descend from an earlier region small enough to have been causally connected, before that patch is stretched to enormous size. The claim concerns a causal history extended before the conventional hot phase; merely accelerating a collection of already disconnected regions would not explain their common <temperature>.

For the <flatness problem>, the Friedmann constraint gives
$$
|\Omega_{\rm tot}-1|=\frac{|k|c^2}{R^2H^2}.
$$
A growing $RH$ drives this curvature fraction toward zero, whereas ordinary matter- or radiation-dominated deceleration makes it grow. <cosmic inflation> therefore suppresses observable curvature without requiring an equally extreme initial tuning within the later hot evolution.

Pre-existing monopoles and other unwanted relics are also diluted by the volume increase: a conserved particle number <density> falls as $e^{-3N}$ over $N$ e-folds. The transition to the hot universe must reheat efficiently, and its <temperature> must not regenerate an excluded relic population. The required number of e-folds is model and <reheating> dependent; “about sixty” is a useful scale for many scenarios rather than a universal exact requirement.

<cosmic inflation> also supplies a mechanism for perturbations. <quantum fluctuations> are stretched beyond the Hubble <radius>, and nearly scale-invariant <comoving curvature perturbations> can later seed cosmic structure. Simple single-field slow-roll models commonly predict approximately adiabatic, nearly Gaussian fluctuations, with a small spectral tilt; tensor perturbations would provide an additional test. Acoustic coherence and the observed scalar spectrum are compatible with this account, but they do not single out one <inflaton> potential. **<cosmic inflation> addresses causal homogeneity, curvature and relic dilution while motivating the initial perturbations; it does not automatically solve the <cosmological constant> problem or determine <reheating>.**

A <radio pulsar> is a rapidly rotating, magnetized <neutron star> whose emission beam periodically crosses the line of sight. Collapse of a massive stellar core can leave such a compact remnant. Angular-momentum conservation during contraction helps explain rapid <rotation>, while amplified <magnetic fields> and a plasma-filled magnetosphere enable particle acceleration and beamed emission. The pulses are not repeated stellar contractions. Not every newborn <neutron star> is seen as a <radio pulsar>, because emission, beaming and observational selection matter.

<rotation> supplies <energy> to <radiation> and the magnetized wind. With approximately constant <moment of inertia> $I$,
$$
E_{\rm rot}=\frac12I\Omega^2,\qquad
\boxed{L_{\rm sd}=4\pi^2I\frac{\dot P}{P^3}}.
$$
An ideal fixed magnetic-dipole torque has $\dot\Omega=-K\Omega^3$. Its integration gives
$$
t=\frac{P}{2\dot P}\left[1-\left(\frac{P_{\rm birth}}P\right)^2\right],
$$
so the <pulsar characteristic age> $\tau_c=P/(2\dot P)$ approximates the true age only for a short birth period, constant torque coefficient and <braking index> three. Real magnetospheres, field evolution and kinematic contributions to observed $\dot P$ complicate both age and inferred <magnetic field>.

As the <star> <spins> down, the available accelerating potential falls. If particle acceleration and <pair production> can no longer sustain the radio-emitting magnetosphere, the object crosses a model-dependent <pulsar death line> and ceases to be detectable as an ordinary <radio pulsar>. It still exists as a cooling <neutron star>; “death” refers to the emission state, not disappearance of the <star>. The death boundary is not a precisely universal spin period.

In a surviving binary, later <Roche-lobe overflow> from a companion can create an <accretion disc>. Transferred <angular momentum> <spins> the old <neutron star> up, and the <accretion> <luminosity> can produce an <X-ray binary>. Long <accretion> episodes can produce millisecond <rotation>, commonly accompanied by a smaller inferred external <magnetic field>. After <mass> transfer declines, the <radio pulsar> can switch on again as a <recycled pulsar>, often with a <white dwarf> companion. Other binary histories can leave a second <neutron star>, an ablated companion or an isolated recycled object; there is no single obligatory evolutionary sequence.

A direct observational bridge is the coherent approximately $2.49\ \mathrm{ms}$ X-ray pulsation discovered with RXTE in SAX J1808.4-3658, an accretion-powered binary. It demonstrated an accreting <neutron star> rotating at a rate appropriate to recycled millisecond <radio pulsars>.<Discovery of the first accretion-powered millisecond X-ray pulsar by Rudy Wijnands and Michiel van der Klis>[Abstract, coherent pulsations and interpretation]{ref} Timing analysis must correct orbital <Doppler shifts>, telescope and solar-system timing, and instrumental aliases. Radio dispersion, sky coverage and beaming selection affect demographic inferences. **The sequence is rotational <pulsar spin-down>, possible loss of radio activity, and possible accretion-driven recycling—not a necessary path for every <neutron star>.**

The <cosmic microwave background> has an almost perfect <blackbody spectrum> at about $2.725\ \mathrm{K}$. This is strong evidence for an earlier hot, dense phase in which <radiation> thermalized, followed by expansion that redshifted the <photon> spectrum while preserving its Planck form. <COBE> <FIRAS> compared the sky with a precision reference <blackbody>; instrument <temperature>, gain, foregrounds and calibrator emissivity were crucial controls. Agreement with a <blackbody> constrains substantial late <energy> injection or photon-production distortions.<Nobel Lecture: From the Big Bang to the Nobel Prize and beyond by John C. Mather>[FIRAS measurement and external-blackbody calibration account]{ref}

After subtracting the dominant dipole associated mainly with observer motion, anisotropies at the level of roughly $10^{-5}$ reveal primordial structure. <COBE> DMR established large-angular-scale anisotropy; multifrequency measurements and independent maps were needed to distinguish it from Galactic emission and instrumental artifacts.<Structure in the COBE Differential Microwave Radiometer First-Year Maps by George F. Smoot and others>[L1–L5, statistical and systematic checks]{ref} Approximate isotropy supports a homogeneous background model under the usual cosmological assumptions, but isotropy at one observing position is not alone a proof of spatial homogeneity everywhere.

The <temperature> <CMB angular power spectrum> has multiple acoustic peaks. Their coherent pattern follows oscillations in the pre-recombination <photon-baryon fluid>. Peak positions compare the <sound horizon> with <angular diameter distance> and hence constrain geometry together with expansion parameters. Relative peak heights test <baryon> inertia, matter–<radiation> equality and gravitational-potential evolution, motivating both baryonic and nonbaryonic matter. <Cosmic microwave background diffusion damping> suppresses small-scale power. <Cosmic microwave background polarization> and <temperature>–polarization correlations provide further tests of scattering, acoustic phase and <reionization>; <gravitational lensing> remaps the observed pattern and probes intervening structure.

By the time of this examination, the Planck 2013 <temperature> and lensing analysis supported a spatially flat six-parameter model with <cold dark matter> and a <cosmological constant>. This is a joint fit within an explicit model, not an inference that each peak uniquely measures one parameter.<Planck 2013 results. XVI. Cosmological parameters by Planck Collaboration>[Abstract and cosmological parameter/extension analysis]{ref} Foreground separation requires frequency coverage, sky masks, beam and noise characterization, and consistency across instruments. The primary microwave background has geometric and other parameter degeneracies, so acoustic-ruler, supernova and structure observations are valuable complements. **The spectrum establishes a thermal hot-universe history; anisotropy and polarization constrain its geometry, contents and <primordial perturbations>.** None of these observations by itself demonstrates a unique <cosmic inflation> mechanism.

**<mass> from motion.** A resolved <Keplerian orbit> gives $M=4\pi^2a^3/(GP_{\rm orb}^2)$ when the orbiting object has negligible <mass>. At the Galactic centre, repeated infrared <astrometry> and <spectroscopy> measure sky motion, acceleration and line-of-sight velocity of <stars> around <Sagittarius A*>. Resolving a compact enclosed <mass> and fitting complete orbits avoids reliance on a single projected velocity. Source confusion, reference-frame stability, distance, inclination and possible extended <mass> must be included in the fit.<Measuring Distance and Properties of the Milky Way's Central Supermassive Black Hole with Stellar Orbits by Andrea M. Ghez and others>[Abstract and orbital-model/systematic-error discussion]{ref}

For an unresolved stellar-mass binary in a circular orbit, the donor's velocity semiamplitude $K_2$ and <orbital period> give the <binary mass function>
$$
f(M_{\rm BH})=\frac{P_{\rm orb}K_2^3}{2\pi G}
=\frac{M_{\rm BH}^3\sin^3i}{(M_{\rm BH}+M_2)^2}.
$$
It provides a lower <mass> bound; donor <mass> and <orbital inclination> are needed for an actual <mass> estimate. For an eccentric orbit, multiply the observational combination $P_{\rm orb}K_2^3/(2\pi G)$ by $(1-e^2)^{3/2}$ when evaluating the <binary mass function>. Resolved gas or maser <rotation> and stellar-dynamical models also measure <masses>, provided <pressure> support, noncircular motion and <line-of-sight projection> are modeled. Resolving the <black-hole sphere of influence> improves separation of central <mass> from the host <galaxy>.

For active nuclei, <reverberation mapping> measures a lag $\tau$ between variable continuum and broad-line response. With a line velocity scale $\Delta v$,
$$
M_{\rm BH}=f_{\rm geom}\frac{c\tau\,\Delta v^2}{G}.
$$
The factor $f_{\rm geom}$ accounts for geometry and kinematics and is not known simply from the lag. Cadence, line responsivity, inclination and winds require control. Rapid variability demonstrates compactness but is not by itself a unique <mass> measurement.

**<black-hole spin> from strong-field emission.** Define $a_*=cJ/(GM^2)$. Large-radius Keplerian motion is principally a <mass> probe and usually insensitive to <black-hole spin>. In the Kerr model, the <radius> and <binding energy> of the <innermost stable circular orbit> vary with $a_*$ and orbital orientation, making inner-disk <radiation> useful.

<black-hole continuum fitting> infers an inner emitting <radius> from the disk's thermal spectrum and flux normalization, then identifies it with the <innermost stable circular orbit> in a relativistic thin-disk model. Independent <mass>, distance and inclination, together with atmospheric spectral hardening, <absorption> and contamination, are needed. Spectra should be selected in a disk-dominated regime where truncation and strong <advection> are limited. A 2011 Cygnus X-1 analysis combined such spectra with independently constrained binary parameters and reported a near-extreme <black-hole spin> conditional on <black-hole spin>–orbit alignment and its disk model.<The Extreme Spin of the Black Hole in Cygnus X-1 by Lijun Gou and others>[Abstract, thermal-continuum fit and dominant mass/distance/inclination/flux-calibration uncertainties]{ref}

<black-hole reflection spectroscopy> instead models a relativistically broadened iron-line and reflection continuum: orbital <Doppler shifts> and beaming broaden the profile, while <gravitational redshift> contributes to the extended red wing. Its innermost emitting <radius> can constrain <black-hole spin> if reflection extends to the stable inner edge. Ionization, abundance, <absorption>, coronal illumination, disk thickness and possible truncation are competing parameters; a broad line is not a direct <black-hole spin> reading independent of these assumptions. Timing or precession models can add information but introduce their own dynamical identification.

**<mass> is principally inferred from gravitating dynamics; <black-hole spin> is inferred from strong-field effects with a specified spacetime and <accretion> model.** Agreement between independent methods is especially valuable because disk inner <radius>, inclination and radiation-transfer assumptions can otherwise mimic a change of <black-hole spin>.
