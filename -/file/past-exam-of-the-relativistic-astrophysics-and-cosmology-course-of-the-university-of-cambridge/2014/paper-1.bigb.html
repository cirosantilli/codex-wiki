= Solution
{parent=1}

Corotation reaches the <speed of light> at $R_c=c/\Omega$. Extrapolating a dipole field gives $B_c\sim B_*(R_*/R_c)^3$ and <magnetic energy> density $\epsilon_c\sim B_c^2/\mu_0$, suppressing order-one factors. An outward <electromagnetic energy> flux of scale $\epsilon_c c$ through area of scale $R_c^2$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{P\sim\frac{B_*^2R_*^6c}{\mu_0R_c^4}
=\frac{B_*^2R_*^6\Omega^4}{\mu_0c^3}.}
$$
This assumes a dipole-dominated field tied to the rotating star and an effective outgoing <Poynting flux> near the <light cylinder>. The dipole extrapolation, angular pattern and surface-field convention affect the coefficient. A strictly aligned vacuum dipole would not radiate, whereas an oblique vacuum dipole does. A plasma-filled magnetosphere opens field lines and can <spin> down even an aligned rotator, so the light-cylinder estimate is a scaling argument, not an exact vacuum emission calculation or literal rigid motion at $c$. Force-free calculations explicitly demonstrate this distinction.<Time-dependent force-free pulsar magnetospheres: axisymmetric and oblique rotators by Anatoly Spitkovsky>[abstract and spin-down luminosity versus inclination]{ref}

The <energy> comes primarily from <stellar rotation>. With constant <moment of inertia> $I$, $E_{\rm rot}=I\Omega^2/2$, and conservation of <energy> gives $I\Omega\dot\Omega=-P$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\dot\Omega\simeq-K\Omega^3,\qquad
K=\frac{B_*^2R_*^6}{I\mu_0c^3}.}
$$
For constant $K$, differentiating gives $\ddot\Omega=-3K\Omega^2\dot\Omega$, hence $n=\Omega\ddot\Omega/\dot\Omega^2=3$. Evolving field strength, inclination, <moment of inertia> or additional <torques> invalidate the constant-$K$ prediction; generally $n=3+(\Omega/\dot\Omega)(\dot K/K)$ in this parametrization.

For radial inflow onto <mass> $M$, take $v_{\rm ff}\sim(GM/r)^{1/2}$ and $\rho\sim\dot M/(4\pi r^2v_{\rm ff})$. <magnetic pressure> decreases as $B_*^2R_*^6/(\mu_0r^6)$, while <ram pressure> scales as $\rho v_{\rm ff}^2\propto\dot M(GM)^{1/2}r^{-5/2}$. Balancing them yields
$$
\boxed{R_M\sim\left(\frac{B_*^4R_*^{12}}{\mu_0^2GM\dot M^2}\right)^{1/7}
\propto B_*^{4/7}R_*^{12/7}\dot M^{-2/7}.}
$$
Numerical factors and disc geometry are not determined by this spherical estimate. The displayed proportionality holds $M$ fixed and requires a magnetospheric boundary outside the stellar surface.

In a disc, inflowing matter near $R_M$ has specific <angular momentum> approximately $\sqrt{GMR_M}$. If it is transferred to a slower star, the material <torque> is $N\sim\dot M\sqrt{GMR_M}$ and $I\dot\Omega\sim N>0$. The <corotation radius> is $R_{\rm co}=(GM/\Omega^2)^{1/3}$, distinct from the light-cylinder radius. As stellar <spin> approaches the inner disc's Keplerian <frequency>, net spin-up weakens. If $R_M$ lies outside corotation, the field rotates faster than the disc and can centrifugally expel matter in the propeller regime. A characteristic equilibrium is
$$
\Omega_{\rm eq}\sim\sqrt{GM/R_M^3},\qquad
T_{\rm eq}\sim2\pi\sqrt{R_M^3/(GM)}
\propto B_*^{6/7}R_*^{18/7}\dot M^{-3/7}
$$
for fixed <mass> and magnetic-coupling convention. A complete <magnetic torque> model can change the coefficient; a star also cannot rotate past its <mass-shedding limit>, of order $\sqrt{GM/R_*^3}$.

For <opacity> per unit <mass> $\kappa$, an isotropic radiative force balances gravity at $L_E=4\pi GMc/\kappa$. Efficient release of surface gravitational binding <energy> gives $L_{\rm acc}\sim GM\dot M/R_*$ and therefore the <surface accretion Eddington rate>
$$
\dot M_{\max}\sim\frac{4\pi cR_*}{\kappa}\propto R_*.
$$
The radius dependence matters: it uses efficiency $\eta\sim GM/(R_*c^2)$, not an arbitrarily fixed reference efficiency. Substituting this maximal rate into the equilibrium period gives
$$
\boxed{T_{\min}\propto B_*^{6/7}R_*^{18/7}(R_*)^{-3/7}
=B_*^{6/7}R_*^{15/7}.}
$$
<mass>, <opacity> and geometry factors are held fixed. This is the classical radiatively efficient Eddington-limited scaling, not a universal upper bound on every <mass> supply rate: beaming, advection, outflows and <opacity> changes can alter it. For sufficiently small field, $R_M\lesssim R_*$ and the magnetic scaling must be replaced by surface-disc and mass-shedding physics.

= Solution
{parent=2}

The metric uses signature $(+---)$ and a time-valued interval $ds$: a comoving clock has $ds=dt$. Here $t$ is cosmic <proper time>, $R(t)$ a <scale factor> with dimensions of <length>, $c$ the <speed of light>, $\chi$ a <dimensionless> comoving radial coordinate, and $\theta,\phi$ the usual polar and azimuthal angles. With curvature normalized to $k=+1,0,-1$, $S(\chi)=\sin\chi,\chi,\sinh\chi$, respectively. Homogeneity and <isotropy> apply to each constant-$t$ spatial slice. A comoving object's fixed coordinates do not imply a constant proper separation: spatial <lengths> scale with $R$.

Radial null propagation obeys $d\chi=c\,dt/R(t)$ in magnitude. Two neighbouring wave crests between the same comoving emitter and observer have the same radial integral, giving $\delta t_0/R_0=\delta t_e/R_e$. The <wavelength> <redshift> is consequently
$$
\boxed{1+z=\frac{\lambda_0}{\lambda_e}=\frac{\delta t_0}{\delta t_e}=\frac{R_0}{R_e}.}
$$
Peculiar Doppler shifts are excluded by the comoving assumption. Define <angular diameter distance> by $d_\theta=\ell_\perp/\delta\theta$, where $\ell_\perp$ is proper transverse size at emission. The transverse metric gives $\ell_\perp=R_eS(\chi)\delta\theta$, hence
$$
\boxed{d_\theta=R_eS(\chi)=\frac{R_0S(\chi)}{1+z}.}
$$
This distance is neither the <photon> travel <time> <times> $c$ nor the object's present proper distance.

The <comoving particle horizon> at $t_1$, with the initial boundary at the <Big Bang>, is $\chi_p(t_1)=\int_0^{t_1}c\,dt/R(t)$. Its proper radial distance is $D_p=R(t_1)\chi_p$; for a power-law <scale factor> $R=At^p$, $0<p<1$,
$$
\boxed{\chi_p=\frac{c\,t_1^{1-p}}{A(1-p)},\qquad D_p=\frac{ct_1}{1-p}.}
$$
Thus a matter-only flat <Einstein-de Sitter universe>, $p=2/3$, has $D_p=3ct_1$, and the flat radiation-only case, $p=1/2$, has $D_p=2ct_1$. Strictly, Einstein-de Sitter denotes the <pressureless matter> model; the radiation calculation uses the flat zero-$\Lambda$ radiation analogue requested here.

For the source seen at <time> $t_1=t_{\rm rec}$, the radial coordinate is the integral from <recombination> to today, not the earlier horizon integral. In these flat models,
$$
\chi_s=\frac{c}{A(1-p)}(t_0^{1-p}-t_1^{1-p}),\qquad
 d_\theta=\frac{ct_1^p}{1-p}(t_0^{1-p}-t_1^{1-p}).
$$
Taking the linear horizon scale in the question to be $D_p$, its small angular extent is
$$
\boxed{\theta_p=\frac{D_p}{d_\theta}
=\frac{(t_1/t_0)^{1-p}}{1-(t_1/t_0)^{1-p}}.}
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\theta_{p,m}=\frac{(t_{\rm rec}/t_0)^{1/3}}{1-(t_{\rm rec}/t_0)^{1/3}},\qquad
\theta_{p,r}=\frac{(t_{\rm rec}/t_0)^{1/2}}{1-(t_{\rm rec}/t_0)^{1/2}}.}
$$
Using the supplied <times> gives $\theta_{p,m}=0.02684\,\mathrm{rad}=1.54^\circ$ and $\theta_{p,r}=0.004244\,\mathrm{rad}=0.243^\circ$. There is a genuine radius/diameter convention to state: the supplied $3ct_1$ is a radial horizon distance. If angular diameter <means> the full diameter of a spherical horizon patch of radius $D_p$, the two answers are instead **$2\theta_{p,m}=3.08^\circ$ and $2\theta_{p,r}=0.486^\circ$**. Both conventions follow from the same distance calculation; a factor of two should not be hidden in the word horizon.

Matter-only evolution is the better of these two approximations because the universe is already matter dominated around <recombination> and evolves mostly through a subsequent matter era, rather than remaining radiation dominated to the present. Neither is exact: pre-recombination radiation changes the <particle horizon>, and late-time <dark energy> changes the distance to the last-scattering surface. No supplied ages have been replaced by later best-fit values.

The <cosmic microwave background> originates near last scattering and samples many horizon-sized regions. In an always-decelerating hot-Big-Bang model, widely separated last-scattering patches lack overlapping early causal histories, motivating the <horizon problem>. The measured acoustic angular scale uses the smaller <sound horizon>, not automatically this light-travel <particle horizon>; baryon-photon pressure changes the propagation speed. Inflation supplies an earlier accelerated expansion during which the <comoving Hubble radius> shrinks, allowing a patch with a common earlier causal history to expand to the size of the observable universe. Quantum perturbations can then seed later acoustic and density fluctuations. This does not require local <signals> to propagate faster than light, and the <Hubble radius> must not be equated indiscriminately with the <particle horizon>. CMB <temperature> and lensing spectra test the resulting perturbation and expansion model.<Planck 2013 results. XVI. Cosmological parameters by Planck Collaboration>[abstract; temperature/lensing spectra and the spatially flat standard cosmological model]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=a}

An <active galactic nucleus> is powered by <accretion> onto a central <supermassive black hole>. Binding-energy release gives $L\sim\eta\dot Mc^2$, so a compact source can outshine its host without converting its entire <mass> into radiation. A disc produces a thermal continuum, a hot corona can Comptonize disc <photons> into X-rays, and surrounding gas produces broad and narrow <emission lines>. Dust can obscure and reradiate the central emission. Orientation explains part of the diversity between unobscured and obscured nuclei, but <accretion> state and intrinsic variability also matter.

<relativistic jets> channel <energy> and <momentum> into collimated magnetized outflows. Disc <rotation> and, in some models, black-hole <rotation> supply the <energy>; magnetic stresses accelerate the plasma and connect the engine to the outflow. <synchrotron radiation> <traces> relativistic <electrons> and <magnetic fields>, while <inverse Compton scattering> can produce higher-energy emission. Bright knots, terminal hotspots and large radio lobes show that the deposited <energy> is not confined to the unresolved nucleus.

For bulk speed $\beta c$ at viewing angle $i$, the <Doppler factor> is $\mathcal D=[\Gamma(1-\beta\cos i)]^{-1}$, and an approaching feature has
$$
\boxed{\beta_{\rm app}=\frac{\beta\sin i}{1-\beta\cos i}.}
$$
Light-travel delay can therefore give <apparent superluminal motion> without any material or <signal> travelling locally faster than light. Beaming also makes approaching jets appear brighter and more rapidly variable; a one-sided image is not proof of a physically one-sided outflow. Spectral, <polarization> and resolved-motion measurements constrain the geometry and emission mechanisms. Jets can inflate cavities and heat surrounding gas, linking <black-hole accretion> to galaxy and cluster feedback.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Write the <dimensionless> <scale factor> as $a=R/R_0$ and the <density contrast> as $\delta=(\rho-\bar\rho)/\bar\rho$. Gravity amplifies overdensities while expansion and pressure oppose growth. In a sub-Hubble Newtonian single-fluid approximation, a comoving Fourier mode obeys
$$
\ddot\delta_k+2H\dot\delta_k+
\left(\frac{c_s^2k^2}{a^2}-4\pi G\bar\rho\right)\delta_k=0.
$$
For <pressureless matter> in an Einstein-de Sitter background, the growing solution is $\delta\propto t^{2/3}\propto a$ and the decaying solution is $t^{-1}$. Jeans support suppresses growth when the pressure term dominates. The equation is not a substitute for relativistic perturbation theory on horizon-sized scales.

<cold dark matter> clusters without baryonic pressure support. Sub-horizon growth is much weaker during <radiation domination>, and <baryons> are initially tied to the <photon> fluid, producing acoustic oscillations rather than unrestricted collapse. Horizon entry, radiation-matter equality, acoustic evolution and <diffusion> therefore give a scale-dependent <transfer function>. The primordial curvature spectrum and the later matter-density spectrum are not the same function: evolution and the gravitational constraints relate them. Late dark-energy domination reduces the growth rate.

When $|\delta|$ approaches unity, linear theory fails. Collapse, <virialization>, mergers and tidal interactions form a hierarchy of haloes. Gas must cool and lose <angular momentum> to make dense stellar systems; feedback then changes its supply and <distribution>. Simulations combine collisionless gravitational dynamics with increasingly complex gas physics rather than extending the linear growing mode indefinitely. Galaxy clustering measures biased tracers of matter; <gravitational lensing> and CMB constraints provide complementary tests. Approximately adiabatic, nearly scale-invariant initial perturbations explain the coherent acoustic pattern and supply seeds for this later growth.<Planck 2013 results. XVI. Cosmological parameters by Planck Collaboration>[abstract and standard-model temperature/lensing analysis]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Without feedback, efficiently cooling gas would turn into stars too readily. Young stars return radiation, winds and <supernova> <energy> and <momentum> to their surroundings. This can heat gas, disrupt dense clouds and drive outflows, with the strongest fractional effect in shallow potential wells. The outflow <mass-loading factor> $\eta_w=\dot M_{\rm out}/\dot M_*$ is useful, but its dependence on escape speed differs between energy-limited and momentum-limited models. Some expelled gas later recycles; feedback also transports metals and changes subsequent cooling.

<active-galactic-nucleus feedback> is important in massive galaxies and hot haloes. Radiative or mechanical power from <black-hole accretion> can suppress the supply of cold gas. Collimated jets inflate radio/X-ray cavities, while broader winds can carry significant <momentum> and kinetic <energy>. For a relativistic cavity fluid with adiabatic index $4/3$, its <enthalpy> is approximately $4pV$, so $4pV/t_{\rm age}$ estimates mechanical power. Comparing it with the gas cooling luminosity tests whether that <energy> budget is sufficient, rather than merely showing that a radio source is present.

Deep Chandra observations of Perseus resolved bubbles, a density jump and weak surface-brightness ripples consistent with repeated mechanical disturbance of the intracluster gas. Their interpretation as heating requires a dissipation mechanism and a coupling history, not just a large instantaneous power.<A deep Chandra observation of the Perseus cluster: shocks and ripples by A. C. Fabian and others>[abstract; density front, radio bubbles and the proposed heating/cooling balance]{ref}

Observed outflow geometry, filling factors, cavity ages and cooling corrections create substantial uncertainties. Feedback can also compress gas and locally promote <star formation>. Simulations must track radiative losses and the scale on which <energy> or <momentum> couples: depositing unresolved thermal <energy> is not automatically equivalent to driving a resolved wind. A successful model should explain stellar <masses>, gas fractions, <metallicity> and circumgalactic or intracluster thermodynamics together.

= Solution
{parent=d}

The <weak equivalence principle> is universality of <free fall> for sufficiently small test bodies, independent of composition, when non-gravitational forces and self-gravity are negligible. Equality of inertial and passive gravitational <mass> makes this possible in the Newtonian limit. The <Einstein equivalence principle> adds <local Lorentz invariance> and <local position invariance> for nongravitational experiments: a small freely falling laboratory has the local physics of <special relativity>.

This motivates a universal <spacetime metric>. Freely falling unforced test particles follow <geodesics>, $u^\nu\nabla_\nu u^\mu=0$. At a point one can choose local inertial coordinates with vanishing connection, but curvature and tidal relative acceleration remain measurable over a finite region. Gravity cannot be transformed away globally. A uniformly accelerating small laboratory and a local gravitational field can have the same nongravitational experimental behaviour without having identical global geometry.

Clock comparisons predict a fractional rate difference of order $g\Delta h/c^2$ in a weak static field. The <Pound-Rebka experiment> tested the associated <photon> <redshift> through resonant gamma-ray emission and absorption at different heights, using controlled Doppler shifts to match the tiny <energy> displacement. Such a result tests the metric <redshift> prediction, not uniquely every component of the gravitational field equations.<Apparent Weight of Photons by Robert Vivian Pound and Glen Rebka>[1960 laboratory gravitational-redshift measurement]{ref}

<general relativity> adds the dynamical <Einstein field equations>,
$$
G_{\mu\nu}+\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}=\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}T_{\mu\nu}.
$$
The <equivalence principle> alone does not derive this unique equation; assumptions about covariance, dynamics and the Newtonian limit are also needed. The <strong equivalence principle> extends the universality claim to self-gravitating systems and gravitational experiments. Alternative metric theories can satisfy the weaker local principles while differing in gravitational dynamics, so composition tests, clock/<redshift> tests and self-gravity tests probe distinct claims.

= Solution
{parent=e}

<core-collapse supernovae> follow loss of pressure support in the evolved core of a massive star. <electron> capture and nuclear dissociation contribute to collapse; formation of a hot compact object launches a <shock> that can stall as dissociation and <neutrino> losses drain it. <neutrino> heating and multidimensional fluid motions can revive the <shock>, but some collapses fail to produce a normal bright explosion. The remnant may be a <neutron star> or <black hole>. The <neutrino> burst detected from <SN 1987A> provides direct evidence for the core-collapse <energy> channel: the water-Cherenkov <signal> is a particle-interaction measurement, requiring detector-response and accidental-background checks rather than an optical light-curve inference.<Observation of a Neutrino Burst from the Supernova SN1987A by K. Hirata and others>[pp.1490–1493, burst observation and detector interpretation]{ref}

<Type Ia supernovae> instead involve thermonuclear destruction of a degenerate <carbon-oxygen white dwarf>. They leave expanding ejecta without an ordinary surviving neutron-star core. The ignition channel need not have a unique total <mass> or binary history. Radioactive nickel/cobalt decay powers much of the later luminosity, and empirical light-curve corrections make these events useful distance indicators. Spectral type is not identical to explosion mechanism: hydrogen-rich Type II events are core collapse, but stripped-envelope Ib/c events can also be core collapse despite lacking conspicuous <hydrogen>.

The ejecta first expand nearly freely while swept-up <mass> is small. A strong <shock> heats ambient gas, and once swept-up material dominates, an approximately adiabatic remnant approaches the Sedov-Taylor scaling
$$
\boxed{R_s\propto\left(\frac{Et^2}{\rho_0}\right)^{1/5}.}
$$
Subsequent <radiative cooling> makes a dense shell and ends that energy-conserving approximation. Thermal X-ray lines probe shocked gas and nucleosynthetic ejecta; radio and nonthermal X-rays <trace> <relativistic particles> and <magnetic fields>. <shock> acceleration makes remnants plausible contributors to Galactic <cosmic rays>. Expansion, ambient-density structure, cooling and cosmic-ray <energy> losses complicate <energy> estimates; a bright synchrotron shell alone is not proof of a specific accelerated-particle composition. Supernovae return metals and mechanical <energy> to the <interstellar medium> and therefore connect stellar death to the feedback cycle.
