= Solution
{parent=1}

Take overdots in this question to mean differentiation with respect to the orbiting particle's <proper time> $\tau$. Write $\mu=GM/c^2$, $x=r/\mu$ and $\ell=hc/(GM)$, with $h$ the <specific angular momentum>. Then the dimensionless timelike effective potential is
$$
u(x)=\frac{U}{c^2}=-\frac1x+\frac{\ell^2}{2x^2}-\frac{\ell^2}{x^3}.
$$
The last term is the relativistic correction to the Newtonian centrifugal-plus-gravitational potential. At large $r$ the two agree. Newtonian <angular momentum> gives an infinite inner <centrifugal barrier> for nonzero $h$; the relativistic correction lowers that barrier and permits plunge. The exterior problem starts at $r=2\mu$, where $U=-c^2/2$; extrapolating the potential towards $r=0$ is not a static exterior description.

Circular orbits obey
$$
U'=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad GMr^2-h^2r+3\mu h^2=0,
\qquad h^2=\frac{GMr^2}{r-3\mu}.
$$
There are two extrema when $h^2>12(GM/c)^2$: an inner maximum representing an unstable circular orbit and an outer minimum representing a stable one. At equality they merge at $r=6\mu$; below it neither exists. More explicitly, evaluate the <curvature> at a circular orbit:
$$
\left.U''\right|_{\rm circ}=\frac{GM(r-6\mu)}{r^3(r-3\mu)}.
$$
Timelike circular orbits require $r>3\mu$. They are radially stable for $r>6\mu$, so **the marginally stable inner limit is $r_{\rm ISCO}=6GM/c^2$**. The Newtonian potential instead has one stable minimum at $r=h^2/(GM)$, without an <innermost stable circular orbit>.

\Image[paper-1-effective-potential.png]
{title=Schwarzschild and Newtonian potentials}

For the <Schwarzschild circular-orbit local speed> in the question, $\gamma$ is the <Lorentz factor> relative to a local <Schwarzschild static observer>, not $dt/d\tau$. That observer's clock obeys $dT=\sqrt{1-2\mu/r}\,dt$. Since the particle passes with local speed $v$, $r\,d\phi/d\tau=\gamma v$. Circular normalization gives
$$
\frac{(r\dot\phi)^2}{c^2}=\frac{\mu}{r-3\mu},\qquad
\gamma^2=1+\frac{(r\dot\phi)^2}{c^2}=\frac{r-2\mu}{r-3\mu},
\qquad \frac{v^2}{c^2}=\frac{\mu}{r-2\mu}.
$$
Thus **$v=c/2$ at the <innermost stable circular orbit>**. Using <Schwarzschild time> in place of the local clock would give a different speed.

For the conserved particle <energy> measured at infinity, substitute $h^2$ in the circular <energy> equation. Setting $y=\mu/r$ gives
$$
k^2=1-2y+\frac{y(1-2y)}{1-3y}
=\frac{(1-2y)^2}{1-3y},
\qquad\boxed{E=mc^2\frac{1-2\mu/r}{\sqrt{1-3\mu/r}}}.
$$
Take the positive root for a future-directed exterior orbit. It approaches $mc^2-GMm/(2r)$ at large radius. The <marginally bound circular Schwarzschild orbit> solves $k=1$, giving $y(4y-1)=0$ and hence **$r_{\rm mb}=4GM/c^2$** at finite radius. It is unstable; strictly bound circular orbits have $r>4\mu$, with $E<mc^2$. The phrase smallest bound orbit here denotes this marginal circular limit, not a prohibition on a bound-energy trajectory plunging below it.

At the <innermost stable circular orbit>,
$$
\boxed{E_{\rm ISCO}=\sqrt{\frac89}\,mc^2\simeq0.94281mc^2},\qquad
\eta=1-\sqrt{\frac89}\simeq\boxed{0.0572}.
$$
A radiatively efficient <thin disc> with a <zero-torque inner boundary condition> can therefore release about 5.7% of the accreted <rest energy> before the plunge. <energy> advection, stress at the inner edge and <photon> capture can alter the escaping <luminosity>, so this is a model efficiency rather than a universal emitted fraction.

For the <Kerr ISCO spin limits> of an equatorial prograde disc around a rapidly rotating <Kerr black hole>, the <innermost stable circular orbit> moves inward and its specific <energy> decreases. In the ideal extremal limit its Boyer-Lindquist radius tends to $\mu$, $E/(mc^2)\to1/\sqrt3$, and the corresponding binding-energy efficiency tends to 42.3%. Retrograde rotation has the opposite trend: the extremal limit is $r_{\rm ISCO}=9\mu$, $E/(mc^2)=5/(3\sqrt3)$, giving about 3.8%. The prograde limiting coordinate radius does not identify a massive orbit with the null horizon generator.<Rotating Black Holes: Locally Nonrotating Frames, Energy Extraction, and Scalar Synchrotron Radiation by James M. Bardeen and others>[§II, Eqs. 2.20–2.23 and Fig. 1, pp. 351–353]{ref}

Finally $\dot t=k/(1-2\mu/r)=1/\sqrt{1-3\mu/r}$. Dividing $\dot\phi=h/r^2$ by this gives
$$
\boxed{\Omega_\infty=\frac{d\phi}{dt}=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r^3}}}.
$$
Its algebraic form is exactly Newtonian, but $r$ is the <Schwarzschild spacetime> <areal radius> and $t$ <Schwarzschild time>, normalized at infinity. Local clock rates, orbit stability and <binding energy> remain relativistic. With $GM_\odot\simeq1.3271\times10^{20}\,\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}$,
$$
\boxed{f_{\rm ISCO,\infty}=\frac{c^3}{2\pi6^{3/2}G(10M_\odot)}\simeq0.220\,\mathrm{kHz}},
\qquad\boxed{\frac{f_{\rm ISCO}}{f_{\rm mb}}=\left(\frac46\right)^{3/2}\simeq0.544}.
$$
The marginally bound circular-orbit <frequency> is about $0.404\,\mathrm{kHz}$. These <Schwarzschild ISCO orbital frequencies> and marginally bound <frequencies> are rotation <frequencies> per distant stationary observer's time, as intended by using $d\phi/dt$. A static observer at finite radius $r_o$ instead divides both <frequencies> by $\sqrt{1-2\mu/r_o}$, leaving their ratio unchanged. Identifying an observed <quasi-periodic oscillation> with the <innermost stable circular orbit> orbital <frequency> is an additional astrophysical assumption.

Here $R(t)$ is the cosmological <scale factor (cosmology)>, $t$ is <cosmic time> and $H=\dot R/R$ is the <Hubble parameter>. With $R$ a length, $k=+1,0,-1$ labels positively curved, flat and negatively curved spatial sections. This $k$ is unrelated to Question 1's specific orbital <energy>. The density $\rho$ is mass-equivalent <energy density>: $\rho c^2$ has energy-per-volume units. In the displayed <Friedmann equation> $\Lambda$ has units $\mathrm{s^{-2}}$; the geometric <cosmological constant> is $\Lambda_{\rm geom}=\Lambda/c^2$. For a separately conserved fluid of constant <equation of state> $p=w\rho c^2$, the <cosmological perfect-fluid continuity equation> gives $\rho\propto R^{-3(1+w)}$, so $\epsilon=3w$: zero for <pressureless matter> and one for radiation. With several fluids, sum their densities in the expansion equation and evolve each separately.

Setting <cosmological spatial curvature> and the <cosmological constant> to zero gives the <mass-equivalent cosmological critical density>,
$$
\boxed{\rho_{\rm crit}(t)=\frac{3H(t)^2}{8\pi G}}.
$$
Its energy-density version is $\rho_{\rm crit}c^2$; keeping this distinction is necessary when dividing a <radiation energy density> by $c^2$ below.

In an <Einstein-de Sitter universe>, $H^2\propto R^{-3}$ integrates to $R\propto t^{2/3}$ and $H=2/(3t)$. With $1+z=R_0/R$,
$$
t(z)=\frac{2}{3H_0}(1+z)^{-3/2},\qquad
\boxed{t_2-t_1=\frac{2}{3H_0}\left[(1+z_2)^{-3/2}-(1+z_1)^{-3/2}\right]}.
$$
Here $z_1>z_2$ are the two epoch redshifts relative to today. This is elapsed <cosmic time>, not <comoving distance> divided by $c$.

For <collisionless preservation of a Planck spectrum>, use <frequency> redshift as well as photon-number conservation. Let $f=R/R_i$, so a <frequency> bin $d\nu$ now corresponds to $d\nu_i=f\,d\nu$ and $\nu_i=f\nu$ initially. Collisionless propagation conserves <photon> number in the corresponding comoving bin:
$$
R^3n_\nu(t)\,d\nu=R_i^3n_{f\nu}(t_i)\,f\,d\nu,
\qquad n_\nu(t)=f^{-2}n_{f\nu}(t_i).
$$
For an initial <blackbody spectrum>, insert its <Planck distribution> to obtain
$$
n_\nu(t)=\frac{8\pi\nu^2}{c^3\left[\exp\{h\nu/(k_B T_i/f)\}-1\right]},
\qquad\boxed{T_{\rm rad}=T_i/f=T_0(1+z)}.
$$
The factors $f^2$ from the numerator cancel the spectral dilution factor, while the exponent changes only the <temperature>. Integrating gives <photon number density> proportional to $T^3\propto R^{-3}$ and <radiation energy density> proportional to $T^4\propto R^{-4}$. Conservation of total <photon> number alone would not guarantee a <blackbody spectrum> shape; the frequency-bin mapping and absence of injection or spectral redistribution are essential.

For the numerical density comparisons, the supplied <Hubble constant> is $H_0=2.33312\times10^{-18}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Taking $G=6.67430\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}$ and $c=2.99792458\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ gives
$$
\rho_{\rm crit,0}=9.7353\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}},\quad
\rho_{\gamma0}=\frac{aT_0^4}{c^2}=4.6760\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}},\quad
\Omega_{\gamma0}=4.8032\times10^{-5}.
$$
The radiation specified here is <cosmic microwave background> <photons> only. Adding a relic <neutrino> density would change the <photon-only matter-radiation equality> result. The rounded present matter and vacuum fractions sum to one without this small <photon> contribution; the requested pairwise density comparisons do not require silently renormalizing them.

For the final unlabelled flight-time request, insert the case-(c) value $1+z_c=(0.7/\Omega_{\gamma0})^{1/4}=10.98735$ into the stipulated Einstein-de Sitter formula:
$$
\Delta t=\frac{2}{3H_0}\left[10.98735^{-3/2}-1101^{-3/2}\right]
=7.838\times10^{15}\,\mathrm s\simeq\boxed{2.48\times10^8\,\mathrm{yr}}.
$$
The two model ages are approximately $2.486\times10^8$ and $2.478\times10^5$ years. This uses exactly the instructed matter-only model with the same $H_0$. It is not an exact age difference in the matter-plus-vacuum cosmology; a high-redshift matter-era normalization using $H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{m0}}$ would be a different approximation.

Density parameters at the same epoch share a common critical-density denominator. Thus compare the densities directly: $\rho_m=\rho_{m0}(1+z)^3$ and $\rho_\gamma=\rho_{\gamma0}(1+z)^4$. Equality gives
$$
\boxed{1+z_{m\gamma}=\frac{\Omega_{m0}}{\Omega_{\gamma0}}=6245.89,\qquad z_{m\gamma}\simeq6244.89\simeq6.24\times10^3}.
$$
This is the <photon-only matter-radiation equality> appropriate to the specified radiation density, not the equality including all relativistic species.

Vacuum density is constant for a true <cosmological constant>. Hence $0.3(1+z)^3=0.7$ gives
$$
\boxed{z_{m\Lambda}=\left(\frac{0.7}{0.3}\right)^{1/3}-1\simeq0.32635}.

Comparing <photons> with the constant vacuum density gives $\Omega_{\gamma0}(1+z)^4=0.7$, so
$$
\boxed{z_{\gamma\Lambda}=\left(\frac{0.7}{4.8032\times10^{-5}}\right)^{1/4}-1\simeq9.98735}.

A donor star can feed a compact companion through <Roche-lobe overflow> near the inner <Lagrange point>, or through capture of a <stellar wind>. The transferred <gas> usually carries <angular momentum>; when its circularization radius exceeds the accretor's effective inner radius it forms an <accretion disc>. Outward <angular momentum transport> permits inward <mass> flow, while dissipation converts gravitational <binding energy> into heat and radiation. For a compact object with a surface $L\sim GM\dot M/R$; for a <black hole> write $L=\eta\dot Mc^2$, with efficiency determined by the flow and inner boundary. Optically thick disc regions emit a sum of thermal <radiation spectrum> components; a hot optically thin <accretion corona> can inverse-Compton scatter softer <photons> into a harder <X-ray> component. A <black hole> has no material surface on which to radiate a stellar boundary layer.

An explicit example is the <wind-fed black-hole binary> <Cygnus X-1>. Its massive stellar companion supplies <gas>, and the <accretion> flow produces strong <X-ray> emission.<Wind Accretion and State Transitions in Cygnus X-1 by D. R. Gies and others>[abstract, H-alpha wind monitoring and X-ray-state comparison]{ref} Compact-object evidence comes from orbital dynamics rather than <X-ray> brightness alone. The single-lined <binary mass function> constrains the unseen <mass>; inclination, donor properties and <distance> refine it. <Jerome A. Orosz> and collaborators combined optical photometry, <radial velocities>, rotation and a <distance> constraint to obtain a black-hole <mass> of $14.8\pm1.0M_\odot$ in their 2011 analysis. The inference depends on the stellar and orbital model, and that historical value should not be confused with a latest measurement.<The Mass of the Black Hole in Cygnus X-1 by Jerome A. Orosz and others>[abstract; dynamical modelling and observational constraints]{ref}

**Binary <accretion> converts orbital <mass> transfer into compact gravitational-energy release; a dynamical <mass> measurement identifies the accretor more securely than <luminosity> alone.**

= Solution

A positive <cosmological constant> contributes a constant <vacuum energy> density and negative pressure. In the time-unit convention of Question 2,
$$
\rho_\Lambda=\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi G},\qquad p_\Lambda=-\rho_\Lambda c^2,\qquad
\frac{\ddot R}{R}=-\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left(\rho+\frac{3p}{c^2}\right)+\frac{\Lambda}{3}.
$$
It therefore causes <accelerating expansion> once its negative-pressure effect outweighs the decelerating components. Its density stays constant while matter dilutes, so a small early contribution can dominate late. For an isolated positive-vacuum component the <scale factor (cosmology)> grows exponentially. The <cosmological constant> problem is the mismatch between the small inferred vacuum density and generic quantum-field vacuum-energy expectations, rather than a failure of the background solution itself.

The key direct expansion evidence came from <Type Ia supernova> distance-redshift measurements. After light-curve and colour standardization, distant events were fainter than expected in simple decelerating matter-only models, indicating larger <luminosity distances>. Riess and collaborators' 1998 analysis and Perlmutter and collaborators' independent 42-supernova study favoured an accelerating low-matter-density cosmology with a positive vacuum contribution. Dust, source evolution, selection effects, <photometric calibration> and <peculiar velocities> must be controlled: faintness alone is not a model-independent measurement of $\Lambda$.<Observational Evidence from Supernovae for an Accelerating Universe and a Cosmological Constant by Adam Riess and others>[abstract; distance fitting and systematic-error discussion]{ref}<Measurements of Omega and Lambda from 42 High-Redshift Supernovae by Saul Perlmutter and others>[abstract; cosmological likelihoods and systematic checks]{ref}

<cosmic microwave background> acoustic geometry and independent matter-density constraints help break the supernova parameter degeneracy: nearly flat geometry with substantially less than critical matter requires another component within the FLRW model. <large-scale structure of the universe> and <distance> measurements provide further consistency tests. **The combined evidence supports a dark-energy contribution compatible with a constant; <accelerating expansion> by itself does not prove exactly constant <vacuum energy> or rule out every alternative gravity model.**

A <quasar> is a luminous <active galactic nucleus> powered by <gas> <accretion> onto a <supermassive black hole>. The hot disc produces much of the optical/ultraviolet continuum; surrounding <gas> gives broad and narrow <emission lines>, while some systems have <relativistic jets>. A small emitting region is inferred from rapid variability, $R\lesssim c\Delta t_{\rm obs}/(1+z)$ when beaming is absent. Sustaining a high <luminosity> from such a compact region favours <accretion> over ordinary stellar power. For approximately spherical, ionized hydrogen with electron-scattering opacity,
$$
L_E=\frac{4\pi GMm_pc}{\sigma_T}\simeq1.26\times10^{31}\frac{M}{M_\odot}\,\mathrm W.
$$
Comparing <luminosity> with this Eddington scale constrains <mass> only after assumptions about anisotropy, opacity and <accretion> state. The emitted power obeys $L=\eta\dot Mc^2$; high binding-energy efficiency makes growth of a massive compact accretor energetically plausible.

Broad-line <reverberation mapping> estimates a <gas> radius from a continuum-to-line time lag, $R\sim c\tau$, and combines it with line velocity width to infer $M\sim f_{\rm vir}Rv^2/G$. Geometry, inclination and nongravitational <gas> motions enter the factor $f_{\rm vir}$. Independent evidence for compact massive nuclei comes from resolved stellar or <gas> orbits in nearby galaxies. <Andrea M. Ghez> and collaborators' Galactic-centre stellar-orbit study fitted a common central potential and <distance>, demonstrating a compact dark <mass> of order four million <solar masses>; the Galactic centre is a low-luminosity example rather than a <quasar>.<Measuring Distance and Properties of the Milky Way's Central Supermassive Black Hole with Stellar Orbits by Andrea M. Ghez and others>[abstract; joint potential/distance fits and systematic-error analysis]{ref}

**<quasars> are an <accretion> phase of massive nuclear <black holes>; <luminosity>, variability and dynamical evidence constrain different aspects of that interpretation.** A finite-resolution dark-mass measurement by itself is not a direct image of an <event horizon>.

The <weak equivalence principle> says that freely falling test bodies with identical initial conditions follow the same trajectory independently of composition, when nongravitational forces and appreciable self-gravity are negligible. In Newtonian language it implies universal gravitational-to-inertial <mass> ratio. The <Einstein equivalence principle> additionally requires <local Lorentz invariance> and <local position invariance> of nongravitational experiments: a sufficiently small freely falling laboratory realizes <special relativity>. The <strong equivalence principle> extends universality to gravitational experiments and self-gravitating bodies.

A falling elevator makes the local content clear. Uniform gravitational acceleration can be removed along a freely falling <worldline> by a suitable frame, but <curvature> cannot generally be removed over an extended region. Nearby <geodesics> have relative acceleration
$$
\frac{D^2\xi^\mu}{D\tau^2}=-R^\mu{}_{\alpha\nu\beta}u^\alpha\xi^\nu u^\beta
$$
for the displayed Riemann-sign convention. <tidal forces> therefore remain detectable even in free fall. Equivalence motivates a metric description and gravitational clock shifts, but does not uniquely select Einstein's field equations.

Tests compare accelerations of different materials, <frequencies> of clocks at different gravitational potentials, or the response of self-gravitating bodies. <lunar laser ranging> times pulses reflected by lunar <retroreflectors> and fits Earth-Moon motion in the Sun's field; a differential free-fall violation would add a characteristic periodic range perturbation. The fit must also model tides, station and reflector geometry and propagation delays, with solar-radiation pressure and reflector thermal effects checked as possible mimics. Williams and collaborators' 2012 analysis is a concrete example of this method, published after this examination.<Lunar Laser Ranging Tests of the Equivalence Principle by James G. Williams and others>[§§2–4, pulse ranging, orbital fits and solar-radiation/thermal corrections]{ref}

**Equivalence is a local universality statement; tidal <curvature> and the detailed gravitational field equations are additional physics.**

A <galaxy cluster> deflects light from background galaxies according to its projected gravitational field. In a thin gravitational lens approximation,
$$
\boldsymbol\beta=\boldsymbol\theta-\boldsymbol\alpha(\boldsymbol\theta),\qquad
\kappa=\frac{\Sigma}{\Sigma_{\rm crit}},\qquad
\Sigma_{\rm crit}=\frac{c^2D_s}{4\pi GD_lD_{ls}}.
$$
The <distances> are angular-diameter <distances>; only <mass> in the appropriate foreground/background geometry lenses the chosen source this way. Near critical curves, <strong gravitational lensing> produces highly magnified arcs and multiple images of the same source. Farther out, <weak gravitational lensing> produces small coherent shear distortions, inferred statistically because individual galaxies have unknown intrinsic ellipticities. The measured <reduced shear> is $g=\gamma/(1-\kappa)$. Transparent lensing obeys <surface-brightness conservation in gravitational lensing> at fixed source redshift while changing solid angle and integrated flux.

Strong-lensing image positions and source redshifts constrain the central projected <mass>; weak shear extends the map to larger radii. Multiple source planes help constrain geometry and degeneracies, but the <mass-sheet degeneracy> illustrates why image shapes alone do not uniquely fix absolute <mass>. Point-spread-function anisotropy, selection, shape calibration, cluster-member contamination, source redshift errors and unrelated line-of-sight structure require controls. Lensing does not require hydrostatic equilibrium, making it valuable for disturbed clusters, although interpretation still depends on the gravitational model.

An instructive observation is the merging <Bullet Cluster>: weak-lensing <mass> concentrations approximately follow the collisionless galaxies while the dominant visible baryonic <plasma>, traced by <X-rays>, is displaced. Clowe and collaborators combined ground-based and Hubble images for lensing reconstruction and compared them with the <gas> distribution, providing evidence for <mass> beyond the observed <baryons>.<A direct empirical proof of the existence of dark matter by Douglas Clowe and others>[abstract and mass-map/gas comparison]{ref}

**Cluster lensing maps total projected gravitating <mass> and magnifies background sources; it does not by itself identify the microscopic dark-matter particle.**
