Use independent choices with constant <probabilities> $p$ and $q=1-p$. Completing the entire reverse circuit while the forward-moving object never moves requires the first $F$ choices all to be reverse moves. Thus
$$
\boxed{P=q^F=(1-p)^F.}

{parent=12}

= Solution
{parent=b}

After $j$ choices, let $C_j$ be the number of forward moves. The two unwrapped positions are $C_j$ and $F-(j-C_j)$, whose gap is $F-j$. It follows that the first subsequent meeting occurs exactly at step $F$, regardless of the order of the choices. Its <position> is the number of forward moves in that block.

That count has a <binomial distribution> with parameters $F,p$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{p_n^{\rm meet}=\binom Fn p^nq^{F-n},\qquad 0\leq n\leq F.}
$$
The two endpoint labels $0$ and $F$ represent the same physical seat reached by different complete circuits; the unwrapped convention distinguishes their <probabilities>.

= Solution

For the forward-moving object to have been waiting at the meeting <position>, the last move must be a reverse move. The preceding $F-1$ choices must then contain exactly $n$ forward moves and $F-n-1$ reverse moves. Choosing their order gives
$$
\boxed{p_n^{\rm specialMeet}=\binom{F-1}{n}p^nq^{F-n},\qquad 0\leq n\leq F-1.}
$$
At $n=F$ the <probability> is **zero**, since the reverse-moving object was already at that unwrapped <position> initially. At $n=0$ the displayed formula is $q^F$, consistent with the forward object waiting at its initial <position> throughout. Equivalently, use the binomial convention $\binom{F-1}{F}=0$ to include this endpoint in the same formula without a negative power of $q$.

= Solution

Let $N$ be the meeting label. If $N>n$, the forward-moving object has already crossed $n$ while the reverse-moving object has not yet reached it, so the forward object wins the race to $n$. If $N<n$, the reverse object passed $n$ first. If $N=n$, the required event is exactly the special meeting from part (d).

These disjoint possibilities give
$$
\boxed{P(\text{forward object reaches }n\text{ first})
=p_n^{\rm specialMeet}+\sum_{k=n+1}^{F}p_k^{\rm meet}.}
$$
This includes the endpoints: for $n=0$ the <probability> is $1$, and for $n=F$ it is $0$, with the initial positions understood in the unwrapped convention.

= Solution
{parent=f}

First take $1\leq n\leq F-1$. The forward object reaches $n$ at its $n$th move, while the reverse object reaches that label at its $(F-n)$th move. If the forward object wins after $k$ reverse moves, then $0\leq k\leq F-n-1$. The final choice is forward; among the preceding $n+k-1$ choices, exactly $k$ are reverse. By the <negative binomial stopping argument>, this case has <probability> $\binom{n-1+k}{k}p^nq^k$.

Sum these mutually exclusive cases and equate the result to part (e), using parts (b) and (d). This proves the <success-failure stopping identity> here:
$$
\sum_{k=0}^{F-n-1}\binom{n-1+k}{k}p^nq^k
=\binom{F-1}{n}p^nq^{F-n}
+\sum_{k=n+1}^{F}\binom Fk p^kq^{F-k}.
$$
The right side splits the same race into a special meeting at $n$ and all meetings beyond $n$; the left side splits it according to the number of reverse moves at the winning time.

At $n=F$, both sides are zero with an <empty sum> and $\binom{F-1}{F}=0$. At $n=0$, the event has <probability> one; the right side equals $\sum_{k=0}^{F}\binom Fk p^kq^{F-k}=1$. The printed left side includes $\binom{-1}{0}$ and is outside the usual nonnegative-upper-index combinatorial definition. With the explicit generalized convention **$\binom{-1}{0}=1$**, its $k=0$ term is one and all $k\geq1$ terms vanish, so the identity also holds at that endpoint. Without this convention the displayed sum is undefined there; the zero-target race must instead be stated separately. All identities extend to $p=0,1$ by their <polynomial> forms.

$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}

\lambda_-&=3-\sqrt{15},&v_-&=(-5-\sqrt{15},\ 2+\sqrt{15},\ 1)^T,\\
\lambda_+&=3+\sqrt{15},&v_+&=(-5+\sqrt{15},\ 2-\sqrt{15},\ 1)^T.

Every nonzero <scalar> multiple is another valid <eigenvector> in the corresponding one-dimensional <eigenspace>.

Put $d=b\cdot x$ and $B=|b|^2$. Taking the <dot product> of the original equation with $b$ eliminates $b\times x$ and gives $d=a\cdot b-Bd$. Thus
$$
\boxed{b\cdot x=\frac{a\cdot b}{1+|b|^2}.}
$$
Next take its <cross product> with $b$. The term $d b$ disappears, and the <vector triple product identity> gives
$$
b\times x=b\times a-b\times(b\times x)
=b\times a-bd+Bx.
$$
Insert this expression back into $x=a-db-b\times x$. The two longitudinal terms cancel, leaving $(1+B)x=a-b\times a$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{x=\frac{a-b\times a}{1+|b|^2}=\frac{a+a\times b}{1+|b|^2}.}
$$
The denominator is strictly positive for every real $b$, including $b=0$. Substitution using the same triple-product identity verifies the solution and establishes uniqueness. This is the <cross-product operator with a longitudinal rank-one term>.

Let $d=b\cdot x$. Taking a <dot product> with $b$ reduces the <vector> equation to $(1-b\cdot c)d=b\cdot a$. If $b\cdot c\ne1$, this determines a unique <scalar> and hence a unique <vector>:

Multiply the two defining <Gamma integrals>. For $p,q>0$ the nonnegative product integrand allows <Tonelli's theorem>:
$$
\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q)=\int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty x^{p-1}y^{q-1}e^{-(x+y)}dx\,dy.
$$
Use $r=x+y$, $u=x/(x+y)$, so $x=ru$, $y=r(1-u)$ and the positive quadrant becomes $r>0$, $0<u<1$. The <Jacobian determinant> has absolute value
$$
\left|\det\begin{pmatrix}u&r\\1-u&-r\end{pmatrix}\right|=r.
$$
After including this factor, the transformed integral separates:
$$
\left[\int_0^\infty r^{p+q-1}e^{-r}dr\right]
\left[\int_0^1u^{p-1}(1-u)^{q-1}du\right]
=\Gamma(p+q)B(p,q).
$$
This proves the <beta-gamma identity>,
$$
\boxed{\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q)=B(p,q)\Gamma(p+q).}
$$
All changes of variables are made inside the open positive domains; their boundaries do not contribute to the integrals.

Differentiate the <Airy ordinary differential equation> $y''=sy$ to obtain $y^{(3)}=y+sy'$. Combining part (a)'s <derivatives> yields
$$
u_t+u_{xxx}=[at^{a-1}+b^{3a}t^{4a}][y(s)+sy'(s)].
$$

\boxed{a=-\frac13,\qquad b=3,\qquad
u(x,t)=t^{-1/3}y\left(\frac{x}{(3t)^{1/3}}\right).}

$$
u_t=-\frac13t^{-4/3}(y+sy'),\qquad
u_{xxx}=\frac13t^{-4/3}(y+sy').
$$

Seek a separated nonzero solution $u=X(x)Y(y)$. Division by $XY$ on intervals where the factors are nonzero gives $X''/X+Y''/Y=-25$. Write $X''+\lambda X=0$ and $Y''+(25-\lambda)Y=0$, with both factors zero at their endpoints $0,\pi$.

For $X$, a nonpositive $\lambda$ gives only the zero solution under the two endpoint conditions: linear <functions> vanish identically when $\lambda=0$, and the hyperbolic solutions do likewise for $\lambda<0$. For $\lambda>0$, the endpoints force $X=A\sin(mx)$ with positive integer $m$ and $\lambda=m^2$. Applying the same argument to $Y$ gives $Y=B\sin(ny)$ and $25-m^2=n^2$, with positive integer $n$.

$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=A\sin3x\sin4y+B\sin4x\sin3y,\qquad A,B\in\mathbb R.}
$$

Substitution, or <integration> by parts against each <sine> mode, gives $(25-m^2-n^2)c_{mn}=0$ for every <coefficient>.
