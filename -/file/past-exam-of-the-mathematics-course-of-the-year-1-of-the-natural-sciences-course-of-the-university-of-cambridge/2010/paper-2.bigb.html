**As printed, the <function> is undefined at the origin.** For $r^2=x^2+y^2>0$, its <gradient> is
$$
\nabla f=\frac{2e^{-1/r^2}}{r^4}(x,y).
$$
The <scalar> prefactor is positive, and $(x,y)\ne(0,0)$, so this <gradient> never vanishes. Thus there are **no <stationary points> on the stated domain**. Its <infimum> is three, approached as $r\to0$, but that value is not attained there.

If the intended definition includes the natural extension $f(0,0)=3$, that extension has a **unique strict <global minimum> at the origin**, with value three. Indeed, $e^{-1/r^2}>0$ off zero, and $e^{-1/r^2}/r\to0$ proves differentiability at zero with zero <gradient>. More strongly, subtracting three gives the <radial Gaussian flat function>: every <derivative> of that difference vanishes at the origin. The vanishing <Hessian matrix> does not turn this minimum into an <inflection point>. These are two different domain interpretations; the extension must be stated rather than silently inserted.

= Solution

Write $h(x,y)=y(r-4)$, where $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. Because this <hyperbolic function> has strictly positive <derivative> $\cosh$, <stationary points under an increasing outer function> have the same positions and types for $g=\sinh h$ and $h$. For $r>0$,
$$
h_x=\frac{xy}{r},\qquad h_y=r+\frac{y^2}{r}-4.
$$
The first equation requires $x=0$ or $y=0$. If $y=0$, the second gives $|x|=4$. If $x=0$, it gives $2|y|=4$. These are all the nonzero <stationary points>. At the origin, $h=-4y+O(r^2)$, so its <gradient> is $(0,-4)$; the origin is not stationary.

At $(0,2)$ the <Hessian matrix> of $h$ is $\operatorname{diag}(1,2)$, and at $(0,-2)$ it is $\operatorname{diag}(-1,-2)$. At $(4,0)$ and $(-4,0)$ its <Hessian matrices> are respectively $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
(x,y)&g(x,y)&\text{type}\\\hline
(0,2)&-\sinh4&\text{strict local minimum}\\
(0,-2)&\sinh4&\text{strict local maximum}\\
(4,0),\ (-4,0)&0&\text{saddles}
\end{array}}
$$
The extrema are local: $g$ is unbounded above and below on the full <plane>.

For the <level curve> geometry, a level $g=c$ is $y(r-4)=\operatorname{arsinh}c$. In particular, the zero <level curve> is the union of the entire $x$ axis and the <circle> $r=4$, crossing at the two <saddle points>. Inside the <circle>, $g<0$ above the axis and $g>0$ below it; outside, these signs reverse. Levels between $-\sinh4$ and zero have closed loops around the upper minimum as well as unbounded lower-half-plane components. Reflection across the $x$ axis reverses the sign and gives the loops about the lower maximum. At the two extremal heights, the corresponding closed loop shrinks to its extremum. These features determine the sketch.

Use the real <Fourier series> convention $f=a_0/2+\sum_{n\geq1}(a_n\cos nx+b_n\sin nx)$. The <function> is an <even function>, so $b_n=0$. For noninteger real $\mu$,
$$
a_0=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi\cos(\mu x)\,dx=\frac{2\sin\pi\mu}{\pi\mu}.
$$
For $n\geq1$, the product-to-sum identity gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
a_n&=\frac1\pi\left(\frac{\sin\pi(\mu-n)}{\mu-n}
+\frac{\sin\pi(\mu+n)}{\mu+n}\right)\\
&=\frac{2\mu(-1)^n\sin\pi\mu}{\pi(\mu^2-n^2)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the <Fourier series of a noninteger-frequency cosine> is
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\frac{\sin\pi\mu}{\pi\mu}
+\frac{2\mu\sin\pi\mu}{\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
\frac{(-1)^n\cos nx}{\mu^2-n^2}}.
$$
Its <Fourier coefficients> are $O(n^{-2})$, giving <absolute convergence> and <uniform convergence>. The periodic <function> is continuous at its joins because the two endpoint cosine values coincide, so the <Dirichlet-Jordan convergence theorem> gives this value at every point.

Putting $\mu=1/3$ gives
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\frac{3\sqrt3}{2\pi}-\frac{3\sqrt3}{\pi}
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n\cos nx}{9n^2-1}}.
$$
Evaluate at $x=0$, where $f(0)=1$, and multiply by $\pi/\sqrt3$:
$$
\frac\pi{\sqrt3}=\frac32-3\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{9n^2-1}
=\frac32-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{3n^2-1/3}.
$$
Comparing with the required form gives $\boxed{A=3,\qquad B=3}$.

Put $p=P(A)$ and $q=P(B\mid A)$. Decompose the marginal as $P(B)=P(A\cap B)+P(A^c\cap B)=pq+t$, where $0\leq t\leq1-p$. It follows that
$$
\boxed{pq\leq P(B)\leq pq+1-p}.
$$
Both bounds are attainable: choose none or all of the mass outside $A$ to belong to $B$. More explicitly, the four joint cell masses can be $pq,p(1-q),t,1-p-t$, all nonnegative. This is the first case of <sharp bounds from binary conditional probabilities>.

For the usual conditional-probability ratio, the given $q$ requires $p>0$. If $p=1$, the two bounds coincide at $q$. If $p=0$, the conditional is undefined as a ratio, although its formal joint-model bounds reduce to $0\leq P(B)\leq1$.

Write $q=P(B\mid A)$, $s=P(B\mid A^c)$ and $p=P(A)$. The <law of total probability> gives $P(B)=pq+(1-p)s$, a <convex combination> of the two fixed conditional values. Hence
$$
\boxed{\min(q,s)\leq P(B)\leq\max(q,s)}.
$$
These are the closed sharp bounds in the usual mixture-model interpretation, obtained by allowing $p$ to range from zero to one. The joint masses $pq,p(1-q),(1-p)s,(1-p)(1-s)$ realize every such mixture.

There is an endpoint qualification if both conditionals must literally be defined by ratios: then $0<p<1$. If $q\ne s$, the marginal lies strictly between them, so the stated endpoints are an <infimum> and <supremum> rather than achieved extrema. If $q=s$, every allowed mixture has exactly that common value. This is the second case of <sharp bounds from binary conditional probabilities>.

In the physicists' <Hermite polynomial> convention, begin by differentiating the <Gaussian function>:
$$
\frac d{dx}e^{-x^2}=-2xe^{-x^2},\qquad
\frac{d^2}{dx^2}e^{-x^2}=(4x^2-2)e^{-x^2}.
$$
The defining prefactor then gives
$$
\boxed{H_0=1,\qquad H_1=2x,\qquad H_2=4x^2-2}.
$$
The derivative-based definition uses nonnegative integers; negative orders are not part of this <polynomial> definition.

Let $u=e^{-x^2}$, so $u'+2xu=0$. Differentiate this identity $n$ times, for $n\geq1$. By the <Leibniz rule>, only the first two <derivatives> of the linear factor $x$ can contribute:
$$
u^{(n+1)}+2xu^{(n)}+2nu^{(n-1)}=0.
$$
Rearranging proves
$$
\boxed{\left(2x+\frac d{dx}\right)\frac{d^n}{dx^n}e^{-x^2}
=-2n\frac{d^{n-1}}{dx^{n-1}}e^{-x^2}}.
$$
The case $n=0$ is the starting identity $u'+2xu=0$, treated separately without introducing a negative <derivative> order.

Differentiate the product in the definition of $H_n$. Using the identity in (b),
$$
\begin{aligned}
H_n'&=(-1)^ne^{x^2}\left(2xu^{(n)}+u^{(n+1)}\right)\\
&=-2n(-1)^ne^{x^2}u^{(n-1)}
=2n(-1)^{n-1}e^{x^2}u^{(n-1)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The last expression is $2nH_{n-1}$, so
$$
\boxed{H_n'=2nH_{n-1}\qquad(n\geq1)}.
$$
Also $H_0'=0$ separately. This is the <Hermite polynomial derivative recurrence> in the physicists' normalization.
