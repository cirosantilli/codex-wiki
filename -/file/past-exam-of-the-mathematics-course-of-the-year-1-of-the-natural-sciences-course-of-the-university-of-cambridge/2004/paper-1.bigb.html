= Solution
{parent=1}

In components, the <scalar triple product> is
$$
\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)=a_1(b_2c_3-b_3c_2)+a_2(b_3c_1-b_1c_3)+a_3(b_1c_2-b_2c_1).
$$
It is the <determinant> with rows $\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c$. A cyclic permutation of the rows has positive sign, so $\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)=\mathbf c\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)=(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)\cdot\mathbf c$. Equivalently the <Levi-Civita symbol> expression $\epsilon_{ijk}a_ib_jc_k$ is unchanged by that cyclic relabeling.

Each given point satisfies it, and the nonzero cross product confirms that the points determine a unique plane.

= Solution

The <comparison test for series> says that $0\leq v_n\leq Cw_n$ eventually and convergence of $\sum w_n$ imply convergence of $\sum v_n$. Conversely, an eventual positive lower comparison with a divergent positive series proves divergence. The <ratio test> uses $L=\lim_{n\to\infty}v_{n+1}/v_n$: $L<1$ proves convergence, $L>1$ proves divergence, and $L=1$ is inconclusive. Removing finitely many initial zero terms does not affect these conclusions.

For an oriented piecewise smooth surface $S$ with boundary $C=\partial S$ and a vector field $\mathbf A$ with continuous first derivatives near $S$, the <Stokes theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\oint_C\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf r=\int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf A)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS.}
$$
Here $\mathbf n$ is the chosen unit <normal vector>, $dS$ the positive area element, and $d\mathbf r$ the oriented tangent line element. The traversal of $C$ follows the right-hand rule relative to the surface orientation.

= i
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}

Differentiate the Cartesian components to calculate the <curl>:
$$
\nabla\times\mathbf A=\left(-2yz-(-2yz),\ 0-0,\ 0-(-1)\right)=\boxed{(0,0,1)}.
$$

= ii
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Orient the three faces outward from the tetrahedron. The two coordinate-plane faces have normals parallel to $-\mathbf e_x$ or $-\mathbf e_y$, so their flux of the <curl> is zero. On the inclined face $z=1-x-y$, its upward vector area is $(1,1,1)\,dx\,dy$, with $x,y\geq0$, $x+y\leq1$. Thus the direct <surface integral> is
$$
\int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf A)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=\int_0^1\int_0^{1-x}1\,dy\,dx=\frac12.
$$
This surface induces the counterclockwise boundary $O\to X\to Y\to O$ viewed from above, where $X=(1,0,0)$ and $Y=(0,1,0)$. At $z=0$, $\mathbf A=(2x-y,0,0)$. On $OX$, take $(x,y)=(t,0)$, giving integral $\int_0^12t\,dt=1$. On $XY$, take $(x,y)=(1-t,t)$, giving $\int_0^1(-2+3t)dt=-1/2$. On $YO$, $dx=0$ and the integral vanishes. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\oint_C\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf r=1-\frac12+0=\frac12=\int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf A)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS.}
$$
Both sides have been calculated directly, verifying the <Stokes theorem> with consistent orientations.

The <cumulative distribution function> must tend to one at infinity, so $\boxed{k=1}$. Differentiation gives the <probability density function> $f_X(x)=e^{-x}$ for $x>0$ and zero for $x<0$; there is no atom at zero. This is an <exponential distribution> of rate one. Its first two moments are $\int_0^\infty xe^{-x}dx=1$ and $\int_0^\infty x^2e^{-x}dx=2$, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X]=1,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(X)=2-1^2=1.}
$$

The <trace> is unchanged by transposition and is cyclic under a product. With $S^T=S$ and $A^T=-A$,
$$
\operatorname{tr}(AS)=\operatorname{tr}[(AS)^T]=\operatorname{tr}(-SA)=-\operatorname{tr}(AS).

Thus $\boxed{\operatorname{tr}(AS)=0}$. In components this is the vanishing contraction of a <symmetric tensor> with an <antisymmetric tensor>.

The antisymmetric contribution is the negative of twice the <cross-product matrix> for $\mathbf a$:
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}1&2a_3&-2a_2\\-2a_3&1&2a_1\\2a_2&-2a_1&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The <cross-product matrix spectrum> is $0,\pm i|\mathbf a|$, so $M$ has eigenvalues $1,1\pm2i|\mathbf a|$. Multiplication gives
$$
\boxed{\det M=1+4|\mathbf a|^2.}
$$
Direct expansion of the displayed matrix yields the same real positive determinant.

Use index contraction:
$$
(MN)_{ij}=(\delta_{ik}+2\epsilon_{ik\ell}a_\ell)a_kb_j=a_ib_j+2\epsilon_{ik\ell}a_ka_\ell b_j=N_{ij}.
$$
The last term vanishes because $a_ka_\ell$ is symmetric in the contracted indices while the <Levi-Civita symbol> is antisymmetric. Hence $\boxed{MN=N}$.

= Solution

Direct differentiation gives $\boxed{\Phi=x^3y^2z+C}$ with $\nabla\Phi=\mathbf G$, so $\mathbf G$ is a <conservative vector field> and its integral depends only on the endpoints. In contrast,
$$
\nabla\times\mathbf F=\left(-2x^3y,\ 3x^2z(2y-z),\ 3x^2z(2y-z)\right)
$$
is nonzero, for example at $(1,1,1)$. Thus **$\mathbf F$ is not conservative**. Both earlier paths lie in the surface $y=z$, where the two fields happen to coincide. This illustrates why <coinciding path integrals need not imply a conservative field>.

For $ab\ne0$, the hyperbolic identity gives $x^2/a^2-y^2/b^2=1$. The <hyperbolic parametrization of a hyperbola> also imposes $x/a=\cosh\theta\geq1$, so the exact traced locus is

The initial value is positive, so set $z=y^{-2}$. The <chain rule> gives $z'=-2y^{-3}y'=-2(y^{-2}-1)$, making $z'+2z=2$. Its solution is $z=1+Ce^{-2x}$. Since $y(0)=1/2$, $z(0)=4$ and $C=3$. Choosing the positive branch gives

On an interval with $\sin x\ne0$, divide by $\sin x$ and use <integrating factor> $1/\sin x$. Then
$$
\left(\frac y{\sin x}\right)'=\frac{\cos^3x}{\sin^2x}.

Although the derivation divided by $\sin x$, this final expression is smooth and directly satisfies the original undivided equation at every real $x$, including multiples of $\pi$.

{parent=Paper 1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=12}

Use <cylindrical coordinates> with $dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz$, $0\leq\theta<2\pi$, $0\leq z\leq h$ and $0\leq r\leq a(1-z/h)$. Set $s=1-z/h$. Integrating first over radius gives the mass per unit height
$$
\frac{dM}{dz}=2\pi\rho_0\left[\frac{a^2s^2}{2}+\frac{a^2s^3}{3}\right]=\pi\rho_0a^2\left(s^2+\frac23s^3\right).
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{M=\pi\rho_0a^2h\int_0^1\left(s^2+\frac23s^3\right)ds=\frac12\pi\rho_0a^2h.}
$$
For the first moment about the base, use $z=h(1-s)$:
$$
Md=\pi\rho_0a^2h^2\int_0^1(1-s)\left(s^2+\frac23s^3\right)ds=\frac7{60}\pi\rho_0a^2h^2.
$$
Dividing by the total mass gives
$$
\boxed{d=\frac{7h}{30}.}
$$
The <center of mass of a cone with a power-law radial density> gives an independent decomposition: the uniform-density component has centroid $h/4$, while the component proportional to $r$ has centroid $h/5$ and half as much mass. Their mass-weighted mean is $7h/30$. Density increasing toward the wider outer parts consequently moves the center below the uniform cone's $h/4$.
