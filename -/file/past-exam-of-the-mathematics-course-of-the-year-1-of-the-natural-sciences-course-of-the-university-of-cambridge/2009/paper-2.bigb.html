The <characteristic equation> is $r^2+\lambda r+k=0$, whose roots are $r_\pm=(-\lambda\pm\sqrt{\lambda^2-4k})/2$. For distinct real roots, $y=C_1e^{r_+t}+C_2e^{r_-t}$; at the repeated-root threshold $\lambda^2=4k$, $y=(C_1+C_2t)e^{-\lambda t/2}$. In the complex-root case put $\Omega=\sqrt{k-\lambda^2/4}$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{y=e^{-\lambda t/2}(C_1\cos\Omega t+C_2\sin\Omega t),\qquad\lambda^2<4k.}
$$
This is precisely the oscillatory regime of the <damped harmonic oscillator>. The zero solution is of course nonoscillatory; the regime describes its nontrivial motions.

Let $D=1+\omega^2$, with $\omega>0$. A particular solution of the <forced harmonic oscillator> is $y_p=e^{-t}(at+b)$. Substitution gives $Da=2$ and $Db-2a=0$, hence $a=2/D$ and $b=4/D^2$. Adding $A\cos\omega t+B\sin\omega t$ and imposing the two initial conditions gives $A=-4/D^2$ and $B=2(1-\omega^2)/(\omega D^2)$. Thus
$$
y(t)=e^{-t}\left(\frac{2t}{D}+\frac4{D^2}\right)-\frac4{D^2}\cos\omega t+\frac{2(1-\omega^2)}{\omega D^2}\sin\omega t.
$$
The first term tends to zero. The amplitude of the surviving sinusoid is
$$
\boxed{\sqrt{A^2+B^2}=\frac{2}{\omega(1+\omega^2)}.}
$$
As an independent interpretation, the <residual amplitude of an oscillator after an integrable force> is $\omega^{-1}|\int_0^\infty F(t)e^{-i\omega t}dt|$. Here that integral is $2/(1+i\omega)^2$, giving the same result.

A tangent displacement has the form $(dx,dy,z_xdx+z_ydy)$. At the specified point, $z_y=0$ and $z_x=q>0$, so the fastest decrease of height per unit surface distance has $dy=0$ and $dx<0$. The unit downhill tangent is $(-1,0,-q)/\sqrt{1+q^2}$. Therefore, for speed $u$ measured along the surface,

Its <Euclidean norm> is $u$, and it is orthogonal to the surface normal. The general construction is <steepest descent on a graph surface>; using a horizontal speed equal to $u$ would overestimate the full three-dimensional speed.

The horizontal direction of <steepest descent on a graph surface> is parallel to $-\nabla z$. On the line $y=1$, the component $z_y=X(1-y^2)H$ vanishes identically, so that line is invariant under the downhill path. Starting at $X=0$, $z_x=(1-X^2)e^{-(X^2+1)/2}>0$ for $-1<X<0$, so $x$ decreases towards zero. The path reaches the minimum

The coefficient matrix is a <Vandermonde matrix>, with determinant
$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\a&b&c\\a^2&b^2&c^2\end{pmatrix}=(b-a)(c-a)(c-b).

For a continuous <random variable> with <probability density function> $p$, its <expected value> and <variance> are
$$
\boxed{\mu_X=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}xp(x)dx,\qquad\operatorname{Var}X=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(x-\mu_X)^2p(x)dx.}
$$
The mean requires an integrable first absolute moment, and a finite variance requires a finite second moment. When these hold, $\operatorname{Var}X=\mathbb E[X^2]-(\mathbb EX)^2$.

Take $\sigma>0$ and substitute $u=(x-\mu)/\sigma$. The density becomes the standard <Gaussian distribution> density $\varphi(u)=e^{-u^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$. Its normalization follows from the supplied <Gaussian integral> after scaling by $\sqrt2$. Since $u\varphi(u)$ is an integrable <odd function>, its integral is zero, and therefore $\mathbb EX=\mu$.

For the second moment, $\varphi'=-u\varphi$. <Integration by parts> gives
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u^2\varphi(u)du=-[u\varphi(u)]_{-\infty}^{\infty}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(u)du=1.
$$
The endpoint term vanishes by Gaussian decay. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mathbb EX=\mu,\qquad\operatorname{Var}X=\sigma^2.}
$$

\boxed{P(A\mid\overline B)P(\overline B)+P(B)P(A\mid B)=P(A).}

The numerator $P(B\mid A)P(A)$ equals $P(A\cap B)$, while $P(A\mid B)=P(A\cap B)/P(B)$. Thus, wherever the original quotient is defined,

\boxed{\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(A\mid B)}=P(B).}

Under a uniform shuffle, every unordered five-card hand is equally likely, giving $\binom{52}{5}$ possible hands. Choose the triple's rank in 13 ways and its suits in $\binom43$ ways; then choose a different pair rank in 12 ways and its suits in $\binom42$ ways. The count uses <binomial coefficients> because the order of cards within a hand is irrelevant.

&=\int_0^{\pi/2}\left[2-\frac{\cos\theta+\sin\theta}{3}\right]d\theta,
\end{aligned}
$$
so
$$
\boxed{V=\pi-\frac23.}

The <divergence> is $\nabla\cdot F=-1+0+1=0$. Take the hemisphere's outward orientation, and close it with the unit disk $D$ at $z=0$, whose outward normal is $-e_z$. The <divergence theorem> then gives $\int_SF\cdot dS+\int_DF\cdot dS=0$. On the disk, $F\cdot(-e_z)=-x^2$, and <polar coordinates> give
$$
\int_Dx^2dA=\int_0^1\int_0^{2\pi}r^2\cos^2\theta\,r\,d\theta\,dr=\frac\pi4.
$$
Consequently the curved hemisphere has <flux>
$$
\boxed{\int_SF\cdot dS=\frac\pi4.}
$$
The reversed hemisphere orientation would reverse the sign.

Choose the upward orientation. The boundary is the circle $x^2+y^2=4$ at $z=1$, oriented counterclockwise from above. By <Stokes' theorem>, the surface <integral> is $\oint_{\partial S}G\cdot dr$. On this boundary $dz=0$ and $z^2=1$, so
$$
\oint_{\partial S}G\cdot dr=\oint(dx-3xy\,dy).
$$
The first term integrates to zero around a closed curve. By <Green's theorem>, the second equals the disk integral $\int_{x^2+y^2\leq4}(-3y)dx\,dy=0$, by oddness in $y$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\int_S(\nabla\times G)\cdot n\,dS=0.}
$$
Here reversing orientation leaves the zero value unchanged.

Direct <differentiation> of $v=(x^2-1)^l$ gives $(x^2-1)v'=2lxv$; the identity also holds for $l=0$. Differentiate it $l+1$ times using the <Leibniz rule>. The quadratic factor has only its first two derivatives nonzero, so
$$
(x^2-1)v^{(l+2)}+2(l+1)xv^{(l+1)}+l(l+1)v^{(l)}
=2l[xv^{(l+1)}+(l+1)v^{(l)}].
$$
Writing $y=v^{(l)}$ and collecting terms gives $(x^2-1)y''+2xy'-l(l+1)y=0$. Multiplication by $-1$ gives the <Legendre differential equation>:
$$
\boxed{(1-x^2)y''-2xy'+l(l+1)y=0.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=b}

The polynomial $v$ has leading term $x^{2l}$. Its $l$th derivative has leading term $(2l)!x^l/l!$, and all other terms have lower degree. Thus the <Rodrigues formula for Legendre polynomials> defines a polynomial with nonzero leading coefficient
$$
\boxed{P_l(x)=\frac{(2l)!}{2^l(l!)^2}x^l+\text{lower-degree terms}.}
$$
It therefore has degree exactly $l$, including the constant case $P_0=1$.

Write $v=(x+1)^l(x-1)^l$. In the <Leibniz rule> for its $l$th derivative at one, any term with fewer than $l$ derivatives on $(x-1)^l$ vanishes. The sole surviving term leaves $(x+1)^l$ undifferentiated and differentiates the second factor $l$ times, giving
$$
v^{(l)}(1)=2^l l!.
$$
The normalization in <Rodrigues' formula> consequently gives
$$
\boxed{P_l(1)=\frac{2^ll!}{2^ll!}=1.}
$$
The same argument covers $l=0$, with both zeroth powers equal to one.
