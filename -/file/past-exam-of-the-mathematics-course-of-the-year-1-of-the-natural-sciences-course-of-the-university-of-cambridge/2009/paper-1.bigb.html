Neither biscuit type can run out in this case. Treat each freely made choice as an <independent> <Bernoulli trial>, with success meaning chocolate. The number taken after $n$ choices has a <binomial distribution>, so
$$
\boxed{P_{k,n}=\binom nkp^k(1-p)^{n-k},\quad k=0,\ldots,n.}
$$
The <binomial coefficient> counts the possible success positions, and every such arrangement has the same probability. Independence of successive choices is the probabilistic model implicit in the question; identical marginal preferences alone would not imply it.

For the last chocolate to be taken on choice $n$, the first $n-1$ choices must include exactly $C-1$ chocolate choices, and choice $n$ must take chocolate. Before that final success there is still chocolate available, so no forced plain choice has yet occurred. The <negative binomial stopping argument> gives
$$
\boxed{P_{\rm LastChoc,n}=\binom{n-1}{C-1}p^C(1-p)^{n-C},\qquad C\leq n\leq F.}
$$
This is the all-trials convention of the <negative binomial distribution>. The finite examination scenario also has a remaining probability that the tray still contains chocolate after all $F$ choices; the displayed probabilities need not sum to one over $C\leq n\leq F$. At $p=0$ the last chocolate is never taken, and at $p=1$ it is taken on choice $C$.

{parent=b}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

A forced plain choice occurs exactly when the last chocolate is taken before the final Fellow is served: if it is taken by choice $f\leq F-1$, choice $f+1$ has no chocolate available, whatever that person's preference would have been. Exhaustion precisely on choice $F$ forces nobody. The distinct possible exhaustion times are disjoint events, hence
$$
\boxed{P_{\rm Disaster}=\sum_{f=C}^{F-1}\binom{f-1}{C-1}p^C(1-p)^{f-C}.}
$$
Equivalently, imagine independent preferred choices even after stock is exhausted. The event is that at least $C$ preferred chocolate choices occur among the first $F-1$ people, so the <binomial distribution> gives $P_{\rm Disaster}=\sum_{k=C}^{F-1}\binom{F-1}{k}p^k(1-p)^{F-1-k}$. The <success-count and waiting-time duality> explains equality of these two sums. Plain-biscuit depletion cannot interfere because $D>F$.

The <characteristic polynomial> is
$$
\det(A-\lambda I)=(2-\lambda)(4-\lambda)+2=(\lambda-3)^2+1.
$$
Thus the <eigenvalues> and corresponding <eigenvectors> over $\mathbb C$ are
$$
\boxed{\lambda_+=3+i,\ v_+=(-1+i,1)^T;\qquad\lambda_-=3-i,\ v_-=(-1-i,1)^T.}
$$
All nonzero scalar multiples of these vectors are also <eigenvectors>. Distinct <eigenvalues> make them linearly independent. Put them in the columns of
$$
B=\begin{pmatrix}-1+i&-1-i\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B^{-1}=\frac1{2i}\begin{pmatrix}1&1+i\\-1&-1+i\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The equations $Av_\pm=\lambda_\pm v_\pm$ are exactly $AB=BC$, giving the <diagonalization>
$$
\boxed{C=B^{-1}AB=\begin{pmatrix}3+i&0\\0&3-i\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
There is no diagonalization over $\mathbb R$, since the matrix has no real <eigenvalues>.

Three vectors in real three-dimensional space have zero <determinant> exactly when they are <linearly dependent>. Their <linear span> then has dimension at most two, so they lie in a common plane through the origin. Conversely vectors in such a plane span at most two dimensions, so their <scalar triple product> vanishes. This includes parallel nonzero vectors: they need not determine a unique plane, but they lie in at least one. Even zero vectors satisfy the determinant characterization, though the question excludes them.

Negative integers are outside this integral definition and factorial conclusion.

The <homogeneous first-order differential equation> suggests $v=y/x$, so $y'=v+xv'$. Wherever the original equation is defined,
$$
xv'=-\tan v,\qquad\frac{d}{dx}[x\sin v]=\sin v+x\cos v\,v'=0.
$$
Thus the implicit solution is $x\sin(y/x)=C$. The printed initial values force $C=x_0=2/\pi$.

**There is no classical solution through the printed initial point.** There $y/x=\pi/2$, so the tangent on the right is undefined. The implicit relation nevertheless has two neighboring real branches on $x>x_0$ which approach that point continuously:
$$
\boxed{y_1(x)=x\arcsin(x_0/x),\qquad y_2(x)=x[\pi-\arcsin(x_0/x)],\quad x_0=2/\pi.}
$$
Both tend to $y=1$ as $x\downarrow x_0$, but their <derivatives> become unbounded with opposite signs. They solve the equation on the open interval $x>x_0$, not at its endpoint. Other inverse-sine periods cannot approach the specified initial value. This is an <endpoint obstruction in a homogeneous differential equation>. Constant-ratio branches $y=k\pi x$ also solve the general equation where $x\ne0$, but do not meet the initial data.

2\mu\cos y=-(x\mu'+\mu)\cos y.

On an open two-dimensional domain the coefficient relation is $x\mu'+3\mu=0$, so $\mu=Cx^{-3}$ on a connected interval avoiding zero. The normalization gives $C=1$ on the positive interval through $x=1$. Integrating $f_x=2x^{-3}\sin y$ and checking $f_y$ yields
$$
\boxed{\mu(x)=x^{-3},\qquad f(x,y)=-\frac{\sin y}{x^2}+C_0,\quad x>0.}
$$
There is no smooth extension of this normalized integrating factor across $x=0$; an independently specified factor on $x<0$ is not fixed by data at one.

= c
{parent=18}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Let $a=f(b,c)$ and work where both $f_b$ and $f_c$ are nonzero, so the required inverse <partial derivatives> exist. At fixed $a$, the <total differential> gives $0=f_b\,db+f_c\,dc$, hence $(\partial b/\partial c)_a=-f_c/f_b$. At fixed $b$, $(\partial c/\partial a)_b=1/f_c$. Their product with $f_b$ is $-1$: this is the <cyclic partial derivative identity>.

For explicit verification in this example, put $P=b^4-6b^2c^2+c^4$ and $H=(b^2+c^2)^3$. Direct <differentiation> gives
$$
\left(\frac{\partial a}{\partial b}\right)_c=-\frac{cP}{H},\quad
\left(\frac{\partial a}{\partial c}\right)_b=\frac{bP}{H},\quad
\left(\frac{\partial b}{\partial c}\right)_a=\frac bc,\quad
\left(\frac{\partial c}{\partial a}\right)_b=\frac H{bP}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial a}{\partial b}\right)_c\left(\frac{\partial b}{\partial c}\right)_a\left(\frac{\partial c}{\partial a}\right)_b=-1.}
$$
The identity is local to regular branches: for these explicit formulas require $bcP\ne0$. At singular points one cannot justify cancellation of nonexistent inverse derivatives merely by writing a formal product.

Assume $c\ne0$, so $(\xi,\eta)=(x-ct,x+ct)$ is an invertible coordinate change. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\partial_x=\partial_\xi+\partial_\eta,\qquad\partial_t=c(-\partial_\xi+\partial_\eta).
$$
For a twice continuously differentiable solution the mixed <partial derivatives> commute. Squaring the two operators therefore gives
$$
\partial_x^2-c^{-2}\partial_t^2=4\partial_\xi\partial_\eta.
$$
Thus the <one-dimensional wave equation> becomes
$$
\boxed{\psi_{\xi\eta}=0.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=b}

If $\psi=f(\xi)+g(\eta)$ with twice differentiable $f,g$, its mixed <partial derivative> vanishes because each summand depends on only one coordinate. Conversely $\psi_{\xi\eta}=0$ means $\psi_\xi$ is independent of $\eta$; integration in $\xi$ supplies $f(\xi)$ and an integration function $g(\eta)$. Hence, on a characteristic rectangle, the general solution is
$$
\boxed{\psi(x,t)=f(x-ct)+g(x+ct).}
$$
These terms travel in opposite directions without changing profile, as in <D'Alembert's formula>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Take the spatial interval $0<x<L$ with $L>0$, and write $v=|c|>0$. A separated nonzero solution satisfies
$$
\frac{X''}{X}=\frac{T''}{v^2T}=-\lambda.
$$
The zero-endpoint <Dirichlet boundary conditions> rule out nonzero solutions for $\lambda\leq0$: linear or hyperbolic spatial factors cannot vanish at both ends. For $\lambda>0$, they give $X_n=\sin(n\pi x/L)$ and $\lambda_n=(n\pi/L)^2$, $n\geq1$. The temporal factors solve $T_n''+\omega_n^2T_n=0$, with the <fixed-end standing wave frequencies> $\omega_n=n\pi v/L$. Superposition gives
$$
\boxed{\psi(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[A_n\cos\left(\frac{n\pi vt}{L}\right)+B_n\sin\left(\frac{n\pi vt}{L}\right)\right]\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right).}
$$
The constants are arbitrary subject to the convergence and regularity required of the solution. For initial displacement $f$ and initial <velocity> $g$, their <Fourier sine series> give $A_n=(2/L)\int_0^Lf(x)\sin(n\pi x/L)dx$ and $B_n=(2/(L\omega_n))\int_0^Lg(x)\sin(n\pi x/L)dx$. Smooth compatible data give classical solutions; weaker data have the corresponding energy interpretation. A negative printed $L$ describes the interval with endpoints reversed and gives the same modes after using its positive length.
