= Solution

The square roots simplify to the <absolute values> $|a-b|$ and $|a+b|$. Put $s=\sin(\pi\sqrt{a^2+b^2})$. A nontrivial solution of the homogeneous <linear system> exists exactly when its <coefficient matrix> is singular:
$$
0=\det\begin{pmatrix}|a-b|&s\\|a+b|&s\end{pmatrix}
=s(|a-b|-|a+b|).
$$
The second factor vanishes precisely when $(a-b)^2=(a+b)^2$, or $ab=0$, giving both coordinate axes. The first factor vanishes when $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ is a nonnegative integer. Hence the complete <locus> is
$$
\boxed{\{a=0\}\ \cup\ \{b=0\}\ \cup\
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\{a^2+b^2=n^2\}.}
$$
These are the two axes together with concentric <circles> of positive integer radii. The zero-radius case is the origin, already included on the axes; at that point both equations vanish and any nonzero pair $(x,y)$ works.

= Solution
{parent=b}

On a branch with $y\ne0$, the given <derivative> is nonzero wherever the original equation is defined, so use <inverse-function linearization of a first-order equation> and regard $x$ as a <function> of $y$:
$$
\frac{dx}{dy}=\frac{x+y+2}{y},\qquad
\frac{dx}{dy}-\frac xy=1+\frac2y.
$$
The <integrating factor> $1/y$ gives
$$
\frac d{dy}\left(\frac xy\right)=\frac1y+\frac2{y^2},
\qquad \frac xy=\ln|y|-\frac2y+C.
$$
Thus the nonzero solutions are given implicitly by
$$
\boxed{x+2=y(\ln|y|+C),\qquad y\ne0.}
$$
Each branch must also retain **$x+y+2\ne0$**; at those excluded points the original right-hand side is undefined and the inverse graph cannot represent a regular $y(x)$. Differentiating the implicit equation gives $y'=1/(\ln|y|+C+1)$, equal to the required quotient wherever these restrictions hold.

The additional solution **$y=0$**, on any interval avoiding $x=-2$, was lost when dividing by $y$ and must be included. It is not defined through the point $(x,y)=(-2,0)$ in the original equation.

For $y\ne0$, the <Bernoulli differential equation> becomes linear under $u=1/y$. Multiplying the equation by $-y^{-2}$ gives

\boxed{y=0\quad\text{or}\quad
 y(x)=\frac1{x^2(C+\sin x)}.}

The original equation requires $x\ne0$; each nonzero branch additionally requires $C+\sin x\ne0$. The zero solution is restored explicitly because the reciprocal substitution excludes it.

For $h>0$, parametrize the curved surface by $(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,r^2/a)$, with $0\leq r\leq\sqrt{ah}$ and $0\leq\theta\leq2\pi$. The magnitude of the <cross product> of its coordinate tangents gives the <area element of a surface>:

$$
\begin{aligned}
S&=2\pi\int_0^{\sqrt{ah}}r\sqrt{1+4r^2/a^2}\,dr\\
&=\frac{\pi a^2}{6}\left[(1+4r^2/a^2)^{3/2}\right]_0^{\sqrt{ah}}\\
&=\boxed{\frac{\pi a^2}{6}\left[\left(1+\frac{4h}{a}\right)^{3/2}-1\right].}
\end{aligned}

This is the curved <surface area>, not the area of a lid across the horizontal opening. The expression tends to zero at $h=0$ and has dimensions of area. The <volume and centroid of a parabolic cup> use the same geometry.

The <gradient> factors as
$$
f_x=y(3x^2+y^2-1),\qquad f_y=x(x^2+3y^2-1).
$$
If a coordinate is zero, the solutions are $(0,0)$, $(\pm1,0)$ and $(0,\pm1)$. With both coordinates nonzero, subtracting the two bracket equations gives $x^2=y^2$, hence $x^2=y^2=1/4$. These give four more <stationary points>.

The <Hessian matrix> has equal diagonal entries $6xy$ and off-diagonal entry $3(x^2+y^2)-1$. At the origin its <eigenvalues> are $\pm1$, and at the four nonzero axis points they are $\pm2$, so all five are saddles. At the two same-sign interior points the <eigenvalues> are $1,2$, while at the opposite-sign points they are $-1,-2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{points}&\text{type}&f\\\hline
(0,0),(\pm1,0),(0,\pm1)&\text{saddles}&0\\
(1/2,1/2),(-1/2,-1/2)&\text{local minima}&-1/8\\
(1/2,-1/2),(-1/2,1/2)&\text{local maxima}&1/8.
\end{array}}
$$
The extrema are not global, since the quartic takes arbitrarily large positive and negative values along suitable rays.

For the contour sketch, the zero level is exactly the two axes together with the unit <circle>. Inside the <circle>, $f$ is negative in the first and third quadrants and positive in the other two; outside, those signs reverse. Small negative levels contain closed loops around the two minima as well as unbounded branches in the second and fourth quadrants. Small positive levels similarly contain two loops around the maxima and unbounded branches in the first and third quadrants. The closed loops shrink to the extrema at levels $\mp1/8$, and disappear beyond those levels. This is the unit-scale <quartic surface with axes and a circle as its zero set>.

= Solution

For a classical solution take $F,G$ twice differentiable, so that the second <derivatives> exist. Direct use of the <chain rule> gives
$$
y_{tt}=25(F''+G''),\qquad y_{xx}=4(F''+G''),
$$
with the two profile arguments as specified. Thus $4y_{tt}=25y_{xx}$.

To prove generality, introduce <characteristic coordinates> $\xi=2x+5t$, $\eta=2x-5t$, whose Jacobian <determinant> is $-20\ne0$. For $y=U(\xi,\eta)$ the differential operator is
$$
4\partial_t^2-25\partial_x^2=-400\partial_\xi\partial_\eta.
$$
The equation becomes $U_{\xi\eta}=0$. Integrating once in each variable yields
$$
\boxed{U(\xi,\eta)=F(\xi)+G(\eta).}
$$
This is the <countertravelling decomposition of the one-dimensional wave equation>, with <wave speed> $5/2$. Once differentiability alone is not sufficient for a classical second-order equation; the displayed argument uses the required second <derivatives>.

= Solution

Choose $F(s)=G(s)=\tfrac12\sin s$. The two travelling profiles then add by the <sine> addition identity:
$$
\boxed{y(x,t)=\frac12\sin(2x+5t)+\frac12\sin(2x-5t)
=\sin2x\cos5t.}
$$
This is a <standing wave>. At $x=0$ and $x=\pi$ it vanishes for all $t$. At $t=0$ its displacement is $\sin2x$, and its time <derivative> $-5\sin2x\sin5t$ is zero. Also $y_{tt}=-25y$ and $y_{xx}=-4y$, directly checking the original equation.

= Solution

With the standard real normalization, the <Fourier series> of a period-$2L$ <function> is
$$
f(x)\sim\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[a_n\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}+b_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right],
$$
where
$$
\boxed{a_n=\frac1L\int_{-L}^Lf(x)\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx\quad(n\geq0),\qquad
b_n=\frac1L\int_{-L}^Lf(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx\quad(n\geq1).}
$$
For the <partial sum> $S_N$, <Fourier orthogonality> eliminates all cross terms. The constant term contributes $2L(a_0/2)^2$, while every squared <sine> or <cosine> integrates to $L$, giving
$$
\int_{-L}^LS_N(x)^2dx=L\frac{a_0^2}{2}+L\sum_{n=1}^N(a_n^2+b_n^2).
$$
For a square-integrable <function>, completeness of the trigonometric <orthogonal basis> in the <Hilbert space> $L^2[-L,L]$ makes $S_N\to f$ in norm. Passing to the limit proves <Parseval's identity>:
$$
\boxed{\int_{-L}^Lf(x)^2dx=L\left[\frac{a_0^2}{2}
+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(a_n^2+b_n^2)\right].}
$$
Square integrability is the needed hypothesis here; periodicity alone is not an integrability or convergence condition.

{parent=17}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Over one period, the graph rises linearly from the limiting point $(-\pi,0)$ to $(0,\pi)$, then drops to zero and stays there up to $\pi$. Repeat this pattern every $2\pi$. The given open endpoints do not fix values at the jumps, but changing individual point values does not change any <Fourier coefficient>.

Only the negative half-interval contributes. The constant coefficient is
$$
a_0=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^0(x+\pi)dx=\frac\pi2.
$$
For $n\geq1$, <integration by parts> gives
$$
a_n=\frac1\pi\left[\frac{(x+\pi)\sin nx}{n}+\frac{\cos nx}{n^2}\right]_{-\pi}^0
=\frac{1-(-1)^n}{\pi n^2},
$$
and
$$
b_n=\frac1\pi\left[-\frac{(x+\pi)\cos nx}{n}+\frac{\sin nx}{n^2}\right]_{-\pi}^0
=-\frac1n.
$$
Consequently the requested <Fourier series> is
$$
\boxed{\frac\pi4+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
\left[\frac{1-(-1)^n}{\pi n^2}\cos nx-\frac1n\sin nx\right].}
$$
It equals the given <function> on each open <continuity> interval. By the <Dirichlet-Jordan convergence theorem>, at $x=2k\pi$ it takes the jump midpoint $\pi/2$, while at $x=(2k+1)\pi$ it takes zero because the periodic one-sided limits agree there.

All size-six subsets are equally likely under uniform <sampling without replacement>. There are $\binom{12}{6}$ subsets in total, and $\binom63\binom63$ contain the required three members from each group. Thus the <hypergeometric distribution> gives

Let $A,B,C$ denote the mutually exclusive supplier events and $D$ the defective-bulb event. Their <probabilities> and conditional defect rates are
$$
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
 &A&B&C\\\hline
\mathbb P(\text{supplier})&1/5&2/5&2/5\\
\mathbb P(D\mid\text{supplier})&\alpha&\alpha/2&\alpha/4.
\end{array}
$$
These supplier events partition the possible bulbs. The following calculations use the <law of total probability> and <Bayes' theorem> with that partition.

= i
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}

The <law of total probability> gives
$$
\mathbb P(D)=\frac15\alpha+\frac25\frac\alpha2+\frac25\frac\alpha4=\frac\alpha2.
$$
For $\alpha>0$, <Bayes' theorem> therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(A\mid D)=\frac{\alpha/5}{\alpha/2}=\frac25.}
$$
At the allowed endpoint $\alpha=0$, no bulb is defective: $\mathbb P(D)=0$. This <conditional probability> is then **undefined**, rather than $2/5$. The value $2/5$ is its limit as $\alpha\to0^+$, but does not define conditioning on the impossible event within the stated discrete model.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The non-defective event is the complement $D^c$. Using the total defective <probability> found above,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(D^c)=1-\frac\alpha2.}
$$
This formula is valid throughout $0\leq\alpha\leq1$, and its value is at least $1/2$.

{parent=b}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The joint <probability> of supplier $C$ and a non-defective bulb is $\mathbb P(C\cap D^c)=(2/5)(1-\alpha/4)$. Normalize by the positive non-defective <probability>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(C\mid D^c)
=\frac{(2/5)(1-\alpha/4)}{1-\alpha/2}
=\frac{4-\alpha}{5(2-\alpha)}.}
$$
This is defined even at $\alpha=0$, where it reduces to the original supplier fraction $2/5$. At $\alpha=1$ it is $3/5$.

The <Big O notation> means that there exist constants $C>0$ and $\delta>0$ such that
$$
\boxed{|f(x)|\leq C|x|^2\quad\text{whenever}\quad0<|x|<\delta}
$$
within the domain of $f$. Thus $f(x)/x^2$ stays bounded in a punctured neighbourhood of zero. It need not have a limit: $f(x)=x^2\sin(1/x)$ is one example. The statement imposes no condition on a separately assigned value $f(0)$.

{parent=19}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Use the <Maclaurin series> on the positive side:
$$
f(x)=\frac{x^2}{2}+O(x^4),\qquad
g(x)=\frac{x^3}{6}+O(x^5),\qquad
h(x)=\sqrt{x}\,[1+O(x)].
$$
In the last expression the original square root covers $e^x-1$, whose leading term is $x>0$. Hence $g/f\to0$ and $f/h\to0$. All three are positive for sufficiently small positive $x$, so
$$
\boxed{g(x)<f(x)<h(x)\quad\text{for sufficiently small }x>0.}
$$
Thus $h$ is greatest and $g$ least in the stated limit.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Apply the <ratio test> to the infinite series whose <partial sums> are $S_N$. Assuming the terms are eventually nonzero and the limit exists, let
$$
\ell=\lim_{k\to\infty}\left|\frac{u_{k+1}}{u_k}\right|.
$$
If **$\ell<1$**, the series has <absolute convergence>, by eventual comparison with a decaying <geometric series>. If **$\ell>1$**, including an infinite limit, the terms fail to tend to zero and the series diverges. If **$\ell=1$**, the test is inconclusive: both $\sum1/k$ and $\sum1/k^2$ have that ratio limit, but only the second converges. A finite <partial sum> itself requires no convergence test; the issue is the limit as $N\to\infty$.

{parent=19}

= Solution
{parent=d}

For $p\leq0$, the terms $k^{-p}$ do not tend to zero, so the series diverges. For $p>0$, group the positive terms into the dyadic blocks $2^j\leq k<2^{j+1}$. Each block contains $2^j$ terms, and monotonicity gives
$$
2^{-p}\,2^{j(1-p)}\leq
\sum_{k=2^j}^{2^{j+1}-1}k^{-p}
\leq2^{j(1-p)}.
$$
When $p>1$, the upper bounds form a convergent <geometric series> with ratio $2^{1-p}<1$, proving convergence. When $0<p\leq1$, the lower bounds do not tend to zero, so infinitely many blocks each make a nonvanishing contribution and the sum diverges. Thus the <p-series> criterion is
$$
\boxed{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{-p}\text{ converges exactly when }p>1.}
$$
This dyadic comparison is the mechanism of the <Cauchy condensation test>. In particular $p=1$ is the divergent <harmonic series>.

= Solution

Take a face on the flat base and let $x,y$ be its positive half-widths, with height $z>0$. The upper vertices meet the spherical boundary at $x^2+y^2+z^2=a^2$, and the <rectangular parallelepiped> has <volume> $V=4xyz$. A <Lagrange multiplier> gives
$$
4yz=2\lambda x,\qquad4xz=2\lambda y,\qquad4xy=2\lambda z.
$$
Multiplication by $x,y,z$ respectively shows $x^2=y^2=z^2$. Positivity and the constraint give $x=y=z=a/\sqrt3$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{V_{\max}=\frac{4a^3}{3\sqrt3},\qquad
\text{edge lengths }\frac{2a}{\sqrt3},\ \frac{2a}{\sqrt3},\ \frac a{\sqrt3}.}
$$
Boundary choices with a zero dimension give zero <volume>, so this is the maximum in this configuration.

The <maximum-volume cuboid in a hemisphere> also has a bound covering arbitrary orientations. Let $u_i$ be any box's half-edge lengths, $v_i$ its <centre> coordinates in its orthonormal edge <basis>, and $n_i$ the vertical unit-vector coordinates. Remaining above the base <plane> requires $\sum n_iv_i\geq\sum|n_i|u_i$, so $|v_j|\geq u_j$ for at least one index. Its farthest vertex obeys
$$
a^2\geq R_{\max}^2=\sum u_i^2+\sum v_i^2+2\sum u_i|v_i|
\geq\sum_{i\ne j}u_i^2+4u_j^2,
$$
where containment supplies the first inequality. The <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality> applied to the three terms $u_i^2$ for $i\ne j$ and $4u_j^2$ yields $2u_1u_2u_3\leq a^3/(3\sqrt3)$. Thus its full <volume> $8u_1u_2u_3$ cannot exceed the value attained above; tilting cannot improve it. Boundary contact is allowed for the maximum; strict containment would give the same supremum.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The triangular roof has height $(w/2)\tan\theta$ and two slopes of length $(w/2)\sec\theta$. Hence the area constraint and cost are
$$
A=wh+\frac{w^2}{4}\tan\theta,
\qquad C=2\alpha h+\beta w\sec\theta.
$$
The wall height is fixed, so its cost is constant. Assume the physical case $A,h,\beta>0$. Minimize $C$ subject to the area constraint by <Lagrange multipliers>:
$$
\beta\sec\theta=\lambda\left(h+\frac w2\tan\theta\right),\qquad
\beta w\sec\theta\tan\theta=\lambda\frac{w^2}{4}\sec^2\theta.
$$
Eliminating the positive common factors gives
$$
w\sec^2\theta=4h\tan\theta+2w\tan^2\theta,
\qquad w(1-\tan^2\theta)=4h\tan\theta.
$$
Thus the optimum has $0<\theta<\pi/4$ and
$$
\boxed{2\tan2\theta=\frac wh.}
$$
Set $D=A-wh>0$, so the area constraint gives $\tan\theta=4D/w^2$. Substituting into the preceding equation and multiplying by $w^3$ yields $w^4-16D^2=16hwD$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{w^4=16D(D+wh)=16A(A-wh).}
$$
For a global check, the square of the total roof length is
$$
Q(w)=w^2+\frac{16(A-wh)^2}{w^2},\qquad0<w<A/h.
$$
Its <derivative> has the sign of $P(w)=w^4+16Ahw-16A^2$. Since $P'(w)>0$, $P(0)<0$ and $P(A/h)>0$, it has one root in this interval, with $Q$ decreasing before that root and increasing afterwards. This proves the unique global minimum, rather than merely a stationary design. The <minimum roof length at fixed wall height and cross-sectional area> is independent of $\alpha$ and of the positive factor $\beta$. If $\beta=0$, every feasible shape has equal cost; the displayed conditions then describe a possible minimizer but are not necessary.
