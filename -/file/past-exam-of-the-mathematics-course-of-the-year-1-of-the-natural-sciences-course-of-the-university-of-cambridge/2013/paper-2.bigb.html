The <probability density function> is nonnegative for $\alpha>0$. Its <integral> over the support is
$$
\int_1^\infty\alpha x^{-\alpha-1}\,dx
=\left[-x^{-\alpha}\right]_1^\infty=1,
$$
so it is **normalized for every $\alpha>0$**. This is the unit-threshold <Pareto distribution>.

= b
{parent=11}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <expected value> is the <improper integral> $\alpha\int_1^\infty x^{-\alpha}\,dx$. It converges exactly when $\alpha>1$, yielding
$$
\boxed{E[X]=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1},\qquad\alpha>1}.
$$
At $\alpha=1$ this <improper integral> diverges logarithmically, and for $0<\alpha<1$ it diverges as a <power> of its upper endpoint. In both cases the nonnegative variable has infinite <mean>. This is the first <Pareto moment threshold>.

{parent=11}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The second <moment> is $E[X^2]=\alpha\int_1^\infty x^{1-\alpha}\,dx$, finite exactly for $\alpha>2$, where it equals $\alpha/(\alpha-2)$. Subtracting the square of the <mean> gives
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-2}
-\frac{\alpha^2}{(\alpha-1)^2}
=\frac{\alpha}{(\alpha-1)^2(\alpha-2)},\qquad\alpha>2}.
$$
For $1<\alpha\leq2$, the <mean> exists but the <variance> is infinite. For $0<\alpha\leq1$, the ordinary centered <variance> is not defined because the <mean> is not finite. Thus there is no finite <variance> for any $\alpha\leq2$.

The even-power formula reduces every summand to a scalar multiple of $I$:
$$
\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{A^{4n}-2A^{2n}}n
=\left[\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(1/16)^n}{n}

Put $c=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ and denote the distances to $(-c,0)$ and $(c,0)$ by $d_-$ and $d_+$. Direct expansion using $c^2=a^2+b^2$ gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
d_-^2&=(a\cosh t+c)^2+b^2\sinh^2t=(c\cosh t+a)^2,\\
d_+^2&=(a\cosh t-c)^2+b^2\sinh^2t=(c\cosh t-a)^2.
\end{aligned}

Since $c>a>0$ and $\cosh t\geq1$, both expressions inside the final square roots are positive. Hence
$$
\boxed{d_--d_+=2a}
$$
for every point of this right branch. This is the <focal-distance identity for a hyperbola>; on the left branch the signed difference would reverse, while its absolute value would remain $2a$.

Put $R=\sqrt8$ and parametrize the circle counterclockwise, as viewed from positive $z$, by $\mathbf r(\phi)=(R\cos\phi,R\sin\phi,3)$ for $0\leq\phi\leq2\pi$. The restricted field is
$$

\boxed{\oint_\Gamma V\,d\mathbf r=(8\pi,96\pi,0)}.

={}&\partial_x\partial_yF_z-\partial_x\partial_zF_y
+\partial_y\partial_zF_x-\partial_y\partial_xF_z\\
&+\partial_z\partial_xF_y-\partial_z\partial_yF_x.
\end{aligned}
$$
Each positive term cancels a negative term because the corresponding mixed <derivatives> commute. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf G=\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0}.
$$

For fixed amount of substance, take the <internal energy> $U=U(S,V)$. The <first law of thermodynamics> identifies $T=U_S|_V$ and $-P=U_V|_S$. Equality of mixed second <derivatives> gives
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\right)_S
=-\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial S}\right)_V}.
$$
For the <Helmholtz free energy>, subtract the product differential $d(TS)$ from the <first law of thermodynamics> to obtain $dF=-S\,dT-P\,dV$. Its natural variables are $(T,V)$, so $F_T=-S$ and $F_V=-P$. Exactness therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T
=\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V}.
$$
For the <Gibbs free energy>, the <product rule> gives $dG=-S\,dT+V\,dP$. Thus $G_T=-S$, $G_P=V$, and
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\right)_T
=-\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P}.
$$
For the <enthalpy>, $dH=T\,dS+V\,dP$, giving $H_S=T$, $H_P=V$ and
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_S
=\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right)_P}.
$$
These are the four <Maxwell relations>, each following from exactness in the appropriate pair of natural variables.

Along a change in <volume> at fixed <temperature>, the <first law of thermodynamics> gives
$$
\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T
=T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T-P.
$$
Use the Helmholtz <Maxwell relation> derived in (a) to replace the <entropy> <derivative>. This proves
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T
=T\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V-P}.
$$
For the stated <van der Waals equation>, temperature-independent $a,b$ give $P_T|_V=R/(V-b)$. Hence $U_V|_T=a/V^2$. Integrating with respect to $V$ yields
$$
U(V,T)=-\frac aV+h(T).
$$
The dilute-limit condition holds at every fixed $T$ and forces $h(T)=cT$, so
$$
\boxed{U(V,T)=cT-\frac aV}.
$$
The result is the <internal energy of a van der Waals gas> in the fixed-amount convention of the question. Its <volume> <derivative> is positive even though the attractive energy correction itself is negative.

Along a differentiable graph satisfying the implicit equation, the <multivariable chain rule> gives
$$
\frac{d}{dx}F(x,y(x))=F_x+F_y\frac{dy}{dx}=0.
$$
Thus, wherever $F_y\ne0$,

\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{F_x}{F_y}}.

The <implicit function theorem> supplies such a local graph under that nonvanishing condition. At a <singular point> both numerator and denominator vanish, so this division cannot determine its tangent.

For a smooth oriented <surface> $S$ with positively oriented <boundary> $C=\partial S$, <Stokes theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot d\mathbf S
=\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r}.
$$
Here $d\mathbf S=\mathbf n\,dA$ uses the chosen <unit normal vector>, while $d\mathbf r$ is tangent to the directed <boundary>. The <boundary> <orientation> follows the <right-hand rule>: curling the fingers along $C$ makes the thumb point in the normal direction. The field must be continuously differentiable on a neighbourhood of the <surface>, with the usual piecewise-smooth allowance for its <boundary>.

{title=Outward hemisphere orientation, its counterclockwise boundary, and tangent and normal elements}

For the upper <hemisphere> with outward normal, the induced equatorial direction is counterclockwise as viewed from positive $z$.

First calculate the <curl>:
$$
\nabla\times\mathbf F=(0,-2z,3y-1).
$$
By <Stokes theorem>, use the positively oriented equator $\mathbf r(\phi)=(a\cos\phi,a\sin\phi,0)$, $0\leq\phi\leq2\pi$. Its tangent is $a(-\sin\phi,\cos\phi,0)$, while the restricted field is $(0,3a^2\cos\phi\sin\phi-a\cos\phi,0)$. Thus
$$
\iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot d\mathbf S
=\int_0^{2\pi}\left(3a^3\cos^2\phi\sin\phi-a^2\cos^2\phi\right)d\phi
=\boxed{-\pi a^2}.
$$
The first term integrates to zero over a full period and the second uses $\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\phi\,d\phi=\pi$.

One can also calculate directly on the curved <surface>. With <spherical coordinates> $0\leq\theta\leq\pi/2$, $0\leq\phi\leq2\pi$,
$$
d\mathbf S=a^2\sin\theta(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta)\,d\theta\,d\phi.
$$
Substitution gives the scalar integrand $a^3\sin^2\theta\cos\theta\sin\phi-a^2\sin\theta\cos\theta$. Its azimuthal <sine> term vanishes, and the rest gives $-2\pi a^2\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin\theta\cos\theta\,d\theta=-\pi a^2$, confirming the <orientation> and the same result.

For $m>-1$, integrate the base <power> to obtain $J(m)=\int_0^1x^m\,dx=1/(m+1)$. Since $\partial_m^n x^m=x^m(\log x)^n$, differentiating $n$ times gives

Use <differentiation under the integral sign>, justified by the integrable bound $|x\sin(tx)e^{-x^2/2}|\leq xe^{-x^2/2}$. This gives
$$
F'(t)=-\int_0^\infty x\sin(tx)e^{-x^2/2}\,dx
=\int_0^\infty\sin(tx)\,d(e^{-x^2/2}).
$$
<Integration by parts> makes the <boundary> term vanish: <sine> vanishes at zero and the Gaussian tends to zero at infinity. Therefore
$$
\boxed{F'(t)=-t\int_0^\infty\cos(tx)e^{-x^2/2}\,dx=-tF(t)}.
$$
The <integrating factor> $e^{t^2/2}$ makes $(e^{t^2/2}F)'=0$, so $F(t)=Ce^{-t^2/2}$. At $t=0$, the given half-line <Gaussian integral> fixes $C=\sqrt{\pi/2}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{F(t)=\sqrt{\frac\pi2}\,e^{-t^2/2}}.
$$
This is the even cosine-transform form of the <Fourier transform of a Gaussian>.
