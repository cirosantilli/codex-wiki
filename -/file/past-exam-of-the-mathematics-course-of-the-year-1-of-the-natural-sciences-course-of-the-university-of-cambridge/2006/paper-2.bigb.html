For real $x$, the angle $\arctan x$ lies in $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$ and has sine $x/\sqrt{1+x^2}$. Thus the principal <arcsine> in the expression equals the <arctangent> of $x$. Its <derivative> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac1{1+x^2},\qquad x\in\mathbb R.}
$$
The <chain rule> gives the same result directly, using $1-x^2/(1+x^2)=1/(1+x^2)$ with a positive square root.

The lower-order terms cancel, so the numerator must be expanded through sixth order. The <Taylor series> give $\sin(\sin x)=x-x^3/3+x^5/10+O(x^7)$ and $\sin^2x=x^2-x^4/3+2x^6/45+O(x^8)$. Therefore
$$
\frac{x\sin(\sin x)-\sin^2x}{x^4}=\frac{x^2}{18}+O(x^4).
$$
The **first nonzero term is $x^2/18$**, with the quotient continuously extended to zero if needed.

= Solution

Write $P=\mathbf a\mathbf a^T$. Since $\mathbf a$ is a <unit vector>, $P^2=\mathbf a(\mathbf a^T\mathbf a)\mathbf a^T=P$ and $P^T=P$. Thus this <Householder reflection> satisfies
$$
\boxed{A^2=(I-2P)^2=I,\qquad A^T=I-2P^T=A.}
$$
Geometrically, the mirror preserves the component tangent to its plane and reverses the normal component; doing it twice restores the original <vector>.

= b
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Expanding the two products of <Householder reflections> gives
$$
\boxed{BA-AB=4(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)(\mathbf b\mathbf a^T-\mathbf a\mathbf b^T).}
$$
The <commuting plane reflections> criterion follows: the operator vanishes if the normals are <orthogonal>, or if they are parallel. Conversely, if $c=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b\ne0$ and the operator vanishes, applying $\mathbf b\mathbf a^T-\mathbf a\mathbf b^T$ to $\mathbf a$ gives $\mathbf b-c\mathbf a=0$, proving parallelism.

This establishes equality of the reflected directions **for every incident ray** exactly in those two cases. For one fixed ray the claim needs that qualification: if nonparallel normals are not orthogonal, a ray along $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b$ has zero normal component for both mirrors and is unchanged in either order. Algebraically it lies in the <kernel> of the commutator; it is a special direction rather than a pair of commuting reflection operators.

= c
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

For an arbitrary <vector> $\mathbf x$, expansion in the <orthonormal basis> gives $x_i=(a_i a_j+b_i b_j+c_i c_j)x_j$, with summation over $j$. Since this holds for every $\mathbf x$, the <resolution of the identity in an orthonormal basis> is
$$
a_i a_j+b_i b_j+c_i c_j=\delta_{ij}.
$$
Let $P_a=\mathbf a\mathbf a^T$, and similarly for $b,c$. The three <orthogonal projection matrices> sum to $I$ and have pairwise products zero. Thus
$$
\boxed{(I-2P_c)(I-2P_b)(I-2P_a)=I-2(P_a+P_b+P_c)=-I,\qquad\mathbf s_{\rm out}=-\mathbf s_{\rm in}.}
$$
Any ray meeting all three ideal perpendicular mirrors therefore returns in the opposite direction, independently of reflection order. This is the geometry of a <corner reflector>, a kind of <retroreflector> used in <lunar laser ranging>.

Dividing by $f_z$ fixes the upward orientation even if the initially chosen gradient points downward. The restriction excludes a locally vertical graph, where this particular projected-area representation cannot be used.

For $x>0$, the kernel is smooth between the two positive endpoints. The <Leibniz integral rule> gives both endpoint terms and the parameter derivative:
$$
I'(x)=\frac{3\cos(x^4)-2\cos(x^3)}x-\int_{x^2}^{x^3}\sin(xt)dt.

The remaining integral equals $[\cos(x^3)-\cos(x^4)]/x$, so
$$
\boxed{I'(x)=\frac{4\cos(x^4)-3\cos(x^3)}x,\qquad x>0.}
$$
The substitution $u=xt$ gives the same result directly from limits $x^3,x^4$. Negative $x$ would make the original interval cross the nonintegrable $1/t$ singularity, so it is not part of this ordinary-integral domain.

{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For $n<x<n+1$, monotonicity gives $1/x<1/n<1/(x-1)$. Integrating over that unit interval proves the strict inequalities, equivalently $\ln(1+1/n)<1/n<\ln(n/(n-1))$ for $n>1$.

Let $H_N$ be the <harmonic number> and define $u_N=H_N-\ln N$, $v_N=H_N-\ln(N+1)$. The same integral comparison gives $u_{N+1}-u_N=1/(N+1)-\ln(1+1/N)<0$ and $v_{N+1}-v_N=1/(N+1)-\ln(1+1/(N+1))>0$. Also $v_N<u_N$ and $u_N-v_N=\ln(1+1/N)\to0$. The <monotone convergence theorem for sequences> therefore gives a common finite limit, <Euler's constant> $\gamma$.

The <integral bounds for Euler's constant> start at $v_1=1-\ln2$ and $u_1=1$. Strict monotonicity, already at the next index, makes the limit bounds strict:
$$
\boxed{0<1-\ln2<\gamma<1.}
$$
Positivity is explicit because $\ln2=\int_1^2dx/x<1$. This establishes the required limit without assuming its decimal value.

Separate variables away from the equilibrium solutions. Partial fractions give
$$
\frac1{a-b}\ln\left|\frac{y-a}{y-b}\right|=-x+C,\qquad\frac{y-a}{y-b}=K e^{(b-a)x}.
$$
Thus the <constant-coefficient Riccati equation> has real solution family
$$
\boxed{y=\frac{a e^{ax}-bK e^{bx}}{e^{ax}-K e^{bx}},\quad K\in\mathbb R,\qquad\text{together with }y=b,}
$$
on intervals where the denominator is nonzero. The value $K=0$ includes $y=a$. Setting $K=e^{(b-a)c}$ recovers the printed expression after factoring out $e^{ac}$. For real $c$ that substitution covers positive $K$ only; negative $K$ gives additional real branches between the equilibria, and the constant solutions must not be discarded when separating variables.

For the printed family, put $D(x)=e^{a(x+c)}-e^{b(x+c)}$. Its <logarithmic derivative> is $D'/D=y$, so $z'=yz$ integrates to
$$
\boxed{z=C_1\bigl[e^{a(x+c)}-e^{b(x+c)}\bigr].}
$$
This includes $z=0$ and is valid on each interval on which the corresponding $y$ is finite. For the full real family, write $z=Ae^{ax}+Be^{bx}$ and $y=z'/z$, with $(A,B)\ne(0,0)$. The pure exponentials include the two equilibrium values of $y$; arbitrary relative coefficient signs include the branches missing from real $c$ in the printed expression.

A <convergent series> $\sum_{n\geq1}u_n$ has <partial sums> $S_N=\sum_{n=1}^Nu_n$ tending to a finite limit $S$. Explicitly, for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is an integer $N_0$ such that $|S_N-S|<\varepsilon$ whenever $N\geq N_0$. The convergence of the individual terms to zero is necessary but is not sufficient for convergence of the series.

The <comparison test for series> states that if $0\leq u_n\leq v_n$ for all sufficiently large $n$, convergence of $\sum v_n$ implies convergence of $\sum u_n$. Equivalently, divergence of the smaller nonnegative series forces divergence of the larger one. Finitely many initial terms do not affect either convergence conclusion.

Applying the <multivariable chain rule> at fixed $y$ gives
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)_y=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\right)_v\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right)_y+\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial v}\right)_u\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\right)_y.}

The held-fixed variables distinguish the two coordinate descriptions; the same scalar function is being differentiated.

= Solution

Differentiate the first-order <chain rule> again, including derivatives of the coordinate coefficients. The <second derivatives under a two-variable coordinate change> are
$$
\boxed{f_{xx}=f_{uu}u_x^2+2f_{uv}u_xv_x+f_{vv}v_x^2+f_u\,u_{xx}+f_v\,v_{xx},}
$$
and
$$
\boxed{f_{yy}=f_{uu}u_y^2+2f_{uv}u_yv_y+f_{vv}v_y^2+f_u\,u_{yy}+f_v\,v_{yy}.}
$$
The last two terms account for derivatives of the coordinate functions; they vanish for an <affine map>. Smoothness justifies equality of the two mixed partial derivatives.

For <polar coordinates> with $u>0$, $\partial_x=\cos v\,\partial_u-(\sin v/u)\partial_v$ and $\partial_y=\sin v\,\partial_u+(\cos v/u)\partial_v$. Write $c=\cos v$, $s=\sin v$. Applying these operators twice, including their variable coefficients, gives the <Cartesian second derivatives in polar coordinates>:
$$
\boxed{f_{xx}=c^2f_{uu}-\frac{2sc}{u}f_{uv}+\frac{s^2}{u^2}f_{vv}+\frac{s^2}{u}f_u+\frac{2sc}{u^2}f_v,}
$$
$$
\boxed{f_{yy}=s^2f_{uu}+\frac{2sc}{u}f_{uv}+\frac{c^2}{u^2}f_{vv}+\frac{c^2}{u}f_u-\frac{2sc}{u^2}f_v.}
$$
In particular their sum is the <Laplacian in polar coordinates>, $f_{uu}+f_u/u+f_{vv}/u^2$.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $D=1+x^2+y^2$. The <gradient> has components $f_x=[D-2x(x+y)]/D^2$ and $f_y=[D-2y(x+y)]/D^2$. At a <critical point>, subtraction gives $(x-y)(x+y)=0$. The case $x+y=0$ cannot make either numerator zero, so $x=y=t$. The remaining equation is $1+2t^2=4t^2$, giving
$$
\boxed{(x,y)=(1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2),\quad f=1/\sqrt2;\qquad(x,y)=(-1/\sqrt2,-1/\sqrt2),\quad f=-1/\sqrt2.}
$$
These are the only stationary points; the denominator is positive everywhere.

= Solution

At $(t,t)$ with $t^2=1/2$, differentiating the <gradient> yields the <Hessian matrix> $H=-tI$. At the positive point it is <negative definite>, and at the negative point it is <positive-definite>; in both cases $f_{xy}=0$ and $\det H=1/2$. Therefore the **positive point is a maximum, the negative point is a minimum, and there are no saddle points**.

They are global extrema: with $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$, the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $|f|\leq\sqrt2r/(1+r^2)\leq1/\sqrt2$, with equality at exactly those two points. This is the extremum bound underlying the <level sets of a linear numerator over a radial quadratic>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

For a nonzero level $h$, completing the square gives
$$
\boxed{\left(x-\frac1{2h}\right)^2+\left(y-\frac1{2h}\right)^2=\frac1{2h^2}-1.}
$$
Thus the <level sets of a linear numerator over a radial quadratic> are circles centred on $y=x$, real only for $0<|h|\leq1/\sqrt2$. The extreme levels shrink to points. The zero contour is the straight line $x+y=0$, separating positive and negative heights; near it, small nonzero levels are large circles. Heights tend to zero at infinity.
