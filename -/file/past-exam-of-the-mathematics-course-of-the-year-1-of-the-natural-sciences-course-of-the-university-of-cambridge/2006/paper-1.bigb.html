Write $P=I-B$, $Q=I+B$, so $P^T=Q$ and $Q^T=P$. The factors commute because both are <polynomials> in $B$. Also $P$ has a <matrix inverse> since it is the <transpose> of the invertible $Q$. Thus the <Cayley transform of a real skew-symmetric matrix>, with its parameter replaced by $-B$, satisfies
$$
C^TC=P^{-1}QQ^{-1}P=I.
$$
Hence $C$ is an <orthogonal matrix>. Its inverse is $Q P^{-1}$; this lets us distinguish the <symmetric and skew-symmetric Cayley transforms> below.

The <characteristic polynomial> is $\det(\lambda I-M)=\lambda(\lambda^2-2)$. Solving the three <eigenvector> equations gives
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\sqrt2,\ 0,\ -\sqrt2;\qquad v_+=(1,\sqrt2,1)^T,\quad v_0=(1,0,-1)^T,\quad v_-=(1,-\sqrt2,1)^T.}
$$
Each <eigenspace> consists of the <scalar multiples> of its displayed nonzero <vector>. Their pairwise <dot products> vanish: $v_+\cdot v_-=1-2+1=0$ and $v_\pm\cdot v_0=1-1=0$. This also agrees with the <orthogonal diagonalization of a real symmetric matrix>.

For a sufficiently smooth <vector field> on an oriented piecewise-smooth <surface> $S$ with boundary $\partial S$, <Stokes theorem> states
$$
\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS.
$$
Here $\mathbf n$ is the chosen <unit normal>, $dS$ is the scalar surface-area element, and $d\mathbf r$ follows the positively oriented boundary. The <boundary orientation> is fixed by the right-hand rule: the fingers curl along $\partial S$ when the thumb points along $\mathbf n$.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Orient the triangle by the path $A\to B\to C\to A$. The <cross product> $(B-A)\times(C-A)=(0,1,0)$ gives the positive <normal vector>. In the plane $y=1$, the triangular region is $x,z\geq0$, $x+z\leq1$. The surface integral in <Stokes theorem> is therefore
$$
\int_0^1\int_0^{1-z}4xz^2\,dx\,dz=2\int_0^1z^2(1-z)^2\,dz=\frac1{15}.
$$
For the boundary <line integral>, $AB$ has $z=0$, so contributes zero. On $BC$, $x=0$ and only $dz$ is nonzero, so its contribution is also zero. Parametrize $CA$ by $(x,y,z)=(t,1,1-t)$, $0\leq t\leq1$. Its contribution is
$$
\int_0^1[2t(1-t)^3-t^2(1-t)^2]dt=\frac1{15}.
$$
Thus **both oriented integrals equal $1/15$**. Reversing the orientation would reverse both signs.

The <scalar triple product> is a <determinant>:
$$
(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)\cdot\mathbf c=\det(\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c).
$$
A cyclic permutation of its three columns is an <even permutation>, so it leaves the determinant unchanged. Thus $\det(\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c)=\det(\mathbf b,\mathbf c,\mathbf a)=\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)$, proving the identity.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Use the <vector triple product identity> twice:
$$
(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)\times\mathbf c=\mathbf b(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)-\mathbf a(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf c),\qquad \mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)=\mathbf b(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)-\mathbf c(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b).
$$
Subtracting cancels the common term, yielding
$$
\boxed{(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)\times\mathbf c-\mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)=\mathbf c(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)-\mathbf a(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf c).}
$$
It is generally nonzero: the <cross product> does not satisfy the <associative property>.

For $B$ to make a first throw, $A$ must first pass, with <probability> $1/2$. For $B$ then to win on that throw, $B$ must roll one of two winning faces, with probability $1/3$. The throws are <independent>, giving

A first throw of $3$ by $A$ would make $B$ win without throwing, and is excluded by the requested event.

Before $A$'s second throw, $A$ can win either immediately, with <probability> $1/3$, or by passing to $B$ and having $B$ throw $3$, with probability $(1/2)(1/6)$. These are disjoint events, so
$$
\boxed{P=\frac13+\frac1{12}=\frac5{12}.}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

This is a <two-turn renewal game>. A full pair of throws has probability $(1/2)^2=1/4$ of passing twice and returning to the original state. The probability that $A$ wins before that restart is $5/12$. Thus <first-step analysis> gives $p=5/12+p/4$, or
$$
\boxed{P(A\text{ wins})=\frac59.}
$$
Equivalently sum the <geometric series> $(5/12)\sum_{j\geq0}(1/4)^j$. The probability of surviving infinitely many pairs is zero, so the eventual winner is well defined with probability one.

For the <Lagrange multiplier> equations, use $\mathcal L=x^2y+2y-\lambda(x^2+y^2-1)$. They give $2x(y-\lambda)=0$, $x^2+2=2\lambda y$, and $x^2+y^2=1$. If $x\ne0$, then $\lambda=y$, implying $x^2+2=2y^2$ and therefore $y^2=1$, contradicting $x\ne0$. Thus only $x=0$, $y=\pm1$ remain.

The circle is <compact> and the function is <continuous>, so global extrema exist. On it, eliminating $x^2$ gives $f=3y-y^3$, which is increasing for $-1<y<1$ since its <derivative> is $3(1-y^2)>0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{f_{\max}=2\text{ at }(0,1),\qquad f_{\min}=-2\text{ at }(0,-1).}
$$
These constrained extrema are flat to quadratic order along the circle, so a vanishing second derivative alone would not classify them.

Minimize $\frac12\|\mathbf r\|^2$ subject to $\mathbf n_1\cdot\mathbf r=1$, $\mathbf n_2\cdot\mathbf r=2$, where $\mathbf n_1=(2,1,-1)$ and $\mathbf n_2=(1,-1,1)$. The <Lagrange multiplier> equation gives $\mathbf r=\lambda\mathbf n_1+\mu\mathbf n_2$. Since $\mathbf n_1\cdot\mathbf n_2=0$, $\|\mathbf n_1\|^2=6$, and $\|\mathbf n_2\|^2=3$, the constraints imply $\lambda=1/6$, $\mu=2/3$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbf r_*=(1,-1/2,1/2).}
$$
No explicit parametrization of the line was needed. For any other feasible point, $\mathbf h=\mathbf r-\mathbf r_*$ is <orthogonal> to both normals and therefore to $\mathbf r_*$. The <Pythagorean theorem> gives $\|\mathbf r\|^2=\|\mathbf r_*\|^2+\|\mathbf h\|^2$, proving the unique global minimum. This is the geometric principle behind the <minimum-norm least-squares solution>.

<Taylor's theorem> expands about $a$ in powers of $h=x-a$. With the usual differentiability assumptions, its finite form with <Lagrange remainder> is
$$
f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k+\frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1},

where $\xi$ lies between $a$ and $x$. If the remainder tends to zero as $n\to\infty$, this yields the <Taylor series> $f(x)=\sum_{k\geq0}f^{(k)}(a)(x-a)^k/k!$. Smoothness alone does not guarantee that infinite-series equality.

= Solution

Because the function is <even>, all sine <Fourier coefficients> vanish. Its average is zero, so $a_0=0$. The <Fourier cosine coefficients of a sine mode> follow from the product-to-sum identity:
$$
a_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi\sin2x\cos nx\,dx=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac8{\pi(4-n^2)},&n\text{ odd},\\0,&n\text{ even}.\end{cases}
$$
For $n=2$, the integral is directly zero rather than an undefined substitution into a denominator. Thus
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\frac8\pi\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{\cos((2m+1)x)}{4-(2m+1)^2}.}
$$
The <Fourier series> equals the continuous function at every point, including the corners. Its coefficients decay quadratically, so the series is also <absolutely convergent>.

For <Laplace's equation>, seek a nonzero separated product $f=X(x)Y(y)$. Dividing by $XY$ gives $X''/X=-Y''/Y$, so both sides are a <separation constant>. Choosing it as $-k^2$ gives $X''+k^2X=0$, $Y''-k^2Y=0$, with trigonometric and <hyperbolic functions>, respectively. A zero constant gives linear factors; a positive constant interchanges the two types. <Boundary conditions> determine which modes are allowed, and <linearity> permits their superposition to match the remaining boundary data.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The zero values at $x=0,a$ select $X_n=\sin(n\pi x/a)$, and the zero value at $y=0$ selects $Y_n=\sinh(n\pi y/a)$. Expanding the constant upper-edge value as a <Fourier sine series> gives coefficients $b_n=2F[1-(-1)^n]/(n\pi)$. The resulting <harmonic function in a rectangle with one constant boundary value> is
$$
\boxed{f(x,y)=\frac{4F}{\pi}\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{\sin((2m+1)\pi x/a)}{2m+1}\frac{\sinh((2m+1)\pi y/a)}{\sinh((2m+1)\pi)},\qquad0<x,y<a.}
$$
Each term satisfies <Laplace's equation> and the three zero boundary values. At $y=a$, the series equals $F$ for $0<x<a$. On compact subsets of the interior it and its derivatives converge exponentially, which justifies the termwise equation.

For $F\ne0$, the top-corner boundary prescriptions conflict: a corner cannot be both zero on a vertical edge and $F$ on the upper edge. The bounded interior solution has the specified limits along the open sides; it has no single continuous extension at those two corners. Values assigned only at the isolated corners do not change the interior solution.
