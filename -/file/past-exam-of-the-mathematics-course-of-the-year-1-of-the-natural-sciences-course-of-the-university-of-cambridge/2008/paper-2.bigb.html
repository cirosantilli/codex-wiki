Write $\boldsymbol\Delta=\boldsymbol d-\boldsymbol b$. At an intersection, $\boldsymbol\Delta=\lambda\boldsymbol a-\mu\boldsymbol c$, so its <scalar triple product> with $\boldsymbol a,\boldsymbol c$ vanishes. The expression in the question is exactly
$$
\boldsymbol a\cdot(\boldsymbol c\times\boldsymbol d)+\boldsymbol c\cdot(\boldsymbol a\times\boldsymbol b)
=\boldsymbol\Delta\cdot(\boldsymbol a\times\boldsymbol c)=0.
$$
Under a shift of origin by $\boldsymbol h$, the direction vectors $\boldsymbol a,\boldsymbol c$ stay fixed, while $\boldsymbol b,\boldsymbol d$ both change by $-\boldsymbol h$. Thus $\boldsymbol\Delta$ and the condition are invariant. For nonparallel lines this is also sufficient for intersection; for parallel lines it only asserts coplanarity and need not imply intersection.

Put $\delta\lambda=\lambda_2-\lambda_1$ and $\delta\mu=\mu_2-\mu_1$. Then $\boldsymbol x_2-\boldsymbol x_1=\delta\lambda\boldsymbol a$ and $\boldsymbol x_4-\boldsymbol x_1=(\boldsymbol x_3-\boldsymbol x_1)+\delta\mu\boldsymbol c$. Bilinearity of the <cross product> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
[(\boldsymbol x_3-\boldsymbol x_1)\times(\boldsymbol x_4-\boldsymbol x_1)]\cdot(\boldsymbol x_2-\boldsymbol x_1)
&=\delta\mu\,\delta\lambda\,[(\boldsymbol\Delta+\mu_1\boldsymbol c-\lambda_1\boldsymbol a)\times\boldsymbol c]\cdot\boldsymbol a\\
&=-\delta\mu\,\delta\lambda\,\boldsymbol\Delta\cdot(\boldsymbol a\times\boldsymbol c)\\
&=\boldsymbol\Delta\cdot[(\boldsymbol x_4-\boldsymbol x_3)\times(\boldsymbol x_2-\boldsymbol x_1)].
\end{aligned}
$$
The <tetrahedron> volume is one sixth of the <absolute value> of this triple product. Sliding either vertex pair along its line preserves $|\delta\lambda|$ or $|\delta\mu|$, because its separation is fixed and the direction vectors are fixed. Thus the volume is unchanged, including the zero-volume coplanar case. This is <tetrahedron volume under sliding opposite edges>.

The integrand is even and decays at both infinity and zero, since $a,b>0$. On $x>0$ set $u=\sqrt a\,x-\sqrt b/x$, a strictly increasing bijection onto the whole real line. Then
$$
ax^2+b/x^2=u^2+2\sqrt{ab},\qquad
x=\frac{u+\sqrt{u^2+4\sqrt{ab}}}{2\sqrt a},\qquad
\frac{dx}{du}=\frac1{2\sqrt a}\left(1+\frac u{\sqrt{u^2+4\sqrt{ab}}}\right).
$$
Using evenness for the negative half-axis gives
$$
I(a,b)=\frac{e^{-\sqrt{ab}}}{\sqrt a}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-u^2/2}\left(1+\frac u{\sqrt{u^2+4\sqrt{ab}}}\right)du.
$$
The second summand is an integrable <odd function>, so its integral vanishes. The remaining <Gaussian integral> gives
$$
\boxed{I(a,b)=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}a}\,e^{-\sqrt{ab}}.}
$$
For the second integral use $x=1/t$ separately on each half-axis. The factor $x^{-2}dx$ becomes $-dt$, and reversing the transformed limits yields $J(a,b)=I(b,a)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{J(a,b)=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}b}\,e^{-\sqrt{ab}}.}
$$
These identities are instances of the <Gaussian integral with an inverse-square term>. Splitting at zero before either <change of variables> avoids treating a discontinuous transformation as a single bijection.

Write $h_n=a_n\cos(n\pi x/L)+b_n\sin(n\pi x/L)$. For the partial sum $S_N=a_0/2+\sum_{n=1}^Nh_n$, ordinary multiplication gives

$$

For a bounded region $V$ with piecewise smooth closed boundary $S$, outward unit <normal vector> $\boldsymbol n$ and continuously differentiable <vector field> $\boldsymbol F$, the <divergence theorem> states $\int_S\boldsymbol F\cdot\boldsymbol n\,dS=\int_V\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol F\,dV$. Here $dS$ is scalar <surface area>, $dV$ is <volume>, and $d\boldsymbol S=\boldsymbol n\,dS$ is the oriented <area element>.

For $\boldsymbol F=\boldsymbol r$, its <divergence> is three. The <ellipsoid> has semiaxes $a,a,b$, so its <volume> is $4\pi a^2b/3$, obtained by scaling the unit ball. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\int_S\boldsymbol r\cdot d\boldsymbol S=3\operatorname{Vol}(V)=4\pi a^2b.}
$$

Since $\boldsymbol a$ is constant, componentwise differentiation gives $\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol a\phi)=\sum_ia_i\partial_i\phi=\boldsymbol a\cdot\nabla\phi$. Applying the <divergence theorem> to this <vector field> gives
$$
\boldsymbol a\cdot\int_S\phi\,d\boldsymbol S=\boldsymbol a\cdot\int_V\nabla\phi\,dV.

This holds for every constant <vector> $\boldsymbol a$, so the vector integrals agree. This is the <vector gradient form of the divergence theorem>. With $\phi=z$, the <gradient> is the constant unit vector $\boldsymbol e_z$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\int_S z\,d\boldsymbol S=\frac{4\pi a^2b}3\,\boldsymbol e_z.}

The identity $\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol a\times\boldsymbol v)=\boldsymbol v\cdot(\nabla\times\boldsymbol a)-\boldsymbol a\cdot(\nabla\times\boldsymbol v)$ reduces to $-\boldsymbol a\cdot(\nabla\times\boldsymbol v)$ when $\boldsymbol a$ is constant. In components, this follows by differentiating $\varepsilon_{ijk}a_jv_k$ and using the antisymmetry of the <Levi-Civita symbol>. The <divergence theorem> and the cyclic <scalar triple product> identity now give
$$
\boldsymbol a\cdot\int_S\boldsymbol v\times d\boldsymbol S
=\int_S(\boldsymbol a\times\boldsymbol v)\cdot d\boldsymbol S
=-\boldsymbol a\cdot\int_V\nabla\times\boldsymbol v\,dV.
$$

$$
\boxed{\int_S\boldsymbol v\times d\boldsymbol S=-\int_V\nabla\times\boldsymbol v\,dV.}
$$
The order of the <cross product> determines the minus sign, as in the <vector curl form of the divergence theorem>.

\boxed{a_n=\frac2{n\pi c}\int_0^lv(x)\sin(n\pi x/l)\,dx.}
$$
The evaluation property of the given initial velocity means $v(x)=\delta(x-l/2)$ in the sense of a <Dirac delta>, an idealized <impulsively struck fixed-end string>. Thus $a_n=2\sin(n\pi/2)/(n\pi c)$: every even mode vanishes, while the lowest two excited modes have $n=1,3$. Their ordinary <frequencies> and amplitude ratio are
$$
\boxed{f_1=\frac c{2l},\quad f_3=\frac{3c}{2l},\qquad |a_1|:|a_3|=3:1.}
$$
The signed coefficient ratio is $a_3/a_1=-1/3$. The impulse solution is interpreted as a <distributional weak solution>, rather than one with a smooth initial velocity.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For a separated <harmonic function> $V=F(x)G(y)$, <Laplace's equation> gives $F''/F=-G''/G=-k^2$. Bounded periodic dependence in $x$ has $F=A\cos(kx)+B\sin(kx)$; decay on each half-plane selects $G=e^{-k|y|}$ for $k>0$, when the potential is matched continuously at the sheet. These are the separated decaying modes. General decaying solutions can be superpositions of them; decay alone does not force a single value of $k$.

The prescribed cosine density selects $k=1$ and $V=C\cos x\,e^{-|y|}$. Its normal <derivative> jump is $-2C\cos x$. Matching the stated jump $2\pi\Sigma_0\cos x$ gives
$$
\boxed{V(x,y)=-\pi\Sigma_0\cos x\,e^{-|y|}.}
$$
This is the <gravitational potential of a sinusoidal sheet> in the normalization of the question. It is continuous at the sheet and decays on both sides. Since $\Sigma_0\cos x$ changes sign, it can physically describe a Fourier component of a density perturbation rather than an everywhere positive isolated mass sheet.
