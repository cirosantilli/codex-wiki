Since $\boldsymbol n\cdot\boldsymbol n=1$, the <vector> $K\boldsymbol r$ is perpendicular to $\boldsymbol n$:
$$
\boldsymbol n\cdot K\boldsymbol r=\boldsymbol n\cdot\boldsymbol r-(\boldsymbol n\cdot\boldsymbol r)(\boldsymbol n\cdot\boldsymbol n)=0.
$$
It follows that $K^2\boldsymbol r=K\boldsymbol r$. If $\boldsymbol r=t\boldsymbol n$, then $K\boldsymbol r=0$; if $\boldsymbol n\cdot\boldsymbol r=0$, then $K\boldsymbol r=\boldsymbol r$. Thus $K=I-\boldsymbol n\boldsymbol n^T$ is the <orthogonal projection matrix> onto $\boldsymbol n^\perp$: it removes exactly the component normal to the plane.

For the equally inclined plane choose $\boldsymbol n=(1,1,1)/\sqrt3$. Reversing its sign gives the same plane; other sign choices give equivalent reflections of the coordinate axes. One suitable <orthonormal basis> of this plane is
$$
\boldsymbol e_1=\frac{(1,1,-2)}{\sqrt6},\qquad \boldsymbol e_2=\frac{(-1,1,0)}{\sqrt2}.
$$
Their <dot products> with $\boldsymbol n$ vanish, their lengths are one, and their mutual <dot product> is zero. The equal first two components of $\boldsymbol e_1$ give its equal inclinations to $\boldsymbol i,\boldsymbol j$. Because $\boldsymbol e_j$ is already in the projection plane, $\boldsymbol e_j\cdot K\boldsymbol r=\boldsymbol e_j\cdot\boldsymbol r$. The coordinates of the projected point are therefore
$$
\boxed{u=\frac{x+y-2z}{\sqrt6},\qquad v=\frac{-x+y}{\sqrt2}.}
$$
For a <circle> of radius $R$ centered at the origin in $z=0$, its equation $x^2+y^2=R^2$ becomes $3u^2+v^2=R^2$. This is an <ellipse> with semiaxes $R/\sqrt3$ and $R$. Translating the original center only translates its <orthogonal projection>, so the result applies to every circle in that plane. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\frac{\text{minor axis}}{\text{major axis}}=\frac1{\sqrt3}.}
$$
The general reason is the <orthogonal projection of a circle between planes>: the direction common to the planes retains its length, while the perpendicular direction is shortened by the cosine of their angle.

For an integrable <discrete random variable>, its <expectation> and <variance> are $\mu=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}np_n$ and $\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}(n-\mu)^2p_n$, respectively. The first formula requires $\sum|n|p_n<\infty$ for a finite ordinary mean; the variance may be infinite. A <probability density function> gives $P(\alpha<Y<\beta)=\int_\alpha^\beta f(y)\,dy$.

Conditioning on $X$ and using <independence> gives
$$
P(\alpha<X+Y<\beta)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}p_n\int_{\alpha-n}^{\beta-n}f(y)\,dy
=\int_\alpha^\beta\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}p_nf(z-n)\,dz.
$$
The interchange is justified by <Tonelli's theorem>, since all integrands are nonnegative. Consequently $Z$ has an absolutely continuous distribution, with
$$
\boxed{g(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}p_nf(z-n).}
$$
Its integral is $\sum_np_n=1$. This is the <convolution of independent random variables>, specifically a <Gaussian smoothing of an integer-valued distribution> for the given normal density. The specified weights are normalized because $2(3/\pi^2)\sum_{n\geq1}n^{-2}=1$.

The density is the symmetric sum
$$
g(z)=\frac3{\pi^2\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\sum_{n\ne0}\frac{e^{-(z-n)^2/(2\sigma^2)}}{n^2}.
$$
For small $\sigma$, the <normal distributions> are narrow and well separated, giving peaks near each nonzero integer. The largest two peaks lie near $-1$ and $1$; those near $\pm2$ have approximately one quarter of their height, and the envelope falls as $1/n^2$. There is no component centered at zero, and the density there is exponentially small. The left panel shows the resulting smooth comb.

= Solution

For large $\sigma$, neighboring peaks overlap and the density has a broad, smooth, symmetric central hump, approximately $(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{-1}e^{-z^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ on the scale $z=O(\sigma)$. A precise central-profile statement follows from
$$
\sigma g(\sigma t)=\mathbb E\left[\frac{e^{-(t-X/\sigma)^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right]\longrightarrow\frac{e^{-t^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}:
$$
for each fixed $t$, $X/\sigma\to0$ almost surely and the integrand is bounded, so <dominated convergence> applies. The right panel of the preceding figure shows this Gaussian-looking core.

This approximation does not remove the <heavy-tailed distribution>. Here $\mathbb E|X|=(6/\pi^2)\sum_{n\geq1}1/n=\infty$, so the symmetric distribution has **no ordinary mean and no finite variance**. Adding the independent Gaussian, which has finite absolute moments, leaves these conclusions unchanged. In particular a central Gaussian sketch cannot justify assigning variance $\sigma^2$ to $Z$.

On an interval avoiding zero put $v=y/x$. A real solution requires $|v|\leq1$, and the principal <square root> is $\sqrt{x^2-y^2}=|x|\sqrt{1-v^2}$. The <homogeneous first-order differential equation> reduces to
$$
v'=\frac{\sqrt{1-v^2}}{|x|}.
$$
Where $|v|<1$, <separation of variables> gives $\arcsin v=\operatorname{sgn}(x)\log|x|+C$. Thus the nonconstant pieces are
$$
\boxed{y=x\sin\bigl(\operatorname{sgn}(x)\log|x|+C\bigr),}
$$
restricted to intervals where the cosine of the phase is nonnegative. This sign condition is essential when substituting back into the original <square root>.

There are also the solutions $y=x$ and $y=-x$. They can join the nonconstant pieces with a continuous first <derivative>, because the transformed rate vanishes but is not <Lipschitz continuous> at $v=\pm1$. For example, on the positive axis every $A>0$ gives
$$
y(x)=\begin{cases}-x,&0<x\leq A,\\x\sin\bigl(\log(x/A)-\pi/2\bigr),&A<x<Ae^\pi,\\x,&x\geq Ae^\pi.\end{cases}
$$
The <sticking solutions of a bounded square-root differential equation> describe the same joining mechanism on either half-axis after using the increasing variable $t=\operatorname{sgn}(x)\log|x|$.

For functions with the required <derivatives>, the <Leibniz rule> is
$$
(fg)^{(n)}=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk f^{(k)}g^{(n-k)}.
$$
At $n=0$ it is immediate. If it holds at $n$, differentiating each summand gives two sums. The coefficient of $f^{(k)}g^{(n+1-k)}$ after regrouping is $\binom nk+\binom n{k-1}=\binom{n+1}k$ by <Pascal's identity>, including the two endpoint terms. This proves the formula by <mathematical induction>.

Apply it to $(fg)h$ and then expand the derivatives of $fg$. For $p+q+r=n$, the resulting coefficient is $\binom n{p+q}\binom{p+q}p=n!/(p!q!r!)$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{(fgh)^{(n)}=\sum_{p+q+r=n}\frac{n!}{p!q!r!}f^{(p)}g^{(q)}h^{(r)},}
$$
where $p,q,r$ range over nonnegative integers. This is the three-factor <generalized Leibniz rule>.

For real square-integrable $f,g$, nonnegativity of $\int(f-\lambda g)^2dx$ gives a quadratic in the real number $\lambda$. If $\int g^2dx>0$, choose $\lambda=\int fg\,dx/\int g^2dx$ to obtain
$$
\left(\int fg\,dx\right)^2\leq\left(\int f^2dx\right)\left(\int g^2dx\right).
$$
If $\int g^2dx=0$, then $g=0$ almost everywhere and the same inequality is immediate. This proves the integral <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>.

Put $T=t_f-t_i>0$ and $L=x_f-x_i>0$. The forward <speed> satisfies $L=\int_{t_i}^{t_f}v\,dt$, so $V_t=L/T$. The <change of variables> $dx=v\,dt$ gives $V_x=L^{-1}\int_{t_i}^{t_f}v^2dt$. Apply the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> to $v$ and the constant function one:
$$
L^2\leq T\int_{t_i}^{t_f}v^2dt,
\qquad\boxed{V_x\geq V_t.}
$$
Equality holds exactly when $v$ is constant almost everywhere. Equivalently, the <time and distance averages of speed> satisfy $V_x-V_t=\operatorname{Var}_t(v)/V_t$, which identifies the excess as a measure of speed variation.
