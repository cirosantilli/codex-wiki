Find two numbers with sum $-1$ and product $-2$: they are $-2$ and $1$. Thus the <quadratic polynomial> factorizes as
$$
\boxed{x^2-x-2=(x-2)(x+1).}
$$
The product is negative precisely when its two factors have opposite signs. This occurs between its two <polynomial roots>, so
$$
\boxed{-1<x<2.}
$$
The endpoints give zero and are excluded by the strict inequality.

Using the <Pythagorean trigonometric identity> and the <double-angle formula>,
$$
\sin^4\theta+\cos^4\theta=(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta)^2-2\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta=1-\frac12\sin^2(2\theta).
$$
The required value $1/2$ therefore occurs when $\sin^2(2\theta)=1$. Within $0\le\theta\le\pi$, the only possibilities are $2\theta=\pi/2$ and $3\pi/2$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\theta=\frac\pi4\quad\text{or}\quad\frac{3\pi}4.}
$$
Equivalently equality requires $\sin^2\theta=\cos^2\theta=1/2$.

For $f(x)=xe^{-2x^2}$, the <product rule> and <chain rule> give
$$
f'(x)=e^{-2x^2}(1-4x^2).
$$
The positive exponential factor makes $f$ increasing for $0<x<1/2$ and decreasing for $1/2<x<1$. Its only interior <stationary point> is therefore a maximum at $x=1/2$. Checking it together with both endpoints,
$$
f(0)=0,\qquad f(1/2)=\frac12e^{-1/2},\qquad f(1)=e^{-2}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\min_{[0,1]}f=0\text{ at }x=0,\qquad\max_{[0,1]}f=\frac1{2\sqrt e}\text{ at }x=\frac12.}
$$
Checking the endpoints is necessary for extrema on a <closed interval>.

In <spherical coordinates>, with polar angle $0\le\theta\le\pi$ and azimuth $0\le\varphi<2\pi$, the <volume element> is
$$
\boxed{dV=r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi.}
$$
The <mass density> is radial, so integrating over both angles gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
M&=4\pi A\int_0^R(1-e^{-r/r_0})\,dr\\
&=4\pi A\left[R-r_0(1-e^{-R/r_0})\right].
\end{aligned}
$$
Writing $q=r_0/R>0$, this becomes
$$
\boxed{M=4\pi ARf(q),\qquad f(q)=1-q+qe^{-1/q}.}
$$
Although the <mass density> diverges like $A/(r_0r)$ near the origin, multiplication by the spherical <volume element> gives an integrable expression. Thus the central singularity does not produce infinite <mass>.

For $q=r_0/R\to\infty$, expand the <exponential function> in powers of $1/q$:
$$
e^{-1/q}=1-\frac1q+\frac1{2q^2}-\frac1{6q^3}+\frac1{24q^4}+O(q^{-5}).
$$
The constant and linear terms cancel in $1-q+qe^{-1/q}$, giving
$$
\boxed{f(q)=\frac1{2q}-\frac1{6q^2}+\frac1{24q^3}+O(q^{-4})\sim\frac1{2q}.}
$$
For $q\to0^+$, $e^{-1/q}$ is exponentially small:
$$
\boxed{f(q)=1-q+O(qe^{-1/q})\sim1-q,\qquad f(q)\to1.}
$$
The small-$q$ correction $qe^{-1/q}$ is smaller than every positive power of $q$ beyond the displayed linear term. In dimensional terms, the large-$q$ leading <mass> is $2\pi AR^2/r_0$, while the small-$q$ limit is $4\pi AR$.

Use <cylindrical coordinates> $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$. The <Jacobian determinant> gives
$$
\boxed{dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz.}
$$
The inequalities imply $-d<z<0$ and $0\le r<a\sqrt{1+z/d}$. Thus the <volume> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
V&=\int_{-d}^0\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{a\sqrt{1+z/d}}r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz\\

Equivalently, integrating vertically first gives $V=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^a\int_{d(r^2/a^2-1)}^0r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta$. The region is bounded by an <elliptic paraboloid> and its top disc; its <volume> is half that of the cylinder with base radius $a$ and height $d$. Including or excluding the boundary surfaces does not change the <volume>.

= Solution

First retain the equilibrium solution $\boxed{y\equiv0}$. Any nonzero real solution has constant sign on its interval of existence, since the polynomial right-hand side is <locally Lipschitz> and <Picard-Lindelöf theorem> prevents crossing the zero solution.

For a nonzero solution, the <Bernoulli differential equation> substitution $u=y^{-4}$ gives
$$
u'=-4y^{-5}y'=-8u-16.
$$
This <linear ordinary differential equation> has solution $u+2=Ce^{-8x}$. Therefore every nonzero real solution is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\pm\bigl(Ce^{-8x}-2\bigr)^{-1/4},\qquad C>0,\quad x<\frac18\ln\frac C2.}
$$
Either sign may be chosen and remains fixed. The restriction ensures $y^{-4}>0$; nonpositive $C$ gives no nonzero real solution. These are maximal real intervals, and their restrictions are also solutions. As the right endpoint is approached, $|y|$ diverges in <finite-time blowup>. The substitution would have lost $y=0$ if it had not been checked separately.

For the <force> magnitude in <Newton's law of universal gravitation>, the <total differential> is
$$
\boxed{dF=\frac{Gm_2}{r^2}\,dm_1+\frac{Gm_1}{r^2}\,dm_2-\frac{2Gm_1m_2}{r^3}\,dr.}

Dividing by the positive value $F$ gives the particularly useful relative form
$$
\boxed{\frac{dF}{F}=\frac{dm_1}{m_1}+\frac{dm_2}{m_2}-2\frac{dr}{r}.}
$$
For finite small changes this gives the first-order approximation to the fractional change; higher-order products of increments are omitted.

Put $D=\sigma^2>0$ and $\xi=x-x_0$. Logarithmic <differentiation> of the positive <Gaussian function> gives
$$
\phi_t=\left(-\frac1{2t}+\frac{\xi^2}{4Dt^2}\right)\phi,\qquad\phi_x=-\frac{\xi}{2Dt}\phi.

For $f$ sufficiently differentiable on the interval between $a$ and $x$, <Taylor's theorem> with its first $n$ terms reads
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k+R_n(x),\qquad R_n(x)=\frac{f^{(n)}(\xi)}{n!}(x-a)^n,}
$$
where $\xi$ lies between $a$ and $x$. This is the <Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder>. For example, $|f^{(n)}|\le M$ on that interval gives $|R_n(x)|\le M|x-a|^n/n!$. The finite formula does not assume that an infinite <Taylor series> converges to the function; that further conclusion requires the remainders to tend to zero.

The <Maclaurin series> of the <hyperbolic cosine> and <cosine> are

Put $w=e^x-1=x+x^2/2+x^3/6+O(x^4)$. The local <logarithmic series> $\ln(1-w)=-w-w^2/2-w^3/3+O(w^4)$ gives
$$
\boxed{\ln(2-e^x)=-x-x^2-x^3+O(x^4).}
$$
For the infinite <Taylor series>, it is necessary to distinguish the real domain of the logarithm, $x<\ln2$, from its convergence interval about zero. Write $a=\ln2$. The complex zeros of $2-e^z$ are $a+2\pi i k$ for integers $k$, so the nearest <logarithmic singularity> to zero is at $a$. The <radius of convergence> is therefore $a$, giving convergence for $|x|<a$ and divergence for $|x|>a$.

The two endpoints need separate checking. Factor off the nearest singularity:
$$
h(z)=\ln(1-z/a)+H(z),\qquad H(z)=\ln\left(\frac{2-e^z}{1-z/a}\right),\qquad H(0)=0.
$$
The quotient has a nonzero removable value $2a$ at $z=a$; its other zeros are at $a+2\pi i k$ with $k\ne0$. Consequently it has a <holomorphic logarithm> on a zero-free disk larger than $|z|\le a$, which is a <simply connected domain>, and the <Taylor series> of $H$ converges absolutely at both real endpoints. At $x=-a$, the remaining series $-\sum_{n\ge1}(-1)^n/n$ converges by the <alternating series test>. At $x=a$, it is $-\sum_{n\ge1}1/n$ and diverges. Thus the full real convergence set is
$$
\boxed{-\ln2\le x<\ln2.}
$$
Convergence is absolute in the open interval and conditional at the left endpoint, where the sum equals $\ln(3/2)$. This endpoint distinction is an instance of <isolating a logarithmic singularity to test a power-series endpoint>.

Assume the three standard dice are fair and <independent>. For one die $X$, a <discrete uniform distribution> on $1,\ldots,6$ gives
