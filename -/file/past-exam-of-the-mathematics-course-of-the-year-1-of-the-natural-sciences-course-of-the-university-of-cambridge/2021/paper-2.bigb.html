The two orthogonal directions $(1,1)^T$ and $(1,-1)^T$ are preserved by this <symmetric matrix>. Direct multiplication, using the <hyperbolic function> identities $\cosh u\pm\sinh u=e^{\pm u}$, gives the <eigenvalue>–<eigenvector> pairs
$$
\boxed{\lambda_+=e^u,\quad \boldsymbol v_+=\binom11;
\qquad \lambda_-=e^{-u},\quad \boldsymbol v_-=\binom1{-1}.}

Each <eigenvector> can be multiplied by any nonzero scalar. For $u\ne0$ these give the two one-dimensional <eigenspaces>. When $u=0$, the <matrix> is the identity: the only <eigenvalue> is $1$, with multiplicity two, and every nonzero <vector> is an <eigenvector>.

Complete the square in the velocity variable:
$$
H=V(x)+pv-\frac12mv^2
=V(x)+\frac{p^2}{2m}-\frac m2\left(v-\frac pm\right)^2.
$$
Since $m>0$, the final term is nonpositive and vanishes at exactly one velocity. Therefore
$$
\boxed{v_*=\frac pm,\qquad H_{\max}(x,p)=\frac{p^2}{2m}+V(x).}
$$
This is the <Legendre transform in mechanics> that constructs the <Hamiltonian mechanics> energy from the kinetic-minus-potential Lagrangian.

= Solution
{parent=a}

At a differentiable interior <stationary point>, all first-order changes of the scalar field vanish. In particular, the <directional derivative> along each coordinate axis vanishes, so $f_x=f_y=0$ and hence $\nabla f=\boldsymbol0$. Equivalently, the first-order differential $df=\nabla f\cdot d\boldsymbol r$ vanishes for every displacement. A differentiable interior maximum or minimum must satisfy this condition; a stationary point need not be an extremum.

The standard nondegenerate local <contour plot> models are $-x^2-y^2$ for a maximum, $x^2+y^2$ for a minimum, and $x^2-y^2$ for a <saddle point of a scalar function>. Near a maximum, nested contours have lower values farther from the centre; near a minimum they have higher values. Near a saddle, <hyperbolas> occur on opposite sides of the critical level, whose contour has two intersecting tangent directions. More general nondegenerate quadratic forms deform these circles into <ellipses>; degenerate stationary points can have more complicated contours.

Set $D=1+x^2+y^2>0$. The <quotient rule> gives the <partial derivatives>
$$
f_x=-\frac{4x(1+y+y^2)}{D^2},\qquad
f_y=\frac{2(1+x^2-y^2+2x^2y)}{D^2}.
$$
Since $1+y+y^2=(y+1/2)^2+3/4>0$, the first equation $f_x=0$ forces $x=0$. The second then reduces to $1-y^2=0$. Thus the only <stationary points> and their field values are
$$
\boxed{(x,y)=(0,1),\ f=1;\qquad (x,y)=(0,-1),\ f=-1.}
$$
No division by a potentially vanishing factor of $y$ was used, so no stationary points have been discarded.

The <level set> $f=c$ can be rearranged, without changing its domain, as
$$
(c+2)x^2+cy^2-2y+c=0.
$$
The requested special contours are therefore
$$
\boxed{f=0:\ y=x^2;\qquad
f=1:\ (x,y)=(0,1);\qquad
f=-1:\ y+1=\pm x.}
$$
The first is a <parabola>, the second a single point, and the last two intersecting straight lines. Moreover
$$
1-f=\frac{3x^2+(y-1)^2}{D}\geq0,
$$
with equality only at $(0,1)$. Hence $(0,1)$ is a strict global maximum. At $(0,-1)$ the crossing critical contour separates sectors with values above and below $-1$, so it is a <saddle point of a scalar function>.

As a <Hessian matrix> check, $H_f(0,1)=\operatorname{diag}(-3,-1)$ is negative definite, while $H_f(0,-1)=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1)$ is indefinite. There is no stationary minimum.

For $c\ne0$, <completing the square> gives the <conic section>

(c+2)x^2+c\left(y-\frac1c\right)^2=\frac{1-c^2}{c}.

The absolutely convergent <geometric series> has sum $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}r^k=(1-r)^{-1}$ for $0<r<1$. Its <Cauchy product> with itself is
$$
\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}r^k\right)^2
=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{m}1\right)r^m
=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(m+1)r^m.
$$
There are exactly $m+1$ pairs of nonnegative integers whose sum is $m$. Relabeling $j=m+1$ proves
$$
\boxed{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j r^{j-1}=\frac1{(1-r)^2}.}
$$
Absolute convergence justifies the multiplication and regrouping; no differentiation of an infinite series is needed.

For the <heat equation> $\Theta_t=\kappa\Theta_{xx}$, <separation of variables> with homogeneous <Neumann boundary condition> at both ends gives spatial modes $\cos(n\pi x/L)$ and decay factors $e^{-\kappa(n\pi/L)^2t}$. The $n=0$ mode is constant and does not decay.

The initial profile already contains only two such modes:
$$
T_0\sin^2\frac{\pi x}{L}=\frac{T_0}{2}-\frac{T_0}{2}\cos\frac{2\pi x}{L}.
$$
Evolving each mode therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\Theta(x,t)=\frac{T_0}{2}\left[1-e^{-4\kappa\pi^2t/L^2}\cos\frac{2\pi x}{L}\right].}
$$
The endpoint <derivatives> vanish, and substitution checks both the equation and the initial profile. The spatial <integral> remains $LT_0/2$: no heat escapes through insulated ends, so the limiting uniform temperature is $T_0/2$.

With homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary condition>, the eigenfunctions are $\sin(n\pi x/L)$ and all modes decay. Write
$$
\Theta(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}
\,e^{-\kappa(n\pi/L)^2t},\qquad
b_n=\frac{2T_0}{L}\int_0^L\sin^2\frac{\pi x}{L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx.

Reflection about the midpoint makes every even-$n$ coefficient zero. For odd $n$, putting $u=\pi x/L$ and using the product-to-sum identity gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
b_n&=\frac{2T_0}{\pi}\int_0^\pi\sin^2u\sin(nu)\,du\\
&=\frac{2T_0}{\pi}\left[\frac1n-\frac12\left(\frac1{n+2}+\frac1{n-2}\right)\right]
=\frac{8T_0}{\pi n(4-n^2)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\boxed{\Theta(x,t)=\frac{8T_0}{\pi}
