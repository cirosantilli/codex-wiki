The literal expression in the original PDF is undefined. Indeed, <hyperbolic tangent> satisfies $\tanh w=\sinh w/\cosh w$, and
$$
\cosh(in\pi/4)=\cos(n\pi/4)=0\quad\text{when }n\equiv2\pmod4.
$$
Already the $n=2$ summand contains $\tanh(i\pi/2)$, a <pole>. The shifted term has the same issue when $n\equiv3\pmod4$. Thus this is not a finite sum of defined <complex numbers>, and it has **no literal real or imaginary part**. Cancelling two undefined terms is not ordinary arithmetic.

There is nevertheless a natural interpretation by <meromorphic telescoping cancellation>. Insert the same small positive real shift $\varepsilon$ into every <hyperbolic tangent> argument. Each term is then finite, and the <telescoping sum> is
$$
S(\varepsilon)=\tanh(\varepsilon+121i\pi/4)-\tanh\varepsilon.
$$
Since <hyperbolic tangent> has period $i\pi$, its first argument can be reduced to $\varepsilon+i\pi/4$. Taking the <limit> gives
$$
\boxed{\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}S(\varepsilon)=i,\qquad\Re=0,\quad\Im=1.}
$$
This is a common-shift continuation of the sum, plausibly the intended telescoping answer. It requires that additional convention; it is not the value of the expression as printed.

At height $z>0$, the <elliptic paraboloid> has a circular cross-section $0\le r\le\sqrt z$, of <area> $\pi z$. The <cylindrical coordinates> <volume element> therefore reduces the <volume integral> to
$$
K=\int_0^\infty\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\sqrt z}f'(z)r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz
=\pi\int_0^\infty zf'(z)\,dz.
$$
For the specified $f$, the <derivative> is $e^{-z}(z^{-1/2}+\tfrac12z^{-3/2})$. Thus $zf'(z)$ is integrable at zero and decays exponentially at infinity. Apply <integration by parts> first on a truncated interval and then take the endpoint <limits>:
$$
K=\pi[zf(z)]_0^\infty-\pi\int_0^\infty f(z)\,dz
=\pi\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-z}}{\sqrt z}\,dz.
$$
Both boundary terms vanish because $zf(z)=-\sqrt z\,e^{-z}$. With $z=u^2$, <integration by substitution> gives the <Gaussian integral>:
$$
\boxed{K=2\pi\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2}\,du=\pi^{3/2}.}
$$
The integrand is singular at the apex but the resulting <improper integral> is convergent.

In <spherical coordinates>, the <cone> condition forces $z\ge0$ and becomes $0\le\vartheta\le\alpha$. The unit <ball> gives $0\le\rho\le1$. Hence
$$
V=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\alpha\int_0^1\rho^2\sin\vartheta\,d\rho\,d\vartheta\,d\varphi

The equality uses $\sin^3\alpha\cot\alpha=\sin^2\alpha\cos\alpha$. The positive <cosine> on the stated range removes any square-root sign ambiguity.

Put $s=x^2+y^2$. The <gradient> components are
$$
f_x=4x(s-1),\qquad f_y=y\left[4(s-1)+\frac7{3+y^2}\right].
$$
A <stationary point> requires both components to vanish. If $x\ne0$, then $s=1$; the second component subsequently forces $y=0$, giving $(\pm1,0)$. If $x=0$, either $y=0$ or, writing $u=y^2$,
$$
4(u-1)(3+u)+7=4u^2+8u-5=(2u-1)(2u+5)=0.
$$
Only $u=1/2$ is nonnegative. Thus there are five <stationary points>, with values
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
(\pm1,0):&\quad f=\frac72\ln3,\\
(0,0):&\quad f=1+\frac72\ln3,\\
(0,\pm1/\sqrt2):&\quad f=\frac14+\frac72\ln\frac72.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The cases $x=0$ and $s=1$ exhaust the first <partial derivative> equation, so no other <stationary points> have been lost by division.

The <Hessian matrix> has entries

f_{xx}=4(s-1)+8x^2,\qquad f_{xy}=8xy,\qquad f_{yy}=4(s-1)+8y^2+\frac{7(3-y^2)}{(3+y^2)^2}.

The finite form of <Taylor's theorem> with the first $n$ terms is
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k+R_n(x),\qquad R_n(x)=\frac{f^{(n)}(\xi)}{n!}(x-a)^n,}
$$
where $\xi$ lies strictly between $a$ and $x$ for $x\ne a$. A sufficient hypothesis is that $f$ has continuous <derivatives> through order $n-1$ on the interval joining the endpoints and an $n$th <derivative> in its interior. The displayed polynomial has degree at most $n-1$; its <Taylor remainder> uses the $n$th <derivative>, not the $(n+1)$st. At $x=a$ the remainder is zero. This is a finite <Taylor polynomial> identity and does not require an infinite <Taylor series> to converge.

Divide numerator and denominator by $x$ before taking their <Maclaurin series>:
$$
\frac{\sin x}{\sinh x}
=\frac{1-x^2/6+x^4/120+O(x^6)}{1+x^2/6+x^4/120+O(x^6)}.
$$
For the reciprocal denominator, the <geometric series> gives $1-x^2/6+7x^4/360+O(x^6)$. Multiplying gives the <Taylor expansion>
$$
\boxed{\frac{\sin x}{\sinh x}=1-\frac{x^2}{3}+\frac{x^4}{18}+O(x^6).}
$$
Strictly, the original quotient has a <removable singularity> at zero; this is the <Taylor series> of its continuous, analytic extension with value one there. The absence of odd powers also follows because the ratio of these two odd functions is even.

Random <sampling without replacement> assigns each of the ten distinguishable pieces to each lunch with the same <probability>. Five pieces of each type therefore give
$$
\boxed{P(A_1)=\frac12,\qquad P(B_2)=\frac12.}
$$
One can check the second <probability> by conditioning on the first lunch:
$$
P(B_2)=P(B_2\mid A_1)P(A_1)+P(B_2\mid B_1)P(B_1)
=\frac59\frac12+\frac49\frac12=\frac12.
$$
The identical marginal <probabilities> do not imply <independence> of the draws.

Let $N$ count rotten pieces. Each item supplies an independent <Bernoulli trial> with success <probability> $p$, so $N$ has a <binomial distribution> with parameters $10,p$. There are $\binom{10}{n}$ choices of the rotten items, and each such choice has <probability> $p^n(1-p)^{10-n}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{P(N=n)=\binom{10}{n}p^n(1-p)^{10-n},\qquad n=0,\ldots,10.}
$$
For $p=0$ or $p=1$, interpret this as the corresponding distribution concentrated at zero or ten; no ambiguous $0^0$ evaluation is needed.

Write $N=\sum_{j=1}^{10}X_j$, where the independent <Bernoulli random variables> indicate rottenness. Since $X_j^2=X_j$, their <variances> are $E[X_j^2]-E[X_j]^2=p-p^2$. The <independence> makes all cross <covariances> zero, so
$$
\operatorname{Var}(N)=10p(1-p),\qquad\boxed{\sigma_N=\sqrt{10p(1-p)}\text{ pieces}.}
$$
This <standard deviation> vanishes at $p=0$ and $p=1$, when the count is certain.

The product in the integrand simplifies to $\sin x\cos^3x$ below its upper endpoint. With the <integration by substitution> $t=\cos x$, the limits become $c$ and zero and $dt=-\sin x\,dx$. Therefore
$$
I=-\int_c^0t^3\,dt=\int_0^ct^3\,dt=J,
\qquad\boxed{I=J=\frac{c^4}{4}.}
$$
Although the <tangent function> itself is undefined at the upper endpoint, the product has a zero <limit> there, so its continuous extension, or its <improper integral>, gives this same result.

To obtain $J$ directly from a <Riemann sum>, divide $[0,c]$ into $n$ equal pieces of width $c/n$ and use right endpoints $t_k=kc/n$. The sum is
$$
\sum_{k=1}^n\left(\frac{kc}{n}\right)^3\frac cn
=\frac{c^4}{n^4}\frac{n^2(n+1)^2}{4}
=\frac{c^4}{4}\left(1+\frac1n\right)^2.
$$
The integrand is continuous, so the <limit> of these <Riemann sums> is its <definite integral>, namely $c^4/4$, agreeing with the <antiderivative> calculation.

Put $d=c-1$. The <matrix> is real and symmetric, so <orthogonality> means $A^{\mathsf T}A=A^2=I$. Multiplication gives the scalar conditions
$$
a^2+d^2=1,\qquad b^2=1,\qquad2ad=0.

(0,1,0)^{\mathsf T},(0,-1,0)^{\mathsf T},(0,1,2)^{\mathsf T},(0,-1,2)^{\mathsf T}\}.
\end{gathered}}
$$
These conditions are sufficient as well as necessary, since they make every entry of $A^{\mathsf T}A$ equal to the corresponding entry of the <identity matrix>.

A three-dimensional <rotation matrix> is an <orthogonal matrix> with <determinant> $+1$. Here $\det A=b(a^2-d^2)$. For the branch $d=0$, the determinant condition gives $b=1$; for the branch $a=0$, it gives $b=-1$. Hence
$$
\boxed{(a,b,c)^{\mathsf T}\in\{(1,1,1)^{\mathsf T},(-1,1,1)^{\mathsf T},(0,-1,0)^{\mathsf T},(0,-1,2)^{\mathsf T}\}.}
$$
The first is the identity <rotation>; the remaining three are half-turns. Their fixed axes are respectively the $y$ axis, the direction $(1,0,-1)$ and the direction $(1,0,1)$. The identity is included under the usual <rotation matrix> convention.

The sloping height relation on the <cone> is $z=b(1-r/a)$. A <parametric equation> of its curved surface is therefore
$$
\mathbf R(r,\theta)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,b(1-r/a)),\qquad0\le r\le a,\quad0\le\theta<2\pi.
$$
Its two <tangent vectors> are $\mathbf R_r=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,-b/a)$ and $\mathbf R_\theta=r(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0)$. Multiplication by $dr$ and $d\theta$ produces the two infinitesimal sides requested in the paper. Their <cross product> is the <vector area element>:
$$
\boxed{d\mathbf S=(\mathbf R_r\times\mathbf R_\theta)\,dr\,d\theta
=r\left(\frac ba\cos\theta,\frac ba\sin\theta,1\right)\,dr\,d\theta.}
$$
Its radial component is positive, so this <normal vector> points outwards from the solid <cone>. Taking its <norm> gives the scalar <area element>
$$
\boxed{dS=r\sqrt{1+\frac{b^2}{a^2}}\,dr\,d\theta.}
$$
The <parametric equation> degenerates at the apex, but that single point contributes no <area>.

The proposed <parametric equation> traces precisely the same oriented segment, with a different speed. Set $u=t^n$, which increases from zero to one, and $du=nt^{n-1}\,dt$. Direct substitution gives the <line integrals>
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf x&=\int_0^1(2-2t^n)nt^{n-1}\,dt=\int_0^1(2-2u)\,du=1,\\
\int\mathbf G\cdot d\mathbf x&=\int_0^1(2-4t^n+2t^{2n})nt^{n-1}\,dt=\int_0^1(2-4u+2u^2)\,du=\frac23.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus **both values are unchanged**. The <derivative> of $u$ vanishes at the initial endpoint for $n>1$, but the monotone <integration by substitution> is still valid. This is invariance under <reparametrization> of one curve, which applies even to a nonconservative <vector field>; it is not evidence of general path independence.

The three components of the <curl> cancel pairwise:
$$
\nabla\times\mathbf F=(1-1,1-1,1-1)=\mathbf0.
$$
The domain is all of $\mathbb R^3$, which is <simply connected>, so this is consistent with a <conservative vector field>. To construct its <scalar potential>, integrate $\Phi_x=y+z$ to obtain $\Phi=x(y+z)+h(y,z)$. Matching $\Phi_y=x+z$ gives $h_y=z$, hence $h=yz+k(z)$. Finally $\Phi_z=x+y$ forces $k'(z)=0$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\Phi(x,y,z)=xy+xz+yz+C,\qquad\mathbf F=\nabla\Phi.}
$$
Every <scalar potential> differs by a constant on this connected domain. The endpoint values differ by one, agreeing with the <line integral> in part (a).

Take a piecewise linear <path> along three coordinate directions, passing through the origin and then $(0,1,0)$:
$$
\mathbf x(t)=\begin{cases}(1-t,0,0),&0\le t\le1,\\(0,t-1,0),&1\le t\le2,\\(0,1,t-2),&2\le t\le3.\end{cases}
$$
On the first segment $G_x=y^2+z^2=0$, so its <line integral> is zero. On the second $G_y=x^2+z^2=0$, so its contribution is also zero. On the last segment $G_z=x^2+y^2=1$ and $z$ increases from zero to one. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\int\mathbf G\cdot d\mathbf x=0+0+\int_0^1 1\,dz=1\ne\frac23.}
$$
This gives the requested concrete path dependence with the same ordered endpoints.
