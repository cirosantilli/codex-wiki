By the <polynomial remainder theorem>, the first <polynomial remainder> is $f(1)=23-2p$. Equating it to $9$ gives **$p=7$**.

For the separate <polynomial long division>, successively cancel the leading powers of the dividend. Subtracting $x^2(x-1)$ leaves $2x^2+2x+1$; subtracting $2x(x-1)$ leaves $4x+1$; subtracting $4(x-1)$ leaves $5$. Thus
$$
x^3+x^2+2x+1=(x-1)(x^2+2x+4)+5,
\qquad\boxed{\text{quotient }x^2+2x+4,\quad\text{remainder }5.}
$$
The <polynomial remainder> also equals the dividend evaluated at $x=1$.

A repeated linear factor requires both reciprocal powers. Write the <partial fraction decomposition> as
$$
\frac{A}{x-1}+\frac{B}{(x-1)^2}+\frac{C}{x+2}.

Multiplication by the common denominator gives $4x^2-x=A(x-1)(x+2)+B(x+2)+C(x-1)^2$. Setting $x=1$ gives $B=1$, and setting $x=-2$ gives $C=2$. Comparing the coefficients of $x^2$ then gives $A=2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\frac{4x^2-x}{(x-1)^2(x+2)}

$$

The <domain of a function> is $\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$, because the denominator vanishes at zero. Near zero, $e^x-1\sim x$, so $x=0$ is a vertical <asymptote>, approached with value $-\infty$ from the left and $+\infty$ from the right. The horizontal <asymptotes> are $y=-1$ as $x\to-\infty$ and $y=0$ as $x\to+\infty$.

Moreover,
$$
f'(x)=-\frac{e^x}{(e^x-1)^2}<0.
$$
Both branches define a strictly decreasing <monotone function>: the left branch lies below $-1$, and the right branch is positive. There are no zeros or vertical-axis intercept.

Collecting the coefficients gives the <coefficient matrix>
$$
\boxed{A=\begin{pmatrix}a&0&2\\0&b+1&c-1\\0&c+1&b-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad

Let $M=x+y$ and $N=x$. Their cross <partial derivatives> agree: $M_y=N_x=1$. More directly, construct the <scalar> <function>
$$
F(x,y)=\frac{x^2}{2}+xy,\qquad dF=(x+y)\,dx+x\,dy.
$$
This exhibits the expression as an <exact differential>. Along a solution of the <differential equation>,
$$
\frac d{dx}F(x,y(x))=x+y+xy'=0.
$$
Thus the <general solution> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{x^2}{2}+xy=C,\qquad y=\frac Cx-\frac x2\quad(x\ne0).}
$$
For $C=0$, the graph $y=-x/2$ also extends smoothly through $x=0$ and satisfies the original, undivided equation there. Nonzero $C$ cannot extend through zero as a finite-valued <differentiable> solution. The extra vertical component $x=0$ of the implicit zero-level equation is not a graph $y(x)$ on an <interval> and is not an additional solution of this <ordinary differential equation>.

= Solution

The nonzero <vectors> $b,c$ are <normal vectors> to the two <planes>. If $b\times c=0$, then $c=qb$ for some nonzero real $q$. Their equations are therefore $r\cdot b=\lambda$ and $q(r\cdot b)=\mu$.

When $\mu=q\lambda$, the <planes> coincide and their intersection is an entire <plane>. Otherwise they are distinct parallel planes and have no intersection. Neither case has exactly a <line> as its intersection. Hence **$b\times c\ne0$ is necessary** for intersection in a <line>. It means that the two <normal vectors> are not parallel; in that case it is also sufficient.

= Solution

Apply the <vector triple product identity> and the two <plane> constraints:
$$
\boxed{r\times(b\times c)=b(r\cdot c)-c(r\cdot b)=\mu b-\lambda c.}
$$
Put $d=b\times c\ne0$. If $a$ is one intersection point, subtraction gives $(r-a)\times d=0$, so $r-a$ is parallel to $d$. Conversely, adding any multiple of $d$ preserves both <plane> constraints because $d$ is <orthogonal> to both <normal vectors>. The whole intersection is therefore
$$
\boxed{r=a+t(b\times c),\quad t\in\mathbb R,\qquad a\cdot b=\lambda,\quad a\cdot c=\mu.}
$$
Such an $a$ exists: choose $a=\alpha b+\beta c$ and solve the two-by-two <linear system> with <determinant> $|b|^2|c|^2-(b\cdot c)^2=|b\times c|^2>0$. This also proves sufficiency rather than assuming a nonempty intersection.

The squared <Euclidean distance> along the <line> is
$$
|a+td|^2=|a|^2+2t(a\cdot d)+t^2|d|^2,\qquad d=b\times c.
$$
Its minimum occurs at $t=0$ exactly when its <derivative> there vanishes, so **$a\cdot(b\times c)=0$**. Since $b,c$ are independent, their <linear span> is the two-dimensional <orthogonal complement> of $b\times c$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{a=\alpha b+\beta c}
$$
for unique real $\alpha,\beta$. This is the <closest point on the intersection of two planes>; the positive quadratic coefficient $|d|^2$ proves that the stationary distance is a strict <global minimum>.

Convert the <cardioid> from <polar coordinates> to <Cartesian coordinates>:
$$
x=(1+\cos\theta)\cos\theta,\qquad y=(1+\cos\theta)\sin\theta.
$$
The <curve> is symmetric about the horizontal axis. It has a <cusp> at the origin at $\theta=\pi$, passes through $(0,\pm1)$, and reaches $(2,0)$ on the right. Its leftmost horizontal coordinate is $-1/4$, since $x=(\cos\theta+1/2)^2-1/4$.

For the largest $y$, differentiate the parametrization:
$$
\frac{dy}{d\theta}=\cos\theta+\cos2\theta
=(2\cos\theta-1)(\cos\theta+1).
$$
The candidates have $\cos\theta=1/2$ or $-1$. The positive maximum occurs at $\theta=\pi/3$, where $r=3/2$. Comparing with the negative extremum and the zero <cusp> value proves
$$
\boxed{(x_M,y_M)=\left(\frac34,\frac{3\sqrt3}{4}\right).}
$$

Each radial segment from $0$ to $1+\cos\theta$ lies inside the <cardioid>. Integrating the <polar area element> gives

Differentiate $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$:
$$
dx=\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta,\qquad
dy=\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta.

\sqrt{\sin^2\theta+(1+\cos\theta)^2}

-2\int_\pi^{2\pi}\cos(\theta/2)\,d\theta

in coordinate <length> units. The isolated <cusp> does not obstruct this piecewise <smooth> <arc length> integral.

The <product rule> gives the two <partial derivatives>

g_x=x(2-x^2+y^2)e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2},\qquad

H_g(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&-2\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det H_g(0,0)=-4<0.

Assume $L>0$. For any nonzero integer $k$,
$$
\int_{-L}^{L}\cos(k\pi x/L)\,dx
=\frac{L}{k\pi}[\sin(k\pi x/L)]_{-L}^{L}=0.
$$
For $m\ne n$, the product-to-sum identity expresses the integrand as one half the sum of cosines with nonzero frequencies $m-n$ and $m+n$, so the integral is zero. For $m=n>0$, use $\cos^2u=(1+\cos2u)/2$ to get $L$. Finally, for $m=n=0$ the integrand is one. The complete <Fourier orthogonality> formula is
$$
\boxed{\int_{-L}^{L}\cos\frac{m\pi x}{L}\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx
=\begin{cases}2L,&m=n=0,\\L,&m=n\geq1,\\0,&m\ne n.\end{cases}}
$$
The constant mode has twice the squared norm of a positive-frequency <cosine> mode.

f(x)\sim\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}[a_n\cos(n\pi x)+b_n\sin(n\pi x)].
$$
The <Fourier coefficients> have prefactor $1$ on $[-1,1]$. First, $a_0=\int_{-1}^{1}e^x dx=2\sinh1$. For $q=n\pi$ the useful <antiderivatives> are
$$
\int e^x\cos(qx)dx=\frac{e^x[\cos(qx)+q\sin(qx)]}{1+q^2},\qquad
\int e^x\sin(qx)dx=\frac{e^x[\sin(qx)-q\cos(qx)]}{1+q^2}.
$$
At the endpoints $\sin(n\pi)=0$ and $\cos(n\pi)=(-1)^n$, so
$$
a_n=\frac{2(-1)^n\sinh1}{1+n^2\pi^2},\qquad
b_n=-\frac{2n\pi(-1)^n\sinh1}{1+n^2\pi^2}.
$$
Consequently, on $-1<x<1$,
$$
\boxed{e^x=\sinh1+2\sinh1\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
\frac{(-1)^n[\cos(n\pi x)-n\pi\sin(n\pi x)]}{1+n^2\pi^2}.}
$$
The <Dirichlet-Jordan convergence theorem> justifies equality at every point of <continuity> of the periodic extension. At odd integers the extension has a <jump discontinuity> between $e$ and $e^{-1}$, so the series converges to their average $\cosh1$, regardless of any assigned endpoint value. This is the $\kappa=L=1$ case of the <Fourier series of a periodically extended exponential>.

**<Continuity>.** The <function> is <continuous> at $x_0$ when $\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=f(x_0)$. Equivalently, for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $\delta>0$ such that $|x-x_0|<\delta$ within the <domain of a function> implies $|f(x)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$; this is the <epsilon-delta definition of continuity>.

**<Differentiability>.** At an interior point $x_0$, the <difference quotient> must have a finite two-sided <limit of a function>:
$$
f'(x_0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}h.
$$
This defines <differentiability> and the <derivative>. At an endpoint one may instead state a one-sided definition relative to the <interval>. <Differentiability implies continuity>, because the increment is $h$ times a quotient with finite limit, but <continuity> alone does not guarantee <differentiability>.

As $x\to0$ through nonzero values, $1/x^2\to+\infty$ and $e^{-1/x^2}\to0=f(0)$. Thus $f$ is <continuous> at zero.

For the <derivative> at zero use its definition, rather than substituting into a formula obtained away from zero:
$$
f'(0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{e^{-1/h^2}}h=0.
$$
Indeed, putting $t=1/|h|$ makes the <absolute value> $te^{-t^2}$, which tends to zero since <Gaussian decay dominates every power>. Therefore **$f$ is <continuous> and <differentiable> at zero, with $f'(0)=0$**. Away from zero, the <chain rule> gives $f'(x)=2x^{-3}e^{-1/x^2}$.

Take positive dimensions measured in metres. The <volume> is $V=lwh$ and the external <surface area> constraint is $S=lw+2lh+2wh=12$. There is one base and four walls, with no lid. The <Lagrange multiplier> equations for $V-\lambda(S-12)$ are
$$
wh=\lambda(w+2h),\qquad lh=\lambda(l+2h),\qquad lw=2\lambda(l+w).
$$
The first equation implies $\lambda>0$. Multiply the first by $l$ and the second by $w$ and subtract; this gives $2\lambda h(l-w)=0$, hence $l=w=s$. The last equation gives $\lambda=s/4$. Substituting into the first yields $sh=(s/4)(s+2h)$ and therefore $s=2h$.

The constraint now reads $s^2+4sh=3s^2=12$. Thus
$$
\boxed{l=w=2\ \mathrm m,\qquad h=1\ \mathrm m,\qquad V_{\max}=4\ \mathrm{m^3}.}
$$
For a global check, apply the <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality> to $lw$, $2lh$ and $2wh$, whose sum is $12$. Their product is $4V^2\leq4^3=64$, so no feasible box has $V>4$. Equality holds exactly at the dimensions found above. This is the <maximum-volume open rectangular box at fixed surface area>.

Minimize the squared distance $F=x^2+y^2$, which has the same minimizers as the distance. The <Lagrange multiplier> equations for the constraint $G=x^2+xy-1=0$ are
$$
2x=\lambda(2x+y),\qquad 2y=\lambda x,\qquad x^2+xy=1.
$$
The constraint <gradient> cannot vanish on this branch, since its second component is $x>0$. Eliminate $\lambda=2y/x$ to obtain $x^2=2xy+y^2$. Put $t=y/x$; then $t^2+2t-1=0$, so $t=-1\pm\sqrt2$. But the constraint says $x^2(1+t)=1$, requiring $1+t>0$ and selecting $t=\sqrt2-1$. It follows that
$$
\boxed{(x,y)=\left(2^{-1/4},(\sqrt2-1)2^{-1/4}\right),\qquad
 d_{\min}=\sqrt{2(\sqrt2-1)}.}
$$
To prove a <global minimum> on the whole $x>0$ branch, substitute $y=1/x-x$ into $F$:
$$
F=2x^2+x^{-2}-2\geq2\sqrt2-2.
$$
The <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality> proves this bound, with equality only when $2x^2=x^{-2}$, namely $x=2^{-1/4}$. Thus the <stationary point> is the unique closest point on the required branch. Numerically it is approximately $(0.840896,0.348311)$ and its distance is $0.910180$ in coordinate units.
