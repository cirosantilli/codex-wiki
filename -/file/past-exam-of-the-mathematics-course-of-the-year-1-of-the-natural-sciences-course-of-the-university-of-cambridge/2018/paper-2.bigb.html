The direction <vectors> are $\mathbf u=(1,-2,1)$ and $\mathbf v=(0,-2,1)$. Their <cross product> is $(0,-1,-2)$, with <norm> $\sqrt5$. A <unit normal> to the plane is therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbf n=\frac{(0,1,2)}{\sqrt5},\quad\text{or its negative}.}
$$
The two intersecting directions span the plane, so a <vector> <orthogonal> to both is a <normal vector> to that plane.

The exponential definition of <hyperbolic sine> gives $e^z-e^{-z}=0$, or $e^{2z}=1$. Taking every branch of the <complex logarithm> gives
$$
\boxed{\sinh z=0\iff z=i\pi k,\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.}

For <hyperbolic cosine>, multiply $\cosh z+1=0$ by $2e^z$ to obtain $(e^z+1)^2=0$. Thus $e^z=-1$, giving
$$
\boxed{\cosh z+1=0\iff z=i(2k+1)\pi,\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.}
$$

To distinguish the one-variable profile from the resulting two-variable <function>, write $u(x,t)=F(\xi)$ with $\xi=xe^{-t}$. The <chain rule> gives $u_t=-xe^{-t}F'(\xi)$ and $u_x=e^{-t}F'(\xi)$, so $u_t+xu_x=0$. Thus any differentiable profile of $xe^{-t}$ solves the <partial differential equation>.

The <initial condition> selects $F(\xi)=\ln\xi$ on the positive half-line. Hence
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=\ln(xe^{-t})=\ln x-t,\qquad x>0.}
$$
This satisfies both the <partial differential equation> and the given data at $t=0$.

Let $\Delta=[\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c]\ne0$. These three <vectors> are linearly independent, so write $\mathbf d=\alpha\mathbf a+\beta\mathbf b+\gamma\mathbf c$. Taking the <dot product> with $\mathbf b\times\mathbf c$ gives $\alpha=[\mathbf b,\mathbf c,\mathbf d]/\Delta$. Likewise, the <dot products> with $\mathbf c\times\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b$ give
$$
\beta=\frac{[\mathbf c,\mathbf a,\mathbf d]}\Delta=-\frac{[\mathbf c,\mathbf d,\mathbf a]}\Delta,
\qquad\gamma=\frac{[\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf d]}\Delta.
$$
Here the cyclic invariance and sign change under an interchange of the <scalar triple product> fix every sign. Substituting the three coefficients proves
$$
\boxed{\mathbf d=\frac{[\mathbf b,\mathbf c,\mathbf d]}\Delta\mathbf a
-\frac{[\mathbf c,\mathbf d,\mathbf a]}\Delta\mathbf b
+\frac{[\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf d]}\Delta\mathbf c.}
$$
This is precisely coordinate extraction using the <reciprocal basis> $(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c,\mathbf c\times\mathbf a,\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)/\Delta$.

Put $\mathbf v=\mathbf r_0-\mathbf a$. The squared distance to a point on the line is $|\mathbf v-\lambda\widehat{\mathbf t}|^2$. Since $|\widehat{\mathbf t}|=1$, completing the square in $\lambda$ gives its minimum at $\lambda=\widehat{\mathbf t}\cdot\mathbf v$, with value $|\mathbf v|^2-(\widehat{\mathbf t}\cdot\mathbf v)^2$. The <cross product> identity $|\widehat{\mathbf t}\times\mathbf v|^2=|\mathbf v|^2-(\widehat{\mathbf t}\cdot\mathbf v)^2$ therefore proves
$$
\boxed{\ell=|\widehat{\mathbf t}\times(\mathbf r_0-\mathbf a)|.}
$$
For the given line, $\widehat{\mathbf t}=(1,1,0)/\sqrt2$, so its squared distance to $(x,y,z)$ is $(x-y)^2/2+z^2$. Substituting the <parabola> gives
$$
\ell^2(\mu)=6\mu^2+(\mu^2-4)^2=(\mu^2-1)^2+15.
$$
The global minimum occurs at $\mu=\pm1$, hence
$$
\boxed{\ell_{\min}=\sqrt{15}.}
$$
The corresponding points on the <parabola> are $\pm(1,1+2\sqrt3,0)+(0,0,-3)$; their nearest points on the line are $\pm(1+\sqrt3,1+\sqrt3,0)$. The connecting <vectors> are <orthogonal> to the line, as the projection calculation requires.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Write $s=\widehat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf x$. Taking the <dot product> of the equation with the <unit vector> $\widehat{\mathbf n}$ gives $2s+s^2=-1$, so $(s+1)^2=0$ and $s=-1$.

Now take the <cross product> with $\widehat{\mathbf n}$. The <vector triple product> gives $\widehat{\mathbf n}\times(\widehat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf x)=s\widehat{\mathbf n}-\mathbf x=-\widehat{\mathbf n}-\mathbf x$, so
$$
2\widehat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf x-\widehat{\mathbf n}-\mathbf x=\widehat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf b.
$$
The original equation, now with $s^2=1$, also gives $\widehat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf x=\mathbf b-\widehat{\mathbf n}-2\mathbf x$. Substitution eliminates the remaining <cross product> of the unknown and yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbf x=\frac{2\mathbf b-3\widehat{\mathbf n}-\widehat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf b}{5}.}
$$
Its <dot product> with $\widehat{\mathbf n}$ is $-1$, and direct substitution using the <vector triple product> verifies the original equation. The elimination gives a unique solution without choosing components. It is also an application of the <inverse of a scalar plus a cross-product operator> after the scalar component has been fixed.

Write $c=\cos\phi$ and $s=\sin\phi$. The needed coefficient <derivatives> are $c_x=s^2/r$, $s_x=-sc/r$, $c_y=-sc/r$ and $s_y=c^2/r$. Apply the <product rule> to both terms of $f_x=cg_r-(s/r)g_\phi$ and use the <chain rule> for the <derivatives> of $g$. Collecting terms gives
$$
\boxed{f_{xx}=c^2g_{rr}-\frac{2sc}{r}g_{r\phi}+\frac{2sc}{r^2}g_\phi
+\frac{s^2}{r}g_r+\frac{s^2}{r^2}g_{\phi\phi}.}

Similarly, differentiating $f_y=sg_r+(c/r)g_\phi$ gives
$$
\boxed{f_{yy}=s^2g_{rr}+\frac{2sc}{r}g_{r\phi}-\frac{2sc}{r^2}g_\phi

On addition, both the mixed <partial derivative> terms and the first angular <derivative> terms cancel. Using $s^2+c^2=1$, the <Laplacian> in <polar coordinates> is
$$
\boxed{\nabla^2f=g_{rr}+\frac1r g_r+\frac1{r^2}g_{\phi\phi},\qquad r>0.}

Distinguish the production type as well as the stopping position. The <sample space> has the six outcomes
$$
\boxed{\Omega=\{G\cap x_0,G\cap x_1,G\cap x_2,D\cap x_0,D\cap x_1,D\cap x_2\}.}

Here $x_k$ in an event means $X=x_k$. Their joint <probabilities> are $pg_k$ and $(1-p)d_k$, respectively. The conditional stopping laws obey $\sum_{k=0}^2g_k=\sum_{k=0}^2d_k=1$; with the stated strict positivity all six outcomes are possible.

There are $99$ possible stopping points, so the equal defective-ball <probabilities> are $d_k=1/99$. With $g_0>0$, the desired <conditional probability> gives

For the <homogeneous linear differential equation>, the <characteristic equation> is $r^2-2r+2=0$, with <polynomial roots> $1\pm i$. Its <homogeneous solution> is therefore $e^x(A\cos x+B\sin x)$.

Use the <method of undetermined coefficients> for a <particular solution> $y_p=ax^2+bx+c$. Substitution gives
$$

Equating coefficients yields $a=1$, $b=2$ and $c=1$. Hence the <general solution> is
$$
\boxed{y=e^x(A\cos x+B\sin x)+x^2+2x+1.}
$$

The <characteristic polynomial> factors as $(r+1)(r+2)$, so the <homogeneous solution> is $Ae^{-x}+Be^{-2x}$. The extra factor of $x$ accounts for the repeated forcing exponent in the <method of undetermined coefficients>.

Under $y=xu$, the <product rule> gives $y'=u+xu'$ and $y''=2u'+xu''$. Substitute these into the original equation:
$$

The two independent <solutions of a differential equation> supply the <general solution> of this second-order <linear ordinary differential equation>.

The <curl> of the constant <vector field> $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b$ vanishes. The <curl> of the position field $\mathbf r=(x,y,z)$ also vanishes, since all its cross-coordinate <partial derivatives> are zero. Thus
$$
\boxed{\nabla\times[(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)+\mathbf r]=\mathbf0.}

Use the orientation induced by the boundary order $O\to A\to B\to O$. The triangle's <vector area element> integrates to $\tfrac12\mathbf a\times\mathbf b$. The constant term gives

\boxed{\int_{OAB}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf S
=(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)\cdot\frac{\mathbf a\times\mathbf b}{2}

Reversing the orientation reverses this sign. If $\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf b$ are collinear, the triangle has zero <area> and the displayed value is zero by the degenerate-area convention.

Take the outward orientation of the complete closed surface, including its flat base. The constant part of the <vector field> has zero <divergence>, and the radial part has <divergence> $3\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b$. The <divergence theorem> gives its <flux> as that constant times the solid hemisphere's <volume>, $2\pi R^3/3$:
$$
\boxed{\int_{\partial V}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf S=3(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)\frac{2\pi R^3}{3}

The constant field may have nonzero <flux> through the curved part alone, but its curved and base contributions cancel on the closed surface.

With the real <Fourier series> convention,
$$
\boxed{f(\theta)\sim\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty[a_n\cos(n\theta)+b_n\sin(n\theta)],}
$$
where the <Fourier coefficients> are
$$
\boxed{a_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(t)\cos(nt)\,dt\quad(n\ge0),\qquad
b_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(t)\sin(nt)\,dt\quad(n\ge1).}
$$
For a square-integrable periodic <function>, <Parseval's identity> is
$$
\boxed{\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|f(t)|^2\,dt=\frac{|a_0|^2}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty(|a_n|^2+|b_n|^2).}
$$
The <Fourier coefficients> require integrability, and the stated <Parseval identity> requires square integrability. The expansion agrees with $f$ in the <L2 space> sense; for piecewise smooth data it converges pointwise to the average of the two one-sided limits. Without such qualifications an arbitrary periodic <function> need not have a pointwise-convergent <Fourier series>.

= Solution

For fixed $r<1$, $\sum r^n\cos(n\theta)$ converges absolutely and uniformly. Thus for integrable $f$, one may integrate the kernel <series> term by term. The <cosine addition formula> gives
$$
\cos[n(\theta-t)]=\cos(n\theta)\cos(nt)+\sin(n\theta)\sin(nt).
$$
Substitute this into the defining <integral>. The constant kernel term gives $\tfrac1{2\pi}\int f=a_0/2$; the remaining terms give
$$
\boxed{y_f(r,\theta)=\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty r^n[a_n\cos(n\theta)+b_n\sin(n\theta)],}
$$
where
$$
\boxed{a_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(t)\cos(nt)\,dt,\qquad
b_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(t)\sin(nt)\,dt.}
$$
These are the same <Fourier coefficients> as in part (a). The kernel $2K$ is the <Poisson kernel for the unit disk>; the factors $r^n$ suppress the higher angular frequencies inside the <disk>.

The boundary data are odd except at isolated endpoint choices, which do not change <integrals>. Thus every cosine <Fourier coefficient>, including $a_0$, vanishes. For the sine <Fourier coefficients>,
$$
b_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi\sin(nt)\,dt=\frac{2[1-(-1)^n]}{\pi n}
=\begin{cases}4/(\pi n),&n\text{ odd},\\0,&n\text{ even}.\end{cases}

Consequently the extension is

\boxed{y_{f_0}(r,\theta)=\frac4\pi\sum_{\substack{n\ge1\\n\text{ odd}}}\frac{r^n\sin(n\theta)}n.}

It converges absolutely for every $r<1$; its limiting boundary values at jumps are the midpoint of the two one-sided limits.

Let $h=x^2+y^2+z-x$. Using <Lagrange multipliers> with $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla h$ gives
$$
ay=\lambda(2x-1),\qquad ax=2\lambda y,\qquad1=\lambda.
$$
The third equation fixes $\lambda=1$. The first two then reduce to $2x-ay=1$ and $2y=ax$. If $a^2\ne4$, their solution and the constraint give
$$
\boxed{x=\frac2{4-a^2},\qquad y=\frac a{4-a^2},\qquad
z=\frac{4-3a^2}{(4-a^2)^2}.}
$$
At $a=2$, the second equation requires $y=x$ and makes the first inconsistent; at $a=-2$, it requires $y=-x$ and gives the same inconsistency. Thus **there is no constrained stationary point when $a=\pm2$**. The constraint <gradient> never vanishes because its vertical component is one, so the multiplier condition has not excluded any regular constrained <stationary points>.

Eliminate $z$ using the constraint: $z=x-x^2-y^2$. The restricted objective is the <quadratic polynomial> $F(x,y)=axy+x-x^2-y^2$, with constant <Hessian matrix>
$$
H=\begin{pmatrix}-2&a\\a&-2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <eigenvalues> are $-2+a$ and $-2-a$. Hence
$$
\boxed{|a|<2:\text{ strict constrained maximum};\qquad |a|>2:\text{ constrained saddle}.}
$$
For $|a|<2$, both <eigenvalues> are negative and the <quadratic polynomial> is strictly concave, so the maximum is global on the entire constraint surface. For $|a|>2$, the opposite signs give increasing and decreasing directions. At $a=\pm2$ there is no <stationary point>, as already found, rather than a degenerate extremum to classify. No parameter value produces a constrained <local minimum>.

= Solution

Write $\epsilon_k=E_0(k+\tfrac12)$ and use <Lagrange multipliers> $\alpha,\beta$ for the particle-number and energy constraints. The stationary equations are
$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial n_k}\left[-\sum_j(n_j\ln n_j-n_j)-\alpha\left(\sum_jn_j-N\right)-\beta\left(\sum_j\epsilon_jn_j-E\right)\right]
=-\ln n_k-\alpha-\beta\epsilon_k=0.
$$
Thus $n_k=e^{-\alpha}e^{-\beta\epsilon_k}$. Finite normalization over infinitely many levels requires $\beta>0$. Put $q=e^{-\beta E_0}$, so $0<q<1$. The <geometric series> gives
$$
N=e^{-\alpha}\frac{e^{-\beta E_0/2}}{1-q},\qquad
e^{-\alpha}=N(1-q)e^{\beta E_0/2}=2N\sinh\frac{\beta E_0}{2}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{n_k=2N\sinh\left(\frac{\beta E_0}{2}\right)e^{-\beta E_0(k+1/2)}.}
$$
For the energy constraint, use $n_k=N(1-q)q^k$ and $\sum kq^k=q/(1-q)^2$, obtained by differentiating the <geometric series>. This yields
$$
\frac E{NE_0}=(1-q)\sum_{k=0}^\infty\left(k+\frac12\right)q^k
=\frac12+\frac q{1-q}=\frac{1+q}{2(1-q)}.
$$
Recognizing the <hyperbolic cotangent> gives
$$
\boxed{E=\frac{NE_0}{2}\coth\left(\frac{\beta E_0}{2}\right).}
$$
The strict positivity of all occupations requires $E>NE_0/2$; the paper's positivity assumptions alone do not guarantee this feasibility condition. For admissible energy the multiplier is uniquely $\beta=E_0^{-1}\ln[(2E+NE_0)/(2E-NE_0)]>0$. Equality $E=NE_0/2$ is only the limiting state with all occupation in level zero, outside the stipulated strictly positive family; lower energy is impossible.

For rigor beyond formal multiplier equations, $p_k=n_k/N$ has fixed mean $E/(NE_0)-1/2$. The objective differs from $N$ times its <information entropy> only by the fixed constant $N-N\ln N$. The <maximum entropy distribution on the nonnegative integers> is uniquely geometric at that mean, confirming the stationary family and its maximum character.

The <chain rule> for the new coordinates gives $\partial_x=\partial_\xi+\partial_\eta$ and $\partial_y=\partial_\xi-\partial_\eta$. Since the coordinates depend linearly on $x,y$, their coefficients are constant. Expanding the squares of these differential operators gives
$$
u_{xx}-u_{yy}=(u_{\xi\xi}+2u_{\xi\eta}+u_{\eta\eta})-(u_{\xi\xi}-2u_{\xi\eta}+u_{\eta\eta})=4u_{\xi\eta}.
$$
Hence the <partial differential equation> becomes $u_{\xi\eta}=1/4$. Integrating once in each variable introduces independent one-variable functions, giving the <general solution>
$$
\boxed{u=\frac{\xi\eta}{4}+F(\xi)+G(\eta)
=\frac{x^2-y^2}{4}+F(x+y)+G(x-y),}
$$
where $F,G$ are arbitrary twice-differentiable functions on the relevant characteristic intervals. Their sum includes an arbitrary additive constant.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The first <boundary condition> requires $u(x,0)=ax^2+d=0$ for all $x$, so $a=d=0$. Since $u_y=bx+2cy$, the second <boundary condition> gives $b=0$. The <partial differential equation> then reads $2a-2c=1$, fixing $c=-1/2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{a=0,\quad b=0,\quad c=-\frac12,\quad d=0,\qquad u(x,y)=-\frac{y^2}{2}.}
$$

{parent=20}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

On a smooth implicit branch, put $Q=x+z^2$ and require $Q\ne0$. This is exactly the <implicit function theorem> condition, since the defining equation has vertical <partial derivative> $3Q$. Differentiating the cubic relation in $x$ and $y$ gives
$$
Qz_x=-z,\qquad Qz_y=1,
\qquad z_x=-\frac zQ,\quad z_y=\frac1Q.
$$
For a second <derivative>, the dependence of $Q$ on $z$ must also be included: $Q_x=1+2zz_x$ and $Q_y=2zz_y$. Thus
$$
\begin{aligned}
z_{xx}&=-\frac{z_x}{Q}+\frac{z(1+2zz_x)}{Q^2}=\frac{2xz}{Q^3},\\
z_{yy}&=-\frac{2zz_y}{Q^2}=-\frac{2z}{Q^3},\\
z_{xy}&=-\frac{z_y}{Q}+\frac{2z^2z_y}{Q^2}=\frac{z^2-x}{Q^3}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The required cancellation follows immediately:
$$
\boxed{z_{xx}+xz_{yy}=\frac{2xz}{(x+z^2)^3}-\frac{2xz}{(x+z^2)^3}=0.}
$$
The verification is local to smooth branches with $x+z^2\ne0$; the implicit relation does not guarantee a differentiable function at its singular points.
