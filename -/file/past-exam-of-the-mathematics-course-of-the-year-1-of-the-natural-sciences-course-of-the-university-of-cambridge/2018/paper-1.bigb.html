The factor $1+i$ has <modulus> $\sqrt2$ and <principal argument> $\pi/4$. Multiplication by $e^{i\pi/6}$ adds $\pi/6$ to its <complex argument>, while the real factor $1/2$ halves its <modulus>. Hence
$$
\boxed{|z|=\frac1{\sqrt2},\qquad\arg z=\frac{5\pi}{12}+2\pi k\quad(k\in\mathbb Z).}
$$
Taking the <principal logarithm> for $w$ gives
$$
w=-\frac12\ln2+i\frac{5\pi}{12}.
$$
This <complex number> lies in the second quadrant. Its <modulus> follows from the sum of squared components, and its <principal argument> follows by the second-quadrant correction:
$$
\boxed{|w|=\sqrt{\frac{(\ln2)^2}{4}+\frac{25\pi^2}{144}},\qquad
\operatorname{Arg}w=\pi-\arctan\frac{5\pi}{6\ln2}.}
$$
All arguments of this fixed $w$ add integer multiples of $2\pi$ to the displayed one.

If $\ln z$ is interpreted as the multivalued <complex logarithm>, there is instead a family $w_k=-a+ib_k$, where $a=\tfrac12\ln2>0$ and $b_k=5\pi/12+2\pi k$. Then $|w_k|=\sqrt{a^2+b_k^2}$, and the <principal argument> is $\pi-\arctan(b_k/a)$ for $b_k>0$, or $-\pi-\arctan(b_k/a)$ for $b_k<0$. This records the logarithm-branch choice that the paper leaves implicit.

{parent=a}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Reverse the order in the nonnegative <double integral>, using $0\le y<\infty$ and $y\le x\le2y$. For $y>0$, an <antiderivative> in $x$ is $-(x/y+1/y^2)e^{-xy}$. Thus
$$
I=\int_0^\infty\left[\left(1+\frac1{y^2}\right)e^{-y^2}
-\left(2+\frac1{y^2}\right)e^{-2y^2}\right]dy.
$$
Let $J$ denote the requested <improper integral>. Its numerator is $y^2+O(y^4)$ near zero, so its apparent singularity is removable; it also decays at infinity. We may therefore separate the preceding expression into the convergent integrals
$$
I=\int_0^\infty(e^{-y^2}-2e^{-2y^2})\,dy+J
=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}(1-\sqrt2)+J=-I+J.
$$
It follows that
$$
\boxed{J=\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-x^2}-e^{-2x^2}}{x^2}\,dx=\sqrt\pi(\sqrt2-1).}
$$
The two terms divided separately by $x^2$ would each have a divergent <integral> at zero; the cancellation in their difference is essential.

For real solutions with $x>0$, the square root requires $y\ge0$. First retain the solution $y\equiv0$, which would be lost by dividing by a square root of $y$.

For $y>0$, use the <homogeneous first-order differential equation> substitution $v=\sqrt{y/x}>0$, so $y=xv^2$, $\sqrt{xy}=xv$ and $y'=v^2+2xvv'$. The equation becomes
$$
(1+v)(v^2+2xvv')=v^2,
\qquad\frac{2(1+v)}{v^2}\,dv=-\frac{dx}{x}.
$$
This is now a <separable differential equation>. Integration gives $-2/v+2\ln v=-\ln x+C$. Since $xv^2=y$, the positive solutions satisfy
$$
\boxed{\ln y-2\sqrt{\frac xy}=C\quad(y>0),\qquad\text{together with }y\equiv0.}
$$
The implicit family is sufficient here because only parts (a) and (b) request an explicit expression. It also shows why the nonnegative choice of $v$ matters: $\sqrt{xy}$ denotes the positive square root.

Evaluate the <Hessian matrix> at the five <stationary points>. At the origin it is
$$
H(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}-2&-4\\-4&-2\end{pmatrix},
$$
whose <eigenvalues> are $-6$ and $2$, so the origin is a <saddle point>. At the two points with $x=y=\pm\sqrt3/2$,
$$
H=e^{-3/2}\begin{pmatrix}10&2\\2&10\end{pmatrix},
$$
whose <eigenvalues> are $12e^{-3/2}$ and $8e^{-3/2}$. Both are positive, so these are strict <local minima>. At the two points with $x=-y=\pm1/2$,
$$
H=e^{-1/2}\begin{pmatrix}-6&-2\\-2&-6\end{pmatrix},

whose <eigenvalues> are $-8e^{-1/2}$ and $-4e^{-1/2}$. Both are negative, so these are strict <local maxima>. In summary,
$$
\boxed{(0,0):\text{ saddle};\quad x=y=\pm\sqrt3/2:\text{ minima};\quad x=-y=\pm1/2:\text{ maxima}.}
$$
The quadratic changes along the <orthogonal> directions $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ also provide a direct justification of these classifications.

The completed <contour plot> is shown in the right-hand panel. Its geometry follows from the symmetries $f(-x,-y)=f(x,y)$ and $f(y,x)=f(x,y)$, together with $f\to0$ as $x^2+y^2\to\infty$. The first $n$ terms of <Taylor's theorem>, with the <Taylor remainder> in mean-value form, are
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k+R_n(x),\qquad R_n(x)=\frac{f^{(n)}(\xi)}{n!}(x-a)^n,}
$$
where $\xi$ lies between $a$ and $x$ for $x\ne a$. Sufficient hypotheses are continuous <derivatives> through order $n-1$ on the interval between the endpoints and an $n$th <derivative> in its interior. At $x=a$ the remainder is zero. The finite <Taylor polynomial> ends at degree $n-1$; this identity does not require convergence of an infinite <Taylor series>.

Since $a_0=f(0)\ne0$, write $1/f(x)=b_0+b_1x+b_2x^2+O(x^3)$ and multiply the two <power series>. Equating coefficients with the identity $f(x)/f(x)=1$ gives
$$
a_0b_0=1,\qquad a_0b_1+a_1b_0=0,\qquad a_0b_2+a_1b_1+a_2b_0=0.
$$
Solving successively yields
$$
\boxed{\frac1{f(x)}=\frac1{a_0}-\frac{a_1}{a_0^2}x+\frac{a_1^2-a_0a_2}{a_0^3}x^2+O(x^3).}
$$
These are the constant, linear and quadratic terms; particular coefficients may vanish. For a convergent <power series> defining $f$ near zero, continuity and $a_0\ne0$ ensure its reciprocal is also defined in a neighborhood of zero.

The stated derivative formula needs a domain qualification. The original PDF calls $\alpha$ real, but the integral is even in $\alpha$; its derivative therefore cannot equal the printed positive expression on both sides of zero.

First take $\alpha>0$. The <improper integral> converges, with an $O(x^2)$ integrand near zero and $O(\ln x/x^2)$ decay at infinity. On compact positive parameter intervals, an integrable bound also justifies <differentiation under the integral sign>:
$$
E'(\alpha)=\int_0^\infty\frac{2\alpha x^2}{(1+\alpha^2x^2)(1+x^2)}\,dx.
$$
For $\alpha\ne1$, the <partial fraction decomposition> is
$$
\frac{x^2}{(1+\alpha^2x^2)(1+x^2)}
=\frac1{\alpha^2-1}\left(\frac1{1+x^2}-\frac1{1+\alpha^2x^2}\right).
$$
Integrating the rational functions gives
$$
E'(\alpha)=\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha^2-1}\left(\frac\pi2-\frac\pi{2\alpha}\right)
=\boxed{\frac\pi{1+\alpha}\quad(\alpha>0).}
$$
At $\alpha=1$, the same result follows by continuity, or directly from $2\int_0^\infty x^2/(1+x^2)^2\,dx=\pi/2$.

Integrating gives $E(\alpha)=\pi\ln(1+\alpha)+C$ on the positive half-line. To fix $C$, take $\alpha\to0^+$: for $0\le\alpha\le1$ the integrand is bounded by $\ln(1+x^2)/(1+x^2)$, an integrable function. The <dominated convergence theorem> gives $E(\alpha)\to E(0)=0$, so $C=0$. Evenness then extends the <logarithmic parameter integral with Cauchy weight> to its full stated domain:
$$
\boxed{E(\alpha)=\pi\ln(1+|\alpha|)\quad(\alpha\in\mathbb R).}
$$
For negative $\alpha$, the derivative is $-\pi/(1-\alpha)$. At zero the right and left <derivatives> are $\pi$ and $-\pi$, so $E$ is not <differentiable> there. The pointwise derivative of the integrand at zero is zero, but interchanging it with the infinite <integral> at that parameter is invalid. $$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty\frac{\ln(1+x^2)}{1+x^2}\,dx=\pi\ln2.}
$$
