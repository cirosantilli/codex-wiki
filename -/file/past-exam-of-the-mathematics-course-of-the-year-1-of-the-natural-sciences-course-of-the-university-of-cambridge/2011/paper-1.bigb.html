Differentiating the <hyperbolic cosine> with the <chain rule> gives $g'(x)=3\cosh^2x\sinh x$. For real $x$, $\cosh x>0$, so the only zero of the <derivative> is $\sinh x=0$, namely $x=0$. Thus

= Solution
{parent=b}

For $x>0$, <logarithmic differentiation> gives
$$
\ln f(x)=\frac{\ln x}{x},\qquad
\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}=\frac{1-\ln x}{x^2}.
$$
Since $f(x)>0$, the <derivative> vanishes precisely when $\ln x=1$. Hence
$$
\boxed{x_0=e,\qquad f(x_0)=e^{1/e}.}
$$
The <derivative> is positive before $e$ and negative after $e$, so this is a <global maximum> on the given positive domain.

The distance from the origin is $r=4\pi-t$. The direction angle, measured counterclockwise from the positive horizontal axis, is $\theta=\pi/2-t$ modulo $2\pi$. Thus the path is an <Archimedean spiral>, turning clockwise through two complete revolutions as its <radius> decreases linearly from $4\pi$ to zero.

It starts at $(0,4\pi)$, reaches $(7\pi/2,0)$ at $t=\pi/2$, crosses $(0,-3\pi)$ at $t=\pi$, returns to the positive vertical axis at $(0,2\pi)$ when $t=2\pi$, and ends at the origin when $t=4\pi$. These crossings fix both the scale and direction of the sketch.

At the stated point, the radial distance is $7\pi/2$, so $4\pi-t=7\pi/2$ and $t=\pi/2$. Differentiate the <parametric curve>:
$$
\dot x=-\sin t+(4\pi-t)\cos t,\qquad
\dot y=-\cos t-(4\pi-t)\sin t.
$$
At $t=\pi/2$, these are $\dot x=-1$ and $\dot y=-7\pi/2$. Since $\dot x\ne0$, the <tangent line> has finite <slope>
$$
\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{\dot y}{\dot x}=\frac{7\pi}{2}.}
$$

Let the <equilateral triangle> have side <length> $a$. Its altitude is $H=\sqrt3a/2$, and its <incircle> has <radius> $r=H/3=a/(2\sqrt3)$: the <centre> lies on a median, one third of the altitude above the base. The <area> of the triangle is $aH/2=\sqrt3a^2/4$, while the <circle> has <area> $\pi r^2=\pi a^2/12$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{\mathcal A_{\rm circle}}{\mathcal A_{\rm triangle}}
=\frac{\pi}{3\sqrt3}=\frac{\pi\sqrt3}{9}\approx0.604600.}
$$
The side <length> cancels, as required for a dimensionless ratio between similar configurations.

Write $\bar s>0$ for the <mean> in minutes. An <exponential distribution> with that <mean> has rate $1/\bar s$, so its <probability density function> is

= Solution
{parent=c}

For a small rectangle of side lengths $ds,dt$, <independence> makes its <probability> approximately $f_S(s)ds\,f_T(t)dt$. Thus the <joint probability density function> is the product
$$
f_{S,T}(s,t)=\frac1{\bar s\bar t}e^{-s/\bar s-t/\bar t},\qquad s,t\geq0.
$$
Adding the <probabilities> of disjoint small rectangles covering a <region of integration>, and taking their sizes to zero, gives the <double integral> of this product over that region. Without <independence>, the two <marginal distributions> alone would not determine the <joint probability density function>.

{parent=12}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Integrate over the region $0\leq s<t$:
$$
P(T>S)=\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-s/\bar s}}{\bar s}
\left[\int_s^\infty\frac{e^{-t/\bar t}}{\bar t}dt\right]ds
=\frac1{\bar s}\int_0^\infty e^{-s(1/\bar s+1/\bar t)}ds.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{P(\text{no interruption})=\frac{\bar t}{\bar s+\bar t}.}
$$
This is also the two-clock case of <competing exponential clocks>. A larger <mean> time to noise increases this <probability>, while a larger <mean> service <length> decreases it.

= Solution

Use the lower limit $s=t+r_0$ for the inner integral:
$$
P(r>r_0)=\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-t/\bar t}}{\bar t}
\left[\int_{t+r_0}^\infty\frac{e^{-s/\bar s}}{\bar s}ds\right]dt
=\frac{e^{-r_0/\bar s}}{\bar t}\int_0^\infty e^{-t(1/\bar s+1/\bar t)}dt.

Hence
$$
\boxed{P(r>r_0)=\frac{\bar s}{\bar s+\bar t}e^{-r_0/\bar s},\qquad r_0>0.}
$$
The prefactor is the <probability> that interruption occurs; the remaining exponential is its conditional positive tail. This is the <positive difference of independent exponential waiting times>. The exponent is dimensionless because both $r_0$ and $\bar s$ are measured in minutes.

The time already elapsed since the noise is $32$ minutes. Conditional on a particular noise time $T=t$ and on still being in progress, <memorylessness of the exponential distribution> gives
$$

In the real <Fourier series> convention for <period> $2\pi$,
$$
\boxed{y(x)\sim\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}[a_n\cos(nx)+b_n\sin(nx)],}
$$
where the <Fourier coefficients> are
$$
a_n=\frac1\pi\int_0^{2\pi}y(x)\cos(nx)dx\quad(n\geq0),\qquad
b_n=\frac1\pi\int_0^{2\pi}y(x)\sin(nx)dx\quad(n\geq1).
$$
These formulas apply to integrable periodic <functions>. Writing equality at each point requires appropriate convergence hypotheses; the periodic solution sought below is <smooth>, so its series causes no such difficulty.

Differentiate the <Fourier series> term by term and collect <cosine> and <sine> terms. Substitution into the equation gives

\boxed{\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}

<Fourier orthogonality> isolates a <coefficient>. Multiplication by $\sin(mx)$ and <integration> over $[0,2\pi]$ annihilates the constant, all <cosine> terms and all <sine> terms except the one with $n=m$, whose squared integral is $\pi$. Multiplication by $\cos(mx)$ works in the same way, while <integration> against the constant mode extracts $a_0$. This is the uniqueness of the <Fourier coefficients>; it does not follow from comparing a few point values of the series.

The <vector triple product identity> gives
$$
\boxed{a\times(b\times c)=(a\cdot c)b-(a\cdot b)c.}
$$
It is the requested <linear combination> of $b$ and $c$. The notation $\wedge$ in this question denotes the three-dimensional <cross product>.

= b
{parent=15}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Expand the same double <cross product> in two ways. First expanding with $c\times d$ as the inner <cross product> gives
$$
(a\times b)\times(c\times d)
=[(a\times b)\cdot d]c-[(a\times b)\cdot c]d
=[a,b,d]c-[a,b,c]d.
$$
Alternatively, use $(a\times b)\times w=(a\cdot w)b-(b\cdot w)a$ with $w=c\times d$ to get
$$
(a\times b)\times(c\times d)=[a,c,d]b-[b,c,d]a.
$$
Equating these expressions and moving every term to one side proves
$$
[b,c,d]a-[a,c,d]b+[a,b,d]c-[a,b,c]d=0.
$$
This <four-vector scalar triple product identity> is valid without assuming that any three of the four <vectors> are independent.

{parent=15}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

At an intersection, the two <position> <vectors> agree, so $a-b=tn-sm$. The <scalar triple product> with $m,n$ is therefore
$$
[a-b,m,n]=t[n,m,n]-s[m,m,n]=\boxed{0},
$$
since a <determinant> with repeated columns vanishes. Geometrically the displacement of the two anchor points must lie in the same <plane> as the two <line> directions.

This is a necessary condition. If $m,n$ are not parallel, it is also sufficient, because their span is exactly the <plane> perpendicular to $m\times n$. For parallel directions the triple product vanishes automatically, even for distinct <lines>, so the condition alone cannot guarantee intersection.

Put $D=[a,m,n]\ne0$ and rewrite the intersection equation as $b=a+sm-tn$. Take <scalar triple products> with $a$ and $n$:
