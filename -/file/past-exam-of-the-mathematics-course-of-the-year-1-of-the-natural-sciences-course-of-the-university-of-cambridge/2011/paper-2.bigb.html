The <gradient> part contributes zero around a closed path by the <fundamental theorem for line integrals>:
$$
\oint_{\Gamma_1}3\nabla\phi\cdot d\mathbf x=0.
$$
Along the parametrization, $x^2+z^2=1$, so the first two components of $\mathbf w$ vanish wherever the printed expression is defined. The remaining component gives $\mathbf w\cdot d\mathbf x=x\,dz=\cos^2\theta\,d\theta$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\oint_{\Gamma_1}(3\mathbf v+\mathbf w)\cdot d\mathbf x
=\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta=\pi}.
$$
**The printed field needs a qualification:** $\operatorname{arctanh}(x^2)$ is undefined at the isolated parameters where $x^2=1$, so $0\operatorname{arctanh}(1)$ cannot literally be evaluated. The result above is the <improper line integral>, or equivalently the <integral> of the continuous extension of the restricted field along the <curve>. That restricted field tends to $(0,0,\cos\theta)$ at each omitted parameter. No extension of the full field to a neighbourhood is being assumed.

= Solution

Hold $r$ fixed and differentiate the position <vector> $\mathbf X(\theta,\phi)$. Its coordinate tangent <vectors> are
$$
\mathbf X_\theta=r(\cos\theta\cos\phi,\cos\theta\sin\phi,-\sin\theta),\qquad
\mathbf X_\phi=r(-\sin\theta\sin\phi,\sin\theta\cos\phi,0).
$$
Their <dot product> is zero, and their squared <Euclidean norms> are $r^2$ and $r^2\sin^2\theta$. To first order, the displacement is $\delta\mathbf X=\mathbf X_\theta\delta\theta+\mathbf X_\phi\delta\phi+O((|\delta\theta|+|\delta\phi|)^2)$. Squaring gives
$$
\boxed{\delta s^2\simeq r^2(\delta\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\delta\phi^2)}.
$$
This is the <line element on a sphere>. The chord distance and the short surface <arc length> agree to the order retained here.

The <transpose> reverses product order, so $(CB)^{\mathsf T}=B^{\mathsf T}C^{\mathsf T}$. If $B$ is an <orthogonal matrix>, then $B^{-1}=B^{\mathsf T}$ and the given identity becomes
$$
B^{\mathsf T}(C^{\mathsf T}+C)=0.
$$
Multiply on the left by $B$ to obtain $C^{\mathsf T}+C=0$. Therefore **$C$ is antisymmetric**, so it is an <antisymmetric matrix>, namely $\boxed{C^{\mathsf T}=-C}$.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Take <determinants> of $(CB)^{\mathsf T}=-B^{-1}C$. In order three, multiplication of a <matrix> by minus one changes its <determinant>'s sign. Hence
$$
\det C\,\det B=-\frac{\det C}{\det B}.
$$
The assumption $\det(B^{\mathsf T})\ne0$ is the same as $\det B\ne0$, so multiplication by $\det B$ gives
$$
\bigl((\det B)^2+1\bigr)\det C=0.
$$
Since the <matrices> are real, the first factor is strictly positive. Thus $\boxed{\det C=0}$, proving that $C$ is a <singular matrix>. This is <singularity forced by an odd-dimensional transpose-inverse identity>; no <orthogonality> of $B$ was used.

First solve for the <velocity> components. The <integrating factor> $e^{\gamma t}$ gives
$$
\dot x=V_{0x}e^{-\gamma t},\qquad
\dot z=\left(V_{0z}+\frac g\gamma\right)e^{-\gamma t}-\frac g\gamma.
$$
Integrating once more and imposing the initial position gives
$$
\boxed{x(t)=\frac{V_{0x}}\gamma(1-e^{-\gamma t}),\qquad
z(t)=\frac{V_{0z}+g/\gamma}\gamma(1-e^{-\gamma t})-\frac g\gamma t}.
$$
These formulas describe <projectile motion with linear drag>. Differentiation checks both second-order <differential equations> and both initial velocities. At large positive <time>, the horizontal <velocity> tends to zero and the vertical <velocity> tends to the terminal value $-g/\gamma$.

If $V_{0x}\ne0$, the first formula in (a) gives $e^{-\gamma t}=1-\gamma x/V_{0x}$ and
$$

Temporarily label the individual pieces. A random placement is a uniform <injection> of thirty-two distinct pieces into sixty-four squares, giving ${}_{64}P_{32}$ equally likely outcomes. For a colour-matching placement, the black pieces have ${}_{32}P_{16}$ available <injections>, and independently the white pieces have the same number within their disjoint set of squares. Therefore
$$
\boxed{P=\frac{({}_{32}P_{16})^2}{{}_{64}P_{32}}
=\frac{(32!)^3}{(16!)^2\,64!}}.

Here ${}_nP_r=n!/(n-r)!$. Treating same-colour pieces as indistinguishable gives the same <probability>, because the common within-colour labeling factors cancel. The two colour choices in the favourable count occupy disjoint square sets; no independence assumption about unrestricted placements is required.

Let $D=(1-x)^2+y^2$. Direct <partial differentiation>, away from $(1,0)$, gives
$$
u_x=v_y=\frac{2((1-x)^2-y^2)}{D^2},\qquad
u_y=-v_x=-\frac{4(1-x)y}{D^2}.
$$
These are the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>. Equivalently, with $z=x+iy$, the analytic mapping is $u+iv=(1+z)/(1-z)$, the inverse <Cayley transform between the half-plane and disk>.

The supplied harmonicity result can also be checked by the <multivariable chain rule>. The <Cauchy-Riemann equations> imply $\Delta u=\Delta v=0$, $\nabla u\cdot\nabla v=0$, and $|\nabla u|^2=|\nabla v|^2$. Hence
$$
\Delta\Psi=(u_x^2+u_y^2)(\Phi_{uu}+\Phi_{vv})=0.
$$
This is <conformal invariance of harmonicity> for the given mapping.

Simplify the real component to $u=(1-x^2-y^2)/D$. Thus $u=0$ is the unit <circle> with $(1,0)$ excluded from the mapping's domain. Its upper semicircle has $v>0$ and its lower semicircle has $v<0$; the point $(-1,0)$ maps to $u=v=0$. Inside the <disk>, $u>0$, so the principal arctangent is unambiguous. Setting $\alpha=V$ gives
$$
\boxed{\Psi(x,y)=\frac{2V}{\pi}\arctan\frac{2y}{1-x^2-y^2},\qquad x^2+y^2<1}.
$$
The <Laplace equation> follows from (b). Approaching an open upper <boundary> arc makes the ratio tend to $+\infty$, giving $+V$; the lower arc gives $-V$. This solution is $V(2F-1)$ in terms of the <harmonic measure of a semicircle in a disk> $F$.

The <boundary> data jump at $(\pm1,0)$, so no path-independent continuous <boundary> value can be imposed at those two junctions. The displayed bounded <harmonic function> is the solution with the specified limits on the open arcs; those endpoint discontinuities are part of the idealized <capacitor> geometry.

Apply the <Leibniz rule> to the $n$th <derivative> in <Rodrigues' formula>. If $j$ <derivatives> fall on $e^{-x}$ and $n-j$ on $x^n$, the corresponding term is $\binom nj(-1)^je^{-x}(n!/j!)x^j$. Cancelling the prefactor gives
$$
\boxed{L_n(x)=\sum_{j=0}^n(-1)^j\binom nj\frac{x^j}{j!}}.
$$
The leading coefficient $(-1)^n/n!$ is nonzero, so this is a <polynomial> of degree exactly $n$. In particular, the requested coefficients in $L_{10}$ are
$$
\boxed{[x^{10}]L_{10}=\frac1{10!},\qquad [x^9]L_{10}=-\frac{10!}{(9!)^2}}.
$$
These are the ordinary <Laguerre polynomials>, with normalization $L_n(0)=1$.

Differentiating $v=x^ne^{-x}$ gives $xv'=(n-x)v$. Applying the <Leibniz rule> $n+1$ times to this identity gives
$$
xv^{(n+2)}+(n+1)v^{(n+1)}=(n-x)v^{(n+1)}-(n+1)v^{(n)}.
$$
Only two terms survive on each side because the multiplying factors are linear. Rearranging yields $xv^{(n+2)}+(x+1)v^{(n+1)}+(n+1)v^{(n)}=0$. Since $v^{(n)}=n!e^{-x}L_n$, the next two <derivatives> are $n!e^{-x}(L_n'-L_n)$ and $n!e^{-x}(L_n''-2L_n'+L_n)$. Substituting and cancelling $n!e^{-x}$ gives
$$
\boxed{xL_n''+(1-x)L_n'+nL_n=0}.
$$
This is <deriving the Laguerre equation from Rodrigues' formula>, exhibiting $L_n$ as an <eigenfunction> of the <Laguerre differential operator on polynomials>.

Multiply the two displayed equations by $e^{-x}$ and subtract the one with indices reversed. The product terms combine into the <Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator>:
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left[xe^{-x}(L_mL_n'-L_nL_m')\right]
+(n-m)e^{-x}L_mL_n=0.
$$
Integrating from zero to infinity gives
$$
\left[xe^{-x}(L_mL_n'-L_nL_m')\right]_0^\infty
+(n-m)\int_0^\infty e^{-x}L_mL_n\,dx=0.
$$
All factors $L_j,L_j'$ are <polynomials>. The endpoint term at zero vanishes because of the factor $x$, and the one at infinity vanishes because the <exponential function> dominates every <polynomial>. Therefore, if $m\ne n$,
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty e^{-x}L_m(x)L_n(x)\,dx=0}.
$$
The <integral> is absolutely convergent. This is <orthogonality> in the <weighted inner product> with weight $e^{-x}$; the <boundary> cancellation is the self-adjoint mechanism behind the <Laguerre differential operator on polynomials>.
