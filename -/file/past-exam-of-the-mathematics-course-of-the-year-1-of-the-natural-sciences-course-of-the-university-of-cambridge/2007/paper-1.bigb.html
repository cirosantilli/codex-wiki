The displayed <power series> is the <matrix exponential> $G=e^H$. From $H^3=-\alpha^2H$, its odd powers and nonconstant even powers sum separately:
$$
G=I+\left(1-\frac{\alpha^2}{3!}+\frac{\alpha^4}{5!}-\cdots\right)H+\left(\frac1{2!}-\frac{\alpha^2}{4!}+\frac{\alpha^4}{6!}-\cdots\right)H^2.
$$
The scalar <power series> are $\sin\alpha/\alpha$ and $(1-\cos\alpha)/\alpha^2$, with continuous values $1$ and $1/2$ at zero. Substituting $H\mathbf x=\alpha\mathbf n\times\mathbf x$ and the preceding formula for $H^2\mathbf x$ yields
$$
G\mathbf x=\mathbf x+\sin\alpha\,\mathbf n\times\mathbf x+(1-\cos\alpha)\mathbf n\times(\mathbf n\times\mathbf x).
$$
This is the <Rodrigues rotation formula>: the component parallel to $\mathbf n$ is fixed, while the perpendicular component undergoes a <rotation> through $\alpha$. For $\alpha=0$ the <linear operator> is the <identity operator>.

The <exponential series> proves <normalization of a probability distribution>:
$$
\sum_{n=0}^\infty e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^n}{n!}=e^{-\lambda}e^\lambda=1.
$$
Let $M$ and $F$ count the two labels. Conditional on $N=M+F=k+l$, independent labeling gives a <binomial distribution>, so the <joint probability mass function> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(M=k,F=l)&=\mathbb P(N=k+l)\binom{k+l}{k}p^k(1-p)^l\\
&=e^{-\lambda}\frac{(\lambda p)^k}{k!}\frac{[\lambda(1-p)]^l}{l!}\\
&=\left[e^{-\lambda p}\frac{(\lambda p)^k}{k!}\right]\left[e^{-\lambda(1-p)}\frac{[\lambda(1-p)]^l}{l!}\right].
\end{aligned}
$$
Each bracket is a normalized <Poisson distribution>. Summing over $l\geq0$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(M=k)=e^{-\lambda p}\frac{(\lambda p)^k}{k!},\qquad M\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda p).}
$$
The factorization also proves that $M$ and $F$ are <independent random variables>. This is <Poisson thinning>; the phrase about ignoring the other count refers to taking a <marginal distribution>, rather than fixing that count.

Put $S=\theta_1+\theta_2$ and $D=\theta_1-\theta_2$. The <gradient> has components $3\sin S+\sin D$ and $3\sin S-\sin D$, so a <stationary point> requires $\sin S=\sin D=0$. In the closed square, $S\in[0,2\pi]$ and $D\in[-\pi,\pi]$, and the compatible possibilities are precisely the four corners and $(\pi/2,\pi/2)$.

The <Hessian matrix> is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}3\cos S+\cos D&3\cos S-\cos D\\3\cos S-\cos D&3\cos S+\cos D\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <eigenvectors> $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ have <eigenvalues> $6\cos S$ and $2\cos D$. The <second-derivative test> gives the following classification:

|| Point || $U$ || Hessian eigenvalues || Classification
| $(0,0),(\pi,\pi)$ | $-4$ | $6,2$ | <Minima>
| $(\pi,0),(0,\pi)$ | $4$ | $-6,-2$ | <Maxima>
| $(\pi/2,\pi/2)$ | $2$ | $-6,2$ | <Saddle point of a scalar function>

The corner classifications also hold for the smooth extension beyond the square, and hence for the restricted region. The global bounds $-4\leq U\leq4$ show that the stated <minima> and <maxima> are global within it. The central point has opposite curvature along the two diagonal directions.

If $A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v$ for a nonzero <eigenvector>, then $A^2\mathbf v=\lambda^2\mathbf v=A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v$. Thus $\lambda(\lambda-1)=0$, so
$$
\boxed{\text{every eigenvalue is }0\text{ or }1.}

Both values need not occur: the zero <matrix> has only $0$, and the <identity matrix> only $1$. More precisely, an <idempotent linear map> whose <rank of a linear map> is $r$ has $r$ copies of $1$ and $n-r$ copies of $0$. For $x\in(n,n+1)$, strict decrease gives $f(x)<f(n)$, while for $x\in(n-1,n)$ it gives $f(x)>f(n)$. Integrating over these unit intervals proves
$$
S_n=\int_n^{n+1}f(x)\,dx<f(n)<\int_{n-1}^nf(x)\,dx=S_{n-1}.
$$
Summing the rectangle bounds gives
$$
\int_2^{M+1}f(x)\,dx<\sum_{n=2}^Mf(n)<f(2)+\int_2^Mf(x)\,dx.
$$
The <partial sums> and the <improper integral> are nondecreasing. These bounds show that one is bounded exactly when the other is bounded, proving the <integral test for convergence>. In the convergent case, sum the same interval inequalities for $n=N+1,N+2,\ldots$ to obtain
$$
\int_{N+1}^\infty f(x)\,dx<R_N:=\sum_{n=N+1}^\infty f(n)<\int_N^\infty f(x)\,dx.
$$
For $f(x)=1/(x\ln x)$, the <antiderivative> is $\ln(\ln x)$, which grows without bound, so the first <series> **diverges**. For $f(x)=1/[x(\ln x)^2]$, the <antiderivative> is $-1/\ln x$, whose tail <integral> is finite, so the second <series> **converges**, with
$$
\boxed{\frac1{\ln(N+1)}<R_N<\frac1{\ln N},\qquad N\geq2.}
$$
Both <functions> are positive and strictly decreasing on $[2,\infty)$, as required.

The <ratio test> uses $L=\lim_{n\to\infty}|a_{n+1}/a_n|$, when it exists for eventually nonzero terms: $L<1$ gives <absolute convergence>, $L>1$ gives divergence, and $L=1$ is inconclusive.

For $a_n=n/(2^n-1)$,
$$
\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=\frac{n+1}{n}\frac{2^n-1}{2^{n+1}-1}\longrightarrow\frac12.
$$
Thus the first <series> **converges absolutely**. For the alternating second <series>,
$$
\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|=2\left(\frac n{n+1}\right)^5\longrightarrow2,
$$
so it **diverges**. Alternating signs cannot rescue terms whose <absolute values> fail to tend to zero.

For $x>0$ write $f(x)=x^{-a}I(x)$, where $I(x)=\int_0^xe^{-t}t^{a-1}\,dt$. Because $a>0$, the lower endpoint is <integrable>. At each positive upper endpoint the <fundamental theorem of calculus> gives $I'(x)=e^{-x}x^{a-1}$. The <product rule> therefore yields
$$
\boxed{f'(x)=\frac{e^{-x}}x-\frac{a}{x}f(x).}
$$
Differentiating once more and substituting this expression for $f'$ gives
$$
\boxed{f''(x)=-\frac{e^{-x}}x-\frac{(a+1)e^{-x}}{x^2}+\frac{a(a+1)}{x^2}f(x).}
$$
Substitution now cancels the $e^{-x}$ terms and the terms proportional to $f$, proving $xf''+(1+a+x)f'+af=0$.

The <change of variables> $t=xu$ identifies $f$ as the <scaled lower incomplete gamma integral> $\int_0^1e^{-xu}u^{a-1}du$. It has a smooth extension at zero, with $f(0)=1/a$, $f'(0)=-1/(a+1)$ and $f''(0)=1/(a+2)$. Thus the apparent singularities in the derivative formulas cancel there; those formulas with denominators $x$ were derived for $x>0$.

Differentiate the rewritten expression. Its new kernel vanishes at the moving upper endpoint, and its <partial derivative> is an <integrable> inverse square root. The <derivative of an Abel integral> is
$$
\boxed{g'(x)=\frac{h(0)}{\sqrt x}+\int_0^x\frac{h'(t)}{\sqrt{x-t}}\,dt,\qquad x>0.}
$$
For a rigorous route, rewrite $2\int_0^xh'(t)\sqrt{x-t}\,dt$ as $\int_0^x\int_0^s h'(t)/\sqrt{s-t}\,dt\,ds$ using <Fubini's theorem>, and apply the <fundamental theorem of calculus> to the continuous inner <integral>. The assumption $h(0)\ne0$ implies $g(x)=2h(0)\sqrt x+O(x^{3/2})$, so the extension $g(0)=0$ has no finite right <derivative> at zero.
