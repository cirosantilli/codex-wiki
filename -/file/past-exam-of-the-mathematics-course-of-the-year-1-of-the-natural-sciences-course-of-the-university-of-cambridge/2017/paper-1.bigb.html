In <polar coordinates>, $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$, with $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. Thus the <polar form of a complex number> is
$$
\boxed{z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)=re^{i\theta}.}

Here $r=|z|\geq0$ is its <complex modulus>; for $z\ne0$, the <complex argument> $\theta$ is determined modulo $2\pi$. At $z=0$ the angle is undefined, although every value of $\theta$ gives the same point when $r=0$.

= Solution

Assume $F$ is <continuously differentiable> near the point of interest on $F=0$, and that the relevant denominators below are nonzero. The <total differential> on the surface is
$$
dF=F_x\,dx+F_y\,dy+F_z\,dz=0.
$$
Holding $z$ fixed gives $F_x\,dx+F_y\,dy=0$, so the <implicit function theorem> gives
$$
\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right)_z=-\frac{F_x}{F_y},\qquad
\left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\right)_z=-\frac{F_y}{F_x}.
$$
When $F_xF_y\ne0$, their product proves the <reciprocity rule for constrained partial derivatives>:
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right)_z
\left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\right)_z=1.}
$$
Similarly, holding $y$ or $x$ fixed gives $(\partial x/\partial z)_y=-F_z/F_x$ and $(\partial z/\partial y)_x=-F_y/F_z$. With $F_xF_yF_z\ne0$, multiply the three ratios:
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right)_z
\left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial z}\right)_y
\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\right)_x
=\left(-\frac{F_x}{F_y}\right)\left(-\frac{F_z}{F_x}\right)\left(-\frac{F_y}{F_z}\right)=-1.}
$$
This is the <cyclic partial derivative identity>. Nonzero denominators and local differentiable coordinate representations are substantive assumptions: at a singular coordinate representation, a formal cancellation of zero or infinite factors is not a valid proof.

There is a global-domain issue in the printed question. Write $D=a^2x^2+b^2y^2>0$ away from the origin. Direct <partial differentiation> gives
$$
P_y=-\frac{ab(a^2x^2-b^2y^2)}{D^2}
=Q_x.
$$
Thus the <differential one-form> is a <closed differential form> for **every real $a,b\ne0$**. The <Poincaré lemma> makes it locally an <exact differential form> on small neighborhoods avoiding the origin. There is no additional condition such as $a^2=b^2$.

However, the stated domain is the whole <punctured plane>, which is not <simply connected>. To test global exactness, use the closed <ellipse> $x=\cos t/a$, $y=\sin t/b$, $0\leq t\leq2\pi$. Here $D=1$ and
$$
P\,dx+Q\,dy=ab(x\,dy-y\,dx)=dt,\qquad
\oint(P\,dx+Q\,dy)=2\pi.
$$
The <line integral> of a globally <exact differential form> around any closed curve would be zero. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\text{globally exact on }\mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\}:\ \text{no allowed }a,b;\qquad
\text{locally exact}:\ \text{all }a,b\ne0.}
$$
The distinction is precisely that of the <elliptically scaled angular one-form>. The remaining parts can be answered consistently using a local angle branch, as follows.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

On a chart with $x\ne0$, set
$$
\boxed{f(x,y)=\arctan\!\left(\frac{by}{ax}\right)+C.}
$$
The <chain rule> verifies both components:
$$
f_x=\frac1{1+(by/(ax))^2}\left(-\frac{by}{ax^2}\right)
=-\frac{aby}{D}=P,\qquad
f_y=\frac1{1+(by/(ax))^2}\frac{b}{ax}
=\frac{abx}{D}=Q.
$$
A coordinate-independent description of the local potential is a continuous branch of $\arg(ax+iby)$, the <complex argument> of the scaled coordinates. Such branches can also cover points where $x=0$, using a different angular chart. Continuing once around the missing origin changes that angle by a nonzero multiple of $2\pi$, so **there is no global single-valued $f$ on the full stated domain**.

= Solution

The nonzero factor $ab/D$ can be cancelled in the local <differential equation>, leaving $x\,dy-y\,dx=0$. On an interval with $x\ne0$,
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac yx\right)=\frac{xy'-y}{x^2}=0.
$$
Hence the explicit <general solution> is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=Cx,\qquad C\in\mathbb R,}
$$
on any interval that avoids the excluded origin. It also follows from fixing the local angle potential, with slope $C=(a/b)\tan(f-C_0)$. As parameterized curves there are also vertical rays $x=0$, which cannot be represented as single-valued functions $y(x)$. Geometrically the connected <integral curves> are rays, not lines continued through the undefined origin.

For the first-order <linear ordinary differential equation>, seek an <integrating factor> $M(x)$ that makes its left side a <product rule> <derivative>. Multiplying by $M$ gives $My'+MPy$, and comparison with $(My)'=My'+M'y$ requires $M'=PM$. On an interval where $P$ is continuous choose
$$
M(x)=\exp\!\left(\int_{x_0}^xP(t)\,dt\right),\qquad M(x_0)=1.
$$
This factor is never zero. The transformed equation is $(My)'=MQ$, so integration from $x_0$ to $x$ yields
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{1}{M(x)}\left[C+\int_{x_0}^x M(t)Q(t)\,dt\right].}
$$
Here $C=y(x_0)$ for an <initial value problem>. The <indefinite integral> version is $y=e^{-\int Pdx}[C+\int Qe^{\int Pdx}dx]$, with any constant choices inside the <integrating factor> absorbed into $C$. Continuity of $P,Q$ is a convenient sufficient hypothesis for a classical differentiable <solution of a differential equation>.

Since $1+x^2>0$ for all real $x$, divide the equation by that factor. Its linear coefficient can be integrated by <polynomial division>:
$$
P(x)=\frac{3x^3}{1+x^2}=3x-\frac{3x}{1+x^2},\qquad
\int P(x)\,dx=\frac32x^2-\frac32\ln(1+x^2).
$$
Thus a positive <integrating factor> is $M=e^{3x^2/2}(1+x^2)^{-3/2}$. Multiplying the divided equation gives the <product rule> form
$$
\frac d{dx}(My)=\frac{6x}{(1+x^2)^{5/2}}.
$$
The <integration by substitution> $u=1+x^2$ gives
$$
My=-\frac{2}{(1+x^2)^{3/2}}+C,\qquad
y=e^{-3x^2/2}\big[C(1+x^2)^{3/2}-2\big].
$$
The <initial condition> $y(0)=1$ fixes $C-2=1$. The required solution is therefore
$$
\boxed{y(x)=e^{-3x^2/2}\left[3(1+x^2)^{3/2}-2\right],\qquad x\in\mathbb R.}
$$
No real singularity was introduced by the division or the <integrating factor>, so the solution extends over the entire real line.

An interior <stationary point> has vanishing <gradient>:
$$
\boxed{\nabla f(x_0,y_0)=\mathbf0,\qquad f_x(x_0,y_0)=f_y(x_0,y_0)=0.}
$$
For a differentiable <function>, this is the necessary first-order condition for an interior <local minimum> or <local maximum>.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Write $A=f_{xx}$, $B=f_{xy}$, $C=f_{yy}$ at the <stationary point>, and let $D=AC-B^2\ne0$. Under the usual second-order <Taylor expansion> hypothesis, the <second-derivative test> gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
D>0,\ A>0&\text{strict local minimum}\\
D>0,\ A<0&\text{strict local maximum}\\
D<0&\text{saddle point}
\end{array}}
$$
Indeed the quadratic term is $(A\xi^2+2B\xi\eta+C\eta^2)/2$. For $A\ne0$, completing its square gives $[A(\xi+B\eta/A)^2+(D/A)\eta^2]/2$. The two coefficients have the same positive sign, the same negative sign, or opposite signs in the respective cases. A negative $D$ also forces the two <Hessian> <eigenvalues> to have opposite signs, including the case $A=0$.

There is a qualification to the printed assumptions: existence of second <partial derivatives> only at the point does not ensure the needed quadratic approximation. Continuous second <partial derivatives> in a neighborhood are sufficient. The <existence of second partial derivatives does not justify the Hessian test>: for example, the function $g=x^2+y^2-6x^2y^2/(x^2+y^2)$ away from the origin, extended by $g(0,0)=0$, has <Hessian> $2I$ at the origin but $g(t,t)=-t^2$ and $g(t,0)=t^2$. Thus the classification above uses the standard additional regularity assumption, which the smooth function in part (b) satisfies.

Put $G=(x^2+y^2)/2+ax^4/4$, so $f=e^G>0$. Since $f_x=fG_x$ and $f_y=fG_y$, its <stationary points> satisfy
$$
y=0,\qquad x(1+ax^2)=0.
$$
The origin occurs for every real $a$. There are two additional points only for $a<0$, at $x=\pm1/\sqrt{-a}$.

At a <stationary point>, the <Hessian matrix> simplifies because $\nabla G=0$:
$$
H_f=fH_G=f\begin{pmatrix}1+3ax^2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
At the origin this is $I$, so **$(0,0)$ is a strict local minimum for every $a$**, with $f=1$. If $a\geq0$ it is also the unique global minimum, since $G\geq0$ with equality only there. For $a<0$, the two extra points have $1+3ax^2=-2$, so their <Hessian> has opposite-sign <eigenvalues>. Thus
$$
\boxed{a<0:\ \left(\pm\frac1{\sqrt{-a}},0\right)\text{ are saddle points},\qquad
f=\exp\!\left(-\frac1{4a}\right).}
$$
There are no <local maxima>. When $a<0$, the origin is not a global minimum: $f(x,0)\to0$ as $|x|\to\infty$, but $f$ never attains zero.

Because the <exponential function> is strictly increasing, the <level sets> $f=L>0$ are exactly those of $G=\ln L$. For $a=1$ they satisfy
$$
y^2=2\ln L-x^2-\frac{x^4}{2}.
$$
There are no contours for $L<1$, the level $L=1$ is the origin, and $L>1$ gives nested closed ovals symmetric about both axes. Their stronger fourth-power growth in $x$ makes them relatively narrower horizontally as the level rises.

For $a=-1$, the contour equation becomes
$$
y^2=2\ln L-x^2+\frac{x^4}{2}.

\boxed{y(x)=\sqrt[3]{x^3+Cx},\qquad C\in\mathbb R,}

The seven requested <parabolas> are $y=cx^2$ for $c=-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3$; the case $c=0$ is the horizontal axis. Positive $c$ opens upward, negative $c$ downward, and larger $|c|$ gives a narrower <parabola>. All pass through the origin with a horizontal <tangent line>.

To find <orthogonal trajectories>, use the continuous family $y=cx^2$ containing these seven representative members. Its slope is $y'=2cx=2y/x$ away from $x=0$. Two finite slopes of perpendicular tangents multiply to $-1$, so the orthogonal slope is
$$
\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{x}{2y}.
$$
This <separable differential equation> gives $2y\,dy=-x\,dx$, hence
$$
\boxed{x^2+2y^2=C,\qquad C>0.}

These <ellipses> are the regular real trajectories; $C=0$ is a single point, not a regular curve, and $C<0$ has no real points. One example is $x^2+2y^2=2$, with semiaxes $\sqrt2$ horizontally and $1$ vertically.

The implicit tangent relation $2x\,dx+4y\,dy=0$ also describes points with vertical tangents, where the slope quotient is unavailable.
