Expand the <vector triple product> in Cartesian components. Its first component is
$$
\begin{aligned}
[\mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)]_x
&=a_y(b_xc_y-b_yc_x)-a_z(b_zc_x-b_xc_z)\\
&=b_x(a_yc_y+a_zc_z)-c_x(a_yb_y+a_zb_z)\\
&=b_x(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)-c_x(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b),
\end{aligned}
$$
where adding $a_xb_xc_x$ to both products introduces cancelling terms. Likewise the other two components are
$$
\begin{aligned}
[\mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)]_y
&=a_z(b_yc_z-b_zc_y)-a_x(b_xc_y-b_yc_x)
=b_y(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)-c_y(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b),\\
[\mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)]_z
&=a_x(b_zc_x-b_xc_z)-a_y(b_yc_z-b_zc_y)
=b_z(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)-c_z(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b).
\end{aligned}
$$
Together these prove
$$
\boxed{\mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)
=(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)\mathbf b-(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)\mathbf c}.
$$
Now use the cyclic property of the <scalar triple product> and the just-proved identity:
$$
\begin{aligned}
(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)\cdot(\mathbf c\times\mathbf d)
&=\mathbf a\cdot[\mathbf b\times(\mathbf c\times\mathbf d)]\\
&=\mathbf a\cdot[(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf d)\mathbf c-(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf c)\mathbf d].
\end{aligned}
$$
This proves <Lagrange's identity> in the requested form,
$$
\boxed{(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)\cdot(\mathbf c\times\mathbf d)
=(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf d)
-(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf d)(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf c)}.
$$

= Solution

The <sphere> of centre $\mathbf a$ and radius $p$ has equation
$$
\boxed{|\mathbf r-\mathbf a|^2=p^2}.
$$
Let $d=|\mathbf b-\mathbf a|$ be the distance between the centres. A nondegenerate <circle of intersection of two spheres> exists exactly when
$$
\boxed{|p-q|<d<p+q}.
$$
The upper inequality excludes disjoint or externally tangent <spheres>. The lower excludes strict containment and internal tangency. It also excludes coincident centres, which give either no intersection or an entire common <sphere> rather than one <circle>. These inequalities are sufficient because a triangle with side lengths $p,q,d$ then exists with nonzero area, and rotating its third vertex about the line of centres traces a <circle>.

For a point on both <spheres>, expand their equations:
$$
\mathbf r^2-2\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf r+a^2=p^2,\qquad
\mathbf r^2-2\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf r+b^2=q^2.
$$
Subtracting cancels $\mathbf r^2$ and gives the <plane>
$$
\boxed{2(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)\cdot\mathbf r=p^2-q^2+b^2-a^2}.
$$
Its normal is parallel to the line of centres. Under the strict inequalities, the <plane> cuts either <sphere> in a genuine <circle>.

Height above the surface is $h=r-R$. Integrate the <density> over all exterior spherical shells:
$$
M=\int_R^\infty\int_0^\pi\int_0^{2\pi}
\rho_0e^{-(r-R)/h_0}r^2\sin\theta\,d\phi\,d\theta\,dr
=4\pi\rho_0\int_0^\infty(R+h)^2e^{-h/h_0}\,dh.
$$
Repeated <integration by parts>, or the substitution $h=h_0s$, gives
$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-h/h_0}\,dh=h_0,\quad
\int_0^\infty he^{-h/h_0}\,dh=h_0^2,\quad
\int_0^\infty h^2e^{-h/h_0}\,dh=2h_0^3.
$$
Expand $(R+h)^2$ and use these moments to obtain the <mass of an exponential spherical atmosphere>:
$$
\boxed{M=4\pi\rho_0h_0(R^2+2Rh_0+2h_0^2)}.
$$
For $h_0\ll R$, the leading term is surface area $4\pi R^2$ times column mass $\rho_0h_0$; the other terms retain the finite-curvature corrections.

For a twice continuously <differentiable> scalar potential, the <curl> of its <gradient> is
$$
\nabla\times\nabla\Phi

Evaluate the <line integral> along the three coordinate segments, treating coordinates not changing along a segment as constants. On the first segment, $y=z=0$, so $F_x=0$ and the contribution is zero. On the second segment,
$$
\int_0^yF_y(x,\eta,0)\,d\eta=\int_0^y(x^2-2\eta)\,d\eta=x^2y-y^2.

On the final segment,
$$
\int_0^zF_z(x,y,\zeta)\,d\zeta
=\int_0^z(-3x\zeta^2-1)\,d\zeta=-xz^3-z.
$$
The <fundamental theorem for line integrals> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\Phi(x,y,z)=x^2y-y^2-xz^3-z+C}.
$$
Taking its <gradient> verifies each component of the field. The constant is $C=\Phi(0,0,0)$; the field specifies only potential differences because the <gradient> of every constant is zero.

Write the differential form as $M\,dx+N\,dy$, with the coefficients as printed. A <monomial integrating factor> can be found from $\mu=x^\alpha y^\beta$. The exactness condition $\partial_y(\mu M)=\partial_x(\mu N)$ gives
$$
2(\beta+2)=-(\alpha+2),\qquad -(\beta+1)=2(\alpha+1).
$$
Solving yields $\alpha=0,\beta=-3$. Thus, on $y\ne0$,
$$
\boxed{\mu(y)=y^{-3}}.
$$
After multiplication, the differential form is
$$
\left(\frac{2x}{y}-\frac1{y^2}\right)dx+
\left(\frac{2x}{y^3}-\frac{x^2}{y^2}\right)dy
=d\left(\frac{x^2}{y}-\frac{x}{y^2}\right).
$$
Hence the implicit solutions are
$$
\boxed{\frac{x^2}{y}-\frac{x}{y^2}=C\quad(y\ne0)}.
$$
The branch $\boxed{y=0}$ was excluded by the <integrating factor> but satisfies the original equation and must be restored. On any graph branch with $y\ne0$, differentiation of the boxed <first integral> recovers the original <differential equation>.

Use $u=\cos x$, so $du=-\sin x\,dx$. Up to an overall minus sign, the differential form becomes
$$
(2y+3y^4u)\,du+(u+4y^3u^2)\,dy=0.

Seeking a <monomial integrating factor> $u^\alpha y^\beta$, equality of mixed <derivatives> gives
$$
2(\beta+1)=\alpha+1,\qquad3(\beta+4)=4(\alpha+2).

$$
\boxed{\mu(x)=\cos x}.

$$
\cos x\,[M\,dx+N\,dy]
=-d\left(y\cos^2x+y^4\cos^3x\right).

Thus the implicit <first integral> is
$$
\boxed{y\cos^2x+y^4\cos^3x=C}.

The equivalence with the <differential equation> holds directly where $\cos x\ne0$. The zero solution $y=0$ is included at $C=0$. At a zero of $\cos x$, the original graph equation requires $y=0$ for any finite <differentiable> continuation, so no extra finite graph with nonzero $y$ is created by multiplying by the vanishing factor. Put $p=y'$ and differentiate the general form of <Clairaut's equation>:
$$
p=p+xp'+f'(p)p'.
$$
Cancellation gives
$$
\boxed{[x+f'(p)]\frac{dp}{dx}=0}.
$$
For $f(p)=1/(p-1)$ the equation is defined only when $p\ne1$. There are two types of smooth branch.

First, constant $p=C\ne1$ gives the straight-line family
$$
\boxed{y=Cx+\frac1{C-1},\qquad C\in\mathbb R\setminus\{1\}}.
$$
Second, $x+f'(p)=0$ gives $x=1/(p-1)^2$, so $x>0$ and $p=1\pm x^{-1/2}$. Substituting in the original equation gives its two singular <envelope> branches,
$$
\boxed{y=x\pm2\sqrt x\qquad(x>0)}.
$$
Their <derivatives> are indeed $1\pm1/\sqrt x$, verifying the required value of $p$. There is no finite-slope continuation of either <envelope> through $x=0$, and no real <envelope> branch for $x<0$.

$$
f(x)\sim\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty[a_n\cos nx+b_n\sin nx],

with <Fourier coefficients>
$$
\boxed{a_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\cos nx\,dx\quad(n\geq0),\qquad
b_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\sin nx\,dx\quad(n\geq1)}.

$$
\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f_N(x)^2\,dx
=\frac{a_0^2}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^N(a_n^2+b_n^2).
$$

$$
\boxed{\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)^2\,dx
=\frac{a_0^2}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty(a_n^2+b_n^2)}.
$$
Square integrability is the natural hypothesis for this finite norm identity; additional smoothness can be used when pointwise convergence is required.

For a nonzero separated solution $u=X(x)T(t)$ of the <reaction-diffusion equation>, substitution and division by $XT$ gives
$$
\frac{T'}T+1=\frac{X''}X.
$$
The two sides depend on different variables, so both equal a separation constant. The homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions> select a negative spatial constant, written $-k^2$:
$$
X''+k^2X=0,\qquad T'+(1+k^2)T=0.
$$
For $k>0$, $X=A\sin kx+B\cos kx$. The boundary value at zero makes $B=0$; the boundary value at $L$ then requires $kL=n\pi$, with $n=1,2,\ldots$. A zero spatial constant gives only a linear <function> vanishing at both endpoints, hence the zero <function>; a positive spatial constant similarly gives no nonzero admissible hyperbolic solution. Therefore
$$
\boxed{X_n(x)=A_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\qquad
T_n(t)=B_n\exp\left[-\left(1+\frac{n^2\pi^2}{L^2}\right)t\right]}.
$$
By <superposition>, the <general solution> with the stated boundary conditions is
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty c_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}
\exp\left[-\left(1+\frac{n^2\pi^2}{L^2}\right)t\right]}.
$$
The coefficients are fixed only if initial data are supplied: for $u(x,0)=f(x)$, they are the <Fourier sine series> coefficients $c_n=(2/L)\int_0^L f(x)\sin(n\pi x/L)\,dx$. Here they remain arbitrary subject to convergence. Every mode has the ordinary diffusive decay rate plus the additional reaction decay rate one.
