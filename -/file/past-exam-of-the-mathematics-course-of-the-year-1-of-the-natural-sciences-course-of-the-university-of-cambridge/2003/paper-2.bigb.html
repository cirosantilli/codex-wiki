With <normal vector> $n=(-1,-2,2)$, a <plane> through $A=(2,-1,1)$ has equation $n\cdot(r-A)=0$. As $n\cdot A=2$, its <plane equation> is
$$
\boxed{-x-2y+2z=2.}
$$
The <distance from a point to a plane> is the absolute signed offset divided by the <Euclidean norm> of its <normal vector>. Hence the <Euclidean distance> from the origin is $\boxed{2/3}$, since $|n|=3$. The closest point is $2n/9=(-2,-4,4)/9$, whose <Euclidean distance> from the origin is $2/3$ and which satisfies the <plane equation>.

The displacement <vectors> are $B-A=(-1,2,-2)$ and $C-A=(1,0,1)$. Their <cross product> is $(2,-1,-2)$, with <Euclidean norm> three. Thus either sign of
$$
\boxed{\hat n_2=\frac{(2,-1,-2)}3}
$$
is a <unit normal vector> to the second <plane>, whose equation is $2x-y-2z=3$. A <direction vector> for the intersection is the <cross product> of the two <normal vectors>:
$$
\boxed{(-1,-2,2)\times(2,-1,-2)=(6,2,5).}
$$
It is <orthogonal> to each <normal vector>. Since both <planes> contain $A$, the whole intersection line is $r=(2,-1,1)+t(6,2,5)$, $t\in\mathbb R$.

= Solution
{parent=2}

For an <odd> integrable <function>, both $f(x)$ and $f(x)\cos nx$ are <odd>. Their <integrals> over $[-\pi,\pi]$ therefore vanish, so the constant and <cosine> <Fourier coefficients> are zero. For $f(x)=\sin ax$, the remaining <Fourier sine coefficients> are
$$
b_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi\sin ax\sin nx\,dx=\frac1\pi\left[\frac{\sin\pi(a-n)}{a-n}-\frac{\sin\pi(a+n)}{a+n}\right]=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}n\sin\pi a}{\pi(n^2-a^2)}.
$$
The product-to-sum identity in the first step reduces the <integral> to two <cosines>. Here $a$ is real and noninteger, so no denominator vanishes. Consequently the <Fourier series of a noninteger-frequency sine> is
$$
\boxed{\sin ax=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}n\sin\pi a}{\pi(n^2-a^2)}\sin nx,\qquad -\pi<x<\pi.}
$$
At $x=\pm\pi$ every term is zero. The <Fourier series> converges there to the average of the two limits of the <periodic extension>, namely zero, rather than to the nonzero endpoint values $\pm\sin\pi a$.

The unique <symmetric and antisymmetric parts of a matrix> are $S=(M+M^T)/2$ and $A=(M-M^T)/2$, giving
$$
\boxed{S=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}0&a+b\\a+b&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad A=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}0&b-a\\a-b&0\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Their <matrix products> with their <transposes> are $S^TS=(a+b)^2I/4$ and $A^TA=(a-b)^2I/4$. Each is an <orthogonal matrix> precisely when the corresponding factor is one. Thus $a+b=\pm2$ and $a-b=\pm2$, with independent sign choices, and the simultaneous possibilities are
$$
\boxed{(a,b)=(2,0),\ (-2,0),\ (0,2),\ (0,-2).}
$$

= Solution

The <characteristic polynomial> is $(5-\lambda)(2-\lambda)-4=\lambda^2-7\lambda+6=(\lambda-1)(\lambda-6)$. For $\lambda=1$, the <eigenvector> equation gives $y=2x$; for $\lambda=6$, it gives $x=-2y$. The normalized <eigenvectors> can therefore be chosen as
$$
\boxed{\lambda_1=1,\quad v_1=\frac{(1,2)}{\sqrt5};\qquad\lambda_2=6,\quad v_2=\frac{(-2,1)}{\sqrt5}.}
$$
Both have unit <Euclidean norm>, and their <dot product> is $(-2+2)/5=0$, explicitly verifying <orthogonality>. This also follows from the <spectral theorem> for a real <symmetric matrix> with distinct <eigenvalues>.

= Solution

For a bounded <volume> $V$ with piecewise smooth closed boundary $\partial V$, oriented by the outward <unit normal vector>, and a continuously differentiable <vector field> on a neighborhood of $\overline V$, the <divergence theorem> states
$$
\int_V\nabla\cdot F\,dV=\int_{\partial V}F\cdot n\,dS.
$$
For an oriented piecewise smooth <surface> $S$ with piecewise smooth boundary $C=\partial S$, and a continuously differentiable <vector field> on a neighborhood of $S$, <Stokes theorem> states
$$
\int_S(\nabla\times F)\cdot n\,dS=\oint_CF\cdot dr.
$$
The boundary orientation follows the right-hand rule relative to the chosen <normal vector>. Reversing that orientation reverses both sides of the latter identity.

= b
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <divergence> is $\nabla\cdot F=2xyz+2xyz+2xyz=6xyz$. With outward orientation, the <divergence theorem> converts the <surface flux> into a <volume integral>:
$$
\boxed{\int_SF\cdot dS=6\int_0^1\int_0^1\int_0^1xyz\,dx\,dy\,dz=6\left(\frac12\right)^3=\frac34.}
$$
Directly, the three faces lying in coordinate <planes> contribute zero, and each opposite face contributes $1/4$, confirming the sign and normalization.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Take the outward orientation on the upper <hemisphere>; its boundary <circle> is then counterclockwise viewed from above. Parametrize that <circle> by $r(\phi)=(r\cos\phi,r\sin\phi,0)$, $0\leq\phi\leq2\pi$. Then $G\cdot dr=r^2(3\cos^2\phi-4\sin^2\phi)d\phi$, so <Stokes theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\int_S(\nabla\times G)\cdot dS=\int_0^{2\pi}r^2(3\cos^2\phi-4\sin^2\phi)d\phi=-\pi r^2.}
$$
Equivalently $\nabla\times G=(0,0,-1)$, and the upward projected area of the <hemisphere> is $\pi r^2$. The opposite orientation gives the negative of this answer.

Let $D$ denote having the <disease> and $+$ a positive result. Assume I am sampled uniformly from the stated population, so $\mathbb P(D)=n/N$. By the <law of total probability>, $\mathbb P(+)=a n/N+b(N-n)/N$. Thus <Bayes' theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(D\mid+)=\frac{an}{an+b(N-n)}.}
$$
The denominator must be positive; otherwise the observed event has zero <probability> in this model and the <conditional probability> is undefined. The answer depends on <disease> prevalence as well as the test's two error rates.

The <survival function> of this <exponential distribution> is $\mathbb P(X\geq t)=e^{-kt}$ for $t\geq0$. Since $\{X\geq3\}\subseteq\{X\geq1\}$, the <conditional probability> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X\geq3\mid X\geq1)=\frac{e^{-3k}}{e^{-k}}=e^{-2k}.}
$$
This is the <memoryless property>: after surviving the first unit, surviving two additional units has the same <probability> as it did initially.

The <expectation> is $\mathbb E[X]=k\int_0^\infty te^{-kt}dt$. An <integration by parts>, with the boundary term $te^{-kt}$ tending to zero, gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X]=\int_0^\infty e^{-kt}dt=\frac1k.}
$$

Use <Gaussian elimination>. The last equation gives $y=c-z$, and the second then gives $x=b-4c-2z$. Substitute these into the first equation to obtain $3b-10c-z=a$. Back-substitution yields
$$
\boxed{x=2a-5b+16c,\qquad y=a-3b+11c,\qquad z=-a+3b-10c.}
$$
The <linear system> has <matrix> form
$$
\begin{pmatrix}3&2&7\\1&4&6\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\\c\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The coefficients of the arbitrary right-hand side in the solved expressions are the rows of the <matrix inverse>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}3&2&7\\1&4&6\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}2&-5&16\\1&-3&11\\-1&3&-10\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Multiplying the original <matrix> by this candidate gives the <identity matrix>; its <determinant> is $-1$, so the solution is unique for every $(a,b,c)$.

= Solution

For $h=x^2/a^2+y^2/b^2+z^2-1$, the <gradient> $\nabla h=2(x/a^2,y/b^2,z)$ is a <normal vector> to the <ellipsoid>. The outward <unit normal vector> is $(x/a^2,y/b^2,z)/\sqrt{x^2/a^4+y^2/b^4+z^2}$. The conditions $a,b>\sqrt2$ ensure that $z=\sqrt{1-x^2/a^2-y^2/b^2}>0$ everywhere on the rectangular projection.

The <dot product> $F\cdot\nabla h=2xy(1/b^2-1/a^2)$ vanishes pointwise when $a=b$. In that case $F$ has no normal component, so **the <surface flux> is zero**. For unequal axes, the given <vector surface element> gives
$$
\Phi=\left(\frac1{b^2}-\frac1{a^2}\right)\int_0^1\int_0^1\frac{xy}{\sqrt{1-x^2/a^2-y^2/b^2}}\,dy\,dx.
$$
Put $u=x^2/a^2$, $v=y^2/b^2$. Then $xy\,dxdy=a^2b^2\,dudv/4$, and
$$
\Phi=\frac{a^2-b^2}{4}\int_0^{1/a^2}\int_0^{1/b^2}(1-u-v)^{-1/2}\,dv\,du.
$$
Two elementary <integrations> now give
$$
\boxed{\Phi=\frac{a^2-b^2}{3}\left[1-\left(1-\frac1{a^2}\right)^{3/2}-\left(1-\frac1{b^2}\right)^{3/2}+\left(1-\frac1{a^2}-\frac1{b^2}\right)^{3/2}\right].}
$$
This is the <rotational flux through an ellipsoidal graph patch> with unit vertical semiaxis and unit projection lengths. It also tends continuously to zero when $a=b$, agreeing with the pointwise argument.

Write $P=I-Q$ with $Q=aa^T+bb^T$. The <orthonormality> conditions $a^Ta=b^Tb=1$ and $a^Tb=b^Ta=0$ give

Q^2=a(a^Ta)a^T+a(a^Tb)b^T+b(b^Ta)a^T+b(b^Tb)b^T=aa^T+bb^T=Q.

Consequently $P^2=I-2Q+Q^2=I-Q=P$. It is the <orthogonal projection matrix> onto the <orthogonal complement> of the <linear span> of $a,b$.

Apply <trace orthogonality of symmetric and antisymmetric matrices>. In detail, invariance of the <trace> under <transpose> and its cyclic property give
$$
\operatorname{tr}(SA)=\operatorname{tr}((SA)^T)=\operatorname{tr}(A^TS^T)=-\operatorname{tr}(AS)=-\operatorname{tr}(SA).

= Solution

At fixed particle number, the differential of the <internal energy> identifies $T=(\partial U/\partial S)_V$ and $p=-(\partial U/\partial V)_S$. Equality of continuous <mixed partial derivatives> gives the first <Maxwell relation>:
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\right)_S=\frac{\partial^2U}{\partial V\partial S}=-\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial S}\right)_V.}
$$
For the <enthalpy>, $dH=dU+p\,dV+V\,dp=T\,dS+V\,dp$. Thus $T=(\partial H/\partial S)_p$, $V=(\partial H/\partial p)_S$, and the same argument gives the <enthalpy Maxwell relation>
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial p}\right)_S=\frac{\partial^2H}{\partial p\partial S}=\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right)_p.}
$$
The smoothness and independent thermodynamic coordinates are understood within a single phase.

= b
{parent=9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Differentiate $H=U+pV$ at fixed <pressure> and subtract the <heat capacity at constant volume>:
$$
C_p-C_V=\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_p+p\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_p-\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_V.
$$
Now use $U=U(T,V)$ and the <multivariable chain rule> along a constant-pressure path:
$$
\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_p=\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_V+\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_p.
$$
Substitution cancels the fixed-volume <derivative> and gives the <volume derivative form of the heat-capacity difference>,
$$
\boxed{C_p-C_V=\left[\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T+p\right]\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_p.}
$$
For the <ideal gas>, the first term in brackets vanishes and $V=NkT/p$, so $(\partial V/\partial T)_p=Nk/p$. Consequently $\boxed{C_p-C_V=Nk}$ for the entire gas, with fixed particle number $N$. The extra <heat capacity> accounts for expansion <work> at constant <pressure>.

Away from the origin, the <chain rule> gives $r_x=x/r$ and $r_y=y/r$. Differentiating once more yields the two-dimensional case of the <Hessian of the Euclidean norm>:
$$
\boxed{r_x=\frac xr,\quad r_y=\frac yr,\quad r_{xx}=\frac{y^2}{r^3},\quad r_{yy}=\frac{x^2}{r^3},\quad r_{xy}=-\frac{xy}{r^3}.}
$$
These <partial derivatives> are smooth wherever $r>0$, so <Clairaut's theorem> gives $\boxed{r_{yx}=r_{xy}=-xy/r^3}$. None of these formulas supplies a <derivative> at the origin: even the restriction $r(x,0)=|x|$ has no two-sided <derivative> there.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Write $c=\cos\theta$, $s=\sin\theta$. Since $\theta$ is constant, the <multivariable chain rule> gives $\partial_x=c\partial_u-s\partial_v$ and $\partial_y=s\partial_u+c\partial_v$. Applying these operators twice to a twice continuously differentiable <function> gives
$$
g_{xx}=c^2g_{uu}-2csg_{uv}+s^2g_{vv},\qquad g_{yy}=s^2g_{uu}+2csg_{uv}+c^2g_{vv}.
$$
The mixed terms cancel and $c^2+s^2=1$, so
$$
\boxed{g_{xx}+g_{yy}=g_{uu}+g_{vv}.}
$$
This is <rotational invariance of the Laplacian>: an <orthogonal change of coordinates> preserves the <trace> of the <Hessian matrix>. A nonconstant angle would introduce additional <derivatives> and is not covered by this constant rotation calculation.

= Solution

Use the <method of undetermined coefficients>. Substitution of $y_p=(a+bx)\sin x+(c+dx)\cos x$ gives
$$
y_p''-2y_p'+y_p=(-2b+2c-2d+2dx)\sin x+(-2a+2b-2d-2bx)\cos x.
$$
Equating the coefficients of $x\sin x$, $x\cos x$, $\sin x$, and $\cos x$ to the forcing gives $d=1$, $b=0$, $c=1$, $a=-1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{y_p=-\sin x+(1+x)\cos x.}
$$
The <characteristic equation> of the homogeneous equation is $(r-1)^2=0$, with a <repeated root>. Its <complementary function> is $(C_1+C_2x)e^x$. Since $y_p(0)=1$ and $y_p'(0)=0$, the initial conditions give $C_1=-1$ and $C_2=1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{y=(x-1)e^x-\sin x+(1+x)\cos x.}
$$
Direct differentiation verifies both zero initial data and the original forcing.

Expand $u=3x^2-2xy+3y^2$. The <Lagrange multiplier> equations for $u-\lambda v$ are $(6-2\lambda)x-2y=0$ and $-2x+6y+\lambda=0$, together with the <constraint> $y=x^2-1/4$. The first two imply $y=(3-\lambda)x$ and $(6\lambda-16)x=\lambda$. The value $\lambda=8/3$ would make the latter equation inconsistent, so division is allowed:
$$
\boxed{x=\frac{\lambda}{6\lambda-16},\qquad y=\frac{\lambda(3-\lambda)}{6\lambda-16}.}
$$
Insert these into $x^2-y-1/4=0$ and multiply by $(6\lambda-16)^2$. Simplification gives $2(3\lambda^3-21\lambda^2+48\lambda-32)=0$, hence
$$
\boxed{3\lambda^3-21\lambda^2+48\lambda-32=0.}
$$
The <gradient> of the <constraint> $(2x,-1)$ is never zero, so the <Lagrange multiplier> condition is necessary at every constrained <extremum>. To establish a global <minimum>, parametrize the whole <parabola> by $y=x^2-1/4$. The restricted <function> becomes
$$
q(x)=3x^4-2x^3+\frac32x^2+\frac12x+\frac3{16}.
$$
It is <coercive>: $q(x)\to\infty$ as $|x|\to\infty$, so it attains a global <minimum>. Moreover $q''(x)=36(x-1/6)^2+2>0$, making it <strictly convex> and ensuring that this <minimum> is unique. This proves more than the permitted assumption of one real multiplier root.

The <level sets> of $u$ are nested <ellipses> centered at the origin. In the rotated coordinates $p=(x+y)/\sqrt2$, $q=(x-y)/\sqrt2$, they have equation $2p^2+4q^2=\text{constant}$, with the longer axis along $x=y$. The smallest one meeting the <parabola> is tangent to it at the unique minimizing point, approximately $(x,y)=(-0.12654,-0.23399)$, where $u\simeq0.15307$ and $\lambda\simeq1.15085$.
