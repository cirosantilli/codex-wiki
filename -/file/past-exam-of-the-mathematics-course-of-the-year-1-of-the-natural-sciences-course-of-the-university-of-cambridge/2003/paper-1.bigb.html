= Solution
{parent=a}

Put $D=\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)\ne0$. The <scalar triple product> is unchanged by cyclic permutation, so
$$
\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf a=\frac{\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)}D=1,\quad\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf b=\frac{\mathbf b\cdot(\mathbf c\times\mathbf a)}D=1,\quad\mathbf C\cdot\mathbf c=\frac{\mathbf c\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)}D=1.
$$
These are the diagonal pairings of a <reciprocal basis>.

= b
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <cross product> $\mathbf b\times\mathbf c$ is <orthogonal> to both factors. Division by the nonzero <scalar> $D$ preserves that perpendicularity, giving $\boxed{\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf b=\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf c=0}$. Cyclic permutation gives the other off-diagonal pairings needed for the <reciprocal basis>.

{parent=1}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Suppose $r\mathbf A+s\mathbf B+t\mathbf C=0$. Take its <dot product> successively with $\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c$. The reciprocal pairings give $r=s=t=0$. Thus $\mathbf A,\mathbf B,\mathbf C$ are <linearly independent> and therefore noncoplanar in three dimensions.

= Solution
{parent=1}

For the numerical <vectors>, $\mathbf b\times\mathbf c=(-2,1,-2)$ and $D=(-1,1,0)\cdot(-2,1,-2)=3\ne0$, proving noncoplanarity. The <reciprocal basis> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf A=\frac{(-2,1,-2)}3,\qquad\mathbf B=\frac{(1,1,1)}3,\qquad\mathbf C=\frac{(1,1,-2)}3.}
$$
Reciprocal <dot products> extract the coefficients of any <vector> in the original <basis>. Here $\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf d=-1/3$, $\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf d=2/3$, and $\mathbf C\cdot\mathbf d=5/3$, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbf d=-\frac13\mathbf a+\frac23\mathbf b+\frac53\mathbf c.}
$$
Substitution in Cartesian components recovers $(2,1,-1)$.

= Solution

Write $R(x)=(x^2+3)(2x^2-1)/(3x^4-x^3-1)$. This <rational function> is <continuous> at both finite points because its denominator is nonzero there. Substitution gives the first two <limits>; at infinity divide numerator and denominator by $x^4$ and use their leading coefficients. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\lim_{x\to0}R(x)=3,\qquad\lim_{x\to1}R(x)=4,\qquad\lim_{x\to\infty}R(x)=\frac23.}
$$

= Solution

For fixed real $a$, the base is positive for all sufficiently large positive $x$. Take its <logarithm> and use the <Taylor expansion> $\ln(1-u)=-u+O(u^2)$:
$$
\ln\left[\left(1-\frac ax\right)^x\right]=x\ln\left(1-\frac ax\right)=-a+O(x^{-1}).
$$
<Continuity> of the <exponential function> then gives $\boxed{\lim_{x\to\infty}(1-a/x)^x=e^{-a}}$. This includes $a=0$.

Both numerator and denominator have a double zero at one. Applying <L'Hôpital's rule> twice gives
$$
\boxed{\lim_{x\to1}\frac{1-\sin(\pi x/2)}{x^3-3x+2}=\lim_{x\to1}\frac{-(\pi/2)\cos(\pi x/2)}{3x^2-3}=\lim_{x\to1}\frac{(\pi^2/4)\sin(\pi x/2)}{6x}=\frac{\pi^2}{24}.}
$$
Alternatively $x^3-3x+2=(x-1)^2(x+2)$ and $1-\sin(\pi x/2)\sim\pi^2(x-1)^2/8$, giving the same result from a <Taylor expansion>.

For $f(x)=x^3e^{-3x^2/2}/3$, the <product rule> gives $f'(x)=x^2(1-x^2)e^{-3x^2/2}$, so the <stationary points> occur at $x=-1,0,1$. Its sign is negative for $|x|>1$ and positive for $0<|x|<1$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\left(-1,-\frac{e^{-3/2}}3\right)\text{ is a minimum},\qquad\left(1,\frac{e^{-3/2}}3\right)\text{ is a maximum}.}
$$
They are also the global <extrema>, since the function tends to zero at both infinities and is a <monotone function> on the intervals just identified. At zero the <derivative> does not change sign. Since $f(x)=x^3/3+O(x^5)$, **$(0,0)$ is a stationary <inflection point>**, not an <extremum>.

It decreases to its negative <minimum> at $x=-1$, rises through the stationary <inflection point> at zero to its positive <maximum> at $x=1$, then decreases toward zero.

The Gaussian decay makes the <integral> <absolutely convergent>, so <odd> symmetry gives $\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)dx=0}$. On the positive half-line substitute $u=3x^2/2$, so $x^3dx=2u\,du/9$. Thus

The last <integral> is $\Gamma(2)=1$, an instance of the <odd Gaussian moments on a half-line>.

Put $u=\cos x$, $du=-\sin x\,dx$, and use $\sin^2x=1-u^2$. Then
$$
\int\frac{\sin^3x}{\cos^4x}dx=-\int\frac{1-u^2}{u^4}du=\frac1{3u^3}-\frac1u+C.

Therefore
$$
\boxed{\int\frac{\sin^3x}{\cos^4x}dx=\frac1{3\cos^3x}-\frac1{\cos x}+C.}
$$
This holds on intervals where $\cos x\ne0$.

Direct <matrix multiplication> gives $MN=\begin{pmatrix}5&18\\7&25\end{pmatrix}$. The three <determinants> are $\det M=8-9=-1$, $\det N=4-3=1$, and $\det(MN)=125-126=-1$. Hence $\boxed{\det(MN)=(-1)(1)=\det M\det N}$, as required.

M^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}-4&3\\3&-2\end{pmatrix},\quad N^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}4&-3\\-1&1\end{pmatrix},\quad(MN)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}-25&18\\7&-5\end{pmatrix}.

The order reversal is needed because applying the inverse undoes the original two <linear maps> in reverse sequence.

For a nonzero separated solution $U=X(x)T(t)$, <separation of variables> gives $X''/X=T'/T=-\lambda$. The zero <Dirichlet boundary conditions> <force> $X=\sin nx$, $\lambda=n^2$, with <integers> $n\geq1$; the time factors are $e^{-n^2t}$. Expanding the initial profile in this <Fourier sine series> requires only the two already displayed spatial modes. Thus
$$
\boxed{U(x,t)=3e^{-t}\sin x-e^{-9t}\sin3x.}
$$
Both terms individually solve the <heat equation>, vanish at the endpoints and give the required initial profile at $t=0$. The initial function is also $4\sin^3x\geq0$ on the bar; the later solution stays nonnegative and tends to zero as <heat> leaves through the fixed-temperature ends.

Parametrize the line by $\mathbf r(t)=(t,t,t)$, $0\leq t\leq1$. Its <tangent vector> is $(1,1,1)$. On this line the three <force> components sum to $\mu t+(1-\lambda)t+(\lambda-1-\mu)t=0$. Therefore the <line integral> is $\boxed{\int\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=0}$ for every parameter choice.

{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

On the specified <curve> $\mathbf r(t)=(t,t,t^2)$, the <tangent vector> is $(1,1,2t)$ and
$$
\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))=(\mu t^2,\ t-\lambda t^2,\ t^2+(\lambda-2-\mu)t).
$$
Their <dot product> is $t+(\lambda-\mu-4)t^2+2t^3$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\int\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\int_0^1[t+(\lambda-\mu-4)t^2+2t^3]dt=\frac{\lambda-\mu-1}{3}.}
$$

= Solution

The field is smooth on the <simply connected> domain $\mathbb R^3$. Its <curl> is $\nabla\times\mathbf F=(2\lambda-2,2\mu,0)$, so a necessary and sufficient condition for a <conservative vector field> is
$$
\boxed{\lambda=1,\qquad\mu=0.}
$$
At those values it is explicitly the <gradient> of $\Phi=xy-x^2/2-yz+z^2/2$. Equality of the two previous path <integrals> would impose only $\lambda-\mu=1$, which is weaker: <coinciding path integrals need not imply a conservative field>. For example $\lambda=2,\mu=1$ makes those two <integrals> equal but leaves nonzero curl.

The <exponential function> definitions of the <hyperbolic functions> and <Euler's formula> give
$$
\sinh(ix)=\frac{e^{ix}-e^{-ix}}2=i\sin x,\qquad\cosh(ix)=\frac{e^{ix}+e^{-ix}}2=\cos x.
$$
Dividing these identities wherever $\cos x\ne0$ gives $\boxed{\tanh(ix)=i\tan x}$. The two undivided identities hold for every <complex number> $x$; the quotient identity excludes its poles.

= Solution

Adding and subtracting the <exponential function> <power series> cancels the appropriate <odd> or <even> terms:
$$
\boxed{\sinh x=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{x^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}=x+\frac{x^3}{3!}+\cdots,\qquad\cosh x=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{x^{2n}}{(2n)!}=1+\frac{x^2}{2!}+\cdots.}
$$
Both series converge absolutely for every <complex number> $x$, as follows from the <ratio test>.

Use the <slope substitution for equations involving x y'-y>: put $v=y/x$ on the positive interval containing the initial point. Then $xy'-y=x^2v'$, so $x^2v'=\cot v$. Near $v(1)=\pi/4$, separating variables gives $\tan v\,dv=dx/x^2$. Integration yields $\ln|\cos v|=1/x+C$. The initial value fixes the sign and constant, giving
$$
\cos\frac yx=\frac1{\sqrt2}e^{1/x-1}.
$$
Choose the <inverse cosine> branch that agrees with $v(1)=\pi/4$. The solution is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=x\arccos\left(\frac{e^{1/x-1}}{\sqrt2}\right),\qquad x>\frac1{1+\tfrac12\ln2}.}
$$
On this interval the argument lies strictly between zero and one and the original cotangent is defined. At the finite lower endpoint, $v\to0$ and the cotangent becomes singular, so that endpoint is excluded from the maximal interval of the <initial value problem>. The slope substitution here is valid without classifying the original equation as homogeneous in the usual $y'=F(y/x)$ sense.

= Solution

The region lies in the first quadrant, inside the unit <circle> and above the positive branch of $xy=1/4$. In <polar coordinates>, $xy=(r^2/2)\sin2\theta$, so its radial bounds are $(2\sin2\theta)^{-1/2}\leq r\leq1$. Such an interval exists when $\sin2\theta\geq1/2$, hence
$$
\boxed{\frac\pi{12}\leq\theta\leq\frac{5\pi}{12},\qquad\frac1{\sqrt{2\sin2\theta}}\leq r\leq1.}
$$
At the intersections with the <circle>, $r=1$ and $\sin2\theta=1/2$, giving exactly the two stated polar angles. Their Cartesian coordinates are $(\cos(\pi/12),\sin(\pi/12))$ and the swapped pair.

\Image[paper-1-circle-hyperbola-region.png]
{title=The first-quadrant region between a hyperbola and the unit-circle arc}
{description=Original shaded integration region. The dashed rays pass through the two intersections, at polar angles $\pi/12$ and $5\pi/12$.}

The <Jacobian determinant> contributes $dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta$, while $xy(x^2+y^2)=r^4\sin2\theta/2$. Thus the <double integral> is
$$
I=\int_{\pi/12}^{5\pi/12}\int_{(2\sin2\theta)^{-1/2}}^1\frac{r^5}{2}\sin2\theta\,dr\,d\theta=\int_{\pi/12}^{5\pi/12}\left(\frac{\sin2\theta}{12}-\frac{\csc^2(2\theta)}{96}\right)d\theta.
$$
Its <antiderivative> is $-\cos2\theta/24+\cot2\theta/192$. Evaluating the bounds gives
$$
\boxed{I=\frac{\sqrt3}{24}-\frac{\sqrt3}{96}=\frac{\sqrt3}{32}.}
$$
