The horizontal cross-section is a <triangle>, so its <area> is half its width times its depth:
$$
A(z)=LD\left(1-\frac zH\right)\sqrt{1-\frac z{2H}}.

On an <interval> with $x\ne0$, division by $x^2$ gives the <separable differential equation> $y'=(4/x^2-1/x)y^2$. For $y\ne0$, take <antiderivatives>:
$$
\int\frac{dy}{y^2}=\int\left(\frac4{x^2}-\frac1x\right)dx,\qquad -\frac1y=-\frac4x-\ln|x|+C.
$$
Renaming the constant yields the explicit nonzero family
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac1{4/x+\ln|x|+C}.}
$$
For $x\ne0$, the <interval> of definition must avoid zeros of the denominator. Division by $y^2$ excluded the additional solution $\boxed{y(x)\equiv0}$, which must also be included.

The original undivided <differential equation> also makes sense at $x=0$, where it requires $y(0)=0$. For <continuation through a zero of the leading ODE coefficient>, rewrite the nonzero family as
$$
y(x)=\frac{x}{4+x\ln|x|+Cx}.
$$
It extends differentiably with $y(0)=0$ and $y'(0)=1/4$, since $y(x)/x\to1/4$. One may choose the integration constants independently on the two sides of zero and obtain a differentiable solution through zero. The zero solution also extends through zero, with <derivative> $0$; joining a nonzero branch on just one side to a zero branch on the other would fail <differentiability>.

For an <exact differential form>, equality of the mixed <partial derivatives> requires $P_y=Q_x$. Here
$$
P_y=\frac{y}{a\sqrt x\sqrt{1+y^2}},\qquad Q_x=\frac{y}{2\sqrt x\sqrt{1+y^2}}.
$$
Equality for all $x>0$ and real $y$ forces $1/a=1/2$, so $\boxed{a=2}$. To construct the potential, integrate $P$ with respect to $x$:
$$
v(x,y)=\sqrt x\left(1+\sqrt{1+y^2}\right)+C(y).
$$
Differentiation with respect to $y$ gives $v_y=Q+C'(y)$, so $C'(y)=0$. Thus the most general potential on the connected domain $x>0$ is
$$
\boxed{v(x,y)=\sqrt x\left(1+\sqrt{1+y^2}\right)+C,\qquad C\in\mathbb R.}
$$
Its explicit <partial derivatives> verify sufficiency as well as necessity.

The <chain rule> expresses the coordinate <differential operators> as
$$
\partial_x=a\partial_\xi+b\partial_\eta,\qquad \partial_y=-b\partial_\xi+a\partial_\eta.
$$
The coefficients are constant, so a second application gives
$$
u_{xx}=a^2u_{\xi\xi}+ab(u_{\xi\eta}+u_{\eta\xi})+b^2u_{\eta\eta},
$$
and
$$
u_{yy}=b^2u_{\xi\xi}-ab(u_{\xi\eta}+u_{\eta\xi})+a^2u_{\eta\eta}.
$$
Adding cancels the mixed <partial derivatives>, and $a^2+b^2=1$ gives
$$
\boxed{u_{xx}+u_{yy}=u_{\xi\xi}+u_{\eta\eta}.}
$$
The transformation has <rotation matrix> $\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{pmatrix}$, so this identity expresses the <rotational invariance of the Laplacian>.

<L'Hôpital's rule> applies to a quotient $f(x)/g(x)$ of <differentiable functions> near a limiting <point>, excluding that <point> itself, provided $g'(x)\ne0$ there. If the quotient has an indeterminate form $0/0$ or $\infty/\infty$, and the <limit> of $f'(x)/g'(x)$ exists as a finite or infinite value $L$, then

\boxed{\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}=L.}

The rule also applies to one-sided <limits> and to limits at infinity, under the corresponding hypotheses. Existence of the derivative-ratio <limit> and the indeterminate-form hypotheses are required; an arbitrary quotient cannot be replaced by a quotient of <derivatives>.

For a differentiable objective $f$ and regular constraint $g=r$, a constrained <stationary point> satisfies
$$
\boxed{\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g,\qquad g=r,}
$$
where $\lambda$ is a <Lagrange multiplier>. Solve these equations for both the coordinates and $\lambda$.

Geometrically, the <gradient> $\nabla g$ is normal to the constraint <level set> when $\nabla g\ne0$. Every feasible infinitesimal displacement $\mathbf v$ is tangent to that <level set>, so $\nabla g\cdot\mathbf v=0$. At a constrained <stationary point>, the directional change $\nabla f\cdot\mathbf v$ must also vanish for every such tangent displacement. Thus $\nabla f$ is normal to the same tangent <plane> and is a <scalar multiple> of $\nabla g$. This is the geometric basis of the <Lagrange multiplier> method.

For a second constraint $h=s$, with <linearly independent vectors> $\nabla g$ and $\nabla h$, the feasible tangent directions are perpendicular to both. Their normal space is the <linear span> of these two <gradients>, so introduce a second <Lagrange multiplier>:
$$
\boxed{\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g+\mu\nabla h,\qquad g=r,\quad h=s.}
$$
These equations find candidates; deciding whether they are maxima, minima or <saddle points> requires further analysis. Constraint <points> where the required gradient independence fails must be examined separately.

The <angles> in the PDF are measured from the vertical normal to the interface. The horizontal displacements of the two path segments are $d_1\tan\theta$ and $d_2\tan\phi$, while their <lengths> are $d_1\sec\theta$ and $d_2\sec\phi$. Thus the geometric constraint and travel <time> are
$$
g(\theta,\phi)=d_1\tan\theta+d_2\tan\phi=D,\qquad T(\theta,\phi)=\frac{2d_1\sec\theta+\sqrt2d_2\sec\phi}{c}.
$$
The <Lagrange multiplier> equations $\nabla T=\lambda\nabla g$ give
$$
\frac{2d_1}{c}\sec\theta\tan\theta=\lambda d_1\sec^2\theta,\qquad\frac{\sqrt2d_2}{c}\sec\phi\tan\phi=\lambda d_2\sec^2\phi.
$$
Cancelling the positive geometric factors gives $\lambda=2\sin\theta/c=\sqrt2\sin\phi/c$, hence
$$
\boxed{\sin\phi=\sqrt2\sin\theta.}
$$
This is <Snell's law>, $\sin\theta/(c/2)=\sin\phi/(c/\sqrt2)$. The relation depends only on the ratio of the two <speeds>, so changing $D,d_1,d_2$ leaves the relation unchanged; it changes which <angles> satisfy it together with the geometric constraint.

For a minimum check, let $u$ be the horizontal distance to the crossing <point>. Then
$$
T(u)=\frac2c\sqrt{u^2+d_1^2}+\frac{\sqrt2}{c}\sqrt{(D-u)^2+d_2^2},
$$
and
$$
T''(u)=\frac{2d_1^2}{c(u^2+d_1^2)^{3/2}}+\frac{\sqrt2d_2^2}{c((D-u)^2+d_2^2)^{3/2}}>0.
$$
Thus the travel <time> is a <strictly convex function> of $u$, and its <stationary point> is the unique global minimum. For positive $D,d_1,d_2$, $T'(0)<0<T'(D)$, placing the crossing between the two endpoint projections.
