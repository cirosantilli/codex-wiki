Where the original expression is defined, multiplication by $\cos x$ gives
$$
y=\frac{\cos x}{\cos x+\sin x}
=\frac12\left[1+\cot\left(x+\frac\pi4\right)\right].
$$
Thus the graph repeats with period $\pi$ and has vertical <asymptotes>
$$
\boxed{x=-\frac\pi4+k\pi,\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
Between consecutive <asymptotes> the extended <cotangent> branch decreases from $+\infty$ to $-\infty$, since $y'=-1/(\cos x+\sin x)^2<0$. It passes through $(k\pi,1)$ and $(\pi/4+k\pi,1/2)$.

It is essential to <preserve the domain when simplifying a function>. At $x=\pi/2+k\pi$, the original tangent is undefined. The finite limiting value is zero, so the graph has **holes at $(\pi/2+k\pi,0)$**, rather than actual zeros. The simplified expression extends across those points, but the original one does not.

= Solution

The symmetry between the first and third coordinates makes $(1,0,-1)^{\mathsf T}$ an <eigenvector>, with <eigenvalue> $-6$. In the remaining invariant <plane>, write a <vector> as $(s,t,s)^{\mathsf T}$. Its image is $(8s-4t,-8s+4t,8s-4t)^{\mathsf T}$, so the reduced <eigenvalue> equation has <determinant>
$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}8-\lambda&-4\\-8&4-\lambda\end{pmatrix}
=\lambda(\lambda-12).
$$
The corresponding directions have $t=2s$ for $\lambda=0$ and $t=-s$ for $\lambda=12$. Normalizing gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
\lambda=-6&\mathbf e_-=(1,0,-1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt2\\[2pt]
\lambda=0&\mathbf e_0=(1,2,1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt6\\[2pt]
\lambda=12&\mathbf e_+=(1,-1,1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt3.
\end{array}}
$$
Their squared unnormalized norms are $2,6,3$. The pairwise unnormalized <dot products> are $1-1=0$, $1-1=0$, and $1-2+1=0$, so these unit <eigenvectors> are mutually orthogonal. The <eigenvalues> sum to $6$ and have product zero, agreeing with part (a).

The nonparallel direction <unit vectors> have nonzero <cross product>. Let
$$
\mathbf n=\frac{\widehat{\mathbf b}\times\widehat{\mathbf d}}
{|\widehat{\mathbf b}\times\widehat{\mathbf d}|}.
$$
For any joining <vector> $\mathbf s=\mathbf c-\mathbf a+\mu\widehat{\mathbf d}-\lambda\widehat{\mathbf b}$, its normal component is fixed:
$$
\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf n=(\mathbf c-\mathbf a)\cdot\mathbf n.
$$
Thus its length is at least the absolute value of that component. Since the two line directions span $\mathbf n^\perp$, suitable $\lambda,\mu$ cancel the entire component in that <plane>, attaining the bound. Therefore the <distance between skew lines>, including the zero-distance intersecting case, is
$$
\boxed{d_{\min}=\frac{|(\mathbf c-\mathbf a)\cdot
(\widehat{\mathbf b}\times\widehat{\mathbf d})|}
{|\widehat{\mathbf b}\times\widehat{\mathbf d}|}.}
$$
The numerator is a <scalar triple product>. The nonparallel hypothesis is essential to the denominator and to the spanning argument.

Choose any nonzero auxiliary <vector> $\mathbf q$ not collinear with $\mathbf p$. Then $\mathbf p,\mathbf q,\mathbf p\times\mathbf q$ form a <basis>, so write
$$
\mathbf x=\alpha\mathbf p+\beta\mathbf q+\gamma(\mathbf p\times\mathbf q).
$$
Taking the <dot product> with $\mathbf p$ gives $\alpha|\mathbf p|^2+\beta\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf q=k$. This separates the two cases requested in the hint.

If $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf q\ne0$, choose $\alpha,\gamma$ freely and solve for $\beta$:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf x=\alpha\mathbf p+
\frac{k-\alpha|\mathbf p|^2}{\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf q}\,\mathbf q
+\gamma(\mathbf p\times\mathbf q),\qquad \alpha,\gamma\in\mathbb R.}
$$
If $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf q=0$, the coefficient of $\mathbf p$ is fixed and the other two are free:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf x=\frac{k}{|\mathbf p|^2}\mathbf p
+\beta\mathbf q+\gamma(\mathbf p\times\mathbf q),\qquad\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb R.}
$$
These formulas describe the whole <plane> with normal $\mathbf p$, including $k=0$. Completeness follows from the <basis> expansion, not only from checking that the displayed <vectors> satisfy the equation. The <parametrization of a plane with an auxiliary vector> also gives a single formula valid in both cases.

Use the convention $x=r\sin\theta\cos\phi$, $y=r\sin\theta\sin\phi$, $z=r\cos\theta$, with $\theta$ measured from the positive vertical axis. The squared <radius> is
$$
r^2=\frac{\cos^2t+\sin^2t+t^2}{1+t^2}=1.
$$
Since the horizontal radial distance is $1/\sqrt{1+t^2}>0$ for finite $t$,
$$
\cos\theta=\frac{t}{\sqrt{1+t^2}},\qquad
\sin\theta=\frac1{\sqrt{1+t^2}}.
$$
The horizontal angle advances with $t$. Thus the <spherical coordinates> are
$$
\boxed{r(t)=1,\qquad\theta(t)=\frac\pi2-\arctan t,\qquad
\phi(t)=t\pmod{2\pi}.}
$$
An unwrapped continuous azimuth is simply $\phi=t$; a principal-angle convention introduces harmless jumps by $2\pi$. The polar angle stays in $(0,\pi)$ and approaches the two poles only as $t\to\pm\infty$.

Substitute $x=r\cos\phi$, $y=r\sin\phi$ and include the <Jacobian determinant> factor $r$. The integrand times the area element becomes
$$
\big(\cos\phi+\sin\phi+r\cos\phi\sin\phi\big)dr\,d\phi.

$$

The domain stays away from $r=0$, so the denominator of the original integrand causes no singularity in this region.

Write the formal <Maclaurin series> as $\sum_{n\geq0}a_nx^n$, where $a_n=f^{(n)}(0)/n!$. If the successive nonzero coefficient ratios have a limit, the <ratio test> gives
$$
\left|\frac{a_{n+1}x^{n+1}}{a_nx^n}\right|
=|x|\left|\frac{f^{(n+1)}(0)}{(n+1)f^{(n)}(0)}\right|\longrightarrow\frac{|x|}{R},
$$
so the series converges absolutely for $|x|<R$, where
$$
\boxed{R=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{(n+1)f^{(n)}(0)}{f^{(n+1)}(0)}\right|.}
$$
If that ratio does not exist, the <Cauchy-Hadamard theorem> gives the general formula $R^{-1}=\limsup|a_n|^{1/n}$. The <radius of a Maclaurin series from successive derivatives> concerns convergence of the series; equality to the original <function> additionally requires <analyticity> or a vanishing <Taylor remainder>. Mere differentiability, or even smoothness, does not ensure that equality.

For the specified logarithm,
$$
\ln(2+x)=\ln2+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}x^n}{n2^n},\qquad |x|<2.
$$
The absolute successive-term ratio tends to $|x|/2$, so **the interval of <absolute convergence> is $(-2,2)$**. At $x=2$ the nonconstant terms are the alternating harmonic series, which converges conditionally to $\ln2$, giving the full sum $\ln4$. At $x=-2$ all nonconstant terms are $-1/n$, so the series diverges to $-\infty$; the original logarithm is also undefined there. Thus the full real convergence interval is **$(-2,2]$**.

Assume positive corridor widths $a,b$. Write the line through the two wall-contact endpoints in intercept form. Requiring it to pass through the inner corner gives
$$
\frac a{a+X}+\frac b{b+Y}=1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad XY=ab,
\qquad X,Y>0.
$$
Its squared length is $F=(a+X)^2+(b+Y)^2$. Apply <Lagrange multipliers> to this <function> subject to $G=XY-ab=0$:
$$
2(a+X)=\lambda Y,\qquad2(b+Y)=\lambda X.
$$
Multiplying by $X$ and $Y$ respectively eliminates the multiplier, giving $X(a+X)=Y(b+Y)$. Substitute $Y=ab/X$ and multiply by $X^2$:
$$
X^3(a+X)=ab^2(a+X),\qquad
\boxed{(X^3-ab^2)(X+a)=0.}
$$
The branch $X=-a$ is geometrically inadmissible. Thus $X=a^{1/3}b^{2/3}$ and $Y=a^{2/3}b^{1/3}$. If $A=a^{1/3}$ and $B=b^{1/3}$, the two intercepts become $A(A^2+B^2)$ and $B(A^2+B^2)$, so
$$
F_{\min}=(A^2+B^2)^3,
\qquad\boxed{L=(a^{2/3}+b^{2/3})^{3/2}.}
$$
Along $XY=ab$ in the positive quadrant, $F\to\infty$ as $X\to0^+$ or $X\to\infty$, and this is its only <stationary point>. It is therefore the minimum of the longest sticks that fit at each orientation. A stick being turned must pass through every intermediate orientation, so this narrowest clearance is precisely the maximum carryable length, rather than a maximum of $F$. This is the <ladder around a right-angled corner> problem, with a zero-width stick and wall contact allowed as in the ideal geometry.
