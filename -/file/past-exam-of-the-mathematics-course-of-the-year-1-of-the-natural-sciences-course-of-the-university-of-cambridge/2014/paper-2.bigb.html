Normalize the <Rayleigh distribution> by substituting $s=x^2/(2\sigma^2)$:
$$
1=A\int_0^\infty xe^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}\,dx=A\sigma^2,
\qquad\boxed{A=\sigma^{-2}}.
$$
For its sketch, the density starts at zero, is positive for $x>0$, and tends to zero at infinity. <Differentiation> gives
$$
f'(x)=\frac{e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}}{\sigma^2}
\left(1-\frac{x^2}{\sigma^2}\right).
$$
It therefore has a single maximum at $x=\sigma$, with height $e^{-1/2}/\sigma$.

\Image[paper-2-rayleigh-density.png]
{title=Rayleigh density in units of its scale parameter, with its mode and mean}

The <expected value> is
$$
E[X]=\frac1{\sigma^2}\int_0^\infty x^2e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}\,dx.
$$
Integrating by parts with $x\,dx\,e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}=-\sigma^2\,d(e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)})$ makes the boundary term vanish and leaves $\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}\,dx$. The <Gaussian integral> gives
$$
\boxed{E[X]=\sigma\sqrt{\frac\pi2}}.
$$

= Solution
{parent=c}

Use the usual model of independent games, each with Sheila-win <probability> $\theta$. After $m+n$ games, an outcome with $m$ wins and $n$ losses has $\binom{m+n}{m}$ possible orders. Since both totals are below eleven, none of those sequences has already ended the match. Thus
$$
\boxed{P(m\text{ wins},n\text{ losses})=\binom{m+n}{m}\theta^m(1-\theta)^n}.
$$
For a completed Sheila win with $n$ losses, the last game must be her eleventh win. The preceding $10+n$ games contain ten wins and $n$ losses, so their <probability> is $\binom{10+n}{10}\theta^{10}(1-\theta)^n$, followed by one further factor $\theta$. The allowed loss counts are $0\leq n\leq10$, and these terminal events are disjoint. Hence
$$
\boxed{P(\text{Sheila wins match})=
\sum_{n=0}^{10}\binom{10+n}{10}\theta^{11}(1-\theta)^n}.
$$
This is the <match win probability with a fixed winning score>, obtained by the <negative binomial stopping argument>. Constant marginal game-win <probabilities> alone would not justify the product without the usual independence assumption.

A real square <orthogonal matrix> $Q$ satisfies
$$
\boxed{Q^TQ=I},
$$
equivalently $Q^{-1}=Q^T$. Its columns, and also its rows, form an <orthonormal basis>. Consequently it preserves <dot products> and <Euclidean norms>.

\boxed{M^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}
3/8&1/4&3/8\\1/8&-1/4&1/8\\-1/2&0&1/2

The <fundamental theorem of calculus> states that integrating a continuous <function> from a fixed lower endpoint produces an <antiderivative>. More precisely, if $f$ is continuous at the interior point $x$, then
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(y)\,dy=f(x)}.

Indeed, the difference quotient is the average of $f$ over the short interval from $x$ to $x+h$, which tends to $f(x)$ by <continuity>. The accompanying evaluation rule is $\int_a^b f=F(b)-F(a)$ whenever $F'=f$ on the interval.

Exactness after multiplication requires $\partial_y(\mu M)=\partial_x(\mu N)$. Since $\mu$ depends only on $x$,
$$
\mu M_y=\mu' N+\mu N_x.
$$
For $\mu=\exp(\int f(x)\,dx)$, the ratio $\mu'/\mu$ is $f(x)$, so
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\frac{M_y-N_x}{N}},\qquad
\boxed{\mu(x)=\exp\int\frac{M_y-N_x}{N}\,dx}.
$$
The assumed existence means this ratio is a <function> only of $x$ wherever the division is valid. For a factor depending only on $y$, the corresponding equation is $\psi'M+\psi M_y=\psi N_x$, giving
$$
\boxed{\psi(y)=\exp\int\frac{N_x-M_y}{M}\,dy}.
$$
These are the <integrating factor for a differential one-form> formulas. Nonzero constant multiples are equivalent. Exactness follows locally from closedness, and globally on a suitable <simply connected domain>.

With sufficiently many <derivatives>, <Taylor's theorem> gives a finite approximation and a controlled remainder. For example, if $f$ is $N+1$ times continuously differentiable between $a$ and $a+\delta$, then
$$
\boxed{f(a+\delta)=\sum_{j=0}^N\frac{f^{(j)}(a)}{j!}\delta^j
+\frac{f^{(N+1)}(a+\theta\delta)}{(N+1)!}\delta^{N+1}},\qquad0<\theta<1.
$$
Thus <derivatives> at the nearby base point determine the <polynomial> approximation; a bound on the next <derivative> bounds its error. The smallness of $\delta$ makes this useful, provided the <derivatives> remain controlled.

The statement's bare assumption of differentiability alone guarantees only $f(a+\delta)=f(a)+f'(a)\delta+o(\delta)$. Higher-order formulas need higher regularity. Even smoothness does not by itself guarantee equality with an infinite <Taylor series>; that stronger conclusion requires the remainder to tend to zero.

For a short string element of length $\Delta x$, the small-slope approximation makes the upward components of <tension> at its two ends $T y_x(x+\Delta x,t)$ and $-Ty_x(x,t)$. Its <mass> is $\rho\Delta x$. <Newton's second law> gives
$$
\rho\Delta x\,y_{tt}=T\left[y_x(x+\Delta x,t)-y_x(x,t)\right].
$$
Divide by $\Delta x$ and take the limit to obtain the <wave equation on a string>,
$$
\boxed{y_{tt}=c^2y_{xx},\qquad c=\sqrt{\frac T\rho}}.
$$
Here $\rho$ is <linear mass density>, and the derivation assumes small transverse slopes, uniform <tension>, and a flexible string.

At the right endpoint the string pulls the ring toward the interior. Its vertical force is therefore $-Ty_x(L,t)$ to first order; the frictionless wire supplies no vertical force. The ring obeys
$$
\boxed{M y_{tt}(L,t)=-Ty_x(L,t)}.
$$
This is the dynamic endpoint law for a <string with an endpoint mass>.

For a separated oscillatory solution, write $y=X(x)q(t)$ with $q''=-\omega^2q$ and $X''+(\omega/c)^2X=0$. The fixed left end removes the spatial <cosine>, so the nonzero <normal modes> have
$$
y_n(x,t)=\left[A_n\cos(\omega_nt)+B_n\sin(\omega_nt)\right]
\sin\frac{\omega_nx}{c}.
$$
The endpoint law gives $M\omega_n^2\sin(\omega_nL/c)=T(\omega_n/c)\cos(\omega_nL/c)$. Since $\sin(\omega_nL/c)=0$ would also force the <cosine> to vanish, it can be divided out for a nonzero <mode>. Using $T=\rho c^2$ gives
$$
\boxed{\cot\frac{\omega_nL}{c}=\frac{M\omega_n}{\rho c}}.
$$
For physical $M\geq0$, these give all positive modal frequencies. There is no nonzero static linear <mode>: the left value and right slope conditions would force it to vanish. Positivity also follows by <integration by parts> from
$$
\omega^2\left(\rho\int_0^L X^2\,dx+MX(L)^2\right)
=T\int_0^L(X')^2\,dx.
$$

For $M=0$, the right endpoint is free, with the <Neumann boundary condition> $y_x(L,t)=0$. At $L=\pi$, the allowed wave numbers are $k_n=n+1/2$, $n\geq0$, and $\omega_n=ck_n$. Zero initial displacement removes every cosine-in-time coefficient. The initial velocity contains only the modes $k=1/2$ and $k=5/2$, each with coefficient one. Dividing those velocity coefficients by their angular frequencies gives
$$
\boxed{y(x,t)=\frac2c\sin\frac{ct}{2}\sin\frac x2
+\frac{2}{5c}\sin\frac{5ct}{2}\sin\frac{5x}{2}}.
$$
At $x=0$ each term vanishes, at $x=\pi$ each spatial <derivative> vanishes, and at $t=0$ the displacement and its time <derivative> reproduce the two <initial conditions>.

Assume real square-integrable functions on the same integration domain. Put $A=\int f^2$, $B=\int g^2$, and $C=\int fg$. If $B=0$, then $g=0$ almost everywhere, so $C=0$ and the claim follows. If $B>0$, nonnegativity of a square gives

Multiplying by $B$ proves the <integral> form of the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>:
$$
\boxed{\left(\int f^2\,dx\right)\left(\int g^2\,dx\right)
\geq\left(\int fg\,dx\right)^2}.

For nonzero functions, equality holds exactly when they are proportional almost everywhere. The zero-function cases also give equality.

The parameter <derivative> of the integrand is $\cos(a\log x)$, whose absolute value is bounded by one on $0<x<1$. This integrable bound justifies <differentiation under the integral sign>, giving
$$
I'(a)=\int_0^1\cos(a\log x)\,dx.
$$
The original <integral> also exists: $|\sin(a\log x)/\log x|\leq|a|$, and its apparent singularity at $x=1$ is removable. Set $x=e^{-t}$ to evaluate the <derivative>:
$$
I'(a)=\int_0^\infty e^{-t}\cos(at)\,dt
=\operatorname{Re}\int_0^\infty e^{-(1-ia)t}\,dt
=\operatorname{Re}\frac1{1-ia}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{I'(a)=\frac1{1+a^2}}.
$$
Since $I(0)=0$, integration in the parameter fixes the constant and gives the <logarithmic sine integral> identity
$$
\boxed{I(a)=\arctan a,\qquad I(1)=\frac\pi4}.
$$
