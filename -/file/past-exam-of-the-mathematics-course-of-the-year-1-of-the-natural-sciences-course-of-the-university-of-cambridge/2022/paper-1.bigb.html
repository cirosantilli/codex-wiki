$$
\boxed{a^3-b^3=(a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2).}

Expanding the right side cancels the two mixed terms of each degree. The quadratic factor satisfies $a^2+ab+b^2=(a+b/2)^2+3b^2/4$.

Use the full set of <complex logarithm> values of $i$:
$$
\log i=i\left(\frac\pi2+2\pi k\right),\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.
$$
By the definition of <complex exponentiation>, the inner power can therefore take any value $i^i=\exp[-(\pi/2+2\pi k)]$, a positive real number. Its <complex logarithm> values are $-(\pi/2+2\pi k)+2\pi im$, where $m\in\mathbb Z$. Thus
$$
(i^i)^i=\exp\left[-i\left(\frac\pi2+2\pi k\right)-2\pi m\right]=-ie^{-2\pi m}.
$$
All possible <real parts> and <imaginary parts> are consequently
$$
\boxed{\Re((i^i)^i)=0,\qquad\Im((i^i)^i)=-e^{-2\pi m},\quad m\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
Choosing the principal <complex logarithm> at both stages gives the particular value $-i$. The independent logarithm choices are needed because the question asks for all possible values.

The inner power takes the values $r_k=e^{-(\pi/2+2\pi k)}$. For each such real exponent, all values of the outer <complex exponentiation> are obtained from all <complex logarithm> values of its base $i$:
$$
i^{(i^i)}=\exp\left[i\left(\frac\pi2+2\pi m\right)e^{-(\pi/2+2\pi k)}\right],\qquad k,m\in\mathbb Z.
$$
Writing $\Theta_{k,m}=(\pi/2+2\pi m)e^{-(\pi/2+2\pi k)}$, the answer is
$$
\boxed{\Re(i^{(i^i)})=\cos\Theta_{k,m},\qquad\Im(i^{(i^i)})=\sin\Theta_{k,m},\qquad k,m\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
All values lie on the <unit circle>. The principal choice is $k=m=0$, giving $\cos[(\pi/2)e^{-\pi/2}]+i\sin[(\pi/2)e^{-\pi/2}]$. In particular the two nestings in parts (i) and (ii) are different: <complex exponentiation is not associative>.

The PDF diagram includes a solid base of thickness $d$, occupying $-d\leq z\leq0$ and $0\leq r\leq R$. Above it, $0\leq z\leq h$, the metal lies between the two specified curved surfaces. In <cylindrical coordinates>, the <Jacobian determinant> contributes a factor $r$, so the <mass> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
M={}&\rho\int_{-d}^0\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^Rr\,dr\,d\phi\,dz\\
&+\rho\int_0^h\int_0^{2\pi}\int_{(9/10)R(1+z^2/h^2)}^{R(1+z^2/h^2)}r\,dr\,d\phi\,dz.
\end{aligned}

The base contributes $\pi\rho R^2d$. The wall contributes
$$
\pi\rho R^2\left(1-\frac{81}{100}\right)\int_0^h\left(1+\frac{z^2}{h^2}\right)^2dz=\pi\rho R^2\frac{19}{100}h\left(1+\frac23+\frac15\right)=\frac{133}{375}\pi\rho R^2h.

\boxed{M=\pi\rho R^2\left(d+\frac{133h}{375}\right).}

Because the original <differential equation> divides by $xy$, its domain requires $x\ne0$ and $y\ne0$. Set $u=y^2$, so $u'=2yy'$. The equation becomes the <first-order linear differential equation>

u'+\frac2x u=-4x-2.

$$
(x^2u)'=-4x^3-2x^2,\qquad x^2u=-x^4-\frac23x^3+C.

Returning to $y$ gives the explicit general real solutions
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\pm\sqrt{\frac C{x^2}-x^2-\frac23x},\qquad C\in\mathbb R.}

Each sign is fixed on a connected <interval> where $x\ne0$ and the radicand is strictly positive. The strict condition excludes $y=0$, where the original <differential equation> is undefined. There is no additional zero solution.

For $a\ne0$, factor out $|a|$, not $a$, and apply the <binomial series> with exponent $1/2$:
$$
\sqrt{a^2+x^2}=|a|\left(1+\frac{x^2}{a^2}\right)^{1/2}=\boxed{|a|+\frac{x^2}{2|a|}-\frac{x^4}{8|a|^3}+O(x^6).}
$$
For each fixed nonzero $a$, these are the first three nonzero terms of the <Taylor series> about zero; the full <binomial series> converges when $|x|<|a|$.

For $a=0$, the <function> is $|x|$. Its right <derivative> at zero is $1$ and its left <derivative> is $-1$, so **no Taylor series about zero exists when $a=0$**. The one-sided formulas are exactly $x$ for $x\geq0$ and $-x$ for $x\leq0$; they do not form a single two-sided <Taylor expansion>, nor can they supply three nonzero terms. The parameter case explicitly included in the question must therefore be treated as this exception.

Under the usual regularity assumptions, the <Leibniz integral rule> is
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dx}\int_{g(x)}^{f(x)}h(x,t)\,dt=h(x,f(x))f'(x)-h(x,g(x))g'(x)+\int_{g(x)}^{f(x)}h_x(x,t)\,dt.}
$$
There is a genuine domain problem in the following integral as printed in the PDF. Put
$$
q(t)=\frac{2t^4+1}{(t-2)(t^2+3)}.
$$
The point $t=2$ is a simple pole, with
$$
\lim_{t\to2}(t-2)q(t)=\frac{33}{7}\ne0.
$$
For every $x>4$, the lower endpoint $\sin(1/x)$ is less than $2$ and the upper endpoint $\sqrt x$ is greater than $2$. The interval therefore crosses the pole. Since $q(t)=33/[7(t-2)]+O(1)$ near $2$, the two one-sided <improper integrals> diverge logarithmically, with opposite signs. Their divergences do not define an ordinary <integral>. Consequently **the ordinary limit in the question is undefined as written**: its underlying integral has no finite value for any $x\geq4$.

A separately specified <Cauchy principal value> does exist for $x>4$. Under that interpretation, <differentiating a principal-value integral across a fixed simple pole> gives
$$
\frac d{dx}\operatorname{PV}\int_{\sin(1/x)}^{\sqrt x}q(t)\,dt=\frac{q(\sqrt x)}{2\sqrt x}+\frac{\cos(1/x)}{x^2}q(\sin(1/x)).
$$
The first term is
$$
\frac{2x^2+1}{2\sqrt x(\sqrt x-2)(x+3)}\longrightarrow1,
$$
and the second tends to zero because $q$ is bounded near $t=0$. Thus the principal-value version has limit $\boxed{1}$. The two conclusions are distinct: this regularised answer requires the additional principal-value convention, which the printed question does not state.

First suppose $\alpha>0$. <Differentiation under the integral sign>, justified on compact positive parameter <intervals> by an integrable bound, gives
$$
f'(\alpha)=\int_0^\infty\frac{\ln(\alpha^2+x^2)}{1+x^2}\,dx+2\alpha^2\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{(x^2+\alpha^2)(x^2+1)}.
$$
The first <integral> is $f(\alpha)/\alpha$. For $\alpha\ne1$, use the <partial fraction decomposition>
$$
\frac1{(x^2+\alpha^2)(x^2+1)}=\frac1{1-\alpha^2}\left(\frac1{x^2+\alpha^2}-\frac1{x^2+1}\right).
$$
The standard <inverse tangent> <antiderivatives> then give
$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{(x^2+\alpha^2)(x^2+1)}=\frac1{1-\alpha^2}\left(\frac\pi{2\alpha}-\frac\pi2\right)=\frac\pi{2\alpha(1+\alpha)}.
$$
The same value holds at $\alpha=1$ by <continuity>, or by direct <integration>. Hence the proposed <first-order linear differential equation> is valid on the positive parameter range:
$$
\boxed{f'(\alpha)-\frac{f(\alpha)}\alpha=\frac{\pi\alpha}{1+\alpha},\qquad\alpha>0.}
$$
Divide by $\alpha$ to recognise a <quotient rule> derivative:
$$
\left(\frac{f(\alpha)}\alpha\right)'=\frac\pi{1+\alpha},\qquad f(\alpha)=\alpha[\pi\ln(1+\alpha)+C].
$$
To fix $C$, the value $f(0)=0$ alone is insufficient, since every displayed solution tends to zero there. Instead consider
$$
\lim_{\alpha\to0^+}\frac{f(\alpha)}\alpha=\int_0^\infty\frac{\ln(x^2)}{1+x^2}\,dx.
$$
This limit follows from the <dominated convergence theorem>: for $0<\alpha\leq1$ and $0<x<1$, the logarithm's <absolute value> is bounded by $2|\ln x|+\ln2$; for $x\geq1$, it is at most $\ln(1+x^2)$. After division by $1+x^2$, these are integrable. The limiting <improper integral> is absolutely convergent, and the <substitution rule> $x=1/t$ changes it into its own negative. Its value is therefore zero, giving $C=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty\frac{\alpha\ln(\alpha^2+x^2)}{1+x^2}\,dx=\pi\alpha\ln(1+\alpha),\qquad\alpha>0.}
$$
The question calls $\alpha$ a real parameter, but this last formula and the proposed differential equation do not hold on the entire real axis. The defining <integral> is odd in $\alpha$, because its logarithm depends on $\alpha^2$. Applying the positive-parameter result to $|\alpha|$ therefore gives the correct extension
$$
\boxed{f(\alpha)=\pi\alpha\ln(1+|\alpha|),\qquad\alpha\in\mathbb R,}
$$
with $f(0)=0$. In particular, for $\alpha<0$, the right-hand side of the differential equation must be $-\pi\alpha/(1-\alpha)$; for every $\alpha\ne0$ the unified expression is $\pi|\alpha|/(1+|\alpha|)$. The <logarithmic quadratic parameter integral> therefore provides the requested identity on its valid range and the corrected answer elsewhere.
