= Solution
{parent=2}

On the basic interval the graph is the pair of straight segments joining $(-\pi,0)$, $(0,1)$ and $(\pi,0)$. Its <periodic extension> is an <even> <triangular wave>.

\Image[paper-1-triangular-wave.png]
{title=The even triangular wave and its periodic extension}
{description=Original plot marking the basic interval and the repeated linear pieces.}

The <Fourier coefficients> satisfy $b_n=0$ by evenness, and
$$
a_0=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi(1-x/\pi)dx=1,\qquad a_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi(1-x/\pi)\cos(nx)dx=\frac{2[1-(-1)^n]}{\pi^2n^2}.
$$
For the second formula, <integration by parts> gives $\int_0^\pi x\cos(nx)dx=[(-1)^n-1]/n^2$. Only odd indices survive, giving this rescaled <Fourier cosine series of a triangular wave>:
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\frac12+\frac4{\pi^2}\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{\cos[(2m+1)x]}{(2m+1)^2}.}
$$
The <pointwise convergence of a piecewise smooth Fourier series> applies throughout the interval, including the continuous joined endpoints. The coefficients also imply <uniform convergence> by the <Weierstrass M-test>. At $x=0$, the value is one, so
$$
\boxed{\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac1{(2m+1)^2}=\frac{\pi^2}8.}
$$

Put $c=\cos\theta$, $s=\sin\theta$. The <characteristic polynomial> is $(c-\lambda)^2-s^2$, giving
$$
\boxed{\lambda_+=c+s,\qquad\lambda_-=c-s.}
$$
When $s=0$, these <eigenvalues> coincide and $A=cI=\pm I$.

= Solution

Adding and subtracting the two coordinates shows that $A(1,1)^T=(c+s)(1,1)^T$ and $A(1,-1)^T=(c-s)(1,-1)^T$. The normalized <eigenvectors> can therefore be chosen as
$$
\boxed{v_+=\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)^T,\qquad v_-=\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)^T.}
$$
Their <inner product> is $(1-1)/2=0$, and their squared <norms> are one, proving <orthonormality>. If $s=0$, every nonzero <vector> is an <eigenvector>; this chosen <orthonormal basis> still works.

The <fundamental theorem of calculus> differentiates the upper endpoint, while <differentiation under the integral sign> differentiates the parameter:
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial\theta}\right)_k=g(\theta,k),\qquad \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial k}\right)_\theta=\int_0^\theta\frac{\partial g}{\partial k}(x,k)dx.}
$$
These formulas hold, for example, when $g$ and its displayed parameter <partial derivative> are continuous on a neighborhood of the integration interval and the parameter value.

{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Work first with $|k|<1$, so the real integrands and their parameter <derivatives> are smooth on every finite amplitude interval. For the <elliptic integral of the second kind>,
$$
E_k=-k\int_0^\theta\frac{\sin^2x}{\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2x}}dx.
$$
On the other hand, subtracting the integrands of the second-kind and first-kind <elliptic integrals> gives
$$
E-F=\int_0^\theta\frac{(1-k^2\sin^2x)-1}{\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2x}}dx=-k^2\int_0^\theta\frac{\sin^2x}{\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2x}}dx.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{E_k=\frac{E-F}{k}\quad(k\ne0).}
$$
This is the <modulus derivative of the incomplete elliptic integral of the second kind>. At $k=0$ its right-hand side has a removable limit zero, agreeing with $E_k(\theta,0)=0$. Both $E$ and $F$ here use amplitude $\theta$ and modulus $k$; differentiating with respect to the alternative parameter $k^2$ would give a different prefactor.

{parent=4}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The <fundamental theorem of calculus> and part (a) give
$$
I_\theta=E(\theta,k)\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2\theta}=E(\theta,k)E_\theta(\theta,k)=\partial_\theta\left(\frac12E(\theta,k)^2\right).
$$
The difference is constant in $\theta$. Since $I(0,k)=E(0,k)=0$, the constant is zero, proving
$$
\boxed{I(\theta,k)=\frac12E(\theta,k)^2.}
$$
This is simply the <chain rule> integrated, with $E$ itself as the antiderivative of the square-root factor.

= Solution

For $f=x\ln x$, the <derivatives> are $f'=1+\ln x$ and $f''=1/x>0$. Its unique <stationary point> is the global <minimum> $(e^{-1},-e^{-1})$. For $g=\ln x/x$, $g'=(1-\ln x)/x^2$ changes from positive to negative at $x=e$, giving the global <maximum> $(e,e^{-1})$. Their endpoint <limits> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|cc}&x\to0^+&x\to\infty\\\hline f(x)&0\text{ from below}&+\infty\\g(x)&-\infty&0\text{ from above}\end{array}}
$$
An intersection requires $(x^2-1)\ln x=0$, and on $x>0$ both factors vanish only at $x=1$. There $f=g=0$ and $f'=g'=1$, proving a common <tangent line> $y=x-1$. In fact,
$$
f-g=\frac{(x^2-1)\ln x}{x}>0\quad(x>0,\ x\ne1),
$$
because the two numerator factors always have the same sign. Thus the curves touch at $(1,0)$ without exchanging their order.

{parent=5}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Use $u=1/x$, so $dx=-du/u^2$ and $\ln x=-\ln u$. Reversing the integration bounds gives, without evaluating either side,
$$
\boxed{\int_1^\infty\frac{\ln x}{x^2}dx=-\int_0^1\ln u\,du.}
$$
For $J=\int_0^\infty\ln x/(1+x^2)dx$, the same <change of variables> gives
$$
J=-\int_0^\infty\frac{\ln u}{1+u^2}du=-J,
$$
so $\boxed{J=0}$. These are legitimate transformations of <improper integrals>: $|\ln x|$ is integrable near zero, and $(\ln x)/x^2$ is integrable at infinity. In particular $J$ is absolutely convergent, so no cancellation of divergent endpoints is being assumed.

= Solution

Take the outward <orientation of a surface>. The <divergence> is $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=y^2+z^2+x^2$, so the <divergence theorem> converts the closed <surface flux> into an integral over the box:
$$
\int_S\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf S=\int_{-a}^a\int_{-b}^b\int_{-c}^c(x^2+y^2+z^2)\,dz\,dy\,dx.
$$
Each term separates; for example the $x^2$ term is $(2a^3/3)(2b)(2c)$. Summing gives
$$
\boxed{\int_S\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf S=\frac{8abc}3(a^2+b^2+c^2).}
$$
Here $a,b,c>0$ are the box half-lengths. Reversing <orientation of a surface> would negate the result.

= b
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Differentiate the ellipsoidal parametrization. Its outward <vector surface element> is
$$
\mathbf r_\theta\times\mathbf r_\phi=(bc\sin^2\theta\cos\phi,\ ac\sin^2\theta\sin\phi,\ ab\sin\theta\cos\theta).
$$
For $a,b,c>0$, its <dot product> with $\mathbf r$ simplifies to $abc\sin\theta$. Thus the requested direct <surface integral> is
$$
\boxed{\int_S\mathbf r\cdot d\mathbf S=abc\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi=4\pi abc.}
$$
As a check, the <divergence> of $\mathbf r$ is three, and the <ellipsoid> has <volume> $4\pi abc/3$, so the <divergence theorem> gives the same value.

Row reduction, or the <cofactor> formula for a <matrix inverse>, gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\1&2&3\\1&4&9\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}3&-5/2&1/2\\-3&4&-1\\1&-3/2&1/2\end{pmatrix}.}

= Solution
{parent=8}

Write $r=|\mathbf r|$ and $q=\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf r$. Away from the origin, $\nabla q=\mathbf p$ and $\nabla r^{-m}=-m\mathbf r/r^{m+2}$. The <product rule> gives
$$
\nabla\cdot(q\mathbf r r^{-5})=q r^{-5}+q(3r^{-5}-5r^{-5})=-q r^{-5},\qquad \nabla\cdot(\mathbf p r^{-3})=-3q r^{-5}.
$$
Consequently the <divergence> of $\mathbf E=3q\mathbf r r^{-5}-\mathbf p r^{-3}$ is zero. For any closed surface bounding a region that excludes the origin, the field is smooth throughout that region, and the <divergence theorem> proves
$$
\boxed{\int_A\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf S=0.}
$$
For the infinite cylinder, use $\mathbf r=(a\cos\phi,a\sin\phi,z)$ and outward <unit normal> $(\cos\phi,\sin\phi,0)$. With $\mathbf p=(0,0,1)$, $q=z$, $dS=a\,d\phi\,dz$, and $\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf n=3az/(a^2+z^2)^{5/2}$. Hence the direct <surface flux> is
$$
\int_0^{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{3a^2z}{(a^2+z^2)^{5/2}}dz\,d\phi=\boxed0.
$$
The integrand is <odd> in $z$ and absolutely integrable. This calculation treats the unbounded cylindrical side directly, without applying the bounded-region <divergence theorem> across the field's singularity.

For an $N+1$ times continuously differentiable <function>, <Taylor's theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^N\frac{f^{(n)}(x_0)}{n!}(x-x_0)^n+\frac{f^{(N+1)}(\xi)}{(N+1)!}(x-x_0)^{N+1},}
$$
where $\xi$ lies between $x_0$ and $x$. An infinite <Taylor series> represents the function when its remainder tends to zero; this holds locally for an <analytic function>, rather than for every smooth function automatically.

Factor the <Gaussian function> as $e^{-(x-a)^2}=e^{-a^2}e^{2ax}e^{-x^2}$. Multiplying the two <exponential series> gives
$$
e^{-(x-a)^2}=e^{-a^2}\left[1+2ax+(2a^2-1)x^2+\left(\frac43a^3-2a\right)x^3+O(x^4)\right].
$$
For $a\ne0,\pm1/\sqrt2$, its first three nonzero terms are
$$
\boxed{e^{-a^2}\bigl[1+2ax+(2a^2-1)x^2\bigr].}
$$
Vanishing coefficients require separate treatment in this <Taylor expansion of a translated Gaussian>. If $a=0$, the first three nonzero terms are $\boxed{1-x^2+x^4/2}$. If $a=\pm1/\sqrt2$, they are
$$
\boxed{e^{-1/2}\left(1+2ax-\frac{4a}3x^3\right).}
$$
The latter expression retains the cubic term because the quadratic coefficient is zero.

$$
\operatorname{tr}(ABC)=a_{ij}b_{jk}c_{ki}=c_{ki}a_{ij}b_{jk}=\operatorname{tr}(CAB),

Expand $(I+B)^2$ in components:
$$
(A^2)_{ij}=(\delta_{ik}+b_{ik})(\delta_{kj}+b_{kj})=\boxed{\delta_{ij}+2b_{ij}+b_{ik}b_{kj}}.
$$
If $B^2=0$, all powers $B^k$ for $k\geq2$ vanish. The matrix <binomial theorem>, valid here because $I$ commutes with $B$, therefore yields
$$
\boxed{A^n=I+nB,\qquad(A^n)_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+nb_{ij}.}
$$
These <powers of the identity plus a square-zero matrix> can also be proved by induction: $(I+nB)(I+B)=I+(n+1)B$. For the displayed example, $B^2=0$ directly, and
$$
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}1&p\\0&1\end{pmatrix}^{n}=\begin{pmatrix}1&np\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
This is the repeated <function composition> of a <shear mapping>. The argument gives every nonnegative integer $n$; since $(I+B)^{-1}=I-B$, the same formula also holds for negative integers.

For a <probability density function> $P$, the <mean> and <variance> are
$$
\boxed{\mu=\int_{-\infty}^\infty xP(x)dx,\qquad \sigma^2=\int_{-\infty}^\infty(x-\mu)^2P(x)dx=\mathbb E[X^2]-\mu^2.}
$$
These formulas assume the required <moments> are finite; finiteness of the second moment ensures a finite mean as well.

= b
{parent=12}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The triangular <probability density function> is normalized because $2\int_0^1(1-x)dx=1$. It is <even>, so the mean integrand is <odd> and $\mu=0$. Its <variance> is consequently its second <moment>:
$$
\boxed{\mu=0,\qquad\sigma^2=2\int_0^1x^2(1-x)dx=2\left(\frac13-\frac14\right)=\frac16.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For the standard <normal density>, $P'(x)=-xP(x)$. Using <integration by parts>,
$$
\mathbb E[X^4]=-\int_{-\infty}^\infty x^3P'(x)dx=-[x^3P(x)]_{-\infty}^\infty+3\int_{-\infty}^\infty x^2P(x)dx.
$$
The boundary term vanishes because <Gaussian decay dominates every power>. Since the given <mean> is zero and <variance> is one, $\mathbb E[X^2]=1$, yielding the <Gaussian fourth moment>
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X^4]=3.}
$$
