The two <linearly independent vectors> $\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2$ span the <plane> perpendicular to $\mathbf w$. Suitable $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ cancel the component of $\mathbf d$ in that <plane>, attaining the lower bound. Thus the <distance between skew lines> formula, also valid when the <lines> intersect, gives
$$
\boxed{d(L_1,L_2)=\frac{|(\mathbf a_2-\mathbf a_1)\cdot(\mathbf v_1\times\mathbf v_2)|}{\|\mathbf v_1\times\mathbf v_2\|}.}
$$

Write $F(x)=\ln(e^{ax}-b)$. Direct <differentiation>, using the <chain rule>, gives
$$
F'(x)=\frac{ae^{ax}}{e^{ax}-b},\qquad F''(x)=-\frac{a^2be^{ax}}{(e^{ax}-b)^2}.
$$
The stated parameter restrictions ensure that the <logarithm> is defined near $x=1$, and that $F'(1)>0$ and $F''(1)<0$. With $h=x-1$, the <Taylor series> begins
$$
F(1+h)=\ln(e^a-b)+\frac{ae^a}{e^a-b}h-\frac{a^2be^a}{2(e^a-b)^2}h^2+O(h^3).
$$
If $e^a-b\ne1$, the constant term is nonzero, and the first two nonzero terms are
$$
\boxed{\ln(e^a-b)+\frac{ae^a}{e^a-b}(x-1)+O((x-1)^2).}
$$
This covers both $a>\ln(1+b)$ and $0<a<\ln(1+b)$; the constant term is respectively positive or negative.

If $a=\ln(1+b)$, which is possible precisely with $0<a<\ln2$ and $b=e^a-1$, the constant term vanishes. The first two nonzero terms are then
$$
\boxed{a(1+b)(x-1)-\frac{a^2b(1+b)}2(x-1)^2+O((x-1)^3).}
$$
No further case is needed, since the linear and quadratic coefficients cannot vanish under the given restrictions. All remainders use <Big O notation>.

Where $\cos x\ne0$, the <separable differential equation> is $y'=y\tan x$. Separating and taking <antiderivatives> gives $\ln|y|=-\ln|\cos x|+C$, for nonzero $y$. Including the zero solution, the explicit general solution is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=C\sec x,\qquad C\in\mathbb R,}
$$
on any <interval> with $\cos x\ne0$. It can also be obtained without dividing by $y$: the <product rule> gives $(y\cos x)'=0$.

A nonzero solution cannot extend through a zero of $\cos x$. If a differentiable solution is required on an <interval> containing such a zero, the original equation there requires $y=0$, and the only such solution across that zero is $y\equiv0$.

= Solution

For $x\ne0$ and $y\ne0$, put $v=y/x$. This is the standard substitution for a <homogeneous first-order differential equation>. Since $y'=v+xv'$, the equation becomes
$$
1+v^2-v(v+xv')=0,\qquad vv'=\frac1x.
$$
Taking <antiderivatives> yields $v^2=2\ln|x|+C$. The explicit general solution is therefore
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\pm x\sqrt{2\ln|x|+C},}
$$
on any <interval> where $x\ne0$ and $2\ln|x|+C>0$, with a fixed choice of sign. For a check that does not divide by $y$, set $w=y^2$ to get $w'-2w/x=2x$ and hence $w=x^2(2\ln|x|+C)$.

There is no missing zero solution: at $y=0$ with $x\ne0$, the original equation would require $x^2=0$. Also no real solution on an <interval> about $x=0$ can arise from this family, since $2\ln|x|+C$ becomes negative near $0$.

A <Fourier series> represents a periodic <function> as a constant plus <sine> and <cosine> waves whose frequencies are integer multiples of its fundamental frequency. For period $2L$, use the convention
$$

$$

b_n&=\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}f(x)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\,dx,\quad n\geq1.
\end{aligned}}

The coefficient formulas follow by multiplying by a basis <sine> or <cosine> and using their <orthogonality> over a full period. A <Fourier series> need not equal an arbitrary <function> at every <point>. The <pointwise convergence of a piecewise smooth Fourier series> theorem says that for a piecewise continuously differentiable <function>, the <Fourier series> converges at each <point> to the average of the two one-sided limits, and hence to $f(x)$ wherever $f$ is continuous.
