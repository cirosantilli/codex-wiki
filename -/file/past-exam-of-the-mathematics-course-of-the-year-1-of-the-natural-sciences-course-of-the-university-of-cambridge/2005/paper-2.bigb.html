= Solution

A differentiable <gradient field> must have equal mixed derivatives. Here $\partial F_y/\partial z=3z^2$ but $\partial F_z/\partial y=-x$, which are not identically equal on any open region. Equivalently,
$$
\nabla\times\mathbf F=(-x-3z^2,\ x+y-3z^2,\ 0).
$$
Thus **no scalar potential exists for this field on an open region**. Vanishing of the <curl> on a special lower-dimensional set would not supply a three-dimensional potential.

The $y$ component integrates to $\phi=xy e^{z-x}+g(x,z)$. Matching the $x$ component gives $g_x=2x$, and matching the $z$ component then gives the remaining derivative zero. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\phi(x,y,z)=xy e^{z-x}+x^2+C.}
$$
Direct differentiation recovers all three components, proving this is a <gradient field> globally on $\mathbb R^3$.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Write $\mathbf F=\mathbf r\,r^{-\gamma-1}$. For $r>0$, direct Cartesian differentiation gives the <divergence and curl of a radial vector field>:
$$
\partial_i(x_i r^{-\gamma-1})=3r^{-\gamma-1}-(\gamma+1)r^{-\gamma-3}x_ix_i=(2-\gamma)r^{-\gamma-1}.
$$
The volume element in <spherical coordinates> therefore gives, for $\gamma\ne2$,
$$
\boxed{\int_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV=4\pi(2-\gamma)\int_\epsilon^R r^{1-\gamma}dr=4\pi(R^{2-\gamma}-\epsilon^{2-\gamma}).}
$$
For the direct <surface integral>, the outer sphere has outward normal $+\mathbf n$ and contributes $4\pi R^{2-\gamma}$. The inner boundary's outward normal relative to the shell is $-\mathbf n$, so it contributes $-4\pi\epsilon^{2-\gamma}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\int_S\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf A=4\pi(R^{2-\gamma}-\epsilon^{2-\gamma}).}
$$
For $\gamma=2$, the field has zero ordinary divergence everywhere in this shell. The separate surface contributions are $4\pi$ and $-4\pi$, so **both full integrals are zero**. This <radial power-law flux through a spherical shell> was obtained by direct differentiation and integration; the excluded origin is never part of the volume.

A homogeneous <linear system> has a nonzero solution exactly when its coefficient <matrix> is singular. Expansion of the <determinant> gives
$$
\det M=5\alpha^2+3\alpha-8=(\alpha-1)(5\alpha+8).
$$
Thus the allowed values are $\boxed{\alpha=1\text{ or }-8/5}$. Solving the first two equations and checking the third gives
$$
\boxed{\alpha=1:\ x=\frac z5,\ y=-\frac{7z}5;\qquad \alpha=-\frac85:\ x=-5z,\ y=\frac{5z}2.}
$$
In each case $z\ne0$ gives a nonzero solution and spans the one-dimensional <null space>; $z=0$ gives only the zero vector.

= Solution

Since $A^2=-\epsilon^2I$, its even powers are $A^{2k}=(-1)^k\epsilon^{2k}I$ and its odd powers are $A^{2k+1}=(-1)^k\epsilon^{2k}A$. Splitting the <matrix exponential> into these two absolutely convergent subseries gives the <matrix exponential for a scalar square>:
$$
\boxed{S=e^A=I\cos\epsilon+\frac{\sin\epsilon}{\epsilon}A=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\epsilon&\sin\epsilon\\-\sin\epsilon&\cos\epsilon\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
At $\epsilon=0$, the coefficient $\sin\epsilon/\epsilon$ has limiting value one and $A=0$, so $S=I$. The result is a planar rotation through angle $-\epsilon$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Now $B^2=\epsilon^2I$, so splitting its <matrix exponential> into even and odd powers yields hyperbolic functions:
$$
\boxed{T=e^B=I\cosh\epsilon+\frac{\sinh\epsilon}{\epsilon}B=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh\epsilon&\sinh\epsilon\\\sinh\epsilon&\cosh\epsilon\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The limiting coefficient at $\epsilon=0$ is again one, and $T=I$. The <eigenvectors> $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ have factors $e^\epsilon$ and $e^{-\epsilon}$, respectively, showing how this hyperbolic transformation differs from a rotation.

= Solution

The <Lagrange multiplier> equations are $2x=2\lambda x$, $-2y=2\lambda y$, together with $x^2+y^2=1$. If $x\ne0$, then $\lambda=1$ and $y=0$; if $y\ne0$, then $\lambda=-1$ and $x=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{z_{\max}=1\text{ at }(\pm1,0),\qquad z_{\min}=-1\text{ at }(0,\pm1).}
$$
The constraint is compact and its gradient never vanishes, so these exhaust the possible global extrema. Equivalently, substituting $x=\cos\theta$, $y=\sin\theta$ gives $z=\cos2\theta$ along the boundary.

= b
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Minimize the squared distance $D^2=(x-x_0)^2+(y-y_0)^2$ subject to $g=y-mx-c=0$. The <Lagrange multiplier> condition $\nabla D^2=\lambda\nabla g$ gives $x=x_0-m\lambda/2$ and $y=y_0+\lambda/2$. Enforcing the line constraint gives $\lambda=2(mx_0+c-y_0)/(1+m^2)$, hence
$$
\boxed{x_*=\frac{x_0+m(y_0-c)}{1+m^2},\qquad y_*=\frac{mx_0+m^2y_0+c}{1+m^2}.}
$$
The displacement to this point is parallel to the line's <normal vector> $(-m,1)$, so it is the <orthogonal projection>. The squared distance restricted to the line has positive quadratic coefficient $1+m^2$, proving this stationary point is the unique global minimum.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Let $w>0$ denote the stated cost per area of the cylindrical wall. Charge for the curved wall and cap as specified; no floor cost is supplied. The volume and cost are $V=\pi r^2h+2\pi r^3/3$ and $C=2\pi wrh+4\pi wr^2$. To minimize cost at fixed volume, apply a <Lagrange multiplier> to $C-\lambda(\pi r^2h+2\pi r^3/3-V)$. Differentiation with respect to $h$ gives $\lambda=2w/r$. The $r$ derivative then gives $2\pi wh+8\pi wr=\lambda(2\pi rh+2\pi r^2)$, reducing to $h=2r$. Thus the <optimal cylinder and hemispherical cap with unequal surface costs> has
$$
\boxed{r=\left(\frac{3V}{8\pi}\right)^{1/3},\qquad h=2\left(\frac{3V}{8\pi}\right)^{1/3}.}
$$
For a global check, eliminate $h$ to obtain $C(r)=2wV/r+(8\pi w/3)r^2$. Its second derivative is positive for $r>0$, and its critical radius gives $h>0$, so the constrained optimum lies inside the physically feasible range and is the unique minimum.

= Solution
{parent=a}

The required <Legendre transform> is the <Helmholtz free energy>, $\boxed{F=U-TS}$ up to an additive constant. Its differential is $dF=dU-TdS-SdT=-SdT-pdV$, so $F_T|_V=-S$ and $F_V|_T=-p$. Equality of mixed partial derivatives for a smooth thermodynamic state gives the <Maxwell relation>
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T=\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_V.}
$$
Next use the <total differential> $dS=S_T|_VdT+S_V|_TdV$. Along a fixed-pressure path, divide by $dT$ to get $S_T|_p=S_T|_V+S_V|_T V_T|_p$. Substitute the Maxwell relation to obtain
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_p-\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_V=\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_V\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_p.}
$$
Multiplying by $T$ gives the usual difference between the <heat capacity at constant pressure> and the <heat capacity at constant volume>.

= b
{parent=7}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

At fixed entropy, the <total differential> in coordinates $(T,p)$ is $0=S_T|_p\,dT+S_p|_T\,dp$. Dividing by $dT$ along that path gives $S_T|_p=-S_p|_T p_T|_S$. The same argument in coordinates $(T,V)$ gives $S_T|_V=-S_V|_T V_T|_S$. These are the two required intermediate identities.

To prove the final result without cancelling a possibly vanishing thermal-expansion derivative, write $a=S_T|_V$, $b=S_V|_T$, $c=p_T|_V$, $d=p_V|_T$. In a regular thermodynamic region with $a,d\ne0$, fixed pressure gives $V_T|_p=-c/d$, while fixed entropy gives $T_V|_S=-b/a$. The <chain rule> then gives $S_T|_p=a-bc/d$ and $p_V|_S=d-bc/a$. Both cross-products equal $ad-bc$, proving
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_p\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial V}\right)_T=\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial V}\right)_S\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_V.}
$$
The <heat-capacity and compressibility ratio identity> rewrites this as $C_p/C_V=\kappa_T/\kappa_S$: keeping entropy fixed rather than temperature fixed changes the mechanical stiffness by the same factor as the two thermal responses.

The form is **not exact**, because $P_y=1$ and $Q_x=2$. For an <integrating factor depending on one coordinate>, the condition $(\mu P)_y=(\mu Q)_x$ with $\mu=\mu(x)$ gives $\mu'/\mu=(P_y-Q_x)/Q=-1/(2x)$. Thus

\boxed{\mu(x)=|x|^{-1/2}\quad\text{on either half-plane }x\ne0.}

A real <normal matrix> satisfies $MM^T=M^TM$. For a <symmetric matrix>, $M^T=M$ and both products equal $M^2$. For an <antisymmetric matrix>, $M^T=-M$ and both equal $-M^2$. For an <orthogonal matrix>, both products are the identity. Thus **each of the three classes consists of normal matrices**.

The upper $2\times2$ block has trace zero and determinant $-\cos^22\theta-\sin^22\theta=-1$, so its characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2-1$. The third coordinate has eigenvalue two. Angle-addition identities give the normalized <eigenvectors>
$$
\boxed{\lambda_+=1:\ \mathbf u_+=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0),\qquad\lambda_-=-1:\ \mathbf u_-=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0),\qquad\lambda_3=2:\ \mathbf u_3=(0,0,1).}

These formulas apply to every real $\theta$, including values where an expression obtained by dividing by $\sin2\theta$ would fail. Geometrically the planar block is a reflection: it fixes the axis at angle $\theta$ and reverses its perpendicular direction.

For a strictly decreasing function, $f(n+1)<f(x)<f(n)$ in the interior of $[n,n+1]$. Integrating over the unit interval and summing from $n=r$ to $s-1$ gives the <integral comparison for a nonincreasing nonnegative function>:
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=r+1}^sf(n)<\int_r^sf(x)dx<\sum_{n=r}^{s-1}f(n).}
$$
Geometrically, right-endpoint rectangles lie below the curve and left-endpoint rectangles above it. If “decreasing” permits flat pieces, use non-strict inequalities unless strictness is ensured on some interval. When $r=1$, the endpoint value $f(1)$ is also needed; the power function below is defined there.

For $f(x)=x^{-1-p}$ with $p>0$, the <integral comparison> on $[1,N]$ gives $\sum_{n=2}^Nn^{-1-p}<I_N<\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}n^{-1-p}$, where $I_N=(1-N^{-p})/p$. Add the missing endpoint term on each side to obtain
$$
\boxed{\frac{1-N^{-p}}p+N^{-1-p}<\sum_{n=1}^Nn^{-1-p}<\frac{1-N^{-p}}p+1.}
$$
The lower bound uses the right inequality for $I_N$ and adds $N^{-1-p}$; the upper uses the left inequality and adds one.

{parent=10}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

For fixed $p>0$, the partial sums are increasing and bounded above by $1+1/p$. The <monotone convergence theorem for sequences> therefore proves convergence to a finite sum $S(p)$. Passing to the limit $N\to\infty$ in the two bounds gives
$$
\frac1p\leq S(p)\leq\frac1p+1,\qquad1\leq pS(p)\leq1+p.
$$
The <squeeze theorem> now yields
$$
\boxed{\lim_{p\downarrow0}p\sum_{n=1}^\infty n^{-1-p}=1.}
$$
Since $S(p)=\zeta(1+p)$, this is the <real-axis singular limit of the Riemann zeta function at one>. The limiting sum is taken at fixed $p$ before sending $p$ to zero; setting $p=0$ inside the convergent-series argument would instead give the divergent harmonic series.

= Solution

Work with $t>0$ and write $u=t^{-1/2}e^{-x^2/(4t)}$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
u_x=-\frac{x}{2t}u,\qquad u_{xx}=\left(\frac{x^2}{4t^2}-\frac1{2t}\right)u,\qquad u_t=\left(\frac{x^2}{4t^2}-\frac1{2t}\right)u.
$$
Thus $u_{xx}=u_t$: it is a constant multiple of the one-dimensional <Gaussian heat kernel>.

For the modified equation, put $f=g(t)u$. Substitution gives $g u_{xx}=g'u+gu_t-gu/t$. The heat-equation terms cancel, and $u>0$ permits division to obtain $\boxed{g'=g/t}$. Solving this <separable differential equation> gives
$$
\boxed{g(t)=Ct,\qquad f(x,t)=C\sqrt t\,e^{-x^2/(4t)},\quad t>0.}
$$
The zero solution is included by $C=0$. This is the <scalar integrating factor for a reaction-diffusion equation> with reaction coefficient $1/t$, because the modified equation is $f_t=f_{xx}+f/t$. Its sign produces growth. Indeed $\int_{\mathbb R}u\,dx=2\sqrt\pi$ is constant, while $\int_{\mathbb R}f\,dx=2C\sqrt\pi\,t$ increases linearly for $C>0$; neither profile is being assigned unit normalization here.
