= Solution
{parent=a}

Three <vectors> in three-dimensional <Euclidean space> are non-coplanar precisely when their <scalar triple product> is nonzero. Put $\Delta=a\cdot(b\times c)\ne0$. In particular, $a\ne0$. The identity for <cross products with a common vector> gives
$$
(a\times b)\times(a\times c)=\bigl[a\cdot(b\times c)\bigr]a=\Delta a.
$$
For completeness, this follows from the <vector triple product identity>: writing $p=a\times b$ gives $p\times(a\times c)=a(p\cdot c)-c(p\cdot a)$, with $p\cdot c=\Delta$ and $p\cdot a=0$. Therefore the new <scalar triple product> is
$$
\boxed{a\cdot\bigl[(a\times b)\times(a\times c)\bigr]=|a|^2\Delta\ne0.}
$$
Thus $a,a\times b,a\times c$ are non-coplanar, or equivalently <linearly independent>.

Take the <dot product> of the equation with $a$. Since $a\cdot(a\times r)=0$, we obtain $a\cdot r=(a\cdot c)/\lambda$. Next take its <cross product> with $a$ and apply the <vector triple product identity>:
$$
a(a\cdot r)-|a|^2r+\lambda(a\times r)=a\times c.
$$
Substitute $a\times r=c-\lambda r$ and the known <dot product>, then rearrange:
$$
\boxed{r=\frac{\lambda c-a\times c+\lambda^{-1}(a\cdot c)a}{\lambda^2+|a|^2}.}
$$
Here the <vectors> and $\lambda\ne0$ are real, so the denominator is positive. The formula also covers $a=0$, giving $r=c/\lambda$. To check existence as well as uniqueness, write its numerator as $N$. The <vector triple product identity> gives $a\times N=\lambda a\times c-a(a\cdot c)+|a|^2c$, so $a\times N+\lambda N=(\lambda^2+|a|^2)c$. Substitution verifies the original equation. This is an instance of the <inverse of a scalar plus a cross-product operator>.

Let $F(a)$ be the given <integral>. Its integrand extends continuously to $x=0$, with value $a$. The <Leibniz integral rule> includes both the moving upper endpoint and the <derivative> of the integrand. For $a\ne0$ it gives
$$
F'(a)=2a\frac{\sin(a\cdot a^2)}{a^2}+\int_0^{a^2}\cos(ax)\,dx=\frac{2\sin(a^3)}a+\frac{\sin(a^3)}a.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{F'(a)=\begin{cases}3\sin(a^3)/a,&a\ne0,\\0,&a=0.\end{cases}}
$$
The zero case follows directly from $F(a)=a^3+O(a^9)$ near zero: expand $\sin(ax)/x=a+O(a^3x^2)$ and integrate up to $a^2$. Equivalently, the <change of variables> $t=ax$ gives $F(a)=\int_0^{a^3}(\sin t)/t\,dt$, whose <derivative> is the same expression, interpreted continuously at zero. This substitution works for negative $a$ as an oriented <integral> too.

Use the <complex exponential> representation of <cosine>. For real $\alpha$ and $\beta>0$, exponential decay makes the <improper integral> absolutely convergent, and
$$
I(\alpha,\beta)=\operatorname{Re}\int_0^\infty e^{-(\beta-i\alpha)x}\,dx=\operatorname{Re}\frac1{\beta-i\alpha}=\boxed{\frac\beta{\beta^2+\alpha^2}}.
$$
For the second <integral>, use <differentiation under the integral sign> with respect to $\beta$. The minus sign is essential: $\partial_\beta e^{-\beta x}=-xe^{-\beta x}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty x\cos(\alpha x)e^{-\beta x}\,dx=-\frac{\partial I}{\partial\beta}=\frac{\beta^2-\alpha^2}{(\beta^2+\alpha^2)^2}.}
$$
To justify this step near any fixed $\beta_0>0$, restrict $\beta\geq\beta_0/2$. The absolute value of the differentiated integrand is at most $xe^{-\beta_0x/2}$, an integrable bound, so <dominated convergence> permits the differentiation. At $\alpha=0$ the answers reduce to $1/\beta$ and $1/\beta^2$, respectively.

The number $R$ of successes has a <binomial distribution>. Each arrangement of $r$ successes and $n-r$ failures has <probability> $p^rq^{n-r}$ by <independence>, and the <binomial coefficient> counts the arrangements. Therefore
$$
\boxed{p_r=\binom nr p^rq^{n-r},\quad 0\leq r\leq n;\qquad\sum_{r=0}^n p_r=(p+q)^n=1.}
$$
The final equality is the <binomial theorem>. Writing $R$ as a sum of <independent> <Bernoulli random variables> gives its <expectation> and <variance>: $\mathbb E R=np$, $\operatorname{Var}(R)=npq$. Thus the matching <normal distribution> has
$$
\boxed{\mu=np,\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{npq}.}
$$
The <normal approximation> is appropriate when both expected counts $np$ and $nq$ are sufficiently large; large $n$ alone is insufficient if $p$ is extremely close to zero or one.

For the guessing student, $n=60$, $p=1/4$, $q=3/4$. Therefore the expected number of correct answers is $\boxed{15}$, and $\sigma^2=45/4$, $\sigma=3\sqrt5/2$. The inclusive integer range $13,14,15,16,17$ becomes $12.5\leq X\leq17.5$ after the <continuity correction>. Standardizing the <normal distribution> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(13\leq R\leq17)&\approx\mathbb P(12.5\leq X\leq17.5)\\
&=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\sqrt5/3}^{\sqrt5/3}e^{-z^2/2}\,dz\\
&=\frac2{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_0^{\sqrt5/3}e^{-z^2/2}\,dz>2\cdot\frac14=\boxed{\frac12}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $(5/2)/(3\sqrt5/2)=\sqrt5/3$, and the last step uses the supplied bound. The approximate <probability> is $0.54394$. The <normal approximation> provides the requested conclusion; as a separate check, summing the five exact <binomial probabilities> gives approximately $0.54370$, also exceeding one half.

The required <matrix> form of the <linear system> is
$$
\boxed{A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&2\\1&0&\lambda\\1&\lambda&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf x=\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf b=\begin{pmatrix}1\\\mu\\2\end{pmatrix},\qquad A\mathbf x=\mathbf b.}
$$
Subtract the first row from the third and expand the <determinant> along the resulting row:
$$
\det A=-(\lambda-1)\det\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\1&\lambda\end{pmatrix}=(\lambda-1)(2-\lambda).
$$
Thus the <linear system> has a unique solution, for every real $\mu$, precisely when $\boxed{\lambda\ne1,2}$.

For $\lambda=0$, the second equation gives $x=\mu$. Subtracting the first equation from the third gives $-y=1$, and the first equation then determines $z$:
$$
\boxed{\lambda=0:\quad(x,y,z)=(\mu,-1,1-\mu/2),\qquad\mu\in\mathbb R.}
$$
For $\lambda=1$, the first and third left-hand sides are identical but their right-hand sides are $1$ and $2$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\lambda=1:\quad\text{no solution for any }\mu.}
$$
For $\lambda=2$, subtracting the first equation from the third gives $y=1$. The first equation now gives $x+2z=0$, while the second requires $x+2z=\mu$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\lambda=2:\quad\begin{cases}(x,y,z)=(-2t,1,t),\quad t\in\mathbb R,&\mu=0,\\\text{no solution},&\mu\ne0.\end{cases}}
$$
The <free variable in a linear system> in the consistent singular case describes a <line> of solutions. These direct reductions distinguish an inconsistent <linear system> from one with infinitely many solutions, which a zero <determinant> alone cannot do.

Compute the <curl> component by component from the given <vector field>:
$$
\boxed{B=\nabla\times A=(2xz,\ 2yz,\ 2(1-z^2)).}
$$
For example, $B_x=\partial_y A_z-\partial_z A_y=0-(-2xz)$ and $B_z=\partial_x A_y-\partial_y A_x=(1-z^2)-(z^2-1)$. Its <divergence> is
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot B=2z+2z-4z=0,}
$$
as expected because the <divergence of a curl is zero>.

Take $C$ counterclockwise when viewed from positive $z$, the <disc> $D$ upward oriented, and the <hemisphere> $H$ outward oriented. These choices give both <surfaces> the same positive boundary orientation. Parametrize $C$ by $r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t,0)$, $0\leq t\leq2\pi$. On it, $A=(-\sin t,\cos t,0)$ and $dr=(-\sin t,\cos t,0)\,dt$, so the <line integral> is
$$
\boxed{\oint_C A\cdot dr=\int_0^{2\pi}1\,dt=2\pi.}
$$
On $D$, use <polar coordinates> $(x,y)=(r\cos\phi,r\sin\phi)$ and $d\mathbf S=(0,0,1)r\,dr\,d\phi$. Since $B_z=2$ there, the <surface flux> is
$$
\boxed{\int_D B\cdot d\mathbf S=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 2r\,dr\,d\phi=2\pi.}
$$
On the unit <hemisphere>, the outward <unit normal vector> is $n=(x,y,z)$. Since $x^2+y^2=1-z^2$, we have $B\cdot n=4z(1-z^2)$. In <spherical polar coordinates>, $z=\cos\theta$ and $dS=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$, giving the explicit <surface integral>
$$
\boxed{\int_H B\cdot d\mathbf S=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi/2}4\cos\theta\sin^3\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi=2\pi.}
$$
Indeed the inner <integral> is $[\sin^4\theta]_0^{\pi/2}=1$.

These equalities illustrate <Stokes theorem> twice: $B=\nabla\times A$ and either upward $D$ or outward $H$ spans the oriented <circle> $C$, so each <surface flux> equals the same <line integral>. For the <divergence theorem>, close the upper half-ball by adjoining the downward oriented base, namely $-D$. Its outward <surface flux> is
$$
\int_{H\cup(-D)}B\cdot d\mathbf S=2\pi-2\pi=0=\int_V\nabla\cdot B\,dV.
$$
The minus sign on the base is required because its outward <normal vector> points down. Reversing a specified orientation reverses that integral's sign.

The <characteristic equation> of the associated homogeneous <linear ordinary differential equation> is $m^2+5m+6=(m+2)(m+3)=0$, so the <complementary function> is $C_1e^{-2x}+C_2e^{-3x}$. For a <particular solution>, try $y_p=ax+b$. Substitution gives $6ax+5a+6b=6x$, so $a=1$, $b=-5/6$. The unrestricted solution is therefore
$$
y=C_1e^{-2x}+C_2e^{-3x}+x-\frac56.

Its <derivative> at zero is $-2C_1-3C_2+1$. The single prescribed condition imposes $2C_1+3C_2=1$ and leaves one arbitrary constant. Thus the most general permitted solution is
$$
\boxed{y=Ce^{-2x}+\frac{1-2C}{3}e^{-3x}+x-\frac56,\qquad C\in\mathbb R.}
$$
There is no condition fixing $y(0)$, so the remaining arbitrary constant is essential.

Write $y(\theta)=Y(x)$ with $x=\cos\theta$. By the <chain rule>,
$$
\frac{dy}{d\theta}=-\sin\theta\,Y_x,\qquad\frac{d^2y}{d\theta^2}=-\cos\theta\,Y_x+\sin^2\theta\,Y_{xx}.
$$
The terms involving $Y_x$ cancel in the original <ordinary differential equation>, leaving
$$
\sin\theta\,y_{\theta\theta}-\cos\theta\,y_\theta+2y\sin^3\theta=\sin^3\theta\,(Y_{xx}+2Y)=0.
$$
On each interval where $\sin\theta\ne0$, the transformed <linear ordinary differential equation> is $Y_{xx}+2Y=0$. Its <characteristic equation> has roots $\pm i\sqrt2$, yielding
$$
\boxed{y(\theta)=C_1\cos(\sqrt2\cos\theta)+C_2\sin(\sqrt2\cos\theta).}
$$
Direct substitution shows these expressions also extend smoothly through $\theta=k\pi$ and satisfy the original equation there. The two constants describe the general solution on any interval where the coordinate substitution is invertible.

An <orthogonal matrix> satisfies $A^{-1}=A^T$, while a <symmetric matrix> satisfies $A^T=A$. Combining them gives $\boxed{A^{-1}=A}$. For the product with the <antisymmetric matrix> $B$, the rule for the <transpose> of a <matrix product> gives
$$
(AB)^T=B^TA^T=-BA.
$$
Thus $AB$ is an <antisymmetric matrix> exactly when $AB=BA$. The hypotheses do not force this <commutativity>. For a counterexample, take
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad AB=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Here $A$ is a <symmetric matrix> and an <orthogonal matrix>, and $B$ is an <antisymmetric matrix> and an <orthogonal matrix>, but $AB$ is a nonzero <symmetric matrix>, so it is not an <antisymmetric matrix>. On the other hand, the product of two <orthogonal matrices> is always an <orthogonal matrix>:
$$
(AB)^T(AB)=B^TA^TAB=B^TB=I.
$$
Therefore the answers are $\boxed{\text{(i) no; (ii) yes.}}$

Call the given <matrix> $M$. Its middle coordinate decouples, immediately giving the <eigenvalue> $3$ with normalized <eigenvector> $(0,1,0)^T$. The remaining <characteristic polynomial> comes from the $xz$ block:
$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}-1-\lambda&-3\sqrt3\\-3\sqrt3&5-\lambda\end{pmatrix}=(-1-\lambda)(5-\lambda)-27=\lambda^2-4\lambda-32=(\lambda+4)(\lambda-8).

For $\lambda=-4$, the first row of $(M+4I)v=0$ gives $x=\sqrt3z$; for $\lambda=8$, the first row of $(M-8I)v=0$ gives $z=-\sqrt3x$. After <vector normalization>, one possible complete set is

\lambda_3&=8,&v_3&=\frac12(-1,0,\sqrt3)^T.

Each has unit <Euclidean norm>. The <dot products> with $v_2$ vanish because the other two middle components are zero, and $v_1\cdot v_3=(-\sqrt3+\sqrt3)/4=0$. This explicitly verifies <orthonormality>, also predicted by the <spectral theorem> for real <symmetric matrices> with distinct <eigenvalues>. Changing any <eigenvector>'s sign gives an equally valid normalized choice.

The <Taylor expansion> about $a$ has coefficients given by the successive <derivatives> at $a$:
$$
f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^N\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k+R_N(x).
$$
When $f$ is $N+1$ times continuously differentiable on the interval between $a$ and $x$, <Taylor's theorem> gives $R_N(x)=f^{(N+1)}(\xi)(x-a)^{N+1}/(N+1)!$ for an intermediate point $\xi$. If the remainders tend to zero, this yields the infinite <Taylor series> $f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f^{(k)}(a)(x-a)^k/k!$. The three <functions> below are <real analytic> near zero after taking the continuous extension in part (i).

$$

Hence $A=1/2$, $B=1/12$, $C=0$. The first three nonzero terms of the <Taylor expansion> are

$$
Although the quotient is undefined at zero as written, its limit is one, and this <Taylor expansion> belongs to its continuous, <real analytic> extension.

The <comparison test for series> states that if $0\leq a_n\leq b_n$ for all sufficiently large $n$, then convergence of $\sum b_n$ implies convergence of $\sum a_n$; conversely, divergence of $\sum a_n$ implies divergence of $\sum b_n$. A summable bound on $|a_n|$ proves <absolute convergence> of $\sum a_n$.

For the <ratio test>, suppose the terms are eventually nonzero and $L=\lim_{n\to\infty}|a_{n+1}/a_n|$ exists. If $L<1$, the <series> converges absolutely. If $L>1$, including $L=\infty$, the terms fail to tend to zero and the <series> diverges. If $L=1$, the test is inconclusive. An eventually zero <series> converges separately. Convergence means that the sequence of partial sums has a finite limit; the terms tending to zero is only a necessary condition for a <convergent series>.

Differentiate $v_t=yu_x-xu_y$ with respect to $t$, remembering that its coefficients also depend on $t$. The <product rule> and <multivariable chain rule> give
$$
\begin{aligned}
v_{tt}&=y_tu_x+y(u_{xx}x_t+u_{xy}y_t)-x_tu_y-x(u_{yx}x_t+u_{yy}y_t)\\
&=-xu_x+y(yu_{xx}-xu_{xy})-yu_y-x(yu_{yx}-xu_{yy}).
\end{aligned}
$$
For a twice continuously differentiable $u$, equality of <mixed partial derivatives> gives $u_{yx}=u_{xy}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{v_{tt}=y^2u_{xx}-2xyu_{xy}+x^2u_{yy}-xu_x-yu_y.}
$$
The first-order terms come from differentiating $x$ and $y$ themselves; treating $y\partial_x-x\partial_y$ as an operator with constant coefficients would miss them.

The last two terms in the preceding result equal $v_s$. Therefore
$$
v_{tt}=y^2u_{xx}-2xyu_{xy}+x^2u_{yy}+v_s.
$$
The left-hand side of the original <partial differential equation> is consequently $v_{tt}-v_s$, giving
$$
\boxed{v_{tt}-v_s=0,\qquad\text{equivalently }v_s=v_{tt}.}
$$
In these variables it has the form of a <heat equation>, with logarithmic radial coordinate $s$ playing the evolution variable and angular coordinate $t$ playing the spatial variable. If $u$ is single-valued around the whole punctured <plane>, then $v(s,t+2\pi)=v(s,t)$ as well; that periodicity is a global coordinate condition, not an extra local transformation term.

= Solution

Near a nondegenerate <stationary point>, the quadratic part of the <Taylor expansion> determines the local <contour plot>. For a strict <local maximum>, the model $f=f_0-X^2-Y^2$ gives nested closed <level sets>, with values decreasing as one moves outward. For a strict <local minimum>, the model $f=f_0+X^2+Y^2$ gives the same nested shape with values increasing outward. Unequal nonzero quadratic coefficients stretch the <circles> into <ellipses> after a rotation to the <Hessian matrix>'s <eigenvectors>.

For a nondegenerate <saddle point of a scalar function>, the model $f=f_0+X^2-Y^2$ gives <hyperbolas>. Levels above $f_0$ open along the positive-curvature direction; levels below $f_0$ open along the negative-curvature direction. The critical <level set> is the two crossing lines $Y=\pm X$. Degenerate <stationary points> can have different local geometry, so these are the standard nondegenerate sketches.

= Solution

Set both first <partial derivatives> equal to zero:
$$
f_x=4x^3-2x=2x(2x^2-1)=0,\qquad f_y=-4y^3+8y=-4y(y^2-2)=0.
$$
Thus $x=0,\pm1/\sqrt2$ and $y=0,\pm\sqrt2$, independently, giving exactly nine <stationary points>. The <Hessian matrix> is diagonal everywhere:
$$
H_f=\begin{pmatrix}12x^2-2&0\\0&8-12y^2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its diagonal entries are its <eigenvalues>, so the <second-derivative test> immediately classifies the points:

* At $(0,\pm\sqrt2)$, the <Hessian matrix> has <eigenvalues> $-2,-16$. These two points are strict <local maxima>, each with $f=4$.
* At $(\pm1/\sqrt2,0)$, the <Hessian matrix> has <eigenvalues> $4,8$. These two points are strict <local minima>, each with $f=-1/4$.
* At $(0,0)$, the <Hessian matrix> has <eigenvalues> $-2,8$, so it is a <saddle point of a scalar function> with $f=0$.
* At $(\pm1/\sqrt2,\pm\sqrt2)$, with all four sign combinations, the <Hessian matrix> has <eigenvalues> $4,-16$. These four points are <saddle points of a scalar function>, each with $f=15/4$.

In particular,
$$
\boxed{\text{2 local maxima, 2 local minima, and 5 saddle points.}}
$$
All nine <stationary points> are nondegenerate, so no higher-order test is needed. Neither type of <local extremum> is global: $f(x,0)=x^4-x^2\to+\infty$ as $|x|\to\infty$, whereas $f(0,y)=-y^4+4y^2\to-\infty$ as $|y|\to\infty$.

To organize the <contour plot>, complete the squares:
$$
f(x,y)=\frac{15}{4}+\left(x^2-\frac12\right)^2-(y^2-2)^2.
$$
The <level set> through all four outer <saddle points of a scalar function> factors exactly:
$$
f-\frac{15}{4}=\left(x^2-y^2+\frac32\right)\left(x^2+y^2-\frac52\right).
$$
It is therefore the union of the <circle> $x^2+y^2=5/2$ and the <hyperbola> $y^2-x^2=3/2$, which cross at those four <saddle points of a scalar function>. Near the origin, $f=-x^2+4y^2+O((x^2+y^2)^2)$, so the zero <contours> have tangents $y=\pm x/2$. These two exact structural features, together with the nested <contours> around the four <local extrema>, determine the requested sketch. They are an example of a <separable quartic landscape with nine stationary points>.
