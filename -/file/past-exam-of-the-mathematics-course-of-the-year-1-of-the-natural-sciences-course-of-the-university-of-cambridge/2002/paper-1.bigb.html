= Solution
{parent=a}

Write $\mathbf r=\mu\mathbf n+\mathbf v$. Since $\mathbf n$ is a <unit vector>, the equation becomes $\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf n=0$. Thus $\mathbf v$ ranges over the two-dimensional <orthogonal complement> of $\mathbf n$: the locus is an <affine hyperplane> through $\mu\mathbf n$, with <unit normal> $\mathbf n$. Conversely, every such vector satisfies the equation. Its perpendicular distance from the origin is $\boxed{|\mu|}$; $\mu$ is the signed distance in the chosen normal direction.

= Solution
{parent=i}

The <normal vector> must be parallel to $\mathbf b$, and the plane must contain $\mathbf a$. Therefore one choice is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf n=\frac{\mathbf b}{|\mathbf b|},\qquad \mu=\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{|\mathbf b|}.}
$$
The nonzero <scalar triple product> ensures $\mathbf b\ne0$. Replacing both $\mathbf n$ and $\mu$ by their negatives describes the same <affine hyperplane>.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Two directions in the plane are $\mathbf b-\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf c-\mathbf a$. Their <cross product> $\mathbf N=(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)\times(\mathbf c-\mathbf a)$ is a <normal vector>. Moreover,
$$
\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf N=\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)=D\ne0,
$$
so $\mathbf N$ cannot vanish. The required <unit normal> and offset are
$$
\boxed{\mathbf n=\frac{(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)\times(\mathbf c-\mathbf a)}{|(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)\times(\mathbf c-\mathbf a)|},\qquad \mu=\frac D{|(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)\times(\mathbf c-\mathbf a)|}.}
$$
Taking <dot products> with $\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c$ gives the same offset for all three points.

= iii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

This plane contains the direction $\mathbf b-\mathbf a$ and is parallel to $\mathbf c$. Put $\mathbf N=(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)\times\mathbf c$. Its <scalar triple product> with $\mathbf a$ is again $D\ne0$, proving $\mathbf N\ne0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbf n=\frac{(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)\times\mathbf c}{|(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)\times\mathbf c|},\qquad \mu=\frac D{|(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)\times\mathbf c|}.}
$$
The <dot product> with $\mathbf c$ vanishes, while the <dot products> with $\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf b$ equal $\mu$ after normalization. These verify both incidence and parallelism.

{parent=Paper 1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2}

The periodically extended $x^2$ is <even>, so every sine coefficient is zero. With the convention $f=a_0/2+\sum_{n\geq1}(a_n\cos nx+b_n\sin nx)$, the <Fourier coefficients> are
$$
a_0=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi x^2dx=\frac{2\pi^2}3,\qquad a_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi x^2\cos nx\,dx=\frac{4(-1)^n}{n^2}.
$$
For the second integral, two applications of <integration by parts> give $\int_0^\pi x^2\cos nx\,dx=2\pi(-1)^n/n^2$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{x^2=\frac{\pi^2}3+4\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n\cos nx}{n^2},\qquad -\pi\leq x\leq\pi.}
$$
The periodic extension is continuous even at the joined endpoints, where both one-sided values equal $\pi^2$. The <pointwise convergence of a piecewise smooth Fourier series> therefore gives the displayed values throughout the interval; the coefficient bound also gives <uniform convergence> by the <Weierstrass M-test>. Setting $x=\pi$ and then $x=0$ yields
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}6,\qquad \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{12}.}
$$

= Solution

At an interior <local extremum> of a <differentiable function>, $f'(x)=0$. The <second-derivative test> classifies a <stationary point>: a positive <second derivative> gives a strict <local minimum>, and a negative <second derivative> gives a strict <local maximum>. A zero <second derivative> is inconclusive: $x^4$, $-x^4$, and $x^3$ at zero illustrate a minimum, a maximum, and neither.

Here
$$
f'(x)=3x^2-10x+3=(3x-1)(x-3),\qquad f''(x)=6x-10.
$$
Thus $f''(1/3)=-8$ and $f''(3)=8$. Substitution into $f$ gives
$$
\boxed{(1/3,-14/27)\text{ is a local maximum},\qquad (3,-10)\text{ is a local minimum}.}
$$

Use <implicit differentiation> on $F(x,y)=xy^3-yx^3-6=0$. Along the curve,
$$
y'=-\frac{F_x}{F_y}=-\frac{y^3-3yx^2}{3xy^2-x^3}.
$$
At $(1,2)$, $F_x=2$ and $F_y=11\ne0$, so the slope is $-2/11$. The <tangent line> is
$$
\boxed{y-2=-\frac2{11}(x-1),\quad\text{equivalently }2x+11y=24.}
$$

For $F(x,y)=e^{xy}+y\ln x-x^2$, the real domain has $x>0$. Its <partial derivatives> are $F_x=ye^{xy}+y/x-2x$ and $F_y=xe^{xy}+\ln x$. At $(1,0)$ they equal $-2$ and $1$, respectively. Thus <implicit differentiation> gives $y'=2$, and the <tangent line> is

= 4
{parent=Paper 1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=4}

For two independent smooth constraints $g=h=0$, the tangent direction is orthogonal to both <gradients>. At a constrained <stationary point>, the <gradient> of the objective is also orthogonal to that direction, hence lies in their span: $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g+\mu\nabla h$. This is the two-constraint <Lagrange multiplier> condition. Alternatively, <distance extrema on a central plane section of an ellipsoid> follow from a two-dimensional <eigenvalue> problem.

Minimizing distance is equivalent to minimizing $f=x^2+y^2+z^2$. Put $g=x-y+z$ and $h=x^2+(y^2+z^2)/4-1$. The <Lagrange multiplier> equations are
$$
2(1-\mu)x=\lambda,\qquad (2-\mu/2)y=-\lambda,\qquad (2-\mu/2)z=\lambda.
$$
Adding the latter two gives $(2-\mu/2)(y+z)=0$. If $\mu=4$, those equations give $\lambda=x=0$; the constraints then give $y=z=\pm\sqrt2$ and $f=4$. Otherwise $z=-y$ and $x=2y$. The ellipsoidal constraint gives $y=\pm\sqrt2/3$, and hence
$$
(x,y,z)=\pm\frac{\sqrt2}3(2,1,-1),\qquad f=\frac43.
$$
The intersection is <compact>. The two constraint <gradients> are independent there: if $\nabla h=t\nabla g$, then $(x,y,z)=(t/2,-2t,2t)$, and $g=0$ forces $t=0$, contradicting $h=0$. Thus every global extremum is among these candidates. Taking square roots gives
$$
\boxed{d_{\min}=\frac2{\sqrt3},\qquad d_{\max}=2.}
$$

= Solution

Let $I=\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}dx$. Positivity allows the squared integral to be written as a first-quadrant <double integral>. In <polar coordinates> the <Jacobian determinant> is $r$, so
$$
I^2=\int_0^{\pi/2}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}r\,dr\,d\theta=\frac\pi4,\qquad \boxed{I=\frac{\sqrt\pi}2.}
$$
Using <integration by parts>, with the boundary term $xe^{-x^2}$ vanishing at both endpoints,
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty x^2e^{-x^2}dx=\frac12\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}dx=\frac{\sqrt\pi}4.}
$$
The substitution $u=x^2$ also gives the two <antiderivatives>
$$
\boxed{\int xe^{-x^2}dx=-\frac12e^{-x^2}+C,\qquad \int x^3e^{-x^2}dx=-\frac12(x^2+1)e^{-x^2}+C.}
$$
These values also follow from the <Gaussian moment recurrence> and can be checked by <differentiation>.

The upper boundary follows $y=x$ until its intersection with $y=1/x$ at $(1,1)$, then follows the <hyperbola> to $(2,1/2)$. The lower boundary is $y=0$. Therefore the two vertical-slice descriptions are $0\leq x\leq1$, $0\leq y\leq x$, and $1\leq x\leq2$, $0\leq y\leq1/x$.

Using the <antiderivatives> from part (a),

\boxed{I=\frac{1-e^{-1}-e^{-4}}2.}

{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For $\mathbf u=(1,1)^T$ and $\mathbf v=(1,-1)^T$, the <outer products> are
$$
\mathbf u\mathbf u^T=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathbf v\mathbf v^T=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Their <inner product> is zero and both squared <norms> are two. Thus $P_u=\mathbf u\mathbf u^T/2$ and $P_v=\mathbf v\mathbf v^T/2$ are orthogonal <spectral projectors>, with $P_u+P_v=I$. Since $A\mathbf u=5\mathbf u$ and $A\mathbf v=\mathbf v$, the <eigenvalues> are $\lambda=5$, $\mu=1$, and
$$
\boxed{A=\frac52\mathbf u\mathbf u^T+\frac12\mathbf v\mathbf v^T,\qquad A^{-1}=\frac1{10}\mathbf u\mathbf u^T+\frac12\mathbf v\mathbf v^T.}
$$
Multiplying these decompositions uses $P_uP_v=0$ and $P_u^2=P_u$, $P_v^2=P_v$, giving the <identity matrix>.

On an interval avoiding $x=\pm1$, <separation of variables> gives, for $y\ne-1$,
$$
\ln|y+1|=\int\frac{dx}{x^2-1}=\frac12\ln\left|\frac{x-1}{x+1}\right|+C_0.
$$
Absorb the sign into the integration constant and include the <equilibrium solution> $y=-1$ by allowing $C=0$. The complete real family is
$$
\boxed{y=-1+C\sqrt{\left|\frac{x-1}{x+1}\right|},\qquad C\in\mathbb R.}
$$
Each solution is considered on a connected interval in $\mathbb R\setminus\{-1,1\}$, where the original equation is defined.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Divide by $\cos x$ on an interval where it is nonzero. For the resulting <first-order linear differential equation>, use the <integrating factor> $M=\sec x+\tan x$, since $M'=\sec x\,M$. The right-hand side after division is also $M'$, so
$$
(My)'=MM',\qquad My=\frac12M^2+C.
$$
The identity $(\sec x+\tan x)(\sec x-\tan x)=1$ shows $M$ never vanishes on the interval, giving
$$
\boxed{y=\frac12(\sec x+\tan x)+C(\sec x-\tan x).}
$$
An <integrating factor> may have either fixed sign; if obtained by integrating $\sec x$, the real <logarithm> is $\ln|\sec x+\tan x|$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <Bernoulli differential equation> becomes linear after $z=1/y$: multiplying by $-y^{-2}$ gives $z'-2z=-x^2$. A quadratic <particular solution> is $z_p=x^2/2+x/2+1/4$, while the <homogeneous solution> is $Ce^{2x}$. The <initial condition> gives $z(0)=1$ and $C=3/4$. Hence
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac4{3e^{2x}+2x^2+2x+1}.}
$$
The denominator is positive for all real $x$, since $2x^2+2x+1=2(x+1/2)^2+1/2$. Thus this <initial value problem> has the displayed solution on the entire real line. The separate solution $y=0$ does not satisfy its <initial condition>.

= Solution

Let $N=\mathbf a\mathbf b^T$. The orthogonality condition gives $N^2=\mathbf a(\mathbf b^T\mathbf a)\mathbf b^T=0$: this is the nilpotent part of a <transvection> when both vectors are nonzero. In two dimensions,
$$
\operatorname{tr}L=2+a_1b_1+a_2b_2=\boxed2.
$$
Expanding its <determinant>, the fourth-degree terms cancel:
$$
\det L=(1+a_1b_1)(1+a_2b_2)-a_1b_2a_2b_1=1+\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=\boxed1.
$$
These formulas also cover either vector being zero.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Since $M^T=I-\mathbf a\mathbf b^T=I-N$, nilpotence already gives $(I+N)(I-N)=I$. Explicitly, in <index notation>,
$$
(LM^T)_{ij}=\sum_k(\delta_{ik}+a_ib_k)(\delta_{jk}-b_ja_k)=\delta_{ij}-b_ja_i+a_ib_j-a_ib_j\sum_ka_kb_k=\delta_{ij}.
$$
Thus $\boxed{LM^T=I}$, or equivalently $M^T=L^{-1}$. Here $\delta_{ij}$ is the <Kronecker delta>.

{parent=Paper 1}

= Solution
{parent=9}

In Cartesian components, $\mathbf F=(z-y,x,-x)$, so
$$
\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf F=(0,2,2)\ne0.}
$$
It cannot be a <conservative vector field>. For the second field, put $\Phi(\mathbf r)=-(1+|\mathbf r|^2)^{-1/2}$. Its <gradient> is $\nabla\Phi=\mathbf r/(1+|\mathbf r|^2)^{3/2}=\mathbf G$. This smooth global scalar function proves $\mathbf G$ is <conservative>, with $\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf G=0}$. The following path integrals exhibit the different behavior directly.

= i
{parent=9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}

Parametrize the segment by $\mathbf r(t)=(1-t,t,0)$, $0\leq t\leq1$. Its tangent is $(-1,1,0)$ and $\mathbf F=(-t,1-t,t-1)$, so $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf r'=1$. Hence its <line integral> is
$$
\boxed{\int\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=1.}
$$
For the other <line integral>, $|\mathbf r|^2=(1-t)^2+t^2$ and $\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf r'=2t-1$. Thus
$$
\int\mathbf G\cdot d\mathbf r=\int_0^1\frac{2t-1}{[1+(1-t)^2+t^2]^{3/2}}dt=\left[-\frac1{\sqrt{1+(1-t)^2+t^2}}\right]_0^1=\boxed0.
$$
This also equals the change in $\Phi$, by the <fundamental theorem for line integrals>.

= ii
{parent=9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

On each of the two radial legs, $\mathbf F=\mathbf a\times\mathbf r$ is perpendicular to the tangent. Each contribution is zero, giving $\boxed{\int\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=0}$. This differs from the straight-path answer, confirming path dependence.

For $\mathbf G$, use $\mathbf r=(s,0,0)$ with $s$ decreasing from one to zero, followed by $\mathbf r=(0,s,0)$ with $s$ increasing from zero to one. The two contributions are
$$
\int_1^0\frac{s\,ds}{(1+s^2)^{3/2}}=-1+\frac1{\sqrt2},\qquad \int_0^1\frac{s\,ds}{(1+s^2)^{3/2}}=1-\frac1{\sqrt2}.
$$
They cancel: $\boxed{\int\mathbf G\cdot d\mathbf r=0}$. This agreement is a consequence of the <conservative vector field> property already proved, rather than evidence sufficient by itself to establish it.

= Solution
{parent=10}

Put $\tau=t+b$ and $q=x+a$. For $\theta=f(t)e^{-q^2/(4\tau)}$, <partial differentiation> gives
$$
\theta_t=\left(f'+\frac{q^2f}{4\tau^2}\right)e^{-q^2/(4\tau)},\qquad \theta_{xx}=\left(-\frac f{2\tau}+\frac{q^2f}{4\tau^2}\right)e^{-q^2/(4\tau)}.
$$
The <heat equation> therefore requires $f'=-f/(2\tau)$, giving $f=C/\sqrt{|t+b|}$ on an interval not containing $-b$. A decaying spatial <Gaussian function> uses $t+b>0$, so $f=C/\sqrt{t+b}$ there.

The initial profile is a difference of two translated <Gaussian functions>. Set $b=1/4$, $a=-2$ and $a=2$, with respective constants $C=1/2$ and $C=-1/2$. The <superposition principle> gives
$$
\boxed{\theta(x,t)=\frac{e^{-(x-2)^2/(1+4t)}-e^{-(x+2)^2/(1+4t)}}{\sqrt{1+4t}},\qquad t\geq0.}
$$
This is an <odd pair of Gaussian profiles under diffusion>, obtained from <Gaussian initial data for the heat equation>. It is <odd> in $x$, vanishes at zero, is positive for $x>0$ and negative for $x<0$. The two lobes broaden and decrease in height, and $\theta(x,t)\to0$ at every fixed $x$ as $t\to\infty$. Their extrema move; they do not remain exactly at $\pm2$.

<Complex conjugation> reverses the <argument>, reciprocation also inverts the <modulus>, and multiplication by $-1$ adds $\pi$ to the <argument>. With all arguments chosen in $[0,2\pi)$ and $0<\phi<\pi/2$,
$$
\boxed{z^*=2e^{i(2\pi-\phi)},\qquad z^{-1}=\frac12e^{i(2\pi-\phi)},\qquad -z=2e^{i(\pi+\phi)}.}
$$
Thus $z$ is in the first quadrant, $z^*$ and $z^{-1}$ lie on the same fourth-quadrant ray at radii two and one-half, and $-z$ is diametrically opposite $z$.

<Euler's formula> and its version with $-\theta$ give
$$
\cos\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}2,\qquad \sin\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}{2i}.

Squaring $e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ and comparing real and imaginary parts with $e^{2i\theta}$ yields the <double-angle formulas>
$$
\boxed{\cos2\theta=\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta=2\cos^2\theta-1=1-2\sin^2\theta,\qquad \sin2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta.}
$$

{parent=11}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Factor $e^{i\phi/2}$ from the numerator and denominator:
$$
w=\frac{e^{i\phi}-1}{e^{i\phi}+1}=\frac{2ie^{i\phi/2}\sin(\phi/2)}{2e^{i\phi/2}\cos(\phi/2)}=i\tan\frac\phi2.
$$
For $0<\phi<\pi$, the tangent ranges over all positive real values. Thus $\boxed{\operatorname{Re}w=0,\quad\operatorname{Im}w>0}$. Using the principal <complex logarithm>,
$$
\boxed{\log w=\ln\tan(\phi/2)+\frac{i\pi}2.}
$$
The real part ranges over the entire real line, while the imaginary part is fixed: the locus is the horizontal line $\operatorname{Im}\log w=\pi/2$. Any other fixed <logarithm branch> gives the parallel line $\operatorname{Im}\log w=\pi/2+2\pi k$ for one integer $k$; allowing every branch gives the union of those lines.

= Solution
{parent=12}

For $k>0$, the two ordinary differential equations have solutions
$$
\boxed{h(x)=A\cosh kx+B\sinh kx,\qquad g(x)=C\cos kx+D\sin kx.}
$$
Their products $h(x)g(y)$ and $g(x)h(y)$ solve <Laplace's equation>, since the two second derivatives contribute opposite $k^2$ terms. For $k=0$, both factors are linear functions. This is <separation of variables> with positive, negative and zero separation constants.

= i
{parent=12}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}

The zero value at $x=0$ removes the even factor in $x$, and the zero value at $y=0$ removes the even factor in $y$. Up to multiplicative constants, the nonzero separated modes are
$$
\boxed{\sinh(kx)\sin(ky),\qquad \sin(kx)\sinh(ky),\quad k>0.}
$$
The zero-separation case also supplies $\boxed{xy}$. Each is a <harmonic function> and vanishes on both axes.

= ii
{parent=12}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For the first family, vanishing at $y=\pi$ requires $\sin(k\pi)=0$, so $k=n$ is a positive integer. The second family would require $\sinh(k\pi)=0$, impossible for real $k>0$; the mode $xy$ also fails unless its coefficient is zero. Conveniently normalized surviving modes are
$$
\boxed{f_n(x,y)=\frac{\sinh(nx)}{\sinh(n\pi)}\sin(ny),\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
At $x=\pi$, these become the <Fourier sine series> basis functions $\sin(ny)$.

= iii
{parent=12}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Expand the constant right-edge value in a <Fourier sine series>. Its coefficients are
$$
b_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi\sin(ny)dy=\frac{2[1-(-1)^n]}{n\pi}.
$$
The <superposition principle> combines the normalized modes, giving
$$
\boxed{f(x,y)=\frac4\pi\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{\sinh[(2m+1)x]}{(2m+1)\sinh[(2m+1)\pi]}\sin[(2m+1)y].}
$$
This is a <harmonic function in a rectangle with one constant boundary value>, with the coordinate roles interchanged. The series and its derivatives converge uniformly on compact subsets of $0<x<\pi$, so it satisfies <Laplace's equation> there. It vanishes on the other three edges and approaches one at every interior point of the right edge. At the two right corners the one-sided prescribed values disagree; no continuous extension can satisfy both. The stated open-edge condition avoids that incompatibility, and isolated corner assignments do not change the bounded interior <Dirichlet problem>.
