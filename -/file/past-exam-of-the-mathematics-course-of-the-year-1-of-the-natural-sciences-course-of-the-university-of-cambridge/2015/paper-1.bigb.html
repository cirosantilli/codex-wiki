Group the <polynomial> into two pairs, then use the <difference of two squares>:
$$
x^3-x^2-x+1=x^2(x-1)-(x-1)
=(x-1)(x^2-1)=\boxed{(x-1)^2(x+1).}
$$
Thus $1$ is a double <polynomial root> and $-1$ is a simple <polynomial root>.

The repeated factor requires both a simple-pole and a double-pole term in the <partial fraction decomposition>. Write
$$
\frac1{(x-1)^2(x+1)}=\frac A{x+1}+\frac B{x-1}+\frac C{(x-1)^2}.
$$
Multiplying through gives $1=A(x-1)^2+B(x^2-1)+C(x+1)$. Substituting $x=-1$ and $x=1$ gives $A=1/4$ and $C=1/2$; the quadratic coefficient then gives $B=-1/4$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac1{x^3-x^2-x+1}
=\frac1{4(x+1)}-\frac1{4(x-1)}+\frac1{2(x-1)^2},\qquad x\ne\pm1.}
$$

For the <indefinite integral>, substitute $u=x^2$, so $x^3dx=u\,du/2$. An <integration by parts> then gives
$$
\int x^3\cos(x^2)dx=\frac12\int u\cos u\,du
=\boxed{\frac12\big[x^2\sin(x^2)+\cos(x^2)\big]+C.}
$$
For the <definite integral>, the original PDF's upper limit is $\sqrt{\pi/2}$, so the corresponding upper value of $u$ is $\pi/2$. Therefore
$$
\int_0^{\sqrt{\pi/2}}x^3\cos(x^2)dx
=\frac12\left[\frac\pi2\sin\frac\pi2+\cos\frac\pi2-1\right]
=\boxed{\frac{\pi-2}{4}.}
$$
Both requested integrals are thus evaluated with the same <antiderivative>.

= b
{parent=7}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The usual interior condition for the <binomial series> is $|3x|<1$, giving **$-1/3<x<1/3$** as its open interval of convergence. In this particular square-root case the full series also converges absolutely at both endpoints, so the maximal real range on which its sum equals the original <function> is
$$
\boxed{-\frac13\leq x\leq\frac13.}
$$
For an endpoint check, write $\sqrt{1-u}=1-\sum_{n\geq1}b_nu^n$, where $b_n=a_{n-1}-a_n>0$ and $a_n=\binom{2n}{n}/4^n\to0$. Then $\sum b_n=1$ by telescoping, establishing <absolute convergence> at $u=\pm1$. The <square-root binomial series at its endpoints> proves <continuity> of the sum there. The endpoint values are $\sqrt2$ at $x=-1/3$ and $0$ at $x=1/3$. Outside this range the power-series terms do not tend to zero. A three-term truncation remains an approximation, rather than an exact formula throughout the interval.

Differentiate the <polar form of a complex number> using the <product rule> and <chain rule>:
$$
\boxed{\dot z=(\dot r+ir\dot\theta)e^{i\theta}.}
$$
A second <differentiation> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\ddot z
&=\bigl[\ddot r+i(\dot r\dot\theta+r\ddot\theta)
+i\dot\theta(\dot r+ir\dot\theta)\bigr]e^{i\theta}\\
&=\boxed{\bigl[(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2)
+i(2\dot r\dot\theta+r\ddot\theta)\bigr]e^{i\theta}.}
\end{aligned}
$$
The two contributions to $2\dot r\dot\theta$ arise separately from differentiating $r\dot\theta$ and from differentiating the moving exponential factor.

On this <circle> the radial coordinate is constant, $r=2$, so **$\dot r=\ddot r=0$**. The <velocity> is a <tangent vector> to the <circle> in the anticlockwise direction, represented by $ie^{i\theta}$ when $\dot\theta>0$. Substituting into part (b),

The inward component is the <centripetal acceleration>; the transverse component allows the <speed> to change. Anticlockwise motion does **not** imply uniform <speed> or $\ddot\theta=0$. The preceding figure includes the required tangent arrow.

= Solution

The perpendicular <unit vectors> represented by $e^{i\theta}$ and $ie^{i\theta}$ point radially outwards and transversely in the increasing-angle direction. Reading off their real coefficients in part (b) gives the signed components
$$
\boxed{v_r=\dot r,\qquad v_\theta=r\dot\theta,\qquad
a_r=\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2,\qquad
a_\theta=2\dot r\dot\theta+r\ddot\theta.}
$$
These are <velocity in polar coordinates> and <acceleration in polar coordinates>. In particular $r\dot\theta$ is a transverse <speed> component; $\dot\theta$ alone is an <angular velocity>. The moving <basis>, rather than any extra force assumption, accounts for the additional <acceleration> terms. At the origin the polar directions themselves are undefined, although the Cartesian <velocity> and <acceleration> can remain well defined.

For a smooth <exact differential form> $Mdx+Ndy$, its coefficients must satisfy $M_y=N_x$ by <Clairaut's theorem>. Here

= Solution

The <partial derivatives> are $g_x=\sin x$ and $g_y=y$, so all <stationary points> are $(n\pi,0)$ for integers $n$. Their <Hessian matrix> is $\operatorname{diag}(\cos x,1)$. At even multiples of $\pi$ it is a <positive-definite matrix>, whereas at odd multiples it has one positive and one negative <eigenvalue>. Consequently
$$
\boxed{(2n\pi,0):\text{ strict minima, }g=0;\qquad
((2n+1)\pi,0):\text{ saddle points, }g=2.}
$$
There are no <local maxima>. Since both $1-\cos x$ and $y^2/2$ are nonnegative, these are also <global minima>. In the requested open plotting window, the <stationary points> are $(0,0)$ and $(\pm\pi,0)$; the <local minima> at $(\pm2\pi,0)$ lie on excluded edges.

For a <level set> $g=c$, solving for the vertical coordinate gives
$$
y=\pm\sqrt{2(c-1+\cos x)},\qquad c\geq1-\cos x.
$$
For $0<c<2$ these <level sets> are closed ovals around each <local minimum>. At $c=0$ the <level sets> shrink to the <local minima>. The <level set> through the <saddle points> is
$$
g=2:\qquad y=\pm2\cos(x/2),
$$
where the two signed branches together describe the same set as $y=\pm2|\cos(x/2)|$. They cross at $(\pm\pi,0)$ and reach $(0,\pm2)$. The crossing slopes at a <saddle point> are $\pm1$, as also follows from the local <quadratic form> $(-\delta x^2+\delta y^2)/2$. For $c>2$, the upper and lower <level sets> remain separate and extend across the plotting window without meeting the horizontal axis.

Set $T=t+a>0$. The <similarity solution> uses $y=(x+b)T^{-1/2}$, whose <partial derivatives> at fixed $x$ or $t$ are
$$
y_x=T^{-1/2},\qquad y_t=-\frac y{2T}.
$$
Apply the <multivariable chain rule> to $u=T^{-1/2}v(y)$:
$$
u_t=-\frac12T^{-3/2}v+T^{-1/2}v'\left(-\frac y{2T}\right)
=-\frac12T^{-3/2}(v+yv'),
$$
$$
u_x=T^{-1}v',\qquad u_{xx}=T^{-3/2}v''.
$$
Substituting in the <heat equation> and cancelling the positive common factor $T^{-3/2}$ gives
$$
\boxed{-\frac12(v+yv')=\lambda v''.}
$$

For a <Gaussian function>, the required <derivatives> are
$$
v'=-2cyv,\qquad v''=(-2c+4c^2y^2)v.
$$
Substitute these into the reduced <ordinary differential equation> and compare the constant and quadratic coefficients:
$$
-\frac12+cy^2=\lambda(-2c+4c^2y^2).
$$
The constant terms give $c=1/(4\lambda)$, and this value also satisfies the quadratic terms. Hence
$$
\boxed{c=\frac1{4\lambda},\qquad
u(x,t)=\frac1{\sqrt{t+a}}
\exp\left[-\frac{(x+b)^2}{4\lambda(t+a)}\right],\qquad t>-a.}
$$
A constant multiple is also a solution by <linearity>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The <heat equation> is linear and homogeneous in $u$: the operator $L=\partial_t-\lambda\partial_x^2$ satisfies $L(Au_1+Bu_2)=ALu_1+BLu_2$. Thus the <superposition principle> applies to its solutions. Spatial translations are also allowed because the coefficients are constant.

Choose $a=1/(4\lambda)$ in part (b) and multiply by $\sqrt a$ to make the initial amplitude one. Taking the two shifts $b=1$ and $b=-1$ then gives
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=\frac1{\sqrt{1+4\lambda t}}
\left\{\exp\left[-\frac{(x+1)^2}{1+4\lambda t}\right]
+\exp\left[-\frac{(x-1)^2}{1+4\lambda t}\right]\right\}.}
$$
At $t=0$ this reproduces both initial peaks. Each term is an instance of <Gaussian initial data for the heat equation>, so their sum satisfies the equation. Its extension to negative times is a real smooth solution precisely on the connected interval
$$
\boxed{-\frac1{4\lambda}<t<0.}
$$
At the lower endpoint the peaks narrow without bound and their heights diverge; there is no finite classical continuation there. For positive times each peak spreads and decreases in height while preserving its integral.

The <cycloid> has limiting endpoints $(0,0)$ and $(2\pi a,0)$, with its highest point $(\pi a,2a)$ at $t=\pi$. For $n\geq3$, <Taylor's theorem> after $n$ terms, counting the constant term, is
$$
f(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)+\frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^2
+\cdots+\frac{f^{(n-1)}(a)}{(n-1)!}(x-a)^{n-1}+R_n(x).
$$
Under the usual hypothesis that $f$ is $n-1$ times continuously differentiable on the interval joining $a$ and $x$, and $n$ times differentiable in its interior, the <Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder> gives
$$
\boxed{R_n(x)=\frac{f^{(n)}(\xi)}{n!}(x-a)^n,
\qquad \xi\text{ strictly between }a\text{ and }x.}
$$
This is a finite expansion with a controlled remainder; it does not assume an infinite <Taylor series> converges. For $x=a$ the remainder is zero.

For a sufficiently smooth <vector field> on a volume $V$ and its closed boundary $S$, the <divergence theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\int_S\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf n\,dS
=\int_V\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\mathbf v\,dV,}
$$
with the <unit normal> directed outwards. Here
$$
\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\mathbf v
=\partial_x(-\alpha yz)+\partial_y(\alpha xz)+\partial_z0=0.
$$
Hence **the net volume <flux> through every <closed surface> in the liquid is zero**. This verifies <incompressible flow> directly. It does not require the <velocity> to vanish pointwise on an arbitrary <closed surface>; inward and outward contributions can cancel.

For an oriented surface $S'$ with compatible boundary direction, the <Stokes theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\oint_{\partial S'}\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf r
=\int_{S'}(\boldsymbol\nabla\times\mathbf v)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS.}
$$
Let $H$ denote the removed patch and $S'=S\setminus H$ the remaining cylinder. Keep the outward orientation inherited from the closed cylinder. Everywhere on the removed curved patch, the normal <curl> component is $-\alpha R$, so its <flux> is $-\alpha RA$, where $A$ is its actual curved area. Part (d) then gives
$$
\int_{S'}(\boldsymbol\nabla\times\mathbf v)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS
=0-(-\alpha RA)=\alpha RA.
$$
Consequently the <circulation around a hole in an oriented surface> is
$$
\boxed{\oint_{\partial S'}\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf r=\alpha RA.}
$$
The positive direction here is induced by the **remaining** outward-oriented surface; locally it runs clockwise around the hole when viewed from outside. If the edge is instead traversed positively around the **removed** patch, the direction reverses and the answer is **$-\alpha RA$**. The question does not specify an edge direction, so the sign must be accompanied by this convention. The result depends only on the removed curved area, not on its shape. If the patch has several boundary components, use the sum of their induced <line integrals>. Since $[\alpha]=1/(\text{length}\cdot\text{time})$, the result has the units of a <velocity> <line integral>, length squared per time.
