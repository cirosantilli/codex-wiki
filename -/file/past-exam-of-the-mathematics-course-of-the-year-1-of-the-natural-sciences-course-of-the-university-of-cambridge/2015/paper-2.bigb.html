The <Taylor series> of the logarithm gives
$$
\log(1+x^2)=x^2-\frac{x^4}{2}+O(x^6).
$$
The first nonzero term is therefore $\boxed{x^2}$. It is positive on either side of the origin, so the <stationary point> is a **<strict local minimum>**. In fact $\log(1+x^2)\geq0$, with equality only at zero, making it the <global minimum> as well.

Set $s=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf r$. Taking the <dot product> of the vector equation with $\mathbf a$ gives
$$
(1+\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)s=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c.
$$
Here $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=5$ and $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c=6$, so $s=1$. Substitution then gives

Geometrically the possible vector must lie on the line $\mathbf c-s\mathbf b$, and the scalar <constraint> picks one point. Equivalently the <rank-one matrix update> $I+\mathbf b\mathbf a^T$ is invertible because $1+\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=6\ne0$. This is the nonsingular case of <solvability of an affine rank-one vector equation>.

Orient the common axis of the wedge faces along $z$ and its azimuthal bisector along positive $x$. Reflection symmetries give $\bar y=\bar z=0$. In <spherical coordinates>, use $0\leq r\leq a$, $0\leq\theta\leq\pi$, and $-\beta/2\leq\varphi\leq\beta/2$. The <volume> is
$$
V=\int_0^a r^2\,dr\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta\int_{-\beta/2}^{\beta/2}d\varphi
=\frac{2\beta a^3}{3}.
$$
Since $x=r\sin\theta\cos\varphi$, the first moment is
$$
\int_Vx\,dV=\left(\frac{a^4}{4}\right)
\left(\int_0^\pi\sin^2\theta\,d\theta\right)
\left(\int_{-\beta/2}^{\beta/2}\cos\varphi\,d\varphi\right)
=\frac{\pi a^4}{4}\sin\frac\beta2.
$$
Dividing by the <volume> locates the <centre of mass>:
$$
\boxed{\bar{\mathbf x}=\frac{3\pi a}{8\beta}\sin\frac\beta2\,\mathbf e_x},\qquad
\boxed{f(\beta)=\frac{3\pi}{8\beta}\sin\frac\beta2}.
$$
This is the centroid formula for a <spherical wedge>.

For the sketch, $f$ is positive and decreases from $\lim_{\beta\to0^+}f=3\pi/16$ to $\lim_{\beta\to2\pi^-}f=0$, with $f(\pi)=3/8$. Indeed, on putting $q=\beta/2$, the sign of $f'$ is the sign of $q\cos q-\sin q$. The negative of this expression has <derivative> $q\sin q>0$ for $0<q<\pi$ and starts at zero, proving strict decrease.

On the first straight segment, $y=z=0$ and only $dx$ is nonzero, so both integrals vanish. On the second segment, $x=1,z=0$ and only $dy$ is nonzero; each integrand is one, contributing $1$. On the last segment, $x=y=1$ and only $dz$ is nonzero; each integrand is $\sinh z$, contributing $\cosh1-1$. Thus

The second result also follows from the <fundamental theorem for line integrals>, using the potential found in (a). Agreement on this one path does not make $\mathbf F$ conservative.

Parametrize the diagonal by $\mathbf x(t)=(t,t,t)$ for $0\leq t\leq1$. Then
$$
\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf x'=2t^2\cosh t+t^3\sinh t,
\qquad \mathbf G\cdot\mathbf x'=3t^2\cosh t+t^3\sinh t.
$$
The second expression is $(t^3\cosh t)'$, immediately giving $\int\mathbf G\cdot d\mathbf x=\cosh1$. The first differs from it by $t^2\cosh t$. Two applications of <integration by parts> give
$$
\int_0^1t^2\cosh t\,dt
=\left[t^2\sinh t-2t\cosh t+2\sinh t\right]_0^1
=3\sinh1-2\cosh1.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf x=3(\cosh1-\sinh1)=\frac3e},\qquad
\boxed{\int_C\mathbf G\cdot d\mathbf x=\cosh1}.
$$
The changed value for $\mathbf F$ explicitly demonstrates path dependence, while $\mathbf G$ gives the same endpoint-dependent result.

**The product formula requires independent failure times.** This assumption is not stated in the original PDF. Under that intended assumption, put $s=t/T$ and use the <failure probabilities for two independent lifetimes>:
$$
\boxed{P(t)=P_1(t)P_2(t)=\begin{cases}

\end{cases}}

Its graph is a cubic increasing from $(0,0)$ to $(T,1)$, convex before reaching its plateau.

Without independence, the marginal distributions do not determine $P(t)$. The <Fréchet inequalities> give the sharp pointwise bounds
$$
\boxed{\max(0,s+s^2-1)\leq P(t)\leq s^2,\qquad0<s<1}.
$$
For example, taking $t_1=TU$ and $t_2=T\sqrt U$ with the same uniform random variable $U$ preserves both prescribed marginals but gives $P(t)=s^2$, rather than $s^3$.

For a continuously differentiable <differential one-form>, exactness requires $\boxed{P_y=Q_x}$. It is locally sufficient, and globally sufficient on a <simply connected domain>; on a domain with holes, closedness alone need not imply exactness.

An <integrating factor for a differential one-form> depending only on $x$ must satisfy
$$
\partial_y(\mu P)=\partial_x(\mu Q),\qquad
\mu P_y=\mu'Q+\mu Q_x.
$$
Thus, where $Q\ne0$,
$$
\boxed{\frac{\mu'}\mu=\frac{P_y-Q_x}{Q},\qquad
\mu(x)=\exp\int\frac{P_y-Q_x}{Q}\,dx}.
$$
If the ratio depends only on $x$, this defines a nonzero factor and makes it a <closed differential form>, hence locally exact. For a factor depending only on $y$, the analogous calculation gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{\mu'}\mu=\frac{Q_x-P_y}{P},\qquad
\mu(y)=\exp\int\frac{Q_x-P_y}{P}\,dy},
$$
provided the ratio is a <function> only of $y$ on the region where $P\ne0$. A nonzero constant multiple of either factor works equally well.

An <eigenvalue equation> has the form $A\mathbf a=\lambda\mathbf a$, where $\mathbf a\ne0$ is an <eigenvector> and $\lambda$ its <eigenvalue>. It means that the <linear map> preserves the vector's line and acts there by multiplication by $\lambda$. 2-\sqrt2&\tfrac12(1,\sqrt2,1)&(k/m)(2-\sqrt2)\\
2&\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,0,-1)&2k/m\\
2+\sqrt2&\tfrac12(1,-\sqrt2,1)&(k/m)(2+\sqrt2)
\end{array}}.
$$
For each mode both signs of $\omega_j$ give the same <cosine> displacement. An arbitrary overall mode amplitude remains free. These vectors are the $N=3$ instances of the <sine eigenvectors of the fixed-end spring chain>.

The pairwise <dot products> of the normalized vectors are
$$
\frac{1-1}{2\sqrt2}=0,\qquad
\frac{1-2+1}{4}=0,\qquad
\frac{1-1}{2\sqrt2}=0.
$$
Their squared norms are respectively $(1+2+1)/4$, $(1+1)/2$, and $(1+2+1)/4$, all one. Hence they form an **<orthonormal basis>**. This also follows from the <spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices>, because $D$ is symmetric and its three <eigenvalues> are distinct.

= Solution

The <sine> and <cosine> functions are orthogonal over a complete period. For positive integers $m,n$,
$$
\int_0^T\cos(m\omega t)\cos(n\omega t)\,dt
=\int_0^T\sin(m\omega t)\sin(n\omega t)\,dt
=\frac T2\delta_{mn},
$$
while every mixed sine-cosine integral and every integral of a nonconstant single harmonic is zero. These identities follow from the product-to-sum formulas and the integer-period endpoint values.

Multiply the <Fourier series> by $\cos(m\omega t)$ and integrate. All terms except $a_m\cos^2(m\omega t)$ vanish, giving $Ta_m/2$. Repeating with <sine> isolates $b_m$. Thus
$$
\boxed{a_n=\frac2T\int_0^T V_1(t)\cos(n\omega t)\,dt,\qquad
b_n=\frac2T\int_0^T V_1(t)\sin(n\omega t)\,dt},\quad n\geq1.
$$
Integrating without a harmonic also gives $a_0=(2/T)\int_0^T V_1(t)\,dt$. The factor $1/2$ on the constant term in the chosen convention is essential.

Put $s=\omega t$, so the nonzero half-cycle is $0\leq s\leq\pi$. The constant coefficient is
$$
a_0=\frac{V_0}{\pi}\int_0^\pi\sin s\,ds=\frac{2V_0}{\pi}.
$$
The <sine> coefficients satisfy
$$
b_n=\frac{V_0}{\pi}\int_0^\pi\sin s\sin ns\,ds
=\begin{cases}V_0/2,&n=1,\\0,&n\ne1,\end{cases}
$$
by <sine> <orthogonality>. For the <cosine> coefficients, the product-to-sum identity gives, when $n\ne1$,
$$
\int_0^\pi\sin s\cos ns\,ds
=\frac12\int_0^\pi[\sin((1+n)s)+\sin((1-n)s)]\,ds
=\frac{1+(-1)^n}{1-n^2}.
$$
The case $n=1$ is zero directly. Hence
$$
\boxed{a_{2m}=-\frac{2V_0}{\pi(4m^2-1)},\quad a_{2m-1}=0,\quad
b_1=\frac{V_0}{2},\quad b_n=0\ (n\ne1)}.
$$
The first five nonzero terms, counting the constant as the zero-frequency term, are
$$
\boxed{V_1(t)=\frac{V_0}{\pi}+\frac{V_0}{2}\sin\omega t
-\frac{2V_0}{3\pi}\cos2\omega t
-\frac{2V_0}{15\pi}\cos4\omega t
-\frac{2V_0}{35\pi}\cos6\omega t+\cdots}.
$$
The general coefficient formula specifies every remaining term. This is the <Fourier series of a half-wave rectified sinusoid>; the waveform is continuous at the joins, so the series gives its actual value there.

At a regular constrained <stationary point>, the change in $f$ along every allowed tangent direction must vanish. Thus its <gradient> is normal to the <constraint> curve, giving the <Lagrange multiplier> equations
$$
\boxed{\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g,\qquad g=c},
$$
provided $\nabla g\ne0$. Solve these equations for $x,y,\lambda$, then classify the candidates using the <constraint> or a constrained second-derivative test. Points where the <constraint> <gradient> vanishes require separate analysis.

For $n$ variables and $r$ regular independent constraints $g_j=c_j$, use one multiplier per <constraint>:
$$
\boxed{\nabla f=\sum_{j=1}^r\lambda_j\nabla g_j,\qquad g_j=c_j}.
$$
There are $n+r$ scalar equations in the $n+r$ unknowns. Independence of the <constraint> gradients is the regularity condition; boundary points must be treated separately when present.

= b
{parent=19}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Use $\mathcal L=-\sum_i x_i\log x_i-\lambda(\sum_i x_i-1)$. Differentiating with respect to each positive $x_i$ gives
$$
-\log x_i-1-\lambda=0.
$$
Every $x_i$ is therefore equal; normalization forces
$$
\boxed{x_i=\frac1n\quad\text{for all }i},\qquad
\boxed{f_{\rm stationary}=\log n}.
$$
The <Hessian matrix> of $f$ is diagonal with entries $-1/x_i$, so its quadratic form is strictly negative for every nonzero perturbation. In particular it is negative along the tangent space $\sum_i\delta x_i=0$, making this the unique global maximum on the positive <probability simplex>. This is the <maximum entropy distribution on a finite set>, using natural logarithms.

{parent=19}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Introduce a second multiplier $b$ and use
$$
\mathcal L=-\sum_i x_i\log x_i-\lambda\left(\sum_i x_i-1\right)
-b\left(\sum_i x_i y_i-Y\right).
$$
The stationary equations become $-\log x_i-1-\lambda-by_i=0$. Writing $a=e^{-1-\lambda}>0$ gives
$$
\boxed{x_i=ae^{-by_i}}.
$$
The two constraints determine the constants through
$$
\boxed{a\sum_{i=1}^n e^{-by_i}=1,\qquad
a\sum_{i=1}^n y_i e^{-by_i}=Y}.
$$
Equivalently, $a=1/Z(b)$ with $Z(b)=\sum_i e^{-by_i}$, and $-d\log Z/db=Y$. This is the <maximum entropy distribution with a prescribed finite mean>.

For nonconstant $y_i$, a strictly positive solution exists exactly when $\min_i y_i<Y<\max_i y_i$. The mean's <derivative> with respect to $b$ is $-\operatorname{Var}_b(y)<0$, so $b$ is unique in that case. At either extreme mean no strictly positive solution exists. If all $y_i=y_0$, feasibility requires $Y=y_0$ and the extra <constraint> is redundant: $x_i=1/n$, while $a,b$ are not separately identifiable. The negative entropy Hessian ensures that any feasible interior <stationary point> is the unique constrained maximum.

In a steady state, $\nu u_s''=-G\sin(n\pi x)$. Since the second <derivative> of the <sine> is $-(n\pi)^2$ times itself,
$$
u_s(x)=\frac{G}{\nu n^2\pi^2}\sin(n\pi x)+Ax+B.
$$
The zero boundary values at $x=0,1$ force $A=B=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{u_s(x)=\frac{G}{\nu n^2\pi^2}\sin(n\pi x)}.
$$

= b
{parent=20}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The steady profile has no time <derivative>. Substituting $u=\widetilde u+u_s$ and using its steady equation cancels the forcing, giving
$$
\boxed{\widetilde u_t=\nu\widetilde u_{xx},\qquad
\widetilde u(0,t)=\widetilde u(1,t)=0}.
$$
The initial condition is
$$
\boxed{\widetilde u(x,0)=-u_s(x)=-\frac{G}{\nu n^2\pi^2}\sin(n\pi x)}.
$$
The resulting <heat equation> is independent of $G$, as are its <boundary conditions>. Its initial amplitude still depends on $G$; removing a source from the evolution equation does not remove it from the whole problem.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Separate $\widetilde u=f(t)g(x)$. On a nonzero product region,
$$
\frac{f'}{\nu f}=\frac{g''}{g}=-\lambda.
$$
A decaying temporal factor requires $\lambda>0$, with $f=Ce^{-\nu\lambda t}$. The zero spatial boundary values give $g=A\sin(\sqrt\lambda x)$ and $\sin\sqrt\lambda=0$, hence $\lambda=j^2\pi^2$ with $j=1,2,\ldots$. Zero or negative separation parameters give no nonzero spatial mode with both boundary values zero.

The initial profile is already exactly the $j=n$ <sine> <eigenfunction>. Its coefficient therefore determines a single separated solution:
$$
\boxed{\widetilde u(x,t)=-\frac{G}{\nu n^2\pi^2}
e^{-\nu n^2\pi^2t}\sin(n\pi x)}.
$$
The exponential tends to zero because $\nu>0$. More generally <sine> <orthogonality> would determine a sum of such modes, but all other coefficients vanish here. This is the transient of the <heat equation with a single stationary sine forcing>.

Adding the steady and transient parts gives
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=\frac{G}{\nu n^2\pi^2}
\left(1-e^{-\nu n^2\pi^2t}\right)\sin(n\pi x)}.
$$
Integrating the <sine> across the channel gives $\int_0^1\sin(n\pi x)\,dx=[1-(-1)^n]/(n\pi)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{Q(t)=\frac{G[1-(-1)^n]}{\nu n^3\pi^3}
\left(1-e^{-\nu n^2\pi^2t}\right)}.
$$
For even $n$ the signed flow rate is zero, because positive and negative velocity lobes cancel. For odd $n$ the bracket is two. Both expressions vanish initially and approach their steady values exponentially; $G=0$ gives the trivial zero solution throughout.
