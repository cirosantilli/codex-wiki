Over the <complex numbers>, the <polynomial factorization> $z^2+1=(z-i)(z+i)$ gives the <partial fraction decomposition>
$$
\frac{z+1}{z^2+1}=\frac{A}{z-i}+\frac{B}{z+i}.
$$
Multiplication by $(z-i)(z+i)$ and comparison of <polynomial> coefficients give $A+B=1$ and $i(A-B)=1$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\frac{z+1}{z^2+1}=\frac{1-i}{2(z-i)}+\frac{1+i}{2(z+i)}},\qquad z\ne\pm i.
$$

Substitute $x^3$ into the <Taylor series> for the <exponential function>:
$$
\boxed{e^{x^3}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{3n}}{n!}}.
$$
The general nonzero term is $x^{3n}/n!$; the <power series> coefficients of powers not divisible by three are zero. The <radius of convergence> is infinite.

= Solution

The <power series> for $e^{y^3}$ has <uniform convergence> on every compact interval, so <termwise integration> from zero to $x$ is valid. Thus
$$
\int_0^x e^{y^3}\,dy=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int_0^x y^{3n}\,dy
=\boxed{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{3n+1}}{(3n+1)n!}}.
$$
This <power series> converges for every real $x$, including negative $x$ with the usual orientation of a <definite integral>.

The <inclusion-exclusion principle for probabilities> gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
P(A\cup B\cup C)={}&P(A)+P(B)+P(C)\\
&-P(A\cap B)-P(A\cap C)-P(B\cap C)\\
&+P(A\cap B\cap C).
\end{aligned}}
$$
Each outcome belonging to two events is initially counted twice and must lose one count. An outcome in all three is counted three times and then subtracted three times, so the last term restores its single count.

Write $U=A\cup B\cup C$. Since $D\subseteq U$, their <intersection> is $U\cap D=D$. Therefore the definition of <conditional probability> gives
$$
\boxed{P(U\mid D)=\frac{P(U\cap D)}{P(D)}=1},\qquad P(D)>0.
$$
The positive-probability condition is necessary: if $P(D)=0$, this elementary definition of <conditional probability> does not assign a value.

This is a <generalized eigenvalue problem>. A nonzero <vector> lies in the <kernel> of $A-\lambda B$ precisely when its <determinant> vanishes:
$$
0=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&2-2\lambda\\2-2\lambda&1\end{pmatrix}

Let $\mathbf d=\mathbf f-\mathbf e$. A point on the line has <position vector> $\mathbf r=\mathbf e+t\mathbf d$. By bilinearity and antisymmetry of the <cross product>,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf r\times\mathbf d=\mathbf e\times\mathbf d+t\mathbf d\times\mathbf d=\mathbf e\times\mathbf f}.
$$
For a point with <position vector> $\mathbf c$, the area of the <parallelogram> spanned by $\mathbf c-\mathbf e$ and $\mathbf d$ equals base length times perpendicular <Euclidean distance>. Hence the shortest <Euclidean distance> to the line is
$$
\boxed{d=\frac{|(\mathbf c-\mathbf e)\times(\mathbf f-\mathbf e)|}{|\mathbf f-\mathbf e|}}.
$$
For $\mathbf c=(0,0,1)$, these quantities are
$$
(\mathbf c-\mathbf e)\times(\mathbf f-\mathbf e)=\left(\frac14,\frac14,\frac14\right),\qquad
|\mathbf f-\mathbf e|=\frac1{\sqrt2}.
$$
Thus $\boxed{d=\sqrt6/4}$. The perpendicular meets $EF$ at $(1/4,1/4,1/2)$, whose displacement from $C$ has precisely this length.

The <Hessian matrix> is
$$
H(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}1+2y&2x\\2x&1-2y\end{pmatrix}.
$$
At the origin it is the identity, so the origin is a strict **<local minimum>**. At $(0,1)$ it is $\operatorname{diag}(3,-1)$, so this point is a **<saddle point>**. At either remaining <stationary point>,

$$
which again gives a **<saddle point>**. All three <saddle points> have <eigenvalues> $3$ and $-1$; there are no <local maxima>.

The most useful guide to the <contour plot> is the common saddle value. Direct <polynomial factorization> gives
$$
6f-1=(2y+1)\bigl[3x^2-(y-1)^2\bigr].
$$
Thus the <level set> $f=1/6$ is exactly the three complete lines
$$
\boxed{y=-\frac12,\qquad y=1+\sqrt3x,\qquad y=1-\sqrt3x}.

Their intersections are the three <saddle points>, and they enclose the origin in an equilateral triangle. Near the <local minimum>, the <level sets> are nearly circles because the leading term is $(x^2+y^2)/2$. For $0<c<1/6$, a closed contour surrounds the origin, while three separate open branches lie outside the triangular barrier. At the saddle level these components meet at the <saddle points>; above it the central closed contour has opened into the three outer channels.

The identity in <polar coordinates>

f=\frac12r^2+\frac13r^3\sin3\theta

shows the rotation symmetry through $2\pi/3$. It identifies the function as the <Hénon-Heiles potential> and helps organize all three channels in the original sketch below. The dashed straight lines are the saddle level; labels on the other contours give $f$.

The <independence of random variables> means that fixing $Y=y$ leaves the <conditional probability density> of $X$ equal to $f$. Given that value of $Y$, $Z$ is the translate $X+y$. Hence
$$
\boxed{P(z\leq Z\leq z+dz\mid Y=y)=\int_{z-y}^{z+dz-y}f(x)\,dx}.

Use the <law of total probability> to average the conditional interval probability over $Y$:

P(z\leq Z\leq z+dz)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(y)\int_z^{z+dz}f(v-y)\,dv\,dy.

The <probability density> is an <even function>. Its <derivative> simplifies to
$$
h'(z)=\frac1\pi\left[\frac1{1+(z+1/2)^2}-\frac1{1+(z-1/2)^2}\right]

\Image[paper-2-q14-cauchy-smoothing.png]
{title=Density of a standard Cauchy variable after an independent uniform displacement}

There is a genuine qualification to the request for a mean: **the mean does not exist**. The integral representation shows that $h(z)\sim1/(\pi z^2)$ as $|z|\to\infty$, so
$$
\int_0^\infty z h(z)\,dz=\infty,\qquad
\int_{-\infty}^0 z h(z)\,dz=-\infty.
$$
The two divergent integrals cannot be subtracted to define an <expected value>. Symmetry does give the <Cauchy principal value> $\lim_{A\to\infty}\int_{-A}^A zh(z)\,dz=0$, but that is not the mean. Equivalently, adding the bounded variable $Y$ cannot make the first absolute moment of a <Cauchy distribution> finite.

For period two, the <Fourier series> convention is
$$
f(x)=\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl[a_n\cos(n\pi x)+b_n\sin(n\pi x)\bigr].
$$
Since $f$ is an <even function>, every $b_n$ is zero. Also $a_0=\int_{-1}^1\cosh x\,dx=2\sinh1$. For $n\geq1$, an <antiderivative> useful for the cosine coefficient is
$$
\int\cosh x\cos(n\pi x)\,dx=
\frac{\sinh x\cos(n\pi x)+n\pi\cosh x\sin(n\pi x)}{1+n^2\pi^2}.
$$
Evaluation at $\pm1$ gives $a_n=2(-1)^n\sinh1/(1+n^2\pi^2)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\sinh1\left[1+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n\cos(n\pi x)}{1+n^2\pi^2}\right]}.
$$
The periodic extension is continuous at $x=1$, so its <Fourier series> converges to $\cosh1$ there. Since $\cos(n\pi)=(-1)^n$,
$$
\cosh1=\sinh1\left[1+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{1+n^2\pi^2}\right].
$$
Therefore $\boxed{p=2,\ q=\pi}$, with $q=-\pi$ giving the same expression because only $q^2$ occurs. This is the $\beta=1$ case of <Fourier series of periodic hyperbolic sine and cosine>.

$$

\boxed{S_{\min}=6\pi\left(\frac{V}{2\pi}\right)^{2/3}}.

For the Gaussian ansatz, <partial differentiation> gives
$$
T_t=(F'-x^2FH')e^{-x^2H},\qquad
T_{xx}=F(-2H+4x^2H^2)e^{-x^2H}.
$$
Cancel the positive exponential and compare the constant and quadratic powers of $x$ in the <diffusion equation>. The two coupled <ordinary differential equations> are
$$
\boxed{F'=-2\kappa FH,\qquad H'=-4\kappa H^2}.
$$
The initial profile requires $F(0)=T_0$ and $H(0)=L^{-2}$. The second equation is a <separable differential equation>:
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac1H\right)=4\kappa,
\qquad \boxed{H(t)=\frac1{L^2+4\kappa t}}.
$$
Substitute this into the first equation and integrate its <logarithmic derivative>:
$$
\ln\frac{F(t)}{T_0}=-2\kappa\int_0^t\frac{ds}{L^2+4\kappa s}
=-\frac12\ln\frac{L^2+4\kappa t}{L^2}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{F(t)=\frac{T_0L}{\sqrt{L^2+4\kappa t}}},
$$
and the solution is
$$
\boxed{T(x,t)=\frac{T_0L}{\sqrt{L^2+4\kappa t}}\exp\left[-\frac{x^2}{L^2+4\kappa t}\right]},\qquad t\geq0.
$$
This is <Gaussian diffusion of a smooth initial profile>: its width grows while its peak decreases. The <Gaussian integral> gives $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}T(x,t)\,dx=\sqrt\pi T_0L$, independent of time, which checks conservation of the total amount under the <diffusion equation>.
