The <complex conjugate> is $z^*=x-iy$. From the complex <sine> and <cosine> identities,
$$
\sin(x-iy)=\sin x\cosh y-i\cos x\sinh y,\qquad
\cos(x-iy)=\cos x\cosh y+i\sin x\sinh y.

Divide these expressions and multiply by the <complex conjugate> of the denominator. Its squared <modulus> is $\cos^2x\cosh^2y+\sin^2x\sinh^2y=(\cos2x+\cosh2y)/2$. The numerator simplifies using $\cosh^2y-\sinh^2y=1$ and the <double-angle formulas>. Thus

These expressions apply except at the <poles> $y=0$, $x=\pi/2+k\pi$ with integer $k$, where the original <tangent function> is undefined.

Take $r>0$ and select a <complex argument> $\theta$ for $z$. To specify the <complex exponentiation>, use $z^{1+i}=\exp((1+i)(\ln r+i\theta))$. Expanding its exponent gives
$$
z^{1+i}=e^{\ln r-\theta}e^{i(\theta+\ln r)}.
$$
Its <complex logarithm> therefore has values
$$
\boxed{\Re f=\ln r-\theta,\qquad\Im f=\theta+\ln r+2\pi k,\quad k\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
The continuous logarithm obtained by retaining the displayed exponent uses $k=0$. If the outer logarithm is the <principal logarithm>, choose $k$ to put the <imaginary part> in $(-\pi,\pi]$ instead; the <real part> is unchanged. In particular one cannot generally assert $\operatorname{Log}(e^w)=w$ without checking its <imaginary part>.

The condition on the <real part> gives $\ln r=\theta$, so the required <logarithmic spiral> has <polar coordinates>
$$
\boxed{r=e^\theta,\qquad z(\theta)=e^\theta(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta).}
$$
As $\theta$ increases, it runs from the negative <real axis> near radius $e^{-\pi}$ through $-ie^{-\pi/2}$, $1$ and $ie^{\pi/2}$ to the negative <real axis> at radius $e^\pi$. The <angle> increases by one complete turn. Using the conventional <principal argument> $-\pi<\theta\le\pi$, the inner negative-axis endpoint is excluded and the outer endpoint is included. The convention $-\pi\le\theta<\pi$ reverses those endpoint choices. If both limiting arguments are admitted by the paper's wording, both limiting endpoints are drawn; the interior curve is the same. The origin is never included because the <complex logarithm> is undefined there.

The height is constant along each circular ring, whose <area element> after angular integration is $2\pi r\,dr$. The <volume integral> in <cylindrical coordinates> is therefore
$$
V=2\pi\int_0^R rR e^{-r/R}\,dr=2\pi R^3\int_0^1ue^{-u}\,du.
$$
Here $u=r/R$. Using <integration by parts>, $\int_0^1ue^{-u}\,du=[-(u+1)e^{-u}]_0^1=1-2/e$. Hence
$$
\boxed{R=\left[\frac{V}{2\pi(1-2/e)}\right]^{1/3}.}
$$
The dimensionless coefficient is positive, and the cube root of a <volume> has the required <physical dimension> of <length>.

Integrate by horizontal slices. At a fixed height $0\le z\le H$, the parabolic boundary gives $|x|\le L\sqrt{z/H}$; the other inequality gives $|y|\le b(2-z/H)$. Thus the cross-section is a rectangle of <area>

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{\rm bank}&=\int_0^H A(z)\,dz
=4LbH\int_0^1(2u^{1/2}-u^{3/2})\,du\\

$$
This has units of <volume>. As a check on the geometry, the width decreases with height while the length of the horizontal slice increases from zero to $2L$.

In the $x,z$ plane, the embankment lies above the <parabola> $z=Hx^2/L^2$ and below $z=H$. The bore has circular cross-section
$$
\mathcal D=\left\{(x,z):x^2+(z-D/2)^2\le D^2/4\right\}.
$$
It touches the valley floor at the origin. To check that this entire <disk> lies inside the projected embankment, its equation gives $x^2\le Dz-z^2\le Dz$. Since $HD/L^2<1$, it follows that $Hx^2/L^2\le z$, with strict inequality away from the bottom contact. Also $z\le D<H$. Thus no circular segment needs to be subtracted from the tunnel section.

For each $(x,z)$ in the <disk>, the length of bore inside the material is the local width $2b(2-z/H)$. The required <volume integral> is therefore
$$
V_{\rm cut}=\iint_{\mathcal D}2b\left(2-\frac zH\right)\,dx\,dz.
$$
The <disk> has <area> $A=\pi D^2/4$ and <centroid> height $D/2$, so $\iint_{\mathcal D}z\,dx\,dz=AD/2$. Applying the <volume of a solid with affine thickness> formula gives
$$
\boxed{V_{\rm cut}=2bA\left(2-\frac{D}{2H}\right)=\pi bD^2\left(1-\frac D{4H}\right).}
$$
The width varies along the circular section; multiplying its <area> by the top width alone would miss this variation.

At the initial point $F_y=e^{\pi/2}\ne0$, so the <implicit function theorem> guarantees a unique local branch $y(x)$ through that point.

Write the <scalar field> as $u=R(r)Y(\theta,\phi)$, where $R=r+r^{-2}$ and $Y=\sin\theta\cos\phi$. Its radial contribution to the <Laplacian> in <spherical coordinates> is
$$
\frac{Y}{r^2}(r^2R')'=Y\left(\frac2r+\frac2{r^4}\right)=\frac{2R}{r^2}Y.
$$
For the angular contribution, $Y_\theta=\cos\theta\cos\phi$ and $Y_{\phi\phi}=-\sin\theta\cos\phi$, so
$$
\frac1{\sin\theta}\partial_\theta(\sin\theta\,Y_\theta)
+\frac{Y_{\phi\phi}}{\sin^2\theta}
=\frac{\cos\phi}{\sin\theta}(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta-1)=-2Y.
$$
Combining the radial and angular terms proves
$$
\boxed{\nabla^2u=\frac{2R}{r^2}Y-\frac{2R}{r^2}Y=0\qquad(r>0).}
$$
The calculation uses $0<\theta<\pi$ to avoid coordinate singularities. In <Cartesian coordinates>, $u=x(1+r^{-3})$, which extends smoothly across the polar axis for $r>0$. It therefore satisfies <Laplace's equation> there as well. The origin is a genuine singularity and is excluded.

Take the <total differential> of the <logarithm> of the <relativistic energy>, with the <speed of light> fixed:
$$
\ln E=\ln m+2\ln c-\frac12\ln\left(1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right).

Work on an open domain with $xy>0$ and $x\ne0$, where the displayed square root is real and differentiable. Split $u=v+h$ with $v=\phi(xy)$ and $h=\sqrt{xy}\,\psi(y/x)$. For the first term, the <chain rule> gives
$$
v_{xx}=y^2\phi''(xy),\qquad v_{yy}=x^2\phi''(xy),
$$
so $x^2v_{xx}-y^2v_{yy}=0$.

For the second term, set $t=y/x$. Its first <partial derivatives> are
$$
h_x=\frac{\sqrt{xy}}x\left(\frac12\psi(t)-t\psi'(t)\right),\qquad
h_y=\frac{\sqrt{xy}}y\left(\frac12\psi(t)+t\psi'(t)\right).
$$
They show $xh_x+yh_y=h$, the degree-one case of <Euler theorem for homogeneous functions>. Differentiating that identity with respect to each variable yields
$$
xh_{xx}+yh_{xy}=0,\qquad xh_{xy}+yh_{yy}=0.
$$
The mixed <partial derivatives> agree here by the <chain rule>. Multiplying these equations by $x$ and $y$, respectively, proves the <second derivative identity for a degree-one homogeneous function>:
$$
x^2h_{xx}=-xyh_{xy}=y^2h_{yy}.
$$
Adding the results for $v$ and $h$ verifies the <partial differential equation>
$$
\boxed{x^2u_{xx}-y^2u_{yy}=0.}
$$
Equivalently, $h(\lambda x,\lambda y)=\lambda h(x,y)$ for positive $\lambda$, so the same proof follows directly from its <homogeneous function> property. The statement is local to a domain where the given expression is defined; it does not require assigning values on $x=0$.

The finite form of <Taylor's theorem> is
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k+R_n(x),\qquad R_n(x)=\frac{f^{(n)}(\xi)}{n!}(x-a)^n,}
$$
where $\xi$ lies between $a$ and $x$ when $x\ne a$. Sufficient hypotheses are continuous <derivatives> up to order $n-1$ on the interval joining the endpoints and an $n$th <derivative> in its interior. The first $n$ terms end at degree $n-1$; the <Taylor remainder> uses the $n$th <derivative>. At $x=a$ the remainder is zero. This finite <Taylor polynomial> identity does not require convergence of an infinite <Taylor series>.

Use the intended model of uniform draws and a replacement decision independent of the first ball's colour, with <probability> $r$. The marginal specification $P(R)=r$ alone would not determine the experiment if replacement depended on that colour. This independence assumption is used throughout the question.

Write $\overline B_j$ for a non-blue draw and $\overline R$ for no replacement. The <sample space> consists of the eight ordered outcomes obtained by choosing one of $B_1,\overline B_1$, then one of $R,\overline R$, then one of $B_2,\overline B_2$. The <chain rule for probabilities> gives their <probabilities>:
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{outcome}&\text{probability}\\\hline
B_1\cap R\cap B_2&4r/25\\
B_1\cap R\cap\overline B_2&6r/25\\
\overline B_1\cap R\cap B_2&6r/25\\
\overline B_1\cap R\cap\overline B_2&9r/25\\
B_1\cap\overline R\cap B_2&(1-r)/10\\
B_1\cap\overline R\cap\overline B_2&3(1-r)/10\\
\overline B_1\cap\overline R\cap B_2&3(1-r)/10\\
\overline B_1\cap\overline R\cap\overline B_2&3(1-r)/10
\end{array}}

Let $C=\overline B_1\cap B_2$. Under replacement its joint <probability> is
$$
P(R\cap C)=\frac{N-N_B}{N}\,r\,\frac{N_B}{N},
$$
whereas without replacement it is
$$
P(\overline R\cap C)=\frac{N-N_B}{N}\,(1-r)\,\frac{N_B}{N-1}.
$$
In the latter case the first removed ball is non-blue, so all $N_B$ blue balls remain. Applying <Bayes' theorem> and cancelling the positive common factors yields the <posterior replacement probability after different-colour draws>:
$$
\boxed{g(r)=P(R\mid C)=\frac{r/N}{r/N+(1-r)/(N-1)}=\frac{r(N-1)}{N-r}.}
$$
It does not depend on $N_B$. To prove the requested bound directly,
$$
\boxed{r-g(r)=\frac{r(1-r)}{N-r}\ge0,\qquad0\le r\le1.}
$$
Equality holds at $r=0$ and $r=1$, and the inequality is strict between them. Its sketch joins $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$, stays below the diagonal, and is increasing and convex because
$$
g'(r)=\frac{N(N-1)}{(N-r)^2}>0,\qquad g''(r)=\frac{2N(N-1)}{(N-r)^3}>0.
$$
The interpretation is that removing a non-blue ball increases the chance of blue on the second draw; seeing the two different colours therefore favours the no-replacement explanation.
