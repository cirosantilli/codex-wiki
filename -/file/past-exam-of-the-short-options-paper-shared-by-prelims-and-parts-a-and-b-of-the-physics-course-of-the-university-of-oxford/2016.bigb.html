===== Solution

The <Simple pole>[simple poles] occur at $z=n-\alpha$, $n\in\mathbb Z$, since the <derivative> of the sine there is $\pi(-1)^n$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Res}_{n-\alpha}f=\frac{(-1)^n}{\pi(n-\alpha)^2}.}
$$
There is also a <double pole> at zero, not included in that list because $0<\alpha<1$. Its <residue>, needed for the next <contour> calculation, is the <derivative> of $\csc\pi(z+\alpha)$ at zero, namely $-\pi\csc(\pi\alpha)\cot(\pi\alpha)$.

==== 2
{scope}

===== Solution

On the square, $|z|\geq N$. The identity $|\sin\pi(x+\alpha+iy)|^2=\sin^2\pi(x+\alpha)+\sinh^2\pi y$ bounds the sine away from zero on the vertical sides because $x=\pm N$; on horizontal sides it grows exponentially. Therefore the whole <contour integral> is $O(N^{-1})$ or smaller and vanishes. The <residue theorem> then gives
$$
0=2\pi i\left[-\pi\csc\pi\alpha\cot\pi\alpha+\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\frac{(-1)^n}{\pi(n-\alpha)^2}\right],
$$
which proves $\boxed{\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}(-1)^n/(n-\alpha)^2=\pi^2\csc\pi\alpha\cot\pi\alpha}$. <Absolute convergence> justifies the limiting <residue> sum.

==== 3
{scope}

===== Solution

Separate the $n=0$ term and pair $n$ with $-n$. For small $\alpha$,
$$
\frac{(-1)^n}{(n-\alpha)^2}+\frac{(-1)^n}{(n+\alpha)^2}=\frac{2(-1)^n}{n^2}+\frac{6\alpha^2(-1)^n}{n^4}+O(\alpha^4/n^6).
$$
Compare the constant and quadratic coefficients with the supplied expansion after subtracting $\alpha^{-2}$. It follows that
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{(-1)^n}{n^2}=-\frac{\pi^2}{12},\qquad\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{(-1)^n}{n^4}=-\frac{7\pi^4}{720}.}
$$
The paired expansion is uniformly summable on any sufficiently small <closed interval> of $\alpha$, so coefficient comparison is legitimate.

===== Solution

A <conformal map> preserves local oriented angles and has a nonzero local <Geometric scale factor>[scale factor]. For an orientation-preserving complex map this is equivalent to <holomorphy> with $f'(z)\ne0$ at the point concerned. Infinitesimal figures are rotated and uniformly scaled there, but finite shapes need not be preserved: <derivative> magnitude and direction can vary across a region. <Antiholomorphic map>[Antiholomorphic maps] preserve unoriented angles while reversing orientation; the convention being used must be stated.

==== 2
{scope}

===== Solution

For real $x\ne-1$, $w=1-2/(x+1)$ is real. Its finite image is the entire <real axis> except $w=1$, which is approached only as $x\to\pm\infty$. The point $x=-1$ maps to infinity. On the extended <complex plane> the extended <real axis> maps onto itself, with $z=\infty$ mapping to one.

==== 3
{scope}

===== Solution

The identity $w-1=-2/(z+1)$ gives $\boxed{|w-1|=2/r}$ on $|z+1|=r$. Thus the image circle has center one and <radius> $2/r$. Points strictly inside the original circle satisfy $|w-1|>2/r$; the center itself maps to infinity. The interior therefore becomes the exterior together with infinity on the <Riemann sphere>, rather than the interior of the image circle. This inversion is a useful warning against assuming conformality preserves global inside/outside assignments.

==== 4
{scope}

===== Solution

For $z=e^{i\theta}$, $w=i\tan(\theta/2)$, which is purely imaginary; $z=-1$ supplies the point at infinity. Conversely, the <inverse map> is $z=(1+w)/(1-w)$. Requiring $|w|=1$ is equivalent to $|z-1|=|z+1|$, hence $\boxed{\operatorname{Re}z=0}$, including the appropriate infinite point in the extended-plane formulation. The <unit circle> and <imaginary axis> are exchanged, but the <real axis> is not.

===== Solution

Set $z=e^{i\theta}$. The <integral> becomes
$$
\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{dz}{i[(1-i/2)z^2+3z+(1+i/2)]}.
$$
The roots are $z=(-3\pm2)/(2-i)$, only $z_+=-1/(2-i)$ lying inside. The <derivative> of the quadratic at $z_+$ is two, so the <residue> is $1/(2i)$. Consequently $\boxed{\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta/(3+2\cos\theta+\sin\theta)=\pi}$. The denominator is positive since $3>\sqrt5$, so no principal-value qualification is required.

===== 1
{scope}

====== Solution

Cauchy's theorem says that a <holomorphic function> integrates to zero around a closed <contour> homologous to zero in its domain. Thus deforming one <contour> into another without crossing a singularity preserves the <integral>. More generally for a <meromorphic> $f$, the exact condition is
$$
\boxed{\sum_a[n_\Gamma(a)-n_{\Gamma'}(a)]\operatorname{Res}_a f=0.}
$$
Equal <Winding number>[winding numbers] about every singularity are sufficient. They are not necessary for one particular function because <Residue>[residues] can cancel; even arbitrary <Contour>[contours] have equal <Integral>[integrals] for $f=0$. A topological deformation criterion must not be confused with a necessary condition for accidental equality.

===== 2
{scope}

====== Solution

The finite singularities are square-root <Branch point>[branch points] at $z=\pm1$. Choose the cut between them and the branch $f(z)\sim z$ at infinity. The two banks then have values $+i\sqrt{1-x^2}$ above and $-i\sqrt{1-x^2}$ below. A counterclockwise loop around the cut traverses its upper bank right-to-left, so its <integral> is $-2iI$, where $I$ is the required real <integral>. At large <radius>,
$$
f(z)=z-\frac1{2z}+O(z^{-3}),\qquad\oint f(z)dz=-\pi i.
$$
Therefore $\boxed{I=\pi/2}$. Infinity is not another square-root <branch point>: this branch has an ordinary <pole> there on its sheet. Small detours about the finite endpoints vanish as their <radius> to power $3/2$.

The first law gives elliptical relative <orbit-dynamical-system>[orbits] with the center of <force> at a focus; the second gives equal swept areas in equal times, expressing angular-momentum conservation. For circular binary motion, let $r_1+r_2=a$ and $M_1r_1=M_2r_2$. The <force> equation $M_1(2\pi/P)^2r_1=GM_1M_2/a^2$ gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{a^3}{P^2}=\frac{G(M_1+M_2)}{4\pi^2}.}
$$
The corresponding relation for ellipses uses the relative <semimajor axis> rather than instantaneous separation.

===== Solution

Eclipses justify an approximately <edge-on orbit>, and equal <velocity> <Amplitude>[amplitudes] imply equal masses. The relative <speed> is 200 km s$^{-1}$, so $a=(v_1+v_2)P/(2\pi)\simeq1.50\times10^{10}$ m, or $\boxed{a\simeq0.100\ \mathrm{AU}}$. Inclination corrections scale the dynamical masses as $\sin^{-3}i$ if exact edge-on viewing is relaxed.

<Hydrostatic balance> is $dP/dr=-GM(r)\rho/r^2$. A free-fall estimate gives $t_{\mathrm{dyn}}\sim\sqrt{R_\odot^3/(GM_\odot)}\simeq1.6\times10^3$ s; a <test particle> falling in a fixed point-mass field adds factor $\pi/(2\sqrt2)$, giving about 30 minutes.

For an ideal nonrelativistic gas with negligible boundary <pressure>, the <virial theorem> is $2U+\Omega=0$, so total <energy> is $\Omega/2$. Since $\Omega=-\int_0^M Gm\,dm/r(m)$ and $r\leq R$, $|\Omega|\geq GM^2/(2R)$. This is a *minimum magnitude*, not a <lower bound> on the negative <energy>: unrestricted central concentration can make the latter arbitrarily negative. A uniform-density estimate gives $\Omega=-3GM^2/(5R)$ and available contraction <energy> $3GM^2/(10R)$. Its release at present solar <luminosity> lasts $\boxed{t\sim10^7\ \mathrm{yr}}$ (order $10^7$–$10^8$ years for alternative structural estimates), much less than a nuclear-burning lifetime.

===== Solution

<Blackbody radiation> defeated classical equipartition because it predicts excessive short-wavelength emission. Quantized oscillator <energy> exchange $h\nu$, and ultimately the <photon> occupation law, gives the <Planck spectrum>. In the <photoelectric effect>, <electron> <energy> follows light <frequency> with a threshold, rather than simply increasing with beam intensity; individual <Photon>[photons] supply $h\nu$ and the maximum <kinetic energy> is $h\nu-W$. Intensity chiefly changes the event rate at a fixed <frequency>. These explain two distinct failures of a purely continuous classical wave-plus-matter model, while retaining <wave propagation> and interference.

==== 2
{scope}

===== Solution

Approximate <helium> <mass> by $2m_p+2m_n\simeq6.69\times10^{-27}$ kg. Nonrelativistic <momentum> is $p=\sqrt{2mT}$, giving $\boxed{\lambda=h/p\simeq0.203\ \mathrm{nm}}$. A transverse width of one micrometre implies a characteristic <position uncertainty> of that order and a minimum <momentum> uncertainty of order $\hbar/a\sim10^{-28}$ kg m s$^{-1}$, with factors depending on profile convention. For a uniform interval $\Delta x=a/\sqrt{12}$, but an exactly abrupt rectangular <wavefunction> actually has infinite rms <momentum> spread. Width alone does not uniquely specify both rms uncertainties; a smooth near-minimum-uncertainty beam is needed for a finite order-of-magnitude estimate.

==== 3
{scope}

===== Solution

Immediately after a uniformly illuminated hard slit, the normalized transmitted state is $\psi(x)=w^{-1/2}$ for $|x|<w/2$, zero elsewhere. With Fourier convention $\widetilde\psi(p)=(2\pi\hbar)^{-1/2}\int e^{-ipx/\hbar}\psi(x)dx$,
$$
\boxed{\widetilde\psi(p_x)=\sqrt{\frac{w}{2\pi\hbar}}\operatorname{sinc}\frac{p_xw}{2\hbar},\quad \widetilde p(p_x)=\frac1{\delta p_x}\operatorname{sinc}^2\frac{\pi p_x}{\delta p_x},\quad\delta p_x=\frac hw.}
$$
The supplied sinc <integral> verifies unit <normalization>. The first zeros occur at $\pm\delta p_x$, but this finite central-lobe width is not the rms uncertainty: $p_x^2\widetilde p$ fails to have a convergent <integral>.

==== 4
{scope}

===== Solution

For an aligned narrower slit placed immediately after the first, uniform illumination gives <Transmission coefficient>[transmission probability] $\boxed{d/w}$. Conditional on passage the same normalized formula applies with $w$ replaced by $d$ and central-lobe <momentum> scale $h/d$. Position rms is $d/\sqrt{12}$, while the exact hard-edge rms $\Delta p_x$ is infinite. Thus the source's finite “uncertainty” can only mean a characteristic diffraction width; the rigorous Heisenberg inequality is not violated, and smoothing the edges restores finite rms moments satisfying it.

A 10 keV X-ray has $\lambda\simeq0.124$ nm. The ideal position-filter <probability> and <momentum> formula at fixed aperture widths are unchanged, since the <Fourier transform> involves $h$ and the aperture geometry. Its larger total <momentum> makes the angular diffraction width smaller, approximately $\lambda/d$. Real slit-material transmission and absorption are additional experimental issues.

==== 5
{scope}

===== Solution

To distinguish $2^b$ equal transverse bins across width $w$, $\boxed{d=w/2^b}$. Six bits gives one-nanometre pixels; ten gives 0.0625 nm, below the <helium> wavelength. In the stipulated fixed-energy freely propagating beam, the latter entails transverse diffraction <momenta> comparable to or exceeding the available total <momentum> and lies outside the paraxial aperture model. Six-bit localization is less demanding, although fine detector resolution and losses remain practical constraints. This is not a universal theorem that an <atom-physics>[atom] can carry at most six bits in every encoding: accessible modes, <energy>, aperture, measurements and success probabilities define the channel.

Integrating $F=-kx$ gives $V=kx^2/2$ up to a constant. Thus $\boxed{-\hbar^2\phi''/(2m)+m\omega^2x^2\phi/2=E\phi}$, with $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$. The potential is a symmetric upward parabola and tends to infinity in both directions; normalizable states decay at large $|x|$.

A classically accelerating Coulomb <electron> radiates and loses <orbital energy>, so a stable nonradiating <orbit-dynamical-system>[orbit] is impossible within that unmodified theory. Bohr instead postulates stationary nonradiating <orbit-dynamical-system>[orbits], quantized <angular momentum> $\mu vr=n\hbar$ and <photon emission>/absorption with $h\nu=E_i-E_f$. The angular-momentum condition is the standing-wave relation $\boxed{2\pi r=n\lambda_{\mathrm{dB}}}$ for the relative <orbit-dynamical-system>[orbit]. These are additional quantum postulates, not consequences of classical radiation theory.

Coulomb centripetal balance and $\mu vr=n\hbar$ give

===== Solution

A tokamak confines a hot, approximately toroidal <plasma-physics>[plasma] using a strong <toroidal magnetic field> and a poloidal field associated with <plasma-physics>[plasma] current and external coils. Helical field lines and suitable magnetic surfaces reduce rapid transverse escape. D-T fusion releases 17.6 MeV, mostly in an uncharged <neutron> that escapes magnetic confinement and transfers <energy> to a surrounding blanket. A <heat exchanger> and thermal power cycle convert some of that <energy> into electricity.

Essential systems include the vacuum vessel, toroidal and poloidal magnets, current-drive/transformer and auxiliary heating equipment, fueling and exhaust systems, divertor for concentrated <heat> and particle removal, neutron-absorbing/breeding blanket and shielding. <Alpha particle>[Alpha particles] retained in the <plasma-physics>[plasma] contribute self-heating. Confinement and controlled exhaust are as important as reaching a high <temperature>; a <magnetic field> does not confine the <Neutron>[neutrons] or make material <heat> loads vanish.

Lithium in the blanket breeds tritium through <neutron> reactions, notably $^6\mathrm{Li}+n\to{}^4\mathrm{He}+T$. <Homogeneity> makes the inertial transformation linear. Align origins at $t=t'=0$, let the second origin have <velocity> $v$, and impose <isotropy> and reciprocity. Then $x'=\gamma(v)(x-vt)$, $y'=y$, $z'=z$. If the same limiting signal <speed> $c$ has trajectories $x=\pm ct$ in both systems, substituting these two trajectories fixes $t'=\gamma(v)(t-vx/c^2)$. $$
\boxed{x'=\frac{x-vt}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}},\quad t'=\frac{t-vx/c^2}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}},\quad y'=y,\ z'=z.}
$$
Light enters this derivation through the empirical/in-principle operational identification of the invariant <speed> and <Einstein synchronization>. Group, <homogeneity> and <isotropy> arguments can instead derive a family with a universal parameter $K$, $t'=\gamma(t-Kvx)$ and $\gamma=(1-Kv^2)^{-1/2}$. They do not by themselves determine whether $K=0$ or a positive finite value. Empirical light propagation or other relativistic dynamics identifies $K=1/c^2$ for our world. Conventionality enters when defining distant <clock> <Einstein synchronisation>[synchronization]. Reparameterizing distant times shows a conventional element in their coordinate representation; it does not eliminate every empirical constraint from relativity. ==== Solution

A <constructive theory> attempts to build observed phenomena from a specified underlying mechanism or constitution. <Kinetic theory>, for example, explains <gas pressure> and thermal behavior through molecular motions and interactions. A <principle theory> begins from general constraints that processes must satisfy and derives restrictions on possible descriptions, rather than first proposing a detailed microscopic mechanism. <Thermodynamics> is an example through its conservation and transformation principles.

Einstein presented <special relativity> in the principle style: equivalence of <Inertial frame>[inertial frames] and the light-speed principle constrain the kinematics and the form of admissible laws. The strategy was useful because existing accounts of electromagnetic phenomena and moving matter were in tension, while a trustworthy constructive account of all rods, clocks and binding <Force>[forces] was unavailable. It allowed broad conclusions without assuming a detailed ether mechanism whose observable effects had not been established.

The distinction is methodological rather than a division between empirical and nonempirical theories. Principles need empirical motivation and coherent interpretation, while constructive models also rely on general assumptions. Nor does a successful <principle theory> make questions about the dynamics of rods and clocks illegitimate. A constructive account may explain how particular material systems realize the constraints, provided it reproduces the same operational relations.

Einstein's reason for introducing the distinction was therefore partly to explain the character and limitations of the relativistic strategy. It supplied a way to unify and constrain <Physical law>[physical laws] without prematurely asserting an underlying mechanical model. It was not a proof that constructive explanations can never be valuable or that relativistic effects are only coordinate conventions.<What Is the Theory of Relativity? by Albert Einstein>[1919, constructive versus principle theories and the thermodynamic analogy]{ref}

The <measurement problem> arises from applying quantum dynamics to both a measured system and a physical apparatus. If $|0\rangle|M_*\rangle\to|0\rangle|M_0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle|M_*\rangle\to|1\rangle|M_1\rangle$, linearity maps a <superposition> to $\alpha|0,M_0\rangle+\beta|1,M_1\rangle$. That is generally not one selected apparatus record, although ordinary experience and the usual stochastic outcome rule assign a definite record in each run.

The conflict concerns the combination of a complete <wavefunction> description, universal <unitary> evolution and a unique ordinary outcome. They cannot all be used naively in the indicated example. Declaring that a measurement collapses the state can be a practical predictive rule, but a complete physical account must say when that nonunitary rule applies and why the apparatus is exempt from the system's ordinary equation.

<Decoherence> helps by correlating records with a large environment and suppressing observable interference between them. It explains approximate stability of record bases and why coherent reversals are hard, but the <reduced density matrix> is an <improper mixture> arising from an <entangled state>. Suppression of off-diagonal terms alone does not select one global component.

Physicists can make excellent laboratory predictions while leaving this issue open. It still matters when extending the theory to universal systems, precision tests of collapse, or claims about what the state represents. Responses include new configurations and guidance, modified stochastic dynamics or branch-relative outcomes in a <unitary> theory. These differ in what they assert physically and sometimes in additional testable effects. The problem is therefore not simply ignorance of which detector clicked, but the relation between a purportedly universal law and the definite evidence used to test it.

==== Solution

In the <many-worlds interpretation>, the universal state evolves unitarily. A measurement transforms $(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle)|M_*\rangle$ into $\alpha|0,M_0\rangle+\beta|1,M_1\rangle$. <Environmental decoherence> suppresses interference between macroscopically distinct records, so each branch contains a consistent observer record of one outcome. There is no stochastic selection of a unique global result in that dynamics. Thus fundamental indeterministic collapse is not recovered; it is deliberately absent.

The appearance of indeterminism can nevertheless be described branch-relatively. Before examining a record, an observer lacks information about which recorded result will be encountered in a future branch-relative history. After measurement, each descendant has one stable record and experiences a definite result. This is more than saying that a classical observer does not know a predetermined hidden bit, because the global state includes multiple continuing record structures rather than one unknown selected trajectory.

The hard question is why uncertainty or rational expectation should follow the <Born rule>[Born weights] $|\alpha|^2,|\beta|^2$. <Decoherence> explains approximately autonomous records and a preferred coarse-grained basis, but does not by itself turn all branch counts into a unique measure. Literal counting is ill-defined because branches do not have a uniquely fixed number. Decision-theoretic, <symmetry> or typicality arguments try to justify squared-amplitude weights and their role in observed frequencies, but their auxiliary assumptions must be assessed rather than silently called consequences of <unitary> dynamics alone.

The interpretation can therefore account structurally for the appearance of definite unpredictable outcomes without adding dynamical randomness. Whether that completely accounts for <probability> depends on a successful connection from branch weights to expectation and confirmation. Its strength is a uniform dynamical law for system and observer; its cost is an ontology and <probability> account that go beyond the experience of one apparently unique outcome.<Relative State Formulation of Quantum Mechanics by Hugh Everett III>[1957, measurement correlations, relative states and statistical predictions]{ref}

<Bohmian mechanics> retains definite particle positions and deterministic trajectories, which makes it resemble <classical mechanics> at one level. But the guiding dynamics depends on a <wavefunction> obeying the <Schrödinger equation>, with <velocity> schematically $\dot Q_i=(\hbar/m_i)\operatorname{Im}(\nabla_i\psi/\psi)(Q)$. Writing $\psi=R e^{iS/\hbar}$ also yields a Hamilton-Jacobi-like equation with <quantum potential> $Q=-\sum_i\hbar^2\nabla_i^2R/(2m_iR)$. The <quantum potential> is not generally a sum of ordinary <Pair potential>[pair potentials] in three-space; its configuration dependence carries the nonclassical correlations. A stationary real bound-state <wavefunction> can give a stationary particle configuration, unlike the <orbit-dynamical-system>[orbit] one might infer from a classical Coulomb picture. Measurement outcomes are contextual: the actual apparatus interaction helps determine which position record is produced.

With the quantum-equilibrium distribution $\rho=|\psi|^2$, the guidance and <Wave equation>[wave equations] preserve that distribution and reproduce the usual <quantum statistics>. The significant departure is therefore not simply replacing chance by hidden classical motion. In quantum <equilibrium> the distribution of those occupied outcome regions is the Born distribution. Thus the account supplies a definite record without requiring stochastic <wavefunction> collapse, while paying the costs of added configuration ontology, nonlocal guidance and a justified <equilibrium> measure.
