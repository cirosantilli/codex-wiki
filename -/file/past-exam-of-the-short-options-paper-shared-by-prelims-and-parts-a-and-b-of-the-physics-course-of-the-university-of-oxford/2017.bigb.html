===== Solution

<Complex differentiability> gives $u_x=v_y$, $u_y=-v_x$. Differentiating once more, for <continuously differentiable> first <Derivative>[derivatives] with the needed <Second derivative>[second derivatives], yields $u_{xx}+u_{yy}=v_{yx}-v_{xy}=0$ and $v_{xx}+v_{yy}=-u_{yx}+u_{xy}=0$. Thus both components satisfy <Laplace equation>[Laplace's equation]. The result is local on the domain of <holomorphy>; it makes no assertion across a <branch cut>.

==== 2
{scope}

===== Solution

Choose a continuous <argument> $\theta$ on a <simply connected domain> avoiding zero, and put $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. With $u=x\log r-y\theta$, direct <differentiation> gives
$$
u_x=1+\log r,\quad u_y=-\theta,\quad u_{xx}=x/r^2,\quad u_{yy}=-x/r^2.
$$
Here $\theta_x=-y/r^2$, $\theta_y=x/r^2$; these identities specify the branch needed in the written <arctangent>. Integrating the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> gives
$$
\boxed{f(z)=z\operatorname{Log}z+iC,\qquad v=y\log r+x\theta+C,\quad C\in\mathbb R.}
$$
The literal principal $\arctan(y/x)$ works only separately on suitable half-planes. It cannot stand for a single continuous <argument> across the <imaginary axis>.

==== 3
{scope}

===== Solution

For the <upper-half-plane-complex-analysis>[upper half-plane] use $0<\theta<\pi$, and interpret the source's <arctangent> as this <argument>. Then $V=(V_0/\pi)\operatorname{Im}(z\operatorname{Log}z)$ is <harmonic>, so $\mathbf E=-\nabla V$ has zero curl and divergence where there is no <Electric charge>[charge]. On the positive boundary $\theta\to0$ and $y\log r\to0$; on the negative boundary $\theta\to\pi$. Hence the two <Boundary value>[boundary values] are respectively zero and $V_0x$.

**Branch qualification:** the principal real <arctangent> alone gives zero on *both* sides of $y=0$, contradicting the stated negative-axis <boundary condition>, for example at $x=-1$. The continuous upper-half-plane <argument> repairs this notation; it is not an optional <additive constant>.

==== 4
{scope}

===== Solution

Along a <level curve>, $V_x dx+V_y dy=0$, with $V_x=V_0\theta/\pi$ and $V_y=V_0(1+\log r)/\pi$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{\theta}{1+\log r}.}
$$
In the intended <upper-half-plane-complex-analysis>[upper half-plane] $0<\theta<\pi$, the slope is positive for $0<r<e^{-1}$, vertical at $r=e^{-1}$, and negative for $r>e^{-1}$. At the positive real boundary $\theta=0$ the slope is zero except at the <stationary point> $(e^{-1},0)$, where both <Derivative>[derivatives] vanish and the quotient is indeterminate. More generally, outside the chosen <half-plane> its sign is the sign of $-\theta(1+\log r)$, with the <argument> branch held fixed. A convenient parametric description of the curves to sketch is $V\pi/V_0=r(\sin\theta\log r+\theta\cos\theta)$.

===== Solution

A <branch point> obstructs a single-valued <analytic continuation> around a closed circuit. Continuing $\log z=\log|z|+i\theta$ once around zero increases its value by $2\pi i$. A <branch cut> removes a curve joining <Branch point>[branch points] or extending to infinity so that a continuous <argument> can be chosen on the remainder. Zero and infinity are <Logarithmic branch point>[logarithmic branch points]; the positive-axis cut used here selects $0<\arg z<2\pi$. The cut is a choice of representation, not a line of <Pole>[poles] of the multivalued <logarithm>.

==== 2
{scope}

===== Solution

The large circle contributes $O((\log R)^n/R)$ and the small circle contributes $O(\epsilon|\log\epsilon|^n)$, so both disappear. The enclosed <Simple pole>[simple poles] are $i$ and $-i$, with <Logarithm>[logarithms] $i\pi/2$ and $3i\pi/2$. Their <Residue>[residues] are $(i\pi/2)^n/(2i)$ and $-(3i\pi/2)^n/(2i)$. The positive orientation of the keyhole therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\oint_\Gamma\frac{(\operatorname{Log}z)^n}{1+z^2}\,dz
=\pi^{n+1}(i/2)^n(1-3^n).}
$$
The upper bank runs outward and the lower bank inward. Their <Logarithm>[logarithms] differ by $2\pi i$; keeping that orientation is essential for the sign.

==== 3
{scope}

===== Solution

Taking the difference of the two bank <Integral>[integrals] at $n=2$ gives
$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{(\log x)^2-(\log x+2\pi i)^2}{1+x^2}\,dx
=4\pi^2 I_0-4\pi i I_1=2\pi^3.
$$
The $I_j$ are real and convergent. Comparing real and <Imaginary part>[imaginary parts] proves $\boxed{I_0=\pi/2,\ I_1=0}$. Independently, $x\mapsto1/x$ changes the sign of each odd logarithmic moment and preserves the measure, so every odd $I_j$ vanishes.

==== 4
{scope}

===== Solution

At $n=3$ the bank difference is $-6\pi i I_2+12\pi^2 I_1+8\pi^3 i I_0$, while the <residue> result is $13i\pi^4/4$. Substitution of $I_0,I_1$ yields $I_2=\pi^3/8$.

At $n=5$, the odd moments vanish and the remaining relation is
$$
-10\pi i I_4+80\pi^3 i I_2-32\pi^5 i I_0=-\frac{121}{16}i\pi^6.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{I_2=\frac{\pi^3}{8},\qquad I_4=\frac{5\pi^5}{32}.}
$$
As an independent generating-function check, $F(s)=\int_0^\infty x^s/(1+x^2)\,dx=\pi/[2\cos(\pi s/2)]$ for $|\operatorname{Re}s|<1$. Differentiating twice and four times at zero gives the same moments.

For a <meromorphic function> with no <Pole>[poles] on a positively oriented closed <contour>, the <contour integral> equals $2\pi i$ times the sum of enclosed <Residue>[residues], weighted by <winding number> for a general <contour>. ==== 2
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $z=e^{i\theta}$, so $d\theta=dz/(iz)$, $\cos\theta=(z+z^{-1})/2$ and $\sin\theta=(z-z^{-1})/(2i)$. Rational expressions in these <Trigonometric function>[trigonometric functions] become rational <contour> integrands on the <unit circle>. The method is especially useful when the resulting denominator has a small number of identifiable <Pole>[poles]. A <pole> on the <integration> circle requires a separately specified <principal value> or indentation; the <residue theorem> does not automatically assign an ordinary <integral> there.

==== 3
{scope}

===== Solution

First remove the removable singularities introduced by tangent and secant: the integrand is $\cos\theta/(3+\cos\theta+2\sin\theta)$. The denominator stays positive since $3>\sqrt5$. With $z=e^{i\theta}$ the <contour> integrand is
$$
\frac{z^2+1}{iz\{(1-2i)z^2+6z+(1+2i)\}}.
$$
Its interior <Pole>[poles] are zero and $r=-1/(1-2i)$; the other quadratic root is $-5/(1-2i)$ and lies outside. The <Residue>[residues] are $1/[i(1+2i)]$ and $(r^2+1)/(4ir)$, whose sum is $i/10$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{1+2\tan\theta+3\sec\theta}=-\frac\pi5.}
$$
Assigning arbitrary finite values at the isolated removable points does not change the <integral>.

==== 4
{scope}

===== Solution

Split the sine into exponentials. The $e^{iz}$ term closes in the <upper-half-plane-complex-analysis>[upper half-plane] and picks up the <pole> $ia$, giving $2\pi i e^{-a}$; the $e^{-iz}$ term closes clockwise below and encloses no <pole>. <Jordan's lemma> applies to these separate <Contour>[contours]. Hence
$$
\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x-ia}\,dx=\pi e^{-a},\quad a>0.}
$$
To pass to $a\downarrow0$ without an invalid dominated-convergence <argument> on an infinite interval, pair positive and negative $x$ first: the <imaginary part> is odd and the <real part> is $2\int_0^\infty x\sin x/(x^2+a^2)dx$. On a bounded interval the limit is harmless, while <integration by parts> bounds the oscillatory tail uniformly for $0<a\leq1$. This gives the convergent improper <Dirichlet integral> $\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^\infty\sin x/x\,dx=\pi}$.

For two <isolated point> masses in a Newtonian <bound orbit>, $\boxed{P^2=4\pi^2a^3/[G(M_s+M_p)]}$, where $a$ is the relative <orbit-dynamical-system>[orbit]'s <semimajor axis>. The <mass> in the denominator is the total gravitating <mass>, not the planet's <mass> alone.

==== 2
{scope}

===== Solution

Circular edge-on motion gives $V_s=2\pi a_s/P$ and center-of-mass balance gives $a_s=aM_p/(M_s+M_p)$. Eliminate $a$ using the preceding period relation:
$$
\frac{M_p^3}{(M_s+M_p)^2}=\frac{PV_s^3}{2\pi G}.
$$
Thus $\boxed{M_p^3\simeq PV_s^3M_s^2/(2\pi G)}$ when $M_p\ll M_s$. For a non-edge-on <orbit-dynamical-system>[orbit] the measured <velocity> instead determines $M_p\sin i$ to this accuracy; neglecting eccentricity is also part of the stipulated model.

===== Solution

A <luminosity> does not determine a stellar <mass> without a <Mass-luminosity relation>[mass-luminosity] <calibration-differential-geometry>[calibration]. Using the usual approximate <Main sequence>[main-sequence] law $L/L_\odot=(M_s/M_\odot)^\alpha$ gives $M_s=3^{1/\alpha}M_\odot$. With $\alpha=3.5$, the numerical results are

The angular <radius> follows directly from $a(\mathrm{AU})/d(\mathrm{pc})$. The <effective temperature> is defined by $\boxed{L=4\pi R_*^2\sigma T_{\mathrm{eff}}^4}$, with <bolometric luminosity> $L$, photospheric <radius> $R_*$ and <Stefan-Boltzmann constant> $\sigma$. It is the blackbody <temperature> giving the same total <surface flux>, not a claim that each <spectral line> forms at that <temperature>.

===== Solution

The parallax gives $d=20$ pc and the <distance modulus> gives $M_{\mathrm{bol}}=-0.641-5\log_{10}2=-2.146$. Therefore $L/L_\odot=10^{(4.72-M_{\mathrm{bol}})/2.5}\simeq558$. Taking $T_\odot\simeq5772$ K,

==== 3
{scope}

===== Solution

A continuum peak gives a color <temperature> through Wien's law, provided the continuum is approximately Planckian and extinction is corrected. Broad-band colors or a fitted <spectral energy distribution> compare flux ratios with model atmospheres; they require a reddening estimate, abundance and gravity assumptions, and calibrated passbands. Spectroscopic line ratios or excitation/ionization balances provide another <thermometer>, requiring atomic data and a suitable atmosphere model, often <local thermodynamic equilibrium>.

For this cool giant, line blanketing and molecular absorption substantially distort a simple continuum peak, so Wien's law is the least reliable of these approximations. A detailed spectroscopic analysis, checked against a multi-band atmosphere fit, would generally be the strongest of the three if good data and model parameters are available. There is no unconditional accuracy ranking without <Measurement error>[measurement errors] and model systematics; an accurately measured <angular diameter> plus bolometric flux would offer a more direct effective-temperature determination, if obtainable.

At a fixed cosmological time, homogeneous expansion has $\mathbf v=H(t)\mathbf r$. A comoving galaxy has $\mathbf r=R(t)\mathbf x$ with constant $\mathbf x$, hence $\mathbf v=\dot R\mathbf x=(\dot R/R)\mathbf r$. Thus $\boxed{H=\dot R/R}$. Peculiar velocities must be removed before applying this ideal relation to individual galaxies.

{scope}

===== Solution

Set the spatial-curvature constant to zero in the stipulated matter-only <Friedmann equation> to get $\rho_c=3H^2/(8\pi G)$. Conservation of <pressureless matter> with $R(t_0)=1$ gives $\rho=\rho_0/R^3$, and therefore
$$
\dot R^2=\frac{8\pi G\rho_0}{3R},\qquad R^{3/2}=\frac32\sqrt{\frac{8\pi G\rho_0}{3}}\,t.
$$
Choosing the <big bang> as $t=0$ yields $R=(t/t_0)^{2/3}$ and $H=2/(3t)$. Consequently $\boxed{t_0=2/(3H_0)\simeq9.3\ \mathrm{Gyr}}$ for the supplied $H_0$. ===== Solution

The <Bohr model> assumes Coulomb attraction and classical <circular motion> between transitions; selected stationary <orbit-dynamical-system>[orbits] do not radiate; their <angular momentum> is $mvr=n\hbar$; and a transition exchanges a <photon> of <energy> $h\nu=E_i-E_f$. The first two <Dynamical equation>[dynamical equations] give
$$
\frac{mv^2}r=\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^2},\qquad mvr=n\hbar
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{r_n=\frac{4\pi\epsilon_0\hbar^2n^2}{me^2}.}
$$
Here $n=1,2,\ldots$, $m$ is the <electron mass> under the fixed-proton approximation, $e$ its <Electric charge>[charge] magnitude, and $\epsilon_0$ the <vacuum permittivity>. The stationary-orbit postulate deliberately suspends the classical radiation prediction; this is a historical model, not a consistent deduction from classical electrodynamics.

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

Substitute the quantized <radius> in $mvr=n\hbar$ to get $v_n=e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0\hbar n)$. <Coulomb potential energy> is $-e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0r_n)$ and the stipulated virial relation makes the total one half of this value. Hence
$$
\boxed{v_n=\frac{\alpha c}{n},\qquad E_n=-\frac{me^4}{2(4\pi\epsilon_0)^2\hbar^2n^2}=-\frac{\mathrm{Ry}}{n^2}.}
$$
The negative sign means a <bound state> relative to a separated stationary <proton> and <electron>.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

The emitted <photon> has <energy> $E_3-E_2=\mathrm{Ry}(1/4-1/9)=5\mathrm{Ry}/36$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\frac{36hc}{5\mathrm{Ry}}\simeq656\ \mathrm{nm}.}
$$
This is the Balmer-alpha transition in this approximation. Absorption in the reverse direction has the same <photon energy>; <fine structure> and <Radiative correction>[radiative corrections] are beyond the model.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Separate <center-of-mass motion> and replace $m$ by the <reduced mass> $\mu=mM/(m+M)$ in the relative-coordinate formulas. Then $r_n$ is the proton-electron separation, <Energy level>[energy levels] and the Rydberg constant scale by $\mu/m$, and transition wavelengths increase by $m/\mu$. The <electron>'s own <orbital radius> about the <center of mass> is $Mr_n/(M+m)$; the <proton> <radius> is $mr_n/(M+m)$. The relative <speed> retains $\alpha c/n$, while the <electron>'s individual <speed> acquires $M/(M+m)$. Replacing the <mass> in the <radius> alone but leaving energies unchanged would be inconsistent.

For a stationary nonrelativistic state, $\boxed{[-\hbar^2\nabla^2/(2m)+V(\mathbf r)]\psi=E\psi}$. The <wavefunction> $\psi$ is a <probability amplitude>, $m$ the particle <mass> and $E$ its <energy eigenvalue>; the <potential energy> $V$ is measured in the same <energy> units. ===== Solution

Inside the well, $\psi''+k^2\psi=0$. Vanishing at zero removes the cosine term; vanishing at $L$ requires $k_nL=n\pi$. The nonzero states have <positive integer> $n$ because $n=0$ gives the <zero function> and negative $n$ only repeats a state up to sign. <Normalization> follows from $\int_0^L\sin^2(n\pi x/L)dx=L/2$. The <wavefunction> vanishes outside the infinite barriers, so there is no additional contribution to its <norm>.

{scope}

===== Solution

A sudden wall displacement leaves the initial <wavefunction> unchanged: it is $\sqrt{2/L}\sin(\pi x/L)$ on $[0,L]$ and zero on $(L,2L]$. It is not an <eigenfunction> of the new <Hamiltonian>, so there is **no single certain new <energy>**. ===== Solution

For a fixed propagation direction and spatial mode, a <photon> has two independent <transverse polarization> <Amplitude>[amplitudes]. Orthogonal horizontal and vertical states span its two-dimensional <Hilbert space>: $\alpha|H\rangle+\beta|V\rangle$, with $|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1$. <polarization-waves>[Polarization] analyzers measure bases in this space, and <Phase plate>[phase plates] implement transformations of its relative <Amplitude>[amplitudes]. Additional spatial or <frequency> modes enlarge the space and must not be silently discarded.

==== 2
{scope}

===== Solution

Choose the upper route for horizontal <polarization-waves>[polarization], the lower route for vertical <polarization-waves>[polarization], and absorb fixed optical phases into the path kets. The normalized output is
$$
\boxed{\frac{|H\rangle|\uparrow_1\rangle+|V\rangle|\downarrow_1\rangle}{\sqrt2}.}
$$
This is <entanglement> between two <degrees of freedom> of one <photon>. Interchanging the path convention interchanges the labels but not any probabilities.

==== 3
{scope}

===== Solution

Orthogonal path projectors give an upper-path <probability> $1/2$. A successful non-destructive lower-path detection projects onto $|V\rangle|\downarrow_1\rangle$ and its conditional <polarization-waves>[polarization] is therefore pure vertical. Retaining the detection record removes the upper <amplitude> for that run; it would be incorrect to continue evolving the original coherent two-path state as though the detection had not occurred.

==== 4
{scope}

===== Solution

A <Waveplate>[quarter-wave plate] needs a specified axis angle. If its fast axis makes angle $\eta$ to horizontal, choose its <Jones matrix> $Q=R(\eta)\operatorname{diag}(1,i)R(-\eta)$. Then
$$
Q|V\rangle=a|H\rangle+b|V\rangle,\quad a=(1-i)\sin\eta\cos\eta,\quad b=\sin^2\eta+i\cos^2\eta.
$$
After the preceding successful lower-path detection the total state is $(aH+bV)|\downarrow_1\rangle$. The next crystal sends it to
$$
\boxed{a|H,\uparrow_2\rangle+b|V,\downarrow_2\rangle,\quad P(\downarrow_2\mid\downarrow_1)=|b|^2.}
$$
For axes at $45^\circ$, the state before splitting is circular up to phase, $a=(1-i)/2$, $b=(1+i)/2$, and the <probability> is $1/2$. Detection in $\downarrow_2$ always leaves vertical <polarization-waves>[polarization]. If no earlier lower-path measurement was actually performed, retain the untouched upper <amplitude>: the final state is $(|H,\uparrow_1\rangle+a|H,\uparrow_2\rangle+b|V,\downarrow_2\rangle)/\sqrt2$, and the last <probability> is $|b|^2/2$, or $1/4$ at $45^\circ$. At axis angle zero the <conditional probability> is instead one, a concrete demonstration that an unspecified orientation does not determine a unique number.

===== Solution

In <cylindrical coordinates> the orthogonal <velocity> components are $\dot r$, $r\dot\theta$, $\dot z$. Their squared magnitudes add, and $\dot{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf A=\dot z A(r)$. Thus $\boxed{\mathcal L=m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2+\dot z^2)/2+q\dot z A(r)}$. Taking the curl gives $B_\theta=-A'(r)$, consistent with the prescribed field.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

<Velocity> <differentiation> gives $\boxed{p_r=m\dot r,\ p_\theta=mr^2\dot\theta,\ p_z=m\dot z+qA(r)}$. The coordinates $\theta$ and $z$ are cyclic, so $p_\theta,p_z$ are conserved. The radial <momentum> generally changes. In particular the conserved axial <canonical momentum> differs from the axial <mechanical momentum> by $qA$.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

The <Legendre transform> cancels the velocity-linear magnetic term. Expressing velocities in terms of <momenta> gives
$$
\boxed{H=\frac{p_r^2}{2m}+\frac{p_\theta^2}{2mr^2}+\frac{[p_z-qA(r)]^2}{2m}.}
$$
The <Hamiltonian> is time independent and hence conserved. It equals <kinetic energy> because a static <magnetic field> does no <work>; its dependence on $A$ is through the <mechanical momentum> combination.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

The <initial condition> makes $p_\theta=0$ at all times. Accessible radii obey $|p_z-qA(r)|\leq\sqrt{2mH}$. For $q>0$ and $p_z>\sqrt{2mH}$, monotonicity of $A$ gives exactly the two stated turning equations and confinement to the interval between their unique roots.

The squared condition alone omits a sign qualification. More generally both <Positive root>[positive roots] require $p_z/q>0$, and are the sorted roots $A(r)=(p_z\pm\sqrt{2mH})/q$. For example $q=-1$, $A=r$, $p_z=-3$, $2mH=1$ confines the <orbit-dynamical-system>[orbit] to $2\leq r\leq4$, but the source's named $r_1,r_2$ equations reverse those labels. This is a convention issue, not a failure of confinement.

==== e
{scope}

===== Solution

Expand $A(r)=A(r_0)+A'(r_0)s+O(s^2)$ about $qA(r_0)=p_z$. Then $p_z-qA\simeq-qA'(r_0)s$ and <conservation of energy> gives
$$
\boxed{\dot s^2+\Omega^2s^2=\frac{2H}{m}=\Omega^2\rho^2,\quad \rho=\frac{\sqrt{2H/m}}{|\Omega|},\quad s(t)=\rho\cos(|\Omega|t+\phi).}
$$
Here $\Omega=qA'(r_0)/m$ can be signed, but the physical <oscillation> <frequency> and <amplitude> are nonnegative. The small-energy condition ensures that this local expansion samples only a narrow range about $r_0$, provided the field varies smoothly there.

Bell's theorem makes precise an obstruction to reproducing quantum correlations with a local causal completion. Let measurement settings be $a,b$, outcomes $A,B=\pm1$ and a more complete state $\lambda$. Bell locality factorizes $P(A,B|a,b,\lambda)=P(A|a,\lambda)P(B|b,\lambda)$; setting independence takes the distribution of $\lambda$ to be independent of the freely chosen settings. These assumptions imply a <Bell inequality>. For example deterministic assignments satisfy $|A(B+B')+A'(B-B')|\leq2$, and averaging gives the <CHSH inequality>[CHSH] bound $|S|\leq2$. The stochastic version follows by averaging local response probabilities too.

An entangled <Spin singlet state>[spin singlet] predicts $E(a,b)=-a\cdot b$ and suitable four directions give $|S|=2\sqrt2$. Thus the quantum predictions are incompatible with the stated local model, not merely with an unnecessarily specific mechanical hidden-variable proposal. Perfect anticorrelation in the EPR <argument> had motivated inferring pre-existing properties from remote predictions under locality and a completeness criterion. Bell shows that retaining such a local completion cannot recover the broader quantum correlation pattern.

It would overstate the result to say that it proves the impossibility of every hidden-variable theory. <Bohmian mechanics> supplies a nonlocal example, and rejecting setting independence or changing the causal model changes the theorem's premises. <Quantum mechanics> has a highly successful predictive calculus, but its standard informal presentation does not transparently supply one consistent physical description of all processes. An <isolated system> evolves unitarily, while a measurement is often said to select one outcome stochastically.
