= Solution

The <classification of compact surfaces> states that every compact connected <topological surface> without boundary is <homeomorphic> to exactly one of the <sphere>, a <connected sum of surfaces> consisting of $g\geq1$ <tori>, or a connected sum of $h\geq1$ real <projective planes>. The <torus> family is <orientable> and the projective-plane family is nonorientable; their respective genera are unique. Including $g=0$ makes the <sphere> the <genus>-zero <orientable> case. If boundaries are allowed, the number of removed open discs is an additional invariant.

= ii
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

A finite subdivision into vertices, edges and polygonal faces defines the <Euler characteristic>
$$
\boxed{\chi(M)=V-E+F.}
$$
Count cells after all identifications. Subdividing an edge adds one vertex and one edge; splitting a face adds one edge and one face. Each operation leaves $V-E+F$ unchanged. Comparing subdivisions through a common refinement proves independence of the subdivision.

{parent=1}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For the <connected sum of surfaces>, remove an embedded open disc from each <surface> and identify the resulting boundary circles by a homeomorphism. The resulting closed connected <surface> is denoted $M\#N$.

Removing a disc decreases the <Euler characteristic> by one. Gluing the remaining surfaces along their common boundary subtracts the characteristic of a circle, which is zero. Thus
$$
\boxed{\chi(M\#N)=\chi(M)+\chi(N)-2.}
$$
This can be verified by choosing matching subdivisions of the boundary circles and counting the vertices and edges identified in the gluing.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

A square model of the <torus> has one vertex, two edges and one face, so its <Euler characteristic> is zero. The connected-sum formula gives
$$
\boxed{\chi(\#^gT^2)=g\cdot0-2(g-1)=2-2g.}
$$
The real <projective plane> has a bigon model with one vertex, one edge and one face, hence characteristic one. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\chi(\#^h\mathbb{RP}^2)=h-2(h-1)=2-h.}
$$

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=v}

Take $M=T^2$, $N=\mathbb{RP}^2\#\mathbb{RP}^2$ and $S=\mathbb{RP}^2$. The first is <orientable>, while $N$, the <Klein bottle>, is not, so $M$ and $N$ are not <homeomorphic>.

Both $M\#S$ and $N\#S$ are nonorientable and have <Euler characteristic> $-1$. By the <classification of compact surfaces>, both are homeomorphic to the connected sum of three projective planes. Hence
$$
\boxed{M\#S\cong N\#S,\qquad M\not\cong N.}
$$
This is <failure of cancellation for connected sums of surfaces>.

For a nonconstant <holomorphic map> of degree $d$ between compact connected <Riemann surfaces>, the <Riemann-Hurwitz formula> is
$$
\boxed{2-2g_X=d(2-2g_Y)-\sum_{p\in X}(e_p-1).}
$$
Here $e_p$ is the <local degree>, characterized by local coordinates in which the map is $z\mapsto z^{e_p}$. The sum is finite and its terms are nonnegative; a <ramification point> has $e_p>1$.

= ii
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

If $g_X=0$, the <Riemann-Hurwitz formula> gives $2=d(2-2g_Y)-R$, where $R\geq0$. If $g_Y\geq1$, the right side is nonpositive, a contradiction. Therefore
$$
\boxed{g_Y=0.}
$$

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The given <Weierstrass elliptic function> is even, by replacing the lattice index $\omega$ with $-\omega$. Differentiation gives
$$
\wp'(z)=-2\sum_{\omega\in\Lambda}(z-\omega)^{-3}.
$$
For any $\lambda\in\Lambda$, reindexing the sum shows $\wp'(z+\lambda)=\wp'(z)$. Thus $\wp(z+\lambda)-\wp(z)=c_\lambda$ is constant off the lattice. Replacing $z$ by $-z-\lambda$ and using evenness changes this difference to its negative, so $c_\lambda=-c_\lambda$ and $c_\lambda=0$. Hence **$\wp$ is doubly periodic**.

It therefore descends to a <meromorphic function> on $\mathbb C/\Lambda$, with a double pole at zero modulo the lattice and no others. A <meromorphic function> is a <holomorphic map> to the <Riemann sphere>, sending poles to infinity. Locally at zero, $1/\wp(z)=z^2+O(z^4)$ is holomorphic and vanishes there, so the sphere chart at infinity supplies the required extension. The map is nonconstant because it has a pole.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

The total pole order in one period cell is two, so the map has degree two. The <Riemann-Hurwitz formula> for <genus> one to <genus> zero gives $0=2\cdot2-R$, hence $R=4$. Each <local degree> is at most two, so every ramification contribution is exactly one. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\deg\wp=2,\qquad\text{four ramification points}.}
$$
They are zero and the three nonzero half-period classes. Zero is ramified because its reciprocal has a double zero. At a nonzero half-period, oddness and periodicity of $\wp'$ force $\wp'=0$; the count shows these exhaust the remaining points.

For a regular parametrization $r(u,v)$, the <first fundamental form> is the induced metric
$$
\boxed{ds^2=E\,du^2+2F\,du\,dv+G\,dv^2,\quad E=r_u\cdot r_u,\ F=r_u\cdot r_v,\ G=r_v\cdot r_v.}
$$
It measures the lengths and angles of tangent vectors. A <geodesic> is a curve whose unit tangent has zero covariant derivative along itself. For an arc-length parametrized <surface> curve in Euclidean three-space, this means its acceleration is normal to the <surface>, or $\gamma''\cdot r_u=\gamma''\cdot r_v=0$.

= ii
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Since $\gamma'=r_uu'+r_vv'$, we have $\gamma'\cdot r_u=Eu'+Fv'$. The normal-acceleration characterization of a <geodesic> gives
$$
\frac d{ds}(Eu'+Fv')=\gamma'\cdot(r_{uu}u'+r_{uv}v').
$$
Expanding and differentiating the metric coefficients yields $r_u\cdot r_{uu}=E_u/2$, $r_v\cdot r_{uu}+r_u\cdot r_{uv}=F_u$, and $r_v\cdot r_{uv}=G_u/2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac d{ds}(Eu'+Fv')=\tfrac12E_u(u')^2+F_uu'v'+\tfrac12G_u(v')^2.}
$$
Applying the same calculation to $\gamma'\cdot r_v=Fu'+Gv'$ gives
$$
\boxed{\frac d{ds}(Fu'+Gv')=\tfrac12E_v(u')^2+F_vu'v'+\tfrac12G_v(v')^2.}
$$
These are also the <Euler-Lagrange equations> of the quadratic metric energy.

= iii
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For the <surface of revolution>, the arc-length condition on its profile gives $E=1$, $F=0$, $G=f(u)^2$. Thus the <geodesic equations> reduce to
$$
u''=ff'(v')^2,\qquad\frac d{ds}(f^2v')=0.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{f(u)^2v'=\text{constant},}
$$
the <Clairaut first integral for a surface of revolution>.

On a meridian, take $v=b$ and $u=\pm s+u_0$. Both equations hold, so **every meridian is a <geodesic>**. On a cross-section $u=c$, <arc length> requires $v'=\pm1/f(c)$. The first equation then holds exactly when $f'(c)=0$, while the second holds automatically. Hence
$$
\boxed{u=c\text{ is a geodesic}\iff f'(c)=0.}
$$

= Solution

For every $q\in L$, the line $pq$ meets the nonsingular <conic> in two points counted with multiplicity by the <Bézout theorem>. One is $p$; define $\alpha(q)$ as the second, using $\alpha(q)=p$ for the unique tangent line at $p$. Conversely, for $r\in C\setminus\{p\}$, intersect the line $pr$ with $L$, and send $p$ itself to the intersection of its tangent with $L$. Since $p\notin L$, each intersection with $L$ is unique. These constructions are inverse, so **$\alpha:L\to C$ is a bijection** preserving the stated collinearity. This is <parametrization of a conic by projection>.

For the particular <conic> take $p=[1:0:1]$ and $L=\{x=0\}$. If $q=[0:s:t]$, points on $pq$ have coordinates $[a:bs:a+bt]$. Substitution into the <conic> gives
$$
b\bigl[-a(s+2t)+b(s^2-t^2)\bigr]=0.
$$
Taking the second intersection gives the explicit map
$$
\boxed{\alpha([0:s:t])=[s^2-t^2:s(s+2t):s^2+st+t^2].}
$$
The three coordinates do not vanish simultaneously. If $s+2t=0$, the formula gives $p$, as required for the tangent direction.

For rational points, put $r=s/t$ when $t\ne0$. The full affine list is
$$
\boxed{x=\frac{r^2-1}{r^2+r+1},\qquad y=\frac{r(r+2)}{r^2+r+1},\qquad r\in\mathbb Q,}
$$
together with the parameter $r=\infty$, which gives $(x,y)=(1,1)$. The denominator is $(r+1/2)^2+3/4>0$ for rational $r$. Conversely, every rational point other than $p$ determines a rational joining line and hence a rational point of $L$; the point $p$ is obtained at $r=-2$. Thus this lists **all rational solutions**.

An <inflection point of an algebraic plane curve> is a smooth point where the tangent has <intersection multiplicity> at least three. For a homogeneous defining polynomial $P$ of degree $d$, its <Hessian curve> is defined by the determinant of the matrix of second partial derivatives, of degree $3(d-2)$. In characteristic zero, the smooth inflection points are exactly the smooth points of $C\cap\operatorname{Hess}(C)$.

For a local verification, move a smooth point to $[0:0:1]$ and its tangent to $y=0$, and scale so $P(x,y,1)=y+ax^2+bxy+cy^2+\cdots$. The tangent contact is at least three exactly when $a=0$. The homogeneous Euler identities give the Hessian matrix at that point as $\begin{pmatrix}2a&b&0\\b&2c&d-1\\0&d-1&0\end{pmatrix}$, whose determinant is $-2a(d-1)^2$. This proves the criterion for $d>1$.

For the given cubic, the singularity equations are $x^2+\lambda yz=y^2+\lambda xz=z^2+\lambda xy=0$. No coordinate can vanish at a projective solution, since that would force all three to vanish. Multiplying the equations therefore gives $1=(-\lambda)^3$, so singularity requires $\lambda^3=-1$. Conversely, under that condition the point $[1:1:-1/\lambda]$ satisfies all three equations, and the Euler identity places it on the cubic. Thus
$$
\boxed{C\text{ is nonsingular}\iff\lambda^3\ne-1.}
$$

{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The second-derivative matrix is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}6x&3\lambda z&3\lambda y\\3\lambda z&6y&3\lambda x\\3\lambda y&3\lambda x&6z\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its determinant is $54[(4+\lambda^3)xyz-\lambda^2(x^3+y^3+z^3)]$. Hence the <Hessian curve> has equation
$$
\boxed{\lambda^2(x^3+y^3+z^3)-(\lambda^3+4)xyz=0.}
$$
If $\lambda=0$, this is the union $xyz=0$ of three lines and is singular. If $\lambda\ne0$, divide by $\lambda^2$ and write it as a Hesse cubic with parameter $\mu=-(\lambda^3+4)/(3\lambda^2)$. The preceding singularity criterion gives $\mu^3=-1$, equivalently
$$
(\lambda^3+4)^3-27\lambda^6=(\lambda^3+1)(\lambda^3-8)^2=0.
$$
Thus the complete condition is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Hess}(C)\text{ singular}\iff\lambda=0\text{ or }\lambda^3=-1\text{ or }\lambda^3=8.}
$$
The other values beyond the source's first family are zero and the three cube roots of eight.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

On the nonsingular cubic, $x^3+y^3+z^3=-3\lambda xyz$. Substituting this into the Hessian equation leaves $-4(\lambda^3+1)xyz=0$. Since $\lambda^3+1\ne0$, a flex has a zero coordinate. The remaining cubic equation then gives the nine points
$$
\boxed{[0:1:-\omega^k],\quad[1:0:-\omega^k],\quad[1:-\omega^k:0],\qquad k=0,1,2,}
$$
where $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$. All are distinct, smooth, and lie on both the cubic and its <Hessian curve>, so they are exactly the <inflection points>. In particular the nine flexes are independent of $\lambda$ within the nonsingular family. This is the <flex configuration of the Hesse pencil>.

A <divisor on an algebraic curve> is a finite formal integer sum $D=\sum_pn_pp$. Its degree is $\sum_pn_p$. The <Riemann-Roch space> $L(D)$ consists of zero and the meromorphic functions $h$ with $(h)+D\geq0$, and $\ell(D)=\dim L(D)$. The <Riemann-Roch theorem> states
$$
\ell(D)-\ell(K-D)=\deg D+1-g,
$$
where $K$ is a <canonical divisor>, of degree $2g-2$. In <genus> one, $\deg(K-D)=-n<0$ for $\deg D=n>0$. A negative-degree <divisor on an algebraic curve> has no nonzero section, since a <principal divisor> has degree zero and an <effective divisor> has nonnegative degree. Hence
$$
\boxed{\ell(D)=n\quad(g=1,\ n>0).}
$$
A <nonsingular plane cubic> has <genus> one by the <genus-degree formula>. Therefore $\ell(p+q)=2$, and there is a nonconstant $f\in L(p+q)$. It has at most simple poles at $p,q$ and no other poles. It cannot have no poles, since a <holomorphic function> on a compact <Riemann surface> is constant. It cannot have just one simple pole either, by the allowed fact, or by $\ell(p)=\ell(q)=1$. Thus **$f$ has simple poles at both $p$ and $q$, and no others**.

{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Apply the preceding argument to $q+r$ to obtain a nonconstant <meromorphic function> $g$ with simple poles exactly at $q,r$. Both $f,g$, and the constant function one, belong to $L(p+q+r)$.

If $a+bf+cg=0$, examination at $p$, where only $f$ has a pole, forces $b=0$. Examination at $r$, where only $g$ has a pole, forces $c=0$, and then $a=0$. Thus the three functions are <linearly independent>. Since the <Riemann-Roch theorem> gives $\ell(p+q+r)=3$,
$$
\boxed{\{1,f,g\}\text{ is a basis of }L(p+q+r).}
$$

= iii
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Away from $p,q,r$, all three coordinate functions are holomorphic and the first is one, so they define a projective point. Near $p$, take a local coordinate $z$ vanishing there. Write $f=a/z+O(1)$ with $a\ne0$, while $g$ is holomorphic. Then
$$
[1:f:g]=[z:zf:zg]\longrightarrow[0:a:0]=[0:1:0].
$$
The scaled coordinates are holomorphic and do not all vanish, proving a holomorphic extension. Similarly $F(r)=[0:0:1]$. At $q$, write $f=b/z+O(1)$ and $g=c/z+O(1)$ with $b,c\ne0$. The same scaling gives $F(q)=[0:b:c]$, again well-defined. Hence
$$
\boxed{F:C\longrightarrow\mathbb P^2\text{ is a well-defined holomorphic map}.}
$$

= Solution

Every degree-three monomial evaluated at $(1,f,g)$ is $f^ag^b$ with $a+b\leq3$. Its possible pole orders at $p,q,r$ are at most $a$, $a+b$, $b$, respectively, and hence at most three each. Therefore $Q(1,f,g)\in L(3p+3q+3r)$.

The <vector space> of homogeneous degree-three polynomials in three variables has <dimension> $\binom52=10$. The target <Riemann-Roch space> has <dimension> nine, because its <divisor on an algebraic curve> has degree nine and <genus> is one. Thus the evaluation <linear map>
$$
Q\longmapsto Q(1,f,g)
$$
has nontrivial kernel. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\text{there is a nonzero homogeneous cubic }Q\text{ with }Q(1,f,g)\equiv0.}
$$

Away from the three poles, the identity from the preceding part puts $F(u)$ on the cubic $Q=0$. The local extensions and continuity put the three pole images there too. Thus **$F(C)\subseteq\{Q=0\}$**, a cubic curve.

The local calculations gave
$$
F(p)=[0:1:0],\qquad F(q)=[0:b:c],\qquad F(r)=[0:0:1],\quad bc\ne0.
$$
They are three distinct points on the line with first coordinate zero. Hence **$F(p),F(q),F(r)$ are collinear**. This is <three-point Riemann-Roch construction of a plane cubic>.
