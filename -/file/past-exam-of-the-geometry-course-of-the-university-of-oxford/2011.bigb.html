= Solution

For a finite <CW complex> with $V$ vertices, $E$ edges and $F$ faces,
$$
\boxed{\chi=V-E+F.}
$$
This <Euler characteristic> is unchanged by subdividing an edge, which adds one vertex and one edge, or subdividing a face by an edge, which adds one edge and one face. Common refinements therefore give the same value for different subdivisions of the same <surface>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Form the <dual graph> of the finite <triangulation>: its vertices are the triangles, and two vertices are joined when their triangles share an edge. Connectedness of the <surface> makes this graph connected. Choose a <spanning tree> and begin with one disjoint triangle. Attach the remaining triangles along the tree edges, one at a time, always joining a new triangle along one boundary edge. At every step the result is a topological disk, so the final union is a polygonal disk.

The edges not used for these attachments still occur twice on the boundary, once for each incident triangle. Identify each pair as in the original <triangulation>. The result recovers the original <surface>. This proves **a compact connected closed <surface> is a polygon with its boundary edges identified in pairs**. Closedness is the convention here; a boundary would leave some edges unpaired.

Count incidences of triangular faces with edges. Every triangle contributes three and every edge of a closed <surface> occurs in two triangles, hence $3F=2E$. Substituting $F=2E/3$ into the <Euler characteristic> gives
$$
\chi=V-E+\frac{2E}{3}=V-\frac E3,
\qquad \boxed{E=3(V-\chi).}
$$

= Solution

The simplicial intersection conditions prohibit loops and multiple edges between the same pair of vertices, so the one-skeleton is a <simple graph> and
$$
E\leq\binom V2.
$$
Using the preceding identity gives $V^2-7V+6\chi\geq0$. A closed <surface> has $\chi\leq2$ and a simplicial <triangulation> has $V\geq4$. The smaller root is at most three, so this inequality forces $V$ to lie above the larger root:
$$
\boxed{V\geq\frac{7+\sqrt{49-24\chi}}2.}
$$
For completeness, fewer than four vertices could give at most one triangle under the intersection rules, which cannot form a closed <surface>.

Here the <surface> is the <real projective plane>, with <Euler characteristic> one. The bound gives $V\geq6$. The following ten triangles on vertices $1,\ldots,6$ attain it:
$$
\boxed{123,\ 134,\ 145,\ 156,\ 126,\ 235,\ 245,\ 246,\ 346,\ 356.}
$$
Take these as the maximal simplices of an abstract <simplicial complex>, identifying edges with the same vertex labels. Every one of the 15 pairs of vertices occurs as an edge in exactly two triangles. The links of the six vertices are single five-cycles; listed cyclically they are
$$
\begin{array}{c|l}
1&2,3,4,5,6\\
2&1,3,5,4,6\\
3&1,2,5,6,4\\
4&1,3,6,2,5\\
5&1,4,2,3,6\\
6&1,5,3,4,2
\end{array}
$$
Thus every point has a disk neighborhood, and the realization is a connected closed <surface>. Its <Euler characteristic> is $6-15+10=1$, so the <classification of compact surfaces> identifies it with the <real projective plane>. This is an explicit <triangulation>, proving $\boxed{V_{\min}=6}$.

For a nonconstant <holomorphic map> $f:X\to Y$ between compact connected <Riemann surfaces>, let $n$ be its degree and $e_p$ its local degree at $p$. The <Riemann–Hurwitz formula> is
$$
\boxed{\chi(X)=n\chi(Y)-\sum_{p\in X}(e_p-1).}
$$
Equivalently, $2g_X-2=n(2g_Y-2)+\sum_p(e_p-1)$. Only finitely many terms are nonzero; their sum is the total ramification. In local coordinates the map has the form $w=z^{e_p}$ after suitable coordinate changes.

Let $\Lambda=\mathbb Z\omega_1+\mathbb Z\omega_2$ and $T=\mathbb C/\Lambda$. Its fundamental parallelogram with opposite sides identified is a <torus>, so $\chi(T)=0$. The <Weierstrass elliptic function> is even and doubly periodic. One way to verify the latter from its series is to differentiate: the locally normally convergent derivative series is
$$
\wp'(z)=-2\sum_{\omega\in\Lambda}(z-\omega)^{-3},
$$
which is absolutely convergent away from the <lattice> and periodic by reindexing. Thus $\wp(z+\omega_j)-\wp(z)$ is constant. Evaluating at $z=-\omega_j/2$ and using evenness shows that this constant is zero for each generating period.

Therefore $[z]\mapsto\wp(z)$ descends to a <meromorphic function> on $T$, with one double pole at $[0]$. Viewed as a map to the <Riemann sphere>, a pole is a holomorphic point in the target coordinate $1/w$. The map is consequently holomorphic everywhere and
$$
\boxed{T\longrightarrow\mathbb{CP}^1,\qquad[z]\longmapsto\wp(z),\qquad\deg\wp=2.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <Riemann–Hurwitz formula> gives total ramification four because $0=2\cdot2-R$. The double pole at $[0]$ has local degree two and contributes one. Since $\wp'$ is odd and periodic, it vanishes at each nonzero point satisfying $[h]=[-h]$. These are
$$
\boxed{\frac{\omega_1}{2},\quad\frac{\omega_2}{2},\quad
\frac{\omega_1+\omega_2}{2}\pmod\Lambda.}
$$
They are three distinct finite points, each contributing at least one to ramification. Together with $[0]$ they exhaust the total of four. Each therefore has local degree exactly two, meaning its derivative has a simple zero. There are **no other zeros of $\wp'$**, and their translates by $\Lambda$ give the full list in the complex plane.

Multiplication by two on the <complex torus> is a holomorphic unramified covering
$$
[2]:T\to T,\qquad[z]\mapsto[2z].
$$
Its <kernel> is $(\tfrac12\Lambda)/\Lambda$, of order four; its derivative is the nonzero constant two. Therefore $\deg[2]=4$. Composition with the degree-two <Weierstrass elliptic function> gives
$$
\boxed{\deg\bigl([z]\mapsto\wp(2z)\bigr)=4\cdot2=8.}
$$
It is well-defined because changing $z$ by $\omega\in\Lambda$ changes $2z$ by the period $2\omega$.

A pole occurs exactly when $2z\in\Lambda$. Thus the poles on $T$ are
$$
\boxed{[0],\ [\omega_1/2],\ [\omega_2/2],\ [(ω_1+ω_2)/2].}
$$
Each is a double pole, since the multiplication-by-two map is locally unramified and $\wp$ has a double pole at $[0]$. There are **four distinct poles, each of order two**, consistent with the degree eight.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Put $R(z)=\tfrac14(\wp''/\wp')^2-2\wp(z)$. This is an <elliptic function>. Its only possible poles modulo $\Lambda$ are $0$ and the three simple zeros $h$ of $\wp'$. At such a nonzero half-period, periodicity and evenness give $\wp(h+t)=\wp(h-t)$, so
$$
\wp'(h+t)=At+O(t^3),\quad A\ne0,
\qquad \frac{\wp''(h+t)}{\wp'(h+t)}=\frac1t+O(t).
$$
It follows that $R(h+t)=1/(4t^2)+O(1)$ with no simple-pole term. This is exactly the principal part of $\wp(2h+2t)=\wp(2t)$.

At zero the source expansion yields
$$
\wp=z^{-2}+O(z^2),\quad\wp'=-2z^{-3}+O(z),\quad
\wp''=6z^{-4}+O(1),\quad\wp''/\wp'=-3z^{-1}+O(z^3).
$$
Consequently $R(z)=1/(4z^2)+O(z^2)$, again matching $\wp(2z)$. Thus $R(z)-\wp(2z)$ is a <holomorphic function> on a compact connected <Riemann surface>, hence constant. Its limit at zero is zero, proving
$$
\boxed{\wp(2z)=\frac14\left(\frac{\wp''(z)}{\wp'(z)}\right)^2-2\wp(z).}
$$
The equality extends over the apparent singularities as an identity of <meromorphic functions>.

For a regular parametrization $\mathbf r(u,v)$, the <first fundamental form> is the induced <Riemannian metric>
$$
I=d\mathbf r\cdot d\mathbf r=E\,du^2+2F\,du\,dv+G\,dv^2,
\quad E=\mathbf r_u\cdot\mathbf r_u,\quad F=\mathbf r_u\cdot\mathbf r_v,
\quad G=\mathbf r_v\cdot\mathbf r_v.
$$
It measures squared lengths of <tangent vectors> and therefore lengths of <curves>. In the plane put $x=\rho\cos\theta$, $y=\rho\sin\theta$. The derivatives are orthogonal, of lengths one and $\rho$, so
$$
\boxed{dx^2+dy^2=d\rho^2+\rho^2d\theta^2.}
$$
These <polar coordinates> are regular for $\rho>0$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Two regular coordinate patches are locally isometric under a coordinate correspondence exactly when it preserves their <first fundamental forms>. More explicitly, a <diffeomorphism> $\Phi:U\to\widetilde U$ of parameter domains induces a <local isometry> if and only if
$$
\boxed{\Phi^*\widetilde I=I.}
$$
In common coordinates this means equality of the three coefficient functions $E,F,G$. The criterion follows because the <first fundamental form> gives the <inner product> of every pair of <tangent vectors>. Equality therefore preserves lengths of all <curves>; conversely a smooth <isometry> preserves tangent-vector lengths and their <inner products> by polarization.

Using the two <first fundamental forms> calculated below, choose
$$
\boxed{f(s)=\cosh s.}
$$
The <surface of revolution> is then a <catenoid>, with metric $\cosh^2s\,(ds^2+dv^2)$. On the <helicoid>, take $u=\sinh s/a$, assuming $a\ne0$ so that its parametrization is regular. Substitution gives
$$
a^2du^2+(1+a^2u^2)dv^2
=\cosh^2s\,ds^2+\cosh^2s\,dv^2.
$$
Thus the coordinate map
$$
\left(\sinh s\cos v,\sinh s\sin v,v\right)
\longmapsto
\left(\cosh s\cos v,\cosh s\sin v,s\right)
$$
is a <local isometry> on sufficiently small patches. The constant $a$ only changes the radial parametrization of the given <helicoid>, whose pitch is fixed at one. For $a=0$ the displayed <helicoid> parametrization is not a regular <surface>.

Choose representatives $v_1,\ldots,v_{n+1}$ of the first $n+1$ points. The general-position condition says that these vectors form a <basis> of $\mathbb C^{n+1}$. Write a representative of the last point as $v_{n+2}=\sum_ic_iv_i$. Every $c_i$ is nonzero; otherwise the last point and the other $n$ <basis> points would lie in a <hyperplane>. Rescaling $v_i$ by $c_i$ gives representatives with $v_{n+2}=\sum_iv_i$. Normalize representatives $w_i$ of the second frame in the same way.

There is a unique invertible <linear map> $L$ with $L(v_i)=w_i$ for $i\leq n+1$. It also maps $v_{n+2}$ to $w_{n+2}$, so its projectivization has the required effect. For uniqueness, the quotient of two candidate <projective transformations> fixes the normalized frame. A representing matrix must be diagonal in the $v_i$ <basis>, with entries $a_i$. Fixing $[\sum_iv_i]$ forces all $a_i$ to be equal. Such a scalar represents the identity <projective transformation>. Thus **the ordered <projective frame> determines the transformation uniquely**.

= Solution

Represent each point by a nonzero column $v_i$. The source's <cross-ratio> is
$$
\frac{\det(v_1,v_3)\det(v_2,v_4)}{\det(v_1,v_4)\det(v_2,v_3)}.
$$
Rescaling representatives cancels between numerator and denominator. If the <projective transformation> is represented by $A\in\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb C)$, then $\det(Av_i,Av_j)=\det(A)\det(v_i,v_j)$. Two factors of $\det(A)$ occur in both numerator and denominator and cancel. Hence **the <cross-ratio> is invariant under every <projective transformation>**.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Injectivity makes the images of any three distinct points distinct. By the ordered-frame result, there is a <projective transformation> $L$ agreeing with the given map on three chosen points. Composing by $L^{-1}$ reduces to an injective map fixing those points and preserving every <cross-ratio>. Change coordinates so that the three points are $\infty,0,1$.

For any other point with affine coordinate $z$, the convention in the source gives
$$
(\infty\,0:1\,z)=z.
$$
Preservation therefore forces the image of $z$ to have the same coordinate $z$. The normalized map fixes every point and is the identity. Undoing the normalization proves **the original map is exactly $L$**, hence projective. No prior continuity or surjectivity assumption is needed.

Choose $s\in\mathbb C$ with $s^2=\lambda$. Since $\lambda\ne0$, the invertible diagonal <projective transformation>
$$
\boxed{\sigma_\lambda[x:y:z]=[x/\lambda:y/(\lambda s):z]}
$$
fixes $[0:1:0]$. Writing its image as $[X:Y:Z]$, direct substitution gives
$$
Y^2Z=\lambda^{-3}y^2z
=X(X-Z)(X-\lambda^{-1}Z).
$$
Thus it maps $C_\lambda$ onto $C_{1/\lambda}$. Either choice of square root works. We choose $s=i$ at $\lambda=-1$ and $s=e^{\pi i/6}$ at $\lambda=e^{\pi i/3}$ for the later order calculations.

On the parameter, the two transformations are $S(\lambda)=1/\lambda$ and $T(\lambda)=1-\lambda$. Their compositions yield
$$
\lambda,\quad S\lambda=1/\lambda,\quad T\lambda=1-\lambda,
\quad TS\lambda=1-1/\lambda,\quad ST\lambda=1/(1-\lambda),
\quad STS\lambda=\lambda/(\lambda-1).

Every step lifts to an invertible <projective transformation> of the corresponding cubic <curves>. Therefore **all six parameter values give isomorphic <curves>**. Some values coincide for special $\lambda$; this does not affect the conclusion.

At $\lambda=-1$ and $s=i$, the chosen map is
$$
\sigma[x:y:z]=[-x:iy:z],\qquad
\sigma^2[x:y:z]=[x:-y:z],\qquad\sigma^4=1.
$$
The square is not the identity on the cubic, since a generic point has $y\ne0$. Thus $\boxed{\operatorname{ord}(\sigma)=4}$.

Choose the cubic's point $[0:1:0]$ as the origin in its <complex torus> description. Since $\sigma$ fixes it, its torus form is $z+\Lambda\mapsto az+\Lambda$, with $a\Lambda=\Lambda$. Exact order four implies $a=\pm i$; $a^2=-1$. Therefore $\Lambda$ is stable under multiplication by $i$.

To identify its shape without an unproved uniqueness assertion, choose a shortest nonzero period $\omega$. For any $\lambda_0\in\Lambda$, approximate $\lambda_0/\omega$ by a <Gaussian integer> $m+ni$ within distance $1/\sqrt2<1$. The difference $\lambda_0-(m+ni)\omega$ belongs to $\Lambda$ and is shorter than $\omega$, so it must vanish. Hence
$$
\boxed{\Lambda=\omega(\mathbb Z+i\mathbb Z),\qquad
C_{-1}\cong\mathbb C/(\mathbb Z+i\mathbb Z).}
$$
The <lattice> is determined up to a nonzero complex scaling: it is square.

Take $\lambda=e^{\pi i/3}$ and the chosen $s=e^{\pi i/6}$. Then $\lambda s=i$, so $i/(\lambda s)=1$, and $-1/\lambda=\lambda^2$. In affine coordinates the chosen lift becomes
$$
\upsilon(x,y)=(1+\lambda^2x,y).
$$
Since $\lambda^2$ is a primitive cube root of unity, $1+\lambda^2+\lambda^4=0$. Three iterations fix $x$ and $y$, whereas one does not. Thus
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ord}(\upsilon)=3\quad\text{for the lift chosen above}.}
$$
The other square-root choice replaces this map by $(x,y)\mapsto(1+\lambda^2x,-y)$. Its cube is $(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y)$, so that lift has order six. This sign dependence is why a lift was specified explicitly.

Both fix the point at infinity. In the <complex torus> model their linear multipliers have order three or six, so the <lattice> is stable under a primitive cube root $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/3}$ and hence under $e^{\pi i/3}=1+\zeta$. Choose a shortest nonzero period $\omega$. The triangular <lattice> $\mathbb Z+\zeta\mathbb Z$ has covering radius $1/\sqrt3<1$: its equilateral triangles of side one each have circumradius $1/\sqrt3$. Approximating any period divided by $\omega$ by this <lattice> leaves a strictly shorter period, which must be zero. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Lambda=\omega(\mathbb Z+e^{\pi i/3}\mathbb Z).}
$$
Thus the cubic has the hexagonal <lattice> shape, independently of which of the two lifts is chosen.

The <genus-degree formula> for a nonsingular plane <curve> gives
$$
\boxed{g=\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}2.}
$$
A <divisor> is a finite formal sum $D=\sum_pn_p[p]$ with integer coefficients; its degree is $\deg D=\sum_pn_p$. An effective <divisor> has all $n_p\geq0$. A <hyperplane divisor> is the intersection with a projective line not containing the <curve>, counted with local intersection multiplicities. Its degree is $d$.

Define $L(D)=\{0\}\cup\{f\text{ meromorphic}:(f)+D\geq0\}$ and $\ell(D)=\dim_{\mathbb C}L(D)$. For a <canonical divisor> $K$, the <Riemann–Roch theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\ell(D)-\ell(K-D)=\deg D+1-g.}
$$
Here $(f)$ records zero orders positively and pole orders negatively, and $\deg K=2g-2$.

A <meromorphic function> on a compact <Riemann surface> has finitely many poles, say $p_j$ of orders $r_j$. For each $p_j$, choose a projective line through it that does not contain $C$. Its <hyperplane divisor> contains $p_j$ with multiplicity at least one. Include $r_j$ copies of this <divisor>. The sum then dominates the pole <divisor> of $f$.

Add further arbitrary <hyperplane divisors> until their number $m$ satisfies $m\geq d$ and $md>2g-2$. Adding effective <divisors> cannot destroy the pole bound, so for $D=H_1+\cdots+H_m$ we have
$$
(f)+D\geq0,\qquad\boxed{f\in L(D).}
$$
For a constant function, including zero, no initial poles need to be covered; simply add sufficiently many lines.

The <divisor> $D=\sum_iH_i$ has degree $md$. The hypothesis gives $\deg(K-D)=2g-2-md<0$. A <divisor> of negative degree has no nonzero section: if $h\in L(K-D)\setminus\{0\}$, then $(h)+K-D$ would be effective, but its degree would be negative because the <divisor> of a <meromorphic function> has degree zero. The monomials $x^iy^jz^k$ with nonnegative integers $i+j+k=m$ form a <basis> for the <vector space> of homogeneous <polynomials> of degree $m$. For each $i=0,\ldots,m$, there are $m-i+1$ choices of $(j,k)$. Hence

= Solution

Write $L_i=a_ix+b_iy+c_iz$ and $S=\prod_iL_i$. The numerator and denominator have the same homogeneous degree, so their ratio is well-defined on projective points away from its poles and extends as a <meromorphic function> on $C$. Locally the pole order cannot exceed the zero order of $S|_C$, whose <divisor> is $D$. Thus $f_Q\in L(D)$.

Equality of two ratios is equivalent to $Q-Q'$ vanishing on $C$. A nonsingular projective plane <curve> is irreducible: distinct positive-degree components would intersect by <Bézout's theorem>, producing a singular point. Its homogeneous vanishing ideal is therefore generated by its irreducible defining <polynomial> $P$. One can also see this using <Bézout's theorem>: a homogeneous <polynomial> not divisible by $P$ has only finitely many intersections with $C$, so cannot vanish on the whole <curve>. Consequently
$$
\boxed{f_Q=f_{Q'}\iff Q-Q'=PR,\qquad R\in V_{m-d}.}
$$
Multiplication by the nonzero <polynomial> $P$ is injective. The <linear map> $V_m\to L(D)$ therefore has <kernel> $PV_{m-d}$ and image <dimension>
$$
\boxed{\binom{m+2}{2}-\binom{m-d+2}{2}
=md+\frac{3d-d^2}{2}=md+1-g.}
$$
The assumption $m\geq d$ makes both <polynomial>-space <dimensions> applicable directly.

Given $f$, choose the <hyperplane divisors> as in (a). The ratios $f_Q$ form a <linear subspace> of $L(D)$ by (d), and its <dimension> equals $md+1-g=\ell(D)$ by (b). A <linear subspace> of a finite-dimensional <vector space> with the same <dimension> is the whole space. Hence $f=f_Q$ for some homogeneous <polynomial> $Q$ of degree $m$, giving
$$
\boxed{f([x:y:z])=\frac{Q(x,y,z)}{\prod_{i=1}^m(a_ix+b_iy+c_iz)}.}
$$
The zero function corresponds to $Q=0$. Thus every allowed <meromorphic function> is a ratio of homogeneous <polynomials> of equal degree, with a denominator that can be chosen as a product of linear forms.
