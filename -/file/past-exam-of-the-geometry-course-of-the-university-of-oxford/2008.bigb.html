= Solution

The <classification of compact surfaces> says that every compact connected <surface> without boundary is homeomorphic either to the <sphere> with $g\geq0$ handles, with <Euler characteristic> $2-2g$, or to a <connected sum> of $k\geq1$ real projective planes, with <Euler characteristic> $2-k$. The first family is <orientable> and the second is nonorientable; the displayed invariants uniquely determine the type. With boundary one additionally removes a uniquely determined number of open disks.

Number the vertices of the fourteen-gon $v_0,\ldots,v_{13}$ in boundary order. The printed edge identifications imply
$$
v_0\sim v_5\sim v_2\sim v_3\sim v_4\sim v_1\sim v_6\sim v_{13}\sim v_7\sim v_{12}\sim v_9\sim v_{10}\sim v_{11}\sim v_8\sim v_0.
$$
Thus there is one vertex, seven edges and one face in the quotient. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\chi(S)=1-7+1=-5.}
$$
The two occurrences of $a_1$ have the same sign, as do those of $a_5$, so the <orientation> on the polygon cannot extend consistently across those paired edges. Thus **the <surface> is nonorientable**, and classification identifies it as a <connected sum> of seven projective planes. The inverse on the first occurrence of $a_4$ and the positive second occurrence have been retained in the vertex calculation.

A <Riemann surface> is a connected Hausdorff second-countable space equipped with complex coordinate charts whose changes of coordinate are holomorphic with holomorphic inverses. Such charts give it the structure of a one-dimensional <complex manifold>.

A holomorphic change of coordinate has nonzero derivative. In real coordinates its derivative matrix has determinant $|f'(z)|^2>0$ by the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>. Thus every overlap preserves the standard <orientation> of the <complex plane>. These chart orientations fit together, proving **every <Riemann surface> is <orientable>**.

For a regular parametrization choose the <unit normal> $n=(r_u\times r_v)/|r_u\times r_v|$. The <first fundamental form> is the squared length induced on tangent vectors:
$$
I=E\,du^2+2F\,du\,dv+G\,dv^2,\qquad E=r_u\cdot r_u,\quad F=r_u\cdot r_v,\quad G=r_v\cdot r_v.
$$
The <second fundamental form> records their normal bending:
$$
II=L\,du^2+2M\,du\,dv+N\,dv^2,\qquad L=n\cdot r_{uu},\quad M=n\cdot r_{uv},\quad N=n\cdot r_{vv}.
$$
The <Gaussian curvature> is
$$
\boxed{K=\frac{LN-M^2}{EG-F^2}.}
$$
Regularity ensures $EG-F^2>0$.

Expanding the characteristic determinant gives
$$
(EG-F^2)\kappa^2-(LG+NE-2MF)\kappa+(LN-M^2)=0.
$$
The product and sum of its two roots therefore give
$$
\boxed{\kappa_1\kappa_2=\frac{LN-M^2}{EG-F^2}=K,\qquad H=\frac{\kappa_1+\kappa_2}{2}=\frac{LG+NE-2MF}{2(EG-F^2)}.}
$$
Reversing the normal changes both principal curvatures and the <mean curvature> in sign, while leaving their product unchanged.

Put $b=\gamma'+va'$. Then $r_u=b$ and $r_v=a$, so
$$
\boxed{I=|\gamma'+va'|^2du^2+2(\gamma'+va')\cdot a\,du\,dv+|a|^2dv^2.}

= b
{parent=iii}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For the <ruled surface>, $r_{vv}=0$, so $N=0$. Also
$$
M=\frac{(b\times a)\cdot a'}{|b\times a|}=\frac{\gamma'\cdot(a\times a')}{|b\times a|},\qquad EG-F^2=|b\times a|^2.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{K=-\frac{[\gamma'\cdot(a\times a')]^2}{|b\times a|^4}.}
$$
The denominator is positive at every regular point, proving that zero <Gaussian curvature> is equivalent to the vanishing of the stated scalar triple product. In particular the <Gaussian curvature> of a regular <ruled surface> is always nonpositive.

For the <helicoid>,
$$
r_u=(-v\sin u,v\cos u,1),\qquad r_v=(\cos u,\sin u,0),\qquad n=\frac{(-\sin u,\cos u,-v)}{\sqrt{1+v^2}}.
$$
Hence $E=1+v^2$, $F=0$, $G=1$. Since $r_{uu}=(-v\cos u,-v\sin u,0)$ and $r_{vv}=0$, both $L$ and $N$ vanish. The mean-curvature formula therefore gives
$$
\boxed{H=0.}
$$
For comparison, $M=1/\sqrt{1+v^2}$, so $K=-1/(1+v^2)^2$; zero <mean curvature> does not mean zero <Gaussian curvature>.

For the unit-speed curve, the <normal curvature> is $\kappa_n=t'\cdot n$, and the <geodesic curvature> is $\kappa_g=t'\cdot(n\times t)$. Differentiating $\gamma=r(u,v)$ twice and taking the normal component kills the terms in $r_u,r_v$, giving
$$
\boxed{\kappa_n=L(u')^2+2Mu'v'+N(v')^2.}
$$
An affinely parametrized <geodesic> has zero tangential acceleration, or equivalently its <covariant derivative> of velocity is zero. For unit speed, $t'\cdot t=0$, and $t,n\times t,n$ form an <orthonormal frame>. Therefore $t'=\kappa_n n+\kappa_g(n\times t)$, so zero tangential acceleration is exactly **$\kappa_g=0$**.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Since $Eu'+Fv'=\gamma'\cdot r_u$, differentiation gives
$$
\frac d{ds}(Eu'+Fv')=\gamma''\cdot r_u+\frac12E_u(u')^2+F_uu'v'+\frac12G_u(v')^2.
$$
The corresponding calculation with $\gamma'\cdot r_v=Fu'+Gv'$ gives
$$
\frac d{ds}(Fu'+Gv')=\gamma''\cdot r_v+\frac12E_v(u')^2+F_vu'v'+\frac12G_v(v')^2.
$$
Thus the two requested equations hold exactly when $\gamma''$ is perpendicular to both tangent <basis> vectors, which is precisely the <geodesic> condition.

On expanding the left sides, the coefficient matrix of $(u'',v'')$ is the positive-definite metric matrix. It is invertible, so the equations form a smooth second-order <ordinary differential equation> in local coordinates. The local existence and uniqueness theorem gives a unique solution for each initial point and coordinate velocity. For an arbitrary initial tangent vector $w$ this is an affine parameter of constant speed $|w|$; it is arclength when $|w|=1$. For $w=0$ the solution is the constant curve. This specifies how the arbitrary-velocity statement relates to the preceding unit-speed convention.

Let $R$ be reflection in the plane. Choose a point of the regular intersection curve and a <unit tangent vector> $w$ to it. Both the point and $w$ are fixed by $R$. The unique local <geodesic> with these initial data is carried by the <surface> <isometry> $R$ to another <geodesic> with the same initial data. Uniqueness therefore gives $R\gamma=\gamma$. Its image lies in the fixed-point set of the reflection, namely the plane, hence locally in the intersection curve. Thus **the intersection curve is a <geodesic>**, when parametrized by arclength.

For a positively oriented curvilinear polygon bounding a disk $D$ in one coordinate patch, with exterior turning angles $\theta_i$, the <Gauss-Bonnet theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\int_DK\,dA+\int_{\partial D}\kappa_g\,ds+\sum_i\theta_i=2\pi.}
$$
For a closed compact <surface> the global formula is
$$
\boxed{\int_SK\,dA=2\pi\chi(S).}
$$
Triangulate the <surface> with each triangle contained in a coordinate patch. For each face, writing its exterior angles as $\pi$ minus its interior angles gives $\int K+\int\kappa_g=\sum\text{interior angles}-\pi$. On summing, the two contributions on each internal edge cancel, and all angles at each vertex sum to $2\pi$. Thus $\int_SK=2\pi V-\pi F$. Since $3F=2E$, this equals $2\pi(V-E+F)$, as required. For a <nonorientable surface> apply the <orientable> version to its <orientation double cover>: both the curvature integral and <Euler characteristic> double. With boundary, the same summation retains the boundary <geodesic>-curvature integral and the exterior angles at boundary corners, giving the corresponding global boundary formula with right side $2\pi\chi(S)$.

= Solution

For the <sphere>, choose the inward normal so that the <second fundamental form> is $I/a$. Both principal curvatures are $1/a$, giving **$K=1/a^2$**; the outward choice reverses both signs and gives the same product.

The octant has area $4\pi a^2/8=\pi a^2/2$, so its curvature integral is $\pi/2$. Its sides are great-circle geodesics, hence have zero <geodesic curvature>. Each interior angle is $\pi/2$, so each exterior angle is also $\pi/2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\int_DK\,dA+\int_{\partial D}\kappa_g\,ds+\sum_i\theta_i=\frac\pi2+0+\frac{3\pi}2=2\pi,}
$$
verifying the polygon formula directly.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

If two distinct <geodesic> arcs enclosed a disk, regard its boundary as a curvilinear two-sided polygon, with interior angles $\alpha,\beta$. These are positive; zero angle would make the arcs tangent and local <geodesic> uniqueness would make them coincide. The <geodesic>-curvature terms vanish, so Gauss-Bonnet gives
$$
\int_DK\,dA+(\pi-\alpha)+(\pi-\beta)=2\pi,\qquad\int_DK\,dA=\alpha+\beta>0.
$$
This contradicts $K\leq0$. Thus **no such disk-bounding <geodesic> bigon exists**. The disk hypothesis matters because it fixes the <Euler characteristic> on the right side.

For a <projective frame>, choose representative vectors $v_1,\ldots,v_{n+1}$ forming a <basis>. Express the final point by $\sum c_iv_i$. Every $c_i$ is nonzero, since otherwise some $n+1$ of the given representatives would be dependent. Rescale the first representatives to $c_iv_i$, so the last point is represented by their sum. Do the same for the target frame, with <basis> $w_i$. The <linear map> taking each rescaled source <basis> vector to the corresponding target vector induces the required <projective transformation>. For uniqueness, a transformation fixing the first $n+1$ coordinate points is represented by a diagonal matrix. Fixing their sum forces every diagonal entry to agree. It is therefore scalar, giving **a unique <projective transformation>**.

If three intersection points of the conics lay on a line, Bézout's theorem would force that line to be a component of either nonsingular <conic>, impossible. Thus the four points form a <projective frame> and can be moved to the three coordinate points and $[1:1:1]$. Vanishing at the coordinate points removes the square terms from each <conic> equation; vanishing at the last point gives $a+b+c=0$.

Substitution gives, by direct expansion,
$$
ayz+bzx+cxy=au^2+bv^2+cw^2+(a+b+c)(uv+uw+vw)=au^2+bv^2+cw^2.
$$
The substitution matrix
$$
S=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&1\\1&0&1\\1&1&0\end{pmatrix}
$$
has determinant two, hence is invertible over $\mathbb C$. If the initial frame transformation is represented in the direction old coordinates equal $Q$ times new coordinates, take $P=QS$. Both quadratic forms then have no mixed terms, proving **$P^TAP$ and $P^TBP$ are simultaneously diagonal**. The same argument applies when an individual <conic> is reducible but the intersection consists of exactly four distinct points: a line containing three would be a common component, contradicting finiteness of the intersection.

An <inflection point> is a smooth point where the tangent line has <intersection multiplicity> at least three with the curve. For a nonsingular plane curve of degree $n\geq3$, these are the intersections with its <Hessian curve>, whose degree is $3(n-2)$. The original curve is not a component of its Hessian: otherwise in each graph chart the tangent direction would have zero derivative identically, making the curve locally a line and hence a line globally. Bézout therefore gives the upper bound **$3n(n-2)$**, counting multiplicity. A nonsingular <conic> has no inflection points; lines are excluded from this finite-contact definition.

Take $F=y^2z-x^3+xz^2$. Its Hessian determinant is
$$
\det D^2F=8(3xy^2-3x^2z-z^3).
$$
At infinity the curve has only $O=[0:1:0]$, which is an <inflection point>: its tangent is $z=0$ and the restricted equation is $-x^3=0$. In the chart $z=1$, substitute $y^2=x^3-x$ into the Hessian equation to obtain
$$
3x^4-6x^2-1=0,\qquad x^2=1\pm\frac2{\sqrt3}.
$$
Thus the full answer is
$$
\boxed{O=[0:1:0],\qquad [x:y:1]\text{ with }x^2=1\pm2/\sqrt3,\quad y=\pm\sqrt{x^3-x}.}
$$
The four complex values of $x$ are distinct and none is $0,1,-1$, so each gives two distinct nonzero values of $y$. Together with $O$ these are all nine inflection points, exhausting the Bézout bound.

For a degree-$d$ nonconstant <holomorphic map> from a compact connected <Riemann surface> of <genus> $g$ to the <projective line>, the <Riemann-Hurwitz formula> is
$$
\boxed{2g-2=-2d+\sum_{p\in X}(e_p-1),}
$$
where $e_p$ is the <local degree>. A <meromorphic function> defines a map to the <sphere> by giving its ordinary finite values away from poles and value infinity at each pole. The reciprocal function is holomorphic in a neighborhood of a pole, proving holomorphicity there. For use in Riemann-Hurwitz the function must be nonconstant; a nonzero constant still defines a <holomorphic map>, but of degree zero.

Let $X=\mathbb C/\Lambda$. The <double pole> of $\wp$ gives a degree-two map to the <sphere>. The <torus> has <genus> one, so total ramification is four. One simple <ramification point> is zero, above infinity. At the three nonzero half-period classes $h_i$, evenness and periodicity give $\wp'(h_i)=0$. Each is a <ramification point> of <local degree> two, and these account for all remaining ramification. Put $e_i=\wp(h_i)$. They are distinct, since a degree-two fiber cannot contain two points each of <local degree> two.

At each $h_i$, $\wp-e_i$ has a double zero and $\wp'$ has a simple zero. Consequently the elliptic quotient
$$
R(z)=\frac{\wp'(z)^2}{(\wp(z)-e_1)(\wp(z)-e_2)(\wp(z)-e_3)}
$$
has removable singularities there and no zeros or poles elsewhere except possibly zero. Near zero, $\wp=z^{-2}+O(z^2)$ and $\wp'=-2z^{-3}+O(z)$, so $R\to4$. Thus $R$ is holomorphic on the compact <torus> and constant, proving
$$
\boxed{\wp'^2=4(\wp-e_1)(\wp-e_2)(\wp-e_3).}
$$
**The printed additional requirement that all three $e_i$ be nonzero is false.** For the square lattice $\Lambda=\mathbb Z+i\mathbb Z$, invariance of the defining series under multiplication by $i$ gives $\wp(iz)=-\wp(z)$. At $h=(1+i)/2$, one has $ih=h-1$, so periodicity gives $\wp(h)=\wp(ih)=-\wp(h)$ and hence $\wp(h)=0$. This is one of the three branch values. Distinctness, which is all the cubic construction needs, remains valid.

Define
$$
\Phi(z)=[\wp(z):\wp'(z):1]\quad(z\notin\Lambda),\qquad\Phi(0)=[0:1:0].
$$
The image lies on $Y^2Z=4(X-e_1Z)(X-e_2Z)(X-e_3Z)$. Distinct roots make its affine part smooth, and the point at infinity is smooth because the derivative of its equation with respect to $Z$ is nonzero there. Near zero the projective coordinates $X/Y=\wp/\wp'\sim-z/2$ and $Z/Y=1/\wp'\sim-z^3/2$ extend holomorphically. Every finite $X/Z$ value has two preimages under $\wp$ except at a branch value. Evenness pairs those preimages as $z,-z$, and oddness of $\wp'$ distinguishes them unless they are the same half-period class. Therefore the two coordinates together give injectivity and reach both signs of $Y$, proving surjectivity onto the cubic. Thus **$\Phi$ is a bijective <holomorphic map> to a nonsingular cubic**, including when one branch value is zero.

A <divisor on an algebraic curve> is a finite formal sum $D=\sum n_pp$ with integer coefficients. Its degree is $\deg D=\sum n_p$. The <principal divisor> of a nonzero <meromorphic function> is $(f)=\sum\operatorname{ord}_p(f)p$. For a nonconstant $f$, zeros and poles are fibers over zero and infinity of the same map to the <sphere>, each of total multiplicity equal to its degree; hence $\deg(f)=0$. A nonzero constant has zero divisor.

Write $L(D)=\{f:(f)+D\geq0\}\cup\{0\}$ and $\ell(D)=\dim L(D)$. The <Riemann-Roch theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\ell(D)-\ell(K_C-D)=\deg D+1-g,}
$$
where $K_C$ is a <canonical divisor>. Taking $D=p_1+\cdots+p_{g+1}$ gives $\ell(D)=2+\ell(K_C-D)\geq2$. Constants span only one <dimension>, so there is a nonconstant $f\in L(D)$. It has no poles outside these points and at most a <simple pole> at each. Thus **the required nonconstant function exists**, with actual poles at some nonempty subset because a <holomorphic function> on a compact connected curve is constant.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Since every $p_i$ is fixed, $f\circ\varphi$ has poles only among the first $g+1$ points, with order at most one: a biholomorphic change of coordinate preserves pole order. Thus $h=f-f\circ\varphi$ also has at most $g+1$ poles, counted with multiplicity. At every $p_i$ with $i>g+1$, both terms are finite and equal, so $h(p_i)=0$.

If $h$ were not zero, its <principal divisor> would have degree zero. It would therefore have at most $g+1$ zeros counted with multiplicity. But it has at least $n-(g+1)$ distinct zeros. When $n>2g+2$ this number exceeds $g+1$, a contradiction. Consequently
$$
\boxed{n>2g+2\implies f=f\circ\varphi.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Choose an actual pole $p_i$ of $f$; one exists by nonconstancy and compactness. In a local coordinate $z$ centered there, its <simple pole> has expansion $f(z)=a_{-1}/z+O(1)$ with $a_{-1}\ne0$. Hence
$$
w(z)=1/f(z)=z/a_{-1}+O(z^2),\qquad w'(0)\ne0.
$$
The <holomorphic inverse function theorem> makes **$1/f$ a local coordinate**. Here the source notation $f^{-1}$ denotes the reciprocal, rather than an inverse map of the <meromorphic function>.

The chosen pole is among the fixed points, so $\varphi$ maps a sufficiently small neighborhood of it into the coordinate neighborhood. If $f=f\circ\varphi$, then $w=w\circ\varphi$ there. Injectivity of this coordinate forces $\varphi(x)=x$ throughout a nonempty open set. The stated identity principle then gives **$\varphi$ equal to the identity everywhere**.

If a nonidentity holomorphic <automorphism> had more than $2g+2$ fixed points, choose that many distinct ones and apply the function constructed above. The zero-and-pole count would give $f=f\circ\varphi$, and the local reciprocal coordinate would force $\varphi$ to be the identity, a contradiction. Therefore

\boxed{\#\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)\leq2g+2\quad\text{for every nonidentity holomorphic automorphism}.}
