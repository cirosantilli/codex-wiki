= Chemical potential
{wiki}
{title2=$\mu$}

The <chemical potential> measures how a <thermodynamic potential> changes when matter is added under specified constraints. It plays for matter exchange the role that <temperature> plays for <heat> exchange: a difference supplies a direction, and equality characterizes <thermodynamic equilibrium> when the corresponding exchange is allowed. This connects <diffusion>, <phase coexistence> and <chemical equilibrium> through one quantity.

For a single component, the particle convention is
$$
\mu_{\rm particle}=\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial N}\right)_{S,V},
$$
where $U$ is <internal energy>, $N$ counts particles, $S$ is <entropy> and $V$ is <volume (thermodynamics)>. This <partial derivative> holds <entropy> and <volume (thermodynamics)> fixed; it measures an incremental change rather than the average <energy> per particle. See https://davidtong.org/pdfs/teaching/statistical-physics/statphys1.pdf[David Tong, Statistical Physics, section 1.4].

In <chemistry> the molar convention instead uses <amount of substance> $n$, measured in <moles>:
$$
\mu_{\rm m}=\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial n}\right)_{T,p},
\qquad n=\frac{N}{N_{\rm A}},
\qquad \mu_{\rm m}=N_{\rm A}\mu_{\rm particle}.
$$
Here $G$ is <Gibbs free energy>, $T$ is <temperature>, $p$ is <pressure> and $N_{\rm A}$ is the <Avogadro constant>. The units are $\mathrm J$ per particle versus $\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}$. For several <chemical species>, define each <chemical potential> by varying only its own particle count or <amount of substance>, with all other components' counts or amounts fixed. Many texts use $\mu$ for either convention, so the units and the variable being differentiated must identify it.

For a <simple compressible system> with variable component inventories, the fundamental differentials are
$$
dU=T\,dS-p\,dV+\sum_i\mu_{{\rm particle},i}\,dN_i,
\qquad
dG=-S\,dT+V\,dp+\sum_i\mu_{{\rm m},i}\,dn_i.
$$
They describe changes between states in <thermodynamic equilibrium> with only <pressure>-<volume (thermodynamics)> <work>. If every component inventory is fixed, the sums vanish. The <Gibbs free energy> form follows by differentiating $G=U-TS+pV$ and using $dN_i=N_{\rm A}\,dn_i$; this is why the two definitions describe the same <chemical potential> in different units.

A <chemical potential> can be negative. Holding <entropy> fixed while adding matter need not increase <internal energy>; Tong explains this explicitly for a classical <ideal gas> in https://davidtong.org/pdfs/teaching/statistical-physics/statphys2.pdf[Statistical Physics, section 2.2.4]. Its absolute sign also depends on the reference <energy>: replacing $U$ by $U+\epsilon_0N$ shifts $\mu_{\rm particle}$ by $\epsilon_0$. A common reference shift cancels from differences for the same <chemical species>, which are what determine exchange. Thus “energy cost” means a signed increment, not an always positive amount that must be supplied.

= Water activity
{parent=Chemical potential}
{title2=$\mu_w=\mu_w^\circ+RT\ln a_w$}

Water activity measures the chemical potential of water relative to a chosen pure-water standard state. Under ideal-vapour conditions above a flat solution, it is the equilibrium vapour pressure divided by that above pure liquid water at the same temperature. Dissolved solute usually lowers activity. <Raoult's law> gives the ideal dilute-solution limit; nonideal interactions require an activity model.

= Standard chemical potential
{parent=Chemical potential}
{title2=$\mu^\circ$}

The <chemical potential> assigned to a specified <standard state>. With dimensionless <thermodynamic activity> $a$, the per-particle convention is $\mu=\mu^\circ+k_BT\ln a$; a molar convention uses $RT$. The <standard state> changes both $\mu^\circ$ and the activity definition without changing observable <equilibrium>.

= Grand-canonical chemical potential of a classical ideal gas
{parent=Chemical potential}
{title2=$\mu=k_BT\ln(n\lambda^3)$}

For one internal species, the <grand canonical partition function> of a classical <ideal gas> satisfies $\ln\Xi=zV/\lambda^3$, with <quantum gas fugacity> $z=e^{\beta\mu}$ and <thermal de Broglie wavelength> $\lambda=\sqrt{2\pi\hbar^2/(mk_BT)}$. Differentiating the <grand potential> gives $N=zV/\lambda^3$, hence $\mu=k_BT\ln(n\lambda^3)$. An internal partition factor must be included consistently if internal states are available.

= Quasi-Fermi level
{parent=Chemical potential}

A <quasi-Fermi level> is a local electron or hole population parameter in a semiconductor driven out of equilibrium, assuming each population is internally close enough to thermal equilibrium to use a <Fermi-Dirac distribution>. Separate electron and hole parameters replace one common equilibrium <chemical potential>. Their spatial profiles depend on transport, generation and recombination as well as electrostatics. For ideal ohmic contacts with the p voltage $V$ relative to n, the contact electron <electrochemical potentials> obey $\mu_p-\mu_n=-eV$.

= Quasi-Fermi-level splitting
{parent=Quasi-Fermi level}

The difference between electron and hole quasi-Fermi levels under nonequilibrium conditions. For ideal selective contacts their terminal separation sets $qV$; poor contacts can lose part of the internal splitting.

= Chemical potential equality for particle exchange
{parent=Chemical potential}
{title2=$\mu_{{\rm m},A}=\mu_{{\rm m},B}$}

Consider two internally equilibrated reservoirs A and B at the same imposed <temperature> and <pressure>. They may have different compositions or represent different phases. Transfer a small positive <amount of substance> $\delta n$ of the same <chemical species> from A to B, holding the other inventories fixed. To first order, their total <Gibbs free energy> changes by
$$
\delta G_{\rm total}
=-\mu_{{\rm m},A}\delta n+\mu_{{\rm m},B}\delta n
=(\mu_{{\rm m},B}-\mu_{{\rm m},A})\delta n.
$$
Hence exchange toward lower <chemical potential> lowers <Gibbs free energy>. If transfer is allowed in both directions and the two reservoirs contain the exchangeable component, <thermodynamic equilibrium> requires equal <chemical potentials> for that component. See https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-60-thermodynamics-kinetics-spring-2008/e763a06aa3b5de09cd6bfea6280f219c_5_60_lecture14.pdf[MIT 5.60, Lecture 14, pages 1–2].

An impermeable partition can maintain unequal <chemical potentials>; equality is a condition on an allowed exchange. Two pure samples of the same <ideal gas> already have equal <chemical potentials> at the same <temperature> and <pressure>. Different compositions at a common <pressure> can instead give different <chemical potentials> for one component through its <thermodynamic activity>.

Equality concerns the same exchangeable component in different places, not all different <chemical species>. For a permitted <chemical reaction>, the corresponding interior <chemical equilibrium> criterion is $\sum_i\nu_i\mu_{{\rm m},i}=0$, with signed <stoichiometric vector> entries $\nu_i$. The existing <chemical equilibrium> and <constrained Gibbs minimization for chemical equilibrium> sections develop those composition constraints.

= Open-system equilibrium contacts
{parent=Chemical potential equality for particle exchange}

Unconstrained thermal, mechanical and particle-exchange contacts require equality of <temperature>, <pressure> and the <chemical potential> of each exchangeable species. Walls, external fields, interfaces and chemical reactions change which variations are allowed.

= Chemical potential and Helmholtz free energy
{parent=Chemical potential equality for particle exchange}
{title2=$\mu_{\rm m}=(\partial F/\partial n)_{T,V}$}

Rigid compartments impose a different constraint: fixed <volume (thermodynamics)> rather than fixed <pressure>. With <Helmholtz free energy> $F=U-TS$,
$$
dF=-S\,dT-p\,dV+\sum_i\mu_{{\rm m},i}\,dn_i,
\qquad
\mu_{{\rm m},i}=\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial n_i}\right)_{T,V,n_{j\ne i}}.
$$
This follows immediately by differentiating $F=U-TS$ in the <chemical potential> differential above. It expresses the same <chemical potential> through the <thermodynamic potential> appropriate to fixed <temperature> and <volume (thermodynamics)>.

Consider a <closed system> consisting of two rigid compartments of equal <volume (thermodynamics)> $V$, maintained at a common fixed <temperature> $T$. A valve permits exchange of the same <ideal gas>; the partition stays in place. Initially $n_A=9\,\mathrm{mol}$ and $n_B=3\,\mathrm{mol}$, with conserved total <amount of substance> $n_{\rm tot}=12\,\mathrm{mol}$. These are macroscopic <moles>, modeled continuously, not twelve individual particles. By the <ideal gas law>, $p_B/p_A=n_B/n_A=1/3$, so initially
$$
\mu_{{\rm m},B}-\mu_{{\rm m},A}=-RT\ln3.
$$
At $300\,\mathrm K$ with the stipulated $R$, this is approximately $-2740.311\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}$, favoring transfer from A to B.

At fixed $T$ and equal $V$, integrating the ideal-gas <chemical potential> gives the total <Helmholtz free energy>, up to a term independent of the allocation,
$$
F_{\rm total}
=RT\left[n_A\ln\frac{n_A}{n_{\rm ref}}+n_B\ln\frac{n_B}{n_{\rm ref}}-n_{\rm tot}\right]+C.
$$
Here $n_{\rm ref}$ is a positive reference <amount of substance>; any omitted common term linear in $n_A+n_B$ is constant along this exchange. Let $x=n_A/n_{\rm tot}$ be the <dimensionless> fraction in A. The relative <Helmholtz free energy> then takes the particularly simple form
$$
\frac{F_{\rm total}(x)-F_{\rm total}(1/2)}{n_{\rm tot}RT}
=x\ln x+(1-x)\ln(1-x)+\ln2.
$$
Its <derivative> along the allowed transfer and its second <derivative> are
$$
\frac{dF_{\rm total}}{dn_A}
=\mu_{{\rm m},A}-\mu_{{\rm m},B}
=RT\ln\frac{x}{1-x},
\qquad
\frac{d^2F_{\rm total}}{dn_A^2}
=RT\left(\frac{1}{n_A}+\frac{1}{n_B}\right)>0.
$$
Thus the <Helmholtz free energy> is a strictly <convex function> of the allocation for positive amounts. Its unique minimum has $x=1/2$, or $n_A=n_B=6\,\mathrm{mol}$, and equal <chemical potentials>. Initially its excess above this minimum is $n_{\rm tot}RT[\tfrac34\ln(3/2)+\tfrac14\ln(1/2)]\simeq3915.474\,\mathrm J$ at $300\,\mathrm K$. Relaxation decreases the total <Helmholtz free energy> by this amount in the model; the final <pressures> are also equal because both compartments have the same <volume (thermodynamics)>.

The beautiful connection is that the direction of matter exchange and the slope of a <free energy> curve are the same calculation. The <entropy> gained by redistributing the <ideal gas> makes equal allocation favorable here. As with <chemical equilibrium>, a <free energy> minimum identifies the favored state, while transport properties determine how quickly it is reached. The fixed-<temperature>, fixed-<pressure> <Gibbs free energy> criterion is summarized in <Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/16.4 Free Energy>; the rigid experiment instead uses <Helmholtz free energy>.

= Chemical potential of an ideal gas
{parent=Chemical potential}
{title2=$\mu_{\rm m}=\mu_{\rm m}^\circ+RT\ln(p/p^\circ)$}

For a pure <ideal gas> at <temperature> $T$ and <pressure> $p$,
$$
\mu_{\rm m}(T,p)=\mu_{\rm m}^\circ(T)+RT\ln\frac{p}{p^\circ},
$$
where $R$ is the <molar gas constant> and $p^\circ$ specifies the reference <pressure> of the <standard state>. The ratio inside the <natural logarithm> is <dimensionless>. The <chemical potential> difference therefore depends on a ratio of <pressures>, not an absolute difference. See https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-60-thermodynamics-kinetics-spring-2008/e763a06aa3b5de09cd6bfea6280f219c_5_60_lecture14.pdf[MIT 5.60, Lecture 14, page 4].

For a <chemical species> in an ideal <gas> <mixture>, replace $p$ by its <partial pressure>. More generally write $\mu_{{\rm m},i}=\mu_{{\rm m},i}^\circ+RT\ln a_i$ using its <thermodynamic activity> $a_i$ and a consistent <standard state>. For an ideal dilute <chemical solution>, $a_i=c_i/c^\circ$ with reference <concentration> $c^\circ$; the <thermodynamic activity> section describes departures from this idealization.

As a computed comparison, take identical <chemical species> at $T=300\,\mathrm K$, with $p_A=2p^\circ$ and $p_B=p^\circ$. Stipulate $R=8.314462618\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$. Then
$$
\mu_{{\rm m},B}-\mu_{{\rm m},A}
=RT\ln\frac{p_B}{p_A}
=-RT\ln2
\simeq-1728.944\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}.
$$
Halving the <pressure> gives the same decrement whether starting from $2p^\circ$ or $20p^\circ$, provided the <ideal gas> approximation remains valid. This comparison uses different <pressures>; it is not the common-<pressure> reservoir setup of <chemical potential equality for particle exchange>. The fixed-<volume (thermodynamics)> example in <chemical potential and Helmholtz free energy> supplies an exchange experiment with unequal initial <pressures>.

= Electrochemical potential
{parent=Chemical potential}
{wiki}
{title2=$\widetilde\mu_{\rm m}=\mu_{\rm m}+zF_{\rm Far}\psi$}

The molar <electrochemical potential> combines the molar <chemical potential> $\mu_{\rm m}$ with the electrical contribution $zF_{\rm Far}\psi$, where $z$ is the signed charge number of an <ion>, $F_{\rm Far}=N_{\rm A}e$ is the <Faraday constant> and $\psi$ is <electric potential>. Both terms have units $\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}$. In the particle convention the corresponding expression is $\widetilde\mu_{\rm particle}=\mu_{\rm particle}+ze\psi$, with <elementary charge> $e$; multiplying it by the <Avogadro constant> gives the molar form. Transfer between locations at the same <temperature> and <pressure> is driven by differences in <electrochemical potential>. The <membrane transport> examples use it to distinguish neutral <solute> transfer from <ion> transfer.

= Sodium-motive force
{parent=Electrochemical potential}
{title2=$\mathcal E_{\rm Na}=(k_BT/e)\ln(c_o/c_i)-V_m$}

Define the displayed quantity as the free energy available per unit positive charge for sodium entry, with $V_m=\psi_i-\psi_o$. Its negative is the signed inward electrochemical cost per charge. A reversal of the transport direction reverses the sign. At equilibrium it vanishes without requiring equal concentrations.

= Sodium export cost from a capacitive vesicle
{parent=Sodium-motive force}
{title2=$\Delta G(n)=ne^2/C+k_BT\ln[n_0/(n_0-n)]$}

Start at sodium electrochemical equilibrium with $n_0$ internal ions. Exporting $n$ ions changes the <membrane potential> by $-ne/C$ and the internal number density to $(n_0-n)/V$. Subtracting the initially balanced chemical and electrical contributions gives the displayed incremental cost. An initial zero potential further implies $n_0=c_oV$ for an ideal fixed external reservoir. Nonzero initial equilibrium potential permits unequal concentrations, so that latter equality is an additional assumption.

= Electrochemical gradient
{parent=Electrochemical potential}
{wiki}
{title2=$\nabla\widetilde\mu_{\rm m}$}

An <electrochemical gradient> combines spatial changes in <chemical potential> and <electric potential> for a charged species. <Passive transport> proceeds downhill in <electrochemical potential> when an appropriate route is available; a <concentration> difference alone does not determine the direction for an <ion>.

= Proton-motive force
{title2=$\Delta p$}
{parent=Electrochemical gradient}

A <proton-motive force> is a <proton> <electrochemical potential> difference per unit <electric charge>. It combines the <electric potential difference> and the <chemical potential> difference across a <cell membrane>; the direction and sign convention must be specified.

= Chemical potential of a composition field
{parent=Chemical potential}
{title2=$\mu=\delta F/\delta\phi$}

For a composition <free energy> $F=\int[f(\phi)+\kappa|\nabla\phi|^2/2]$, the local chemical potential is the <functional derivative>
$$
\mu=f'(\phi)-\kappa\nabla^2\phi.
$$
At <phase coexistence>, exchanges between the bulk phases require equal <chemical potentials>. Mechanical equilibrium additionally requires equal <pressure> for a flat interface, or a <Young–Laplace equation> pressure jump for a curved interface.

= Chemical equilibrium
{parent=Chemical potential}
{wiki}

At <chemical equilibrium>, a reversible <chemical reaction> has equal forward and reverse <reaction rates>, so its macroscopic composition stays constant. Individual <molecules> can keep changing identity: the reaction has not stopped. This dynamic balance is the definition described by the https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/C01023[IUPAC Gold Book, chemical equilibrium]{ref}. Equal <reaction rates> also need not mean equal <concentrations>. A small population converting rapidly can balance a large population converting slowly.

This distinction gives two useful questions. Which composition is favored at the specified <temperature> and other constraints? <Thermodynamics> answers through <chemical potentials> and an <equilibrium constant>. How quickly does a prepared mixture approach that composition? <Chemical kinetics> answers through <reaction rates>. Knowing the destination does not determine the journey's duration. A composition that barely changes during observation may reflect extremely slow <chemical kinetics>, so constancy alone does not establish <chemical equilibrium>.

Consider the idealized elementary <chemical reaction>
$$
\mathrm A\rightleftharpoons\mathrm B,
\qquad k_f=0.3\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},\qquad k_r=0.1\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}.
$$
Assume a well-mixed <closed system> at fixed <temperature> and <volume>, with first-order <law of mass action> rates in both directions and no competing <chemical reactions>. Write $a=[\mathrm A]$ and $b=[\mathrm B]$ for <concentrations> in $\mathrm M=\mathrm{mol}/\mathrm L$. The total $a+b=C=1\,\mathrm M$ is conserved because each conversion replaces one <molecule> by one <molecule>. This conservation is special to the chosen <stoichiometry>.

The forward and reverse <reaction rates>, measured as conversions per unit <volume> per unit time, are $J_f=k_fa$ and $J_r=k_rb$. Hence
$$
\frac{da}{dt}=-k_fa+k_rb=-(k_f+k_r)a+k_rC.
$$
Setting this <derivative> to zero gives
$$
a_{\rm eq}=\frac{k_rC}{k_f+k_r}=0.25\,\mathrm M,
\qquad b_{\rm eq}=0.75\,\mathrm M.
$$
With $a(0)=1\,\mathrm M$ and $b(0)=0$, solving the <ordinary differential equation> yields
$$
a(t)=\bigl(0.25+0.75e^{-0.4t/\mathrm s}\bigr)\,\mathrm M,
\qquad b(t)=\bigl(0.75-0.75e^{-0.4t/\mathrm s}\bigr)\,\mathrm M.
$$
At $t=5\,\mathrm s$, these are approximately $0.3515\,\mathrm M$ and $0.6485\,\mathrm M$. Initially $J_f=0.3\,\mathrm{M/s}$ and $J_r=0$. At <chemical equilibrium>, both are $0.075\,\mathrm{M/s}$, while the net <reaction rate> $J_f-J_r$ is zero. The persistent exchange is visible in the lower panel even after the upper panel has nearly flattened.

Why does <chemical equilibrium> also involve a <free energy> minimum? For a <closed system> at fixed <temperature> and <pressure>, without imposed non-expansion <work>, stable equilibrium minimizes <Gibbs free energy> over compositions allowed by conserved elemental inventories. See https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/3-00-thermodynamics-of-materials-fall-2002/14c95abd6fac023d84bd33c5d74d787d_lecture_02_oneside.PDF[W. C. Carter, MIT thermodynamics notes, sections 2-1.14 and 2-1.19]. This is <constrained Gibbs minimization for chemical equilibrium>: freely minimizing over unrelated species amounts would change the inventory. At fixed <temperature> and <volume>, the appropriate potential is <Helmholtz free energy>. In either setting, varying an allowed <extent of reaction> gives the corresponding <free energy> slope $\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i$, with molar <chemical potentials> and signed <stoichiometric vector> entries. An interior equilibrium has zero slope; stability requires a minimum. For A converting to B, this says $\mu_{\mathrm B}=\mu_{\mathrm A}$.

The beauty of this connection is that molecular exchange, a macroscopic composition, and a geometric minimum describe the same balance. In the ideal concentration model, let $x=b/C$ and choose the standard molar <chemical potential> difference consistent with $K=3$. Up to a composition-independent constant, the fixed-volume <Helmholtz free energy> then obeys
$$
\frac{F}{nRT}=(1-x)\ln(1-x)+x\ln x-x\ln3,
$$
where $n$ is the conserved total <amount of substance> and $R$ the <molar gas constant>. Its <derivative> is $\ln[x/(1-x)]-\ln3$, vanishing at $x=3/4$. Its second <derivative> is $1/x+1/(1-x)>0$ inside $0<x<1$, so this balance is the unique minimum. The mixing <entropy> terms explain why the favored composition still contains A.

= Dehydration equilibrium with ideal-gas fluid
{parent=Chemical equilibrium}
{title2=$\Delta_rG=0$}

For a <mineral> reaction producing one mole of water, let $H^\circ,S^\circ$ refer to the $P^\circ$ <standard state> and let the solids’ volume change $\Delta V_s$ be constant. If $A_s$ is the product/reactant solid-activity ratio, the <Gibbs energy of reaction> is
$$
\Delta_rG=H^\circ-TS^\circ+\Delta V_s(P-P^\circ)
+RT\ln\left(A_s\frac{f_{\mathrm{H_2O}}}{P^\circ}\right).
$$
For temperature-independent specified activities and fugacity, equilibrium gives
$$
T=\frac{H^\circ+\Delta V_s(P-P^\circ)}
{S^\circ-R\ln(A_sf_{\mathrm{H_2O}}/P^\circ)}.
$$
The gas <pressure> work is already represented by the fugacity term; adding a second constant water-volume term double counts it. For an <ideal gas>, $f=P$; a given <fugacity coefficient> gives $f=\phi_fP$. If these quantities depend on <temperature>, the equilibrium equation must instead be solved implicitly. <Pressure> units in the solid volume correction must match those used for $\Delta V_s$.

= Number-based dissociation equilibrium ratio
{parent=Chemical equilibrium}

For ideal-gas single-particle functions $Z_1^{(i)}=C_iVT^{3/2}$ and reaction $A\rightleftharpoons2B$, equilibrium gives $K_N=N_B^2/N_A=(C_B^2/C_A)VT^{3/2}$. This particle-number ratio contains a <volume> factor; the concentration ratio $n_B^2/n_A=K_N/V$ does not.

= Reaction formula-unit conservation
{parent=Chemical equilibrium}

For $A\rightleftharpoons2B$, the conserved amount is $M=N_A+N_B/2$. The total particle count $N_A+N_B$ changes during reaction. Defining a dissociated fraction by $\alpha=N_B/(2M)$ gives $N_A=M(1-\alpha)$.

= Dissociation fraction versus conserved density
{parent=Reaction formula-unit conservation}

For $A\rightleftharpoons2B$, $\alpha^2/(1-\alpha)=K_N/(4M)$. The left side increases monotonically on $0<\alpha<1$, so increasing the conserved formula-unit <density> at fixed <temperature> reduces dissociation in the ideal model.

= Ionic product of water

The product of hydrogen-ion and hydroxide-ion activities at a specified temperature. In dilute aqueous solution at 25 degrees Celsius, its usual <concentration> approximation is ten to the minus fourteen.

= Dimerization
{parent=Chemical equilibrium}

Reversible association of two molecular units to form a <dimer>.

= Ocean carbonate chemistry
{parent=Chemical equilibrium}

The linked equilibria among dissolved <carbon dioxide>, bicarbonate, carbonate and hydrogen ions in seawater. At a fixed alkalinity, added carbon changes speciation and acidity, limiting how much extra carbon can be held at a given atmospheric <partial pressure>. Exchange and chemical partitioning therefore do not behave like irreversible destruction by a single first-order sink.

= Carbonate compensation
{parent=Ocean carbonate chemistry}

Adjustment of ocean and sediment carbonate chemistry through dissolution or burial of carbonate minerals. Following a carbon-dioxide perturbation, additional carbonate dissolution can increase alkalinity and permit further ocean uptake. The adjustment is much slower than surface gas exchange, operating over millennial timescales.

{c}

The derivative of <Gibbs energy> with respect to reaction extent at fixed <temperature> and <pressure>: $\Delta_rG=\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i$. For a reversible electrochemical cell reaction, $\Delta_rG=-nFE$.

= Pressure correction to a mineral reaction
{parent=Gibbs energy of reaction}
{title2=$\Delta G_{\rm solids}=\int_{P_0}^P\Delta V_{\rm solids}\,dP$}

At fixed <temperature>, $dG=V\,dP$ gives the pressure correction for the participating solids. Constant reaction volume reduces the integral to $\Delta V_{\rm solids}(P-P_0)$. A gas product treated by <chemical fugacity> instead contributes $\nu RT\ln(f/P_0)$ relative to its reference state. Adding a separate gas-volume correction to that term would double count the same pressure dependence.

= Chemical equilibrium of a classical spin gas
{parent=Chemical equilibrium}
{title2=$n_\pm=n_Qe^{\beta\mu}e^{\pm\beta\mu_BB}$}

For a nondegenerate spin-one-half ideal gas with energies $\mp\mu_BB(\mathbf r)$, the two component <chemical potentials> are $k_BT\ln(n_\pm/n_Q)\mp\mu_BB$. Diffusive and spin-conversion equilibrium require one spatially constant common chemical potential, provided spin conversion is allowed. Hence the displayed densities give total <number density> $n=2n_Qe^{\beta\mu}\cosh(\beta\mu_BB)$ and <magnetization> $M=2\mu_Bn_Qe^{\beta\mu}\sinh(\beta\mu_BB)=n\mu_B\tanh(\beta\mu_BB)$. The high-temperature fixed-density limit is $M\simeq n\mu_B^2B/(k_BT)$, a <Curie law>. At fixed chemical potential the density itself varies with temperature, so the unnormalized magnetization need not have a pure $1/T$ dependence.

= Chemical disequilibrium
{parent=Chemical equilibrium}

A chemical system is out of local <chemical equilibrium> when reaction adjustment cannot keep pace with transport, imposed changes or external driving. Comparing the <chemical relaxation time> with transport and irradiation times identifies plausible departures. <Disequilibrium chemistry in an exoplanet atmosphere> combines this principle with mixing, horizontal advection and <atmospheric photochemistry>.

= Zero chemical potential from number-changing reactions
{parent=Chemical equilibrium}
{title2=$\mu_\phi=0$}

For one particle species in <chemical equilibrium> under $2\phi\leftrightarrow3\phi$, chemical balance gives $2\mu_\phi=3\mu_\phi$, hence $\mu_\phi=0$. Elastic $2\phi\leftrightarrow2\phi$ imposes no such constraint. In an expanding universe the number-changing reaction must remain fast compared with the <Hubble parameter>; after chemical freeze-out a nonzero <chemical potential> can develop even if elastic collisions maintain <kinetic equilibrium>.

= Chemical relaxation time
{title2=$\tau_{\rm chem}$}
{parent=Chemical equilibrium}

The chemical relaxation time estimates how rapidly a perturbed abundance returns toward <chemical equilibrium>. Comparing it with the <eddy mixing time> identifies a <chemical quench level>.

For the A-to-B model in <chemical equilibrium>, deviations decay by <exponential decay> with $\tau_{\rm chem}=1/(k_f+k_r)=2.5\,\mathrm s$. After one such interval, $1/e$ of the initial deviation remains; reaching one percent takes $\ln(100)\tau_{\rm chem}\simeq11.51\,\mathrm s$. Multiplying both <rate constants> by two preserves their ratio and the equilibrium composition, but halves $\tau_{\rm chem}$ to $1.25\,\mathrm s$. The equilibrium exchange rates double to $0.15\,\mathrm{M/s}$. Faster exchange therefore coexists with the same final composition and the same <free energy> minimum.

= Equilibrium constant
{title2=$K$}
{parent=Chemical equilibrium}
{wiki}

For a specified <chemical reaction> and <standard state> convention, the dimensionless <equilibrium constant> is the equilibrium value of the <reaction quotient> $Q=\prod_i a_i^{\nu_i}$, where $a_i$ denotes <thermodynamic activity> and $\nu_i$ a signed <stoichiometric vector> entry. <Thermodynamic activities> incorporate the reference state and departures from ideal behavior. See https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/E02177[IUPAC Gold Book, equilibrium constant].

In the ideal concentration model for A converting to B, use $a_{\mathrm A}=[\mathrm A]/c^\circ$ and $a_{\mathrm B}=[\mathrm B]/c^\circ$ with the same reference $c^\circ=1\,\mathrm M$. Their ratio gives $Q=[\mathrm B]/[\mathrm A]$ and $K=0.75/0.25=3$. Equality $K=k_f/k_r$ applies to this elementary first-order pair; it is not a universal ratio of two measured <rate constants> for an arbitrary multistep <chemical reaction>.

At a specified constant <temperature>, with consistent <standard states>, the standard molar reaction <Gibbs free energy> is
$$
\Delta_rG^\circ=\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i^\circ=-RT\ln K,
\qquad \Delta_rG=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\ln Q=RT\ln(Q/K).
$$
Here $\Delta_rG$ is the local slope per <mole> of <extent of reaction> at fixed <temperature> and <pressure>, rather than the finite total <Gibbs free energy> change between two mixtures. At equilibrium $\Delta_rG=0$, while $\Delta_rG^\circ$ generally remains nonzero. For $K=3$ at $298\,\mathrm K$, it is about $-2.72\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}$. For positive activities, $Q<K$ favors forward conversion and $Q>K$ favors reverse conversion; neither sign supplies a <reaction rate>. See https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-111sc-principles-of-chemical-science-fall-2014/ec12f084d867a5e5532b12ee99bdc767_MIT5_111F14_Lec18.pdf[MIT 5.111, Lecture 18, reaction free energy and equilibrium].

= Dimensionless concentration equilibrium constant
{parent=Equilibrium constant}
{title2=$K_c=\prod_i(c_i/c^\circ)^{\nu_i}$}

For ideal <concentration> activities and signed stoichiometric coefficients $\nu_i$, $K_c=\prod_i(c_i/c^\circ)^{\nu_i}$ at equilibrium. Its numerical value depends on the stated <standard concentration> when $\sum_i\nu_i\ne0$. Number concentrations require a number-concentration reference; molar concentrations require the corresponding molar reference.

= Association constant
{parent=Equilibrium constant}
{title2=$K_a$}

For $A+B\rightleftharpoons AB$, a concentration <association constant> is $K_a=[AB]/([A][B])$. Comparisons require matched solvent, temperature and standard-state conventions.

= Standard state
{parent=Chemical equilibrium}

A standard state is a specified reference state used to define a species' standard <chemical potential> $\mu_i^\circ$. The reference convention, including <temperature>, must be consistent when calculating <thermodynamic activities>, <reaction quotients>, and <equilibrium constants>.

= Standard pressure
{parent=Standard state}
{title2=$p^\circ$}

The reference <pressure> used to define a standard gas activity; a common convention is one bar. Normalizing <pressure> by this reference keeps a thermodynamic <equilibrium constant> dimensionless.

= Standard concentration
{parent=Standard state}
{title2=$c^\circ$}

The reference concentration $c^\circ$ used to make concentration activities dimensionless. Changing this reference changes the numerical dimensionless <equilibrium constant> when the reaction changes the total number of solute or gas molecules.

= Thermodynamic activity
{parent=Standard state}
{title2=$a_i=\exp[(\mu_i-\mu_i^\circ)/(RT)]$}

The thermodynamic activity is the dimensionless quantity defined by $\mu_i=\mu_i^\circ+RT\ln a_i$, where $R$ is the <molar gas constant>. For an ideal dilute solution with a specified <concentration> reference, $a_i=c_i/c^\circ$; departures from ideality are represented by an <activity coefficient> multiplying this ratio.

= Chemical fugacity
{parent=Thermodynamic activity}
{title2=$f$}

An effective gas <pressure> defined by $\mu=\mu^\circ+RT\ln(f/p^\circ)$ in a specified standard-state convention. It approaches the gas <pressure> at low density and differs from the dimensionless <fugacity (statistical mechanics)>.

= Fugacity coefficient
{parent=Chemical fugacity}
{title2=$\phi=f/P$}

The dimensionless ratio of a gas species' <chemical fugacity> to its pressure in the pure-fluid convention. Its <thermodynamic activity> relative to a gas reference pressure $P^\circ$ is $\phi P/P^\circ$. For mixtures the pressure denominator uses the species' partial pressure. The ideal-gas limit has $\phi=1$.

= Reaction quotient
{parent=Chemical equilibrium}
{title2=$Q=\prod_i a_i^{\nu_i}$}

For a <chemical reaction> with signed <stoichiometric vector> entries $\nu_i$, positive for products and negative for reactants, the reaction quotient is the product of the species' <thermodynamic activities> raised to these powers. It equals the <equilibrium constant> at <chemical equilibrium>, with all quantities using the same <standard state> convention.

= Photon chemical potential
{parent=Chemical equilibrium}

The chemical potential of photons in thermal equilibrium is zero because their number is not conserved.
