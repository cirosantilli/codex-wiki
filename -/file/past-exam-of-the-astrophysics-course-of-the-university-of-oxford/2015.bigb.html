== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A close <binary star> can produce two <compact stellar objects> if both initial stellar <masses> are sufficiently large. The initially more massive star evolves first, transfers material or loses its envelope, and leaves a <neutron star> or <black hole> after core collapse. The system must remain bound despite <mass loss> and any natal kick. Subsequent evolution of the companion can produce a <common envelope>: orbital <energy> transferred to the envelope can expel it and greatly reduce the separation. A second collapse must again leave a bound pair. A sufficiently close surviving <binary star> then loses orbital <energy> and <angular momentum> through <gravitational waves> and may merge. Stable transfer, winds and kicks make this a possible channel rather than an inevitable outcome; dynamical encounters in dense stellar systems provide another channel.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

A <neutron star merger>, or a <neutron star> tidally disrupted by a <black hole> outside its capture region, can leave a hot <accretion disk> around the remnant. Rapid <accretion> can power a relativistic jet and a short <gamma-ray burst>. Beaming means that an observer must lie sufficiently near the jet axis, and not every merger produces an observable burst. A clean <binary black hole> merger has no substantial baryonic fuel and is not ordinarily expected to power this mechanism. Disruption in a neutron-star–black-hole pair depends on <binary mass ratio>, <black-hole spin> and the <neutron star>'s <stellar radius>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The changing <mass quadrupole moment> produces an inspiral <gravitational-wave> signal whose <frequency> and amplitude increase toward merger. The <chirp mass> controls the leading <frequency> evolution, while higher-order phasing and the merger carry information about <binary mass ratio> and spins. <Neutron stars> also introduce tidal effects. These signals motivate a search with an interferometer such as <LIGO>; the supplied ranges quantify the paper's proposed detection prospects, without assuming any particular later observing-run performance.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Neutron-rich material ejected dynamically or in remnant-disk outflows can undergo the <r-process>: successive neutron captures, followed by beta decays, make heavy nuclei. The yield depends on the amount ejected, its entropy, expansion and <electron> fraction; not all outflow is equally neutron rich. Radioactive products can power a <kilonova>. A neutron-star–black-hole event contributes if matter escapes capture. An isolated <binary black hole> merger has no analogous reservoir of neutron-rich baryonic material, so it contributes essentially no such <nucleosynthesis>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use constant component <masses> and a circular <Keplerian orbit>. Then $J=\mu\sqrt{GMA}$ gives $\dot J/J=\dot A/(2A)$, while $P=2\pi A^{3/2}/\sqrt{GM}$ gives $\dot P/P=3\dot A/(2A)=3\dot J/J$. Dividing the supplied loss law by $J$ therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\frac{\dot P}{P}=-\frac{96}{5}\frac{G^3\mu M^2}{c^5A^4}},\qquad
\dot A=-\frac{64}{5}\frac{G^3\mu M^2}{c^5A^3}.
$$
The positive instantaneous <orbital decay timescale> is $\tau_P=P/|\dot P|$, since $\dot P<0$. For $t_G=10^{10}$ yr, the requested prescription gives
$$
A_{\max}=\left(\frac{96G^3\mu M^2t_G}{5c^5}\right)^{1/4},\qquad
P_{\max}=\frac{2\pi A_{\max}^{3/2}}{\sqrt{GM}}.
$$
It is important to distinguish this prescription from the integrated <gravitational-wave inspiral time>. Integrating $\dot A=-C/A^3$ gives
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm merge}=\frac{5c^5A^4}{256G^3\mu M^2}=\frac38\tau_P}.
$$
Finite contact radii supply a small lower-limit correction when the initial separation is much larger than the compact objects. Thus the periods below satisfy the printed instantaneous-timescale criterion and have a formal merger time $3.75$ Gyr. If instead the requirement is literally $t_{\rm merge}=10$ Gyr, every period limit is larger by $(8/3)^{3/8}=1.445$. Eccentric systems require a different decay calculation.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Treat the quoted distances as sharp isotropic detection radii in a homogeneous Euclidean population. With galaxy <number density> $n_g$, the number surveyed is $N_g=(4\pi/3)n_gD^3$. One event per year requires a per-galaxy merger rate $\mathcal R=1/N_g$ per year. Thus
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|r|l}
\text{pair}&N_g&\mathcal R\ (\mathrm{yr^{-1}\,galaxy^{-1}})\\\hline
\mathrm{NS+NS}&6.79\times10^5&1.47\times10^{-6}\\
\mathrm{NS+BH}&6.90\times10^6&1.45\times10^{-7}\\
\mathrm{BH+BH}&1.23\times10^8&8.10\times10^{-9}
\end{array}}
$$
The larger <gravitational-wave> range gives enormous volume leverage: even a rare merger in a typical galaxy can yield frequent detections across the surveyed population. This supports promising detection prospects if astrophysical rates exceed these thresholds. The estimate is not a prediction of the rates themselves. Real sensitivity depends on sky direction, binary inclination, detector duty cycle and a signal-to-noise selection function; a quoted horizon distance is not automatically a volume-averaged range. Cosmological volume and source-time corrections also matter toward the largest quoted distance. The calculation follows the simple assumptions specified by the paper.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Infer the component <masses> and spins from the <gravitational-wave> phasing, using the <chirp mass> together with <binary mass ratio> information. The paper's representative pairs have total <masses> $2.8$, $11.4$ and $20M_\odot$, and the more massive mergers terminate their inspirals at lower frequencies. A black-hole ringdown and its quasi-normal modes identify the final compact remnant, although both a neutron-star–black-hole merger and a sufficiently massive neutron-star pair can also leave a <black hole>.

Tidal deformability and any disruption cutoff provide a more direct signature of a <neutron star> than total <mass> alone. A disrupted <neutron star> may supply a <gamma-ray burst>, <kilonova> and afterglow; these help distinguish matter-containing mergers from a clean black-hole pair. Their absence does not establish a black-hole pair, because jets are beamed and ejecta may be faint or swallowed. Measured <gravitational-wave> <masses> are redshifted detector-frame <masses>; association with a host can supply the <redshift> needed for source-frame <masses>. Classification should combine the waveform and any electromagnetic evidence, allowing for parameter degeneracies.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

An <ultraluminous X-ray source> is a compact, apparently off-nuclear source in another galaxy with an inferred isotropic X-ray <luminosity> typically above $10^{32}$ W, exceeding the classical <Eddington luminosity> of an ordinary stellar-mass accretor. Imaging X-ray surveys revealed unusually bright sources away from galactic nuclei; distance association with the host converted the observed fluxes into surprisingly high <luminosities>. Better angular resolution helps exclude blends and background active nuclei. The 2014 discovery of coherent pulsations established a <neutron star> in M82 X-2, so the class cannot be identified exclusively with <black holes>.<Ultraluminous X-ray Sources in M82 Galaxy by NASA JPL>[8 October 2014: NuSTAR pulsations identify X-2 as a pulsar and establish diversity among the ultraluminous sources]{ref}

An <intermediate-mass black hole> can radiate approximately isotropically below its own <Eddington luminosity>: this avoids extreme super-Eddington emission, but the inferred <mass> requires dynamical or suitable spectral/timing support and is not proved by <luminosity> alone. A stellar-mass accretor with a supercritical <accretion disk> and a wind funnel can combine genuinely super-Eddington output with geometrical beaming; this is compatible with a neutron-star accretor but requires a consistent radiation/flow/<torque> model. Relativistic beaming from a jet is another possibility for some objects, but orientation, spectral and variability evidence must support it. These mechanisms need not apply identically to every member of the observed class.

For spherical radiation with flux $F=L/(4\pi r^2)$, the outward acceleration is $\kappa F/c$. Equating it to $GM/r^2$ gives
$$
\boxed{L_{\rm Edd}=\frac{4\pi GMc}{\kappa}}.
$$
The limit assumes the stated <opacity>, approximate <spherical symmetry> and coupling between the radiatively accelerated plasma and the accreting matter. It is not a universal ceiling on isotropic-equivalent <luminosity> for an anisotropic magnetized flow.

Write the available <accretion> power as $L=\eta\dot M c^2$. Then
$$
\boxed{\dot M_{\rm Edd}=\frac{4\pi GM}{\eta\kappa c}}.
$$
<Accretion> as the <energy> source alone does not fix $\eta$. For radiatively efficient Newtonian <accretion> onto a slowly rotating stellar surface, $\eta\simeq GM/(Rc^2)$ and the corresponding <surface accretion Eddington rate> is
$$
\boxed{\dot M_{\rm Edd}\simeq\frac{4\pi Rc}{\kappa}}.
$$
This is the appropriate convention for the numerical neutron-star estimates below, rather than imposing a separate reference efficiency of $0.1$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Using $M=1.4M_\odot$, $R=10^4$ m and the stated <opacity> gives
$$
L_{\rm Edd}=2.06\times10^{31}\ \mathrm W,\qquad
\dot M_{\rm Edd}=1.11\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}=1.76\times10^{-8}M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}.
$$
If the observed X-rays represent isotropic, radiatively efficient surface <accretion> power, then
$$
\boxed{\frac{\dot M}{\dot M_{\rm Edd}}=\frac{L_X}{L_{\rm Edd}}=48.6,\qquad
\dot M\simeq5.38\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}}.
$$
A bolometric correction, beaming, radiation advected into the flow or <energy> carried by a wind would change the mapping from the measured band <luminosity> to the supplied <mass> rate. This estimate keeps the assumptions needed for comparison with the <torque> calculation.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

In <magnetically channelled accretion>, an outer <accretion disk> supplies nearly Keplerian material. Near the <magnetospheric truncation radius>, magnetic stresses disrupt the disk and guide matter along field lines onto polar <accretion columns>. Shocks and settling near the surface release gravitational binding <energy>, and an oblique magnetic geometry makes the rotating emission pattern pulsate.

The <corotation radius> satisfies $\Omega_K(r_{\rm co})=\Omega_*=2\pi/P$, hence
$$
\boxed{r_{\rm co}=\left(\frac{GMP^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3}=2.07\times10^6\ \mathrm m}.
$$
The <accretion Alfvén radius> estimates where magnetic stress becomes comparable with the inflow's dynamical stress; it is the magnetic truncation scale in this application, not the Alfvén critical point of an outward stellar wind. The supplied seventh-root scaling follows from a dipole's rapidly decreasing <magnetic pressure> and an accretion-pressure estimate. Its coefficient depends on disk geometry. Ordinary inward surface <accretion> requires the magnetosphere to remain outside the star and usually inside or near corotation, $R\lesssim r_{\rm Alf}\lesssim r_{\rm co}$. Beyond corotation, a rapidly moving magnetosphere can transfer <angular momentum> outward and inhibit inflow through the <propeller effect>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $\tau_*=P/|\dot P|=300$ yr. With constant moment of inertia, the observed positive spin-up <torque> is
$$
N_*=I\dot\Omega_* =\frac{I\Omega_*}{\tau_*}=4.84\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{N\,m}.
$$
Nearly Keplerian material at the inner disk carries specific <angular momentum> $\ell=\sqrt{GMr_{\rm Alf}}$. Equating its inflow rate to the observed <torque> gives
$$
\dot M_{\rm acc}=\frac{I(2\pi/P)}{\tau_*\sqrt{GMr_{\rm Alf}}}
=\frac{I(2\pi/P)}{\tau_*\sqrt{GMr_{\rm co}}}\left(\frac{r_{\rm Alf}}{r_{\rm co}}\right)^{-1/2}.
$$
In the <surface accretion Eddington rate> convention,
$$
\boxed{\dot M_{\rm acc}=2.23\left(\frac{r_{\rm Alf}}{r_{\rm co}}\right)^{-1/2}\dot M_{\rm Edd}
\simeq2\left(\frac{r_{\rm Alf}}{r_{\rm co}}\right)^{-1/2}\dot M_{\rm Edd}}.
$$
The printed coefficient is therefore recovered at its intended approximate precision. This is a material-torque model; magnetic torques, wind angular-momentum loss and a changing stellar moment of inertia are not included.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The <accreting-pulsar luminosity and material-torque consistency> test shows a concrete tension between the simplest isotropic <luminosity> estimate and the material-torque estimate. Making their rates equal requires
$$
\frac{r_{\rm Alf}}{r_{\rm co}}=\left(\frac{2.23}{48.6}\right)^2=2.11\times10^{-3},\qquad
\boxed{r_{\rm Alf}\simeq4.4\ \mathrm{km}<R_{\rm NS}}.
$$
An exterior <accretion Alfvén radius> cannot lie inside a 10-km star. Even taking its smallest exterior value, $r_{\rm Alf}=R$, the observed <torque> permits only about $32\dot M_{\rm Edd}$ in this model, below the $49\dot M_{\rm Edd}$ isotropic estimate. The discrepancy is modest enough to be sensitive to the approximate radius and moment of inertia, but it prevents claiming exact simultaneous agreement under the stated central values.

Near corotation, the <torque> gives only $2.23\dot M_{\rm Edd}$. If its <radiative efficiency> is unchanged, beaming into a fraction about $2.23/48.6\simeq0.046$ of the sky would reconcile that intrinsic <luminosity> with the apparent isotropic value. The given truncation formula would then require $B\sim2.6\times10^8$ T for a magnetosphere near corotation. Other possibilities include substantial opposing magnetic <torque>, outflows separating the disk supply rate from the surface rate, a different <radiative efficiency> and magnetically altered <opacity>. These are changes to the elementary model that need independent evidence, rather than a license to use an unphysical truncation radius. Coherent pulsations already identify the accretor as a <neutron star>; they do not by themselves choose between these accretion-flow refinements.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use $c=1$ and a spatially flat background dominated by the stated perfect fluid, so $4\pi Ga^2\rho_0=3\mathcal H^2/2$. In <Newtonian gauge>, the scalar <energy> and momentum constraints from the linearized <Einstein field equations> combine to eliminate the potential's time derivative and give the stated relativistic Poisson constraint. Its source contains the velocity correction to the coordinate density perturbation. On sub-horizon scales that correction is small for the ordinary growing modes, recovering the <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity>; near or outside the horizon the gravitational field cannot be sourced by $\delta$ alone. Equality of the two metric potentials also assumes negligible <scalar anisotropic stress>.

The fluid equations are the linear energy-continuity and momentum-Euler parts of $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=0$. Here $c_s^2=\delta p/\delta\rho$ is the relevant dimensionless sound-speed squared in the adopted fluid closure; for a constant-$w$ adiabatic barotropic fluid it equals $w$. A fluid with entropy perturbations needs additional <pressure> information. The equations actually use $\theta=\nabla\cdot\mathbf v$ in Fourier space, the <peculiar-velocity divergence>. Calling it simply momentum density is imprecise: it is the divergence of the velocity, with momentum weighted by $\rho_0+p_0$ before normalization. In particular $\theta$ must have inverse-conformal-time units for the displayed continuity equation to be dimensionally consistent.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For the <shifted density perturbation in Newtonian gauge>, set $\Delta_R=\delta-4\Psi$. The radiation continuity equation gives $\Delta_R'=-4\theta/3$, and its Euler equation gives
$$
\Delta_R''=-\frac{k^2}{3}\Delta_R-\frac{8k^2}{3}\Psi.
$$
Writing $D_R=k^2+6\mathcal H^2$, the relativistic constraint becomes
$$
\Psi=-\frac{3\mathcal H^2}{2D_R}\left(\Delta_R-\frac{3\mathcal H}{k^2}\Delta_R'\right).
$$
Eliminating the potential therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\Delta_R''+\frac{12\mathcal H^3}{k^2+6\mathcal H^2}\Delta_R'
+\frac{k^2}{3}\left(1-\frac{12\mathcal H^2}{k^2+6\mathcal H^2}\right)\Delta_R=0}.
$$
This has the requested $\mathcal F=12\mathcal H^2/(k^2+6\mathcal H^2)$. Well inside the <sound horizon>, $k\gg\mathcal H$, the potential contribution is suppressed relative to the density term and $\delta\simeq\Delta_R$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\delta=C\cos(k\eta/\sqrt3)+D\sin(k\eta/\sqrt3)}.
$$
These are sound waves: radiation-pressure gradients restore a compression, while inertia carries the fluid through equilibrium. The perfect-fluid approximation neglects diffusion damping and freely streaming species' anisotropic stress. It gives the high-frequency limiting oscillations, not a claim that every radiation-era perturbation is an undriven sinusoid at horizon entry.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For pressureless matter, $\Delta_M=\delta-3\Psi$ obeys $\Delta_M'=-\theta$. The Euler equation gives
$$
\Delta_M''+\mathcal H\Delta_M'=-k^2\Psi.
$$
The constraint is now
$$
\Psi=-\frac{3\mathcal H^2}{2k^2+9\mathcal H^2}\left(\Delta_M-\frac{3\mathcal H}{k^2}\Delta_M'\right).
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\Delta_M''+(1+\mathcal G)\mathcal H\Delta_M'-\frac13\mathcal Gk^2\Delta_M=0,\qquad
\mathcal G=\frac{9\mathcal H^2}{2k^2+9\mathcal H^2}}.
$$
For a matter-dominated flat background, $a\propto\eta^2$ and $\mathcal H=2/\eta$. On small scales $\mathcal G\ll1$ but $\mathcal Gk^2\to9\mathcal H^2/2$: the last term must not be dropped with $\mathcal G$. The equation becomes
$$
\Delta_M''+\frac2\eta\Delta_M'-\frac6{\eta^2}\Delta_M=0,
\qquad \Delta_M=C\eta^2+D\eta^{-3}.
$$
Since $\Psi$ is small relative to the growing density term on these scales, this recovers $\boxed{\delta_{\rm grow}\propto\eta^2\propto a}$.

There is a printed error in the requested super-horizon decaying mode. At $k\ll\mathcal H$, $\mathcal G\to1$ and the leading equation is $\Delta_M''+4\Delta_M'/\eta=0$, hence
$$
\boxed{\Delta_M=\alpha+\beta\eta^{-3}\quad\text{to leading super-horizon order}}.
$$
The printed $\alpha+\beta/\eta$ fails its own equation: for $\Delta_M=1/\eta$, the left side is $-2/\eta^3\ne0$ at leading order. The $1/\eta$ decaying solution would instead result from using the radiation-era background $\mathcal H=1/\eta$ in this limiting damping term. Finite-$k$ corrections restore terms of relative order $(k\eta)^2$ and do not repair the wrong leading power.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

A <power spectrum> describes the variance of Fourier amplitudes as a function of wavenumber. In a statistically homogeneous continuum convention,
$$
\langle\Delta_M(\mathbf k)\Delta_M^*(\mathbf k')\rangle=(2\pi)^3\delta_D(\mathbf k-\mathbf k')P_M(k).
$$
The paper's $\langle|\Delta_M(k)|^2\rangle$ is the corresponding finite-volume or normalization-suppressed statement. For statistical isotropy only $k=|\mathbf k|$ remains. The dimensionless variance per logarithmic interval is $k^3P_M(k)/(2\pi^2)$, a different quantity.

The response changes around $2k^2\sim9\mathcal H^2$, or $k\sim2.1\mathcal H$: the velocity contribution to the relativistic constraint and the effective growth equation change relative importance across the horizon-scale transition. Large-scale $\Delta_M$ is approximately constant for its regular mode, while short-scale density perturbations grow gravitationally. A smooth initial potential spectrum can therefore acquire a different $k$ dependence in the density spectrum. For illustration, a constant matter-era potential gives $\Delta_M=-(5+k^2\eta^2/6)\Psi$, so sufficiently small-scale $P_M$ has four additional powers of $k$ relative to $P_\Psi$, while the very large-scale limits have the same power dependence.

This identifies a dynamical transition, not a universal prediction of the primordial spectrum or an exact spectral break at $2\mathcal H$ for arbitrary initial data. The familiar late-time matter-spectrum turnover also retains the history of horizon entry around radiation–matter equality; it is not generically tied to the current Hubble scale. Initial conditions and the intervening expansion history are needed for an actual spectrum.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $R=CE^a\rho_0^bt^d$ for a uniform ambient <mass density>. Equating <mass>, length and time dimensions gives $a+b=0$, $2a-3b=1$ and $-2a+d=0$. Thus the <Sedov-Taylor blast wave> has
$$
\boxed{R=\xi\left(\frac{Et^2}{\rho_0}\right)^{1/5},\qquad V_s=\dot R=\frac{2R}{5t}}.
$$
The dimensionless coefficient depends on the gas's <adiabatic index> and on whether $R$ denotes the shock radius. Solving the similarity profile for a monatomic ideal gas gives $\xi\simeq1.15$, so taking it as unity is an order-of-magnitude approximation rather than an extra dimensional result.

Timed photographs of the 1945 atomic explosion provide a direct scale test: the measured fireball radius follows $R\propto t^{2/5}$ over the relevant blast-wave interval, and inserting the independently established explosion <energy> and atmospheric density gives a coefficient of order unity. The published photographic analysis tested the radius-time law and used its normalization to infer an explosion <energy> of the correct nuclear-test scale. This illustrates both the success and the limits of dimensional reasoning: the power law is fixed dimensionally, whereas the numerical coefficient requires a profile calculation or measured normalization.https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1950.0050[Part II, photographic radius-time measurements and explosion-energy estimate; the 20–185 m interval tests the predicted similarity scaling]{ref}

Neglecting the exterior <temperature> requires a strong shock, $\mathcal M_s=V_s/c_{s,u}\gg1$, or $\rho_0V_s^2\gg p_u$. The swept-up <mass> must dominate the initial ejecta for this energy-conserving similarity stage, while radiative losses, gravity, ambient gradients and continuing <energy> input must be unimportant. A very hot exterior or a late weak disturbance introduces a sound-speed scale and invalidates the cold-upstream similarity assumptions.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the local shock rest frame. Let $u$ and $d$ label upstream and downstream gas, with positive normal speeds $v_u,v_d$ through the shock. The unshocked ISM is almost stationary in the stellar frame, so $v_u\simeq V_s$, whereas the shocked gas moves outward at $V_s-v_d$ in that frame. For an ideal monatomic gas, $\gamma=5/3$; neglect upstream <pressure> and enthalpy in the strong-shock limit. Conservation of <mass>, momentum and <energy> per unit <mass> gives
$$
\rho_uv_u=\rho_dv_d,\qquad p_d+\rho_dv_d^2=\rho_uv_u^2,\qquad
\frac{v_u^2}{2}=\frac{v_d^2}{2}+\frac\gamma{\gamma-1}\frac{p_d}{\rho_d}.
$$
Put $q=\rho_d/\rho_u=v_u/v_d$. The momentum equation gives $p_d=\rho_uv_u^2(1-1/q)$. Substitution into the <energy> equation gives
$$
1=\frac1{q^2}+\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}\frac{q-1}{q^2}.
$$
Discarding the unshocked root $q=1$ leaves
$$
\boxed{q=\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}=4,\quad v_d=\frac{V_s}{4},\quad
p_d=\frac34\rho_uV_s^2}.
$$
The downstream speed in the stellar frame is therefore $3V_s/4$, not $V_s/4$. These conditions describe immediate shock heating; subsequent cooling and evolution behind the front require the downstream flow profile. Cosmic-ray <pressure>, radiative <energy> loss inside the shock layer or a different equation of state would alter the compression ratio.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Let the mean particle <mass> be $\bar m=\mu m_H$, with $n_u$ counting total particles so that $\rho_u=n_u\bar m$. The ideal-gas law and the strong-shock <pressure> give the immediate post-shock <temperature>
$$
\boxed{T_d=\frac{p_d\bar m}{\rho_dk_B}=\frac{3\bar mV_s^2}{16k_B}
=\frac{3\bar m\xi^2}{100k_B}\left(\frac E{\rho_u}\right)^{2/5}t^{-6/5}}.
$$
The robust prediction is $T_d\propto t^{-6/5}$. For a transparent numerical convention take $\mu=1$, $n_u=10^6\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$ and $\xi=1$. Then
$$
R\simeq4.63\left(\frac t{10^3\ \mathrm{yr}}\right)^{2/5}\ \mathrm{pc},\qquad
\boxed{T_d\simeq7.45\times10^7\left(\frac t{10^3\ \mathrm{yr}}\right)^{-6/5}\ \mathrm K}.
$$
For an ionized mixture with $\mu=0.6$ under the same total-particle-density convention, these normalizations become $5.13$ pc and $5.49\times10^7$ K. The paper does not specify composition or whether its quoted density instead counts hydrogen nuclei; that convention must be stated, not hidden in a precise age.

The <ambient-temperature limit of an adiabatic blast wave> gives a formal matching estimate, $T_d\sim10^4$ K, gives
$$
\boxed{t_T\sim1.7\times10^6\ \mathrm{yr},\qquad R_T\sim90\ \mathrm{pc}\quad(\mu=1,\ \xi=1)}.
$$
With $\mu=0.6$ the age is about $1.3\times10^6$ yr and the radius stays about 90 pc. This matching criterion already lies outside a very strong shock: $T_d=T_u$ in the strong-shock formula corresponds to $\mathcal M_s=\sqrt{16/(3\gamma)}\simeq1.79$. A separate sonic estimate $V_s\sim c_{s,u}$ gives about $4.4\times10^6$ yr and 133 pc for $\mu=1$; neither extrapolation is a precise transition solution. Using $\xi=1.15$ increases these inferred matching ages and radii by factors $\xi^{5/3}$.

In a real supernova remnant, substantial radiation losses commonly end the energy-conserving phase before these formal pressure-merging limits. The relevant comparison is $t_{\rm cool}\sim(3/2)nk_BT/(n_en_i\Lambda(T))$ against the expansion time. A cooling function, composition and ionization state are needed to calculate that earlier age and radius; they are not supplied. The numerical values above answer the ambient-temperature breakdown estimate under explicit assumptions and must not be presented as a uniquely determined radiative shell-formation age.

After cooling, an interior-pressure-driven shell can approximately follow $R\propto t^{2/7}$ while <pressure> remains important. In the limiting momentum-conserving stage, $M_s\dot R\simeq\mathrm{const}$ and $M_s\propto\rho_uR^3$ give $\boxed{R\propto t^{1/4}}$. Ultimately ambient <pressure> and turbulent motions make the disturbance merge with the ISM. These slower stages respect the reduced <energy> budget rather than continuing an adiabatic strong-shock law indefinitely.

== 5
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

A <planetary nebula> combines a hot post-asymptotic-giant-branch stellar core, its ionized expelled envelope, neutral/molecular material and <interstellar dust>. Its ultraviolet-to-infrared <spectral energy distribution> is therefore not a single-temperature blackbody. The principal components are:

* **Central-star continuum.** A hot stellar photosphere emits strongly in the ultraviolet, especially the ionizing extreme ultraviolet, with its longer-wavelength tail extending through the optical and near infrared. Stellar-atmosphere absorption features and wind lines can be superposed. A companion can add a distinct cool stellar continuum.
* **Nebular continuum.** Thermal <bremsstrahlung>, <nebular free-bound continuum> emission and hydrogen two-photon emission produce a broad continuum. Free-bound edges, especially the Balmer discontinuity at 364.6 nm and the Paschen discontinuity near 820 nm, can be apparent. Hydrogen two-photon decay redistributes the Ly-alpha transition <energy> into a continuum of pairs rather than one 121.6-nm <photon>.
* **Gas emission lines.** Hydrogen and helium <recombination lines> include H-alpha, H-beta and He II 468.6 nm. Collisionally excited lines of metal ions can dominate individual bands: examples include optical \[O III\] 495.9/500.7 nm and \[N II\] 654.8/658.3 nm, ultraviolet C III\] near 190.9 nm, and infrared fine-structure transitions such as \[Ne II\] 12.8 micrometres. A broad-band photometric peak may therefore be a line rather than continuum.
* **Dust emission and solid-state structure.** Grains absorb stellar/nebular radiation and reradiate it as a broad infrared continuum, often from a distribution of <temperatures> of order tens to hundreds of kelvin; compact hot dust can add near-infrared emission. Carbon-rich material may show aromatic bands around 3.3, 6.2, 7.7, 8.6 and 11.3 micrometres, while oxygen-rich material may show silicate features near 9.7 and 18 micrometres. These are compositional alternatives, not mandatory features of every nebula.
* **Neutral and molecular emission.** Surviving molecular material in dense clumps or a photodissociation region can contribute infrared rotational/rovibrational lines, notably molecular-hydrogen emission such as the 2.12-micrometre line. Shocks as well as ultraviolet pumping can excite it. Colder dust continues into the far infrared, and low-lying CO rotational lines extend the overall spectrum into the submillimetre/millimetre range.

\Image[2015-planetary-nebula-spectral-components.png]
{title=Schematic stellar, nebular and dust components of a planetary-nebula spectrum; arbitrary relative normalizations}

The observed mixture also contains attenuation by internal and foreground <interstellar extinction>. A spectrum containing all these components need not spatially coincide: stellar light, ionized gas, dusty clumps and the molecular envelope sample different regions.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

**Stellar radiation and nebular ionization.** The <planetary-nebula spectral energy distribution> is constrained by a shared radiation and thermal balance. The central star's <effective temperature>, <luminosity> and evolutionary state set its continuum and ionizing-photon supply. A hotter star shifts the ionizing spectrum and allows higher ionization stages; the total recombination output also depends on how many ionizing <photons> the gas actually absorbs. In an ionization-bounded nebula, approximate <photoionization> balance is
$$
Q_H\simeq\int n_en_p\alpha_B(T_e)\,dV,
$$
whereas a density-bounded nebula can leak <photons>. Dust absorption of ionizing <photons> changes this relation further. Stellar-atmosphere and wind effects make a blackbody an approximation, especially in the extreme ultraviolet. A changing core <temperature> and expanding envelope explain why nebular spectra evolve.

**Nebular continua and <recombination lines>.** Their strength depends on the <emission measure>, roughly $\int n_en_i\,dV$, <electron temperature>, ionic abundances and radiative branching probabilities. For a <recombination line>,
$$
L_{ul}\simeq h\nu_{ul}\alpha^{\rm eff}_{ul}(T_e)\int n_en_{\rm ion}\,dV.
$$
Case-B line ratios assume appropriate trapping of Lyman-series <photons> and escape of the lower-series radiation. Density, optical-depth effects and extinction can alter observed ratios. The free-free emissivity has the schematic dependence $j_\nu^{\rm ff}\propto n_en_iT_e^{-1/2}\exp[-h\nu/(k_BT_e)]g_{\rm ff}$; the <nebular free-bound continuum> also carries ionization thresholds and recombination cross-sections. Two-photon emission depends on the population of the hydrogen $2s$ state and can be reduced by collisional mixing into $2p$. Density clumping matters because these rates are weighted approximately by density squared, not simply by total gas <mass>.

**Collisionally excited lines.** Below a line's <critical density of an atomic transition>,
$$
j_{ul}\simeq n_en(X^i)q_{lu}(T_e)h\nu_{ul},\qquad
q_{lu}\propto T_e^{-1/2}\exp[-\Delta E/(k_BT_e)].
$$
Elemental abundance, ionic fraction and <electron temperature> therefore all enter. The ionizing spectrum and local <ionization parameter> help determine which ion is present. Above the <critical density of an atomic transition>, collisional de-excitation competes with radiative decay and can suppress a forbidden line. Low-energy infrared fine-structure lines are generally less exponentially <temperature> sensitive than high-excitation optical/ultraviolet lines. Metal-line cooling also helps set the <temperature>, so changing an abundance changes both the number of emitters and the thermal balance; line <luminosity> need not be proportional to abundance at fixed everything else.

**Dust and molecules.** <Nebular dust spectral features> identify the carriers as well as contributing to the integrated <luminosity>. In an optically thin dust component,
$$
F_\nu\propto\int \kappa_\nu B_\nu(T_d)\,dM_d.
$$
The grain <mass> sets its normalization, while the radiation field, stellar distance, grain size and absorption efficiency determine its <temperature> distribution. For approximately grey heating, radiative equilibrium suggests $T_d\propto L_*^{1/4}r^{-1/2}$; non-grey grains change this scaling. Long-wavelength <opacity> is often approximated as $\kappa_\nu\propto\nu^{\beta_d}$. Small aromatic grains undergo transient heating, so one equilibrium <temperature> is not adequate for every infrared feature. Carbon-to-oxygen chemistry, grain processing and shielding affect aromatic versus silicate bands. Molecular line emission further depends on molecule abundance, collisional density, <kinetic temperature>, ultraviolet pumping and shocks, while shielding and dissociation set whether molecules survive at all.

Finally, <interstellar extinction> preferentially suppresses ultraviolet and optical light, and dust reradiates some absorbed <energy> in the infrared. Aperture, angular resolution and distance must be treated consistently before comparing stellar, nebular and dust components. These dependencies make the combined spectrum an <energy> and abundance diagnostic, but also prevent a unique reconstruction from a single broad-band colour.

== 6
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Cold <molecular hydrogen> is difficult to observe directly: its homonuclear symmetry forbids electric-dipole rotational transitions, and its lowest useful quadrupole rotational excitation is high compared with a cold cloud's <temperature>. <Carbon monoxide> is relatively abundant, has a permanent dipole moment and has low-lying rotational transitions that can be collisionally excited in cold gas. The low-$J$ lines lie in accessible millimetre bands and suffer much less dust extinction than visible light. They therefore reveal molecular-gas velocities and spatial structure efficiently.

The advantages do not turn CO into a direct <mass> counter without a model. Common-isotope lines can be optically thick; excitation can be subthermal or have multiple <temperatures>. Abundance changes with metallicity, chemistry, shielding and photodissociation, and CO can freeze onto grains. <Molecular hydrogen> can also survive where CO is weak, giving CO-dark gas. A calibrated <carbon-monoxide tracer of molecular hydrogen> conversion may be useful for appropriate cloud populations but should not be transplanted uncritically to newly formed supernova ejecta. The line calculations here infer CO <mass>, not total molecular <mass> unless a CO-to-hydrogen abundance is separately known.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Assume a single-temperature optically thin <local thermodynamic equilibrium> population, negligible line absorption/stimulated corrections, and that the measured line <luminosity> is from the remnant's CO. Each spontaneous $J=1\to0$ decay emits $h\nu_{10}$, giving
$$
N_1=\frac{L_{10}}{A_{10}h\nu_{10}}
=\frac{1.33\times10^{24}}{(7.2\times10^{-8})(6.6261\times10^{-34})(115.3\times10^9)}
=2.42\times10^{53}.
$$
For a <rigid rotor>, $E_J/k_B=T_rJ(J+1)$ with $T_r=h\nu_{10}/(2k_B)=2.767$ K. The degeneracy is $g_J=2J+1$, and the <rotational partition function> is
$$
Z(T)=\sum_{J=0}^\infty(2J+1)e^{-T_rJ(J+1)/T},\qquad
f_1(T)=\frac{3e^{-2T_r/T}}{Z(T)}.
$$
At 20 K the direct sum gives $Z\simeq7.57$, $f_1=0.3005$; the approximation $Z\simeq T/T_r+1/3$ is already adequate. Thus, with molecular <mass> $m_{\rm CO}\simeq28m_u$,
$$
\boxed{M_{\rm CO}=\frac{N_1}{f_1}m_{\rm CO}=3.74\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{kg}\simeq1.88\times10^{-2}M_\odot}.
$$
The remnant age does not enter this optically thin population count. It becomes relevant when estimating emitting volume, density, expansion and optical depth to test the assumptions.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For <optically thin CO rotational thermometry> of the same emitting population, the upper-level abundance ratio is
$$
\frac{N_3}{N_1}=\frac73\exp\left[-\frac{E_3-E_1}{k_BT}\right].
$$
The difference $E_3-E_1$ can be obtained directly from the two successive transition frequencies, $h(\nu_{32}+\nu_{21})$, or approximately from $10k_BT_r$. Using the supplied rounded molecular data gives $(E_3-E_1)/k_B=27.66$ K. Therefore
$$
\frac{L_{32}}{L_{10}}=\frac{7A_{32}\nu_{32}}{3A_{10}\nu_{10}}e^{-27.66/T}
=242.99\,e^{-27.66/T}.
$$
The observed ratio is $2.46\times10^{26}/(1.33\times10^{24})=184.96$, hence
$$
\boxed{T=\frac{27.66}{\ln(242.99/184.96)}\simeq101\ \mathrm K}.
$$
At 20 K the predicted ratio would be only about 61, so the two detections reject that single-temperature optically thin 20-K model. The inferred value is an excitation <temperature> under the stated assumptions, not a model-independent measurement of the gas's <kinetic temperature>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Raising the <temperature> populates higher rotational states and reduces the fraction in $J=1$. At $T\simeq101$ K, the <rotational partition function> gives $f_1\simeq0.0768$, so the same low-$J$ <luminosity> requires
$$
\boxed{M_{\rm CO}\simeq7.36\times10^{-2}M_\odot},
$$
about $3.9$ times the 20-K estimate. Applying the independently computed $J=3$ fraction to the measured high-$J$ line gives the same <mass> within the model, providing a consistency check rather than an independent proof of its assumptions.

Optical depth, density-dependent excitation, multiple <temperature> components, beam dilution, aperture mismatch, line blending and flux calibration all need investigation. For one optically thick isothermal source with equal emitting area and velocity width, the Rayleigh-Jeans limit instead gives line-energy <luminosity> approximately proportional to $\nu^3$, so the 3–2/1–0 ratio is of order 27, far below 185. That illustrates why optical-depth and emitting-area assumptions cannot be ignored; different source areas or excitation structures can change this simple comparison. Optically thick clumps can hide <mass>, and a high-temperature component may dominate the 3–2 line while colder material contributes much of the 1–0 emission.

Additional rotational lines, especially the available 2–1 transition and higher-$J$ lines, test a one-temperature excitation diagram. Less abundant isotopologues help constrain <opacity>; resolved imaging and line profiles constrain emitting volume and homologous expansion, using the remnant age. Density information permits non-LTE statistical-equilibrium/radiative-transfer modelling. Dust emission can provide a complementary <mass> constraint with its own <opacity> and dust-to-gas uncertainties. A total gas <mass> additionally needs a justified CO abundance, accounting for molecular formation, destruction and unobserved material.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For a steady spherical, nonrotating, bounded collisionless galaxy, the mean velocity vanishes, the mixed velocity moments vanish, and $\sigma_{\theta\theta}^2=\sigma_{\phi\phi}^2\equiv\sigma_t^2$. A steady radial stellar flux would satisfy $r^2\nu\overline v_r=\mathrm{const}$; regularity and no central source/sink set that constant to zero in the equilibrium model. The <spherical Jeans equation> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{d(\nu\sigma_r^2)}{dr}+\frac{2\nu}{r}(\sigma_r^2-\sigma_t^2)=-\nu\frac{d\Phi}{dr}}.
$$
Here $\nu$ is the tracer <number density>, not necessarily the gravitating <mass> density. Random motions supply the velocity stress balancing the gravitational field.

Two conventions in the printed general equation need correction before interpreting it outside this special limit. Its first standalone derivative of mean radial velocity should be a time derivative, $\nu\,\partial_t\overline v_r$, not $\nu\,\partial_r\overline v_r$: the latter has incorrect acceleration dimensions. Also a <velocity dispersion> is the centered moment $\overline{v_iv_j}-\overline v_i\,\overline v_j$. The printed raw-moment definition equals it only when the mean velocities vanish, as they do in the equilibrium used here. These issues do not change the simplified result above.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For an ensemble of nearly circular stellar orbits with randomly oriented planes, radial motion is small while tangential motion is substantial: $\boxed{\sigma_r^2\ll\sigma_t^2}$. The two tangential variances remain equal by <spherical symmetry>. There need not be bulk rotation even though individual stars move on nearly circular paths. The anisotropy parameter $\beta=1-\sigma_t^2/\sigma_r^2$ is strongly negative; its exactly circular limit is singular as $\sigma_r\to0$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Nearly radial orbits have small transverse motion: $\boxed{\sigma_t^2\ll\sigma_r^2,\quad\beta\to1}$. A bounded steady population contains both inward and outward phases, so large radial dispersion is compatible with zero mean radial velocity. Positivity of the distribution function and stability can impose further restrictions on particular radial models.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

An isotropic velocity distribution has equal variance in every direction and zero mixed moments:
$$
\boxed{\sigma_r^2=\sigma_{\theta\theta}^2=\sigma_{\phi\phi}^2,\qquad\beta=0}.
$$
This is a statement about random velocity directions at each radius, not a requirement that every star follows a circular orbit.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For spherical gravity, $d\Phi/dr=GM(\le r)/r^2$. Inserting $\beta=1-\sigma_t^2/\sigma_r^2$ into the <spherical Jeans equation> and solving for the total enclosed <mass> gives
$$
\boxed{M(\le r)=-\frac{r^2}{G\nu}\left[\frac{d(\nu\sigma_r^2)}{dr}+\frac{2\beta\nu\sigma_r^2}{r}\right]
=-\frac{r\sigma_r^2}{G}\left(\frac{d\ln\nu}{d\ln r}+\frac{d\ln\sigma_r^2}{d\ln r}+2\beta\right)}.
$$
The <mass> includes all sources of the potential, including <dark matter> or a central compact object; the stellar tracer density need not follow total <mass>. The bracket must yield nonnegative enclosed <mass> in a physically admissible model. Density, radial stress and anisotropy are independent observational/model inputs before this equation becomes a <mass> estimate.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Its exact drawn layout cannot be checked or reproduced. The surviving coordinate definitions suffice for the following standard spherical-projection derivation; no missing plotted values or drawn dimensions are supplied here.

The <spherical density projection> schematic below is original geometry constructed from these definitions, not a recovered examination figure.

\Image[2015-line-of-sight-geometry.png]
{title=Original line-of-sight geometry from the surviving coordinate definitions; not the recovered examination figure}

Let $s=0$ be closest approach on a line of sight at projected radius $R$. Euclidean spherical geometry gives $r^2=R^2+s^2$. On either half-line, $|ds/dr|=r/\sqrt{r^2-R^2}$, and the two halves contribute equally. With identical stellar <luminosity> $L$, $\mathcal L=L\nu$, so
$$
\boxed{I(R)=2L\int_R^\infty\frac{\nu(r)r\,dr}{\sqrt{r^2-R^2}}}.
$$
For an isotropic velocity distribution, the variance of any unit-direction projection equals $\sigma_r^2(r)$. Explicitly, the line-of-sight contributions have weights $s^2/r^2$ from the radial component and $R^2/r^2$ from a tangential component; equality of their variances makes the sum $\sigma_r^2$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{I(R)\sigma_{\rm los}^2(R)=2L\int_R^\infty\frac{\nu(r)\sigma_r^2(r)r\,dr}{\sqrt{r^2-R^2}}}.
$$
Without isotropy the integrand instead has the factor $1-\beta(r)R^2/r^2$. Thus the projection assumption is substantive, rather than something that can be inferred from spherical <surface brightness> alone. The missing illustration does not prevent recovering these algebraic expressions from the prose.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For the <isotropic Jeans-Abel mass inversion>, let $J(R)=I(R)\sigma_{\rm los}^2(R)$ and $p_*(r)=\nu(r)\sigma_r^2(r)$. The two preceding projections have the same <Abel integral equation> kernel, giving
$$
\boxed{\nu(r)=-\frac1{\pi L}\int_r^\infty\frac{I'(R)}{\sqrt{R^2-r^2}}\,dR},\qquad
\boxed{p_*(r)=-\frac1{\pi L}\int_r^\infty\frac{J'(R)}{\sqrt{R^2-r^2}}\,dR}.
$$
Their ratio recovers $\sigma_r^2=p_*/\nu$, and the isotropic Jeans equation gives
$$
\boxed{M(\le r)=-\frac{r^2}{G\nu(r)}\frac{dp_*}{dr}}.
$$
Equivalently put $A(r)=\int_r^\infty I'(R)/\sqrt{R^2-r^2}\,dR$ and $B(r)=\int_r^\infty J'(R)/\sqrt{R^2-r^2}\,dR$. Then $M=-r^2B'(r)/(GA(r))$: the common stellar <luminosity> cancels from the inferred <mass>. Differentiating these singular-kernel integrals is understood through suitable regularity or a nonsingular variable change, not by naively keeping an infinite lower-end boundary term.

The inversion assumes adequate radial coverage, convergence and a smooth spherical equilibrium. In practice derivatives amplify noise and the unobserved outer tail needs treatment or a model. Unknown anisotropy reintroduces a <mass>–anisotropy degeneracy; the uniqueness just derived is conditional on the explicitly isotropic velocity distribution.

==== Solution

Use the supplied <comoving distance> $D_C=5421$ Mpc. In a flat background, $D_A=D_C/(1+z)=1807$ Mpc and $D_L=(1+z)D_C=16263$ Mpc. The observed wavelength is stretched by $1+z$, so
$$
\boxed{\lambda_{\rm em}=600/3=200\ \mathrm{nm}}.
$$
Care is needed with a <luminosity> *per <frequency>*. For corresponding emitted and observed frequencies, $d\nu_{\rm em}=(1+z)d\nu_{\rm obs}$. Combining that bandwidth relation with the bolometric definition of $D_L$ gives
$$
F_{\nu,\rm obs}=\frac{(1+z)L_{\nu,\rm em}}{4\pi D_L^2},\qquad
\boxed{L_{\nu,\rm em}=\frac{4\pi D_L^2F_{\nu,\rm obs}}{1+z}=1.06\times10^{24}\ \mathrm{W\,Hz^{-1}}}.
$$
This is the spectral <luminosity> at the rest-frame <frequency> corresponding to 200 nm. A value at some different rest wavelength would require the <quasar>'s spectral shape. The factor is different for a density per unit wavelength; interchanging these two conventions would give an incorrect <redshift> factor.

{scope}

==== Solution

A transverse physical size is angle times <angular diameter distance>, not <luminosity distance> or <comoving distance>. Converting the apparent diameter into radians gives
$$
\boxed{d=5\left(\frac{\pi}{180\times3600}\right)D_A=43.8\ \mathrm{kpc}}.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The projected quasar-to-cloud separation is
$$
R_\perp=10\left(\frac{\pi}{180\times3600}\right)D_A=87.6\ \mathrm{kpc}.
$$
For the usual transverse-separation estimate, take the cloud to lie at the same line-of-sight proper distance as the <quasar>. Since the cloud and galaxy are gravitationally bound, the local source-to-cloud path does not acquire the <redshift> factor $1+z=3$ that separates that system from us. Neglect internal relative Doppler shifts and attenuation. The observer in the cloud sees
$$
\boxed{\lambda_{\rm cloud}\simeq200\ \mathrm{nm},\qquad
F_{\nu,\rm cloud}\simeq\frac{L_{\nu,\rm em}}{4\pi R_\perp^2}=1.15\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,Hz^{-1}}}.
$$
This uses the <quasar>'s isotropic intrinsic emission at the same rest <frequency>; anisotropic radiation or obscuration would modify it.

An angular separation only fixes $R_\perp$. If $s$ is a source-to-cloud displacement toward us, the true local distance is $r=\sqrt{R_\perp^2+s^2}$ and the general flux is $L_\nu/(4\pi r^2)$. The quoted transverse value is therefore an upper estimate at fixed projected separation. Equality of cosmological <redshift> and gravitational binding do not by themselves measure the small internal line-of-sight displacement; a <peculiar velocity> can also mask it.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For a <bound-cloud light echo>, the relevant observed delay is the *excess* source–cloud–observer path over the direct source–observer path, with the interval dilated by cosmological expansion. For a cloud displaced by $s$ toward us,
$$
\boxed{\Delta t_{\rm obs}=(1+z)\frac{\sqrt{R_\perp^2+s^2}-s}{c}}.
$$
The formula assumes source and cloud separation is small compared with their cosmological distance, so their distant observer directions are effectively parallel. For the transverse configuration $s=0$,
$$
\boxed{\Delta t_{\rm obs}=3\frac{87.6\ \mathrm{kpc}}c\simeq8.57\times10^5\ \mathrm{yr}}.
$$
The local quasar-to-cloud travel time is about $2.86\times10^5$ yr; the factor three is needed for the interval measured by us. A foreground cloud shortens the echo delay and a background cloud lengthens it. A unique value beyond this transverse estimate needs the otherwise unspecified depth, even if the cloud's intrinsic response is instantaneous.

==== Solution

For a small peculiar line-of-sight velocity $v_\parallel$ within the group, cosmological and Doppler factors multiply:
$$
1+z_{\rm obs}=(1+z_g)(1+z_{\rm pec}),\qquad z_{\rm pec}\simeq v_\parallel/c.

At fixed group <redshift> this gives $\delta z=(1+z_g)v_\parallel/c$. Therefore

\boxed{\sigma_v=\frac{c\sigma_z}{1+z_g}=99.9\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}}.

Using $c\sigma_z$ directly would give a detector-frame <redshift> interval without removing the cosmological stretch. This is the one-dimensional line-of-sight <velocity dispersion> requested, not the three-dimensional RMS speed. <Redshift> measurement errors or a large unbound line-of-sight extent would require additional corrections; neither is supplied here.
