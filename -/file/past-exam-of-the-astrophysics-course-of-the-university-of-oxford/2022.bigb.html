=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <specific intensity> $I_\nu$ is <energy> transported per unit time, projected area, <solid angle> and <frequency>. The path coordinate $s$ increases along the ray. The <emission coefficient> $j_\nu$ is emitted <energy> per unit time, volume, <solid angle> and <frequency>, whereas the <absorption coefficient> $\alpha_\nu$ has units of inverse length. Thus $j_\nu ds$ adds <specific intensity> and $\alpha_\nu I_\nu ds$ removes it. <Stimulated emission> can be included as negative <absorption>.

Define the <optical depth> between two points by $\tau_\nu(s_1,s_2)=\int_{s_1}^{s_2}\alpha_\nu(u)\,du$. Multiplying the <radiative transfer equation> by the <integrating factor> $\exp[\tau_\nu(s_0,s)]$ and integrating gives
$$
\boxed{I_\nu(s)=I_\nu(s_0)e^{-\tau_\nu(s_0,s)}+\int_{s_0}^{s}j_\nu(u)e^{-\tau_\nu(u,s)}\,du.}
$$
Each local contribution is attenuated only by the material between its emission point and the observer. Where $\alpha_\nu\ne0$, the <source function> is $S_\nu=j_\nu/\alpha_\nu$ and the same <formal solution of the radiative transfer equation> is $I_\nu(\tau)=I_\nu(0)e^{-\tau}+\int_0^\tau S_\nu(t)e^{-(\tau-t)}dt$. A constant <source function> gives $I_\nu=I_\nu(0)e^{-\tau}+S_\nu(1-e^{-\tau})$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the <Einstein coefficients> convention in which $B_{ij}$ multiplies spectral <radiation energy density> $u_\nu$, with $u_\nu=c^{-1}\int I_\nu\,d\Omega$. A normalized <spectral line profile> obeys $\int\varphi(\nu)d\nu=1$. The <spontaneous emission> probability per upper-state atom per unit time is $A_{21}$; the <stimulated emission> and upward <absorption> rates per atom are respectively $B_{21}\int u_\nu\varphi(\nu)d\nu$ and $B_{12}\int u_\nu\varphi(\nu)d\nu$.

For <isotropic> <spontaneous emission>, $n_2A_{21}$ transitions occur per unit volume and time, each supplying <photon energy> $h\nu_{12}$. Dividing among $4\pi$ steradians gives
$$
\boxed{j_\nu=\frac{h\nu_{12}}{4\pi}n_2A_{21}\varphi(\nu).}
$$
For a small incident beam, its contribution to $u_\nu$ is $I_\nu d\Omega/c$. <Absorption> removes and <stimulated emission> adds <photons> in the incident direction. Matching the net loss to $\alpha_\nu I_\nu d\Omega$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\alpha_\nu=\frac{h\nu_{12}}{c}(n_1B_{12}-n_2B_{21})\varphi(\nu).}
$$
Replacing $\nu_{12}$ by $\nu$ across the narrow <spectral line profile> gives the printed expressions. The negative sign is essential: an inverted population can amplify the incident <specific intensity>. <Thermal equilibrium> and the <Planck function> imply $g_1B_{12}=g_2B_{21}$ and $A_{21}/B_{21}=8\pi h\nu_{12}^3/c^3$, where $g_i$ is the <state degeneracy>. A convention using <mean intensity> instead changes both $B$ coefficients by $4\pi/c$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

With the <spontaneous emission> term suppressed, $j_\nu=0$. A uniform <absorption coefficient> over length $\ell$ gives directly
$$
\boxed{I_\nu=I_\nu^0e^{-\tau_\nu},\qquad \tau_\nu=\ell\alpha_\nu.}
$$
The normalized <specific intensity> deficit is unchanged by conversion between <wavelength> and <frequency>, since both numerator and denominator acquire the same <Jacobian determinant>. The <Boltzmann distribution> and the <Einstein coefficients> give $n_2B_{21}/(n_1B_{12})=e^{-h\nu_{12}/(k_BT_e)}\ll1$. Consequently, in the <optically thin> limit,
$$
W=\int(1-e^{-\tau_\nu})\frac{c}{\nu^2}\,d\nu
\simeq\frac{hN_1B_{12}}{\nu_{12}}\int\varphi(\nu)d\nu
=\boxed{\frac{hB_{12}N_1}{\nu_{12}}}.
$$
Here $N_1=n_1\ell$ is the <column density>, and $\nu\simeq\nu_{12}$ has been used in slowly varying factors. This linear relation between <equivalent width> and <column density> fails once the central <optical depth> becomes appreciable.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For the unresolved <Lyman-alpha line>, the lower $1s$ and combined upper $2p$ <state degeneracies> are $g_1=2$ and $g_2=6$, so the <Einstein coefficients> give
$$
B_{12}=3\frac{A_{21}c^3}{8\pi h\nu_{12}^3}=2.03\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^3\,J^{-1}\,s^{-2}}.
$$
The measured <equivalent width> $W=7.0\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm m$ then implies
$$
N_{\rm HI}=\frac{8\pi W\nu_{12}^4}{3A_{21}c^3}
=1.29\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{m^{-2}}=1.29\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{cm^{-2}}.
$$
Assume a central transverse <line of sight> through a uniform filament, so $\ell=d=0.5\ \mathrm{Mpc}=1.54\times10^{22}\ \mathrm m$. Nearly all neutral <hydrogen> is in its ground state at this <temperature>, hence
$$
\boxed{n_{\rm HI}=N_{\rm HI}/d=8.3\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}=8.3\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}.}
$$
The cylinder's length $L$ determines its total volume but does not enter a transverse <column density>. Geometry must be specified: an axial <line of sight> has $\ell=L$ and gives $n_{\rm HI}=4.2\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$ for the same measured <equivalent width>. Intermediate chords give intermediate path-dependent estimates. The numbers above treat $W$ as a rest-frame <equivalent width>; an observed value at <redshift> $z$ must first be divided by $1+z$.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

True <thermal equilibrium> requires a thermal ionizing <radiation> field as well as chemical balance; equality of <photoionization> and recombination rates alone would require a specified external <radiation> field. Under the stronger equilibrium assumption, apply the <Saha equation> to pure <hydrogen>. The electron spin factor cancels the ground-state electronic degeneracy, giving
$$
\frac{n_en_p}{n_{\rm HI}}=S(T),\qquad
S(T)=\left(\frac{2\pi m_ek_BT}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}e^{-\chi/(k_BT)}.
$$
At $T=10^4\ \mathrm K$, $k_BT=0.862\ \mathrm{eV}$ and $S=3.38\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$. Charge neutrality gives $n_e=n_p$. Importantly, the known quantity from <1/d> is the *neutral* <number density>, not the total <number density>; use <Saha neutral fraction from a measured neutral density> to obtain $n_p=\sqrt{Sn_{\rm HI}}=5.31\times10^7\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{1-x_e=\frac{n_{\rm HI}}{n_p+n_{\rm HI}}=\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{S/n_{\rm HI}}}\simeq1.57\times10^{-13}.}
$$
This extraordinarily small neutral fraction is the result of applying <thermal equilibrium> to dilute intergalactic gas; it is not a realistic inference from <photoionization equilibrium> with a nonthermal cosmic background. For the axial geometry of <1/d>, the equilibrium neutral fraction is smaller by $\sqrt{20}$.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

Adopt the stipulated neutral fraction $f_{\rm HI}=10^{-6}$, independently of the equilibrium estimate in <1/e>. For pure <hydrogen>, $n_e=(1-f_{\rm HI})n_{\rm HI}/f_{\rm HI}\simeq8.3\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$. The <thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect> measures integrated electron <pressure>:
$$
y=\frac{\sigma_T}{m_ec^2}\int n_ek_BT_e\,ds
\simeq\frac{\sigma_Tk_BT_e}{m_ec^2}\frac{N_{\rm HI}}{f_{\rm HI}}.
$$
Using the same absorbing <line of sight> as in <1/d>,
$$
\boxed{y=1.44\times10^{-11},\qquad y/(10^{-9})=0.0144.}
$$
Thus one such filament is below the stated measurement sensitivity. If the <number density> inferred transversely is instead used to view the *same cylinder* end-on, its path is twenty times longer and $y=2.9\times10^{-10}$, still below the stated sensitivity. Changing the assumed path of the original absorption measurement while keeping its <column density> fixed does not change its predicted $y$. The quoted neutral-fraction floor is a premise of this estimate, not a universal bound derivable from <thermal equilibrium>.

=== a

==== Solution

There are two different physical interpretations. The supplied logarithmic integral signals <free fall in a fixed singular isothermal sphere>, with a test particle moving through a frozen background. Write $C=\rho_0r_0^2$. By the <shell theorem>, the enclosed <mass> is $M(r)=4\pi Cr$ and the radial <equation of motion> is
$$
\ddot r=-\frac{4\pi GC}{r},\qquad
\frac{\dot r^2}{2}=4\pi GC\ln\frac{r_0}{r},\qquad \dot r<0.
$$
The second equality follows by multiplying by $\dot r$ and using the initial rest condition. The <free fall> trajectory is given implicitly by
$$
t(r)=\frac{r_0}{\sqrt{8\pi GC}}\int_{r/r_0}^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\ln(1/x)}}
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{8G\rho_0}}\operatorname{erf}\!\sqrt{\ln(r_0/r)}.
$$
Thus the intended fixed-profile answer is
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm ff,fixed}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{8G\rho_0}}.}
$$
**A collapsing material shell gives a different answer.** If the entire cloud undergoes pressureless <gravitational collapse> from rest, the edge follows the fixed enclosed <mass> $M_0=4\pi\rho_0r_0^3$, not the instantaneous fixed-profile value $4\pi Cr$. With no <shell crossing>, $\ddot r=-GM_0/r^2$, so
$$
t_{\rm ff,shell}=\int_0^{r_0}\frac{dr}{\sqrt{2GM_0(1/r-1/r_0)}}
=\frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{\frac{r_0^3}{2GM_0}}
=\boxed{\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{32G\rho_0}}}.
$$
This is a concrete counterexample to treating the fixed-profile result as the exact collapse time of the whole nebula. The initial mean <density> inside $r_0$ is $3\rho_0$, consistent with the usual <free-fall time of a uniform sphere> expressed using that mean <density>. The logarithmic integral is valid for the explicitly frozen background approximation.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Assume an isolated, bound, nonrotating Newtonian gas cloud, negligible surface <pressure>, negligible magnetic and radiation stresses, no persistent bulk flow, and a stationary or time-averaged virialized state. The <stellar virial theorem> then reduces to
$$
3\int P(\mathbf x)\,dV+\Omega=0,
$$
where $\Omega<0$ is the total <gravitational potential energy>. Define the volume-averaged <pressure> $\overline P=V^{-1}\int P\,dV$ and average gravitational <energy density> $u_g=\Omega/V$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\overline P=-u_g/3.}
$$
Equivalently, for a nonrelativistic <monatomic gas>, the thermal <internal energy> $U=(3/2)\int P\,dV$ obeys $2U+\Omega=0$. This second derivation must not count the thermal motions again as a separate bulk <kinetic energy>. If the external <pressure> is $P_s$, the correct result is $\overline P-P_s=-u_g/3$; non-negligible bulk motion adds $2T_{\rm bulk}$ to the <virial theorem>. The initial sharp-edged $r^{-2}$ profile need not survive virialization; maintaining that profile hydrostatically to its edge would generally require a surface term.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For a family of spherical profiles with a fixed dimensionless shape, write the <gravitational potential energy> as $\Omega=-\alpha GM^2/R$, where $\alpha>0$ depends on that shape. The <virial theorem> in <2/b> and the sphere volume $V=4\pi R^3/3$ give
$$
\boxed{\overline P=\frac{\alpha}{4\pi}\frac{GM^2}{R^4}\propto\frac{GM^2}{R^4}.}
$$
For a uniform <density>, $\alpha=3/5$; formally integrating $-\int_0^R GM(r)\,dM(r)/r$ for $M(r)=Mr/R$ gives $\alpha=1$. Neither the <virial theorem> nor the initial profile guarantees a specific final shape, so the proportionality requires the stated homologous-profile assumption. Surface <pressure> must be handled consistently with <2/b> before using either coefficient as an equilibrium model.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For a regular spherical star, let $\rho_c=\rho(0)$ and $P_c=P(0)$. The <stellar mass conservation equation> and <stellar hydrostatic equation> are
$$
M'(r)=4\pi r^2\rho(r),\qquad P'(r)=-\frac{GM(r)\rho(r)}{r^2}.
$$
Assuming the smoothness needed for the requested <Taylor series>, write $M=a_0+a_1r+a_2r^2+a_3r^3+\cdots$. There is no point <mass> at the centre, so $a_0=0$, and matching the <stellar mass conservation equation> gives $a_1=a_2=0$, $a_3=4\pi\rho_c/3$. Substitution in the <stellar hydrostatic equation> gives $P'(r)=-(4\pi G/3)\rho_c^2r+O(r^3)$, hence
$$
\boxed{M(r)=\frac{4\pi}{3}\rho_cr^3+O(r^5),\qquad
P(r)=P_c-\frac{2\pi G}{3}\rho_c^2r^2+O(r^4).}
$$
The remainder orders use a smooth spherically symmetric scalar <density>, for which $\rho(r)=\rho_c+O(r^2)$. Merely finite continuous <density> gives the same leading coefficients with $o(r^3)$ and $o(r^2)$ remainders. In particular $P'(0)=0$, despite the apparent $r^{-2}$ in the <stellar hydrostatic equation>.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

For a gas-pressure-supported <stellar core> of fixed <mass> and composition, neglect boundary terms for the illustrative whole-core estimate. The <virial theorem> gives $E=U+\Omega=-U$, and the <monatomic gas> gives $U=3M_ck_B\overline T/(2\mu m_u)$. Thus the effective <heat capacity> along the equilibrium sequence is
$$
\boxed{\frac{dE}{d\overline T}=-\frac{3M_ck_B}{2\mu m_u}<0.}
$$
This <negative heat capacity of a virialized gravitational system> means that net <energy> loss makes a self-gravitating core contract and heat. It is different from the positive local material <specific heat capacity>. On the <main sequence>, <hydrogen burning> supplies most of the radiated <energy>, so rapid contraction is arrested. Hydrogen depletion increases the <mean molecular weight>; slow structural readjustment usually raises the central <temperature> rather than making it fall.

After core hydrogen exhaustion, a massive <stellar core> contracts and heats more rapidly until a later nuclear fuel ignites. There is no general reversal to cooling simply upon leaving the <main sequence>. If <radiation pressure> becomes dominant, the <internal energy> becomes $U=U_{\rm gas}+U_{\rm rad}$ with $3\int P\,dV=2U_{\rm gas}+U_{\rm rad}$. The <virial theorem> therefore gives $E=-U_{\rm gas}$; the radiation-dominated limit is marginally bound and its effective <adiabatic index> approaches $4/3$.

Quasistatic contraction can still maintain <virial equilibrium>. It fails dynamically only when sufficient support is lost, for example if pair creation reduces the effective <adiabatic index> below $4/3$, or when an iron core undergoes <electron capture> and photodisintegration. A fixed-composition gas-dominated contracting core with $T\propto1/R$ is already a counterexample to any assertion that leaving the <main sequence> alone reverses the heating or destroys <virial equilibrium>.

=== a

==== Solution

Primordial <density contrasts> perturb the intrinsic photon <temperature>, the <gravitational potential>, and the <velocity> of the last-scattering material. Intrinsic photon-density fluctuations obey $\delta T/T=\delta_\gamma/4$ because $\rho_\gamma\propto T^4$; adiabatic initial conditions relate these to matter <density contrasts>. Subsequent acoustic evolution, <Doppler effect> and gravitational frequency shifts determine the observed <cosmic microwave background anisotropy>. There is no scale-independent identification of the final sky map with matter <density contrast> alone.

The <spherical harmonic> coefficients are random variables inherited from these perturbations. After subtracting the mean sky <temperature>, $\langle a_{lm}\rangle=0$ for the anisotropy modes. Gaussian primordial perturbations give approximately <Gaussian random variables>; this extra Gaussian assumption is not needed for diagonal covariance. Reality of the sky map implies $a_{l,-m}=(-1)^ma_{lm}^*$.

For a statistically isotropic sky, its two-point function depends only on $\hat n\cdot\hat m$. Rotations mix the $m$ components at fixed $l$ and do not mix different irreducible $l$ spaces. A rotation-invariant <covariance matrix> is therefore a multiple of the identity in each $l$ block:
$$
\boxed{\langle a_{lm}a_{l'm'}^*\rangle=C_l\delta_{ll'}\delta_{mm'}.}
$$
On our one sky, average the $2l+1$ angular components at fixed $l$, or equivalently suitable rotated sky pairs, rather than treating different universes as observed samples. The <CMB angular power spectrum> is estimated by
$$
\boxed{\widehat C_l=\frac{1}{2l+1}\sum_{m=-l}^l|a_{lm}|^2.}
$$
Under the Gaussian, full-sky, noiseless assumptions, there are $2l+1$ independent real degrees of freedom and $\operatorname{Var}(\widehat C_l)=2C_l^2/(2l+1)$. This <cosmic variance> remains even with perfect detectors; a finite sky mask and detector noise add further uncertainty. The estimator is unbiased, $\langle\widehat C_l\rangle=C_l$, but does not turn one realization into the exact ensemble mean.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Insert the diagonal covariance from <3/a> into the two-point function and apply the <spherical harmonic addition theorem>:
$$
C(\alpha)=\sum_l C_l\sum_{m=-l}^l\mathcal Y_{lm}(\hat n)\mathcal Y_{lm}^*(\hat m)
=\boxed{\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{l=0}^\infty(2l+1)C_lP_l(\cos\alpha)}.
$$
The <Legendre polynomials> encode the angular separation. Since $P_l(1)=1$, the zero-separation value $C(0)=\langle\Theta^2\rangle$ is the total <variance>. For measured <cosmic microwave background anisotropy>, remove the monopole and usually the kinematic dipole, so the corresponding sum starts at $l=2$; an instrument's angular response supplies an additional window function.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The ordinary <Sachs-Wolfe effect> combines intrinsic last-scattering <temperature> with the gravitational redshift of <photons> escaping the last-scattering <gravitational potential>. For adiabatic large-scale perturbations in matter domination, with the potential convention in the question, the combination is $\Theta=\phi/(3c^2)$, up to the observer monopole. It is distinct from the <integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect> caused by a time-varying potential along the later <line of sight>.

Use the <Fourier transform> convention in which $P_\delta(k)=Ak$ is the <matter power spectrum> and $\langle\delta_{\mathbf k}\delta_{\mathbf k'}^*\rangle=(2\pi)^3\delta_D(\mathbf k-\mathbf k')P_\delta(k)$. The <Poisson equation> becomes $-k^2\phi_{\mathbf k}=4\pi G\bar\rho_0\delta_{\mathbf k}$, giving
$$
P_\phi(k)=\frac{(4\pi G\bar\rho_0)^2A}{k^3},\qquad
\Delta_\phi^2=\frac{(4\pi G\bar\rho_0)^2A}{2\pi^2c^4}.
$$
With the matter-dominated critical-density approximation $\bar\rho_0=3H_0^2/(8\pi G)$,
$$
\boxed{\Delta_\phi^2=\frac{9AH_0^4}{8\pi^2c^4},}
$$
independent of $k$. Thus the <Harrison-Zeldovich spectrum> gives scale-invariant potential fluctuations and the requested $H_0^4$ dependence. Keeping a general present matter fraction would insert $\Omega_m^2$; the answer here follows the stipulated critical matter-dominated normalization. In this <Fourier transform> convention, $A$ has dimensions of length to the fourth power.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The finite angular resolution of <COBE DMR> suppresses small angular scales, so only low <spherical harmonic> multipoles can be recovered usefully. The approximate relation $\theta\sim\pi/l$ associates $l\simeq17$ with scales of order ten degrees; this is an effective sensitivity and beam-window limit, not a mathematical hard truncation.

Interpret the printed superscript uncertainty as $(1.1\pm0.1)\times10^{-5}$. Its square estimates the band-limited zero-separation <variance>, rather than the full $C(0)$:
$$
\sigma_{2:17}^2=\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{l=2}^{17}(2l+1)C_l
=\frac{0.732\times4.43}{4\pi}\Delta_\phi^2.
$$
Use the coefficient supplied in the paper for this numerical inference. With $\sigma_{2:17}=1.1\times10^{-5}$,
$$
\Delta_\phi^2=4.69\times10^{-10},\qquad H_0=2.269\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},
$$
so <3/c> gives
$$
\boxed{A=\frac{8\pi^2c^4\Delta_\phi^2}{9H_0^4}=1.25\times10^{96}\ \mathrm{m^4}=1.38\times10^6\ \mathrm{Mpc^4}.}
$$
The stated amplitude uncertainty gives approximately $18\%$ relative uncertainty in $A$, or $A\simeq(1.38\pm0.25)\times10^6\ \mathrm{Mpc^4}$, keeping $H_0$ fixed. The ideal matter-era <Sachs-Wolfe plateau> with precisely the <3/c> spectrum convention has coefficient $2\pi/9=0.698$, rather than $0.732$; the supplied numerical coefficient is about $5\%$ larger and has been treated as the paper's prescribed approximation. A real beam-convolved analysis retains its window function and additional cosmological transfer effects.

=== a

==== Solution

An ultrarelativistic <electron> has <kinetic energy> much larger than its rest <energy>: $(\gamma-1)m_ec^2\gg m_ec^2$, equivalently $\gamma\gg1$. Its <speed> obeys $v/c=\sqrt{1-\gamma^{-2}}\simeq1-1/(2\gamma^2)$ and its total <energy> is approximately $pc$. At $\gamma=10^6$, $E=\gamma m_ec^2\simeq511\ \mathrm{GeV}$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

<Diffusive shock acceleration> is viable in principle at $511\ \mathrm{GeV}$: repeated scattering off magnetic irregularities on opposite sides of a strong nonrelativistic <shock wave> can reach such <energies>. Actual viability requires a sufficiently large and long-lived accelerator with an acceleration time shorter than its escape and loss times; $\gamma$ alone does not establish those conditions.

Take positive <velocity> toward the upstream material, and let the front move at $+v_s$ in the upstream rest frame. For a strong adiabatic <shock wave> in a gas with <adiabatic index> $5/3$, the <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions> give $\rho_d/\rho_u=4$ and the shock-frame inflow <speeds> satisfy $u_1=v_s$, $u_2=v_s/4$. The signed <velocities> in the three frames are
$$
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
\text{rest frame}&v_u&v_d&v_{\rm front}\\ \hline
\text{upstream}&0&3v_s/4&v_s\\
\text{downstream}&-3v_s/4&0&v_s/4\\
\text{front}&-v_s&-v_s/4&0
\end{array}
$$
Thus the two scattering media converge at $V=u_1-u_2=3v_s/4$. For a crossing particle with incident <energy> $E$ in its *local plasma rest frame*, a <Lorentz transformation> to the approaching other plasma gives
$$
E'=\Gamma_V(E+Vp\mu)=E\left[1+\frac{V}{c}\mu+O(V^2/c^2)\right],
$$
where $\mu>0$ is the incident direction cosine measured toward the approaching medium and $pc\simeq E$. For an almost <isotropic> crossing population, flux weighting gives $\langle\mu\rangle=\int_0^1\mu^2d\mu/\int_0^1\mu d\mu=2/3$. Scattering randomizes directions without changing <energy> in that plasma frame, and the reverse crossing has the same mean gain. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\left\langle\frac{\Delta E}{E}\right\rangle_{\rm crossing}=\frac{2V}{3c},\qquad
\left\langle\frac{\Delta E}{E}\right\rangle_{\rm cycle}=\frac{4V}{3c}=\frac{v_s}{c}.}
$$
These are first-order mean gains. They are not an increase of <energy> in one fixed frame caused by an instantaneous boundary crossing. Literally choosing the <electron>'s instantaneous rest frame gives incident $E=m_ec^2$, and the other gas approaches at nearly $c$, so the expansion in small $V/c$ is invalid. This concrete frame check explains why the printed description of $V$ must instead refer to the upstream/downstream scattering frames.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The magnetic <Lorentz force> is perpendicular to <velocity>, so it does no <work> and $\gamma$ remains constant over an orbit when radiative losses are neglected. For the transverse <momentum>, $p_\perp=\gamma m_ev_\perp$ and $|\dot{\mathbf p}_\perp|=\omega_gp_\perp=eBv_\perp$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\nu_g=\frac{\omega_g}{2\pi}=\frac{eB}{2\pi\gamma m_e}.}
$$
Since $10\ \mu\mathrm G=10^{-9}\ \mathrm T$,
$$
\boxed{\nu_g=2.80\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{Hz},\qquad
\omega_g=1.76\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},\qquad T_g=3.57\times10^4\ \mathrm s\simeq9.9\ \mathrm h.}
$$
This is the <gyromotion> <frequency>, not the much higher characteristic <synchrotron radiation> <frequency>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

<Relativistic beaming> concentrates the emission into a forward cone about the instantaneous <velocity>. The <aberration of light> maps a rest-frame ray at $\theta'=\pi/2$ to $\cos\theta=\beta$, so the characteristic cone half-angle is $\theta\simeq\gamma^{-1}$. Thus its conventional full width is $2/\gamma=2\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{rad}$.

**The isotropic-emission assumption has no beam nulls.** For example, its received bolometric angular <power> is proportional to $D^4$, where the <Doppler factor> is $D=[\gamma(1-\beta\cos\theta)]^{-1}>0$. In particular it is nonzero at $\theta=1/\gamma$, disproving a literal null there. A true dipole pattern for an accelerated <electron> is not isotropic in its rest frame and needs a specified polarization and viewing plane. The width just quoted is a characteristic beaming angle, not a null-to-null width under the stated premise.

For a circular orbit seen in its orbital plane, let the <velocity> direction sweep past the observer through $-1/\gamma<\psi<1/\gamma$. The emission interval is $\Delta t_{\rm em}\simeq2/(\gamma\omega_g)=1.14\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm s$. The observed interval must also include light-travel compression:
$$
\Delta t_{\rm obs}=\frac{1}{\omega_g}\int_{-1/\gamma}^{1/\gamma}(1-\beta\cos\psi)d\psi
=\frac{2}{\omega_g}\left[\frac{1}{\gamma}-\beta\sin\frac{1}{\gamma}\right]
\simeq\boxed{\frac{4}{3\omega_g\gamma^3}=7.6\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm s}.
$$
Its ratio to the <gyromotion> period is $\Delta t_{\rm obs}/T_g\simeq2/(3\pi\gamma^3)=2.1\times10^{-19}$. Since the boundary of a smooth beam is conventional, only the scale $\omega_g^{-1}\gamma^{-3}$ is invariant under reasonable pulse-width definitions. For helical motion the viewing geometry and pitch angle are additional inputs; the numerical estimate assumes transverse motion and a sightline intercepted by the cone.<Essential Radio Astronomy by James J. Condon and Scott M. Ransom>[Chapter 5, section 5.1.3: relativistic beaming and the difference between emission duration and pulse arrival duration]{ref}

{scope}

==== Solution

One explanation is an upper <electron> <energy> imposed by the accelerator: finite age, finite confinement size, escape, or a balance of acceleration and losses limits $\gamma$ to $\gamma_{\max}$. Since the <synchrotron critical frequency> scales as $\nu_c\propto B\gamma^2\sin\alpha$, the absence of higher-energy <electrons> produces a corresponding high-<frequency> cutoff, broadened by the single-particle spectrum.

The other is <synchrotron cooling>, with <inverse Compton scattering> often contributing: $\dot\gamma=-b\gamma^2$ gives $t_{\rm cool}=1/(b\gamma)$. High-energy <electrons> disappear fastest, so a population of age $t$ develops a cooling scale $\gamma_b\sim1/(bt)$ and $\nu_b\propto B/(b^2t^2)$. A burst population can develop a strong cutoff. With continuous power-law injection, the simplest steady cooling model instead steepens the <electron> <energy> index by one and the <synchrotron radiation> spectral index by $1/2$, giving a break rather than necessarily an exponential cutoff. The detailed spectral shape therefore distinguishes finite acceleration from aging and injection history.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $n(\mathbf x)=\int f\,d^3v$ and $\overline A=n^{-1}\int Af\,d^3v$. Assume the <one-particle distribution function> and its weighted tails decay enough that all required <velocity> integrals converge and boundary terms at infinite <velocity> vanish. Multiply the steady <collisionless Boltzmann equation> by $v_j$ and integrate over <velocity>. In <Cartesian coordinates>, spatial differentiation commutes with this integration, while <integration by parts> gives
$$
\int v_j\frac{\partial f}{\partial v_i}d^3v=-\delta_{ij}n.
$$
Consequently the three force-balance equations are
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^3\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\left(n\overline{v_iv_j}\right)=-n\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x_j},\qquad j=1,2,3.}
$$
Explicitly the $x$ component is $\partial_x(n\overline{v_x^2})+\partial_y(n\overline{v_xv_y})+\partial_z(n\overline{v_xv_z})=-n\Phi_x$, with cyclic counterparts for $y,z$. For equal stellar <masses>, multiplying by the common <mass> replaces $n$ by the <mass density> $\rho$; for multiple stellar <masses>, use a mass-weighted <one-particle distribution function> or derive a separate equation for each tracer population.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Define mean streaming <velocity> $u_i=\overline{v_i}$ and the <velocity dispersion> tensor $\sigma_{ij}^2=\overline{(v_i-u_i)(v_j-u_j)}=\overline{v_iv_j}-u_iu_j$. The zeroth <velocity> moment of the steady <collisionless Boltzmann equation> gives the <continuity equation> $\sum_i\partial_i(nu_i)=0$. Therefore
$$
\sum_i\partial_i(nu_iu_j)=n\sum_i u_i\partial_i u_j.
$$
Substituting in <5/a> yields the standard steady <Jeans equations>:
$$
\boxed{\sum_i u_i\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}=-\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x_j}-\frac1n\sum_i\frac{\partial(n\sigma_{ij}^2)}{\partial x_i},\qquad j=1,2,3.}
$$
The unsteady version adds $\partial_tu_j$ on the left. Defining the stellar <pressure tensor> $P_{ij}=\rho\sigma_{ij}^2$ makes the analogy with fluid momentum balance explicit. The off-diagonal terms are correlations between random <velocity> components; they need not vanish or equal the diagonal terms.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Jeans equations> express local momentum conservation for a <collisionless stellar system>, just as the <Euler equations> do for a gas. The mean streaming <velocity> plays the role of fluid flow, the gravitational force is a body force, and random stellar motions supply a <pressure tensor> through $\rho\sigma_{ij}^2$. Taking moments smooths over individual stellar orbits and produces fields of <density>, mean <velocity> and random-motion stress; this is the sense of “stellar hydrodynamics”.

Ordinary gas <pressure> is usually nearly isotropic because collisions relax its local <velocity> distribution. A <collisionless stellar system> can retain an anisotropic <velocity ellipsoid>, so its <pressure tensor> is not generally a scalar times the identity. There are more unknown second moments than momentum equations, and their evolution introduces higher moments. The <Jeans equations> therefore require further dynamical assumptions or a specified <one-particle distribution function> for closure; they do not by themselves establish local <thermal equilibrium> or a gas <equation of state>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Use fixed <masses> in an isolated Newtonian $N$-body system, with origin at its <centre of mass>. Define the $xx$ moment $I_{xx}=\sum_i m_ix_i^2$ and $K_{xx}=\tfrac12\sum_i m_iv_{x,i}^2$. Twice differentiating gives
$$
\frac12\ddot I_{xx}=\sum_i m_iv_{x,i}^2+\sum_i m_ix_i\dot v_{x,i}=2K_{xx}+W_{xx}.
$$
Insert the gravitational <equation of motion> and pair each ordered term $(i,k)$ with $(k,i)$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
W_{xx}&=-G\sum_i\sum_{k\ne i}\frac{m_im_kx_i(x_i-x_k)}{r_{ik}^3}\\
&=-\frac G2\sum_i\sum_{k\ne i}\frac{m_im_k(x_i-x_k)^2}{r_{ik}^3}
=-G\sum_{i<k}\frac{m_im_k(x_i-x_k)^2}{r_{ik}^3}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus one component of the <tensor virial theorem> is
$$
\boxed{\frac12\ddot I_{xx}=2K_{xx}-G\sum_{i<k}\frac{m_im_k(x_i-x_k)^2}{r_{ik}^3}.}
$$
A stationary or time-averaged bounded system has $\langle\ddot I_{xx}\rangle=0$, yielding $2\langle K_{xx}\rangle+\langle W_{xx}\rangle=0$. If ordered and random motions are separated, $2K_{xx}=2T_{xx}+\Pi_{xx}$, where $T_{xx}=\tfrac12\int\rho u_x^2dV$ and $\Pi_{xx}=\int\rho\sigma_{xx}^2dV$. Summing the three diagonal equations recovers the scalar <virial theorem>, with the pair <gravitational potential energy> counted once.

=== a

==== Solution

Treat the long-wavelength nearly uniform spherical perturbation as a separate locally homogeneous expanding region, neglecting pressure gradients and tidal corrections. Its local <Friedmann equation> is
$$
\boxed{H_s^2+\frac{K}{a_s^2}=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho_s.}
$$
The curvature term represents the <spatial curvature of an FLRW universe> in the local patch; for the convention $a_s$ dimensionless, $K=kc^2$ has units of inverse time squared. Positive $K$ corresponds to a closed, more tightly bound patch. Here and below the printed $\rho$ is interpreted as <mass density> or <energy density> divided by $c^2$; literal SI <energy density> requires $G\rho/c^2$ on the right.

The region generally has its own <scale factor> and <Hubble parameter>. The two printed equations with an identical $H(t)$ can be compared as a leading-order *equal-expansion* construction: on a slicing where $H_s=H$, use $a_s=a$ in the already first-order curvature term to obtain $K/a^2=(8\pi G/3)\delta\rho$. This <equal-expansion curvature overdensity> construction does not make them both exact evolution equations with a permanently identical expansion history at common proper time. For example, a growing dust <density contrast> obeys the <continuity equation> $H_s-H=-\dot\delta/3$, which is nonzero when $\delta\propto a$. This explicit example prevents an inconsistent identification of the two local histories.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Within the equal-expansion construction of <6/a>, subtract the flat-background <Friedmann equation> to obtain
$$
\delta\rho=\frac{3K}{8\pi Ga^2},\qquad
\delta=\frac{3K}{8\pi Ga^2\rho_b}.
$$
The background <density> scales as $\rho_b\propto a^{-4}$ during <radiation domination> and $\rho_b\propto a^{-3}$ during <matter domination>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\delta\propto a^2\quad(a\ll a_{\rm eq}),\qquad
\delta\propto a\quad(a\gg a_{\rm eq}).}
$$
These are the regular adiabatic growing-mode scalings on scales outside the <Hubble radius>, with a specified time slicing. In a consistent comoving description the numerical relation between curvature and <density contrast> differs from the naive subtraction, but the powers agree. The result does not imply that every gauge's <density contrast>, every decaying mode, or subhorizon dark matter during <radiation domination> has these powers.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For $r_s\gg d_H$, a regular adiabatic perturbation follows the large-scale <density contrast> growth in <6/b>; this does not require causal communication across the perturbation. Since $d_H=c/H$, the <Friedmann equation> gives $d_H\propto a^2$ during <radiation domination> and $d_H\propto a^{3/2}$ during <matter domination>. A fixed comoving perturbation has physical size $r_s=aR$, so its ratio to $d_H$ decreases as $a^{-1}$ or $a^{-1/2}$ until <cosmological horizon entry>.

**The numerical “exactly at equality” assertion is inconsistent with the supplied parameters and $d_H=c/H$.** Normalize $a_0=1$ and use the supplied $\Omega_\gamma$ as the whole radiation component. Then
$$
a_{\rm eq}=\frac{\Omega_\gamma}{\Omega_m}=\frac1{3000},\qquad
H^2=H_0^2\left(\Omega_\gamma a^{-4}+\Omega_ma^{-3}\right).
$$
Both terms must be retained at <matter-radiation equality>. The present comoving size corresponding to its <Hubble radius> is
$$
\boxed{R_{H,\rm eq}=\frac{c}{a_{\rm eq}H_{\rm eq}}
=\frac{c}{H_0}\frac{\sqrt{\Omega_\gamma}}{\sqrt2\,\Omega_m}=106.4\ \mathrm{Mpc}.}
$$
Its physical size then is $0.0355\ \mathrm{Mpc}$. A mode with $R=79\ \mathrm{Mpc}$ has physical size only $0.0263\ \mathrm{Mpc}$ at equality, already inside the <Hubble radius>. More explicitly, putting $q=RH_0/c$ in $aR=c/H(a)$ gives
$$
a_{\rm ent}=\frac{q^2\Omega_m+\sqrt{q^4\Omega_m^2+4q^2\Omega_\gamma}}2
=2.27\times10^{-4}=0.681a_{\rm eq}.
$$
This is a direct numerical counterexample using the stated data. Neglecting one component exactly at equality instead gives $150.5\ \mathrm{Mpc}$, which also does not produce $79\ \mathrm{Mpc}$. Late-time dark energy is negligible at these early times.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For conserved nonrelativistic <dark matter> and <radiation>, $\rho_{\rm DM}\propto a^{-3}$ and $\rho_\gamma\propto a^{-4}$, so $y=\rho_{\rm DM}/\rho_\gamma=a/a_{\rm eq}$ obeys $\dot y=yH$. Denote differentiation with respect to $y$ by a prime. Then
$$
\dot\delta=yH\delta',\qquad
\ddot\delta=y^2H^2\delta''+y(\dot H+H^2)\delta'.
$$
The supplied <Friedmann equations> imply
$$
\frac{\dot H}{H^2}=-\frac{4+3y}{2(1+y)},\qquad
\frac{4\pi G\rho_{\rm DM}}{H^2}=\frac{3y}{2(1+y)}.
$$
Substitution in the linear growth equation and division by $y^2H^2$ give the <Mészáros equation>
$$
\boxed{\delta''+\frac{2+3y}{2y(1+y)}\delta'-\frac{3}{2y(1+y)}\delta=0.}
$$
For $D_1=1+3y/2$, $D_1'=3/2$ and $D_1''=0$, making the two remaining terms cancel exactly. For <reduction of order>, put $\delta=fD_1$ and $P=(2+3y)/[2y(1+y)]=1/y+1/[2(1+y)]$. The equation reduces to $f''+(2D_1'/D_1+P)f'=0$, whence
$$
f'=\frac{C}{y\sqrt{1+y}(1+3y/2)^2}.
$$
Using the supplied antiderivative and reversing an irrelevant overall sign gives the independent solution
$$
\boxed{D_2(y)=\left(1+\frac32y\right)\ln\frac{\sqrt{1+y}+1}{\sqrt{1+y}-1}-3\sqrt{1+y},\qquad
\delta=A_1D_1+A_2D_2.}
$$
Its <Wronskian> is $D_1D_2'-D_1'D_2=-1/[y\sqrt{1+y}]\ne0$. The asymptotic expansions are
$$
\begin{array}{c|cc}
&D_1&D_2\\ \hline
y\ll1&1+3y/2&\ln(4/y)-3+O(y\ln(1/y))\\
y\gg1&3y/2+1&\frac4{15}y^{-3/2}+O(y^{-5/2})
\end{array}
$$
Thus subhorizon <dark matter> during <radiation domination> has constant and logarithmic modes, rather than the superhorizon $a^2$ growth of <6/b>. A combination with negative $A_2$ supplies positive logarithmic growth. During <matter domination>, the growing mode is proportional to $a$, agreeing with <6/b>, while the second mode decays as $a^{-3/2}$. These results neglect subhorizon radiation clustering; the <Mészáros equation> is the approximation stated in the question, not a full photon–matter perturbation system through horizon entry.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

For a $1\ \mathrm{Mpc}$ mode, evolve the regular superhorizon <density contrast> as $\delta\propto a^2$ until <cosmological horizon entry>. In the <radiation domination> approximation its entry scale is $a_{\rm ent}\simeq(H_0R/c)\sqrt{\Omega_\gamma}=2.2\times10^{-6}$, well before <matter-radiation equality>. At that point use the <Mészáros equation> basis of <6/d>, fixing both constants by continuity of $\delta$ and $\dot\delta$, equivalently $\delta$ and $d\delta/d\ln a$.

Explicitly, for entry data $\delta_e$ and $\delta'_e=2\delta_e/y_e$, solve
$$
\begin{pmatrix}D_1(y_e)&D_2(y_e)\\D_1'(y_e)&D_2'(y_e)\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}A_1\\A_2\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}\delta_e\\2\delta_e/y_e\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The nonzero <Wronskian> guarantees a unique match. This produces slow logarithmic growth before equality, then $\delta\propto a$ with a decaying transient after equality. For example, at $y_e\ll1$, $A_2\simeq-2\delta_e$ and $A_1\simeq\delta_e+2\delta_e[\ln(4/y_e)-3]$. An abrupt match estimates the growth history; a precise amplitude requires a common gauge and the coupled relativistic perturbation equations through entry. Linear evolution also ceases to apply once $|\delta|$ approaches unity, and late <dark energy> further suppresses growth.
