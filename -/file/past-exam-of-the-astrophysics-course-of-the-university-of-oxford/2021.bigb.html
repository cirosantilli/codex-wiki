== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A hydrogen <recombination line> is a discrete <spectral line> produced when <radiative recombination> captures an <electron> into an excited bound level and a subsequent <radiative cascade> emits <photons>. The free-to-bound capture itself produces a continuum; the later bound-to-bound transitions produce the <recombination lines>. For example, <H-alpha> is the $n=3\to2$ transition in the <Balmer series>.

For the neutral hydrogen absorbing an ionizing stellar continuum, the principal bound-free transition is <photoionization> from the $1s$ <ground state> into the continuum. Most neutral hydrogen is in its <ground state>, and the <photoionization> <cross section> is largest near its $13.6\ \mathrm{eV}$ threshold, falling approximately as $\nu^{-3}$ above it. Dominance of the *population-weighted opacity* is intended here; this does not mean that every excited-state microscopic <cross section> is smaller at every <frequency>.

In an ionization-bounded, optically thick nebula, direct <radiative recombination> into $1s$ emits an ionizing <photon> that promptly photoionizes another hydrogen atom. One ion disappears and another is created: there is no net removal of an ion from the global budget. <Case B recombination> therefore uses $\alpha_B=\sum_{n\geq2}\alpha_n$, with local recycling of ground-state recombination <photons>. This is the on-the-spot approximation, rather than an unconditional property of <radiative recombination>: if those <photons> escape, direct capture does produce net neutralization and a Case A treatment is appropriate.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $Q=\dot N_\gamma$ for the <hydrogen-ionizing photon production rate>, counting only <photons> above the hydrogen <ionization energy>, not all stellar <photons>. In steady <photoionization equilibrium>, the external supply balances the volume integral of net <Case B recombination>:
$$
Q=\int_{r_{\rm in}}^{r_S}\alpha_rn_en_p\,4\pi r^2dr.
$$
For uniform, almost completely ionized pure hydrogen, $n_e=n_p=n_H$. Hence
$$
Q=\frac{4\pi}{3}\alpha_rn_H^2(r_S^3-r_{\rm in}^3),\qquad
\boxed{r_S=\left(r_{\rm in}^3+\frac{3Q}{4\pi\alpha_rn_H^2}\right)^{1/3}
\simeq\left(\frac{3Q}{4\pi\alpha_rn_H^2}\right)^{1/3}.}
$$
The final approximation requires $r_{\rm in}\ll r_S$. The <Strömgren sphere> also assumes spherical symmetry, a sharp <ionization front>, a steady source older than the <recombination time>, uniform <number density> and <temperature> so that $\alpha_r$ is constant, negligible dust absorption of ionizing <photons>, negligible leakage, and no important helium correction or <collisional ionization>. Clumping would replace $n_H^2$ by a volume average of $n_en_p$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

An additional atomic input is needed: the total <Case B recombination> coefficient does not specify the number of <H-alpha> <photons> per net <radiative recombination>. Let $y_\alpha=\alpha_{\rm H\alpha}^{\rm eff}/\alpha_r$, where $\alpha_{\rm H\alpha}^{\rm eff}$ is the <effective recombination coefficient>. At the approximately $10^4\ \mathrm K$ <temperature> implicit in the supplied $\alpha_r$, take $y_\alpha\simeq0.45$. The coefficient 2.2 used later in the paper corresponds to $y_\alpha=1/2.2\simeq0.455$, which we use consistently here.

For an unextinguished, fully filled <Strömgren sphere> with negligible central cavity, the <H-alpha> <luminosity> and <observed radiative flux> satisfy
$$
4\pi D^2F_{\rm H\alpha}=y_\alpha\frac{hc}{\lambda_{\rm H\alpha}}\alpha_rn_e^2\frac{4\pi r_S^3}{3},\qquad
r_S=\frac{\theta D}{2}.
$$
Substitution eliminates $r_S$ and gives
$$
\boxed{D=\frac{24F_{\rm H\alpha}\lambda_{\rm H\alpha}}
{y_\alpha hc\alpha_rn_e^2\theta^3}.}
$$
Convert the angular diameter to <radians>: $\theta=56\times10^{-3}/206265=2.7150\times10^{-7}$. Also $hc/\lambda_{\rm H\alpha}=3.0268\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm J$. Thus
$$
\boxed{D=1.5422\times10^{19}\ \mathrm m\simeq500\ \mathrm{pc},\qquad
r_S\simeq2.09\times10^{12}\ \mathrm m\simeq14.0\ \mathrm{au}.}
$$
The implied <hydrogen-ionizing photon production rate> is $Q\simeq1.00\times10^{39}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Using $y_\alpha=0.45$ instead gives $D\simeq505\ \mathrm{pc}$. These values use the printed milliarcseconds and integrated line <observed radiative flux>. Assuming one <H-alpha> <photon> per recombination would give a different, unjustified distance. An appreciable cavity or a nonunit filling factor must also enter the emitting volume.

The <Brackett series> consists of transitions ending at $n=4$, at infrared <wavelengths>. They suffer much less <interstellar extinction> than <H-alpha>, so their corrected <observed radiative fluxes> can give a more reliable version of the same inference. At fixed measured angular size and <number density>, the formula has $D\propto F$: uncorrected <interstellar extinction> therefore makes this particular distance estimate too small. Infrared lines are weaker and need their own <effective recombination coefficients>; their advantage is reduced sensitivity to dust.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $q_{\rm in}$ be the <photoionization rate> per neutral hydrogen atom just outside the empty cavity. With an isotropic central source and no attenuation inside that cavity,
$$
q_{\rm in}=\int_0^\infty\frac{\sigma_\nu L_\nu^*}{4\pi r_{\rm in}^2h\nu}\,d\nu
=\frac{\langle\sigma\rangle Q}{4\pi r_{\rm in}^2}.
$$
The <cross section> is zero below threshold; this permits the lower integration limit zero. Pure hydrogen with <hydrogen ionization fraction> $\zeta$ has $n_{\rm HI}=n_H(1-\zeta)$ and $n_e=n_p=n_H\zeta$. Local <photoionization equilibrium> is therefore
$$
n_H(1-\zeta_{\rm in})q_{\rm in}=\alpha_rn_H^2\zeta_{\rm in}^2.
$$
Define the dimensionless quantity $\Theta_{\rm phys}=\alpha_rn_H/q_{\rm in}$. Solving the resulting <quadratic equation>, $\Theta_{\rm phys}\zeta_{\rm in}^2+\zeta_{\rm in}-1=0$, gives the physical root
$$
\boxed{\zeta_{\rm in}=\frac{\sqrt{1+4\Theta_{\rm phys}}-1}{2\Theta_{\rm phys}}
=\frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1+4\Theta_{\rm phys}}}.}
$$
The rationalized form avoids numerical cancellation for small $\Theta_{\rm phys}$. Strong illumination gives $\zeta_{\rm in}\simeq1-\Theta_{\rm phys}$; weak illumination gives $\zeta_{\rm in}\simeq\Theta_{\rm phys}^{-1/2}$.

Eliminating $Q$ with the <H-alpha> <luminosity>, $Q=2.2(4\pi D^2F_{\rm H\alpha})\lambda_{\rm H\alpha}/(hc)$, yields
$$
\boxed{\Theta_{\rm phys}=\frac{n_H\alpha_rhc}{2.2\langle\sigma\rangle F_{\rm H\alpha}\lambda_{\rm H\alpha}}
\left(\frac{r_{\rm in}}D\right)^2.}
$$
**The printed definition of $\Theta$ is inconsistent with the defined radii.** The source calls $r_*$ a physical stellar radius, but its factor $(2\pi r_*D)^2$ gives $\Theta$ units of length to the fourth power, rather than a dimensionless value. It also omits the physically necessary cavity radius $r_{\rm in}$. For a concrete counterexample, double $r_{\rm in}$ while holding the source, $D$, and $n_H$ fixed: the incident <photoionization rate> falls by four, so $\Theta_{\rm phys}$ rises by four; the printed expression does not change. The root has the requested form with the corrected $\Theta_{\rm phys}$ above. There is no supplied relation allowing $r_{\rm in}$ to be replaced by $r_*$.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Retain the consistent $\Theta_{\rm phys}$ of <1/d>. Across a sufficiently thin layer, the neutral hydrogen <column density> is $N_{\rm HI}=n_H(1-\zeta_{\rm in})\Delta r+O(\Delta r^2)$. The direct stellar continuum acquires <optical depth> $\tau_\nu=N_{\rm HI}\sigma_\nu$. Define the photon-weighted second moment
$$
\langle\sigma^2\rangle=\frac1Q\int_0^\infty\sigma_\nu^2\frac{L_\nu^*}{h\nu}\,d\nu.
$$
Expanding the attenuating exponential in the <photoionization rate> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{q(r_{\rm in}+\Delta r)}{q_{\rm in}}
&=\left(\frac{r_{\rm in}}{r_{\rm in}+\Delta r}\right)^2
\frac{\int\sigma_\nu L_\nu^*e^{-N_{\rm HI}\sigma_\nu}/(h\nu)\,d\nu}{Q\langle\sigma\rangle}\\
&=1-\frac{2\Delta r}{r_{\rm in}}-n_H(1-\zeta_{\rm in})\Delta r
\frac{\langle\sigma^2\rangle}{\langle\sigma\rangle}+O(\Delta r^2).
\end{aligned}
$$
The second <cross section> moment appears because the radiation both undergoes absorption and is weighted by the <photoionization> <cross section> in computing the rate.

In a local plane-parallel approximation that suppresses geometrical dilution, set
$$
\Gamma=1-n_H(1-\zeta_{\rm in})\Delta r\frac{\langle\sigma^2\rangle}{\langle\sigma\rangle}.
$$
Then local <photoionization equilibrium> becomes $\Theta_{\rm phys}\zeta^2=\Gamma(1-\zeta)$, so
$$
\boxed{\zeta=\frac{-\Gamma+\sqrt{\Gamma^2+4\Theta_{\rm phys}\Gamma}}{2\Theta_{\rm phys}}.}
$$
This gives the requested algebraic dependence, with the corrected definition of $\Theta$. Besides $\Delta r\ll r_{\rm in}$, linear attenuation requires $N_{\rm HI}\sigma_\nu\ll1$ over the <frequencies> contributing appreciably to the rate. In particular $\Gamma$ must remain positive.

The source's $\Gamma$ neglects $-2\Delta r/r_{\rm in}$, which is also first order in layer thickness. A consistent spherical first-order answer uses $\Gamma_{\rm sph}=\Gamma-2\Delta r/r_{\rm in}$ in the same root. Neglecting dilution relative to absorption additionally requires $n_H(1-\zeta_{\rm in})\langle\sigma^2\rangle/\langle\sigma\rangle\gg2/r_{\rm in}$. As a concrete limiting test, with negligible absorption the source's $\Gamma=1$ predicts no change, while inverse-square dilution still decreases $q$ and thus $\zeta$.

=== a

==== Solution

Take the initially expanding branch $\dot r_i=H_ir_i$; the printed squared <velocity> alone also permits an initially contracting branch, which would not reach a future maximum radius. Multiply the <equation of motion> by $\dot r$ and integrate. The conserved outer-shell <specific orbital energy> is
$$
\epsilon=\frac12\dot r^2-\frac{GM}{r}.
$$
The initial <mass> follows from the background <critical density>:
$$
M=\frac{4\pi}{3}r_i^3\rho_{m,i}(1+\delta_i)
=\frac{H_i^2r_i^3}{2G}(1+\delta_i).
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\epsilon=-\frac12H_i^2r_i^2\delta_i<0.}
$$
If $E$ means the <energy> of a test shell of <mass> $m$, $E=m\epsilon$. If it means the total <energy> of the entire uniform, homologously moving perturbation, direct integration instead gives $K=3M\dot r^2/10$, $U=-3GM^2/(5r)$ and
$$
\boxed{E_{\rm sphere}=\frac35M\epsilon=-\frac3{10}MH_i^2r_i^2\delta_i.}
$$
The common convention of writing the first integral as $E$ is therefore an <energy> *per unit shell mass*, not the total <energy> of the sphere.

At <spherical-collapse turnaround>, $\dot r=0$, so $\epsilon=-GM/r_m$. Hence
$$
\boxed{r_m=\frac{GM}{|\epsilon|}=r_i\frac{1+\delta_i}{\delta_i}.}
$$
Any positive initial <density contrast> binds this pressureless perturbation in the stated <Einstein-de Sitter universe>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For $r=A(1-\cos\theta)$ and $t+T=B(\theta-\sin\theta)$, differentiation gives
$$
\dot r=\frac AB\frac{\sin\theta}{1-\cos\theta},\qquad
\ddot r=-\frac A{B^2(1-\cos\theta)^2}.
$$
Comparison with $-GM/[A^2(1-\cos\theta)^2]$ proves $A^3=GMB^2$. Substitution into the conserved <specific orbital energy> gives $\epsilon=-GM/(2A)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{A=\frac{r_i(1+\delta_i)}{2\delta_i},\qquad
B=\frac{1+\delta_i}{2H_i\delta_i^{3/2}}.}
$$
These constants exactly reproduce both the initial radius and expanding <velocity>. The initial parameter obeys
$$
\cos\theta_i=\frac{1-\delta_i}{1+\delta_i},\qquad
\theta_i=2\arctan\sqrt{\delta_i}.
$$
In an <Einstein-de Sitter universe> whose background big bang is $t=0$, $t_i=2/(3H_i)$. Thus the exact required offset is
$$
\boxed{T=B(\theta_i-\sin\theta_i)-t_i.}
$$
Expanding $\theta_i-\sin\theta_i=\frac43\delta_i^{3/2}-\frac85\delta_i^{5/2}+O(\delta_i^{7/2})$ gives
$$
T=-\frac15t_i\delta_i+O(t_i\delta_i^2).
$$
**One cannot set $T=0$ exactly at finite $\delta_i$ while retaining the stated initial Hubble velocity and background time origin.** For example $\delta_i=0.01$, $t_i=1$ gives $T=-0.00199148$. Shifting to $t'=t+T$ removes the offset from the local orbit, but the background then scales as $a\propto(t'-T)^{2/3}$, not $t'^{2/3}$. Setting $T\simeq0$ is justified in the early-initial-time limit for subsequent evolution; the following standard growing-mode formulae use that approximation.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

First retain the exact initial normalization. The <scale factor> obeys $a/a_i=(t/t_i)^{2/3}$, so the <cosmological redshift> is
$$
1+z=(1+z_i)\left[\frac{t_i}{B(\theta-\sin\theta)-T}\right]^{2/3}.
$$
Also $\rho=3M/(4\pi r^3)$ and $\rho_m=1/(6\pi Gt^2)$. Using $A^3=GMB^2$ gives
$$
1+\delta=\frac92\frac{[\theta-\sin\theta-T/B]^2}{(1-\cos\theta)^3}.
$$
These equations exactly accommodate the finite-time initial conditions in <2/b>.

Now neglect $T$ and retain leading order in $\delta_i\ll1$. Since $t_i/B=4\delta_i^{3/2}/[3(1+\delta_i)]$, the <cosmological redshift> relation reduces to
$$
1+z\simeq\left(\frac43\right)^{2/3}\frac{\delta_i(1+z_i)}{(\theta-\sin\theta)^{2/3}}.
$$
Define $\delta_0=\frac35\delta_i(1+z_i)$. Then
$$
\boxed{1+z\simeq\frac53\left(\frac43\right)^{2/3}
\frac{\delta_0}{(\theta-\sin\theta)^{2/3}},\qquad
\delta\simeq\frac92\frac{(\theta-\sin\theta)^2}{(1-\cos\theta)^3}-1.}
$$
The second equality is exact for the ideal common-bang growing-mode <spherical-collapse model>; it is approximate for the paper's finite-time initial Hubble-velocity data.

The factor $3/5$ has a physical origin. Linear mass conservation gives $\dot\delta_i=0$ when the initial local expansion equals the background <Hubble flow>. The general linear dust solution is $\delta_L=C_g(t/t_i)^{2/3}+C_d(t/t_i)^{-1}$. Imposing $\delta_L(t_i)=\delta_i$ and $\dot\delta_L(t_i)=0$ yields
$$
\delta_L(t)=\frac35\delta_i\left(\frac t{t_i}\right)^{2/3}
+\frac25\delta_i\left(\frac t{t_i}\right)^{-1}.
$$
Thus $\delta_0$ is the present extrapolation of the *growing component*, after neglecting the decaying transient. It is not the exact present value of the full linear solution for finite $t_i$.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

In the growing-mode <spherical-collapse model>, use $\theta-\sin\theta=\theta^3/6-\theta^5/120+\cdots$ and $1-\cos\theta=\theta^2/2-\theta^4/24+\cdots$. Their ratio in <2/c> gives $\delta=3\theta^2/20+O(\theta^4)$. Meanwhile $t\propto\theta-\sin\theta$, and the linear growing <density contrast> scales as $t^{2/3}$. Matching at small $\theta$ therefore fixes
$$
\boxed{\delta_L=\frac35\left[\frac34(\theta-\sin\theta)\right]^{2/3}.}
$$
This is a linearly extrapolated growing-mode amplitude at any epoch, including epochs where the actual <density contrast> is nonlinear. It carries the initial-condition qualification in <2/c>.

Numerical comparison is
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrr}
\theta&\delta&\delta_L&1-\delta_L/\delta\\\hline
1/4&0.00942684&0.00935549&0.00757\\
\pi/2&0.466138&0.340810&0.2689\\
2\pi/3&1.01186&0.568083&0.4386\\
\pi&4.55165&1.06241&0.7666
\end{array}
$$
The last column measures the fractional underestimate of the actual <density contrast>. Linear theory is accurate at $\delta\ll1$, but substantially underpredicts it by the time $\delta$ approaches unity. At $\theta=\pi$, the shell reaches <spherical-collapse turnaround>; $1+\delta=9\pi^2/16\simeq5.55$.

At $\theta=2\pi$, $r=0$ and the ideal pressureless <density> diverges at finite time $t_c=2\pi B$. Yet the extrapolated linear value remains finite:
$$
\boxed{\delta_c=\frac35\left(\frac{3\pi}{2}\right)^{2/3}=1.68647.}
$$
This is the <linear spherical-collapse threshold>, not the true <density contrast> at collapse. Actual nonspherical collisionless systems undergo <shell crossing> and <virialization> rather than becoming uniform point singularities. The ideal <virial theorem> and <conservation of energy> estimate a final radius $r_m/2$ if the structural form is preserved.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Work in the source rest frame and neglect the source's small <cosmological redshift>. Over an emission interval $\Delta t$, a ballistic <relativistic jet> feature travels $\beta c\Delta t$, advancing $\beta c\Delta t\cos\theta$ toward the observer and $\beta c\Delta t\sin\theta$ across the sky. The second <photon> has a shorter journey, so its arrival is delayed by only
$$
\Delta t_{\rm obs}=\Delta t(1-\beta\cos\theta).
$$
Dividing the projected displacement by this arrival interval gives
$$
\boxed{\beta_{\rm app}=\frac{\beta\sin\theta}{1-\beta\cos\theta}.}
$$
<Apparent superluminal motion> is a combination of projection and light-travel delay. The feature's local <speed> remains $\beta c<c$; its inferred transverse displacement divided by the observer's arrival interval is not a local signal <velocity>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For fixed $\beta$, differentiate the apparent <speed> with respect to angle:
$$
\frac{d\beta_{\rm app}}{d\theta}=\frac{\beta(\cos\theta-\beta)}{(1-\beta\cos\theta)^2}.
$$
The maximum lies at $\cos\theta=\beta$ and has value $\beta_{\rm app,max}=\beta/\sqrt{1-\beta^2}=\gamma\beta$. Hence strict <apparent superluminal motion> requires
$$
\boxed{\beta>\frac1{\sqrt2}\simeq0.7071.}
$$
The threshold is an infimum: equality yields exactly $\beta_{\rm app}=1$ at $\theta=\pi/4=45^\circ$, rather than a strictly superluminal value. For a chosen larger $\beta$, the optimal inclination is $\theta=\arccos\beta$, and the full superluminal interval satisfies $\sin\theta+\cos\theta>1/\beta$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For intrinsically symmetric oppositely directed <relativistic jets>, their <proper motions> are
$$
\mu_{\rm app}=\frac{\beta c\sin\theta}{D(1-\beta\cos\theta)},\qquad
\mu_{\rm rec}=\frac{\beta c\sin\theta}{D(1+\beta\cos\theta)}.
$$
The ratio removes $D$ and yields
$$
x\equiv\beta\cos\theta=\frac{\mu_{\rm app}-\mu_{\rm rec}}{\mu_{\rm app}+\mu_{\rm rec}}=0.4.
$$
Taking the product instead gives
$$
\mu_{\rm app}\mu_{\rm rec}=\frac{c^2}{D^2}\frac{\beta^2-x^2}{1-x^2}.
$$
<Causality> requires $\beta\leq1$, so the limiting distance and inclination are
$$
\boxed{D_{\max}=\frac{c}{\sqrt{\mu_{\rm app}\mu_{\rm rec}}},\qquad
\theta_{\max}=\arccos x.}
$$
Here and in <3/e>, “maximum” denotes the limiting $\beta\to1$ bound; massive ejecta obey strict inequalities. The conversion is $1\ \mathrm{mas\,day^{-1}}=5.6113\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$, hence
$$
\boxed{D_{\max}=3.435\ \mathrm{kpc},\qquad\theta_{\max}=66.42^\circ.}
$$
Both bounds occur together in the light-speed limit. At any specified smaller distance, $\beta^2=x^2+(1-x^2)(D/D_{\max})^2$ determines the intrinsic <speed>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Doppler factors> for the two discrete blobs are $\mathcal D_\pm=[\gamma(1\mp\beta\cos\theta)]^{-1}$. Their ratio is $(1+x)/(1-x)=77/33=7/3$. <Lorentz-invariant specific intensity> means $I_\nu/\nu^3$ is unchanged; with $\nu=\mathcal D\nu'$ and the same rest-frame <power-law spectrum>,
$$
I_\nu(\nu)=\mathcal D^3I'_{\nu'}(\nu/\mathcal D)
\propto\mathcal D^{3+\alpha}\nu^{-\alpha}.
$$
The brightness ratio at the common observed <frequency> therefore gives
$$
24=\left(\frac73\right)^{3+\alpha},\qquad
\boxed{\alpha=\frac{\ln24}{\ln(7/3)}-3=0.75081.}
$$
The stated identical intrinsic <specific intensities> must refer to comparable intrinsic states. If the blobs fade with their proper ages, equal observer times sample different ages and need that correction. A steady continuous <jet> has a different boosting exponent, $2+\alpha$; the discrete ejecta and the specified <specific intensity> transformation justify $3+\alpha$ here.

For <optically thin> <synchrotron radiation> from an isotropic <electron> distribution $dN/dE\propto E^{-p}$, the characteristic emitted <frequency> scales as $BE^2$ and the total single-particle <power> as $B^2E^2$. Thus $j_\nu\propto E^{-p}E^2/(d\nu/dE)\propto\nu^{-(p-1)/2}$; integration over the full single-particle spectrum preserves this exponent. Hence
$$
\boxed{p=2\alpha+1\simeq2.50.}
$$
The test-particle strong nonrelativistic <diffusive shock acceleration> prediction is $p=(r+2)/(r-1)=2$ for compression ratio $r=4$, giving $\alpha=0.5$. The inferred spectrum is steeper. If interpreted literally within that uncooled formula, $p=2.50$ would correspond to $r\simeq3$, but cooling, escape, and other acceleration conditions can also steepen it.

The observed Galactic <cosmic ray> differential <energy> spectrum below the knee is approximately $E^{-2.7}$, steeper still; its formal <synchrotron radiation> equivalent would be $\alpha\simeq0.85$. Thus $p\simeq2.50$ lies between the simplest source prediction and the propagated <cosmic ray> slope. The comparison is qualitative: most measured <cosmic rays> are nuclei, whereas the radio slope here probes <electrons>, and propagation and radiative losses alter the spectra differently.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Apply the same symmetric-ejection and <causality> argument to the launch <proper motions>, when the proposed ejecta are fastest. One obtains
$$
x_0=\frac{101-59}{101+59}=0.2625,
$$
and
$$
\boxed{\theta_{\max}=\arccos(0.2625)=74.78^\circ,\qquad
D_{\max}=\frac{c}{\sqrt{101\times59}\ \mathrm{mas\,day^{-1}}}=2.243\ \mathrm{kpc}.}
$$
The fitted angular decelerations describe subsequent evolution; they do not relax the bound already imposed at launch. They are observer-time phenomenological coefficients, not directly identical physical decelerations in the source frame.

The <stellar parallax> distance $2.96\pm0.33\ \mathrm{kpc}$ is compatible with the constant-<velocity> bound $3.435\ \mathrm{kpc}$, but its central value exceeds the decelerating-launch bound by $0.717\ \mathrm{kpc}$, or about $2.17$ quoted standard deviations if the uncertainty is treated as symmetric Gaussian. Even its one-standard-deviation lower endpoint, $2.63\ \mathrm{kpc}$, exceeds $2.243\ \mathrm{kpc}$. At $D=2.96\ \mathrm{kpc}$ the respective inferred intrinsic <speeds> are $\beta=0.885$ and $\beta=1.300$. The latter is a concrete violation of <causality> under the symmetric model assumptions.

Thus the supplied distance favours the constant-<velocity> fit. This is a consistency comparison, not a full statistical model selection: uncertainties in the fitted <proper motions>, their correlations, intrinsic asymmetry and the distance likelihood would be required for that.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Einstein-de Sitter universe> has $a\propto t^{2/3}$ and $H=\dot a/a\propto a^{-3/2}$. Normalizing $a_0=1$ therefore gives $H(z)=H_0(1+z)^{3/2}$. Differentiating $1+z=1/a$ gives
$$
\frac{dt}{dz}=-\frac1{(1+z)H(z)}=-\frac1{H_0(1+z)^{5/2}}.
$$
In the local comoving rest frame, a <photon> crosses proper path length $d\ell=c|dt|$. Galaxies with proper <number density> $n(z)$ and geometric <cross section> $\sigma_G=\pi r_G^2$ contribute the mean number of intersections $d\tau=n(z)\sigma_Gd\ell$. The infinitesimal probability of an intersection is the same to first order, giving
$$
\boxed{dP=d\tau=\frac{c\pi r_G^2n(z)}{H_0(1+z)^{5/2}}\,dz.}
$$
The radius is a physical radius, and $n(z)$ is a proper <number density> evaluated at epoch $z$, rather than a density already multiplied by $dz$. For a finite path with overlaps, the integrated mean count $\tau$ need not itself be the probability of at least one intersection.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Conservation of the comoving galaxy population gives $n(z)=n_0a^{-3}=n_0(1+z)^3$. Holding the proper galaxy radius fixed, integrate <4/a>:
$$
\tau(z_s)=\frac{c\pi r_G^2n_0}{H_0}\int_0^{z_s}(1+z)^{1/2}dz
=\boxed{\frac{2c\pi r_G^2n_0}{3H_0}\left[(1+z_s)^{3/2}-1\right].}
$$
With $r_G=0.015\ \mathrm{Mpc}$, $n_0=0.01\ \mathrm{Mpc^{-3}}$, and $c/H_0=4282.75\ \mathrm{Mpc}$, set $C=c\pi r_G^2n_0/H_0=0.030273$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\tau(1)=0.0369013.}
$$
For random uncorrelated intersections, the zero-intersection probability is $e^{-\tau}$ from the <Poisson distribution>, so the fraction of the sky covered by foreground galaxies *out to* $z=1$ is
$$
\boxed{f=1-e^{-\tau(1)}=0.03623\simeq3.6\%.}
$$
Ignoring overlaps gives $f\simeq\tau\simeq3.7\%$. A literal shell of zero thickness exactly at $z=1$ has zero coverage; the integrated foreground interpretation is needed. Clustering changes the exact overlap probability, although the mean <optical depth> above still follows from the mean <number density>.

Finally,
$$
\boxed{\tau=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad z_s=\left(1+\frac3{2C}\right)^{2/3}-1=12.67.}
$$
At this <optical depth>, the Poisson sky coverage is $1-e^{-1}\simeq63\%$, not $100\%$. The large inferred <cosmological redshift> extrapolates conserved galaxy counts and fixed radii far beyond their plausible physical range; it is the answer within the prescribed toy model.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the dimensionally correct flat <Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker metric>
$$
ds^2=-c^2dt^2+a^2(t)\left[d\chi^2+\chi^2(d\vartheta^2+\sin^2\vartheta\,d\varphi^2)\right].
$$
The source's $-c\,dt^2$ is missing a factor $c$, and its angular term must mean the unit-sphere line element $d\Omega^2$, not the <solid angle> element $d\Omega=\sin\vartheta\,d\vartheta\,d\varphi$. For example, $c\,dt^2$ has dimensions length times time, while $ds^2$ has dimensions length squared. The corrected convention above removes both ambiguities.

A radial light ray has $ds^2=0$ and $|d\chi|=c|dt|/a$. Thus
$$
\frac{d\chi}{dz}=\frac c{H(z)},\qquad
\chi(z)=\frac c{H_0}\int_0^z\frac{du}{(1+u)^{3/2}}
=\frac{2c}{H_0}\left(1-\frac1{\sqrt{1+z}}\right).
$$
A proper shell volume at emission is $dV_{\rm proper}=a^3\chi^2d\chi\,d\Omega$. Multiplying by $n(z)=n_0a^{-3}$ cancels the expansion factors, equivalently using the comoving volume element directly. Hence
$$
\frac{d^2N}{d\Omega\,dz}=n_0\chi^2\frac{d\chi}{dz}
=\boxed{\frac{4c^3n_0(\sqrt{1+z}-1)^2}{H_0^3(1+z)^{5/2}}.}
$$
This counts every galaxy in the shell per <solid angle>, rather than intersections weighted by individual galaxy areas. Near $z=0$ it reduces to $n_0(c/H_0)^3z^2$, as expected for a Euclidean volume shell.

The main intrinsic parameters for old <globular clusters> are age, <metallicity>, helium abundance and the distribution of envelope <masses> after <red giant branch> mass loss. Distance and foreground <interstellar extinction> alter the appearance and must first be removed before assigning intrinsic differences.

Age controls the <main-sequence turnoff>: a more massive <star> has a shorter <main-sequence lifetime>, approximately fuel divided by <luminosity>. An older coeval population therefore has a smaller turnoff <mass> and a fainter, generally redder <main-sequence turnoff>. The separation in <astronomical magnitude> between the <main-sequence turnoff> and <horizontal branch> is useful because a common <distance modulus> cancels.

At comparable age, larger <metallicity> increases stellar-envelope <opacity> and atmospheric line blanketing. This generally makes the <red giant branch> and <main sequence> redder; metal-poor populations tend to have hotter, bluer <red giant branches>. <Metallicity> also affects <hydrogen burning> through the <CNO cycle>, so a quantitative age inference needs composition-dependent stellar models.

The <horizontal branch> is especially sensitive to envelope <mass>: at similar helium-core <mass>, a thinner hydrogen envelope gives a higher <effective temperature> and hence a bluer location. <Red giant branch> mass loss, age and helium abundance all influence that envelope and produce the familiar “second parameter” complexity. Enhanced helium also changes mean molecular weight, <luminosity> and evolutionary lifetime, and can shift <main sequences> and <horizontal branches>.

<Interstellar extinction> dims the diagram, while wavelength-dependent <interstellar extinction> reddens $B-V$; distance primarily supplies a vertical <distance modulus> shift. A systematic bend or different <horizontal branch> colour distribution cannot be explained by distance alone. Because the source hides the evolved branches, it is not possible to assign “older”, “more metal rich”, or “bluer horizontal branch” to a particular panel reliably. === d
{scope}

==== Solution

On the <main sequence>, central <hydrogen burning> converts four protons into helium, positrons, <neutrinos> and released <energy>. The net reaction is $4p\to{}^4\mathrm{He}+2e^++2\nu_e$, with positron annihilation contributing to the total roughly $26.7\ \mathrm{MeV}$ release before subtracting escaping <neutrino> <energy>. In the low-<mass> <main-sequence stars> of an old <globular cluster>, the <proton-proton chain> generally dominates. The <CNO cycle> makes the same net conversion and becomes important in hotter burning regions.

After central hydrogen exhaustion, a <subgiant> contracts its helium core and establishes a <hydrogen-burning shell>. On the <red giant branch>, that shell supplies most of the <luminosity>, commonly through the temperature-sensitive <CNO cycle>, while the inert helium core grows and becomes supported mainly by <electron degeneracy pressure>. The envelope expands and its surface cools. The helium core is not yet powered by helium <nuclear fusion>.

Helium ignition in a sufficiently low-<mass> degenerate core produces the <helium flash>, removing the strong <electron degeneracy pressure> dominance. A <horizontal-branch star> subsequently burns helium in its core through the <triple-alpha process>, $3\,{}^4\mathrm{He}\to{}^{12}\mathrm C+\gamma$, and through ${}^{12}\mathrm C(\alpha,\gamma){}^{16}\mathrm O$. A surrounding <hydrogen-burning shell> can continue simultaneously.

After central helium exhaustion, the <asymptotic giant branch> has an inert carbon-oxygen core with a <helium-burning shell> and an outer <hydrogen-burning shell>. Alternation between quiescent burning and helium-shell thermal pulses drives mixing and substantial mass loss. Subsequent <post-asymptotic giant branch evolution> exposes a hot core that may ionize its expelled envelope as a <planetary nebula>. Finally a <white dwarf> cools mainly by loss of stored thermal <energy>, rather than sustained central <nuclear fusion>.

==== Solution

For ordinary single <stars> in these old <globular clusters>, **no current <core-collapse supernovae> are expected, and no new <black holes> are expected from the normal plotted population**. Their <main-sequence turnoff> <masses> are typically around $0.8M_\odot$, far below the roughly $8M_\odot$ initial-<mass> scale for advanced burning and core collapse. More massive original <stars> exhausted their much shorter <main-sequence lifetimes> long ago. The precise transition <mass> depends on composition and mass loss, but this large separation makes the conclusion robust.

The low-<mass> <stars> now leaving the <main sequence> evolve through the <red giant branch>, helium ignition, <horizontal branch> and <asymptotic giant branch>. ==== Solution

For a spherical <mass> distribution, the <shell theorem> says the circular gravitational acceleration is $GM(<R)/R^2$. Balancing it against the centripetal acceleration $V_c^2/R$ gives
$$
M(<R)=\frac{RV_c^2(R)}G.
$$
At $R=16\ \mathrm{kpc}=4r_0$, the <galaxy rotation curve> gives $V_c=200(1-e^{-4})=196.337\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. With $R=4.93708\times10^{20}\ \mathrm m$,
$$
\boxed{M(<16\ \mathrm{kpc})=2.8515\times10^{41}\ \mathrm{kg}=1.4340\times10^{11}M_\odot.}
$$
This is the total dynamically inferred enclosed <mass>, including any <dark matter>; the <galaxy rotation curve> alone does not separate its components.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $V_0=200\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. A <Taylor expansion> near the centre gives
$$
V_c(r)=V_0\left[\frac r{r_0}-\frac12\left(\frac r{r_0}\right)^2+O\left(\frac{r^3}{r_0^3}\right)\right].
$$
Therefore the circular <angular frequency> is
$$
\boxed{\Omega(r)=\frac{V_c(r)}r=\frac{V_0}{r_0}\left[1-\frac r{2r_0}+O\left(\frac{r^2}{r_0^2}\right)\right]\longrightarrow\frac{V_0}{r_0}.}
$$
Thus the central motion approaches solid-body rotation. Numerically $\Omega_0=1.6204\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, and the central orbital period tends to
$$
\boxed{P_0=\frac{2\pi}{\Omega_0}=\frac{2\pi r_0}{V_0}=3.8776\times10^{15}\ \mathrm s\simeq1.229\times10^8\ \mathrm{yr}.}
$$
The finite limit of $\Omega$ accompanies $V_c\to0$ at $r\to0$; it does not imply a finite central linear <speed>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

At large radius $V_c\to V_0$, so the spherical enclosed <mass> is $M(<r)\simeq V_0^2r/G$. A spherical <mass density> satisfies $dM/dr=4\pi r^2\rho(r)$, and hence
$$
\boxed{\rho(r)\simeq\frac{V_0^2}{4\pi Gr^2}\propto r^{-2}.}
$$
Equivalently, a local power law $\rho\propto r^{-p}$ with enclosed <mass> dominated by that extended profile has $M\propto r^{3-p}$ and $V_c^2\propto r^{2-p}$. A flat <galaxy rotation curve> requires $p=2$.

Differentiating the exact <mass> profile verifies the limit and the finite central <density>:
$$
\rho(r)=\frac{V_0^2}{4\pi Gr^2}\left[(1-e^{-x})^2+2x(1-e^{-x})e^{-x}\right],\qquad x=r/r_0.
$$
As $x\to0$, the bracket is $3x^2+O(x^3)$, so $\rho(0)=3V_0^2/(4\pi Gr_0^2)$. As $x\to\infty$, it tends to one, recovering the $r^{-2}$ tail. Extending the flat <galaxy rotation curve> to infinity makes total <mass> diverge linearly; a real finite halo must eventually depart from this idealization.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Let the uniform thin <galactic disk> have outer radius $R_d$ and <surface mass density> $\Sigma=M/(\pi R_d^2)$, where $M$ is the <mass> calculated in <6/a>. If the redistribution retains the stated outer radius, set $R_d=16\ \mathrm{kpc}$; otherwise $M$ alone does not determine both $R_d$ and $\Sigma$.

A ring of radius $s$ and width $ds$ has <mass> $dM=2\pi\Sigma s\,ds$. By axial symmetry its transverse gravitational force components cancel. At signed height $z$, its vertical force on a <star> of <mass> $m$ is
$$
dF_z=-\frac{Gm\,z\,dM}{(s^2+z^2)^{3/2}}.
$$
Integrating the rings gives, for $z\ne0$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
F_z&=-2\pi Gm\Sigma z\int_0^{R_d}\frac{s\,ds}{(s^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\\
&=-2\pi Gm\Sigma z\left(\frac1{|z|}-\frac1{\sqrt{z^2+R_d^2}}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F(z)=-2\pi Gm\Sigma\left[\operatorname{sgn}z-\frac z{\sqrt{z^2+R_d^2}}\right]\hat{\mathbf z}.}
$$
For a positive distance above the plane, this is $\mathbf F=-2\pi Gm\Sigma[1-z/\sqrt{z^2+R_d^2}]\hat{\mathbf z}$, directed toward the disk. With $R_d=16\ \mathrm{kpc}$, the inferred $\Sigma\simeq178.3M_\odot\,\mathrm{pc^{-2}}$ sets its normalization.

The far-field expansion gives $F_z\simeq-GMm\operatorname{sgn}(z)/z^2$, as required for a point-like total <mass>. Close to either face, $F_z\to\mp2\pi Gm\Sigma$, the infinite-sheet limit. The ideal zero-thickness model has a jump in gravitational field at $z=0$; the formula is understood through its one-sided limits. Its symmetric mean is zero on the plane, and a disk of finite thickness resolves the discontinuity.
