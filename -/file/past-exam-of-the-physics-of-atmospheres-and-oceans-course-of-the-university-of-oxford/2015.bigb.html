== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $b$ denote <buoyancy> in a stably stratified <Boussinesq approximation>. <Geostrophic balance> and <hydrostatic balance> imply the <thermal wind> relation $f_0U_z=-\bar b_y$. Consequently the specified vertical shear requires
$$
\boxed{\bar b_y=-f_0\Lambda,\qquad \bar b_z=N^2>0}.
$$
For northern-hemisphere $f_0>0$ and $\Lambda>0$, the mean <potential temperature> decreases northward and increases upward: $\bar\Theta_y=-(\Theta_0/g)f_0\Lambda$, $\bar\Theta_z=(\Theta_0/g)N^2$. The corresponding mean isentropes slope upward toward the north, with $dz/dy=f_0\Lambda/N^2$. Stable potential-temperature stratification does not require the actual <temperature> to increase upward; the dry adiabatic lapse also enters that comparison.

In the <rigid-lid constant-shear baroclinic model>, perturbation <quasi-geostrophic potential vorticity> is
$$
q'=\nabla_h^2\psi'+\frac{f_0^2}{N^2}\psi'_{zz}.
$$
The mean interior potential-vorticity gradient vanishes on this constant-$f_0$, constant-$N$, linear-shear basic state. Linear <advection> of that perturbation therefore becomes $(\partial_t+\Lambda z\partial_x)q'=0$, the supplied interior equation. It retains slow balanced motion and omits <friction>, planetary-vorticity gradients and nonlinear disturbance <advection>.

Substitution of $\psi'=\Psi(z)\cos(ly)e^{ik(x-ct)}$ gives
$$
ik(\Lambda z-c)\left[\frac{f_0^2}{N^2}\Psi''-(k^2+l^2)\Psi\right]=0.
$$
A possible branch has zero interior perturbation <potential vorticity>, so
$$
\boxed{\Psi''-K^2\Psi=0,\qquad
\Psi=A\cosh(Kz)+B\sinh(Kz),\qquad K=\frac{N\sqrt{k^2+l^2}}{|f_0|}}.
$$
Both supplied applications have $f_0>0$, so this equals the source's expression. For an unstable mode the nonzero <imaginary part> of the <phase speed> prevents a real critical level $\Lambda z=c$; for real speeds the displayed solution remains a valid zero-interior-PV branch away from, and smoothly through, any critical level. Taking the <real part> gives the physical disturbance.

The rigid lids enforce $w'=0$. Linearized adiabatic <buoyancy> conservation is
$$
(\partial_t+\Lambda z\partial_x)b'+v'\bar b_y+N^2w'=0.
$$
Use the geostrophic conventions $v'=\psi'_x$ and $b'=f_0\psi'_z$. At a lid this gives $(\partial_t+\Lambda z\partial_x)\psi'_z-\Lambda\psi'_x=0$, hence
$$
\boxed{-c\Psi'(0)-\Lambda\Psi(0)=0,\qquad
(\Lambda H-c)\Psi'(H)-\Lambda\Psi(H)=0}.
$$
These are the no-normal-flow condition expressed through boundary <buoyancy> <advection>, not conditions of zero horizontal velocity. Boundary <temperature> anomalies can interact even though this solution has no interior perturbation PV, allowing <baroclinic instability> to extract mean <available potential energy>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For $l=0$ put $L_D=NH/f_0$, $\eta=kL_D$ with $k>0$, and write the discriminant as
$$
D(\eta)=\left(\frac\eta2-\coth\frac\eta2\right)\left(\frac\eta2-\tanh\frac\eta2\right)
=1+\frac{\eta^2}{4}-\eta\coth\eta.
$$
The second factor is positive for every $\eta>0$, since $x>\tanh x$. The first changes sign at the positive solution of $x=\coth x$, $x=\eta/2$. Numerically $x_c=1.19968$, so $\eta_c=2.39936$. The growing branch exists for $0<kL_D<\eta_c$, giving
$$
\boxed{\lambda>\lambda_{\min}=\frac{2\pi}{\eta_c}L_D=2.619L_D}.
$$
Equality is neutral, so this is an infimum <wavelength>, rather than an attained shortest growing mode.

In the unstable interval,
$$
\boxed{c_r=\frac{\Lambda H}{2},\qquad
\sigma\equiv kc_i=\frac{\Lambda f_0}{N}\sqrt{-D(\eta)}}.
$$
Both phase-speed branches have the same <real part> there; the imaginary parts have opposite signs. At and beyond the short-wave cutoff the growth rate is zero and the two real speeds are
$$
\frac{c_\pm}{\Lambda H}=\frac12\pm\frac{\sqrt{D(\eta)}}\eta.
$$
They split continuously from $1/2$ and approach the boundary-flow speeds zero and $\Lambda H$ at large $\eta$. As $\eta\to0$, $D=-\eta^2/12+O(\eta^4)$, so $\sigma\to0$ linearly with $k$, despite a finite limiting $c_i=\Lambda H/\sqrt{12}$. A zonally uniform disturbance has no exponential growth from this mechanism.

The maximum growth minimizes $D$. Its derivative is
$$
D'(\eta)=\frac\eta2-\coth\eta+\eta\,\operatorname{csch}^2\eta.
$$
The positive interior minimum is at $\eta_m\simeq1.606$, yielding
$$
\boxed{\lambda_m=\frac{2\pi}{\eta_m}L_D\simeq3.91L_D,\qquad
\sigma_{\max}=0.310\frac{\Lambda f_0}{N}}.
$$
The supplied phase-speed formula therefore predicts fastest growth at a <wavelength> of order four deformation lengths, not at the short-wave cutoff.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Write the vertical complex amplitude as $\Psi(z)=a(z)e^{i\phi(z)}$. A constant <streamfunction> phase has $kx-kc_rt+\phi(z)=\mathrm{const}$, hence $dx/dz=-\phi_z/k$. For $k>0$, westward tilt with height implies $\phi_z>0$. With $v'=\psi'_x$ and $b'=f_0\psi'_z$, a zonal average over the wave gives
$$
\boxed{\overline{v'b'}=\frac{f_0k}{2}a^2\phi_z\cos^2(ly)e^{2kc_it}>0}
$$
for the northern-hemisphere conventions used here. This is the <westward tilt and poleward eddy heat flux> relation. Warm anomalies move northward and cold anomalies southward, transporting heat down the mean gradient $\bar b_y=-f_0\Lambda<0$. The tilt describes the relative phases needed for a nonzero flux, not the literal trajectories of fluid particles along a tilted <streamfunction> crest.

The flux reduces the mean meridional <temperature> contrast and converts mean <available potential energy> into eddy <available potential energy>. In the perturbation buoyancy-variance budget its production term is $-\overline{v'b'}\bar b_y/N^2>0$. Correlated rising warm anomalies and sinking cold anomalies have positive $\overline{w'b'}$, converting that eddy potential <energy> into <kinetic energy>. The flow is therefore energized by the baroclinic reservoir associated with tilted <density>/potential-temperature surfaces. It is not simply a claim that the disturbance amplifies while leaving the basic state's thermal structure unchanged.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For nonzero meridional <wavenumber>,
$$
\eta=L_D\sqrt{k^2+l^2}<\eta_c,
\qquad k^2<\frac{\eta_c^2}{L_D^2}-l^2.
$$
At least one growing zonally varying mode can exist only if
$$
\boxed{|l|<l_{\sup}=\frac{2.39936}{L_D}}.
$$
The upper value is a supremum approached as $k\to0$, where the growth itself tends to zero. Thus not every permitted meridional <wavenumber> has the same fastest-growing zonal scale; nonzero $l$ restricts the available band of $k$ and weakens the growth factor $k/\sqrt{k^2+l^2}$ at fixed $\eta$.

Using $N=\sqrt{N^2}$, not the tabulated squared value itself, gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|r|r|r}
&L_D\ (\mathrm{km})&l_{\sup}\ (\mathrm{m^{-1}})&2\pi/l_{\sup}\ (\mathrm{km})\\\hline
\text{Earth oceans}&54.8&4.38\times10^{-5}&143\\
\text{Jupiter}&1129&2.12\times10^{-6}&2958
\end{array}}
$$
The planetary radii put these into angular context: $l_{\sup}R$ is about 280 for Earth and 149 for Jupiter, corresponding to minimum meridional angular <wavelengths> about $1.28^\circ$ and $2.42^\circ$, respectively. They are reciprocal-length wave numbers; the dimensionless angular counts should not be substituted for $l$ in the Cartesian dispersion relation. If a channel admits a half-wave across its width, the corresponding minimum width is $\pi/l_{\sup}$ rather than a full <wavelength>.

The oceanic scale is mesoscale, while the much larger deformation length makes the Jovian modes thousands of kilometres across. Both are small fractions of the planetary radius, consistent with using a local patch as a first approximation. Real boundaries, planetary-vorticity gradients, varying stratification and jets can change these limits; this simple model alone does not predict the complete observed flow pattern.

Finally, the source's phrase $c^2<0$ must be interpreted in the mean-flow frame. The actual criterion is $D<0$, or $(c-\Lambda H/2)^2<0$. In the original frame $c$ has nonzero <real part>, so $c^2$ is generally complex, not an ordered negative <real number>. For example, at $\eta=1$ and $\Lambda H=1$, $c=0.5+0.2511i$ and $c^2\simeq0.1870+0.2511i$. This is an unstable wave even though the literal printed inequality is inapplicable.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

<Meridional overturning circulation> links relatively warm upper-ocean transport to a colder return flow at depth, with exchanges among basins and vertical branches. Surface heat loss and <salinity> changes alter water <density>; sufficiently dense water can sink and spread along deep pathways. Freshwater input lowers <salinity> and can inhibit dense-water formation. Winds, particularly in the Southern Ocean, and <energy> supplied to mixing are also important to the global return and upwelling pathways. The circulation is not powered by surface <density> forcing alone in an otherwise closed, unmixed ocean.

The <Atlantic meridional overturning circulation> carries heat northward, helping regulate ocean-atmosphere heat exchange and regional climate. Overturning also ventilates the deep ocean and redistributes carbon, oxygen and nutrients. Changes in its strength can alter regional sea-surface <temperatures>, air-sea heat release and atmospheric circulation. They need not eliminate every western-boundary current or every source of European warmth: wind-driven <ocean circulation> and atmospheric <heat transport> are distinct, interacting contributions. The two-box model below isolates a <density>/<salinity> feedback rather than representing all these mechanisms.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

**Illustration TODO.** The source's two-volume circulation sketch on page 4 is unavailable. Its exact arrows and layout cannot be recovered. The surviving flow law, <salinity>/<temperature> definitions and printed steady polynomial allow the calculation below under an explicit freshwater sign convention; no unavailable diagram values are invented.

Use $\Delta T=T_2-T_1>0$, $\Delta S=S_2-S_1$, and let positive $q$ be the thermally driven state with cold box 1 denser than warm box 2. Take positive $F$ to freshen box 1, represented in a fixed-volume <salt budget> by an equivalent salt removal $FS_1$ from that box and addition to the other reservoir. This is the freshwater convention compatible with the printed $+BF$ and positive $B$. It can represent a compensating saline <volume> transfer or the usual virtual-salt-flux approximation. The exact pipe assignment is not claimed to have been read from the missing illustration.

Exchange in either circulation direction reduces a <salinity> difference at rate proportional to $|q|\Delta S$. Steady salt balance therefore gives
$$
\boxed{|q|\Delta S=FS_1,\qquad \Delta S=\frac{FS_1}{|q|}}\qquad(q\ne0).
$$
The supplied linear <equation of state> gives
$$
\frac{\rho_1-\rho_2}{\rho_0}=\alpha\Delta T-\beta\Delta S,
\qquad q=k(\alpha\Delta T-\beta\Delta S).
$$
Eliminating $\Delta S$ yields the <two-box overturning model> equation
$$
\boxed{q|q|-A|q|+BF=0,\qquad A=k\alpha\Delta T,\qquad B=k\beta S_1}.
$$
Here $A$ and $B$ both have transport units when $q,F,k$ do, while $\alpha\Delta T$ and $\beta S_1$ are dimensionless. Although the source calls $q$ a mass flow, its stated Sverdrup units are <volume> per time; the common reference <density> cancels in the Boussinesq salt/<volume> approximation. At $F>0$, $q=0$ cannot be a finite-salinity steady state in this model. At $F=0$, zero flow needs separate treatment instead of division by $|q|$.

The circulation weakens when freshwater freshens the cold reservoir, and the weaker circulation removes the resulting <salinity> contrast less effectively. This <salt-advection feedback> is the mechanism behind multiple steady states, not an additional externally imposed switch.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Use all coefficients exactly as printed and use Sv consistently for transports. The present salt contrast and density-driven <friction> coefficient are
$$
\Delta S=\frac{0.5\times35}{15}=1.1667\ \mathrm{g\,kg^{-1}},\qquad
k=\frac{15}{(2\times10^{-7})(15)-(8\times10^{-7})(1.1667)}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{k=7.26\times10^6\ \mathrm{Sv}=7.26\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}},\quad
A=21.774\ \mathrm{Sv},\quad B=203.226\ \mathrm{Sv}}.
$$
The large numerical value of $k$ reflects division by a small dimensionless <density> contrast, about $2.07\times10^{-6}$ with the supplied effective expansion coefficients. It is not the actual overturning transport, which remains $15$ Sv.

For $q>0$, the steady equation is a quadratic with roots
$$
\boxed{q_\pm=\frac{A\pm\sqrt{A^2-4BF}}2},\qquad 0\le F\le F_c=\frac{A^2}{4B}.
$$
The <critical freshwater forcing in a two-box overturning model> occurs where the two roots coalesce:
$$
\boxed{F_c=0.58324\ \mathrm{Sv},\qquad q_c=A/2=10.887\ \mathrm{Sv}}.
$$
There is also a negative-flow branch for every $F>0$:
$$
\boxed{q_{\rm rev}=\frac{A-\sqrt{A^2+4BF}}2<0}.
$$
At the present forcing, the three roots are $15.000$, $6.774$ and $-3.950$ Sv. The prescribed present state is therefore the upper positive branch, not an arbitrary choice among the roots.

Stability needs a <salinity> evolution law, not just the algebraic steady equation. For fixed <temperature> contrast and reference <salinity>, the standard salt-contrast dynamics can be written
$$
\dot{\Delta S}=\frac{FS_1-|q|\Delta S}{V_{\rm eff}},\qquad
\dot q=-\frac{q|q|-A|q|+BF}{V_{\rm eff}},
$$
where $V_{\rm eff}>0$ summarizes the reservoir volumes. The linear slope at a positive root is $(A-2q)/V_{\rm eff}$: the upper branch is stable and the lower branch unstable. At a negative root it is $(2q-A)/V_{\rm eff}<0$, so the reversed branch is stable. The two stable states below the fold have different attraction basins, demonstrating <bistability>. Their stability is conditional on this fixed-temperature, fixed-reference-salinity closure.

As forcing increases quasistatically from $0.5$ Sv, the normal branch weakens and reaches a <saddle-node bifurcation> at $F_c$. Beyond it no positive steady circulation remains, so the reduced dynamics move toward the stable reversed branch. The reversal is <salinity> dominated: the fresh cold reservoir is no longer the denser one. Recovery under a later forcing change can depend on the system's state and feedback history.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Interpret a uniform increase of $0.3\%$ per year from the present value as a constant slope, $F(t)=F_0(1+0.003t)$ with $t$ in years. Under quasistatic tracking of the stable branch, the time to its fold is
$$
\boxed{t_c=\frac{F_c/F_0-1}{0.003}=55.5\ \mathrm{yr}}.
$$
If instead the phrase means a compounded fractional increase, $F=F_0e^{0.003t}$, the corresponding time is $\ln(F_c/F_0)/0.003=51.3$ yr. These are two different forcing prescriptions, not two different critical freshwater fluxes.

The branch does not remain exactly at $15$ Sv until that date. Differentiating its quadratic gives
$$
\frac{dq_+}{dF}=-\frac{B}{2q_+-A},
$$
which is $-24.71$ at the present state. The initial decrease is about $0.0371$ Sv per year under the linear ramp. At the fold the flow is only $10.887$ Sv, about 27 percent below its present intensity. Thus maintained close has no unique duration without a tolerance: for example, a ten-percent decrease to $13.5$ Sv occurs at
$$
F=\frac{13.5(A-13.5)}B,
\qquad \boxed{t_{10\%}=33.1\ \mathrm{yr}}.
$$
A five-percent criterion gives about $18.4$ yr. The 55-year value estimates persistence of the positive equilibrium branch, not a perfectly flat present-day circulation.

These times are model extrapolations, not forecasts. The reservoirs' finite adjustment times are unspecified; near a fold, weakening restoring rates can invalidate quasistatic tracking and produce a delayed transition. Real ocean <temperatures>, reference <salinity>, winds, mixing, ice, geometry, regional freshwater pathways and the density-to-transport relation do not stay fixed. The imposed freshwater trend and the two-box reduction need independent justification before an actual transition time can be inferred.

A strong weakening of northward Atlantic <heat transport> could cool the North Atlantic and northern Europe relative to a climate with stronger overturning, altering atmospheric circulation and precipitation. It does not imply that all wind-driven transport vanishes or that greenhouse forcing ceases. The defensible implication is sensitivity of regional climate to ocean heat-transport changes, while the toy model supplies a transparent example of feedback and a threshold rather than a reliable dated prediction.
