**Notation.** The source uses $p_s$ for both the hypothetical dry surface <pressure> and the actual surface <pressure> after <evaporation>. Distinguish them as $P_d$ and $P$, respectively, and interpret the specified <pressure> thickness $\Delta p$ as fixed. This conserves the original <hydrogen> inventory and permits $P$ to vary. Reading both occurrences as one fixed number would be inconsistent with adding <water vapor> to a closed <atmospheric column mass> budget.

Let $E(T)$ denote the <saturation pressure> of <water>, let $x$ be the <mole fraction> of <water vapor> in the mixed layer, and let $M_d,M_v$ be the <molar masses> of <hydrogen> and <water>. Surface <phase equilibrium> and the <ideal gas law> give
$$
x=\frac{E(T_s)}P,\qquad p_v(p)=xp.
$$
Because the layer has constant <temperature>, its <relative humidity> is
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{RH}(p)=\frac{p_v(p)}{E(T_s)}=\frac pP},\qquad P-\Delta p\leq p\leq P.
$$
Thus every level strictly above the surface is subsaturated: <hydrostatic balance> makes $p<P$, whereas the <saturation pressure> stays constant. The result does not require a dilute <water vapor> approximation.

The <Clausius-Clapeyron relation>, neglecting the <specific volume> of <liquid water> and treating the <water vapor> as an <ideal gas>, gives
$$
\frac{d\ln E}{dT}=\frac{L_v}{R_vT^2},\qquad
E(T)=E_t\exp\!\left[\frac{L_v}{R_v}\left(\frac1{T_t}-\frac1T\right)\right],\qquad R_v=\frac R{M_v}.
$$
Here $T_t,E_t$ are the <triple point> values, $L_v$ is the specific <latent heat>, and $R$ is the <molar gas constant>. All <temperatures> in these expressions are absolute. As throughout the question, this is a constant-<latent heat>, <ideal gas> model, not an extrapolation valid through the real <critical point> of <water>.

For a well-mixed <ideal gas> mixture, its mean <molar mass> and <water vapor> <mass fraction> are
$$
\overline M=M_d(1+ax),\qquad w_v=\frac{\epsilon x}{1+ax},\qquad
\epsilon=\frac{M_v}{M_d}\simeq9,\qquad a=\epsilon-1\simeq8.
$$
By <hydrostatic balance>, the mixed layer has total <mass> per unit area $\Delta p/g$, of which $w_v\Delta p/g$ is <water vapor>. The remaining <atmosphere> contains no <water vapor>. Consequently conservation of <hydrogen> <mass> gives
$$
\frac P g=\frac{P_d}g+\frac{\Delta p}g w_v,
\qquad
P=P_d+\Delta p\frac{\epsilon E(T_s)}{P+aE(T_s)}.
$$
Equivalently the requested <quadratic equation> is
$$
\boxed{(P-P_d)(P+aE)=\epsilon\Delta p\,E.}
$$
For each $T_s$, calculate $E$, take the positive <root> of this <quadratic equation>, and retain it only if $0\leq x<1$ and $P>\Delta p$. The last condition leaves a nonempty dry layer. In particular the dry constituent's surface <partial pressure> is not generally $P_d$: $P_d$ measures its column weight, and the <hydrogen> and <water vapor> have different <molar masses>.

A convenient parameter is the <water vapor> <mole fraction> itself. The <mass conservation> calculation yields
$$
P(x)=P_d+\frac{\epsilon\Delta p\,x}{1+ax},\qquad E(x)=xP(x).
$$
Both functions increase for $0\leq x\leq1$. At the endpoint,
$$
P(1)=E(1)=P_d+\Delta p.
$$
Therefore the physically admissible <hydrogen>–<water vapor> mixture requires
$$
\boxed{E(T_s)<P_d+\Delta p.}
$$
Beyond this bound, the formal positive <quadratic equation> <root> requires $x>1$, equivalently a negative <hydrogen> <partial pressure>. It is the physical constraints, rather than loss of real <roots>, that end the solution branch. For a concrete example, put $P_d=\Delta p=1$ in consistent <pressure> units, $\epsilon=9$, and $E=3$. The positive <root> of $P^2+23P-51=0$ is about $2.037$, giving $x\simeq1.473$, which is impossible.

The limiting <temperature> is obtained from $E(T_*)=P_d+\Delta p$:
$$
\frac1{T_*}=\frac1{T_t}-\frac{R_v}{L_v}\ln\!\frac{P_d+\Delta p}{E_t}.
$$
This finite limit exists above $T_t$ if $E_t<P_d+\Delta p<E_t\exp[L_v/(R_vT_t)]$. The upper qualification matters mathematically: the stipulated constant-<latent heat> formula has a finite limiting <saturation pressure> as $T\to\infty$, so for an arbitrarily large unspecified $P_d+\Delta p$ the printed assertion of a finite breakdown <temperature> is not unconditional. For a concrete formal counterexample, choose $P_d+\Delta p=10^{15}\,\mathrm{Pa}$: with the usual triple-point constants the model has $E(\infty)\simeq2.5\times10^{11}\,\mathrm{Pa}$, so it never reaches the proposed inventory limit. This is a counterexample inside the stipulated equations, not a physically credible high-<pressure> <ideal gas> ocean. Actual <liquid water> also ceases to exist above its <critical point>, before such an extrapolation would make sense.

Physically, the imposed finite-<pressure>-thickness layer can hold no more than $\Delta p/g$ of <water vapor> per unit area. As $x\to1$, it expels all its <hydrogen> into the dry upper layer. A still larger surface <saturation pressure> cannot coexist with this geometry. The layer must deepen, the <temperature> profile must change, or the assumed <phase equilibrium> must fail. If $P_d<\Delta p$, the additional restriction $P>\Delta p$ can also exclude the low-<temperature> end of the branch.

At the interface the <pressure> is continuous. Immediately below it the <temperature> is $T_s$ and mean <molar mass> is $M_d(1+ax)$; immediately above it the dry <hydrogen> has <temperature> $T_1$. The <ideal gas law> therefore gives
$$
\rho_- =\frac{p_1M_d(1+ax)}{RT_s},\qquad
\rho_+=\frac{p_1M_d}{RT_1}.
$$
An infinitesimal interchange is unstable to <convection> when the lower fluid is less dense than the upper fluid. Hence
$$
\boxed{\frac{T_s}{T_1}>1+a\frac{E(T_s)}{P(T_s)}.}
$$
Equality gives neutral interfacial <buoyancy>; reversing the inequality gives a stable <density> jump. This is an interfacial criterion, not a claim that the whole dry upper <atmosphere> is stable. Its vertical <temperature> profile has not been specified. Also, $\mathrm{RH}(p_1)=p_1/P<1$, so infinitesimal displacement at the interface initially involves no <condensation> or release of <latent heat>.

There are two competing effects. Increasing $T_s$ directly reduces the lower <density>, favouring <convection>. Increasing the <mole fraction> of heavy <water vapor> increases its mean <molar mass>, opposing <convection>. With $x\ll1$, the criterion is approximately $T_s>T_1$. With $x\to1$, it tends to $T_s>\epsilon T_1\simeq9T_1$: substantial surface heating need not overcome the compositional stabilization in <hydrogen>.

For a specified $P_d,\Delta p,T_1$, solve $T_s/T_1-1-ax(T_s)=0$ on the admissible branch to locate the transitions. Its <derivative> is $1/T_1-a x'(T_s)$, so there is no universal monotone trend: rapid growth of the <saturation pressure> can suppress an instability that was present at lower $T_s$. Without numerical parameter values, neither the number of crossings nor a unique interval of unstable <temperatures> can be asserted.

Write $\kappa=R_d/c_p$, where $R_d$ is the <specific gas constant> and $c_p$ the <specific heat capacity at constant pressure>. Conservation of <potential temperature> on a dry <adiabatic process> gives
$$
T(p)=T_g\left(\frac p{p_s}\right)^\kappa,\qquad T_c=qT_g,\qquad q=\left(\frac{p_c}{p_s}\right)^\kappa.
$$
Let $Q=(1-\alpha_c)S$ be the absorbed stellar <irradiance>, and let $T_0=(Q/\sigma)^{1/4}$, with $\sigma$ the <Stefan-Boltzmann constant>. The transparent gas contributes no <thermal radiation>. For a <cloud> with <emissivity> $e$, <Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation> gives thermal absorptivity $e$; assume thermal transmissivity $1-e$ and negligible thermal reflection, as in the usual one-layer <grey body> model. The outgoing <radiant flux> is the transmitted surface emission plus upward <cloud> emission:
$$
Q=(1-e)\sigma T_g^4+e\sigma T_c^4.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{T_g=T_0\left[1-e+eq^4\right]^{-1/4},\qquad T_c=qT_g.}
$$
No factor of four is needed: $S$ is the incident <irradiance> of the specified column, rather than a stellar-beam <irradiance> that still needs a spherical average.

For fixed $\alpha_c,p_c,p_s$, increasing $e$ monotonically warms the ground when $0<q<1$. At $e=0$, $T_g=T_0$; at $e=1$, $T_c=T_0$ and $T_g=T_0/q$. A <cloud> at the surface, $q=1$, produces no such warming. Comparing with a genuinely cloud-free column also requires including its different <albedo>: relative to $(S/\sigma)^{1/4}$, the ratio is $[(1-\alpha_c)/(1-e+eq^4)]^{1/4}$. Reflection can outweigh the <greenhouse effect>.

The governing balance here is the whole-column <radiation budget>. Imposing an additional purely radiative surface balance would incorrectly remove the <convective heat transfer> that the prescribed dry <adiabatic process> may require.

The <cloud> absorbs $e\sigma T_g^4$ from below and emits $e\sigma T_c^4$ from each face. It absorbs no stellar <radiation>. Its net radiative heating per unit area is therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathcal R_c=e\sigma(T_g^4-2T_c^4)=e\sigma T_g^4(1-2q^4).}
$$
For $e>0$, a <cloud> with $q^4<1/2$ warms in the absence of other energy transfers; one with $q^4>1/2$ cools. For the thin <pressure> thickness $\delta p$, its initial <temperature> tendency is approximately $g\mathcal R_c/(c_p\delta p)$ if its sensible <heat capacity> dominates. <Latent heat> and changing condensate modify that conversion from heating to <temperature> change, but not the sign of the radiative input.

With $T_g$ fixed, pure <radiative equilibrium> requires
$$
\boxed{T_{c,\mathrm{rad}}=2^{-1/4}T_g.}
$$
This equals the ambient dry-<adiabatic process> <temperature> precisely when
$$
q^4=\frac12,\qquad \boxed{\frac{p_c}{p_s}=2^{-1/(4\kappa)}}.
$$
For ordinary dry air, $\kappa\simeq2/7$, so the ratio is about $0.545$. If $e=0$, the <cloud> exchanges no thermal <radiation> and its <radiation budget> does not select any equilibrium <temperature>.

At fixed ground <temperature>, warming the <cloud> increases its downward <radiant flux> $e\sigma T_c^4$, giving a positive contribution to the ground's radiative heating. That is the immediate radiative effect. A self-consistent final answer must also remove the <convective heat transfer> used to pin the original <cloud> to the dry <adiabatic process>.

For pure <radiative equilibrium> of both bodies, with no net nonradiative energy exchange, their two balances are
$$
\sigma T_g^4=Q+e\sigma T_c^4,\qquad
e\sigma T_g^4=2e\sigma T_c^4.
$$
For $e>0$ this gives
$$
\boxed{T_g=T_0(1-e/2)^{-1/4},\qquad T_c=T_0(2-e)^{-1/4}.}
$$
The $e\to0^+$ limit gives the selected limiting <cloud> <temperature>; at exactly $e=0$, only $T_g=T_0$ is fixed radiatively. The maximum <greenhouse effect> at fixed $Q$ occurs at $e=1$:
$$
\boxed{T_g=2^{1/4}T_0\simeq1.189T_0.}
$$
The pinned dry-<adiabatic process> case instead has maximum factor $1/q=(p_s/p_c)^\kappa$. It exceeds $2^{1/4}$ for a high, cold <cloud> with $q<2^{-1/4}$, equals it at the level found in part b, and is smaller for a low, warm <cloud>.

In particular, for $q^4<1/2$ the final purely radiative ground is **cooler** than the pinned solution, even though a warmer <cloud> increases downward <radiation> at fixed $T_g$. To see why these statements are compatible, let $H$ be the net upward nonradiative energy transfer from the ground to the <cloud> in the pinned state. Its <conservation of energy> equation requires
$$
H=e\sigma(2T_c^4-T_g^4),\qquad
Q+e\sigma T_c^4-\sigma T_g^4=H.
$$
For the cold <cloud>, $H<0$: maintaining it on the assumed dry <adiabatic process> requires removing its net radiative heating and returning energy elsewhere. Setting $H=0$ changes that energy redistribution. It is therefore incorrect to infer the final ground <temperature> by changing only the downward <radiant flux>. Indeed the final <cloud> is warmer than the pinned one for $q^4<1/2$ and $0<e<1$, while the final ground is cooler. At $e=1$ the <cloud> <temperature> is $T_0$ in both whole-column equilibria.

Fixed <cloud> properties are an artificial constraint. Warming a saturated <cloud> increases the <saturation pressure>, tending to evaporate its <cloud droplets>; the released <water vapor>, changing <latent heat> budget and altered <buoyancy> can change its altitude and <emissivity>. Cooling favours <condensation> and potentially <atmospheric precipitation>. A warm perturbation that survives <evaporation> can rise by <convection>, but neither a unique vertical displacement nor survival of the <cloud> follows without moisture supply and an environmental <temperature> profile. Finally, pure <radiative equilibrium> of the two bodies does not require the intervening transparent gas to remain on the original dry <adiabatic process>; imposing that extra constraint is generally inconsistent except at the matching level in part b.

Before <condensation>, assume a closed parcel with fixed <atmospheric mixing ratio>, constant <specific heat capacities>, and negligible irreversible heating. The <first law of thermodynamics> for a dry <adiabatic process> is
$$
c_p\,dT-\frac1\rho\,dp=0.
$$
The parcel remains in approximate <pressure equilibrium> with a hydrostatic environment. With the standard approximation that parcel and environmental <densities> are close, $dp/dz\simeq-\rho g$, hence
$$
\frac{dT}{dz}=-\frac g{c_p}=-\Gamma_d,\qquad
\boxed{T(z)=T_s-\Gamma_d z.}
$$
This is the <dry adiabatic lapse rate>, about $9.8\,\mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$ for air. More precisely the environmental <hydrostatic balance> gives $dT/dz=-g\rho_{\rm env}/(c_p\rho_{\rm parcel})$; the familiar constant <dry adiabatic lapse rate> uses their ratio close to one.

Let $e_v$ be the parcel's <water vapor> <partial pressure>. Fixed <mole fraction> before <condensation> means $e_v/p$ is constant. Its <dew point> $T_D$ is defined by $E(T_D)=e_v$, where $E$ is the <saturation pressure>. The <Clausius-Clapeyron relation> gives
$$
\frac{L_v}{R_vT_D^2}\frac{dT_D}{dz}=\frac{d\ln e_v}{dz}=\frac{d\ln p}{dz}
\simeq-\frac g{R_d T}.
$$
The last approximation uses <hydrostatic balance>, the <ideal gas law>, and an environmental <temperature> close to the parcel's. Rearrangement yields
$$
\frac{d\ln T_D}{dz}=-\frac{gR_v}{L_vR_d}\frac{T_D}{T}.
$$
For a modest initial <dew-point depression>, $T_D/T\simeq1$ in absolute <temperature>, so
$$
\boxed{T_D(z)\simeq T_{D,s}e^{-z/H},\qquad H=\frac{L_vR_d}{gR_v}.}
$$
With $L_v\simeq2.5\times10^6\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$, $R_d\simeq287\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, and $R_v\simeq461.5\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, $H\simeq1.58\times10^5\,\mathrm m$. This <dew point> scale is much longer than the atmospheric <pressure scale height>.

The <exponential function> is an approximation, not the exact consequence of a dry <adiabatic process>. Retaining $T=T_s-\Gamma_d z$ gives $p/p_s=(T/T_s)^{c_p/R_d}$ and the useful comparison
$$
\frac1{T_D(z)}=\frac1{T_{D,s}}-\frac{R_v}{L_v}\frac{c_p}{R_d}\ln\!\frac{T(z)}{T_s}.
$$
It also shows explicitly that expansion lowers the <dew point> even before any <water vapor> condenses.

At the <lifting condensation level>, the parcel <temperature> first equals its <dew point>. For $z/H\ll1$, the first-order <Taylor expansion> gives $T_D\simeq T_{D,s}(1-z/H)$. Equating this with the dry-<adiabatic process> result gives
$$
T_s-\Gamma_d z=T_{D,s}-\frac{T_{D,s}}H z,\qquad
\boxed{z_{\rm LCL}\simeq\frac{T_s-T_{D,s}}{\Gamma_d-T_{D,s}/H}.}
$$
Near terrestrial surface <temperatures>, $T_{D,s}/H\simeq1.7$–$1.9\,\mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$, whereas $\Gamma_d\simeq9.8\,\mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{z_{\rm LCL}\simeq125\,\mathrm{m\,K^{-1}}(T_s-T_{D,s}).}
$$
Use absolute <temperature> for $T_{D,s}/H$. The <temperature> difference can be evaluated in <kelvins> or degrees Celsius, whose increments agree. The coefficient is an approximate near-surface terrestrial value, not independent of composition or ambient conditions for all <atmospheres>.

= Solution

{c}

A rising parcel cools, lowering the <saturation pressure>. Once its <water vapor> supply exceeds that value, <atmospheric supersaturation> develops. <Homogeneous nucleation> of pure <water> is strongly inhibited at the small <atmospheric supersaturations> typical of <clouds>; existing <aerosol> particles provide <cloud condensation nuclei>. Their dry sizes and composition, together with the parcel's ascent rate, determine how many <cloud droplets> activate.

<Köhler theory> combines the <Kelvin equation>, which raises equilibrium <vapor pressure> over a curved surface, with <Raoult's law>, which lowers it for a <chemical solution>. In a dilute diameter convention, with dry diameter $D_d$ and wet diameter $D$,
$$
s_{\rm eq}(D)\simeq\frac{A_K}D-\frac{\kappa_hD_d^3}{D^3},\qquad
A_K=\frac{4\sigma_wM_w}{RT\rho_w},\qquad
s_c(D_d,\kappa_h)=\left(\frac{4A_K^3}{27\kappa_hD_d^3}\right)^{1/2}.
$$
Here $s=e_v/E(T)-1$ is <atmospheric supersaturation>, $\sigma_w$ is <surface tension>, and $\kappa_h$ measures <aerosol hygroscopicity>, not the dry-<adiabatic process> exponent. The maximum of the <Köhler curve> is the <critical supersaturation>. Larger or more hygroscopic particles generally have lower thresholds. <Cloud-droplet activation> requires overcoming that maximum; total <aerosol> number is not the activated number.

Given an observed <aerosol> <number size distribution> $n_j(D_d)=dN_j/d\ln D_d$ for composition classes $j$, first assign each class a hygroscopicity and compute its <critical supersaturation>. If the parcel reaches maximum <atmospheric supersaturation> $s_{\max}$, the equilibrium-threshold estimate is
$$
D_{d,\min,j}=\left(\frac{4A_K^3}{27\kappa_{h,j}s_{\max}^2}\right)^{1/3},\qquad
\boxed{N_{\rm act}=\sum_j\int_{D_{d,\min,j}}^\infty n_j(D_d)\,d\ln D_d.}
$$
For each fitted <lognormal distribution>, this is its upper-tail <integral>; it can be evaluated analytically with an <error function> or numerically from measured bins.

The value $s_{\max}$ must be calculated, not guessed from the <aerosol> distribution alone. Prescribe parcel upward <velocity> $w$, initial <pressure>, <temperature> and <humidity>, then integrate expansion cooling, <latent heat> release, conservation of total <water>, and diffusional <cloud droplet> growth. Schematically,
$$
\frac{ds}{dt}=a_s w-b_s\frac{dq_l}{dt},\qquad
\frac{d r_i^2}{dt}=2G\,[s-s_{{\rm eq},i}(r_i)],
$$
where $a_s,b_s>0$, $G$ includes <water vapor> <diffusion> and <thermal conduction>, and $q_l$ is liquid <mass fraction>. Rising motion produces <atmospheric supersaturation>; growing particles consume it. Their competition selects the peak and which particles cross their activation barriers. Assume a well-mixed closed parcel, liquid <water>, soluble spherical particles, specified mixing state, and negligible <collision-coalescence> and fallout during activation. Slow kinetic growth, weakly soluble particles or <entrainment> require a more detailed treatment than the threshold <integral>.

The <optical telescope> supplies collecting area and spatial selection, the spectral selector supplies a passband, and the <photodetector> converts received <radiant flux> into a measured signal. For an extended source, a useful schematic photon count is
$$
N_s\simeq\eta\tau A\Omega\int_{\rm band}\frac{L_\lambda\lambda}{hc}\,d\lambda,
$$
where $A\Omega$ is the <etendue>, $\tau$ the integration time, $\eta$ the net transmission and <quantum efficiency>, and $L_\lambda$ the <spectral radiance>. An illustrative independent-noise model gives
$$
\mathrm{SNR}\simeq\frac{N_s}{\sqrt{N_s+N_b+N_d+\sigma_{\rm read}^2}}.
$$
Here $N_b$ and $N_d$ denote background and dark counts, and $\sigma_{\rm read}$ is readout <standard deviation>. This makes the roles of the three components explicit.

A larger <optical telescope> collects more photons at fixed angular pixel size and improves the <diffraction-limited resolution>. Its limits include <optical aberrations>, alignment, launch size, <stray light> and pointing. Conservation of <etendue> constrains simultaneous collecting area and angular acceptance. Increasing spatial resolution by narrowing a pixel's <solid angle> reduces its photons.

A narrower spectral passband or higher <spectral resolving power> separates diagnostic features and rejects unwanted background, but usually reduces photons per channel and hence <signal-to-noise ratio>. Selector transmission, leakage, slit width, detector format and the required time cadence limit the trade. A wider band helps only insofar as it adds useful signal rather than mostly background.

The <photodetector>'s <quantum efficiency>, dark current, read noise, dynamic range and response time determine conversion losses and added noise. Cooling can suppress thermal background and dark current, but cannot remove <photon shot noise>. Longer integration improves a stationary signal's <signal-to-noise ratio> until motion, saturation, drift or required cadence becomes limiting.

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

A <geostationary orbit> provides continuous viewing of approximately the same terrestrial region, favouring fast <cloud> evolution, storm tracking and the diurnal cycle. The large range makes fine spatial resolution demanding, high latitudes have poor viewing geometry, and a single platform has no global coverage. A <polar orbit> gives much smaller range and broad latitude coverage as <Earth> rotates beneath it, favouring detailed global mapping; any one location is sampled intermittently. A <Sun-synchronous orbit> can hold approximately fixed local solar time for comparing observations, but that sampling can miss much of the diurnal evolution. The science therefore sets a trade between spatial coverage, spatial resolution and temporal sampling.

The <Moon>'s very slow, tidally locked rotation makes a lunar stationary <orbit> fundamentally problematic. Let its <orbital angular velocity> around <Earth> be $n$, approximately also its spin rate. For lunar <mass> $m$, terrestrial <mass> $M$, and separation $a$, the two-body estimates give
$$
n^2\simeq\frac{GM}{a^3},\qquad r_{\rm sync}=\left(\frac{Gm}{n^2}\right)^{1/3}\simeq a\left(\frac mM\right)^{1/3},\qquad
r_H\simeq a\left(\frac m{3M}\right)^{1/3}.
$$
Thus $r_{\rm sync}\simeq3^{1/3}r_H$ lies outside the lunar <Hill sphere>. Earth's tidal perturbations prevent the desired simple stable, lunar-centred stationary <orbit>. This is stronger than merely saying that the required <orbit> is high; specially maintained <three-body orbits> are a different construction.

For a prograde equatorial <synchronous circular orbit>, equate <gravitational acceleration> and centripetal <acceleration>. With planetary radius $R_M$, $GM=g_sR_M^2$, and rotation period $P$,
$$
\frac{GM}{r_o^2}=\left(\frac{2\pi}P\right)^2r_o,\qquad
r_o=\left[\frac{g_sR_M^2P^2}{4\pi^2}\right]^{1/3}.
$$
The tabulated $g_s$ is a <gravitational acceleration>; its printed unit must be read as $\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, rather than $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Using $P=88642\,\mathrm s$ gives
$$
r_o\simeq20398.7\,\mathrm{km},\qquad
\boxed{h=r_o-R_M\simeq17008.7\,\mathrm{km}.}
$$
The <orbital altitude> is measured from the surface, rather than the centre.

A calibrated multispectral filter <radiometer> is a natural choice for rapid thermal mapping: a few selected bands provide higher photon throughput and faster imaging than spreading the light over many <spectrometer> channels. Several bands can help separate <temperature>, dust <emissivity> and atmospheric effects. A <spectrometer> would be justified if identifying composition or resolving spectral structure were a central additional objective; one broadband <radiance> alone cannot generally determine both <temperature> and unknown <emissivity>.

$$
1.22\frac\lambda D\lesssim\frac{500}{h},\qquad
\boxed{\lambda\lesssim12.0\,\mathrm{\mu m}.}
$$
A band near $10\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ is therefore a reasonable compromise between useful planetary thermal emission and the required <diffraction-limited resolution>. At that <wavelength>, the nominal surface <Rayleigh criterion> separation is about $415\,\mathrm m$; at $15\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ it is about $623\,\mathrm m$ and fails the target. The exact bands must avoid or model strong atmospheric absorption and account for the dust spectrum. Take a Northern-Hemisphere cyclonic <vortex>, with positive <relative vorticity>. For a fixed-depth incompressible layer, conservation of <absolute vorticity> gives
$$
\frac D{Dt}(\xi+f)=0,\qquad \frac{D\xi}{Dt}=-\beta v.
$$
The anticlockwise flow on the east side moves parcels northward, increasing their <planetary vorticity> and creating negative <relative vorticity> anomalies. On the west side it moves parcels southward and creates positive <relative vorticity> anomalies. Initially,
$$
\partial_t\xi\simeq-\beta v,
$$
so the positive anomaly grows on the western flank while the eastern flank loses positive <vorticity>. The cyclonic pattern consequently shifts westward. The induced anomalies themselves drive meridional flow and further <relative vorticity> anomalies, transmitting a <Rossby wave> pattern through the surrounding fluid. The restoring process is this exchange between <planetary vorticity> and <relative vorticity>, rather than a parcel executing a permanently westward translation.

{title=<Relative vorticity> generated by a Northern-Hemisphere cyclonic <vortex> on a <beta plane>}

The sketch shows the initial tendency, with the generated signs separated from the initial positive core. An isolated disturbance contains many <wavevectors> and disperses; westward <phase velocity> does not imply that all its <wave energy> travels westward. A Southern-Hemisphere cyclonic <vortex> reverses the signs of the initial and generated <relative vorticities>, while the same <beta plane> mechanism remains.

Write $u=U+u'$, $v=v'$, and $\xi=\xi'$, with $U=\bar u$ constant. Discarding products of perturbations in the <vorticity equation> gives
$$
(\partial_t+U\partial_x)\xi'+\beta v'=0.
$$
Introduce the <streamfunction> convention
$$
u'=-\partial_y\psi',\qquad v'=\partial_x\psi',\qquad \xi'=\nabla_h^2\psi'.
$$
It enforces <incompressibility> identically. The linear <partial differential equation> is
$$
(\partial_t+U\partial_x)\nabla_h^2\psi'+\beta\partial_x\psi'=0.
$$
For the <plane wave> $\psi'=\operatorname{Re}\{\widehat\psi\exp[i(kx+ly-\omega t)]\}$, put $K^2=k^2+l^2>0$. Substitution yields
$$
i(\omega-Uk)K^2\widehat\psi+i\beta k\widehat\psi=0,\qquad
\boxed{\omega=Uk-\frac{\beta k}{k^2+l^2}.}
$$
The first term is the <Doppler shift> by the background flow; the second is the intrinsic <Rossby wave> <frequency>. The source's convention $\omega\geq0$ selects representatives of real waves, rather than making this expression nonnegative for arbitrary $k,l$. For $U=0$ and $\beta>0$, nonzero positive <frequency> requires $k<0$; the conjugate mode has opposite signs of $k,l,\omega$ and describes the same real field.

For $U=0$ and fixed $\omega>0$, completing the square in the <dispersion relation> gives
$$
\boxed{\left(k+\frac\beta{2\omega}\right)^2+l^2=\left(\frac\beta{2\omega}\right)^2.}
$$
These <Rossby-wave isofrequency circles> lie in the $k\leq0$ half-plane and touch the origin, where the <dispersion relation> is undefined. Their radii decrease as <frequency> increases. The zero-<frequency> line is $k=0,l\ne0$.

The <group velocity> is normal to the constant-<frequency> curves, toward increasing <frequency>:
$$
\boxed{c_{gx}=\frac{\beta(k^2-l^2)}{(k^2+l^2)^2},\qquad
c_{gy}=\frac{2\beta kl}{(k^2+l^2)^2}.}
$$
At $l=0$, the <group velocity> is eastward; close to $k=0$ it is westward. For the positive-<frequency> circles its arrows point inward toward the circle centre. Its meridional component is southward in $k<0,l>0$ and northward in $k<0,l<0$.

\Image[2021-rossby-dispersion.png]
{title=Positive-<frequency> <Rossby-wave isofrequency circles> and <group velocity> directions in dimensionless <wavenumber> coordinates}

For $k\ne0$, the zonal <phase velocity>, meaning propagation at fixed $y$, is
$$
\boxed{c_{px}=\frac\omega k=-\frac\beta{k^2+l^2}<0.}
$$
It is westward everywhere it is defined. The zonal <group velocity> is eastward for $|k|>|l|$, westward for $|k|<|l|$, and zero on $|k|=|l|$. There is no region of eastward zonal <phase velocity>. At $k=0$, $\omega/k$ is undefined, though its continuous limiting expression is westward and $c_{gx}=-\beta/l^2$. Using instead the normal-to-wavecrest <phase velocity> vector gives $\mathbf c_p=\omega(k,l)/(k^2+l^2)$; its zonal component is also westward for $k\ne0$, but is not the same scalar as $\omega/k$.

For a nonzero zonal <wavenumber> $k$, stationarity requires
$$
0=\frac\omega k=U-\frac\beta{k^2+l^2},\qquad K_s^2=\frac\beta U.
$$
A real propagating solution therefore requires a westerly $U>0$. For $0<|k|<K_s$ there are two meridional <wavenumbers>:
$$
\boxed{l_\pm=\pm\sqrt{\frac\beta U-k^2}.}
$$
The source's assertion of two stationary solutions needs these qualifications: at $|k|=K_s$ they coincide, and at $|k|>K_s$ there is no real meridionally propagating solution. For example $k=2\sqrt{\beta/U}$ gives $l^2=-3\beta/U$. Modes with $k=0$ are a separate zonally invariant degeneracy and do not have a defined zonal <phase velocity>.

Adding $U$ to the zonal <group velocity> and imposing stationarity gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf c_g=\frac{2U k}{K_s^2}(k,l),\qquad
\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{c_{gy}}{c_{gx}}=\frac lk.}
$$
Thus a localized midlatitude disturbance radiates stationary <Rossby wave> activity downstream, along one poleward and one equatorward branch. For $k>0$ in the Northern Hemisphere, $l_+$ gives the northeastward branch and $l_-$ the southeastward branch. With strictly constant $U,\beta$, these <wave rays> are straight. Changing latitude cannot bend them within that constant-coefficient model.

To discuss a planet, extend it locally to slowly varying $\beta(\phi)=2\Omega\cos\phi/a_p$ and possibly $U(\phi)$. The conserved zonal <wavenumber> in a locally zonally uniform medium then has
$$
l^2(\phi)=\frac{\beta(\phi)}{U(\phi)}-k^2.
$$
For constant $U$ and northward propagation in the Northern Hemisphere, decreasing $\beta$ reduces $l$ until $l=0$: the <wave ray> becomes zonal at a turning latitude and can refract equatorward. The equatorward branch initially encounters larger allowed $K_s$ and becomes more meridional. This local midlatitude description eventually needs replacement near the equator or wherever the background varies rapidly.

Orographic forcing by a mountain range or persistent spatially localized heating can supply such a disturbance. Its steady forcing selects the ground-stationary part of the response. The corresponding local total <wavelength> is
$$
\boxed{\Lambda_s=\frac{2\pi}{K_s}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac U\beta}.}
$$
For fixed $U$ it increases toward the pole as $\beta$ decreases. Locally the zonal integer scale has upper bound $n_{\max}\simeq a_p\cos\phi\,K_s=\sqrt{2\Omega a_p\cos^3\phi/U}$, so fewer planetary-scale zonal waves fit around higher-latitude circles. This is not an unconditional trend in actual weather: changing $U$, its curvature, stratification and the forcing spectrum can change the dominant <Rossby wave> <wavelengths>.

The parameter $r>0$ is an inverse drag time, so the frictional acceleration is $-r(u,v)$. On the equatorial <beta plane>, $f=\beta y$.

\boxed{\beta\psi_x+r\nabla_h^2\psi=F_\zeta(y),\qquad F_\zeta=-\frac{dF_u}{dy}.}

The right side is the vertical <curl> of the imposed acceleration, with units $\mathrm{s^{-2}}$. The left side balances <planetary vorticity> <advection> and frictional removal of <relative vorticity>. The <streamfunction> has units $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, since it describes horizontal <velocity>, rather than depth-integrated <volume flux>.

Seek $\psi=\Psi(x)\cos(my)$, with $m=\pi/b$. The <ordinary differential equation> is
$$
r\Psi''+\beta\Psi'-rm^2\Psi=F_0.
$$
Its constant particular solution and characteristic exponents are
$$
\boxed{A=-\frac{F_0}{rm^2}=-\frac{F_0b^2}{r\pi^2},\qquad
\lambda=\frac{\beta+\sqrt{\beta^2+4r^2m^2}}{2r},\qquad
\gamma=\frac{\sqrt{\beta^2+4r^2m^2}-\beta}{2r}.}
$$
Both $\lambda,\gamma$ are positive; the two homogeneous solutions are $e^{-\lambda x}$ and $e^{\gamma x}$. Their product satisfies $\lambda\gamma=m^2$. At small $rm/\beta$,
$$
\boxed{\lambda\simeq\frac\beta r,\qquad \gamma\simeq\frac{rm^2}\beta.}
$$
For the rectangular basin, choose zero <streamfunction> on its connected impermeable boundary. The factor $\cos(my)$ already gives zero at $y=\pm b/2$. Enforcing $\Psi(0)=\Psi(L)=0$ yields the exact result
$$
\Psi=A\left[1-C_W e^{-\lambda x}-C_E e^{\gamma(x-L)}\right],
$$
where
$$
C_W=\frac{1-e^{-\gamma L}}{1-e^{-(\lambda+\gamma)L}},\qquad
C_E=\frac{1-e^{-\lambda L}}{1-e^{-(\lambda+\gamma)L}}.
$$
The narrow <Stommel boundary layer> approximation requires
$$
\boxed{\lambda^{-1}\ll L\ll\gamma^{-1}.}
$$
Then $e^{-\lambda L}$ is negligible, $C_W\simeq\gamma L$ and $C_E\simeq1$, giving
$$
\boxed{\psi\simeq A\left[1-\gamma L e^{-\lambda x}-e^{\gamma(x-L)}\right]\cos(my).}
$$
It is an asymptotic formula: at $x=0$ the remaining mismatch is of order $A(\gamma L)^2$. Expanding the eastern exponential as well gives the familiar leading <Sverdrup balance> interior plus western correction,
$$
\psi\simeq\frac{F_0}\beta\left[(x-L)+Le^{-x/\delta_W}\right]\cos(my),\qquad \delta_W=\frac r\beta.
$$
**Printed inequality.** The original PDF explicitly has $\lambda^{-1}\gg L$ as well as $\gamma^{-1}\gg L$. The first sign is reversed for the requested small-friction limit. This can also be disproved directly: if $\lambda L=0.02$ and $\gamma L=0.01$, the printed approximate bracket at the western wall is $1-0.01-e^{-0.01}\simeq-4.98\times10^{-5}$, while at the eastern wall it is $-0.01e^{-0.02}\simeq-0.00980$. The latter is of the same order as the retained basin-scale signal, violating the boundary condition at leading order. The exact expression above remains available without imposing the erroneous inequalities.
