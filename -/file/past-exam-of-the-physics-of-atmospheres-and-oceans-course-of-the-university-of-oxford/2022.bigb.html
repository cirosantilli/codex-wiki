For the <variable-latent-heat saturation curve> of a pure saturated <water> <atmosphere>, the total <pressure> equals the <saturation pressure>. The exact <Clausius-Clapeyron relation> along the <phase coexistence curve> is
$$
\frac{dp}{dT}=\frac{L(T)}{T(v_v-v_c)},
$$
where $v_v,v_c$ are the specific volumes of the <vapor> and condensed <thermodynamic phase>. Neglecting $v_c$ and using the <ideal gas law>, $v_v=R_{\rm wv}T/p$, gives
$$
\frac{d\ln p}{dT}=\frac{L(T)}{R_{\rm wv}T^2},\qquad
\ln\frac p{p_*}=\int_{T_*}^{T}\frac{L(\theta)}{R_{\rm wv}\theta^2}\,d\theta.
$$
The <triple point> provides the integration constant. Below it, the prescribed <latent heat> is constant, so
$$
\boxed{\ln\frac p{p_*}=\frac{L_s}{R_{\rm wv}}\left(\frac1{T_*}-\frac1T\right),\quad T\leq T_*.}
$$
Above it, write $K=L_*/[R_{\rm wv}(T_c-T_*)]$. Integrating $K(T_c/T^2-1/T)$ gives
$$
\boxed{\ln\frac p{p_*}=K\left[T_c\left(\frac1{T_*}-\frac1T\right)-\ln\frac{T}{T_*}\right],\quad T_*\leq T<T_c.}
$$
These are the requested implicit <temperature> profiles $T(p)$. Both branches meet at $(T_*,p_*)$, and each is monotone before the limiting <liquid-gas critical point>. The lower branch describes equilibrium with <ice>, rather than a stable <liquid> surface. A stable <liquid> <ocean> surface belongs to the upper branch; the colder upper <atmosphere> may use the lower branch. <Hydrostatic balance> is needed to convert this relation into a height profile, but not to obtain $T(p)$.

Let $Y=-\ln(p/p_*)$. For the temperature-dependent <latent heat>,
$$
Y(T)=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{L_s}{R_{\rm wv}}\left(\dfrac1T-\dfrac1{T_*}\right),&T\leq T_*,\\[4pt]
K\left[T_c\left(\dfrac1T-\dfrac1{T_*}\right)+\ln\dfrac T{T_*}\right],&T_*\leq T<T_c.
\end{cases}
$$
Keeping the <latent heat> of vaporization fixed changes only the upper branch to
$$
Y_{\rm fixed}(T)=\frac{L_*}{R_{\rm wv}}\left(\frac1T-\frac1{T_*}\right).
$$
Both curves fall from $+\infty$ as $T\to0^+$, pass through $Y=0$ at the <triple point>, and are convex. At the <triple point> there is a corner because the <latent heat> changes from $L_s$ to $L_*$. Their right-hand slopes there agree. Above the <triple point>,
$$
Y'_{\rm variable}=-K\frac{T_c-T}{T^2},\qquad
Y'_{\rm fixed}=-\frac{L_*}{R_{\rm wv}T^2},\qquad
Y''_{\rm variable}=K\frac{2T_c-T}{T^3}>0.
$$
The variable-<latent heat> curve lies **above** the fixed-<latent heat> curve: its downward slope has smaller magnitude at every $T>T_*$. It reaches a horizontal tangent at $T_c$, whereas the fixed-<latent heat> curve is still descending. Both limiting ordinates are finite; this model gives $Y_{\rm variable}(T_c)\simeq-7.30$ and $Y_{\rm fixed}(T_c)\simeq-11.40$. These are extrapolations of the stipulated model, not accurate <water> <liquid-gas critical point> data. Extending the variable branch beyond $T_c$ would give negative <latent heat> and is physically inadmissible.

For constant <gravitational acceleration>, <hydrostatic balance> gives the <atmospheric column mass> $m=p_{\rm sfc}/g$, with negligible <pressure> at the top. For the same surface <temperature> $T_*<T_{\rm sfc}<T_c$, the variable <latent heat> gives a smaller integrated $\ln(p_{\rm sfc}/p_*)$, and hence
$$
\boxed{\frac{m_{\rm variable}}{m_{\rm fixed}}=
\exp\left\{K\left[T_c\left(\frac1{T_*}-\frac1{T_{\rm sfc}}\right)-\ln\frac{T_{\rm sfc}}{T_*}\right]
-\frac{L_*}{R_{\rm wv}}\left(\frac1{T_*}-\frac1{T_{\rm sfc}}\right)\right\}<1.}
$$
Multiplying by the same planetary surface area gives the same comparison for total <mass>. The models agree for a surface at or below the <triple point>, where they use the same <sublimation> branch. The comparison assumes the same surface <temperature>, rather than the same surface <pressure>.

The <pressure-linear grey opacity> model interprets $\kappa$ as a <mass absorption coefficient>. If it is instead a molecular <absorption cross section>, replace $\kappa$ below by that cross section divided by molecular <mass>. Assume a plane-parallel <atmosphere>, <hydrostatic balance>, local <thermodynamic equilibrium>, and thermal <absorption> and emission without <scattering of light>. With height $z$ increasing upwards,
$$
d\tau=\kappa\rho\,dz=-\frac{\kappa}{g}\,dp.
$$
Integrating upwards from the surface gives
$$
\tau(p)=\frac{\kappa_s}{gp_s}\int_p^{p_s}p'\,dp'
=\frac{\kappa_sp_s}{2g}\left[1-\left(\frac p{p_s}\right)^2\right].
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\tau_\infty=\frac{\kappa_sp_s}{2g},\qquad
\tau(p)=\tau_\infty[1-(p/p_s)^2].}
$$
The vertical <optical depth> is dimensionless. The <atmosphere> is optically thick when $\tau_\infty\gg1$ and optically thin when $\tau_\infty\ll1$. A ray with zenith-angle cosine $\mu$ sees <optical depth> $\tau_\infty/\mu$, so even a vertically thin layer can be opaque along a sufficiently grazing ray.

It is convenient to use the <optical depth> **above** a level,
$$
t(p)=\tau_\infty-\tau(p)=\tau_\infty(p/p_s)^2.
$$
Thermal <radiation> escapes mainly from levels with $t$ of order unity: emission much deeper is attenuated, while the column far above contains little emitting material. Hence, in an optically thick <atmosphere>,
$$
\boxed{p_{\rm rad}\sim\frac{p_s}{\sqrt{\tau_\infty}}
=\sqrt{\frac{2gp_s}{\kappa_s}},\qquad
T_{\rm rad}\sim T_s\tau_\infty^{-a/2}.}
$$
For a particular viewing direction, $t\sim\mu$ instead gives $p_{\rm rad}\sim p_s\sqrt{\mu/\tau_\infty}$. These are characteristic levels, not sharp emitting surfaces; the contribution function depends on the <temperature> exponent $a$. If $\tau_\infty\lesssim1$, the formal level lies below the surface and the surface itself contributes strongly. A useful estimate then clips $p_{\rm rad}$ at $p_s$.

Write $\tau_0=\tau_\infty$, $n=2a$, and $t=\tau_0(p/p_s)^2$. The integrated <Planck function> is $B(T)=\sigma T^4/\pi$ by the <Stefan-Boltzmann law>, and
$$
B(t)=\frac{\sigma T_s^4}{\pi}\left(\frac t{\tau_0}\right)^n.
$$
The formal <radiative transfer equation> solution for upward <radiance> at the top is
$$
I(0,\mu)=\frac{\sigma T_s^4}{\pi}e^{-\tau_0/\mu}
+\int_0^{\tau_0}B(t)e^{-t/\mu}\frac{dt}{\mu},\qquad0<\mu\leq1.
$$
The first term is transmitted surface emission; the second is emission from the <atmosphere>. Integrating <radiance> over the upward hemisphere gives the outgoing <radiant flux> per unit area,
$$
\boxed{F=2\sigma T_s^4\left[E_3(\tau_0)+\tau_0^{-n}
\int_0^{\tau_0}t^nE_2(t)\,dt\right],\qquad
E_j(t)=\int_0^1\mu^{j-2}e^{-t/\mu}\,d\mu.}
$$
Here $E_j$ is an <exponential integral>. For fixed $a$ and $\tau_0\to\infty$, surface transmission is negligible and the upper limit can be extended to infinity. Positivity permits interchanging the <integrals>; putting $t=\mu x$ gives
$$
\int_0^\infty t^nE_2(t)\,dt
=\int_0^1\mu^{n+1}\,d\mu\int_0^\infty x^ne^{-x}\,dx
=\frac{\Gamma(n+1)}{n+2}.
$$
Consequently the exact hemispheric optically thick result is
$$
\boxed{F\sim\sigma T_s^4\tau_0^{-2a}\,
\frac{\Gamma(2a+1)}{a+1}.}
$$
This is a dimensional scale times a dimensionless definite <integral> depending only on $a$. In the commonly used vertical-ray approximation, define $F_{\rm v}=\pi I(0,1)$. Then
$$
F_{\rm v}=\sigma T_s^4\left[e^{-\tau_0}+\tau_0^{-2a}\int_0^{\tau_0}t^{2a}e^{-t}\,dt\right]
\sim\sigma T_s^4\tau_0^{-2a}\Gamma(2a+1).
$$
A vertical-ray approximation therefore has the same scaling but omits the angular factor $1/(a+1)$. Stating which quantity is used prevents a purely angular normalization discrepancy.

At fixed $T_s$ and $a>0$, the outgoing <radiant flux> scales as $F\propto\tau_\infty^{-2a}$. Increasing the <optical depth> shifts the effective emitting region to lower <pressure> and lower <temperature>. A surface <temperature> cannot be inferred from emission alone: specify the absorbed stellar <radiant flux> $F_0$ and impose <radiative equilibrium>, $F=F_0$. If the no-<atmosphere> <blackbody> has $T_0=(F_0/\sigma)^{1/4}$, then
$$
\boxed{\frac{T_s}{T_0}\sim
\left[\frac{a+1}{\Gamma(2a+1)}\right]^{1/4}\tau_\infty^{a/2}.}
$$
The vertical-ray approximation omits $a+1$ from this prefactor. For fixed $a>0$ and sufficiently large <optical depth>, the surface is warmer than $T_0$: this is the thermal <greenhouse effect> within the prescribed <temperature> profile. It does not by itself establish a self-consistent vertical heating or <convection> balance.

For $a=0,\frac12,1,\frac32,2,\ldots$, put $n=2a=0,1,2,3,4,\ldots$. Repeated <integration by parts> gives $\Gamma(n+1)=n!$. The hemispheric thick-limit results are
$$
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
a&0&1/2&1&3/2&2\\ \hline
F/(\sigma T_s^4)&1&2/(3\tau_0)&1/\tau_0^2&12/(5\tau_0^3)&8/\tau_0^4\\
T_s/T_0&1&(3\tau_0/2)^{1/4}&\tau_0^{1/2}&(5\tau_0^3/12)^{1/4}&(\tau_0^4/8)^{1/4}
\end{array}
$$
The second row assumes fixed surface <temperature>; the third assumes fixed absorbed <radiant flux>. In the vertical-ray approximation the first of these rows becomes $1,1/\tau_0,2/\tau_0^2,6/\tau_0^3,24/\tau_0^4$, and the surface <temperature> factors are the inverse fourth roots.

Thus increasing $a$ makes the <atmosphere> colder at every fixed $0<p<p_s$, decreases outgoing <radiation> at fixed $T_s$, and requires a warmer surface at fixed absorbed <radiant flux>. The case $a=0$ is isothermal and gives $F=\sigma T_s^4$ exactly, including finite <optical depth>.

There is an essential approximation limit. The extension of the <integral> to infinity is valid for fixed $a$ as $\tau_0\to\infty$, and is not uniform for arbitrarily large $a$ at fixed $\tau_0$. The vertical integrand peaks at $t=2a$, so one needs that peak well above the surface in <optical depth>, with a negligible tail beyond $\tau_0$. For example, at $\tau_0=10$ the thick formula predicts $F/(\sigma T_s^4)=20!/(11\,10^{20})\simeq0.00221$ for $a=10$ but approximately $0.04773$ for $a=12$. This artificial increase contradicts the exact monotonicity: in the finite-<optical depth> expression every atmospheric emission factor $(t/\tau_0)^{2a}$ decreases with $a$. At fixed finite $\tau_0$, the exact limiting outgoing <radiant flux> is the transmitted surface term $2\sigma T_s^4E_3(\tau_0)$, rather than factorial divergence.

The <dilute Köhler curve> follows because the <Kelvin equation> multiplies the planar-solution <saturation pressure> by the curvature correction. Combining it with <Raoult's law> therefore gives
$$
S_{\rm eq}(r)=\frac{e_S^{\rm sol}(r)}{e_S(\infty)}
=\left(1-\frac B{r^3}\right)\exp\left(\frac Ar\right).
$$
For a dilute <chemical solution> and a small curvature correction, $B/r^3\ll1$ and $A/r\ll1$. A first-order <Taylor expansion> gives
$$
S_{\rm eq}=1+\frac Ar-\frac B{r^3}
+O\left((A/r)^2,(A/r)(B/r^3)\right).
$$
Thus the simplified <Köhler theory> relation is
$$
\boxed{S_{\rm eq}(r)\simeq1+\frac Ar-\frac B{r^3}.}
$$
The curvature term raises the required ambient <vapor pressure>; the solute term lowers it. These laws combine multiplicatively before linearization, so the displayed simplified relation is an approximation, not an exact identity.

For $A,B>0$, differentiating the simplified <Köhler theory> curve gives
$$
\frac{dS_{\rm eq}}{dr}=-\frac A{r^2}+\frac{3B}{r^4},\qquad
\boxed{r_c=\sqrt{\frac{3B}{A}}.}
$$
The <derivative> is positive below $r_c$ and negative above it, so $r_c$ is the <Köhler activation radius>, the unique maximum. At that point $B/r_c^3=A/(3r_c)$, giving
$$
\boxed{S_c=1+\frac{2A}{3r_c}=1+\sqrt{\frac{4A^3}{27B}}.}
$$
The curve crosses $S=1$ at $r=\sqrt{B/A}=r_c/\sqrt3$, reaches $S_c$ at $r_c$, and approaches $1$ from above as $r\to\infty$. Its formal divergence to negative values as $r\to0$ is outside the dilute-<chemical solution> approximation; a finite dry solute core and composition-dependent properties must replace that extrapolation.

Let $1<S<S_c$ be the ambient <saturation ratio>. There are two equilibrium radii: $r_-<r_c<r_+$. <Condensation> occurs if $S>S_{\rm eq}(r)$; <evaporation> occurs if $S<S_{\rm eq}(r)$. Therefore droplets with $r<r_-$ grow towards $r_-$, while those with $r_-<r<r_c$ shrink towards $r_-$. The smaller equilibrium is stable.

Above $r_c$, droplets with $r_c<r<r_+$ still undergo <evaporation> and ultimately approach $r_-$. Droplets with $r>r_+$ grow, and the larger equilibrium $r_+$ is unstable. Merely exceeding $r_c$ is therefore **not sufficient** for continued growth at subcritical ambient <atmospheric supersaturation>. For example, if $(S-1)/(S_c-1)=0.6$, the two intersections have approximately $r_-/r_c=0.676$ and $r_+/r_c=2.349$; a droplet of radius $1.5r_c$ undergoes <evaporation>. If instead $S>S_c$, there is no equilibrium barrier in this approximation and droplets activate into growing <cloud droplets>.

<Köhler theory> explains activation of <cloud condensation nuclei>, rather than rapid production of <atmospheric precipitation>. <Diffusion-controlled droplet growth> is limited by <diffusion> of <water> <vapor> and removal of released <latent heat>; approximately $d(r^2)/dt$ is proportional to the ambient <atmospheric supersaturation>. It slows in radius and tends to narrow a droplet size distribution. The available <water> <vapor>, changing <temperature> and shared depletion of <atmospheric supersaturation> also invalidate unlimited growth at prescribed $S$. Formation of rain-sized drops normally requires <collision-coalescence>, while colder <clouds> can additionally use <cloud ice nucleation versus ice growth>.

A <Michelson interferometer> uses a <beam splitter> to divide collimated input <light> between a fixed mirror and a moving mirror. Returning beams recombine at the <beam splitter>; one output reaches a <photodetector>. A compensator plate can equalize the glass traversed in the two arms. Moving one mirror by $x$ changes the <optical path difference> by $2x$.

\Image[2022-michelson-layout.png]
{title=Michelson interferometer and the corresponding measurement chain}

The usual nominal <Fourier transform spectroscopy> specification is $\delta\nu\simeq1/d_{\max}$, where $d_{\max}$ is the maximum <optical path difference> measured from equal arms. It gives
$$
\boxed{d_{\max}\simeq\frac1{0.5\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}}=2\,\mathrm{cm},\qquad
x_{\max}\simeq1\,\mathrm{cm}.}
$$
This is a nominal <spectral resolution element> convention.<FTIR Moving Mirror Travel Distance Calculation by Thermo Fisher Scientific>[optical path difference equals reciprocal resolution; mirror travel is half the optical path difference]{ref} A symmetric rectangular scan from $-d_{\max}$ to $d_{\max}$ instead has a sinc instrumental line shape with first-zero offset $1/(2d_{\max})$. Quoting that first-zero criterion would give $d_{\max}=1$ cm; <interferogram apodization> and other linewidth criteria change the numerical factor. An exact mathematical minimum requires such a criterion.

In the measurement chain, the <Michelson interferometer> is the spectral analyzer or modulator, between the collection optics and the <photodetector>. It encodes the incoming <radiation spectrum> into an <interferogram>; detector electronics digitize that signal and a <Fourier transform> reconstructs the <radiation spectrum>. It does not itself turn <radiant power> into an electrical signal.

Let the two returning monochromatic fields at the chosen detector port be
$$
E_1=a\cos(\omega t),\qquad E_2=a\cos(\omega t+\varphi),\qquad
\varphi=2\pi\nu\Delta d.
$$
Here $\nu$ is <wavenumber>, rather than temporal <frequency>. An optical-cycle average uses $\langle\cos^2\omega t\rangle=1/2$ and $\langle\cos\omega t\cos(\omega t+\varphi)\rangle=\cos\varphi/2$. In units absorbing the detector's field-to-power conversion,
$$
\boxed{P=\langle(E_1+E_2)^2\rangle=a^2[1+\cos(2\pi\nu\Delta d)].}
$$
The choice of output port can reverse the cosine sign. For mutually incoherent components of the <radiation spectrum>, add powers rather than instantaneous fields, giving
$$
P(\Delta d)=\int_0^\infty Q(\nu)[1+\cos(2\pi\nu\Delta d)]\,d\nu.
$$
Subtracting the constant term leaves the <Fourier cosine transform> of the detected spectral weighting $Q(\nu)$, including throughput and detector response.

The printed prefactor requires a normalization convention. In the derivation, $a$ is the amplitude of each returning field at the detector, not an entrance amplitude with input power $a^2$. For a lossless balanced <beam splitter> and literal input power $P_{\rm in}$, <conservation of energy> instead gives $P_{\rm out}=P_{\rm in}[1+\cos\varphi]/2$, so the source formula identifies $a^2=P_{\rm in}/2$. Taking both $P_{\rm in}=a^2$ and the printed formula literally would give $P_{\rm out}=2P_{\rm in}$ at zero <optical path difference>, a concrete normalization contradiction.

Ignoring <refraction>, the line of sight is perpendicular to the radius at its tangent point. For a surface tangent, <Pythagorean theorem> gives the satellite-to-tangent distance
$$
\boxed{D=\sqrt{(R+H)^2-R^2}
=\sqrt{7171^2-6371^2}\,\mathrm{km}
=3291\,\mathrm{km}\simeq3300\,\mathrm{km}.}
$$
For tangent altitude $z_t$, replace $R^2$ by $(R+z_t)^2$; lower <atmospheric scale heights> change the estimate little. The required angular resolution is
$$
\theta\simeq\frac{8.5\,\mathrm{km}}{3291\,\mathrm{km}}
=2.58\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{rad}.
$$
Using the <Rayleigh criterion> for a circular telescope aperture, $\theta=1.22\lambda/d$. The longest <wavelength> imposes the strongest condition, so
$$
\boxed{d_{\rm diffraction}\gtrsim
\frac{1.22(20\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm m)}{2.58\times10^{-3}}
=9.45\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm m\simeq9.5\,\mathrm{mm}.}
$$
The corresponding requirement at $7\,\mu\mathrm m$ is $3.3$ mm. This is a <diffraction> limit, without allowance for scan sampling, aberrations or instrumental field of view.

Use one <spectral resolution element>, $\Delta\nu=0.5\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$, and assume comparable angular resolution horizontally and vertically, so the pixel <solid angle> is $\Omega\simeq\theta^2=6.67\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{sr}$. The question specifies only the vertical angle; without an assumption about horizontal field of view the diameter is not unique. Interpret throughput $\eta=0.5$ as dimensionless transmission. With primary collecting area $A_p=\pi d^2/4$, the power in that element is
$$
P_s=\eta B_\nu\Delta\nu\,A_p\Omega
=1.30\times10^{-7}A_p\,\mathrm W,
$$
when $A_p$ is in square metres. There is no extra factor of $\pi$ multiplying the supplied <spectral radiance>.

For an integration time $t=1$ s, the order-of-magnitude <effective noise bandwidth> is $\Delta f\simeq1/t=1$ Hz. The root-mean-square <noise-equivalent power> is then $P_n={\rm NEP}\sqrt{\Delta f}=5\times10^{-11}$ W.<NEP – Noise Equivalent Power by Thorlabs>[p. 1, NEP Definition; p. 2, equations (2)–(3): minimum power and averaging time]{ref} A <signal-to-noise ratio> of 10 requires $P_s=5\times10^{-10}$ W, and therefore
$$
\boxed{A_p\simeq3.84\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^2},\qquad d\simeq7.0\,\mathrm{cm}.}
$$
An ideal rectangular average of a one-sided white <noise> spectral density has $\Delta f=1/(2t)$, giving $d\simeq5.9$ cm instead. A circular field with diameter $\theta$ changes the diameter by another order-unity factor. These convention choices all require an aperture substantially larger than the $9.5$ mm <diffraction> estimate, so signal collection is the stronger constraint. The estimate treats the quoted NEP as applicable to the reconstructed channel; a detailed <Fourier transform spectroscopy> design would also specify scan weighting, background and how interferogram noise propagates into that channel.

The constants $R_a,R_b,R_d$ are effective reservoir capacities: stored <carbon dioxide> <mass> anomaly per unit effective concentration anomaly, in GtCO$_2$ per ppmv. Only $C_a$ is a literal atmospheric mole fraction; $C_b,C_d$ are scaled reservoir variables chosen to share its equilibrium value. The exchange coefficients $r,s$ are conductances, in GtCO$_2$ per ppmv per year: $r(C_a-C_b)$ is transfer from the <atmosphere> to the fast reservoir and $s(C_b-C_d)$ transfer from that reservoir to the deep <ocean>. They are not by themselves inverse times; division by a reservoir capacity gives an inverse-time rate.

<Hydrostatic balance> estimates total atmospheric <mass> as $M_{\rm air}=4\pi R_E^2\bar p/g$. One ppmv of a dilute <ideal gas> is a mole fraction $10^{-6}$, so, with dry-air <molar mass> $M_{\rm mol,air}\simeq28.97$ g/mol and <carbon dioxide> <molar mass> $44.01$ g/mol,
$$
R_a=\frac{4\pi(6.371\times10^6)^2(98500)}{9.81}
\frac{44.01}{28.97}\frac{10^{-6}}{10^{12}}
\simeq\boxed{7.8\,\mathrm{GtCO_2/ppmv}.}
$$
The final factor converts kilograms to gigatonnes. This is <carbon dioxide> <mass>, not the smaller <carbon> <mass>.

For the <linear three-reservoir carbon model>, with concentrations as a column <vector>, the linear <ordinary differential equation> is
$$
\boxed{\dot{\mathbf x}=J\mathbf x+\mathbf f,\qquad
J=\begin{pmatrix}
-r/R_a&r/R_a&0\\
r/R_b&-(r+s)/R_b&s/R_b\\
0&s/R_d&-s/R_d
\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf f=\begin{pmatrix}E/R_a\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
A homogeneous <normal mode> has $\mathbf x=\mathbf v e^{\lambda t}$, where $J\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v$. For negative real <eigenvalues> the e-folding relaxation time is $\tau=-1/\lambda$. The row sums vanish, so $\mathbf v_3=(1,1,1)^T$ has $\lambda_3=0$ and
$$
\boxed{\tau_3=\infty.}
$$
Equal effective concentrations generate no exchange. Summing the reservoir budgets gives
$$
\frac{d}{dt}(R_aC_a+R_bC_b+R_dC_d)=E.
$$
Thus injected <carbon dioxide> is conserved after emissions cease: the model redistributes it but contains no permanent removal. The zero <normal mode> records this conserved excess <mass>, rather than a long but finite decay.

To check the other <normal modes> are damped, set $D=\operatorname{diag}(R_a,R_b,R_d)$ and $L=-DJ$. Then
$$
\mathbf z^TL\mathbf z=r(z_a-z_b)^2+s(z_b-z_d)^2\geq0.
$$
The <similarity transformation> $D^{1/2}JD^{-1/2}=-D^{-1/2}LD^{-1/2}$ is a symmetric negative-semidefinite <matrix> with a one-dimensional <kernel>, so the remaining two <eigenvalues> are strictly negative.

Define the two concentration differences $u=C_a-C_b$ and $v=C_b-C_d$. For zero emissions and $R_a=R_b=R$, their <ordinary differential equation> is
$$
\frac d{dt}\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}-2r/R&s/R\\r/R&-s(1/R+1/R_d)\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Writing the positive decay rates as $\mu_i=1/\tau_i$, their characteristic equation is
$$
\mu^2-P\mu+Q=0,\qquad
P=\frac{2r+s}{R}+\frac s{R_d},\qquad
Q=\frac{rs(R_d+2R)}{R^2R_d}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\tau_1=\frac2{P+\sqrt{P^2-4Q}},\qquad
\tau_2=\frac2{P-\sqrt{P^2-4Q}}.}
$$
For $r\gg s$, the first <normal mode> rapidly equalizes the fast reservoirs, giving $\mu_1\simeq2r/R$. The slower evolution treats them as a combined capacity $2R$ exchanging with $R_d$, giving $\mu_2\simeq s[1/(2R)+1/R_d]$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\tau_1\simeq\frac R{2r},\qquad
\tau_2\simeq\frac{2RR_d}{s(R_d+2R)}\simeq\frac{2R}s\quad(R_d\gg R).}
$$
The timescale ratio is $\tau_2/\tau_1\simeq4r/s\gg1$ in the joint limit. Since the conserved <normal mode> has $\tau_3=\infty$, the hierarchy is $\tau_1\ll\tau_2\ll\tau_3$.

Here $R=7.8$, $R_d=78$, $r=0.4$, and $s=0.04$, in the stated reservoir units. The exact characteristic coefficients are $P=0.10820513\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ and $Q=0.000315582\,\mathrm{yr}^{-2}$, giving

\boxed{\tau_1=9.51\,\mathrm{yr},\qquad\tau_2=333.4\,\mathrm{yr},\qquad\tau_3=\infty.}

The leading separation estimates give $9.75$ yr and $325$ yr if finite $R_d$ is retained, or $390$ yr if $R_d/R$ is also sent to infinity. Thus $\tau_1\ll100\,\mathrm{yr}<\tau_2$; a ratio of ten warrants using the exact values for the requested sketch.

Assume initially zero anomalies in all reservoirs. The atmospheric impulse response, obtained by decomposing $e^{Jt}$ into the three <normal modes>, is
$$
G(t)=w_0+w_1e^{-t/\tau_1}+w_2e^{-t/\tau_2},\qquad t\geq0,
$$
with $w_0=1/93.6=0.01068376$, $w_1=0.06087812$, and $w_2=0.05664324$, in ppmv/GtCO$_2$. The checks $w_0+w_1+w_2=1/R_a$ and $w_1/\tau_1+w_2/\tau_2=r/R_a^2$ fix the instantaneous injection response and its initial decay.

The concentration is the <convolution> $C_a(t)=\int_0^tG(t-t')E(t')\,dt'$. To evaluate it transparently, define $H_0(q)=q^2/2$ for $q>0$, and
$$
H_i(q)=\tau_iq-\tau_i^2(1-e^{-q/\tau_i}),\qquad q>0,\quad i=1,2,
$$
setting every $H_i(q)=0$ when $q\leq0$. Expressing the triangular emission history as $E(t)=0.5[t_+-2(t-100)_++(t-200)_+]$ gives
$$
C_a(t)=\frac12\sum_{i=0}^2w_i[H_i(t)-2H_i(t-100)+H_i(t-200)].
$$
Here times are in years and $0.5$ has the corresponding emission-ramp units. Differentiating or evaluating the <convolution> gives
$$
\boxed{\max\dot C_a\simeq3.27\,\mathrm{ppmv/yr}\ \text{at year }100,\qquad
C_a(200)\simeq267.6\,\mathrm{ppmv},\qquad C_a(400)\simeq169.4\,\mathrm{ppmv}.}
$$
The maximum concentration occurs earlier than the end of emissions, at approximately year $172$, with $C_a\simeq280.7$ ppmv. Initially there is a short adjustment over $\tau_1$; the curve then rises, turns over as emissions fall, and continues declining after year 200 as the deep <ocean> takes up <carbon dioxide>.

The total emitted <mass> is the area of the emission triangle, $\frac12(200)(50)=5000$ GtCO$_2$. All reservoirs eventually share one concentration, so <conservation of mass> gives
$$
\boxed{C_a(\infty)=\frac{5000}{7.8+7.8+78}=53.4\,\mathrm{ppmv}.}
$$

Use a shallow, homogeneous active upper layer of mean thickness $h_0$ and <mass density> $\rho_1$, over a much deeper, effectively stationary layer with <mass density> $\rho_2>\rho_1$. The <Boussinesq approximation> keeps the small density contrast only in <buoyancy>, giving <reduced gravity> $g'=g(\rho_2-\rho_1)/\rho_0$. The <hydrostatic approximation> and <shallow-water approximation> make horizontal <velocity> nearly depth independent and turn the interface slope into the horizontal force $-g'\nabla h$.

Linearize about rest and uniform $h_0$, with $|h-h_0|\ll h_0$, neglecting products of perturbations and <convective acceleration>. The <f-plane> approximation sets the <Coriolis parameter> to constant $f_0$. Neglect wind forcing, <viscosity>, entrainment and bottom drag. The two horizontal <momentum> equations then balance local <acceleration>, the <Coriolis force> and the <pressure gradient>. The signs $-f_0v$ and $+f_0u$ follow from eastward and northward coordinates.

The thickness equation is the linearized <conservation of mass> law: horizontal <divergence> decreases $h$, while horizontal convergence increases it. Here $h$ is active-layer thickness, not total ocean depth, and $g'$ rather than $g$ gives the restoring force because the motion principally displaces the <thermocline>.

Put $\eta=h-h_0$ and $c=\sqrt{g'h_0}$. With $u=0$ and complex dependence $e^{i(\omega_Kt-\ell_Ky)}$, the northward <momentum> equation and <continuity equation> give
$$
\omega_Kv=\ell_Kg'\eta,\qquad
\omega_K\eta=\ell_Kh_0v.
$$
Thus $\omega_K^2=c^2\ell_K^2$. Write the signed <phase velocity> as $c_y=\omega_K/\ell_K=\pm c$, so $v=(c_y/h_0)\eta$. The eastward <momentum> equation is the transverse <geostrophic balance> $g'\eta_x=f_0v$. Hence
$$
\boxed{A(x)=A_b\exp\left[\frac{f_0}{c_y}(x-x_b)\right],\qquad
\frac{\omega_K}{\ell_K}=c_y=\pm\sqrt{g'h_0}.}
$$
The decaying sign is selected by which side of $x_b$ contains the <ocean>. For an eastern boundary the <ocean> occupies $x<x_b$, so decay offshore requires $f_0/c_y>0$. Consequently the <Kelvin wave> moves northwards in the northern hemisphere and southwards in the southern hemisphere, with the coast respectively on its right and left. The positive offshore decay length is
$$
\boxed{L_d=\frac{\sqrt{g'h_0}}{|f_0|}.}
$$
The sign of $f_0$ reverses the propagation direction and the sign of $v$ relative to $\eta$, but not the speed or offshore decay length. The real physical fields are the real parts of the complex solution. At the equator itself this local <f-plane> coastal solution loses its finite trapping scale.

Use Earth's rotation rate $\Omega=7.292\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ and the <Coriolis parameter> $f=2\Omega\sin\phi$. At $|\phi|=10^\circ$,

|f|=2.53\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},\qquad
\boxed{c=|f|L_d\simeq2.53\,\mathrm{m\,s}^{-1}.}

The <Kelvin waves> propagate polewards along the eastern boundary: northwards at $10^\circ$ N and southwards at $10^\circ$ S. The positive <Rossby deformation radius> uses $|f|$; the source's expression $c/f$ must be interpreted as a magnitude in the southern hemisphere.

The east-west e-folding distance is approximately $100$ km, or
$$
\Delta\lambda\simeq\frac{100\,\mathrm{km}}{R_E\cos10^\circ}
=0.0159\,\mathrm{rad}\simeq0.91^\circ
$$
of longitude. With the coast at $x=0$ and <ocean> at $x<0$, a northern-hemisphere snapshot can be written
$$
\eta=\eta_0e^{x/L_d}\cos[\ell_K(y-ct)],\qquad
u=0,\qquad v=\frac c{h_0}\eta.
$$
At a positive thickness crest, the <thermocline> is depressed and the alongshore <velocity> points polewards; a negative thickness trough reverses that <velocity>. In the southern hemisphere replace $c$ by $-c$ in the propagation and in $v/\eta$. The envelopes of both thickness and alongshore <velocity> decay offshore, and the cross-shore <velocity> vanishes.

<Coastal Kelvin-wave radiation of long Rossby waves> requires a common <phase> convention to match the two <waves>. The supplied <Rossby wave> formula conventionally multiplies $e^{i(k_Rx+\ell_Ry-\omega_Rt)}$, while the coastal expression uses $e^{i(\omega_Kt-\ell_Ky)}$. Since physical fields are real, matching may use the complex-conjugate representative; in either convention the boundary forcing fixes the absolute <frequency> and alongshore <wavenumber>. Thus $|\omega_R|=|\omega_K|$ and $|\ell_R|=|\ell_K|$.

For $k_R^2+\ell_R^2\ll L_d^{-2}$, the <shallow-water Rossby-wave dispersion relation> reduces to
$$
\omega_R\simeq-\beta L_d^2k_R,
\qquad
\boxed{|\omega_K|\simeq\beta|k_R|L_d^2
=\frac{\beta|k_R|g'h_0}{f_0^2}.}
$$
Choosing $k_R>0$ recovers the printed magnitude relation. Both the zonal <phase velocity> and zonal <group velocity> are approximately $-\beta L_d^2$, so the long <Rossby waves> carry the coastal signal westwards into the basin.

The coastal condition $|\ell_K|L_d\ll1$ alone does not guarantee a long zonal <wavelength>. Since $|\omega_K|=c|\ell_K|$, matching requires $|k_R|=c|\ell_K|/(\beta L_d^2)$ and hence the stronger consistency condition
$$
|\ell_K|\ll\frac{\beta L_d}{c},\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad |\omega_K|\ll\beta L_d.
$$
For example, at $10^\circ$ a coastal <wavenumber> with $|\ell_K|L_d=0.05$ gives $|k_R|L_d\simeq0.56$, which is not asymptotically small even though the meridional <wavelength> is long. Thus the source's proposed excitation is possible at sufficiently low <frequency>; it is not an automatic consequence of its meridional long-wave assumption.

At $10^\circ$ N or S the <beta plane> parameter is
$$
\beta=\frac{2\Omega\cos10^\circ}{R_E}
=2.25\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1},\qquad
\beta L_d^2\simeq0.225\,\mathrm{m\,s}^{-1}.
$$
Taking a representative Pacific basin width $W\simeq1.5\times10^7$ m gives
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm cross}\simeq\frac W{\beta L_d^2}
=6.65\times10^7\,\mathrm s\simeq2.1\,\mathrm{yr}.}
$$
A width of $1.4$–$1.7\times10^7$ m gives roughly $2.0$–$2.4$ years. This is propagation near $10^\circ$ latitude, not an equatorial $f=0$ calculation. Slow <Rossby wave> adjustment supplies a basin-scale delay relevant to <El Niño-Southern Oscillation>: changing <thermocline> depth can affect later <sea surface temperature> and coupled wind forcing. Reflection and coupling may help produce interannual memory, but this travel-time estimate alone does not establish a climate oscillation or its period.
