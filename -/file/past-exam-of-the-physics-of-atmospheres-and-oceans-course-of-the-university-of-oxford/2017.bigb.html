= Solution
{parent=a}

Use $
u$ as a spectroscopic <wavenumber>, in the same units throughout. Write the mass <absorption coefficient> of the absorbing species as
$$
\boxed{\kappa_\nu=S\phi(\nu-\nu_0),\qquad
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(\delta\nu)\,d\delta\nu=1,\qquad
S=\int\kappa_\nu\,d\nu.}
$$
Here $S$ is the <spectral line strength>, the integrated mass <absorption coefficient>; it is not the line-centre coefficient. If $\kappa_\nu$ has units $\mathrm{m^2\,kg^{-1}}$ and $\nu$ is in $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, then $S$ has units $\mathrm{m^2\,kg^{-1}\,cm^{-1}}$. For absorber <mass density> $\rho_a$, the volume coefficient in <Beer-Lambert law> is $\rho_a\kappa_\nu$.

**<Lifetime broadening>.** Finite lifetimes of the upper and lower states destroy optical coherence, even for an isolated molecule. Exponential coherence decay gives a <Lorentzian line shape>. For population decay rates $\Gamma_u,\Gamma_l$ and no extra dephasing, its <wavenumber> half-width is $(\Gamma_u+\Gamma_l)/(4\pi c)$, with $c$ expressed in the length unit reciprocal to $\nu$. This sets a natural linewidth and can matter at extremely low collision rates; it is generally much smaller than atmospheric collisional or thermal widths.

**<Thermal Doppler broadening>.** A <Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution> of molecular line-of-sight velocities gives
$$
\phi_D(\delta\nu)=\frac{e^{-(\delta\nu/\Delta_D)^2}}{\sqrt\pi\Delta_D},\qquad
\Delta_D=\frac{\nu_0}{c}\sqrt{\frac{2k_BT}{m_a}}.
$$
This <Gaussian distribution> has a $1/e$ half-width $\Delta_D$, determined by absorber <molecular mass> $m_a$ and <temperature>. It is especially relevant in the rarefied upper atmosphere, where collisions are infrequent.

**<Pressure broadening>.** Collisions perturb transition <Frequency>[frequencies] and phases. In the impact regime, exponential phase decorrelation gives
$$
\phi_L(\delta\nu)=\frac1\pi\frac{\Delta_L}{\delta\nu^2+\Delta_L^2}.
$$
Here $\Delta_L$ is the half-width at half maximum, proportional to the coherence-destroying collision rate. It depends on perturber <density>, molecular collision properties, and <temperature>; it is often parameterized by $\Delta_L\propto p(T_*/T)^{n_T}$ for a specified broadener. It dominates much of the dense lower atmosphere. The <Lorentzian line shape> has algebraic $\delta\nu^{-2}$ wings, whereas the <Thermal Doppler broadening>[Doppler profile] has rapidly decreasing <Gaussian distribution>[Gaussian] wings. Where both mechanisms matter, their convolution is a <Voigt profile>. A pure pressure-broadened profile should not be extrapolated to arbitrarily low <pressure> while discarding the finite Doppler core.

= Solution

Let $m$ be the absorber <mass path> along a ray. If the <spectral line profile> is independent of position, <Beer-Lambert law> gives $\mathfrak T_\nu(m)=e^{-m\kappa_\nu}$. A finite-band mean and the line's <equivalent width> are respectively
$$
\boxed{\overline{\mathfrak T}_{\mathcal B}(m)=\frac1{\Delta\nu_{\mathcal B}}\int_{\mathcal B}e^{-mS\phi(\nu)}\,d\nu,
\qquad E(m)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}[1-e^{-mS\phi(\nu)}],d\nu.}
$$
When the band contains all significant <absorption>, $\int_{\mathcal B}\mathfrak T_\nu d\nu\simeq\Delta\nu_{\mathcal B}-E$. An unqualified integral of <Transmittance>[transmission] over the entire infinite line domain diverges because <Transmittance>[transmission] tends to one far from <Spectral line>[line centre]; $E$ is the finite and physically useful line deficit.

This is <nonexponential band transmission>: each monochromatic <Transmittance>[transmission] is exponential, but their average generally is not. Differentiating with respect to <mass path> gives
$$
\overline{\mathfrak T}'=-\langle\kappa e^{-m\kappa}\rangle_{\mathcal B}<0,
\qquad\overline{\mathfrak T}''=\langle\kappa^2e^{-m\kappa}\rangle_{\mathcal B}>0.
$$
More decisively, define the <absorption coefficient> averaged over the transmitted spectrum by
$$
a_{\rm eff}(m)=-\frac{d\log\overline{\mathfrak T}}{dm}
=\frac{\langle\kappa e^{-m\kappa}\rangle}{\langle e^{-m\kappa}\rangle}.
$$
Then $\boxed{a_{\rm eff}'(m)=-\operatorname{Var}_{m}(\kappa)\le0}$. The core saturates first and the surviving radiation increasingly samples weakly absorbing wings. The effective attenuation per additional mass therefore decreases. <Jensen's inequality> also gives $\overline{\mathfrak T}\ge e^{-m\langle\kappa\rangle}$, with equality for a spectrally constant coefficient. Thus the band <Transmittance>[transmission] decays more slowly than the exponential with its initial mean attenuation.

The corresponding <curve of growth> has $E(m)\simeq mS$ for an optically weak line, and sublinear growth after saturation. For the <Thermal Doppler broadening>[Gaussian profile], with $\tau_0=mS/(\sqrt\pi\Delta_D)\gg1$, <absorption> extends to $|\delta\nu|\simeq\Delta_D\sqrt{\log\tau_0}$, so $E\sim2\Delta_D\sqrt{\log\tau_0}$. For a strongly saturated pure <Lorentzian line shape>[Lorentz profile] the wings instead give $E\sim2\sqrt{mS\Delta_L}$. These asymptotes apply while the line remains isolated and its important wings remain within the range where the assumed profile and nearly constant <Planck function> are appropriate. The integrated <absorption> is not an exponential either: $E'>0$ and $E''\le0$.

For the <single-line greenhouse deficit>, use <hydrostatic balance>, fixed <Pressure>[surface pressure] $p_s$, constant <gravity> $g$, and the <dry adiabat>
$$
T(p)=T_s(p/p_s)^\kappa,\qquad \kappa=R_b/c_{p,b}>0.
$$
The gas constant and <Specific heat capacity>[specific heat] refer to the background gas. Let $\mu_d=\cos\tilde\theta>0$ be the supplied effective ray cosine. The vertical <optical depth> and slant <transmittance> from level $p$ to space are
$$
\tau_\nu(p)=\frac qg\int_0^p\kappa_\nu(p',T(p'))\,dp',\qquad
\mathfrak T_\nu(p)=\exp[-\tau_\nu(p)/\mu_d].
$$
This permits pressure-dependent <Spectral line broadening>[line widths]. With <local thermodynamic equilibrium>, negligible infrared <scattering>, a black thermal surface, and no incident <thermal radiation> from space, the formal <radiative transfer> solution is
$$
I_\nu(0)=B_\nu(T_s)\mathfrak T_\nu(p_s)+
\int_0^{p_s}B_\nu(T(p))[-\partial_p\mathfrak T_\nu(p)]\,dp.
$$
The weights add to one. Subtract the transparent-surface <radiance> and put $B(p)=B_{\nu_0}(T(p))$, using the narrow-line approximation only in the line deficit. Define its pressure-dependent <equivalent width>
$$
E(p,q)=\int[1-\mathfrak T_\nu(p)]\,d\nu,\qquad
\partial_pE=\int\frac{q\kappa_\nu(p)}{g\mu_d}\mathfrak T_\nu(p)\,d\nu\ge0.
$$
Approximating each hemispheric flux by $\pi$ times the effective-ray <radiance> preserves the black-surface flux. The outgoing longwave flux is therefore
$$
F_{\rm out}=\sigma_{\rm SB}T_s^4-D(T_s,q),\qquad
\boxed{D=\pi\int_0^{p_s}[B(p_s)-B(p)]\,\partial_pE(p,q)\,dp.}
$$
Integration by parts gives the particularly transparent form
$$
\boxed{D=\pi\int_0^{p_s}E(p,q)\frac{dB(p)}{dp}\,dp\ge0.}
$$
Both boundary terms vanish: $E(0,q)=0$ and $B(p_s)-B(p_s)=0$. The <dry adiabat> and monotonic <Planck function> give $dB/dp>0$. Atmospheric emission replaces part of the warm surface radiation by emission from cooler levels, reducing emission to space for a specified $T_s$. The inequality is strict when <opacity> occurs over a nonzero range with <temperature> contrast; an <Isothermal process>[isothermal] absorbing layer at the surface <temperature> would not produce such a contrast.

For a spherical <planet> the mean absorbed instellation is $F_*=(1-\alpha)L_{\circledast}/4$. The required global <energy balance> is
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{\rm SB}T_s^4=\frac{(1-\alpha)L_{\circledast}}4+D(T_s,q).}
$$
Consequently every equilibrium of this model has $T_s\ge T_{\rm bare}=[(1-\alpha)L_{\circledast}/(4\sigma_{\rm SB})]^{1/4}$, with strict warming when $D>0$. At fixed <Temperature>[temperature profile], increasing $q$ increases $E$ and hence $D$. This argument does not assume that the greenhouse gas changes the stellar-band <albedo>, <Pressure>[surface pressure], or background thermodynamics; those are held fixed. For sufficiently broad or overlapping saturated wings the full frequency-dependent <Planck function> must replace the narrow-line approximation.

Take a weak line in the radiative sense: the integrated slant <optical depth> is small wherever <absorption> materially contributes, not merely a small greenhouse-gas <mass mixing ratio>. Normalize every local <spectral line profile> so that $\int\kappa_\nu(p)\,d\nu=S$, with the <Spectral line strength>[line strength] independent of <pressure> and, as stipulated, <temperature>. Keep a finite Doppler or natural core, or a <Voigt profile>, so that a pressure-only zero-width core at the top does not invalidate a uniform optical-depth expansion. The line must also be narrow enough to use $B_{\nu_0}$ across it.

To first order,
$$
E(p,q)\simeq\frac q{g\mu_d}\int_0^p\int\kappa_\nu(p')\,d\nu\,dp'
=\frac{qSp}{g\mu_d}.
$$
Thus the detailed normalized <Spectral line profile>[line shape] drops out, even if its width varies with <pressure>. Substituting in the deficit in <1/c> gives
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{\rm SB}T_s^4\simeq F_*+
\frac{\pi qSp_s}{g\mu_d}\left[B_{\nu_0}(T_s)-
\int_0^1B_{\nu_0}(T_s r^\kappa)\,dr\right].}
$$
The neglected term is second order when the relevant squared optical depths are integrable and uniformly small. A very narrow saturated line need not be weak simply because $q\ll1$. More generally the displayed first-order integrated limit follows from the finite integrated <opacity> even if an idealized <Spectral line>[line centre] makes a pointwise expansion nonuniform; a finite physical core is the straightforward sufficient assumption.

For an explicitly perturbative <temperature> estimate, evaluate the bracket at $T_{\rm bare}$:
$$
\boxed{T_s-T_{\rm bare}\simeq\frac{\pi qSp_s}{4g\mu_d\sigma_{\rm SB}T_{\rm bare}^3}
\left[B_{\nu_0}(T_{\rm bare})-\int_0^1B_{\nu_0}(T_{\rm bare}r^\kappa)\,dr\right]>0.}
$$
At fixed $p_s,g,q,S$ and radiative conditions, increasing $\kappa=R_b/c_{p,b}$ makes every level $0<r<1$ cooler relative to the surface, enlarging the bracket and the warming. Increasing $c_{p,b}$ at fixed $R_b$ has the opposite effect. The limit $\kappa\to0$ is <Isothermal process>[isothermal] and gives zero line-induced deficit. A change of background <molecular mass> alone need not change this leading result: for <Ideal gas>[ideal gases] with the same active <degrees of freedom>, $R_b$ and $c_{p,b}$ scale together and their ratio is unchanged. Comparing only the height-coordinate lapse rate $g/c_{p,b}$ would miss that the absorber column is naturally a pressure-coordinate quantity.

= Solution

Below <Lifting condensation level>[cloud base], an initially unsaturated closed parcel expands as ambient <pressure> falls. In the absence of heat exchange its <temperature> falls approximately at the <dry adiabatic lapse rate> $g/c_{p,d}$. Its vapour mixing ratio is conserved, but its saturation mixing ratio decreases strongly as it cools. Hence <relative humidity> rises until the <lifting condensation level> is reached. The <density> normally decreases through expansion; the environmental profile determines the parcel's <buoyancy> and thus whether ascent can continue.

Near saturation, slight <atmospheric supersaturation> permits suitable <cloud condensation nuclei> to activate. <Köhler theory> describes the competition between curvature, which raises equilibrium <Vapor pressure>[vapour pressure], and dissolved solute, which lowers it. Cooling generates <Atmospheric supersaturation>[supersaturation], while <condensation> onto the new <Cloud droplet>[droplets] consumes it. Their balance normally keeps in-cloud <relative humidity> close to, but not identically at, saturation. The activated <Number density>[droplet number] depends on the <aerosol> population and the maximum <Atmospheric supersaturation>[supersaturation] produced by the ascent.

Above <Lifting condensation level>[cloud base], <condensation> lowers the vapour content and raises the <liquid-water content>. The <latent heat> released partly offsets expansion cooling, so the <moist adiabat> has a smaller lapse-rate magnitude than the dry one. In a dilute approximation,
$$
\Gamma_m\simeq\frac g{c_{p,d}}
\frac{1+L_vq_s/(R_dT)}{1+L_v^2q_s/(c_{p,d}R_vT^2)}.
$$
A closed adiabatic parcel conserves total water $q_t=q_v+q_l$ and approximately its <Entropy>[moist entropy]; retaining condensate makes this a reversible-cloud idealization, rather than a precipitating pseudoadiabat. In a shallow <cloud>, the saturation-mixing-ratio decrease and slow <density> variation yield nearly constant <condensation> per height, so <liquid-water content> is approximately linear above base. Once <Cloud-droplet activation>[activation] is complete, assuming nearly fixed <Number density>[droplet number] per volume gives increasing <Cloud droplet>[droplet] size as condensate accumulates. <Fluid entrainment>[Entrainment], continued <Cloud-droplet activation>[activation], <collision-coalescence> and <atmospheric precipitation> can all violate that simplified description; they are excluded from the stated retrieval model.

Using the <adiabatic cloud droplet moments>, define $N=\int n(r)\,dr$ and the <Adiabatic cloud droplet moments>[mean volume radius] by $r_v^3=N^{-1}\int r^3n(r)\,dr$. Summing the liquid mass of spherical <Cloud droplet>[droplets] gives
$$
w=\frac{4\pi\rho_w}{3}\int r^3n(r)\,dr
=\frac{4\pi\rho_w}{3}Nr_v^3.
$$
Therefore, with the shallow-cloud <liquid-water content> $w=c_wh$,
$$
\boxed{r_v(h)=\left(\frac{3c_wh}{4\pi\rho_wN}\right)^{1/3}.}
$$
The growth is $h^{1/3}$ at fixed <Number density>[number concentration], and the radius scales as $N^{-1/3}$ at fixed <Liquid-water content>[water content]. The dimensions are consistent: $c_w$ has units $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-4}}$, so $c_wh/(\rho_wN)$ has units $\mathrm{m^3}$. The formula is a third-moment radius; a monodisperse distribution is not required. Fixed $N$ per volume is the shallow-cloud approximation, not exact conservation of number per parcel volume during an arbitrarily deep expansion.

= Solution

Let $r_s^2=N^{-1}\int r^2n(r)\,dr$. The <Cloud droplet effective radius>[effective droplet radius] and <Adiabatic cloud droplet moments>[shape factor] give $r_e=r_v^3/r_s^2$ and $k^{1/3}=r_v/r_e$, so $r_s^2=k^{1/3}r_v^2$. If the <Particle extinction coefficient>[extinction] efficiency is approximately independent of <Cloud droplet>[droplet] size over the distribution,
$$
\sigma_{\rm ext}=\pi Q_{\rm ext}Nr_s^2
=\pi Q_{\rm ext}k^{1/3}\left(\frac{3c_w}{4\pi\rho_w}\right)^{2/3}N^{1/3}h^{2/3}.
$$
For visible <Wavelength>[wavelengths] and <Cloud droplet>[droplets] large compared with the <wavelength>, <geometrical optics> makes $Q_{\rm ext}\simeq2$ a useful approximation. Retaining it as the specified constant, and taking $N,k,c_w$ constant over the <cloud>,
$$
\boxed{\tau_c=\int_0^H\sigma_{\rm ext}\,dh
=\frac35\pi Q_{\rm ext}k^{1/3}
\left(\frac{3c_w}{4\pi\rho_w}\right)^{2/3}N^{1/3}H^{5/3}.}
$$
The $3/5$ comes from integrating $h^{2/3}$; replacing the whole <cloud> by its cloud-top <Particle extinction coefficient>[extinction] would incorrectly omit it. The effective-radius relation treats the distribution shape through $k$, without assuming all <Cloud droplet>[droplets] have the same size. Liquid phase, negligible vertical variation in that <Adiabatic cloud droplet moments>[shape factor], and negligible departures from the adiabatic water profile are essential assumptions.

In the <adiabatic cloud droplet number retrieval>, the independently retrieved <liquid-water path> fixes the geometrical thickness in the adiabatic model:
$$
W=\int_0^Hw(h)\,dh=\tfrac12c_wH^2,
\qquad \boxed{H=(2W/c_w)^{1/2}.}
$$
Define $C_c=(3/5)\pi Q_{\rm ext}k^{1/3}[3c_w/(4\pi\rho_w)]^{2/3}$. Since $\tau_c=C_cN^{1/3}H^{5/3}$, eliminating $H$ gives
$$
\boxed{N=\left(\frac{\tau_c}{C_c}\right)^3
\left(\frac{c_w}{2W}\right)^{5/2}
=\frac{250\sqrt2}{243\pi Q_{\rm ext}^3k}\,
\rho_w^2c_w^{1/2}\frac{\tau_c^3}{W^{5/2}}.}
$$
Use SI units to obtain $N$ in $\mathrm{m^{-3}}$, then divide by $10^6$ for $\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. The sensitivity is substantial: $d\log N=3d\log\tau_c-(5/2)d\log W+(1/2)d\log c_w-d\log k-3d\log Q_{\rm ext}$. Thus a numerical retrieval additionally needs the adiabatic <condensation> coefficient, <Adiabatic cloud droplet moments>[shape factor] and <Particle extinction coefficient>[extinction] efficiency. A nonadiabatic water profile, mixed phase, horizontal inhomogeneity or a biased water-path retrieval can produce a large number-concentration bias.

Denote <aerosol optical depth> by $\tau_a$ to distinguish it from <Optical depth>[cloud optical depth]. Retrieve $N$ as in <2/d>, collocate it with $\tau_a$, and estimate an <Aerosol-cloud interaction>[aerosol-to-droplet susceptibility]
$$
s_a=\left.\frac{d\log N}{d\log\tau_a}\right|_{\text{matched meteorology and cloud-water conditions}}.
$$
A <regression> should control for <Liquid-water content>[water content], updraught and <cloud> regime; <aerosol optical depth> is not itself a direct measurement of activated nuclei. For an approximately constant $s_a$ over a factor two in <aerosol>, $N_2/N_1=2^{s_a}$.

Integrate the supplied <cloud albedo susceptibility> rather than treating a finite doubling as infinitesimal:
$$
d\log\frac A{1-A}=\tfrac13d\log N,
\qquad
\boxed{A_2=\frac{2^{s_a/3}A_1}{1-A_1+2^{s_a/3}A_1}.}
$$
For a small response, $\Delta A\simeq A_1(1-A_1)s_a\log2/3$. If $F_{\rm sw}$ is the appropriate mean incoming shortwave flux and $f_c$ the fraction of the scene occupied by these <Cloud>[clouds], the leading top-of-atmosphere perturbation, positive downward, is
$$
\boxed{\Delta F_{\rm TOA}\simeq-f_cF_{\rm sw}\,\mathcal T_{\rm above}^{\downarrow}\mathcal T_{\rm above}^{\uparrow}\,(A_2-A_1).}
$$
The two <Transmittance>[transmissions] account approximately for the atmosphere above the <cloud> and may be absorbed into an effective illuminating flux. In the simplest global transparent-overburden estimate $F_{\rm sw}=L_{\circledast}/4$. Positive $s_a$ gives increased reflection and negative <Radiative forcing>[shortwave forcing], the <Twomey effect>. Numerical forcing cannot be determined from the given susceptibility alone: it needs baseline <albedo>, <cloud> fraction, illumination and an empirically constrained $s_a$. Humidity swelling, <cloud> contamination of <aerosol> retrievals and common meteorological influences can create spurious correlations. Changes in <cloud> amount, <Liquid-water path>[water path] or lifetime are additional adjustments, not the fixed-water Twomey term derived here.

Put the rigid lid at $z=0$, the interface at $z=-h(x,y,t)$ and $\rho_1=\rho_0-\Delta\rho$. Assume <Hydrostatic equilibrium>[hydrostatic vertical balance] and a resting deep layer. Its <pressure> is $p_2=C-\rho_0gz$, with horizontally uniform $C$. <Pressure> continuity at the interface then fixes the upper-layer <pressure>:
$$
p_1=C+\rho_0gh-\rho_1g(z+h)
=C-\rho_1gz+\Delta\rho\,gh.
$$
Hence its <Gradient>[horizontal pressure gradient] at fixed $z$ is $\nabla_hp_1=\Delta\rho\,g\nabla_hh$, independent of depth. Taking the horizontal momentum equation and a vertically uniform upper-layer velocity gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}+f\mathbf k\times\mathbf u+g'\nabla_hh=0,
\qquad g'=g\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho_1}\simeq g\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho_0}.}
$$
The exact <density> denominator for upper-layer acceleration is $\rho_1$; the familiar $\rho_0$ denominator is the small-density-contrast <Boussinesq approximation>. The restoring <pressure> is weaker than for a free surface because only the <density> difference contributes.

The horizontal shallow-layer equations use the vertical component of planetary rotation: $f=2\Omega\sin\varphi$. Thus the source's <vector> notation is interpreted as the projected <vector> $f\mathbf k$ after the <traditional approximation>. Taking the full magnitude $2\Omega$ as a midlatitude <Coriolis parameter> would discard the <latitude> dependence. Together with <Volume conservation>[layer-volume conservation], the model has $\partial_th+\nabla_h\cdot(h\mathbf u)=0$.

= Solution

Both descriptions are depth-averaged <Hydrostatic equilibrium>[hydrostatic] shallow-layer models. In a one-layer free-surface <shallow water equations>, a homogeneous layer overlies a rigid bottom and its upper surface moves. Surface elevation changes give a restoring acceleration based on full gravity $g$, with external-wave speed $\sqrt{gH_0}$. <Barotropic mode>[Barotropic] coastal storm surges, long surface <Gravity wave>[gravity waves] or a basin seiche are examples.

In the rigid-lid <reduced gravity> model, the movable boundary is a <density> interface beneath the active upper layer, while the much deeper lower layer remains at rest. The internal interface mode instead has speed $\sqrt{g'h_0}$ and represents, for example, thermocline displacements and an upper-ocean <Baroclinic mode>[baroclinic eddy]. The mathematical shallow-water form is retained with $g$ replaced by $g'$ and with the appropriate interface-depth convention. A freely moving two-layer surface would support both external and internal modes; the rigid lid filters out the fast external mode, and the resting abyss removes its independent dynamics.

= Solution

Write $h=h_0+\eta$, with small interface displacement $\eta$ and horizontal variation scale $L$. The geostrophic pressure-Coriolis balance gives
$$
fU\sim g'\eta/L,\qquad U\sim\frac{g'\eta}{|f|L}.
$$
Subtracting the uniform reference state and the mass-conserving linear potential-energy term, the perturbation <energy> densities per unit horizontal area are
$$
\mathrm{KE}\simeq\tfrac12\rho_1h_0U^2,\qquad
\mathrm{PE}\simeq\tfrac12\rho_1g'\eta^2.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{\mathrm{KE}}{\mathrm{PE}}\sim
\frac{g'h_0}{f^2L^2}=\left(\frac{L_R}{L}\right)^2,
\qquad L_R=\frac{\sqrt{g'h_0}}{|f|}.}
$$
The internal <deformation radius> $L_R$ is the distance an internal <gravity wave> travels in an inverse-Coriolis time. Balanced broad disturbances $L\gg L_R$ mainly store available <potential energy>; balanced fine structures $L\ll L_R$ require relatively large <kinetic energy>. This scale result presupposes small <Rossby number> $U/(|f|L)$; reducing the length at fixed displacement eventually violates <Geostrophic flow>[geostrophy]. It does not say an initially resting small-scale height perturbation already has that large <kinetic energy>: such a perturbation undergoes <geostrophic adjustment>.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Let $K=(k^2+l^2)^{1/2}$ and $c=\sqrt{g'h_0}$. The three branches are
$$
\boxed{\omega=0,\qquad \omega_\pm=\pm\sqrt{f^2+c^2K^2}.}
$$
For nonzero $K$ and $f$, the zero-frequency mode is stationary <geostrophic flow>, with $f\mathbf k\times\mathbf u=-g'\nabla\eta$ and zero horizontal divergence. It carries the linear potential-vorticity disturbance. There is no Rossby-wave propagation on a constant-$f$ plane; a varying <Coriolis parameter> would be needed for that.

The other two branches are oppositely phased <inertia-gravity waves>. Their phase-speed magnitude and radial <group velocity> are $|\omega|/K=\sqrt{c^2+f^2/K^2}$ and $|\partial\omega/\partial K|=c^2K/\sqrt{f^2+c^2K^2}$. When $KL_R\gg1$, gravity dominates: $|\omega|\simeq cK$ and the waves are approximately nondispersive, with both speeds approaching $c$. When $KL_R\ll1$, rotation dominates: $|\omega|\simeq|f|+c^2K^2/(2|f|)$, the <frequency> approaches the <Coriolis parameter>[inertial frequency] and the <group velocity> tends to zero. The large long-wave <Phase velocity>[phase speed] is not a large signal speed.

Without rotation the propagating branches become nondispersive <Gravity wave>[gravity waves], $\omega=\pm cK$, and the stationary branch is a nondivergent vortical velocity disturbance rather than a finite-pressure <geostrophic balance>. At $K=0$, the modes reduce to constant interface height and spatially uniform <Inertial oscillation>[inertial oscillations]. The wave branches have zero linear potential-vorticity perturbation; it is the stationary branch that retains the initial <potential vorticity>.

A numerical scale estimate requires a reference ocean <density> and <latitude> not specified exactly. Taking $\rho_0\simeq1025\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, $g\simeq9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ and a representative midlatitude $|f|\simeq10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ gives
$$
g'\simeq9.6\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}},\qquad
c=\sqrt{g'h_0}\simeq2.2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\qquad
\boxed{L_R\simeq22\,\mathrm{km}.}
$$
For a small-amplitude interface displacement of scale $L=1\,\mathrm{km}\ll L_R$, gravity drives rapid horizontal acceleration and radiates most of the displacement in <Inertia-gravity wave>[inertia-gravity waves]. The gravity adjustment time is $L/c\sim8$ minutes, much shorter than $1/|f|\sim2.8$ hours. A weak balanced remnant remains in an unbounded inviscid adjustment problem; <energy> is carried away by waves, not dissipated merely by <geostrophic adjustment>.

For $L=100\,\mathrm{km}\gg L_R$, rotational adjustment produces a largely retained interface displacement with a <Geostrophic flow>[geostrophic current]. Wave emission and <Inertial oscillation>[inertial oscillations] accompany the adjustment, but the balanced structure is much more important. To quantify that distinction under an initially resting, linear single-Fourier-component assumption, potential-vorticity conservation gives
$$
\frac{\eta_{\rm balanced}}{\eta_{\rm initial}}
=\frac{1}{1+(KL_R)^2},\qquad K\sim1/L.
$$
This is about $0.002$ for the kilometre scale and $0.95$ for the hundred-kilometre scale. These fractions use $L$ as an inverse <wavenumber> scale, not a full <wavelength>, and are illustrative rather than unique predictions for an unspecified disturbance or bounded basin.

For a unit mass of <ideal gas>, the <first law of thermodynamics> in enthalpy form is $\delta Q=c_p\,dT-v\,dp$. A reversible adiabatic displacement has $\delta Q=0$, and the <ideal gas law> gives $v=RT/p$. Thus
$$
\frac{dT}{T}=\frac R{c_p}\frac{dp}{p},\qquad
\boxed{T(p)=T_0(p/p_0)^\gamma,\quad \gamma=R/c_p.}
$$
The atmospheric profile approaches this <dry adiabat> when <convection> efficiently mixes potential <temperature> and there is no <condensation> or other strong diabatic source. The first law by itself determines a parcel path, not every possible environmental <Temperature>[temperature profile]; the <Convection>[convective mixing] assumption is needed. <Hydrostatic equilibrium>[Hydrostatic balance] gives the corresponding height lapse rate $dT/dz=-g/c_p$.

For rigid linear molecules with translation and rotation active but vibration frozen, the molecular <Heat capacity>[heat capacities] are $C_V=(5/2)R_u$ and $C_P=(7/2)R_u$, hence $R/c_p=2/7$. <Earth> is dominated by the diatomic gases nitrogen and oxygen. <Mars> is dominated by carbon dioxide, which is triatomic but linear and has the same two <Equipartition theorem>[rotational degrees of freedom]. In the rigid-molecule approximation it therefore has the same ratio. The value is not exact for carbon dioxide when its bending vibrations contribute appreciably to <heat capacity>, and variable <Heat capacity>[heat capacities] give a nonconstant dry-adiabatic exponent. Here $\gamma$ denotes $R/c_p$, not the distinct adiabatic index $c_p/c_v$.

Assume a plane-parallel atmosphere in <local thermodynamic equilibrium>, negligible infrared <scattering>, a <Blackbody>[black surface], and surface <temperature> equal to the adjacent air <temperature>. For a specified ray, let $\mathfrak T$ be its <transmittance> from a level to space, increasing from $\mathfrak T_0$ at the surface to one at the top. The formal <radiative transfer> solution is
$$
L=B_0\mathfrak T_0+\int_{\mathfrak T_0}^{1}B\,d\mathfrak T.
$$
Using $\mathfrak T_0+\int_{\mathfrak T_0}^1d\mathfrak T=1$ yields
$$
\boxed{L=B_0+\int_{\mathfrak T_0}^1(B-B_0)\,d\mathfrak T.}
$$
This expresses <absorption> and re-emission together: <opacity> does not simply remove surface radiation without adding atmospheric radiation. An absorber close to a thermally coupled surface has nearly the same <Planck function> as that surface, so $B-B_0$ is small. Its emission nearly compensates the surface radiation it absorbs, giving weak <radiance> sensitivity to near-surface concentration. In an exactly <Atmosphere>[isothermal atmosphere] at surface <temperature>, $L=B_0$ for every <optical depth>, a concrete example of complete concentration ambiguity in this thermal measurement. Finite surface emissivity, reflection, <scattering> or a surface-air <temperature> discontinuity would require additional terms.

A <Hydrostatic equilibrium>[hydrostatic] <pressure> interval contains $dp/(gM)$ moles of background gas per area. Its absorbing column is $x(p)\,dp/(gM)$, so for a nadir ray
$$
d\tau=\frac{k_0x(p)}{p_0gM}\,p\,dp,
\qquad \mathfrak T(p)=e^{-\tau(p)}.
$$
The assumed Planck-temperature power and <dry adiabat> give $B(p)/B_0=[T(p)/T_0]^{3.5}=p/p_0$. To first order in <optical depth>, $-d\mathfrak T\simeq d\tau$, and the <radiance> solution becomes
$$
\boxed{L\simeq B_0+\frac{k_0B_0}{p_0gM}\int_0^{p_0}
 x(p)\left(\frac p{p_0}-1\right)p\,dp.}
$$
For a uniform mixing ratio this also evaluates to $L\simeq B_0[1-k_0xp_0/(6gM)]$. Its total <optical depth> is $\tau_0=k_0xp_0/(2gM)$, so the deficit is $B_0\tau_0/3$.

A pressure-resolved perturbation defines the <infrared absorber sensitivity kernel> by $\delta L=\int K_p(p)\delta x(p)\,dp$, with
$$
\boxed{K_p(p)=\frac{k_0B_0}{p_0gM}p\left(\frac p{p_0}-1\right).}
$$
It is zero at the top, negative in the interior, and zero again at the surface. The magnitude is largest where $dK_p/dp=0$, namely
$$
\boxed{p_{\rm strongest}=p_0/2,\qquad K_p(p_0/2)=-\frac{k_0B_0}{4gM}.}
$$
**Sign and coordinate qualification.** Literally the maximum of the signed kernel is zero at the endpoints; $p_0/2$ is its minimum and the maximum of its absolute value. The derivative with respect to a single vertically uniform $x$ is the integrated constant $-k_0B_0p_0/(6gM)$ and has no <pressure> location. For equal-width logarithmic <pressure> perturbations the kernel is instead $K_{\log p}=pK_p$ and its magnitude peaks at $2p_0/3$. The stated pressure-interval convention is therefore essential.

The printed phrase that <temperature> decreases with <pressure> reverses the actual stipulated relation: with positive exponent, <temperature> decreases as <pressure> decreases upward. Take an <Isothermal process>[isothermal] <Hydrostatic equilibrium>[hydrostatic] reference atmosphere, $\rho_0(z)=\rho_s e^{-z/H}$, and <Linearization>[linearize] about rest. The horizontal acceleration and pressure-gradient force retain the equatorial <Coriolis parameter> $f\simeq\beta y$, with $\beta=2\Omega/a$. The <traditional approximation> retains only its vertical component. Quadratic perturbation advection is neglected. The vertical acceleration is balanced by perturbation <buoyancy> and <Gradient>[pressure gradient] after removing the reference <Hydrostatic equilibrium>[hydrostatic balance].

The continuity constraint is the <anelastic approximation>, $\nabla\cdot(\rho_0\mathbf u)=0$, giving $u_x+v_y+w_z-w/H=0$. It retains stratification-driven expansion while filtering acoustic <density> fluctuations. It is therefore not the full compressible continuity equation. Adiabatic displacement in a stably stratified background gives $b_t+N^2w=0$, with $N$ the <Brunt-Väisälä frequency>; upward displacement develops restoring negative <buoyancy>.

**<Pressure> convention matters.** With the entropy-buoyancy equation just described, a consistent reduced-pressure form writes the <pressure> acceleration as $-\nabla\Phi$, where $\Phi=p'/\rho_0$. Its vertical term is $-\partial_z(p'/\rho_0)$, not simply $-\rho_0^{-1}p'_z$. The original source explicitly specifies variable $\rho_0(z)$ but prints the latter. Those differ by $p'/(\rho_0H)$, so the two cannot silently be equated. Parts <5/b> and <5/c> display the consequence and then solve the explicitly corrected reduced-pressure model that yields the requested wave equation. First check the printed equations literally. Set $v=0$ and use $\rho_0'/\rho_0=-1/H$. Differentiating continuity in time and using horizontal momentum gives
$$
p_{xx}=\rho_0(w_{zt}-w_t/H).
$$
Vertical momentum and <buoyancy> give $w_{tt}+N^2w=-p_{zt}/\rho_0$. Combining them therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\partial_t^2\left(w_{xx}+w_{zz}-\frac2H w_z+\frac1{H^2}w\right)+N^2w_{xx}=0,}
$$
which differs from the requested equation. The factor two arises when differentiating both $\rho_0$ and $w_z-w/H$. Keeping variable <density> in continuity while treating it as constant during that differentiation would be inconsistent.

For the intended <anelastic reduced-pressure Kelvin wave>, make the explicit correction $w_t-b+\Phi_z=0$, while $u_t+\Phi_x=0$ and $\Phi=p'/\rho_0$. Continuity then gives $\Phi_{xx}=w_{zt}-w_t/H$, and vertical momentum gives $w_{tt}+N^2w=-\Phi_{zt}$. Cross differentiation now produces
$$
\boxed{\partial_t^2\left(w_{xx}+w_{zz}-\frac1H w_z\right)+N^2w_{xx}=0.}
$$
This proves the advertised equation for the corrected model, without claiming it follows from the incompatible printed set.

A concrete counterexample makes the distinction testable. In nondimensional units $H=N=k=1$, take $m=0$, $\omega=2/\sqrt5$ and $w=e^{z/2}e^{i(x-\omega t)}$, multiplied by its <Equatorial Kelvin wave>[Gaussian meridional envelope]. This satisfies the last boxed wave equation, but substitution into the literal printed-system wave operator gives $-2w/5\ne0$. No choice of accompanying <pressure> and horizontal velocity can then make it a solution of all five printed equations. Conversely, the literal operator has vertical factors $e^{z/H+imz}$ and dispersion $\omega^2=N^2k^2/(k^2+m^2)$; its scaling is different from the requested half-scale-height factor.

Work with the explicitly corrected reduced-pressure system in <5/b>. Write $d=1/(2H)$ and use the complex wave amplitude, taking real parts at the end. The vertical factor satisfies $(\partial_z^2-H^{-1}\partial_z)w=-(m^2+d^2)w$, so the wave equation gives
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=\frac{N^2k^2}{k^2+m^2+d^2},\qquad
c=\frac\omega k=\frac{N}{\sqrt{k^2+m^2+d^2}}>0.}
$$
For a <Equatorial Kelvin wave>[Kelvin wave], continuity, zonal momentum and the <buoyancy> equation then determine all polarizations:
$$
\boxed{u=-\frac{m+id}{k}\,w,\qquad
\Phi=\frac\omega k u=-\frac{\omega(m+id)}{k^2}w,
\qquad p'=\rho_0\Phi,\qquad b=-i\frac{N^2}{\omega}w,\qquad v=0.}
$$
In particular $u,w,b,\Phi$ have amplitude proportional to $e^{z/(2H)}$, whereas <pressure> $p'$ has amplitude proportional to $e^{-z/(2H)}$. Directly substituting these expressions into vertical momentum gives $\Phi_z=-i\omega(m^2+d^2)w/k^2=i(\omega-N^2/\omega)w$, verifying both the sign and the <dispersion relation>.

Meridional momentum is $\beta yu+\Phi_y=0$. Since $\Phi=cu$, it integrates to a <Gaussian distribution>[Gaussian] $\exp[-\beta y^2/(2c)]$. Thus
$$
\boxed{L_D^2=\frac c\beta=\frac{N}{\beta\sqrt{k^2+m^2+1/(4H^2)}}.}
$$
The eastward intrinsic branch $c>0$ is selected by decay away from the equator; choosing $c<0$ would make the meridional amplitude grow without bound. Although the squared <dispersion relation> has both <frequency> signs, a meridionally trapped <Equatorial Kelvin wave>[Kelvin wave] has this one <Phase velocity>[phase-propagation] direction.

For **short vertical <Wavelength>[wavelengths]**, $m^2\gg k^2+d^2$, so $\omega^2\simeq N^2k^2/m^2$, $c\simeq N/|m|$ and $L_D^2\simeq N/(\beta|m|)$. The wave is a slowly oscillating, <Hydrostatic equilibrium>[hydrostatic] internal <gravity wave> with strong vertical variation and relatively narrow trapping. Here $u\simeq-(m/k)w$ and $p'/\rho_0\simeq-(\omega m/k^2)w$; the <buoyancy> expression above remains valid.

For **long vertical <Wavelength>[wavelengths]**, $|m|\ll\sqrt{k^2+d^2}$, the exact limiting speed is $c=N/\sqrt{k^2+d^2}$. To obtain the stated height-controlled limit also requires long horizontal <wavelength>, $|k|H\ll1$. With both $|m|H\ll1$ and $|k|H\ll1$,
$$
\boxed{\omega\simeq2NHk,\qquad L_D^2\simeq\frac{2NH}{\beta}.}
$$
The finite <Scale height>[density scale height] prevents the <Hydrostatic equilibrium>[hydrostatic] $1/|m|$ <Phase velocity>[phase speed] from diverging as $m\to0$. The polarizations tend to $u\simeq-iw/(2Hk)$, $\Phi\simeq-iNw/k$ and $b\simeq-iNw/(2Hk)$. Long vertical <wavelength> alone at fixed large $kH$ would not justify $c=2NH$. In the opposite horizontally short limit the <frequency> approaches $N$ instead. The growth $e^{z/(2H)}$ compensates decreasing reference <density> in wave-energy <density>; a radiating wave train is a local modal description, not a finite-total-energy eigenfunction over an infinite vertical domain.

Weather-forecast errors arise from uncertainty in the initial atmospheric state, including <Measurement error>[measurement noise], incomplete spatial coverage and imperfect <data assimilation>; from model error, including unresolved <Cloud>[clouds], <convection> and turbulence, approximate physical parameterizations and imperfect boundary forcing; and from <discretization>, <Numerical integration>[time stepping] and <Rounding error>[roundoff]. <Sensitive dependence on initial conditions> amplifies suitable small errors through nonlinear dynamics, limiting deterministic predictability even with a good model. It is an amplification mechanism rather than a separate observational noise source. <Ensemble forecasting>[Ensemble forecasts] sample some initial-state and model uncertainty, but their spread need not represent every systematic error.

= Solution

Expand the <vector field> about the reference trajectory:
$$
\dot{\delta\mathbf x}=\mathbf f(\mathbf x+\delta\mathbf x)-\mathbf f(\mathbf x)
=J(t)\delta\mathbf x+O(\|\delta\mathbf x\|^2),\qquad
J_{ij}=\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}.
$$
Over a sufficiently short interval, the <Linearization>[tangent-linear] <Fundamental matrix>[propagator] is
$$
\boxed{\delta\mathbf x(t+\delta t)=A\delta\mathbf x(t),\qquad
A=I+J\delta t+O(\delta t^2).}
$$
Consequently
$$
A^TA=I+(J+J^T)\delta t+O(\delta t^2).
$$
If $S=J+J^T$ has $S\mathbf e=\lambda\mathbf e$ and the perturbation is initially parallel to $\mathbf e$, its squared <Euclidean norm> obeys
$$
\boxed{\|\delta\mathbf x(t+\delta t)\|^2
=(1+\lambda\delta t)\|\delta\mathbf x(t)\|^2+O(\delta t^2\|\delta\mathbf x(t)\|^2).}
$$
Positive $\lambda$ means instantaneous norm-squared growth, negative $\lambda$ decay, and zero $\lambda$ no first-order change. The corresponding <norm> growth rate is $\lambda/2$, not $\lambda$. These are <Eigenvalue>[eigenvalues] of the <Symmetric matrix>[symmetric part] governing the chosen error metric, not generally the <Eigenvalue>[eigenvalues] of the nonsymmetric <Jacobian matrix> or the long-time <Lyapunov exponent>[Lyapunov exponents]. The direction and the reference <Jacobian matrix> evolve during a finite forecast.

For the <instantaneous Lorenz error growth at a zero-y crossing>, direct differentiation of the <Lorenz system> gives
$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}-\sigma&\sigma&0\\r-z&-1&-x\\y&x&-b\end{pmatrix}.
$$
At a crossing of $y=0$ its <Symmetric matrix>[symmetric part] is particularly simple:
$$
\boxed{S=J+J^T=\begin{pmatrix}-2\sigma&\sigma+r-z&0\\
\sigma+r-z&-2&0\\0&0&-2b\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Thus a perturbation initially in the $z$ direction has norm-squared rate $-2b$ and initially decays in <norm> at rate $b$. The $x$-dependent couplings between $y$ and $z$ are <Skew-symmetric matrix>[skew-symmetric] at the crossing and do not contribute to the instantaneous total <norm> derivative. They can rotate the perturbation later: a pure $\delta z$ immediately induces $\dot{\delta y}=-x\delta z$. It is therefore incorrect to claim that such an error remains an independently decaying $z$ mode along the complete trajectory.

For the $(x,y)$ block, the <trace> is $-2(\sigma+1)<0$ and the <determinant> is $4\sigma-(\sigma+r-z)^2$. A negative <determinant> gives one positive and one negative <eigenvalue>. Equivalently,
$$
\lambda_\pm=-(\sigma+1)\pm\sqrt{(\sigma-1)^2+(\sigma+r-z)^2},
\qquad \lambda_+>0\iff|\sigma+r-z|>2\sqrt\sigma.
$$
In particular the requested sufficient lower-height condition is
$$
\boxed{z<F(\sigma,r),\qquad F(\sigma,r)=\sigma+r-2\sqrt\sigma.}
$$
There is also a second growth range $z>\sigma+r+2\sqrt\sigma$; it must not be lost by removing the absolute value. At either endpoint the largest rate is zero. Between them both block <Eigenvalue>[eigenvalues] are negative. At a crossing in either growing range, some planar error directions have positive instantaneous exponential rate. This statement is local in time: a single instantaneous positive <eigenvalue> of $S$ does not prove sustained exponential error growth over an evolving Lorenz trajectory.

= Solution

If one retains the <Euler method>[Euler approximation] $A=I+J\delta t$, its exact <norm> <matrix> is
$$
A^TA=I+(J+J^T)\delta t+J^TJ\delta t^2.
$$
The additional term can mix the instantaneous <Symmetric matrix>[symmetric-part] <Eigenvector>[eigenvectors], so they no longer generally diagonalize finite-time error amplification. However, when $\delta t^2$ is material, a reliable physical <Fundamental matrix>[propagator] must itself be calculated beyond first-order Euler: a large <Euler method>[Euler-step] amplification can be a numerical artifact. For constant $J$, the exact map is $\Phi=e^{J\Delta t}$; for a changing reference trajectory it solves $\dot\Phi=J(t)\Phi$, $\Phi(0)=I$.

Use its <singular value decomposition>, $\Phi=U\Sigma V^T$. The initial orthonormal <Right singular vector>[right singular vectors] $\mathbf v_i$ diagonalize $\Phi^T\Phi$ and satisfy
$$
\boxed{\Phi\mathbf v_i=s_i\mathbf u_i,\qquad
\frac{\|\Phi\mathbf v_i\|^2}{\|\mathbf v_i\|^2}=s_i^2.}
$$
A <singular value> $s_i>1$ gives finite-time <norm> growth, $s_i<1$ decay, and the leading <right singular vector> is the initial perturbation with greatest amplification in the stated <norm>. The <left singular vector> describes the resulting final error pattern. <Non-normal matrix>[Non-normal] dynamics can give transient growth even if every <Jacobian matrix> <eigenvalue> has negative real part, so selecting observations from those <Eigenvalue>[eigenvalues] alone is insufficient. Over a genuinely short interval, $s_i^2=1+\lambda_i\delta t+O(\delta t^2)$, recovering the <Symmetric matrix>[symmetric-part] result. Initial observations should target the relevant growing right singular projections, with <Covariance matrix>[prior covariance] and observational precision included when they are not isotropic.
