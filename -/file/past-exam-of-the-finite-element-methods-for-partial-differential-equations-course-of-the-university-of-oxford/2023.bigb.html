Solutions to all three questions of the <finite element methods for partial differential equations course of the University of Oxford> Part C examination, sat on 6 June 2023. Source: https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/request_for_mathematics_examinat/response/3129786/attach/3/FOI%20202507%20740%20Data.pdf#page=325[original released PDF, bundle pages 325–328; paper pages 1–4]. The original numbering is retained; question statements are not reproduced.

All <function spaces> and <bilinear forms> below are real. Write $(p,q)_0=\int_\Omega pq$ for the <L2 inner product>, and $\|p\|_1^2=\|p\|_0^2+\|\nabla p\|_0^2$ for the first-order <Sobolev norm>; the notation applies componentwise to <vector fields>. We use the <Poincaré inequality> explicitly allowed by the paper. On an arbitrary unbounded <Lipschitz domain> that inequality would need an additional assumption. The convergence assertions use the usual full <conforming finite element spaces>, or <vector subspaces> containing the indicated <interpolants>; merely being a <vector subspace> of the stated <piecewise polynomial functions> is insufficient. Part 1(d) explains the intended meaning of positivity for its <nonsymmetric matrix>.

== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution
{c}

For a sufficiently regular solution, put $w=-\Delta u$, with $\Delta$ the <Laplacian>. Then $-\Delta u-w=0$ and $u-\Delta w=u+\Delta^2u=f$. The zero <Dirichlet boundary conditions> on $u$ and $\Delta u$ become zero <Dirichlet boundary conditions> on $u$ and $w$. Conversely, eliminating $w$ from these two equations recovers the fourth-order equation with the <biharmonic operator>. For the <weak solution> we will construct, these interior identities hold as <distributional identities>; zero <Sobolev traces> are imposed on $u$ and $w$, without assuming classical fourth <derivatives> of $u$.

Let $V=H_0^1(\Omega)\times H_0^1(\Omega)$, the <Cartesian product> of two <zero-boundary Sobolev spaces>, with <norm>
$$
\|(p,q)\|_V^2=\|p\|_1^2+\|q\|_1^2.
$$
This is a <Hilbert space>. Applying <integration by parts> separately to the two equations gives the <weak formulation>
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
(\nabla u,\nabla v)_0-(w,v)_0&=0&&\text{for every }v\in H_0^1(\Omega),\\
(u,z)_0+(\nabla w,\nabla z)_0&=(f,z)_0&&\text{for every }z\in H_0^1(\Omega).
\end{aligned}}
$$
Equivalently, for $U=(u,w)$ and $T=(v,z)$, define the <bilinear form> and <linear functional>
$$
\begin{aligned}
b((p,q),(v,z))&=(\nabla p,\nabla v)_0+(\nabla q,\nabla z)_0-(q,v)_0+(p,z)_0,\\
F(v,z)&=(f,z)_0.
\end{aligned}
$$
We seek $U\in V$ with $b(U,T)=F(T)$ for every $T\in V$.

The <skew-coupled elliptic system> has a useful <bounded bilinear form> estimate with constant one. Indeed, write the <integrand> as the <dot product> of $(\nabla p,\nabla q,-q,p)$ and $(\nabla v,\nabla z,v,z)$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives
$$
|b((p,q),(v,z))|\leq\|(p,q)\|_V\|(v,z)\|_V,
\qquad |F(v,z)|\leq\|f\|_0\|(v,z)\|_V.
$$
For equal arguments, the two zeroth-order coupling terms cancel. If $K$ is the constant in the supplied <Poincaré inequality>, then
$$
b((p,q),(p,q))=\|\nabla p\|_0^2+\|\nabla q\|_0^2
\geq\frac1{1+K}\|(p,q)\|_V^2.
$$
Thus $b$ is a <coercive bilinear form>, although it is not a <symmetric bilinear form>. The <Lax-Milgram theorem> applies and yields
$$
\boxed{\text{There is exactly one }(u,w)\in H_0^1(\Omega)\times H_0^1(\Omega)\text{ solving the weak system.}}
$$
In particular, $\|(u,w)\|_V\leq(1+K)\|f\|_0$. Testing with compactly supported smooth <test functions> identifies $-\Delta u=w$ and $-\Delta w=f-u$ as <distributional identities>. This gives the asserted relation to the original <boundary value problem> at the available regularity.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution
{c}

Take $V_h=\mathcal U_h\times\mathcal W_h\subset V$. The <Galerkin method> asks for $U_h=(u_h,w_h)\in V_h$ such that
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
(\nabla u_h,\nabla v_h)_0-(w_h,v_h)_0&=0&& (v_h\in\mathcal U_h),\\
(u_h,z_h)_0+(\nabla w_h,\nabla z_h)_0&=(f,z_h)_0&& (z_h\in\mathcal W_h).
\end{aligned}}
$$
Since a <finite-dimensional subspace is closed>, $V_h$ is itself a <Hilbert space>. The <bounded bilinear form> and <coercive bilinear form> estimates in part (a) restrict to $V_h$ with the same constants. The <Lax-Milgram theorem> therefore gives exactly one $U_h$ for every $h$.

Put $E=U-U_h=(e_u,e_w)$. Subtracting the continuous and discrete <weak formulations> gives <Galerkin orthogonality>:
$$
b(E,T_h)=0\qquad(T_h\in V_h).
$$
For any $Y_h=(v_h,z_h)\in V_h$, the difference $Y_h-U_h$ also belongs to $V_h$, so
$$
\begin{aligned}
\|\nabla e_u\|_0^2+\|\nabla e_w\|_0^2
&=b(E,E)\\
&=b(E,U-Y_h)+b(E,Y_h-U_h)\\
&=b(E,U-Y_h)\\
&\leq\|E\|_V\|U-Y_h\|_V.
\end{aligned}
$$
The last step uses the constant-one <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> estimate from part (a). Bounding the four terms separately by a sum would obscure the precise constant requested here.

The two <minimization problems> over $\mathcal U_h$ and $\mathcal W_h$ are independent. Each has a <best approximation> because its <vector subspace> is finite-dimensional. Consequently, if
$$
D_h^2=\min_{v_h\in\mathcal U_h}\|u-v_h\|_1^2+\min_{z_h\in\mathcal W_h}\|w-z_h\|_1^2,
$$
then the preceding inequality gives exactly
$$
\boxed{\|\nabla(u-u_h)\|_0^2+\|\nabla(w-w_h)\|_0^2\leq\|U-U_h\|_V D_h.}
$$
Both errors belong to the <zero-boundary Sobolev space>. Applying the <Poincaré inequality> componentwise now yields
$$
\frac1{1+K}\|U-U_h\|_V^2\leq\|U-U_h\|_V D_h.
$$
When $U=U_h$ the desired estimate is automatic; otherwise divide by the nonzero <norm>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\left(\|u-u_h\|_1^2+\|w-w_h\|_1^2\right)^{1/2}\leq(1+K)\left(\min_{v_h\in\mathcal U_h}\|u-v_h\|_1^2+\min_{z_h\in\mathcal W_h}\|w-z_h\|_1^2\right)^{1/2}.}
$$
The constant $C=1+K$ is independent of $h$. This is the <Céa lemma> estimate for this particular <skew-coupled elliptic system>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution
{c}

Use the continuous <piecewise linear> nodal <interpolants> $I_hu$ and $I_hw$ on the <shape-regular mesh>. Since $u,w\in H^2(\Omega)$ in two dimensions, the <Sobolev embedding theorem> supplies continuous representatives, so the nodal values are well-defined. Their zero <Sobolev traces> imply zero boundary nodal values. Hence $I_hu\in\mathcal U_h$ and $I_hw\in\mathcal W_h$ for the usual full <conforming finite element spaces>.

The permitted <finite element interpolation estimates> give constants $C_u,C_w$ determined by the <shape regularity> and $\Omega$, independent of $h$, such that
$$
\|u-I_hu\|_1\leq C_u h\|u\|_{H^2(\Omega)},\qquad
\|w-I_hw\|_1\leq C_w h\|w\|_{H^2(\Omega)}.
$$
Substitute these choices in the <best approximation> bound from part (b):
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\left(\|u-u_h\|_1^2+\|w-w_h\|_1^2\right)^{1/2}&\leq C_*h,\\
C_*&=(1+K)\left(C_u^2\|u\|_{H^2(\Omega)}^2+C_w^2\|w\|_{H^2(\Omega)}^2\right)^{1/2}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
For fixed $u,w$, this constant is independent of $h$. A <shape-regular mesh> suffices; no uniformity of the individual element sizes is needed, since $h$ is their maximum diameter.

The inclusion of these <interpolants> is an essential assumption. If the wording “consist of” were read as permitting arbitrary smaller <vector subspaces>, taking $\mathcal U_h=\mathcal W_h=\{0\}$ for every $h$ would prevent the claimed convergence for a nonzero solution. The full <conforming finite element space> interpretation is also needed for the dimension assertion in part (d).

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution
{c}

Let $x_1,\ldots,x_N$ be the interior nodes, and let $\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_N$ be the nodal <basis> of the common <conforming finite element space>, with $\phi_j(x_i)=\delta_{ij}$ and zero boundary values. These <piecewise-linear hat functions> give the expansions
$$
u_h=\sum_{j=1}^N U_j\phi_j,\qquad w_h=\sum_{j=1}^N W_j\phi_j.
$$
Testing the first equation with $\phi_i$ and then the second equation with $\phi_i$ gives the <linear system>
$$
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}S&-M\\M&S\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}U\\W\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\F\end{pmatrix},\qquad
S_{ij}=\int_\Omega\nabla\phi_j\cdot\nabla\phi_i,\quad
M_{ij}=\int_\Omega\phi_j\phi_i,\quad
F_i=\int_\Omega f\phi_i.}
$$
Here $S$ is the diffusion <stiffness matrix> and $M$ the <mass matrix>. Their entries are symmetric in $i,j$, so both are <symmetric matrices>.

For $c\in\mathbb R^N$, put $v_c=\sum_jc_j\phi_j$. The two <quadratic forms> are
$$
c^{\mathsf T}Mc=\|v_c\|_0^2,\qquad c^{\mathsf T}Sc=\|\nabla v_c\|_0^2.
$$
If $c\ne0$, the <linear independence> of the <basis> ensures $v_c\ne0$, so $c^{\mathsf T}Mc>0$. If $c^{\mathsf T}Sc=0$, the <Poincaré inequality> and zero <Dirichlet boundary condition> force $v_c=0$, contradicting $c\ne0$. Thus both $S$ and $M$ are <positive-definite matrices>.

For $X=(p,q)\in\mathbb R^N\times\mathbb R^N$, symmetry of $M$ cancels the cross terms:
$$
\begin{aligned}
X^{\mathsf T}AX
&=p^{\mathsf T}Sp-p^{\mathsf T}Mq+q^{\mathsf T}Mp+q^{\mathsf T}Sq\\
&=p^{\mathsf T}Sp+q^{\mathsf T}Sq.
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{X^{\mathsf T}AX>0\quad(X\ne0),\qquad \frac{A+A^{\mathsf T}}2=\begin{pmatrix}S&0\\0&S\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
This is the positivity of a <quadratic form> intended by the last sentence of the question. For $N>0$, $A$ is a <nonsymmetric matrix>, since $M\ne0$. Under the convention that “<positive-definite matrix>” includes symmetry, that last sentence needs this qualification: the <symmetric part of a matrix> is positive definite. The <quadratic lower bound for a nonsymmetric matrix> still implies that $A$ is an <invertible matrix>: $AX=0$ would give $X^{\mathsf T}AX=0$, hence $X=0$.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution
{c}

Label the <polygon vertices> of the nondegenerate <triangle> $K$ as $a_1,a_2,a_3$, and put $m_{ij}=(a_i+a_j)/2$ for its three edge <midpoints>. The six <monomials> $1,x_1,x_2,x_1^2,x_1x_2,x_2^2$ form a <basis> of $\mathcal P_2(K)$, so its <dimension> is six. Nondegeneracy matters: $K$ has nonempty interior, and a <polynomial> vanishing on that interior is the zero <polynomial>.

Let $\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3$ be the <affine functions> of the <barycentric coordinate system>, characterized by $\lambda_i(a_j)=\delta_{ij}$ and $\sum_i\lambda_i=1$. Define six <quadratic polynomials>
$$
N_i=\lambda_i(2\lambda_i-1)\quad(i=1,2,3),\qquad
N_{ij}=4\lambda_i\lambda_j\quad(1\leq i<j\leq3).
$$
At $a_k$, $N_i(a_k)=\delta_{ik}$ and all $N_{ij}(a_k)$ vanish. At $m_{kl}$, exactly $\lambda_k$ and $\lambda_l$ equal $1/2$, while the third is zero. Thus every $N_i$ vanishes at all three edge <midpoints>, and $N_{ij}(m_{kl})$ is one for the matching unordered pair and zero otherwise.

These six <polynomials> have the cardinal property for the six <point evaluation functionals>. Evaluating any vanishing <linear combination> at the nodes forces each of its <coefficients> to vanish. They are therefore six <linearly independent> members of a six-dimensional <vector space>, hence a <basis>.

In particular, every $p\in\mathcal P_2(K)$ has the unique expansion
$$
\boxed{p=\sum_{i=1}^3p(a_i)\lambda_i(2\lambda_i-1)+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq3}p(m_{ij})\,4\lambda_i\lambda_j.}
$$
This proves both existence and uniqueness for arbitrary six prescribed nodal values, which is precisely an <unisolvent finite element>. It also identifies the <finite element degrees of freedom> and shape functions of the <quadratic Lagrange triangle> explicitly.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution
{c}

Use the <Sobolev space with a partial Dirichlet condition>
$$
\boxed{V=\{v\in H^1(\Omega):Tv=0\text{ almost everywhere on }\Gamma_D\},}
$$
where $T$ is the <Sobolev trace operator>. The restricted <Sobolev trace operator> is continuous into $L^2(\Gamma_D)$; its <kernel> is consequently a <closed vector subspace> of $H^1(\Omega)$. Thus $V$, with the first-order <Sobolev norm>, is a <Hilbert space>. The endpoint convention for $\Gamma_D$ and $\Gamma_N$ does not affect this definition: finitely many boundary points have zero boundary length. It would be inappropriate to impose pointwise boundary values on arbitrary $H^1$ functions in two dimensions.

For a smooth solution, <Green's first identity> gives
$$
\int_\Omega(-\Delta u+u)v
=\int_\Omega(\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+uv)-\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_nu\,Tv.
$$
The boundary term vanishes because $Tv=0$ on $\Gamma_D$ and the <normal derivative> is zero on $\Gamma_N$. This suggests the <weak formulation>
$$
\boxed{u\in V,\qquad a(u,v)=\ell(v)\quad(v\in V),\qquad
a(p,v)=(\nabla p,\nabla v)_0+(p,v)_0,\quad\ell(v)=(f,v)_0.}
$$
Here $a$ is exactly the first-order <Sobolev inner product>. In particular the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives
$$
|a(p,v)|\leq\|p\|_1\|v\|_1,\qquad a(v,v)=\|v\|_1^2,\qquad
|\ell(v)|\leq\|f\|_0\|v\|_1.
$$
Thus $a$ is a <bounded bilinear form> and a <coercive bilinear form>, both with constant one. The <Lax-Milgram theorem> proves existence and uniqueness, together with
$$
\boxed{\|u\|_1\leq\|f\|_0.}
$$
The positive reaction term already controls the entire <Sobolev norm>, so no <Poincaré inequality> is needed here. The zero <Neumann boundary condition> is the natural part of the <weak formulation>; it is not an additional restriction on the test space. For this <weak solution>, $-\Delta u+u=f$ holds as a <distributional identity>, and the zero <normal derivative> on $\Gamma_N$ is understood through the corresponding <Green's first identity>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution
{c}

For a conforming triangular <finite element mesh>, take
$$
V_h=\{v_h\in C^0(\overline\Omega):v_h|_K\in\mathcal P_2(K)\text{ for every }K\in\mathcal M_h,\quad v_h|_{\Gamma_D}=0\}.
$$
This is the quadratic <conforming finite element space>. Across a common <edge of a polygon>, the restrictions from neighboring <triangles> are univariate <quadratic polynomials>. Their agreement at the two endpoints and the <midpoint> makes their difference a degree-at-most-two <polynomial> with three distinct <roots of a polynomial>, so it vanishes on the entire common <edge of a polygon>. Thus matching the local <finite element degrees of freedom> produces the required continuity. Similarly, setting the three nodal values on every boundary edge carrying the <Dirichlet boundary condition> to zero makes its entire restriction vanish.

Continuity across element interfaces means the piecewise <gradients> give the global <weak gradient>, with no jump contribution, so $V_h\subset V$. There are finitely many local <finite element degrees of freedom>, hence $V_h$ is finite-dimensional. The <Galerkin method> seeks
$$
\boxed{u_h\in V_h,\qquad (\nabla u_h,\nabla v_h)_0+(u_h,v_h)_0=(f,v_h)_0\quad(v_h\in V_h).}
$$
The <bounded bilinear form> and <coercive bilinear form> estimates from part (b) remain valid on $V_h$. Since a <finite-dimensional subspace is closed>, the <Lax-Milgram theorem> gives exactly one $u_h$. Alternatively, the <stiffness matrix> plus the <mass matrix> is a <positive-definite matrix> in any <basis> of $V_h$. The same existence and uniqueness conclusion holds for any finite-dimensional <vector subspace> of this full space.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution
{c}

Let $e=u-u_h$. Subtracting the two <weak formulations> gives <Galerkin orthogonality>, $a(e,v_h)=0$ for every $v_h\in V_h$. Since $a$ is the first-order <Sobolev inner product>, for every $v_h\in V_h$ we have
$$
\begin{aligned}
\|e\|_1^2
&=a(e,u-v_h)+a(e,v_h-u_h)\\
&=a(e,u-v_h)\leq\|e\|_1\|u-v_h\|_1.
\end{aligned}
$$
If $e\ne0$, division proves $\|e\|_1\leq\|u-v_h\|_1$; if $e=0$, the same conclusion is immediate. Thus the <Céa lemma> has constant one in this case.

Use the quadratic nodal <interpolant> $I_hu$. The assumed $H^3(\Omega)$ regularity gives continuous nodal values by the <Sobolev embedding theorem>, and its zero <Sobolev trace> makes the nodal values on $\Gamma_D$ zero. The <quadratic Lagrange triangle> construction then gives $I_hu\in V_h$. The allowed quadratic <finite element interpolation estimate> on a <shape-regular mesh> is
$$
\|u-I_hu\|_1\leq C_Ih^2\|u\|_{H^3(\Omega)},
$$
where $C_I$ depends on the fixed <triangle> and <shape regularity>, but not on $h$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\|u-u_h\|_{H^1(\Omega)}\leq\|u-I_hu\|_{H^1(\Omega)}\leq C_1h^2\|u\|_{H^3(\Omega)},\qquad C_1=C_I.}
$$
This step uses the inclusion $I_hu\in V_h$ explicitly permitted by the question. Arbitrary smaller <vector subspaces> without the same approximation property would not give this rate.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution
{c}

Put $e=u-u_h\in V$. The <Aubin–Nitsche duality argument> uses the dual <weak formulation>
$$
z\in V,\qquad a(v,z)=(e,v)_0\quad(v\in V).
$$
By symmetry of $a$ and the <Lax-Milgram theorem>, this has a unique solution. Taking $v=z$ and using the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> shows
$$
\|z\|_1^2=(e,z)_0\leq\|e\|_0\|z\|_0\leq\|e\|_0\|z\|_1,
\qquad \|z\|_1\leq\|e\|_0.
$$
We must justify $H^2$ <elliptic regularity> for this dual problem from the result actually supplied. That result concerns $\Delta$, whereas the dual equation is $-\Delta z+z=e$. Put $g=z-e\in L^2(\Omega)$. The given result produces $w\in H^2(\Omega)\cap V$ with $\Delta w=g$, zero <normal derivative> on $\Gamma_N$, and
$$
\|w\|_{H^2(\Omega)}\leq C_3\|z-e\|_0\leq2C_3\|e\|_0.
$$
By <Green's first identity>, for every $v\in V$,
$$
(\nabla w,\nabla v)_0=-(g,v)_0=(e-z,v)_0=(\nabla z,\nabla v)_0.
$$
Set $v=w-z$. Then $\nabla(w-z)=0$. On the connected <triangle>, a <Sobolev function with zero weak gradient> is constant, and its zero <Sobolev trace> on $\Gamma_D$ forces that constant to be zero. Hence $z=w$, proving
$$
\boxed{z\in H^2(\Omega)\cap V,\qquad\|z\|_{H^2(\Omega)}\leq2C_3\|e\|_0.}
$$
This proves the needed <reaction-diffusion regularity from Laplacian regularity> without assuming the dual solution already has second <derivatives> in the <L2 space>.

Only $H^2$ regularity is available for $z$. Use its continuous <piecewise linear> <interpolant> $L_hz$, which belongs to the quadratic <conforming finite element space> $V_h$ because $\mathcal P_1(K)\subset\mathcal P_2(K)$. It has the same zero <Dirichlet boundary condition> and satisfies the <finite element interpolation estimate>
$$
\|z-L_hz\|_1\leq C_Lh\|z\|_{H^2(\Omega)}.
$$
Thus no $H^3$ assumption on the dual solution is necessary. Taking $v=e$ in the dual <weak formulation> and using <Galerkin orthogonality> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\|e\|_0^2
&=a(e,z)=a(e,z-L_hz)\\
&\leq\|e\|_1\|z-L_hz\|_1\\
&\leq2C_3C_Lh\|e\|_1\|e\|_0.
\end{aligned}
$$
If $\|e\|_0=0$, the desired bound holds. Otherwise divide by $\|e\|_0$ and use part (d):
$$
\boxed{\|u-u_h\|_{L^2(\Omega)}\leq2C_3C_Lh\|u-u_h\|_{H^1(\Omega)}\leq C_2h^3\|u\|_{H^3(\Omega)},\qquad C_2=2C_3C_LC_1.}
$$
All constants depend only on the fixed <triangle> and <shape regularity>. The acute-angle assumption enters through the supplied mixed-boundary <elliptic regularity> result.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution
{c}

The <infinitesimal strain tensor> $\varepsilon(v)=\tfrac12(\nabla v+(\nabla v)^{\mathsf T})$ and the <divergence> are linear in $v$. Consequently the given $a$ is a <symmetric bilinear form>, and
$$
a(v,v)=2\mu\|\varepsilon(v)\|_0^2+\lambda\|\operatorname{div}v\|_0^2\geq0.
$$
For a matrix-valued <function>, $\|\cdot\|_0$ here uses the <Frobenius inner product> pointwise and then the <L2 norm>. All expressions are finite for $v\in H^1(\Omega;\mathbb R^3)$.

Expand the quadratic term at $\theta v+(1-\theta)w$. The <linearity> of $\ell$ cancels its contribution to the difference, giving
$$
\begin{aligned}
\theta J(v)+(1-\theta)J(w)-J(\theta v+(1-\theta)w)
&=\frac{\theta(1-\theta)}2\bigl(a(v,v)-2a(v,w)+a(w,w)\bigr)\\
&=\frac{\theta(1-\theta)}2a(v-w,v-w)\\
&=\theta(1-\theta)\left(\mu\|\varepsilon(v-w)\|_0^2+\frac\lambda2\|\operatorname{div}(v-w)\|_0^2\right).
\end{aligned}
$$
For $0\leq\theta\leq1$, this is nonnegative. Therefore
$$
\boxed{J(\theta v+(1-\theta)w)\leq\theta J(v)+(1-\theta)J(w),}
$$
so $J$ is a <convex functional> on the whole <Sobolev space>. No boundary condition is needed for this conclusion. The argument uses nonnegativity of the quadratic term; uniqueness on the <zero-boundary Sobolev space> will instead use the <Korn inequality> in part (b).

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution
{c}

Write $V=H_0^1(\Omega;\mathbb R^3)$. For any $v\in V$ and any real $t$, $u+tv$ is admissible. Expanding the functional gives
$$
J(u+tv)-J(u)=t\bigl(a(u,v)-\ell(v)\bigr)+\frac{t^2}2a(v,v).
$$
Since the left side is nonnegative for positive and negative $t$, its <derivative> at zero vanishes. Thus the <first variation> gives the <weak formulation>
$$
\boxed{u\in V,\qquad a(u,v)=\ell(v)\quad(v\in V).}
$$

To identify its differential equation, define the <stress tensor>
$$
\sigma(u)=2\mu\varepsilon(u)+\lambda(\operatorname{div}u)I.
$$
This is a <symmetric matrix> field. The <Frobenius inner product> of a <symmetric matrix> with a <skew-symmetric matrix> is zero, so $\sigma(u):\nabla v=\sigma(u):\varepsilon(v)$. Since $\operatorname{tr}\varepsilon(v)=\operatorname{div}v$, we obtain
$$
a(u,v)=\int_\Omega\sigma(u):\nabla v.
$$
For smooth compactly supported <test functions>, <integration by parts> in each row gives
$$
\int_\Omega\sigma(u):\nabla v=-\langle\operatorname{div}\sigma(u),v\rangle.
$$
Hence $-\operatorname{div}\sigma(u)=f$ as a <distributional identity>, that is,
$$
-2\mu\operatorname{div}\varepsilon(u)-\lambda\nabla(\operatorname{div}u)=f.
$$
Membership in $V$ supplies the zero <Sobolev trace>. This identifies precisely the required <weak solution> of the <linear elasticity> problem, with row-wise <divergence>.

For uniqueness, use the exact form of the <Korn inequality> supplied in the paper. Put $E=\varepsilon(v)$ and $d=\operatorname{tr}E=\operatorname{div}v$. The <deviatoric strain> is $D=E-\tfrac13dI$. Because $\operatorname{tr}D=0$ and $I:I=3$, <orthogonality> for the <Frobenius inner product> gives
$$
|E|_F^2=|D|_F^2+\frac13d^2,\qquad |D|_F^2\leq|E|_F^2.
$$
If $C_K$ is the supplied <Korn inequality> constant and $K$ the permitted <Poincaré inequality> constant, then
$$
\|v\|_1^2\leq(1+K)\|\nabla v\|_0^2
\leq(1+K)C_K\|D\|_0^2
\leq(1+K)C_K\|\varepsilon(v)\|_0^2.
$$
Consequently,
$$
\boxed{a(v,v)\geq\alpha\|v\|_1^2,\qquad\alpha=\frac{2\mu}{(1+K)C_K}>0.}
$$
If $u_1,u_2$ are two <weak solutions>, their difference $d_u\in V$ satisfies $a(d_u,v)=0$ for every $v\in V$. Choosing $v=d_u$ and using this <coercive bilinear form> estimate forces $d_u=0$. The minimizer therefore gives the unique <weak solution>.

For later use, $|\varepsilon(v)|_F\leq|\nabla v|_F$ and $|\operatorname{div}v|\leq\sqrt3|\nabla v|_F$ also give the <bounded bilinear form> and <linear functional> estimates
$$
|a(p,v)|\leq(2\mu+3\lambda)\|p\|_1\|v\|_1,\qquad
|\ell(v)|\leq\|f\|_0\|v\|_1.
$$
Together with the <coercive bilinear form> estimate and the <Lax-Milgram theorem>, these would establish existence independently of the assumed minimizer.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution
{c}

The finite-dimensional <vector subspace> $V_h\subset V$ is closed, so it is a <Hilbert space> with the inherited <Sobolev norm>. The <bounded bilinear form>, <coercive bilinear form>, and bounded <linear functional> estimates from part (b) restrict to $V_h$. Applying the <Lax-Milgram theorem> proves
$$
\boxed{\text{There is exactly one }u_h\in V_h\text{ with }a(u_h,v_h)=\ell(v_h)\quad(v_h\in V_h).}
$$

For any $v_h\in V_h$, put $d_h=v_h-u_h\in V_h$. Expansion using symmetry of the <bilinear form> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
J(v_h)-J(u_h)
&=a(u_h,d_h)-\ell(d_h)+\frac12a(d_h,d_h)\\
&=\frac12a(d_h,d_h)\geq0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus $u_h$ minimizes $J$ on $V_h$, uniquely by the <coercive bilinear form> estimate. Similarly, the continuous <weak formulation> gives the exact identity
$$
J(u_h)-J(u)=\frac12a(u_h-u,u_h-u)\geq0.
$$
Combining the two identities proves
$$
\boxed{J(v_h)\geq J(u_h)\geq J(u)\qquad(v_h\in V_h).}
$$
More precisely, $J(v_h)-J(u_h)=\tfrac12\|v_h-u_h\|_a^2$ and $J(u_h)-J(u)=\tfrac12\|u_h-u\|_a^2$, with the <energy norm> justified next. These identities connect the energy excess directly to the squared error.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution
{c}

On $V$, the <bilinear form> $a$ is symmetric, and part (b) proves $a(v,v)\geq\alpha\|v\|_1^2$. Thus $a$ is an <inner product>: $a(v,v)\geq0$, and equality forces $v=0$. Its induced <energy norm> is $\|v\|_a=\sqrt{a(v,v)}$. For any real scalar $t$,
$$
\|tv\|_a=|t|\|v\|_a.
$$
The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> for this <inner product> follows directly by expanding $a(p-tq,p-tq)\geq0$ and minimizing over $t$ when $q\ne0$. Hence
$$
\begin{aligned}
\|p+q\|_a^2
&=\|p\|_a^2+2a(p,q)+\|q\|_a^2\\
&\leq\bigl(\|p\|_a+\|q\|_a\bigr)^2,
\end{aligned}
$$
which proves the <triangle inequality>. This verifies every <norm> axiom. In fact, the estimates from part (b) give the <equivalent norms>
$$
\boxed{\sqrt\alpha\|v\|_1\leq\|v\|_a\leq\sqrt{2\mu+3\lambda}\|v\|_1.}
$$

Subtract the discrete <weak formulation> from the continuous one to get <Galerkin orthogonality>,
$$
a(u-u_h,v_h)=0\qquad(v_h\in V_h).
$$
For any $v_h\in V_h$, write $u-v_h=(u-u_h)+(u_h-v_h)$. The second summand is in $V_h$, so expansion and <Galerkin orthogonality> give the <Pythagorean theorem> in the <energy norm>:
$$
\boxed{\|u-v_h\|_a^2=\|u-u_h\|_a^2+\|u_h-v_h\|_a^2.}
$$
The last term is nonnegative and vanishes exactly when $v_h=u_h$. Choosing $v_h=u_h$ attains the lower bound, proving
$$
\boxed{\|u-u_h\|_a=\min_{v_h\in V_h}\|u-v_h\|_a.}
$$
Thus $u_h$ is the unique <best approximation>; the map $u\mapsto u_h$ is the <orthogonal projector> onto $V_h$ for the <linear elasticity> <energy norm>.
