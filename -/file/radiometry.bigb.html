= Radiometry
{wiki}

<Radiometry> measures the <energy> and <power> carried by <electromagnetic radiation>, including <light>. It asks how much radiation a source supplies, where it travels, and how much reaches a receiving surface. The measurements are physical rather than weighted by the sensitivity of the human eye: equal <radiant fluxes> at different <wavelengths> need not look equally bright. https://www.nist.gov/publications/radiometry-and-photometry-review-vision-optics[Yoshihiro Ohno, Radiometry and Photometry: Review for Vision Optics, 2000]{ref}.

The five quantities below answer different questions about the same radiation. The <International System of Units> makes the distinctions visible: a watt is a joule per second, while a steradian measures <solid angle>. https://www.nist.gov/pml/special-publication-811/nist-guide-si-chapter-4-two-classes-si-units-and-si-prefixes[NIST Guide to the SI, Chapter 4: derived units]{ref}.

Resolving <wavelength> or <frequency> adds a further “per unit interval”, as in <spectral radiance>. The notation is consistent with NIST's table of spectral quantities. In particular, <radiant flux> is <power>, whereas the existing astronomical <radiative flux> is <power> per <area>. https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=901354[NISTIR 6657, A Rational Methodology for Estimating the Luminous Flux Based upon Color Primaries from Digital Projection Displays, 2009, Table 1]{ref}.

This bookkeeping makes an <optical camera>'s collected signal calculable from a source's <radiance> and the geometry of the receiving aperture. It also lets radiation measurements from different instruments be compared. For example, NIST describes separate calibrations of <spectral radiance> and spectral <irradiance> for instruments used to observe Earth. https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=104550[Johnson, Brown and Rice, Metrology for remote sensing radiometry, §§3.4.1–3.4.2]{ref}.

= Ocean optics
{parent=Radiometry}

The study of <light> propagation, absorption and scattering in marine water. It connects spectral measurements to dissolved material, suspended particles and biological activity.

= Photic zone
{parent=Ocean optics}

The illuminated region of a water body. In ecological usage its photosynthetically usable part is often identified with the <euphotic zone>; the operational light threshold must be stated.

= Euphotic zone
{parent=Photic zone}

The upper water interval with sufficient light for substantial <photosynthesis>. A common operational lower boundary is the <euphotic depth> at 1% of surface <photosynthetically active radiation>.

= Euphotic depth
{parent=Euphotic zone}
{title2=$z_{\mathrm{eu}}$}

The depth of a chosen light threshold, commonly 1% of just-below-surface <photosynthetically active radiation>. It depends on spectral attenuation and is not a universal species-specific compensation depth.

= Ocean colour
{parent=Ocean optics}

The spectral distribution of light emerging from a water body, determined by absorption and scattering by water, dissolved material, particles and sometimes the bottom. It supports remote inference of biological and optical properties.

= Diffuse attenuation coefficient
{parent=Ocean colour}
{title2=$K_d=-\partial\ln E_d/\partial z$}

The logarithmic rate at which downwelling spectral <irradiance> decreases with depth. It depends on absorption, scattering and the angular light field, rather than equaling the <absorption coefficient>.

= Chlorophyll retrieval
{parent=Ocean colour}

Inference of <chlorophyll a> concentration from measured optical signals. Simple blue/green ratios rely on assumptions about covarying absorbers and scatterers; coastal mixtures can violate those assumptions.

= Backscattering coefficient
{parent=Ocean colour}
{title2=$b_b$}

The rate per unit path length at which light is scattered into the backward hemisphere. It influences water-leaving <radiance> and must be distinguished from the <absorption coefficient>.

= Remote-sensing reflectance
{parent=Ocean colour}
{title2=$R_{rs}=L_w/E_d$}

The ratio of water-leaving <radiance> to incident downwelling <irradiance> just above the surface. It depends on both absorption and backscattering and has units of inverse steradian.

= Non-algal particle
{parent=Ocean colour}

= NAP
{synonym}
{c}

An optically defined non-phytoplankton particle category, including detritus and suspended mineral material. Its absorption is often strongest in the blue; scattering can also strongly affect <remote-sensing reflectance>.

= Coloured dissolved organic matter
{parent=Ocean colour}

= CDOM
{synonym}
{c}

The light-absorbing fraction of dissolved organic material. Its visible absorption commonly decreases approximately exponentially from blue to red, and can confound <chlorophyll retrieval>.

= Solar irradiance
{parent=Radiometry}
{title2=$I_h=I_n\sin\alpha$}

Solar radiant <power> incident per unit area. Horizontal, beam-normal and tilted-plane irradiances are different quantities: for a direct beam on a horizontal plane, $I_h=I_n\sin\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is solar elevation.

= Remote sensing
{parent=Radiometry}
{wiki}

Inferring properties of a distant object from measured signals, often <electromagnetic radiation>. Spatial sampling, spectral information and observation cadence must jointly resolve the physical process of interest. Received <radiance> alone need not uniquely separate <temperature>, <emissivity> and atmospheric attenuation.

= Lidar
{parent=Remote sensing}

= Light detection and ranging
{synonym}

Range measurement using transmitted <laser> light and its returned signal. For a pulse traveling a round trip in air, the elementary range is $r\simeq c\Delta t/2$, where $c$ is the propagation speed and $\Delta t$ the measured travel time. Sensor position, pointing and time synchronization place returns in a three-dimensional point cloud. Scanning <lidar> can record multiple returns from vegetation and the ground; a return is an observation of a reflecting surface, not automatically bare-earth elevation.

= Airborne lidar
{parent=Lidar}

<Lidar> acquired from an aircraft with surveyed trajectory and orientation. Ground-return classification removes canopy and structure returns before constructing a bare-ground <digital elevation model>. Penetration through forest is sampling through canopy gaps, not unrestricted transmission through leaves. Areas with few or no ground returns require uncertainty assessment rather than treating interpolated heights as observations.<What is the difference between lidar data and a digital elevation model by United States Geological Survey>[Point-cloud returns from vegetation, structures and ground, and filtering to a bare-earth model]{ref}

= Photogrammetry
{parent=Remote sensing}

Recovery of object positions and shape from photographs with overlapping views. Known or estimated camera geometry and matched image features constrain three-dimensional point positions. A metric reconstruction requires scale and a coordinate reference; image pixel size is not automatically the positional accuracy of the reconstructed surface. <Stereo photogrammetry> and <structure-from-motion photogrammetry> use related geometry with different acquisition and camera-estimation strategies.

= Ground control point
{parent=Photogrammetry}

An identifiable image feature or target with independently surveyed coordinates, used to georeference and constrain a <photogrammetry> model. A check point is withheld from the fit to assess reconstruction accuracy. Distribution across the site, accurate survey and consistent height datums matter as well as the number of points.

= Structure-from-motion photogrammetry
{parent=Photogrammetry}

A <photogrammetry> workflow jointly estimating camera geometry and scene structure from many overlapping images, commonly followed by dense surface reconstruction. <Ground control points> or accurately surveyed camera positions establish scale and georeferencing. Independent check points test accuracy rather than participating in the fit. A fine image ground-sampling interval does not alone establish equal vertical accuracy.

= Stereo photogrammetry
{parent=Photogrammetry}

Three-dimensional reconstruction using two or more overlapping photographs from different viewing positions. Corresponding image points define viewing directions whose intersection estimates the surface position. Surveyed camera positions and orientations, camera calibration and <ground control points> establish the coordinate geometry. Ordinary optical stereo models the visible surface, which can be forest canopy rather than bare ground.

= Hyperspectral imaging
{parent=Remote sensing}

Imaging in many narrow, usually adjacent wavelength bands. Relative to broad-band <multispectral imaging>, it can resolve narrower <mineral> absorption features in <spectral reflectance>, helping distinguish surface <lithologies>, at the cost of data volume and often reduced signal per band.

= Atmospheric correction
{parent=Remote sensing}

Removal or modeling of atmospheric absorption and scattering contributions to a measured signal to estimate surface or water-leaving optical quantities.

= GOES
{parent=Remote sensing}
{c}

The Geostationary Operational Environmental Satellite series, used for frequent weather observations from <geostationary orbits>.

= Meteosat
{parent=Remote sensing}
{c}

A European meteorological satellite series including <geostationary orbit> missions for repeated regional weather observations.

= Sentinel-1
{parent=Remote sensing}
{c}

A European radar mission using <sun-synchronous orbits> and supporting <interferometric synthetic-aperture radar>.

= Sentinel-2
{parent=Remote sensing}
{c}

A European optical <multispectral imaging> mission using <sun-synchronous orbits> for repeated observations of land and coastal areas.

= Landsat
{parent=Remote sensing}
{c}

A series of <Earth>-observation missions using repeated optical and <multispectral imaging> from near-polar <sun-synchronous orbits>.

= Multispectral imaging
{parent=Remote sensing}

Imaging in several wavelength bands. Differences in spectral <reflectance> can distinguish surface materials, including vegetation and water.

= Band ratio image
{parent=Multispectral imaging}
{title2=$L_1/L_2$}

An image formed by dividing corresponding calibrated pixels in two spectral bands. If their signals have a common multiplicative factor, $L_1=k\rho_1$ and $L_2=k\rho_2$, their ratio is $\rho_1/\rho_2$ and does not depend on $k$. This explains suppression of some common illumination effects and enhancement of spectral contrasts. Additive atmospheric radiance and small denominators prevent exact cancellation and can amplify errors. <Normalized difference vegetation index> is a related normalized spectral contrast.

= Normalized difference vegetation index
{parent=Multispectral imaging}
{title2=$\mathrm{NDVI}=(\rho_{\mathrm{NIR}}-\rho_{\mathrm{red}})/(\rho_{\mathrm{NIR}}+\rho_{\mathrm{red}})$}

= NDVI
{synonym}
{c}

The normalized contrast of red and <near-infrared> <reflectances>. Large positive values indicate a vegetation-like spectrum, but soil, atmosphere, shadows and canopy saturation affect its interpretation.

= False-colour composite
{parent=Multispectral imaging}

A displayed image assigning measured bands to colour channels that differ from ordinary visual perception. Assigning <near-infrared> to red is a common way to highlight vegetation.

= Red edge
{parent=Multispectral imaging}

The steep increase of healthy green vegetation <reflectance> between red absorption and the high <near-infrared> scattering plateau, typically around 0.68–0.75 micrometres.

= Interferometric synthetic-aperture radar
{parent=Remote sensing}

= InSAR
{synonym}
{c}

Comparison of coherent radar <phases> from repeated observations to infer relative <line-of-sight displacement>. A complete deformation fringe represents half a radar wavelength after other phase contributions have been removed.

= Radar interferogram
{parent=Interferometric synthetic-aperture radar}
{title2=$\Delta\phi=\pm4\pi d_{\mathrm{LOS}}/\lambda_r$}

An image of the relative radar phase between co-registered coherent observations. A displacement changes the two-way path length, giving phase change proportional to $4\pi d_{\mathrm{LOS}}/\lambda_r$, with sign set by the adopted range convention. A full wrapped phase cycle therefore corresponds to half a radar wavelength of <line-of-sight displacement>. Orbital geometry, topography, atmospheric delay and scattering changes must be separated from deformation; a fringe is not uniquely an elevation contour or an earthquake displacement.<Interferometry by NASA>[Interferograms, half-wavelength fringes and removal of orbital and topographic contributions]{ref}

= Interferometric coherence
{parent=Interferometric synthetic-aperture radar}

A normalized measure of consistency of complex radar returns over a neighborhood. Low coherence makes interferometric <phase> unreliable and can arise from vegetation, surface changes, viewing differences or extreme displacement gradients.

= Line-of-sight displacement
{parent=Interferometric synthetic-aperture radar}
{title2=$u_{\mathrm{LOS}}$}

The component of a displacement vector along a specified sensor–target viewing direction. Its sign must state whether motion toward or away from the sensor is positive.

= Interferometric phase
{parent=Interferometric synthetic-aperture radar}
{title2=$\Delta\phi$}

The <phase> difference of complex radar returns. It includes deformation, topography, orbital geometry and propagation contributions; the displayed wrapped phase is defined modulo $2\pi$.

= Optical haze
{parent=Radiometry}

The fraction of transmitted light scattered sufficiently far from the forward direction under a specified measurement geometry. A transparent electrode can have high total transmittance yet blur an image if scattering is strong. State the collection angles and wavelength when comparing haze measurements.

= Actinic flux
{parent=Radiometry}

The photon flux incident from all propagation directions, resolved by wavelength when needed for <photodissociation>. It is distinct from the net radiative flux through a horizontal surface; scattered and diffuse photons can contribute even when their net vertical flux cancels.

= Fluence
{parent=Radiometry}
{title2=$F=\int I\,dt$}

For an optical beam, time-integrated <light intensity>, giving incident <energy> per unit area.

= Photon energy fluence
{parent=Fluence}
{title2=$\Psi$}

Energy carried by incident <photons> per unit area. For monoenergetic <photons>, $\Psi=E_\gamma\Phi$, where $\Phi$ is <photon fluence>. With <charged-particle equilibrium>, $D=\Psi\mu_{\rm en}/\rho$ relates it to <absorbed dose>.

= Photon fluence
{parent=Fluence}
{title2=$\Phi$}

The number of <photons> incident per unit area in a specified geometry. It is distinct from a rate per unit time. For a monoenergetic beam, <photon energy fluence> is $\Psi=E_\gamma\Phi$.

= Radiometer
{parent=Radiometry}
{wiki}

A <radiometer> measures <electromagnetic radiation> through its received <power>. A radio <radiometer> can express that <power> as a <noise temperature>, allowing an unknown source to be compared with calibrated reference <temperatures>.

= Radiometer two-point calibration
{parent=Radiometer}

For a stable linear <radiometer>, known cold and hot <radiances> give $B_S=B_C+(B_H-B_C)(V_S-V_C)/(V_H-V_C)$. Put $a=(B_H-B_C)/(V_H-V_C)$ and $b=-a(V_S-V_C)/(V_H-V_C)$. Then $\delta B_S=a\delta V_S+b\delta V_H-(a+b)\delta V_C$; all three measured voltages contribute to the retrieved uncertainty.

= Noise temperature
{parent=Radiometer}
{title2=$T_N$}
{wiki}

A <noise temperature> expresses received or internally added noise <power> on an equivalent <temperature> scale. For one matched receiving channel in the classical thermal limit, an ideal rectangular <bandwidth> $\Delta\nu$ gives $P=k_{\rm B}T_N\Delta\nu$, with <Boltzmann constant> $k_{\rm B}$. An equivalent <noise temperature> need not equal a component's physical <temperature>.

= System noise temperature
{parent=Noise temperature}
{title2=$T_{\rm sys}$}

The <system noise temperature> includes all noise contributions referred to a specified receiver input. In a simple additive model with negligible intervening losses, $T_{\rm sys}=T_A+T_r$, where $T_A$ is the <antenna temperature> and $T_r$ the <receiver noise temperature>.

= Receiver noise temperature
{parent=Noise temperature}
{title2=$T_r$}

The <receiver noise temperature> represents noise added by the receiver as an equivalent <noise temperature> referred to its input. Separating it from the <antenna temperature> prevents internal receiver noise from being mistaken for radiation from the sky.

= Two-load radiometer calibration
{parent=Radiometer}
{title2=$T_s=T_c+(T_h-T_c)(S_s-S_c)/(S_h-S_c)$}

For a stable <radiometer> with affine output $S=g(T+T_r)$, let $g>0$ be the fixed response coefficient and $T_r$ the <receiver noise temperature>. Two reference loads with known equivalent input <noise temperatures> $T_c<T_h$ give outputs $S_c$ and $S_h$. Subtracting their outputs eliminates $T_r$; dividing source and reference differences eliminates $g$:
$$
S_h-S_c=g(T_h-T_c),\qquad
T_s=T_c+(T_h-T_c)\frac{S_s-S_c}{S_h-S_c}.
$$
This <two-load radiometer calibration> assumes unchanged response and <receiver noise temperature> between readings, with the loads and source referred to the same input. It also eliminates an additional constant output offset. Physical load <temperatures> must first be converted to the applicable equivalent input <noise temperatures> when the classical thermal approximation fails. The <Penzias-Wilson microwave background experiment> gives an original worked example.

= Radiant power
{synonym}

<Radiant flux> is the <power> carried by <electromagnetic radiation>: radiation <energy> transferred per unit <time>, measured in <watts>, $\mathrm W=\mathrm{J\,s^{-1}}$. Specify what is counted: all radiation emitted by a source, radiation within one beam, or radiation intercepted by a <photodetector>. Writing $Q(t)$ for the cumulative <energy> crossing the specified boundary gives
$$
\Phi_e(t)=\frac{dQ}{dt},\qquad
\overline\Phi_e=\frac{Q(t_2)-Q(t_1)}{t_2-t_1},\qquad
Q(t_2)-Q(t_1)=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\Phi_e(t)\,dt.
$$
The first expression is an instantaneous <derivative>; the second is an average over the stated <time> interval. If $0.020\,\mathrm J$ arrives during $0.010\,\mathrm s$, the mean <radiant flux> is $2\,\mathrm W$. The <photodetector>'s absorbed <power> equals this incident <radiant flux> only if it absorbs all of the incident radiation.

Keeping <radiant flux> fixed while widening a beam reduces its <irradiance>, the incident <power> per receiving <area>. <Radiance> additionally resolves the propagation direction and has units $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}}$; an <optical camera> can collect different <radiant fluxes> from sources with the same <radiance> but different apparent <solid angles>. <Luminous flux>, measured in lumens, weights the <optical spectrum> by the human eye's standardized response instead of counting <energy> equally at every <wavelength>. Equal <radiant fluxes> need not give equal <luminous fluxes>.<OSA Handbook of Optics, Volume III Visual Optics and Vision/Radiometry and Photometry: Review for Vision Optics>{ref}

<Radiant power> also differs from the <optical power> of an <optical lens>, which is reciprocal <focal length> in <dioptres>. Likewise, astronomical <radiative flux> is <power> per <area>. Stating the units prevents these shared words from hiding different physical quantities.

= Radiant flux through an oriented surface
{parent=Radiant flux}
{title2=$\Phi_{\rm net}=\Phi_+-\Phi_-$}

In <geometrical optics>, suppose radiation has a nonnegative <radiance> $L(\mathbf x,\mathbf s)$ whose directional contributions add as <power>. Here $\mathbf s$ is the actual propagation <unit vector>, $d\Omega$ is a <solid angle> element, and the collecting <oriented surface> $A$ has chosen <unit normal> $\mathbf n(\mathbf x)$. Assume the following <integrals> exist. The outward and inward <radiant fluxes> are
$$
\Phi_+=\int_A\!\int_{\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf s>0}
L(\mathbf x,\mathbf s)(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf s)\,d\Omega\,dA,
\qquad
\Phi_-=\int_A\!\int_{\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf s<0}
L(\mathbf x,\mathbf s)(-\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf s)\,d\Omega\,dA.
$$
Both quantities are nonnegative; their difference is the signed net <radiant flux>. The <dot product> supplies the <cosine> of the <angle> with the <unit normal>, hence the <projected area>. For incoming radiation the outward <unit normal> gives a negative <dot product>; the minus sign makes the collected <power> positive. Measuring the incoming <angle> from a source-facing <unit normal> instead gives the familiar positive $\cos\theta$. The units check is $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}}\times\mathrm{m^2}\times\mathrm{sr}=\mathrm W$.

Equivalently, the incoming <surface integral> is $\Phi_-=\int_A E\,dA$ with local <irradiance> $E$; the emitted part of $\Phi_+$ is $\int_A M\,dA$ with <radiant exitance> $M$. For a small flat patch and a narrow accepted cone, nearly constant <radiance> and <angle> give $\Phi\simeq LA\cos\theta\,\Delta\Omega$. This approximation requires uniformity across the patch and cone. Direction-independent outgoing <radiance> over a whole <hemisphere> gives the different exact result $\Phi_+=\pi LA$, by <Lambert's cosine law> and the <spherical coordinates> calculation in <radiant exitance>. Replacing the <cosine>-weighted <integral> by the <hemisphere>'s $2\pi$ <solid angle> would double the answer.<Radiometry and Photometry>{ref}

These <radiance> formulas presume a description in additive directional <power>; they do not reconstruct arbitrary coherent <wave interference> from separate ray contributions. For general <electromagnetic fields>, the signed instantaneous <power> crossing an <oriented surface> is $\int_A\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf n\,dA$, using the <Poynting vector> $\mathbf S$; optical measurements commonly average over many <wave periods>. A zero net <radiant flux> can conceal equal nonzero incoming and outgoing <radiant fluxes>.

= Beam collection at fixed radiant flux
{parent=Radiant flux}
{title2=$\Phi_{\rm collected}=P\min(1,a^2/b^2)$}

Consider an ideal parallel circular beam with uniform <irradiance> inside radius $b$, zero outside, and total <radiant flux> $P=2\,\mathrm W$. A centred circular collector of radius $a$, perpendicular to the <light rays>, intercepts
$$
E_b=\frac{P}{\pi b^2},\qquad
\Phi_{\rm collected}=E_b\pi\min(a,b)^2
=P\min\!\left(1,\frac{a^2}{b^2}\right).
$$
For $b=1\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $a=0.5\,\mathrm{cm}$, $E_b=20000/\pi\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\simeq6366.20\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ and the collected <radiant flux> is $0.50\,\mathrm W$. Expanding the beam to $b=2\,\mathrm{cm}$ while keeping $P$ fixed divides <irradiance> and the same collector's <radiant flux> by four: $E_b\simeq1591.55\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ and $\Phi_{\rm collected}=0.125\,\mathrm W$. A collector covering either entire beam still receives $2\,\mathrm W$.

Tilting a flat collector of actual <area> $A$ by an <angle> $\theta$ to the beam direction gives $\Phi_{\rm collected}=E_b A\cos\theta$ if its entire <projected area> remains inside the uniform beam. At $60^\circ$ the small collector's <radiant flux> halves. A collector large enough to intercept the whole beam after tilting still receives $P$: the footprint on its surface grows as $1/\cos\theta$ and cancels the <irradiance> factor. This model ignores <diffraction>, beam-edge structure, and changes in absorption with incidence <angle>; it counts incident rather than absorbed <power>.

= Spectral radiant flux
{parent=Radiant flux}
{title2=$\Phi_{e,\lambda},\ \Phi_{e,\nu}$}

<Spectral radiant flux> resolves <radiant flux> by <wavelength> or <frequency>:
$$
\Phi_e=\int_0^\infty\Phi_{e,\lambda}(\lambda)\,d\lambda
=\int_0^\infty\Phi_{e,\nu}(\nu)\,d\nu.
$$
Its units are $\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}}$ per metre of <wavelength> or $\mathrm{W\,Hz^{-1}}$ per hertz of <frequency>. A density quoted per nanometre must be integrated with <wavelength> intervals in nanometres; its numerical value is $10^{-9}$ times the per-metre density. The <frequency and wavelength spectral radiance conversion> applies equally to <spectral radiant flux>, through the <change of variables formula>:
$$
\Phi_{e,\nu}(\nu)=\Phi_{e,\lambda}(c/\nu)\frac{c}{\nu^2},
$$
with $c$ the <speed of light> in <vacuum> and $\Phi_{e,\lambda}$ expressed per metre. The <Jacobian determinant> preserves integrated <power> rather than the height or location of an <optical spectrum>'s peak.

For two explicitly synthetic <optical spectra>, let $x=\lambda/(1\,\mathrm{nm})$ and quote the following densities in $\mathrm{mW\,nm^{-1}}$:
$$
f_A(x)=\begin{cases}0.020(1-|x-500|/100),&400\leq x\leq600,\\0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}
\qquad
f_B(x)=\begin{cases}0.010,&600\leq x\leq800,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
$$
The <triangle> under $f_A$ and the <rectangle> under $f_B$ both have <area> $2\,\mathrm{mW}$: $200\times0.020/2=200\times0.010=2$. Their band <radiant fluxes> differ: $450$–$550\,\mathrm{nm}$ contains $1.5\,\mathrm{mW}$ of $A$, while $650$–$750\,\mathrm{nm}$ contains $1.0\,\mathrm{mW}$ of $B$. These are specified functions, not measured source spectra.

The dark-subtracted <electric current> of a linear <photodiode> is
$$
i=\int R(\lambda)\Phi_{e,\lambda}(\lambda)\,d\lambda,
\qquad [R]=\mathrm{A\,W^{-1}},
$$
using its <spectral responsivity>. The figure's stipulated $R$ gives $i_A=0.60\,\mathrm{mA}$ and $i_B=1.00\,\mathrm{mA}$ despite equal total <radiant flux>. Symmetry places $A$'s mean <wavelength> at $500\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $B$'s at $700\,\mathrm{nm}$; a linear $R$ averages to its value at that mean. A single <electric current> therefore cannot determine an unknown broadband <radiant flux> without additional spectral information or a sufficiently flat calibrated response.

= Calibrated radiant-flux measurement
{parent=Radiant flux}

An <optical power meter> makes the definition operational: collect a specified beam on a calibrated <photodiode>, measure its <electric current>, and infer incident <radiant flux> using <spectral responsivity>. In the manufacturer-authored <Model 1830-R Series Optical Power Meters User's Manual by Newport Corporation>, §5.5.1 specifies a concrete sequence: connect the detector and its calibration module, select watt mode and the source <wavelength>, block the beam and press ZERO, then unblock it and read the display. The stored background includes <dark current> and ambient <light> present during zeroing. For narrowband radiation,
$$
\Phi_{\rm indicated}=\frac{i_{\rm illuminated}-i_{\rm zero}}{R(\lambda)}.
$$
The <electric current> difference divided by <spectral responsivity> has units $\mathrm A/(\mathrm{A\,W^{-1}})=\mathrm W$. For a stipulated $R=0.40\,\mathrm{A\,W^{-1}}$ and difference $0.20\,\mathrm{mA}$, the inferred <radiant flux> is $0.50\,\mathrm{mW}$; these numbers illustrate the inference and are not reported measurements. The entire beam must hit the active <area>, and §5.6 calls for correct <wavelength>, background subtraction, and operation below detector saturation. If an <optical attenuator> is fitted, §5.4 requires the calibrated detector–attenuator pair and the matching meter setting.

The calibration itself must connect <electric current> to independently known incident <power>. NIST's <Spectroradiometric Detector Measurements: Ultraviolet, Visible, and Near-Infrared Detectors for Spectral Power by Thomas C. Larason and others> describes this chain. A <cryogenic radiometer>, an <electrical substitution radiometer>, compares <temperature> changes from absorbed <light> with those from known <Joule heating> (§5.2). Transfer and working detectors carry that <watt> scale to a spectral comparator (§5.1). A source, <monochromator>, focusing optics, shutter, <beam splitter>, and monitor <photodiode> illuminate a standard and test detector in turn; calibrated current-to-voltage electronics and voltmeters provide the readout (§§3.2.2–3.2.4, 4.3.1). At each <wavelength>, subtract blocked-beam signals and normalize each detector signal by the simultaneously sampled monitor signal. Comparison with the standard yields the test detector's <spectral responsivity>. NIST uses two working standards and alignment normal to the beam (§6.1.1).

Controls distinguish a smaller collected <radiant flux> from a faulty conversion: block and unblock the beam to check the baseline; check that its footprint fits the active <area>; verify the calibrated <optical attenuator> configuration; and test proportional response within the specified operating range. Source drift faster than monitor sampling is not removed. NIST discusses finite spectral bandwidth, stray <light>, <temperature>, and spatial response variations (§§3.1.1, 6.2, 7.2). A broadband <optical spectrum> requires the weighted <integral> in <spectral radiant flux>, rather than dividing by an arbitrary single <spectral responsivity>. These limitations explain why the meter's displayed <watts> need a stated collection boundary, spectral assumptions, and calibration conditions.

= Radiant intensity
{parent=Radiometry}
{title2=$I=d\Phi/d\Omega$}
{wiki}

<Radiant intensity> describes how a source distributes its <radiant flux> over directions. A small cone of <solid angle> $\Delta\Omega$ carries approximately $I\Delta\Omega$ when $I$ varies little across it; integrating $I$ over all emitting directions recovers the total <radiant flux>. For a source with <isotropy> emitting $4\pi\,\mathrm W$ over a full <sphere>, $I=1\,\mathrm{W\,sr^{-1}}$ and a $0.01\,\mathrm{sr}$ cone carries $0.01\,\mathrm W$. A planar source satisfying <Lambert's cosine law> instead has direction-dependent <radiant intensity>, even though its <radiance> is direction-independent.

= Point emitter
{parent=Radiant intensity}

An ideal <point emitter> emits <radiant flux> from one location. It approximates a physical source when its emitting dimensions are small relative to the distances relevant to the calculation. With <isotropy>, its <radiant intensity> is $I=\Phi/(4\pi)$ for total <radiant flux> $\Phi$. An extended source generally requires integrating contributions from different emitting positions.

= Radiance
{parent=Radiometry}
{wiki}
{title2=$L$}

<Radiance> resolves both position and direction: it is <radiant flux> per <projected area> and per <solid angle>. For a surface element of <area> $dA$, and a small outgoing cone $d\Omega$ at <angle> $0\leq\theta<\pi/2$ to its outward <unit normal>,
$$
d^2\Phi=L(\mathbf x,\mathbf s)\cos\theta\,dA\,d\Omega,
\qquad [L]=\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}}.
$$
Here $\mathbf x$ locates the patch and $\mathbf s$ is a <unit vector> along the outgoing direction. The factor $dA\cos\theta$ is the patch's apparent <area> viewed along that direction. The same local definition applies to transmitted or received radiation, choosing the positive <projected area> for the crossing direction. https://labs.phys.utk.edu/mbreinig/phys421/modules/m5/Radiometry.html[Marianne Breinig, University of Tennessee, Radiometry: radiance and Lambert's law]{ref}.

For a planar patch of <area> $A$ with spatially uniform, direction-independent <radiance> $L$, integrating over the patch gives
$$
I(\theta)=LA\cos\theta.
$$
Take $L=10\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}}$ and $A=0.020\,\mathrm{m^2}$. The <radiant intensities> are $I(0)=0.20\,\mathrm{W\,sr^{-1}}$ and $I(60^\circ)=0.10\,\mathrm{W\,sr^{-1}}$. A sufficiently narrow $0.010\,\mathrm{sr}$ cone therefore carries approximately $2.0\,\mathrm{mW}$ or $1.0\,\mathrm{mW}$, respectively. Both views have the same <radiance> $L$: the oblique view presents half the <projected area>. This is <Lambert's cosine law>, not a loss of <radiance> with viewing <angle>.

<Radiance> is useful because it separates a source's local directional output from the geometry of an observation. Integrating over outgoing directions gives <radiant exitance>; integrating incoming <radiance> at a receiver gives <irradiance>. In the stationary <vacuum> setting of <vacuum conservation of specific intensity>, a resolved uniform source retains its <radiance> along each <light ray>, while the <radiant flux> received from the whole source can decrease as its apparent <solid angle> shrinks.

= Projected area
{parent=Radiance}
{title2=$A_\perp=A\cos\theta$}

The <projected area> of a planar patch viewed from a direction is the <area> of its perpendicular shadow onto a plane normal to that direction. If $\mathbf n$ and $\mathbf s$ are its <unit normal> and viewing <unit vector>, then $A_\perp=A|\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf s|$ by the <dot product>. On the outward side, this is $A\cos\theta$. For a <rectangle> tilted around one edge, the width perpendicular to that edge contracts by $\cos\theta$ and the edge length stays fixed, proving the formula. At grazing view $A_\perp=0$; interpret the <radiance> definition by a limit rather than dividing by zero.

= Spectral radiance
{parent=Radiance}
{title2=$L_\nu,\ L_\lambda$}
{wiki}

= Specific intensity
{synonym}

<Spectral radiance> is <radiance> per unit <frequency> or <wavelength> interval. Its astronomical synonym <specific intensity> is usually written $I_\nu=L_\nu$ or $I_\lambda=L_\lambda$; this $I_\nu$ is a spectral <radiance>, distinct from the <radiant intensity> $I$ in <radiometry>. For positive small intervals,
$$
d^3\Phi=L_\nu\cos\theta\,dA\,d\Omega\,d\nu
=L_\lambda\cos\theta\,dA\,d\Omega\,d\lambda.
$$
The two expressions refer to the same spectral slice, with the positive widths related through <frequency> $\nu$ and <wavelength> $\lambda$. For <frequency> measured in hertz, $\mathrm{Hz}=\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, the units are $[L_\nu]=\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}\,Hz^{-1}}$. For <wavelength> measured in metres, $[L_\lambda]=\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}\,m^{-1}}$. Integrating over a spectral band gives its <radiance>, and then multiplying by the collecting <projected area> and accepted <solid angle> gives a small uniform beam's <radiant flux>. A spectral measurement therefore retains information that the band-integrated <power> alone loses.

For an ideal <blackbody>, <Planck's law> gives $L_\nu=B_\nu(T)$ and $L_\lambda=B_\lambda(T)$ at <temperature> $T$, independent of outgoing direction. Its existing <frequency and wavelength peaks of Planck's law> illustrate why the coordinate matters. The simpler flat-band calculation below isolates that same <change of variables> without the thermal physics.

= Frequency and wavelength spectral radiance conversion
{parent=Spectral radiance}
{title2=$L_\nu=L_\lambda(c/\nu)\,c/\nu^2$}

In <vacuum>, $\nu=c/\lambda$, where $c$ is the <speed of light>. Increasing <wavelength> decreases <frequency>, so the ordinary signed <differentials> satisfy $d\nu=-c\lambda^{-2}d\lambda$. A spectral slice's positive contribution to <radiance> must be unchanged. The <change of variables formula> therefore gives the absolute <Jacobian determinant>
$$
L_\nu(\nu)=L_\lambda(c/\nu)\left|\frac{d\lambda}{d\nu}\right|
=L_\lambda(c/\nu)\frac{c}{\nu^2},
\qquad
L_\lambda(\lambda)=L_\nu(c/\lambda)\frac{c}{\lambda^2}.
$$
These formulas use $L_\lambda$ per metre and $L_\nu$ per hertz. The factor $c/\nu^2$ has units $\mathrm{m\,s}=\mathrm{m\,Hz^{-1}}$, which converts the former density into the latter. If $\ell_{\rm nm}$ denotes the numerical density per nanometre in $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}\,nm^{-1}}$, its per-metre value is $10^9\ell_{\rm nm}$. Conversely, the per-nanometre value is $10^{-9}$ times the per-metre value. Likewise, a density per terahertz is $10^{12}$ times its per-hertz value because $1\,\mathrm{THz}=10^{12}\,\mathrm{Hz}$.

For a concrete stipulated spectrum, let $L_\lambda=C=10^9\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}\,m^{-1}}$ between $\lambda_1=500\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $\lambda_2=600\,\mathrm{nm}$, and zero outside. This is exactly $1\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}\,nm^{-1}}$ throughout the band. Using the exact <International System of Units> value $c=299792458\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, the corresponding <frequency> bounds are
$$
\nu_1=\frac{c}{\lambda_2}=499.654096666\ldots\,\mathrm{THz},
\qquad
\nu_2=\frac{c}{\lambda_1}=599.584916\,\mathrm{THz}.
$$
The transformed <spectral radiance> is $L_\nu=Cc/\nu^2$ on this interval. At $\nu_1$ it is approximately $1.200830743\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}\,Hz^{-1}}$, and at $\nu_2$ it is $8.339102380\times10^{-13}$ in the same units. These are respectively $1.200830743$ and $0.833910238$ per terahertz. Thus a flat <wavelength> density becomes a decreasing <frequency> density; relabelling the horizontal axis alone would miss both the shape and the units. https://www.nist.gov/si-redefinition/meet-constants[NIST, Meet the Constants: exact speed of light]{ref}.

The equality of band <radiances> has a short exact proof:
$$
\int_{\lambda_1}^{\lambda_2}C\,d\lambda
=C(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)
=100\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}},
$$
$$
\int_{\nu_1}^{\nu_2}\frac{Cc}{\nu^2}\,d\nu
=Cc\left(\frac1{\nu_1}-\frac1{\nu_2}\right)
=C(\lambda_2-\lambda_1).
$$
The reversed bounds and the <Jacobian determinant> preserve the same spectral measure. Each half-band, $500$–$550\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $550$–$600\,\mathrm{nm}$, carries $50\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}}$, although the corresponding <frequency> widths are about $54.508$ and $45.423\,\mathrm{THz}$.

A spectrum's peak is consequently a statement about a particular density and coordinate. The <frequency and wavelength peaks of Planck's law> differ for precisely this reason. Integrated <radiance> over a specified physical band supplies a comparison that survives the <change of variables>.

= Vacuum conservation of specific intensity
{parent=Spectral radiance}

In a transparent, stationary <vacuum> with <Euclidean geometry> and observers at rest in the same frame, the <radiative transfer equation> has zero absorption and emission, so $dI_\nu/ds=0$ along a <light ray>. The same <specific intensity> reaches the observer without geometric dilution. More generally, $I_\nu/\nu^3$ is a <Lorentz invariant>; collisionless propagation preserves this ratio along a ray even with gravitational or cosmological <redshift>, rather than preserving $I_\nu$ at shifted <frequencies>. https://www.roe.ac.uk/japwww/teaching/ram5/ram5_2018.pdf#page=27[John Peacock, Radiation and Matter, 2018–2019, §4.1, Conservation of intensity and equation (140)]{ref}.

The elementary geometry is instructive. Two facing surface elements separated by distance $s$ exchange spectral <radiant flux>
$$
d^3\Phi=I_\nu\frac{\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2}{s^2}\,dA_1\,dA_2\,d\nu.
$$
The receiver subtends $d\Omega_2=\cos\theta_2\,dA_2/s^2$ at the source. Describing that same traveling beam at the receiver instead uses $d\Omega_1=\cos\theta_1\,dA_1/s^2$. Both descriptions have identical geometric factors, so conservation of its <radiant flux> gives equal <specific intensities> at the two ends. This compares the same propagation direction at both ends, not emission in the opposite direction.

A uniform source remains equally bright per resolved direction under these assumptions, but occupies a smaller apparent <solid angle> farther away. For a small source of <projected area> $A_\perp$ at distance $d$ and a small receiver facing it, <irradiance> is approximately $E=L\Omega_{\rm source}\simeq LA_\perp/d^2$. The <radiant flux> intercepted by receiver <area> $A_d$ is approximately $EA_d$. With $L=10\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}}$, $A_\perp=0.020\,\mathrm{m^2}$ and $A_d=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$, distances $10$ and $20\,\mathrm m$ give $E\simeq0.002$ and $0.0005\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$, and received <radiant fluxes> of $0.20$ and $0.050\,\mu\mathrm W$. The <inverse-square law> concerns collected radiation from the whole source; $L$ remains $10$ at either distance.

= Limb darkening
{parent=Spectral radiance}
{wiki}

Limb darkening is the decrease of emergent <specific intensity> toward the apparent edge of a stellar disc. A transit depth then depends on the intensity sampled by the planet as well as its projected area; the uniform-disc area ratio is only an approximation.

For a spherical <star> of <radius> $R_\star$, let $r$ be distance from the centre of its projected disk and let $\theta$ be the <angle> between the outward <unit normal> and the <line of sight>. The angular coordinate is
$$
\mu=\cos\theta=\sqrt{1-(r/R_\star)^2}.
$$
Disk centre has $\mu=1$; the apparent edge approaches $\mu=0$. A resolved image measures <specific intensity> in each direction. <Limb darkening> means that this <specific intensity> itself falls toward the edge; the <projected area> factor used when collecting <radiant flux> is a separate geometric effect.

A simple explanation assumes a horizontally uniform <plane-parallel atmosphere>, a sufficiently deep <optically thick medium>, negligible <scattering of light>, and thermal absorption and emission in <local thermodynamic equilibrium>. Let $\tau_\nu$ be inward vertical <optical depth> at fixed <frequency> $\nu$. An oblique <light ray> has <line of sight> <optical depth> $\tau_\nu/\mu$, so it encounters the same attenuation at a smaller vertical <optical depth>. The <formal solution of the radiative transfer equation> gives
$$
I_\nu(0,\mu)=\int_0^\infty S_\nu(\tau_\nu)e^{-\tau_\nu/\mu}\frac{d\tau_\nu}{\mu},\qquad 0<\mu\le1.
$$
Here $S_\nu$ is the <source function>; under these assumptions it is the <Planck function> $B_\nu[T(\tau_\nu)]$. If <temperature> increases inward, an oblique view preferentially samples cooler layers and hence lower <specific intensity>. <Radiative Transfer in Stellar Atmospheres by Robert J. Rutten (8th edition)>[§2.2.2, pp. 17–19, equations (2.37)–(2.44) and Figure 2.4]{ref}

The weight $e^{-\tau_\nu/\mu}/\mu$ is an <exponential distribution> in vertical <optical depth>, with <expected value> $\mu$ and <standard deviation> $\mu$. Thus “seeing down to <optical depth> one” refers to the <line of sight>, and represents a broad sampling of layers rather than a sharp emitting surface. For a <source function> that is linear throughout the atmosphere, the <Eddington-Barbier relation> is exact:
$$
S_\nu(\tau_\nu)=a_\nu+b_\nu\tau_\nu
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
I_\nu(0,\mu)=a_\nu+b_\nu\mu=S_\nu(\mu).
$$
This follows by integrating the constant and linear terms separately. A constant <source function> instead gives constant <specific intensity>, as in the <blackbody limit of an isothermal atmosphere>. An outward-rising <temperature> or important <scattering of light> changes the argument. Close enough to the actual edge, a <plane-parallel atmosphere> also loses validity because of curvature. Neither one <limb-darkening coefficient> nor a universal dependence on <wavelength> follows from this simple model.

= Linear limb-darkening law
{parent=Limb darkening}
{title2=$I(\mu)=I_c[1-u(1-\mu)]$}

The <linear limb-darkening law> parametrizes angular <specific intensity> by a stipulated <limb-darkening coefficient> $u$:
$$
I(\mu)=I_c[1-u(1-\mu)],\qquad I_c=I(1),\qquad 0\le u\le1.
$$
Here $I$ denotes <specific intensity> at one <frequency>, or <radiance> integrated over a specified interval of <frequency> or <wavelength>; it does not denote <radiant intensity>. Within this stipulated range, $u=0$ gives a uniform disk and $I(0)/I_c=1-u$. The <linear limb-darkening law> is linear in $\mu$, while its radial profile $I(r)=I_c[1-u+u\sqrt{1-(r/R_\star)^2}]$ is curved. In the linear <source function> example above, $u=b_\nu/(a_\nu+b_\nu)$ when $a_\nu,b_\nu\ge0$ and $a_\nu+b_\nu>0$.

For the illustrative choice $u=0.6$, the <specific intensity> at $r/R_\star=0$, $0.8$, and the limiting edge is respectively $I_c$, $0.76I_c$, and $0.4I_c$. These are synthetic values, not measurements of the <Sun>. Real <optical spectra> and <stellar atmospheres> need not obey a <linear limb-darkening law>; more flexible profiles can be necessary for accurate <exoplanet transit photometry>. <Analytic Light Curves for Planetary Transit Searches by Kaisey Mandel and Eric Agol>[§1, p. L171: the linear law is insufficient for the cited high-quality transit observations; §4 defines a quadratic law]{ref}

= Limb-darkening coefficient
{parent=Linear limb-darkening law}
{title2=$u$}

The parameter $u$ in the <linear limb-darkening law> specifies its contrast between disk centre and the limiting edge. Its interpretation requires the chosen law and interval of <frequency> or <wavelength>. A <limb-darkening coefficient> fitted to an <exoplanet transit> may trade off against the <transit impact parameter> and <planet> <radius>; it is not a direct measurement of one layer's <temperature>.

= Limb-darkened disk flux
{parent=Limb darkening}
{title2=$\overline I=I_c(1-u/3)$}

For a circular stellar disk whose <specific intensity> is independent of <azimuth>, its mean <specific intensity> over <projected area> is
$$
\overline I=\frac{1}{\pi R_\star^2}\int_{\rm disk} I\,dA_{\rm proj}
=2\int_0^1 I(\mu)\mu\,d\mu.
$$
The <polar coordinates> element is $dA_{\rm proj}=2\pi r\,dr$ after angular integration, and $r\,dr=-R_\star^2\mu\,d\mu$. Substitution of the <linear limb-darkening law> gives
$$
\overline I=2I_c\int_0^1(1-u+u\mu)\mu\,d\mu=I_c(1-u/3).
$$
For a distant observer at distance $D\gg R_\star$ receiving <light> normally to the detecting plane, the <observed radiative flux> is
$$
f_\nu\simeq\frac{\pi R_\star^2}{D^2}\overline I_\nu
=\pi\left(\frac{R_\star}{D}\right)^2I_{\nu,c}(1-u_\nu/3).
$$
It is spectral <irradiance>, with units $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,Hz^{-1}}$ when $I_\nu$ is per hertz. The local outward <radiative flux> per actual surface <area> is instead $F_\nu=2\pi\int_0^1 I_\nu(\mu)\mu\,d\mu=\pi\overline I_\nu$, a spectral <radiant exitance>. In a spherical model with the same <stellar atmosphere> everywhere, the <luminosity> is $L_\nu=4\pi R_\star^2F_\nu$ and $f_\nu=L_\nu/(4\pi D^2)$, consistent with the <inverse-square law>. Compare disks at fixed $I_c$ or fixed total <observed radiative flux> explicitly: increasing $u$ lowers the total in the first comparison, while the second requires a larger $I_c$.

= Small-planet transit weighting
{parent=Limb darkening}
{title2=$\delta\simeq k^2I(\mu_p)/\overline I$}

A dark, opaque circular <planet> of <radius> $R_p$ blocks <specific intensity> over its overlap with the projected <star>. With $k=R_p/R_\star$ and no <transit dilution>, the instantaneous <exoplanet transit depth> is exactly
$$
\delta=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\rm overlap}I\,dA_{\rm proj}}{\pi R_\star^2\overline I}.
$$
When $k\ll1$, the entire planetary disk is inside the stellar disk, and <specific intensity> varies little across that disk, <small-planet transit weighting> reduces to
$$
\delta\simeq k^2\frac{I(\mu_p)}{\overline I}
=k^2\frac{1-u+u\mu_p}{1-u/3},\qquad
\mu_p=\sqrt{1-z^2},\qquad z<1-k.
$$
Here $z$ is the projected centre separation divided by $R_\star$. The numerator samples the local <specific intensity>; the denominator normalizes by the whole <star>'s mean <specific intensity>. The small-size argument and the distinction between full overlap and ingress are explicit in the original analytic treatment. <Analytic Light Curves for Planetary Transit Searches by Kaisey Mandel and Eric Agol>[§2, p. L171, geometry; §5, p. L174, small-planet approximation restricted to the interior $z<1-p$]{ref}

For a short approximately straight crossing at constant transverse <velocity> $v_\perp$, write $x=v_\perp t/R_\star$ with $t=0$ at mid-transit, and let $b$ be the <transit impact parameter>. Then $z^2=x^2+b^2$ and $\mu_p=\sqrt{1-b^2-x^2}$. With stipulated $u=0.6$ and $k=0.05$, a central crossing has mid-transit <exoplanet transit depth> approximately $0.003125$, whereas $b=0.8$ gives $0.002375$. A uniform disk would give $k^2=0.0025$ for either fully overlapping crossing. The brighter central chord produces a deeper dip and the off-centre chord a shorter crossing at the same $v_\perp$.

At fixed $z<1$ away from the edge, a two-dimensional <Taylor's theorem> expansion over the symmetric planetary disk cancels the first <derivatives>; the first correction to the blocked <radiant flux> is of order $k^4$ if the second <derivatives> remain bounded. This error estimate is not uniform at the edge, where the radial <derivative> of the <linear limb-darkening law> diverges. Ingress, egress, a grazing <exoplanet transit>, and a crossing of a <starspot> require the overlap <integral> or a suitable more complete model. Inferring $k=\sqrt\delta$ without the local-to-mean <specific intensity> ratio can bias the estimated <planet> <radius>.

= Solar limb-darkening drift-scan experiment
{parent=Limb darkening}

A motivating measurement used the McMath solar <optical telescope> at Kitt Peak. The <Sun>'s image drifted across a $10\,\mathrm{mm}$-high, $0.2\,\mathrm{mm}$-wide <optical slit>; a double-pass <spectrograph> selected <wavelength> intervals from $0.0015\,\mathrm{nm}$ wide at $303\,\mathrm{nm}$ to $0.007\,\mathrm{nm}$ at $1099\,\mathrm{nm}$. An EMI 9750 QB <photodetector> or <silicon> <photodiode> supplied a digitized signal: $1\,\mathrm{kHz}$ samples were summed into 4096 positions per crossing, including dark and off-limb sky readings. <Solar limb darkening 1986–1990 (λλ303 to 1099 nm) by Heinz Neckel and Dietrich Labs>[§2, p. 93, instrumental compilation: slit dimensions, double pass, detectors, sampling and drift-curve layout; p. 113, acknowledgements identifying the telescope]{ref}

Controls included daily realignment of <optical telescope> mirrors, <wavelength> settings between <spectral lines> insensitive to the rotational <Doppler shift>, selection of detectors without detectable <hysteresis>, and measured electronic nonlinearity corrections below $0.1\%$ of maximum signal. Limb-position calibration combined centres of gravity of the edge <derivative> profiles with almanac semidiameters; the preliminary edge shifts were consistent with <astronomical seeing> of approximately $5$ <arcseconds> <full width at half maximum>. <Solar limb darkening 1986–1990 (λλ303 to 1099 nm) by Heinz Neckel and Dietrich Labs>[§2, pp. 93–94, precautions (1), (6)–(8); §3.1, p. 94, two-step limb positions and seeing estimate]{ref}

The decreasing centre-to-limb readout gives a <specific intensity> profile, constraining stratification of the <photosphere> under a <radiative transfer> model. <Astronomical seeing>, instrumental <scattering of light>, and <solar granulation> limit this inference; brightness alone does not uniquely determine <temperature> versus geometric height. These observations need not follow the stipulated <linear limb-darkening law>.

= Radiant exitance
{parent=Radiometry}
{title2=$M=M_e=d\Phi_{\rm emitted}/dA$}
{wiki}

<Radiant exitance> is total outward emitted <radiant flux> per actual emitting <area>, measured in $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. Integrate <radiance> over the outward <hemisphere>, keeping the <projected area> factor:
$$
M=\int_{\Omega_{\rm outward}}L(\theta,\varphi)\cos\theta\,d\Omega.
$$
If the <radiance> is direction-independent, the <spherical coordinates> element is $d\Omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi$, so
$$
M=L\int_0^{2\pi}\!d\varphi\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta=\pi L.
$$
The weighted <solid angle> is $\pi\,\mathrm{sr}$, although the whole <hemisphere> covers $2\pi\,\mathrm{sr}$. Directions near grazing contribute less because of the <projected area>. This geometric factor is the bridge between <Lambert's cosine law> and the total output of a diffuse patch. https://labs.phys.utk.edu/mbreinig/phys421/modules/m5/Radiometry.html[Breinig, Radiometry: hemispherical integration of radiance]{ref}.

For the uniform patch with $L=10\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}}$, <radiant exitance> is $M=10\pi\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. Its <area> $0.020\,\mathrm{m^2}$ gives total <radiant flux> $\Phi=MA=0.20\pi\,\mathrm W\approx0.628319\,\mathrm W$ over the outward <hemisphere>. This total differs from its <radiant intensity> $I(0)=0.20\,\mathrm{W\,sr^{-1}}$, which describes only one direction.

For an ideal <blackbody>, integrate <Planck's law> over <frequency> as well: $M=M_e=j^*=\sigma T^4$ by the <Stefan–Boltzmann law>. An ideal $6000\,\mathrm K$ emitting patch of <area> $1\,\mathrm{mm^2}$ emits about $73.4881\,\mathrm W$ outward. <Irradiance> counts incoming radiation separately; for purely emitted output the net outward exchange per <area> is $M-E$. Reflected output must also be included if the surface reflects incident radiation.

= Spectral radiant exitance
{parent=Radiant exitance}
{title2=$M_\nu=\frac c4U_\nu$}

Radiant <power> leaving a surface per surface <area> and per unit <frequency> or <wavelength>. Isotropic cavity radiation obeys $M_\nu=cU_\nu/4$; the factor $1/4$ combines the outward hemisphere and projected area.

= Irradiance
{parent=Radiometry}
{title2=$E=d\Phi_{\rm incident}/dA$}
{wiki}

<Irradiance> measures incident <radiant flux> per actual receiving <area>. Its units $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ match <radiant exitance>, but it counts the incoming side of the radiation exchange. Measure incoming <angles> from the <unit normal> pointing toward the incident hemisphere. Then
$$
E=\int_{\Omega_{\rm incident}}L_{\rm incoming}(\theta,\varphi)\cos\theta\,d\Omega.
$$
For $2\,\mathrm W$ distributed uniformly over $0.010\,\mathrm{m^2}$, $E=200\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. If the receiving plane is tilted $60^\circ$ in the same broad uniform parallel beam, the incident <power> per actual <area> is halved to $100\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. This receiving-surface <cosine> factor is geometric and does not assume <Lambertian reflectance>. Together, <irradiance> and <radiant exitance> describe how radiation supplies and removes <energy> at a surface, while <radiance> preserves the directional information.

= Spectral irradiance
{parent=Irradiance}
{title2=$E_\lambda,\ E_\nu$}

Incident <radiant flux> per unit area and unit <wavelength> or <frequency>. Integrating $E_\lambda$ over a wavelength interval gives the contribution to <irradiance> in that interval. Conversion between $E_\lambda$ and $E_\nu$ requires the absolute <Jacobian determinant> associated with $\nu=c/\lambda$, as for <spectral radiance>.

= Horizontal versus beam-normal solar irradiance
{parent=Irradiance}

A parallel beam incident at elevation $\alpha$ has horizontal irradiance $F_h=F_\perp\sin\alpha$. Its path optical depth in a plane-parallel atmosphere is $\tau_v/\sin\alpha$. Confusing these projections changes both attenuation and intercepted power.

= Radiometric absorptance
{parent=Radiometry}
{title2=$a=P_{\rm abs}/P_{\rm in}$}

<Radiometric absorptance> is the fraction of incident <radiant flux> converted to <heat> in the specified receiver: $P_{\rm abs}=aP_{\rm in}$, with $0\leq a\leq1$ for a passive receiver. Specify the entrance boundary, <wavelength>, incidence <angle> and receiver <temperature>. Radiation reflected or transmitted out of that receiver is not absorbed <power>. A cavity can improve <radiometric absorptance> by giving <light> repeated opportunities for <wave absorption>; a dark coating alone does not establish $a=1$.

For broadband <light>, <spectral radiant flux> matters: $P_{\rm abs}=\int a(\lambda)\Phi_{e,\lambda}(\lambda)\,d\lambda$. A single effective <radiometric absorptance> therefore depends on the incident <optical spectrum> as well as on the receiver. If the stated incident boundary lies before a window of known power transmission $\tau_w$, the receiver absorbs $a\tau_wP_{\rm before}$ when other optical losses are absent. Window loss and receiver <radiometric absorptance> are separate corrections.

= Electrical substitution radiometer
{parent=Radiometry}

= Electrical substitution radiometry
{synonym}

An <electrical substitution radiometer> measures <radiant flux> by replacing absorbed optical <power> with calibrated <Joule heating>. At fixed <temperature>, <feedback control> holds the receiver equally warm with the beam blocked or admitted: after corrections, absorbed optical <power> equals the reduction in supplied electrical heating. Incident <radiant flux> additionally requires <radiometric absorptance> and entrance-path corrections. This connects <radiometry> to electrical standards.<Spectroradiometric Detector Measurements: Ultraviolet, Visible, and Near-Infrared Detectors for Spectral Power by Thomas C. Larason and others>[§5.2, printed p. 30: electrical substitution and electrical standards]{ref}

Picture a small absorbing cavity connected to a colder reference block by a <radiometer thermal link>. A heater supplies <Joule heating>, a <thermometer> senses the receiver, and <feedback control> adjusts the heater. When <light> supplies some of the heating, the controller supplies less. The readout is the change in heater <power>, rather than the beam's brightness or its number of <photons>. NIST's institutional description gives a concrete receiver with a conical interior, absorbing coating and weak Kapton support, operated with a receiver heater and <thermometer>.<Pico-Watt ACR by National Institute of Standards and Technology>[Description, Design Parameters for Improved ACR Sensitivity; Key Design Features: conical receiver, coating, constant-temperature loop and thin-walled Kapton link]{ref}

= Electrical substitution heat balance
{parent=Electrical substitution radiometer}
{title2=$P_{\rm abs}=P_{h,d}-P_{h,l}$}

An explicit lumped model makes the cancellation understandable. Treat the receiver as having uniform <temperature> $T$, <heat capacity> $C$, heater <power> $P_h$ deposited in the receiver, net parasitic input $P_{\rm par}$, and outward <heat transfer> $Q_{\rm out}(T,T_b)$ to a reference at <temperature> $T_b$. With incident <radiant flux> $P_{\rm in}$ and <radiometric absorptance> $a$, <conservation of energy> gives
$$
C\frac{dT}{dt}=P_h+aP_{\rm in}+P_{\rm par}-Q_{\rm out}(T,T_b).
$$
The dark state has no admitted beam. Compare settled dark and light states, denoted $d$ and $l$; each has $dT/dt=0$. Subtracting their balances gives
$$
P_{\rm abs}=aP_{\rm in}=\Delta P_h+\Delta Q_{\rm out}-\Delta P_{\rm par},\qquad
\Delta P_h=P_{h,d}-P_{h,l},
$$
where $\Delta Q_{\rm out}=Q_{{\rm out},l}-Q_{{\rm out},d}$ and $\Delta P_{\rm par}=P_{{\rm par},l}-P_{{\rm par},d}$. At identical $T$ and $T_b$, with unchanged surroundings and parasitic heating, both correction differences vanish. Thus $P_{\rm abs}=\Delta P_h$, and $P_{\rm in}=\Delta P_h/a$. Notice the signs: extra stray <thermal radiation> admitted with the beam also reduces heater <power>, so it must be subtracted from that reduction before attributing the result to the intended beam.

Measure the heater's own terminal voltage $V_h$ and <electric current> $I_h$: for steady electrical heating, $P_h=V_hI_h=I_h^2R_h$, where $R_h$ is its <electrical resistance>. A supply voltage that includes wire voltage drops counts <Joule heating> in the leads too; only the portion deposited in the receiver belongs in this balance. Changes in that deposited lead <heat> belong in $\Delta P_{\rm par}$. <Optical-electrical heating non-equivalence> adds a further instrument-specific correction when the uniform-<temperature> model is insufficient.<Spectroradiometric Detector Measurements: Ultraviolet, Visible, and Near-Infrared Detectors for Spectral Power by Thomas C. Larason and others>[§5.2, printed p. 30: heater wires, window transmission and optical/electrical non-equivalence]{ref}

= Radiometer thermal link
{parent=Electrical substitution heat balance}
{title2=$Q_{\rm link}\simeq G(T-T_b),\ \tau=C/G$}

A <radiometer thermal link> is the intentional path for <thermal conduction> from receiver to reference block. Near a chosen operating point, write $Q_{\rm link}\simeq G(T-T_b)$ with local conductance $G$ in $\mathrm{W\,K^{-1}}$. A weaker link increases the steady <temperature> rise per added <power>, $\delta T\simeq\delta P/G$, but also increases the uncontrolled relaxation <time> $\tau=C/G$ when the link dominates other <heat transfer>. These are local linear approximations: actual <thermal conductivity> depends on <temperature>, and active <feedback control> introduces its own response. NIST explicitly describes the tradeoff between weaker links and smaller receiver <heat capacity>.<Pico-Watt ACR by National Institute of Standards and Technology>[Key Design Features, final paragraph: response time proportional to receiver heat capacity and inverse thermal conductance]{ref}

= Fixed-temperature electrical substitution example
{parent=Electrical substitution heat balance}
{title2=$P_{\rm in}=(2-1.005)/0.995\,\mathrm{mW}=1\,\mathrm{mW}$}

Stipulate a model receiver held at $T=5.4\,\mathrm K$ with a reference at $T_b=5.0\,\mathrm K$. Choose $G=5\,\mathrm{mW\,K^{-1}}$, so outward <thermal conduction> is $G(T-T_b)=2\,\mathrm{mW}$. Ignore other <heat transfer>, take $P_{\rm par}=0$ in both states, and stipulate perfect optical/electrical thermal equivalence. These values specify an original example; they are not measurements or claimed performance of a NIST instrument.

With the beam blocked, supply $P_{h,d}=2\,\mathrm{mW}$. With <light> admitted, the required heater <power> is $P_{h,l}=1.005\,\mathrm{mW}$. For stipulated <radiometric absorptance> $a=0.995$ at the chosen single <wavelength>,
$$
P_{\rm abs}=2-1.005=0.995\,\mathrm{mW},\qquad
P_{\rm in}=\frac{0.995\,\mathrm{mW}}{0.995}=1.000\,\mathrm{mW}.
$$
The illuminated receiver still gets $1.005+0.995=2\,\mathrm{mW}$ of heating. The remaining $0.005\,\mathrm{mW}$ of incident <radiant flux> escapes without heating it. Calling the measured heater reduction “incident <power>” would underestimate the stipulated incident <radiant flux> by $0.5\%$.

Two variations expose different pitfalls. First, if admitting the beam also adds $0.010\,\mathrm{mW}$ of stray heating while the same $0.995\,\mathrm{mW}$ is absorbed from the intended beam, the heater falls to $0.995\,\mathrm{mW}$. The apparent reduction is now $1.005\,\mathrm{mW}$; subtracting $\Delta P_{\rm par}=0.010\,\mathrm{mW}$ recovers the beam's absorbed <power>. Second, keep the original heater readings but stipulate a light-state receiver offset $\delta T=+1\,\mathrm{mK}$ at fixed reference <temperature>. The link carries an extra $G\delta T=0.005\,\mathrm{mW}$; the inferred absorbed <power> becomes $1.000\,\mathrm{mW}$, and incident <radiant flux> becomes $1.000/0.995\simeq1.005025\,\mathrm{mW}$. This second variation deliberately violates exact fixed-<temperature> substitution.

The heater can supply only nonnegative <power> in this model. Consequently an absorbed beam above the available dark heater <power> cannot be compensated at the chosen setpoint merely by reducing the heater. The operating point, link, or admitted <radiant flux> must change; zero heater demand is a range boundary, not evidence of greater <radiant flux> sensitivity.

= Optical-electrical heating non-equivalence
{parent=Electrical substitution radiometer}

<Optical-electrical heating non-equivalence> means that equal absorbed optical and electrical <powers> do not necessarily give identical <thermometer> responses. <Light> and the heater may deposit <heat> in different places, producing different <temperature gradients> and different routes of <thermal conduction> to the support. Holding one <thermometer> reading fixed need not hold every part of the cavity at the same <temperature>. This differs from incomplete <radiometric absorptance>: one concerns where absorbed <heat> goes, the other how much incident <light> becomes absorbed <heat>.

A useful accounting convention is
$$
P_{\rm in}=\frac{\Delta P_h+\Delta Q_{\rm out}-\Delta P_{\rm par}+\delta_{\rm ne}}{a},
$$
where the signed correction $\delta_{\rm ne}$ is determined by characterization of the instrument and represents the residual departure from the lumped <electrical substitution heat balance>. It is not a universal extra source of <energy>. Varying the beam location or heater location, comparing equivalent temperature excursions, and checking repeatable dark returns can test the assumptions; no single small correction proves that every other correction vanishes.

= Cryogenic radiometer
{parent=Electrical substitution radiometer}

A <cryogenic radiometer> uses low-<temperature> operation to improve <electrical substitution radiometry> through particular material and thermal properties. Lower <heat capacity> of a copper receiver permits a substantial cavity without proportionately slow relaxation; cold shields reduce unwanted <thermal radiation>, and heater leads with <superconductivity> can suppress resistive lead heating. These features do not make <radiometric absorptance> exactly one or abolish <optical-electrical heating non-equivalence>.<Spectroradiometric Detector Measurements: Ultraviolet, Visible, and Near-Infrared Detectors for Spectral Power by Thomas C. Larason and others>[§5.2, printed pp. 30–32: cryogenic advantages and remaining window/non-equivalence corrections]{ref}

Cold surroundings matter as much as a cold receiver. In a simplified gray-surface model, net outward <thermal radiation> is $\epsilon\sigma A(T^4-T_s^4)$, using <emissivity> $\epsilon$, <area> $A$, shield <temperature> $T_s$ and the <Stefan-Boltzmann constant> $\sigma$. Cold shields reduce the incoming term as well as the receiver's emission; cooling the receiver alone while leaving it exposed to warm surroundings does not remove incoming radiation. An entrance window still provides an optical and thermal path. <Vacuum> reduces gas-mediated <heat transfer>, while an intentional <radiometer thermal link> remains necessary. This model explains specific losses, rather than promising a universal improvement factor or perfect accuracy.

= Cryogenic laser-power substitution experiment
{parent=Cryogenic radiometer}

In NIST's 2001 comparison, the Laser-Optimized Cryogenic Radiometer held its cavity near $5.4\,\mathrm K$ and controlled the heat sink separately. Opening the beam reduced heater <power> at fixed cavity <temperature>; an AC bridge read the <temperature>. Tests used stabilized, spatially filtered <lasers> at $488$, $514$ and $633\,\mathrm{nm}$, with horizontal input and a movable <photodiode> transfer detector in the cavity's optical plane. The paper contrasts this with the High Accuracy Cryogenic Radiometer's optical heating followed by two electrical heating cycles and interpolation.<Comparison of Two Cryogenic Radiometers at NIST by Jeanne M. Houston and others>[§§2–3, printed pp. 642–643: apparatus, wavelengths, readout and the two substitution protocols]{ref}

The two laboratories compared calibrations of the same transfer detectors, and checked their spatial response. Alignment differences and transported dust limited that comparison; its agreement did not prove universal equivalence. Its correction tables retain window transmission, cavity <radiometric absorptance>, alignment, electrical readout and <optical-electrical heating non-equivalence>. The heat sink was controlled, but §3 does not specify its numerical setpoint: the $5.0\,\mathrm K$ reference in the preceding example is stipulated.<Comparison of Two Cryogenic Radiometers at NIST by Jeanne M. Houston and others>[§§3–5, printed pp. 643–646, especially Tables 2–3 and spatial-uniformity checks]{ref}

For a reproducible substitution measurement, record the beam's <wavelength> and entrance boundary, receiver and reference <temperatures>, heater terminal voltage and <electric current>, dark readings before and after illumination, and the settling rule. Check beam collection at the <aperture>, window transmission, <radiometric absorptance>, deposited lead heating, and sensitivity to beam position. Repeatability alone misses a stable correction error. <Photometric calibration> can subsequently use this <radiant flux> scale, but <photometry> still needs the appropriate spectral visual weighting; the cold receiver does not itself measure <luminous flux>.
