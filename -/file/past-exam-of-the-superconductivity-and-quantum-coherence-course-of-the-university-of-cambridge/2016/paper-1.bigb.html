= Solution
{parent=a}

An <Abrikosov vortex> combines a core of suppressed superconducting <order parameter>, circulating <electric current> and a $2\pi$ winding of the <quantum phase>. In the bulk limit a singly wound vortex carries the <superconducting flux quantum> $\Phi_0=h/(2e)$. More precisely it is the <fluxoid> that is quantized; appreciable current on the integration contour prevents identifying enclosed flux alone with the integer.

For a <type-II superconductor>, the <Ginzburg-Landau parameter> is $\kappa=\lambda/\xi>1/\sqrt2$. The <superconducting mixed state> allows vortices between the <lower critical magnetic field> and <upper critical magnetic field>. A short <superconducting coherence length> permits a high orbital upper field,
$$
\boxed{B_{c2}=\frac{\Phi_0}{2\pi\xi^2}.}
$$
Thus surviving a large applied field generally requires accommodating many vortices, not excluding flux everywhere. The <Abrikosov vortex lattice> provides their equilibrium arrangement in the simplest isotropic model. Material anisotropy, disorder and thermal fluctuations can change that arrangement and its stability. Spin-polarization limits can also constrain an actual upper field, beyond this orbital estimate.

Carrying a large transport <current density> is a separate problem. For a straight vortex with tangent $\widehat{\mathbf t}$, the driving <Lorentz force> per unit length is $\mathbf f_L=\mathbf J\times\Phi_0\widehat{\mathbf t}$. Vortex motion produces a mean <electric field> $\mathbf E=\mathbf B\times\mathbf v_L$ and hence dissipative power density $\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E$. A large $B_{c2}$ alone therefore does not imply a large usable <superconducting critical current>.

<Flux pinning> holds vortices at favorable defects: suppressing the order parameter costs less where superconductivity is already weakened. Precipitates, dislocations, grain boundaries and deliberately introduced damage can provide pinning, subject to material-specific trade-offs. A defect scale comparable with a core size can be effective; a defect that destroys connected superconducting paths is harmful. In a coarse force balance, a maximum pinning force density $F_p$ gives $J_cB\simeq F_p$ for transverse transport. <Vortex depinning> and thermally activated <flux creep> can produce a voltage below an ideal static threshold.

High-field magnet conductors consequently need both a large upper field and strong, persistent pinning while retaining mechanical integrity and electrical connectivity. <Niobium-titanium> and <niobium-tin> conductors illustrate why superconducting composition and microstructural processing are developed together. A critical-current measurement should specify field, temperature and voltage criterion; it is not simply a measurement of the temperature at which resistance first vanishes.

The <superconducting gap function> is a momentum-dependent pairing amplitude $\Delta(\mathbf k)$, including a phase as well as a magnitude. A fully gapped isotropic state and a state with symmetry-required nodes can have different low-temperature responses. <Heat capacity>, thermal transport, <London penetration depth> and tunnelling spectra probe the low-energy <quasiparticle> spectrum. Activated behavior suggests a nonzero minimum gap; power laws can indicate nodes. For example, clean line nodes give a penetration-depth correction linear in temperature, whereas disorder can change the apparent power.

Such magnitude-sensitive probes require care: impurities, several bands, anisotropic full gaps and surface states can imitate or modify simple expectations. A node does not by itself establish a particular sign pattern, and the absence of a coherence peak is not a complete symmetry determination. The strongest identification combines momentum-sensitive spectra with <phase-sensitive superconducting gap experiments>.

For an illustrative $d_{x^2-y^2}$ state, $\Delta(\varphi)\propto\cos2\varphi$ changes sign under a $90^\circ$ rotation. A <Josephson junction> to a conventional s-wave reference can turn that sign into an intrinsic phase shift. In a corner <DC SQUID> with junctions on orthogonal crystal faces, one branch can acquire an extra $\pi$ relative to the other. For equal junctions and negligible loop inductance,
$$
I_c(\Phi)=2I_0\left|\cos\left(\frac{\pi\Phi}{\Phi_0}+\frac{\delta_{\rm int}}2\right)\right|.
$$
Changing $\delta_{\rm int}$ from zero to $\pi$ shifts the interference pattern by half a flux period. The 1993 YBCO–Pb experiment compared phase-coherent junction geometries and found evidence for that sign reversal, while explicitly discussing SQUID asymmetry and trapped vortices. Those controls matter: an arbitrary shifted trace is not by itself a proof of pairing symmetry.<Experimental determination of the superconducting pairing state in YBCO by D. A. Wollman and others>[1993, phase coherence of YBCO–Pb dc SQUIDs; abstract identifies asymmetry and vortex-trapping qualifications]{ref}

A <tricrystal half-flux experiment> arranges grain-boundary junctions so that a sign-changing order parameter produces an odd total $\pi$ shift around a ring. Fluxoid consistency then favors a half-integer flux state, with the detailed spontaneous flux depending on loop inductance and junction strengths. Choosing comparison geometries without that frustration and controlling trapped external flux separates the pairing-phase effect from ordinary flux trapping.<Pairing symmetry and flux quantization in a tricrystal superconducting ring by C. C. Tsuei and others>[1994 primary tricrystal-ring pairing-symmetry test]{ref}

The conclusion is strongest when the same candidate symmetry explains nodes, crystal-axis dependence and these phase-sensitive interference results. The phase-sensitive observation distinguishes a sign-changing gap from a merely anisotropic positive gap; no single temperature power law supplies that distinction.

The <two-fluid model of superfluidity> separates mass current into a <superfluid fraction> and a <normal component of a superfluid>:
$$
\mathbf j=\rho_s\mathbf v_s+\rho_n\mathbf v_n,\qquad \rho=\rho_s+\rho_n.
$$
The normal component carries entropy and responds to shear viscosity; an ideal superfluid flow is entropy-free. These are interpenetrating hydrodynamic responses, not two permanently distinguishable sets of atoms. Thermal <elementary excitations> carry momentum relative to the condensate and generate the normal response.

The <Andronikashvili experiment> gives an operational picture. A closely spaced stack of disks oscillates torsionally in He-II. Viscous coupling entrains the normal response between disks, changing the oscillator's inertia, while vortex-free superfluid need not follow the rotation. The temperature-dependent inertia measures the normal component under the apparatus's coupling conditions. Counterflow also permits <second sound>, primarily an entropy/temperature wave rather than the ordinary density wave of first sound.

<Condensate depletion> is a different quantity. The condensate fraction counts occupation of a single-particle mode, while $\rho_s$ measures the response to phase gradients or motion. In a weak three-dimensional homogeneous Bose gas at zero temperature,
$$
\frac{n-n_0}{n}\simeq\frac8{3\sqrt\pi}\sqrt{na_s^3},
$$
so interactions produce quantum depletion even without thermal excitations. In a translation-invariant Galilean system at zero temperature, the superfluid mass density can nevertheless equal the total density. He-II has strong interaction-induced depletion but a nearly complete zero-temperature superfluid response. Lattices, disorder and finite temperature require their own response calculation; condensate and superfluid fractions should not be equated in general.

The <Landau criterion> follows by considering an excitation of momentum $\mathbf p$ in a moving fluid. Its laboratory-frame creation cost can be $\epsilon(\mathbf p)-\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf p$. A necessary energetic condition against this decay channel is
$$
\boxed{v<v_L,\qquad v_L=\min_{p>0}\frac{\epsilon(p)}p.}
$$
For the low-momentum <Bogoliubov spectrum> of a weakly interacting Bose gas, $\epsilon\simeq c_sp$, giving the sound-speed threshold for that channel. The phonon–roton spectrum of He-II can instead have its minimum ratio near the roton region. Vortex nucleation, boundaries and a moving obstacle can lower a measured critical velocity; the ideal excitation criterion is not a guarantee of dissipation-free flow at every smaller speed in every apparatus. An ideal noninteracting Bose gas has a condensate but quadratic excitations with $v_L=0$, another distinction between condensation and robust superfluidity.

A cold-gas test moved a blue-detuned laser beam through a trapped condensate and measured heating as its speed changed. The repulsive optical obstacle supplies a controlled perturbation; strong heating appeared above a threshold. Its interpretation must account for the inhomogeneous cloud and excitation/nucleation mechanism, rather than identify every observed threshold with the central sound speed.<Evidence for a Critical Velocity in a Bose-Einstein Condensed Gas by C. Raman and others>[1999 abstract: moving blue-detuned laser beam and onset of strong heating]{ref}

A <Josephson junction> weakly couples two superconducting condensates. In a consistent orientation convention, its <gauge-invariant Josephson phase> $\delta$ obeys
$$
I=I_c\sin\delta,\qquad \dot\delta=\frac{2eV}{\hbar},\qquad
E_J=\frac{\hbar I_c}{2e}.
$$
The <DC Josephson effect> permits a current at zero voltage; the <AC Josephson effect> gives oscillations at frequency $2eV/h$. The junction's energy is $-E_J\cos\delta$. Adding a capacitance gives a charging scale $E_C=e^2/(2C)$ and, in the simplest isolated-island model, Hamiltonian $4E_C(n-n_g)^2-E_J\cos\delta$. Competition between phase locking and charge fluctuations permits quantized circuit dynamics, rather than an ordinary linear resistor.

Two junctions in a loop form a <DC SQUID>. When junctions are equal and loop self-inductance is negligible, their phase difference is fixed by external flux and their currents interfere:
$$
\boxed{I_c(\Phi)=2I_0\left|\cos\frac{\pi\Phi}{\Phi_0}\right|.}
$$
This supplies sensitive magnetometry and flux-tunable circuit elements. Unequal junctions leave nonzero minima; finite inductance and screening change the simple pattern. The phase-sensitive gap test in the preceding alternative adds an internal pairing-phase offset to this same interference principle.

<Matter-wave interference> also compares coherent complex amplitudes. For overlapping condensates with local densities $n_1,n_2$,
$$
n(\mathbf r)=n_1+n_2+2\sqrt{n_1n_2}\cos\big(\Delta\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r+\Delta\theta\big).
$$
An atomic interferometer can split a trapped condensate, evolve its two components, release them and image the overlapping density by absorption of a probe beam. For two initially separated sources a distance $d$ apart, free expansion for time $t$ gives an approximate far-field fringe spacing $ht/(md)$. The measured fringe phase encodes relative phase and accumulated dynamical phase; population imbalance lowers visibility. Interference between expanding condensates was an important demonstration of atomic coherence.<Nobel lecture: When atoms behave as waves: Bose-Einstein condensation and the atom laser by Wolfgang Ketterle>[Condensate interference and absorption images of overlapping expanding clouds]{ref}

Josephson oscillation requires an ongoing weak link that exchanges particles; interference after release need not have such a link. Independent condensates can show fringes with a different relative phase in each realization, so a single image is not evidence of a reproducible phase reference over many runs. In both settings the useful object is relative phase, but superconducting circuits also couple electrically to magnetic flux and charge, while neutral-atom devices accumulate matter-wave phase from motion and external potentials.

An <elementary excitation> is an approximately independent low-energy normal mode above a many-body equilibrium state. A <Bogoliubov transformation> mixes creation and annihilation operators to diagonalize a quadratic fluctuation Hamiltonian. For weakly interacting bosons,
$$
E_k=\sqrt{\varepsilon_k(\varepsilon_k+2gn_0)},\qquad \varepsilon_k=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m},
$$
interpolating between sound and particle-like motion. Bosonic canonical commutators require $|u_k|^2-|v_k|^2=1$. In <BCS theory>, fermionic particle–hole mixtures instead have $|u_k|^2+|v_k|^2=1$ and excitation energies $E_k=\sqrt{\xi_k^2+|\Delta_k|^2}$. These fermionic quasiparticles must be distinguished from collective amplitude and phase oscillations of the pair field.

For the stated dimensionless first-order equation, a nonzero uniform static solution obeys $(|\psi_0|^2-1)\psi_0=0$. Thus **$\psi_0=e^{i\theta_0}$**. The zero field is also stationary, but is not the condensed minimum of the local potential $\beta(|\psi|^2-1)^2/2$.

Choose $\theta_0=0$. To linear order, $\psi=1+\rho/2+i\theta$, so $|\psi|^2-1=\rho$. Substitution and separation of real and imaginary parts give
$$
\frac i2\dot\rho-\dot\theta=-\frac12\rho''-i\theta''+\beta\rho,
\qquad
\boxed{\dot\rho=-2\theta'',\quad \dot\theta=\frac12\rho''-\beta\rho.}
$$
Differentiating the first relation and using the second yields
$$
\ddot\rho=-\rho''''+2\beta\rho''.
$$
The same elimination holds for $\theta$. A plane wave $e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=k^4+2\beta k^2,\qquad \omega=|k|\sqrt{k^2+2\beta}.}
$$
The density and phase components share this frequency because of <density-phase conjugacy>. They are coupled components of one positive-frequency <Bogoliubov mode>, not two independent excitations that happen to have equal energies. At $k=0$, a constant phase change is a zero mode; the nonzero-$k$ elimination should not be used to assign an independent oscillation to a uniform density change.

Replacing the first-order time derivative by $-\gamma\ddot\psi$ changes the number and structure of dynamical degrees of freedom. The linearized real and imaginary equations now decouple:
$$
-\frac\gamma2\ddot\rho=-\frac12\rho''+\beta\rho,\qquad
-\gamma\ddot\theta=-\theta''.
$$
The <amplitude and phase modes of a second-order complex field> consequently have
$$
\boxed{\omega_\rho^2=\frac{k^2+2\beta}{\gamma},\qquad
\omega_\theta^2=\frac{k^2}{\gamma}.}
$$
The amplitude mode has a nonzero gap $\sqrt{2\beta/\gamma}$; the phase mode is gapless with speed $1/\sqrt\gamma$. Frequencies are nonnegative, so the printed expression $k/\sqrt\gamma$ is understood for $k\ge0$ or replaced by $|k|/\sqrt\gamma$.

In the complex plane, an amplitude oscillation moves radially relative to the chosen equilibrium $\psi=1$, and a phase oscillation moves tangentially, or along a short unit-circle arc beyond linear order. A first-order coupled mode combines them in quadrature. For a fixed spatial factor and $k\ne0$, one possible parametrization is
$$
\rho=R\cos\omega t,\qquad
\theta=-\frac{\omega R}{2k^2}\sin\omega t,
$$
so $\delta\psi=\rho/2+i\theta$ traces a small ellipse about $1$. Choose $R$ small enough that both components remain within the linear approximation.

{title=Coupled density–phase motion versus separate amplitude and phase branches}
{description=Original linearized models with $\beta=\gamma=1$. The first-order trajectory is shown at a fixed position for $k=1$; the second-order trajectories are separate possible modes, not simultaneous motions of one excitation.}

A weak neutral Bose condensate realizes the first-order structure and its sound-like <Bogoliubov spectrum>. A particle–hole-symmetric effective complex field can realize the second-order structure, with a gapless <Goldstone mode> and a distinct <amplitude mode>. A neutral BCS superfluid likewise has collective phase and amplitude responses, although the amplitude response near pair-breaking energy need not be a sharp undamped particle; its fermionic quasiparticles are separate excitations.

In a charged bulk superconductor, long-range Coulomb interactions and electromagnetic dynamics remove the neutral sound-like phase branch from the low-frequency spectrum. The <Anderson-Higgs mechanism in a superconductor> produces a longitudinal plasma mode, schematically $\omega^2\simeq\omega_p^2+c_s^2k^2$ in an appropriate long-wavelength model. The amplitude scale is typically associated with $2\Delta/\hbar$ in a simple BCS regime, with damping and mixing dependent on material and symmetry. One should not simply retain the neutral Goldstone dispersion after charging the field, nor identify the static Ginzburg-Landau functional alone with a complete time-dependent microscopic theory.

<Ginzburg-Landau theory of superconductivity> organizes a continuous superconducting transition through a slowly varying complex <order parameter>. In the simplest isotropic single-component SI model,
$$
F-F_n=\int d^3x\left[\alpha|\psi|^2+\frac\beta2|\psi|^4+\frac{|(-i\hbar\nabla-q\mathbf A)\psi|^2}{2m_*}+\frac{|\mathbf B|^2}{2\mu_0}\right],
\qquad \mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A.
$$
Here $\beta>0$, $m_*>0$, $\alpha$ changes sign at $T_c$, and $q=-2e$ fixes a charge/phase convention. The phenomenological normalization of $\psi$ determines the coefficient values; it should not be confused without qualification with a literal density of independently identifiable pairs.

<Microscopic derivation of Ginzburg-Landau theory> expands the BCS response near $T_c$ in small gap amplitude and slow spatial gradients. It identifies the complex order parameter with a scaled pair amplitude, supplies the coefficients from the electronic microscopic parameters, and explains the pair charge in the gauge coupling. The agreement is a controlled near-transition limit, not a statement that the truncated local functional remains exact at all temperatures. <Lev Gor'kov> established this connection explicitly in 1959.<Microscopic Derivation of the Ginzburg-Landau Equations in the Theory of Superconductivity by Lev Gor'kov>[1959 abstract and derivation: near-$T_c$ reduction and twice-electron charge]{ref}

Below the transition, minimization gives $|\psi_0|^2=-\alpha/\beta$ and condensation-energy density $\alpha^2/(2\beta)$. A variation with respect to $\psi^*$ gives
$$
\frac{(-i\hbar\nabla-q\mathbf A)^2}{2m_*}\psi+\alpha\psi+\beta|\psi|^2\psi=0.
$$
Variation with respect to $\mathbf A$ gives the <supercurrent>
$$
\mathbf j=\frac{q\hbar}{m_*}\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\nabla\psi)-\frac{q^2}{m_*}|\psi|^2\mathbf A,
$$
coupled to the <magnetostatic Ampère law>. For nearly uniform amplitude this reduces to <London screening> and the <Meissner effect>. The theory supplies two different length scales:
$$
\xi^2=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_*|\alpha|},\qquad
\lambda^2=\frac{m_*}{\mu_0q^2|\psi_0|^2},\qquad \kappa=\frac\lambda\xi.
$$
The amplitude can vary over the <superconducting coherence length>, whereas the <magnetic field> is screened over the <London penetration depth>.

The surface-energy distinction at $\kappa=1/\sqrt2$ explains why type-I and type-II behavior are possible within the same framework. In the type-II regime, quantized winding produces an <Abrikosov vortex> with a finite core and circulating current. Periodic solutions yield an <Abrikosov vortex lattice>. Linearization about the normal state gives $B_{c2}=\Phi_0/(2\pi\xi^2)$, while core and field energetics determine the lower entry field. These predictions connect a spatial field theory to measurable vortex patterns and critical fields, beyond a spatially uniform BCS gap equation. Thin-film critical-field observations helped motivate the large-$\kappa$ regime; magnetic-decoration experiments subsequently made vortex positions visible.<Nobel Lecture: Type-II superconductors and the vortex lattice by Alexei Alexeyevich Abrikosov>[pp.975–977, film motivation, mixed-state solutions and experimental vortex evidence]{ref}

These phenomena also enable applications. Type-II conductors support high-field magnets, but <flux pinning> is needed to limit vortex-motion losses. Phase coherence and fluxoid quantization permit persistent-current loops and interference devices. A weak link supplies <Josephson coupling energy>, allowing SQUID magnetometry and nonlinear quantum circuits; a junction's detailed current–phase law requires a weak-link model, rather than following automatically from the spatially uniform bulk functional. Microstructure can raise a useful critical current without greatly changing the equilibrium upper field, illustrating the difference between thermodynamic stability and transport performance.

The simple functional has identifiable limits. Far below $T_c$, the small-gap expansion is uncontrolled; rapidly varying fields can require nonlocal microscopic electrodynamics. Very near a transition in a fluctuating low-dimensional or strongly anisotropic material, mean-field minimization can miss important thermal or quantum fluctuations. Unconventional or multiband pairing can require several order-parameter components and additional gradient couplings. Strong fields can introduce spin-polarization pair breaking omitted by the orbital model. Finally, the static functional predicts equilibrium extrema, not quasiparticle spectra, dissipation, vortex velocities or a unique amplitude-mode frequency without further dynamical input. These are reasons to choose an appropriate extension or a microscopic calculation, not reasons to discard the successful near-transition predictions.
