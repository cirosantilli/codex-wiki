= Solution
{parent=a}

A <Josephson junction> converts a relative condensate phase into a measurable current. For superconductors the relevant coherent object is a <Cooper pair>, with electric charge $q=-2e$. Its <gauge-invariant Josephson phase> is
$$
\delta=\phi_2-\phi_1-\frac q\hbar\int_1^2\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l.
$$
With a consistent directed-current convention, the weak-link relation is $I_s=I_c\sin\delta$. A voltage $V=U_2-U_1$ gives $\dot\delta=2eV/\hbar$, so a constant voltage produces the <AC Josephson effect> at <frequency> $2e|V|/h$. For neutral atom reservoirs the phase evolution instead follows their chemical-potential difference, $\hbar\dot\delta=\mu_1-\mu_2$ with the displayed phase labeling. The current transfers atoms rather than electric charge, but still probes coherent matter-wave amplitudes.

A <DC SQUID> provides two coherent paths around a superconducting loop. Neglect loop self-inductance, take equal junction critical currents $I_0$, and let $\Phi$ be the enclosed flux. The relative junction phase differs by $2\pi\Phi/\Phi_0$, with <superconducting flux quantum> $\Phi_0=h/(2e)$. Adding the two sinusoidal currents and maximizing over their common phase gives
$$
\boxed{I_{c,\rm loop}(\Phi)=2I_0\left|\cos\frac{\pi\Phi}{\Phi_0}\right|.}
$$
For unequal junctions the corresponding result is $\sqrt{I_1^2+I_2^2+2I_1I_2\cos(2\pi\Phi/\Phi_0)}$. Finite inductance and junction asymmetry alter the ideal modulation, so a measured minimum need not be zero. Flux changes a phase difference between the paths; the detector reads the resulting critical-current or voltage modulation. The <Jaklevic Josephson interference experiment> established superconducting <interference> experimentally.<Quantum Interference Effects in Josephson Tunneling by R. C. Jaklevic and others>[1964 primary report]{ref}

A <bosonic Josephson junction> uses two weakly linked Bose-Einstein condensates. In the <Albiez double-well Josephson experiment>, the population distribution was measured in situ and the relative phase inferred from <interference> fringes. The apparatus therefore tests both number transfer and phase coherence, rather than interpreting a density oscillation alone as a phase measurement. Interactions make the dynamics nonlinear: large imbalance can produce self-trapping instead of simple tunneling oscillations.<Direct Observation of Tunneling and Nonlinear Self-Trapping in a Single Bosonic Josephson Junction by Michael Albiez and others>[2005 abstract: double-well implementation, density measurement, phase from fringes and nonlinear self-trapping]{ref}

The usefulness is interferometric sensitivity: a small phase perturbation can change a measurable current. It requires a coherent weak link, controlled phase noise and the appropriate particle's phase evolution; a generic insulating barrier between incoherent reservoirs is not already a matter-wave interferometer.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <superconducting critical current> depends on <temperature>, <magnetic field>, sample geometry and the chosen dissipation criterion. For a magnet, a large <superconducting critical current density> matters because it permits a useful winding current with manageable conductor <volume>. The intrinsic depairing limit is an upper bound, but many practical type-II conductors become dissipative first through vortex motion.

A current exerts a transverse <force> per unit vortex length $\mathbf f_L=\mathbf J\times\boldsymbol\Phi_0$. Moving flux produces an <electric field> and dissipation. <Flux pinning> holds vortices at energetically favorable defects, allowing a larger transport current before motion. Useful conductors therefore need pinning as well as a sufficiently high transition <temperature> and upper critical field. Simply increasing disorder indefinitely is not a prescription: it can damage connectivity or superconductivity. High-angle grain boundaries in some copper-oxide materials also act as weak links, motivating controlled texture and connected current paths.

Conventional magnet conductors include <niobium-titanium> and <niobium-tin>; high-temperature coated conductors offer other operating regimes. Their performance must be compared on a <superconducting critical surface> in <temperature>, field and current density, with strain and thermal constraints also relevant. A high self-field current measured in a short sample does not by itself establish the current available throughout a high-field coil.

A <superconducting magnet> produces a strong, stable field without the normal winding's steady resistive power loss. Applications include <magnetic resonance imaging>, <nuclear magnetic resonance> and charged-particle beam steering or focusing in accelerators. A persistent-current loop is valuable when field stability matters. Refrigeration still requires power, and the magnet stores magnetic <energy>, approximately $LI^2/2$ in a linear inductance model. A local transition to a resistive state can release that stored <energy> as heat, so <superconducting quench> detection and protection are essential to operation. These requirements connect the microscopic vortex physics with useful engineering performance.

= Solution

The superconducting order is a coherent pair amplitude, schematically $\Delta\propto\langle c_\downarrow c_\uparrow\rangle=|\Delta|e^{i\phi}$. Under a global <fermion> phase change $c\mapsto e^{i\alpha}c$, it acquires phase $2\alpha$. A symmetry-broken thermodynamic description selects a pair phase. This must be distinguished from electromagnetic gauge freedom: a local gauge transformation changes the phase and potentials together and is a redundancy of the description, not an observable symmetry that the material literally violates.

A conventional centrosymmetric <s-wave superconductor> usually has an even-parity orbital pair state and a <spin singlet state>, consistent with the antisymmetry of two <fermions>. Its ordinary zero-field equilibrium state preserves time reversal. The electromagnetic coupling makes phase dynamics and screening part of the <Anderson-Higgs mechanism in a superconductor>; the <Meissner effect> is a measurable consequence. The condensate phase description does not imply that electric charge is no longer conserved.

In an <unconventional superconductor>, the gap can transform nontrivially under the crystal point group or have other nonstandard pairing structure. A common example is <d-wave superconductivity>, with sign changes and possible nodes in the gap. This does not automatically imply an additional observable fourfold-rotation breaking: a rotation that reverses the gap's sign can be accompanied by a gauge phase change. A nematic superconducting state instead breaks an actual crystal rotation symmetry in its gauge-invariant responses.

A chiral combination such as $p_x+ip_y$ can break time reversal, while a spin-triplet <order parameter> can also distinguish spin directions when the applicable spin-rotation and spin-orbit constraints permit it. <Helium-3 superfluidity> illustrates paired-fermion order with richer spin and orbital structure. These possibilities must be tested rather than equated: **unconventional pairing does not by itself mean spin-triplet pairing, time-reversal breaking, or nematicity**. Gap angular dependence, phase-sensitive <interference>, spin response and spontaneous magnetic signatures probe different symmetry questions. The <phase-sensitive test of d-wave pairing> is more specific than inferring a gap sign merely from its magnitude.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For a neutral condensate with <order parameter> $\psi=\sqrt{n_s}e^{i\phi}$, the <superfluid velocity> away from singularities is $\mathbf v_s=\hbar\nabla\phi/m$. Single-valuedness around a closed contour gives $\oint\nabla\phi\cdot d\mathbf l=2\pi n$, hence <quantization of circulation>:
$$
\boxed{\oint\mathbf v_s\cdot d\mathbf l=n\frac hm.}
$$
The <mass> is that of the condensed object: an atom for a bosonic atomic superfluid, or a pair for a paired-fermion condensate. A nonzero winding cannot be continuously removed while the <order parameter> remains nonzero throughout a spanning region. A vortex allows its phase to wind while its amplitude vanishes in a core.

For a charged condensate, minimal coupling gives $m\mathbf v_s+q\mathbf A=\hbar\nabla\phi$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\Phi+\frac mq\oint\mathbf v_s\cdot d\mathbf l=n\frac hq.}
$$
Using $\mathbf j_s=n_sq\mathbf v_s$ and $\lambda_L^2=m/(\mu_0n_sq^2)$ gives the <fluxoid and flux quantization in a superconductor> relation $\Phi+\mu_0\lambda_L^2\oint\mathbf j_s\cdot d\mathbf l=nh/q$. The signs of oriented flux and winding depend on the signed pair charge; the magnitude of the elementary superconducting flux quantum is **$\Phi_0=h/(2e)$**. Flux alone is quantized when the current term is negligible on a contour deep in the bulk. A thin ring or a contour near a core can instead require the full fluxoid.

In a neutral superfluid, vortex flow carries <kinetic energy> and <angular momentum>. Rotation can favor vortices because equilibrium minimizes the rotating-frame quantity $E-\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot\mathbf L$. The core size is set by a coherence or healing scale. In a type-II superconductor, an <Abrikosov vortex> carries quantized <magnetic flux> and circulating supercurrent; its core scale is the <coherence length> and its magnetic screening scale is the penetration depth. Between the lower and upper critical fields, a vortex lattice can be favored. <Flux pinning> affects how that structure responds to current. Type-I bulk superconductors instead favor macroscopic normal/superconducting separation rather than the usual stable dilute Abrikosov-vortex regime.

= Solution

<Condensate depletion> means particles outside a macroscopically occupied bosonic single-particle mode. Interactions can deplete that mode even at zero <temperature>: the <Bogoliubov transformation> mixes creation and <annihilation operators>, so its <quasiparticle> vacuum contains particles at nonzero <momentum>. A weak homogeneous three-dimensional gas with repulsive scattering length $a_s$ has leading <quantum depletion of a Bose condensate>
$$
\frac{n-n_0}{n}=\frac8{3\sqrt\pi}\sqrt{na_s^3},\qquad na_s^3\ll1.
$$
Thermal <quasiparticles> add another contribution. Depletion and normal-fluid density are distinct: a Galilean-invariant fluid can have quantum depletion while retaining its full superfluid <mass> response at zero <temperature>.

For the fermionic pairing problem, mean-field decoupling of the <reduced BCS pairing Hamiltonian> introduces the anomalous amplitude $\Delta$. Each paired Nambu block has diagonal entries $\xi_{\mathbf k},-\xi_{\mathbf k}$ and off-diagonal pairing entries of magnitude $\Delta$. A phase convention with negative off-diagonal entries gives eigenvalues $\pm E_{\mathbf k}$, where $E_{\mathbf k}=\sqrt{\xi_{\mathbf k}^2+\Delta^2}$. Its canonical Bogoliubov rotation yields the <BCS coherence factors>
$$
u_{\mathbf k}=\frac1{\sqrt{1+s_{\mathbf k}^2}},\qquad
v_{\mathbf k}=\frac{s_{\mathbf k}}{\sqrt{1+s_{\mathbf k}^2}},\qquad
\frac{v_{\mathbf k}}{u_{\mathbf k}}=\frac{E_{\mathbf k}-\xi_{\mathbf k}}\Delta.
$$
The partner transformation has the complementary sign required by fermionic anticommutation. At zero <temperature> all positive-energy <BCS quasiparticle> modes are empty. Expanding the supplied <electron> operator therefore gives
$$
\langle a^\dagger_{\mathbf k\sigma}a_{\mathbf k\sigma}\rangle
=u_{\mathbf k}^2\langle\alpha^\dagger\alpha\rangle
+v_{\mathbf k}^2\langle\alpha\alpha^\dagger\rangle=v_{\mathbf k}^2.
$$
Since $E_{\mathbf k}^2-\xi_{\mathbf k}^2=\Delta^2$,
$$
\boxed{n_{\mathbf k\sigma}=\frac{s_{\mathbf k}^2}{1+s_{\mathbf k}^2}
=\frac12\left(1-\frac{\xi_{\mathbf k}}{\sqrt{\xi_{\mathbf k}^2+\Delta^2}}\right).}
$$
The <electron> occupation is close to one far below the normal-state <Fermi surface>, equals one half at $\xi=0$, and decays toward zero above it. Pairing smooths the Fermi step over an <energy> range of order $\Delta$. The positive-energy <quasiparticle> occupation is instead zero everywhere at this <temperature>; the <electron> sea and the <quasiparticle> vacuum are different descriptions of the same state. At finite <temperature>, $n_{\mathbf k\sigma}=[1-(\xi/E)\tanh(E/(2k_BT))]/2$ in the same mean-field model.

For a three-dimensional parabolic normal band, the total <density of states> including both spins is $D(\mu)=3N/(2\mu)$. In the weak-gap limit, replace it by this constant across the narrow pairing region and use $\mu\simeq E_F$. The number of <electrons> above the normal-state <Fermi surface> is
$$
\Delta N\simeq D(\mu)\int_0^\infty\frac12\left(1-\frac\xi{\sqrt{\xi^2+\Delta^2}}\right)d\xi.
$$
The integral is $[\xi-\sqrt{\xi^2+\Delta^2}]_0^\infty/2=\Delta/2$. Therefore the <BCS population above the normal-state Fermi surface> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{\Delta N}{N}\simeq\frac{3\Delta}{4\mu}.}
$$
The constant-density-of-states approximation and a small gap relative to the <chemical potential> are essential. This is the question's explicitly defined fermionic population measure; it is not the fraction of <electrons> outside a single bosonic condensate orbital.

For an order-of-magnitude mercury estimate, use $T_c\simeq4.2\,\mathrm K$ and the weak-coupling scale $\Delta_0\simeq1.76k_BT_c\simeq0.64\,\mathrm{meV}$, with a representative metallic $\mu\simeq7\,\mathrm{eV}$. These input approximations give **$\Delta N/N\sim7\times10^{-5}$, of order $10^{-4}$**. The use of a weak-coupling gap is an estimate, not an assertion of mercury's exact measured gap.

For a copper oxide, an illustrative gap $20\,\mathrm{meV}$ and effective chemical-potential scale $0.5\,\mathrm{eV}$ give **about three per cent** in this formula. Choosing $0.2$–$1\,\mathrm{eV}$ instead gives roughly $7.5$–$1.5$ per cent. These are explicit example inputs: the paper specifies no compound, doping or band parameters from which a unique number follows. A real cuprate's anisotropic gap, quasi-two-dimensional band and strong correlations require a band-resolved occupation integral, so the reliable contrast is the much larger gap-to-electronic-energy scale, rather than a universal cuprate percentage.

A bosonic <coherent state> is an eigenstate of its annihilation operator,
$$
|\alpha\rangle=e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}e^{\alpha a^\dagger}|0\rangle
=e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}}|n\rangle,
\qquad a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle.
$$
Its mean occupation is $|\alpha|^2$ and its number variance is also $|\alpha|^2$, giving a Poisson number distribution. It has a well-defined complex mean field but fluctuating particle number. Its overlap with another coherent state is $\exp[-(|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2)/2+\alpha^*\beta]$, so distinct amplitudes need not be orthogonal. For large occupation, a phase-space wave packet has small relative number fluctuations; this does not require an exact self-adjoint phase operator conjugate to a number bounded below.

For a bosonic field, a useful <coherent-field condensate variational state> is
$$
|\psi\rangle=e^{-\bar N/2}\exp\left[\int d^3r\,\psi(\mathbf r)\widehat\Psi^\dagger(\mathbf r)\right]|0\rangle,
\qquad \bar N=\int d^3r\,|\psi|^2.
$$
It obeys $\langle\widehat\Psi(\mathbf r)\rangle=\psi(\mathbf r)$. For a contact-interacting dilute Bose gas, its normal-ordered <energy> expectation is
$$
E[\psi]=\int d^3r\left[\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}|\nabla\psi|^2+V|\psi|^2+\frac g2|\psi|^4\right].
$$
Minimizing $E-\mu\bar N$ gives the stationary <Gross-Pitaevskii equation>, $[-\hbar^2\nabla^2/(2m)+V+g|\psi|^2]\psi=\mu\psi$. Its phase gradient determines superfluid flow, while weak-link phase differences give Josephson currents. The complex microscopic expectation supplies the order field used by a mean-field description of a bosonic superfluid.

An exactly fixed-number condensate can instead use the <Hartree product state> $(a^\dagger_\varphi)^N|0\rangle/\sqrt{N!}$. Its one-point field expectation vanishes by number conservation, but its one-body <density matrix> has a macroscopic eigenvalue. <Off-diagonal long-range order> expresses that number-conserving criterion. Selecting a phase is a useful thermodynamic symmetry-broken description; it is not a claim that every finite isolated condensate literally has indefinite total number. A plain coherent product also misses interaction-induced quantum depletion, which needs correlations such as those in the Bogoliubov <quasiparticle> vacuum.

In a spin-singlet superconductor, the corresponding variational state is the <BCS ground state>
$$
|\mathrm{BCS}\rangle=\prod_{\mathbf k}\left(u_{\mathbf k}
+v_{\mathbf k}a^\dagger_{\mathbf k\uparrow}a^\dagger_{-\mathbf k\downarrow}\right)|0\rangle,
\qquad |u_{\mathbf k}|^2+|v_{\mathbf k}|^2=1.
$$
Each block superposes an empty and an occupied pair state. Where $u_{\mathbf k}\ne0$, it can be rewritten as $(\prod u_{\mathbf k})\exp[\sum(v_{\mathbf k}/u_{\mathbf k})a^\dagger_{\mathbf k\uparrow}a^\dagger_{-\mathbf k\downarrow}]|0\rangle$, treating any fully occupied blocks separately. The exponential truncates within each block because a fermionic pair cannot be created twice in the same two modes. This is a <Cooper-pair pseudospin coherent state>, rather than a coherent state of unrestricted ideal <bosons>.

The one-electron expectation remains zero in a fermion-parity-even BCS state. Its nonzero microscopic order is the <BCS anomalous average> $F_{\mathbf k}=\langle a_{-\mathbf k\downarrow}a_{\mathbf k\uparrow}\rangle=u_{\mathbf k}^*v_{\mathbf k}$. For an attractive interaction written with matrix elements $V_{\mathbf k\mathbf k'}$, the gap is $\Delta_{\mathbf k}=-\sum_{\mathbf k'}V_{\mathbf k\mathbf k'}F_{\mathbf k'}$. Minimization yields the coherence factors and gap equation. <Fermionic coherent states> using Grassmann variables are valuable integration tools, but they are not ordinary complex-eigenvalue <electron> states replacing this physical paired <wavefunction>.<Theory of Superconductivity by John Bardeen and others>[1957, variational paired ground state and elementary excitations]{ref}

Near the transition, a slowly varying pair field connects the microscopic anomalous average to <Ginzburg-Landau theory of superconductivity>. The phenomenological complex field has pair charge $-2e$, a phase that controls supercurrent and a magnitude whose normalization is model dependent. It is not the expectation of a single-electron field. <Microscopic derivation of Ginzburg-Landau theory> supplies this connection under its stated near-critical assumptions. Pair phase coherence, Josephson <interference> and flux quantization then become different manifestations of the same macroscopic paired order. Paired-fermion superfluids likewise use a pair order field, while bosonic atomic superfluids can use a one-particle condensate field.
