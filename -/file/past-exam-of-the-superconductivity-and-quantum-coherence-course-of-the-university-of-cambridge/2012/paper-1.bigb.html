= Solution
{parent=a}

A bosonic <coherent state> obeys $a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle$ and has expansion $e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}\sum_n\alpha^n|n\rangle/\sqrt{n!}$. Its occupation is Poisson distributed, with mean and <variance> $|\alpha|^2$. Its nonzero field amplitude makes phase coherence natural, although physically observable phase comparisons require a reference.

For paired <fermions>, the corresponding <Cooper-pair pseudospin coherent state> is
$$
|\mathrm{BCS}\rangle=\prod_{\mathbf k}(u_{\mathbf k}+v_{\mathbf k}c^\dagger_{\mathbf k\uparrow}c^\dagger_{-\mathbf k\downarrow})|0\rangle,
\qquad |u_{\mathbf k}|^2+|v_{\mathbf k}|^2=1.
$$
It coherently superposes empty and occupied pair states and has an anomalous average $\langle c_{-\mathbf k\downarrow}c_{\mathbf k\uparrow}\rangle\ne0$. It is not an ordinary complex-eigenvalue <coherent state> of a single <fermion> <annihilation operator>. The broken-symmetry state has indefinite <particle number>; number projection can restore a fixed-number description without discarding the underlying pairing correlations.

<Hartree-Fock-Bogoliubov theory> approximates a two-body interaction using both the normal density $\rho_{ij}=\langle c_j^\dagger c_i\rangle$ and anomalous pairing tensor $\kappa_{ij}=\langle c_jc_i\rangle$. Direct and exchange contractions determine a normal one-particle field, while anomalous contractions determine the <pair potential>. The resulting quadratic <Hamiltonian> is diagonalized by a fermionic <Bogoliubov transformation>. In a homogeneous singlet case its Nambu block is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{\mathbf k}&\Delta_{\mathbf k}\\\Delta_{\mathbf k}^*&-\xi_{\mathbf k}\end{pmatrix},\qquad
E_{\mathbf k}=\sqrt{\xi_{\mathbf k}^2+|\Delta_{\mathbf k}|^2}.
$$
The fields are determined self-consistently, rather than selected independently of the <quasiparticle> state. For example,
$$
\Delta_{\mathbf k}=-\sum_{\mathbf k'}V_{\mathbf k\mathbf k'}\frac{\Delta_{\mathbf k'}}{2E_{\mathbf k'}}\tanh\frac{E_{\mathbf k'}}{2k_BT},
$$
together with the particle-number equation. The pairing field extends ordinary Hartree-Fock theory to states with pair correlations. This mean-field treatment omits fluctuations beyond its self-consistent quadratic description.

For <bosons>, write the field as a coherent condensate plus fluctuations, $\widehat\psi=\phi+\delta\widehat\psi$. The same normal/anomalous strategy retains $\widetilde n=\langle\delta\widehat\psi^\dagger\delta\widehat\psi\rangle$ and $\widetilde m=\langle\delta\widehat\psi\delta\widehat\psi\rangle$, with a bosonic, commutation-preserving <Bogoliubov transformation>. In the leading weakly depleted contact-gas limit, the condensate density $n_0$ and effective coupling $g>0$ give $E_k=\sqrt{\epsilon_k(\epsilon_k+2gn_0)}$, with $\epsilon_k=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$. This <Bogoliubov theory> connects low-momentum sound to high-momentum particle excitations. More elaborate self-consistent truncations must respect the symmetry constraints needed for a gapless condensate mode.

= Solution

The <electron-phonon interaction> can produce a retarded attraction. A carrier distorts the ionic lattice; a second carrier can lower its <energy> in that distortion. Integrating out a <phonon> gives a frequency-dependent interaction with an attractive low-frequency region. A schematic Matsubara-frequency contribution is $V_{\rm ph}(\mathbf q,i\omega_n)\propto-|g_{\mathbf q}|^2\omega_{\mathbf q}/(\omega_n^2+\omega_{\mathbf q}^2)$. It must be combined with the screened Coulomb repulsion, not treated as a statement that the bare electron-electron <force> is attractive.

Retardation separates the electronic and <phonon> <energy> scales and reduces the repulsive contribution in the pairing channel to a Coulomb pseudopotential. In the simplest weak-coupling approximation, a positive net dimensionless pairing strength gives
$$
k_BT_c\sim1.14\hbar\omega_D\exp[-1/(\lambda_{\rm ep}-\mu^*)].
$$
The formula assumes a <phonon> cutoff and simplified coupling; strong-coupling calculations need the spectral interaction rather than this one-number replacement. The <phonon> <frequency>'s dependence on ionic <mass> motivates an <isotope effect>, whose actual exponent can be modified by other physics.

An interaction need not be attractive in every <momentum> channel to support pairing. Spin fluctuations or other electronic correlations can favor a sign-changing gap: in the gap equation, repulsive scattering between two regions can contribute constructively when their gap signs differ. Such mechanisms are candidates for <unconventional superconductivity>. Observing a d-wave gap identifies symmetry information but does not, by itself, establish a unique microscopic pairing mechanism in a cuprate.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Write the condensate <order parameter> as $\Psi=|\Psi|e^{i\theta}$. Away from <vortex> cores, single-valuedness around a closed loop gives $\oint\nabla\theta\cdot d\mathbf l=2\pi n$. For neutral condensed objects of <mass> $M$, $\mathbf v_s=\hbar\nabla\theta/M$, hence the <quantized circulation>
$$
\boxed{\oint\mathbf v_s\cdot d\mathbf l=n\frac hM.}
$$
Here $M$ is the atomic <mass> for a simple bosonic atomic condensate, or the pair <mass> for a paired-fermion condensate. A nonzero winding can surround a <vortex> core where the order-parameter amplitude falls to zero. The result concerns collective condensate phase winding, not separate orbit quantization of every constituent particle.

For charge $q$ per condensed object, the gauge-invariant relation is $M\mathbf v_s=\hbar\nabla\theta-q\mathbf A$. Integrating gives
$$
M\oint\mathbf v_s\cdot d\mathbf l+q\Phi=nh,
\qquad \Phi+\frac Mq\oint\mathbf v_s\cdot d\mathbf l=\frac{nh}{q}.
$$
This is <fluxoid and flux quantization in a superconductor>. With Cooper-pair charge magnitude $2e$, the flux quantum's magnitude is
$$
\boxed{\Phi_0=\frac h{2e}.}
$$
The orientation fixes its sign. A loop taken deep in a thick superconductor has negligible superflow and therefore encloses an integer flux quantum. In a thin loop or one with appreciable screening current, it is the <fluxoid>, including the circulation term, that is quantized; the <magnetic flux> alone need not be exactly an integer multiple.

= Solution
{parent=d}

A <Josephson junction> weakly couples two superconducting condensates. Its coupling <energy> is $-E_J\cos\varphi$, with a gauge-invariant phase difference across the junction. Choose current and voltage orientations consistently so that
$$
\boxed{I=I_c\sin\varphi,\qquad E_J=\frac{\hbar I_c}{2e},\qquad
\dot\varphi=\frac{2eV}{\hbar}.}
$$
The <DC Josephson effect> permits a stationary zero-voltage supercurrent up to $I_c$. A constant nonzero voltage produces the <AC Josephson effect>, with <frequency> $f=2e|V|/h$. Resistive and capacitive currents are additional circuit contributions, not part of the ideal sinusoidal supercurrent alone.

In a symmetric <DC SQUID> with negligible loop <self-inductance>, the two junction phases differ by $2\pi\Phi/\Phi_0$. Adding their currents and maximizing over the common phase gives
$$
\boxed{I_{c,\rm SQUID}(\Phi)=2I_c\left|\cos\frac{\pi\Phi}{\Phi_0}\right|.}
$$
Flux changes therefore modulate the critical current or a suitably biased output voltage. This converts phase <interference> into sensitive flux measurement. Finite loop <inductance>, unequal junctions, noise and readout conditions alter the simple modulation; the loop flux then includes screening currents. A <SQUID> is useful in magnetometry, but achieving sensitivity requires both a suitable circuit and controlled environmental noise.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Momentum-resolved photoemission and directional tunneling can map the magnitude and nodes of the cuprate gap. Low-temperature <heat capacity>, thermal transport and penetration-depth measurements constrain the low-energy <quasiparticle> spectrum. These probes do not all determine the relative sign of the gap. A <phase-sensitive test of d-wave pairing> uses Josephson <interference> between differently oriented crystal regions. In the <tricrystal half-flux experiment>, a superconducting film crosses three designed grain boundaries; a frustrated ring can produce a half-flux signal detected with a scanning <SQUID>. Unfrustrated geometries supply controls. This probes phase structure beyond merely finding a V-shaped tunneling spectrum.<Pairing symmetry and flux quantization in a tricrystal superconducting ring by C. C. Tsuei and others>[1994, tricrystal geometry and flux measurement]{ref}

For the ideal nearest-neighbor <square lattice> at one <electron> per site including both spins, particle-hole symmetry puts the <chemical potential> at zero. The two requested cuts are
$$
\varepsilon(k_x,0)=-2t(1+\cos k_xa),\qquad
\varepsilon(k_x,k_x)=-4t\cos k_xa.
$$
The first runs from $-4t$ at the zone center to zero at $k_xa=\pm\pi$; the diagonal cut crosses zero at $k_xa=\pm\pi/2$. The <Fermi surface> obeys $\cos k_xa+\cos k_ya=0$, giving the diamond $|k_x|+|k_y|=\pi/a$ in the <first Brillouin zone>.

\Image[paper-1-nodal-square-lattice.png]
{title=Half-filled square-lattice band cuts, diamond Fermi surface and anisotropic nodal quasiparticle contours}

Near the specified node define $\delta\mathbf k=\mathbf k-\mathbf k_0$ and
$$
q_\parallel=(\delta k_x+\delta k_y)/\sqrt2,\qquad
q_\perp=(\delta k_y-\delta k_x)/\sqrt2.
$$
Expanding $\cos(\pi/2+\delta k a)=-a\delta k+O((a\delta k)^3)$ gives
$$
\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}\simeq2ta(\delta k_x+\delta k_y)=\alpha q_\parallel,\qquad
\Delta_{\mathbf k}\simeq\Delta_0a(\delta k_y-\delta k_x)=\beta q_\perp,
$$
where $\alpha=2\sqrt2ta$ and $\beta=\sqrt2\Delta_0a$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{E^2\simeq\alpha^2q_\parallel^2+\beta^2q_\perp^2.}
$$
There are four such nodes. An <energy> contour about one node encloses <momentum> area $\pi E^2/(\alpha\beta)$. Counting all four nodes and two independent spin <quasiparticles>, with one state per $(2\pi)^2$ of <momentum> area per unit real-space area, gives the positive-excitation count and <nodal quasiparticle density of states in a two-dimensional d-wave superconductor>
$$
\frac{\mathcal N(E)}{\mathcal A}=\frac{2E^2}{\pi\alpha\beta},\qquad
\boxed{\frac{g_{\rm qp}(E)}{\mathcal A}=\frac{4E}{\pi\alpha\beta}\quad(E>0).}
$$
This counts thermally excitable Bogoliubov modes, including particle-like and hole-like excitations, not the electron-addition spectral density alone. That distinction prevents a factor-of-two ambiguity. It is a per-layer result; dividing by the layer spacing gives the corresponding <volume> density in the independent-layer approximation.

The thermal <energy> density above its zero-temperature value follows from Fermi occupation:
$$
\frac{U(T)-U(0)}{\mathcal A}
=\frac4{\pi\alpha\beta}\int_0^\infty\frac{E^2\,dE}{e^{E/(k_BT)}+1}
=\frac{4I_2}{\pi\alpha\beta}(k_BT)^3,
\qquad I_2=\frac32\zeta(3)\simeq1.80.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\frac C{\mathcal A}=\frac{12I_2}{\pi\alpha\beta}k_B^3T^2
\simeq\frac{21.6}{\pi\alpha\beta}k_B^3T^2.}
$$
This clean nodal contribution assumes $k_BT$ small compared with the scale over which <linearization> fails; impurity states and other excitations can add further terms.

In the <vortex> state, each flux quantum occupies area of order $\pi R^2=\Phi_0/B$, so $R\propto B^{-1/2}$. Outside a core of radius $\xi$, the phase flow has magnitude $v_s(r)\simeq\kappa/(2\pi r)$ in the simple circular-cell estimate, with $\kappa=h/M_{\rm pair}$ and condensed-pair <mass> $M_{\rm pair}$. Its cell average is
$$
\langle|v_s|\rangle\simeq\frac1{\pi R^2}\int_\xi^R\frac\kappa{2\pi r}\,2\pi r\,dr
=\frac{\kappa(R-\xi)}{\pi R^2}\propto\sqrt B.
$$
The **signed vector average can vanish** around a <vortex>; the relevant quantity is a typical magnitude or Doppler shift, not a net transport velocity. Flow shifts nodal energies by order $\hbar\mathbf v_s\cdot\mathbf k_F$. Because the zero-field nodal <density of states> is linear in <energy>, averaging the magnitude of these shifts produces a residual <density of states> proportional to $\sqrt B$. The resulting <Volovik effect> gives
$$
\boxed{C(T,B)=\gamma(B)T+\cdots,\qquad \gamma(B)\propto\sqrt B.}
$$
This is the clean semiclassical nodal regime, at <temperatures> below the field-induced Doppler scale and in an appropriate mixed-state field range. Core states, disorder, <Zeeman splitting> and screening can modify it. Its replacement by a square root of applied field additionally assumes the mean induction tracks that field away from the lower-critical-field onset; it is not an exact all-field law.

= Solution

<Ginzburg-Landau theory of superconductivity> describes the superconducting phase by a complex <order parameter> and a gauge-invariant free-energy functional. A common normalization is
$$
F=\int d^3x\left[f_n+\alpha|\Psi|^2+\frac\beta2|\Psi|^4
+\frac1{2m_*}|(-i\hbar\nabla-q\mathbf A)\Psi|^2+\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}\right],
$$
with $\beta>0$, pair charge $q=-2e$ and $\alpha\simeq a(T-T_c)$. In zero field the uniform minimum has $|\Psi|^2=-\alpha/\beta$ below $T_c$. Variation gives the order-parameter equation and supercurrent. The characteristic lengths are
$$
\xi=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_*|\alpha|}},\qquad
\lambda_L=\sqrt{\frac{m_*}{\mu_0q^2|\Psi|^2}},\qquad
\kappa_{\rm GL}=\lambda_L/\xi.
$$
The Meissner response screens static <magnetic fields> over $\lambda_L$. In the simple isotropic theory, $\kappa_{\rm GL}<1/\sqrt2$ gives type-I behavior; $\kappa_{\rm GL}>1/\sqrt2$ allows type-II vortices and a mixed state between lower and upper critical fields. Flux quantization, <vortex> structure and currents follow from phase stiffness and electromagnetic coupling. This is a phenomenological expansion most directly controlled near $T_c$; static coefficients alone do not determine every dynamical mode or dissipation rate.

<BCS theory> supplies a microscopic pairing description. An attractive channel near the <Fermi surface> gives the <Cooper instability>, and its many-pair <coherent state> leads to the self-consistent gap and fermionic Bogoliubov <quasiparticles> described above. For an isotropic weak-coupling model with constant single-spin <density of states> $N_s(0)$, attraction $V>0$ and cutoff $\hbar\omega_D$,
$$
\Delta(0)\simeq2\hbar\omega_D e^{-1/[N_s(0)V]},\qquad
k_BT_c\simeq1.14\hbar\omega_D e^{-1/[N_s(0)V]}.
$$
It predicts $2\Delta(0)/(k_BT_c)\simeq3.53$ in that specified model. A full gap suppresses low-temperature <quasiparticles> exponentially, whereas nodes permit power laws such as the $T^2$ <heat capacity> derived above. The weak-coupling ratio is not a universal value for all superconductors. Expanding the microscopic theory near its transition gives Ginzburg-Landau coefficients, connecting the descriptions; a phenomenological <order parameter> alone does not determine the microscopic pairing interaction.

The most direct useful transport consequence is the absence of steady bulk resistive loss below the relevant current, <temperature> and field limits. <Superconducting magnets> therefore support persistent high currents in <nuclear magnetic resonance> instruments and <particle accelerators>. Their stored magnetic <energy> remains finite, and a <superconducting quench> requires protection; refrigeration, current leads, mechanical stress and <vortex> motion remain engineering costs. Zero dc resistance does not eliminate all ac loss or justify unlimited current.

<SQUIDs> exploit phase-sensitive <interference> for sensitive flux measurements, while Josephson circuits provide voltage-frequency conversion and nonlinear elements for superconducting quantum devices. These applications use coherence, not just low resistance. Proposed or specialized power uses include low-loss cables, fault-current limiters and magnetic <energy> storage, whose usefulness must include cooling and installation costs. Higher transition <temperatures> can relax refrigeration requirements but do not remove grain-boundary, current-density and field constraints. Neither BCS nor Ginzburg-Landau theory alone supplies a complete device design or guarantees that a particular material is economical.
