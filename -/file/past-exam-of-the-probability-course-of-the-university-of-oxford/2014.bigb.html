= Solution
{parent=i}

<Convergence in distribution> means that the <cumulative distribution functions> satisfy
$$
\mathbb P(X_n\leq x)\longrightarrow\mathbb P(X\leq x)
$$
at every $x$ where the <cumulative distribution function> of $X$ is <continuous>. Values at its discontinuities need not converge. This definition uses only the <probability distributions>, so the <random variables> need not be defined on the same <probability space>.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let $\mu=\mathbb E[X_1]$ and $\sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}(X_1)\in(0,\infty)$. For <independent and identically distributed random variables>, the <central limit theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1).}
$$
The limit is the <standard normal distribution>. If the <variance> is zero, then $X_i=\mu$ <almost surely>; the centered sums are zero, and division by $\sigma$ is not defined.

= iii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The implication <convergence in probability implies convergence in distribution> follows by comparing nearby <events>. Fix a <continuity> point $x$ of the <cumulative distribution function> $F_X$ and let $\varepsilon>0$. If $X\leq x-\varepsilon$ and $|X_n-X|\leq\varepsilon$, then $X_n\leq x$. Conversely, $X_n\leq x$ and $|X_n-X|\leq\varepsilon$ imply $X\leq x+\varepsilon$. Thus
$$
F_X(x-\varepsilon)-\mathbb P(|X_n-X|>\varepsilon)
\leq F_{X_n}(x)
\leq F_X(x+\varepsilon)+\mathbb P(|X_n-X|>\varepsilon).
$$
The error <probability> tends to zero by <convergence in probability>. Taking lower and upper <limits> and then letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$, <continuity> at $x$ squeezes both bounds to $F_X(x)$. This is exactly <convergence in distribution>.

= Solution

Subtract adjacent values of the <cumulative distribution function>. Its value at zero is zero, so the same calculation applies also to $k=1$:
$$
\boxed{p_k=\left(1-\frac1{k+1}\right)-\left(1-\frac1k\right)=\frac1{k(k+1)},\qquad k\geq1.}
$$
The <probability mass function> sums to one by a <telescoping series>.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For $x>0$, the event $M_n/n\leq x$ says that each of the <independent random variables> is at most $\lfloor nx\rfloor$. Hence, for all sufficiently large $n$,
$$
\mathbb P(M_n/n\leq x)=\left(1-\frac1{\lfloor nx\rfloor+1}\right)^n.
$$
Since $n/(\lfloor nx\rfloor+1)\to1/x$ and $\log(1-u)=-u+o(u)$ as $u\to0$, taking <logarithms> gives the <limit> $e^{-1/x}$. For $x\leq0$ the <probability> is zero. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{M_n}{n}\xrightarrow{d}W,\qquad F_W(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x\leq0,\\ e^{-1/x},&x>0.\end{cases}}
$$
This is the <Fréchet limit for a reciprocal integer tail>, with a <Fréchet distribution> of shape one. Its <cumulative distribution function> is continuous even at zero, so the pointwise calculation establishes <convergence in distribution>.

= Solution

Write $S_{n+1}=S_n+X_{n+1}$. The new <random variable> $X_{n+1}$ is <independent> of the entire history generated by $X_1,\ldots,X_n$, hence of $(S_1,\ldots,S_n)$. This is a <Markov chain obtained from independent innovations>: conditional on the past, only the current sum enters the next state. Its time-homogeneous <transition probabilities> are
$$
\boxed{P_{st}=\begin{cases}\dfrac1{(t-s)(t-s+1)},&t>s,\\0,&t\leq s,\end{cases}\qquad s,t\in\{1,2,\ldots\}.}
$$
Every jump is a positive integer, and each row sums to one by the <probability mass function> from part (b).

= ii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Now $M_{n+1}=\max(M_n,X_{n+1})$. Again $X_{n+1}$ is <independent> of the whole previous history, so the recursion is a <Markov chain obtained from independent innovations>. To remain at $s$, the new observation must be at most $s$; to jump to $t>s$, it must equal $t$. Thus the <transition probabilities> are
$$
\boxed{P_{st}=\begin{cases}0,&t<s,\\\dfrac{s}{s+1},&t=s,\\\dfrac1{t(t+1)},&t>s.\end{cases}}
$$
The upper tail sums to $1/(s+1)$, complementing the holding <probability> $s/(s+1)$.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Integrating $e^{tx}$ against the <Gamma density> gives the <moment-generating function of a gamma distribution>:
$$
M_X(t)=\frac{\lambda^r}{(r-1)!}\int_0^\infty x^{r-1}e^{-(\lambda-t)x}\,dx
=\boxed{\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-t}\right)^r},\qquad t<\lambda.
$$
The <change of variables> $y=(\lambda-t)x$ reduces the integral to $\Gamma(r)=(r-1)!$ using the <gamma function>. For $t\geq\lambda$ the defining integral is infinite. In particular the <moment-generating function> is finite on a <neighbourhood> of zero.

= b
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

<Independence> makes the <moment-generating function> of the sum the product of the two <moment-generating functions>. For $t<\lambda$,
$$
M_{X_1+X_2}(t)=\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-t}\right)^{r_1}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-t}\right)^{r_2}
=\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-t}\right)^{r_1+r_2}.
$$
The <expectations> are finite on, for example, $(-\lambda/2,\lambda/2)$, as is the <moment-generating function> of the candidate <Gamma distribution>. The <uniqueness theorem for moment-generating functions> therefore applies, proving
$$
\boxed{X_1+X_2\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(r_1+r_2,\lambda).}
$$
This is <additivity of independent gamma distributions with a common rate>; a common rate is essential to this specific formula.

= c
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

An <exponential distribution> of rate $\lambda$ is a <Gamma distribution> with shape one. Repeated <additivity of independent gamma distributions with a common rate> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{E_1+\cdots+E_n\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(n,\lambda).}
$$
With integer shape this is also called an <Erlang distribution>.

The <Poisson interarrival times> of a homogeneous <Poisson process> are <independent and identically distributed> with the <exponential distribution> of rate $\lambda$. Consequently $Y_1,Y_2$ are <independent random variables> and their <joint probability density> is
$$
\boxed{f_{Y_1,Y_2}(y_1,y_2)=\lambda^2e^{-\lambda(y_1+y_2)}\mathbf1_{\{y_1>0,\ y_2>0\}}.}
$$
Here $Y_2$ is the second interarrival duration, rather than the time of the second arrival.

= ii
{parent=d}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The inverse <change of variables> is $y_1=uv$, $y_2=(1-u)v$, on $0<u<1$, $v>0$. Its absolute <Jacobian determinant> is
$$
\left|\det\begin{pmatrix}v&u\\-v&1-u\end{pmatrix}\right|=v.
$$
The <change of variables for probability densities> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{f_{U,V}(u,v)=\lambda^2v e^{-\lambda v}\mathbf1_{\{0<u<1,\ v>0\}}.}
$$
This factorizes on a product domain into a unit <uniform density> in $u$ and a shape-two <Gamma density> in $v$. Thus **$U$ and $V$ are independent**, with $U\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1)$ and $V\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(2,\lambda)$. This is the shape-$(1,1)$ case of <beta-gamma independence>.

= Solution
{parent=e}

By part (c) with $\lambda=1$, let $T_n=E_1+\cdots+E_n\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(n,1)$ for <independent and identically distributed> <exponential random variables>. The requested integral is $\mathbb P(T_n\leq n)$. Each summand has <expectation> one and <variance> one, so the <central limit theorem> gives
$$
\frac{T_n-n}{\sqrt n}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1).
$$
Zero is a <continuity> point of the <standard normal distribution>, and consequently
$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1{(n-1)!}\int_0^n x^{n-1}e^{-x}\,dx=\Phi(0)=\frac12.}
$$

Write the <stationary distribution> as a row <vector> $\pi=(a,b,c,d)$. For the first <transition matrix>, $\pi P=\pi$ gives $a=a/2+d$, $b=a/2+b/2$, $c=b/2$ and $d=c$. Hence $b=a$ and $c=d=a/2$. Normalization yields
$$
\boxed{\pi=\left(\frac13,\frac13,\frac16,\frac16\right).}
$$
By the stated common-<stationary distribution> property, this is also the answer for the second <transition matrix>.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The first <Markov chain> is a finite <irreducible Markov chain>: its positive-probability directed cycle reaches all four states. The self-loop at state 1 makes it an <aperiodic Markov chain>. <Convergence to an invariant distribution> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{p_{11}^{(n)}\longrightarrow\pi_1=\frac13\quad\text{for the first matrix}.}
$$
The second chain alternates between the <sets> $\{1,3\}$ and $\{2,4\}$. It is a <periodic Markov chain> with period two, so $p_{11}^{(2m+1)}=0$. From either state 2 or state 4 the next-step <probability> of entering state 1 is $2/3$. Therefore $p_{11}^{(2m)}=2/3$ for every $m\geq1$. The subsequences have different <limits>, so **the second return-probability sequence does not converge**.

= b
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Start the <Markov chain> at a state $i$ and define the strictly positive <first return time> $T_i^+=\inf\{n\geq1:X_n=i\}$. A <recurrent state> satisfies $\mathbb P_i(T_i^+<\infty)=1$; a <transient state> has this <probability> strictly below one. Equivalently a <recurrent state> is visited infinitely often <almost surely> when started there, whereas a <transient state> receives finitely many visits <almost surely>. The <recurrence criterion by return probabilities> gives the equivalent test
$$
i\text{ recurrent}\iff\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{ii}^{(n)}=\infty.
$$
States in a <communicating class> share this classification. In an <irreducible Markov chain> all states communicate, so the chain is called a <recurrent Markov chain> if all its states are recurrent and a <transient Markov chain> if all are transient.

= Solution

Represent the <simple random walk> by $X_n=\xi_1+\cdots+\xi_n$, where the increments are <independent and identically distributed> and satisfy $\mathbb P(\xi_k=1)=p$, $\mathbb P(\xi_k=-1)=1-p$. They are integrable and have <expectation> $2p-1$, so the <strong law of large numbers> gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{X_n}{n}\longrightarrow\mu=2p-1\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$
If $p\ne1/2$, this nonzero drift implies that $X_n$ eventually leaves every fixed finite interval and tends to the appropriate infinity. Hence it visits zero only finitely many times <almost surely>, so zero is a <transient state>. By translation of the starting state, every state is transient: **the chain is transient for $p\ne1/2$**. This also covers the deterministic endpoint cases $p=0,1$, without assuming <Markov chain irreducibility> at those endpoints.

= ii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For the symmetric <simple random walk on the integer line>, odd return times are impossible. A return after $2m$ steps requires exactly $m$ positive increments, so
$$
p_{00}^{(2m)}=\binom{2m}{m}2^{-2m}\sim\frac1{\sqrt{\pi m}}
$$
by <Stirling's approximation>. The resulting sum diverges by the <p-series> criterion. The <recurrence criterion by return probabilities> therefore makes zero a <recurrent state>. The <simple random walk> is an <irreducible Markov chain> on $\mathbb Z$, so **the symmetric chain is recurrent**.

= iii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

This is the <synchronous product of simple symmetric random walks>, rather than a walk that changes only one coordinate at a time. Independence of the three coordinate processes gives
$$
\mathbb P\bigl((X_{2m},Y_{2m},Z_{2m})=(0,0,0)\bigr)
=\left[\binom{2m}{m}2^{-2m}\right]^3
\sim\frac1{(\pi m)^{3/2}}.
$$
Odd-time return probabilities vanish. The <p-series> with exponent $3/2$ converges, so the sum of all return probabilities is finite. By the <recurrence criterion by return probabilities>, the origin is a <transient state>: **the three-dimensional process is transient**. From the origin its <communicating class> consists of integer triples whose coordinates have the same parity; translation gives the same classification for every other class.
