== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

By <independence of random variables>, the <joint probability density> is the product of the two <probability density functions> of the rate-one <exponential distributions>:
$$
\boxed{f_{X,Y}(x,y)=e^{-(x+y)}\mathbf1_{\{x>0,\ y>0\}}.}
$$
Its values on the boundary are immaterial, because the boundary has zero area.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the inverse <change of variables for probability densities>
$$
x=vw,\qquad y=(1-v)w,\qquad 0<v<1,\quad w>0.
$$
Its absolute <Jacobian determinant> is
$$
\left|\det\begin{pmatrix}w&v\\-w&1-v\end{pmatrix}\right|=w.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{f_{V,W}(v,w)=w e^{-w}\mathbf1_{\{0<v<1,\ w>0\}}.}
$$
Since $\int_0^\infty we^{-w}\,dw=1$, its <marginal densities> are $f_V(v)=\mathbf1_{(0,1)}(v)$ and $f_W(w)=we^{-w}\mathbf1_{(0,\infty)}(w)$. The joint density factorizes as $f_Vf_W$, so **$V$ and $W$ are <independent random variables>**, with $V$ uniform on $[0,1]$ and $W\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(2,1)$ in the shape-rate convention. This is <beta-gamma independence> with both shape parameters equal to one.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $W=X+Y$. The independent pair $(U,W)$ has the same <joint distribution> as $(V,W)$ from part (ii), because both first coordinates are uniform and independent of the same sum law. Hence
$$
U(X+Y)\ \stackrel d=\ VW=X.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{U(X+Y)\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1).}
$$
The rate is one. This multiplication identity is the mechanism behind the <exponential invariant law of a random affine recursion>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The answer is
$$
\boxed{X_n\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1)\quad\text{for every }n\geq0.}
$$
It holds initially by construction. If it holds at step $n-1$, then $X_{n-1}$ is a function of $E_0,\ldots,E_{n-1},U_1,\ldots,U_{n-1}$, so it is independent of the fresh inputs $E_n,U_n$. Part (a)(iii) therefore gives $U_n(X_{n-1}+E_n)\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1)$. This completes the <mathematical induction> and identifies the <stationary distribution> of the recursion.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The two recursions use a <synchronous coupling>: they receive exactly the same fresh inputs. Subtraction cancels the additive term:
$$
Z_n-X_n=U_n(Z_{n-1}-X_{n-1}).
$$
The variable $U_n$ is independent of the preceding difference and has <expected value> $1/2$. Thus <expectation of a product of independent random variables> gives
$$
\mathbb E|Z_n-X_n|=\frac12\mathbb E|Z_{n-1}-X_{n-1}|
=2^{-n}\mathbb E|c-E_0|\leq2^{-n}(|c|+1).
$$
By <Markov's inequality>, for every $\varepsilon>0$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(|Z_n-X_n|>\varepsilon)\leq\frac{|c|+1}{\varepsilon\,2^n}\longrightarrow0.}
$$
This proves the requested <convergence in probability>; in fact the difference has <L1 convergence>. It is <synchronous contraction of a random affine recursion>, and the estimate holds for every real $c$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Every $X_n$ has the same <exponential distribution>, so $X_n\xrightarrow d X$ for $X\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1)$. Part (ii) gives $Z_n-X_n\xrightarrow p0$. Applying <Slutsky's theorem> to $Z_n=X_n+(Z_n-X_n)$ yields
$$
\boxed{Z_n\xrightarrow d\operatorname{Exp}(1).}
$$
The limiting <probability distribution> does not depend on $c$. This is precisely the attraction to the <exponential invariant law of a random affine recursion>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The update defines a time-homogeneous <Markov process>: given its present value, the next value depends only on independent fresh input. Part (b)(i) identifies an invariant law: initializing with the rate-one <exponential distribution> preserves that distribution at every time. Part (b)(iii) shows convergence of the one-time laws to this invariant law from every deterministic initial value.

For a countable <state space>, the analogous <countable-state Markov chain convergence theorem> says that an <irreducible Markov chain> that is <aperiodic> and <positive recurrent> converges to its unique <stationary distribution> from every initial state. Here the state space is continuous, so that countable-state theorem does not directly supply the proof; the explicit <coupling> does. The two conclusions have the same equilibrium interpretation.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <moment-generating function> of a real <random variable> $X$ is
$$
\boxed{M_X(t)=\mathbb E[e^{tX}]}
$$
for real $t$ where the expectation is finite. One may regard it as an extended nonnegative function elsewhere. In particular $M_X(0)=1$; finiteness on a neighborhood of zero is the usual hypothesis for recovering moments and using its local expansion.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Differentiating the <moment-generating function> at zero gives
$$
\boxed{M_X'(0)=\mathbb E[X],\qquad M_X''(0)=\mathbb E[X^2].}
$$
The second derivative is the second raw <moment>, so the <variance> is $M_X''(0)-(M_X'(0))^2$. The assumed exponential integrability justifies these derivatives.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Integrate against the <standard normal density> by <completing the square>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
M_X(t)&=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{tx-x^2/2}\,dx\\
&=e^{t^2/2}\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-(x-t)^2/2}\,dx=e^{t^2/2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the <moment-generating function of a standard normal variable> is
$$
\boxed{M_X(t)=e^{t^2/2}\quad(t\in\mathbb R).}
$$

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $Y_1,Y_2,\ldots$ be real <independent and identically distributed random variables> with finite <expected value> $\mu$ and <variance> $0<\sigma^2<\infty$. The <central limit theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\frac{\sum_{k=1}^nY_k-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\xrightarrow dN(0,1).}
$$
Equivalently, the <cumulative distribution functions> of these normalized sums converge at every real point to the <standard normal distribution function>. The theorem itself does not require a finite moment-generating function.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The given <expected value> and <variance> imply $M_X(0)=1$, $M_X'(0)=0$ and $M_X''(0)=1$. The <Taylor expansion> therefore gives
$$
M_X(t)=1+\frac{t^2}{2}+o(t^2).
$$
Since $\log(1+u)=u+o(u)$ as $u\to0$, it follows that
$$
\boxed{\log M_X(t)=\frac{t^2}{2}+o(t^2),\qquad\alpha=\frac12.}
$$
This is the <quadratic expansion of a cumulant-generating function> for a centered variable of variance one.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $S_n=n^{-1/2}\sum_{k=1}^nX_k$. By <independence of random variables>, its <moment-generating function> is
$$
M_{S_n}(t)=M_X(t/\sqrt n)^n.
$$
For each fixed real $t$, the expansion in part (i) yields
$$
\log M_{S_n}(t)
=n\left(\frac{t^2}{2n}+o(n^{-1})\right)
\longrightarrow\frac{t^2}{2}.
$$
For $t=0$ the equality is immediate. Exponentiation gives $M_{S_n}(t)\to e^{t^2/2}$, the <moment-generating function of a standard normal variable>. The functions are finite on a common neighborhood of zero, so the <convergence theorem for moment-generating functions> proves
$$
\boxed{S_n\xrightarrow dN(0,1).}
$$
This proves the <central limit theorem> under the exponential-moment hypothesis of this part.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the deterministic weights $a_{n,k}=\sqrt{k}/n$. Their squared sum and maximum are
$$
\sum_{k=1}^na_{n,k}^2=\frac{n+1}{2n}\longrightarrow\frac12,
\qquad \max_{k\leq n}|a_{n,k}|=n^{-1/2}\longrightarrow0.
$$
To apply the <quadratic expansion of a cumulant-generating function> rigorously, write $\log M_X(u)=u^2/2+r(u)$, with $r(u)=o(u^2)$. Fix $t\ne0$ and let
$$
\eta_n=\sup_{0<|u|\leq|t|/\sqrt n}\frac{|r(u)|}{u^2}\longrightarrow0.
$$
All arguments $t\sqrt{k}/n$ lie in this shrinking interval. Consequently
$$
\left|\sum_{k=1}^nr\!\left(\frac{t\sqrt{k}}n\right)\right|
\leq\eta_n\frac{t^2}{n^2}\sum_{k=1}^nk
=\eta_n\frac{t^2(n+1)}{2n}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Taking logs of the <moment-generating function> and using <independence of random variables> now gives
$$
\log M_{T_n}(t)=\frac{t^2}{2n^2}\sum_{k=1}^nk+o(1)
\longrightarrow\frac{t^2}{4}.
$$
The case $t=0$ is immediate. Since $e^{t^2/4}$ is the <moment-generating function> of a <normal distribution> of variance $1/2$, the <convergence theorem for moment-generating functions> gives
$$
\boxed{T_n\xrightarrow dN\!\left(0,\frac12\right).}
$$
This is a <weighted central limit theorem with negligible maximum weight>. Its limit variance also agrees with the exact value $\operatorname{Var}(T_n)=(n+1)/(2n)$ from <variance additivity for independent random variables>.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

A <transition matrix> $P$ defines an <irreducible Markov chain> if every state is accessible from every other state:
$$
\boxed{\text{for every }i,j\in S,\ \text{there is }n\geq0\text{ with }(P^n)_{ij}>0.}
$$
Equivalently, all states belong to one <communicating class>. When $i\ne j$, a positive number of steps is necessarily used.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

A <stationary distribution> is a nonnegative row vector $\pi=(\pi_i)_{i\in S}$ with total mass one that is preserved by one transition:
$$
\boxed{\pi_i\geq0,\quad\sum_{i\in S}\pi_i=1,\quad\pi_j=\sum_{i\in S}\pi_iP_{ij}\quad(j\in S).}
$$
In matrix notation, $\pi P=\pi$. If the initial state has distribution $\pi$, so does the state at each later time.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

For an <irreducible Markov chain> on a countable <state space>, **the set has either zero or one element**. By <uniqueness of stationary distributions for an irreducible countable chain>, a stationary probability distribution exists exactly in the <positive recurrent> case, and then
$$
\pi_i=\frac1{\mathbb E_iT_i^+}
$$
for the <first return time> $T_i^+$. The right-hand side fixes every entry, proving uniqueness.

For a finite state space, existence is guaranteed, so there is **exactly one**. A countably infinite state space can have none: the <simple symmetric random walk> on the integers is irreducible and null recurrent. Periodicity does not affect this existence-and-uniqueness statement.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

**The difference process $D_n$ is a <Markov chain>.** If the columns are equal, the next block creates a height difference of one. If the difference is $k\geq1$, landing in the taller column increases it to $k+1$, while landing in the shorter column makes it zero. Therefore its only nonzero <transition probabilities> are
$$
\boxed{P(0,1)=1,\qquad P(k,k+1)=P(k,0)=\frac12\quad(k\geq1).}
$$
These probabilities depend only on the present difference, not on the absolute heights or their history. This is the <reset-or-increment Markov chain> with $p=1/2$.

For its <stationary distribution>, the incoming-flow equations give $\pi_1=\pi_0$ and $\pi_k=\pi_{k-1}/2$ for $k\geq2$. Normalization uses a <geometric series>:
$$
1=\pi_0+\sum_{k=1}^\infty\pi_0\,2^{-(k-1)}=3\pi_0.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\pi_0=\frac13,\qquad\pi_k=\frac1{3\,2^{k-1}}\quad(k\geq1).}
$$
The remaining balance equation is also satisfied: the flow into zero is $(1-\pi_0)/2=1/3$. Every state can reach zero and every positive state can be reached from zero, so it is an <irreducible Markov chain>. Its invariant probability distribution makes it <positive recurrent>, and that distribution is unique.

**The maximum-height process $H_n$ is not Markov.** Starting empty, condition first on the positive-probability history $(H_1,H_2,H_3)=(1,1,2)$. After two blocks the columns were equal, so after three their difference is one. Hence $\mathbb P(H_4=3\mid H_1=1,H_2=1,H_3=2)=1/2$.

For the history $(1,2,2)$, the first two blocks built one column and the third caught the other column up, leaving equal heights. Hence $\mathbb P(H_4=3\mid H_1=1,H_2=2,H_3=2)=1$. Both histories have the same time and present height $H_3=2$, but different predictions. This violates the <Markov property> and illustrates why <a function of a Markov chain need not be Markov>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The precise conclusion is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\!\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{H_n}{n}=\frac23\right)=1.}
$$
To prove it, observe that the maximum rises exactly when the new height difference is positive:
$$
H_m-H_{m-1}=\mathbf1_{\{D_m>0\}}.
$$
A block landing in a shorter column produces $D_m=0$ and no rise; every other case raises the maximum and leaves $D_m>0$. Starting from $H_0=0$, telescoping gives
$$
\frac{H_n}{n}=\frac1n\sum_{m=1}^n\mathbf1_{\{D_m>0\}}.
$$
The difference chain is irreducible and positive recurrent by part (i). Apply the <countable-state Markov chain ergodic theorem> to the bounded <indicator function> $g(k)=\mathbf1_{\{k>0\}}$. Its stationary mean is $\sum_{k\geq1}\pi_k=1-\pi_0=2/3$, giving the asserted <almost sure convergence>. No <aperiodicity> assumption is needed for this time average. This establishes the <growth rate of two-column sticky deposition>.
