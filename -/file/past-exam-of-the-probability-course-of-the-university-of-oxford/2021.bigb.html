The <cumulative distribution function> of the <uniform distribution> on $[0,1/n]$ is
$$
F_n(t)=\begin{cases}0,&t<0,\\nt,&0\leq t\leq1/n,\\1,&t>1/n.\end{cases}
$$
The constant zero has <cumulative distribution function> $F(t)=\mathbf1_{[0,\infty)}(t)$. Its <continuity points> are precisely $t\ne0$. For fixed $t<0$, $F_n(t)=0=F(t)$; for fixed $t>0$, $F_n(t)=1=F(t)$ eventually. This proves <convergence in distribution> to zero directly from the definition.

Also, $\mathbb P(0\leq U_n\leq1/n)=1$. For every $\varepsilon>0$ and $n>1/\varepsilon$, $\mathbb P(|U_n|>\varepsilon)=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{U_n\longrightarrow0\text{ both in distribution and in probability}.}
$$
The <convergence in probability> conclusion holds for any realization of these <random variables> on a common <probability space>; no <independence of random variables> is needed.

Fix $\delta>0$. Once $|c_n-c|<\delta$, the following event inclusions hold outside $\{|X_n-X|>\delta\}$:
$$
\{X\leq c-2\delta\}\subseteq\{X_n\leq c_n\}\subseteq\{X\leq c+2\delta\}.
$$
Taking <probabilities> gives the explicit bounds
$$
F(c-2\delta)-\mathbb P(|X_n-X|>\delta)\leq F_n(c_n)\leq F(c+2\delta)+\mathbb P(|X_n-X|>\delta).
$$
By the assumed <convergence in probability>, the error tends to zero. Therefore
$$
F(c-2\delta)\leq\liminf_nF_n(c_n)\leq\limsup_nF_n(c_n)\leq F(c+2\delta).
$$
The <continuity> of the <cumulative distribution function> $F$ at $c$ lets $\delta$ decrease to zero, proving $\boxed{F_n(c_n)\to F(c).}$ This proves the needed implication directly, without invoking any theorem comparing <convergence of random variables>.

The definition of <convergence in distribution> gives $F_n(t)\to F(t)$ at each <continuity point> $t$ of $F$. We must handle a moving argument rather than assume this convergence is uniform.

A <cumulative distribution function> has at most countably many <discontinuities>: each is a jump of positive size, and there are at most $r$ jumps of size at least $1/r$, since their sizes sum to at most one. Thus we can choose <continuity points> $u_j<c<v_j$ with $u_j\uparrow c$ and $v_j\downarrow c$. For fixed $j$, eventually $u_j<c_n<v_j$. The <monotonicity> of each <cumulative distribution function> implies
$$
F_n(u_j)\leq F_n(c_n)\leq F_n(v_j).
$$
First let $n\to\infty$, using the definition of <convergence in distribution> at $u_j,v_j$. Then let $j\to\infty$, using <continuity> at $c$:
$$
F(u_j)\leq\liminf_nF_n(c_n)\leq\limsup_nF_n(c_n)\leq F(v_j),\qquad
\boxed{F_n(c_n)\longrightarrow F(c).}
$$
No result relating different <convergence of random variables> has been assumed.

Separate a long future count from the early <Poisson arrival time>. Let $\mathcal F_t=\sigma(N_s:0\leq s\leq t)$ be the <natural filtration>, and define
$$
A_n=\frac{N_{n^4}-N_{n^2}-(n^4-n^2)}{n^2},\qquad
B_n=\frac{\min(T_n,n^2)-n}{\sqrt n},\qquad
C_n=\frac{N_{n^2}-n^2}{n^2}.
$$
The truncated <Poisson arrival time> in $B_n$ is $\mathcal F_{n^2}$-measurable. The <independent increments> property makes $A_n$ independent of $\mathcal F_{n^2}$, and hence of $B_n$.

The numerator of $A_n$ is a centered <Poisson random variable> of parameter $n^4-n^2$. Apply the <central limit theorem> to the $n^4-n^2$ unit-time increments, then use $\sqrt{n^4-n^2}/n^2\to1$ and <Slutsky's theorem>. This proves $A_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm d}N(0,1)$. Also, <Markov's inequality> and $\mathbb ET_n=n$ give
$$
\mathbb P(T_n>n^2)\leq\frac1n\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus $B_n$ equals $(T_n-n)/\sqrt n$ outside an event of vanishing <probability>, so part (i) and <Slutsky's theorem> give $B_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm d}N(0,1)$. Exact <independence of random variables> for each $n$ now gives the joint limit $(A_n,B_n)\xrightarrow{\mathrm d}(Z_1,Z_2)$ with independent <standard normal random variables>.

Finally, $\mathbb EC_n=0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(C_n)=n^{-2}$, so <Chebyshev's inequality> gives $C_n\to0$ in <probability>. The first coordinate in the question is $A_n+C_n$. The second differs from $B_n$ only on $\{T_n>n^2\}$, and so its difference tends to zero in <probability>. The vector version of <Slutsky's theorem> proves the required joint <convergence in distribution>. The boundary of $(-\infty,x]\times[y,\infty)$ has zero limiting <probability>, since both <marginal distributions> have <continuous> <cumulative distribution functions>. The <Portmanteau theorem> therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\!\left(\frac{N_{n^4}-n^4}{n^2}\leq x,\frac{T_n-n}{\sqrt n}\geq y\right)\longrightarrow\Phi(x)\bigl(1-\Phi(y)\bigr).}
$$
This is $\mathbb P(Z_1\leq x,Z_2\geq y)$ by <independence of random variables>.

The possible limiting <correlation coefficients> are
$$
\boxed{-1\leq\rho\leq0.}
$$
The direction of the dependence comes from inversion of the same <counting process>: extra arrivals produce a larger count but an earlier $n$-th <Poisson arrival time>. Shared fluctuations therefore enter the two coordinates with opposite signs. Positive limiting <correlation> cannot arise; this can be formalized using <negative quadrant dependence of a Poisson count and an arrival time>.

At the same time scale, $f(n)=n$, part (iii) rules out every positive-positive quadrant. A <bivariate normal distribution> with standard <normal> <marginal distributions> and $\rho>-1$ assigns positive <probability> to such a quadrant. Thus, under the assumed joint limit, this choice forces $\rho=-1$. At very different time scales, $f(n)=n^4$, part (iv) gives $\rho=0$.

Intermediate time scales retain an intermediate fraction of the same arrival fluctuations. In particular, the <Poisson count-arrival joint central limit theorem> gives $\rho=-1/\sqrt\lambda$ for $f(n)=\lambda n$ with $1\leq\lambda<\infty$. These choices realize every $\rho\in[-1,0)$, and $f(n)=n^4$ supplies zero. This identifies the whole interval without requiring an additional limit calculation in this part.

The premise about a <bivariate normal distribution> is understood to apply when a joint limit exists. Merely requiring $f(n)\to\infty$ cannot guarantee one: taking $f(n)=n$ for even $n$ and $f(n)=n^4$ for odd $n$ gives different limits along the two <subsequences> just identified.

The <uniqueness theorem for moment-generating functions> states: if two real <random variables> $V,W$ have finite <moment-generating functions> on a common open <interval> $(-\delta,\delta)$ with $\delta>0$, and
$$
M_V(t)=\mathbb E e^{tV}=\mathbb E e^{tW}=M_W(t)\qquad(|t|<\delta),
$$
then $\boxed{V\text{ and }W\text{ have the same probability distribution}.}$ Finiteness throughout a neighborhood of zero is a hypothesis; the identity $M_V(0)=M_W(0)=1$ alone carries no information.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let $X_f$ have the specified <probability density function> $f_m$. Splitting the <integral> into the positive and negative half-lines, and using the <Gamma integral> $\int_0^\infty x^re^{-sx}\,dx=r!/s^{r+1}$ for a nonnegative <integer> $r$ and $s>0$, gives
$$
M_f(t)=\frac{2^m}{m!}\int_0^\infty x^m\bigl(e^{-(2-t)x}+e^{-(2+t)x}\bigr)\,dx
=2^m\left(\frac1{(2-t)^{m+1}}+\frac1{(2+t)^{m+1}}\right),\quad |t|<2.
$$
At $t=2$ or $t=-2$, one term becomes the divergent <integral> of $x^m$; beyond these endpoints it grows exponentially. Consequently, the full extended-valued <moment-generating function> is
$$
\boxed{M_f(t)=\begin{cases}2^m\bigl((2-t)^{-m-1}+(2+t)^{-m-1}\bigr),&|t|<2,\\+\infty,&|t|\geq2.\end{cases}}
$$
In particular $M_f(0)=1$, checking <normalization of a probability distribution>. All <absolute moments> exist. Direct integration yields
$$
\mathbb E|X_f|^k=\frac{2^{m+1}}{m!}\frac{(m+k)!}{2^{m+k+1}}=\frac{(m+k)!}{2^k m!}.
$$
The <symmetric distribution> makes every odd <moment> zero, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbb EX_f^k=\begin{cases}0,&k\text{ odd},\\(m+k)!/(2^k m!),&k\text{ even},\end{cases}\qquad k\geq1.}
$$

Now let $X_g$ have <probability density function> $g_m$. Its <moment-generating function> would be
$$
M_g(t)=\frac{m+1}{2}\int_1^\infty x^{-m-2}\bigl(e^{tx}+e^{-tx}\bigr)\,dx.
$$
For every $t\ne0$, one summand has exponential growth, which dominates the polynomial decay. Hence
$$
\boxed{M_g(0)=1,\qquad M_g(t)=+\infty\quad(t\ne0).}
$$
There is no finite <moment-generating function> on any neighborhood of zero, but some <moments> still exist. Specifically,
$$
\mathbb E|X_g|^k=(m+1)\int_1^\infty x^{k-m-2}\,dx
=\frac{m+1}{m+1-k}\quad\text{if }k<m+1.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbb EX_g^k=\begin{cases}0,&k\text{ odd and }k<m+1,\\(m+1)/(m+1-k),&k\text{ even and }k<m+1.\end{cases}}
$$
For even $k\geq m+1$, the <moment> is $+\infty$, so it is not a finite <moment>. For odd $k\geq m+1$, both the positive and negative contributions have infinite <integral>, so the <expected value> is undefined. Cancellation in a <Cauchy principal value> does not define an <expected value>. In particular, when $m=0$ there are no finite positive-order <moments>.

First justify that the <moments> being used exist. For every $r>0$,
$$
\mathbb E e^{r|Y|}\leq\mathbb E e^{rY}+\mathbb E e^{-rY}=M_Y(r)+M_Y(-r)<\infty.
$$
The bound $|y|^k\leq k!r^{-k}e^{r|y|}$ gives $\mathbb E|Y|^k<\infty$ for all $k$.

If $Y$ has a <symmetric distribution>, then $Y$ and $-Y$ have the same <probability distribution>, so for odd $k$,
$$
\mathbb EY^k=\mathbb E(-Y)^k=-\mathbb EY^k,
$$
which forces $\mathbb EY^k=0$.

Conversely, suppose every odd <moment> vanishes. For fixed $t\in\mathbb R$, the absolute values of the partial <Taylor series> for $e^{tY}$ are bounded by $e^{|t||Y|}$, whose <expected value> is finite by the preceding inequality. The <dominated convergence theorem> therefore permits termwise <expectation>:
$$
M_Y(t)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{t^k\mathbb EY^k}{k!}
=\sum_{j=0}^\infty\frac{t^{2j}\mathbb EY^{2j}}{(2j)!}=M_Y(-t)=M_{-Y}(t).
$$
The <uniqueness theorem for moment-generating functions> implies equality of the <probability distributions> of $Y$ and $-Y$. Hence
$$
\boxed{Y\text{ has a symmetric distribution}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \mathbb EY^k=0\text{ for every odd }k.}
$$

It suffices that the <moment-generating function> be finite on some open <interval> containing zero:
$$
\boxed{M_Y(t)<\infty\text{ for every }|t|<\delta\text{, for some }\delta>0.}

The history $\sigma(S_0,\ldots,S_n)$ is contained in $\sigma(Y_1,\ldots,Y_n)$. The increment $Y_{n+1}$ is independent of this history by the given <independence of random variables>. Therefore
$$
\mathbb P(S_{n+1}=j\mid S_0,\ldots,S_n)
=\mathbb P(Y_{n+1}=j-S_n\mid S_0,\ldots,S_n)
=p\mathbf1_{\{j=S_n+a\}}+q\mathbf1_{\{j=S_n-b\}}.

This depends only on the current state and not on time. It is precisely the <time-homogeneous Markov property>, so $S_n$ is a <Markov chain> with <transition probabilities>
$$
\boxed{p_{i,j}=\begin{cases}b/(a+b),&j=i+a,\\a/(a+b),&j=i-b,\\0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad i,j\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
The conditional identity is an identity that holds <almost surely>; histories of zero <probability> require no division by their <probabilities>.

Fix any two <integers> $i,j$. By <Bézout's identity>, there are <integers> $u,v$ with $au-bv=j-i$. Replacing $(u,v)$ by $(u+kb,v+ka)$ leaves this identity unchanged. This applies in both directions, so every pair of states communicates. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{The Markov chain is irreducible, with sole communicating class }\mathbb Z.}

A return path with $u$ upward steps and $v$ downward steps satisfies $au=bv$. Since $a,b$ are <coprime>, this is equivalent to $u=bk$ and $v=ak$ for an <integer> $k\geq1$. Its length is therefore $k(a+b)$.

$$
\boxed{\text{Every state has period }a+b\geq2.}
$$
In particular, none of the states is <aperiodic>.

Suppose a <stationary distribution> $\pi$ exists. Its <stationarity equations> are
$$
\pi_j=p\pi_{j-a}+q\pi_{j+b}\qquad(j\in\mathbb Z).
$$
Since $\sum_j\pi_j=1$, its masses tend to zero as $|j|\to\infty$, and a positive maximum $M=\pi_{j_0}$ is attained. Both terms on the right are at most $M$. Since $p,q>0$ and $p+q=1$, equality forces $\pi_{j_0-a}=\pi_{j_0+b}=M$. Applying the same argument repeatedly at $j_0-a,j_0-2a,\ldots$ gives
$$
\pi_{j_0-ka}=M>0\qquad(k\geq0).
$$
These are infinitely many distinct states, contradicting $\sum_j\pi_j=1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{There is no stationary distribution.}}
$$
This <maximum principle> proof uses neither a finite <expected value> of the state nor the <coprimality> assumption.

First identify the recurrence type, rather than infer an occupation limit from a one-time <probability>. At time $k(a+b)$, return to zero requires exactly $kb$ upward steps. The <binomial distribution> gives
$$
\mathbb P_0(S_{k(a+b)}=0)=\binom{k(a+b)}{kb}\left(\frac b{a+b}\right)^{kb}\left(\frac a{a+b}\right)^{ka}
\sim\sqrt{\frac{a+b}{2\pi abk}},
$$
where the last step is <Stirling's approximation>. At all other positive times the return <probability> is zero. Hence $\sum_{n\geq0}\mathbb P_0(S_n=0)=\infty$. The <recurrence criterion by return probabilities> proves the <recurrent> property; by the <irreducible Markov chain> property established in (i), every state is <recurrent>. The equivalence of <positive recurrence> and existence of a <stationary distribution> for an <irreducible Markov chain> on a <countable> <state space>, together with (iii), makes this chain <null recurrent>.

For completeness, the zero occupation proportion follows directly by treating returns as a <renewal process>. Let $L_1,L_2,\ldots$ be successive strictly positive return-cycle lengths to zero. The <strong Markov property> makes them <independent and identically distributed>, the <recurrent> property makes them finite <almost surely>, and the <null recurrent> property gives $\mathbb EL_1=\infty$. For each positive <integer> $K$, the <strong law of large numbers> applied to $\min(L_j,K)$ gives
$$
\liminf_{r\to\infty}\frac{L_1+\cdots+L_r}{r}\geq\mathbb E\min(L_1,K)\quad\text{almost surely}.
$$
Take a common event of <probability> one for these countably many $K$. By the <monotone convergence theorem>, the right side increases to infinity. Thus the number of completed return cycles by time $n$, divided by $n$, tends to zero. Counting the initial visit changes this ratio by at most $1/n$. Consequently,
$$
\boxed{\frac1n\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\mathbf1_{\{S_r=0\}}\longrightarrow0\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$
The walk visits zero infinitely often, but the asymptotic fraction of time spent there is zero.

= c
{parent=3}
{scope}

Take an <integer> $c\geq\max\{a,b\}$. On $I=\{-c,\ldots,c\}$, the modified <transition matrix> $Q=(q_{i,j})$ agrees with $P$. Above $c$, it stays put with <probability> $a/(a+b)$ and jumps to each of $c-b+1,\ldots,c$ with <probability> $1/(a+b)$. Below $-c$, it stays put with <probability> $b/(a+b)$ and jumps to each of $-c,\ldots,-c+a-1$ with <probability> $1/(a+b)$. All unspecified entries are zero. These rows sum to one. The <integer> qualification is necessary: if $c$ were nonintegral, $i=\lfloor c\rfloor+1$ would fit none of the printed row conditions.

= i
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}
{c}

Define
$$
L=\{-c-b,\ldots,-c-1\},\quad U=\{c+1,\ldots,c+a\},\quad
C=L\cup I\cup U=\{-c-b,\ldots,c+a\}.
$$
An interior jump cannot go below $-c-b$ or above $c+a$, and every exterior reset lands in $I$. Thus $C$ is closed.

To prove that it is one <communicating class>, start anywhere in $C$. From $L$ or $U$, a reset can reach $I$ with positive <probability>. From any state of $I$, repeated $+a$ steps reach some state of $U$, after staying in $I$ up to the last step. A reset from this state can reach every point of the consecutive block
$$
B=\{c-b+1,\ldots,c\}.
$$
Every <integer> $j\in\{-c-b,\ldots,c\}$ has the form $t-kb$ for some $t\in B$ and an <integer> $k\geq0$. Repeated $-b$ steps from $t$ reach $j$, with all departure states in $I$ until a possible last step into $L$. This gives access to all of $I\cup L$. Finally, each $j\in U$ can be reached from $j-a\in I$. Every path described has strictly positive <probability>. Therefore every state in $C$ reaches every other state in $C$.

Each state outside $C$ can only stay put or reset into $I$, and no transition from $C$ returns to it. Different states outside $C$ cannot reach one another. Hence the complete list of <communicating classes> is
$$
\boxed{C=\{-c-b,\ldots,c+a\}\text{, and }\{i\}\text{ for each }i\notin C.}
$$
The finite class $C$ is the unique <closed communicating class>. The exterior singleton classes are <transient communicating classes>, since their self-transition <probabilities> are strictly less than one. Notice that this reachability proof does not require $a,b$ to be <coprime>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

The restriction $Q_C$ is a <finite Markov chain> that is an <irreducible Markov chain>. It is <aperiodic>, because its upper states have self-transition <probability> $a/(a+b)>0$, and the <period is constant on a communicating class>. We now find its <stationary distribution>.

Every column of $Q_C$ sums to one. Here is the full incoming-mass check, with each total measured in units of $1/(a+b)$. For $j\in L$, the incoming contributions are $a$ from $j+b\in I$ and $b$ from the self-transition. For $j\in U$, they are $b$ from $j-a\in I$ and $a$ from the self-transition.

For $j\in I$, the upward predecessor $j-a$ lies in $I$ exactly when $j\geq-c+a$, contributing $b$ units. When $j\leq-c+a-1$, that predecessor is replaced by resets from all $b$ states of $L$, again contributing $b$ units. Independently, the downward predecessor $j+b$ contributes $a$ units when $j\leq c-b$; when $j\geq c-b+1$, resets from the $a$ states of $U$ contribute those $a$ units instead. Thus every column total is $a+b$ units.

Therefore $Q_C$ is a <doubly stochastic matrix>, and the <uniform distribution> on $C$ is stationary:
$$
\boxed{\pi_j=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac1{2c+a+b+1},&-c-b\leq j\leq c+a,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}}
$$
The <countable-state Markov chain convergence theorem>, applied to the finite closed class $C$, gives $Q_C^n(i,j)\to\pi_j$ for every $i\in C$. Its hypotheses hold: $C$ is finite and irreducible, hence <positive recurrent>, and it is <aperiodic>.

If $i_0>c+a$, the <hitting time> $H$ of $C$ has $\mathbb P(H>n)=(a/(a+b))^n$. If $i_0<-c-b$, the corresponding tail is $(b/(a+b))^n$. Thus $H<\infty$ <almost surely> from every exterior initial state. Conditioning on $H$ and $R_H$, use convergence within $C$ after entrance and the <bounded convergence theorem>; equivalently, first restrict to $H\leq K$ and then let $K\to\infty$. This proves, for every $i_0\in\mathbb Z$,
$$
\boxed{R_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm d}\operatorname{Unif}\{-c-b,\ldots,c+a\}.}
$$
The <limiting distribution> is independent of $i_0$.

= Solution

Let $d=\gcd(a,b)>1$ and put $a'=a/d$, $b'=b/d$. Then $a',b'$ are <coprime integers>, and $p=b'/(a'+b')$. The six requested revisions are as follows.

**(b)(i): <communicating classes>.** Every increment is divisible by $d$, so the <residue class> modulo $d$ is preserved. Conversely, if $j-i$ is divisible by $d$, <Bézout's identity> applied to $a',b'$ gives a path just as in (b)(i). Thus the original <Markov chain> is reducible on $\mathbb Z$, with exactly the $d$ closed <communicating classes>
$$
\boxed{r+d\mathbb Z,\qquad r=0,1,\ldots,d-1.}
$$
The specified walk starting at zero stays in $d\mathbb Z$.

**(b)(ii): <period of a state in a Markov chain>.** The return equation $au=bv$ reduces to $a'u=b'v$. Hence $u=b'k$, $v=a'k$. For every state, the <period of a state in a Markov chain> is therefore
$$
\boxed{a'+b'=\frac{a+b}{d}.}
$$
This is still at least two, so no state is <aperiodic>.

**(b)(iii): <stationary distribution>.** The conclusion is unchanged: there is no <stationary distribution> on $\mathbb Z$. The maximum-mass proof in (b)(iii) applies verbatim, without using <coprimality>. Equivalently, each closed <communicating class>, after translating and dividing its state labels by $d$, is the same centered <random walk> with step sizes $a',-b'$ and has no <stationary distribution>.

**(b)(iv): occupation of zero.** On $d\mathbb Z$, the rescaled walk $S_n/d$ has exactly the <transition probabilities> of the coprime walk with parameters $a',b'$. The return-probability argument in (b)(iv) therefore makes this class <null recurrent>. The <renewal process> proof of the occupation limit applies unchanged:
$$
\boxed{\frac1n\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\mathbf1_{\{S_r=0\}}\longrightarrow0\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$
Starting in another <residue class> would make every visit to zero impossible, also giving occupation proportion zero.

**(c)(i): modified <communicating classes>.** These are unchanged: $C=\{-c-b,\ldots,c+a\}$ is one finite <closed communicating class>, and all other states are transient singleton classes. The original proof used consecutive reset destinations and never used <coprimality>. Resetting to the $b$ consecutive integers $c-b+1,\ldots,c$ connects different <residue classes>, so it would be incorrect to split $C$ according to <residue classes> modulo $d$.

**(c)(ii): <limiting distribution>.** The column-sum calculation, the <irreducible Markov chain> property on a finite class, positive self-transition <probabilities>, and geometric entrance argument also required no <coprimality>. Thus for every initial state the same <convergence in distribution> holds:
$$
\boxed{R_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm d}\operatorname{Unif}\{-c-b,\ldots,c+a\}.}
$$
In particular, the denominator $2c+a+b+1$ is unchanged; it is not divided by $d$.
