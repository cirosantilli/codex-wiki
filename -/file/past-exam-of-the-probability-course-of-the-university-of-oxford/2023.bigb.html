= Solution

The <strong law of large numbers> states that, for <independent and identically distributed random variables> $X_1,X_2,\ldots$ with $\mathbb E|X_1|<\infty$,
$$
\boxed{\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^nX_j\longrightarrow\mathbb E X_1\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$
Thus, outside an event of <probability> zero, the <sample mean> converges to the common <expected value>.

= Solution

For a <Borel set> $A\subseteq\mathbb R$, the <joint probability density> gives
$$
\mathbb P(XY\in A)=\int_{\mathbb R}\int_{\mathbb R}\mathbf1_A(xy)f(x,y)\,dy\,dx.
$$
For each $x\ne0$, substitute $z=xy$. The <change of variables for probability densities> contributes $dy=dz/|x|$, with the absolute value accounting for the reversed orientation when $x<0$. The line $x=0$ has two-dimensional <Lebesgue measure> zero, so omitting it changes no <probability>. By the <Tonelli theorem>,
$$
\mathbb P(XY\in A)=\int_A\left[\int_{\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}}\frac1{|x|}f\left(x,\frac zx\right)\,dx\right]dz.
$$
The bracket is nonnegative and its <integral> over $\mathbb R$ is one. This proves the <probability density of a product of random variables> formula:
$$
\boxed{f_Z(z)=\int_{\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}}\frac1{|x|}f\left(x,\frac zx\right)\,dx.}
$$
As usual, a <probability density function> is determined only almost everywhere; exceptional infinite values are harmless.

For the <independent random variables> with the specified <uniform distribution>, $f(x,y)=b^{-2}$ on $(0,b)^2$ and zero elsewhere. When $0<z<b^2$, the conditions $0<x<b$ and $0<z/x<b$ are equivalent to $z/b<x<b$. Therefore
$$
f_Z(z)=\frac1{b^2}\int_{z/b}^{b}\frac{dx}{x}=\frac1{b^2}\log\frac{b^2}{z},
\qquad
\boxed{f_Z(z)=\begin{cases}b^{-2}\log(b^2/z),&0<z<b^2,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}}
$$
The value assigned at zero is arbitrary. Substitution $s=z/b^2$ checks <normalization of a probability distribution>: $\int_0^1-\log s\,ds=1$. This is the <product of independent uniform random variables> law.

Direct integration of the <uniform distribution> yields $\mathbb E U_j=b/2$ and $\mathbb E U_j^2=b^2/3$. The <expectation of a product of independent random variables> gives
$$
\mathbb EW_n=\prod_{j=1}^n\mathbb E U_j=(b/2)^n,\qquad \mathbb EW_n^2=(b^2/3)^n.
$$
Subtracting the square of the <expected value> gives the <variance>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb EW_n=(b/2)^n,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(W_n)=(b^2/3)^n-(b^2/4)^n.}
$$
Since $W_n\geq0$, <Markov's inequality> implies, for every $\varepsilon>0$,
$$
\mathbb P(W_n>\varepsilon)\leq\frac{(b/2)^n}{\varepsilon}\longrightarrow0\quad(0<b<2).
$$
Hence $\boxed{W_n\to0\text{ in probability for }0<b<2.}$ The first <moment> suffices: the <variance> need not tend to zero throughout this range.

If $0<b\leq1$, then $0<U_j\leq1$ <almost surely>. Intersecting these events over all $j$ still gives an event of <probability> one. On it, $0\leq W_{n+1}\leq W_n$, so the products have a finite nonnegative limit $W_\infty$.

For every $\varepsilon>0$, the inclusion $\{W_\infty>\varepsilon\}\subseteq\{W_n>\varepsilon\}$ and part (c)(i) imply $\mathbb P(W_\infty>\varepsilon)=0$. Taking $\varepsilon=1/m$ for positive integers $m$ proves $W_\infty=0$ <almost surely>. Thus
$$
\boxed{W_n\longrightarrow0\quad\text{almost surely for }0<b\leq1.}
$$
The monotonicity is the extra ingredient that upgrades <convergence in probability> to <almost sure convergence> here.

For $\alpha>0$, integration and <independence of random variables> give
$$
\mathbb EU_1^\alpha=\frac1b\int_0^bu^\alpha\,du=\frac{b^\alpha}{1+\alpha},\qquad \mathbb EW_n^\alpha=\left(\frac{b^\alpha}{1+\alpha}\right)^n.
$$
By the <fractional moment criterion for convergence in probability>,
$$
\mathbb P(W_n>\varepsilon)\leq\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\left(\frac{b^\alpha}{1+\alpha}\right)^n.
$$
The parenthesized factor is less than one precisely when $\alpha\log b-\log(1+\alpha)<0$. This expression vanishes at $\alpha=0$ and has <derivative> $\log b-1$ there. If $b<e$, the <derivative> is negative, so some sufficiently small $\alpha>0$ makes the factor strictly less than one. The bound then tends to zero geometrically, proving
$$
\boxed{W_n\longrightarrow0\quad\text{in probability for every }0<b<e.}
$$

The inverse <transformation of a random variable> is $u=e^{-v}$, with absolute <derivative> $e^{-v}$. The condition $0<u<b$ becomes $v>-\log b$. The <change of variables for probability densities> gives
$$
\boxed{f_{V_n}(v)=\begin{cases}b^{-1}e^{-v},&v>-\log b,\\0,&v\leq-\log b.\end{cases}}
$$
Equivalently, $V_n+\log b$ has the <exponential distribution> of rate one. Applying a separate measurable <transformation of a random variable> to each independent $U_n$ preserves <independence of random variables>, so the $V_n$ are <independent and identically distributed>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Taking <logarithms> of the product gives
$$
\boxed{W_n=\exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^nV_j\right).}
$$
By part (d)(i), $V_j=E_j-\log b$ in <probability distribution>, where $E_j$ has the <exponential distribution> of rate one. Thus $\mathbb E|V_j|\leq1+|\log b|<\infty$ and $\mathbb EV_j=1-\log b$. The <strong law of large numbers> gives
$$
\frac1n\log W_n=-\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^nV_j\longrightarrow\log b-1\quad\text{almost surely}.
$$
If $b<e$, choose $\delta=(1-\log b)/2>0$. Eventually $\log W_n\leq-\delta n$, so $W_n\leq e^{-\delta n}\to0$. If $b>e$, choose $\delta=(\log b-1)/2>0$; eventually $W_n\geq e^{\delta n}\to\infty$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{W_n\to0\text{ a.s. if }0<b<e;\qquad W_n\to\infty\text{ a.s. if }b>e.}
$$
In particular, the second range cannot have <almost sure convergence> to zero. This is the <almost-sure growth rate of independent products>. At $b=e$ the logarithmic limit is zero, which by itself decides neither behavior; that boundary case is not requested.

The <uniqueness theorem for moment-generating functions> states that if $M_X$ and $M_Y$ are finite and equal on an open <real interval> containing zero, then
$$
\boxed{X\overset d=Y.}
$$
For the <convergence theorem for moment-generating functions>, suppose that the <moment-generating functions> $M_{X_n}$ are finite on a common open <real interval> $(-a,a)$, $a>0$, and $M_{X_n}(t)\to M_X(t)$ for every $|t|<a$, where $M_X$ is finite there. Then
$$
\boxed{X_n\overset d\longrightarrow X.}
$$
One may equivalently assume convergence to a finite function $M$ on that interval: the theorem then asserts existence of a unique limiting <probability distribution> with <moment-generating function> $M$ as well as <convergence in distribution>.

$$
M_U(t)=\frac12\int_{-1}^1e^{tu}\,du=\frac{e^t-e^{-t}}{2t},\qquad \boxed{M_U(t)=\begin{cases}\sinh(t)/t,&t\ne0,\\1,&t=0.\end{cases}}

The value at zero follows either directly from the definition of the <moment-generating function> or by continuity. This answer needs no assumption about $B$.

If $B$ and $U$ are <independent random variables>, conditioning on the <Bernoulli random variable> $B$ gives
$$
\boxed{M_Z(t)=(1-p)+pM_U(t)=1-p+p\frac{\sinh t}{t},}
$$
with the continuous value $M_Z(0)=1$. Indeed $Z=0$ when $B=0$, while the conditional <probability distribution> of $Z$ given $B=1$ is the original <uniform distribution>.

Under the literal assumptions alone, the <law of total expectation> gives only
$$
M_Z(t)=1-p+p\mathbb E[e^{tU}\mid B=1].
$$
This is not determined by $M_U$ and $p$. For an explicit counterexample, put $p=1/2$ and $B=\mathbf1_{\{U>0\}}$. Then $B$ has the required <Bernoulli distribution>, but
$$
M_Z(t)=\frac12+\frac12\int_0^1e^{tu}\,du=\frac12+\frac{e^t-1}{2t}\quad(t\ne0),
$$
which differs from the independent-case formula.

Under <independence of random variables>, the <law of a random variable> $Z$ is the <mixed probability distribution> $(1-p)\delta_0+p\operatorname{Unif}(-1,1)$. For negative $z$, only the uniform component can contribute to $\mathbb P(Z\leq z)$; at zero the <probability mass> $1-p$ is included. Thus its <cumulative distribution function> is
$$
\boxed{F_Z(z)=\begin{cases}0,&z<-1,\\p(z+1)/2,&-1\leq z<0,\\1-p+p(z+1)/2,&0\leq z<1,\\1,&z\geq1.\end{cases}}
$$
The sketch consists of a horizontal segment at height zero before $-1$, a line of slope $p/2$ ending at the open point $(0,p/2)$, a jump of size $1-p$ to the closed point $(0,1-p/2)$, another line of slope $p/2$ ending at $(1,1)$, and a horizontal segment thereafter. The <cumulative distribution function> is right-continuous at the jump.

The jump at zero is still exactly $1-p$, because $\mathbb P(U=0)=0$. In the dependent counterexample from (ii), $F_Z(z)=0$ for $z<0$, $F_Z(z)=(1+z)/2$ for $0\leq z<1$, and $F_Z(z)=1$ for $z\geq1$.

Fix $0<c<1$ and choose $R_c$, independently of $X$, with the <normal distribution>
$$
\boxed{R_c\sim\mathcal N\bigl((1-c)\mu,(1-c^2)\sigma^2\bigr).}
$$
The <linear combination of independent normal random variables> $cX+R_c$ is normal. Its <expected value> is $c\mu+(1-c)\mu=\mu$ and its <variance> is $c^2\sigma^2+(1-c^2)\sigma^2=\sigma^2$. Hence $cX+R_c\overset d=X$ for every $c$, proving that every specified <normal distribution> is a <self-decomposable distribution>.

= Solution

Fix $|t|<t_0$. Since $|ct|<t_0$, $0<M_X(ct)<\infty$. Using <independence of random variables> and the <Tonelli theorem> for the positive exponential integrands gives the extended-real identity
$$
M_X(t)=M_{cX+R}(t)=M_X(ct)M_R(t).
$$
The left side is finite and the first factor is finite and strictly positive. Consequently $M_R(t)$ is finite and
$$
M_R(t)=\frac{M_X(t)}{M_X(ct)}.
$$
The same reasoning applies to $R'$, so their <moment-generating functions> are finite and equal throughout $(-t_0,t_0)$. The <uniqueness theorem for moment-generating functions> yields
$$
\boxed{R\overset d=R'.}
$$
This proves <uniqueness of a self-decomposition remainder> while also checking the required finiteness; no independence between $R$ and $R'$ is needed.

For $X\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)$, integration gives $M_X(t)=\lambda/(\lambda-t)$ for $t<\lambda$. For $0<c<1$, the candidate remainder <moment-generating function> is
$$
\frac{M_X(t)}{M_X(ct)}=\frac{\lambda-ct}{\lambda-t}=c+(1-c)\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-t}.
$$
This is the <moment-generating function> of the <mixture distribution>
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L(R_c)=c\delta_0+(1-c)\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda).}
$$
Construct it by taking an <exponential random variable> $E$ of rate $\lambda$ and a <Bernoulli random variable> $J$ with $\mathbb P(J=1)=1-c$, with $E,J,X$ mutually independent, and setting $R_c=JE$. Then $R_c$ is independent of $X$, and
$$
M_{cX+R_c}(t)=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-ct}\frac{\lambda-ct}{\lambda-t}=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-t}=M_X(t)
$$
on a neighborhood of zero. By the <uniqueness theorem for moment-generating functions>, $cX+R_c\overset d=X$, so the <exponential distribution> is a <self-decomposable distribution>.

For <independent and identically distributed random variables> $X_j\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$, the <linear combination of independent normal random variables> $n^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^nX_j$ has <expected value> zero and <variance> $n^{-1}n\sigma^2=\sigma^2$. Thus

\boxed{n^{-1/2}(X_1+\cdots+X_n)\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\quad(n\geq1).}

This is exactly the requested $2$-stability. The paper's definition is a <strictly stable distribution>, since it allows no centering term.

First take integers $1\leq k<n$. For independent copies $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ of $X$, define
$$
A=k^{-1/\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^kX_j,\qquad B=(n-k)^{-1/\alpha}\sum_{j=k+1}^nX_j.
$$
The two blocks involve disjoint families of <independent random variables>, so $A$ and $B$ are independent. The assumed <strictly stable distribution> gives $A\overset d=X$, $B\overset d=X$, and
$$
(k/n)^{1/\alpha}A+(1-k/n)^{1/\alpha}B=n^{-1/\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^nX_j\overset d=X.
$$
Therefore, on any fixed <probability space> carrying $X$ and an independent copy $X'$, their <joint probability distribution> gives
$$
(k/n)^{1/\alpha}X+(1-k/n)^{1/\alpha}X'\overset d=X.
$$
Now fix an arbitrary $0<c<1$ and choose rationals $r_m=k_m/n_m\in(0,1)$ tending to $c^\alpha$. Continuity of the real powers gives, on that fixed <probability space>,
$$
r_m^{1/\alpha}X+(1-r_m)^{1/\alpha}X'\longrightarrow cX+(1-c^\alpha)^{1/\alpha}X'\quad\text{almost surely}.
$$
The left sides all have the <probability distribution> of $X$. Since <almost sure convergence> implies <convergence in distribution>, their limit has that same <probability distribution>. Hence
$$
\boxed{R_c=(1-c^\alpha)^{1/\alpha}X',\qquad R_c\text{ independent of }X,\qquad cX+R_c\overset d=X.}
$$
This proves <strict stability implies self-decomposability>. The limit argument uses no <moment-generating function> or finite <moments>, which is essential for general <stable distributions>.

A <probability distribution> on the countable <state space> $\mathcal S$ is precisely a row vector satisfying
$$
\boxed{\pi_i\geq0\quad(i\in\mathcal S),\qquad\sum_{i\in\mathcal S}\pi_i=1.}
$$
A <transition matrix> is a <stochastic matrix>, meaning
$$
\boxed{p_{i,j}\geq0\quad(i,j\in\mathcal S),\qquad\sum_{j\in\mathcal S}p_{i,j}=1\quad(i\in\mathcal S).}
$$
Every row therefore gives the conditional <probability distribution> of the next state given the present state. These conditions do not require $\pi$ to be an <invariant distribution> of $P$.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The assumed identities are the <detailed balance equations>. Summing over $i$ and using the row sums of the <transition matrix> gives, for every $j$,
$$
(\pi P)_j=\sum_i\pi_ip_{i,j}=\sum_i\pi_jp_{j,i}=\pi_j\sum_ip_{j,i}=\pi_j.
$$
All summands are nonnegative, so these countable sums are justified. Thus
$$
\boxed{\pi P=\pi,}
$$
and $\pi$ is an <invariant distribution>.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For the <countable-state Markov chain ergodic theorem>, let the <Markov chain> be irreducible and positive recurrent, with its unique <stationary distribution> $\pi$. From any initial state, for every real $f$ satisfying $\sum_j\pi_j|f(j)|<\infty$,
$$
\boxed{\frac1n\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}f(X_m)\longrightarrow\sum_j\pi_jf(j)\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$
In particular, $n^{-1}\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\mathbf1_{\{X_m=j\}}\to\pi_j$ <almost surely>. This time-average conclusion requires no <aperiodicity>.

The <countable-state Markov chain convergence theorem> adds the hypothesis of an <aperiodic Markov chain> and concludes
$$
\boxed{P^n(i,j)\longrightarrow\pi_j\quad\text{for every }i,j.}
$$
It also follows that $\mathbb P_\nu(X_n=j)\to\pi_j$ for every initial <probability distribution> $\nu$. For an irreducible recurrent chain, the occupation-frequency theorem can also be stated as a limit $1/\mathbb E_jT_j^+$, interpreted as zero for a <null recurrent state>; in the positive recurrent case this equals $\pi_j$.

Under the specified evolution rule, conditional on the entire history, the next event's <probability distribution> depends only on the current population size. This is the <time-homogeneous Markov property>. Birth and immigration both increase the size by one, while death decreases it by one. Writing $q=1-p$, the <transition probabilities> on $\mathbb Z_{\geq0}$ are
$$
\boxed{P(k,k+1)=u_k=\frac{kp+\theta}{k+\theta}\quad(k\geq0),\qquad P(k,k-1)=d_k=\frac{kq}{k+\theta}\quad(k\geq1),}
$$
and all other entries are zero. In particular $P(0,1)=1$ and $d_0=0$. The row sum is $[kp+\theta+kq]/(k+\theta)=1$. Every neighboring pair communicates, so this is an <irreducible Markov chain>, specifically a <birth-death-immigration jump chain>.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

We use the <stationary weights of a reflected birth-death chain>, also verifying why they decide the question. If a <stationary distribution> $\pi$ exists, its equation at zero is $\pi_0=\pi_1d_1=\pi_0u_0$. At $k\geq1$, stationarity gives
$$
\pi_ku_k-\pi_{k+1}d_{k+1}=\pi_{k-1}u_{k-1}-\pi_kd_k.
$$
Thus the edge flow is constant, and its value at the boundary is zero. Every <stationary distribution> must therefore satisfy <detailed balance> on each edge. Since irreducibility forces $\pi_0>0$, it must be proportional to
$$
w_0=1,\qquad w_k=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{u_j}{d_{j+1}}.
$$
Conversely, if $\sum_kw_k<\infty$, normalizing these positive weights gives a <probability distribution> satisfying <detailed balance>, hence a <stationary distribution>. Existence of a <stationary distribution> for an <irreducible Markov chain> on a countable <state space> is equivalent to <positive recurrence>.

The ratio is
$$
\frac{w_{k+1}}{w_k}=\frac{(kp+\theta)(k+1+\theta)}{(k+\theta)(k+1)q}\longrightarrow\frac pq.
$$
For $p<1/2$, the limit is less than one, so the <ratio test> gives summability. For $p\geq1/2$, observe that $u_k=p+\theta q/(k+\theta)>p\geq1/2$, whereas $d_{k+1}<q\leq1/2$. Thus $w_{k+1}/w_k>1$ for every $k$, so the weights cannot be summable. This also resolves $p=1/2$ without making an inconclusive appeal to the limiting ratio. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{Positive recurrence holds exactly when }0<p<\tfrac12,\quad\theta>0.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Assume $p<1/2$, and put $a=\theta/p$ and $r=p/q\in(0,1)$. The <detailed balance equations> give
$$
\frac{\pi_{k+1}}{\pi_k}=\frac{p}{q}\frac{k+a}{k+1}\frac{k+1+\theta}{k+\theta}.
$$
Multiplying from $0$ to $k-1$, the last factor telescopes, while the product involving $a$ is evaluated by the <Gamma function>:
$$
\pi_k=\pi_0\frac{k+\theta}{\theta}\frac{\Gamma(k+a)}{\Gamma(a)k!}r^k\quad(k\geq0).
$$
Consequently the requested <invariant distribution> can be expressed using the paper's power series as
$$
\boxed{\pi_k=\frac{(k+\theta)\Gamma(k+\theta/p)}{k!\,g_{p,\theta}(p/q)}\left(\frac pq\right)^k,\quad k\geq0.}
$$
The assumed convergence of $g_{p,\theta}(r)$ ensures that this normalization is finite and positive.

For an explicit normalizing constant, the <binomial series> gives
$$
H(r)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{\Gamma(k+a)}{k!}r^k=\Gamma(a)(1-r)^{-a}.
$$
The <termwise differentiation of a power series> is valid for $|r|<1$, so
$$
\begin{aligned}
g_{p,\theta}(r)&=rH'(r)+\theta H(r)\\
&=\Gamma(a)(1-r)^{-a-1}\bigl[ar+\theta(1-r)\bigr]\\
&=2\theta\Gamma(a)(1-r)^{-a-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The last equality uses $r=p/q$ and $a=\theta/p$: $ar+\theta(1-r)=\theta/q+\theta(q-p)/q=2\theta$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\pi_k=\frac{(1-r)^{a+1}}{2\theta\Gamma(a)}\frac{(k+\theta)\Gamma(k+a)}{k!}r^k,\qquad \pi_0=\frac12(1-r)^{a+1}.}
$$
These positive terms sum to one and satisfy the <detailed balance equations>, so part (a)(ii) verifies stationarity. Irreducibility makes the <invariant distribution> unique.

Every step changes the population by one, so $X_n$ has the parity of $n$ when $X_0=0$. Hence $\mathbb P(X_{2m+1}=0)=0$. A return in two steps has positive <probability> from every state, and all return times are even, so the <period of a state in a Markov chain> is exactly two.

Each transition exchanges the even and odd classes. Since $\pi$ is an <invariant distribution>, their masses are equal: $\pi(\text{even})=\pi(\text{odd})=1/2$. The chain sampled at times $2m$, restricted to the even states, is irreducible and positive recurrent, and it is aperiodic because its diagonal entries are positive. Its <stationary distribution> is $2\pi_k$ on even $k$. The <countable-state Markov chain convergence theorem> applied to this sampled chain yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X_{2m}=0)\longrightarrow2\pi_0=(1-r)^{a+1},\qquad \mathbb P(X_{2m+1}=0)=0.}
$$
Since $\pi_0>0$, the full sequence has no limit. Its time average nevertheless tends to $\pi_0$ by the <countable-state Markov chain ergodic theorem>.

Let $I_n$ count immigration events during the first $n$ transitions, and let $A_n$ count upward steps during those transitions. Write $h(k)=\theta/(k+\theta)$ for the conditional immigration probability. If $J_m$ is the indicator that transition $m$ to $m+1$ is immigration, then $J_m-h(X_m)$ is a bounded <martingale difference> with respect to the history that records event types as well as population sizes. The <strong law for martingales with bounded increments> implies
$$
\frac{I_n}{n}-\frac1n\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}h(X_m)\longrightarrow0\quad\text{almost surely}.
$$
Since $h$ is bounded, the <countable-state Markov chain ergodic theorem> gives
$$
\frac{I_n}{n}\longrightarrow\sum_{k=0}^\infty\pi_k\frac{\theta}{k+\theta}\quad\text{almost surely}.
$$
Applying the same argument to upward-step indicators gives $A_n/n\to\sum_k\pi_ku_k$ <almost surely>. To evaluate this denominator, sum <detailed balance> over all neighboring edges:
$$
\sum_{k=0}^\infty\pi_ku_k=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\pi_{k+1}d_{k+1}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\pi_kd_k.
$$
Since $u_k+d_k=1$ and $\sum_k\pi_k=1$, both sums equal $1/2$. The denominator limit is positive, so division is valid and the <immigration fraction among upward steps> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{I_n}{A_n}\longrightarrow2\sum_{k=0}^\infty\pi_k\frac{\theta}{k+\theta}\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$
This is the requested answer in terms of $\pi$. Substituting part (iii) additionally gives $\sum_k\pi_k\theta/(k+\theta)=(1-r)/2$, so the fraction simplifies to $1-r=(1-2p)/(1-p)$.
