== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <sample maximum> has <cumulative distribution function>
$$
F_{M_n}(x)=\mathbb P(X_1\leq x,\ldots,X_n\leq x)
=\begin{cases}0,&x<0,\\x^n,&0\leq x\leq1,\\1,&x>1.\end{cases}
$$
The product $x^n$ follows from <independence of random variables> and the <continuous uniform distribution> of each observation. For every $x<1$, this <cumulative distribution function> tends to zero; for $x>1$ it equals one. These are exactly the values of the <cumulative distribution function> of the constant <random variable> $1$ at its continuity points. Therefore, by the definition of <convergence in distribution>,
$$
\boxed{M_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm d}1.}
$$
At $x=1$ the limiting <cumulative distribution function> is discontinuous, so the definition does not require checking that point, although $F_{M_n}(1)=1$ also holds.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $Z_n=n(1-M_n)$. Its <survival function> is
$$
\mathbb P(Z_n>z)=\begin{cases}
1,&z<0,\\
(1-z/n)^n,&0\leq z\leq n,\\
0,&z>n.
\end{cases}
$$
For each fixed $z\geq0$, eventually $n\geq z$, and the <exponential limit> gives $(1-z/n)^n\to e^{-z}$. Thus its <cumulative distribution function> tends to $0$ for $z<0$ and to $1-e^{-z}$ for $z\geq0$. This continuous limiting <cumulative distribution function> identifies the <exponential distribution> with rate one:
$$
\boxed{n(1-M_n)\xrightarrow{\mathrm d}\operatorname{Exp}(1).}
$$
This is the <exponential endpoint limit of a uniform maximum>: the gap from the upper endpoint is typically of order $1/n$, although the maximum itself approaches a constant.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <moment-generating function> of a real <random variable> $X$ is
$$
\boxed{M_X(t)=\mathbb E[e^{tX}]}
$$
for real $t$ for which this <expected value> is finite. Because the integrand is nonnegative, the extended <expected value> is always defined, but it may equal $+\infty$. We always have $M_X(0)=1$; the usual uniqueness and convergence theorems require finiteness on an open <interval> containing zero. When this condition holds, derivatives at zero recover the <moments>, $M_X^{(r)}(0)=\mathbb E[X^r]$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Using the <probability mass function> of the <Poisson distribution> and the power series of the <exponential function>,
$$
M_X(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}e^{tj}e^{-\mu}\frac{\mu^j}{j!}
=e^{-\mu}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\mu e^t)^j}{j!}
=\boxed{\exp\{\mu(e^t-1)\}.}
$$
This <moment-generating function of a Poisson distribution> is finite for every real $t$. It also covers $\mu=0$, when $X=0$ with <probability> one and the <moment-generating function> is identically one.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Complete the square in the integral against the <standard normal density>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
M_X(t)&=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{tx-x^2/2}\,dx\\
&=e^{t^2/2}\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-(x-t)^2/2}\,dx
=\boxed{e^{t^2/2}.}
\end{aligned}
$$
The remaining integral is one because its integrand is the <probability density function> of the <normal distribution> $N(t,1)$. Hence the <moment-generating function of a standard normal variable> is finite on the whole real line.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <Poisson process> gives $N_k\sim\operatorname{Pois}(k)$, with <expected value> and <variance> both equal to $k$. Consequently $Y_k=(N_k-k)/\sqrt{k}$. Scaling and translating the argument in the <moment-generating function of a Poisson distribution> gives, for every real $t$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{Y_k}(t)
&=e^{-t\sqrt{k}}M_{N_k}(t/\sqrt{k})\\
&=\boxed{\exp\!\left\{k\left(e^{t/\sqrt{k}}-1-\frac{t}{\sqrt{k}}\right)\right\}.}
\end{aligned}
$$
The centering term must remain inside the exponent: omitting it would leave a divergent first-order contribution.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For fixed real $t$, <Taylor's theorem> for the <exponential function> gives
$$
e^{t/\sqrt{k}}=1+\frac t{\sqrt{k}}+\frac{t^2}{2k}+O(k^{-3/2}).
$$
Substituting into the previous <moment-generating function>,
$$
\log M_{Y_k}(t)=\frac{t^2}{2}+O(k^{-1/2}),\qquad
M_{Y_k}(t)\longrightarrow e^{t^2/2}.
$$
The <continuity theorem for moment-generating functions> states that, if the <moment-generating functions> of $Z_n$ and $Z$ are finite on a common open <interval> containing zero and $M_{Z_n}(t)\to M_Z(t)$ throughout that interval, then $Z_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm d}Z$. Here finiteness holds for all real $t$, and the limit is the <moment-generating function of a standard normal variable>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{Y_k\xrightarrow{\mathrm d}N(0,1).}
$$
This proves the required instance of the <Poisson central limit theorem> directly from <moment-generating functions>; no appeal to a general <central limit theorem> is needed.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $A_k=N_{2k}-N_k$ and $B_k=N_k$. The <stationary increments> and <independent increments> of the <Poisson process> make $A_k$ and $B_k$ <independent random variables>, each with <Poisson distribution> of parameter $k$. Thus
$$
\frac{N_{2k}-2N_k}{\sqrt{k}}=\frac{A_k-B_k}{\sqrt{k}}.
$$
Multiplication of the two <moment-generating functions> gives
$$
M_k(t)=\exp\!\left\{k\left(e^{t/\sqrt{k}}+e^{-t/\sqrt{k}}-2\right)\right\}.
$$
The odd terms cancel in <Taylor's theorem>, so $\log M_k(t)=t^2+O(k^{-1})$. The limit $e^{t^2}$ is the <moment-generating function of a normal distribution> with <mean> zero and <variance> two. The same <continuity theorem for moment-generating functions> therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\frac{N_{2k}-2N_k}{\sqrt{k}}\xrightarrow{\mathrm d}N(0,2).}
$$
In particular, the two equal-length increments must be separated before using <independence>: $N_{2k}$ and $N_k$ themselves are dependent.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <joint probability density> of the two <independent> <standard normal random variables> is $(2\pi)^{-1}e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2}$. The <polar coordinates> map has absolute <Jacobian determinant> $r$, so the <change of variables for probability densities> gives
$$
\boxed{f_{R,\Theta}(r,\theta)=\frac r{2\pi}e^{-r^2/2}
\mathbf1_{\{r>0,\ 0\leq\theta<2\pi\}}.}
$$
At the origin the angle may be assigned arbitrarily: that point has <probability> zero. The <joint probability density> factorizes as a <Rayleigh distribution> density $r e^{-r^2/2}$ and a <continuous uniform distribution> density $1/(2\pi)$. Hence $R$ and $\Theta$ are <independent>.

For $u>0$, substitute $s=r^2/2$ to find
$$
\mathbb P(R^2\leq u)=\int_0^{\sqrt u}r e^{-r^2/2}\,dr
=\boxed{1-e^{-u/2}.}
$$
Therefore $R^2\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1/2)$, equivalently $\chi_2^2$, and applying a measurable function to $R$ preserves its <independence> from $\Theta$. Explicitly, with $S=R^2$,
$$
\boxed{f_{\Theta,S}(\theta,s)=\frac1{4\pi}e^{-s/2}
\mathbf1_{\{0\leq\theta<2\pi,\ s>0\}}.}
$$
Thus $\Theta\sim\operatorname{Unif}[0,2\pi)$ and $S\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1/2)$ independently. This <polar decomposition of a planar standard normal vector> separates the uniformly distributed direction from its random squared distance.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Set $\alpha=\arctan c\in(0,\pi/2)$. In <polar coordinates>, the event is $\sin\Theta>c|\cos\Theta|$, the angular sector
$$
\alpha<\Theta<\pi-\alpha.
$$
Because $\Theta$ has the <continuous uniform distribution> on an interval of length $2\pi$, the <probability> is the sector's angular length divided by $2\pi$:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(Y>c|X|)=\frac{\pi-2\arctan c}{2\pi}
=\frac12-\frac1\pi\arctan c.}
$$
The <symmetry> of the <standard normal distribution> in $Y$ makes the lower sector $Y<-c|X|$ equally likely. Their disjoint union is $|Y/X|>c$, apart from $X=0$, an event of <probability> zero. Therefore the requested <cumulative distribution function> is
$$
\boxed{F_{|Y/X|}(z)=\begin{cases}0,&z<0,\\[2pt]
\dfrac2\pi\arctan z,&z\geq0.
\end{cases}}
$$
This is the unit-scale <half-Cauchy distribution>. At $z=0$ there is no atom, and as $z\to\infty$ the <cumulative distribution function> tends to one.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The inverse <linear transformation> is
$$
x=2u-v,\qquad y=v-u,
\qquad
\left|\det\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|
=\left|\det\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}\right|=1.
$$
The positive support $x>0,y>0$ is exactly $u>0$ and $u<v<2u$. The <independence> and rate-one <exponential distributions> give $f_{X,Y}(x,y)=e^{-x-y}$ there. Since $x+y=u$, the <change of variables for probability densities> yields
$$
\boxed{f_{U,V}(u,v)=e^{-u}\mathbf1_{\{u>0,\ u<v<2u\}}.}
$$
Although the density's value depends only on $u$, its support does not factor as a product of fixed intervals. Thus this expression does not imply <independence> of $U$ and $V$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Integrating the <joint probability density> across $u<v<2u$ gives the <marginal density>
$$
f_U(u)=\int_u^{2u}e^{-u}\,dv=u e^{-u},\qquad u>0.
$$
For every $u>0$, the <conditional probability density> is consequently
$$
f_{V\mid U}(v\mid u)=\frac{f_{U,V}(u,v)}{f_U(u)}
=\frac1u\mathbf1_{\{u<v<2u\}}.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{V\mid U=u\sim\operatorname{Unif}(u,2u).}
$$
This also follows from <two independent exponential variables conditioned on their sum>: given $X+Y=u$, the variable $Y$ is uniform on $(0,u)$, and $V=u+Y$. Conditioning on $U=u$ here is defined through the <conditional probability density>, rather than division by $\mathbb P(U=u)$, which is zero. The endpoint $u=0$ lies outside the positive marginal support and needs no specified conditional law.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

For fixed $v>0$, the support inequalities become $v/2<u<v$. The <marginal density> is therefore
$$
\boxed{f_V(v)=\int_{v/2}^{v}e^{-u}\,du
=\begin{cases}e^{-v/2}-e^{-v},&v>0,\\0,&v\leq0.\end{cases}}
$$
It is nonnegative and integrates to $2-1=1$. Another interpretation is that $V=X+2Y$ sums <independent> <exponential random variables> of rates $1$ and $1/2$, so it has a <hypoexponential distribution>. The <mean> is $1+2=3$, which agrees with $\int_0^\infty v(e^{-v/2}-e^{-v})\,dv=4-1=3$.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <strong law of large numbers> says that, for real <independent and identically distributed random variables> $Z_1,Z_2,\ldots$ with $\mathbb E|Z_1|<\infty$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\!\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i\longrightarrow\mathbb E Z_1\right)=1.}
$$
The assertion is <almost sure convergence> of the entire sequence of <sample means>. Finite first absolute <moment> suffices; a finite <variance> is not required.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $q=1-p$ and $\xi_i=Y_i-Y_{i-1}$. The <Markov property> and the state-independent transition probabilities show that, conditional on the whole previous history, $\xi_i$ is $+1$ with <probability> $p$ and $-1$ with <probability> $q$. Multiplying these conditional probabilities for any finite step sequence proves that the $\xi_i$ are <independent and identically distributed random variables>, with
$$
\mathbb E\xi_i=p-q=2p-1>0.
$$
Because $Y_n=k+\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_i$, the <strong law of large numbers> gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{Y_n}{n}=\frac kn+\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_i
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\mu=2p-1.}
$$
In particular, $Y_n\to+\infty$ <almost surely>. This stronger pathwise consequence will determine both eventual crossings and the asymptotic <first-passage times>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $k=0$, the target is reached at time zero, so $h_0=1$. For $k<0$, the preceding <almost sure convergence> gives $Y_n\to+\infty$ with <probability> one. A <sample path> with unit upward and downward steps must visit every intermediate <integer> when it crosses from a negative value to a positive value. It must therefore visit zero at a finite time. Thus
$$
\boxed{h_k=1\quad\text{for every }k\leq0.}
$$
The unit-step property matters: positive long-run displacement alone would not force a walk with larger upward jumps to land exactly on zero.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <first-step analysis> gives $h_0=1$ and, for $k\geq1$,
$$
h_k=p h_{k+1}+q h_{k-1}.
$$
The <minimal nonnegative solution for hitting probabilities> theorem states that the probability of ever hitting a target is the pointwise smallest nonnegative solution of this transition recurrence with value one at the target. It follows by comparing any such solution with the probabilities of hitting within $n$ steps, then taking their increasing limit.

The characteristic equation $pr^2-r+q=0$ has distinct roots $1$ and $\beta=q/p\in(0,1)$. Hence every solution on the nonnegative integers with boundary value one has the form
$$
g_k=A+(1-A)\beta^k.
$$
If it is nonnegative for all $k$, taking $k\to\infty$ forces $A\geq0$, and therefore $g_k-\beta^k=A(1-\beta^k)\geq0$. The candidate $g_k=\beta^k$ is itself nonnegative and satisfies the recurrence and boundary value. It is consequently the minimal solution, giving the <biased random walk hitting probability>
$$
\boxed{h_k=\left(\frac{1-p}{p}\right)^k,\qquad k>0;\qquad
\beta=\frac{1-p}{p}.}
$$
On the negative half-line the already established values $h_k=1$ satisfy the same recurrence away from zero. No unproved boundary condition at infinity has been imposed.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Since $Y_n\to+\infty$ <almost surely>, only finitely many early values can attain the global <minimum>; it is a finite <integer>. Also $W\leq Y_0=0$. For every <nonnegative integer> $m$, the unit-step property and translation invariance of the increments give
$$
\mathbb P(-W\geq m)
=\mathbb P_0(\text{ever visit }-m)
=\mathbb P_m(\text{ever visit }0)=\beta^m,
\qquad\beta=\frac{1-p}{p}.
$$
Subtracting consecutive tails yields the <probability mass function>
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(-W=m)=(1-\beta)\beta^m,\qquad m=0,1,\ldots.}
$$
Thus $-W$ has the <geometric distribution> counting failures before the first success, with success parameter
$$
\boxed{1-\beta=\frac{2p-1}{p}.}
$$
This support convention is essential: the <minimum of an upward-biased random walk> can equal its starting position, so $-W=0$ has positive <probability>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Work on the single event of <probability> one where $Y_n/n\to\mu=2p-1>0$. On every <sample path> in that event, $Y_n\to+\infty$ and the unit-step property ensures that every positive <integer> $j$ is reached, so $T_j<\infty$. Starting from zero requires at least $j$ steps to reach $j$, hence $T_j\geq j$ and $T_j\to\infty$.

The convergent numerical sequence $Y_n/n$ on this fixed <sample path> also converges along its possibly random subsequence $n=T_j$. Since $Y_{T_j}=j$,
$$
\frac{j}{T_j}=\frac{Y_{T_j}}{T_j}\longrightarrow\mu.
$$
Taking the reciprocal is valid because $\mu>0$. We obtain
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\!\left(\frac{T_j}{j}\longrightarrow\alpha\right)=1,
\qquad\alpha=\frac1{2p-1}.}
$$
This is a <first-passage law of large numbers for an upward skip-free random walk>. No independence between the <hitting times> and the path is needed: <almost sure convergence> already supplies convergence along every diverging subsequence on the same event.
