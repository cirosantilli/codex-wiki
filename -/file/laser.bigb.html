= Laser
{wiki}

A laser generates amplified <light> through <stimulated emission>. Its name abbreviates “light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation”. Lasers supply the intense beams used in <optical tweezers>; <optical coherence> also makes them useful for <wave interference> measurements. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/8-6-lasers[OpenStax University Physics 3, §8.6: lasers]{ref}.

In the usual material picture, a gain medium stores <energy> supplied by pumping, for example by incident <light> or an <electric current>. <Stimulated emission> transfers that stored <energy> to a selected optical <normal mode>, while competing <photon absorption> removes <light>. Producing net <optical gain> requires a suitable population distribution, such as <population inversion> in the simple model below.

A familiar <laser> oscillator uses mirrors to return <light> through the gain medium. This optical feedback favours resonant <normal modes>; a partly transmitting mirror lets useful <power> escape. Feedback and mode selection have different implementations, including ring arrangements: every <laser> need not resemble the same two-mirror device. The <laser threshold> example treats just that device. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-6-006-video-demonstrations-in-lasers-and-optics-spring-2008/37b4c2aa5a6e3e0c119688024ce90ce5_Iqp7NxnwaGY.pdf[Shaoul Ezekiel, MIT, Laser fundamentals I: Simple laser, transcript]{ref} https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-974-fundamentals-of-photonics-quantum-electronics-spring-2006/18f30fad63a62ef4dd894d3752b55a60_chapter7.pdf[Franz Kärtner, MIT 6.974, Chapter 7, §7.1: laser oscillator concept]{ref}.

The useful combination is concentrated <power> and controlled optical <normal modes>. It gives <optical tweezers> a localized source of <force> and <wave interference> measurements a source with useful <optical coherence>. A <laser> can nevertheless emit several <frequencies>; “laser” alone does not guarantee a single <frequency>. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-6-006-video-demonstrations-in-lasers-and-optics-spring-2008/674c0c322cf0d87072e31de3e51f8e27_--Zi_cn4kPE.pdf[Shaoul Ezekiel, MIT, Laser fundamentals I: Spectrum of laser light, transcript]{ref}.

An original experimental landmark is T. H. Maiman's 1960 report of stimulated optical radiation from optically pumped ruby at about $694.3\,\mathrm{nm}$. His paper describes both the pumping path and the observed emission, connecting stored material <energy> with amplified <light>. <Stimulated Optical Radiation in Ruby by Theodore Harold Maiman>[T. H. Maiman, Stimulated Optical Radiation in Ruby, Nature 187, 493–494, 1960, original paper]{ref}.

Sustained stimulated optical emission in a resonator mode, supplied by the pump and reinforced through optical feedback. Its onset is governed by the <laser threshold>; an amplifying single pass alone does not establish oscillation.

= Four-level laser
{parent=Laser}

A pumping scheme with an auxiliary pump level, an upper laser level, a rapidly emptied lower laser level and a ground reservoir. = Laser gain medium

The material in which pumping produces <optical gain> through <stimulated emission>. Its inversion density, mode-overlap volume and emission cross section determine whether the gain overcomes <optical cavity>[cavity] losses.

= Laser rate equation
{parent=Laser}

A <laser rate equation> approximates coupled evolution of <population inversion> and intracavity <photon number>, including pumping, <spontaneous emission>, <stimulated emission> and <optical cavity> losses. A simple model is $\dot S=[g(N)-1/\tau_c]S$, $\dot N=R_p-N/\tau_u-g(N)S$, where compatible normalizations absorb mode-volume factors. Large excess <optical gain> drives rapid field growth until inversion depletion reduces it below threshold.

= Fibre-laser gain-volume rate equations
{parent=Laser rate equation}
{title2=$N_{\mathrm{th}}=(fv_g\sigma\tau_c)^{-1}$}

If a fraction $f$ of an otherwise uniform <optical cavity> contains gain medium, define upper-state <density> per gain volume but <photon> <density> per whole cavity volume. Stimulated upper-state depletion is $v_g\sigma Nn$, while cavity <photon> production is $fv_g\sigma Nn$. Thus $\dot N=S-v_g\sigma Nn-N/\tau_2$ and $\dot n=(fv_g\sigma N-1/\tau_c)n$. Above threshold $N=N_{\mathrm{th}}$ and $n=f\tau_cN_{\mathrm{th}}(r-1)/\tau_2$, where $r=S\tau_2/N_{\mathrm{th}}$.

= Laser relaxation oscillation
{parent=Laser rate equation}

Small perturbations of a class-B <laser> exchange excitation between the upper-state population and cavity <photon number>. <Linearization of a dynamical system>[Linearization] about a four-level lasing state gives $\ddot w+(r/\tau_2)\dot w+(r-1)w/(\tau_c\tau_2)=0$. Its damped <oscillation> <frequency> is $(2\pi)^{-1}\sqrt{(r-1)/(\tau_c\tau_2)-r^2/(4\tau_2^2)}$. Long population lifetime compared with <photon lifetime> permits underdamped relaxation.

= Laser spiking
{parent=Laser relaxation oscillation}

Large startup excursions of a <laser> can accumulate population above threshold and then release it through a short stimulated-emission pulse. Inversion depletion and pump recovery can produce a damped sequence of spikes. This is a nonlinear transient, distinguished from small-signal <laser relaxation oscillations> and from externally imposed <Q-switching>.

= Q-switching
{c}
{parent=Laser}
{wiki}

<Q-switching> creates a short high-energy <optical pulse> by first storing <population inversion> while an <optical cavity> has high loss, then increasing its <quality factor> so that <stimulated emission> rapidly extracts the stored excitation. Its duration depends on <photon lifetime>, excess <optical gain>, inversion depletion and switching time; it is not fixed by a universal time-bandwidth equality.

= Q-switched energy extraction efficiency
{c}
{parent=Q-switching}
{title2=$\eta=\beta^{-1}(1-N_f^*/N_i^*)$}

For an ideal pulse with negligible stored seed <photons> and useful output accounting for all <optical cavity>[cavity] <photon> loss, the extracted <energy> is $(N_i^*-N_f^*)V_g\hbar\omega_L/\beta$. The factor $\beta=1+g_2/g_1$ converts inversion depletion into stimulated transitions. With over-pumping $r=N_i^*/N_{\rm th}^*$, the nonzero extraction branch satisfies $r\beta\eta=-\ln(1-\beta\eta)$. A fraction $0.95$ of the maximum <energy> requires $r=-\ln(0.05)/0.95$.

= Three-level laser
{parent=Laser}

A <three-level laser> pumps emitters out of their <ground state> into a higher pump state, which rapidly relaxes into the upper lasing state. The lower lasing state is the <ground state> itself. <Photon absorption> on the lasing transition therefore competes with <stimulated emission> until enough emitters have been removed from the ground state. This is the <ground state> depletion problem implicit in <population inversion>.

The model below treats nondegenerate lasing <energy levels> with equal absorption and emission cross-sections. Its inversion condition is $N_2>N_1$. Real materials require the relevant sublevel populations, degeneracies and transition strengths; the simple half-population condition should not be imposed on every optical transition. Extra levels let the pump and the emitted <light> act on different transitions, avoiding the steady two-level saturation limit discussed in <population inversion>.

= Three-level ground-state inversion threshold
{parent=Three-level laser}
{title2=$R\tau_{21}>1$}

In a <three-level laser> whose lower lasing level is the ground state, fast relaxation from a pumped higher level feeds a long-lived upper lasing level. With equal lasing-level degeneracies, negligible pump-level population and pump rate R per ground-state emitter, the no-light stationary rate equation gives $N_2/N_1=R\tau_{21}$. Positive <population inversion> therefore requires more than half the active emitters to leave the ground state. This is a transparency threshold; <optical resonator> loss imposes an additional positive-gain requirement. Degenerate sublevels require population comparisons weighted by their statistical weights and transition strengths.

= Three-level laser rate equation
{parent=Three-level laser}
{title2=$\dot u=r(1-u)-u-C(2u-1)s$}

Consider an original reduced model with a fixed number $N$ of active emitters. Let $u=N_2/N$ be the upper lasing-state fraction, $1-u=N_1/N$ the ground-state fraction, and $s=n/N$ the number of cavity <photons> per emitter. Assume the pump state empties rapidly, so its population can be neglected. Measure time by $x=t/\tau$, where $\tau$ is the upper state's spontaneous lifetime. Let $r=W\tau$ be the effective pump rate, $C>0$ the dimensionless stimulated-coupling coefficient, $K>0$ the photon-loss rate in these units, and $0\leq\beta\leq1$ the fraction of spontaneous decays feeding this optical <normal mode>. The <rate equations> are
$$
\frac{du}{dx}=r(1-u)-u-C(2u-1)s,
\qquad
\frac{ds}{dx}=\bigl[C(2u-1)-K\bigr]s+\beta u.
$$
Each term has a physical role. Pumping transfers emitters out of the ground state; <spontaneous emission> removes upper-state population. The net stimulated term includes emission from fraction $u$ and absorption by fraction $1-u$, giving their difference $2u-1$. A stimulated transition removes one stored excitation and creates one cavity <photon>, or does the reverse. The factor $\beta$ accounts for spontaneous emission into the selected mode without pretending that all <spontaneous emission> enters it.

For negligible cavity light, the upper-state equation is linear. A constant pump applied to $u(0)=0$ gives
$$
u(x)=\frac{r}{r+1}\left(1-e^{-(r+1)x}\right),\qquad
u_\infty=\frac{r}{r+1}.
$$
Thus this model first permits <population inversion> at $r>1$. Positive <optical gain> is still insufficient for an oscillator: the gain must exceed the complete <laser threshold>, including photon loss. The small-signal photon growth coefficient changes sign when
$$
C(2u-1)=K,\qquad
u_{\rm th}=\frac{1+K/C}{2},\qquad
\boxed{r_{\rm th}=\frac{C+K}{C-K}\quad(C>K).}
$$
If $C\leq K$, even complete inversion cannot exceed the assumed loss in this model. For chosen $C=100$ and $K=20$, the threshold population is $u_{\rm th}=0.6$ and the pump threshold is $r_{\rm th}=1.5$. At $r=2$, the unsaturated population first reaches $0.6$ at $x=(\log10)/3\simeq0.7675$. These numbers are stipulated examples, not ruby material parameters.

= Laser gain clamping
{parent=Three-level laser rate equation}

= Inversion clamping
{synonym}

In the steady limit with nonzero cavity light and negligible spontaneous seeding, the photon equation requires $C(2u-1)=K$. The upper-state fraction is therefore fixed near $u_{\rm th}$ while increased pumping principally increases the photon population. Substituting into the population equation gives
$$
u=u_{\rm th},\qquad
s=\frac{r(1-u_{\rm th})-u_{\rm th}}{K}\quad(r>r_{\rm th}).
$$
For $C=100$, $K=20$ and $r=3$, this predicts $u=0.6$ and $s=0.03$. The inversion does not rise indefinitely above threshold. A finite $\beta$ supplies seed photons and smooths the ideal onset; with $\beta=0$ and exactly $s(0)=0$, the deterministic equations remain on the zero-photon solution even when that solution is unstable. This distinguishes instability of an unseeded state from actual startup of a noisy optical field.

= Stored excitation in a pulsed laser
{parent=Three-level laser rate equation}

A short optical pulse can draw on previously stored excitation. In the original calculation below, $C=100$, $K=20$, $\beta=10^{-4}$, $u(0)=s(0)=0$, and $r=3$ for $0\leq x<4$, followed by $r=0$. The cavity field initially remains small while pumping fills the upper state. Once sufficient gain develops, the photon population grows and depletes the stored inversion. Continuing pump action can refill it, giving <relaxation oscillations> in this simple model. This is not a fit to Maiman's recorded output.

The equations expose the excitation budget underlying <conservation of energy>. Adding them cancels the stimulated exchange:
$$
\frac{d(u+s)}{dx}=r(1-u)-(1-\beta)u-Ks.
$$
Pumping supplies excitations; nonlasing decay and cavity loss remove them. Integrating gives the bound
$$
\int_0^X Ks\,dx\leq u(0)+s(0)+\int_0^Xr(1-u)\,dx.
$$
Only part of the cavity loss may be useful output; passive absorption is also a loss. The pulse's instantaneous power can draw on stored excitation, while its total extracted energy still obeys the integrated budget. The model also preserves $0\leq u\leq1$ and $s\geq0$: at each boundary its derivatives point inward or along the boundary.

There is a further energy cost in the pump cycle. For one absorbed pump photon producing at most one emitted laser photon, the ideal energy-conversion bound is
$$
\eta\leq\frac{h\nu_{\rm laser}}{h\nu_{\rm pump}}
=\frac{\lambda_{\rm pump}}{\lambda_{\rm laser}}.
$$
Chosen wavelengths $550\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $694.3\,\mathrm{nm}$ give about $0.7922$ before other losses. The missing energy can leave through nonradiative relaxation and heating. Actual efficiency also depends on how much pump light is absorbed, unwanted decays and optical losses; a population model alone cannot predict it.

= Stimulated emission
{parent=Laser}
{wiki}

Stimulated emission is emission induced by incident <light>, in contrast with the unprompted decay in <spontaneous emission>. It supplies the amplification mechanism of a <laser>. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/8-6-lasers[OpenStax, §8.6: stimulated emission]{ref}.

For an emitter with upper and lower <energy> values $E_2>E_1$, the resonant <photon> has <frequency> $\nu$ satisfying
$$
\Delta E=E_2-E_1=h\nu,
$$
where $h$ is the <Planck constant>. In the elementary <photon> picture, an incident <photon> stimulates an emitter in the upper <quantum state> to emit an additional <photon> into the same optical <normal mode>, with the same <frequency>, propagation direction and <photon polarization>. The emitter supplies the extra <energy>; the stimulating <photon> is not consumed. This builds the existing field and its <optical coherence>. An emitter in the lower <quantum state> can instead undergo <photon absorption>, so <stimulated emission> alone does not establish net <optical gain>.

For example, a $2.0\,\mathrm{eV}$ gap corresponds to $\nu\approx4.8360\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and a <vacuum> <wavelength> of about $620\,\mathrm{nm}$. One ideal <stimulated emission> event converts one incoming <photon> into two outgoing <photons> in the selected optical <normal mode>, adding $2.0\,\mathrm{eV}$ of <light> <energy> while lowering the emitter's <energy> by that amount.

= Population inversion
{parent=Stimulated emission}
{title2=$N_2>N_1$}
{wiki}

A <population inversion> puts more emitters in the upper than in the lower <quantum state> of a transition in the nondegenerate two-level model. Let $N_2,N_1$ be their number densities. Equal stimulated-transition strengths make the emission rate proportional to $N_2$ and the competing <photon absorption> rate proportional to $N_1$, so net <optical gain> is proportional to $N_2-N_1$. For $N_2=6\times10^{20}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$ and $N_1=4\times10^{20}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$, the difference is positive; swapping them makes the medium absorb. Equal populations give zero net stimulated contribution. With degenerate levels, the relevant comparison uses populations per <statistical weight of an atomic level>, rather than simply $N_2>N_1$.

Why pump through extra levels? Ordinary steady resonant pumping of a nondegenerate two-level system drives both <photon absorption> and <stimulated emission>. With equal induced rate $W$ and upper-state decay rate $\Gamma$, its elementary population equation is
$$
\dot N_2=W(N_1-N_2)-\Gamma N_2,
\qquad
\frac{N_2}{N_1}=\frac{W}{W+\Gamma}\leq1
$$
at a stationary population. Increasing this pump saturates towards $N_2=N_1$; it cannot sustain <population inversion> in this model. This is a steady rate-equation conclusion, not a universal prohibition on all driven two-level dynamics or all possible <laser> gain mechanisms. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-974-fundamentals-of-photonics-quantum-electronics-spring-2006/2641db6431ea70989c29e8512d5d91a7_chapter6.pdf[Franz Kärtner, MIT 6.974, Chapter 6, §§6.7–6.8: saturation and rate equations]{ref}.

In a familiar three-level path, pumping excites a higher level which relaxes into the upper lasing level; the lower lasing level is the ground state, so substantial ground-state depletion is needed. A four-level path also provides rapid emptying of the lower lasing level into a separate ground state. Suitable lifetimes then allow accumulation above and depletion below the lasing transition. Pumping still supplies the <energy>: extra levels provide a route to <population inversion>, not free <energy>. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-974-fundamentals-of-photonics-quantum-electronics-spring-2006/18f30fad63a62ef4dd894d3752b55a60_chapter7.pdf[Franz Kärtner, MIT 6.974, §7.2.1: three- and four-level laser media]{ref}.

= Lower-level depopulation in a four-level laser
{parent=Population inversion}

A pump feeds an auxiliary high level, rapid relaxation populates the upper <laser> level, and rapid decay empties the lower <laser> level. This separates pumping from the <laser> transition and helps sustain net stimulated gain. Gain saturation and cavity loss still determine the oscillating steady state.

= Optical gain
{parent=Laser}
{title2=$g$}

<Optical gain> is amplification of <light> by a medium. Here the small-signal intensity gain coefficient $g$ has units $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$: for fixed populations, a weak beam obeys
$$
\frac{dI}{dz}=gI,
\qquad I(z)=I(0)e^{gz},
\qquad g=\sigma(N_2-N_1)
$$
in the nondegenerate resonant two-level model, with common transition cross-section $\sigma$. Thus $g$ includes the competition between <stimulated emission> and <photon absorption> on that transition. Separate passive intensity loss $\alpha$ gives $I(z)=I(0)e^{(g-\alpha)z}$. Both coefficients describe <light intensity>, not <wave amplitude>; the corresponding field-amplitude exponent is half as large. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-974-fundamentals-of-photonics-quantum-electronics-spring-2006/2641db6431ea70989c29e8512d5d91a7_chapter6.pdf[Franz Kärtner, MIT 6.974, §§6.7–6.8: amplification and stimulated-transition cross-section]{ref}.

For $g=0.30\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, $\alpha=0.10\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ and $z=0.50\,\mathrm m$, the single-pass intensity multiplier is $e^{0.10}\approx1.10517$. This is amplification during one passage, before mirror losses. <Population inversion> can therefore yield positive <optical gain> while the complete oscillator remains below <laser threshold>.

= Optical amplifier
{parent=Optical gain}

A medium in which stimulated emission can increase an incident optical signal. Its unsaturated gain, saturation response and loss budget determine the propagation. A population balance with negative net gain instead describes absorption.

= Saturated amplifier with exclusive upper-to-lower decay
{parent=Optical gain}
{title2=$I_s=\hbar\omega A_{21}/\sigma_{21}$}

An open pumped <two-level system> amplifier with fixed upper-state source $R_2$, upper <spontaneous emission> only into the lower <energy level>, and lower-state removal rate $N_1/\tau_1$. Let $r=g_2/g_1$, $W=\sigma_{21}I/(\hbar\omega)$ and $D=N_2-rN_1$. Steady <rate equations> give $N_1=R_2\tau_1$ and $D=R_2(1-rA_{21}\tau_1)/(A_{21}+W)$. Consequently the <optical gain> is $\alpha=\alpha_0/(1+I/I_s)$ with $\alpha_0=\sigma_{21}R_2(A_{21}^{-1}-r\tau_1)$. Lower-state removal controls whether <population inversion> is possible, while its lifetime cancels from this model's <saturation intensity>. Different upper-state branching or a nonconstant pump generally changes that conclusion.

= Laser threshold
{parent=Optical gain}
{title2=$g_{\mathrm{th}}$}

In a simple two-mirror oscillator, <laser threshold> is the small-signal <optical gain> at which a resonant optical <normal mode> exactly replaces its round-trip intensity loss. Assume constant intensity coefficients $g$ and $\alpha$ throughout a one-way distance $L$, and mirror intensity reflectivities $0<R_1,R_2\leq1$. One round trip has multiplier
$$
M=R_1R_2\exp\bigl(2(g-\alpha)L\bigr),
\qquad
I_{n+1}=MI_n.
$$
Two traversals give the distance $2L$; multiplying the two mirror factors completes the bookkeeping. Setting $M=1$ and taking a <logarithm> gives
$$
g_{\mathrm{th}}=\alpha-\frac{\ln(R_1R_2)}{2L}.
$$
The reflectivities here are intensity fractions, not field-amplitude reflectivities. A shorter gain region or nonuniform loss requires replacing the path exponent by the appropriate path integral. The balance also presumes a permitted resonant optical <normal mode>. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-974-fundamentals-of-photonics-quantum-electronics-spring-2006/18f30fad63a62ef4dd894d3752b55a60_chapter7.pdf[Franz Kärtner, MIT 6.974, §§7.1 and 7.5: round-trip balance and steady state; intensity convention specified here]{ref}.

For $L=0.50\,\mathrm m$, $R_1=1$, $R_2=0.90$ and $\alpha=0.10\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, the threshold is $g_{\mathrm{th}}\approx0.2053605\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$. At $g=0.15\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, $M\approx0.946144$: a small circulating signal decays even though the material has positive <optical gain>. At $g=0.30\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, $M\approx1.099262$: the signal initially grows. Raising passive loss or lowering mirror reflectivity raises <laser threshold>.

= Mode locking
{parent=Optical frequency comb}
{wiki=Mode-locking}

<Mode locking> fixes relative <phases> between longitudinal <normal modes> of a <laser>, allowing short <optical pulses>. The same <optical spectrum> with wandering relative <phases> would not supply that stable pulse train.<Passion for Precision by Theodor W. Hänsch>[Passion for Precision, p.157: coupled modes]{ref}

Here is an original finite-mode model, with the convention that a positive <frequency> has time dependence $e^{2\pi i f t}$:
$$
E(t)=\sum_{n=24}^{40}a_n e^{2\pi i(n f_r+f_0)t},\qquad
a_n=\frac{e^{-(n-32)^2/18}}{\sum_{j=24}^{40}e^{-(j-32)^2/18}}.
$$
These field coefficients follow a <Gaussian function> and have aligned <phases> at $t=0$. Choose $f_0=f_r/4$ and write $T=1/f_r$. Since $e^{2\pi i n}=1$ for every <integer> $n$,
$$
E(t+T)=e^{2\pi i f_0T}E(t)=iE(t),\qquad |E(t+T)|^2=|E(t)|^2.
$$
The pulse <light intensity> repeats every $T$ although the field needs four periods to repeat. At the pulse centers, $E(0)=1$, $E(T)=i$, $E(2T)=-1$ and $E(3T)=-i$. A detector of <light intensity> alone therefore cannot distinguish this offset from zero. This model has far fewer teeth than a practical optical comb; its normalized time and <frequency> scales are chosen to make the carrier oscillations visible.

= Optical frequency measurement with a comb
{parent=Optical frequency comb}
{title2=$f_L=n f_r+f_0+b$}

Mix an unknown single-frequency <laser> with a nearby comb tooth on a <photodiode>. Define the signed <beat> $b=f_L-f_n$, so
$$
f_L=n f_r+f_0+b.
$$
The <beat> arises from the cross term in <light intensity>:
$$
\left|A e^{2\pi i f_Lt}+B e^{2\pi i f_nt}\right|^2
=|A|^2+|B|^2+2\operatorname{Re}\!\left(AB^*e^{2\pi i b t}\right).
$$
An electronic detector can follow this slow difference even though it cannot resolve either optical oscillation. The square-law expression also shows why a real-valued intensity trace by itself loses the sign of $b$. A small known upward shift of the chosen tooth decreases $|b|$ when the unknown lies above it and increases $|b|$ when it lies below, provided the tooth assignment remains unchanged.

For an original example, stipulate
$$
f_r=250\,\mathrm{MHz},\qquad f_0=20\,\mathrm{MHz},\qquad b=+30\,\mathrm{MHz},\qquad n=2{,}000{,}000.
$$
Then $f_n=500{,}000.020\,\mathrm{GHz}$ and $f_L=500{,}000.050\,\mathrm{GHz}$. Choosing the opposite sign would give $499{,}999.990\,\mathrm{GHz}$ instead. The stipulated optical <frequency> is countable because the large optical value is expressed using the <integer> $n$ and three electronic <frequencies>; the diagram's two controls establish the comb, and this additional <beat> places the unknown against it.

Both comb readouts use one calibrated <microwave> reference. Feedback fixes $f_r$ and $f_0$, determining each tooth once $n$ is identified. A <frequency counter> measures the slow <beat>, transferring the calibration to the optical <frequency>.<Passion for Precision by Theodor W. Hänsch>[Passion for Precision, p.158: microwave link]{ref}

= Comb tooth index ambiguity
{parent=Optical frequency measurement with a comb}

One <beat> magnitude does not tell us the <integer> $n$. In the example, every proposed optical <frequency> $n(250\,\mathrm{MHz})+50\,\mathrm{MHz}$ produces the same signed <beat> with its corresponding tooth. A coarse independent optical measurement can select the <integer>: once the <beat> sign is known, an error bound below $f_r/2$ suffices if the candidate is selected by nearest distance.

There is also a useful two-spacing check. Keep the unknown <laser> and $f_0$ fixed, track the same tooth continuously, and increase $f_r$ by a stipulated $5\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Its <frequency> rises by $10\,\mathrm{MHz}$, so the signed <beat> falls from $30$ to $20\,\mathrm{MHz}$. Hence
$$
n=-\frac{\Delta b}{\Delta f_r}=\frac{10{,}000{,}000}{5}=2{,}000{,}000.
$$
This inference fails if the unknown drifts appreciably, the offset changes unnoticed, the <beat> sign is lost, or the tracker jumps to another tooth. The assumed tooth motion stays well below half the spacing in this example. If $f_0$ also changes by a measured amount, use $n=-(\Delta b+\Delta f_0)/\Delta f_r$.

For a fixed verified tooth number, the first-order <frequency> error is $\delta f_L=n\delta f_r+\delta f_0+\delta b$. Errors in these readings can be correlated because they share a reference. A small <beat> uncertainty therefore does not imply the same absolute optical accuracy: the reference's calibration and the factor $n$ remain in the error budget. A wrong tooth number is a separate discrete error, not a small correction. The beauty of the comb is that two controlled parameters organize millions of optical reference points, while the unknown's position is read electronically.

= Chirped pulse amplification energy budget
{parent=Chirped pulse amplification}
{title2=$U=\int P(t)\,dt$}

Let $P(t)$ be the pulse's <power> integrated over its transverse section, and $U$ its <energy>. Its <peak power> is $\max_t P(t)$. For a chosen Gaussian power envelope with <full width at half maximum> $\tau$,
$$
P(t)=P_{\rm pk}e^{-4\ln2\,t^2/\tau^2},\qquad
U=P_{\rm pk}\tau\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4\ln2}},\qquad
P_{\rm pk}=\sqrt{\frac{4\ln2}{\pi}}\frac{U}{\tau}.
$$
The numerical shape factor is approximately $0.9394$. It matters when comparing quoted <peak powers>; the estimate $U/\tau$ assumes a shape and a width convention.

If an otherwise unchanged pulse is stretched by a factor $S$ without loss, its <energy> stays fixed and its <peak power> falls by $S$. For energy gain $G$ and compressor energy transmission $\eta$, ideal restoration of the original shape gives
$$
U_{\rm out}=\eta G U_{\rm in},\qquad
\frac{P_{{\rm pk},\,\rm out}}{P_{{\rm pk},\,\rm amplified}}=\eta S.
$$
These relations require a specified shape and preserved spectrum; they do not model amplifier saturation. For the diagram's chosen $1\,\mathrm{nJ}$, $30\,\mathrm{fs}$ seed, $S=9.2959$, $G=1000$ and $\eta=0.8$, the successive <peak powers> are $31.31\,\mathrm{kW}$, $3.369\,\mathrm{kW}$, $3.369\,\mathrm{MW}$ and $25.05\,\mathrm{MW}$. The last pulse contains $0.8\,\mathrm{\mu J}$: the compressor increases <peak power> while losing some <energy>.

<Fluence> is time-integrated <light intensity>, $F(x,y)=\int I(x,y,t)\,dt$, measured in $\mathrm{J\,m^{-2}}$. For a uniform beam of area $\mathcal A$, $F=U/\mathcal A$ and $I_{\rm pk}=P_{\rm pk}/\mathcal A$. At fixed transverse profile, lossless temporal stretching lowers peak <light intensity> but leaves <fluence> unchanged. Absorbed <energy>, heat removal, beam size, coatings and pulse duration still constrain optical components. For nonoverlapping identical pulses at repetition <frequency> $f_r$, the average <power> is $U f_r$; it is a different quantity from <peak power>.

= Spectral phase in pulse compression
{parent=Chirped pulse amplification}
{title2=$\phi(\Omega)=\phi_0+\phi_1\Omega+\phi_2\Omega^2/2+\cdots$}

Use a complex envelope $A(t)$ normalized so that $|A(t)|^2=P(t)$; its units are $\mathrm{W}^{1/2}$. The optical carrier convention is $e^{-i\omega_0t}$: the physical <electric field> is proportional to the real part of $A(t)e^{-i\omega_0t}$ for a fixed transverse mode. With <angular frequency> offset $\Omega=\omega-\omega_0$, fix the <Fourier transform> convention
$$
\widetilde A(\Omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}A(t)e^{i\Omega t}\,dt,\qquad
A(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widetilde A(\Omega)e^{-i\Omega t}\,d\Omega.
$$
Write $\widetilde A=B e^{i\phi}$ with real $B\geq0$. A dispersive element adds <spectral phase>
$$
\phi(\Omega)=\phi_0+\phi_1\Omega+\frac{\phi_2}{2}\Omega^2+\frac{\phi_3}{6}\Omega^3+\cdots.
$$
Constant <phase> rotates the envelope; the linear term delays it by $\phi_1$. The <group delay> is $d\phi/d\Omega$. The quadratic coefficient $\phi_2$ is <group delay dispersion>, abbreviated GDD, with units of time squared. Positive GDD makes higher <frequencies> arrive later with this convention. It is accumulated dispersion of a component; material <group velocity dispersion> is its corresponding coefficient per unit length.

To undo a quadratic stretcher <spectral phase>, the compressor must supply opposite GDD. Include the amplifier and all intervening optics in the sum:
$$
\phi_{2,\rm total}=\phi_{2,\rm stretch}+\phi_{2,\rm amplifier}+\phi_{2,\rm compress}.
$$
Setting this sum to zero does not cancel <third-order dispersion>, $\phi_3$, or still higher orders. Residual <spectral phase> can produce asymmetry and satellite pulses even when the quadratic term vanishes. A <diffraction grating> system can make path length depend on <frequency>; matching the complete <spectral phase> over the occupied spectrum is more demanding than merely choosing opposite quadratic coefficients.

There is a useful proof of what phase correction can accomplish. By <Parseval's identity>,
$$
U=\int |A|^2\,dt=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int B^2\,d\Omega.
$$
For a smooth decaying spectrum, the <Fourier transform> of $tA$ is $-i\,d\widetilde A/d\Omega$. Consequently the power-weighted mean arrival time and <variance> obey
$$
\bar t=\frac{\int B^2\phi'\,d\Omega}{\int B^2\,d\Omega},\qquad
\sigma_t^2=\frac{\int (B')^2\,d\Omega}{\int B^2\,d\Omega}
+\frac{\int B^2(\phi'-\bar t)^2\,d\Omega}{\int B^2\,d\Omega}.
$$
At fixed spectral amplitude, constant <group delay> minimizes this <root mean square> duration. Such a pulse is a <transform-limited pulse>, up to a time shift and constant <phase>. The nonnegative second term measures temporal broadening from unequal arrival times; the first term remains even after perfect phase correction.

= Gaussian chirped pulse compression
{parent=Chirped pulse amplification}
{title2=$\tau(\phi_2)=\tau_0\sqrt{1+(4\ln2\,\phi_2/\tau_0^2)^2}$}

Choose an initially <transform-limited pulse> with Gaussian <power> and complex envelope
$$
A_0(t)=\sqrt{\frac{U}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_0}}\,e^{-t^2/(4\sigma_0^2)},\qquad
\tau_0=2\sqrt{2\ln2}\,\sigma_0.
$$
Here $\sigma_0$ is the standard deviation of the power-weighted time distribution; $\tau_0$ is its <full width at half maximum>. Neither is a field-amplitude width. The spectral amplitude is proportional to $e^{-\sigma_0^2\Omega^2}$, so the spectral power has <standard deviation> $\sigma_\Omega=1/(2\sigma_0)$. The Gaussian <time-bandwidth product> in ordinary <frequency> is $\tau_0\Delta\nu=2\ln2/\pi\simeq0.4413$, where both widths are power FWHMs.

Multiplication by $e^{i\phi_2\Omega^2/2}$ preserves spectral power and <energy>. Substituting $\phi'=\phi_2\Omega$ into the moment identity gives
$$
\sigma_t^2=\sigma_0^2+\frac{\phi_2^2}{4\sigma_0^2},\qquad
\tau(\phi_2)=\tau_0\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{4\ln2\,\phi_2}{\tau_0^2}\right)^2}.
$$
This is an exact result for a Gaussian spectrum with quadratic <spectral phase>. It also follows by completing the square in its inverse <Fourier transform>.

A real amplifier also changes spectral amplitude. For a simple small-signal example, stipulate an intensity gain profile
$$
G(\Omega)=G_0 e^{-\Omega^2/(2\sigma_g^2)},\qquad
\widetilde A_{\rm amplified}=\sqrt{G(\Omega)}\,\widetilde A_{\rm input}.
$$
This specifies finite <gain bandwidth>, with no saturation or extra amplifier <phase>. Multiplying two Gaussian spectra gives
$$
\frac{1}{\sigma_{\Omega,\rm out}^2}=\frac{1}{\sigma_\Omega^2}+\frac{1}{\sigma_g^2}.
$$
For chosen $\sigma_g=\sigma_\Omega$, the amplified spectrum is narrower by $\sqrt2$, the shortest Gaussian power FWHM becomes $30\sqrt2=42.43\,\mathrm{fs}$, and the total <energy> is $G_0U/\sqrt2$. Perfect <spectral phase> correction cannot recreate the suppressed spectral amplitude. <High harmonic generation> converts an intense driving <electric field> into radiation at high integer multiples of its <frequency>. <High harmonic generation> in a <gas> is a process in <nonlinear optics>: an <electron> can leave a neutral target by <tunnel ionization>, gain <kinetic energy> in the driving <electric field>, and return to radiate upon recombination. This three-step picture is an approximate account of a <probability amplitude> for emission in <quantum mechanics>, not a measured path of one <electron>.<Generation and Measurement of Isolated Attosecond Light Pulses by Michael Hentschel>[Generation and Measurement of Isolated Attosecond Light Pulses, slide 5: tunnelling, acceleration and return]{ref}

Why odd multiples? In an ideal centrosymmetric <gas> driven by a single <frequency>, assume the radiating <electric dipole moment> satisfies $d(t+T/2)=-d(t)$, where $T$ is the driving period. Its <Fourier series> coefficient obeys $d_q=-(-1)^q d_q$, so every even coefficient vanishes. A finite envelope, an oriented target or an additional driving <frequency> can invalidate that symmetry argument. Producing many odd components also does not prove that their <spectral phases> form a short <optical pulse>.

The 1988 report involving <Anne L'Huillier> observed odd multiples of a $1064\,\mathrm{nm}$ driving <wavelength> in rare gases, reaching order 33 in <argon>. The unexpectedly slow fall of yield beyond the fifth order supplied a broad spectral plateau. A spectral observation alone did not establish the relative <spectral phases> needed for an <attosecond pulse train>.<Multiple-harmonic conversion of 1064 nm radiation in rare gases by M. Ferray and others>[Multiple-harmonic conversion of 1064 nm radiation in rare gases, abstract]{ref}

= Three-step model of high harmonic generation
{parent=High harmonic generation}

For an original classical calculation of the return step, neglect the residual <ion>'s <Coulomb force> after release. Take a spatially uniform driving <electric field> $E(t)=E_0\cos\omega t$, an <electron> of <electric charge> $-e$ and <mass> $m$, and zero initial position and <velocity> at $t_i$. With $\alpha=\omega t_i$ and $\beta=\omega t$, integrating <Newton's second law> gives
$$
v(t)=-\frac{eE_0}{m\omega}(\sin\beta-\sin\alpha),\qquad
x(t)=\frac{eE_0}{m\omega^2}\left[\cos\beta-\cos\alpha+(\beta-\alpha)\sin\alpha\right].
$$
A return requires $\beta>\alpha$ and $x(t)=0$. Define the cycle-averaged free-electron quiver <kinetic energy>
$$
U_p=\frac{e^2E_0^2}{4m\omega^2},\qquad
K_{\rm return}=2U_p(\sin\beta-\sin\alpha)^2.
$$
Searching the first-return solutions for release phases $0<\alpha<\pi/2$ gives a maximum near $\alpha=0.3134$ and $\beta=4.3990$, with $K_{\rm return}\simeq3.1731U_p$. For a chosen <ionization energy> $I_p$, recombination into the initial state can therefore emit a <photon> with <energy> up to approximately $I_p+3.17U_p$ in this model. This is an upper return-energy scale, not a prediction of the emitted yield. It omits release probabilities, <quantum interference>, propagation, <phase matching> and depletion of the neutral <gas>.

The calculation explains why a long driving <wavelength> can increase the available return <energy>: at fixed $E_0$, $U_p$ grows as $\omega^{-2}$. It does not say that conversion efficiency improves. The <electron> must return with a <probability amplitude> for emission that survives the microscopic dynamics and adds coherently across the <gas>.

= Attosecond pulse train
{parent=Attosecond physics}

An <attosecond pulse train> contains repeated short <optical pulses>. For a group of odd multiples of a common driving <frequency>, the separation is half the driving period when the relative <spectral phases> recur. The construction resembles <mode locking>, but the participating components come from <high harmonic generation>, rather than separate resonant modes of a <laser> oscillator.

= Coherent harmonic pulse-train model
{parent=Attosecond pulse train}
{title2=$I(t)\propto[\sin(N\omega t)/\sin(\omega t)]^2$}

Choose $N$ equal-amplitude, monochromatic odd components with orders $q_j=q_0+2j$, $j=0,\ldots,N-1$, and odd $q_0$. Fix the complex-field convention
$$
\mathcal E(t)=a\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{i\phi_j-iq_j\omega t},\qquad I(t)\propto|\mathcal E(t)|^2.
$$
Here $I$ is a normalized <light intensity> after averaging the fast carrier oscillations. Setting every $\phi_j=0$ and summing a <geometric series> gives
$$
\frac{I(t)}{N^2|a|^2}=\left[\frac{\sin(N\omega t)}{N\sin(\omega t)}\right]^2.
$$
At the removable singularities take the continuous limit. Maxima repeat every $T/2=\pi/\omega$; the first zeros on either side of a maximum lie at $t=\pm T/(2N)$. The zero-to-zero width is $T/N$, which is not the <full width at half maximum>.

Choose orders $13,15,17,19,21$ and driving <wavelength> $\lambda=800\,\mathrm{nm}$. Using $T=\lambda/c$ with the <speed of light> $c$ gives $T=2.66851\,\mathrm{fs}$ and burst spacing $1.33426\,\mathrm{fs}$. Solving the exact half-maximum equation gives a <full width at half maximum> of approximately $240.6$ attoseconds. These are values of the specified five-component model, not measurements from the 2001 experiment.

A deliberately chosen <spectral phase> $\phi_q=q\omega t_0$ translates the whole <attosecond pulse train> by $t_0$ without changing its shape. Adding $\kappa(q-17)^2/2$ makes neighboring phase increments unequal: the same component intensities then produce a different temporal profile. For the choice $t_0=0.20\,\mathrm{fs}$ and $\kappa=0.08$ radians per squared harmonic order, the adjacent-component delay estimates are
$$
\frac{\phi_{q+2}-\phi_q}{2\omega}
=t_0+\frac{\kappa(q+1-17)}{\omega},
$$
namely $0.0981$, $0.1660$, $0.2340$ and $0.3019\,\mathrm{fs}$ for $q=13,15,17,19$. This <finite difference> estimates the <group delay> at the pair's midpoint. It is not a single common arrival time.

The infinite monochromatic sum is periodic forever and has infinite total <energy>. A real <attosecond pulse train> has a finite envelope and finite component linewidths. This calculation isolates coherent addition; it cannot describe an isolated burst or establish its contrast against satellites. As a further check, averaging $I(t)$ over one half-period gives $N|a|^2$ for any constant component <spectral phases>, because the cross terms integrate to zero by <Fourier orthogonality>. <Spectral phase> redistributes the <light intensity> in time while leaving that average fixed.

= Isolated attosecond pulse
{parent=Attosecond physics}

An <isolated attosecond pulse> has one dominant short burst within the observation window, with any satellite emission quantified. A broad <optical spectrum> alone cannot distinguish one burst from several. Selecting a generation interval and an appropriate spectral band can suppress neighboring bursts, but the resulting temporal contrast must still be tested.

For a simple calculation independent of any apparatus, let $\mathcal E(t)=f(t)+r e^{i\theta}f(t-\Delta)$, with two nonoverlapping copies of a short <optical pulse>. Its <optical spectrum> has intensity
$$
|\widetilde{\mathcal E}(\Omega)|^2
=|\widetilde f(\Omega)|^2\left[1+r^2+2r\cos(\Omega\Delta+\theta)\right].
$$
A satellite produces spectral fringes of angular spacing $2\pi/\Delta$ when the two bursts are coherent and resolved. For chosen field-amplitude ratio $r=0.2$, the satellite/main <energy> ratio is $r^2=0.04$, while fringe visibility is $2r/(1+r^2)\simeq0.3846$. Weak satellite <energy> can thus leave substantial fringes. Averaging an uncontrolled $\theta$ removes the interference term without removing the satellite: absence of fringes alone does not prove isolation.

= RABBITT
{c}
{synonym}

<RABBITT> measures adjacent harmonic <spectral phases> through two-photon <photoionization>. A weak delayed <infrared> reference opens two interfering paths to a shared <photoelectron> <energy>; the <two-path attosecond phase reconstruction> spells out the inference.

= Two-path attosecond phase reconstruction
{parent=Reconstruction of attosecond beating by interference of two-photon transitions}
{title2=$S_q(\tau)=C_q+D_q\cos(2\omega\tau-\Delta\phi_q+\delta_q)$}

An original two-amplitude model makes the timing inference explicit. Use the <spectral phase> convention of the <coherent harmonic pulse-train model>, and define positive delay $\tau$ to retard the reference <electric field>. Let the amplitudes leading to the same final <photoelectron> state be
$$
M_-(\tau)=b_-e^{i(\phi_{q-1}+\omega\tau+\eta_-)},\qquad
M_+(\tau)=b_+e^{i(\phi_{q+1}-\omega\tau+\eta_+)},
$$
with real $b_\pm\geq0$. Squaring their sum gives
$$
S_q(\tau)=b_-^2+b_+^2+2b_-b_+\cos(2\omega\tau-\Delta\phi_q+\delta_q),
\qquad \Delta\phi_q=\phi_{q+1}-\phi_{q-1},\quad\delta_q=\eta_--\eta_+.
$$
The $2\omega$ oscillation comes from opposite signs of the reference <photon>'s <phase>. For $\Delta\phi_q=2\omega t_0$ and $\delta_q=0$, the signal peaks at $\tau=t_0$ modulo $T/2$. A fitted phase contains the target contribution $\delta_q$; setting it to zero is an assumption, not an observation. In an experiment, unobserved final channels can add as probabilities, so the displayed formula is one effective-channel idealization.

For a chosen example, impose $\Delta\phi_q=0.80$ radians and $\delta_q=0.15$ radians at $800\,\mathrm{nm}$. The observed oscillation has argument $2\omega\tau-0.65$. Its peak shift is $0.1380\,\mathrm{fs}$, while the corrected finite-difference <group delay> is $\Delta\phi_q/(2\omega)=0.1699\,\mathrm{fs}$. Ignoring the target contribution would bias the inferred delay by $-31.9$ attoseconds. These stipulated phases demonstrate a reconstruction error; they are not atomic calculations or measured delays.

Recovering all neighboring phase differences determines the component <spectral phases> up to an overall <phase> and the chosen timing origin, provided the oscillation phases are consistently unwrapped. Known component amplitudes then give a temporal field by a finite inverse <Fourier transform>. This is why measuring the <optical spectrum> alone is insufficient: it omits the very information that decides when constructive <wave interference> occurs.

= 2001 attosecond pulse-train experiment
{parent=Reconstruction of attosecond beating by interference of two-photon transitions}

<Pierre Agostini>'s experiment illuminated an <argon> jet with an annular $800\,\mathrm{nm}$ beam. A pinhole removed the annulus; a tungsten-coated mirror relayed harmonics and weak central reference into another <argon> jet. A magnetic-bottle <electron spectrometer> used <time of flight> and <microchannel plate detectors>. Glass plates varied delay. Reference-on sidebands contrasted with reference-off peaks. Atomic-phase-corrected reconstruction yielded $250$-attosecond bursts.<Observation of a Train of Attosecond Pulses from High Harmonic Generation by P. M. Paul and others>[pp.1690–1691, Figs.1–4 and Table 1]{ref}

The <two-path attosecond phase reconstruction> explains the control's purpose: adding the reference opens an interference channel. Its fitted oscillation tests phase relationships under the weak-field and atomic-response model. It does not independently reveal every target phase or an arbitrary nonperiodic <optical pulse>.

= Attosecond streaking
{parent=Attosecond physics}

<Attosecond streaking> uses a synchronized reference <electric field> to change a <photoelectron>'s outgoing <momentum>. Scanning relative delay produces an <energy>-versus-delay distribution. A short emission interval samples only a small part of the reference cycle; a longer interval samples a range of kicks and can broaden or reshape the distribution.

For an explicit classical limit, use the <electric dipole> approximation and choose the <magnetic vector potential> $A(t)$ along the detection axis with $E(t)=-dA/dt$ and $A(\infty)=0$. Neglect the residual <ion>'s <Coulomb force>. An <electron> released at $t_i$ with mechanical <momentum> $p_0$ obeys
$$
p_f=p_0-eA(t_i),\qquad
K_f=\frac{[p_0-eA(t_i)]^2}{2m}.
$$
Indeed, integrating $\dot p=-eE=e\dot A$ from release to the end of the field gives the stated minus sign. The useful clock variable here is $A$, rather than the instantaneous $E$ itself. If $A$ is nearly linear across a narrow emission window with temporal <standard deviation> $\sigma_t$, and initial <momentum> is sharp, then
$$
\sigma_p\simeq e|E(t_i)|\sigma_t.
$$
This local mapping fails near a zero of $E$, where first-order timing sensitivity vanishes. A real inversion must include the incident bandwidth, initial <photoelectron> distribution, collection angles and target–field response.

For an original dimensionless example in <atomic units>, choose $p_0=2$, $m=e=1$ and $A(t_i)=0.10$. Then $p_f=1.9$ and $K_f=1.805$, compared with initial <kinetic energy> $2$. Reversing $A$ gives $K_f=2.205$: the shifts are asymmetric because of the $A^2/2$ term. A measurement that keeps only the term linear in $A$ would miss $0.005$ in both cases.

= 2001 isolated attosecond pulse experiment
{parent=Attosecond streaking}

The experiment involving <Ferenc Krausz> combined a subfemtosecond soft-<X-ray> pulse with a few-cycle visible <electric field>. Its delay-dependent <krypton> <photoelectron> spectra tested the field-controlled response. The reported pulse duration was $650\pm150$ attoseconds. Changing delay compared the emitted <electron> distributions at different reference phases; the spectral-width trace supplied a further duration-sensitive observable.<Attosecond metrology by Michael Hentschel and others>[Attosecond metrology, abstract and Figs.3–5: krypton spectra, delay scan and width]{ref}

The distinction from <RABBITT> is the measurement regime and reconstruction, rather than merely calling one trace a clock. The <attosecond streaking> <momentum> map is a limiting model; a finite-duration <wave packet> requires a quantum transition calculation. Inferring an emission delay also requires separating the illuminating pulse's <spectral phase> from the target response. The measurement supports time-dependent <photoionization> in a specified model, with no unique classical <electron> trajectory inside an <atomic orbital> implied.
