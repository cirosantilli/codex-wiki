For a nonempty <set> $A\subseteq\mathbb R$, a <supremum> is a <real number> $s$ such that $a\leq s$ for every $a\in A$, and every <upper bound> $u$ of $A$ satisfies $s\leq u$. Equivalently, $s$ is an <upper bound> and, for every $\varepsilon>0$, some $a\in A$ satisfies $a>s-\varepsilon$. By <completeness of the real numbers>, it exists whenever $A$ is nonempty and bounded above.

An increasing <monotone sequence> satisfies $a_{n+1}\geq a_n$ for every $n$; a <bounded sequence> satisfies $|a_n|\leq M$ for some finite $M$ and every $n$. Put $s=\sup\{a_n:n\geq1\}$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, the defining property of the <supremum> supplies $N$ with $a_N>s-\varepsilon$. For every $n\geq N$, <monotonicity> gives
$$
s-\varepsilon<a_N\leq a_n\leq s.
$$
This is exactly the definition of <convergent sequence>, so the <bounded monotone sequence theorem> gives $\boxed{a_n\to s}$.

= Solution

Choose $M$ with $|b_n|\leq M$. Every <tail of a sequence> is nonempty and bounded, so its <supremum> $c_n$ exists and lies in $[-M,M]$. Removing one term cannot increase a <supremum>, hence $c_{n+1}\leq c_n$. The <bounded monotone sequence theorem>, applied to $-c_n$, proves
$$
c_n\longrightarrow L=\inf_n c_n=\limsup_{n\to\infty}b_n.
$$
Thus $L$ is the <limit superior> of $(b_n)$.

For $\varepsilon>0$, eventually $c_n<L+\varepsilon$, and then $b_n\leq c_n<L+\varepsilon$. Only finitely many indices can satisfy $b_n\geq L+\varepsilon$. If only finitely many satisfied $b_n>L-\varepsilon$, some entire <tail of a sequence> would have $b_n\leq L-\varepsilon$. Its <supremum> would also be at most $L-\varepsilon$, contradicting $c_n\geq L$.

Inductively choose $n_k>n_{k-1}$ so late that $c_{n_k}<L+1/k$ and $b_{n_k}>L-1/k$. There are infinitely many choices for the latter inequality, so both requirements can be met. Then $|b_{n_k}-L|<1/k$, proving $\boxed{b_{n_k}\to L}$. This constructs a <subsequence> attaining the <limit superior>.

A <series> $\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n$ converges when its <partial sums> $s_N=\sum_{n=1}^N a_n$ have a finite <limit of a sequence>.

The <comparison test> says that if $0\leq a_n\leq b_n$ eventually and $\sum b_n$ converges, then $\sum a_n$ converges. Indeed, the <partial sums> of the nonnegative terms $a_n$ are increasing, and are bounded above by a fixed initial contribution plus the sum of the comparison <series>. The <bounded monotone sequence theorem> proves their convergence. Finite initial terms do not affect convergence. Applying this result to $|a_n|$ also proves <absolute convergence> whenever $|a_n|\leq b_n$ eventually with $\sum b_n<\infty$.

For a <power series> $\sum a_nz^n$, its <radius of convergence> $R\in[0,\infty]$ separates <absolute convergence> for $|z|<R$ from divergence for $|z|>R$. One may define $R=\sup\{r\geq0:\sum|a_n|r^n<\infty\}$, allowing $R=\infty$.

To justify convergence inside the radius, choose $|z|<r<R$ with $\sum|a_n|r^n<\infty$. In particular $|a_n|r^n\leq M$ for some $M$, so
$$
|a_nz^n|\leq M(|z|/r)^n.
$$
The <comparison test> with a <geometric series> proves <absolute convergence>. Conversely, convergence at a point of modulus $\rho$ makes $a_nz^n$ bounded there and gives <absolute convergence> at all smaller moduli by the same estimate. Consequently a convergent point of modulus greater than $R$ would contradict the definition of $R$.

For $0<R<\infty$, the real <domain of convergence> is one of $(-R,R)$, $[-R,R)$, $(-R,R]$, or $[-R,R]$. At $R=1$ these are realized respectively by
$$
\sum z^n,\qquad \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{z^n}{n},\qquad \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^nz^n}{n},\qquad \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{z^n}{n^2}.
$$
The endpoint assertions follow from the <harmonic series>, <alternating series test>, and <p-series>. Replacing $z$ by $z/R$ realizes every finite positive radius. The degenerate domain $\{0\}$ occurs for $\sum n!z^n$, while the entire <real line> occurs for $\sum z^n/n!$. Their radii are zero and infinity by the <ratio test>.

A <limit point> $p$ of $E\subseteq\mathbb R$ is a point such that, for every $\varepsilon>0$, the punctured <neighbourhood> $(p-\varepsilon,p+\varepsilon)\setminus\{p\}$ meets $E$. A <closed set> contains all of its <limit points>.

Every nonempty <open interval> contains a <rational number>; applying this to $(p,p+\varepsilon)$ shows that every $p\in\mathbb R$ is a <limit point> of $\mathbb Q$. Thus **the <limit points> of $\mathbb Q$ form $\mathbb R$, and $\mathbb Q$ is not closed**.

If $p\in\mathbb Z$, radius $1/2$ excludes all other <integers>. If $p\notin\mathbb Z$, its distances to the two neighbouring <integers> have a positive minimum, and a smaller radius excludes every <integer>. Thus **$\mathbb Z$ has no <limit points> in $\mathbb R$ and is closed**.

Suppose $E$ is a <closed set> and $p\notin E$. Then $p$ is not a <limit point> of $E$, so some punctured <neighbourhood> of $p$ misses $E$. As $p\notin E$, the entire <neighbourhood> misses $E$. Thus $\mathbb R\setminus E$ is an <open set>.

Conversely, suppose every point $p\notin E$ has a <neighbourhood> disjoint from $E$. No such point can be a <limit point> of $E$, so every <limit point> lies in $E$. This proves the equivalence between containing all <limit points> and having an complement that is an <open set>.

A point is in the <closure> $\overline E$ exactly when every <neighbourhood> meets $E$. This is equivalent to belonging to $E$ or being a <limit point> of $E$.

If $p\notin\overline E$, choose $\varepsilon>0$ with $(p-\varepsilon,p+\varepsilon)\cap E=\varnothing$. Every $q$ with $|q-p|<\varepsilon/2$ has a smaller <neighbourhood> contained in this interval and disjoint from $E$. Thus $(p-\varepsilon/2,p+\varepsilon/2)$ misses $\overline E$, and the complement of $\overline E$ is <open>. Hence $\overline E$ is <closed>.

Every <closed set> $F$ containing $E$ contains all <limit points> of $E$: a <limit point> outside $F$ would have a <neighbourhood> missing $F$, and hence missing $E$. Therefore $\overline E\subseteq F$. Since $\overline E$ is itself one of these <closed sets>,
$$
\boxed{\overline E=\bigcap\{F:E\subseteq F,\ F\text{ closed in }\mathbb R\}.}
$$

The <limit of a function> $f:E\to\mathbb R$ at a <limit point> $p$ of $E$ is $\ell$ when
$$
\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0\ \forall x\in E:\quad
0<|x-p|<\delta\ \Longrightarrow\ |f(x)-\ell|<\varepsilon.
$$
The <function> converges as $x\to p$ if such a finite $\ell$ exists. The puncture excludes any value assigned at $p$.

For $p\in E$, <continuity> at $p$ means
$$
\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0\ \forall x\in E:\quad
|x-p|<\delta\ \Longrightarrow\ |f(x)-f(p)|<\varepsilon.
$$
When $p$ is also a <limit point>, this says that the <limit of a function> is $f(p)$. At an <isolated point> of $E$, every <function> is <continuous> relative to $E$, even though the punctured limit does not have a unique forced value.

The <sequence of functions> $(f_n)$ <converges uniformly> to $f$ on $E$ if
$$
\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall n\geq N\ \forall x\in E:\quad |f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon.
$$
The same $N$ must work for every $x$. Equivalently, the <supremum norm> of $f_n-f$ tends to zero. This is stronger than <pointwise convergence>, where $N$ may depend on $x$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <uniform Cauchy criterion> says that $(f_n:E\to\mathbb R)$ <converges uniformly> on $E$ if and only if
$$
\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall m,n\geq N\ \forall x\in E:\quad |f_n(x)-f_m(x)|<\varepsilon.
$$
For necessity, use the <triangle inequality> through the <uniform limit>. For sufficiency, the condition makes $(f_n(x))_n$ a <Cauchy sequence> for each $x$, so <completeness of the real numbers> defines a <pointwise limit> $f(x)$. Apply the condition with $\varepsilon/2$ and let $m\to\infty$ to obtain $|f_n(x)-f(x)|\leq\varepsilon/2<\varepsilon$ uniformly in $x$.

Write $f_n\to f$ and $g_n\to g$ with <uniform convergence>. The <triangle inequality> gives
$$
\sup_E|(f_n+g_n)-(f+g)|\leq\sup_E|f_n-f|+\sup_E|g_n-g|\longrightarrow0.
$$
Hence $f_n+g_n\to f+g$ with <uniform convergence>.

If every $f_n,g_n$ is a <bounded function>, part <5/a/iii> gives constants $M_f,M_g$ bounding the two families. Their <pointwise limits> satisfy $|f|\leq M_f$ and $|g|\leq M_g$. Consequently
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sup_E|f_ng_n-fg|
&\leq\sup_E\bigl(|f_n|\,|g_n-g|+|g|\,|f_n-f|\bigr)\\
&\leq M_f\sup_E|g_n-g|+M_g\sup_E|f_n-f|\longrightarrow0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus **both sums and products have <uniform convergence> under the stated hypotheses**. The product estimate is the <uniform convergence product rule for bounded functions>.

The <mean value theorem> states that if $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ is <continuous> on $[a,b]$ and <differentiable> on $(a,b)$, where $a<b$, there is $c\in(a,b)$ such that
$$
\boxed{f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)(b-a).}
$$
No <continuity> assumption on the <derivative> is needed.

The <constancy theorem> states that a <differentiable function> on an <interval> whose <derivative> vanishes everywhere is <constant>. At any included endpoints, <continuity> there is understood.

Indeed, take arbitrary $x<y$ in the <interval>. The <mean value theorem> applies on $[x,y]$ and gives $f(y)-f(x)=f'(c)(y-x)=0$. Every pair of values is equal, proving the assertion. The <interval> hypothesis ensures that the whole segment $[x,y]$ lies in the <domain>.

By the <mean value theorem>, $|f(x)-f(y)|\leq A|x-y|$ for all real $x,y$. Thus $f$ is a <contraction mapping> on the <complete metric space> $\mathbb R$. Starting from arbitrary $x_1$, define $x_{n+1}=f(x_n)$. Induction gives $|x_{n+1}-x_n|\leq A^{n-1}|x_2-x_1|$. For $m>n$, the <triangle inequality> and a <geometric series> yield
$$
|x_m-x_n|\leq\frac{A^{n-1}}{1-A}|x_2-x_1|\longrightarrow0.
$$
Hence $(x_n)$ is a <Cauchy sequence> and <completeness of the real numbers> supplies a <limit> $x$. The <Lipschitz continuity> of $f$ implies $f(x)=\lim f(x_n)=\lim x_{n+1}=x$. Thus the limit is a <fixed point>, independently of the initial value. If $y$ were another, $|x-y|\leq A|x-y|$ would force $x=y$. This proves the <contraction mapping theorem> in this setting, with error estimate
$$
\boxed{|x-x_n|\leq\frac{A^{n-1}}{1-A}|x_2-x_1|.}
$$

Assume, as required for the displayed integrals, that the $f_n$ are <Riemann integrable> on $[a,b]$. Their <uniform limit> $f$ is also <Riemann integrable>: if $\sup|f-f_N|<\varepsilon$, the upper and lower <Darboux sums> for a <partition of an interval> differ for $f$ by at most the corresponding difference for $f_N$ plus $2\varepsilon(b-a)$. Choosing $N$ and then a sufficiently fine <partition of an interval> proves the <Riemann integrability criterion> for $f$.

The integral estimate then gives
$$
\left|\int_a^b f_n(x)\,dx-\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\right|
\leq(b-a)\sup_{x\in[a,b]}|f_n(x)-f(x)|\longrightarrow0.
$$
This proves that <uniform convergence preserves integrals on a compact interval>. Some integrability hypothesis is necessary: <uniform convergence> alone cannot make the integral of an arbitrary starting <function> exist.

These are <continuous functions> with <compact support>, and $\sup_{\mathbb R}|f_n|=1/n\to0$, so the <sequence> has <uniform convergence> to zero. However, the <change of variables> $t=x/n$ gives
$$
\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_n(x)\,dx=\int_{-1}^1(1-|t|)\,dt=1.}
$$
The integral of the limit is zero. The supports widen as the heights decrease; a uniform bound on the heights gives no bound on the total area over an infinite <interval>.

The functions $g_n$ are nonnegative. The <change of variables> $u=nx$ and the given <Gaussian integral> imply
$$
M_n:=\int_0^1g_n(x)\,dx=\frac2{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^n e^{-u^2}\,du\longrightarrow1,
\qquad 0\leq M_n\leq1.
$$
For every fixed $\delta>0$,
$$
\int_\delta^1g_n(x)\,dx\leq\frac2{\sqrt\pi}\int_{n\delta}^\infty e^{-u^2}\,du\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus the mass concentrates at the endpoint zero. This is a <one-sided approximate identity>.

Because $f$ is <continuous> on the <compact interval> $[0,1]$, it is a <bounded function>; put $B=\sup_{[0,1]}|f|$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $\delta\in(0,1)$ with $|f(x)-f(0)|<\varepsilon$ for $0\leq x<\delta$. Splitting the integral near and away from zero yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
\left|\int_0^1 f(x)g_n(x)\,dx-f(0)\right|
&\leq\int_0^1|f(x)-f(0)|g_n(x)\,dx+|f(0)|\,|M_n-1|\\
&\leq\varepsilon M_n+2B\int_\delta^1g_n(x)\,dx+|f(0)|\,|M_n-1|.
\end{aligned}
$$
Taking the <limit superior> gives a bound of $\varepsilon$, and $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\int_0^1 f(x)g_n(x)\,dx\longrightarrow f(0).}
$$
Only <continuity> at zero and boundedness on $[0,1]$ were needed for this final estimate; the assumed <continuity> everywhere also guarantees the ordinary integrals exist.
