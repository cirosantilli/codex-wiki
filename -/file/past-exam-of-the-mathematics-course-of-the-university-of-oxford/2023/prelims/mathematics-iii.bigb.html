With the <change of variables> $z=y^2$, multiplication of the <differential equation> by $2y$ gives $z'+2xz=2x$. The <integrating factor> is $e^{x^2}$, so
$$
(ze^{x^2})'=2xe^{x^2},\qquad z=1+Ce^{-x^2}.
$$
The <initial condition> gives $C=3$. Since $y$ starts negative and $z\geq1$, <continuity> selects the negative <square root> throughout:
$$
\boxed{y(x)=-\sqrt{1+3e^{-x^2}}\quad(x\in\mathbb R).}
$$
This never reaches the singular value $y=0$.

<Separation of variables> gives
$$
\frac{y\,dy}{3y^2+1}=\frac{x\,dx}{x^2-1},\qquad
\frac16\log(3y^2+1)=\frac12\log(x^2-1)+C.
$$
Here $x>1$ makes the argument of the second <logarithm> positive. Substituting the <initial condition> yields $3y^2+1=4(x^2-1)^3$. The positive branch is therefore
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\sqrt{\frac{4(x^2-1)^3-1}{3}},\qquad x>\sqrt{1+4^{-1/3}}.}
$$
This is the maximal connected <interval> containing $\sqrt2$ on which this branch solves the original <differential equation>. At its left endpoint $y=0$, the original right-hand side has a zero denominator, so one cannot extend the solution through that point merely by continuing the squared equation.

For $y=x$, the first and second <derivatives> are one and zero, giving $-2x+2x=0$. To find an independent solution, use <reduction of order> and write $y=xv(x)$. Cancellation of the terms proportional to $v$ leaves
$$
x(1-x^2)v''+(2-4x^2)v'=0.
$$
Writing $w=v'$ and using <separation of variables> gives
$$
\frac{w'}w=-\frac2x+\frac{2x}{1-x^2},\qquad
w=\frac C{x^2(1-x^2)}.
$$
Since $1/[x^2(1-x^2)]=x^{-2}+(1-x^2)^{-1}$, integration yields the <general solution>
$$
\boxed{y(x)=C_1x+C_2\left(\frac x2\log\frac{1+x}{1-x}-1\right),\qquad0<x<1.}
$$
The second factor divided by $x$ has nonzero <derivative> $1/[x^2(1-x^2)]$, so the two solutions are <linearly independent>.

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

For a twice <continuously differentiable function>, an interior <local extremum> requires $f_x=f_y=0$. At such a <critical point>, put $D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2$. The <second derivative test> gives a strict <local minimum> if $D>0$ and $f_{xx}>0$, a strict <local maximum> if $D>0$ and $f_{xx}<0$, and a <saddle point> if $D<0$. When $D=0$, the test is inconclusive. More generally the <Hessian matrix> is necessarily <positive semidefinite> at a <local minimum> and <negative semidefinite> at a <local maximum>, but these non-strict signs alone are not sufficient.

For this <polynomial>,
$$
f_x=2x+2y-y^2,\quad f_y=2x+2y-2xy,\quad
H=\begin{pmatrix}2&2-2y\\2-2y&2-2x\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Subtracting the two stationarity equations gives $y(2x-y)=0$. If $y=0$, then $x=0$; if $y=2x$, the remaining equation gives $x=0$ or $x=3/2$. Thus the only <critical points> are $(0,0)$ and $(3/2,3)$.

At $(3/2,3)$, $\det H=-18<0$, so it is a <saddle point>. At $(0,0)$ the determinant is zero, but along $y=-x$ the value is $-x^3$, which takes both signs arbitrarily near zero. Therefore
$$
\boxed{(0,0)\text{ and }(3/2,3)\text{ are both saddle points}.}
$$
In particular a vanishing <Hessian determinant> does not exclude a <saddle point>.

Assume $f,F$ are <continuously differentiable> near the point and $F_z\ne0$. The <implicit function theorem> supplies a local <function> $z(x,y)$ with
$$
z_x=-F_x/F_z,\qquad z_y=-F_y/F_z.
$$
At an interior <extremum> of $g(x,y)=f(x,y,z(x,y))$, the <multivariable chain rule> gives
$$
0=g_x=f_x-f_zF_x/F_z,\qquad0=g_y=f_y-f_zF_y/F_z.
$$
Multiply these identities by $F_y$ and $F_x$, respectively, and subtract. The terms containing $f_z$ cancel, giving
$$
\boxed{f_xF_y-f_yF_x=0.}
$$
No assumption $f_z\ne0$ is needed for this elimination; dividing by $f_z$ would unnecessarily exclude valid cases. If $F_z=0$, this particular local graph argument is unavailable and a different chart or the full <Lagrange multiplier> condition is needed.

On the <constraint> $x^2-z^2=1$, substitute $x^2=1+z^2$ to obtain
$$
f=1+z^2+y^2+z=\left(z+\frac12\right)^2+y^2+\frac34.
$$
Therefore the two <global minima> are
$$
\boxed{(x,y,z)=\left(\pm\frac{\sqrt5}{2},0,-\frac12\right),\qquad f_{\min}=\frac34.}

Every pair $(y,z)$ is allowed on either smooth branch $x=\pm\sqrt{1+z^2}$, so these are also the only <local extrema>. There is **no <local maximum> or <global maximum>**: the displayed positive quadratic in $(y,z)$ has no other stationary point and is unbounded above. At the minima $F_z=-2z=1$, consistent with the hypothesis in part <3/c/i>.

The <joint probability mass function> is
$$
p_{X,Y}(x,y)=\mathbb P(X=x,Y=y),\qquad x,y\in S.
$$
It is nonnegative and its sum over $S\times S$ is one. Summing over one coordinate gives the other <marginal probability mass function>.

For $\mathbb P(X=x)>0$, the <conditional probability mass function> is
$$
\boxed{p_{Y\mid X=x}(y)=\frac{\mathbb P(X=x,Y=y)}{\mathbb P(X=x)}.}
$$
The denominator is the sum of the numerator over all possible $y$, so these <conditional probabilities> sum to one.

A <partition of a sample space> is a finite or countable family $(A_j)$ of pairwise disjoint <events> whose union is the whole <sample space>. If $X$ is <integrable>, the <law of total expectation> states
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X]=\sum_{j:\mathbb P(A_j)>0}\mathbb P(A_j)\,\mathbb E[X\mid A_j].}
$$
This follows by summing $X\mathbf1_{A_j}$ over the <partition of a sample space>; absolute integrability justifies the countable sum. For nonnegative $X$, the identity also holds with infinite expectations. Zero-probability parts contribute zero without requiring a conditional value on them.

Conditional on $R_k=r$, urn 1 has $r$ red and $n-r$ blue balls, while urn 2 has $n-r$ red and $r$ blue balls. The independent selections therefore give
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}

The equilibrium of the <first-order linear recurrence> is $n/2$. Subtracting it gives
$$
r_{k+1}-n/2=(1-2/n)(r_k-n/2).

With independent <Bernoulli trials> of success probability $p$, $N$ counts the failures before the first success. There are $n$ failures followed by one success with probability $(1-p)^np$, giving the stated <geometric distribution> on $\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$. It counts failures, rather than the total number of trials including the success.

Writing $q=1-p$, the <probability generating function> is a <geometric series>:
$$
\boxed{G_N(s)=\mathbb E[s^N]=\sum_{n=0}^\infty p(qs)^n=\frac p{1-qs},\qquad |s|<q^{-1}.}
$$
The displayed restriction ensures <absolute convergence>.

Differentiate the <probability generating function> inside its <radius of convergence>:
$$
G_N'(1)=\frac qp,\qquad G_N''(1)=\frac{2q^2}{p^2}.
$$
Since these are $\mathbb E[N]$ and $\mathbb E[N(N-1)]$, respectively,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[N]=\frac{1-p}{p},\qquad\operatorname{Var}(N)=G_N''(1)+G_N'(1)-G_N'(1)^2=\frac{1-p}{p^2}.}
$$

Take independent <Bernoulli random variables> $X_i$ of success probability $r$, independently of $N$. On the event $N=n$, the <random sum> $\sum_{i=1}^N X_i$ has the <binomial distribution> with parameters $(n,r)$, including the empty sum zero at $n=0$. It therefore has exactly the specified joint distribution with $N$.

Thus one may realize $\boxed{Z=\sum_{i=1}^N X_i}$ on a suitable probability space. This is a distributional construction; it does not require a pre-existing $Z$ to be independent of $N$.

For an independent nonnegative integer-valued count $N$ and identically distributed independent summands, the <random sum probability generating function> is $G_Z(s)=G_N(G_{X_1}(s))$. Conditioning on $N=n$ proves this, since the conditional <probability generating function> is $G_{X_1}(s)^n$.

Here $G_{X_1}(s)=1-r+rs$, so
$$
\boxed{G_Z(s)=\frac p{1-(1-p)(1-r+rs)}=\frac p{p+(1-p)r-(1-p)rs}.}
$$
This identity holds throughout $|s|\leq1$, and its power-series extension has radius $[p+(1-p)r]/[(1-p)r]$.

In each trial the presence indicators of $A$ and $B$ are independent <Bernoulli random variables>, with probabilities $p$ and $r$. Independence across trials makes their two entire indicator sequences independent. Let $N$ be the number of trials before the first $A$; then $N\sim\operatorname{Geom}_0(p)$. Among these $N$ preceding trials, the $B$ indicators are independent with probability $r$.

Thus the count is the <random sum> from part <5/b/i>, and
$$
\boxed{\text{count}\sim\operatorname{Geom}_0\!\left(\frac p{p+(1-p)r}\right).}
$$
An $AB$ outcome on the stopping trial contributes nothing, because the question counts only outcomes strictly before the first $A$.

For the <exponential distribution> with rate $\theta$, the <change of variables> $u=\theta x$ gives
$$
\mathbb E[X]=\frac1\theta\int_0^\infty ue^{-u}\,du=\frac1\theta,\qquad

For an <estimator> $T$ of $\theta$, its <bias> and <mean squared error> are
$$
\boxed{b_\theta(T)=\mathbb E_\theta[T]-\theta,\qquad
\operatorname{MSE}_\theta(T)=\mathbb E_\theta[(T-\theta)^2].}

When the second moment exists, expanding around $\mathbb E_\theta[T]$ gives the <bias-variance decomposition> $\operatorname{MSE}_\theta(T)=\operatorname{Var}_\theta(T)+b_\theta(T)^2$.

For the independent <random sample>, the <likelihood function> on its support is $L(\theta)=\theta^ne^{-\theta Y}$, where $Y=\sum X_i$. Its <log-likelihood> is $\ell(\theta)=n\log\theta-\theta Y$, with
$$

$$
\boxed{\hat\theta=n/Y.}

The exceptional all-zero sample has probability zero; if $Y=0$ were nevertheless supplied, there would be no finite maximizing rate.

$$
\boxed{\left[\hat\theta\left(1-\frac z{\sqrt n}\right),\quad
\hat\theta\left(1+\frac z{\sqrt n}\right)\right]\cap(0,\infty).}
$$
Its coverage is approximately $1-\alpha$ for large $n$. The intersection respects the positive rate parameter; asymptotically the lower endpoint is positive. This is an asymptotic interval, not an exact finite-sample <confidence interval>.

For known $\sigma^2>0$, maximizing the <normal likelihood> over $\mu$ is equivalent to minimizing $\sum_i(X_i-\mu)^2$. The identity
$$
\sum_i(X_i-\mu)^2=\sum_i(X_i-\overline X)^2+n(\mu-\overline X)^2
$$
shows that the unique minimum occurs at $\boxed{\hat\mu=\overline X}$. Equivalently, the <log-likelihood> has second <derivative> $-n/\sigma^2<0$.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

The <sample mean> is exactly $N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)$, so $(\overline X-\mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)$ has the <standard normal distribution>. The given <quantile> gives probability $0.95$ to the interval $[-1.96,1.96]$. Inverting this <pivot> yields the exact $95\%$ <confidence interval>
$$
\boxed{\left[\overline X-1.96\frac\sigma{\sqrt n},\quad\overline X+1.96\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}\right].}
$$

Put $D_j=X_j-\overline X$. The supplied linear-transformation result shows that $(\overline X,D_2,\ldots,D_n)$ is jointly <multivariate normal>. Its marginal <distributions> are
$$
\boxed{\overline X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n),\qquad D_j\sim N(0,\sigma^2(1-1/n))\quad(2\leq j\leq n).}
$$
Indeed, $\operatorname{Cov}(X_j,\overline X)=\sigma^2/n$, so
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\overline X,D_j)=\sigma^2/n-\sigma^2/n=0.
$$
For a jointly <Gaussian vector>, zero cross-covariance between blocks implies their <independence>. Thus $\overline X$ is independent of the entire vector $(D_2,\ldots,D_n)$, not merely of each coordinate separately. The residual coordinates themselves need not be independent: for $j\ne k$, their <covariance> is $-\sigma^2/n$.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Each residual has mean zero and <variance> $\sigma^2(1-1/n)$. By <linearity of expectation>, without needing independence of residuals,
$$
\mathbb E[S^2]=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb E[D_i^2]
=\frac n{n-1}\sigma^2(1-1/n)=\boxed{\sigma^2}.
$$
Hence $S^2$ is an <unbiased estimator> of $\sigma^2$.

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

Since $\sum_{i=1}^nD_i=0$, we have $D_1=-\sum_{i=2}^nD_i$. Squaring and adding the other residual squares gives
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^nD_i^2=\left(\sum_{i=2}^nD_i\right)^2+\sum_{i=2}^nD_i^2.}
$$
Therefore $S^2$ is a measurable <function> of $(D_2,\ldots,D_n)$. By the block <independence> proved in part <8/b/i>, it is independent of $\overline X$. This is the <independence of the normal sample mean and sample variance>; pairwise independence alone would not justify passing to an arbitrary function of several residuals.

Minimize the <residual sum of squares>
$$
Q(b_0,b_1,b_2)=\sum_i(Y_i-b_0-b_1x_{i1}-b_2x_{i2})^2.
$$
Setting its three <partial derivatives> to zero gives the <normal equations>
$$
\boxed{\sum_i(Y_i-\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1x_{i1}-\hat\beta_2x_{i2})=0,\quad
\sum_i x_{ij}(Y_i-\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1x_{i1}-\hat\beta_2x_{i2})=0\quad(j=1,2).}
$$
These equations characterize the <linear least squares> minimizers, since $Q$ is a convex quadratic. A unique coefficient vector additionally requires full <column rank> of the <design matrix>.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

The centered regressors make the first <normal equation> read $n\hat\beta_0=\sum_iY_i$. Define
$$
A=\sum_i x_{i1}^2,\quad B=\sum_i x_{i1}x_{i2},\quad C=\sum_i x_{i2}^2,\quad
u=\sum_i x_{i1}Y_i,\quad v=\sum_i x_{i2}Y_i.
$$
The remaining equations are $A\hat\beta_1+B\hat\beta_2=u$ and $B\hat\beta_1+C\hat\beta_2=v$. Provided $D=AC-B^2>0$, <matrix inversion> gives
$$
\boxed{\hat\beta_0=\overline Y,\qquad
\hat\beta_1=\frac{Cu-Bv}{D},\qquad \hat\beta_2=\frac{Av-Bu}{D}.}
$$
By the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>, $D\geq0$. If $D=0$, the two regressor columns are <linearly dependent>, and their separate slopes are not uniquely identifiable. One still has well-defined <linear least squares> fitted values, but cannot use the displayed divisions by $D$.

= iii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

If the two regressors are strongly associated, the data may provide little support for varying one while holding the other fixed. <Multicollinearity> makes the separate slope estimates unstable, and exact <linear dependence> makes them unidentifiable. A conditional slope may also be an extrapolation beyond combinations actually observed.

Furthermore, a slope in a <linear regression model> is not automatically a <causal effect>. A causal interpretation needs appropriate design or assumptions excluding <confounding>; independence of the errors across observations and zero mean alone do not establish those assumptions. The coefficients describe the specified linear mean relation, but changing a regressor by intervention can have a different interpretation.

<Principal component analysis> finds orthogonal directions of greatest sample variation, ordered by the variance explained. It can summarize correlated variables, reveal lower-dimensional structure, and provide a reduced representation by retaining only the first few <principal components>. Centering is standard; scaling may also be appropriate when the variables have incomparable units.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Let $X_c$ be the column-centered <data matrix>. Its sample <covariance matrix> is $S=X_c^TX_c/(n-1)$. The <loadings matrix> $V=(v_1,\ldots,v_p)$ has orthonormal <eigenvectors> of $S$ as columns, ordered by nonincreasing <eigenvalues>:
$$
Sv_j=\lambda_jv_j,\qquad V^TV=I.
$$
Each <principal component loading> specifies the coefficients of a principal direction in the original variables. Signs are arbitrary, and equal <eigenvalues> permit orthogonal changes of basis within their <eigenspaces>.

= iii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

The <principal component scores> are the projections of centered observations onto the loading directions:
$$
\boxed{Z=X_cV,\qquad Z_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^p(X_{ik}-\overline X_k)V_{kj}.}
$$
If the symbol $X$ already denotes centered data, this simplifies to $Z=XV$. The <principal component loadings> are directions; the <principal component scores> are coordinates of individual observations in those directions.

{parent=9}
{scope}

= i
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}
{c}

Here both regressor columns are already centered, so $Z=XV$ and $X=ZV^T$. Writing $\beta=(\beta_1,\beta_2)^T$, the linear predictor is $X\beta=ZV^T\beta$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\widetilde\beta=V^T\beta,\qquad
\widetilde\beta_1=V_{11}\beta_1+V_{21}\beta_2,\quad
\widetilde\beta_2=V_{12}\beta_1+V_{22}\beta_2.}
$$
The intercept stays $\beta_0$, because centering made the transformation a rotation of the regressor columns without an added translation.

= ii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

The new score columns are orthogonal, since $Z^TZ=V^TX^TXV$ is diagonal. This decouples the slope <normal equations>; with positive eigenvalues and the stated error <covariance>, the two estimated score coefficients are uncorrelated. It also makes variance explained and possible dimension reduction explicit.

The trade-off is interpretation: each <principal component> is a mixture of the original variables, so its regression coefficient is less directly connected to changing one original input. Keeping both components is an invertible reparameterization and gives **exactly the same fitted values and residual sum of squares**. It does not repair a zero <eigenvalue> or remove instability associated with a very small one. Dropping a low-variance component may improve numerical stability and reduce estimator variance, but changes the model and can discard predictive information, since <principal component analysis> uses predictor variance rather than the response. This is the distinction between a full rotation and <principal component regression> with truncation.
