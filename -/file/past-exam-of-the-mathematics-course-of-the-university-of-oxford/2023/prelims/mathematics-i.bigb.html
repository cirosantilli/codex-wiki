= Solution
{parent=i}

The three <elementary row operations> on an $m\times n$ <real matrix> are:

* Interchange two distinct rows: $R_i\leftrightarrow R_j$.
* Multiply one row by a nonzero <real number>: $R_i\leftarrow cR_i$, with $c\ne0$.
* Add a <scalar multiple> of one row to a different row: $R_i\leftarrow R_i+cR_j$, with $i\ne j$.

Each operation changes whole rows, so it acts on every column by the same rule. Each can be undone by an <elementary row operation>.

= ii
{parent=a}
{c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

For an $m\times n$ <matrix>, the corresponding <elementary matrices> are $m\times m$, because the operation is implemented by left <matrix multiplication>. Apply the desired <elementary row operation> to the <identity matrix> $I_m$. Writing $E_{ij}$ for the <matrix unit> with its only nonzero entry, $1$, in position $(i,j)$ gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{ij}&=I_m-E_{ii}-E_{jj}+E_{ij}+E_{ji},
& P_{ij}^{-1}&=P_{ij},\\
D_i(c)&=I_m+(c-1)E_{ii},
& D_i(c)^{-1}&=D_i(c^{-1})\quad(c\ne0),\\
L_{ij}(c)&=I_m+cE_{ij},
& L_{ij}(c)^{-1}&=L_{ij}(-c)\quad(i\ne j).
\end{aligned}
$$
Indeed, these <matrix products> interchange rows $i,j$, scale row $i$, and add $c$ times row $j$ to row $i$, respectively. The displayed inverses undo precisely those operations. Algebraically, $E_{ij}^2=0$ when $i\ne j$, so $(I_m+cE_{ij})(I_m-cE_{ij})=I_m$; the other two inverse identities follow immediately from their row actions. Thus every <elementary matrix> is an <invertible matrix>.

Perform the <elementary row operations> $R_2\leftarrow R_2+2R_1$ and $R_3\leftarrow R_3-R_1$, then $R_2\leftarrow R_2/2$, $R_1\leftarrow R_1-R_2$, and finally $R_1\leftarrow R_1+R_3$, $R_2\leftarrow R_2-3R_3$:
$$
\begin{pmatrix}1&1&2\\-2&0&2\\1&1&3\end{pmatrix}
\longrightarrow
\begin{pmatrix}1&1&2\\0&2&6\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}
\longrightarrow
\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1\\0&1&3\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}
\longrightarrow I_3.
$$
Consequently the <reduced row echelon form> is $I_3$. Applying the same operations to the augmented column $b=(b_1,b_2,b_3)^T$ gives
$$
\boxed{v=\begin{pmatrix}
b_3-b_1-\tfrac12b_2\\
4b_1+\tfrac12b_2-3b_3\\
b_3-b_1
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Thus **every** $b\in\mathbb R^3$ admits exactly one solution. This follows because the <elementary row operations> preserve the solution set of the augmented <linear system>, and the reduced coefficient <matrix> is the <identity matrix>.

= Solution

If $A$ is a <strictly upper triangular matrix>, then $A_{ij}=0$ unless $j-i\ge1$. We prove by <mathematical induction> that
$$
(A^k)_{ij}=0\qquad\text{whenever }j-i<k.
$$
The assertion for $k=1$ is the definition. In the <matrix multiplication> formula
$$
(A^{k+1})_{ij}=\sum_{\ell=1}^n(A^k)_{i\ell}A_{\ell j},
$$
a nonzero summand would require $\ell-i\ge k$ and $j-\ell\ge1$, hence $j-i\ge k+1$. This proves the induction step. Since $j-i\le n-1$ for all entries, $\boxed{A^n=0}$. In particular, every <strictly upper triangular matrix> is a <nilpotent matrix>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Write $A=I_3+N$, where
$$
N=\begin{pmatrix}0&a&b\\0&0&c\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
N^2=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&ac\\0&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad N^3=0.
$$
The <binomial theorem for commuting matrices> applies because $I_3N=NI_3$. All terms of degree at least three vanish, so for every positive <integer> $n$,
$$
\boxed{A^n=I_3+nN+\binom n2N^2
=\begin{pmatrix}
1&na&nb+\dfrac{n(n-1)}2ac\\
0&1&nc\\
0&0&1
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The same expression directly gives $A$ when $n=1$, so no small exponent is excluded.

A <vector space> over a <field> $\mathbb F$ is a set $V$ with <vector addition> $V\times V\to V$ and <scalar multiplication> $\mathbb F\times V\to V$ satisfying the following <vector space axioms>. Under <vector addition>, $V$ is an <abelian group>: addition is associative and commutative, there is a <zero vector> $0_V$, and each $v$ has an additive inverse $-v$. For all $a,b\in\mathbb F$ and $u,v\in V$,
$$
a(u+v)=au+av,\qquad (a+b)v=av+bv,\qquad
(ab)v=a(bv),\qquad 1_{\mathbb F}v=v.
$$
The operations are defined for every indicated pair and take their values in $V$.

= ii
{parent=a}
{c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

A map $T:V\to W$ between <vector spaces> over the same <field> $\mathbb F$ is a <linear map> precisely when it preserves <vector addition> and <scalar multiplication>:
$$
T(u+v)=T(u)+T(v),\qquad T(av)=aT(v)
\quad(u,v\in V,\ a\in\mathbb F).
$$
Equivalently, $T(au+bv)=aT(u)+bT(v)$ for all $u,v\in V$ and $a,b\in\mathbb F$.

{parent=a}
{c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

Every $v\in V$ has a unique <basis> expansion $v=\sum_{i=1}^n a_iv_i$. Define the <linear functional>
$$
\boxed{T\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_iv_i\right)=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\mu_i.}
$$
Uniqueness of the <coordinates> makes this definition well defined. The <coordinates> of a sum or a <scalar multiple> are the corresponding sums or <scalar multiples> of the <coordinates>, so this formula defines a <linear map>. It has $T(v_i)=\mu_i$. Conversely, any <linear map> with those values must satisfy the same formula by <linearity>, proving uniqueness.

If the <finite field> $\mathbb F$ has $q$ elements, each of the $n$ values $\mu_i$ can be chosen independently in $q$ ways. The number of <linear maps> $V\to\mathbb F$ is therefore $\boxed{q^n}$, including the zero <linear map>.

Use <basis extension> to choose a <basis> $(v,v_2,\ldots,v_n)$ of the <vector space> $V$ over the <finite field> $\mathbb F_p=\mathbb Z_p$. The condition $T(v)=0$ fixes the first value of a <linear functional>; its remaining $n-1$ values are arbitrary. There are $p^{n-1}$ such <linear maps>, exactly one of which is zero. Hence the required number is
$$
\boxed{p^{n-1}-1.}
$$
For $n=1$ this is zero, as it should be: vanishing at a <basis vector> then forces the entire <linear functional> to vanish.

{parent=b}
{c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Put $L=\operatorname{span}\{v\}$. Every two-dimensional <vector subspace> containing $v$ is $W=\operatorname{span}\{v,w\}$ for some $w\notin L$. There are $p^n-p$ choices for $w$ in $V$. Within any one such $W$, exactly $p^2-p$ choices of $w\in W\setminus L$ yield that same $W$: each forms a <linearly independent> pair with $v$, and that pair is a <basis> of $W$.

These sets of choices partition $V\setminus L$, because $w$ determines $\operatorname{span}\{v,w\}$ uniquely. Thus the number is
$$
\boxed{\frac{p^n-p}{p^2-p}=\frac{p^{n-1}-1}{p-1}.}
$$
Equivalently these <vector subspaces> correspond to the one-dimensional <vector subspaces> of the <quotient vector space> $V/L$. For $n=1$ there are none and the formula gives zero.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

If $T'=\lambda T$ with $\lambda\ne0$, then $T'(x)=0$ if and only if $T(x)=0$, so their <kernels> agree. Conversely, suppose both nonzero <linear functionals> have <kernel> $K$. Choose $u$ with $T(u)=1$, by scaling a <vector> on which $T$ is nonzero. For each $x\in V$,
$$
x=T(x)u+\bigl(x-T(x)u\bigr),\qquad x-T(x)u\in K.
$$
Therefore $T'(x)=T(x)T'(u)$. Moreover $T'(u)\ne0$, since $u\notin K$. Thus $\boxed{T'=T'(u)T}$, with a nonzero <scalar>.

Every nonzero <linear functional> $V\to\mathbb F_p$ is surjective, so its <kernel> has <dimension> $n-1$ by the <rank-nullity theorem>. Conversely, every <hyperplane> $K$ is such a <kernel>: extend a <basis> of $K$ by one <vector> $u$, and assign value zero on that <basis> and value one on $u$. Hence the <hyperplanes> containing $v$ are exactly the <kernels> of the $p^{n-1}-1$ nonzero <linear functionals> counted above. Each <kernel> occurs for exactly $p-1$ of them, its distinct nonzero <scalar multiples>. The required number is
$$
\boxed{\frac{p^{n-1}-1}{p-1}.}
$$

For the <linear map> $T_A:x\mapsto Ax$ on the <finite-dimensional vector space> $\mathbb R^n$, the <rank-nullity theorem> implies that injectivity and surjectivity are equivalent. Consequently $T_A$ is invertible if and only if $\ker T_A=\{0\}$.

Using the permitted equivalence between invertibility and a nonzero <determinant>, we obtain
$$
\det A=0
\iff T_A\text{ is not invertible}
\iff\ker T_A\ne\{0\}
\iff \exists v\ne0:\ Av=0.
$$

{parent=a}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

We use the <monic polynomial> convention for the <characteristic polynomial> of an $n\times n$ <matrix>:
$$
\boxed{\chi_A(t)=\det(tI_n-A).}
$$
It has degree $n$, since the only contribution to the coefficient of $t^n$ in the <determinant> is the product of the $n$ diagonal terms $t$. The alternative convention $\det(A-tI_n)=(-1)^n\chi_A(t)$ has the same <polynomial roots>.

{parent=a}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

A <scalar> $\lambda$ is an <eigenvalue> of $A$ if and only if there is a nonzero <vector> $v$ with $Av=\lambda v$. By the preceding <kernel> criterion,
$$
Av=\lambda v\text{ for some }v\ne0
\iff (\lambda I_n-A)v=0\text{ for some }v\ne0
\iff\det(\lambda I_n-A)=0.
$$
The last condition is $\chi_A(\lambda)=0$, exactly the definition of a <polynomial root>. For a <real matrix>, this proves the assertion for real <eigenvalues>; the same proof over the <complex numbers> gives the usual complex version.

= Solution

Write $B=[b_1,\ldots,b_n]$. Since $AB=[Ab_1,\ldots,Ab_n]$ and $b\mapsto Ab$ is a <linear map>, the <multilinearity> of the <determinant> gives, for any column position $j$,
$$
D(b_1,\ldots,\alpha u+\beta w,\ldots,b_n)
=\alpha D(b_1,\ldots,u,\ldots,b_n)
+\beta D(b_1,\ldots,w,\ldots,b_n).
$$
If $b_i=b_j$ for $i\ne j$, the corresponding columns $Ab_i,Ab_j$ also agree, so $D(B)=0$. Thus $D$ is an <alternating multilinear map> of the columns of $B$, even when $A$ is not invertible.

= Solution
{parent=v}
{c}

We have $D(I_n)=\det A$. Define a function of the columns of $B$ by
$$
F(B)=D(B)+(1-\det A)\det B.
$$
It is an <alternating multilinear map>, because both summands are, and $F(I_n)=\det A+1-\det A=1$. Hence $F$ is a normalized <determinantal function>. By the asserted uniqueness of the <determinant>, $F(B)=\det B$ for every $B$. Rearranging yields
$$
\boxed{\det(AB)=\det A\det B.}
$$
This argument also covers $\det A=0$ without dividing by it.

Take the nonzero <vector> $\mathbf1=(1,\ldots,1)^T$. Its $i$th image entry under <matrix multiplication> is the sum of row $i$:
$$
(A\mathbf1)_i=\sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij}=\lambda.
$$
Thus $A\mathbf1=\lambda\mathbf1$, exhibiting $\mathbf1$ as an <eigenvector> with <eigenvalue> $\boxed{\lambda}$.

{parent=b}
{c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Yes. The sums of the columns of $A$ are the sums of the rows of its <transpose> $A^T$. By the previous argument, $A^T\mathbf1=\lambda\mathbf1$, so $\det(\lambda I_n-A^T)=0$. The <determinant> is unchanged by <transpose>, giving
$$
\det(\lambda I_n-A)
=\det\bigl((\lambda I_n-A)^T\bigr)
=\det(\lambda I_n-A^T)=0.
$$
Therefore $\boxed{\lambda\text{ is an eigenvalue of }A}$. The <vector> $\mathbf1$ need not itself be an <eigenvector> of $A$ in the column-sum case.

{parent=b}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

If $A$ is an <invertible matrix>, then
$$
BA=A^{-1}(AB)A.
$$
If instead $B$ is an <invertible matrix>, then
$$
AB=B^{-1}(BA)B.
$$
Thus in either case $AB$ and $BA$ are <similar matrices>. Explicitly, whenever $Y=P^{-1}XP$ and $Xu=\lambda u$ with $u\ne0$, one has $Y(P^{-1}u)=\lambda P^{-1}u$ and $P^{-1}u\ne0$. Applying the same argument with $P^{-1}$ in place of $P$ proves equality of the sets of <eigenvalues>, including zero.

{parent=b}
{c}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

The same <matrix similarity> preserves being a <diagonalisable matrix>. If $S^{-1}(AB)S=D$ is a <diagonal matrix>, then
$$
(A^{-1}S)^{-1}(BA)(A^{-1}S)
=S^{-1}A(BA)A^{-1}S
=S^{-1}(AB)S=D.
$$
Therefore $BA$ is a <diagonalisable matrix> whenever $AB$ is, and the converse follows by interchanging $A,B$. Thus they are either both <diagonalisable matrices> or neither is. In fact the previous part shows that one invertible factor suffices.

The real <spectral theorem> states that a <symmetric matrix> has an <orthonormal basis> of real <eigenvectors>, and hence is a <diagonalisable matrix>. Here is a proof in this setting.

For $n\ge1$, the <quadratic form> $q(x)=x^TAx$ attains a maximum on the <unit sphere>, by <compactness> and the <extreme value theorem>. Choose a unit maximizer $u$ and put $\lambda=u^TAu$. For any $w\perp u$, the <Rayleigh quotient> along $u+tw$ has a maximum at $t=0$, so
$$
0=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\frac{(u+tw)^TA(u+tw)}{\|u+tw\|^2}\right|_{t=0}
=2w^TAu.
$$
The last equality uses $A=A^T$ and $u^Tw=0$. Thus $Au$ is orthogonal to every $w\perp u$, so $Au=\lambda u$.

The <orthogonal complement> $u^\perp$ is an <invariant subspace>, because for $x\perp u$,
$$
u^TAx=(Au)^Tx=\lambda u^Tx=0.
$$
The <restriction> of $A$ to $u^\perp$ is again symmetric with respect to the induced <inner product>. By <mathematical induction> on <dimension>, it has an <orthonormal basis> of <eigenvectors>; adjoining $u$ gives one for $\mathbb R^n$. If $Q$ has those <basis vectors> as columns, then $Q^TAQ$ is a real <diagonal matrix> and $Q^{-1}=Q^T$.

Let $A$ be a <skew-symmetric matrix>. For every real <vector> $x$, the real <scalar> $x^TAx$ equals its own <transpose>, but
$$
x^TAx=(x^TAx)^T=x^TA^Tx=-x^TAx.
$$
Hence $x^TAx=0$. If $Av=\lambda v$ is a real <eigenvector> equation with $v\ne0$, it follows that
$$
0=v^TAv=\lambda v^Tv,
$$
so $\lambda=0$. If $A$ is a <diagonalisable matrix> over $\mathbb R$, it has a real <basis> of <eigenvectors>, all with <eigenvalue> zero. Thus $A$ vanishes on a <basis> and $\boxed{A=0_n}$.

The <field> matters: a nonzero real <skew-symmetric matrix> can be a <diagonalisable matrix> over the <complex numbers>, for example $\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ with <eigenvalues> $i,-i$.

The <matrix units> $E_{ij}$, $1\le i,j\le n$, form a <basis> of $M_n(\mathbb R)$: each <matrix> has the unique expression $A=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}E_{ij}$. There are $n^2$ of them, so
$$
\boxed{\dim M_n(\mathbb R)=n^2.}
$$

= ii
{parent=b}
{c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Take the <vector subspace> of <skew-symmetric matrices>,
$$
K=\{A\in M_n(\mathbb R):A^T=-A\}.
$$
It is closed under <vector addition> and <scalar multiplication>, and a <basis> is
$$
\{E_{ij}-E_{ji}:1\le i<j\le n\}.
$$
Indeed, its diagonal entries vanish, and its entries below the diagonal are forced by those above it. Hence $\boxed{\dim K=\binom n2}$. Part (a)(ii) proves that the only <diagonalisable matrix> in $K$ is $0_n$.

{parent=b}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Let $S$ be the <vector subspace> of <symmetric matrices>. A <basis> consists of $E_{ii}$ for $1\le i\le n$ and $E_{ij}+E_{ji}$ for $i<j$, so
$$
\dim S=n+\binom n2=\frac{n(n+1)}2.
$$
The <dimension formula for a sum of subspaces> now gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dim(V\cap S)
&=\dim V+\dim S-\dim(V+S)\\
&\ge\dim V+\frac{n(n+1)}2-n^2\\
&=\dim V-\binom n2>0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Choose $0\ne A\in V\cap S$. It belongs to $V$ and is a <symmetric matrix>, so part (a)(i) makes it a <diagonalisable matrix>. The preceding example shows that the strict threshold is sharp.

Write $r=(r_1,\ldots,r_n)^T$. The <linear map> represented by $A$ has the form
$$
Ax=(r^Tx)e_i.
$$
The <image of a linear map> here is contained in the one-dimensional <linear span> of $e_i$, so its <matrix rank> is at most one. The <rank-nullity theorem> yields
$$
\boxed{\dim\ker A=n-\operatorname{rank}A\ge n-1.}
$$
Thus the solution space of $Ax=0$, namely the zero <eigenspace> when zero is an <eigenvalue>, contains at least $n-1$ <linearly independent vectors>. If $r=0$ its <dimension> is $n$; otherwise it is $n-1$. When $n=1$ the asserted lower bound is zero, even if zero is not an <eigenvalue>.

Since $Ae_i=r_ie_i$, the <vector> $e_i$ is an <eigenvector> with nonzero <eigenvalue> $r_i$. The assumption $r_i\ne0$ also makes $r\ne0$, so the <kernel> has <dimension> $n-1$. Choose its <basis> $u_1,\ldots,u_{n-1}$.

The <vector> $e_i$ is outside this <kernel>. Hence $(u_1,\ldots,u_{n-1},e_i)$ is a <basis> of $\mathbb R^n$ consisting of <eigenvectors>, with corresponding <eigenvalues> $0,\ldots,0,r_i$. In that <basis>, $A$ is the <diagonal matrix>
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{diag}(0,\ldots,0,r_i).}
$$
This proves that $A$ is a <diagonalisable matrix>, including the case $n=1$.

Yes. Consider the following $n^2$ <matrices>:
$$
\boxed{\mathcal B=\{E_{ii}:1\le i\le n\}
\ \cup\ \{E_{ii}+E_{ij}:1\le i,j\le n,\ i\ne j\}.}

Each has only row $i$ nonzero and has $i$th diagonal entry $1$, so part (c)(ii) proves that it is a <diagonalisable matrix>. The collection is a <spanning set>, because it contains every $E_{ii}$ and
$$

Thus it spans the <matrix unit> <basis>. A <spanning set> of $n^2$ <vectors> in an $n^2$-dimensional <vector space> is a <basis>. Alternatively, in a vanishing <linear combination> of $\mathcal B$, each off-diagonal entry first forces its own coefficient to be zero, and then each diagonal entry forces the remaining coefficient to be zero.

In the <finite field> $\mathbb Z_3=\{0,1,2\}$, perform ordinary addition and multiplication and then take the remainder modulo $3$. The tables are
$$
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
+&0&1&2\\\hline
0&0&1&2\\
1&1&2&0\\
2&2&0&1
\end{array}
\qquad
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
\cdot&0&1&2\\\hline
0&0&0&0\\
1&0&1&2\\
2&0&2&1
\end{array}.
$$
The nonzero elements have multiplicative inverses $1^{-1}=1$ and $2^{-1}=2$.

Parametrize the <matrices> by
$$
h(a,b,c)=\begin{pmatrix}1&a&b\\0&1&c\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad a,b,c\in\mathbb Z_3.
$$
Direct <matrix multiplication> gives

Conjugating a <cycle> simply relabels its entries. With <permutations> composed from right to left,
$$
\rho(a_1\ a_2\ \cdots\ a_\ell)\rho^{-1}
=(\rho(a_1)\ \rho(a_2)\ \cdots\ \rho(a_\ell)).
$$
This follows by applying each side to $\rho(a_j)$ and to points outside the displayed <cycle>. Consequently <conjugation> preserves the lengths in the <disjoint cycle decomposition>, including fixed points, and therefore preserves <cycle type>.

Conversely, suppose two <permutations> have the same <cycle type>. Pair their <cycles> of equal length, including their one-cycles. Define a <bijection> $\rho$ by mapping the entries of each <cycle> of the first <permutation>, in their cyclic order, to the entries of the paired <cycle> of the second. Because the <cycles> partition $\{1,\ldots,n\}$, these assignments combine into a <permutation>. The displayed identity then conjugates the first <permutation> to the second. Thus they are <conjugate group elements> exactly when their <cycle types> agree.

Assume first $n\ge2$. All <transpositions> form a single <conjugacy class> $T_1$. A <group automorphism> preserves <order of a group element>, so the image of a <transposition> is a nonidentity <involution>. By the order formula, its <disjoint cycle decomposition> contains only one-cycles and two-cycles, with at least one two-cycle. Thus it lies in some $T_k$, where $1\le k\le\lfloor n/2\rfloor$.

Moreover an <automorphism> maps the entire <conjugacy class> onto the <conjugacy class> of the image: for a fixed <transposition> $t$,
$$
\phi(gtg^{-1})=\phi(g)\phi(t)\phi(g)^{-1},
$$
and $\phi(g)$ runs over all of $S_n$ by surjectivity. Part (a)(i) identifies that latter <conjugacy class> as $T_k$. Hence $\boxed{\phi(T_1)=T_k}$, with equality rather than just inclusion. If $n=1$, $T_1$ is empty and the equality holds trivially with $k=1$.

{parent=b}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

For $n\ge2$ with $n\ne6$, bijectivity of $\phi$ and the previous part imply
$$
\frac{n!}{2^k k!(n-2k)!}=|T_k|=|T_1|=\frac{n(n-1)}2.
$$
The supplied numerical fact forces $k=1$. Thus $\phi$ permutes the <transpositions>.

Suppose $n\ge3$, and write $s_r=\phi((1r))$ for $r=2,\ldots,n$. These are distinct <transpositions>. For $r\ne t$, the product $(1r)(1t)$ has <order of a group element> three, so $s_rs_t$ also has order three. Two distinct <transpositions> have this property precisely when their two-element sets of moved points meet in one point: disjoint ones have a product of order two, whereas ones sharing one point have a three-cycle as product. The two-element <support of a permutation> associated with each $s_r$ therefore meets that of every other $s_t$.

We now show that they have a common point. Choose two; the <support of a permutation> is $\{a,b\}$ for one and $\{a,c\}$ for the other, where $a,b,c$ are distinct. Any further two-element set meeting both either contains $a$ or is $\{b,c\}$. If $\{b,c\}$ occurs, any two-element set meeting all three must itself be one of $\{a,b\},\{a,c\},\{b,c\}$: a set containing $a$ must also contain $b$ or $c$, and a set omitting $a$ must contain both $b,c$. Hence a pairwise-intersecting family with no common point consists of exactly those three edges of a triangle.

For $n\ge5$, there are $n-1\ge4$ distinct $s_r$, ruling out that triangle. For $n=3$, the two sets already have a common point. For $n=4$, the triangle alternative would make all three $s_r$ fix the fourth point. They would then generate a proper <subgroup> of $S_4$, contrary to surjectivity of $\phi$ and part (a)(iv), which imply that the $s_r$ generate $S_4$. Thus in every case $n\ge3$ there is a common point $a$, and we may write
$$
s_r=(a\ b_r)\qquad(2\le r\le n).
$$
The $b_r$ are distinct and different from $a$, so they exhaust the remaining $n-1$ points. Define a <permutation> $\sigma$ by
$$
\sigma(a)=1,\qquad \sigma(b_r)=r.
$$
Then
$$
\sigma^{-1}(1r)\sigma
=(\sigma^{-1}(1)\ \sigma^{-1}(r))
=(a\ b_r)=\phi((1r)).
$$
The two <group homomorphisms> $\tau\mapsto\phi(\tau)$ and $\tau\mapsto\sigma^{-1}\tau\sigma$ agree on a <generating set of a group>, so agree on every product of its elements, hence throughout $S_n$:
$$
\boxed{\phi(\tau)=\sigma^{-1}\tau\sigma\qquad(\tau\in S_n).}
$$
Finally, $S_1$ is trivial and $S_2$ has a unique nonidentity element, so every <automorphism> of either is the identity and we can take $\sigma=e$. This completes all degrees $n\ne6$ and proves that every such <automorphism> is an <inner automorphism>.
