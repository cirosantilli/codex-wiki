Let $D$ be a bounded planar region with a piecewise smooth, simple closed boundary $C$, oriented counterclockwise, and let $P,Q$ have continuous first <partial derivatives> on an open neighbourhood of its closure. <Green's theorem> states that
$$
\boxed{\oint_C(P\,dx+Q\,dy)=\iint_D\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)dx\,dy.}
$$
For a region with holes, orient every boundary component with $D$ on its left: the outer component is counterclockwise and the inner components are clockwise.

Choose $P=-y/2$ and $Q=x/2$. Their indicated <partial derivatives> differ by one, so the <area> is
$$
\boxed{|D|=\iint_D1\,dx\,dy=\frac12\oint_C(x\,dy-y\,dx).}
$$
This is <signed area as a boundary integral>; reversing the direction of $C$ changes the sign of the integral.

In <polar coordinates>, put $x=r\cos\theta$ and $y=r\sin\theta$. Differentiating along the curve gives
$$
x\,dy-y\,dx=r^2\,d\theta.
$$
The <cardioid> has $r\geq0$ and is traversed once counterclockwise as $\theta$ increases from $0$ to $2\pi$. Its cusp at $\theta=\pi$ causes no difficulty: split the <line integral> there, or exclude a small arc and take its limit. Thus <signed area as a boundary integral> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
|D|&=\frac12\int_0^{2\pi}(1+\cos\theta)^2\,d\theta\\
&=\frac12\int_0^{2\pi}\left(\frac32+2\cos\theta+\frac12\cos2\theta\right)d\theta
=\boxed{\frac{3\pi}{2}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For an oriented piecewise smooth <parametric curve> $\mathbf r:[a,b]\to\mathbb R^3$ and a continuous <vector field> $\mathbf F$ along it, the vector-field <line integral> is
$$
\boxed{\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\int_a^b\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t)\,dt.}
$$
For a piecewise smooth curve, sum these integrals over its smooth pieces. The <dot product> selects the component of the <vector field> in the direction of motion.

If $\mathbf F=\nabla\phi$ for a <smooth function> $\phi$, the <multivariable chain rule> gives
$$
\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\phi(\mathbf r(t)).
$$
The <fundamental theorem of calculus>, with cancellation at intermediate endpoints, therefore proves the <fundamental theorem for line integrals>:
$$
\int_C\nabla\phi\cdot d\mathbf r=\phi(\mathbf r(b))-\phi(\mathbf r(a)).
$$
On a closed curve $\mathbf r(a)=\mathbf r(b)$, so $\boxed{\oint_C\nabla\phi\cdot d\mathbf r=0}$.

The displayed <vector field> is defined on the punctured plane $U=\mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}$; it is undefined at the origin. Take the counterclockwise unit <circle> $\mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t)$, $0\leq t\leq2\pi$. Along it,
$$
\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))=(-\sin t,\cos t)=\mathbf r'(t),\qquad
\boxed{\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\int_0^{2\pi}1\,dt=2\pi.}
$$
By the <fundamental theorem for line integrals>, it has no single-valued <potential of a conservative vector field> on $U$. In particular it cannot be the restriction of the <gradient> of a <smooth function> on all of $\mathbb R^2$.

For $(x,y)\ne(0,0)$, direct differentiation gives
$$
\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}
=\frac{x^2+y^2-2x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}
=\frac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}
=\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}.
$$
There is no contradiction with <Green's theorem>: $\mathbf F$ is not defined, let alone continuously differentiable, throughout the disk enclosed by $C$. On an annulus the theorem does apply; the counterclockwise outer integral is $2\pi$, and the clockwise inner integral is $-2\pi$, so their sum is zero. The hole prevents this <curl-free vector field> from being a <conservative vector field> on its entire domain.

For a continuous <vector field> along an oriented piecewise continuously differentiable <parametric curve> $\gamma:[a,b]\to\mathbb R^3$, define its <line integral> by
$$
\boxed{\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\int_a^b\mathbf F(\gamma(t))\cdot\gamma'(t)\,dt,}
$$
adding the integrals over the smooth pieces when necessary. The value refers to the specified traversal: going around a curve twice counts twice.

An orientation-preserving <reparametrization> has the form $\widetilde\gamma(s)=\gamma(h(s))$, where $h:[\alpha,\beta]\to[a,b]$ is an increasing, piecewise smooth change of parameter with $h(\alpha)=a$, $h(\beta)=b$. For regular parametrizations one may take $h$ and its inverse continuously differentiable on each piece. The <chain rule> gives $\widetilde\gamma'(s)=\gamma'(h(s))h'(s)$. The <change of variables> $t=h(s)$ therefore yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_\alpha^\beta\mathbf F(\widetilde\gamma(s))\cdot\widetilde\gamma'(s)\,ds
&=\int_\alpha^\beta\mathbf F(\gamma(h(s)))\cdot\gamma'(h(s))h'(s)\,ds\\
&=\int_a^b\mathbf F(\gamma(t))\cdot\gamma'(t)\,dt.
\end{aligned}
$$
This proves <reparametrization invariance of a vector line integral>. If $h$ is decreasing, the endpoints in the final integral are reversed and the sign changes. Thus the same direction and the same multiplicity of traversal are essential.

Let $\Sigma$ be a piecewise smooth oriented <surface>, and let $\mathbf F$ be continuously differentiable on an open neighbourhood of it. <Stokes theorem> states that
$$
\boxed{\oint_{\partial\Sigma}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
=\iint_\Sigma(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS.}
$$
The <boundary orientation> is induced by the chosen <unit normal vector> through the <right-hand rule>. Smoothness on a neighbourhood makes the spatial <partial derivatives> in the <curl> well defined.

Choose the outward <orientation of a surface> on the lower half of the <ellipsoid>. At its lowest point the normal points down, so its boundary ellipse is traversed clockwise when viewed from above. Use
$$
\mathbf r(t)=(2\cos t,-3\sin t,0),\qquad0\leq t\leq2\pi.
$$
The <line integral> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\oint_{\partial\Sigma}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
&=\int_0^{2\pi}\bigl[(-6\sin t)(-2\sin t)+(6\cos t)(-3\cos t)\bigr]dt\\
&=12\pi-18\pi=\boxed{-6\pi}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For an independent <surface integral> calculation, first compute $\nabla\times\mathbf F=(2y,-2x,1)$. Parametrize the lower half by
$$
\mathbf R(u,v)=(2\sin u\cos v,3\sin u\sin v,-3\sqrt3\cos u),
\quad0\leq u\leq\frac\pi2,\quad0\leq v\leq2\pi.
$$
The outward vector <area element> is
$$
\mathbf n\,dS=(\mathbf R_v\times\mathbf R_u)\,du\,dv
=\bigl(9\sqrt3\sin^2u\cos v,6\sqrt3\sin^2u\sin v,-6\sin u\cos u\bigr)\,du\,dv.
$$
Thus
$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_\Sigma(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS
&=\int_0^{\pi/2}\int_0^{2\pi}
\bigl(30\sqrt3\sin^3u\sin v\cos v-6\sin u\cos u\bigr)\,dv\,du\\
&=-12\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin u\cos u\,du
=\boxed{-6\pi}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The two sides agree. Choosing the inward <unit normal vector> and its corresponding counterclockwise <boundary orientation> changes both answers to $6\pi$.

The sufficiency claim requires a domain qualification. For a continuously differentiable <vector field> on all of $\mathbb R^3$, or more generally on a <simply connected> open domain, the equivalence is valid. On an arbitrary domain, vanishing <curl> is necessary but may fail to be sufficient.

**Necessity.** Suppose every closed-curve <line integral> vanishes. Fix a point $p$ in the open domain and a <unit vector> $a$. A sufficiently small planar disk $D_\varepsilon$ of radius $\varepsilon$, centred at $p$ and normal to $a$, lies in the domain. <Stokes theorem> gives
$$
0=\oint_{\partial D_\varepsilon}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
=\iint_{D_\varepsilon}(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot a\,dS.
$$
Divide by $\pi\varepsilon^2$ and let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. By <continuity>, the average tends to $(\nabla\times\mathbf F)(p)\cdot a$. Taking the three coordinate directions proves $\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf F=0}$ everywhere.

**Sufficiency on $\mathbb R^3$.** A direct proof constructs a <potential of a conservative vector field>. Put
$$
\phi(x)=\int_0^1\mathbf F(tx)\cdot x\,dt.
$$
Write $F_j$ for the components of $\mathbf F$. Vanishing <curl> says $\partial_iF_j=\partial_jF_i$. Differentiation under the integral, followed by the <chain rule>, gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_i\phi(x)
&=\int_0^1\left[F_i(tx)+t\sum_{j=1}^3x_j\partial_iF_j(tx)\right]dt\\
&=\int_0^1\left[F_i(tx)+t\frac d{dt}F_i(tx)\right]dt
=\bigl[tF_i(tx)\bigr]_0^1=F_i(x).
\end{aligned}
$$
This is the <radial integral potential for a curl-free field>. The <fundamental theorem for line integrals> now gives zero on every closed curve. On a <simply connected> open domain the analogous argument uses path integrals: every loop can be contracted within the domain, and applying <Stokes theorem> to a piecewise smooth contraction makes its <circulation> zero. Equivalently, whenever a particular loop bounds an oriented spanning surface entirely in the domain, <Stokes theorem> immediately gives zero for that loop.

**Why the domain matters.** On $U=\mathbb R^3\setminus\{(0,0,z):z\in\mathbb R\}$, the <smooth> <vector field>
$$
\mathbf F=\left(-\frac y{x^2+y^2},\frac x{x^2+y^2},0\right)
$$
has zero <curl>, but its <line integral> around the unit <circle> in $z=0$ is $2\pi$, as in <1/b/ii>. No spanning surface for this loop can lie entirely in $U$. The literal claim without a condition on the domain therefore has this counterexample.

Let $a$ be an arbitrary constant <vector>. By the cyclic identity for the <scalar triple product>,
$$
a\cdot\oint_{\partial\Sigma}d\mathbf r\times\mathbf F
=\oint_{\partial\Sigma}(\mathbf F\times a)\cdot d\mathbf r.
$$
Apply <Stokes theorem> to $\mathbf F\times a$. Since $a$ is constant, the <curl> product identity gives
$$
\nabla\times(\mathbf F\times a)
=(a\cdot\nabla)\mathbf F-a(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F).
$$
Consequently
$$
\begin{aligned}
a\cdot\oint_{\partial\Sigma}d\mathbf r\times\mathbf F
&=\iint_\Sigma\left[\mathbf n\cdot(a\cdot\nabla)\mathbf F
-(\mathbf n\cdot a)(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)\right]dS\\
&=a\cdot\iint_\Sigma(\mathbf n\times\nabla)\times\mathbf F\,dS.
\end{aligned}
$$
For precision, the operator in the last line has components
$$
\bigl[(\mathbf n\times\nabla)\times\mathbf F\bigr]_i
=\sum_{j=1}^3n_j\partial_iF_j-n_i\sum_{j=1}^3\partial_jF_j.
$$
The <partial derivatives> act on $\mathbf F$, with $\mathbf n$ multiplying their values; they do not differentiate $\mathbf n$. Since the <dot products> agree for every constant $a$, the <cross-product form of Stokes theorem> follows:
$$
\boxed{\oint_{\partial\Sigma}d\mathbf r\times\mathbf F
=\iint_\Sigma(\mathbf n\times\nabla)\times\mathbf F\,dS.}
$$
Here $\wedge$ in the paper denotes the <cross product>, and the printed surface integral has its <area element> $dS$ understood.

For the <Fourier cosine series>, first take the <even extension> $F_e(x)=f(|x|)$ on $[-\pi,\pi]$, and then its $2\pi$-<periodic extension>. Evenness eliminates the <sine> terms. With the constant term written as $a_0/2$, the <Fourier coefficients> and <series> are
$$
\boxed{a_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi f(x)\cos(nx)\,dx\quad(n=0,1,2,\ldots),\qquad
\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n\cos(nx).}
$$
For the <Fourier sine series>, take the <odd extension> $F_o(x)=f(x)$ for $0<x<\pi$ and $F_o(x)=-f(-x)$ for $-\pi<x<0$, and extend with period $2\pi$. Values assigned at isolated endpoints do not change the <Fourier coefficients>. Oddness eliminates the constant and <cosine> terms, leaving
$$
\boxed{b_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi f(x)\sin(nx)\,dx\quad(n\geq1),\qquad
\sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n\sin(nx).}
$$
Both formulas follow by <orthogonality> on $[-\pi,\pi]$ and the parity of the extensions.

Using the usual piecewise smooth interpretation of the derivative hypothesis, <Dirichlet's theorem on Fourier series> gives the average of the two one-sided limits of the appropriate <periodic extension>. The <even extension> is continuous at $0$ and at the identified endpoints $\pm\pi$, so the <Fourier cosine series> converges to
$$
\boxed{f(x)\quad\text{for every }0\leq x\leq\pi.}
$$
For the <odd extension>, the one-sided limits at zero are $-f(0)$ and $f(0)$, while at $\pi$ the periodic one-sided limits are $f(\pi)$ and $-f(\pi)$. Hence the <Fourier sine series> converges to
$$
\boxed{\begin{cases}f(x),&0<x<\pi,\\0,&x=0\text{ or }x=\pi.\end{cases}}
$$
At an endpoint this equals $f$ precisely when $f$ is zero there. One can also see the zero endpoint values directly: every <partial sum> consists of <sines> that vanish at both endpoints.

Put $a=\pi/2$. The constant <Fourier coefficient> is
$$
a_0=\frac2\pi\left(\int_0^a x\,dx+\int_a^\pi a\,dx\right)=\frac{3\pi}{4}.
$$
For $n\geq1$, <integration by parts> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^a x\cos(nx)\,dx
&=\frac{a\sin(na)}n+\frac{\cos(na)-1}{n^2},\\
\int_a^\pi a\cos(nx)\,dx&=-\frac{a\sin(na)}n.
\end{aligned}
$$
The $n^{-1}$ terms cancel. Thus
$$
a_n=\frac{2}{\pi n^2}\left(\cos\frac{n\pi}{2}-1\right),\qquad
\boxed{f(x)=\frac{3\pi}{8}+\frac2\pi\sum_{n=1}^\infty
\frac{\cos(n\pi/2)-1}{n^2}\cos(nx)\quad(0\leq x\leq\pi).}
$$
This <Fourier cosine series> has $a_n=0$ when $n$ is divisible by four, $a_n=-4/(\pi n^2)$ when $n\equiv2\pmod4$, and $a_n=-2/(\pi n^2)$ when $n$ is odd.

Similarly,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^a x\sin(nx)\,dx&=-\frac{a\cos(na)}n+\frac{\sin(na)}{n^2},\\
\int_a^\pi a\sin(nx)\,dx&=\frac{a\cos(na)}n-\frac{a(-1)^n}n.
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore
$$
b_n=\frac{2\sin(n\pi/2)}{\pi n^2}-\frac{(-1)^n}{n},\qquad
\boxed{f(x)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\left(\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n+
\frac{2\sin(n\pi/2)}{\pi n^2}\right)\sin(nx)\quad(0<x<\pi).}
$$
The <Fourier sine series> also equals $f(0)=0$ at zero, but has value zero at $\pi$, where $f(\pi)=\pi/2$.

The <Fourier cosine series> converges to the continuous even $2\pi$-<periodic extension>. If $r\in[-\pi,\pi]$ represents $x$ modulo $2\pi$, its value is
$$
F_e(x)=\min(|r|,\pi/2).
$$
It has value zero at $x=2j\pi$, rises linearly with distance from these points until height $\pi/2$, and then stays flat until the next descending ramp. Thus the sketch on $[-3\pi,3\pi]$ has minima at $-2\pi,0,2\pi$, and plateaux centred at the odd multiples of $\pi$. Its large <partial sums> round the corners and closely follow the whole graph.

The <Fourier sine series> converges, away from the odd multiples of $\pi$, to the odd $2\pi$-<periodic extension>
$$
F_o(x)=\operatorname{sgn}(r)\min(|r|,\pi/2)\quad(-\pi<r<\pi).
$$
At every odd multiple of $\pi$ its value is zero, the midpoint of the jump from $\pi/2$ to $-\pi/2$. At even multiples of $\pi$ it passes continuously through zero. Thus on every interval $((2j-1)\pi,(2j+1)\pi)$ it has a negative plateau, a rising linear segment through $2j\pi$, and a positive plateau. The jump size is $\pi$.

The truncated <Fourier cosine series> gives the better approximation over the whole closed interval. Its <Fourier coefficients> obey $|a_n|\leq4/(\pi n^2)$, so <absolute convergence> and the tail bound
$$
\boxed{\sup_{0\leq x\leq\pi}|f(x)-C_N(x)|
\leq\frac4\pi\sum_{n>N}\frac1{n^2}
\leq\frac4{\pi N}}
$$
give <uniform convergence>. The even <periodic extension> is continuous; its slope changes only cause corners.

By contrast $b_n=(-1)^{n+1}/n+O(n^{-2})$. The odd <periodic extension> has a jump at $\pi$, giving slower coefficient decay and the <Gibbs phenomenon> near that endpoint. Every truncated <Fourier sine series> satisfies $S_N(\pi)=0$, so
$$
\boxed{|f(\pi)-S_N(\pi)|=\pi/2\quad\text{for every }N.}
$$
It therefore cannot converge uniformly to $f$ on $[0,\pi]$, although it converges at every point of $[0,\pi)$. This comparison concerns approximation across the interval; it does not assert that the cosine truncation has smaller error at every individual interior point for every $N$.

Take a bar of constant cross-sectional <area> $A$, thermally insulated along its sides, with no internal heat production. <Fourier's law> says that the <heat flux> in the positive $x$ direction is
$$
q(x,t)=-k(x)T_x(x,t).
$$
The <thermal conductivity> $k(x)$ is positive. On a small slice $[x,x+\Delta x]$, <conservation of energy> gives
$$
A\int_x^{x+\Delta x}\rho c_pT_t(s,t)\,ds
=A\bigl[q(x,t)-q(x+\Delta x,t)\bigr].
$$
Here $\rho c_p$ is the volumetric <heat capacity>, the product of <mass density> and <specific heat capacity>. Divide by $A\Delta x$, take $\Delta x\to0$, and use $\rho c_p=1$. The resulting <heat equation> is
$$
\boxed{T_t=-q_x=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bigl(k(x)T_x\bigr).}
$$
For differentiable $k$, this is $T_t=kT_{xx}+k'T_x$; retaining the divergence form also expresses <conservation of energy> correctly across an interface where $k$ jumps.

\boxed{T_s(x)=\frac Qk(2\ell-x).}

= Solution

Write $T=T_s+T_u$. The transient satisfies the <heat equation> and homogeneous mixed <boundary conditions>
$$
(T_u)_t=k(T_u)_{xx},\qquad
(T_u)_x(0,t)=0,\qquad T_u(2\ell,t)=0,\qquad
T_u(x,0)=-\frac Qk(2\ell-x).
$$
The left condition is a <Neumann boundary condition> and the right one a <Dirichlet boundary condition>. <Separation of variables>, $T_u=X(x)G(t)$, gives
$$
\frac{G'}{kG}=\frac{X''}{X}=-\mu^2,\qquad
X''+\mu^2X=0,\qquad G=e^{-k\mu^2t}.
$$
The condition $X'(0)=0$ removes the <sine> term. The condition $X(2\ell)=0$ then selects
$$
\mu_n=\frac{(2n+1)\pi}{4\ell},\qquad
X_n(x)=\cos(\mu_nx),\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.
$$
There is no nonzero mode with $\mu=0$, because a linear $X$ satisfying both conditions is zero. There is no growing mode either: <integration by parts> gives $\int_0^{2\ell}(X')^2dx=\mu^2\int_0^{2\ell}X^2dx$, so every nonzero mode has $\mu^2>0$.

These <Sturm-Liouville eigenfunctions> have <orthogonality> and squared <norm>
$$
\int_0^{2\ell}\cos(\mu_nx)\cos(\mu_jx)\,dx=\ell\,\delta_{nj}.
$$
Expand the initial transient in this half-integer <Fourier cosine series>. Its coefficients are
$$
\begin{aligned}
A_n&=-\frac Q{k\ell}\int_0^{2\ell}(2\ell-x)\cos(\mu_nx)\,dx\\
&=-\frac Q{k\ell}\frac{1-\cos(2\ell\mu_n)}{\mu_n^2}
=-\frac{16Q\ell}{k\pi^2(2n+1)^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The integration formula follows by <integration by parts>; the cosine at the endpoint is zero. Thus the <half-integer cosine expansion of a linear profile> gives
$$
\boxed{T(x,t)=\frac Qk(2\ell-x)
-\frac{16Q\ell}{k\pi^2}\sum_{n=0}^\infty
\frac{\cos\!\left(\frac{(2n+1)\pi x}{4\ell}\right)}{(2n+1)^2}
\exp\!\left(-\frac{(2n+1)^2\pi^2kt}{16\ell^2}\right).}
$$
At $t=0$ the <Fourier series> reproduces $-T_s$ everywhere, including the endpoints; its $n^{-2}$ coefficient decay gives <uniform convergence>. For every $t>0$ the exponential factors allow termwise differentiation and verify the <heat equation> and both <boundary conditions>. The sudden switch-on of nonzero <heat flux> is incompatible with the spatial derivative of the constant initial temperature at the corner $(0,0)$. Accordingly, the positive-time boundary derivative must not be inferred by differentiating the initial <Fourier series> term by term at that corner.

= iii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

For the stated inward heating, take $Q\geq0$ and put $\tau=\pi^2kt/(16\ell^2)$. Since $|\cos(\mu_nx)|\leq1$, the transient from <5/b/ii> satisfies
$$
|T(x,t)-T_s(x)|\leq\frac{16Q\ell}{k\pi^2}
\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{e^{-(2n+1)^2\tau}}{(2n+1)^2}.
$$
Equality holds at $x=0$, where every cosine is one and all coefficients have the same sign. Thus the maximum is attained at the heated end, and its exact value and late-time form are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\max_{0\leq x\leq2\ell}|T(x,t)-T_s(x)|
&=\frac{16Q\ell}{k\pi^2}e^{-\tau}
\left(1+\frac19e^{-8\tau}+O(e^{-24\tau})\right)\\
&\sim\boxed{\frac{16}{\pi^2}\frac{Q\ell}{k}
\exp\!\left(-\frac{\pi^2}{16}\frac{kt}{\ell^2}\right)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
This is dominance of the slowest-decaying <eigenfunction> when $kt\gg\ell^2$. More explicitly, the omitted relative correction is nonnegative and bounded by $(\pi^2/8-1)e^{-8\tau}$, using $\sum_{n\geq0}(2n+1)^{-2}=\pi^2/8$. For a signed imposed <heat flux>, the maximum-deviation formula uses $|Q|$.

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

In a steady state the <heat equation> says $(kT_s')'=0$, so the positive-$x$ <heat flux> $q=-kT_s'$ is the same constant $Q$ in both materials. Assume perfect thermal contact: temperature is continuous at $x=\ell$, and <conservation of energy> requires continuous <heat flux> there. Integrating from the right endpoint gives
$$
\boxed{T_s(x)=\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\frac{Q\ell}{k_2}+\frac Q{k_1}(\ell-x),&0\leq x\leq\ell,\\[6pt]
\displaystyle\frac Q{k_2}(2\ell-x),&\ell\leq x\leq2\ell.
\end{cases}}
$$
The two formulas agree at the interface. Their slopes differ, but $-k_1T_s'(\ell^-)=-k_2T_s'(\ell^+)=Q$. The total temperature drop is $Q\ell(1/k_1+1/k_2)$, the sum of the two layers' contributions in <steady heat conduction through layers>.

= Solution

Use the ideal transverse-motion model of the question: constant <tension>, small slopes, no bending stiffness, and no transverse body-force term. On a short segment away from $x=0$, the vertical components of the endpoint tensions are, to first order in $y_x$, $Ty_x(x+\Delta x,t)$ and $-Ty_x(x,t)$. Its mass is $\rho\Delta x$, since $\rho$ is <linear mass density>. <Newton's second law> gives
$$
\rho\Delta x\,y_{tt}=T\bigl[y_x(x+\Delta x,t)-y_x(x,t)\bigr].
$$
Dividing by $\Delta x$ and taking its limit proves the <wave equation on a string> on each side of the mass:
$$
\boxed{y_{tt}=c^2y_{xx},\qquad c^2=\frac T\rho,\qquad -\ell<x<0\text{ or }0<x<\ell.}
$$
The string on the right pulls the <point mass> with vertical component $Ty_x(0^+,t)$, while the string on the left contributes $-Ty_x(0^-,t)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{m\ddot Y(t)=T\bigl[y_x(0^+,t)-y_x(0^-,t)\bigr].}
$$
The fixed supports and attachment impose the <boundary conditions>
$$
\boxed{y(-\ell,t)=y(\ell,t)=0,\qquad y(0^-,t)=y(0^+,t)=Y(t).}
$$
Together with the mass equation, these are the endpoint and matching conditions. The slope can jump at $x=0$: the jump supplies the mass's vertical <acceleration>. Requiring the slope to be continuous would incorrectly remove its dynamics.

= b
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

Use <separation of variables> to seek a <normal mode> $y(x,t)=X(x)\cos(\omega t+\delta)$, with <angular frequency> $\omega>0$ and <wavenumber> $k=\omega/c$. The endpoint <boundary conditions> are built into
$$
X(x)=\begin{cases}
A\sin(k(x+\ell)),&-\ell\leq x\leq0,\\
B\sin(k(\ell-x)),&0\leq x\leq\ell.
\end{cases}
$$
Let $z=k\ell$ and denote the mass's displacement amplitude by $X_0$. Continuity and the mass equation become
$$
A\sin z=B\sin z=X_0,\qquad
Tk(A+B)\cos z=m\omega^2X_0.
$$
The two possibilities must be separated before dividing by $\sin z$.

**Antisymmetric modes.** If $\sin z=0$, then $z=n\pi$, $n=1,2,\ldots$, and $X_0=0$. The force equation gives $A+B=0$, since $\cos z\ne0$. Thus $X(-x)=-X(x)$ and the mass stays at rest. These allowed <angular frequencies> satisfy
$$
\boxed{\sin(k\ell)=0,\qquad k\ell=n\pi\ (n\geq1),\qquad\omega_n^{\mathrm{odd}}=\frac{cn\pi}{\ell}.}
$$
The restoring forces on the mass cancel even though the string vibrates.

**Symmetric modes.** If $\sin z\ne0$, continuity forces $A=B$. Cancelling the nonzero amplitude in the mass equation and using $\omega^2=(T/\rho)k^2$ gives
$$
2Tk\cos z=m\frac T\rho k^2\sin z,
\qquad
\boxed{\cot(k\ell)=\frac{mk}{2\rho}.}
$$
These modes have $X(-x)=X(x)$ and move the mass. In dimensionless form their equation is $\cot z=\alpha z$, where $\alpha=m/(2\rho\ell)>0$. This is the spectral splitting for a <string with a central point mass>.

\Image[mathematics-v-string-frequencies.png]
{title=Antisymmetric zeros and symmetric frequency intersections}

For the antisymmetric family, the graph of $\sin z$ crosses zero at every positive multiple of $\pi$. For the symmetric family, on each interval $(n\pi,n\pi+\pi/2)$, $n=0,1,\ldots$, the graph of $\cot z$ decreases continuously from $+\infty$ to zero while $\alpha z$ increases. By the <intermediate value theorem> and strict <monotonicity>, there is exactly one intersection in each interval. On the remaining half-intervals $\cot z<0<\alpha z$, so there are no further positive roots. This proves graphically and analytically that both families contain infinitely many <normal modes>. The figure uses $\alpha=1$ for illustration.

The algebraic case $k=0$ is not a mode: a zero-frequency profile would be linear on each half, continuous and zero at the endpoints; the zero-acceleration mass equation also makes its slopes agree, forcing the entire profile to be zero. More generally, <integration by parts> on the two halves and the matching condition give
$$
\omega^2\left(\rho\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}X^2\,dx+mX_0^2\right)
=T\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}(X')^2\,dx>0
$$
for a nonzero real mode. Thus no nonzero static or exponentially growing mode has been omitted.

{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

The lowest positive root belongs to the symmetric family and lies in $(0,\pi/2)$; all antisymmetric roots are at least $\pi$. Let $\alpha=m/(2\rho\ell)\gg1$. The equation $\cot z=\alpha z$ forces its first root to tend to zero: for any fixed $\varepsilon>0$, the right side eventually exceeds $\cot\varepsilon$ at $z=\varepsilon$, so the decreasing-minus-increasing difference has its zero below $\varepsilon$.

The <Taylor series> $\cot z=z^{-1}-z/3+O(z^3)$ then gives
$$
1=\left(\alpha+\frac13\right)z^2+O(z^4),\qquad
z\sim\alpha^{-1/2}=\sqrt{\frac{2\rho\ell}{m}}.
$$
Since $\omega=cz/\ell$ and $c=\sqrt{T/\rho}$, the lowest <angular frequency> is
$$
\boxed{\omega\sim\frac1\ell\sqrt{\frac T\rho}\sqrt{\frac{2\rho\ell}{m}}
=\sqrt{\frac{2T}{m\ell}}=\omega_0.}
$$
Physically, at such a low <frequency> each half-string is nearly straight. If the mass moves up by $Y$, the two slopes are approximately $Y/\ell$ and $-Y/\ell$, so the restoring <force> is $-2TY/\ell$. The resulting <harmonic oscillator equation>, $m\ddot Y+(2T/\ell)Y=0$, has exactly the leading <angular frequency> above. The next correction from the same expansion is $\omega/\omega_0=1-\rho\ell/(3m)+O((\rho\ell/m)^2)$.
