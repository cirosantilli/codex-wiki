= Solution

For a finite set $S=\{s_1,\ldots,s_k\}$ of <vectors> in a real <vector space>, its <span> is the set of all <linear combinations>:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Sp}(S)=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^ka_is_i:a_i\in\mathbb R\right\}.}
$$
The <span> of the empty set is $\{0\}$. The set $S$ is <linearly independent> if $\sum_i a_is_i=0$ implies $a_i=0$ for every $i$. It is a <spanning set> for $V$ if $\operatorname{Sp}(S)=V$. A <basis> is both a <linearly independent> set and a <spanning set>.

For <basis extension>, start with the given <linearly independent> set. If it does not span $V$, choose $v\notin\operatorname{Sp}(S)$ and adjoin it. The enlarged set remains <linearly independent>: a relation $av+\sum_i a_is_i=0$ with $a\ne0$ would express $v$ as a <linear combination> of $S$, a contradiction; if $a=0$, independence of $S$ forces all other coefficients to be zero.

Repeat whenever the current <span> is proper. Each step increases the set's size by one, while the allowed bound says its size cannot exceed $\dim V$. Thus the process terminates with a <linearly independent> set spanning $V$, hence a <basis> containing the original set. This also covers an initially empty set and the zero-dimensional <vector space>.

Choose a <basis> $u_1,\ldots,u_r$ of $U$, extend it to a <basis> $u_1,\ldots,u_r,w_1,\ldots,w_s$ of $W$, and extend again to a <basis> of $V$ by adding $v_1,\ldots,v_t$. Define
$$
\boxed{W'=\operatorname{span}\{u_1,\ldots,u_r,v_1,\ldots,v_t\}.}
$$
Every member of the full <basis> lies in $W$ or $W'$, so the <sum of vector subspaces> is $W+W'=V$. If $x\in W\cap W'$, express it in both displayed <bases>. Uniqueness of the coordinates in the full <basis> forces its $w_j$ and $v_j$ coefficients to vanish. Thus $x\in U$. The reverse inclusion is built into the construction, proving $W\cap W'=U$. This is a <subspace supplement with a prescribed intersection>.

For the matrix example, let
$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
I=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The <skew-symmetric matrices> are exactly $U=\operatorname{span}\{J\}$, and the traceless <matrices> form $W$. A suitable answer is
$$
\boxed{W'=\operatorname{span}\{I,J\}
=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\-b&a\end{pmatrix}:a,b\in\mathbb R\right\}.}
$$
The <matrix trace> of $aI+bJ$ is $2a$, so $W\cap W'=\operatorname{span}\{J\}=U$. Every $2\times2$ real <matrix> has the decomposition
$$
A=\left(A-\frac{\operatorname{tr}A}{2}I\right)+\frac{\operatorname{tr}A}{2}I,
$$
whose first term lies in $W$ and second in $W'$. Therefore $W+W'=M_2(\mathbb R)$, as required.

For a <linear map> $T:V\to V$, its <kernel of a linear map> and <image of a linear map> are
$$
\ker T=\{v\in V:T(v)=0\},\qquad\operatorname{im}T=\{T(v):v\in V\}.
$$
Its <nullity of a linear map> is $n(T)=\dim\ker T$, and its <rank of a linear map> is $r(T)=\dim\operatorname{im}T$.

Choose a <basis> $u_1,\ldots,u_k$ of $\ker T$ and extend it to a <basis> $u_1,\ldots,u_k,v_1,\ldots,v_m$ of $V$. The <vectors> $T(v_1),\ldots,T(v_m)$ span $\operatorname{im}T$, because applying $T$ to an arbitrary <linear combination> of the full <basis> eliminates the $u_i$ terms. They are also <linearly independent>: if $\sum_jb_jT(v_j)=0$, then $\sum_jb_jv_j\in\ker T$, so $\sum_jb_jv_j=\sum_i a_i u_i$ for some coefficients. Independence of the extended <basis> forces all $a_i,b_j$ to vanish.

Consequently $r(T)=m$, $n(T)=k$ and $\dim V=k+m$. This proves the <rank-nullity theorem>:
$$
\boxed{\dim V=r(T)+n(T).}
$$

Restrict $S$ to the <vector subspace> $W=\operatorname{im}T$. This restriction has <kernel of a linear map> $W\cap\ker S$ and <image of a linear map> $S(W)=\operatorname{im}(ST)$. The <rank-nullity theorem> on $W$ therefore gives
$$
r(ST)=r(T)-\dim(\operatorname{im}T\cap\ker S).
$$
Apply the <rank-nullity theorem> to $T$ and $ST$ on $V$ to obtain the exact <nullity of a composition> identity:
$$
n(ST)=n(T)+\dim(\operatorname{im}T\cap\ker S).
$$
Since the intersection is a <vector subspace> of $\ker S$, its <dimension> is at most $n(S)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{n(ST)\leq n(T)+n(S).}
$$

The <image of a linear map> $\operatorname{im}(ST)=S(\operatorname{im}T)$ lies inside $\operatorname{im}S$. Therefore $r(ST)\leq r(S)$. Also $S$ restricted to $\operatorname{im}T$ cannot have <rank of a linear map> greater than its domain's <dimension>, so $r(ST)\leq\dim\operatorname{im}T=r(T)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{r(ST)\leq\min\{r(S),r(T)\}.}
$$
Equivalently, apply the <rank-nullity theorem> to that restriction: $r(ST)=r(T)-\dim(\operatorname{im}T\cap\ker S)\leq r(T)$. This proof keeps track of the intermediate <vector subspace>, rather than assuming an invertible factor.

An <elementary row operation> is one of three operations: interchange two rows, multiply one row by a nonzero <scalar>, or add a scalar multiple of one row to a different row. Each operation is reversed by another operation of the same kind.

Performing such an operation on an $m\times n$ <matrix> $A$ is equivalent to left multiplication by the $m\times m$ <elementary matrix> obtained by applying that operation to the <identity matrix>. Each <elementary matrix> is invertible, with inverse corresponding to the reverse operation. Thus a sequence of operations gives
$$
B=E_kE_{k-1}\cdots E_1A,
\qquad
\boxed{B=PA,\quad P=E_k\cdots E_1\text{ invertible}.}
$$
For square <matrices>, if $A$ were invertible, then $B=PA$ would have inverse $A^{-1}P^{-1}$. Hence **if $B$ is singular, $A$ must be singular as well**. In fact the two are invertible together, because $A=P^{-1}B$ gives the converse.

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

Solve $Ax=b$ for a general right-hand side $b=(b_1,b_2,b_3,b_4)^T$. Subtracting the fourth equation from the second gives $x_3=b_2-b_4$. The first and fourth then give
$$
x_1=b_1-b_2+b_4-x_4,\qquad x_2=b_4-x_4.
$$
Substitution into the third equation reduces the remaining condition to
$$
(t-2)x_4=b_3-b_1-b_4.
$$
If $t\ne2$, every right-hand side therefore has a unique solution, and collecting its four coefficients yields the <matrix inverse>
$$
\boxed{A^{-1}=\frac1{t-2}
\begin{pmatrix}
t-1&2-t&-1&t-1\\
1&0&-1&t-1\\
0&t-2&0&2-t\\
-1&0&1&-1
\end{pmatrix},\qquad t\ne2.}
$$
For $t=2$, the nonzero <vector> $(-1,-1,0,1)^T$ lies in the <kernel of a linear map> represented by $A$, so $A$ is singular. Hence **$A$ is invertible exactly when $t\ne2$**. The same calculation gives $\det A=t-2$; the formula above can also be checked by multiplying its numerator by $A$ to obtain $(t-2)I$.

A real number $\lambda$ is an <eigenvalue> of $A$ if there is a nonzero <vector> $v$ with $Av=\lambda v$; such a $v$ is an <eigenvector> for $\lambda$. The nonzero condition is essential.

For distinct <eigenvalues> $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r$, choose corresponding nonzero <eigenvectors> $v_1,\ldots,v_r$. Prove their <linear independence> by induction on $r$. The claim for one vector is immediate. Given a relation $\sum_{i=1}^r a_iv_i=0$, apply $A-\lambda_rI$ to get
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}a_i(\lambda_i-\lambda_r)v_i=0.
$$
By the induction hypothesis, every coefficient here is zero. Distinctness gives $a_i=0$ for $i<r$, and the original relation then gives $a_r=0$ since $v_r\ne0$. Thus the <eigenvectors> are <linearly independent>.

With $n$ distinct real <eigenvalues>, the $n$ real <eigenvectors> form a <basis> of $\mathbb R^n$. Put them into the columns of $P$. Then $P$ is invertible and
$$
AP=P\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n),
\qquad
\boxed{P^{-1}AP=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n).}
$$
This proves <diagonalizability> directly.

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

Suppose $A^T=A$. For <eigenvectors> $v_i,v_j$ with distinct real <eigenvalues>, symmetry gives
$$
\lambda_i v_i^Tv_j=(Av_i)^Tv_j=v_i^TA^Tv_j=v_i^TAv_j
=\lambda_jv_i^Tv_j.
$$
Thus $(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)v_i^Tv_j=0$, and the <eigenvectors> are <orthogonal>. Divide each by its nonzero <Euclidean norm> to obtain an <orthonormal basis>. The <matrix> $P$ having these normalized columns satisfies $P^TP=I$, so it is an <orthogonal matrix> and $P^{-1}=P^T$. As in part (a),
$$
\boxed{P^TAP=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n).}
$$
This proves the needed <orthogonal diagonalization> from symmetry and distinct <eigenvalues>, without invoking an unproved <spectral theorem>.

Among the $|G|+1$ powers $e,g,g^2,\ldots,g^{|G|}$ in the finite <group> $G$, the <pigeonhole principle> forces two to be equal: $g^i=g^j$ with $0\leq i<j\leq|G|$. Multiply by $g^{-i}$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{g^{j-i}=e,\qquad j-i>0.}
$$
This proves the existence of a positive power equal to the <identity element>, without presupposing the <order of a group element>.

The <order of a group element> $o(g)$ is the least positive integer $n$ for which $g^n=e$, whose existence follows from part (i). If $g^k=e$, use the <Euclidean division> to write $k=qn+r$, where $0\leq r<n$. Then
$$
e=g^k=(g^n)^qg^r=g^r.
$$
Minimality of $n$ forces $r=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{o(g)\mid k.}
$$
The argument also works for negative $k$, using integer powers and the same remainder convention.

<Lagrange's theorem> states that for a <subgroup> $H$ of a finite <group> $G$,
$$
|G|=[G:H]|H|,
$$
where $[G:H]$ is the <index of a subgroup>, the number of its <left cosets>. In particular, $|H|$ divides $|G|$.

The <cyclic subgroup> $\langle g\rangle$ consists of the distinct elements $e,g,\ldots,g^{n-1}$, where $n=o(g)$. Every power reduces to one of these by the <Euclidean division>, and a repetition among them would contradict minimality of $n$. Thus $|\langle g\rangle|=o(g)$. Applying <Lagrange's theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{o(g)\mid|G|.}
$$

Let $n=o(g)$. A <group homomorphism> preserves powers and the <identity element>, so
$$
\phi(g)^n=\phi(g^n)=\phi(e)=e_H.
$$
The preceding divisibility property gives
$$
\boxed{o(\phi(g))\mid o(g).}
$$
Now assume $\phi$ is surjective and choose $h\in H$ of order $m$. Take a preimage $g$ of $h$, and write $o(g)=mr$, as the divisibility result permits. The element $g^r$ has order exactly $m$: its $m$th power is $e$, while $(g^r)^s=e$ implies $mr\mid rs$, hence $m\mid s$. Therefore **$G$ contains an element of order $m$**, namely $g^r$.

The preimage $g$ itself need not have order $m$; taking the suitable power is the essential step. The constructed element need not map to the original $h$. This proves that <element orders in a finite quotient occur in the original group>.

A <group homomorphism> from an additive <cyclic group> $\mathbb Z_n$ is determined by the image $a$ of $1$, subject exactly to $a^n=e$ in multiplicative notation, or $na=0$ in an additive target.

For $\mathbb Z_{18}\to\mathbb Z_{15}$, the relation is $18a\equiv0\pmod{15}$, equivalently $3a\equiv0\pmod{15}$. The possibilities are $a=0,5,10$, and each defines a <homomorphism between cyclic groups>. Thus there are **three** such maps, an instance of the <number of homomorphisms between finite cyclic groups>.

For $\mathbb Z_9\to S_4$, the image of $1$ must be a <permutation> whose <order of a group element> divides $9$. A <permutation> on four symbols can have order $1,2,3$ or $4$, so the allowed images are the identity and the <three-cycles>. There are $\binom43(3-1)!=8$ <three-cycles>: choose their three symbols and one of the two cyclic orientations. Hence there are **nine** maps.

Finally, each <transposition> in $S_5$ has order $2$, so its image in $\mathbb Z_5$ must have order dividing both $2$ and $5$, and is therefore the identity. Every <permutation> is a product of <transpositions>, so every element must map to the identity. There is **one** map. In the order asked, the counts are
$$
\boxed{3,\qquad9,\qquad1.}
$$

A <subgroup> $N$ of $G$ is a <normal subgroup> if conjugation by every element preserves it:
$$
\boxed{gNg^{-1}=N\quad\text{for every }g\in G.}
$$
Equivalently, $gN=Ng$ for every $g\in G$. Here equality means equality of sets; it does not require each individual element of $N$ to commute with every element of $G$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <center of a group> consists of its elements commuting with every element. First check it is a <subgroup>. The <identity element> is central. If $a,b\in Z(G)$ and $x\in G$, then $b^{-1}$ commutes with $x$ and
$$
(ab^{-1})x=a(xb^{-1})=x(ab^{-1}).
$$
Thus $ab^{-1}\in Z(G)$, proving closure under the subgroup criterion. Moreover, if $z\in Z(G)$, then $xzx^{-1}=z$ for every $x\in G$. Hence conjugation fixes the center elementwise, so
$$
\boxed{xZ(G)x^{-1}=Z(G)\quad(x\in G).}
$$
Therefore the <center of a group> is a <normal subgroup>.

If $g\in Z(G)$, it commutes with each $x\in G$, so $x^{-1}gx=g$. Its <conjugacy class> is therefore $C(g)=\{g\}$.

Conversely, if $C(g)=\{g\}$, then $x^{-1}gx=g$ for every $x$. Multiplication by $x$ on the left gives $gx=xg$ for every $x$, which is exactly membership in the <center of a group>. Thus
$$
\boxed{g\in Z(G)\iff C(g)=\{g\}.}
$$

<Conjugation> of a <permutation> relabels each symbol by the inverse conjugating permutation. Specifically,
$$
\boxed{\tau^{-1}(a_1a_2\cdots a_n)\tau
=(\tau^{-1}(a_1)\ \tau^{-1}(a_2)\ \cdots\ \tau^{-1}(a_n)).}
$$
To verify the direction of the inverse, apply the left side to $\tau^{-1}(a_j)$: $\tau$ first takes it to $a_j$, the original cycle takes it to $a_{j+1}$, and $\tau^{-1}$ takes it to $\tau^{-1}(a_{j+1})$, with cyclic indexing. Any symbols fixed by the original <permutation> are similarly relabelled and remain fixed. More generally, conjugate every cycle of a disjoint-cycle decomposition in this way; disjointness is preserved because $\tau^{-1}$ is a <bijection>.

These have the respective <cycle types> $(3,1)$ and $(2,2)$ in the <symmetric group> $S_4$.

Suppose $\sigma\ne e$. Choose a symbol $i$ moved by $\sigma$ and write $j=\sigma(i)\ne i$. Since $n\geq3$, choose a third symbol $k$ distinct from $i,j$. Let $\tau=(jk)$, a <transposition> that fixes $i$. Then
$$
(\tau^{-1}\sigma\tau)(i)=\tau^{-1}(\sigma(i))=\tau^{-1}(j)=k\ne j=\sigma(i).
$$
Thus $\tau^{-1}\sigma\tau\ne\sigma$, proving that the <conjugacy class> of any nonidentity <permutation> has more than one element. By part (a)(iii), no such <permutation> lies in the <center of a group>. The identity always does, so
$$
\boxed{Z(S_n)=\{e\}\quad(n\geq3).}
$$
The restriction on $n$ is necessary: $S_2$ is an <abelian group> and equals its own center.

A left <group action> of $G$ on a set $S$ is a function $\phi:G\times S\to S$ satisfying
$$
\boxed{\phi(e,s)=s,\qquad\phi(gh,s)=\phi(g,\phi(h,s))}
$$
for all $s\in S$ and $g,h\in G$. Writing $g\cdot s=\phi(g,s)$, these are $e\cdot s=s$ and $(gh)\cdot s=g\cdot(h\cdot s)$. No commutativity is assumed, so the order of composition matters.

The <group action> law gives
$$
\phi_g\circ\phi_{g^{-1}}=\phi_e=\operatorname{id}_S,
\qquad
\phi_{g^{-1}}\circ\phi_g=\operatorname{id}_S.
$$
Thus $\phi_g$ is a <bijection> with inverse $\phi_{g^{-1}}$, hence a <permutation> of $S$. Also, for every $s$,
$$
\phi_{gh}(s)=\phi(g,\phi(h,s))=(\phi_g\circ\phi_h)(s).
$$
Therefore the assignment $\Phi(g)=\phi_g$ takes values in the <symmetric group> $\operatorname{Sym}(S)$ and satisfies
$$
\boxed{\Phi(gh)=\Phi(g)\Phi(h).}
$$
It is a <group homomorphism>, with the group product on the right interpreted as composition of <permutations>.

Define $g\cdot(g_1H)=(gg_1)H$ on the set of <left cosets>. It is well-defined: if $g_1H=g_2H$, then $g_2=g_1h$ for some $h\in H$, so $(gg_2)H=(gg_1h)H=(gg_1)H$. The identity fixes each coset, and associativity in $G$ gives
$$
g\cdot(h\cdot(g_1H))=(ghg_1)H=(gh)\cdot(g_1H).
$$
Hence this is a left <group action>, and part (a) supplies its <group homomorphism> $\Phi:G\to\operatorname{Sym}(S)$.

An element of the <kernel of a group homomorphism> $\Phi$ fixes every coset, in particular $H$ itself. Thus $g\in\ker\Phi$ implies $gH=H$, which implies $g\in H$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\ker\Phi\leq H.}
$$
The subgroup symbol is justified because a <kernel of a group homomorphism> is a <subgroup>, and the argument proves containment.

By <Lagrange's theorem>, the <index of a subgroup> is $[G:H]=75/25=3$. Thus the set of <left cosets> has three elements, and the preceding <group action> yields a <group homomorphism> $\Phi:G\to S_3$ after labelling them.

Its image is a <subgroup> of $S_3$, so its order divides $6$. By the <first isomorphism theorem>, $|\operatorname{im}\Phi|=|G|/|\ker\Phi|$ also divides $75$. Hence its order divides $\gcd(6,75)=3$, giving possibilities $1$ and $3$. The action on cosets is <transitive>: $g_2g_1^{-1}$ sends $g_1H$ to $g_2H$. It cannot be the trivial action on a three-element set, so the image does not have order $1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{|\operatorname{im}\Phi|=3.}
$$
It follows that $|\ker\Phi|=75/3=25$. Part (b)(i) gives $\ker\Phi\leq H$, and $|H|=25$; equal finite orders force equality. A <kernel of a group homomorphism> is a <normal subgroup>, so
$$
\boxed{H=\ker\Phi\mathrel{\trianglelefteq}G.}
$$
This is an instance of the fact that a <subgroup of index the smallest prime divisor is normal>.
