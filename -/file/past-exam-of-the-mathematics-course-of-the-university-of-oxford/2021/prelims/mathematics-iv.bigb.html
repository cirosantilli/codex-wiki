Write $\mathbf u=\mathbf b-\mathbf a$. Since the <triangle> is nondegenerate, $\mathbf u\ne0$. Every <point> on the <line> $AB$ has a unique <position vector> $\mathbf p=\mathbf a+t\mathbf u$. The condition that $CP$ is <orthogonal> to $AB$ is the <dot product> <equation>
$$
(\mathbf c-\mathbf a-t\mathbf u)\cdot\mathbf u=0.
$$
Its <coefficient> of $t$ is $-\|\mathbf u\|^2\ne0$, so it has exactly one solution. The <foot of a perpendicular> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbf p=\mathbf a+\frac{(\mathbf c-\mathbf a)\cdot(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)}{\|\mathbf b-\mathbf a\|^2}(\mathbf b-\mathbf a).}
$$
This is the <orthogonal projection> of $C$ onto the <line> $AB$, expressed using the <Euclidean norm>.

With $t$ as in <1/a/i>, the <foot of a perpendicular> lies strictly between the endpoints precisely when $0<t<1$. Since the <denominator> is positive, this gives
$$
\boxed{0<(\mathbf c-\mathbf a)\cdot(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)<\|\mathbf b-\mathbf a\|^2.}
$$
Equivalently, both <dot products>
$$
(\mathbf c-\mathbf a)\cdot(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)>0,\qquad
(\mathbf c-\mathbf b)\cdot(\mathbf a-\mathbf b)>0
$$
are positive: the <angles> of the <triangle> at $A$ and $B$ are both less than $\pi/2$. If “between” includes the endpoints, replace both strict <inequalities> by non-strict ones; equality gives $P=A$ or $P=B$.

The <altitude of a triangle> through $A$ has <equation> $(\mathbf a-\mathbf d)\cdot(\mathbf b-\mathbf c)=0$, and the <altitude of a triangle> through $B$ has <equation> $(\mathbf b-\mathbf d)\cdot(\mathbf a-\mathbf c)=0$. Their <normal vectors> $\mathbf b-\mathbf c$ and $\mathbf a-\mathbf c$ are <linearly independent> because the <triangle> is nondegenerate. Consequently these two <lines> meet in exactly one <point> $D$.

Substitute its <position vector> $\mathbf d$ in the supplied identity. Its first term vanishes by the first <altitude of a triangle> <equation>, and its second term vanishes by the second <altitude of a triangle> <equation>. Hence
$$
(\mathbf a-\mathbf b)\cdot(\mathbf c-\mathbf d)=0.
$$
This is exactly the condition that $D$ lies on the <altitude of a triangle> through $C$. **The three <lines> therefore meet in a unique <point>, the <orthocenter>.** This argument includes <right triangles> and <triangles> whose <orthocenter> lies outside them.

Let $A=(Y+Z)/2$, $B=(Z+X)/2$, and $C=(X+Y)/2$, interpreting these expressions as <position vectors>. The resulting <medial triangle> is nondegenerate: for example,
$$
\mathbf b-\mathbf c=\tfrac12(\mathbf z-\mathbf y).
$$
Thus $BC$ is parallel to $YZ$, and the same calculation holds cyclically. The <altitude of a triangle> through $A$ is consequently the <line> through the <midpoint> of $YZ$ that is <orthogonal> to $YZ$, namely its <perpendicular bisector>. Each remaining <altitude of a triangle> similarly gives one of the other two <perpendicular bisectors>.

By <1/a/iii>, the <medial triangle> has a unique <orthocenter>, so **the three <perpendicular bisectors> meet in one <point>**. Every <point> on the <perpendicular bisector> of $YZ$ is equally far from $Y$ and $Z$: expanding the squares of the two <Euclidean distances> gives $2\mathbf q\cdot(\mathbf z-\mathbf y)=\|\mathbf z\|^2-\|\mathbf y\|^2$. Applying this to the three sides shows that the common <point> is equally far from $X,Y,Z$. It is the <circumcenter>.

Write $z=x+iy$, with $y\ne0$ because the <triangle> is nondegenerate. Let $w=u+iv$ be its <circumcenter>. Equality of the <complex modulus> values $|w|=|w-1|$ gives $u=1/2$. Equality of $|w|=|w-z|$, after expanding the squares, gives
$$
2\operatorname{Re}(w\bar z)=|z|^2,\qquad x+2vy=x^2+y^2.
$$
Thus the <real part> and <imaginary part> determine
$$
w=\frac12+i\frac{x^2+y^2-x}{2y}.
$$
Using the <complex conjugate> $\bar z=x-iy$, we also have
$$
\frac{z(1-\bar z)}{z-\bar z}
=\frac{x-x^2-y^2+iy}{2iy}
=\frac12+i\frac{x^2+y^2-x}{2y}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{w=\frac{z(1-\bar z)}{z-\bar z}.}
$$
The nonzero <denominator> is exactly the nondegeneracy condition. If $z$ is real, the three distinct collinear <points> do not have a finite <circumcenter>.

Represent the <quadratic form> by the <symmetric matrix>
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta/2\\\beta/2&\gamma\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf x^TM\mathbf x=1.
$$
The <spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices> gives an <orthogonal matrix> $Q$ and real <eigenvalues> $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ such that $Q^TMQ=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\lambda_2)$. In the <orthogonal change of coordinates> $\mathbf x=Q\mathbf u$, the <equation> becomes $\lambda_1u_1^2+\lambda_2u_2^2=1$.

If both <eigenvalues> are positive, this is an <ellipse> with semiaxes $\lambda_1^{-1/2}$ and $\lambda_2^{-1/2}$. Conversely, a zero <eigenvalue> makes the set either empty or unbounded along that <coordinate>, while two negative <eigenvalues> give the empty set. Opposite signs give an unbounded <hyperbola>. A negative <eigenvalue> together with a zero one also gives the empty set. None of those cases is an <ellipse>. Therefore the exact condition is that $M$ be a <symmetric positive-definite matrix>.

The sum and product of its <eigenvalues> are its <matrix trace> and <determinant>:
$$
\lambda_1+\lambda_2=\alpha+\gamma,\qquad
\lambda_1\lambda_2=\alpha\gamma-\frac{\beta^2}{4}.
$$
Two real numbers are both positive precisely when their sum and product are positive. Thus
$$
\boxed{\alpha+\gamma>0,\qquad 4\alpha\gamma>\beta^2.}
$$
An <orthogonal transformation> preserves <area>. Using <2/a/i> gives the <quadratic-form ellipse area>
$$
\boxed{\mathcal A=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{\det M}}=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{4\alpha\gamma-\beta^2}}.}
$$

Eliminate $z$ using the <plane> <equation>. The <orthogonal projection> consists exactly of the <points> satisfying
$$
x^2+xy+y^2+x+y=1,
$$
since each such $(x,y)$ lifts uniquely to $(x,y,1-x-y)$ on the intersection. Translating to the <center of an ellipse> with $X=x+1/3$ and $Y=y+1/3$ gives
$$
X^2+XY+Y^2=\frac43.
$$
The <symmetric matrix> of this <quadratic form> is $M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1/2\\1/2&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, whose <eigenvalues> are $3/2$ and $1/2$. Thus it is a <symmetric positive-definite matrix>, and the projected curve is an <ellipse>. The <quadratic-form ellipse area>, with the right-hand side scaled by $4/3$, is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal A(C')=\frac{(4/3)\pi}{\sqrt{3/4}}=\frac{8\pi}{3\sqrt3}.}
$$
For example, the <orthogonal change of coordinates> $u=(X+Y)/\sqrt2$, $v=(X-Y)/\sqrt2$ gives semiaxes $2\sqrt2/3$ and $2\sqrt6/3$, with the same product.

Let $D$ be the filled projected <ellipse>. The part of the <plane> bounded by $C$ is the <parametrized surface>
$$
\mathbf R(x,y)=(x,y,1-x-y),\qquad (x,y)\in D.
$$
Its <tangent vectors> satisfy
$$
\mathbf R_x=(1,0,-1),\qquad \mathbf R_y=(0,1,-1),\qquad
\|\mathbf R_x\times\mathbf R_y\|=\|(1,1,1)\|=\sqrt3.
$$
The <surface area> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathcal A(C)=\iint_D\sqrt3\,dx\,dy=\sqrt3\frac{8\pi}{3\sqrt3}=\frac{8\pi}{3}.}
$$
This also illustrates the <orthogonal projection area formula>: the <unit normal> to the <plane> has vertical component $1/\sqrt3$, so projection multiplies <area> by $1/\sqrt3$.

For a regular <parametrized surface> that covers its image once, up to sets of zero <area>, its <surface area> is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Area}(S)=\iint_U\left\|\frac{\partial\mathbf r}{\partial u}\times\frac{\partial\mathbf r}{\partial v}\right\|\,du\,dv.}
$$
The integrand is the <area element of a surface>, the <area> of the small <parallelogram> spanned by the two <tangent vectors>. A <parametrization> that covers a region more than once counts that multiplicity; the geometric <surface area> requires a single covering or a division into nonoverlapping patches.

The endpoint <radii> are $5$ and $13$, but **substituting those two numbers in the finite <conical frustum> formula is invalid**. A rotation about the $z$-axis preserves the horizontal <angle> between the endpoint <position vectors>; here $(3,4)$ and $(12,5)$ are not parallel. Parametrize the actual segment by
$$
(x,y,z)=(3+9t,4+t,1+6t),\qquad0\leq t\leq1.
$$
Its rotated <circle> at height $1+6t$ has <radius>
$$
\rho(t)=\sqrt{(3+9t)^2+(4+t)^2}=\sqrt{25+62t+82t^2},\qquad
\rho'(t)=\frac{31+82t}{\rho(t)}.
$$
Thus the rotated image is the <surface of revolution> $\mathbf R(t,\theta)=(\rho(t)\cos\theta,\rho(t)\sin\theta,1+6t)$. Applying the <conical frustum> formula to infinitesimal pieces of this radial profile, or directly using the <cross product> as in <3/b/ii>, gives the <surface area of a surface of revolution>
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal A
&=2\pi\int_0^1\rho(t)\sqrt{\rho'(t)^2+36}\,dt\\
&=2\pi\int_0^1\sqrt{9676t^2+7316t+1861}\,dt.
\end{aligned}
$$
To evaluate it explicitly, put $u=82t+31$. Then $9676t^2+7316t+1861=(59u^2+19602)/41$. Define
$$
G(u)=u\sqrt{59u^2+19602}+\frac{19602}{\sqrt{59}}\log\bigl(\sqrt{59}\,u+\sqrt{59u^2+19602}\bigr).

The <chain rule> gives $G'(u)=2\sqrt{59u^2+19602}$, so the answer is

\boxed{\mathcal A=\frac{\pi}{82\sqrt{41}}\bigl(G(113)-G(31)\bigr)\simeq561.8721076764.}

The positive <logarithm> arguments make this a real-valued expression. Rotating the segment's endpoints independently into one vertical <plane> before joining them would instead create a different surface, with <surface area> $180\pi\simeq565.4867$.

Let $\mathbf r(u,v)$ parametrize $S$. Then $A\mathbf r$ parametrizes its image, with <tangent vectors> $A\mathbf r_u$ and $A\mathbf r_v$. An <orthogonal matrix> preserves <dot products>, so the <Lagrange identity for the cross product> gives, for any $\mathbf p,\mathbf q$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\|A\mathbf p\times A\mathbf q\|^2
&=\|A\mathbf p\|^2\|A\mathbf q\|^2-(A\mathbf p\cdot A\mathbf q)^2\\
&=\|\mathbf p\|^2\|\mathbf q\|^2-(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf q)^2
=\|\mathbf p\times\mathbf q\|^2.
\end{aligned}
$$
The <area element of a surface> is therefore the same pointwise before and after applying $A$. Integrating proves
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Area}(A S)=\operatorname{Area}(S).}
$$
This includes <orthogonal transformations> with <determinant> $-1$: reversal of <orientation> does not affect the <Euclidean norm> in the <surface area> formula.

Let $B=A^TA$. It is a <symmetric matrix>, and $\mathbf v^TB\mathbf v=\|A\mathbf v\|^2\geq0$. By the <spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices>, choose an <orthonormal basis> $\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\mathbf v_3$ of <eigenvectors> of $B$, with nonnegative <eigenvalues> $\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3$.

For each $i\ne j$, take the unit <parallelogram> parametrized by $s\mathbf v_i+t\mathbf v_j$, $0\leq s,t\leq1$. Its <surface area> is $1$. Moreover,
$$
A\mathbf v_i\cdot A\mathbf v_j=\mathbf v_i^TB\mathbf v_j=0,\qquad
\|A\mathbf v_i\|^2=\lambda_i.
$$
The image <parallelogram> consequently has <area> $\sqrt{\lambda_i\lambda_j}$. By the assumption this equals $1$ for all three pairs, giving
$$
\lambda_1\lambda_2=\lambda_1\lambda_3=\lambda_2\lambda_3=1.
$$
No <eigenvalue> can be zero. Dividing the <equations> and using positivity yields $\lambda_1=\lambda_2=\lambda_3=1$. Hence
$$
\boxed{A^TA=I,\quad\text{so }A\text{ is orthogonal}.}
$$
Thus testing all surfaces is stronger than necessary: the three unit <parallelograms> in these principal directions already determine the result. This is the three-dimensional <characterization of linear surface-area isometries>.

Write $q=|\dot{\mathbf r}|=\sqrt{\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2}$. Differentiating the <angular momentum> and applying <Newton's second law> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot{\mathbf L}
&=m\dot{\mathbf r}\times\dot{\mathbf r}+m\mathbf r\times\ddot{\mathbf r}
=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F\\
&=\mathbf r\times(-\alpha q\dot{\mathbf r})
=-\frac\alpha m q\mathbf L.
\end{aligned}
$$
The <central force> contributes no <torque>, because it is parallel to $\mathbf r$. The <angular momentum> direction is fixed whenever it is nonzero; the motion is consequently planar. In the <plane>'s <polar coordinates>, $\mathbf L=mh\mathbf e_z$, so
$$
\boxed{\dot h=-\frac\alpha m\sqrt{\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2}\,r^2\dot\theta.}
$$
For the <mechanical energy>, use the <chain rule>, <Newton's second law>, and $V'(r)=-F_c(r)$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot E
&=m\dot{\mathbf r}\cdot\ddot{\mathbf r}+V'(r)\dot r\\
&=F_c(r)\dot r-\alpha q|\dot{\mathbf r}|^2+V'(r)\dot r
=-\alpha q^3.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\dot E=-\alpha(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2)^{3/2}.}
$$
For a trajectory on a time interval, both $h$ and $E$ are each a <constant of motion> precisely when $\alpha=0$ or the <velocity> vanishes throughout that interval. Under the stated strictly attractive <central force> at a regular <point> $r>0$, a permanently stationary trajectory is impossible by <Newton's second law>. **For the stated attractive motion, both are conserved if and only if $\alpha=0$.** An isolated instant of zero <speed> only makes the <derivatives> vanish at that instant. A radial trajectory has $h=0$ even with <quadratic drag>, but its <mechanical energy> still decreases whenever it moves.

With $\alpha=0$, <4/a> gives constant $h$, and the <polar coordinates> <basis> satisfies $\dot{\mathbf e}_r=\dot\theta\mathbf e_\theta$, $\mathbf e_r\times\mathbf e_z=-\mathbf e_\theta$. Also $\ddot{\mathbf r}=(F_c/m)\mathbf e_r$. Differentiating the proposed <Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector> therefore gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot{\mathbf A}
&=m^2h\ddot{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf e_z-mk\dot{\mathbf e}_r\\
&=-mhF_c(r)\mathbf e_\theta-mk\dot\theta\mathbf e_\theta\\
&=-mh\left(F_c(r)+\frac{k}{r^2}\right)\mathbf e_\theta.
\end{aligned}
$$
For this to vanish for arbitrary $h$, including nonzero $h$, the <central force> must satisfy
$$
\boxed{F_c(r)=-\frac{k}{r^2},\qquad V(r)=-\frac{k}{r}+\text{constant}.}
$$
Attraction requires $k>0$. The condition “for any value of $h$” matters: with $h=0$ alone, the <Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector> is constant on each radial ray for any <central force>, so that special case would not determine the force law.

Assume $h\ne0$. The <velocity in polar coordinates> is $\dot{\mathbf r}=\dot r\mathbf e_r+r\dot\theta\mathbf e_\theta$. Hence
$$
(\dot{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf e_z)\cdot\mathbf r=r^2\dot\theta=h.

Taking the <dot product> of the conserved <Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector> with $\mathbf r$ gives
$$
Ar\cos\gamma=m^2h^2-mkr.
$$
Consequently the <polar equation of a conic> is
$$
\boxed{r(\gamma)=\frac{m^2h^2}{mk+A\cos\gamma}=\frac{mh^2/k}{1+(A/(mk))\cos\gamma}.}
$$
Only <angles> for which the <denominator> is positive describe the physical trajectory. Its <eccentricity> is $A/(mk)$, giving an <ellipse>, <parabola>, or <hyperbola> when this ratio is respectively less than, equal to, or greater than $1$.

If $A=0$, the <angle> $\gamma$ is undefined, but the same <dot product> identity gives the <circular orbit> $\boxed{r=mh^2/k}$. If $h=0$, the trajectory is radial and the displayed division degenerates; for $k>0$, $\mathbf A=-mk\mathbf e_r$ and $\gamma=\pi$ on a fixed ray, so the identity does not determine $r$ as a function of a varying <angle>.

\ddot{\mathbf r}=(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2)\mathbf e_r+(r\ddot\theta+2\dot r\dot\theta)\mathbf e_\theta+\ddot z\mathbf e_z.

$$
\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2+H'(H'\ddot r+H''\dot r^2)=-gH'.

Using $\dot\theta=h/r^2$ yields
$$
\boxed{[1+(H')^2]\ddot r+H'H''\dot r^2-\frac{h^2}{r^3}=-gH'.}

S(r)=2gH(r)+\frac{b^2v^2}{r^2},\qquad B=S(b)=2gH(b)+v^2.

It satisfies

S''(r)=2gH''(r)+\frac{6b^2v^2}{r^4}>0,
$$
so it is a <strictly convex function>. Also $S(r)\to\infty$ as $r\to\infty$. To justify the other endpoint carefully, <convexity> gives $H(r)\geq H(b)+H'(b)(r-b)$ for $0<r<b$. This supplies a finite lower bound near $0$, and the positive $r^{-2}$ term consequently forces $S(r)\to\infty$ there.

Thus $S$ has a unique <minimum> at some $r_m>0$. Its <derivative> is strictly increasing and zero there, so $S$ decreases to $r_m$ and increases afterwards. Its <sublevel set> $S(r)\leq B$ is a single interval $[b_1,b_2]$, with $0<b_1\leq b\leq b_2<\infty$. If $B>S(r_m)$, the two boundary <equations> $S(b_i)=B$ have distinct solutions; if $B=S(r_m)$, both boundaries are $r_m$. The <conservation of energy> identity therefore proves
$$
\boxed{b_1\leq r(t)\leq b_2.}
$$
The two <radii> coincide exactly when $b=r_m$, equivalently $S'(b)=0$. This gives
$$
\boxed{b_1=b_2=b\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v^2=gbH'(b).}
$$
The <equation> in <5/b> then has the constant solution $r=b$, with $\dot\theta=\pm v/b$: this is a <circular orbit>.

**A positive inner bound requires $v>0$.** If zero launch <speed> is allowed, $h=0$ and the displayed <inequality> only gives $r\leq b$ because $H$ is strictly increasing; one may take the limiting inner bound $b_1=0$. For example, $H(r)=r^2$ satisfies all the stated hypotheses but permits inward motion from rest all the way towards the excluded axis. The two-positive-turning-radii argument therefore uses the natural interpretation of “launched with <speed> $v$” as $v>0$.

The separate <Coriolis force> and <centrifugal force> terms in the stated <equation> account for the motion of the <basis> relative to an inertial observer.

Continue with $f=p\sin\phi$ and $q=p\cos\phi$. The <tangent vector> to the hoop is $\mathbf r_\phi=f'\mathbf e_1-q'\mathbf e_3$. Smooth contact means that the hoop's <normal force> has zero <dot product> with this <tangent vector>. The remaining applied force is $-mg\mathbf e_3$, whose <dot product> with $\mathbf r_\phi$ is $mgq'$.

Since $\boldsymbol\omega=\omega\mathbf e_3$, the <Coriolis force> is perpendicular to the hoop's <plane> and hence to $\mathbf r_\phi$. Also
$$
\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)=-\omega^2f\mathbf e_1.
$$
Take the <dot product> of the rotating-frame <Newton's second law> with $\mathbf r_\phi$, substitute <6/a>, and divide by $m$:
$$
(f'^2+q'^2)\ddot\phi+(f'f''+q'q'')\dot\phi^2-\omega^2ff'=gq'.
$$
Define
$$
K=f'^2+q'^2,\qquad J=-\frac12\omega^2f^2-gq.
$$
Then $K'/2=f'f''+q'q''$ and $J'=-\omega^2ff'-gq'$, so
$$
\boxed{K(\phi)\ddot\phi+\tfrac12K'(\phi)\dot\phi^2+J'(\phi)=0.}
$$
Expanding the squares makes the cancellation of the mixed terms explicit:
$$
\boxed{K(\phi)=(p'\sin\phi+p\cos\phi)^2+(p'\cos\phi-p\sin\phi)^2=(p')^2+p^2>0.}
$$
The strict <inequality> follows from $p>0$. Here $J$ is the <rotating-frame effective potential> per unit <mass>. Multiplication of the <equation> by $\dot\phi$ gives the useful first <integral> $\tfrac12K(\phi)\dot\phi^2+J(\phi)=\text{constant}$.

An <equilibrium point> has $\dot\phi=0$ and $\boxed{J'(\phi_0)=0}$. Since $K(\phi_0)>0$, the linearized displacement $\psi=\phi-\phi_0$ satisfies
$$
K(\phi_0)\ddot\psi+J''(\phi_0)\psi=0.
$$
Thus $J''(\phi_0)>0$ gives a <stable equilibrium>, while $J''(\phi_0)<0$ gives an unstable <equilibrium point>. More generally, a <strict local minimum> of $J$ is sufficient for stability by the conserved first <integral> in <6/b>. **When $J''(\phi_0)=0$, the second-<derivative> test alone is inconclusive**, and the local shape of $J$ must be examined.

For the specified <ellipse>, put $D=1+e\cos\phi>0$. Direct <differentiation> gives
$$
f'=\frac{\cos\phi+e}{D^2},\qquad q'=-\frac{\sin\phi}{D^2}.
$$
Consequently, with $\eta=g/\omega^2$ and $\omega\ne0$,
$$
J'(\phi)=\frac{\omega^2\sin\phi}{D^3}\left[\eta(1+e\cos\phi)-(\cos\phi+e)\right].
$$
Two <equilibria> always occur at $\boxed{\phi=0,\pi\pmod{2\pi}}$. Any further <equilibria> must satisfy
$$
\boxed{\cos\phi=\frac{\eta-e}{1-e\eta}.}
$$
To decide exactly when this is possible, view the <equation> as $\eta=(c+e)/(1+ec)$ with $c=\cos\phi$. Its <derivative> $(1-e^2)/(1+ec)^2$ is positive on $[-1,1]$, and it takes endpoint values $-1$ and $1$. Since $g>0$ implies $\eta>0$, the complete list is:

* If $0<\eta<1$, there are **four distinct <equilibria> modulo $2\pi$**: $0$, $\pi$, and $\displaystyle\pm\arccos\frac{\eta-e}{1-e\eta}$. For $0<\eta<e$ the additional positive <angle> lies in $(\pi/2,\pi)$; at $\eta=e$ the additional pair is $\pm\pi/2$; for $e<\eta<1$ it lies in $(0,\pi/2)$.
* If $\eta=1$, the additional pair merges with $\phi=0$, leaving **two distinct <equilibria>**, $0$ and $\pi$.
* If $\eta>1$, there are **only the two <equilibria>** $0$ and $\pi$. In particular, $\eta=1/e$ does not introduce a missing case: the undivided bracket is then the positive constant $1/e-e$, so there is no extra root.

These are positions of the bead at rest relative to the rotating hoop. No stability classification of the particular <ellipse> is needed.

To find a <critical point>, apply <multivariable Newton iteration> to the <gradient>, rather than to the scalar value $F$. The <gradient> and <Hessian matrix> are
$$
\nabla F=\begin{pmatrix}2x+2y\\2x-2y+3y^2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\nabla^2F=\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\2&-2+6y\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Write the correction as $\mathbf s_0=\mathbf x_1-\mathbf x_0$. At $\mathbf x_0=(-1,-1)$, <Newton's method in optimization> requires
$$
\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\2&-8\end{pmatrix}\mathbf s_0=-\begin{pmatrix}-4\\3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}4\\-3\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Subtracting the second <equation> from the first gives $10s_y=7$, so $s_y=7/10$ and $s_x=13/10$. Hence
$$
\boxed{(x_1,y_1)=\left(\frac3{10},-\frac3{10}\right).}
$$
The <gradient> there is
$$
\nabla F(3/10,-3/10)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\147/100\end{pmatrix}\ne\mathbf0.
$$
**A <critical point> has not been found after one iteration.**

At the alternative starting <point> $(0,2/3)$, the <Hessian matrix> and <gradient> are
$$
\nabla^2F=\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\nabla F=\begin{pmatrix}4/3\\0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The correction would need to satisfy $2s_x+2s_y=-4/3$ and $2s_x+2s_y=0$ simultaneously. This <linear system> is inconsistent. **The standard iteration cannot take even its first step**: the <Hessian matrix> is a <singular matrix>, and the required correction does not exist. This is a <Newton correction compatibility condition> failure, not merely an ambiguity between several corrections.
