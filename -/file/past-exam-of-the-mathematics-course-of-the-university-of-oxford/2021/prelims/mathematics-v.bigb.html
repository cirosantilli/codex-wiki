Solutions to all six questions and all optional parts. The surface-<volume> formula in Question 2(b) requires an appropriate enclosing surface and orientation. The symmetry claim in Question 6(a), as printed, also needs the coefficient $b(x,t)$ to be even in $x$; a counterexample to the unrestricted claim is given.

Use the <change of variables> $u=x^2-y^2$, $v=xy$. Its <Jacobian determinant> in the first quadrant is $2(x^2+y^2)>0$, and the inverse is uniquely determined by
$$
x^2=\frac{\sqrt{u^2+4v^2}+u}{2},\qquad
y^2=\frac{\sqrt{u^2+4v^2}-u}{2}.
$$
Both are positive for $v>0$, so the transformation is one-to-one on the region. It becomes the <rectangle> $-2\leq u\leq4$, $1\leq v\leq3$. The <integrand> is $uv$, and $x^2+y^2=\sqrt{u^2+4v^2}$. Thus
$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_A(x^3y-xy^3)\,dx\,dy
&=\int_1^3\int_{-2}^4\frac{uv}{2\sqrt{u^2+4v^2}}\,du\,dv\\
&=\int_1^3v\left(\sqrt{v^2+4}-\sqrt{v^2+1}\right)\,dv\\
&=\boxed{\frac{13\sqrt{13}-10\sqrt{10}-5\sqrt5+2\sqrt2}{3}.}
\end{aligned}
$$

<Green's theorem> for a positively oriented simple closed planar curve states
$$
\oint_C(P\,dx+Q\,dy)=\iint_D(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\,dy.
$$
Here $Q_x-P_y=1-(-1)=2$. The <trapezium> has parallel sides of lengths two and one, separated by height one, so its <area> is $3/2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=2\cdot\frac32=3.}
$$
**Every congruent <trapezium> gives the same answer when its boundary is traversed anticlockwise**, since the scalar <curl> is constant and <geometric congruence> preserves <area>. Reversing the traversal would instead reverse the sign.

A <parametrization> of the cylindrical patch is
$$
\mathbf r(x,\theta)=(x,2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta),\qquad
0<\theta<\pi/2,\quad0<x<2\cos\theta.
$$
The last range uses the planes $x=0$ and $x=y$. The <surface area element> is $|\mathbf r_x\times\mathbf r_\theta|\,dx\,d\theta=2\,dx\,d\theta$. Hence
$$
\boxed{A=\int_0^{\pi/2}\int_0^{2\cos\theta}2\,dx\,d\theta=4.}
$$
Including or excluding the boundary curves does not change the <surface area>.

For a bounded region $R\subseteq\mathbb R^3$ with piecewise smooth boundary and a <continuously differentiable vector field> $\mathbf F$ on a neighborhood of its closure, the <divergence theorem> says
$$
\boxed{\iiint_R\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV=\iint_{\partial R}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,dS,}
$$
where $\mathbf n$ is the outward <unit normal vector>. The left side integrates <divergence> over the <volume>; the right side is the outward <flux>.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Apply the <divergence theorem> to $\mathbf F=u\nabla v-v\nabla u$. The <product rule> gives
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=u\nabla^2v-v\nabla^2u,
$$
because the two <gradient> <inner products> cancel. Also $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=u\partial_nv-v\partial_nu$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\iiint_R(u\nabla^2v-v\nabla^2u)\,dV
=\iint_{\partial R}(u\partial_nv-v\partial_nu)\,dS.}
$$
This is <Green's second identity>, under the usual regularity assumptions permitting these derivatives and boundary <integrals>.

{parent=a}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

Take $v=|\mathbf x|^2$ in <Green's second identity>. Since $\nabla^2u=0$, $\nabla^2v=6$, $v=1$ on the unit <sphere>, and $\partial_nv=2$ there,
$$
6\iiint_Bu\,dV=2\iint_{\partial B}u\,dS-\iint_{\partial B}\partial_nu\,dS.
$$
The final term vanishes by the <divergence theorem>, because it equals $\iiint_B\nabla^2u\,dV=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\iiint_Bu\,dV=\frac13\iint_{\partial B}u\,dS.}
$$
We assume sufficient boundary regularity for the stated boundary values and <integrals>, or use the corresponding trace/<limit> formulation. This is the equality of <volume> and surface averages for a <harmonic function>, since the <unit ball> and <sphere> have <volume> $4\pi/3$ and <area> $4\pi$.

Parametrize the <surface of revolution> by
$$
\mathbf X(t,\theta)=(\cos t\cos\theta,\cos t\sin\theta,f(t)),\quad
-\pi/2\leq t\leq\pi/2,\quad0\leq\theta<2\pi.
$$
An oriented <vector surface area element> is
$$
\mathbf X_\theta\times\mathbf X_t
=(\cos t f'(t)\cos\theta,\cos t f'(t)\sin\theta,\sin t\cos t).
$$
Use the <vector field> $\mathbf F=(x/2,y/2,0)$, whose <divergence> is one. If this <parametrization> has the outward orientation on an enclosing surface, the <divergence theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{V=\pi\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos^2t\,f'(t)\,dt
=\pi\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}f(t)\sin2t\,dt.}
$$
The second equality is <integration by parts>, using the endpoint values. For example this outward convention applies when the positive-$t$ branch lies above the negative-$t$ branch, $f(t)>f(-t)$ for $0<t<\pi/2$.

The stated $C^1$ regularity and endpoint conditions alone do not guarantee an embedded enclosing surface: branches can cross or coincide. Without an appropriate orientation assumption the <flux> formula is an algebraic signed <volume>. If “inside” is defined as the region between the two graph branches for each radius, its nonnegative geometric <volume> is instead
$$
V=2\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin t\cos t\,|f(t)-f(-t)|\,dt.
$$
This explicitly handles a reversal or change in the order of the two branches.

For $f(t)=\sin2t$, the positive-$t$ branch is above the negative one, so the orientation in part <2/b/i> is outward. Consequently
$$
\boxed{V=\pi\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\sin^22t\,dt=\frac{\pi^2}{2}.}
$$
The <integral> follows from $\sin^22t=(1-\cos4t)/2$.

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

If two solutions exist, their difference $w$ satisfies $\nabla^2w-w=0$ inside $B$ and $w=0$ on the boundary. Suppose $w$ has a positive value. <Continuity> on the closed ball gives a positive <maximum> at an interior point, where the <Hessian matrix> is negative semidefinite and hence $\nabla^2w\leq0$. But the equation gives $\nabla^2w=w>0$, a contradiction. A negative <minimum> similarly contradicts $\nabla^2w=w<0$.

Thus $\boxed{w=0}$, proving uniqueness by the <maximum principle for the modified Helmholtz equation>. Here a classical solution is understood to be $C^2$ inside and continuous up to the boundary; mere first differentiability would not define the displayed <Laplacian> classically. With enough boundary regularity, the alternative <Green's first identity> proof is $0=\int_Bw(\nabla^2w-w)=-\int_B(|\nabla w|^2+w^2)$.

The <curl> is $\nabla\times\mathbf F=(x,y,-2z)$. For the upward orientation of the paraboloid, the <vector surface area element> is $(2x,2y,1)\,dx\,dy$. Therefore <Stokes' theorem> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
&=\iint_{x,y\geq0,\ x^2+y^2\leq4}\{2x^2+2y^2-2(4-x^2-y^2)\}\,dx\,dy\\
&=\int_0^{\pi/2}\int_0^2(4r^2-8)r\,dr\,d\theta
=\boxed{0}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The upward normal gives the prescribed anticlockwise boundary orientation viewed from above.

Take the boundary pieces in the order
$$
(2,0,0)\longrightarrow(0,2,0)\longrightarrow(0,0,4)\longrightarrow(2,0,0).
$$
On the circular arc $C_1$, use $(x,y,z)=(2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,0)$ for $0\leq\theta\leq\pi/2$. Then $\mathbf F=(0,0,1)$ and $dz=0$, so its <line integral> is zero.

On $C_2$, use $x=0$, $z=4-y^2$, with $y$ decreasing from two to zero. Since $dx=0$ and $F_y=0$, $\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=dz$, giving four. On $C_3$, use $y=0$, $z=4-x^2$, with $x$ increasing from zero to two. Again $\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=dz$, giving minus four. The total is $\boxed{0+4-4=0}$, agreeing with <Stokes' theorem>.

Write $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}>0$, so $\mathbf F=(f(z)x/r,f(z)y/r,0)$. The Cartesian <curl> is
$$
\nabla\times\mathbf F
=\left(-f'(z)\frac yr,\ f'(z)\frac xr,\
\partial_x\frac{f(z)y}r-\partial_y\frac{f(z)x}r\right).
$$
The last component is zero because both differentiated terms are $-f(z)xy/r^3$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf F=f'(z)(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0)=f'(z)\mathbf e_\theta.}
$$
The condition $r>0$ excludes the cylindrical-axis coordinate singularity.

The graph repeats every two units: it rises linearly from the right-hand <limit> $-1$ at an <odd integer> to value one at the next <even integer>, then falls linearly to value zero at the next <odd integer>. The defined value at each <odd integer> is zero, while its right-hand <limit> is minus one.

The <Dirichlet convergence theorem for Fourier series> gives the midpoint of the two <one-sided limits> at a jump. Hence, on the requested <interval>, the Fourier sum differs from $f$ precisely at
$$
\boxed{x\in\{-3,-1,1,3\},\qquad\text{Fourier sum}=-\frac12\ne f(x)=0.}
$$
At every other point it equals $f(x)$; in particular it equals one at the continuous corners with <even integer> coordinates.

= Solution

For period two, write $f\sim a_0/2+\sum_{n\geq1}[a_n\cos(n\pi x)+b_n\sin(n\pi x)]$. Direct integration gives $a_0=1/2$. For $0<x<1$, $f(x)+f(-x)=2-3x$ and $f(x)-f(-x)=x$, so
$$
a_n=\int_0^1(2-3x)\cos(n\pi x)\,dx=\frac{3(1-(-1)^n)}{n^2\pi^2},\qquad
b_n=\int_0^1x\sin(n\pi x)\,dx=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi}.
$$
Thus the <Fourier series> is
$$
\boxed{\frac14+\sum_{n=1}^\infty\left[
\frac{3(1-(-1)^n)}{n^2\pi^2}\cos(n\pi x)+\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi}\sin(n\pi x)\right].}
$$
It converges to the values specified in part <4/a>. Changing the assigned values at the jumps would not change these <integral> coefficients.

{parent=4}

= Solution
{parent=c}

At $x=0$, the Fourier sum is $f(0)=1$, and only the odd cosine coefficients contribute. Therefore $1=1/4+(6/\pi^2)\sum_{k\geq0}(2k+1)^{-2}$, giving
$$
\boxed{\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac1{(2k+1)^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{8}.}
$$
At $x=1/2$, the sum is $f(1/2)=1/2$. All nonzero cosine terms vanish, and the sine terms have alternating signs over odd indices. Thus $1/2=1/4+(1/\pi)\sum_{k\geq0}(-1)^k/(2k+1)$, so
$$
\boxed{\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1}=\frac\pi4.}
$$
This is the <Leibniz formula for pi> obtained from the same <Fourier series>.

For $c>0$ and $L>0$, <D'Alembert's formula> for the <wave equation> on the full <real line> is
$$
y(x,t)=\frac{f(x-ct)+f(x+ct)}2+\frac1{2c}\int_{x-ct}^{x+ct}g(s)\,ds.
$$
For the specified initial data, define the cumulative initial-velocity <integral>
$$
A(s)=\begin{cases}
0,&s\leq-L,\\
(L^2-s^2)/2,&-L\leq s\leq0,\\
(L^2+s^2)/2,&0\leq s\leq L,\\
L^2,&s\geq L.
\end{cases}
$$
Then $\boxed{y(x,t)=[A(x+ct)-A(x-ct)]/(2c)}$. The <initial velocity> jumps at $\pm L$, so the resulting solution is interpreted piecewise classically, or as a weak wave-equation solution across the corresponding <characteristic curves>.

At $ct=L/2$, the <interval> of integration has half-width $L/2$. Evaluating the cumulative <integral> in part <5/a> gives the <even function>
$$
\boxed{y\!\left(x,\frac L{2c}\right)=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{x^2+L^2/4}{2c},&|x|\leq L/2,\\[4pt]
\dfrac{L^2-(|x|-L/2)^2}{4c},&L/2\leq|x|\leq3L/2,\\[4pt]
0,&|x|\geq3L/2.
\end{cases}}
$$
It has value $L^2/(8c)$ at zero and maxima $L^2/(4c)$ at $x=\pm L/2$, then falls to zero at the two wavefronts.

At $x=L/2$, the integration <interval> first moves through positive arguments, then crosses zero, and finally contains all of $[-L,L]$. The result is

\boxed{y\!\left(\frac L2,t\right)=\begin{cases}

The final plateau is the <integral> of the entire <initial velocity> profile divided by $2c$.

The <multivariable chain rule> gives $\partial_x=\partial_\xi+\partial_\eta$ and $\partial_t=\alpha\partial_\xi+\beta\partial_\eta$. Substitution into the <differential equation> yields
$$
\boxed{(\alpha^2+2B\alpha+C)y_{\xi\xi}
+2\{\alpha\beta+B(\alpha+\beta)+C\}y_{\xi\eta}
+(\beta^2+2B\beta+C)y_{\eta\eta}=0.}
$$
The <coordinate transformation> is invertible exactly when $\alpha\ne\beta$.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Eliminate the two pure second derivatives by taking $\alpha,\beta$ to be the <roots> of $z^2+2Bz+C=0$. They are distinct <real numbers> exactly when
$$
\boxed{B^2>C,\qquad\alpha=-B+\sqrt{B^2-C},\quad\beta=-B-\sqrt{B^2-C}.}
$$
Thus the required <function> is $H(B)=B^2$. The mixed coefficient becomes $4(C-B^2)\ne0$, so the transformed equation is $y_{\xi\eta}=0$. Integrating once in each coordinate gives
$$
\boxed{y(x,t)=F(x+\alpha t)+G(x+\beta t),}
$$
with arbitrary sufficiently smooth <functions> $F,G$. This is the characteristic form of a strictly <hyperbolic partial differential equation>.

For uniqueness, subtract two solutions and call their difference $w$. It has zero boundary and initial data, with $w_t=w_{xx}+aw_x+bw$. Its squared <L2 norm> obeys the <energy estimate>
$$
\frac12\frac d{dt}\int_{-1}^1w^2\,dx
=-\int_{-1}^1w_x^2\,dx+\frac a2[w^2]_{-1}^1+\int_{-1}^1bw^2\,dx\leq0.
$$
The boundary term vanishes and $b\leq0$. A nonnegative quantity starting at zero must stay zero, proving uniqueness in the usual classical or corresponding energy class.

The printed evenness claim for $a=0$ is **false for an arbitrary coefficient $b(x,t)\leq0$**. Take $T_0>0$ and $b(x,t)=-2-x$. The <strong maximum principle> makes the solution positive in the interior for $t>0$. If it were even, $T_t$ and $T_{xx}$ would both be even. Subtracting the equation at $x$ and $-x$ would give $0=[b(x,t)-b(-x,t)]T(x,t)=-2xT(x,t)$, impossible for $x\ne0$.

With the additional assumption $b(-x,t)=b(x,t)$, the intended argument is valid. For $a=0$, <reflection> $\widetilde T(x,t)=T(-x,t)$ solves the same equation with the same initial and boundary data. Uniqueness then proves $\boxed{T(-x,t)=T(x,t)}$. If $T_0\ne0$, initial and boundary values disagree at the two time-zero corners; the <initial condition> is understood in the interior or in the <L2 norm> <limit>.

With $x=2z-1$, the <chain rule> gives $T_{xx}=V_{zz}/4$. Thus
$$
V_t=\frac14V_{zz},\quad0<z<1,\qquad V(0,t)=V(1,t)=0,\qquad V(z,0)=T_0.
$$
<Separation of variables> gives the <eigenfunctions> $\sin(n\pi z)$ with time factors $e^{-n^2\pi^2t/4}$. The <Fourier sine coefficients> of the initial constant are
$$
b_n=2\int_0^1T_0\sin(n\pi z)\,dz=\frac{2T_0(1-(-1)^n)}{n\pi}.
$$
Only odd modes survive, so
$$
\boxed{V(z,t)=\frac{4T_0}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{\sin((2k+1)\pi z)}{2k+1}e^{-(2k+1)^2\pi^2t/4}.}
$$
Returning to $x$, $\sin((2k+1)\pi(x+1)/2)=(-1)^k\cos((2k+1)\pi x/2)$, hence
$$
\boxed{T(x,t)=\frac{4T_0}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1}\cos\!\left(\frac{(2k+1)\pi x}{2}\right)e^{-(2k+1)^2\pi^2t/4}.}
$$
For every $t>0$ the exponential coefficients give <uniform convergence> of the differentiated series on later-time compact sets, verifying the PDE and <boundary conditions>. As $t\downarrow0$, the solution tends to $T_0$ at every interior point and in <L2 norm>, while remaining zero at the endpoints. The expression is visibly even in $x$, consistent with the corrected symmetry argument for $b=0$.
