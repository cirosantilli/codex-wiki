The <Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem> states that every <bounded sequence> of <real numbers> has a <convergent subsequence>. It is enough to select infinitely many terms of the <tangent> <sequence> that stay uniformly bounded.

For each positive <integer> $k$, choose $n_k=\lfloor k\pi+1/2\rfloor$, using the <floor function>. Then $|n_k-k\pi|\leq1/2$. Moreover $n_{k+1}-n_k\geq\pi-1>0$, so these are strictly increasing indices. By the period and <monotonicity> of the <tangent> on $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$,
$$
|\tan n_k|=|\tan(n_k-k\pi)|\leq\tan(1/2).
$$
Thus $(\tan n_k)$ is a <bounded subsequence>. Applying the <Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem> to it gives a <convergent subsequence>, which is also a <subsequence> of the original <sequence>. **Such a <convergent subsequence> therefore exists.**

If $L<1$, set $\varepsilon=(1-L)/2>0$. The definition of <limit of a sequence> gives $a_n<L+\varepsilon=(1+L)/2<1$ for all sufficiently large $n$, contradicting $a_n\geq1$. Hence $\boxed{L\geq1}$.

Factor the difference of cubes. Since both <cube roots> are at least one,
$$
\left|a_n^{1/3}-L^{1/3}\right|
=\frac{|a_n-L|}{a_n^{2/3}+a_n^{1/3}L^{1/3}+L^{2/3}}
\leq\frac{|a_n-L|}{3}.
$$
Given $\varepsilon>0$, the definition of <limit of a sequence> supplies $N$ with $|a_n-L|<3\varepsilon$ for $n\geq N$. The displayed <inequality> then gives $|a_n^{1/3}-L^{1/3}|<\varepsilon$, proving directly that $\boxed{a_n^{1/3}\to L^{1/3}}$.

Let $\varepsilon>0$. By the definition of <Cauchy sequence>, choose an <integer> $K\geq1$ such that $|x_j-x_k|<\varepsilon/2$ whenever $j,k\geq K$. Apply the given <inequality> with $N=K+1$. It supplies $n>K+1$ for which
$$
0<x_n\leq\frac12\min_{k<K+1}x_k\leq\frac{x_K}{2}.
$$
Both $n$ and $K$ are in the <Cauchy sequence> estimate, so
$$
\frac{x_K}{2}\leq x_K-x_n<\frac\varepsilon2,
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad x_n<\frac\varepsilon2.
$$
For every $m\geq K$ the <triangle inequality> gives
$$
0<x_m\leq|x_m-x_n|+x_n<\varepsilon.
$$
This is the definition of <convergence> to zero: $\boxed{x_m\to0}$. The proof uses only the <Cauchy sequence> estimate, positivity, and the stipulated <inequality>; it does not assume the general <completeness of the real numbers> theorem for <Cauchy sequences>. Taking $N=K+1\geq2$ also avoids the empty initial <set> in a literal reading of the condition for very small $N$.

A <series> $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n$ **converges** if its <partial sums> $s_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n$ have a finite <limit of a sequence> $S$ as $N\to\infty$; its sum is then $S$. It has **<absolute convergence>** if the <series> $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_n|$ converges, where $|a_n|$ is the <absolute value> of the term.

{parent=b}
{c}

Assume first that the original nonnegative <series> converges to $S$. Its <partial sums> $s_m$ increase to $S$. Write $t_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{g(n)}$ for the <partial sums> of the <rearrangement of a series>. Since $g$ is an <injection>, the indices $g(1),\ldots,g(N)$ are distinct, so
$$
0\leq t_N\leq s_{\max\{g(1),\ldots,g(N)\}}\leq S.
$$
Thus $(t_N)$ is an increasing <bounded sequence>, and the <bounded monotone sequence theorem> gives $t_N\to T\leq S$.

For any fixed $m$, <surjectivity> of $g$ gives
$$
N_m=\max\{g^{-1}(1),\ldots,g^{-1}(m)\}.
$$
For $N\geq N_m$, nonnegativity implies $t_N\geq s_m$. Taking <limits of sequences> first in $N$ and then in $m$ gives $T\geq S$. Hence $T=S$.

Conversely, $g^{-1}$ is a <bijection>, and the original <series> is the <rearrangement of a series> of $\sum b_n$ defined by $g^{-1}$. The same argument proves its <convergence> whenever $\sum b_n$ converges. **Both <series> converge together, and their sums are equal.**

A <function> $f:(a,b)\to\mathbb R$ is **<differentiable> on $(a,b)$** if, for every $x\in(a,b)$, the finite <limit of a function>
$$
\boxed{f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}h}
$$
exists. Here $h\ne0$ and is sufficiently small that $x+h\in(a,b)$. This <limit of a function> is the <derivative> of $f$ at $x$.

The **<mean value theorem>** states: if a real-valued <function> $f$ is <continuous> on a closed <interval> $[u,v]$, where $u<v$, and <differentiable> on $(u,v)$, then there is $c\in(u,v)$ with
$$
\boxed{f(v)-f(u)=f'(c)(v-u).}
$$

Write the symmetric <difference quotient> as the average of two ordinary <difference quotients>:
$$
\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}
=\frac12\left(\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}h+\frac{f(x-h)-f(x)}{-h}\right).
$$
If $f$ is <differentiable> at $x$, both terms tend to the <derivative> $f'(x)$. Hence the <symmetric derivative> satisfies $\boxed{f^s(x)=f'(x)}$.

The <absolute value function> $f(x)=|x|$ is <continuous>, and $f^s(0)=0$ because $|h|=|-h|$. Its right <one-sided derivative> at zero is $1$ and its left <one-sided derivative> is $-1$, so it is not <differentiable> at zero. Away from zero it is <differentiable>, hence has a <symmetric derivative> there as well. This supplies an example meeting the hypotheses on, for example, $(-1,1)$.

We prove the <symmetric derivative criterion for monotonicity> using an <antiderivative>. Fix $a<u<v<b$ and define
$$
F(t)=\int_u^t f(s)\,ds.
$$
The <fundamental theorem of calculus> gives $F'=f$ because $f$ is <continuous>. For each interior $x$,
$$
F(x+h)-2F(x)+F(x-h)=\int_0^h\bigl(f(x+t)-f(x-t)\bigr)\,dt.
$$
Since $f^s(x)>0$, the definition of <symmetric derivative> gives $\delta>0$ such that $f(x+t)-f(x-t)>t f^s(x)$ for $0<t<\delta$. Therefore, for $0<h<\delta$,
$$
F(x+h)-2F(x)+F(x-h)>\frac12 f^s(x)h^2>0.
$$

We next show that $F$ is a <convex function>. On any closed <interval> $[r,s]\subset(a,b)$ let $\ell$ be the <affine function> joining $(r,F(r))$ to $(s,F(s))$. If $F-\ell$ were positive anywhere, the <extreme value theorem> would give a positive <maximum> at an interior point $x$. For every sufficiently small $h>0$, that <maximum> would imply
$$
(F-\ell)(x+h)-2(F-\ell)(x)+(F-\ell)(x-h)\leq0.
$$
The <affine function> contributes zero to this symmetric difference, contradicting the strict positive <inequality> above. Thus $F\leq\ell$ on every such <interval>, exactly <convexity>.

For a <differentiable> <convex function>, secant slopes bound endpoint <derivatives>. Explicitly, the chord <inequality> at $u+h$ and at $v-h$, followed by $h\downarrow0$, gives
$$
f(u)=F'(u)\leq\frac{F(v)-F(u)}{v-u}\leq F'(v)=f(v).
$$
So $f$ is <nondecreasing>. If $f(u)=f(v)$ for some $u<v$, <monotonicity> would make $f$ constant on $[u,v]$, forcing $f^s(x)=0$ for $u<x<v$, a contradiction. Consequently $\boxed{f(u)<f(v)\text{ for every }u<v}$: $f$ is <strictly increasing>. This proof requires no <continuity> of $f^s$ and never applies the ordinary <mean value theorem> to a merely symmetrically <differentiable> <function>.

The construction is the partial middle-third construction of the <Cantor function>. After $n$ rounds of assigning middle thirds, every remaining open <interval> has endpoint values
$$
\frac{k}{2^n},\quad\frac{k+1}{2^n}
$$
for some $k=0,\ldots,2^n-1$, and every such consecutive pair occurs. This follows by <mathematical induction>. Initially the endpoint values are $0,1$. An <interval> with those consecutive endpoint values receives the value
$$
\frac12\left(\frac{k}{2^n}+\frac{k+1}{2^n}\right)=\frac{2k+1}{2^{n+1}}
$$
on its closed middle third. Its two remaining open <intervals> therefore have consecutive endpoint values at denominator $2^{n+1}$. All previously assigned endpoint values persist. Hence all even numerators at the next denominator were already present, and all odd numerators have just been assigned. Including the initial values zero and one proves
$$
\boxed{\{k/2^n:0\leq k\leq2^n\}\subseteq f(\mathbb R\setminus C)\quad(n\geq0).}
$$
These are precisely the <dyadic rational> values between zero and one.

All these endpoints are in the domain of the partial <function>: $L_m$ is the right endpoint of a middle third assigned at round $2m$, and $R_m$ is the left endpoint of a middle third assigned at round $2m-1$.

\boxed{\lim_{\substack{t\to1/4\\t\notin C}}f(t)=\frac13.}

**<Rolle's theorem>** states that a <continuous function> $g:[u,v]\to\mathbb R$ with $u<v$, <differentiable> on $(u,v)$, and satisfying $g(u)=g(v)$ has $g'(c)=0$ for some $c\in(u,v)$.

Fix $x>0$ and set $r=(\sqrt{1+x}-1)/x$. The <function>
$$
g(t)=\sqrt{1+t}-1-rt,\qquad0\leq t\leq x,
$$
is <continuous> and <differentiable> as required, and $g(0)=g(x)=0$. By <Rolle's theorem>, some $c\in(0,x)$ satisfies
$$
0=g'(c)=\frac1{2\sqrt{1+c}}-r.
$$
Since $c>0$, this gives $r<1/2$. Multiplying by $x>0$ and adding one proves the strict <square root> bound
$$
\boxed{\sqrt{1+x}<1+x/2\qquad(x>0).}
$$

Apply <Rolle's theorem> on each $[x_i,x_{i+1}]$. This gives $n$ ordered zeros of the <derivative> $f'$ strictly between the original $n+1$ zeros of $f$. Apply <Rolle's theorem> to $f'$ between each consecutive pair of its zeros to obtain $n-1$ ordered zeros of $f''$. Continue in this way.

To justify the iteration, for $j<n$, the <derivative> $f^{(j)}$ is <differentiable> throughout $(a,b)$ and hence <continuous> there by <differentiability implies continuity>. Every new closed <interval> lies strictly inside $(a,b)$, so <Rolle's theorem> applies at each step. After $j$ steps there are $n+1-j$ zeros of $f^{(j)}$. At $j=n$ this gives
$$
\boxed{\exists c\in(a,b):\quad f^{(n)}(c)=0.}
$$
This is the distinct-zero case of the <Rolle root count with multiplicities>.

Use the endpoint <continuity> of $f$ from part <6/b>, and interpret the displayed endpoint <derivatives> as one-sided endpoint values. Starting with $f(a)=f(b)=0$, <Rolle's theorem> gives $c_1\in(a,b)$ with $f'(c_1)=0$. Since also $f'(b)=0$, apply <Rolle's theorem> to $f'$ on $[c_1,b]$ to obtain $c_2\in(c_1,b)$ with $f''(c_2)=0$. Repeating gives
$$
a<c_1<\cdots<c_{n+1}<b,\qquad f^{(j)}(c_j)=0\quad(1\leq j\leq n+1).
$$
For $j<n$, the lower <derivative> $f^{(j)}$ is <continuous> in the interior by <differentiability implies continuity>, and at $b$ by the existence of its next one-sided <derivative>. At the final step, the stipulated <continuity> of $f^{(n)}$ on $[a,b]$ and its <differentiability> on $(a,b)$ give exactly the hypotheses of <Rolle's theorem> on $[c_n,b]$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{f^{(n+1)}(c_{n+1})=0.}
$$
The endpoint regularity matters: merely assigning arbitrary values to $f$ at $a,b$ while prescribing only the interior <derivatives> would not justify the first application of <Rolle's theorem>.

The <extreme value theorem> gives finite <real numbers>
$$
\alpha=\min_{x\in[a,b]}f(x),\qquad\beta=\max_{x\in[a,b]}f(x),\qquad\alpha\leq\beta.
$$
Since these values are attained, the <set> of strict <upper bounds> in the definition of $M$ is precisely $(\beta,\infty)$, and the <set> of strict <lower bounds> in the definition of $m$ is precisely $(-\infty,\alpha)$. They are nonempty and bounded on the required sides. By the definitions of <infimum> and <supremum>,
$$
\boxed{M=\inf(\beta,\infty)=\beta,\qquad m=\sup(-\infty,\alpha)=\alpha,\qquad m\leq M.}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

**A <maximum> or <minimum> can occur at an endpoint.** The rule that a <derivative> vanishes at an extremum requires an interior extremum and <differentiability> there. The hypotheses give no two-sided <derivative> at the endpoints, and even an existing one-sided <derivative> need not vanish there.

For example, $f(x)=x$ on $[0,1]$ has $m=0$ at $x_m=0$ and $M=1$ at $x_M=1$, but $f'(x)=1$ at every interior point; its endpoint one-sided <derivatives> are also one. Its graph meets both horizontal bounding lines without being tangent to either. Thus the claimed conclusion about two zeros of the <derivative> fails.

There is a second logical issue: the argument never establishes two distinct extremizing points. For a <constant function>, the <maximum> and <minimum> coincide and may be represented by the same point. The <constant function> does have many <stationary points>, but that needs its own argument; it does not follow from counting the same selected point twice. In <Rolle's theorem>, equal endpoint values ensure an interior extremum when the <function> is nonconstant, which is the missing mechanism here.

Let $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ be a bounded <function>, with $a<b$. A <partition of an interval> is $\mathcal P=(a=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_N=b)$; its <mesh of a partition> is $\|\mathcal P\|=\max_i(x_i-x_{i-1})$. Choose arbitrary tags $\xi_i\in[x_{i-1},x_i]$. The corresponding <Riemann sum> is
$$
\sum(f;\mathcal P,\vec\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^N f(\xi_i)(x_i-x_{i-1}).
$$
The <Riemann sum convergence theorem> says that $f$ is <Riemann integrable> with <Riemann integral> $I$ if and only if, for every $\varepsilon>0$, some $\delta>0$ satisfies
$$
\boxed{\left|\sum(f;\mathcal P,\vec\xi)-I\right|<\varepsilon}
$$
for every <partition of an interval> with $\|\mathcal P\|<\delta$ and every permitted choice of tags. Equivalently, along every <sequence> of such tagged <partitions of an interval> with <mesh of a partition> tending to zero, the <Riemann sums> tend to $I$. Both the shrinking <mesh of a partition> and freedom of tags are essential.

The stated weighted <inequality> is the <Jensen inequality> for a <concave function>. Assume $a<b$; if $a=b$, both sides of the required <inequality> are zero. Write $x=\lambda a+(1-\lambda)b$ with $\lambda=(b-x)/(b-a)$. Applying the weighted <inequality> at the two endpoints gives
$$
f(x)\geq\ell(x)=\frac{b-x}{b-a}f(a)+\frac{x-a}{b-a}f(b).
$$
Thus the <affine function> $\ell$ joining the endpoint values lies below $f$. By <monotonicity of the Riemann integral>,
$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\geq\int_a^b\ell(x)\,dx
=\boxed{\frac{b-a}{2}\bigl(f(a)+f(b)\bigr).}
$$
This is the lower bound in the <Hermite-Hadamard inequality> for a <concave function>.

For $a<b$, choose the uniform <partition of an interval> into $n$ pieces and its midpoint tags
$$
\xi_i=a+\left(i-\frac12\right)\frac{b-a}{n},\qquad1\leq i\leq n.
$$
Their <arithmetic mean> is exactly $(a+b)/2$. Apply the given <Jensen inequality> with equal weights $\lambda_i=1/n$:
$$
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n f(\xi_i)\leq f\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_i\right)=f\left(\frac{a+b}2\right).
$$
Multiplying by $b-a$ gives a midpoint <Riemann sum> on the left. Its <mesh of a partition> is $(b-a)/n\to0$, so the <Riemann sum convergence theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\leq(b-a)f\left(\frac{a+b}2\right).}
$$
For $a=b$ the equality is immediate. Together with part <7/c/i>, this is the <Hermite-Hadamard inequality> with the directions appropriate to a <concave function>.
