Use the <vector triple product identity>
$$
\mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)=\mathbf b(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)-\mathbf c(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b).
$$
Since $\mathbf n$ is a <unit vector>, put
$$
\mathbf w=\mathbf n\times(\mathbf v\times\mathbf n)=\mathbf v-(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf n,\qquad \mathbf q=\mathbf n\times\mathbf v.
$$
Thus $\mathbf w$ is the component of $\mathbf v$ in the <orthogonal complement> of $\mathbf n$, and $\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf w=0$. A <cross product> is <orthogonal> to both its factors, so $\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf q=0$ and $\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf q=0$. Consequently
$$
\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf w=\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf v-(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf n)(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf n)=0.
$$
**All three pairs are <orthogonal>.** This remains true when $\mathbf v$ is parallel to $\mathbf n$: then $\mathbf q$ and $\mathbf w$ are <zero vectors>. Pairwise <orthogonality> does not assert <linear independence> in this case.

Use the convention $[\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c]=\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)$ for the <scalar triple product>. With $\mathbf q,\mathbf w$ as above, $\mathbf q=\mathbf n\times\mathbf w$. The <vector triple product identity> gives
$$
\mathbf q\times\mathbf w=-\mathbf w\times(\mathbf n\times\mathbf w)=-\mathbf n|\mathbf w|^2+\mathbf w(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf n)=-\mathbf n|\mathbf w|^2.
$$
The <Lagrange identity for the cross product>, $|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b|^2=|\mathbf a|^2|\mathbf b|^2-(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)^2$, also gives
$$
|\mathbf q|^2=|\mathbf v|^2-(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v)^2=|\mathbf w|^2.
$$
Taking the <dot product> with the <unit vector> $\mathbf n$ proves
$$
\boxed{[\mathbf n,\mathbf n\times\mathbf v,\mathbf n\times(\mathbf v\times\mathbf n)]=-|\mathbf n\times\mathbf v|^2.}
$$

Decompose $\mathbf v$ by <orthogonal projection> onto the <straight line> spanned by $\mathbf n$ and its <orthogonal complement>:
$$
\mathbf v=(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf n+\mathbf w,\qquad \mathbf w=\mathbf n\times(\mathbf v\times\mathbf n).
$$
The first term is fixed by the <rotation>. If $\mathbf w\ne0$, the <vectors> $\mathbf w/|\mathbf w|$ and $(\mathbf n\times\mathbf w)/|\mathbf w|$ form an <orthonormal basis> of the perpendicular <plane>, oriented in the positive sense about $\mathbf n$. A <planar rotation> through $\theta$ therefore sends
$$
\mathbf w\longmapsto\cos\theta\,\mathbf w+\sin\theta\,(\mathbf n\times\mathbf w).
$$
Here $\mathbf n\times\mathbf w=\mathbf n\times\mathbf v$. Adding the fixed parallel component proves the <Rodrigues rotation formula> in the required form:
$$
\boxed{R(\mathbf v)=(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf n+\sin\theta(\mathbf n\times\mathbf v)+\cos\theta\bigl(\mathbf n\times(\mathbf v\times\mathbf n)\bigr).}
$$
When $\mathbf w=0$, both perpendicular terms vanish, so the same formula fixes $\mathbf v$ as required. Positive <angles> use the right-hand convention about $\mathbf n$.

$$
It fixes $(1,2,2)^T$ and negates every <vector> in its <orthogonal complement>, which also checks the <half-turn> geometrically.

First find the fixed <axis of rotation> by solving $(M-I)\mathbf n=0$. After removing a nonzero common factor, the <linear equations> are
$$
-2n_1+n_2-3n_3=0,\qquad -3n_1-2n_2-n_3=0,\qquad n_1+3n_2-2n_3=0.
$$
They give $n_2=n_3=-n_1$. Choose the following <unit vectors>:
$$
\mathbf p_1=\frac1{\sqrt3}(1,-1,-1)^T,\quad \mathbf p_2=\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,1,0)^T,\quad \mathbf p_3=\mathbf p_1\times\mathbf p_2=\frac1{\sqrt6}(1,-1,2)^T.
$$
Their <dot products> show that they form an <orthonormal basis>; hence $P=(\mathbf p_1\ \mathbf p_2\ \mathbf p_3)$ is an <orthogonal matrix>. Since $M$ is an <orthogonal matrix> fixing $\mathbf p_1$, it preserves the <orthogonal complement> of $\mathbf p_1$. Its restriction there has <determinant> $1$, so it is a <planar rotation>.

The <trace> gives $1+2\cos\theta=\operatorname{tr}M=9/7$, so $\cos\theta=1/7$. To determine the sign of the <sine>, rather than just its magnitude, calculate
$$
M\mathbf p_2=\frac1{7\sqrt2}(5,-3,8)^T,\qquad \mathbf p_3^TM\mathbf p_2=\frac{24}{7\sqrt{12}}=\frac{4\sqrt3}{7}>0.
$$
Thus a suitable <angle> and <change of basis> are
$$
\boxed{\theta=\arccos(1/7),\qquad P=\begin{pmatrix}1/\sqrt3&1/\sqrt2&1/\sqrt6\\-1/\sqrt3&1/\sqrt2&-1/\sqrt6\\-1/\sqrt3&0&2/\sqrt6\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
In fact direct <matrix multiplication> gives
$$
P^TMP=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1/7&-4\sqrt3/7\\0&4\sqrt3/7&1/7\end{pmatrix}=\operatorname{diag}(1,R_\theta).
$$

Write $T_{\theta,\mathbf b}(\mathbf v)=R_\theta\mathbf v+\mathbf b$. The <sine addition formula> and <cosine addition formula> give $R_\theta R_\psi=R_{\theta+\psi}$. Therefore <function composition> satisfies
$$
T_{\theta,\mathbf b}\circ T_{\psi,\mathbf c}=T_{\theta+\psi,\,R_\theta\mathbf c+\mathbf b}.
$$
The resulting <angle> is real and the new <translation> <vector> lies in $\mathbb R^2$, so $G$ is closed under <function composition>. <Associativity> holds because, for any <functions> $S,T,U$ and any $\mathbf v$, both $(S\circ T)\circ U$ and $S\circ(T\circ U)$ take $\mathbf v$ to $S(T(U(\mathbf v)))$.

The <identity element> is $T_{0,\mathbf0}$, since $R_0=I$. Every element has the two-sided <inverse element>
$$
\boxed{T_{\theta,\mathbf b}^{-1}=T_{-\theta,\,-R_{-\theta}\mathbf b}.}
$$
Indeed $R_\theta R_{-\theta}=R_{-\theta}R_\theta=I$, and substituting into the composition formula makes the <translation> <vector> zero in either order. These verify all the <group axioms>. Thus **$G$ is a <group> under <function composition>**, the <special Euclidean group> in two dimensions.

A possible center $\mathbf c$ must be a <fixed point>, so $(I-R_\theta)\mathbf c=\mathbf b$. Let $c=\cos\theta$ and $s=\sin\theta$. The <determinant> is
$$
\det(I-R_\theta)=(1-c)^2+s^2=2(1-c)=4\sin^2(\theta/2)>0
$$
when $\theta$ is not an <integer multiple> of $2\pi$. The unique center is therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbf c=(I-R_\theta)^{-1}\mathbf b=\frac1{2(1-\cos\theta)}\begin{pmatrix}1-\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&1-\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\mathbf b.}
$$
The <fixed point> equation implies $\mathbf b=\mathbf c-R_\theta\mathbf c$, whence
$$
T(\mathbf v)=\mathbf c+R_\theta(\mathbf v-\mathbf c).
$$
Relative to $\mathbf c$, this is precisely a <planar rotation> through $\theta$. This also explains the excluded case: when $\theta\in2\pi\mathbb Z$, $T$ is a <translation>, with no <fixed point> unless $\mathbf b=0$.

Use the <parametric curve>
$$
X=2\sqrt2\cos t,\quad Y=\sqrt2\sin t,\qquad x(t)=2\cos t+\sin t,\quad y(t)=-2\cos t+\sin t,\quad 0\leq t\leq2\pi.
$$
Here $|y(t)|\leq\sqrt5<3$, so the <ellipse> stays strictly above the <axis of rotation> $y=-3$. Rotating in three dimensions gives a <surface of revolution> with <parametrization>
$$
\mathbf s(t,\varphi)=\bigl(x(t),-3+(y(t)+3)\cos\varphi,(y(t)+3)\sin\varphi\bigr),\qquad 0\leq\varphi\leq2\pi.
$$
The two parameter <tangent vectors> are <orthogonal>, with <Euclidean norms> $\sqrt{x'(t)^2+y'(t)^2}$ and $y(t)+3$. Hence the <area element of a surface> is
$$
|\mathbf s_t\times\mathbf s_\varphi|=(y(t)+3)\sqrt{x'(t)^2+y'(t)^2}.
$$
The generating <ellipse> is simple and has positive distance from the <axis of rotation>; its <rotation> sweeps the <surface> once, apart from the identified parameter endpoints. Also
$$
x'(t)^2+y'(t)^2=(-2\sin t+\cos t)^2+(2\sin t+\cos t)^2=8\sin^2t+2\cos^2t.
$$
Writing $w(t)=\sqrt{8\sin^2t+2\cos^2t}$, the <surface area> is therefore $2\pi\int_0^{2\pi}(3+y(t))w(t)\,dt$. Since $y(t+\pi)=-y(t)$ and $w(t+\pi)=w(t)$,
$$
\int_0^{2\pi}y(t)w(t)\,dt=\int_0^\pi\bigl(y(t)w(t)+y(t+\pi)w(t+\pi)\bigr)\,dt=0.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Area}(S)=6\pi\int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{8\sin^2t+2\cos^2t}\,dt.}
$$
The cancellation is an instance of the <surface area of revolution of a centrally symmetric curve>: the <surface area> is the generating curve’s <arc length> times the distance $6\pi$ traveled by its center.

For <polar coordinates>, the rotating <unit vectors> satisfy $\dot{\mathbf e}_r=\dot\theta\mathbf e_\theta$ and $\dot{\mathbf e}_\theta=-\dot\theta\mathbf e_r$. Applying the <product rule> twice to $\mathbf r=r\mathbf e_r$ gives the <velocity> and <acceleration>
$$
\dot{\mathbf r}=\dot r\mathbf e_r+r\dot\theta\mathbf e_\theta,\qquad \ddot{\mathbf r}=(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2)\mathbf e_r+(r\ddot\theta+2\dot r\dot\theta)\mathbf e_\theta.
$$
<Newton's second law> for a <central force> now gives
$$
m(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2)=F(r),\qquad m(r\ddot\theta+2\dot r\dot\theta)=0.
$$
Multiplying the azimuthal <force> equation by $r/m$ yields
$$
\frac{d}{dt}(r^2\dot\theta)=0,\qquad \boxed{r^2\dot\theta=h.}
$$
This is conservation of <specific angular momentum>. Here $F(r)$ is the signed radial component of the <force>; a negative value points inward.

When $h\ne0$, $\theta$ is a valid local parameter along the <trajectory>. Put $u=1/r$ and let primes denote <derivatives> with respect to $\theta$. The <chain rule> yields
$$
\dot\theta=hu^2,\qquad \dot r=-\frac{u'}{u^2}\dot\theta=-hu',\qquad \ddot r=-h^2u^2u'',\qquad r\dot\theta^2=h^2u^3.
$$
Substituting into the radial <Newton's second law> equation and dividing by $-mh^2u^2$ proves the <Binet equation>:
$$
\boxed{u''+u=-\frac{F(1/u)}{mh^2u^2}.}
$$

Here $mh^2=b$, so the <Binet equation> reduces to
$$
u''+u=-\frac{(b/2)u^2e^{-u}-bu^3}{bu^2}=u-\frac12e^{-u},\qquad u''=-\frac12e^{-u}.
$$
The <initial conditions> become
$$
u(0)=\frac1a,\qquad u'(0)=-\frac1{a^2}\frac{dr}{d\theta}(0)=e^{-1/(2a)}>0.
$$
Multiply the <differential equation> by $2u'$ and use the <chain rule>:
$$
\frac{d}{d\theta}(u'^2)=-e^{-u}u'=\frac{d}{d\theta}(e^{-u}),\qquad u'^2-e^{-u}=C.
$$
The <initial conditions> give $C=0$. Since $e^{-u}>0$, $u'$ cannot change sign at a finite value of $u$. Thus $u'=e^{-u/2}$ on the physical branch. <Separation of variables> gives
$$
\frac{d}{d\theta}(e^{u/2})=\frac12,\qquad e^{u/2}=e^{1/(2a)}+\frac\theta2.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{r(\theta)=\frac1{2\log\left(e^{1/(2a)}+\theta/2\right)},\qquad \theta>2\left(1-e^{1/(2a)}\right).}
$$
The <natural logarithm> must be positive because $r>0$. For the forward <trajectory>, $\theta\geq0$ and $h=\sqrt{b/m}>0$, so $\dot\theta=h/r^2>0$: the particle travels counterclockwise while $r$ strictly decreases.

For $a=1$, it starts at $(x,y)=(1,0)$, with <tangent vector> proportional to $(-e^{-1/2},1)$. Its successive crossings of the positive $x$ <coordinate axis> have radii
$$
r(2\pi k)=\frac1{2\log(\sqrt e+\pi k)},\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots.
$$
It makes infinitely many inward turns, approaching the origin as $\theta\to\infty$. There is no finite-time arrival at the origin: $dt/d\theta=r^2/h$ behaves as $1/(4h\log^2\theta)$, whose <improper integral> diverges. If extended backwards, it comes from infinity as $\theta\downarrow2(1-\sqrt e)$.

Adding the <forces> on each particle and applying <Newton's second law> gives

The <massless inextensible string> has a common <tension> magnitude $T$. Since $\mathbf r_1=r_1(\mathbf e_r-\mathbf e_z)$, the <unit vector> from the first particle towards the apex is $(-\mathbf e_r+\mathbf e_z)/\sqrt2$. The second particle lies below the apex, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbf T_1=\frac{T}{\sqrt2}(-\mathbf e_r+\mathbf e_z),\qquad \mathbf T_2=T\mathbf e_z.}
$$
The <string length constraint> is
$$
l=\sqrt2r_1-z_2=-\sqrt2z_1-z_2,\qquad \dot r_1=-\dot z_1,\qquad \dot z_2=-\sqrt2\dot z_1.
$$
The <velocity> of the first particle in <cylindrical coordinates> is $\dot r_1\mathbf e_r+r_1\dot\theta\mathbf e_\theta+\dot z_1\mathbf e_z$. Therefore the <powers> delivered by the two <tension> <forces> are
$$
\mathbf T_1\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}_1=\frac T{\sqrt2}(-\dot r_1+\dot z_1)=\sqrt2T\dot z_1,\qquad \mathbf T_2\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}_2=T\dot z_2=-\sqrt2T\dot z_1.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbf T_1\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}_1=-\mathbf T_2\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}_2.}
$$
The <tension> can transfer <energy> between the particles, but its total <power> on the pair is zero.

All <forces> on the first particle lie in the <plane> spanned by $\mathbf e_r$ and $\mathbf e_z$: this holds for <gravity>, <tension>, and the <normal reaction>. Hence the azimuthal component of <Newton's second law> vanishes:
$$
m_1(r_1\ddot\theta+2\dot r_1\dot\theta)=0.
$$
Using the <product rule> gives
$$
\boxed{\dot h=\frac{d}{dt}(r_1^2\dot\theta)=r_1(r_1\ddot\theta+2\dot r_1\dot\theta)=0.}
$$
Strictly, $h$ is the axial <specific angular momentum>; the actual axial <angular momentum> is $m_1h$, which is conserved as well.

Differentiate the stated total <energy> using the <chain rule> and then substitute the two <Newton's second law> equations:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot E&=m_1\dot{\mathbf r}_1\cdot\ddot{\mathbf r}_1+m_1g\dot z_1+m_2\dot{\mathbf r}_2\cdot\ddot{\mathbf r}_2+m_2g\dot z_2\\
&=\mathbf N\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}_1+\mathbf T_1\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}_1+\mathbf T_2\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}_2.
\end{aligned}
$$
The <gravitational potential energy> terms have canceled the <power> of <gravity>. Also $\mathbf N$ is proportional to $\mathbf e_r+\mathbf e_z$, whose <dot product> with $\dot{\mathbf r}_1$ is $\dot r_1+\dot z_1=0$. Thus the stationary <smooth mechanical constraint> does no <work>. The remaining <tension> <powers> cancel by part (b), proving **$\dot E=0$ and conservation of total <energy>** while the <massless inextensible string> remains taut and contact is maintained.

\boxed{E=\frac12m_1|\mathbf v|^2+m_2v_z^2-m_1ga+m_2g(\sqrt2a-l).}

If $v$ denotes only the initial <speed>, the printed wording does not specify its direction within the <tangent plane>. To make that dependence explicit, let $\beta$ be the <angle> from the downward generator of the <cone> towards the azimuthal <unit vector>, so
$$
\mathbf v=v\cos\beta\,\frac{\mathbf e_r-\mathbf e_z}{\sqrt2}+v\sin\beta\,\mathbf e_\theta.

Then $v_z=-v\cos\beta/\sqrt2$, and
$$
\boxed{E=\frac12(m_1+m_2\cos^2\beta)v^2-m_1ga+m_2g(\sqrt2a-l).}

The solid <cylinder> has constant <mass density> $\rho=m/(\pi a^2l)$. A thin cylindrical shell of radius $s$ and thickness $ds$ has <mass> $dm=2\pi\rho ls\,ds$. Its contribution to the axial <moment of inertia> is $s^2dm$. Therefore
$$
\mathcal I_{33}=\int_0^a s^2(2\pi\rho ls)\,ds=2\pi\rho l\frac{a^4}{4}=\boxed{\frac12ma^2.}
$$
The attached wheels are massless, so they add no <moment of inertia>. This is the <moment of inertia of a uniform solid cylinder> about its central longitudinal <axis of rotation>.

= Solution

Apply the general <linear momentum> equation $d\mathbf p/dt=\sum\mathbf F$ to the whole <rigid body>. Its center of mass has <acceleration> $\ddot x\mathbf e_1$, and each rail contributes its own <static friction> and <normal force>. Hence
$$
m\ddot x\mathbf e_1=(T\cos\alpha-2F)\mathbf e_1+(T\sin\alpha+2N-mg)\mathbf e_2,
$$
so
$$
\boxed{m\ddot x=T\cos\alpha-2F,\qquad N=\frac{mg-T\sin\alpha}{2}.}
$$
Here $N$ is the <normal force> at each rail, not the combined support.

Take <torques> about the center of mass, with positive <angular velocity> along $\mathbf e_3$. The thread leaves the lower side shown in the source diagram: its contact <position vector> relative to the center is
$$
\mathbf q=a\sin\alpha\,\mathbf e_1-a\cos\alpha\,\mathbf e_2.
$$
Thus $\mathbf q\times\mathbf T=aT\mathbf e_3$. Each contact point has relative <position vector> $-R\mathbf e_2$, so each <friction> <torque> is $-RF\mathbf e_3$. The <gravitational weight> acts through the center of mass and the <normal forces> have zero <torque> about it. The <angular momentum> equation becomes
$$
\boxed{\mathcal I_{33}\ddot\phi=aT-2RF.}
$$
The condition of <rolling without slipping> makes the contact point instantaneously stationary:
$$
\mathbf v_P=\dot x\mathbf e_1+\dot\phi\mathbf e_3\times(-R\mathbf e_2)=(\dot x+R\dot\phi)\mathbf e_1=0.
$$
Integrating and using $x(0)=\phi(0)=0$ gives $x=-R\phi$. Thus rightward <translation> corresponds to clockwise <rotation>. Eliminating $F$ from the two dynamical equations with $\ddot x=-R\ddot\phi$ yields
$$
(\mathcal I_{33}+mR^2)\ddot\phi=aT-RT\cos\alpha,
$$
and therefore
$$
\boxed{\ddot\phi=-\frac{T(R\cos\alpha-a)}{mR^2+\mathcal I_{33}}.}
$$

Put $D=mR^2+\mathcal I_{33}=m(R^2+a^2/2)$. The <angular acceleration> is constant. The initial rest conditions and $x(0)=\phi(0)=0$ give
$$
\boxed{\phi(t)=-\frac{T(R\cos\alpha-a)}{2D}t^2,\qquad x(t)=\frac{TR(R\cos\alpha-a)}{2D}t^2.}
$$
For the upward rightward pull in the diagram, $0\leq\alpha\leq\pi/2$, the sign of $x(t)$ is the sign of $R\cos\alpha-a$. Consequently **it rolls towards the horizontal pull when $0\leq\alpha<\arccos(a/R)$**. At $\alpha=\arccos(a/R)$ it remains at rest, with balanced <forces> and <torques>. For $\arccos(a/R)<\alpha\leq\pi/2$, it rolls to the left. In particular **a horizontal pull, $\alpha=0$, makes it roll to the right**, since $R>a$. If other pulling <angles> are allowed, the general condition for motion in the same horizontal direction as the pull is $\cos\alpha(R\cos\alpha-a)>0$.

The required <static friction> at each rail follows from the translational equation:
$$
\boxed{F_{\mathrm{req}}=\frac{T(\mathcal I_{33}\cos\alpha+maR)}{2D}.}
$$
For $0\leq\alpha\leq\pi/2$ it is positive, agreeing with the leftward <force> convention. The assumed <rolling without slipping> is physically possible only if the rails can supply this <force> while maintaining contact. With <coefficient of static friction> $\mu_s$, the necessary contact inequalities are
$$
N=\frac{mg-T\sin\alpha}{2}\geq0,\qquad |F_{\mathrm{req}}|\leq\mu_sN.
$$
For the usual pulling range, these become
$$
\boxed{T\sin\alpha<mg,\qquad T(\mathcal I_{33}\cos\alpha+maR)\leq\mu_sD(mg-T\sin\alpha).}
$$
If either fails, the proposed motion requires lift-off or more <static friction> than the rails provide. Equality $T\sin\alpha=mg$ is also incompatible here: it gives $N=0$ but a nonzero required <friction>. The question supplies no <coefficient of static friction>, so it determines the required <friction> exactly but gives no numerical slip threshold. If $F$ is regarded as a prescribed <force> rather than an available contact response, it must equal $F_{\mathrm{req}}$ for the <rolling constraint> to hold.

Let $d$ be any positive <integer>. If $d$ divides $a$ and $b$, it divides the <integer linear combination> $a-qb=r$. Conversely, if $d$ divides $b$ and $r$, it divides $qb+r=a$. Thus $(a,b)$ and $(b,r)$ have exactly the same positive common <divisors>. Their greatest elements coincide:
$$
\boxed{\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,r).}
$$
This <greatest common divisor> invariance is the step behind the <Euclidean algorithm>; it does not require $r<b$, although that additional bound ensures the algorithm terminates. If the convention $0\in\mathbb N$ permits $r=0$, the same argument works and $\gcd(b,0)=b$ for $b>0$.

The successive <division with remainder> steps of the <Euclidean algorithm> are
$$
\begin{aligned}
657&=2\cdot306+45,\\
306&=6\cdot45+36,\\
45&=1\cdot36+9,\\
36&=4\cdot9+0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Each step preserves the <greatest common divisor> by part (a). The final nonzero <remainder> is $9$, so
$$
\boxed{\gcd(306,657)=9.}
$$

Back-substitute in the <Euclidean algorithm> to obtain an <integer linear combination>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
9&=45-36=45-(306-6\cdot45)\\
&=7\cdot45-306=7(657-2\cdot306)-306\\
&=7\cdot657-15\cdot306.
\end{aligned}
$$
Multiplying by $3$ gives the particular solution $(x_0,y_0)=(-45,21)$ of the <linear Diophantine equation>. Subtracting it from any other solution and dividing by $9$ gives
$$
34(x+45)+73(y-21)=0.
$$
The <integers> $34$ and $73$ are <coprime>, so $73$ divides $x+45$. Writing $x+45=73t$ forces $y-21=-34t$. Conversely these expressions satisfy the original equation for every <integer> $t$. Thus <all solutions of a linear Diophantine equation> here are
$$
\boxed{(x,y)=(-45+73t,\ 21-34t),\qquad t\in\mathbb Z.}
$$

Rearrange the <polynomial> equation as a <fixed point> equation:
$$
\boxed{g(x)=\frac{1-x^5}{7},\qquad [a,b]=[0,1/7].}
$$
First establish existence and uniqueness of the target <root of a polynomial>. Since $f$ is a <continuous function>, $f(0)=-1<0$ and $f(1/7)=1/7^5>0$, the <intermediate value theorem> gives a <root of a polynomial> $\xi\in(0,1/7)$. Moreover $f'(x)=5x^4+7>0$ for all real $x$, so $f$ is <strictly increasing> and this is its only real <root of a polynomial>.

For $x\in[0,1/7]$,
$$
0<\frac{1-7^{-5}}7\leq g(x)\leq\frac17,
$$
so $g$ maps the <closed interval> into itself. Also
$$
|g'(x)|=\frac{5x^4}{7}\leq\frac5{7^5}=q<1.
$$
The <mean value theorem> therefore gives $|g(x)-g(y)|\leq q|x-y|$ on the <closed interval>: $g$ is a <contraction mapping>. For any starting value $x_0\in[0,1/7]$, all iterates remain in that <closed interval>, and since $g(\xi)=\xi$,
$$
|x_k-\xi|=|g(x_{k-1})-g(\xi)|\leq q|x_{k-1}-\xi|\leq q^k|x_0-\xi|\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus **the <fixed-point iteration> converges to the unique real <root of a polynomial> from every $x_0\in[0,1/7]$**. This explicit error estimate proves the needed convergence directly.
