Divide by $x>1$. The <product rule> turns the <linear ordinary differential equation> into
$$
(\ln x\,y)'=\frac{\ln x}{x}.
$$
Equivalently, $\ln x$ is an <integrating factor> after division by $x\ln x$. An <antiderivative> gives $\ln x\,y=\tfrac12(\ln x)^2+C$. The <initial condition> at $e$ gives $C=-1/2$, hence
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac12\left(\ln x-\frac1{\ln x}\right),\qquad x>1.}
$$
Its <derivative> is $y'=[1+(\ln x)^{-2}]/(2x)$, so substitution gives $x\ln x\,y'+y=\ln x$, and $y(e)=0$. Every <solution of a differential equation> has the integrated form above, and the <initial condition> fixes its only constant. This agrees with the <uniqueness theorem for ordinary differential equations> on the stated <interval>.

The <product rule> gives $y'=x^nz'+nx^{n-1}z$ and $y''=x^nz''+2nx^{n-1}z'+n(n-1)x^{n-2}z$. On an <interval> avoiding $x=0$, substitution and division by $x^{n+2}$ give
$$
z''+\left(2+\frac{2n+4}{x}\right)z'
+\left(2+\frac{2n+4}{x}+\frac{n^2+3n+2}{x^2}\right)z=0.
$$
For a <constant-coefficient differential equation>, the coefficient of $z'$ forces $2n+4=0$. At $n=-2$, the remaining coefficient $n^2+3n+2$ also vanishes. Thus
$$
\boxed{n=-2,\qquad z''+2z'+2z=0.}
$$
The <characteristic equation of a constant-coefficient differential equation> is $r^2+2r+2=0$, with <complex conjugate> <roots of a polynomial> $-1\pm i$. Its real solutions $e^{-x}\cos x$ and $e^{-x}\sin x$ are <linearly independent>. Therefore the <general solution> is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{e^{-x}}{x^2}(A\cos x+B\sin x),\qquad x\ne0.}
$$
The constants can be chosen independently on the two sides of the singular point. The <power substitution reducing a differential equation to constant coefficients> here is invertible wherever $x\ne0$.

Use the <change of variables> $u=x+y$. Then $u'=1+y'=1+\cos u=2\cos^2(u/2)$, a <separable differential equation>. Where $u$ is not an odd multiple of $\pi$, <separation of variables> gives
$$
\int\frac{du}{1+\cos u}=\int dx,
\qquad
\boxed{\tan\frac{x+y}{2}=x+C.}
$$
Indeed the <derivative> of $\tan(u/2)$ with respect to $u$ is $1/(1+\cos u)$. The nonconstant branches can also be written $y=2\arctan(x+C)+2k\pi-x$, $k\in\mathbb Z$. Dividing by $1+\cos u$ excluded its zeros: the missing <equilibrium solutions> for $u$ give the additional <solutions of a differential equation>
$$
\boxed{y=(2k+1)\pi-x,\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
They satisfy $y'=-1=\cos(x+y)$ directly. The transformed right side is continuously differentiable, so the <uniqueness theorem for ordinary differential equations> prevents a nonconstant branch from crossing an <equilibrium solution> at finite $x$.

Put $h(s)=\sin x(s)-\cos y(s)$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
h'=x'\cos x+y'\sin y,\qquad
f'=-h'\sin h=-(x'\cos x+y'\sin y)g.

Consequently $f''=-(h')^2f-h''g$, so the requested coefficients are

\end{aligned}}
$$
This is the <second derivative chain rule> along the <parametric curve> $(x(s),y(s))$.

Write $A=a^2>0$ and $E=e^{-(x^2+y^2)/A}>0$. The <partial derivatives> factor as
$$
f_x=2xE\left(1-\frac{x^2-2y^2}{A}\right),\qquad

A <critical point> has both <partial derivatives> zero. If $x,y\ne0$, this would require $x^2-2y^2=A$ and $x^2-2y^2=-2A$, which is impossible. On the axes the remaining conditions give
$$

H_f=\begin{pmatrix}-4/e&0\\0&-6/e\end{pmatrix}.

For completeness, the <Hessian matrix> at $(0,\pm|a|)$ is $\operatorname{diag}(6/e,8/e)$, giving strict <local minima>, while at the origin it is $\operatorname{diag}(2,-4)$, giving a <saddle point>.

A <discrete random variable> on a <probability space> $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb P)$ is a real-valued <measurable function> $X:\Omega\to\mathbb R$ whose <probability distribution> is concentrated on some finite or countable set $S$:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X\in S)=1,\qquad S\text{ countable}.}
$$
<Measurability> means $\{\omega:X(\omega)\le t\}\in\mathcal F$ for every real $t$. Its <probability mass function> is $p_X(x)=\mathbb P(X=x)$, and $\sum_{x\in S}p_X(x)=1$. Requiring a countable image everywhere, rather than outside a <null set>, is an equivalent convention after harmless modification on a <null set>.

**The printed numerical example reverses the digits and contradicts both definitions.** With the stated last-digit definition, $[Z]=17$ gives $X=7$, $Y=1$. Use those definitions throughout.

Put $q=e^{-\lambda}\in(0,1)$ and $N=\lfloor Z\rfloor$. The <exponential distribution> gives
$$
\mathbb P(N=n)=\mathbb P(n\le Z<n+1)=(1-q)q^n,\qquad n\ge0.

The <Euclidean division> $N=10Y+X$ has unique remainder $X\in\{0,\ldots,9\}$ and quotient $Y\in\{0,1,\ldots\}$. Thus the <joint probability mass function> is
$$
\boxed{p_{X,Y}(x,y)=(1-q)q^{x+10y},\quad
x=0,\ldots,9,\ y=0,1,\ldots,}
$$
and zero otherwise. This is <decimal digit decomposition of a geometric random variable>. Following the erroneous example instead would require different definitions and would not answer the stated question.

An <expected value> of a signed <random variable> is finite precisely when its absolute value is <integrable>. With the <standard normal distribution> <probability density function>,
$$
\mathbb E|Ze^{\alpha Z^2}|
=\frac2{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_0^\infty z\exp\bigl[-(1/2-\alpha)z^2\bigr]\,dz.
$$
For $\alpha<1/2$, this <integral> equals $1/[\sqrt{2\pi}(1/2-\alpha)]<\infty$. For $\alpha\ge1/2$, its <integrand> does not decay sufficiently and the <integral> diverges. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[Ze^{\alpha Z^2}]\text{ exists exactly for }\alpha<\frac12,
\quad\text{and then equals }0.}
$$
The value is zero by the <odd function> and the <symmetric distribution>. For the other $\alpha$, both positive and negative parts have infinite <integrals>; formal cancellation would give only a <Cauchy principal value>, not an <expected value>. This distinction is the <integrability threshold for exponential powers of a Gaussian>.

=\frac16\sum_{k=1}^6\frac{ks}{6-(6-k)s}.}

As a <power series>, its exact domain of absolute convergence is

Indeed the slowest decaying component has failure <probability> $5/6$, so $\mathbb P(T=t)\ge(1/36)(5/6)^{t-1}$. At $|s|=6/5$, the terms of the <power series> fail to tend to zero; outside that disk they grow. All six component series converge inside it. In particular, the <probability generating function> is defined on the usual real <interval> $0\le s\le1$, with $G_T(1)=1$. The rational expression beyond the convergence disk is only an algebraic continuation, not the defining <expected value>.

For observations $\mathbf x_i\in\mathbb R^p$, <K-means clustering> seeks nonempty <clusters in cluster analysis> $C_1,\ldots,C_K$ and <centroids> $\mathbf m_k$ minimizing
$$
\boxed{W=\sum_{k=1}^K\sum_{i\in C_k}\|\mathbf x_i-\mathbf m_k\|^2,\qquad
\mathbf m_k=\frac1{|C_k|}\sum_{i\in C_k}\mathbf x_i.}
$$
At fixed <centroids>, assigning each point to a nearest <centroid> minimizes its squared <Euclidean distance>. At fixed assignments, the <centroid sum-of-squares identity> shows that the <sample mean> uniquely minimizes each group's contribution. These alternating steps cannot increase $W$.

For <finite termination of K-means clustering>, more than a lower bound on $W$ is needed. Keep an assignment unchanged when nearest <centroids> tie, and stop when assignments no longer change. Every change then strictly decreases $W$. After updating the <centroids>, $W$ is determined by the <partition of a set> of $n$ indices; there are only finitely many choices for that <partition of a set>, so no strictly improving <partition of a set> can recur. Empty <clusters in cluster analysis> require a convention, such as moving a point from a nonsingleton <cluster in cluster analysis> to become its own <centroid>, which cannot increase $W$. With such a consistent convention the algorithm terminates, although it may reach only a <local minimum>.

For this rate parametrization of the <Laplace distribution>, $\mu$ is its <mean> and <median>. By <symmetry>, $\mathbb E(X-\mu)=0$, while $|X-\mu|$ has <exponential distribution> with rate $\lambda$, so

By <independence>, the <likelihood function> is the product of the individual <probability density functions>:
$$
\boxed{L(\mu,\lambda;x_1,\ldots,x_n)
=\left(\frac\lambda2\right)^n
\exp\!\left[-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^n|x_i-\mu|\right],\quad

Writing $A(\mu)=\sum_i|x_i-\mu|$, the <log-likelihood function> is $\ell=n\ln\lambda-n\ln2-\lambda A(\mu)$. Here $\lambda$ is a rate, the reciprocal of the scale in the usual <Laplace distribution> notation.

For each fixed $\lambda>0$, maximizing the <likelihood function> over $\mu$ is equivalent to minimizing $A(\mu)=\sum_i|x_i-\mu|$. Order the distinct observations as $x_{(1)}<\cdots<x_{(n)}$ and write $n=2m+1$. Between successive <order statistics>, with exactly $k$ observations to the left,
$$
A'(\mu)=k-(n-k)=2k-n.
$$
Thus $A$ decreases up to $x_{(m+1)}$ and increases after it. Its unique <minimum> is at the <sample median>:
$$
\boxed{\widehat\mu=x_{(m+1)}.}
$$
At a fixed $\mu$ with $A(\mu)>0$, the <log-likelihood function> has <derivative> $n/\lambda-A(\mu)$ and second <derivative> $-n/\lambda^2<0$, so its unique maximizing rate is $n/A(\mu)$. The maximized <likelihood function> is $(n/[2eA(\mu)])^n$, which increases when $A(\mu)$ decreases. Hence the joint <maximum likelihood estimates> are
$$
\boxed{\widehat\mu=x_{(m+1)},\qquad
\widehat\lambda=\frac{n}{\sum_i|x_i-x_{(m+1)}|}.}
$$
This is <Laplace location and scale maximum likelihood>, expressed with the reciprocal scale.

**A finite rate <maximum likelihood estimate> requires a positive total absolute deviation.** For distinct observations and odd $n\ge3$, that condition holds. For $n=1$, however, $\mu=x_1$ gives $A(\mu)=0$ and $L=\lambda/2\to\infty$ as $\lambda\to\infty$.
