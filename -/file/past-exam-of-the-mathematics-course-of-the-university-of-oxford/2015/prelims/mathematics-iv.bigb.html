Let
$$
\mathbf r_0=\frac{\mathbf a\wedge\mathbf b}{|\mathbf a|^2},\qquad
\mathbf N=\mathbf b+\mathbf a\wedge\mathbf y.
$$
The <vector triple product> identity $(\mathbf a\wedge\mathbf b)\wedge\mathbf a=|\mathbf a|^2\mathbf b-(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)\mathbf a$ shows that $\mathbf r_0\wedge\mathbf a=\mathbf b$. Moreover, $(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0)\wedge\mathbf a=\mathbf0$ exactly when $\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0$ is a <scalar multiple> of $\mathbf a$. Thus the given <line> is
$$
L=\{\mathbf r_0+t\mathbf a:t\in\mathbb R\}.
$$
Also $\mathbf a\wedge\mathbf r_0=-\mathbf b$, so $\mathbf N=\mathbf a\wedge(\mathbf y-\mathbf r_0)$. The assumption $\mathbf N\ne\mathbf0$ says precisely that $\mathbf y$ is outside $L$. Consequently $L$ and $\mathbf y$ determine a unique <plane>, with <normal vector> $\mathbf N$.

To identify its equation, use cyclic invariance of the <scalar triple product>:
$$
\mathbf r\cdot(\mathbf a\wedge\mathbf y)
=\mathbf y\cdot(\mathbf r\wedge\mathbf a).
$$
For $\mathbf r\in L$, this equals $\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf b$, while $\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf b=\mathbf r\cdot(\mathbf r\wedge\mathbf a)=0$. Hence $\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf N=\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf b$ throughout $L$. The <point> $\mathbf y$ also satisfies that equation because $\mathbf y\cdot(\mathbf a\wedge\mathbf y)=0$. Since $\mathbf N\ne\mathbf0$, the required <plane> is therefore exactly
$$
\boxed{\mathbf r\cdot(\mathbf b+\mathbf a\wedge\mathbf y)=\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf b.}
$$
This also proves the reusable <plane through a point and a line given by a cross product> construction.

The three <lines> have the convenient <parametric equations>
$$
L_1:\ (s,0,1),\qquad L_2:\ (1,s,0),\qquad L_3:\ (0,1,s),\qquad s\in\mathbb R.
$$
For $L_1$, substitute $\mathbf a=\mathbf i$, $\mathbf b=\mathbf j$ in the <plane through a point and a line given by a cross product> formula. Its <normal vector> is $\mathbf j+\mathbf i\wedge\mathbf y=(0,1-y_3,y_2)$. Cyclically permuting the <coordinates> gives all three <planes>:
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\Pi_1:&\quad(1-y_3)r_2+y_2r_3=y_2,\\
\Pi_2:&\quad y_3r_1+(1-y_1)r_3=y_3,\\
\Pi_3:&\quad(1-y_2)r_1+y_1r_2=y_1.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Each <normal vector> is nonzero because $\mathbf y$ is not on the corresponding <line>. Thus these are genuine <planes>, and each is unique.

Suppose a <line> $T$ through $\mathbf y$ meets each $L_i$. Its intersection with $L_i$ is different from $\mathbf y$, so $T$ lies in $\Pi_i$. A nonzero <direction vector> $\mathbf d$ for $T$ must therefore satisfy
$$
M\mathbf d=\mathbf0,\qquad
M=\begin{pmatrix}
0&1-y_3&y_2\\
y_3&0&1-y_1\\
1-y_2&y_1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
A square <matrix> admitting a nonzero <vector> $\mathbf d$ with $M\mathbf d=\mathbf0$ has zero <determinant>. Expanding gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
0=\det M
&=(1-y_1)(1-y_2)(1-y_3)+y_1y_2y_3\\
&=1-y_1-y_2-y_3+y_1y_2+y_2y_3+y_3y_1.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the necessary condition is
$$
\boxed{y_1+y_2+y_3-y_1y_2-y_2y_3-y_3y_1=1.}
$$
**The condition is not sufficient.** Take $\mathbf y=(2,1,0)$, which is on none of the three original <lines>. It satisfies the condition, since $2+1-2=1$. The three <plane> equations become
$$
r_2+r_3=1,\qquad -r_3=0,\qquad 2r_2=2.
$$
Their common intersection is the single <line> $T=\{(s,1,0):s\in\mathbb R\}$. Any proposed <line> through $\mathbf y$ meeting all three original <lines> would have to be this $T$. But $T$ never meets $L_1=\{(s,0,1):s\in\mathbb R\}$: their second and third <coordinates> differ. The zero <determinant> guarantees a common direction for the three <planes>; it does not guarantee the required intersections. This is a <parallel obstruction to a transversal of three skew lines>.

By <Newton's second law>, the <central force> obeys
$$
m\ddot{\mathbf r}=-\Phi'(r)\frac{\mathbf r}{r}.
$$
Differentiating the <cross product> gives
$$
\dot{\mathbf n}=\dot{\mathbf r}\wedge\dot{\mathbf r}+\mathbf r\wedge\ddot{\mathbf r}=\mathbf0,
$$
since the <acceleration> is parallel to $\mathbf r$. Hence $\mathbf n=\mathbf n(0)$ is constant. Since $\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n=0$, the entire motion lies in the fixed <plane> through the origin with nonzero <normal vector> $\mathbf n(0)$.

Choose <polar coordinates> in that <plane>, orienting $\theta$ so that its positive <normal vector> points along $\mathbf n(0)$. The <velocity> is
$$
\dot{\mathbf r}=\dot r\,\mathbf e_r+r\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta,
\qquad
\mathbf r\wedge\dot{\mathbf r}=r^2\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_z.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{r^2\dot\theta=h=|\mathbf n(0)|>0.}
$$
Multiplication by $m$ identifies this with <conservation of angular momentum>; geometrically, the swept <area> per unit time is $h/2$.

Taking the <dot product> of <Newton's second law> with the <velocity> gives
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac m2|\dot{\mathbf r}|^2+\Phi(r)\right)
=m\dot{\mathbf r}\cdot\ddot{\mathbf r}+\Phi'(r)\dot r=0.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\frac m2\left(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2\right)+\Phi(r)=E.}
$$
This expresses <conservation of mechanical energy>, the sum of <kinetic energy> and <potential energy>.

Finally, for $u=1/r$ and a prime denoting a <derivative> with respect to $\theta$, the <chain rule> gives
$$
\dot\theta=hu^2,\qquad
\dot r=-u^{-2}u'\dot\theta=-hu',\qquad
r\dot\theta=hu.
$$
Substitution into the conserved <energy> yields
$$
\boxed{\frac{mh^2}{2}\left[\left(\frac{du}{d\theta}\right)^2+u^2\right]+\Phi(1/u)=E.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=2/b}

The initial transverse <velocity> gives $h=a(\sqrt{2k}/a)=\sqrt{2k}$. The initial <mechanical energy> is
$$
E=\frac m2\left(\frac{2k}{a^2}+\frac{2k}{a^2}\right)-\frac{mk}{a^2}
=\frac{mk}{a^2}.
$$
Since $\Phi(1/u)=-mku^2$ and $mh^2/2=mk$, the $u^2$ terms cancel in the conserved <energy> equation:
$$
mk(u')^2=\frac{mk}{a^2}.
$$
The initial radial <velocity> fixes the sign, because $\dot r=-hu'$ gives $u'(0)=1/a$. The <continuous> <derivative> $u'$ cannot change sign while its square remains $1/a^2$. As $u(0)=1/a$, this gives
$$
u=\frac{1+\theta}{a},\qquad
\boxed{r(\theta)=\frac{a}{1+\theta}\quad(\theta\ge0).}
$$
The future <orbit> is a <hyperbolic spiral>: it starts at $(a,0)$, turns counterclockwise and winds indefinitely towards the origin. Its radial <distance> decreases on every turn. The positive-$r$ continuation has $\theta>-1$, but only $\theta\ge0$ is needed after release.

\Image[mathematics-iv-q2-orbit.png]
{title=Future orbit in question 2, scaled by the initial radius}

To decide whether infinitely many turns require infinite time, use the conserved <angular momentum>:
$$
\frac{dt}{d\theta}=\frac{r^2}{h}
=\frac{a^2}{\sqrt{2k}(1+\theta)^2}.
$$
Integrating from $0$ gives
$$
t=\frac{a^2}{\sqrt{2k}}\frac{\theta}{1+\theta}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{t_* =\frac{a^2}{\sqrt{2k}}<\infty,\qquad
r(t)=a-\frac{\sqrt{2k}}a\,t\quad(0\le t<t_*).}
$$
**The particle reaches the origin in finite time**, in the sense that $r\to0$ as $t\uparrow t_*$. Meanwhile $\theta=t/(t_*-t)\to\infty$. The <central force> is undefined at the origin, so these formulas describe motion up to collision. This is <finite-time collision in an inverse-cube central force>.

In <cylindrical coordinates>,
$$
\mathbf e_r=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0),\quad
\mathbf e_\theta=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0),\quad
\dot{\mathbf e}_r=\dot\theta\mathbf e_\theta,\quad
\dot{\mathbf e}_\theta=-\dot\theta\mathbf e_r.
$$
Differentiating the <position vector> $\mathbf r=r\mathbf e_r+z\mathbf e_z$ twice gives the <velocity> and <acceleration>
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot{\mathbf r}&=\dot r\mathbf e_r+r\dot\theta\mathbf e_\theta+\dot z\mathbf e_z,\\
\ddot{\mathbf r}&=(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2)\mathbf e_r
+(r\ddot\theta+2\dot r\dot\theta)\mathbf e_\theta+\ddot z\mathbf e_z.
\end{aligned}
$$
This derives <acceleration in cylindrical coordinates> rather than assuming it.

At a fixed time, the wire has <parametrization> $r\mapsto r\mathbf e_r+f(r)\mathbf e_z$. Its <derivative> is the nonzero <tangent vector>
$$
\mathbf t=\mathbf e_r+f'(r)\mathbf e_z.
$$
A smooth wire exerts a <constraint force> $\mathbf R$ with $\mathbf R\cdot\mathbf t=0$. Because $g$ is the <gravitational acceleration>, the gravitational <force> is $-mg\mathbf e_z$; the printed $-g\mathbf e_z$ is interpreted per unit mass. Projecting <Newton's second law>, $m\ddot{\mathbf r}=\mathbf R-mg\mathbf e_z$, along $\mathbf t$ eliminates $\mathbf R$:
$$
\ddot r-r\omega^2+f'(r)\ddot z=-gf'(r).
$$
The <chain rule> gives $\ddot z=f'(r)\ddot r+f''(r)\dot r^2$. Rearrangement yields
$$
\boxed{\left(1+f'(r)^2\right)\ddot r
=r\omega^2-f'(r)\left(g+f''(r)\dot r^2\right).}
$$
This is the governing equation for a <bead on a uniformly rotating wire>. Its azimuthal <acceleration> contributes nothing to the projection because $\mathbf e_\theta\cdot\mathbf t=0$.

Here <equilibrium> means fixed position relative to the rotating wire: $r=a$ and $z=f(a)$ are constant, while $\theta=\omega t$ continues to change. Substituting $\dot r=\ddot r=0$ into the equation gives
$$
\boxed{f'(a)=\frac{a\omega^2}{g}.}
$$
For the <linear stability> calculation, set $r=a+\epsilon$ and retain terms of first order in $\epsilon$ and $\dot\epsilon$. The term $\dot r^2$ contributes only at second order, and variation of the factor multiplying $\ddot\epsilon$ also contributes only at second order. Thus
$$
\boxed{\left(1+f'(a)^2\right)\ddot\epsilon
=\left(\omega^2-gf''(a)\right)\epsilon.}
$$
The two strict sign cases are treated in the following parts. If $gf''(a)=\omega^2$, this <linearization> has no restoring term and higher orders are needed to classify the <equilibrium>.

For the specified wire,
$$
f'(r)=\frac{\omega^2r}{g}-\sin(r/b),\qquad
f''(r)=\frac{\omega^2}{g}-\frac1b\cos(r/b).
$$
Hence the complete set of positive <equilibrium> radii is
$$
\boxed{a_n=n\pi b,\qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots.}
$$
At $a_n$,
$$
\left[1+\left(\frac{n\pi b\omega^2}{g}\right)^2\right]\ddot\epsilon
=\frac gb(-1)^n\epsilon.
$$
Therefore **odd $n$ give a <stable equilibrium>, and even $n$ give an <unstable equilibrium>**. There is no degenerate sign case in this example. The corresponding heights are $f(a_n)=\omega^2n^2\pi^2b^2/(2g)+b(-1)^n$.

If $gf''(a)>\omega^2$, define
$$
\nu^2=\frac{gf''(a)-\omega^2}{1+f'(a)^2}>0.
$$
The <linearized equation> is $\ddot\epsilon+\nu^2\epsilon=0$, with bounded <simple harmonic motion>
$$
\epsilon(t)=\epsilon(0)\cos(\nu t)+\frac{\dot\epsilon(0)}\nu\sin(\nu t).
$$
Thus the strict <stable equilibrium> condition is
$$
\boxed{f''(a)>\frac{\omega^2}{g}.}
$$
This is also a sufficient condition for local nonlinear <Lyapunov stability>. Indeed the equation for the <bead on a uniformly rotating wire> has the <conserved quantity>
$$
\mathcal H=\frac12\left(1+f'(r)^2\right)\dot r^2+gf(r)-\frac12\omega^2r^2.
$$
Its <derivative> is $\dot r$ times the governing equation moved to one side, so it vanishes. At $a$, the last two terms have zero first <derivative> and positive second <derivative> $gf''(a)-\omega^2$. Their strict local minimum traps sufficiently small perturbations. The motion is bounded rather than asymptotically returning to rest.

If $gf''(a)<\omega^2$, put
$$
\lambda^2=\frac{\omega^2-gf''(a)}{1+f'(a)^2}>0.

The <linearized equation> becomes $\ddot\epsilon-\lambda^2\epsilon=0$, so
$$
\epsilon(t)=C_1e^{\lambda t}+C_2e^{-\lambda t}.
$$
Arbitrarily small initial perturbations can have $C_1\ne0$ and grow exponentially. Hence the strict <unstable equilibrium> condition is
$$
\boxed{f''(a)<\frac{\omega^2}{g}.}
$$
For the particular wire, this occurs precisely at $a=2j\pi b$, $j=1,2,\ldots$; the alternating radii $a=(2j-1)\pi b$ satisfy the <stable equilibrium> condition.

Define the classical axial spin by the <axial angular velocity of a symmetric top>,
$$
\boxed{n=\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf e.}
$$
Since $\mathbf e$ is a <unit vector>, $\mathbf e\cdot\dot{\mathbf e}=0$. The <vector triple product> identity applied to $\dot{\mathbf e}=\boldsymbol\omega\wedge\mathbf e$ gives
$$
\mathbf e\wedge\dot{\mathbf e}
=\boldsymbol\omega-(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf e)\mathbf e,
\qquad
\boldsymbol\omega=\mathbf e\wedge\dot{\mathbf e}+n\mathbf e.
$$
The <inertia tensor> multiplies the component perpendicular to $\mathbf e$ by $A$ and the component along $\mathbf e$ by $C$. Therefore the <angular momentum> about $Q$ is
$$
\mathbf L_Q=A\mathbf e\wedge\dot{\mathbf e}+Cn\mathbf e.
$$
Its time <derivative> is
$$
\dot{\mathbf L}_Q=A\mathbf e\wedge\ddot{\mathbf e}+C\dot n\mathbf e+Cn\dot{\mathbf e},
$$
because $\dot{\mathbf e}\wedge\dot{\mathbf e}=\mathbf0$.

The gravitational <torque> about $Q$ is $h\mathbf e\wedge(-mg\mathbf k)$, and the applied <torque> is $\ell\mathbf e\wedge(\kappa\mathbf s)$. The support <force> acts at $Q$, so has zero <torque> about $Q$. The <angular momentum> equation is consequently
$$
\boxed{A\mathbf e\wedge\ddot{\mathbf e}+C\dot n\mathbf e+Cn\dot{\mathbf e}
=-mgh\mathbf e\wedge\mathbf k+\kappa\ell\mathbf e\wedge\mathbf s.}
$$

{parent=4}

= Solution
{parent=4/b}
{c}

Write $c=\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf e$. Since $\mathbf s=\ell(\mathbf k-\mathbf e)$, the total <torque> simplifies to
$$
\boldsymbol\tau=(\kappa\ell^2-mgh)\mathbf e\wedge\mathbf k.
$$
Taking the <dot product> of the equation of motion with $\mathbf e$ gives $C\dot n=0$: both <torque> terms are perpendicular to $\mathbf e$, as are the other terms on the left. Hence
$$
\boxed{n=\text{constant}.}
$$
Taking the <dot product> of $\dot{\mathbf L}_Q=\boldsymbol\tau$ with the fixed <vector> $\mathbf k$ gives
$$
\dot p=\mathbf k\cdot\dot{\mathbf L}_Q=0,\qquad
\boxed{p=\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf L_Q=\text{constant}.}
$$
These are conservation of the axial spin and of the vertical component of <angular momentum>.

For <energy>, the <kinetic energy> and <potential energy> are
$$
T=\frac12\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf L_Q
=\frac A2|\dot{\mathbf e}|^2+\frac C2n^2,
\qquad
V=mghc+\frac\kappa2|\mathbf s|^2
=mghc+\kappa\ell^2(1-c).
$$
Dot the equation of motion, with $\dot n=0$, with $\mathbf e\wedge\dot{\mathbf e}$. The <cross product> identity $(\mathbf e\wedge\mathbf u)\cdot(\mathbf e\wedge\mathbf v)=\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v-(\mathbf e\cdot\mathbf u)(\mathbf e\cdot\mathbf v)$ gives
$$
A\ddot{\mathbf e}\cdot\dot{\mathbf e}
=(\kappa\ell^2-mgh)\mathbf k\cdot\dot{\mathbf e}.
$$
It follows that
$$
\dot T=(\kappa\ell^2-mgh)\dot c,\qquad
\dot V=(mgh-\kappa\ell^2)\dot c,
$$
so
$$
\boxed{E=T+V=\frac12\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf L_Q+mgh\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf e+\frac\kappa2|\mathbf s|^2=\text{constant}.}
$$
The applied <force> is conservative even if $\kappa$ is negative: its <potential energy> is $\kappa|\mathbf s|^2/2$. This is a <symmetric top with a quadratic alignment potential>.

For steady <precession> at rate $\Omega$ about the fixed vertical <unit vector> $\mathbf k$,
$$
\dot{\mathbf e}=\Omega\mathbf k\wedge\mathbf e,\qquad
\ddot{\mathbf e}=\Omega^2(\cos\alpha\,\mathbf k-\mathbf e).
$$
Consequently $\mathbf e\wedge\ddot{\mathbf e}=\Omega^2\cos\alpha\,\mathbf e\wedge\mathbf k$ and $\dot{\mathbf e}=-\Omega\mathbf e\wedge\mathbf k$. Since the <axial angular velocity of a symmetric top> $n$ is constant, substitution in the equation of motion gives
$$
\left(A\Omega^2\cos\alpha-Cn\Omega\right)\mathbf e\wedge\mathbf k
=(\kappa\ell^2-mgh)\mathbf e\wedge\mathbf k.
$$
As $0<\alpha<\pi/2$, the <cross product> can be cancelled. For a specified axial spin, the required value is
$$
\boxed{\kappa=\frac{mgh+A\Omega^2\cos\alpha-Cn\Omega}{\ell^2}.}
$$
The corresponding total <angular velocity> is
$$
\boldsymbol\omega=\Omega\mathbf k+(n-\Omega\cos\alpha)\mathbf e.
$$
If the stated <angular velocity> $\Omega\mathbf k$ is the total <angular velocity> of the top, then $n=\Omega\cos\alpha$ and the requested value specializes to
$$
\boxed{\kappa=\frac{mgh+(A-C)\Omega^2\cos\alpha}{\ell^2}.}
$$
This specialization permits a direct check: $\mathbf L_Q=A\Omega\mathbf k+(C-A)\Omega\cos\alpha\,\mathbf e$, whose time <derivative> is $(A-C)\Omega^2\cos\alpha\,\mathbf e\wedge\mathbf k$, exactly the required <torque>. The first formula keeps the separate axial spin explicit when $\Omega$ is understood as the rate of <precession>.

If $A$ has $m$ rows and $n$ columns, attach a nonnegative <dual variable> $y_i$ to each of the $m$ upper-bound constraints. The <dual linear program> is
$$
\boxed{\text{minimise }\mathbf b^T\mathbf y
\quad\text{subject to }A^T\mathbf y\ge\mathbf c,\quad\mathbf y\ge\mathbf0.}
$$
Here $A^T$ is the <transpose> of $A$, and all <vector> inequalities hold componentwise. Nonnegative <dual variables> preserve the direction of the original inequalities when taking weighted sums.

= ii
{parent=5/a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=5/a/ii}
{c}

For <feasible solutions> $\mathbf x,\mathbf y$, nonnegativity gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf c^T\mathbf x
\le (A^T\mathbf y)^T\mathbf x
=\mathbf y^TA\mathbf x
\le\mathbf y^T\mathbf b
=\mathbf b^T\mathbf y.}
$$
The first inequality uses $A^T\mathbf y\ge\mathbf c$ and $\mathbf x\ge\mathbf0$; the second uses $A\mathbf x\le\mathbf b$ and $\mathbf y\ge\mathbf0$. This is <weak duality>.

= iii
{parent=5/a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=5/a/iii}
{c}

For every primal <feasible solution> $\mathbf x$, <weak duality> gives
$$
\mathbf c^T\mathbf x\le\mathbf b^T\mathbf y^*=\mathbf c^T\mathbf x^*.
$$
Thus no primal <feasible solution> improves on $\mathbf x^*$. Similarly, for every dual <feasible solution> $\mathbf y$,
$$
\mathbf b^T\mathbf y\ge\mathbf c^T\mathbf x^*=\mathbf b^T\mathbf y^*.
$$
Therefore **both candidates are optimal**, with the same <objective function> value. Equality is a <linear programming optimality certificate> and does not require a separate existence argument.

The <complementary slackness> conditions are
$$
\boxed{y_i\bigl(b_i-(A\mathbf x)_i\bigr)=0\quad(1\le i\le m),\qquad
x_j\bigl((A^T\mathbf y)_j-c_j\bigr)=0\quad(1\le j\le n).}
$$
To see their role, decompose the <duality gap> as
$$
\mathbf b^T\mathbf y-\mathbf c^T\mathbf x
=\sum_i y_i\bigl(b_i-(A\mathbf x)_i\bigr)
+\sum_j x_j\bigl((A^T\mathbf y)_j-c_j\bigr).
$$
Every summand is nonnegative for <feasible solutions>, so the gap is zero exactly when every displayed product vanishes. Zero gap implies optimality by <weak duality>. Conversely, <strong duality> states that primal and dual optima, when both are attained, have equal values, and hence satisfy these conditions.

= Solution

The inequality $3y_1+y_2\ge6$, together with $y_2\ge0$, implies
$$
y_1+y_2=\frac13(3y_1+y_2)+\frac23y_2\ge2>1.
$$
Also $-y_1+y_2\ge-1$ implies
$$
-y_1+3y_2=(-y_1+y_2)+2y_2\ge-1>-5.
$$
Thus **both inequalities are strictly slack throughout the feasible region**. They are redundant: the remaining inequalities imply them, even with strict inequality. By <complementary slackness>, any primal optimum must therefore have $x_1=x_3=0$; this is the principle that <strictly slack dual constraints force zero primal variables>.

(0,6),\qquad (4/3,2),\qquad (16/7,9/7).

Along its three successive pieces, the <objective function> $2y_1+y_2$ has respective slopes $-1$, $5/4$ and $3$ as $y_1$ increases. Thus it decreases to the middle <extreme point> and increases thereafter. For fixed $y_1$, moving above the boundary only increases the <objective function>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbf y^*=(4/3,2),\qquad \min(2y_1+y_2)=\frac{14}{3}.}
$$
As an algebraic check of the graphical answer, the active inequalities give
$$
2y_1+y_2=\frac19(3y_1+4y_2)+\frac59(3y_1+y_2)
\ge\frac{12}{9}+\frac{30}{9}=\frac{14}{3},
$$
with equality at the stated <extreme point>.

$$
Solving this <linear system> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf x^*=\left(0,0,0,\frac19,\frac59\right),\qquad

All components are nonnegative, the two original upper bounds are met exactly, and the <objective function> equals the dual value. The <weak duality> bound therefore certifies optimality. At any division step $u=qv+r$, an <integer> divides both $u,v$ exactly when it divides both $v,r$: one direction uses $r=u-qv$, and the other uses $u=qv+r$. Hence
$$
\gcd(u,v)=\gcd(v,r).
$$
Starting with $r_0=a$, $r_1=b$, the <Euclidean division> produces
$$
r_{j-1}=q_jr_j+r_{j+1},\qquad 0\le r_{j+1}<r_j.
$$
The nonzero <remainders> form a strictly decreasing sequence of positive <integers>. Such a sequence must terminate, since there are only finitely many positive <integers> below $b$. Suppose $r_N$ is the last nonzero <remainder>, so $r_{N+1}=0$. The unchanged <greatest common divisor> at each step gives
$$
\boxed{\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(r_1,r_2)=\cdots=\gcd(r_N,0)=r_N.}
$$
The final equality holds because the positive common <divisors> of $r_N$ and $0$ are precisely the positive <divisors> of $r_N$. This proves both termination and correctness of the <Euclidean algorithm>.

Apply the <Euclidean algorithm> to $19$ and $11$:

Thus $(x,y)=(-4,7)$ is one solution of the <Diophantine equation>. Subtracting this solution from any other gives

\boxed{x=-4+11t,\qquad y=7-19t,\qquad t\in\mathbb Z.}

Every such pair satisfies the original <Diophantine equation>, since the $t$ terms cancel.

First recall why the two-variable <Bézout's identity> follows from the <Euclidean algorithm>. The initial values $a,b$ are <integer linear combinations> of $a,b$. At every subsequent step, $r_{j+1}=r_{j-1}-q_jr_j$ is again an <integer linear combination> of $a,b$. Therefore the last nonzero <remainder>, which is their <greatest common divisor>, has that form.

Put $e=\gcd(a,b)$. The preceding argument supplies <integers> $u,v$ with $e=au+bv$. Applying the same argument to $e,c$ gives <integers> $p,q$ such that
$$
\gcd(e,c)=ep+cq.
$$
An <integer> divides $a,b,c$ exactly when it divides $e,c$: if it divides $a,b$, it divides $e=au+bv$, while $e$ itself divides both $a,b$. Hence $\gcd(e,c)=\gcd(a,b,c)=d$. Substituting the expression for $e$ proves
$$
d=(au+bv)p+cq=a(up)+b(vp)+cq.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{x=up,\qquad y=vp,\qquad z=q}
$$
are <integers> satisfying $ax+by+cz=d$. This also explains how to construct the coefficients using two applications of the <Euclidean algorithm>.

For $0\le x\le7$, we have $2\le2+x\le9$. Taking the nonnegative <square root> gives

Set $H(x)=g(x)-x$. This <function> is <continuous> on $[a,b]$. Since $g$ takes values in $[a,b]$,
$$
H(a)=g(a)-a\ge0,\qquad H(b)=g(b)-b\le0.
$$
The <intermediate value theorem> therefore supplies $\xi\in[a,b]$ with $H(\xi)=0$, equivalently $g(\xi)=\xi$. This includes the possibility that one endpoint is already a <fixed point>.

For uniqueness, the <mean value theorem> gives, for any $x,y\in[a,b]$,
$$
|g(x)-g(y)|\le\gamma|x-y|.
$$
If both $\xi$ and $\eta$ are <fixed points>, then
$$
|\xi-\eta|=|g(\xi)-g(\eta)|\le\gamma|\xi-\eta|.
$$
Since $\gamma<1$, this forces $|\xi-\eta|=0$. Thus **there exists exactly one <fixed point>**. When $a=b$, the conclusion follows immediately from $g(a)=a$; otherwise the argument above applies. The <contraction mapping> estimate also proves convergence of the associated <fixed-point iteration>, as used next.

The <fixed-point iteration> stays inside $[0,7]$ because $G$ maps that interval to itself. A <fixed point> satisfies $\xi=\sqrt{2+\xi}$, so $\xi\ge0$ and
$$
\xi^2-\xi-2=(\xi-2)(\xi+1)=0.
$$
Only $\xi=2$ is admissible; $-1$ fails the unsquared equation. With $q=1/(2\sqrt2)$, the <mean value theorem> gives the <error bound>
$$
|x_{k+1}-2|=|G(x_k)-G(2)|\le q|x_k-2|.
$$
Induction yields $|x_k-2|\le q^k|x_0-2|\le5q^k\to0$, since $0<q<1$. Therefore, for every allowed starting value,
$$
\boxed{\lim_{k\to\infty}x_k=2.}
$$
This estimate proves convergence directly, without assuming in advance that the sequence has a limit.

For <Newton's method for nonlinear systems>, solve $J(\mathbf z_0)\boldsymbol\delta=-\mathbf F(\mathbf z_0)$ and set $\mathbf z_1=\mathbf z_0+\boldsymbol\delta$. The <Jacobian matrix> here is
$$
J(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}
3x^2+3&-2\\
3x^2&6y-1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
At $(0,0)$,
$$
\mathbf F(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
J(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}3&-2\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The correction <linear system> is $3\delta_x-2\delta_y=1$, $-\delta_y=1$. Hence $\delta_y=-1$, $\delta_x=-1/3$, and one iteration gives
$$
\boxed{(x_1,y_1)=(-1/3,-1).}
$$
At the alternative starting value $(1,0)$,
$$
\mathbf F(1,0)=\begin{pmatrix}3\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
J(1,0)=\begin{pmatrix}6&-2\\3&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
This <Jacobian matrix> is a <singular matrix>: its first row is twice its second. In fact the correction equations would be
$$
6\delta_x-2\delta_y=-3,\qquad 3\delta_x-\delta_y=0,
$$
which are inconsistent, since twice the second left side equals the first while twice its right side is $0$, rather than $-3$. Thus **Newton's iteration cannot take its first step from $(1,0)$**. This is failure of the correction <linear system>, not merely nonuniqueness of a possible correction.
