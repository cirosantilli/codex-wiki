= Solution

Call $k$ a <peak of a sequence> when $a_k\geq a_m$ for every $m>k$. If there are infinitely many <peaks of a sequence>, list their indices in increasing order, $k_1<k_2<\cdots$. Since $k_{r+1}>k_r$,
$$
\boxed{a_{k_1}\geq a_{k_2}\geq\cdots.}
$$
This is a decreasing <subsequence>; throughout this argument, a decreasing <monotone sequence> may have equal successive terms.

If there are only finitely many <peaks of a sequence>, choose $N$ beyond all their indices; take $N=1$ if there are none. Every $n\geq N$ fails to be a <peak of a sequence>, so some $m>n$ satisfies $a_m>a_n$. Starting with $n_1=N$, choose such an $n_{r+1}>n_r$ recursively. This gives
$$
\boxed{a_{n_1}<a_{n_2}<\cdots.}
$$
The two cases prove the <monotone subsequence theorem> without any boundedness hypothesis.

= Solution

The <Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem> states: **every <bounded sequence> of <real numbers> has a <convergent subsequence>.**

By the <monotone subsequence theorem> proved in the preceding part, a <bounded sequence> $(a_n)$ has a <monotone subsequence> $(b_r)$. It inherits the bounds of $(a_n)$. For completeness, the <bounded monotone sequence theorem> follows directly from <completeness of the real numbers>. If $(b_r)$ is increasing, let $L=\sup\{b_r:r\geq1\}$. For every $\varepsilon>0$, the definition of <supremum> supplies $r_0$ with $L-\varepsilon<b_{r_0}\leq L$. Then
$$
r\geq r_0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad L-\varepsilon<b_{r_0}\leq b_r\leq L,
$$
so $b_r\to L$. If $(b_r)$ is decreasing, apply the increasing case to $(-b_r)$, or use its <infimum>. Thus in either case
$$
\boxed{\text{some }n_1<n_2<\cdots\text{ and }L\in\mathbb R\text{ satisfy }a_{n_r}\longrightarrow L.}
$$

It is useful to prove the <contrapositive>. Suppose $k$ is a <peak of a sequence> $(a_n)$ and $m$ is a <peak of a sequence> $(-a_n)$. The second condition says $a_j\geq a_m$ for every $j>m$. Hence
$$
j>\max\{k,m\}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a_m\leq a_j\leq a_k.
$$
Only finitely many earlier terms remain, and their <absolute values> have a finite <maximum>. Together these give a bound for every term, so $(a_n)$ is a <bounded sequence>. Consequently,
$$
\boxed{(a_n)\text{ unbounded}\ \Longrightarrow\ (a_n)\text{ or }(-a_n)\text{ has no peaks}.}
$$

If $a_n\to L$, every <subsequence> also has <limit of a sequence> $L$: its increasing indices eventually exceed any prescribed threshold. This applies in particular to every <monotone subsequence>.

Conversely, suppose $(a_n)$ is a <bounded sequence> and all its <monotone subsequences> converge to the same $L$. At least one exists by the <monotone subsequence theorem>. If $a_n$ did not tend to $L$, there would be $\varepsilon_0>0$ and a <subsequence> $(a_{n_j})$ such that
$$
|a_{n_j}-L|\geq\varepsilon_0\qquad(j\geq1).
$$
Apply the <monotone subsequence theorem> to $(a_{n_j})$. The resulting further <subsequence> is also a <monotone subsequence> of $(a_n)$, so it tends to $L$, contradicting the displayed bound. Therefore
$$
\boxed{a_n\to L\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \text{every monotone subsequence tends to }L.}
$$
The <bounded monotone sequence theorem> ensures that these <subsequences> have finite <limits of sequences>; the hypothesis requires those values to agree.

Suppose for a contradiction that $a_n\to L$. Starting at $a_N$, recursively select a strictly increasing <subsequence> and a strictly decreasing <subsequence>. $$
L\geq u>a_N,\qquad L\leq v<a_N,
$$
a contradiction. Hence **$(a_n)$ does not converge.** This argument includes the possibility that either <set> of <peaks of a sequence> is empty.

Enumerate both <sets> of <peaks of a sequence> in increasing order of index. If $a_n\to L$, the two selected <subsequences> both tend to $L$.

For the converse, suppose $a_{k_r}\to L$ and $a_{m_r}\to L$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $r$ large enough that both selected terms differ from $L$ by less than $\varepsilon$. For every $n>\max\{k_r,m_r\}$, the two <peak of a sequence> conditions imply
$$
\boxed{L-\varepsilon<a_{m_r}\leq a_n\leq a_{k_r}<L+\varepsilon.}
$$
The lower bound comes from the <peak of a sequence> of $(-a_n)$ at $m_r$. This is precisely the definition of $a_n\to L$. Thus **convergence is equivalent to a common finite <limit of a sequence> for the upper and lower peak <subsequences>.**

A <sequence> $(a_n)$ of <real numbers> is a <convergent sequence> with <limit of a sequence> $\ell$ when
$$
\boxed{\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\in\mathbb N\ \forall n\geq N:\quad |a_n-\ell|<\varepsilon.}
$$
Divergence to positive <infinity> means
$$
\boxed{\forall M\in\mathbb R\ \exists N\in\mathbb N\ \forall n\geq N:\quad a_n>M.}
$$
In both definitions the same $N$ must control every later term. The second condition is not convergence to a <real number>.

For a <series> of <complex numbers>, form the <partial sums> $S_N=\sum_{n=1}^Na_n$. The <series> converges when these <partial sums> have a finite <limit of a sequence> $S\in\mathbb C$:
$$
\boxed{\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N_0\ \forall N\geq N_0:\quad |S_N-S|<\varepsilon.}
$$
The <series> has <absolute convergence> when $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_n|$ converges as a <series> of nonnegative <real numbers>. A <series> beginning at $n=0$ uses the same definitions with that starting index.

Let $T_N=\sum_{n=1}^N|a_n|$ and $S_N=\sum_{n=1}^Na_n$. If $(T_N)$ converges, it is a <Cauchy sequence>. Thus, for every $\varepsilon>0$, some $N$ makes
$$
\left|S_q-S_p\right|=\left|\sum_{n=p+1}^qa_n\right|\leq\sum_{n=p+1}^q|a_n|=T_q-T_p<\varepsilon
\qquad(q>p\geq N),
$$
where the <triangle inequality> gives the bound. Therefore $(S_N)$ is a <Cauchy sequence> in the <complex numbers>. These form a <complete metric space>, so $(S_N)$ has a finite <limit of a sequence>. Equivalently, apply the <Cauchy criterion for series>. Hence **<absolute convergence> implies convergence.**

The <radius of convergence> of a <power series> $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nz^n$ is the number $R\in[0,\infty]$ such that the <series> has <absolute convergence> for $|z|<R$ and diverges for $|z|>R$. Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{R=\sup\left\{r\geq0:\ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|a_n|r^n<\infty\right\}.}
$$
Use $r^0=1$, including at $r=0$. The <supremum> may be $+\infty$. There is no general conclusion at $|z|=R$: boundary convergence depends on the <coefficients>. If $R=0$, only the centre is guaranteed; if $R=\infty$, every finite $z$ lies inside the <disk of convergence>.

{parent=b}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

The <comparison test for series> states that, for $0\leq u_n\leq v_n$ eventually, convergence of $\sum v_n$ implies convergence of $\sum u_n$. Equivalently, divergence of $\sum u_n$ implies divergence of $\sum v_n$.

For $|z|<1$, the coefficient bound gives
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_nz^n|\leq M\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|z|^n<\infty
$$
by the <geometric series> formula and the <comparison test for series>. The single term $a_0$ does not affect convergence. For $|z|\geq1$,
$$
|a_nz^n|>m|z|^n\geq m>0\qquad(n\geq1),
$$
so the terms do not tend to zero. The fact that <terms of a convergent series tend to zero> excludes convergence there. Thus
$$
\boxed{R=1.}
$$

The upper bound and the <comparison test for series> give <absolute convergence> when $\sqrt2|z|<1$. The lower bound prevents the terms tending to zero when $\sqrt2|z|\geq1$. Hence the <radius of convergence> is exactly $1/\sqrt2$.

A <function> $f:E\to\mathbb R$ is <continuous> on $E$ if it is <continuous> at every $p\in E$, meaning
$$
\boxed{\forall p\in E\ \forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0\ \forall x\in E:\quad |x-p|<\delta\ \Longrightarrow\ |f(x)-f(p)|<\varepsilon.}
$$
The <function> has <uniform continuity> on $E$ when
$$
\boxed{\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0\ \forall x,y\in E:\quad |x-y|<\delta\ \Longrightarrow\ |f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon.}
$$
For <continuity>, $\delta$ may depend on both $p$ and $\varepsilon$. For <uniform continuity>, one $\delta$ must work for every pair of points in $E$.

= Solution

Suppose the <continuous function> $f$ did not have <uniform continuity> on $[a,b]$. Negating its definition gives $\varepsilon_0>0$ such that for every $n\geq1$ there are $x_n,y_n\in[a,b]$ with
$$
|x_n-y_n|<1/n,\qquad |f(x_n)-f(y_n)|\geq\varepsilon_0.
$$
The <Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem>, which states that every <bounded sequence> of <real numbers> has a <convergent subsequence>, gives $x_{n_k}\to p$. The inequalities $a\leq x_{n_k}\leq b$ imply $p\in[a,b]$. By the <triangle inequality>,
$$
|y_{n_k}-p|\leq|y_{n_k}-x_{n_k}|+|x_{n_k}-p|\longrightarrow0.
$$
Since <continuous functions preserve convergent sequences>, both $f(x_{n_k})$ and $f(y_{n_k})$ tend to $f(p)$. Their difference therefore tends to zero, contradicting its <absolute value> being at least $\varepsilon_0$. This proves the <Heine-Cantor theorem> in the required setting: **$f$ has <uniform continuity> on $[a,b]$.**

Write the <partial sums> as $S_N(x)=\sum_{n=1}^Nf_n(x)$. The <series> has <uniform convergence> to $s:E\to\mathbb R$ if
$$
\boxed{\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N_0\in\mathbb N\ \forall N\geq N_0\ \forall x\in E:\quad |S_N(x)-s(x)|<\varepsilon.}

The <Weierstrass M-test> states: if <functions> $f_n:E\to\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$ satisfy
$$
|f_n(x)|\leq M_n\quad\text{for every }x\in E,
\qquad M_n\geq0,
\qquad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}M_n<\infty,
$$
then $\sum f_n$ has <uniform convergence> and <absolute convergence> on $E$. In fact $\sum|f_n|$ also has <uniform convergence>, since every tail is bounded by the corresponding tail of $\sum M_n$:
$$
\boxed{\sup_{x\in E}\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}|f_n(x)|\leq\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}M_n\longrightarrow0.}
$$

Take $0\leq r<R$. At the point $r$, the <power series> has <absolute convergence>, so $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|a_n|r^n<\infty$. On the whole <interval> $[-r,r]$,
$$
|a_nx^n|\leq|a_n|r^n.
$$
The <Weierstrass M-test> with $M_n=|a_n|r^n$ proves **<uniform convergence> on $[-r,r]$.** It also gives the explicit tail estimate
$$
\boxed{\sup_{|x|\leq r}\left|\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}a_nx^n\right|\leq\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}|a_n|r^n\longrightarrow0.}
$$
For $r=0$ the <interval> consists only of zero, and all positive-degree terms vanish. If $R=\infty$, the same argument applies to every finite $r$; if $R=0$, there is no $r\geq0$ with $r<R$.

\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f_n(x)g(x)=g(x)s(x)\quad\text{uniformly on }E.}

{parent=ii}
{c}

Choose the unbounded <function> $\boxed{g(x)=1/x}$ on $E=(0,1/2]$. It is unbounded because $g(1/k)=k$ for all <integers> $k\geq2$. For each $x\in E$, the <geometric series> gives the sum and its remainder after degree $N$:
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^ng(x)=\frac1{x(1-x)},
\qquad
\frac1{x(1-x)}-\sum_{n=0}^Nx^{n-1}=\frac{x^N}{1-x}.
$$
Since $0<x\leq1/2$,
$$
\boxed{\sup_{x\in E}\left|\frac1{x(1-x)}-\sum_{n=0}^Nx^{n-1}\right|\leq2^{1-N}\longrightarrow0.}
$$
This proves <uniform convergence>. Although the first term $1/x$ and the sum are unbounded, the late tails have a bound tending to zero independently of $x$. <Uniform convergence> concerns those tails and does not require the individual <functions> to be bounded.

For real-valued <functions>, the <uniform Cauchy criterion> applied to <partial sums> says that $\sum u_n$ has <uniform convergence> exactly when
$$
\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall q>p\geq N\ \forall x\in E:\quad\left|\sum_{n=p+1}^q u_n(x)\right|<\varepsilon.
$$
Sufficiency uses <completeness of the real numbers>.

Let $M$ be a common bound for the <uniformly bounded family of functions> $(g_n)$; increase it if necessary so that $M>0$. The assumed <uniform absolute convergence> of $\sum f_n$ gives $N$ such that
$$
\sum_{n=p+1}^q|f_n(x)|<\frac{\varepsilon}{M+1}
\qquad(q>p\geq N,\ x\in E).
$$
The <triangle inequality> now yields
$$
\boxed{\left|\sum_{n=p+1}^q f_n(x)g_n(x)\right|\leq M\sum_{n=p+1}^q|f_n(x)|<\varepsilon.}
$$
The <uniform Cauchy criterion> therefore proves **<uniform convergence> of $\sum f_ng_n$.** The same estimate with $|f_ng_n|$ proves <uniform absolute convergence> as well.

A <function> $f:(a,b)\to\mathbb R$ is <differentiable> on $(a,b)$ when, at each $c\in(a,b)$, its <difference quotient> has a finite <limit of a function>:
$$
\boxed{f'(c)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}h\in\mathbb R.}
$$
Here $h\ne0$ and $c+h\in(a,b)$.

For sufficiently small nonzero $h$, the <difference quotient> has <absolute value> at most $|f'(c)|+1$, because it tends to $f'(c)$. Consequently
$$
|f(c+h)-f(c)|\leq|h|\bigl(|f'(c)|+1\bigr)\longrightarrow0.
$$
This is <continuity> at $c$. Since $c$ was arbitrary, **<differentiability> implies <continuity> on $(a,b)$.**

On $(0,1)$, the <product rule> and <chain rule> give $f'(x)=2x\sin(1/x)-\cos(1/x)$. On $(-1,0)$, the <function> is constant and has <derivative> zero. At zero,
$$
\frac{f(h)-f(0)}h=\begin{cases}h\sin(1/h),&h>0,\\0,&h<0.\end{cases}
$$
Its <absolute value> is at most $|h|$, so the <squeeze theorem> gives $f'(0)=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{f'(x)=\begin{cases}2x\sin(1/x)-\cos(1/x),&0<x<1,\\0,&-1<x\leq0.\end{cases}}
$$
For $x_n=1/(2\pi n)$, we have $x_n\to0$ but $f'(x_n)=-1$ for every $n$. This differs from $f'(0)=0$, so $f'$ is not <continuous> at zero. Indeed, $y_n=1/((2n+1)\pi)$ gives $f'(y_n)=1$, so the right-hand <limit of a function> does not exist. Thus **$f$ is <differentiable> everywhere on $(-1,1)$, while $f'$ is discontinuous at zero.**

Fix $d$ strictly between $f'(a)$ and $f'(b)$, and set $F(x)=f(x)-dx$. The <function> $F$ is <continuous> on $[a,b]$: interior <differentiability> gives interior <continuity>, and the finite one-sided <derivatives> give <continuity> at the endpoints. The <extreme value theorem> therefore gives a point $c$ where $F$ attains its <minimum>.

Since $F'_+(a)=f'(a)-d<0$, for sufficiently small $h>0$ we have $F(a+h)<F(a)$, so $a$ cannot be a minimizing point. Since $F'_-(b)=f'(b)-d>0$, the quotient $(F(b-h)-F(b))/(-h)$ is positive for sufficiently small $h>0$, giving $F(b-h)<F(b)$. Thus $b$ cannot be a minimizing point either, and $c\in(a,b)$.

<Fermat's theorem on stationary points> states that a <differentiable function> at an interior <local minimum> or <local maximum> has <derivative> zero there. Applying it to $F$ gives
$$
\boxed{0=F'(c)=f'(c)-d,\qquad f'(c)=d.}
$$
This proves the relevant case of <Darboux's theorem>; the argument uses <continuity> of $f$, with no assumption of <continuity> of $f'$.

Choose $h_n=\min\{1/n,(b-c)/2\}>0$. Then $h_n\to0$ and $[c,c+h_n]\subset(a,b)$. The <mean value theorem> states that a <continuous function> on a <closed interval>, <differentiable> in its interior, has an interior <derivative> equal to its endpoint <difference quotient>. It gives $x_n\in(c,c+h_n)$ with
$$
f'(x_n)=\frac{f(c+h_n)-f(c)}{h_n}.
$$
Here $x_n\ne c$, $x_n\to c$, and the right-hand side tends to $f'(c)$ by <differentiability> at $c$. If $L=\lim_{x\to c}f'(x)$ exists, the same values $f'(x_n)$ must tend to $L$. Uniqueness of the <limit of a sequence> gives
$$
\boxed{\lim_{x\to c}f'(x)=f'(c).}
$$
Thus a <derivative> cannot have a <removable discontinuity> at a point where the original <function> is <differentiable>. The proof also excludes an infinite <limit of a function> there, since the displayed <difference quotients> have a finite <limit of a sequence>.

The <continuous function> $|h|$ attains a finite <maximum> $M$ on $[a,b]$ by the <extreme value theorem>. Additivity and the <triangle inequality> for the <Riemann integral> imply, for any $x,y\in[a,b]$,
$$
\boxed{|H(y)-H(x)|=\left|\int_x^yh(t)\,dt\right|\leq M|y-x|.}
$$
For reversed endpoints, reverse the orientation of the <integral> before taking its <absolute value>. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $\delta=\varepsilon/(M+1)$. Then $|x-y|<\delta$ implies $|H(x)-H(y)|<\varepsilon$. Thus **$H$ is <continuous> on $[a,b]$**, including one-sided <continuity> at the endpoints; it is even <Lipschitz continuous>. If $a=b$, the <function> is defined on one point and the conclusion is immediate.

Write $G=\int_a^bg(t)\,dt\geq0$ and $I=\int_a^bf(t)g(t)\,dt$. By the <extreme value theorem>, $f$ attains a <minimum> $m$ and a <maximum> $M$ on $[a,b]$. Because $g\geq0$, the <monotonicity of the Riemann integral> gives
$$
mG\leq I\leq MG.
$$
If $G=0$, this forces $I=0$, and every $c\in[a,b]$ satisfies the required identity. This includes $a=b$.

If $G>0$, divide to obtain $m\leq I/G\leq M$. The <intermediate value theorem>, applied between points where the <continuous function> $f$ attains $m$ and $M$, supplies $c\in[a,b]$ with $f(c)=I/G$. Hence the <weighted mean value theorem for integrals> gives
$$
\boxed{\int_a^bf(t)g(t)\,dt=f(c)\int_a^bg(t)\,dt.}
$$

Again let $G=\int_a^bg(t)\,dt\geq0$ and $I=\int_a^bf(t)g(t)\,dt$. Since $f$ is a nondecreasing <monotonic function>, $f(a)\leq f(t)\leq f(b)$ on $[a,b]$. The condition $g\geq0$ and the <monotonicity of the Riemann integral> give
$$
f(a)G\leq I\leq f(b)G.
$$
Set $H(x)=\int_a^xg(t)\,dt$. By part (a), $H$ is <continuous>. Therefore the <function>
$$
K(x)=f(a)H(x)+f(b)\bigl(G-H(x)\bigr)
$$
is <continuous> on $[a,b]$. Its endpoint values are $K(a)=f(b)G$ and $K(b)=f(a)G$, which enclose $I$. The <intermediate value theorem> provides $d\in[a,b]$ with $K(d)=I$, so
$$
\boxed{\int_a^bf(t)g(t)\,dt=f(a)\int_a^dg(t)\,dt+f(b)\int_d^bg(t)\,dt.}
$$
If the two endpoint values coincide, the preceding bounds force $I$ to equal that common value and either endpoint works. Thus the proof also covers $G=0$, $f(a)=f(b)$ and $a=b$, without dividing by any possibly zero quantity.
