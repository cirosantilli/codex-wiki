Here $\phi$ is a <scalar field>: the source's description as a <vector field> is a typographical slip, since the displayed formula is for its <gradient>. For <cylindrical coordinates>, write $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$. Away from $r=0$, a local choice of $\theta$ gives the <partial derivatives>
$$
r_x=\cos\theta,\quad r_y=\sin\theta,\quad
\theta_x=-\frac{\sin\theta}{r},\quad
\theta_y=\frac{\cos\theta}{r}.
$$
The <chain rule> therefore gives
$$
\phi_x=\Phi_r\cos\theta-\frac{\Phi_\theta}{r}\sin\theta,
\qquad
\phi_y=\Phi_r\sin\theta+\frac{\Phi_\theta}{r}\cos\theta,
\qquad \phi_z=\Phi_z.
$$
Use the <Cartesian coordinates> definition $\nabla\phi=\phi_x\mathbf e_x+\phi_y\mathbf e_y+\phi_z\mathbf e_z$ and the <unit vectors>
$$
\mathbf e_r=\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_x+\sin\theta\,\mathbf e_y,
\qquad
\mathbf e_\theta=-\sin\theta\,\mathbf e_x+\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_y.
$$
Collecting their coefficients proves
$$
\boxed{\nabla\phi=\Phi_r\mathbf e_r+\frac1r\Phi_\theta\mathbf e_\theta+\Phi_z\mathbf e_z,\qquad r>0.}
$$
This is the <gradient in cylindrical coordinates>. The factor $1/r$ converts a change in angle into a change per unit <arc length>.

In <cylindrical coordinates>, $(-y,x,0)=r\mathbf e_\theta$ and $x+r=r(1+\cos\theta)$. Hence the <vector field> simplifies to
$$
\mathbf F=\frac{\sqrt{1+\cos\theta}}{r}\mathbf e_\theta.
$$
Take the <orientation> of $C$ to be increasing $\theta$, as in its given <parametrization>. On the <circle>, $d\mathbf r=a\mathbf e_\theta\,d\theta$, so its <line integral> is
$$
\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sqrt{1+\cos\theta}\,d\theta
=\sqrt2\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos(\theta/2)\,d\theta
=\boxed{4\sqrt2}.
$$
We used $\sqrt{1+\cos\theta}=\sqrt2|\cos(\theta/2)|$; the <cosine> is nonnegative on this interval. In particular, the answer does not depend on $a$.

Here a <potential of a conservative vector field> means a single-valued <scalar field> $\psi$ with $\nabla\psi=\mathbf F$. By the <fundamental theorem for line integrals>, its <line integral> around every closed <curve> must vanish. Here the <angular circulation obstruction to a cylindrical potential> lets us test this using a <circle> about the axis.

On $V_1$, the <circle> from <i> lies entirely in the region and has <line integral> $4\sqrt2$. Therefore **there is no <potential of a conservative vector field> on $V_1$**.

On $V_2$, choose the continuous angular branch $-\pi<\theta<\pi$. By <a>, the equation for a <potential of a conservative vector field> reduces to $\psi_r=\psi_z=0$ and $\psi_\theta=\sqrt2\cos(\theta/2)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\psi=2\sqrt2\sin(\theta/2)+C
=\frac{2y}{\sqrt{r(r+x)}}+C,\qquad r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2},\quad\text{on }V_2.}
$$
The denominator is positive throughout $V_2$, including when $y=0$ and $x>0$, so this is a well-defined differentiable <potential of a conservative vector field> on the entire region.

For $V_3$, choose the particular <circle> $x=2\cos\theta$, $y=2\sin\theta$, $z=0$. Its only point on the removed half-plane's supporting cut is $(-2,0,0)$, where $(x+2)^2+z^2=0<1$; this point is retained by $V_3$. Thus the whole <circle> lies in $V_3$ and again has <line integral> $4\sqrt2$. Consequently **there is no <potential of a conservative vector field> on $V_3$**. The opening in the cut allows a closed <curve> to go around the excluded axis.

Finally $V_4\subset V_2$. Restricting the <potential of a conservative vector field> already constructed on $V_2$ gives
$$
\boxed{\psi=\frac{2y}{\sqrt{r(r+x)}}+C\quad\text{on }V_4.}
$$
The extra excluded <cylinder> does not invalidate an existing <potential of a conservative vector field>. Thus being <simply connected> is a sufficient condition in the usual <curl>-free criterion, not a necessary condition for a particular <vector field> to have a <potential of a conservative vector field>.

For a bounded region $V\subset\mathbb R^3$ with piecewise smooth boundary, suppose $\mathbf F$ is a <continuously differentiable vector field> on an open neighbourhood of its <closure>. The <divergence theorem>, with outward <unit normal vector> $\mathbf n$, is
$$
\boxed{\iiint_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV
=\iint_{\partial V}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,dS.}
$$
The <surface integral> measures total outward <flux>. Edges and isolated vertices have zero <surface area> and do not affect the <integral>.

In <Cartesian coordinates>, write the constant <vector> as $\mathbf c=(c_1,c_2,c_3)$. The <product rule> and constancy of its components give
$$
\nabla\cdot(\phi\mathbf c)
=\partial_x(c_1\phi)+\partial_y(c_2\phi)+\partial_z(c_3\phi)
=c_1\phi_x+c_2\phi_y+c_3\phi_z.

The last expression is the <dot product> with the <gradient>, so
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot(\phi\mathbf c)=\mathbf c\cdot\nabla\phi.}
$$

Apply the <divergence theorem> to $\phi\mathbf c$, using <ii>. Because the <vector> $\mathbf c$ is constant, it can be taken outside both <integrals>:
$$
\mathbf c\cdot\iiint_V\nabla\phi\,dV
=\iiint_V\nabla\cdot(\phi\mathbf c)\,dV
=\iint_{\partial V}\phi\mathbf c\cdot\mathbf n\,dS
=\mathbf c\cdot\iint_{\partial V}\phi\mathbf n\,dS.
$$
This holds for every $\mathbf c$, in particular each of the three Cartesian <basis vectors>. Equality of the three components proves the <gradient volume-to-boundary identity>
$$
\boxed{\iiint_V\nabla\phi\,dV=\iint_{\partial V}\phi\mathbf n\,dS.}
$$
It suffices that $\phi$ be a <continuously differentiable function> on a neighbourhood of the <closure> of $V$.

Use <cylindrical coordinates>. The real square root restricts the <projection> to $0\leq r\leq1$, and there $r-1\leq0\leq\sqrt{1-r^2}$. Since $\nabla x=\mathbf e_x$, the <volume integral> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\iiint_V\nabla x\,dV
&=2\pi\mathbf e_x\int_0^1r\bigl(\sqrt{1-r^2}-r+1\bigr)\,dr\\
&=2\pi\mathbf e_x\left(\frac13-\frac13+\frac12\right)
=\boxed{\pi\mathbf e_x}.
\end{aligned}
$$
For an independent boundary calculation, split $\partial V$ into an upper <hemisphere> $S_+$ and a lower <right circular conical surface> $S_-$. They meet in the <circle> $r=1,z=0$ and there is no additional planar face.

On $S_+$, use the <parametrization>
$$
\mathbf R(\alpha,\theta)
=(\sin\alpha\cos\theta,\sin\alpha\sin\theta,\cos\alpha),
\qquad 0\leq\alpha\leq\pi/2,\quad0\leq\theta\leq2\pi.
$$
The outward <unit normal vector> is $\mathbf R$, and the <surface area element> is $\sin\alpha\,d\alpha\,d\theta$. The $y$ and $z$ components of $\iint_{S_+}x\mathbf n\,dS$ vanish by the angular <integrals> of $\sin\theta\cos\theta$ and $\cos\theta$. The $x$ component is
$$
\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^3\alpha\,d\alpha
=\pi\cdot\frac23=\frac{2\pi}{3}.
$$
On $S_-$, the <parametrization> $(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,r-1)$ has outward <vector area element>
$$
\mathbf n\,dS=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,-1)r\,dr\,d\theta.
$$
Its negative vertical component points out below the solid. Multiplying by $x=r\cos\theta$ and using the same angular <symmetry in integration> gives
$$
\iint_{S_-}x\mathbf n\,dS
=\mathbf e_x\int_0^1r^2\,dr\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta
=\frac\pi3\mathbf e_x.
$$
Therefore the independently evaluated <surface integral> is
$$
\boxed{\iint_{\partial V}x\mathbf n\,dS
=\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}+\frac\pi3\right)\mathbf e_x
=\pi\mathbf e_x,}
$$
in agreement with the <volume integral>. The isolated apex of the <cone> does not contribute to the <surface integral>.

The original <double integral> covers the <triangle> $0\leq y\leq x\leq1$. By <Fubini's theorem>, after reversing the order, $y$ runs from $0$ to $1$ and $x$ from $y$ to $1$. Since a <continuous function> is integrable on this bounded region,
$$
\int_0^1\int_0^x f(y)\,dy\,dx
=\int_0^1\int_y^1 f(y)\,dx\,dy
=\boxed{\int_0^1(1-y)f(y)\,dy}.

For the <square pyramid>, a horizontal section at height $z$ is a <square> of side $2(1-z)$: each vertex moves linearly from the base towards $(1,1,1)$. Hence its <cross-sectional area> is $4(1-z)^2$, and its <volume> is $4\int_0^1(1-z)^2\,dz$. To use the identity just proved, choose $f(y)=1-y$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Vol}
&=4\int_0^1\int_0^x(1-y)\,dy\,dx\\
&=4\int_0^1\left(x-\frac{x^2}{2}\right)dx
=4\left(\frac12-\frac16\right)

\end{aligned}

Call this second <disk> $E$. Its centre is distance $\sqrt2$ from the centre of $D$, and
$$
\sqrt2+\sqrt3<\sqrt{11}

because $5+2\sqrt6<11$. Thus $E$ lies wholly inside $D$. In particular the lower <plane> never exceeds the upper <plane> on $E$, so the subtraction describes exactly the requested solid.

Assume the rod has constant <cross-sectional area> $A$, no internal source of <heat>, no bulk motion, and no lateral <heat flux>. Assume also that each section has a uniform <temperature>, so that $T$ depends only on $x,t$, and that <Fourier's law> applies with the stated constant <thermal conductivity> $k$. The longitudinal <heat flux density> is then $q=-kT_x$.

By <conservation of energy>, the rate of increase of <internal energy> in a slice $[x,x+\Delta x]$ equals incoming minus outgoing <heat>:
$$
\rho c A\int_x^{x+\Delta x}T_t(s,t)\,ds
=A\bigl(q(x,t)-q(x+\Delta x,t)\bigr).
$$
Divide by $A\Delta x$ and take the <limit> $\Delta x\to0$. For a sufficiently differentiable <temperature> field this gives $\rho cT_t=-q_x=kT_{xx}$, and hence
$$
\boxed{T_t=\kappa T_{xx},\qquad\kappa=\frac{k}{\rho c},\qquad0<x<L,\quad t>0.}
$$
Here $\rho$ is <mass density>, $c$ is <specific heat capacity>, and $\kappa$ is <thermal diffusivity>.

A <steady state> has $T_s''=0$, so $T_s=a+bx$. The fixed <temperature> gives $T_s(0)=T_0$, and <thermal insulation> gives $T_s'(L)=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{T_s(x)=T_0.}
$$
Subtracting this <steady state> produces the homogeneous <mixed boundary conditions>
$$
\boxed{U_t=\kappa U_{xx},\quad U(0,t)=0,\quad U_x(L,t)=0,\quad
U(x,0)=-\frac{T_0x}{L}.}
$$
The <partial differential equation> and <boundary conditions> apply for $t>0$. The <initial condition> is attained as $t\downarrow0$. Its slope at $x=L$ is $-T_0/L$, so it does not satisfy the insulated-end condition at the instant $t=0$. The resulting <heat equation> solution is smooth for $t>0$ and approaches the initial <temperature> continuously; its <spatial derivative> need not be continuous at $(L,0)$.

= Solution

For <separation of variables>, put $U=X(x)H(t)$ and set $X''/X=H'/(\kappa H)=-\lambda$. The <boundary conditions> are $X(0)=0$, $X'(L)=0$. Multiplying $-X''=\lambda X$ by $X$ and using <integration by parts> gives
$$
\lambda\int_0^L X^2\,dx=\int_0^L(X')^2\,dx.
$$
A nonzero solution therefore has $\lambda>0$: the zero case forces $X$ constant, and then $X(0)=0$ forces $X=0$. Writing $\lambda=\mu^2$, the <eigenfunctions> and their temporal factors are
$$
X_n(x)=\sin(\mu_nx),\qquad
\mu_n=\frac{(n+1/2)\pi}{L},\qquad
H_n(t)=e^{-\kappa\mu_n^2t},\qquad n=0,1,\ldots.
$$
These <sine> functions are <orthogonal> on $[0,L]$, with squared <norm> $L/2$. Expanding the <initial condition> and multiplying by $\sin(\mu_mx)$ gives, using the permitted interchange of the <sum> and <integral>,
$$
\begin{aligned}
b_n
&=\frac2L\int_0^L\left(-\frac{T_0x}{L}\right)\sin(\mu_nx)\,dx\\
&=-\frac{2T_0}{L^2}
\left[-\frac{x\cos(\mu_nx)}{\mu_n}
+\frac{\sin(\mu_nx)}{\mu_n^2}\right]_0^L\\
&=-\frac{2T_0(-1)^n}{L^2\mu_n^2}
=-\frac{8T_0(-1)^n}{\pi^2(2n+1)^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $\cos(\mu_nL)=0$ and $\sin(\mu_nL)=(-1)^n$. Thus the <Fourier series> solution is
$$
\boxed{U(x,t)=-\frac{8T_0}{\pi^2}\sum_{n=0}^\infty
\frac{(-1)^n}{(2n+1)^2}
\sin\left(\frac{(2n+1)\pi x}{2L}\right)
\exp\left(-\frac{\kappa(2n+1)^2\pi^2t}{4L^2}\right),\qquad T=T_0+U.}
$$
The <exponential decay> permits differentiation of the <series> for $t>0$, and each term satisfies both <boundary conditions>. At $t=0$ it is the mixed-end <Fourier expansion> of $-T_0x/L$.

The gained <heat> is the increase in <internal energy>. Since the paper specifies no <cross-sectional area>, first give the answer per unit <cross-sectional area>; multiply by $A$ for a rod of <cross-sectional area> $A$. The initial <temperature> has <integral> $T_0L/2$, whereas $\int_0^L\sin(\mu_nx)\,dx=1/\mu_n$. At $t_*=L^2/\kappa$ this gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{\Delta Q}{A}
=\rho cT_0L\left[
\frac12-\frac{16}{\pi^3}\sum_{n=0}^\infty
\frac{(-1)^n}{(2n+1)^3}
\exp\left(-\frac{(2n+1)^2\pi^2}{4}\right)
\right].}
$$
Numerically $\Delta Q/A\simeq0.45623855\,\rho cT_0L$. This is positive and less than the eventual gain $\rho cT_0L/2$, as expected from relaxation towards the uniform <steady state>.

Assume $\rho$ is <mass> per unit equilibrium <length>, the <tension> has constant magnitude $T$, the string has negligible bending stiffness, and there is no damping or external transverse <force>. Assume small slopes $|y_x|\ll1$ and purely transverse motion. Then a short segment has <mass> $\rho\Delta x$ to first order. If $\alpha$ is the angle of its tangent, the small-slope approximation gives $\sin\alpha\simeq\tan\alpha=y_x$.

The vertical components of <tension> at the two ends give net transverse <force> $T[y_x(x+\Delta x,t)-y_x(x,t)]$. <Newton's second law> therefore gives
$$
\rho\Delta x\,y_{tt}
=T\bigl[y_x(x+\Delta x,t)-y_x(x,t)\bigr]
$$
to first order. Dividing by $\Delta x$ and taking its <limit> to zero yields
$$
\boxed{y_{tt}=c^2y_{xx},\qquad c=\sqrt{\frac{T}{\rho}}.}
$$
Thus $c$ is the propagation <speed> of small transverse <waves>.

Write $y(x,t)=Y(\xi,\eta)$ with $\xi=x-ct$, $\eta=x+ct$. The <chain rule> gives the <differential operators>
$$
\partial_x=\partial_\xi+\partial_\eta,
\qquad
\partial_t=-c\partial_\xi+c\partial_\eta.
$$
Their squared difference is
$$
y_{tt}-c^2y_{xx}=-4c^2Y_{\xi\eta}.
$$
Consequently the <wave equation> becomes $Y_{\xi\eta}=0$. Integrating first in $\eta$ gives $Y_\xi=F'(\xi)$; integrating in $\xi$ gives the general solution
$$
\boxed{y(x,t)=F(x-ct)+G(x+ct).}
$$
The arbitrary twice differentiable <functions> describe right-moving and left-moving <waves>, respectively. Conversely the <chain rule> shows that each such sum satisfies the <wave equation>.

Enforce the fixed-end <Dirichlet boundary condition> by taking the <odd extension> of the initial <velocity> to the whole line:
$$
v_o(s)=\kappa s e^{-|s|/L}.
$$
This agrees with the specified <initial condition> for $s\geq0$. To derive the relevant form of <d'Alembert's formula>, the zero initial displacement in <b> gives $G=-F$, up to a cancelling constant. The initial <velocity> then gives $-2cF'=v_o$. Hence
$$
y(x,t)=\frac1{2c}\int_{x-ct}^{x+ct}v_o(s)\,ds.
$$
An <antiderivative>, valid across $s=0$, is
$$
\int s e^{-|s|/L}\,ds=-L(|s|+L)e^{-|s|/L}.
$$
Evaluation therefore gives the solution throughout $x>0,t>0$:
$$
\boxed{y(x,t)=\frac{\kappa L}{2c}
\left[(|x-ct|+L)e^{-|x-ct|/L}
-(x+ct+L)e^{-(x+ct)/L}\right].}
$$
For $ct\leq x$, replace $|x-ct|$ by $x-ct$; for $ct\geq x$, replace it by $ct-x$. Thus the same expression includes the reflected <wave> after it reaches the fixed end.

At $x=0$ the two terms agree, so $y(0,t)=0$. At $t=0$ they again agree, so $y(x,0)=0$. Differentiating the <integral> with respect to $t$ gives
$$
y_t(x,t)=\frac12\bigl[v_o(x+ct)+v_o(x-ct)\bigr],
$$
which recovers $y_t(x,0)=\kappa xe^{-x/L}$. Since $v_o$ is a <continuously differentiable function>, the formula is twice continuously differentiable even across $x=ct$, and satisfies the <wave equation> there as well.

= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

Let $w$ be the difference of two solutions. It is a <harmonic function> in the open <rectangle>, continuous on its <closure>, with
$$
w(x,0)=0,\qquad w_y(x,\pi)=0,\qquad
w_x(0,y)=w_x(\pi,y)=0.
$$
Interpret these <Neumann boundary conditions> classically on the open edges, with the corresponding first <derivatives> extending there. We use the <strong maximum principle for harmonic functions> together with <even reflection across a homogeneous Neumann boundary>. This avoids imposing differentiability at the two bottom corners, where the original data fail a <corner compatibility condition for mixed boundary data>.

By continuity on the compact <rectangle>, $w$ attains a maximum $M$. Suppose $M>0$. It cannot occur on the bottom edge, where $w=0$. If it occurs in the interior, the <strong maximum principle for harmonic functions> makes $w$ constant, contradicting its bottom value. If it occurs on an open vertical or top edge, the zero <normal derivative> allows an even <reflection> across that edge. The reflected <harmonic function> then has an interior maximum, giving the same contradiction.

The only remaining possibilities are the two top corners. Reflect evenly across both meeting edges. This gives a bounded <harmonic function> on a punctured neighbourhood of the corner. The <removable singularity for a bounded harmonic function> fills the centre with its continuous value. The corner is now an interior maximum, again impossible. Therefore $w\leq0$. Applying the identical argument to $-w$ gives $w\geq0$.

Thus $\boxed{w=0}$, and **the solution is unique** among the continuous classical solutions with the stated edge conditions.

For $0<y<\pi$, define $J(y)=\int_0^\pi u_y(x,y)\,dx$. Differentiate under the <integral> and use <Laplace's equation>:
$$
J'(y)=\int_0^\pi u_{yy}(x,y)\,dx
=-\int_0^\pi u_{xx}(x,y)\,dx
=-\bigl[u_x(\pi,y)-u_x(0,y)\bigr]=0.
$$
Thus $J$ is constant. The top <Neumann boundary condition> gives $J(\pi)=0$, so
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\pi u_y(x,y^*)\,dx=0,\qquad0<y^*<\pi.}
$$
For the included endpoint $y^*=0$, the <integral> is interpreted as an <improper integral> at the bottom corners if necessary. The <Fourier series> constructed in <c> gives, on $0<x<\pi$,
$$
u_y(x,0)=-2\log(2\sin x)
+2\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{\tanh(2m\pi)-1}{m}\cos(2mx).
$$
The second <series> is absolutely and uniformly convergent; the first term has only integrable <logarithmic singularities>. Its <integral> is zero since $\int_0^\pi\log(2\sin x)\,dx=0$, while every <cosine> term also has zero <integral>. This proves the endpoint as well:
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\pi u_y(x,y^*)\,dx=0,\qquad0\leq y^*<\pi.}
$$
For completeness, the logarithmic <integral> follows without any endpoint differentiation. Put $I=\int_0^{\pi/2}\log(\sin x)\,dx$ and substitute $x\mapsto\pi/2-x$ to obtain $I=\int_0^{\pi/2}\log(\cos x)\,dx$. Adding and using $\sin x\cos x=\sin(2x)/2$ gives $2I=I-(\pi/2)\log2$, hence $I=-(\pi/2)\log2$ and the required full-interval result.

= c
{parent=Question 6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

For <separation of variables>, set $u=X(x)Y(y)$, with $X''/X=-Y''/Y=-\lambda$. The side <Neumann boundary conditions> give $X'(0)=X'(\pi)=0$. As in the standard <Sturm-Liouville problem>, <integration by parts> shows $\lambda\geq0$. The zero <eigenvalue> gives constant $X$, and $Y''=0$ together with $Y'(\pi)=0$ makes $Y$ constant too.

For positive <eigenvalues>, the side conditions give $\lambda=n^2$, $X_n=\cos(nx)$, $n\geq1$. The top condition chooses the <hyperbolic cosine> factor $\cosh(n(\pi-y))$. Normalize it to one at $y=0$, obtaining
$$
u(x,y)=A_0+\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n\cos(nx)
\frac{\cosh(n(\pi-y))}{\cosh(n\pi)}.
$$
The <orthogonality of integer Fourier modes> and the permitted interchange of <summation> and <integration> give
$$
A_0=\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi x(\pi-x)\,dx=\frac{\pi^2}{6},
\qquad
 a_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi x(\pi-x)\cos(nx)\,dx.
$$
For the remaining <Fourier coefficients>, integrate by parts twice. The <sine> boundary term vanishes, and with $f=x(\pi-x)$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^\pi f(x)\cos(nx)\,dx
&=-\frac1n\int_0^\pi f'(x)\sin(nx)\,dx\\
&=\frac{[f'(x)\cos(nx)]_0^\pi}{n^2}
-\frac1{n^2}\int_0^\pi f''(x)\cos(nx)\,dx\\
&=-\frac{\pi(1+(-1)^n)}{n^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The last <integral> vanishes because $f''=-2$ and $n$ is a positive <integer>. Thus $a_n=-2(1+(-1)^n)/n^2$: only even <Fourier modes> survive, as expected from <reflection symmetry> about $x=\pi/2$. Writing $n=2m$ gives
$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=\frac{\pi^2}{6}
-\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{\cos(2mx)}{m^2}
\frac{\cosh(2m(\pi-y))}{\cosh(2m\pi)}.}
$$
The factor involving <hyperbolic cosine> lies between zero and one on the closed <rectangle>. Comparison with $\sum m^{-2}$ proves <uniform convergence>, so the solution is continuous on the entire boundary and takes the prescribed bottom values. On every strip $y\geq\varepsilon>0$, the <Fourier coefficients> have additional <exponential decay>, allowing differentiation and verifying <Laplace's equation> and all three <Neumann boundary conditions>.

To justify the endpoint calculation in <b>, differentiate at $0<x<\pi$, $y=0$ in the interior-edge sense:
$$
u_y(x,0)=2\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{\tanh(2m\pi)}m\cos(2mx).
$$
Subtracting $1$ from the <hyperbolic tangent> leaves an absolutely convergent <series>. For the remaining part, the <power series> $-\log(1-z)=\sum_{m\geq1}z^m/m$ gives, by taking real parts at $z=re^{2ix}$ and then $r\uparrow1$,
$$
\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{\cos(2mx)}m=-\log(2\sin x),\qquad0<x<\pi.
$$
The same formula follows as the limit of the differentiated interior solution, since its leading multiplier is $e^{-2my}$. This establishes the boundary <normal derivative> used in <b>. It also explains why the failure of the <corner compatibility condition for mixed boundary data> affects first <derivatives> at the bottom corners without spoiling continuity of $u$ itself.

Write the observation point as $(0,0,h)$ with $h>0$, reserving source coordinates for the <integral>. For the straight segment, $ds'=dx'$ and the separation is $\sqrt{x'^2+h^2}$. Therefore its <Newtonian gravitational potential> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi(h)
&=-G\gamma\int_{-a}^a\frac{dx'}{\sqrt{x'^2+h^2}}\\
&=-2G\gamma\left[\log\bigl(x'+\sqrt{x'^2+h^2}\bigr)\right]_0^a\\
&=\boxed{-2G\gamma\log\left(\frac{a+\sqrt{a^2+h^2}}{h}\right)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Equivalently $\phi(h)=-2G\gamma\operatorname{arsinh}(a/h)$. At large $h$ it approaches $-G(2a\gamma)/h$, the <Newtonian potential of a point mass> with the segment's total <mass>.

Use source <polar coordinates> $x'=s\cos\theta$, $y'=s\sin\theta$ on the <semicircular disk>, with $0\leq s\leq a$ and $0\leq\theta\leq\pi$. Its <surface area element> is $s\,ds\,d\theta$, and the separation from $(0,0,h)$ is $\sqrt{s^2+h^2}$. Thus
$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi(h)
&=-G\sigma\int_0^\pi\int_0^a\frac{s\,ds\,d\theta}{\sqrt{s^2+h^2}}\\
&=-\pi G\sigma\left[\sqrt{s^2+h^2}\right]_0^a
=\boxed{-\pi G\sigma\bigl(\sqrt{a^2+h^2}-h\bigr)},\qquad h>0.
\end{aligned}
$$
This <Newtonian gravitational potential> is half that of the full <disk> because the separation is independent of the polar angle. At large $h$ it approaches $-G(\pi a^2\sigma/2)/h$, with the correct total <mass>.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

Use source <spherical coordinates> with radius $s$, polar angle $\alpha$ measured from the positive $z$-axis, and azimuth $\theta$. The lower half of the <spherical shell> has $a\leq s\leq2a$, $\pi/2\leq\alpha\leq\pi$, and $0\leq\theta\leq2\pi$. Its <volume element> is $s^2\sin\alpha\,ds\,d\alpha\,d\theta$. At the observation point $(0,0,h)$, $h>0$, the separation is $\sqrt{s^2+h^2-2sh\cos\alpha}$, so
$$
\phi(h)=-2\pi G\rho\int_a^{2a}s^2
\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi}\frac{\sin\alpha\,d\alpha}
{\sqrt{s^2+h^2-2sh\cos\alpha}}\,ds.
$$
The angular <integral> is elementary: put $q=\cos\alpha$ to obtain
$$
\int_{-1}^0\frac{dq}{\sqrt{s^2+h^2-2shq}}
=\frac{s+h-\sqrt{s^2+h^2}}{sh}.
$$
We used $\sqrt{(s+h)^2}=s+h$ since both $s,h$ are positive. Hence
$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi(h)
&=-\frac{2\pi G\rho}{h}\int_a^{2a}
\bigl(s^2+hs-s\sqrt{s^2+h^2}\bigr)\,ds\\
&=-\frac{2\pi G\rho}{h}
\left[\frac{s^3}{3}+\frac{hs^2}{2}
-\frac{(s^2+h^2)^{3/2}}{3}\right]_a^{2a}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the <axial potential of a uniform hemispherical shell>, at every point of the positive $z$-axis, is
$$
\boxed{\phi(h)=-\frac{2\pi G\rho}{3h}
\left[7a^3+\frac92ha^2-(4a^2+h^2)^{3/2}+(a^2+h^2)^{3/2}\right],\qquad h>0.}
$$
There is no need to split into $h<a$, $a<h<2a$, and $h>2a$: the observation point is outside the lower <hemisphere> in all three cases. As checks, $\phi(h)\to-3\pi G\rho a^2$ as $h\downarrow0$, and at large $h$,
$$
\phi(h)\sim-\frac{G}{h}\,\frac{14\pi\rho a^3}{3}.
$$
The last factor is exactly the total <mass> of the lower half of the <spherical shell>.
