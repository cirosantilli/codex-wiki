= Solution

{c}

The <integrating factor> for this <linear ordinary differential equation> is $\mu(x)=e^{x^3/3}$. Multiplication gives an exact <derivative>:
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left(e^{x^3/3}y\right)=x^2e^{x^3/3}=\frac{d}{dx}e^{x^3/3}.
$$
Consequently $y=1+Ce^{-x^3/3}$. The <initial condition> $y(1)=2$ gives $C=e^{1/3}$, so the <initial value problem> has solution
$$
\boxed{y(x)=1+\exp\left(\frac{1-x^3}{3}\right),\qquad x\in\mathbb R.}
$$
The <integrating factor> calculation determines every solution, and the <initial condition> fixes its remaining <constant of integration> uniquely.

For the given <matrix> $A$, the <characteristic polynomial> is
$$
\det(A-\lambda I)=(2-\lambda)(1-\lambda)-6=\lambda^2-3\lambda-4=(\lambda-4)(\lambda+1).
$$
For $\lambda=4$, the <eigenvector> equation gives $v_2=v_1$; for $\lambda=-1$, it gives $3v_1+2v_2=0$. Thus the <eigenvalues> and their corresponding <eigenspaces> are
$$
\boxed{\lambda=4:\ \operatorname{span}\{(1,1)^T\},\qquad \lambda=-1:\ \operatorname{span}\{(2,-3)^T\}.}
$$
For any <eigenvector> $v$ with <eigenvalue> $\lambda$, the vector-valued <function> $e^{\lambda x}v$ satisfies $Y'=AY$, since its <derivative> is $\lambda e^{\lambda x}v=e^{\lambda x}Av$. The two displayed <eigenvectors> are <linearly independent>, so the <general solution> of the <linear system of ordinary differential equations> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}y_1(x)\\y_2(x)\end{pmatrix}=C_1e^{4x}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}+C_2e^{-x}\begin{pmatrix}2\\-3\end{pmatrix},\qquad C_1,C_2\in\mathbb R.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=c}

The <function> $y_1=x$ solves the <linear ordinary differential equation>. Apply <reduction of order> by writing $y=xv(x)$. Substituting $y'=v+xv'$ and $y''=2v'+xv''$ and cancelling the terms in $v$ gives
$$
x^2(1-x)v''+(2x-3x^2)v'=0.
$$
For $w=v'$, this becomes a first-order <linear ordinary differential equation>:
$$
w'+\left(\frac2x+\frac1{x-1}\right)w=0,\qquad w=\frac{C}{x^2(x-1)}.
$$
A <partial fraction decomposition> provides the required <antiderivative>:
$$
\frac1{x^2(x-1)}=-\frac1x-\frac1{x^2}+\frac1{x-1},\qquad v=C_1+C_2\left(\log\frac{x-1}{x}+\frac1x\right).
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{y(x)=C_1x+C_2\left(1+x\log\frac{x-1}{x}\right),\qquad x>1.}
$$
The <logarithm> has positive argument on the required <interval>. The <Wronskian> of $x$ and $1+x\log((x-1)/x)$ is $1/(x-1)\ne0$, proving their <linear independence> and hence completeness of this <general solution>.

In a <partial derivative>, the variable held fixed matters. Holding $u$ fixed gives $x_v=-v$. To find $v_y$, hold $x$ fixed and invert the <Jacobian matrix>:
$$
\begin{pmatrix}dx\\dy\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}u&-v\\v&u\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}du\\dv\end{pmatrix},\qquad \begin{pmatrix}du\\dv\end{pmatrix}=\frac1{u^2+v^2}\begin{pmatrix}u&v\\-v&u\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}dx\\dy\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\left.\frac{\partial x}{\partial v}\right|_u=-v,\qquad \left.\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}\right|_x=\frac{u}{u^2+v^2}.}
$$
The second formula holds on each local inverse branch away from $(u,v)=(0,0)$, where the <Jacobian determinant> vanishes. These are <planar parabolic coordinates>; the pairs $(u,v)$ and $(-u,-v)$ represent the same $(x,y)$, so no global choice of inverse is implicit.

The <multivariable chain rule> first gives
$$
g_u=uf_x+vf_y,\qquad g_v=-vf_x+uf_y.
$$
Taking a second <partial derivative> in each variable, with the <partial derivatives> of $f$ evaluated at $(x(u,v),y(u,v))$, gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
g_{uu}&=f_x+u^2f_{xx}+uv(f_{xy}+f_{yx})+v^2f_{yy},\\
g_{vv}&=-f_x+v^2f_{xx}-uv(f_{xy}+f_{yx})+u^2f_{yy}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The <mixed partial derivatives> cancel even without using their equality. Hence the <Laplacians> satisfy
$$
\boxed{g_{uu}+g_{vv}=(u^2+v^2)(f_{xx}+f_{yy}).}
$$
Away from the origin the factor $u^2+v^2$ is positive, so **$g$ is a <harmonic function> if and only if $f$ is a <harmonic function>** on the corresponding local coordinate patch. This is the <Laplacian in planar parabolic coordinates>.

Using the first <partial derivatives> from part (b),
$$
vg_u-ug_v=2uvf_x+(v^2-u^2)f_y=2yf_x-2xf_y.

Also $uvg=yf$. Therefore the first <partial differential equation> becomes
$$
\boxed{2y\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}-2x\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}+yf=0.}

For the second <partial differential equation>, the same <chain rule> gives
$$
ug_u-vg_v=(u^2+v^2)f_x,\qquad (u^4-v^4)g=2x(u^2+v^2)f.

Away from the origin, cancellation yields $f_x+2xf=0$. Treating $y$ as fixed, an <integrating factor> gives $\partial_x(e^{x^2}f)=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{f(x,y)=e^{-x^2}F(y),}
$$
where $F$ is an arbitrary sufficiently <differentiable function>. This is the local <general solution>, and the global form when $f$ is defined on the whole plane. If $f$ and its first <partial derivatives> are continuous at the origin, the reduced <partial differential equation> also holds there by <continuity>.

The <gradient> of the given <polynomial> is
$$
\nabla f=(2x+y^2+1,\ 2y(1+x)).
$$
Its second component vanishes when $y=0$ or $x=-1$. The first component then gives exactly three <critical points>:
$$
(-\tfrac12,0),\qquad (-1,1),\qquad (-1,-1).
$$
The <Hessian matrix> and its <determinant> are
$$
H_f=\begin{pmatrix}2&2y\\2y&2+2x\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det H_f=4(1+x-y^2).
$$
At $(-1/2,0)$ the <Hessian matrix> is $\operatorname{diag}(2,1)$, whose <positive definiteness> proves a strict <local minimum> by the <second-derivative test>. At $(-1,\pm1)$ its <determinant> is $-4$, so both points are <saddle points of a scalar function>. Their values and classifications are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
f(-\tfrac12,0)&=-\tfrac14&&\text{strict local minimum},\\
f(-1,\pm1)&=0&&\text{saddle points}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
There are **no <local maxima>**.

At a regular point of the <optimization constraint>, meaning $\nabla g\ne0$, the necessary <Lagrange multiplier> condition is
$$
\boxed{\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\quad\text{for some }\lambda\in\mathbb R.}
$$
Indeed, every allowed <tangent vector> $t$ to the <level set> $g=0$ satisfies $\nabla g\cdot t=0$. At a constrained <extremum>, the <directional derivative> $\nabla f\cdot t$ must vanish too. Thus both <gradients> are normal to the same <tangent line> and are parallel. This includes $\nabla f=0$. At a singular point with $\nabla g=0$, the displayed condition need not be necessary; those points require a separate examination.

= Solution

For $g=(x-1)^2+y^2-29$, the <gradient> $\nabla g=2(x-1,y)$ never vanishes on the <circle>. The <Lagrange multiplier> equations are
$$
2x+y^2+1=2\lambda(x-1),\qquad 2y(1+x)=2\lambda y,\qquad (x-1)^2+y^2=29.
$$
If $y=0$, the two possibilities are $(1\pm\sqrt{29},0)$. If $y\ne0$, then $\lambda=1+x$, and the first equation gives $y^2=2x^2-2x-3$. Combining this with $y^2=28+2x-x^2$ yields
$$
3x^2-4x-31=0.
$$
Put
$$
s=\sqrt{97},\qquad r_-=\frac{2-s}{3},\qquad r_+=\frac{2+s}{3},\qquad h_-=\frac{\sqrt{163-2s}}3,\qquad h_+=\frac{\sqrt{163+2s}}3.
$$
Both $h_-$ and $h_+$ are positive, giving the other four points $(r_-,\pm h_-)$ and $(r_+,\pm h_+)$. To classify all six, restrict $f$ to the <circle> by eliminating $y^2$:
$$
f=H(x)=-x^3+2x^2+31x+28,\qquad 1-\sqrt{29}\leq x\leq1+\sqrt{29}.
$$
Its <derivatives> are $H'=-3x^2+4x+31$ and $H''=4-6x$. In the interior, $H''(r_-)=2s>0$ and $H''(r_+)=-2s<0$. At either endpoint $H'<0$; along the <circle>, $x$ has a strict <local minimum> at the left endpoint and a strict <local maximum> at the right endpoint. Consequently the six constrained <extrema> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{point}&f&\text{classification on the circle}\\ \hline
(1-\sqrt{29},0)&31-3\sqrt{29}&\text{strict local maximum}\\
(1+\sqrt{29},0)&31+3\sqrt{29}&\text{strict local minimum}\\
(r_-,\pm h_-)&\dfrac{1330-194\sqrt{97}}{27}&\text{global minima}\\
(r_+,\pm h_+)&\dfrac{1330+194\sqrt{97}}{27}&\text{global maxima}
\end{array}}
$$
The <circle> is a <compact set>, so the <extreme value theorem> guarantees that its <global minimum> and <global maximum> occur among these six points. Their values give the corrected sharp bound
$$
\boxed{f(x,y)\geq\frac{1330-194\sqrt{97}}{27}\approx-21.50660791\quad\text{on the circle}.}
$$
**The printed lower bound $-20$ is false.** A particularly short counterexample is
$$
(x,y)=\left(-\frac52,\frac{\sqrt{67}}2\right),\qquad (x-1)^2+y^2=\frac{49+67}{4}=29,\qquad f(x,y)=-\frac{171}{8}<-20.
$$
The sole <local minimum> in part (a), $(-1/2,0)$, has value $-1/4$, larger than values on this <circle>; it is therefore **not a <global minimum>**. In fact $f(-2,y)=2-y^2\to-\infty$, so $f$ has **no <global minimum> on the plane**.

The <events> $B_1,B_2,\ldots$ form a <partition of a sample space> $\Omega$ when they are pairwise <disjoint sets> and cover the whole <sample space>:
$$
\boxed{B_i\cap B_j=\varnothing\ (i\ne j),\qquad \bigcup_{i\geq1}B_i=\Omega.}
$$
Thus exactly one of these <events> occurs on every outcome. Under the usual <set partition> convention the parts are nonempty; empty parts can simply be discarded.

For an <integrable> <random variable> $X$, the <law of total expectation> for the <partition of a sample space> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X]=\sum_{i:\,\mathbb P(B_i)>0}\mathbb E[X\mid B_i]\,\mathbb P(B_i).}
$$
Parts of zero <probability> contribute zero and need no definition of $\mathbb E[X\mid B_i]$. To see the identity, write $X=\sum_i X\mathbf1_{B_i}$ and use <linearity of expectation>; integrability justifies the countable sum since $\sum_i\mathbb E[|X|\mathbf1_{B_i}]=\mathbb E[|X|]<\infty$.

First the <expected value> of the <absorption time> is finite. From any interior state, consecutive leftward moves lead to absorption within at most $n$ steps, with <probability> at least $p=3^{-n}$. By <independence> of the subsequent steps, the <probability> of surviving $jn$ steps is at most $(1-p)^j$. Summing this <geometric series> bounds the <expected value> of the <absorption time> by $n/p$.

Now apply <first-step analysis>. Starting from $k$ in the interior, one step is always used. Conditional on that step, the remaining <expected values> are $e_{k+1}$, $e_k$, and $e_{k-1}$, each with <probability> $1/3$. The <law of total expectation> therefore gives
$$
e_k=1+\frac13e_{k+1}+\frac13e_k+\frac13e_{k-1}.
$$
The term $1$ remains even when the particle stays at $k$, because a step has elapsed. Rearranging gives
$$
\boxed{\frac13e_{k+1}-\frac23e_k+\frac13e_{k-1}=-1,\qquad 1\leq k\leq n-1.}
$$

At an <absorbing state>, the remaining <absorption time> is zero, so $e_0=e_n=0$. Multiplying the <linear recurrence relation> by three gives
$$
e_{k+1}-2e_k+e_{k-1}=-3.

The second <finite difference> of $k^2$ is two. A <particular solution> is therefore $-3k^2/2$, while every solution of the corresponding homogeneous <linear recurrence relation> has the form $A+Bk$. The <boundary conditions> give $A=0$ and $B=3n/2$, hence
$$
\boxed{e_k=\frac32k(n-k),\qquad 0\leq k\leq n.}
$$
This is the <mean absorption time of a lazy symmetric random walk> with holding <probability> $1/3$.

For a nonnegative integer-valued <random variable>, its <probability generating function> is
$$
\boxed{G(s)=\mathbb E[s^X]=\sum_{j=0}^\infty p(j)s^j.}
$$
The convention $s^0=1$, including at $s=0$, gives $G(0)=p(0)$. The <power series> has <absolute convergence> for every real $|s|\leq1$, since $\sum_jp(j)|s|^j\leq1$. More generally its real domain as a finite <expected value> is $\{s\in\mathbb R:\sum_jp(j)|s|^j<\infty\}$, which may extend beyond $[-1,1]$. No extension beyond that guaranteed <interval> is automatic.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Under the extra hypothesis that $G(s)$ exists for every real $s$, its <power series> has infinite <radius of convergence>. <Termwise differentiation of a power series> is therefore valid to every finite order. For $k\geq1$,
$$
G^{(k)}(s)=\sum_{j=k}^\infty j(j-1)\cdots(j-k+1)p(j)s^{j-k}.
$$
At $s=1$ this is the <expected value> of the <falling factorial> of $X$:
$$
\boxed{G^{(k)}(1)=\mathbb E[(X)_k]=\mathbb E[X(X-1)\cdots(X-k+1)].}
$$
In particular $\boxed{G'(1)=\mathbb E[X]}$. The terms with $j<k$ vanish because their <falling factorials> contain a zero factor. These <factorial moments> are finite under the stated hypothesis.

= Solution

{c}

Every assignment determines a unique <set partition> into its nonempty colour classes. If there are $k$ such <blocks of a set partition>, there are $A(n,k)$ choices for that <set partition>. For each fixed choice, assign distinct colours to the $k$ <blocks of a set partition> in
$$
(x)_k=x(x-1)\cdots(x-k+1)
$$
ways. Although the <blocks of a set partition> are unlabelled, they are distinguishable by their elements: ordering them temporarily, for instance by their least elements, counts each assignment exactly once. The <falling factorial> is zero when $k>x$, as it should be. Summing over the possible numbers of used colours proves
$$
\boxed{x^n=\sum_{k=1}^n A(n,k)(x)_k.}
$$
Here $A(n,k)$ is the <Stirling number of the second kind>. Both sides are <polynomials> in $x$ and agree at infinitely many integers $x>2$, so the identity also holds for every real $x$, including $0,1,2$. This extension will justify substituting the <random variable> $X$ in part (iii).

The <probability density function> must integrate to one, so
$$
1=\frac1{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^\infty y^{-1/2}e^{-y}\,dy,\qquad \boxed{\int_0^\infty t^{-1/2}e^{-t}\,dt=\sqrt\pi.}
$$
Also, <linearity of expectation> and the <mean> and <variance> of the <standard normal distribution> give
$$
\mathbb E[Y]=\frac12\mathbb E[Z^2]=\frac12\left(\operatorname{var}(Z)+(\mathbb E[Z])^2\right)=\frac12.
$$
Using the <probability density function> to compute this <expected value> gives
$$
\frac12=\frac1{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^\infty y^{1/2}e^{-y}\,dy,\qquad \boxed{\int_0^\infty t^{1/2}e^{-t}\,dt=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}.}
$$
These are the values $\Gamma(1/2)$ and $\Gamma(3/2)$ of the <gamma function>.

The <central limit theorem> states that if $W_1,W_2,\ldots$ are <independent and identically distributed random variables> with finite <mean> $\mu$ and finite positive <variance> $\sigma^2$, then
$$
\boxed{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^nW_i-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1).}
$$
Here the arrow means <convergence in distribution>: for every real $z$, the <probability> that the expression on the left is at most $z$ tends to $\Phi(z)$, the <standard normal distribution function>. If $\sigma^2=0$, the variables equal $\mu$ <almost surely> and their <sample mean> is exactly $\mu$.

Assuming $g$ is <measurable>, applying it separately to <independent and identically distributed random variables> preserves those properties. Thus $W_i=g(X_i)$ have common <mean> $\mu_g$ and <variance> $\sigma_g^2$. For $\sigma_g^2>0$, the <central limit theorem> gives
$$
\frac{\sqrt n}{\sigma_g}\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^ng(X_i)-\mu_g\right)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1).
$$
Hence, for large $n$, the approximate <probability distribution> is
$$
\boxed{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^ng(X_i)\ \approx\ N\left(\mu_g,\frac{\sigma_g^2}{n}\right).}
$$
The second argument is the <variance>. If $\sigma_g^2=0$, this <sample mean> equals $\mu_g$ <almost surely>, so the appropriate description is a <point mass> at $\mu_g$.

For an observed <random sample> with all $x_i>0$, which happens <almost surely>, <independence> gives the <likelihood function>
$$
L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^n\frac{2x_i}{\theta}e^{-x_i^2/\theta}=2^n\left(\prod_{i=1}^nx_i\right)\theta^{-n}\exp\left(-\frac{S}{\theta}\right),\qquad S=\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2>0.
$$
Ignoring a term independent of $\theta$, the <log-likelihood> and its <derivative> are
$$
\ell(\theta)=-n\log\theta-\frac S\theta+C,\qquad \ell'(\theta)=\frac{S-n\theta}{\theta^2}.
$$
This <derivative> is positive for $0<\theta<S/n$ and negative for $\theta>S/n$. Thus there is a unique <global maximum>, and the <maximum-likelihood estimator> is
$$
\boxed{\widehat\theta(\mathbf X)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_i^2.}
$$

Apply the <central limit theorem> to the <independent and identically distributed random variables> $X_i^2$, whose <mean> and <variance> are $\theta$ and $\theta^2$. It gives the approximate <standard normal distribution>
$$
\frac{\sqrt n(\widehat\theta-\theta)}{\theta}\ \approx\ N(0,1).
$$
The supplied <standard normal quantile> $c=1.96$ leaves <probability> $0.025$ in each tail. Hence
$$
\mathbb P\left(-1.96<\frac{\sqrt n(\widehat\theta-\theta)}{\theta}<1.96\right)\approx0.95.
$$
Since $\theta>0$, this event is equivalent to
$$
\theta\left(1-\frac{1.96}{\sqrt n}\right)<\widehat\theta<\theta\left(1+\frac{1.96}{\sqrt n}\right).
$$
For $n>1.96^2$, equivalently integer $n\geq4$, both denominators below are positive. Inverting the inequalities gives the approximate $95\%$ <confidence interval>
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\widehat\theta}{1+1.96/\sqrt n},\ \frac{\widehat\theta}{1-1.96/\sqrt n}\right),\qquad c=1.96.}
$$
The approximation is justified for large $n$; positivity at $n\geq4$ by itself does not guarantee accurate coverage. This construction is a <confidence interval from a relative normal pivot>: the unknown scale remains inside the pivotal ratio until the inequalities are inverted. Its meaning is that the random interval covers the fixed <statistical parameter> $\theta$ with <probability> approximately $0.95$ under repeated sampling.
