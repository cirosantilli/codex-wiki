For an <oriented surface> $\Sigma$ with piecewise smooth closed <boundary> $\partial\Sigma$, and a <vector field> $\mathbf F$ that is <continuously differentiable> on an open neighborhood of $\Sigma$, <Stokes theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\iint_\Sigma(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS
=\oint_{\partial\Sigma}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r.}
$$
Here $\mathbf n$ is the chosen <unit normal vector>. The <orientation> of $\partial\Sigma$ follows the <right-hand rule>: with the right thumb pointing along $\mathbf n$, the fingers curl in the positive direction. Equivalently, walking along the <boundary> with one's head along $\mathbf n$ leaves the <surface> on the left. Each <boundary component> receives this induced <orientation>.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

Write $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}>0$. The <cylindrical coordinates> give $\mathbf e_\theta=(-y/r,x/r,0)$ and $\mathbf e_r=(x/r,y/r,0)$, so the <vector components> of the <vector field> are
$$
F_x=-\frac{yf(z)}r,\qquad F_y=\frac{xf(z)}r,\qquad F_z=0.
$$
The first two components of the <curl>, calculated in <Cartesian coordinates>, are $-xf'(z)/r$ and $-yf'(z)/r$. For the third component, the <chain rule> gives
$$
\partial_x(x/r)=\frac1r-\frac{x^2}{r^3},\qquad
\partial_y(y/r)=\frac1r-\frac{y^2}{r^3},
$$
and hence
$$
(\nabla\times\mathbf F)_z=f(z)\left(\frac2r-\frac{x^2+y^2}{r^3}\right)=\frac{f(z)}r.
$$
Combining these <vector components> in the <cylindrical basis> yields
$$
\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf F=\frac{f(z)}r\mathbf e_z-f'(z)\mathbf e_r,\qquad r>0.}
$$
The restriction $r>0$ matters because the <cylindrical basis> is undefined on the axis.

Use the <spherical coordinates> parametrization
$$
\mathbf R(\phi,\theta)=(\sin\phi\cos\theta,\sin\phi\sin\theta,\cos\phi),
\qquad \alpha\leq\phi\leq\beta,\quad0\leq\theta\leq2\pi.
$$
Then $\mathbf R_\phi\times\mathbf R_\theta=\sin\phi\,\mathbf R$, so the outward <unit normal vector> is $\mathbf n=\mathbf R=\sin\phi\,\mathbf e_r+\cos\phi\,\mathbf e_z$ and the <surface area element> is $\sin\phi\,d\phi\,d\theta$. Since $\sin\phi>0$ throughout this band, the <vector field> is smooth near it. The <surface integral> of the <curl> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_\Sigma(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS
&=2\pi\int_\alpha^\beta\bigl[f(\cos\phi)\cos\phi-f'(\cos\phi)\sin^2\phi\bigr],d\phi\\
&=2\pi\int_\alpha^\beta\frac{d}{d\phi}\bigl[\sin\phi f(\cos\phi)\bigr],d\phi\\
&=\boxed{2\pi\bigl[\sin\beta f(\cos\beta)-\sin\alpha f(\cos\alpha)\bigr].}
\end{aligned}
$$
For the induced <boundary orientation>, the lower circle $\phi=\beta$ is traversed with increasing $\theta$, and the upper circle $\phi=\alpha$ with decreasing $\theta$. One way to check the signs is that the outward <conormal> is $\mathbf e_\phi$ at the lower circle and $-\mathbf e_\phi$ at the upper circle; the positive <unit tangent vector> is $\mathbf n\times\text{conormal}$.

Along a circle of fixed $\phi$, $d\mathbf r=\sin\phi\,\mathbf e_\theta\,d\theta$. Therefore the <circulation> is
$$
\oint_{\partial\Sigma}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
=\int_0^{2\pi} f(\cos\beta)\sin\beta\,d\theta
-\int_0^{2\pi}f(\cos\alpha)\sin\alpha\,d\theta,
$$
which equals the boxed <surface integral> and verifies <Stokes theorem> with the required <orientation>.

For a <continuously differentiable vector field> $\mathbf F$ on a neighborhood of the closure of $V$, the <divergence theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\iiint_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV=\iint_{\partial V}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,dS.}
$$
Here $\mathbf n$ is the outward <unit normal vector>; the right side is the outward <flux> through the <boundary>.

= Solution

Apply the <product rule> to each <vector component> and sum:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla\cdot(\phi\mathbf F)
&=\sum_{j=1}^3\partial_{x_j}(\phi F_j)\\
&=\phi\sum_{j=1}^3\partial_{x_j}F_j+\sum_{j=1}^3(\partial_{x_j}\phi)F_j\\
&=\boxed{\phi\nabla\cdot\mathbf F+\nabla\phi\cdot\mathbf F.}
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the <divergence> of a <scalar field> times a <vector field> contains a <divergence> term and a <dot product> with the <gradient> of the <scalar field>.

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

Choose $\mathbf F=\nabla\psi$ in the preceding <product rule> and apply the <divergence theorem>. Since $\nabla\cdot\nabla\psi=\Delta\psi$, this gives the first of <Green's identities>:
$$
\boxed{\iiint_V\bigl(\phi\Delta\psi+\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\psi\bigr),dV
=\iint_{\partial V}\phi\,\partial_n\psi\,dS.}
$$
Here $\partial_n\psi=\mathbf n\cdot\nabla\psi$ is the outward <normal derivative>. Interchange $\phi$ and $\psi$, then subtract. The <gradient> <dot products> cancel, yielding the second of <Green's identities>:
$$
\boxed{\iiint_V(\phi\Delta\psi-\psi\Delta\phi)\,dV
=\iint_{\partial V}(\phi\partial_n\psi-\psi\partial_n\phi)\,dS.}
$$
For example, $\phi,\psi\in C^2(\overline V)$ with the needed extensions suffice for these applications of the <divergence theorem>.

\mathbf r'(t)=(-\sin t,\cos t,\sinh t).

For the <surface area of a tricylinder>, permutation <symmetry> shows that each of the three <cylindrical surfaces> contributes the same <surface area> to the <boundary>. On the face $x^2+y^2=1$, put $x=\cos\theta$, $y=\sin\theta$. The remaining two inequalities require
$$
z^2\leq1-y^2=\cos^2\theta,\qquad
z^2\leq1-x^2=\sin^2\theta,
$$
so $|z|\leq\min(|\sin\theta|,|\cos\theta|)$. The <surface area element> on this unit <circular cylinder> is $d\theta\,dz$. There are eight congruent angular sectors, with the minimum equal to $\sin\theta$ for $0\leq\theta\leq\pi/4$. Thus the <area> of this face is
$$
A_1=\int_0^{2\pi}2\min(|\sin\theta|,|\cos\theta|)\,d\theta
=16\int_0^{\pi/4}\sin\theta\,d\theta
=8(2-\sqrt2).
$$
Every <boundary point> lies on at least one of these three <cylindrical surfaces>; their pairwise intersections are <curves> of zero <surface area>. Adding the three faces therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Area}(\partial R_2)=24(2-\sqrt2).}
$$

The <Fourier cosine series> on $[0,L]$ is
$$
f(x)\sim\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L},
\qquad
\boxed{a_n=\frac2L\int_0^Lf(x)\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx\quad(n\geq0).}
$$
It is the <Fourier series> of the <even extension> to $[-L,L]$, repeated with <period> $2L$. The <Fourier sine series> is
$$
f(x)\sim\sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},
\qquad
\boxed{b_n=\frac2L\int_0^Lf(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx\quad(n\geq1).}
$$
It comes from the <odd extension> and its $2L$-<periodic extension>. These <Fourier coefficient> formulas follow by <Fourier orthogonality>, since every nonconstant <sine> or <cosine> has squared <integral> $L/2$ on $[0,L]$, while the <constant function> has squared <integral> $L$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Under the stated piecewise smooth hypotheses, the <Dirichlet convergence theorem for Fourier series> gives the average of the two one-sided limits of the corresponding <periodic extension>. The <even extension> is continuous at both endpoints, so the <Fourier cosine series> converges to
$$
\boxed{f(x)\quad\text{for every }x\in[0,L].}
$$
The <odd extension> agrees with $f$ on $(0,L)$, but its left and right limits are $-f(0),f(0)$ at zero and $f(L),-f(L)$ at the join $L$. Therefore the <Fourier sine series> converges to
$$
\boxed{\begin{cases}f(x),&0<x<L,\\0,&x=0\text{ or }x=L.\end{cases}}
$$
Indeed every <partial sum> of the <Fourier sine series> is already zero at both endpoints. The endpoint values agree with $f$ only when the corresponding <boundary values> vanish.

Set $u=x/L$ and $q=n\pi$. The required <integrals> follow from
$$
\int e^u\cos(qu)\,du=\frac{e^u(\cos(qu)+q\sin(qu))}{1+q^2},\qquad
\int e^u\sin(qu)\,du=\frac{e^u(\sin(qu)-q\cos(qu))}{1+q^2}.

Consequently the <Fourier coefficients> are
$$
\boxed{a_0=2(e-1),\qquad
 a_n=\frac{2[(-1)^ne-1]}{1+n^2\pi^2},\qquad
 b_n=\frac{2n\pi[1-(-1)^ne]}{1+n^2\pi^2}\quad(n\geq1).}
$$
Thus the <Fourier cosine series> is
$$
\boxed{e-1+2\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^ne-1}{1+n^2\pi^2}\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L},}
$$
and its sum is $e^{x/L}$ everywhere on $[0,L]$. The <Fourier sine series> is
$$
\boxed{2\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{n\pi[1-(-1)^ne]}{1+n^2\pi^2}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},}
$$
and its sum is $e^{x/L}$ on $(0,L)$ and zero at the two endpoints. The open and filled circles in the sketches distinguish these endpoint limits from the actual values of the sum.

= Solution

First, the <Fourier coefficients> of the <Fourier cosine series> decay as $O(n^{-2})$, whereas those of the <Fourier sine series> are of order $n^{-1}$. In particular, the <absolute convergence> bound for the <Fourier cosine series> gives a uniform tail estimate
$$
\sup_{0\leq x\leq L}|g(x)-C_N(x)|
\leq2(e+1)\sum_{n>N}\frac1{1+n^2\pi^2}=O(N^{-1}).
$$
Thus its tail is absolutely controlled using fewer <Fourier harmonics>. The <Fourier sine series> does not have an absolutely summable sequence of <Fourier coefficients>.

Second, the <even extension> is continuous, so its <Fourier cosine series> converges uniformly on the closed interval and approximates the nonzero endpoint limits correctly. The <odd periodic extension> has jumps, producing the <Gibbs phenomenon> in its <Fourier sine series> near zero and $L$. Every <Fourier sine series> <partial sum> is zero at both endpoints, so by approaching $L$ from inside $(0,L)$ one obtains
$$
\sup_{0<x<L}|g(x)-S_N(x)|\geq e.
$$
Hence the <Fourier sine series> approximation cannot converge uniformly even on the whole open interval, despite <pointwise convergence> at each interior point. **The <Fourier cosine series> approximation has faster <coefficient decay> and avoids the persistent errors near the endpoints.**

Take $\boxed{p(x)=x(L-x)}$. It vanishes at both endpoints. Its <odd periodic extension> is continuously differentiable across the joins, whereas its <even extension> has corners because $p'(0)=L$ and $p'(L)=-L$ are nonzero.

Two <integrations by parts>, with $q=n\pi/L$, give
$$
\int_0^Lp(x)\sin(qx)\,dx=\frac{2[1-(-1)^n]}{q^3},\qquad
\int_0^Lp(x)\cos(qx)\,dx=-\frac{L[1+(-1)^n]}{q^2}.
$$
The <Fourier coefficients> are therefore
$$
\boxed{b_n=\frac{4L^2[1-(-1)^n]}{n^3\pi^3},\qquad
 a_0=\frac{L^2}{3},\qquad
 a_n=-\frac{2L^2[1+(-1)^n]}{n^2\pi^2}.}
$$
The <Fourier sine series> has a uniform absolute tail $O(N^{-2})$, and each of its <partial sums> satisfies both zero <boundary values> exactly. The <Fourier cosine series> has an absolute tail bound $O(N^{-1})$; at $x=0$ its omitted even-index <Fourier coefficients> have the same sign and yield an error of order $N^{-1}$. This verifies a strictly faster uniform error bound for the sine approximation in this example.

Let $w$ be the difference of two sufficiently regular solutions of the <wave equation> with the prescribed data. By <linearity>, $w$ has zero <initial displacement>, zero <initial velocity>, and homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions>. Define its <wave energy> by
$$
E(t)=\frac12\int_0^\pi(w_t^2+w_x^2)\,dx\geq0.
$$
For $\alpha=0$, <differentiation under the integral sign> and <integration by parts> yield
$$
E'(t)=\int_0^\pi w_t(w_{tt}-w_{xx})\,dx+[w_tw_x]_0^\pi=0.
$$
The <boundary term> vanishes because $w=0$ at both fixed endpoints implies $w_t=0$ there. The zero <initial data> give $E(0)=0$, so $w_t=w_x=0$ for all times. Thus $w$ is spatially constant, and its zero <boundary values> give $\boxed{w=0}$. This proves <uniqueness> for the undamped <wave equation>.

For odd $n$, use $\sin^2x=(1-\cos2x)/2$ and the <product-to-sum formula>:
$$

\int_0^\pi\sin^2x\sin(nx)\,dx
&=\frac1n-\frac12\left(\frac1{n+2}+\frac1{n-2}\right)\\
&=-\frac4{n(n^2-4)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{b_n=\begin{cases}-\dfrac8{\pi n(n^2-4)},&n\text{ odd},\\0,&n\text{ even}.\end{cases}}
$$
In particular $b_1=8/(3\pi)$ and $b_3=-8/(15\pi)$. An explicit version of the <solution of a differential equation> is
$$
\boxed{y(x,t)=e^{-3t}\left[
\frac8{3\pi}\frac{\sinh(\sqrt8\,t)}{\sqrt8}\sin x
-\frac8{15\pi}t\sin3x
-\frac8\pi\sum_{\substack{n\geq5\\n\text{ odd}}}
\frac{\sin(\sqrt{n^2-9}\,t)\sin(nx)}{n(n^2-4)\sqrt{n^2-9}}
\right].}
$$
Only the first, <overdamped> <normal mode>, the third, <critically damped> <normal mode>, and the odd <underdamped> <normal modes> of index at least five occur. No odd-index <Fourier coefficient> vanishes.

Conservation of <thermal energy> and <Fourier's law> give the <heat equations>
$$
\boxed{\rho_1c_1\,T_{1t}=k_1T_{1xx}\quad(-L<x<0),\qquad
\rho_2c_2\,T_{2t}=k_2T_{2xx}\quad(0<x<L).}
$$
The <density> times the <specific heat capacity>, $\rho_i c_i$, is the <volumetric heat capacity>, and $k_i$ is the <thermal conductivity>.

The condition $T_1(-L,t)=0$ holds the left exterior face at a fixed zero reference <temperature>; $T_2(L,t)=0$ does the same for the right exterior face. The condition $T_1(0_-,t)=T_2(0_+,t)$ expresses perfect <thermal contact>, with no <temperature> jump at the interface. Finally,
$$
-k_1T_{1x}(0_-,t)=-k_2T_{2x}(0_+,t)
$$
expresses continuity of the signed <heat flux> in the positive $x$ direction: the interface neither stores nor produces <heat>.

For $\omega\ne0$, substitution of the <separated solutions> into the <heat equations> gives
$$
G_i''+\omega^2\alpha_i^2G_i=0,\qquad
\alpha_i=\sqrt{\frac{\rho_ic_i}{k_i}}.
$$
The zero exterior <boundary values> are incorporated by
$$
G_1(x)=A\sin[\omega\alpha_1(x+L)],\qquad
G_2(x)=B\sin[\omega\alpha_2(L-x)].
$$
To obtain a <eigenvalue equation for heat conduction in two joined slabs>, write $u=\omega\alpha_1L$ and $v=\omega\alpha_2L$. Continuity of <temperature> and <heat flux> at zero gives the homogeneous <linear system>
$$
A\sin u-B\sin v=0,\qquad
k_1\alpha_1A\cos u+k_2\alpha_2B\cos v=0.
$$
A nonzero amplitude pair exists precisely when its <determinant> vanishes, equivalently
$$
\boxed{D(\omega):=k_1\alpha_1\cos u\sin v+k_2\alpha_2\sin u\cos v=0,\qquad\omega\ne0.}
$$
If $\cos u\cos v\ne0$, division by $k_1k_2\alpha_1\alpha_2\cos u\cos v$ gives the printed <transcendental equation>
$$
\boxed{\frac{\tan(\omega\alpha_1L)}{\alpha_1k_1}
+\frac{\tan(\omega\alpha_2L)}{\alpha_2k_2}=0.}
$$
**The printed necessity claim needs the qualification that both <tangents> are defined.** If just one of $\cos u,\cos v$ vanishes, $D(\omega)\ne0$. If both <cosines> vanish, however, $D(\omega)=0$ and legitimate <normal modes> remain. Their <temperature> at the <thermal interface> need not be zero; the <temperature> condition fixes the amplitude ratio, while each <heat flux> is zero there.

For a concrete counterexample, take identical slabs: $\rho_1=\rho_2=\rho$, $c_1=c_2=c$, $k_1=k_2=k$, and $\alpha=\sqrt{\rho c/k}$. Then
$$
\boxed{T_1(x,t)=T_2(x,t)=e^{-\omega^2t}\cos\frac{\pi x}{2L},\qquad
\omega=\frac{\pi}{2\alpha L},}
$$
with each formula restricted to its own slab, satisfies both <heat equations> and all four <boundary conditions>. But $\omega\alpha L=\pi/2$, so neither <tangent> is defined. This directly disproves the unqualified printed assertion. The <determinant> condition retains these missing <normal modes>.

Finally, $\omega=0$ must be checked before dividing by it. Then $G_1=A(x+L)$, $G_2=B(L-x)$, and interface continuity gives $A=B$, while <heat flux> continuity gives $k_1A=-k_2B$. Positivity of the <thermal conductivities> forces $A=B=0$. Thus $\boxed{\omega=0\text{ gives only the trivial solution}.}$ In the <determinant> formula, its apparent zero at $\omega=0$ is spurious because the chosen sine basis degenerates there.

Choose the positive weights
$$
\boxed{a=\rho_1c_1,\qquad b=\rho_2c_2.}
$$
These are the <volumetric heat capacities>. The <heat equations> and <integration by parts> give
$$
\begin{aligned}
I'(t)
&=2k_1\int_{-L}^0T_1T_{1xx}\,dx+2k_2\int_0^LT_2T_{2xx}\,dx\\
&=2k_1[T_1T_{1x}]_{-L}^{0}+2k_2[T_2T_{2x}]_0^L
-2k_1\int_{-L}^0T_{1x}^2\,dx-2k_2\int_0^LT_{2x}^2\,dx.
\end{aligned}
$$
The exterior <boundary terms> vanish because the <temperatures> there are zero. At the <thermal interface>, the remaining term is
$$
2T(0,t)\,[k_1T_{1x}(0_-,t)-k_2T_{2x}(0_+,t)]=0
$$
by continuity of <temperature> and <heat flux>. Hence the weighted <energy estimate> is
$$
\boxed{I'(t)=-2k_1\int_{-L}^0T_{1x}^2\,dx-2k_2\int_0^LT_{2x}^2\,dx\leq0.}
$$
Both weights are positive, so $I\geq0$. Zero initial <temperatures> give $I(0)=0$, forcing $I(t)=0$ for all $t>0$. Each <temperature> therefore vanishes throughout its slab. Applying this argument to a difference also proves <uniqueness> for arbitrary common <initial data> in this <thermal interface problem>.

For any bounded <volume> $V$ with piecewise smooth closed <boundary> and outward <unit normal vector> $\mathbf n$, <Gauss's law> in integral form is
$$
\boxed{\iint_{\partial V}\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf n\,dS
=\frac{Q_V}{\epsilon_0},\qquad Q_V=\iiint_V\rho\,dV.}
$$
Thus the outward <electric flux> equals the enclosed <electric charge> divided by the <vacuum permittivity>. To prove it, apply the <divergence theorem> to the <electric field> and use its given <divergence>:
$$
\iint_{\partial V}\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf n\,dS
=\iiint_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf E\,dV
=\frac1{\epsilon_0}\iiint_V\rho\,dV.
$$
The classical proof assumes the requisite differentiability of the <electric field>. For piecewise smooth <charge densities>, one may apply the <divergence theorem> piece by piece and cancel the internal <fluxes>.

The space $\mathbb R^3$ is <simply connected>, and the <electric field> has zero <curl>. Hence it is a <conservative vector field>. More explicitly, <Stokes theorem> gives zero <circulation> around any closed loop, since such a loop bounds a spanning <surface> in $\mathbb R^3$. Therefore the <line integral> between two fixed endpoints is independent of the chosen path.

Fix a reference point $\mathbf r_0$ and define the <electric potential>
$$
\varphi(\mathbf r)=-\int_{\mathbf r_0}^{\mathbf r}\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell+C.
$$
Taking a short straight terminal segment in each coordinate direction shows $\partial_j\varphi=-E_j$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E=-\nabla\varphi.}
$$
The <electric potential> is determined up to an additive constant; the later condition at infinity fixes that constant for the finite <charge distribution> in part (b).

By the assumed <spherical symmetry> of the <electric potential>, the <electric field> is radial: $\mathbf E=E_r(r)\mathbf e_r$ for $r>0$. On a <sphere> of radius $r$, <Gauss's law> therefore reads
$$
4\pi r^2E_r(r)=\frac{Q(r)}{\epsilon_0},\qquad
Q(r)=4\pi\int_0^r\rho(s)s^2\,ds.
$$
Put $R=a(1+\delta)$. For $r<a$, the enclosed <electric charge> is $4\pi\rho_0r^3/3$. In the <spherical shell>, the factor $s^2$ in the <volume element> cancels $s^{-2}$ in the <charge density>, so
$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(r)&=\frac{4\pi\rho_0a^3}{3}
+\frac{4\pi\rho_0a^2}{\delta}\int_a^rh'(s/a)\,ds\\
&=4\pi\rho_0a^3\left[\frac13+\frac{h(r/a)-h(1)}{\delta}\right].
\end{aligned}
$$
It follows that
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(\mathbf r)=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\rho_0}{3\epsilon_0}\mathbf r,&0\leq r<a,\\
\dfrac{\rho_0a^3}{\epsilon_0r^2}\left[\dfrac13+\dfrac{h(r/a)-h(1)}{\delta}\right]\mathbf e_r,&a\leq r<R,\\
\dfrac{\rho_0a^3}{\epsilon_0r^2}\left[\dfrac13+\dfrac{h(1+\delta)-h(1)}{\delta}\right]\mathbf e_r,&r\geq R.
\end{cases}}
$$
At the origin the first formula gives $\mathbf E=\mathbf0$ without needing a radial <unit vector>. At both shell interfaces the adjacent formulas agree: for every fixed $\delta>0$, there is no <surface charge> concentrated on either <sphere>, and the <electric field> is continuous there. No sign restriction on $h'$ has been assumed, so the shell <charge density> and the radial <electric field> can be negative.

Set $r=R$ in the enclosed <electric charge> formula:
$$
\boxed{Q=4\pi\rho_0a^3\left[\frac13+\frac{h(1+\delta)-h(1)}{\delta}\right].}
$$
Outside the entire <charge distribution>, <Gauss's law> gives $E_r(r)=Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)$. Integrating $E_r=-\varphi'$ with $\varphi(\infty)=0$ gives
$$
\boxed{\varphi(r)=\int_r^\infty\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0s^2}\,ds
=\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0r},\qquad r>R.}
$$
Thus the exterior <electric potential> and <electric field> are exactly those of a <point charge> $Q$ placed at the center. This is the <electrostatic shell theorem>: outside a <spherically symmetric charge distribution>, only its total <electric charge> affects the <electric field>, not how that <electric charge> is distributed radially. If positive and negative <electric charges> cancel to give $Q=0$, both exterior quantities vanish.

For the <thin spherical charge layer limit>, smoothness of $h$ gives
$$
\frac{h(1+\delta)-h(1)}\delta\longrightarrow h'(1).
$$
Thus the shell's total <electric charge> tends to $4\pi\rho_0a^3h'(1)$ while its thickness tends to zero. At each fixed $r>a$, the observation point eventually lies outside the shell, whereas at each fixed $r<a$ the interior <electric field> is unchanged. The limiting <electric field> away from the <sphere> is therefore
$$
\mathbf E_0(\mathbf r)=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\rho_0}{3\epsilon_0}\mathbf r,&r<a,\\
\dfrac{\rho_0a^3}{\epsilon_0r^2}\left[\dfrac13+h'(1)\right]\mathbf e_r,&r>a.
\end{cases}
$$
Its outward-minus-inward jump is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E_0(a^+)-\mathbf E_0(a^-)=\frac{\rho_0a h'(1)}{\epsilon_0}\mathbf e_r.}
$$
Physically, the limit is a uniformly charged <ball> of <charge density> $\rho_0$, together with an infinitesimally thin <spherical shell> of uniform <surface charge density>
$$
\boxed{\sigma=\rho_0a h'(1).}
$$
Indeed its <electric charge> is $4\pi a^2\sigma$, and the standard normal <electric field> jump is $\sigma/\epsilon_0$. The <surface charge density> can have either sign, or vanish, according to $h'(1)$.

**The order of limits matters.** For every finite $\delta>0$, taking the two one-sided limits at $r=a$ first gives zero jump. The displayed nonzero jump is obtained by first taking $\delta\to0$ at fixed points on either side, then approaching $a$. The value at the <surface> itself need not coincide with either trace of the limiting <electric field>.
