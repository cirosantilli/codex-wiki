For the <linear map> $T:V\to W$, its <image of a linear map> and <kernel of a linear map> are
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{im}T=\{T(v):v\in V\},\qquad\ker T=\{v\in V:T(v)=0\}.}
$$
Both contain the <zero vector>, since $T(0)=0$. If $y_1=T(v_1)$ and $y_2=T(v_2)$ belong to the <image of a linear map>, then for any <real numbers> $a,b$,
$$
ay_1+by_2=T(av_1+bv_2)\in\operatorname{im}T.
$$
Thus $\operatorname{im}T$ is a <vector subspace> of $W$. If $u_1,u_2\in\ker T$, then
$$
T(au_1+bu_2)=aT(u_1)+bT(u_2)=0,
$$
so $\ker T$ is a <vector subspace> of $V$.

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

The <rank of a linear map> and <nullity of a linear map> are
$$
r(T)=\dim\operatorname{im}T,\qquad n(T)=\dim\ker T.
$$
The <rank-nullity theorem> states that
$$
\boxed{r(T)+n(T)=\dim V=n.}
$$
To prove it, choose a <basis> $u_1,\ldots,u_k$ of the <kernel of a linear map> and use <basis extension> to obtain a <basis>
$$
u_1,\ldots,u_k,v_1,\ldots,v_{n-k}
$$
of $V$. The <vectors> $T(v_1),\ldots,T(v_{n-k})$ <span> the <image of a linear map>: applying $T$ to an arbitrary <linear combination> of the displayed <basis> removes all the $u_i$ terms. They are also <linearly independent>. Indeed,
$$
\sum_j a_jT(v_j)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\sum_j a_jv_j\in\ker T,
$$
so $\sum_j a_jv_j=\sum_i b_iu_i$ for some <scalars> $b_i$. The <linear independence> of the extended <basis> forces every $a_j$ and $b_i$ to vanish. Consequently $r(T)=n-k$ and $n(T)=k$, proving the identity. The same argument covers an empty <basis> when one of these <dimensions> is zero.

The necessary and sufficient condition is
$$
\boxed{\ker T=\{0\}.}
$$
For necessity, if $ST=\operatorname{id}_V$ and $v\in\ker T$, then $v=S(Tv)=S(0)=0$.

For sufficiency, let $e_1,e_2$ be a <basis> of $V$ and suppose the <kernel of a linear map> is trivial. The <vectors> $Te_1,Te_2$ are <linearly independent>, since $aTe_1+bTe_2=0$ implies $ae_1+be_2\in\ker T$. Extend them to a <basis> $Te_1,Te_2,w_3$ of $W$. Define a <linear map> $S$ by
$$
S(Te_1)=e_1,\qquad S(Te_2)=e_2,\qquad S(w_3)=0.
$$
Then $ST$ agrees with the <identity operator> on a <basis>, hence on all of $V$. Thus a <left inverse> exists precisely when $T$ is <injective>; this is the <linear left inverse criterion>.

An <elementary row operation> is one of three reversible changes: interchange two rows; multiply one row by a nonzero <scalar>; or add a <scalar multiple> of one row to a different row. Their inverses are, respectively, the same interchange, multiplication by the reciprocal, and addition of the negative <scalar multiple>.

Applied to an <augmented matrix>, these changes interchange <linear equations>, rescale an equation by a nonzero factor, or add a multiple of one equation to another. Every old solution satisfies the new equations. Applying the inverse <elementary row operation> gives the converse, so **the solution set is unchanged** at every step. Equivalently, all the operations together give
$$
[\widetilde A\mid\widetilde b]=E[A\mid b],\qquad\widetilde Ax=\widetilde b\iff EAx=Eb\iff Ax=b,
$$
where $E$ is an <invertible matrix>, a product of <elementary matrices>.

\boxed{\begin{aligned}

\alpha=2,\ \beta=0&:\quad\{(-1,0,0)+t(1,1,1):t\in\mathbb R\}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The second case is an <affine line in a vector space>, so it contains infinitely many solutions. These cases exhaust all <real numbers> $\alpha,\beta$.

The prescribed $b=(-1,\beta,1)^T$ satisfies the displayed condition precisely when $\beta=0$. If $\beta\ne0$, it lies outside the <image of a linear map> and there is no solution. The two nonzero proper <invariant subspaces> are
$$
\boxed{U=\operatorname{span}\{e_3\},\qquad W=\operatorname{span}\{e_1,e_2\}.}
$$
Both are preserved by the <rotation>. To show there are no others, note that
$$
P=\frac{I+T^2}{2},\qquad P(x,y,z)=(0,0,z)
$$
is the <linear projection> onto $U$, and $I-P$ is the <linear projection> onto $W$. Every <invariant subspace> $L$ is preserved by $I,T^2$, and hence by both of these <linear projections>. Therefore
$$
L=(L\cap U)\oplus(L\cap W).
$$
Because $U$ has <dimension> one, $L\cap U$ is either $0$ or $U$. If $L\cap W$ contains a nonzero $v=(a,b,0)$, it also contains $Tv=(-b,a,0)$. These two <vectors> are <linearly independent>, because their two-dimensional <determinant> is $a^2+b^2>0$. They <span> $W$, so $L\cap W$ is either $0$ or $W$. The only possibilities for $L$ are therefore $0,U,W,V$.

Finally every $(x,y,z)$ has the unique decomposition $(0,0,z)+(x,y,0)$, with one summand in each of $U,W$. Hence
$$
\boxed{V=U\oplus W.}
$$
This also illustrates how <invariant subspaces of a quarter-turn in three dimensions> can be classified by <linear projections>.

For a <subgroup> $H$ of $G$, the <left coset> represented by $g$ is
$$
\boxed{gH=\{gh:h\in H\}.}
$$
Suppose two <left cosets> intersect. There are $h_1,h_2\in H$ such that $g_1h_1=g_2h_2$, so
$$
g_1=g_2h_2h_1^{-1}.
$$
Because $H$ is a <subgroup>, $h_2h_1^{-1}\in H$. Thus, for every $h\in H$, $g_1h=g_2(h_2h_1^{-1}h)\in g_2H$, proving $g_1H\subseteq g_2H$. Interchanging the two representatives gives the reverse inclusion. Therefore **two <left cosets> are equal or disjoint**.

<Lagrange's theorem> states that if $H$ is a <subgroup> of a <finite group> $G$, then
$$
\boxed{|G|=[G:H]|H|,\qquad |H|\mid|G|,}
$$
where the <index of a subgroup> $[G:H]$ is the number of distinct <left cosets>.

Every $g\in G$ lies in $gH$, since $g=ge$. By <5/a/i>, distinct <left cosets> are disjoint, so these <sets> form a <partition of a set> $G$. Each has <cardinality> $|H|$: the <function> $h\mapsto gh$ from $H$ to $gH$ is a <bijection>, with inverse $gh\mapsto h$, by cancellation in a <group>. Counting the elements in this finite <partition of a set> gives $|G|=[G:H]|H|$, proving the theorem.

The <order of a group element> $g$ is the smallest positive integer $d$ with $g^d=e$. Such an integer exists in a <finite group>: two powers in $e,g,g^2,\ldots$ must agree, and cancellation gives a positive power equal to $e$.

By minimality, $e,g,\ldots,g^{d-1}$ are distinct. Every integer power of $g$ is one of them, by division of its exponent by $d$. They form the <cyclic subgroup>
$$
\langle g\rangle=\{e,g,\ldots,g^{d-1}\},\qquad|\langle g\rangle|=d.
$$
Applying <Lagrange's theorem> to this <subgroup> gives
$$
\boxed{o(g)=|\langle g\rangle|\mid|G|.}
$$

are distinct: $ab=e$ would give $a=b$, while $ab=a$ or $ab=b$ would make $b=e$ or $a=e$. Commutativity and $a^2=b^2=e$ show that products stay in $H$, and every element is its own inverse. More explicitly,
$$
\boxed{C_2\times C_2\longrightarrow H,\qquad(i,j)\longmapsto a^ib^j\quad(i,j\in\{0,1\})}
$$
is a <group isomorphism>, with addition of exponents modulo two. Hence $G$ contains a <subgroup> isomorphic to the <Klein four-group>.

The converse is false. The <direct product of groups> $C_2\times C_2\times C_3$ contains $C_2\times C_2\times\{e\}$, but it has elements of <order of a group element> three. Thus the existence of the specified <subgroup> does not force every nonidentity element to have <order of a group element> two.

The complete classification up to <group isomorphism> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|l}
|G|&G\\\hline
1&C_1\\
2&C_2\\
3&C_3\\
4&C_4,\ C_2\times C_2\\
5&C_5\\
6&C_6,\ S_3
\end{array}}
$$
Here $C_1$ is the <trivial group>. The <prime number> cases follow from <6/a/i>. For four elements, an element of <order of a group element> four generates $C_4$. If there is none, <Lagrange's theorem> makes every nonidentity element have <order of a group element> two. Then <6/a/iv> produces a four-element <subgroup>, which must be all of $G$, giving the <Klein four-group>.

For six elements, an element of <order of a group element> six gives $C_6$. Suppose there is none. If there were no element of <order of a group element> three either, all nonidentity elements would have <order of a group element> two. Part <6/a/iv> would then produce a <subgroup> of <cardinality> four, contradicting <Lagrange's theorem>. Thus choose $a$ of <order of a group element> three. There is also an <involution> $b$: otherwise the five nonidentity elements could be paired into distinct pairs $\{g,g^{-1}\}$, impossible for an odd number of elements.

The <subgroup> $H=\langle a\rangle$ has <index of a subgroup> two, so it is a <normal subgroup>. Indeed, for $g\notin H$, both $gH$ and $Hg$ are the complement of $H$, and for $g\in H$ both equal $H$. Consequently $bab^{-1}$ is either $a$ or $a^{-1}$. The first possibility makes $a,b$ commute, and $ab$ then has <order of a group element> six: $(ab)^k=e$ forces $a^k=b^{-k}\in\langle a\rangle\cap\langle b\rangle=\{e\}$, so both $3$ and $2$ divide $k$. This contradicts our assumption. Therefore
$$
bab^{-1}=a^{-1}.
$$
Every element is uniquely $a^i$ or $a^ib$, with $0\leq i<3$, and the relation $ba=a^{-1}b$ determines all their products. Sending $a$ to $(123)$ and $b$ to $(12)$ gives a <group isomorphism> with the <symmetric group> $S_3$. This completes the classification of <groups of order at most six>.

Of these <groups>, **exactly $C_1,C_2,C_3,C_4,C_2\times C_2,S_3$ embed in $S_4$**. Concrete <subgroups> are
$$
\{e\},\quad\langle(12)\rangle,\quad\langle(123)\rangle,\quad
\langle(1234)\rangle,\quad
\{e,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\},\quad
\{\sigma\in S_4:\sigma(4)=4\}.
$$
There is no $C_5$ <subgroup>, since $5$ does not divide $24$. There is no $C_6$ <subgroup> either: the <order of a group element> in a <symmetric group> is the <least common multiple> of the lengths in its <disjoint cycle decomposition>, and on four letters the only possibilities are $1,2,3,4$.

Label the vertices of a <regular tetrahedron> $1,2,3,4$, and include all its <Euclidean isometries>, both <rotations> and <reflections>. The following list describes every <subgroup> of <cardinality> at most six, allowing arbitrary relabelling of the vertices. The numbers count actual <subgroups>, rather than just their <group isomorphism> types.

* **One trivial <subgroup>** contains only the <identity element>.
* **Six reflection <subgroups> $C_2$** are generated by a <transposition>, for example $(12)$. Its mirror plane contains vertices $3,4$ and the midpoint of the opposite edge $12$.
* **Three half-turn <subgroups> $C_2$** are generated by a product of two disjoint <transpositions>, for example $(12)(34)$. The <rotation> axis joins the midpoints of the opposite edges $12$ and $34$.
* **Four <cyclic subgroups> $C_3$** consist of the three <rotations> about an axis joining a vertex to the centre of its opposite face. For example, $\langle(123)\rangle$ fixes vertex $4$.
* **Three <cyclic subgroups> $C_4$** are generated by an <improper orthogonal transformation>: a quarter-turn about an axis through opposite edge midpoints, composed with a <reflection> in the perpendicular plane through the centre. For $\langle(1234)\rangle$, the axis joins the midpoints of edges $13$ and $24$. Its square is the half-turn $(13)(24)$.
* **One rotational <Klein four-group>** consists of the <identity element> and the three half-turns about opposite-edge midpoint axes:
  $$
  \{e,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}.
  $$
* **Three further <Klein four-groups>** arise from a pair of opposite edges. For edges $12,34$, the <subgroup> is
  $$
  \{e,(12),(34),(12)(34)\}.
  $$
  It contains two <reflections> in perpendicular planes and their product, the half-turn about the line where those planes meet. It preserves each of the two opposite edges as a <set>.
* **Four <subgroups> $S_3$** are the <stabilizer subgroups> of individual vertices. Each contains three <rotations> about the vertex-to-opposite-face axis and three <reflections> in planes containing that axis. For the fixed vertex $4$, this is every <permutation> of $1,2,3$.

Here is why this is exhaustive. Part <6/b> leaves only the <trivial group>, $C_2,C_3,C_4,C_2\times C_2,S_3$. The <disjoint cycle decomposition> gives exactly the two listed kinds of <involutions>, the three-cycles, and the four-cycles, accounting for all the <cyclic subgroups>. In a <Klein four-group>, the nonidentity elements commute. If it contains $(12)$, any other nonidentity element must be a commuting <involution>, hence $(34)$ or $(12)(34)$; either choice yields the displayed opposite-edge <subgroup>. If it has no <transposition>, its three nonidentity elements must be all three double <transpositions>, yielding the rotational <subgroup>.

Finally, an $S_3$ <subgroup> has a unique <cyclic subgroup> of <cardinality> three. Its two three-cycles fix the same unique vertex. Every element of the $S_3$ <subgroup> preserves this vertex, since <conjugation> preserves that unique <cyclic subgroup>. Thus it is the full <stabilizer subgroup> of that vertex. The counts in the list are consequently exhaustive, giving
$$
\boxed{1+6+3+4+3+1+3+4=25\text{ subgroups of order at most }6.}
$$
The two kinds of $C_2$ and the two kinds of <Klein four-groups> have different geometric behavior despite having the same abstract <group isomorphism> type within each pair. This is the distinction recorded by the <small subgroups of tetrahedral symmetry>.

A left <group action> of $G$ on a <set> $S$ is a <function> $G\times S\to S$, written $(g,s)\mapsto g\cdot s$, satisfying
$$
\boxed{e\cdot s=s,\qquad(gh)\cdot s=g\cdot(h\cdot s)}
$$
for all $g,h\in G$, $s\in S$. The <orbit of a group action> through $s$ and the <stabilizer subgroup> of $s$ are, respectively,
$$
\boxed{G\cdot s=\{g\cdot s:g\in G\},\qquad G_s=\{g\in G:g\cdot s=s\}.}
$$
The <identity element> belongs to $G_s$. If $g,h\in G_s$, then $(gh)\cdot s=g\cdot(h\cdot s)=g\cdot s=s$, so $gh\in G_s$. If $g\in G_s$, applying $g^{-1}$ to $g\cdot s=s$ gives $s=g^{-1}\cdot s$, so $g^{-1}\in G_s$. These facts prove that the <stabilizer subgroup> is a <subgroup> of $G$.

The <orbit-stabilizer theorem> identifies the <orbit of a group action> of $s$ with the <set> of <left cosets> of $G_s$. Explicitly, there is a <bijection>
$$
\boxed{G/G_s\longrightarrow G\cdot s,\qquad gG_s\longmapsto g\cdot s.}
$$
Here $G/G_s$ denotes a <set> of <cosets>; it is not asserted to be a <quotient group>. To prove the identification, observe that
$$
gG_s=hG_s\iff h^{-1}g\in G_s
\iff(h^{-1}g)\cdot s=s
\iff g\cdot s=h\cdot s.
$$
This equivalence proves both well-definedness and <injectivity>. Every element of the <orbit of a group action> has the form $g\cdot s$, proving <surjectivity>.

In particular, for a <finite group>, the <cardinalities> satisfy
$$
\boxed{|G\cdot s|=[G:G_s]=\frac{|G|}{|G_s|},\qquad|G|=|G\cdot s|\,|G_s|.}
$$
The last equality follows from <Lagrange's theorem> applied to the <stabilizer subgroup>.

The <orbits of a group action> form a <partition of a set> $S$: every element lies in its own <orbit of a group action>, and two intersecting <orbits of a group action> coincide because each point can be moved back and then forward using <inverse elements>. By the <orbit-stabilizer theorem>, each <orbit of a group action> has <cardinality> dividing $8$, so its size is $1,2,4$ or $8$.

If every <orbit of a group action> had size $2,4$ or $8$, their sizes would add to an even number, contrary to $|S|=17$. Therefore there is a one-element <orbit of a group action>, say $\{s'\}$. Every $g\in G$ sends $s'$ into this same singleton, so
$$
\boxed{g\cdot s'=s'\quad\text{for every }g\in G.}
$$
Thus $s'$ is a <fixed point of a group action>. In fact the number of <fixed points of a group action> is odd, since all other <orbits of a group action> have even <cardinality>.

For a <free group action>, every <stabilizer subgroup> is $\{e\}$. By the <orbit-stabilizer theorem>, every <orbit of a group action> has <cardinality> $|G|$. Hence the two <free group actions> have the same number
$$
r=\frac{|S|}{|G|}
$$
of <orbits of a group action>. Choose one representative $s_i$ from each $\cdot$ <orbit of a group action> and one representative $t_i$ from each $*$ <orbit of a group action>, and match them using $1\leq i\leq r$.

Every $s\in S$ has a unique expression $s=g\cdot s_i$. The <orbit of a group action> determines $i$, and the <free group action> property determines $g$: if $g\cdot s_i=h\cdot s_i$, then $(h^{-1}g)\cdot s_i=s_i$, forcing $g=h$. Therefore the rule
$$
\boxed{f(g\cdot s_i)=g*t_i}
$$
defines a <function> $f:S\to S$ without ambiguity. For $h\in G$,
$$
f\bigl(h\cdot(g\cdot s_i)\bigr)
=f\bigl((hg)\cdot s_i\bigr)
=(hg)*t_i
=h*(g*t_i)
=h*f(g\cdot s_i),
$$
which proves the required identity for every $s$.

Indeed, $f$ is a <bijection>: its inverse is $g*t_i\mapsto g\cdot s_i$, well defined because the second is also a <free group action>. If $S$ is empty, the unique empty <function> already satisfies the identity. Thus the result holds in every case, and proves the stronger statement that <free finite actions with equal cardinality are isomorphic>.
