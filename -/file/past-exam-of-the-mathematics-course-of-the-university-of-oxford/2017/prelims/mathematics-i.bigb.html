The <vectors> are <linearly independent> if
$$
\sum_{j=1}^n a_jv_j=0,\quad a_j\in\mathbb R
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a_1=\cdots=a_n=0.
$$
They <span> $V$ if every $v\in V$ is a <linear combination> $v=\sum_{j=1}^n a_jv_j$ with real <coefficients>. They form a <basis> of $V$ if they are <linearly independent> and <span> $V$. Equivalently, every $v\in V$ has exactly one such representation: existence is the <spanning set> condition, and subtracting two representations shows that uniqueness is precisely <linear independence>.

The <Steinitz exchange lemma> states that if $u_1,\ldots,u_r$ are <linearly independent> and $w_1,\ldots,w_s$ <span> a <vector space> $V$, then $r\leq s$ and some $r$ of the $w_j$ can be replaced by $u_1,\ldots,u_r$ while preserving the <spanning set> property.

Here is the exchange argument. Suppose that after $k$ replacements, a <spanning set> is
$$
u_1,\ldots,u_k,w_{k+1},\ldots,w_s,
$$
where the remaining $w_j$ have been relabelled if necessary. Express $u_{k+1}$ as a <linear combination> of this family. At least one remaining $w_j$ has a nonzero <coefficient>; otherwise $u_{k+1}$ belongs to the <span> of $u_1,\ldots,u_k$, contradicting <linear independence>. Relabel this entry $w_{k+1}$. Solving for $w_{k+1}$ shows that it lies in the <span> of
$$
u_1,\ldots,u_{k+1},w_{k+2},\ldots,w_s.
$$
The new family therefore still <spans> $V$. Starting at $k=0$ repeats this step until all $r$ independent <vectors> have been inserted. If $r>s$, after $s$ steps the next $u_{s+1}$ would lie in the <span> of $u_1,\ldots,u_s$, a contradiction. Thus $r\leq s$, and the construction proves the replacement assertion as well.

Put $w_1=v_1$ and $w_j=v_j-v_1$ for $j\geq2$. Since $v_1=w_1$ and $v_j=w_j+w_1$, the two families have the same <linear span>. Moreover,
$$
\sum_{j=1}^n a_jw_j
=\left(a_1-\sum_{j=2}^n a_j\right)v_1+\sum_{j=2}^n a_jv_j.
$$
If the $v_j$ are <linearly independent>, a vanishing expression forces $a_j=0$ for $j\geq2$ and then $a_1=0$. Conversely,
$$
\sum_{j=1}^n b_jv_j
=\left(\sum_{j=1}^n b_j\right)w_1+\sum_{j=2}^n b_jw_j,
$$
so <linear independence> of the $w_j$ forces all $b_j=0$. Thus both the <spanning set> condition and <linear independence> are equivalent for the two families, proving the desired equivalence of <bases>.

Write $u=(a,b)^T$, $v=(c,d)^T$ and $\Delta=ad-bc$. If $xu+yv=0$, then
$$
ax+cy=0,\qquad bx+dy=0.
$$
Eliminating $y$ gives $\Delta x=0$, and eliminating $x$ gives $\Delta y=0$. Hence $\Delta\ne0$ implies <linear independence>.

For the converse, suppose $\Delta=0$. If $u=0$, the family already contains the <zero vector> and is <linearly dependent>. If $a\ne0$, the equality $ad=bc$ gives $v=(c/a)u$. If $a=0$ but $u\ne0$, then $b\ne0$ and $bc=0$ forces $c=0$, so $v=(d/b)u$. Both cases give a nontrivial <linear relation>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{u,v\text{ are linearly independent}\iff ad-bc\ne0.}
$$
This is the two-dimensional <determinant> criterion, proved directly without assuming that criterion.

The <preimage> is
$$
T^{-1}(X)=\{u\in U:T(u)\in X\}.
$$
It contains $0$, and if $u,v$ belong to it and $a,b\in\mathbb R$, then $T(au+bv)=aT(u)+bT(v)\in X$, since $X$ is a <vector subspace>. Thus $T^{-1}(X)$ is a <vector subspace>. It contains the <kernel> of $T$ because $0\in X$.

To establish the <dimension of the preimage of a vector subspace> formula without assuming the theorem to be deduced, choose a <basis> $k_1,\ldots,k_{n_T}$ of $\ker T$ and a <basis> $y_1,\ldots,y_d$ of $X\cap\operatorname{im}T$, where $d=\dim(X\cap\operatorname{im}T)$. For each $j$, choose $u_j\in U$ with $T(u_j)=y_j$. These $u_j$ lie in $T^{-1}(X)$.

The family $k_1,\ldots,k_{n_T},u_1,\ldots,u_d$ is <linearly independent>: applying $T$ to a vanishing <linear combination> forces all $u_j$ <coefficients> to vanish by <linear independence> of the $y_j$, and then all remaining <coefficients> vanish by <linear independence> of the $k_i$. It also <spans> $T^{-1}(X)$: for $u$ in that <preimage>, write $T(u)=\sum_j b_jy_j$. Then $u-\sum_j b_ju_j\in\ker T$, so it is a <linear combination> of the $k_i$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\dim T^{-1}(X)=\dim(X\cap\operatorname{im}T)+n_T.}
$$
Set $X=V$. Then $T^{-1}(V)=U$ and $V\cap\operatorname{im}T=\operatorname{im}T$, giving the <rank-nullity theorem>:
$$
\boxed{\dim U=r_T+n_T.}
$$

Choose an <ordered basis> $\mathcal B$ of $V$ and let $A=[T]_{\mathcal B}$. Define the <characteristic polynomial> by
$$
\boxed{\chi_T(x)=\det(xI-A).}
$$
If a different <basis> gives $B=P^{-1}AP$ for an <invertible matrix> $P$, multiplicativity of the <determinant> gives
$$
\det(xI-B)=\det\bigl(P^{-1}(xI-A)P\bigr)
=\det(P^{-1})\det(xI-A)\det P
=\det(xI-A).
$$
Thus the definition is independent of the chosen <basis>. The alternate convention $\det(A-xI)$ differs by the constant factor $(-1)^{\dim V}$ and gives the same roots.

A real number $\lambda$ is an <eigenvalue> of $T$ if $Tv=\lambda v$ for some nonzero <vector> $v\in V$. Its <eigenspace> is
$$
E_\lambda=\{v\in V:Tv=\lambda v\}=\ker(T-\lambda I).
$$
It is a <vector subspace> because it contains $0$ and $T(av+bw)=aTv+bTw=\lambda(av+bw)$ whenever $v,w\in E_\lambda$ and $a,b\in\mathbb R$.

If $v\in E_\lambda\cap E_\mu$, then $\lambda v=Tv=\mu v$, so $(\lambda-\mu)v=0$. Since $\lambda\ne\mu$, this forces $v=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{E_\lambda\cap E_\mu=\{0\}.}
$$

A <transposition> in the <symmetric group> $S_n$ exchanges two distinct symbols and fixes all others. A <permutation cycle> $(a_1\ a_2\ \cdots\ a_k)$ sends $a_j$ to $a_{j+1}$, sends $a_k$ to $a_1$, and fixes the other symbols. Its entries are distinct. For $k=1$ this is the identity <permutation>.

A <group element> has <order of a group element> $k$ if $k$ is the least positive integer for which its $k$th power is the <identity element>. For the displayed <permutation cycle>, its $j$th power sends $a_i$ forward $j$ places, with subscripts read modulo $k$. Hence the $k$th power fixes every symbol, whereas for $0<j<k$ the image of $a_1$ is different from $a_1$. Thus its <order of a group element> is exactly $k$, including $k=1$.

For each symbol $a$, iterate the <permutation> $\sigma$. Since the symbol set is finite, some iterate repeats. Applying an inverse power of $\sigma$ shows that the first return is to $a$ itself, giving
$$
a,\sigma(a),\ldots,\sigma^{k-1}(a),\qquad\sigma^k(a)=a,
$$
with the displayed symbols distinct. This is a <permutation cycle>. If two such lists share a symbol, applying powers of $\sigma$ shows that they contain exactly the same symbols. Hence these <group orbits> form a <partition of a set> $\{1,\ldots,n\}$. On each <group orbit>, $\sigma$ acts as the corresponding <permutation cycle>, and these <disjoint permutation cycles> multiply to $\sigma$.

The <disjoint cycle decomposition> is **unique up to reordering the cycles and cyclically rotating the entries within each cycle**, with fixed points optionally omitted. Indeed, each cycle must consist of one of the uniquely determined <group orbits>, and its successive entries must follow the action of $\sigma$.

$$
\boxed{\text{every element of order }n\text{ in }S_n\text{ is an }n\text{-cycle}\iff n=p^r.}

Acting separately on disjoint sets of four and eight symbols embeds the <direct product of groups> $S_4\times S_8$ into $S_{12}$. The action is injective since fixing all twelve symbols forces both factors to be identities.

\boxed{n_{\min}=12.}

in particular, $|K|$ divides $|G|$. Here $[G:K]$ is the <subgroup index>, the number of left <cosets> of $K$.

The <order of a group element> $g\in G$ is the least positive integer $k$ such that $g^k=e$. It exists because $G$ is finite. The <cyclic subgroup> $\langle g\rangle$ consists of exactly $e,g,\ldots,g^{k-1}$, so has order $k$. Applying <Lagrange's theorem> to this <subgroup> gives
$$
\boxed{o(g)\mid|G|.}
$$

A left <group action> is a map $a:G\times X\to X$ satisfying
$$
a(e,x)=x,\qquad a(gh,x)=a\bigl(g,a(h,x)\bigr)
\quad(g,h\in G,\ x\in X).
$$
Define $\rho_a(g):X\to X$ by $\rho_a(g)(x)=a(g,x)$. The action axioms give
$$
\rho_a(g)\circ\rho_a(g^{-1})=\rho_a(e)=\operatorname{id}_X
=\rho_a(g^{-1})\circ\rho_a(g).
$$
Hence each $\rho_a(g)$ is a <bijection>, with inverse $\rho_a(g^{-1})$, and belongs to the <symmetric group> $\operatorname{Sym}(X)$. Finally,
$$
\rho_a(gh)(x)=a(gh,x)=a\bigl(g,a(h,x)\bigr)
=\bigl(\rho_a(g)\circ\rho_a(h)\bigr)(x),
$$
so $\rho_a:G\to\operatorname{Sym}(X)$ is a <group homomorphism>.

For any integer $n\geq1$, let $R=R_{2\pi/n}$ in the <stabilizer subgroup> from <7/b/i>. Since $R_\theta R_\phi=R_{\theta+\phi}$ and $R_\theta=I$ exactly when $\theta\in2\pi\mathbb Z$, this <rotation matrix> has order exactly $n$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\langle R_{2\pi/n}\rangle\cong C_n}
$$
is a <subgroup> of $G$ of order $n$, including the trivial case $n=1$.

For infinitely many non-Abelian examples, let $n\geq3$, retain $R=R_{2\pi/n}$, and take the half-turn
$$
J=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,-1)\in G.
$$
Direct multiplication gives $J^2=I$ and $JRJ=R^{-1}$. Every word in $R,J$ therefore reduces to $R^k$ or $R^kJ$, $0\leq k<n$. The $R^k$ are distinct, as are the $R^kJ$, and no element from the first family equals one from the second: they send $N$ to $N$ and $-N$, respectively. Hence $\langle R,J\rangle$ has exactly $2n$ elements and is a <dihedral group>.

It is non-Abelian because $R\ne R^{-1}$ for $n\geq3$, so $JR\ne RJ$. Different values of $n$ give different <group orders>, and therefore different <subgroups>. Thus **$G$ has infinitely many non-Abelian subgroups**, already among its finite <subgroups>.
