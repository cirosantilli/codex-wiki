= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

Put $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. The definitions of the <parabolic polar coordinates> give $u^2=x+r$ and $v^2=-x+r$. Subtracting and multiplying these equations yields
$$
u^2-v^2=2x,\qquad u^2v^2=r^2-x^2=y^2.
$$
Since $u,v,y\geq0$, taking the nonnegative <square root> gives
$$
\boxed{x=\frac{u^2-v^2}{2},\qquad y=uv}.
$$
The <Jacobian matrix> has columns $(u,v)$ and $(-v,u)$. Its <Jacobian determinant> is therefore
$$
\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}
=\det\begin{pmatrix}u&-v\\v&u\end{pmatrix}
=\boxed{u^2+v^2}.
$$
Here the displayed fraction denotes the <Jacobian determinant>, as in the paper. The <change of variables formula> consequently gives $dx\,dy=(u^2+v^2)\,du\,dv$. The <Jacobian determinant> is positive except at the origin, and the definitions supply the unique nonnegative inverse throughout the region $y\geq0$.

The first <parabola> has vertex $(1/2,0)$ and opens to the left; the second <parabola> has vertex $(-1/2,0)$ and opens to the right. Both pass through $(0,1)$ and are reflections of one another across the $y$-axis.

The first boundary is $u=1$. On $y=0$ in the first <quadrant>, the inverse <parabolic polar coordinates> give $v=0$. To identify the remaining <curve>, put $t=y^{1/3}\geq0$. Its <Cartesian coordinates> satisfy

x=\frac{t^2-t^4}{2},\qquad y=t^3,\qquad

Hence $u=t$ and $v=t^2$, so this boundary is $v=u^2$. The condition $x\geq0$ on this <curve> requires $0\leq t\leq1$. Its endpoints are $(0,0)$ and $(0,1)$, while $u=1$ meets $v=0$ at $(1/2,0)$. These three arcs enclose exactly
$$
0\leq u\leq1,\qquad0\leq v\leq u^2.

Every point in this region has $v\leq u$, and therefore $x=(u^2-v^2)/2\geq0$, as required. Using the <Jacobian determinant>, the <area> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
A&=\int_0^1\int_0^{u^2}(u^2+v^2)\,dv\,du\\

=\frac15+\frac1{21}

\end{aligned}

Let $V$ be a bounded region with piecewise smooth closed boundary $\partial V$, and let $\mathbf F$ be a <continuously differentiable vector field> on an open neighbourhood of its <closure>. The <divergence theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\iiint_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV
=\iint_{\partial V}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,dS},
$$
where $\mathbf n$ is the outward <unit normal vector>. Thus the <volume integral> of the <divergence> equals the outward boundary <flux integral>. For a region containing a hole, the outward <unit normal vector> on its inner boundary points into that hole.

For $r>0$, the <chain rule> gives $\partial r/\partial x_i=x_i/r$. Applying the <product rule> to the components of the <vector field> yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla\cdot\mathbf u
&=\sum_{i=1}^3\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}(x_i r^{-\alpha})\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^3\left(r^{-\alpha}-\alpha x_i^2r^{-\alpha-2}\right)\\
&=3r^{-\alpha}-\alpha r^{-\alpha-2}\sum_{i=1}^3x_i^2
=\boxed{\frac{3-\alpha}{r^\alpha}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The calculation is pointwise away from the origin; any extension of the <vector field> to the origin must be considered separately.

= iii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

On the unit <sphere>, the outward <unit normal vector> is $\mathbf n=\mathbf r$ and $\mathbf u=\mathbf r$. Hence the outward <flux integral> is
$$
\iint_{r=1}\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n\,dS
=\iint_{r=1}1\,dS=4\pi.
$$
Using <spherical polar coordinates> and the <divergence> from <ii>, the <volume integral> over the unit <ball> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\iiint_{r\leq1}\nabla\cdot\mathbf u\,dV
&=4\pi(3-\alpha)\int_0^1r^{2-\alpha}\,dr\\
&=4\pi(3-\alpha)\left[\frac{r^{3-\alpha}}{3-\alpha}\right]_0^1
=\boxed{4\pi}\qquad(\alpha<3).
\end{aligned}
$$
This <improper integral> converges precisely because $2-\alpha>-1$. When the <vector field> is singular or fails to be continuously differentiable at the origin, justify the equality by applying the <divergence theorem> on $\varepsilon<r<1$. The outward <flux> through its inner <sphere> is $-4\pi\varepsilon^{3-\alpha}$, so
$$
\iiint_{\varepsilon<r<1}\nabla\cdot\mathbf u\,dV
=4\pi\bigl(1-\varepsilon^{3-\alpha}\bigr)\longrightarrow4\pi.
$$
Thus **the two integrals agree for every $\alpha<3$**, with the limiting interpretation where needed; the classical smoothness hypothesis does not hold for every such $\alpha$.

For $\alpha=3$, the <divergence> vanishes at every point with $r>0$, so its ordinary <volume integral>, ignoring the undefined single point, is zero. Nevertheless, the outward <flux integral> through the unit <sphere> is still $4\pi$:
$$
\boxed{\iiint_{0<r\leq1}\nabla\cdot\mathbf u\,dV=0,
\qquad\iint_{r=1}\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=4\pi}.
$$
**The hypotheses of the divergence theorem fail at the origin.** Indeed $|\mathbf u|=r^{-2}$ there, so no continuous extension is possible. On the punctured region $\varepsilon<r<1$, the <divergence theorem> remains valid: the outer <flux> is $4\pi$ and the inner <flux> is $-4\pi$. Unlike <iii>, this inner contribution does not tend to zero. The discrepancy therefore comes from the persistent <flux> at the singular point, rather than a failure of the <divergence theorem> under its hypotheses.

Write $\partial_i=\partial/\partial x_i$ and use $\times$ for the paper's wedge notation, which denotes the <cross product>. The given expression for the <curl> and the <product rule> give
$$
\nabla\times(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w)
=\sum_i\mathbf e_i\times\bigl((\partial_i\mathbf v)\times\mathbf w
+\mathbf v\times(\partial_i\mathbf w)\bigr).
$$
Apply the <vector triple product identity> $\mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)=\mathbf b(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)-\mathbf c(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)$ separately to the two terms:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf e_i\times((\partial_i\mathbf v)\times\mathbf w)
&=w_i\partial_i\mathbf v-\mathbf w\,\partial_i v_i,\\
\mathbf e_i\times(\mathbf v\times\partial_i\mathbf w)
&=\mathbf v\,\partial_i w_i-v_i\partial_i\mathbf w.
\end{aligned}
$$
Summing over the Cartesian <basis vectors> identifies the two <directional derivatives> and the two <divergences>:
$$
\boxed{\nabla\times(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w)
=(\mathbf w\cdot\nabla)\mathbf v+\mathbf v(\nabla\cdot\mathbf w)
-(\mathbf v\cdot\nabla)\mathbf w-\mathbf w(\nabla\cdot\mathbf v)}.
$$
Here, for example, $((\mathbf w\cdot\nabla)\mathbf v)_j=\sum_iw_i\partial_i v_j$. This proves the <curl of a cross product> directly from the formula supplied.

Let $S$ be a piecewise smooth <oriented surface> with piecewise smooth boundary $\partial S$, and let $\mathbf F$ be a <continuously differentiable vector field> on a neighbourhood of $S$. The <Stokes theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
=\iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS}.
$$
The <boundary orientation> is induced by the chosen <unit normal vector> $\mathbf n$ through the <right-hand rule>: with the right thumb along $\mathbf n$, the curled fingers indicate positive boundary traversal. Equivalently, when walking along the boundary with one's head pointing along $\mathbf n$, the <surface> lies to the left. An upward oriented <disk> therefore has counterclockwise boundary traversal when viewed from above. Reversing the <orientation> reverses both the <line integral> and the <surface integral>.

Choose the outward <orientation> on the upper half of the <ellipsoid>, giving counterclockwise <boundary orientation> on $C$ as viewed from $z>0$. Parametrize the boundary <circle> by
$$
\mathbf c(t)=(\cos t,\sin t,0),\qquad0\leq t\leq2\pi.
$$
Then $\mathbf u(\mathbf c(t))=(-\sin t,\cos t,0)=\mathbf c'(t)$, so the <line integral> is
$$
\oint_C\mathbf u\cdot d\mathbf r
=\int_0^{2\pi}(\sin^2t+\cos^2t)\,dt=2\pi.
$$
For a direct <surface integral>, use the <parametrized surface>
$$
\mathbf s(\varphi,t)=(\sin\varphi\cos t,\sin\varphi\sin t,\alpha\cos\varphi),
\qquad0\leq\varphi\leq\frac\pi2,\quad0\leq t\leq2\pi.
$$
Its outward <vector surface element> is supplied by the <cross product>
$$
\mathbf s_\varphi\times\mathbf s_t
=(\alpha\sin^2\varphi\cos t,\alpha\sin^2\varphi\sin t,
\sin\varphi\cos\varphi).
$$
Using the <curl> from <Question 3/a>,
$$
\iint_{S_\alpha}(\nabla\times\mathbf u)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS
=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi/2}2\sin\varphi\cos\varphi\,d\varphi\,dt
=\boxed{2\pi}.
$$
The two integrals agree for every $\alpha>0$, verifying the <Stokes theorem>. Only the vertical component of the <vector surface element> enters, which explains the absence of $\alpha$ in the answer.

On the unit <circle>, the positively oriented unit <tangent vector> is $\mathbf u=(-y,x,0)$, and $|\mathbf u|=1$. Hence $d\mathbf r=\mathbf u\,ds$ and
$$
\oint_C(\psi\mathbf u)\cdot d\mathbf r=\oint_C\psi\,ds.

Since the <scalar field> $\psi$ has no $z$ dependence, the <curl product rule for a scalar field> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla\times(\psi\mathbf u)
&=\psi(0,0,2)+(\psi_x,\psi_y,0)\times(-y,x,0)\\
&=(0,0,2\psi+x\psi_x+y\psi_y).
\end{aligned}
$$
On the unit upper <hemisphere> $S_1$, the outward <unit normal vector> is $(x,y,z)$, so its vertical component is $z=\cos\theta$. Applying the <Stokes theorem> to the <vector field> $\psi\mathbf u$ therefore proves
$$
\boxed{\oint_C\psi\,ds
=\iint_{S_1}\left(2\psi+x\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}
+y\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}\right)\cos\theta\,dS}.
$$
The smoothness needed here is that $\psi$ extend as a continuously differentiable <scalar field> to a neighbourhood of the projected unit <disk>. The left side is a scalar <line integral> with respect to <arc length>; the positive <boundary orientation> is used when identifying it with the vector <line integral>.

= Solution

For a nonzero integer $k$, direct <integration> gives
$$
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos(kx)\,dx=0,\qquad
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin(kx)\,dx=0,
$$
whereas $\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}1\,dx=2\pi$. The <product-to-sum formulas>
$$
\begin{aligned}
2\cos(mx)\cos(nx)&=\cos((m-n)x)+\cos((m+n)x),\\
2\sin(mx)\sin(nx)&=\cos((m-n)x)-\cos((m+n)x),\\
2\sin(mx)\cos(nx)&=\sin((m+n)x)+\sin((m-n)x)
\end{aligned}
$$
therefore establish <Fourier orthogonality>: for positive integers $m,n$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos(mx)\cos(nx)\,dx&=\pi\delta_{mn},\\
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin(mx)\sin(nx)\,dx&=\pi\delta_{mn},\\
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin(mx)\cos(nx)\,dx&=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $\delta_{mn}$ is the <Kronecker delta>. In particular the <constant function> is orthogonal to every nonconstant <sine> and <cosine> in this system. Integrating the given <Fourier series>, then multiplying it by $\cos(mx)$ or $\sin(mx)$ and integrating, isolates its <Fourier coefficients>. Using the permitted interchange of the <sum> and <integral> gives
$$
\boxed{a_0=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\,dx},\qquad
\boxed{a_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\cos(nx)\,dx},\qquad
\boxed{b_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\sin(nx)\,dx}\quad(n\geq1).
$$
If $f$ is an <even function>, then $f(x)\sin(nx)$ is an <odd function>, while $f(x)\cos(nx)$ is an <even function>. Thus
$$
\boxed{b_n=0,\qquad a_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi f(x)\cos(nx)\,dx\quad(n\geq0)}.
$$
The <Fourier series> consequently reduces to a <Fourier cosine series>.

The <function> $x\sin(px)$ is an <even function>, so every <Fourier sine coefficient> is zero. Introduce the auxiliary <integral>
$$
I_k=\int_0^\pi x\sin(kx)\,dx.
$$
For a nonzero integer $k$, <integration by parts> gives
$$
I_k=\left[-\frac{x\cos(kx)}k+\frac{\sin(kx)}{k^2}\right]_0^\pi
=-\frac{\pi(-1)^k}{k},\qquad I_0=0.
$$
Consequently
$$
a_0=\frac2\pi I_p=\frac{2(-1)^{p+1}}p.
$$
For $n\geq1$, a <product-to-sum formula> yields
$$
a_n=\frac1\pi(I_{p+n}+I_{p-n}).
$$
If $n\ne p$, this becomes
$$
a_n=-(-1)^{p+n}\left(\frac1{p+n}+\frac1{p-n}\right)
=\frac{2p(-1)^{p+n}}{n^2-p^2}.
$$
The exceptional <Fourier coefficient> at $n=p$ must be evaluated before dividing by $p-n$:
$$
a_p=\frac1\pi(I_{2p}+I_0)=-\frac1{2p}.
$$
Thus the full <Fourier series> is
$$
\boxed{x\sin(px)=\frac{(-1)^{p+1}}p-\frac{\cos(px)}{2p}
+2p\!\sum_{\substack{n\geq1\\n\ne p}}
\frac{(-1)^{p+n}\cos(nx)}{n^2-p^2}}.
$$
This equality holds on $[-\pi,\pi]$ and by <periodicity> everywhere. Indeed the <periodic extension> is continuous at the joined endpoints, since both endpoint values are zero, and is piecewise smooth. The theorem on <pointwise convergence of a piecewise smooth Fourier series> therefore applies, including at the endpoints. The <Fourier coefficients> also decay as $n^{-2}$, making the resulting <series> absolutely and uniformly convergent.

Let the uniform string have constant <tension> $\tau>0$ and <linear mass density> $\mu>0$. Assume small transverse motion with $|u_x|\ll1$, negligible longitudinal motion, and no external transverse force. If the string makes angle $\beta$ with the horizontal, then $\tan\beta=u_x$, and the <small-angle approximation> gives $\sin\beta\simeq u_x$ and $ds\simeq dx$ to first order.

The net upward force on the segment from $x$ to $x+\Delta x$ is therefore
$$
\tau\bigl(u_x(x+\Delta x,t)-u_x(x,t)\bigr).
$$
By <Newton's second law>, its mass $\mu\Delta x$ times its transverse <acceleration> equals this force, to the order retained in the small displacement model. Divide by $\Delta x$ and let $\Delta x\to0$ to obtain
$$
\mu u_{tt}=\tau u_{xx}.
$$
Thus the <wave equation on a string> is
$$
\boxed{u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx},\qquad c=\sqrt{\frac\tau\mu}},
$$
where $c$ is the <wave speed>. The horizontal force balance makes the horizontal component of <tension> constant; to first order it equals $\tau$.

= Solution

**The printed arguments $x-t$ and $x+t$ assume unit wave speed.** For the stated <wave equation> with general $c>0$, the correct arguments are $x-ct$ and $x+ct$, as in the paper's subsequent formula. Equivalently, use rescaled time $\widetilde t=ct$ to obtain the printed unit-speed form.

Introduce coordinates $\xi=x-ct$, $\eta=x+ct$ along the <characteristic lines for the one-dimensional wave equation>, and write $u(x,t)=U(\xi,\eta)$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\partial_x=\partial_\xi+\partial_\eta,\qquad
\partial_t=-c\partial_\xi+c\partial_\eta,
$$
so the <wave equation> becomes
$$
u_{tt}-c^2u_{xx}=-4c^2U_{\xi\eta}=0.
$$
Thus $U_\xi$ is independent of $\eta$, and <integration> gives the <countertravelling decomposition of the one-dimensional wave equation>
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=F(x-ct)+G(x+ct)}.
$$
Conversely, differentiating this expression twice verifies the <wave equation> for any sufficiently differentiable $F,G$.

The two <initial conditions> give
$$
F(x)+G(x)=f(x),\qquad -cF'(x)+cG'(x)=g(x).
$$
Differentiating the first and solving these two equations for the <derivatives> gives
$$
F'(x)=\frac12f'(x)-\frac1{2c}g(x),\qquad
G'(x)=\frac12f'(x)+\frac1{2c}g(x).
$$
For a fixed reference point $x_0$, their <antiderivatives> can be written
$$
F(s)=\frac12f(s)-\frac1{2c}\int_{x_0}^s g(q)\,dq+C,\qquad
G(s)=\frac12f(s)+\frac1{2c}\int_{x_0}^s g(q)\,dq-C.
$$
Substituting the two travelling arguments cancels the constants and combines the <integrals>. This proves <D'Alembert's formula> with initial velocity:
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=\frac12\bigl[f(x-ct)+f(x+ct)\bigr]
+\frac1{2c}\int_{x-ct}^{x+ct}g(s)\,ds}.
$$
At $t=0$ the <integral> vanishes, giving $u=f$; differentiating by the <fundamental theorem of calculus> gives $u_t=g$ there. These checks also fix the sign and the factor $1/(2c)$.

For a nonzero separable solution of the <Laplace equation in polar coordinates>, write $T(r,\theta)=R(r)\Theta(\theta)$ with $r>0$. Multiplying the <partial differential equation> by $r^2$ and dividing where $R\Theta\ne0$ gives
$$
\frac{r^2R''+rR'}R=-\frac{\Theta''}{\Theta}=\lambda,
$$
where $\lambda$ is a real <separation constant>. The resulting <ordinary differential equations> are
$$
r^2R''+rR'-\lambda R=0,\qquad\Theta''+\lambda\Theta=0.
$$
The equations continue across zeros of the factors by continuity. The radial equation is a <Cauchy-Euler equation>; putting $s=\log r$ changes it to $d^2R/ds^2-\lambda R=0$.

For **positive separation constant**, write $\lambda=k^2$ with $k>0$. The radial factors are powers, while the angular factors are <sine> and <cosine>:
$$
\boxed{T=(A r^k+B r^{-k})(C\cos(k\theta)+D\sin(k\theta))}.
$$
For **zero separation constant**, each transformed equation has zero second <derivative>, giving
$$
\boxed{T=(A+B\log r)(C+D\theta)}.
$$
For **negative separation constant**, write $\lambda=-k^2$ with $k>0$. The radial factors oscillate in $\log r$, and the angular factors are <exponential functions>:
$$
\boxed{T=(A\cos(k\log r)+B\sin(k\log r))(C e^{k\theta}+D e^{-k\theta})}.
$$
These exhaust the nonzero real separated solutions before imposing angular <periodicity> or regularity at the origin. The zero solution is also allowed. Since <Laplace's equation> is linear, sums of these separated solutions are solutions wherever the sums and required <derivatives> converge appropriately.

A temperature on the full unit <disk> must be single valued, so its angular factor and angular <derivative> are $2\pi$-periodic. For positive <separation constant> $\lambda=k^2$, this requires $k=n$ to be a positive integer. For $\lambda=0$, <periodicity> removes $D\theta$. For $\lambda<0$, no nonzero linear combination of $e^{k\theta}$ and $e^{-k\theta}$ is $2\pi$-periodic, so that case disappears.

The origin belongs to the <disk>, so a classical <harmonic function> must remain bounded and extend smoothly there. This excludes $r^{-n}$ and $\log r$. The remaining individual separated solutions are constants and $r^n(A_n\cos(n\theta)+B_n\sin(n\theta))$. By <superposition>, the regular <harmonic expansion on a disk> is
$$
\boxed{T(r,\theta)=A_0+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}r^n
\bigl(A_n\cos(n\theta)+B_n\sin(n\theta)\bigr)}.
$$
The admissible <Fourier coefficients> are fixed by the <boundary conditions>. Smoothness at the origin can also be seen from $r^n\cos(n\theta)$ and $r^n\sin(n\theta)$ being the real and imaginary parts of $(x+iy)^n$, hence <harmonic polynomials>.

The boundary data consist of the third angular <Fourier mode>. Its regular radial factor in the <harmonic expansion on a disk> is $r^3$, so
$$
\boxed{T(r,\theta)=r^3\sin(3\theta)}.
$$
For a direct check of <Laplace's equation>,
$$
T_{rr}+\frac1rT_r+\frac1{r^2}T_{\theta\theta}
=6r\sin(3\theta)+3r\sin(3\theta)-9r\sin(3\theta)=0.
$$
Also $T(1,\theta)=\sin(3\theta)$. At the origin the apparent coordinate singularity is harmless: this is the <harmonic polynomial> $3x^2y-y^3$. The allowed uniqueness for the <Dirichlet problem> completes the identification.

The <trigonometric identity>
$$
\sin^3\theta=\frac34\sin\theta-\frac14\sin(3\theta)
$$
shows that only angular <Fourier modes> $1$ and $3$ are required. Differentiating the <harmonic expansion on a disk> at $r=1$ multiplies the coefficient of each nonconstant <Fourier mode> by $n$. Thus $B_1=3/4$ and $3B_3=-1/4$, giving
$$
\boxed{T(r,\theta)=C+\frac34r\sin\theta-\frac1{12}r^3\sin(3\theta),\qquad C\in\mathbb R}.
$$
Each term is a regular <harmonic function>, and the radial <derivative> is
$$
T_r(1,\theta)=\frac34\sin\theta-\frac14\sin(3\theta)=\sin^3\theta.
$$
The <Neumann compatibility condition for Laplace's equation> holds because
$$
\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^3\theta\,d\theta=0,
$$
as is necessary by the <divergence theorem> applied to the <gradient> of a <harmonic function>.

**The additive constant is undetermined.** This <Neumann boundary condition> is unchanged by $T\mapsto T+C$, so the paper's blanket uniqueness assertion requires a normalization in this case. The <uniqueness of the Neumann problem> gives uniqueness up to a constant; prescribing $T(0)=0$, or zero mean temperature, selects the displayed answer with $C=0$.
