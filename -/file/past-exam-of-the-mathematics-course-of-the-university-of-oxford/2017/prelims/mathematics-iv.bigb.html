For <vectors> $\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c\in\mathbb R^3$, the <scalar product>, <cross product> and <scalar triple product> are respectively
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b&=a_1b_1+a_2b_2+a_3b_3,\\
\mathbf a\wedge\mathbf b&=(a_2b_3-a_3b_2,\ a_3b_1-a_1b_3,\ a_1b_2-a_2b_1),\\
(\mathbf a\wedge\mathbf b)\cdot\mathbf c&=\det\begin{pmatrix}a_1&a_2&a_3\\b_1&b_2&b_3\\c_1&c_2&c_3\end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Expanding the <dot product> gives
$$
(\mathbf a\wedge\mathbf b)\cdot\mathbf a
=a_1a_2b_3-a_1a_3b_2+a_2a_3b_1-a_1a_2b_3+a_1a_3b_2-a_2a_3b_1=0.
$$
Interchanging $\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf b$ changes the sign of their <cross product>, so its <dot product> with $\mathbf b$ is also zero. Thus the <cross product> is <orthogonal> to both <vectors>, including when it is the <zero vector>.

The displayed <scalar triple product> is a <determinant>. A square <matrix> has nonzero <determinant> exactly when its rows are <linearly independent>. Hence
$$
\boxed{(\mathbf a\wedge\mathbf b)\cdot\mathbf c\ne0\iff\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c\text{ are linearly independent}.}
$$

Subtracting the first row from the other two in the <determinant> gives
$$
f(\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c)
=\det\begin{pmatrix}b_1-a_1&b_2-a_2\\c_1-a_1&c_2-a_2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
This vanishes exactly when the two <displacements> $\mathbf b-\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf c-\mathbf a$ are <linearly dependent>. If $\mathbf a\ne\mathbf b$, that means $\mathbf c-\mathbf a=t(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)$ for some <real number> $t$. Thus all three <points> lie on the <line>
$$
\mathbf x=\mathbf a+s(\mathbf b-\mathbf a),\qquad s\in\mathbb R.
$$
Conversely, putting three <points> on any one <line> makes their <displacements> <linearly dependent>, so the <determinant> vanishes.

**The equivalence remains true without distinctness.** If exactly two <points> coincide, the two distinct locations determine a <line> containing all three, and two rows of the original <determinant> coincide. If all three <points> coincide, any <line> through that <point> works and the <determinant> is again zero. Existence persists, although uniqueness of the <line> can fail.

The value $f(\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c)$ is twice the <signed area> of the <triangle> traversed in that order; it is positive for counterclockwise <orientation>. Choose <points> on the <parabola>
$$
\boxed{\mathbf p_i=(i,i^2),\qquad 1\le i\le n.}
$$
For $i<j<k$, subtracting the first row in the <determinant> and factoring gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
f(\mathbf p_i,\mathbf p_j,\mathbf p_k)
&=(j-i)(k^2-i^2)-(k-i)(j^2-i^2)\\
&=(j-i)(k-i)(k-j)>0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Each of the three factors is positive, proving the required property for every <integer> $n\ge3$.

For a fixed <point> $F$ not on a fixed <line> $D$, the <ellipse> with <focus of a conic> $F$, <directrix of a conic> $D$ and <eccentricity> $0<e<1$ is the <locus> of <points> $P$ satisfying
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{dist}(P,F)=e\operatorname{dist}(P,D).}
$$
Set $c=\sqrt{a^2-b^2}$ and $e=c/a$. One matched <focus of a conic> and <directrix of a conic> pair is
$$
\boxed{F=(c,0),\qquad D:\ x=\frac{a^2}{c},\qquad e=\sqrt{1-\frac{b^2}{a^2}}.}
$$
Indeed the squared <distance> condition is
$$
(x-c)^2+y^2=\frac{c^2}{a^2}\left(x-\frac{a^2}{c}\right)^2.
$$
Expanding and cancelling the terms linear in $x$ gives
$$
\left(1-\frac{c^2}{a^2}\right)x^2+y^2=a^2-c^2=b^2,
$$
which is exactly $x^2/a^2+y^2/b^2=1$. Both sides of the unsquared <distance> condition are nonnegative, so squaring introduces no extra <points>. Reflecting across the $y$-axis gives the other matched pair: $F=(-c,0)$ and $D:x=-a^2/c$, with the same <eccentricity>.

**The required <lines> are the $x$-axis and every <line> through the origin in the <plane> $x=0$.** Explicitly,
$$
\boxed{L=\{(t,0,0):t\in\mathbb R\}\quad\text{or}\quad L=\{(0,t\cos\phi,t\sin\phi):t\in\mathbb R\},\quad0\le\phi<\pi.}
$$
The $x$-axis meets the <ellipsoid> at its two poles and is a <normal line> at each. A <line> of the second kind meets it at the antipodal <points> $(0,\pm\cos\phi,\pm\sin\phi)$, whose <normal vectors> are parallel to that <line>.

To prove completeness, suppose a common <normal line> passes through distinct <points> $P=\mathbf r(\theta,\phi)$ and $Q=\mathbf r(\theta',\phi')$. If either is a pole, <3/b> forces the <line> to be the $x$-axis. Otherwise <3/b> says that the <line> meets the $x$-axis at $(-\cos\theta/\sqrt2,0,0)$ and at $(-\cos\theta'/\sqrt2,0,0)$. A <line> other than the $x$-axis has at most one such intersection, so $\cos\theta=\cos\theta'$.

The <points> $P,Q$ consequently have equal $x$-<coordinates>. Since they are distinct, their joining <line> has zero $x$-component in its direction. Its <normal vector> at $P$ therefore has $\sqrt2\cos\theta=0$. Hence $P,Q$ lie in $x=0$, and the <normal line> there passes through the origin. This gives exactly the listed family.

Put $C=q^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)>0$. The <spring> exerts the <force> $k(\ell-x)$ by <Hooke's law>, while <Coulomb's law> gives the attractive <force> $-C/x^2$. <Newton's second law> therefore gives the <equation of motion>
$$
\boxed{m\ddot x=f(x)=k(\ell-x)-\frac C{x^2}.}
$$
For $x>0$, the <derivative> is $f'(x)=-k+2C/x^3$. Thus $f$ increases up to $x_*=(2C/k)^{1/3}$ and decreases thereafter. Also $f(x)\to-\infty$ at both ends of the positive half-axis. Its unique <maximum> is
$$
f(x_*)=k\left(\ell-\frac32x_*\right).
$$
There are therefore two <equilibrium points> precisely when
$$
\boxed{\ell>\ell_1=\frac32\left(\frac{q^2}{2\pi\epsilon_0k}\right)^{1/3}.}
$$
Writing them $x_-<x_*<x_+$, the <linearization> $m\ddot\eta=f'(x_\pm)\eta$ has exponentially growing solutions at $x_-$ and oscillatory solutions at $x_+$. Equivalently, the <potential energy> has a strict <local maximum> at $x_-$ and a strict <local minimum> at $x_+$. Hence **the smaller <equilibrium point> is an <unstable equilibrium>; the larger is a <stable equilibrium>**, with no <damping> to make it attracting.

At equality $\ell=\ell_1$ there is one degenerate <equilibrium point> $x_*$; it is unstable, since a displacement to its left gives a leftward <force>. For $\ell<\ell_1$ there are no <equilibrium points>.

Multiply the <equation of motion> by $\dot x$ and integrate. The conserved <mechanical energy> is
$$
\frac12m\dot x^2+V(x)=E,\qquad V(x)=\frac k2(\ell-x)^2-\frac Cx.
$$
The <initial conditions> $x=\ell$, $\dot x=0$ give $E=-C/\ell$, so
$$
\boxed{\frac12m\dot x^2=-\frac k2(\ell-x)^2+C\left(\frac1x-\frac1\ell\right).}
$$
Initially $\ddot x=-C/(m\ell^2)<0$, so the <particle> starts moving towards the origin. For $0<x<\ell$, factor its <kinetic energy> as
$$
E-V(x)=\frac{\ell-x}{2\ell x}\left[2C-k\ell x(\ell-x)\right].
$$
The <quadratic function> $x(\ell-x)$ has <maximum> $\ell^2/4$ at $x=\ell/2$. Consequently <kinetic energy> is strictly positive throughout $0<x<\ell$ exactly when $k\ell^3<8C$. Thus the collision threshold is
$$
\boxed{\ell_2=\left(\frac{8C}{k}\right)^{1/3}=\left(\frac{2q^2}{\pi\epsilon_0k}\right)^{1/3}.}
$$
For $\ell_1<\ell<\ell_2$ there is no intervening <turning point>, and the <particle> collides with the fixed <particle>. The collision takes finite time: near zero, $|\dot x|\sim\sqrt{2C/(mx)}$, so the remaining time is bounded by a constant times $\int_0^\delta\sqrt{x}\,dx$.

For $\ell>\ell_2$, the first <turning point> encountered is
$$
\boxed{x_T=\frac{\ell+\sqrt{\ell^2-8C/(k\ell)}}2.}
$$
The <particle> **oscillates periodically between $x_T$ and $\ell$**, around the larger <stable equilibrium>, without collision. The zeros of $E-V$ at the two endpoints of this allowed region are simple, so travel to each <turning point> takes finite time. At the boundary $\ell=\ell_2$, $x=\ell/2$ is a double zero of $E-V$ and an <unstable equilibrium>; the inward <trajectory> approaches it only as $t\to\infty$.

Write $\mu=GM$. In <polar coordinates>, <Newton's second law> per unit <mass> gives the radial and transverse <equations of motion>
$$
\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2=-\frac\mu{r^2},\qquad
r\ddot\theta+2\dot r\dot\theta=0.
$$
The transverse <equation> implies
$$
\frac d{dt}(r^2\dot\theta)=r(r\ddot\theta+2\dot r\dot\theta)=0,
\qquad\boxed{h=r^2\dot\theta\text{ is constant}.}
$$
This is <conservation of angular momentum> per unit <mass>. For the nonradial <trajectory> in the next part, choose <orientation> so $h>0$. With $u=1/r$ and primes denoting <derivatives> with respect to $\theta$, the <chain rule> gives
$$
\dot\theta=hu^2,\qquad \dot r=-hu',\qquad \ddot r=-h^2u^2u''.
$$
Substituting into the radial <equation> yields $-h^2u^2(u''+u)=-\mu u^2$, hence
$$
\boxed{u''+u=\frac{GM}{h^2}.}
$$
The change of independent variable requires $h\ne0$; a purely radial <trajectory> instead has constant $\theta$.

Far from the planet, the magnitude of <angular momentum> per unit <mass> is the magnitude of the <cross product> of <position vector> and <velocity>. The perpendicular distance from the incoming <asymptote> is the <impact parameter> $d$, so
$$
\boxed{h=dv.}
$$
Choose $\theta=0$ at closest approach. The general solution of the <differential equation> in <5/a>, with $u'(0)=0$, is
$$
u=\frac\mu{h^2}(1+e\cos\theta).
$$
The <specific orbital energy> is
$$
\varepsilon=\frac12(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2)-\frac\mu r
=\frac{h^2}2(u'^2+u^2)-\mu u
=\frac{\mu^2}{2h^2}(e^2-1).
$$
At infinity $\varepsilon=v^2/2$, giving $e=\sqrt{1+h^2v^2/\mu^2}>1$. Thus the <hyperbolic orbit> has
$$
\boxed{u(\theta)=\frac{GM}{d^2v^2}\left[1+\sqrt{1+\frac{d^2v^4}{G^2M^2}}\cos\theta\right].}
$$
A different zero of <polar angle> simply replaces $\theta$ by $\theta-\theta_0$.

The incoming and outgoing <asymptotes> have radial directions $-\theta_\infty$ and $\theta_\infty$, where $\cos\theta_\infty=-1/e$ and $\pi/2<\theta_\infty<\pi$. The incoming <velocity> points opposite its radial direction, so the <deflection angle> between incoming and outgoing <velocities> is $\delta=2\theta_\infty-\pi$. Hence
$$
\sin\frac\delta2=\frac1e,\qquad
\tan\frac\delta2=\frac{GM}{dv^2}.
$$
Putting $\delta=\pi/2$ gives
$$
\boxed{d=\frac{GM}{v^2}.}
$$

In <cylindrical coordinates>, $\theta=\omega t$ and $z=r^2/(2a)$. Thus

The <linearization> at rest at $r=0$ is
$$
\ddot r=\left(\omega^2-\frac ga\right)r.
$$
For $\omega^2<g/a$, small disturbances oscillate. To verify nonlinear <stability>, multiply the full <equation of motion> by $\dot r/a^2$ and integrate to obtain the <first integral>
$$
J=\frac12\left(1+\frac{r^2}{a^2}\right)\dot r^2+\frac12\left(\frac ga-\omega^2\right)r^2.
$$
For $\omega^2<g/a$, $J$ is positive away from the <equilibrium point> in a sufficiently small <neighborhood> of $(r,\dot r)=(0,0)$; its small <level sets> confine the motion. Thus
$$
\boxed{r=0\text{ is stable if }\omega^2<g/a,\text{ and unstable if }\omega^2>g/a.}
$$
At equality, every resting location on the wire is an <equilibrium point>, so a displacement at zero relative <velocity> simply remains there: the equilibrium is **neutral with respect to resting displacements**. It is **unstable with respect to general perturbations in <phase space>**, however. An arbitrarily small outward initial <velocity> $v_0>0$ gives $\dot r=v_0/\sqrt{1+r^2/a^2}>0$ from the <first integral>, eventually taking the bead outside any prescribed small <neighborhood>. Thus strict <phase space> <stability> holds only for $\omega^2<g/a$.

Put $\lambda=\omega^2-g/a>0$. The zero value of the <first integral> in <6/b> gives
$$
\boxed{\left(1+\frac{r^2}{a^2}\right)\dot r^2=\lambda r^2.}

At the endpoint $r=a$, this gives $\dot r^2=\lambda a^2/2$. The absolute <speed> includes radial, azimuthal and vertical components. Since $\dot z=\dot r$ there,
$$
|\mathbf v|^2=\dot r^2+(a\omega)^2+\dot z^2
=2\dot r^2+a^2\omega^2=a^2\left(2\omega^2-\frac ga\right).
$$
Hence the limiting exit <speed> is
$$
\boxed{|\mathbf v|=a\sqrt{2\omega^2-\frac ga}.}
$$
There is a small <initial condition> qualification in the wording. The exact <initial conditions> $r=\dot r=0$ give the stationary solution forever, by <Picard-Lindelöf theorem>. The displayed zero-<first integral> moving branch instead approaches that <unstable equilibrium> as $t\to-\infty$.

For a precise small disturbance, release the bead with zero relative <velocity> at $r=\epsilon>0$. Its <first integral> is $J=-\lambda\epsilon^2/2$, giving
$$
\left(1+\frac{r^2}{a^2}\right)\dot r^2=\lambda(r^2-\epsilon^2),\qquad
|\mathbf v_{\rm exit}|^2=a^2\left(2\omega^2-\frac ga\right)-\lambda\epsilon^2.
$$
The bead initially accelerates outward and reaches the end. Letting $\epsilon\to0^+$ recovers exactly the stated exit <speed>. The conserved $J$ is a <Jacobi integral> associated with the imposed rotation; ordinary <mechanical energy> need not be conserved because the apparatus driving the wire can do <work>.

The <euclidean algorithm> computes the <greatest common divisor> of two <integers> by repeated <division with remainder>. Start with $r_0=|A|$, $r_1=|B|$, interchanging them if necessary, and repeatedly write
$$
r_{j-1}=q_jr_j+r_{j+1},\qquad 0\le r_{j+1}<r_j,
$$
until a zero remainder occurs. The positive remainders strictly decrease, so the process terminates. At each step the common <divisors> of $r_{j-1},r_j$ are exactly the common <divisors> of $r_j,r_{j+1}$, because $r_{j+1}=r_{j-1}-q_jr_j$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\gcd(A,B)=\text{the last nonzero remainder}.}
$$
If one input is zero, the answer is the <absolute value> of the other; the usual convention sets $\gcd(0,0)=0$. Back-substitution also expresses the <greatest common divisor> as an <integer linear combination> of the inputs, as in <Bézout's identity>.

The <polynomial> factors as $p(x)=(3x-2)^2(3x+1)$, so $\alpha=2/3$ is a <multiple root> of multiplicity two. The <Newton root-finding iteration> is
$$
x_{k+1}=x_k-\frac{p(x_k)}{p'(x_k)},\qquad p'(x)=81x^2-54x.
$$
For $x\ne0,2/3$, cancelling a factor gives
$$
\boxed{g(x)=\frac{2x}{3}+\frac19+\frac{2}{27x}.}
$$
This expression extends continuously to $g(2/3)=2/3$. Let $I=[1/3,1]$. On this <closed interval>,
$$
g'(x)=\frac23-\frac{2}{27x^2},\qquad 0\le g'(x)\le\frac{16}{27}<1.
$$
Thus $g$ is increasing, and
$$
g(I)=[g(1/3),g(1)]=[5/9,23/27]\subset I.
$$
By the <mean value theorem>, $|g(x)-g(y)|\le(16/27)|x-y|$ for all $x,y\in I$. The <contraction mapping theorem> states that a <contraction mapping> from a nonempty <complete metric space> into itself has a unique <fixed point>, and every <fixed-point iteration> converges to it. The <closed interval> $I$ with ordinary <Euclidean distance> is complete, and $g(2/3)=2/3$, so
$$
\boxed{x_k\longrightarrow\frac23\quad\text{for every }x_0\in[1/3,1].}
$$
In fact $|x_k-2/3|\le(16/27)^k|x_0-2/3|$. The <Newton iteration at a multiple root> here has <linear convergence>, since $g'(2/3)=1/2$. Thus **the convergence assertion is true for the simplified <fixed-point iteration>**, and for the usual <Newton root-finding iteration> that stops upon reaching an exact <root of a polynomial>. If one insists on applying the unsimplified quotient at every step, it is undefined for the allowed starting value $x_0=2/3$, since both $p$ and $p'$ vanish there; that literal version of an infinite <sequence> requires the stated stopping convention or continuous extension.
