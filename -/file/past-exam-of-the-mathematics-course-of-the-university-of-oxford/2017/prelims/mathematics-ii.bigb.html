The <infimum> of $A$ is its greatest <lower bound>: $\alpha=\inf A$ means that $\alpha\leq x$ for every $x\in A$, and that every <lower bound> $l$ of $A$ satisfies $l\leq\alpha$. The <supremum> is its least <upper bound>: $\beta=\sup A$ means that $x\leq\beta$ for every $x\in A$, and every <upper bound> $u$ satisfies $\beta\leq u$. The <completeness of the real numbers> ensures that both exist for a nonempty bounded <set> of <real numbers>.

If $l$ is any <lower bound> of $A$, then $-x\leq-l$ for every $x\in A$, making $-l$ an <upper bound> of $-A$. The definition of <supremum> gives $s\leq-l$, or $l\leq-s$. Thus $-s$ is the greatest <lower bound>, and
$$
\boxed{\inf A=-\sup(-A).}
$$

A <sequence> $(a_n)$ has <limit of a sequence> $L$ if
$$
\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\in\mathbb N\ \forall n\geq N:\quad |a_n-L|<\varepsilon.
$$
For the reciprocal rule, the required condition is $\boxed{L\ne0}$. Indeed, eventually $|a_n-L|<|L|/2$, so $|a_n|\geq|L|/2>0$. The reciprocals are then defined on this tail, and
$$
\left|\frac1{a_n}-\frac1L\right|=\frac{|a_n-L|}{|a_n||L|}\leq\frac{2|a_n-L|}{|L|^2}\longrightarrow0.
$$
A finite number of zero initial terms does not affect this tail <limit of a sequence>; if a reciprocal <sequence> is required at every index, those terms must be excluded or assigned arbitrary values separately.

A real or complex <sequence> $(a_n)$ is a <Cauchy sequence> if
$$
\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\in\mathbb N\ \forall m,n\geq N:\quad |a_m-a_n|<\varepsilon.
$$
For $z_n=x_n+iy_n$, the <complex modulus> gives
$$
|x_m-x_n|\leq|z_m-z_n|,\qquad |y_m-y_n|\leq|z_m-z_n|.
$$
Thus the same $N$ works for both coordinate <Cauchy sequences>. Conversely, if both real coordinate <Cauchy sequences> converge to $x$ and $y$, then $|z_n-(x+iy)|\leq|x_n-x|+|y_n-y|\to0$. This proves the needed completeness for <complex numbers> from the assumed <completeness of the real numbers>.

Now suppose $\sum_{n\geq0}|z_n|$ converges. Its real <partial sums> form a <Cauchy sequence>. Given $\varepsilon>0$, sufficiently late tails therefore satisfy $\sum_{k=n+1}^m|z_k|<\varepsilon$. For $s_m=\sum_{k=0}^mz_k$, the <triangle inequality> yields
$$
|s_m-s_n|\leq\sum_{k=n+1}^m|z_k|<\varepsilon\qquad(m>n\geq N).
$$
The complex <partial sums> are a <Cauchy sequence>, hence converge by the coordinate argument. Therefore **<absolute convergence> implies convergence** for a complex <series>.

Define the <radius of convergence> of the <power series> by
$$
R=\sup\left\{|w|:w\in\mathbb C,\ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nw^n\text{ converges}\right\}\in[0,\infty],
$$
with $R=\infty$ when this <set> is unbounded. It is nonempty because the <power series> converges at zero.

If $|z|<R$, the definition of <supremum> supplies a point $w$ of convergence with $|z|<|w|$. The terms $a_nw^n$ of a convergent <series> tend to zero and are bounded, say $|a_nw^n|\leq M$. Therefore
$$
|a_nz^n|\leq M\left(\frac{|z|}{|w|}\right)^n.
$$
The right-hand side has a convergent <geometric series>, since $|z|/|w|<1$. The <comparison test> proves <absolute convergence> at $z$.

If $|z|>R$ and the <power series> converged at $z$, then $|z|$ would belong to the <set> whose <supremum> is $R$, an immediate contradiction. Thus
$$
\boxed{|z|<R\Rightarrow\text{absolute convergence},\qquad |z|>R\Rightarrow\text{divergence}.}
$$
No assertion about the boundary $|z|=R$ follows from this argument. If $R=0$ the first assertion is vacuous; if $R=\infty$ the second is vacuous.

For $x_0>a$, define
$$
l=\sup\{f(t):a\leq t<x_0\}.
$$
This nonempty <set> is bounded above by $f(x_0)$, so $l\leq f(x_0)$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, the <supremum> property supplies $s<x_0$ with $l-\varepsilon<f(s)\leq l$. Whenever $s<t<x_0$, <monotonicity> gives
$$
l-\varepsilon<f(s)\leq f(t)\leq l.
$$
This proves the left-hand <one-sided limit> $f^-(x_0)=l$.

For $x_0<b$, define
$$
r=\inf\{f(t):x_0<t\leq b\}.
$$
Here $r\geq f(x_0)$. Choose $u>x_0$ with $r\leq f(u)<r+\varepsilon$, using the <infimum> property. For $x_0<t<u$, <monotonicity> gives $r\leq f(t)\leq f(u)<r+\varepsilon$, proving the right-hand <one-sided limit> $f^+(x_0)=r$. Thus
$$
\boxed{f^-(x_0)\leq f(x_0)\leq f^+(x_0)}
$$
where the relevant <one-sided limits> exist, including the indicated single <one-sided limit> at each endpoint.

At an interior point, <continuity> implies both <one-sided limits> equal $f(x_0)$. Conversely, equality $f^-(x_0)=f^+(x_0)$ and the displayed inequalities force both to equal $f(x_0)$, giving <continuity>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{f\text{ continuous at }x_0\in(a,b)\iff f^-(x_0)=f^+(x_0).}
$$

If $f$ is a <continuous function>, the <intermediate value theorem> ensures that every value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ is attained. <Monotonicity> excludes values outside that range, so $f([a,b])=[f(a),f(b)]$.

Conversely, suppose this equality of <sets> holds. At an interior point, if $f^-(x_0)<f(x_0)$, any $y$ strictly between these two numbers lies in $[f(a),f(b)]$. Yet inputs below $x_0$ have values at most $f^-(x_0)$ and inputs at or above $x_0$ have values at least $f(x_0)$, so no input attains $y$. This contradicts <surjectivity>. The same argument excludes $f(x_0)<f^+(x_0)$, so part (b) gives <continuity> at every interior point.

At $a$, a strict gap $f(a)<f^+(a)$ likewise leaves every value between them unattained; hence $f^+(a)=f(a)$, giving right <continuity>. At $b$, the corresponding argument gives $f^-(b)=f(b)$ and left <continuity>. These are the appropriate endpoint notions on $[a,b]$. Hence
$$
\boxed{f\text{ continuous on }[a,b]\iff f([a,b])=[f(a),f(b)].}
$$

The <sequence> $(f_n)$ converges by <uniform convergence> to $f$ on $E$ if
$$
\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists M\in\mathbb N\ \forall n\geq M\ \forall x\in E:\quad |f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon.
$$
The index $M$ must work simultaneously for every $x\in E$.

Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $m\geq N$ so large that $|f_m(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon/3$ for every $x\in E$. The <continuity> of this one fixed $f_m$ at $x_0$ gives $\delta>0$ such that $x\in E$ and $|x-x_0|<\delta$ imply $|f_m(x)-f_m(x_0)|<\varepsilon/3$. The <triangle inequality> then gives
$$
|f(x)-f(x_0)|\leq|f(x)-f_m(x)|+|f_m(x)-f_m(x_0)|+|f_m(x_0)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon.
$$
Thus **$f$ is continuous at $x_0$**, with <continuity> understood relative to $E$. This is the pointwise form of the <uniform limit theorem>.

The <function> $f$ is <differentiable> at $c$ if the finite <limit of a function>
$$
f'(c)=\lim_{x\to c,\ x\ne c}\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}
$$
exists. If it exists, put $L=f'(c)$ and define
$$
\epsilon(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}-L,&x\ne c,\\0,&x=c.\end{cases}
$$
The definition of <derivative> gives $\epsilon(x)\to0$ as $x\to c$. For $x\ne c$, rearrangement gives the desired <local linear approximation>
$$
f(x)=f(c)+L(x-c)+\epsilon(x)(x-c).
$$
At $x=c$, this identity holds because both terms containing $x-c$ vanish. Conversely, if such an identity holds, then for $x\ne c$ its <difference quotient> is $L+\epsilon(x)\to L$. Hence $f$ is <differentiable> at $c$, and necessarily $\boxed{L=f'(c)}$.

By the <Heine-Cantor theorem>, a <continuous function> on the <compact interval> $[a,b]$ is <uniformly continuous>. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $\delta>0$ such that $|f(s)-f(t)|<\varepsilon$ whenever $s,t\in[a,b]$ and $|s-t|<\delta$.

Choose a finite <partition of an interval> $a=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_m=b$ with every width below $\delta$. Define the <step function>
$$
\phi(t)=f(t_{j-1})\quad(t_{j-1}\leq t<t_j),\qquad\phi(b)=f(b).
$$
For each $t<b$, its distance from the chosen left endpoint is below $\delta$, so $|f(t)-\phi(t)|<\varepsilon$; at $b$ the error is zero. Hence
$$
\boxed{|f(t)-\phi(t)|<\varepsilon\quad\text{for every }t\in[a,b].}
$$
A <step function> may have separately assigned values at its finitely many partition points; these values do not affect its <Riemann integral>. If $a=b$, simply take $\phi=f$ on that singleton.

Put $E_n=\sup_{t\in[a,b]}|f(t)-\phi_n(t)|\to0$, so the hypothesis is <uniform convergence>. Every <step function> is bounded and <Riemann integrable>: away from its finitely many partition points it is constant, and those exceptional points can be covered by <intervals> of arbitrarily small total length. A <partition of an interval> containing their boundaries makes the difference of its <upper Darboux sum> and <lower Darboux sum> arbitrarily small.

Choose one $n$ with $E_n<\infty$. Boundedness of $\phi_n$ and $|f|\leq|\phi_n|+E_n$ show that $f$ is a <bounded function>. Assume $a<b$, since for $a=b$ all the <integrals> are zero. Given $\eta>0$, choose $n$ so that $2E_n(b-a)<\eta/2$, and then a <partition of an interval> $P$ such that $U(\phi_n,P)-L(\phi_n,P)<\eta/2$. On every partition subinterval, $|f-\phi_n|\leq E_n$ gives
$$
U(f,P)\leq U(\phi_n,P)+E_n(b-a),\qquad L(f,P)\geq L(\phi_n,P)-E_n(b-a).
$$
Thus
$$
U(f,P)-L(f,P)\leq U(\phi_n,P)-L(\phi_n,P)+2E_n(b-a)<\eta.
$$
The <Riemann integrability criterion> proves that $f$ is <Riemann integrable>. Finally, <linearity of the integral> and <monotonicity of the Riemann integral> give
$$
\left|\int_a^bf(t)\,dt-\int_a^b\phi_n(t)\,dt\right|\leq\int_a^b|f(t)-\phi_n(t)|\,dt\leq(b-a)E_n\longrightarrow0.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\int_a^bf(t)\,dt=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_a^b\phi_n(t)\,dt.}
$$
This proves the relevant case of <uniform convergence preserves integrals on a compact interval>.

**The printed assertion needs an endpoint qualification:** the enumeration includes zero, but $\mathbf1_{[0,0)}$ is identically zero. In fact $f$ is continuous at zero; the claimed <discontinuities> occur at every $r_j>0$.

Define the finite <step function> sums
$$
\phi_N(t)=\sum_{k=1}^N\frac1{k^2}\mathbf1_{[0,r_k)}(t).
$$
The assumed convergence of $\sum_k1/k^2$ and the bound on each <indicator function> give
$$
0\leq f(t)-\phi_N(t)\leq\sum_{k>N}\frac1{k^2},\qquad\|f-\phi_N\|_\infty\leq\sum_{k>N}\frac1{k^2}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus the defining <series> converges everywhere and has <uniform convergence>. Part (b) proves that **$f$ is <Riemann integrable>**; it also gives, if desired,
$$
\boxed{\int_0^1f(t)\,dt=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{r_k}{k^2}.}
$$
For $r_j>0$ and $0\leq t<r_j$, every <indicator function> is <nonincreasing>, and the $j$th drops by one between $t$ and $r_j$. Hence
$$
f(t)-f(r_j)=\sum_{k\geq1}\frac{\mathbf1_{[0,r_k)}(t)-\mathbf1_{[0,r_k)}(r_j)}{k^2}\geq\frac1{j^2}.
$$
Taking inputs from the left tending to $r_j$ proves a <discontinuity> there.

For completeness, <continuity> at zero follows directly from a finite-head and tail argument. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ with $\sum_{k>N}1/k^2<\varepsilon$. Choose $\delta>0$ below every positive $r_k$ with $k\leq N$. For $0\leq t<\delta$, the first $N$ <indicator functions> have exactly their values at zero; the one with $r_k=0$, if present, is always zero. Thus $|f(t)-f(0)|\leq\sum_{k>N}1/k^2<\varepsilon$. This confirms that the endpoint exception is real. Replacing the printed enumeration of $[0,1)$ by one of $(0,1)$ would remove it.

Finally, the <density of the rational numbers> supplies a positive rational $r_j$ in every nonempty open subinterval of $[0,1]$. Thus **a <Riemann integrable> <function> can have a <discontinuity> in every open subinterval**: <dense jumps do not prevent Riemann integrability>.
