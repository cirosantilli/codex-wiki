= Solution

For $r>0$, the <partial derivatives> $r_x=x/r$ and $r_y=y/r$ give
$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\frac{x^2}{r}=\frac{2x}{r}-\frac{x^3}{r^3},\qquad
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\frac{xy}{r}=\frac{x}{r}-\frac{xy^2}{r^3}.
$$
Adding these <partial derivatives> yields the <divergence>:
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf f=\frac{3x}{r}-\frac{x(x^2+y^2)}{r^3}=\frac{2x}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}},\qquad (x,y)\ne(0,0).}
$$
The original <vector field> is undefined at the origin. Since $\|\mathbf f\|=|x|\leq r$, it has the <continuous extension> $\mathbf f(0,0)=\mathbf0$. This extension is not <differentiable>: on $y=0$ its first component is $|x|$, whose <derivative> at zero does not exist. Thus the displayed <divergence> has no classical value at the origin.

The <triangle> is $0\leq y\leq x\leq1$. In <polar coordinates> this becomes
$$
0\leq\theta\leq\frac\pi4,\qquad 0\leq r\leq\sec\theta,
$$
because the outer side is $x=r\cos\theta=1$. The <vector field> and the <Jacobian determinant> give
$$
\mathbf f=r\cos^2\theta\,\mathbf e_1+r\cos\theta\sin\theta\,\mathbf e_2,\qquad dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta.
$$
Evaluate the <double integral> component by component:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_R\mathbf f\,dx\,dy
&=\frac13\int_0^{\pi/4}\bigl(\sec\theta\,\mathbf e_1+\sec\theta\tan\theta\,\mathbf e_2\bigr)d\theta\\
&=\frac13\left[\log(\sec\theta+\tan\theta)\,\mathbf e_1+\sec\theta\,\mathbf e_2\right]_0^{\pi/4}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Consequently,
$$
\boxed{\iint_R\mathbf f\,dx\,dy=\frac{\log(1+\sqrt2)}3\,\mathbf e_1+\frac{\sqrt2-1}3\,\mathbf e_2.}
$$
The origin causes no ambiguity in this <double integral>: the <continuous extension> from part (a) is bounded there, and changing a value at a single point does not change the <integral>.

Pair each point $\mathbf x$ of the <square>'s boundary with $-\mathbf x$. The <vector field> obeys $\mathbf f(-\mathbf x)=\mathbf f(\mathbf x)$, whereas the outward <unit normal vector> changes sign: $\mathbf n(-\mathbf x)=-\mathbf n(\mathbf x)$. Corresponding <arc length> elements are equal. Thus the two contributions to the <flux> cancel, proving without evaluating any boundary <integral> that
$$
\boxed{\int_C\mathbf f\cdot\mathbf n\,ds=0.}
$$
The same cancellation appears in the <divergence>, which is an <odd function> of $x$ over a region symmetric under $x\mapsto-x$. To justify using the planar <divergence theorem> despite the lack of <differentiability> at the origin, remove a disk of radius $\varepsilon<1$. The <vector field> is continuously differentiable on the remaining region, its <divergence> has zero <double integral> by this symmetry, and the magnitude of the inner <flux> is at most $2\pi\varepsilon^2$, since $\|\mathbf f\|\leq\varepsilon$ there. Letting $\varepsilon\to0$ recovers the result. This is <flux around a removable point singularity>.

Let $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^3$ be a bounded region with a piecewise smooth closed boundary, and let $\mathbf F$ be a continuously differentiable <vector field> on an open neighbourhood of its closure. With $\mathbf n$ the outward <unit normal vector>, the <divergence theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\iiint_\Omega\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV=\iint_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,dA.}
$$
The boundary <surface integral> is the total outward <flux>. All boundary components are included; on the boundary of a cavity, the outward <unit normal vector> points into the cavity.

The <specific heat capacity> $c$ is the <energy> needed per unit <mass> per unit increase in <temperature>. With $c$ constant, a small element of <volume> $dV$ has <mass> $\rho\,dV$ and thermal <energy> $\rho cT\,dV$, measured relative to the reference at which $T=0$. Summing over $\Omega$ gives
$$
\boxed{E_\Omega(t)=\iiint_\Omega\rho cT(\mathbf x,t)\,dV.}
$$
Choosing a different reference <temperature> only adds a time-independent constant to this <energy>, so it does not affect the <heat balance with volumetric heat generation>.

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

For any fixed subregion $\Omega$, with no internal <heat> generation, <conservation of energy> says that the rate of stored thermal <energy> equals the incoming <heat flux>:
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\iiint_\Omega\rho cT\,dV=-\iint_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf n\,dA.
$$
The minus sign is needed because $\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf n>0$ denotes outgoing <heat flux>. Substitute <Fourier's law> $\mathbf q=-k\nabla T$ and apply the <divergence theorem>:
$$
\iiint_\Omega\rho cT_t\,dV
=\iint_{\partial\Omega}k\nabla T\cdot\mathbf n\,dA
=\iiint_\Omega\nabla\cdot(k\nabla T)\,dV.
$$
Here differentiation under the <integral> uses a fixed region and sufficient regularity of $T$. Since the identity holds for every $\Omega$, continuity of the integrands gives the local <heat equation>
$$
\boxed{\rho cT_t=\nabla\cdot\bigl(k(\mathbf x)\nabla T\bigr).}
$$
For spatially varying <thermal conductivity>, the <product rule> gives $\nabla\cdot(k\nabla T)=k\nabla^2T+\nabla k\cdot\nabla T$; the second term cannot generally be discarded.

Suppose $T^{(1)}$ and $T^{(2)}$ are two sufficiently regular <steady-state> solutions with the same prescribed <temperature> on the boundary. Their difference $w=T^{(1)}-T^{(2)}$ satisfies
$$
\nabla\cdot(k\nabla w)=0\quad\hbox{in }R,\qquad w=0\quad\hbox{on }\partial R.
$$
The identical <PDE source terms> cancel. Apply the <product rule> and the <divergence theorem> to $wk\nabla w$; this is the weighted form of <Green's first identity>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
0&=\iiint_R w\nabla\cdot(k\nabla w)\,dV\\
&=\iint_{\partial R}wk\nabla w\cdot\mathbf n\,dA-\iiint_Rk\|\nabla w\|^2\,dV
=-\iiint_Rk\|\nabla w\|^2\,dV.
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $k>0$, the nonnegative integrand must vanish, so $\nabla w=0$. Hence $w$ is constant on each connected component of $R$, and its zero <Dirichlet boundary condition> makes each constant zero. Therefore $\boxed{T^{(1)}=T^{(2)}}$: **the solution, if it exists, is unique**. This proves <Dirichlet uniqueness for variable-conductivity heat conduction>; it does not require a constant <thermal conductivity>.

Integrate the <steady-state> equation over $R$ and use the <divergence theorem>:
$$
0=\iint_{\partial R}k\nabla T\cdot\mathbf n\,dA+\rho\iiint_R S(\mathbf x)\,dV.
$$
The zero <heat flux> condition makes the boundary term vanish. But $\rho>0$ and $S>0$ throughout a region of positive <volume>, so
$$
\rho\iiint_R S\,dV>0,
$$
a contradiction. Thus **no steady-state solution exists**. This is the <insulated steady heat balance>: positive internal <heat> generation continually increases the stored thermal <energy> when no <heat> can escape.

For any closed surface enclosing a region $V$, <Gauss's law for gravity> states that the outward <flux> of the <gravitational field> is
$$
\boxed{\iint_{\partial V}\mathbf g\cdot\mathbf n\,dA=-4\pi G\iiint_V\rho\,dV=-4\pi GM_V.}
$$
Here $G$ is the <gravitational constant> and $M_V$ is the enclosed <mass>; the negative sign expresses attractive <gravity>.

Use the <gravitational potential sign conventions> specified by this paper: $\mathbf g=\nabla\varphi$. Thus $\varphi$ is the negative of the usual <Newtonian gravitational potential> $\Phi$ defined by $\mathbf g=-\nabla\Phi$. For example, setting the value at infinity to zero gives $\varphi=GM/r$ for a <point mass>. The <divergence theorem> now gives
$$
\iiint_V\nabla^2\varphi\,dV=-4\pi G\iiint_V\rho\,dV.
$$
Since $V$ is arbitrary, continuity of the integrands implies the local <Poisson's equation>
$$
\boxed{\nabla^2\varphi=-4\pi G\rho.}
$$
Conversely, integrating this <Poisson's equation> over any $V$ and applying the <divergence theorem> recovers <Gauss's law for gravity>. At a discontinuity of the <mass density>, the local equation is interpreted piecewise, or globally in the sense of <distributions>.

The <mass density> depends only on $r$, so it has <spherical symmetry>. For this isolated planet, choose the <Newtonian gravitational potential> to vanish at infinity, or equivalently choose $\varphi=-\Phi$ to do so. There is no imposed external <gravitational field>. Rotating the <mass distribution> therefore leaves $\varphi$ unchanged: $\varphi=\varphi(r)$.

The <gradient> in <spherical coordinates> consequently has no angular components, and the paper's <gravitational potential sign conventions> give
$$
\boxed{\mathbf g=\nabla\varphi=\frac{d\varphi}{dr}\mathbf e_r,\qquad r>0.}
$$
At the centre there is no distinguished radial direction, and <spherical symmetry> makes $\mathbf g(0)=\mathbf0$.

On a <sphere> of radius $r$, the radial <gravitational field> has constant magnitude. Thus <Gauss's law for gravity> becomes
$$
4\pi r^2\varphi'(r)=-4\pi GM(r),\qquad
\varphi'(r)=-\frac{GM(r)}{r^2},
$$
where $M(r)$ is the enclosed <mass>. In the hollow core $M(r)=0$. In the material shell, put $u=r/r_0$ and use the <spherical coordinates> volume element:
$$
\begin{aligned}
M(r)&=4\pi\rho_0r_0\int_{r_0}^r s e^{-s/r_0}\,ds\\
&=4\pi\rho_0r_0^3\int_1^u v e^{-v}\,dv
=4\pi\rho_0r_0^3\left[\frac2e-(u+1)e^{-u}\right],\qquad 1\leq u\leq2.
\end{aligned}
$$
Outside the shell the enclosed <mass> is the total
$$
M_*=4\pi\rho_0r_0^3\left(\frac2e-\frac3{e^2}\right).
$$
Consequently,
$$
\boxed{\varphi'(r)=
\begin{cases}
0,&0\leq r<r_0,\\
-\dfrac{4\pi G\rho_0r_0^3}{r^2}\left[\dfrac2e-\left(1+\dfrac r{r_0}\right)e^{-r/r_0}\right],&r_0\leq r\leq2r_0,\\
-\dfrac{4\pi G\rho_0r_0^3}{r^2}\left(\dfrac2e-\dfrac3{e^2}\right),&r>2r_0.
\end{cases}}
$$
At $r=0$ the displayed value refers to the radial <derivative> of the constant core value. The expressions match at both shell boundaries. There is no surface sheet of <mass>, so a jump in the volume <mass density> does not produce a jump in the <gravitational field>. The core has zero <gravitational field>, and the exterior <gravitational field> equals that of a central <point mass> $M_*$, as in the <shell theorem>.

The core contributes no change in $\varphi$, since $\varphi'=0$ there. Integrating the radial <gravitational field> across the shell, with $u=r/r_0$, gives

\varphi(2r_0)-\varphi(0)

The negative sign agrees with $\varphi'(r)<0$ in the material shell under the paper's <gravitational potential sign conventions>.

Write $a=2\pi m+r$, where $m$ is an <integer> and $0\leq r<2\pi$. The <change of variables> $x=y+2\pi m$ and the fact that $f$ is a <periodic function> give
$$
\int_a^{a+2\pi}f(x)\,dx=\int_r^{r+2\pi}f(y)\,dy.
$$
Split at $2\pi$, then substitute $y=z+2\pi$ in the second piece:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_r^{r+2\pi}f(y)\,dy
&=\int_r^{2\pi}f(y)\,dy+\int_{2\pi}^{2\pi+r}f(y)\,dy\\
&=\int_r^{2\pi}f(y)\,dy+\int_0^r f(z+2\pi)\,dz\\
&=\int_r^{2\pi}f(y)\,dy+\int_0^r f(z)\,dz.
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\int_a^{a+2\pi}f(x)\,dx=\int_0^{2\pi}f(x)\,dx\quad\text{for every }a\in\mathbb R.}
$$
This proves <translation invariance of an integral over one period> without assuming that $f$ is <differentiable>.

On $(-\pi,\pi)$ the <function> is <odd>, so its mean and all its <cosine> <Fourier coefficients> vanish:
$$
a_0=0,\qquad a_n=0\quad(n\geq1).
$$
The assigned endpoint value does not affect these <integrals>. The product of the two <odd functions> $x|x|$ and $\sin nx$ is <even>, hence the <sine> <Fourier coefficients> are
$$
b_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi x^2\sin nx\,dx.
$$
Two applications of <integration by parts> give
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^\pi x^2\sin nx\,dx
&=\left[-\frac{x^2\cos nx}{n}\right]_0^\pi+\frac2n\int_0^\pi x\cos nx\,dx\\
&=-\frac{\pi^2(-1)^n}{n}+\frac2n\left[\frac{x\sin nx}{n}+\frac{\cos nx}{n^2}\right]_0^\pi\\
&=-\frac{\pi^2(-1)^n}{n}+\frac{2[(-1)^n-1]}{n^3}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{f(x)\sim\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{2\pi(-1)^{n+1}}n+\frac{4[(-1)^n-1]}{\pi n^3}\right]\sin nx.}
$$
The <periodic extension> has a <jump discontinuity> at every odd multiple of $\pi$: its left and right limits are $\pi^2$ and $-\pi^2$. Its assigned value at each such point is $-\pi^2$. Thus it is <odd> away from these points, but it is not a globally <odd function> pointwise. The <Fourier series> will take the midpoint value zero at these jumps.

By <Dirichlet's theorem on Fourier series>, a piecewise continuously differentiable <periodic function> has a <Fourier series> converging at each point to the average of its one-sided limits. In particular it converges to the <function> at every point of <continuity>. Our <periodic extension> satisfies these hypotheses, and $x=\pi/2$ is a point of <continuity> with $f(\pi/2)=\pi^2/4$.

At this point $\sin(n\pi/2)=0$ for even $n$. For $n=2j-1$,
$$
\sin\frac{(2j-1)\pi}{2}=(-1)^{j+1},\qquad
b_{2j-1}=\frac{2\pi}{2j-1}-\frac8{\pi(2j-1)^3}.

Substituting in the <Fourier series> from part (c) proves

The sum is taken in its displayed order. Discarding its zero even-indexed terms is valid and does not rearrange the remaining terms.

Write $u(x,t)=U(\xi,\eta)$, where $\xi=x-t$ and $\eta=x+t$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\partial_x=\partial_\xi+\partial_\eta,\qquad
\partial_t=-\partial_\xi+\partial_\eta.
$$
For a twice continuously differentiable <classical solution>, mixed <partial derivatives> commute, so the <wave equation> becomes
$$
0=u_{tt}-u_{xx}=-4U_{\xi\eta}.
$$
Hence $U_\xi$ depends only on $\xi$. Integrating first in $\xi$ yields the <countertravelling decomposition of the one-dimensional wave equation>:
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=F(x-t)+G(x+t).}
$$
On a rectangular domain in $(\xi,\eta)$ this follows immediately; on the full line for $t\geq0$, the connected characteristic slices give the same representation. The two terms travel at <wave speed> one in opposite directions.

Impose the <initial conditions>. At $t=0$,
$$
F(x)+G(x)=f(x),\qquad -F'(x)+G'(x)=g(x).
$$
Differentiate the first identity and combine:
$$
F'(x)=\frac{f'(x)-g(x)}2,\qquad G'(x)=\frac{f'(x)+g(x)}2.
$$
If $H(x)=\int_{x_*}^xg(s)\,ds$, then $F=(f-H)/2+C$ and $G=(f+H)/2-C$. Substitution cancels $C$ and the arbitrary base point $x_*$, giving <D'Alembert's formula>:
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=\frac{f(x-t)+f(x+t)}2+\frac12\int_{x-t}^{x+t}g(s)\,ds.}
$$
For example, $f\in C^2$ and $g\in C^1$ make this a <classical solution> of the <wave equation> satisfying both <initial conditions>. The same formula defines a <weak solution> for less regular data, including the continuous piecewise smooth data of part (b).

Since the initial <velocity> vanishes, <D'Alembert's formula> reduces to
$$
u(x,t)=\frac12\bigl[f(x-t)+f(x+t)\bigr].
$$
Each translated copy has half the original <amplitude>. To identify each requested cross-section, track the translated support intervals and add the values where they overlap.

At $t=\pi$, $f(x-\pi)$ is supported on $[-\pi,0]\cup[2\pi,3\pi]$ and $f(x+\pi)$ on $[-3\pi,-2\pi]\cup[0,\pi]$. On these intervals $\sin(x\pm\pi)=-\sin x$, so
$$
\boxed{u(x,\pi)=
\begin{cases}
-\tfrac12\sin x,&x\in[-3\pi,-2\pi]\cup[-\pi,\pi]\cup[2\pi,3\pi],\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}}
$$
The pieces meet continuously at zero, where both contributions vanish.

At $t=3\pi/2$, the support of $f(x-3\pi/2)$ is $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]\cup[5\pi/2,7\pi/2]$; that of $f(x+3\pi/2)$ is $[-7\pi/2,-5\pi/2]\cup[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$. In the central overlap,
$$
\sin(x-3\pi/2)=\cos x,\qquad\sin(x+3\pi/2)=-\cos x,
$$
so the two contributions cancel. The remaining <wave> profile is
$$
\boxed{u(x,3\pi/2)=
\begin{cases}
-\tfrac12\cos x,&-7\pi/2\leq x\leq-5\pi/2,\\
\tfrac12\cos x,&5\pi/2\leq x\leq7\pi/2,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}}
$$
For the time cross-section at the origin, the initial profile is an <odd function>, so
$$
\boxed{u(0,t)=\tfrac12[f(-t)+f(t)]=0\qquad(t\geq0).}
$$
For the time cross-section at $x=\pi/2$, the left-travelling contribution is nonzero for $\pi/2\leq t\leq3\pi/2$ and equals $\cos t/2$. The right-travelling contribution is nonzero for $3\pi/2\leq t\leq5\pi/2$ and also equals $\cos t/2$. At the common endpoint both vanish. Hence
$$
\boxed{u(\pi/2,t)=
\begin{cases}
\tfrac12\cos t,&\pi/2\leq t\leq5\pi/2,\\
0,&0\leq t<\pi/2\ \text{or }t>5\pi/2.
\end{cases}}
$$
The following graphs show all four cross-sections. In particular, the last trace reaches $-1/2$ at $t=\pi$ and $1/2$ at $t=2\pi$.

\Image[mathematics-v-wave-sections.png]

Because $u(0,t)=0$, restriction to the half-line solves the following <initial-boundary value problem> with a homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary condition>:
$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
u_{tt}&=u_{xx},&&x>0,\ t>0,\\
u(0,t)&=0,&&t>0,\\
u(x,0)&=
\begin{cases}\sin x,&\pi\leq x\leq2\pi,\\0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}&&x\geq0,\\
u_t(x,0)&=0,&&x\geq0.
\end{aligned}}
$$
This is <wave reflection by odd extension>. If a condition at infinity is desired, take the solution with finite <wave energy>; here the profile has bounded support at every finite time.

The initial profile is <continuous> and piecewise continuously differentiable, but its first <derivative> jumps at the support endpoints. Thus the formula gives a <weak solution> globally and a piecewise <classical solution> away from the <characteristic lines for the one-dimensional wave equation> issuing from those endpoints. The initial <velocity> is zero in the <square-integrable function> sense and pointwise wherever the initial profile is <differentiable>; at the kink points a pointwise right time <derivative> need not equal zero. This regularity qualification is needed for the given data.

Let $A$ be the constant cross-sectional <area>, and suppose that the <temperature> is uniform on each cross-section. Consider a slice $[a,b]$. Its stored thermal <energy> is $\rho cA\int_a^bT(x,t)\,dx$. Since the lateral surface is insulated and there is no internal <heat> generation, <conservation of energy> gives
$$
\rho cA\frac{d}{dt}\int_a^bT(x,t)\,dx=Aq(a,t)-Aq(b,t),
$$
where $q$ is the <heat flux> in the positive $x$ direction. By <Fourier's law>, $q=-kT_x$, so
$$
\rho c\int_a^bT_t\,dx=kT_x(b,t)-kT_x(a,t)=k\int_a^bT_{xx}\,dx.
$$
The slice is arbitrary; therefore the local <heat equation> is
$$
\boxed{T_t=\kappa T_{xx},\qquad\kappa=\frac{k}{\rho c}>0.}
$$
The constant $\kappa$ is the <thermal diffusivity>. The cancellation of $A$ uses a rod of uniform cross-section; the one-dimensional model assumes no transverse <temperature> variation.

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

Subtract the constant <steady-state> profile: set $v=T-T_0$. Then
$$
v_t=\kappa v_{xx},\qquad v(0,t)=0,\qquad v_x(L,t)=0.
$$
The last equation follows from <Fourier's law> and zero <heat flux> at the insulated end. For a nonzero separated solution $v=X(x)H(t)$, <separation of variables> gives
$$
\frac{H'}{\kappa H}=\frac{X''}{X}=-\mu,\qquad
-X''=\mu X,\qquad X(0)=0,\quad X'(L)=0.
$$
To see that $\mu>0$ for a nonzero <eigenfunction>, multiply the spatial equation by $X$ and use <integration by parts>:
$$
\mu\int_0^L X^2\,dx=\int_0^L(X')^2\,dx.
$$
Both boundary terms vanish. If the right side were zero, $X$ would be constant and $X(0)=0$ would make it zero. Thus write $\mu=\lambda^2$ with $\lambda>0$. Solving gives $X=A\sin(\lambda x)$, and $X'(L)=0$ requires $\cos(\lambda L)=0$. The <mixed Dirichlet-Neumann modes on an interval> therefore have
$$
\lambda_n=\frac{(2n-1)\pi}{2L},\qquad X_n(x)=\sin(\lambda_nx),\qquad
H_n(t)=e^{-\kappa\lambda_n^2t},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.
$$
The <eigenvalues> of $-d^2/dx^2$ are $\mu_n=\lambda_n^2$; $\lambda_n$ are their positive square roots. By <superposition>, with coefficients for which the resulting <series> and its required <derivatives> converge at positive times,
$$
\boxed{T(x,t)=T_0+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n\sin\left(\frac{(2n-1)\pi x}{2L}\right)\exp\left[-\frac{\kappa(2n-1)^2\pi^2t}{4L^2}\right].}
$$
The <eigenfunctions> are <orthogonal> on $[0,L]$ and have squared norm $L/2$. Expanding the initial excess <temperature> $v(x,0)=T_0x/L$ gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
b_n&=\frac2L\int_0^L\frac{T_0x}{L}\sin(\lambda_nx)\,dx\\
&=\frac{2T_0}{L^2}\left[-\frac{x\cos(\lambda_nx)}{\lambda_n}+\frac{\sin(\lambda_nx)}{\lambda_n^2}\right]_0^L\\
&=\frac{2T_0(-1)^{n-1}}{L^2\lambda_n^2}
=\boxed{\frac{8T_0(-1)^{n-1}}{(2n-1)^2\pi^2}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the requested <temperature> is
$$
\boxed{T(x,t)=T_0+\frac{8T_0}{\pi^2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{(2n-1)^2}\sin\left(\frac{(2n-1)\pi x}{2L}\right)e^{-\kappa(2n-1)^2\pi^2t/(4L^2)}.}
$$
At the insulated endpoint $\sin(\lambda_nL)=(-1)^{n-1}$ cancels the alternating coefficient sign, leaving
$$
\boxed{T(L,t)=T_0+\frac{8T_0}{\pi^2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\kappa(2n-1)^2\pi^2t/(4L^2)}}{(2n-1)^2}>T_0\quad\text{for every finite }t\geq0.}
$$
Every summand is positive. **The endpoint temperature never drops below $T_0$**; it decreases towards $T_0$ as $t\to\infty$ and starts at $T(L,0)=2T_0$.

For completeness, the <Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion> gives the initial profile in the $L^2$ sense. Here the coefficients are absolutely summable, so the <Weierstrass M-test> also gives <uniform convergence> on $[0,L]\times[0,\infty)$, and the initial values are recovered everywhere. For positive times the exponential factors justify all required termwise <derivatives>, giving a <classical solution>. There is, however, an <initial incompatibility with a Neumann boundary condition> at $(L,0)$: the initial slope is $T_0/L$, while the insulated <Neumann boundary condition> requires zero slope for $t>0$. The <temperature> is continuous to the initial edge, but $T_x$ cannot extend continuously to that corner. One must not differentiate the initial <series> term by term at $x=L$.
