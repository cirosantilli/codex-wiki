= Mathematical logic
{wiki}

Mathematical logic studies <formal languages>, <formal proofs>, <model theory>, <computability theory>, and the <foundations of mathematics>. It makes the rules of reasoning explicit enough that reasoning itself becomes an object of <mathematics>. <Proof theory> studies deductions; <model theory> studies interpretations in which statements hold.

Three questions organize the subject: is an expression a well-formed <first-order formula>, is it true in a specified <first-order structure>, and does it have a <formal proof> from specified <axioms>? These questions are distinct. For example, $\exists n\,(n+n=1)$ is a <first-order sentence> in an appropriate <first-order language>; it is false in the <natural numbers> but true in the <rational numbers>, with $n=1/2$.

The beauty of <mathematical logic> is that the same <logical connectives> and rules of <universal quantification> and <existential quantification> organize arguments across <mathematics>. <Classical propositional logic> uses two truth values; <intuitionistic propositional logic> interprets <logical implication> through constructions on proofs, without unrestricted <law of excluded middle>. This proof-based framework is developed in \a[https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~fp/courses/15317-s23/lectures/02-natded.pdf][Frank Pfenning, Natural Deduction, sections 1–3]{ref}.

= Proof theory
{parent=Mathematical logic}
{wiki}

Proof theory studies <formal proofs> and the deductive systems that produce them. It examines which conclusions follow from <axioms>, how proofs can be transformed, and what those transformations reveal about the chosen logic. <Natural deduction> organizes a proof through introduction and elimination rules for <logical connectives> and quantifiers.

= Axiom
{parent=Mathematical logic}
{wiki}

An axiom is a sentence admitted as a starting assumption of a formal theory. An axiom schema specifies a collection of such sentences, usually one for each formula of an allowed form. A <model of a first-order theory> satisfies all its axioms; a <relative consistency> construction can show that the other axioms do not imply a particular omitted one.

= Definition
{disambiguate=mathematics}
{parent=Mathematical logic}

= Definition
{synonym}

A mathematical definition fixes the meaning of a term or symbol using previously specified concepts. A definition by a <universal property> can still require a <mathematical proof> that an object with that property exists. For example, the definition of a <product in a category> specifies its mapping behavior, while existence is a further question about that <category>.

= Second-order logic
{parent=Mathematical logic}
{wiki}

A logic allowing quantification over relations or <subsets> of the object domain as well as over its individual objects. The range of relation quantifiers depends on the chosen semantics.

= Existential second-order logic
{parent=Second-order logic}
{title2=$\exists\mathrm{SO}$}

An existential second-order sentence consists of existentially quantified finite-arity relation symbols followed by a <first-order sentence>. Over finite structures, witness relations have polynomial-length encodings for a fixed sentence. <Fagin's theorem> identifies the properties definable in this logic with those decidable in <NP>.

= Fagin's theorem
{parent=Existential second-order logic}
{c}

A class of finite relational structures closed under isomorphism is definable by an <existential second-order logic> sentence exactly when its standard encodings form a language in <NP>. No order on the structures is required; an auxiliary order can itself be included among the existentially guessed relations.

= Henkin semantics
{c}
{parent=Second-order logic}

Second-order quantifiers range over specified collections of relations, which can be proper subcollections of all external relations. With these collections as additional sorts, this is a form of <first-order logic>. Full-class conclusions cannot be inferred merely from truth with <Henkin semantics>.

= Full semantics for second-order logic
{parent=Second-order logic}

Every <subset> or relation of the appropriate arity on the object domain is allowed as a value of a second-order variable. For a domain $V_\lambda$, an external functional graph with all its individual <ordered pairs> in $V_\lambda$ is therefore available, even if the whole graph does not belong to $V_\lambda$.

= Second-order arithmetic
{parent=Mathematical logic}
{title2=$\mathsf Z_2$}
{wiki}

<Second-order arithmetic> has variables for <natural numbers> and for sets of <natural numbers>. Subsystems restrict comprehension and induction; <arithmetical transfinite recursion theory> is one important subsystem stronger than the first-order system <Peano arithmetic>.

= Arithmetical transfinite recursion theory
{parent=Second-order arithmetic}
{title2=$\mathsf{ATR}_0$}

= ATR0
{synonym}
{c}

<Arithmetical transfinite recursion theory> permits <arithmetic> recursive definitions along coded countable well-orders, together with the usual <arithmetic> comprehension background. <Friedman's finite form of Kruskal's theorem> is not provable in this theory.

= Formal proof
{parent=Mathematical logic}

A formal proof is a finite sequence of formulae in which every line is an axiom, an assumption, or follows from earlier lines by a specified inference rule.

= Infinitary logic
{parent=Mathematical logic}
{wiki}

<Infinitary logic> permits infinitary syntactic operations, such as infinite conjunctions, together with an interpretation of its formulas. The <infinitary language> $L_{\kappa,\lambda}$ specifies the permitted conjunction and quantifier lengths.

= Infinitary language
{title2=$L_{\kappa,\lambda}$}
{parent=Infinitary logic}

The infinitary language $L_{\kappa,\lambda}$ permits conjunctions and disjunctions of fewer than $\kappa$ formulas and simultaneous quantification over fewer than $\lambda$ variables. Ordinary first-order logic is $L_{\omega,\omega}$.

= Countable infinitary logic
{parent=Infinitary language}
{title2=$L_{\omega_1,\omega}$}

The logic $L_{\omega_1,\omega}$ extends <first-order logic> by countable <logical conjunctions> and <logical disjunctions>. Each formula has finitely many <free variables>, and quantifier strings are finite. In a countable <first-order language>, every countable structure has a <Scott sentence> in this logic that characterizes its <isomorphism> class among countable structures.

= Scott isomorphism theorem
{parent=Countable infinitary logic}
{c}

= Scott's isomorphism theorem
{synonym}
{c}

Every countable structure in a countable <first-order language> has a <Scott sentence>. Hence countable structures satisfying the same sentences of <countable infinitary logic> are <isomorphic>. The proof uses <Scott formulas>, stabilization on the countable set of finite <tuples>, and the <back-and-forth method>.

= Scott sentence
{parent=Countable infinitary logic}
{c}
{title2=$\sigma_M$}

A Scott sentence for a countable structure is a sentence of <countable infinitary logic> satisfied, among countable structures in the same <first-order language>, exactly by its <isomorphic> copies. It may also have uncountable models. <Scott formulas> and stabilized <back-and-forth method> equivalences give its construction.

= Scott formula
{parent=Countable infinitary logic}
{c}
{title2=$\phi_{\bar a}^{\alpha}(\bar x)$}

A Scott formula describes a finite <tuple> at a transfinite stage of the <back-and-forth method>. Stage zero specifies the complete atomic description; successor stages specify matching one-element extensions in both directions; limit stages conjoin the earlier descriptions. For a countable structure the equivalences on its finite <tuples> stabilize at a countable <ordinal>.

= Propositional logic
{parent=Mathematical logic}
{wiki}

= Contradiction
{parent=Propositional logic}
{title2=$\bot$}
{wiki}

A contradiction is an assertion or collection of assumptions from which <logical falsity> follows. Deriving both a statement $P$ and its <logical negation> is a standard example. A contradiction under a temporary assumption can establish that assumption's negation; classical <proof by contradiction> additionally uses this to establish positive conclusions.

= Lindenbaum algebra
{parent=Propositional logic}
{c}

Identify classical propositional formulae when their equivalence is provable from a fixed theory. Their equivalence classes form the <Lindenbaum algebra>: meet and join are induced by conjunction and disjunction, complementation by negation, and the order by provable implication. Provable equivalence is preserved by each connective, so these operations are well-defined; the familiar classical propositional identities make this a <Boolean algebra>. A <homomorphism of lattices> to $\{0,1\}$ preserving zero and one also preserves complements and therefore specifies a truth valuation of the atomic propositions. All axioms of the fixed theory represent one. Separating the class of an unprovable formula from one consequently gives a countervaluation, proving the <completeness theorem for propositional logic>.

= Propositional theory
{parent=Propositional logic}

A <propositional theory> is a set of <propositional formulas>, sometimes identified with its deductive closure. It is a <consistent set of formulae> when contradiction is not derivable from it. The <completeness theorem for propositional logic> identifies consistency with existence of a satisfying <Boolean valuation>.

= Propositional type
{parent=Propositional theory}
{title2=$\Sigma$}

A <propositional type> is a collection of <propositional formulas> to be jointly realized by a <Boolean valuation>. A valuation realizes it when every member is true and omits it when some member is false. For propositional omission arguments, the collection need not itself be consistent.

= Extended omitting types theorem for propositional logic
{parent=Propositional type}

= Propositional omitting types theorem
{synonym}

If a consistent <propositional theory> locally omits every member of a countable family of <propositional types>, it has a <Boolean valuation> omitting them all. One may also impose any finite condition consistent with the theory. Successively extend the condition by a negated member of the next type; local omission preserves consistency. The <propositional compactness theorem> then supplies the valuation. Countability of the ambient language and decidability of consistency are not needed for this argument.

= Nonprincipal propositional type
{parent=Propositional type}

Relative to a consistent <propositional theory> $T$, a <nonprincipal propositional type> has no formula consistent with $T$ that implies every member modulo $T$. Equivalently, for each finite condition $\theta$ consistent with $T$, some member $\sigma$ leaves $T\cup\{\theta,\neg\sigma\}$ consistent. This is the local omission condition for the <extended omitting types theorem for propositional logic>.

= Propositional formula
{parent=Propositional logic}

A <propositional formula> is built from atomic propositions using <logical connectives>. Unlike a <first-order formula>, it has no quantifiers over individuals. A <Boolean valuation> interprets its atoms as truth values and evaluates its connectives recursively.

= Propositional variable elimination
{parent=Propositional formula}
{title2=$A\equiv(A[p:=\top]\wedge p)\vee(A[p:=\bot]\wedge\neg p)$}

The displayed identity follows by testing the two possible truth values of $p$. Existentially forgetting $p$ gives $A[p:=\top]\vee A[p:=\bot]$. Repeating this operation produces a <propositional formula> on a specified remaining vocabulary. Logical truth constants are needed if that vocabulary is empty.

= Propositional interpolation
{parent=Propositional variable elimination}
{title2=$A\vdash C\vdash B$}

If $A\vdash B$, eliminate the letters private to $A$ by
$$
C=\bigvee_{\varepsilon:\operatorname{Var}(A)\setminus\operatorname{Var}(B)\to\{0,1\}}A[\varepsilon].
$$
Every assignment satisfying $A$ satisfies one disjunct of $C$. If an assignment satisfies $C$, change only the eliminated letters to its witnessing assignment. The modified assignment satisfies $A$ and hence $B$, whose letters were unchanged. Thus $A\models C\models B$, and the <propositional completeness theorem> gives the two derivations. The interpolant uses only common letters; an empty common vocabulary is handled by logical truth constants.

= Deduction theorem for propositional logic
{parent=Propositional logic}
{title2=$S\cup\{\phi\}\vdash\psi\iff S\vdash\phi\Rightarrow\psi$}

An implication is derivable from a theory precisely when its conclusion is derivable after adjoining its antecedent. Together with classical contradiction rules, this identifies failure to prove a proposition with consistency after adjoining its negation.

= Classical propositional logic
{parent=Propositional logic}

Classical propositional logic interprets propositions as true or false and validates principles such as the <law of excluded middle> and <double-negation elimination>.

= Law of excluded middle
{title2=$A\vee\neg A$}
{parent=Classical propositional logic}
{wiki}

The law of excluded middle asserts that every proposition $A$ satisfies $A\vee\neg A$.

= Double-negation elimination
{title2=$\neg\neg A\to A$}
{parent=Classical propositional logic}

Double-negation elimination is the inference from $\neg\neg A$ to $A$.

= Boolean valuation
{parent=Propositional logic}
{c}

A Boolean valuation assigns each primitive proposition one of the truth values zero and one and extends recursively through the logical connectives.

= Truth table
{parent=Boolean valuation}
{wiki}

A truth table lists a <propositional formula>'s value for every <Boolean valuation> of its distinct primitive <propositions>. With $k$ primitive <propositions>, there are $2^k$ rows: each independently receives T or F. Evaluate compound columns from their subexpressions using the classical rules for <logical connectives>. This is exhaustive checking, so a formula true on every row is valid in <classical propositional logic>.

For a computed example over the <natural numbers>, let $A(n)$ mean that $n$ is an <even number> and $B(n)$ mean $n>2$. The choices $n=4,2,3,1$ realize respectively $(A,B)=(\mathrm T,\mathrm T),(\mathrm T,\mathrm F),(\mathrm F,\mathrm T),(\mathrm F,\mathrm F)$: all four possible inputs.

\Image[logical-implication-truth-table.png]
{title=Classical <logical implication>, its <contrapositive>, and its <converse implication> on all four inputs}

The <logical implication> $A\to B$ and its <contrapositive> agree on every row; the <converse implication> $B\to A$ differs on two rows. A <truth table> therefore checks equivalence or supplies a <counterexample>. Classical truth conditions are given in \a[https://forallx.openlogicproject.org/html/Ch31.html][forall x: Calgary, section 31.2]{ref}. These two-valued <truth tables> do not characterize validity in <intuitionistic propositional logic>.

A proposition is a statement to which a logical truth value can be assigned.

= Logical connective
{parent=Proposition}
{wiki}

A logical connective forms a compound proposition from one or more propositions.

= Logical implication
{title2=$\to$}
{parent=Logical connective}

Logical implication forms the <proposition> $A\to B$, read “if $A$, then $B$”. In <classical propositional logic>, it is false exactly when $A$ is true and $B$ is false. Equivalently, it has the same <truth table> as $\neg A\vee B$, using <logical negation> and <logical disjunction>. A false hypothesis makes the <logical implication> true regardless of its conclusion; thus it asserts neither the hypothesis nor a causal connection.

For example, for every <natural number> $n$, <divisibility> by four implies that $n$ is an <even number>: $n=4k=2(2k)$. At $n=8$, both statements are true. At $n=6$, the hypothesis is false and the conclusion true; at $n=3$, both are false. The only forbidden case is a multiple of four that is not an <even number>.

In <intuitionistic propositional logic>, a proof of $A\to B$ consists of a construction transforming any proof of $A$ into a proof of $B$. The <implication introduction rule> proves $B$ under a temporary assumption $A$; the <modus ponens rule> applies the result when $A$ is available. The classical identification with $\neg A\vee B$ is not generally intuitionistically valid.

= Contrapositive
{parent=Logical implication}
{title2=$\neg B\to\neg A$}

The contrapositive of a <logical implication> $A\to B$ reverses the direction and applies <logical negation> to both sides: $\neg B\to\neg A$. In <classical propositional logic> they are equivalent, so either can be proved in place of the other. The <contrapositive> of “<divisibility> by four implies being an <even number>” says that a number that is not an <even number> cannot be divisible by four.

In <intuitionistic propositional logic>, $A\to B$ still implies its <contrapositive>: assumptions $A$ and $\neg B$ would yield $B$ and hence a <contradiction>. The reverse direction is not generally valid. From $\neg B\to\neg A$ and $A$, one obtains only $\neg\neg B$; obtaining $B$ additionally requires <double-negation elimination>.

= Converse implication
{parent=Logical implication}
{title2=$B\to A$}

= Converse of an implication
{synonym}

The converse implication of $A\to B$ is $B\to A$. It exchanges hypothesis and conclusion without <logical negation>. It need not follow from the original <logical implication>: $n=2$ is an <even number> but is not divisible by four. Thus “being an <even number> implies <divisibility> by four” is false, although the original implication holds for all <natural numbers>. Proving both directions establishes an “if and only if” statement; proving a <contrapositive> establishes the original implication classically, without establishing its <converse implication>.

= Logical conjunction
{title2=$\wedge$}

The conjunction $A\wedge B$ holds when both $A$ and $B$ hold.

= Logical disjunction
{title2=$\vee$}
{parent=Logical connective}
{wiki}

The disjunction $A\vee B$ holds when at least one of $A$ and $B$ holds. Constructively, a proof records which disjunct holds and provides its proof.

= Logical truth

Logical truth is the nullary connective that is always true.

= Logical falsity

= Natural deduction
{parent=Propositional logic}
{wiki}

Natural deduction presents logic through introduction and elimination rules for each logical connective.

= Identity weakening rule
{parent=Natural deduction}
{title2=$\dfrac{C\quad D}{C}$}

The rule with premises $C,D$ and conclusion $C$ retains the first derivation while allowing an unused second assumption. Under the <Curry-Howard correspondence>, the output term is the first premise term, with the enlarged context. This allows a <lambda abstraction> whose bound variable does not occur in its body.

= Implication elimination rule
{parent=Natural deduction}
{title2=$\to E$}

= Modus ponens rule
{synonym}

From $A\to B$ and $A$, implication elimination derives $B$. Under the <Curry-Howard correspondence>, this is application: from <lambda terms> $f:A\to B$ and $a:A$ form $f\,a:B$.

= Implication introduction rule
{parent=Natural deduction}
{title2=$\to I$}

The implication introduction rule discharges a temporary assumption $A$ from a derivation of $B$, producing $A\to B$. Under the <Curry-Howard correspondence>, a body $t:B$ under $a:A$ gives the <lambda abstraction> $\lambda a^A.t:A\to B$. Unused assumptions may also be discharged.

= Lattice
{parent=Intuitionistic propositional logic}
{wiki=Lattice_(order)}

A lattice is a partially ordered set in which every pair has a greatest lower bound, called its meet, and a least upper bound, called its join.

= Join and meet
{parent=Lattice}
{wiki}

In a <lattice>, joins and meets are the pairwise <least upper bounds> and <greatest lower bounds>.

= Join in a lattice
{parent=Join and meet}
{title2=$a\vee b$}

The join of two elements of a <lattice> is their <least upper bound>. In a <lattice> of closed <set families>, it is the closure of their union.

= Meet in a lattice
{parent=Join and meet}
{title2=$a\wedge b$}

The meet of two elements of a <lattice> is their <greatest lower bound>. For <set families> ordered by inclusion, it is their intersection whenever that intersection lies in the specified <lattice>.

= Homomorphism of lattices
{parent=Lattice}

A homomorphism between <lattices> preserves binary joins and meets: $f(x\vee y)=f(x)\vee f(y)$ and $f(x\wedge y)=f(x)\wedge f(y)$. It is order preserving, since $x\leq y$ implies $x=x\wedge y$ and hence $f(x)=f(x)\wedge f(y)$. For a map into the two-element lattice, the inverse image of zero is a <lattice ideal>, and meet preservation says this ideal is <prime>: if a meet belongs to it, at least one factor does. Conversely the indicator of the complement of a <prime> <lattice ideal> preserves both operations. Preservation of distinguished top and bottom elements is an additional requirement for homomorphisms of bounded lattices.

= Complete lattice
{parent=Lattice}
{wiki}

A complete lattice is a <partially ordered set> in which every subset has a meet and a join, including the empty subset.

= Knaster–Tarski fixed-point theorem
{c}
{parent=Complete lattice}
{wiki}

Every <order-preserving function> from a <complete lattice> to itself has a least and a greatest fixed point. More strongly, its set of fixed points is itself a complete lattice.

= Distributive lattice
{parent=Lattice}
{wiki}

A distributive lattice is a <lattice> in which meet distributes over join and, equivalently, join distributes over meet.

= Distributive law for lattices
{parent=Distributive lattice}

In a <distributive lattice>,
$$
a\wedge(b\vee c)=(a\wedge b)\vee(a\wedge c),
\qquad
a\vee(b\wedge c)=(a\vee b)\wedge(a\vee c).
$$

= Boolean algebra
{parent=Distributive lattice}
{wiki}

A Boolean algebra is a bounded <distributive lattice> in which every element has a complement. Its meet, join, and complement abstract conjunction, disjunction, and negation.

= Boolean prime ideal theorem
{c}
{parent=Boolean algebra}
{wiki}

Every proper <lattice ideal> of a <Boolean algebra> extends to a prime <lattice ideal>. More generally, an ideal disjoint from a <lattice filter> extends to a prime ideal still disjoint from that filter. This Boolean case of the <Stone prime filter theorem> is equivalent to the <ultrafilter lemma> over <Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory> and is weaker than the <axiom of choice>.

= Ideal frame of a Boolean algebra
{parent=Boolean algebra}
{title2=$\operatorname{Idl}(B)$}

The <lattice ideals> of a <Boolean algebra> form a <frame> under inclusion. Meets are intersections and joins are ideals generated by unions. A complemented ideal is principal: if $I\vee J=B$ and $I\cap J=\{0\}$, choose $b\in I,c\in J$ with $b\vee c=1$; then $c=\neg b$ and $I=\mathord\downarrow b$. This ideal frame defines an <extremally disconnected locale> exactly when $B$ is complete.

= Complete Boolean algebra
{parent=Boolean algebra}
{wiki}

A Boolean algebra in which every set-indexed family has a join and a meet. <Double-negation Boolean algebra> is an intuitionistic example, with joins formed by regularizing unions. Completeness makes truth values for quantifiers possible in a <Boolean-valued inner universe over intuitionistic set theory>.

= Double-negation Boolean algebra
{parent=Boolean algebra}
{title2=$B=\{p\in\mathcal P(1):\neg\neg p=p\}$}

Inside <IZF>, truth values are subsets of a singleton. Their double-negation-stable elements form a complete Boolean algebra: arbitrary meets are intersections and arbitrary joins are double-negation closures of unions. Negation gives Boolean complement. The join of $p$ and its complement is top although their ordinary union need not be top. The closure here is taken inside the fixed singleton; it does not assume that the class of all objects double-negatively belonging to an arbitrary set is itself a set.

= Lattice filter
{parent=Distributive lattice}

A lattice filter is a nonempty upward-closed subset closed under finite meets. A proper filter omits the bottom element.

A <lattice filter> is a <filter in an ordered set> when that order is a lattice; directedness and upward closure then imply closure under finite meets.

= Prime filter of a distributive lattice
{parent=Lattice filter}

A prime filter is a proper <lattice filter> $P$ such that $a\vee b\in P$ implies $a\in P$ or $b\in P$.

= Stone prime filter theorem
{c}
{parent=Prime filter of a distributive lattice}

If a <lattice filter> $F$ and <lattice ideal> $I$ in a <distributive lattice> are disjoint, there is a <prime filter of a distributive lattice> containing $F$ and disjoint from $I$.

The <Boolean prime ideal theorem> is the Boolean-algebra version; distributivity gives this corresponding filter-extension statement.

= Lattice ideal
{parent=Distributive lattice}

A lattice ideal is a nonempty downward-closed subset closed under finite joins. It is the order-dual notion to a <lattice filter>.

= Prime lattice ideal
{parent=Lattice ideal}

A proper <lattice ideal> $I$ is prime if $x\wedge y\in I$ implies $x\in I$ or $y\in I$. Its complement is a <prime filter of a distributive lattice> when the lattice is distributive: downward closure of the ideal makes the complement upward closed, primeness makes it meet-closed, and join closure makes that filter prime. Equivalently, its complement indicator is a <homomorphism of lattices> to the two-element lattice. Given $b\nleq a$ in a distributive lattice, maximize an ideal containing $a$ and avoiding $b$ using <Zorn lemma>. If $x,y$ lie outside it, the two enlarged ideals contain $b$, so $b\leq i\vee x$ and $b\leq j\vee y$ for some $i,j\in I$. Distributivity shows that $x\wedge y\in I$ would force $b\in I$, a contradiction. Thus this maximal avoiding ideal is prime and separates the two specified elements.

= Priestley duality
{c}
{parent=Distributive lattice}
{wiki}

Priestley duality is a dual equivalence between bounded <distributive lattice>[distributive lattices] and compact totally order-disconnected ordered topological spaces.

= Priestley dual space
{c}
{parent=Priestley duality}

The Priestley dual space $\widehat L$ of a bounded <distributive lattice> $L$ consists of its <prime filter of a distributive lattice>[prime filters], ordered by inclusion and topologized by the sets $a^*=\{P:a\in P\}$ and their complements.

This construction yields a <Priestley space>: a compact ordered topological space in which suitable clopen upward-closed sets separate distinct comparable-order failures.

= Clopen up-set
{parent=Priestley dual space}

A clopen up-set is both <clopen set>[clopen] in the topology and upward closed in the order. The clopen up-sets of a Priestley space form a <distributive lattice> under intersection and union.

= Stone map of a distributive lattice
{c}
{parent=Priestley dual space}

The Stone map sends an element $a$ of a <distributive lattice> to the <clopen up-set> $a^*=\{P:a\in P\}$ of its <Priestley dual space>. The <Stone prime filter theorem> makes this lattice homomorphism injective.

= Heyting algebra
{c}
{parent=Intuitionistic propositional logic}
{wiki}

A Heyting algebra is a bounded distributive lattice with an implication $a\Rightarrow b$ characterized by $x\leq(a\Rightarrow b)$ exactly when $x\wedge a\leq b$.

= Heyting implication
{c}
{parent=Heyting algebra}
{title2=$a\to b$}

In a <Heyting algebra>, $a\to b$ is the greatest $x$ with $x\wedge a\leq b$. Thus $x\leq a\to b$ if and only if $x\wedge a\leq b$. Every <frame> has this operation, obtained by taking the join of all such $x$. The <open nucleus> attached to $a$ is $x\mapsto a\to x$.

= Pseudocomplement
{parent=Heyting implication}
{title2=$\neg a=a\to0$}

The pseudocomplement of $a$ is the greatest element disjoint from $a$. It need not satisfy $a\vee\neg a=1$. In a <frame>, double pseudocomplementation defines the <Booleanization of a locale>; its fixed elements are <regular open elements>.

= Heyting operations on subsheaves
{c}
{parent=Heyting algebra}
{title2=$\operatorname{Sub}_{\operatorname{Sh}(\mathcal C,J)}(B)$}

Meets of subsheaves are pointwise intersections. Joins are J-closures of pointwise unions: a section lies in the join when its restrictions locally belong to some member, with the member allowed to vary. Implication consists of sections every restriction of which belongs to $D$ whenever it belongs to $A$; this gives $C\leq(A\Rightarrow D)$ exactly when $C\cap A\leq D$. Negation is implication into the initial <subobject>. Empty covers mean that the initial sheaf need not be pointwise empty.

= Regular element of a Heyting algebra
{parent=Heyting algebra}

= Regular open element
{synonym}

An element $a$ of a Heyting algebra is regular when $\neg\neg a=a$. The regular elements form its Booleanization.

= Distributivity of a Heyting algebra
{parent=Heyting algebra}

In a <Heyting algebra>, meet with a fixed element is left adjoint to Heyting implication, so it preserves joins:
$$
a\wedge(b\vee c)=(a\wedge b)\vee(a\wedge c).
$$
The dual distributive law follows from this one by the lattice absorption laws.

= Implicational Curry-Howard correspondence
{c}
{parent=Intuitionistic propositional logic}

In the implicational fragments, assumptions correspond to typed variables, implication introduction to lambda abstraction, and implication elimination to function application. Typing derivations in the simply typed lambda calculus correspond inductively to natural-deduction proofs.

= Curry-Howard correspondence
{c}
{parent=Intuitionistic propositional logic}
{wiki}

The Curry-Howard correspondence identifies propositions with types and proofs with typed programs; implication corresponds to a function type, conjunction to a product type, and falsity to the empty type.

= Kripke model for intuitionistic propositional logic

A Kripke model is a poset of worlds with a persistent valuation of atoms. Conjunction and disjunction are forced pointwise, while $w\Vdash\alpha\to\beta$ exactly when every $v\geq w$ that forces $\alpha$ also forces $\beta$.

The forcing relation is the intuitionistic instance of <Kripke semantics>; persistence and the order on worlds determine its implication clause.

= Kripke forcing relation
{c}
{title2=$\Vdash$}
{parent=Kripke model for intuitionistic propositional logic}

The Kripke forcing relation $w\Vdash A$ records that formula $A$ holds at world $w$. For intuitionistic implication, $w\Vdash A\to B$ exactly when every extension of $w$ that forces $A$ also forces $B$.

= Persistence of intuitionistic Kripke forcing
{parent=Kripke forcing relation}

If $w\leq v$ and $w\Vdash A$, then $v\Vdash A$. The proof is by structural induction on $A$.

= Kripke countermodel
{c}
{parent=Kripke forcing relation}

A Kripke countermodel to a formula $A$ is a <Kripke model for intuitionistic propositional logic> containing a world $w$ such that $w\nVdash A$.

= Kripke completeness theorem for intuitionistic propositional logic
{c}
{parent=Kripke model for intuitionistic propositional logic}

A proposition is derivable in intuitionistic propositional logic exactly when it is forced at every world of every intuitionistic Kripke model.

= Filtration of a Kripke model
{parent=Kripke model for intuitionistic propositional logic}

Filtration identifies worlds that force the same formulas in a fixed finite subformula-closed set. With order induced by inclusion of these finite theories, the quotient preserves forcing of every retained formula and has at most $2^n$ worlds for $n$ formulas.

= Unravelling of a Kripke model
{parent=Kripke model for intuitionistic propositional logic}

The unravelling of a rooted Kripke model has finite increasing paths from the root as worlds, ordered by extension and labelled by their endpoints. It is tree-like and preserves forcing.

= Finite model property of intuitionistic propositional logic
{c}
{parent=Kripke model for intuitionistic propositional logic}

Every intuitionistically underivable proposition has a finite rooted Kripke countermodel. Filtration and witness pruning bound the model in terms of the number of subformulas.

= Disjunction property of intuitionistic propositional logic
{parent=Intuitionistic propositional logic}

The disjunction property says that if $\vdash_{\mathrm{IPC}}A\vee B$, then $\vdash_{\mathrm{IPC}}A$ or $\vdash_{\mathrm{IPC}}B$.

This is the disjunction part of <disjunction and existence properties>, specialized to <intuitionistic propositional logic>.

= Implication-free fragment of intuitionistic propositional logic
{title2=$\mathrm{IPC}\setminus\{\to\}$}
{parent=Intuitionistic propositional logic}

The implication-free fragment uses only conjunction, disjunction, truth, and falsity, with their intuitionistic natural-deduction rules.

= Completeness of implication-free intuitionistic propositional logic for distributive lattices
{parent=Implication-free fragment of intuitionistic propositional logic}

An implication-free formula is provable exactly when every valuation into every bounded <distributive lattice> assigns it the top element.

= Implicational fragment of intuitionistic propositional logic
{title2=$\mathrm{IPC}(\to)$}
{parent=Intuitionistic propositional logic}

The implicational fragment of intuitionistic propositional logic contains formulas built from atoms using only <logical implication>, with only implication introduction, implication elimination, and assumptions as proof rules.

= Canonical Kripke model for implicational intuitionistic logic
{parent=Implicational fragment of intuitionistic propositional logic}

The canonical worlds are deductively closed implicational theories ordered by inclusion. A world forces an atom exactly when the atom belongs to that theory. The truth lemma says that a world forces an implicational formula exactly when the formula belongs to the theory.

= Kripke completeness of implicational intuitionistic logic
{parent=Implicational fragment of intuitionistic propositional logic}

For implicational formulas $\Gamma$ and $\varphi$,
$$
\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm{IPC}(\to)}\varphi
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\Gamma\models_{\mathrm{Kripke}}\varphi.
$$
The reverse implication follows from the <canonical Kripke model for implicational intuitionistic logic> and its truth lemma.

= Conservativity of intuitionistic propositional logic over its implicational fragment
{parent=Implicational fragment of intuitionistic propositional logic}

If $\Gamma$ and $\varphi$ are implicational and $\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm{IPC}}\varphi$, then $\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm{IPC}(\to)}\varphi$. Soundness for Kripke semantics followed by <Kripke completeness of implicational intuitionistic logic> proves the claim.

= Soundness theorem for propositional logic
{parent=Propositional logic}

If $\Gamma\vdash t$, every valuation that satisfies all formulae in $\Gamma$ also satisfies $t$.

This is <soundness> for the propositional proof system and valuation semantics.

= Completeness theorem for propositional logic
{parent=Propositional logic}

= Propositional completeness theorem
{synonym}

For a set of formulae $\Gamma$ and a formula $\varphi$,
$$
\Gamma\models\varphi\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Gamma\vdash\varphi.
$$
Together with the <soundness theorem for propositional logic>, this says that semantic consequence and syntactic provability coincide. Equivalently, every <consistent set of formulae> has a satisfying Boolean valuation.

= Maximal consistent set truth lemma
{parent=Completeness theorem for propositional logic}

Assign a primitive proposition true exactly when it belongs to a maximal consistent set. An induction on formulas proves that truth agrees with membership: maximal consistency gives the negation dichotomy, and deductive closure gives the implication truth table. This builds a model of every consistent propositional theory.

= Maximum consistent propositional theory gives a model
{parent=Maximal consistent set truth lemma}

Consistency is preserved under unions of chains because proofs use finitely many assumptions. A maximal consistent extension then gives a valuation through its truth lemma, proving propositional completeness.

= Uncountable-language proof of propositional completeness
{parent=Completeness theorem for propositional logic}

When the primitive propositions are not countable, replace sequential enumeration of the formulae by <Zorn lemma>. The union of a chain of consistent extensions is consistent because every formal proof is finite, so every consistent theory extends to a <maximal consistent set in propositional logic>. Declaring an atom true exactly when it belongs to that maximal set and proving the truth lemma by structural induction produces a model.

= Decidability theorem for propositional logic
{parent=Propositional logic}

There is an algorithm deciding whether any finite propositional formula is a theorem. A formula contains only finitely many atoms, so a finite <truth table> decides whether it is valid; the <completeness theorem for propositional logic> identifies validity with provability.

The finite valuation space of <propositional logic> gives the decision procedure; this theorem is a property of that logic rather than an alternative name for it.

= Deductively closed set of formulae
{title2=$S=\operatorname{Cn}(S)$}
{parent=Propositional logic}

A set $S$ of formulae is deductively closed when
$$
S\vdash\varphi\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\varphi\in S.
$$
Equivalently, $S$ equals its deductive closure $\operatorname{Cn}(S)$.

= Consistent set of formulae
{parent=Propositional logic}

A set $S$ is syntactically consistent when $S\not\vdash\bot$. Equivalently in classical logic, there is no formula $\varphi$ for which both $S\vdash\varphi$ and $S\vdash\neg\varphi$.

= Finitary set of formulae
{parent=Propositional logic}

A set $S$ is finitary when it is deductively equivalent to some finite set $F$: both $S\vdash F$ and $F\vdash S$.

= Increasing union of deductively closed sets
{parent=Propositional logic}

If
$$
T_0\subsetneq T_1\subsetneq T_2\subsetneq\cdots
$$
are deductively closed, then each $T_i$ is consistent and their union is consistent and deductively closed. The union is not finitary. Each conclusion follows from the <finite character of formal proofs>.

= Maximal consistent set in propositional logic
{parent=Propositional logic}

A consistent set $T$ is maximal consistent when no proper extension is consistent. It is deductively closed and decides every formula: exactly one of $t$ and $\neg t$ belongs to $T$. Therefore $v(t)=1$ exactly when $t\in T$ defines a model of $T$.

= Propositional compactness theorem
{parent=Propositional logic}

A set of propositions has a model exactly when every finite subset has a model. Syntactically, a contradiction uses only finitely many assumptions, and every consistent set extends by <Zorn lemma> to a <maximal consistent set in propositional logic>.

= Compactness obstruction to expressing totality over an infinite codomain
{parent=Propositional compactness theorem}

With atoms $p_{x,y}$, finitary propositions can enforce that the true pairs form an injective partial function. If the codomain is infinite, they cannot enforce that every $x$ has a value: adjoining every $\neg p_{x_0,y}$ remains finitely satisfiable, so compactness produces a model omitting $x_0$.

= First-order logic
{parent=Mathematical logic}
{wiki}

First-order logic permits quantification over elements of a structure while its nonlogical symbols describe operations, constants, and relations.

= Finite-variable logic
{parent=First-order logic}
{title2=$L^k$}

The fragment $L^k$ permits at most $k$ distinct variable names, but allows bound names to be reused. It can contain formulas of arbitrarily large quantifier depth. On a linear order, alternating two variable names can express an increasing chain of any prescribed finite length.

= Least representatives of finite equivalence classes
{parent=Finite-variable logic}

On a finite linearly ordered structure with an equivalence relation, the formula $\forall y(y<x\to\neg E(y,x))$ selects exactly the least element of each equivalence class. Variable-recycling counting therefore expresses any prescribed lower bound on the number of classes. The order and equivalence-relation axioms use three variable names. Finiteness matters: densely interleaved infinite classes need not have least elements.

= Variable-recycling count on a linear order
{parent=Finite-variable logic}

For a formula $\phi(x)$ in $L^k$, $k\ge2$, with only $x$ free, define $C_1^x=\phi(x)$ and $C_{m+1}^x=\phi(x)\land\exists y(x<y\land C_m^y)$. Obtain the superscript-$y$ formula by globally interchanging the two variable names in the superscript-$x$ version. Then $\exists x\,C_m^x$ counts at least $m$ distinct satisfying elements without introducing any further variable names. The recursion works on finite and infinite linear orders.

= Geometric logic
{parent=First-order logic}

The fragment uses finite conjunction, set-indexed disjunction and existential quantification, with equality and atomic relations. It is preserved under <inverse image functors of geometric morphisms>. The universal closure of an axiom is expressed by its variable context, not an unrestricted universal-quantifier constructor.

= Geometric theory
{parent=Geometric logic}
{title2=$\mathbb T=\{\phi\vdash_{\vec x}\psi\}$}

A set of sequents between <geometric formulas> over a many-sorted signature. Its <geometric syntactic category> and <geometric syntactic topology> yield its <classifying topos>. A model satisfies a sequent when the interpreted antecedent <subobject> is contained in the consequent in the indicated context.

= Propositional geometric theory
{parent=Geometric theory}

A geometric theory with only nullary relation symbols has formulas built from primitive propositions by finite conjunctions and set-indexed disjunctions. Its sequents present a <frame>; its <locale> carries a canonical model in its <sheaf topos>, where a proposition is interpreted as the corresponding open subterminal sheaf.

= Prime-filter locale of a commutative ring
{parent=Propositional geometric theory}
{title2=$X_R$}

For a <commutative ring> $R$, this <locale> is presented by basic opens $U_r$ recording membership in a multiplicatively closed prime filter. Products give intersections. A ring map induces a <locale> map in the opposite direction. The basic opens satisfy $U_r\vee U_s=1$ exactly when $(r,s)=R$, making unit-ideal calculations a substitute for choosing prime ideals.

= Geometric quotient theory
{parent=Geometric theory}
{title2=$\mathbb T'=\mathbb T+\text{additional geometric axioms}$}

A geometric quotient adds geometric sequents over the same signature. Quotients are compared modulo provable equivalence. In a syntactic <site> the extra sequents become additional covers, giving a <subtopos> of the original <classifying topos>. Stronger axioms correspond to a larger <site> topology and a smaller <subtopos>.

= Geometric syntactic category
{parent=Geometric theory}
{title2=$\mathcal C_{\mathbb T}$}

Objects are <geometric formulas> in context modulo provable equivalence. Morphisms are provably total, single-valued geometric relations; composition is existential conjunction. Conjunction and equality give <finite limits>. Interpreting formulas in a model gives a finite-limit-preserving <functor>, with the additional continuity condition supplied by the <geometric syntactic topology>.

= Geometric syntactic topology
{parent=Geometric syntactic category}
{title2=$J_{\mathbb T}$}

A family of definable arrows covers a formula when the theory proves that their images jointly exhaust that formula. Such covers encode disjunction and existential quantification. The resulting <site> presents the <classifying topos> of the <geometric theory>. Adding geometric axioms adds covering sieves, yielding the <duality between geometric quotients and subtoposes>.

= Geometric formula
{parent=Geometric logic}

A <geometric formula> is built from atoms by finite conjunction, arbitrary small disjunction and existential quantification. Empty conjunction and disjunction give truth and falsity. In a <Grothendieck topos>, these constructions use <finite limits>, unions and images. Formula contexts have finitely many variables.

= Regular logic
{parent=First-order logic}

Regular logic has atomic formulas and equality, truth, finite conjunction and unrestricted existential quantification. Its categorical semantics uses <regular categories>: conjunction is intersection and existential quantification is image. Disjunction and falsity are not added as formula constructors in the regular fragment.

= Regular theory
{parent=Regular logic}
{title2=$\mathbb T$}

A regular theory has axioms that are sequents between <regular formulas>. It has a <regular syntactic category> and a classifying topos of sheaves for the <regular coverage>. Its regular formulas enjoy the <disjunction property of regular theories> when a finite disjunction is allowed as an outer coherent conclusion.

= Chase completeness for regular logic
{parent=Regular theory}

Classically, a <regular formula> sequent that is not provable has a set-valued countermodel, with empty sorts allowed. Start with the finite diagram of its antecedent, successively adjoin witnesses to the existential consequences of each axiom, and identify elements when equality axioms require it. Every new fact about the original parameters has a finite derivation, so the nonderivable consequent remains false. The filtered union of this witness construction is a model. The conservativity property of this chase construction is studied in https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.03851[Forssell and Lumsdaine's paper].

= Disjunction property of regular theories
{parent=Regular theory}
{title2=$\mathbb T\vdash\phi\Rightarrow\bigvee_i\psi_i\ \Longrightarrow\ \exists i\ \mathbb T\vdash\phi\Rightarrow\psi_i$}

If a regular theory proves an outer coherent sequent $\phi\vdash\bigvee_{i=1}^n\psi_i$ with all formulas regular, it proves $\phi\vdash\psi_i$ for some $i$. In the classifying topos, the corresponding subobjects cover the representable associated with $\phi$. Irreducibility of that representable and full faithfulness of the syntactic Yoneda embedding produce the desired derivation.

= Regular syntactic category
{parent=Regular theory}
{title2=$\mathcal C_{\mathbb T}$}

The regular syntactic category has regular formulas-in-context as objects and provably functional regular relations as arrows. It has finite limits and stable image factorizations, with existential quantification supplying images. The <regular coverage> on this category presents the classifying topos of the theory.

= Regular formula
{parent=Regular logic}

A regular formula is built using the constructors of <regular logic>, with a finite variable context. The image of a definable relation interprets an existential quantifier. Regular formulas remain regular after substitution, conjunction and existential quantification.

= Cartesian theory
{parent=First-order logic}
{c}
{title2=$\mathbb T$}

A Cartesian theory uses a many-sorted <first-order signature> and sequents between <Cartesian formulas>. The fragment contains equality, atomic relations, truth, finite conjunction and existential quantification with provably unique witnesses. It has a <Cartesian syntactic category> with finite limits, whose <finite-limit-preserving functors> classify its internal models. Arbitrary existential quantification, disjunction and falsity are not added as finite-limit constructors.

= Horn theory
{c}
{parent=Cartesian theory}

In the positive finite-limit convention, a <Horn theory> uses atomic finite-conjunction sequents; uniquely witnessed existential extensions remain Cartesian. Its models are the finite-limit-preserving <functors> out of its <Cartesian syntactic category>. This convention does not insert arbitrary existential witnesses or falsity as Cartesian constructors. Its classifier is the <presheaf classifier of a Horn theory>.

= Presheaf classifier of a Horn theory
{parent=Horn theory}
{title2=$\mathbf{Set}[\mathbb T]\simeq[(\mathcal C_{\mathbb T}^{\mathrm{cart}})^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbf{Set}]$}

Internal Horn models are finite-limit-preserving <functors> out of the <Cartesian syntactic category>. Since that category has <finite limits>, these are exactly <flat functors>; the presheaf <Diaconescu equivalence for geometric morphisms> proves the classifying property. Equivalently the classifier is the covariant <functor> category on finitely presented set-based models.

= Cartesian syntactic category
{parent=Cartesian theory}
{c}
{title2=$\mathcal C_{\mathbb T}$}

Objects are Cartesian formulas-in-context modulo provable equivalence, and arrows are provably total single-valued Cartesian relations. Composition quantifies the unique intermediate tuple. Context concatenation, conjunction and equality give finite limits. Its canonical model interprets a formula as its own context subobject, so factorization of antecedent through consequent is exactly sequent derivability.

= Completeness of Cartesian theories
{parent=Cartesian syntactic category}

If a Cartesian sequent fails to factor its antecedent subobject $A\hookrightarrow C$ through its consequent, the covariant <representable functor> $\mathcal C_{\mathbb T}(A,-)$ is a set-valued countermodel. It preserves finite limits, and the element named by the antecedent inclusion belongs to the antecedent but cannot belong to the consequent. Hence validity in every set model implies Cartesian derivability.

= Cartesian formula
{parent=Cartesian theory}
{c}

A Cartesian formula is built from atoms and equality using truth, finite conjunction and uniquely witnessed existential quantification. Uniqueness is proved relative to the theory before admitting the quantifier. In a finite-limit category, such a quantified relation projects monomorphically into the remaining context, so its semantics needs no arbitrary image operation.

= Coherent logic
{parent=First-order logic}

Coherent logic uses atomic formulas and equality, finite <logical conjunctions> and <logical disjunctions>, truth, falsity and <existential quantification>. Sequents are universally interpreted in a finite variable context. Its categorical semantics is provided by <coherent categories>, where conjunction is intersection, disjunction is finite union and existential quantification is image.

= Frobenius rule in coherent logic
{parent=Coherent logic}
{c}
{title2=$\theta\wedge\exists y\,\phi\dashv\vdash\exists y(\theta\wedge\phi)$}

When $y$ is not free in $\theta$, the coherent quantifier obeys $\theta\wedge\exists y\,\phi\dashv\vdash\exists y(\theta\wedge\phi)$. Categorically this is the interaction of existential images with pullback, ensuring stable image factorizations in a <coherent category>.

= Coherent theory
{parent=Coherent logic}
{title2=$\mathbb T$}

A coherent theory is a <first-order signature> with a set of <coherent sequents> as axioms. It has a <coherent syntactic category> containing a <conservative syntactic model>. Theories of groups, rings and integral domains admit coherent axiomatizations.

= Coherent theory of local rings
{parent=Coherent theory}

Use the signature of unital <commutative rings>, its equational axioms, and the <coherent sequents> $0=1\vdash\bot$ and $\top\vdash_x\operatorname{Inv}(x)\vee\operatorname{Inv}(1-x)$, where $\operatorname{Inv}(x)$ abbreviates $\exists y\,(xy=1)$. In a set-based model the nonunits form its unique <maximal ideal>; this is the usual nonzero <local ring>. In a <topos> the disjunction expresses local existence of a unit witness. Its <classifying topos> is defined by the <Zariski coverage on finitely presented rings>.

= Classical completeness of coherent theories
{parent=Coherent theory}

A coherent sequent true in all set models of a coherent theory is coherently derivable. A generic underivable sequent remains false after the <Boolean cover by a generic quasi-closed subtopos>. Slice over its nonzero Boolean counterexample to show classical consistency of the theory with a named tuple satisfying the antecedent and negated consequent. The <Henkin construction> then gives a set-valued countermodel. This argument does not assume that every Boolean topos has points.

= Coherent syntactic category
{parent=Coherent theory}
{title2=$\mathcal C_{\mathbb T}$}

Objects are <coherent formulas> in context and arrows are provably total, single-valued relations, modulo provable equivalence. Composition existentially eliminates the intermediate tuple. Conjunction constructs finite limits, existential formulas construct images and disjunction constructs finite unions. It is a <coherent category>.

= Conservative syntactic model
{parent=Coherent syntactic category}

The canonical model interprets each sort as its truth-context object, each operation by its term graph and each relation by its atomic subobject. A coherent formula is interpreted by its own formula-in-context object. Consequently a <coherent sequent> holds in this model exactly when it is derivable in the theory.

= Coherent sequent
{parent=Coherent logic}
{title2=$\phi\vdash_{\vec x}\psi$}

A coherent sequent is an implication $\phi\vdash_{\vec x}\psi$ between <coherent formulas> in a common context. Its semantics is inclusion of the two interpreted subobjects of that context. The context is universally quantified outside the formulas.

= Coherent formula
{parent=Coherent logic}

A coherent formula is built from atomic relations and equality using $\top$, $\bot$, finite conjunction, finite disjunction and existential quantification. General negation and universal quantification are not constructors. Substitution of well-typed terms preserves coherent formulas.

= Logical equality
{parent=First-order logic}
{title2=$s=t$}

= First-order equality
{synonym}

In <first-order logic> with <logical equality>, $s=t$ asserts that the terms denote the same domain element. <Logical equality> is interpreted as identity rather than as an arbitrary binary predicate. Its <natural deduction> rules include reflexivity and substitution: equal terms can replace one another in a <first-order formula>, subject to <capture-avoiding substitution>. Double-negating <logical equality> atoms in a <Gödel-Gentzen negative translation> preserves these rules when the transported translated <first-order formula> is stable.

= Intuitionistic first-order logic
{parent=First-order logic}
{title2=$\vdash_{\mathrm{IL}}$}

<Intuitionistic first-order logic> has the usual introduction and elimination rules for logical connectives, quantifiers and <logical equality>, but no unrestricted <double-negation elimination> or <law of excluded middle>. Its proofs retain the witness and <logical disjunction> information that can be lost in <classical first-order logic>.

= Gödel-Gentzen negative translation
{parent=Intuitionistic first-order logic}
{c}
{title2=$\varphi^N$}

= Negative interpretation
{synonym}

= Negative translation
{synonym}

For an <atomic formula> $P$ (including <logical equality>), put $P^N=\neg\neg P$, and put $\bot^N=\bot$. Preserve <logical conjunction>, <logical implication> and <universal quantification> recursively, while setting
$$
(A\lor B)^N=\neg\neg(A^N\lor B^N),\qquad (\exists x\,A)^N=\neg\neg\exists x\,A^N.
$$
<Logical negation> is <logical implication> to <logical falsity>, so $(\neg A)^N=\neg A^N$. The displayed <logical disjunction> and existence clauses are intuitionistically equivalent to the usual negative forms $\neg(\neg A^N\land\neg B^N)$ and $\neg\forall x\,\neg A^N$. Every translated <first-order formula> is stable by <stability of a formula under double negation>. In classical logic the translation is equivalent to the original <first-order formula> by <mathematical induction> and <double-negation elimination>.

= Negative translation of a classical proof
{parent=Gödel-Gentzen negative translation}
{title2=$\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm{CL}}A\Rightarrow\Gamma^N\vdash_{\mathrm{IL}}A^N$}

The <Gödel-Gentzen negative translation> transforms $\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm{CL}}A$ into $\Gamma^N\vdash_{\mathrm{IL}}A^N$. Induct on a <natural deduction> derivation. The translated <logical conjunction>, <logical implication> and universal rules are ordinary intuitionistic rules. <Logical disjunction> and existential introduction add double <logical negation>; their elimination rules first derive the double <logical negation> of the stable translated conclusion, then use its stability. Classical <double-negation elimination> translates precisely to stability. If <logical equality> atoms are double-negated, transport a stable translated conclusion through a <logical equality> by contradiction and then remove the resulting double <logical negation>. Original nonlogical axioms must also be translated; the result does not say arbitrary untranslated classical <first-order theories> are intuitionistically valid.

= Stability of a formula under double negation
{parent=Intuitionistic first-order logic}
{title2=$\neg\neg A\to A$}

A <first-order formula> $A$ is stable when <intuitionistic first-order logic> proves $\neg\neg A\to A$. Double negations and <logical falsity> are stable. <Logical conjunction> of stable <first-order formulas> is stable; a <logical implication> is stable when its consequent is stable; and <universal quantification> preserves stability. For <logical implication>, from $\neg\neg(A\to B)$ and $A$, assuming $\neg B$ contradicts the first hypothesis, giving $\neg\neg B$ and then $B$. For universality, obtain $\neg\neg A(t)$ from $\neg\neg\forall x\,A(x)$ for arbitrary $t$, then use stability and generalize.

= Classical first-order logic
{parent=First-order logic}

<Classical first-order logic> has the quantifier and <logical equality> rules of <natural deduction> together with unrestricted <double-negation elimination>, or equivalently the <law of excluded middle>. It is interpreted in ordinary two-valued <first-order structures>.

= First-order formula
{parent=First-order logic}
{wiki}

A first-order formula is built from atomic formulas using logical connectives and quantifiers over the elements of a structure.

= Quantifier rank
{parent=First-order formula}
{title2=$\operatorname{qr}(\varphi)$}
{wiki}

The quantifier rank of a <first-order formula> is the maximum nesting depth of quantifiers. Atomic formulas have rank zero; negation preserves rank, binary Boolean connectives take the maximum, and either quantifier increases rank by one. It is not the total number of quantifiers.

= Rank-n equivalence of structures
{parent=Quantifier rank}
{title2=$\mathcal M\equiv_n\mathcal N$}

= Rank-n equivalent
{synonym}

Two <first-order structures> are rank-n equivalent if they agree on all sentences of <quantifier rank> at most $n$. The tuple version compares formulas at fixed tuples. For finite relational vocabulary this is equivalent to a Duplicator winning strategy in the $n$-round <Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game>.

= Primitive positive formula
{parent=First-order formula}
{title2=$\exists\bar y\,\bigwedge_j\theta_j$}

= Tame formula
{synonym}

A primitive positive formula is generated from <atomic formulas> using only <logical conjunction> and <existential quantification>. It can be put in the form $\exists\bar y\,\bigwedge_j\theta_j(\bar x,\bar y)$ with each $\theta_j$ atomic. The exam terminology tame formula refers to this fragment. Its <reduced product> transfer needs only filter intersection and upward closure, together with coordinatewise choices of witnesses.

= Existential quantification
{parent=First-order formula}
{title2=$\exists x\,\varphi$}
{wiki}

<Existential quantification> forms $\exists x\,\varphi(x,\mathbf y)$. In a <first-order structure>, it is true at an assignment to $\mathbf y$ when some domain element makes the instance true. In <natural deduction>, elimination uses a fresh variable for the hypothetical witness; that variable may not escape into the conclusion or the remaining undischarged assumptions.

Existence requires at least one witness, not exactly one. Over the <natural numbers> $\mathbb N=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$, $\exists m\,(m>5)$ is true: $m=6$ works, as do $7,8,\ldots$. In <intuitionistic first-order logic>, a direct proof supplies a witness together with a proof of the corresponding instance; merely rejecting nonexistence is generally weaker.

Placing <existential quantification> before <universal quantification> requires a single witness that works throughout:
$$
\exists m\in\mathbb N\,\forall n\in\mathbb N\;(m>n).
$$
This <first-order sentence> is false. For any proposed $m$, choose $n=m$: the required inequality becomes $m>m$. For example, $m=3$ works for $n=0,1,2$ but fails at $n=3$. No finite list of successful checks establishes <universal quantification> over the <natural numbers>.

= Existential formula
{parent=Existential quantification}
{title2=$\exists\mathbf y\,\theta(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)$}

An existential formula is an existential block followed by a <quantifier-free formula>, with remaining free variables serving as parameters. Truth is preserved by <structure embeddings>. Reflection of truth for every such formula defines an <existential embedding>.

= Universal quantification
{parent=First-order formula}
{title2=$\forall x\,\varphi$}
{wiki}

<Universal quantification> forms $\forall x\,\varphi(x,\mathbf y)$. In a <first-order structure>, it is true at an assignment to $\mathbf y$ when every domain element assigned to $x$ makes the instance true. Its introduction rule requires that the generalized variable not occur freely in undischarged assumptions.

Over the <natural numbers>, reversing the preceding <existential quantification> and <universal quantification> gives a true <first-order sentence>:
$$
\forall n\in\mathbb N\,\exists m\in\mathbb N\;(m>n).
$$
Choose $m=n+1$. The computed witnesses for $n=0,1,2,3$ are $m=1,2,3,4$. The choice may depend on the previously given $n$. The formula states that every <natural number> has a larger <natural number>; the reversed formula demands one larger than all of them.

= Quantifier-free formula
{parent=First-order formula}

A quantifier-free <first-order formula> is built from <atomic formulas> using logical connectives, with no quantifiers. An embedding of <first-order structures> preserves and reflects its truth, by <mathematical induction> on the <first-order formula>. Both positive and negated atomic information are needed for this conclusion in a relational language.

= Closed-term witness property for quantifier-free existential sentences
{parent=Quantifier-free formula}

In a first-order language having at least one closed term, validity of a quantifier-free existential sentence forces inconsistency of the set of negations of all its closed-term instances. Otherwise take a model of those negations and its substructure generated by the closed terms. Quantifier-free truth is preserved there, so the valid existential sentence has a closed-term witness, a contradiction. Compactness then gives a finite disjunction of instances. The nonempty closed-term hypothesis is essential: in a language with no constants or nullary functions, the valid sentence $\exists x\,(x=x)$ has no closed-term instance.

= Quantifier-free sentence
{parent=Quantifier-free formula}

A quantifier-free sentence is a <quantifier-free formula> with no free variables. In a language whose only closed group terms denote zero, all quantifier-free sentences have the same truth value in every group, even when existential sentences distinguish them.

= Atomic formula
{parent=First-order formula}
{wiki}

An atomic <first-order formula> is a relation applied to terms, or a <logical equality> of two terms when <logical equality> is included in the logic. It contains no logical connective or quantifier. Its truth is preserved and reflected by an embedding of <first-order structures>.

= First-order language
{parent=First-order logic}

A first-order language specifies its constant, operation, and relation symbols, together with the arity of each nonconstant symbol.

= First-order signature
{parent=First-order language}

A signature specifies the sorts, operation symbols with their input and output sorts, and relation symbols with their input sorts. The logical connectives and quantifiers are supplied separately by the chosen logic. This separates the nonlogical vocabulary from the rules defining a <coherent theory>.

= Language of ordered rings
{parent=First-order language}

For coding <arithmetic> computations, the signature in use is $0,1,+,\times,<,=$. Bounded remainder tests and polynomial equations use only these symbols. This is a statement about the language: interpretation in the <natural numbers> does not impose the axioms of a ring.

= First-order term
{parent=First-order language}

A first-order term is built recursively from variables and constant symbols by applying function symbols to existing terms. Evaluation in a <first-order structure> follows the same recursion. An <atomic formula> applies a relation to terms, or asserts their <logical equality>.

= Finite language support
{parent=First-order language}

A finite set of formulas mentions only finitely many nonlogical symbols. This observation lets compactness reduce arguments in a language indexed by a large set to a finite sublanguage.

= Free variable

An occurrence of a variable is free when it is outside the scope of a quantifier binding that variable.

The distinction belongs to <free variables and bound variables>; an occurrence bound by an enclosing quantifier is not free.

= First-order sentence

A first-order sentence is a formula with no free variables, so its truth in a structure is independent of a variable assignment.

A <sentence (mathematical logic)> has no free variables; a <first-order sentence> is its first-order instance.

= Existential sentence

An existential sentence has a block of existential quantifiers followed by a <quantifier-free formula>. Empty blocks are allowed. These sentences are preserved when a <first-order structure> embeds into another structure.

An <arithmetic> theory is sound if all its theorems hold in the standard <natural numbers>. <Arithmetic soundness> implies consistency and rules out a proof of the negation of a true sentence. It is a semantic property, distinct from effective axiomatizability.

= Robinson arithmetic
{parent=First-order theory}
{c}
{title2=$\mathsf Q$}
{wiki}

<Robinson arithmetic> is a weak finitely axiomatized first-order theory sufficient for numeralwise representation of computable finite calculations and the <diagonal lemma>. Its extensions are sufficiently strong for the soundness-based incompleteness argument.

= Partial type
{parent=First-order theory}
{title2=$p(\mathbf x)$}

A partial type over a <first-order theory> $T$ is a set of <first-order formulas> in a fixed finite tuple of variables which is jointly consistent with $T$. A tuple realizes it when it satisfies every formula in the set. A <complete type> additionally decides every formula in those variables.

= Quantifier-free type
{parent=Partial type}

The quantifier-free type of a tuple records all atomic and negated atomic formulas it satisfies, equivalently all quantifier-free formulas. Equal such types identify the generated substructures by sending each term value at one tuple to the value of the same term at the other tuple. Equality formulas make this correspondence well defined, and the relational formulas make it an isomorphism.

= A-type over a structure
{parent=Partial type}
{c}
{title2=$p(\bar x)\text{ over }A\subseteq M$}

For $A\subseteq M$, an A-type over <first-order structure> $M$ is a set of formulas in a fixed finite tuple of variables, with parameters in $A$, consistent with the complete theory of $M$ expanded by constants for $A$. Every finite subset is realized in $M$. A <complete type> decides all formulas in its variables over those parameters.

= Nonprincipal partial type
{parent=Partial type}

A <partial type> is nonprincipal when it is not a <principal partial type>. Equivalently, for every formula $\theta(\mathbf x)$ consistent with $T$, some $\psi\in p$ makes $\theta\land\neg\psi$ consistent with $T$. This is the exact extension property needed in the <Henkin construction> proving the <omitting types theorem>.

= Local omission of a partial type
{parent=Nonprincipal partial type}

A <first-order theory> locally omits a family $\Gamma(\mathbf x)$ if every formula $\theta(\mathbf x)$ consistent with it has a consistent refinement $\theta\land\neg\gamma$ for some $\gamma\in\Gamma$. Equivalently no consistent formula implies all of $\Gamma$. In a countable language the <omitting types theorem> converts this local condition into a countable model with no realization of the family.

= Principal partial type
{parent=Partial type}

A <partial type> $p(\mathbf x)$ is principal over $T$ if some formula $\theta(\mathbf x)$ with $T\cup\{\exists\mathbf x\,\theta\}$ consistent implies each formula of $p$ modulo $T$. For complete types this is the usual notion of an <isolated type>. For an incomplete type, the isolating formula need not belong to the type itself.

= Semidecidable axiomatization

A semidecidable axiomatization is a computably enumerable set of axioms. Its set of formal theorems is also computably enumerable by dovetailing over finite proofs.

= Craig trick
{c}
{parent=Semidecidable axiomatization}

The Craig trick converts an enumeration $A_1,A_2,\ldots$ of axioms into a decidable equivalent set by replacing $A_i$ with a syntactically self-indexing repetition containing $i$ copies of $A_i$. A candidate of length $m$ can only encode one of the first $m$ axioms, so membership is decidable after computing that finite prefix.

The construction proves <Craig's theorem>: every computably enumerable axiomatizable theory has an equivalent decidable axiom set.

= Independent tagged axiomatization
{parent=Semidecidable axiomatization}

In a conservative language extension, attach a fresh nullary predicate $P_i$ to the $i$th axiom $A_i$ and use a self-indexing copy of $P_i\wedge A_i$. The fresh tag makes each new axiom independent of all the others, while forgetting the tags recovers exactly the original-language consequences. The <Craig trick> makes the resulting axiom set decidable.

= Gödel numbering
{c}
{parent=First-order theory}

= Gödel encoding
{c}
{synonym}

A Gödel numbering assigns <natural number>[natural numbers] effectively to symbols, formulas, and finite proofs. Syntactic predicates such as “$p$ codes a proof in $T$ of the formula with code $q$” can then be represented by arithmetical formulas.

= Lambda quotation
{parent=Gödel numbering}
{title2=$d(M)=c_{\#M}$}

For an effective <Gödel numbering> $M\mapsto\#M$, the quotation $d(M)=c_{\#M}$ is a <Church numeral> coding the syntax of $M$. It is not a code of a <beta equivalence> class. The operations taking syntax codes to the code of an application, or to the code of the numeral itself, are <total computable functions> and hence <lambda-definable functions>.

= Self-quoting fixed point
{parent=Lambda quotation}

Let $A$ code application and $C$ code numeral quotation. For a <lambda term> $F$, choose a fresh $x$ and put $H=\lambda x.F(Ax(Cx))$ and $Q=H d(H)$. Then $A d(H)(C d(H))\equiv_\beta d(Q)$, so $Q\equiv_\beta Fd(Q)$. This <diagonal argument> uses the syntax of the actual term $Q$, including any free variables, not an equivalence-class code.

= Gödel number
{c}
{parent=Gödel numbering}

A <natural number> coding a finite syntactic object under a fixed <Gödel numbering>. For program descriptions it records a <function in intension>; different codes may denote the same <function in extension>.

= Arithmetical provability predicate
{parent=Gödel numbering}
{title2=$\operatorname{Pr}_T(v)$}

For an effectively axiomatized <arithmetic> theory, a <arithmetical provability predicate> expresses that a <formal proof> of the sentence with a specified code exists. If axioms are only enumerated, proof certificates also record finite stages at which their axioms appear. The resulting predicate is arithmetically expressible.

= Prime-exponent encoding of a finite list
{parent=Gödel numbering}
{title2=$2^r\prod_{j<r}p_{j+2}^{a_j+1}$}

For a finite list $(a_0,\ldots,a_{r-1})$ of <nonnegative integers>, encode its length with the exponent of two and its entries with the following <prime numbers>: $2^r\prod_{j<r}p_{j+2}^{a_j+1}$, where $p_i$ is the $i$th <prime number>. <Unique prime factorization> recovers both the length and entries. Requiring positive exponents on exactly the prescribed consecutive <primes> also makes validity decidable. Nesting list descriptors therefore gives a <Gödel encoding> of <deterministic finite automata> and other finite syntactic objects.

= Gentzen ordinal reduction for arithmetic
{parent=Peano arithmetic}
{c}

Gentzen's consistency argument associates finite arithmetic derivations with effective <ordinal> notations below <epsilon zero>. An alleged contradiction admits an effective proof reduction preserving the contradictory end-sequent and strictly decreasing the assigned ordinal. Termination would therefore produce a normal contradiction, excluded by inspection of the logical rules and true atomic axioms. This explains why the relevant <transfinite induction> principle implies <formal consistency statement>. For a finite slow-well-ordering formulation one additionally needs the controlled-termination miniaturization theorem, rather than assuming that ordinary proof reductions have linearly growing size. The reduction program is discussed in https://math.stanford.edu/~feferman/papers/reductive.pdf .

= Prime-divisibility coding of a finite set
{parent=Peano arithmetic}
{title2=$e\in F\iff p_e\mid d$}

An internally finite subset of $\{0,\ldots,c-1\}$ can be represented in <Peano arithmetic> by a product of distinct <prime numbers>: multiply the $e$th <prime number> exactly when $e$ belongs to the subset. Divisibility by that prime recovers membership. Arithmetic induction proves existence of this code even for a definable subset with a nonstandard cutoff in a <nonstandard model of Peano arithmetic>.

A nonstandard model of Peano arithmetic is a model not isomorphic to the standard natural-number structure. It contains the standard numerals as an initial segment and also contains elements larger than every standard numeral.

= Nonstandard element type
{parent=Non-standard model of arithmetic}
{title2=$p_\infty(x)=\{x>\overline n:n\in\mathbb N\}$}

In a model of <Peano arithmetic>, an element realizes this <partial type> exactly when it is not a standard numeral. A model omitting it is isomorphic to the standard natural-number structure. For a complete consistent extension $T$ of arithmetic, the type is nonprincipal exactly when $T$ is the <theory of true arithmetic>: apply the <omitting types theorem> in one direction and use an actual standard witness to refute any proposed isolating formula in the other. = Overspill lemma
{parent=Non-standard model of arithmetic}

This is the arithmetic case of <overspill>, which also applies to internal subsets of nonstandard extensions in analysis.

= Diagonal lemma
{parent=First-order theory}
{wiki}

For every one-variable arithmetical formula $\theta(x)$, a sufficiently strong theory of arithmetic has a sentence $\gamma$ satisfying $\gamma\leftrightarrow\theta(\ulcorner\gamma\urcorner)$.

= Gödel sentence
{c}
{parent=Diagonal lemma}
{title2=$G\leftrightarrow\neg\operatorname{Prov}_T(\ulcorner G\urcorner)$}

A fixed point of the negated arithmetic provability predicate for a computably enumerable arithmetic theory $T$. Consistency excludes a proof of $G$, because a genuine proof is arithmetically verifiable and contradicts the fixed-point assertion. Omega-consistency excludes a proof of its negation. For a consistent theory extending Robinson arithmetic, this sentence is true in the standard natural numbers.

= Rosser sentence
{c}
{parent=Gödel sentence}
{title2=$R\leftrightarrow\forall p(\operatorname{Prf}_T(p,R)\to\exists q\le p\,\operatorname{Prf}_T(q,\neg R))$}

This diagonal sentence says that any proof of itself has a no-later proof of its negation. A proof of $R$ contradicts finite verification that no earlier opposite proof exists. A proof of its negation, together with finite verification that no earlier proof of $R$ exists, proves the displayed universal condition and hence $R$. Thus consistency alone excludes both proofs, strengthening the original omega-consistency form of incompleteness.

= Effective diagonal lemma
{parent=Diagonal lemma}

The <diagonal lemma> can be carried out uniformly: from a one-variable formula code, compute a sentence code whose assertion is equivalent to the original formula applied to that sentence code. The effective substitution operation is what turns a semantic diagonal argument into a productive <function>.

= Crude incompleteness theorem
{parent=Diagonal lemma}

Every consistent recursively axiomatized extension of elementary arithmetic is incomplete. If it were complete, its theorem set would be decidable; representing that decision procedure and diagonalizing against it yields a contradiction.

= Tennenbaum's theorem

{parent=First-order theory}
{wiki}

= Tennenbaum theorem
{synonym}
{c}

Tennenbaum's theorem says that no countable nonstandard model of <Peano arithmetic> can have both addition and multiplication computable under a presentation of its domain by the natural numbers.

= Recursively inseparable-set proof of Tennenbaum theorem
{parent=Tennenbaum's theorem}

Choose disjoint computably enumerable, recursively inseparable sets $A,B$. A nonstandard arithmetic model internally codes the elements enumerated into $A$ below a nonstandard stage by divisibility by standard primes. If the model's addition and multiplication were computable, division by each standard prime would decide the coded standard set. It would contain $A$ and avoid $B$, contradicting recursive inseparability.

= Consistent first-order theory
{parent=First-order theory}

A theory is consistent when it does not derive a contradiction. By first-order completeness, this is equivalent to having a model.

= Formal consistency statement
{title2=$\operatorname{Con}(T)$}
{parent=Consistent first-order theory}

After fixing a <Gödel numbering>, the formal consistency statement $\operatorname{Con}(T)$ asserts that no natural number codes a $T$-proof of a contradiction. For a recursively axiomatized theory, this is an arithmetical sentence expressible inside any sufficiently strong base theory.

= Gödel second incompleteness theorem
{c}
{parent=Formal consistency statement}

If $T$ is a consistent, recursively axiomatized theory containing enough arithmetic, then $T$ does not prove $\operatorname{Con}(T)$. The consistency assumption is external: an inconsistent theory proves every sentence, including its formal consistency statement.

= Consistency-strength preorder
{title2=$\leq_{\mathrm{Cons}}$}
{parent=Formal consistency statement}

For theories $T,S$ extending a fixed base theory, one consistency-strength comparison is
$$
T\leq_{\mathrm{Cons}}S
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\mathrm{Cons}\cap C_T\subseteq\mathrm{Cons}\cap C_S,
$$
where $C_T$ is the set of consequences of $T$ and $\mathrm{Cons}$ is the chosen class of formal consistency statements. Its strict part $T<_{\mathrm{Cons}}S$ means $T\leq_{\mathrm{Cons}}S$ but not $S\leq_{\mathrm{Cons}}T$.

= Relative consistency
{parent=Consistency-strength preorder}
{title2=$\operatorname{Con}(T)\Rightarrow\operatorname{Con}(S)$}

A relative-consistency result constructs a <model of a first-order theory> $S$, or interprets its proofs, from the assumed consistency of a background theory $T$. It establishes $\operatorname{Con}(T)\Rightarrow\operatorname{Con}(S)$ in the stated metatheory, not an unconditional proof of consistency. For independence of an axiom $\varphi$ from $S$, obtain consistent models of both $S+\varphi$ and $S+\neg\varphi$, relative to the background assumption.

= Consistency strength of a worldly cardinal
{parent=Consistency-strength preorder}

Let $T=\mathrm{ZFC}+\text{“there is a worldly cardinal”}$ and $T^*=\mathrm{ZFC}+\operatorname{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$. A worldly $\kappa$ makes $V_\kappa$ a model of ZFC, and <arithmetic absoluteness for a rank-initial model> makes it a model of $T^*$, so $T$ proves $\operatorname{Con}(T^*)$. If $T^*$ proved $\operatorname{Con}(T)$, then the stronger theory $T$ would prove its own consistency, contradicting the <Gödel second incompleteness theorem>. Thus, assuming consistency, $T^*<_{\mathrm{Cons}}T$.

= Iterated consistency progression
{parent=Consistency-strength preorder}

Starting from a recursively axiomatized theory $T_0$, define
$$
T_{n+1}=T_n+\operatorname{Con}(T_n).
$$
Assuming consistency, <Gödel second incompleteness theorem> makes every step strict in the <consistency-strength preorder>, while the effective union $T_\omega=\bigcup_{n<\omega}T_n$ lies strictly above every finite stage.

A universal theory can be axiomatized by sentences whose quantifiers are all universal. Such sentences often forbid specified finite configurations.

= Universal consequences of a theory
{parent=Universal first-order theory}
{title2=$T_\forall$}

The universal consequences $T_\forall$ of a <first-order theory> $T$ are all universal <first-order sentences> entailed by $T$. Their model class is exactly the class of <substructures> of models of $T$, up to <structure embedding>. This characterization follows from the <diagram embedding criterion for universal theories>.

= Łoś-Tarski preservation theorem
{parent=Universal first-order theory}
{c}
{title2=$T\equiv T_\forall$}

A <first-order theory> has its class of <first-order models> closed under <substructures of a first-order structure> exactly when it is equivalent to its universal consequences $T_\forall$. If $A\models T_\forall$, then $T\cup\operatorname{Diag}(A)$ is consistent. Otherwise a finite <logical conjunction> $\delta$ of signed <diagram of a structure> sentences yields a universal consequence $\forall\mathbf x\,\neg\delta(\mathbf x)$ false in $A$. The <compactness theorem> gives a <first-order model> of $T$ containing an embedded copy of $A$, and closure under <substructures of a first-order structure> implies $A\models T$. The converse is preservation of <universal sentences>. The <diagram of a structure> must include negated as well as positive atomic sentences so that the resulting map is an embedding, not just a homomorphism.

= Universal sentence
{parent=Universal first-order theory}
{title2=$\forall\mathbf x\,\psi(\mathbf x)$}

A <universal sentence> has the form $\forall\mathbf x\,\psi(\mathbf x)$ with <quantifier-free formula> $\psi$, including an empty quantifier block. Its truth passes from a <first-order structure> to every <substructure of a first-order structure>: tuples from the smaller domain are also tuples of the larger domain, and quantifier-free truth agrees. Therefore a <first-order theory> of <universal sentences> has a <first-order model> class closed under <substructures of a first-order structure>.

= Universal-existential sentence
{parent=Universal sentence}
{title2=$\forall\mathbf x\,\exists\mathbf y\,\theta(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)$}

A universal-existential sentence has a universal block followed by an existential block and a <quantifier-free formula>. Such axioms are preserved under unions of chains of structures: the universally chosen tuple occurs in one stage, whose existential witnesses remain in the union. An <inductive first-order theory> has an axiomatization by these sentences.

= Finite forbidden-pattern axiomatization
{parent=Universal first-order theory}

If failure of a property has a finite witness, one can often axiomatize the property by a universal sentence forbidding each possible finite witness. Compactness supplies finite witnesses when failure is expressed by inconsistency of an associated theory.

= First-order structure

A structure supplies a nonempty domain and an interpretation of every constant, operation, and relation symbol in its language.

This is a <structure (mathematical logic)> for a <first-order language>, with finitary interpretations of its nonlogical symbols.

A structure embedding is an injective map respecting constants and operations and preserving and reflecting relations. Equivalently it preserves all <atomic formulas> and their negations. = Substructure of a first-order structure
{parent=First-order structure}

= Substructure
{synonym}

= Substructures of a first-order structure
{synonym}

An $L$-<substructure of a first-order structure> $A\subseteq B$ has a nonempty domain containing all named constants and closed under the <functions> of $B$, and each relation of $A$ is the restriction of the corresponding relation of $B$. Consequently every <quantifier-free formula> with parameters in $A$ has the same truth value in $A$ and $B$. This is weaker than being an <elementary substructure>, which preserves all <first-order formulas>.

= Simple closure
{parent=Substructure of a first-order structure}
{title2=$M\preccurlyeq_s N$}

= Simply closed
{synonym}

A <substructure> $M\subseteq N$ is simply closed when each existential <quantifier-free formula> over $M$ realized in $N$ is already realized in $M$. A single quantified variable suffices in the definition; iterating covers finite tuples. This property is also called existential closure in this specified extension. It is weaker than being an <elementary substructure>.

= Existential embedding
{parent=Simple closure}
{title2=$A\preccurlyeq_1 B$}

= 1-embedding
{synonym}

An embedding of <first-order structures> is existential when every existential formula with parameters from the source has the same truth value in the source and target. Forward preservation follows from being a <structure embedding>; reflection is the additional requirement. Applying <compactness theorem> to the target's <diagram of a structure> and the source's <elementary diagram> places the target inside an elementary extension of the source.

= Satisfaction for a set structure
{parent=First-order structure}
{title2=$\operatorname{Sat}(A,e,\vec a)$}

The satisfaction relation says that the finite code $e$ of a <first-order formula> is true in a set-sized <first-order structure> $A$ at assignment $\vec a$. It is defined uniformly in set theory by recursion on the finite syntax tree: evaluate atomic relations, Boolean operations and quantifiers over the given domain. A code includes only finitely many subformulas, and their truth relations are <sets>. This does not define a truth predicate for the ambient universe, whose domain is not a <set>. <Finite relation closure for set-theoretic coding> gives an explicit closure criterion ensuring that these truth relations are computed correctly in a <transitive set>.

= Elementary substructure
{title2=$M\prec N$}
{parent=First-order structure}

A substructure $M\subseteq N$ is elementary when every <first-order formula> with parameters from $M$ has the same truth value in $M$ and $N$.

= Partial elementary map
{parent=Elementary substructure}

An injective map between subsets of structures is partial elementary when it preserves every first-order formula on tuples from its domain. This is stronger than a <partial embedding>, which only preserves quantifier-free formulas. In countable models, successive one-point extensions in both directions produce an elementary isomorphism by the <back-and-forth method>.

= Tarski-Vaught test
{c}
{parent=Elementary substructure}

A substructure $M\subseteq N$ is elementary if and only if, whenever $N\models\exists x\,\varphi(x,\bar a)$ with parameters $\bar a$ from $M$, some witness $b\in M$ satisfies $N\models\varphi(b,\bar a)$.

The witness criterion characterizes an <elementary substructure>, and hence preservation of all first-order formulas with parameters from the substructure.

After choosing a Skolem function for each existential formula, the Skolem hull of $A\subseteq N$ is the smallest subset containing $A$ and closed under those functions. The <Tarski-Vaught test> makes it an <elementary substructure> of $N$, and in a countable language an infinite hull has cardinality at most $|A|+\aleph_0$.

= Skolem function
{parent=Skolem hull}
{c}
{title2=$f_\varphi$}

For each existential <first-order formula> $\exists y\,\varphi(y,\mathbf x)$ in a structure, a <Skolem function> selects a witness when one exists, with an arbitrary default when none exists. An expansion containing these <functions> satisfies
$$
\forall\mathbf x\bigl(\exists y\,\varphi(y,\mathbf x)\to\varphi(f_\varphi(\mathbf x),\mathbf x)\bigr).
$$
A subset closed under all such <functions> is elementary in the original language by the <Tarski-Vaught test>. Simultaneously choosing these <functions> is performed in the ordinary classical metatheory with choice.

= Skolem expansion
{parent=Skolem function}
{c}

A <Skolem expansion> adjoins a <Skolem function> for every existential <first-order formula>. Iterating through the successively enlarged languages, and taking their union, gives a language with witness <functions> even for <first-order formulas> involving previously added <functions>. Starting from a language of size $\kappa$, the resulting language has size at most $\kappa+\aleph_0$. A hull closed under its <functions> is a <Skolem hull> and is elementary in the expanded structure by the <Tarski-Vaught test>.

= Model of a first-order theory
{parent=First-order structure}

= First-order model
{synonym}

A structure $M$ is a model of $T$ when $M\models\sigma$ for every sentence $\sigma\in T$.

= Definable element of a first-order structure
{parent=Model of a first-order theory}

An element $a$ of a structure $M$ is definable without parameters when some <first-order formula> $\varphi(x)$ uniquely identifies it:
$$
M\models\forall x\,(x=a\leftrightarrow\varphi(x)).
$$

= Paris model
{c}
{parent=Definable element of a first-order structure}

A Paris model of a complete extension $T$ of <ZFC> is a set model $M\models T$ in which every internal <ordinal> is a <definable element of a first-order structure> without parameters.

= Paris models are well-founded when their complete theory has a well-founded model
{c}
{parent=Paris model}

Let $N$ be a <well-founded model of set theory> of a complete theory $T\supseteq\mathsf{ZFC}$, and let $M$ be a Paris model of $T$. If the internal ordinals of $M$ admitted an external descending sequence $\alpha_0\ni^M\alpha_1\ni^M\cdots$, choose formulas $\varphi_n$ defining them. Completeness makes $M$ and $N$ satisfy the same sentences, so the unique ordinals defined by the $\varphi_n$ in $N$ would form an externally descending membership sequence. This contradicts well-foundedness of $N$.

= Paris models are rigid when their complete theory has a well-founded model
{c}
{parent=Paris model}

Under the hypothesis of <Paris models are well-founded when their complete theory has a well-founded model>, a Paris model $M$ is well-founded. Every automorphism of $M$ is then the identity by external <epsilon induction>: once all members of $x$ are fixed, extensionality gives $\pi(x)=\{\pi(y):y\in^M x\}=x$. Hence $M$ is a <rigid first-order structure>.

= Automorphism of a first-order structure
{parent=First-order structure}

= Structure automorphism
{synonym}

= Automorphisms of a first-order structure
{synonym}

An automorphism is a bijection from a structure to itself that preserves every operation and relation in its language.

= Automorphism group of a first-order structure
{title2=$\operatorname{Aut}(M)$}
{parent=Automorphism of a first-order structure}

The automorphism group $\operatorname{Aut}(M)$ consists of all <automorphism of a first-order structure>[automorphisms] of $M$, with composition as its group operation.

= First-order axiomatization of an automorphism
{parent=Automorphism of a first-order structure}

After adjoining a unary function symbol $f$, injectivity, surjectivity, preservation of each operation, and preservation in both directions of each relation form an axiom scheme whose models interpret $f$ as an automorphism.

= Faithful automorphism action
{parent=Automorphism of a first-order structure}

A family $(f_g)_{g\in G}$ of automorphisms satisfying $f_gf_h=f_{gh}$ defines a group action. It is faithful exactly when distinct indices define distinct automorphisms, equivalently when $G$ embeds in the automorphism group.

= Rigid first-order structure
{parent=Automorphism of a first-order structure}

A first-order structure is rigid when its identity map is its only automorphism. Every finite linear order is rigid, whereas $(\mathbb Z,<)$ is nonrigid because every translation is an order automorphism.

Rigidity here means absence of nonidentity <automorphisms of a first-order structure>; it does not assert the mechanical rigidity of a rod-and-hinge framework.

= Compactness transfer of finite nonrigidity
{parent=Rigid first-order structure}

If a theory has arbitrarily large finite nonrigid models, expand its language by a unary function $f$, axiomatize that $f$ is an automorphism, require $f$ to move some element, and add sentences demanding at least $n$ elements for every $n$. Every finite fragment has a sufficiently large finite nonrigid model, so compactness supplies an infinite nonrigid model.

= Soundness theorem for first-order logic
{parent=First-order logic}

Every sentence derivable from a first-order theory is true in every model of that theory.

This is <soundness> of the first-order deduction system relative to its structure semantics.

= Gödel's completeness theorem
{parent=First-order logic}
{c}
{wiki}

= Godel completeness theorem
{synonym}
{c}

For first-order logic, every sentence true in every model of a theory is derivable from that theory. Equivalently, every consistent theory has a model.

= Henkin construction
{c}
{parent=Gödel's completeness theorem}

A Henkin construction enlarges a consistent <first-order theory> by fresh constants, decides sentences while preserving consistency, and supplies constant witnesses for existential sentences. Countably many additional dense requirements can be interleaved, as in the <omitting types theorem>. A complete consistent extension with witnesses has a <term model>.

= Term model
{parent=Henkin construction}

For a complete consistent theory with the <Henkin witness property>, the term model has closed terms modulo provable equality as elements. Functions are evaluated by forming terms, and atomic relations hold exactly when the corresponding atomic sentences belong to the theory. Provable equality makes these interpretations well defined.

= Truth lemma for a Henkin term model
{parent=Term model}

A sentence with closed-term parameters holds in a <term model> if and only if it belongs to the complete consistent theory defining that model. Prove this by <structural induction>: atoms are the definition, Boolean steps use completeness and consistency, and existential sentences use the <Henkin witness property>. Conversely any existential witness represented by a term gives the existential sentence by logical inference.

= Henkin witness property
{c}
{parent=Henkin construction}

A theory in a language with constants has the Henkin witness property when every existential sentence $\exists x\,\phi(x)$ belonging to it has an instance $\phi(c)$ belonging to it for some constant $c$. This property proves the existential step of the <truth lemma for a Henkin term model>.

= Compactness theorem
{parent=First-order logic}
{wiki}

A set of first-order sentences has a model if and only if every finite subset has a model.

= Syntactic proof of first-order compactness
{parent=Compactness theorem}

If a theory were inconsistent, a proof of contradiction would use a finite inconsistent subset. Therefore consistency of every finite subset implies consistency of the whole theory, and completeness supplies a model.

= Compactness obstruction to axiomatizing finitely many maximal elements
{parent=Compactness theorem}

If a theory claimed that a poset has only finitely many maximal elements, adjoining countably many constants required to be distinct and maximal would remain finitely satisfiable. Compactness would produce a model with infinitely many maximal elements, contradicting the claimed axiomatization.

= Compactness obstruction to an exclusive disjunction of infinitude
{parent=Compactness theorem}

Suppose a class requires exactly one of two definable sets to be infinite. Add constants witnessing arbitrarily many distinct members of both sets. Every finite fragment can keep one set infinite and make the other large but finite, whereas compactness makes both sets infinite. Therefore such an exclusive disjunction is not first-order axiomatizable.

= Finite structures are not first-order axiomatizable
{parent=Compactness theorem}

If a first-order theory had exactly the finite structures in a class containing arbitrarily large members, adjoin constants $c_1,c_2,\ldots$ and require them to be pairwise distinct. Every finite fragment has a sufficiently large finite model, while <compactness theorem>[compactness] produces an infinite model, a contradiction. In particular, finite groups are not first-order axiomatizable.

= Well-orders are not first-order axiomatizable
{parent=Compactness theorem}

If a theory axiomatized the <well-orders>, adjoin constants $c_0,c_1,\ldots$ and the sentences $c_{n+1}<c_n$. Every finite fragment has a finite well-ordered model, but <compactness theorem>[compactness] would produce a model with an infinite descending chain.

= Finite subaxiomatization from an equivalent finite theory
{parent=Compactness theorem}

Suppose theories $S$ and finite $T$ have the same models. Let $\tau$ be the conjunction of $T$. Since $S\models\tau$, compactness supplies a finite $S_0\subseteq S$ with $S_0\models\tau$. Conversely $\tau\models S$, so $S_0$ and $S$ have the same models. Thus an axiomatization equivalent to a finite theory contains a finite equivalent subaxiomatization.

= Löwenheim-Skolem theorem
{parent=First-order logic}
{c}
{wiki=Löwenheim–Skolem_theorem}

The Löwenheim--Skolem theorems control the cardinalities of models of a first-order theory without changing its first-order truths.

= Upward Lowenheim-Skolem theorem
{parent=Löwenheim-Skolem theorem}
{c}

If an $L$-theory has an infinite model, then it has models of arbitrarily large cardinality. For a cardinal $\kappa$, add constants $c_\alpha$ for $\alpha<\kappa$ and all inequalities $c_\alpha\ne c_\beta$. Every finite fragment is satisfiable in the given infinite model, so compactness gives a model with at least $\kappa$ elements. Applying the downward theorem produces a model of exactly $\kappa$ when $\kappa\geq|L|+\aleph_0$.

= Downward Lowenheim-Skolem theorem
{parent=Löwenheim-Skolem theorem}
{c}

If $A$ is an infinite $L$-structure and $|L|+\aleph_0\leq\kappa\leq|A|$, then $A$ has an elementary substructure of cardinality $\kappa$. One proof adds Skolem functions for existential formulas and closes a chosen $\kappa$-element subset under them; the closure still has size $\kappa$, and the Tarski--Vaught test makes it elementary.

= Finite satisfiability problem for first-order logic
{parent=First-order logic}

The finite satisfiability problem asks whether a first-order sentence has a finite model. It is computably enumerable because finite structures and their truth relations can be searched effectively.

= Trakhtenbrot's theorem
{c}
{parent=Finite satisfiability problem for first-order logic}
{wiki}

= Trakhtenbrot theorem
{synonym}
{c}

The finite satisfiability problem is undecidable, and the set of first-order sentences valid in every finite structure is not computably enumerable. A halting computation can be encoded as a finite ordered tableau satisfying finitely many local first-order constraints; the resulting sentence has a finite model exactly when the computation halts.

= Finite validity problem for first-order logic
{parent=Trakhtenbrot's theorem}

The finite validity problem asks whether a sentence holds in every finite structure. Its complement is computably enumerable by searching for a finite countermodel, but <Trakhtenbrot theorem> says that finite validity itself is not computably enumerable.

= Priestley space
{c}
{parent=Mathematical logic}
{wiki}

A <Priestley space> is a compact topological <partially ordered set> that is totally order-disconnected: if $x\not\leq y$, a clopen upward-closed set contains $x$ and omits $y$. A <Priestley dual space> is a space of this kind constructed from the prime filters of a bounded distributive lattice.

= Kripke semantics
{c}
{parent=Mathematical logic}
{wiki}

<Kripke semantics> interprets logical formulas at worlds related by an accessibility relation or an information order. Modal logics use accessibility to interpret modal operators; intuitionistic logics use persistent valuations and quantify over stronger worlds for implication.

= Disjunction and existence properties
{parent=Mathematical logic}
{wiki}

A theory has the disjunction property when provability of a closed disjunction implies provability of one disjunct. It has the existence property when a provable existential sentence has a witness whose substitution is provably valid. These are distinct constructive properties; <disjunction property of intuitionistic propositional logic> is one instance of the former.

= Soundness
{parent=Mathematical logic}
{wiki}

= Sound
{synonym}

A proof system is <sound> with respect to its semantics when every derivable conclusion is true in every interpretation satisfying its premises. Both <soundness theorem for propositional logic> and <soundness theorem for first-order logic> establish this property for particular languages and semantics.

= Free variables and bound variables
{parent=Mathematical logic}
{wiki}

A variable occurrence is bound when it falls within the scope of a binding operation that binds that occurrence; otherwise it is free. Quantifiers and lambda abstractions are common binders. In first-order formulas, a <free variable> occurrence is outside the scope of a corresponding quantifier.

= Sentence
{disambiguate=mathematical logic}
{parent=Mathematical logic}
{wiki}

A <sentence (mathematical logic)> is a closed formula, with no free variable occurrences. Its truth does not depend on a variable assignment. A <first-order sentence> uses a first-order formal language.

= Theory
{disambiguate=mathematical logic}
{parent=Mathematical logic}
{wiki}

A <theory (mathematical logic)> is a set of sentences in a formal language, sometimes understood to be closed under deduction. A <first-order theory> uses a first-order language; consistency and completeness are separate properties of a theory.

= Craig's theorem
{c}
{parent=Mathematical logic}
{wiki}

<Craig's theorem> says that a theory with a computably enumerable axiomatization has an equivalent decidable axiomatization. The <Craig trick> gives a constructive proof by encoding an enumeration index in repeated conjunctions of the enumerated axiom.

= Overspill
{parent=Mathematical logic}
{wiki}

<Overspill> is the principle that an internal subset of a nonstandard extension containing every standard natural number must contain some nonstandard natural number. The <overspill lemma> is its definable-set instance in nonstandard arithmetic.

= Structure
{disambiguate=mathematical logic}
{parent=Mathematical logic}
{wiki}

A <structure (mathematical logic)> consists of a domain together with interpretations of the symbols of a formal language. In the usual finitary setting it interprets constant, function, and relation symbols of their declared arities. A <first-order structure> is such an interpretation for first-order logic.
