== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

During the short <collision>, the pivot's <impulse> has zero <torque> about the pivot, so <angular momentum conservation> about that point gives
$$
mvL=I\Omega,\qquad I=\frac{ML^2}{3}.
$$
The bullet stops rather than becoming embedded: immediately after impact only the rod contributes to the <kinetic energy>. Because this is an <elastic collision>,
$$
\frac12mv^2=\frac12I\Omega^2.
$$
Eliminating $\Omega$ yields $mL^2=I$: the projectile mass equals the <effective impact mass of a pivoted body>, $I/L^2$. Hence **the bullet has one third of the rod's mass**, and
$$
\boxed{m=\frac M3,\qquad \Omega=\frac vL.}
$$
Ordinary <momentum conservation> for the bullet and rod together would be incorrect: the fixed pivot supplies an external <impulse>. The short-impact approximation neglects the change in <gravitational potential energy> during impact.

== 2
{scope}

The source's tangential arrows $a,c$ respectively point along and against the original motion; $b,d$ point radially outward and inward. Write $r_0$ for the initial <orbital radius>, $v_c=\sqrt{GM/r_0}$ for the <circular orbital speed>, and $\delta>0$ for the small added <speed>. The resulting trajectories follow the <velocity kick on a circular Kepler orbit> formulas. Here <orbital energy> includes <kinetic energy> and <gravitational potential energy>; <angular momentum> refers to the central object.

\Image[2017-resit-orbital-impulses.png]
{title=Four Kepler orbits following small tangential or radial velocity changes}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Adding tangential <velocity> along the existing motion increases both <orbital energy> and <angular momentum>:
$$
\Delta E=m v_c\delta+\frac12m\delta^2>0,\qquad \Delta L=m r_0\delta>0.
$$
For a sufficiently small kick the <Kepler orbit> remains an <ellipse>. At the kick point its radial <velocity> vanishes and its tangential <speed> exceeds the <circular orbital speed>, so that point becomes the <pericentre>. **The ellipse extends farther outward than the original circle.**

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

A radial outward kick has no <angular impulse> about the centre. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Delta L=0,\qquad \Delta E=\frac12m\delta^2>0.}
$$
The initial position is on the outward-moving portion of a bound <elliptic Kepler orbit>, between <pericentre> and <apocentre>, rather than at either turning point. The <eccentricity> is $e=\delta/v_c$ and the <semilatus rectum> remains $r_0$. The <orbital energy> change is second order: it vanishes to first order in the impulse but is positive for any nonzero kick.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For a small opposing tangential kick,
$$
\Delta E=-m v_c\delta+\frac12m\delta^2<0,\qquad \Delta L=-m r_0\delta<0.
$$
The new tangential <speed> is less than the <circular orbital speed>, so the kick point is the <apocentre> of an <ellipse>. **The orbit falls inward from the original circle.** The signs assume $0<\delta\ll v_c$; a large velocity reversal is outside the stated small-impulse regime.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The inward radial kick also leaves <angular momentum> unchanged and increases <orbital energy> by $m\delta^2/2$. Its <eccentricity> and <semi-major axis> equal those in case $b$, but the orientation of its <eccentricity vector> is reflected about the initial radius. The satellite initially moves inward on its <ellipse>, toward <pericentre>. **Neither radial kick decreases the orbital energy.**

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

For planets with negligible <mass> compared with the <Sun>, <Kepler's third law> gives $T^2\propto a^3$, where $a$ is the <semi-major axis>. Relative to <Earth>,
$$
\boxed{\frac{a_{\rm Mercury}}{a_\oplus}=0.24^{2/3}\simeq0.386,\qquad
\frac{a_{\rm Jupiter}}{a_\oplus}=11.9^{2/3}\simeq5.21.}
$$
Thus the requested orbital-distance estimates are **about 0.39 and 5.2 times Earth's**. Strictly, the period fixes the <semi-major axis>, not a time-averaged radius of an arbitrary <eccentric orbit>; identifying the two as a mean distance is the usual elementary orbital-size convention.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

In the <pion> <rest frame>, <momentum conservation> makes the <muon> and <neutrino> momenta opposite, with common magnitude $p$. For a massless <neutrino>, $E_\nu=pc$; <energy conservation> and the <relativistic energy-momentum relation> give
$$
E_\mu+pc=m_\pi c^2,\qquad E_\mu^2-p^2c^2=m_\mu^2c^4.
$$
Substitute $pc=m_\pi c^2-E_\mu$ into the second equation. The $E_\mu^2$ terms cancel, leaving
$$
\boxed{E_\mu=\frac{m_\pi^2+m_\mu^2}{2m_\pi}c^2.}
$$
For completeness, the complementary <relativistic two-body decay> energy is $E_\nu=(m_\pi^2-m_\mu^2)c^2/(2m_\pi)$. The result is a total <energy>, including the <muon> <rest energy>, rather than its <kinetic energy>.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

Treat the ladder as a uniform slender <rigid body>. While both contacts remain imposed, its <centre of mass> is $(l\sin\theta,l\cos\theta)$, with <speed> $l|\dot\theta|$. Its central <moment of inertia> is $m(2l)^2/12=ml^2/3$. Adding translational and rotational <kinetic energy> gives
$$
K=\frac12ml^2\dot\theta^2+\frac12\frac{ml^2}{3}\dot\theta^2
=\frac23ml^2\dot\theta^2.
$$
Taking the floor as zero <gravitational potential energy>, the <Lagrangian>, <conjugate momentum> and <Euler-Lagrange equation> are
$$
\boxed{L=\frac23ml^2\dot\theta^2-mgl\cos\theta,\qquad
p_\theta=\frac43ml^2\dot\theta,\qquad
\ddot\theta=\frac{3g}{4l}\sin\theta.}
$$
The positive sign is appropriate: gravity increases the angle from the vertical. These are the constrained equations of a <frictionless sliding ladder>. Real wall contact is unilateral: a wall cannot pull the ladder. Once the computed <normal force> would become negative, the two-contact model ceases to apply; the question explicitly assumes that contact persists.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

Write $\omega$ for the imposed constant <angular velocity>. In inertial polar coordinates, the bead has $v^2=\dot r^2+\omega^2r^2$. Its constrained <Lagrangian> is
$$
L=\frac m2(\dot r^2+\omega^2r^2),\qquad
\ddot r=\omega^2r.
$$
The reduced <Lagrangian> has no explicit time dependence, so its <Lagrangian energy function> is conserved:
$$
\boxed{J=\dot r\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot r}-L
=\frac m2(\dot r^2-\omega^2r^2).}
$$
This is the <Jacobi integral> $J=E-\omega L_z$, since the bead's actual inertial <energy> and axial <angular momentum> are
$$
E=\frac m2(\dot r^2+\omega^2r^2),\qquad L_z=m\omega r^2.
$$
The motor maintaining the stick's <angular velocity> supplies <torque> and does <work>. Indeed $\dot E=2m\omega^2r\dot r=\omega\dot L_z$, generally nonzero. A time-independent reduced <Lagrangian> therefore need not imply conservation of the bead's inertial <kinetic energy> when its imposed constraint is moving.

== 7
{scope}

=== Solution

Choose upward <velocity> positive during ascent. Gravity and <linear drag> both point downward, giving the <linear differential equation>
$$
m\dot v=-mg-\alpha v,\qquad
v(t)=\left(v_0+\frac{mg}{\alpha}\right)e^{-\alpha t/m}-\frac{mg}{\alpha}.
$$
At the highest point $v=0$, so
$$
\boxed{t_{\max}=\frac m\alpha\log\left(1+\frac{\alpha v_0}{mg}\right).}
$$
The <vertical ascent under linear drag> time is smaller than $v_0/g$ for $\alpha>0$, because $\log(1+x)<x$. Its zero-drag <limit> is $v_0/g$, as required by constant-gravity <projectile motion>.

== 8
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose downward <velocity> $v$ positive. The remaining rocket <mass> is $m(t)=m_0-\alpha t$, and the upward <rocket thrust> has magnitude $\alpha u$, where $u>0$ is the relative <rocket exhaust speed>. The <rocket equation> including gravity is
$$
m\dot v=mg-\alpha u,\qquad
\boxed{\dot v=g-\frac{\alpha u}{m_0-\alpha t}.}
$$
This follows by applying <momentum conservation> to the rocket and its just-expelled <rocket propellant>, with gravity supplying the external <impulse>. Applying $d(mv)/dt=mg$ to the rocket alone would omit the exhaust <momentum flux>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Integrating the <rocket equation> with $v(0)=v_0$ gives
$$
\boxed{v(t)=v_0+gt-u\log\frac{m_0}{m_0-\alpha t}.}
$$
This formula holds during the constant-flow burn, while $m_0-\alpha t>0$ and fuel remains. The negative logarithmic term represents the slowing due to upward <rocket thrust>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Height decreases at rate $\dot h=-v$. Set $m=m_0-\alpha t$; an antiderivative needed for the <rocket equation> is
$$
\int_0^t\log\frac{m_0}{m_0-\alpha s}\,ds
=t-\frac m\alpha\log\frac{m_0}{m}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{h(t)=h_0-(v_0-u)t-\frac12gt^2
-\frac{u(m_0-\alpha t)}{\alpha}\log\frac{m_0}{m_0-\alpha t}.}
$$
Differentiating this expression recovers $\dot h=-v$ and substituting $t=0$ gives $h(0)=h_0$, which check both the sign and the integration constant.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The specified <rocket fuel fraction> gives $m(T)=2m_0/3$, so $\alpha T=m_0/3$. Imposing $v(T)=0$ yields
$$
T=\frac{u\log(3/2)-v_0}{g}.
$$
Then $h(T)=0$ fixes
$$
h_0=(v_0-u)T+\frac12gT^2+2uT\log(3/2).
$$
Numerically,
$$
\boxed{T\simeq60.07\,\mathrm s,\qquad h_0\simeq1.159\times10^3\,\mathrm m.}
$$
The associated constant <mass loss rate> is $\alpha=m_0/(3T)\simeq88.78\,\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$. The initial upward <thrust acceleration> already exceeds $g$ and increases as <mass> falls, so the downward <speed> decreases monotonically to zero. This calculation assumes constant lunar <gravitational acceleration>, constant relative <rocket exhaust speed>, and negligible <drag>.

== 9
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For two particles, let $M=m_1+m_2$ and $\mathbf V_{\rm cm}=(m_1\mathbf v_1+m_2\mathbf v_2)/M$. The <centre-of-mass frame> velocities are $\mathbf v_i^*=\mathbf v_i-\mathbf V_{\rm cm}$, so $m_1\mathbf v_1^*+m_2\mathbf v_2^*=0$. Thus before and after an isolated <collision> the two <momentum> <vectors> are equal and opposite. For an <elastic collision> their common magnitude is also unchanged: only their direction rotates.

The <kinetic energy decomposition about the center of mass> separates unchanged bulk translation from relative motion:
$$
K=\frac12M V_{\rm cm}^2+\frac12\mu|\mathbf v_1-\mathbf v_2|^2,
\qquad \mu=\frac{m_1m_2}{M}.
$$
The <reduced mass> $\mu$ reduces the calculation to one relative <velocity>. After solving the geometry in the <centre-of-mass frame>, add $\mathbf V_{\rm cm}$ to each <velocity> to return to the laboratory. The four panels below illustrate this for the subsequent example.

\Image[2017-resit-collision-frames.png]
{title=Velocity vectors before and after an elastic collision in laboratory and centre-of-mass frames}

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For equal <masses> $m$, the <reduced mass> is $m/2$. The <kinetic energy> of their <centre of mass> cannot be removed by an isolated <collision>, since total <momentum> is fixed. Therefore the maximum <energy> available for conversion to internal forms is the initial relative <kinetic energy>,
$$
\boxed{K_{\rm available}=\frac m4|\mathbf v_1-\mathbf v_2|^2.}
$$
It is attained by a <perfectly inelastic collision> in which the final relative <velocity> is zero. This concerns the convertible initial <kinetic energy>, not an arbitrary pre-existing internal <energy> reservoir.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For <masses> $m$ and $2m$, the constant <centre-of-mass velocity> is $(V/3)\mathbf e_x$. Initially the laboratory <velocities> are $V\mathbf e_x$ and zero, while the <centre-of-mass frame> <velocities> are $(2V/3)\mathbf e_x$ and $-(V/3)\mathbf e_x$. After the <elastic collision> they are
$$
\mathbf v_1^*=\frac{2V}{3}(\cos\chi,\sin\chi),\qquad
\mathbf v_2^*=-\frac V3(\cos\chi,\sin\chi).
$$
Adding $(V/3,0)$ produces the lower-left panel's laboratory <velocity vectors>. The given $30^\circ$ laboratory angle implies
$$
\tan30^\circ=\frac{2\sin\chi}{1+2\cos\chi},\qquad
\boxed{\chi=30^\circ+\arcsin(1/4)\simeq44.48^\circ.}
$$
The <centre-of-mass frame> scattering angle is therefore different from the laboratory angle. Reflection across the $x$-axis gives the equally possible negative-angle configuration.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $u>0$ be the projectile's final <speed>, taking its deflection above the $x$-axis. <Momentum conservation> gives
$$
\mathbf v_1=u(\cos30^\circ,\sin30^\circ),\qquad
\mathbf v_2=\frac12\bigl(V-u\cos30^\circ,-u\sin30^\circ\bigr).
$$
Substituting these into <kinetic energy conservation>, $V^2=u^2+2|\mathbf v_2|^2$, gives $3u^2-2Vu\cos30^\circ-V^2=0$. Its positive root is $u=V(\sqrt3+\sqrt{15})/6$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbf v_1=V\left(\frac{1+\sqrt5}{4},\frac{\sqrt3+\sqrt{15}}{12}\right),\qquad
\mathbf v_2=V\left(\frac{3-\sqrt5}{8},-\frac{\sqrt3+\sqrt{15}}{24}\right).}
$$
These vectors satisfy $\mathbf v_1+2\mathbf v_2=(V,0)$ and $|\mathbf v_1|^2+2|\mathbf v_2|^2=V^2$. For the reflected deflection, reverse both $y$ components.

== 10
{scope}

=== Solution

<Einstein's postulates> state that the laws of physics have the same form in every <inertial frame>, and that light in vacuum has the same <speed of light> $c$ in every <inertial frame>, independently of the motion of its source. For a primed frame moving with <velocity> $v\mathbf e_x$ relative to an unprimed frame, with coincident origins at $t=t'=0$, the <Lorentz transformations> are
$$
\boxed{x'=\gamma(x-vt),\quad t'=\gamma(t-vx/c^2),\quad y'=y,\quad z'=z,\qquad
\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}.}
$$
The following <worldlines> use Earth-frame time in years and distance in light years, so numerically $c=1$. The instantaneous turnaround is the idealized limit of a short acceleration stage; the elapsed <proper time> is calculated along each inertial segment.

\Image[2017-resit-twin-worldlines.png]
{title=Earth-frame worldlines and clock readings for the twins' reunion}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Alison reaches the station at $t_1=1/(5/13)=13/5$ years. Her return <worldline> is
$$
x_A=1-\frac{12}{13}\left(t-\frac{13}{5}\right)=\frac{17}{5}-\frac{12t}{13},
$$
while Belinda's is $x_B=-5t/13$. Equating them gives
$$
\boxed{T=\frac{221}{35}\,\mathrm{yr}\simeq6.3143\,\mathrm{yr},\qquad
x_{\rm meeting}=-\frac{17}{7}\,\mathrm{ly}.}
$$
This is the elapsed Earth-frame time, measurable by synchronized clocks in that <inertial frame>. A signal announcing the reunion reaches a clock physically on Earth later; its light-travel delay is not part of the requested frame-time comparison.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Belinda remains in one <inertial frame> with $v=5c/13$, so the <time dilation> factor is $\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}=12/13$. Her elapsed <proper time> is
$$
\boxed{\tau_B=\frac{12}{13}T=\frac{204}{35}\,\mathrm{yr}\simeq5.8286\,\mathrm{yr}.}
$$
The reunion event is shared by both twins, making their clock readings directly comparable without any convention for synchronizing separated moving clocks.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Alison's outbound <proper time> is $(13/5)(12/13)=12/5$ years. Her return Earth-frame duration is $T-13/5=26/7$ years, with <time dilation> factor $\sqrt{1-(12/13)^2}=5/13$. Adding the two segments,
$$
\boxed{\tau_A=\frac{12}{5}+\frac{26}{7}\frac5{13}
=\frac{134}{35}\,\mathrm{yr}\simeq3.8286\,\mathrm{yr}.}
$$
**Alison is two years younger than Belinda at reunion.** Her turnaround changes <inertial frames>, and the faster return segment accumulates less <proper time> per unit Earth-frame time. The <twin paradox> is resolved by comparing the complete <worldlines>, not by applying one constant <time dilation> factor to Alison's whole journey.

== 11
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For a planar lamina in the $xy$-plane and three mutually perpendicular axes through one common point, the <perpendicular axis theorem> is $I_z=I_x+I_y$. Indeed $I_z=\int(x^2+y^2)\,dm$, while $I_x=\int y^2\,dm$ and $I_y=\int x^2\,dm$. Planarity and the common axis intersection are essential.

For two parallel axes, one through the <centre of mass> $G$ and one a perpendicular distance $d$ away, the <parallel axis theorem> is
$$
\boxed{I=I_G+Md^2,}
$$
where $M$ is the total <mass>. Expanding the squared distance in the defining <moment of inertia> integral leaves a cross term proportional to $\int\mathbf r_G\,dm=0$. The second axis need not pass through the body, and uniform <density> is not required, but the reference axis for $I_G$ must pass through its <centre of mass>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For a uniform disk, the surface <mass density> is $\sigma=m/(\pi a^2)$. Integrate concentric annuli of <mass> $dm=2\pi\sigma r\,dr$ about the central perpendicular axis:
$$
I_O=\int_0^a r^2(2\pi\sigma r\,dr)
=\frac{\pi\sigma a^4}{2}
=\boxed{\frac12ma^2.}
$$
The result is specific to a uniform thin disk and that axis; it is not the diameter-axis <moment of inertia>, which is $ma^2/4$ by planar symmetry and the <perpendicular axis theorem>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $O$ be the original disk centre and $C$ the hole centre. The source geometry has $OS=a$, $OC=CS=a/2$, all collinear. The removed disk has <mass> $m/4$, since its <area> is one quarter of the original disk's. Use the <parallel axis theorem> for both the original disk and the removed disk:
$$
I_S^{\rm full}=\frac12ma^2+ma^2=\frac32ma^2,
\qquad
I_S^{\rm hole}=\frac12\frac m4\left(\frac a2\right)^2+\frac m4\left(\frac a2\right)^2
=\frac{3ma^2}{32}.
$$
Subtracting the missing material's <moment of inertia> gives
$$
\boxed{I_S=\frac{45ma^2}{32}.}
$$
Here $m$ continues to denote the original unperforated disk <mass>; the earring itself has <mass> $3m/4$.

\Image[2017-resit-perforated-pendulum.png]
{title=Equilibrium geometry and mass subtraction for a perforated-disk physical pendulum}

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Take the positive coordinate from $O$ toward $S$. The <centre of mass> of the remaining material has coordinate
$$
x_X=\frac{m(0)-(m/4)(a/2)}{3m/4}=-\frac a6.
$$
Thus $SX=a+a/6=7a/6$. If $\phi$ is the small angular displacement from the stable hanging orientation, gravity gives <torque> $-(3m/4)g(7a/6)\sin\phi$ about the suspension point. The <physical pendulum> equation is
$$
I_S\ddot\phi=-\frac{7mga}{8}\sin\phi.
$$
Using the <small-angle approximation> $\sin\phi\simeq\phi$ gives <simple harmonic motion> with
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=\frac{(3m/4)g(7a/6)}{45ma^2/32}
=\frac{28g}{45a},\qquad \omega=\sqrt{\frac{28g}{45a}}.}
$$
Both the reduced <mass> and displaced <centre of mass> matter; changing only the <moment of inertia> would give an incorrect <frequency>.
