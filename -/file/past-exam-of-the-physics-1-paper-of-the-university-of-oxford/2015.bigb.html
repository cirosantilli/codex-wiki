== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

Take the launch point as $(x,y)=(0,0)$ and ignore air <drag>. In <projectile motion> with constant <gravitational acceleration> $g$, a launch at <angle> $\theta$ above the horizontal gives
$$
x=v_0t\cos\theta,\qquad y=v_0t\sin\theta-\frac12gt^2.
$$
At the wall $x=l$, the height is
$$
y(l)=l\tan\theta-\frac{gl^2}{2v_0^2}\sec^2\theta
=lq-\frac{gl^2}{2v_0^2}(1+q^2),\qquad q=\tan\theta.
$$
This is a concave <quadratic polynomial> in $q$, so its unique maximum satisfies $l-gl^2q/v_0^2=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\theta_{\max}=\arctan\frac{v_0^2}{gl},\qquad y_{\max}(l)=\frac{v_0^2}{2g}-\frac{gl^2}{2v_0^2}.}
$$
For $l=v_0^2/g$, the best trajectory has $\theta=45^\circ$ and reaches the wall at launch height. For $l>v_0^2/g$, the mathematical maximum height is negative. If the ball must stay above level ground, it lands before reaching the wall: $v_0^2/g$ is the maximum same-level <projectile range>. If there is no intervening ground and the wall extends below the launch height, the same optimum still applies, but its impact point is below that height.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

Idealize the pole and stick as having negligible transverse thickness. Put the pole at the origin, the stick initially along the $y$ axis, and the struck lower end at $y=-l/2$, as in the source diagram. A short horizontal <impulse> $J$ along $+x$ gives the <centre of mass> a constant <speed> $V=J/m$. The <angular impulse> about the centre is $Jl/2$, giving constant counterclockwise <angular velocity>
$$
\Omega=\frac{Jl}{2I}=\frac{J}{2\beta ml},\qquad I=\beta ml^2.
$$
The pole exerts no further <force> while the stick moves clear of it. Let $\psi=\Omega t$. The trailing upper end has coordinates
$$
x_{\mathrm{end}}=Vt-\frac l2\sin\psi,\qquad y_{\mathrm{end}}=\frac l2\cos\psi.
$$
The pole can lie on the translating straight stick again only when the stick becomes horizontal: for other orientations, its intersection with $y=0$ is the moving midpoint $x=Vt>0$. At the first horizontal orientation, $\psi=\pi/2$, the trailing end is at
$$
x_{\mathrm{end}}=\frac{\pi V}{2\Omega}-\frac l2=\pi\beta l-\frac l2.
$$
Consequently the <grazing clearance of an impulsively struck rod> occurs at
$$
\boxed{\beta_{\mathrm{crit}}=\frac1{2\pi}\simeq0.15915.}
$$
For $\beta>1/(2\pi)$ the whole stick has passed the pole by its first horizontal orientation, and the continually advancing centre makes all later passes safe. Equality is the limiting grazing contact of the endpoint; strict avoidance requires the strict inequality. If $\beta<1/(2\pi)$, the pole lies within the stick at that first horizontal pass, so contact occurs before the freely moving trailing end can pass the pole. A uniform thin stick has $\beta=1/12$, below the threshold: sufficient <mass> near the ends is needed to increase its <moment of inertia> and reduce the rotation relative to translation.

== 3
{scope}

Take $M$ as the constant <mass> of the sled and rider when snow is immediately removed. Assume the falling snow has zero horizontal <velocity> in the ice frame, and its arrival rate $\sigma$ is the specified constant <mass> flux onto the sled. In a <snow-collecting sled>, horizontal <momentum flux>, including the departing snow, determines the change in sled <momentum>.

=== A
{scope}
{c}

==== Solution

Snow leaves perpendicular to the motion in the rider's frame, so its outgoing horizontal <velocity> in the ice frame is $v$. During $dt$, incoming snow brings zero horizontal <momentum> and outgoing snow carries $\sigma v\,dt$. Thus
$$
M\dot v=-\sigma v,
\qquad
\boxed{v_A(t)=v_0e^{-\sigma t/M}.}
$$
Although the sled does not retain snow, it must continually supply the snow's departing horizontal <momentum>.

=== B
{scope}
{c}

==== Solution

Snow now leaves with zero horizontal <velocity> in the ice frame, the same as its incoming horizontal <velocity>. The net horizontal <momentum flux> is zero:
$$
M\dot v=0,\qquad\boxed{v_B(t)=v_0.}
$$
Any forward <momentum> given to the snow on capture is returned to the sled while sweeping it off. “Perpendicular” specifies a different outgoing <velocity> here than in case A; changing the reference frame without transforming that <velocity> would miss this difference.

=== C
{scope}
{c}

==== Solution

The snow remains aboard, so the moving <mass> grows as $M+\sigma t$. Incoming snow contributes no horizontal <momentum>, and there is no horizontal external <force>. Therefore
$$
\frac d{dt}\bigl[(M+\sigma t)v\bigr]=0,
\qquad
\boxed{v_C(t)=\frac{Mv_0}{M+\sigma t}.}
$$
The combined sled–snow system follows a <variable-mass system> balance; setting $(M+\sigma t)\dot v=0$ would omit the incoming <mass> term.

== 4
{scope}

Use a circular <Earth> orbit of radius $a$ about the <Sun>, neglect Earth's rotation, the other planets and atmospheric <drag>, and treat the launch as an initial impulse followed by unpowered flight of constant <mass>. Define
$$
v_E=\sqrt{\frac{GM_\odot}{a}},\qquad v_{e,E}=\sqrt{\frac{2GM_E}{R_E}}.
$$
In the <patched conic approximation>, a surface launch at <speed> $u$ leaves Earth's gravitational field with Earth-relative <hyperbolic excess speed> $w$, where
$$
u^2=v_{e,E}^2+w^2.
$$
Thus the necessary solar-orbit change must be supplied in addition to escaping Earth's gravity; simply adding the two escape speeds is incorrect. The asymptotic departure direction can be chosen to minimize $w$. Representative values are $v_E\simeq29.78\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and $v_{e,E}\simeq11.186\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$.<Earth Fact Sheet by National Aeronautics and Space Administration>[Orbital parameters: mean orbital velocity 29.78 km/s; bulk parameters: escape velocity 11.186 km/s]{ref}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

To escape the <Solar System> in the single-Sun approximation, the heliocentric <specific orbital energy> must reach zero. At Earth's orbital radius, the required heliocentric <escape speed> is $\sqrt2\,v_E$. The smallest change from Earth's initial <velocity> is prograde, parallel to its motion:
$$
w_{\mathrm{escape}}=(\sqrt2-1)v_E.
$$
Combining the terrestrial and solar <energy> requirements gives
$$
\boxed{u_{\mathrm{escape,min}}=\sqrt{v_{e,E}^2+(\sqrt2-1)^2v_E^2}\simeq16.65\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The asymptotic heliocentric trajectory is a <parabolic Kepler orbit>; larger launch <speeds> can give positive solar <orbital energy>.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

**Direct fall towards a point Sun.** If the <Sun> is treated as a point target, the projectile must lose its heliocentric <angular momentum>. The cheapest change is retrograde, cancelling Earth's tangential orbital <velocity>. It then starts its radial fall with zero heliocentric <speed>, so $w=v_E$ and
$$
\boxed{u_{\mathrm{radial\ fall}}=\sqrt{v_{e,E}^2+v_E^2}\simeq31.81\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
This is the usual radial-fall estimate. Merely launching towards the <Sun> while retaining Earth's tangential <velocity> does not remove the <angular momentum> barrier.

**Minimum for impact on a finite Sun.** A literal collision with the solar surface does not require zero <angular momentum>. The limiting <solar impact by a retrograde launch> instead has <apocentre> $a$ at Earth's orbit and <pericentre> $R_\odot$ at the solar surface. The <vis-viva equation> with <semi-major axis> $(a+R_\odot)/2$ gives the heliocentric departure <speed>
$$
v_a=v_E\sqrt{\frac{2R_\odot}{a+R_\odot}}.
$$
It remains prograde about the <Sun>, but the Earth-relative departure is retrograde with $w=v_E-v_a$. Therefore the surface launch <speed> for grazing the finite solar surface is
$$
\boxed{u_{\mathrm{impact,min}}=\sqrt{v_{e,E}^2+v_E^2\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{2R_\odot}{a+R_\odot}}\right)^2}\simeq29.15\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
Here $a\simeq149.598\times10^6\,\mathrm{km}$ and $R_\odot\simeq695700\,\mathrm{km}$.<Earth Fact Sheet by National Aeronautics and Space Administration>[Orbital parameters: semimajor axis 149.598 million km]{ref}<Sun Fact Sheet by National Aeronautics and Space Administration>[Bulk parameters: volumetric mean radius 695700 km]{ref} Slightly reducing the departure <speed> below $v_a$ makes the trajectory enter the solar surface rather than just graze it. The $31.81$ and $29.15\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ values correspond to the explicitly different point-target and finite-radius idealizations, not to two ways of adding escape speeds. These are direct-flight estimates; planetary gravity assists and Earth's rotation are outside the model.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

Let the projectile's initial laboratory <velocity> be $u\hat{\mathbf x}$. The <centre-of-momentum frame> moves at
$$
V=\frac{Mu}{M+m}.
$$
In that frame, the projectile's initial <speed> is $w=u-V=mu/(M+m)$. An <elastic collision> preserves that <speed>, but can change its direction. Transforming the outgoing projectile <velocity> back by <Galilean velocity addition> gives
$$
\mathbf u_{\mathrm{out}}=V\hat{\mathbf x}+w\mathbf n,\qquad |\mathbf n|=1.
$$
This is the <velocity-circle construction for elastic scattering>: the outgoing tip lies on a <circle> of radius $w$, centred a distance $V$ from the origin in the velocity plane. Since $M>m$, $V>w$ and the origin lies outside this circle. The largest <scattering angle> occurs when the ray from the origin is tangent to it. The resulting <right triangle> gives
$$
\boxed{\sin\theta_{\max}=\frac wV=\frac mM,\qquad 0\leq\theta\leq\arcsin\frac mM.}
$$
Equality is kinematically possible; in the <centre-of-momentum frame> it corresponds to $\cos\chi=-w/V=-m/M$, where $\chi$ is the outgoing projectile <angle> to its initial direction. In particular, a heavier projectile cannot reverse its laboratory direction in an isolated <elastic collision> with an initially stationary lighter target.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

Align both frames' $x$ axes with the motion and make their origins coincide at $t=t'=0$. With $S'$ moving along $+x$ at <speed> $v_2$ relative to $S$, the <Lorentz transformation> and its inverse are
$$
x'=\gamma_2(x-v_2t),\quad t'=\gamma_2\left(t-\frac{v_2x}{c^2}\right),\qquad
x=\gamma_2(x'+v_2t'),\quad t=\gamma_2\left(t'+\frac{v_2x'}{c^2}\right),
$$
where $\gamma_2=(1-v_2^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$; the transverse coordinates are unchanged. Along the object's path, $dx'/dt'=v_1$. Taking differentials of the inverse transformation gives
$$
\frac{dx}{dt}=\frac{\gamma_2(dx'+v_2dt')}{\gamma_2(dt'+v_2dx'/c^2)},
\qquad
\boxed{v=\frac{v_1+v_2}{1+v_1v_2/c^2}.}
$$
This is <relativistic velocity addition>. For $v_1,v_2\ll c$ it reduces to the Galilean sum; if $v_1=c$, the result is still $c$. Two subluminal positive <speeds> give a subluminal result.

== 7
{scope}

=== Solution

Work in the parent's <rest frame>, with $M_A>0$. <Four-momentum conservation> gives
$$
E_B+E_C=M_Ac^2,\qquad \mathbf p_B=-\mathbf p_C,
$$
so both daughter <momenta> have magnitude $p$. Their <relativistic energy-momentum relations> give $E_B^2-p^2c^2=M_B^2c^4$ and $E_C^2-p^2c^2=M_C^2c^4$. Subtracting these two relations and using the total <energy> yields
$$
\boxed{E_B=\frac{M_A^2+M_B^2-M_C^2}{2M_A}c^2,\qquad
E_C=\frac{M_A^2+M_C^2-M_B^2}{2M_A}c^2.}
$$
Substitution back gives the <relativistic two-body decay> momentum:
$$
\boxed{p=\frac{c}{2M_A}\sqrt{\bigl[M_A^2-(M_B+M_C)^2\bigr]\bigl[M_A^2-(M_B-M_C)^2\bigr]},\qquad
\mathbf p_B=p\mathbf n,\quad\mathbf p_C=-p\mathbf n.}
$$
The unit <vector> $\mathbf n$ is not fixed by <four-momentum conservation> alone. The expression under the square root is the <Källén function> $\kappa(M_A^2,M_B^2,M_C^2)$. The physical <two-body decay threshold> is $M_A\geq M_B+M_C$; for massive daughters equality gives zero outgoing <momentum>. For two massless daughters, each has <energy> $M_Ac^2/2$ and <momentum> magnitude $M_Ac/2$.

== 8
{scope}

=== Solution

Let $x_0$ be the equilibrium, so $V'(x_0)=0$. For $\xi=x-x_0$, the <Taylor expansion> of the potential gives
$$
V(x)=V(x_0)+\frac12V''(x_0)\xi^2+O(\xi^3),\qquad
m\ddot\xi=-V''(x_0)\xi+O(\xi^2).
$$
An ordinary small-amplitude <simple harmonic motion> therefore requires a nondegenerate local minimum, $V''(x_0)>0$, with the quadratic term dominating the higher terms over the excursion. Then
$$
\boxed{\omega_0^2=\frac{V''(x_0)}m,\qquad f_0=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{V''(x_0)}m}.}
$$
A local minimum with $V''(x_0)=0$, such as a quartic minimum, need not give small-amplitude harmonic motion with a nonzero limiting <angular frequency>. If $V''(x_0)<0$, the equilibrium is unstable rather than oscillatory.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <central force> confines nonradial motion to a <plane>. In <plane polar coordinates>, the <Lagrangian> for $V(r)=\beta r^k$ is
$$
\mathcal L=\frac12m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\phi^2)-\beta r^k.
$$
The <azimuth> $\phi$ is a <cyclic coordinate>, so $L=mr^2\dot\phi$ is conserved <angular momentum>. The Lagrangian has no explicit <time> dependence, giving conserved <energy>
$$
\boxed{E=\frac12m\dot r^2+\frac{L^2}{2mr^2}+\beta r^k=\frac12m\dot r^2+V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r),\qquad
V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)=\frac{L^2}{2mr^2}+\beta r^k.}
$$
At fixed $L$, the radial <equation of motion> is $m\ddot r=-V_{\mathrm{eff}}'(r)$. The <effective potential> packages the transverse <kinetic energy> as a centrifugal term alongside the actual <potential energy>. It gives a one-dimensional radial problem without introducing an extra physical interaction.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

A <circular orbit> of radius $r_0>0$ is a stationary point of the <effective potential>:
$$
0=-\frac{L^2}{mr_0^3}+\beta k r_0^{k-1}.
$$
For $L\ne0$, $\beta k>0$ and $k\ne-2$, this gives
$$
\boxed{r_0=\left(\frac{L^2}{m\beta k}\right)^{1/(k+2)}.}
$$
The condition $\beta k>0$ makes the radial <force> attractive. At $k=-2$, the equation instead requires the fine tuning $L^2/m=-2\beta$; then the centrifugal and potential terms cancel at every radius and there is no isolated restoring minimum. If that equality fails, there is no positive-radius <circular orbit> for this special power.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $r=r_0+\eta$ and keep $L$ fixed. The <small radial oscillations in a power-law potential> have squared <angular frequency>
$$
\omega_r^2=\frac{V_{\mathrm{eff}}''(r_0)}m
=\frac{3L^2}{m^2r_0^4}+\frac{\beta k(k-1)}m r_0^{k-2}
=\frac{\beta k(k+2)}m r_0^{k-2}.
$$
The circular orbital <angular frequency> has magnitude $\Omega=|L|/(mr_0^2)$, so $\Omega^2=(\beta k/m)r_0^{k-2}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\omega_r=\Omega\sqrt{k+2},\qquad\frac{\omega_r}{\Omega}=\sqrt{k+2},\qquad
f_r=\frac{\Omega}{2\pi}\sqrt{k+2}.}
$$
A restoring radial oscillation exists for $k>-2$, subject to the attractive-force condition in part (b). For $k<-2$ the radial stationary point is unstable; $k=-2$ is the degenerate boundary. This agrees with <circular-orbit stability for a power-law central force>, since the actual force is proportional to $-r^{k-1}$.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The two positive powers below require $\beta>0$; the negative power requires $\beta<0$, so that the <force> is attractive. To leading order, $r\simeq r_0[1+\epsilon\cos(\sqrt{k+2}\,\phi+\delta)]$. The azimuthal advance from one radial maximum to the next is
$$
\Delta\phi=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{k+2}}.
$$
For $k=2$, $\omega_r/\Omega=2$: there are two radial oscillations per revolution. The <isotropic harmonic oscillator> has orbits that are exact <ellipses> centred on the force centre, and the perturbed circle is such an <ellipse>.

For $k=7$, $\omega_r/\Omega=3$: the leading perturbation has three radial lobes per revolution, with consecutive radial maxima separated by $2\pi/3$. Its small-amplitude sketch is a rounded three-lobed orbit.

For $k=-7/4$, $\omega_r/\Omega=1/2$: the radius completes one oscillation while the body makes two revolutions. The leading curve closes after $\Delta\phi=4\pi$, with a double-wound, generally self-crossing shape; the nearest and furthest points occur in the same spatial direction one revolution apart.

The rational ratios for $k=7$ and $k=-7/4$ describe closure of the linearized near-circular curves. They do not imply that every finite-amplitude orbit closes exactly: higher-order changes in the radial-to-orbital frequency ratio generally produce <apsidal precession>. <Bertrand's theorem> identifies the harmonic potential $k=2$ and the Kepler potential $k=-1$ as the exceptional attractive central potentials with all nearby bounded orbits closed.

== 9
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The lines of centres from either lower cylinder to the upper cylinder make $60^\circ$ with the horizontal. Let $N_L,N_R$ be the upward <normal forces> on the top cylinder from the left and right cylinders, and let $Q$ be the lower cylinders' mutual normal <force>. While all three contacts remain closed, the triangular configuration translates horizontally with common <acceleration> $a$; frictionless normals exert no <torques> about the cylinder centres.

For the top cylinder, <Newton's second law> gives
$$
\frac{N_L-N_R}{2}=ma,\qquad\frac{\sqrt3}{2}(N_L+N_R)=mg.
$$
Thus $N_L=m(g/\sqrt3+a)$ and $N_R=m(g/\sqrt3-a)$. The right lower cylinder has horizontal equation $N_R/2+Q=ma$, so
$$
Q=\frac m2\left(3a-\frac g{\sqrt3}\right).
$$
A contact may push but cannot pull: $N_L,N_R,Q\geq0$. The limiting inequalities are therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{g}{3\sqrt3}\leq a\leq\frac g{\sqrt3}.}
$$
At the lower bound, $Q=0$ and the lower pair are just losing compression; at the upper bound, $N_R=0$ and the upper-right contact is just losing compression. All three pairwise normal <forces> are strictly positive only inside the open interval. The ground normal <forces> are positive throughout this range. The applied horizontal <force> on the left cylinder is $F=3ma$, as follows from the whole system's horizontal <momentum> balance.

=== b
{scope}

Assume the cylinders are released from rest. In each case, “there is friction” is interpreted as sufficient <static friction> for <rolling without slipping> at the rough contacts. Without a coefficient or a no-slip assumption, sliding cases would not have a unique specified acceleration. On release the two lower cylinders separate; their mutual contact must not be retained as a rigid constraint.

Let the top cylinder's initial downward <acceleration> be $w$, and each lower cylinder's outward horizontal <acceleration> be $u$. The top cylinder initially has no horizontal <acceleration>. Differentiating the fixed upper-to-lower centre separation twice at the initial zero <velocity> gives
$$
\boxed{u=\sqrt3\,w.}
$$
By reflection symmetry the top cylinder has zero <angular acceleration>, while the lower cylinders rotate in opposite senses.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

**Rough ground, smooth cylinder contacts.** Let $N$ be either upper-to-lower normal <force>, and $f_g$ the magnitude of the inward ground <friction> <force> on each lower cylinder. Pure rolling on the ground requires $|\alpha|=u/R$. The <torque> equation about a lower cylinder's centre is
$$
f_gR=(\beta mR^2)\frac uR,\qquad f_g=\beta mu.
$$
The outward translation equation is $mu=N/2-f_g$, giving $N=2m(1+\beta)u$. The top cylinder's vertical equation is $mw=mg-\sqrt3N$. Using $u=\sqrt3w$ yields
$$
(7+6\beta)w=g,
\qquad
\boxed{a_{\mathrm{top,down}}=\frac{g}{7+6\beta}.}
$$
For uniform solid cylinders, $\beta=1/2$ and $a_{\mathrm{top,down}}=g/10$. The left lower cylinder rotates counterclockwise while rolling left; the right rotates clockwise while rolling right.

The assumed no-slip ground contact is possible if its <coefficient of static friction> satisfies $\mu_g\geq\beta\sqrt3/(10+9\beta)$ at release. This follows from $f_g=\beta m\sqrt3w$ and ground normal <force> $N_g=mg+\sqrt3N/2=mw(10+9\beta)$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

**Smooth ground, rough cylinder contacts.** Write $f$ for the tangential contact <force> magnitude at each upper-to-lower contact. On the top cylinder, its tangential components point upward and outward, while their horizontal components and <torques> cancel by symmetry. For a lower cylinder, the tangential <force> has an inward horizontal component $\sqrt3f/2$ and a downward vertical component $f/2$.

The rolling condition is now at the contact between cylinders, not at the ground. At the initial zero <velocity>, equality of the tangential contact-point <accelerations> gives
$$
R|\alpha|=\frac{\sqrt3u+w}{2}=2w.
$$
Thus $fR=\beta mR^2|\alpha|$ gives $f=2\beta mw$. The lower cylinder's outward translation equation and the top cylinder's downward equation are
$$
mu=\frac N2-\frac{\sqrt3}2f,\qquad mw=mg-\sqrt3N-f.
$$
Using $u=\sqrt3w$ gives $N=2\sqrt3m(1+\beta)w$ and hence
$$
(7+8\beta)w=g,
\qquad
\boxed{a_{\mathrm{top,down}}=\frac{g}{7+8\beta}.}
$$
For solid cylinders this is $g/11$. Here the left lower cylinder rotates clockwise while moving left, and the right rotates counterclockwise while moving right. They may slip freely on the smooth ground, so imposing the ground-rolling relation $R|\alpha|=u$ would be incorrect. No slip at the cylinder contacts requires $\mu_c\geq f/N=\beta/[\sqrt3(1+\beta)]$ initially.

These are the two cases of the <initial acceleration of three stacked cylinders>. As a check, as $\beta\to0$ the rotational contribution disappears in both formulas and they approach the smooth-contact release result $w=g/7$.

== 10
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let the pivot's prescribed horizontal <position> be $x(t)$ and measure $\theta$ from the downward vertical. The bob's coordinates are $X=x+l\sin\theta$ and $Y=-l\cos\theta$. Therefore
$$
\dot X^2+\dot Y^2=\dot x^2+2l\dot x\dot\theta\cos\theta+l^2\dot\theta^2.
$$
With zero <gravitational potential energy> at pivot height, the bob's <Lagrangian> is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L=\frac m2\bigl(\dot x^2+2l\dot x\dot\theta\cos\theta+l^2\dot\theta^2\bigr)+mgl\cos\theta.}
$$
Here $x(t)$ is a prescribed input, not yet a free coordinate. Terms depending only on that prescribed <time> function can be dropped without changing the $\theta$ equation. The moving block's own <kinetic energy> becomes relevant when its motion is free in part (d).

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Euler-Lagrange equation> for $\theta$ is
$$
l\ddot\theta+\ddot x\cos\theta+g\sin\theta=0.
$$
For small <angles>, set $\cos\theta\simeq1$ and $\sin\theta\simeq\theta$. With $x=A\cos\omega t$ and $\omega_0^2=g/l$, the <harmonic forcing of a horizontally driven pendulum> obeys
$$
\ddot\theta+\omega_0^2\theta=\frac{A\omega^2}{l}\cos\omega t.
$$
For $\omega\ne\omega_0$, its general solution is
$$
\boxed{\theta(t)=C\cos\omega_0t+D\sin\omega_0t+\frac{A\omega^2}{l(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)}\cos\omega t.}
$$
The constants $C,D$ set the initial <angle> and <angular velocity>. If the bob starts vertical with zero angular <velocity> at $t=0$, this reduces to
$$
\theta(t)=\frac{A\omega^2}{l(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)}\bigl(\cos\omega t-\cos\omega_0t\bigr).
$$
The solution remains an approximation only while the resulting <angle> is small.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

As $\omega$ approaches $\omega_0=\sqrt{g/l}$, the driving motion couples resonantly to the pendulum. The particular-solution amplitude has denominator $\omega_0^2-\omega^2$, so a steady harmonic-response formula alone is singular. For a specified finite initial state, the near-resonant motion instead develops <beats>, and its exact resonant limit has a growing envelope.

For example, with the vertical zero-angular-velocity initial condition above, taking the limit gives
$$
\boxed{\theta_{\mathrm{res}}(t)=\frac{A\omega_0}{2l}\,t\sin\omega_0t.}
$$
The ideal undamped linear theory predicts amplitude growing linearly with <time>, not an infinite displacement at the first instant. In reality the motion leaves the small-angle regime, and <damping> and nonlinear changes of the pendulum's <oscillation period> alter the response. Below and above resonance, the sign of the harmonic response coefficient changes, corresponding to a phase reversal.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

When the block is free, $x$ becomes a dynamical <generalized coordinate> and its <kinetic energy> $M\dot x^2/2$ must be included. Expanding to quadratic order about the downward stationary state and dropping a constant gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L_2=\frac12(M+m)\dot x^2+ml\dot x\dot\theta+\frac12ml^2\dot\theta^2-\frac12mgl\theta^2.}
$$
The coordinate $x$ is cyclic. Its conserved <canonical momentum> is the total horizontal <momentum>,
$$
P=(M+m)\dot x+ml\dot\theta.
$$
The two linear <Euler-Lagrange equations> are
$$
(M+m)\ddot x+ml\ddot\theta=0,\qquad l\ddot\theta+\ddot x+g\theta=0.
$$
Eliminating $\ddot x$ gives
$$
\boxed{\ddot\theta+\Omega^2\theta=0,\qquad \Omega^2=\frac{(M+m)g}{Ml}.}
$$
Thus the <pendulum with a freely sliding support> has a zero-frequency translational mode and an oscillatory mode. With four independent constants $X_c,V,C,D$, the general solution is
$$
\boxed{\theta(t)=C\cos\Omega t+D\sin\Omega t,\qquad
x(t)=X_c+Vt-\frac{ml}{M+m}\theta(t).}
$$
Here $V=P/(M+m)$ and $X_c+Vt$ is the horizontal <centre-of-mass> coordinate to first order, since $X_{\mathrm{CM}}=x+[ml/(M+m)]\theta$. The oscillation is faster than that of a pendulum with a fixed pivot; the limit $M\to\infty$ restores $\Omega\to\sqrt{g/l}$.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Take the natural release interpretation: both block and bob are initially at rest, the block starts at $x_0$, and the bob has small horizontal displacement $\delta=l\theta_0$ relative to the downward line through the pivot. Then $P=0$, $\dot\theta(0)=0$ and
$$
\boxed{\theta(t)=\theta_0\cos\Omega t,\qquad
x(t)=x_0+\frac{m\delta}{M+m}(1-\cos\Omega t).}
$$
The bob's horizontal <position> is
$$
X(t)=x(t)+l\theta(t)=x_0+\frac{m\delta}{M+m}+\frac{M\delta}{M+m}\cos\Omega t.
$$
The block and bob therefore oscillate in opposite phase about the fixed horizontal <centre of mass> $x_0+m\delta/(M+m)$. Their horizontal oscillation amplitudes are $m\delta/(M+m)$ and $M\delta/(M+m)$ respectively. They pass the centre-of-mass line together when the rod is vertical. If the bob were also given an unspecified initial <velocity>, the additional sine and translational terms in part (d) would be needed; the source's displacement-release case selects the cosine solution.

== 11
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the tunnel frame $S$, with the entrance at $x=0$, the far end at $x=L$, and $t=0$ at both runner entry and photon emission. The runner has $x_R=vt$, while the approaching <photon> has $x_\gamma=L-ct$. At detection,
$$
t_D=\frac{L}{c+v},\qquad x_D=\frac{vL}{c+v},
\qquad
\boxed{f=\frac{x_D}{L}=\frac{v}{c+v}=\frac{\beta}{1+\beta},\quad\beta=\frac vc.}
$$
For $0<v<c$, $0<f<1/2$: the photon meets the runner before the runner reaches the tunnel midpoint.

=== b
{scope}

Let $S'$ be the runner's <inertial frame>, with origin at the entry event. The <Lorentz transformation> is
$$
x'=\gamma(x-vt),\qquad t'=\gamma\left(t-\frac{vx}{c^2}\right),\qquad\gamma=(1-\beta^2)^{-1/2}.
$$
The emission event $E=(L,0)$ has coordinates $x'_E=\gamma L$, $t'_E=-\gamma vL/c^2$. It is earlier than entry in $S'$: entry and far-end emission are simultaneous only in the tunnel frame. This is <relativity of simultaneity>, not an extra delay of the light source.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The entrance clock is at $x=0$. Let $G$ be the event on that clock simultaneous with emission in the runner's frame. Since $t'_G=\gamma t_G=t'_E$, its reading is
$$
\boxed{t_G=-\frac{vL}{c^2}.}
$$
This is the tunnel clock's reading, not the runner-frame coordinate time $t'_E=-\gamma vL/c^2$. It is also not a calculation of what a photograph of that distant clock would show, which would require an additional light-travel condition.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

At that simultaneous entrance-clock event, $x_G=0$ and $t_G=-vL/c^2$. Its runner-frame <position> is
$$
\boxed{x'_G=\gamma(0-vt_G)=\gamma\frac{v^2L}{c^2}=\gamma\beta^2L.}
$$
The runner is at $x'=0$, so the entrance is still ahead by distance $\gamma\beta^2L$ at the runner-frame emission time. The simultaneously measured distance between the two tunnel ends is $x'_E-x'_G=\gamma L(1-\beta^2)=L/\gamma$, consistent with <length contraction>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The photon starts at $x'_E=\gamma L$ and travels left to the runner at the fixed <position> $x'=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{d'_\gamma=\gamma L,\qquad \Delta t'_\gamma=\frac{\gamma L}{c}.}
$$
This distance is not the simultaneous contracted tunnel length $L/\gamma$, because the runner is not at the entrance when the photon is emitted in $S'$. Adding the entrance-to-runner gap from part (ii) to $L/\gamma$ indeed gives $\gamma L$.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

During the photon's runner-frame flight, the tunnel entrance moves left by $v\Delta t'_\gamma=\gamma\beta L$. It began ahead of the runner by $\gamma\beta^2L$, so at detection it lies behind by
$$
d'_{\mathrm{in}}=\gamma\beta L-\gamma\beta^2L=\gamma\beta L(1-\beta).
$$
Divide this distance into the tunnel by the simultaneously measured tunnel length $L/\gamma$:
$$
\boxed{f'=\frac{d'_{\mathrm{in}}}{L/\gamma}=\gamma^2\beta(1-\beta)=\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}=f.}
$$
The runner is stationary in $S'$, but their location relative to the moving tunnel still has this fraction. Omitting the initial entrance gap would incorrectly count the runner's progress from an emission event at which they had not yet entered in this frame. These simultaneous positions give the <tunnel photon timing in two inertial frames>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The photon propagates along $-x$ in the tunnel frame and has <energy> $E_\gamma=hc/\lambda_0$ and <momentum> $p_x=-E_\gamma/c$. A <Lorentz transformation> into the approaching runner's frame gives
$$
E'_\gamma=\gamma(E_\gamma-vp_x)=\gamma(1+\beta)E_\gamma.
$$
Since $E'_\gamma=hc/\lambda'$, the approaching-source <relativistic Doppler effect> is
$$
\boxed{\lambda'=\frac{\lambda_0}{\gamma(1+\beta)}=\lambda_0\sqrt{\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}}.}
$$
It is a blueshift. With $q=\lambda'/\lambda_0=500.0/589.1$, solving $q^2=(1-\beta)/(1+\beta)$ gives
$$
\boxed{\beta=\frac{1-q^2}{1+q^2}=\frac{589.1^2-500.0^2}{589.1^2+500.0^2}\simeq0.16253,
\qquad v\simeq4.873\times10^7\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
This shift depends on the relative <velocity> along the light ray, not on the tunnel length or the location of detection.
