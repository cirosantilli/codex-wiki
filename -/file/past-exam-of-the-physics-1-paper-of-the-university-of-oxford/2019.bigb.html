= 1
{parent=2019}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=1}

For a launch angle $\theta$, the <projectile motion> equations give a flight time $2u\sin\theta/g$, a <projectile range> $R=u^2\sin(2\theta)/g$, and an apex height $u^2\sin^2\theta/(2g)$. Thus the ceiling requires $\sin^2\theta\leq2gH/u^2$.

Set $z=\sin^2\theta$. Then $R^2=4u^4z(1-z)/g^2$, which increases up to $z=1/2$. The optimum is therefore $z=\min(1/2,2gH/u^2)$, giving the <projectile range under a ceiling>
$$
\boxed{R_{\max}=\begin{cases}
\sqrt{8H(u^2/g-2H)},&0\leq H\leq u^2/(4g),\\
u^2/g,&H\geq u^2/(4g).
\end{cases}}
$$
The printed expression is valid in the first regime. A higher ceiling leaves the usual $45^\circ$ launch admissible, so it cannot reduce the maximum range. The angle in the ceiling-limited regime is $\arcsin\sqrt{2gH/u^2}$. Equality at the roof is allowed in this ideal geometrical model; if touching the roof is forbidden, the same value is a <supremum> approached from below.

{parent=2019}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2}

Let $\mathbf w=\mathbf v_1-\mathbf v_2$ be the <relative velocity> and $\mathbf V=(M_1\mathbf v_1+M_2\mathbf v_2)/(M_1+M_2)$ the <centre-of-mass velocity>. Inverting gives
$$
\mathbf v_1=\mathbf V+\frac{M_2}{M_1+M_2}\mathbf w,\qquad
\mathbf v_2=\mathbf V-\frac{M_1}{M_1+M_2}\mathbf w.
$$
Substitute into the sum of the two <kinetic energies>. The cross terms cancel, while the coefficient of $|\mathbf w|^2/2$ is the <reduced mass> $\mu=M_1M_2/(M_1+M_2)$. Hence
$$
T=\frac12(M_1+M_2)|\mathbf V|^2+\frac12\mu|\mathbf w|^2.
$$
The first term is translation of the <centre of mass>; the second is the <kinetic energy> measured in the <centre-of-momentum frame>. For an isolated <perfectly inelastic collision>, the bodies finish with a common <velocity>, while their total <momentum> and thus $\mathbf V$ are unchanged. The <kinetic energy> converted to internal forms is therefore
$$
\boxed{T_i-T_f=\frac12\mu v^2,}
$$
where $v$ is their initial relative <speed>. Total <energy> remains conserved; it is the translational-plus-relative <kinetic energy> that decreases.

= Solution

Measure position $x$ along the ideal thin rod from its <centre of mass>. Its perpendicular distance from the inclined axis is $|x|\sin\alpha$, and its <linear mass density> is $M/L$. The <moment of inertia> is therefore
$$
I=\frac ML\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}x^2\sin^2\alpha\,dx
=\boxed{\frac{ML^2}{12}\sin^2\alpha.}
$$
This tends to zero for an axis along an infinitely thin rod and to $ML^2/12$ for a perpendicular central axis. A finite rod thickness would add a contribution absent from the stated thin-rod model.

= b
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The parallel axis through the centre is the rod's own long axis, with zero <moment of inertia> in the thin-rod approximation. By the <parallel axis theorem>, the displaced parallel axis has
$$
\boxed{I_D=0+MD^2=MD^2.}
$$
Equivalently, every <mass> element is the same perpendicular distance $D$ from that axis, so the defining integral immediately gives this result.

{parent=2019}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=4}

Write the incoming particle <speed> as $u>0$, its final signed <speed> as $v$, and the rod's final <centre-of-mass velocity> along the impact direction as $V$. Let $b$ be the signed impact distance from the centre, and choose the sign of the <angular velocity> $\Omega$ so that the contact point's <velocity> component is $V+b\Omega$. The rod's central <moment of inertia> is $I=ML^2/12$.

The <impulse> delivered to the rod is $MV$. Conservation of linear and angular <momentum> gives
$$
u=v+V,\qquad I\Omega=MbV.
$$
Using these in conservation of <kinetic energy>,
$$
u^2=v^2+V^2+\frac IM\Omega^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
V=\frac{2u}{2+Mb^2/I},\qquad
v=\frac{(Mb^2/I)u}{2+Mb^2/I}.
$$
This is the equal-mass instance of the <effective contact mass> calculation. For $u>0$, both final speeds are nonnegative, so equal speeds require $v=V=u/2$. Therefore $Mb^2/I=2$ and
$$
\boxed{|b|=\frac L{\sqrt6}.}
$$
Both symmetric impact points lie on the rod, since $L/\sqrt6<L/2$.

More generally, the relative contact <velocity> immediately afterwards is
$$
v-(V+b\Omega)=u-(2+Mb^2/I)V=-u.
$$
Thus its magnitude equals the incoming relative <speed> $u$ and its direction reverses. At the specified impact point, $V+b\Omega=3u/2$ and $v=u/2$, which makes the reversal particularly explicit.

{parent=2019}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=5}

With metric convention $(+---)$, the invariant interval is $c^2\Delta\tau^2=c^2\Delta t^2-|\Delta\mathbf r|^2$ along an inertially moving clock. For constant <speed> $v$ in the ground frame, $|\Delta\mathbf r|=v\Delta t$, so
$$
\boxed{\Delta t=\gamma\Delta\tau,\qquad\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}.}
$$
The aircraft records <proper time> $\Delta\tau=1000\,\mathrm s$. The ground duration is measured by a network of <clocks> at rest in the ideal ground frame, with <Einstein synchronisation>. Its excess is
$$
\Delta t-\Delta\tau=1000(\gamma-1)\,\mathrm s
\simeq1000\frac{300^2}{2c^2}\,\mathrm s
\simeq5.0\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm s.
$$
Thus **the ground-frame duration is longer by about $0.50\,\mathrm{ns}$**. This is the special-relativistic <time dilation> contribution under the stated constant-speed assumptions; gravitational potential differences and Earth's rotation are outside this model.

{parent=2019}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=6}

First work in the receiver's <rest frame>. If a source recedes at <speed> $\beta c$, two successive emissions separated by source <proper time> $T_0$ occur $\gamma T_0$ apart in this frame. During that interval the source retreats by $\beta c\gamma T_0$, so the arrival interval is $T_R=\gamma T_0(1+\beta)$. The longitudinal <relativistic Doppler effect> is consequently
$$
\boxed{\frac{\nu_R}{\nu_0}=\sqrt{\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}},\qquad
\frac{\lambda_R}{\lambda_0}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}}.}
$$
A negative $\beta$ describes approach and gives a blueshift.

Interpret $633\,\mathrm{nm}$ as the laser's rest-frame wavelength and the stated detector <velocity> as measured in the initial laboratory frame. Choose $+x$ from source to sensor, with detector <velocity> $-0.2c$. The required relative recession <speed> follows from
$$
q=(1000/633)^2,\qquad\beta_{\rm rec}=\frac{q-1}{q+1}=0.4278687\ldots.
$$
By <relativistic velocity addition>, the source <velocity> $\beta_Sc$ in the laboratory obeys $(\beta_S+0.2)/(1+0.2\beta_S)=-\beta_{\rm rec}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\beta_S=-\frac{\beta_{\rm rec}+0.2}{1+0.2\beta_{\rm rec}}=-0.578375\ldots.}
$$
The laser must move **away from the sensor at about $0.5784c$ in that laboratory frame**. Although the sensor moves toward the source's initial location, the retreating source produces the required net redshift. The frame qualification matters: a <velocity> permanently fixed relative to the moving source would specify the Doppler factor already, rather than leave it adjustable.

= Solution
{parent=a}

During $dt$, the remaining rocket has <mass> $M+dM$ and <velocity> $v+dv$, while the expelled positive <mass> $-dM$ has <velocity> $v-u$ to first order. Subtract the initial <momentum> $Mv$ from their final <momentum> to obtain $M\,dv+u\,dM$. Equating this to the external <impulse> gives the <rocket equation>
$$
F=M\dot v+u\dot M.
$$
With $F=-Mg-kMv$ and $\dot M/M=-b$, this reduces to $\dot v+kv=ub-g$. Therefore, for initial <velocity> $v_0$ and $k>0$,
$$
v(t)=\frac{ub-g}{k}+\left(v_0-\frac{ub-g}{k}\right)e^{-kt}.
$$
For a launch from rest,
$$
\boxed{v(t)=\frac{ub-g}{k}(1-e^{-kt}),\qquad b>g/u\text{ for upward launch}.}
$$
The inequality makes the initial upward <acceleration> positive. Equality gives no upward launch from rest. In the zero-drag limit, $v=(ub-g)t$. These expressions apply while the prescribed burn law and constant-$g$ approximation remain valid.

= b
{parent=7}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The escaping sand initially has the bucket's <velocity>, so it produces no relative-velocity thrust. This distinction is essential in a <leaking Atwood machine>. With upward <velocity> $v$ for the leaky side, the two bucket equations are
$$
M\dot v=T-Mg,\qquad M_0\dot v=M_0g-T.
$$
Their sum gives $\dot v=g(M_0-M)/(M+M_0)$. Since $\dot M=-\alpha$, it follows that
$$
\frac{dv}{dM}=\frac g\alpha\frac{M-M_0}{M+M_0}.
$$
Integrate from $M_0$, where the <velocity> is zero:
$$
v(M)=\frac g\alpha\left[M-M_0-2M_0\log\left(\frac{M+M_0}{2M_0}\right)\right].
$$
At emptying, $M=(1-\epsilon)M_0$, giving
$$
\boxed{v_{\rm empty}=\frac{gM_0}{\alpha}\left[-\epsilon-2\log(1-\epsilon/2)\right].}
$$
This is upward and positive for $0<\epsilon<1$. For no initial sand, $\epsilon=0$, the emptying time is zero and the <speed> is zero. As the dry bucket <mass> tends to zero,
$$
\boxed{\lim_{\epsilon\to1^-}v_{\rm empty}=\frac{gM_0}{\alpha}(2\log2-1).}
$$
This finite limit refers to the idealized model; the endpoint itself would describe a massless empty bucket. For small $\epsilon$, $v_{\rm empty}\sim gM_0\epsilon^2/(4\alpha)$, consistent with initially zero <acceleration> that grows as <mass> is lost.

= Solution
{parent=a}

A <central force> exerts zero <torque> about its centre, so <angular momentum> is conserved and the motion is planar. In <plane polar coordinates>, $J=mr^2\dot\theta$ and
$$
T=\frac12m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2),\qquad
\boxed{E=\frac12m\dot r^2+\frac{J^2}{2mr^2}+U(r).}
$$
Use $F=-U'$ and either the radial <Euler-Lagrange equation> or <differentiation> of the <energy> away from <turning points> to get
$$
\boxed{m\ddot r=F(r)+\frac{J^2}{mr^3}.}
$$
The radial <acceleration> is $\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2$, so this is exactly $m(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2)=F(r)$, not an additional physical outward force in an inertial frame. The variational derivation remains valid at <turning points> where dividing an <energy> <derivative> by $\dot r$ would be illegitimate.

= b
{parent=8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <effective potential> includes the angular part of the <kinetic energy>. Taking $U(0)=0$ gives $U=kr^4/4$, hence
$$
U_{\rm eff}(r)=\frac{J^2}{2mr^2}+\frac{k r^4}{4}.
$$
For $J\ne0$, this diverges at both zero and infinity and has a unique minimum. Finite-energy motion therefore remains between two radial <turning points>, or at the minimum itself. For $J=0$, the <centrifugal barrier> is absent, but $U\to\infty$ still bounds every finite-energy trajectory; radial motion can pass through the force centre.

A <circular orbit> requires $U_{\rm eff}'(r_c)=0$, so
$$
\boxed{r_c=\left(\frac{J^2}{mk}\right)^{1/6},\qquad
E_c=\frac34kr_c^4=\frac34 k^{1/3}\left(\frac{J^2}{m}\right)^{2/3}.}
$$
It is stable because $U_{\rm eff}''(r_c)=6kr_c^2>0$. These are nonzero-radius orbits for $J\ne0$; $J=0$ also permits the stationary particle at the origin. Boundedness does not mean that every noncircular orbit is closed.

{parent=8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The diagram places the force centre on the circumference, not at the centre of the circle. Choose the polar axis through the circle's geometric centre. Its equation is $r=2a\cos\theta$, so $u=\sec\theta/(2a)$ and
$$
u''+u=\frac{\sec^3\theta}{a}=8a^2u^3.
$$
Substitute into <Binet's equation>:
$$
F(1/u)=-\frac{J^2u^2}{m}(u''+u)
=-\frac{8J^2a^2}{m}u^5.
$$
Consequently a <circular orbit through the force centre> is generated by
$$
\boxed{n=-5,\qquad C=\frac{8J^2a^2}{m},\qquad F(r)=-\frac{8J^2a^2}{mr^5}.}
$$
The result applies to the regular arc where $r>0$ and $J\ne0$. The force is singular at the point where the geometrical circle meets the origin, so the curve is not a regular periodic orbit continued through that point. Indeed, $U=-C/(4r^4)$ and this trajectory has zero total <energy>, with kinetic and negative potential terms diverging as the centre is approached.

For a <Lagrangian> $L(q_i,\dot q_i,t)$ expressed in independent <generalized coordinates>, stationary <action> gives the <Euler-Lagrange equations>
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}=0.}
$$
Ideal constraints can either be incorporated into the independent coordinates or treated using <Lagrange multipliers>, as in <9/c/solution>.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}

A <cyclic coordinate> is absent from the <Lagrangian> itself: $\partial L/\partial q_i=0$. Its <conjugate momentum> $p_i=\partial L/\partial\dot q_i$ therefore obeys $\dot p_i=0$. In the rolling-wedge calculation, the cyclic horizontal translation gives conservation of the system's horizontal <momentum>.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

A <holonomic constraint> can be expressed as $f(q_1,\ldots,q_n,t)=0$, rather than as a genuinely nonintegrable relation among velocities. A fixed rod length is an example. Rolling along a single straight incline also gives an integrable relation $s+R\theta=\text{constant}$ with the sign convention used below; arbitrary rolling in more dimensions need not be holonomic.

= b
{parent=9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Take $s$ positive uphill, $X$ positive to the right, and the cylinder's rotation positive anticlockwise. Contact with the fixed-angle surface determines its normal position; no slipping gives $\dot\theta=-\dot s/R$. These two constraints leave $X,s$ as the two independent coordinates of the <cylinder rolling on a freely translating wedge>.

The cylinder centre is at $(X+s\cos\alpha-R\sin\alpha,\ s\sin\alpha+R\cos\alpha)$. Since the angle is constant, its squared <speed> is $\dot X^2+2\dot X\dot s\cos\alpha+\dot s^2$. Including the block's <kinetic energy> and $I_c=M_cR^2/2$, and dropping a constant from the potential, gives
$$
\boxed{L=\frac12(M_b+M_c)\dot X^2+M_c\cos\alpha\,\dot X\dot s+\frac34M_c\dot s^2-M_cgs\sin\alpha.}
$$
The <cyclic coordinate> is $X$. Its conserved <conjugate momentum> is $p_X=(M_b+M_c)\dot X+M_c\cos\alpha\dot s$, the total horizontal <momentum>. The two <Euler-Lagrange equations> are
$$
(M_b+M_c)\ddot X+M_c\cos\alpha\ddot s=0,\qquad
\cos\alpha\ddot X+\frac32\ddot s=-g\sin\alpha.
$$
Eliminating the accelerations, with $D=3(M_b+M_c)-2M_c\cos^2\alpha>0$, gives
$$
\boxed{\ddot s=-\frac{2(M_b+M_c)g\sin\alpha}{D},\qquad
\ddot X=\frac{2M_cg\sin\alpha\cos\alpha}{D}.}
$$
Thus the cylinder rolls downhill and the wedge accelerates rightward. For $M_b\gg M_c$, $\ddot X\to0$ and $\ddot s\to-(2/3)g\sin\alpha$, the ordinary fixed-incline result.

= c
{parent=9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

With the wedge fixed, let $r$ be the signed gap between the cylinder bottom and the surface, positive outwards. The centre height is $s\sin\alpha+(R+r)\cos\alpha$. Retaining all three coordinates before imposing constraints gives
$$
L=\frac12M_c(\dot r^2+\dot s^2)+\frac12I_c\dot\theta^2
-M_cg[s\sin\alpha+(R+r)\cos\alpha].
$$
Use the <holonomic constraints> $f_1=r=0$ and $f_2=s+R\theta-s_0=0$. With the convention $d(\partial L/\partial\dot q)/dt-\partial L/\partial q=\sum_j\lambda_j\partial f_j/\partial q$, the <Lagrange multiplier> equations are
$$
M_c\ddot r+M_cg\cos\alpha=\lambda_1,\qquad
M_c\ddot s+M_cg\sin\alpha=\lambda_2,\qquad
I_c\ddot\theta=R\lambda_2.
$$
The constraint gradients identify $\lambda_1=N$, the <normal reaction>, and $\lambda_2=f$, the uphill <static friction> force. The latter supplies the rolling <torque>. Setting $\ddot r=0$ and $\ddot\theta=-\ddot s/R$ yields
$$
\boxed{N=M_cg\cos\alpha,\qquad \ddot s=-\frac23g\sin\alpha,\qquad f=\frac13M_cg\sin\alpha.}
$$
The <static friction> condition $|f|\leq\mu_sN$ therefore becomes
$$
\boxed{\mu_s\geq\frac13\tan\alpha,}
$$
for $0\leq\alpha<\pi/2$. <Static friction> is required to create rotation, but does not dissipate <energy> here because the contact point is instantaneously at rest on the fixed plane.

= Solution
{parent=a}

For a boost along $+x$ at <speed> $v$, the <energy-momentum four-vector> transforms as
$$
\boxed{E'=\gamma(E-vp_x),\quad p_x'=\gamma(p_x-vE/c^2),\quad p_y'=p_y,\quad p_z'=p_z.}
$$
The total four-vector is the sum over the particles. Write $E=\sum_iE_i$ and $\mathbf P=\sum_i\mathbf p_i$. A centre-of-momentum <rest frame> exists when this total is timelike, with $\mathbf V_{\rm CM}=c^2\mathbf P/E$. Boost along $\mathbf P$ and use $P=EV_{\rm CM}/c^2$:
$$
E_{\rm CM}=\gamma_{\rm CM}(E-V_{\rm CM}P)=E/\gamma_{\rm CM}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\gamma_{\rm CM}=\frac{\sum_iE_i}{E_{\rm CM}}=\frac1{\sqrt{1-V_{\rm CM}^2/c^2}}.}
$$
The timelike qualification is needed: exactly parallel <photons> alone have null total <four-momentum> and no such <rest frame>.

= b
{parent=10}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Use the squared total <energy-momentum four-vector>, expressed in energy-squared units. With the <proton> initially at rest,
$$
W^2=(m_pc^2+E_\gamma)^2-E_\gamma^2=m_p^2c^4+2m_pc^2E_\gamma.
$$
At the <reaction threshold>, the outgoing <proton> and <pion> have no relative <kinetic energy> in their <centre-of-momentum frame>, so $W=(m_p+m_\pi)c^2$. Solving gives
$$
\boxed{E_\gamma^{\rm th}=m_\pi c^2\left(1+\frac{m_\pi}{2m_p}\right)=144.715\,\mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
The numerical value uses the supplied $938$ and $135\,\mathrm{MeV}$ <rest energies>. The threshold exceeds the <pion>'s <rest energy> because the outgoing system must carry the incident <photon>'s nonzero laboratory <momentum>.

= c
{parent=10}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

At threshold, the <pion> is at rest in the final <centre-of-momentum frame>, which moves in the laboratory with
$$
\beta=\frac{E_\gamma^{\rm th}}{m_pc^2+E_\gamma^{\rm th}}=0.1336592\ldots,\qquad
\gamma=\frac{m_pc^2+E_\gamma^{\rm th}}{(m_p+m_\pi)c^2}=1.0090539\ldots.
$$
In the <pion>'s <rest frame>, its <two-photon decay> produces opposite <photons> of equal <energy> $m_\pi c^2/2$. The specified forward and backward directions remain collinear under this boost, so
$$
E_\pm=\frac{m_\pi c^2}{2}\gamma(1\pm\beta).
$$
At threshold, $\gamma(1+\beta)=(m_p+m_\pi)/m_p$ and $\gamma(1-\beta)=m_p/(m_p+m_\pi)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{E_+=77.2148\,\mathrm{MeV},\qquad E_-=59.0075\,\mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
The forward <photon> is the more energetic one. Their product remains $(m_\pi c^2/2)^2$, as required by the invariant <mass> of an oppositely directed <photon> pair, while their sum equals the laboratory <pion> <energy>.
