== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

The <parallel axis theorem> relates the <moment of inertia> about an axis through the <centre of mass> to that about a parallel axis displaced by perpendicular distance $d$:
$$
\boxed{I=I_G+Md^2.}
$$
The reference axis must pass through the <centre of mass>. To see why, write the displacement from it as $\mathbf r=\mathbf R+\mathbf d$ in the plane perpendicular to the axes. Expanding $\int|\mathbf R+\mathbf d|^2\,dm$ gives $I_G+Md^2$ because $\int\mathbf R\,dm=0$.

For a thin planar <mass distribution>, let $x,y$ be perpendicular axes in its plane and $z$ the normal axis through **their same intersection**. The <perpendicular axis theorem> states
$$
\boxed{I_z=I_x+I_y.}
$$
Indeed, $I_z=\int(x^2+y^2)\,dm$, while $I_x=\int y^2\,dm$ and $I_y=\int x^2\,dm$. Their common intersection need not be the <centre of mass>, but an arbitrary three-dimensional body does not satisfy the planar equality.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Take the square to have uniform <surface mass density> $\sigma=M/a^2$, and put its centre at $x=y=0$. For an in-plane axis through the centre parallel to a side, direct <integration> gives
$$
I_x=\frac M{a^2}\int_{-a/2}^{a/2}\int_{-a/2}^{a/2}y^2\,dx\,dy=\frac{Ma^2}{12}.
$$
The same holds for $I_y$, and the mixed <integral> $\int xy\,dm$ vanishes by reflection symmetry. The squared perpendicular distance to an axis making angle $\psi$ with the $x$ axis is $(-x\sin\psi+y\cos\psi)^2$. Its <integral> therefore gives
$$
I_\psi=\frac{Ma^2}{12}(\sin^2\psi+\cos^2\psi)=\frac{Ma^2}{12}.
$$
Thus a diagonal through opposite corners and its perpendicular **through the centre** have
$$
\boxed{I_{\rm diagonal}=I_{\rm central\ perpendicular}=\frac{Ma^2}{12}.}
$$
This is the <in-plane inertia isotropy of a square plate>.

**Axis-location qualification:** perpendicularity fixes a direction, not a line's position. If the second axis is instead shifted by distance $d$ from the centre, the <parallel axis theorem> gives $I_2=Ma^2/12+Md^2$. A perpendicular through a corner on that diagonal has $d=a/\sqrt2$ and hence $I_2=7Ma^2/12$, a concrete different value satisfying the stated directional description. If “through the corners” means an edge rather than a diagonal, that edge has $I=Ma^2/3$; two adjacent edges both have that value. The general displaced-axis formula and these choices make the location convention explicit rather than silently assigning a unique value to an unspecified second axis.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use two central perpendicular axes in the plate. The <perpendicular axis theorem> and the preceding <moments of inertia> give
$$
\boxed{I_{z,G}=\frac{Ma^2}{12}+\frac{Ma^2}{12}=\frac{Ma^2}{6}.}
$$
Alternatively, axes along adjacent edges have <moments of inertia> $Ma^2/3$ each. The normal axis through their shared corner therefore has $2Ma^2/3$. It lies a distance $a/\sqrt2$ from the parallel normal axis through the centre, so the <parallel axis theorem> yields $I_{z,G}=2Ma^2/3-M(a^2/2)=Ma^2/6$. This normal central result is independent of the in-plane axis-location ambiguity.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

For the attractive <central force> $\mathbf F=-k\mathbf r$, with $k>0$, the <potential energy> is $U(r)=kr^2/2$ up to an additive constant. Conservation of <angular momentum> confines the motion to a plane; writing its magnitude as $J=mr^2\dot\varphi$, the <kinetic energy> is
$$
T=\frac12m\dot r^2+\frac12mr^2\dot\varphi^2=\frac12m\dot r^2+\frac{J^2}{2mr^2}.
$$
At fixed $J$, the radial <energy> equation becomes $E=m\dot r^2/2+U_{\rm eff}(r)$, where
$$
\boxed{U_{\rm eff}(r)=\frac{J^2}{2mr^2}+\frac12kr^2.}
$$
The <effective potential> combines the true <potential energy> with the angular-motion contribution. Its negative derivative gives the radial equation $m\ddot r=-U_{\rm eff}'=J^2/(mr^3)-kr$; the first term is the centrifugal contribution arising from elimination of the angular coordinate.

A <circular orbit> is a fixed radial position at a stationary point:
$$
\boxed{r_0=\left(\frac{J^2}{mk}\right)^{1/4},\qquad
\Omega=\frac J{mr_0^2}=\sqrt{\frac km}.}
$$
For $J\ne0$, $U_{\rm eff}''(r_0)=4k>0$, so this is a stable minimum. Small radial disturbances have <angular frequency> $2\sqrt{k/m}$. For $J=0$, there is no nonzero-radius <circular orbit>.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

In the <centre-of-mass frame>, the two equal incoming <rest masses> have opposite <momenta> and total <energy> $2E$. The squared total <four-momentum> fixes the composite <invariant mass> $\mathcal M$:
$$
\mathcal M^2c^4=E_{\rm tot}^2-c^2|\mathbf P_{\rm tot}|^2=4E^2,
\qquad \boxed{\mathcal M=\frac{2E}{c^2}.}
$$
A change of <inertial frame>, including going to either particle's rest frame, cannot change this <invariant mass>. It includes the internal <kinetic energy> and is generally larger than $2M_1$.

There is a terminology convention worth making explicit. The older “relativistic mass” means frame-dependent total <energy> divided by $c^2$. To stop particle 1 requires a boost with <Lorentz factor> $\gamma=E/(M_1c^2)$, so the system has total <energy> $2\gamma E=2E^2/(M_1c^2)$ in that frame. Under that alternative convention, its relativistic mass would be $2E^2/(M_1c^4)$. This is not its <invariant mass>; both quantities coincide only when $E=M_1c^2$ and the incoming particles already have zero relative motion.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

<Four-momentum conservation> fixes the outgoing system's <invariant mass> to the same $2E/c^2$. In the rest frame of particle 3, let particle 4 have <energy> $E_4'$ and <momentum> $\mathbf p_4'$. Each outgoing particle has <rest mass> $M_2$, so
$$
4E^2=(M_2c^2+E_4')^2-c^2|\mathbf p_4'|^2
=2M_2^2c^4+2M_2c^2E_4'.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{E_4'=\frac{2E^2}{M_2c^2}-M_2c^2.}
$$
Here $E_4'$ is the total <energy>, including rest energy. At the production threshold $E=M_2c^2$, it reduces to $M_2c^2$, as it should. Production is kinematically possible only for $E\ge M_2c^2$. The initial <rest mass> $M_1$ drops out because the conserved total <centre-of-mass energy>, rather than the identity of the incoming particles, determines this outgoing relative motion.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

Let the source's proper emission period be $T_0$, and let it approach a stationary observer directly with speed $v=\beta c>0$. In the observer's <inertial frame>, consecutive emissions are separated by $\gamma T_0$ through <time dilation>. The second crest starts $v\gamma T_0$ nearer the observer, reducing the arrival interval to
$$
T_{\rm obs}=\gamma T_0(1-\beta).
$$
Since light travels at $c$ in each <inertial frame>, the longitudinal <relativistic Doppler effect> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{\nu_{\rm obs}}{\nu_0}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}},\qquad
\frac{\lambda_{\rm obs}}{\lambda_0}=\sqrt{\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}}.}
$$
Recession reverses the sign of $\beta$. More generally, if $\Theta$ is the angle between the source velocity and the photon propagation direction in the observer's frame, a <Lorentz transformation> of the photon <four-momentum> gives $\nu_0=\gamma\nu_{\rm obs}(1-\beta\cos\Theta)$.

The <common Doppler scaling of spectral-line separations> follows because every line receives the same wavelength factor $D$. The supplied wavelengths have rest separation $50\,\mathrm{nm}$; the measured separation gives $D=51/50=1.02$. Defining $\beta_{\rm rec}>0$ for recession, inversion gives
$$
\boxed{\beta_{\rm rec}=\frac{D^2-1}{D^2+1}=\frac{101}{5101}=0.0198000,\qquad
v_{\rm rec}=5.93590\times10^6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
**Source contradiction:** a strictly approaching source has $D<1$ and must reduce the wavelength separation below $50\,\mathrm{nm}$, so no positive approach speed satisfies the stated $51\,\mathrm{nm}$. For example, approach at $0.02c$ gives a separation of $49.0098\,\mathrm{nm}$. The speed inferred from the numerical data is therefore about $0.0198c$ **receding**; retaining the word “approaching” would require a negative signed approach velocity. The two observed wavelengths consistent with the given separation are $357$ and $408\,\mathrm{nm}$.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

Measure the vertical displacement $x$ downward from the spring's unstretched position. The <kinetic energy> is $m\dot x^2/2$, while the spring and gravitational <potential energies> are $kx^2/2$ and $-mgx$. Thus the <Lagrangian> is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L=\frac12m\dot x^2-\frac12kx^2+mgx.}
$$
Applying the <Euler-Lagrange equation>, $d(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot x)/dt-\partial\mathcal L/\partial x=0$, gives
$$
\boxed{m\ddot x+kx=mg.}
$$
Gravity shifts the equilibrium to $x_*=mg/k$. For displacement $\xi=x-x_*$ from that equilibrium, the <equation of motion> becomes $\ddot\xi+(k/m)\xi=0$, giving <simple harmonic motion> with <angular frequency> $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$. Choosing an upward coordinate instead reverses the gravitational term, but leaves the equilibrium-relative <angular frequency> unchanged.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

Let the initial <velocity> be $\mathbf v$ and the two final <velocities> be $\mathbf u,\mathbf w$. In the stated nonrelativistic <elastic collision>, <conservation of momentum> and <conservation of energy> give
$$
\mathbf v=\mathbf u+\mathbf w,\qquad
v^2=u^2+w^2.
$$
Squaring the first equation and comparing it with the second yields
$$
v^2=u^2+w^2+2\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w,
\qquad \boxed{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w=0.}
$$
If both final <velocities> are nonzero, their directions are perpendicular. If one is zero, the moving particle retains the initial <velocity> and a scattering angle for the stationary particle is undefined. This includes the zero-deflection and head-on exchange limits rather than incorrectly assigning a direction to a zero vector. Equal <masses> and a stationary initial target are essential to the dot-product cancellation.

== 7
{scope}

=== Solution

Use coordinates $s$ down the <inclined plane> and $n$ along its upward normal, with the launch point as origin. Gravity has components $g\sin\phi$ and $-g\cos\phi$. For launch angle $\theta$ above the downhill direction,
$$
s(t)=v_0\cos\theta\,t+\frac12g\sin\phi\,t^2,
\qquad n(t)=v_0\sin\theta\,t-\frac12g\cos\phi\,t^2.
$$
The nonzero root of $n(t)=0$ is $t_f=2v_0\sin\theta/(g\cos\phi)$. Substituting into $s(t)$ and combining the two trigonometric terms gives the distance along the slope:
$$
\boxed{R_{\rm slope}=\frac{2v_0^2\sin\theta\cos(\theta-\phi)}{g\cos^2\phi}.}
$$
This assumes $0\le\phi<\pi/2$, $\sin\theta>0$, and a downhill landing with $\cos(\theta-\phi)>0$, as well as the neglected <air resistance>. It reduces to the horizontal <projectile range> $v_0^2\sin(2\theta)/g$ at $\phi=0$. The angle relative to the horizontal is $\theta-\phi$, which explains the shifted cosine. The original diagram uses stipulated illustrative angles; no examination figure is being reconstructed.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Take $\theta$ from the downward vertical. Under the conventional light-rod idealization, only the endpoint <point mass> contributes appreciably. Its speed is $l\dot\theta$, and its gravitational <potential energy> is $-mgl\cos\theta$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L=\frac12ml^2\dot\theta^2+mgl\cos\theta.}
$$
An additive constant in the <potential energy> has no effect on the <Euler-Lagrange equation>.

**Rod-mass qualification:** “thin” and “uniform” do not imply negligible <mass>. If the uniform rod has a finite unspecified <mass> $M_r$, its pivot <moment of inertia> is $M_rl^2/3$ and its <centre of mass> lies $l/2$ from the pivot. For a <pendulum with a uniform rod and an endpoint mass>, the general answer is
$$
\mathcal L=\frac12I\dot\theta^2+A\cos\theta,\qquad
I=l^2\left(m+\frac{M_r}{3}\right),\qquad
A=gl\left(m+\frac{M_r}{2}\right).
$$
This formula covers either convention without inventing a rod <mass>. A result involving only the specified $m,l,g$ uses $M_r=0$ unless an additional relation for $M_r$ is stated.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For the general <Lagrangian> just obtained, the <Euler-Lagrange equation> gives
$$
I\ddot\theta+A\sin\theta=0.
$$
Using the <small-angle approximation> $\sin\theta\simeq\theta$, this is <simple harmonic motion> with $\omega_0^2=A/I$. Thus
$$
\boxed{T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l(m+M_r/3)}{g(m+M_r/2)}}\quad\longrightarrow\quad
T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac lg}\ \text{for a negligible-mass rod}.}
$$
The endpoint <mass> cancels in the light-rod limit. A concrete comparison shows why the finite rod cannot silently be included: the same specified endpoint $m$ with $M_r=m$ gives $T=2\pi\sqrt{8l/(9g)}$, distinct from the negligible-rod result. This is a <physical pendulum> when the rod contributes, and a <simple pendulum> in the light-rod limit. Both expressions require small oscillation amplitude; the full sine equation remains nonlinear.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Allowing azimuthal motion makes the endpoint speed satisfy $v^2=l^2(\dot\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2)$. The light-rod <spherical pendulum> therefore has
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L=\frac12ml^2\left(\dot\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2\right)+mgl\cos\theta.}
$$
The azimuth $\varphi$ is a <cyclic coordinate>, since the <Lagrangian> is independent of it. Its conjugate <canonical momentum> is the conserved vertical <angular momentum>:
$$
\boxed{p_\varphi=ml^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi=J_z=\text{constant}.}
$$
For a finite uniform rod, replace $ml^2$ by $I$ and $mgl$ by $A$ from <8/a/i>. The same azimuthal symmetry then gives $p_\varphi=I\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi$.

The <Hamiltonian> is the <Legendre transform> of this <Lagrangian>. With $p_\theta=I\dot\theta$,
$$
H=\frac{p_\theta^2}{2I}+\frac{p_\varphi^2}{2I\sin^2\theta}-A\cos\theta
=\frac I2\left(\dot\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2\right)-A\cos\theta.
$$
There is no explicit time dependence, so $dH/dt=\partial H/\partial t=0$. Here $H$ also equals the total mechanical <energy>: the constraint is stationary, the <kinetic energy> is quadratic in generalized velocities, and gravity is conservative. The apparent singularity of the momentum expression at $\sin\theta=0$ is a spherical-coordinate singularity; trajectories reaching a pole must be described consistently, and nonzero $J_z$ prevents reaching it at finite <energy>.

== 9
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $P=m_pc^2$ denote the proton rest energy and $W=220\,\mathrm{GeV}$ the desired particle's rest energy. For an antiproton of total <energy> $E_{\bar p}$ striking a stationary proton, the incoming squared <centre-of-mass energy> is
$$
s=(E_{\bar p}+P)^2-c^2p_{\bar p}^2=2P^2+2PE_{\bar p}.
$$
For production of the stated single particle, <four-momentum conservation> requires $s=W^2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{E_{\bar p}^{\rm fixed}=\frac{W^2-2P^2}{2P}\simeq25.7912\,\mathrm{TeV}.}
$$
The numerical value uses $P=0.938272\,\mathrm{GeV}$. It is a **total** incident <energy>; the corresponding <kinetic energy> is $E_{\bar p}^{\rm fixed}-P\simeq25.7902\,\mathrm{TeV}$. Much of the laboratory <energy> supplies motion of the outgoing system rather than its <invariant mass>.

For equal and opposite beam <momenta>, the laboratory is the <centre-of-mass frame>. Each beam has the same total <energy> and their sum is $W$, giving
$$
\boxed{E_{\bar p}^{\rm collider}=\frac W2=110\,\mathrm{GeV},\qquad
K_{\bar p}^{\rm collider}=110-P\simeq109.062\,\mathrm{GeV}.}
$$
These are the <fixed-target production threshold> and collider kinematic comparison for the specified final state. Extra final particles or restrictions from conserved quantum numbers would require a separately specified reaction.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

In the parent's rest frame, the identical daughters have equal and opposite <momenta>, and each has total <energy> $110\,\mathrm{GeV}$. With daughter rest energy $Q=90\,\mathrm{GeV}$, each speed in this frame is
$$
\beta_*c=c\sqrt{1-\frac{Q^2}{110^2}},\qquad \beta_*=0.5749596.
$$
Transform to the rest frame of one daughter using the collinear <relativistic velocity addition> law. The other daughter then has speed
$$
\boxed{\frac wc=\frac{2\beta_*}{1+\beta_*^2}=\frac{44\sqrt{10}}{161}=0.864225.}
$$
An independent <invariant mass> check is useful. In the rest frame of one daughter the other's total <energy> satisfies $W^2=2Q^2+2QE'$, so $E'=178.8889\,\mathrm{GeV}$ and the relative <Lorentz factor> is $\gamma_{\rm rel}=E'/Q=161/81$. Hence $w/c=\sqrt{1-\gamma_{\rm rel}^{-2}}$, the same result.

The answer is unchanged if the parent is produced by a fixed-target collision. Its laboratory <velocity> then differs, but its rest <mass> and two-body decay kinematics do not. The scalar product of the daughters' <four-momenta>, equivalently their relative <Lorentz factor>, depends only on $W$ and $Q$. A common boost cannot change the speed of one measured in the rest frame of the other.<Relative speed from four-velocity contraction>[Invariant relative Lorentz factor and associated relative speed]{ref}

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

<Conservation of angular momentum> means that the total <angular momentum> of a system about a chosen fixed origin in an <inertial frame> stays constant. Its dynamical condition is
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\mathbf J}{dt}=\boldsymbol\tau_{\rm ext}=0.}
$$
Internal torques cancel for the complete isolated mechanical system. If only the component of external <torque> about an axis vanishes, only that <angular momentum> component is necessarily conserved. The skater's upright spin uses this axial statement: vertical weight and support provide negligible vertical-axis torque, and frictional torque is neglected. The skater need not conserve <rotational kinetic energy> while changing the internal configuration.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The uniform cylindrical torso contributes $I_c=Mr^2/2=50(0.20)^2/2=1.00\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}$. Adding both arm <point masses> about the same vertical axis gives
$$
I_i=1+2(7)(0.70)^2=7.86\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2},\qquad
I_f=1+2(7)(0.30)^2=2.26\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}.
$$
With initial <angular frequency> $\omega_i=2\pi\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$, <conservation of angular momentum> gives $I_i\omega_i=I_f\omega_f$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\omega_f=2\pi\frac{7.86}{2.26}=21.8521\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
This corresponds to $3.47788$ rotations per second; it is important not to confuse that <frequency> with the requested <angular frequency>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Using <rotational kinetic energy> $K=I\omega^2/2$ at the two steady endpoint configurations,
$$
\boxed{K_i=155.150\,\mathrm J,\qquad K_f=539.593\,\mathrm J,\qquad
\Delta K=384.443\,\mathrm J.}
$$
At fixed <angular momentum> $J$, $K=J^2/(2I)$ increases when the <moment of inertia> decreases. The skater supplies <work> by pulling the arms inward; chemical energy in the muscles is converted into additional <rotational kinetic energy>, with possible extra dissipation in a real skater. This does not require an external axial <torque>.

For the ideal rotating <point masses>, an inward radial <force> supplies the required centripetal acceleration. During contraction both that inward <force> and the radial displacement point inward, so their scalar product gives positive <work>. At the initial and final steady configurations radial motion has ceased; any transient radial <kinetic energy> must also be included in a full intermediate-time balance. The quoted difference compares the rotational endpoint energies only.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The first term, $m\dot r^2/2$, is radial <kinetic energy>. The second, $L^2/(2mr^2)$, is tangential <kinetic energy>, since conservation of <angular momentum> gives $L=mr^2\dot\varphi$. The final term, $-GMm/r$, is the gravitational <potential energy>, with zero at infinite separation. Thus
$$
E=\frac12m\dot r^2+U_{\rm eff}(r),\qquad
\boxed{U_{\rm eff}(r)=\frac{L^2}{2mr^2}-\frac{GMm}{r}.}
$$
The second and third terms together form the radial <effective potential> for a <Kepler orbit>. At an apsis the radial <velocity> vanishes, so $E=U_{\rm eff}$; for a bound noncircular orbit the two accessible roots give the nearest and furthest radii.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Write the lost <mass> fraction as $f$, with $f=0.02$, and let $\mu=GM$. Initially the <circular orbit> has $v_0^2=\mu/R$, zero radial <velocity>, and specific <angular momentum> $h^2=R^2v_0^2=\mu R$. The stipulated rapid symmetric loss leaves the satellite's instantaneous position and <velocity> unchanged, but reduces the gravitational parameter to $\mu'=(1-f)\mu$. There is no impulsive kick from contact with ejecta in this model.

The new specific orbital <energy> is
$$
\varepsilon'=\frac{v_0^2}{2}-\frac{\mu'}R
=\frac\mu R\left(f-\frac12\right),
$$
and $h$ remains unchanged because the post-loss <force> is still central. At a radial turning point,
$$
\varepsilon'=\frac{h^2}{2r^2}-\frac{\mu'}r.
$$
Inserting the known constants and factoring yields
$$
(1-2f)r^2-2(1-f)Rr+R^2
=(r-R)\bigl[(1-2f)r-R\bigr]=0.
$$
For $0<f<1/2$ the two radii are $R$ and $R/(1-2f)$. The initial radius is the <pericentre>: immediately after the loss, $\ddot r=h^2/R^3-\mu'/R^2=f\mu/R^2>0$, so the satellite begins moving outward. Therefore
$$
\boxed{r_{\min}=R,\qquad r_{\max}=\frac R{0.96}=\frac{25}{24}R.}
$$
Equivalently, the new <semi-major axis> is $49R/48$ and the <orbital eccentricity> is $1/49$. These values also satisfy $r_{\min}=a(1-e)$ and $r_{\max}=a(1+e)$. For loss of half the original <mass> the ideal trajectory becomes parabolic, and larger loss makes it unbound. Slow adiabatic loss would give a different, nearly circular expansion; it is not the instantaneous limit stipulated here.
