= Solution
{parent=1}
{c}

Neglecting the planets' <masses> compared with the <Sun>'s <mass>, <Newton's law of universal gravitation> supplies the <centripetal acceleration> of each <circular orbit>:
$$
\frac{GM_\odot}{r^2}=\frac{4\pi^2r}{T^2}.
$$
Thus <Kepler's third law>, calibrated by the <Earth>'s <orbital period> of one year and <orbital radius> of one <astronomical unit>, gives
$$
\frac{T}{1\,\mathrm{yr}}=\left(\frac{r}{1\,\mathrm{AU}}\right)^{3/2},\qquad
\boxed{T_J=5.2^{3/2}=11.86\,\mathrm{yr},\quad T_S=9.5^{3/2}=29.28\,\mathrm{yr}.}
$$
Both <orbits> are taken to have the same direction. Their relative <angular speed> is $2\pi(1/T_J-1/T_S)$, so the <Sun>-centred interval between successive <conjunctions> is the <synodic period>
$$
\boxed{T_{\rm syn}=\frac{1}{1/T_J-1/T_S}=19.93\,\mathrm{yr}.}
$$
Using the supplied rounded <orbital radii> explains the small difference from the commonly quoted approximately twenty-year interval.

From the <Earth>, the two lines of sight are displaced differently because <Jupiter> and <Saturn> are at different <distances>. The <Earth>'s annual <motion> along its <orbit> therefore shifts the dates of apparent <conjunctions> by amounts of order months. The broad recurrence remains about twenty years, but successive apparent <conjunctions> need not be equally spaced: <apparent retrograde motion> can produce a group of three <conjunctions> separated by months during one encounter. The <Earth>'s daily rotation adds a much smaller change of viewpoint. This is a geometrical comparison in the stipulated coplanar model; daylight and the horizon can prevent a particular <conjunction> from being visible at a chosen surface location.

Take downward <distance> $s$ and downward <speed> $v=ds/dt\geq0$, with constant <gravitational acceleration> $g>0$ and <quadratic drag> coefficient $k>0$. The printed vector square has no standard vector meaning; the directionally consistent <drag force> is $\mathbf F_d=-mk|\mathbf v|\mathbf v$. For this downward fall, <Newton's second law> becomes
$$
\frac{dv}{dt}=g-kv^2.
$$
The <terminal velocity> is obtained by setting the net <force> to zero:
$$
\boxed{v_\infty=\sqrt{g/k}\quad\text{downward}.}
$$
From rest, the <speed> increases monotonically towards this limiting value. In fact $v(t)=v_\infty\tanh(\sqrt{gk}\,t)$; thus it is a <supremum> approached at infinite time, and a tower of finite height need not permit it to be reached.

For the required <distance>, use the <chain rule> $dv/dt=v\,dv/ds$. Writing $w=v^2$ gives a <linear ordinary differential equation>:
$$
\frac{dw}{ds}+2kw=2g,\qquad w(0)=0,
\qquad
v^2(s)=\frac gk\left(1-e^{-2ks}\right).
$$
Consequently,
$$
\frac{v^2}{v_\infty^2}=\frac14=1-e^{-2ks},\qquad
\boxed{s_{1/2}=\frac{1}{2k}\log\frac43.}
$$
Here $k$ has units of inverse <length>, so the result has the required units of <distance> and is independent of the falling <mass>.

= Solution
{parent=3}
{c}

First take $a\ne0$, put $A=|a|$, and suppose $k>0$. The <force> is the negative <derivative> of the <potential energy>:
$$
U(x)=-\frac{kx}{A^2+x^2},\qquad
\boxed{F(x)=-U'(x)=\frac{k(A^2-x^2)}{(A^2+x^2)^2}.}
$$
The <potential energy> is an <odd function>, passes through $U(0)=0$, and approaches zero from above as $x\to-\infty$ and from below as $x\to+\infty$. Its <stationary points> are $x=\pm A$:
$$
U(-A)=\frac{k}{2A},\qquad U(A)=-\frac{k}{2A},\qquad
U''(-A)=-\frac{k}{2A^3},\qquad U''(A)=\frac{k}{2A^3}.
$$
Thus the sole <stable equilibrium> is the <local minimum> at $x=A$; the <local maximum> at $x=-A$ is an <unstable equilibrium>. The requested values at the <stable equilibrium> are
$$
\boxed{x_{\rm stable}=A,\qquad U_{\rm stable}=-\frac{k}{2A},\qquad F_{\rm stable}=0.}
$$
The <force> is an <even function>, positive for $|x|<A$, negative for $|x|>A$, and zero at $x=\pm A$. Its central maximum is $F(0)=k/A^2$. Since
$$
F'(x)=\frac{2kx(x^2-3A^2)}{(A^2+x^2)^3},
$$
its two minima occur at $x=\pm\sqrt3A$, with $F=-k/(8A^2)$, and $F\to0^-$ at both ends. These values fix the shapes of both requested sketches.

\Image[2021-potential-and-force.png]
{title=Dimensionless potential energy and force for positive k and nonzero a, with the stable and unstable equilibria marked}

In one dimension, **attraction towards the origin** means $xF(x)<0$, while **repulsion from the origin** means $xF(x)>0$. With that explicitly stated convention,
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\text{attraction:}&\quad -A<x<0\quad\text{or}\quad x>A,\\
\text{repulsion:}&\quad x<-A\quad\text{or}\quad 0<x<A.
\end{aligned}}
$$
A positive <force> alone means rightward, not necessarily outward. At the origin $F(0)\ne0$, so it is not an <equilibrium>; the inward/outward distinction is undefined exactly there.

The sign assumptions matter because the paper calls $a,k$ merely constants. For $k<0$ both curves reverse sign, the <stable equilibrium> is $x=-A$, its <potential energy> is $-|k|/(2A)$, and the attraction/repulsion intervals interchange. For $k=0$ the <force> vanishes everywhere on the nonsingular domain and there is no isolated <potential energy> minimum. If $a=0$ and $k\ne0$, then $U=-k/x$ for $x\ne0$ and $F=-k/x^2$, with no finite <equilibrium>.

= Solution
{parent=4}
{c}

Choose the vertical $z$ axis downward, and measure the <polar angle> $\theta$ from it. With <azimuth> $\phi$ and fixed rod <length> $r$, the <Cartesian coordinates> are
$$
\boxed{x=r\sin\theta\cos\phi,\qquad y=r\sin\theta\sin\phi,\qquad z=r\cos\theta.}
$$
The massless rod has no <kinetic energy>. Differentiating these <coordinates> and adding the squared components of <velocity> yields
$$
v^2=\dot x^2+\dot y^2+\dot z^2
=r^2\left(\dot\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2\right).
$$
The <gravitational potential energy> is $V=-mgz=-mgr\cos\theta$, up to an arbitrary constant. The <Lagrangian> of this <spherical pendulum> is therefore
$$
\boxed{L=\frac12mr^2\left(\dot\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2\right)+mgr\cos\theta.}
$$
Applying the <Euler-Lagrange equation> to the two <generalized coordinates> gives
$$
mr^2\ddot\theta-mr^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2+mgr\sin\theta=0,
\qquad
\frac{d}{dt}\left(mr^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi\right)=0.
$$
Equivalently, away from the <coordinate singularities> at the poles,
$$
\boxed{\ddot\theta=\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2-\frac gr\sin\theta,\qquad
\ddot\phi+2\cot\theta\,\dot\theta\dot\phi=0.}
$$
Since $L$ is independent of $\phi$, the <azimuth> is a <cyclic coordinate>. Its <conjugate momentum>
$$
p_\phi=mr^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi
$$
is the conserved component of <angular momentum> about the downward vertical axis. The <polar angle> is not a <cyclic coordinate>. Using an upward $z$ axis instead reverses the sign of the cosine term in $L$ and of the corresponding gravity term; it describes the same motion with the opposite <polar angle> convention.

Orient the $x$ axis along the displacement from the earlier event to the later one, so $\Delta x=d=1000\,\mathrm m$ and $\Delta t=10^{-6}\,\mathrm s$. For a parallel <Lorentz transformation>, <simultaneity> in the second <inertial frame> requires
$$
\Delta t'=\gamma\left(\Delta t-\frac{v\Delta x}{c^2}\right)=0,
\qquad
\boxed{v=\frac{c^2\Delta t}{d}=8.99\times10^7\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\simeq0.300c.}
$$
The second <inertial frame> moves towards the later event's location. The positive value $d^2-c^2\Delta t^2$ establishes <spacelike separation>, which permits this change of <simultaneity>. For a general <velocity> $\mathbf V$, the condition is $\mathbf V\cdot\Delta\mathbf r=c^2\Delta t$: only its component along the separation is fixed at the displayed value, and transverse components are allowed provided $|\mathbf V|<c$. Thus the parallel <velocity> has the smallest possible <speed>.

A physical <inertial frame> must have $|v|<c$, giving
$$
\boxed{|\Delta t|<\frac dc=3.336\,\mu\mathrm s.}
$$
**There is no attained maximum:** $d/c$ is the <supremum>. For the concrete boundary case $\Delta t=1000\,\mathrm m/c$, the events have <null separation> and the formula would require $v=c$; no <inertial frame> can make them simultaneous.

Use the <Earth>'s <inertial frame>, with the <Earth> at $x=0$ and the star at $x=D=4.2$ light years, neglecting their relative motion. Take the stated relative <velocity> of $B$ to be forward along the same direction as $A$. The <relativistic velocity addition> law gives
$$
\boxed{v_B=\frac{0.25c+0.50c}{1+(0.25)(0.50)}=\frac23c.}
$$
In units of light years and years, $c=1$ and the spacecraft <worldlines> are $x_A=t/4$ and $x_B=2t/3$. Their passages at the star occur at
$$
t_{B*}=\frac{4.2}{2/3}=6.3\,\mathrm{yr},\qquad
t_{A*}=\frac{4.2}{1/4}=16.8\,\mathrm{yr}.
$$
The return <radio wave> from $B$ follows the leftward light <worldline>
$$
x=4.2-(t-6.3)=10.5-t.
$$
Intersecting this with $A$'s <worldline> gives $t/4=10.5-t$, hence
$$
\boxed{t_{A\leftarrow B}=8.4\,\mathrm{yr},\qquad x_{A\leftarrow B}=2.1\,\text{light years}.}
$$
This is the <Earth>-frame coordinate time of reception on $A$, as requested; it is not the reading of $A$'s own clock. If an observer on the <Earth> watched that reception through a returning light signal, they would see it later.

Each transmission from the star takes another $D/c=4.2$ years to reach the <Earth>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{t_{\oplus\leftarrow B}=10.5\,\mathrm{yr},\qquad
 t_{\oplus\leftarrow A}=21.0\,\mathrm{yr}.}
$$
All times are measured from the common passage by the <Earth>; the spacecraft continue at their stated <velocities> during the calculation.

= Solution
{parent=7}
{c}

<Inertia> is resistance to a change of motion. Translational <inertia> is measured by <mass> in <Newton's second law>; rotational <inertia> about a specified fixed axis is measured by the <moment of inertia>
$$
I=\int r_\perp^2\,dm,
$$
where $r_\perp$ is perpendicular <distance> from that axis. For a thin uniform rod, take axes perpendicular to the rod. With <linear mass density> $\lambda=m/l$,
$$
I_{\rm CM}=\frac ml\int_{-l/2}^{l/2}x^2\,dx=\frac{ml^2}{12},\qquad
\boxed{I_{\rm end}=I_{\rm CM}+m\left(\frac l2\right)^2=\frac{ml^2}{3}.}
$$
The second result uses the <parallel axis theorem>; direct <integration> over $0\leq x\leq l$ gives the same value. The axis specification is essential: an ideal thin rod has zero <moment of inertia> about its own longitudinal axis.

During the short <elastic collision>, neglect the <impulse> from gravity. The pivot can exert an external <impulse>, so total linear <momentum> of rod plus particle need not be conserved. Its <torque> about the pivot is zero, so <conservation of angular momentum> about that point applies. Let $w$ be the particle's signed outgoing horizontal <velocity>, and $\omega$ the rod's outgoing <angular velocity>, positive towards the incident motion. Then
$$
mlv_0=mlw+I_{\rm end}\omega,\qquad
\frac12mv_0^2=\frac12mw^2+\frac12I_{\rm end}\omega^2.
$$
Substituting $\omega=3(v_0-w)/l$ into the <kinetic energy> equation gives
$$
v_0^2=w^2+3(v_0-w)^2,
\qquad (v_0-w)(v_0-2w)=0.
$$
Discarding the zero-<impulse> branch $w=v_0$, the actual <collision> gives
$$
\boxed{w=\frac{v_0}{2}\quad\text{forward},\qquad
\omega=\frac{3v_0}{2l}.}
$$
The rod's lower end initially moves forward at $l\omega=3v_0/2$, so the bodies separate. Afterwards the free particle undergoes <projectile motion>. With the pivot as origin and $z$ downward, its <trajectory> is $x=(v_0/2)t$, $z=l+gt^2/2$, where $t=0$ at the <collision>. The rod instead swings about the fixed pivot in a vertical plane as a <physical pendulum>.

The rod's initial rotational <kinetic energy> is $I_{\rm end}\omega^2/2=3mv_0^2/8$. Reaching the horizontal raises its <centre of mass> by $l/2$, increasing <gravitational potential energy> by $mgl/2$. Consequently,
$$
\boxed{v_0^2\geq\frac{4gl}{3}\ \text{to reach the horizontal},\qquad
v_0=\sqrt{\frac{4gl}{3}}\ \text{to just reach it at rest}.}
$$
For the threshold case, measure the rod's <angle> $\theta$ from the downward vertical and reset $t=0$ at its first horizontal <turning point>, so $\theta(0)=\pi/2$ and $\dot\theta(0)=0$. The restoring <torque> about the pivot gives the exact <equation of motion>
$$
\boxed{\ddot\theta+\frac{3g}{2l}\sin\theta=0.}
$$
At the horizontal, $\ddot\theta=-3g/(2l)$, so the rod immediately swings back. <Conservation of mechanical energy> yields
$$
\frac12\frac{ml^2}{3}\dot\theta^2+\frac{mgl}{2}(1-\cos\theta)=\frac{mgl}{2},
\qquad
\dot\theta^2=\frac{3g}{l}\cos\theta.
$$
On the initial descent its explicit time dependence is specified by the <definite integral>
$$
\boxed{t=\sqrt{\frac{l}{3g}}\int_{\theta(t)}^{\pi/2}\frac{d\alpha}{\sqrt{\cos\alpha}}.}
$$
It continues through the vertical to $-\pi/2$, reverses, and repeats without damping. An alternative expression valid throughout the oscillation uses the <Jacobi elliptic sine>. Put $k=1/\sqrt2$, $\omega_r=\sqrt{3g/(2l)}$, and let $K(k)$ denote the <complete elliptic integral of the first kind>. Then
$$
\boxed{\theta(t)=2\arcsin\!\left[k\,\operatorname{sn}\!\left(K(k)-\omega_rt,k\right)\right],\qquad
T_r=4\sqrt{\frac{2l}{3g}}\,K\!\left(\frac1{\sqrt2}\right).}
$$
This is not <simple harmonic motion> at an <amplitude> of $\pi/2$. Its <oscillation period> exceeds its own small-<amplitude> value $2\pi\sqrt{2l/(3g)}$ by the factor $2K(1/\sqrt2)/\pi\simeq1.18034$.

A <simple pendulum> of <length> $l/2$ obeys $\ddot\theta+(2g/l)\sin\theta=0$. At the same <amplitude> $\pi/2$, its <oscillation period> is $T_p=4\sqrt{l/(2g)}K(1/\sqrt2)$, so
$$
\boxed{\frac{T_r}{T_p}=\frac{2}{\sqrt3}>1.}
$$
The rod swings more slowly. Its equivalent <simple pendulum> <length> is $I_{\rm end}/[m(l/2)]=2l/3$, not the <distance> $l/2$ to its <centre of mass>. The same <oscillation period> ratio holds in the common small-<amplitude> limit, but replacing the actual 90-degree motion by that limit would underestimate both <oscillation periods>.

= Solution

{c}

For a satellite of <mass> $m$ moving in the <Newtonian gravitational field> of a much larger, effectively fixed <mass> $M$, put $\mu=GM$. Its conserved <mechanical energy>, with <gravitational potential energy> zero at infinity, is
$$
\boxed{E=\frac12mv^2-\frac{GMm}{r}=-\frac{GMm}{2a}.}
$$
Here $r$ is the instantaneous centre-to-centre <distance>, $v$ the satellite's <speed>, $G$ the <gravitational constant>, and $a$ the <semi-major axis> of its bound <elliptic orbit>. The <kinetic energy> and <potential energy> vary around the <orbit>, while their sum remains constant. Dividing by $m$ and solving for $v$ gives the <vis-viva equation>, $v^2=\mu(2/r-1/a)$.

For this <Hohmann transfer orbit>, the <periapsis> and <apoapsis> <distances> are $r_1=1\,\mathrm{AU}$ and $r_2=1.52\,\mathrm{AU}$, so
$$
a=\frac{r_1+r_2}{2}=1.26\,\mathrm{AU},\qquad
v_1=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r_1}}.
$$
The <vis-viva equation> gives the endpoint <speeds>
$$
\boxed{v_{\rm p}=v_1\sqrt{2-\frac1{1.26}}=v_1\sqrt{\frac{76}{63}},\qquad
v_{\rm a}=v_1\sqrt{\frac2{1.52}-\frac1{1.26}}.}
$$
As a check, <conservation of angular momentum> at the two endpoints gives $r_1v_{\rm p}=r_2v_{\rm a}$ because each <velocity> is perpendicular to its radius. Using $1\,\mathrm{AU}=1.49598\times10^{11}\,\mathrm m$ and $\mu=1.32712\times10^{20}\,\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}$ gives $v_1=29.78\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and
$$
\boxed{v_{\rm p}=32.71\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}},\qquad
v_{\rm a}=21.52\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The departure <impulse> must increase the <speed> in the direction of the initial <circular orbit>:
$$
\boxed{\Delta v_1=v_{\rm p}-v_1=2.93\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
These are <Sun>-centred <speeds>; leaving the <Earth>'s own <gravitational field> would require an additional calculation beyond this idealized solar <orbit> transfer.

At $r_2$, the desired <circular orbit> <speed> is $v_2=\sqrt{\mu/r_2}=24.16\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. The arrival <impulse> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\Delta v_2=v_2-v_{\rm a}=2.64\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The required thrust on the spacecraft is forward, tangent to its <orbit>, to accelerate it. The rocket ejects exhaust backwards, opposite to the spacecraft's <velocity>; this distinguishes the direction of thrust from the direction in which the exhaust is fired.

The <Hohmann transfer time> is half the <orbital period> of the transfer <ellipse>. From <Kepler's third law>, relative to the <Earth>'s one-year <orbital period>,
$$
t_H=\frac12(1.26)^{3/2}\,\mathrm{yr}=0.7072\,\mathrm{yr}\simeq258\,\text{days}.
$$
Meanwhile <Mars>'s <orbital period> is $T_M=1.52^{3/2}\,\mathrm{yr}=1.8740\,\mathrm{yr}$. The spacecraft arrives $\pi$ <radians> from its departure <position>. For a rendezvous, <Mars> must reach that same <position> after advancing through $2\pi t_H/T_M$. Thus its initial lead <angle> over the departing spacecraft is
$$
\boxed{\phi_0=\pi-\frac{2\pi t_H}{T_M}
=\pi\left[1-\left(\frac{1.26}{1.52}\right)^{3/2}\right]
=44.15^\circ.}
$$
<Mars> must be ahead by this <angle> in the common direction of <motion> around their <orbits>. At departure the spacecraft and the <Earth> share the inner <orbital position>; arrival occurs on the opposite side of the <Sun>.

= Solution

{c}

Take $+y$ upwards in the source diagram. Write $w$ for $B$'s incoming <speed>, so the initial <velocities> are
$$
\mathbf u_A=(-u,0),\qquad
\mathbf u_B=\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}w,\frac12w\right).
$$
**A contact-direction assumption is needed for a unique answer.** The diagram specifies the incoming <velocities> but does not specify the contact normal or an outgoing <angle>. First adopt the usual intended model: the <impulse> is horizontal, so neither particle changes its $y$ component of <velocity>. This is the smooth <oblique collision> model with a horizontal line of centres at impact. Bringing $A$ to rest in the $x$ direction then brings it entirely to rest.

Let $b$ be $B$'s outgoing $x$ component of <velocity>. <Conservation of momentum> and conservation of <kinetic energy> give
$$
-3u+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}w=b,
\qquad
3u^2+w^2=b^2+\frac14w^2.
$$
Put $q=(\sqrt3/2)w$. The second equation becomes $3u^2+q^2=(q-3u)^2$, so $q=u$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{w=\frac{2u}{\sqrt3},\qquad
\mathbf v_A=(0,0),\qquad
\mathbf v_B=\left(-2u,\frac{u}{\sqrt3}\right).}
$$
The outgoing <kinetic energies> are
$$
\boxed{K_A=0,\qquad K_B=\frac12m\left(4u^2+\frac{u^2}{3}\right)=\frac{13}{6}mu^2.}
$$
The outgoing <speed> of $B$ is $u\sqrt{13/3}$, directed above the negative $x$ axis at <angle> $\tan^{-1}[1/(2\sqrt3)]\simeq16.1^\circ$. The initial <kinetic energy> is also $(3/2+2/3)mu^2=13mu^2/6$, as required for an <elastic collision>.

For the <perfectly inelastic collision> with these same incoming <velocities>, both particles share a common outgoing <velocity>. <Conservation of momentum> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf V=\frac{3m\mathbf u_A+m\mathbf u_B}{4m}
=\left(-\frac u2,\frac{u}{4\sqrt3}\right),\qquad
|\mathbf V|=\frac{\sqrt{13}}{4\sqrt3}u.}
$$
Its direction is again $16.1^\circ$ above the negative $x$ axis. The separate translational <kinetic energies> of the constituents and their total are
$$
\boxed{K_A=\frac{13}{32}mu^2,\qquad K_B=\frac{13}{96}mu^2,\qquad
K_{\rm final}=\frac{13}{24}mu^2.}
$$
The loss of translational <kinetic energy> is
$$
\boxed{K_{\rm initial}-K_{\rm final}=\frac{13}{8}mu^2.}
$$
This becomes internal <energy>, for example deformation and heating; total <energy> is conserved. In the <elastic collision> no <kinetic energy> is lost and the particles separate with different <velocities>. In the <perfectly inelastic collision> relative motion disappears and the common <velocity> is the original <centre of mass> <velocity>.

The limitation of the printed data can be made explicit. Without the horizontal-<impulse> assumption, write the final <velocity> of $A$ as $(0,h)$. Then <conservation of momentum> determines
$$
\mathbf v_B=\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}w-3u,\frac12w-3h\right),
$$
and conservation of <kinetic energy> gives only
$$
\boxed{w(\sqrt3u+h)=2u^2+4h^2.}
$$
There is a family of possibilities. For a concrete second solution, $h=u/\sqrt3$ gives
$$
w=\frac{5\sqrt3}{6}u,\qquad
\mathbf v_A=\left(0,\frac{u}{\sqrt3}\right),\qquad
\mathbf v_B=\left(-\frac74u,-\frac{7\sqrt3}{12}u\right).
$$
These <velocities> conserve both components of <momentum> and conserve <kinetic energy>, and they satisfy the specified vanishing $x$ component for $A$, while requiring a different incoming <speed>. They can arise from a smooth contact normal proportional to $(1,1/\sqrt3)$. Thus the horizontal-<impulse> result is conditional, rather than uniquely implied by the printed statement. For any member of this family the corresponding sticking result remains well determined once $w$ is chosen:
$$
\mathbf V=\frac14\left(-3u+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}w,\frac12w\right),\qquad
K_{\rm final}=2m|\mathbf V|^2,\qquad
\Delta K=\frac{3m}{8}\left(\left[u+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}w\right]^2+\frac{w^2}{4}\right).
$$
The last expression is the lost relative <kinetic energy> in a <perfectly inelastic collision> of these two <point masses>.

= Solution
{parent=10}
{c}

The laboratory is the parent's <rest frame>. In this <relativistic two-body decay>, <conservation of momentum> gives equal and opposite daughter <momenta>, of magnitude $p$. Their total <relativistic energies> satisfy
$$
E_1+E_2=4mc^2,\qquad
E_1^2=p^2c^2+m^2c^4,\qquad
E_2^2=p^2c^2+4m^2c^4.
$$
Subtracting the <relativistic energy-momentum relations> eliminates $p$:
$$
(E_2-E_1)(E_2+E_1)=3m^2c^4,
\qquad E_2-E_1=\frac34mc^2.
$$
Solving the two <linear equations> gives
$$
E_1=\frac{13}{8}mc^2,\qquad E_2=\frac{19}{8}mc^2,\qquad
p=\frac{\sqrt{105}}8mc.
$$
Since <three-momentum> obeys $pc^2=Ev$, the daughter <speeds> are
$$
\boxed{v_1=\frac{\sqrt{105}}{13}c=0.7882c,\qquad
v_2=\frac{\sqrt{105}}{19}c=0.5393c.}
$$
Their directions are opposite; the initial state does not specify their common axis. The <kinetic energies> are $K_1=5mc^2/8$ and $K_2=3mc^2/8$. Their sum $mc^2$ is the loss of <rest energy> when the total daughter <rest mass> is $3m$ instead of $4m$.

The <proper mean lifetime> $\tau_0$ is the <mean lifetime> measured in the unstable particle's own <rest frame>, or equivalently the mean elapsed <proper time> before decay. In the <exponential decay> model, its survival <probability> to <proper time> $\tau$ is $e^{-\tau/\tau_0}$. For the daughter of <rest mass> $m$, the <Lorentz factor> is $\gamma_1=E_1/(mc^2)=13/8$, so <time dilation> gives a laboratory <mean lifetime> of $\gamma_1\tau_0=3.25\,\mu\mathrm s$.

At constant <speed> $v_1$, travelling a <distance> $L$ requires laboratory time $L/v_1$ and <proper time> $L/(\gamma_1v_1)$. Its <mean decay length> is
$$
\ell_d=\gamma_1v_1\tau_0
=\frac{\sqrt{105}}8c\tau_0
=768.0\,\mathrm m
$$
for $\tau_0=2\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm s$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(\text{distance before decay}>1000\,\mathrm m)
=\exp\!\left[-\frac{1000\,\mathrm m}{\ell_d}\right]
=0.272\quad\text{approximately}.}
$$
The <mean lifetime> sets the scale of an <exponential distribution>; it is not a fixed deadline, so travelling beyond one <mean decay length> has a nonzero <probability>.
