= Solution
{parent=1}

The dimensions of <mass density>, <time> and <energy> are $\mathsf M\mathsf L^{-3}$, $\mathsf T$ and $\mathsf M\mathsf L^2\mathsf T^{-2}$. Matching powers in the proposed <dimensional analysis> gives
$$
\mathsf L=\mathsf M^{\alpha+\gamma}\mathsf L^{-3\alpha+2\gamma}\mathsf T^{\beta-2\gamma},\qquad
\alpha+\gamma=0,\quad -3\alpha+2\gamma=1,\quad \beta-2\gamma=0.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\alpha=-\frac15,\qquad\beta=\frac25,\qquad\gamma=\frac15,\qquad R=C\left(\frac{Et^2}{\rho}\right)^{1/5}.}
$$
This is the <Sedov-Taylor blast wave> scaling for a fixed released <energy>. <Dimensional analysis> cannot determine the dimensionless $C$; that requires the fluid equations and thermodynamic assumptions. The assumed list of controlling parameters excludes, for example, an independent ambient sound speed or a continuing energy supply.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Write the <linear drag> as $\mathbf F_d=-b\mathbf v$, with $b>0$, and let $m$ be the projectile's <mass>. Taking upward as positive, <Newton's second law> gives
$$
m\dot v_y=-mg-bv_y,\qquad v_y(0)=u_0\sin\alpha.
$$
The downward <terminal velocity> is $-v_t$, where
$$
\boxed{v_t=\frac{mg}{b}.}
$$
Equivalently, if the drag <acceleration> is specified as $-k\mathbf v$, then $v_t=g/k$. Solving the first-order equation,
$$
v_y(t)=(u_0\sin\alpha+v_t)e^{-bt/m}-v_t.
$$
The highest point occurs at $v_y=0$, so
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm top}=\frac mb\ln\left(1+\frac{u_0\sin\alpha}{v_t}\right)
=\frac{v_t}{g}\ln\left(1+\frac{u_0\sin\alpha}{v_t}\right).}
$$
For an upward launch this root is positive and $\dot v_y=-g$ there, confirming a maximum height. As $b\to0$, expanding the logarithm gives the drag-free result $t_{\rm top}\to u_0\sin\alpha/g$. A horizontal launch has its highest point at launch; a downward launch from the ground does not have a later airborne maximum.

= Solution
{parent=3}

Treat the asteroid as a <point mass> with negligible influence on the planet. Outside a spherical planet, <Newton's law of universal gravitation> gives the specific <gravitational potential> $-GM/r$. The conserved specific <energy> and specific <angular momentum> are
$$
\mathcal E=\frac12v_0^2,\qquad h=dv_0.
$$
At the <periapsis> $r_p$, the radial <velocity> vanishes, so <conservation of energy> gives
$$
\frac12v_0^2=\frac{d^2v_0^2}{2r_p^2}-\frac{GM}{r_p},\qquad
\boxed{d^2=r_p^2+\frac{2GMr_p}{v_0^2}.}
$$
The right-hand side increases with $r_p>0$. The grazing threshold $r_p=R$ is therefore the <grazing impact parameter in gravitational focusing> relation. For $d>R$ its equivalent speed is
$$
\boxed{v_{\rm graze}=\sqrt{\frac{2GMR}{d^2-R^2}},\qquad
\text{strict avoidance requires }v_0>v_{\rm graze}.}
$$
The threshold is customarily called the minimum avoiding <speed>, with tangency counted as its limiting boundary. If any contact with the surface counts as a hit, it is an infimum, not an attained minimum. For $d\le R$, **no finite incoming speed avoids contact**: <gravitational focusing> bends the path inward, and even its infinite-speed straight-line limit has closest approach $d\le R$. The finite radius of the asteroid has been neglected.

= Solution

Use Cartesian coordinates along the edges from the pivot. With <surface mass density> $M/L^2$, the <moment of inertia> is
$$
I_O=\frac M{L^2}\int_0^L\!\int_0^L(x^2+y^2)\,dx\,dy
=\boxed{\frac23ML^2}.
$$
Equivalently, the <moment of inertia of a uniform square plate> about its central perpendicular axis is $ML^2/6$, and the <parallel axis theorem> adds $M(L/\sqrt2)^2$.

The source figure measures $\theta$ from the upward vertical to $OC$, with $OC=L/\sqrt2$. The centre's height above the pivot is $(L/\sqrt2)\cos\theta$. The smooth fixed pivot does no work. <Conservation of energy>, with the initially upright configuration as reference, gives
$$
\frac12I_O\dot\theta^2
=\frac{MgL}{\sqrt2}(1-\cos\theta),\qquad
\boxed{|\dot\theta|=\sqrt{\frac{3g}{\sqrt2L}(1-\cos\theta)}.}
$$
For rotation in the positive $\theta$ direction on the first descent, take the positive root. Its equivalent form is $\dot\theta=\sqrt{3\sqrt2g/L}\sin(\theta/2)$ for $0<\theta<\pi$.

Strictly, release from rest at a small angle $\theta_0$ replaces $1-\cos\theta$ by $\cos\theta_0-\cos\theta$. The displayed result is the $\theta_0\to0$ limiting <energy> relation. Exactly upright and exactly at rest is an unstable <equilibrium>; without a perturbation it stays there. This distinction prevents the infinitesimal displacement convention from being mistaken for spontaneous motion.

= Solution

The retained <mass> at time $t$ is $M(t)=M_0+kt$. Incoming rain has zero horizontal <momentum> in the ground <reference frame>. During $dt$, horizontal <momentum conservation> for the carriage and the rain about to enter gives
$$
M(t)u(t)=[M(t)+k\,dt][u(t)+du]+O(dt^2),
$$
so
$$
M\dot u+ku=0,\qquad
\boxed{u(t)=\frac{M_0u_0}{M_0+kt}.}
$$
This is a <variable-mass system>: the entering <mass> must be accelerated horizontally, even though no external horizontal <force> acts on the carriage-plus-rain system. Integrating the <velocity>,
$$
\boxed{x(t)-x(0)=\int_0^t\frac{M_0u_0}{M_0+ks}\,ds
=\frac{M_0u_0}{k}\ln\left(1+\frac{kt}{M_0}\right).}
$$
For $u_0\ge0$ this is also the distance travelled. Its $k\to0$ limit is $u_0t$. Vertical impact can dissipate <kinetic energy>; horizontal <momentum conservation> does not require <kinetic energy> conservation.

= Solution

Put $\beta=v/c$ and $\gamma=(1-\beta^2)^{-1/2}$, the common <Lorentz factor>. The initial total <relativistic energy> and <momentum> are
$$
E=\gamma mc^2+\gamma\frac m2c^2=\frac32\gamma mc^2,\qquad
P_x=\gamma mv-\gamma\frac m2v=\frac12\gamma mv.
$$
Assume that coalescence emits no additional radiation. By <four-momentum conservation>, the final <rest mass> $m_f$ is the <rest mass of a coalescing relativistic collision>:
$$
m_f^2c^4=E^2-c^2P_x^2,
\qquad
\boxed{m_f=\frac{\gamma m}{2}\sqrt{9-\beta^2}
=\frac m2\sqrt{\frac{9-\beta^2}{1-\beta^2}}.}
$$
The final <velocity> is $V=c^2P_x/E=v/3$, the <centre-of-momentum frame> <velocity>.

For $v\ll c$,
$$
\boxed{m_f=\frac32m+\frac23m\frac{v^2}{c^2}+O(v^4/c^4).}
$$
The leading term is the classical sum of <masses>. The first correction is the internal <energy> retained after sticking, divided by $c^2$: the initial classical <kinetic energy> is $3mv^2/4$, while the common-motion <kinetic energy> is $\tfrac12(3m/2)(v/3)^2=mv^2/12$. Their difference is $2mv^2/3$, consistent with <mass-energy equivalence>.

= Solution

Let $L_0=100\,\mathrm m$ be the train's <proper length>, $D=90\,\mathrm m$ the tunnel's length in the track <reference frame>, and $v=0.99c$. The <Lorentz factor> is $\gamma=7.0888$. At one track-frame <time>, <length contraction> makes the train
$$
L=\frac{L_0}{\gamma}=14.107\,\mathrm m<D.
$$
**Yes, the whole train fits inside at a single track-frame time.** Set $t=0$ when the rear enters the near portal. Until the front reaches the far portal, the rear and front are at $vt$ and $vt+L$, respectively. Complete containment lasts
$$
0\le t\le\frac{D-L}{v},
$$
a track-frame interval of approximately $256\,\mathrm{ns}$, counting boundary contact.

In the train's <reference frame>, its length is $100\,\mathrm m$, while the moving tunnel has simultaneous length $D/\gamma=12.696\,\mathrm m$. **No single train-frame time has the whole train between the portals.** The two answers refer to different simultaneity slices.

For an explicit check, denote the rear-entry event by $A$ and the front-exit event by $B$. In track coordinates,
$$
\Delta t=\frac{D-L}{v}>0,\qquad \Delta x=D.
$$
The <Lorentz transformation> gives
$$
\boxed{\Delta t'=\gamma\left(\Delta t-\frac{vD}{c^2}\right)
=\frac{D/\gamma-L_0}{v}<0.}
$$
Thus the front leaves before the rear enters in train coordinates. These spatially separated events have a frame-dependent order, as allowed by <relativity of simultaneity>; neither answer asserts a different local event at a portal. No collision with closed doors has been stipulated.

= Solution
{parent=8}

In the <laboratory frame>, the initial target is at rest and the outgoing directions are directly related to detector positions. The <centre-of-momentum frame> has zero total <momentum>, so the two outgoing <momenta> are equal and opposite; it cleanly separates bulk motion from relative <kinetic energy>. Transforming back is needed to predict laboratory angles and <speeds>. Neither <reference frame> removes the need for an appropriate collision law.

Take the incident direction as $\widehat{\mathbf x}$ and put $S=M_1+M_2$. <Momentum conservation> fixes the <centre-of-momentum frame> <velocity>, and a <Galilean transformation> gives the initial <velocities> in that frame:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf V=\frac{M_1u_1}{S}\widehat{\mathbf x},\qquad
\mathbf w_1=\frac{M_2u_1}{S}\widehat{\mathbf x},\qquad
\mathbf w_2=-\frac{M_1u_1}{S}\widehat{\mathbf x}.}
$$
After the <collision>, $M_1\mathbf w'_1+M_2\mathbf w'_2=0$. Hence both particles have the same final-to-initial <speed> ratio,
$$
q=\frac{|\mathbf w'_1|}{|\mathbf w_1|}
=\frac{|\mathbf w'_2|}{|\mathbf w_2|}
=\frac{|\mathbf v'_1-\mathbf v'_2|}{u_1}.
$$
The relative <kinetic energy> is multiplied by $q^2$. This common ratio exists independently of how the outgoing direction is selected.

**The meaning of restitution matters here.** The usual <coefficient of restitution> $e_n$ is a ratio of normal separation and approach <speeds>, not total <speeds>. In a smooth <oblique collision>, the tangential relative <velocity> is unchanged. If the incoming relative <velocity> makes angle $\chi$ to the contact normal, then
$$
\boxed{q^2=e_n^2\cos^2\chi+\sin^2\chi.}
$$
For example, $e_n=1/2$ and $\chi=\pi/4$ give $q=\sqrt{5/8}\ne1/2$. Therefore the requested identification is not generally true with the standard normal definition. The printed equation is valid if its $e$ denotes the <relative speed retention in an oblique collision>, namely $e=q$, or if an additional collision model supplies that equality. The elastic cases have $q=e_n=1$ and are unaffected by this qualification.

To derive the scattering formula under that convention, write $\mathbf v'_1=\mathbf V+\mathbf w'_1$. Its components give the <laboratory scattering circle>
$$
(v_1\cos\theta_1-V)^2+(v_1\sin\theta_1)^2
=q^2\left(\frac{M_2u_1}{S}\right)^2.
$$
Solving the resulting quadratic for the nonnegative <speed> yields
$$
\boxed{\frac{v_1}{u_1}=\frac{M_1}{S}\left[\cos\theta_1\pm
\sqrt{\cos^2\theta_1-1+q^2\left(\frac{M_2}{M_1}\right)^2}\right].}
$$
With $q=e$, this is the requested formula. A real square root and $v_1\ge0$ are both necessary: a negative algebraic root is not a second physical <speed>, and $v_1=0$ does not define a <scattering angle>. The subsequent limits are taken using this <laboratory scattering circle>.

For equal <masses> and an <elastic collision>, $q=1$ and
$$
\frac{v_1}{u_1}=\frac12(\cos\theta_1\pm|\cos\theta_1|).
$$
Thus the nonzero outgoing branch is
$$
\boxed{v_1=u_1\cos\theta_1,\qquad 0\le\theta_1<\frac\pi2,}
$$
and the other nonnegative algebraic root is $v_1=0$. The latter is the head-on transfer of the incoming <momentum> and <kinetic energy> to particle 2: $v_2=u_1$. A stationary particle has no defined outgoing direction, so this root is not a separate physical outcome for every chosen $\theta_1$.

On the nonzero branch, <momentum conservation> gives
$$
\mathbf v'_2=u_1\widehat{\mathbf x}-\mathbf v'_1,
\qquad v_2=u_1\sin\theta_1.
$$
The two nonzero outgoing <velocities> are perpendicular: squaring <momentum conservation> and comparing with <kinetic energy> conservation gives $\mathbf v'_1\cdot\mathbf v'_2=0$. For $\cos\theta_1<0$, the nonzero root is negative and must be rejected; backward scattering of a moving incident particle is impossible in this equal-mass, initially stationary-target case.

For an <elastic collision> with $M_2>M_1$, the positive root in the <laboratory scattering circle> formula is the only nonnegative one. With $\varepsilon=M_1/M_2\ll1$,
$$
\frac{v_1}{u_1}

Consequently
$$
\boxed{v_1\longrightarrow u_1\quad\text{as }M_2/M_1\to\infty.}
$$
The heavy target can absorb a finite <momentum> with negligible <velocity> and negligible <kinetic energy>. Thus the projectile may change direction substantially while retaining its <speed>; a backward reflection also has $v_1\simeq u_1$.

A totally <inelastic collision> in the sticking sense has zero final relative <velocity>, so $q=0$. Both <velocities> are the conserved <centre-of-momentum frame> <velocity>:

\boxed{\mathbf v'_1=\mathbf v'_2=\frac{M_1u_1}{M_1+M_2}\widehat{\mathbf x}.}

= Solution
{parent=9}

For a particle of <rest mass> $m_0$ and <velocity> $\mathbf u$, the <relativistic energy> and <momentum> are
$$
\boxed{E=\gamma_um_0c^2,\qquad\mathbf p=\gamma_um_0\mathbf u,\qquad
\gamma_u=(1-u^2/c^2)^{-1/2}.}
$$
For a <photon> of wavelength $\lambda$ travelling along $\widehat{\mathbf n}$,
$$
\boxed{E_\gamma=\frac{hc}{\lambda},\qquad\mathbf p_\gamma=\frac h\lambda\widehat{\mathbf n},\qquad E_\gamma=c|\mathbf p_\gamma|.}
$$
Here $h$ is <Planck constant> and $c$ is the <speed of light>.

Let $\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon'$ be the incident and scattered <photon> <energies>. The stationary <electron> initially has <energy> $m_ec^2$. By <four-momentum conservation>, its final <energy> and <momentum> are
$$
E_e'=m_ec^2+\varepsilon-\varepsilon',\qquad
\mathbf p_e'=\frac{\varepsilon}{c}\widehat{\mathbf n}
-\frac{\varepsilon'}c\widehat{\mathbf n}',
\quad\widehat{\mathbf n}\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}'=\cos\theta.
$$
Use the electron's <relativistic energy-momentum relation>, rather than assuming its recoil is nonrelativistic:
$$
(m_ec^2+\varepsilon-\varepsilon')^2
=m_e^2c^4+\varepsilon^2+\varepsilon'^2-2\varepsilon\varepsilon'\cos\theta.
$$
Cancelling common terms gives
$$
m_ec^2(\varepsilon-\varepsilon')
=\varepsilon\varepsilon'(1-\cos\theta),\qquad
\frac1{\varepsilon'}-\frac1\varepsilon=\frac{1-\cos\theta}{m_ec^2}.
$$
Since $\lambda=hc/\varepsilon$, the <Compton wavelength shift> is
$$
\boxed{\lambda'-\lambda=\frac{h}{m_ec}(1-\cos\theta).}
$$
The word elastic here means that the final <electron> has the same <rest mass> as initially and the complete photon-electron system conserves total <energy>; the <photon> alone loses <energy> to recoil.

For backscattering, $1-\cos\theta=2$, and hence
$$
\boxed{\varepsilon'=\frac{\varepsilon}{1+2\varepsilon/(m_ec^2)}
=\frac{0.511}{2.511}\,\mathrm{MeV}=0.2035\,\mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
Choose the incident direction as $+x$, so that the scattered <photon> travels in the $-x$ direction. An observer moving with <velocity> $+\beta c\widehat{\mathbf x}$ meets that <photon> head-on. The <Lorentz transformation> of <four-momentum> gives the observed <energy>
$$
\varepsilon_{\rm obs}=\gamma_\beta(\varepsilon'-\beta c p_x')
=\gamma_\beta\varepsilon'(1+\beta)
=\varepsilon'\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}}.
$$
This is the approaching-observer <relativistic Doppler effect>. Set $D=\varepsilon/\varepsilon'=2.511/0.511$ and solve:
$$
\boxed{\beta=\frac{D^2-1}{D^2+1}=0.9205\ \text{approximately}.}
$$
The observer must move **opposite to the scattered photon**, along the original incident direction, at approximately $0.9205c$. Moving with the scattered <photon> would redshift it instead.

The <massless inextensible string> has hanging length $y=l-r$, measured downward from the table. Thus $\dot y=-\dot r$. In <polar coordinates>, the tabletop <mass> has squared <speed> $\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2$. Adding the two <kinetic energies> and setting the hanging <gravitational potential energy> to $-Mgy$ gives
$$
T=\frac12(M+m)\dot r^2+\frac12mr^2\dot\theta^2,\qquad
V=-Mg(l-r),
$$
and the <Lagrangian> is
$$
\boxed{L=T-V=\frac12(M+m)\dot r^2+\frac12mr^2\dot\theta^2+Mg(l-r).}
$$
The <canonical momenta> are $p_r=(M+m)\dot r$ and $p_\theta=mr^2\dot\theta$. Performing the <Legendre transform> gives the <Hamiltonian>
$$
\boxed{\mathcal H=p_r\dot r+p_\theta\dot\theta-L
=\frac{p_r^2}{2(M+m)}+\frac{p_\theta^2}{2mr^2}-Mg(l-r).}
$$
It equals the total mechanical <energy> and is conserved because $L$ has no explicit <time> dependence. Also $\theta$ is a <cyclic coordinate>, so $J=p_\theta=mr^2\dot\theta$ is conserved <angular momentum> about the vertical through the hole. The table and the string guide supply external <forces>; conservation of the system's total linear <momentum> does not follow. These are the two independent generic integrals needed for the reduced <tabletop mass coupled to a hanging mass>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The radial and angular <Euler-Lagrange equations> are
$$
(M+m)\ddot r=mr\dot\theta^2-Mg,\qquad
\frac d{dt}(mr^2\dot\theta)=0.
$$
Writing the conserved <angular momentum> as
$$
\boxed{J=mr^2\dot\theta,}
$$
we obtain
$$
\boxed{(M+m)\ddot r=\frac{J^2}{mr^3}-Mg.}
$$
A positive constant radius requires zero radial <acceleration>. Hence the <circular motion> has
$$
\boxed{r_0=\left(\frac{J^2}{mMg}\right)^{1/3},}
$$
provided $J\ne0$ and $0<r_0<l$. Its angular <velocity> is $\dot\theta_0=J/(mr_0^2)$. No positive circular radius exists for $J=0$ with $M,g>0$.

For later use, the conserved radial <energy> is
$$
\mathcal H=\frac12(M+m)\dot r^2+U_J(r),\qquad
\boxed{U_J(r)=\frac{J^2}{2mr^2}+Mgr-Mgl.}
$$
The <effective potential> has $U_J''(r)=3J^2/(mr^4)>0$, so its stationary radius is a stable minimum. The <tension> remains positive in the ideal allowed region: the hanging <Newton's second law> gives $T_s=M(g+\ddot r)=Mm(g+r\dot\theta^2)/(M+m)>0$.

Initially $r_i=l/2$, $\dot r_i=0$ and $\dot\theta_i=v_0/r_i$, so the conserved <angular momentum> is $J=mlv_0/2$. For $v_0\ne0$, the <effective potential> contains the <centrifugal barrier>

$$
\boxed{v_0\ne0\ \Longrightarrow\ r(t)>0\ \text{throughout the ideal motion}.}

The exceptional case $v_0=0$ has no <centrifugal barrier>. Then $J=0$ and $\ddot r=-Mg/(M+m)$; starting at rest at $l/2$, the tabletop <mass> reaches the hole after $t=\sqrt{(M+m)l/(Mg)}$. The model ends at that event. Thus the usual nonzero initial tangential <speed> assumption matters.

The initial <angular momentum> is $J=mlv_0/2$, and the initial total <energy> is
$$
\mathcal H_i=\frac12mv_0^2-\frac12Mgl.

When the hanging <mass> reaches the table, $r=l$. Conservation of <angular momentum> fixes the tabletop tangential <speed> there to $J/(ml)=v_0/2$. At the limiting case of just reaching, radial <velocity> is zero. Equating the two <energies>,
$$
\frac12mv_0^2-\frac12Mgl=\frac12m\left(\frac{v_0}{2}\right)^2,

yields
$$
\boxed{v_{0,\rm just}^{\,2}=\frac{4Mgl}{3m}.}

More generally, the boundary radial <kinetic energy> is
$$
\frac12(M+m)\dot r(l)^2=\frac38mv_0^2-\frac12Mgl,

so reaching requires $v_0^2\ge4Mgl/(3m)$, with equality for just reaching and strict inequality for nonzero outward radial <speed> at contact. This condition is also sufficient: $U_J$ is strictly convex, and the allowed interval connecting the initial radius to $l$ has no intervening higher <effective potential> barrier. At the threshold the initial radial <acceleration> is outward and $r=l$ is the outer <turning point>.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For the initial radius $r_0=l/2$ to be circular, the radial equation requires
$$
\frac{mv_0^2}{r_0}=Mg,\qquad
v_0^2=\frac{Mgl}{2m}.
$$
Consider a small radial perturbation at fixed conserved <angular momentum> $J$. Using $J^2/(mr_0^3)=Mg$ and $r=r_0+\epsilon$ in the radial equation gives
$$
(M+m)\ddot\epsilon
=Mg\left[\left(1+\frac\epsilon{r_0}\right)^{-3}-1\right]
=-\frac{3Mg}{r_0}\epsilon+O(\epsilon^2).
$$
Consequently the linearized radial motion is <simple harmonic motion>:
$$
\boxed{\ddot\epsilon+\omega_r^2\epsilon=0,\qquad
\omega_r=\sqrt{\frac{3Mg}{(M+m)r_0}}=\sqrt{\frac{6Mg}{(M+m)l}}.}
$$
The positive curvature of the <effective potential> establishes radial stability. This is the small-amplitude limit, not an exact finite-amplitude sinusoid. A perturbation that changes $J$ shifts the circular radius; its radial oscillations are about the new radius to the same order. A radial displacement or radial impulse preserving $J$ gives the stated oscillations about $l/2$.

About a fixed origin in an <inertial frame>, the <angular momentum> and <torque> are
$$
\boxed{\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times m\mathbf v,\qquad
\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F.}
$$
For a specified fixed axis with unit vector $\widehat{\mathbf n}$, use the projections $L_n=\widehat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf L$ and $\tau_n=\widehat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\tau$. The <mass> is constant. Differentiating and applying <Newton's second law>,
$$
\frac{d\mathbf L}{dt}
=\mathbf v\times m\mathbf v+\mathbf r\times m\dot{\mathbf v}
=0+\mathbf r\times\mathbf F
=\boxed{\boldsymbol\tau}.
$$
Hence $\dot L_n=\tau_n$ for the fixed axis. If the net <torque> vanishes, the corresponding <angular momentum> is conserved. Fixing the origin and axis is essential to this unmodified form of the law.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Take rightward translation as positive and counterclockwise <angular acceleration> $\alpha$ as positive. Let $f$ denote signed ground <friction>, positive to the right. The source's lower-tangent winding makes the <tension> exert counterclockwise <torque> $Tr$. For a horizontal pull, translation and rotation obey
$$
Ma=T+f,\qquad I\alpha=Tr+Rf,\qquad a=-R\alpha.
$$
The final relation is the <rolling without slipping> constraint. Eliminating $f$ and $\alpha$ gives
$$
\boxed{a=\frac{TR(R-r)}{I+MR^2}>0,\qquad
f=-T\frac{I+MrR}{I+MR^2}.}
$$
Thus the requested magnitude of <static friction> is
$$
\boxed{F=|f|=T\frac{I+MrR}{I+MR^2}.}
$$
It acts leftward. Released from rest, the spool accelerates **rightward, toward the pulling hand**, and rotates clockwise. There is no contradiction between leftward <friction> and rightward motion: <static friction> prevents slipping of the contact point, and the horizontal <tension> is larger than $|f|$ because $r<R$.

= Solution

Keep the signs of part <11/b/i>. The <tension> has horizontal component $T\cos\theta$ but still has counterclockwise <torque> $Tr$, since it is tangent to the axle. Thus
$$
Ma=T\cos\theta+f,\qquad I\alpha=Tr+Rf,\qquad a=-R\alpha.
$$
Solving these equations gives the <critical pulling angle of a rolling spool> law
$$
\boxed{a=\frac{TR(R\cos\theta-r)}{I+MR^2},\qquad
f=-T\frac{I\cos\theta+MrR}{I+MR^2}.}
$$
The zero-acceleration angle is therefore
$$
\boxed{\theta_c=\cos^{-1}\left(\frac rR\right).}
$$
At this angle the line of action of the pull passes through the ground contact, so its <torque> about that point vanishes. A spool initially at rest stays at rest while the contact constraints hold; one already rolling has zero <acceleration>, rather than being brought to rest.

For equilibrium the vertical <normal force> is $N=Mg-T\sin\theta_c$ and $|f|=T\cos\theta_c$. The <static friction> inequality $|f|\le\mu N$ becomes
$$
T(\cos\theta_c+\mu\sin\theta_c)\le\mu Mg.
$$
Hence, for $0<r<R$,
$$
\boxed{T_{\max}=\frac{\mu Mg}{\cos\theta_c+\mu\sin\theta_c}
=\frac{\mu MgR}{r+\mu\sqrt{R^2-r^2}}.}
$$
At this limit the <normal force> is still nonnegative; for finite $\mu$ and positive $r$ it is positive. For $\theta_c<\theta\le\pi/2$, the numerator of $a$ is negative. Released from rest, the spool rolls leftward, **away from the pulling hand**, with counterclockwise rotation, provided the assumed <rolling without slipping> and ground contact remain possible. The nonzero <acceleration> is not obtained by assuming that <friction> simply opposes the centre's motion.
