== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

Measure $\theta$ from the downward vertical. The falling <mass> loses <gravitational potential energy> $3mgl(1-\cos\theta)$. The fixed pivot does no <work>, so <conservation of energy> gives its incident <speed> $u$:
$$
3mgl(1-\cos\theta)=\frac12(3m)u^2,
\qquad u=\sqrt{2gl(1-\cos\theta)}.
$$
At the bottom, the rod is vertical and the impact is horizontal. Its impulsive reaction therefore has no horizontal component. Let $U$ and $w$ be the subsequent horizontal <velocities> of the heavy and light balls. Horizontal <momentum conservation> and unit <coefficient of restitution> give
$$
3mu=3mU+mw,\qquad w-U=u.
$$
Thus $U=u/2$, and the light ball's outgoing <speed> is
$$
\boxed{w=\frac32\sqrt{2gl(1-\cos\theta)}
=3\sqrt{gl}\,\left|\sin\frac\theta2\right|.}
$$
The calculation neglects the sizes of the balls, treats the rod as massless, and uses an <elastic collision>. The expression vanishes for release at the bottom; a ball placed exactly at the unstable upright <equilibrium> requires a perturbation before it can fall.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

Write the <electron> <electric charge> as $-e$, with $e>0$ the <elementary charge>, and choose $\mathbf B=B\hat{\mathbf z}$ and initial <velocity> $u\hat{\mathbf x}$. In the nonrelativistic approximation, <Newton's second law> with the magnetic <Lorentz force> gives
$$
m_e\dot v_x=-eBv_y,\qquad m_e\dot v_y=eBv_x,\qquad \dot v_z=0.
$$
The magnetic <force> is perpendicular to the <velocity>, so it does no <work>: $d(m_e|\mathbf v|^2/2)/dt=0$. Setting $\omega_c=eB/m_e$, the solution with $x(0)=y(0)=0$ is
$$
v_x=u\cos\omega_ct,\qquad v_y=u\sin\omega_ct,
\qquad x=\frac u{\omega_c}\sin\omega_ct,\qquad
y=\frac u{\omega_c}(1-\cos\omega_ct).
$$
Consequently $x^2+(y-r)^2=r^2$, where $r=u/\omega_c$: this is a <cyclotron orbit>. Its <frequency> and <cyclotron radius> are
$$
\boxed{f=\frac{eB}{2\pi m_e},\qquad r=\frac{m_eu}{eB},\qquad
u=\sqrt{\frac{2eV}{m_e}}.}
$$
The accelerating <voltage> changes $u$ and $r$ but cancels out of $f$. Using $m_e=9.1094\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $e=1.6022\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$ gives
$$
B=\frac{2\pi m_e(2.5\times10^9\,\mathrm{s^{-1}})}e
\simeq0.0893\,\mathrm T,\qquad
u\simeq1.876\times10^7\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},
\qquad\boxed{r\simeq1.19\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm m.}
$$
Only the magnitude of the <magnetic field> is determined; its direction is perpendicular to the orbital plane, with the sense of rotation fixed by the negative <electric charge>. The claimed voltage independence is a nonrelativistic result: exactly, $\gamma=1+eV/(m_ec^2)$ and $f=eB/(2\pi\gamma m_e)$. At $1\,\mathrm{kV}$ the correction is about $0.2\%$. The stated model also neglects the <energy> lost to emitted <electromagnetic radiation>.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

Let $\mu$ be the instantaneous total <mass> of the rocket, including its remaining <rocket propellant>. During a short ejection it changes by $d\mu<0$. The expelled <mass> $-d\mu$ has <velocity> $v-v_0$ to first order in a fixed <inertial frame>. With no external <force>, <momentum conservation> reads
$$
\mu v=(\mu+d\mu)(v+dv)+(-d\mu)(v-v_0).
$$
Discarding the second-order product gives $\mu\,dv=-v_0\,d\mu$. Integrate from initial total <mass> $m_0+M$ to <rocket dry mass> $m_0$:
$$
v_f=-v_0\int_{m_0+M}^{m_0}\frac{d\mu}{\mu},
\qquad\boxed{v_f=v_0\ln\left(1+\frac M{m_0}\right).}
$$
For constant <mass flow rate> $\alpha=-\dot\mu>0$, the same <rocket equation> gives $\mu(t)=m_0+M-\alpha t$ and $v(t)=v_0\ln[(m_0+M)/\mu(t)]$ until $t=M/\alpha$. The final result depends on the <rocket mass ratio>, not on how rapidly the fuel is ejected. These are Newtonian <velocities>; a very large <rocket mass ratio> would require a relativistic treatment even if the exhaust is slow in the instantaneous rocket frame.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

Use the <inertial frame> in which the departure point and destination are stationary, with separation $d$. The astronauts' elapsed <proper time> is $\tau=t/\gamma$, whereas $d=vt$. Hence
$$
\gamma\beta=\frac d{c\tau}=\frac45,\qquad
\gamma^2-(\gamma\beta)^2=1,
\qquad \gamma=\frac{\sqrt{41}}5,\qquad \beta=\frac4{\sqrt{41}}.
$$
The Earth-frame <travel time> is $t=\sqrt{41}\,\mathrm{yr}\simeq6.40\,\mathrm{yr}$ and the <speed> is approximately $0.625c$. The spacecraft's <relativistic kinetic energy> is therefore
$$
\boxed{K=(\gamma-1)mc^2
=\left(\frac{\sqrt{41}}5-1\right)(10^5\,\mathrm{kg})c^2
\simeq2.52\times10^{21}\,\mathrm J.}
$$
The five years are not the time to use in $v=d/t$ in the departure frame. This is the cruising <kinetic energy>, with departure and arrival <acceleration> intervals omitted, rather than a complete propulsion <energy> budget.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

Choose zero <potential energy> at the equilibrium position. For the <simple harmonic oscillator>, the <kinetic energy> and <elastic potential energy> give the <Lagrangian>
$$
L=T-V=\frac12m\dot x^2-\frac12kx^2.
$$
Its <canonical momentum> is $p=\partial L/\partial\dot x=m\dot x$. The <Legendre transform> defining the <Hamiltonian> consequently gives
$$
\boxed{H(x,p)=p\dot x-L=\frac{p^2}{2m}+\frac12kx^2=T+V.}
$$
The <Euler-Lagrange equation> is $m\ddot x+kx=0$. Thus along the motion
$$
\frac{dH}{dt}=m\dot x\ddot x+kx\dot x
=\dot x(m\ddot x+kx)=0.
$$
This proves <conservation of energy>. Equivalently, the <Lagrangian> has no explicit time dependence, so $dH/dt=-\partial L/\partial t=0$. The <Lagrangian> itself need not be constant: the <kinetic energy> and <potential energy> exchange periodically.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <parallel axis theorem> relates the <moment of inertia> $I$ about an axis to $I_{\rm CM}$ about a parallel axis through the <centre of mass>, separated by perpendicular distance $d$:
$$
\boxed{I=I_{\rm CM}+M_{\rm tot}d^2.}
$$
In the defining mass <integral>, expanding the squared distance produces a cross term whose first mass moment about the <centre of mass> is zero.

For a thin planar <rigid body>, the <perpendicular axis theorem> applies to two perpendicular axes $x,y$ in its plane and the perpendicular axis $z$, all meeting at the same point:
$$
\boxed{I_z=I_x+I_y.}
$$
Indeed, $I_x=\int y^2\,dm$, $I_y=\int x^2\,dm$, and $I_z=\int(x^2+y^2)\,dm$. The planar condition is essential; this is not an identity for an arbitrary three-dimensional <rigid body>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the usual dumbbell geometry: the uniform thin rod joins the disc centres and is perpendicular to both disc faces. The required axis through the rod midpoint lies in a direction parallel to a diameter of each disc. For either disc, <rotational symmetry> and the <perpendicular axis theorem> give
$$
I_{\rm diameter}=\frac12I_{\rm normal}=\frac14Ma^2.
$$
Each disc centre lies a distance $l/2$ from the requested axis. The <parallel axis theorem> therefore gives a disc contribution $Ma^2/4+M(l/2)^2$. The rod contributes
$$
I_{\rm rod}=\frac{m_0}{l}\int_{-l/2}^{l/2}x^2\,dx
=\frac1{12}m_0l^2.
$$
Add the two disc contributions and the rod's <moment of inertia>:
$$
\boxed{I=\frac1{12}m_0l^2+\frac12Ml^2+\frac12Ma^2.}
$$
Using $Ma^2/2$ for each disc's central contribution would instead use an axis perpendicular to its face, which is parallel to the rod and is not the requested axis.

== 7
{scope}

=== Solution

Take $x$ horizontal, $y$ upward and $g>0$. Resolve <Newton's second law> into components for <projectile motion with linear drag>:
$$
m\dot v_x=-\alpha v_x,\qquad
m\dot v_y=-mg-\alpha v_y,
\qquad v_x(0)=v_0\cos\theta,\quad v_y(0)=v_0\sin\theta.
$$
For $\alpha>0$, let $\tau_d=m/\alpha$. An <integrating factor> $e^{t/\tau_d}$ gives
$$
\boxed{v_x(t)=v_0\cos\theta\,e^{-t/\tau_d},\qquad
v_y(t)=\left(v_0\sin\theta+g\tau_d\right)e^{-t/\tau_d}-g\tau_d.}
$$
The relaxation <time> is $\tau_d$, and the <terminal velocity> is $(0,-g\tau_d)$. For fixed finite $t$, expanding the <exponential function> as $\alpha\to0$ gives the continuous limit $v_x=v_0\cos\theta$ and $v_y=v_0\sin\theta-gt$; the two terms proportional to $\tau_d$ cancel before taking the limit.

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

With no <drag force>, the horizontal <velocity> graph is a horizontal line at $v_0\cos\theta$. The vertical <velocity> graph is a straight line with initial value $v_0\sin\theta$ and gradient $-g$:
$$
\boxed{v_x=v_0\cos\theta,\qquad v_y=v_0\sin\theta-gt.}
$$
For an upward launch, it crosses zero at the highest point, $t=v_0\sin\theta/g$. The ground-to-ground flight ends at $t=2v_0\sin\theta/g$, where $v_y=-v_0\sin\theta$. The left panel below shows these components; any continuation after ground contact is only the formal free-flight model.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

For positive <linear drag>, $v_x$ decays exponentially toward zero. The vertical <velocity> decreases monotonically toward $-mg/\alpha$, with an initially steeper gradient $-g-(\alpha/m)v_0\sin\theta$ and positive second <time derivative> for an upward launch. It crosses zero at
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm top}=\frac m\alpha\ln\left(1+\frac{\alpha v_0\sin\theta}{mg}\right).}
$$
The right panel shows the two components and the formal <terminal velocity>. To identify the end of the physical flight, integrate the vertical <velocity> once:
$$
y(t)=\tau_d(v_0\sin\theta+g\tau_d)(1-e^{-t/\tau_d})-g\tau_dt.
$$
The first positive root of $y(t)=0$ is ground contact. The <terminal velocity> need not be reached before that event; dashed portions in the sketch are mathematical extensions without a ground surface.

== 8
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For a uniform thin disc, the <surface mass density> is $\sigma=m/(\pi a^2)$. An annulus of radius $r$ and thickness $dr$ has <mass> $dm=2\pi\sigma r\,dr$, all at distance $r$ from the normal central axis. Its contribution to the <moment of inertia> is $r^2dm$, so
$$
I=\int_0^a r^2(2\pi\sigma r\,dr)
=\frac{2m}{a^2}\left[\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^a,
\qquad\boxed{I=\frac12ma^2.}
$$
This establishes the <moment of inertia of a uniform disc>; the normal axis and uniform <surface mass density> are part of the result.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Take the rotation axis as $z$. A slice of the uniform sphere at coordinate $z$ is a disc of radius $R(z)=\sqrt{a^2-z^2}$ and <mass> $dm=\rho\pi(a^2-z^2)\,dz$, where $\rho=3M/(4\pi a^3)$ is its <mass density>. Applying <8/a> to the slice gives
$$
dI=\frac12R(z)^2\,dm
=\frac{\rho\pi}{2}(a^2-z^2)^2\,dz.
$$
The slices' axes coincide, so no displaced-axis correction is needed. Integrating,
$$
\begin{aligned}
I&=\frac{\rho\pi}{2}\int_{-a}^a(a^4-2a^2z^2+z^4)\,dz\\
&=\frac{\rho\pi}{2}\left(2a^5-\frac43a^5+\frac25a^5\right)
=\frac{8\pi\rho a^5}{15}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the <moment of inertia of a uniform solid sphere> is
$$
\boxed{I=\frac25Ma^2.}
$$
The sphere's <rotational symmetry> makes this valid about every central axis. A thin spherical shell would have a different <mass distribution> and a different <moment of inertia>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose positive horizontal <velocity> to the right and positive spin <angular velocity> anticlockwise, as in the source drawing. At the bottom of the sphere, the rotational <velocity> is then to the right: the initial tangential <contact-point velocity> is
$$
w_1=v_1+a\Omega_1.
$$
For the illustrated positive $v_1,\Omega_1$, the contact slips to the right, so the tangential contact <force> acts leftward. The upward <normal force> acts through the sphere centre and produces no spin <torque>. The tangential contact <force> acts at lever arm $a$ and produces a clockwise <torque>, reducing the positive spin. The weight acts vertically downward through the <centre of mass>.

During the short impact the contact <impulses> dominate the weight's <impulse>. In an ideal elastic rough contact, the tangential deformation can store and return <energy>; its contact <force> need not be a dissipative sliding-friction law throughout the collision. For other signed incoming states, reverse the initial tangential <force> if $w_1<0$; if $w_1=0$, no tangential <impulse> is required.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Write the sphere's <mass> as $M$ and use $I=2Ma^2/5$ from <8/b>. Let $K_n$ and $K_t$ be the upward and rightward contact <impulses>. The initial vertical <velocity> is $-u_1$ and the outgoing vertical <velocity> is $u_2$. Perfect normal elasticity gives
$$
u_2=u_1,\qquad K_n=M(u_2+u_1)=2Mu_1.
$$
The tangential and angular <impulse> equations are
$$
M(v_2-v_1)=K_t,\qquad I(\Omega_2-\Omega_1)=aK_t.
$$
For the ideal <elastic rough-sphere bounce>, the tangential translational plus rotational <kinetic energy> is restored on separation. Substituting these two equations into that requirement gives
$$
0=K_t(v_1+a\Omega_1)
+\frac{K_t^2}{2}\left(\frac1M+\frac{a^2}{I}\right).
$$
The interacting rough-contact branch, rather than the smooth branch $K_t=0$, therefore has
$$
K_t=-\frac{2(v_1+a\Omega_1)}{1/M+a^2/I}
=-\frac47M(v_1+a\Omega_1).
$$
Consequently, with all tangential quantities signed in the convention of <8/c>,
$$
\boxed{u_2=u_1,\qquad
v_2=\frac{3v_1-4a\Omega_1}{7},\qquad
\Omega_2=-\frac{10v_1+3a\Omega_1}{7a}.}
$$
In particular, $v_2+a\Omega_2=-(v_1+a\Omega_1)$: the tangential <contact-point velocity> reverses. The outgoing $v_2$ may be negative; the sphere rebounds backward when $a\Omega_1>3v_1/4$. Substitution also checks both total <kinetic energy> and <angular momentum> about the contact point, $I\Omega-Mav$.

This result uses the conventional perfectly elastic, fully rough contact idealization. The word *rough* alone, with an unspecified finite <coefficient of friction>, would not determine the outgoing tangential state. For example, imposing sticking at separation would instead give $K_t=-2M(v_1+a\Omega_1)/7$ and dissipate $M(v_1+a\Omega_1)^2/7$, contradicting total elasticity unless $w_1=0$. The displayed elastic branch requires enough tangential <impulse>; a Coulomb bound $|K_t|\leq\mu K_n$ would at least require $\mu\geq2|v_1+a\Omega_1|/(7u_1)$.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For a massive free particle with <rest mass> $m$ and <velocity> $\mathbf v$,
$$
\mathbf p=\gamma m\mathbf v,\qquad E=\gamma mc^2,
\qquad\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}.
$$
The <relativistic energy-momentum relation> follows directly:
$$
E^2-c^2|\mathbf p|^2
=\gamma^2m^2c^4(1-v^2/c^2)
=\boxed{m^2c^4.}
$$
This is the squared Minkowski norm of the <four-momentum> multiplied by $c^2$. The <Lorentz transformation> preserves that norm, so it has the same value in every <inertial frame>. In the particle's <rest frame>, the <momentum> is zero and the total <energy> is its <rest energy> $mc^2$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Choose particle 1's incoming <velocity> positive and particle 2's negative. The final particle is stationary, so <four-momentum conservation> requires zero total incoming <momentum>:
$$
m\gamma_1v_1-2m\gamma_2\frac c3=0,\qquad
\gamma_2=\frac3{2\sqrt2}.
$$
It follows that $\gamma_1\beta_1=1/\sqrt2$. Squaring gives $\beta_1^2/(1-\beta_1^2)=1/2$, hence
$$
\boxed{v_1=\frac c{\sqrt3},\qquad \gamma_1=\sqrt{\frac32}.}
$$
The initial total <energy> becomes the final <rest energy>. Therefore
$$
Mc^2=mc^2\gamma_1+2mc^2\gamma_2,
\qquad\boxed{M=\frac{3+\sqrt3}{\sqrt2}\,m\simeq3.346m.}
$$
The extra <invariant mass> is the incoming <kinetic energy> retained as internal <energy> of the merged particle: $(M-3m)c^2$. It does not violate <conservation of energy> or <four-momentum conservation>; the sum of the separate <rest masses> is not a conserved quantity in an <inelastic collision>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $S'$ move at $+v$ along $x$ relative to $S$. The <Lorentz transformation> of <energy> and <momentum>, from primed to unprimed components, is
$$
E=\gamma(E'+vp_x'),\qquad
p_x=\gamma\left(p_x'+\frac{vE'}{c^2}\right),\qquad
p_y=p_y',\qquad p_z=p_z'.
$$
The inverse equations have $v$ replaced by $-v$ and the primes interchanged. A <photon> making angle $\theta'$ with $+x$ in $S'$ has $p_x'=(E'/c)\cos\theta'$. Thus its general <Doppler factor> is
$$
D=\frac E{E'}=\gamma(1+\beta\cos\theta').
$$
The requested blueshifted expression assumes propagation parallel to the boost, $\theta'=0$:
$$
\boxed{D=\gamma(1+\beta)=\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}}.}
$$
The propagation-direction assumption matters: for an oppositely travelling <photon>, $D=\gamma(1-\beta)$, the reciprocal of the displayed factor. Relative frame <speed> alone does not select the longitudinal <relativistic Doppler effect>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For the forward ray of <9/b/i>, the <ultrarelativistic longitudinal Doppler factor> follows from the exact expression in terms of the <Lorentz factor>:
$$
\boxed{D=\gamma+\sqrt{\gamma^2-1}.}
$$
Indeed, $\gamma\beta=\sqrt{\gamma^2-1}$ for a positive boost. If $\beta=1-\epsilon$, the exact formulas and their consistent <asymptotic expansions> are
$$
D=\sqrt{\frac{2-\epsilon}{\epsilon}}
=\sqrt{\frac2\epsilon}\left(1-\frac\epsilon4+O(\epsilon^2)\right),
\qquad
\gamma=\frac1{\sqrt{2\epsilon-\epsilon^2}}
=\frac1{\sqrt{2\epsilon}}\left(1+\frac\epsilon4+O(\epsilon^2)\right).
$$
Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{D=2\gamma-\frac1{2\gamma}+O(\gamma^{-3})\sim2\gamma.}
$$
**The printed equality is not exact.** For a concrete ultrarelativistic example, $\gamma=10$ gives the correct $D=10+\sqrt{99}\simeq19.949874$, whereas $\sqrt{4\gamma^2-1}=\sqrt{399}\simeq19.974984$. The latter expands as $2\gamma-1/(4\gamma)+O(\gamma^{-3})$, with the wrong first correction.

One can obtain the printed expression by using $D^2=2/\epsilon-1$ but replacing $\gamma^2$ by only its leading approximation $1/(2\epsilon)$. This retains a constant term in the first equation while discarding a same-order term in the second. Thus $D\approx\sqrt{4\gamma^2-1}$ is admissible only as a leading relative-accuracy estimate, of the same order as $D\sim2\gamma$; it is not the stated identity or a correct next-order approximation.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Let the incident <photon energies> be $E$ and $\varepsilon$, meeting at angle $\psi$ in the <laboratory frame>. Add their <four-momenta>; because each is null, the squared total invariant <energy> is
$$
\mathcal S=(E+\varepsilon)^2-c^2|\mathbf p_1+\mathbf p_2|^2
=2E\varepsilon(1-\cos\psi).
$$
For <photon-photon pair production>, its threshold value is $(2m_ec^2)^2$: the <electron> and <positron> have zero relative <momentum> in their <centre-of-momentum frame>. The minimum possible $E$ occurs for a head-on collision, $\psi=\pi$, so
$$
4E\varepsilon=4m_e^2c^4,
\qquad\boxed{E_{\min}=\frac{(m_ec^2)^2}{\varepsilon}.}
$$
With $m_ec^2\simeq0.511\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and $\varepsilon=10^{-3}\,\mathrm{eV}$,
$$
\boxed{E_{\min}\simeq2.61\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{eV}
=261\,\mathrm{TeV}\simeq41.8\,\mathrm J.}
$$
The quoted background <photon energy> is used directly, without substituting a different characteristic value of the <cosmic microwave background>. A less opposed collision has a higher threshold; parallel <photons> cannot produce this massive pair.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

At the threshold found in <9/c/i>, both products are stationary in the <centre-of-momentum frame>, so they have the same <velocity> in the <laboratory frame>. Take the energetic incident <photon> along $+x$. The total <energy> is $E+\varepsilon$ and total <momentum> is $(E-\varepsilon)/c$, so the <centre-of-momentum frame> moves at
$$
\boxed{\mathbf V=c\frac{E-\varepsilon}{E+\varepsilon}\hat{\mathbf x}.}
$$
For the numerical threshold,
$$
\frac Vc=1-\frac{2\varepsilon}{E+\varepsilon}
\simeq1-7.66\times10^{-18},\qquad
\gamma_{\rm CM}=\frac{E+\varepsilon}{2m_ec^2}\simeq2.56\times10^8.
$$
This is extremely close to, but strictly below, the <speed of light>. Each product has <energy> $(E+\varepsilon)/2$ in the <laboratory frame>. Above threshold the same ratio of total <momentum> to total <energy> determines the motion of the pair's <centre-of-momentum frame>, but the individual <electron> and <positron> need not have a common <velocity>.

== 10
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For independent <generalized coordinates> $q_i$, their <generalized velocities> $\dot q_i$ and a <Lagrangian> $L(q,\dot q,t)$, the <Euler-Lagrange equations> are
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}\right)
-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}=0.}
$$
Here the partial <derivatives> treat $q_i,\dot q_i,t$ as independent arguments, whereas $d/dt$ follows the motion. For a conservative mechanical system, choose coordinates satisfying the <mechanical constraints>, express the <kinetic energy> $T$ and <potential energy> $V$ in those coordinates, set $L=T-V$, and evaluate these equations to obtain the <equations of motion>. If nonconservative <generalized forces> $Q_i$ are present, they appear on the right-hand side.

A <cyclic coordinate> is absent explicitly from $L$: $\partial L/\partial q_i=0$, even though $L$ may depend on $\dot q_i$. Its <canonical momentum> is consequently conserved,
$$
p_i=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i},\qquad\boxed{\dot p_i=0.}
$$
This does not require $\dot q_i=0$ or even that $\dot q_i$ be constant; the relation between <canonical momentum> and <generalized velocity> depends on the <Lagrangian>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Take positive $x$ horizontally toward the downhill end of the plane, with $0\leq\theta<\pi/2$. The <normal force> is $N=mg\cos\theta$, since there is no <acceleration> perpendicular to the stationary plane. Along the plane, the only tangential <force> is $mg\sin\theta$, so <Newton's second law> gives $a_\parallel=g\sin\theta$.

Its horizontal projection is therefore
$$
\boxed{a_x=g\sin\theta\cos\theta.}
$$
Equivalently, the <normal force> has horizontal component $N\sin\theta$ while gravity has none, giving $ma_x=N\sin\theta$. For a plane whose height increases in the positive $x$ direction, the answer instead carries a minus sign. A horizontal plane gives zero <acceleration>; the horizontal projection also tends to zero for a vertical plane.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Model the cart as a <particle on a fixed plane curve>, remaining constrained to the track. Its vertical <velocity> is $\dot y=y'(x)\dot x$, so its <kinetic energy>, <gravitational potential energy> and <Lagrangian> are
$$
T=\frac m2[1+y'(x)^2]\dot x^2,\qquad
V=mgy(x),\qquad
L=\frac m2[1+y'^2]\dot x^2-mgy.
$$
Consequently the <canonical momentum> conjugate to the horizontal coordinate is
$$
\boxed{p_x=m[1+y'^2]\dot x.}
$$
It is not simply the horizontal component $m\dot x$ of the mechanical <momentum>, because a change in $x$ also moves the cart vertically. Differentiate carefully in the <Euler-Lagrange equation>:
$$
\frac{d p_x}{dt}=m(1+y'^2)\ddot x+2my'y''\dot x^2,
\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial x}=my'y''\dot x^2-mgy'.
$$
Subtracting yields
$$
\boxed{(1+y'^2)\ddot x=-gy'-y'y''\dot x^2.}
$$
The term involving $y''$ accounts for the changing track geometry. An independent check is <conservation of energy>:
$$
H=\frac m2(1+y'^2)\dot x^2+mgy,
\qquad
\frac{dH}{dt}=m\dot x\big[(1+y'^2)\ddot x+y'y''\dot x^2+gy'\big]=0.
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

If $y''=0$ over the subsequent segment, its slope is a constant $s$ and the <equation of motion> reduces to
$$
\boxed{\ddot x=-\frac{gs}{1+s^2}.}
$$
There is uniform horizontal <acceleration>, not generally zero <acceleration>. Starting that segment at $(t_0,x_0,\dot x_0)$,
$$
x(t)=x_0+\dot x_0(t-t_0)-\frac{gs}{2(1+s^2)}(t-t_0)^2.
$$
For a downhill straight track with $s=-\tan\theta$, this gives $\ddot x=g\sin\theta\cos\theta$, agreeing with <10/b>. Only $s=0$ gives constant horizontal <velocity>.

The condition $y''\to0$ must describe an actually straight limiting segment, with bounded <velocity>, for that subsequent constant-<acceleration> conclusion. If $y''$ vanishes only at an isolated inflection point, the boxed expression describes the instantaneous <acceleration> there; it does not make the rest of the track straight. Likewise, a vanishing $y''$ alone does not make the product $y''\dot x^2$ negligible in a limit where $\dot x$ diverges.

== 11
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use a <reference frame> moving with Jupiter, neglect the spacecraft's effect on the planet and neglect the Sun during the short encounter. The Newtonian <central force> makes the <orbit> planar and conserves the <angular momentum> $J=mr^2\dot\vartheta$. In plane <polar coordinates>, the <kinetic energy> is $m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\vartheta^2)/2$. Choosing zero <gravitational potential energy> at infinity gives
$$
\boxed{E=\frac12m\dot r^2+\frac{J^2}{2mr^2}-\frac{GmM_J}{r}.}
$$
The terms are respectively radial <kinetic energy>, transverse <kinetic energy>, and attractive <gravitational potential energy>. The second term is often combined with the third into the radial <effective potential>. Far from Jupiter,
$$
\boxed{E=\frac12mv_0^2,\qquad J=mbv_0.}
$$
Here $b$ is the perpendicular <impact parameter> of the incoming asymptote, not the eventual <periapsis distance>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $\mu_J=GM_J$. At the <periapsis distance>, the radial <velocity> vanishes. Insert $E=mv_0^2/2$ and $J=mbv_0$ into <11/a>:
$$
\frac12v_0^2=\frac{b^2v_0^2}{2r_{\min}^2}-\frac{\mu_J}{r_{\min}},
\qquad v_0^2r_{\min}^2+2\mu_Jr_{\min}-b^2v_0^2=0.
$$
The positive root is
$$
\boxed{r_{\min}=\frac{\sqrt{\mu_J^2+b^2v_0^4}-\mu_J}{v_0^2}
=\sqrt{b^2+\left(\frac{\mu_J}{v_0^2}\right)^2}-\frac{\mu_J}{v_0^2}.}
$$
Rationalizing gives the numerically stable alternative $r_{\min}=b^2v_0^2/[\sqrt{\mu_J^2+b^2v_0^4}+\mu_J]$. Attraction makes $r_{\min}<b$. This is a point-planet <scattering> calculation; a noncolliding physical encounter must also have $r_{\min}$ outside Jupiter's surface. At $b=0$, the limiting formula gives a radial collision, not a flyby.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $h=J/m=bv_0$ be the <specific angular momentum>, and measure the orbital angle $\vartheta$ from <periapsis>. Set $w=1/r$. From $\dot\vartheta=h/r^2$, differentiating gives $\dot r=-h\,dw/d\vartheta$. The radial <equation of motion> $\ddot r-r\dot\vartheta^2=-\mu_J/r^2$ becomes the <Binet equation>
$$
\frac{d^2w}{d\vartheta^2}+w=\frac{\mu_J}{h^2}.
$$
Its solution symmetric about <periapsis> is
$$
w=\frac{\mu_J}{h^2}(1+e\cos\vartheta),\qquad
e=\sqrt{1+\frac{v_0^2h^2}{\mu_J^2}}
=\sqrt{1+\frac{b^2v_0^4}{\mu_J^2}}.
$$
For example, the expression for the <orbital eccentricity> follows by substituting $w$ and $\dot r=-hw'$ into the <energy> equation: $E/m=\mu_J^2(e^2-1)/(2h^2)=v_0^2/2$. Since $e>1$, this is a <hyperbolic Kepler orbit>.

The outgoing asymptote occurs at $\vartheta_\infty$ with $\cos\vartheta_\infty=-1/e$, where $\pi/2<\vartheta_\infty<\pi$. The incoming <velocity> points inward along the incoming radial asymptote, whereas the outgoing <velocity> points outward. Therefore the change in <velocity> direction is $\phi=2\vartheta_\infty-\pi$, not $2\vartheta_\infty$. It follows that
$$
\sin\frac\phi2=\frac1e,\qquad
\boxed{\cot\frac\phi2=\sqrt{e^2-1}=\frac{bv_0^2}{GM_J}.}
$$
For $b>0$, this gives $0<\phi<\pi$: a large <impact parameter> produces a small deflection, and the ideal point-mass limit $b\to0^+$ approaches a reversal.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

A $90^\circ$ <gravitational scattering angle> has $\cot(\phi/2)=1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{b=\frac{GM_J}{v_0^2}
=\frac{(6.6743\times10^{-11})(2\times10^{27})}{(10^4)^2}\,\mathrm m
\simeq1.33\times10^9\,\mathrm m.}
$$
The denominator uses the squared <speed> in $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, not its numerical value in $\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. From <11/b>, the corresponding <periapsis distance> is $r_{\min}=(\sqrt2-1)b\simeq5.53\times10^8\,\mathrm m$; this differs substantially from the <impact parameter> because of gravitational focusing.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Take Jupiter's heliocentric <velocity> as $\mathbf V_J=13\hat{\mathbf x}\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. <Conservation of energy> in the Jupiter frame makes the incoming and outgoing asymptotic <speeds> both $10\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. For the $90^\circ$ encounter, an outgoing <velocity> in Jupiter's direction requires the incoming Jupiter-frame <velocity> to be perpendicular to it:
$$
\mathbf u_{\rm in}=\pm10\hat{\mathbf y}\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}},\qquad
\mathbf u_{\rm out}=10\hat{\mathbf x}\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.
$$
Use a <Galilean transformation> back to the Sun frame:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf v_{\rm in}=13\hat{\mathbf x}\pm10\hat{\mathbf y}\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}},\qquad
\mathbf v_{\rm out}=23\hat{\mathbf x}\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The heliocentric <speeds> are therefore
$$
\boxed{v_{\rm in}=\sqrt{13^2+10^2}\simeq16.4\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}},\qquad
v_{\rm out}=23.0\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The incoming direction makes an angle $\arctan(10/13)\simeq37.6^\circ$ with Jupiter's motion; its two signs describe mirror-image encounter planes. The <gravitational assist> transfers orbital <energy> from Jupiter to the spacecraft: the spacecraft's specific <kinetic energy> increases by $\tfrac12(23^2-16.4^2)\,\mathrm{km^2\,s^{-2}}$, or, using the unrounded squared incoming <speed>, exactly $130\,\mathrm{km^2\,s^{-2}}=1.30\times10^8\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$. There is no gain in asymptotic <speed> in the Jupiter frame; the gain is a consequence of changing frames while the planet is moving.
