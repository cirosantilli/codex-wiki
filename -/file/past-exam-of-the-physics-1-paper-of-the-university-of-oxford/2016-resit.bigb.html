== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

Take upward <velocity> as positive. The linear <drag force> is $-mkv$, so it opposes the motion both during ascent and descent. <Newton's second law> gives $\dot v+kv=-g$. With $v(0)=u$, the <integrating factor> $e^{kt}$ yields
$$
\boxed{v(t)=\left(u+\frac gk\right)e^{-kt}-\frac gk,\qquad k>0.}
$$
The <terminal velocity> is $-g/k$. The limit $k\to0$ at fixed $t$ recovers $u-gt$; expanding the exponential is necessary to see the cancellation of the apparently singular terms.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

The familiar right-angle result requires an **elastic collision** with conserved horizontal <momentum> and the appropriate treatment of <rotational kinetic energy>. In the ideal smooth-sphere model the spins do not change during the collision, so the translational <kinetic energy> is separately conserved. For equal masses, with initial <velocity> $\mathbf u$ and outgoing <velocities> $\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2$,
$$
\mathbf u=\mathbf v_1+\mathbf v_2,\qquad u^2=v_1^2+v_2^2.
$$
Squaring the first equality and using the second gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_2=0.}
$$
Thus the two nonzero outgoing directions are **perpendicular**. In the head-on limiting case one sphere stops, so an angle for its zero velocity is undefined. An ideal elastic model that enforces <rolling without slipping> at both ends gives the same energy equation: each solid sphere has total <kinetic energy> $7mv^2/10$, and the common factor cancels.

Elasticity is not stated explicitly in the question. It cannot be omitted from the argument: for a smooth equal-mass collision with normal incoming component $u_n$ and <coefficient of restitution> $e<1$, the outgoing scalar product is $(1-e^2)u_n^2/4>0$. Moreover, after a smooth impact the spheres need not still be rolling; subsequent frictional relaxation can change their directions. The right-angle statement therefore concerns the specified ideal elastic collision, not arbitrary later rolling trajectories.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

In <spherical coordinates>, the <Lagrangian> is
$$
\mathcal L=\frac m2\left(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2+r^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2\right)-V(r).
$$
A <central force> exerts zero <torque>, so the <angular momentum> vector is constant and the orbit lies in a fixed plane. Choose that plane as $\theta=\pi/2$; then $\ell=mr^2\dot\phi$ is conserved. Substituting this relation into the <energy> gives
$$
\boxed{E=\frac m2\dot r^2+V_{\rm eff}(r),\qquad V_{\rm eff}(r)=V(r)+\frac{\ell^2}{2mr^2}.}
$$
The <effective potential> reduces radial motion to a one-dimensional problem. Allowed radii have $E\geq V_{\rm eff}$, radial turning points satisfy equality, and a <circular orbit> occurs at $V_{\rm eff}'=0$. A strict local minimum gives stable radial oscillations. Eliminating $\dot\phi$ directly inside the original <Lagrangian> would require a <Routhian>; the energy derivation avoids giving the centrifugal term the wrong sign.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

Let the motor's constant <power> be $P>0$ and put $\omega=\dot\theta$. The braking coefficient in the proposed formula must describe a retarding <torque> $k\omega$. This <constant-power rotational acceleration> obeys the <energy balance>:
$$
\frac d{dt}\left(\frac I2\omega^2\right)=P-k\omega^2.
$$
Consequently the steady <angular velocity> is $\omega_0=\sqrt{P/k}$, and the ordinary rotational <frequency> is $\omega_0/(2\pi)$. Solving the linear equation for $\omega^2$ from rest gives
$$
\omega^2(t)=\omega_0^2\left(1-e^{-2kt/I}\right),\qquad
\boxed{t=-\frac I{2k}\ln\left(1-\frac{\omega^2}{\omega_0^2}\right).}
$$
**The printed time formula is missing a minus sign.** Its logarithm is negative for $0<\omega<\omega_0$, whereas elapsed time must be positive. The terminal speed is approached only as $t\to\infty$.

A constant-power motor starting exactly from rest is an idealization: its driving <torque> $P/\omega$ would diverge. The energy equation has a regular solution for $\omega^2$, although the initial angular acceleration is singular. If $k\omega$ were literally a tangential force, a lever arm would also be needed; the dimensions of the requested expression identify $k$ as a rotational damping coefficient.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

Centre the square on the $xy$ plane, with uniform surface <mass density> $m/a^2$. For an in-plane axis at angle $\alpha$ to the $x$ direction, the perpendicular distance of $(x,y)$ from the axis is $-x\sin\alpha+y\cos\alpha$. Therefore its <moment of inertia> is
$$
I_\alpha=\frac m{a^2}\int_{-a/2}^{a/2}\int_{-a/2}^{a/2}(-x\sin\alpha+y\cos\alpha)^2\,dx\,dy.
$$
The mixed integral vanishes by symmetry, while the $x^2$ and $y^2$ integrals are equal. Thus
$$
\boxed{I_\alpha=\frac{ma^2}{12}(\sin^2\alpha+\cos^2\alpha)=\frac{ma^2}{12}.}
$$
This is the in-plane isotropy of the square's <inertia tensor>: its two central in-plane principal moments coincide. It does not assert isotropy about arbitrary three-dimensional axes, nor about parallel axes that miss the centre.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

Choose the emitted beam direction as positive $x$. In the original laboratory frame the sensor moves with <velocity> $-0.2c$; let the laser recede from it with <velocity> $-\beta c$. The collinear <relativistic Doppler shift> from source to laboratory and then to sensor gives
$$
\frac{\nu_{\rm sensor}}{\nu_0}=\sqrt{\frac{1.2}{0.8}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}}.
$$
At the detection threshold this ratio is $633/1000$. Hence
$$
q=\frac{(633/1000)^2}{1.5}=0.267126,\qquad
\boxed{\beta=\frac{1-q}{1+q}\simeq0.578375.}
$$
The minimum collinear speed is therefore **about $0.578c$, away from the approaching sensor**, while the laser is still aimed towards it. The source must recede fast enough to overcome the sensor's approach-induced blueshift. A larger recession speed gives a longer received <wavelength>, if the detector has no additional upper cutoff.

As a check, the required relative recession speed is $(1-0.633^2)c/(1+0.633^2)$; <relativistic velocity addition> gives this same value from $(\beta-0.2)c/(1-0.2\beta)$.

== 7
{scope}

=== Solution

The initial total <momentum> is zero, so the outgoing <neutron> recoils with momentum magnitude $E_\gamma/c$. Put $W=(m_p+m_\pi)c^2$. Conservation of <relativistic energy> gives
$$
W=E_\gamma+\sqrt{m_n^2c^4+E_\gamma^2},\qquad
\boxed{E_\gamma=\frac{W^2-m_n^2c^4}{2W}\simeq129.4\,\mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
For example the rest energies $938.2721$, $139.5704$ and $939.5654\,\mathrm{MeV}$ for the proton, charged pion and neutron give this value.<Review of Particle Physics by Particle Data Group (2022)>[Charged-pion, proton and neutron listings, page 1 of each: mass values in MeV]{ref} The result is smaller than the initial-to-final rest-energy difference of about $138.3\,\mathrm{MeV}$, because some released <energy> becomes neutron recoil <kinetic energy>. Atomic binding of a captured pion is neglected in the stated stationary-particle model.

== 8
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

The symbols in the question are diameters: write drum radius $a=R/2$, axle radius $b=r/2$. For a thin hollow drum, $I=Ma^2$. A rightward horizontal <impulse> $J$ applied at height $h$ above the centre gives $v=J/M$ and clockwise <angular velocity> $\omega=Jh/I$. Rolling on the axle requires $v=b\omega$, so
$$
\boxed{h=\frac I{Mb}=\frac{a^2}{b}=\frac{R^2}{2r}.}
$$
The frictionless rails do not create the required rotation; the initial impulse must give exactly this ratio of linear to angular motion. For the usual case $r<R$, the required height is greater than the drum radius. It cannot be reached by striking the bare cylindrical surface horizontally; an extension or equivalent applied couple would be needed. This geometric limitation should accompany the formal result.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

With clockwise rotation taken positive and $v_0=b\omega_0$, the total <kinetic energy> is
$$
\boxed{K_0=\frac M2v_0^2+\frac I2\omega_0^2=\frac M2(a^2+b^2)\omega_0^2.}
$$
<Angular momentum> needs an origin. Its spin about the centre is $I\omega_0$, in the clockwise direction. About a fixed point in the horizontal rail plane, spin and orbital contributions add to
$$
\boxed{L_{\rm rail}=I\omega_0+Mbv_0=M(a^2+b^2)\omega_0.}
$$
Here the drum centre is height $b$ above that plane. The signed clockwise convention fixes the displayed signs; the associated vector points perpendicular to the vertical plane of motion.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

For the <frictional conversion of spin to rolling>, consider the horizontal slipping stage on the rough surface, whose contact radius is now $a$, and neglect extra vertical impact or height-change energies at the transition. A friction impulse $J_f$ changes $Mv$ by $J_f$ and clockwise spin $I\omega$ by $-aJ_f$. Thus $I\omega+Mav$ is conserved. At the end $v_f=a\omega_f$, giving
$$
\boxed{v_f=\frac{v_0+a\omega_0}{2},\qquad \omega_f=\frac{\omega_0+v_0/a}{2}.}
$$
Because $I=Ma^2$, the final total <kinetic energy> is $K_f=Mv_f^2$. Subtraction yields
$$
\boxed{\Delta K=-\frac M4(v_0-a\omega_0)^2=-\frac{M(R-r)^2\omega_0^2}{16}.}
$$
The result is independent of the friction coefficient, provided sufficient contact time allows <rolling without slipping> to be regained. Initially $v_0=b\omega_0<a\omega_0$, so the contact point slips backwards. Friction acts forwards and can increase translational <kinetic energy>, while decreasing rotational <kinetic energy> by more. A finite vertical drop or inelastic landing would require additional geometric information; the boxed expression describes the horizontal rolling-conversion loss.

== 9
{scope}

=== Solution

Let $\lambda=1\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ be the launch rate and $\mu=\lambda m$. Treat many small impacts as a continuous momentum flux, justified by $m\ll M$. Write $x(t)$ for truck displacement from its initial position and $U=\dot x$, with $t=0$ at the first impact. First neglect vertical motion, so every projectile has horizontal <velocity> $v$.

A fixed **launch rate** is different from a fixed impact rate on a receding truck. Projectiles reaching the truck at time $t$ were launched at retarded time $s=t-x(t)/v$, after an irrelevant constant initial-flight offset. The <moving-target interception rate> is therefore
$$
\frac{dN}{dt}=\lambda\left(1-\frac Uv\right).
$$
For an <elastic collision> with a much heavier truck, each ball transfers <momentum> approximately $2m(v-U)$. The elastic-ball truck keeps mass $M$, so
$$
M\dot U=\frac{2\mu}{v}(v-U)^2.
$$
Solving from rest and integrating once more gives
$$
\boxed{U(t)=v\left(1-\frac1{1+2\mu t/M}\right),\qquad x(t)=vt-\frac{Mv}{2\mu}\ln\left(1+\frac{2\mu t}{M}\right).}
$$
The rebound reverses the ball's motion relative to the truck; its laboratory velocity $2U-v$ can still be forwards once $U>v/2$. The truck approaches $v$ asymptotically, while its decreasing impact rate prevents exponential approach in laboratory time. The approximation omits the velocity jumps of individual finite-mass impacts.

For sticking snowballs, let $A(t)$ be the instantaneous truck-plus-snow mass, with $A(0)=M$. The <variable-mass system> obeys
$$
\dot A=\mu(1-U/v),\qquad \frac{d(AU)}{dt}=v\dot A.
$$
Integrating the second equation gives $AU=v(A-M)$. Therefore $A\dot A=\mu M$, and with $z=\sqrt{1+2\mu t/M}$,
$$
\boxed{A=Mz,\quad U=v\left(1-\frac1z\right),\quad x=vt-\frac{Mv}{\mu}(z-1)=\frac{Mv}{2\mu}(z-1)^2.}
$$
Retaining the accumulated mass is necessary even though each individual snowball is small.

For the range calculation, take the intended idealization of horizontal launch from height $h$, no air resistance, and interception possible until the projectile reaches the ground. Its flight time is $\tau=\sqrt{2h/g}$ and horizontal range is $D=v\tau$. If the initial truck distance from the boy is $X_0<D$, substitute $x=D-X_0$ in the last expression. The speed at the range limit is
$$
\boxed{U_{\rm out}=v\frac{a}{1+a},\qquad a=\sqrt{\frac{2\mu(D-X_0)}{Mv}}.}
$$
For an initially nearby truck, $X_0=0$ in this point-target model and $a=\sqrt{2\mu\tau/M}$. A different launch angle or finite interception height changes the available range and must be specified separately.

If instead one idealizes one **impact** each second, regardless of the truck's changing position, the answers are different: elastic balls give $U=v(1-e^{-2\mu t/M})$ and $x=v[t-M(1-e^{-2\mu t/M})/(2\mu)]$; sticking balls give $U=v\mu t/(M+\mu t)$ and $x=vt-(Mv/\mu)\ln(1+\mu t/M)$. Those expressions assume controlled arrival times, whereas the boxed results respect the stated fixed launch rate.

== 10
{scope}

=== Solution

Use the <test-particle approximation>, neglecting the planet's recoil. The conserved specific <angular momentum> is $j=bv$ and the specific <energy> at infinity is $v^2/2$. At closest approach the radial <velocity> vanishes, so the <effective potential> gives
$$
\frac{v^2}{2}=\frac{b^2v^2}{2r_0^2}-\frac{GM}{r_0}.
$$
The positive root and the tangential speed are
$$
\boxed{r_0=\frac{\sqrt{G^2M^2+b^2v^4}-GM}{v^2}=\frac{b^2v^2}{GM+\sqrt{G^2M^2+b^2v^4}},\quad v_{\max}=\frac{bv}{r_0}=\sqrt{v^2+\frac{2GM}{r_0}}.}
$$
The second form for $r_0$ avoids subtracting two nearly equal numbers for a small impact parameter. These formulas describe a nonradial orbit; $b=0$ gives a collision rather than a finite nonzero <pericentre> in the point-planet model.

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $V=v_{\max}$ and let $u_c=\sqrt{GM/r_0}$ be the <circular orbit> speed. Take the captured half to move in the original tangential direction, the minimum-energy choice. Conservation of <momentum> during the short explosion gives the other half speed $2V-u_c$. The gravitational <potential energy> is unchanged at the instant of separation, so the necessary generated <energy> is the increase in <kinetic energy>:
$$
\boxed{E_{\rm explosion}=\frac m4[u_c^2+(2V-u_c)^2]-\frac m2V^2=\frac m2(V-u_c)^2.}
$$
The captured half has zero radial velocity and exactly the required tangential speed, so it can enter the stable Newtonian <circular orbit> at this radius. Internal self-binding and any mass lost to radiation are neglected.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

For the prograde captured orbit, the departing half has instantaneous <velocity> tangent to the original orbit, in the original direction, with speed
$$
\boxed{u_{\rm depart}=2\sqrt{v^2+\frac{2GM}{r_0}}-\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_0}}.}
$$
It exceeds the local <escape speed>: since $V\geq\sqrt2u_c$, $u_{\rm depart}\geq(2\sqrt2-1)u_c>\sqrt2u_c$. This is its speed immediately after the explosion, not its later speed at infinity.

If a counterrotating circular captured orbit is allowed, its signed velocity is $-u_c$, the outgoing speed is instead $2V+u_c$, and the required generated energy is $m(V+u_c)^2/2$. The usual answer takes the prograde orbit; specifying that choice removes the directional ambiguity.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use emission, reflection and return-to-source events $A,B,C$. In the source-and-mirror rest frame their $(ct,x)$ coordinates are $A=(0,0)$, $B=(d,d)$ and $C=(2d,0)$. The laser and mirror have vertical <worldlines>; the two light rays have slopes $\pm1$ on a diagram with equal spatial and $ct$ scales.

For a frame moving with <velocity> $+v$ along the outgoing beam, let $\beta=v/c$ and $\gamma=(1-\beta^2)^{-1/2}$. A <Lorentz transformation> gives
$$
B'=(\gamma d(1-\beta),\gamma d(1-\beta)),\qquad C'=(2\gamma d,-2\gamma\beta d).
$$
Both laser and mirror now move towards negative $x'$, with worldlines $x'=-vt'$ and $x'=d/\gamma-vt'$. Light still has slopes $\pm1$. The moving observer's own worldline is $x'=0$; it should not be confused with the moving source worldline.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

In the original rest frame each leg takes $d/c$, so the round trip takes $2d/c$. For the moving frame, use the coordinate differences between the same three events. The <Lorentz transformation> gives
$$
\boxed{\Delta t'_{AB}=\frac{\gamma d}{c}(1-\beta),\quad\Delta t'_{BC}=\frac{\gamma d}{c}(1+\beta),\quad\Delta t'_{AC}=\frac{2\gamma d}{c}.}
$$
The outbound leg is shorter because the mirror approaches the outgoing ray; the inbound leg is longer because the source retreats from the returning ray. Their sum is larger than the original round-trip <proper time> by the <time dilation> factor $\gamma$.

These are coordinate times for return to the original source, not a claim that the pulse returns to the second observer's body at event $C$. If that observer starts at $A$, it intercepts the returning light at a different event, earlier than $C$, at its own time $2\gamma d(1-\beta)/c$. Distinguishing those events is necessary when interpreting “seen” or “return”.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Replace the frame speed by $-2v$, requiring $|2v|<c$. With $\Gamma=(1-4\beta^2)^{-1/2}$,
$$
\boxed{\Delta t''_{AB}=\frac{\Gamma d}{c}(1+2\beta),\quad\Delta t''_{BC}=\frac{\Gamma d}{c}(1-2\beta),\quad\Delta t''_{AC}=\frac{2\Gamma d}{c}.}
$$
The outbound leg is now the longer one and the inbound leg the shorter one. Doubling the speed also changes the <Lorentz factor>; it is not enough to swap the two original leg times. A proposed doubled speed at or above $c$ does not define an inertial observer frame.
