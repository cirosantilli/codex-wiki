= Solution
{parent=1}

Interpret $h$ as the vertical drop of the cylinder's <centre of mass>, and initially take the suspended rod to be vertical. The <moment of inertia of a uniform solid cylinder> is $I=mr^2/2$. Before impact, <rolling without slipping> gives $\omega=u/r$, so <conservation of mechanical energy> gives
$$
mgh=\frac12mu^2+\frac12I\frac{u^2}{r^2}=\frac34mu^2,
\qquad u=\sqrt{\frac{4gh}{3}}.
$$
Use the usual instantaneous smooth <elastic collision> model: the horizontal impact <impulse> passes through the cylinder's <centre of mass>, and the floor supplies negligible tangential <impulse> during impact. The cylinder's <angular velocity> is then unchanged during impact; its <rotational kinetic energy> does not transfer to the bob. The rod supplies no horizontal <impulse> when vertical. If $v$ and $V$ are the signed cylinder and bob <velocities> immediately afterwards, <conservation of momentum> and the unit <coefficient of restitution> give
$$
mu=mv+2mV,\qquad V-v=u,
\qquad v=-\frac u3,\quad V=\frac{2u}{3}.
$$
During the subsequent <simple pendulum> swing, <conservation of mechanical energy> gives
$$
\frac12(2m)V^2=2mgh(1-\cos\theta_{\max}),
\qquad
\boxed{\cos\theta_{\max}=\frac{19}{27},\quad
\theta_{\max}=\cos^{-1}\frac{19}{27}\simeq45.28^\circ.}
$$
The cylinder generally slips immediately after this impact, which is consistent with imposing <rolling without slipping> only on the descent.

**Impact convention matters.** The <floor-impulse dependence of a rolling-cylinder collision> gives a different answer if a different constraint is imposed. If instead the floor enforces <rolling without slipping> throughout the impulsive <collision>, it supplies a nonzero horizontal <impulse>. The cylinder's effective translational <mass> is then $m+I/r^2=3m/2$. Writing $J$ for the <impulse> delivered to the bob gives $(3m/2)(v-u)=-J$, $2mV=J$ and $V-v=u$. Hence $V=6u/7$ and $\cos\theta_{\max}=25/49$, giving $59.32^\circ$. The original wording specifies the descent constraint but does not specify an impulsive floor-friction law; the first boxed value uses the smooth-impact convention.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Choose the heavier particle's outgoing <velocity> above the initial direction. Let its <speed> be $V$, and let the lighter particle have components $(v\cos\theta,-v\sin\theta)$. Thus $\theta$ and $\phi$ are nonnegative deflections on opposite sides. <Conservation of momentum> gives
$$
mu=mv\cos\theta+MV\cos\phi,
\qquad mv\sin\theta=MV\sin\phi.
$$
The <elastic collision> conserves <kinetic energy>:
$$
mu^2=mv^2+MV^2.
$$
Taking the squared magnitude of the vector <momentum> equation yields
$$
m^2v^2=m^2u^2+M^2V^2-2mMuV\cos\phi.
$$
Eliminate $v^2$ with the <kinetic energy> equation. For a nontrivial <collision>, $V\ne0$, so
$$
(M+m)V=2mu\cos\phi.
$$
Substituting back into the two <momentum> components gives
$$
v\cos\theta=\frac{u(m-M\cos2\phi)}{m+M},
\qquad
v\sin\theta=\frac{uM\sin2\phi}{m+M}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\tan\theta=\frac{\sin2\phi}{m/M-\cos2\phi}.}
$$
The ratio alone does not determine the quadrant: for $M>m$, the lighter particle can move backwards. The two component equations select the correct $0\leq\theta\leq\pi$. At $m/M=\cos2\phi$, the outgoing direction is perpendicular to the initial direction; the displayed <tangent> has a pole there.

= Solution
{parent=3}

Let $t=0$ be the start of deposition and $I_0=MR^2/2$ be the original <moment of inertia of a uniform disc>. Assume that all deposited sand sticks and remains at radius $r$, with negligible thickness and no losses. The sand's incoming <angular momentum> about the vertical axis is zero. Neither the bearing nor <gravity> supplies an axial <torque>, so <conservation of angular momentum> gives
$$
I(t)=I_0+\alpha r^2t,\qquad
I(t)\omega(t)=I_0\omega_0.
$$
Consequently the <angular velocity> and <angular acceleration> are
$$
\boxed{\omega(t)=\frac{I_0\omega_0}{I_0+\alpha r^2t}
=\frac{MR^2\omega_0}{MR^2+2\alpha r^2t},}
\qquad
\boxed{\dot\omega(t)=-\frac{\alpha r^2I_0\omega_0}{(I_0+\alpha r^2t)^2}.}
$$
The <rotational kinetic energy> is
$$
\boxed{K(t)=\frac12I(t)\omega(t)^2
=\frac{I_0^2\omega_0^2}{2(I_0+\alpha r^2t)}
=K(0)\frac{I_0}{I_0+\alpha r^2t}.}
$$
Thus, for $\alpha r^2>0$,
$$
\boxed{t_{1/2}=\frac{I_0}{\alpha r^2}=\frac{MR^2}{2\alpha r^2}.}
$$
This is <mass-loaded rotation>: the sticking process dissipates <mechanical energy> even though <angular momentum> is conserved. Indeed $\dot K=-\alpha r^2\omega^2/2$. If $r=0$ or $\alpha=0$, no additional axial <moment of inertia> is deposited and the nonzero initial <rotational kinetic energy> never halves.

= Solution
{parent=4}

In the inertial <centre of mass> frame, the <Lagrangian mechanics> description starts from <kinetic energy> minus <potential energy>:
$$
L=\frac12m_1|\dot{\mathbf r}_1|^2
+\frac12m_2|\dot{\mathbf r}_2|^2-V(|\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2|).
$$
Define the <two-particle relative coordinate> $\mathbf r=\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2$. The <centre of mass> condition gives
$$
m_1\mathbf r_1+m_2\mathbf r_2=0,
\qquad
\mathbf r_1=\frac{m_2}{m_1+m_2}\mathbf r,
\quad
\mathbf r_2=-\frac{m_1}{m_1+m_2}\mathbf r.
$$
Substitution therefore gives
$$
\boxed{L=\frac12\mu|\dot{\mathbf r}|^2-V(r),
\qquad\mu=\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2},\quad r=|\mathbf r|.}
$$
The <Euler-Lagrange equation> is simply $\mu\ddot{\mathbf r}=-\nabla V(r)$. Thus <center-of-mass reduction> turns a <two-body problem> into a one-particle <central force> problem with <reduced mass> $\mu$, reducing six position components to three relative components. In an arbitrary <inertial frame> one adds the independent <centre of mass> term $\tfrac12(m_1+m_2)|\dot{\mathbf R}|^2$; that free motion does not affect the relative solution. Since $V$ depends only on separation, <conservation of angular momentum> further confines a nonradial relative trajectory to a plane.

Interpret the percentage relative to the classical <kinetic energy>, so that $K_{\rm rel}=1.02K_{\rm cl}$. Set $\beta=v/c$ and use the <Lorentz factor> $\gamma=(1-\beta^2)^{-1/2}$. The <relativistic kinetic energy> and classical <kinetic energy> give
$$
(\gamma-1)mc^2=1.02\left(\frac12mv^2\right),
\qquad \gamma-1=0.51\beta^2.
$$
For the positive nonzero <speed>, insert $\beta^2=1-\gamma^{-2}$ and cancel $\gamma-1$:
$$
\frac{\gamma^2}{\gamma+1}=0.51,
\qquad \gamma^2-0.51\gamma-0.51=0,
\qquad \gamma=\frac{0.51+\sqrt{2.3001}}2\simeq1.01330403.
$$
It follows that
$$
\boxed{v=c\sqrt{1-\gamma^{-2}}\simeq0.161513c
\simeq4.8420\times10^7\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},}
\qquad
\boxed{E=\gamma mc^2\simeq950.479\,\mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
Here $mc^2=938\,\mathrm{MeV}$ is the <proton>'s <rest energy>; the resulting <relativistic kinetic energy> is $12.479\,\mathrm{MeV}$. The zero-<speed> equality before cancellation has no meaningful percentage excess because both <kinetic energies> then vanish.

= Solution
{parent=6}

Take positive <velocity> towards $B$, and interpret $100\,\mathrm{MHz}$ as the emitted <frequency> in the probe's rest frame. The <relativistic velocity-addition formula> gives the probe's station-frame <velocity>:
$$
u_P=\frac{0.5c+0.5c}{1+(0.5)(0.5)}=0.8c.
$$
For reception at the station, photons propagate backwards while the probe recedes. The longitudinal <relativistic Doppler effect> gives
$$
\boxed{f_{\rm station}=100\sqrt{\frac{1-0.8}{1+0.8}}\,\mathrm{MHz}
=\frac{100}{3}\,\mathrm{MHz}\simeq33.33\,\mathrm{MHz}.}
$$
Before the probe overtakes $B$, their separation decreases. The probe's <velocity> in $B$'s <inertial frame> is
$$
u_{P|B}=\frac{0.8c-0.4c}{1-(0.8)(0.4)}=\frac{10}{17}c.
$$
The approaching-source <relativistic Doppler effect> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{f_B=100\sqrt{\frac{1+10/17}{1-10/17}}\,\mathrm{MHz}
=100\sqrt{\frac{27}{7}}\,\mathrm{MHz}\simeq196.40\,\mathrm{MHz}.}
$$
An independent frame calculation gives the same result: forward photons have station-frame <frequency> $300\,\mathrm{MHz}$, and a receiver moving at $0.4c$ measures $300\sqrt{0.6/1.4}\,\mathrm{MHz}$. If reception occurs after the probe passes $B$, backwards photons instead give $f_B=100\sqrt{7/27}\,\mathrm{MHz}\simeq50.92\,\mathrm{MHz}$. The direction of propagation and the reception geometry are essential to choosing the <Doppler factor>.

= Solution

**Powered ascent.** Use upward signed <velocity>, constant <gravitational acceleration> $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, constant relative <rocket exhaust speed> $u=2500\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, vertical flight from rest, no <drag force>, and no <impulse> at stage separation. The constant-$g$ approximation is an explicit modelling assumption; a varying <Newtonian gravitational field> would require integrating a different ascent equation. Put $m_0=540000\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $q=2500\,\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$. During ascent $m(t)=m_0-qt$, and <momentum> balance gives the <rocket equation> with <gravity>:
$$
m\dot v=uq-mg,
\qquad v(t)=u\ln\frac{m_0}{m_0-qt}-gt.
$$
The launch <rocket thrust> $uq=6.25\times10^6\,\mathrm N$ exceeds $m_0g$, so immediate lift-off is consistent. At ascent burnout the entire attached vehicle still has <mass> $m_b=140000\,\mathrm{kg}$, not $40000\,\mathrm{kg}$; the latter is the separated first stage. The burn duration and endpoint <speed> are
$$
t_b=\frac{400000}{2500}=160\,\mathrm s,
\qquad
\boxed{v_b=u\ln\frac{m_0}{m_b}-gt_b
=1805.22\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$

**Coasting height.** Integrating the ascent <velocity> from zero height gives
$$
z(t)=u\left[t-\frac{m_0-qt}{q}\ln\frac{m_0}{m_0-qt}\right]-\frac12gt^2.
$$
For example this follows by substituting $m=m_0-qt$ in $\int_0^t\ln[m_0/(m_0-qs)]\,ds$. At separation,
$$
z_b=u\left[t_b-\frac{m_b}{q}\ln\frac{m_0}{m_b}\right]-\frac12gt_b^2
=85442.26\,\mathrm m.
$$
The separated stage then undergoes <free fall in a constant gravitational field> with initial upward <speed> $v_b$. <Conservation of mechanical energy> during this coast gives
$$
\boxed{H=z_b+\frac{v_b^2}{2g}=251538.47\,\mathrm m\simeq251.54\,\mathrm{km}.}
$$
Its smaller <mass> after separation does not change that coasting height.

**Reserve burn.** On descending to $z_i=100000\,\mathrm m$, <conservation of mechanical energy> gives downward <speed>
$$
w_i=\sqrt{2g(H-z_i)}=1724.29255\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.
$$
Reset time to $s=0$ at reserve ignition. Now $m_i=40000\,\mathrm{kg}$, $m_f=14000\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $q_r=26000/92\,\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$. Downward ejection supplies upward <rocket thrust>. The upward signed <velocity> is
$$
v_r(s)=-w_i+u\ln\frac{m_i}{m_i-q_rs}-gs.
$$
Thus the formal free-flight burnout value is
$$
\boxed{v_r(92)=-w_i+u\ln\frac{40000}{14000}-92g
=-2.25724\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},}
$$
corresponding to a **formal downward speed of $2.26\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$**. Keeping $g=9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ throughout instead gives $3.82\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ downward; premature rounding is especially damaging because three large <velocity> terms almost cancel.

**The free-flight endpoint is below ground.** Integrating the same reserve-burn <velocity> gives
$$
z_r(s)=z_i-w_is+u\left[s-\frac{m_i-q_rs}{q_r}\ln\frac{m_i}{m_i-q_rs}\right]-\frac12gs^2.
$$
At the stated endpoint this gives $z_r(92)=-167.27\,\mathrm m$ for $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. The surface is crossed at approximately $s=89.15\,\mathrm s$, before reserve burnout. The stage has already hit the surface, so a physical post-impact burnout <speed> cannot be inferred without a ground-contact model. The boxed <velocity> is the intended ideal continuation, not an achievable free-flight landing result under these exact assumptions. In particular, the given numbers do not describe an exact soft landing. The <constant-rate retro-rocket landing> must satisfy both its <velocity> and height conditions.

= Solution
{parent=8}

**Axis theorems.** The <parallel axis theorem> states
$$
I_O=I_G+md^2,
$$
where $I_G$ is the <moment of inertia> about an axis through the <centre of mass>, $I_O$ is about a parallel axis, $m$ is the body's total <mass>, and $d$ is the perpendicular separation of the axes. Expanding $\int[(x-d)^2+y^2]dm$ proves the result because $\int x\,dm=0$ at the <centre of mass>. The <perpendicular axis theorem> applies to a <planar lamina> in the $xy$ plane:
$$
I_z=I_x+I_y.
$$
All three axes meet at the same point, $x,y$ lie in the lamina and $z$ is normal to it; the identity follows from $x^2+y^2$ being the squared distance to the $z$ axis. It is not a theorem for an arbitrary three-dimensional body.

**Cylinder integrals.** Put the cylinder's midpoint at $z=0$ and its symmetry axis along $z$. Its uniform <mass density> is $\rho=m/(\pi r^2l)$. The <transverse moment of inertia of a uniform solid cylinder> about the $x$ axis is
$$
I_{\perp,G}=\rho\int_{-l/2}^{l/2}dz\int_0^r s\,ds\int_0^{2\pi}d\varphi\,(z^2+s^2\sin^2\varphi)
=\frac{ml^2}{12}+\frac{mr^2}{4}.
$$
Equivalently each thin circular slice has diametral <moment of inertia> $r^2dm/4$, by the <perpendicular axis theorem> and symmetry, and shifting it by $z$ adds $z^2dm$. The <parallel axis theorem> then gives the transverse end-axis <moment of inertia>:
$$
\boxed{I_{\perp,G}=\frac{m}{12}(l^2+3r^2),\qquad
I_{\perp,\rm end}=\frac{m}{12}(4l^2+3r^2).}
$$
The axial <moment of inertia of a uniform solid cylinder> is $I_\parallel=mr^2/2$. Equating it to $I_{\perp,G}$ gives
$$
\boxed{l^2=3r^2,\qquad l=\sqrt3\,r.}
$$
Here the source's principal axis means the cylinder's symmetry axis; its transverse centre axes are also <principal axes>. This equality describes a short cylinder rather than the thin-rod limit.

**Hammer dynamics and forces.** Treat the compact head as a <point mass> and neglect its intrinsic <moment of inertia>. Let $\theta$ be the displacement from downward vertical, and let $\mathbf e_r$ point along the rod from pivot to head, with $\mathbf e_\theta$ in the direction of increasing $\theta$. Define
$$
m_T=M+m,\qquad A=Ml+\frac{ml}{2},\qquad d=\frac{A}{m_T},\qquad
I=Ml^2+m\left(\frac{l^2}{3}+\frac{r^2}{4}\right).
$$
The two external weights $Mg$ and $mg$ act at distances $l$ and $l/2$ from the pivot. The frictionless pivot supplies a reaction $\mathbf R$ but no <torque> about its axis. The total restoring <torque> and <rotational equation of motion> are
$$
\tau=-gA\sin\theta,
\qquad \boxed{I\ddot\theta+gA\sin\theta=0.}
$$
For completeness, <Newton's second law> for the <centre of mass> gives both components of the pivot <force>:
$$
\boxed{R_r=-m_Td\dot\theta^2-m_Tg\cos\theta,\qquad
R_\theta=m_Td\ddot\theta+m_Tg\sin\theta.}
$$
The negative radial component points towards the pivot. These follow from the <centre of mass> <acceleration> $-d\dot\theta^2\mathbf e_r+d\ddot\theta\mathbf e_\theta$ and the total weight $m_Tg(\cos\theta\mathbf e_r-\sin\theta\mathbf e_\theta)$. They specify the external <forces> even when the <oscillation> is not small.

With the <small-angle approximation>, $\sin\theta\simeq\theta$, this is <simple harmonic motion>. The <physical pendulum> has small-amplitude <oscillation period>
$$
\boxed{T_0=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{gA}}
=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{Ml^2+m(l^2/3+r^2/4)}{gl(M+m/2)}}.}
$$
For release from a finite amplitude $0<\theta_0<\pi$, <conservation of mechanical energy> instead gives $\tfrac12I\dot\theta^2=gA(\cos\theta-\cos\theta_0)$. Integrating a quarter-cycle gives the exact <oscillation period>
$$
T(\theta_0)=4\sqrt{\frac{I}{gA}}K\!\left(\sin\frac{\theta_0}{2}\right),
\qquad K(k)=\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\psi}{\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2\psi}},
$$
where $K$ is the complete <elliptic integral of the first kind>; $K(0)=\pi/2$ recovers $T_0$.

For $m=M/10$ and $r=l/20$, compare with the <simple pendulum> value $T_s=2\pi\sqrt{l/g}$:
$$
\boxed{\frac{T_0}{T_s}
=\sqrt{\frac{1+\frac1{10}(\frac13+\frac1{1600})}{1+\frac1{20}}}
=\sqrt{\frac{49603}{50400}}\simeq0.992062.}
$$
For small <oscillations>, the hammer's <oscillation period> is about $0.794\%$ shorter. The extra rod weight adds restoring <torque> proportionately faster than it adds pivot <moment of inertia>.

= Solution

Assume the station's <mass> remains fixed through an instantaneous kick, with subsequent motion governed only by the Earth's <Newtonian gravitational field>. Put $\kappa=GM_E$, and work per unit station <mass>. The initial <circular orbit> has <specific angular momentum> $j$ and <specific orbital energy> $\varepsilon_0$ given by
$$
v_0^2=\frac{\kappa}{r_o},\qquad
j^2=\kappa r_o,\qquad
\varepsilon_0=-\frac{\kappa}{2r_o}.
$$
Let $\delta$ denote the fractional increase of <kinetic energy>. The position and <potential energy> are unchanged during the kick, so
$$
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_0+\delta\frac{\kappa}{2r_o}
=-\frac{\kappa(1-\delta)}{2r_o}.
$$
Unchanged <angular momentum> at unchanged position fixes the tangential <velocity>; the added <kinetic energy> must be radial. Thus this is a radial kick, not a purely tangential speed increase. The new <orbital eccentricity> follows from
$$
e^2=1+\frac{2\varepsilon j^2}{\kappa^2}=\delta.
$$
At either radial <turning point>, the <Kepler radial energy equation> gives $\varepsilon=j^2/(2r^2)-\kappa/r$. Its two roots, equivalently the <periapsis> and <apoapsis> of the <elliptic orbit>, are
$$
\boxed{r_p=\frac{r_o}{1+\sqrt\delta},\qquad
r_a=\frac{r_o}{1-\sqrt\delta}.}
$$
For $\delta=0.01$, $e=0.1$ and
$$
\boxed{r_p=\frac{10}{11}r_o,\qquad r_a=\frac{10}{9}r_o.}
$$
An inward or outward kick gives the same pair of radial <turning points>, although it places the station at a different phase of the <orbit>. These two finite positive radii require $0\leq\delta<1$.

Retain the unchanged-<angular momentum> assumption of the preceding part. The initial <orbit> has radius $r_o=6400+500=6900\,\mathrm{km}$, and the desired <pericentre distance> is $r_p=6400+100=6500\,\mathrm{km}$. Using $r_p=r_o/(1+e)$ gives

e=\frac{6900}{6500}-1=\frac4{65}.

The required fractional increase in initial <kinetic energy> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{\Delta K}{K_0}=e^2=\frac{16}{4225}
\simeq0.00378698=0.378698\%.}
$$
Here $\Delta E=\Delta K>0$ because the instantaneous kick does not change <potential energy>. Since the initial total <orbital energy> is $E_0=-K_0$, an alternative convention for the requested fractional <energy> change gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{\Delta E}{|E_0|}=+0.00378698,\qquad
\frac{\Delta E}{E_0}=-0.00378698.}
$$
The total <orbital energy> becomes less negative. Explicitly specifying its denominator avoids interpreting a positive <energy> input as a decrease of <energy>.

= Solution

Let $D$ be the separation of the <Earth> and <Moon>, and let $x$ be the spacecraft's distance from the Earth's centre towards the Moon. On $0<x<D$, the two <gravitational forces> oppose each other. Their equality, by <Newton's law of universal gravitation>, is
$$
\frac{GM_E}{x^2}=\frac{GM_M}{(D-x)^2}.
$$
Taking the positive <square root> gives
$$
\boxed{x=\frac{D\sqrt{M_E}}{\sqrt{M_E}+\sqrt{M_M}}
=\frac{D}{1+\sqrt{M_M/M_E}}.}
$$
The <centre of mass> of the two bodies is at $x_G=DM_M/(M_E+M_M)$ from the Earth's centre. Thus the <gravitational field null between two point masses> lies a distance
$$
\boxed{x-x_G=D\left[\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{M_M/M_E}}
-\frac{M_M}{M_E+M_M}\right]}
$$
from their <centre of mass>, on the Moon's side. With $M_E/M_M=81$, $x=9D/10$ and $x_G=D/82$, so
$$
\boxed{x=3.456\times10^8\,\mathrm m,\qquad
x-x_G=\frac{182}{205}D\simeq3.40917\times10^8\,\mathrm m.}
$$
The point is $3.84\times10^7\,\mathrm m$ from the Moon. It is an instantaneous zero of the physical <gravitational field>, not a co-rotating <Lagrange point>: a spacecraft remaining fixed relative to the Earth-Moon line would need a nonzero <centripetal acceleration>. With zero net <force> it cannot follow that circular co-rotation.

= Solution
{parent=10}

**Invariant and collision.** For a particle of <rest mass> $m$, <special relativity> gives <relativistic energy> and <momentum>
$$
E=\gamma mc^2,\qquad \mathbf p=\gamma m\mathbf v,
\qquad \gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}.
$$
Therefore the <relativistic energy-momentum relation> is
$$
\boxed{E^2-p^2c^2=\gamma^2m^2c^4(1-v^2/c^2)=m^2c^4.}
$$
It is the squared <Minkowski norm> of the <four-momentum>, multiplied by $c^2$, and hence is preserved by every <Lorentz transformation>. Explicitly a boost with <speed> $U$ along $x$ gives $E'=\Gamma(E-Up_x)$, $p'_x=\Gamma(p_x-UE/c^2)$, with $p'_y=p_y$, $p'_z=p_z$. Expanding $E'^2-c^2p_x'^2$ gives $E^2-c^2p_x^2$ because $\Gamma^2(1-U^2/c^2)=1$. Spatial rotations preserve $p^2$, completing the invariance argument for all <inertial frames>.

For the incoming moving particle, $v=3c/5$ and $\gamma=5/4$. The total laboratory <energy> and <momentum> before the <perfectly inelastic collision> are
$$
E_i=\left(\frac54+2\right)m_0c^2=\frac{13}{4}m_0c^2,
\qquad p_i=\frac54m_0\frac{3c}{5}=\frac34m_0c.
$$
<Four-momentum conservation> identifies these with the final particle's <energy> and <momentum>. Its <rest mass> is therefore
$$
\boxed{m'=\frac{\sqrt{E_i^2-p_i^2c^2}}{c^2}
=\frac{m_0}{4}\sqrt{13^2-3^2}=\sqrt{10}\,m_0.}
$$
The result exceeds the sum $3m_0$ of the incoming <rest masses>; some initial <kinetic energy> has become internal <rest energy>. Since $pc^2/E=v$ for a massive particle,
$$
\boxed{v'=\frac{p_ic^2}{E_i}=\frac3{13}c,\qquad
\gamma'=\frac{13}{4\sqrt{10}}.}
$$

**Two-body decay.** Work first in the parent's rest frame, denoted by a star. <Four-momentum conservation> requires equal and opposite daughter <momenta>. Their equal <rest masses> give equal <energies>:
$$
E_* =\frac{m'c^2}{2}=\frac{\sqrt{10}}2m_0c^2,
\qquad
p_*c=\sqrt{E_*^2-\left(\frac{m_0c^2}{2}\right)^2}
=\frac32m_0c^2.
$$
Thus their rest-frame <velocities> are $v_{*\pm}=\pm3c/\sqrt{10}$. Applying the <relativistic velocity-addition formula> with boost $v'=3c/13$ gives the two laboratory <velocities>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{v_\pm}{c}
=\frac{3/13\pm3/\sqrt{10}}{1\pm9/(13\sqrt{10})}.}
$$
Their laboratory <energies> follow directly from the <Lorentz transformation> of <four-momentum>:
$$
\boxed{E_\pm=\gamma'(E_*\pm v'p_*)
=\left(\frac{13}{8}\pm\frac{9}{8\sqrt{10}}\right)m_0c^2.}
$$
The corresponding signed laboratory <momenta> are
$$
p_\pm=\left(\frac38\pm\frac{39}{8\sqrt{10}}\right)m_0c.
$$
Their sums recover $E_i$ and $p_i$, and each satisfies $E_\pm^2-p_\pm^2c^2=(m_0c^2/2)^2$. One daughter moves forwards and the other backwards in the laboratory; both are collinear with the original flight axis. Numerically, $v_+\simeq0.967615c$, $v_-\simeq-0.919138c$, $E_+\simeq1.980756m_0c^2$ and $E_-\simeq1.269244m_0c^2$.
