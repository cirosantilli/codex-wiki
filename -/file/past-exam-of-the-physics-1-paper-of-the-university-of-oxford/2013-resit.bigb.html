Solutions for the sitting of 9 September 2013, including all four alternatives in Section B. Lettered subsections also separate unlettered requests in the source.

== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

Take the upward <velocity> as positive during ascent. <Newton's second law> with <linear drag> gives
$$
\dot v+kv=-g,\qquad v(0)=v_0.
$$
Multiplication by the <integrating factor> $e^{kt}$ and integration give
$$
v(t)=\left(v_0+\frac gk\right)e^{-kt}-\frac gk.
$$
The maximum height occurs at the first zero of this <velocity>, hence
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm top}=\frac1k\log\left(1+\frac{kv_0}{g}\right).}
$$
Here $k$ has units of inverse <time>. The limit $k\to0$ gives $v_0/g$, and positive <drag> shortens the ascent relative to motion in a vacuum. The expression applies only up to the turning point; the <drag force> opposes the subsequent downward motion.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

Let $\dot M=500\,\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$ and $V=10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Incoming material has zero horizontal <momentum>, while incorporation requires horizontal <momentum> $V\,dM$. Thus the engine supplies <force> $F=V\dot M$, and its <power> is
$$
\boxed{F=5000\,\mathrm N,\qquad P=FV=\dot M V^2=50\,\mathrm{kW}.}
$$
No duration or loading distance is given, so a single total <work> cannot be assigned. Over an interval $t$ in seconds,
$$
\boxed{W=50{,}000t\,\mathrm J,\qquad \Delta K_{\rm horizontal}=\tfrac12\dot M V^2t=25{,}000t\,\mathrm J.}
$$
The remaining $25{,}000t\,\mathrm J$ becomes internal <energy> in the <inelastic collision> that brings the falling material to the wagon's horizontal <velocity>. Vertical falling <kinetic energy>, if any, is a separate contribution to dissipation; it does not alter this horizontal <work> balance. The source alternates between stone and coal; the calculation depends only on the given loading rate.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

Use the angle $\theta$ from the vertical as the <generalized coordinate>. For a uniform ladder, its <centre of mass> is $(l\sin\theta,l\cos\theta)$, so $v_G^2=l^2\dot\theta^2$. Its central <moment of inertia> is $I_G=m(2l)^2/12=ml^2/3$. With ideal contact constraints that do no <work>, the <Lagrangian>, <conjugate momentum>, and <Euler-Lagrange equation> are
$$
T=\tfrac12ml^2\dot\theta^2+\tfrac12I_G\dot\theta^2=\tfrac23ml^2\dot\theta^2,\quad U=mgl\cos\theta,
$$
$$
\boxed{L=\tfrac23ml^2\dot\theta^2-mgl\cos\theta,\qquad p_\theta=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\theta}=\tfrac43ml^2\dot\theta,}
$$
$$
\boxed{\ddot\theta=\frac{3g}{4l}\sin\theta.}
$$
Release from rest at $\theta_0$ also gives $\dot\theta^2=3g(\cos\theta_0-\cos\theta)/(2l)$ by <conservation of energy>. These results hold while both contacts persist. The ideal wall reaction is normal to the wall; an unspecified tangential wall friction would require an additional generalized <force> and would prevent determination from the supplied data alone.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

The <Lorentz factor> is $\gamma=E/(m_\mu c^2)=20000/106=188.6792$ and $\beta=\sqrt{1-\gamma^{-2}}$. Traversal of the height $H$ takes <proper time> $H/(\beta\gamma c)$. Using the <exponential decay> law with the given <muon lifetime>,
$$
\boxed{P=\exp\left[-\frac{H}{c\tau_0\sqrt{\gamma^2-1}}\right]\simeq0.8180.}
$$
The laboratory flight lasts about $83.39\,\mu\mathrm s$, while the elapsed <proper time> is about $0.4420\,\mu\mathrm s$. This is the probability of surviving decay to the surface, with atmospheric <energy> loss and detector efficiency neglected.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

Let $\mathbf a,\mathbf b$ be the outgoing <velocities>. In the nonrelativistic <elastic collision>, equal <masses> give
$$
\mathbf v=\mathbf a+\mathbf b,\qquad v^2=a^2+b^2
$$
by <conservation of momentum> and <conservation of energy>. Squaring the first relation and comparing with the second yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=0.}
$$
If both particles move, their paths are perpendicular. If either <velocity> vanishes, its direction is undefined and the other particle carries all the incident <momentum> and <kinetic energy>. This conclusion depends on the stated $v\ll c$ approximation.

== 6
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The external <work> equals the change in <relativistic kinetic energy>, $W=m_ec^2(\gamma_f-\gamma_i)$. With $m_ec^2=511\,\mathrm{keV}$,
$$
\boxed{W_a=511\left(\frac1{\sqrt{1-0.11^2}}-\frac1{\sqrt{1-0.10^2}}\right)\mathrm{keV}\simeq0.5456\,\mathrm{keV}\simeq8.742\times10^{-17}\,\mathrm J.}
$$
The <rest energy> cancels between endpoints. At these <speeds>, the nonrelativistic estimate $\tfrac12m_e c^2(0.11^2-0.10^2)=0.53655\,\mathrm{keV}$ is close but slightly smaller.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Applying the same <work-energy theorem> at the higher pair of <speeds> gives
$$
\boxed{W_b=511\left(\frac1{\sqrt{1-0.99^2}}-\frac1{\sqrt{1-0.98^2}}\right)\mathrm{keV}\simeq1055\,\mathrm{keV}\simeq1.689\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm J.}
$$
The identical increment $0.01c$ now needs about two thousand times as much <work>. The growth follows from the rapidly increasing <Lorentz factor> near the <speed of light>.

== 7
{scope}

=== Solution

Treat the carriage as translating along the circular track, neglecting wheel <rotational kinetic energy> and its finite size. At the top, gravity and the inward normal <force> supply the <centripetal acceleration>:
$$
mg+N=\frac{mv_{\rm top}^2}{r}.
$$
For unilateral contact, $N\ge0$, so the limiting <speed> is $v_{\rm top}^2=gr$. <Conservation of energy> from rest at height $h$ then gives
$$
mgh=2mgr+\tfrac12mgr,\qquad \boxed{h_{\min}=\tfrac52r.}
$$
This top contact condition is the most restrictive: at angle $\phi$ measured from the bottom, $N/(mg)=2h/r-2+3\cos\phi$, whose minimum occurs at the top. A mechanically captive carriage able to sustain an outward rail reaction has a different constraint; the result here uses the usual requirement of positive normal contact.

== 8
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose parallel axes along $z$, one through the <centre of mass> and one displaced transversely by $\mathbf a$. Write the perpendicular position as $\mathbf r_\perp=\mathbf r'_{\perp}+\mathbf a$. Direct expansion of the <moment of inertia> gives
$$
I=\int|\mathbf r'_\perp+\mathbf a|^2\,dm=I_G+2\mathbf a\cdot\int\mathbf r'_\perp\,dm+ma^2.
$$
The integral in the cross term is zero by the definition of the <centre of mass>. Therefore the <parallel axis theorem> is
$$
\boxed{I=I_G+ma^2.}
$$
The axes must be parallel and the reference axis must pass through the <centre of mass>; the formula is not generally valid between arbitrary parallel axes using either axis as the reference.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $\theta=0$ place the <centre of mass> vertically below a horizontal pivot axis. The restoring <torque> and <rotational kinetic energy> are $-mgh\sin\theta$ and $I\dot\theta^2/2$, so
$$
I\ddot\theta+mgh\sin\theta=0.
$$
For small angles, $\sin\theta=\theta+O(\theta^3)$, and the equation reduces to <simple harmonic motion>. Thus the <physical pendulum> has
$$
\boxed{\omega_0=\sqrt{\frac{mgh}{I}},\qquad T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{mgh}}.}
$$
This assumes negligible friction and a horizontal axis, with $h>0$. For a tilted axis the projected gravitational restoring <torque> changes; an axis through the <centre of mass> has no gravitational restoring <torque>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For the shorter uniform rod, the <parallel axis theorem> gives $I=m(a^2/12+x^2)$. Its equivalent <simple pendulum> length is $I/(mx)=x+a^2/(12x)$. The rod of length $2a$ pivoted at an end has equivalent length $2(2a)/3=4a/3$. Matching the <periods> therefore requires
$$
x+\frac{a^2}{12x}=\frac{4a}{3},\qquad 12x^2-16ax+a^2=0,
$$
$$
x=\frac{a}{6}(4\pm\sqrt{13}).
$$
A pivot on the shorter rod requires $0<x\le a/2$, leaving
$$
\boxed{x=\frac{4-\sqrt{13}}6a\simeq0.0657415a.}
$$
Either side of the rod's <centre of mass> gives this distance. The other algebraic root is beyond the rod and would require a support extension of negligible <mass>.

== 9
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For a frame moving at <velocity> $V\hat{\mathbf x}$, put $\Gamma=(1-V^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$. The <Lorentz transformation> of the <energy-momentum four-vector> is
$$
\boxed{E'=\Gamma(E-Vp_x),\quad p'_x=\Gamma(p_x-VE/c^2),\quad p'_y=p_y,\quad p'_z=p_z.}
$$
Expansion gives
$$
E'^2-c^2p_x'^2=\Gamma^2\left(1-\frac{V^2}{c^2}\right)(E^2-c^2p_x^2)=E^2-c^2p_x^2.
$$
The transverse components are unchanged, hence $\boxed{E'^2-c^2|\mathbf p'|^2=E^2-c^2|\mathbf p|^2}$. For a particle of <rest mass> $m$ this invariant is $m^2c^4$; the same algebra also applies to the total <four-momentum> of a system.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $p$ be the incident <momentum> and $q_1,q_2$ the outgoing magnitudes in the <laboratory frame>. Transverse <conservation of momentum> gives $(q_1-q_2)\sin\theta=0$. For the nondegenerate symmetric scattering, $q_1=q_2=q$, and the two outgoing <energies> are equal, $\epsilon=(E+E_0)/2$. Then
$$
p=2q\cos\theta,\quad p^2c^2=E^2-E_0^2,\quad q^2c^2=\frac{(E+E_0)^2}{4}-E_0^2.
$$
Combining these <mass shell> relations yields
$$
\boxed{\cos^2\theta=\frac{E+E_0}{E+3E_0},\qquad \tan^2\theta=\frac{2E_0}{E+E_0}.}
$$
Here $E$ includes the incident electron's <rest energy>. As $E\downarrow E_0$ the limiting angle is $45^\circ$, consistent with the nonrelativistic equal-mass <elastic collision>; at large $E/E_0$ the outgoing tracks become forward directed.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For $\theta=30^\circ$, the result in <9/b> gives $1/3=2E_0/(E+E_0)$, hence $E=5E_0$. Acceleration from rest supplies only the change in <kinetic energy>:
$$
e|\Delta V|=E-E_0=4E_0,\qquad \boxed{|\Delta V|=\frac{4m_ec^2}{e}\simeq2.044\,\mathrm{MV}.}
$$
The accelerating <electric potential difference> is a magnitude; its sign depends on which electrode is taken as the reference, since the electron has negative <electric charge>.

== 10
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

About a chosen origin $O$, a particle has <angular momentum> $\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p$. A body has $\mathbf L=\sum_i\mathbf r_i\times\mathbf p_i$, or the corresponding integral for a continuous <mass> distribution. For a fixed origin in an <inertial frame>,
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\mathbf L}{dt}=\boldsymbol\tau_{\rm external};\qquad\boldsymbol\tau_{\rm external}=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathbf L=\text{constant}.}
$$
Internal central pair <forces> cancel in the total <torque>. About a fixed origin, a <central force> conserves a particle's <angular momentum>; zero net <force> alone is insufficient if an external couple remains.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $\mu=GM$. At the turning point of a radial launch the <speed> vanishes, so <conservation of energy> gives
$$
\frac12v_0^2-\frac\mu R=-\frac\mu{r_0},\qquad
\boxed{r_0=\left(\frac1R-\frac{v_0^2}{2GM}\right)^{-1}=\frac{R}{1-v_0^2/v_{\rm esc}^2},\quad v_{\rm esc}=\sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}}.}
$$
This result is exact within the exterior Newtonian <gravitational potential>. The condition $r_0\gg R$ means $v_0$ is just below the <escape velocity>: $1-v_0^2/v_{\rm esc}^2\ll1$. Replacing gravity by a constant would be inappropriate in this regime.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For a tangential launch, conserved specific <angular momentum> is $j=Rv$. At the outer turning point the radial <velocity> vanishes but the tangential <speed> is $j/r_0$. Thus
$$
\frac12v^2-\frac\mu R=\frac{R^2v^2}{2r_0^2}-\frac\mu{r_0}.
$$
Using <10/a> eliminates the <gravitational potential> difference and gives
$$
\boxed{v=\frac{v_0}{\sqrt{1-R^2/r_0^2}}=v_0\left[1+\frac{R^2}{2r_0^2}+O\left(\frac{R^4}{r_0^4}\right)\right].}
$$
The expression printed in the source is the leading correction for $r_0\gg R$, rather than an exact identity. Equivalently, $v^2=2\mu r_0/[R(r_0+R)]$, so the launch <speed> exceeds the circular value and the surface is the <periapsis> of the nonintersecting ideal orbit.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

At a fixed launch radius the escape threshold is zero total <mechanical energy>. It depends on the initial <speed> alone:
$$
\boxed{v_{\rm esc}=\sqrt{2GM/R}\quad\text{independent of outward launch direction in the nonrotating model}.}
$$
A direction aimed into the planet can of course cause impact before escape. If the planet rotates, the ground-relative launch <velocity> must be added to $\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf R$ to obtain the inertial <velocity>. The condition becomes $|\mathbf u+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf R|^2\ge2GM/R$: prograde launch needs less ground-relative <speed> than retrograde launch. For a spherical planet the threshold expressed as inertial <speed> at the same radius remains independent of direction.

== 11
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $H$ be the common drop height in the small-gap, small-ball approximation and put $u=\sqrt{2gH}$. Use upward positive <velocity>. Just before ground impact both balls have <velocity> $-u$. The massive fixed ground reverses the lower ball's <velocity> elastically, so immediately before the ball-ball encounter
$$
u_1=-u,\qquad u_2=+u.
$$
One-dimensional <conservation of momentum> and reversal of relative <velocity> in an <elastic collision> give
$$
\boxed{v_1=\frac{3m_2-m_1}{m_1+m_2}u,\qquad v_2=\frac{m_2-3m_1}{m_1+m_2}u.}
$$
The sequence is shown for the optimal ratio in <11/b>. At that ratio the lower ball stops and the upper ball departs upward, so no immediate third encounter needs to be included. Finite gaps introduce unequal contact times and gravitational corrections; no gap or ball radii are supplied to calculate those corrections.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $r=m_1/m_2>0$. The upper ball's fraction of the available <kinetic energy> after the separated <elastic collisions> is
$$
f(r)=\frac{\tfrac12m_1v_1^2}{\tfrac12(m_1+m_2)u^2}=\frac{r(3-r)^2}{(1+r)^3},\qquad
1-f(r)=\frac{(1-3r)^2}{(1+r)^3}\ge0.
$$
Thus the maximal possible fraction is unity, attained at
$$
\boxed{\frac{m_1}{m_2}=\frac13,\qquad f_{\max}=1,\qquad v_1=2u,\quad v_2=0.}
$$
The identity proves the global bound without selecting a spurious stationary point. At the optimum the upper ball receives all the initial translational <kinetic energy>. Its subsequent rise is $4H$ above the mutual-collision level. For arbitrary large mass ratios further ground encounters may occur; the unity bound is already attained in the stated two-impact sequence.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

As $m_1/m_2\to0$, <11/a> gives $v_1\to3u$. The upper ball then rises through height $v_1^2/(2g)$ above the near-ground mutual encounter:
$$
\boxed{H_{\rm rebound}\longrightarrow\frac{9u^2}{2g}=9H.}
$$
The rise above its original release level is therefore $8H$ in this idealization. Although its <speed> triples, the light ball's fraction of the total <energy> tends to zero, $f(r)\sim9r$. This distinguishes maximum rebound height from the maximum energy-transfer fraction in <11/b>. With finite radii, $9H$ is the leading approximation in drop height relative to the collision height.
