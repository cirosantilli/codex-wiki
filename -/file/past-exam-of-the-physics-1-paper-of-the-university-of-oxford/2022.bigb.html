= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

For motion in a <central potential>, <conservation of angular momentum> gives $L=mr^2\dot\theta$. In <polar coordinates>, the conserved <mechanical energy> is
$$
E=\frac m2\dot r^2+\frac m2r^2\dot\theta^2-G(r)
=\frac m2\dot r^2+\frac{L^2}{2mr^2}-G(r).
$$
Consequently, for $L\ne0$,
$$
\dot r=\pm\sqrt{\frac{2}{m}[E+G(r)]-\frac{L^2}{m^2r^2}},\qquad
\boxed{\frac{dr}{d\theta}=\frac{mr^2}{L}\dot r
=\pm\frac{r^2}{L}\sqrt{2m[E+G(r)]-\frac{L^2}{r^2}}.}
$$
The sign in $\dot r$ distinguishes inward and outward motion. Choosing the orientation so $L>0$, it is also the sign of $dr/d\theta$. The expression under the <square root> must be nonnegative. If $L=0$, the motion is radial and $\theta$ is constant, so $r$ cannot be parametrized by $\theta$ this way.

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

Take $\beta>0$. At a radial <turning point>, $\dot r=0$, and the <effective potential> gives
$$
E=\frac{L^2}{2mr^2}-\frac\beta r,\qquad
\boxed{Er_{\min}^2+\beta r_{\min}-\frac{L^2}{2m}=0.}
$$
For $L\ne0$, the smaller positive radial <turning point> is conveniently written without division by $E$:
$$
\boxed{r_{\min}=\frac{L^2}{m\beta+\sqrt{m^2\beta^2+2mEL^2}}.}
$$
This includes $E=0$, where $r_{\min}=L^2/(2m\beta)$. For $E<0$ there is also an outer radial <turning point>; the two coincide for the <circular orbit> at $E=-m\beta^2/(2L^2)$. Smaller <energies> are inaccessible at that $L$. For $E>0$ there is just one positive radial <turning point>. If $L=0$, there is no <centrifugal barrier> and an inward trajectory can reach the singular centre; its limiting closest distance is zero.

**The requested omission of $\beta$ is not possible.** For example, at $E=0$ and fixed nonzero $L,m$, doubling $\beta$ halves $r_{\min}$. Thus $E,L,m$ alone cannot determine closest approach.

Use upward <generalized coordinates>. Let $h$ be the displacement of the lower <pulley> and $z$ that of the $4m$ <mass>. The two inextensible <mechanical strings> impose
$$
x+y-2h=0,\qquad z+h=0,
\qquad h=\frac{x+y}{2},\qquad z=-\frac{x+y}{2}.
$$
For ideal massless <pulleys> and <mechanical strings>, only the three <masses> contribute <kinetic energy>. Hence
$$
\begin{aligned}
K&=\frac12(4m)\left(\frac{\dot x+\dot y}{2}\right)^2
+\frac12(5m)\dot x^2+\frac12(3m)\dot y^2\\
&=m(3\dot x^2+\dot x\dot y+2\dot y^2).
\end{aligned}
$$
Up to a constant, their <gravitational potential energy> is
$$
U=4mgz+5mgx+3mgy=mg(3x+y).
$$
The <Lagrangian> of this <compound Atwood machine> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L=K-U=m(3\dot x^2+\dot x\dot y+2\dot y^2)-mg(3x+y).}
$$

= Solution

A constant displacement proportional to $(1,-3)$ leaves the <velocities> unchanged and changes $3x+y$ by zero. It is therefore a continuous symmetry of the <Lagrangian>. The <generalized momentum> components are
$$
p_x=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot x}=m(6\dot x+\dot y),\qquad
p_y=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot y}=m(\dot x+4\dot y).
$$
<Noether's theorem> gives the conserved quantity
$$
\boxed{P=p_x-3p_y=m(3\dot x-11\dot y)=\text{constant}.}
$$
A direct check using the <Euler-Lagrange equations> is
$$
6\ddot x+\ddot y=-3g,\qquad \ddot x+4\ddot y=-g
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 3\ddot x-11\ddot y=0.
$$
The normalization of a symmetry generator is arbitrary. One can take the transformation parameter to have <length> units and $(K_x,K_y)=(1,-3)$ dimensionless, giving $P$ the units of <momentum>. Alternatively, for the paper's convention with dimensionless parameter and <length>-valued $K_i$, use $(K_x,K_y)=(\ell,-3\ell)$; the conserved quantity is then $\ell P$. The conserved content is identical.

The <special relativity> expression for <momentum> is $p=\gamma mv$, with <Lorentz factor> $\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$. Equal <speeds> imply equal <Lorentz factors>, so the <pion> and <proton> <momenta> are in the ratio of their <rest masses>:

Let the object be at rest in $S'$, with <proper length> $L_0=x'_2-x'_1$. Measuring its length in $S$ requires two endpoint <events> simultaneous in $S$, so $\Delta t=0$. The <Lorentz transformation> then gives

L_0=\Delta x'=\gamma(\Delta x-v\Delta t)=\gamma L,

$$
This is <length contraction>. The simultaneous endpoint <events> in $S$ are not simultaneous in $S'$; that <relativity of simultaneity> is essential to the measurement.

For the two rockets, take the first rocket's Earth-frame <velocity> as $+c/2$ and the second's as $-c/2$. The <velocity-addition formula> gives the second rocket's <velocity> in the first rocket's <rest frame>:
$$
u=\frac{-c/2-c/2}{1-(-c/2)(c/2)/c^2}=-\frac45c.
$$
Its <Lorentz factor> is $\gamma_{\mathrm{rel}}=5/3$, and therefore its measured length is
$$
\boxed{L_{\mathrm{other}}=\frac35L_0.}
$$
Here “appear” is interpreted as a length measured using simultaneous endpoint positions in the observer's <inertial frame>. A photograph, involving unequal light travel times, is a different measurement.

Write the incident and scattered <photon> <energies> as $\varepsilon=hc/\lambda$ and $\varepsilon'=hc/\lambda'$. <Conservation of relativistic energy> gives the recoiling <electron> <energy> $E_e=mc^2+\varepsilon-\varepsilon'$. By <momentum conservation>, its <momentum> is the difference of the two <photon> <momenta>; hence
$$
p_e^2c^2=\varepsilon^2+\varepsilon'^2-2\varepsilon\varepsilon'\cos\theta.
$$
Insert these into the <relativistic energy-momentum relation> for the <electron>:
$$
(mc^2+\varepsilon-\varepsilon')^2
=m^2c^4+\varepsilon^2+\varepsilon'^2-2\varepsilon\varepsilon'\cos\theta.
$$
Expanding and cancelling terms gives
$$
mc^2(\varepsilon-\varepsilon')=\varepsilon\varepsilon'(1-\cos\theta),\qquad
\frac1{\varepsilon'}-\frac1\varepsilon=\frac{1-\cos\theta}{mc^2}.
$$
Since $1/\varepsilon=\lambda/(hc)$, the <Compton scattering> formula follows:
$$
\boxed{\lambda'=\lambda+\frac{h}{mc}(1-\cos\theta).}
$$
The scale $h/(mc)$ is the <electron> <Compton wavelength>. At $\theta=0$, $\lambda'=\lambda$: there is no <energy> or <momentum> transfer in this ideal forward limit, so the <electron> remains at rest. At $\theta=\pi$, the <wavelength> shift is maximal:
$$
\boxed{\Delta\lambda_{\max}=\frac{2h}{mc},\qquad
\varepsilon'_{\min}=\frac{\varepsilon}{1+2\varepsilon/(mc^2)}.}
$$
The <electron> gains its largest <kinetic energy>, $\varepsilon-\varepsilon'_{\min}$, and recoils forward while the <photon> reverses direction.

Define $f(r)>0$ as the magnitude of the inward <central force>, so $\mathbf F=-f(r)\widehat{\mathbf r}$ and $V'(r)=f(r)$. The conserved <angular momentum> is $J=mr^2\dot\theta$. Eliminating $\dot\theta$ from the <mechanical energy> yields

The added term in the <effective potential> is the <centrifugal barrier>. If instead $f$ denotes the signed radial <force> component, the convention becomes $V'=-f$.

A <circular orbit> has constant $r=r_0>0$. Both its initial radial <velocity> and radial <acceleration> must vanish, giving
$$
\boxed{r(0)=r_0,\quad\dot r(0)=0,\quad
V_{\mathrm{eff}}'(r_0)=V'(r_0)-\frac{J^2}{mr_0^3}=0.}
$$
Equivalently, the initial tangential <speed> and <angular velocity> obey
$$
v_{\theta,0}^2=\frac{r_0f(r_0)}m,\qquad
\dot\theta(0)=\frac{J}{mr_0^2},\qquad
J^2=mr_0^3f(r_0).
$$
The initial <polar angle> is arbitrary, and the <energy> is fixed at $E=V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r_0)$. Either orientation is possible. These conditions give an exact <circular orbit> even if it is unstable; stability requires an additional positive second <derivative> of the <effective potential>.

For the <power law> <central potential> $V=\beta r^k$, differentiate the <effective potential>:
$$
V_{\mathrm{eff}}'(r)=\beta k r^{k-1}-\frac{J^2}{mr^3}.
$$
The <circular orbit> condition is $m\beta k r_0^{k+2}=J^2$. Thus, when $J\ne0$, $\beta k>0$ and $k\ne-2$,
$$
\boxed{r_0=\left(\frac{J^2}{m\beta k}\right)^{1/(k+2)}.}
$$
The sign condition $\beta k>0$ expresses attraction. At $k=0$ the <force> vanishes and there is no nontrivial <circular orbit>. At $k=-2$, attraction requires $\beta<0$ and the condition reduces to $J^2=-2m\beta$. In that exceptional case the <effective potential> is identically zero and every $r_0>0$ is a possible circular radius for the tuned $J$; there is no isolated preferred radius. A small radial <velocity> then gives $\ddot r=0$, rather than an oscillation.

= Solution
{parent=d}
{c}

Put $r=r_0+\eta$, where $|\eta|\ll r_0$. The <Taylor expansion> at a <circular orbit> is
$$
V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r_0+\eta)=V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r_0)
+\frac12V_{\mathrm{eff}}''(r_0)\eta^2+O(\eta^3).
$$
The constant $V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r_0)$ has no effect on the <force>. The paper's displayed <harmonic potential> should be read as this expansion after subtracting that constant and neglecting higher orders; it is not an equality for the absolute <effective potential> in general. For example, when $m=J=\beta=1$ and $k=2$, $r_0=2^{-1/4}$ and $V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r_0)=\sqrt2$, whereas the printed quadratic expression is zero at $r_0$.

Differentiating again and then using $\beta k r_0^{k-2}=J^2/(mr_0^4)$ gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{\mathrm{eff}}''(r_0)&=\beta k(k-1)r_0^{k-2}+\frac{3J^2}{mr_0^4}\\
&=(k+2)\frac{J^2}{mr_0^4}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The linearized radial <equation of motion> is $m\ddot\eta=-V_{\mathrm{eff}}''(r_0)\eta$, so the <radial frequency in a power-law central potential> is
$$
\boxed{\omega_r=\sqrt{k+2}\,\frac{|J|}{mr_0^2},\qquad k>-2.}
$$
For $k<-2$ the <circular orbit> is unstable and radial disturbances grow exponentially, with growth rate $\sqrt{-(k+2)}|J|/(mr_0^2)$. At $k=-2$ the linear restoring <force> vanishes, as described in part (c). Thus an attractive <central force> alone does not guarantee <small oscillations>.

Choose the orientation $J>0$. To leading order in the radial disturbance, the azimuthal <angular frequency> is $\omega_\theta=J/(mr_0^2)$, so
$$
\boxed{\frac{\omega_r}{\omega_\theta}=\sqrt{k+2}.}
$$
For either orientation the positive <frequency> ratio is $\omega_r/|\omega_\theta|$. At $k=-1$, the ratio is $1$: there is one radial oscillation per revolution. The bound <Kepler orbit> is an <ellipse> with the force centre at a focus, with one closest and one furthest approach per revolution. Its exact <polar coordinates> equation is
$$
r(\theta)=\frac{p}{1+e\cos\theta},\qquad
p=\frac{J^2}{m|\beta|},\qquad 0<e\ll1.
$$
At $k=2$, the ratio is $2$: there are two radial oscillations per revolution. The <isotropic harmonic oscillator> trajectory is an <ellipse> centred on the force centre. An exact parametrization is
$$
x(t)=a\cos\Omega t,\qquad y(t)=b\sin\Omega t,\qquad
\Omega=\sqrt{\frac{2\beta}{m}},\qquad a\simeq b.
$$
Here $r^2=a^2\cos^2\Omega t+b^2\sin^2\Omega t$ repeats twice during one full circuit. The two different placements of the force centre distinguish these <ellipses>. These are the two all-bound-orbit cases of <Bertrand's theorem>.

The diagram makes $x$ the remaining distance to the wall: $x$ decreases from $L$ to $0$. With the endpoint condition $m(0)=0$, integrating the supplied <mass> gradient gives
$$
m(x)=\frac{Mx}{L}.
$$
Assume escaping sand has the same horizontal <velocity> as the bucket at release. There is then no horizontal recoil: the <momentum> flux of the outgoing sand cancels the $\dot m\dot x$ contribution to the bucket's <momentum> <derivative>. The <variable-mass system> equation is therefore
$$
m\ddot x=-T.
$$
Set $u=-\dot x\ge0$, the <speed> towards the wall. Since $\ddot x=u\,du/dx$, the initial condition $u(L)=0$ gives
$$
u\frac{du}{dx}=-\frac{TL}{Mx},\qquad
\boxed{u^2(x)=\frac{2TL}{M}\ln\frac Lx.}
$$
Consequently the remaining sand has <kinetic energy>
$$
\boxed{K(x)=\frac12m(x)u^2(x)=Tx\ln\frac Lx.}
$$
In terms of $z=x/L$, $K/(TL)=-z\ln z$. Its <derivative> is $-\ln z-1$ and its second <derivative> is $-1/z<0$, giving
$$
\boxed{x_K=\frac Le,\qquad K_{\max}=\frac{TL}{e}.}
$$
The magnitude of the remaining sand's <momentum> is
$$
\boxed{p(x)=m(x)u(x)=x\sqrt{\frac{2MT}{L}\ln\frac Lx}.}
$$
It is easier to maximize its square, $p^2/(2MTL)=-z^2\ln z$. Its <derivative> is $-z(2\ln z+1)$, giving the unique interior maximum
$$
\boxed{x_p=\frac L{\sqrt e},\qquad p_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{MTL}{e}}.}
$$
Both <kinetic energy> and <momentum> tend to zero at $x\to0^+$, even though the idealized <speed> diverges. There is no finite-<mass> bucket left at the endpoint, so that divergence signals the limit of the idealization.

The <leaking bucket under constant tension> has $x_K=L/e<L/\sqrt e=x_p$: the <kinetic energy> maximum is closer to the wall. As the bucket loses <mass>, its increasing <speed> continues to boost $K=pu/2$ after $p$ has begun to fall. Equivalently, $p\propto z\sqrt{-\ln z}$ and $K\propto z(-\ln z)$, so <kinetic energy> has the stronger dependence on the growing <speed>. The work $T(L-x)$ is shared between the remaining sand and the sand already lost; it is not all the bucket's instantaneous <kinetic energy>.

Take upward <velocity> as positive, and let $w>0$ be the rearward <rocket exhaust speed>. During $dt$, the ejected parcel has <mass> $-dm>0$ and laboratory <velocity> $v-w$ to first order. The <momentum> change of the rocket and this parcel equals the gravitational <impulse>:
$$
(m+dm)(v+dv)+(-dm)(v-w)-mv=-mg\,dt.
$$
Discarding the second-order product $dm\,dv$ gives the vertical <rocket equation>
$$
\boxed{m\frac{dv}{dt}=-w\frac{dm}{dt}-mg.}
$$
With $\dot m=-\alpha$ and $m=m_0-\alpha t$, integration for free flight starting from rest at $t=0$ gives
$$
\boxed{v(t)=w\ln\frac{m_0}{m_0-\alpha t}-gt,\qquad 0\le t\le T.}
$$
The logarithmic term is the ideal <Tsiolkovsky rocket equation> contribution; $gt$ is the gravity loss. Immediate upward departure requires positive initial <acceleration>, or
$$
\boxed{\alpha w>m_0g,\qquad w_{\mathrm{threshold}}=\frac{m_0g}{\alpha}.}
$$
At exact equality the initial <acceleration> is zero, but further burning reduces the weight and permits departure immediately thereafter. If departure is allowed to be delayed while fuel burns on the pad, the condition is instead $\alpha w>m(t)g$ at some time before burnout. With final <rocket dry mass> $m_f$, any powered departure before burnout requires $w>gm_f/\alpha$; the free-flight formula then starts at the actual departure time $t_\ell$ with $m(t_\ell)$, giving $v=w\ln[m(t_\ell)/m(t)]-g(t-t_\ell)$.

For the specified data, the initial <mass> includes the payload. The <rocket fuel fraction> gives
$$
m_f=(1-f)m_0=2500\,\mathrm{kg},\qquad
T=\frac{fm_0}{\alpha}=15\,\mathrm s.
$$
The $100\,\mathrm{kg}$ payload is already part of the $2500\,\mathrm{kg}$ final <mass>; it is not the entire <rocket dry mass> and must not be added a second time. Here $m_0g/\alpha=196\,\mathrm{m/s}$, well below the stated <rocket exhaust speed>, so departure is immediate. The burnout <speed> is
$$
\boxed{v(T)=2500\ln4-9.8(15)\simeq3319\,\mathrm{m/s}.}
$$
This is the <speed> at burnout. Subsequent motion under the assumed uniform <gravity> has $v(t)=v(T)-g(t-T)$.

For an <equal-speed elastic impact on a free rod>, let $h$ be the signed distance of the impact point from the rod's <center of mass>, $I=ml^2/12$, and $\mathcal J$ the horizontal <impulse> delivered to the rod. For a smooth impact the <impulse> is perpendicular to the rod. If the ball's signed final <velocity> is $u$, the rod's <center of mass> <velocity> is $V$ and its <angular velocity> is $\omega$, <momentum> balance and angular <impulse> give
$$
m(v_0-u)=\mathcal J=mV,\qquad I\omega=\mathcal J h.
$$
Both objects have positive final translational <velocities> for a nonzero impact away from the centre. More explicitly, restitution in an <elastic collision> gives $V+\omega h-u=v_0$, and hence
$$
V=\frac{2v_0}{2+mh^2/I},\qquad
u=\frac{(mh^2/I)v_0}{2+mh^2/I}\ge0.
$$
Equal final <speeds> thus require $u=V=v_0/2$. One can also see that the alternative $u=-V$ would violate $u+V=v_0$.

By conservation of <kinetic energy> in the <elastic collision>,
$$
\frac12mv_0^2=\frac12m\left(\frac{v_0}{2}\right)^2
+\frac12m\left(\frac{v_0}{2}\right)^2+\frac12I\omega^2.
$$
Thus $I\omega^2=mv_0^2/2$. Since $I\omega=mVh=mv_0h/2$, elimination of $\omega$ gives
$$
\boxed{h^2=\frac{2I}{m}=\frac{l^2}{6},\qquad |h|=\frac l{\sqrt6}.}
$$
Either side of the <center of mass> works; the nearer end is a distance $l(1/2-1/\sqrt6)$ away. The final <angular speed> is $|\omega|=\sqrt6\,v_0/l$. These values also satisfy the restitution condition because the struck point's <velocity> is $V+\omega h=3v_0/2$, reversing the initial relative contact <velocity>.

The free rod has the same <mass> $m$ and length $l$ as in part (a). There is no pivot. Put the rod's initial <center of mass> at $y=0$ and the impact end at $y=l/2$. At impact the combined <center of mass> is
$$
y_C=\frac{m(0)+m(l/2)}{2m}=\frac l4.
$$
<Conservation of momentum> gives the combined <center of mass> <velocity> $V_C=v_0/2$. Evaluate <angular momentum> about this combined <center of mass>; the incident ball's perpendicular lever arm is $l/4$, so its initial magnitude is $mv_0l/4$.

After the <perfectly inelastic collision>, the <parallel axis theorem> gives the combined <moment of inertia>
$$
I_C=\left(\frac{ml^2}{12}+m\frac{l^2}{16}\right)+m\frac{l^2}{16}
=\frac{5ml^2}{24}.
$$
With negligible external angular <impulse>, <conservation of angular momentum> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{|\omega|=\frac{mv_0l/4}{5ml^2/24}=\frac{6v_0}{5l}.}
$$
Its sense is that of the incident ball's <angular momentum>, clockwise in the source diagram. As a consistency check, the final <kinetic energy> is
$$
K_f=\frac12(2m)\left(\frac{v_0}{2}\right)^2
+\frac12\frac{5ml^2}{24}\left(\frac{6v_0}{5l}\right)^2
=\frac25mv_0^2.
$$
Thus $mv_0^2/10$, or one fifth of the initial <kinetic energy>, is dissipated in sticking. Treating the rod's original centre as a fixed pivot would give an incorrect <angular velocity>.

Let $a$ be the <center of mass> <acceleration> downhill and $F$ the uphill <static friction>. Translation and rotation about the <center of mass> obey

ma=mg\sin\theta-F,\qquad I\alpha=Fr.

The <rolling without slipping> constraint is $a=r\alpha$. For the coin's <moment of inertia> $I=mr^2/2$, this gives $F=ma/2$, so
$$
\boxed{a=\frac23g\sin\theta,\qquad F=\frac13mg\sin\theta.}
$$
The normal <force> is $N=mg\cos\theta$. <Static friction> can enforce <rolling without slipping> only if $F\le\mu N$, giving
$$
\frac13mg\sin\theta\le\mu mg\cos\theta,
\qquad
\boxed{\theta_{\max}=\arctan(3\mu).}
$$
The <static friction> acts uphill to provide the <torque>; it is not automatically equal to $\mu N$, attaining that limiting value only at the threshold. For finite $\mu\ge0$ the result lies between $0$ and $\pi/2$.
