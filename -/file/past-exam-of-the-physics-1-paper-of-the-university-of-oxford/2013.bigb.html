== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

The <parallel axis theorem> states $I=I_G+Md^2$ for parallel axes separated by $d$, with the reference axis through the <centre of mass>. The <perpendicular axis theorem> states $I_z=I_x+I_y$ for a planar lamina in the $xy$ plane, about mutually perpendicular axes through one point. It does not apply directly to a three-dimensional solid.

For the <transverse moment of inertia of a uniform solid cylinder>, divide the cylinder into thin disks at axial positions $z\in[-h/2,h/2]$. Symmetry and the <perpendicular axis theorem> give the diametral <moment of inertia> $r^2\,dM/4$ of each disk; the <parallel axis theorem> adds $z^2\,dM$. Hence
$$
I_\perp=\frac Mh\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}\left(\frac{r^2}{4}+z^2\right)dz,\qquad
\boxed{I_\perp=\frac M{12}(3r^2+h^2).}
$$
The thin-disk limit is $Mr^2/4$, while the slender-rod limit is $Mh^2/12$.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <cyclic coordinate> $q_j$ is absent explicitly from the <Lagrangian>: $\partial L/\partial q_j=0$, even though $\dot q_j$ may occur. The <Euler-Lagrange equation> then gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_j}\right)=0,\qquad p_j=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_j}=\text{constant}.}
$$
Thus a continuous coordinate-translation symmetry gives a conserved <conjugate momentum>. The conserved quantity need not be ordinary linear <momentum>: an absent azimuthal angle can give conserved <angular momentum>. The derivative is taken holding the other coordinates and <velocities> fixed.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Define the <energy function> $\mathcal E=\sum_i\dot q_i p_i-L$. Along solutions of the <Euler-Lagrange equations>,
$$
\frac{d\mathcal E}{dt}=\sum_i\left[\ddot q_i p_i+\dot q_i\dot p_i-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}\dot q_i-\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}\ddot q_i\right]-\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\partial L/\partial t=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathcal E=\text{constant}.}
$$
For an ordinary isolated <mechanical system> with $L=T-U$, a time-independent quadratic <kinetic energy> and a velocity-independent <potential energy>, $\sum_i\dot q_i p_i=2T$, so $\mathcal E=T+U$. For a more general <Lagrangian>, the conserved quantity is the displayed <energy function>; identifying it with $T+U$ requires these assumptions.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For the <relativistic head-on elastic collision with a stationary target>, use $c=1$ as stipulated, so <masses> are measured in <energy> units. The incoming <momentum> is $p=\sqrt{E^2-M^2}$ and the invariant squared total <four-momentum> is
$$
s=(E+m)^2-p^2=M^2+m^2+2mE.
$$
The <centre-of-mass frame> moves at $\beta_*=p/(E+m)$, with $\gamma_*=(E+m)/\sqrt s$. In this frame the incident particle has
$$
E_M^*=\frac{M^2+mE}{\sqrt s},\qquad p_M^*=\gamma_*(p-\beta_*E)=\frac{mp}{\sqrt s}.
$$
A nontrivial head-on <elastic collision> reverses $p_M^*$ and preserves $E_M^*$. Boosting back to the <laboratory frame> gives
$$
E'=\gamma_*(E_M^*-\beta_*p_M^*)=\frac{(E+m)(M^2+mE)-m(E^2-M^2)}s,
$$
$$
\boxed{E'=\frac{2mM^2+E(m^2+M^2)}{2Em+m^2+M^2}.}
$$
The no-interaction forward branch instead leaves $E'=E$; the requested formula describes the actual one-dimensional encounter and recoil.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

With $M\gg m$ and $Em\gg M^2$, one also has $E\gg M^2/m\gg M$. The denominator in <3/a> is then dominated by $2Em$, and the numerator by $EM^2$:
$$
\boxed{E'\sim\frac{M^2}{2m}.}
$$
This residual total <energy> is still much larger than the incident particle's <rest energy> $M$, but is a small fraction of its incident <energy> $E$. The target acquires most of the available <energy> in this high-energy, heavy-projectile limit.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $m\gg M$, retain the ratio $E/m$ until a further limit is specified. Since $E\ge M$, the leading expression from <3/a> is
$$
\boxed{E'\simeq\frac{Em}{m+2E}=\frac{E}{1+2E/m}.}
$$
For an infinitely heavy target at fixed incident <energy>, $E/m\to0$ and $\boxed{E'\to E}$: the projectile reverses with negligible target recoil <energy>. If the incident particle is ultrarelativistic even relative to the target, $E\gg m\gg M$, the same leading expression instead gives $E'\sim m/2$. The mass hierarchy alone does not justify replacing $E/m$ by zero.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

For <maximum projectile range from an elevated launch>, let $X$ be horizontal <projectile range> from the tower and $\alpha$ the launch angle above horizontal. The <projectile motion> trajectory is
$$
y=h+X\tan\alpha-\frac{gX^2}{2u^2}(1+\tan^2\alpha).
$$
Put $z=\tan\alpha$. For a fixed $X$, its largest possible $y$ occurs at $z=u^2/(gX)$; the envelope is $y_{\max}=h+u^2/(2g)-gX^2/(2u^2)$. The furthest attainable ground point satisfies $y_{\max}=0$, so
$$
\boxed{X_{\max}=\frac ug\sqrt{u^2+2gh},\qquad \tan\alpha_{\rm opt}=\frac{u}{\sqrt{u^2+2gh}}.}
$$
The optimum angle is less than $45^\circ$ for $h>0$ and approaches $45^\circ$ as $h\to0$. The result is the maximum horizontal distance, using uniform gravity and no <drag>.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

If $S'$ moves at $+v$ relative to $S$, the <Lorentz transformations> are
$$
\boxed{x'=\gamma(x-vt),\quad t'=\gamma(t-vx/c^2),\quad \gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}.}
$$
A rod at rest in $S$ has length $L$. To measure it in $S'$, select endpoint events simultaneous there: $\Delta t'=0$, so $\Delta t=vL/c^2$ and $\Delta x'=\gamma(L-v\Delta t)=L/\gamma$. This is <length contraction>.

Now let a clock at rest in $S'$ cross the two rod endpoints. These are different events, with $\Delta x=L$, $\Delta t=L/v$ and $\Delta x'=0$. Their transformed time interval is
$$
\Delta t'=\gamma\left(\frac Lv-\frac{vL}{c^2}\right)=\frac{L}{\gamma v}=\frac{L'}v.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{L'=L/\gamma,\qquad \Delta t=\gamma\Delta t'.}
$$
The moving clock measures the <proper time> $\Delta t'$. The rod's contracted length divided by its <speed> in that clock's frame gives exactly the same crossing <time>; <time dilation> and <length contraction> are consistent consequences of the same transformation. Measuring a length and measuring a traversal interval require different simultaneity conditions.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

Choose the positive axis from the approaching source towards the observer. The photon that reaches the observer is emitted forward, with source-frame <four-momentum> $(E/c,E/c,0,0)$. Boosting to the observer frame gives $E_{\rm obs}=\gamma E(1+\beta)$, where $\beta=v/c$. Since photon <energy> is $h_{\rm P}\nu$,
$$
\boxed{\nu=\frac E{h_{\rm P}}\gamma(1+\beta)=\frac E{h_{\rm P}}\sqrt{\frac{1+v/c}{1-v/c}}.}
$$
This is the approaching-source <relativistic Doppler effect>. A photon emitted in a general source-frame direction would instead have the factor $\gamma(1+\beta\cos\theta_*)$; forward emission is the geometry of the photon directed to the observer here.

== 7
{scope}

=== Solution

The <alpha particle> has <electric charge> $2e$ and acquires <kinetic energy> $K=2e(20\,\mathrm{kV})=40\,\mathrm{keV}$. For <Coulomb head-on closest approach with target recoil>, the initially stationary <beryllium> nucleus is free to recoil. Approximating the nuclear <masses> by $4u$ and $9u$, the initial relative-motion <kinetic energy> is
$$
K_* = \frac{m_{\rm Be}}{m_\alpha+m_{\rm Be}}K=\frac9{13}(40\,\mathrm{keV})=27.6923\,\mathrm{keV}.
$$
At closest separation the relative radial <velocity> vanishes, although the <centre of mass> continues moving. <Conservation of energy> for the relative motion gives
$$
\frac{(2e)(4e)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r_{\min}}=K_*,\qquad
\boxed{r_{\min}=\frac{8e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0K_*}\simeq4.16\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm m.}
$$
Using $e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)=1.43996\,\mathrm{MeV\,fm}$ gives $r_{\min}\simeq416\,\mathrm{fm}$. If the nucleus were externally held fixed, the relevant <energy> would be $K$, yielding $288\,\mathrm{fm}$ instead. Initially stationary is not the same as constrained stationary; the given proton and neutron counts supply the recoil correction. Nuclear sizes and strong interactions are negligible at this separation.

== 8
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <geostationary orbit> has the same <angular velocity> as the <Earth>. Let $M_E=4\pi\rho R^3/3$ and $r_g=\eta R$. Balancing the required <centripetal acceleration> with the exterior <gravitational field> gives
$$
\omega^2r_g=\frac{GM_E}{r_g^2},\qquad
\boxed{\eta=\left(\frac{GM_E}{\omega^2R^3}\right)^{1/3}=\left(\frac{4\pi G\rho}{3\omega^2}\right)^{1/3}.}
$$
The <orbital plane> must be equatorial and the motion prograde for the satellite to remain over one point. The uniform density assumption supplies $M_E$ in terms of the specified $\rho$ and $R$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Taking the <Earth>'s sidereal rotation period as $86164\,\mathrm s$, $\omega=2\pi/(86164\,\mathrm s)$, and $G=6.6743\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}$ gives
$$
\boxed{\eta\simeq6.61.}
$$
The <orbital radius> is therefore about $6.61R$, or an altitude of about $5.61R$. Replacing the sidereal day by $86400\,\mathrm s$ changes this estimate by less than $0.2\%$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For the <uniform unloaded space-elevator cable>, let the constant <linear mass density> be $\lambda$ and the outer endpoint be $b=\eta'R$. In the co-rotating frame the outward effective <force> per unit length is $\lambda(\omega^2r-GM_E/r^2)$. The cable is just clear of the surface, with neither a ground load nor an endpoint counterweight, so both ends have zero applied <tension>. The integrated effective <force> must vanish:
$$
0=\lambda\int_R^b\left(\omega^2r-\frac{GM_E}{r^2}\right)dr=\lambda\left[\frac{\omega^2}{2}(b^2-R^2)+GM_E\left(\frac1b-\frac1R\right)\right].
$$
Using $GM_E=\omega^2\eta^3R^3$, this becomes
$$
0=(\eta'-1)\left[\frac{\eta'+1}{2}-\frac{\eta^3}{\eta'}\right].
$$
The root $\eta'=1$ is a zero-length cable. The physical nonzero cable has
$$
\boxed{\eta'=\frac{\sqrt{1+8\eta^3}-1}{2}\simeq23.6.}
$$
It extends well beyond the <geostationary orbit> so its outer <centrifugal force> balances the inner segment's weight. If a cable is anchored with a nonzero endpoint load or has a counterweight, that load changes the integrated balance; its length is not fixed by the zero-end-load formula.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

An element of the rotating cable satisfies
$$
\frac{dT}{dr}=\lambda\left(\frac{GM_E}{r^2}-\omega^2r\right).
$$
With $T(R)=0$, integration gives
$$
T(r)=\lambda\left[GM_E\left(\frac1R-\frac1r\right)-\frac{\omega^2}{2}(r^2-R^2)\right].
$$
It increases below $r_g$, decreases above $r_g$, and returns to zero at $b$. Hence
$$
\boxed{r_{T_{\max}}=r_g=\eta R,\qquad T_{\max}=\lambda\omega^2R^2\left(\eta^3-\frac32\eta^2+\frac12\right).}
$$
The <tension> is greatest at the <geostationary orbit>, where local effective gravity is zero. A zero local effective <force> does not mean zero <tension>: that section transmits the loads of the entire inner and outer cable portions.

== 9
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $\theta$ measure the rim <point mass>'s displacement from vertically below the fixed axis. The disk has <moment of inertia> $I_D=\tfrac12(2M)R^2=MR^2$, and the rim <mass> contributes another $MR^2$. Its varying <gravitational potential energy> is $-MgR\cos\theta$. Thus
$$
\boxed{L=MR^2\dot\theta^2+MgR\cos\theta.}
$$
The disk's <centre of mass> remains fixed, so its own gravitational contribution is a constant and has been omitted. The total fixed-axis <moment of inertia> is $2MR^2$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Euler-Lagrange equation> from <9/a> is $2MR^2\ddot\theta+MgR\sin\theta=0$. Linearizing about $\theta=0$ gives
$$
\boxed{\omega_{\rm fixed}=\sqrt{\frac{g}{2R}},\qquad f_{\rm fixed}=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{2R}},\qquad T_{\rm fixed}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2R}{g}}.}
$$
Both <angular frequency> and ordinary <frequency> are given to distinguish the two conventions. The stable position places the extra <mass> below the pivot.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For the <rolling disk with a rim point mass>, impose <rolling without slipping> and take the clockwise angular displacement $\theta$ from the configuration with the extra <mass> at the bottom. The disk centre has $x_C=R\theta$ and $y_C=R$. The extra <mass> has
$$
x_M=R(\theta-\sin\theta),\qquad y_M=R(1-\cos\theta),\qquad v_M^2=2R^2(1-\cos\theta)\dot\theta^2.
$$
The disk's translational and <rotational kinetic energies> sum to $3MR^2\dot\theta^2/2$. Including the rim <mass> gives
$$
T=\frac12MR^2(5-2\cos\theta)\dot\theta^2,\qquad U=MgR(1-\cos\theta).
$$
The exact <Euler-Lagrange equation> is
$$
MR^2(5-2\cos\theta)\ddot\theta+MR^2\sin\theta\,\dot\theta^2+MgR\sin\theta=0.
$$
To first order, $3MR^2\ddot\theta+MgR\theta=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\omega_{\rm roll}=\sqrt{\frac{g}{3R}},\qquad \frac{T_{\rm roll}}{T_{\rm fixed}}=\sqrt{\frac32}.}
$$
At the bottom the extra <mass> is instantaneously at the contact point and has no first-order <velocity>. The increased effective inertia comes from the moving disk. Static <friction> enforces the rolling constraint without doing <work> at the stationary contact.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

In the <rim-mass pulley coupled to a hanging mass>, the printed figure shows the smaller pulley fixed overhead, and the <mechanical string> leaves the right-hand tangent of the larger pulley. Let $\theta$ increase counterclockwise from the downward vertical to the radius carrying $M$. Then the hanging <mass> $m$ moves downward by $R\theta$. The string is taken to grip the rim without slipping. Its <tension> must be solved dynamically, rather than set equal to $mg$ during motion.

The <kinetic energy> and <potential energy> are
$$
T=\frac12(M+m)R^2\dot\theta^2,\qquad U=-MgR\cos\theta-mgR\theta.
$$
The <Euler-Lagrange equation> and the condition for <equilibrium> are
$$
\boxed{(M+m)R\ddot\theta+Mg\sin\theta-mg=0,\qquad \sin\theta_e=\frac mM.}
$$
One can also eliminate the <tension> from $MR^2\ddot\theta=TR-MgR\sin\theta$ and $mR\ddot\theta=mg-T$. For $0<m<M$, the solution with $\cos\theta_e>0$ is stable; the other solution in a full turn is unstable.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $\theta=\theta_e+\delta$ at the stable <equilibrium>. Since $M\sin\theta_e=m$, linearization of <9/d> gives
$$
(M+m)R\ddot\delta+Mg\cos\theta_e\,\delta=0.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\omega_0=\sqrt{\frac{Mg\cos\theta_e}{(M+m)R}}=\sqrt{\frac{g\sqrt{M^2-m^2}}{(M+m)R}},\qquad f_0=\frac{\omega_0}{2\pi}.}
$$
As $m\to0$, the result approaches $\sqrt{g/R}$, the <simple pendulum> value. As $m\uparrow M$, the restoring curvature tends to zero and this small-amplitude <frequency> tends to zero; the endpoint itself is not a stable quadratic minimum.

== 10
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For the approaching ship, $\beta_s=0.85$ and $\gamma_s=(1-0.85^2)^{-1/2}$. Its station-frame <length contraction> gives $L_s=100/\gamma_s\,\mathrm m$. Adding the two station-frame clearances gives
$$
\boxed{L_b=100\sqrt{1-0.85^2}+4\simeq56.6783\,\mathrm m.}
$$
This is the berth's <proper length>, since the berth is at rest in the station. It is a simultaneous station-frame allocation, not a ship-frame guarantee of a safe docking maneuver.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The berth moves at $0.85c$ in the ship's initial <rest frame>, so it too is subject to <length contraction>:
$$
\boxed{L_b'=\frac{L_b}{\gamma_s}=100(1-0.85^2)+4\sqrt{1-0.85^2}\simeq29.8571\,\mathrm m.}
$$
The ship remains $100\,\mathrm m$ long in this frame. The two length comparisons use different simultaneous endpoint events; this is the same <relativity of simultaneity> that resolves the <ladder paradox>. Physical docking also requires deceleration, whose later effects are outside these initial inertial measurements.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Interpret the one-light-day separation $D$ as measured in the station frame at capsule launch, and retain the ship's initial inertial <rest frame> until reception at the station. The phrase does not explicitly name a frame; an alternative is given below. The ship-frame simultaneous separation is $D'=D/\gamma_s$. The capsule moves at $0.9c$ towards a station approaching at $0.85c$, so their separation decreases at $1.75c$ in this frame. This is a separation rate, not either object's <speed>. Hence the elapsed ship-frame coordinate <time> is
$$
\boxed{\Delta t_s=\frac{D}{\gamma_s(0.9+0.85)c}\simeq0.301018\,\mathrm{day}\simeq7.2244\,\mathrm h.}
$$
It is the interval assigned using synchronized clocks in the ship frame to the launch and remote arrival. A single onboard clock does not directly mark the remote arrival event; observing its arrival signal would add a light-travel delay. If the stated one-light-day separation were instead measured in the ship frame, the answer would be $\Delta t_s=(1/1.75)\,\mathrm{day}=0.571429\,\mathrm{day}$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Launch and arrival occur on the capsule's own worldline, so its clock measures the <proper time>. In the ship frame its <Lorentz factor> is $\gamma_{c/s}=(1-0.9^2)^{-1/2}$; therefore
$$
\boxed{\Delta\tau_c=\frac{\Delta t_s}{\gamma_{c/s}}=\Delta t_s\sqrt{1-0.9^2}\simeq0.131211\,\mathrm{day}\simeq3.1491\,\mathrm h.}
$$
This uses the station-frame distance convention stated in <10/c/i>. With a one-light-day distance in the ship frame instead, it becomes $\Delta\tau_c=\sqrt{0.19}/1.75\,\mathrm{day}\simeq0.249080\,\mathrm{day}$. The uncertainty is a distance-frame convention in the wording, not a missing figure or an unanswered mathematical part.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The <velocity-addition formula> combines the ship's station-frame <velocity> and the capsule's ship-frame <velocity>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{v_c}{c}=\frac{0.85+0.9}{1+0.85(0.9)}=\frac{350}{353}\simeq0.9915014.}
$$
For the distance convention in <10/c/i>, station-frame arrival takes $D/v_c=(353/350)\,\mathrm{day}=1.008571\,\mathrm{day}$. Transforming its two endpoint events back to the ship frame gives $\gamma_s[D/v_c-0.85D/c]$, agreeing with <10/c/i>. The capsule remains below the <speed of light> in either frame.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The capsule continues inertially at $v_c/c=350/353$ while the ship reduces its station-frame <speed> to $0.7c$. Transform the ship's new <velocity> into the capsule frame:
$$
\boxed{\frac{v_{s/c}}c=\frac{0.7-350/353}{1-0.7(350/353)}=-\frac{343}{360}\simeq-0.952778.}
$$
Thus its <speed> is $0.952778c$, and the negative sign means it recedes behind the capsule along the chosen axis. The same transformation compares the subsequent local inertial <velocities>; the details of the intervening deceleration are not needed.

== 11
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $\mu=GM_*$ and use plane polar coordinates in the orbital plane. The Newtonian <gravitational potential energy> is $U=-\mu M_p/r$, so
$$
\boxed{L=\frac{M_p}{2}(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\phi^2)+\frac{GM_*M_p}{r}.}
$$
A <central force> keeps the orbit in a plane. The star is treated as stationary, appropriate when $M_p\ll M_*$; otherwise the two-body reduction uses the <reduced mass> and the relative separation.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Lagrangian> has no explicit <time> dependence, so its <energy function> is constant. With total <speed> $v^2=\dot r^2+r^2\dot\phi^2$, division by $M_p$ gives
$$
\epsilon=\frac12v^2-\frac\mu r=\text{constant}.
$$
Consequently, between the initial point $(r_i,v_i)$ and any other point,
$$
\boxed{v^2(r)=v_i^2+2GM_*\left(\frac1r-\frac1{r_i}\right).}
$$
This energy relation holds for nonradial motion too. Radially outward motion is the stipulated approximation for the subsequent probe calculation; <angular momentum> is then zero.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Retain the source's idealized solar radial trajectory. Use $\mu_\odot\simeq1.32712\times10^{11}\,\mathrm{km^3\,s^{-2}}$, $r_i=1.5\times10^9\,\mathrm{km}$ and $v_i=22\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. At $r=1.8\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{km}$,
$$
\boxed{v=\sqrt{22^2+2(1.32712\times10^{11})\left(\frac1{1.8\times10^{10}}-\frac1{1.5\times10^9}\right)}\simeq17.94\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The <speed> has decreased because the probe gains <gravitational potential energy> while moving away from the <Sun>. This is the supplied simplified model, rather than a reconstruction of <Voyager 1>'s actual three-dimensional trajectory or planetary encounters.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Setting $1/r\to0$ in <11/b> gives the asymptotic <speed>
$$
\boxed{v_\infty=\sqrt{22^2-\frac{2(1.32712\times10^{11})}{1.5\times10^9}}\simeq17.52\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The radicand is positive, so the ideal probe has positive total <mechanical energy> and escapes the <Sun>. The later dust-cloud model takes this limiting <speed> as its initial <speed>.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

For <constant-area capture of stationary dust>, let $x=0$ at cloud entry, $M(0)=M_p$, $v(0)=v_\infty$, and define $k=\rho A$ with fixed collecting area $A$. Every swept volume $A\,dx$ adds stationary <mass>, so
$$
\frac{dM}{dt}=kv,\qquad M=M_p+kx.
$$
There is no external longitudinal <force>, and the incoming dust has zero <momentum> in the cloud frame. The <variable-mass system> therefore satisfies $d(Mv)/dt=0$, rather than $M\dot v=0$. Using $Mv=M_pv_\infty$ gives
$$
M\dot M=kM_pv_\infty,\qquad M^2=M_p^2+2kM_pv_\infty t.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{M(t)=M_p\sqrt{1+\frac{2\rho Av_\infty t}{M_p}},\qquad v(t)=\frac{v_\infty}{\sqrt{1+2\rho Av_\infty t/M_p}},}
$$
$$
\boxed{x(t)=\frac{M_p}{\rho A}\left[\sqrt{1+\frac{2\rho Av_\infty t}{M_p}}-1\right].}
$$
The <kinetic energy> decreases during sticking: $dK/dt=-\tfrac12\dot M v^2$. The lost <energy> becomes internal <energy>. Radiation, gravity, sputtering and changes in area are excluded by the ideal complete-collection model. The zero-density limit recovers $M=M_p$, $v=v_\infty$ and $x=v_\infty t$.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

Use a year of $365.25$ days, so $t=9.46728\times10^8\,\mathrm s$, and $k=\rho A=10^{-11}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}}$. With $M_p=722\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $v_\infty\simeq1.752\times10^4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, <11/e> gives
$$
\boxed{M_f\simeq872\,\mathrm{kg},\qquad v_f\simeq14.5\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The corresponding cloud thickness is $x_f=(M_f-M_p)/k\simeq1.50\times10^{13}\,\mathrm m$. Checking $M_fv_f=M_pv_\infty$ verifies <conservation of momentum>; using the initial <speed> for all thirty years would overestimate the collected <mass>.
