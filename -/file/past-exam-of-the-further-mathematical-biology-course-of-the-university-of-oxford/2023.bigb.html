= Solution
{parent=a}

The quantities $n_i(\tau,t)$ are <cell-cycle age densities>: $n_i(\tau,t)\,d\tau$ counts <biological cells> whose time since entering phase $i$ lies in $[\tau,\tau+d\tau]$. Here $\tau$ is time spent in the current phase, rather than time since birth. In this two-stage approximation to the <cell cycle>, a <biological cell> passes from <G1 phase> to <G2 phase>, then completes <cell division> and produces two daughters entering <G1 phase>.

The time <derivative> measures change at fixed phase age. The age <derivative> transports <biological cells> towards larger $\tau$ at unit speed, because $d\tau/dt=1$. Thus each equation is an <age-structured population equation>. The nonnegative <hazard function> $\Gamma_i(\tau)$ means that, conditional on still being in phase $i$ at age $\tau$, the probability of leaving during the next $dt$ is $\Gamma_i(\tau)dt+o(dt)$. Its product with $n_i$ is the loss from that phase-age class; it is not an additional death term.

The <boundary condition> at $\tau=0$ specifies the incoming <flux>. Every <G1 phase> exit contributes one entrant to <G2 phase>, whereas every <G2 phase> exit contributes two entrants to <G1 phase>. The <initial conditions> specify the phase-age composition at $t=0$. Consequently <integration> over phase age gives the useful population balance
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}(N_1+N_2)=\int_0^\infty\Gamma_2(\tau)n_2(\tau,t)\,d\tau.}
$$
Each completed <cell division> increases the total number of <biological cells> by one.

The unit integrals of the profiles make $N_i(t)=\int_0^\infty n_i(\tau,t)\,d\tau$ the phase populations, and $f_i$ their normalized <cell-cycle age densities>. Assume the age tails vanish and the following integrals are finite. For a fixed profile define its mean exit <hazard function> by
$$
\boxed{\Lambda_i=\int_0^\infty\Gamma_i(\tau)f_i(\tau)\,d\tau,\qquad i=1,2.}
$$
These constants have units of inverse time. Integrating the <age-structured population equations> and using the <boundary conditions> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
N_1'&=-[n_1(\tau,t)]_0^\infty-\int_0^\infty\Gamma_1n_1\,d\tau
=n_1(0,t)-\Lambda_1N_1,\\
N_2'&=-[n_2(\tau,t)]_0^\infty-\int_0^\infty\Gamma_2n_2\,d\tau
=n_2(0,t)-\Lambda_2N_2.
\end{aligned}
$$
The incoming <fluxes> are $n_1(0,t)=2\Lambda_2N_2$ and $n_2(0,t)=\Lambda_1N_1$, so
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}N_1\\N_2\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}-\Lambda_1&2\Lambda_2\\\Lambda_1&-\Lambda_2\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}N_1\\N_2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The <separable-profile closure for an age-structured population> has a qualification: arbitrary age-dependent <hazard functions> do not give this constant-coefficient <ordinary differential equation> for arbitrary <initial conditions>. Without fixed $f_i$, the effective exit rates are $\int\Gamma_i n_i\,d\tau/N_i$ and can vary with time. Moreover, an exact positive <separation of variables> with both profiles fixed forces $N_2/N_1$ to be constant through the incoming <fluxes>, hence selects a common exponential mode. The displayed <ordinary differential equation> is the necessary integrated system for such profiles; it is also exact for arbitrary populations when both <hazard functions> are age independent.

Write $A$ for the population <matrix> and assume $\Lambda_1,\Lambda_2>0$. Its <characteristic polynomial> is
$$
\det(sI-A)=(s+\Lambda_1)(s+\Lambda_2)-2\Lambda_1\Lambda_2
=s^2+(\Lambda_1+\Lambda_2)s-\Lambda_1\Lambda_2.
$$
The two <eigenvalues> have opposite signs. The <dominant eigenvalue> is
$$
\boxed{\mu=\frac{\sqrt{\Lambda_1^2+6\Lambda_1\Lambda_2+\Lambda_2^2}-\Lambda_1-\Lambda_2}{2}>0,}
$$
and the other <eigenvalue> is $\nu=-(\Lambda_1+\Lambda_2)-\mu<0$. A positive <eigenvector> for $\mu$ satisfies
$$
\boxed{\frac{N_2}{N_1}\longrightarrow\frac{\Lambda_1}{\mu+\Lambda_2}
=\frac{\mu+\Lambda_1}{2\Lambda_2}.}
$$
For any nonzero nonnegative population vector in this constant-coefficient system, the coefficient of the positive <eigenvector> is positive: a positive left <eigenvector> annihilates the other <eigenvector> and has positive pairing with that population vector. Thus $N_i(t)=C_i e^{\mu t}+O(e^{\nu t})$, with $C_i>0$, and $N_i'/N_i\to\mu$. The long-time leading term therefore obeys $N_i'=\mu N_i$.

Substituting that exponential mode into the <age-structured population equation> gives
$$
f_i'(\tau)=-[\mu+\Gamma_i(\tau)]f_i(\tau).
$$
Define the phase-residence <survival function> and its discounted integral by
$$
S_i(\tau)=\exp\left[-\int_0^\tau\Gamma_i(a)\,da\right],\qquad
Z_i(\mu)=\int_0^\infty e^{-\mu a}S_i(a)\,da.
$$
Then the normalized <cell-cycle age densities> are
$$
\boxed{f_i(\tau)=\frac{e^{-\mu\tau}S_i(\tau)}{Z_i(\mu)},\qquad i=1,2.}
$$
Since <integration> of $f_i'=-(\mu+\Gamma_i)f_i$ gives $f_i(0)=\mu+\Lambda_i$, this is equivalently
$$
\boxed{f_i(\tau)=(\mu+\Lambda_i)\exp\left[-\mu\tau-\int_0^\tau\Gamma_i(a)\,da\right].}
$$
The constants must be consistent with both incoming <fluxes>. Put
$$
H_i(\mu)=\int_0^\infty\Gamma_i(a)e^{-\mu a}S_i(a)\,da
=1-\mu Z_i(\mu).
$$
The <boundary conditions> for amplitudes $n_i=e^{\mu t}\phi_i$ give $\phi_1(0)=2H_2\phi_2(0)$ and $\phi_2(0)=H_1\phi_1(0)$. Hence the <two-stage Euler-Lotka equation> is
$$
\boxed{2H_1(\mu)H_2(\mu)=1.}
$$
It determines $\mu$ directly from the <hazard functions>; the formula in terms of $\Lambda_i$ is not permission to choose those constants independently of the profiles. If each phase is eventually left with probability one, $H_i(0)=1$ and $H_i(\mu)$ decreases to zero, giving a unique positive root under the usual finite, non-instantaneous residence-time assumptions. More generally positive growth requires $2H_1(0)H_2(0)>1$. Nonnegative <hazard functions> alone do not ensure it: if one phase has zero exit <hazard function>, growth cannot have the asserted positive exponent. Convergence of the complete age distribution to these profiles needs the usual mixing assumptions on phase-residence times; the two-dimensional <eigenvalue> calculation establishes the long-time claim for the fixed-profile system.

Because the <hazard functions> are constant, normalization gives $\Lambda_1=2\alpha$ and $\Lambda_2=3\alpha$. The population <characteristic polynomial> factors as
$$
s^2+5\alpha s-6\alpha^2=(s-\alpha)(s+6\alpha).
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\mu=\alpha,\qquad f_1(\tau)=3\alpha e^{-3\alpha\tau},\qquad f_2(\tau)=4\alpha e^{-4\alpha\tau}.}
$$
These <exponential distributions> describe ages among currently present <biological cells>. Their rates differ from the phase-exit rates because <exponential growth> overrepresents younger entrants. The stable phase ratio is $N_2/N_1=1/2$, so the <G1 phase> and <G2 phase> fractions approach $2/3$ and $1/3$.

Increasing $\alpha$ rescales all times by $1/\alpha$: the population undergoes faster <exponential growth>, its <doubling time> is $\boxed{(\log2)/\alpha}$, and the profile <means> $1/(3\alpha)$ and $1/(4\alpha)$ decrease. The phase fractions do not depend on $\alpha$. The population <matrix> has exact solution
$$
\begin{pmatrix}N_1\\N_2\end{pmatrix}
=C e^{\alpha t}\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}
+E e^{-6\alpha t}\begin{pmatrix}-3\\2\end{pmatrix},
$$
so population-ratio transients decay relative to the growing term at rate $7\alpha$. This exact population formula does not require initially stationary <cell-cycle age densities>.

Put $k=\beta/\gamma$. The <nullclines> of the space-clamped <FitzHugh-Nagumo model> are
$$
\dot v=0:\quad w=f(v)+I,\qquad
\dot w=0:\quad w=kv.
$$
The first <nullcline> is a downward-leading <cubic polynomial>, shifted vertically by $I$; the second <nullcline> is a line through the origin with slope $k>0$. Their intersections satisfy
$$
I=h(v),\qquad h(v)=kv-f(v)=v^3-(1+\alpha)v^2+(\alpha+k)v.
$$
Writing $M=(1-\alpha+\alpha^2)/3$, differentiation gives
$$
f'(v)=-3\left(v-\frac{1+\alpha}{3}\right)^2+M,\qquad h'(v)=k-f'(v).
$$
If $k>M$, $h$ increases strictly, so there is exactly one <equilibrium>. If $k=M$, $h$ is still strictly increasing, although its <derivative> vanishes at one point, so the <equilibrium> is again unique. If $0<k<M$, define
$$
u_\pm=\frac{1+\alpha\pm\sqrt{1-\alpha+\alpha^2-3k}}3,\qquad
I_{\rm low}=h(u_+),\quad I_{\rm high}=h(u_-).
$$
Here $h$ has a local maximum at $u_-$ and a local minimum at $u_+$; therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{ll}
I_{\rm low}<I<I_{\rm high}:&\text{three distinct equilibria},\\
I<I_{\rm low}\ \text{or}\ I>I_{\rm high}:&\text{one equilibrium}.
\end{array}}
$$
At either endpoint there are two distinct <equilibria>, one a double root. These are the <saddle-node bifurcation> boundaries omitted by the generic one-or-three wording. At $k=M$ and $I=h((1+\alpha)/3)$ there is one triple root. This is the <cubic FitzHugh-Nagumo nullcline criterion>.

At an <equilibrium> $(v_*,kv_*)$, set $p=f'(v_*)$. The <Jacobian matrix>, its <trace> and its <determinant> are
$$
J_* =\begin{pmatrix}p&-1\\\beta&-\gamma\end{pmatrix},\qquad
T=p-\gamma,\qquad \Delta=\beta-\gamma p=\gamma(k-p).
$$
The <eigenvalues> and their <discriminant> are
$$
s_\pm=\frac{p-\gamma\pm\sqrt{(p+\gamma)^2-4\beta}}2,\qquad
\mathcal D=(p+\gamma)^2-4\beta.
$$
Thus the complete local <linear stability> test is
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{ll}
p>k:&\text{saddle},\\
p<k,\ p<\gamma:&\text{asymptotically stable},\\
\gamma<p<k:&\text{unstable}.
\end{array}}
$$
In the last two cases, $\mathcal D>0$ gives a <node>, while $\mathcal D<0$ gives a <focus>; the sign of $T$ distinguishes inward and outward trajectories. At $\mathcal D=0$, a nonzero repeated <eigenvalue> gives a degenerate <node>. At $p=\gamma<k$ the <eigenvalues> are purely imaginary, so <linear stability analysis> alone does not decide <asymptotic stability>; with the required crossing and nondegeneracy this is a <Hopf bifurcation>. At $p=k$ there is a zero <eigenvalue>, requiring a nonlinear test and generically marking the <saddle-node bifurcation> in the preceding answer.

For a unique simple <equilibrium>, $p<k$, so it can be a <sink equilibrium> or a <source equilibrium>. The number of <equilibria> alone does not decide <stability>. If $k\le\gamma$, the unique simple <equilibrium> is always a <sink equilibrium>. If $\gamma<k$, it can become a <source equilibrium> where $\gamma<p<k$. The same conclusion follows particularly simply if $\gamma>M$: the <trace> is negative at every nonsaddle <equilibrium>.

For three simple <equilibria> ordered by their $v$ coordinates, the middle one has $p>k$ and is a <saddle equilibrium>; the two outer ones have $p<k$. When both outer <equilibria> are <sink equilibria>, each has an attracting neighborhood, giving <bistability>. In the usual two-basin portrait, the <stable manifold> of the <saddle equilibrium> separates their basins. Each branch of its <unstable manifold> initially leaves towards a different side; its eventual destination requires a global argument. If $k\le\gamma$, both outer <equilibria> must be <sink equilibria>. If $\gamma<k$, either or both outer <equilibria> can instead be <source equilibria>; the same test $p\lessgtr\gamma$ applies separately to each. The three lower panels illustrate all three possibilities up to exchanging left and right.

The arrows in the <phase plane> follow directly from the <nullclines>: below $w=f(v)+I$ the horizontal component points right, and above it left; below $w=kv$ the vertical component points up, and above it down. Near a <stable focus> trajectories spiral inward; near an unstable <focus> they spiral outward. Near a <saddle equilibrium> they approach along its <stable manifold> and leave along its <unstable manifold>. These directions, together with the local tests, determine the sketches.

{title=<Phase portraits> of the <FitzHugh-Nagumo model> with one or three <equilibria>, including changes of <linear stability>}

There is also a useful global conclusion when the unique <equilibrium> is a <source equilibrium>. With
$$
E(v,w)=\frac{\beta v^2+w^2}{2},\qquad
\dot E=\beta v f(v)+\beta Iv-\gamma w^2,
$$
the leading term in $v$ is $-\beta v^4$, so sufficiently large ellipses are <trapping regions>. A small repelling neighborhood of the hyperbolic <source equilibrium> can be removed to form an invariant annulus. The <Poincaré-Bendixson theorem> then guarantees at least one <periodic orbit> in that annulus. The numerical sketch illustrates the resulting sustained oscillation; this argument does not assert uniqueness of a <limit cycle>. A stable <equilibrium> can instead represent a resting <membrane potential>, with perturbations producing damped relaxation; near a threshold, a sufficiently large perturbation can produce a large excursion before recovery. Global basin boundaries and coexistence of <periodic orbits> need more information than the number and local <stability> of <equilibria>.

There is a consistency issue in the printed model. If $w\equiv w_0$ is imposed while retaining its evolution equation, then $0=\beta v-\gamma w_0$ forces $v\equiv\gamma w_0/\beta$. Thus the full two-equation system has no nonconstant <travelling wave> with exactly constant $w$. Its constant solution exists only when $I=w_0-f(\gamma w_0/\beta)$. The intended wave calculation must treat the <recovery variable> as externally frozen, leaving the scalar <reaction-diffusion equation>
$$
v_t=Dv_{xx}+g(v),\qquad g(v)=f(v)+I-w_0.
$$
For this frozen-recovery reduction, let
$$
a_\pm=\frac{1+\alpha\pm\sqrt{1-\alpha+\alpha^2}}3.
$$
The <cubic polynomial> $f$ has a local minimum at $a_-$ and a local maximum at $a_+$. Hence the condition
$$
\boxed{w_0-f(a_+)<I<w_0-f(a_-)}
$$
gives three distinct real roots $r_1<r_2<r_3$ of $g$, with
$$
g(v)=-(v-r_1)(v-r_2)(v-r_3).
$$
The outer roots are stable for the scalar spatially uniform dynamics and the middle root is unstable. Seek a <travelling wave> $v(x,t)=V(z)$, where $z=x-ct$. The <travelling-wave reduction of a reaction-diffusion system> gives
$$
DV''+cV'-(V-r_1)(V-r_2)(V-r_3)=0.
$$
Let $\delta=r_3-r_1$ and choose
$$
V(z)=r_1+\frac{\delta}{1+\exp[\delta(z-z_0)/\sqrt{2D}]}.
$$
It has $V(-\infty)=r_3$, $V(+\infty)=r_1$ and
$$
V'=-\frac{(V-r_1)(r_3-V)}{\sqrt{2D}},\qquad
V''=\frac{(V-r_1)(r_3-V)(r_1+r_3-2V)}{2D}.
$$
Substitution leaves
$$
(V-r_1)(r_3-V)\left[\frac{r_1+r_3}{2}-r_2-\frac c{\sqrt{2D}}\right]=0.
$$
Consequently the <frozen-recovery cubic front> is
$$
\boxed{v(x,t)=r_1+\frac{r_3-r_1}{1+\exp\left[\frac{r_3-r_1}{\sqrt{2D}}(x-ct-z_0)\right]},\qquad
c=\sqrt{\frac D2}(r_1+r_3-2r_2).}
$$
Reflection $x\mapsto-x$ reverses the <front> and its speed. A nonzero speed requires $r_2\ne(r_1+r_3)/2$; equality gives a stationary <front> and is the <Maxwell balance for a scalar reaction-diffusion front>. Since the roots sum to $1+\alpha$, this stationary value is $I=w_0-f((1+\alpha)/3)$. For the simple admissible choice $I=w_0$, the roots are $0,\alpha,1$ and
$$
\boxed{V(z)=\frac1{1+e^{(z-z_0)/\sqrt{2D}}},\qquad c=\sqrt{\frac D2}(1-2\alpha).}
$$
This particular <front> moves when $\alpha\ne1/2$. At $\alpha=1/2$, other currents inside the displayed open interval give moving <fronts>.

The <bacteria> disperse by <diffusion> with coefficient $D_B$. Their additional <chemotaxis> <flux> is
$$
J_{B,\mathrm{chem}}=\frac{\chi_B B}{(N_0+N)^2}N_X.
$$
It points up the <nutrient> <concentration> <gradient>, and its negative spatial <derivative> enters the balance equation. The positive $N_0$ prevents singular sensitivity at zero <nutrient> <concentration>; increasing the background <nutrient> <concentration> reduces the drift response to a fixed <gradient>. The <logistic growth equation> term $rB(1-B/K)$ represents net reproduction with low-population growth rate $r$ and <carrying capacity> $K$.

The <nutrient> undergoes <diffusion> with coefficient $D_N$. Its production term $aB^2/(B_0^2+B^2)$ is an increasing, bounded response to the <bacteria>, reaching half its maximum $a$ at $B=B_0$. It has the algebraic form of a <Hill equation> with exponent two; the equation alone does not specify a molecular mechanism. The term $dBN$ removes <nutrient> at a rate proportional to both <bacteria> abundance and <nutrient> <concentration>.

The full <fluxes> are
$$
\boxed{J_B=-D_B B_X+\frac{\chi_BB}{(N_0+N)^2}N_X,\qquad J_N=-D_NN_X.}
$$
The homogeneous <Neumann boundary conditions> make both <fluxes> vanish at the endpoints, so neither <bacteria> nor <nutrient> crosses the boundaries. The <initial conditions> specify their starting spatial distributions. This model includes <nutrient> production by <bacteria>, rather than a separate external supply term.

Use $K$ as the population scale, $N_0$ as the <nutrient> <concentration> scale, $r^{-1}$ as the time scale, and $L$ as the length scale. The <nondimensionalization> is
$$
\boxed{B=Kb,\qquad N=N_0n,\qquad T=t/r,\qquad X=Lx.}
$$
For example, the <chemotaxis> term becomes
$$
-\chi_B\partial_X\left(\frac{B}{(N_0+N)^2}N_X\right)
=-\frac{\chi_BK}{N_0L^2}\partial_x\left(\frac b{(1+n)^2}n_x\right).
$$
Dividing the population equation by $rK$ and the <nutrient> equation by $rN_0$ gives the requested system with the following six <dimensionless quantities>:
$$
\boxed{d_b=\frac{D_B}{rL^2},\qquad d_n=\frac{D_N}{rL^2},\qquad
\chi_b=\frac{\chi_B}{rL^2N_0},\qquad
\lambda=\frac a{rN_0},\qquad b_0=\frac{B_0}{K},\qquad \mu=\frac{dK}{r}.}
$$
The rescaled domain is $0<x<1$, $t>0$. Its <boundary conditions> and <initial conditions> are
$$
\boxed{b_x(0,t)=b_x(1,t)=n_x(0,t)=n_x(1,t)=0,}
$$
$$
\boxed{b(x,0)=\frac{B_{\mathrm{initial}}(Lx)}K,\qquad
n(x,0)=\frac{N_{\mathrm{initial}}(Lx)}{N_0},\qquad 0\le x\le1.}
$$

For a spatially uniform <steady state> with nonzero <bacteria> abundance, $b(1-b)=0$ forces $b_*=1$. The <nutrient> balance then gives
$$
\boxed{(b_*,n_*)=\left(1,\frac{\lambda}{\mu(1+b_0^2)}\right).}
$$
To test spatially uniform <linear stability>, write
$$
F(b,n)=b(1-b),\qquad G(b,n)=\frac{\lambda b^2}{b_0^2+b^2}-\mu bn.
$$
At the positive <equilibrium>, the <Jacobian matrix> is
$$
J_0=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\\eta&-\mu\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\eta=\frac{2\lambda b_0^2}{(1+b_0^2)^2}-\mu n_*
=\frac{\lambda(b_0^2-1)}{(1+b_0^2)^2}.
$$
Its <eigenvalues> are $\boxed{-1,\ -\mu}$, both negative, so the positive <equilibrium> is asymptotically stable against spatially uniform perturbations. This remains true at $\mu=1$, where a repeated <eigenvalue> may produce a decaying $te^{-t}$ contribution. The printed <stability> claim must be understood in this spatially uniform sense: sufficiently strong <chemotaxis> can destabilize nonuniform perturbations, as the next answer shows.

Write $b=1+u$, $n=n_*+v$ and define
$$
\kappa=\frac{\chi_b}{(1+n_*)^2},\qquad
\eta=\frac{\lambda(b_0^2-1)}{(1+b_0^2)^2}.
$$
The first-order perturbation equations are
$$
u_t=d_bu_{xx}-\kappa v_{xx}-u,\qquad
v_t=d_nv_{xx}+\eta u-\mu v.
$$
The homogeneous <Neumann boundary conditions> select <Fourier modes> proportional to $\cos(m\pi x)$, with squared <wavenumber> $q_m=(m\pi)^2$ for $m=0,1,\ldots$. For a mode $e^{\sigma t}(U,V)\cos(m\pi x)$, the <eigenvalue> problem is
$$
\sigma\begin{pmatrix}U\\V\end{pmatrix}
=A(q_m)\begin{pmatrix}U\\V\end{pmatrix},\qquad
A(q)=\begin{pmatrix}-1-d_bq&\kappa q\\\eta&-\mu-d_nq\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <trace> is $-[1+\mu+(d_b+d_n)q]<0$. Therefore a growing mode exists exactly when its <determinant> is negative. Set
$$
C=\kappa\eta-d_n-\mu d_b.
$$
The <dispersion relation> is
$$
\sigma^2+[1+\mu+(d_b+d_n)q]\sigma+P(q)=0,\qquad
P(q)=d_bd_nq^2-Cq+\mu.
$$
The constant mode has $P(0)=\mu>0$; very short <wavelengths> also decay because $P(q)\sim d_bd_nq^2$. For a negative interval at positive $q$, the upward-opening <quadratic polynomial> must have its minimum below zero at positive $q$. This gives
$$
\boxed{\kappa\eta>d_n+\mu d_b+2\sqrt{\mu d_bd_n}
=\left(\sqrt{d_n}+\sqrt{\mu d_b}\right)^2.}
$$
In particular, $\boxed{b_0>1}$ is necessary, since otherwise $\eta\le0$ and every coefficient of $P(q)$ is positive. When $b_0>1$, a locally increased population must increase net <nutrient> production, so attraction to that <nutrient> reinforces the population increase. This feedback must overcome both <diffusion> and local relaxation. The calculation is an instance of <chemotactic instability with logistic growth and saturating production>.

The displayed continuous-<wavenumber> inequality is necessary on the finite domain, but an allowed mode must actually lie in the unstable band:
$$
q_\pm=\frac{C\pm\sqrt{C^2-4\mu d_bd_n}}{2d_bd_n},\qquad
\boxed{q_-<(m\pi)^2<q_+\quad\text{for some integer }m\ge1.}
$$
Equivalently, the exact discrete threshold is
$$
\boxed{\chi_b>\frac{(1+n_*)^2}{\eta}
\min_{m\ge1}\left[d_n+\mu d_b+d_bd_n(m\pi)^2+\frac{\mu}{(m\pi)^2}\right],\qquad \eta>0.}
$$
Equality gives a marginal zero <eigenvalue> rather than positive growth. At the unrestricted onset, $q_c=\sqrt{\mu/(d_bd_n)}$, so $k_c=(\mu/(d_bd_n))^{1/4}$. On the finite interval, <discrete Neumann mode selection> first destabilizes the mode minimizing the discrete threshold; it need not attain $k_c$ exactly.

To predict the early form of a perturbation, use the larger growth <eigenvalue>
$$
\boxed{\sigma_+(q)=-\frac{1+\mu+(d_b+d_n)q}{2}
+\frac12\sqrt{[\mu-1+(d_n-d_b)q]^2+4\kappa\eta q}.}
$$
For $\eta>0$, the square root is real. Choose $m_{\rm fast}$ maximizing $\sigma_+(q_m)$ among allowed modes with positive growth. The corresponding perturbation has <wavelength> $2/m_{\rm fast}$ in $x$, or $2L/m_{\rm fast}$ in $X$. Its <eigenvector> satisfies
$$
\frac VU=\frac{1+d_bq_m+\sigma_+(q_m)}{\kappa q_m}>0,
$$
so <bacteria> and <nutrient> peaks initially coincide. A mode with larger $m$ has finer spatial structure. The two signs of $\cos(m\pi x)$ are both allowed, exchanging peaks and troughs.

The illustration holds $d_b=0.002$, $d_n=0.04$, $\mu=0.5$, $b_0=2$ and $\lambda=2.5$ fixed, giving $n_*=1$ and $\eta=0.3$, and varies $\chi_b$. Crossing the discrete threshold makes a nonuniform <Fourier mode> grow; stronger <chemotaxis> widens the unstable band and can favor finer structure. Increasing either <diffusion> coefficient raises the threshold of each fixed mode. Varying $\lambda$ can have a nonmonotone effect: with $s=1+b_0^2$,
$$
\kappa\eta=\frac{\chi_b\lambda(b_0^2-1)}{s^2(1+\lambda/(\mu s))^2}
$$
is maximal at $\lambda=\mu s$, where $n_*=1$. Additional production beyond that point weakens sensitivity enough to reduce this feedback. Changes in $L$ also change the allowed dimensional <wavenumbers> $m\pi/L$ and all three scaled transport coefficients. These are predictions of <linear stability analysis>: eventual pattern amplitude, competition between growing modes and possible merging require the nonlinear system. The threshold is a necessary condition for spontaneous pattern growth from arbitrarily small perturbations, rather than a claim that every finite-amplitude nonuniform state is excluded below it.
