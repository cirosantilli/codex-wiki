Solutions to all three questions of the <topology and groups course of the University of Oxford> Part B examination, sat on 31 May 2023. Source: https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/request_for_mathematics_examinat/response/3129786/attach/3/FOI%20202507%20740%20Data.pdf#page=221[original released PDF, bundle pages 221–223; paper pages 1–3]. The Mathematics and Statistics version is labelled B3.4. All eighteen parts are treated below. Question 3(a)(iii), as printed, needs an additional hypothesis such as <local path connectedness>; its solution includes both the intended argument and a counterexample to the unrestricted assertion.

Throughout Question 1, $I=[0,1]$ has its usual <topology>, and the <mapping cylinder> uses the paper's convention that $(x,1)$ is attached to $f(x)$.

Two <continuous maps> $f_0,f_1:X\to Y$ are <homotopic> if there is a <continuous map>
$$
H:X\times I\longrightarrow Y,\qquad H(x,0)=f_0(x),\quad H(x,1)=f_1(x).
$$
Here the <Cartesian product> has the <product topology>. Such an $H$ is a <homotopy>, written $f_0\simeq f_1$.

The <topological spaces> $X,Y$ are <homotopy equivalent> if there are <continuous maps> $f:X\to Y$ and $g:Y\to X$ such that
$$
\boxed{gf\simeq\operatorname{id}_X,\qquad fg\simeq\operatorname{id}_Y.}
$$
Each is then a <homotopy inverse> of the other.

For $A\subseteq X$ with its <subspace topology> with <inclusion map> $i:A\hookrightarrow X$, a <homotopy retract>, in the course's convention, is a <continuous map> $r:X\to A$ with
$$
\boxed{ri=\operatorname{id}_A,\qquad ir\simeq\operatorname{id}_X.}
$$
Thus $r$ is an actual <retraction>, and $i,r$ give a <homotopy equivalence>. Requiring the latter <homotopy> to fix $A$ pointwise at all times gives the stronger condition of a <deformation retraction> as used in this wiki. The course's definition of <homotopy retract> requires no such restriction on that <homotopy>.

**Yes.** Take $X=S^1$, the <circle>, and $A=\{1\}\subset S^1$. The <constant function> $r(z)=1$ is a <continuous map> and satisfies $ri=\operatorname{id}_A$, so $A$ is a <retract> of $X$.

However, their <fundamental groups> are
$$
\pi_1(S^1,1)\cong\mathbb Z,\qquad \pi_1(\{1\},1)=1.
$$
A <homotopy equivalence> between <path-connected spaces> induces a <group isomorphism> of their <fundamental groups>, allowing the usual change of <base point>. The <infinite cyclic group> is not the <trivial group>. Therefore **a <retract> need not have the <homotopy type> of its ambient space**.

Let $i:A\hookrightarrow X$ be the <inclusion map> and $r:X\to A$ the <retraction>. Since $X$ is <contractible>, choose a <contraction of a topological space>
$$
H:X\times I\longrightarrow X,\qquad H(x,0)=x,\quad H(x,1)=x_0.
$$
The <function composition>
$$
K:A\times I\longrightarrow A,\qquad K(a,t)=r(H(i(a),t))
$$
is a <continuous map>. Its endpoints satisfy
$$
K(a,0)=ri(a)=a,\qquad K(a,1)=r(x_0).
$$
Consequently $K$ is a <homotopy> from the <identity map> of $A$ to a <constant function>. This proves
$$
\boxed{A\text{ is contractible}.}
$$
The contracting point of $X$ need not belong to $A$: applying the <retraction> sends it to the contracting point $r(x_0)$ of $A$. This is the <retract of a contractible space> principle.

Write
$$
q:(X\times I)\sqcup Y\longrightarrow M(f)
$$
for the <quotient map> defining the <mapping cylinder>, and put $j(x)=q(x,0)$. Since the <equivalence relation> only attaches points at height $1$ to $Y$, distinct points at height $0$ remain distinct. Thus $j$ is an <injective> <continuous map>.

Define the <homotopy>
$$
H(x,s)=q(x,1-s).
$$
It is a <continuous map>, with $H(x,0)=q(x,1)=i(f(x))$ and $H(x,1)=j(x)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{i\circ f\simeq j,\qquad j\text{ injective}.}
$$
Even if $f$ identifies many points, the free end of the <mapping cylinder> retains a separate copy of every point of $X$.

Define
$$
r:M(f)\longrightarrow Y,\qquad r(q(x,t))=f(x),\quad r(i(y))=y.
$$
The two formulas agree on the attached end, so the <universal property of the quotient topology> makes $r$ a <continuous map>. Also $ri=\operatorname{id}_Y$.

Slide each cylinder point towards its attached end using
$$
K(q(x,t),s)=q(x,t+s(1-t)),\qquad K(i(y),s)=i(y).
$$
At $t=1$ these formulas both give $i(f(x))$, so they define a <homotopy> on the <mapping cylinder>. For continuity, the <product of a quotient map with the unit interval> is a <quotient map>; the formulas before taking the <quotient topology> are <continuous maps> and agree on every identified pair. Thus
$$
K(-,0)=\operatorname{id}_{M(f)},\qquad K(-,1)=ir.
$$
Moreover $Y$ stays fixed throughout. We have obtained the stronger conclusion
$$
\boxed{M(f)\text{ deformation retracts onto }Y.}
$$
In particular $r$ is the requested <homotopy retract>.

Identify the bottom $j(X)=q(X\times\{0\})$ with $X$; its natural map $j$ is a <topological embedding>. To verify this directly, an <open subset> $O\subseteq X$ gives the saturated <open subset> $O\times[0,1/2)$ of the space before taking the <quotient topology>; its image is open in $M(f)$ and meets the bottom in $j(O)$.

Suppose first that there is a <retraction> $R:M(f)\to j(X)$. Define the <continuous map>
$$
g=j^{-1}Ri:Y\longrightarrow X.
$$
Then
$$
L(x,t)=j^{-1}R(q(x,t))
$$
is a <homotopy> with $L(x,0)=x$ and $L(x,1)=g(f(x))$. Hence $gf\simeq\operatorname{id}_X$.

Conversely, suppose a <continuous map> $g:Y\to X$ has $gf\simeq\operatorname{id}_X$. Reverse the parameter if necessary to choose a <homotopy> $L$ with $L(x,0)=x$ and $L(x,1)=gf(x)$. Set
$$
R(q(x,t))=j(L(x,t)),\qquad R(i(y))=j(g(y)).
$$
At the glued end, $j(L(x,1))=j(gf(x))$, exactly the value prescribed at $i(f(x))$. The <universal property of the quotient topology> therefore gives a <continuous map> $R:M(f)\to j(X)$. At the bottom, $R(j(x))=j(x)$, proving that $R$ is a <retraction>. We have established the <mapping-cylinder bottom retraction criterion>:
$$
\boxed{j(X)\text{ is a retract of }M(f)\iff\exists g:Y\to X\text{ continuous with }gf\simeq\operatorname{id}_X.}
$$

A <group presentation> of $G$ consists of a <set> $S$ of symbols and a <subset> $R\subseteq F(S)$ of <relators>, together with a <group isomorphism>
$$
\boxed{G\cong\langle S\mid R\rangle:=F(S)/\langle\!\langle R\rangle\!\rangle.}
$$
Here $F(S)$ is the <free group> on $S$, and $\langle\!\langle R\rangle\!\rangle$ is the <normal closure> of $R$. The images of $S$ form a <generating set of a group>; the <relators> prescribe which words become the <identity element>.

For the <canonical presentation of a group>, use one symbol $s_g$ for every <group element> $g\in G$. The <universal property of a free group> gives a <surjective> <group homomorphism>
$$
\varepsilon:F(\{s_g:g\in G\})\longrightarrow G,\qquad s_g\longmapsto g.
$$
Let $R(G)=\ker\varepsilon$, namely all words whose evaluation in $G$ is the <identity element>. Then
$$
\boxed{G\cong\langle s_g\ (g\in G)\mid R(G)\rangle.}
$$
Indeed $R(G)$ is already a <normal subgroup>, so its <normal closure> is itself, and the <first isomorphism theorem> identifies the resulting <quotient group> with $G$. This is the course's <canonical presentation of a group>.

An equivalent, smaller collection of <relators> is $s_gs_hs_{gh}^{-1}$ for all $g,h\in G$. These encode the multiplication table. They imply $s_1=1$ and $s_g^{-1}=s_{g^{-1}}$, so every word reduces to the single symbol recording its product. This proves directly that they normally generate $R(G)$, although the canonical collection above consists of every word in that <group homomorphism kernel>.

Take the <free product> $F=G_1*G_2$, with its natural <group homomorphisms> $k_j:G_j\to F$, and form the <normal closure>
$$
N=\left\langle\!\left\langle
k_1(\phi_1(h))\,k_2(\phi_2(h))^{-1}:h\in G_0
\right\rangle\!\right\rangle.
$$
The <pushout of groups> is the <quotient group>
$$
\boxed{P=(G_1*G_2)/N,}
$$
equipped with the <group homomorphisms> $j_1,j_2$ obtained by composing $k_1,k_2$ with the <quotient map of groups>. The defining <relators> ensure
$$
j_1\phi_1=j_2\phi_2.
$$
Thus the two prescribed images of every <group element> of $G_0$ agree in $P$. This construction works for arbitrary <group homomorphisms> $\phi_1,\phi_2$; no <injectivity> is assumed, and $j_1,j_2$ need not be <injective>. Its <universal property> is proved in the next part.

The <universal property of a pushout of groups> says: for every <group> $H$ and <group homomorphisms> $u_j:G_j\to H$ satisfying $u_1\phi_1=u_2\phi_2$, there is exactly one <group homomorphism> $u:P\to H$ such that
$$
\boxed{uj_1=u_1,\qquad uj_2=u_2.}
$$
To prove existence, the <universal property of a free product> supplies a unique <group homomorphism> $v:G_1*G_2\to H$ with $vk_j=u_j$. Every defining <relator> of $N$ maps to
$$
u_1(\phi_1(h))\,u_2(\phi_2(h))^{-1}=1.
$$
Since $\ker v$ is a <normal subgroup>, it contains the entire <normal closure> $N$. The <universal property of a quotient group> therefore gives a <group homomorphism> $u:P\to H$ through which $v$ factors. Its restrictions are the required $u_j$.

For uniqueness, $j_1(G_1)$ and $j_2(G_2)$ together generate $P$: their preimages already generate the <free product>. Consequently a <group homomorphism> out of $P$ is determined by its restrictions to these two <subgroups>. Any two candidates for $u$ therefore coincide.

This also proves that the <pushout of groups> is unique up to a unique compatible <group isomorphism>. Given another such object $P'$, apply the <universal property> in both directions. Both composites restrict to the original maps from $G_1,G_2$, so uniqueness makes them the respective <identity maps>.

Put $A=T^2\setminus B^2$ and let $E=\partial A=S^1$ denote the attaching <circle>. The version of the <Seifert-van Kampen theorem> used here states that if $X=U\cup V$ is an <open cover> with $U,V,U\cap V$ <path-connected> and a common <base point> in $U\cap V$, then
$$
\pi_1(X)\cong\pi_1(U)*_{\pi_1(U\cap V)}\pi_1(V).
$$
The expression on the right means the <pushout of groups> of the two inclusion-induced <group homomorphisms>; they need not be <injective>.

Choose a thin <collar neighbourhood> $C_A$ of $E$ inside $A$, and a thin equatorial band $C_S$ inside $S^2$. For example, before the square's side identifications take $C_A=\{v:1\leq|v|<1+\epsilon\}$ with $0<\epsilon<1/2$, and take $C_S=\{(x,y,z)\in S^2:|z|<\epsilon\}$. In the glued <topological space> set
$$
U=A\cup C_S,\qquad V=S^2\cup C_A.
$$
These are <open subsets>: their preimages in the two pieces are open and respect the gluing. They cover $X$, and the three <deformation retractions>
$$
U\simeq A,\qquad V\simeq S^2,\qquad U\cap V=C_A\cup_E C_S\simeq E
$$
fix $E$. Thus all three spaces are <path-connected>, and a <base point> $b\in E$ is preserved.

For completeness, the <fundamental group of a once-punctured torus> is $F(a,b)$, and its <boundary loop of a once-punctured torus> represents $[a,b]=aba^{-1}b^{-1}$, up to <orientation> and a change of <base point>. Here is the geometry behind both facts. In the square model, move each point of $A$ radially outwards to the square boundary:
$$
v\longmapsto\left((1-t)+\frac{2t}{\max(|v_1|,|v_2|)}\right)v,\qquad 0\leq t\leq1.
$$
The denominator never vanishes on $A$. The <homotopy> stays outside the missing <open disk>, stays inside the square, and fixes the square boundary, so it descends through the side identifications. The resulting <retract> is a <wedge of two circles>. Its <fundamental group> is the <free group> on the two oriented side <based loops> $a,b$. The inner <boundary loop> travels around the square once under this <deformation retraction>; its successive sides read $a,b,a^{-1},b^{-1}$.

The <sphere> $S^2$ is <simply connected>. Hence the <group homomorphism> from $\pi_1(E)\cong\mathbb Z$ to $\pi_1(V)$ is trivial, while its map to $\pi_1(U)$ sends a <group generator> to $[a,b]$. Applying the <Seifert-van Kampen theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\pi_1(X,b)\cong F(a,b)/\langle\!\langle[a,b]\rangle\!\rangle
=\langle a,b\mid aba^{-1}b^{-1}=1\rangle\cong\mathbb Z^2.}
$$
The final <group isomorphism> follows because the single <relator> makes $a,b$ commute, leaving the <free abelian group> on two <group generators>. The two hemispheres kill the same <boundary loop>; they do not create another <group generator>.

Let $n=(0,0,1)$ and use the <open cover>
$$
U=Y\setminus\{n\},\qquad V=\{(x,y,z)\in S^2:z>1/2\}.
$$
The cap $V$ is open in $Y$: it equals $Y\cap\{z>1/2\}$ because the attached chord has $z=0$. The cap is <homeomorphic> to an <open disk>, and $U\cap V=V\setminus\{n\}$ is a punctured <open disk>, hence <homotopy equivalent> to a <circle>. Both sets and their <intersection> are <path-connected>. Choose the <base point> $b$ in that <intersection>.

We first calculate $\pi_1(U,b)$. A <stereographic projection> identifies $S^2\setminus\{n\}$ with $\mathbb R^2$ and sends the two chord endpoints to $(-1,0),(1,0)$. Thus $U$ becomes the <Euclidean plane> with an extra <closed interval> attached at those two points. This description is a <homeomorphism>: gluing the two closed pieces along their endpoints gives exactly the original <subspace topology>.

The <Euclidean plane> has a <deformation retraction> onto $L=[-1,1]\times\{0\}$ while fixing its endpoints. An explicit <homotopy> is
$$
D((u,v),t)=\big((1-t)u+t\,c(u),(1-t)v\big),\qquad
c(u)=\max(-1,\min(1,u)).
$$
Extend it by the <identity map> on the extra chord. The <pasting lemma for closed subspaces> gives a <deformation retraction> of $U$ onto two distinct <closed intervals> sharing just their endpoints, a <circle>. Consequently $\pi_1(U,b)\cong\mathbb Z$, with the routine <base point> transport if the <retraction> moves $b$.

The inclusion-induced <group homomorphism> $\pi_1(U\cap V,b)\to\pi_1(U,b)$ is trivial: it factors through $S^2\setminus\{n\}\cong\mathbb R^2$, which is <contractible>. Also $\pi_1(V,b)=1$. All hypotheses of the <Seifert-van Kampen theorem> are now checked, and it gives the <fundamental group of a sphere with an attached chord>:
$$
\boxed{\pi_1(Y,b)\cong\mathbb Z*_{\mathbb Z}1\cong\mathbb Z,}
$$
where both maps from the amalgamating <infinite cyclic group> are trivial. A <group generator> follows the chord from one endpoint to the other and returns along any <path> on the <sphere>, with a connecting <path> from the chosen <base point>.

A <covering map> is a <continuous> <surjection> $p:\widetilde X\to X$ such that every $x\in X$ has an <open> <neighbourhood> $U$ for which
$$
\boxed{p^{-1}(U)=\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in A}U_\alpha,\qquad
p|_{U_\alpha}:U_\alpha\longrightarrow U\text{ a homeomorphism},}
$$
where the $U_\alpha$ are pairwise disjoint <open subsets> of $\widetilde X$. Such a $U$ is an <evenly covered neighbourhood>, and the sets $U_\alpha$ are its <covering sheets>. The index <set> may be infinite. The requirement that every sheet map onto all of $U$ is part of the definition.

For a <covering map> over a nonempty <connected space>, the <degree of a covering map> is
$$
\boxed{\deg p=|p^{-1}(x)|,}
$$
the <cardinality> of any <fibre of a function>. To show independence of $x$, choose an <evenly covered neighbourhood> $U$. Each <covering sheet> contains exactly one point over every $y\in U$, so $|p^{-1}(y)|$ equals the number of <covering sheets> over $U$. The <fibre of a function> cardinality is therefore <locally constant>. For a fixed <cardinal number> $\kappa$, the points with that cardinality form an <open subset>, and their complement is also open. <Connectedness> forces the value to be constant everywhere.

For a <path-connected> base there is also an explicit <fibre bijection by path lifting>: lift a <path> from $x$ to $y$ starting at each point over $x$ and take its endpoint. The <path lifting theorem> gives existence and uniqueness; <path reversal> gives the inverse <bijection>. The bijection may depend on the <path>, but the resulting equality of <cardinalities> does not.

**A <connected> base is needed for one global degree.** Without it, use a degree on each <connected component> on which the cardinality is constant. For example, a <covering map> of a discrete two-point <topological space> can have one point over the first point and two over the second. Thus the unqualified printed definition needs the usual connected-base convention, or the explicit assumption that every <fibre of a function> has the same cardinality.

**With <local path connectedness>, the intended conclusion holds.** Choose a <base point> $x_0$ and the standard <covering map>
$$
p:\mathbb R\longrightarrow S^1,\qquad p(t)=e^{2\pi it}.
$$
The <fundamental group> of the <circle> is $\mathbb Z$. Since the image of a <finite group> under a <group homomorphism> is finite and $\mathbb Z$ has no nontrivial finite <subgroups>,
$$
f_*:\pi_1(X,x_0)\longrightarrow\pi_1(S^1,f(x_0))\cong\mathbb Z
$$
is trivial. If $X$ is also <locally path-connected>, the <lifting criterion for a covering space> supplies a <continuous map> $\widetilde f:X\to\mathbb R$ with $p\widetilde f=f$, because $p_*\pi_1(\mathbb R)=0$. The <homotopy>
$$
\boxed{H(x,s)=p\big((1-s)\widetilde f(x)+s\widetilde f(x_0)\big)}
$$
runs from $f$ to the <constant function> $f(x_0)$. This proves the claimed <null-homotopy> under that additional hypothesis, in particular for <CW complexes> and the <topological graphs> considered later in the paper.

**For arbitrary <path-connected spaces>, the printed assertion is false.** Here is an explicit <Warsaw circle> counterexample, including the obstruction that the usual lifting argument misses. In the <Euclidean plane> take
$$
G=\{(x,\sin(\pi/x)):0<x\leq1\},\qquad
L=\{0\}\times[-1,1].
$$
Join $P=(1,0)$ to $Q=(0,-1)$ by the polygonal <arc> $A$ passing successively through $(2,0),(2,-2),(0,-2)$, and put $W=G\cup L\cup A$ with its <subspace topology>. The <arc> meets $G\cup L$ only at $P,Q$. The <topologist's sine curve> $G\cup L$ has two <path components>, but the added <arc> joins them, making $W$ <path-connected>.

Every <loop (topology)> in $W$ is contained in
$$
T_\delta=L\cup A\cup\{(x,\sin(\pi/x)):\delta\leq x\leq1\}
$$
for some $\delta>0$. To justify the assertion, a <path> cannot reach $L$ along the oscillating tail: the <intermediate value theorem> for its $x$-coordinate would force its $y$-coordinate to attain both $1$ and $-1$ arbitrarily close to the supposed arrival time, contradicting <continuity>. The only alternative route from the tail to $L$ uses $P$ and the joining <arc>. If a <loop (topology)> visited tail points with $x\to0$, a convergent subsequence of their parameter values would approach a time at which the <loop (topology)> lies in $L$. On a sufficiently small parameter <interval> around that time its image avoids $P$. That would give a forbidden <path> from $L$ into the tail without passing through $P$. Thus those tail coordinates have a positive lower bound. The space $T_\delta$ is a finite <tree>, in fact a single <arc>, so every such <loop (topology)> contracts. Consequently
$$
\pi_1(W)=1.
$$
Now define a <continuous map> $h:W\to S^1$ to be $1$ on $G\cup L$, and to make one full turn on $A$: for a parametrization $a:[0,1]\to A$ from $P$ to $Q$, set $h(a(t))=e^{2\pi it}$. The pieces $G\cup L$ and $A$ are closed in $W$, and the formulas agree at $P,Q$, so the <pasting lemma for closed subspaces> proves <continuity>.

There is no <continuous lift> $\widetilde h:W\to\mathbb R$. Any such lift is <integer>-valued on $G\cup L$, so it is constant on each of the <connected spaces> $G,L$. Since $G$ accumulates on $L$, <continuity> forces those two constants to be the same. But along $A$ a lift of $e^{2\pi it}$ has the form $t+k$, giving values at $Q$ and $P$ differing by $1$, a contradiction. Finally, if $h$ had a <null-homotopy>, the <homotopy lifting theorem for a covering map>, starting with a lift of the final <constant function> and reversing time, would produce a <continuous lift> of $h$. This theorem needs no <local path connectedness> of the parameter space. Hence $h$ is not <null-homotopic>, although $W$ has finite, indeed trivial, <fundamental group>.

The native PDF explicitly assumes only <path connectedness> here. The missing <local path connectedness> cannot be inferred from the finiteness of the <fundamental group>.

A finite <tree> is a nonempty <connected graph> with no <graph cycles>. We show by <mathematical induction> on its number $v$ of <vertices> that it has $v-1$ <edges>. For $v=1$ there are no <edges>, so the assertion holds.

If $v>1$, take a <path in a graph> of maximal length among those without repeated <vertices>. Each endpoint is a <leaf of a tree>: an additional incident <edge> would either extend the <path in a graph> or create a <graph cycle>. Delete one such <leaf of a tree> and its unique incident <edge>. The remaining <graph> stays <connected>, since a <path in a graph> between remaining <vertices> never needs that terminal <edge>, and it has no <graph cycles>. It is therefore a <tree> with $v-1$ <vertices> and, by <mathematical induction>, $v-2$ <edges>. Restoring the deleted <edge> gives $e=v-1$.

Thus its <Euler characteristic> is
$$
\boxed{\chi(X)=v-e=v-(v-1)=1.}
$$

Choose a <spanning tree> $T$ of the finite <connected graph> $X$. It contains every <vertex> of $X$ and has $v(X)-1$ <edges> by part (i). Collapsing $T$ to a point is a <homotopy equivalence>: $T$ is a <contractible> <subcomplex>, and <collapsing a contractible cofibration> in a <CW complex> preserves <homotopy type>.

Every <edge> outside $T$ then becomes a <circle> attached to the common point. Hence the <quotient space> $X/T$ is a <bouquet of circles> with
$$
r=e(X)-e(T)=e(X)-v(X)+1
$$
petals. The <fundamental group of a bouquet of circles> is the <free group> with one member of a <free basis of a group> for each petal. Therefore the <rank of the fundamental group of a finite connected graph> is
$$
\boxed{\pi_1(X)\cong F_{\,1-\chi(X)},\qquad r=1-\chi(X).}
$$
More concretely, choose a <vertex> as <base point>; follow the unique <path in a graph> in $T$ to one end of a non-tree <edge>, traverse it, and return through $T$. These <based loops> give the <free basis of a group>. Thus $r$ is the number of free <group generators>, equivalently the minimum size of a <generating set of a group>, rather than the size of every possible redundant <generating set of a group>. Minimality also follows by <abelianization>, since $F_r$ has <abelianization> $\mathbb Z^r$.

{c}

Assume $p$ has a common finite <degree of a covering map> $d$. Declare the lifted <vertices> to be $p^{-1}(V(X))$, so there are exactly $dv(X)$ of them. Each open <edge> is <homeomorphic> to $(0,1)$, a <simply connected> <interval>. The restriction of the <covering map> above it is a disjoint union of $d$ copies of that open <edge>.

These copies acquire endpoints as follows. Lift the parametrizing <path> $[0,1]\to X$ of each oriented <edge>, starting at each of the $d$ points over its initial <vertex>. The <path lifting theorem> gives a unique lift all the way to $1$. Its endpoint lies over the terminal <vertex>, and the interiors of these lifted <paths> are exactly the $d$ open <edges> already described. This also handles a <graph loop>, whose two endpoints may coincide downstairs or upstairs. Local <covering sheets> above a small star at a <vertex> show that the lifted incidence relations have precisely the original <topology>. Thus the <graph structure lifted through a covering map> is a finite <topological graph> with
$$
v(\widetilde X)=dv(X),\qquad e(\widetilde X)=de(X).
$$
Subtracting gives the <Euler characteristic under a finite covering> formula
$$
\boxed{\chi(\widetilde X)=d\,\chi(X).}
$$
The formula holds even when $\widetilde X$ is disconnected. If a disconnected base is allowed to have degrees $d_C$ that vary with its <connected components> $C$, the precise replacement is
$$
\chi(\widetilde X)=\sum_C d_C\chi(C).
$$
The printed formula with a single $d$ uses the constant-degree convention explained in part (a)(ii).

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

Use a <topological graph> consisting of the ray $[0,\infty)$ with <vertices> $v_n=n$ and one oriented <graph loop> $\ell_n$ attached at each $v_n$, for $n=0,1,2,\ldots$. Set the <base point> to $b=v_0$. This is a <connected>, <locally finite graph>; it is deliberately infinite.

Define <continuous maps> $f,g:X\to X$ by
$$
\begin{aligned}
f(t)&=t+1&&\text{on the ray},&
f(\ell_n(s))&=\ell_{n+1}(s),\\
g(t)&=\max(t-1,0)&&\text{on the ray},&
g(\ell_n(s))&=\begin{cases}b,&n=0,\\\ell_{n-1}(s),&n\geq1.\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$
Here each <graph loop> is parametrized by $s\in[0,1]$ with its endpoints at the attaching <vertex>. The formulas agree at every <vertex>, and their restrictions to every closed <edge> are <continuous>. The <weak topology of a CW complex> therefore makes $f,g$ <continuous maps>. Directly on the ray and on each <graph loop>,
$$
\boxed{gf=\operatorname{id}_X.}
$$

We check the two <fundamental group> maps at exactly the specified <base point>s. The ray is a <spanning tree>. At $b=v_0$ a <free basis of a group> consists of $a_n$, obtained by going along the ray to $v_n$, following $\ell_n$, and returning. At $f(b)=v_1$, use the corresponding <free basis of a group> $c_n$ with connecting <paths> starting at $v_1$; in particular $c_0$ travels backwards along the first <edge> before following $\ell_0$. The standard <spanning tree> description applies to this infinite <topological graph> as well: every <loop (topology)> and every <homotopy> between <based loops> has <compact> <image>, so lies in a finite <subcomplex>.

The induced <group homomorphisms> are
$$
\boxed{f_*(a_n)=c_{n+1},\qquad
g_*(c_0)=1,\quad g_*(c_n)=a_{n-1}\ (n\geq1).}
$$
Thus $f_*$ misses $c_0$: words in $c_1,c_2,\ldots$ cannot equal $c_0$ by the <normal form for free groups and free product of groups>. Consequently $f_*$ is not <surjective>. Meanwhile $c_0$ is a nontrivial <free group> element killed by $g_*$, so $g_*$ is not <injective>. **Neither induced map is a <group isomorphism>**, while <functoriality of the fundamental group> correctly gives $g_*f_*=\operatorname{id}$.
