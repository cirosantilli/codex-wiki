= Electrochemistry

<Electrochemistry> studies how <chemical reactions>, transport, and <chemical equilibrium> couple to <electric charge> and <electric potential>. In a cell, <electrons> travel through the external circuit, while mobile <ions> carry <electric current> through the <electrolyte>. At the <electrodes>, <redox> reactions connect these two forms of <electric charge> transport. This connection makes it possible both to obtain electrical <work> from a <chemical reaction> and to use electrical <work> to drive a <chemical reaction>.

An <anode> is an <electrode> where <oxidation> occurs; a <cathode> is an <electrode> where <reduction> occurs. These definitions refer to the <chemical reactions>, rather than universal positive or negative terminal signs. In a <galvanic cell> delivering electrical <work>, the <anode> is negative and the <cathode> positive. In an <electrolytic cell> driven by an external source, the <anode> is positive and the <cathode> negative. See https://doi.org/10.1515/pac-2018-0109[Pingarrón et al., Terminology of electrochemical methods of analysis (IUPAC Recommendations 2019), §§3.1 and 3.4] and https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/17-2-galvanic-cells[Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, §17.2: galvanic cells].

For a worked example, consider an <electrolytic cell> containing molten <sodium chloride>, with the <anode> connected to the positive terminal and the <cathode> to the negative terminal of an external source. At the <cathode>, <sodium> <cations> gain <electrons>; at the <anode>, <chlorine> <anions> lose <electrons>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{cathode, reduction:}\quad&2\mathrm{Na}^++2e^-\longrightarrow2\mathrm{Na},\\
\text{anode, oxidation:}\quad&2\mathrm{Cl}^-\longrightarrow\mathrm{Cl}_2+2e^-,\\
\text{overall:}\quad&2\mathrm{Na}^++2\mathrm{Cl}^-\longrightarrow2\mathrm{Na}+\mathrm{Cl}_2.
\end{aligned}
$$
The first line has net <electric charge> zero on each side; the second has $-2e$ on each side. Adding the lines cancels the transferred <electrons> and preserves both <chemical atom> counts and net <electric charge>. The <sodium> product is neutral, and the <chlorine> product is a diatomic <molecule>. These equations specify molten <sodium chloride>; aqueous <chemical solutions> introduce competing <water> reactions. See <Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/17.7 Electrolysis> (§17.7: electrolysis of molten sodium chloride).

<Electrochemistry> underlies <battery> charging and the manufacture of chemicals by <electrolysis>. Its particularly useful idea is that an <electron> inventory in a balanced <chemical equation> also accounts for transported <electric charge>. Transport alone is a different question from an <electrode> <chemical reaction>: an <ion> may carry <electric current> through the <electrolyte> without itself being consumed at an <electrode>.

Historically, Michael Faraday introduced the terms <electrode>, <electrolyte>, <anion>, <cation>, and <ion> in the terminology section of his 1834 paper, <Experimental Researches in Electricity, Seventh Series by Michael Faraday> (§§662–667). His §667 explicitly treats the direction assigned to <electric current> as a convention. His <ion> language predates the modern <electron> and <atomic nucleus> picture used in the charge-counting example above.

For an <electrolyte> near <chemical equilibrium>, a <Poisson-Boltzmann equation> can approximate the distribution of <ions> in an <electric potential>. Such an equilibrium model does not by itself describe a cell carrying sustained <electric current>.

= Cerium dioxide electrodeposition
{parent=Electrochemistry}

Electrochemical growth of cerium dioxide from dissolved cerium ions. <Oxidation> of cerium(III) to the solid oxide transfers one <electron> per cerium; <proton> activity affects the potential strongly. A poorly conducting film can slow further growth.

= Galvanic replacement

Coupled dissolution of a less noble solid metal and <reduction>/deposition of a more noble metal <ion> without an external <power> supply.

Transfer of an electron between chemical species or between a species and an electrode. Its rate depends on driving force, electronic coupling and nuclear/solvent reorganization.

<Electron transfer> through a ligand shared by two metal centres. Bridge formation, <electron transfer> and bridge cleavage can leave transferred-ligand connectivity in a substitution-inert product.

An abundant electrolyte used to carry solution current and suppress migration of the dilute electroactive species.

= Cyclic voltammetry
{parent=Electrochemistry}

An electrochemical method that reverses a potential sweep and records the current response. Peak positions and return waves probe electron-transfer kinetics and coupled chemistry.

= ECEC mechanism
{parent=Cyclic voltammetry}
{c}

A sequence of <electron transfer>, chemical reaction, <electron transfer> and chemical reaction. Binding reactions can shift reduction potentials and separate reduction from product-release waves.

= Rotating disk electrode
{parent=Electrochemistry}

An electrode whose controlled rotation establishes a steady hydrodynamic boundary layer. In its diffusion-limited regime, current varies with the square root of angular speed.

= Passivation
{parent=Electrochemistry}

Suppression of further interfacial reaction by a protective surface layer. A passive film can also impede useful charge transfer; its role depends on the electrode and operating conditions.

= Oxide-film transport limitation
{parent=Passivation}

Slowing of electrochemical film growth as the deposited oxide restricts <electron> or ion transport. Increasing thickness increases the transport barrier even while dissolved precursor remains available.

= Fuel cell
{parent=Electrochemistry}

An electrochemical energy converter supplied continuously with fuel and oxidant; operating voltage and current depend on kinetics and transport as well as equilibrium potential.

= Electrochemical polarization
{parent=Electrochemistry}

Displacement of electrode or cell voltage from equilibrium under current flow. Kinetic, transport and resistive contributions should be separated.

= Butler-Volmer equation
{parent=Electrochemical polarization}
{c}
{title2=$j=j_0(e^{\alpha_anF\eta/(RT)}-e^{-\alpha_cnF\eta/(RT)})$}

A kinetic model for the net <electrode> current near and away from equilibrium. For a single effective reaction with consistent activities and $\alpha_a+\alpha_c=1$, $j=j_0[\exp(\alpha_anF\eta/RT)-\exp(-\alpha_cnF\eta/RT)]$. Near equilibrium it gives $j\simeq j_0nF\eta/(RT)$; at sufficiently large <overpotential> one exponential gives the <Tafel equation>. Multistep reactions can require a different kinetic interpretation.

= Limiting current density
{parent=Electrochemical polarization}
{title2=$j_L$}

A limiting rate of charge transfer per area imposed by reactant transport or another bottleneck. In a simple <diffusion> film, $j_L=nFDc_b/\delta$ and a consuming <electrode> has $c_s/c_b=1-j/j_L$. Increasing internal roughness does not necessarily increase external mass-transfer flux proportionately.

= Exchange current density
{parent=Electrochemical polarization}
{title2=$j_0$}

The equal magnitudes of the opposing partial electrode-reaction <current densities> at equilibrium. Their net current is zero. The area convention, activities and <temperature> must be specified; an <exchange current density> alone does not specify a nonlinear current-potential curve.

= Tafel equation
{parent=Electrochemical polarization}
{c}
{title2=$\eta=b\log_{10}(j/j_0)$}

An activation-controlled large-overpotential relation $|\eta|=b\log_{10}(|j|/j_0)$. The <Tafel slope> $b$ uses volts per decade when the logarithm is base ten. Exchange <current density> and operating <current density> must use the same area convention.

= Tafel slope
{c}
{parent=Tafel equation}
{title2=$b$}

The change in <overpotential> per decade of <current density> in the activation-controlled regime of a <Tafel equation>. In a single-step <Butler-Volmer equation> with constant transfer coefficient, the anodic slope is $b_a=RT\ln10/(\alpha_a nF)$, where $T$ is <temperature>, $R$ is the <molar gas constant>, $n$ is the number of transferred <electrons> and $F$ is the <Faraday constant>. The magnitude of the cathodic slope is $b_c=RT\ln10/(\alpha_c nF)$. Inferring one from the other requires the model's relation between the two coefficients; the equality $\alpha_a+\alpha_c=1$ applies to this effective single-step model and need not describe a multistep reaction.

= Phase-transformation polarization
{parent=Electrochemical polarization}

Additional voltage needed to nucleate or grow a new phase, or overcome a phase-transformation barrier. It can cause an initial voltage dip and voltage hysteresis.

= Ohmic polarization
{parent=Electrochemical polarization}

A voltage loss from ionic and electronic resistance, ideally $IR$.

= Concentration overpotential
{parent=Electrochemical polarization}

A voltage loss from differences between interfacial and bulk reactant or product activities caused by finite mass transport.

= Activation overpotential
{parent=Electrochemical polarization}

A voltage loss associated with finite charge-transfer reaction kinetics.

= Nernst equation
{parent=Electrochemistry}
{c}
{title2=$E=E^\circ-(RT/nF)\ln Q$}

For a cell reaction transferring $n$ electrons, $E=E^\circ-(RT/nF)\ln Q$, with dimensionless <reaction quotient> $Q$ and consistently chosen <standard states>.

= Electrode potential
{parent=Electrochemistry}

An electrode’s potential relative to a specified reference, conventionally tabulated for its reduction <half-reaction>.

= Overpotential
{parent=Electrode potential}

The difference between an applied electrode potential and the relevant equilibrium potential. Increasing its magnitude can drive <electron transfer> toward a mass-transport limit.

= Midpoint potential
{parent=Electrode potential}
{title2=$E_m$}

A potential characterizing equal oxidized and reduced activities under stated conditions. For a reversible cyclic voltammogram, $(E_{pa}+E_{pc})/2$ estimates its formal potential.

= Redox mediator
{parent=Electrochemistry}

A <redox mediator> transfers reducing equivalents between components through changes in oxidation state. A current-enhancing additive is not necessarily a mediator unless its redox participation and regeneration are established.

= Biophotovoltaic device
{parent=Electrochemistry}

A <biophotovoltaic device> couples photosynthetic biological activity to an external electrical circuit. Measured current depends on intracellular electron supply, export, electrode reactions and abiotic background.

= Electrode
{parent=Electrochemistry}
{wiki}

An <electrode> is an electronic <electrical conductor> in contact with an <electrolyte>. In a cell carrying sustained <redox> <electric current>, <chemical reactions> at this interface exchange <electrons> with <chemical species>. An <electrode> can participate in the <chemical reaction>, as the dissolving <zinc> <anode> of a <Daniell cell> does, or provide a conducting surface without appearing in the net <chemical equation>. This distinction is illustrated by active and inert <electrodes> in https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/17-2-galvanic-cells[Flowers, Theopold, Langley and Robinson, Chemistry 2e, §17.2].

The interface connects two kinds of <electric charge> transport: <electrons> in the external <electrical circuit> and <ions> in the <electrolyte>. The same <electron> does not travel through the wire, swim through the <salt bridge>, and complete a circuit! The <half-reactions> account for how electronic and ionic transport meet. This is the useful idea behind both obtaining electrical <work> in a <galvanic cell> and supplying electrical <work> in an <electrolytic cell>.

Name an <electrode> from its net <chemical reaction>: <oxidation> means <anode>, and <reduction> means <cathode>. State the operating mode before assigning terminal signs. For a <galvanic cell> supplying a passive load, the <anode> is negative and the <cathode> positive; for an ordinary two-terminal <electrolytic cell> driven by a source, the <anode> is positive and the <cathode> negative. Here positive means higher terminal <electric potential> than the other <electrode>, not a claim that the bulk material has a large positive net <electric charge>. See https://doi.org/10.1515/pac-2018-0109[Pingarrón et al., Terminology of electrochemical methods of analysis (IUPAC Recommendations 2019), §§3.1 and 3.4].

= Electrode current collector
{parent=Electrode}

An electronically conducting support connecting active material to an external circuit.

= Anode
{parent=Electrode}
{wiki}

The <anode> is the <electrode> at which the net <electrode> <chemical reaction> is <oxidation>: the reacting <chemical species> loses <electrons>. This definition is independent of terminal sign. See https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/A00370[IUPAC Gold Book: anode].

During <Daniell cell> discharge, a <zinc> <chemical atom> becomes a $\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}$ <cation>, leaving two <electrons> in the <electrode>; those <electrons> leave through the external <electrical circuit>. The <zinc> <anode> is the negative terminal. In an externally driven <electrolytic cell>, <electrons> instead leave the <anode> through the lead to the source's positive terminal. <Conventional current> in that lead points toward the <anode>, opposite to <electron> motion. The <anode> can therefore be positive while still being the site of <oxidation>. See <Daniell cell> for the balanced <half-reaction> and <electrode> for the operating-mode sign conventions.

= Cathode
{parent=Electrode}
{wiki}

The <cathode> is the <electrode> at which the net <electrode> <chemical reaction> is <reduction>: the reacting <chemical species> gains <electrons>. This definition is independent of terminal sign. During <Daniell cell> discharge, $\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}$ <cations> accept <electrons> arriving at the <copper> <cathode> and become neutral <copper>. The <cathode> is then the positive terminal. In an externally driven <electrolytic cell>, the source's negative terminal supplies <electrons> to the negative <cathode>. See https://doi.org/10.1515/pac-2018-0109[Pingarrón et al., §3.4: cathode] and <Daniell cell>.

An <ion> carrying <electric current> need not undergo <reduction> at the <cathode>. For example, in the ideal <Daniell cell> charge-balance picture, <potassium> <cations> arriving from a <salt bridge> remain dissolved while $\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}$ <cations> are consumed. Follow the <half-reaction> to identify the reacting species; follow <electric charge conservation> to account for the other moving <ions>.

= Galvanic cell
{parent=Electrochemistry}
{wiki}

A <galvanic cell> supplies electrical <work> from a spontaneous <redox> <chemical reaction> under the specified conditions. In discharge through a passive load, <oxidation> occurs at its negative <anode> and <reduction> at its positive <cathode>. Separating the reacting compartments makes the <electrons> take an external route, where their transport can deliver useful <work>. See https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/17-2-galvanic-cells[Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, §17.2: separated reactions and electrical work].

{title=<Daniell cell> discharge: <electron> flow outside and <ion> transport inside}
{description=Original schematic of a <zinc>/<copper> <galvanic cell> connected to a passive load. A chosen <potassium> <nitrate> <salt bridge> supplies compensating <anions> to the <anode> compartment and <cations> to the <cathode> compartment. The arrows represent charge-balance roles, not measured trajectories or relative fluxes. The bottom inventory is an ideal accounting example for one <mole> of net cell reaction.}

In the metallic external <electrical circuit>, <electrons> move from <anode> to <cathode>, while <conventional current> goes from <cathode> through the load to <anode>. Inside the cell, net ionic <conventional current> closes the loop from the <anode> compartment toward the <cathode> compartment. Oppositely moving <cations> and <anions> can both contribute to that direction; see <electrolyte> for the signed <current density> calculation. A cell can maintain a nonzero terminal <electric potential difference> near open circuit while delivering almost no <power>; the <Daniell cell voltage and load experiment> makes this distinction concrete.

= Concentration cell
{parent=Galvanic cell}

A <galvanic cell> whose reversible <electromotive force> arises from different reactant activities at otherwise corresponding interfaces. The <Nernst equation> relates the measured <voltage> to the activity ratio, provided the transported charge and reaction stoichiometry are specified.

= Oxygen concentration cell
{parent=Concentration cell}

A cell comparing <oxygen> <chemical potentials> across an oxygen-ion-conducting <electrolyte>. With oxide-ion conduction and four <electrons> transferred per <oxygen> molecule, $E=RT\ln(p_2/p_1)/(4F)$ for consistently ordered terminals. Reference <pressure>, interface equilibrium and electronic leakage must be controlled.

= Standard cell potential
{parent=Galvanic cell}
{title2=$E^\circ$}

The reversible cell voltage with reactants and products in specified <standard states>. It is the cathode reduction potential minus the anode reduction potential.

= Daniell cell
{c}
{parent=Galvanic cell}
{wiki}

A <Daniell cell> uses the <zinc>/$\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}$ and $\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}$/<copper> pairs. In the separated-compartment teaching version, a <zinc> <electrode> contacts an aqueous <chemical solution> containing $\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}$, and a <copper> <electrode> contacts one containing $\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}$. A <salt bridge> supplies an ionic connection; a wire and load supply an electronic connection. During discharge,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{anode, oxidation:}\quad&\mathrm{Zn}(s)\longrightarrow\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}(aq)+2e^-,\\
\text{cathode, reduction:}\quad&\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}(aq)+2e^-\longrightarrow\mathrm{Cu}(s),\\
\text{net:}\quad&\mathrm{Zn}(s)+\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}(aq)\longrightarrow\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}(aq)+\mathrm{Cu}(s).
\end{aligned}
$$
The <zinc> <electrode> loses material and <copper> is deposited at the <cathode>. Each <half-reaction> conserves <chemical atom> counts and <electric charge>: the first has net charge zero on each side, the second $+2e-2e=0$ on the left and zero on the right. The net reaction has $+2e$ on both sides, with no <electrons> left in its <chemical equation>. The Zn/Cu teaching cell and these <half-reactions> are described in https://www.chem.fsu.edu/chemlab/chm1lab/redox/background.html[Florida State University, Redox Reactions in Voltaic Cells: Construction of a Potential Series, lab background].

Creating one <mole> of $\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}$ adds $2F$ of positive <electric charge> to the <anode> compartment's ionic inventory; consuming one <mole> of $\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}$ removes $2F$ of positive <electric charge> from the <cathode> compartment. Here $F$ is the <Faraday constant>. In an ideal bookkeeping example using a <potassium> <nitrate> <salt bridge>, two <moles> of <nitrate> <anions> entering the <anode> compartment compensate the first change, and two <moles> of <potassium> <cations> entering the <cathode> compartment compensate the second. Other <ions> in the cell can share the actual transport. The two compensating inventory changes do not mean that $4F$ flowed through the load: the same series <electric current> transports $2F$ through each cross-section of the circuit per <mole> of net reaction. See <salt bridge>.

Under standard-state conditions near $298\,\mathrm K$, tabulated reduction potentials give a cell <electromotive force> of approximately $1.10\,\mathrm V$. This is a reversible reference value, not a promised loaded reading; actual composition matters through <thermodynamic activity>. The tabulated <copper> and <zinc> values and their subtraction are given in https://chemed.chem.purdue.edu/genchem/topicreview/bp/ch20/electro.php[Purdue University, Electrochemical Reactions: standard-state reduction potentials]. For the net <chemical equation> as written, the molar <Gibbs free energy> change obeys
$$
\Delta_r G=-2F\mathcal E.
$$
Thus $\mathcal E=1.10\,\mathrm V$ corresponds to about $-212\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ of net reaction. At fixed <temperature> and <pressure>, $-\Delta_r G$ is the maximum reversible electrical <work> per <mole> for this cell reaction. It is not automatically all the <heat> released by a finite-current cell. See <Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/17.4 Potential, Free Energy, and Equilibrium> (§17.4: cell potential and free energy).

= Daniell cell voltage and load experiment
{c}
{parent=Daniell cell}

The motivating observation is that a <chemical reaction> can produce a signed electrical reading even when its reacting materials occupy separate vessels. UC San Diego's teaching demonstration places a <copper> strip in $0.1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ $\mathrm{CuSO_4}$ and a <zinc> strip in $0.1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ $\mathrm{ZnSO_4}$, using two $400\,\mathrm{mL}$ beakers with about $300\,\mathrm{mL}$ of <chemical solution> in each. The strips connect to a voltage meter. Initially there is no ionic connection; the demonstration specifies a zero reading. A filter-paper <salt bridge> containing aqueous $\mathrm{Na_2SO_4}$ then connects the <chemical solutions>. With the meter's positive lead on <copper> and negative lead on <zinc>, its reported readings are about $+1.1\,\mathrm V$; exchanging the leads reverses the reading. These are observations reported by the university, not data generated for this article. See https://chemistryandbiochemistry.ucsd.edu/undergraduate/teaching-labs/demos/demo9b[UC San Diego, demonstration 9B: apparatus, bridge control and meter polarity].

The controls answer different questions. Exchanging only the meter leads checks the sign convention; it does not reverse the <chemical reaction> or swap the <anode> and <cathode>. Interrupting only the <salt bridge> tests the need for an ionic return path. Its absence prevents sustained cell <electric current>, but a very high-input-resistance meter may still show a transient or unstable reading while interfaces store <electric charge>. The zero reading specified for the university's apparatus is therefore not a universal theorem for every meter. Reconnecting the same <salt bridge> distinguishes a reversible interruption from a disconnected wire or exhausted <chemical solution>. This interpretation follows from <electric charge conservation> in a model allowing interfacial <electric charge> storage; see https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/I03068[IUPAC Gold Book: integral capacitance of an electrode] for the <capacitance> associated with <electrode> charge.

To distinguish terminal <electric potential difference> from delivered <power>, compare the nearly open-circuit reading with readings across a known <resistor> load. In the nearly open-circuit condition, a meter with input <electrical resistance> much larger than the cell's effective internal <electrical resistance> draws little <electric current>. With a load, measure its terminal <electric potential difference> $V$ and infer $I=V/R$ using <Ohm's law>; track the readings over <time> and repeat the nearly open-circuit reading afterwards. Keep the <temperature>, <chemical solution> compositions, immersed <electrode> areas, bridge geometry and electrical contacts comparable. This is a model-based extension of the documented demonstration, not a claim that UC San Diego published a load sweep. A finite-current drop can reflect ionic <electrical resistance>, changes of interfacial <reaction rates>, or depletion near an <electrode>; a single voltage reading cannot identify which dominates. The interfacial and composition contributions are distinguished in https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/O04358[IUPAC Gold Book: overpotential] and https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/C01230[IUPAC Gold Book: concentration overpotential]{ref}.

For a deliberately simplified calculation, model the cell by a constant <electromotive force> $\mathcal E$ in series with a constant internal <electrical resistance> $r$, and a passive load $R>0$. Take $I$ positive from the <cathode> through the load to the <anode>. Then
$$
I=\frac{\mathcal E}{R+r},\qquad V=IR=\mathcal E-Ir,\qquad P_{\rm load}=IV=I^2R.
$$
The nearly open-circuit limit $R\to\infty$ gives $V\to\mathcal E$ but $I\to0$ and $P_{\rm load}\to0$. A straight $V$-versus-$I$ line is a prediction of constant $r$, not a general law for <electrochemistry>. See https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/10-1-electromotive-force[Ling, Sanny and Moebs, University Physics II, §10.1: internal resistance, terminal voltage and load].

Holding these toy parameters fixed for $600\,\mathrm s$ gives transported <electric charge> $Q=It=3.30\,\mathrm C$ and an <extent of reaction> $\xi=Q/(2F)\simeq17.1\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$, assuming all the <electric current> serves the two specified <half-reactions>. \mathcal E Q=3.630\,\mathrm J

The load's electrical <work> also becomes <Joule heating> when the load is a <resistor>. Here $\mathcal E Q$ accounts for the reaction's available reversible electrical <work>; a full cell thermal budget would also require its thermodynamic <entropy> contribution. Real measurements need uncertainty estimates, a specified meter input <electrical resistance> and calibration, and allowance for changing composition and unwanted <chemical reactions>. The toy value $r=100\,\Omega$ is not a measured property of the university's cell.

= Electrolysis
{parent=Electrochemistry}
{wiki}

<Electrolysis> uses an external source of electrical <work> to drive <redox> <chemical reactions> at the <electrodes> of an <electrolytic cell>. Its characteristic application is forcing a <chemical reaction> in a direction that is nonspontaneous under the specified conditions: a discharging <galvanic cell> supplies electrical <work>, whereas <electrolysis> consumes electrical <work>. This makes <electrolysis> useful for producing otherwise difficult-to-obtain <chemical substances>, charging a rechargeable <battery>, and depositing a metal coating on a conducting object.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/17.7 Electrolysis>[§17.7]{ref}

Always identify the <anode> by <oxidation> and the <cathode> by <reduction>. In ordinary driven two-terminal operation, the source withdraws <electrons> from the positive <anode> and delivers <electrons> to the negative <cathode>. In a discharging <galvanic cell>, the <anode> is negative and the <cathode> positive. Thus terminal signs change with operating mode, while the reaction definitions remain valid. Through the <electrolyte>, <ions> transport <electric charge>; they need not all be consumed at an <electrode>. The <electrolyte> and <electrolytic cell> articles explain this transport and its <conventional current> direction.

For a net <chemical equation> transferring $z$ <electrons> per reaction event, the molar reaction <Gibbs free energy> obeys
$$
\Delta_rG=-zF E_{\mathrm{chem}},
$$
where $F$ is the <Faraday constant> and $E_{\mathrm{chem}}$ is the reversible cell <electromotive force> for the reaction in the written direction. A nonspontaneous direction has $\Delta_rG>0$ and $E_{\mathrm{chem}}<0$ at fixed <temperature> and <pressure>. The ideal opposing voltage magnitude is $\Delta_rG/(zF)$; sustaining a finite <electric current> generally needs additional drive because of <electrical resistance> and finite <electrode> <reaction rates>. This thermodynamic voltage concerns the actual composition, rather than necessarily standard conditions.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/17.4 Potential, Free Energy, and Equilibrium>[§17.4]{ref} Not every use of <electrolysis> increases bulk chemical <Gibbs free energy>: transferring <copper> between equivalent surfaces can have zero bulk $\Delta_rG$ while the source still supplies dissipated electrical <work>. See the existing <electrolytic cell> example.

A particularly revealing experiment measures <copper> transfer instead of merely looking for a coating. Two separated <copper> <electrodes> stand in aqueous $\mathrm{CuSO_4}$ <electrolyte> in a vessel, with insulated leads to a regulated direct-current power supply, a series ammeter and a switch. A balance reads each <electrode>'s <mass> before and after a timed run. The Institute of Physics describes copper cells with ammeters, adjustable current and removable <cathodes>; its comparison uses three similar cells at $1\,\mathrm A$ for ten minutes, $0.5\,\mathrm A$ for ten minutes, and $0.5\,\mathrm A$ for twenty minutes. The specified observation to test is that deposited <copper> <mass> is proportional to <electric current> times <time>. These are documented experimental settings, not measurements generated here.<Electrolysis of copper sulfate solution by Institute of Physics>{ref}

The ideal <half-reactions> are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{anode, oxidation:}\quad&\mathrm{Cu}(s)\longrightarrow\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}(aq)+2e^-,\\
\text{cathode, reduction:}\quad&\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}(aq)+2e^-\longrightarrow\mathrm{Cu}(s).
\end{aligned}
$$
Each deposited <copper> <chemical atom> requires two <electrons>, so $z=2$. The <anode> loses <copper> and the <cathode> gains it; ideally their <mass> changes have equal magnitudes, and the bulk <copper> <cation> inventory stays constant. Local <concentrations> near the <electrodes> can nevertheless differ. The sulfate <anions> participate in ionic transport without appearing in these net <half-reactions>; electronic <electric current> in the wires becomes ionic <electric current> in the <electrolyte> through the two interfaces.<Elektrolyse, Bestimmung von e by Karlsruhe Institute of Technology, Faculty of Physics teaching laboratory>{ref}

Clean, rinse, dry and identify the <electrodes> before their initial weighing. After interrupting the <electric current>, rinse away adhering <electrolyte>, dry again and weigh without rubbing off the deposit. KIT's university practical explicitly calls for distilled-water rinsing, careful drying and weighing both <electrodes>; it warns that loose <copper> grains can be lost during handling.<Elektrolyse, Bestimmung von e by Karlsruhe Institute of Technology, Faculty of Physics teaching laboratory>{ref} Useful additional controls are repeated drying and weighing until the reading stabilizes, and a matched unpowered immersion followed by the same rinse/dry cycle. These controls test retained liquid, residual dissolved salts, handling loss and chemical changes unrelated to passed <electric charge>; they are proposed checks, not additional observations reported by KIT. Keep immersed <electrode> area, spacing, <electrolyte> composition and <temperature> comparable across runs, and keep the <electrodes> from touching.

Record the <electric current> as a function of <time>, together with the exact switch-on and switch-off times. Taking $I(t)\geq0$ in the chosen deposition direction, the transported <electric charge> is
$$
Q=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}I(t)\,dt.
$$
For constant <electric current>, $Q=I(t_1-t_0)$; with sampled readings, use <numerical integration> rather than assuming that one ammeter reading remained constant. The two equal-charge Institute of Physics settings each give $600\,\mathrm C$, while its shorter $0.5\,\mathrm A$ setting gives $300\,\mathrm C$. With $M_{\mathrm{Cu}}=63.546\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$, the <mass> per <amount of substance> tabulated by KIT, and $F\approx96485.33212\,\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}}$, ideal <Faraday's laws of electrolysis> predict
$$
m_{\mathrm{Cu}}=\frac{M_{\mathrm{Cu}}Q}{2F}
\approx\begin{cases}
0.09879\,\mathrm g,&Q=300\,\mathrm C,\\
0.19758\,\mathrm g,&Q=600\,\mathrm C.
\end{cases}
$$
These are calculated predictions for complete conversion of the measured <electric charge> into retained <copper>, not experimental results. For a stable process, doubling the <electric charge> doubles the deposited <mass>; equal <electric charge> gives equal deposited <mass> even when the <electric current> and duration differ.

Right: ideal <copper> <mass> versus <electric charge> and a stipulated 80 percent current-efficiency comparison. The balance reading is a test of the whole model. Competing <reduction> of <water> or other <chemical species> can divert <electric current> from <copper> deposition; transient <electrode> <capacitance> can also consume part of a short pulse without producing <copper>. A fragile deposit can detach even when its formation used the expected <electric charge>. Conversely, retained <electrolyte> or salts can increase an apparent <mass> gain. Comparing <anode> loss with <cathode> gain helps detect such problems but does not identify every cause. A slope below $M_{\mathrm{Cu}}/(2F)$ alone cannot distinguish competing <chemical reactions> from lost deposit. Current efficiency measures the fraction of transported <electric charge> assigned to a specified <electrode> reaction, rather than the fraction of electrical <energy> stored chemically.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology, fifth edition (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry/current efficiency>{ref}

The beautiful connection is that an electrical measurement counts chemical events: integrating <electric current> and reading a balance provide two ways of following the same <electron> <stoichiometry>. This makes <electrolysis> useful for controlling coating quantity and for testing a charge-to-<amount of substance> relation. It does not determine coating uniformity, adhesion or the required voltage, which depend on transport and <electrode> <chemical kinetics> as well.

= Faraday's laws of electrolysis
{c}
{parent=Electrolysis}
{wiki}
{title2=$m=MQ/(zF)$}

<Faraday's laws of electrolysis> state that the <mass> of material formed or consumed by a specified <electrode> reaction is proportional to the transported <electric charge>, and that equal <electric charge> produces <masses> proportional to $M/z$ for different reactions. Here $M$ is the product's <mass> per <mole>, and $z$ is the positive number of <electrons> transferred per product entity. For complete use of the <electric charge> in that reaction,
$$
n_{e^-}=\frac QF,\qquad n_{\mathrm{product}}=\frac{Q}{zF},\qquad m=\frac{MQ}{zF}.
$$
The first relation uses the <Faraday constant>; the second uses <half-reaction> <stoichiometry>; the third converts <amount of substance> to <mass>. For equal transported <electric charge> in two ideal cells,
$$
\frac{m_1}{m_2}=\frac{M_1/z_1}{M_2/z_2}.
$$
The number $z$ belongs to the balanced <half-reaction>, rather than to the metal's name alone: <copper> deposited from $\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}$ needs $z=2$. These modern forms of both laws are given in KIT's practical.<Elektrolyse, Bestimmung von e by Karlsruhe Institute of Technology, Faculty of Physics teaching laboratory>{ref}

If other same-direction <electrode> reactions share the measured <electric current>, define an integrated current efficiency $\eta=Q_{\mathrm{product}}/Q$. Provided the product is neither subsequently consumed nor lost, the retained <mass> is
$$
m=\eta\frac{MQ}{zF},\qquad
\eta=\frac{\int I_{\mathrm{product}}(t)\,dt}{\int I(t)\,dt}.
$$
Here $0\leq\eta\leq1$ follows from the specified same-direction partition of <electric current>. It is not a universal bound on every signed partial-current convention: IUPAC notes that simultaneous <oxidation> and <reduction> at one <electrode> can produce a current-efficiency ratio above one.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology, fifth edition (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry/current efficiency>{ref} When efficiency varies during a run, use the charge-weighted ratio above, rather than an unweighted average over <time>. If deposition removes material from a multicomponent <chemical solution>, specify which <chemical species> is being counted and balance its <half-reaction> before choosing $z$.

Michael Faraday's accessible 1834 paper, Experimental Researches in Electricity, Seventh Series, §783 states proportionality between chemical action and the quantity of electricity passed. In §§789–791 he describes connecting a molten tin-chloride cell in series with a water-decomposition charge indicator, weighing the receiving <electrode> before and after, and comparing the deposited metal with the indicator's gas readout. The common series <electric current> makes these two readouts comparable. This was a quantitative test of chemical equivalents, rather than merely an observation that a deposit appeared. It is historical evidence, not a suggested classroom procedure; the modern <electron>-counting derivation above uses concepts unavailable to Faraday.<Experimental Researches in Electricity, Seventh Series by Michael Faraday>[§783; §§789–791; pp. 101–103]{ref}

= Electrolytic cell
{parent=Electrolysis}
{wiki}

An <electrolytic cell> uses an external source of electrical <work> to drive <electrode> <chemical reactions>. In the ordinary two-terminal driven mode, its positive <anode> supports <oxidation> and its negative <cathode> supports <reduction>. The source removes <electrons> from the <anode> and supplies <electrons> to the <cathode>; through the <electrolyte>, net <conventional current> goes from <anode> toward <cathode>. Terminal signs refer to the driven cell's terminals relative to each other. The source supplies electrical <power> to the cell. See <Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/17.7 Electrolysis> (§17.7: electrolysis and electroplating).

For example, transferring <copper> from a soluble <copper> <anode> onto a conducting object at the <cathode> has the ideal <half-reactions>
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{positive anode:}\quad&\mathrm{Cu}(s)\longrightarrow\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}(aq)+2e^-,\\
\text{negative cathode:}\quad&\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}(aq)+2e^-\longrightarrow\mathrm{Cu}(s).
\end{aligned}
$$
Their sum transfers <copper> between surfaces. For identical bulk states, this transfer need not have a positive bulk <Gibbs free energy> change; finite <electric current> still needs applied drive to overcome losses. Thus "consumes electrical <work>" does not mean every <electrolytic cell> stores all that <work> as chemical <Gibbs free energy>. <Electrolysis> can instead redistribute material. The charge-to-amount relation remains $\xi=Q/(2F)$ when only these <half-reactions> carry the <electric current>; competing <water> reactions would change the deposited amount. The electroplating principle and charge accounting are described in <Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/17.7 Electrolysis> (§17.7: electroplating and quantitative aspects).

The University of Washington's teaching demonstration makes this material transfer visible with a <copper> <electrode> and a stainless-steel <electrode> in a glass cell containing $0.5\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ $\mathrm{CuSO_4}$. The external source connects its positive lead to <copper> and negative lead to steel; the reported observation is a <copper> coating on the steel. Reversing the source polarity removes the deposited <copper> again: the coated surface now supports <oxidation> and acts as the <anode>. This polarity reversal changes the drive, unlike swapping the measurement leads in the <Daniell cell voltage and load experiment>. Coating appearance alone does not measure charge efficiency or exclude competing <chemical reactions>. See https://chem.washington.edu/lecture-demos/electroplating-copper-stainless-steel[University of Washington, Electroplating Copper on Stainless Steel: apparatus and reversible coating demonstration].

The <copper> <cathode> in this driven example is negative, while the <copper> <cathode> of a discharging <Daniell cell> is positive: both support exactly the same <reduction> <half-reaction>. Likewise, an <anode> need not lose material if its conducting surface is absent from its net <half-reaction>. Neither terminal sign nor a universal mass-change rule defines an <anode> or <cathode>.

= Half-reaction
{parent=Electrochemistry}
{wiki}

A <half-reaction> is a balanced <chemical equation> for either the <oxidation> or the <reduction> part of a <redox> <chemical reaction>, explicitly including exchanged <electrons>. Match the electron counts before adding the two <half-reactions>; the net equation then contains no transferred <electrons>. A <half-reaction> is bookkeeping, not necessarily an <elementary reaction>. See https://www.chem.fsu.edu/chemlab/chm1lab/redox/background.html[Florida State University, lab background: separate electrode reactions] and <Daniell cell>.

= Salt bridge
{parent=Electrochemistry}
{wiki}

A <salt bridge> is an ionic connection between <electrolyte> compartments that limits bulk mixing while permitting <electric charge> transport. Its mobile <ions> compensate changes caused by the <electrode> <half-reactions>, keeping the bulk <chemical solutions> approximately electrically neutral during sustained <electric current>. It does not conduct <electrons> like the external wire, and its <ions> need not be consumed at either <electrode>. The ionic return path and compensating ion flows are explained in https://chemed.chem.purdue.edu/genchem/topicreview/bp/ch20/electro.php[Purdue University, Electrochemical Reactions: salt bridge and charge balance].

In a discharging <Daniell cell>, <anions> moving toward the <anode> compartment and <cations> moving toward the <cathode> compartment both contribute to the internal <conventional current> from <anode> toward <cathode>. This charge-balance role is compatible with a negative <anode>: terminal signs do not imply that the whole <anode> <chemical solution> is negatively charged. The simple compensating-inventory example in <Daniell cell> does not fix all individual <ion> fluxes; <diffusion> and the cell's other mobile <ions> can contribute. A real bridge also introduces <electrical resistance> and may introduce a junction <electric potential difference>, so its presence does not guarantee an exact thermodynamic cell reading. See https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/09080[IUPAC Gold Book: salt bridge and liquid-junction potential].

= Electrolyte
{parent=Electrochemistry}

An <electrolyte> is a conducting medium in which mobile <ions> carry <electric current>, according to https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/09061[IUPAC Gold Book: electrolyte]. In introductory <chemistry>, a substance that yields <ions> when dissolved in <water> is also called an <electrolyte>. Both usages point to the same mechanism: an <electric field> gives mobile <cations> and <anions> directed <drift velocities>.

Mobility matters as much as the presence of <electric charge>. In a simple ideal model of solid <sodium chloride>, <ions> are confined to their sites and do not carry a sustained transport <electric current>. Molten <sodium chloride> has mobile <ions>; dissolved <sodium chloride> gives mobile $\mathrm{Na}^+$ and $\mathrm{Cl}^-$ <ions> in <water>. Real solids can have some ionic transport through imperfections; the immobile-solid picture is an introductory limit, rather than a claim that all solids are perfect insulators.

A <strong electrolyte> yields essentially all <ions> from the dissolved substance, whereas a <weak electrolyte> yields only a fraction under the specified conditions. Dissolved <sodium chloride> is a <strong electrolyte>. <Sucrose> is a <nonelectrolyte>: it dissolves as neutral <molecules>, so dissolving something does not automatically create mobile <ions>. <Water>'s own small population of <hydronium> and <hydroxide> remains possible. See <Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/11.2 Electrolytes> (§11.2: strong, weak, and nonelectrolytes) and https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/11-4-colligative-properties[Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, §11.4, Table 11.3: sucrose and sodium chloride].

<Acetic acid> is a <weak electrolyte> and a <weak acid>. In <water>, some neutral <acetic acid> <molecules> transfer a <proton> to <water>, producing <hydronium> and the negatively charged partner:
$$
\mathrm{CH_3COOH}+\mathrm{H_2O}\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}+\mathrm{H_3O^+}.
$$
Both sides have net <electric charge> zero. At <chemical equilibrium>, neutral <acetic acid> coexists with these <ions>; the fraction converted depends on the conditions. <Electrolyte> strength describes this extent of ion production, while <concentration> describes how much substance is present per specified quantity of <chemical solution>. A dilute <strong electrolyte> can contain fewer <ions> per volume than a concentrated <weak electrolyte>. Neither the words strong and weak nor a conductivity comparison alone specifies <concentration>.

Now specify an <electrolytic cell> with a positive <anode> on the left, a negative <cathode> on the right, and an approximately uniform <electric field> pointing right in the bulk <electrolyte>. With a steady external source and neglecting <diffusion> and <convection>, positive <cations> drift right toward the negative <cathode>; negative <anions> drift left toward the positive <anode>. Yet both populations contribute a rightward <conventional current>! For <number densities> $n_+,n_-$ and <drift velocities> $\mathbf v_+,\mathbf v_-$, the transport <current density> is
$$
\mathbf J=n_+(+e)\mathbf v_++n_-(-e)\mathbf v_-.
$$
The product of <electric charge> and <velocity> determines <conventional current>: for the <anion>, both the charge and its rightward velocity component are negative, making its rightward contribution positive. This is the same sign rule used for <electron> <conventional current>. It follows directly from the existing <charge carrier> formula.

For an independent numerical example, stipulate $n_+=n_-=10^{25}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$, $v_{+,x}=2\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, and $v_{-,x}=-3\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. With the exact <elementary charge> $e=1.602176634\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$, the contributions are
$$
J_{+,x}=320.4353268\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}},\qquad
J_{-,x}=480.6529902\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}},\qquad
J_x=801.088317\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}.
$$
Equal <number densities> of singly charged <cations> and <anions> give zero bulk <charge density>, while their motion gives nonzero <current density>. This is why a neutral <electrolyte> can conduct. These numbers are chosen toy inputs, not measurements of <sodium chloride>; maintaining sustained cell <electric current> also requires suitable <electrode> reactions. Blocking <electrodes> can instead lead to charge accumulation and decay of the <electric current>.

An <electrolyte> connects the microscopic motion of <ions> to observable <electrical conductivity>. A counterion-only model keeps just the mobile <counterions> needed to oppose a fixed <electric charge>; <Poisson-Boltzmann equations> use ideal <Boltzmann distributions> to model their equilibrium distributions, rather than the drift example above.

= Strong electrolyte
{parent=Electrolyte}
{wiki}

A <strong electrolyte> yields essentially all <ions> from its dissolved substance in the specified <solvent> and conditions. Strength does not specify <concentration>.

For dissolved <sodium chloride> in dilute <water>, one dissolved formula unit supplies one <sodium> <cation> and one <chloride> <anion>. For <hydrogen chloride> in <water>, <ions> instead arise by <proton> transfer to the <solvent>, giving <hydronium> and <chloride>. Thus separating existing <ions> by <dissociation of an ionic compound> and producing <ions> from neutral <molecules> by <ionization> can both give a <strong electrolyte>.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/11.2 Electrolytes>[Chemistry 2e, §11.2, Ionic Electrolytes and Covalent Electrolytes]{ref}

The denominator in “essentially all” is the dissolved inventory. <Solubility> asks how much enters the <chemical solution>; <electrolyte> strength asks what happens to the part that enters. A sparingly soluble <ionic compound> can therefore have an essentially fully dissociated dissolved portion. Undissolved solid does not count as neutral dissolved <chemical species>, and low <electrical conductivity> alone does not establish a <weak electrolyte>.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/11.2 Electrolytes>[Chemistry 2e, §11.2, Ionic Electrolytes, paragraph on sparingly soluble ionic compounds]{ref}

= Weak electrolyte
{parent=Electrolyte}
{wiki}

A <weak electrolyte> yields only a fraction of <ions> from its dissolved substance under the specified conditions, as illustrated by <acetic acid> in <water>.

For a neutral <weak acid>, unconverted <molecules> coexist with <hydronium> and the <conjugate base> at <chemical equilibrium>. The <weak acid dissociation fraction> describes the partition of the dissolved inventory; the <acid dissociation constant> describes the <chemical equilibrium> at fixed <solvent>, <temperature> and <standard states>. A single fraction is not a permanent label for every <concentration>: <dilution> can increase that fraction while the <acid dissociation constant> stays fixed. Calling a substance a <weak electrolyte> does not mean its <chemical solution> must be dilute.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/14.3 Relative Strengths of Acids and Bases>[Chemistry 2e, §14.3, Acid and Base Ionization Constants, percent-ionization discussion preceding Example 14.7]{ref}

The following <ideal weak electrolyte dilution law> makes the distinction quantitative for a <monoprotic acid>. Other <weak electrolytes>, mixed <chemical solutions> and additional proton-transfer steps require their own <chemical equilibrium> and inventory balances.

= Ideal weak electrolyte dilution law
{parent=Weak electrolyte}
{title2=$K_a=c\alpha^2/(1-\alpha)$}

Consider a neutral <monoprotic acid> $\mathrm{HA}$ dissolved in <water>, with no added salts or other acids. Assume dilute ideal <thermodynamic activities>, fixed <temperature>, <water> <thermodynamic activity> one, and negligible <self-ionization of water>. Write $C_T$ for its <analytical concentration>, $c=C_T/c^\circ$ with $c^\circ=1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, and $\alpha$ for its <weak acid dissociation fraction>. Conservation of acid units and <electric charge> gives
$$
[\mathrm{HA}]=C_T(1-\alpha),\qquad
[\mathrm A^-]=[\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^+]=C_T\alpha.
$$
Here $\mathrm A^-$ is the <conjugate base>, and $\mathrm H^+$ is conventional shorthand for hydrated <hydrogen ions>, represented by <hydronium> in this model. Substitution into the <dimensionless> <acid dissociation constant> gives
$$
K_a=\frac{[\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^+][\mathrm A^-]}{c^\circ[\mathrm{HA}]}
=\frac{c\alpha^2}{1-\alpha},\qquad
c\alpha^2+K_a\alpha-K_a=0.
$$
Solving this <quadratic equation> and rejecting its negative <root of a polynomial> gives an exact result within the stated model:
$$
\alpha=\frac{\sqrt{K_a^2+4K_ac}-K_a}{2c}
=\frac{2K_a}{K_a+\sqrt{K_a^2+4K_ac}}.
$$
The second form avoids subtracting nearly equal numbers. Unlike the approximation $\alpha\approx\sqrt{K_a/c}$, it retains depletion of neutral <weak acid> and always satisfies $0<\alpha<1$ for positive $K_a,c$. The broader <weak acid mass and charge balances> restore <self-ionization of water> when that omitted contribution matters.

\Image[weak-electrolyte-fraction-and-ion-concentration.png]
{title=<Dilution> increases the ionized fraction while decreasing the absolute <hydronium> <concentration> in an ideal <weak electrolyte> model}
{description=Original calculated curves for stipulated <dimensionless> <acid dissociation constant> $K_a=10^{-5}$ at fixed <temperature>, not measurements of a named <chemical acid>. The shared horizontal <analytical concentration> axis decreases to the right. Separate panels show <weak acid dissociation fraction> and $[\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^+]=C_T\alpha$; a dashed comparison shows a completely ionized <monoprotic acid> at the same $C_T$. Both omit <self-ionization of water>, other <ions> and nonideal <thermodynamic activities>.}

For an original worked example, stipulate $K_a=10^{-5}$ without identifying a real substance. At $C_T=10^{-2}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, the <weak acid dissociation fraction> is approximately $0.0311267$, or $3.11267\%$, and $[\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^+]=3.11267\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$. A hundredfold <dilution> to $C_T=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ raises the fraction to $0.270156$, or $27.0156\%$, but lowers $[\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^+]$ to $2.70156\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$. A completely ionized <strong acid> at $C_T=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ would have $[\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^+]\approx10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$: less than the more concentrated <weak acid> in the first calculation, despite its greater converted fraction.

The directions of both changes follow without relying on the plot. Put $x=[\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^+]/c^\circ=c\alpha$. Rearranging the <chemical equilibrium> relation gives
$$
c=x+\frac{x^2}{K_a},\qquad
\frac{\mathrm dc}{\mathrm dx}=1+\frac{2x}{K_a}>0,\qquad
\alpha=\frac{K_a}{K_a+x}.
$$
Thus smaller $c$ forces smaller $x$, which in turn makes $\alpha$ larger. The <derivative> proves that total <hydronium> <concentration> and converted fraction move in opposite directions during <dilution>. In this model, adding <solvent> also makes additional acid units ionize at fixed total acid <amount of substance>, even though their <ions> are spread over more <volume (thermodynamics)>. That distinction explains why “more ionized” alone does not predict greater <electrical conductivity>.

Do not extend the no-background model to unlimited <dilution>: its prediction $[\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^+]\to0$ omits <water>'s own <ions>. The <weak acid mass and charge balances> include that background. Added salts, other acids or bases, and nonideal <activity coefficients> also change the relation between <analytical concentration>, converted fraction and <hydronium> <concentration>.

= Nonelectrolyte
{parent=Electrolyte}

A <nonelectrolyte> supplies neutral <molecules> rather than <ions> on dissolving in the specified <solvent>, as illustrated by <sucrose> in <water>.

For example, dissolving an idealized $1\,\mathrm{mmol}$ <sucrose> inventory gives $1\,\mathrm{mmol}$ dissolved <sucrose> <molecules>, rather than separate <cations> and <anions>. Dissolving the same <amount of substance> of a fully dissociated one-to-one <strong electrolyte> instead gives $1\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of each <ion>. Both can disappear visibly into the <solvent>; the resulting <chemical species> distinguish them.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/11.2 Electrolytes>[Chemistry 2e, §11.2, opening definitions of electrolytes and nonelectrolytes]{ref}

A <nonelectrolyte> is not necessarily insoluble: <sucrose> provides a soluble counterexample. Nor does the label assert exactly zero measured <electrical conductivity>. <Self-ionization of water>, impurities or residual <electrolyte> from a probe can supply background <ions>. Comparing with a <solvent> blank and rinsing between samples, as in the <strong electrolyte> experiment, tests whether the dissolved substance itself produces appreciable additional mobile <ions>. This separates visible <dissolution> from ion production without treating lamp darkness as an absolute absence of <electric charge>.
