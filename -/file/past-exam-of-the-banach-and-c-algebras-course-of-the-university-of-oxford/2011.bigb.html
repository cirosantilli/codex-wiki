= Solution
{parent=1}

A unital **<Banach star-algebra>** is a complex unital <Banach algebra> with a continuous conjugate-linear <algebra involution> satisfying
$$
(ab)^*=b^*a^*,\qquad(a^*)^*=a,\qquad1^*=1.
$$
The algebra <norm> is complete and submultiplicative, with the unit conventionally normalized to <norm> one. Many definitions additionally require the involution to be isometric, $\|a^*\|=\|a\|$; the function algebra here satisfies this stronger convention as well. A <Banach star-algebra> does not by definition require the extra <C-star identity> $\|a^*a\|=\|a\|^2$.

Here is the lattice proof of the **<Stone-Weierstrass theorem>** in its complex self-adjoint form. Let $B$ be the uniform <closure> of the given unital star-subalgebra in $C(\Omega)$, and let $B_{\mathbb R}$ be its real-valued part. <Closure> preserves multiplication and <complex conjugation>, so $B$ is still a unital <star-algebra>. Real and imaginary parts lie in $B_{\mathbb R}$, and separation by the complex algebra implies separation by its real-valued part.

For $h\in B_{\mathbb R}$, <polynomial> approximation of absolute value on a bounded interval gives $|h|\in B_{\mathbb R}$. For example, this <polynomial> approximation follows from the binomial series for the square root, whose coefficients give <uniform convergence> on the relevant closed interval. Thus
$$
\max(h,k)=\tfrac12(h+k+|h-k|),\qquad
\min(h,k)=\tfrac12(h+k-|h-k|)
$$
also lie in $B_{\mathbb R}$. It is therefore a lattice of functions.

To see how the lattice gives density, fix a real continuous target $f$ and a tolerance $\epsilon>0$. For any two distinct points $x,y$, separation and constants provide $h_{xy}\in B_{\mathbb R}$ with $h_{xy}(x)=f(x)$ and $h_{xy}(y)=f(y)$, by an affine rescaling of a separating function. For $x=y$, use the constant $f(x)$.

Fix $x$. The sets where $h_{xy}>f-\epsilon$ cover $\Omega$ as $y$ varies. A finite subcover and a pointwise maximum give $g_x\in B_{\mathbb R}$ with $g_x>f-\epsilon$ everywhere and $g_x(x)=f(x)$. The neighborhoods where $g_x<f+\epsilon$ now cover $\Omega$ as $x$ varies. A second finite subcover and a pointwise minimum give $h\in B_{\mathbb R}$ satisfying
$$
f-\epsilon<h<f+\epsilon\quad\text{everywhere on }\Omega.
$$
This is <lattice approximation from two-point approximation>. Using half the desired tolerance gives strict uniform error below the desired bound. Since $B_{\mathbb R}$ is closed, it contains every real <continuous function>. Splitting a complex function into real and imaginary parts proves $\boxed{B=C(\Omega)}$.

**The annulus test.** For the closed annulus, the family holomorphic in the interior is **not dense** in the full continuous-function algebra. A uniform limit on the annulus is locally uniform in its interior, so <locally uniform convergence of holomorphic functions> makes that limit holomorphic there. Thus the proposed family is closed in $C(\Omega)$. It is proper because $z\mapsto\overline z$ is continuous but not holomorphic on the annulus interior.

There is also an explicit continuous functional detecting the obstruction. Choose a small closed disk of radius $r$ contained in the annulus interior and let $L(h)$ be the integral of $h(z)\,dz$ around its positively oriented boundary. The <Cauchy integral theorem> gives $L(h)=0$ for every member of the holomorphic family, while parametrizing the circle gives $L(\overline z)=2\pi i r^2\ne0$. Since $|L(h)|\leq2\pi r\|h\|_\infty$, no <uniform approximation> of $\overline z$ by that family is possible.

**The even-power family is dense on $[1,2]$.** It is the algebra $A=\{p(t^2):p\in\mathbb C[s]\}$. It contains constants, is closed under <complex conjugation>, and separates points because the real function $t\mapsto t^2$ is injective on this positive interval. The complex Stone-Weierstrass theorem therefore applies.

Equivalently, the squaring map is a <homeomorphism> from $[1,2]$ onto $[1,4]$. Given $f\in C([1,2])$, approximate the <continuous function> $s\mapsto f(\sqrt s)$ uniformly on $[1,4]$ by complex <polynomials>, using the <Weierstrass approximation theorem> on the real and imaginary parts. Substitution $s=t^2$ produces exactly the required even-power approximants. The positive interval is what lets even powers distinguish all its points.

**The odd-power family is also dense on $[1,2]$.** It is $B=tA$, where $A$ is the even-power family in part (i). Multiplication by $t$ is an invertible bounded <linear map> on $C([1,2])$, with inverse division by $t$, since $t\geq1$.

For an explicit approximation, take any $f\in C([1,2])$ and $\epsilon>0$. The quotient $f(t)/t$ is continuous, so part (i) gives an even <polynomial> $a$ with $\|a-f/t\|_\infty<\epsilon/2$. Then $ta$ belongs to the odd-power family and
$$
\boxed{\|ta-f\|_\infty\leq2\|a-f/t\|_\infty<\epsilon.}
$$
Although this family is not a <subalgebra> and does not contain the constant function exactly, it can approximate every <continuous function>, including constants. This proves <even and odd polynomial density on a positive interval> without incorrectly applying the unital algebra theorem directly to the odd family.

= Solution

An element $a$ is a **<normal element of a C-star algebra>** if $a^*a=aa^*$. It is a **<unitary element of a C-star algebra>** if $a^*a=aa^*=1$. Its <Banach algebra spectrum> is
$$
\boxed{\sigma_A(a)=\{z\in\mathbb C:a-z1\notin A^\times\},}
$$
where $A^\times$ is the <group of invertible elements of a Banach algebra>.

For normal $a$, the **<continuous functional calculus>** is the unique unital isometric star-isomorphism
$$
\Phi_a:C(\sigma_A(a))\longrightarrow C^*(1,a)\subseteq A,
\qquad f\longmapsto f(a),
$$
which sends the coordinate function $z\mapsto z$ to $a$. It preserves sums, products, complex <scalars> and the involution: $\Phi_a(\overline f)=\Phi_a(f)^*$. In particular $\|f(a)\|=\max_{z\in\sigma_A(a)}|f(z)|$. The <spectral mapping theorem> in this setting states
$$
\boxed{\sigma_A(f(a))=f(\sigma_A(a))\quad(f\in C(\sigma_A(a))).}
$$
The common unit and spectral permanence for C-star <subalgebras> permit computing this spectrum in $A$ itself.

For unitary $u$, the C-star identity gives $\|u\|=\|u^*\|=1$. If $|z|>1$, $u-z1=-z(1-u/z)$ has a multiplicative inverse by the <Neumann series>. If $|z|<1$, write $u-z1=u(1-zu^*)$ and apply the same series. Therefore $\sigma_A(u)$ lies on the <unit circle>.

For $|t|=1$, the denominator $1+\overline\lambda t$ is nonzero because $|\lambda|<1$. The function is consequently continuous on the spectrum, and
$$
|t+\lambda|^2=1+|\lambda|^2+\overline\lambda t+\lambda\overline t
=|1+\overline\lambda t|^2.
$$
Thus $|f(t)|=1$ throughout $\sigma_A(u)$, so $\overline f f=f\overline f=1$ there. Applying the star-homomorphism $\Phi_u$ proves
$$
\boxed{f(u)^*f(u)=f(u)f(u)^*=1.}
$$
Hence $f(u)$ is unitary. Explicitly, the same calculus gives
$$
\boxed{f(u)=(u+\lambda1)(1+\overline\lambda u)^{-1},}
$$
with the denominator's inverse also supplied by a norm-convergent Neumann series. This is the <Möbius transform of a unitary element>. The conjugation on $\lambda$ in the denominator is essential to the unit-modulus identity.

= Solution
{parent=3}

A **<state on a C-star algebra>** is a positive <linear functional> of <norm> one. In the unital case this is equivalent to positivity and $x(1)=1$. A unital star-homomorphism $\pi:A\to\mathcal B(K)$ is a **<cyclic representation of a C-star algebra>** if it has a <vector> $\xi$ with $\overline{\pi(A)\xi}=K$. For the representation associated with a state, the cyclic <vector> is taken to have <norm> one.

Use <inner products> linear in the first argument, as in the displayed formula for the projection in the source. In the **<Gelfand–Naimark–Segal construction>**, set
$$
N_x=\{a\in A:x(a^*a)=0\},\qquad
\langle[a],[b]\rangle=x(b^*a).
$$
The <Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for positive C-star functionals> makes this <inner product> well-defined on $A/N_x$. Indeed a null <vector> pairs to zero with every <vector>. The set $N_x$ is a <left ideal>: $c^*c\leq\|c\|^21$ implies
$$
x((ca)^*(ca))\leq\|c\|^2x(a^*a),
$$
so $a\in N_x$ implies $ca\in N_x$. The same estimate shows that left multiplication induces a <bounded operator>
$$
\pi_x(c)[a]=[ca],\qquad\|\pi_x(c)\|\leq\|c\|.
$$
Complete the quotient pre-Hilbert space to $H_x$, and extend these operators by <continuity>. The formula for the <inner product> gives $\pi_x(c)^*=\pi_x(c^*)$, while multiplication and the unit are preserved. Put $\xi_x=[1]$. Then $\|\xi_x\|=1$, the <vectors> $\pi_x(a)\xi_x=[a]$ are dense in $H_x$, and
$$
\boxed{x(a)=\langle\pi_x(a)\xi_x,\xi_x\rangle.}
$$
This gives the requested cyclic representation for an arbitrary state.

For the specified unit <vector> $\eta$, the functional $x_\eta$ is complex-linear and obeys $|x_\eta(a)|\leq\|a\eta\|\|\eta\|\leq\|a\|$. Moreover
$$
x_\eta(1)=1,\qquad x_\eta(a^*a)=\|a\eta\|^2\geq0.
$$
Thus it is positive and has <norm> exactly one, proving that **$x_\eta$ is a <vector state on a C-star algebra>**.

The rank-one projection satisfies
$$
(ap_\eta)(\zeta)=\langle\zeta,\eta\rangle a\eta.
$$
If $a\eta=0$, this is zero for every $\zeta$, so $ap_\eta=0$. Conversely, evaluation at $\zeta=\eta$ gives $(ap_\eta)\eta=a\eta$. Hence
$$
\boxed{a\eta=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad ap_\eta=0.}
$$
In particular $N_{x_\eta}=\{a:a\eta=0\}=\{a:ap_\eta=0\}$.

The concrete GNS space is the <Hilbert space completion>[completion] of $A/N_{x_\eta}$ with
$$
\langle[a],[b]\rangle=x_\eta(b^*a)=\langle a\eta,b\eta\rangle.
$$
Define $U_0[a]=a\eta$. The description of the null ideal proves it is well-defined, and the displayed equality proves it is a <linear isometry> with range $A\eta$. It therefore extends to an <isometry> $U:H_{x_\eta}\to\overline{A\eta}$. The extended range is closed, since its domain is complete, and contains the dense set $A\eta$. Thus **$U$ is onto the required <closure>**.

In fact here $A=\mathcal B(H)$, so $A\eta=H$: given any $\zeta\in H$, the bounded rank-one operator $b(v)=\langle v,\eta\rangle\zeta$ sends $\eta$ to $\zeta$. Thus $U$ is a unitary map onto all of $H$, and $U\xi_{x_\eta}=\eta$.

Finally, on the dense quotient <vectors>,
$$
U\pi_{x_\eta}(a)[b]=U[ab]=ab\eta=aU[b].
$$
Both sides are <bounded operators>, so this identity extends to all of $H_{x_\eta}$. Multiplication by the inverse unitary map gives
$$
\boxed{\pi_{x_\eta}(a)=U^{-1}aU\qquad(a\in A).}
$$
This is the <GNS representation of a vector state>, with the <vector> state, null ideal, cyclic <vector> and intertwining <isometry> all made explicit.
