= Solution
{parent=1}

Inside the infinite well, the time-independent <Schrodinger equation> is $-\hbar^2\Psi''/(2m)=E\Psi$ with Dirichlet endpoints. Negative or zero <energy> gives no nonzero solution satisfying both endpoints. For $E>0$, the sine solution requires $ka=n\pi$. Thus
$$
\boxed{E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2},\qquad\Psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac2a}\sin\frac{n\pi x}a,\quad n\geq1.}
$$
The <normalization> follows from $\int_0^a\sin^2(n\pi x/a)\,dx=a/2$. These functions form an <orthonormal basis>. Substitute the expansion into $i\hbar\partial_t\psi=H\psi$ and take the <inner product> with each basis vector: orthonormality gives $i\hbar\dot c_n=E_nc_n$, equivalently
$$
\boxed{c_n(t)=c_n(0)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}.}
$$
For a general infinite expansion this is interpreted in the usual <Hamiltonian> domain or its <unitary> evolution; the particular state below has only two terms.

Put $\theta=\pi x/a$. The identity $\sin\theta(16\cos^2\theta-1)=4\sin3\theta+3\sin\theta$ gives $c_1=3/5$, $c_3=4/5$, all others zero. Their squared magnitudes sum to one. Hence
$$
\boxed{\langle H\rangle=\frac9{25}E_1+\frac{16}{25}E_3=\frac{153\pi^2\hbar^2}{50ma^2}.}
$$
The evolved state is $(3e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}\Psi_1+4e^{-iE_3t/\hbar}\Psi_3)/5$. Its position <probability> includes an <interference> term with frequency $\Omega=(E_3-E_1)/\hbar=4\pi^2\hbar/(ma^2)$. Specifically,
$$
P(t)=\frac2{25\pi}\int_0^{\pi/4}\left[9\sin^2\theta+16\sin^23\theta+24\sin\theta\sin3\theta\cos(\Omega t)\right]d\theta.
$$
The three required integrals are $\pi/8-1/4$, $\pi/8+1/12$ and $1/4$, respectively. Therefore
$$
\boxed{P(0\leq X\leq a/4;t)=\frac14-\frac{11}{150\pi}+\frac{12}{25\pi}\cos\left(\frac{4\pi^2\hbar t}{ma^2}\right).}
$$
The <energy> <expectation> is constant while the spatial <probability> oscillates, because relative <energy> phases affect <interference> but not the <energy> probabilities.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Let $u=rF$. Multiplication of the radial equation by $-2mr/\hbar^2$ gives $u''+[A/r-\lambda(\lambda+1)/r^2-\alpha^2]u=0$. With $u=e^{-\alpha r}f$ the constant terms cancel, leaving
$$
\boxed{f''-2\alpha f'-\lambda(\lambda+1)f/r^2+Af/r=0.}
$$
The <indicial equation> is $c(c-1)=\lambda(\lambda+1)$, with roots $c=\lambda+1$ and $c=-\lambda$. The usual physical <Coulomb radial origin condition> selects $c=\lambda+1$: it imposes the regular reduced radial boundary condition $u(0)=0$. Normalizability alone at the origin is insufficient to make this selection when an irregular branch is locally <square-integrable>, notably at $\lambda=0$.

Write $f=r^{\lambda+1}\sum_{j\geq0}a_jr^j$. Coefficient comparison gives
$$
a_{j+1}=\frac{2\alpha(j+\lambda+1)-A}{(j+1)(j+2\lambda+2)}a_j.
$$
A nonterminating regular series has the exponentially growing asymptotic branch, suggested by $a_{j+1}/a_j\sim2\alpha/j$: it supplies an $e^{2\alpha r}$ component, which the prefactor $e^{-\alpha r}$ cannot normalize. Equivalently the regular confluent-hypergeometric solution grows unless its first parameter is a nonpositive integer. Thus a bound state requires the numerator to vanish at some $j=k\geq0$, giving
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac{A}{2(k+\lambda+1)},\qquad E=-\frac{\hbar^2A^2}{8m(k+\lambda+1)^2}.}
$$
This is <series-termination quantization of the Coulomb radial equation>. It requires $A>0$ for the assumed $\alpha>0$; at $A=0$ there are no such negative-<energy> Coulomb bound states.

For $k=0$, $F=Nr^\lambda e^{-\alpha r}$. The angular integral cancels when computing $\langle1/r\rangle$, while the radial volume element contributes $r^2dr$. Consequently
$$
\left\langle\frac1r\right\rangle=\frac{\int_0^\infty r^{2\lambda+1}e^{-2\alpha r}\,dr}{\int_0^\infty r^{2\lambda+2}e^{-2\alpha r}\,dr}=\frac{\alpha}{\lambda+1}.
$$
The ratio follows by one <integration by parts> and remains valid for real $\lambda\geq0$; no integer-only factorial identity is needed. Since now $\alpha=A/[2(\lambda+1)]$,
$$
\boxed{\langle V\rangle=-\frac{\hbar^2A\alpha}{2m(\lambda+1)}=-\frac{\hbar^2A^2}{4m(\lambda+1)^2}=2E.}
$$
For a classical particle with that negative total <energy>, nonnegative <kinetic energy> requires $E-V=\hbar^2(A/r-\alpha^2)/(2m)\geq0$, hence $\boxed{r\leq A/\alpha^2}$. This is a necessary radial bound; extra angular momentum can impose a stronger restriction.

At $k=\lambda=0$, $\alpha=A/2$ and the classical boundary is $r=2/\alpha$. The normalized radial <probability> density is $4\alpha^3r^2e^{-2\alpha r}$. With $z=2\alpha r$, the exterior quantum <probability> is
$$
\boxed{\int_{2/\alpha}^\infty4\alpha^3r^2e^{-2\alpha r}\,dr=\frac12\int_4^\infty z^2e^{-z}\,dz=13e^{-4}.}
$$
The final integration gives $(4^2+2\cdot4+2)e^{-4}/2$. This is the nonzero quantum tail beyond the classical turning region.

= Solution

With the specified ladder-operator <normalization>, $A_+^\dagger=A_-$ and
$$
A_+A_-=P^2+m^2\omega^2X^2-m\hbar\omega=2mH-m\hbar\omega.
$$
Therefore $\|A_-\psi\|^2=2m(E-\hbar\omega/2)\|\psi\|^2\geq0$. This proves $\boxed{E\geq\hbar\omega/2}$, with equality exactly when $A_-\psi=0$. In position representation this equation is $\hbar\psi'+m\omega x\psi=0$, whose general solution is
$$
\boxed{\psi(x)=C\exp[-m\omega x^2/(2\hbar)].}
$$
For a unit vector $|C|=(m\omega/\pi\hbar)^{1/4}$; its phase is arbitrary. The <commutators> $[H,A_\pm]=\pm\hbar\omega A_\pm$ make $A_+$ raise and $A_-$ lower energies by $\hbar\omega$. Repeated raising of the Gaussian produces all levels $(n+1/2)\hbar\omega$. Conversely lowering any nonzero <energy> <eigenvector> must eventually terminate, since otherwise it would produce an <energy> below the lower bound. A terminating vector is annihilated by $A_-$ and hence has <energy> $\hbar\omega/2$. Thus no other <eigenvalues> occur.

Bilinearity of the two-dimensional <commutators> gives
$$
[P_\theta,X_\varphi]=-i\hbar(\cos\theta\cos\varphi+\sin\theta\sin\varphi)\mathbf1=-i\hbar\cos(\theta-\varphi)\mathbf1,
$$
while all momentum-momentum and position-position <commutators> remain zero. Equal angles give a canonical position-momentum pair. Perpendicular angles give commuting components, which belong to independent coordinate directions.

The orthogonal coordinates $X_+=(X_1+X_2)/\sqrt2$, $X_-=(X_1-X_2)/\sqrt2$ preserve the kinetic sum, and
$$
5(X_1^2+X_2^2)-8X_1X_2=X_+^2+9X_-^2.
$$
Hence $\boxed{\omega_+=\omega,\quad\omega_-=3\omega}$ and $H=H_++H_-$. Every cross-mode position/momentum pair commutes, so $[H_+,H_-]=0$. On the tensor-product oscillator basis,
$$
E=\hbar\omega(n_++\tfrac12)+3\hbar\omega(n_-+\tfrac12)=(n_++3n_-+2)\hbar\omega.
$$
For each $N\geq0$, allowed pairs have $n_-=0,1,\ldots,\lfloor N/3\rfloor$ and $n_+=N-3n_-$. Thus
$$
\boxed{E_N=(N+2)\hbar\omega,\qquad g_N=\lfloor N/3\rfloor+1.}
$$
Each pair contributes one product <eigenvector>; counting them accounts for the <energy-level degeneracy>.

= Solution
{parent=4}

Let $U(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar}$ and take an observable with no explicit time dependence. In the <Schrödinger picture>, $\psi_S(t)=U(t)\psi_0$ and $A_S=A$. In the <Heisenberg picture>, $\psi_H=\psi_0$ and $A_H(t)=U(t)^\dagger A U(t)$. For the <interaction picture> choose a time-independent splitting $H=H_0+V$, put $U_0=e^{-iH_0t/\hbar}$, and define
$$
\psi_I=U_0^\dagger\psi_S,\qquad A_I=U_0^\dagger A U_0,\qquad V_I=U_0^\dagger VU_0.
$$
Then $i\hbar\dot\psi_I=V_I\psi_I$ and $\dot A_I=(i/\hbar)[H_0,A_I]$. Unitarity gives the identical <expectation> in all three descriptions:
$$
\boxed{\langle\psi_S,A\psi_S\rangle=\langle\psi_0,A_H\psi_0\rangle=\langle\psi_I,A_I\psi_I\rangle.}
$$
Different choices of the split interpolate between pictures; $H_0=H$ makes the state constant, while $H_0=0$ gives the Schrödinger description.

Differentiate $U^\dagger AU$ to obtain the <Heisenberg equation of motion>
$$
\boxed{\dot A_H=\frac{i}{\hbar}[H,A_H].}
$$
For an explicitly time-dependent observable there is additionally $U^\dagger(\partial_tA)U$. Thus $H_H=H$ is constant. In a normalized <energy> <eigenstate>, $\langle[H,A_H]\rangle=E\langle A_H\rangle-E\langle A_H\rangle=0$, so the <expectation> of its Heisenberg derivative vanishes whenever the products are defined.

For the <harmonic oscillator>, the canonical <commutator> gives
$$
\boxed{\dot X=P/m,\qquad\dot P=-m\omega^2X,\qquad\frac d{dt}(XP)=P^2/m-m\omega^2X^2.}
$$
The last identity follows from the operator product rule without commuting $X$ and $P$. Although $XP$ is not itself <self-adjoint>, the same calculation can be applied to the <self-adjoint> dilation observable $(XP+PX)/2$ and gives the same derivative.

Stationarity of the three <expectations> gives $\langle P\rangle=\langle X\rangle=0$ and $\langle P^2\rangle=m^2\omega^2\langle X^2\rangle$. Substitution into $E=\langle P^2\rangle/(2m)+m\omega^2\langle X^2\rangle/2$ yields the <quantum virial theorem>
$$
\boxed{\left\langle\frac{P^2}{2m}\right\rangle=\frac E2=\frac12m\omega^2\langle X^2\rangle.}
$$
The vanishing means make the <variances> equal to the second moments, so
$$
\boxed{(\Delta P)^2=mE,\qquad(\Delta X)^2=\frac E{m\omega^2},\qquad\Delta X\Delta P=\frac E\omega=(n+\tfrac12)\hbar\geq\hbar/2.}
$$
This satisfies the position-momentum <Heisenberg uncertainty relation>, with equality only for the <ground state>.

= Solution
{parent=5}

For a nonzero trial vector use the <Rayleigh quotient> $\mathcal E(\psi)=\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle/\langle\psi,\psi\rangle$. Its first variation in any allowed direction $\eta$ is
$$
\delta\mathcal E=\frac{2\operatorname{Re}\langle\eta,(H-\mathcal E)\psi\rangle}{\|\psi\|^2}.
$$
Vanishing for all complex directions implies $(H-\mathcal E)\psi=0$; an <eigenvector> conversely makes it vanish. The ground <energy> is the infimum of this quotient for a lower-bounded <Hamiltonian>, so minimizing over a trial family provides an upper bound to it, exact when that family contains a ground <eigenvector>.

Set $a=\langle\phi,H\phi\rangle$, $b=\langle\phi,I\phi\rangle$ and $c=\langle\phi,HI\phi\rangle$. All three are real: $H,I$ are <self-adjoint> and commute. Also $I^\dagger I=I^2=1$. Expansion of the numerator and norm gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E(t)=\frac{(1+t^2)a+2tc}{1+t^2+2tb}.}
$$
Differentiate the quotient, retaining only nonzero trial vectors:
$$
\mathcal E'(t)=\frac{2(c-ab)(1-t^2)}{(1+t^2+2tb)^2}.
$$
Thus the stationarity equation is $\boxed{(t^2-1)(c-ab)=0}$. If $c=ab$, the quotient is identically $a$ wherever defined. Otherwise $t=\pm1$ are the two extrema, with
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E_\pm=\frac{a\pm c}{1\pm b},\qquad \mathcal E_+-\mathcal E_-=\frac{2(c-ab)}{1-b^2}.}
$$
In this nonconstant case $|b|<1$, so both denominators are positive. If $c-ab>0$, the plus value is the maximum and the minus value the minimum; the assignments reverse when $c-ab<0$. One can also see global extremality by resolving the trial vector into the orthogonal $I=\pm1$ sectors: its <energy> is a weighted mean of their two fixed projected energies.

The null-vector exception matters. If $|b|=1$, then $I\phi=\pm\phi$, the covariance $c-ab$ vanishes and one of $\phi\pm I\phi$ is zero. That value has no <Rayleigh quotient> and is not an extra stationary physical state.

For particle exchange, change of integration variables shows that $I\phi(x_1,x_2)=\phi(x_2,x_1)$ is <unitary> and $I^2=1$, hence <self-adjoint>. Exchanging coordinates interchanges the two kinetic derivatives and leaves the symmetric potential unchanged, so $[I,H]=0$. The vectors $\phi\pm I\phi$ lie in its symmetric and antisymmetric sectors. Their normalized nonzero versions are respectively <bosonic> and <fermionic> spatial trial <wavefunctions>.

Because the <energy> of a general trial state is the weighted average of its sector energies, at least one nonzero symmetry <projection> has <energy> no greater than the original trial. Thus minimizing can be performed in a definite exchange-symmetry sector. This is a variational approximation within the trial subspace; it does not assert that projecting an arbitrary trial vector produces an exact <eigenvector> of $H$. For particles with a prescribed statistics sector one minimizes within that physical sector.

= Solution
{parent=6}

For classical events, $A\setminus C\subset(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus C)$: an outcome in $A$ but not in $C$ either fails $B$ or belongs to $B\setminus C$. <Monotonicity> and the <union bound> prove the <Fréchet-Bell inequality>
$$
P(A\setminus C)\leq P(A\setminus B)+P(B\setminus C).
$$
<Malus' law> gives transmission $\cos^2\gamma$ for a photon polarized by the first filter, so its conditional rejection <probability> at the second is $\boxed{\sin^2\gamma}$. Conditional and joint probabilities must be distinguished: $P(A\setminus B)=P(A)P(B^c\mid A)$.

For equal unbiased marginals $P(A)=P(B)=1/2$, the quantum two-setting rejection probabilities are $\tfrac12\sin^2(\theta_A-\theta_B)$. A classical common assignment of all three outcomes would therefore require $\sin^2(\theta_A-\theta_B)+\sin^2(\theta_B-\theta_C)\geq\sin^2(\theta_A-\theta_C)$. Choose directions $0,\gamma,2\gamma$. Then
$$
\boxed{2\sin^2\gamma<\sin^2(2\gamma)\quad\text{when }0<\gamma<\pi/4.}
$$
For example $\gamma=\pi/6$ gives $1/2<3/4$. More generally $\sin^2a+\sin^2b-\sin^2(a+b)=-2\sin a\sin b\cos(a+b)$. In a triangle whose side directions are these filters, the internal angle supplementary to $a+b$ is obtuse exactly in this violation range. This specifies the angle convention behind the triangle description: with polarization axes regarded as unoriented lines, a claim that every sufficiently large physical axis separation violates the inequality would not be correct. The contradiction concerns a single classical joint model for all settings, not the validity of ordinary <probability> or a comparison of arbitrarily renormalized sequential experiments.

Use $|0\rangle=|\alpha_0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle=|\alpha_1\rangle$. Projecting the first photon with $|0\rangle\langle0|$ gives the unnormalized product vector $c_0|00\rangle$, with success <probability> $|c_0|^2$. <Projection> of the second onto $|\theta\rangle=\cos\theta|0\rangle+\sin\theta|1\rangle$ gives
$$
\boxed{c_0\cos\theta\,|0\rangle|\theta\rangle,\qquad P(\text{both transmissions})=|c_0|^2\cos^2\theta.}
$$
Conditioned on success, normalize these vectors; a zero amplitude means that outcome never occurs. For the maximally <entangled> equal-amplitude pair, rotation of both real polarization bases gives the same correlations for arbitrary settings. Comparing the appropriately normalized coincidence/rejection frequencies at three setting pairs tests the inequality above. A general unequal-amplitude state need not reproduce the unbiased rotationally invariant probabilities, so the maximally <entangled> choice supplies the clean explicit example.

For swapping, label the four systems in order $\xi_1,\eta_1,\xi_2,\eta_2$ and let $|\Phi\rangle_\eta=2^{-1/2}\sum_{j=0}^1|\eta_j\eta_j\rangle$. Its overlap with $|\eta_r\eta_s\rangle$ is $\delta_{rs}/\sqrt2$. Applying its rank-one <projector> to Alice's two systems gives
$$
\boxed{\frac12\sum_{r,k}b_r^2|\xi_r\rangle|\eta_k\rangle|\xi_r\rangle|\eta_k\rangle.}
$$
After regrouping the remote and local systems, this factorizes into $[\sum_rb_r^2|\xi_r\xi_r\rangle/\sqrt2]\otimes|\Phi\rangle_\eta$. The selected outcome has <probability> $p=\tfrac12\sum_r|b_r|^4$. Therefore the normalized conditional remote state is
$$
\boxed{|\chi\rangle_\xi=\frac{\sum_rb_r^2|\xi_r\xi_r\rangle}{\sqrt{\sum_r|b_r|^4}}.}
$$
It has <Schmidt rank> at least two whenever the original pair was <entangled>, since squaring a nonzero coefficient does not make it zero. Thus the two photons that never interacted become <entangled> **conditional on Alice's joint measurement outcome**. This is <entanglement swapping>. Alice is projecting onto a specified <Bell state>, not performing a universal test of whether an arbitrary unknown state is <entangled>. Averaging all her outcomes leaves the remote <reduced density operator> unchanged, so no faster-than-light signal is available without her classical outcome label.
