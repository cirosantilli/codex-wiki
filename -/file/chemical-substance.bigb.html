= Alloy sublattice order parameter
{parent=Chemical ordering in alloys}
{title2=$\rho$}

For a stoichiometric $A_3B$ <alloy> with one corner sublattice and three equivalent face sublattices, let $p_c$ and $p_f$ be the respective occupation <probabilities> of species $B$. Define $\rho=(4p_c-1)/3$. Conservation of composition, $p_c+3p_f=1$, gives $p_c=(1+3\rho)/4$ and $p_f=(1-\rho)/4$. Thus the random <alloy> has $\rho=0$ and the fully ordered corner-$B$ pattern has $\rho=1$. The coherent <superlattice reflection> amplitude is $\rho(f_B-f_A)$, so its <wave intensity> scales as $\rho^2$, while the equal-parity fundamental amplitude $f_B+3f_A$ is composition-fixed. The definition assumes equivalent face sublattices; endpoint normalization alone does not uniquely specify an arbitrary order coordinate.

= Martensitic variant
{parent=Martensite}

A <martensitic variant> is one of the lattice deformations corresponding to the low-temperature <crystal> phase. The parent <crystal>'s discrete symmetries generate different variants. In a nonlinear <elasticity> model each representative $U_i$ supplies a <hyperelastic energy well> $SO(3)U_i$. A representative need not itself be a <symmetric> <stretch tensor>; multiplication by a <proper rotation> leaves the same well.

= Deformation twinning
{parent=Martensitic variant}

<Deformation twinning> joins differently oriented <crystal> variants across a coherent interface. In a piecewise affine nonlinear <elasticity> model the two <deformation gradients> must differ by $a\otimes N$, where $N$ is the reference interface <normal>. The <Hadamard jump condition> gives this requirement from <continuity> along every interface tangent. Compatible alternating layers can then make a mixture of variants whose macroscopic shape differs from either variant alone.

= Twin plane
{parent=Martensite}

A twin plane is a compatible planar interface between differently oriented crystal variants. In a piecewise homogeneous elastic deformation its reference normal is the $N$ in a <rank-one connection> between the two variant deformation gradients. Continuity of the deformation holds along this plane even though its derivative changes. Other material planes crossing the interface can kink after deformation.

= Planarity test across a cubic-to-tetragonal twin
{parent=Twin plane}
{title2=$p=(p_1,p_2,0),\quad\Pi=p^\perp\text{ remains plane}\iff p\parallel N$}

Let $B=\operatorname{diag}(b,a,a)$, with $a,b>0$, $a\ne b$, and $A=R\operatorname{diag}(a,b,a)$ be a twin-related gradient with $\cos\theta=2ab/(a^2+b^2)$, $\sin\theta=\kappa(b^2-a^2)/(a^2+b^2)$, $\kappa=\pm1$. Their jump is $(b^2-a^2)(-b,\kappa a,0)\otimes(1,\kappa,0)/(a^2+b^2)$.

A plane through the origin with normal $p=(p_1,p_2,0)$ is spanned by $e_3$ and $t=(-p_2,p_1,0)$. Both gradients map $e_3$ to the same direction, so the two image half-planes are coplanar exactly when $At$ and $Bt$ are parallel in their transverse section. Direct calculation gives
$$
\det_2(Bt,At)=\frac{\kappa ab(b^2-a^2)}{a^2+b^2}(p_1-\kappa p_2)^2.
$$
Its nonzero prefactor makes planarity equivalent to $p\parallel(1,\kappa,0)$, the twin normal. On that plane the two affine maps agree, proving sufficiency as well. The restriction on the third normal component matters; this statement is not a classification of every possible material plane.

<Lipids> are a chemically diverse family of <chemical substances>, conventionally associated with biological origin and solubility in <nonpolar> <solvents>. Thus a <lipid> is a chemical building material, while a <lipid bilayer> is an organization of many <molecules>. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/3-3-lipids[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 3.3: lipid structures and functions].

= Phospholipid
{parent=Lipid}
{wiki}

<Phospholipids> are <lipids> containing a <phosphate> group as part of their structure linked by <covalent bonds>. The familiar membrane-forming <phospholipids> are <amphiphilic>: a <hydrophilic> head region with <chemical polarity> coexists with a <hydrophobic>, largely <nonpolar> <hydrocarbon> region. These descriptions refer to different parts of the same <molecule>, not to two separate substances. See https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/P04559[IUPAC Gold Book: phospholipids] and https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/A00303[IUPAC Gold Book: amphiphilic].

A typical two-chain <glycerophospholipid> provides a useful example: a <glycerol> scaffold carries two <fatty acid>-derived <acyl groups> through <ester> linkages, plus a <phosphate>-containing head region. <Phosphatidylcholine> is one example. This scaffold describes an important subclass, rather than all <phospholipids>: <sphingomyelin> has a different backbone, and even <glycerophospholipids> need not all have two <acyl groups>. See https://iupac.qmul.ac.uk/lipid/lip1n2.html[IUPAC–IUB, Nomenclature of Lipids (1976), Lip-1.5 and Lip-2.1–2.6].

Polar does not mean negatively charged overall. At near-neutral <pH>, <phosphatidylcholine> is <zwitterionic>: its <phosphate> region carries negative <electric charge> and its <choline> region positive <electric charge>, with zero net <electric charge> for the <molecule>. Counting those opposite charges gives $(-1)+(+1)=0$; it does not remove their local interactions with <water>. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK21726/[Lodish et al., Molecular Cell Biology: membrane lipids].

In a <lipid bilayer>, two <membrane leaflets> orient their <hydrophilic> heads toward the <water> on either side and their <hydrophobic> chains toward the shared interior. The <hydrophobic effect> helps explain this organization through the <free energy> of the <water> and <lipids> together. It is not a special repulsive <chemical bond> between chains and <water>. Which organization is favored also depends on <molecular geometry>, <temperature>, <concentration>, and the surrounding <chemical solution>; a <phospholipid> label alone does not guarantee a <lipid bilayer>. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26871/[Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: the lipid bilayer].

This organization supplies a flexible barrier for compartments. A fluid <lipid bilayer> allows <lipids> to undergo <diffusion> within each <membrane leaflet>; barrier function therefore coexists with motion of <molecules>, as in the <fluid mosaic model>. It is not completely impermeable: small <nonpolar> <molecules> such as <molecular oxygen> and <carbon dioxide> cross much more readily than <ions>. In a <cell membrane>, <ion channels> and other <membrane transport proteins> provide selective routes; the existing <membrane transport> article explains their driving forces. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26815/[Alberts et al.: principles of membrane transport].

In 1925, E. Gorter and F. Grendel extracted <lipids> from <red blood cells> and compared the <surface area> of a one-<molecule>-thick film on <water> with the estimated total <surface area> of the <red blood cells>. Their table gave ratios near two, supporting a two-layer covering; they also proposed heads facing the aqueous surroundings on both sides. The inference depends on recovering the relevant <lipids>, estimating the <red blood cell> <surface area> correctly, and comparing compatible packing in the film and the <cell membrane>. It was indirect evidence, not an image of the <lipid bilayer>. The paper itself notes possible losses during evaporation. See https://doi.org/10.1084/jem.41.4.439[Gorter and Grendel, On Bimolecular Layers of Lipoids on the Chromocytes of the Blood, Journal of Experimental Medicine 41 (1925), 439–443].

{wiki}

<Carbohydrates> are a family of <chemical substances> encompassing <monosaccharides>, combinations of <monosaccharides>, and their derivatives. <Glucose> is a small <carbohydrate>; <cellulose> is a long-chain <polysaccharide>. <Deoxyribose> illustrates why composition alone does not define the family: replacing a <hydroxy group> in <ribose> by <hydrogen> still leaves a <carbohydrate>. Many familiar <monosaccharides> have a <molecular formula> of the form $\mathrm C_n\mathrm H_{2n}\mathrm O_n$, equivalently $\mathrm C_n(\mathrm H_2\mathrm O)_n$. = Deoxyribose
{parent=Sugar}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mathrm C_5\mathrm H_{10}\mathrm O_4$}

In the usual <DNA> context, <deoxyribose> means 2-deoxy-D-<ribose>, a five-<carbon> <monosaccharide> with <molecular formula> $\mathrm C_5\mathrm H_{10}\mathrm O_4$. Relative to D-<ribose>, the <hydroxy group> at C2 is replaced by <hydrogen>. The locant 2 matters: it identifies which position loses its <oxygen>. See https://iupac.qmul.ac.uk/BlueBook/P10.html#P-102.5.3[Favre and Powell, IUPAC Blue Book, P-102.5.3: deoxy sugars] and https://iupac.qmul.ac.uk/class/nucle.html[IUPAC: nucleic acids and their component sugars].

The paired <Fischer projections> in <ribose> make the change concrete. C2 has <hydrogen> on both sides, while C3 and C4 retain their <hydroxy groups> on the right. C2 is therefore not a <stereocenter>: exchanging its two identical <hydrogen> substituents cannot produce a distinct configuration. The unchanged C3 and C4 still carry <stereochemical> information.

Replacing $\mathrm{OH}$ by $\mathrm H$ removes one <oxygen> but leaves the total <hydrogen> count unchanged: the <hydrogen> belonging to the removed <hydroxy group> is replaced by a <hydrogen> bonded directly to <carbon>. Thus $\mathrm C_5\mathrm H_{10}\mathrm O_5$ becomes $\mathrm C_5\mathrm H_{10}\mathrm O_4$. This comparison describes structures, rather than a complete <chemical reaction> or its mechanism.

In <DNA>, <deoxyribose> is incorporated as a ring in each <nucleotide>, with <phosphate> connections building the backbone. Its C2′ bears <hydrogen> where <RNA>'s <ribose> bears a <hydroxy group>. This small local difference lets the <sugar> structure identify the two kinds of <nucleic acid>; the <nucleobases> supply their sequence information. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26883/[Alberts et al.: nucleotides as the subunits of DNA and RNA].

= Glucose mutarotation experiment
{parent=Monosaccharide ring-chain interconversion}

An endpoint experiment uses a <polarimeter> with a $1.0\,\mathrm{dm}$ sample cell and supplied $4\%$, $6\%$ and $8\%$ D-<glucose> <chemical solutions>. Collect about $15\,\mathrm{mL}$ of each. Fill the cell first with distilled <water>, dry its windows, move bubbles to the air trap, and retain its orientation. Scan <light intensity> against <polarimeter analyzer> <angle> through $360^\circ$. The protocol fits a <Gaussian function> to the upper third of a symmetric peak to locate its maximum. Rinse with the next sample, repeat for each <glucose> <chemical solution>, and subtract the <water> maximum's <angle> from each sample maximum. This is an operational peak-location method; the <Gaussian function> is not the physical transmission law.<Equilibrium of Sugar Mutarotation by PASCO>[PASCO, Equilibrium of Sugar Mutarotation, p. 1 path length; pp. 2–4 Materials and Equipment and Procedure 1–18]{ref}

The existing <optical activity> and <polarimetry> articles explain the apparatus, <Malus's law> and instrument limitations. For an extension that observes <mutarotation> in time, freshly dissolve a specified crystalline <anomer>, record the mixing time, and repeat the <optical rotation> readout at fixed <concentration>, path length, <wavelength> and recorded <temperature>. Keep <pH> consistent and record the delay before the first reading. These are proposed additions to the endpoint protocol, not its published steps or a claim of measured results. Recheck the <water> blank, repeat the preparation, and compare with a separately equilibrated portion under the same conditions. A stable endpoint alone does not show the time evolution.

Interpret each <concentration> separately. If $\theta$ is blank-corrected <optical rotation>, $l$ is path length and $c$ is total <glucose> <mass> <concentration>, first calculate <specific rotation> $r=\theta/(lc)$. In an additive two-<anomer> approximation, let $r_\alpha,r_\beta$ be reference <specific rotations> under the same conditions and $x_\alpha$ the <mole fraction> of alpha within that pair. Equal <molar masses> make its <mass> fraction equal to $x_\alpha$, so
$$
r=x_\alpha r_\alpha+(1-x_\alpha)r_\beta,\qquad x_\alpha=\frac{r-r_\beta}{r_\alpha-r_\beta}.
$$
This is a population estimate from an assumed response model, not direct imaging of an <anomer>. For a simple consistency check, doubling $c$ at fixed $l$ and unchanged composition doubles $\theta$ in this model while leaving $r$ unchanged. Averaging raw <optical rotations> at different <concentrations> would fail this test; normalize first.

Changing <temperature>, <pH>, contamination, incomplete dissolution, window stress, bubbles or instrumental drift can imitate or obscure a changing signal. Reference <specific rotations> must match the sample conditions. Minor open-chain and other ring forms limit the two-<anomer> approximation; a single <optical rotation> cannot separately determine every population. Also, these <anomers> are <diastereomers>, not an <enantiomer> pair: the equal-and-opposite cancellation formula for <enantiomeric excess> does not apply.

Its <formula unit> is the accounting entity; the ordinary crystal is an extended arrangement of <ions>, rather than a collection of discrete $\mathrm{NaCl}$ <molecules>. Specifying the entity matters when assigning an <amount of substance>. See <formula unit> and <Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/2.6 Ionic and Molecular Compounds> (§2.6: ionic and molecular compounds).

A <molecular formula> gives an atom inventory, not the full arrangement of <chemical bonds> or the <molecular geometry>. For example, $\mathrm C_6\mathrm H_{12}\mathrm O_6$ counts six <carbon>, twelve <hydrogen>, and six <oxygen> <chemical atoms> in a <glucose> <molecule>, but does not specify the pattern shown by its <Fischer projection>. Different structures can share a <molecular formula>. This compact inventory is useful for <chemical equation> accounting precisely because it leaves the structural detail to other representations.

The choice of letters has an early explanation in https://web2.lemoyne.edu/~giunta/berzelius.html[Jöns Jacob Berzelius, Essay on the Cause of Chemical Proportions (1813–1814), §III, transcribed by Carmen Giunta]: letters made chemical signs easier to write and print, and formulas compressed information about composition. His numerical notation differed from modern subscripts; the lasting idea was to make proportions readable in ordinary text.

{wiki}

An <amino acid> combines <carboxylic acid> and <amine> functionality in the same <molecule>; the name alone does not specify their separation. = Glycine protonation
{parent=Alpha-amino acid}

The three principal aqueous <glycine> forms differ by loss of one <proton> at each step:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{H_3N^+{-}CH_2{-}COOH}+\mathrm H_2\mathrm O&\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{H_3N^+{-}CH_2{-}COO^-}+\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^+,\\
\mathrm{H_3N^+{-}CH_2{-}COO^-}+\mathrm H_2\mathrm O&\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{H_2N{-}CH_2{-}COO^-}+\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^+.
\end{aligned}
$$
Call these <glycine> forms $G^+$, $G^0$ and $G^-$, respectively: a <cation>, a <zwitterion> and an <anion>, with net <electric charges> $+e$, $0$ and $-e$, where $e$ is the <elementary charge>. $G^0$ specifically denotes the <zwitterion>, not an uncharged amino/carboxyl drawing. The first dissociation removes the carboxyl <proton>; the second removes a <proton> from the positively charged <nitrogen>. The <chemical equilibrium> changes are reversible, not three permanently separate kinds of <glycine>. <Organic Chemistry by John McMurry>[§26.1, aqueous zwitterion protonation/deprotonation; §26.2, two successive amino-acid dissociations]{ref}

For all calculations and both diagrams here, explicitly stipulate the <pKa> values $\mathrm pK_{a1}=2.35$ and $\mathrm pK_{a2}=9.78$. These are illustrative model inputs, not measurements from the <glycine titration experiment> or a claim of universal constants. Actual <acid dissociation constants> depend on <temperature> and on the convention used for <thermodynamic activity>; apparent <concentration>-based values also depend on <ionic strength>. For comparison, the inspected textbook tabulates $2.34$ and $9.60$ for <glycine>, which would give a different <isoelectric point>. <Organic Chemistry by John McMurry>[§26.1, Table 26.1, glycine row: pKa 2.34, 9.60 and pI 5.97]{ref}

= Glycine speciation model
{parent=Glycine protonation}

In an ideal aqueous <chemical solution>, set $h=[\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^+]/c^\circ=10^{-\mathrm{pH}}$, with $c^\circ=1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, and approximate <activity coefficients> by one. Put $K_1=10^{-2.35}$ and $K_2=10^{-9.78}$ for the stipulated <dimensionless> <acid dissociation constants>. The <Henderson-Hasselbalch equation> gives the adjacent-state <ratios>
$$
\frac{[G^0]}{[G^+]}=\frac{K_1}{h},\qquad\frac{[G^-]}{[G^0]}=\frac{K_2}{h}.
$$
With <analytical concentration> $C_T=[G^+]+[G^0]+[G^-]$, normalization gives the three fractions and mean <electric charge> in units of the <elementary charge>:
$$
D=h^2+K_1h+K_1K_2,\qquad
\alpha_+=\frac{h^2}{D},\quad\alpha_0=\frac{K_1h}{D},\quad\alpha_-=\frac{K_1K_2}{D},\qquad
\bar z=\alpha_+-\alpha_-.
$$
All three fractions are nonnegative and sum to one. At <pH> $7$, the model gives about $99.832\%$ <zwitterion>, $0.00224\%$ <cation> and $0.1657\%$ <anion>; $\bar z\approx-0.00163$. Predominantly <zwitterionic> therefore need not mean exactly at the <isoelectric point>.

Setting $\bar z=0$ requires $h^2=K_1K_2$. Taking the <logarithm> yields
$$
\mathrm{pI}=\frac{\mathrm pK_{a1}+\mathrm pK_{a2}}2=\frac{2.35+9.78}2=6.065.
$$
At this model <isoelectric point>, $\alpha_+=\alpha_-\approx0.0001927$ and $\alpha_0\approx0.9996146$: about $0.0193\%$ of each charged form remains. At either <pKa>, the relevant adjacent fractions are equal, but the third fraction is not mathematically zero. This three-state model neglects the minor uncharged form $\mathrm{H_2N{-}CH_2{-}COOH}$; macroscopic <pKa> values alone do not determine its fraction separately from the <zwitterion>.

= Glycine titration
{parent=Glycine protonation}

Adding <sodium hydroxide> to acidified <glycine> successively removes its two titratable <protons>. Two broad <buffer solution> regions appear around the two <pKa> values, separated by a steep <pH> rise near the <isoelectric point>. This is useful evidence for two separated <acid-base chemistry> steps even though the changing <chemical species> are invisible. In the approximation that added <hydroxide> only changes <glycine protonation>, $b=1-\bar z$ is the added base <amount of substance> per initial <amount of substance> of <glycine>. Then $b\approx0.5$, $1$ and $1.5$ correspond to $\mathrm pK_{a1}$, $\mathrm{pI}$ and $\mathrm pK_{a2}$; endpoints are not exactly pure <chemical species>. <Organic Chemistry by John McMurry>[§26.2, Figure 26.2: calculated two-leg titration and half-equivalent interpretation]{ref}

For the solid-curve model, start with $n_G=0.00200\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <glycine> in $V_0=0.0200\,\mathrm L$ and exactly $n_G$ of fully dissociated <hydrochloric acid>; this fixes the <chloride> inventory, but does not force every <glycine> <molecule> to remain $G^+$. Let the <sodium hydroxide> <concentration> be $C_B=0.100\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ and $b=n_{\mathrm{NaOH}}/n_G$. Assume <volume (thermodynamics)> is additive and no other <ions> are present; stipulate $K_w=10^{-14}$ for <self-ionization of water> at a <temperature> of $25\,\mathrm{{}^\circ C}$. Then
$$
V=V_0+\frac{bn_G}{C_B},\qquad C_T=\frac{n_G}{V},\qquad
[\mathrm{Na^+}]=bC_T,\qquad[\mathrm{Cl^-}]=C_T.
$$
Balancing all positive and negative <electric charges> requires
$$
c^\circ\left(h-\frac{K_w}{h}\right)+C_T\left(b+\bar z-1\right)=0.
$$
Solving this equation gives the solid curve. It remains finite at $b=0$ and beyond $b=2$, where ignoring free <hydronium> and <hydroxide> would fail. At the first <pKa>, the full model gives $b\approx0.436$, rather than $0.5$: appreciable free <hydronium> makes that half-equivalent shortcut inaccurate. At the second <pKa>, $b\approx1.502$. Known acid inventory, <dilution>, and <thermodynamic activity> assumptions are therefore part of any quantitative interpretation, not optional corrections inferred from the curve's shape.

= Glycine titration experiment
{parent=Glycine titration}

The Amrita Virtual Lab procedure uses $0.1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ <glycine>, <hydrochloric acid> and <sodium hydroxide>, two <burettes>, a <pH> meter, beaker and stirrer. A <pipette> transfers $20\,\mathrm{mL}$ of <glycine> into a $100\,\mathrm{mL}$ beaker. Calibrate the meter with standard <buffer solutions> at <pH> $4$, $7$ and $10$, then record the starting <pH>. Add <hydrochloric acid> in $0.3\,\mathrm{mL}$ increments, recording <pH> after each addition until $1.6$. Rinse the <electrode> in distilled <water>, take a separate fresh $20\,\mathrm{mL}$ portion, and add <sodium hydroxide> in $0.3\,\mathrm{mL}$ increments until <pH> $12.5$. Its observed readout is paired added <volume (thermodynamics)> and <pH>, plotted as an <acid-base titration> curve; it does not measure each <glycine> form directly. <Titration Curves of Aminoacids, Amrita Virtual Lab/Procedure>[Materials Required, items 1–8; Working steps, items 1–9: separate acid and base titrations]{ref}

These are two titrations from the starting <glycine> <chemical solution>, rather than the single acidified-sample protocol assumed by the solid curve in <glycine titration>. Retain each portion's initial <volume (thermodynamics)> and the signed added acid/base <amount of substance>; do not concatenate raw <burette> readings and assume the result begins at $b=0$. For this procedure's $0.00200\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <glycine>, each $0.3\,\mathrm{mL}$ addition supplies $0.000030\,\mathrm{mol}$ of acid or base, a $0.015$ increment relative to the <glycine> inventory. This calculated resolution is not a reported measurement.

Useful additional controls are a fresh repeat portion, independently checked acid/base <concentrations>, a <water> blank, and a calibration-check <buffer solution> after the run. Stir without striking the <glass electrode>, keep its sensing region immersed and wait for stable readings; record <temperature> and background salts. Reduce addition size near a steep <pH> change. These are suggested controls for testing the model, beyond the published steps. A <water> blank can reveal contamination but must not be subtracted directly from <pH>, which is a <logarithm>.

The standards at <pH> $4$, $7$ and $10$ do not bracket either endpoint of this protocol. Extreme readings therefore require independently checked instrument performance, and alkaline samples are sensitive to atmospheric <carbon dioxide> uptake. <Electrode> drift, incomplete mixing, <volume (thermodynamics)> error, <dilution> and changing <ionic strength> can shift apparent <pKa> values. A titration constrains the acid/base model; it cannot establish <chirality (chemistry)> or separate every microscopic <protonation> form. The two original diagrams show calculated predictions to compare with measured points, not reconstructed experimental data.

= Chemical element
{parent=Chemical substance}
{wiki}

A <chemical element> is an identity shared by all <chemical atoms> whose <atomic nuclei> contain the same number of <protons>. That number is its <atomic number>, $Z$: six means <carbon>, eleven means <sodium>. The identity survives differences in <neutron> count, which distinguish <isotopes>, and differences in <electron> count, which distinguish neutral <chemical atoms> from <ions>. See the https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/C01022[IUPAC Gold Book, chemical element].

The same name can also denote a pure <chemical substance> containing only that <chemical element>. These two uses answer different questions. <Molecular oxygen>, $\mathrm O_2$, is a <chemical substance> made entirely of <oxygen>; <water>, $\mathrm H_2\mathrm O$, is a <chemical compound> containing both <hydrogen> and <oxygen>. A <water> <molecule> contains three <chemical atoms> but only two <chemical elements>. Joining <chemical atoms> through <chemical bonds> changes their arrangement without changing their <atomic numbers>.

This distinction makes <stoichiometry> possible: ordinary <chemical reactions> rearrange <chemical atoms>, so one can count each <chemical element> before and after a transformation. Changing the <proton> count instead changes the <chemical element> and requires a <nuclear reaction>. The <periodic table> organizes these identities into a useful map, while <atomic number> bookkeeping keeps identity, <isotope>, and <electric charge> separate.

= Periodic table
{parent=Chemical element}
{wiki}

The <periodic table> arranges <chemical elements> in increasing <atomic number>, across successive <periodic table periods>. Its columns are <periodic groups>, numbered 1–18. The layout places recurring outer <electron configurations> above one another, making similarities in <chemical bonds> and <chemical reactions> easier to recognize. Ordering is by <proton> count, not by <mass>; the <mass number> of a particular <isotope> does not determine its box. See the https://iupac.org/what-we-do/periodic-table-of-elements/[official IUPAC periodic table and numbering conventions].

For the first eighteen neutral <chemical atoms> in their <ground states>, a compact description counts <electrons> in successive <electron shells>. Thus <carbon> has $(2,4)$, meaning two <electrons> in the first <electron shell> and four in the second; the sum $2+4=6$ agrees with its <atomic number>. <Sodium> has $(2,8,1)$, whose sum is $11$. These are occupancy counts, not circular trajectories of <electrons>.

Reading each row from left to right gives:
* <Periodic table period> 1: <hydrogen> $(1)$ and <helium> $(2)$.
* <Periodic table period> 2: <lithium> $(2,1)$, <beryllium> $(2,2)$, <boron> $(2,3)$, <carbon> $(2,4)$, <nitrogen> $(2,5)$, <oxygen> $(2,6)$, <fluorine> $(2,7)$, and <neon> $(2,8)$.
* <Periodic table period> 3: <sodium> $(2,8,1)$, <magnesium> $(2,8,2)$, <aluminium> $(2,8,3)$, <silicon> $(2,8,4)$, <phosphorus> $(2,8,5)$, <sulfur> $(2,8,6)$, <chlorine> $(2,8,7)$, and <argon> $(2,8,8)$.

The counting outline is short: the occupied <electron subshells> in this range are $1s$, $2s$, $2p$, $3s$, and $3p$, filled in that order for these neutral <ground states>. Each $s$ <electron subshell> holds up to two <electrons> and each $p$ <electron subshell> up to six, giving $2$, $2+6=8$, and $2+6=8$ along these three rows. This $2,8,8$ summary applies only through <argon>; it is not a general rule that each <electron shell> fills completely before the next begins, nor a claim that the third <electron shell> can hold only eight <electrons>. See https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/6-4-electronic-structure-of-atoms-electron-configurations[Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, electronic structure and electron configurations].

The recurring outer counts make the arrangement useful. <Lithium> and <sodium> each have one <valence electron>, whereas <fluorine> and <chlorine> each have seven <valence electrons>. Such similarities help anticipate <chemical reactions>, without making all properties identical throughout a <periodic group>. <Helium> is an instructive exception to an eight-<electron> shortcut: its two <electrons> complete the first <electron shell>, so it belongs above <neon> and <argon> in <periodic group> 18. <Hydrogen> shares column 1 with <lithium> and <sodium> but has its own distinct chemistry. See https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/2-5-the-periodic-table[Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, periodic-table organization].

= Rayleigh-Ramsay argon density experiment
{c}
{parent=Argon}

In his 1895 lecture, <Lord Rayleigh> compared <gas> masses in a globe under matched <pressure> and <temperature>. Removing <oxygen> from air left gas about half a percent heavier than <nitrogen> prepared entirely from chemical sources. Several preparation routes reproduced the difference; <hydrogen> contamination was a concern. An eight-month storage test re-established the filling conditions: simply reweighing a sealed globe could not test a change in <molar mass>. <William Ramsay> concentrated the residual gas by circulating the mixture over hot <magnesium>, which consumed <nitrogen>. A spectral test checked remaining <nitrogen>. Agreement of spectra from this route and the alternative spark-and-alkali separation supported a common residual gas.<Argon by Lord Rayleigh>[Argon, Royal Institution Proceedings 14 (1895), 524–538: gas-weight table; storage test; magnesium circulation; nitrogen spectrum test and comparison of isolation routes]{ref}

Consider an original simplified model containing only <nitrogen> and <argon>, with <argon> <mole fraction> $x$. Let their <molar masses> be $M_N$ and $M_A$. Adding the component <masses> gives the mixture's mean <molar mass>
$$
\overline M=(1-x)M_N+xM_A.
$$
For an <ideal gas> mixture at the same <pressure> and <temperature> as a pure-<nitrogen> reference, the <gas density> ratio is
$$
r=\frac{\rho}{\rho_N}=\frac{\overline M}{M_N}
=1+x\left(\frac{M_A}{M_N}-1\right),\qquad
\boxed{x=\frac{r-1}{M_A/M_N-1}.}
$$
The inversion requires known identities, the binary-mixture assumption and $M_A\ne M_N$. A density ratio cannot uniquely recover an arbitrary multicomponent <mixture>. Nor should $x$ be interpreted as the <argon> fraction of untreated air: the model sample has only the two specified components.

Choose round model values $M_N=28\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$ and $M_A=40\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$. For $x=0.010$, the calculated relative density excess is
$$
\boxed{r-1=0.010\left(\frac{40}{28}-1\right)
=0.00428571\ldots\simeq0.429\%.}
$$
Thus a one-percent <mole fraction> does not mean a one-percent <gas density> change. In a chosen $2.00\,\mathrm L$ globe at $100\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and $300\,\mathrm K$, the <ideal gas law> gives approximately $2.24508\,\mathrm g$ for pure <nitrogen> and $2.25471\,\mathrm g$ for this mixture: only $9.62\,\mathrm{mg}$ separates the weighings. These are model calculations with rounded <molar masses>, not historical measurements.

A direct control calculation explains why the filling conditions matter. At fixed <pressure>, pure <nitrogen> at
$$
T'=\frac{300\,\mathrm K}{1+0.00428571\ldots}
\simeq298.720\,\mathrm K
$$
has the same <gas density> as that mixture at $300\,\mathrm K$. A roughly $1.28\,\mathrm K$ mismatch can therefore imitate the entire stipulated composition signal. For small changes, the <ideal gas law> gives
$$
\frac{d\rho}{\rho}=\frac{dp}{p}+\frac{d\overline M}{\overline M}-\frac{dT}{T}.
$$
An independently measured <pressure> and <temperature>, matched weighing procedure, and checks for residual vapour distinguish composition from these alternative causes. The model explains the need for controls; it does not supply the complete corrections for a real gas-weighing instrument.

= Atomic number
{parent=Chemical element}
{title2=$Z$}
{wiki}

The <atomic number> $Z$ is the number of <protons> in an <atomic nucleus>, and fixes the <chemical element>. It is a count, with no units. An <atomic nucleus> with six <protons> is always <carbon>, regardless of how many <neutrons> accompany them or how many <electrons> surround it. See the https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/A00499[IUPAC Gold Book, atomic number].

Three independent bookkeeping questions become easy once their symbols are kept separate. Let $N$ count <neutrons>, $n_e$ count <electrons>, $A$ be the <mass number>, and $Q$ be the net <electric charge>. A <proton> carries <electric charge> $+e$ and an <electron> carries $-e$, where $e$ is the positive <elementary charge>; a <neutron> carries zero net <electric charge>. Hence
$$
A=Z+N,\qquad N=A-Z,\qquad Q=(Z-n_e)e,\qquad n_e=Z-Q/e.
$$
The reasoning is just counting: add <protons> and <neutrons> for $A$, and subtract negative <electron> contributions from positive <proton> contributions for $Q$. A neutral <chemical atom> has $Q=0$, so $n_e=Z$. For a positively charged <ion>, fewer <electrons> remain. Notation ${}^{A}_{Z}\mathrm X$ gives the <mass number> at upper left and <atomic number> at lower left, with $\mathrm X$ standing for the <chemical element>; a right superscript such as $+$ specifies the <electric charge> in units of $e$. See https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/2-3-atomic-structure-and-symbolism[Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, atomic structure and symbolism].

For neutral <carbon-12>, ${}^{12}_{6}\mathrm C$, the <atomic number> gives six <protons>, the subtraction $N=12-6=6$ gives six <neutrons>, and neutrality gives six <electrons>. Neutral <carbon-13>, ${}^{13}_{6}\mathrm C$, instead has $N=13-6=7$ <neutrons>, with the same six <protons> and six <electrons>. Thus these two <isotopes> occupy the same <carbon> box in the <periodic table>.

For neutral <sodium> of <mass number> $23$, ${}^{23}_{11}\mathrm{Na}$, the counts are eleven <protons>, $23-11=12$ <neutrons>, and eleven <electrons>. Removing one <electron> gives the <ion> ${}^{23}_{11}\mathrm{Na}^{+}$: $Q/e=1$, so $n_e=11-1=10$. Both its <atomic number> and its twelve <neutrons> stay unchanged. This is <ionization>, not conversion into the <chemical element> <neon>, even though a neutral <neon> <chemical atom> also has ten <electrons>.

This bookkeeping is useful whenever a symbol must be translated into a physical inventory. <Mass number> answers how many <protons> plus <neutrons> there are; <electric charge> answers the imbalance between <protons> and <electrons>; <atomic number> alone answers which <chemical element> it is. Keeping those questions distinct prevents an <isotope> or an <ion> from being mistaken for a different <chemical element>.

{wiki}

Three labels answer different questions. The <chemical element> fixes $Z$; the <isotope> specifies which <neutron number> accompanies that $Z$; the <ion> label specifies the net <electric charge>. = Nuclear mass defect
{parent=Atomic mass}

= Isotopic abundance
{parent=Isotope}
{title2=$f_i$}

An <isotopic abundance> is an atom fraction within a specified population of one <chemical element>.<Compendium of Chemical Terminology (Gold Book), second edition/isotopic abundance>{ref} If $n_i$ counts its <chemical atoms> of <isotope> $i$, use
$$
f_i=\frac{n_i}{\sum_j n_j},\qquad f_i\geq0,\qquad\sum_i f_i=1.
$$
The denominator includes every <isotope> of that <chemical element> included in the stated population, not every <chemical atom> of every <chemical element> in the sample. Thus an <oxygen> atom fraction among all <chemical elements> is a different quantity.

For an illustrative neutral <neon> population, take the displayed central <isotopic abundances> $f_{20}=0.9048$, $f_{21}=0.0027$, and $f_{22}=0.0925$ from the reference table, so $0.9048+0.0027+0.0925=1$. Its mean <atomic mass> is the <weighted arithmetic mean>
$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar m&=\sum_i f_i m_i\\
&=\bigl(0.9048\times19.9924401762+0.0027\times20.993846685+0.0925\times21.991385114\bigr)\,\mathrm u\\
&=20.18004638052026\,\mathrm u\approx20.1800\,\mathrm u.
\end{aligned}
$$
These are rounded reference inputs with uncertainties, not exact universal <isotopic abundances>; the long decimal shows their arithmetic result, not measurement precision.<Atomic Weights and Isotopic Compositions for Neon>{ref}

The population convention matters. If instead $w_i$ is the fraction of total neutral-atom <mass> supplied by <isotope> $i$, then $w_i=f_i m_i/\bar m$ and
$$
\bar m=\left(\sum_i\frac{w_i}{m_i}\right)^{-1}.
$$
This is a <weighted harmonic mean> in the <mass> fractions. Using $\sum_i w_i m_i$ with those <mass> fractions would answer a different question. The conversion follows directly by counting <chemical atoms> and adding their <masses>.

= Nearest-neighbour model of graphene
{parent=Graphene}
{title2=$E_\pm(\mathbf k)=\pm t|f(\mathbf k)|$}

Take a planar <honeycomb lattice> with two sites $A,B$ per primitive cell and one localized <atomic orbital> per site. In this explicitly idealized <tight-binding model>, all three nearest-neighbour hopping matrix elements are $-t$, with $t>0$; there is no on-site offset or further-neighbour hopping. Ignore <electron> interactions and treat physical <spin> as a separate degeneracy. Let the bond length be $b$, and choose the three vectors from an $A$ site to its neighbours as
$$
\boldsymbol\delta_1=b(0,1),\qquad
\boldsymbol\delta_2=b(\sqrt3/2,-1/2),\qquad
\boldsymbol\delta_3=b(-\sqrt3/2,-1/2).
$$
The <Bloch state> amplitudes on $A,B$ form a two-component <vector>. A consistent phase convention gives the <Hamiltonian>
$$
H(\mathbf k)=-t\begin{pmatrix}0&f(\mathbf k)\\f(\mathbf k)^*&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
f(\mathbf k)=\sum_{j=1}^3e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\delta_j}
=e^{ibk_y}+2e^{-ibk_y/2}\cos(\sqrt3bk_x/2).
$$
Its characteristic equation is $E^2=t^2|f|^2$, giving the two displayed <energy bands>. The two-component structure comes from the two sites, rather than two values of physical <spin>. This is a particularly small matrix model in which a crystal's geometry creates an unexpected low-energy law.

= Dirac valleys in graphene
{c}
{parent=Nearest-neighbour model of graphene}
{title2=$\mathbf K_\tau=(\tau4\pi/(3\sqrt3b),0),\quad\tau=\pm1$}

At either displayed <wave vector>, the three complex hopping amplitudes add to zero: $1+2\cos(2\pi/3)=0$. Therefore the <energy bands> touch. There are two inequivalent <electronic valleys>; other equivalent corners are related by reciprocal-lattice translations. Setting $\mathbf k=\mathbf K_\tau+\mathbf q$ and retaining first-order terms gives
$$
H_\tau(\mathbf q)\simeq\hbar v_F(\tau q_x\sigma_x+q_y\sigma_y),\qquad
v_F=\frac{3tb}{2\hbar},\qquad E_\pm\simeq\pm\hbar v_F|\mathbf q|,
$$
where $\sigma_x,\sigma_y$ are <Pauli matrices>. For example, at $\mathbf K_+$, $f\simeq-3b(q_x-iq_y)/2$. The matrix has the form of a massless two-dimensional <Dirac Hamiltonian>. Here $v_F$ is a material-model <velocity>, rather than the vacuum <speed of light>; this description does not change an <electron>'s vacuum <rest mass>. It describes the <dispersion relation> of electronic excitations near a band crossing.

= Sublattice pseudospin in graphene
{parent=Dirac valleys in graphene}
{title2=$\langle\boldsymbol\sigma\rangle=(\cos\phi,\sin\phi,0)$}

At $\mathbf K_+$ and positive <energy>, a normalized <eigenvector> for $\mathbf q=|\mathbf q|(\cos\phi,\sin\phi)$ is $(1,e^{i\phi})/\sqrt2$. Its <Pauli matrix> expectation values give the displayed direction. This <pseudospin> records the relative amplitudes and phase on the two sublattices. Real <spin> supplies another label; confusing these two labels would double-count states and misinterpret the two-band <Hamiltonian>.

= Carrier density in a graphene cone
{parent=Dirac valleys in graphene}
{title2=$n=\operatorname{sgn}(E_F)E_F^2/[\pi(\hbar v_F)^2]$}

Assume zero <temperature>, the linear <Dirac cone>, and both real-<spin> states and both <electronic valleys>. Define $n$ as excess <electron> number per unit <area>, negative for an excess of <electron holes>, relative to neutrality. A filled circular region of radius $k_F$ in <wave vector> space contains
$$
|n|=4\frac{\pi k_F^2}{(2\pi)^2}=\frac{k_F^2}{\pi},\qquad |E_F|=\hbar v_F k_F.
$$
Thus the excess <carrier density> grows quadratically with the <Fermi energy>, unlike the constant <density of states> of a parabolic two-dimensional band. Differentiating gives the positive <density of states> per unit <area>, $D(E)=2|E|/[\pi(\hbar v_F)^2]$. Both the geometry of occupied states and the fourfold degeneracy matter to the coefficient.

= Cyclotron mass in a graphene cone
{parent=Dirac valleys in graphene}
{title2=$m_c=|E_F|/v_F^2$}

A massless-cone description permits a nonzero <cyclotron effective mass>. For a circular constant-<energy> contour of <area> $S(E)=\pi E^2/(\hbar v_F)^2$, the semiclassical definition gives
$$
m_c=\frac{\hbar^2}{2\pi}\left|\frac{dS}{dE}\right|=\frac{|E_F|}{v_F^2}=\frac{\hbar\sqrt{\pi|n|}}{v_F}.
$$
The vanishing mass parameter in a <Dirac Hamiltonian> and the finite cyclotron response away from neutrality are different statements. This makes the <temperature> dependence of <Shubnikov-de Haas oscillations> a useful test of the dispersion rather than a measurement of an <electron>'s vacuum <rest mass>.

= Gate control of graphene carrier density
{parent=Graphene}
{title2=$V_g-V_0=en/c_g+E_F/e$}

Consider an ideal uniform <graphene> sheet connected to an <electron> reservoir, with an insulated gate. Let $c_g>0$ be geometric <capacitance> per unit <area>, $e>0$ the <elementary charge>, and $V_0$ the neutral-point offset. Neglect <temperature>, trapped <electric charge> and spatial inhomogeneity. A change in gate voltage pays for both the electrostatic potential drop, $en/c_g$, and the change in the sheet's <Fermi energy>, $E_F/e$. Here $E_F$ is measured relative to the band crossing; it is negative for excess <electron holes>.

Combine this relation with the <carrier density in a graphene cone>. Write $u=|E_F|/e$, measured in volts, $V=|V_g-V_0|$, and $\alpha=e^3/[\pi(\hbar v_F)^2c_g]$. Then
$$
V=u+\alpha u^2,\qquad u=\frac{2V}{1+\sqrt{1+4\alpha V}},\qquad
n=\operatorname{sgn}(V_g-V_0)\frac{e^2u^2}{\pi(\hbar v_F)^2}.
$$
The positive root is unique because $u+\alpha u^2$ is strictly increasing for $u\ge0$. The often useful geometric approximation $n\simeq c_g(V_g-V_0)/e$ neglects the <Fermi energy> term. It overestimates $|n|$ in this model, especially near neutrality where relatively few states are available.

For a deliberately chosen model with $c_g=0.010\,\mathrm{F/m^2}$, $v_F=10^6\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $V_0=0$ and $V_g=1\,\mathrm V$, the formulas give **$E_F\simeq0.252\,\mathrm{eV}$ and $n\simeq4.67\times10^{12}\,\mathrm{cm^{-2}}$**. The purely geometric value would be $6.24\times10^{12}\,\mathrm{cm^{-2}}$. These are chosen-model predictions, not measurements of the historical devices. Numerical conversion uses the exact <Planck constant> and <elementary charge>.<Fundamental Physical Constants — Complete Listing by National Institute of Standards and Technology and others>[Exact h and e entries; material parameters in this example are stipulated]{ref}

= Quantum capacitance of a graphene cone
{parent=Gate control of graphene carrier density}
{title2=$c_q=2e^2|E_F|/[\pi(\hbar v_F)^2]$}

The <quantum capacitance> per unit <area> is $c_q=e^2D(E_F)$. Differentiating the gate equation gives
$$
\frac{1}{c_{\rm diff}}=\frac{1}{c_g}+\frac{1}{c_q},\qquad
c_{\rm diff}=e\frac{dn}{dV_g}.
$$
Thus the available electronic states act as a second contribution in series with geometric <capacitance>. At exact neutrality the ideal zero-<temperature> model has $c_q=c_{\rm diff}=0$; actual finite-<temperature> or spatially nonuniform samples need a broader description. A capacitance measurement can therefore probe the <density of states>, rather than geometry alone.

= Helium atom
{parent=Helium}

A neutral <helium atom> contains two <electrons> bound to a <nucleus> of charge $+2e$. Even with a fixed <nucleus> and nonrelativistic kinetics, the repulsion $e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0r_{12})$ prevents exact separation into two hydrogenic one-electron problems. Its ground configuration is $1s^2$ with total <spin> zero, while distinct occupied orbitals allow both <singlet helium states> and <triplet helium states>.

= Helium metastability
{parent=Helium atom}

The low $1s2s$ levels of a <helium atom> lack an allowed direct electric dipole decay to the ground $1s^2\,{}^1S_0$ level. The triplet is excluded by the <spin selection rule>, and the singlet-to-singlet $S$ transition is excluded by <parity> and orbital selection. Weaker radiative channels and collisions can still de-excite these states; metastable does not mean infinitely lived.

= Singlet-triplet exchange splitting in helium
{parent=Helium atom}
{title2=$E_S-E_T=2K_{ab}$}

For fixed orthonormal orbitals $a,b$, symmetrizing or antisymmetrizing $a(1)b(2)$ gives electron-repulsion expectations $J_{ab}+K_{ab}$ and $J_{ab}-K_{ab}$. Antisymmetry of the complete electronic state therefore places the singlet above the triplet by $2K_{ab}$, with $K_{ab}$ the positive Coulomb <exchange integral>. Orbital relaxation and further correlation refine this comparison. Exchange splitting here is a consequence of <Fermi statistics> and the electrostatic interaction, not a separate classical spin-spin force.

= Ideal multisite mineral activity
{parent=Solid solution}
{title2=$a=X^q$}

For ideal independent mixing on crystallographic sublattices, an endmember’s <thermodynamic activity> is the <product> of its required site fractions, with multiplicities. If one component occupies $q$ equivalent mixing positions per formula unit and has site fraction $X$, then $a=X^q$, not generally $X$.

For a binary example, the mixing <Gibbs free energy> per mole of formula units is $qRT[X\ln X+(1-X)\ln(1-X)]$. Differentiating the total free energy with respect to the amount of the all-component endmember gives a mixing chemical-potential term $qRT\ln X$. Comparison with $RT\ln a$ proves $a=X^q$. This derivation assumes ideal mixing and equal site fractions; nonideal interactions and preferential site occupancy require a different model. Mg endmembers of <biotite> and <garnet> commonly use $X_{\mathrm{Mg}}^3$ in this simplified three-site approximation.

= Mixing on crystallographic sites
{parent=Solid solution}

An activity model in which distinct atomic positions in a <crystal structure> are occupied independently by specified species. For ideal random mixing, a pure <mineral endmember> requiring $\nu_s$ specified atoms on site type $s$ has <thermodynamic activity> $a=\prod_sX_s^{\nu_s}$ relative to that pure endmember, with other occupations held fixed. Its mixing contribution to the <chemical potential> is $RT\ln a$. The powers count occupied positions per formula unit; they are not always the mole fraction of whole endmember formulae.

= Tropospheric ozone
{parent=Ozone}

<Ozone> in the <troposphere> is a secondary chemical pollutant and a radiatively active gas. Its abundance reflects photochemical production, chemical loss, <dry deposition> and transport. = Ozone titration
{parent=Tropospheric ozone}

Local <ozone> consumption by the reaction $\mathrm{NO+O_3\to NO_2+O_2}$. Reducing fresh NO emissions can raise local measured <ozone> even while decreasing regional <ozone> production. This distinction matters when interpreting precursor-control experiments and observations.

= Photochemical ozone production
{parent=Tropospheric ozone}

Net daylight <ozone> production occurs when <peroxy radicals> oxidize NO to NO2 without using <ozone>, followed by NO2 <photodissociation> and oxygen-atom addition to O2. The competing reaction NO + O3 to NO2 + O2 only recycles odd <oxygen>. Radical initiation, termination, precursor loading and transport determine the net rate.

= VOC-limited ozone production
{parent=Photochemical ozone production}
{c}

A regime in which precursor/<radical> supply and NOx-dependent <radical termination> make net <ozone> production more responsive to additional reactive organic compounds than to additional <nitrogen oxides>. Reducing <nitrogen oxides> can initially raise <ozone> in some such conditions. Diagnose the chemical regime before extrapolating an emission reduction to every location.

= NOx-limited ozone production
{parent=Photochemical ozone production}
{c}

A regime in which additional available <nitrogen oxides> tends to increase net <photochemical ozone production> when other factors are fixed. Reducing <nitrogen oxides> generally suppresses that production, but local <ozone> <concentration> also depends on transport, deposition and NO titration.

= Ozone isopleth diagram
{parent=Photochemical ozone production}

A contour plot of a specified <ozone> outcome against the loadings of <nitrogen oxides> and <volatile organic compounds>. The ridge separating opposite NOx sensitivities distinguishes an <NOx-limited ozone-production regime> from a <VOC-limited ozone-production regime>. The outcome may be peak <concentration>, a time-integrated measure or a model production rate; axes and averaging conditions must be stated. A schematic diagram is not a calibrated emissions-response prediction.

= VOC-limited ozone-production regime
{parent=Photochemical ozone production}
{c}

A regime with abundant <nitrogen oxides> in which volatile-organic oxidation and radical supply limit net <tropospheric ozone> production. Reducing <volatile organic compounds> lowers production. A small NOx reduction can initially increase <ozone> by weakening OH termination and NO titration; sufficiently large reductions can cross into an <NOx-limited ozone-production regime>. Local <concentration> and regional emission sensitivities need not coincide.

= NOx-limited ozone-production regime
{parent=Photochemical ozone production}
{c}
{title2=$\mathrm{NO}_x$}

A regime in which additional <nitrogen oxides> promote net <tropospheric ozone> production because NO availability limits peroxy-radical oxidation of NO. Reducing NOx then generally lowers <ozone> production, subject to transport and chemical history. At much larger NOx burdens, OH removal by nitric-acid formation can instead produce a <VOC-limited ozone-production regime>.
