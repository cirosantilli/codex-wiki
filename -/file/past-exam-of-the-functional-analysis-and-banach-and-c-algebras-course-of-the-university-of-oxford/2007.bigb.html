= Solution
{parent=i}

The triangular kernel is nonnegative, has integral one, support in $[-\delta,\delta]$, and supremum $1/\delta$. Thus the <convolution> is defined at every point and
$$
\boxed{|(f*g)(t)|\leq\int|f(s)|g(t-s)ds\leq\|f\|_1/\delta.}
$$

= ii
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The kernel is globally Lipschitz with constant $1/\delta^2$: this holds on each of its linear pieces, and summing across the finitely many intervening breakpoints proves it across pieces as well. Consequently
$$
\boxed{|(f*g)(t_1)-(f*g)(t_2)|\leq\int|f(s)|\frac{|t_1-t_2|}{\delta^2}ds=\|f\|_1\frac{|t_1-t_2|}{\delta^2}.}
$$

= iii
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Since the kernel has integral one, The <Tonelli theorem> and the <triangle inequality> give
$$
\|f*g-f\|_1\leq\int g(s)\left(\int|f(t-s)-f(t)|dt\right)ds\leq\sup_{|h|\leq\delta}\|f(\cdot+h)-f\|_1.
$$
This proves **the stated <convolution> approximation estimate**.

For the unlabelled compactness assertion let $M$ bound the <norms> of $S$, and fix $\eta>0$. Choose the kernel width $\delta$ smaller than the translation radius supplied for $\eta$, so that the same bound holds for all shifts of size at most this width. The preceding estimate gives $\|f*g-f\|_1<\eta$ uniformly. The smoothed family has uniform bound $M/\delta$ and uniform Lipschitz constant $M/\delta^2$. Choose $K$ with all original tails outside $[-K,K]$ below $\eta$, and put $L=K+\delta$. The <Tonelli theorem> gives
$$
\int_{|t|\geq L}|(f*g)(t)|dt\leq\int g(s)\int_{|u|\geq K}|f(u)|du\,ds<\eta.
$$
On $[-L,L]$ the smoothed family is relatively compact in the uniform <norm> by the <Arzelà-Ascoli theorem>. Take a finite uniform net with error below $\eta/(2L)$ and extend its centers by zero outside that interval. It is a finite $L^1$ net for the smoothed-and-truncated family with error $\eta$. Each original $f$ is within another $2\eta$ of its corresponding truncated smoothing. Thus **$S$ is <totally bounded> in $L^1$**. The displayed (a),(b) are the two hypotheses used together in this argument.

For the final family, <Young convolution inequality> gives $\|f_0*f\|_1\leq\|f_0\|_1$. Its translations satisfy
$$
\|\tau_h(f_0*f)-f_0*f\|_1\leq\|\tau_hf_0-f_0\|_1\|f\|_1\leq\|\tau_hf_0-f_0\|_1,
$$
which tends to zero uniformly over the family. Since $f$ vanishes outside $[-1,1]$,
$$
\int_{|t|\geq K}|(f_0*f)(t)|dt\leq\|f\|_1\int_{|u|\geq K-1}|f_0(u)|du\longrightarrow0
$$
uniformly as well. Both compactness hypotheses apply, proving **$S_0$ is <totally bounded>**.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Regard the spaces as real and set $u=\operatorname{Re}\phi$. Its real <norm> equals $\|\phi\|$, since rotating any test vector makes its functional value real and nonnegative without changing its <norm>. By the allowed real <Hahn-Banach theorem> extend $u$ to a real-linear $v:X\to\mathbb R$ with that <norm>, and define $\psi(x)=v(x)-iv(ix)$. Real linearity gives $\psi(ix)=i\psi(x)$, so $\psi$ is complex linear. On $Y$ this formula recovers $\phi$. Also $\operatorname{Re}\psi=v$; rotating a test vector again gives $|\psi(x)|\leq\|v\|\|x\|$. The reverse <norm> inequality follows by restriction to $Y$, proving **a <norm>-preserving complex extension**.

We use two standard consequences: a point outside a <closed subspace> can be separated by a bounded functional annihilating that subspace, and the <norm> of a vector is the supremum of its pairings with dual unit-ball functionals. If $\psi\in X^*$ annihilates $Y$, the scalar <holomorphic function> $\psi\circ f$ vanishes at the prescribed sequence tending to zero. Its <identity theorem> makes it identically zero. Separation then gives **$f(z)\in Y$ for every $z\in D$**.

For $\phi\in Y^*$ extend to $X^*$ with the same <norm> and put $F=\phi\circ f$. Then $F$ is holomorphic with $|F|\leq\|\phi\|$. For the understood nonzero increments $h,k$, the <Cauchy integral formula> on $|\zeta|=r$ gives
$$
\frac{F(z+h)-F(z)}h-\frac{F(z+k)-F(z)}k=\frac{h-k}{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta|=r}\frac{F(\zeta)}{(\zeta-z)(\zeta-z-h)(\zeta-z-k)}d\zeta.
$$
Taking absolute values, bounding the contour length by $2\pi r$, and each denominator below by $r-|z|$, $r-|z|-|h|$, $r-|z|-|k|$ proves **exactly the stated estimate**.

Fix $z,r$ and restrict $|h|,|k|<(r-|z|)/2$. Taking the supremum over $\|\phi\|\leq1$ bounds the <norm> of the difference of the two $Y$-valued difference quotients by a fixed constant times $|h-k|$. They are therefore Cauchy as $h\to0$. Since $Y$ is a <closed subspace> of a <Banach space>, it is complete. Consequently **the derivative exists as a <norm> limit in $Y$**. The labelled (a)-(c) are the simultaneous hypotheses, not separate claims to prove.

= Solution
{parent=3}

A <linear operator> has closed graph when $\{(x,Tx):x\in X\}$ is closed in $X\times Y$. The <closed graph theorem> states that an everywhere-defined <linear operator> between <Banach spaces> is bounded if and only if it has closed graph. Boundedness gives the closed-graph property by continuity. Conversely a closed graph is Banach in the product <norm> $\|(x,y)\|=\|x\|+\|y\|$. Projection of the graph onto $X$ is a bounded linear bijection, so the <open mapping theorem> makes its inverse bounded. Composing that inverse with the second-coordinate projection proves **$T$ is bounded**.

For the composition assertion, suppose $x_n\to x$ and $Tx_n\to y$. Boundedness of $S$ gives $STx_n\to Sy$, and boundedness of $ST$ gives $STx_n\to STx$. Injectivity of $S$ forces $y=Tx$. Hence $T$ has closed graph and is bounded by the theorem.

For the derivative bound take $Y=C^1[0,1]$ with <norm> $\|v\|_{C^1}=\|v\|_\infty+\|v'\|_\infty$, and $Z=C[0,1]$ with its <supremum norm>; both are Banach. Regard $T(x)=Ux$ as a map into $Y$, and let $S:Y\to Z$ be inclusion. This inclusion is bounded and injective, and $ST=U$ is bounded by hypothesis. The preceding result gives a constant $c$ with $\|Ux\|_{C^1}\leq c\|x\|$. In particular **$|(Ux)'(t)|\leq c\|x\|$ uniformly in $t$**.

A <bounded linear operator> is compact when the closure of the image of its <unit ball> is compact. Equivalently every bounded sequence has a subsequence whose images converge; on a Banach target it also suffices that the image of its <unit ball> be <totally bounded>.

For a <finite-rank operator> that image is bounded in a finite-dimensional subspace, whose closed bounded sets are compact. Thus **every <finite-rank operator> is compact**.

Given a bounded sequence, first pass to a subsequence on which the images under $T_1$ converge. From that subsequence extract another on which the images under $T_2$ converge. Both image sequences, and hence their sum, converge on the latter subsequence. Thus **$T_1+T_2$ is compact**.

**Compact.** Its column indexed by $m$ has entries $n!/m!$ for $1\leq n<m$, and zero otherwise. The sum of that column is $c_m=(1!+\cdots+(m-1)!)/m!\leq2/m$ for $m\geq2$, by the elementary induction $1!+\cdots+k!\leq2k!$. For operators from $\ell^1$ the supremum of the absolute column sums is the <operator norm>: one inequality is the <triangle inequality>, and coordinate unit vectors give the reverse. Keeping just the first $N$ input columns is finite rank and its remainder <norm> is at most $\sup_{m>N}c_m\leq2/(N+1)$. Hence it converges to $T$ in <norm>, proving compactness.

A <C-star algebra> is a complex <Banach algebra> with a conjugate-linear <involution> satisfying $(ab)^*=b^*a^*$, $(a^*)^*=a$ and $\|a^*a\|=\|a\|^2$. It is unital when it has an identity for multiplication. For $B(H)$ use composition and the Hilbert adjoint. Completeness in the <operator norm> is given; composition is submultiplicative and the adjoint has the required algebraic properties. For a unit vector $\xi$,
$$
\|T\xi\|^2=\langle T^*T\xi,\xi\rangle\leq\|T^*T\|.
$$
Taking the supremum and using the opposite bound $\|T^*T\|\leq\|T^*\|\|T\|=\|T\|^2$ proves the <C-star identity>. Its identity is $I_H$, proving **$B(H)$ is a unital <C-star algebra>**.

<Compact operators> form a <norm>-closed linear space and are stable under composition with bounded operators. They are also adjoint stable. To see this directly, choose a finite $\varepsilon$-net of the compact image of the <unit ball> under $T$, and let $P$ project orthogonally onto the span of its centers. Then $\|(I-P)T\|\leq\varepsilon$. The <finite-rank operator> $T^*P$ approximates $T^*$ with the same error, so $T^*$ is compact by <norm> closure. Thus **$\mathcal K(H)$ is a <C-star algebra>**, with the inherited <norm> and operations.

If it has a multiplicative identity $E$, apply $ER=R$ to every <rank-one operator> with arbitrary prescribed range vector $\xi$. Evaluating that equation on a vector sent to $\xi$ gives $E\xi=\xi$. Hence $E=I_H$. This identity is compact exactly when $H$ is finite dimensional: finite dimension gives compactness of the <unit ball>, while in infinite dimension an infinite <orthonormal sequence> has pairwise distance $\sqrt2$ and no convergent subsequence. Therefore **$\mathcal K(H)$ is unital exactly in finite dimension**, with the zero-algebra convention understood if $H=0$.

Use the source's notation for the vanishing-at-zero ideal $I=\{f\in C[0,1]:f(0)=0\}$. It is the kernel of bounded evaluation at zero, hence closed. It is stable under pointwise multiplication and <complex conjugation>, so inherits the <C-star identity> and completeness. If it had an identity $e$, multiplication against $f(t)=t$ would give $e(t)=1$ for $t>0$, contradicting continuity and $e(0)=0$. Thus **$I$ is a non-unital <C-star subalgebra>**. This explicit meaning of the notation matters because the usual notation $C_0$ for a compact space has a different convention.

Define the <unitization> map by $\Phi(f,\lambda)=f+\lambda\mathbf1$. Its inverse is $h\mapsto(h-h(0),h(0))$. The specified multiplication and <involution> are preserved by direct substitution. The pullback <norm> $\|(f,\lambda)\|=\|f+\lambda\mathbf1\|_\infty$ is a C-star <norm>, so equals the unique <unitization> <norm> supplied in the question. Therefore **$\Phi$ is a unital isometric <C-star isomorphism> onto $C[0,1]$**.

For the approximation put $h(s)=a(\sqrt s)$ on $[0,1]$, so $h$ is continuous and $h(0)=0$. The <Stone-Weierstrass theorem> supplies a <polynomial> $p(s)$ with $\|h-p\|_\infty<\varepsilon/2$. Then $b(t)=p(t^2)-p(0)$ is a <polynomial> in the positive even powers of $t$, with zero constant term, and
$$
\boxed{\|a-b\|_\infty\leq\|h-p\|_\infty+|p(0)|<\varepsilon.}
$$
This gives exactly the required finite form.

The <continuous functional calculus> for a <Hermitian element of a C-star algebra> identifies its generated unital <C-star algebra> with <continuous functions> on its real spectrum, preserves the <involution> and <norm>, and sends functions to the corresponding spectral values by spectral mapping. Consequently an element is a square of a <Hermitian element of a C-star algebra> exactly when it is a <Hermitian element of a C-star algebra> with nonnegative spectrum: the reverse direction uses the continuous square root on that spectrum.

Nonnegative scalar multiples preserve this property directly. For positive $a,b$ put $C=\|a\|+\|b\|$. Functional calculus gives $\|\|a\|1-a\|\leq\|a\|$ and similarly for $b$, so $\|C1-(a+b)\|\leq C$. The sum is a <Hermitian element of a C-star algebra>, and spectral mapping now places its spectrum in $[0,2C]$. It therefore has a <positive square root in a C-star algebra>. If $C=0$ both elements are zero. Thus **$A_+$ is a cone**. It is pointed because an element positive with its negative has spectrum contained in $\{0\}$ and hence is zero by the self-adjoint <norm> formula. It is closed as well: for a converging sequence of <positive elements of a C-star algebra> use a common <norm> bound $C$ and pass to the limit in $\|C1-a_n\|\leq C$.

For the two-by-two <matrix> the exact criterion is
$$
\boxed{a_{11},a_{22}\in[0,\infty),\qquad a_{21}=\overline{a_{12}},\qquad a_{11}a_{22}\geq|a_{12}|^2.}
$$
A positive <matrix> is Hermitian with nonnegative <eigenvalues>; evaluating its quadratic form at the coordinate vectors gives the diagonal conditions, and its <determinant>, the <eigenvalue> product, gives the final inequality. Conversely if $a_{11}>0$, completing the square writes the quadratic form as $a_{11}|z_1+(a_{12}/a_{11})z_2|^2+(a_{22}-|a_{12}|^2/a_{11})|z_2|^2$, which is nonnegative. If $a_{11}=0$, the <determinant> inequality forces $a_{12}=0$ and the conclusion is immediate. The <spectral theorem> then gives nonnegative <eigenvalues> and their Hermitian square root, proving positivity in the question's sense.

A <state on a C-star algebra> is a <positive linear functional> of <norm> one. A <cyclic representation of a C-star algebra> is a representation of a <C-star algebra> $\pi:A\to B(H)$ with a vector $\xi$ whose orbit $\pi(A)\xi$ has dense linear span in $H$. On $c$, pointwise multiplication and coordinatewise <complex conjugation> preserve convergence; the constant sequence one is the unit. The <supremum norm> is submultiplicative and obeys $\|\alpha^*\alpha\|_\infty=\|\alpha\|_\infty^2$. Completeness is given, so **$c$ is a commutative unital <C-star algebra>**.

If $F=\phi(\beta)$ is a state, testing its positivity on the coordinate projections gives $\beta_{j+1}\geq0$. To recover the independent coefficient at infinity, let $q_N$ have its first $N$ coordinates zero and all later ones one. This is a positive element of $c$, and $F(q_N)=\beta_1+\sum_{j>N}\beta_{j+1}\geq0$. Passing to the limit gives $\beta_1\geq0$. By the given isometry, $\|F\|=\|\beta\|_1=\sum_j\beta_j$, which is one. Conversely these nonnegative unit-sum coefficients make $F(\alpha^*\alpha)$ a sum of nonnegative weighted squares, and the isometry gives <norm> one. Thus **the stated coefficient conditions are necessary and sufficient**.

For the specified state, all other coefficients are zero because they are nonnegative and the first three already sum to one. Its GNS null ideal is
$$
N_x=\{\alpha:x(\alpha^*\alpha)=0\}=\{\alpha:\alpha_\infty=\alpha_1=\alpha_2=0\}.
$$
Evaluation at infinity and the first two coordinates identifies $c/N_x$ with $\mathbb C^3$, with <inner product> $\tfrac12u_\infty\overline{v_\infty}+\tfrac14u_1\overline{v_1}+\tfrac14u_2\overline{v_2}$. Left multiplication induces the diagonal representation; the class of the unit is $(1,1,1)$. In the equivalent standard <inner product> the resulting GNS triple is
$$
\boxed{H_x=\mathbb C^3,\quad\pi_x(\alpha)=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha_\infty,\alpha_1,\alpha_2),\quad\xi_x=(1/\sqrt2,1/2,1/2).}
$$
Its coefficient is exactly $\tfrac12\alpha_\infty+\tfrac14\alpha_1+\tfrac14\alpha_2=F(\alpha)$. The three evaluations can be assigned arbitrary independent values by a convergent sequence, and all three entries of $\xi_x$ are nonzero. Therefore its orbit spans $\mathbb C^3$, verifying **cyclicity and the required state identity** directly.
