= Solution
{parent=i}

The <quotient norm> is
$$
\boxed{\|x+Y\|_{X/Y}=\inf_{y\in Y}\|x-y\|.}
$$
Changing the representative does not change the infimum. The <triangle inequality> and absolute homogeneity follow from those of the original <norm>. Vanishing means $x$ belongs to the closure of $Y$, hence to $Y$ because the subspace is closed. Thus this is a <norm>.

Use the absolutely convergent series criterion for completeness. If $\sum\|\xi_n\|_{X/Y}<\infty$, choose representatives $z_n$ of $\xi_n$ satisfying $\|z_n\|\leq\|\xi_n\|_{X/Y}+2^{-n}$. The series $\sum z_n$ converges in the <Banach space> $X$ to some $z$. Since the <quotient map> is contractive, $\sum\xi_n$ converges to $z+Y$. Consequently **the quotient is a <Banach space>**.

A set is <totally bounded> if for every $\varepsilon>0$ it can be covered by finitely many open balls of radius $\varepsilon$.

Here is the needed separated-sequence argument, including the relevant <Riesz lemma>. For a proper <closed subspace> $Y$, choose $z\notin Y$, put $d=\operatorname{dist}(z,Y)>0$, and choose $y\in Y$ with $\|z-y\|<2d$. Then $w=(z-y)/\|z-y\|$ is a <unit vector> at distance greater than $1/2$ from $Y$. Starting with one <unit vector>, apply this to the span of the vectors already chosen. These spans are closed and proper because they are finite-dimensional while $X$ is infinite-dimensional. The resulting unit vectors satisfy $\|w_m-w_n\|>1/2$ for $m\ne n$. A ball of radius $1/4$ contains at most one of them. Hence **the <closed unit ball> is not <totally bounded>**.

Take $S=T(B_X)$. A <bounded linear operator> is compact exactly when the closure of this set is compact. In a <complete metric space> this is equivalent to its being <totally bounded>.

If $T$ is compact, choose a finite $\varepsilon$-net $z_1,\ldots,z_m$ for $T(B_X)$ and let $Y$ be their span. Then $\operatorname{dist}(Tx,Y)<\varepsilon$ for $\|x\|\leq1$, giving $\|\pi_YT\|\leq\varepsilon$.

Conversely, fix $\delta>0$ and take $Y$ with $\|\pi_YT\|\leq\delta/4$. For every $x\in B_X$ choose $y_x\in Y$ with $\|Tx-y_x\|<\delta/2$. These approximants satisfy $\|y_x\|\leq\|T\|+\delta/2$, so form a bounded subset of a finite-dimensional space and have a finite $\delta/2$-net. The same centers give a finite $\delta$-net for $T(B_X)$. Thus it is <totally bounded> and $T$ is compact. In particular
$$
\boxed{T\text{ compact}\iff\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\dim Y<\infty:\ \|\pi_YT\|\leq\varepsilon.}
$$

The natural <canonical embedding into the bidual> is $J_Xx(f)=f(x)$. It is linear and $\|J_Xx\|\leq\|x\|$. A norming functional from the <Hahn-Banach theorem> gives the reverse inequality, so it is an <isometry>. A <reflexive Banach space> is one for which this canonical embedding is onto.

**A nonreflexive example is $c_0$.** Its dual is $\ell^1$ and its bidual is $\ell^\infty$; the canonical map is the natural inclusion, which misses the constant sequence one. This also shows why merely having an isometric embedding in the bidual is insufficient.

= Solution
{parent=b}

**An infinite-dimensional reflexive example is $\ell^2$**, by the <Riesz representation theorem>.

The <weak topology> on $X$ is the coarsest topology making every $f\in X^*$ continuous. The <weak-star topology> on $X^*$ is the coarsest making every evaluation $f\mapsto f(x)$, $x\in X$, continuous. In each case basic neighborhoods impose finitely many evaluation inequalities. The <Banach-Alaoglu theorem> states that the <closed unit ball> of a dual space is compact for its <weak-star topology>. The separation theorem needed here says: if $K$ is a nonempty weak-star compact <convex> subset of a dual space $E^*$ and $z\notin K$, then some $e\in E$ strictly separates them,
$$
\operatorname{Re}z(e)>\sup_{k\in K}\operatorname{Re}k(e).
$$
The real-part convention covers complex spaces; for real spaces omit it.

Let $K$ be the weak-star closure of $J_X(B_X)$ inside $B_{X^{**}}$. This is compact by Banach-Alaoglu, <convex>, and nonempty. If $z\in B_{X^{**}}\setminus K$, separation in the dual pair $(X^{**},X^*)$ gives $f\in X^*$ with
$$
\operatorname{Re}z(f)>\sup_{x\in B_X}\operatorname{Re}f(x)=\|f\|.
$$
But $|z(f)|\leq\|z\|\|f\|\leq\|f\|$, a contradiction. Therefore $K=B_{X^{**}}$, proving the required <Goldstine theorem> density.

If $X$ is reflexive, $J_X$ identifies its <weak topology> with the <weak-star topology> of $X^{**}$ and carries $B_X$ onto $B_{X^{**}}$, so Banach-Alaoglu gives weak compactness. Conversely, if $B_X$ is weakly compact, its image is weak-star compact, hence closed in the <Hausdorff> bidual topology. Density then forces $J_X(B_X)=B_{X^{**}}$. Scaling gives surjectivity on the entire bidual. Thus
$$
\boxed{B_X\text{ weakly compact}\iff X\text{ reflexive}.}
$$

The <Baire category theorem> says that a nonempty <complete metric space> is not a countable union of closed sets with empty interior; equivalently, every countable intersection of open dense sets is dense. To prove the latter, start with any nonempty open set $V$ and open dense sets $G_n$. Choose closed balls of positive radii with
$$
\overline B_1\subset V\cap G_1,\qquad\overline B_{n+1}\subset B_n\cap G_{n+1},\qquad r_n<2^{-n}.
$$
Each choice is possible by density and openness. Their centers form a <Cauchy sequence>; its limit exists by completeness and belongs to every closed ball, hence to $V\cap\bigcap G_n$. This proves density and the theorem.

The <uniform boundedness principle> says that a pointwise bounded family $\mathcal T$ of bounded linear maps from a <Banach space> $X$ to a <normed space> $Z$ has uniformly bounded operator norms. Put $E_m=\{x:\sup_{T\in\mathcal T}\|Tx\|\leq m\}$. These sets are closed and cover $X$. Baire gives an open ball $B(x_0,r)\subset E_m$. For $\|h\|<r$, the <triangle inequality> applied to $T(x_0+h)-Tx_0$ gives $\|Th\|\leq2m$. Taking $h=(r/2)u$ for unit $u$ proves $\sup_T\|T\|\leq4m/r$.

For the particular expansion, uniqueness makes all coefficient maps and partial-sum maps linear. Since $P_kx\to x$, the norms $\|P_kx\|$ form a bounded sequence with limit $\|x\|$. Also, with $P_0=0$,
$$
|\varphi_n(x)|=\|\varphi_n(x)x_n\|=\|(P_n-P_{n-1})x\|\leq2\sup_k\|P_kx\|.
$$
This proves both preliminary bounds. The supremum therefore defines a finite <norm> $\|\cdot\|'$, with $\|x\|\leq\|x\|'$.

To prove **(i) implies (ii)**, the identity map from $(X,\|\cdot\|')$ to $(X,\|\cdot\|)$ is a bounded linear bijection. If (i) holds both spaces are Banach, so the <bounded inverse theorem> makes its inverse bounded: $\|x\|'\leq C\|x\|$. This is precisely (ii).

**(ii) implies (i):** together with the preliminary estimate, (ii) gives <equivalent norms>,
$$
\|x\|\leq\|x\|'\leq C\|x\|.

An equivalent <norm> on a <Banach space> is complete: a primed-<norm> <Cauchy sequence> has an original-<norm> limit, and the upper bound makes convergence hold also in the primed <norm>. **(ii) implies (iii)** immediately because $\|P_k\|\leq C$ for every $k$.

**(v) implies (iv).** Fix $n$ and consider any sequence $y_r\to y$ with $\varphi_n(y_r)\to\gamma$. Apply (v) to $y_r-y$ and $\beta=\gamma-\varphi_n(y)$. It gives $\gamma=\varphi_n(y)$. **(vi) implies (iv)** without assuming beforehand that any coordinate functional is continuous. Define the everywhere-defined <linear map>
$$
\Phi:X\to\ell^\infty,\qquad\Phi(x)=(\varphi_n(x))_{n\geq1}.
$$
The preliminary coordinate bound ensures it really takes values in $\ell^\infty$. Condition (vi) states exactly that its graph is sequentially closed: $y_r\to x$ and $\Phi(y_r)\to\beta$ in the supremum <norm> imply $\Phi(x)=\beta$. The product is a metric space, so sequentially closed means closed. The <closed graph theorem> therefore makes $\Phi$ bounded. Every coordinate projection on $\ell^\infty$ is bounded, hence so is each $\varphi_n$.

Together with the preceding implications this proves **all six conditions are equivalent**. No continuity of the coordinate functionals was assumed during the preliminary bounds.

A <character of an algebra> is a nonzero <multiplicative> complex-<linear map> $\chi:A\to\mathbb C$. A maximal proper ideal is a proper ideal contained in no larger proper ideal. For a character, $\chi(1)=1$ and $\chi(a)\in\sigma(a)$: otherwise $a-\chi(a)1$ would be invertible although its character value is zero. The spectral bound gives $|\chi(a)|\leq\|a\|$, so the character is continuous. It is onto, and its kernel is a closed <maximal ideal> because $A/\ker\chi\cong\mathbb C$.

Conversely let $M$ be maximal. Its closure is an ideal and remains proper: otherwise an element of $M$ would lie within distance one of the unit, hence would be invertible by the <Neumann series>, forcing $1\in M$. Maximality then gives $\overline M=M$. The <Banach algebra> $A/M$ is a field. For any $b$ in this quotient choose $\lambda\in\sigma(b)$, using nonemptiness of the complex spectrum. In a field only zero is noninvertible, so $b-\lambda1=0$. Hence every quotient element is a scalar multiple of the unit. The <quotient map> therefore determines a character $\chi_M$, uniquely normalized by $\chi_M(1)=1$. These constructions are inverse:
$$
\boxed{\Delta(A)\longleftrightarrow M(A),\qquad\chi\longmapsto\ker\chi.}
$$
For the convolution algebra, write $e_j$ for the sequence supported at $j$ with value one. Then $e_j e_k=e_{j+k}$, $e_0=1$, and $e_1e_{-1}=1$. If $z=\chi(e_1)$, continuity and $\|e_{\pm1}\|_1=1$ imply $|z|\leq1$ and $|z^{-1}|\leq1$. Thus $|z|=1$, so uniquely $z=e^{it}$ for $0\leq t<2\pi$, and $\chi(e_j)=e^{ijt}$ for every integer $j$. <Absolute convergence> in $\ell^1$ now gives
$$
\boxed{\chi_t(\alpha)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z}\alpha_je^{ijt}.}
$$
Conversely each displayed map is bounded, nonzero, and <multiplicative>, since the absolutely convergent double sum can be rearranged along $k+l=j$. Thus these are all characters, and all maximal proper ideals are
$$
\boxed{M_t=\left\{\alpha\in\ell^1(\mathbb Z):\sum_j\alpha_je^{ijt}=0\right\},\qquad0\leq t<2\pi.}
$$
They are distinct because the character is uniquely recovered from its kernel and its value on the unit.

An element is a <positive element of a C-star algebra> if it is <self-adjoint> and its spectrum lies in $[0,\infty)$, equivalently if $a=b^*b$ for some $b$.

For a normal $a$, the <continuous functional calculus> is the unique unital isometric *-isomorphism $C(\sigma(a))\to C^*(1,a)$ sending the coordinate function $z$ to $a$. It respects sums, products, conjugation and the unit, satisfies $\|f(a)\|=\max_{\sigma(a)}|f|$, and has $\sigma(f(a))=f(\sigma(a))$. For <self-adjoint> $a$ it preserves the order of real-valued functions.

The piecewise definition of $g_n$ is consistent for $n\geq2$. **At $n=1$ the printed branches conflict**, even assigning both zero and one at $t=1/2$. All the requested limit and face arguments use only $n\geq2$; discarding this first index resolves that harmless initial-index defect without altering any limit.

For $n\geq2$, interpolate linearly from $(1/(2n),0)$ to $(1/n,g(1/n))$, and from $(1-1/n,g(1-1/n))$ to $(1-1/(2n),1)$. On the middle interval the error is zero. On the left both $g$ and $g_n$ lie between zero and $g(1/n)$; on the right both lie between $g(1-1/n)$ and one. Thus
$$
\|g-g_n\|_\infty\leq\max\{g(1/n),1-g(1-1/n)\}\longrightarrow0
$$
by continuity at the endpoints.

Put $\delta=1/(2n)$. To prove the bounds on $h_n$, it suffices to show $0\leq t-\delta g_n(t)\leq1-\delta$. For $t\leq\delta$, $g_n=0$; for $t\geq1-\delta$, $g_n=1$. On the remaining interval, $\delta\leq t\leq1-\delta$ and $0\leq g_n\leq1$, so both inequalities follow. Consequently **$g_n$ and $h_n$ are positive contractions**.

For the face assertion, write $r=\|a\|$. Since $a\in[0,1_A]$ and $0\notin F$, $0<r\leq1$. If $r<1$, then $a=r(a/r)+(1-r)0$, with both endpoints in $[0,1_A]$. The face property would force $0\in F$, a contradiction. Thus $\|a\|=1$. Its spectrum contains one by positivity and the <norm> formula, and contains zero because it is noninvertible.

Applying functional calculus to the scalar identity gives
$$
a=\delta g_n(a)+(1-\delta)h_n(a),\qquad g_n(a),h_n(a)\in[0,1_A].
$$
Since $0<\delta<1$ and $a\in F$, the face property gives $g_n(a)\in F$. <Isometry> of functional calculus gives $g_n(a)\to g(a)$ in <norm>; closedness of $F$ therefore proves
$$
\boxed{\|a\|=1,\qquad g(a)\in F.}
$$

= Solution

For a <bounded linear functional> on a unital C*-algebra, the state criterion is
$$
\boxed{x(1_A)=\|x\|=1.}
$$
Equivalently it is positive and takes the unit to one.

In the <Gelfand-Naimark-Segal construction>, set $N_x=\{b:x(b^*b)=0\}$. This is a <left ideal>. Give $A/N_x$ the <inner product> $\langle[b],[c]\rangle=x(c^*b)$, linear in the first variable, and complete it to a <Hilbert space> $H_x$. Left multiplication gives the bounded *-representation $\pi_x(a)[b]=[ab]$. The vector $\xi=[1_A]$ is cyclic and satisfies $x(a)=\langle\pi_x(a)\xi,\xi\rangle$.

The Hermitian matrix $\rho$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_\pm=1/2\pm|\alpha|$ and <trace> one. In an <orthonormal eigenbasis> $v_+,v_-$,
$$
x_\rho(a)=\lambda_+\langle av_+,v_+\rangle+\lambda_-\langle av_-,v_-\rangle.
$$
If $|\alpha|\leq1/2$, both weights are nonnegative and sum to one, so this functional is positive and normalized, hence a state. If $|\alpha|>1/2$, evaluating on the positive <rank>-one projection onto the negative-<eigenvalue> eigenspace gives a negative value, so it is not a state. Therefore
$$
\boxed{x_\rho\text{ is a state}\iff|\alpha|\leq1/2.}
$$
To obtain the representation dimension, cyclicity of <trace> gives
$$
x_\rho(b^*b)=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho b^*b)=\|b\rho^{1/2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2.
$$
Thus $A/N_{x_\rho}$ identifies isometrically with the matrix space $\{b\rho^{1/2}:b\in M_2(\mathbb C)\}$, with left multiplication as the representation. If $r=\operatorname{rank}\rho$, this space consists, after a <unitary> change of basis, of arbitrary first $r$ columns and zero remaining columns. Its complex dimension is $2r$. It is already complete because it is finite-dimensional. <Rank> is one at $|\alpha|=1/2$ and two at $|\alpha|<1/2$, proving
$$
\boxed{\dim H_{x_\rho}=2\text{ at }|\alpha|=1/2,\qquad\dim H_{x_\rho}=4\text{ at }|\alpha|<1/2.}
$$
