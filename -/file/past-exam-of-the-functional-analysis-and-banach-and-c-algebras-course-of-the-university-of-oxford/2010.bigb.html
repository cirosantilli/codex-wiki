= Solution
{parent=a}

An <isomorphic embedding> of <normed vector spaces> is a <bounded linear operator> that is <injective> and whose <inverse function> on its <image> is bounded. Equivalently, **$T$ is bounded below**: there is $c>0$ such that
$$
\boxed{c\|x\|\leq\|Tx\|\quad(x\in X).}
$$
Indeed this inequality gives <injectivity> and $\|T^{-1}z\|\leq c^{-1}\|z\|$ for $z\in T(X)$; the converse follows by applying the boundedness of $T^{-1}$ to $Tx$. Together with $\|Tx\|\leq\|T\|\|x\|$, it makes $T$ an <isomorphic embedding> onto its <image> with the inherited <norm>. The <image> is necessarily closed when $X$ is a <Banach space>; that extra conclusion requires completeness.

A <quotient operator> is a <surjective> <bounded linear operator> that is an <open map>. For a <linear map>, being an <open map> is equivalent to the existence of $c>0$ such that
$$
B_Y(0,c)\subseteq T(B_X(0,1)).
$$
The inclusion gives a neighbourhood of $Tx$ inside the <image> of every sufficiently small ball about $x$, by translation and scaling. Conversely, an <open map> sends $B_X(0,1)$ to a neighbourhood of zero. A linear <open map> onto $Y$ is automatically <surjective>, since its <image> contains a ball and is a <linear subspace>.

The <Banach-space adjoint> is $T^*g=g\circ T$. For $g\in Y^*$, the ball inclusion gives
$$
\|T^*g\|=\sup_{\|x\|<1}|g(Tx)|\geq\sup_{\|y\|<c}|g(y)|=c\|g\|.
$$
Also $\|T^*g\|\leq\|T\|\|g\|$. Thus **the <Banach-space adjoint> is an <isomorphic embedding>**, with lower bound $c$.

We use <successive approximation for quotient operators>. Fix $y$ with $\|y\|<1$. The hypothesis gives $x_0$ with $\|x_0\|<1$ and $\|y-Tx_0\|<\delta$. Inductively, if
$$
r_n=y-T\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}x_k,\qquad\|r_n\|<\delta^n,
$$
apply the hypothesis to $r_n/\delta^n$, obtaining $x_n$ with $\|x_n\|<\delta^n$ and $\|r_n-Tx_n\|<\delta^{n+1}$. If a residual is zero, take all remaining terms zero.

The <series> $\sum x_n$ converges in the <Banach space> $X$ because $\sum\|x_n\|<\sum\delta^n=(1-\delta)^{-1}$. Write its sum as $x$. The <continuity> of $T$ and the residual estimate give
$$
Tx=y,\qquad\|x\|<(1-\delta)^{-1}.
$$
Scaling now yields
$$
\boxed{B_Y(0,1-\delta)\subseteq T(B_X(0,1)).}
$$
This ball inclusion proves that **$T$ is a <quotient operator>**. Completeness of $Y$ was unnecessary: the convergent <series> is constructed in $X$ and its <image> has limit $y$.

The real <Hahn-Banach separation theorem> says that if $C$ is a nonempty open <convex set> in a real <normed vector space> and $y\notin C$, there is a nonzero <continuous linear functional> $g$ such that $g(y)\geq\sup_{z\in C}g(z)$.

Put $\delta=1-1/M\in(0,1)$. The prescribed set is open because it is a union of translates of an open ball, and it is <convex> because both summands are <convex>. If $\|y\|<1$ and $y\notin C$, <Hahn-Banach separation> gives a nonzero $g\in Y^*$ with
$$
g(y)\geq\sup_{z\in C}g(z)=\|T^*g\|+\delta\|g\|\geq(M^{-1}+\delta)\|g\|=\|g\|.
$$
The displayed supremum is a sum of independent suprema; the symmetry of the unit balls identifies them with the respective <operator norms>. But $g(y)\leq\|g\|\|y\|<\|g\|$, a contradiction. Hence $y=Tx+r$ for some $\|x\|\leq1$ and $\|r\|<\delta$.

To obtain the strict bound on $x$ required in the approximation hypothesis, choose $t<1$ sufficiently close to $1$. Then $\|tx\|<1$ and
$$
\|T(tx)-y\|\leq\|r\|+(1-t)\|Tx\|<\delta.
$$
Thus the approximation condition holds with exactly $\delta=1-1/M$. When $X$ is a <Banach space>, the preceding <successive approximation for quotient operators> proves $T$ is a <quotient operator>. Combining this with the <Banach-space adjoint> estimate proves
$$
\boxed{T\text{ is a quotient operator}\iff T^*\text{ is an isomorphic embedding}.}
$$

The <Baire category theorem> states that a nonempty complete <metric space> cannot be a countable union of closed <nowhere dense sets>; equivalently, every countable intersection of open dense sets is dense.

Let $E_n=\operatorname{span}\{e_1,\ldots,e_n\}$ in the space of <finitely supported sequences>. Each $E_n$ is a closed <linear subspace> for every <norm>, since a <finite-dimensional subspace is closed>. Hence
$$
F_n=T^{-1}(E_n)
$$
is a closed <linear subspace> of the <Banach space> $X$. Every <finitely supported sequence> lies in some $E_n$, so $X=\bigcup_{n\geq1}F_n$. By the <Baire category theorem>, some $F_N$ has nonempty <interior>.

A <linear subspace> with nonempty <interior> is the whole space: translate a ball in it to zero and scale any vector into that ball. Thus $F_N=X$, giving $T(X)\subseteq E_N$ and
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{rank}T\leq N<\infty.}
$$
The proof uses no particular choice of <norm> on the space of <finitely supported sequences>.

Under <duality of sequence spaces>, $a=(a_n)\in\ell_1$ represents $f_a(x)=\sum a_nx_n$ on $c_0$, with <operator norm> $\|f_a\|=\sum|a_n|$. Here $c_0$ is the <space of sequences converging to zero>, equipped with its supremum <norm>.

If $\|x\|_\infty\leq1$ and $f_a(x)=\sum|a_n|$, then
$$
0=\sum_{n\geq1}(|a_n|-a_nx_n).
$$
Every summand is nonnegative, so $x_n=\operatorname{sgn}(a_n)$ whenever $a_n\ne0$. Infinitely many such coordinates would give infinitely many $|x_n|=1$, contradicting $x_n\to0$. Therefore $a$ has finite support.

Conversely, if $a$ has finite support, choose $x_n=\operatorname{sgn}(a_n)$ on that support and $x_n=0$ elsewhere. Then $x\in c_0$, $\|x\|_\infty\leq1$, and $f_a(x)=\|f_a\|$. The zero functional is included by taking $x=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{f_a\text{ attains its norm on }B_{c_0}\iff a\in c_{00}.}
$$
This is <norm attainment on the vanishing sequence space>.

The compact set $S(B_{c_0})$ is bounded, so $S$ is a <bounded linear operator>. For each $g\in Y^*$, the <continuous linear functional> $g$ attains its maximum on that <compact set>. Symmetry of $S(B_{c_0})$ implies that maximum is $\|S^*g\|$, so $S^*g$ attains its <norm> on $B_{c_0}$. By <norm attainment on the vanishing sequence space>,
$$
S^*(Y^*)\subseteq c_{00}\subset\ell_1.
$$
Regard the <Banach-space adjoint> $S^*$ as a <bounded linear operator> from the <Banach space> $Y^*$ into $c_{00}$ with its inherited $\ell_1$ <norm>. The <completeness of the dual space> holds even if $Y$ is incomplete. The preceding <Baire category theorem> argument shows that $S^*$ has finite rank.

If $S^*(Y^*)=\operatorname{span}\{f_1,\ldots,f_r\}$, set $N=\bigcap_{j=1}^r\ker f_j$. For $x\in N$ and every $g\in Y^*$, $g(Sx)=(S^*g)(x)=0$. The <Hahn-Banach theorem> implies that <continuous linear functionals> separate points of $Y$, so $Sx=0$. Thus $N\subseteq\ker S$, and
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{rank}S\leq\operatorname{codim}N\leq r<\infty.}
$$
This proves <compact closed-ball image forces finite rank on c0>.

Define a <diagonal operator on sequence space> by
$$
\boxed{S:c_0\longrightarrow\ell_2,\qquad Sx=(2^{-n}x_n)_{n\geq1}.}
$$
It is bounded because $\|Sx\|_2^2\leq\|x\|_\infty^2\sum_{n\geq1}4^{-n}=\|x\|_\infty^2/3$. Its truncation $S_mx=(2^{-1}x_1,\ldots,2^{-m}x_m,0,\ldots)$ is a <finite-rank operator>, and
$$
\|S-S_m\|=\left(\sum_{n>m}4^{-n}\right)^{1/2}=\frac{2^{-m}}{\sqrt3}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Equality follows by using <finitely supported sequences> equal to $1$ on longer and longer finite tails. An <operator norm> limit of <compact operators> into the <Banach space> $\ell_2$ is compact; therefore **$S$ is a <compact operator>**. The vectors $Se_n=2^{-n}e_n$ are <linearly independent>, so its rank is infinite.

There is no conflict with the preceding result about a compact closed-ball <image>. Indeed, take $x^{(m)}=(1,\ldots,1,0,\ldots)$ with $m$ initial ones. Then $Sx^{(m)}\to(2^{-n})_{n\geq1}$ in $\ell_2$, but that limit has no preimage in $c_0$: its only possible preimage is the constant sequence $1$. Thus $S(B_{c_0})$ is relatively compact but is not closed and hence is not compact.

The <weak topology> $\sigma(X,X^*)$ is the coarsest <topology> making every $f\in X^*$ continuous. Its basic neighbourhoods impose finitely many inequalities $|f_j(x-x_0)|<\varepsilon$. The <weak-star topology> $\sigma(X^*,X)$ is the coarsest <topology> making each evaluation $f\mapsto f(x)$ continuous, for $x\in X$.

The <canonical embedding into the bidual> is
$$
J_Xx\in X^{**},\qquad(J_Xx)(f)=f(x).
$$
It is linear and $\|J_Xx\|\leq\|x\|$. For $x\ne0$, the <Hahn-Banach theorem> supplies a <norming functional> $f$ with $\|f\|=1$ and $f(x)=\|x\|$, proving equality. Thus $J_X$ is an <isometric embedding>. The coordinates defining the <weak-star topology> on $X^{**}$, restricted to $J_X(X)$, are precisely $J_Xx\mapsto f(x)$ for $f\in X^*$. These are exactly the coordinates defining the <weak topology> on $X$, so $J_X$ is also a <topological embedding> for these <topologies>.

A <normed vector space> is a <reflexive space> if $J_X$ is onto $X^{**}$. Such a space is automatically a <Banach space>, since it is isometric to the complete <continuous dual space> $X^{**}$. The <Banach-Alaoglu theorem> states that the closed unit ball of a <continuous dual space> is compact for its <weak-star topology>. The <Goldstine theorem> states that $J_X(B_X)$ is dense in the <weak-star topology> in $B_{X^{**}}$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{X\text{ is reflexive}\iff B_X\text{ is weakly compact}.}
$$
If $J_X$ is onto, its weak-to-weak-star <homeomorphism> carries $B_X$ onto the <Banach-Alaoglu theorem>'s compact ball $B_{X^{**}}$. Conversely, if $B_X$ is <weakly compact>, $J_X(B_X)$ is compact and hence closed in the <Hausdorff> <weak-star topology>. Its density from the <Goldstine theorem> makes it all of $B_{X^{**}}$, and scaling gives surjectivity of $J_X$.

Now suppose $X$ is separable and nonzero. Choose a countable dense set $(u_n)$ in its unit sphere and, using the <Hahn-Banach theorem>, choose <norming functionals> $f_n$ with $\|f_n\|=1$ and $f_n(u_n)=1$. These <continuous linear functionals> separate points: for $x\ne0$, choose $\|u_n-x/\|x\|\|<1/2$ to get $f_n(x)>\|x\|/2$. Define
$$
d(x,x')=\sum_{n\geq1}\min\{2^{-n},|f_n(x-x')|\}.
$$
The <series> converges uniformly, separates distinct points, and obeys the <triangle inequality> because $\min(c,a+b)\leq\min(c,a)+\min(c,b)$. Thus $d$ is a <metric>. Its <topology> is coarser than the <weak topology>: a finite set of coordinate controls makes the finite initial sum small, while the remaining sum is at most $\sum_{n>N}2^{-n}=2^{-N}$. On a <weakly compact set> $K$, the identity $(K,\text{weak})\to(K,d)$ is therefore a continuous bijection from a <compact space> into a <Hausdorff space>, hence a <homeomorphism>. This proves that **$K$ is metrizable in its <weak topology>**, an instance of <countable separating family metrizes a weakly compact set>.

If $X$ is also a <reflexive space>, its closed unit ball is <weakly compact> and therefore metrizable by this construction. Compactness in a <metric space> implies <sequential compactness>, so every sequence in $B_X$ has a <weakly convergent> subsequence. Scaling proves the claim for every bounded sequence.

For the converse, assume every bounded sequence in $X$ has a <weakly convergent> subsequence. Use the same <metric> $d$ on $B_X$. A <weak limit> of points of $B_X$ remains in $B_X$: $|f(x)|\leq1$ for every $\|f\|\leq1$, and the <Hahn-Banach theorem> recovers $\|x\|\leq1$. Thus every sequence in $B_X$ has a subsequence convergent in $d$ to a point of $B_X$, so $(B_X,d)$ is sequentially compact and hence compact.

We must still recover the entire <weak topology>, rather than just the selected coordinates. Suppose $x_n\to x$ in $d$ but not weakly. There is a neighbourhood in the <weak topology> $U$ of $x$ and a subsequence outside $U$. By hypothesis that subsequence has a further subsequence converging weakly to $y\in B_X$. It then converges in $d$ to $y$, so uniqueness of <metric> limits gives $y=x$, contradicting eventual membership in $U$. Hence convergence in $d$ implies <weak convergence>. Every $f\in X^*$ is therefore sequentially continuous on $(B_X,d)$ and hence continuous, since the domain is a <metric space>. The <weak topology> and $d$-topology on $B_X$ coincide. The ball is <weakly compact>, so the criterion above proves
$$
\boxed{X\text{ separable}:\quad X\text{ reflexive}\iff\text{every bounded sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence}.}
$$
The zero space satisfies all statements directly. The converse did not assume completeness, nor use the full <Eberlein-Šmulian theorem>.

A unital <C-star algebra> is a complex <Banach algebra> $A$ with a multiplicative identity $1_A$ and a conjugate-linear <involution> $a\mapsto a^*$ satisfying $(ab)^*=b^*a^*$ and the <C-star identity> $\|a^*a\|=\|a\|^2$. A <C-star isomorphism> $\Phi:A\to B$ is a bijective complex-linear map preserving products and the <involution>. It carries $1_A$ to $1_B$ and is automatically isometric.

For a commutative unital <C-star algebra>, let $\Omega$ be its <maximal ideal space>, equivalently the space of <Gelfand characters>, with the <weak-star topology>. A <Gelfand character> has $\chi(1)=1$ and $\|\chi\|=1$. The equations $\chi(ab)=\chi(a)\chi(b)$ and $\chi(1)=1$ define a closed in the <weak-star topology> subset of the dual unit ball. The <Banach-Alaoglu theorem> makes $\Omega$ a <compact Hausdorff space>. Maximal ideals give characters by the <Gelfand-Mazur theorem>, and the <Banach algebra spectrum> satisfies $\sigma(a)=\{\chi(a):\chi\in\Omega\}$.

The <Gelfand transform> is $\widehat a(\chi)=\chi(a)$. It preserves products, constants and the <involution>; the latter uses the fact that <characters of a C-star algebra respect the involution>. Every element of a commutative <C-star algebra> is normal, so the <C-star identity> and the <spectral radius formula> give
$$
\|\widehat a\|_\infty=\sup_{\chi\in\Omega}|\chi(a)|=r(a)=\|a\|.
$$
Thus its <image> is a closed self-adjoint subalgebra of $C(\Omega)$, contains the constants, and separates characters. The complex <Stone-Weierstrass theorem> makes that <image> dense and therefore all of $C(\Omega)$. This is the <commutative Gelfand--Naimark theorem>.

For the <convergent sequence space> $c$, coordinatewise multiplication satisfies $\|\alpha\beta\|_\infty\leq\|\alpha\|_\infty\|\beta\|_\infty$, and coordinatewise complex conjugation is an <involution>. The constant sequence $1$ is the identity, and
$$
\|\alpha^*\alpha\|_\infty=\sup_n|\alpha_n|^2=\|\alpha\|_\infty^2.
$$
It is complete: a uniform limit of convergent sequences is convergent, so $c$ is a closed <linear subspace> of the <Banach space> $\ell_\infty$. Hence it is a commutative unital <C-star algebra>.

Take
$$
\boxed{\Omega=\mathbb N\cup\{\infty\},\quad\text{the one-point compactification of discrete }\mathbb N.}
$$
Every finite point is isolated, and the neighbourhoods of $\infty$ contain all but finitely many integers. This is a <compact Hausdorff space>: an open cover has a set containing $\infty$, leaving only finitely many points to cover. Define
$$
\Phi(\alpha)(n)=\alpha_n,\qquad\Phi(\alpha)(\infty)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha_n.
$$
Continuity at $\infty$ is exactly convergence of the sequence. Thus $\Phi$ is onto $C(\Omega)$, and pointwise operations show it is a <C-star isomorphism> with $\|\Phi\alpha\|_\infty=\|\alpha\|_\infty$. Explicitly, these are all <Gelfand characters>: each $\chi(e_n)$ is $0$ or $1$ and at most one is $1$. If $\chi(e_n)=1$, multiplication by $e_n$ gives $\chi(\alpha)=\alpha_n$. If all vanish, <density of finitely supported sequences in l-p> in $c_0$ gives $\chi|_{c_0}=0$, so $\chi(\alpha)=\lim\alpha_n$.

For a <self-adjoint C-star element> $a$, the following are three equivalent characterizations of a <positive element of a C-star algebra>:
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
&\sigma(a)\subseteq[0,\infty);\\
&\|a-\|a\|1_A\|\leq\|a\|;\\
&\text{there is }t>0\text{ with }\|a-t1_A\|\leq t.
\end{aligned}}
$$
If $a=b^2$ with $b=b^*$, the <spectral mapping theorem> gives nonnegative spectrum. Conversely, the <continuous functional calculus> applied to $\sqrt{\lambda}$ on $\sigma(a)$ gives a <self-adjoint C-star element> $b$ with $b^2=a$. For real $\sigma(a)$,
$$
\|a-t1_A\|=\max_{\lambda\in\sigma(a)}|\lambda-t|.
$$
Thus the third condition means $\sigma(a)\subseteq[0,2t]$, while the first implies the second by taking the interval $[0,\|a\|]$. The second implies the third with $t=\|a\|$ if $a\ne0$; if $a=0$, any $t>0$ works. This proves every equivalence.

The <positive cone of a C-star algebra> contains zero and is closed under multiplication by nonnegative real scalars. To verify addition without assuming that positive elements commute, put $r=\|a\|$, $s=\|b\|$ for $a,b\geq0$. Then
$$
\|(a+b)-(r+s)1_A\|\leq\|a-r1_A\|+\|b-s1_A\|\leq r+s.
$$
If $r+s>0$, the third characterization makes $a+b$ positive; otherwise both are zero. Hence it is a <convex cone>. If $a$ and $-a$ are positive, $\sigma(a)=\{0\}$ and $a=0$ by the <self-adjoint> spectral <norm> formula; therefore the cone is pointed and defines a <partial order> on $A_{\mathrm{sa}}$ by $h\leq k$ if $k-h\in A_+$. It is generating because the <continuous functional calculus> gives
$$
|h|=(h^2)^{1/2},\qquad h_\pm=\tfrac12(|h|\pm h)\geq0,\qquad h=h_+-h_-.
$$
For $a\geq0$, the <spectral mapping theorem> yields
$$
\boxed{\|a\|\leq1\iff\sigma(a)\subseteq[0,1]\iff1_A-a\geq0\iff0\leq a\leq1_A.}
$$
Finally, let $a\in M_2(\mathbb C)$ be <Hermitian>, and write $\tau=\operatorname{tr}a$, $d=\det a$. Its two <eigenvalues> are real. Both are nonnegative exactly when their sum $\tau$ and product $d$ are nonnegative. The corresponding sum and product for $I-a$ are $2-\tau$ and $1-\tau+d$. Therefore the necessary and sufficient conditions for a <positive contraction> are
$$
\boxed{0\leq\tau\leq2,\qquad d\geq0,\qquad1-\tau+d\geq0.}
$$
Indeed, nonnegative sum and product force two real numbers to be nonnegative, including the zero-product cases. Applying this to $a$ and $I-a$ places both <eigenvalues> in $[0,1]$.

Let $c=x(1_A)\geq0$. Positivity on $a^*a$ implies positivity on every <positive element of a C-star algebra>, by taking its positive <square root>. For a <self-adjoint C-star element> $h$, the <positive cone of a C-star algebra> gives
$$
-\|h\|1_A\leq h\leq\|h\|1_A.
$$
Both $x(\|h\|1_A+h)$ and $x(\|h\|1_A-h)$ are nonnegative real numbers. Their difference shows $x(h)$ is real, and the same inequalities give $|x(h)|\leq c\|h\|$.

For an arbitrary $a\in A$, write $a=h+ik$ with $h=(a+a^*)/2$ and $k=(a-a^*)/(2i)$ self-adjoint. Since the <involution> is isometric, $\|h\|,\|k\|\leq\|a\|$. Hence
$$
|x(a)|\leq|x(h)|+|x(k)|\leq2c\|a\|.
$$
Thus **every <positive functional on a C-star algebra> is bounded**, with the preliminary bound $\|x\|\leq2x(1_A)$. This proof does not presuppose the <Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for positive C-star functionals>. It also gives $x(a^*)=\overline{x(a)}$, since $x(h)$ and $x(k)$ are real.

Set $A_0=x(a^*a)$, $B_0=x(b^*b)$ and $c=x(a^*b)$. The preceding reality argument gives $x(b^*a)=\bar c$. Positivity therefore implies, for every $\lambda\in\mathbb C$,
$$
0\leq x((b+\lambda a)^*(b+\lambda a))=B_0+\lambda\bar c+\bar\lambda c+|\lambda|^2A_0.
$$
If $A_0>0$, choose $\lambda=-c/A_0$ to obtain $0\leq B_0-|c|^2/A_0$. If $A_0=0$ and $c\ne0$, choosing $\lambda=-tc$ and letting $t\to\infty$ contradicts positivity. Thus $c=0$ in that case too. This proves the <Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for positive C-star functionals>:
$$
\boxed{|x(a^*b)|^2\leq x(a^*a)x(b^*b).}
$$
Apply it with $a=1_A$, $b=z$. Since $0\leq z^*z\leq\|z\|^21_A$, positivity gives
$$
|x(z)|^2\leq x(1_A)x(z^*z)\leq x(1_A)^2\|z\|^2.
$$
Thus $\|x\|\leq x(1_A)$, while evaluation on $1_A$ gives the reverse inequality. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\|x\|=x(1_A).}
$$
The zero functional is covered as well. In the trivial zero algebra all assertions are immediate, and its state space is empty.

For a nonzero unital <C-star algebra>, the set $S_A$ of <state on a C-star algebra> functionals is <convex>, since both positivity and evaluation $x(1_A)=1$ are preserved by <convex combinations>. The <norm> identity places it in the closed dual unit ball. Suppose a state satisfies $x=ty+(1-t)z$, with $0<t<1$ and $\|y\|,\|z\|\leq1$. Evaluating at the unit and taking real parts gives
$$
1=t\operatorname{Re}y(1_A)+(1-t)\operatorname{Re}z(1_A)\leq t+(1-t)=1.
$$
Since both weights are strictly positive, both real parts equal $1$. Also $|y(1_A)|,|z(1_A)|\leq1$, so $y(1_A)=z(1_A)=1$. Hence
$$
1=|y(1_A)|\leq\|y\|\leq1,\qquad1=|z(1_A)|\leq\|z\|\leq1.
$$
The stipulated converse to the <norm> identity makes $y,z$ positive, so both belong to $S_A$. Therefore **$S_A$ is a <face of a convex set>, namely the closed dual unit ball**. More precisely it is the <exposed face of the dual ball defined by the unit>, since it is cut out by $\operatorname{Re}x(1_A)=1$.
