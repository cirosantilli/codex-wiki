=== 1

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

A <complex derivative> computed along the real and imaginary directions gives $f'=u_x+iv_x=v_y-iu_y$. Equality yields the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>
$$
\boxed{u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x.}
$$
The <gradients> satisfy $\nabla u\cdot\nabla v=u_xv_x+u_yv_y=0$. Since a <gradient> is normal to a regular <level set>, the two families meet at right angles by <orthogonality> wherever $f'\ne0$. At a <critical point> the regular-curve argument fails: for $f=z^2$ both <gradients> vanish at zero, so the assertion requires this qualification.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Set $A=x^2-y^2$ and $B=2xy$. Direct <differentiation> gives
$$
u_{xx}=e^{-B}[(4y^2-4x^2)\sin A+(2-8xy)\cos A],\qquad
u_{yy}=e^{-B}[(4x^2-4y^2)\sin A+(8xy-2)\cos A].
$$
Their sum vanishes, proving that $u$ is a <harmonic function>. More generally the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> give $u_{xx}+u_{yy}=v_{yx}-v_{xy}=0$ and $v_{xx}+v_{yy}=-u_{yx}+u_{xy}=0$; the <mixed partial derivatives> commute because a <holomorphic function> is smooth. Since $e^{iz^2}=e^{-B}(\cos A+i\sin A)$, a <harmonic conjugate> and the resulting <holomorphic function> are
$$
\boxed{v=-e^{-2xy}\cos(x^2-y^2)+C,\qquad f(z)=-ie^{iz^2}+iC,\quad C\in\mathbb R.}
$$
These expressions also verify the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> directly.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

The <chain rule> in <polar coordinates> gives $u_r=u_x\cos\theta+u_y\sin\theta$ and $u_\theta=r(-u_x\sin\theta+u_y\cos\theta)$, with analogous expressions for $v$. Substituting the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> gives
$$
\boxed{ru_r=v_\theta,\qquad rv_r=-u_\theta,\quad r>0.}
$$
The restriction excludes the coordinate singularity at the origin.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

Choose a continuous <branch of a multivalued function> of the <complex logarithm> on a simply connected region avoiding zero. Then
$$
g=e^{-\theta}e^{i\ln r}=e^{i\operatorname{Log}z},\qquad
\boxed{g'(z)=\frac{i}{z}g(z).}
$$
Alternatively $u_r=-e^{-\theta}\sin(\ln r)/r=v_\theta/r$ and $v_r=e^{-\theta}\cos(\ln r)/r=-u_\theta/r$, verifying the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>. The <holomorphy> is local to a chosen <branch of a multivalued function>: increasing $\theta$ by $2\pi$ multiplies $g$ by $e^{-2\pi}$, so this is not a single-valued <holomorphic function> on the whole punctured plane.

=== 2
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

For $n\ge1$, $f=z^ne^{n/z}$ is <holomorphic> at every finite nonzero point. At zero its <Laurent series> has infinitely many negative powers, so zero is an <essential singularity>. If the <Riemann sphere> is included, $f(1/w)=w^{-n}e^{nw}$ has a <pole> of order $n$ at $w=0$, so infinity is a <pole> of order $n$.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Expanding the <exponential function> produces the <Laurent series>
$$
\boxed{f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{n^k}{k!}z^{n-k},\qquad 0<|z|<\infty.}
$$
In particular its <residue> is $a_{-1}=n^{n+1}/(n+1)!$.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

For a positively oriented circle of any radius $R>0$, the <residue theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\oint_{|z|=R}f(z)\,dz=2\pi i\frac{n^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}.}
$$
Only the $z^{-1}$ coefficient in the <Laurent series> contributes. Reversing the <orientation> changes the sign.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

If $f(z)=\sum a_jz^j$, then $f(-z)=\sum(-1)^ja_jz^j$ and its <residue> at zero is $-a_{-1}$. The circle $|z-1|=2$ encloses zero and no other finite <singularity>. Thus the <residue theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\oint_{|z-1|=2}[f(z)-f(-z)]\,dz=4\pi i\frac{n^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}}
$$
for positive <orientation>.

=== 3
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

<Cauchy's integral theorem> states that a <holomorphic function> on a simply connected open region has zero <contour integral> around every closed piecewise smooth <contour> in that region. More generally the same holds for a <contour> homologous to zero in its domain of <holomorphy>. Having no singularity on the curve alone is insufficient: $1/z$ has a nonzero <contour integral> around the unit circle.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $z=e^{i\theta}$, so $d\theta=dz/(iz)$, $\cos2\theta=(z^2+z^{-2})/2$ and $\cos^23\theta=(z^6+2+z^{-6})/4$. The <contour integral> is
$$
\frac1{4i}\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{z^8+2z^2+z^{-4}}{z(5z^2-2z^4-2)}\,dz.
$$
The enclosed <poles> are $0$ and $\pm1/\sqrt2$. For the rational function displayed without the prefactor $1/(4i)$, the <residue> at zero is $-21/8$ and the two other <residues> sum to $27/8$. Therefore the <residue theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\cos^23\theta}{5-4\cos2\theta}\,d\theta=\frac1{4i}(2\pi i)\frac34=\frac{3\pi}{8}.}
$$
A useful independent check is the <Fourier series> $(5-4\cos\phi)^{-1}=\frac13[1+2\sum_{k\ge1}2^{-k}\cos k\phi]$; only its constant and third <Fourier coefficients> survive.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

The square root produces <branch points> at $z=\pm1$, with local behaviour proportional to $(z\mp1)^{-1/2}$. One <branch cut> can join them along $[-1,1]$. Another choice uses the two rays $(-\infty,-1]\cup[1,\infty)$. Each choice prevents a loop from enclosing just one <branch point> within a single-valued domain. For the finite-segment choice fix $\sqrt{z^2-1}\sim z$ at infinity. Infinity is then a <pole> of order three, not a <branch point>, since
$$
f(z)=z^3+\frac12z+\frac{3}{8z}+O(z^{-3}).
$$

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the <branch cut> $[-1,1]$ and the <branch of a multivalued function> normalized by $\sqrt{z^2-1}\sim z$. The <Laurent series> from the preceding solution has $z^{-1}$ coefficient $3/8$. Consequently every sufficiently large positively oriented circle gives
$$
\boxed{\oint_{C_\infty}f(z)\,dz=\frac{3\pi i}{4}.}
$$
Although $f$ grows cubically, its other <Laurent series> terms have zero <contour integral>. Choosing the opposite <branch of a multivalued function> reverses the sign; neither the <branch of a multivalued function> nor the <orientation> can be omitted from the answer.

==== e
{scope}

===== Solution

Shrink the positive outer <contour> to the <branch cut> $[-1,1]$. On its upper bank the chosen <branch of a multivalued function> has $\sqrt{x^2-1}=i\sqrt{1-x^2}$ and on the lower bank it has the negative of this value. The upper bank runs from $1$ to $-1$, and the lower bank runs from $-1$ to $1$. The endpoint circles contribute $O(\sqrt\epsilon)$ and vanish. Hence
$$
\frac{3\pi i}{4}=2i\int_{-1}^1\frac{x^4}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx,\qquad
\boxed{\int_{-1}^1\frac{x^4}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx=\frac{3\pi}{8}.}
$$
The <change of variables> $x=\sin t$ independently reduces the result to the <integral> of $\sin^4t$ over $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$.

==== a

===== Solution

<Kepler's third law> for two <point masses> in a <Newtonian gravitational field> is
$$
\boxed{P^2=\frac{4\pi^2a^3}{G(M_1+M_2)}.}
$$
Here $a$ is the <semimajor axis> of their relative <orbit>, $P$ the <orbital period> and $G$ the <gravitational constant>. Equivalently $(P/\mathrm{yr})^2=(a/\mathrm{AU})^3/[(M_1+M_2)/M_\odot]$, to the accuracy with which these astronomical units normalize the <solar mass>.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Assume a circular <orbit> and an <orbital inclination> $i\simeq90^\circ$, as appropriate for a simple <eclipsing binary> estimate. At quadrature the two <radial velocities> are opposite and equal to the <orbital speeds>. The more massive component has the smaller amplitude. With the <centre of mass> as origin,
$$
a_1=\frac{K_1P}{2\pi},\quad a_2=\frac{K_2P}{2\pi},\quad a=a_1+a_2,\quad \frac{M_1}{M_2}=\frac{K_2}{K_1}=2.
$$
Thus $a_1=0.1103\,\mathrm{AU}$, $a_2=0.2206\,\mathrm{AU}$ and <Kepler's third law> gives
$$
\boxed{a=0.3309\,\mathrm{AU},\quad M_1=8.06M_\odot,\quad M_2=4.03M_\odot.}
$$
The <orbital plane> contains the observer's line of sight; at quadrature the two <velocities> point towards and away from that observer, as labelled in the original schematic below. For an <elliptic orbit>, the measured amplitudes instead give $a\sin i=P(K_1+K_2)\sqrt{1-e^2}/(2\pi)$; the numerical answer requires the circular assumption.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Adopt the approximate <mass-luminosity relation> $L/L_\odot\simeq(M/M_\odot)^3$ and equal available fuel fractions. The <main-sequence lifetime> then scales as $M/L$, giving
$$
\boxed{t_1\simeq1.54\times10^8\,\mathrm{yr},\qquad t_2\simeq6.16\times10^8\,\mathrm{yr}.}
$$
Using an exponent $3.5$ instead gives about $5.4\times10^7$ and $3.1\times10^8$ years; these are model-dependent estimates. At $r=3r_s=6GM/c^2$ the <Newtonian gravitational potential energy> released from infinity is $GMm/r=mc^2/6$. Half remains as <kinetic energy> in a circular <orbit>, so the radiatable <binding energy> is $GMm/(2r)$:
$$
\boxed{\eta_{\rm Newtonian}=\frac1{12}\simeq8.3\%.}
$$
Using the whole <potential energy> loss would give $1/6$, which does not subtract the final <kinetic energy>. A fully relativistic calculation for a nonrotating <black hole> gives $1-\sqrt{8/9}\simeq5.7\%$. When the surviving star exhausts <hydrogen burning>, it expands off the <main sequence> and can fill its <Roche lobe>. <Binary mass transfer> to the <black hole> can form an <accretion disk> and an <X-ray binary>; unstable <binary mass transfer> may instead produce a <common envelope>. This is conditional on the <binary star> remaining bound after the first remnant forms. The supplied <black hole> endpoint is an assumption, not a universal outcome for an isolated $8M_\odot$ star.

{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

A thin spherical shell has outward <pressure force> $-4\pi r^2(dP/dr)dr$ and inward <gravitational force> $GM(r)\,4\pi r^2\rho(r)dr/r^2$. <Hydrostatic equilibrium> requires
$$
\boxed{\frac{dP}{dr}=-\frac{GM(r)\rho(r)}{r^2},\qquad \frac{dM}{dr}=4\pi r^2\rho(r).}
$$
A uniform-<density> <solar model> gives $M(r)=M_\odot(r/R_\odot)^3$, $\bar\rho=3M_\odot/(4\pi R_\odot^3)$, and after <integration> from $R_\odot$ to zero,
$$
P_c=\frac{3GM_\odot^2}{8\pi R_\odot^4}=1.34\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{Pa},\qquad
T_c=\frac{P_c\mu m_p}{\bar\rho k_B}=6.9\times10^6\,\mathrm K\quad(\mu=0.6).
$$
Thus the appropriate orders of magnitude are $10^{14}$–$10^{16}\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and $10^7\,\mathrm K$. The uniform-<density> approximation underestimates the actual <central pressure> because the Sun is centrally concentrated. <Radiation pressure> is neglected in applying the <ideal gas law>.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

A <free-fall timescale> estimate follows from $R/t^2\sim GM/R^2$:
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm dyn}\sim\sqrt{\frac{R_\odot^3}{GM_\odot}}\simeq1.6\times10^3\,\mathrm s.}
$$
Falling to a central <point mass> gives the more precise factor $\pi/(2\sqrt2)$, or $1.77\times10^3\,\mathrm s$; a distributed star changes an order-one coefficient. For any spherical <mass distribution> with radius $R$,
$$
|\Omega|=\int_0^M\frac{Gm}{r(m)}\,dm\ge\frac GR\int_0^M m\,dm=\frac{GM^2}{2R}.
$$
The inequality is strict for an extended star; equality is approached by a thin shell at $R$. For an <monatomic gas> the <virial theorem> is $2U+\Omega=0$, so half the <gravitational potential energy> released by contraction heats the star and half can be radiated. The <Kelvin-Helmholtz cooling time> is therefore of order
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm KH}\sim\frac{GM_\odot^2}{2R_\odot L_\odot}\simeq1.6\times10^7\,\mathrm{yr}.}
$$
A uniform-<density> model gives $3GM^2/(10RL)\simeq9.4\times10^6\,\mathrm{yr}$. The given bound fixes a scale, rather than an exact contraction history; both estimates are far shorter than the Sun's <main-sequence lifetime>.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

The right panel of the figure in <past-exam-of-the-short-options-paper-of-the-physics-prelims-of-the-university-of-oxford/2019/s2/1/b/solution> has <luminosity> increasing upwards and <effective temperature> decreasing to the right. An old <globular cluster> contains a low-mass <main sequence>, a <main-sequence turnoff>, <subgiants>, a <red giant branch>, a <horizontal branch>, an <asymptotic giant branch> and a faint hot <white dwarf> sequence. Its high-mass <main sequence> is absent because those stars have evolved.

If a fraction $f_c=0.1$ of the star participates in <hydrogen burning>, with initial <hydrogen mass fraction> $X=0.7$, the available <nuclear binding energy> is $0.007f_cXM c^2$. Assuming a roughly constant <luminosity> and coeval stars,
$$
\boxed{t\simeq\frac{0.007f_cX(0.8M_\odot)c^2}{1.6\times10^{26}\,\mathrm W}\simeq1.39\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{yr}.}
$$
Taking the quoted core fraction as hydrogen fuel itself, $f_cX=0.1$, gives $1.98\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{yr}$. Stating the fuel convention matters: burning the whole stellar mass overestimates the age by about an order of magnitude. Evolving <luminosity> and detailed <stellar structure> are needed for an accurate age.

=== 3
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

The <Milky Way> has a thin stellar <galactic disk>, radius roughly $15$–$20\,\mathrm{kpc}$ and scale height about $0.3\,\mathrm{kpc}$, containing gas, dust, young and old stars, <spiral arms> and most current <star formation>. Its stellar mass is a few $10^{10}M_\odot$. A thicker, older <galactic disk> has scale height around $1\,\mathrm{kpc}$ and stellar mass of order $10^{10}M_\odot$, with little cold gas.

The central <galactic bulge> and bar extend a few thousand <parsecs> and contain about $10^{10}M_\odot$, largely older stars. The central <supermassive black hole> has mass about $4\times10^6M_\odot$ and is negligible in the total galactic budget. A diffuse old metal-poor <stellar halo>, extending tens to over $100\,\mathrm{kpc}$, contains <globular clusters> and roughly $10^9M_\odot$ in stars. An extended <dark matter halo> reaches roughly $200\,\mathrm{kpc}$ and dominates the total mass, of order $10^{12}M_\odot$. These are approximate component scales; the gaseous <galactic halo> also extends beyond the stellar <galactic disk>.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Treat the <gravitational field> as approximately spherical. The <circular orbit> condition gives
$$
\boxed{M(<8\,\mathrm{kpc})=\frac{v_c^2r}{G}=9.0\times10^{10}M_\odot,\qquad \Upsilon=\frac{M/M_\odot}{L/L_\odot}=4.50.}
$$
If every star had a representative mass $m$ and obeyed $L/L_\odot=(m/M_\odot)^3$, its <mass-to-light ratio> would be $(m/M_\odot)^{-2}$, giving $\boxed{m\simeq0.47M_\odot}$. This is a one-mass estimate, not an actual average inferred uniquely from the data: a <galactic stellar mass function>, stellar remnants, dust and nonstellar mass alter the relation. The flattening of the <galactic disk> also changes the spherical conversion by an order-one factor.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Assume an isotropic, stationary spherical <galaxy cluster>. Its three-dimensional <velocity dispersion> obeys $\langle v^2\rangle=3\sigma_{\rm los}^2$. For a uniform sphere, the <virial theorem> gives $3M\sigma_{\rm los}^2=3GM^2/(5R)$, hence
$$
\boxed{M_{\rm dyn}\simeq\frac{5R\sigma_{\rm los}^2}{G}=9.4\times10^{14}M_\odot.}
$$
A different <density profile> changes the coefficient, so $10^{15}M_\odot$ is the meaningful scale. For isothermal <X-ray> gas in <hydrostatic equilibrium>,
$$
M(r)=-\frac{k_BTr}{G\mu m_p}\frac{d\ln\rho_g}{d\ln r}.
$$
Using $\mu=0.6$ and a representative slope $-d\ln\rho_g/d\ln r=2$–$3$ at $1\,\mathrm{Mpc}$ gives $M_X\simeq(1.3$–$1.9)\times10^{15}M_\odot$. The <temperature> and extent alone cannot fix this coefficient without a <density profile>. The total <luminosity> is $5\times10^{12}L_\odot$, so the previously inferred <mass-to-light ratio> gives
$$
\boxed{M_\star\simeq4.50(5\times10^{12})M_\odot=2.25\times10^{13}M_\odot.}
$$

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

Three physically distinct contributions are unseen <dark matter>, hot diffuse <intracluster gas> omitted from the summed stellar <mass>, and faint stars or <stellar remnants> missed by transferring the <Milky Way> <mass-to-light ratio> to the cluster. The first two are actual additional components; the third changes the inferred stellar budget. Departures from <virial equilibrium>, <velocity anisotropy> or extra nonthermal <pressure> can also bias the dynamical or <X-ray> estimates, although they need not remove the discrepancy.

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

A <blackbody> is an ideal body that absorbs all incident <electromagnetic radiation>, at every <frequency> and direction. Its <absorptivity> is one, so <Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation> makes its equilibrium <emissivity> one as well. At a specified <temperature> its thermal <spectrum> is universal: it depends on <temperature>, not material or shape. This does not mean it looks black when hot; it can glow brightly while remaining a perfect absorber.

A small hole in a nearly isothermal opaque cavity approximates a <blackbody>, because radiation entering the hole undergoes many interactions before it could escape. In <thermal equilibrium> the cavity contains an approximately isotropic <Planck spectrum>. The emitted <radiance> follows <Planck's law>, its integrated outward <radiant exitance> follows the <Stefan–Boltzmann law>, and its wavelength peak follows <Wien's displacement law>. A real surface can have wavelength- and direction-dependent <emissivity> below this ideal limit.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

The constants are the <Planck constant> $h$, photon <frequency> $\nu$, <speed of light> $c$, <Boltzmann constant> $k_B$ and absolute <temperature> $T$. The supplied $U_\nu$ is a <spectral energy density>, with units $\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}\,Hz^{-1}}$. It describes equilibrium radiation per volume and frequency interval, rather than emitted <power> itself. For isotropic cavity radiation the corresponding surface <spectral radiant exitance> is $M_\nu=cU_\nu/4$.

At low <frequency> the curve rises as $\nu^2$; it has one positive maximum and an exponentially decaying high-frequency tail. The left panel below shades the area $u(T)=\int_0^\infty U_\nu\,d\nu$ under one curve. Put $x=h\nu/(k_BT)$; then
$$
U_\nu=\frac{8\pi(k_BT)^3}{h^2c^3}\frac{x^3}{e^x-1},\qquad d\nu=\frac{k_BT}{h}\,dx.
$$
The remaining dimensionless <integral> is independent of <temperature>, so
$$
\boxed{u(T)\propto T^4,\qquad M(T)=\frac c4u(T)=\sigma T^4.}
$$
Thus doubling <temperature> doubles the characteristic frequency scale, raises the spectrum's height by eight and increases its area by sixteen. Total emitted <energy> also depends on emitting <area> and elapsed time.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

For $x=h\nu/(k_BT)\ll1$, the <Taylor expansion> $e^x-1=x+O(x^2)$ gives the <Rayleigh-Jeans law> for equilibrium radiation,
$$
\boxed{U_\nu\simeq\frac{8\pi k_BT\nu^2}{c^3}.}
$$
This is the classical limit: each electromagnetic mode has mean thermal <energy> approximately $k_BT$. Extending it to arbitrarily large <frequencies> would give a divergent integrated <energy density>, the <ultraviolet catastrophe>.

For $x\gg1$, $e^x-1\simeq e^x$, giving the <Wien approximation>
$$
\boxed{U_\nu\simeq\frac{8\pi h\nu^3}{c^3}e^{-h\nu/(k_BT)}.}
$$
<Planck's quantum hypothesis> assigns equally spaced mode energies $E_n=nh\nu$. When the spacing is small relative to $k_BT$, many levels are occupied and the classical approximation works. When $h\nu\gg k_BT$, exciting even one quantum is exponentially unlikely, suppressing the high-frequency tail. The single expression $\langle E\rangle=h\nu/(e^x-1)$ contains both limits. Comparing <temperature> directly to $h\nu$ would be dimensionally incorrect; the low-temperature condition means $k_BT\ll h\nu$.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

First convert from a <spectral energy density> per unit <frequency> to one per unit <wavelength>, including the <Jacobian matrix and determinant>:
$$
U_\lambda=U_\nu\left|\frac{d\nu}{d\lambda}\right|=\frac{8\pi hc}{\lambda^5}\frac1{e^{hc/(\lambda k_BT)}-1}.
$$
Set $dU_\lambda/d\lambda=0$ and define $x=hc/(\lambda k_BT)$. The nonzero maximum satisfies $5(1-e^{-x})=x$, with numerical root $x=4.96511$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\lambda_{\max}T=\frac{hc}{4.96511k_B}=2.89777\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m\,K}.}
$$
This is <Wien's displacement law>. One must maximize the wavelength density, rather than find the frequency maximum and merely put $\lambda=c/\nu_{\max}$: the <frequency and wavelength peaks of Planck's law> are different because a spectral density changes under a change of coordinate.

==== e
{scope}

===== Solution

Using the stipulated $570\,\mathrm{nm}$ as a wavelength-density peak, <Wien's displacement law> gives
$$
\boxed{T_{\rm inferred}=\frac{0.002897}{570\times10^{-9}}\simeq5.08\times10^3\,\mathrm K.}
$$
This is a rough estimate from the supplied characteristic <wavelength>, not an accurate measurement of the Sun's <effective temperature>. The Sun emits a broad, approximately thermal visible <spectrum> and appears white outside the atmosphere; its familiar yellow appearance does not identify a monochromatic thermal peak. A photospheric <effective temperature> near $5770\,\mathrm K$ instead corresponds to a wavelength-density maximum near $502\,\mathrm{nm}$.<Sun colouring page by NASA Space Place>[Statement that the Sun emits all visible colours and appears white]{ref}<Solar physics facts by NASA Marshall>[Surface temperature approximately 5770 K]{ref}

The core must be much hotter to provide the <pressure> required for <hydrostatic equilibrium> and to sustain <nuclear fusion>. Outward <energy transport> through an opaque interior proceeds down a <temperature gradient>, by <radiative diffusion> and then <convection> through much of the outer envelope. The observable <photosphere> radiates to space at the temperature needed to carry the solar <luminosity>, $L_\odot=4\pi R_\odot^2\sigma T_{\rm eff}^4$; it is not an unobstructed view of the hot core. The printed core estimate above $20$ million degrees is also too high for the present Sun: the standard central <temperature> is about $1.56\times10^7\,\mathrm K$. This correction leaves the large core-to-surface contrast and its explanation intact.<Solar physics facts by NASA Marshall>[Central temperature approximately 15,600,000 K]{ref}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

<Compton scattering> transfers <energy> and <momentum> from a photon to an electron. For an initially stationary, approximately free <electron>, a photon scattered through a nonzero angle generally has lower <frequency> and longer <wavelength>; the recoiling electron carries the difference. The effect demonstrates the photon <momentum> $p_\gamma=h\nu/c=h/\lambda$, beyond a purely elastic classical scattering description.

The middle panel of the figure in <past-exam-of-the-short-options-paper-of-the-physics-prelims-of-the-university-of-oxford/2019/s3/1/b/solution> shows the incident photon along $+x$, the outgoing photon at angle $\theta$ above that direction, and the recoiling <electron> at deflection angle $\phi$ below it. Each arrow labels its particle and <momentum>; the initial electron is at rest at the interaction point. The scattered photon and electron have opposite transverse momentum components, while their forward components sum to the incident photon <momentum>.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Take a stationary initial electron of <mass> $m_e$, a photon incident along $x$, and a nonrelativistic recoil <velocity> of magnitude $v$. Choose $\theta$ above $x$ for the scattered photon and $\phi$ below $x$ for the electron. <Conservation of momentum> gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{h\nu}{c}=\frac{h\nu'}{c}\cos\theta+m_ev\cos\phi,\qquad
0=\frac{h\nu'}c\sin\theta-m_ev\sin\phi.}
$$
The electron's unchanged rest <energy> cancels in <conservation of energy>, leaving
$$
\boxed{h\nu=h\nu'+\frac12m_ev^2.}
$$
Equivalently use $h\nu/c=h/\lambda$ and $h\nu=hc/\lambda$. The <nonrelativistic limit> requires $v/c\ll1$, and treating the electron as free requires its binding <energy> to be negligible on the scale of the collision.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

For complete photon backscatter $\theta=\pi$, the electron recoils forward and <momentum conservation> gives
$$
m_ev=h\left(\frac1\lambda+\frac1{\lambda'}\right).
$$
Combining this with <conservation of energy> and writing $\Delta\lambda=\lambda'-\lambda$ gives
$$
\frac{hc\Delta\lambda}{\lambda\lambda'}=\frac{h^2}{2m_e}\left(\frac1\lambda+\frac1{\lambda'}\right)^2,
\quad
\Delta\lambda=\frac{h}{2m_ec}\frac{(\lambda+\lambda')^2}{\lambda\lambda'}.
$$
For $\Delta\lambda\ll\lambda,\lambda'$, the final ratio is approximately four, so
$$
\boxed{\Delta\lambda\simeq\frac{2h}{m_ec}=2\lambda_C\simeq4.85\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm m.}
$$
Within the stated nonrelativistic equations the unsimplified relation is implicit and has higher-order recoil corrections; the displayed final approximation is all that the small-shift assumption supports. Exact relativistic <Compton recoil kinematics> gives $\Delta\lambda=(h/m_ec)(1-\cos\theta)$, and therefore exactly $2\lambda_C$ at backscatter. Agreement at leading order does not make the nonrelativistic derivation exact at arbitrary photon <energy>.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

The ordinary electron <Compton wavelength> is
$$
\boxed{\lambda_C=\frac{h}{m_ec}=2.4263\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm m.}
$$
A photon of that <wavelength> has <energy> equal to the electron rest <energy>, $hc/\lambda_C=m_ec^2$. It therefore sets the characteristic scale at which photon recoil cannot generally be treated nonrelativistically, and it sets the magnitude of the <Compton wavelength shift>. The reduced <Compton wavelength>, $\bar\lambda_C=\hbar/(m_ec)=\lambda_C/(2\pi)$, is a related but different convention. Localization on this reduced scale needs momenta of order $m_ec$, so a nonrelativistic single-particle description becomes inadequate. Neither wavelength is a measured geometrical radius of the electron.

==== e
{scope}

===== Solution

Retain the backscattering geometry from part (c). With $\lambda=10\,\mathrm{nm}$, $\lambda'=\lambda+2\lambda_C$ differs from $\lambda$ by only $4.85\times10^{-4}$ of its value. <Momentum conservation> gives
$$
v\simeq\frac{h}{m_e}\left(\frac1\lambda+\frac1{\lambda'}\right)\simeq\frac{2h}{m_e\lambda},\qquad
\boxed{v\simeq1.45\times10^5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\quad\frac vc\simeq4.85\times10^{-4}.}
$$
The Lorentz-factor correction is of order $v^2/c^2\simeq2.4\times10^{-7}$, so the electron recoil is safely nonrelativistic. Its <kinetic energy> is only about $0.060\,\mathrm{eV}$, compared with incoming photon <energy> about $124\,\mathrm{eV}$. This validates the free-electron recoil approximation's relativistic aspect; actual binding or thermal motion in matter can require separate corrections. No larger recoil occurs at another scattering angle for the same initial photon.

{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

Inside the <infinite square well>, $V=0$, and the <time-independent Schrödinger equation> is
$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}=E\psi,\qquad\psi(0)=\psi(L)=0.
$$
For $E>0$, write $k^2=2mE/\hbar^2$ and $\psi=A\sin kx+B\cos kx$. The first <Dirichlet boundary condition> gives $B=0$, and the second requires $kL=n\pi$ for a nonzero state. The case $n=0$ is identically zero; negative $n$ only changes an overall <quantum phase>. For $E=0$ a linear solution satisfying both walls is zero, and for $E<0$ the hyperbolic solutions likewise cannot satisfy both walls nontrivially.

<Wavefunction normalization>, $\int_0^L|\psi|^2dx=1$, fixes $|A|=\sqrt{2/L}$. The orthonormal <energy eigenstates> and <energy eigenvalues> are therefore
$$
\boxed{\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac2L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}L,\qquad E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
Each state vanishes outside the well. Its stationary time-dependent form is $\Psi_n(x,t)=\psi_n(x)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$. General states are normalized <superpositions> of these modes, rather than additional single <energy eigenstates>.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Assume the given <wavefunction> is zero outside $[0,L]$. <Wavefunction normalization> requires
$$
1=\int_0^L|Nx|^2dx=|N|^2\frac{L^3}{3},\qquad
\boxed{|N|=\sqrt{\frac3{L^3}}.}
$$
An arbitrary overall <quantum phase> is unobservable, so choose the positive real representative $N=\sqrt3/L^{3/2}$.

The <linear ramp state in an infinite square well> has a boundary subtlety: the limiting value at $L$ is $\sqrt{3/L}$, rather than zero. The function is still a normalized element of the well's <Hilbert space> $L^2(0,L)$, so <position measurement> and energy-projection probabilities are meaningful. It is not in the domain of the Dirichlet <Hamiltonian>, nor is it a finite-energy box state. Altering the value at the single endpoint does not cure its nonzero boundary trace; a physical finite-energy preparation must smooth the function to zero near the wall. The following probabilities refer to the supplied idealized state.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Apply the position version of the <Born rule> to the normalized <wavefunction>:
$$
\boxed{\Pr(0\le x\le L/3)=\int_0^{L/3}|\Psi(x)|^2dx=\frac3{L^3}\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^{L/3}=\frac1{27}\simeq0.0370.}
$$
The result is independent of $L$ because the state and the measurement interval scale together. Most <position probability> lies towards the right wall, since the <probability density> grows as $x^2$.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

An <energy measurement> selects the <ground state> with <probability> equal to the squared <inner product> with $\psi_1$. Using $N=\sqrt3/L^{3/2}$ and <integration by parts>,
$$
c_1=\int_0^L\psi_1(x)^*\Psi(x)\,dx=\frac{\sqrt6}{L^2}\int_0^Lx\sin\frac{\pi x}L\,dx
=\frac{\sqrt6}{\pi}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\Pr(E=E_1)=|c_1|^2=\frac6{\pi^2}\simeq0.6079.}
$$
The full <Fourier sine series> has $c_n=(-1)^{n+1}\sqrt6/(n\pi)$, so $\sum_n|c_n|^2=(6/\pi^2)\sum_n n^{-2}=1$, an independent <wavefunction normalization> check. However $E_n|c_n|^2=3\hbar^2/(mL^2)$ for every $n$, hence $\sum_nE_n|c_n|^2$ diverges. This explicitly verifies the infinite-<energy expectation> qualification in part (b), without invalidating the individual projection <probabilities>.

==== e
{scope}

===== Solution

The two <probabilities> concern different <quantum measurements>, so there is no requirement that they match or that one bound the other. The spatial interval $[0,L/3]$ contains little of the state: its <probability density> is smallest near zero and increases towards $L$. By contrast, the positive <ground state> extends across the whole well. It has substantial same-sign overlap with the positive linear <wavefunction>, with no cancellation between lobes in the overlap <integral>. The right panel of the figure in <past-exam-of-the-short-options-paper-of-the-physics-prelims-of-the-university-of-oxford/2019/s3/1/b/solution> illustrates these two amplitudes and marks the small position interval.

Consequently a large global <ground-state probability> can coexist with a small probability of finding the particle in the left third. This is not because the <ground state> is spatially confined to that third; a ground-state particle can be found throughout the well. In operator language the two events are projections onto a spatial subspace and onto one <energy eigenstate>, respectively.
