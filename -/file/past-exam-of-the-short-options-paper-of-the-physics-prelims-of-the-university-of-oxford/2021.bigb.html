=== 1

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

The apparent <singularity> at zero cancels: the <Taylor series> of the <sine> gives
$$
\frac{\sin z}{z^2}-\frac1z=-\frac z6+\frac{z^3}{120}-\cdots.
$$
Thus zero is a <removable singularity>, with extension $f(0)=1/2$. At $z=-2$, the other terms are <holomorphic>, whereas $(z+2)^{-1}$ has a <simple pole>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{z=0:\ \text{removable},\qquad z=-2:\ \text{simple pole with residue }1.}
$$
There are no other finite <singularities>. If “all” includes the point at infinity on the <Riemann sphere>, it is an <essential singularity>: $f(1/w)=w/(1+2w)+w^2\sin(1/w)-w$ has infinitely many negative powers in its <Laurent series> at $w=0$.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

The <geometric series> for $(2+z)^{-1}$ converges for $|z|<2$, while the cancelled <sine> terms have an everywhere convergent <Taylor series>. Hence the requested <Laurent series> has no negative powers:
$$
\boxed{f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n z^n}{2^{n+1}}+\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k z^{2k-1}}{(2k+1)!},\qquad 0<|z|<2.}
$$
For example,
$$
f(z)=\frac12-\frac{5z}{12}+\frac{z^2}{8}-\frac{13z^3}{240}+\frac{z^4}{32}+O(z^5).
$$
The extension through zero also makes this a <Taylor series>. The <pole> at $-2$ fixes its <radius of convergence> at two.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Assume positive, counterclockwise <orientation of a contour>. Factor the <polynomial> denominator as
$$
z^4-10z^2+1=\bigl(z^2-(5-2\sqrt6)\bigr)\bigl(z^2-(5+2\sqrt6)\bigr).
$$
Only $z=\pm(\sqrt3-\sqrt2)$ lie inside the <unit circle>. These are <simple poles>. Writing the denominator as $P(z)$, their <residues> are
$$
\frac{z}{P'(z)}=\frac1{4z^2-20}=-\frac1{8\sqrt6}.
$$
The <residue theorem> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{I=2\pi i\left(-\frac1{4\sqrt6}\right)=-\frac{\pi i}{2\sqrt6}.}
$$
Reversing <orientation of a contour> changes the sign.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

Parametrize the positively oriented <unit circle> by $z=e^{i\theta}$. Then $dz=iz\,d\theta$ and
$$
\frac{z^4-10z^2+1}{z^2}=2\cos(2\theta)-10=-4(2+\sin^2\theta).
$$
Consequently the <contour integral> already evaluated satisfies
$$
I=-\frac i4\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{2+\sin^2\theta},\qquad
\boxed{\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{2+\sin^2\theta}=4iI=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt6}.}
$$

=== 2
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $f$ be <holomorphic> on a neighbourhood of a positively oriented, piecewise smooth <simple closed curve> and its interior except at finitely many <isolated singularities> $a_j$ strictly inside. The <residue theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\oint_\Gamma f(z)\,dz=2\pi i\sum_j\operatorname{Res}(f,a_j).}
$$
The <residue> is the coefficient of $(z-a_j)^{-1}$ in the local <Laurent series>. No <singularity> may lie on the <contour>. For more general closed <contours>, each <residue> is multiplied by the corresponding <winding number>.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

The <poles> are the four roots of $z^4=-1$. The upper <half-plane> contains $a=e^{i\pi/4}$ and $b=e^{3i\pi/4}$, both enclosed when $R>1$. At a <simple pole> $q$,
$$
\operatorname{Res}\left(\frac{z^2}{z^4+1},q\right)=\frac{q^2}{4q^3}=\frac1{4q}.
$$
The sum of the two <residues> is
$$
\frac14(e^{-i\pi/4}+e^{-3i\pi/4})=-\frac{i}{2\sqrt2}.
$$
The <residue theorem>, with the real segment traversed from $-R$ to $R$ and the upper arc from $R$ to $-R$, yields
$$
\boxed{\oint_{\Gamma_R}\frac{z^2}{z^4+1}\,dz=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt2}.}
$$

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

On the upper semicircular <contour>, $|z|=R$ and the <reverse triangle inequality> gives $|z^4+1|\ge R^4-1>0$. Its <arc length> is $\pi R$, so the <estimation lemma> gives
$$
\boxed{\left|\int_{\gamma_R}\frac{z^2}{z^4+1}\,dz\right|\le\frac{\pi R^3}{R^4-1}\longrightarrow0.}
$$
The arc is open, so an ordinary integral sign is appropriate even though the source uses a closed-integral symbol there. Subtracting the vanishing arc contribution from the closed <contour integral> gives the real-line <improper integral> $\pi/\sqrt2$. Its integrand is an <even function>, and its tail is $O(x^{-2})$, ensuring convergence. Thus
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty\frac{x^2}{x^4+1}\,dx=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt2}.}
$$

=== 3
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the principal <complex square root>, $\sqrt z=\sqrt r\,e^{i\theta/2}$ with $-\pi<\theta<\pi$. For $F(z)=e^{z-\sqrt z}/z$, the local expansion on this <holomorphic branch> is
$$
F(z)=\frac1z-\frac1{\sqrt z}+\frac32+O(\sqrt z).
$$
The fractional power prevents a single-valued <Laurent series> in a punctured disc. In particular, zero is a <branch point>, not an <isolated singularity> classified as a <pole>. Merely observing that $zF(z)\to1$ on the slit plane would not establish a <simple pole>: $zF(z)$ does not extend as a <holomorphic function> through a whole disc around zero.

The negative real half-line is a chosen <branch cut>. Its upper and lower boundary values differ because $\sqrt{-x+i0}=i\sqrt x$ and $\sqrt{-x-i0}=-i\sqrt x$ for $x>0$. Continuation once around zero changes the sign of the <complex square root>, and hence changes $F$ in general. Other <branch cuts> are possible; this one defines the principal <holomorphic branch>.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Excise a small disc about zero and a thin slit along the negative real axis, using the orientations shown in the source: $C_1$ upwards, $C_3$ towards zero above the slit, $C_4$ clockwise around zero, and $C_5$ away from zero below the slit. On the resulting region $F$ is a single-valued <holomorphic function>; zero lies in the excluded disc and there are no <poles> in the region. <Cauchy's integral theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\int_{C_1}F\,dz+\int_{C_2}F\,dz+\int_{C_3}F\,dz+\int_{C_4}F\,dz+\int_{C_5}F\,dz+\int_{C_6}F\,dz=0.}
$$
Here the paths on the cut mean limiting paths from its two sides. Apply <Cauchy's integral theorem> before taking those limits, when the entire <contour> lies inside the slit domain.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

For $x>0$, the upper and lower boundary values on the <branch cut> are
$$
F(-x+i0)=-\frac{e^{-x-i\sqrt x}}x,\qquad F(-x-i0)=-\frac{e^{-x+i\sqrt x}}x.
$$
Write $x=A_R$ for the outer endpoint distance on the cut. The upper path runs from $x=A_R$ to $x=\epsilon$, while the lower path runs back from $\epsilon$ to $A_R$; in both cases $dz=-dx$. Therefore
$$
\int_{C_3}F\,dz=-\int_\epsilon^{A_R}\frac{e^{-x-i\sqrt x}}x\,dx,\qquad
\int_{C_5}F\,dz=\int_\epsilon^{A_R}\frac{e^{-x+i\sqrt x}}x\,dx,
$$
and
$$
\boxed{\int_{C_3}F\,dz+\int_{C_5}F\,dz=2i\int_\epsilon^{A_R}e^{-x}\frac{\sin\sqrt x}{x}\,dx.}
$$
The source's limits zero and infinity require $\epsilon\to0$ and $R\to\infty$; they are not an exact identity on a finite <contour>. For a concrete counterexample take $\epsilon=1/4$ and $A_R=1$: the finite real integral is at most $\int_{1/4}^1x^{-1/2}\,dx=1$, whereas the full-range integral is $\pi\operatorname{erf}(1/2)\simeq1.6352$, as evaluated in the next part. Near zero the combined integrand is $O(x^{-1/2})$, so the combined <improper integral> converges, although the individual cut integrals have logarithmic divergences. At infinity the factor $e^{-x}$ ensures convergence. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\lim_{R\to\infty,\,\epsilon\to0}\left(\int_{C_3}F\,dz+\int_{C_5}F\,dz\right)=2i\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\frac{\sin\sqrt x}{x}\,dx.}
$$

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

On the small clockwise <contour> $z=\epsilon e^{i\theta}$, $\theta$ decreases from $\pi$ to $-\pi$. Uniformly on that arc, $e^{z-\sqrt z}=1+O(\sqrt\epsilon)$, and hence
$$
\int_{C_4}F(z)\,dz=i\int_\pi^{-\pi}\bigl(1+O(\sqrt\epsilon)\bigr)\,d\theta\longrightarrow-2\pi i.
$$
The large arcs also vanish. To make this precise for the displayed geometry, take them on the left semicircle $z=\gamma+Re^{i\theta}$, $\pi/2\le\theta\le3\pi/2$, split at the cut. On the principal <holomorphic branch>, $\operatorname{Re}\sqrt z\ge0$, $|z|\ge R-\gamma$, and $\operatorname{Re}z=\gamma+R\cos\theta$. The <estimation lemma> gives
$$
\left|\int_{C_2\cup C_6}F\,dz\right|\le\frac{Re^\gamma}{R-\gamma}\int_{\pi/2}^{3\pi/2}e^{R\cos\theta}\,d\theta=O(R^{-1}).
$$
For the last bound, symmetry and $\sin u\ge2u/\pi$ on $0\le u\le\pi/2$ imply that the angular integral is at most $\pi/R$. The exact choice of large arcs does not affect the limiting result. The vertical <improper integral> converges absolutely because $e^{-\operatorname{Re}\sqrt{\gamma+iy}}/|\gamma+iy|$ decays as $O(e^{-c\sqrt{|y|}}/|y|)$.

Taking limits in the closed <contour integral> now yields $2\pi iJ+2iK-2\pi i=0$, where $K=\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\sin(\sqrt x)\,dx/x$. Thus
$$
\boxed{J=1-\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\frac{\sin\sqrt x}{x}\,dx.}
$$
As an optional explicit evaluation, put $x=t^2$ and define $K(a)=2\int_0^\infty e^{-t^2}\sin(at)\,dt/t$. <Differentiation under the integral sign> and the <Gaussian integral> give $K'(a)=\sqrt\pi e^{-a^2/4}$ and $K(0)=0$. Therefore $K=K(1)=\pi\operatorname{erf}(1/2)$ and $J=\operatorname{erfc}(1/2)\simeq0.479500$ in terms of the <error function> and <complementary error function>.

The <astronomical magnitude> convention is $m_1-m_2=-2.5\log_{10}(F_1/F_2)$, with both <fluxes> measured through the same <photometric passband>. A five-unit <magnitude difference> therefore corresponds to

The brighter endpoint has one hundred times the visual <flux> of the fainter endpoint. Absolute <fluxes> require a <magnitude zero point>, which is not given.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

For spherical <hydrostatic equilibrium>, the <stellar structure equations> include
$$
\frac{dP}{dr}=-\frac{Gm(r)\rho(r)}{r^2},\qquad \frac{dm}{dr}=4\pi r^2\rho(r).
$$
Eliminate $\rho$ to obtain $dP/dm=-Gm/(4\pi r^4)$. Since $r(m)<R_\odot$ through the interior, integration gives
$$
P_c-P_s=\int_0^{M_\odot}\frac{Gm}{4\pi r(m)^4}\,dm>\frac{G}{4\pi R_\odot^4}\int_0^{M_\odot}m\,dm.
$$
With negligible surface <pressure>, this proves
$$
\boxed{P_{c,\odot}>\frac{GM_\odot^2}{8\pi R_\odot^4}\simeq4.48\times10^{13}\,\mathrm{Pa}.}
$$
This is a conservative lower bound; concentrating <mass> near the centre increases the required <pressure>.

For the <temperature> inference assume predominantly <ideal gas> support, a nearly fully ionized hydrogen–helium mixture with <mean molecular weight> $\mu\simeq0.61$, and the hottest region at the centre. A rigorous scale estimate follows from the <stellar virial theorem>, rather than substituting the mean <mass density> for the unknown central <mass density>. With negligible surface <pressure>,
$$
3\int P\,dV=-W,\qquad -W=\int_0^{M_\odot}\frac{Gm}{r(m)}\,dm>\frac{GM_\odot^2}{2R_\odot}.
$$
The <ideal gas law> gives $\int P\,dV=k_B\int T\,dm/(\mu m_p)$. The mass-weighted mean <temperature> consequently obeys
$$
\boxed{T_c\ge\langle T\rangle_M>\frac{\mu m_pGM_\odot}{6k_BR_\odot}\simeq2.35\times10^6\,\mathrm K.}
$$
This establishes several million <kelvin> under the stated physical assumptions. The central <pressure> inequality alone does not give a central <temperature> bound without an <equation of state> and information about <mass density> or an integrated argument.

==== a

===== Solution

The intact source figure gives <orbital period> $P=6.0\,\mathrm{yr}$, stellar <radial velocity> semiamplitude $K=30.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, and <orbital eccentricity> $e=0$. A planetary <exoplanet transit> implies a nearly edge-on orbit, so $\sin i\simeq1$. Adopt a <solar mass> for the <Solar-type star> and initially neglect the companion's <mass> relative to it.

For a circular solution of the <two-body problem>, the star's distance from the <centre of mass> is $a_*=aM_p/(M_*+M_p)$, and the measured semiamplitude is $K=2\pi a_*\sin i/P$. <Kepler's third law> gives
$$
a^3=\frac{G(M_*+M_p)P^2}{4\pi^2},\qquad
\frac{M_p^3\sin^3 i}{(M_*+M_p)^2}=\frac{PK^3}{2\pi G}.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{a\simeq\left(\frac{GM_\odot P^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3}=3.30\,\mathrm{AU},\qquad
M_p\simeq K\left(\frac{P}{2\pi G}\right)^{1/3}M_\odot^{2/3}=3.64\times10^{27}\,\mathrm{kg}\simeq1.92M_J.}
$$
The inferred <binary mass ratio> is only $0.00183$, validating the approximation. Since $e=0$, the star–planet separation is constant and equals the orbital <semimajor axis>. Without the <exoplanet transit>, <Doppler spectroscopy> would generally give only a minimum companion <mass> through $M_p\sin i$.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Assume uniform reradiation over the planetary surface, unit thermal <emissivity>, and zero <Bond albedo>. The planet absorbs over its projected disc and emits over its full sphere. <Radiative equilibrium> and the <Stefan–Boltzmann law> give
$$
\pi R_p^2\frac{L_\odot}{4\pi a^2}=4\pi R_p^2\sigma T_p^4,\qquad
T_p=T_\odot\sqrt{\frac{R_\odot}{2a}}.
$$
Using the liquid-water range $273.15\le T_p\le373.15\,\mathrm K$ as the crude thermal criterion gives
$$
\boxed{a_{\rm in}\simeq0.562\,\mathrm{AU},\qquad a_{\rm out}\simeq1.05\,\mathrm{AU}.}
$$
For nonzero <Bond albedo> $A_B$, both radii acquire the factor $\sqrt{1-A_B}$. For example $A_B=0.3$ gives approximately $0.470$ to $0.877\,\mathrm{AU}$. An airless planet need not have uniform surface <temperature>; inefficient heat redistribution makes the dayside hotter and the nightside colder, so a single equilibrium <temperature> is an averaging approximation.

This simple <habitable zone> contains the Earth's orbit under the zero-<albedo> assumption, but also contains Venus's orbit, although its <greenhouse effect> produces an uninhabitably hot surface. Mars lies beyond the calculated outer boundary. These comparisons show the usefulness of the orbital scale and the limitations of a <temperature>-only criterion. The stipulated airless model also omits the <atmospheric pressure> needed for stable surface liquid water: this is an effective thermal window, not a sufficient habitability condition.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

**Transit photometry.** A planet crossing the stellar disc produces a periodic reduction in <flux>, approximately $\delta=(R_p/R_*)^2$ for a small, dark planet away from the limb. The <orbital period> and <stellar radius> allow an inferred planetary <radius>, but not its <mass> by this method alone. The geometric <geometric transit probability> is roughly $(R_*+R_p)/a$ for a circular orbit, so the method strongly favours close orbits and large planets. Small signals compete with instrumental noise and <stellar activity>; eclipsing stellar binaries and blended sources can mimic a planetary <exoplanet transit>, and long <orbital periods> require long observing baselines.

**Doppler spectroscopy.** Periodic shifts of stellar <spectral lines> reveal the stellar <radial velocity> caused by motion around the <centre of mass>. For a light companion, $K\propto M_p\sin i\,P^{-1/3}M_*^{-2/3}/\sqrt{1-e^2}$, favouring massive, short-period companions and low-mass host stars. Unknown <orbital inclination> gives a minimum <mass>; <stellar activity>, line broadening, and instrumental calibration limit precision. Long <orbital periods> require years of measurements.

**Direct imaging.** Spatially resolved reflected light or thermal emission separates a planet from its host. The large star–planet brightness contrast and small <angular separation> demand starlight suppression and precise correction of optical aberrations. The method favours wide orbits around nearby stars, and young, hot, massive planets with stronger thermal emission. Translating brightness to <mass> depends on planetary cooling models and age; it is difficult to image an Earth analogue close to a bright star.

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

For the estimate implied by the supplied <Hubble constant>, use the low-<redshift> <Hubble law> $D\simeq cz/H_0$. Then
$$
D_L\simeq800\,\mathrm{Mpc},\qquad D_S\simeq2000\,\mathrm{Mpc},\qquad D_{LS}\simeq1200\,\mathrm{Mpc}.
$$
With $\theta_E=\pi/10800$ radians, inversion of the <Einstein angular radius> relation gives
$$
\boxed{M_E=\frac{c^2\theta_E^2}{4G}\frac{D_LD_S}{D_{LS}}\simeq5.89\times10^{14}M_\odot.}
$$
The corresponding projected <Einstein radius> is $R_E=D_L\theta_E\simeq233\,\mathrm{kpc}$.

This is the projected enclosed <mass> of the <galaxy cluster>, including all matter, not just its stars. Strictly, the <gravitational lensing> formula uses <angular diameter distances>, and at $z=0.5$ the low-<redshift> approximation is only an estimate. An exact value requires a specified cosmological expansion history and a consistent lens-to-source <angular diameter distance>; it cannot be determined from $H_0$ alone. The later estimates use the same distance approximation consistently.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Assume <virial equilibrium>, structurally homologous <early-type galaxies>, a fixed relation between the measured central <velocity dispersion> $\sigma_c$ and the global random stellar motions, and a constant total <mass-to-light ratio> $\Upsilon$. The <virial theorem> gives $M=k_v\sigma_c^2R_e/G$, where the dimensionless structure factor $k_v$ is common to the sample. With $\mathcal I_e$ denoting mean **linear** <surface brightness> inside the <effective radius>, $L=2\pi R_e^2\mathcal I_e$. Thus
$$
\boxed{R_e=\frac{k_v}{2\pi G\Upsilon}\frac{\sigma_c^2}{\mathcal I_e},\qquad \log R_e=2\log\sigma_c-\log\mathcal I_e+\mathrm{constant}.}
$$
The usual observed <fundamental plane of elliptical galaxies> has a tilt relative to these exponents because <mass-to-light ratio> varies with <stellar population>, chemical composition, and <dark matter> fraction; structural and dynamical nonhomology can also change $k_v$.

**Printed convention.** The source places the logarithm of a value expressed in $\mathrm{mag\,arcsec^{-2}}$ in the empirical fit. This differs from the usual logarithm of linear <surface brightness>. If $\mu_e$ is the quoted magnitude <surface brightness>,
$$
\log_{10}\frac{\mathcal I_e}{L_{V,\odot}\,\mathrm{pc^{-2}}}=-0.4(\mu_e-M_{V,\odot}-21.572).
$$
A coefficient $-0.8$ on $\log\mathcal I_e$ therefore becomes $+0.32$ on $\mu_e$, with an adjusted intercept; it does **not** become $-0.8\log\mu_e$. For a concrete distinction, changing $\mu_e$ from 20 to 25 decreases linear <surface brightness> by a factor 100, so the linear-intensity fit changes $R_e$ by $100^{0.8}\simeq39.8$, whereas the literal printed logarithm changes it by $(25/20)^{-0.8}\simeq0.837$.

The following part evaluates the printed numerical fit literally and labels that convention. If the fit was intended for linear <surface brightness>, its calibration must be specified; a conditional result for using the same coefficients with linear units is also given. No equality between these two conventions is assumed.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Evaluate the source's printed logarithm using the numerical magnitude value 21.0, with base-ten <logarithms>:
$$
\log_{10}\frac{R_e}{\mathrm{kpc}}=1.3\log_{10}(250)-0.8\log_{10}(21)-1.36=0.69955,
\qquad \boxed{R_e\simeq5.01\,\mathrm{kpc}.}
$$
This is the literal printed-fit convention identified above. Convert the mean visual <surface brightness> to a linear <luminosity> per projected <area>. At 10 <parsecs>, one square <arcsecond> subtends $100/(206264.806)^2\,\mathrm{pc^2}$. The definition of <absolute magnitude> consequently gives
$$
\frac{\mathcal I_e}{L_{V,\odot}\,\mathrm{pc^{-2}}}=10^{-0.4(\mu_e-M_{V,\odot}-21.572)}
=10^{-0.4(21.0-4.8-21.572)}\simeq140.9.
$$
The constant $21.572=5\log_{10}(206264.806)-5$ is the conversion between the angular and physical projected <area>. Since the <effective radius> encloses half the total <luminosity>,
$$
\boxed{L_V=2\pi R_e^2\mathcal I_e\simeq2.22\times10^{10}L_{V,\odot}.}
$$
For the stipulated <stellar population> of <Solar-type stars>, approximate the stellar <mass-to-light ratio> by $M_\odot/L_{V,\odot}$. Summing 100 such galaxies then gives
$$
\boxed{M_{*,\mathrm{cluster}}\simeq100\frac{L_V}{L_{V,\odot}}M_\odot=2.22\times10^{12}M_\odot.}
$$
This assumes negligible <interstellar extinction> and that the supplied <surface brightness> is an intrinsic or already corrected value; an observed cosmological <surface brightness> requires additional dimming and passband corrections before this conversion.

For comparison only, if the empirical coefficients and intercept were instead meant for $\log_{10}(\mathcal I_e/[L_{V,\odot}\,\mathrm{pc^{-2}}])$, substituting $140.9$ in that fit would give $R_e=1.09\,\mathrm{kpc}$, $L_V=1.06\times10^9L_{V,\odot}$, and $M_{*,\mathrm{cluster}}=1.06\times10^{11}M_\odot$. Those are conditional alternative results, not an unannounced correction of the printed units.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

The <gravitational lensing> <mass> includes <dark matter>, hot intracluster gas, stars, and any other material in the projected aperture. Most is not stellar: in the literal convention above the stellar fraction is only $2.22\times10^{12}/(5.89\times10^{14})\simeq0.00376$.

Assume the cluster shares the cosmic <cosmic baryon fraction>, $f_b=\Omega_b/\Omega_m=1/6$, and compare the same aperture for all components. Then
$$
M_b\simeq\frac16M_E=9.82\times10^{13}M_\odot,\qquad
\boxed{M_{\rm gas}\simeq\frac16M_E-M_*\simeq9.60\times10^{13}M_\odot.}
$$
Neglecting stars entirely would give $9.82\times10^{13}M_\odot$. Thus the assumed hot gas dominates the <baryonic matter>, and the remainder $M_{\rm DM}\simeq(5/6)M_E=4.91\times10^{14}M_\odot$ is <dark matter>. The budget is consistent: stars are about $2.26\%$ of the inferred <baryonic matter>, and all components sum to $M_E$.

For a thermal scale, take gas extent $R_g\simeq2R_E=465\,\mathrm{kpc}$ and mean particle <mass> $\mu m_p$ with $\mu\simeq0.61$ for ionized gas. A simple spherical <virial theorem> estimate with gas binding <energy> $W_g\sim-GM_EM_g/R_g$ and thermal <kinetic energy> $K_g=3M_gk_BT/(2\mu m_p)$ gives
$$
\boxed{T\sim\frac{\mu m_pGM_E}{3k_BR_g}\simeq1.34\times10^8\,\mathrm K,\qquad k_BT\sim12\,\mathrm{keV}.}
$$
This is the scale of a very hot, massive X-ray emitting <galaxy cluster>. The numerical prefactor depends on the radial <mass density> profile, gas distribution and boundary <pressure>; for example an isothermal-sphere estimate $k_BT\sim\mu m_pGM/(2R_g)$ gives about $2.0\times10^8\,\mathrm K$. Moreover $M_E$ is a projected aperture <mass> at $R_E$, whereas the estimate needs a spherical enclosed <mass> at $2R_E$; the given data do not determine that conversion. The quoted <temperature> is therefore an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a uniquely measured value. Nor do the data determine the total gas <mass> outside the <Einstein ring> without a profile. The gas budget above pertains to the matched aperture under the cosmic-fraction assumption.

=== 1
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

A <blackbody> absorbs all incident <electromagnetic radiation>, at every <frequency>, direction and polarization: its absorptivity is one. In <thermal equilibrium>, <Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation> makes its <emissivity> one as well. Its emitted <spectrum> depends only on its absolute <temperature>, through <Planck's law>, rather than on the material of its surface.

A small aperture in a large isothermal cavity approximates a <blackbody> because radiation entering it undergoes many reflections and is very unlikely to escape before absorption. The equilibrium <radiation field> is isotropic and unpolarized. The total emitted <radiant exitance> is $\sigma T^4$ by the <Stefan–Boltzmann law>; the peak of the wavelength <spectrum> shifts to shorter <wavelengths> as <temperature> increases according to <Wien's displacement law>. An ideal absorber can therefore glow brightly when hot: “black” describes absorption, not the absence of thermal emission.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Here $h$ is the <Planck constant>, $\nu$ is the radiation <frequency>, $c$ is the <speed of light> in vacuum, $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant>, and $T$ is the absolute <temperature> in <kelvin>. The quantity $U_\nu$ is spectral <energy density> per unit <frequency>, so $U_\nu\,d\nu$ is <energy> per unit <volume> in the interval $[\nu,\nu+d\nu]$, and $h\nu/(k_BT)$ is dimensionless.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $x=h\nu/(k_BT)=hc/(\lambda k_BT)$. In the long-<wavelength> limit $x\ll1$, the <Taylor expansion> $e^x-1=x+O(x^2)$ in <Planck's law> gives the <Rayleigh-Jeans law>,
$$
\boxed{U_\nu\simeq\frac{8\pi k_BT}{c^3}\nu^2.}
$$
This is the classical <equipartition theorem> result: each electromagnetic mode carries mean <energy> $k_BT$. Extending it to arbitrarily high <frequency> would make the total <energy density> diverge, the <ultraviolet catastrophe>.

In the short-<wavelength> limit $x\gg1$, $e^x-1\simeq e^x$, giving the <Wien approximation>,
$$
\boxed{U_\nu\simeq\frac{8\pi h}{c^3}\nu^3e^{-h\nu/(k_BT)}.}
$$
Large <photon energies> are thermally suppressed, removing the classical high-<frequency> divergence.

If the requested “form” is expressed per unit <wavelength>, the change of variables includes its <Jacobian determinant>: $U_\lambda=U_\nu|d\nu/d\lambda|=U_\nu c/\lambda^2$. Thus
$$
U_\lambda=\frac{8\pi hc}{\lambda^5}\frac1{e^{hc/(\lambda k_BT)}-1},\qquad
\boxed{U_\lambda\simeq\begin{cases}8\pi k_BT/\lambda^4,&x\ll1,\\8\pi hc\,e^{-x}/\lambda^5,&x\gg1.\end{cases}}
$$
Substituting $\nu=c/\lambda$ without the <Jacobian determinant> would give an incorrect spectral density.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

Convert the skin <temperature> to $T_s=308.15\,\mathrm K$. To locate the peak per unit <wavelength> in <Planck's law>, write $x=hc/(\lambda k_BT)$ and maximize $x^5/(e^x-1)$. Its positive stationary point obeys
$$
5(1-e^{-x})=x,\qquad x=4.965114\ldots.
$$
This gives <Wien's displacement law>, $\lambda_{\max}T=2.89777\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m\,K}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\lambda_{\max}=9.40\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm m\simeq9.4\,\mathrm{\mu m},}
$$
in the thermal <infrared>. A peak per unit <frequency> would occur at a different spectral position; this answer uses the conventional wavelength peak.

==== e
{scope}

===== Solution

For an ideal <blackbody> surface completely surrounded by an isothermal environment, the <Stefan–Boltzmann law> gives emitted <power> $A\sigma T_s^4$ and absorbed <power> $A\sigma T_e^4$. The net outward <radiative heat transfer> is therefore
$$
\boxed{P_{\rm net}=A\sigma(T_s^4-T_e^4)=2(5.670374419\times10^{-8})(308.15^4-293.15^4)\simeq185\,\mathrm W.}
$$
The gross emitted <power> is about $1022\,\mathrm W$, but about $837\,\mathrm W$ is absorbed back from the surroundings. The difference, rather than the emitted term alone, is the relevant <energy> loss.

==== f
{scope}

===== Solution

If the <temperatures>, exposed <area>, and surroundings remain fixed, the net radiated <energy> over one day is
$$
\boxed{E=P_{\rm net}(86400\,\mathrm s)\simeq1.60\times10^7\,\mathrm J.}
$$
Using the supplied conversion gives
$$
\boxed{E\simeq3.82\times10^6\,\mathrm{cal}=3.82\times10^3\,\mathrm{kcal}.}
$$
A dietary Calorie is one <kilocalorie>, so the result is about 3820 dietary Calories, rather than 3.82 million dietary Calories.

==== g
{scope}

===== Solution

The estimate assumes the entire $2\,\mathrm{m^2}$ surface remains exposed at $35\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ continuously. Clothing reduces direct exposure and presents a cooler outer emitting surface; the skin <temperature> also varies across the body and decreases in cooler surroundings through changes in blood flow. Body posture and nearby surfaces reduce the effective exchange <area>, while blankets and warm surrounding surfaces reduce net <radiative heat transfer>. These effects make the full-area, constant-<temperature> daily radiative estimate too large for ordinary clothed conditions.

The unit <emissivity> approximation is already fairly good for skin in the thermal <infrared>, so reducing <emissivity> alone is not the main correction. Also, <convection>, <thermal conduction>, and evaporation are additional channels of <heat transfer>; their existence does not subtract from the calculated radiative term. The computed number is a simplified **radiative** loss, not a complete metabolic <energy> budget.

=== 2
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

In the <photoelectric effect>, incident light ejects <electrons> from a material when each absorbed <photon> supplies sufficient <energy> to overcome its <work function> $W$. <Conservation of energy> gives the maximum emitted <kinetic energy>,
$$
\boxed{K_{\max}=h\nu-W,\qquad \nu\ge\nu_0=W/h.}
$$
At fixed <frequency> above threshold, increasing light <intensity> primarily increases the electron emission rate; it does not increase $K_{\max}$ in the single-<photon> regime. Emission occurs without the long accumulation delay predicted for weak classical illumination.

Classical <electromagnetism> distributes incident <energy> continuously. By itself, that picture suggests that sufficiently intense or sufficiently long illumination could eject <electrons> at any <frequency>, and that emitted <electron> <energies> should increase with light <intensity>. It does not account for the observed threshold <frequency> and the linear dependence of $K_{\max}$ on <frequency> with slope $h$. Quantized <photon absorption> accounts for all three features.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

The incident <photon energy> is
$$
E_\gamma=\frac{hc}{\lambda}=\frac{1239.841984\,\mathrm{eV\,nm}}{456\,\mathrm{nm}}=2.71895\,\mathrm{eV}.
$$
Subtracting the <work function> gives $K_{\max}=0.768952\,\mathrm{eV}=1.2320\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J$. This is much less than the electron rest <energy>, so nonrelativistic <kinetic energy> is adequate:
$$
\boxed{v_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{2K_{\max}}{m_e}}\simeq5.20\times10^5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The magnitude of the retarding <stopping potential> satisfies $eV_s=K_{\max}$, so
$$
\boxed{V_s\simeq0.769\,\mathrm V.}
$$
The collector must be negative relative to the emitting surface to retard the <electrons>; the quoted number is the magnitude. Contact-potential corrections are neglected.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

The incident <photon> rate is $\dot N_\gamma=P/(hc/\lambda)\simeq2.30\times10^{15}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. The stated <quantum efficiency> is $\eta=0.005$, so the emitted <electron> rate is $\eta\dot N_\gamma\simeq1.15\times10^{13}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. If all emitted <electrons> are collected, the <photocurrent> magnitude is
$$
\boxed{I=e\eta\frac{P\lambda}{hc}\simeq1.84\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm A=1.84\,\mathrm{\mu A}.}
$$
The light <power> controls the number of events; the <work function> already determined the <kinetic energy> per emitted <electron> and should not be subtracted from the <photon energy> when counting incident <photons>. Incomplete collection would reduce the measured <photocurrent> below this value.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

An <electron> accelerated from negligible initial <kinetic energy> through $V$ gains $eV$. The largest possible single <photon energy> in an X-ray tube is this entire <kinetic energy>, so the <Duane-Hunt law> gives
$$
\boxed{\lambda_{\min}=\frac{hc}{eV}=\frac{1239.841984\,\mathrm{eV\,nm}}{25000\,\mathrm{eV}}=0.0496\,\mathrm{nm}=4.96\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm m.}
$$
The massive anode can absorb the required recoil <momentum> with negligible recoil <energy>; most actual events produce lower-<energy> <bremsstrahlung> <photons>. The acceleration <energy> $eV$ is valid without approximating the electron speed nonrelativistically.

==== e
{scope}

===== Solution

Work in the initial rest frame of the free <electron>. If it absorbed a <photon> of positive <energy> $E_\gamma$, <conservation of momentum> would give the final electron <momentum> magnitude $p_f=E_\gamma/c$, while <conservation of energy> would give $E_f=m_ec^2+E_\gamma$. But the final <electron> must obey the relativistic <energy-momentum relation>,
$$
E_f^2=m_e^2c^4+p_f^2c^2=m_e^2c^4+E_\gamma^2.
$$
Combining these statements gives
$$
(m_ec^2+E_\gamma)^2=m_e^2c^4+E_\gamma^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2m_ec^2E_\gamma=0,
$$
which contradicts $E_\gamma>0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{An isolated free electron cannot absorb a single real photon.}}
$$
The initial rest frame always exists for an <electron>, so this excludes the process in every inertial frame. <Compton scattering> is allowed because an outgoing <photon> carries additional <energy> and <momentum>. Absorption by an <electron> bound in matter is also possible because the rest of the system can share recoil.

=== 3
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

For a <particle on a circle>, the tangential speed is $v=r\dot\phi$, the <moment of inertia> is $I=mr^2$, and the <angular momentum> is $L=I\dot\phi=mrv$. Hence its <kinetic energy> is
$$
\boxed{K=\frac12mv^2=\frac12I\dot\phi^2=\frac{L^2}{2I}=\frac{L^2}{2mr^2}.}
$$
There is no <potential energy> variation along the circle in the stated model.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Replacing $L$ by the <angular momentum operator> gives the <Hamiltonian operator>
$$
\hat H=\frac{\hat L^2}{2I}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2I}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}.
$$
The time-dependent <Schrödinger equation> is therefore
$$
\boxed{i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2mr^2}\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial\phi^2}.}
$$
For a <stationary state>, separate variables as $\Psi(\phi,t)=\psi(\phi)T(t)$. Dividing by the product separates a function of $t$ from one of $\phi$:
$$
i\hbar\frac{\dot T}{T}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2I}\frac{\psi''}{\psi}=E.
$$
Thus $T=e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ up to a constant, and the time-independent <Schrödinger equation> is $-\hbar^2\psi''/(2I)=E\psi$.

Diagonalize the commuting <angular momentum operator> at the same time: $-i\hbar\psi'=\ell\psi$ has solutions $Ae^{i\ell\phi/\hbar}$. A <single-valued wavefunction> requires $e^{2\pi i\ell/\hbar}=1$, hence $\ell=n\hbar$ with $n\in\mathbb Z$. Normalizing with the angular probability measure $d\phi$ gives
$$
\int_0^{2\pi}|A|^2\,d\phi=1,\qquad
\boxed{\psi_n(\phi)=\frac{e^{in\phi}}{\sqrt{2\pi}},\qquad
\Psi_n(\phi,t)=\frac{e^{in\phi-iE_nt/\hbar}}{\sqrt{2\pi}},\quad n\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
These form an <orthonormal basis> under $\int_0^{2\pi}\psi_m^*\psi_n\,d\phi=\delta_{mn}$. The <periodic boundary conditions> apply to both the <wavefunction> and its first <derivative>, ensuring the physical self-adjoint <Hamiltonian operator>. For probability per <arc length> $ds=r\,d\phi$, the normalization instead reads $\psi_n=e^{in\phi}/\sqrt{2\pi r}$; the two conventions describe the same probabilities. General states are <superpositions> of these <energy eigenstates>.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Applying the <Hamiltonian operator> to $\psi_n$ gives $\psi_n''=-n^2\psi_n$, and therefore
$$
\boxed{E_n=\frac{\hbar^2n^2}{2I}=\frac{\hbar^2n^2}{2mr^2},\qquad n\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
The <ground state> $n=0$ has zero <kinetic energy>. Every positive <energy level> is of <quantum degeneracy> two, because $n$ and $-n$ correspond to opposite <angular momenta> with equal squared magnitude.

The confinement scale suggests a tangential <momentum> scale $\hbar/r$ and thus an excitation <energy> scale $\hbar^2/(mr^2)$, consistent with the <Heisenberg uncertainty principle>. More precisely the <de Broglie wavelength> must fit an integer number of times around the circumference: $2\pi r=|n|\lambda$, so $|p|=h/\lambda=|n|\hbar/r$. This explains both the $r^{-2}$ dependence and the $n^2$ sequence.

One must not infer a nonzero <ground-state energy> by applying $\Delta\phi\Delta L\ge\hbar/2$ naively. Angle is periodic and is not a globally canonical position operator on the circle. The uniform $n=0$ <wavefunction>, with $\Delta L=0$, is a concrete counterexample to that naive inequality. The ordinary Cartesian <Heisenberg uncertainty principle> is not violated by the zero-<angular-momentum> state.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

The emitted <photon energy> is the difference of the two <energy levels>:
$$
\Delta E=E_3-E_2=\frac{(9-4)\hbar^2}{2mr^2}=\frac{5\hbar^2}{2mr^2}.
$$
Using $E_\gamma=hc/\lambda$ gives
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\frac{2hcmr^2}{5\hbar^2}=\frac{4\pi cmr^2}{5\hbar}.}
$$
No numerical <mass> or <radius> is supplied, so this is the determinate result. It is the wavelength conditional on the transition occurring. An isolated <particle on a circle> with no coupling would remain in its <energy eigenstate>; a charged particle coupled to <electromagnetic radiation> can emit subject to its transition <selection rules>. The $3\to2$ change has $\Delta n=-1$, compatible with an electric-dipole angular factor for a charged ring particle.
