= Geophysics
{wiki}

Geophysics applies <physics> and <mathematics> to the structure, composition, and evolution of planets.

= Solid Earth
{c}
{parent=Geophysics}
{wiki}

The Solid Earth comprises a planet's solid inner core, liquid outer core, viscously deforming mantle, and mechanically stronger crust and lithosphere.

= Mantle convection
{parent=Solid Earth}
{wiki}

Mantle convection is the slow buoyancy-driven motion of a rocky planetary mantle. On geological timescales the solid mantle behaves as a highly viscous <Newtonian fluid> to first approximation.

= Rayleigh number
{title2=$\operatorname{Ra}$}
{c}
{parent=Mantle convection}
{wiki}

The Rayleigh number compares buoyancy driving with viscous and thermal diffusion. For a layer of thickness $h$, temperature drop $\Delta T$, density $\rho$, thermal expansion coefficient $\alpha_T$, gravitational acceleration $g$, thermal diffusivity $\kappa$, and dynamic viscosity $\mu$,
$$
\operatorname{Ra}=\frac{\rho g\alpha_T\Delta T h^3}{\mu\kappa}.
$$
Thermal convection begins when this exceeds a geometry-dependent critical value.

= Local Rayleigh-number closure
{c}
{parent=Rayleigh number}

A local Rayleigh-number closure estimates the thickness $\delta$ of a turbulent <thermal boundary layer> by setting
$$
\operatorname{Ra}_\delta
=\frac{g\Delta\rho\,\delta^3}{\rho\nu\kappa}
=\operatorname{Ra}_c,
$$
where $\operatorname{Ra}_c$ is an order-one or geometry-dependent critical value. Combining this with <Fourier's law> converts the boundary-layer temperature and density differences into a <heat flux> law.

= Penetrative convection
{parent=Rayleigh number}
{wiki}

Penetrative convection occurs when an unstable convecting region extends into, or is capped by, a stably stratified region. Water near its temperature of maximum density provides a common example: a cold stable layer can lie above a warmer turbulent convecting layer.

= Density anomaly of water
{parent=Penetrative convection}

Fresh water reaches maximum <mass density> near $4\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ at atmospheric pressure. A useful local <equation of state> is
$$
\rho=\rho_m\left[1-\alpha(T-T_m)^2\right],
$$
so water becomes less dense when its temperature moves either above or below $T_m$.

= Penetrative convection in an ice-covered lake
{parent=Penetrative convection}

In an ice-covered freshwater lake, a stagnant layer colder than the density-maximum temperature can cap a warmer turbulent region. Maximizing the boundary-layer <heat flux> obtained from a <local Rayleigh-number closure> gives an interface temperature $T_h$ satisfying
$$
T_m-T_h=\frac35(T_w-T_m)
$$
and a maximum upward <heat flux> proportional to $(T_w-T_m)^{5/3}$.

= Planetary ice shell
{parent=Geophysics}

A planetary ice shell is a solid water-rich layer overlying an ocean or deeper planetary interior. Its thickness can be controlled by <thermal conduction>, basal heat flux, surface radiation, and solid-state <mantle convection>.

= Planetary surface energy balance
{parent=Planetary ice shell}

A planetary surface energy balance equates absorbed short-wave radiation and heat supplied from below with reflected short-wave radiation and emitted long-wave radiation. With incident flux $F_r$, <Bond albedo> $\alpha$, conductive upward flux $F_c$, and <emissivity> $\epsilon$,
$$
(1-\alpha)F_r+F_c=\epsilon\sigma T_s^4.
$$

= Stefan problem
{c}
{parent=Planetary ice shell}
{wiki}

A Stefan problem is a <moving-boundary problem> in which a phase boundary moves as <latent heat> is released or absorbed. Temperature satisfies a <heat equation> in each phase and the <Stefan condition> supplies the interfacial energy balance.

= Stefan condition
{c}
{parent=Stefan problem}
{wiki}

The Stefan condition equates latent heat generated by motion of a phase boundary to the jump in conductive heat flux across it. With interface speed $V$ and normal directed from solid to liquid, one common sign convention is
$$
\rho L V=k_s\partial_nT_s-k_l\partial_nT_l.
$$

= Kinetic undercooling
{parent=Stefan problem}
{wiki=Kinetic_undercooling}

Kinetic undercooling makes the speed of a phase boundary proportional to the difference between its equilibrium and actual interface temperatures. A curvature correction raises the required undercooling for protrusions and therefore stabilizes short wavelengths.

= Morphological instability of a kinetically limited solidification front
{parent=Kinetic undercooling}

A planar front growing into a supercooled melt can amplify corrugations because protrusions enter colder liquid. Interface curvature opposes this effect. Linearization couples the perturbation growth rate to the decaying thermal exponent ahead of the front.

= Icicle ripple
{parent=Stefan problem}

An icicle ripple is a corrugation of the ice-water interface produced while a thin flowing water film freezes. Coupling <lubrication theory>, <surface tension>, <thermal conduction>, and the <Stefan condition> determines its growth and migration.

= Thin-film icicle-ripple instability
{parent=Icicle ripple}

For a water film of thickness $h$, lateral freezing speed $V$, ripple wavenumber $\alpha$, and $\Gamma=\gamma/(3\rho g)$, a long-wave model gives the <dispersion relation>
$$
\frac{h\sigma}{V}
=\frac{\alpha h}{1-i\Gamma\alpha^3h}.
$$
The fastest-growing mode has $\alpha_m=5^{-1/6}(\Gamma h)^{-1/3}$ and travels upward at speed $\sqrt5V/6$ when the vertical coordinate points downward.

= Phase boundary
{parent=Stefan problem}
{wiki}

A phase boundary is an interface separating distinct phases of matter. Its temperature, velocity, and flux jumps are constrained by phase equilibrium and conservation laws.

= Neumann solution of the Stefan problem
{c}
{parent=Stefan problem}

The Neumann solution places a planar phase boundary at $h(t)=2\lambda\sqrt{\kappa t}$. Similarity reduction of the <heat equation> gives temperature profiles in terms of the <error function>, while the <Stefan condition> determines $\lambda$ implicitly.

= Constitutional supercooling
{parent=Stefan problem}
{wiki}

Constitutional supercooling occurs ahead of a solidification front when rejected solute raises the local <liquidus> above the actual liquid temperature. The planar front can then become morphologically unstable because a displaced piece of solid enters supercooled liquid.

= Saline Stefan problem
{parent=Stefan problem}

A saline Stefan problem couples a <heat equation>, a solute <diffusion equation>, a <Stefan condition>, solute conservation, and a concentration-dependent <liquidus> at a moving solid-liquid boundary. Different thermal and solutal diffusivities produce nested boundary layers.

= Diffusion-controlled iceberg growth and ablation
{parent=Saline Stefan problem}

For a planar ice-ocean interface with no fluid motion, similarity places the boundary at $a(t)=2\lambda\sqrt{Dt}$. The sign of $\lambda$ distinguishes ice growth from ablation, while rejected salt during growth can cause <constitutional supercooling> in the adjacent ocean.

= Glaciology
{parent=Geophysics}
{wiki}

Glaciology studies glaciers, ice sheets, snow, and their interaction with climate, oceans, and the solid Earth.

= Ice sheet dynamics
{parent=Glaciology}
{wiki}

Ice sheet dynamics describes the gravity-driven deformation and basal motion of large terrestrial ice masses together with their mass balance.

= Ice cap
{parent=Ice sheet dynamics}
{wiki}

An ice cap is a dome-shaped terrestrial ice mass that flows outward from one or more ice divides and is smaller than an ice sheet.

= Shallow-ice approximation
{parent=Ice sheet dynamics}
{wiki}

The shallow-ice approximation uses the small ratio of ice thickness to horizontal extent to make pressure hydrostatic and balance its horizontal gradient primarily by vertical shear stress.

= Newtonian shallow-ice flux
{title2=$q=-gh^3h_x/(3\nu)$}
{parent=Shallow-ice approximation}

For a two-dimensional Newtonian ice sheet of thickness $h$ on a flat no-slip bed, hydrostatic pressure and vertical shear give the depth-integrated flux
$$
q=-\frac{gh^3}{3\nu}h_x,
$$
where $\nu$ is the kinematic viscosity. Mass conservation is $h_t+q_x=a$ for accumulation rate $a$.

= Ice divide
{parent=Ice sheet dynamics}
{wiki}

An ice divide is a line or point of maximum ice-surface elevation from which ice flows in opposite directions. Symmetry gives zero horizontal ice flux at the divide.

= Snowline
{parent=Ice sheet dynamics}
{wiki}

The snowline separates a region of net annual snow accumulation from a region of net ablation. A height-based idealization places it where the ice surface has a prescribed elevation $h^*$.

= Basal sliding
{parent=Ice sheet dynamics}
{wiki}

Basal sliding is motion of glacier ice relative to its bed. A sliding law relates the basal velocity to basal shear stress, effective pressure, roughness, or other bed properties.

= Ice shelf
{parent=Ice sheet dynamics}
{wiki}

An ice shelf is the floating extension of a grounded ice sheet. Hydrostatic flotation makes its surface slope and horizontal driving stress depend on the density contrast between ice and seawater.

= Grounding line
{parent=Ice shelf}
{wiki}

The grounding line is the transition where an ice sheet detaches from its bed and begins to float as an <ice shelf>. Ice thickness there obeys the hydrostatic flotation condition.

= Newtonian grounding-line flux
{parent=Grounding line}

Matching a slowly varying grounded Newtonian sheet to a freely extending Newtonian shelf gives, in a standard two-dimensional model,
$$
q_G=\frac{\sqrt{gg'}}{6\nu}h_G^3,
$$
where $h_G$ is the flotation thickness and $g'=g(\rho_w-\rho)/\rho_w$ is the <reduced gravity>.

= Shelfy stream
{parent=Grounding line}

A shelfy stream is a grounded, rapidly sliding ice stream whose internal resistance is dominated by longitudinal extension, as in an <ice shelf>, but which retains a basal drag. It is described by the <shallow-shelf approximation>.

= Shallow-shelf approximation
{parent=Ice shelf}
{wiki}

The shallow-shelf approximation treats horizontal velocity as nearly depth-independent and balances membrane stress, hydrostatic driving stress, and basal traction. For Newtonian ice of thickness $h$ and speed $u$, the extensional resultant is $4\mu h u_x$.

= Ice-shelf corrugation relaxation
{parent=Ice shelf}

Short-wavelength corrugations on the underside of a highly viscous ice shelf relax through local <Stokes flow>. For a wavelength much smaller than the shelf thickness, the deformation occupies a depth of order the inverse <wavenumber> and the ice may be treated as a viscous half-space.

= Postglacial rebound
{parent=Geophysics}
{wiki}

Postglacial rebound is uplift following removal of an ice load. It reflects viscous flow in the mantle and elastic or viscoelastic deformation of the lithosphere.

= Glacial isostatic adjustment
{parent=Postglacial rebound}
{wiki}

Glacial isostatic adjustment is the broader solid-Earth and sea-level response to growth and retreat of ice sheets.

= Flexural isostasy
{parent=Glacial isostatic adjustment}
{wiki}

Flexural isostasy models the lithosphere as an <elastic plate> that bends over a buoyant substrate. For a sinusoidal deflection of wavenumber $k$, bending contributes a restoring stress proportional to $Bk^4$, where $B$ is the plate's bending stiffness.

= Laurentide Ice Sheet
{c}
{parent=Glacial isostatic adjustment}
{wiki}

The Laurentide Ice Sheet covered much of northern North America during the last glacial period and left a major postglacial-rebound signal after retreat.

= Fennoscandian Ice Sheet
{c}
{parent=Glacial isostatic adjustment}
{wiki}

The Fennoscandian Ice Sheet covered northern Europe during the last glacial period and produced ongoing uplift around the Baltic region after retreat.
