= Graph minor
{wiki}
{title2=$H\preccurlyeq G$}

A <graph minor> compresses connected regions of a <graph> into single <vertices> while retaining selected connections between them. For finite <simple graphs>, $H\preccurlyeq G$ means that some sequence of deleting <vertices>, deleting <edges>, and performing <edge contractions> produces $H$ up to <graph isomorphism>. Deleting a <vertex> also deletes all its incident <edges>; deleting an <edge> leaves its endpoints. The <edge contraction> convention here removes resulting <self-loops> and duplicate <edges>.<Graph Theory by Reinhard Diestel/The Basics (author-hosted chapter preview)>{ref}

For an exact small example, let $G$ be the <cycle graph> $C_4$ with <vertices> $u,v,a,b$ and <edges> $uv,va,ab,bu$. Contract $uv$ to a new <vertex> $w$. The surviving <edges> are $wa,ab,bw$, giving the <complete graph> $K_3$. Thus $K_3\preccurlyeq C_4$, although $C_4$ has no <triangle in a graph> as a <subgraph>. The <branch sets of a graph minor> are $\{u,v\},\{a\},\{b\}$, with the original <edges> $va,ab,bu$ witnessing all three required adjacencies. The first panel of the <edge contraction> diagram shows this operation.

A <subgraph> uses existing <vertices> and <edges>; a <topological minor> permits a <graph subdivision> of the target as a <subgraph>, so target <edges> become <graph paths> with mutually disjoint interiors and no target <vertex> in an interior. An ordinary <graph minor> permits whole connected <branch sets of a graph minor> in place of target <vertices>. Every <topological minor> is a <graph minor>, by contracting the replacement <graph paths>.<Graph Theory by Reinhard Diestel/The Basics (author-hosted chapter preview)>[Proposition 1.7.3(i)]{ref}

The converse fails. Take the <tree> with <edges> $uv,ua,ub,vc,vd$. Contracting $uv$ gives the <star graph> $K_{1,4}$. Yet the original <tree> has <maximum degree> three, whereas every <graph subdivision> of $K_{1,4}$ retains a centre of <degree of a vertex> four. Hence $K_{1,4}$ is a <graph minor> but not a <topological minor> of this <tree>. The difference is that a connected <branch set of a graph minor> may gather connections incident to several original <vertices>.

The counts of <vertices> and <edges> never increase under these operations. However, the $C_4$ example shows that being a <bipartite graph> is not preserved: its <graph minor> $K_3$ contains an <odd cycle>. Its <chromatic number> also increases from two to three. A <connected graph> stays connected under an <edge contraction>, because the image of each <graph path> still connects its endpoints, but deletion can disconnect it. Thus one must check the particular <graph property> before treating it as preserved by taking <graph minors>.

Being a <planar graph> is preserved: in a crossing-free drawing, shrink a contracted <edge> and bring its incident <edges> into the merged <vertex>; deletion merely removes parts of the drawing. This makes a nonplanar <graph minor> a certificate that the original <graph> is nonplanar. More strongly, for a finite <simple graph>, being a <planar graph> is equivalent to having neither the <complete graph> $K_5$ nor the <complete bipartite graph> $K_{3,3}$ as a <graph minor>. Finding the corresponding <branch sets of a graph minor> settles nonplanarity even when no literal $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$ <subgraph> is present. The converse is a substantial theorem, rather than a consequence of preservation alone.<Graph Theory by Reinhard Diestel/Planar Graphs (author-hosted chapter preview)>[Theorem 4.4.6, equivalence of planarity and exclusion of K5 and K3,3 minors]{ref}

= Topological minor
{parent=Graph minor}
{title2=$H\preccurlyeq_{\mathrm{top}}G$}

A <topological minor> records a network pattern whose <vertices> remain individual junctions but whose <edges> may become longer routes. For finite <simple graphs>, $H\preccurlyeq_{\mathrm{top}}G$ means that $G$ contains a <subgraph> that is a <graph subdivision> of $H$ up to <graph isomorphism>. Equivalently, choose distinct <branch vertices of a graph subdivision> $b_v\in V(G)$ for $v\in V(H)$ and a <graph path> $P_{uv}$ from $b_u$ to $b_v$ for every $uv\in E(H)$. These are <internally vertex-disjoint paths>, and no internal <vertex> of any $P_{uv}$ is a <branch vertex of a graph subdivision>. Only endpoints corresponding to a shared target <vertex> may be shared.<Graph Theory by Reinhard Diestel/The Basics (author-hosted chapter preview)>[§1.7, printed p. 18: subdivisions; printed p. 21: topological-minor embeddings]{ref}

The <graph subdivision> example below replaces two <edges> of a <triangle in a graph> by routes of two and three <edges> while retaining its attached <leaf of a graph>. If the host <graph> also contains an extra <edge> $BD$, the same routes still witness a <topological minor>: delete $BD$ when selecting the <subgraph>. An <induced subgraph> is not required. However, routes that merely connect the right pairs are insufficient: using a <branch vertex of a graph subdivision> as an intermediate junction, or making two routes share an internal <vertex>, can change the local network structure.

Every <topological minor> is a <graph minor>. In each <branch path of a graph subdivision>, contract all but one <edge>; the disjoint interiors let this shorten each route without merging distinct <branch vertices of a graph subdivision>. This recovers $H$ after deleting unused <vertices> and <edges>.<Graph Theory by Reinhard Diestel/The Basics (author-hosted chapter preview)>[Proposition 1.7.3(i), printed p. 21]{ref} The converse fails because an ordinary <branch set of a graph minor> can gather several junctions into one.

For the <tree> $T$ with <vertices> $u,v,a,b,c,d$ and <edges> $uv,ua,ub,vc,vd$, contracting $uv$ produces a <star graph> $K_{1,4}$: the <branch sets of a graph minor> are $\{u,v\},\{a\},\{b\},\{c\},\{d\}$. Both $u,v$ have <degree of a vertex> three; each of the other four <vertices> is a <leaf of a graph> with <degree of a vertex> one. The new centre $w$ has four distinct <graph neighbours> $a,b,c,d$; its <degree of a vertex> is $3+3-2=4$, in agreement with the <edge contraction> formula.

\Image[topological-minor-tree-contraction-degree-obstruction.png]
{title=A <graph minor> can combine two junctions: contracting $uv$ creates a <star graph> with a centre of <degree of a vertex> four}

Yet $K_{1,4}$ is not a <topological minor> of $T$. In a <graph subdivision> of $K_{1,4}$, four <branch paths of a graph subdivision> must leave the centre through four different <edges>; sharing their first internal <vertex> would violate internal disjointness. Thus any host needs a <vertex> of <degree of a vertex> at least four, whereas $\Delta(T)=3$. More generally, $H\preccurlyeq_{\mathrm{top}}G$ implies $\Delta(H)\leq\Delta(G)$, where $\Delta$ is the <maximum degree of a graph>. This bound concerns the host <graph>; extra unused <edges> may make its <branch vertices of a graph subdivision> have larger <degrees of vertices> than their target counterparts.

This distinction matters when a network's individual junctions have a limited number of connections: replacing a connection by a route preserves the number of routes meeting at each chosen junction, while contracting a connected region can combine connections from separate junctions. It also explains why a small nonplanar pattern can hide inside long routes. By <Kuratowski's theorem>, a finite <simple graph> is a <planar graph> exactly when it has neither the <complete graph> $K_5$ nor the <complete bipartite graph> $K_{3,3}$ as a <topological minor>. The obstruction is a <graph subdivision> inside the network, regardless of the number of intermediate <vertices>.

= Rooted branch-set reduction
{parent=Graph minor}
{title2=$m=h-q/2$}

Let $q\ge2$ be even, let $S=\{x_1,\ldots,x_q\}$, and suppose a <graph> has $h$ disjoint nonempty <vertex> <sets> $C_1,\ldots,C_h$, where $h\ge d+3q/2$. Suppose each $G[C_i]$ is connected or every one of its <graph components> meets $S$, and each $C_i$ is adjacent to all but at most $d$ of the other $C_j$ not meeting $S$. Assume also that no <rooted separation of a graph> of order below $q$ avoids $d+1$ of these <sets>. Then there are $m=h-q/2$ disjoint connected <vertex> <sets> $D_1,\ldots,D_m$ such that $x_i\in D_i$ for $1\le i\le q$ and each of these first $q$ <sets> is adjacent to all but at most $d$ of $D_{q+1},\ldots,D_m$.

Here adjacency of <sets> means existence of an <edge> between them. A <rooted separation of a graph> is $(A,B)$ with $A\cup B=V(G)$, $S\subseteq A$, no <edges> between $A\setminus B$ and $B\setminus A$, and order $|A\cap B|$. It avoids $C_i$ when $A\cap C_i=\varnothing$. We prove the reduction by <induction>, first on the <vertex> count and then on the <edge> count; any counterexample chosen minimal in this ordering will be impossible.

We may delete <edges> joining two <vertices> of $S$. They cannot affect <rooted separations of a graph>, since $S$ lies wholly on the first side. Within a branch <set>, their deletion can only split a <graph component> into <graph components> each containing an endpoint in $S$, so the branch-set hypothesis also persists. An isolated <vertex> outside $S$ and all branch <sets> can be deleted. An isolated <vertex> $v\in S$ is also impossible, since $(S,V(G)\setminus\{v\})$ has order $q-1$ and avoids at least $h-q\ge d+q/2\ge d+1$ <sets>. Thus in a minimal counterexample $S$ is independent, and every isolated <vertex> outside $S$ belongs to a singleton branch <set>.

Consider a <rooted separation of a graph> $(A,B)$ of order exactly $q$ avoiding at least $d+1$ branch <sets>, and put $S'=A\cap B$. Restrict to $G'=G[B]-E(G[S'])$ and $C'_i=C_i\cap B$. Each $C'_i$ is nonempty: any $C_i$ not itself avoided is adjacent to at least one of the $d+1$ avoided <sets>, and the endpoint of such an <edge> in $C_i$ must lie in $B$. If a <graph component> of $G'[C'_i]$ misses $S'$, it lies in $B\setminus A$ and is a whole <graph component> of $G[C_i]$, with no <vertex> of $S$. The original hypothesis then forces $G[C_i]$ to be connected and $C'_i=C_i$, entirely outside $A$. Thus each restricted <set> is connected or all its <graph components> meet $S'$. In particular every $C'_j$ missing $S'$ equals an original <set> outside $A$, so all its required adjacencies from $C'_i$ persist.

A <rooted separation of a graph> $(A',B')$ of $G'$ avoiding $d+1$ restricted <sets> lifts to $(A\cup A',B')$ in $G$, of the same order: an avoided restricted <set> misses $S'$, hence is an original <set> outside $A$. The <edges> removed inside $S'$ do not obstruct lifting, since $S'\subseteq A'$. This proves the required separation hypothesis for $G'$. If $G'$ is smaller, <induction> gives the desired connected <sets> rooted at $S'$. Moreover $G[A]$ has $q$ disjoint <graph paths> from $S$ to $S'$, by the <Menger theorem>. Indeed a separator of smaller order between these <sets> would give a <rooted separation of a graph> of $G$ of smaller order still avoiding those $d+1$ original <sets>. Truncate the <graph paths> at their first visits to $S'$, label their endpoints accordingly, and adjoin each <graph path> to the corresponding rooted connected <set>. They meet $B$ only in their distinct endpoints, so this gives the desired <sets> for $G$, a contradiction. Consequently in a minimal counterexample every such separation of order $q$ has $B=V(G)$ and $A=S$, an <independent set>.

Now contract any <edge> whose endpoints do not belong to two different branch <sets>. Its endpoints cannot both lie in $S$, so the image of $S$ still has size $q$. The branch-set conditions persist. If the contraction created a forbidden <rooted separation of a graph> of order below $q$, its lift would have order below $q$, except possibly when the contracted <vertex> lies in its separator. In that case the lifted order increases by one and is at most $q$. Equality would put the contracted <edge> inside $A$, contradicting the just-proved independence of $A$ for an order-$q$ separation avoiding $d+1$ <sets>. Thus the separation hypothesis also persists. Applying <induction> in the contracted <graph> and expanding the contracted <vertex> would produce the required disjoint connected <sets>. Hence **every <edge> in a minimal counterexample joins two different branch <sets>**.

Every <vertex> outside the branch <sets> would be isolated, since every <edge> joins two branch <sets>; such an isolated <vertex> could be deleted. Thus the branch <sets> cover the <graph>. Each nonsingleton branch <set> is therefore an <independent set>, and all its <vertices> belong to $S$, because each of its singleton <graph components> must meet $S$. Let $C$ be the union of the nonsingleton branch <sets>, and write $c=|C|\le q$. There is a <matching in a graph> from $C$ into $V(G)\setminus S$. Otherwise the <Hall marriage theorem> gives $X\subseteq C$ with neighbourhood $Y\subseteq V(G)\setminus S$ satisfying $|Y|<|X|$. Since $S$ is independent, $(S\cup Y,V(G)\setminus X)$ is a <rooted separation of a graph> of order $q-|X|+|Y|<q$. It avoids every singleton branch <set> outside $S\cup Y$, of which there are at least
$$
|V(G)|-q-|Y|\ge |V(G)|-q-c+1\ge h-\frac c2-q+1\ge h-\frac{3q}2+1\ge d+1.
$$
The penultimate counting bound uses that the nonsingleton <sets> number at most $c/2$, so $|V(G)|-c\ge h-c/2$. This contradicts the separation hypothesis.

Use that <matching in a graph> to make a two-vertex connected <set> for each terminal in $C$; terminals outside $C$ remain singleton <sets>. There are at least
$$
|V(G)|-q-c\ge h-\frac{3q}2=m-q
$$
unused <vertices>, all singleton original branch <sets>. Choose $m-q$ of them for the remaining $D_i$. Every rooted $D_i$ contains a singleton original branch <set>: either its terminal, or its terminal's matched <neighbour>. That singleton misses at most $d$ of the selected nonterminal singleton <sets>. This constructs the desired family and completes the proof.

= Complete graph minor density threshold
{parent=Graph minor}
{title2=$c(t)$}

The complete graph minor density threshold is
$$
c(t)=\inf\{c:\text{every nonempty finite graph }G\text{ with }e(G)\geq c|G|\text{ has a }K_t\text{ minor}\}.
$$
The density here is <edge> count divided by order, half the average <degree of a vertex>. For large $t$, this threshold has order $t\sqrt{\log t}$, with the logarithm taken to base $e$.

= Dense minor with bounded order and high minimum degree
{parent=Complete graph minor density threshold}

For each positive integer $k$, a nonempty <graph> satisfying $e(G)\geq11k|G|$ has a <graph minor> $H$ satisfying
$$
|H|\leq11k+2,\qquad 2\delta(H)\geq |H|+4k-1.
$$
The density hypothesis is a lower bound. Reversing its inequality would be false for an edgeless <graph>. This auxiliary lemma can be used to establish a $7t\sqrt{\log t}$ upper bound for the <complete graph minor density threshold>.

= Branch set of a graph minor
{parent=Graph minor}

= Branch sets of a graph minor
{synonym}

A <branch set of a graph minor> is the connected region representing one target <vertex>. For finite <simple graphs> $G$ and $H$, a witness to $H\preccurlyeq G$ assigns a nonempty <set> $B_x\subseteq V(G)$ to each $x\in V(H)$, such that the <sets> are pairwise disjoint, each <induced subgraph> $G[B_x]$ is a <connected graph>, and every $xy\in E(H)$ has a witnessing <edge> of $G$ between $B_x$ and $B_y$. Extra <edges> between <branch sets of a graph minor> are allowed: they can be deleted. Unused <vertices> are also allowed.<Minor Containment and Disjoint Paths in almost-linear time by Tuukka Korhonen and others>{ref}

\Image[graph-minor-connected-branch-set-adjacency.png]
{title=Connected <branch sets of a graph minor> and their witnessing <edges>, with an optional <edge> deletion}

In the diagram, the host <graph> has <vertex set> $\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$ and <edges>
$$
12,\ 34,\ 56,\ 23,\ 45,\ 61,\ 24.
$$
Choose $B_A=\{1,2\}$, $B_B=\{3,4\}$, and $B_C=\{5,6\}$. Their internal <edges> $12,34,56$ establish connectedness. The <edges> $23,45,61$ witness $AB,BC,CA$, respectively, so they form a model of the <complete graph> $K_3$. The additional <edge> $24$ also joins $B_A$ to $B_B$; it supplies another witness for the same target <edge>, not a fourth target <edge>. Contracting $12,34,56$ and merging duplicate <edges> gives exactly $\{AB,BC,CA\}$.

The same <branch sets of a graph minor> also witness the <path graph> $A,B,C$: retain the witnesses $23,45$, and delete $61$ to remove the unwanted $AC$ adjacency. This demonstrates why the definition requires an <edge> for every target adjacency, without forbidding other adjacencies in the host <graph>. For a <complete graph> $K_t$, there are no unwanted pairs: its model consists of $t$ disjoint connected <branch sets of a graph minor> with an <edge> between every pair.

To turn a model into an operation sequence, keep a <spanning tree> inside each $G[B_x]$, keep one witnessing <edge> for each <edge> of $H$, and delete all other <edges> and unused <vertices>. Contract each chosen <spanning tree> to one <vertex>. Disjointness prevents two target <vertices> from merging; connectedness provides the <edge contractions> that merge each individual region. Conversely, track the original <vertices> merged into every surviving <vertex> during a deletion-and-<edge contraction> sequence. They form disjoint connected <branch sets of a graph minor>, and each surviving <edge> comes from an original <edge> between its two regions. A list of these regions, their connecting <trees>, and their witnessing <edges> therefore gives a directly checkable certificate.

= Random branch-set construction of a complete graph minor
{parent=Branch set of a graph minor}

In a dense <graph> with many <common neighbours> for every pair of <vertices>, choose disjoint random small sets. A set is good if few <vertices> have no neighbour in it. The <Markov inequality> controls the number of bad sets, and random sampling makes almost every pair of good sets adjacent. Add unused <common neighbours> to connect each set and to repair the remaining missing adjacencies. If the number of used <vertices> stays below every common-neighbour count, this constructs disjoint <branch sets of a graph minor> representing a large <complete graph>.

= Edge contraction
{parent=Graph minor}
{wiki}
{title2=$G/e$}

An <edge contraction> merges the two endpoints of an existing <edge>. For a finite <simple graph> $G$ and $e=uv$, replace $u,v$ by a new <vertex> $w$. Retain every <edge> with neither endpoint in $\{u,v\}$, and join $w$ to every member of
$$
N_{G/e}(w)=(N_G(u)\cup N_G(v))\setminus\{u,v\}.
$$
Here $N_G(u)$ and $N_G(v)$ denote the original open <graph neighbourhoods>, and $N_{G/e}(w)$ is the new <graph neighbourhood>. Remove the <self-loop> arising from $uv$ and merge duplicate <edges>, so the result $G/e$ is again a <simple graph>. This is not deletion of $uv$, which keeps both original <vertices>.<Graph Theory by Reinhard Diestel/The Basics (author-hosted chapter preview)>{ref}

\Image[graph-edge-contraction-common-neighbour-count.png]
{title=<Edge contraction> of a four-cycle and a <triangle in a graph>: <common neighbours> determine the lost <edge> count}

The left example contracts $uv$ in the <cycle graph> $C_4$ to give $\{wa,ab,bw\}$. Its endpoints have no <common neighbours>, so four <edges> become three. The right example contracts $uv$ in the <triangle in a graph> with <edges> $uv,ua,va$. Its endpoints have the single <common neighbour> $a$, so the two <edges> $ua,va$ become one <edge> $wa$: three <edges> become one.

For any such <edge contraction>, the exact counts are
$$
|V(G/e)|=|V(G)|-1,\qquad |E(G/e)|=|E(G)|-1-|N_G(u)\cap N_G(v)|.
$$
The contracted <edge> accounts for one loss. Each <common neighbour> $z$ contributes exactly one further loss, since $uz,vz$ become a single <edge> $wz$. All other <edges> correspond one-to-one to surviving <edges>. In particular the new <degree of a vertex> is
$$
\deg_{G/e}(w)=\deg_G(u)+\deg_G(v)-2-|N_G(u)\cap N_G(v)|.
$$
These formulas depend on simplifying to a <simple graph>; a <multigraph> convention that retains duplicate <edges> has different counts. In the six-<vertex> <branch set of a graph minor> diagram, contracting $12$ loses one <edge>, contracting the resulting $34$ loses two because of its <common neighbour> $A$, and contracting $56$ loses one. Hence its seven <edges> become the three <edges> of $K_3$: $7-1-2-1=3$.

An <edge contraction> can increase a <degree of a vertex> even while decreasing both total counts. For example, contracting $uv$ in the <tree> with <edges> $uv,ua,ub,vc,vd$ merges two <vertices> of <degree of a vertex> three into one of <degree of a vertex> four. This is the mechanism behind the distinction between a <graph minor> and a <topological minor>.
