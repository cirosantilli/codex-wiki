= Quantum scattering
{wiki}

Quantum scattering compares a wavefunction's incoming and outgoing asymptotic components.

= Dwell time
{disambiguate=quantum mechanics}
{parent=Quantum scattering}
{title2=$\tau_D=\int_\Omega|\psi(x)|^2dx/j_{\rm in}$}

= Dwell time
{synonym}

For a stationary one-dimensional <scattering state> with incident <probability current> $j_{\rm in}>0$, the dwell time in a region $\Omega$ is its integrated <probability density> divided by incident flux. It measures occupancy per incoming rate, not a classical trajectory or a uniquely defined tunnelling traversal time.

= Dwell time in a semi-infinite potential step
{parent=Dwell time (quantum mechanics)}
{title2=$\tau_D=2mk/[\hbar\kappa(k^2+\kappa^2)]$}

For $0<E<V_0$, set $k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ and $\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar$. Continuity at the <potential step> gives the evanescent amplitude $c=2k/(k+i\kappa)$ for unit incident amplitude. Integrating $|c|^2e^{-2\kappa x}$ over $x>0$ gives $|c|^2/(2\kappa)$. Dividing by the incident <probability current> $\hbar k/m$ proves the displayed <dwell time>.

= Amplitude poles and analytic continuation of transmission probability
{parent=Quantum scattering}
{title2=$T(k)=t(k)t(-k)$}

For a real one-dimensional potential, a real-axis <transmission probability> is a modulus square. Its algebraic complex continuation need not have only physical <Bound-state pole>[bound-state poles]. In a square well, $t=e^{-ika}/D(k)$ while the continued <probability> is $1/[D(k)D(-k)]$. Zeros of the second factor can encode a different <boundary condition>. In units $\hbar^2/(2m)=1$, a well of width two and depth $1+\cot^2 1$ has a continued-probability <pole> at $k=i\cot1$ which satisfies neither decaying even nor decaying odd matching. The physical bound-state criterion uses the outgoing <amplitude> on its decaying sheet.

= Angular distribution
{parent=Quantum scattering}
{title2=$d\Gamma/d\Omega,\quad d\sigma/d\Omega$}

The decay probability or scattering <cross section> per solid angle, relative to stated polar and azimuthal axes. Spin correlations and angular-momentum selection can make it anisotropic; changing the polar axis reverses terms odd in its direction cosine.

= Forward-backward asymmetry
{parent=Angular distribution}
{title2=$A_{\rm FB}=(\sigma_F-\sigma_B)/(\sigma_F+\sigma_B)$}

The normalized difference of forward and backward <cross sections> relative to a specified incoming-particle axis. For $d\sigma/dc=K[(A+B)(1+c^2)+2(A-B)c]$ with $c=\cos\theta$, integration gives $\sigma_F-\sigma_B=2K(A-B)$ and $\sigma_F+\sigma_B=8K(A+B)/3$, hence $A_{\rm FB}=3(A-B)/(4(A+B))$. The <pole forward-backward asymmetry for charged leptons> is its specialized <Z boson> result.

= Quarter-wave matching layer for a quantum potential step
{parent=Quantum scattering}
{title2=$k_1a=\pi/2,\quad k_1^2=k_0k_2$}

For a constant-mass one-dimensional particle propagating through three regions of potentials $0,V_1,V_0$, let $k_j$ be the real positive wavevectors. A layer with $k_1a=\pi/2$ makes the two interface reflection amplitudes cancel exactly when $k_1^2=k_0k_2$. Equivalently $E-V_1=\sqrt{E(E-V_0)}$. For an increasing intermediate step this requires $E>V_0$ and $V_0/2<V_1<V_0$. The result follows from $r=(r_{01}+r_{12}e^{2ik_1a})/(1+r_{01}r_{12}e^{2ik_1a})$ and $r_{ij}=(k_i-k_j)/(k_i+k_j)$.

= Discrete-state continuum scattering amplitude
{parent=Quantum scattering}
{title2=$f_k=-A/(E-\widetilde\epsilon+iAk)$}

For a real point coupling $g$ between a three-dimensional continuum and one discrete state, $A=mg^2/(2\pi\hbar^2)$. After the short-distance level shift is absorbed, the isotropic amplitude has the displayed form. The imaginary part is fixed by the outgoing <boundary condition>, and $\operatorname{Im}(1/f_k)=-k$ supplies elastic <unitarity>.

= Scattering cross-section
{parent=Quantum scattering}
{title2=$\sigma=R_{\rm sc}/j_{\rm in}$}

A scattering cross-section divides the scattered rate per target by the incident <particle flux>. Its differential form resolves that rate by outgoing direction. It has units of area for three-dimensional scattering and length for two-dimensional scattering. In two dimensions, the convention $\psi\sim e^{ikr\cos\theta}+e^{i\pi/4}f(\theta)e^{ikr}/\sqrt{kr}$ gives $d\sigma/d\theta=|f(\theta)|^2/k$ by comparing outgoing and incident <probability currents>.

= Scattering plane
{parent=Scattering cross-section}

The plane spanned by incoming and outgoing propagation directions when those directions are not parallel or antiparallel. Polarization perpendicular or parallel to it is useful when expressing a <differential scattering cross-section>. The plane is not unique at exact forward or backward scattering.

= Step potential
{parent=Quantum scattering}
{wiki}
{title2=$V(x)=V_0\Theta(x)$}

= Potential step
{synonym}

For constant <mass> $m$, a finite jump from <potential energy> zero at $x<0$ to $V_0>0$ at $x>0$ requires continuity of the <wavefunction> and its first <derivative>. With incidence only from the left and $E>V_0$, put $k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ and $q=\sqrt{2m(E-V_0)}/\hbar$. Matching $A+B=C$ and $k(A-B)=qC$ gives reflected <probability amplitude> $B/A=(k-q)/(k+q)$ and <transmission amplitude> $C/A=2k/(k+q)$. The <transmission probability> is $(q/k)|C/A|^2$, conserving <probability current>.

For $0<E<V_0$, replace $q$ by $i\kappa$, where $\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar$ is the <tunnelling decay constant>. The right-hand <wavefunction> is $Ce^{-\kappa x}$, with zero <probability current>. Its <probability density> decays on length $1/(2\kappa)$, while its amplitude decays on length $1/\kappa$. The semi-infinite raised region gives total reflection, unlike a finite <potential barrier> allowing <quantum tunnelling> into a second allowed region.

= Evanescent amplitude versus transmitted current
{parent=Step potential}
{title2=$j[Te^{-\alpha x}]=0$}

For a real positive decay coefficient alpha, a stationary exponential tail has zero <probability current> because $\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\partial_x\psi)=0$. Matching a subthreshold <potential step> can nevertheless give a nonzero coefficient T with $|T|^2>1$ relative to a unit incident plane-wave amplitude. This measures the density just beyond the step, not a flux-transmission probability. It describes penetration without a propagating outgoing channel in the semi-infinite high-potential region.

= Quantum reflection at a downward step
{parent=Step potential}
{title2=$E\to0^+:\quad r\to-1,\ t\to0,\ R\to1,\ T\to0$}

For $V_0<0$, $k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ tends to zero as $E\to0^+$ while $q=\sqrt{2m(E+|V_0|)}/\hbar$ stays finite. Matching gives <probability amplitudes> $r=(k-q)/(k+q)$ and $t=2k/(k+q)$, so <quantum reflection probability> $R=r^2\to1$ and <transmission probability> $T=(q/k)|t|^2\sim4k/q\to0$. The final region is classically allowed; reflection results from the abrupt spatial change in the <wavefunction>'s <wavenumber>. These are limits of positive-energy incoming states, not a flux ratio evaluated at exactly zero incident current.

= Potential-step threshold wavefunction
{parent=Step potential}
{title2=$\psi(x)=2A\cos(kx)\ (x\leq0),\quad\psi(x)=2A\ (x>0)$}

For a positive <step potential> and left incidence, take $E\to V_0^+$ at fixed incident <probability amplitude> $A$. The transmitted <wavenumber> tends to zero while the incident wavenumber tends to $k=\sqrt{2mV_0}/\hbar$. <Wavefunction matching at a finite potential step> gives reflected amplitude $A$ and transmitted amplitude $2A$, producing the spatial <wavefunction> in the title. The left <probability density> is $4|A|^2\cos^2kx$ and the right density $4|A|^2$. The transmitted <probability current> still tends to zero because its velocity tends to zero. Over adjacent intervals of length $2\pi/k$, the right integrated density is twice the left. These ratios use finite local integrals of a non-normalizable stationary scattering state.

= Coulomb distortion in electron scattering
{c}
{parent=Quantum scattering}

The change in incident and outgoing <electron> <wavefunctions> caused by the target <Coulomb potential>. It can invalidate a <plane wave> treatment in the <Born approximation>, especially for a large target <atomic number>.

= Scattering channel
{parent=Quantum scattering}

A specified asymptotic configuration of the particles and their internal <quantum states> in <quantum scattering>. An incoming channel can scatter into several outgoing channels; <S-matrix> elements give their <probability amplitudes> with a stated <probability current> normalization. A channel can be energetically inaccessible even when other channels are open.

= Scattering angle
{parent=Quantum scattering}
{title2=$\theta$}

The <angle> between specified incoming and outgoing directions in a <collision>; the reference frame and outgoing particle must be stated.

= X-ray scattering techniques
{c}
{parent=Quantum scattering}
{wiki}

<X-ray scattering techniques> use the change in a scattered <photon>'s direction and, in inelastic measurements, its <energy> to probe matter. <Inelastic X-ray scattering> transfers energy to excitations such as <phonons>; elastic methods probe spatial structure without that energy transfer.

= Inelastic X-ray scattering
{parent=X-ray scattering techniques}

<Inelastic X-ray scattering> measures <energy> and <momentum> transferred from <X-rays> to matter. With sufficiently high <energy resolution>, it can map a <phonon dispersion relation> by measuring inelastic peaks at different momentum transfers. <Crystal momentum> is determined modulo a <reciprocal lattice> vector.

= Inelastic scattering
{parent=Quantum scattering}
{wiki}

In <inelastic scattering>, the participants exchange <energy> with internal degrees of freedom, so their total translational <kinetic energy> need not be conserved.

= Quantum reflection probability
{parent=Quantum scattering}
{title2=$R=|r|^2$}

= Reflection probability
{synonym}

For one-dimensional scattering from a real potential, write the left exterior <wavefunction> as $e^{ikx}+re^{-ikx}$ with $k>0$. The reflected-to-incident probability-current ratio is $R=|r|^2$, since both components have the same speed and their currents have opposite signs. This probability differs from the complex reflection amplitude $r$. With transmission into a right exterior <wavenumber> $q$, the <transmission probability> is $T=(q/k)|t|^2$ and conservation of current gives $R+T=1$ when there is no absorption.

= Quantum reflection amplitude
{parent=Quantum reflection probability}
{title2=$r=A_{\rm reflected}/A_{\rm incident}$}

A <quantum reflection amplitude> is the complex ratio of the outgoing reflected wave to the incident wave in a chosen scattering convention. In one reflected channel with the same exterior speed, the reflection <probability> is $|r|^2$. Its phase affects <interference> even when its modulus is one; shifting the reference plane changes that phase.

= Quantum step-reflection phase
{parent=Quantum reflection probability}
{title2=$r=(k-i\kappa)/(k+i\kappa),\quad\delta=-2\arctan(\kappa/k)$}

A particle incident below a semi-infinite step has a unit-modulus <quantum reflection amplitude> but a nontrivial phase. Continuity of the oscillating and evanescent solutions yields the displayed coefficient, for the incident phase origin at the interface. Its decaying exterior wave has zero net <probability current>. The phase depends on the chosen scattering convention; the unit reflection <probability> does not.

= Transmission amplitude
{parent=Quantum scattering}
{title2=$\psi\sim e^{ik_LX}+r e^{-ik_LX}\ (X\to-\infty),\quad\psi\sim t e^{ik_RX}\ (X\to+\infty)$}

For one-dimensional <quantum scattering>, $t$ is the complex amplitude of the outgoing transmitted wave relative to the incoming wave. The <transmission probability> is $|t|^2$ times the ratio of transmitted and incident flux velocities; with equal asymptotic dispersion it is $|t|^2$. A <reflectionless potential> has $r=0$ and a unit-modulus transmission amplitude whose phase shifts continuum quantization. The <supersymmetric factorization of the one-soliton potential> gives $t(k)=(k+im)/(k-im)$ for its single-bound-state potential.

= Scattering matrix
{synonym}

= Scattering operator
{synonym}

The <S-matrix> maps incoming asymptotic states to outgoing asymptotic states. Its connected transition matrix elements contain an overall energy-momentum <Dirac delta function> and an invariant <scattering amplitude>. In <quantum field theory>, the <LSZ reduction formula> obtains these elements from amputated field correlators.

= Unitarity cut
{parent=S-matrix}

A contribution to the <unitarity> relation for the <S-matrix> obtained by placing a set of intermediate lines <on-shell> and summing their intermediate states. In a physical-state formulation only physical states enter the sum. When covariant <gauge boson> index sums include auxiliary polarizations, matching <Faddeev-Popov ghost field> contributions are needed to reproduce the physical result in a <non-Abelian gauge theory>.

= Redundant pole of a scattering matrix
{parent=S-matrix}

A pole of a <scattering matrix> arising from the numerator $F(-k)$ rather than a zero of the incoming <Jost function> $F(k)$ can fail to correspond to a <bound state>. For $F(k)=(k-i\kappa)/(k+i\alpha)$, the pole at $i\kappa$ is associated with the incoming zero; the pole at $i\alpha$ comes from the numerator. The regular Eckart realization has $\alpha>\kappa$. The distinction and this explicit example are discussed in https://dipot.ulb.ac.be/dspace/bitstream/2013/373712/4/2306.12216.pdf.

= Jost function
{parent=S-matrix}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$F(k)$}

For radial <quantum scattering>, the incoming coefficient of the regular solution defines the Jost function, with convention $S(k)=F(-k)/F(k)$. A zero at $k=i\kappa$, $\kappa>0$, removes the exponentially growing component and produces a <bound state>, subject to regularity and normalizability. Poles of the outgoing numerator alone need not produce <bound states>.

= Unit-modulus hyperbolic scattering block
{parent=S-matrix}
{title2=$S_u(\theta)=\sinh[(\theta+iu)/2]/\sinh[(\theta-iu)/2]$}

For real $u$, this scalar <S-matrix> block satisfies $S_u(\theta)S_u(-\theta)=1$ and <Hermitian analyticity of a two-particle S-matrix>. It therefore has modulus one for real <rapidity> differences. For $0<u<\pi$ its <pole> at $\theta=iu$ has <residue> $2i\sin u$ and admits a direct-channel <bound-state pole> interpretation. Its crossed block is $\cosh[(\theta-iu)/2]/\cosh[(\theta+iu)/2]$. The endpoints $u=0$ and $u=\pi$ are removable degeneracies giving constant amplitudes, so a particle cannot be inferred there from the uncancelled denominator alone. This block specifies an amplitude; a complete theory must also obey the remaining <crossing symmetry> and <bootstrap fusion> constraints.

= Physical rapidity strip
{parent=S-matrix}
{title2=$0<\operatorname{Im}\vartheta<\pi$}

For relativistic two-body scattering in one spatial dimension, this strip is the standard domain containing bound-state and crossed-channel <poles>. A stable-particle interpretation requires appropriate <pole> kinematics and residues; not every singularity automatically represents a new particle. Crossing relates <rapidity> $\vartheta$ to $i\pi-\vartheta$.

= Crossed-channel pole in diagonal factorized scattering
{parent=Physical rapidity strip}
{title2=$t(i\alpha)=m_a^2+m_b^2-2m_am_b\cos\alpha$}

A physical-strip <pole> of a diagonal <S-matrix> can come from exchange of an existing particle in the crossed channel. For external <masses> $m_a,m_b$, use the momentum difference, rather than the sum, to identify the exchanged <mass> through the displayed invariant. <Crossing symmetry> turns the pole at $i\alpha$ into a direct pole at $i(\pi-\alpha)$ of the particle-antiparticle channel. For $m_B=2m\cos(u/2)$, the <pole> of $S_{BA}$ at $iu/2$ has $t=m^2$ and exchanges the existing charge-one particle; it is not a new charge-three <bound state>.

= Relativistic bound-state mass from a rapidity pole
{parent=Physical rapidity strip}
{title2=$m_c^2=m_a^2+m_b^2+2m_am_b\cos u$}

At relative <rapidity> $iu$, the analytically continued sum of the constituent <four-momentum> vectors is on the bound-state <mass shell> with the displayed <mass>. For equal constituents, <rapidities> $\chi\pm iu/2$ sum to $2m_a\cos(u/2)(\cosh\chi,\sinh\chi)$. A <pole> at $u=0$ is at threshold rather than a strictly <bound state>.

= Hermitian analyticity of a two-particle S-matrix
{parent=S-matrix}

In a real species basis, the two-particle <S-matrix> satisfies $S(-\theta^*)=S(\theta)^\dagger$ under the usual scattering analyticity assumptions. On the real <rapidity> axis this relates inverse-rapidity amplitudes to <complex conjugates>. Combined with the algebraic inverse relation $S(\theta)S(-\theta)=I$, it gives physical <unitarity>.

= Scattering wavefunction
{parent=Quantum scattering}

= Scattering eigenfunction
{synonym}

For a localized target and a plane wave incident along the $z$-axis, the large-radius scattering wavefunction has the form
$$
\psi(\mathbf r)\sim e^{ikz}+f(\theta,\phi)\frac{e^{ikr}}r,
$$
where $f$ is the <scattering amplitude>.

= Scattering state
{parent=Quantum scattering}

A <scattering state> is a state used to describe <quantum scattering>. Its asymptotic incoming and outgoing components determine a <scattering amplitude>.

= Outgoing boundary condition
{parent=Scattering state}

A <Green function> for stationary scattering is chosen to contain waves travelling away from its source. In one dimension at positive <energy> it is proportional to $e^{ik|x-x^{\prime}|}$; the condition selects the $E+i0$ <resolvent> boundary value.

= Partial wave
{parent=Quantum scattering}

A partial wave is a component of a scattering state with definite <orbital angular momentum>. A <central potential> preserves each angular-momentum channel, so the scattering problem decomposes into independent partial waves.

= D wave
{parent=Partial wave}
{c}
{title2=$\ell=2$}

A partial wave with <orbital angular momentum> $\ell=2$. Its orbital <parity> is positive. Coupling it to a spin-one pair can include total $J=1$, allowing a <D wave> component in the <deuteron>.

= Partial-wave scattering
{parent=Quantum scattering}

<Partial-wave scattering> decomposes a <scattering state> into <partial waves> with definite <orbital angular momentum>. For a <central potential>, each channel has its own <scattering phase shift> and <partial-wave S-matrix>.

= P-wave scattering
{parent=Partial-wave scattering}
{c}
{title2=$\ell=1$}

The <partial-wave scattering> channel with <orbital angular momentum> quantum number one. Its angular dependence is built from degree-one <spherical harmonics>. For a finite-range potential away from a threshold pole, the <finite-range partial-wave threshold law> gives phase shift $\delta_1=O(k^3)$ and a partial elastic <scattering cross section> proportional to $k^4$ as the relative <wavenumber> $k$ tends to zero.

= Two-dimensional partial-wave scattering formula
{parent=Partial-wave scattering}
{title2=$f(\theta)=\sqrt{2/\pi}\sum_{m\geq0}w_me^{i\delta_m}\sin\delta_m\cos(m\theta)$}
{title2=$\sigma=(4/k)\sum_{m\geq0}w_m\sin^2\delta_m$}

For a real central potential and the outgoing convention $e^{i\pi/4}f(\theta)e^{ikr}/\sqrt{kr}$, the <partial-wave S-matrix> is $S_m=e^{2i\delta_m}$. The cosine form of the <Jacobi-Anger expansion> fixes $w_0=1$ and $w_m=2$ for $m\geq1$. Comparing the outgoing part of each phase-shifted radial cosine with the incident <plane wave> gives the displayed <scattering amplitude>. Integrating $|f|^2/k$ gives the displayed <scattering cross-section>, since the constant angular mode has squared norm $2\pi$ and each positive-order cosine has squared norm $\pi$. Reversing the multiplicities would already fail to reproduce the free <plane wave> at zero <scattering phase shift>.

= Differential scattering cross-section
{title2=$d\sigma/d\Omega$}
{parent=Quantum scattering}

The differential scattering cross-section is the effective incident area scattered per unit <solid angle>. It is defined by the ratio of the outgoing particle rate through $d\Omega$ to the incident flux.

= Central-potential classical differential cross-section
{parent=Differential scattering cross-section}

For an azimuthally symmetric classical scattering map from <impact parameter> $b$ to scattering angle $\theta$,
$$
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}
=\frac{b}{\sin\theta}\left|\frac{db}{d\theta}\right|.
$$
When several impact parameters produce the same angle, their nonnegative contributions are summed.

= Quantum differential cross-section from a scattering amplitude
{parent=Differential scattering cross-section}

If
$$
\psi\sim e^{ikz}+f(\theta)\frac{e^{ikr}}r,
$$
then the ratio of scattered radial <probability current> through $r^2d\Omega$ to the incident probability flux is
$$
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=|f(\theta)|^2.
$$

= Reflected wave
{parent=Quantum scattering}

A reflected wave is an outgoing component travelling back toward the side from which the incident wave arrived.

= Transmission probability
{parent=Quantum scattering}

The transmission probability is the ratio of transmitted to incident <probability current>. When the asymptotic wavenumbers agree, it is the squared modulus of the transmission amplitude.

= One-dimensional S-matrix
{parent=Quantum scattering}
{c}

For a reflection-invariant localized potential, order incoming amplitudes from the left and right and outgoing amplitudes toward the left and right. Then
$$
S=\begin{pmatrix}r&t\\t&r\end{pmatrix},
$$
where $r$ and $t$ are the reflection and transmission amplitudes. Probability-current conservation makes $S$ unitary for a real potential.

= Parity basis of a one-dimensional S-matrix
{c}
{parent=One-dimensional S-matrix}

For a <parity>[parity-symmetric] one-dimensional potential, the even and odd parity states diagonalize the <one-dimensional S-matrix>:
$$
S_{+-}=S_{-+}=0,
\qquad
S=\operatorname{diag}(S_{++},S_{--}).
$$
For a real potential, <unitary operator>[unitarity] further gives $|S_{++}|=|S_{--}|=1$ at real positive energy.

= One-dimensional transfer matrix from scattering amplitudes
{parent=One-dimensional S-matrix}
{c}

If the left and right travelling-wave coefficients are related by
$$
B_L=rA_L+tB_R,
\qquad
A_R=tA_L+rB_R,
$$
then
$$
\begin{pmatrix}A_R\\B_R\end{pmatrix}
=\frac1t
\begin{pmatrix}t^2-r^2&r\\-r&1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}A_L\\B_L\end{pmatrix}.
$$

= Partial-wave S-matrix
{parent=Quantum scattering}
{c}

For a central potential, each angular-momentum channel has a scalar scattering coefficient $S_l(k)=e^{2i\delta_l(k)}$.

Each channel coefficient is a component of the <scattering matrix> in a basis adapted to rotational symmetry.

= Scattering phase shift
{title2=$\delta_l$}
{parent=Partial-wave S-matrix}

For elastic scattering by a real central potential, the phase shift $\delta_l(k)$ measures the phase difference between the asymptotic radial wave in angular-momentum channel $l$ and the corresponding free wave. Its channel <partial-wave S-matrix> is $S_l=e^{2i\delta_l}$.

The phase shift parametrizes elastic <partial-wave scattering>, rather than general scattering phenomena.

= Finite-range partial-wave threshold law
{parent=Scattering phase shift}
{title2=$\delta_\ell(k)=O(k^{2\ell+1})$}

For regular low-energy elastic scattering by a finite-range <central potential>, higher <partial waves> are suppressed by their <centrifugal barriers>. Matching the regular interior radial solution to small-argument exterior spherical Bessel functions gives the displayed scaling of <scattering phase shift>, in the generic absence of a threshold singularity. Since the channel amplitude is proportional to $\sin\delta_\ell/k$, it scales as $k^{2\ell}$. The $\ell=0$ term is isotropic and normally leads. A tuned vanishing leading amplitude or a threshold resonance requires examining the next terms rather than applying the generic statement blindly.

= Periodic-box phase-shift quantization
{parent=Scattering phase shift}
{title2=$k_nL+\delta(k_n)=2\pi n$}

For a reflectionless <scattering wavefunction> whose two asymptotic phases differ by $\delta(k)$, a large periodic box gives $k_nL+\delta(k_n)=2\pi n$. Compared with the free value $k_n^{(0)}=2\pi n/L$, $k_n-k_n^{(0)}=-\delta(k_n^{(0)})/L+O(L^{-2})$ away from thresholds. Discrete <bound states> and phase-branch changes alter mode indexing and must be counted when comparing complete spectra.

= S-wave scattering
{c}
{synonym}

An S wave is the rotationally invariant angular-momentum channel $l=0$. At sufficiently low energy it normally dominates short-range scattering because higher partial waves are suppressed by their centrifugal barriers.

= Phase shift of a delta-shell potential
{parent=S wave (quantum scattering)}
{title2=$\tan\delta_0=-\frac{g\sin^2(kR)}{kR+g\sin(kR)\cos(kR)}$}

For $V(r)=V_0\delta(r-R)$, put $g=2mV_0R/\hbar^2$. The regular reduced radial <wave function> is proportional to $\sin kr$ inside the shell and to $\sin(kr+\delta_0)$ outside. It is continuous at $R$, while its radial derivative jumps by $(g/R)u(R)$. Dividing the jump condition by the common value gives $k\cot(kR+\delta_0)-k\cot(kR)=g/R$, which implies the displayed <phase shift> modulo $\pi$. At nodes, the undivided matching equations give the continuous limiting interpretation. For $g\ne0$, an equivalent formula is $k\cot\delta_0=-k\cot(kR)-k(kR)/[g\sin^2(kR)]$. The exact <S-wave scattering> amplitude is $[k\cot\delta_0-ik]^{-1}$.

= Partial-wave expansion of a scattering amplitude
{parent=Partial-wave S-matrix}

For a central potential and the convention $S_l=1+2if_l=e^{2i\delta_l}$, the <scattering amplitude> is
$$
f(\theta)=\frac1k\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)f_lP_l(\cos\theta),
\qquad
f_l=e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l.
$$
The identity $|S_l|=1$ is equivalent to $\operatorname{Im}f_l=|f_l|^2$.

= Partial-wave total scattering cross-section
{title2=$\sigma_T$}
{parent=Partial-wave expansion of a scattering amplitude}

For elastic scattering by a central potential,
$$
\sigma_T=\frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l.
$$
At low momentum, the <S wave> normally supplies the leading term.

= Partial-wave unitarity
{parent=Partial-wave S-matrix}

Partial-wave unitarity bounds each angular-momentum component of a scattering amplitude because its elastic S-matrix eigenvalue has modulus one. Perturbative violation of this bound signals that interactions become strong or that additional degrees of freedom must enter.

= Unitarity and reflection identities for a partial-wave S-matrix
{parent=Partial-wave S-matrix}

For a real central potential and real $k$, radial-current conservation gives $S_l(k)^*S_l(k)=1$. Invariance of the radial equation under $k\mapsto-k$ gives $S_l(k)S_l(-k)=1$, so a continuous phase convention has $\delta_l(-k)=-\delta_l(k)$.

= Scattering length from a partial-wave S-matrix
{parent=Partial-wave S-matrix}

= Scattering length
{synonym}

The $s$-wave scattering length is
$$
a_s=-\lim_{k\to0}\frac{\tan\delta_0(k)}k,
$$
and the low-energy total cross-section is $4\pi a_s^2$.

= Bethe-Peierls boundary condition
{parent=Scattering length from a partial-wave S-matrix}
{c}
{title2=$\lim_{r\to0}\partial_r(r\psi)/(r\psi)=-1/a$}

This zero-range <boundary condition> replaces a short-range interaction by its <S-wave scattering> length. If $\psi=A/r+B+O(r)$, it requires $B=-A/a$. An outgoing spherical wave also has a constant $ik$ term; retaining that term gives the unitary scattering-length amplitude $f_0=-a/(1+ika)$. The model neglects effective-range corrections and is applied outside the microscopic range, not literally inside a finite potential.

= Two-centre scattering-length model
{parent=Bethe-Peierls boundary condition}
{title2=$(a_j^{-1}+ik)c_j+(e^{ikd}/d)c_{3-j}=-ks_j$}

Two separated short-range scatterers are coupled because the outgoing wave from either centre supplies part of the regular incoming field at the other. Applying the <Bethe-Peierls boundary condition> at both centres gives the displayed two-by-two linear system, where $s_j$ is the incident plane-wave phase. <matrix> inversion sums repeated scattering between the centres. Near a small <determinant>, effective-range corrections may matter even if the nominal microscopic range is short.

= Angular interference integral for two point scatterers
{parent=Two-centre scattering-length model}
{title2=$\int e^{ik\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf d}d\Omega=4\pi\sin(kd)/(kd)$}

Taking the polar axis along the separation vector reduces the solid-angle <integral> to $2\pi\int_{-1}^1e^{ikdu}du$. Thus the total <scattering cross-section> contains the <interference> term $2\operatorname{Re}(c_1c_2^*)\sin(kd)/(kd)$ as well as the two self terms. This is an outgoing-angle <integral> for fixed incident direction, rather than an average over the incoming beam.

= S-wave scattering length
{parent=Scattering length from a partial-wave S-matrix}
{title2=$k\cot\delta_0(k)=-1/a_s+O(k^2)$}

The zero-energy parameter of the angular-momentum-zero partial wave. For a short-range interaction in three dimensions, its leading elastic <scattering amplitude> is $f_0(k)\simeq- a_s/(1+ika_s)$ when effective-range corrections are negligible. For identical dilute <bosons> this parameter fixes the leading contact coupling $g_B=4\pi\hbar^2a_s/m_B$.

= Scattering length of a delta-shell potential
{parent=Scattering length from a partial-wave S-matrix}
{title2=$a_0=R\frac{g}{1+g},\quad g=2mV_0R/\hbar^2$}

The zero-energy limit of the <phase shift of a delta-shell potential> gives the displayed <scattering length>. It vanishes at zero coupling and has a pole at $g=-1$. For repulsion $g>0$, $0<a_0<R$; for weak attraction $-1<g<0$, $a_0<0$; for $g<-1$, $a_0>R$. Both infinite-coupling limits tend to $R$. The pole is the threshold for an S-wave <bound state>: writing its energy as $-\hbar^2\kappa^2/(2m)$ gives $g=-2\kappa R/[1-e^{-2\kappa R}]$, with threshold $g\to-1$ as $\kappa\downarrow0$. The <delta-shell Born amplitude> agrees with the exact low-energy amplitude only in their common weak-coupling and low-energy regime, $|g|\ll1$ and $kR\ll1$, where both tend to $-gR$.

= Repulsive spherical barrier scattering length
{parent=Scattering length from a partial-wave S-matrix}
{title2=$a_s=a-\tanh(\gamma a)/\gamma$}

For a barrier of radius $a$ and potential $\hbar^2\gamma^2/(2m)$, matching the regular inner hyperbolic-sine wave to the outer sine wave gives $a_s=a-\tanh(\gamma a)/\gamma$. The low-energy $s$-wave <scattering amplitude> is $-a_s$.

= Zero-energy scattering resonance
{parent=Scattering length from a partial-wave S-matrix}

A zero-energy scattering resonance occurs when an S-wave bound state reaches the continuum threshold. The zero-energy radial solution then approaches a nonzero constant rather than a generic linear function, and the <scattering length from a partial-wave S-matrix>[scattering length] diverges and changes sign.

= Bound-state poles of a partial-wave S-matrix
{parent=Partial-wave S-matrix}

A pole at $k=i\kappa$ with $\kappa>0$ represents a normalizable bound state of energy $-\hbar^2\kappa^2/(2m)$. Resonance poles instead lie away from the imaginary axis on the analytically continued unphysical sheet.

= Hyperbolic-tangent S-wave exterior solution
{c}
{parent=Bound-state poles of a partial-wave S-matrix}

For the exterior wave
$$
\psi(r)=\frac{e^{-ikr}}r
+\frac{k+i\lambda\tanh(\lambda r)}{k-i\lambda}
\frac{e^{ikr}}r,
$$
the partial-wave scattering matrix is
$$
S_0(k)=-\frac{k+i\lambda}{k-i\lambda}
=\frac{\lambda-ik}{\lambda+ik}.
$$
Thus $\tan\delta_0=-k/\lambda$, the scattering length is $1/\lambda$, and the pole at $k=i\lambda$ represents a bound state of energy $-\hbar^2\lambda^2/(2m)$ whose exterior wavefunction is proportional to $\operatorname{sech}(\lambda r)/r$.

= Scattering resonance
{parent=Partial-wave S-matrix}

A scattering resonance is a metastable state represented by a pole of the analytically continued scattering matrix. Near an isolated narrow resonance, a cross-section has a Breit-Wigner peak whose energy width is the inverse lifetime up to $\hbar$.

= Breit-Wigner resonance cross section
{parent=Scattering resonance}
{c}
{title2=$\sigma_{ab\to R\to X}=\frac{\pi g}{p_i^2}\frac{\Gamma_{ab}\Gamma_X}{(E-E_R)^2+\Gamma^2/4}$}

For an isolated <particle resonance> of <spin> $J_R$, the Breit-Wigner approximation relates a production-and-decay <cross section> to its partial <decay widths>. In <natural units>, $p_i$ is either incident particle's momentum magnitude in the <centre-of-momentum frame>, $g=(2J_R+1)/[(2J_a+1)(2J_b+1)]$ for unpolarized incident particles, and $\Gamma$ is the total width. The formula assumes the stated spin counting and neglects nonresonant backgrounds and their interference. Multiplication by $(\hbar c)^2$ converts an inverse-squared-energy cross section to area when momenta are expressed in energy units.

= Integrated narrow vector resonance area
{parent=Breit-Wigner resonance cross section}
{title2=$\int\sigma_{ee}\,dE=6\pi^2B_{ee}^2\Gamma/M^2$}

For a narrow <vector meson> formed and observed in $e^+e^-$, neglecting external <masses> and slowly varying kinematic factors gives $\sigma_{ee}=3\pi\Gamma_{ee}^2/[M^2((E-M)^2+\Gamma^2/4)]$. Integrating the <Lorentz line shape> gives the displayed result. A normalized instrumental <convolution> preserves this full area, although it changes the observed peak and width. A constant uncorrected acceptance multiplies the area and must be divided out before extracting the <decay width>.

= Peak and integrated resonance width dependence
{parent=Breit-Wigner resonance cross section}

At fixed entrance and exit <partial decay widths>, a resonance's peak <cross section> is proportional to their product divided by the square of its total width. Integrating a narrow Lorentzian over energy supplies one factor of the total width, leaving an inverse first power. Extracting a width from a peak ratio therefore differs from extracting it from an integrated ratio.

= Inclusive and exclusive resonance branching factors
{parent=Breit-Wigner resonance cross section}

Inclusive formation sums over all resonance decays and has exit <branching fraction> one. Selecting a particular final channel instead multiplies by that channel's branching fraction. Thus resonant elastic scattering contains the square of the entrance branching fraction, while inclusive formation contains only one power.

= Same-resonance entrance-channel cross-section ratio
{parent=Breit-Wigner resonance cross section}
{title2=$\sigma_a/\sigma_b=(g_a/g_b)(p_b^2/p_a^2)(\Gamma_a/\Gamma_b)$}

Compare two entrance channels feeding the same isolated resonance and the same selected exit channel, each at its own resonance <energy>. The exit <partial decay width> and the resonant denominator cancel. Different entrance momenta, <spin> weights and entrance widths remain. The formula assumes the same resonance, the stated unpolarized <spin> average and negligible nonresonant <interference>; a ratio at one shared laboratory <energy> would generally be different.

= Resonance spin statistical factor
{parent=Breit-Wigner resonance cross section}
{title2=$g=(2J_R+1)/[(2J_a+1)(2J_b+1)]$}

For unpolarized entrance particles, average over their $(2J_a+1)(2J_b+1)$ <spin> orientations and sum over the isolated resonance's $2J_R+1$ magnetic substates. The resulting weight multiplies the partial-wave <cross section>. It can exceed one because orbital <angular momentum> participates; it is not a standalone probability of forming a state. Polarization or several unresolved resonance <spins> requires more detailed channel counting.

= Unpolarized resonance spin factor
{parent=Breit-Wigner resonance cross section}
{title2=$g=(2J_R+1)/[(2s_a+1)(2s_b+1)]$}

For unpolarized incident particles, the resonant cross section sums over the resonance's magnetic substates and averages over the incident spin states. This gives the displayed statistical weight under the conventional <Breit-Wigner resonance cross section> normalization. A spin-zero resonance formed from two spin-half particles has $g=1/4$, whereas a spin-one resonance has $g=3/4$. This counting is distinct from final-state <color charge> multiplicities.

= Peak resonance cross section and branching fractions
{parent=Breit-Wigner resonance cross section}
{title2=$\sigma_{\rm peak}=4\pi g B_{ab}B_X/p_i^2$}

At $E=E_R$, the <Breit-Wigner resonance cross section> loses its explicit dependence on the total <decay width> after writing $\Gamma_{ab}=B_{ab}\Gamma$ and $\Gamma_X=B_X\Gamma$. For an unpolarized ultrarelativistic <electron>-<positron> initial state and a spin-one resonance, $g=3/4$ and $p_i=E_R/2$, giving $\sigma_{\rm peak}=12\pi B_eB_X/E_R^2$ in <natural units>. An elastic resonant channel therefore measures the square $B_e^2$, rather than one power of the electronic <branching fraction>.

= Resonance pole
{parent=Scattering resonance}
{title2=$E=E_R-i\Gamma/2,\quad\Gamma>0$}

A pole of the continued <scattering matrix> associated with outgoing boundary conditions and an unstable trapped state. A pole below the real energy axis gives time dependence $e^{-iE_Rt/\hbar}e^{-\Gamma t/(2\hbar)}$, so the probability lifetime is $\hbar/\Gamma$. Bound-state poles instead lie on the positive imaginary wavenumber axis and produce square-integrable decaying spatial waves.

= Half-line delta-well amplitude enhancement
{parent=Resonance pole}
{title2=$|A|^2=\lambda^2x^2/[(1-\cos2x)^2+(\lambda x-\sin2x)^2]$}

A hard wall at the origin and an attractive <delta potential> at distance $a$ form a leaky quantum cavity. For unit incident amplitude from outside, the interior <standing wave> has coefficient $A=-i\lambda x/[1-e^{2ix}+i\lambda x]$, where $x=ka$ and $\lambda=\hbar^2/(mqa)>0$. Its <modulus> squared is the title expression. A <resonance pole> $p_n=x_n-i\gamma_n$ produces an enhancement with a locally <Lorentzian line shape>, proportional to $[(x-x_n)^2+\gamma_n^2]^{-1}$. At fixed low $n$ and small $\lambda$, $x_n\simeq n\pi(1+\lambda/2+\lambda^2/4)$ and $\gamma_n\simeq(\lambda n\pi/2)^2$.

The coefficient can exceed one because it measures a standing-wave amplitude, while the net interior <probability current> is zero. Conservation of <probability current> gives unit reflected flux: no current passes through the hard wall. Near zero, $|A|^2\to\lambda^2/(2-\lambda)^2$ when $\lambda\ne2$; the threshold case $\lambda=2$ requires a separate limit.

= Logarithmic-derivative matching
{parent=Quantum scattering}

At an interface where a finite potential changes discontinuously, continuity of a radial wavefunction and its derivative is equivalent to matching $u'/u$ on the two sides. This removes the arbitrary normalization constants and gives an equation for the <scattering phase shift>.

= Hard-sphere limit
{parent=Quantum scattering}

The hard-sphere limit of a repulsive spherical potential makes penetration into a sphere of radius $a$ vanish. Its low-energy <S wave> scattering length tends to $a$, and its quantum total cross-section tends to $4\pi a^2$.

= Hard-sphere S-wave scattering
{parent=Hard-sphere limit}
{title2=$\sigma_0=4\pi R^2[\sin(kR)/(kR)]^2$}

For a hard sphere of <radius> $R$, the exterior radial <S wave> is proportional to $\sin[k(r-R)]$. Matching it to $\sin(kr+\delta_0)$ gives <scattering phase shift> $\delta_0=-kR$ modulo $\pi$ and <scattering length> $R$. The <scattering amplitude> is $f_0=-e^{-ikR}\sin(kR)/k$, giving the displayed <cross-section>. The zero-energy limit is $4\pi R^2$, its zeros occur at positive integer multiples $kR=n\pi$, and its high-energy envelope decreases as $k^{-2}$. This is one <partial wave>, not the total high-energy hard-sphere result obtained by summing all <partial waves>.

= Lippmann-Schwinger equation
{parent=Quantum scattering}
{c}
{wiki}

The Lippmann-Schwinger equation rewrites the Schrodinger differential equation as an integral equation using a free Green function and a prescribed incoming state.

= Scattering amplitude
{parent=Quantum scattering}
{wiki}

A scattering amplitude is the coefficient of an outgoing asymptotic wave relative to the specified incoming wave.

= Virtual state
{parent=Scattering amplitude}

A pole reached on an unphysical sheet of the <scattering amplitude>, often on the negative imaginary <momentum> axis for a short-range one-dimensional potential. Unlike a positive-imaginary-momentum bound-state pole, it does not give a <normalizable> exponentially decaying state on both sides.

= Forward scattering amplitude
{parent=Scattering amplitude}
{title2=$f(0)$}

The <scattering amplitude> at zero deflection angle. Its real part changes the phase of a coherent beam in matter; its imaginary part is related by the <optical theorem> to removal from that beam. Different forward amplitudes for a <kaon> and a <neutral antikaon> allow <neutral kaon regeneration> without fundamental <CP violation>.

= Helicity amplitude
{parent=Scattering amplitude}
{title2=$\mathcal M_{h_3h_4;h_1h_2}$}

A <scattering amplitude> evaluated with definite external <helicities>. Rotational covariance relates its angular dependence to a <Wigner D-matrix>. Summing final <helicities> and averaging an unpolarised initial mixture yields a spin-averaged squared <matrix element>.

= Invariant scattering amplitude
{parent=Scattering amplitude}
{title2=$\langle f|S-1|i\rangle=i(2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}(P_f-P_i)\mathcal M$}

With relativistically normalized external states, $\mathcal M$ is the reduced <S-matrix> element after removing the <four-momentum conservation> distribution. A <relativistic scattering cross-section> combines $|\mathcal M|^2$ with the <Lorentz-invariant phase-space measure> and the <invariant flux factor>. Identical-leg factorials in <Feynman vertices> and the <identical final-state symmetry factor> in the event integral have different roles.

= Optical theorem
{parent=Scattering amplitude}

Write the <S-matrix> as $S=1+iT$. <Unitarity> gives $-i(T-T^\dagger)=T^\dagger T$, so the diagonal forward matrix element obeys $2\operatorname{Im}T_{ii}=\sum_f\int d\Phi_f\,|T_{fi}|^2$. Thus the imaginary part of a forward <scattering amplitude> measures the total inclusive transition rate. For an electromagnetic source, the <hadronic electromagnetic-current spectral density> supplies the inclusive spectral sum.

= Optical theorem at second Born order
{parent=Optical theorem}
{title2=$\operatorname{Im}f^{(2)}(0)=k\sigma^{(1)}_{\rm total}/(4\pi)$}

For a real <elastic scattering> potential the first Born forward amplitude is real, while the leading total <scattering cross-section> is quadratic in the potential. The second Born amplitude supplies an imaginary part from its outgoing <Green function>. Symmetrizing the two interaction positions makes its imaginary part equal to $k/(4\pi)$ times the integrated squared first Born amplitude. The identity therefore compares quantities at the same perturbative order; a real first Born amplitude alone cannot test it against a nonzero <scattering cross-section>.

= Resonant s-wave unitarity check
{parent=Optical theorem}
{title2=$\operatorname{Im}f=k|f|^2$}

For an isotropic elastic amplitude normalized by an incident unit <plane wave> and outgoing $fe^{ikr}/r$, total cross section is $4\pi|f|^2$. The <optical theorem> gives the displayed identity. At a <resonance> where the real part of $1/f$ vanishes, $f=i/k$ and the cross section is $4\pi/k^2$.

= Scattering-amplitude factorization
{parent=Scattering amplitude}

Near a one-particle pole, a <scattering amplitude> factors into amplitudes coupling each side to the intermediate state, summed over its physical polarizations. The pole position gives its squared <mass>, while its <residue> encodes its spin and couplings.

= Crossing symmetry

{wiki}

= Crossing-symmetric scattering amplitude
{synonym}

Crossing symmetry relates <scattering amplitudes> by analytic continuation exchanging incoming and outgoing particles. A symmetric four-point scalar amplitude has equivalent descriptions under permutations of the <Mandelstam variables>.

= Relativistic scattering cross-section
{parent=Scattering amplitude}

= Relativistic cross-section
{synonym}

With relativistically normalized external states, a two-particle initial state has differential cross-section $d\sigma=|\mathcal M|^2d\Phi_n/\mathcal F$, where $d\Phi_n$ is the <Lorentz-invariant phase-space measure> and $\mathcal F$ is the <invariant flux factor>. For identical unobserved final particles, divide the full labeled phase-space integral by their permutation multiplicity.

= Particle-production threshold
{parent=Relativistic scattering cross-section}
{title2=$\sqrt{s_{\rm th}}=\sum_i m_i$}

= Production threshold
{synonym}

The lowest initial <centre-of-mass frame> <energy> at which a specified final state can be made. For free massive final particles in <natural units>, the minimum is the sum of their <masses>, attained when their relative <momenta> vanish. The <cross section> at the exact threshold can vanish even though the channel becomes kinematically accessible there.

= Inclusive electron-positron annihilation into hadrons
{parent=Relativistic scattering cross-section}
{title2=$\sigma_h(s)$}

At leading electromagnetic order and with negligible electron mass, the photon-channel <relativistic cross-section> is $\sigma_h=4\pi^2\alpha\rho_h=16\pi^3\alpha^2\rho_J/s$, in the stated <hadronic photon spectral density> and <hadronic electromagnetic-current spectral density> conventions. Initial electron and positron spins are averaged; final hadronic states are summed inclusively. Electroweak channels beyond photon exchange are separate contributions.

= Hadronic R ratio
{parent=Inclusive electron-positron annihilation into hadrons}
{title2=$R(s)=\sigma_h(s)/[4\pi\alpha^2/(3s)]$}

The <hadronic R ratio> normalizes the photon-channel hadronic <relativistic cross-section> to the leading massless muon-pair value. The free active-quark result is $R=N_c\sum_fQ_f^2$. It depends on flavor charges as well as the number of active flavors; it is not simply $N_cN_f$.

= Elastic scattering from a quartic scalar contact interaction
{parent=Relativistic scattering cross-section}
{title2=$d\sigma_{\rm event}/d\Omega=\lambda^2/(128\pi^2s)$}

The factorial-normalized quartic vertex gives an angle-independent tree amplitude of magnitude $|\lambda|$. For equal incoming and outgoing masses, the momentum ratio in the two-body cross-section cancels. The event density for identical outgoing particles over a full sphere is $\lambda^2/(128\pi^2s)$. On a hemisphere representing each event once, the density is $\lambda^2/(64\pi^2s)$. Both conventions give total event cross-section $\lambda^2/(32\pi s)$.

= Invariant flux factor
{parent=Relativistic scattering cross-section}
{title2=$\mathcal F=4\sqrt{(p\cdot q)^2-m_1^2m_2^2}$}

For two incident particles in relativistic normalization, this is $2\sqrt{\kappa(s,m_1^2,m_2^2)}$, where $\kappa$ is the <Källén function>. For equal masses it is $2\sqrt{s(s-4m^2)}$.

= Lorentz-invariant phase-space measure
{c}
{parent=Relativistic scattering cross-section}
{title2=$d\Phi_n(P)=(2\pi)^4\delta^4(P-\sum_i p_i)\prod_i\frac{d^3p_i}{(2\pi)^3 2E_i}$}

The on-shell measure $d^3p/(2E)$ is Lorentz invariant. The product measure together with the four-dimensional <Dirac delta distribution> enforces <four-momentum conservation>. For equal masses in two-body scattering, $d\Phi_2=\sqrt{1-4m^2/s}\,d\Omega/(32\pi^2)$.

= Relativistic two-body phase space
{parent=Lorentz-invariant phase-space measure}
{title2=$\frac{d\Phi_2}{d\Omega}=\frac{k}{16\pi^2\sqrt s}$}

For two outgoing particles with centre-of-mass momentum magnitude $k$ and total energy $\sqrt s$, integrating the four-momentum delta function gives $d\Phi_2/d\Omega=k/(16\pi^2\sqrt s)$. A factor $1/2!$ for identical outgoing particles is inserted when counting events over the full phase space, not into this labelled kinematic measure itself.

= Positive-energy on-shell delta function identity
{parent=Lorentz-invariant phase-space measure}
{title2=$\frac{\delta(\omega-E)}{E}=2\delta(E^2-\omega^2),\quad \omega>0$}

For $E>0$ and the positive-energy branch, the root Jacobian of the <Dirac delta function> gives this identity. In a <massless collinear parton approximation>, it produces $\delta(x-\xi)/(P\cdot q)$.

= Two-body decay phase space
{parent=Lorentz-invariant phase-space measure}
{title2=$\Gamma=\frac{|\boldsymbol k|}{8\pi m_A^2}|\mathcal M|^2$}

This total decay-width formula applies in the parent's rest frame when the squared <scattering amplitude> is independent of direction and the daughters are distinct. For identical daughters an additional symmetry factor is needed.

= Identical-particle factor in a final-state phase-space integral
{parent=Lorentz-invariant phase-space measure}

= Identical final-state symmetry factor
{synonym}

When the full <Lorentz-invariant phase-space measure> treats $n$ identical final particles as labeled, each physical final configuration is counted $n!$ times. The unlabelled <relativistic scattering cross-section> therefore includes $1/n!$. For two identical scalars, this divides the full-angle result by two; equivalently, integrate over a region containing only one representative of each exchanged pair.

= Momentum transfer
{title2=$Q$}
{parent=Scattering amplitude}
{wiki}

The momentum transfer in a scattering process is the difference between the incoming and outgoing <wavevector>[wavevectors], commonly $Q=k-k'$ up to a sign convention and a factor of $\hbar$.

= Energy transfer
{parent=Momentum transfer}
{title2=$\nu=E-E'$}

The <energy> component of a transferred <four-momentum> in a specified frame. In fixed-target <deep inelastic scattering> the target rest frame gives $\nu=E-E'$, where $E,E'$ are incoming and scattered <electron> <energies>. It is frame dependent even though $Q^2=-q^2$ is <Lorentz invariant>.

= Spatial resolution from momentum transfer
{parent=Momentum transfer}
{title2=$\ell\sim\hbar/q$}

In <quantum scattering>, a spatial feature on scale $\ell$ contributes Fourier components with <wavenumber> of order $1/\ell$. A <momentum transfer> of magnitude $q$ supplies <wavenumber> $q/\hbar$, giving the characteristic estimate $\ell\sim\hbar/q$. With the energy-like quantity $Q=qc$, this becomes $\ell\sim\hbar c/Q$. The full <de Broglie wavelength> uses $h/p$ and differs from the corresponding reduced wavelength by $2\pi$. Beam <energy> only bounds accessible <momentum transfer>; it does not determine the resolution of every event.

= Elastic scattering
{parent=Scattering amplitude}
{wiki}

Elastic scattering preserves the total kinetic energy. For one particle scattered by a fixed target, the incoming and outgoing <wavevector>[wavevectors] therefore have equal magnitudes.

= Neutrino-electron elastic scattering
{parent=Elastic scattering}
{title2=$T_{\max}=2E_\nu^2/(m_e+2E_\nu)$}

An elastic <weak interaction> of a <neutrino> with an <electron>. For a stationary <electron> of <mass> $m$ and an effectively massless incident <neutrino> of <energy> $E$, the maximum recoil <kinetic energy> in <natural units> is $T_{\max}=2E^2/(m+2E)$. This kinematic limit does not specify the interaction probability.

= Coherent elastic scattering
{parent=Elastic scattering}

In <coherent elastic scattering>, amplitudes from the scattering constituents combine while their internal state is unchanged. For <X-rays>, recoil taken by a whole <chemical atom> can produce an effectively unshifted component alongside the <Compton scattering> component.

= Diffraction
{parent=Scattering amplitude}
{wiki}

Diffraction is interference produced when waves scatter from spatial structure. A periodic structure concentrates the scattered intensity at reciprocal-lattice conditions.

= Diffraction pattern
{parent=Diffraction}

The spatial distribution of <intensity> produced by interference of a wave passing an aperture or obstacle. A circular aperture's far-field point-source pattern is an <Airy disk>; a <Rayleigh criterion> comparison superposes the intensities of two mutually incoherent sources.

= Thermal diffuse scattering
{parent=Diffraction}

Diffuse scattering generated by fluctuating lattice displacements. Resolved one-phonon creation and annihilation transfer both energy and crystal momentum, with Bose factors $n_B+1$ and $n_B$. An energy-integrating diffraction measurement can record their summed intensity without resolving the small energy transfer. Phonon polarization, soft-mode frequencies and atomic scattering weights determine the pattern; static disorder supplies a distinct possible diffuse contribution.

= Ewald sphere curvature
{parent=Diffraction}
{c}

<Ewald sphere curvature> is the curvature of the reciprocal-space surface sampled by elastic scattering. Accounting for its departure from a plane can improve very high-resolution <electron microscopy> reconstructions.

= Obliquity factor
{parent=Diffraction}

The <obliquity factor> weights the directional contribution of an aperture element in a scalar <diffraction> integral. In a fixed observation direction in the <Fraunhofer diffraction> regime, its variation across a sufficiently small aperture can be neglected along with the variation of the geometric amplitude denominator. Its angular dependence can still matter when comparing widely separated observation directions.

= Electron diffraction
{parent=Diffraction}
{wiki}

<Electron diffraction> is <interference> between scattered <electron> <probability amplitudes>. It connects the <de Broglie wavelength> of the incident beam to the arrangement of the scattering material. In <elastic scattering> from a fixed crystal, $|\mathbf k_{\rm out}|=|\mathbf k_{\rm in}|=2\pi/\lambda$, and constructive <crystal scattering> selects <wavevector> transfers $\mathbf Q=\mathbf k_{\rm out}-\mathbf k_{\rm in}$ in the <reciprocal lattice>. The physical <momentum transfer> to the <electron> is $\hbar\mathbf Q$ in this convention.

<Bragg's law> expresses the same condition as $2d\sin\theta_B=n\lambda$, where $d$ is the spacing of the chosen lattice planes, $n$ the <diffraction order>, and $\theta_B$ the angle to those planes. The angle between incoming and outgoing propagation directions is $\chi=2\theta_B$ for that reflection geometry. In normal-incidence backscattering, a detector's angle $\phi$ from the outward surface normal instead gives $\chi=\pi-\phi$; neither detector colatitude nor angle to the surface normal should silently replace $\theta_B$. A <finite-row electron diffraction model> uses a different spacing, the spacing between sites along a surface row, and makes that distinction explicit.

= Dynamical diffraction
{parent=Electron diffraction}

<diffraction> in which repeated scattering and coupling between transmitted and diffracted beams significantly affect their amplitudes. Electron-diffraction intensity can then vary strongly with thickness and excitation error; it is not generally just the squared single-scattering <crystal basis structure factor>.

= Selected-area electron diffraction
{parent=Electron diffraction}

Transmission-electron diffraction from a region selected by an aperture in an image plane. Downstream lenses image the objective <back focal plane> to the detector, producing reciprocal-space rather than real-space information.

= Davisson–Germer experiment
{parent=Electron diffraction}
{c}
{wiki}

The <Davisson–Germer experiment> turned <elastic scattering> from <nickel> into evidence for a <matter wave>. The search became a deliberate wave test in 1926, with <diffraction> beams found early in 1927. The earlier crystal investigation had begun in 1925; the <de Broglie wavelength> proposal was already present in the 1924 thesis.<The discovery of electron waves by Clinton J. Davisson>[The discovery of electron waves, printed p. 390: retrospective chronology and the change to a deliberate wave test]{ref}

A heated <tungsten> ribbon supplied <electrons>; gun apertures selected a narrow beam. Two $1\,\mathrm{mm}$ apertures were $8\,\mathrm{mm}$ apart; the gun ended $7\,\mathrm{mm}$ from a <nickel> crystal cut parallel to its $\{111\}$ planes. An adjustable <electric potential difference> set the speed. The beam struck normally inside an evacuated, field-free enclosure with target and outer electrodes at one potential. A double-box collector, $11\,\mathrm{mm}$ from the target with a $1\,\mathrm{mm}$ entrance, selected directions. A retarding <electric potential difference> rejected slower <electrons>; the inner collector fed a sensitive <galvanometer>. Moving the collector scanned colatitude; rotating the crystal scanned azimuth.<Diffraction of Electrons by a Crystal of Nickel by C. Davisson and L. H. Germer>[The apparatus, printed pp. 707–710, figures 2–4: source, apertures, potentials, collector and target preparation]{ref}

The readout was collected <electric current>, not a photograph of <electron> paths. Voltage scans held the gun-plate current constant because incident-beam current could not be held constant; the overall current trend therefore needed caution. = Finite-row electron diffraction model
{parent=Electron diffraction}
{title2=$I(\phi)/I(0)=N^{-2}|\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{ij\delta}|^2$}

Take $N$ equally spaced scattering sites at $x_j=jd$ on a line, a normally incident <plane wave>, equal site <scattering amplitudes>, and observation far enough away that outgoing directions are effectively parallel. Retain only one scattering event per amplitude, as in the <Born approximation>. For angle $\phi$ from the outward normal, write the adjacent-site <quantum phase> difference as $\delta=kd\sin\phi$, with $k=2\pi/\lambda$; reversing its overall sign leaves the intensity unchanged. Summing a <geometric series> gives
$$
\frac{I(\phi)}{I(0)}=\frac{1}{N^2}\left|\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{ij\delta}\right|^2
=\left[\frac{\sin(N\delta/2)}{N\sin(\delta/2)}\right]^2,\qquad \delta=\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin\phi.
$$
At $\delta=2\pi m$ use the continuous limit $I/I(0)=1$, rather than treating $0/0$ as a missing peak. The integer $m$ is the <diffraction order>: $d\sin\phi=m\lambda$. This is a surface-row spacing, not the plane spacing in <Bragg's law>. The plotted intensity is normalized to its central value; it is not an integrated detection probability or an absolute collector <electric current>.

= Crystal scattering
{parent=Scattering amplitude}

In the <Born approximation>, scattering from identical atomic potentials translated to lattice sites factors into one atomic form factor times a finite sum of phases over the crystal sites.

= Kinematic approximation
{parent=Crystal scattering}

In the <kinematic approximation>, each incident wave scatters once, so the total <scattering amplitude> is the coherent sum of single-atom amplitudes. Multiple scattering and depletion of the incident beam are neglected. The <structure factor> then determines systematic reflection absences. Strong multiple scattering can violate an absence predicted in this approximation.

= Diffraction intensity
{parent=Crystal scattering}
{title2=$I\propto |F|^2$}

For coherent elastic <diffraction>, the <intensity> associated with a given <scattering amplitude> is proportional to its squared modulus. Integrated <powder diffraction> intensities additionally include the <powder diffraction multiplicity>, <Lorentz-polarization factor>, <Debye–Waller factor>, absorption and instrument corrections. A peak's height is not its integrated <intensity> unless its profile width and shape are also specified.

= Occupational diffuse scattering
{parent=Crystal scattering}

Static fluctuations in which <chemical element> occupies a site produce scattering beyond coherent <Bragg diffraction> from the mean <crystal structure>. For independent binary occupations with <probabilities> $p$ and $1-p$, the <variance> of the site's <atomic scattering factor> is $p(1-p)|f_A-f_B|^2$. Writing each site amplitude as its mean plus a zero-mean fluctuation shows that the mean squared total amplitude is the coherent contribution plus a term proportional to the number of sites and this <variance>. Consequently a forbidden reflection of the average <crystal structure> can still sit on a diffuse background. Correlated occupations add nontrivial <scattering vector> dependence.

= Bragg diffraction
{parent=Crystal scattering}

Coherent scattering from lattice planes satisfying $2d\sin\theta=n\lambda$. The angle between incident and diffracted rays is $2\theta$, not $\theta$.

= Bragg reflection
{parent=Bragg diffraction}

A diffracted beam or powder peak associated with a reciprocal-lattice vector satisfying the elastic scattering condition. Its position follows from lattice spacings and its amplitude from the unit-cell structure factor. A reciprocal point whose structure factor vanishes does not produce an allowed reflection in the kinematic model.

= Bravais lattice
{parent=Crystal scattering}
{c}
{wiki}

A three-dimensional Bravais lattice is
$$
\Lambda=\{n_1a_1+n_2a_2+n_3a_3:n_i\in\mathbb Z\},
$$
where the primitive vectors $a_1,a_2,a_3$ are linearly independent.

= Body-centered tetragonal lattice
{parent=Bravais lattice}

A tetragonal <Bravais lattice> represented by conventional-cell points $(0,0,0)$ and $(1/2,1/2,1/2)$, with $a=b\ne c$ and right angles. A motif attached to each point determines the atomic structure and can give more than two atoms per conventional cell.

= Two-dimensional Bravais lattice
{parent=Bravais lattice}
{title2=$\Lambda=\mathbb Z\mathbf a_1+\mathbb Z\mathbf a_2$}

The set $\{n_1\mathbf a_1+n_2\mathbf a_2:n_1,n_2\in\mathbb Z\}$ for two independent planar translation vectors. Its primitive-cell area is $|\det(\mathbf a_1,\mathbf a_2)|$.

= Square Bravais lattice
{parent=Two-dimensional Bravais lattice}
{title2=$\Lambda=\ell R\mathbb Z^2$}

A <two-dimensional Bravais lattice> with perpendicular primitive vectors of the same length $\ell$. An orthogonal matrix $R$ gives its orientation, and its primitive area is $\ell^2$. Its nearest-neighbor graph is a <square lattice>; the set of crystal translations and the graph with chosen adjacency are related but distinct objects.

= Centered rectangular lattice
{parent=Two-dimensional Bravais lattice}

= Centred rectangular lattice
{synonym}

A lattice represented by a rectangular conventional cell with corner points and one center point. For conventional sides $2a,2b$, primitive vectors can be $(a,b)$ and $(-a,b)$. The special case $a=b$ is a <square lattice> with a rotated primitive basis.

= Lattice vector
{parent=Bravais lattice}
{title2=$\mathbf R=\sum_i n_i\mathbf a_i,\quad n_i\in\mathbb Z$}

A direct lattice vector is an integer <linear combination> of primitive translation vectors $\mathbf a_i$ of a <Bravais lattice>. Translating by such a vector preserves the lattice. Its dot product with every <reciprocal lattice vector> is an integer multiple of $2\pi$ in the angular-wavevector convention.

= Hard-sphere atomic volume in cubic lattices
{parent=Bravais lattice}

For identical touching atoms represented by <hard spheres> of radius $r$, a <face-centered cubic lattice> has conventional side $a_F=2\sqrt2r$ and four atoms per conventional cell. A <body-centered cubic lattice> has side $a_B=4r/\sqrt3$ and two atoms per conventional cell. Their <primitive unit cell> volumes are respectively
$$
\Omega_F=4\sqrt2r^3,\qquad \Omega_B=\frac{32}{3\sqrt3}r^3.
$$
Each primitive cell contains one atom in the monatomic model, so its <mass density> is atomic <mass> divided by $\Omega$. These are contact-geometry estimates; a fixed hard-sphere radius does not encode <thermal expansion> or phase-dependent electronic bonding.

= Unit cell
{parent=Bravais lattice}

A region whose translates tile a lattice's ambient space, together with the lattice points represented in it. A <primitive unit cell> represents one lattice point; a conventional unit cell may represent several to display symmetry.

= Conventional unit cell
{parent=Unit cell}

A crystallographic <unit cell> chosen to display the symmetry or a standard setting of a <crystal structure>. It tiles the periodic arrangement but need not be primitive. A conventional FCC cube contains four <lattice points> and a BCC cube contains two; a <primitive unit cell> contains one. These are lattice-point counts, not a universal count of atoms in a chemical motif.

= Primitive unit cell
{parent=Unit cell}

A fundamental cell of a <Bravais lattice>, of volume equal to the absolute <determinant> of any primitive lattice basis. Direct and <reciprocal lattice> primitive cell volumes multiply to $(2\pi)^d$ in dimension $d$ with the angular-wavenumber convention.

= Face-centered tetragonal lattice
{parent=Bravais lattice}

A tetragonal conventional cell with lattice points at its vertices and face centers describes a <Bravais lattice>. It is equivalent, after a rotation and a change of conventional cell, to a body-centered tetragonal description. The <reciprocal lattice> of a body-centered tetragonal lattice with conventional sides $a,a,b$ consists of $2\pi(h/a,k/a,l/b)$ with $h+k+l$ even; these points form a face-centered tetragonal conventional cell with sides $4\pi/a,4\pi/a,4\pi/b$.

= Face-centered cubic lattice
{parent=Bravais lattice}

= FCC lattice
{synonym}
{c}

A face-centered cubic lattice has lattice points at every corner and face center of a conventional cube. One primitive cell has one quarter of the conventional cube's volume.

= Face-centred cubic selection rule
{parent=Face-centered cubic lattice}

For equivalent scatterers on the four conventional fcc sites, the centering factor is $1+e^{i\pi(h+k)}+e^{i\pi(h+l)}+e^{i\pi(k+l)}$. It is four when the integer indices are all even or all odd, and zero otherwise. An additional <atomic basis of a crystal> can impose further extinctions. This rule concerns conventional cubic indexing.

A lattice point is a vector $l=n_1a_1+n_2a_2+n_3a_3$ whose coefficients in a chosen primitive <basis> are <integer>[integers].

= Reciprocal lattice
{title2=$\Lambda^*$}
{parent=Bravais lattice}
{wiki}

The reciprocal lattice is
$$
\Lambda^*=\{q:q\cdot l\in2\pi\mathbb Z\text{ for every }l\in\Lambda\}.
$$
If $\Omega=a_1\cdot(a_2\times a_3)$, its primitive vectors are
$$
b_1=2\pi\frac{a_2\times a_3}{\Omega},
\quad
b_2=2\pi\frac{a_3\times a_1}{\Omega},
\quad
b_3=2\pi\frac{a_1\times a_2}{\Omega},
$$
and satisfy $a_i\cdot b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}$.

= Reciprocal lattice rod

= Relrod

An elongated reciprocal-space reflection caused by finite extent in the corresponding real-space direction. A foil of thickness $t$ has reflection breadth of order $1/t$ along its normal, increasing the range over which a reflection can intersect an <Ewald sphere>. = Lattice-periodic plane wave
{parent=Reciprocal lattice}
{title2=$e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}=1$}

A <plane wave> whose phase is unchanged by every direct lattice translation: $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}=1$ for all $\mathbf R\in\Lambda$. Its <wavevector> is a <reciprocal lattice vector>. In one dimension $ka=2\pi m$.

= Reciprocal lattice vector
{parent=Reciprocal lattice}
{title2=$\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R\in2\pi\mathbb Z$}

A reciprocal lattice vector $\mathbf G$ has the displayed property for every direct <lattice vector> $\mathbf R$. Thus $e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R}=1$. Fourier components of a lattice-periodic potential carry these wavevectors and couple plane-wave electronic states whose momenta differ by $\hbar\mathbf G$. This is the origin of the Bragg-plane mixing in a <nearly-free electron model>.

= Bragg plane
{c}
{parent=Reciprocal lattice}
{title2=$\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf G=G^2/2$}

For free-electron dispersion proportional to $k^2$, states at $\mathbf k$ and $\mathbf k-\mathbf G$ are degenerate on this plane, obtained by equating their squared norms. A nonzero periodic-potential Fourier component can mix them and open an avoided crossing in the <nearly-free electron model>. First-zone boundaries are selected planes of this kind.

= Reciprocal lattice change in a structural phase transition
{parent=Reciprocal lattice}

The <reciprocal lattice> of a <body-centered cubic lattice> is face-centred cubic, and that of a <face-centered cubic lattice> is body-centred cubic. In both cases its conventional cubic side is $4\pi/a$ if the direct conventional side is $a$. Thus a direct BCC-to-FCC <phase transition> reverses the reciprocal lattice types. The reciprocal primitive cell volume is $(2\pi)^3/\Omega$, so <thermal expansion> contracts reciprocal spacings, while a discontinuous change in direct <primitive unit cell> volume gives an inverse change in reciprocal cell volume.

A first-order structural transition need not continuously deform one perfect reciprocal array into another. Phase coexistence, new orientation variants, domains and loss of single-crystal alignment can produce two sets of diffraction peaks or many differently oriented sets. The two endpoint lattice types alone do not specify the transformation path or orientation relationship.

= Structure factor
{parent=Crystal scattering}
{title2=$F(\mathbf Q)=\sum_jf_j(\mathbf Q)e^{i\mathbf Q\cdot\mathbf r_j}$}

The coherent <scattering amplitude> associated with a specified spatial arrangement, obtained by summing the site amplitudes with their relative <wave phases>. Distinguish the <crystal lattice structure factor>, which sums over lattice sites, from the <crystal basis structure factor>, which sums the <chemical atoms> inside a <unit cell>. In a periodic <crystal> the two factors multiply. A basis factor can vanish even at an allowed <reciprocal lattice> vector, causing an extinct reflection.

= Crystal lattice structure factor
{parent=Crystal scattering}

For a finite set $S$ of crystal sites, the lattice structure factor at momentum transfer $Q$ is
$$
\Delta(Q)=\sum_{l\in S}e^{iQ\cdot l}.
$$
It multiplies the single-atom <scattering amplitude>.

= Reciprocal-lattice peaks of a finite crystal
{parent=Crystal lattice structure factor}

For $S=\{\sum_il_ia_i:-L_i/2\leq l_i\leq L_i/2\}$ and $\alpha_i=Q\cdot a_i$,
$$
\Delta(Q)=\prod_{i=1}^3
\frac{\sin((L_i+1)\alpha_i/2)}{\sin(\alpha_i/2)}.
$$
For large $L_i$, each factor is sharply peaked when $\alpha_i\in2\pi\mathbb Z$, exactly the condition $Q\in\Lambda^*$.

= Crystal basis structure factor
{parent=Crystal lattice structure factor}
{title2=$F_{hkl}$}

= Chemical ordering and body-centering extinction
{parent=Crystal basis structure factor}

Sites at 000 and one half of each cubic coordinate give $F=f_A+(-1)^{h+k+l}f_B$. Identical sites produce a <body-centered cubic lattice> with absent odd-parity reflections. Distinct chemical species generally preserve only the <simple cubic lattice> translations, so those same odd reflections need not vanish. A geometric body center is not by itself a symmetry translation of the decorated crystal.

= Accidental diffraction extinction from equal atomic scattering factors
{parent=Crystal basis structure factor}

A vanishing <crystal basis structure factor> can depend on approximate equality of <atomic scattering factors>, rather than on an exact <space group> condition. For two inequivalent site classes, an amplitude $F=C(f_A-f_B)$ vanishes when $f_A=f_B$ but becomes weakly observable if bonding <electron densities> or thermal displacements differ. Chemical identity alone does not imply identical local <atomic scattering factors>. The <diffraction intensity> is quadratic in the small difference.

= Atomic scattering factor

The scattering amplitude assigned to one atom or ion as a function of momentum transfer, normalized relative to a stated scattering convention. X-ray and electron factors are different; multiplying by basis phase factors produces the total unit-cell amplitude.

= Superlattice reflection
{parent=Crystal basis structure factor}

A <diffraction> reflection enabled by a larger chemical or structural repeat than that of a reference parent structure. In ordered $L1_2$ $A_3B$, sites $(0,0,0)$ and the three face centres give $F=f_B+f_A[(-1)^{h+k}+(-1)^{h+l}+(-1)^{k+l}]$. Equal-parity indices give $f_B+3f_A$; mixed parity gives $f_B-f_A$. = Elastic Bragg scattering condition
{parent=Crystal scattering}

For elastic scattering with $k-k'=q\in\Lambda^*$ and $|k|=|k'|=k$,
$$
2k\cdot q=|q|^2,
\qquad
|q|=2k\sin\frac\theta2.
$$
The smallest nonzero diffraction angle is determined by the shortest reciprocal-lattice vector.

This reciprocal-lattice condition is equivalent to <Bragg's law>, expressed in terms of the lattice-plane spacing and the wavelength.

= Bragg's law
{c}
{parent=Elastic Bragg scattering condition}
{title2=$n\lambda=2d\sin\theta$}
{wiki}

<Bragg's law> selects directions of <constructive interference> in <coherent elastic scattering> from equally spaced <lattice planes>:
$$
n\lambda=2d\sin\theta,\qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots.
$$
Here $d>0$ is the perpendicular <interplanar spacing>, $\lambda>0$ the incident <wavelength>, $n$ the positive integer <diffraction order>, and $\theta$ the <Bragg angle>, measured between the incoming beam and the <lattice planes>. The scattering deflection, between incoming and outgoing propagation directions, is $2\theta$. Thus a plotted detector angle $2\theta$ must be halved before insertion into <Bragg's law>. The $\theta$ in the parent <elastic Bragg scattering condition> denotes the full deflection; the present $\theta$ denotes half of it. <Online Dictionary of Crystallography by International Union of Crystallography/Bragg's law>[Online Dictionary of Crystallography, Bragg's law, Definition: spacing, wavelength, glancing angle and reflection order]{ref}

The <lattice planes> organize <wave interference> throughout a <crystal structure>; they are not literal metal mirrors or separate reflecting surfaces. The picture assumes <elastic scattering>, hence an unchanged <wavelength>, and sufficiently stable relative <wave phases> across the contributing <lattice planes>. <X-ray> <wavelengths> comparable with atomic <interplanar spacings> make these directions experimentally accessible. The <sine> cannot exceed one: an order exists geometrically only if $n\lambda/(2d)\leq1$. For $\lambda>2d$, even the first <diffraction order> is inaccessible. A geometrically possible direction need not have nonzero <wave intensity>; the <crystal lattice structure factor> and <crystal basis structure factor> also matter. <Online Dictionary of Crystallography by International Union of Crystallography/Structure factor>[Online Dictionary of Crystallography by International Union of Crystallography; Definition and Derivation: coherent addition of the atoms within the unit cell]{ref}

= Bragg angle
{c}
{parent=Bragg's law}
{title2=$\theta$}

The <Bragg angle> is the glancing angle from the incident beam to the selected <lattice planes>, with $0<\theta\leq90^\circ$. If $\alpha$ is instead the angle from the beam to the plane normal, $\alpha=90^\circ-\theta$ and <Bragg's law> becomes $n\lambda=2d\cos\alpha$. Defining the angle explicitly prevents a <sine>-versus-<cosine> error as well as a factor-of-two error in the detector deflection.

Choose $\lambda=0.154\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $d=0.200\,\mathrm{nm}$ as an illustrative model, not historical measurements. For the first <diffraction order>,
$$
\sin\theta=\frac{0.154}{2(0.200)}=0.385,\qquad
\theta\simeq22.643740^\circ,\qquad
2\theta\simeq45.287481^\circ.
$$
The second <diffraction order> has $\sin\theta=0.770$ and $2\theta\simeq100.707778^\circ$; the third would require $\sin\theta=1.155$ and is impossible. These are different <diffraction orders> of the same <interplanar spacing> at the same <wavelength>.

Conversely, a first-order reflection at a measured deflection $2\theta$ gives the <interplanar spacing>
$$
d=\frac{\lambda}{2\sin\theta}.
$$
Using $2\theta\simeq45.287481^\circ$ recovers $d\simeq0.200\,\mathrm{nm}$. This is the useful reversal: a known <wavelength> turns an angular measurement into an atomic length. An unassigned <diffraction order> or an unknown <wavelength> prevents that single measurement from determining $d$ uniquely.

= Isotropic-compression Bragg-angle shift
{parent=Bragg angle}
{title2=$d\theta/dp=\kappa\tan\theta/3$}

For an isotropically compressed cubic <crystal>, $d\ln a/dp=-\kappa/3$. Differentiating <Bragg's law> at fixed <wavelength> gives the displayed derivative. Constant <isothermal compressibility> gives $a(p)=a_0e^{-\kappa p/3}$, not an exactly linear lattice parameter. The integrated prediction presumes no intervening structural phase change.

= Bragg path difference
{c}
{parent=Bragg's law}
{title2=$\Delta\ell=2d\sin\theta$}

Compare two rays scattered into parallel outgoing directions from neighboring <lattice planes>. In the diagram, $A$ lies on the upper plane and $B$ on the lower plane, directly beneath $A$ with $AB=d$. Draw through $A$ the incoming and outgoing equal-<wave phase> fronts, perpendicular to their respective rays. They meet the lower ray at $C$ and $D$. Each right triangle gives $CB=BD=d\sin\theta$, so the lower ray travels the extra distance
$$
\Delta\ell=CB+BD=2d\sin\theta.
$$
The resulting <phase difference> is $\Delta\varphi=2\pi\Delta\ell/\lambda$. <Constructive interference> requires $\Delta\varphi=2\pi n$, which proves <Bragg's law> in this ray construction. Comparing equal-<wave phase> fronts, rather than arbitrary ray endpoints, is what makes the distance comparison meaningful.

\Image[bragg-rays-and-extra-path-geometry.png]

The same geometry connects to the <reciprocal lattice>. With <wavenumber> $k=2\pi/\lambda$, the <elastic Bragg scattering condition> gives a transfer magnitude $|q|=2k\sin\theta$. A reciprocal vector normal to these <lattice planes> has $|q|=2\pi n/d$. Equating them again yields <Bragg's law>. The ray and <reciprocal lattice> descriptions therefore express the same <wave phase> matching.

= Primitive cubic Bragg peak positions
{c}
{parent=Bragg's law}
{title2=$d_{hkl}=a/\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}$}

Consider a chosen <primitive cubic lattice> $a\mathbb Z^3$ with $a=0.400\,\mathrm{nm}$ and one identical point scatterer per <primitive unit cell>. Retain the incident <wavelength> $\lambda=0.154\,\mathrm{nm}$. This is an ideal point model. Its <reciprocal lattice> vectors are $q=(2\pi/a)(h,k,l)$ for integer triples other than $(0,0,0)$. Write $S=h^2+k^2+l^2$. The associated <interplanar spacing> and first-order <Bragg angle> satisfy
$$
d_{hkl}=\frac{2\pi}{|q|}=\frac{a}{\sqrt S},\qquad
\sin^2\theta=\frac{\lambda^2}{4a^2}S=0.03705625S.
$$
The reflection labels $(hkl)$ use the <Miller index> convention, keeping unreduced triples to encode <diffraction order>. With that convention, set $n=1$ in <Bragg's law> for each label. The $(200)$ reflection is equivalently the second <diffraction order> of $(100)$: $d_{200}=a/2$ does not imply extra scatterers halfway between the occupied $(100)$ planes. Counting both descriptions as distinct reflections would count the same direction twice.

For $(100)$, $(110)$, $(111)$ and $(200)$, respectively, $S=1,2,3,4$ and the calculated <interplanar spacings> are $0.400000$, $0.282843$, $0.230940$ and $0.200000\,\mathrm{nm}$. Their calculated deflections $2\theta$ are $22.197435^\circ$, $31.594744^\circ$, $38.952948^\circ$ and $45.287481^\circ$. Equal steps in $S$ give equal steps in $\sin^2\theta$, not equal steps in $2\theta$. This regularity makes an angular pattern a test of a proposed <crystal structure>.

A real <crystal structure> has a <Bravais lattice> together with an <atomic basis of a crystal>. = Bragg crystal reflection experiment of 1913
{c}
{parent=Bragg's law}

<William Lawrence Bragg> introduced the plane interpretation; his father <William Henry Bragg> built the <X-ray> spectrometer. Their joint 1913 paper, https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1913.0040[The Reflection of X-rays by Crystals], reports the experiment. <The Bragg law (University of Leeds Library Special Collections)>[The Bragg law: Lawrence's 1912 interpretation and the angle convention]{ref} <William Bragg's spectrometer (Royal Institution collection)>[Description: William Henry Bragg designed and made the spectrometer]{ref}

An <X-ray tube> supplied radiation through lead slits to a crystal mounted with its cleavage face through the rotation axis. A rotating <ionization chamber> replaced a spectrometer telescope; <electric current> measured the scattered signal, and a circular scale supplied angular readings. They first scanned the chamber at fixed crystal orientation, then swept crystal and chamber together to follow the reflection. <The Reflection of X-rays by Crystals by William Henry Bragg and William Lawrence Bragg>[Bragg and Bragg (1913), pp. 428–430: preparation, source, slits, chamber, angular scale and scanning]{ref}

Changing chamber gas checked detector dependence; readings on opposite sides checked face-setting errors. Repeated peaks with <sines> of half-deflections in integer ratios, together with matching <absorption coefficients>, distinguished repeated <diffraction orders> of the same <wavelength> from different radiation components. Comparing crystal faces constrained <interplanar spacings>. Their limits included slit broadening, face alignment and incomplete interception at glancing incidence; assigning absolute <wavelengths> still required a structural choice for $d$. <The Reflection of X-rays by Crystals by William Henry Bragg and William Lawrence Bragg>[pp. 428–430: gas and alignment controls; pp. 435–437 and Fig. 3: order comparison, absorption test, finite-face limitation and spacing ambiguity]{ref}

= X-ray diffraction
{c}
{parent=Crystal scattering}

<X-ray diffraction> is the angular redistribution of <X-ray> <wave intensity> through <wave interference> between scattered waves. In a <crystal structure>, repeated <lattice translations> select discrete outgoing directions by the <Laue equations>. A <photographic plate> can therefore turn invisible atomic periodicity into a visible constellation of spots. Their geometry constrains the <Bravais lattice> and its orientation; their <wave intensities> also depend on the <crystal basis structure factor>. Determining atomic positions belongs to <X-ray crystallography> and requires more information than recognizing a spot pattern.

The <Laue X-ray diffraction experiment of 1912> established this phenomenon experimentally. The <Laue method> uses a fixed <crystal structure> with a range of incident <wavelengths>; the <Bragg crystal reflection experiment of 1913> instead measured angularly resolved signals with a moving detector. These are different ways of finding the same <coherent elastic scattering> directions.

= Debye-Scherrer camera

A powder-diffraction camera with photographic film wrapped around a cylindrical specimen axis. <Diffraction> cones intersect the film in paired curved lines; angular position along the cylindrical film differs from the $R=L\tan2\theta$ relation for a forward flat plate. Back reflections can also be recorded.

= Single-crystal diffraction
{parent=X-ray diffraction}

Diffraction from an oriented <single crystal>. A reflection requires both an allowed nonzero <structure factor> and access to its reciprocal-lattice direction and <Bragg angle>.

= Transmission diffraction geometry
{parent=X-ray diffraction}

A geometry in which the diffracted beam traverses the specimen and exits another surface. Absorption and thickness affect whether it is observable.

= Asymmetric reflection geometry
{parent=X-ray diffraction}

Reflection diffraction from planes inclined to the specimen surface. Incident and exit angles relative to the surface differ; both must correspond to rays on the accessible side of an opaque specimen.

= Symmetric reflection geometry
{parent=X-ray diffraction}

A diffraction geometry in which the incident and diffracted rays make equal angles with the specimen surface. The selected reciprocal-lattice vector is normal to that surface.

= X-ray diffractometer
{parent=X-ray diffraction}

An instrument measuring <X-ray diffraction> intensity against scattering angle, sample orientation or <photon> energy.

= Powder X-ray diffraction
{parent=X-ray diffraction}

<Powder X-ray diffraction> measures <X-ray diffraction> from many crystal grains with different orientations. For each allowed set of lattice spacings, suitable grains satisfy <Bragg's law> and produce a cone of diffracted directions, seen as a ring on an area detector or a peak in an angular scan. Several peak positions and systematic absences can identify a crystal structure even when a sample no longer retains one single-crystal orientation. Preferred orientation, strain and phase coexistence can complicate interpretation.

= Diffraction thermal-expansion thermometry
{parent=Powder X-ray diffraction}
{title2=$d(2\theta)/dT=-2\alpha_L\tan\theta$}

At fixed radiation <wavelength>, differentiating <Bragg's law> for an isotropically expanding cubic specimen gives the displayed angular sensitivity, with angles in radians. Accurate temperature inference requires a calibrated lattice-parameter versus temperature curve, stable wavelength and angular zero, and separation of thermal expansion from stress or composition shifts. Counting statistics can permit centroid precision finer than the observed diffraction-line width.

A common sequence starts $3,8,11,16,19$ for111,220,311,400,331. The absolute parameter remains proportional to the explicitly selected radiation wavelength.

{wiki}

= Laue condition
{c}
{synonym}

For incident and outgoing unit propagation vectors $\mathbf s_{\rm in}$ and $\mathbf s_{\rm out}$, <wavelength> $\lambda>0$, and <primitive lattice translation vectors> $a_1,a_2,a_3$, the <Laue equations> are
$$
a_1\cdot(\mathbf s_{\rm out}-\mathbf s_{\rm in})=h\lambda,\qquad
a_2\cdot(\mathbf s_{\rm out}-\mathbf s_{\rm in})=k\lambda,\qquad
a_3\cdot(\mathbf s_{\rm out}-\mathbf s_{\rm in})=l\lambda,
\qquad (h,k,l)\in\mathbb Z^3.
$$
All three must hold for the same <wavelength> and outgoing direction. The integers label a <reciprocal lattice> vector; they need not be coprime. For <wavevectors> $\mathbf k_{\rm in,out}=(2\pi/\lambda)\mathbf s_{\rm in,out}$, equivalently
$$
\mathbf G=\mathbf k_{\rm out}-\mathbf k_{\rm in}=h b_1+k b_2+l b_3,\qquad a_i\cdot b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}.
$$
This uses angular <wavenumbers>; the crystallographic convention without $2\pi$ instead uses $\mathbf s/\lambda$.<Online Dictionary of Crystallography by International Union of Crystallography/Laue equations>[Online Dictionary of Crystallography by International Union of Crystallography; Definition: three scalar conditions and reciprocal form]{ref}

For a short proof, translate a scatterer through $a_i$. Its incident <wave phase> changes by $\mathbf k_{\rm in}\cdot a_i$ and propagation to the same distant observation direction contributes $-\mathbf k_{\rm out}\cdot a_i$. The relative <wave phase> factor is therefore $\exp(-i\mathbf G\cdot a_i)$. It equals one for all <lattice translations> exactly when $\mathbf G$ belongs to the <reciprocal lattice>. A finite <crystal lattice structure factor> broadens the ideal maxima; the <crystal basis structure factor> can cancel them. Geometric phase matching is necessary for a sharp reflection, but does not guarantee a visible spot.

For <elastic scattering>, the additional constraint is
$$
|\mathbf k_{\rm in}+\mathbf G|^2=|\mathbf k_{\rm in}|^2,
\qquad 2\mathbf k_{\rm in}\cdot\mathbf G+|\mathbf G|^2=0.
$$
It is an <Ewald sphere> centered at $-\mathbf k_{\rm in}$ in the space of transfer vectors $\mathbf G$. The parent <elastic Bragg scattering condition> uses $q=\mathbf k_{\rm in}-\mathbf k_{\rm out}=-\mathbf G$, so its displayed condition has the opposite sign. Both give <Bragg's law> for the same <lattice planes>.

= Laue method
{c}
{parent=X-ray diffraction}

In the <Laue method>, a fixed single <crystal structure> receives a collimated beam containing many <wavelengths>. Each <reciprocal lattice> vector can select its own <wavelength>; waves with different <wavelengths> need not interfere with one another. For $\mathbf G\ne0$, the <elastic scattering> constraint gives the particularly useful test
$$
\lambda_{\mathbf G}=-\frac{4\pi\,\mathbf s_{\rm in}\cdot\mathbf G}{|\mathbf G|^2}.
$$
A candidate reflection requires $\lambda_{\mathbf G}>0$ within the source spectrum and an outgoing ray that reaches the detector. Varying <wavelength> changes the radius and center of the <Ewald sphere> while leaving the <reciprocal lattice> fixed. A <monochromatic wave> instead fixes the radius: rotating the <crystal structure> or varying the incident <wavelength> in a scan finds reflections sequentially. A white-beam photograph integrates many spectral selections in one exposure; it is not an angular scan at one known <wavelength>.

The <Laue method> is useful for orienting a single <crystal structure>: tilting it changes which <reciprocal lattice> vectors meet the spectral and detector conditions. Absolute scale has an ambiguity if the <wavelengths> are unknown. Scaling all direct <lattice translations> by $c>0$ and all selected <wavelengths> by $c$ leaves every outgoing direction unchanged. Independently known spectral information or length calibration is needed to remove this ambiguity.

= Calculated transmission Laue pattern
{parent=Laue method}
{title2=$\lambda=2am/(h^2+k^2+m^2)$}

Choose a <primitive cubic lattice> with $a=0.400\,\mathrm{nm}$, one identical point scatterer per <primitive unit cell>, incident direction $\mathbf s_{\rm in}=(0,0,1)$, and the continuous <wavelength> band $0.080\leq\lambda\leq0.160\,\mathrm{nm}$. This is a hypothetical model, not the <zinc blende> specimen of the <Laue X-ray diffraction experiment of 1912>. Write a candidate <reciprocal lattice> vector as $\mathbf G=(2\pi/a)(h,k,-m)$ with $m>0$, and set $S=h^2+k^2+m^2$. The <Laue equations> and <elastic scattering> give
$$
\lambda=\frac{2am}{S},\qquad
\mathbf s_{\rm out}=\left(\frac{2mh}{S},\frac{2mk}{S},1-\frac{2m^2}{S}\right).
$$
A transmission <photographic plate> at $z=L>0$ intercepts only $s_{{\rm out},z}>0$. Its coordinates relative to the direct beam are
$$
X=L\frac{s_{{\rm out},x}}{s_{{\rm out},z}}=\frac{2Lmh}{h^2+k^2-m^2},\qquad
Y=\frac{2Lmk}{h^2+k^2-m^2}.
$$
For $(h,k,-m)=(2,1,-1)$ and $L=50\,\mathrm{mm}$, $S=6$, $\lambda=0.13333\ldots\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $\mathbf s_{\rm out}=(2/3,1/3,2/3)$, so $(X,Y)=(50,25)\,\mathrm{mm}$. Its length is one, and each component difference satisfies the corresponding <Laue equations>. This verifies a direction and a selected <wavelength>, without predicting an exposure or measured <wave intensity>.

= Laue X-ray diffraction experiment of 1912
{c}
{parent=X-ray diffraction}

<Max von Laue> proposed crystal <X-ray diffraction> and wrote the theoretical section; <Walther Friedrich> and <Paul Knipping> performed the experiments.<Interferenz-Erscheinungen bei Röntgenstrahlen by Walther Friedrich, Paul Knipping and Max von Laue>[pp. 303–304, 311: section authorship]{ref}

An <X-ray tube>, lead apertures and shielding supplied a narrow beam through a crystal on a <goniometer>. <Photographic plates> recorded the direct beam and separate spots. <Copper(II) sulfate> trials preceded oriented <zinc blende>. Optical alignment was checked with <X-rays>. Removing the crystal removed the extra spots; grinding destroyed their ordered arrangement; translation preserved it, while tilt changed it. Rotation about the beam rotated the pattern.<Interferenz-Erscheinungen bei Röntgenstrahlen by Walther Friedrich, Paul Knipping and Max von Laue>[pp. 311–318: apparatus and controls]{ref}

Spots and orientation dependence supported <wave interference> and three-dimensional crystal periodicity. They determined neither all atomic positions nor an absolute <wavelength> scale. The authors left open whether the selected radiation originated as crystal <fluorescence> or was already in the incident beam. This differs from the modern <Laue method>.<Interferenz-Erscheinungen bei Röntgenstrahlen by Walther Friedrich, Paul Knipping and Max von Laue>[pp. 310–311, 320–322: inference and spectral uncertainty]{ref}

The 1914 <Nobel Prize in Physics> went to <Max von Laue>; the discovery's experimental account remains jointly credited to <Walther Friedrich> and <Paul Knipping>.<The Nobel Prize in Physics 1914 by Nobel Prize Outreach>[1914 award and laureate]{ref} The subsequent inference of particular <crystal structures> is developed under <X-ray crystallography>.
