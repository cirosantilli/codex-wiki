= Integral transform
{wiki}

An integral transform maps a function to a new function by integration against a kernel.

= Linear canonical transform
{synonym}

A family of <integral transforms> associated with a two-by-two real <matrix> of determinant one. For $B\ne0$, one optical normalization has kernel proportional to $\exp[ik(Ax^2+Dx'^2-2xx')/(2B)]$. It includes free-space <Fresnel diffraction>, quadratic phase multiplication and the <Fourier transform>. The optical amplitude normalization in one transverse dimension is $1/\sqrt{i\lambda B}$ with a consistent phase branch.

= Weierstrass transform
{c}
{parent=Integral transform}
{wiki}

The <Weierstrass transform> is <Gaussian> <convolution>, $Wf(x)=(4\pi)^{-1/2}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-(x-y)^2/4}f(y)\,dy$. It smooths appropriate functions and is the <heat semigroup> at unit time. The <Brownian transition semigroup> uses variance-$t$ Gaussian kernels and therefore supplies the scaled time family.

= Spherical mean
{title2=$M_rf(x)$}
{parent=Integral transform}
{wiki}

The spherical mean of a function on $\mathbb R^n$ is its average over a sphere:
$$
M_rf(x)=\frac1{|S^{n-1}|}\int_{S^{n-1}}f(x+r\omega)\,d\omega.
$$

= Spherical mean of a Poisson solution outside its source
{parent=Spherical mean}
{title2=$\bar v(s)=\frac{M}{4\pi}(s^{-1}-R^{-1})$}

In three dimensions let $v\in H_0^1(B_R)$ solve $-\Delta v=f$, where $f\in L^{6/5}(B_R)$ is supported in $B_a$ and $0<a<R$. Write $M=\int f$ and $\bar v(s)=(4\pi)^{-1}\int_{S^2}v(s\omega)\,d\omega$. Outside $B_a$, the <weak solution> is a <harmonic function>. The <weak formulation> against radial <test functions> gives $-4\pi s^2\bar v'(s)=M$ for $a<s<R$. The zero <Sobolev trace> gives $\bar v(R)=0$; integration proves the displayed formula, without assuming the source is a <radial function>.

<Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> on spheres gives $\|v\|_2^2\geq M^2(4\pi)^{-1}\int_a^R(1-s/R)^2\,ds$. For fixed nonzero $M$ and fixed $a$, this grows linearly in $R$. Such solutions on expanding <balls> cannot remain bounded in the full $H^1(\mathbb R^3)$ <norm> after <zero extension of H01>.

= Radon transform
{title2=$\mathcal Rf(s,\omega)$}
{c}
{parent=Integral transform}
{wiki}

The Radon transform integrates a function over affine hyperplanes:
$$
\mathcal Rf(s,\omega)=\int_{x\cdot\omega=s}f(x)\,dA_x.
$$

= Filtered backprojection
{parent=Radon transform}
{title2=$f(x)=(4\pi)^{-1}\int_0^{2\pi}(\partial_\rho H\mathcal Rf)(x\cdot n_\theta,\theta)d\theta$}

The <Radon transform> records line integrals of a planar density. Apply the transverse <Hilbert transform> and a derivative to each projection, then average the filtered values over all lines through the reconstruction point. With $Hh(\rho)=\pi^{-1}\operatorname{PV}\int h(r)/(\rho-r)dr$, the filter has <Fourier multiplier> $|\kappa|$. The <Fourier inversion theorem>, or the jump of the <complexified transport Cauchy kernel>, proves the displayed formula. The factor is $1/(2\pi)$ when directions are integrated over a half-circle. The growing <Fourier multiplier> explains amplification of measurement noise and the need for <regularization of an inverse problem>.

= Attenuated Radon transform
{parent=Radon transform}
{title2=$\mathcal R_\mu f$}

The integral of a source over a directed line, weighted by exponential absorption between each source point and a detector. For line coordinates $x=p\boldsymbol n+s\boldsymbol d$ and a detector at the negative end, it is $\int f(p,s)\exp[-\int_{-\infty}^s\mu(p,r)dr]ds$. The <integrating factor> of a <transport equation> derives the weight. Reversing the detector direction changes the weight for spatially varying attenuation, so the two directed measurements need not coincide. Setting $\mu=0$ recovers the <Radon transform>.

= Spectral reconstruction of an attenuated Radon transform
{parent=Attenuated Radon transform}
{title2=$L_k=k\partial_z+k^{-1}\partial_{\bar z}$}

Complexifying the directional <transport equation> yields spectral solutions analytic inside and outside the unit circle. An <integrating factor> built from known attenuation removes its multiplication term. The limiting Green kernels encode weighted line measurements and transverse <Hilbert transforms>. Their jump gives a <Riemann-Hilbert problem>; its solution recovers the source from an asymptotic coefficient. A general primary treatment of this spectral construction is https://www.stat.uchicago.edu/~guillaumebal/PAPERS/AttRadTh.pdf .

= Projection formula for inverse attenuated Radon transform
{parent=Spectral reconstruction of an attenuated Radon transform}
{title2=$g=(4\pi)^{-1}(\partial_{x_1}-i\partial_{x_2})\int_0^{2\pi}e^{i\theta}J\,d\theta$}

Let $F(\rho,\tau,\theta)=\int_\tau^\infty f(s\boldsymbol d+\rho\boldsymbol n)ds$, $S=\mathcal Rf$, and $A=\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-F}g\,d\tau$ be the directed <attenuated Radon transform>. With the <signed Cauchy boundary operators>, set $J=e^F[e^{P^-S}P^-(e^{-P^-S}A)+e^{-P^+S}P^+(e^{P^+S}A)]$. An <integrating factor> turns the attenuated equation into unattenuated directional transport; its spectral solution has jump $-J$ on the unit circle. A normalized <Cauchy integral formula> and the coefficient at spectral infinity then prove the reconstruction formula. Known attenuation and full directed angular data determine the source; attenuation itself is not reconstructed by this pair.

= Complexified transport Cauchy kernel
{parent=Spectral reconstruction of an attenuated Radon transform}
{title2=$E_k(w)=\operatorname{sgn}(1-|k|^2)\,k/[\pi(w-k^2\bar w)]$}

For $w=x_1+ix_2$ and $0<|k|\ne1$, the operator $D_k=(k+k^{-1})\partial_{x_1}/2+(k-k^{-1})\partial_{x_2}/(2i)$ has the displayed decaying <fundamental solution>. A rotated real-linear coordinate turns it into a multiple of a <Wirtinger derivative>, reducing the kernel identity to $\partial_{\bar z}(1/(\pi z))=\delta$. The kernel is analytic in $k$ separately inside and outside the unit circle, is $O(k)$ at zero and $O(k^{-1})$ at infinity, and develops a jump at the real directional-transport limit.

= Mellin transformation
{c}
{synonym}

The <Mellin transform> is an <integral transform> suited to changes of scale: replacing $f(x)$ by $f(ax)$ multiplies its transform by $a^{-s}$ for $a>0$. For a <locally integrable function> $f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb C$, define
$$
\mathcal Mf(s)=\int_0^\infty x^{s-1}f(x)\,dx,\qquad s=\sigma+i\tau,
$$
where the <improper integral> converges. The <complex number> $x^{s-1}=\exp((s-1)\log x)$ uses the real <natural logarithm> because $x>0$. Its <absolute value> is $x^{\sigma-1}$, so <absolute convergence> depends on the <real part> $\sigma$ rather than the <imaginary part> $\tau$. The <Mellin fundamental strip> records the open range of such <real parts>.<NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions>[Chapter 2, §2.5(i), equation 2.5.1 and the following strip-of-analyticity paragraph]{ref}

A familiar example is the <exponential function> $f(x)=e^{-x}$:
$$
\mathcal M\{e^{-x}\}(s)=\Gamma(s),\qquad \operatorname{Re}s>0.
$$
This is exactly the <Gamma integral>. Near zero $e^{-x}$ tends to one, so <absolute convergence> requires $\sigma>0$; at infinity $x^r e^{-x}\to0$ for every real $r$, so there is no upper boundary. The <Gamma function> has a <meromorphic continuation> with <simple poles> at $0,-1,-2,\ldots$, but a finite continued value elsewhere in $\sigma\leq0$ does not make the defining <improper integral> converge.<NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions>[Chapter 5, §5.2(i), equation 5.2.1, its Re z > 0 condition, and the following analytic-continuation paragraph]{ref}

The <Mellin transform of an interval indicator> gives an elementary example with <absolute convergence> for every <complex number> $s$. The <Mellin transform in logarithmic coordinates> explains why changes of scale become <translations>, connecting the <scaling property of the Mellin transform> to the <Fourier transform>.

= Restricted Mellin transform on the unit interval
{parent=Mellin transform}
{title2=$H(s)=\int_0^1\lambda^{s-1}h(\lambda)\,d\lambda$}

This is the <Mellin transform> of the function extended by zero beyond the unit interval. The <change of variables> $\lambda=e^{-t}$ identifies it with the unilateral <Laplace transform> of $h(e^{-t})$. The <Bromwich inversion formula> therefore gives $h(\lambda)=(2\pi i)^{-1}\int_{\gamma-i\infty}^{\gamma+i\infty}\lambda^{-s}H(s)\,ds$ for $0<\lambda<1$, with $\gamma$ in the convergence half-plane and suitable inversion hypotheses. At a jump, symmetric inversion returns the mean of the two limiting values. In particular, for $0<c<1$, the cutoff $h(\lambda)=\lambda^q\mathbf1_{(0,c)}(\lambda)$ has transform $c^{s+q}/(s+q)$ when $\operatorname{Re}(s+q)>0$. Multiplication by $c^s$ shifts the support threshold in logarithmic coordinates, the <Laplace transform time-shift rule> expressed multiplicatively.

= Mellin integral of a reciprocal binomial
{c}
{parent=Mellin transform}
{title2=$\int_0^\infty\frac{x^{s-1}}{1+x^p}dx=\frac\pi{p\sin(\pi s/p)}$}

For real $p>0$ and $0<s<p$, the displayed <improper integral> converges: its endpoint powers are $x^{s-1}$ near zero and $x^{s-p-1}$ near infinity. The <change of variables> $t=x^p$ gives
$$
\frac1p\int_0^\infty\frac{t^{s/p-1}}{1+t}dt=\frac1pB(s/p,1-s/p)=\frac{\Gamma(s/p)\Gamma(1-s/p)}p=\frac\pi{p\sin(\pi s/p)}.
$$
Here the <beta function>, the <beta-gamma identity>, and the <Gamma reflection formula> evaluate the integral. The same formula holds for complex $s$ in the <Mellin fundamental strip> $0<\operatorname{Re}s<p$, by absolute convergence and analytic continuation within the strip. For integer $p$, a sector contour of angle $2\pi/p$ gives a direct <residue theorem> proof, with a consistent branch of $z^{s-1}$.

= Mellin transform of an interval indicator
{c}
{parent=Mellin transform}
{title2=$\mathcal M\mathbf1_{[b,c]}(s)=(c^s-b^s)/s$}

For $0<b<c<\infty$, the <indicator function> $f(x)=\mathbf1_{[b,c]}(x)$ has <Mellin transform>
$$
\mathcal Mf(s)=\begin{cases}(c^s-b^s)/s,&s\ne0,\\ \log(c/b),&s=0.\end{cases}
$$
The <interval> stays away from both zero and infinity, so the <integral> is <absolutely convergent> for every <complex number> $s$. Its <Mellin fundamental strip> is the whole <complex plane>. Evaluating the <antiderivative> $x^s/s$ proves the formula for $s\ne0$; at $s=0$, integrating $1/x$ gives the real <natural logarithm> $\log(c/b)$. The apparent division by zero is a <removable singularity>, since $c^s-b^s=s(\log c-\log b)+O(s^2)$ by the <Taylor series> of the <exponential function>. The transform is an <entire function>.

For example, $\mathbf1_{[1,4]}$ gives $(4^s-1)/s$ with value $\log4$ at zero. On $s=i\tau$, the <Mellin transform> sums oscillations over the width $\log4$ of the <interval> in <natural logarithm> coordinates; this is the same cancellation mechanism as in the <Fourier transform of an interval indicator>.

= Fundamental strip of a Mellin transform
{parent=Mellin transform}
{title2=$\alpha<\operatorname{Re}s<\beta$}

= Mellin fundamental strip
{c}
{synonym}

The fundamental strip of a <Mellin transform> is the interior of the <set> of <real parts> $\sigma$ for which
$$
\int_0^\infty x^{\sigma-1}|f(x)|\,dx<\infty.
$$
Viewed in the <complex plane>, it is a vertical strip $\alpha<\operatorname{Re}s<\beta$; either bound may be infinite, and the interior may be empty. Both endpoints of the <improper integral> must be checked. Convergence on a boundary line, whether <absolute convergence> or <conditional convergence>, requires a separate test. The <Mellin transform> is <holomorphic> throughout a nonempty fundamental strip.<NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions>[Chapter 2, §2.5(i), equation 2.5.1 and the following description of the vertical domain of analyticity]{ref}

A practical sufficient test uses <Big O notation>: if $f(x)=O(x^p)$ near zero and $f(x)=O(x^{-q})$ near infinity, with real $p,q$, then comparison with elementary <improper integrals> gives
$$
\int_0^1x^{\sigma+p-1}\,dx<\infty\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \sigma>-p,\qquad
\int_1^\infty x^{\sigma-q-1}\,dx<\infty\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \sigma<q.
$$
Thus $-p<\sigma<q$ guarantees <absolute convergence>. These upper bounds on $|f|$ need not locate the maximal <Mellin fundamental strip>; faster actual decay can widen it. The strip property itself follows by bounding the weight at any intermediate $\sigma$ by the sum of two weights already known to be integrable. On a closed narrower strip, neighboring integrable weights also dominate the <natural logarithm> factors introduced by <differentiation under the integral sign>, making the transform a <holomorphic function>.

An exact two-endpoint example is
$$
f(x)=\begin{cases}x^2,&0<x\leq1,\\x^{-3},&x>1,\end{cases}\qquad
\mathcal Mf(s)=\frac1{s+2}+\frac1{3-s},\qquad -2<\operatorname{Re}s<3.
$$
Here both bounds are sharp: at $\sigma=-2$ the zero-end <absolute convergence> test contains $\int_0^1dx/x$, and at $\sigma=3$ the infinity-end test contains $\int_1^\infty dx/x$. The displayed <rational function> continues meromorphically outside the <Mellin fundamental strip> and has <simple poles> at $-2$ and $3$. Its other finite continued values outside the strip are values of a <meromorphic continuation>, not of a convergent defining <integral>. More general endpoint information leads to <meromorphic continuation of a Mellin transform from an asymptotic expansion>.

= Scaling property of the Mellin transform
{parent=Mellin transform}
{title2=$\mathcal M\{f(a\,\cdot)\}(s)=a^{-s}\mathcal Mf(s)$}

For $a>0$, let $g(x)=f(ax)$. Whenever the <Mellin transform> of $f$ is <absolutely convergent>,
$$
\mathcal Mg(s)=a^{-s}\mathcal Mf(s),\qquad a^{-s}=\exp(-s\log a).
$$
The <Mellin fundamental strip> is unchanged: the corresponding <integrals> of <absolute values> differ by the finite positive factor $a^{-\sigma}$. The <change of variables> $y=ax$ gives $x^{s-1}dx=a^{-s}y^{s-1}dy$, proving the rule. For $s=\sigma+i\tau$, the transform's <absolute value> changes by $a^{-\sigma}$, while the factor $e^{-i\tau\log a}$ changes its <phase>. On the line $\sigma=0$, wherever <absolute convergence> holds, the transform's <absolute value> is preserved.

For the <Mellin transform of an interval indicator>, replacing $\mathbf1_{[1,4]}(x)$ by $\mathbf1_{[1,4]}(2x)=\mathbf1_{[1/2,2]}(x)$ gives
$$
\frac{2^s-(1/2)^s}{s}=2^{-s}\frac{4^s-1}{s}.
$$
At the <removable singularity> $s=0$, both sides have value $\log4$. Scaling leaves the width measured by the <natural logarithm> unchanged. Likewise, the <exponential function> $e^{-ax}$ has <Mellin transform> $a^{-s}\Gamma(s)$ on $\operatorname{Re}s>0$.

= Mellin transform in logarithmic coordinates
{c}
{parent=Mellin transform}
{title2=$\mathcal Mf(\sigma+i\tau)=\widehat h_\sigma(-\tau)$}

The <change of variables> $x=e^u$, using the <exponential function>, turns the <Mellin transform> into
$$
\mathcal Mf(s)=\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{su}f(e^u)\,du.
$$
The factor $dx=e^u du$ changes $x^{s-1}$ to $e^{su}$; missing this factor would shift every exponent by one. With $h_\sigma(u)=e^{\sigma u}f(e^u)$ and the <Fourier transform> convention $\widehat h(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb R}h(u)e^{-i\omega u}\,du$, the identity is
$$
\mathcal Mf(\sigma+i\tau)=\int_{\mathbb R}h_\sigma(u)e^{i\tau u}\,du=\widehat h_\sigma(-\tau).
$$
Choosing $\sigma$ inside the <Mellin fundamental strip> makes $h_\sigma$ a <Lebesgue integrable function>. The minus sign in the <Fourier transform> argument follows from the stated convention. For the <Mellin transform of an interval indicator> with $[b,c]=[1,4]$ and $\sigma=0$, $h_0=\mathbf1_{[0,\log4]}$: the <Fourier transform of an interval indicator> is literally the same computation in a different coordinate.

Splitting the <real line> at $u=0$ also connects the <Mellin transform> to two unilateral <Laplace transforms>. If $k_+(v)=f(e^v)$ and $k_-(v)=f(e^{-v})$ for $v\geq0$, then
$$
\mathcal Mf(s)=\mathcal Lk_+(-s)+\mathcal Lk_-(s)
$$
where both <integrals> converge absolutely. Their <Laplace convergence regions> impose opposite endpoint conditions, explaining the two sides of the <Mellin fundamental strip>. Finally, $f(ae^u)=f(e^{u+\log a})$ shows that a change of scale is a <translation> in $u$. Weighted <Fourier transforms> of this <translation> give exactly the factor $a^{-s}$ in the <scaling property of the Mellin transform>. For a smooth <function> with <compact support> inside $(0,\infty)$, the <Mellin inversion theorem> follows from <Fourier inversion>; the existing formula states the corresponding hypotheses.

= Imaginary-part regularization at a zero Mellin exponent
{parent=Mellin transform}
{title2=$\operatorname{Im}\!\left[-\frac1a\frac1{ia+\log x}\right]=\frac1{a^2+\log^2x}$}

At exponent zero the complex <integral> $\int_0 x^{-1}e^{-tx}/(ia+\log x)\,dx$ fails absolute and ordinary convergence through its real part, proportional to $dx/(x\log x)$. The imaginary-part kernel displayed above instead decays as $dx/(x\log^2x)$ and is integrable. Thus a positive-exponent family can have a well-defined imaginary-part boundary limit even when its full complex limit is invalid. Endpoint expansion coefficients need not be uniform in that limiting exponent: a sine zero can cancel a gamma-function pole and introduce a new nonzero constant term.

{c}

For $C(k)=\mathbb E(e^\xi-e^k)^+$ and $\operatorname{Re}z>1$ in the <exponential moment> domain, the <moment-generating function> satisfies $\mathbb Ee^{z\xi}=\int_{\mathbb R}C(k)z(z-1)e^{(z-1)k}dk$.

= Meromorphic continuation of a Mellin transform from an asymptotic expansion
{parent=Mellin transform}
{title2=$\operatorname{Res}_{s=-\sigma_j}\mathcal Mf(s)=c_j$}

Suppose $f$ decays faster than every power at infinity and, near zero, $f(y)=\sum_{j=1}^r c_jy^{\sigma_j}+O(y^{\sigma_{r+1}})$ for every $r$, with $\sigma_j$ strictly increasing and tending to infinity. Subtracting finitely many terms in the integral over $(0,1)$ gives
$$
\mathcal Mf(s)=\int_1^\infty f(y)y^{s-1}\,dy+\sum_{j=1}^r\frac{c_j}{s+\sigma_j}+\int_0^1\left(f(y)-\sum_{j=1}^r c_jy^{\sigma_j}\right)y^{s-1}\,dy.
$$
The last integral is <holomorphic> on $\operatorname{Re}s>-\sigma_{r+1}$. These expressions continue the <Mellin transform> meromorphically to the whole plane, with simple poles precisely at $-\sigma_j$ for nonzero $c_j$.

= Accumulating asymptotic exponents obstruct Mellin continuation
{parent=Meromorphic continuation of a Mellin transform from an asymptotic expansion}

Strict increase alone does not suffice in the preceding theorem. Let $\sigma_j=1-1/j$, $c_j=2^{-j}$ and $f(y)=\eta(y)\sum_{j\geq1}2^{-j}y^{1-1/j}$, where $\eta$ is continuous, equals one on $(0,1]$ and vanishes on $[2,\infty)$. After division by the next power, every finite remainder extends continuously to zero. Nevertheless the <Mellin transform> has genuine poles at $-\sigma_j$ accumulating at $-1$, so it cannot be a <meromorphic function> on the whole plane.

= Mellin inversion formula
{c}
{synonym}

Under standard decay and regularity hypotheses,
$$
f(x)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}
\mathcal Mf(s)x^{-s}\,ds.
$$
If $f$ is smooth and compactly supported in $(0,\infty)$, its Mellin transform is entire, decays faster than every power on vertical lines, and the formula holds for every real $\sigma$ and every $x>0$.

= Laplace transform
{c}
{title2=$\mathcal L$}
{parent=Integral transform}
{wiki}

The unilateral <Laplace transform> of a <function> $f:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb C$ is the <integral transform>
$$
F(s)=\mathcal L\{f\}(s)=\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f(t)\,dt,\qquad s=\sigma+i\omega\in\mathbb C.
$$
Here $s$ is a <complex number>, $\sigma$ is its <real part>, and $i$ is the <imaginary unit>. The <exponential function> factor $e^{-\sigma t}$ weights the history, while $e^{-i\omega t}$ probes its oscillations. The <integral> starts at zero, so it uses only the history from that starting time onward. This section concerns ordinary <functions>; their value at the single endpoint does not change the <integral>. See https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03sc-differential-equations-fall-2011/d40ad07a62996f6dad93b832f8de0e04_MIT18_03SCF11_s27_1text.pdf[MIT 18.03SC, definition and elementary examples].

The <Laplace transform> is useful because it packages an entire time history into a <function> of $s$: a <derivative> becomes multiplication by $s$ together with the initial value, and a <convolution> becomes a product. Thus a <linear differential equation> can become an algebraic equation that already remembers its <initial value problem>. The worked example below shows both descriptions of the same response.

If $f$ is a <piecewise continuous function> and has <exponential order> $a$, then its defining <integral> is <absolutely convergent> for $\operatorname{Re}s>a$. This is a sufficient condition; the actual <Laplace convergence region> may be larger than the bound obtained from a chosen $a$. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03sc-differential-equations-fall-2011/fff4420903d82c5c5c483e54566bc5ec_MIT18_03SCF11_s27_2text.pdf[MIT 18.03SC, domain and growth conditions]{ref}.

\Image[laplace-transform-exponential-weight.png]
{title=<Exponential function> weighting controls the <Laplace convergence region>}
{description=Left: for $f(t)=e^{-2t}$, the weighted <function> $e^{-\sigma t}f(t)$ decays when $\sigma>-2$, stays at one when $\sigma=-2$, and grows when $\sigma<-2$. Right: the real-parameter <Laplace transforms> of $1$ and $e^{-2t}$ grow without bound as $s$ approaches their respective convergence boundaries from the right. Curves are drawn only where the defining <integrals> converge.}

The bound has a short explanation. For sufficiently large $t$, $|f(t)|\leq Me^{at}$, so $|e^{-st}f(t)|\leq Me^{-(\sigma-a)t}$, whose tail <integral> is finite for $\sigma>a$. The finite initial <interval> is covered by <piecewise continuity>.

Three calculations make the convergence condition concrete. For the input $f(t)=1$, evaluate the finite-history <integral> first:
$$
\int_0^T e^{-st}\,dt=\frac{1-e^{-sT}}s\quad(s\ne0),\qquad \mathcal L\{1\}(s)=\frac1s\quad(\operatorname{Re}s>0).
$$
At $s=0$ the finite-history <integral> is $T$. For the <exponential function> input $f(t)=e^{-2t}$, the same calculation replaces $s$ by $s+2$, giving
$$
\mathcal L\{e^{-2t}\}(s)=\frac1{s+2},\qquad \operatorname{Re}s>-2.
$$
In particular, $s=-1$ is allowed for this decaying input: its weighted <integral> is $\int_0^\infty e^{-t}\,dt=1$. For the <sine> input, <Euler's formula> gives $\sin t=(e^{it}-e^{-it})/(2i)$; <linearity> then yields
$$
\mathcal L\{\sin t\}(s)=\frac1{2i}\left(\frac1{s-i}-\frac1{s+i}\right)=\frac1{s^2+1},\qquad \operatorname{Re}s>0.
$$
The <Laplace convergence region> is part of the answer. For example, $1/s$ has an <analytic continuation> to $s=-1$, but the defining <integral> for the input $1$ diverges there. The <sine> calculation is also developed in https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03sc-differential-equations-fall-2011/2343f70cc426b469402f731015ac9719_MIT18_03SCF11_s27_3text.pdf[MIT 18.03SC, further elementary transforms].

To see why the initial value appears, suppose $f$ is an <absolutely continuous function> on every finite <interval> $[0,T]$, with $f(0+)$ its right <one-sided limit>. At the chosen $s$, assume $e^{-st}f(t)$ and $e^{-st}f'(t)$ are <Lebesgue integrable functions> and $e^{-sT}f(T)\to0$. The <Laplace transform of a derivative> is then
$$
\mathcal L\{f'\}(s)=sF(s)-f(0+).
$$
For example, <exponential order> bounds on both $f$ and $f'$ guarantee these conditions whenever $\operatorname{Re}s$ exceeds both growth bounds. The initial value is the lower-end boundary contribution of <integration by parts>.

Consider the <initial value problem>
$$
y'(t)+2y(t)=1,\qquad y(0)=0,\qquad t\geq0.
$$
Write $Y=\mathcal L\{y\}$. The <Laplace transform of a derivative> and <linearity> give $sY-0+2Y=1/s$ for $\operatorname{Re}s>0$. A <partial fraction decomposition> therefore gives
$$
Y(s)=\frac1{s(s+2)}=\frac12\left(\frac1s-\frac1{s+2}\right),\qquad y(t)=\frac{1-e^{-2t}}2.
$$
We recognize the two elementary <Laplace transforms> already computed. Directly verify the result: its <derivative> is $y'(t)=e^{-2t}$, so
$$
y'(t)+2y(t)-1=e^{-2t}+1-e^{-2t}-1=0,\qquad y(0)=\frac{1-1}2=0.
$$
This verifies both the <ordinary differential equation> and the initial value, and shows that the resulting bounded <function> has the <Laplace transform> used above. The response rises from zero toward $1/2$: at that value the input $1$ balances the term $2y$. The general method is described in https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03sc-differential-equations-fall-2011/pages/unit-iii-fourier-series-and-laplace-transform/laplace-transform-solving-initial-value-problems/[MIT 18.03SC, solving initial value problems].

The same solution is a <convolution>. For <functions> defined on $[0,\infty)$, the unilateral <convolution> is
$$
(f*g)(t)=\int_0^t f(\tau)g(t-\tau)\,d\tau,\qquad t\geq0.
$$
The main <convolution> article integrates over the whole <real line>; applying a <causal extension by zero> to both <functions> makes its formula identical to this one. Nonzero contributions require both $\tau\geq0$ and $t-\tau\geq0$, hence $0\leq\tau\leq t$. If $e^{-\sigma t}f(t)$ and $e^{-\sigma t}g(t)$ are <Lebesgue integrable functions> on $[0,\infty)$, the <convolution theorem for Laplace transforms> states
$$
\mathcal L\{f*g\}(s)=F(s)G(s),\qquad \operatorname{Re}s=\sigma.
$$
For <piecewise continuous functions> of <exponential order> $a$ and $b$, it holds throughout $\operatorname{Re}s>\max(a,b)$. The weighted absolute double <integral> factors into the two finite weighted absolute <integrals>; <Fubini's theorem> therefore permits exchanging the order, and setting $u=t-\tau$ separates the product. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03sc-differential-equations-fall-2011/0d4d90053f2bb24f7551c78e2d1b21a2_MIT18_03SCF11_s30_5text.pdf[MIT 18.03SC, convolution and system response]{ref}.

For the <linear time-invariant system> $y'+2y=f$ starting at zero, the <convolution kernel> is its <impulse response> $h(t)=e^{-2t}$ for $t\geq0$, extended by zero for $t<0$. With the <Heaviside step function> input, $f(t)=1$ for $t\geq0$, the accumulated response is
$$
y(t)=(h*f)(t)=\int_0^t e^{-2(t-\tau)}\,d\tau=\frac{1-e^{-2t}}2.
$$
Thus $Y(s)=(1/(s+2))(1/s)$ on $\operatorname{Re}s>0$ is both the algebraic solution and the <Laplace transform> of a finite-history <convolution>. Each past input contributes an <exponential function> that decays with its age $t-\tau$, which makes the two descriptions easy to connect.

Finally, compare the <Fourier transform> convention $\widehat g(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb R}g(t)e^{-i\omega t}\,dt$. If $f_0$ is the <causal extension by zero> of $f$, define $g_\sigma(t)=e^{-\sigma t}f_0(t)$. Whenever $g_\sigma$ is a <Lebesgue integrable function>,
$$
F(\sigma+i\omega)=\widehat{g_\sigma}(\omega).
$$
A vertical slice of the <Laplace transform> is therefore the <Fourier transform> of the zero-extended, weighted input. Positive $\sigma$ damps its positive-time history. A finite <window function> instead gives $g_{\sigma,T}(t)=e^{-\sigma t}f_0(t)\mathbf1_{[0,T]}(t)$ and $\widehat{g_{\sigma,T}}(\omega)=\int_0^Te^{-(\sigma+i\omega)t}f(t)\,dt$; this approaches $F(\sigma+i\omega)$ in the <Laplace convergence region>. Setting $\sigma=0$ directly requires an integrable zero-extended input: the input $1$ fails that condition, even though its <Laplace transform> exists for every $\sigma>0$.

The history of the <Laplace transform> spans several stages. Schiff's historical summary notes that Laplace's 1812 treatise acknowledged earlier work on <integral transforms> and describes later development of the modern <Laplace transform> method. https://oldversion.stu.edu.vn/uploads/documents/170509-091205.pdf#page=9[Schiff, The Laplace Transform: Theory and Applications, historical summary, pp. x–xi]{ref}.

= Boundedness selects a Laplace transform at a singular endpoint
{parent=Laplace transform}
{title2=$|Y(p)|\le\|y\|_\infty/p\quad(p>0)$}

A bounded <function> on $[0,\infty)$ has a <Laplace transform> satisfying the displayed bound, by integration of $|y(t)e^{-pt}|$. Consequently its <Laplace transform> cannot have a pole of order two or higher at $p=0$. This can eliminate the arbitrary constant introduced when multiplication by $t$ turns an <ordinary differential equation> into a first-order <differential equation> for its <Laplace transform>.

For example, transforming $ty''+(t-1)y'-y=0$ with $y(0)=1$ gives $p(p+1)Y'+(3p+2)Y=2$. An <integrating factor> $p^2(p+1)$ yields
$$
Y(p)=\frac1{p+1}+\frac{C}{p^2(p+1)}.
$$
The bound forces $C=0$, selecting $y=e^{-t}$. This selection can be justified without assuming unproved endpoint regularity for every solution: $e^{-t}$ and $t-1$ have nonzero <Wronskian> $te^{-t}$ on $(0,\infty)$, so they form a <fundamental system of solutions>. Boundedness removes $t-1$, and the limit at zero fixes the remaining coefficient. The endpoint limit by itself does not select a unique solution.

= Uniqueness of the Laplace transform
{parent=Laplace transform}

If two piecewise continuous <functions> of <exponential order> on $[0,\infty)$ have the same unilateral <Laplace transform> for every sufficiently large positive real parameter, they agree at every common point of continuity and agree almost everywhere. For continuous <functions>, the equality holds everywhere. Values at an isolated discontinuity cannot be recovered from an ordinary <integral transform>.

= Laplace transform of the Bessel function J0
{c}
{parent=Laplace transform}
{title2=$\mathcal L[J_0](p)=(1+p^2)^{-1/2}$}

For real $p>0$, the <Laplace transform> $G$ of the order-zero <Bessel function of the first kind> satisfies $(1+p^2)G'+pG=0$. This follows by transforming $xJ_0''+J_0'+xJ_0=0$, using $\mathcal L[xg]=-\partial_p\mathcal L[g]$ and $J_0(0)=1$, $J_0'(0)=0$. Thus $G=C/\sqrt{1+p^2}$; the <initial value theorem for Laplace transforms> gives $pG(p)\to1$ and fixes $C=1$.

= Initial value theorem for Laplace transforms
{parent=Laplace transform}
{title2=$f(0+)=\lim_{p\to+\infty}p\mathcal Lf(p)$}

For a <function> continuous at zero and of exponential order, the displayed limit recovers its initial value from its <Laplace transform>. Substitute $u=pt$ to obtain $p\mathcal Lf(p)=\int_0^\infty e^{-u}f(u/p)\,du$ and apply the <dominated convergence theorem>; an exponential-order bound controls the tail. The limit along positive real $p$ suffices for this version.

= Exponential order
{parent=Laplace transform}
{title2=$|f(t)|\leq Me^{at}\quad(t\geq T)$}

A <function> has exponential order $a\in\mathbb R$ if some $M>0$ and $T\geq0$ give the displayed bound. Bounded <functions>, including <sine>, have exponential order zero; $e^{-2t}$ has exponential order $-2$. The bound need not use the smallest possible $a$. Together with <piecewise continuity>, it guarantees a <Laplace transform> for $\operatorname{Re}s>a$.

= Region of absolute convergence of a Laplace transform
{parent=Laplace transform}
{title2=$\int_0^\infty e^{-\sigma t}|f(t)|\,dt<\infty$}

= Laplace convergence region
{c}
{synonym}

The <set> of <complex numbers> $s$ for which the displayed <integral> is finite is the region of absolute convergence of a <Laplace transform>. It depends only on the <real part> $\sigma=\operatorname{Re}s$. Once the condition holds for one $\sigma$, it holds for every larger <real part>. For the <exponential function> $e^{-2t}$ it is exactly $\operatorname{Re}s>-2$; in general, convergence on the boundary must be checked separately. An <analytic continuation> outside this <set> need not equal a convergent defining <integral>.

= Causal extension by zero
{parent=Laplace transform}
{title2=$f_0(t)=f(t)\ (t\geq0),\quad f_0(t)=0\ (t<0)$}

Extend a <function> given for nonnegative time to the <real line> by setting it to zero before the starting time. This turns unilateral <convolution> into whole-line <convolution> and identifies a <Laplace transform> slice with a <Fourier transform> after <exponential function> weighting.

= Ramanujan's function
{parent=Laplace transform}
{c}
{title2=$N(t)=\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-tx}}{x(\pi^2+\log^2x)}dx$}

For $t>0$, this <Laplace transform> converges at zero because $dx/[x\log^2x]$ is integrable, and at infinity because of exponential damping. Its large-$t$ behavior is governed by a logarithmic zero-endpoint singularity and begins with $1/\log t$, rather than a power of $t$.

= Logarithmic expansion of Ramanujan's function
{parent=Ramanujan's function}
{title2=$N(t)\sim\sum_{n\ge0}c_n(\log t)^{-n-1},\quad\sum_{n\ge0}c_nz^n/n!=1/\Gamma(1-z)$}

The <Gamma reflection formula> gives $(\sin\pi z/\pi)\Gamma(z)=1/\Gamma(1-z)$, analytic at zero with value one. A finite-parameter <Watson's lemma> representation of <Ramanujan's function> therefore gives the displayed coefficients. In particular $c_0=1$, $c_1=-\gamma$ and $c_2=\gamma^2-\pi^2/6$, with $\gamma$ <Euler's constant>. Obtaining this representation requires taking an imaginary part before sending its Mellin exponent to zero; the original complex <integral> is divergent at that boundary.

= Newman Tauberian theorem
{c}
{parent=Laplace transform}
{title2=$\int_0^\infty f(t)\,dt=F(0)$}

For bounded locally integrable $f$ on the nonnegative real line, suppose its <Laplace transform> $F$ extends holomorphically to an open neighborhood of the closed right half-plane. Then the ordinary improper integral converges to $F(0)$. <Contour damping for bounded Laplace transforms> bounds the difference from the finite-interval transform by $2\|f\|_\infty/R$ in the limit for every radius $R$. Extension merely to the open right half-plane is insufficient, as the constant function and transform $1/z$ show.

= Contour damping for bounded Laplace transforms
{parent=Newman Tauberian theorem}
{title2=$e^{Tz}(1+z^2/R^2)/z$}

The displayed kernel has residue one at zero and vanishes at the endpoints of a radius-$R$ imaginary-axis segment. On either radius-$R$ semicircle, $|1+z^2/R^2|=2|\operatorname{Re}z|/R$, cancelling the inverse real-part bound on a bounded function's <Laplace transform> tail. Use the full transform only on a right semicircle and a thin analytic leftward path; deform the finite-interval entire transform to the large left semicircle. This proves the <Newman Tauberian theorem> without assuming analytic continuation across an entire left half-disk.

= Convolution theorem for Laplace transforms
{parent=Laplace transform}
{title2=$\mathcal L(f*g)=\mathcal Lf\,\mathcal Lg$}

= Laplace transform convolution theorem
{c}
{synonym}

For <functions> $f,g$ on $[0,\infty)$ with $e^{-\sigma t}f(t)$ and $e^{-\sigma t}g(t)$ <Lebesgue integrable functions>, their unilateral <convolution>, defined <almost everywhere> if necessary, satisfies
$$
\mathcal L\{f*g\}(s)=F(s)G(s),\qquad (f*g)(t)=\int_0^tf(\tau)g(t-\tau)\,d\tau,\qquad \operatorname{Re}s=\sigma.
$$
Taking a <causal extension by zero> of both <functions> gives the existing whole-line <convolution> definition. <Absolute convergence> and <Fubini's theorem> justify the product rule: put $t=\tau+u$ in the double <integral>, so $e^{-st}=e^{-s\tau}e^{-su}$. For <piecewise continuous functions> of <exponential order> $a$ and $b$, the hypotheses hold for $\operatorname{Re}s>\max(a,b)$. The <Laplace transform> article works out the concrete example $h(t)=e^{-2t}$ and the <Heaviside step function> input, whose output is $(1-e^{-2t})/2$. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03sc-differential-equations-fall-2011/0d4d90053f2bb24f7551c78e2d1b21a2_MIT18_03SCF11_s30_5text.pdf[MIT 18.03SC, convolution and system response]{ref}.

= Bromwich inversion formula
{c}
{parent=Laplace transform}

Under suitable regularity and growth conditions, a function with <Laplace transform> $F$ is recovered by the inverse contour integral
$$
f(t)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma-i\infty}^{\gamma+i\infty}e^{st}F(s)\,ds,
$$
where the vertical line lies to the right of every singularity of $F$.

= Causality in finite-time Laplace contour inversion
{parent=Bromwich inversion formula}
{title2=$e^{pt}G_T(p)$}

A <Laplace transform> $G_T(p)=\int_0^Te^{-ps}g(s)ds$ can grow exponentially as $\operatorname{Re}p\to-\infty$. At an evaluation time $t<T$, multiplying by $e^{pt}$ does not automatically permit closing the inverse contour to the left. Either retain the growth-bearing contour pieces, use a causal time convolution, or justify a deformation with the time-truncated transform. Resolvent-pole information alone is insufficient.

= Long-time asymptotics from a Laplace transform
{parent=Laplace transform}

Terms singular at $s=0$ in a <Laplace transform> encode persistent late-time behaviour. In particular,
$$
F(s)=\frac{A}{s^2}+\frac{B}{s}+O(1)
$$
contributes $At+B$ to the inverse transform; singularities farther into the left half-plane contribute decaying terms under the usual analyticity and growth assumptions.

= Laplace transform of a derivative
{parent=Laplace transform}
{c}
{title2=$\mathcal L\{f'\}(s)=sF(s)-f(0+)$}

Suppose $f$ is an <absolutely continuous function> on every finite <interval> $[0,T]$, both $e^{-st}f(t)$ and $e^{-st}f'(t)$ are <Lebesgue integrable functions>, and $e^{-sT}f(T)\to0$. Then <integration by parts> on $[0,T]$ followed by a <limit> gives
$$
\int_0^T e^{-st}f'(t)\,dt=e^{-sT}f(T)-f(0+)+s\int_0^Te^{-st}f(t)\,dt,\qquad \mathcal L\{f'\}(s)=sF(s)-f(0+).
$$
Here $f(0+)$ is the right <one-sided limit>. <Exponential order> bounds on $f$ and $f'$ suffice when $\operatorname{Re}s$ exceeds both bounds. For $f(t)=e^{-2t}$, the identity reads $-2/(s+2)=s/(s+2)-1$ on $\operatorname{Re}s>-2$. This is the ordinary <derivative> on nonnegative time; differentiating a <causal extension by zero> at its jump instead introduces a <Dirac delta function>. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03sc-differential-equations-fall-2011/22aba4126352ce0f76930d858d8dffa5_MIT18_03SCF11_s29_1text.pdf[MIT 18.03SC, derivative rules and initial data]{ref}.

= Matrix-valued Laplace transform
{parent=Laplace transform}

The Laplace transform of a matrix-valued function is obtained by transforming each entry. A constant matrix may be taken outside on the corresponding side of the integral; in particular, $\mathcal L\{AB\}=A\mathcal L\{B\}$ when $A$ is constant.

= Laplace-transform solution of a constant-coefficient vector ODE
{parent=Matrix-valued Laplace transform}

For
$$
y'=Ay+g,qquad y(0)=y_0,
$$
the derivative rule gives
$$
(sI-A)Y(s)=y_0+G(s),
$$
and hence $Y(s)=(sI-A)^{-1}(y_0+G(s))$ wherever the transforms converge and $sI-A$ is invertible.
