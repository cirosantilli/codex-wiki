= Solution
{parent=i}
{c}

Let $V(t)$ have a boundary moving with <velocity> $\mathbf v_b$, and let $\mathbf n$ be its outward unit <normal vector>. For a smooth scalar field $q$, the <Reynolds transport theorem> is
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V(t)}q\,dV
=\int_{V(t)}\partial_tq\,dV
+\int_{\partial V(t)}q\mathbf v_b\cdot\mathbf n\,dS
=\int_{V(t)}[\partial_tq+\nabla\cdot(q\mathbf v_b)]\,dV.
$$
In the last expression, extend $\mathbf v_b$ smoothly into $V(t)$ and use the <divergence theorem>. For a <material volume>, its boundary moves with the fluid <velocity> $\mathbf u$, so $\mathbf v_b=\mathbf u$. Equivalently, the <Reynolds transport theorem> for a <material volume> reads
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V(t)}q\,dV
=\int_{V(t)}\left(\frac{Dq}{Dt}+q\nabla\cdot\mathbf u\right)dV,
\qquad \frac D{Dt}=\partial_t+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla,
$$
where $D/Dt$ is the <material derivative>.

For an arbitrary moving region, <mass conservation> includes the relative <mass flow rate> across its boundary:
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V(t)}\rho\,dV
=-\int_{\partial V(t)}\rho(\mathbf u-\mathbf v_b)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS.
$$
Substitution of the <Reynolds transport theorem> and the <divergence theorem> gives
$$
\int_{V(t)}[\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)]\,dV=0.
$$
Since the region is arbitrary, a continuous integrand must vanish: if it had a positive or negative value at a point, a sufficiently small surrounding region would have an <integral> of the same sign. This gives the <continuity equation>. With constant positive <mass density> $\rho$, it reduces to
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0.}
$$
Thus **the flow is incompressible**. For a <material volume>, the same argument starts directly from conservation of its total <mass>, with zero relative boundary flux.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

At an oriented internal surface with unit <normal vector> $\mathbf n$, the <traction> $\mathbf t(\mathbf x,t;\mathbf n)$ is the limiting contact <force> per unit <surface area> exerted by the material on the positive-$\mathbf n$ side on the material on the negative-$\mathbf n$ side:
$$
\mathbf t(\mathbf n)=\lim_{\Delta A\to0}\frac{\Delta\mathbf F}{\Delta A}.
$$
For a boundary of a chosen <material volume>, this convention gives the external <traction> when $\mathbf n$ is outward. The <Cauchy stress tensor> has components
$$
\sigma_{ij}=\mathbf e_i\cdot\mathbf t(\mathbf e_j),
$$
so the second index specifies the surface orientation and the first specifies the <force> component. The vectors $\mathbf e_i$ form the standard <Cartesian coordinate system> basis of <unit vectors>.

<Cauchy's stress theorem> states that the dependence of <traction> on the <normal vector> is linear:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf t(\mathbf n)=\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n,
\qquad t_i=\sigma_{ij}n_j.}
$$
Repeated indices are summed according to <Einstein notation>. In particular, reversing the <normal vector> reverses the <traction>, and knowing the <tractions> on three mutually perpendicular coordinate planes determines the <traction> on every plane. Symmetry of the <Cauchy stress tensor>, for an ordinary fluid without body couples, follows separately from <angular momentum conservation>.

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

For an arbitrary <material volume> $V(t)$, <Newton's second law> equates the rate of change of <linear momentum> to the resultant contact <force>, since there is no <body force>:
$$
\frac d{dt}\int_{V(t)}\rho u_i\,dV
=\int_{\partial V(t)}t_i(\mathbf n)\,dS
=\int_{\partial V(t)}\sigma_{ij}n_j\,dS.
$$
Use the <Reynolds transport theorem> on the left. Expanding the product and using the <continuity equation> leaves
$$
\partial_t(\rho u_i)+\partial_j(\rho u_i u_j)
=\rho(\partial_tu_i+u_j\partial_ju_i)
+u_i[\partial_t\rho+\partial_j(\rho u_j)]
=\rho\frac{Du_i}{Dt}.
$$
On the right, the <divergence theorem> applied to each row of the <Cauchy stress tensor> gives
$$
\int_{\partial V(t)}\sigma_{ij}n_j\,dS
=\int_{V(t)}\partial_j\sigma_{ij}\,dV.
$$
Consequently the <volume integral> of $\rho Du_i/Dt-\partial_j\sigma_{ij}$ vanishes on every <material volume>. Localizing as in part (i) gives the <Cauchy momentum equation>:
$$
\boxed{\rho\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf u}{\partial t}
+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\right)
=\mathbf e_i\frac{\partial\sigma_{ij}}{\partial x_j}.}
$$
The left side is <mass density> times <material acceleration>; the right side is the <divergence> of the <Cauchy stress tensor>.

= Solution

For an isotropic <Newtonian fluid> in <incompressible flow>, the <constitutive equation> is
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{ij}=-p\delta_{ij}+2\mu E_{ij},
\qquad E_{ij}=\frac12(\partial_j u_i+\partial_i u_j).}
$$
Here $p$ is <pressure>, $\delta_{ij}$ is the <Kronecker delta>, $\mu$ is the constant <dynamic viscosity>, and $E$ is the <strain-rate tensor>. The more general compressible <constitutive equation> can include a term proportional to $(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)\delta_{ij}$; that term vanishes here.

Differentiate the <Cauchy stress tensor>, retaining the constant <dynamic viscosity> outside the <derivatives>:
$$
\partial_j\sigma_{ij}
=-\partial_i p+\mu\partial_j\partial_j u_i
+\mu\partial_i(\partial_j u_j)
=-\partial_i p+\mu\nabla^2u_i.
$$
The last equality uses <incompressibility>. Substitution into the <Cauchy momentum equation> gives the <Navier-Stokes equation>
$$
\boxed{\rho\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf u}{\partial t}
+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\right)
=-\nabla p+\mu\nabla^2\mathbf u.}
$$
The <pressure gradient> and the <Laplacian> of the <velocity field> therefore give the two contact <force> densities in this model.

For the <unidirectional flow> $\mathbf u=(0,0,w)$, <incompressibility> gives $\partial_z w=0$. Hence $w=w(x,y,t)$, and the <convective acceleration> $w\partial_z w$ vanishes. The transverse components of the <Navier-Stokes equation> give $p_x=p_y=0$, while the axial component gives
$$
\rho\partial_t w=G+\mu(w_{xx}+w_{yy}).
$$
**A steady-flow assumption is implicit in the question.** Neither <unidirectional flow> nor a time-independent <pressure gradient> alone eliminates $\partial_t w$: a decaying transient can be superposed on the final pipe profile. The native PDF does not explicitly say <steady flow> in this part. For the intended <steady flow>, $w_t=0$, and therefore
$$
\boxed{w_{xx}+w_{yy}=-\frac G\mu.}
$$
Taking the pipe walls to be stationary, the <no-slip boundary condition> is
$$
\boxed{w(\pm a,y)=0\quad(-b\leq y\leq b),\qquad
w(x,\pm b)=0\quad(-a\leq x\leq a).}
$$
The <no-penetration boundary condition> is already satisfied by the assumed <velocity field>. This is the <Poisson equation> defining <rectangular duct flow>.

= Solution

For a profile depending only on $y$, the <Poisson equation> becomes $w_1''=-G/\mu$. Integrating twice gives
$$
w_1(y)=-\frac{G}{2\mu}y^2+C_1y+C_2.
$$
The <no-slip boundary conditions> $w_1(b)=w_1(-b)=0$ imply $C_1=0$ and $C_2=Gb^2/(2\mu)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{w_1(y)=\frac{G}{2\mu}(b^2-y^2).}
$$
This is <plane Poiseuille flow> between the two horizontal walls. It is positive in the interior for $G>0$, so the <velocity> points down the <pressure gradient>. It does not satisfy the side-wall <no-slip boundary conditions>, which is precisely what the correction in part (iii) repairs.

= iii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

Subtract the particular <plane Poiseuille flow> profile from the full <rectangular duct flow>. The remainder is a <harmonic function>:
$$
\begin{gathered}
w_2=w-w_1,\qquad w_{2,xx}+w_{2,yy}=0,\\
w_2(x,\pm b)=0,\qquad
w_2(\pm a,y)=-\frac G{2\mu}(b^2-y^2).
\end{gathered}
$$
The data are even in $x$ and $y$. The <boundary value problem> is unique: if two solutions differed by $v$, <Green's first identity> and the zero <Dirichlet boundary condition> would give
$$
\int_{-a}^{a}\int_{-b}^{b}|\nabla v|^2\,dy\,dx
=\int_{\partial\Omega}v\partial_n v\,ds-\int_\Omega v\nabla^2v\,dA=0.
$$
Thus $v$ is constant, and its zero boundary values force $v=0$. Reflection of a solution in either coordinate axis therefore gives the same solution. We may seek even separated <eigenfunctions>.

Use <separation of variables> with
$$
k_n=\frac{(2n+1)\pi}{2b},\qquad
w_2(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A_n
\frac{\cosh(k_nx)}{\cosh(k_na)}\cos(k_ny).
$$
Each summand solves the <Laplace equation>, vanishes at $y=\pm b$, and has the required evenness. The <hyperbolic cosine> ratio equals one at either side wall. The functions $\cos(k_ny)$ have <orthogonality> relation
$$
\int_{-b}^{b}\cos(k_ny)\cos(k_my)\,dy
=\begin{cases}b,&m=n,\\0,&m\ne n.\end{cases}
$$
They are the even <Dirichlet eigenfunctions> on $[-b,b]$, so their <Fourier series> represents the even boundary data. Projecting that data onto the $n$th <eigenfunction> yields the <Fourier coefficient>
$$
A_n=-\frac{G}{2\mu b}\int_{-b}^{b}(b^2-y^2)\cos(k_ny)\,dy
=-\frac{16Gb^2}{\mu\pi^3}\frac{(-1)^n}{(2n+1)^3}.
$$
For completeness, two <integrations by parts> give the <quadratic arch expansion in half-integer cosine modes>: the displayed <integral> is $4(-1)^n/k_n^3$, agreeing with the identity supplied in the paper.

Adding the particular solution gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
w(x,y)&=\frac{G}{2\mu}(b^2-y^2)\\
&\quad-\frac{16Gb^2}{\mu\pi^3}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
\frac{(-1)^n}{(2n+1)^3}
\frac{\cosh(k_nx)}{\cosh(k_na)}\cos(k_ny),\\
k_n&=\frac{(2n+1)\pi}{2b}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The coefficients are absolutely summable and the <hyperbolic cosine> ratio is at most one throughout the closed rectangle, giving <uniform convergence> and the prescribed boundary values. On compact subsets of the interior, the <hyperbolic cosine> ratio supplies exponential decay in $n$, justifying differentiation of the <series> and the <Laplace equation>. The side-wall correction slows the <plane Poiseuille flow> enough to satisfy all four <no-slip boundary conditions>.

= Solution
{parent=iv}
{c}

The <volumetric flow rate> is the <integral> of axial <velocity> over the cross-section:
$$
Q=\int_{-a}^{a}\int_{-b}^{b}w(x,y)\,dy\,dx.
$$
The <plane Poiseuille flow> contribution is
$$
\int_{-a}^{a}\int_{-b}^{b}\frac{G}{2\mu}(b^2-y^2)\,dy\,dx
=\frac{4ab^3G}{3\mu}.
$$
For each correction <eigenfunction>,
$$
\int_{-a}^{a}\frac{\cosh(k_nx)}{\cosh(k_na)}\,dx
=\frac{2\tanh(k_na)}{k_n},\qquad
\int_{-b}^{b}\cos(k_ny)\,dy=\frac{2(-1)^n}{k_n}.
$$
<Uniform convergence> permits <termwise integration>. Multiplying these two <integrals> by $A_n$ gives
$$
A_n\frac{4(-1)^n}{k_n^2}\tanh(k_na)
=-\frac{256Gb^4}{\mu\pi^5}
\frac{\tanh(k_na)}{(2n+1)^5}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{Q=\frac{4ab^3G}{3\mu}
-\frac{256b^4G}{\pi^5\mu}
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2n+1)^5}
\tanh\!\left(\frac{(2n+1)\pi a}{2b}\right).}
$$
Every summand in the subtracted quantity is positive. Thus **the side walls reduce the flux** below the infinite-width <plane Poiseuille flow> prediction. Its dimensions are <volume> per unit <time>, as required for a <volumetric flow rate>.

For the proposed <velocity field>, $u_x+w_z=1-1=0$, so <incompressibility> holds. Its <convective acceleration> is
$$
u u_x+w u_z=x,\qquad u w_x+w w_z=z,
$$
and both components have zero <Laplacian>. The <Navier-Stokes equation> therefore requires $p_x=-x$ and $p_z=-z$. Hence
$$
\boxed{p(x,z)=p_* -\frac12(x^2+z^2),}
$$
where $p_*$ is an arbitrary constant reference <pressure>. The same <pressure gradient> balances the same <convective acceleration> in the <Euler equations for an inviscid fluid>. Since the viscous terms vanish identically, this is an exact solution for every positive <Reynolds number>, as well as for the inviscid limit.

At $z=0$, the normal <velocity> is zero, and the prescribed dimensionless tangential <traction> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf e_1\cdot\mathbf t(\mathbf e_3)=u_z+w_x=0.}
$$
This <stagnation point> flow therefore has **zero surface shear stress**, although its <strain-rate tensor> and its viscous <normal stresses> are nonzero.

The held-flat interface imposes the <no-penetration boundary condition>, while its specified tangential <traction> gives a <shear stress> condition:
$$
\boxed{w(x,0)=0,\qquad u_z(x,0)+w_x(x,0)=\beta x.}
$$
A differentiable boundary value $w(x,0)\equiv0$ implies $w_x(x,0)=0$, so the second <boundary condition> is also $u_z(x,0)=\beta x$. There is no imposed <no-slip boundary condition> at this interface; tangential <velocity> is determined by the <shear stress> and the fluid equations.

The solution in part (a) has zero tangential <traction>, and so remains valid for the whole interface **if and only if $\beta=0$**. At the single point $x=0$ the imposed <shear stress> vanishes for any $\beta$, but this does not satisfy a <boundary condition> that must hold for every $x$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

In a thin <boundary layer>, take streamwise <velocity> and distance to be order one, set $z=\delta\zeta$, and use <incompressibility> to set $w=\delta W$. The streamwise <convective acceleration> is order one, whereas its dominant viscous contribution after division by the <Reynolds number> is $\mathrm{Re}^{-1}\delta^{-2}u_{\zeta\zeta}$. Their <dominant balance> selects
$$
\boxed{\delta=\mathrm{Re}^{-1/2}.}
$$
With this choice, the exact rescaled <Navier-Stokes equations> are
$$
\begin{aligned}
u_x+W_\zeta&=0,\\
u u_x+W u_\zeta&=-p_x+u_{\zeta\zeta}+\delta^2u_{xx},\\
\delta^2(uW_x+WW_\zeta)&=-p_\zeta+\delta^2W_{\zeta\zeta}+\delta^4W_{xx}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the leading <pressure> is independent of $\zeta$. Matching it to the <outer solution> from part (a), evaluated near $z=0$, gives $p=p_*-x^2/2$, hence $-p_x=x$. The leading <boundary-layer equation> is therefore
$$
\boxed{u_x+W_\zeta=0,\qquad
u u_x+W u_\zeta=x+u_{\zeta\zeta},\qquad \zeta<0.}
$$
The exact surface <shear stress> condition rescales to $u_\zeta+\delta^2W_x=\delta\beta x=\gamma x$. Since $W(x,0)=0$ for all $x$, its tangential <derivative> also vanishes there. The leading surface <boundary conditions> are
$$
\boxed{W(x,0)=0,\qquad u_\zeta(x,0)=\gamma x.}
$$
Matching to the <outer solution> requires
$$
u(x,\zeta)\longrightarrow x\quad(\zeta\to-\infty),
\qquad W(x,\zeta)\sim-\zeta\quad(\zeta\to-\infty).
$$
The second matching condition specifies the leading growth of $W$, allowing a constant displacement correction. Requiring $W+\zeta\to0$ would impose an extra condition not supplied by the leading <outer solution>. This is the <stress-driven stagnation-point boundary layer>.

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

With $u=xf'(\zeta)$ and $W=-f(\zeta)$, <incompressibility> is automatic because $u_x=f'$ and $W_\zeta=-f'$. In the streamwise <boundary-layer equation>,
$$
u u_x+W u_\zeta=x(f')^2-xff'',\qquad
u_{\zeta\zeta}=xf'''.
$$
Equating coefficients of $x$ gives
$$
(f')^2-ff''=1+f''',\qquad
\boxed{f'''+ff''+1-(f')^2=0.}
$$
This coefficient comparison determines the equation even though the streamwise equation vanishes identically at the <stagnation point> $x=0$.

The <no-penetration boundary condition> gives $f(0)=0$, the surface <shear stress> gives $xf''(0)=\gamma x$, and matching the tangential <velocity> to the <outer solution> gives $f'\to1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{f(0)=0,\qquad f''(0)=\gamma,\qquad
f'(\zeta)\longrightarrow1\quad(\zeta\to-\infty).}
$$
If the correction $f'-1$ is integrable at the far end, then $f=\zeta+O(1)$ there, which is consistent with $W\sim-\zeta$. The small-<shear stress> solution below has precisely this property.

= Solution

At zero surface <shear stress>, the solution connected to part (a) is
$$
\boxed{f_0(\zeta)=\zeta.}
$$
Indeed, it has $f_0'=1$, $f_0''=f_0'''=0$ and $f_0(0)=0$. Insert the <regular perturbation> $f=\zeta+\gamma f_1+O(\gamma^2)$ into the nonlinear <ordinary differential equation>. The terms are
$$
f'''=\gamma f_1'''+O(\gamma^2),\qquad
ff''=\gamma\zeta f_1''+O(\gamma^2),\qquad
1-(f')^2=-2\gamma f_1'+O(\gamma^2).
$$
The coefficient of $\gamma$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{f_1'''+\zeta f_1''-2f_1'=0.}
$$
Expanding the surface <boundary conditions> and the far-field matching condition gives
$$
\boxed{f_1(0)=0,\qquad f_1''(0)=1,\qquad
f_1'(\zeta)\longrightarrow0\quad(\zeta\to-\infty).}
$$
**The printed $+\infty$ limit is a sign error.** It is present in the native PDF on paper page 3, not introduced by transcription. The fluid occupies $z<0$, the <inner variable> is $\zeta=z/\delta<0$, and part (b)(iii) correctly matches at $-\infty$. We retain that physical domain throughout. A change to the positive coordinate $\eta=-\zeta$ would also change the differential equation and surface <derivative> signs; it cannot be made by changing only the far-field sign.

The inequality $\delta^2\ll\gamma$ ensures that effects discarded at order $\delta^2$ in the <boundary-layer scaling> are smaller than the retained order-$\gamma$ perturbation. The inequality $\gamma\ll1$ justifies this <regular perturbation>. The two conditions control different approximations.

Set $g=f_1'$. The <boundary value problem> becomes
$$
\boxed{g''+\zeta g'-2g=0,\qquad g'(0)=1,\qquad
g(\zeta)\longrightarrow0\quad(\zeta\to-\infty).}
$$
For $y_1=1+\zeta^2$, direct substitution gives $y_1''+\zeta y_1'-2y_1=2+2\zeta^2-2(1+\zeta^2)=0$. This growing <polynomial> solution is useful for <reduction of order>.

Write $g=y_1v$. Substitution into the <linear ordinary differential equation> cancels the terms proportional to $v$ and gives
$$
y_1v''+(2y_1'+\zeta y_1)v'=0.
$$
An <integrating factor> for this first-order equation for $v'$ yields
$$
v'(\zeta)=C\frac{e^{-\zeta^2/2}}{(1+\zeta^2)^2}.
$$
The far-field <boundary condition> selects the convergent <integral> from $-\infty$:
$$
g(\zeta)=C(1+\zeta^2)\int_{-\infty}^{\zeta}
\frac{e^{-s^2/2}}{(1+s^2)^2}\,ds.
$$
Any additional multiple of $1+\zeta^2$ would grow at $-\infty$. The chosen expression tends to zero: for $r=-\zeta\geq1$,
$$
0\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\zeta}\frac{e^{-s^2/2}}{(1+s^2)^2}\,ds
=\int_r^\infty\frac{e^{-t^2/2}}{(1+t^2)^2}\,dt
\leq\frac{e^{-r^2/2}}{r(1+r^2)^2}.
$$
Here $(1+t^2)^{-2}\leq(1+r^2)^{-2}$ and $\int_r^\infty e^{-t^2/2}\,dt\leq r^{-1}\int_r^\infty t e^{-t^2/2}\,dt$. Thus the far-field <boundary condition> is verified without a cancellation of large terms.

At $\zeta=0$, differentiating gives $g'(0)=C$, so $C=1$. Using the antiderivative supplied in the paper gives the explicit answer
$$
\boxed{g(\zeta)=\frac{\zeta}{2}e^{-\zeta^2/2}
+\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}4(1+\zeta^2)
\left[1+\operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt2}\right)\right],
\qquad \zeta\leq0.}
$$
Here $\operatorname{erf}$ is the <error function>. Finally,
$$
f_1(\zeta)=\int_0^{\zeta}g(s)\,ds
$$
recovers the unique correction satisfying $f_1(0)=0$. The decay bound also shows that $f_1$ approaches a finite constant at $-\infty$, as allowed by the normal <velocity> matching. This is the <weak surface-shear perturbation of stagnation flow>.

If the printed $+\infty$ condition is instead imposed literally on a positive half-line, <reduction of order> gives
$$
g_+(\zeta)=-(1+\zeta^2)\int_{\zeta}^{\infty}
\frac{e^{-s^2/2}}{(1+s^2)^2}\,ds,
\qquad g_+(0)=-\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}4.
$$
It satisfies $g_+'(0)=1$ but grows as $\zeta\to-\infty$, so it cannot describe the fluid domain specified in this paper. This makes the consequence of the printed sign error explicit.

At the interface, the tangential <velocity> is $u(x,0)=xf'(0)$ and the normal <velocity> remains zero. The preceding <error function> expression gives
$$
g(0)=f_1'(0)=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}4=\sqrt{\frac\pi8}.
$$
Hence, within the leading <boundary layer> model,
$$
\boxed{u(x,0)=x\left[1+\sqrt{\frac\pi8}\,\gamma+O(\gamma^2)\right],
\qquad w(x,0)=0.}
$$
Allowing the formally discarded finite-<Reynolds number> terms gives an additional order-$\delta^2$ error, smaller than the order-$\gamma$ correction under the assumed ordering. The coefficient is $\sqrt{\pi/8}\approx0.626657$.

Thus **positive surface shear increases the outward surface speed linearly**: for $x>0$ it increases $u$, and for $x<0$ it makes $u$ more negative. The <stagnation point> remains at $x=0$. A negative coefficient would result from using the printed $+\infty$ condition literally, and would describe the different half-line problem identified in part (ii).

= Solution

The flat plate moves tangentially and the curved plate is stationary. The <no-slip boundary condition> and <no-penetration boundary condition> give
$$
\boxed{u=-U,\quad w=0\quad(z=0),\qquad
u=0,\quad w=0\quad(z=h(x)).}
$$
At the curved plate, impermeability can also be written $w=u h_x$; together with zero tangential <velocity>, this gives both components zero. The native PDF writes $v'=0$ in the scaled wall conditions although the normal component is defined as $w'$. We use $w'$ consistently.

The <parabolic lubrication gap> changes by order $h_0$ when $x^2/R$ is order $h_0$. Its natural horizontal scale is therefore
$$
\boxed{L=\sqrt{Rh_0},\qquad \delta=\frac{h_0}{L}=\sqrt{\frac{h_0}{R}}\ll1.}
$$
The proposed normal <velocity> scale $h_0U/L$ then makes the two terms of the <continuity equation> comparable. Balancing the horizontal <pressure gradient> against the transverse viscous term in the <Navier-Stokes equation> requires $P/L=\mu U/h_0^2$, so
$$
\boxed{P=\frac{\mu UL}{h_0^2}.}
$$
Here <pressure> is measured from a common reference value, which is chosen at the outer fluid in part (b).

For clarity, define the dimensionless <material derivative>
$$
\mathcal D'=\partial_{t'}+u'\partial_{x'}+w'\partial_{z'},
\qquad \mathrm{Re}=\frac{\rho LU}{\mu}.
$$
Substitution of all the scalings gives the exact dimensionless equations
$$
\begin{aligned}
u'_{x'}+w'_{z'}&=0,\\
\delta^2\mathrm{Re}\,\mathcal D'u'&=-p'_{x'}+u'_{z'z'}+\delta^2u'_{x'x'},\\
\delta^4\mathrm{Re}\,\mathcal D'w'&=-p'_{z'}+\delta^2w'_{z'z'}+\delta^4w'_{x'x'}.
\end{aligned}
$$
For example, the factor in the last left side is obtained by dividing the vertical <acceleration> scale $\rho h_0U^2/L^2$ by the vertical <pressure gradient> scale $P/h_0$.

Taking $\delta\ll1$ and $\delta^2\mathrm{Re}\ll1$ leaves the <lubrication approximation>
$$
\boxed{u'_{x'}+w'_{z'}=0,\qquad
0=-p'_{x'}+u'_{z'z'},\qquad 0=-p'_{z'}.}
$$
The scaled gap and <boundary conditions> are
$$
\boxed{h'(x')=1+\frac{x'^2}{2},\qquad
(u',w')=(-1,0)\ (z'=0),\qquad
(u',w')=(0,0)\ (z'=h'(x')).}
$$
The <Reynolds number> itself need not be small: it is $\delta^2\mathrm{Re}$ that measures inertia relative to transverse viscous resistance in this thin geometry.

= Solution

Write $H(x')=h'(x')$ and $p'_x=dp'/dx'$ below, so the subscript $x$ in this shorthand denotes differentiation with respect to $x'$. The vertical <pressure gradient> is zero, and integrating $u'_{z'z'}=p'_x$ twice with the wall <boundary conditions> gives the local <Couette-Poiseuille flow>
$$
\boxed{u'(x',z')=-1+\frac{z'}{H}
+\frac{p'_x}{2}z'(z'-H).}
$$
The first two terms are <Couette flow> due to the moving plate; the last is <plane Poiseuille flow> due to the local <pressure gradient>. Its dimensionless <volume flux per unit width> is
$$
q(x')=\int_0^{H(x')}u'(x',z')\,dz'
=-\frac H2-\frac{H^3}{12}\frac{dp'}{dx'}.
$$
Use the <Leibniz integral rule> and <incompressibility> to differentiate this <integral>:
$$
\frac{dq}{dx'}=\int_0^H u'_{x'}\,dz'+u'(x',H)\frac{dH}{dx'}
=-w'(x',H)+w'(x',0)=0.
$$
Thus **the flux is independent of position**, and
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dx'}\left(\frac{H^3}{12}\frac{dp'}{dx'}+\frac H2\right)=0.}
$$
The normal <velocity> can then be recovered from <incompressibility>:
$$
w'(x',z')=-\int_0^{z'}u'_{x'}(x',s)\,ds.
$$
It is zero on the lower wall by construction and zero on the upper wall by the constant-<volume flux> relation. This supplies the remaining component of the leading <velocity field>.

The endpoint <pressure> conditions are matching conditions, not an assertion that the <lubrication approximation> remains valid at arbitrarily large dimensional $x$. On an outer length scale $R$, a viscous <pressure> variation is of size $\mu U/R$, whose ratio to the inner scale is
$$
\frac{\mu U/R}{P}=\frac{h_0^2}{RL}=\delta^3\ll1.
$$
Even an outer inertial <pressure> scale $\rho U^2$ has ratio
$$
\frac{\rho U^2}{P}=\delta^2\mathrm{Re}\ll1.
$$
Consequently the gap <pressure> is much larger than either outer variation. Matching to fluid with a common ambient <pressure> at both ends, and taking that value as zero <gauge pressure>, gives
$$
\boxed{p'(x')\longrightarrow0\quad(x'\to\pm\infty).}
$$
There is an <overlap region> $1\ll|x'|\ll\delta^{-1}$ before the wall slope becomes order one. The infinite endpoints represent matching through that region. An externally imposed <pressure> difference comparable with $P$ would change these conditions and the resulting <volume flux>; equal outer <pressure> is the physical assumption being used here.

Let the constant in the integrated <Reynolds equation> be $C$:
$$
\frac{H^3}{12}\frac{dp'}{dx'}+\frac H2=C,
\qquad \frac{dp'}{dx'}=12C H^{-3}-6H^{-2},
\qquad H=1+\frac{x'^2}{2}.
$$
Both endpoint <pressures> are zero, so the <integral> of $dp'/dx'$ on the real line vanishes. Define
$$
I_m=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx'}{(1+x'^2/2)^m}.
$$
The substitution $x'=\sqrt2\tan\theta$ gives
$$
I_m=\sqrt2\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos^{2m-2}\theta\,d\theta,
\qquad I_2=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt2},\qquad
I_3=\frac{3\pi\sqrt2}{8}.
$$
The second value follows by integrating the supplied identity for $\cos^4\theta$; the nonconstant cosine terms have zero <integral> over this interval. Hence $12CI_3-6I_2=0$, giving
$$
\boxed{C=\frac{I_2}{2I_3}=\frac23,\qquad q=-C=-\frac23.}
$$
The <pressure gradient> is now
$$
\frac{dp'}{dx'}=\frac{8-6H}{H^3}
=\frac{2-3x'^2}{(1+x'^2/2)^3}.
$$
Direct differentiation shows
$$
\frac d{dx'}\left(\frac{2x'}{H^2}\right)
=\frac2{H^2}-\frac{4x'^2}{H^3}
=\frac{2-3x'^2}{H^3}.
$$
The candidate tends to zero at both ends, so the additive constant is zero and
$$
\boxed{p'(x')=\frac{2x'}{(1+x'^2/2)^2}.}
$$
Together with the local <Couette-Poiseuille flow> profile, this solves the <sliding-plate flow through a parabolic gap>.

To restore dimensions, integrate $u=Uu'$ across $dz=h_0\,dz'$. The signed dimensional <volume flux per unit width>, positive in the $+x$ direction, is
$$
\boxed{Q=Uh_0q=-\frac23Uh_0.}
$$
Thus the magnitude drawn in the direction of the moving plate is $2Uh_0/3$. For a plate width $B$ large enough to neglect side-wall effects, the total <volumetric flow rate> is $-2BUh_0/3$; without a specified width the answer is per unit width.

The <Couette flow> and <plane Poiseuille flow> contributions to the dimensional <volume flux per unit width> are respectively
$$
Q_C=-\frac{Uh(x)}2<0,\qquad
Q_P=-\frac{h(x)^3}{12\mu}\frac{dp}{dx}
=Uh_0\left(\frac H2-\frac23\right)
=Uh_0\left(\frac{x'^2}{4}-\frac16\right).
$$
They point in the same direction precisely when $Q_P<0$, giving
$$
\boxed{-\sqrt{\frac{2Rh_0}{3}}<x<\sqrt{\frac{2Rh_0}{3}}.}
$$
At the endpoints the <plane Poiseuille flow> contribution is zero. Outside that interval it opposes the <Couette flow> contribution, while their sum remains the constant negative <volume flux per unit width> $-2Uh_0/3$. Thus **pressure assists the moving plate near the narrowest gap** and drives a compensating return contribution farther away.
