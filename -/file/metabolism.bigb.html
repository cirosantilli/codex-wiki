= Metabolism
{wiki}

The network of <chemical reactions> that maintains a living system, supplies energy and builds or processes its material. Gas emissions can arise as <primary metabolic byproducts> or <secondary metabolic byproducts>, with different implications for an <atmospheric biosignature gas>.

<Enzymes> make this network operate on biologically useful timescales. Following material through successive <substrates> and <reaction products> explains why a single fast <enzyme> need not make the entire network fast: another step may constrain the flow. Changing <enzyme> amounts or activity therefore changes which routes can carry material, connecting molecular <chemical kinetics> to the maintenance and growth of a <biological cell>. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26911/[Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: multienzyme complexes and cell metabolism].

= Metabolic control analysis
{parent=Metabolism}

Quantitative analysis of how local changes in reaction capacities alter steady-state metabolic fluxes and concentrations. Its sensitivities depend on the operating condition rather than identifying one invariant limiting step.

= Flux summation theorem
{parent=Metabolic control analysis}

Under the scaling assumptions of <metabolic control analysis>, the complete set of independently scaled reaction-capacity <flux control coefficients> for one steady-state flux sums to one. Uniformly scaling all capacities scales the flux, and differentiation with respect to the scale factor gives the sum.

= Flux control coefficient
{parent=Metabolic control analysis}
{title2=$C_i^J$}

A local logarithmic sensitivity of a steady-state flux to a specified reaction capacity: $C_i^J=\partial\ln J/\partial\ln E_i$. It is not the fraction of flux physically passing through that step; a zero value does not make an essential step dispensable.

= Sulfate activation

= Fermentation
{parent=Metabolism}

In its biochemical sense, <fermentation> is <metabolism> that obtains <ATP> from breakdown of organic <substrates> without a respiratory electron transport chain. The alcoholic route is one example; not every <fermentation> produces <ethanol>. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/7-5-metabolism-without-oxygen[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 7.5: fermentation versus anaerobic respiration].

= Photosynthesis
{parent=Metabolism}
{wiki}

<Photosynthesis> captures <light> <energy> and transforms it into chemical forms that a <biological cell> can use. In <plants>, <algae> and <cyanobacteria>, this supports <carbon fixation>: incorporation of <carbon> from <carbon dioxide> into <chemical compounds> used to build living material. Capturing <light> and incorporating <carbon> are distinct processes coupled through <metabolism>. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/8-1-overview-of-photosynthesis[OpenStax Biology 2e, 8.1: overview of photosynthesis].

For <oxygenic photosynthesis>, a simplified net equation writes a six-<carbon> <sugar>, <glucose>, as the representative product:
$$
6\mathrm{CO}_2+6\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}+\text{light}\longrightarrow\mathrm{C}_6\mathrm{H}_{12}\mathrm{O}_6+6\mathrm{O}_2.
$$
This is net <stoichiometry>, not a one-step mechanism. A worked bookkeeping check counts six <carbon> <chemical atoms>, twelve <hydrogen> <chemical atoms>, and eighteen <oxygen> <chemical atoms> on each side: the product side has six <oxygen> <chemical atoms> in the <sugar> and twelve in <molecular oxygen>. The <light> label supplies <energy>, not additional <chemical atoms>.

Crucially, the released <molecular oxygen> comes from <water>, not <carbon dioxide>. Cancelling <water> made and consumed in different steps hides this distinction in the net equation. The <Calvin cycle> supplies a net three-<carbon> <triose phosphate>, such as <glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate>, for subsequent <sugar> synthesis; it does not directly export <glucose>. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/8-2-the-light-dependent-reactions-of-photosynthesis[OpenStax Biology 2e, 8.2: light-dependent reactions] and https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/8-3-using-light-energy-to-make-organic-molecules[OpenStax Biology 2e, 8.3: using light energy to make organic molecules].

\Image[photosynthesis-energy-and-matter.png]
{title=<Photosynthesis>: <water> supplies released <molecular oxygen>; <carbon dioxide> supplies <sugar> <carbon>}
{description=Original schematic of <oxygenic photosynthesis>. Solid blue arrows trace selected material transfers; dashed orange arrows show <energy> transfer and the coupling through <ATP> and <NADPH>. These carriers are also <molecules>, not pure <energy>. Arrows omit intermediate <chemical reactions> and do not specify rates or yields.}

The useful achievement is connecting incoming <light> to both usable <energy> and new biological material. <ATP> is an immediate <energy> currency; <NADPH> supplies <electrons> for synthesis; <carbon dioxide> supplies the <carbon> incorporated into <carbohydrates>. These roles are complementary. <Conservation of energy> still holds: <photosynthesis> transforms <energy>, never creates it, and some absorbed <energy> is dissipated as <heat>. A <plant> also carries out <cellular respiration>, including in <biological cells> that perform <photosynthesis>. Gas exchange reflects both processes. <Oxygenic photosynthesis> remains central to interpreting terrestrial <molecular oxygen> as an <atmospheric biosignature gas>. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/8-3-using-light-energy-to-make-organic-molecules[OpenStax Biology 2e, 8.3: photosynthesis and respiration].

= Photosynthetically active radiation
{parent=Photosynthesis}

= PAR
{synonym}

Radiation in the approximate 400–700 nm interval conventionally counted for <photosynthesis>. Organisms differ in spectral use, so equal integrated light does not imply equal biological utility.

= Photosynthetic acclimation
{parent=Photosynthesis}

Adjustment of the composition, capacity or organization of photosynthetic machinery to prevailing conditions. It acts alongside faster regulation of existing components.

= Photoinhibition
{parent=Photosynthesis}

A light-associated reduction in photosynthetic capacity, often involving damage and recovery processes. It should be distinguished from rapidly reversible protective energy dissipation.

= Photoprotection
{parent=Photosynthesis}

Processes limiting light-induced damage when absorbed excitation exceeds safe photochemical use. Energy dissipation, regulated electron flow and repair have complementary roles.

= Chloroplast relocation
{parent=Photoprotection}

Light-dependent movement of chloroplasts within plant cells. Accumulation can increase interception under weak light, whereas avoidance can reduce exposure under strong light.

= Photosynthetic reaction centre
{parent=Photosynthesis}

The pigment–protein machinery converting excitation into separated redox equivalents. Productive charge separation competes with decay of the excited state.

= Light-harvesting complex
{parent=Photosynthesis}

A pigment–protein assembly collecting light and transferring excitation towards a photosynthetic reaction centre. Excitation transfer between pigments differs from electron transfer after charge separation.

= Stomatal conductance
{parent=Photosynthesis}
{title2=$g_s$}

Conductance for gas exchange through <stomata>, influencing carbon uptake and water loss. Responses to carbon dioxide and atmospheric dryness can affect growth and water-use efficiency.

= Gross primary production
{parent=Photosynthesis}
{title2=$\mathrm{GPP}$}

Total photosynthetic carbon uptake over the stated area and interval, before subtraction of producer respiration.

= Chlorophyll fluorescence
{parent=Photosynthesis}

A measurable signal used to investigate the state of the photosynthetic machinery. Recovery after drying can be informative, but should be complemented by measurements of growth and reproductive success.

= Photosynthetic state transition
{parent=Photosynthesis}

A <photosynthetic state transition> redistributes excitation between photosystems in response to their relative activity. In plants, regulated phosphorylation of a mobile LHCII fraction helps alter its functional association with the two photosystems.

= Photorespiration
{parent=Photosynthesis}

<Photorespiration> is the metabolic route processing products of Rubisco oxygenation and recovering part of their carbon. It consumes energy and releases some carbon dioxide; under particular conditions it also provides a sink for energy and reducing power.

= Cyclic electron flow around photosystem I
{parent=Photosynthesis}

<Cyclic electron flow around photosystem I> returns photosystem-I-derived electrons to the intersystem electron-transport chain. It can support proton-gradient formation without net NADPH production, helping adjust ATP supply and photoprotection.

= Photosynthetic control
{parent=Photosynthesis}

<Photosynthetic control> refers to regulation of electron flow, especially lumen-pH-dependent restriction at the cytochrome b6f complex. Slowing electron delivery can protect downstream acceptors from excessive reduction.

= Xanthophyll cycle
{parent=Photosynthesis}

The <xanthophyll cycle> reversibly changes antenna-associated xanthophyll composition with light conditions. Lumen-acidification-dependent de-epoxidation towards zeaxanthin can support photoprotective quenching in plants.

= Non-photochemical quenching
{parent=Photosynthesis}

<Non-photochemical quenching> describes pathways that reduce chlorophyll fluorescence through routes other than photochemical charge separation. Rapid protective components can dissipate excitation as heat, but a fluorescence decrease alone does not uniquely identify the pathway.

= Oxygenic photosynthesis
{parent=Photosynthesis}

<Oxygenic photosynthesis> uses <water> as an <electron> source and releases <molecular oxygen>. <Plants>, <algae> and <cyanobacteria> provide examples. The defining distinction from <anoxygenic photosynthesis> is <molecular oxygen> release, not merely capture of <light>.

= Anoxygenic photosynthesis
{parent=Photosynthesis}
{wiki}

<Anoxygenic photosynthesis> captures <light> without producing <molecular oxygen>. It occurs in some <bacteria> and uses <electron> sources other than <water>. Whether <carbon fixation> accompanies this <light> capture depends on the <organism> and conditions; <photosynthesis> does not imply one universal <carbon>-assimilation pathway. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26819/[Alberts et al.: chloroplasts and photosynthetic bacteria].

= Light-dependent reactions
{parent=Photosynthesis}
{wiki}

In a <chloroplast>, <light-dependent reactions> take place at <thylakoid> membranes. <Chlorophyll> captures <light>; <electron> transfer helps establish an <electrochemical gradient> across the <thylakoid> membrane, enabling formation of <ATP>. The <oxygenic photosynthesis> pathway also forms <NADPH> and releases <molecular oxygen> from <water>. <ATP> and <NADPH> become available in the <chloroplast stroma> for the <Calvin cycle>. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26819/[Alberts et al.: chloroplast light reactions].

= Photosystem
{parent=Light-dependent reactions}

An organized light-harvesting and reaction-center assembly supporting photosynthetic energy conversion.

= Photosystem I
{parent=Photosystem}

<Photosystem I> is a light-driven reaction-centre complex in oxygenic photosynthesis. Excitation supports electron transfer to acceptors including ferredoxin; the balance between linear and cyclic routes affects reductant and ATP supply.

= Photosystem II
{parent=Photosystem}

A <photosystem> involved in water oxidation during <oxygenic photosynthesis>. Its reaction-center components include <D1 protein>.

= Oxygen-evolving complex
{parent=Photosystem II}

The manganese–calcium-containing catalytic machinery that extracts electrons from water in oxygenic photosynthesis. Four oxidizing equivalents permit formation of one oxygen molecule from two water molecules.

= LHCII
{c}
{parent=Photosystem II}

<LHCII> is a major light-harvesting antenna complex in plants. A regulated mobile fraction participates in photosynthetic state transitions; antenna redistribution is distinct from simply dissipating excitation.

= PsbS
{c}
{parent=Photosystem II}

<PsbS> participates in rapid proton-dependent regulation of photosynthetic antenna quenching in plants. Loss of PsbS-dependent quenching can be separated experimentally from loss of light-harvesting capacity.

= Photosystem II repair
{parent=Photosystem II}

Processes replacing damaged <photosystem II> components and restoring functional complexes. Regulated <D1 protein> synthesis is one part of this cycle.

= D1 protein
{parent=Photosystem II}
{c}

A reaction-center <protein> of <photosystem II>, encoded by <psbA messenger RNA> in chloroplast expression systems. Damage creates demand for replacement during <photosystem II repair>.

= Chlorophyll
{parent=Light-dependent reactions}
{wiki}

<Chlorophyll> is a family of <molecules> that absorb <light> for <photosynthesis>. In <chloroplasts>, <chlorophyll> works with <proteins> in <thylakoid> membranes; absorbing <light> initiates <energy> capture rather than directly constructing a <sugar>. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/8-2-the-light-dependent-reactions-of-photosynthesis[OpenStax Biology 2e, 8.2: photosynthetic pigments].

= Chlorophyll a
{parent=Chlorophyll}
{wiki}

A widespread <photosynthetic pigment> with prominent blue and red absorption bands. Its concentration is a useful index of <phytoplankton biomass>, but pigment per unit carbon varies with light acclimation and physiological condition.

= Carbon fixation
{parent=Metabolism}
{wiki}

<Carbon fixation> incorporates <carbon> from <carbon dioxide> into organic <chemical compounds>. It identifies a material transformation, rather than a particular source of <energy>: <carbon fixation> can be supported by <photosynthesis> or by other <metabolism>. The <Calvin cycle> is one pathway, not the definition of all <carbon fixation>.

= Calvin cycle
{c}
{parent=Carbon fixation}
{wiki}

= Calvin-Benson cycle
{c}
{synonym}

The <Calvin cycle> couples <carbon fixation> to use of <ATP> and <NADPH>. In a <chloroplast> it takes place in the <chloroplast stroma>. Its <chemical reactions> incorporate <carbon dioxide>, use <NADPH>-supplied <electrons>, and regenerate the <molecules> that accept further <carbon dioxide>.

In the standard <Calvin cycle> bookkeeping, three <carbon dioxide> <molecules> require nine <ATP> and six <NADPH> for one net three-<carbon> <triose phosphate>. A six-<carbon> <sugar> equivalent therefore corresponds to six <carbon dioxide>, eighteen <ATP> and twelve <NADPH>; subsequent <sugar> synthesis is separate. These are pathway requirements, not a universal measured <photosynthesis> yield. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/8-3-using-light-energy-to-make-organic-molecules[OpenStax Biology 2e, 8.3: Calvin cycle bookkeeping].

Calling the <Calvin cycle> light-independent means its individual <chemical reactions> do not directly capture <light>; it does not mean the <Calvin cycle> runs only at night or independently of the supplies from <light-dependent reactions>.

= RuBisCO
{parent=Calvin cycle}
{c}

Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase, the enzyme catalysing carbon-dioxide addition to ribulose bisphosphate in the Calvin cycle. Its competing oxygenase reaction links carbon assimilation to photorespiration.

= Cellular respiration
{parent=Metabolism}
{wiki}

<Cellular respiration> transfers <energy> from chemical fuels into usable forms including <ATP>. <Oxidation> of the fuel is coupled to <reduction> of an <electron acceptor>; the <energy> transfer can support <ATP> synthesis. <Plants> carry out <cellular respiration> as well as <photosynthesis>. Their <biological cells> need usable <energy> in darkness too, and a <seed> can draw on stored fuel during <germination>.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[Biology 2e, §7.1, Electrons and Energy and ATP in Living Systems: redox and energy carriers]{ref}<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[Biology 2e, §8.3, The Energy Flow, Figures 8.21–8.22: plant respiration and energy transfer]{ref}

Follow three different accounts: <carbon> moving from fuel to products, <electrons> moving to an <electron acceptor>, and <energy> becoming available for cellular work. They are connected, but an <oxygen> uptake measurement alone does not identify the fuel or count the <ATP> made. The <pea seed respirometry> experiment makes this distinction visible with simple apparatus.

= Heterotrophic respiration
{parent=Cellular respiration}
{title2=$R_h$}

Respiratory carbon loss by organisms using organic matter produced by others. It includes important decomposer fluxes and must be distinguished from <autotrophic respiration>.

= Autotrophic respiration
{parent=Cellular respiration}
{title2=$R_a$}

Respiratory carbon loss by primary producers. Subtracting it from <gross primary production> gives <net primary production>.

= Aerobic respiration
{parent=Cellular respiration}

<Aerobic respiration> uses <molecular oxygen> as the final <electron acceptor>. For complete <oxidation> of <glucose>, the net material balance is
$$
\mathrm{C}_6\mathrm{H}_{12}\mathrm{O}_6+6\mathrm{O}_2\longrightarrow6\mathrm{CO}_2+6\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}.
$$
This records the overall conversion of <glucose>, <molecular oxygen>, <carbon dioxide> and <water>; it is not one <enzyme>-catalyzed step, and it omits the separate <ATP> accounting. For an original <stoichiometry> check, each side contains six <carbon>, twelve <hydrogen> and eighteen <oxygen> <chemical atoms>. If exactly $1\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of <glucose> undergoes this complete conversion, the equation requires $6\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of <molecular oxygen> and yields $6\,\mathrm{mmol}$ each of <carbon dioxide> and net <water>. This is an equation-based calculation, not an observed seed measurement.

The <molecular oxygen> is reduced to <water>, rather than being the source of the <carbon dioxide>'s <carbon>. A formal <redox electron balance>, using acidic half-reaction notation, makes the roles explicit:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{C}_6\mathrm{H}_{12}\mathrm{O}_6+6\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}&\longrightarrow6\mathrm{CO}_2+24\mathrm{H}^{+}+24e^{-},\\
6\mathrm{O}_2+24\mathrm{H}^{+}+24e^{-}&\longrightarrow12\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Adding and cancelling gives the net equation above. Both half-reactions conserve <chemical atoms> and <electric charge>. The $24$ <electrons> balance a net transfer; they are carried through cellular intermediates, rather than released as a pool of free <electrons>. The final respiratory <reduction> can be written $\mathrm{O}_2+4\mathrm{H}^{+}+4e^{-}\longrightarrow2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}$. These equations are bookkeeping, not a tracing experiment that assigns every product <oxygen> <chemical atom> to its original <molecule>.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[Biology 2e, §7.4, Electron Transport Chain, Figure 7.12: oxygen reduction to water]{ref}

= Respiration and ATP coupling
{parent=Aerobic respiration}
{title2=$\mathrm{ADP}+P_i\longrightarrow\mathrm{ATP}$}

<Glycolysis> converts <glucose> into two <pyruvate> <molecules>, with a net two <ATP> and reduced <NADH>; it does not directly consume <molecular oxygen>. <Pyruvate> processing and the <citric acid cycle>, also called the <TCA cycle>, release <carbon dioxide> and supply reduced carriers. Thus releasing <carbon dioxide> and consuming <molecular oxygen> occur at different stages.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[Biology 2e, §7.2, Outcomes of Glycolysis; §7.3, Breakdown of Pyruvate and Citric Acid Cycle]{ref}

\Image[cellular-respiration-carbon-redox-energy-flow.png]
{title=<Cellular respiration>: separate <carbon>, <electron> and <energy> accounts}
{description=Original pathway schematic. Blue arrows trace the fuel pathway and <carbon dioxide> release; purple arrows indicate reduced-carrier delivery and terminal <molecular oxygen> <reduction>; orange dashed arrows denote <energy coupling>. Carrier chemistry and intermediate steps are abbreviated. Arrow widths encode no rates or yields.}

An <electron transport chain> reoxidizes carriers such as <NADH>. Coupling some of its <redox> steps to <proton> transport establishes an <electrochemical gradient>; <ATP synthase> can use that gradient to form <ATP> from <adenosine diphosphate> and <inorganic phosphate>. This is <oxidative phosphorylation>. A separate route, <substrate-level phosphorylation>, transfers a <phosphate> group from a reacting <substrate> to <adenosine diphosphate>. In typical <eukaryotic> <biological cells>, the respiratory chain occupies the inner membrane of a <mitochondrion>.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[Biology 2e, §7.1, Substrate Phosphorylation; §7.4, Electron Transport Chain and Chemiosmosis, Figures 7.12–7.14]{ref}

The useful achievement is controlled transfer: fuel <oxidation> can replenish an <ATP> supply that supports <active transport> and synthesis. <ATP> is a reusable chemical carrier, not newly created <energy>; <ATP> <hydrolysis> and the coupled cellular process have their own net <free energy> balance. Some fuel <energy> is dissipated as <heat>.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[Biology 2e, §7.1, ATP in Living Systems and Energy from ATP; §7.4, ATP Yield]{ref}

There is no universal yield of $36$ or $38$ <ATP> per <glucose>. Actual <ATP> recovery depends on carrier transfer routes, coupling and diversion of intermediates to other <metabolism>. In particular, an <oxygen> uptake rate cannot be converted into an <ATP> production rate by assuming one fixed multiplier.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[Biology 2e, §7.4, ATP Yield: shuttle differences and use of intermediates]{ref}

= Anaerobic respiration
{parent=Cellular respiration}
{wiki}

<Anaerobic respiration> uses a respiratory <electron transport chain> with a final <electron acceptor> other than <molecular oxygen>. External acceptors can include <sulfate>, depending on the <organism>. “External” distinguishes a supplied terminal acceptor from the fuel-derived organic intermediates used in <fermentation>; it does not mean that the <reduction> must take place outside the <biological cell>. In <fermentation>, regeneration of carriers such as <NADH> to their oxidized form permits <glycolysis> to continue without a respiratory <electron transport chain>. Both routes can operate without <molecular oxygen>, but they are different mechanisms.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[Biology 2e, §7.5, opening distinction, Anaerobic Cellular Respiration and Figure 7.16]{ref}

= Respirometer
{parent=Cellular respiration}
{wiki}

A <respirometer> measures gas changes associated with <cellular respiration>. In the absorber-based <pea seed respirometry> apparatus, the changing gas inventory is inferred from a liquid boundary's displacement. A <respirometer> is therefore a physical measurement coupled to a biological process, not a direct <ATP> counter.

= Respiratory exchange ratio
{parent=Respirometry}
{title2=$\mathrm{RER}$}

The ratio of outward <carbon dioxide> transfer to inward <oxygen> transfer measured at the mouth. It can differ from the cellular <respiratory quotient> during transient changes in body gas stores.

= Pea seed respirometry
{parent=Respirometer}

The University of Oklahoma K20 Center's handout describes three sealed vial-and-pipette <respirometers> in each <temperature> bath: soaked, germinating <peas>; nongerminating <peas> plus inert plastic beads; and beads alone. Displaced <water> establishes equal occupied <volumes>. Absorbent cotton holds <potassium hydroxide>, which removes <carbon dioxide>; nonabsorbent cotton separates it from the <peas>. The source equilibrates the vials before submersion and again before the initial reading. Colored <water> makes the boundary visible in each calibrated pipette. Readers then record positions every five minutes for twenty minutes.<Cell Respiration by the K20 Center>[Cell Respiration, K20 Center handout adapted from AP Biology Lab Manual, pp. 3–4, procedure 1–16: preparation, equilibration and readings]{ref}

\Image[pea-respirometer-absorber-and-controls.png]
{title=<Pea seed respirometry>: absorber, gas readout and matched controls}
{description=Original schematic of the university handout's vial-and-pipette apparatus. <Carbon dioxide> is removed from the gas; <molecular oxygen> consumption draws the <water> boundary toward the vial. The three sample groups share a bath and matched occupied <volumes>. Geometry, particle counts and boundary position are illustrative; no measured readings are plotted.}

Bath <temperature>, vial position and seal integrity matter. The beads-only <respirometer> detects common <temperature> or atmospheric <pressure> drift; its change is subtracted from each pea reading. Nongerminating <peas> provide a biological comparison, not an assumption of zero <cellular respiration>. A larger corrected uptake slope for germinating <peas> would support greater <molecular oxygen> consumption by that sample under the tested conditions.<Cell Respiration by the K20 Center>[Cell Respiration, pp. 1, 3–4 and 6: living dormant seeds, position/leaks, corrected differences and slope]{ref}

Equal displaced <volumes> help match the gas spaces; they do not establish equal living <mass> or equal developmental state. Comparing uptake per <seed> and uptake per dry <mass> asks different questions. Leaks, incomplete <carbon dioxide> absorption, gas dissolution and changing water-vapour contributions can distort the <respirometer> inference. A blank correction cannot repair a sample-specific leak. These are consequences of the <respirometer gas balance>, rather than measurements reported in the handout.

= Respirometer gas balance
{parent=Respirometer}

For an original idealized <mathematical model>, let $n_{\mathrm O_2}(t)$ be the <amount of substance> of <molecular oxygen> consumed since the initial reading and $n_{\mathrm{CO}_2}(t)$ the <carbon dioxide> produced. Suppose a fraction $f$ of that <carbon dioxide> is removed from the gas, all other gas inventories remain fixed, and <temperature> $T$ and gas <pressure> $P$ stay constant. The <ideal gas law> gives the positive gas contraction
$$
D(t)=V(0)-V(t)=\frac{RT}{P}\left[n_{\mathrm O_2}(t)-(1-f)n_{\mathrm{CO}_2}(t)\right],
$$
where $R$ is the <molar gas constant> and $V$ the gas <volume>. With complete absorption, $f=1$, the contraction reflects <molecular oxygen> uptake regardless of the amount of <carbon dioxide> made. For a uniform pipette bore of <area> $A$, an inward boundary displacement $x$ gives $D=Ax$. A graduated pipette can instead supply the displaced <volume> directly.

Let $D_s$ denote sample contraction and $D_b$ the matched blank contraction. Define $D_{\mathrm{corr}}=D_s-D_b$ with both taken relative to their own initial readings. Under the preceding assumptions,
$$
n_{\mathrm O_2}(t)\simeq\frac{P D_{\mathrm{corr}}(t)}{RT},\qquad
\overline{r}_{\mathrm O_2}=\frac{P}{RT}\frac{D_{\mathrm{corr}}(t_2)-D_{\mathrm{corr}}(t_1)}{t_2-t_1}.
$$
The <slope> of corrected <volume> against <time> estimates uptake over the chosen interval. The approximate sign acknowledges that a liquid boundary also responds to hydrostatic and capillary <pressure>, while the gas can exchange <water> vapour. A fixed-<volume> apparatus would instead show a <pressure> decrease; its ideal conversion is $n_{\mathrm O_2}\simeq V\bigl(P(0)-P(t)\bigr)/(RT)$ after complete absorption and appropriate blank correction.

The <ideal gas law> also exposes drift without any biology: for fixed gas inventory, $V\propto T/P$. A one-percent rise in absolute <temperature> at fixed <pressure> produces a one-percent expansion. Matching the initial gas spaces makes such common changes easier to subtract. Subtracting unmatched blanks can leave a physical signal that looks biological.

= Respiratory quotient
{parent=Respirometer gas balance}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mathrm{RQ}=n_{\mathrm{CO}_2}/n_{\mathrm O_2}$}

The <respiratory quotient> compares <carbon dioxide> production with <molecular oxygen> consumption over the same interval. Complete <glucose> <oxidation> in the net equation above gives $\mathrm{RQ}=6/6=1$. In the ideal <respirometer gas balance> with no absorber, $f=0$, that conversion gives $D=0$: substantial <cellular respiration> can occur without any net gas contraction. A zero contraction therefore does not by itself demonstrate zero <metabolism>.

Other fuels, incomplete conversion and concurrent synthesis can alter the ratio. Absorber-based <respirometry> isolates <molecular oxygen> uptake by deliberately removing the other gas signal; it cannot independently determine the <respiratory quotient> or identify <glucose> as the consumed fuel. Separate <carbon dioxide> measurement would supply the missing account.

= Secondary metabolic byproduct
{parent=Metabolism}

A compound released through specialized functions such as signaling, defense or stress adaptation rather than the core reactions needed for energy and biomass. <Dimethyl sulfide> and <chloromethane> are examples discussed as atmospheric biosignatures. Their specialized origins may improve specificity while lowering the global emission flux.

= Primary metabolic byproduct
{parent=Metabolism}

A product released by reactions needed for energy acquisition, growth or biomass construction. <Methane> from energy-yielding microbial reactions and <molecular oxygen> from oxygenic <photosynthesis> are examples. Abiotic chemistry can make some of the same molecules, so production mechanism and environmental abundance matter.
