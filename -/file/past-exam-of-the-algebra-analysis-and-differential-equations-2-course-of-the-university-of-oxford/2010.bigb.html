Write $n=\dim V$. If $Rv=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$, then $0=R^av=\lambda^av$, hence $\lambda=0$. The <characteristic polynomial> splits over $\mathbb C$ and has degree $n$, so
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{spec}(R)=\{0\},\qquad\chi_R(t)=t^n.}
$$
There is indeed an <eigenvector> for the <eigenvalue> zero: a nonzero <vector> in the last nonzero <image> of $R$ lies in its <kernel of a linear map>. For the bound, choose $v$ with $R^{a-1}v\ne0$, using minimality. The vectors $v,Rv,\ldots,R^{a-1}v$ are <linearly independent>. Otherwise choose the least $j$ with nonzero coefficient in a relation and apply $R^{a-1-j}$; only its nonzero multiple of $R^{a-1}v$ survives, a contradiction. Therefore $\boxed{a\le n}$.

Suppose $S^b=0$. Use the increasing chain of <vector subspaces>
$$
0=\ker S^0\subseteq\ker S\subseteq\ker S^2\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\ker S^b=V.
$$
Choose a <basis> successively, extending a <basis> of each <kernel of a linear map> to one of the next <kernel of a linear map>. If a <basis> <vector> first enters at level $j$, its <image> under $S$ belongs to $\ker S^{j-1}$, spanned entirely by earlier <basis> vectors. Thus every <matrix> column has nonzero entries only above its diagonal: the <matrix> is <strictly upper triangular matrix>[strictly upper triangular].

Conversely, for such a <matrix> and its ordered <basis> $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, one has $S\langle e_1,\ldots,e_j\rangle\subseteq\langle e_1,\ldots,e_{j-1}\rangle$. Iterating $n$ times kills every <vector>. Hence $\boxed{S^n=0}$, establishing both directions without assuming a Jordan form.

Write $f=u+iv$ and $z=x+iy$. The <complex derivative> computed along a real increment is $f'=u_x+iv_x$, while along an imaginary increment it is $f'=v_y-iu_y$. Equality gives
$$
\boxed{u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x.}
$$
These are the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>[Cauchy–Riemann equations] at every point where $f$ is <holomorphic>. The directional calculations also establish the existence of those <partial derivatives>. More explicitly, $f(z+h)-f(z)=f'(z)h+o(|h|)$ gives both real-direction formulas directly. If a converse is desired, <continuous> first <partial derivatives> together with these equations make the real differential multiplication by $u_x+iv_x$, hence give <complex differentiability>; the necessity above is what is requested.

The allowed theorem makes $f'$ <holomorphic> and hence <continuous>; applying it repeatedly gives <continuous> <derivatives> of every order. Since $u_x=\Re f'$, $v_x=\Im f'$, $u_y=-\Im f'$ and $v_y=\Re f'$, the real and imaginary parts have <continuous> second <partial derivatives>. Differentiate the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>[Cauchy–Riemann equations] and commute mixed <derivatives>:
$$
u_{xx}+u_{yy}=(v_y)_x-(v_x)_y=0,
\qquad
v_{xx}+v_{yy}=-(u_y)_x+(u_x)_y=0.
$$
Thus $\boxed{\Delta u=\Delta v=0}$, with the required $C^2$ regularity, proving harmonicity.

Write $f=u+iv$ and evaluate all <derivatives> of $\psi$ at $(u,v)$. The real <chain rule> gives
$$
\Delta(\psi\circ f)=\psi_{uu}(u_x^2+u_y^2)+2\psi_{uv}(u_xv_x+u_yv_y)+\psi_{vv}(v_x^2+v_y^2)+\psi_u\Delta u+\psi_v\Delta v.
$$
The <Cauchy-Riemann equations>[Cauchy–Riemann equations] give equal squared lengths $u_x^2+u_y^2=v_x^2+v_y^2=|f'|^2$ and zero cross term $u_xv_x+u_yv_y=0$. Part (ii) removes the last two terms. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Delta(\psi\circ f)=|f'|^2(\Delta\psi)\circ f=0.}
$$
The composition is $C^2$, so it is <harmonic>. No assumption that $f'$ is nonzero is required.

Use the branch in (i) and the positively oriented slit-annulus contour. Its upper ray runs from $r$ to $R$, its outer circle counterclockwise, its lower ray from $R$ back to $r$, and its inner circle clockwise. Initially keep their arguments at $\alpha$ and $2\pi-\alpha$ as in the figure, with $0<\alpha<\pi/2$; let $\alpha\downarrow0$ for fixed $0<r<1<R$. Both poles $i,-i$ lie inside.

Write $\sigma=\Re w\in(-1,1)$. On the outer circle the absolute integral is $O(R^{\sigma-1})$, since $|z^w|\le e^{2\pi|\Im w|}R^\sigma$ and $|z^2+1|\ge R^2-1$. On the inner circle it is $O(r^{\sigma+1})$. Both tend to zero. The real integral converges absolutely because its endpoint behaviors are $x^\sigma$ and $x^{\sigma-2}$.

The two residues, with the chosen arguments, are
$$
\frac{e^{i\pi w/2}}{2i},\qquad-\frac{e^{3i\pi w/2}}{2i}.

The <residue theorem> and the two ray orientations therefore give
$$
(1-e^{2\pi iw})I(w)=\pi e^{i\pi w/2}(1-e^{i\pi w}),\qquad I(w)=\int_0^\infty\frac{x^w}{1+x^2}\,dx.

\boxed{I(w)=\frac{\pi e^{i\pi w/2}}{1+e^{i\pi w}}=\frac\pi{2\cos(\pi w/2)}.}
$$
At $w=0$ the same result follows directly from $I(0)=\int_0^\infty(1+x^2)^{-1}dx=\pi/2$, or by <dominated convergence>. The denominator has no zero in the open strip.

If $Au+Bv=0$, evaluation at zero gives $A=0$ and differentiation at zero gives $B=0$. Thus the solutions are <linearly independent>. For $w=uv'-u'v$,
$$
w'=uv''-u''v=-p(uv'-u'v)=-pw,\qquad w(0)=1,
$$
so $\boxed{w(x)=\exp(-\int_0^xp(s)\,ds)>0}$.

<Variation of parameters> sets $y=A(x)u+B(x)v$ with $A'u+B'v=0$, $A'u'+B'v'=f$. Solving gives $A'=-vf/w$ and $B'=uf/w$. Zero integration constants enforce both <initial conditions>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\int_0^x\frac{v(x)u(t)-u(x)v(t)}{w(t)}f(t)\,dt,\qquad K(x,t)=\frac{v(x)u(t)-u(x)v(t)}{w(t)}.}
$$
For fixed $t$, this <Green's function>[kernel] is a <linear combination> of homogeneous solutions, so $K_{xx}+pK_x+qK=0$. On the diagonal $K(t,t)=0$ and $K_x(t,t)=(v'(t)u(t)-u'(t)v(t))/w(t)=1$. Differentiating the integral twice now verifies the forced equation as well as $y(0)=y'(0)=0$. Standard uniqueness of the linear <initial value problem>[initial-value problem] makes this the solution, for <continuous> $f$.

If $u,v$ have <periodic function>[period] $T>0$, their <derivatives> do also, hence $w$ is <periodic>. The <Green's function>[kernel] is <periodic> in its first variable. With $f=v$, split the integral at $x$ and use this periodicity to get
$$
y(x+T)-y(x)=-u(x)\int_x^{x+T}\frac{v(t)^2}{w(t)}\,dt+v(x)\int_x^{x+T}\frac{u(t)v(t)}{w(t)}\,dt.
$$
At $x=0$, the right side is $-\int_0^T v(t)^2/w(t)\,dt<0$, because $w>0$ and $v'(0)=1$ ensures $v$ is not identically zero. Thus $\boxed{y(T)\ne y(0)}$, so $y$ cannot have <periodic function>[period] $T$. This direct sign argument also rules out any apparent cancellation between the two terms.

= Solution

Use a parameter $t$. The characteristic equations are
$$
\boxed{\dot x=x+cy-bz,\quad\dot y=y+az-cx,\quad\dot z=z+bx-ay.}
$$
For a <graph of a function> $z=f(x,y)$ satisfying the equation, solve the first two equations after substituting $z=f(x,y)$. The <chain rule> and the <partial differential equation> give $d f(x(t),y(t))/dt=f+bx-ay$, exactly the third equation. Uniqueness for the resulting <linear ordinary differential equation> system shows that its characteristic through the initial point agrees with this curve on the <graph of a function>. This proves invariance as long as the <graph of a function> remains defined along the curve. A claim about the entire curve additionally requires a <surface> continued along that flow; a restricted <graph of a function> patch alone cannot imply global continuation.

Let $\mathbf k=(a,b,c)$, $\boldsymbol\ell=(l,m,n)$ and $\mathbf r=(x,y,z)$. Then $\dot{\mathbf r}=\mathbf r-\mathbf k\times\mathbf r$. Since $|\mathbf k|=1$,
$$
\frac d{dt}(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r)=\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r,\qquad\frac d{dt}|\mathbf r|^2=2|\mathbf r|^2.
$$
Along the characteristic starting from $s\boldsymbol\ell$ these give $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r=e^ts(\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\ell)$ and $|\mathbf r|=e^t|s|$. Consequently the characteristic <surface> generated by the given line satisfies
$$
\boxed{|ax+by+cz|=|al+bm+cn|\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}.}
$$
To see that the whole displayed <cone> is generated, not just contained in it, decompose $\boldsymbol\ell=\kappa\mathbf k+\mathbf v$ with $\mathbf v\perp\mathbf k$, $\kappa=\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\ell$. The full solution is
$$
\mathbf r(s,t)=se^t\bigl(\kappa\mathbf k+\cos t\,\mathbf v-\sin t\,(\mathbf k\times\mathbf v)\bigr).
$$
When $0<|\kappa|<1$, variation of $t$ gives all azimuths about the axis, while the freely chosen $s$ supplies every positive radius on both halves. This is precisely the two-sided circular <cone>, with
$$
\boxed{\text{vertex }(0,0,0),\quad\text{axis }\mathbb R(a,b,c),\quad\text{semi-angle }\arccos|al+bm+cn|.}
$$
The edge cases matter: $\kappa=0$ gives the <plane> $ax+by+cz=0$ (semi-angle $\pi/2$), and $|\kappa|=1$ generates only the axis (semi-angle zero), not a two-dimensional <surface>. In the latter case the initial line is itself characteristic, so it does not determine a unique integral <surface>. The nondegenerate <cone> is smooth away from its vertex and can be used as a <graph of a function> only on patches whose <tangent plane> is not vertical. Thus the printed geometric conclusion has its ordinary characteristic-<surface> meaning in the nondegenerate case, with these domain and degeneracy qualifications; it cannot be asserted for every global smooth <graph of a function> without them.

For an azimuth-independent product $u=R(r)\Theta(\theta)$, multiply <Laplace's equation> by $r^2/(R\Theta)$ where the factors are nonzero. The radial and angular expressions must equal opposite constants:
$$
\boxed{r^2R''+2rR'=\lambda R,\qquad(\sin\theta\,\Theta')'=-\lambda\sin\theta\,\Theta.}
$$
The resulting differential identities extend across isolated zeros by <continuity>. Put $\rho=\cos\theta$. Then $\Theta'=-\sin\theta P'$ and
$$
(\sin\theta\,\Theta')'=\sin\theta\frac d{d\rho}\bigl((1-\rho^2)P'\bigr).
$$
This gives $((1-\rho^2)P')'+\lambda P=0$ on $(-1,1)$.

A nonzero angular factor must be regular at both poles: it must be bounded as $\rho\to\pm1$ and extend smoothly as an axisymmetric function on the <sphere>. Equivalently one may use the regular endpoint solution at each end; the logarithmic endpoint solutions are excluded. These two endpoint conditions select $\boxed{\lambda=n(n+1),\ n=0,1,2,\ldots}$ and the multiples of $P_n$. It is not sufficient to impose no condition at the endpoints, since local solutions exist for arbitrary $\lambda$. One way to see the quantization is the regular Legendre series about one endpoint: unless it terminates at $\lambda=n(n+1)$, continuation to the other endpoint has a logarithmic singularity.

Direct differentiation verifies
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
P&\lambda&R(r)\\\hline
1&0&A+B/r\\
\rho&2&Ar+Br^{-2}\\
(3\rho^2-1)/2&6&Ar^2+Br^{-3}
\end{array}
$$
Indeed $R=r^m$ gives $m(m+1)=\lambda$, so the two powers for degree $n$ are $r^n$ and $r^{-n-1}$.

For the shell assume $a>1$. The outer datum decomposes as $\rho^2=\tfrac13P_0(\rho)+\tfrac23P_2(\rho)$. Normalize each <radial function>[radial solution] to vanish at $1$ and equal $1$ at $a$; <superposition> gives
$$
\boxed{u(r,\theta)=\frac13\frac{1-r^{-1}}{1-a^{-1}}+\frac23\frac{r^2-r^{-3}}{a^2-a^{-3}}P_2(\cos\theta).}
$$
Every term is <harmonic> in the shell and regular at the angular poles; substitution at each boundary gives precisely the two prescribed data. For uniqueness among functions <harmonic> in the shell and <continuous> on its closure, subtract any two solutions. Their difference is <harmonic>, zero on both boundary spheres, and by the <maximum principle> is both at most zero and at least zero on the bounded shell. It vanishes identically. Thus **the classical <Dirichlet problem>[Dirichlet solution] is unique**. The usual <continuity> at the boundary is the interpretation of the stated <boundary conditions>; allowing singular boundary behavior would remove that hypothesis.
