= Solution
{parent=1}

Put $p(t)=\prod_{j=1}^m(t-\mu_j)$ and define the <Lagrange interpolation polynomials>
$$
\ell_j(t)=\prod_{k\ne j}\frac{t-\mu_k}{\mu_j-\mu_k}.
$$
Their denominators are nonzero and $\ell_j(\mu_i)=\delta_{ij}$. The <polynomial> $\sum_j\ell_j-1$ has degree at most $m-1$ and vanishes at all $m$ distinct <scalars>, so it is zero. Suppose condition (a) holds, and put $E_j=\ell_j(T)$. Since $(t-\mu_j)\ell_j(t)$ is a constant multiple of $p(t)$,
$$
(T-\mu_jI)E_j=0,\qquad\sum_jE_j=I.
$$
Thus each $E_jv$ lies in $V_j$, and every <vector> is a sum of such <vectors>. If $\sum_jv_j=0$ with $v_j\in V_j$, applying $E_i$ gives $v_i=0$, because $\ell_i(T)v_j=\ell_i(\mu_j)v_j$. Hence the sum is direct, proving (b). Conversely, on each $V_j$ the factor $T-\mu_jI$ vanishes; the factors commute, so $p(T)$ vanishes on every summand and therefore on $V$. This proves the equivalence **without invoking primary decomposition**.

If $ST=TS$ and $v\in V_j$, then $(T-\mu_jI)Sv=S(T-\mu_jI)v=0$, so $S[V_j]\subseteq V_j$. Conversely, if each summand is preserved, then on $V_j$ both $ST$ and $TS$ act as $\mu_jS$. They agree on all summands and hence on $V$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{ST=TS\iff S[V_j]\subseteq V_j\text{ for every }j.}
$$

= 2
{parent=Algebra}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2}

The <dual space> $V^*$ is the <vector space> of <linear maps> $V\to\mathbb F$, with pointwise operations. For the given <basis> define coordinate forms $e^i$ by $e^i(\sum_ja_je_j)=a_i$. These are linear and satisfy $e^i(e_j)=\delta_{ij}$. If $\sum_ic_ie^i=0$, evaluation at $e_j$ gives $c_j=0$. For every <linear form> $\phi$,
$$
\phi(v)=\sum_i\phi(e_i)e^i(v),
$$
so the coordinate forms span the dual and form its <dual basis>.

In the <polynomial> example write $p(X)=a+bX+cX^2$. Then
$$
\phi_1(p)=a,\qquad\phi_2(p)=a+b+c,\qquad\phi_3(p)=a-b+c.
$$
These recover all three <coefficients>, so they span $V^*$ and, since the dual has <dimension> three, form a <basis>. Explicitly the <basis> dual to $1,X,X^2$ is
$$
\boxed{e^1=\phi_1,\qquad e^2=\frac{\phi_2-\phi_3}2,\qquad e^3=\frac{\phi_2+\phi_3}2-\phi_1.}
$$
Evaluation on the three monomials verifies the dual-<basis> identities directly.

= Solution

An element $a$ of an <integral domain> is an <irreducible element> when it is nonzero and not a <unit>, and any <factorization> $a=bc$ has at least one <unit> factor. The exclusions of zero and <units> are part of the definition.

= b
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

An element $a$ is a <prime element> when it is nonzero and not a <unit>, and $a\mid bc$ implies $a\mid b$ or $a\mid c$. If $a=bc$ and $a$ is a <prime element>, assume for example $b=ad$. Cancellation in $a=adc$ gives $dc=1$, so $c$ is a <unit>. This proves every <prime element> is an <irreducible element>.

In a <Euclidean domain>, each nonzero <ideal> has a generator of least Euclidean value: division of another member by that generator leaves a remainder in the <ideal> of smaller value, so the remainder must vanish. Let $a$ be an <irreducible element> and suppose $a\mid bc$ but $a\nmid b$. Write $(a,b)=(d)$. Since $d\mid a$, irreducibility says $d$ is a <unit> or is associate to $a$. The second case would give $a\mid b$, so $d$ is a <unit>. Hence $1=ra+sb$ for some $r,s$. Multiplying by $c$ shows $a\mid c$. Thus **irreducibles in a <Euclidean domain> are prime**.

For $R=\mathbb Z[i\sqrt7]$, use the multiplicative integer <field norm> $N(m+ni\sqrt7)=m^2+7n^2$. <Units> have <field norm> one and are exactly $\pm1$. If $2=uv$ were a product of nonunits, their norms would be integers greater than one multiplying to four, hence both would have <field norm> two. But $m^2+7n^2=2$ has no integer solution: $n\ne0$ makes the left side at least seven, and $n=0$ would give $m^2=2$. Therefore **2 is an <irreducible element>**.

On the other hand,
$$
(1+i\sqrt7)(1-i\sqrt7)=8
$$
is divisible by 2, while neither factor is, because <divisibility> by 2 requires both integer <coefficients> to be even. Thus **2 is not a <prime element>** in this <ring>. It is also **not a <prime element> in $\mathbb Z[i]$**: $2\mid(1+i)^2=2i$, but $2\nmid1+i$. In fact $2=(1+i)(1-i)$ is already a nontrivial <factorization> there.

= Solution
{parent=i}

A function is <holomorphic> on an <open set> if it is <complex differentiable> at every point of that set. At $z_0=x_0+iy_0$, take difference quotients first along real increments. Their limit is $f'(z_0)$, so $u_x=\operatorname{Re}f'(z_0)$ and $v_x=\operatorname{Im}f'(z_0)$. Along imaginary increments,
$$
\frac{f(z_0+ih)-f(z_0)}h\longrightarrow if'(z_0),
$$
so $u_y=-\operatorname{Im}f'(z_0)$ and $v_y=\operatorname{Re}f'(z_0)$. These limits prove existence of all four <partial derivatives> and the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> $u_x=v_y$, $u_y=-v_x$.

For the first example, the identity function and the reciprocal function are <holomorphic> away from zero, so **$f$ is <holomorphic> on the given domain**, with
$$
\boxed{f'(z)=1-z^{-2}.}
$$

= ii
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Write $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}>0$. For $u=x/(1+r)$, $v=y/(1+r)$,
$$
u_x=\frac1{1+r}-\frac{x^2}{r(1+r)^2},\qquad v_y=\frac1{1+r}-\frac{y^2}{r(1+r)^2},\qquad u_y=v_x=-\frac{xy}{r(1+r)^2}.
$$
The two <Cauchy-Riemann equations> would require $x^2=y^2$ and $xy=0$ simultaneously, impossible when $r>0$. Thus $f$ is not <complex differentiable> at any nonzero point.

At zero, however,
$$
\frac{f(h)-f(0)}h=\frac1{1+|h|}\longrightarrow1.
$$
So **$f$ is <complex differentiable> at zero, with <derivative> one, but is not <holomorphic> on $\mathbb C$ or on any nonempty open subset**. <Complex differentiability> at a single point is weaker than <holomorphy> in a neighborhood.

= 5
{parent=Analysis}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=5}

Choose a small positively oriented circle $C_\varepsilon$ about $z_0$ inside the given region. Cauchy's theorem on the region between the two <contours> gives
$$
\int_\gamma\frac{f(z)}{z-z_0}\,dz=\int_{C_\varepsilon}\frac{f(z)}{z-z_0}\,dz=i\int_0^{2\pi}f(z_0+\varepsilon e^{it})\,dt.
$$
<Continuity> makes the last integrand converge uniformly to $f(z_0)$ as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. Its limit is $2\pi if(z_0)$, proving the <Cauchy integral formula>.

The same formula holds with an interior point $w$ near $z_0$. Subtract its value at $z_0$ and divide by $w-z_0$:
$$
\frac{f(w)-f(z_0)}{w-z_0}=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\gamma\frac{f(z)}{(z-w)(z-z_0)}\,dz.
$$
The <contour> is a positive distance from $z_0$, so the integrands converge uniformly as $w\to z_0$. Passing to the limit gives the differentiated formula
$$
\boxed{f'(z_0)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\gamma\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^2}\,dz.}
$$
On the <unit circle>, $\bar z=1/z$ and $\operatorname{Im}z=(z-\bar z)/(2i)$, proving the requested identity. Hence the requested integral is
$$
\frac1{2i}\left[\int_{|z|=1}\frac{dz}{3z-1}-\int_{|z|=1}\frac{dz}{z^2(3z-1)}\right].
$$
The first integral is $2\pi i/3$. For the second use
$$
\frac1{z^2(3z-1)}=-\frac1{z^2}-\frac3z+\frac9{3z-1}.
$$
The differentiated formula applied to the constant function gives $\int z^{-2}\,dz=0$; the remaining two terms integrate to $-6\pi i+6\pi i=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\int_{|z|=1}\frac{\operatorname{Im}z}{z(3z-1)}\,dz=\frac\pi3.}
$$

= 6
{parent=Analysis}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=6}

At a zero $a$, <Taylor's theorem> gives $f(z)=\sum_{n\geq0}c_n(z-a)^n$ near $a$. If some <coefficient> is nonzero, let $m$ be the least such index. Then $f(z)=(z-a)^mq(z)$ with $q(a)\ne0$, and <continuity> makes $q$ nonzero on a smaller disc. Thus $a$ is an isolated zero. A <limit point> of zeros is also a zero by <continuity> and cannot be isolated, so all Taylor <coefficients> there vanish and $f$ vanishes on a nonempty open disc.

Let $A$ be the set of points having a neighborhood on which $f$ vanishes. It is nonempty and open. A point in its relative closure either already belongs to $A$ or is a <limit point> of distinct zeros; the preceding argument puts it in $A$ in either case. Thus $A$ is also relatively closed. <Connectedness> of $D$ gives $A=D$, proving **$f$ is identically zero**.

For conjugate reflection, the disc is invariant under conjugation, and
$$
\frac{h(z+w)-h(z)}w=\overline{\frac{g(\bar z+\bar w)-g(\bar z)}{\bar w}}\longrightarrow\overline{g'(\bar z)}.
$$
Hence **$h$ is <holomorphic>**, with <derivative> $\overline{g'(\bar z)}$. If $g$ is real on the real diameter, then for real $x\in(-1,1)$, $h(x)=\overline{g(x)}=g(x)$. The <holomorphic> difference $g-h$ therefore has zeros accumulating at zero, and the <identity theorem> just proved gives **$g=h$ on the entire disc**.

= Solution
{parent=7}

Assume the <vector field> and multiplier are continuously differentiable, as required for <Green's theorem> and <uniqueness> of trajectories. A nonconstant periodic <trajectory> of a planar <autonomous system> is a simple closed orbit: <uniqueness> prevents crossings, and one traversal can be taken over its least period. Let $\Omega$ be its bounded interior. Along the orbit $dx=f\,dt$, $dy=g\,dt$, so
$$
\oint_{\partial\Omega}\phi f\,dy-\phi g\,dx=\int\phi(fg-gf)\,dt=0.
$$
<Green's theorem> identifies this with $\iint_\Omega[\partial_x(\phi f)+\partial_y(\phi g)]\,dx\,dy$, which is strictly positive by hypothesis, a contradiction. Thus there is **no nonconstant <periodic orbit>**. This standard periodic-orbit criterion does not by itself exclude stationary solutions.

For the first concrete system choose $\phi=-1$. Then
$$
\partial_x(\phi f)+\partial_y(\phi g)=e^{-x}>0.
$$
There are also no <equilibria>: $f=0$ requires $y=1$, but then $g=-e^{-x}-x^2<0$. Consequently it has **no <periodic solution>, including constant ones**.

For the second system the origin is outside the domain of the printed <vector field>. For $r>0$,
$$
\dot r=\frac{x\dot x+y\dot y}{r}=1-r,\qquad\dot\theta=\frac{x\dot y-y\dot x}{r^2}=1.
$$
Thus for an admissible initial point with $\rho=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}>0$,
$$
\boxed{r(t)=1+(\rho-1)e^{-t},\qquad\theta(t)=\alpha+t,}
$$
where $a=\rho\cos\alpha$, $b=\rho\sin\alpha$. These formulas are valid while $r>0$, in particular for every $t\geq0$. Trajectories rotate counterclockwise, spiral outward when $0<r<1$, and inward when $r>1$, approaching the <unit circle>. On $r=1$ they give $x(t)=\cos(\alpha+t)$, $y(t)=\sin(\alpha+t)$, a **<periodic orbit> with least period $2\pi$**. It is an attracting <limit cycle>. There is no contradiction with the preceding criterion: this <vector field> is singular at the origin and has the exhibited closed orbit.

= 8
{parent=Differential equations}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=8}

With parameter $t$ along a <characteristic curve>, the equations are
$$
\frac{dx}{dt}=y,\qquad\frac{dy}{dt}=1,\qquad\frac{du}{dt}=u,\qquad(x,y,u)|_{t=0}=(s,0,s).
$$
Their solutions are $y=t$, $x=s+t^2/2$ and $u=se^t$. Thus the projected curve satisfies $y^2=2(x-s)$, as required. Eliminating the parameters gives
$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=\left(x-\frac{y^2}2\right)e^y.}
$$
Indeed $u_x=e^y$ and $u_y=e^y(x-y^2/2-y)$, so $yu_x+u_y=u$, and the initial values hold.

The parameter map $(s,t)\mapsto(s+t^2/2,t)$ has Jacobian one and is globally invertible. Every point whose <characteristic curve> meets the initial segment therefore lies in the strip
$$
\boxed{1\leq x-\frac{y^2}2\leq2,\qquad -\infty<y<\infty.}
$$
The uniquely determined open region is its interior, with the boundary values determined on the two endpoint <characteristic curves>. Both signs of $y$ are included. Outside the strip, a general solution is $u=e^yF(x-y^2/2)$ with $F(s)=s$ only required for $1\leq s\leq2$. Smooth perturbations of $F$ supported outside that interval produce different solutions with the same prescribed data, proving the stated region is exact.

= Solution
{parent=i}

For a function for which the integral converges, the <Laplace transform> is
$$
\bar f(p)=\int_0^\infty e^{-pt}f(t)\,dt.
$$
For a <continuous> function of <exponential order> this exists in a sufficiently far right half-plane. For the first input,
$$
\boxed{\overline1(p)=\frac1p\qquad(\operatorname{Re}p>0).}
$$
This follows by evaluating the exponential antiderivative and using its decay at infinity.

= ii
{parent=9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

<Integration by parts>, whose boundary term vanishes for $\operatorname{Re}p>0$, gives
$$
\boxed{\overline t(p)=\int_0^\infty te^{-pt}\,dt=\frac1p\int_0^\infty e^{-pt}\,dt=\frac1{p^2}.}
$$

= iii
{parent=9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Combining the exponential factors gives
$$
\boxed{\overline{e^{-ct}}(p)=\int_0^\infty e^{-(p+c)t}\,dt=\frac1{p+c}\qquad(\operatorname{Re}(p+c)>0).}
$$

= iv
{parent=9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

The same <integration by parts> with $p$ replaced by $p+c$ gives
$$
\boxed{\overline{te^{-ct}}(p)=\frac1{(p+c)^2}\qquad(\operatorname{Re}(p+c)>0).}
$$
For the remaining unnumbered requests, the <derivative> and <convolution> formulas, under the usual convergence hypotheses, are
$$
\overline{f'}(p)=p\bar f(p)-f(0),\qquad\overline{f*g}(p)=\bar f(p)\bar g(p),\qquad(f*g)(t)=\int_0^tf(t-s)g(s)\,ds.
$$
Applying them to the general equation yields $p\bar x-a+\bar k\bar x=\bar y$ and therefore
$$
\boxed{\bar x(p)=\frac{\bar y(p)+a}{p+\bar k(p)}.}
$$
For the particular data, $\bar y=1/p$, $\bar k=1/(p+2)$ and $a=0$, so
$$
\bar x(p)=\frac{p+2}{p(p+1)^2}=\frac2p-\frac2{p+1}-\frac1{(p+1)^2}.
$$
The four transforms above give
$$
\boxed{x(t)=2-(2+t)e^{-t},\qquad\lim_{t\to\infty}x(t)=2.}
$$
As a direct check, $x(0)=0$, $x'=(1+t)e^{-t}$, and
$$
\int_0^te^{-2(t-s)}x(s)\,ds=1-(1+t)e^{-t}.
$$
Adding the last two expressions gives one, verifying the original equation as well as the required limit.
