= Solution

Choose a <basis> and let $A$ represent $T$. Define the <characteristic polynomial> by
$$
\boxed{\chi_T(t)=\det(tI-A).}
$$
Under a change of basis with invertible change matrix $S$, the new matrix is $S^{-1}AS$. Hence
$$
\det(tI-S^{-1}AS)=\det(S^{-1}(tI-A)S)
=\det(S)^{-1}\det(tI-A)\det(S)=\det(tI-A).
$$
Thus the polynomial is independent of the chosen coordinates. It is monic of degree $\dim V$.

To prove the sum is direct, suppose $\sum_{j=1}^rv_j=0$ with $v_j\in V_{\lambda_j}$. Fix $i$ and apply the polynomial in the <linear operator>
$$
P_i(T)=\prod_{j\neq i}(T-\lambda_jI).
$$
Every $v_j$ with $j\neq i$ is killed by one factor. On $v_i$, each factor is multiplication by $\lambda_i-\lambda_j$, giving
$$
0=P_i(T)\sum_jv_j=\left(\prod_{j\neq i}(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)\right)v_i.
$$
The scalar coefficient is nonzero because the <eigenvalues> are distinct. Thus $v_i=0$ for every $i$, proving
$$
\boxed{V_{\lambda_1}+\cdots+V_{\lambda_r}=V_{\lambda_1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{\lambda_r}.}
$$
In particular, choosing a basis in each <eigenspace> yields a <linearly independent> combined list.

If $\chi_T(\lambda)=0$, then $\det(\lambda I-A)=0$, so $\lambda I-A$ is singular. By the <rank-nullity theorem> it has a nonzero kernel vector $v$. Thus
$$
(\lambda I-A)v=0,\qquad\boxed{Tv=\lambda v\quad(v\neq0).}
$$
This is exactly the definition of an <eigenvalue>. Here $\lambda$ lies in the scalar field: for the stated real <vector space> it is real. A nonreal root instead gives an <eigenvalue> of the complexified operator, by the same argument over $\mathbb C$.

Expanding the <determinant> along the second column gives
$$
\chi_X(t)=(t+3)(t-b)(t-2).
$$
If $b\notin\{-3,2\}$, there are three distinct real <eigenvalues>. Part (b)(ii) gives a nonzero <eigenvector> for each, and part (b)(i) makes them independent. They form a basis, so the matrix is diagonalisable.

When $b=-3$,
$$
X+3I=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\2a&0&a\\10&0&5\end{pmatrix}
$$
has rank one for every $a$: the third row is nonzero, and the second row is $a/5$ times the third. Therefore the <eigenspace> for $-3$ has dimension two. The <eigenvalue> $2$ provides at least one further independent <eigenvector> by part (b), so again there is an <eigenbasis>.

When $b=2$,
$$
X-2I=\begin{pmatrix}-5&0&0\\2a&0&a\\10&0&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its rank is one if $a=0$ and two otherwise. Thus the <eigenspace> for $2$ has dimension two exactly when $a=0$. For $a\neq0$ it has dimension one, while the <eigenspace> for $-3$ also has dimension one, since $X+3I$ then has rank two. There are no other <eigenvalues> by the <characteristic polynomial>. A diagonal matrix in an <eigenbasis> would need three independent <eigenvectors>, whereas these two <eigenspaces> supply only two; diagonalisation is impossible. Conversely, at $a=0$ their dimensions add to three and part (b) makes their sum direct. Therefore
$$
\boxed{X_{a,b}\text{ is diagonalisable}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b\neq2\text{ or }a=0.}
$$
The argument uses only the <determinant>, rank-nullity, the established direct-sum result, and the definition of diagonalisation.

Use the convention that the <inner product> is linear in its first argument, as required by the displayed representation of a <linear functional>. Choose an <orthonormal basis> $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, for instance by <Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization>. Define
$$
v=\sum_{j=1}^n\overline{f(e_j)}e_j.
$$
For $u=\sum_ju_je_j$, conjugate linearity in the second argument gives
$$
\langle u,v\rangle=\sum_ju_jf(e_j)=f(u).
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{f(u)=\langle u,v\rangle\quad\text{for every }u\in V.}
$$
The representation is unique as well: if two vectors represent the same functional, their difference is orthogonal to every vector and therefore to itself, so it is zero.

The <adjoint operator> is characterized by
$$
\boxed{\langle Tu,v\rangle=\langle u,T^*v\rangle\quad\text{for all }u,v\in V.}
$$
For each fixed $v$, the map $u\mapsto\langle Tu,v\rangle$ is a <linear functional>. Part (i) gives a representing vector, which we define to be $T^*v$. Hence such a map exists on every vector of $V$. Part (b) proves that this constructed map is linear.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For scalars $\alpha,\beta$ and vectors $v,w,u$, use the adjoint identity and conjugate linearity in the second argument:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle u,T^*(\alpha v+\beta w)\rangle
&=\langle Tu,\alpha v+\beta w\rangle\\
&=\overline\alpha\langle Tu,v\rangle+\overline\beta\langle Tu,w\rangle\\
&=\overline\alpha\langle u,T^*v\rangle+\overline\beta\langle u,T^*w\rangle\\
&=\langle u,\alpha T^*v+\beta T^*w\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$
The difference of the two second-argument vectors is orthogonal to every $u$. Choosing $u$ to be that difference shows its squared <norm> is zero. Therefore
$$
\boxed{T^*(\alpha v+\beta w)=\alpha T^*v+\beta T^*w.}
$$
The two conjugations are why the adjoint is a linear map, despite the conjugate-linear dependence of the representing functional on its second argument.

The finite-dimensional <spectral theorem> says that a <self-adjoint operator> on a complex inner-product space has an <orthonormal basis> of <eigenvectors> and all its <eigenvalues> are real. Equivalently, its matrix is unitarily diagonalisable with real diagonal entries.

For $a\in\mathbb R$, the matrix is equal to its conjugate transpose:
$$
M_a^*=\begin{pmatrix}1&i&a\\-i&1&0\\a&0&1\end{pmatrix}=M_a.
$$
Thus it represents a self-adjoint operator for the standard <inner product> on $\mathbb C^3$. The theorem applies directly, proving
$$
\boxed{M_a\text{ is diagonalisable for every real }a.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Set $N=M_i-I$. Direct multiplication gives
$$
N=\begin{pmatrix}0&i&i\\-i&0&0\\i&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
N^2=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&1&1\\0&-1&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad N^3=0,
$$
with $N\neq0$. If $M_i$ were diagonalisable, then subtracting the identity in the same basis would diagonalise $N$. A diagonal matrix whose cube is zero has every diagonal entry zero and is itself zero. Similarity would then imply $N=0$, a contradiction. Hence
$$
\boxed{M_i\text{ is not diagonalisable}.}
$$
This proves failure directly; merely observing that it is not self-adjoint would not suffice, because non-self-adjoint matrices can still be diagonalisable.

An <ideal> $I$ of a commutative ring is an additive subgroup such that $rx\in I$ for every $r\in R$ and $x\in I$. A <prime ideal> is a proper <ideal> $A\neq R$ such that $xy\in A$ implies $x\in A$ or $y\in A$.

If $(x+A)(y+A)=0+A$ in the <quotient ring>, then $xy\in A$. Primality implies $x+A=0+A$ or $y+A=0+A$. Therefore there are no two nonzero cosets whose product is zero:
$$
\boxed{R/A\text{ has no nonzero zero-divisors}.}
$$
Properness also ensures its identity coset is not zero, so this quotient is an <integral domain>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The set $\sqrt I$ contains zero. If $x^m\in I$, then $(-x)^m=(-1)^mx^m\in I$, giving closure under additive inverses; also $(rx)^m=r^mx^m\in I$ for every $r\in R$.

To prove closure under addition, let $x^m,y^n\in I$. Expand $(x+y)^{m+n-1}$ by the <binomial theorem>. In every monomial $x^jy^{m+n-1-j}$, either $j\geq m$ or $m+n-1-j\geq n$. Thus each term contains a factor $x^m$ or $y^n$ and belongs to $I$. Hence $(x+y)^{m+n-1}\in I$, so $x+y\in\sqrt I$. The additive subgroup and absorption properties are proved, giving
$$
\boxed{\sqrt I\text{ is an ideal of }R.}
$$
This <ideal> is the <radical of an ideal>. The finite exponent for a sum uses commutativity of the ring.

= Solution
{parent=c}

A coset is a <nilpotent element> of the quotient exactly when some positive power is zero:
$$
x+J\text{ nilpotent}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ (x+J)^n=J\text{ for some }n\geq1
\ \Longleftrightarrow\ x\in\sqrt J.
$$
If $J=\sqrt J$, this forces every nilpotent coset to equal zero. Conversely, if there is no nonzero nilpotent coset, every $x\in\sqrt J$ has $x+J=0$, hence $x\in J$. Since $J\subseteq\sqrt J$ always, equality follows. Therefore
$$
\boxed{J=\sqrt J\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad R/J\text{ has no nonzero nilpotent elements}.}
$$
In standard terms, an <ideal> is radical exactly when its quotient is a <reduced ring>.

Certainly $A\subseteq\sqrt A$, by taking exponent one. If $x\in\sqrt A$, choose $n\geq1$ with $x^n\in A$. Primality applied to $x\cdot x^{n-1}$ gives $x\in A$ or $x^{n-1}\in A$. In the latter case repeat; induction reduces to $x\in A$. Thus $\sqrt A\subseteq A$ as well, proving
$$
\boxed{\sqrt A=A.}
$$
Every <prime ideal> is therefore a <radical ideal>.

For the preliminary definition, put $S_N(z)=\sum_{k=1}^Nf_k(z)$. The series converges <uniform convergence>[uniformly] to $f$ on $U$ when, for every $\epsilon>0$, some $N_0$ satisfies $|S_N(z)-f(z)|<\epsilon$ for all $z\in U$ and all $N\geq N_0$. Equivalently, $\sup_{z\in U}|S_N(z)-f(z)|\to0$.

Let $L(\gamma)$ be the finite length of the piecewise-smooth path. Finite linearity of the <contour integral> and the integral bound give
$$
\left|\sum_{k=1}^N\int_\gamma f_k(z)\,dz-\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz\right|
=\left|\int_\gamma(S_N-f)\,dz\right|
\leq L(\gamma)\sup_U|S_N-f|\longrightarrow0.
$$
The uniform limit is continuous by the stated allowed result, so its integral is defined. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_\gamma f_k(z)\,dz=\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz.}
$$

= Solution

<Cauchy's integral theorem> states that if a function is holomorphic on a simply connected open set, its integral around every closed piecewise-smooth path in that set is zero. A local form sufficient here is: if a function is holomorphic on an open set containing a triangle together with its interior, then
$$
\boxed{\int_{\partial\Delta}f(z)\,dz=0.}
$$
For a general domain with holes, an arbitrary closed path need not have zero integral; the triangle or simple-connectedness hypothesis supplies the required interior.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

<Morera's theorem> states that if $f$ is continuous on an open set $U$ and its integral around every triangle whose interior and boundary lie in $U$ is zero, then $f$ is holomorphic on $U$.

For a proof, choose a disk $B\subseteq U$ and a base point $z_0\in B$. Define $F(z)=\int_{[z_0,z]}f(\zeta)\,d\zeta$ along the straight segment. Convexity keeps every triangle with vertices $z_0,z,z+h$ inside $B$. Its zero boundary integral implies
$$
F(z+h)-F(z)=\int_{[z,z+h]}f(\zeta)\,d\zeta
=h\int_0^1f(z+th)\,dt.
$$
By continuity, the last integral tends to $f(z)$ as $h\to0$, so $F'(z)=f(z)$. Thus $F$ is holomorphic on $B$. The allowed result that the <derivative> of a <holomorphic function> is holomorphic now gives that $f=F'$ is holomorphic there. Such disks cover $U$, proving
$$
\boxed{f\text{ is holomorphic on }U.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=iv}

For each triangle compactly contained in $U$, part (i) permits the interchange of the series and its boundary integral. <Cauchy's integral theorem> makes every term zero. The uniform limit is continuous, so <Morera's theorem> yields that $f$ is holomorphic on $U$.

For the final application, each function $(z-k)^{-2}$ is holomorphic away from its pole. Fix a positive integer $n$. For $z\in U_n$ and $k>2n$,
$$
|z-k|\geq k-|z|>k-n\geq k/2,\qquad
\left|\frac1{(z-k)^2}\right|\leq\frac4{k^2}.
$$
The numerical series $\sum_{k>2n}4/k^2$ converges, so the <Weierstrass M-test> gives <uniform convergence> of the tail on $U_n$. Adding the finitely many initial terms preserves <uniform convergence>. Those initial terms need not be bounded near their excluded poles; only the tail needs the uniform bound. By the first conclusion, the full sum is holomorphic on each $U_n$. Their union is $\mathbb C\setminus\mathbb N$, hence
$$
\boxed{\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{(z-k)^2}\text{ is holomorphic on }\mathbb C\setminus\mathbb N.}
$$

= Solution

The <residue> at $a$ is the coefficient $c_{-1}$ of $(z-a)^{-1}$ in the <Laurent series>. A <simple pole> means $f(z)=c_{-1}/(z-a)+h(z)$ near $a$, with $c_{-1}\neq0$ and $h$ holomorphic across $a$.

For the specified positively oriented arc, direct parametrization gives $\int_{\gamma_\epsilon}(z-a)^{-1}\,dz=i(\beta-\alpha)$. The remaining integral is bounded by $\epsilon(\beta-\alpha)\sup|h|$, which tends to zero because $h$ is bounded near $a$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\int_{\gamma_\epsilon}f(z)\,dz\longrightarrow i(\beta-\alpha)\operatorname{res}(f;a).}
$$
The simple-pole hypothesis matters. For $f(z)=(z-a)^{-2}$, with $\alpha=0$, $\beta=\pi$, the <residue> is zero but
$$
\int_{\gamma_\epsilon}\frac{dz}{(z-a)^2}=\frac2\epsilon\longrightarrow\infty.
$$
For the particular function, expanding the exponential gives
$$
1+iz-e^{iz}=\frac{z^2}{2}+\frac{iz^3}{6}+O(z^4),\qquad
f(z)=\frac1{2z}+\frac i6+O(z).
$$
Thus $\boxed{\operatorname{res}(f;0)=1/2}$, and the pole is simple. On the large upper semicircle, $|e^{iz}|\leq1$, giving
$$
\left|\int_{\Gamma_R}f(z)\,dz\right|
\leq\pi R\frac{R+2}{R^3}=\frac{\pi(R+2)}{R^2}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Close the real-axis path from $-R$ to $-\epsilon$ and $\epsilon$ to $R$ by this upper arc and a clockwise upper indentation at zero. The function is holomorphic in the indented region, so the sum of all four integrals is zero. The clockwise indentation tends to $-i\pi/2$. For real $x$,
$$
f(x)=\frac{1-\cos x}{x^3}+i\frac{x-\sin x}{x^3}.
$$
The real part is odd and cancels on the symmetric real pieces; the imaginary part is even. Its integral converges since $(x-\sin x)/x^3\to1/6$ at zero and is $O(x^{-2})$ at infinity. Letting $\epsilon\to0$ and $R\to\infty$ therefore yields $2iI-i\pi/2=0$, where $I$ is the requested real integral. Hence
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty\frac{x-\sin x}{x^3}\,dx=\frac\pi4.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=6}

A <conformal map> preserves angles between intersecting regular curves, including their orientation. If $f$ is holomorphic with $f'(a)\neq0$, the image tangent vectors are $f'(a)$ times the original tangent vectors. Multiplication by this nonzero complex number gives a common positive scaling and rotation, so their oriented angle is unchanged. This proves conformality throughout the stated domain.

At a zero <derivative> this can fail: $f(z)=z^2$ at zero sends rays of arguments $0$ and $\pi/4$ to rays of arguments $0$ and $\pi/2$. The angle is doubled, so this <holomorphic function> is not conformal there.

For the disk map $C(z)=i(1+z)/(1-z)$, compute
$$
\operatorname{Im}C(z)=\frac{1-|z|^2}{|1-z|^2}>0,
\qquad C'(z)=\frac{2i}{(1-z)^2}\neq0.
$$
Its inverse is $z=(w-i)/(w+i)$; for $\operatorname{Im}w>0$, the inequality $|w-i|<|w+i|$ puts this inverse in the unit disk. Both rational maps are holomorphic on their respective domains. Thus
$$
\boxed{C:D\to\mathbb H\text{ is a conformal bijection}.}
$$
For the final region, writing $z=x+iy$ gives $|e^z|=e^x\in(0,1)$ and $\arg(e^z)=y\in(0,\pi)$. Hence its exponential image is exactly the upper half of the unit disk,
$$
V=\{w:|w|<1,\ \operatorname{Im}w>0\}.
$$
The exponential is <injective> on this strip, since two equal exponential values differ by $2\pi i$ times an integer while the imaginary parts here differ by less than $\pi$. Its <derivative> never vanishes and its inverse on $V$ is the logarithm with argument in $(0,\pi)$.

Now $L(w)=(1+w)/(1-w)$ maps $V$ conformally onto the first quadrant. Indeed
$$
\operatorname{Re}L(w)=\frac{1-|w|^2}{|1-w|^2}>0,\qquad
\operatorname{Im}L(w)=\frac{2\operatorname{Im}w}{|1-w|^2}>0,
$$
and the inverse $(s-1)/(s+1)$ has modulus below one and positive imaginary part for every first-quadrant $s$. Squaring maps that quadrant bijectively onto $\mathbb H$, with nonzero <derivative>. Therefore a required composition is
$$
\boxed{F(z)=\left(\frac{1+e^z}{1-e^z}\right)^2.}
$$
Every component is a conformal bijection between the stated domains, so the composition is one as well.

First prove the general uniqueness claim. The bound $Mh\leq k$ prevents a solution from leaving the dependent-variable interval before $|x-a|=h$: up to any first exit, $|y(x)-b|\leq M|x-a|\leq k$. Let $y_1,y_2$ be two solutions with the same initial value. Their integral equations and the <Lipschitz condition> give, for $x\geq a$,
$$
|y_1(x)-y_2(x)|\leq A\int_a^x|y_1(s)-y_2(s)|\,ds.
$$
Since their difference is bounded by $2k$, repeated substitution into this inequality proves, for every integer $n\geq0$,
$$
|y_1(x)-y_2(x)|\leq2k\frac{(A|x-a|)^n}{n!}.
$$
The same argument with reversed limits works for $x\leq a$. Letting $n\to\infty$ forces the difference to zero on the whole interval. This proves at most one solution without assuming that $Ah<1$.

For case (i), choose a rectangle around $(0,1)$ that stays in $y>0$. There $f_y=(2/3)xy^{-1/3}$ is bounded, giving a uniform Lipschitz constant by the mean value theorem. For example $h=k=1/2$ works: $M\leq\tfrac12(3/2)^{2/3}$ and $Mh<1/2$. Separate variables using $u=y^{1/3}$, obtaining $3u'=x$. The initial condition gives $u=1+x^2/6$, hence
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\left(1+\frac{x^2}{6}\right)^3.}
$$
It remains positive for all real $x$, so the same local uniqueness argument continues along this solution.

No rectangle of positive width around $(0,0)$ has a uniform Lipschitz constant in $y$. For any nonzero $x$ in such a rectangle,

= Solution
{parent=8}

For a nonzero second-order principal part, the discriminant is $Q^2-PR$: positive gives a <hyperbolic partial differential equation>, negative an <elliptic partial differential equation>, and zero the <parabolic type> when the principal part has rank one. If all three coefficients vanish, the equation is instead degenerate to lower order.

Here $P=x$, $2Q=x^2$, $R=0$, so $Q^2-PR=x^4/4>0$ for $x>0$. The characteristic equation is
$$
P\,dy^2-2Q\,dx\,dy+R\,dx^2=x\,dy(dy-x\,dx)=0.
$$
Thus choose
$$
\boxed{\xi=y,\qquad\eta=y-\frac{x^2}{2}.}
$$
Their Jacobian <determinant> with respect to $(x,y)$ is $x\neq0$, so they are valid coordinates on the specified half-plane. The level sets are horizontal lines and the right-hand halves of parabolas $y=x^2/2+\eta$.

\Image[2011-ac2-characteristics.png]
{title=The two characteristic families for the hyperbolic equation}

Writing $z(x,y)=Z(\xi,\eta)$, the <chain rule> gives
$$
z_x=-xZ_\eta,\quad z_{xx}=-Z_\eta+x^2Z_{\eta\eta},\quad
z_{xy}=-x(Z_{\xi\eta}+Z_{\eta\eta}).
$$
The original equation reduces to $-xZ_\eta-x^3Z_{\xi\eta}=-xZ_\eta+x^3$, hence the canonical form is $\boxed{Z_{\xi\eta}=-1}$. Integrating once in each characteristic variable gives
$$
Z=f(\xi)+g(\eta)-\xi\eta,
$$
or
$$
\boxed{z=f(y)+g(y-x^2/2)-y^2+yx^2/2.}
$$
On $x=1$, differentiation gives $z_x=y-g'(y-1/2)$. The specified <derivative> therefore forces $g'=0$ for every argument, so $g$ is a constant. The specified value then gives $f(y)+g=1+y^2-y/2$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{z(x,y)=1+\frac y2(x^2-1).}
$$
Its derivatives are $z_x=xy$, $z_{xx}=y$ and $z_{xy}=x$, which verify both the equation and the data directly.

The <Laplace transform> is
$$
\overline y(p)=\int_0^\infty e^{-pt}y(t)\,dt.
$$
A sufficient condition is that $y$ be piecewise continuous on each bounded interval and of exponential order $c$, meaning $|y(t)|\leq Ce^{ct}$ for all sufficiently large $t$. Then the integral is absolutely convergent for $\operatorname{Re}p>c$, by comparison with $e^{-(\operatorname{Re}p-c)t}$.

For the constant function, direct integration gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L[1](p)=\frac1p\quad(\operatorname{Re}p>0).}
$$

Combine the exponentials and integrate:

\qquad\operatorname{Re}p>-a.}
$$
The parameter $a>0$ makes this convergence half-plane larger than the one for the constant function.

= iii
{parent=9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Integration by parts, where the transform boundary term at infinity vanishes, gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L[\dot y](p)=p\overline y(p)-y(0).}
$$
For a sufficiently regular function with the required exponential growth bounds, apply this again to $\dot y$ to deduce
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L[\ddot y](p)=p^2\overline y(p)-py(0)-\dot y(0).}
$$
These identities hold in a right half-plane where the displayed transforms converge; the regular solutions obtained below satisfy those requirements.

= iv
{parent=9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Differentiating the transform with respect to $p$ gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L[ty](p)=-\frac d{dp}\overline y(p).}
$$
Applying this to $\mathcal L[1]=1/p$ also gives $\boxed{\mathcal L[t]=1/p^2}$ for $\operatorname{Re}p>0$.

Now put $Y=\overline y$, $y_0=y(0)$ and $v_0=\dot y(0)$. Transforming each term of the differential equation, and using the <derivative> identities, gives
$$
-\frac d{dp}(p^2Y-py_0-v_0)-\frac d{dp}(pY-y_0)+(pY-y_0)+Y=\frac1{p^2}.
$$
The initial-value terms cancel, leaving
$$
-p(p+1)Y'-pY=\frac1{p^2},\qquad
\frac d{dp}\{(p+1)Y\}=-\frac1{p^3}.
$$
Integrating once yields $(p+1)Y=C+1/(2p^2)$. Partial fractions give
$$
Y=\frac C{p+1}+\frac1{2p^2(p+1)}
=\frac{C+1/2}{p+1}-\frac1{2p}+\frac1{2p^2}.
$$
The transform pairs therefore imply, on writing $A=C+1/2$,
$$
\boxed{y(t)=Ae^{-t}+\frac{t-1}{2}.}
$$
For the final parameter count, the differential equation is the <derivative> of $t(\dot y+y)$: it says $[t(\dot y+y)]'=t$. Integrating gives $t(\dot y+y)=t^2/2+B$. A regular solution at $t=0$ has finite $y$ and $\dot y$, forcing $B=0$. Thus $\dot y+y=t/2$ is first order and leaves only one constant. Equivalently, the original equation at zero requires $\dot y(0)+y(0)=0$, so the two usual initial values cannot be chosen independently. The displayed solution satisfies this constraint and verifies the original equation directly.
