Write $V^*=V'$ for the <dual space>. Define $\theta(v)(f)=f(v)$. For fixed $v$, this is a <linear functional> on $V^*$; evaluation is also <linear> in $v$, so $\theta:V\to V^{**}$ is a <linear map>. If $v\ne0$, extend $v$ to a <basis> and take its first coordinate <linear functional> $f$; then $f(v)=1$. Thus $\theta$ is <injective>. A <dual basis> has the same number of elements as the original <basis>, so $\dim V=\dim V^*=\dim V^{**}$. The <rank-nullity theorem> now makes $\theta$ an <linear isomorphism>.

The <annihilator of a vector subspace> of a <linear subspace> $U\le V$ is
$$
U^\circ=\{f\in V^*: f(u)=0\text{ for every }u\in U\}.
$$
Extend a <basis> $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ of $U$ to a <basis> $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ of $V$, with <dual basis> $f_1,\ldots,f_n$. A <linear functional> belongs to $U^\circ$ exactly when its first $k$ coordinates in this <dual basis> vanish. Hence $U^\circ$ has <basis> $f_{k+1},\ldots,f_n$, proving
$$
\boxed{\dim U+\dim U^\circ=\dim V.}
$$
The <dual map> is $\alpha^*(h)=h\circ\alpha$; here this is the algebraic <dual map>, denoted $\alpha'$ in the source. If $v\in\ker\alpha$, then $(\alpha'h)(v)=h(\alpha v)=0$. Consequently $\operatorname{im}\alpha'\subseteq(\ker\alpha)^\circ$. Moreover,
$$
\theta(v)\in(\operatorname{im}\alpha')^\circ
\iff h(\alpha v)=0\text{ for every }h\in W^*
\iff\alpha v=0,
$$
because coordinate <linear functionals> separate <vectors>. This proves $(\operatorname{im}\alpha')^\circ=\theta(\ker\alpha)$, including the requested second inclusion. Taking <dimensions> gives
$$
\dim\operatorname{im}\alpha'=\dim V-\dim\ker\alpha=\dim\operatorname{im}\alpha.
$$
The first inclusion has equal <dimensions> on both sides, so
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{im}\alpha'=(\ker\alpha)^\circ,\qquad
(\operatorname{im}\alpha')^\circ=\theta(\ker\alpha).}
$$
For a <matrix> $A=(a_{ij})$ representing $\alpha$, the matrix of $\alpha'$ in the corresponding <dual bases> is $A^{\mathsf T}$: its $ji$ entry is $h_i(\alpha v_j)=a_{ij}$. Thus the <column rank> of $A^{\mathsf T}$ is the <row rank> of $A$, while the <column rank> of $A$ is $\dim\operatorname{im}\alpha$. Applying the established equality to these two <linear maps> proves **row rank equals column rank** over every <field>.

The <adjoint on an inner product space> $\alpha^*:V\to V$ is characterized by
$$
\langle\alpha v,w\rangle=\langle v,\alpha^*w\rangle\qquad(v,w\in V).
$$
Here is a finite-dimensional construction that supplies every required <basis> fact. If $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ is any <basis>, its coordinate <linear functionals> form a <basis> of $V^*$: a <linear functional> is uniquely specified by its values on these $n$ <vectors>. Hence $\dim V^*=n$. The <linear map>
$$
J:V\to V^*,\qquad J(w)(v)=\langle v,w\rangle
$$
is <injective>, because $J(w)=0$ implies $\langle w,w\rangle=0$, and <positive definiteness> implies $w=0$. Equal <dimensions> make $J$ an <linear isomorphism>. If $\alpha'$ is the <dual map>, then
$$
\alpha^*=J^{-1}\alpha'J.
$$
This establishes existence and <linearity>. If two possible <vectors> $u,w$ represent the same <linear functional> through the <inner product>, then $J(u-w)=0$, so $u=w$. Applied to every argument, this proves uniqueness of the <adjoint on an inner product space>.

For <linear maps> $S,T$, two applications of the defining <inner product> identity give
$$
\langle STv,w\rangle=\langle Tv,S^*w\rangle=\langle v,T^*S^*w\rangle.
$$
Uniqueness therefore gives $(ST)^*=T^*S^*$. Likewise $(S+T)^*=S^*+T^*$, $(cT)^*=cT^*$ for real $c$, and $I^*=I$. An <induction> gives $(\alpha^n)^*=(\alpha^*)^n$, and applying additivity to a <polynomial> with real coefficients gives
$$
\boxed{f(\alpha)^*=f(\alpha^*).}
$$
Symmetry of the real <inner product> also gives $(\alpha^*)^*=\alpha$. If $m$ is the <minimal polynomial> of $\alpha$, the displayed identity gives $m(\alpha^*)=0$, so the <minimal polynomial> of $\alpha^*$ divides $m$. Applying the same argument to $\alpha^*$ gives reverse divisibility. Both <polynomials> are <monic>, hence **their minimal polynomials are equal**.

The distinct <eigenvalues> are exactly the roots of the <minimal polynomial> in the chosen <field>. An <eigenvector> $v$ with <eigenvalue> $\lambda$ satisfies $m(\alpha)v=m(\lambda)v$, so $m(\lambda)=0$. Conversely, if $m(X)=(X-\lambda)q(X)$ and $\alpha-\lambda I$ were a <linear isomorphism>, then $m(\alpha)=0$ would imply $q(\alpha)=0$, contradicting minimal degree. Thus $\alpha-\lambda I$ is not <injective>, giving an <eigenvector>. It follows that $\alpha$ and $\alpha^*$ have **the same distinct eigenvalues**. This includes the complex <eigenvalues> after <complexification of a real vector space>; over the real <field> it states equality of their real <eigenvalues>.

A <maximal ideal> is a proper <ideal> $I$ of $R$ such that an <ideal> $J$ with $I\subseteq J\subseteq R$ is either $I$ or $R$. Suppose first that $I$ is a <maximal ideal>. If $a\notin I$, then $I+(a)=R$, so $1=i+ra$ for some $i\in I$ and $r\in R$. In the <quotient ring>, $(r+I)(a+I)=1+I$. Every nonzero element of $R/I$ therefore has a <multiplicative inverse>, so $R/I$ is a <field>. Conversely, if $R/I$ is a <field> and $J$ properly contains $I$, choose $a\in J\setminus I$. The element $a+I$ has a <multiplicative inverse> $r+I$, whence $1-ra\in I\subset J$, giving $1\in J$ and $J=R$. Thus
$$
\boxed{I\text{ is maximal}\iff R/I\text{ is a field}.}
$$
The squares in $\mathbb F_7$ are $0,1,2,4$, which do not include $-1=6$. Hence $X^2+1$ has no root in $\mathbb F_7$. A <polynomial> of degree two or three over a <field> is an <irreducible polynomial> exactly when it has no root: any proper <polynomial factorization> of such a degree has a factor of degree one. The <polynomial ring> over a <field> is a <Euclidean domain>, so the <ideal> generated by an <irreducible polynomial> is a <maximal ideal>. It follows that
$$
F=\mathbb F_7[X]/(X^2+1)
$$
is a <field>. Every <coset> has a unique representative $a+bX$, by <polynomial division>, so $|F|=7^2=49$.

Let $j$ be the <coset> of $X$ in this <field>. Since $j^2=-1$, the map
$$
\phi:\mathbb Z[i]\to F,\qquad a+bi\longmapsto\overline a+\overline b\,j
$$
is a <ring homomorphism>. It is <surjective> by the representative description. Since $1,j$ are <linearly independent> over $\mathbb F_7$, its <kernel of a ring homomorphism> is
$$
\boxed{\ker\phi=7\mathbb Z[i]=(7).}
$$
There is also a <field> with $343$ elements. The cubes in $\mathbb F_7$ are $0,1,-1$, so $X^3-2$ has no root and is an <irreducible polynomial>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbb F_7[X]/(X^3-2)}
$$
is a <field> whose unique representatives have degree below three. There are $7^3=343$ such representatives.

Use the following boundary version of <Cauchy's integral theorem>: if a function is <holomorphic> on a neighborhood of a compact region bounded by finitely many piecewise smooth simple closed curves, its integral around the positively oriented boundary is zero. On an <annulus> this means that its integrals around the two circles, both taken counterclockwise, are equal.

For $0<\rho<r$, the function $f(z)/(z-w)$ is <holomorphic> on a neighborhood of $\rho\le|z-w|\le r$. The theorem therefore gives
$$
\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|z-w|=r}\frac{f(z)}{z-w}\,dz
=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(w+\rho e^{it})\,dt.
$$
As $\rho\downarrow0$, continuity of $f$ makes the integrand converge uniformly to $f(w)$. The right side tends to $f(w)$, proving <Cauchy integral formula> without assuming that formula in an intermediate step.

Let $I$ denote the requested boundary integral. Parametrize the <unit circle> by $z=e^{it}$. Since $dz=ie^{it}dt$,
$$
I=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}
\frac{\overline{f(e^{it})}}{1-ae^{-it}}\,dt.
$$
Taking <complex conjugates> and using $\overline{e^{it}}=e^{-it}$ gives
$$
\overline I=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}
\frac{f(e^{it})}{1-\overline a e^{it}}\,dt
=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=1}\frac{f(z)}{z(1-\overline a z)}\,dz.
$$
This is the <conjugated Cauchy integral on a circle> identity. The function $h(z)=f(z)/(1-\overline a z)$ is <holomorphic> on a neighborhood of the closed <unit disc>, because $|a|<1$. Applying <Cauchy integral formula> at zero gives $\overline I=h(0)=f(0)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{I=\overline{f(0)}.}
$$
The <complex conjugates> in these formulas are essential; the boundary integrand contains $\overline{f(z)}$.

For a <holomorphic function> on $|z-a|<R$, the complex <Taylor theorem> states
$$
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(z-a)^n\qquad(|z-a|<R),
$$
with uniform convergence on each smaller closed <disc>. Here is a proof. Choose $r<\rho<R$. For $|z-a|\le r$ and $|\zeta-a|=\rho$, the <geometric series>
$$
\frac1{\zeta-z}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(z-a)^n}{(\zeta-a)^{n+1}}
$$
converges uniformly. Substitute it into <Cauchy integral formula> and integrate term by term to get
$$
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n(z-a)^n,\qquad
c_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta-a|=\rho}\frac{f(\zeta)}{(\zeta-a)^{n+1}}\,d\zeta.
$$
If $M_\rho=\max_{|\zeta-a|=\rho}|f(\zeta)|$, then $|c_n|\le M_\rho\rho^{-n}$. These bounds justify differentiating the <power series> any fixed number of times on a smaller closed <disc>, by comparison with convergent differentiated <geometric series>. Evaluation at $a$ gives $f^{(n)}(a)=n!c_n$. Since every $|z-a|<R$ belongs to some such smaller <disc>, the formula holds throughout the stated <domain>.

The <identity theorem> says that two <holomorphic functions> on a connected open subset of $\mathbb C$ are equal everywhere if their equality set has an accumulation point in that subset. Equivalently, a <holomorphic function> with an interior accumulation point of zeros is identically zero there.

The proposed <Taylor coefficients> at $i$, including the specified value for $n=0$, give
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n(z-i)^n}{i^{n+1}}
=\frac{1/i}{1+(z-i)/i}=\frac1z\qquad(|z-i|<1).
$$
The function $1/z$ is <holomorphic> on $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$ and has exactly the given <derivatives>, so it supplies existence. Any other admissible <holomorphic function> has the same <Taylor series> on $|z-i|<1$. The punctured plane is connected, so the <identity theorem> gives uniqueness:
$$
\boxed{f(z)=\frac1z\quad(z\ne0).}
$$

On the <unit circle>, put $z=e^{i\theta}$. Then $d\theta=dz/(iz)$ and $\cos\theta=(z+z^{-1})/2$, so
$$
\frac{d\theta}{(2-\cos\theta)^2}
=\frac4i\frac{z\,dz}{(z^2-4z+1)^2}.
$$
Write $r=2-\sqrt3$ and $s=2+\sqrt3$. Only $r$ lies inside the <unit circle>; the <rational function> $h(z)=z/[(z-r)^2(z-s)^2]$ has a double <pole> there. Its <residue> is
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=r}h
=\left.\frac{d}{dz}\frac{z}{(z-s)^2}\right|_{z=r}
=\frac1{(r-s)^2}-\frac{2r}{(r-s)^3}
=-\frac{r+s}{(r-s)^3}=\frac1{6\sqrt3}.
$$
By the <residue theorem>,
$$
\boxed{\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{(2-\cos\theta)^2}
=\frac4i\,2\pi i\frac1{6\sqrt3}=\frac{4\pi}{3\sqrt3}.}
$$

Choose the branch $\operatorname{Log}z=\log|z|+i\arg z$ with $-\pi/2<\arg z<3\pi/2$. It is <holomorphic> off the downward imaginary ray and takes values $\log x$ on the positive real axis and $\log x+i\pi$ at $-x$ for $x>0$.

Take $R>2$ and $0<\varepsilon<2$. The positively oriented <contour> consists of the real segments $[-R,-\varepsilon]$ and $[\varepsilon,R]$, the small upper semicircle from $-\varepsilon$ to $\varepsilon$ traversed clockwise, and the large upper semicircle from $R$ to $-R$ traversed counterclockwise. For $F(z)=\operatorname{Log}z/(4+z^2)$, the only enclosed <pole> is $2i$, with
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=2i}F=\frac{\log2+i\pi/2}{4i}.
$$
The integral over the large arc is $O(\log R/R)$, since its length is $\pi R$ and $|4+z^2|\ge R^2-4$. The small arc contributes $O(\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|)$, since its length is $\pi\varepsilon$ and $|4+z^2|\ge4-\varepsilon^2$. Both tend to zero.

Let $J=\int_0^\infty\log x/(4+x^2)\,dx$; it converges because $|\log x|$ is integrable near zero and $\log x/x^2$ is integrable at infinity. The two real segments, after substituting $z=-x$ on the negative one, tend to
$$
2J+i\pi\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{4+x^2}=2J+\frac{i\pi^2}{4}.
$$
The <residue theorem> therefore gives
$$
2J+\frac{i\pi^2}{4}
=2\pi i\frac{\log2+i\pi/2}{4i}
=\frac\pi2\log2+\frac{i\pi^2}{4}.
$$
Taking real parts yields
$$
\boxed{J=\frac\pi4\log2.}
$$

Use <Picard iteration> on $[a-h,a+h]$:
$$
u_0(x)=b,\qquad u_{n+1}(x)=b+\int_a^x f(t,u_n(t))\,dt.
$$
By <induction>, every iterate stays in the prescribed rectangle: if $u_n$ does, then $|u_{n+1}(x)-b|\le M|x-a|\le Mh\le k$. Continuity makes each next integral well defined. The <Lipschitz condition> gives, for $n\ge0$,
$$
|u_{n+1}(x)-u_n(x)|\le\frac{MA^n|x-a|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}.
$$
For $n=0$ this is the previous bound; integration of the bound multiplied by $A$ proves the next step, on either side of $a$.

The sum of these bounds converges uniformly on the entire interval. Thus $u_n$ converges uniformly to a continuous $u$, whose graph remains in the closed rectangle. The <Lipschitz condition> also makes $f(t,u_n(t))$ converge uniformly to $f(t,u(t))$, so passage to the limit gives
$$
u(x)=b+\int_a^x f(t,u(t))\,dt.
$$
The <fundamental theorem of calculus> makes $u$ a continuously differentiable solution with $u(a)=b$.

For uniqueness, let $u,v$ be two solutions and put $D_0=\sup|u-v|$ on the interval. Repeated use of the <Lipschitz condition> in their integral equations gives
$$
|u(x)-v(x)|\le D_0\frac{(A|x-a|)^n}{n!}
$$
for every $n$. Letting $n\to\infty$ gives $u=v$. The global bound $M$ prevents any competing solution whose graph lies in $D$ from exiting the rectangle through a horizontal boundary before the end of the interval. Therefore **a unique solution exists on the full stated interval**. No extra restriction $Ah<1$ is needed; the factorial estimates replace that restriction.

First consider the usual local reduction with sufficiently smooth coefficients. The <hyperbolic partial differential equation> has two distinct characteristic directions because $S^2-RT>0$. Choose independent local <characteristic coordinates> $\xi,\eta$ whose gradients satisfy
$$
R\xi_x^2+2S\xi_x\xi_y+T\xi_y^2=0,\qquad
R\eta_x^2+2S\eta_x\eta_y+T\eta_y^2=0.
$$
Let $J=\xi_x\eta_y-\xi_y\eta_x\ne0$ and
$$
B=R\xi_x\eta_x+S(\xi_x\eta_y+\xi_y\eta_x)+T\xi_y\eta_y.
$$
The determinant of the transformed principal coefficient matrix gives $B^2=J^2(S^2-RT)>0$. By the <chain rule>, the left side of the original <partial differential equation> becomes
$$
2B\,u_{\xi\eta}+A_\xi u_\xi+A_\eta u_\eta,
\qquad A_\xi=R\xi_{xx}+2S\xi_{xy}+T\xi_{yy},
$$
with the analogous expression for $A_\eta$. The original first <derivatives> are $z_x=u_\xi\xi_x+u_\eta\eta_x$ and $z_y=u_\xi\xi_y+u_\eta\eta_y$. Substitution and division by $2B$ yield the requested mixed-derivative canonical form. The construction is local, in a neighborhood where the two characteristic foliations give a <diffeomorphism>.

There is a regularity qualification in the printed hypotheses: merely continuous coefficients need not give twice continuously differentiable <characteristic coordinates>. Such coordinates are needed for the second-derivative <chain rule> above. For example, take $R=0$, $S=1/2$ and $T=|x|$ near the origin. The characteristic equation factors as $\xi_y(\xi_x+|x|\xi_y)=0$. One family is $x=\text{constant}$ and the other has first integrals $\eta=h(y-x|x|/2)$. For an independent coordinate $h'\ne0$, but $\eta_{xx}$ has opposite one-sided limits at $x=0$. Hence no twice continuously differentiable characteristic coordinate system exists there. This illustrates that <continuous hyperbolic coefficients need not admit smooth characteristic coordinates>. The formal general argument requires additional smoothness, for example twice continuously differentiable coefficients. The concrete coefficients below are smooth, so this issue does not affect their solution.

For the concrete <partial differential equation>, put
$$
\xi=\frac{x}{y^2},\qquad\eta=y\quad(y>0).
$$
The <Jacobian determinant> is $1/y^2\ne0$. Direct use of the <chain rule> gives
$$
z_{xx}=\frac{u_{\xi\xi}}{y^4},\qquad
z_{xy}=-\frac{2\xi u_{\xi\xi}}{y^3}+\frac{u_{\xi\eta}}{y^2}-\frac{2u_\xi}{y^3}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{u_{\xi\eta}=\frac2\eta u_\xi.}
$$
To impose the data efficiently, let $v=z_x$. Then $2xv_x+yv_y=0$, whose <characteristic curves> preserve $x/y^2$, so $v=H(x/y^2)$. On the initial line $x=1$, the value $v(1,y)=y^2$ gives $H(\xi)=1/\xi$ for every $\xi>0$. Thus $z_x=y^2/x$, and integration in $x$ gives $z=y^2\log x+C(y)$. The remaining initial condition gives $C(y)=0$:
$$
\boxed{z(x,y)=y^2\log x,\qquad x>0,\ y>0.}
$$
Every point in this region is reached from the data by a characteristic with positive $x/y^2$. The line $x=0$ separates these characteristics from those with $x<0$, and the logarithmic divergence prevents a smooth extension of this solution through $x=0$. The original condition restricts us to $y>0$.

A sufficient condition for existence of the <Laplace transform> is that $y$ be piecewise continuous on each bounded interval and have <exponential order>: $|y(t)|\le Ce^{at}$ for sufficiently large $t$. The defining integral then converges absolutely for real $p>a$, and more generally defines a <holomorphic function> for complex $p$ with $\operatorname{Re}p>a$. The boundary $p=a$ may or may not be included, depending on $y$.

Write $Y(p)=\mathcal L[y](p)$. To solve the <differential equation> by <Laplace transform>, initially seek a sufficiently regular solution with $y(0)=1$ and finite $y'(0)=b$. The <Laplace transform of a derivative> gives
$$
\mathcal L[y']=pY-1,\qquad \mathcal L[y'']=p^2Y-p-b.
$$
Using $\mathcal L[ty]=-Y'$ from part (i), the transformed <differential equation> becomes
$$
-(2pY+p^2Y'-1)-Y-pY'-(pY-1)-Y=0,
$$
or
$$
p(p+1)Y'+(3p+2)Y=2.
$$
After division by $p(p+1)$, an <integrating factor> is $p^2(p+1)$, since $(3p+2)/(p(p+1))=2/p+1/(p+1)$. Consequently
$$
\frac d{dp}\bigl[p^2(p+1)Y\bigr]=2p,\qquad
Y=\frac1{p+1}+\frac{C}{p^2(p+1)}.
$$
For a bounded $y$, $|Y(p)|\le\|y\|_\infty/p$ for $p>0$. Letting $p\downarrow0$ forces $C=0$, an instance of how <boundedness selects a Laplace transform at a singular endpoint>. By the exponential formula in part (iii), this yields
$$
\boxed{y(t)=e^{-t}.}
$$
Direct substitution checks the <differential equation>, boundedness and the required limit.

To complete the uniqueness argument, no unproved endpoint regularity is needed. The two functions $e^{-t}$ and $t-1$ solve the homogeneous <linear differential equation> on $(0,\infty)$, and their <Wronskian> is $te^{-t}\ne0$. They form a <fundamental system of solutions> on that interval, so every classical solution is $Ae^{-t}+B(t-1)$. Boundedness forces $B=0$ and the limit at zero forces $A=1$. Thus **the bounded solution with the specified limit is unique**. The limit alone would not suffice: $e^{-t}+C(t-1+e^{-t})$ has the same limit for every $C$, but is unbounded when $C\ne0$.

= Solution
{parent=2007/9/iii}

Integrating the exponential directly gives
$$
\mathcal L[e^{-nt}](p)=\int_0^\infty e^{-(p+n)t}\,dt
=\boxed{\frac1{p+n}},\qquad\operatorname{Re}p>-n.
$$
The inequality is the absolute-convergence domain of this <Laplace transform>.
