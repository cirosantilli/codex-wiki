= Solution
{parent=2008/1/i}

Proceed by induction on $n=\dim V$. The zero-dimensional case is immediate. Over $\mathbb C$, the <fundamental theorem of algebra> supplies a root $\lambda$ of the <characteristic polynomial>. Since $\det(T-\lambda I)=0$, there is a nonzero vector $v_1$ with $Tv_1=\lambda v_1$. The line $W=\mathbb Cv_1$ is invariant, so $T$ induces a <linear map> $\overline T$ on $V/W$. By induction, choose an upper triangular <basis> for that quotient and lift its vectors to $v_2,\ldots,v_n$. Then $Tv_j\in\operatorname{span}(v_1,\ldots,v_j)$ for every $j$. Thus $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ is the required <basis> and $\boxed{T\text{ is triangularizable}}$.

The same conclusion holds over $F$ when the <characteristic polynomial> splits over $F$. Indeed an <eigenvalue> then lies in $F$, and the <block matrix> arising from the invariant line gives $\chi_T(X)=(X-\lambda)\chi_{\overline T}(X)$. The quotient <characteristic polynomial> therefore also splits, so the induction works over $F$ without requiring <algebraic closure>.

= Solution
{parent=2008/1/ii}

Write $N=T-I$. <Cayley-Hamilton theorem> gives $N^3=0$. Its <minimal polynomial> is therefore $X^k$ for exactly one $k\in\{1,2,3\}$, so the possibilities for that of $T$ are
$$
\boxed{X-1,\quad(X-1)^2,\quad(X-1)^3.}
$$
Let $d=\dim\ker N$, which is the <dimension> of the only <eigenspace>. If $d=3$, then $N=0$ and $T=I$.

If $d=2$, <rank-nullity theorem> gives $\dim\operatorname{im}N=1$. The restriction of the nilpotent map $N$ to this invariant line is zero, so $N^2=0$. Choose $v_3$ with $v_2=Nv_3\ne0$, and choose $v_1\in\ker N$ independent of $v_2$. Then $v_3\notin\ker N$, so these three vectors form a <basis>; its <matrix> for $T$ is the printed $J_1(1)\oplus J_2(1)$.

If $d=1$, then $\operatorname{im}N$ has <dimension> two, so $N^2$ cannot vanish: otherwise $\operatorname{im}N\subseteq\ker N$. Choose $v_3$ with $N^2v_3\ne0$ and set $v_2=Nv_3$, $v_1=N^2v_3$. Applying $N^2$ and then $N$ to a putative linear relation proves these three vectors independent. The <matrix> is the printed $J_3(1)$.

These cases exhaust the possible <eigenspace> dimensions. They are pairwise dissimilar because similarity preserves the <eigenspace> <dimension>, equivalently the three different minimal <polynomials>. Hence every <matrix> is similar to **exactly one** of the three displayed forms.

= Solution

The functional $\ell(u)=\langle u,v\rangle$ is linear and nonzero because $\ell(v)=\langle v,v\rangle>0$. It therefore has rank one, and <rank-nullity theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{v^\perp=\ker\ell,\qquad\dim v^\perp=n-1.}
$$
No Gram–Schmidt argument is needed. Every vector decomposes uniquely as $u=w+av$, where $w\perp v$ and $a=\langle u,v\rangle/\langle v,v\rangle$. Substitution gives $S_v(w+av)=w-av$. Thus the <eigenspace> for $-1$ is $\mathbb Rv$, and that for $+1$ is $v^\perp$. The latter is an actual <eigenvalue> when $n\ge2$; when $n=1$ its <eigenspace> is zero and only $-1$ occurs. In the plane the map is **<reflection> in the line perpendicular to $v$**.

= Solution
{parent=2008/2/ii}

For <equal-length crystallographic root configurations in a plane>, taking $u=v$ in the closure condition gives $S_v(v)=-v$, so every vector occurs with its negative. For fixed $v\in\Phi$, a vector $u\in\Phi$ has $\langle u,v\rangle\in\{-1,-1/2,0,1/2,1\}$, because both vectors are <unit vectors>. Thus its angle relative to $v$ belongs to the finite list $0,\pm\pi/3,\pm\pi/2,\pm2\pi/3,\pi$.

If all vectors are collinear, the set is exactly $\{v,-v\}$, giving two opposite points of the <unit circle>.

If an <orthogonal> vector $w$ occurs, closure supplies $\pm v,\pm w$. A <unit vector> making an angle of $\pi/3$ or $2\pi/3$ with $v$ would have <inner product> $\pm\sqrt3/2$ with $w$, contradicting integrality of twice that <inner product>. Thus these four points are the whole set: the vertices of a <square>.

In the remaining case a noncollinear pair makes an angle of $\pi/3$ or $2\pi/3$. Changing a sign, take their angles as $0,\pi/3$. Reflecting the second in the line perpendicular to the first supplies the angle $2\pi/3$, and negation supplies the other three hexagon vertices. No perpendicular vector can occur, by the preceding inner-product obstruction. The initial finite list now leaves only these six points. Conversely the opposite pair, <square> and <regular hexagon> each satisfy both <reflection> closure and the integrality condition. Hence
$$
\boxed{|\Phi|\in\{2,4,6\}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=2008/3}

For the <polynomial ring>, use the <Euclidean function> $\delta(f)=\deg f$ on nonzero <polynomials>. If $a,b\in F[X]$ and $b\ne0$, subtract the multiple of $b$ that cancels the leading term of $a$. The leading <coefficient> of $b$ is invertible in the <field>, and every subtraction strictly lowers the degree of the remainder. This process terminates with $a=qb+r$ and either $r=0$ or $\deg r<\deg b$. Thus $\boxed{F[X]\text{ is a Euclidean domain}}$.

For the <Gauss lemma for polynomials>, call an <integer> <polynomial> <primitive polynomial>[primitive] when its <coefficient> gcd is one. If two <primitive polynomial>[primitive] <polynomials> had a nonprimitive product, a prime $p$ would divide every product <coefficient>. Reducing modulo $p$ would give a zero product of two nonzero <polynomials> in the <integral domain> $\mathbb F_p[X]$, a contradiction. Therefore **the product of <primitive polynomial>[primitive] <integer> <polynomials> is <primitive polynomial>[primitive]**.

To transfer a rational factorization, write each nonzero rational factor as a rational scalar times a <primitive polynomial>[primitive] <integer> <polynomial> by clearing denominators and removing the <coefficient> gcd. Thus $f=qAB$ with <primitive polynomial>[primitive] $A,B\in\mathbb Z[X]$ and $q\in\mathbb Q$. Their product is <primitive polynomial>[primitive] by the lemma. If $q=m/n$ in lowest terms, integrality of every <coefficient> of $f$ forces $n$ to divide every <coefficient> of $AB$, so $n=1$. Hence $q\in\mathbb Z$; absorbing $q$ into either factor produces the required <integer> factorization with the same positive factor degrees. In particular irreducibility of a <primitive polynomial> over $\mathbb Z$ and over $\mathbb Q$ is equivalent.

Finally, let $a/b$ in lowest terms, $b>0$, be a rational root of a monic degree-$d$ <integer> <polynomial>. Multiplying the equation by $b^d$ shows $b\mid a^d$. <Coprimality> forces $b=1$. Thus $\boxed{\text{every rational root of a monic integer polynomial is an integer}}$.

= Solution

Let $h=f^{-1}$, $w_0=f(z_0)$ and $z=h(w)$. <Continuity> of $h$ gives $z\to z_0$ as $w\to w_0$, and injectivity gives $z\ne z_0$ when $w\ne w_0$. Therefore
$$
\frac{h(w)-h(w_0)}{w-w_0}=\left(\frac{f(z)-f(z_0)}{z-z_0}\right)^{-1}\longrightarrow\frac1{f'(z_0)}.
$$
The assumed nonvanishing <derivative> makes this limit well defined. It exists at every $w_0\in V$, so $h$ is <holomorphic>, with $\boxed{h'(w)=1/f'(h(w))}$.

Squaring maps the first <quadrant> bijectively onto the <upper half-plane>. For $\zeta$ in that half-plane, $|\zeta-i|<|\zeta+i|$, and the <Cayley transform> $(\zeta-i)/(\zeta+i)$ maps it bijectively onto the <unit disc>. Their composition is
$$
\boxed{F(z)=\frac{z^2-i}{z^2+i}.}
$$
The inverse is the unique first-<quadrant> <square root> of $i(1+w)/(1-w)$, and is <holomorphic> by the first argument. Hence $H(w)=g(F^{-1}(w))$ is <holomorphic> throughout the disc. Its <Taylor series> at zero converges for $|w|<1$, locally uniformly there. Substituting $w=F(z)$ gives the stated expansion, with $c_n=H^{(n)}(0)/n!$, convergent at every point of the <quadrant> and locally uniformly on it.

= Solution
{parent=2008/4/b}

If a <holomorphic> <bijection> existed, its inverse would be an entire function taking values in the <unit disc>. <Liouville theorem> would make that inverse constant, contradicting bijectivity. Thus **no <holomorphic> <bijection> exists**.

A <continuous> <bijection> does exist, even a <homeomorphism>:
$$
\boxed{h(z)=\frac{z}{1-|z|},\qquad h^{-1}(w)=\frac{w}{1+|w|}.}
$$
Both maps preserve the argument and change the radius by the mutually inverse strictly increasing functions $r\mapsto r/(1-r)$ and $s\mapsto s/(1+s)$. The displayed formulas also prove <continuity> at zero and everywhere else.

= Solution

An <isolated singularity> at $a$ means that the function is <holomorphic> throughout some punctured disc $0<|z-a|<r$. Its <residue> is the <Laurent coefficient> $c_{-1}$, equivalently $(2\pi i)^{-1}\int_{|z-a|=\rho}f(z)\,dz$. The singularity is removable if all negative <Laurent coefficients> vanish, a <pole> of order $k$ if only finitely many are nonzero and the lowest power is $-k$, and essential if infinitely many negative <coefficients> are nonzero.

For the final <contour integral>, expand $e^{tz}e^{t/z}$. On the <circle> these exponential series converge absolutely and uniformly for $t$ in every bounded set, so their product can be integrated term by term. In
$$
\sum_{j,k\ge0}\frac{t^{j+k}}{j!k!}z^{j-k-2},
$$
only $j-k-2=-1$ contributes. Set $k=m$, $j=m+1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{b_m=\frac{2\pi i}{m!(m+1)!},\qquad m\ge0.}
$$
The resulting power series converges for every complex $t$ by the ratio test. The <coefficients> are independent of the positive radius $R$.

= Solution
{parent=2008/6}

The <identity theorem for holomorphic functions> says that a <holomorphic function> on a connected <open set> which has distinct zeros accumulating at a point of the set is identically zero. Equivalently, two <holomorphic> functions agreeing on such a set of points agree everywhere in that connected <open set>.

To prove it, expand at an accumulation point $a$. If the first nonzero <Taylor coefficient> had index $m$, the function would factor as $(z-a)^m h(z)$ with $h(a)\ne0$. <Continuity> would make $h$ nonzero near $a$, so no other zeros could approach $a$, a contradiction. Thus all <coefficients> vanish and the function vanishes on a neighborhood of $a$. Let $E$ be the set of points having such a zero neighborhood. It is nonempty and open. If a point in the domain is a limit point of $E$, it is either already in $E$ or is an accumulation point of distinct zeros, and the same Taylor argument puts it in $E$. Thus $E$ is relatively closed as well. <Connectedness> forces $E$ to be the whole domain. Apply this result to the difference of two functions for the equivalent formulation.

= Solution
{parent=2008/7}

Differentiate the <Wronskian>:
$$
w'=uv''-u''v=u(-qv)-(-qu)v=0.
$$
It is therefore constant. <Linear dependence> plainly makes it zero. Conversely, if it is zero, the two initial-data vectors $(u(0),u'(0))$ and $(v(0),v'(0))$ are linearly dependent. Some constants $A,B$, not both zero, make $Au+Bv$ and its <derivative> zero at zero. Uniqueness for a linear second-order equation with <continuous> <coefficients> then makes $Au+Bv$ identically zero. Thus $\boxed{w=0\iff u,v\text{ are linearly dependent}}$.

For the prescribed integral put $K(x,s)=[u(x)v(s)-u(s)v(x)]/w$. Then $K(x,x)=0$, $K_x(x,x)=-1$, and $K_{xx}(x,s)=-q(x)K(x,s)$. <Differentiation> under the integral, using <continuity> of the <coefficients> and forcing, gives
$$
y'(x)=\int_0^xK_x(x,s)f(s)\,ds,\qquad y''(x)=-f(x)-q(x)y(x).
$$
This proves the required inhomogeneous equation, with the sign of the forcing as printed.

For the explicit forcing take $u=\cosh x$, $v=\sinh x$, so $w=1$ and $K(x,s)=-\sinh(x-s)$. Since $2/(e^s+e^{-s})=\operatorname{sech}s$, the integral is
$$
-\int_0^x\sinh(x-s)\operatorname{sech}s\,ds=-x\sinh x+\cosh x\log\cosh x.
$$
Indeed expand $\sinh(x-s)=\sinh x\cosh s-\cosh x\sinh s$ and integrate $1$ and $\tanh s$. Adding the entire homogeneous solution gives
$$
\boxed{y(x)=A\cosh x+B\sinh x+\cosh x\log\cosh x-x\sinh x,\quad A,B\in\mathbb R.}
$$
Finally multiply the other equation by $\sin x$ and integrate from zero to $\pi$. <Integration by parts> gives
$$
\int_0^\pi(y''+y)\sin x\,dx=[y'\sin x-y\cos x]_0^\pi=y(\pi)+y(0)=0.
$$
Thus $\boxed{\int_0^\pi f(s)\sin s\,ds=0}$ is necessary. It is also sufficient: the solution with $y(0)=0$ is $B\sin x-\int_0^x\sin(x-s)f(s)\,ds$, and the integral condition gives its value zero at $\pi$.

= Solution

Use the characteristic parameter $t$ and write
$$
\dot x=3y-4u,\qquad\dot y=-3x+5u,\qquad\dot u=4x-5y.
$$
Direct <differentiation> gives
$$
\frac d{dt}(5x+4y+3u)=0,\qquad\frac d{dt}(x^2+y^2+u^2)=0.
$$
A characteristic beginning at $(s,0,0)$ therefore satisfies $5x+4y+3u=5s$ and $x^2+y^2+u^2=s^2$. Eliminating $s$ gives
$$
16u^2-(30x+24y)u+9y^2-40xy=0.
$$
Its discriminant as a quadratic in $u$ is $100(9x^2+40xy)$. Requiring the root to be zero when $y=0$ selects
$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{15x+12y-5\sqrt{9x^2+40xy}}{16},&x>0,\\[5pt]\dfrac{15x+12y+5\sqrt{9x^2+40xy}}{16},&x<0.\end{cases}}
$$
The selected branch is differentiable in each of the two regions where the radical is strictly positive, and substitution into the differentiated quadratic identity verifies the <partial differential equation> there. On the initial line away from the origin the characteristic vector has nonzero $y$ component $-3s$, so these are the locally unique branches determined by that line.

The maximal real-valued continuation of these branches has the printed domain
$$
\boxed{D=\{9x^2+40xy\ge0\}=\{x\ge0,\ y\ge-9x/40\}\cup\{x\le0,\ y\le-9x/40\}.}
$$
At $x=0$ the <continuous> limiting value is $u(0,y)=3y/4$. The two bounding lines are $x=0$ and $y=-9x/40$, and the included sectors are the one above the sloping line on the right and the one below it on the left.

As in the <fold boundary of a characteristic graph>, the closed-domain statement concerns the formula's real values. A <classical solution> requires first derivatives on an <open set>. Along the nonzero boundary points $9x^2+40xy=0$, the radical's transverse <derivative> is singular, so the formula is generally not a <classical solution> there; its two classical domains are the interiors of the shaded sectors. The origin is characteristic for the initial data and does not supply a differentiable neighborhood solution. This qualification preserves the source's stated closed set while distinguishing it from the domain of a <classical solution>.

A <equilibrium>[critical point] is an <equilibrium>: $\boxed{f(a,b)=g(a,b)=0}$. Put $X=x-a$, $Y=y-b$. The <Jacobian matrix> $J$ gives the first-order linearized equation $(\dot X,\dot Y)^\mathsf T=J(X,Y)^\mathsf T$. The following <eigenvalue> tests classify that linearized system. For a continuously differentiable nonlinear system, the cases with nonzero real parts persist as the usual local node, <saddle equilibrium>[saddle] or spiral classifications. The purely imaginary case needs extra nonlinear information, as explained in (iv).

= Solution

For the linearized system a <centre equilibrium>[centre] occurs when $\boxed{\lambda_{1,2}=\pm i\beta,\ \beta\ne0}$. Its real solutions are periodic ellipses after an invertible linear coordinate change.

As explained by <a centre in the linearization need not be a nonlinear centre>, for a nonlinear system these <eigenvalues> alone do **not** guarantee a <centre equilibrium>[centre]. For example $\dot X=-Y-X(X^2+Y^2)$, $\dot Y=X-Y(X^2+Y^2)$ has <eigenvalues> $\pm i$ at zero but polar equations $\dot r=-r^3$, $\dot\theta=1$, so trajectories spiral inward. Removing the cubic terms gives a genuine <centre equilibrium>[centre] with the same Jacobian. Thus there is no <eigenvalue>-only sufficient condition for a nonlinear <centre equilibrium>[centre] in the stated generality.

= Solution

The <equilibrium> equations are $x(1+x-2y)=0$ and $(x-1)y=0$. If $x=0$, the second equation gives $y=0$. Otherwise either $y=0$, giving $x=-1$, or $x=1$, giving $y=1$. Thus there are exactly three equilibria. The Jacobian is
$$
J(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}1+2x-2y&-2x\\y&x-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
At $(0,0)$ it is $\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$: **a <saddle equilibrium>[saddle]**. At $(-1,0)$ it is $\begin{pmatrix}-1&2\\0&-2\end{pmatrix}$, with <eigenvalues> $-1,-2$: **a <stable node>**. At $(1,1)$ it is $\begin{pmatrix}1&-2\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, with <characteristic polynomial> $\lambda^2-\lambda+2$ and <eigenvalues> $(1\pm i\sqrt7)/2$: **an <unstable spiral>**. All three are hyperbolic, so the nonlinear local classifications follow from <linearization>.

= Solution
{parent=2008/9/c}

Suppose a nonconstant <periodic orbit> exists for the continuously differentiable planar <vector field>. Uniqueness implies that its image over a minimal period is a simple closed curve, enclosing a region $\Omega$ of positive area. Along the orbit, the differential form $f\,dy-g\,dx$ vanishes because $dx=f\,dt$ and $dy=g\,dt$. <Green's theorem>, with positive orientation, therefore gives
$$
0=\oint_{\partial\Omega}(f\,dy-g\,dx)=\iint_\Omega(f_x+g_y)\,dx\,dy<0,
$$
a contradiction. This is the <Bendixson-Dulac criterion> in the whole plane; the fact that the enclosed region also lies in the domain is essential.

For the second-order equation set $y=\dot x$. The equivalent system is $\dot x=y$, $\dot y=-(1+x^2)y-\cos x$. Its <divergence> is $\boxed{-(1+x^2)<0}$ throughout the plane. Hence **there are no nonconstant periodic solutions**.

The nonconstant qualification is necessary: the <equilibrium> solutions $x(t)\equiv\pi/2+k\pi$, $k\in\mathbb Z$, do exist and are periodic in the literal sense of satisfying every positive period. The source's usual dynamical interpretation of “<periodic solution>” here is a nonconstant <periodic orbit>.
