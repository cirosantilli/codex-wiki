= Solution
{parent=2009/algebra/1}

If $c(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-T)=0$, then $\lambda I-T$ has nonzero <kernel of a linear map>. A nonzero vector $v$ in that <kernel of a linear map> satisfies $Tv=\lambda v$, proving that $\lambda$ is an <eigenvalue>. Extend $v$ to a <basis>. Its first column is $(\lambda,0,\ldots,0)^T$, so the <matrix> has block form
$$
[T]=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&*\\0&B\end{pmatrix},\qquad c(X)=(X-\lambda)\det(XI-B).
$$
Here $B$ represents the induced operator on $V/\langle v\rangle$.

If the <algebraic multiplicity> is $r$, repeat this argument in that quotient: its <characteristic polynomial> still has $\lambda$ with multiplicity $r-1$. Induction and lifting a quotient <basis> gives a <basis> whose first $r$ columns have the prescribed upper-triangular form. With $U$ the span of those $r$ vectors, the full <matrix> is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}\lambda I+N&*\\0&C\end{pmatrix},\qquad N^r=0,\qquad\det(C-\lambda I)\ne0.
$$
Thus $U$ is invariant and is contained in $W_\lambda$. Conversely, if $(T-\lambda I)^sv=0$, project to $V/U$. The induced map $C-\lambda I$ is invertible, so its $s$th power cannot kill a nonzero class. Hence $v\in U$. Consequently $\boxed{W_\lambda=U,\ \dim W_\lambda=r}$; this proves simultaneously that it is a <vector subspace>[subspace] and $T$-invariant. It also proves $(T-\lambda I)^r=0$ on this space, without using <Cayley-Hamilton theorem> in advance.

To prove independence of the different spaces, take a relation $\sum_\lambda v_\lambda=0$ with $v_\lambda\in W_\lambda$. Fix $\lambda$ and apply $P_\lambda(T)=\prod_{\mu\ne\lambda}(T-\mu I)^{r_\mu}$. It kills all terms except $v_\lambda$. On $W_\lambda$, each factor $T-\mu I=(\lambda-\mu)I+N$ is invertible: its inverse is a finite <geometric series> in the <nilpotent operator>[nilpotent] $N$. Hence $P_\lambda(T)v_\lambda=0$ implies $v_\lambda=0$. The sum is direct, and its <dimension> is $\sum r_\lambda=\dim V$, so
$$
\boxed{V=\bigoplus_\lambda W_\lambda.}
$$
Finally $c(T)=\prod_\lambda(T-\lambda I)^{r_\lambda}$ kills each summand, hence every vector. This proves $\boxed{c(T)=0}$ without circularly assuming the theorem during the decomposition.

The <orthogonal complement> is $W^\perp=\{v\in V:\langle v,w\rangle=0\text{ for every }w\in W\}$. Put $Pv=\sum_i\langle v,e_i\rangle e_i$, so $T=I-P$. <Orthonormality> gives $P^2=P$, and therefore $T^2=T$. Also $\langle Tv,e_j\rangle=0$ for every $j$, so $Tv\in W^\perp$. Every $v=Pv+Tv$ is thus a sum from $W$ and $W^\perp$, and their intersection is zero, since a vector <orthogonal> to itself has squared <norm> zero. This proves $V=W\oplus W^\perp$. The decomposition is unique, so its two <orthogonal projection>[projection] maps depend only on $W$, not on the chosen <orthonormal basis>.

Finite dimensionality of $W$ cannot simply be dropped. Take the real <Hilbert space> $V=\ell^2(\mathbb N;\mathbb R)$ and $W=c_{00}$, the finitely supported sequences. This is an infinite-dimensional proper <vector subspace>[subspace]. A vector <orthogonal> to every coordinate <unit vector> is zero, so $W^\perp=\{0\}$. Yet, for example, $(2^{-j})_{j\ge1}$ belongs to $V\setminus W$. Hence $W\oplus W^\perp=W\ne V$. The failure occurs even though $V$ itself is complete; the proper <dense subspace> $W$ is not closed.

= Solution
{parent=2009/algebra/2/a}

For any $w\in W$, the decomposition $v-w=Tv+(Pv-w)$ is <orthogonal>. The <Pythagorean identity> gives
$$
\|v-w\|^2=\|Tv\|^2+\|Pv-w\|^2\ge\|Tv\|^2.
$$
Equality is attained at $w=Pv$. Thus $\boxed{\inf_{w\in W}\|v-w\|=\|Tv\|}$, and the minimizer in $W$ is uniquely $Pv$.

= Solution
{parent=2009/algebra/2/b}

The assumption $v\notin W^\perp$ means $p=Pv\ne0$. For every <unit vector> $w\in W$, <orthogonality> and Cauchy–Schwarz give $\langle v,w\rangle=\langle p,w\rangle\le\|p\|$. Equality is attained at
$$
\boxed{\hat w=p/\|p\|,\qquad\sup_{w\in W,\ \|w\|=1}\langle v,w\rangle=\|p\|.}
$$
Equality in the real <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>[Cauchy–Schwarz inequality] requires $w$ to be a scalar multiple of $p$, and attaining the positive <supremum> fixes its sign; the <unit> <norm> fixes its magnitude. This proves uniqueness.

{parent=2009/Algebra}
{scope}

Use ordinary <matrix> addition and multiplication on $R=K[A]$. Sums and products of <polynomials> in $A$ remain such <polynomials>, and they commute because powers of the same <matrix> commute. The identity is $A^0=I$. Evaluation $\phi(\sum c_iX^i)=\sum c_iA^i$ is a surjective unital <algebra homomorphism> over $K$. It is unique among maps fixing the scalar <matrices> and sending $X$ to $A$.

The scalar condition is needed for the word “unique”: as an unrestricted ring-homomorphism assertion over an arbitrary <field>, prescribing only the image of $X$ does not suffice. For example, over $\mathbb C$ with a real scalar <matrix> $A$, conjugating every coefficient gives another unital <ring homomorphism> with the same image of $X$. The intended <polynomial>-evaluation map is the $K$-algebra map just defined.

The <kernel of a ring homomorphism> is an <ideal> of the <principal ideal domain> $K[X]$. It is nonzero because finitely many powers of $A$ must become linearly dependent in the finite-dimensional <matrix> space. Its unique <monic polynomial>[monic] generator is precisely the <minimal polynomial> $m$: division by $m$ shows that a <polynomial> killing $A$ has zero remainder, by minimality. Hence $\ker\phi=(m)$ and $R\cong K[X]/(m)$.

If $\gcd(f,m)=1$, <Bézout's identity> gives $uf+vm=1$; evaluation gives $u(A)f(A)=I$. Conversely an inverse in $R$ has form $g(A)$, so $fg-1\in(m)$, which forces $\gcd(f,m)=1$. Thus invertibility in this ring is exactly <coprimality> with the <minimal polynomial>.

The displayed <matrix> is block diagonal with a $1\times1$ block $1$ and a $2\times2$ block $B$ having <characteristic polynomial> $X^2-4X-5=(X-5)(X+1)$. The latter block is not scalar, so its <minimal polynomial> has degree two and is exactly that <polynomial>. Taking the least common multiple of the block minimal <polynomials> gives $m=(X-1)(X-5)(X+1)$ over $\mathbb R$.

<Ideals> of $K[X]/(m)$ correspond to <ideals> of $K[X]$ containing $(m)$, namely the principal <ideals> generated by the monic divisors of $m$. Every one of the three distinct linear factors may be included or omitted, so
$$
\boxed{\#\{\text{ideals of }R\}=2^3=8.}
$$
This includes both the zero <ideal> and the whole ring. Equivalently the <Chinese remainder theorem> identifies this ring with $\mathbb R^3$.

This ring has the four elements $0,I,A,I+A$. <Coprimality> with $X+1$ is equivalent to the representing <polynomial> taking value one at $1$. Thus precisely $I$ and $A$ are <units>: $\boxed{2\text{ invertible matrices}}$. Indeed $A^2=I$, whereas $(I+A)^2=0$.

= Solution
{parent=2009/analysis/4}

<Laurent's theorem> states that a <holomorphic function> on $R<|z-a|<S$ has a unique expansion $\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}a_n(z-a)^n$, converging absolutely there and uniformly on <compact> subannuli, with
$$
a_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|z-a|=r}\frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}dz\quad(R<r<S).
$$
Here $\sin z=z(1-z^2/6+\cdots)$ has a <simple zero> at zero and no other zero in $|z|<\pi$. Hence $1/\sin z$ has only a <simple pole> there, giving the stated series with no powers below $z^{-1}$. Multiplying by $\sin z$ and comparing the constant, linear and quadratic coefficients yields
$$
\boxed{c_{-1}=1,\qquad c_0=0,\qquad c_1=1/6.}
$$

Use the coefficient integrals on the two circles. The annular region between them contains exactly the <simple poles> $\pi,-\pi$, and the <residue> of $1/\sin z$ at each is $1/\cos(\pm\pi)=-1$. Apply the <residue theorem> to $1/(z^{n+1}\sin z)$ between the circles:
$$
\boxed{d_n=c_n-\pi^{-n-1}-(-\pi)^{-n-1}\quad(n\ge-1).}
$$
For lower indices, set $c_n=0$ because the inner <Laurent series> has no such terms; the same contour argument then still applies. Since $f$ is odd, its even-index coefficients vanish. A complete explicit list for the requested range is
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
d_1&=\tfrac16-\frac2{\pi^2},&d_0&=0,&d_{-1}&=-1,\\
d_{-2k}&=0\ (k\ge1),&&d_{-(2k+1)}&=-2\pi^{2k}\ (k\ge1).
\end{aligned}}
$$
The subtraction accounts for the two <poles> crossed; using the inner coefficients unchanged outside their <annulus> would miss precisely these terms.

= Solution
{parent=2009/analysis/5}

For $t>0$, integrate $e^{itz}/(1+z^2)$ over the upper semicircle of radius $R$. The arc tends to zero, since $|e^{itz}|\le1$ there and the denominator is of order $R^2$. The sole <pole> is $i$, of <residue> $e^{-t}/(2i)$, so the real part gives $I(t)=\pi e^{-t}$. The integrand defining $I$ is even in $t$, and $I(0)=\pi$ by the arctangent integral. Hence
$$
\boxed{I(t)=\pi e^{-|t|}\quad(t\in\mathbb R).}
$$

For $J$ use $e^{itz}/[z(1+z^2)]$ with a small clockwise semicircular indentation above zero. Its <residue> at zero is one, so the small arc tends to $-i\pi$. The <pole> $i$ has <residue> $-e^{-t}/2$, and the large arc again vanishes. Thus
$$
\operatorname{PV}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{e^{itx}}{x(1+x^2)}dx-i\pi=-i\pi e^{-t}.
$$
The imaginary part is the ordinary convergent integral $J(t)$: its apparent singularity at zero is removable, with limit $t$, and its tails are absolutely integrable. Therefore $J(t)=\pi(1-e^{-t})$ for $t>0$. Oddness in $t$ and the value zero at $t=0$ give
$$
\boxed{J(t)=\begin{cases}\pi(1-e^{-t}),&t>0,\\0,&t=0,\\-\pi(1-e^{t}),&t<0.\end{cases}}
$$

<Differentiation> is valid at every real $t$, including zero. Indeed the <mean value theorem> bounds the absolute difference quotient of $\sin(tx)/[x(1+x^2)]$ with respect to $t$ by $1/(1+x^2)$, an integrable majorant independent of $t$. <Dominated convergence> therefore gives $\boxed{J'(t)=I(t)\text{ for all }t\in\mathbb R}$. In particular both one-sided derivatives at zero equal $\pi$.

= Solution
{parent=2009/analysis/6/a}

The complex <fundamental theorem of calculus> says that for a <holomorphic> <antiderivative> $F$ and a piecewise $C^1$ path $\gamma$ from $a$ to $b$, one has $\int_\gamma F'(z)dz=F(b)-F(a)$. The integral is therefore zero on a closed path; the <antiderivative> need only be defined on a neighborhood of that path, rather than on all its interior.

The assumptions make $F(z)=z/f(z)^n$ <holomorphic> near $\gamma$. Differentiate:
$$
F'(z)=\frac1{f(z)^n}-n\frac{zf'(z)}{f(z)^{n+1}}.
$$
Integrate this <derivative> around the closed path to obtain
$$
\boxed{\int_\gamma\frac{dz}{f(z)^n}=n\int_\gamma\frac{zf'(z)}{f(z)^{n+1}}dz.}
$$

{parent=2009/analysis/6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2009/analysis/6/b}

For a positively oriented simple closed piecewise smooth curve, a point $a$ inside it and a function $h$ <holomorphic> on a neighborhood of the curve and its interior, Cauchy's differentiated integral formula is
$$
\boxed{h^{(m)}(a)=\frac{m!}{2\pi i}\int_\gamma\frac{h(z)}{(z-a)^{m+1}}dz\quad(m\ge0).}
$$
**The printed denominator uses $n+1$ while the <derivative> and factorial use $m$.** The standard formula requires these indices to agree; the intended exponent in this display is $m+1$. The private transcription preserves the mismatch exactly.

For the following task, write $f(z)=zq(z)$ near zero with $q(0)=f'(0)\ne0$. Thus $H(z)=(z/f(z))^n=q(z)^{-n}$ extends holomorphically at zero, and is <holomorphic> inside the specified contour because $f$ has no other zeros there. Now $f(z)^{-n}=H(z)/z^n$. Apply the correctly indexed formula with <derivative> order $n-1$ to get
$$
\boxed{\int_\gamma\frac{dz}{f(z)^n}=\frac{2\pi i}{(n-1)!}\left[\frac{d^{n-1}}{dz^{n-1}}\left(\frac z{f(z)}\right)^n\right]_{z=0}.}
$$
Positive winding one supplies the orientation and multiplicity used in this expression.

{parent=2009/analysis/6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2009/analysis/6/c}

Choose a small positively oriented circle $|w|=\rho$ in $U$ within the convergence disk of the inverse series. Cauchy's coefficient formula gives
$$
c_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|w|=\rho}\frac{g(w)}{w^{n+1}}dw.
$$
Let $\gamma=g(\{|w|=\rho\})$. The <biholomorphism> property makes it a simple positively oriented closed curve about zero. Under $w=f(z)$, $g(w)=z$ and $dw=f'(z)dz$, so
$$
c_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\gamma\frac{zf'(z)}{f(z)^{n+1}}dz=\frac1{2\pi in}\int_\gamma\frac{dz}{f(z)^n}.
$$
The last equality is (a). Apply (b), giving
$$
\boxed{c_n=\frac1{n!}\left[\frac{d^{n-1}}{dz^{n-1}}\left(\frac z{f(z)}\right)^n\right]_{z=0}.}
$$
This is the local <Lagrange inversion theorem>; the <simple zero> ensures that the expression being differentiated has a removable singularity and a nonzero value at zero.

= Solution
{parent=2009/differential-equations/7}

<Continuity> on the <compact> rectangle gives finite constants $M=\max_R|F|$ and $L=\max_R|F_y|$. The real <mean value theorem> in the second coordinate gives $|F(x,y)-F(x,z)|\le L|y-z|$ within the rectangle. Define the <Picard iterate>[Picard iterates] on the entire interval by
$$
y_0(x)=b,\qquad y_{n+1}(x)=b+\int_a^xF(t,y_n(t))dt.
$$
If the previous iterate stays in the vertical range of $R$, then $|y_{n+1}(x)-b|\le M|x-a|\le Mh\le k$. Induction therefore makes all these iterates well defined on $|x-a|\le h$.

Repeated use of the <Lipschitz bound> gives the factorial estimate
$$
|y_{n+1}(x)-y_n(x)|\le\frac{ML^n|x-a|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\quad(n\ge0).
$$
The case $n=0$ is the direct integral bound; integrating the estimate for $n-1$ proves the next one, on either side of $a$. The series of uniform bounds converges, so the iterates converge uniformly to a <continuous function> $y$ taking values within the closed vertical range. The Lipschitz estimate permits passage through the integral to give $y(x)=b+\int_a^xF(t,y(t))dt$. The <fundamental theorem of calculus> now gives $y'=F(x,y)$ and $y(a)=b$. Thus the solution exists on the full stated interval; no extra restriction $Lh<1$ was used.

For the specified equation take $k=1/4$, so $3/4\le y\le5/4$. On $|x|\le h$,
$$
M=\frac{3h^2}{4(3/4)^3}=\frac{16h^2}{9}.
$$
The condition $Mh\le k$ holds at $h^3=9/64$, namely $\boxed{h=3^{2/3}/4}$. The rectangle avoids $y=0$, so both <continuity> hypotheses hold there.

Direct separation gives $4y^3y'=3x^2$, hence $(y^4)'=(x^3)'$ and the initial value yields $y^4=1+x^3$. The branch through $y(0)=1$ is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=(1+x^3)^{1/4}\quad(x>-1).}
$$
It remains positive on this interval. At $x=-1$ it reaches zero, where the differential equation is undefined, so this is its maximal real interval containing zero. The rectangle theorem supplies a sufficient interval, smaller than that actual interval.

= 8
{parent=2009/Differential equations}
{scope}

For the particular equation, write $D_x=x\partial_x$ and $D_y=y\partial_y$. Its left side is $(D_x^2-D_y^2)u$. Choose $\phi=xy$ and $\psi=x/y$; these are smooth invertible coordinates in the <positive quadrant>, with inverse $x=\sqrt{\phi\psi}$, $y=\sqrt{\phi/\psi}$. In them $D_x=\phi\partial_\phi+\psi\partial_\psi$ and $D_y=\phi\partial_\phi-\psi\partial_\psi$. Subtraction of their squares leaves $4\phi\psi\,u_{\phi\psi}$, so the canonical equation is $u_{\phi\psi}=0$. Integrating it gives $u=F(\phi)+G(\psi)$.

Parameterize the data curve by $(x,y)=(s,s^2)$, $s\ge1$. Its coordinate values are $(\phi,\psi)=(s^3,s^{-1})$, so
$$
F(s^3)+G(s^{-1})=s^6+s^{-1},\qquad s^2F'(s^3)+s^{-2}G'(s^{-1})=2s^5+s^{-2}.
$$
Differentiate the first identity along the curve to get $3s^2F'(s^3)-s^{-2}G'(s^{-1})=6s^5-s^{-2}$. Adding gives $F'(s^3)=2s^3$, and then $G'(s^{-1})=1$. Thus $F(\phi)=\phi^2+C$ for $\phi\ge1$ and $G(\psi)=\psi-C$ for $0<\psi\le1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=x^2y^2+x/y\quad\text{where }xy\ge1,\ x/y\le1.}
$$
The coordinate rectangle gives exactly the region
$$
\boxed{x>0,\qquad y\ge\max(x,1/x).}
$$
Its boundary consists of $xy=1$ up to $(1,1)$ and the ray $y=x$ from that point onward. It is unbounded upward. The original data curve lies inside this region. The sketch displays both characteristic boundaries and that data arc.

To establish the exact extent of uniqueness, outside this region either $\phi<1$ or $\psi>1$. A <smooth function> supported in that unprescribed coordinate range can be added to $F$ or $G$ without changing either datum on the arc. Hence the displayed global formula is one extension, but data alone do not uniquely determine values outside the shaded region. Boundary values are fixed by <continuity>. The following classification criteria are pointwise and assume a nonzero principal coefficient <matrix>.

= Solution
{parent=2009/differential-equations/8/ii}

The equation is parabolic at a point when $\boxed{b^2-ac=0}$ and at least one of $a,b,c$ is nonzero. The principal <matrix> then has rank one, with a repeated <characteristic direction>. If all three vanish, there is no second-order <principal part of a meromorphic function>[principal part] there and the usual parabolic classification does not apply.

= Solution

The equation is hyperbolic at a point when $\boxed{b^2-ac>0}$. The principal form has one positive and one negative <eigenvalue> and two distinct real <characteristic directions>. In the given equation $a=x^2$, $b=0$, $c=-y^2$, so $b^2-ac=x^2y^2>0$ throughout the <positive quadrant>, consistent with the two canonical characteristic families used above.

= Solution
{parent=2009/differential-equations/9}

Under the usual existence and boundary hypotheses for the <Laplace transform>,
$$
\boxed{\overline{f'}(p)=p\bar f(p)-f(0+),\qquad\overline{tf}(p)=-\frac d{dp}\bar f(p).}
$$
The first formula requires a finite right limit and sufficient regularity for <integration by parts>; it is not to be applied blindly to the singular function $t^{-1/2}$. For that function and real $p>0$, substitute $s=\sqrt{pt}$:
$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-pt}t^{-1/2}dt=\frac2{\sqrt p}\int_0^\infty e^{-s^2}ds=\sqrt{\pi/p}.
$$
Use the second transform identity to obtain
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L\{\sqrt t\}(p)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2p^{3/2}}.}
$$

Take the time transform of the <heat equation>, with <differentiation> and boundary limits interpreted in the standard decaying heat-solution class. The initial value is zero, so $v_{xx}=p v-u(x,0)=pv$. Transforming the constant flux gives $v_x(0,p)=-1/p$. Decay selects the decreasing solution of the transformed <ordinary differential equation>, giving
$$
v(x,p)=B(p)e^{-\sqrt p\,x},\qquad-\sqrt p\,B(p)=-1/p.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{v(x,p)=p^{-3/2}e^{-x\sqrt p},\qquad u(0,t)=2\sqrt{t/\pi}.}
$$
The boundary value follows by comparing $v(0,p)=p^{-3/2}$ with the transform of $\sqrt t$.

One can also check the decay and transform steps without relying on a merely pointwise limit under an <improper integral>. Using the <complementary error function>, the classical <heat equation>[heat] solution is
$$
u(x,t)=2\sqrt{t/\pi}\,e^{-x^2/(4t)}-x\,\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt t}\right)=\int_x^\infty\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{s}{2\sqrt t}\right)ds.
$$
<Differentiation> verifies $u_{xx}=u_t$, $u_x(0,t)=-1$, and $u(x,0)=0$ for $x>0$. Its integral representation gives $0\le u(x,t)\le2\sqrt{t/\pi}$ and pointwise decay as $x\to\infty$. Thus $e^{-pt}2\sqrt{t/\pi}$ supplies an integrable majorant, proving $v(x,p)\to0$ by <dominated convergence>. The same representation gives the required regularity for the transforms. This explicitly verifies that the solution selected by the decaying transformed equation lies in the intended heat-solution class.
