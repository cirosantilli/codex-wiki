= Solution
{parent=2004/1}

The <dual basis> is $E^*=\{e_1^*,\ldots,e_n^*\}$, where $e_i^*(e_j)=\delta_{ij}$ and each coordinate <linear functional> extends linearly. Thus $e_i^*(\sum_j a_je_j)=a_i$. These functionals are a <basis> of $V^*$ because a <linear functional> is uniquely determined by its values on $E$.

For a <linear subspace> $U$ of dimension $k$, extend a <basis> $u_1,\ldots,u_k$ to one of $V$. In its <dual basis>, the <annihilator of a vector subspace> $U^\circ$ is spanned by the last $n-k$ coordinate functionals. Hence $\boxed{\dim U+\dim U^\circ=\dim V}$.

The <transpose> in the source is the algebraic <dual map> $T^*:V^*\to V^*$ defined by $T^*f=f\circ T$. Composition of <linear maps> is linear, so $T^*f$ is a <linear functional>; and $T^*(af+bg)=aT^*f+bT^*g$, proving linearity in its input. If $T(e_j)=\sum_i a_{ij}e_i$, then
$$
(T^*e_i^*)(e_j)=e_i^*(Te_j)=a_{ij}.
$$
The coefficient of $e_j^*$ in $T^*e_i^*$ is consequently $a_{ij}$, so $\boxed{[T^*]_{E^*}=([T]_E)^{\mathsf T}}$. No <inner product> is involved.

A <linear functional> $f$ annihilates $\operatorname{Im}T$ exactly when $f(Tv)=0$ for all $v$, equivalently $T^*f=0$. Thus $(\operatorname{Im}T)^\circ=\ker T^*$. If $v\in\ker T$, then $(T^*f)(v)=f(Tv)=0$, giving $\operatorname{Im}T^*\subseteq(\ker T)^\circ$. The first equality and the dimension formula give $\dim\ker T^*=n-\dim\operatorname{Im}T$. By the <rank-nullity theorem>,
$$
\dim\operatorname{Im}T^*=\dim\operatorname{Im}T
=n-\dim\ker T=\dim(\ker T)^\circ.
$$
The inclusion is therefore an equality:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Im}T^*=(\ker T)^\circ.}
$$

= Solution

The <minimal polynomial> of $T$ is the monic nonzero <polynomial> of smallest degree annihilating $T$. Such a <polynomial> exists, since sufficiently many powers of $T$ are linearly dependent in the finite-dimensional space $\operatorname{End}(V)$. If two monic annihilators had the same least degree, their difference would be an annihilator of smaller degree and hence zero. Thus it is unique. Division with remainder also shows that every annihilating <polynomial> is a multiple of it.

The <primary decomposition theorem> states that if $m=\prod_{j=1}^r f_j^{e_j}$ with distinct monic <irreducible polynomials>, then
$$
V=\bigoplus_{j=1}^r V_j,\qquad V_j=\ker f_j(T)^{e_j}.
$$
These subspaces are $T$-invariant, and the restriction to $V_j$ has <minimal polynomial> $f_j^{e_j}$.

Let $r=\gcd(q,m)$, chosen monic. The <Bézout's identity for polynomials> gives $r=aq+bm$. If $q(T)v=0$, then $r(T)v=a(T)q(T)v+b(T)m(T)v=0$. Conversely, $q=rh$, so $r(T)v=0$ implies $q(T)v=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\ker q(T)=\ker\gcd(q,m)(T).}
$$
The following Roman scopes correspond to the three labelled conditions, with their implications proved from the indicated condition.

= Solution
{parent=2004/2/i}

If a <basis> consists of <eigenvectors>, let $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r\in K$ be the distinct <eigenvalues>. A <polynomial> annihilates the diagonal action exactly when it vanishes at each $\lambda_j$. Consequently the <minimal polynomial> is $\prod_j(X-\lambda_j)$, a product of distinct linear factors. It therefore has no repeated irreducible factor, proving **(i) implies (ii)**.

= ii
{parent=2004/2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2004/2/ii}

Suppose $m$ has no repeated irreducible factor. For every <polynomial> $p$ and <integer> $n\ge1$, its monic greatest common divisors satisfy $\gcd(p^n,m)=\gcd(p,m)$, since raising $p$ to a power changes no membership of an irreducible factor of the square-free $m$. This is the <square-free minimal polynomial criterion for polynomial kernel stabilization>. The kernel identity proved above gives
$$
\ker(p(T)^n)=\ker\gcd(p^n,m)(T)
=\ker\gcd(p,m)(T)=\ker p(T).
$$
Thus **(ii) implies (iii)** over every <field>.

Over $\mathbb C$, the distinct irreducible factors of $m$ are linear. The <primary decomposition theorem> therefore reads $V=\bigoplus_j\ker(T-\lambda_jI)$, a direct sum of <eigenspaces>. Taking a <basis> in each summand gives an <eigenbasis>, proving **(ii) implies (i) over the complex numbers**.

Over $\mathbb R$, take $T=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$. Its <minimal polynomial> is $X^2+1$, which is an <irreducible polynomial> over $\mathbb R$ and has no repeated factor. But it has no real <eigenvalues>, so no real <eigenbasis>. This is the required counterexample. Over a general <field>, the phrase “no repeated factors” here refers to irreducible factors in $K[X]$, rather than a prior assumption that those factors split.

= iii
{parent=2004/2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2004/2/iii}

Assume condition (iii), and factor $m=\prod_j f_j^{e_j}$. If some $e_j\ge2$, the <primary decomposition theorem> gives the nonzero summand $V_j=\ker f_j(T)^{e_j}$ on which the <minimal polynomial> is exactly $f_j^{e_j}$. The operator $f_j(T)$ cannot vanish on this whole summand, or its restriction would have <minimal polynomial> dividing $f_j$. Choose $v\in V_j$ with $f_j(T)v\ne0$. Then
$$
v\in\ker f_j(T)^{e_j}\setminus\ker f_j(T),
$$
contradicting (iii) with $p=f_j$ and $n=e_j$. Hence every $e_j=1$, proving **(iii) implies (ii)**.

An <ideal> is an additive subgroup $I\subseteq R$ with $rI\subseteq I$ for all $r\in R$. A <ring homomorphism> preserves addition and multiplication; for the <rings> with identity here use the standard unital convention $\theta(1_R)=1_S$. If $a,b\in\ker\theta$, then $\theta(a-b)=0$, and if $r\in R$ then $\theta(ra)=\theta(r)\theta(a)=0$. Thus the kernel is an <ideal>.

A <maximal ideal> is a proper <ideal> $I\ne R$ such that an <ideal> $J$ satisfying $I\subseteq J\subseteq R$ is either $I$ or $R$.

= ii
{parent=2004/3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2004/3/ii}

Use the standard definition that a <prime ideal> is proper, in addition to the product condition. The quotient operations are
$$
(r+I)+(s+I)=(r+s)+I,\qquad(r+I)(s+I)=rs+I.
$$
Its zero and identity are $I$ and $1+I$, which differ because $I$ is proper. If $(r+I)(s+I)=I$, then $rs\in I$, so primality gives $r\in I$ or $s\in I$. Thus one factor is zero. The commutative <quotient ring> has no nonzero <zero divisors> and is therefore an <integral domain>.

The printed definition omits properness. If that omission were read literally, $I=R$ would satisfy its product condition, but $R/I$ would be the zero <ring>, which is not an <integral domain> under the standard convention $1\ne0$. Properness is the necessary qualification, retained explicitly in this proof.

= iii
{parent=2004/3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2004/3/iii}

The <inverse image> $I=\theta^{-1}(J)$ is an <ideal>, by preservation of differences and multiplication. It is proper since $\theta(1)=1\notin J$. If $ab\in I$, then $\theta(a)\theta(b)\in J$; primality of $J$ gives $\theta(a)\in J$ or $\theta(b)\in J$, hence $a\in I$ or $b\in I$. Therefore **the <inverse image> is prime**.

For maximality, take the inclusion $\mathbb Z\hookrightarrow\mathbb Q$ and $J=(0)$. This is a <maximal ideal> of the <field> $\mathbb Q$, but its <inverse image> is $(0)$ in $\mathbb Z$, strictly contained in the proper <ideal> $2\mathbb Z$, so it is not maximal. Preservation of identity matters for proper inverse images: without it the zero homomorphism could pull a proper <ideal> back to the whole <ring>.

= Solution
{parent=2004/4}

Use the boundary form of <Cauchy's integral theorem>: the integral of a <holomorphic function> around the oriented boundary of a compact region on whose neighborhood it is <holomorphic> is zero, including annular regions with the inner boundary oppositely oriented. Apply it to $f(z)/(z-a)$ between the circles of radii $r$ and $\rho$, both around $a$. It gives
$$
\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|z-a|=r}\frac{f(z)}{z-a}\,dz
=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(a+\rho e^{it})\,dt\longrightarrow f(a)
$$
as $\rho\downarrow0$, by continuity. This proves the <Cauchy integral formula>.

For a bounded <entire function> with $|f|\le M$, apply that formula at variable points inside a fixed circle and differentiate its kernel under the integral. At its center $a$, this gives
$$
f'(a)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|z-a|=R}\frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^2}\,dz,
\qquad |f'(a)|\le\frac MR.
$$
The circle can have any radius because the function is entire. Letting $R\to\infty$ gives $f'(a)=0$ at every $a$, so $f$ is constant. This proves <Liouville's theorem>.

Finally, if a nonconstant <entire function> omitted all points within distance $\epsilon$ of some $w$, then $g=1/(f-w)$ would be entire and bounded by $1/\epsilon$. <Liouville's theorem> would make $g$, and hence $f$, constant. Therefore every such neighborhood contains a value of $f$, proving the <dense image of a nonconstant entire function> property.

= Solution
{parent=2004/5/i}

Integrate $F(z)=e^{iz}/(z^2+1)$ around the real segment from $-R$ to $R$ and the counterclockwise upper semicircle, with $R>1$. The only enclosed <pole> is $i$, whose <residue> is $e^{-1}/(2i)$. On the semicircle, $|e^{iz}|\le1$ and $|z^2+1|\ge R^2-1$, so its integral has magnitude at most $\pi R/(R^2-1)\to0$.

The <residue theorem> thus gives $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{ix}/(x^2+1)\,dx=\pi e^{-1}$. The line integral converges absolutely, so taking its real part is justified:
$$
\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos x}{x^2+1}\,dx=\frac\pi e.}
$$

= ii
{parent=2004/5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2004/5/ii}

For a nonnegative <integer> $n$, put $z=e^{i\theta}$ on the <unit circle>. Since $\cos\theta=(z+z^{-1})/2$ and $d\theta=dz/(iz)$,
$$
\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^{2n}\theta\,d\theta
=\frac1{i4^n}\int_{|z|=1}\frac{(z^2+1)^{2n}}{z^{2n+1}}\,dz.
$$
The <residue> at zero is the coefficient of $z^{2n}$ in $(1+z^2)^{2n}$, namely $\binom{2n}{n}$. The <residue theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^{2n}\theta\,d\theta
=\frac{2\pi}{4^n}\binom{2n}{n}.}
$$
The contour integrand has no other <poles>. The formula also includes $n=0$, giving $2\pi$.

= Solution

A <Möbius transformation> is $M(z)=(az+b)/(cz+d)$ with $ad-bc\ne0$, extended to the <Riemann sphere> at its <pole> and at infinity. Wherever its finite coordinate is defined,
$$
M'(z)=\frac{ad-bc}{(cz+d)^2}\ne0.
$$
At a <pole>, the ordinary complex <derivative> is undefined, rather than zero; using the target coordinate $1/M$ gives a nonzero <derivative> there as well. Thus the source's nowhere-zero assertion is interpreted in local sphere coordinates, or on the finite domain with the <pole> excluded.

Composition corresponds to multiplication of the invertible <matrices> $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$, with scalar multiples giving the same map. The identity <matrix> gives the identity, and the inverse <matrix> gives an inverse Möbius map. This proves the group property on the <Riemann sphere>.

The two boundary circles meet at $\pm i$. Put
$$
\boxed{w=M(z)=\frac{z-i}{z+i},\qquad z=i\frac{1+w}{1-w}.}
$$
For $w=u+iv$, direct substitution gives
$$
|z+1|^2-2=\frac{4(u-v)}{|1-w|^2},\qquad
|z-1|^2-2=\frac{4(u+v)}{|1-w|^2}.
$$
This gives a <lens-to-half-plane map through the intersection points>. Both lens inequalities hold exactly when $u<-|v|$, the open sector with continuous argument between $3\pi/4$ and $5\pi/4$. This proves bijectivity, since the inverse is defined throughout that sector and the excluded endpoint $-i$ is not in the lens.

Rotate the sector through $-3\pi/4$ and square it, obtaining a bijection onto the <upper half-plane>. Therefore a <conformal map> is
$$
\boxed{F(z)=i\left(\frac{z-i}{z+i}\right)^2.}
$$
Squaring is injective on this sector because it contains no pair of opposite points. Its <derivative> and the Möbius <derivative> are nonzero in the interior, so the bijection is conformal.

A <critical point of a dynamical system> is a point where both components of the <vector field> vanish. For a continuously differentiable <vector field>, strictly negative real parts of both <eigenvalues> of its <Jacobian matrix> imply <asymptotic stability> of the equilibrium. If an isolated critical point is a <center of a planar dynamical system> and the <eigenvalues> are distinct, they must be a nonzero purely imaginary conjugate pair. Purely imaginary <eigenvalues> alone do not in general prove nonlinear stability; the <nonlinear ordinary differential equation> system must still be examined.

Here the critical points are $(0,0)$ and $(\pm1,0)$, with Jacobian $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1-3x^2&0\end{pmatrix}$. At zero its <eigenvalues> are $1,-1$, giving an unstable <saddle equilibrium>. The unstable direction is $(1,1)$ and the stable direction is $(1,-1)$. Thus trajectories leaving the origin are tangent to $y=x$, and those returning are tangent to $y=-x$, with both opposite rays included.

An exact <first integral> is
$$
H(x,y)=\frac{y^2}{2}-\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{x^4}{4},\qquad
\frac{dH}{dt}=(-x+x^3)y+y(x-x^3)=0.
$$
The two homoclinic loops belong to $H=0$:
$$
y^2=x^2\left(1-\frac{x^2}{2}\right),\qquad |x|\le\sqrt2.
$$
They confirm the incoming and outgoing tangent directions. For example the right loop is $x=\sqrt2\operatorname{sech}(t-t_0)$, $y=-\sqrt2\operatorname{sech}(t-t_0)\tanh(t-t_0)$; changing both signs gives the left loop.

\Image[2004-double-well-phase-portrait.png]
{title=Periodic energy curves and the two homoclinic loops of the nonlinear planar system}

At $(1,0)$ put $x=1+X$, $y=Y$. The exact equations are $X'=Y$, $Y'=-2X-3X^2-X^3$, so the <linearization of a dynamical system> gives $X''+2X=0$. Its nonconstant solutions have
$$
\boxed{T=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt2}=\sqrt2\pi,\qquad2X^2+Y^2=\text{constant}.}
$$
The same linear classification holds at $(-1,0)$. For the <nonlinear ordinary differential equation> system, $H$ has strict local minima $-1/4$ at both points. Levels $-1/4<H<0$ are two families of smooth closed ovals, proving that both are stable centers, rather than asymptotically stable equilibria. These give actual <periodic orbits> of the <nonlinear ordinary differential equation> system, with small-amplitude periods tending to $\sqrt2\pi$.

The <method of characteristics> uses
$$
\frac{dx}{ds}=a(x,y,u),\qquad\frac{dy}{ds}=b(x,y,u),\qquad\frac{du}{ds}=c(x,y,u).
$$
For a graph parametrized by $(x,y,u(x,y))$, a <normal vector> is $N=(-u_x,-u_y,1)$. The PDE says $N\cdot(a,b,c)=0$, so the characteristic vector is tangent to the solution surface. Flowing a suitable initial curve along these directions constructs a surface; where its projection to the $(x,y)$ plane has nonzero <Jacobian determinant>, the <inverse function theorem> makes it a graph. Local uniqueness of this general flow needs the usual regularity assumptions beyond mere continuity. The particular characteristic system below is smooth and can be solved explicitly.

Label the initial points by $\eta\ge0$. Since $dx/ds=1$, take $x=s$. The other two equations integrate to
$$
u=1+\sin\eta+\frac{x^2}{2},\qquad
y=\eta+x(1+\sin\eta)+\frac{x^3}{6}.
$$
Eliminating $\eta=y-xu+x^3/3$ gives the implicit solution
$$
\boxed{u=1+\frac{x^2}{2}+\sin\!\left(y-xu+\frac{x^3}{3}\right).}
$$
For each fixed $|x|<1$, the <derivative> of the displayed $y$ with respect to $\eta$ is $1+x\cos\eta\ge1-|x|>0$. It increases from its value at $\eta=0$ to infinity, so it is a bijection from $[0,\infty)$ onto $[y_L(x),\infty)$, where
$$
\boxed{y_L(x)=x+\frac{x^3}{6}.}
$$
Thus there is exactly one characteristic label, and hence one solution value, throughout the requested region, including the initial-label boundary as a trace.

The <characteristic fold for sinusoidal data in a forced Burgers equation> is seen by differentiating with respect to $y$:
$$
u_y=\frac{\cos\eta}{1+x\cos\eta}.
$$
For a fixed <integer> $k\ge0$, let $y_*=(2k+1)\pi+7/6$. As $x\to1^-$ at this fixed $y_*$, the label tends to $(2k+1)\pi$, giving $u_y\to-\infty$. To justify the label limit, the limiting map at $x=1$ is strictly increasing despite isolated zero <derivatives>, so its inverse has a unique continuous limit. Similarly, at any fixed $y_*=2k\pi-7/6$ with $k\ge1$, the limit as $x\to-1^+$ has $\eta\to2k\pi$ and $u_y\to+\infty$. These fixed values lie within the region near the respective boundary, proving the requested gradient blow-up.

= Solution

The <derivative> formula requires additional regularity: continuity alone does not define $f'$. Assume $f$ is continuously differentiable, or locally absolutely continuous with its almost-everywhere <derivative>. Integration by parts on $[0,R]$ gives
$$
\int_0^Re^{-pt}f'(t)dt=e^{-pR}f(R)-f(0)+p\int_0^Re^{-pt}f(t)dt.
$$
For $\operatorname{Re}p>c$, the given exponential bound makes the first term tend to zero. Thus the improper transform exists under these regularity assumptions and
$$
\boxed{\widehat{f'}(p)=p\widehat f(p)-f(0).}
$$
For the primitive, absolute integrability justifies reversing the integration order:
$$
\widehat h(p)=\int_0^\infty f(s)\int_s^\infty e^{-pt}dt\,ds
=\boxed{\frac{\widehat f(p)}p}.
$$
Indeed the corresponding absolute double integral is finite when $\operatorname{Re}p>c$.

For real $p>0$, substitution $t=s^2/p$ and the supplied <Gaussian integral> give
$$
\mathcal L[t^{-1/2}](p)=\frac2{\sqrt p}\int_0^\infty e^{-s^2}ds
=\boxed{\sqrt\pi\,p^{-1/2}.}
$$
Both sides are <holomorphic> for $\operatorname{Re}p>0$, so the <identity theorem> extends this equality there, taking the square root with positive real part. The integrable singularity at zero causes no difficulty for this improper integral. The primitive rule then gives $\mathcal L[2\sqrt t]=\sqrt\pi\,p^{-3/2}$.

For the conductor, take the <Laplace transform> in time and write $U(x,p)$. The initial temperature gives $U_{xx}=pU$. Use the usual physical condition that the temperature is bounded far down the conductor; this selects the decaying solution
$$
U(x,p)=A(p)e^{-\sqrt p\,x},\qquad\operatorname{Re}\sqrt p>0.
$$
The constant applied flux has transform $Q/p$, so $-kU_x(0,p)=Q/p$, giving $A(p)=Q/(kp^{3/2})$. This is the <surface temperature under constant heat flux on a half-line>. Using the transform just calculated yields
$$
\boxed{u(0,t)=\frac{2Q}{k\sqrt\pi}\sqrt t\qquad(t>0).}
$$
Here $k>0$ is the <thermal conductivity>. The far-field boundedness is the customary physical selection condition; without a spatial growth restriction the written PDE and initial/boundary data alone do not specify that selection. Within the physical solution, the face temperature tends continuously to zero as $t\downarrow0$.
