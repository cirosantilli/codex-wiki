= 1

= Solution
{parent=1}

Expanding gives $(I-E)^2-(I-E)=E^2-E$, so either operator is <idempotent> exactly when the other is. For an <idempotent> $E$,
$$
v=(v-Ev)+Ev,\qquad E(v-Ev)=0.
$$
If $w\in\ker E\cap\operatorname{Im}E$, write $w=Eu$; then $0=Ew=E^2u=Eu=w$. Hence $V=\ker E\oplus\operatorname{Im}E$.

Next,
$$
(E_1+E_2)^2-(E_1+E_2)=E_1E_2+E_2E_1.
$$
If the right side vanishes, left multiplication by $E_1$ gives $E_1E_2+E_1E_2E_1=0$, while right multiplication gives $E_1E_2E_1+E_2E_1=0$. Subtraction shows $E_1E_2=E_2E_1$, and their original sum now gives $2E_1E_2=0$. Over the real or complex numbers this implies **$E_1E_2=E_2E_1=0$**.

For three <idempotents> summing to $I$, each pair sum equals the complementary <idempotent> $I-E_k$. Thus every product of distinct operators vanishes. Every <vector> equals $E_1v+E_2v+E_3v$. If $v_1+v_2+v_3=0$ with $v_i\in\operatorname{Im}E_i$, applying $E_j$ gives $v_j=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{V=\operatorname{Im}E_1\oplus\operatorname{Im}E_2\oplus\operatorname{Im}E_3.}
$$
Take a <basis> of each summand and unite the three <bases>. A <vector> in the $j$th <basis> has <eigenvalue> one for $E_j$ and <zero> for the other two, so this is the requested common <eigenbasis>. <Zero> summands simply contribute no <basis> <vectors>.

= Solution
{parent=i}

A real <inner product> is a symmetric bilinear map $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:V\times V\to\mathbb R$ with $\langle v,v\rangle>0$ for $v\ne0$. It defines $\|v\|=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle}$. Set $W=\operatorname{span}\{e_1,\ldots,e_k\}$ and $Pv=\sum_i\langle v,e_i\rangle e_i$. Orthonormality gives $v-Pv\perp W$, hence the <Pythagorean identity> gives
$$
\|v\|^2=\|v-Pv\|^2+\sum_i\langle v,e_i\rangle^2.
$$
This proves <Bessel's inequality>. **Equality holds exactly when $v\in W$.**

= ii
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

If $v=0$, equality is immediate. If $v\ne0$, the nonnegative squared <norm>
$$
0\leq\left\|u-\frac{\langle u,v\rangle}{\|v\|^2}v\right\|^2=\|u\|^2-\frac{\langle u,v\rangle^2}{\|v\|^2}
$$
proves the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. Equality holds precisely when the displayed <vector> vanishes. Thus **equality is equivalent to <linear dependence> of $u,v$**, including the cases with a <zero> <vector>.

= iii
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The sum in question is $\langle Pu,Pv\rangle$. Applying Cauchy-Schwarz and then <Bessel's inequality> gives
$$
|\langle Pu,Pv\rangle|^2\leq\|Pu\|^2\|Pv\|^2\leq\|u\|^2\|v\|^2.
$$
If either original <vector> is <zero>, equality holds. If both are nonzero, equality forces both projection <norms> to equal their original <norms> and also forces dependence of the projected <vectors>. Thus **apart from the <zero>-<vector> cases, equality holds exactly when $u,v\in W$ and $u,v$ are <linearly dependent>**.

For the final unnumbered request, apply Cauchy-Schwarz in $\mathbb R^k$ to $p_i=a^{i/2}$ and $q_i=a^{-i/2}$. Their <inner product> is $k$, so
$$
\boxed{\left(\sum_{i=1}^ka^i\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^ka^{-i}\right)\geq k^2.}
$$
Equality requires the positive <vectors> $p,q$ to be proportional, equivalently $a^i$ constant for $i=1,\ldots,k$. For $k\geq2$ this is **$a=1$**. If $k=1$, equality holds for every $a>0$.

= Solution
{parent=i}

An <integral domain> is a nonzero <commutative ring> with identity and no <zero divisors>. A <principal ideal domain> is an <integral domain> in which every <ideal> is generated by one element.

The <leading coefficient> of a product of nonzero real <polynomials> is nonzero, so $\mathbb R[x]$ is an <integral domain>. The <zero> <ideal> is principal. In any nonzero <ideal> $J$, choose a nonzero <polynomial> $g$ of least <degree>. Divide an arbitrary $f\in J$ by $g$: $f=qg+r$ with $r=0$ or $\deg r<\deg g$. Since $r\in J$, minimality forces $r=0$. Thus $J=(g)$ and **$\mathbb R[x]$ is a <principal ideal domain>**.

In $\mathbb Z[x]$ consider $J=(2,x)$. If $J=(h)$, then $h$ divides the constant <polynomial> 2, so $h$ is constant, and divisibility of $x$ forces $h=\pm1$. That would give $J=\mathbb Z[x]$. But the constant coefficient of every element $2p+xq$ is even, so $1\notin J$. This contradiction proves **$\mathbb Z[x]$ is not a <principal ideal domain>**.

= ii
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let $J=\bigcup_nI_n$. It contains <zero>. If $a,b\in J$, both belong to $I_N$ for some common $N$, so $a-b\in I_N\subseteq J$. If $r\in R$, then $ra\in I_N\subseteq J$. These are the <ideal> axioms, proving $J$ is an <ideal>.

In a <principal ideal domain> write $J=(g)$. Since $g\in J$, there is $n_0$ with $g\in I_{n_0}$. For $n\geq n_0$,
$$
(g)\subseteq I_{n_0}\subseteq I_n\subseteq J=(g).
$$
All inclusions are equalities. Hence **the chain stabilizes at $I_{n_0}$**. This includes $J=(0)$ and is the <ascending chain condition> for <ideals>.

= Solution
{parent=i}

If $f$ is <holomorphic> on a neighborhood of the closed disc, the differentiated <Cauchy integral formula> is
$$
f^{(k)}(a)=\frac{k!}{2\pi i}\int_{|z-a|=r}\frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^{k+1}}\,dz,
$$
with positive orientation. On the circle the integrand has modulus at most $M/r^{k+1}$ and the <contour> has length $2\pi r$. Thus
$$
\boxed{|f^{(k)}(a)|\leq\frac{k!M}{r^k}.}
$$

= ii
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Take a circle of radius $R\geq S$ about <zero>. The <Cauchy estimate> gives, for each integer $k>n$,
$$
|f^{(k)}(0)|\leq k!CR^{n-k}\longrightarrow0\quad(R\to\infty).
$$
Therefore all these derivatives vanish. The <Taylor series> of an <entire function> converges to it everywhere, and hence
$$
\boxed{f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}z^k.}
$$
As usual $n$ is a nonnegative integer; if a real exponent is allowed, the <degree> is at most its integer part. A negative growth exponent forces $f=0$.

= iii
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For a bounded <entire function>, the preceding result with $n=0$ gives <Liouville's theorem>. If a nonconstant <polynomial> $p$ had no complex root, $1/p$ would be entire. The leading term gives $|p(z)|\to\infty$ at infinity, so $1/p$ is bounded outside a large disc and is also bounded on the remaining compact disc. <Liouville's theorem> would make $1/p$, and hence $p$, constant. This proves the <fundamental theorem of algebra>.

Finally, away from the <zeros> of $\cos z$, the <holomorphic> quotient $h=f/\cos z$ has modulus at most $C$. At each <zero> it therefore has a <removable singularity>. These isolated points have no finite accumulation, so removing all of them gives a bounded <entire function>. <Liouville's theorem> gives a constant $c$ with $|c|\leq C$, and
$$
\boxed{f(z)=c\cos z.}
$$
Conversely every such multiple satisfies the assumed bound.

= i
{parent=5}

= Solution
{parent=i}

Integrate $e^{iz}/z$ around the upper half-disc with the upper half-disc of radius $\varepsilon$ about <zero> removed. The real segments run from $-R$ to $-\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon$ to $R$; the small semicircle is clockwise. There are no poles inside this indented <contour>.

The large arc contribution tends to <zero>: its modulus is at most $\int_0^\pi e^{-R\sin\theta}\,d\theta$, which tends to <zero> by <dominated convergence>. On the small arc $z=\varepsilon e^{i\theta}$, $\theta$ decreases from $\pi$ to <zero>, so its integral tends to $\int_\pi^0i\,d\theta=-i\pi$. The <contour> theorem consequently gives
$$
\operatorname{PV}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ix}}x\,dx=i\pi.
$$
Taking imaginary parts is legitimate for each truncated integral. The imaginary integrand $\sin x/x$ is continuous at <zero> after assigning its limiting value one, so the inner indentation can be removed. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{-R}^{R}\frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx=\pi.}
$$

= ii
{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$ and integrate $1/(1+z^3)$ around the positively oriented sector from argument <zero> to $2\pi/3$, with outer radius $R>1$. The single pole inside is $z_0=e^{i\pi/3}$, with <residue> $1/(3z_0^2)=1/(3\omega)$. The outward real radial segment contributes $I_R=\int_0^R dx/(1+x^3)$; the return radial segment contributes $-\omega I_R$ because $(\omega x)^3=x^3$.

On the circular arc, $|1+z^3|\geq R^3-1$, so its integral has modulus at most $(2\pi R/3)/(R^3-1)\to0$. Thus the <residue theorem> yields
$$
(1-\omega)I=\frac{2\pi i}{3\omega}.
$$
Since $\omega(1-\omega)=\omega-\omega^2=i\sqrt3$,
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^3}=\frac{2\pi}{3\sqrt3}.}
$$
The ordinary improper integral converges at both endpoints, so no principal-value interpretation is needed here.

= 6
{parent=Analysis}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=6}

For $y''+p(x)y'+q(x)y=0$, a singular point at <zero> is regular when $xp(x)$ and $x^2q(x)$ extend analytically to <zero>. Here
$$
p(x)=\frac{\lambda-x}{x(1-x)},\qquad q(x)=\frac1{x(1-x)},
$$
so these products are analytic at <zero>. The equation remains singular there because $q$ has a pole.

Substitute $y=\sum_{n\geq0}a_nx^{n+\sigma}$. The lowest power gives the <indicial equation> $\sigma(\sigma-1+\lambda)=0$. Comparing the next powers gives
$$
\boxed{\sigma=0\text{ or }1-\lambda,\qquad a_{n+1}=\frac{(n+\sigma)^2-1}{(n+\sigma+1)(n+\sigma+\lambda)}a_n.}
$$
For $0<\lambda<1$ the denominators do not vanish. With $\sigma=0$, $a_1=-a_0/\lambda$ and $a_2=0$, so
$$
y_1=a_0(1-x/\lambda),\qquad\boxed{R_1=\infty.}
$$
With $\sigma=1-\lambda$, the recurrence becomes
$$
a_{n+1}=\frac{(n-\lambda)(n+2-\lambda)}{(n+2-\lambda)(n+1)}a_n=\frac{n-\lambda}{n+1}a_n.
$$
Consequently $y_2=a_0x^{1-\lambda}(1-x)^\lambda$ on $0<x<1$, with the <binomial series> for its last factor. The nonzero coefficient ratios tend to one, giving **radius one for that power-series factor**. The factor $x^{1-\lambda}$ specifies the Frobenius branch; the different leading powers prove independence.

When $\lambda=1$, both indicial roots become <zero> and the analytic Frobenius solution in both cases is a multiple of $1-x$. To obtain the missing solution use <reduction of order>. Away from <zero> and one, $p=1/x$, hence
$$
y_2=(1-x)\int\frac{dx}{x(1-x)^2}=1+(1-x)\log\left|\frac{x}{1-x}\right|.
$$
Thus the real general solution on any interval avoiding the singular points is
$$
\boxed{y=C_1(1-x)+C_2\left[1+(1-x)\log\left|\frac{x}{1-x}\right|\right].}
$$
Its <Wronskian> with $1-x$ is $1/x\ne0$, proving independence. For complex solutions choose a logarithm branch on a suitable simply connected region.

{parent=Differential equations}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=7}

On $[-a,a]$ let $F=\|f\|_\infty$ and bound $|K|$ by $M$ on the compact integration region. Write $Th(x)=\int_0^xK(x,s)h(s)\,ds$. The iteration gives $y_n=\sum_{j=0}^nT^jf$. Induction, integrating absolute values also when $x<0$, gives
$$
|T^jf(x)|\leq F\frac{M^j|x|^j}{j!}.
$$
The exponential series is summable uniformly for $|x|\leq a$. The <Weierstrass M-test> therefore proves <uniform convergence> to a <continuous function> $y$. Also $\|T(y_n-y)\|_\infty\leq Ma\|y_n-y\|_\infty\to0$, so passing to the limit yields $y=f+Ty$.

For the indicated kernel, twice differentiating its integral gives
$$
y'(x)=\int_0^x sy(s)\,ds,\qquad y''(x)=xy(x),\qquad y(0)=1,\quad y'(0)=0.
$$
Conversely integrating the differential equation twice, with these <initial conditions>, gives the integral equation. This is valid also for negative $x$, either by oriented integration or by differentiating the double-integral identity.

For every finite $a$ the continuous kernel $(x-s)s$ meets the preceding hypotheses. Solutions on overlapping intervals agree: if $d=Td$ is their difference, iteration gives $\|d\|_\infty\leq\|d\|_\infty(Ma)^j/j!$ for every $j$, forcing $d=0$. The solutions therefore patch to **one solution defined on all of $\mathbb R$**.

= 8
{parent=Differential equations}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=8}

Put
$$
z(x)=-\int_0^x\sin(x-s)f(s)\,ds.
$$
Leibniz differentiation gives $z'=-\int_0^x\cos(x-s)f(s)\,ds$ and $z''=-f(x)+\int_0^x\sin(x-s)f(s)\,ds=-f-z$. Thus $z$ satisfies both <zero> <initial conditions> and the inhomogeneous equation. Every solution with $y(0)=0$ is $y=A\sin x+z(x)$.

On $[0,1]$, the second endpoint determines $A=-z(1)/\sin1$ uniquely, since $\sin1\ne0$. Collecting the integrals, and using the <sine addition formula> for $s<x$, gives
$$
\boxed{G(x,s)=\frac{\sin(\min\{x,s\})\sin(1-\max\{x,s\})}{\sin1}.}
$$
This continuous kernel vanishes at the endpoints, satisfies $G_{xx}+G=0$ off $x=s$, and has derivative jump $-1$, as required for $-y''-y=f$. The preceding construction proves existence, and a homogeneous solution with both endpoints <zero> must vanish, proving uniqueness.

On $[0,\pi]$, instead, the second boundary value becomes
$$
y(\pi)=z(\pi)=-\int_0^\pi f(s)\sin s\,ds.
$$
Thus **solvability is equivalent to $\int_0^\pi f(s)\sin s\,ds=0$**. When it holds, all solutions are
$$
\boxed{y(x)=A\sin x-\int_0^x\sin(x-s)f(s)\,ds,\qquad A\in\mathbb R.}
$$
There is one free homogeneous mode because the endpoint length is resonant.

= 9
{parent=Differential equations}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=9}

For a nonzero principal part the <discriminant> $\Delta=b^2-ac$ classifies the equation: it is **hyperbolic for $\Delta>0$, parabolic for $\Delta=0$, and elliptic for $\Delta<0$**. These are the three definitions requested by the inline roman labels.

In the hyperbolic case the <characteristic equation> $a(dy)^2-2b\,dx\,dy+c(dx)^2=0$ gives two distinct real tangent directions. Locally, with suitably smooth coefficients, solve the corresponding characteristic <ordinary differential equations> to obtain two independent families of curves. Label their level sets $\phi=\text{constant}$ and $\psi=\text{constant}$. Their gradients satisfy
$$
a\phi_x^2+2b\phi_x\phi_y+c\phi_y^2=0,\qquad a\psi_x^2+2b\psi_x\psi_y+c\psi_y^2=0,
$$
and the coordinate Jacobian is nonzero. The <chain rule> therefore eliminates $u_{\phi\phi}$ and $u_{\psi\psi}$. The remaining mixed coefficient $2[a\phi_x\psi_x+b(\phi_x\psi_y+\phi_y\psi_x)+c\phi_y\psi_y]$ is nonzero: otherwise the invertible coordinate change would send the nondegenerate principal quadratic form to <zero>. Divide by this coefficient and absorb all first-order terms into $g$, giving $u_{\phi\psi}=g(\phi,\psi,u,u_\phi,u_\psi)$. This canonical reduction is local; a global chart is not automatic.

For the specific equation $a=y,b=0,c=-1$, so it is hyperbolic exactly on **$y>0$**, elliptic for $y<0$, and degenerate parabolic on $y=0$. For $\phi=3x-2y^{3/2}$ and $\psi=3x+2y^{3/2}$, the Jacobian is $18\sqrt y\ne0$. Direct differentiation gives
$$
yu_{xx}-u_{yy}=36y\,u_{\phi\psi}+\frac3{2\sqrt y}(u_\phi-u_\psi).
$$
Since $\psi-\phi=4y^{3/2}$, the canonical form is
$$
\boxed{u_{\phi\psi}=\frac{u_\psi-u_\phi}{6(\psi-\phi)}.}
$$
The characteristic families are $3x-2y^{3/2}=c$ and $3x+2y^{3/2}=c$, with slopes $dx/dy=\pm\sqrt y$. They are distinct for $y>0$ and meet the degeneracy line with coincident vertical tangent directions.
