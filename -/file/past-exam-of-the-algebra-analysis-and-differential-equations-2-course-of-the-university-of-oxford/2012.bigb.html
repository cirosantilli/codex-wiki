The <minimal polynomial> $m_T(t)$ is the unique monic <polynomial> of least positive <polynomial degree> satisfying $m_T(T)=0$. Such an annihilating <polynomial> exists without using the <Cayley-Hamilton theorem>: the <vector space> $\operatorname{End}(V)$ has <dimension of a vector space> equal to $n^2$, so $I,T,\ldots,T^{n^2}$ are <linearly dependent>. Normalize a nonzero annihilating <polynomial> to be monic and choose one of least degree. Two distinct monic choices of that degree would have an annihilating difference of lower degree, contradicting minimality. A nonzero constant cannot annihilate $T$ because $V\ne0$.

Choose a <basis> $B$ and let $A=[T]_B$. The <characteristic polynomial> is
$$
\boxed{\chi_T(t)=\det(tI-A).}
$$
It is independent of the chosen <basis>. Indeed a change of basis replaces $A$ by a <similar matrix> $P^{-1}AP$, and
$$
\det(tI-P^{-1}AP)=\det\bigl(P^{-1}(tI-A)P\bigr)=\det(tI-A)
$$
by multiplicativity of the <determinant>. Thus this monic degree-$n$ <polynomial> is an invariant of the <linear transformation> itself.

Since $v$ is a <cyclic vector> and $V\ne0$, $v\ne0$. Finite <dimension of a vector space> forces a first dependence in the sequence $v,Tv,T^2v,\ldots$. Let $k\geq1$ be the smallest index with $T^kv$ in the <linear span> of its predecessors. Then
$$
B=(v,Tv,\ldots,T^{k-1}v)
$$
is <linearly independent>. Its <linear span> $W$ is invariant under $T$: all but the last <basis> candidate map to the next, and the last maps to $T^kv\in W$. Induction therefore puts every $T^jv$ in $W$. Cyclicity gives $W=V$, so **$B$ is a <basis> and $k=n$**.

Write
$$
T^nv=-a_0v-a_1Tv-\cdots-a_{n-1}T^{n-1}v.
$$
The <matrix> of $T$ in this <basis> is the <companion matrix>
$$
C=\begin{pmatrix}
0&0&\cdots&0&-a_0\\
1&0&\cdots&0&-a_1\\
0&1&\cdots&0&-a_2\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1&-a_{n-1}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
For $p(t)=t^n+a_{n-1}t^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0$, the defining relation says $p(T)v=0$. Since $p(T)$ commutes with $T$, it also annihilates every $T^jv$ in $B$, hence $p(T)=0$. No nonzero <polynomial> of degree less than $n$ can annihilate $v$, because that would contradict <linear independence> of $B$. Thus $m_T=p$.

Finally the <characteristic polynomial of a companion matrix> is $p$, by direct <determinant> expansion. Explicitly, expansion along the first row of $tI-C$ gives the recurrence
$$
D_n(t;a_0,\ldots,a_{n-1})
=tD_{n-1}(t;a_1,\ldots,a_{n-1})+a_0,
$$
starting from $D_1=t+a_0$. It yields $D_n=t^n+a_{n-1}t^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{m_T(t)=\chi_T(t).}
$$
This proves that a <cyclic operator has equal minimal and characteristic polynomials> by the cyclic <basis> and a direct determinant calculation, with no appeal to the <Cayley-Hamilton theorem>.

Equivalently, its <minimal polynomial> is $(t-1)^2$, while its <characteristic polynomial> is $(t-1)^3$.

An **<orthonormal basis>** $(q_1,\ldots,q_n)$ is a <basis> with $\langle q_i,q_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}$, where $\delta_{ij}$ is the <Kronecker delta>. Each <vector> has <norm> one and distinct <vectors> are <orthogonal>.

The <Gram-Schmidt process> starts from a <basis> $(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$ and defines
$$
u_1=e_1,\qquad q_1=\frac{u_1}{\|u_1\|},\qquad
u_k=e_k-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\langle e_k,q_j\rangle q_j,\qquad
q_k=\frac{u_k}{\|u_k\|}.
$$
The <coefficients> are in this order because the <inner product> is linear in its first argument. For $i<k$, taking the <inner product> with $q_i$ gives $\langle u_k,q_i\rangle=0$. The residual $u_k$ cannot be zero: otherwise $e_k$ would belong to the <linear span> of $e_1,\ldots,e_{k-1}$, contradicting the initial <basis>. Normalization makes $\|q_k\|=1$, while induction preserves
$$
\operatorname{span}(q_1,\ldots,q_k)=\operatorname{span}(e_1,\ldots,e_k).
$$
Thus the process produces an <orthonormal basis> of all of $V$.

In an <orthonormal basis>, every <vector> $w$ has the coordinate expansion $w=\sum_i\langle w,e_i\rangle e_i$. If $\langle Su,v\rangle=\langle u,Rv\rangle$ for all $u,v$, apply it to $u=e_i$, $v=e_j$ and take complex conjugates:
$$
\langle Re_j,e_i\rangle=\langle e_j,Se_i\rangle.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{Re_j=\sum_{i=1}^n\langle e_j,Se_i\rangle e_i.}
$$
Conversely, this formula gives
$$
\langle e_i,Re_j\rangle=\overline{\langle e_j,Se_i\rangle}=\langle Se_i,e_j\rangle.
$$
Linearity in the first argument and conjugate-linearity in the second extend the equality from <basis> <vectors> to all $u,v$. This proves the equivalence.

The displayed formula specifies exactly one image for each <basis> <vector>, so it defines a unique <linear transformation> $S^*$ and proves the existence and uniqueness of the <adjoint on an inner product space>.

Conjugate symmetry gives
$$
\langle S^*u,v\rangle
=\overline{\langle v,S^*u\rangle}
=\overline{\langle Sv,u\rangle}
=\langle u,Sv\rangle.
$$
By uniqueness of the <adjoint operator>, $\boxed{(S^*)^*=S}$. Also
$$
\langle STu,v\rangle=\langle Tu,S^*v\rangle
=\langle u,T^*S^*v\rangle,
$$
so uniqueness gives $\boxed{(ST)^*=T^*S^*}$. The reversal of order follows directly from moving the two factors across the <inner product> one at a time.

For a nonzero <eigenvector> $u$ of $S^*S$,
$$
\|Su\|^2=\langle Su,Su\rangle
=\langle u,S^*Su\rangle
=\langle u,\lambda u\rangle
=\overline\lambda\|u\|^2.
$$
Therefore $\overline\lambda=\|Su\|^2/\|u\|^2$ is real and nonnegative, and so **every <eigenvalue> $\lambda$ is real and nonnegative**.

The preceding adjoint identities give $(S^*S)^*=S^*S$, so this <linear operator> is <self-adjoint>. The stated finite-dimensional <spectral theorem> supplies an <orthonormal basis> $e_j$ with $S^*Se_j=\lambda_j e_j$. If $v=\sum_jc_je_j$, then
$$
\|v\|^2=\sum_j|c_j|^2,\qquad
\|Sv\|^2=\langle v,S^*Sv\rangle=\sum_j\lambda_j|c_j|^2.
$$
Since $\lambda_-\leq\lambda_j\leq\lambda_+$,
$$
\lambda_-\|v\|^2\leq\|Sv\|^2\leq\lambda_+\|v\|^2.
$$
Taking nonnegative <square roots> proves
$$
\boxed{\sqrt{\lambda_-}\|v\|\leq\|Sv\|\leq\sqrt{\lambda_+}\|v\|.}
$$
The square roots of these extreme <eigenvalues> are the smallest and largest <singular values> of $S$; the lower bound can be zero if $S$ has a nontrivial <kernel of a linear map>.

A **<unit in a ring>** is an element $u$ with a multiplicative inverse in $R$, so $uv=1$ for some $v\in R$.

An **<irreducible element>** is a nonzero nonunit $a$ such that every factorization $a=bc$ has at least one of $b,c$ a <unit in a ring>.

A **<prime element>** is a nonzero nonunit $p$ such that $p\mid bc$ implies $p\mid b$ or $p\mid c$. Equivalently its <principal ideal> $(p)$ is a nonzero <prime ideal>. These are definitions in the given commutative <ring>; the equivalence between irreducibility and primality requires additional hypotheses on the <ring>.

= Solution

A <Euclidean domain> is an <integral domain> $R$ with a <function> $d:R\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb N_0$ such that, for $a\in R$ and nonzero $b\in R$, there exist $q,r\in R$ with $a=qb+r$ and either $r=0$ or $d(r)<d(b)$. This is the Euclidean-ring convention used here.

If an <ideal> $I$ is zero, it is generated by zero. Otherwise choose nonzero $a\in I$ with $d(a)$ minimal. For any $b\in I$, Euclidean division gives $b=qa+r$, with $r\in I$. A nonzero remainder would have $d(r)<d(a)$, contradicting the choice. Hence $r=0$ and $b\in aR$. The reverse inclusion follows from $a\in I$, proving
$$
\boxed{I=aR.}
$$
Now suppose $a$ is an <irreducible element>. Its <principal ideal> is proper because $a$ is not a unit. If $aR\subseteq J\subseteq R$, write $J=bR$ by the result just proved. Then $a=bc$ for some $c\in R$. Irreducibility gives either $b$ a unit, so $J=R$, or $c$ a unit, so $bR=aR$. There is no intermediate proper <ideal>, and therefore **$aR$ is a <maximal ideal>**.

For a <field> $F$, the <polynomial ring> $F[X]$ is an <integral domain> and the <polynomial division algorithm> gives $A=QB+R$ with $R=0$ or $\deg R<\deg B$. Thus the <polynomial degree> is a Euclidean function, proving **$F[X]$ is a <Euclidean domain>**.

= Solution

The <polynomial> $X^n-2$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ by <Eisenstein's criterion> with prime two: all <coefficients> except the leading one are divisible by two, while the constant <coefficient> is not divisible by four. Together with <Gauss's lemma>, this establishes irreducibility in $\mathbb Q[X]$. Part (b) shows that its <principal ideal> is a <maximal ideal>, so
$$
\boxed{K_n=\mathbb Q[X]/(X^n-2)\text{ is a field}.}
$$
Let $\alpha$ denote the residue class of $X$. The <polynomial division algorithm> gives every residue class a representative of degree less than $n$, so $1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^{n-1}$ span $K_n$ over $\mathbb Q$. If a <linear combination> of them vanished, its degree-less-than-$n$ representative would be a multiple of the degree-$n$ <polynomial> $X^n-2$, and hence would be zero. They are therefore a <basis>, giving
$$
\boxed{\dim_{\mathbb Q}K_n=n.}
$$
The <quadratic polynomial> $X^2+1$ has no rational root, so it is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$. The same maximal-ideal argument proves **$L=\mathbb Q[X]/(X^2+1)$ is a <field>**.

These two quadratic fields are **not isomorphic**. In $L$, the class of $X$ squares to $-1$. But $K_2$ embeds in $\mathbb R$ by sending its class of $X$ to $\sqrt2$: evaluation at $\sqrt2$ has kernel $(X^2-2)$, by irreducibility and <polynomial> division. No <real number> squares to $-1$, so no element of $K_2$ does. A <field isomorphism> preserves one, minus one and squaring, so it cannot carry $L$ to $K_2$.

The <Cauchy integral formula> says that if $f$ is <holomorphic> on the <simply connected> <open set> $U$, and $\gamma$ is a positively oriented <simple closed curve> in $U$, then, for every $z$ in its interior,
$$
\boxed{f(z)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\gamma\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}\,d\zeta.}
$$
Here the contour and its enclosed region lie in $U$; the contour can be taken piecewise continuously differentiable. More generally, the same formula needs only <holomorphic function> on an <open neighbourhood> of that closed region. The orientation is counterclockwise, so the <winding number> about an interior point is one.

Write $M=|f(z_0)|$. The local maximum property gives a <disk> around $z_0$ on which $|f|\leq M$, contained in the <unit disk>. If $M=0$, then $f=0$ on that neighbourhood, and the <identity theorem> makes $f$ zero on the connected <unit disk>.

Otherwise multiply $f$ by the unit <complex number> $\overline{f(z_0)}/M$, obtaining a <holomorphic function> $g$ with $g(z_0)=M$ and $|g|\leq M$ nearby. Applying the <Cauchy integral formula> on any sufficiently small circle of radius $r$ gives the <mean value property>
$$
M=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}g(z_0+re^{it})\,dt.
$$
Take real parts. The continuous function $M-\operatorname{Re}g$ is nonnegative on the circle and has <integral> zero, so it vanishes everywhere there. Since $|g|\leq M$, having real part $M$ forces imaginary part zero. Thus $g=M$ throughout the circle. The zeros of $g-M$ have an accumulation point inside its domain, so the <identity theorem> gives **$f$ constant on the entire <unit disk>**. This proves the local <maximum modulus principle> directly from the <Cauchy integral formula>.

The closed <unit disk> is a <compact set>, and $|f|$ is <continuous> on it because $f$ is <holomorphic> on an <open neighbourhood>. The <extreme value theorem> therefore supplies a point where its maximum is attained. If such a point is on the boundary, the conclusion follows. If it is in the interior, part (i) makes $f$ constant on the open disk; <continuity> then gives the same value on the boundary. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\max_{|z|\leq1}|f(z)|=\max_{|z|=1}|f(z)|.}
$$
The assumption that the closed disk lies in $U$ is what makes the boundary values and the compactness argument available.

Because $f(0)=0$, the <Taylor series> of $f$ at zero shows that
$$
h(z)=\begin{cases}f(z)/z,&z\ne0,\\f'(0),&z=0\end{cases}

is <holomorphic> throughout the <unit disk>. Fix $0<r<1$. On $|z|=r$ we have $|h(z)|<1/r$, and the <maximum modulus principle> on the closed disk of radius $r$ gives $|h(z)|\leq1/r$ for $|z|\leq r$. For any fixed $z$ inside the <unit disk> we may let $r$ increase to one, obtaining $|h(z)|\leq1$. Hence the <Schwarz lemma> inequalities are
$$
\boxed{|f(z)|\leq|z|,\qquad |f'(0)|\leq1.}
$$
The <derivative> bound is the same estimate at $z=0$, using the holomorphic extension of the quotient.

The <Laurent theorem> states that a <holomorphic function> on the <annulus> $R<|z-a|<S$ has a unique <Laurent series>
$$
\boxed{f(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_n(z-a)^n,\qquad c_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta-a|=\rho}\frac{f(\zeta)}{(\zeta-a)^{n+1}}\,d\zeta,\quad R<\rho<S.}
$$
The integration circle is counterclockwise. The series converges absolutely at every point of the annulus and uniformly on every compact subannulus. The <coefficient> <integrals> are independent of the choice of $\rho$ by the <Cauchy integral theorem> applied between two such circles. These statements include $R=0$ or $S=\infty$ when the indicated annulus has those endpoints.

An **<isolated singularity>** at $z_0$ means that some radius $r>0$ has $f$ <holomorphic> on the punctured disk $0<|z-z_0|<r$, while $f$ is not initially defined as a <holomorphic function> at $z_0$. The adjective isolated refers to the absence of other singularities in this punctured neighbourhood; it permits a <removable singularity>.

The point is a **<removable singularity>** if assigning a value to $f(z_0)$ makes $f$ <holomorphic> on a full neighbourhood of $z_0$. Equivalently, its <Laurent series> has no nonzero <coefficients> of negative powers. The assigned value is its constant <coefficient>, or equivalently the finite limit $\lim_{z\to z_0}f(z)$.

A **<pole>** of order $n\geq1$ means
$$
\boxed{f(z)=(z-z_0)^{-n}h(z),\qquad h\text{ holomorphic near }z_0,\quad h(z_0)\ne0.}
$$
Equivalently, the <Laurent series> has a finite nonzero <principal part>, with lowest power exactly $(z-z_0)^{-n}$. Thus $(z-z_0)^nf(z)$ extends holomorphically with nonzero value at $z_0$; using a smaller positive power would not remove the singularity.

An **<essential singularity>** is an <isolated singularity> that is neither a <removable singularity> nor a <pole>. In its <Laurent series>, infinitely many <coefficients> of negative powers are nonzero. The alternatives follow because no negative terms give a removable singularity, while finitely many with a lowest nonzero power give a pole.

Inside the unit circle, use the <geometric series> for $1/(1-z)$:
$$
\boxed{\frac1{z^2(z-1)}=-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{n-2}=-\frac1{z^2}-\frac1z-1-z-z^2-\cdots,\qquad0<|z|<1.}
$$
Outside the unit circle, expand in powers of $1/z$ instead:
$$
\boxed{\frac1{z^2(z-1)}=\frac1{z^3}\frac1{1-z^{-1}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{-n-3},\qquad |z|>1.}
$$
The different <Laurent series> reflect different annuli separated by the <pole> at one. Both follow from absolutely convergent <geometric series> in their stated regions.

Take the <Laurent series> of $f$ on $0<|z|<r$. For every integer $k\geq1$, its <coefficient> of $z^{-k}$ satisfies, on a circle of radius $0<\rho<r$,
$$
|c_{-k}|=\left|\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta|=\rho}f(\zeta)\zeta^{k-1}\,d\zeta\right|\leq m\rho^k.
$$
The <coefficient> is independent of $\rho$. Letting $\rho\downarrow0$ therefore gives $c_{-k}=0$ for every $k\geq1$. The <Laurent series> is a <power series> with no negative powers and supplies a <holomorphic extension> at zero. Thus **zero is a <removable singularity>**. This is the <Riemann removable singularity theorem>, with boundedness used to eliminate the entire principal part.

= Solution

Suppose, towards a contradiction, that for some $r,w,\varepsilon$ the punctured-disk image misses the open disk of radius $\varepsilon$ about $w$. Then
$$
h(z)=\frac1{f(z)-w}
$$
is <holomorphic> and bounded by $1/\varepsilon$ on $0<|z|<r$. Part (i) extends it to a <holomorphic function> $H$ on the full disk. If $H(0)\ne0$, then $w+1/H$ extends $f$ holomorphically at zero, making zero a <removable singularity>.

If $H(0)=0$, the function $H$ is not identically zero, since it is nonzero on the punctured disk. The <Taylor series> and <identity theorem> give a finite zero order $k\geq1$, so $H(z)=z^kq(z)$ with $q$ holomorphic and $q(0)\ne0$. Hence
$$
f(z)=w+z^{-k}/q(z)
$$
has a <pole> of order $k$. Both alternatives contradict an <essential singularity>. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\overline{f(\{z:0<|z|<r\})}=\mathbb C\quad\text{for every }0<r<s.}
$$
This is the <Casorati-Weierstrass theorem>: every complex value can be approximated arbitrarily closely in every punctured neighbourhood of an essential singularity.

The <tangent half-angle substitution> $u=\tan(t/2)$ maps $0\leq t<\pi$ to $0\leq u<\infty$, with
$$
\cos t=\frac{1-u^2}{1+u^2},\qquad dt=\frac{2\,du}{1+u^2}.
$$
Because $a>1$, both $a+1$ and $a-1$ are positive. Thus
$$
\int_0^\pi\frac{dt}{a+\cos t}=2\int_0^\infty\frac{du}{(a+1)+(a-1)u^2}=\frac2{\sqrt{a^2-1}}\left[\arctan\left(u\sqrt{\frac{a-1}{a+1}}\right)\right]_0^\infty.
$$
Evaluating the <improper integral> gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{a^2-1}}.}
$$
Although the change of variable has an infinite endpoint, the original integrand is continuous on the closed interval, and the transformed integrand decays as $u^{-2}$, so the limiting substitution is justified.

Use the <branch of the complex logarithm> with $0<\arg z<2\pi$, so the <branch cut> is the positive real axis, and take $\sqrt z=\exp(\operatorname{Log}z/2)$ on the same slit plane. Apply the <residue theorem> to
$$
F(z)=\frac{\operatorname{Log}z}{(z^2+1)\sqrt z}
$$
on a positively oriented <keyhole contour> with inner radius $\rho<1$ and outer radius $R>1$. The upper bank travels from $\rho$ to $R$, the large circle counterclockwise, the lower bank from $R$ to $\rho$, and the small circle clockwise. One may first keep the banks at small nonzero angles and then take their limits.

On the upper bank, $\operatorname{Log}x=\log x$ and $\sqrt x$ is positive. On the lower bank, the corresponding limits are $\log x+2\pi i$ and $-\sqrt x$. Reversing the lower-bank orientation therefore makes the sum of the bank <integrals> tend to
$$
2I+2\pi iJ,\qquad I=\int_0^\infty\frac{\log x}{(x^2+1)\sqrt x}\,dx,\qquad J=\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{(x^2+1)\sqrt x}.
$$
Both are absolutely convergent: near zero compare with $x^{-1/2}(1+|\log x|)$, and near infinity with $x^{-5/2}(1+\log x)$. On the large circle, $|z^2+1|\geq R^2-1$, so its contribution is $O((\log R+1)R^{-3/2})$. On the small circle it is $O(\sqrt\rho(1+|\log\rho|))$. Both tend to zero.

\Image[2012-keyhole.png]
{title=The <keyhole contour> and the two boundary values on its <branch cut>}

The two simple <poles> are $i$ and $-i$. On the chosen branch their arguments are $\pi/2$ and $3\pi/2$, respectively. Consequently their <residues> are
$$
\operatorname{Res}(F,i)=\frac{\pi}{4\sqrt2}(1-i),\qquad \operatorname{Res}(F,-i)=\frac{3\pi}{4\sqrt2}(1+i).
$$
For example, the first follows from $\operatorname{Log}i=i\pi/2$, $\sqrt i=e^{i\pi/4}$ and the <derivative> $2i$ of the denominator factor $z^2+1$. The second uses $\sqrt{-i}=e^{3\pi i/4}$, rather than the value from the principal square-root branch. Adding and applying the <residue theorem> yields
$$
2I+2\pi iJ=2\pi i\left(\frac\pi{\sqrt2}+\frac{i\pi}{2\sqrt2}\right)=-\frac{\pi^2}{\sqrt2}+i\sqrt2\pi^2.
$$
As $J$ is real, taking real parts gives
$$
\boxed{I=-\frac{\pi^2}{2\sqrt2}.}
$$
The imaginary part also gives $J=\pi/\sqrt2$, an independent consistency check on the orientation and branch factors.

A <critical point of a dynamical system> or <equilibrium> is a point $(a,b)$ with $f(a,b)=g(a,b)=0$, so the constant function $(x,y)=(a,b)$ is a solution. Put $u=x-a$, $v=y-b$. For a continuously differentiable <vector field>, its <linearization> is
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}=M\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix},\qquad M=D(f,g)(a,b),
$$
with remainder $o(\sqrt{u^2+v^2})$ in the original nonlinear system. For distinct <eigenvalues>, $M$ is diagonalizable over $\mathbb C$, and the linear solutions are combinations of $e^{\lambda_jt}w_j$, where $w_j$ are corresponding <eigenvectors>. Real parts determine growth or decay; nonzero imaginary parts give rotation. For <hyperbolic equilibria>, meaning no <eigenvalue> has zero real part, the <Hartman-Grobman theorem> transfers the local stability and qualitative orbit pattern to the nonlinear system. A purely imaginary pair is not hyperbolic: it determines a linear centre, while higher-order terms decide whether the nonlinear system actually has a centre.

The <Bendixson-Dulac theorem> states that if $(P,Q)$ is a continuously differentiable planar <vector field> on a <simply connected> open region $D$, and a continuously differentiable <Dulac function> $B$ makes
$$
\frac{\partial(BP)}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial(BQ)}{\partial y}
$$
have one weak sign throughout $D$, without vanishing identically on any nonempty open subset, then there is no nonconstant <periodic orbit> contained in $D$. To see why, a hypothetical simple <periodic orbit> bounds a region in $D$. The <divergence theorem> gives the <integral> of this expression as the outward flux of $B(P,Q)$ across the orbit. The flux is zero because the <vector> field is tangent to its orbit, whereas the one-sign area <integral> is nonzero, a contradiction. The usual stricter version assumes a strictly positive or strictly negative expression everywhere.

For the printed <polynomial> system, the origin is an <equilibrium> and
$$
M(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\lambda=\pm i.
$$
Thus **its <linearization> is a <centre equilibrium>**. For the full system take $B=1$. The <divergence> is
$$
P_x+Q_y=x^4-3x^2+x^2y^2=x^2(x^2+y^2-3).
$$
On the open disk $x^2+y^2<3$ this is nonpositive, and is negative wherever $x\ne0$. Its zero set inside the disk is a line, so it cannot vanish on any open subset. The <Bendixson-Dulac theorem> therefore proves
$$
\boxed{\text{There are no nonconstant periodic orbits contained in }x^2+y^2<3.}
$$
This also exposes a terminology problem in the source: a nonlinear <centre equilibrium> has surrounding closed orbits, so the printed claim of a centre cannot hold in that sense. The imaginary <eigenvalues> establish only a centre of the linearized system. In fact the full <equilibrium> is an **asymptotically stable <weak focus>**, as the following direct check shows.

A <quartic Lyapunov correction for a weak focus> makes the nonlinear damping visible. Put $r^2=x^2+y^2$ and consider the <Lyapunov function>
$$
V(x,y)=\frac{x^2+y^2}{2}-\frac{5x^3y+3xy^3}{8}=\frac{r^2}{2}+O(r^4).
$$
It is positive definite sufficiently close to zero. Differentiating along the <polynomial> <vector> field gives
$$
\dot V=-\frac38(x^2+y^2)^2+O(r^6)=-\frac38r^4+O(r^6).
$$
For clarity, the quartic terms come from $-x^4$ in the <derivative> of $r^2/2$, and from applying $y\partial_x-x\partial_y$ to the quartic correction. Their sum is $-3x^4/8-3x^2y^2/4-3y^4/8$; all other terms have degree at least six. Therefore $\dot V<0$ in a sufficiently small punctured neighbourhood, proving <asymptotic stability> by <second Lyapunov theorem>. Finally
$$
\dot\theta=\frac{xQ-yP}{r^2}=-1+O(r^2),
$$
so nonconstant nearby orbits turn clockwise as they converge. The <equilibrium> is a weak focus despite the centre in its <linearization>. This interpretation resolves both the local dynamics and the absence of closed orbits without changing the printed <coefficients>.

For the <linear differential operator> $L=D^2+4D+5$, the <characteristic equation> is $m^2+4m+5=0$, with roots $-2\pm i$. Take the <fundamental system of solutions>
$$
u(x)=e^{-2x}\cos x,\qquad v(x)=e^{-2x}\sin x,\qquad W(x)=uv'-u'v=e^{-4x}.
$$
The <Wronskian> is never zero, so <variation of parameters> is valid on the whole interval. Write a particular solution as $a(x)u(x)+b(x)v(x)$, and impose the auxiliary condition $a'u+b'v=0$. Substitution then gives $a'u'+b'v'=f$. These two equations give
$$
a'=-\frac{vf}{W},\qquad b'=\frac{uf}{W}.
$$
Choosing both <integrals> to start at zero makes the resulting particular solution and its first <derivative> vanish there:
$$
y_p(x)=-u(x)\int_0^x\frac{v(t)f(t)}{W(t)}\,dt+v(x)\int_0^x\frac{u(t)f(t)}{W(t)}\,dt=e^{-2x}\int_0^xe^{2t}\sin(x-t)f(t)\,dt.
$$
A general solution is $Au+Cv+y_p$. The <boundary condition> at zero sets $A=0$. The condition at $\ell=\pi/2$ gives
$$
C=-\int_0^{\pi/2}e^{2t}\cos t\,f(t)\,dt,
$$
because $\sin\ell=1$ and $\sin(\ell-t)=\cos t$. Combining the two <integrals> yields the <Green's function> representation
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\int_0^{\pi/2}G(x,t)f(t)\,dt,\qquad G(x,t)=-e^{2(t-x)}\sin(\min(x,t))\cos(\max(x,t)).}
$$
Indeed, for $t<x$ the kernel is $-e^{2(t-x)}\sin t\cos x$, since $\sin(x-t)-\sin x\cos t=-\cos x\sin t$; for $x<t$ it is $-e^{2(t-x)}\sin x\cos t$.

For fixed interior $t$, the <Green's function> vanishes at both endpoints, is continuous at $x=t$, solves the homogeneous equation away from $t$, and satisfies
$$
G_x(t^+,t)-G_x(t^-,t)=\sin^2t+\cos^2t=1.
$$
This <derivative> jump is the normalization required by the leading <coefficient> one in $L$. Finally, a <homogeneous solution> satisfying the first <boundary condition> is $Ce^{-2x}\sin x$, and the second <boundary condition> at $\pi/2$ forces $C=0$. Thus the <boundary value problem> has exactly one solution for every continuous forcing, justifying the unique inverse represented by this kernel.

The same <variation of parameters> construction works on $[0,\pi]$. All solutions satisfying the <boundary condition> at zero have the form

For a <quasilinear partial differential equation>, the <characteristic equations for a quasilinear equation> are the ordinary <differential equations>
$$
\boxed{\frac{dx}{ds}=P(x,y,z),\qquad\frac{dy}{ds}=Q(x,y,z),\qquad\frac{dz}{ds}=R(x,y,z).}
$$
A <solution surface> is locally a differentiable graph $z=u(x,y)$ whose tangent plane contains the <vector> $(P,Q,R)$ at every point. Its normal is $(-u_x,-u_y,1)$, so the tangency condition is precisely $Pu_x+Qu_y=R$. Thus every characteristic lying on that graph follows the <vector> field without leaving the graph.

Parameterize the given initial curve by $\gamma(t)=(x_0(t),y_0(t),z_0(t))$. For each $t$, solve the <characteristic equations for a quasilinear equation> with initial value $\gamma(t)$ at $s=0$. The resulting map $(s,t)\mapsto(x(s,t),y(s,t),z(s,t))$ sweeps out a candidate solution surface. If the <coefficients> and initial data are continuously differentiable, the <Picard-Lindelöf theorem> supplies this local family. To make it a graph we also need the projection $(s,t)\mapsto(x,y)$ to be locally invertible. At $s=0$ its <Jacobian determinant> is
$$
J(0,t)=P(\gamma(t))y_0'(t)-Q(\gamma(t))x_0'(t).
$$
The <noncharacteristic Cauchy data> condition is $J(0,t)\ne0$. The <inverse function theorem> then gives $s=s(x,y)$ and $t=t(x,y)$, and hence $u(x,y)=z(s(x,y),t(x,y))$. Its <derivatives> obey the PDE by the <chain rule>. The same characteristic initial-value uniqueness gives local uniqueness of this solution wherever the projection remains invertible and the relevant characteristic meets the prescribed data.

To find the <characteristic domain from data on a half-line>, retain the allowed range of its initial parameter. The portion connected to the initial data before the <fold caustic> has $s>-1$ and $t\geq0$. Inverting the positive branch gives
$$
s=-1+\sqrt{1+x+y},\qquad t=x+1-\sqrt{1+x+y}.
$$
The square root must be positive, and $t\geq0$ requires $x+1\geq\sqrt{1+x+y}>0$. Squaring is now legitimate and gives $y\leq x^2+x$. Thus the full characteristic-covered set on this branch is
$$
\boxed{x>-1,\qquad -x-1<y\leq x^2+x.}
$$
Its open interior, where both the data parameter and the projection are interior and nonsingular, is the classical <domain of dependence>
$$
\boxed{D=\{(x,y):x>-1,\ -x-1<y<x^2+x\}.}
$$
\Image[2012-characteristics.png]
{title=The <domain of dependence> from the initial half-line, bounded by the endpoint characteristic and the <fold caustic>}

Each point of $D$ has exactly one backward characteristic meeting the interior $t>0$ of the prescribed data, so the <Picard-Lindelöf theorem> fixes its value. The upper boundary is the characteristic issuing from the endpoint $t=0$ and is fixed by the endpoint datum, consistently with the continuous trace from $D$. The lower boundary is excluded: $s=-1$ there, the <Jacobian determinant> vanishes, and the explicit solution <derivatives> diverge as $W\downarrow0$.

Points of the half-plane $W>0$ outside this set have $t<0$ on the branch through the data. Their characteristic footpoints would lie on the unprescribed continuation of the initial line, so these values are not determined by the given half-line alone. For example, extend the initial value to negative $t$ as $1+\varepsilon h(t)$, where $h$ is a <smooth function> supported on the negative half-line. Such extensions agree with all the prescribed data and, where noncharacteristic, produce different local extensions by the <method of characteristics>. The displayed formula supplies one possible extension there, but the data do not select it uniquely. This is why retaining the restriction on the initial half-line is essential. Continuing the parameterized surface past $s=-1$ instead produces the other sheet of the fold; it does not give a differentiable graph continuation through the caustic.
