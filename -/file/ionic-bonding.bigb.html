= Ionic bonding
{wiki}

<Ionic bonding> binds oppositely charged <ions> through attraction governed by <electrostatics>. In ordinary solid <sodium chloride>, each <cation> interacts with many <anions>, and also with other <cations>: attraction and repulsion together contribute to the binding of an extended <ionic crystal>. The <chemical formula> $\mathrm{NaCl}$ specifies equal numbers of the two kinds of <ions>; it does not divide the <solid> into isolated two-atom <molecules>.<Chemistry 2e>[§7.1, “The Formation of Ionic Compounds”, Figure 7.3]{ref}

This distinction explains a striking combination of properties: ordinary dry <sodium chloride> conducts <electric current> poorly, while its molten form or a sufficiently concentrated <chemical solution> in <water> conducts well. The <ions> already exist in the <solid>; their ability to move through the sample changes. A <conductivity experiment on salt solutions> tests this transport picture, rather than simply asserting that charged constituents must make every material conductive.

= Radius ratio rule
{parent=Ionic bonding}

A <hard-sphere model> relating the <radius> ratio of opposite-charge <ions> to a possible <coordination number>. For a <cube> of eight mutually touching <anions> around a touching <cation>, $r_c/r_a=\sqrt3-1$; real bonding and coordination-dependent <radii> limit its predictive power.

= Goldschmidt packing principle
{parent=Ionic bonding}
{c}

A geometric heuristic favouring the largest <coordination number> compatible with neighbouring opposite-charge <ions> touching the central <ion> without overlap. A limiting <radius ratio rule> comes from additionally making the surrounding <ions> just touch. It is a <hard-sphere model>, not an exact stability criterion.

= Stoichiometry of ionic compounds
{parent=Salt (chemistry)}
{title2=$\sum_i\nu_i z_i=0$}

For a neutral <formula unit>, let $\nu_i$ count <ions> of type $i$ with <electric charge> $z_i e$, where $e$ is the <elementary charge>. The <stoichiometry> must satisfy
$$
\sum_i\nu_i z_i=0.
$$
For $\mathrm{Na}^+$ and $\mathrm{Cl}^-$, the smallest positive counts are $1:1$. For $\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\mathrm{Cl}^-$, they are $1:2$, giving <calcium chloride> $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$: $1(2)+2(-1)=0$. The formula counts <ions>, not the number of nearest neighbours. A <counterion> is the oppositely charged partner needed to balance a charged species; its identity can change <solubility> and other properties without changing the identity of that species.<Chemistry 2e>[§7.1, neutral ionic formulas and charge balance; §2.6, ionic compound examples]{ref}

= Ionic crystal
{parent=Ionic bonding}
{wiki}

An <ionic crystal> repeats an arrangement of <ions> in three dimensions. A <unit cell> describes that repetition, whereas a <formula unit> describes composition. The two accounting devices answer different questions. <Coulomb's law> is not restricted to an arbitrarily drawn line connecting one <cation> to one <anion>: every <ion> contributes to the <electric field> experienced by the others.

= Rock salt structure
{parent=Ionic crystal}

The <rock salt structure> of ordinary <sodium chloride> has a <coordination number> of six for each kind of <ion>: six nearest neighbours of the opposite sign surround each <ion> along three perpendicular directions. Its conventional cubic <unit cell> contains four <formula units>; sharing corner, face, and edge sites between adjacent cells must be included in that count. Sixfold local coordination is compatible with $1:1$ overall <stoichiometry>.<Chemistry 2e>[§7.1, Figure 7.3; §10.6, “The Structures of Ionic Crystals”, NaCl unit-cell accounting]{ref}

The checkerboard plane in the diagram is a slice through this three-dimensional arrangement. It leaves out the two nearest neighbours above and below the plane. Repeating a complete even-by-even patch gives equal numbers of signs; the displayed odd-by-odd crop has a boundary imbalance and is not a complete neutral <ionic crystal> or a <unit cell>. Other <salts> need not have the <rock salt structure> or the same <coordination number>.

= Ion pair
{parent=Ionic bonding}

An <ion pair> is an associated <cation> and <anion>. An isolated <ion pair> lacks the extended neighbourhood of an <ionic crystal>. In a <chemical solution>, <ion pairs> can coexist with more widely separated <ions>; their abundance depends on conditions. A neutral gas-phase <sodium chloride> <molecule> can be treated as a discrete entity, with its own <quantum mechanics> and dissociation products. That use of <molecule> does not describe the constituents of ordinary solid <sodium chloride>.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume II by Richard Feynman and others>[§8–3, distinction between vaporized NaCl pairs and separated gaseous ions]{ref}<Conductivity of Solutions by Harvard Natural Sciences Lecture Demonstrations>{ref}

= Contact ion pair
{parent=Ion pair}

Oppositely charged ions remaining in direct contact without an intervening complete solvent shell. Their relative positions can bias substitution stereochemistry even when bond cleavage precedes <nucleophile> attack.

= Electron transfer and ionic character
{parent=Ionic bonding}

The bookkeeping steps $\mathrm{Na}\to\mathrm{Na}^++e^-$ and $\mathrm{Cl}+e^-\to\mathrm{Cl}^-$ conserve <electric charge> and describe the <electron> inventories of separated species. They do not establish a chronological mechanism in which a labeled <electron> jumps between two pre-existing <chemical atoms> and becomes a classical object owned exclusively by one of them. In a bound material, <quantum mechanics> determines the <electron density> and the total <energy>.

<Ionic bonding> and a <covalent bond> are useful limiting descriptions with a continuum between them. <Electronegativity> helps anticipate unequal sharing of <electron density>, but a numerical cutoff does not measure a universal fraction of <ionic bonding>. A <partial atomic charge> depends on how the <electron density> is partitioned; integer <formal charges> are a bookkeeping convention. Using $\pm e$ in a point-charge model therefore does not claim that every partition of the actual <electron density> assigns exactly those values.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume II by Richard Feynman and others>[§30–2, “Chemical bonds in crystals”: gradations between covalent and ionic bonding]{ref}<Chemistry 2e>[§7.2, “Electronegativity and Bond Type”: general guide and exceptions]{ref}

= Electrostatic model of ionic binding
{parent=Ionic bonding}
{title2=$U_{ij}=z_i z_j e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0r_{ij})$}

For two point <electric charges> in <vacuum>, <Coulomb's law> gives the interaction <potential energy>
$$
U_{ij}(r_{ij})=\frac{z_i z_j e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_{ij}},
$$
with zero at infinite separation. Here $r_{ij}$ is the distance between them and $\epsilon_0$ is the <vacuum permittivity>. Opposite signs give negative <potential energy> and attraction; equal signs give positive <potential energy> and repulsion. For a finite collection of distinct point <electric charges>, sum over $i<j$ to count each pair once. A bulk <ionic crystal> requires the extended lattice sum, including both signs, rather than multiplying one attractive pair by the <coordination number>.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume II by Richard Feynman and others>[§8–1, equations (8.1)–(8.3); §8–3, many-neighbour lattice sum]{ref}

At short distances, overlapping <electron density> makes the simple point-charge picture inadequate. <Quantum mechanics>, including the <Pauli exclusion principle>, matters to the steep repulsive contribution. Pure attraction proportional to $-1/r$ would collapse as $r\to0$; it cannot set an equilibrium spacing on its own. A useful illustrative model is
$$
U(r)=-\frac{A}{r}+\frac{B}{r^n},\qquad A>0,\quad B>0,\quad n>1.
$$
Here $r$ scales an assumed fixed arrangement, $A$ packages its attractive contribution, and $B/r^n$ represents short-range repulsion. The constants have units <energy> times length and <energy> times length to the power $n$, respectively. This is a model, not a fitted description of a named <salt>.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume II by Richard Feynman and others>[§8–3, close-distance repulsion and limits of a purely electrostatic lattice calculation]{ref}

Differentiating gives $r_0=(nB/A)^{1/(n-1)}$ and $U''(r_0)=(n-1)A/r_0^3>0$, so the stationary spacing is a stable minimum. For the diagram's stipulated $n=9$, put $x=r/r_0$ and $E_0=A(1-1/n)/r_0>0$. Then
$$
\frac{U(r)}{E_0}=\frac{-9/x+1/x^9}{8},\qquad U(r_0)=-E_0.
$$
The <dimensionless> curves show how competing interactions select a spacing. Their <energy> scale and exponent are synthetic; neither is a measured <lattice energy> or a <melting> prediction.

= Madelung constant
{c}
{parent=Electrostatic model of ionic binding}
{wiki}
{title2=$\alpha_M$}

The <Madelung constant>, named for <Erwin Madelung>, is a dimensionless geometrical factor in the electrostatic binding of an <ionic crystal>. For a neutral lattice of <electric charges> $\pm e$ with nearest unlike-ion distance $r_0$, <Coulomb's law> gives the <potential energy> per ion pair as $-\alpha_M e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0r_0)$. At a positive reference site, $\alpha_M=-\sum_{j\ne0}z_jr_0/r_j$, with neighboring <electric charges> $z_je$. Both attraction and repulsion enter this lattice sum; summing with a neutral-bulk prescription is essential. Short-range overlap repulsion and other contributions must be added before inferring a stable spacing or a thermodynamic <lattice energy>.

= Lattice energy
{parent=Ionic bonding}
{wiki}
{title2=$\Delta H_{\rm latt,diss}$}

A <lattice energy> needs a reaction, a sign convention, and a stated amount. Using the lattice-dissociation <enthalpy> convention for <sodium chloride>,
$$
\mathrm{NaCl}(s)\longrightarrow\mathrm{Na}^+(g)+\mathrm{Cl}^-(g),\qquad
\Delta H_{\rm latt,diss}>0.
$$
The reverse lattice-formation <enthalpy> is negative with the same magnitude when initial and final states match. <Chemistry 2e> gives $769\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ for this separation, per <mole> of <sodium chloride> <formula units>. This is about $7.97$ <electronvolts> per <formula unit>, not per individual <ion>. It concerns separated gaseous <ions>, rather than neutral <chemical atoms>, molten <salt>, or hydrated <ions> in <water>.<Chemistry 2e>[§7.5, “Ionic Bond Strength and Lattice Energy”, positive convention, reverse convention and NaCl value]{ref}

Also distinguish an <internal energy> change from an <enthalpy> change: $H=U+pV$. A static point-charge calculation omits motion and thermal contributions. Reversing a reaction reverses its <energy> change; replacing <internal energy> with <enthalpy> additionally changes the defined quantity. The <Born-Haber cycle> infers a lattice-formation <enthalpy> by combining other <enthalpies>, <ionization energies>, and <electron affinities>. These imagined stages are a thermodynamic accounting path, not a claim about the actual sequence of a <chemical reaction>.<Chemistry 2e>[§7.5, “The Born-Haber Cycle”, Figure 7.13 and Table 7.4]{ref}

= Born-Haber cycle
{c}
{parent=Lattice energy}

A <Born-Haber cycle> uses <Hess's law> to infer a <lattice energy> from measurable <enthalpy> differences and isolated-species data. The motivating difficulty is that separating a <mole> of an <ionic crystal> into mutually separated gaseous <ions> is not a convenient direct <calorimetry> experiment. Instead, compare two accounting routes between the same elemental reference states and the same <solid>. Because <enthalpy> is a <state function>, the signed differences close exactly when all inputs describe compatible states. These routes do not assert a <reaction mechanism> or a temporal sequence of <reaction intermediates>.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/7.5 Strengths of Ionic and Covalent Bonds>[“The Born-Haber Cycle”, Figure 7.13 and Table 7.4]{ref}

For one <mole> of <sodium chloride> <formula units>, the overall formation reaction is
$$
\mathrm{Na}(s)+\tfrac12\mathrm{Cl}_2(g)\longrightarrow\mathrm{NaCl}(s),\qquad F=\Delta H_{\rm f}^{\circ}.
$$
The <stoichiometry> requires half a <mole> of diatomic <chlorine> <molecules>, supplying one <mole> of <chlorine> <chemical atoms>. A half coefficient counts an <amount of substance>; it does not describe half of an individual <molecule>. Write each auxiliary <chemical reaction> with its own sign:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Na}(s)&\longrightarrow\mathrm{Na}(g) && S>0,\\
\tfrac12\mathrm{Cl}_2(g)&\longrightarrow\mathrm{Cl}(g) && B>0,\\
\mathrm{Na}(g)&\longrightarrow\mathrm{Na}^+(g)+e^-(g) && I>0,\\
\mathrm{Cl}(g)+e^-(g)&\longrightarrow\mathrm{Cl}^-(g) && A=\Delta H_{\rm attach}^{\circ}<0,\\
\mathrm{Na}^+(g)+\mathrm{Cl}^-(g)&\longrightarrow\mathrm{NaCl}(s) && L_{\rm form}<0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $S$ is the <sublimation> <enthalpy>, $B$ the <enthalpy of atomization> of <chlorine> per <mole> of <chemical atoms>, and $I$ the molar ionization <enthalpy> represented by the <ionization energy> input in elementary treatments. If the <bond-dissociation energy> table gives the compatible molar <enthalpy> for $\mathrm{Cl}_2(g)\to2\mathrm{Cl}(g)$, use half that number for $B$. Both <ions> in the last step are gaseous, and no aqueous <hydration of ions> belongs in that step.

Adding these equations cancels the gaseous <chemical atoms>, the gaseous <ions> and the free <electron>, leaving precisely the overall formation reaction. Thus
$$
F=S+B+I+A+L_{\rm form},\qquad
L_{\rm diss}=-L_{\rm form}=S+B+I+A-F.
$$
The closed traversal returns from the <solid> to the elemental reference states, adding $-F$; therefore $S+B+I+A+L_{\rm form}-F=0$. Notice that the signed attachment term $A$ is negative. Substituting a positive spectroscopic <electron affinity> for $A$ would reverse the attachment contribution.

The diagram deliberately uses invented, internally consistent inputs in $\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$: $S=100$, $B=120$, $I=500$, $A=-350$ and $F=-400$. Preparation of separated gaseous <ions> raises the <enthalpy> by $370$, so $L_{\rm form}=-770$ and $L_{\rm diss}=+770$. The closure check is $100+120+500-350-770+400=0$. = Lattice enthalpy from solution calorimetry
{parent=Born-Haber cycle}

A second indirect route makes the experimental distinction especially clear: a <calorimeter> can measure dissolution of a weighed <salt> into a known amount of <water>, but its final <ions> are hydrated, not separated gaseous <ions>. At compatible <standard states> and the same <temperature>,
$$
\Delta H_{\rm sol}^{\circ}=L_{\rm diss}+\Delta H_{\rm hyd,pair}^{\circ},\qquad
L_{\rm diss}=\Delta H_{\rm sol}^{\circ}-\Delta H_{\rm hyd,pair}^{\circ}.
$$
Here the combined <hydration of ions> term takes one <mole> each of gaseous $\mathrm{Na}^+$ and $\mathrm{Cl}^-$ to the specified aqueous reference state. Its sum suffices: dissolution <calorimetry> alone cannot independently assign single-ion <hydration of ions> <enthalpies>, or determine $L_{\rm diss}$ without another input. A finite-<concentration> measurement also requires dilution corrections before it can supply $\Delta H_{\rm sol}^{\circ}$.

In a simple constant-<pressure> solution <calorimeter>, an insulated vessel, stirrer and <thermometer> follow the <temperature> change after mixing. A calibrated effective <heat capacity> $C_{\rm eff}$ converts the corrected <temperature> change to <heat>. With no applied heating, negligible external <heat transfer>, and only pressure-volume <work>, the approximate molar result is
$$
\Delta H_{\rm sol}\simeq-\frac{C_{\rm eff}\Delta T}{n}.
$$
Here $n$ is the <amount of substance> dissolved and $C_{\rm eff}$ includes the <chemical solution> and the vessel. Cooling means positive dissolution <enthalpy>, because the dissolving sample takes <heat> from its surroundings.<Chemistry 2e>[§5.2, Figures 5.11–5.13 and discussion of including solution and calorimeter heat capacities]{ref}

An actual reference measurement used <potassium chloride>, not <sodium chloride>: a platinum-lined <calorimeter> with an adiabatic shield and a quartz-oscillator <thermometer>. Because dissolution absorbed <heat>, measured electrical heating prevented loss of shield control; the readouts were electrical input and the vessel/shield <temperature> record. Stirring was checked visually in a dummy vessel. The <thermometer> was compared with a calibrated resistance <thermometer>, and electrical calibration bracketed the reaction. The reported result was $+(17.584\pm0.017)\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ at $298.15\,\mathrm K$, for one <mole> of <potassium chloride> in $500$ <moles> of <water>.<The Enthalpy of Solution of SRM 1655 (KCl) in H2O by Marthada V. Kilday>[§3 and §4: apparatus, calibration, readouts and specified dilution; inspected through indexed article text]{ref}

Checks on residual <water>, particle size and final <concentration>, and agreement with a second <calorimeter>, constrained systematic errors. Imperfect shield tracking required <heat transfer> corrections. This experiment measures an <enthalpy of solution>, not a direct <lattice energy>; the hydration and dilution bookkeeping still has to be supplied. The full article could not be opened here; the inspected indexed text supports the stated apparatus and checks, without reconstructing its complete uncertainty budget.<The Enthalpy of Solution of SRM 1655 (KCl) in H2O by Marthada V. Kilday>[§§2–4 and conclusion: moisture, particle-size and dilution studies; confirmation with an isoperibol calorimeter; full-page access blocked]{ref}

= Physical properties of salts
{parent=Ionic bonding}

Many <ionic crystals> have high <melting> <temperatures> compared with small molecular <solids>, because reorganizing their strongly interacting <ions> costs <energy>. But <melting> leaves a dense <liquid> with interactions; it does not perform the complete separation defining a <lattice energy>. At fixed pressure, the transition occurs when the <Gibbs free energies> of the <solid> and <liquid> match. The relevant <enthalpy> and <entropy> differences both matter, so <lattice energy> alone is not a universal ordering of <melting> <temperatures>. Strong <covalent bonds> in extended networks can also give high <melting> <temperatures>.<Chemistry 2e>[§10.5, “Ionic Solids” and “Covalent Network Solid”; §16.4, equations defining Gibbs free energy and its change]{ref}

= Solubility
{parent=Physical properties of salts}
{wiki}

<Solubility> specifies the amount that can remain dissolved at <chemical equilibrium> under stated conditions; it is different from how quickly a sample dissolves. Separating <ions> from an <ionic crystal> costs <energy>, but <solvation> establishes new interactions with the <solvent>. In <water>, <hydration of ions> couples charged <ions> to polar <water> <molecules>. The net <enthalpy> of dissolution can have either sign; <entropy> and <concentration> also enter the <Gibbs free energy> balance. Consequently, being a <salt> does not guarantee high <solubility> in <water> or any other <solvent>.<Chemistry 2e>[§11.1, “The Formation of Solutions”, Figure 11.4; §11.3, definition of solubility; §16.4, free energy]{ref}

Low <solubility> must also be distinguished from weak <electrolyte> behavior. A small amount of an <ionic compound> can dissolve essentially completely as <ions>, giving few charge carriers because little material dissolved. Calling that a <weak electrolyte> solely because its <chemical solution> conducts poorly would conflate two different questions.<Chemistry 2e>[§11.2, “Ionic Electrolytes”: sparingly soluble compounds can be strong electrolytes]{ref}

= Solvation
{parent=Solubility}
{wiki}

<Solvation> is stabilization through interactions between a <solute> and its <solvent>. It involves both the <solute>'s surroundings and reorganization of the <solvent>: neighbors move, orientations change, and existing interactions compete with new ones. <Electric forces>, <van der Waals forces>, and specific interactions such as <hydrogen bonds> can contribute. <Solvation> can also occur at groups on an insoluble material, so it does not require complete <dissolution>.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry/solvation>{ref}

<Solvation> by <water> is called hydration. <Hydration of ions> is the charged special case, but neutral <molecules> such as <sucrose> can also interact with <water> without becoming <ions>. A hydrated <cation> retains its positive net <electric charge>; orienting neutral <water> <molecules> around it does not supply an extra <electron>. <Dissolution>, <desolvation>, and <ionization> describe different changes, as the examples below show.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/11.1 The Dissolution Process>[Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others; Opening sucrose example]{ref}<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/11.2 Electrolytes>[Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others; “Ionic Electrolytes” and “Covalent Electrolytes”]{ref}

The useful question is therefore not just whether a <solute> attracts a <solvent>, but how much the whole system gains after paying for the rearrangements. <Solvation free energy> describes a specified transfer into a <solvent>; <solvation thermodynamic balance> explains why even cooling during <dissolution> can accompany favorable mixing. The existing <solubility> discussion concerns how much remains dissolved at <chemical equilibrium>.

= Solvent cavity formation
{parent=Solvation}

Reorganization of a solvent to accommodate a solute. Its free-energy cost competes with favourable solute–solvent interactions and depends on solute size and shape.

= Solvation shell
{parent=Solvation}

A <solvation shell> is the region of <solvent> constituents surrounding a <solute>, with local arrangements influenced by their interactions. The first <solvation shell> contains the closest neighbors under a stated distance or interaction criterion. A <coordination number> counts those neighbors; it requires such a criterion and can fluctuate. Neither a drawn ring nor a few illustrated <molecules> determines a measured <coordination number>.

In a <liquid>, a <solvation shell> is an evolving neighborhood: <solvent> <molecules> reorient and exchange with the surroundings. Different <ions> can produce different local dynamics. <Spectroscopic measurement of hydration dynamics> provides an experimental way to distinguish some of those environments without pretending that a shell is a fixed cage.

= Hydration shell

= Ion-dipole attraction
{parent=Solvation shell}
{title2=$U=-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E$}

An <ion-dipole attraction> couples an <ion>'s <electric field> to a <molecule>'s <electric dipole moment>. In the elementary point-<electric dipole> approximation, the interaction <potential energy> is $U=-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E$. An orientation with the <electric dipole moment> along the <electric field> lowers this <energy>. For <water>, the <oxygen> end preferentially faces a <cation>, and the <hydrogen> end an <anion>. At nonzero <temperature>, fluctuations and competing <hydrogen bonds> prevent perfect alignment of every <molecule>.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/11.2 Electrolytes>[Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others; “Ionic Electrolytes”, Figure 11.7 and its explanatory paragraph]{ref}

= Spectroscopic measurement of hydration dynamics
{parent=Solvation shell}

What separates a <hydration shell> from the surrounding <water> experimentally? <Electrical conductivity> addresses a different question. The documented lamp demonstration immerses metal <electrodes> in a <chemical solution>, rinses them between samples, and records brightness; adding <sodium chloride> to distilled <water> makes it conductive. A <water> blank and dissolved <sucrose> comparison test whether dissolved material alone explains the signal. Fixed geometry, <temperature>, and stated <concentrations> are additional proposed controls. This reveals mobile <electric charge>, with limits from contamination, electrode interfaces and detection threshold; it supplies no shell positions. <Conductivity experiment on salt solutions> explains the apparatus and controls in detail.<Conductivity of Solutions by Harvard Natural Sciences Lecture Demonstrations>[“Setting it Up” and “Comments”]{ref}

In the authored study <Influence of ions on the hydrogen-bond structure in liquid water by Anne Willem Omta and others>, <pump-probe spectroscopy> uses tunable mid-<infrared> pulses traversing a sample. The probe's <wave polarization> is set at $45^{\circ}$ to the pump; after the sample, splitting and <polarizers> select parallel and perpendicular transmission. Measuring each transmission with and without the pump gives $D_{\parallel}$ and $D_{\perp}$, the respective logarithms of the transmission ratios. Their <dimensionless> <anisotropy> is
$$
R(t)=\frac{D_{\parallel}(t)-D_{\perp}(t)}{D_{\parallel}(t)+2D_{\perp}(t)}.
$$
Its decay probes reorientation rather than merely loss of vibrational excitation. Dilute <semiheavy water> in <heavy water> separates the O–H and O–D bands. Varying the <semiheavy water> <concentration> checked that transfer of excitation between nearby O–H groups did not determine the decay. These apparatus and control details are from the inspected author-uploaded article, not a new experiment.<Influence of ions on the hydrogen-bond structure in liquid water by Anne Willem Omta and others>[§II, printed p. 12458, Figure 1 and equations (2)–(3); author-uploaded article text inspected]{ref}

The related thesis chapter documents two O–H absorption bands in a <sodium perchlorate> <chemical solution>: approximately $3400\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ for groups forming <hydrogen bonds> to <water>, and $3575\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ for groups forming <hydrogen bonds> to the <anion>. Selecting pump and probe bands, then a later delay window, separated their responses. The anion-associated <anisotropy> decayed more slowly; the water-bonded component showed no resolved change in decay time across the tested <sodium perchlorate> <concentrations>. The chapter also used O–D excitation to extend observation beyond the shorter O–H vibrational lifetime. These results support distinct local dynamics rather than a permanently fixed <hydration shell>. Spectral overlap, finite observation windows, and the selected <salts> limit the inference: this does not prove that every <ion> leaves all distant <water> properties unchanged.<Ion solvation in water: femtosecond spectroscopy of hydrogen-bond dynamics by Michel F. Kropman/8 The influence of ions on the hydrogen-bond structure of water>[§8.2 and §8.3, printed pp. 82–89, Figures 8.1–8.7; PDF text inspected, numerical decay-time OCR unreliable and not reproduced]{ref}

= Dissolution
{parent=Solvation}

<Dissolution> transfers material into a <chemical solution>. It can include separation from an <ionic crystal> and <solvation>, or dispersal of neutral <molecules>. For <sucrose> and <sodium chloride>, respectively,
$$
\mathrm{C}_{12}\mathrm{H}_{22}\mathrm{O}_{11}(s)\longrightarrow\mathrm{C}_{12}\mathrm{H}_{22}\mathrm{O}_{11}(aq),\qquad
\mathrm{NaCl}(s)\longrightarrow\mathrm{Na}^{+}(aq)+\mathrm{Cl}^{-}(aq).
$$
Here $(aq)$ specifies <water> as the <solvent>; it does not specify a fixed number of attached <water> <molecules>. The neutral <sucrose> remains molecular. <Dissociation of an ionic compound> separates pre-existing <ions> in the second example; <solvation> stabilizes their new surroundings.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/11.1 The Dissolution Process>[Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others; Opening molecular and ionic dissolution examples]{ref}<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/11.2 Electrolytes>[Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others; “Ionic Electrolytes”]{ref}

<Ionization> instead creates <ions> from neutral species. <Hydrogen chloride> reacting with <water> illustrates <protonation> of <water> rather than mere rearrangement of a <hydration shell>:
$$
\mathrm{HCl}+\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm O\longrightarrow\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm O^{+}+\mathrm{Cl}^{-}.
$$
The <hydronium> and negative <ion> together preserve total <electric charge>. <Solvation> can influence this <chemical reaction>, but an <ion>'s <solvation> does not itself change its net <electric charge>.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/11.2 Electrolytes>[Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others; “Covalent Electrolytes”, HCl proton-transfer example]{ref}

= Dissociation of an ionic compound
{parent=Dissolution}

<Dissociation of an ionic compound> separates its constituent <ions>. It differs from creating charged species by <ionization> of neutral <molecules>. Dissolved <ions> can also associate into <ion pairs>, so a dissociated description does not exclude all subsequent association.

= Desolvation
{parent=Solvation}

<Desolvation> removes some or all of the <solvent> environment of a species. Removing a <hydration shell> does not remove an <ion>'s <electric charge>. Complete reversal of a specified <solvation> transfer reverses its <Gibbs free energy> and <enthalpy> changes when the same states and conventions are used. Partial <desolvation>, exchange of one <solvent> for another, and creation of a gaseous species have different endpoints; their costs need not match.

= Solvation free energy
{parent=Solvation}
{title2=$\Delta G_{\mathrm{solv}}^{\circ}$}

For transfer of the same species $X$ from a gaseous reference state into a <solvent> $S$, define the molar <solvation free energy> by
$$
X(g)\longrightarrow X(S),\qquad
\Delta G_{\mathrm{solv}}^{\circ}(S)=\mu_X^{\circ}(S)-\mu_X^{\circ}(g).
$$
Here $\mu^{\circ}$ is a standard <chemical potential>. Negative <solvation free energy> means that transfer into the stated <solvent> lowers the <Gibbs free energy> relative to the stated gaseous reference. Include <solute>-<solvent> interactions, making room in the <solvent>, and its reorganization; counting only a favorable pair interaction omits part of the transfer.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry/solvation energy>{ref}

State the <temperature>, gaseous reference <pressure>, dissolved reference <concentration> or other <standard state> convention, and species identity. Reference-state changes alter the quoted <solvation free energy>. For example, with an ideal dilute <thermodynamic activity> $a=c/c^{\circ}$, replacing $c^{\circ}$ by $\alpha c^{\circ}$ while preserving the physical <chemical potential> requires $\mu^{\circ}$ to increase by $RT\ln\alpha$. Thus the corresponding <solvation free energy> increases by $RT\ln\alpha$ if the gaseous reference stays fixed. This follows directly from $\mu=\mu^{\circ}+RT\ln a$, with $R$ the <molar gas constant>.

= Solvent-specific stabilization
{parent=Solvation free energy}

<Solvent-specific stabilization> means that the same species need not have the same <solvation free energy> in different <solvents>. <Chemical polarity>, <hydrogen bonds>, and the cost of reorganizing each <solvent> all matter. <Water> can stabilize <ions> by <ion-dipole attractions>; a favorable isolated attraction alone still cannot decide the total <dissolution> balance.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry/solvation energy>{ref}

With a common gaseous reference and compatible dissolved <standard states>, subtraction removes that reference:
$$
\Delta G_{\mathrm{transfer}}^{\circ}(A\to B)
=\mu_X^{\circ}(B)-\mu_X^{\circ}(A)
=\Delta G_{\mathrm{solv}}^{\circ}(B)-\Delta G_{\mathrm{solv}}^{\circ}(A).
$$
A negative result favors <solvent> B under those reference conventions. This comparison assumes the same species $X$: if <protonation>, <ionization>, or formation of <ion pairs> changes its identity, include a <chemical equilibrium> for each corresponding <chemical reaction>.

= Solvation thermodynamic balance
{parent=Solvation}
{title2=$\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S$}

At fixed <temperature> and <pressure>, compare fully specified initial and final states using
$$
\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S.
$$
The <entropy> change is for the whole selected system, including both <solute> and <solvent>. Dispersing a <solute> can increase accessible arrangements while orienting nearby <solvent> <molecules> restricts others. One picture of an ordered <hydration shell> cannot determine the sign of the total <entropy> change. A negative <Gibbs free energy> change favors the specified process; it does not determine its <reaction rate>.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/16.4 Free Energy>[Opening definition and Table 16.3]{ref}

An illustrative <enthalpy> accounting path separates <solute> constituents, reorganizes the <solvent>, and establishes their interactions:
$$
\Delta H=\Delta H_{\mathrm{separate}}+\Delta H_{\mathrm{reorganize}}+\Delta H_{\mathrm{interact}}.
$$
This is a chosen decomposition between compatible states, not a measured sequence. If a gaseous-ion-to-<chemical solution> <solvation> term already includes <solvent> reorganization, do not add that reorganization a second time. <Lattice enthalpy from solution calorimetry> gives the alternative <Hess's law> route that combines lattice separation with the complete <hydration of ions> term.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/11.1 The Dissolution Process>[Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others; “The Formation of Solutions”, Figure 11.4]{ref}

The motivating <calorimetry> experiment is especially instructive. = Conductivity experiment on salt solutions
{parent=Physical properties of salts}

How can a clear <chemical solution> reveal otherwise invisible mobile <ions>? The documented <Conductivity of Solutions by Harvard Natural Sciences Lecture Demonstrations> demonstration uses metal <electrodes> enclosed in tubing, an electrical supply, and a lamp in the circuit. The protocol specifies about $150\,\mathrm{mL}$ samples in labeled $250\,\mathrm{mL}$ beakers and a separate deionized-<water> rinse station. The recorded readout is lamp brightness after the <electrodes> enter each sample; the simplified variant tests <water> before and after adding <sodium chloride>. These are apparatus and procedure specifications, not new measurements reported here.<Conductivity of Solutions by Harvard Natural Sciences Lecture Demonstrations>{ref}

The useful comparison is <water>, a <sucrose> <chemical solution>, and a <sodium chloride> <chemical solution> with the same probe geometry. A documented university account reports no visible light with <water>, a glowing lamp with dissolved <sodium chloride>, and a weak filament glow with its <sucrose> sample. The diagram summarizes the latter as little or no visible light; the precise detection threshold depends on the tester. <Sucrose> is a control for the idea that any dissolved material must conduct: its dispersed neutral <molecules> do not provide the abundant mobile <ions> supplied by the <salt>. Rinsing the <electrodes> between samples controls carryover of dissolved <ions>, and the <water> blank checks the solvent background. Repeating the blank after the <salt> sample would test whether that rinse was effective. Additional methodological controls are fixed immersed area, electrode separation, supply setting, <temperature>, and comparison at stated <concentrations>. These additional controls are proposed checks, not undocumented claims about the source demonstration.

The inference is that mobile <electric charge> carriers complete the circuit. In the <chemical solution>, an <electric field> drives positive <ions> along the field and negative <ions> against it; multiplying each <drift velocity> by its <electric charge> makes both contributions to <conventional current> point the same way. With an alternating supply the direction reverses each half-cycle. The external wires instead carry <electric current> through <electrons>. The lamp alone identifies neither the species nor an exact <electrical conductivity>.

Important limits remain. <Water> has a small intrinsic population of <hydronium> and <hydroxide>, so a dark lamp does not prove absence of <ions>. Changes in probe geometry affect measured <electrical conductance>; <electrolysis>, electrode interfaces, contamination, and heating can also affect a real circuit. The comparison supports a transport model but does not independently measure a <coordination number>, a partitioned <partial atomic charge>, or a <lattice energy>.

Nor does poor conduction by ordinary dry <sodium chloride> establish that all <ionic crystals> are electrical insulators. <Crystallographic defects> can allow transport, and <solid electrolytes> can have substantial ionic <electrical conductivity> without <melting>. The authored abstract of <A lithium superionic conductor by Noriaki Kamaya and others> reports room-<temperature> ionic conduction in a <solid electrolyte>; the full experimental article was not accessible here.<A lithium superionic conductor by Noriaki Kamaya and others>{ref}
