= Solution

The <drift velocity> is the small ensemble-average velocity acquired by <charge carriers> in an applied <electric field>. It is distinct from their much larger random thermal or <Fermi velocity>. For conduction <electrons> of charge $-e$, the isotropic <Drude model> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf v_d=-\frac{e\tau}{m^*}\mathbf E=-\mu_e\mathbf E,}
$$
where $\tau$ is the <momentum relaxation time>.

The positive <electron mobility> $\mu_e=|\mathbf v_d|/|\mathbf E|$ measures the ease of this drift in <linear response>. In the same approximation,
$$
\boxed{\mu_e=\frac{e\tau}{m^*},\qquad\sigma=ne\mu_e.}
$$
Its units are $\mathrm{m^2\,V^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$; in an anisotropic crystal the mobility is a <tensor>.

The <band effective mass> describes the acceleration implied by the curvature of the <dispersion relation>. The <group velocity> is $\mathbf v=\hbar^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf k}E$, while $\hbar\dot{\mathbf k}=-e\mathbf E$. Thus
$$
\boxed{(m^{*-1})_{ij}=\frac1{\hbar^2}\frac{\partial^2E}{\partial k_i\partial k_j}.}
$$
For an isotropic parabolic minimum this reduces to $E=E_c+\hbar^2k^2/(2m^*)$ and $m^*\dot{\mathbf v}=-e\mathbf E$. It incorporates the periodic crystal potential and need not equal the bare <electron mass>. Negative electronic curvature near a band maximum is conveniently described by positive-mass <electron holes>.

Work at zero <temperature> and fixed total <carrier density>. Write $n_\uparrow=n/2+\delta$ and $n_\downarrow=n/2-\delta$. Let $g_s(E_F)$ be the <single-spin density of states> per unit volume in the unpolarised state. Since $d\mathcal E_s/dn_s=E_{Fs}$ and $dE_{Fs}/dn_s=1/g_s$, the linear kinetic-energy shifts cancel and the quadratic cost is
$$
\Delta\mathcal E_{\rm kin}=\frac{\delta^2}{g_s(E_F)}+O(\delta^4).
$$
The repulsive interaction instead decreases under <spin polarization>:
$$
\mathcal E_{\rm int}=U\left(\frac{n^2}{4}-\delta^2\right),\qquad
\Delta\mathcal E=\left[\frac1{g_s(E_F)}-U\right]\delta^2+O(\delta^4).
$$
Hence the <Stoner criterion for an opposite-spin contact repulsion> is
$$
\boxed{U_c=\frac1{g_s(E_F)}=\frac2{g_{\rm tot}(E_F)},\qquad U>U_c\text{ destabilises the unpolarised state}.}
$$
The two forms differ only in whether the stated <density of states> counts one spin or both. For the three-dimensional <free electron model>, $g_{\rm tot}(E_F)=3n/(2E_F)$, so
$$
\boxed{U_c=\frac{4E_F}{3n}.}
$$
Equivalently, for fractional polarisation $\zeta=(n_\uparrow-n_\downarrow)/n$, the kinetic-energy density is $\tfrac3{10}nE_F[(1+\zeta)^{5/3}+(1-\zeta)^{5/3}]$; its quadratic coefficient is $nE_F/3$, while the interaction contributes $-Un^2/4$. With number densities, $U$ has units of energy times volume and the displayed interaction is an energy density; multiplying by the sample volume does not change the threshold.

An <angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy> intensity map locates occupied <electronic band structure> through its bright dispersing ridges. For a nearly isotropic <graphene> cone,
$$
E_\pm(\mathbf q)=E_D\pm\hbar v_F|\mathbf q|,\qquad
\boxed{v_F=\frac1\hbar\left|\frac{dE}{dk}\right|.}
$$
Fit straight lines to the reasonably linear portions of the ridges, avoiding the broad central crossing, and extrapolate them to locate the <Dirac point> energy $E_D$. The PDF places the crossing near $k_y=0$ and plotted energy roughly $1.4$–$1.5\,\mathrm{eV}$. On a lower branch, a representative change from approximately $(|k_y|,E)=(0.10\,\mathrm{\mathring A}^{-1},0.9\,\mathrm{eV})$ towards $(0,1.5\,\mathrm{eV})$ gives a slope near $6\,\mathrm{eV\,\mathring A}$. Allowing for the ridge width gives about $5$–$7\,\mathrm{eV\,\mathring A}$, hence
$$
\boxed{v_F\simeq(0.8\text{--}1.1)\times10^6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\quad\text{approximately }10^6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
Here $1\,\mathrm{eV\,\mathring A}/\hbar=1.52\times10^5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

To determine the carrier <Fermi energy>, identify the <Fermi level> from a calibrated photoemission cutoff, then measure its separation from the Dirac point:
$$
\boxed{E_F^{\rm cone}=|E_{\rm F,plot}-E_D|=\hbar v_F k_F,\qquad p_F=\hbar k_F.}
$$
The separation of the two Fermi crossings gives $2k_F$ for a cut through the cone centre. This determines the doping energy relative to the Dirac point; the absolute plotted energy origin is not itself a Fermi energy.

**The reproduced panel has no marked Fermi level or stated energy-zero calibration.** Its upper ridges end near $1.9\,\mathrm{eV}$, but that alone does not prove that the endpoint is a Fermi cutoff. If it is the intended cutoff, the estimate is $E_F^{\rm cone}\simeq0.4$–$0.5\,\mathrm{eV}$ and $k_F\sim0.06$–$0.08\,\mathrm{\mathring A}^{-1}$. If instead the plotted numbers are binding energies measured from an unstated Fermi zero, the inferred separation would be roughly $1.4$–$1.5\,\mathrm{eV}$. The supplied figure securely supports the slope estimate, but it does not distinguish these energy-reference conventions; an unconditional numerical Fermi energy requires the missing calibration.

Take $q=-e$ as the signed electron charge. The <Lorentz oscillator model> is a driven damped bound charge:
$$
\boxed{m\ddot{\mathbf u}+m\gamma\dot{\mathbf u}+m\omega_0^2\mathbf u=q\mathbf E(t).}
$$
The first term is inertia, the second is a phenomenological frictional loss of coherent motion to other degrees of freedom, and the third is the linear restoring force from binding to the atom. The driving term is the <electric force>. The natural <angular frequency> $\omega_0$ represents the <electronic transition>; this classical oscillator describes its near-resonant <linear response>. The friction convention is $m\gamma\dot u$, so no factor two is hidden in $\gamma$.

= Solution

Substitute harmonic amplitudes with the specified $e^{-i\omega t}$ convention:
$$
m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega)\mathbf u_0=q\mathbf E_0.
$$
One electron contributes a dipole moment $\mathbf p=q\mathbf u_0$, so the <electric polarization> of a density $n$ is
$$
\mathbf P_0=nq\mathbf u_0
=\frac{nq^2}{m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega)}\mathbf E_0.
$$
Using $\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P=\epsilon_0\epsilon_r\mathbf E$ gives
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_r(\omega)=1+\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega},\qquad
\omega_p^2=\frac{nq^2}{m\epsilon_0}.}
$$
Thus the paper's dimensionless $\epsilon_\omega$ is the <relative permittivity>, rather than the absolute <permittivity> $\epsilon_0\epsilon_r$. The electron's negative charge disappears from the <electric susceptibility> because both the force and dipole moment contain $q$.

= Solution

Set $D=(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+\gamma^2\omega^2$. The <real part> and <imaginary part> of the <complex permittivity> are
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_r'=1+\frac{\omega_p^2(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)}D,\qquad
\epsilon_r''=\frac{\omega_p^2\gamma\omega}D.}
$$
For positive frequency the loss part is nonnegative. The real part starts at $1+\omega_p^2/\omega_0^2$, rises below resonance, falls steeply through the resonance region, and approaches one from below at high frequency. The imaginary part is a positive absorption peak near $\omega_0$, tending to zero on both sides.

For a nonmagnetic medium write its <complex refractive index> as $N=n_r+i\kappa$, with $N^2=\epsilon_r$ and <optical extinction coefficient> $\kappa$. In a transparent region with $\kappa\ll n_r$ and $\epsilon_r'>0$,
$$
\boxed{n_r\simeq\sqrt{\epsilon_r'},\qquad
\kappa\simeq\frac{\epsilon_r''}{2\sqrt{\epsilon_r'}},\qquad
\alpha_{\rm abs}=\frac{2\omega\kappa}{c}.}
$$
The real part controls <phase velocity> and <optical dispersion>; the loss part controls <wave absorption> and the cycle-averaged dissipated power density $\omega\epsilon_0\epsilon_r''|E_0|^2/2$. Weak damping gives a narrow line but does not make <wave absorption> weak at its centre. The transparent-medium approximation applies away from the line.

For independent <electronic transitions>, add the <electric dipole moment> responses:
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_r(\omega)=1+\sum_j\frac{S_j}{\omega_j^2-\omega^2-i\gamma_j\omega},\qquad S_j>0.}
$$
The positive constants $S_j$ include electron density and transition <atomic oscillator strength>. Each term adds an absorption peak to $\epsilon_r''$ and a dispersive positive-to-negative contribution to $\epsilon_r'-1$, as in the middle row of the figure.

Between well-separated absorption lines, where the medium is transparent, the <refractive index> generally increases with frequency: $dn_r/d\omega>0$, or $dn_r/d\lambda<0$. This is <normal dispersion>. Near a resonance the real response turns down with increasing frequency, producing an interval of <anomalous dispersion>, $dn_r/d\omega<0$, accompanied by appreciable absorption. The absorptive and dispersive parts are linked by the <Kramers-Kronig relations>; the steep variation is not independent of the loss spectrum.

= Solution
{parent=e}

An unbound conduction <electron> has no local restoring force, so set $\omega_0=0$ while retaining inertia and <momentum relaxation time> $\tau=1/\gamma$. This is the <Drude model>:
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_r(\omega)=1-\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega(\omega+i\gamma)}.}
$$
Separating the response gives
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_r'=1-\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2+\gamma^2},\qquad
\epsilon_r''=\frac{\omega_p^2\gamma}{\omega(\omega^2+\gamma^2)}.}
$$
For a good <metal>, $\gamma\ll\omega_p$. The real part is strongly negative at low frequency, crosses zero at $\sqrt{\omega_p^2-\gamma^2}\simeq\omega_p$, and approaches one from below. The imaginary part diverges as $\omega_p^2/(\gamma\omega)$ at low frequency, reflecting the finite dc <electrical conductivity> $\sigma_0=\epsilon_0\omega_p^2/\gamma$, then decays as $\omega^{-3}$. The low-frequency imaginary response describes conduction loss, not a finite-frequency bound-electron absorption line. Core-electron and interband responses can add a background in a real metal.

= Solution
{parent=f}

At normal incidence from vacuum, the <Fresnel equations> give the <reflectance>
$$
\boxed{R(\omega)=\left|\frac{N(\omega)-1}{N(\omega)+1}\right|^2,\qquad N^2=\epsilon_r.}
$$
A good metal has $R$ close to one below its <plasma frequency>. At very low frequency the electromagnetic field penetrates only a <skin depth>; the local Drude limit gives $1-R\simeq\sqrt{8\epsilon_0\omega/\sigma_0}$. In the collisionless negative-permittivity range the transmitted field is evanescent and reflection remains strong. Near the plasma edge the reflectance drops sharply; above it $\epsilon_r'>0$ and propagating waves become possible. In the ideal free-electron high-frequency limit,
$$
\boxed{R\sim\frac{\omega_p^4}{16\omega^4}\longrightarrow0.}
$$
Finite damping rounds the plasma edge, and interband absorption changes the detailed response of actual metals.

Choose one <atomic orbital> $\phi_A$ and $\phi_B$ on the two atoms and approximate a <molecular orbital> by the <linear combination of atomic orbitals>
$$
\psi=c_A\phi_A+c_B\phi_B.
$$
Projecting the <Schrödinger equation> onto the two orbitals gives a <generalized eigenvalue problem> $H\mathbf c=ES\mathbf c$, where $H_{ij}=\langle\phi_i|\hat H|\phi_j\rangle$ and $S_{ij}=\langle\phi_i|\phi_j\rangle$. In an orthonormal basis the matrix is
$$
H=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_A&t\\t^*&\epsilon_B\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
E_\pm=\frac{\epsilon_A+\epsilon_B}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{(\epsilon_A-\epsilon_B)^2}{4}+|t|^2}.
$$
The coupling mixes the atomic states and produces a lower <bonding molecular orbital> and upper <antibonding molecular orbital>. For equivalent real orbitals with overlap $s$ the normalized combinations are $(\phi_A\pm\phi_B)/\sqrt{2(1\pm s)}$; the precise energy ordering follows from the Hamiltonian matrix elements. This restricted orbital basis is an approximation to the molecule's wavefunction, rather than a claim that exact isolated-atom orbitals remain unchanged in the molecule.

Write the dispersive band as $E_p=Ak^2$ with $A>0$ and define $k_c=\sqrt{E_{\rm fl}/A}$. The uncoupled sketch is an upward-opening parabola and a horizontal <flat band> at positive energy. They cross on the sphere $k=k_c$ in three dimensions, or at $\pm k_c$ in a one-dimensional cut:
$$
\boxed{E_p(k_c)=E_{\rm fl},\qquad \alpha=0.}
$$
Without mixing the crossing is allowed. The left panel of the figure shows this case.

= Solution

At nonzero <band hybridization>, the crossing becomes an <avoided crossing>. The separation at $k_c$ is $2\alpha$. If $\alpha\ll E_{\rm fl}$, appreciable mixing occurs only where $|Ak^2-E_{\rm fl}|\lesssim\alpha$; away from that region each branch retains nearly its original orbital character. For $\alpha\sim E_{\rm fl}$, the avoided crossing is broad and both branches are substantially reshaped, as shown in the figure.

Use $E_-$ for the lower branch and $E_+$ for the upper one. For $\alpha>0$,
$$
E_-(k)<E_{\rm fl}<E_+(k),\qquad
\min_k E_+(k)=E_+(0)=\frac{E_{\rm fl}+\sqrt{E_{\rm fl}^2+4\alpha^2}}2.
$$
The lower branch increases towards $E_{\rm fl}$ from below. Thus an indirect gap exists in the exactly flat-band model. In the unbounded-parabola limit its lower bound is
$$
\boxed{\Delta_{\rm ind}=\frac{\sqrt{E_{\rm fl}^2+4\alpha^2}-E_{\rm fl}}2
\simeq\frac{\alpha^2}{E_{\rm fl}}\quad(\alpha\ll E_{\rm fl}).}
$$
A finite <Brillouin zone> gives a finite maximum of $E_-$ below $E_{\rm fl}$ and hence a slightly larger indirect gap. This <hybridization gap> differs from the local direct gap $2\alpha$.

The material can therefore be a <band insulator> when the lower band is full and the upper band empty. With two <spin>-degenerate orbitals per <unit cell>, the required filling is
$$
\boxed{2\text{ electrons per unit cell, i.e. half of the combined four-state capacity}.}
$$
With spin degeneracy removed, replace two by one for each independent spin sector. Other fillings are metallic in this noninteracting model.

At each wavevector the orbital-basis <Hamiltonian> is
$$
H(\mathbf k)=\begin{pmatrix}E_p(k)&\alpha\\\alpha&E_{\rm fl}\end{pmatrix}.
$$
A phase choice makes the momentum-independent mixing real and nonnegative. The <characteristic equation> is
$$
(E_p-E)(E_{\rm fl}-E)-\alpha^2=0,
$$
so
$$
\boxed{E_\pm(k)=\frac12\left[E_p+E_{\rm fl}\pm\sqrt{(E_p-E_{\rm fl})^2+4\alpha^2}\right].}
$$
For $\alpha>0$, an eigenvector obeys $c_{\rm fl}/c_p=(E_\pm-E_p)/\alpha$. At the bare crossing, the normalized states are $(|p\rangle\pm|{\rm fl}\rangle)/\sqrt2$ with energies $E_{\rm fl}\pm\alpha$. The lower state's parabolic-orbital weight is
$$
|c_{p,-}|^2=\frac12\left[1-\frac{E_p-E_{\rm fl}}{\sqrt{(E_p-E_{\rm fl})^2+4\alpha^2}}\right],
$$
which tends to one below the crossing and zero above it. This makes the exchange of band character in the sketches explicit.

Let the common uncoupled <Fermi wavevector> be $k_0$. Equal <carrier densities> imply equal <Fermi surface> volumes, and hence equal radii for the spherical spin-degenerate electron pockets. The conserved total density is
$$
n_{\rm tot}=\frac{2k_0^3}{3\pi^2}.
$$
If hybridisation leaves only one occupied electron-like band, state counting gives
$$
\boxed{k_F=2^{1/3}k_0,\qquad p_F=2^{1/3}p_0,\qquad
\frac{\Delta p_F}{p_0}=2^{1/3}-1\simeq0.260.}
$$
This is the familiar single-large-Fermi-surface answer. It requires the other hybridised band to be empty.

**For finite heavy-band mass, that conclusion does not follow from an arbitrary nonzero coupling.** At sufficiently small $\alpha$, the upper band still has an occupied pocket, so there are two Fermi momenta, not one. The exact fixed-density statement is <Fermi-volume conservation in two hybridized electron bands>:
$$
\boxed{k_{F,-}^3+k_{F,+}^3=2k_0^3.}
$$
For an explicit weak-coupling check, write $E_p=Ak^2$, $E_h=C+Bk^2$, with $0<B\ll A$, and enforce the uncoupled equality at $k_0$: $C=(A-B)k_0^2$, $\mu_0=Ak_0^2$. Put $\mu=\mu_0+\delta\mu$ and $y=k_F^2-k_0^2$. The Fermi-surface equation is
$$
(\delta\mu-Ay)(\delta\mu-By)-\alpha^2=0.
$$
At fixed density $\delta\mu=O(\alpha^2)$, giving
$$
\boxed{k_{F,\mp}=k_0\pm\frac{\alpha}{2k_0\sqrt{AB}}+O(\alpha^2).}
$$
For arbitrary coupling, put $q_\pm=k_{F,\mp}^2$. The roots of the same <characteristic equation>, before discarding unoccupied pockets, are
$$
q_\pm=\frac{(A+B)\mu-AC\pm\sqrt{[(A-B)\mu-AC]^2+4AB\alpha^2}}{2AB}.
$$
Choose the <chemical potential> so that $[\max(q_+,0)]^{3/2}+[\max(q_-,0)]^{3/2}=2k_0^3$. This specifies the <Fermi momentum> of each pocket for any supplied curvatures and coupling.

The transition to one occupied pocket occurs at $\mu=E_+(0)$. Set $q_*=2^{2/3}k_0^2$; at the transition the two squared radii are $q_*$ and zero. Their sum and product give
$$
\mu_c=\frac{A(C+Bq_*)}{A+B},\qquad
\boxed{\alpha_c^2=\mu_c(\mu_c-C)
=\frac{AB(C+Bq_*)(Aq_*-C)}{(A+B)^2}.}
$$
For $0<\alpha<\alpha_c$ there are two pockets; for $\alpha\geq\alpha_c$ the only nonzero radius is $2^{1/3}k_0$. The outer radius grows continuously and the inner one shrinks. The nearly <flat band> limit can make depopulation occur at a small coupling, but the mass ratio or a single-surface assumption is needed to turn the question into a unique finite-coupling numerical change.

Count wavevectors in a large volume with <periodic boundary conditions>. One spin-degenerate spherical band supplies
$$
\frac{N(k)}{\mathcal V}=\frac{2}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{4\pi k^3}{3}=\frac{k^3}{3\pi^2},\qquad
\frac1{\mathcal V}\frac{dN}{dk}=\frac{k^2}{\pi^2}.
$$
For the <conduction band>, $k=\sqrt{2m_e^*(\epsilon-\epsilon_c)}/\hbar$ and $dk/d\epsilon=m_e^*/(\hbar^2k)$. Transforming the state count to energy gives
$$
\boxed{g_e(\epsilon)=\frac1{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m_e^*}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt{\epsilon-\epsilon_c},\qquad\epsilon\geq\epsilon_c.}
$$
For the <valence band> use $|dk/d\epsilon|=m_h^*/(\hbar^2k)$ and $k=\sqrt{2m_h^*(\epsilon_v-\epsilon)}/\hbar$, obtaining
$$
\boxed{g_h(\epsilon)=\frac1{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m_h^*}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt{\epsilon_v-\epsilon},\qquad\epsilon\leq\epsilon_v.}
$$
Each <density of states> is zero beyond its indicated band edge. The factor two counts spin; an extra valley degeneracy was not assumed in these stipulated isotropic bands.

= Solution

In the nondegenerate <conduction band>, the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> reduces to its <Boltzmann distribution> tail. Thus
$$
\begin{aligned}
n&=\int_{\epsilon_c}^\infty g_e(\epsilon)e^{-(\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)}d\epsilon\\
&=N_c e^{-(\epsilon_c-\mu)/(k_BT)},\qquad
\boxed{N_c=2\left(\frac{m_e^*k_BT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here the substitution $x=(\epsilon-\epsilon_c)/(k_BT)$ leaves $\int_0^\infty x^{1/2}e^{-x}dx=\sqrt\pi/2$. For <electron holes> use the empty-state probability $1-f(\epsilon)\simeq e^{(\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)}$, not the conduction-band Boltzmann factor. This gives
$$
p=N_v e^{-(\mu-\epsilon_v)/(k_BT)},\qquad
\boxed{N_v=2\left(\frac{m_h^*k_BT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}}.
$$
The <chemical potential> cancels in the product. With $E_g=\epsilon_c-\epsilon_v$,
$$
\boxed{np=N_cN_v e^{-E_g/(k_BT)}
=\frac12(m_e^*m_h^*)^{3/2}\left(\frac{k_BT}{\pi\hbar^2}\right)^3e^{-E_g/(k_BT)}=n_i^2.}
$$
This <semiconductor mass-action law> holds in thermal equilibrium for nondegenerate carriers, including a doped <semiconductor>. Only in an <intrinsic semiconductor> must $n=p=n_i$. The factors $N_c,N_v$ are the <effective density of states>; the factor $1/2$ in the final expression follows from the two spin factors already included in (a).

= Solution
{parent=c}

Ordinary doped <semiconductor devices> need a regime where their <donor impurities> or <acceptor impurities> supply mobile <charge carriers> while intrinsic thermal excitation remains sufficiently small.

At low <temperature>, <donor impurities> or <acceptor impurities> may no longer be ionized. <Dopant freeze-out> reduces the <carrier density>, raises <electrical resistance> and changes the intended <carrier injection> and <p-n junction> response. At high <temperature> the <intrinsic carrier concentration> grows as
$$
\boxed{n_i\propto T^{3/2}e^{-E_g/(2k_BT)}}
$$
when <band effective masses> and <band gap> are treated as constant. Once it becomes comparable with the net doping density, it overwhelms the intended electron-hole imbalance; reverse leakage and dissipation increase and useful p/n control is lost.

The usual operating window lies between freeze-out and intrinsic conduction, with mobility and thermal management placing further practical limits. This is a statement about conventional doped-device operation, not a fundamental prohibition on engineered cryogenic or wide-bandgap devices.

An ideal diffusion-dominated <p-n junction> obeys the <Shockley diode equation>
$$
\boxed{I(V,T)=I_s(T)\left[e^{eV/(\eta k_BT)}-1\right],}
$$
with <diode ideality factor> $\eta=1$ in the simplest model. Forward bias gives exponential current growth; reverse bias gives a nearly constant $-I_s$ until <diode reverse breakdown>, outside this ideal expression.

For a long abrupt <p-n junction> in low <carrier injection>, the <diode saturation current> is
$$
\boxed{I_s=e\mathcal A n_i^2\left(\frac{D_p}{L_pN_D}+\frac{D_n}{L_nN_A}\right)=C(T)n_i^2.}
$$
Here $\mathcal A$ is area, $N_D,N_A$ are ionized dopant densities, and $D,L$ are minority-carrier diffusion coefficients and diffusion lengths in the appropriate regions. At each temperature, extract $I_s$ either from the reverse-saturation plateau below breakdown or from a forward exponential fit using its measured <diode ideality factor>.

<Extracting a semiconductor band gap from diode saturation current> requires correcting for the transport prefactor:
$$
\boxed{\log\frac{I_s(T)}{C(T)T^3}=\text{constant}-\frac{E_g}{k_BT}.}
$$
A plot against $1/T$ has slope $-E_g/k_B$ when the gap and effective masses are nearly temperature-independent. Alternatively divide the measured $I_s$ by a known $C(T)$ to recover the full $n_i^2(T)$. The prefactor cannot generally be assumed constant over an arbitrarily broad range. Junction-generation or recombination-dominated current can scale as $n_i$ instead, so the diffusion regime should be identified from the current-voltage behaviour.

= Solution
{parent=e}

A current-carrying sample in a transverse <magnetic field> develops a transverse electric field because the <Lorentz force> deflects its <charge carriers>. In steady state this <Hall voltage> balances the transverse deflection. With <Hall coefficient> $R_H=E_y/(j_xB_z)$, one isotropic electron band gives $R_H=-1/(ne)$ and one hole band gives $R_H=+1/(pe)$.

When <electrons> and <electron holes> both conduct, the weak-field two-carrier result is
$$
\boxed{R_H=\frac{p\mu_h^2-n\mu_e^2}{e(p\mu_h+n\mu_e)^2},\qquad
\sigma=e(n\mu_e+p\mu_h).}
$$
The <two-carrier Hall coefficient> weights each species by mobility squared. Its sign changes when
$$
\boxed{p\mu_h^2=n\mu_e^2.}
$$
<Temperature> changes dopant ionization, intrinsic electron-hole generation, and the two <charge carrier mobilities>, so it can move the system across this condition. For example, a <p-type semiconductor> can be hole-dominated in its extrinsic regime but become negative in its intrinsic regime if $n\simeq p$ and $\mu_e>\mu_h$. A sign change is possible, not mandatory: in many <n-type semiconductors> <electrons> dominate at both temperatures. The density majority alone does not determine the Hall sign in a two-carrier material.
