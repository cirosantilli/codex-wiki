In the three-dimensional <Debye model>, the <lattice> has $3N$ <normal modes> for $N$ atoms. Replacing their dispersion by a linear acoustic dispersion up to a cutoff gives the <Debye heat capacity>
$$
C_m(T)=9Nk_B\left(\frac{T}{\theta_D}\right)^3\int_0^{\theta_D/T}\frac{x^4e^x}{(e^x-1)^2}\,dx.
$$
At low <temperature> the upper limit can be replaced by infinity. Since the integral then equals $4\pi^4/15$,
$$
\boxed{C_m(T)\sim\frac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk_B\left(\frac{T}{\theta_D}\right)^3\quad(T\ll\theta_D).}
$$
At high <temperature> each harmonic mode contributes $k_B$ by the <equipartition theorem>, giving the <Dulong-Petit law>
$$
\boxed{C_m(T)\longrightarrow3Nk_B\quad(T\gg\theta_D).}
$$
For one mole replace $Nk_B$ by $R$. The curve starts with zero slope, rises as $T^3$, and levels off toward the classical limit. This is the <lattice> contribution; a metal's <electronic heat capacity> is a separate low-temperature term.

The <Debye wavevector> follows by counting three polarizations inside the cutoff sphere:
$$
3\frac{\mathcal V}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{4\pi k_D^3}{3}=3N,\qquad k_D=(6\pi^2n)^{1/3}.
$$
With the supplied <speed of sound> treated as the effective Debye speed, $\omega_D=vk_D$ and
$$
\boxed{\theta_D=\frac{\hbar v}{k_B}(6\pi^2n)^{1/3}\simeq\frac{\hbar(3600)}{k_B}(6\pi^2\times5.9\times10^{28})^{1/3}\simeq4.17\times10^2\,\mathrm K.}
$$
Here $k_D\simeq1.52\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$. Real crystals have different longitudinal and transverse sound speeds; the one-speed estimate uses the stated value, rather than inferring those unprovided branches.

Use physical fields proportional to the <real part> of $e^{-i\omega t}$. The <Drude frequency response> is
$$
\sigma(\omega)=\frac{\sigma_0}{1-i\omega\tau},\qquad \boxed{|\sigma(\omega)|^2=\frac{\sigma_0^2}{1+\omega^2\tau^2}.}
$$
Thus the sketch is an even <Lorentzian line shape>, with maximum $\sigma_0^2$ at zero <frequency>, half that value at $|\omega|=\tau^{-1}$, and an $\omega^{-2}$ tail. The positive-frequency half is shown in the preceding figure. Squaring the <modulus> differs from plotting either $|\sigma|$ or the <real part> of $\sigma$.

For a single carrier species, the <plasma frequency> and <electrical conductivity> satisfy
$$
\omega_p^2=\frac{nq^2}{m\epsilon_0},\qquad \sigma_0=\frac{nq^2\tau}{m}=\epsilon_0\omega_p^2\tau.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\omega_{1/2}=\frac1\tau=\frac{\epsilon_0\omega_p^2}{\sigma_0}=\frac{(8.854\times10^{-12})(5.6\times10^{15})^2}{9.9\times10^6}\simeq2.80\times10^{13}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}.}
$$
The corresponding <relaxation time> is $\tau\simeq3.57\times10^{-14}\,\mathrm s$. This is an <angular frequency>; the ordinary <frequency> is $\boxed{f_{1/2}=\omega_{1/2}/(2\pi)\simeq4.46\times10^{12}\,\mathrm{Hz}}$. The units of <electrical conductivity> used in the calculation are $\mathrm{\Omega^{-1}m^{-1}}$, consistently with the SI plasma-frequency relation.

For nondegenerate <electrons> in a parabolic <conduction band>,
$$
n(T)=N_c(T)\exp\left[-\frac{\Delta(T)}{k_BT}\right],\qquad \Delta(T)=E_c(T)-\mu(T),\qquad N_c(T)\propto T^{3/2}
$$
when the density-of-states <effective mass> and valley multiplicity are fixed. The <intrinsic carrier extrapolation across a crossover> must therefore specify how the activation <energy> changes with <temperature>.

The usual intended estimate treats the quoted $0.9\,\mathrm{eV}$ as a constant intrinsic activation <energy> between the two temperatures, and identifies the crossover by $n_i(T_c)\simeq n_{\rm ext}$. Then
$$
\frac{n_i(T_c)}{n_i(T_1)}=\left(\frac{T_c}{T_1}\right)^{3/2}\exp\left[-\frac{0.9\,\mathrm{eV}}{k_B}\left(\frac1{T_c}-\frac1{T_1}\right)\right].
$$
For $T_1=350\,\mathrm K$, $T_c=280\,\mathrm K$ and $k_B=8.6173\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{eV\,K^{-1}}$, the exponent is $-7.46$ and the prefactor is $0.8^{3/2}=0.7155$. Hence
$$
\boxed{n_{\rm ext}\simeq(2.3\times10^{16})(0.7155)e^{-7.46}\simeq9.5\times10^{12}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}\quad\text{under the constant-activation approximation}.}
$$
Equivalently, a fixed-gap intrinsic model with $E_g/2=0.9\,\mathrm{eV}$ gives this result. A crossover is gradual: if $n_{\rm ext}$ denotes the net ionized donor density and $n_i(T_c)=n_{\rm ext}$, <charge neutrality> gives the actual <electron> population $n(T_c)=(1+\sqrt5)n_{\rm ext}/2$, rather than exactly $n_{\rm ext}$.

There are two limits to interpreting the numerical estimate literally. First, the printed data specify $E_c-\mu$ only at $350\,\mathrm K$, without specifying $E_g$ or the valence <density of states>. They do not mathematically determine $\Delta(280\,\mathrm K)$: without the intended activation assumption the answer remains
$$
n_{\rm ext}\simeq n(350)\left(\frac{280}{350}\right)^{3/2}\exp\left[-\frac{\Delta(280)}{k_B(280)}+\frac{0.9\,\mathrm{eV}}{k_B(350)}\right].
$$
Second, using the original PDF's $0.9\,\mathrm{eV}$ and density implies $N_c(350)\simeq2.1\times10^{29}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, extraordinarily large for a conventional <semiconductor>. This confirms that the numerical data should not be treated as a realistic material determination. No unprinted decimal-point correction has been assumed.

A <junction field-effect transistor> has a conducting channel between source and drain, with gate regions forming reverse-biased <p-n junctions> to that channel. In an n-channel device, a more negative gate-source <electric potential difference> increases the <reverse bias> and widens the <depletion region>. The conducting cross-section shrinks, reducing the drain <electric current>. The gate ideally draws only a small leakage <electric current>, giving a high input resistance; the source-drain supply provides the controlled output <power>. Carrier motion in a p-channel device reverses the relevant polarities.

At small positive $V_{DS}$, the channel conducts approximately as a resistor. As the potential rises from source to drain, the local gate-channel <reverse bias> increases toward the drain. The depletion layers are therefore thicker there. For a symmetric n channel of half-thickness $a$, uniformly doped at density $N_D$, the one-sided <depletion approximation> gives
$$
w(y)\simeq\sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_s}{eN_D}\left[V_{\rm bi}+V(y)-V_{GS}\right]},
$$
where $w$ is the depletion width from each side and $V_{\rm bi}$ the built-in junction potential. The neutral channel closes locally when $w=a$. Define the positive geometrical depletion <electric potential difference> $U_P=eN_Da^2/(2\varepsilon_s)$. The approximate drain-end pinch-off condition is
$$
\boxed{V_{DS,\rm sat}\simeq U_P-V_{\rm bi}+V_{GS}.}
$$
More negative gate bias makes this saturation occur at a smaller drain <electric potential difference>.

\Image[2016-jfet-pinch-off.png]
{title=Gate depletion, drain-end pinch-off and approximate current saturation in an n-channel JFET}

<Pinch-off in a junction field-effect transistor> does **not** mean that the drain <electric current> vanishes. <Electrons> arriving through the remaining neutral channel are swept across the high-field depleted region into the drain. Further increases in $V_{DS}$ mostly fall across that region, so the <electric current> changes only weakly: this is the saturation regime used for amplification. Channel shortening gives a finite output slope in real devices. Excessive reverse <electric potential difference> eventually causes breakdown, beyond the normal saturation model.

A sufficiently negative gate <electric potential difference> can instead deplete the entire channel from the source end and produce **cutoff**. In the simplified geometry its threshold is $V_{GS,\rm off}\simeq V_{\rm bi}-U_P<0$. A commonly used approximate transfer law in saturation is
$$
I_D\simeq I_{DSS}\left(1-\frac{V_{GS}}{V_{GS,\rm off}}\right)^2,\qquad V_{GS,\rm off}\leq V_{GS}\leq0.
$$
Here $I_{DSS}$ is the saturated drain <electric current> at zero gate-source bias. The square law is a useful device approximation, not an exact result for arbitrary channel profiles. It connects field control of depletion to a measurable transfer characteristic while keeping drain-end pinch-off distinct from complete gate cutoff.

<Direct exchange> is a quantum-mechanical spin-dependent <energy> arising from the <Coulomb interaction> and the required antisymmetry of a two-electron state. It requires spatial overlap of the relevant orbitals. It is not the classical magnetic dipole <force> between two small magnets, and it can be much stronger than that <force>.

For an explicit fixed-orbital illustration, take orthonormal spatial orbitals $a,b$. <Orthonormality> means $\langle a|b\rangle=0$; it does not require their spatial densities to occupy disjoint regions. The normalized spatial states are
$$
\Psi_\pm(1,2)=\frac{a(1)b(2)\pm b(1)a(2)}{\sqrt2}.
$$
The symmetric spatial state combines with the antisymmetric <spin-one-half singlet state>, and the antisymmetric spatial state combines with a symmetric <spin-one-half triplet state>, so that the complete fermionic state is antisymmetric.

For <Coulomb potential> $V_{12}=e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon|\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2|)$, distinguish the direct electrostatic integral and exchange integral:
$$
D=\int d^3r_1d^3r_2\,|a(1)|^2V_{12}|b(2)|^2,
$$
$$
K=\int d^3r_1d^3r_2\,a^*(1)b(1)V_{12}b^*(2)a(2).
$$
The <Coulomb exchange splitting of two orbitals> gives
$$
E_S=\epsilon_a+\epsilon_b+D+K,\qquad E_T=\epsilon_a+\epsilon_b+D-K,\qquad \boxed{E_S-E_T=2K.}
$$
The repulsive <Coulomb potential> makes $K\geq0$ in this model. Thus the triplet is favored when the orbitals have nonzero exchange overlap. Its antisymmetric spatial <wavefunction> suppresses close <electron> encounters and reduces their repulsion. Since the <Coulomb interaction> contains no explicit <spin> operator, the <spin> dependence enters through the allowed spatial <symmetry>.

For dimensionless <spin> operators $\mathbf s_i=\boldsymbol\sigma_i/2$, whose pair <eigenvalues> are $1/4$ in the triplet and $-3/4$ in the singlet, the same splitting is represented by
$$
\boxed{H_{\rm ex}=C-2K\,\mathbf s_1\cdot\mathbf s_2.}
$$
This explicitly states the sign convention: positive $K$ here favors parallel spins. A convention $H=J\mathbf s_1\cdot\mathbf s_2$ would instead have $J=-2K$.

In a <crystal>, <direct exchange> therefore depends sensitively on orbital extent, overlap and interatomic spacing. It often decreases rapidly as localized orbitals separate. The fixed-orbital example does not prove that every material must be ferromagnetic: orbital rearrangement and competing kinetic or virtual-hopping processes alter the singlet-triplet balance. In particular, <antiferromagnetic> <superexchange> through an intermediate ligand is a different mechanism and should not be confused with direct overlap exchange.

A <magnetic field> quantizes a charged carrier's cyclotron motion into <Landau levels>. For an isotropic parabolic band, neglecting <spin> splitting,
$$
\varepsilon_{\ell,k_z}=\hbar\omega_c\left(\ell+\frac12\right)+\frac{\hbar^2k_z^2}{2m^*},\qquad \omega_c=\frac{eB}{m^*},\qquad \ell=0,1,2,\ldots.
$$
The degeneracy of each orbital level per unit perpendicular area is $eB/h$ per <spin>. Increasing the field changes both the level spacing and the number of states in each level. Successive levels cross the <Fermi energy>, producing an oscillatory <density of states> and hence <quantum oscillations> in thermodynamic and transport quantities.

For a general closed semiclassical orbit, <Onsager quantization> relates its wavevector-space area to field:
$$
A_k(\varepsilon_\ell)=\frac{2\pi eB}{\hbar}(\ell+\gamma).
$$
Setting this area to an extremal Fermi-surface cross-section gives successive inverse-field crossings separated by
$$
\boxed{\Delta\left(\frac1B\right)=\frac{2\pi e}{\hbar A_{\rm ext}},\qquad F=\frac{\hbar A_{\rm ext}}{2\pi e}.}
$$
A typical oscillatory contribution is proportional to $\cos(2\pi F/B+\varphi)$. The oscillations are periodic in $1/B$, not in $B$. In three dimensions, extremal cross-sections dominate because contributions from other longitudinal momenta cancel by phase averaging. Rotating the field therefore maps the shape of the <Fermi surface>.

\Image[2016-quantum-oscillations.png]
{title=Quantized orbital crossings and their periodicity in inverse magnetic field}

Oscillations in <magnetization> are the <de Haas-van Alphen effect>; oscillations in longitudinal <electrical resistivity> are the <Shubnikov-de Haas effect>. They are most readily observed in clean samples at low <temperature>. The thermal <damping> factor is
$$
R_T=\frac{X}{\sinh X},\qquad X=\frac{2\pi^2k_BT}{\hbar\omega_c},
$$
and a common first-harmonic disorder factor is $R_D=\exp[-\pi/(\omega_c\tau_q)]$. Thus the Landau spacing must be sufficiently large compared with thermal broadening and disorder broadening, approximately $k_BT\lesssim\hbar\omega_c$ and $\omega_c\tau_q\gtrsim1$, with the displayed factors giving the quantitative suppression.

The <temperature> dependence measures the <cyclotron effective mass>, and the field-dependent <damping> probes the <quantum lifetime> $\tau_q$. That lifetime need not equal the <momentum relaxation time> controlling <electrical conductivity>: small-angle scattering can broaden <Landau levels> while relaxing <electric current> inefficiently. <Spin> splitting can generate further modulation or resolved <spin> structure. Oscillations are not the same as the ordinary smooth <magnetoresistance> or <cyclotron resonance>; they probe quantized orbital state counting near the <Fermi energy>.

**<mass> as <band curvature>.** In a periodic potential, a weakly forced <electron> is usefully described by a <wave packet> confined to one <energy band>. Its <group velocity> is
$$
v_i=\frac1\hbar\frac{\partial\varepsilon_n}{\partial k_i},\qquad \hbar\dot k_j=F_j.
$$
Differentiating the <velocity> gives
$$
\dot v_i=\sum_j(m^{*-1})_{ij}F_j,\qquad \boxed{(m^{*-1})_{ij}=\frac1{\hbar^2}\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_n}{\partial k_i\partial k_j}.}
$$
This <effective-mass tensor> describes the band response, not a change of the <electron>'s bare <rest mass>. Along a principal direction, a steep upward curvature gives a small positive <mass> and a flatter curvature a larger one. Near a band maximum the <electron> curvature and <effective mass> are negative; the corresponding missing-electron description uses positive <electron hole> masses. Anisotropy produces distinct principal masses, and strong nonparabolicity makes them <momentum> dependent. The single-band semiclassical description assumes fields weak and slowly varying enough to avoid substantial interband transitions.

**<Lattice> <symmetry> and <Bloch's theorem>.** Use the active <spatial translation operator> $T_{\mathbf a}\psi(\mathbf r)=\psi(\mathbf r-\mathbf a)$. A lattice-periodic <scalar> potential satisfies $V(\mathbf r+\mathbf a)=V(\mathbf r)$, while the kinetic term is translation invariant. Therefore $[H,T_{\mathbf a}]=0$. The <lattice> translations also commute with one another, so they can be diagonalized simultaneously with $H$, including within a degenerate <energy> subspace.

For a common <eigenstate>, write $T_{\mathbf a}\psi=t(\mathbf a)\psi$. <Unitarity> gives $|t(\mathbf a)|=1$, and the group law gives $t(\mathbf a+\mathbf b)=t(\mathbf a)t(\mathbf b)$. On primitive Bravais vectors $\mathbf a_j$, choose $t(\mathbf a_j)=e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf a_j}$. Every <lattice> vector is an integer combination of these generators, hence
$$
T_{\mathbf a}\psi_{n\mathbf k}=e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf a}\psi_{n\mathbf k},\qquad \psi_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r+\mathbf a)=e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf a}\psi_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r).
$$
The vector $\mathbf k$ is defined modulo a <reciprocal lattice> vector. The band index labels the different <energy> eigenstates with the same translation character. Define $u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)=e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\psi_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)$. The phase factors cancel under a <lattice translation>, proving <Bloch's theorem> in its usual form:
$$
\boxed{\psi_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)=e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r),\qquad u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r+\mathbf a)=u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r).}
$$
This is the <translation-character proof of Bloch theorem>; the <commutator> guarantees a <basis> of such states, even when an arbitrary <superposition> of different translation characters is not itself a translation <eigenstate>.

**Periodic <Hamiltonian>.** Acting once with <momentum> gives $\mathbf p(e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}u)=e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}(\mathbf p+\hbar\mathbf k)u$. Applying it again and cancelling the common plane-wave factor yields
$$
\boxed{H(\mathbf k)u_{n\mathbf k}=\left[\frac{(\mathbf p+\hbar\mathbf k)^2}{2m}+V(\mathbf r)\right]u_{n\mathbf k}=\varepsilon_n(\mathbf k)u_{n\mathbf k}.}
$$
The <Bloch Hamiltonian> acts on lattice-periodic functions in a <unit cell>. Expanding its square separates a zero-momentum <Hamiltonian>, a term linear in $\mathbf k$ and an identity term quadratic in $\mathbf k$.

**Expansion about the zone centre.** Let $|n\rangle=u_{n0}$, $E_n=\varepsilon_n(0)$, and normalize all these states using a unit-cell <inner product>. For the real <scalar> <Hamiltonian> in the question, a nondegenerate zone-centre eigenfunction can be chosen real. Then
$$
\langle n|\mathbf p|n\rangle=-\frac{i\hbar}{2}\int_{\rm cell}\nabla(u_{n0}^2)\,d^3r=0,
$$
because opposite unit-cell faces cancel. Equivalently, <time-reversal symmetry> forbids a nonzero <electric current> in this nondegenerate spinless state. This explains why no linear <energy> term appears; it should not simply be omitted without a <symmetry> or extremum argument.

The first-order <energy> shift is therefore $\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$. In the second-order sum the quadratic term is proportional to the identity and has no off-diagonal <matrix elements>. Only the linear <momentum> vertex contributes. <Second-order nondegenerate perturbation theory> gives the <momentum matrix-element expansion of band energy>
$$
\boxed{\varepsilon_n(\mathbf k)=E_n+\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}+\frac{\hbar^2}{m^2}\sum_{l\ne n}\frac{|\langle n|\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf p|l\rangle|^2}{E_n-E_l}+O(|\mathbf k|^4).}
$$
<Hermitian operator> makes the two reciprocal <matrix elements> <complex conjugates>. All other periodic eigenstates at $\mathbf k=0$ enter the sum. The remainder is even because <time-reversal symmetry> gives $\varepsilon_n(\mathbf k)=\varepsilon_n(-\mathbf k)$ near this isolated state. At a degeneracy the denominators signal the need for <degenerate perturbation theory> instead.

**<mass> ratio and the printed formula.** Write $p^i_{nl}=\langle n|p_i|l\rangle$. Differentiating the quadratic <energy> yields
$$
\boxed{(m^{*-1})_{ij}=\frac{\delta_{ij}}m+\frac2{m^2}\sum_{l\ne n}\frac{\operatorname{Re}(p^i_{nl}p^j_{ln})}{E_n-E_l}.}
$$
For a specified unit direction $\mathbf e$, the resulting <scalar> <mass> ratio is
$$
\boxed{\frac m{m^*_{\mathbf e}}=1+\frac2m\sum_{l\ne n}\frac{|\langle n|\mathbf e\cdot\mathbf p|l\rangle|^2}{E_n-E_l}.}
$$
In an isotropic three-dimensional band, averaging equivalent directions gives
$$
\boxed{\frac m{m^*}=1+\frac{2}{3m}\sum_{l\ne n}\frac{|\langle n|\mathbf p|l\rangle|^2}{E_n-E_l}.}
$$
Coupling to lower bands contributes positively to inverse <mass>; coupling to higher bands contributes negatively. Their signs and <matrix elements> determine the actual curvature.

The original PDF prints $m^*=\hbar^2[\nabla_{\mathbf k}^2\varepsilon_n]^{-1}$. **Taken literally as a three-dimensional <Laplacian>, this misses a factor of three for an isotropic <scalar> <mass>.** The correct identities are
$$
\frac{\nabla_{\mathbf k}^2\varepsilon_n}{\hbar^2}=\operatorname{tr}(m^{*-1}),\qquad m^*=\frac{3\hbar^2}{\nabla_{\mathbf k}^2\varepsilon_n}\quad\text{in three isotropic dimensions}.
$$
The printed expression would return $m/3$ even for the free dispersion $\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$. It is correct in one dimension, or if its <second derivative> is interpreted directionally. For completeness, its literal three-dimensional trace ratio would instead be $3+(2/m)\sum_{l\ne n}|\langle n|\mathbf p|l\rangle|^2/(E_n-E_l)$; that is the trace of the dimensionless inverse-mass <tensor>, not the usual <scalar> $m/m^*$.

**Relaxation-time model.** The <Drude model> treats mobile charge carriers as independent particles accelerated by the <Lorentz force> between scattering events. Collisions randomize the drift <momentum> toward local equilibrium and are represented by a uniform, field-independent <momentum relaxation time>. For the present calculation the <carrier density>, isotropic <mass> and <relaxation time> are fixed, the fields are uniform and stationary, and quantum orbital quantization and intercarrier correlations are neglected. The steady <drift velocity> obeys
$$
0=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)-\frac m\tau\mathbf v.
$$
Here $n$ is the number of carriers of that species per unit volume, $q$ their **signed** charge, $m>0$ their inertial or isotropic band <mass>, $\tau$ the <momentum relaxation time>, and $\sigma_0$ their single-species <electrical conductivity> at zero field.

**One-carrier <tensor>.** With $\mathbf B=B\hat{\mathbf z}$ and $\mathbf J=nq\mathbf v$, solving for the <electric field> gives
$$
\mathbf E=\frac{m}{nq^2\tau}\mathbf J-\frac1{nq}\mathbf J\times\mathbf B.
$$
Since $(\mathbf J\times\mathbf B)_x=BJ_y$ and $(\mathbf J\times\mathbf B)_y=-BJ_x$,
$$
E_x=\frac{m}{nq^2\tau}J_x-\frac{B}{nq}J_y,\qquad E_y=\frac{B}{nq}J_x+\frac{m}{nq^2\tau}J_y.
$$
Thus the <resistivity tensor>, defined by $\mathbf E=\boldsymbol\rho\mathbf J$, is
$$
\boxed{\boldsymbol\rho_q=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_0^{-1}&-B/(nq)\\B/(nq)&\sigma_0^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\qquad \sigma_0=\frac{nq^2\tau}{m}.}
$$
Its off-diagonal signs follow from the stated axes and signed $q$. For zero transverse <electric current>, $E_y/J_x=B/(nq)$ is the Hall <electrical resistivity>; reversing charge reverses it. The single-species longitudinal <electrical resistivity> is independent of $B$ in this ideal isotropic model.

**Adding <electrons> and <electron holes>.** Let both species have density $n$ and charges $q=\pm e$, with $e>0$. The stipulated equal zero-field <electrical conductivity> means equal positive <charge carrier mobility> $\mu=\sigma_0/(ne)$, even if their masses and <momentum relaxation times> separately differ. Put $r=1/\sigma_0$ and $h=B/(ne)$. Inverting each <electrical resistivity> matrix gives
$$
\boldsymbol\sigma_+=\frac1{r^2+h^2}\begin{pmatrix}r&h\\-h&r\end{pmatrix},\qquad \boldsymbol\sigma_-=\frac1{r^2+h^2}\begin{pmatrix}r&-h\\h&r\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Both species experience the same <electric field>, so their <electric current> densities add. Therefore it is their **<conductivity tensors>**, rather than their <resistivity tensors>, that must be summed. The Hall parts cancel and
$$
\boldsymbol\sigma_{\rm tot}=\frac{2\sigma_0}{1+(\mu B)^2}\mathbf I_2,\qquad \boxed{\boldsymbol\rho_{\rm tot}=\frac{1+(\mu B)^2}{2\sigma_0}\mathbf I_2.}
$$
This is <compensated two-carrier magnetoresistance>. Although the net Hall field vanishes, each carrier species is bent in its own direction; maintaining the total longitudinal <electric current> requires an increasing longitudinal field. Equal carrier numbers alone do not ensure zero Hall response if <charge carrier mobilities> differ; both equalities given here are used.

**Large-field limit and quantum oscillations.** Expanding the exact diagonal entry,
$$
\rho_{xx}=\rho_{yy}=\frac1{2\sigma_0}+\frac{\sigma_0B^2}{2n^2e^2}.
$$
For $\mu B\gg1$,
$$
\boxed{\rho_{xx}\sim\rho_{yy}\sim\frac{\sigma_0B^2}{2n^2e^2},\qquad B\gg\frac{ne}{\sigma_0}=\frac{m}{e\tau_{\rm tr}}.}
$$
Here the last equality applies to either species with its own <mass> and transport time; the stipulated <charge carrier mobilities> give the same threshold. This is the classical strong-field condition $\omega_c\tau_{\rm tr}\gg1$: carriers turn substantially before their drift <momentum> relaxes.

Observable <quantum oscillations> additionally require sufficiently sharp <Landau levels> and a sufficiently cold <Fermi-Dirac distribution>, approximately
$$
\omega_c\tau_q\gtrsim1,\qquad \hbar\omega_c\gtrsim k_BT.
$$
When $\tau_q\simeq\tau_{\rm tr}$, the disorder field scale is comparable to the onset scale for large classical <magnetoresistance>. The thermal condition can demand a much larger field, and when $\tau_q\ll\tau_{\rm tr}$ even the disorder criterion is stronger. The quantitative <damping> factors in Q2(c) show that these are crossover scales rather than sharp visibility boundaries. A large smooth $B^2$ <magnetoresistance> therefore does not itself demonstrate <Landau level>; it can exist while thermal smearing suppresses oscillations. Conversely, the asymptotic condition $\omega_c\tau_{\rm tr}\gg1$ is stronger than merely reaching unity, so there is no universal ordering of the two experimental onset fields without temperatures and lifetime information.

**Scaling function.** The material's actual zero-field longitudinal <electrical resistivity> is $\rho_{\rm tot}(0)=1/(2\sigma_0)$. Dividing the exact answer by this value gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{\rho_{xx}(B)}{\rho_{xx}(0)}=1+\left(\frac{B\sigma_0}{ne}\right)^2=F(B\sigma_0),\qquad F(x)=1+\frac{x^2}{n^2e^2}.}
$$
The assumption that $n$ stays constant is essential to eliminating further temperature-dependent parameters. $B\sigma_0$ is a dimensionful scaling variable; $B\sigma_0/(ne)=\mu B$ is its dimensionless version. The corresponding fractional increase is $[\rho(B)-\rho(0)]/\rho(0)=(\mu B)^2$.

{title=Original equal-mobility two-carrier curves collapse under zero-field resistivity scaling}

**What the supplied data test.** In the original PDF's left panel, the field dependence is strongly <temperature> dependent. In the right panel, plotting the fractional <magnetoresistance> against the squared rescaled field makes the displayed **125, 150, 175, 200 and 225 K curves collapse approximately onto one straight line**, over the field intervals where they overlap. This supports the proposed one-parameter scaling and, on that range, the quadratic field law. The **100 K curve does not collapse**: it bends away and has a different slope in the rescaled plot. The indicated scaling is therefore supported for the displayed **125–225 K** data, not for the entire displayed <temperature> range. The graph alone does not locate a sharp transition between 100 and 125 K or establish behavior above 225 K.

This empirical collapse is <Kohler's rule>. It is consistent with a common temperature-dependent <charge carrier mobility> or relaxation scale and a fixed carrier population; it is not a unique proof of exact compensation or of one microscopic scattering mechanism. A change in relative scattering times, carrier population or Fermi-surface structure could spoil the collapse, and this figure alone does not select the cause of the 100 K departure.

There is a factor-of-two normalization convention in the printed text. The $\sigma_0$ introduced for **each** species gives $\rho_{\rm tot}(0)=1/(2\sigma_0)$, whereas the final paragraph calls $1/\sigma_0$ “$\rho_0$.” If $\rho_0$ denotes the experimentally measured zero-field <electrical resistivity>, the consistent slope is
$$
\frac{\rho(B)-\rho_0}{\rho_0}=\frac1{4n^2e^2}\left(\frac B{\rho_0}\right)^2,\qquad \rho_0=\rho_{\rm tot}(0).
$$
If one instead keeps the literal single-species definition $\rho_s=1/\sigma_0$, the same model has $[\rho(B)-\rho_s]/\rho_s=-1/2+[B/\rho_s]^2/(2n^2e^2)$. The choice rescales and offsets the axes by temperature-independent constants, so it does not change which <temperature> curves collapse, but it must be resolved before inferring a <carrier density> from the slope.
