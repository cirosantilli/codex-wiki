= Solution
{parent=a}

Let the nearest-neighbour spacing be $d$, so the <primitive unit cell> has length $\ell=2d$. Denote the two longitudinal displacements in cell $n$ by $u_n,v_n$, with spring $K$ within each cell and spring $K'$ between cells. <Hooke's law> and <Newton's second law> give
$$
\boxed{m\ddot u_n=K(v_n-u_n)+K'(v_{n-1}-u_n),\qquad
m\ddot v_n=K(u_n-v_n)+K'(u_{n+1}-v_n).}
$$
For <normal modes> $u_n=u e^{i(qn\ell-\omega t)}$ and $v_n=v e^{i(qn\ell-\omega t)}$, the two amplitudes satisfy
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
K+K'-m\omega^2&-(K+K'e^{-iq\ell})\\
-(K+K'e^{iq\ell})&K+K'-m\omega^2
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}=0.
$$
Its vanishing <determinant> gives the <alternating-spring chain dispersion relation>
$$
\boxed{\omega_\pm^2(q)=\frac{K+K'\pm\sqrt{K^2+K'^2+2KK'\cos(q\ell)}}m,\qquad |q|\leq\frac\pi\ell.}
$$
The minus sign is the <acoustic phonon branch>, with $\omega_-(0)=0$ and $u=v$ at the origin. The plus sign is the <optical phonon branch>, with $u=-v$ at the origin. For positive springs the <acoustic phonon branch> rises from zero to $\sqrt{2\min(K,K')/m}$ at the <Brillouin zone> edge; the <optical phonon branch> falls from $\sqrt{2(K+K')/m}$ to $\sqrt{2\max(K,K')/m}$. Their long-wavelength limits are
$$
\omega_-(q)=\ell\sqrt{\frac{KK'}{2m(K+K')}}|q|+O(|q|^3),\qquad
\boxed{\omega_+(0)=\sqrt{\frac{2(K+K')}{m}}\simeq\sqrt{\frac{2K}{m}}\left(1+\frac{K'}{2K}\right).}
$$
Thus the leading frequency in the <strong-bond limit of the alternating-spring chain> is $\sqrt{2K/m}$, the internal stretching frequency of a pair with reduced mass $m/2$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

A positive gate voltage attracts <electrons> towards the oxide interface of the <p-type semiconductor> and repels mobile <electron holes>. The uncovered ionized acceptors form a negatively charged <depletion region>. Increasing the voltage further creates a thin electron-rich <semiconductor inversion layer> at the surface. The <conduction band> and <valence band> both bend downwards towards the positively biased gate, because an electron's electrostatic energy is $-e\psi$.

In an ideal nondegenerate substrate with acceptor density $N_A$, write $\phi_F=(k_BT/e)\ln(N_A/n_i)>0$ and surface potential $\psi_s$ relative to the neutral bulk. The electron and hole surface densities are
$$
n_s=\frac{n_i^2}{N_A}e^{e\psi_s/(k_BT)},\qquad p_s=N_Ae^{-e\psi_s/(k_BT)}.
$$
The first change of majority-carrier type occurs at $\psi_s=\phi_F$, where $n_s=p_s=n_i$. The conventional <MOS inversion threshold> for appreciable strong inversion is
$$
\boxed{\psi_s\simeq2\phi_F,\qquad n_s\simeq N_A.}
$$
At threshold the inversion sheet is just developing, while a much thicker <depletion region> lies between it and the neutral p-type bulk. Inversion does not require the <conduction band> to cross the <Fermi level>: the surface can remain nondegenerate.

\Image[2018-q1b-mos-inversion.png]
{title=Downward band bending and an incipient electron inversion layer in a MOS structure}
{description=Original schematic with a positively biased metal gate, insulating oxide, a thin surface inversion region, and a deeper depletion region. The semiconductor band gap is constant. The separate gate and substrate Fermi levels illustrate applied bias; band offsets and widths are schematic.}

**The metal and semiconductor Fermi levels need not be equal.** The ideal oxide blocks particle exchange, so the gate and substrate can maintain different <electrochemical potentials>. In a biased <MOSFET>, the <gate-to-substrate Fermi-level difference> is $\widetilde\mu_g-\widetilde\mu_s=-eV_g$, when the applied voltage is defined in the electron-reservoir convention. Each region can separately be in internal equilibrium. If an external zero-voltage connection instead equilibrates the reservoirs, their Fermi levels coincide, but different <work functions> can still cause <band bending>. Here the chemical potential shown in a band diagram is the electron electrochemical potential; the purely chemical contribution alone is not the equilibrium criterion for charged particles.

= Solution

For a single carrier species with positive <charge carrier mobility> $\mu$, the <Hall coefficient> and longitudinal <electrical conductivity> are
$$
R_H=\frac1{nq},\qquad \sigma=n|q|\mu.
$$
Take the <electric current density> along $x$, magnetic field along $z$, and no transverse current. Then $E_y=R_Hj_xB$ and $j_x=\sigma E_x$, giving the <Hall angle> relation
$$
\boxed{\frac{E_y}{E_x}=R_H\sigma B=\operatorname{sgn}(q)\mu B=-\mu B\quad(q=-e).}
$$
The sign specifies the electron Hall polarity; the ratio of field magnitudes is $|E_H|/|E|=\mu|B|$. Converting the stated <electron mobility> to <SI> gives $\mu=900\times10^{-4}=0.0900\,\mathrm{m^2\,V^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{|B|=\frac{0.015}{0.0900}=0.167\,\mathrm T.}
$$
This uses the homogeneous single-carrier <Drude model>; the carrier density cancels.

= Solution
{parent=B2}

**Equilibrium junction.** Put the p-type side on the left and n-type side on the right. When they are joined, <electrons> diffuse from n to p and <electron holes> diffuse from p to n. This leaves negative ionized acceptors on the p-side and positive ionized donors on the n-side of the <depletion region>. The resulting built-in <electric field> points from n to p, opposing further diffusion. At equilibrium there is one constant <Fermi level>, and drift and <diffusion currents> cancel for each carrier species.

In the nondegenerate, fully ionized limit, the neutral-region <carrier densities> are
$$
\begin{array}{c|cc}
&\text{p side}&\text{n side}\\ \hline
\text{holes }p&N_A\quad\text{majority}&n_i^2/N_D\quad\text{minority}\\
\text{electrons }n&n_i^2/N_A\quad\text{minority}&N_D\quad\text{majority}.
\end{array}
$$
The <conduction band> and <valence band> lie higher on the p-side and bend down towards the n-side. The gap between them stays fixed for a homojunction. If $\psi$ increases towards the n-side, both edges shift by $-e\psi$, while
$$
n=N_Ce^{-(E_C-E_F)/(k_BT)},\qquad p=N_Ve^{-(E_F-E_V)/(k_BT)},\qquad np=n_i^2.
$$
Thus $n$ rises and $p$ falls continuously across the junction; neither mobile population is an exact step function. Their depletion exposes fixed dopant <charge density>, rather than an absence of every kind of charge. The built-in voltage is
$$
\boxed{V_{\rm bi}=\frac{k_BT}{e}\ln\frac{N_AN_D}{n_i^2}.}
$$

\Image[2018-b2-equilibrium-junction.png]
{title=Equilibrium band bending and carrier concentrations across a p-n homojunction}
{description=Original symmetric illustrative model, with the p-region on the left. A constant Fermi level crosses neither band; log-scale carrier profiles satisfy $np=n_i^2$ and exchange majority and minority roles. The profile is schematic rather than a numerical Poisson solution.}

**Light emission.** Forward bias lowers the barrier and injects <minority carriers> across the <p-n junction>. In a <light-emitting diode>, <electron-hole recombination> converts some of their energy into <photons>. A <direct band gap> permits efficient radiative transitions with nearly conserved <crystal momentum>; nonradiative channels compete. Near-edge emission has $\hbar\omega\simeq E_g$ and $\lambda\simeq hc/E_g$. Material composition and confinement select the emission colour. Reverse-bias saturation is not the ordinary LED operating mode.

**Reverse current.** Take $V=V_R>0$ and $I_R>0$ to mean reverse voltage and reverse current magnitudes. The <Shockley diode equation> in this convention is
$$
I_R=I_S\left(1-e^{-eV_R/(k_BT)}\right).
$$
The bias-independent $I_S$ term is the supply of thermally excited <minority carriers> from the neutral regions, swept across the junction by its <electric field>. The opposing injection current is reduced by the extra barrier energy $eV_R$ and is $I_S e^{-eV_R/(k_BT)}$. Their equality gives zero net current at zero bias; for $eV_R\gg k_BT$, the reverse current approaches $I_S$ until leakage or breakdown invalidates this ideal law. The collecting field need not create the minority carriers; their thermal supply limits the diffusion-dominated current.

For an ideal long junction of area $\mathcal A$, steady diffusion with recombination gives the <diode minority-carrier diffusion current>
$$
\boxed{I_S=e\mathcal A\left(\frac{D_nn_i^2}{L_nN_A}+\frac{D_pn_i^2}{L_pN_D}\right),\qquad L_n=\sqrt{D_n\tau_n},\quad L_p=\sqrt{D_p\tau_p}.}
$$
It depends on doping, geometry, carrier <diffusion coefficients> and lifetimes, and especially the <intrinsic carrier concentration>. <Depletion-region generation current> and surface leakage can add nonideal contributions to the measured reverse current.

**Band-gap estimate from the data.** Read a nearly flat region well above the small-bias knee and below the high-bias upturn, rather than estimating $I_S$ in the breakdown region. For a diffusion-dominated junction with nearly temperature-independent gap and effective masses,
$$
n_i^2=N_CN_Ve^{-E_g/(k_BT)},\qquad N_CN_V\propto T^3,
$$
so $I_S=C(T)T^3e^{-E_g/(k_BT)}$, where $C(T)$ includes transport and doping factors. <Extracting a semiconductor band gap from diode saturation current> amounts to measuring the slope of $\ln[I_S/(C(T)T^3)]$ against $1/T$: it is $-E_g/k_B$. If the prefactor is taken approximately constant over a short interval,
$$
E_g\simeq k_B\frac{\ln[I_S(T_h)/I_S(T_l)]}{1/T_l-1/T_h}.
$$
Approximate readings from the original PDF plateaus are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
T\ ({}^\circ\mathrm C)&T\ (\mathrm K)&I_S\ (\mathrm A)\\\hline
30&303.15&2.5\times10^{-6}\\
0&273.15&1.7\times10^{-7}\\
-45&228.15&\text{roughly }1.2\times10^{-8}.
\end{array}
$$
Using the two warmer curves and $k_B=8.6173\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{eV/K}$ gives
$$
E_g^{\rm app}=k_B\frac{\ln(2.5\times10^{-6}/1.7\times10^{-7})}{1/273.15-1/303.15}
\simeq0.64\,\mathrm{eV}.
$$
Keeping the $T^3$ factor but taking the residual transport factor constant changes this estimate to approximately $0.57\,\mathrm{eV}$. The justified graphical estimate is therefore
$$
\boxed{E_g\ \text{of order }0.6\,\mathrm{eV}\quad\text{from the two warmer curves}.}
$$
The colder curve is a genuine limitation of the supplied data: the $0$ to $-45\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ pair gives only about $0.32\,\mathrm{eV}$ with a constant prefactor, and the endpoint pair gives about $0.42\,\mathrm{eV}$. Thus all three cannot support one precise diffusion-only activation slope. A <depletion-region generation current>, approximately proportional to $n_i$ rather than $n_i^2$, can have a roughly half-gap activation energy; other leakage and temperature-dependent transport can also alter the slope. The PDF alone does not identify their relative contributions. A reliable precision gap measurement would use more temperatures and verify the transport regime instead of forcing one fit through these three readings.

= Solution
{parent=B3}

**Two atomic orbitals.** In the <linear combination of atomic orbitals> approximation, use $|\psi\rangle=c_A|A\rangle+c_B|B\rangle$, where the two basis functions are localized <atomic orbitals>. Projecting $\widehat H|\psi\rangle=E|\psi\rangle$ onto each orbital gives the <generalized eigenvalue problem>
$$
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}H_{AA}&H_{AB}\\H_{BA}&H_{BB}\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}c_A\\c_B\end{pmatrix}
=E\begin{pmatrix}1&S\\S^*&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_A\\c_B\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\det(H-ES)=0.}
$$
Here the scalar overlap in the matrix is $S=\langle A|B\rangle$, while $H_{ab}=\langle a|\widehat H|b\rangle$. Normalization requires $c^\dagger S c=1$, with $S$ now denoting the full overlap matrix. For equivalent real orbitals with diagonal energy $E_a$, real hopping $t$, and real overlap $s$, the eigenstates and energies are
$$
|\psi_\pm\rangle=\frac{|A\rangle\pm|B\rangle}{\sqrt{2(1\pm s)}},\qquad
E_\pm=\frac{E_a\pm t}{1\pm s}.
$$
For the usual negative hopping convention, the symmetric combination is the lower <bonding orbital> and the antisymmetric combination is the higher <antibonding orbital>. In an orthonormal approximation, $s=0$ and the result reduces to a two-by-two <Hermitian matrix> eigenproblem.

**Hybridized bands.** At each $\mathbf k$, take the two Bloch basis states to be orthonormal, as implicit in the stated band model, and write $\epsilon_k=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$. The <Hamiltonian> is
$$
H(\mathbf k)=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_k&V\\V^*&E_d\end{pmatrix}.
$$
For $|\psi_{\mathbf k}\rangle=a_{\mathbf k}|\phi_{\mathbf k}\rangle+b_{\mathbf k}|\chi_{\mathbf k}\rangle$, the <eigenvalue> condition is
$$
(\epsilon_k-E)a+Vb=0,\qquad V^*a+(E_d-E)b=0,
$$
so $(E-\epsilon_k)(E-E_d)-|V|^2=0$. Thus <band hybridization> gives
$$
\boxed{E_\pm(k)=\frac{\epsilon_k+E_d}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{(\epsilon_k-E_d)^2}{4}+|V|^2}.}
$$
For nonzero $V$, a convenient explicit normalized pair of <eigenstates> is
$$
\boxed{|\psi_{\mathbf k,\pm}\rangle=
\frac{V|\phi_{\mathbf k}\rangle+(E_\pm-\epsilon_k)|\chi_{\mathbf k}\rangle}
{\sqrt{|V|^2+(E_\pm-\epsilon_k)^2}}.}
$$
A phase convention can make $V$ real, but the eigenvalues depend only on its modulus. These are exact within the truncated two-band model, and approximate as a description of a material.

**Dispersions and limits.** For $V=0$, the parabola crosses the <flat band> at
$$
k_0=\sqrt{2mE_d}/\hbar.
$$
For $V\ne0$ the crossing is avoided, with a direct <hybridization gap> $2|V|$ at $k_0$. The lower and upper branches exchange orbital character through this <avoided crossing>. At the origin their energies are
$$
E_\pm(0)=\frac{E_d\pm\sqrt{E_d^2+4|V|^2}}2,
$$
so for $|V|\ll E_d$ the small-$k$ expansions are
$$
E_-(k)=-\frac{|V|^2}{E_d}+\left(1-\frac{|V|^2}{E_d^2}\right)\epsilon_k+O(|V|^4/E_d^3,|V|^2\epsilon_k^2/E_d^3),
$$
$$
E_+(k)=E_d+\frac{|V|^2}{E_d}+\frac{|V|^2}{E_d^2}\epsilon_k+O(|V|^4/E_d^3,|V|^2\epsilon_k^2/E_d^3).
$$
At large $k$, where $\epsilon_k-E_d\gg|V|$,
$$
\boxed{E_-(k)=E_d-\frac{|V|^2}{\epsilon_k-E_d}+O\!\left(\frac{|V|^4}{(\epsilon_k-E_d)^3}\right),\qquad
E_+(k)=\epsilon_k+\frac{|V|^2}{\epsilon_k-E_d}+O\!\left(\frac{|V|^4}{(\epsilon_k-E_d)^3}\right).}
$$
The lower branch approaches $E_d$ from below and becomes flat; the upper branch approaches the free-electron parabola from above. At zero coupling the unsorted orbital bands remain exactly flat and parabolic. Sorting them by energy instead gives $E_-=\min(\epsilon_k,E_d)$ and $E_+=\max(\epsilon_k,E_d)$, which cross at $k_0$ without mixing.

\Image[2018-b3-hybridized-bands.png]
{title=Avoided crossing and Fermi-volume redistribution in a parabolic-flat two-band model}
{description=Original model curves for zero coupling and $|V|/E_d=0.12$. Wavevectors are in units of $k_0=\sqrt{2mE_d}/\hbar$. The lower-band Fermi radius $2^{1/3}k_0$ and its chemical potential follow fixed total density under the stated equal-population, zero-temperature assumption.}

**Fermi wavevector and velocity.** A partially occupied perfectly <flat band> has chemical potential $E_d$. Thus before mixing, the occupied broad band has radius $k_0$ defined above. For spin degeneracy two, its particle density is $n_1=k_0^3/(3\pi^2)$; equal population of the flat sector gives total density $n=2n_1$.

For any nonzero mixing, $E_-(k)<E_d$ at finite $k$, while $E_+(k)>E_d$. Moreover $E_-(k)$ increases monotonically. At zero temperature and this fixed density, a single occupied sphere in the lower branch therefore accommodates all particles, and the upper branch is empty. <Fermi-volume conservation in two hybridized electron bands> gives
$$
\frac{k_F^3}{3\pi^2}=\frac{2k_0^3}{3\pi^2},\qquad
\boxed{k_F=2^{1/3}k_0,\qquad \Delta k_F=(2^{1/3}-1)k_0\simeq0.260k_0.}
$$
The new radius is about $26\%$ larger than the old broad-band radius. The strictly flat uncoupled band has no uniquely determined Fermi radius of its own; its prescribed population is what enters the count.

The radial <group velocity> is $v_-(k)=\hbar^{-1}dE_-/dk$. Differentiating the exact dispersion gives
$$
\frac{dE_-}{d\epsilon_k}=\frac12\left[1-\frac{\epsilon_k-E_d}{\sqrt{(\epsilon_k-E_d)^2+4|V|^2}}\right].
$$
At $k_F$, put $\delta=2^{2/3}-1$, so $\epsilon_{k_F}-E_d=\delta E_d$. Relative to the original broad-band <Fermi velocity> $v_F^{(1)}=\hbar k_0/m$,
$$
\frac{v_F}{v_F^{(1)}}=\frac{2^{1/3}}2\left[1-\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{\delta^2+4|V|^2/E_d^2}}\right].
$$
Expanding for $|V|\ll E_d$ gives the <flat-band hybridization Fermi-velocity suppression>
$$
\boxed{\frac{v_F}{v_F^{(1)}}=\frac{2^{1/3}}{(2^{2/3}-1)^2}\frac{|V|^2}{E_d^2}+O\!\left(\frac{|V|^4}{E_d^4}\right).}
$$
The coefficient is about $3.65$. The enlarged occupied volume coexists with a small velocity because the new <Fermi surface> lies on the almost-flat part of the lower branch. This result assumes fixed particle density, sufficient available states before any <Brillouin zone> boundary, and temperature low enough to resolve energy differences of order $|V|^2/E_d$. The limit $V\to0$ at a fixed nonzero temperature need not give this sharp redistribution. Giving the narrow band a finite dispersion can also leave both pockets occupied, so the result is specific to the ideal model.

= Solution
{parent=B4}

**Energy and momentum measurement.** In <angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy>, monochromatic <photons> illuminate a clean surface in vacuum, and an <electron energy analyzer> measures the outgoing kinetic energies and emission directions. Measuring both gives the occupied <electronic band structure>, rather than only a momentum-integrated distribution of removal energies.

Choose a common energy reference for initial and final states. Let $E_i$ be the initial electron energy, $E_F$ the <Fermi energy>, and $E_{\rm vac}=E_F+\phi$ the vacuum level immediately outside the sample. The <binding energy> is $E_B=E_F-E_i\geq0$. For a direct emission event without additional losses, <conservation of energy> gives
$$
E_f=E_i+\hbar\omega=E_{\rm vac}+E_{\rm kin},\qquad
\boxed{E_{\rm kin}=\hbar\omega-\phi-E_B.}
$$
Here the <work function> $\phi$ is the energy from the Fermi level to the local vacuum. The initial and final energies are total single-electron energies on the same scale, while $E_{\rm kin}$ is measured above vacuum. The diagram illustrates each interval independently.

Neglecting ultraviolet photon momentum, <photoemission momentum conversion> gives
$$
\boxed{k_\parallel=\frac{\sqrt{2m_eE_{\rm kin}}}{\hbar}\sin\theta,}
$$
where $\theta$ is measured from the surface normal. Parallel <crystal momentum> is conserved modulo a surface <reciprocal lattice> vector; the surface breaks perpendicular translation symmetry. A three-dimensional sample therefore generally needs a final-state model to infer perpendicular momentum. The stated two-dimensional sheet avoids that ambiguity. The kinetic energy used in this formula is the sample-vacuum value; if the analyzer and sample have different work functions, their contact potential must be included when converting the analyzer's reading, as in <photoelectron binding energy reference>.

**Spectral information.** Define $\epsilon=E_i-E_F=-E_B$ and the electron <spectral function> by $A(\mathbf k,\epsilon)=-\operatorname{Im}G^R(\mathbf k,\epsilon)/\pi$. In the <sudden approximation>, the basic <photoemission intensity and spectral function> relation is
$$
\boxed{I(\mathbf k,\epsilon)=C|\mathcal M(\mathbf k,\hbar\omega,\text{polarization})|^2f(\epsilon)A(\mathbf k,\epsilon),}
$$
before instrumental broadening and background are included. Here $f$ is the <Fermi-Dirac distribution>, and $\mathcal M$ is the transition <matrix element>. The <electron removal spectral function> contains the occupied part of the spectrum. In a noninteracting band it has a delta peak at the band energy; interactions shift and broaden peaks and can add incoherent weight. Thus peak trajectories reveal dispersion, while line shapes can constrain lifetimes after resolution effects are accounted for. Dividing by known matrix and occupation factors can estimate $A$ where those factors are appreciable; intensity alone is not identical to $A$, and a missing peak can reflect a matrix-element suppression. States well above $E_F$ are not accessible to ordinary low-temperature photoemission.

**Two independent band-structure probes.** First, the <de Haas-van Alphen effect> measures oscillatory <magnetization> as <Landau levels> cross the <Fermi level> when a magnetic field changes. The inverse-field frequency is
$$
F=\frac{\hbar}{2\pi e}S_{\rm ext},\qquad \Delta(1/B)=\frac1F,
$$
where $S_{\rm ext}$ is an extremal <Fermi surface> cross-sectional area perpendicular to the field. Changing field orientation maps its shape. This reveals the momentum-space geometry of occupied bands; the related <Shubnikov-de Haas effect> measures oscillatory resistance.

Second, absorption or reflectance spectroscopy detects interband transitions. Photon energies at absorption edges and peaks constrain the <band gap> and interband energy differences. For a <direct band gap>, negligible photon momentum selects approximately vertical transitions in the band diagram, weighted by the <joint density of states> and transition <matrix elements>. An indirect transition needs a <phonon> to carry the extra <crystal momentum>. These measurements provide evidence beyond a free-electron parabola, although an optical spectrum alone does not reconstruct every band's dispersion.

**Reading the graphite spectra.** For each direction, follow a moving peak through successive angles and convert each peak's own kinetic energy to $k_\parallel$; angle is not itself proportional to momentum over this wide range. The prominent dispersing valence feature starts at roughly $7$–$9\,\mathrm{eV}$ binding energy near $\Gamma$ and moves towards lower binding energy as $k_\parallel$ increases. Along $\Gamma K$ it approaches the <Fermi level> at the corner, then turns back to larger binding energy on continuing beyond the corner. Along $\Gamma M$ it reaches a finite minimum binding energy of roughly $3$–$4\,\mathrm{eV}$ near the edge midpoint and turns back; it does not reach the Fermi level there. Additional deeper peaks describe other occupied valence branches, with visibility varying across the spectra.

A useful qualitative description is the upper occupied $\pi$ band of the <nearest-neighbour model of graphene>: $E_\pi=-t|f(\mathbf k)|$. It rises from $-3t$ at $\Gamma$ to zero at $K$, but only to $-t$ at $M$. The schematic curve in the figure captures those two different behaviours. It is not a full multi-orbital assignment or a quantitative fit to graphite, whose deeper valence features are also visible in the supplied spectra.

**Angle needed to reach the corner.** The corner's parallel wavevector is
$$
k_K=\frac{4\pi}{3a}=\frac{4\pi}{3(0.246\,\mathrm{nm})}=17.03\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}=1.703\,\mathrm{\mathring A^{-1}}.
$$
Use the near-zero binding energy of the upper valence band at that point. With the sample <work function> interpreted as the stated $3.8\,\mathrm{eV}$ separation below vacuum,
$$
E_{\rm kin}(K)\simeq19.5-3.8=15.7\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad
\frac{\sqrt{2m_eE_{\rm kin}}}{\hbar}=20.30\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\theta_K\simeq\sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{17.03}{20.30}\right)=57.0^\circ.}
$$
The energy and angle readings in the PDF are approximate. If a peak at the corner were instead assigned a small binding energy $E_B$, the estimate becomes
$$
\sin\theta_K=\frac{\hbar k_K}{\sqrt{2m_e(15.7\,\mathrm{eV}-E_B)}}.
$$
For $E_B=0$–$1\,\mathrm{eV}$ this gives about $57$–$60^\circ$, consistent with the near-Fermi feature in that angular range. Substituting the full photon energy for the kinetic energy would omit the work-function cost and underestimate the required angle.
