= Solution

A positive <electric potential difference> between gate and substrate attracts <electrons> towards the insulating interface and repels the mobile <electron holes> of the <p-type semiconductor> substrate. The exposed, negatively charged <acceptor impurities> form a <depletion region>; the thin mobile <electron> layer at its surface is the <semiconductor inversion layer>. The <conduction band> and <valence band> bend *downwards* towards the positively biased gate, because the <electron> <potential energy> is $-e\psi$.

\Image[2012-mos-inversion.png]
{title=<Band bending> and charge separation at the conventional <MOS inversion threshold>}

To specify the conventional <MOS inversion threshold>, let $N_A$ be the ionized acceptor density, $n_i$ the <intrinsic carrier concentration>, and
$$
\phi_F=\frac{k_BT}{e}\log\frac{N_A}{n_i}>0.
$$
In the neutral bulk the intrinsic level lies $e\phi_F$ above the <semiconductor> <chemical potential>. A positive surface potential $\psi_s$ lowers both band edges by $e\psi_s$, giving, in the nondegenerate approximation,
$$
p_s=N_Ae^{-e\psi_s/(k_BT)},\qquad
n_s=\frac{n_i^2}{N_A}e^{e\psi_s/(k_BT)}.
$$
At $\psi_s=\phi_F$, the surface is intrinsic: $n_s=p_s=n_i$. The usual onset of *strong inversion* instead means
$$
\boxed{\psi_s\simeq2\phi_F,\qquad n_s\simeq N_A.}
$$
Thus the surface <electron density> has grown to the bulk majority-carrier density. The <chemical potential> need not actually enter the <conduction band>. The phrase “just forms” can also refer to the earlier weak-inversion crossover; the diagram uses the conventional strong-inversion threshold and labels it explicitly.

Within the <depletion approximation> the negative <space charge> is approximately $-eN_A$ over a width
$$
W_d=\sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon_s\psi_s}{eN_A}},\qquad
W_{d,\mathrm{th}}=\sqrt{\frac{4\epsilon_s\phi_F}{eN_A}},
$$
where $\epsilon_s$ is the absolute <semiconductor> <permittivity>. Its outer edge is where the bands become flat and mobile <electron holes> recover their bulk density. The <electrical insulator> blocks steady carrier transport while sustaining an <electric field>; the <metal> gate supplies positive surface charge balancing depletion and inversion charge. Under gate bias, the gate and substrate <electrochemical potentials> are different, so a single equilibrium <Fermi level> should not be drawn through the whole biased stack.

For a <hydrogenic donor state>, <dielectric screening> replaces $\epsilon_0$ by $\epsilon_r\epsilon_0$ and the <electron mass> by the <band effective mass>. The characteristic orbital radius is therefore
$$
a^*=\frac{4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r\hbar^2}{m^*e^2}
=\epsilon_r\frac{m_e}{m^*}a_0.
$$
Using $a_0=0.0529177\,\mathrm{nm}$ gives
$$
\boxed{a^*=120a_0=6.35\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
A <donor-state overlap estimate> compares this radius with the mean dopant spacing $d\sim N_D^{-1/3}$. Taking $d\sim a^*$ yields
$$
\boxed{N_D\sim(a^*)^{-3}=3.91\times10^{24}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}
=3.91\times10^{18}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}.}
$$
There is no sharp geometrical onset: hydrogenic <wavefunctions> have exponential tails. If “overlap” is defined by neighbouring orbital *diameters* touching, $d\sim2a^*$, the corresponding estimate is $4.88\times10^{23}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$, smaller by a factor of eight. These are conventions for the onset of appreciable <orbital overlap>, not an exact critical density for a <metal-insulator transition>.

The <band effective mass> is determined by the <second derivative> of the <dispersion relation>:
$$
\frac1{m^*(k)}=\frac1{\hbar^2}\frac{d^2E}{dk^2}.
$$
With the stated sign convention and $t>0$, the band minimum is at $k=0$. Expanding the <cosine>, or differentiating twice, gives
$$
E(k)=E_0-2t+ta^2k^2+O(k^4),\qquad
\left.\frac{d^2E}{dk^2}\right|_0=2ta^2.
$$
Comparison with a free <electron>'s <kinetic energy> $\hbar^2k^2/(2m_e)$ therefore requires
$$
\boxed{t=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_ea^2}
=6.78\times10^{-20}\,\mathrm J
=0.423\,\mathrm{eV}.}
$$
The <hopping integral> has units of <energy>. A negative $t$ would move the minimum to the edge of the <Brillouin zone> and give the same positive bottom-of-band <effective mass> with $|t|$ in this expression.

A <solar cell> uses the <photovoltaic effect> to turn <photon> <energy> into electrical work. In a <semiconductor>, above-gap <photon absorption> generates an <electron> in the <conduction band> and an <electron hole> in the <valence band>. A <p-n junction>, together with selective electrical contacts, collects the two carrier species at opposite terminals. Carrier <diffusion> and the built-in <electric field> help separate them before <electron-hole recombination>. Illumination maintains different <quasi-Fermi levels> for <electrons> and <electron holes>; their splitting, divided by $e$, sets the available terminal voltage in an ideal device. The equilibrium <built-in potential> alone cannot continuously deliver work.

For an ideal junction, the illuminated output <current density> is
$$
J(V)=J_{\rm sc}-J_0\left(e^{eV/(k_BT)}-1\right),\qquad
V_{\rm oc}=\frac{k_BT}{e}\log\left(1+\frac{J_{\rm sc}}{J_0}\right).
$$
Here $J_{\rm sc}$ is the photogenerated short-circuit <current density> and $J_0$ the <diode saturation current>. The useful operating point maximizes $VJ$, rather than either current or voltage separately. With <solar-cell fill factor> $\mathrm{FF}=P_{\max}/(V_{\rm oc}I_{\rm sc})$,
$$
\eta=\frac{P_{\max}}{P_{\rm in}}
=\frac{V_{\rm oc}I_{\rm sc}\mathrm{FF}}{P_{\rm in}}.
$$
Series resistance, shunts, incomplete <photon absorption> and <nonradiative recombination> reduce the attainable efficiency.

\Image[2012-solar-cell-output.png]
{title=Ideal illuminated <solar cell>: current, delivered power and the maximum-power point}

The <Shockley-Queisser limit> removes these avoidable losses but retains the following physical constraints. There is one <semiconductor> junction with one <band gap>; each absorbed above-gap <photon> yields at most one collected <electron-hole pair>; carriers thermalize to the cell <temperature>; and the inverse optical process produces unavoidable <radiative recombination>. If $\Phi_{\odot}(E)$ is the incident <photon> flux per unit <energy>, ideal <photon absorption> gives
$$
J_{\rm sc}=e\int_{E_g}^{\infty}\Phi_{\odot}(E)\,dE.
$$
The same ability to absorb <light> requires the junction to emit it. At a forward voltage $V$, the emitted <photon> population has a <chemical potential> $eV$; in the ordinary nondegenerate limit the resulting recombination current is the exponential diode term above. Maximizing $VJ$ with this radiative $J_0$, and then choosing $E_g$, gives the limiting efficiency.

A small <band gap> collects many <photons> but gives a low voltage and large thermalization losses. A large <band gap> permits a larger voltage but transmits too much of the incident spectrum. Below-gap <photons> are lost to transmission, excess <photon> <energy> above the gap becomes heat, and radiative emission and its associated entropy loss keep $eV_{\rm oc}$ below $E_g$. **The optimum single-junction conversion is roughly thirty percent under ordinary unconcentrated sunlight; the precise ceiling depends on spectrum, cell <temperature> and optical geometry.** The original detailed-balance calculation gave about $30\%$ at $E_g\simeq1.1\,\mathrm{eV}$ under its specified assumptions.https://doi.org/10.1063/1.1736034[Detailed Balance Limit of Efficiency of p-n Junction Solar Cells, Journal of Applied Physics 32 (1961), 510–519; abstract and calculation]{ref} Multiple junctions and optical concentration change the assumptions and consequently change the bound.

= Solution

Different measurements reveal different aspects of <electronic band structure>; a convincing determination combines their constraints.

**Angle-resolved photoemission.** In <angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy>, <photons> of known <energy> eject occupied <electrons>. Measuring their <kinetic energy> and emission angle reconstructs <binding energy> and the component of <crystal momentum> parallel to the surface:
$$
E_B=h\nu-\Phi-E_{\rm kin},\qquad
k_\parallel=\frac{\sqrt{2m_eE_{\rm kin}}}{\hbar}\sin\theta.
$$
Here $\Phi$ is the sample <work function> when <kinetic energy> is referenced to the nearby <vacuum>; an analyzer <energy> scale requires its own work-function calibration. <Momentum> is conserved modulo a surface <reciprocal lattice> vector, neglecting <photon> <momentum>. Tracking the peaks maps occupied <dispersion relations> and the <Fermi surface>. The measured intensity also contains the <matrix element>, the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> and the <spectral function>, so an absent bright feature does not establish an absent band. For a three-dimensional solid, the perpendicular <momentum> requires a final-state model. <Inverse photoemission spectroscopy> probes unoccupied states by injecting <electrons> and detecting emitted <photons>.

**Optical <spectroscopy>.** <Photon absorption> and <ellipsometry> measure <interband transitions>, optical <selection rules> and the complex dielectric response. Because optical <photon> <momentum> is small on the scale of the <Brillouin zone>, direct transitions approximately preserve <crystal momentum>. Their onset constrains a <direct band gap>; a threshold involving a <phonon> can reveal an <indirect band gap>. Peak positions constrain <energy> differences between bands, but the response also depends on the <joint density of states> and optical <matrix elements>, so it does not uniquely determine every $E_n(\mathbf k)$.

**Quantum <oscillations> and <cyclotron resonance>.** In clean samples at low <temperature>, the <de Haas-van Alphen effect> measures <oscillations> in <magnetization> and the <Shubnikov-de Haas effect> measures <oscillations> in <electrical resistivity> as the inverse <magnetic field> is varied. Their period in inverse <magnetic field> is $1/F$, where <Onsager quantization> relates $F$ to an extremal Fermi-surface area perpendicular to the field:
$$
F=\frac{\hbar}{2\pi e}A_{\rm ext}.
$$
Rotating the <magnetic field> maps the geometry of extremal orbits. The thermal <damping> determines the <cyclotron effective mass>,
$$
m_c=\frac{\hbar^2}{2\pi}\frac{\partial A}{\partial E},
$$
with the appropriate magnitude for a hole orbit. <Cyclotron resonance> supplies the corresponding dynamical mass through $\omega_c=eB/m_c$. These are detailed probes near the <Fermi level>, rather than measurements of all occupied bands. <Diffraction> supplies the <reciprocal lattice> needed to label the measured momenta, but <diffraction> by itself is not a measurement of electronic dispersion.

= Solution

In a weak applied field, <magnetic susceptibility> relates <magnetization> to <magnetic field intensity>, $M=\chi H$. Its sign, <temperature> dependence and nonlinear field dependence distinguish several mechanisms.

**<Diamagnetism>** is the orbital response opposing an applied <magnetic field>, with $\chi<0$. Closed-shell atoms and filled bands generally give a weak, nearly temperature-independent response. In a simple free <electron> <metal>, <Landau diamagnetism> contributes $\chi_L=-\chi_P/3$; the factor is specific to the isotropic parabolic-band model and is not universal in crystals.

**<Paramagnetism>** gives $\chi>0$. Localized <magnetic moments> align statistically with the field against thermal disorder. For independent moments of <angular momentum> $J$,
$$
\chi=\frac{\mu_0n(g\mu_B)^2J(J+1)}{3k_BT},
$$
which is the <Curie law>. Exchange interactions often lead approximately to the <Curie-Weiss law>, $\chi=C/(T-\Theta)$, sufficiently far above any ordering <temperature>. The sign of $\Theta$ suggests predominantly parallel or antiparallel exchange, though it is not a complete classification. Conduction <electrons> instead exhibit <Pauli paramagnetism>:
$$
\chi_P=\mu_0\mu_B^2g(E_F),
$$
for $g\simeq2$ and a <spin-summed density of states> per unit volume. Only states close to the <Fermi energy> change occupation, making this response nearly <temperature> independent when $k_BT\ll E_F$.

**<Ferromagnetism>** has spontaneous parallel magnetic order below a <Curie temperature>. <Exchange interaction>, not simply classical dipole attraction, provides the dominant alignment mechanism. In the convention $H=-J_{\rm ex}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_j$, positive $J_{\rm ex}$ favours alignment. <Magnetic domains> can make a ferromagnet's total <magnetization> vanish before an external field aligns its domains. Domain motion and anisotropy produce saturation, <hysteresis>, <remanent magnetization> and a reverse field measured by <magnetic coercivity>.

\Image[2012-magnetic-response.png]
{title=Qualitative linear magnetic responses and a ferromagnetic <hysteresis> loop}

**<Antiferromagnetism>** has oppositely ordered sublattices below a <Néel temperature>; equal opposing moments give zero net <magnetization> while retaining a nonzero staggered <order parameter>. A susceptibility anomaly and <magnetic neutron diffraction> reveal this order even when bulk <magnetization> vanishes. **<Ferrimagnetism>** also has opposing sublattices but unequal contributions, giving a nonzero spontaneous net moment. Above their ordering <temperatures> both can become paramagnetic. Measurements with a <magnetometer> as a function of field and <temperature>, supplemented by magnetic <diffraction>, therefore distinguishes a compensated ordered material from an ordinary paramagnet.

= Solution

Use two <plane waves>, $|\mathbf k\rangle$ and $|\mathbf k-\mathbf G\rangle$, as a <basis> near their Bragg degeneracy. The potential has <Fourier coefficients> $V_{\pm\mathbf G}$; its zero <Fourier coefficient> vanishes. All other <plane waves> are off <resonance> and are neglected in the leading <nearly-free electron model>. With $\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}=\hbar^2k^2/(2m_e)$, the <Schrödinger equation> reduces to the <Hermitian matrix> problem
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}&V_G\\
V_G^*&\varepsilon_{\mathbf k-\mathbf G}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}c_1\\c_2\end{pmatrix}
=E\begin{pmatrix}c_1\\c_2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Setting the <characteristic polynomial> equal to zero gives
$$
(E-\varepsilon_{\mathbf k})(E-\varepsilon_{\mathbf k-\mathbf G})-|V_G|^2=0.
$$
Solving this <quadratic equation> gives
$$
E_\pm=\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}+\varepsilon_{\mathbf k-\mathbf G}}2
\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf k-\mathbf G}}2\right)^2+|V_G|^2}.
$$
For the real <cosine> potential its coefficient $V_G$ is real; the absolute-value form also allows an arbitrary choice of origin and corresponding Fourier phase.

At the Brillouin-zone boundary, $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf G=G^2/2$, the free energies are equal. The two <standing waves> have different <probability densities> relative to the <lattice> potential and are split by
$$
\boxed{\Delta E=2|V_G|.}
$$
Repeating this <avoided crossing> at the <reciprocal lattice> boundaries joins smooth branches into <energy bands>. In one dimension a nonzero <Fourier coefficient> opens a gap at the corresponding crossing. In higher dimensions a direct gap at one <wavevector> need not give a common forbidden <energy> interval at every <wavevector>: different bands may still overlap in <energy>. The present two-dimensional example remains metallic even though the crossings are locally gapped.

For the rectangular <lattice>, choose real-space primitive vectors $a\widehat{\mathbf x}$ and $b\widehat{\mathbf y}$. The <reciprocal lattice> vectors are
$$
\mathbf G_x=\frac{2\pi}{a}\widehat{\mathbf x},\qquad
\mathbf G_y=\frac{2\pi}{b}\widehat{\mathbf y}.
$$
Define the half-widths of the first <Brillouin zone> by $K=\pi/a$ and $L=\pi/b$. Their values and the zone are
$$
\boxed{K=2.41661\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}},\quad L=3.92699\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}},\quad
-K\leq k_x\leq K,\quad -L\leq k_y\leq L.}
$$
A primitive reciprocal cell can be drawn with a corner at the origin; its centred translate is the first <Brillouin zone> here. Its area is $4\pi^2/(ab)=37.9600\,\mathrm{nm^{-2}}$.

\Image[2012-rectangular-cells.png]
{title=Real-space primitive cell and reciprocal-space first <Brillouin zone>}

Including both <spin> states, counting the occupied free-electron states per unit area gives
$$
n_{2D}=2\frac{\pi k_F^2}{(2\pi)^2}=\frac{k_F^2}{2\pi}
=\frac2{ab}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{k_F=\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{ab}}=3.47607\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}},\qquad
E_F^{(0)}=\frac{\hbar^2k_F^2}{2m_e}=0.46036\,\mathrm{eV}.}
$$
The occupied <disk> has precisely the area of the first <Brillouin zone>, but their shapes differ: $K<k_F<L$. It protrudes through both vertical zone boundaries and does not reach the horizontal ones. Thus two <electrons> per cell do not imply a filled first band in the absence of a separating <band gap>.

Fold the protruding caps by $\pm\mathbf G_x$. The original <circle> and the translated <circle> intersect at $k_x=K$, $k_y=\pm\sqrt{k_F^2-K^2}$. The potential mixes these states and replaces each crossing by an <avoided crossing>. The lower branch reconnects into two open sheets running across successive zones in the $k_x$ direction; the upper branch forms a closed lens-shaped pocket about a vertical zone boundary. In a reduced-zone sketch its two halves sit at opposite edges, but periodic identification makes them **one closed sheet**. This is <rectangular-lattice Fermi-surface reconstruction>.

\Image[2012-fermi-reconstruction.png]
{title=Free and reconstructed <Fermi contours>: two open sheets and one closed pocket per reciprocal cell}

The largest pocket in this weak-potential construction is the <lens-shaped intersection of two disks> enclosed by the intersecting free circles. Each has radius $k_F$ and their centres are separated by $2K$. If $\theta=\arccos(K/k_F)$, two sectors minus their two triangles give the <lens bound on a reconstructed Fermi pocket>:
$$
A_{\max}=2k_F^2\theta-2K\sqrt{k_F^2-K^2},\qquad
\boxed{A_{\max}=7.30680\,\mathrm{nm^{-2}}.}
$$
Opening a gap raises the upper branch at fixed <chemical potential> and shrinks its pocket. The reported <quantum oscillation> area satisfies
$$
\boxed{0<A_k=5.74\,\mathrm{nm^{-2}}<A_{\max},\qquad
\frac{A_k}{A_{\max}}=0.786.}
$$
It is therefore consistent with this closed sheet. <Onsager quantization> gives $F=\hbar A_k/(2\pi e)\simeq601\,\mathrm T$. An orbit on this closed contour can return to itself under the <Lorentz force> and occupy quantized <Landau levels>. The open sheets do not enclose a finite orbit area for a perpendicular field in this two-dimensional model. The observation is consistent with the reconstructed pocket, without uniquely determining a completely arbitrary band structure.

For a quantitative rough coupling estimate, use the supplied fixed-chemical-potential approximation and retain the two waves coupled by $\mathbf G_x$. Write
$$
C=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}=0.0380998\,\mathrm{eV\,nm^2},\qquad
v=|V_G|,\qquad k_x=K+q.
$$
Their upper <eigenvalue> is
$$
E_+(q,k_y)=C(K^2+q^2+k_y^2)+\sqrt{4C^2K^2q^2+v^2}.
$$
Setting it equal to $Ck_F^2$ gives the pocket boundary
$$
k_y^2=k_F^2-K^2-q^2-\sqrt{4K^2q^2+(v/C)^2}.
$$
Its intersection with $k_y=0$ occurs at $q=\pm q_m$, where
$$
q_m^2=k_F^2+K^2-\sqrt{4k_F^2K^2+(v/C)^2}.
$$
The <two-wave estimate of a Bragg-gap coupling from a pocket area> therefore uses
$$
A(v)=4\int_0^{q_m}
\sqrt{k_F^2-K^2-q^2-\sqrt{4K^2q^2+(v/C)^2}}\,dq.
$$
At $v=0$ this reproduces the <lens bound on a reconstructed Fermi pocket>; the pocket disappears at $v=C(k_F^2-K^2)=0.23786\,\mathrm{eV}$. <Numerical integration> gives $A(0.075\,\mathrm{eV})=6.22\,\mathrm{nm^{-2}}$ and $A(0.100\,\mathrm{eV})=5.54\,\mathrm{nm^{-2}}$. Matching the measured area gives $v\simeq0.0931\,\mathrm{eV}$, so the justified rough result is
$$
\boxed{|V_G|\sim0.1\,\mathrm{eV}\qquad(\Delta E\sim0.2\,\mathrm{eV}).}
$$
This estimate neglects remote plane-wave admixtures and other <Fourier coefficients>. It also holds the <chemical potential> fixed as requested; an exact fixed-density calculation would determine it self-consistently after reconstruction. The numerical root is a way to implement the approximate model, not a measurement of $V_G$ to four significant figures.

In the <Lorentz oscillator model>, each participating bound <electron> is displaced by an applied <electric field> against a restoring force. The model replaces complicated <interband transitions> by a driven <damped harmonic oscillator>. If $u$ is the <electron> displacement along the field and the <electron> charge is $-e$, its equation of motion is
$$
m\ddot u+m\gamma\dot u+m\omega_0^2u=-eE(t).
$$
Here $\omega_0$ describes binding and $\gamma>0$ describes loss of coherent motion. For $E(t)=\operatorname{Re}(E_0e^{-i\omega t})$, the <complex amplitude> is
$$
u_0=-\frac{eE_0}{m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega)}.
$$
The induced <electric dipole moment> is $-eu$, so the <electric polarization> amplitude for <number density> $n$ is
$$
P_0=\frac{ne^2}{m}\frac{E_0}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega}.
$$
Using $D=\epsilon_0E+P=\epsilon_0\epsilon_rE$ gives
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_r(\omega)=1+\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega},\qquad
\omega_p^2=\frac{ne^2}{\epsilon_0m}.}
$$
The paper's $\epsilon$ is this dimensionless <relative permittivity>, rather than the absolute <permittivity> $\epsilon_0\epsilon_r$. Independent classes of bound <electrons> contribute additive susceptibilities, usually with different <resonance> frequencies and <optical oscillator strengths>.

Writing $D_\omega=(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+\gamma^2\omega^2$ separates the response into
$$
\epsilon_r'=1+\frac{\omega_p^2(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)}{D_\omega},\qquad
\epsilon_r''=\frac{\omega_p^2\gamma\omega}{D_\omega}.
$$
At zero <frequency> the <real part> is $1+\omega_p^2/\omega_0^2$ and the <imaginary part> vanishes. Near <resonance> the <imaginary part> has a positive <photon absorption> peak and the <real part> changes rapidly, passing through $1$ at $\omega_0$. Above <resonance> the <real part> can become negative when <damping> is sufficiently weak; at very high <frequency> it approaches $1$ from below while the <imaginary part> tends to zero. Finite <damping> removes the undamped divergence.

\Image[2012-lorentz-optics.png]
{title=Real and imaginary relative permittivity, optical <reflectance> and attenuation in a <Lorentz oscillator model>}

For a nonmagnetic material take the passive square-root branch $N=\eta+i\kappa=\sqrt{\epsilon_r}$, with $\kappa\geq0$. A <plane wave> then has an intensity attenuation coefficient $\alpha=2\omega\kappa/c$. Thus the imaginary <permittivity> expresses dissipation, whereas the actual <photon absorption> coefficient is determined by the imaginary <refractive index>. The normal-incidence <power> <reflectance> is
$$
R=\frac{(\eta-1)^2+\kappa^2}{(\eta+1)^2+\kappa^2}.
$$
A large refractive-index mismatch produces <wave reflection> even without loss. In the undamped model $\epsilon_r<0$ between $\omega_0$ and $\sqrt{\omega_0^2+\omega_p^2}$, so the field is evanescent and <wave reflection> is total in a semi-infinite sample. With <damping> the response includes both <wave reflection> and <photon absorption>; a peak in the imaginary <permittivity> should not simply be identified with a <reflectance> peak.

To infer the short-wavelength background from $R=0.35$, assume weak <photon absorption> and take the physical <semiconductor> branch $\eta>1$. Then
$$
\sqrt R=\frac{\eta-1}{\eta+1},\qquad
\eta=\frac{1+\sqrt{0.35}}{1-\sqrt{0.35}}=3.8973,
$$
so
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_b\simeq\eta^2=15.19\simeq15.}
$$
This is an estimate of the valence-electron background in a <frequency> window above the doped-carrier response and below the effective bound-electron <resonance>. One <reflectance> value alone does not determine both parts of a complex dielectric constant. Even for real $\epsilon_r$, a reciprocal-index branch gives the same $R$; the ordinary background index greater than unity selects the branch used here.

Using the supplied conventional cubic <lattice> parameter, the participating valence-electron <number density> is
$$
n_v=\frac{4\times4}{(0.56\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm m)^3}
=9.11\times10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}.
$$
Using $m=m_e$ for the stipulated free-electron comparison gives
$$
\omega_{pv}=\sqrt{\frac{n_ve^2}{\epsilon_0m_e}}
=1.703\times10^{16}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}},\qquad
\boxed{\hbar\omega_{pv}=11.21\,\mathrm{eV}.}
$$
An <angular frequency> “in eV” means its quantum <energy> $\hbar\omega$, not $h\omega$. No doped-carrier <effective mass> should be inserted into this valence-electron count.

The low-frequency limit of the *bound-electron contribution* obeys
$$
\epsilon_b=1+\frac{\omega_{pv}^2}{\omega_0^2}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\hbar\omega_0=\frac{11.21\,\mathrm{eV}}{\sqrt{15.19-1}}
=2.98\,\mathrm{eV}\simeq3\,\mathrm{eV}.}
$$
This effective <resonance> is much larger than the quoted $0.2\,\mathrm{eV}$ <band gap>. There is no contradiction: the single oscillator has been assigned the strength of all sixteen participating valence <electrons> per conventional cell, whereas the <band gap> is only the lowest interband excitation threshold. A real spectrum distributes <optical oscillator strength> over many transitions. Equating the oscillator <resonance> to the lowest threshold would misrepresent the assumed background response. Here “low <frequency>” means low relative to $\omega_0$; the static response of the *doped conducting sample* also contains its free carriers.

The additional mobile carriers in doped <indium antimonide> contribute a <Drude frequency response>, obtained by setting their restoring <frequency> to zero. At <infrared> frequencies well below the effective valence <resonance>, the sum is approximately
$$
\epsilon_r(\omega)\simeq\epsilon_b-
\frac{\omega_{pc}^2}{\omega(\omega+i\gamma_c)},\qquad
\omega_{pc}^2=\frac{n_ce^2}{\epsilon_0m_c^*}.
$$
At the shorter wavelengths the carrier term is small and $R$ approaches the background value. As <wavelength> increases, the negative free-carrier contribution lowers the real <permittivity> towards unity. For weak <damping>, refractive-index matching to <vacuum> gives the <Drude-Lorentz reflectivity minimum> at
$$
\boxed{\omega_{\min}\simeq\frac{\omega_{pc}}{\sqrt{\epsilon_b-1}},\qquad
\lambda_{\min}\simeq\frac{2\pi c\sqrt{\epsilon_b-1}}{\omega_{pc}}.}
$$
This minimum is near $\epsilon_r'=1$, not its zero. At the slightly lower screened <plasma frequency> $\omega_{pc}/\sqrt{\epsilon_b}$, the real <permittivity> crosses zero; at longer wavelengths it becomes negative and the material becomes strongly reflective. Finite <damping> prevents a perfectly vanishing minimum and rounds the edge. At still lower <frequency> the large conducting response also drives $R$ towards unity.

For fixed carrier <effective mass> and background <permittivity>, $\omega_{pc}\propto\sqrt{n_c}$ and $\lambda_{\min}\propto n_c^{-1/2}$. The observed shift of the minimum towards shorter <wavelength> as doping increases is therefore the expected free-carrier trend.
