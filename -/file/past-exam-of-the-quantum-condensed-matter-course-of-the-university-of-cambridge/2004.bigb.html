The <radial distribution function> is the conditional <number density> of neighbors at separation $r$, divided by the bulk <number density> $n$. In an isotropic three-dimensional liquid, the mean number in a thin spherical shell is $dN=4\pi nr^2g(r)dr$. Strong short-range repulsion makes $g(r)$ very small inside the effective particle core. A first maximum marks the nearest-neighbor coordination shell. More distant shells give weaker oscillations, which decay toward $g(r)=1$ as long-range positional correlations disappear. A typical simple liquid has no indefinitely repeated sharp lattice peaks.

\Image[2004-liquid-radial-distribution.png]
{title=Excluded core and damped coordination shells in a schematic liquid pair distribution}
{description=An original illustrative hard-core liquid profile, not experimental data. The horizontal limit is one; the neighbor count additionally contains the spherical-shell factor $4\pi nr^2$.}

The <static structure factor> measured by diffraction is related to the Fourier transform of $g(r)-1$. Thus the broad diffraction maxima of a liquid and the damped real-space coordination shells describe the same short-range organization.

Treat $\alpha$ as a constant and use the <canonical ensemble> identity $F=-k_BT\ln Z$. The given formal <partition function> gives $F=\alpha k_BT^3N^2/V$. The <pressure> is its negative <volume> derivative at fixed $T,N$:
$$
\boxed{P=-\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial V}\right)_{T,N}=\alpha k_BT^3\frac{N^2}{V^2},\qquad PV^2=\alpha k_BT^3N^2.}
$$
Equivalently $P=k_BT(\partial\ln Z/\partial V)_{T,N}$. The explicit <temperature> dependence in $\ln Z$ must be retained; this is not the ideal-gas <pressure> law.

In a <p-n junction>, <diffusion> and <electron-hole recombination> near the contact leave a <depletion region> of ionized dopants. Its built-in <electric field> opposes further majority-carrier <diffusion>. At <thermodynamic equilibrium> drift and <diffusion> currents cancel. Forward bias lowers the electrostatic barrier and injects <electrons> and <electron holes> across the junction; reverse bias raises the barrier and suppresses that majority-carrier injection. For an ideal diode below breakdown,
$$
\boxed{I=I_s\left(e^{eV/(k_BT)}-1\right).}
$$
Thus a modest forward voltage gives a rapidly increasing current, whereas reverse current remains approximately $-I_s$. This asymmetric current-voltage relation makes the junction a <rectifier>. Real leakage, <electron-hole recombination> and breakdown limit the ideal expression.

A straight line in $C/T$ against $T^2$ means $C/T=\gamma+\beta T^2$. Reading its intercept and slope gives approximately $\gamma\simeq2.1\,\mathrm{mJ\,mol^{-1}K^{-2}}$ and $\beta\simeq2.7\,\mathrm{mJ\,mol^{-1}K^{-4}}$; the graphical precision is only a few percent. Hence the molar <heat capacity> is approximately
$$
\boxed{C(T)\simeq(2.1\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-2}})T+(2.7\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-4}})T^3.}
$$
The linear <electronic heat capacity> comes from conduction <electrons> within <energy> of order $k_BT$ of the <Fermi energy>; the <Pauli exclusion principle> prevents deeply occupied states from contributing freely. The cubic lattice term is the low-temperature <Debye heat capacity> of three-dimensional acoustic <phonons>, whose low-frequency <density of states> is proportional to <frequency> squared. These are molar coefficients, so the <temperature> powers carry the extra kelvin factors indicated.

For a low <circular orbit>, <Newton's law of universal gravitation> gives $P=2\pi\sqrt{R^3/(GM)}$. Using $M=4\pi\bar\rho R^3/3$ yields
$$
\boxed{P^2=\frac{3\pi}{G\bar\rho}.}
$$
Thus **the Moon and Mars have roughly comparable <mean densities>**: <low circular-orbit period measures mean density>. Equal periods would mean equal <mean densities> in this approximation. This does not require their masses or <radii> to be similar. The orbit altitudes must be small compared with the body <radii>, and the bodies are being treated as approximately spherical.

The <gain bandwidth> must be expressed as a <frequency> range:
$$
\Delta\nu=c\left(\frac1{600\,\mathrm{nm}}-\frac1{700\,\mathrm{nm}}\right)\simeq7.14\times10^{13}\,\mathrm{Hz}.
$$
The <gain bandwidth limit on a laser pulse> then gives
$$
\boxed{\Delta t\sim\frac1{\Delta\nu}\simeq1.4\times10^{-14}\,\mathrm s,\quad\text{of order }10\,\mathrm{fs}.}
$$
The numerical coefficient depends on the pulse shape and width definition. For example a <Gaussian pulse> whose intensity spectral FWHM actually filled this width would have a transform-limited intensity duration about $6.2\,\mathrm{fs}$. A hard gain interval does not by itself specify such a FWHM. Reaching the estimated scale requires coherent <mode locking> and control of <group velocity dispersion>; the cavity round-trip time determines the pulse separation. An <intensity autocorrelation> measurement can estimate the pulse duration once its shape is specified.

This is the <Joule-Thomson coefficient>, $\mu_{JT}=(\partial T/\partial P)_H$, describing a <temperature> change during an isenthalpic <pressure> change. Here $H$ is <enthalpy>, $C_P=(\partial H/\partial T)_P$ is the <heat capacity> at constant <pressure>, and $V$ is <volume>, all for the same chosen amount of substance. Starting with $dH=T\,dS+V\,dP$ and using the <Gibbs free-energy Maxwell relation> $(\partial S/\partial P)_T=-(\partial V/\partial T)_P$ gives
$$
dH=C_PdT+\left[V-T\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P\right]dP.
$$
Setting $dH=0$ proves
$$
\boxed{\mu_{JT}=\frac1{C_P}\left[T\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P-V\right].}
$$
In a steady insulated throttle or porous plug, negligible kinetic- and potential-energy changes make the upstream and downstream enthalpies equal. A positive coefficient gives cooling on a <pressure> decrease; a negative one gives warming. For an <ideal gas>, $V\propto T$ at fixed $P$, so the coefficient is zero. Real-gas interactions permit cooling and an inversion curve, making the effect useful in gas refrigeration and liquefaction.

The <measurement of the Joule-Thomson effect with a porous plug> compares pressures and thermometers on the two sides of an insulated flow restriction. Suppressing external <heat> exchange and separating the readings from high-speed jets are part of making the <enthalpy> argument applicable; an arbitrary adiabatic expansion need not be isenthalpic.

The <Clapeyron equation> gives the slope of a two-phase <phase coexistence curve>. Let $s_l,s_v$ and $v_l,v_v$ be <entropy> and <volume> per molecule, or consistently per mole. Equality of the two <chemical potentials> and $d\mu=-s\,dT+v\,dP$ imply
$$
0=d(\mu_v-\mu_l)=-(s_v-s_l)dT+(v_v-v_l)dP.
$$
At reversible coexistence the latent <heat> in the same amount convention is $\mathcal L=T(s_v-s_l)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{dP}{dT}=\frac{\mathcal L}{T\Delta v},\qquad\Delta v=v_v-v_l.}
$$
The relation assumes <thermodynamic equilibrium> between two bulk phases of a pure substance, neglecting curvature and other work terms. For vaporization both latent <heat> and <volume> change are positive, giving a positive vapor-pressure slope. If the vapor is approximately ideal and its <volume> dominates, the <ideal-vapor Clausius-Clapeyron approximation> gives $d\ln P/dT\simeq\mathcal L/(k_BT^2)$ with latent <heat> per molecule.

Experimentally, a sealed two-phase sample in a temperature-controlled bath supplies the coexistence <pressure> as <temperature> changes. Independent calorimetry and phase-density measurements supply $\mathcal L$ and $\Delta v$, allowing the <pressure> slope to test the equation. The formula connects those distinct measurements and is a practical route to latent heats.

This relates an <thermodynamic equilibrium> <variance> to the local stiffness of an appropriate <thermodynamic free energy>. At fixed bath <temperature>, suppose the effective <thermodynamic free energy> $A(x)$ includes the constraints and the measure for a chosen coordinate. Its <thermodynamic equilibrium> <probability density function> is proportional to $e^{-A(x)/(k_BT)}$. Near a stable minimum $x_0$, write
$$
A(x)\simeq A(x_0)+\frac12A''(x_0)(x-x_0)^2,\qquad A''(x_0)>0.
$$
The resulting <normal distribution> gives the <Gaussian fluctuation approximation>
$$
\boxed{\langle(x-x_0)^2\rangle\simeq\frac{k_BT}{A''(x_0)}.}
$$
This is exact for a quadratic <thermodynamic free energy> with the stated flat coordinate measure; in general it is a small-fluctuation result. A smaller curvature means a softer restoring response and larger fluctuations. Near vanishing curvature, higher-order terms become important, so the quadratic approximation cannot alone predict an actual infinite variance.

For example <optical trap Langevin calibration> uses the Brownian displacement of a trapped bead: $A=\kappa(x-x_0)^2/2$ gives $\kappa=k_BT/\langle\Delta x^2\rangle$. A camera or position detector records the fluctuations. Finite exposure averages rapid motion and can bias the measured variance; the measurement protocol matters as well as the <thermodynamic equilibrium> identity.

<Entropy> is a thermodynamic <state function>. For a reversible <heat> transfer, $dS=\delta Q_{\rm rev}/T$; the <Clausius inequality> generalizes this to irreversible processes. The combined <entropy> of a system and its surroundings does not decrease. A subsystem can lose <entropy> by exporting <heat> or matter, so the second-law statement must specify the whole isolated combination. In a simple compressible system,
$$
dU=T\,dS-P\,dV+\mu\,dN,
$$
which identifies <temperature>, <pressure> and <chemical potential> as derivatives of the <energy> representation of the state function. <Thermodynamic equilibrium> maximizes <entropy> under fixed total <energy>, <volume> and particle number. A <heat> reservoir instead leads to minimizing the <Helmholtz free energy> at fixed $T,V,N$, or the <Gibbs free energy> at fixed $T,P,N$.

Statistical mechanics explains the state count. In a <microcanonical ensemble>, equally probable compatible microstates give <Boltzmann's entropy formula>, $S=k_B\ln\Omega$. With general discrete probabilities, the <Gibbs entropy> is
$$
S=-k_B\sum_jp_j\ln p_j.
$$
The microcanonical formula follows by setting every nonzero probability to $1/\Omega$. For a <canonical ensemble>, $p_j=e^{-\beta E_j}/Z$, so
$$
\boxed{S=k_B\ln Z+\frac UT,\qquad F=U-TS=-k_BT\ln Z.}
$$
This turns microscopic <energy> levels and their populations into a measurable thermodynamic quantity. Classical phase-space counting needs a consistent microscopic normalization and indistinguishability convention; quantum state counting supplies that normalization. Independent systems have multiplicative state counts and therefore additive entropies.

For an <ideal gas> freely expanding from <volume> $V$ to $2V$ in an insulated enclosure, <energy> remains fixed while each particle gains twice the accessible position <volume>. The state count increases by $2^N$, giving $\boxed{\Delta S=Nk_B\ln2}$. The actual irreversible process transfers no <heat>. A reversible isothermal comparison path transfers <heat> $Nk_BT\ln2$ and computes the same <entropy> change because the initial and final <thermodynamic equilibrium> states are the same.

Calorimetry supplies another connection to experiment: at fixed <pressure> and within one phase, $S(T)-S(0)=\int_0^T C_P(T')\,dT'/T'$, with latent-heat contributions $\mathcal L/T$ at first-order transitions. The <third law of thermodynamics> fixes $S(0)=0$ for an ideal perfect crystal with a unique ground state. Degeneracy or frozen disorder can produce <residual entropy>. <Entropy> thus links heat-capacity measurements, phase <thermodynamic equilibrium> and the number of accessible microscopic configurations.

<Charge carrier mobility> measures weak-field drift speed per <electric field>. For <electrons> and <electron holes>,
$$
\mathbf v_e=-\mu_e\mathbf E,\qquad\mathbf v_h=+\mu_h\mathbf E,\qquad\boxed{\sigma=e(n\mu_e+p\mu_h).}
$$
Both mobilities are positive, with SI units $\mathrm{m^2\,V^{-1}s^{-1}}$; the opposite drift signs reflect the opposite carrier charges. In a simple isotropic relaxation-time model, <electron mobility> is $\mu_e=e\tau_e/m_e^*$ and <hole mobility> is $\mu_h=e\tau_h/m_h^*$. These are momentum relaxation times, not the <minority carrier lifetime> for <electron-hole recombination>.

Scattering by <phonons>, ionized impurities and defects limits mobility. Increasing <phonon> populations commonly lowers mobility at high <temperature>; ionized-impurity scattering can dominate at low <temperature> and high <semiconductor doping>. Approximate addition of independent inverse relaxation times is the <Matthiessen's rule>. At sufficiently strong field, velocity saturation invalidates the constant-mobility drift law.

The <Hall effect> provides a measurement: a current-carrying bar in a perpendicular <magnetic field> develops a transverse voltage. In a one-carrier model $|R_H|=1/(en)$ and $\mu=\sigma|R_H|$, combining the transverse voltage with longitudinal <electrical conductivity>. Multiple carrier types and scattering-dependent Hall factors require a more detailed interpretation.

= Solution

Generation increases the mobile <electron> or hole populations; interband excitation creates an <electron-hole pair>. Thermal excitation and absorbed photons are important sources, and <impact ionization> can multiply carriers in a strong field. Ordinary single-photon interband absorption needs enough photon <energy> to bridge the <band gap>, with the required momentum balance.

<Electron-hole recombination> removes a pair. <Radiative electron-hole recombination> emits a photon, with simple rate $Bnp$; <trap-assisted electron-hole recombination> uses localized defect levels and transfers <energy> to the lattice; <Auger recombination> transfers the <electron-hole recombination> <energy> to another carrier. The last process becomes especially important at high injection because its rate contains products such as $n^2p$ and $np^2$. The detailed band structure and available defects determine which channel dominates.

At thermal <thermodynamic equilibrium>, generation and <electron-hole recombination> continue at equal rates. For the simple radiative model the net <electron-hole recombination> rate is $B(np-n_i^2)$, enforcing the <semiconductor mass-action law>. Under weak excitation with the majority density nearly fixed, an excess minority density often decays as $\Delta n(t)=\Delta n(0)e^{-t/\tau}$, defining its <minority carrier lifetime>.

A short light pulse followed by time-resolved <electrical conductivity> or luminescence measures the relaxation. Spatial <diffusion>, trapping and instrumental response must be separated from <electron-hole recombination> before identifying a single fitted decay constant with the intrinsic lifetime. These processes control photodetector response and light-emitting-device efficiency.

An n-channel <MOSFET> has two heavily n-doped source and drain regions in a p-type body, with an insulated gate above the intervening surface. A positive gate voltage first depletes surface <electron holes> and then induces an <electron> <semiconductor inversion layer>. Above threshold $V_T$ this layer connects source to drain. Gate voltage controls the channel charge through the oxide capacitance, while the insulating gate permits a very small steady gate current.

\Image[2004-mosfet-cross-section.png]
{title=An induced electron channel connects the source and drain of an n-channel MOSFET}
{description=Original schematic cross-section in the on state. The oxide separates the gate electrically from the body; the thin surface channel is an inversion layer rather than a permanently n-doped bridge.}

For the <long-channel MOSFET current law>, let $C_{\rm ox}$ be oxide capacitance per area, $W,L$ channel width and length, and use constant mobility $\mu_n$. In the gradual-channel approximation,
$$
I_D=\mu_nC_{\rm ox}\frac WL\left[(V_{GS}-V_T)V_{DS}-\frac{V_{DS}^2}{2}\right],\qquad0<V_{DS}<V_{GS}-V_T.
$$
At larger drain voltage the ideal channel pinches off near the drain and the current saturates at
$$
\boxed{I_{D,\rm sat}=\frac12\mu_nC_{\rm ox}\frac WL(V_{GS}-V_T)^2.}
$$
Carriers still flow through the pinch-off region; pinch-off does not mean zero drain current. These formulas neglect channel-length modulation, short-channel effects and velocity saturation. A p-channel device reverses the carrier type and operating polarities. Gate control makes MOSFETs useful as electronic switches and amplifiers.

<Exchange interactions> arise from quantum electronic motion, Coulomb interactions and fermionic antisymmetry. A localized-spin description can be written $H=\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_j$. In this explicit plus-sign convention $J<0$ favors parallel <spins> and $J>0$ favors antiparallel <spins>; many texts put a minus sign into the Hamiltonian and reverse that convention.

<Direct exchange> involves overlapping orbitals and their Coulomb exchange matrix elements. The <Pauli exclusion principle> correlates <spin> symmetry with spatial symmetry, changing the electronic <energy>. Orbital occupation and competing kinetic processes determine which <spin> state is favored; the preference is not universally ferromagnetic.

<Superexchange> couples moments through virtual hopping, often via a nonmagnetic ligand. For two half-filled orbitals in the simple repulsive one-band model, the antiferromagnetic coupling is $J\simeq4|t|^2/U$: virtual double occupation lowers the antiparallel-spin state more effectively. Orbital geometry and intermediate-state interactions can change the sign in other configurations. Real charge transport can remain insulating while this magnetic coupling acts.

<Double exchange> instead uses actual carrier motion between mixed-valence sites. Local <Hund's coupling> ties the mobile <electron>'s <spin> to a core moment. Parallel neighboring cores allow a larger effective hopping amplitude and a lower carrier <kinetic energy>, favoring ferromagnetism. The original model explicitly connected conduction and magnetic order in manganese perovskites.<Interaction between the d-Shells in the Transition Metals. II. Ferromagnetic Compounds of Manganese with Perovskite Structure by Clarence Zener>[1951 abstract: correlation of electrical conduction with ferromagnetism and its double-exchange interpretation]{ref}

<Conduction-electron-mediated exchange> couples localized moments through a metal's electronic <spin> response. Its strength and sign depend on separation and the <Fermi surface>; in a simple three-dimensional asymptotic model the range factor oscillates as $\cos(2k_Fr)/r^3$. The original nuclear-moment calculation was motivated by resonance-line broadening in silver.<Indirect Exchange Coupling of Nuclear Magnetic Moments by Conduction Electrons by M. A. Ruderman and Charles Kittel>[1954 abstract and introduction: nuclear moments coupled through conduction-electron hyperfine interactions]{ref} In an itinerant metal, an alternative description compares the kinetic-energy cost of unequal <spin> populations with the exchange-energy gain; the <Stoner criterion> expresses when spontaneous polarization is favorable.

The resulting order also depends on the lattice and competing couplings. In <ferromagnetism>, moments align to give a nonzero moment within a domain. In <antiferromagnetism>, opposing sublattices cancel their moments. In <ferrimagnetism>, oppositely ordered sublattices have unequal moments and leave a net one. A bulk zero magnetization alone cannot distinguish antiferromagnetic order from a disordered state or canceling <magnetic domains>.

The <manganese monoxide neutron diffraction experiment> makes that distinction experimentally. A monochromatic neutron beam scatters from powdered material, and a counter records angular intensity. Comparing the same compound at room <temperature> and $80\,\mathrm K$ reveals additional low-temperature magnetic reflections, indexed by a larger magnetic unit cell, while nuclear reflections persist. That spatial periodicity is evidence of alternating order even though the net moment cancels.<Detection of Antiferromagnetism by Neutron Diffraction by Clifford Glenwood Shull and J. Samuel Smart>[1949, Fig. 1 and its room-temperature/80 K comparison]{ref}

= Solution

For a bulk liquid and vapor at common fixed <temperature> and <pressure>, transfer $dN_l$ molecules from vapor to liquid while holding their sum fixed. The <Gibbs free energy> change is $dG=(\mu_l-\mu_v)dN_l$. <Thermodynamic equilibrium> with both phases present requires stationarity under either sign of transfer, hence $\boxed{\mu_l=\mu_v}$. This is the bulk coexistence condition; an interface's curvature adds a surface-energy cost.

In the capillarity approximation, take an incompressible drop with constant liquid <number density> $n_l$, bulk <chemical potential> $\mu_l$ at the specified reservoir conditions, and size-independent <surface tension> $\Gamma$. The liquid bulk is extensive, so its Gibbs <thermodynamic free energy> is $N_l\mu_l$. Creating interface area reversibly at fixed <temperature> adds $\Gamma A$. Equivalently, along growth at fixed bulk parameters the stated differential integrates as $dG_d=\mu_l\,dN_l+\Gamma\,dA$, with the no-drop reference set to zero. For a sphere,
$$
\boxed{G_d=\frac43\pi R^3n_l\mu_l+4\pi R^2\Gamma.}
$$
This approximation uses bulk properties for the interior and neglects the molecular interface thickness, curvature dependence of <surface tension> and higher compressibility corrections. It does not apply quantitatively to drops only a few molecules across.

The vapor contains $N-N_l$ molecules. Treat its <chemical potential> as approximately unchanged by forming the drop, as for a large surrounding reservoir or negligible vapor depletion. Then
$$
\boxed{G_{\rm tot}(R)=N\mu_v+\frac43\pi n_l(\mu_l-\mu_v)R^3+4\pi\Gamma R^2.}
$$
The <radius> is limited by the available molecules in a finite sample; the constant-reservoir expression is a local nucleation model, not a description of arbitrarily complete vapor depletion. The excess over the vapor reference has derivative
$$
\Delta G'(R)=4\pi R[2\Gamma+n_l(\mu_l-\mu_v)R].
$$
If $\mu_l>\mu_v$, it increases for every $R>0$, leaving only the stable boundary minimum $R=0$. If $\mu_l<\mu_v$, $R=0$ is a metastable local minimum and the additional stationary <radius> is
$$
\boxed{R_c=\frac{2\Gamma}{n_l(\mu_v-\mu_l)}.}
$$
At that <radius> $\Delta G''=-8\pi\Gamma<0$, so it is an unstable maximum. The <classical nucleation theory> barrier is $16\pi\Gamma^3/[3n_l^2(\mu_v-\mu_l)^2]$.

\Image[2004-droplet-nucleation-barrier.png]
{title=Surface cost and bulk gain produce a critical droplet in supersaturated vapor}
{description=Original dimensionless capillarity curves. The supersaturated case has a barrier at the critical radius; the other curve rises monotonically. These are reservoir-model free energies, before finite-sample depletion changes the chemical potentials.}

A fluctuation below $R_c$ tends to shrink because reducing its surface cost lowers <thermodynamic free energy>. **A supercritical drop tends to grow**, because its bulk free-energy gain outweighs the added surface cost. Growth continues while material supply and supersaturation allow it, eventually changing the vapor state or leading to macroscopic liquid. Curved-interface <pressure> and chemical <thermodynamic equilibrium> can also be described through the <Young-Laplace equation> and the <Kelvin equation>.

= Solution

Put $z=e^{\beta\mu}$, with $\beta=1/(k_BT)$. For a single quantum state of <energy> $\epsilon$, dilute <Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics> gives $\ln\Xi_\epsilon\simeq ze^{-\beta\epsilon}$. For bosons this is the leading expansion of $-\ln(1-ze^{-\beta\epsilon})$; the occupation must be small for that expansion. Thus the <grand potential> is
$$
\boxed{\Phi(\epsilon)=-k_BT\ln\Xi_\epsilon\simeq-k_BT e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}.}
$$
With the ground-state <energy> set to zero, a dilute classical gas requires $e^{\beta\mu}\ll1$, giving negative $\mu$. This sign is relative to the chosen <energy> zero. For an ideal Bose gas, $\mu$ approaches the ground <energy> from below at condensation, so it approaches zero with this convention.

The printed degeneracy formula requires $n_x,n_y,n_z=0,1,2,\ldots$, rather than strictly positive integers. With zero-point <energy> omitted these are the appropriate oscillator quantum numbers. The <stars and bars> count of three nonnegative integers summing to $n$ gives
$$
\boxed{d_n=\binom{n+2}{2}=\frac{(n+2)!}{n!\,2!}.}
$$
There is one ground state at $n=0$. If the integers were strictly positive, the count would instead be $\binom{n-1}{2}$ for $n\geq3$, inconsistent with both the printed formula and its <energy> reference.

For large $n$, $d_n\simeq n^2/2$. Consecutive <energy> shells are separated by $\hbar\omega$, giving the continuum <density of states>
$$
\boxed{g(E)\simeq\frac{n^2}{2\hbar\omega}=\frac{E^2}{2(\hbar\omega)^3}.}
$$
Use this when $k_BT\gg\hbar\omega$, and integrate the single-state grand potential over <energy>:
$$
\Phi_{\rm tot}\simeq-\frac{k_BT e^{\beta\mu}}{2(\hbar\omega)^3}\int_0^\infty E^2e^{-\beta E}dE=-\frac{(k_BT)^4}{(\hbar\omega)^3}e^{\beta\mu},
$$
because the integral is $2/\beta^3$. Particle number follows by differentiating at fixed <temperature> and trap <frequency>:
$$
N=-\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\mu}=\left(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar\omega}\right)^3e^{\beta\mu},\qquad\boxed{\mu=k_BT\ln\left[N\left(\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_BT}\right)^3\right].}
$$
These are the high-temperature expressions for the <classical grand potential in a harmonic trap>.

Extrapolating to $\mu=0$ gives the requested transition scale
$$
\boxed{T_c\sim\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_B}N^{1/3}.}
$$
It is an order-one estimate because Maxwell-Boltzmann occupations cease to be accurate near condensation. The <Bose-Einstein distribution> gives the more precise ideal-trap result $N_{\rm ex}^{\max}=\zeta(3)(k_BT/\hbar\omega)^3$, and hence $T_c=(\hbar\omega/k_B)[N/\zeta(3)]^{1/3}$, about $0.94$ times the estimate. Excess particles populate the ground state after the excited-state capacity saturates: <Bose condensation in a three-dimensional harmonic trap>.

The <observation of Bose-Einstein condensation in rubidium in 1995> implemented laser precooling followed by radio-frequency evaporation from a magnetic trap. Imaging the released cloud revealed a narrow anisotropic component within a broader thermal population. This provides an experimental connection to the population-counting argument; the measured expansion also reflects interactions and imaging resolution, not just the ideal-trap <density of states>.<Observation of Bose-Einstein Condensation in a Dilute Atomic Vapor by M. H. Anderson and others>[1995, pp. 198–201, Figs. 1–4]{ref}

For an isotropic free-electron gas at zero <temperature>, the <Fermi wavevector> is the <radius> of the occupied region in <wavevector> space. Its <energy> is $E_F=\hbar^2k_F^2/(2m)$. The first <Brillouin zone> is the Wigner-Seitz cell of the <reciprocal lattice>; it contains one distinct crystal momentum per primitive cell and per band, with <spin> counted separately.

For a two-dimensional sample of area $A$, allowed wavevectors have density $A/(2\pi)^2$. Including two <spin> states, filling a circle gives $N=2[A/(2\pi)^2]\pi k_F^2$. Dividing by area therefore yields
$$
\boxed{k_F=\sqrt{2\pi n}.}
$$
The square lattice has one atom per area $a^2$, so for valence $z$ the free-electron density is $z/a^2$. Its <Brillouin zone> has $|k_x|,|k_y|\leq\pi/a$. Thus
$$
\boxed{k_F^{(1)}=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}a\simeq8.36\times10^9\,\mathrm{m^{-1}},\qquad k_F^{(2)}=\frac{\sqrt{4\pi}}a\simeq1.18\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}.}
$$
The monovalent Fermi circle lies inside the square. The divalent circle crosses the faces but misses the corners; its area equals the zone's area. These are the free-electron contours before distortion by the periodic potential.

{description=Original reciprocal-space diagrams in units of $\pi/a$. The divalent circle has the same area as the square zone but a different shape, extending beyond the faces while leaving corner regions outside the circle.}

At a Bragg plane, the periodic potential mixes degenerate plane waves differing by a <reciprocal lattice vector>. The two standing-wave combinations have different charge densities relative to the ions and split in <energy>, opening the gap. In a two-wave approximation, the boundary energies are $E_B\pm E_g/2$, with $E_g=2|V_G|$.

The boundary <wavevector> magnitude is $\pi/a$ along $[10]$ and $\sqrt2\pi/a$ along $[11]$. The free-electron boundary energies are therefore
$$
E_X=\frac{\hbar^2\pi^2}{2ma^2}\simeq4.18\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad E_M=2E_X\simeq8.36\,\mathrm{eV}.
$$
Within the stated direction-independent gap picture, the upper band's lowest face-edge <energy> is approximately $E_X+2\,\mathrm{eV}=6.18\,\mathrm{eV}$, while the lower band's corner-edge <energy> is approximately $E_M-2\,\mathrm{eV}=6.36\,\mathrm{eV}$. There is consequently a small overlap, about $0.18\,\mathrm{eV}$, rather than a global insulating gap. The divalent Fermi level lies in that overlap, producing occupied upper-band states and unfilled lower-band states.

Thus **the intended isotropic-gap approximation predicts a metal**, with small <electron> and hole pockets. <Positive direct gaps can coexist with band overlap>. A fully specified square-lattice potential would require simultaneous treatment of the corner degeneracies; a numerical boundary splitting alone does not uniquely determine an arbitrary two-dimensional band structure. The conclusion here uses the given simplified gap picture, rather than <electron> count alone.

= Solution
{parent=C14}

<Semiconductor doping> introduces electrically active impurities. In <gallium arsenide>, <silicon> has one more valence <electron> than <gallium> but one fewer than <arsenic>. <Silicon> on a <gallium> site can therefore supply a shallow donor <electron>, while <silicon> on an <arsenic> site can accept an <electron> and leave a mobile hole. It is an <amphoteric dopant>, so its net effect depends on site occupation and growth conditions.<Thermal equilibrium concentrations of the amphoteric dopant Si and the associated carrier concentrations in GaAs by Chang-Ho Chen and others>[1999 abstract: neutral and ionized donors on Ga sites and acceptors on As sites]{ref}

For isotropic parabolic bands, shift the <energy> origin in the given free-electron <density of states> to the appropriate band edge and use its effective <mass>:
$$
\boxed{g_c(E)=\frac1{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m_e}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt{E-\epsilon_c},\quad E\geq\epsilon_c,}
$$
and, for valence <electron> states,
$$
\boxed{g_v(E)=\frac1{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m_h}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt{\epsilon_v-E},\quad E\leq\epsilon_v.}
$$
The stated normalization includes the two <spin> states and assumes one effective valley. In a nondegenerate semiconductor, the conduction occupation is approximately $e^{-(E-\mu)/(k_BT)}$ and the probability of a valence hole is approximately $e^{(E-\mu)/(k_BT)}$. Substituting $x=(E-\epsilon_c)/(k_BT)$ in the first integral, and the corresponding hole <energy> in the second, gives
$$
n=N_ce^{-(\epsilon_c-\mu)/(k_BT)},\qquad p=N_ve^{-(\mu-\epsilon_v)/(k_BT)},
$$
where the <effective density of states> values are $N_c=2[m_ek_BT/(2\pi\hbar^2)]^{3/2}$ and $N_v=2[m_hk_BT/(2\pi\hbar^2)]^{3/2}$. The factor $\sqrt\pi/2$ comes from the supplied Gaussian-weighted integral. Multiplying cancels the shared <thermodynamic equilibrium> <chemical potential> and proves the <semiconductor mass-action law>:
$$
\boxed{np=N_cN_ve^{-E_g/(k_BT)}=4\left(\frac{k_BT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\right)^3(m_em_h)^{3/2}e^{-E_g/(k_BT)}.}
$$
For the given sample $N_D=2\times10^{21}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$ and $N_A=10^{21}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$. With complete donor ionization and occupied acceptors, <charge neutrality> gives $n-p=N_D-N_A=10^{21}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$. At $293\,\mathrm K$ the given masses and gap give
$$
N_c\simeq3.92\times10^{23}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}},\quad N_v\simeq8.56\times10^{24}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}},\quad n_i^2\simeq1.26\times10^{24}\,\mathrm{m^{-6}}.
$$
Solving the <compensated semiconductor> equations, or using $n_i\ll N_D-N_A$, yields
$$
\boxed{n\simeq1.00\times10^{21}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}},\qquad p=\frac{n_i^2}{n}\simeq1.26\times10^3\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}.}
$$
The very small minority density is consistent with the wide gap and the strongly n-type balance. Also $n/N_c\simeq2.6\times10^{-3}\ll1$ and $p/N_v\ll1$, checking the nondegenerate approximation. The acceptor levels are occupied in this n-type regime; complete acceptor ionization in the compensation equation is appropriate to the model.

At low <temperature> <dopant freeze-out> reduces the conduction-electron population; a quantitative curve would require dopant binding energies and occupation factors. Once donors are ionized while intrinsic excitation is negligible, $n\simeq N_D-N_A$ gives an extrinsic plateau. At higher <temperature> intrinsic pairs eventually dominate, so $n\simeq p\simeq n_i(T)\propto T^{3/2}e^{-E_g/(2k_BT)}$ if the gap and masses are treated as constant. Real gap and <mass> changes modify that last expression.
