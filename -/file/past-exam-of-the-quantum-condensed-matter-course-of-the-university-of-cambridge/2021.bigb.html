= Solution
{parent=1}

For <state counting in a Bloch band>, take a <primitive cubic lattice> of spacing $a$ and impose <periodic boundary conditions> on lengths $L_i=N_i a$. The allowed <wavevectors> are $k_i=2\pi l_i/L_i$. The first <Brillouin zone> has side $2\pi/a$, so it contains $N_i$ distinct values in each direction and hence $N_xN_yN_z=N$ wavevectors. Each orbital-derived <energy band> has one state per wavevector per <spin> orientation. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{states per band in the whole crystal}=2N,\qquad
\text{states per atom per band}=2.}
$$
The phrase “per atom” attached to $2N$ in the PDF is a counting error: $2N$ is the total, not the per-atom count.

By the <Pauli exclusion principle>, a monovalent crystal supplies $N$ valence <electrons> and half-fills a spin-degenerate band; its <Fermi level> lies within allowed states, giving a <metal> in the independent-electron picture. Two electrons per atom can fill one band. If a <band gap> separates it from all empty bands, the crystal is a <band insulator>. An even valence count only permits insulation: overlapping bands can still produce a <metal>. Conversely, electron interactions can produce a <Mott insulator>, and symmetry breaking can permit insulation outside this simple spin-degenerate <electronic band structure> criterion. The relevant count in a crystal with a basis is per <primitive unit cell>.

= Solution
{parent=2}

In a simple <metal>, conduction <electrons> can move collectively in response to an incident <electromagnetic wave>. The <Drude model> gives, with time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$,
$$
\epsilon_r(\omega)=1-\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega(\omega+i\gamma)},\qquad
\omega_p^2=\frac{ne^2}{\epsilon_0m^*}.
$$
In the low-loss range $\gamma\ll\omega<\omega_p$, the <relative permittivity> is approximately $1-\omega_p^2/\omega^2<0$. The wavevector inside is predominantly imaginary, so the field is evanescent and penetrates only a short distance. Surface currents reradiate the field; little energy enters a good conductor. The normal-incidence <Fresnel equations> give
$$
\boxed{R=\left|\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon_r}-1}{\sqrt{\epsilon_r}+1}\right|^2\simeq1\quad(\omega<\omega_p,\ \gamma\text{ small}).}
$$
Many ordinary metals have their <plasma frequency> in the ultraviolet, placing visible light below this reflection edge. For $\omega>\omega_p$ the free-carrier contribution permits propagation; at $\omega\gg\omega_p$, $\epsilon_r\to1$ and $R\sim\omega_p^4/(16\omega^4)\to0$. The electrons' inertial response becomes too small to screen the rapidly oscillating field.

**Ultraviolet transparency is a simple-metal model prediction, not a universal property of real metals.** <Electronic transitions> between bands and bound-electron absorption can remain strong, shift the plasma edge and prevent transparency; transmission also depends on thickness and attenuation. The robust explanation is the change from a reflective negative-permittivity range to a possible propagating range above the plasma edge.

= Solution
{parent=3}

Let adjacent equal masses $m$ be separated by $a$, with alternating strong and weak spring constants $K$ and $\kappa$, $K\gg\kappa>0$. The pattern repeats after $2a$, even though the masses are identical. Thus the first <Brillouin zone> is
$$
\boxed{-\frac{\pi}{2a}\leq q\leq\frac{\pi}{2a}.}
$$
Choose the strong bond inside each two-atom <primitive unit cell>. With displacement amplitudes $(u,v)e^{i(2naq-\omega t)}$, the <normal mode> equations are
$$
m\omega^2\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}K+\kappa&-K-\kappa e^{-2iqa}\\-K-\kappa e^{2iqa}&K+\kappa\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The <alternating-spring chain dispersion relation> is consequently
$$
\boxed{\omega_\pm^2(q)=\frac{K+\kappa\pm\sqrt{(K-\kappa)^2+4K\kappa\cos^2(qa)}}m.}
$$
The lower <acoustic phonon branch> has in-phase motion within the strongly bound pair and $\omega_-(0)=0$. Its small-$q$ slope is the <speed of sound>,
$$
c_s=a\sqrt{\frac{2K\kappa}{m(K+\kappa)}}\simeq a\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa}{m}}.
$$
The upper <optical phonon branch> has opposite motion within each pair, with $\omega_+(0)=\sqrt{2(K+\kappa)/m}$. Here “optical” names the gapped branch; equal neutral masses need not couple to light through an electric dipole.

In the <strong-bond limit of the alternating-spring chain>, the pairs act almost as rigid dimers in the low-frequency branch, while their internal stretch gives a high, nearly flat branch:
$$
\omega_-^2\simeq\frac{2\kappa}{m}\sin^2(qa),\qquad
\omega_+^2\simeq\frac{2K}{m}+\frac{2\kappa}{m}\cos^2(qa).
$$
At the zone edge the two frequencies are $\sqrt{2\kappa/m}$ and $\sqrt{2K/m}$, separated by a large forbidden frequency interval. Neighbouring cells are out of phase there. Both branches have horizontal tangents at the edge.

= Solution

For a <Hamiltonian> with a lattice-periodic potential $V(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)=V(\mathbf r)$, its simultaneous <energy eigenstates> and lattice-translation eigenstates can be chosen in the <Bloch wave> form
$$
\boxed{\psi_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)=e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r),\qquad
u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)=u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r).}
$$
Equivalently, $\psi_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)=e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}\psi_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)$. The index $n$ labels an <energy band>; <crystal momentum> $\hbar\mathbf k$ is defined modulo a <reciprocal lattice> vector. These are the content of <Bloch's theorem>.

= b
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Use one orthonormalized localized <atomic orbital> $|j\rangle$ at each position $ja$. In the nearest-neighbour <tight-binding model>, translation invariance gives the on-site energy and real <hopping integral>
$$
E_0=\langle j|H|j\rangle,\qquad
 t=\langle j|H|j+1\rangle=\langle j+1|H|j\rangle.
$$
The <Bloch state> is
$$
|k\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt N}\sum_j e^{ikja}|j\rangle.
$$
Since $H|j\rangle=E_0|j\rangle+t|j-1\rangle+t|j+1\rangle$, shifting the two summation indices gives
$$
H|k\rangle=[E_0+te^{ika}+te^{-ika}]|k\rangle,
\qquad\boxed{E(k)=E_0+2t\cos(ka).}
$$
The <hopping integral> is an energy, and its sign sets whether $k=0$ is a minimum or maximum. $E_0$ is the diagonal matrix element in the crystal Hamiltonian, which can differ from an isolated-atom energy.

The <orthogonal tight-binding approximation> matters. If raw neighbouring orbitals retain a nonzero <orbital overlap> $s$ in their normalization, projection instead gives a <generalized eigenvalue problem> and the <nonorthogonal nearest-neighbour tight-binding band> $E(k)=[E_0+2h\cos(ka)]/[1+2s\cos(ka)]$. The stated cosine band follows after orthonormalization and nearest-neighbour truncation, or after neglecting that overlap metric while retaining the hopping it motivates.

= Solution
{parent=c}

A site in a <primitive cubic lattice> has six nearest neighbours, at $\pm a\hat{\mathbf x}$, $\pm a\hat{\mathbf y}$ and $\pm a\hat{\mathbf z}$. Repeating the <Bloch state> calculation with equal <hopping integrals> $t$ along all six bonds gives
$$
\boxed{E(\mathbf k)=E_0+2t[\cos(k_xa)+\cos(k_ya)+\cos(k_za)].}
$$
The cosine sum ranges from $-3$ to $3$ within the first <Brillouin zone>. Therefore
$$
E_{\min}=E_0-6|t|,\qquad E_{\max}=E_0+6|t|,\qquad
\boxed{W=E_{\max}-E_{\min}=12|t|.}
$$
For $t<0$ the minimum is at the zone centre; for $t>0$ the maximum is there.

= d
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

For $|k_i a|\ll1$, expand each cosine to second order:
$$
E(\mathbf k)=E_0+6t-ta^2(k_x^2+k_y^2+k_z^2)+O(ta^4k^4).
$$
To this order a fixed energy fixes $k_x^2+k_y^2+k_z^2$, so the constant-energy surfaces are <spheres>. The <band effective mass> tensor satisfies
$$
(m^{*-1})_{ij}=\frac1{\hbar^2}\frac{\partial^2E}{\partial k_i\partial k_j}
=-\frac{2ta^2}{\hbar^2}\delta_{ij},\qquad
\boxed{m^*=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2ta^2}.}
$$
For bonding hopping $t<0$ this is a positive electron mass at the band bottom. If $t>0$, $k=0$ is a maximum with negative electronic mass, or positive <electron hole> mass $\hbar^2/(2ta^2)$. The spherical shape is a quadratic approximation; cubic anisotropy appears in the fourth-order terms.

= Solution
{parent=e}

Taking $E_1>0$ so that the zone centre is a minimum, the <effective mass for exponentially decaying tight-binding hopping> is
$$
\boxed{m^*(a)=\frac{\hbar^2}{4E_1a^2}e^{a/a_1}.}
$$
Its logarithmic derivative is
$$
\frac1{m^*}\frac{dm^*}{da}=\frac1{a_1}-\frac2a.
$$
The curve decreases for $a<2a_1$, has a unique minimum at $a=2a_1$, and increases for $a>2a_1$:
$$
\boxed{m^*_{\min}=\frac{\hbar^2\exp(2)}{16E_1a_1^2}.}
$$
The formal limits are an $a^{-2}$ divergence as $a\to0$ and an exponential divergence as $a\to\infty$. The small-separation extrapolation eventually invalidates the localized-orbital <tight-binding model>; the sketch describes the specified formula.

The <electronic bandwidth> is $W(a)=24E_1e^{-a/a_1}$. With $a$ reduced from $a_1$ to $0.99a_1$,
$$
\boxed{\frac{\Delta W}{W}=\frac{W(0.99a_1)}{W(a_1)}-1
=e^{0.01}-1\simeq0.01005.}
$$
Thus the <energy band> widens by approximately **1.005 percent**, or **1 percent to first order**. Compression increases the orbital coupling.

= Solution
{parent=a}

For a degenerate <Fermi gas>, the <Pauli exclusion principle> restricts thermally changed occupations to an energy shell of width $k_BT$ around the <Fermi energy>. The number of active <electrons> is of order $g(E_F)k_BT\sim Nk_BT/E_F$; each carries an excitation energy of order $k_BT$. Thus the thermal energy grows as $N(k_BT)^2/E_F$ and the <electronic heat capacity> is proportional to $T$.

More precisely, the fixed-particle-number <Sommerfeld expansion> gives
$$
U(T)-U(0)=\frac{\pi^2}{6}g(E_F)(k_BT)^2+O(T^4),\qquad
C_e=\frac{\pi^2}{3}g(E_F)k_B^2T+O(T^3).
$$
Here $g$ is the total <spin-summed density of states> for the sample. For a three-dimensional parabolic band, $g(E_F)=3N/(2E_F)$, yielding
$$
\boxed{C_e=A\frac{Nk_B^2T}{E_F},\qquad A=\frac{\pi^2}{2},\qquad k_BT\ll E_F.}
$$
The linear law concerns the electronic contribution; the low-temperature lattice <heat capacity> usually adds a $T^3$ term.

{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let $n=N/\mathcal V$ and $c_e=C_e/\mathcal V$ be the <carrier density> and electronic <heat capacity> per unit volume. In isotropic <kinetic theory>, the <angular average> $\langle v_x^2\rangle=v_F^2/3$ gives the electronic <thermal conductivity>
$$
K_e=\frac13c_ev_F\ell_Q=\frac13c_ev_F^2\tau_Q,
$$
where $\ell_Q=v_F\tau_Q$ is the <mean free path> associated with the <heat-current relaxation time>. With $v_F^2=2E_F/m^*$,
$$
K_e=\frac{2A}{3}\frac{nk_B^2T\tau_Q}{m^*}.
$$
The <Drude model> gives the <electrical conductivity> $\sigma=ne^2\tau_\sigma/m^*$, where $\tau_\sigma$ is the <momentum relaxation time>. Therefore
$$
\frac{K_e}{\sigma T}=\frac{2A}{3}\left(\frac{k_B}{e}\right)^2\frac{\tau_Q}{\tau_\sigma}.
$$
When the same approximately elastic collisions relax heat and charge currents, $\tau_Q=\tau_\sigma$. Using $A=\pi^2/2$ gives the <Wiedemann-Franz law>
$$
\boxed{K_e=L_0\sigma T,\qquad L_0=\frac{\pi^2}{3}\left(\frac{k_B}{e}\right)^2
\simeq2.44\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{W\,\Omega\,K^{-2}}.}
$$
$L_0$ is the <Lorenz number>. Degenerate electrons move at the <Fermi velocity>, not the classical thermal velocity; using classical equipartition would give the wrong coefficient.

{parent=5}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The <Wiedemann-Franz law> applies to a normal degenerate <metal> when the same itinerant <electrons> carry heat and charge, scattering is approximately elastic over the thermal shell, and the transport probability varies slowly across the energy interval $k_BT$ around the <Fermi level>. The comparison uses the electronic <thermal conductivity> $K_e$, with any lattice contribution removed.

It works particularly well as $T\to0$ when elastic impurity or defect scattering dominates. It also often holds approximately for $\Theta_D\ll T\ll T_F$, where thermally populated <phonons> act as a nearly elastic classical scattering field. Room temperature need not lie above the <Debye temperature> for every metal.

At intermediate temperatures, inelastic small-angle <electron-phonon scattering> can relax heat much more efficiently than charge. Then $\tau_Q\ne\tau_\sigma$ and $K_e/(\sigma T)$ can be smaller than $L_0$. Strong energy dependence of scattering, additional neutral heat carriers, or a transition out of the normal metallic state also invalidates the simple derivation. An observed deviation alone does not identify which assumption failed.

{parent=5}

= Solution
{parent=d}

For one conduction electron per atom, let $n_a$ be the atom density. <State counting in a Bloch band> gives the <Fermi wavevector> $k_F=(3\pi^2n_a)^{1/3}$. The <Debye model> preserves the three vibrational modes per atom by a spherical <Debye wavevector> cutoff $q_D=(6\pi^2n_a)^{1/3}$, so
$$
\boxed{q_D=2^{1/3}k_F\simeq1.26k_F.}
$$
Both wavevectors are of order the inverse atomic spacing. The numerical factor uses the spherical Debye approximation; a real zone boundary and the largest-frequency mode depend on direction and branch. Because $q_D<2k_F$, short-wavelength acoustic <phonons> can scatter <Fermi surface> electrons through substantial angles. For nearly elastic scattering within a spherical Fermi surface,
$$
q=2k_F\sin(\theta/2),\qquad1-\cos\theta=\frac{q^2}{2k_F^2}.
$$
Near room temperature, when a substantial fraction of the phonon spectrum is occupied, <electron-phonon scattering> is often the main source of <electrical resistance>. For $T\gg\Theta_D$, the phonon population and scattering rate are approximately proportional to $T$, giving $\rho\propto T$, $\sigma\propto T^{-1}$ and roughly constant $K_e$ when the <Wiedemann-Franz law> holds.

For <small-angle electron-phonon transport at low temperature>, thermally active acoustic modes instead have
$$
q_T\sim\frac{k_BT}{\hbar c_s}=q_D\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\ll k_F.
$$
They predominantly scatter forward. The factor $1-\cos\theta$ makes such collisions inefficient at relaxing an electrical current, while a phonon energy of order $k_BT$ can still substantially change an electron's thermal excitation. Heat-current relaxation therefore need not share the charge-current relaxation time. In the clean three-dimensional acoustic-phonon model this gives $\tau_Q^{-1}\propto T^3$ but $\tau_\sigma^{-1}\propto T^5$, so the phonon-limited electrical resistivity scales as $T^5$ and $K_e\propto T^{-2}$. These laws describe the regime before residual elastic scattering takes over; at sufficiently low temperature impurities give nearly constant residual resistivity and $K_e\propto T$, restoring the usual <Lorenz number>.https://home.itp.ac.ru/~grigorev/LectureSlides/MetalsLecture2.pdf[Electron-phonon scattering: distinct low-temperature thermal and electrical transport relaxation rates]{ref}

<Phonons> also transport heat themselves, with $K_{\rm ph}\simeq c_{\rm ph}c_s\ell_{\rm ph}/3$. At low temperature their population decreases and their <phonon mean free path> can become boundary- or defect-limited. At higher temperature <Umklapp scattering> can relax the phonon heat current. Such lattice conduction is an additional contribution, not the quantity constrained by the electronic <Wiedemann-Franz law>.

Write $\varepsilon_s=\epsilon_0\epsilon$ and take $e>0$, $\alpha>0$, so the p side is $x<0$. In the <depletion approximation>, mobile <electrons> and <electron holes> are negligible compared with the fixed ionized dopants. Their <charge density> is
$$
\rho(x)=e[n_D^+(x)-n_A^-(x)]\simeq e\alpha x.
$$
Applying <Poisson equation> gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{d^2V}{dx^2}=-\frac{\rho(x)}{\varepsilon_s}=-\frac{e\alpha x}{\epsilon_0\epsilon}.}
$$
Outside the <depletion region>, mobile carriers approximately neutralize the dopants, so $\rho\simeq0$ and $V''\simeq0$. Full dopant ionization alone does not remove the mobile-carrier charge: the result also requires the <depletion approximation>.

= b
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

At equilibrium, majority <electrons> undergo <diffusion> from n to p, while majority <electron holes> diffuse from p to n. Their departure exposes positive donors on the n side and negative acceptors on the p side. The resulting <electric field> points from n to p and opposes further diffusion. Equilibrium is reached when the <chemical potential> is constant across the junction. The <built-in potential> is this electrostatic adjustment, not a discontinuity of the material's <band gap>.

Since electronic band energies shift by $-eV(x)$, both the <conduction band> and <valence band> undergo <band bending> down from p to n by $eV_b$ at zero applied bias. Their separation remains the same gap. In the nondegenerate <thermal equilibrium> limit the built-in voltage can also be expressed using neutral-edge densities as $eV_b=k_BT\log(n_n/n_p)=k_BT\log(n_np_p/n_i^2)$.

Take the n-contact potential and its <electrochemical potential> as reference. Applying $V_{\rm ext}$ to the p side changes the total depletion-layer drop to
$$
\boxed{U=V_b-V_{\rm ext},\qquad
E_{c,p}-E_{c,n}=E_{v,p}-E_{v,n}=eU.}
$$
Thus $V_{\rm ext}<0$ is <reverse bias>: it increases the barrier and widens the <depletion region>. The bands have horizontal tangents at the neutral edges in this approximation and a smooth cubic bend within the linearly graded layer.

A biased junction is not in global thermal equilibrium. Its contact electron <electrochemical potentials> obey
$$
\boxed{\mu_p-\mu_n=-eV_{\rm ext}.}
$$
Under reverse bias the p-contact level is higher by $e|V_{\rm ext}|$. A single horizontal equilibrium <chemical potential> cannot describe this state; electron and hole <quasi-Fermi levels> describe the nonequilibrium populations. The sketch marks the contact levels. Detailed spatial quasi-Fermi profiles require transport and recombination information beyond the electrostatic doping profile.

= c
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

At equilibrium the <diffusion currents> and <drift currents> cancel separately for each carrier species. Electron diffusion is from n to p, while the <electric field> drives electrons from p to n; hole diffusion is from p to n, while hole drift is from n to p. The net current is zero, although these opposing microscopic fluxes are present. In conventional-current signs, both majority diffusion contributions point p to n and the balancing drift contributions point n to p.

<Forward bias>, $V_{\rm ext}>0$, reduces the barrier and enhances majority-carrier diffusion and <minority carrier> injection. The ideal low-injection <Shockley diode equation> gives
$$
\boxed{J=J_s\left[e^{eV_{\rm ext}/(k_BT)}-1\right],}
$$
where positive current is p to n. For <reverse bias>, majority diffusion is suppressed and the field sweeps thermally supplied minority carriers across the junction, giving approximately $J=-J_s$. This saturation is limited by carrier supply rather than a current proportional to the field. Depletion-layer generation, recombination and eventual <diode reverse breakdown> modify the ideal result.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Let the neutral edges be $-x_p$ and $x_n$ and take the exterior <electric field> to vanish. Integrating $dE_x/dx=\rho/\varepsilon_s=e\alpha x/\varepsilon_s$ between the edges gives $x_n^2=x_p^2$, so $x_n=x_p=w/2$. This is also the condition of equal and opposite integrated depletion charges.

A second integration with $E_x(w/2)=0$ yields
$$
\boxed{E_x(x)=\frac{e\alpha}{2\varepsilon_s}\left(x^2-\frac{w^2}{4}\right),\qquad |x|\leq\frac w2.}
$$
It is negative inside the layer, pointing from n to p, and zero at both edges. Its largest magnitude occurs at the metallurgical junction:
$$
\boxed{x=0,\qquad |E_x|_{\max}=\frac{e\alpha w^2}{8\varepsilon_s}.}
$$
For reference, choosing $V(-w/2)=0$ gives the cubic <electric potential>
$$
V(x)=\frac{e\alpha}{2\varepsilon_s}\left(\frac{w^2x}{4}-\frac{x^3}{3}+\frac{w^3}{12}\right).
$$

= e
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=e}

The <electric potential difference> is the negative integral of the field:
$$
V_b-V_{\rm ext}=-\int_{-w/2}^{w/2}E_x(x)\,dx
=\frac{e\alpha}{2\varepsilon_s}\int_{-w/2}^{w/2}\left(\frac{w^2}{4}-x^2\right)dx
=\frac{e\alpha w^3}{12\varepsilon_s}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{w(V_{\rm ext})=\left[\frac{12\epsilon_0\epsilon(V_b-V_{\rm ext})}{e\alpha}\right]^{1/3},\qquad
w(0)=\left(\frac{12\epsilon_0\epsilon V_b}{e\alpha}\right)^{1/3}.}
$$
The <linearly graded junction depletion width> has this cube-root dependence because of the linearly growing space charge: the charge on one side scales as $w^2$, the field as $w^2$, and its potential integral as $w^3$. The formula assumes $V_b-V_{\rm ext}>0$ and that the <depletion approximation> remains applicable.

{parent=6}

= Solution
{parent=f}

The positive charge per unit interface area on the n side and negative charge on the p side are
$$
Q_n=\int_0^{w/2}e\alpha x\,dx=\frac{e\alpha w^2}{8},\qquad
Q_p=-Q_n.
$$
Use the signed p-side charge $Q=Q_p$ because $V_{\rm ext}$ is the p-side voltage relative to n. Differentiating the width relation gives
$$
\frac{dw}{dV_{\rm ext}}=-\frac{w}{3(V_b-V_{\rm ext})},\qquad
C=\frac{dQ_p}{dV_{\rm ext}}
=\frac{e\alpha w^2}{12(V_b-V_{\rm ext})}.
$$
Using part (e), the positive <junction capacitance> per unit area is
$$
\boxed{C=\frac{\varepsilon_s}{w}
=\left[\frac{e\alpha\varepsilon_s^2}{12(V_b-V_{\rm ext})}\right]^{1/3}.}
$$
It decreases with increasing reverse-bias magnitude as $(V_b+|V_{\rm ext}|)^{-1/3}$. Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{C^{-3}=\frac{12(V_b-V_{\rm ext})}{e\alpha\varepsilon_s^2},}
$$
so a linear $C^{-3}$ versus voltage plot diagnoses this ideal linear grading and can determine $\alpha$.

For this <linearly graded junction capacitance>, the charge convention matters: if “stored charge” means the positive magnitude $Q_n$, then $dQ_n/dV_{\rm ext}=-\varepsilon_s/w$; the positive measured capacitance is $-dQ_n/dV_{\rm ext}=dQ_n/dV_R$ for $V_R=-V_{\rm ext}$. The total net depletion charge is zero and is not the stored terminal charge. At substantial forward bias, <minority carrier> storage and the failure of the sharp <depletion approximation> prevent extrapolation of the apparent divergence at $V_{\rm ext}=V_b$.
