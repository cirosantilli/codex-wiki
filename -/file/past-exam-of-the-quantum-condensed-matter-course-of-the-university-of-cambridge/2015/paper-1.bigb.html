In the isotropic parabolic-band approximation implied by the single supplied curvature, the <hole mass from a valence-band maximum> is
$$
m_h^*=\frac{\hbar^2}{|E''(0)|}=4.56\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\simeq0.500\,m_e.
$$
The <hole> has charge $+e$, so its cyclotron angular frequency is
$$
\boxed{\omega_c=\frac{eB}{m_h^*}=3.52\times10^{11}\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
If “frequency” means cycles per second, **$f_c=\omega_c/(2\pi)=5.59\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{Hz}$**. The curvature unit is $\mathrm{J\,m^2}$, so $\hbar^2/E''$ has mass units. The external constants used are $\hbar=1.054571800\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$, $e=1.6021766208\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$ and $m_e=9.10938356\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$.<CODATA recommended values of the fundamental physical constants 2014 by National Institute of Standards and Technology>[Reduced Planck constant, elementary charge and electron mass]{ref} A genuinely anisotropic band would require the cyclotron mass for the orbit plane, not a single arbitrary directional curvature.

Let the atomic spacing be $a$ and define the nearest-neighbour matrix elements $t_\alpha=\langle j,p_\alpha|H|j+1,p_\alpha\rangle$, using the same translated orbital convention on every site. Then
$$
E_\alpha(k)=\epsilon_\alpha+2t_\alpha\cos(ka),\qquad-\pi/a\leq k\leq\pi/a.
$$
For the <opposite sigma and pi hopping signs in a p-orbital chain>, consider $p_z$ first: neighbouring lobes above the chain have the same sign, so the in-phase Bloch sum is bonding. Thus $t_\pi<0$ and
$$
\boxed{E_{p_z}=\epsilon_z-2|t_\pi|\cos(ka):\quad\text{minimum at }k=0.}
$$
For $p_x$, the lobes facing one another have opposite signs for equal site coefficients. Bonding therefore needs alternating coefficients, giving $t_\sigma>0$ and
$$
\boxed{E_{p_x}=\epsilon_x+2|t_\sigma|\cos(ka):\quad\text{minimum at }k=\pm\pi/a.}
$$
End-on $\sigma$ overlap is normally stronger than side-on $\pi$ overlap, so the $p_x$ band is wider, with <bandwidths> $4|t_\sigma|$ and $4|t_\pi|$. The curve orientations require a stated hopping-sign convention; a Hamiltonian written with an explicit minus sign in front of the hopping uses correspondingly opposite parameter signs.<Solid State Physics by David Tong>[Chapter 2: nearest-neighbour tight binding and Bloch sums]{ref}

A hydrogenic donor has the <effective Bohr radius of a hydrogenic donor>
$$
\boxed{a_B^*=\epsilon_r\frac{m_e}{m^*}a_0=80a_0=4.23\,\mathrm{nm},}
$$
using the external $a_0=0.52917721067\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm m$.<CODATA recommended values of the fundamental physical constants 2014 by National Institute of Standards and Technology>[Bohr radius]{ref} Increasing donor density causes donor states to overlap and screens their binding, allowing a metallic impurity system rather than isolated frozen-out donors.

Using the standard approximate <Mott criterion>, $n_D^{1/3}a_B^*\simeq0.25$, gives
$$
\boxed{n_{D,c}\sim\left(\frac{0.25}{a_B^*}\right)^3\simeq2.1\times10^{23}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}=2.1\times10^{17}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}.}
$$
The factor 0.25 is an externally documented empirical criterion, not supplied exam data or an exact universal threshold.<The Mott criterion: So simple and yet so complex by Alexander Pergament and others>[Abstract and discussion of the effective-Bohr-radius criterion]{ref} An order-of-magnitude overlap argument without that prefactor would give $(a_B^*)^{-3}\simeq1.3\times10^{25}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$. Compensation, disorder and multivalley anisotropy affect a real material's transition; this estimate uses the scalar mass and permittivity actually supplied, without substituting other germanium parameters.

<Heavy fermion materials> are correlated metals whose low-energy <quasiparticles> have exceptionally large effective masses. Partly localized $f$-electron moments hybridize with broader conduction states. The <Kondo effect> and coherence below the <Kondo-lattice coherence temperature> produce a narrow low-energy band, so a small energy change corresponds to a large momentum-space state density.

For the <electronic heat capacity of a heavy Fermi liquid>, the resulting <density of states> gives a large low-<temperature> electronic specific-heat coefficient,
$$
\boxed{C_{\rm el}=\gamma T,\qquad\gamma=\frac{\pi^2k_B^2}{3}g(E_F),}
$$
and often an enhanced <magnetic susceptibility>. Here $g$ includes both spins and must be expressed per sample, mole or volume consistently with $\gamma$. A coherent Fermi-liquid regime often has $\rho=\rho_0+AT^2$ with an enhanced coefficient $A$. Above coherence, scattering by local moments can instead give a Kondo-like resistivity upturn.

The competition between moment screening and intersite magnetic interactions can yield a heavy paramagnetic metal, magnetic order, quantum-critical behaviour or unconventional <superconductivity>. Ce- and U-based compounds supply familiar examples. “Heavy” describes the emergent electronic <quasiparticles>, not the ionic mass; the simple heavy-band picture need not survive at a <quantum critical point>.<Heavy Fermions and the Kondo Lattice: A 21st Century Perspective by Piers Coleman>[Sections 1.1–1.3: heavy electrons, local moments and Kondo-lattice coherence]{ref}

= Solution

The <Hubbard model> combines hopping with a local repulsion,
$$
H=-t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,\sigma}(c_{i\sigma}^\dagger c_{j\sigma}+\mathrm{h.c.})+U\sum_i n_{i\uparrow}n_{i\downarrow},\qquad U>0.
$$
Hopping favours a broad itinerant band; the interaction penalizes opposite-spin <double occupancy>. For <uniform Stoner decoupling of the Hubbard interaction>, let $s=n_\uparrow-n_\downarrow$ be the spin imbalance per site. The interaction is $U(n^2-s^2)/4$, so polarization lowers it by $Us^2/4$, at the expense of kinetic energy.

If $D(E_F)$ is the noninteracting <density of states> **per spin per site**, small polarization costs $s^2/[4D(E_F)]$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Delta E=\frac{s^2}{4}[D(E_F)^{-1}-U]+O(s^4),\qquad UD(E_F)>1}
$$
is the mean-field ferromagnetic instability criterion. The exchange splitting is $\Delta=Us$, and the paramagnetic response is enhanced as $\chi=\chi_0/[1-UD(E_F)]$. A <density of states> including both spins changes the written factor in the criterion.

The model explains how itinerant bands can polarize without imposing pre-existing localized atomic spins. It is not a claim that every large-$U$ metal becomes ferromagnetic: near half filling the <Hubbard model> can instead form a <Mott insulator> with antiferromagnetic <superexchange> of scale $4t^2/U$. <Band filling>, <bandwidth> and fluctuations matter.<Quantum Gases by David Tong>[Section 3.6.6: the free-electron magnetic response]{ref} The interaction-energy and kinetic-cost expansion above is the stated mean-field derivation of the enhancement.<Electron correlations in narrow energy bands by John Hubbard>[Original local-repulsion model and the competition between atomic and itinerant limits]{ref}

For an electron wavepacket in one isolated band under a uniform <electric field>,
$$
\hbar\dot k=-eE,\qquad v(k)=\frac1\hbar\frac{d\epsilon}{dk}.
$$
Because $\epsilon(k+2\pi/a)=\epsilon(k)$, acceleration through successive <Brillouin zones> makes the velocity periodic rather than increasing indefinitely. For $\epsilon=\epsilon_0-2J\cos(ka)$,
$$
\boxed{\omega_B=\frac{e|E|a}{\hbar},\qquad T_B=\frac{h}{e|E|a},\qquad v(t)=\frac{2Ja}{\hbar}\sin(k_0a-\omega_Bt)}
$$
for a positive field along the chosen axis. Integration gives
$$
x(t)-x(0)=\frac{2J}{eE}[\cos(k_0a-\omega_Bt)-\cos(k_0a)].
$$
The <excursion amplitude of a Bloch-oscillating wavepacket> is $2|J|/(e|E|)$ about its mean and peak-to-peak extent $4|J|/(e|E|)$. The turning of velocity corresponds semiclassically to Bragg reflection near a zone boundary.

Coherent <Bloch oscillations> require scattering/<dephasing> times long compared with $T_B$ and a field weak enough to avoid substantial interband tunnelling. Ordinary metals usually scatter too rapidly; <semiconductor> superlattices and cold atoms in periodic optical potentials provide cleaner realizations. A completely filled band has canceling velocities, so its net current does not oscillate simply because individual wavepackets can.<Solid State Physics by David Tong>[Chapter 3: semiclassical band dynamics and Bloch oscillations]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=3}

For a spin-degenerate band, each unit cell contributes two available states. An even electron count per cell can fill an integer number of bands; **a <band insulator> additionally needs a global gap between the highest occupied and lowest unoccupied bands**. Even filling alone is not sufficient when bands overlap.

For a free two-dimensional gas with <spin degeneracy> two,
$$
n=\frac{2\pi k_F^2}{(2\pi)^2},\qquad\boxed{k_F=\sqrt{2\pi n}.}
$$
The reciprocal vectors of the periodic potential are $Q\hat{\mathbf x}$ and $Q\hat{\mathbf y}$. Its <first Brillouin zone> is the square $-Q/2\leq k_x,k_y\leq Q/2$, of side $Q$ and area $Q^2$; the real-space cell has side $a=2\pi/Q$.

At zero potential, the extended-zone <Fermi surface> is the circle of radius $k_F$. Since $\sqrt2<\alpha<2$, it crosses every square face but misses the corners. For <Fermi-surface reconstruction in a weak square potential>, weak positive potential opens gaps at the Bragg crossings: the second band has <electron pockets> at the two inequivalent edge centres $X,Y$, while unfilled regions of the first band form a <hole pocket> at the zone corner $M$. Opposite edges are identified: four edge half-pockets are two <electron pockets>, and four corner pieces are one <hole pocket>.

\Image[paper-1-fermi-pockets.png]
{title=A square Brillouin zone reconstructs the free Fermi circle into edge-centred electron pockets and a corner-centred hole pocket}
{source=paper-1-fermi-pockets.py}

For the <circular-cap carrier count in a square Brillouin zone>, put $R=k_F$ and $s=Q/(2R)=\alpha/2$. Each circular cap outside a square face has area
$$
C=R^2[\arccos s-s\sqrt{1-s^2}].
$$
No two such caps overlap in the specified interval. The total second-band electron area is $A_e=4C$. The first-band <hole> area is the square area minus the occupied central-circle area, $A_h=Q^2-\pi R^2+4C$. Including spin,
$$
\boxed{\frac{n_e}{n}=\frac4\pi[\arccos(\alpha/2)-(\alpha/2)\sqrt{1-\alpha^2/4}],\qquad\frac{n_h}{n}=\frac{\alpha^2}\pi-1+\frac{n_e}{n}.}
$$
Thus at $\alpha=\sqrt3$,
$$
\boxed{n_e=\left(\frac23-\frac{\sqrt3}\pi\right)n\simeq0.11534n,\qquad n_h=\left(\frac{3-\sqrt3}\pi-\frac13\right)n\simeq0.07027n.}
$$
The carrier densities are the electron- and <hole>-pocket counts, not the total electron density. The <carrier compensation and even band filling in a square lattice> follows because their difference obeys $n_e-n_h=n-Q^2/(2\pi^2)$, so **compensation occurs at $\alpha=\sqrt\pi$**. These area formulas are the $V_0\to0^+$ limit, before finite-gap reconstruction changes the pocket shapes.<Solid State Physics by David Tong>[Chapters 2–3: band-state counting, zone folding, nearly free electrons and holes]{ref}

The filling per real-space cell is $\nu=na^2=2\pi/\alpha^2$. In the given interval, the only even integer filling is $\nu=2$, again requiring $\alpha=\sqrt\pi$. At any other fixed filling a full gap cannot yield an ordinary spin-degenerate <band insulator> in this unchanged periodicity. In particular, the $\alpha=\sqrt3$ system retains excess electron carriers even after its <holes> disappear.

A rough gap threshold comes from comparing different symmetry directions, rather than only a direct zone-face gap. Define $E_Q=\hbar^2Q^2/(2m)$. The <zone-face two-plane-wave gap for a cosine potential> follows because each cosine couples plane waves by $-V_0/2$, giving the one-dimensional zone-edge levels $E_Q/4\mp V_0/2$. The <four-plane-wave splitting at a square-zone corner> is the sum of two such pairs. The potential is separable in $x,y$, so to first order
$$
E_{1,\max}(M)\simeq E_Q/2-V_0,\qquad E_{2,\min}(X,Y)\simeq E_Q/4+V_0/2.
$$
The <indirect gap of a separable square cosine potential> is consequently
$$
\boxed{\Delta_{\rm ind}\simeq\frac32V_0-\frac14E_Q,\qquad V_{0,\min}\sim\frac{E_Q}{6}=\frac{\hbar^2Q^2}{12m}.}
$$
At the compensated filling, this is $V_{0,\min}\sim(\pi/6)E_F^{(0)}$, with $E_F^{(0)}=\hbar^2k_F^2/(2m)$. **An insulator needs both the even filling and a positive indirect gap.** At the touching threshold the gap is zero; a positive direct gap at every Bragg crossing is not by itself sufficient. The estimate neglects higher-plane-wave corrections and is not an exact critical amplitude.

= Solution

Assume three-dimensional parabolic band edges, with density-of-states masses $m_c,m_h$ and one valley per edge, each with <spin degeneracy> two. Their effective densities of states are
$$
N_c=2\left(\frac{m_ck_BT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2},\qquad N_v=2\left(\frac{m_hk_BT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}.
$$
For the <semiconductor mass-action law>, integrating the square-root <density of states> against the Boltzmann tail gives $n=N_c e^{(\mu-E_c)/(k_BT)}$ and $p=N_v e^{(E_v-\mu)/(k_BT)}$. Multiplication cancels the <chemical potential>, giving
$$
\boxed{np=A(k_BT)^3e^{-E_g/(k_BT)},\qquad A=\frac{4(m_cm_h)^{3/2}}{(2\pi\hbar^2)^3}.}
$$
If there are additional valley degeneracies, multiply by their product; anisotropic bands use the appropriate density-of-states masses. In the later equal-mass model, $A=4m^3/(2\pi\hbar^2)^3$. No numerical mass or valley multiplicity has been invented.

When $\mu=E_c=0$, the conduction-edge occupation is exactly $1/2$, so the nondegenerate approximation for electrons fails at every positive <temperature>. Define the <normalized complete Fermi-Dirac integral>
$$
F_{1/2}(\eta)=\frac2{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^\infty\frac{\sqrt x}{1+e^{x-\eta}}\,dx,\qquad f_0=F_{1/2}(0)=(1-2^{-1/2})\zeta(3/2)\simeq0.765147.
$$
For the <carrier densities with chemical potential pinned to a band edge>, with the two specified equal masses, put $N(T)=2[mk_BT/(2\pi\hbar^2)]^{3/2}$. Direct Fermi-Dirac integration then gives
$$
\boxed{n(T)=N(T)f_0,\qquad p(T)=N(T)F_{1/2}[-E_g/(k_BT)].}
$$
Thus $n\propto T^{3/2}$ exactly within the parabolic model. At low <temperature>, $p\simeq N(T)e^{-E_g/(k_BT)}$ is thermally activated; at $k_BT\gg E_g$, $p/n\to1$ from below. Both vanish at zero <temperature> and increase at fixed <chemical potential>. The other states that pin $\mu$ can supply the compensating charge; one must not additionally impose $n=p$.

For the <Pauli reduction of the pinned-edge mass-action product>, the exact product relative to the classical law is
$$
\frac{np}{N(T)^2e^{-E_g/(k_BT)}}=f_0e^{E_g/(k_BT)}F_{1/2}[-E_g/(k_BT)]<1.
$$
It approaches $f_0$ at low <temperature> and $f_0^2\simeq0.58545$ in the formal high-<temperature> limit. The low-<temperature> activation factor is unchanged but its prefactor is reduced; the classical mass-action formula overestimates the product. The high-<temperature> limit here refers to the given unbounded parabolic bands, not a claim that real <semiconductor> band structures remain unchanged at arbitrarily high <temperature>.<Quantum Gases by David Tong>[Sections 3.1 and 3.6.1–3.6.4: density of states, Fermi occupation and Sommerfeld expansion]{ref}

Finally, for a fixed positive $\mu$, the zero-<temperature> Fermi sphere has radius $k_\mu=\sqrt{2m\mu}/\hbar$, so
$$
\boxed{n(0)=\frac{k_\mu^3}{3\pi^2}=\frac1{3\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m\mu}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2},\qquad n(T)=N(T)F_{1/2}[\mu/(k_BT)].}
$$
The low-<temperature> expansion at **fixed $\mu$** gives the <fixed-chemical-potential Sommerfeld density increase>:
$$
\boxed{n(T)=n(0)\left[1+\frac{\pi^2}{8}\left(\frac{k_BT}{\mu}\right)^2+O\!\left(\frac{k_BT}{\mu}\right)^4\right].}
$$
The <monotonic density at fixed chemical potential in a square-root density of states> initially rises quadratically from $n(0)$. For $k_BT\gg\mu$, its leading behaviour crosses to $f_0N(T)\propto T^{3/2}$. The fixed <chemical potential> is what allows the particle number to change.
