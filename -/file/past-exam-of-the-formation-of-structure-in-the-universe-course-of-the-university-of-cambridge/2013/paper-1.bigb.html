= Solution
{parent=a}

<heating and cooling balance in the interstellar medium> depends on <density>, <ionization>, radiation and shielding. Write net cooling per volume as $\mathcal C=n^2\Lambda(T)-n\Gamma$. Ultraviolet <grain photoelectric heating> is important in diffuse neutral gas; <photoionization> heats ionized regions, <cosmic rays> penetrate shielded gas, and <shock waves> from <stellar winds> and <supernovae> supply hot gas. Cooling includes collisionally excited atomic lines, fine-structure metal lines at lower <temperatures>, molecular lines in shielded clouds, and continuum radiation and dust emission where appropriate. The heating efficiency and cooling abundances matter as much as a nominal <temperature>.

A <multiphase interstellar medium> can contain a <cold neutral medium> near $10^2\ \mathrm K$, a <warm neutral medium> and <warm ionized medium> near $10^4\ \mathrm K$, a <hot ionized medium> near $10^6\ \mathrm K$, and still colder <molecular clouds>. Cold and warm atomic equilibrium branches can coexist at similar thermal <pressure> over a finite <pressure> interval; this is a calculated heating/cooling balance, not a requirement that all galactic gas have the same <pressure>.<The Neutral Atomic Phases of the ISM in the Galaxy by Mark G. Wolfire and others>[Abstract: cold and warm neutral equilibrium temperatures and coexistence between minimum and maximum pressures]{ref}

For net cooling per unit <mass> $\mathcal L=\mathcal C/\rho$, an isobaric perturbation at equilibrium obeys $c_p\partial_t\delta T=-(\partial\mathcal L/\partial T)_p\delta T$. Thus **a negative constant-pressure cooling <derivative> is thermally unstable**: a cool perturbation cools further and condenses. Sound crossing must be fast enough to maintain <pressure> balance; <thermal conduction> suppresses sufficiently short <wavelengths>. Supernova-driven motions, <magnetic pressure>, finite cooling times and interfaces make the actual medium dynamic, so equilibrium branches describe useful phases rather than a static collection of perfectly separated components.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

<differential rotation> winds a material arm because $d\Omega/dr\ne0$. A <spiral density wave> instead has a <pattern speed> different from the material speed: stars and gas can pass through its crests. Collective <self-gravity>, <orbital resonances> and shear can sustain or repeatedly amplify non-axisymmetric patterns; a visible spiral need not be a single permanent rigid material structure.

For a razor-thin gas disk, local tightly wound disturbances satisfy
$$
(\omega-m\Omega)^2=\kappa^2+c_s^2k^2-2\pi G\Sigma|k|,\qquad
\kappa^2=4\Omega^2+r\frac{d\Omega^2}{dr}.
$$
Rotation stabilizes long <wavelengths> and <pressure> stabilizes short ones. Minimizing the axisymmetric right-hand side over $|k|$ gives $|k|_*=\pi G\Sigma/c_s^2$ and
$$
\boxed{Q_g=\frac{c_s\kappa}{\pi G\Sigma}\geq1}
$$
for local axisymmetric stability. A collisionless stellar disk instead has $Q_*=\sigma_R\kappa/(3.36G\Sigma_*)$; the radial random <velocity dispersion> is not literally a gas <sound speed>.<On the Gravitational Stability of a Disk of Stars by Alar Toomre>[Abstract: collisionless axisymmetric threshold and coefficient 3.36]{ref}

**Local axisymmetric stability does not guarantee global disk stability.** Gas and stars gravitate together, thickness modifies the short-scale force, and a disk with $Q>1$ may still have spiral or bar responses. Dissipation in gas can generate <shock waves> and concentrate <star formation> near a spiral crest; stellar random motions tend to reduce the collective response.

= c
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Galactic <star formation> is limited by how much gas becomes cold, shielded and self-gravitating, and by how long it remains in that state. A useful local estimate is
$$
\dot\rho_*\simeq\epsilon_{\rm ff}\frac{\rho_{\rm sf}}{t_{\rm ff}},\qquad
t_{\rm ff}=\sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{32G\rho_{\rm sf}}}.
$$
The <star-formation efficiency per free-fall time> $\epsilon_{\rm ff}$ absorbs the fact that <turbulence>, magnetic support and <stellar feedback> prevent all eligible gas from collapsing in one <free-fall time of a uniform sphere>. Rotation and disk stability influence the supply of dense clouds, while <gravitational collapse> and <gravitational fragmentation> operate inside them.

For galaxy-averaged measurements, the <Kennicutt–Schmidt law> has $\Sigma_{\rm SFR}\propto\Sigma_g^N$, with the classic mixed spiral/starburst sample giving $N\simeq1.4$. This empirical relation was obtained using <H-alpha> and far-infrared star-formation tracers together with atomic-hydrogen and CO gas measurements.<The Global Schmidt Law in Star Forming Galaxies by Robert C. Kennicutt Jr.>[Abstract: sample, tracer combination and fitted index 1.4 plus or minus 0.15]{ref} Dust attenuation, contamination by older stellar heating, the stellar <initial mass function> and the CO-to-molecular-gas conversion must be controlled. A disk-averaged total-gas exponent is not automatically a small-scale cloud law.

Young stars then photoionize gas, drive winds and eventually inject <supernova> <energy> and <momentum>. These processes can disrupt clouds, sustain random motions or expel gas; <radiative cooling> and fresh inflow rebuild the cold supply. **Large-scale <star formation> is a regulated conversion of gas, rather than unimpeded free fall of the entire disk.** A long <gas-depletion time> $\Sigma_g/\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$ is compatible with short <galactic dynamical times> in the dense fraction.

= Solution
{parent=d}

A dense cloud core first collapses when <self-gravity> overwhelms its effective support. While radiation escapes readily, compression can remain approximately isothermal and the <Jeans mass> decreases as <density> rises, encouraging <gravitational fragmentation>. Angular <momentum> usually leads to a disk rather than unrestricted spherical infall.

As the central <optical depth> increases, cooling becomes slower than compression and <pressure> produces a <first hydrostatic core>. <molecular hydrogen> dissociation near $2000\ \mathrm K$ absorbs compressional <energy> and lowers the effective adiabatic response, permitting a <second collapse of a protostar>. After dissociation the gas stiffens and a much more compact stellar core forms. An <accretion> shock and surrounding disk then convert infall <energy> into radiation. A small central core forms before most of the envelope arrives, as demonstrated in radiation-hydrodynamic collapse calculations.<Numerical Calculations of the Dynamics of a Collapsing Proto-Star by Richard B. Larson>[Abstract: nonhomologous central core formation, subsequent envelope accretion and infrared reprocessing]{ref}

The isothermal <inside-out collapse of a singular isothermal sphere> model provides one idealized supply law, $\dot M\sim a_T^3/G$, but rotation, <magnetic fields> and an unsteady reservoir modify real <accretion>. Its <luminosity> scale is $L_{\rm acc}\sim GM\dot M/r_*$. Dust can absorb the emerging short-wavelength radiation and re-emit it in the infrared, so an embedded source need not have a directly visible stellar photosphere.

As envelope <accretion> declines, gravitational contraction becomes important on the <Kelvin-Helmholtz cooling time> $t_{\rm KH}\sim GM^2/(r_*L)$. <deuterium-burning energy-generation rate> can temporarily influence the radius; sustained core <hydrogen burning> eventually establishes the <main sequence> if the final <mass> is sufficient. A low-mass convective object approaches along a Hayashi-like track, while more massive objects need not share that track. **<accretion>, contraction and nuclear burning are distinct <energy> sources**, and a <protostar> is not generally a fixed-mass object.

= Solution
{parent=2}

**<mass> and <energy> signs.** The enclosed <mass> is $M(r)=\int_0^r4\pi s^2\rho(s)\,ds$, so $dM/dr=+4\pi r^2\rho$. The negative sign printed in that equation cannot describe positive enclosed <mass>. Use the physical sign and define <gravitational potential energy> by
$$
\Omega=-\int_0^M\frac{GM_r}{r}\,dM_r<0.
$$

**Boundary-pressure <virial theorem>.** Multiply <hydrostatic equilibrium> by $4\pi r^3$ and integrate. The left side is $[4\pi r^3p]_0^{r_0}-3\int_0^{r_0}4\pi r^2p\,dr$, while the right side is $\Omega$. The central boundary contribution vanishes when $r^3p\to0$ and the outer <pressure> is $p(r_0)=p_0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{3\int\frac p\rho\,dM+\Omega=4\pi r_0^3p_0=3p_0V}.
$$
A finite external <pressure> is compatible with a gravitationally self-contained cloud, but is not the zero-surface-force notion of isolation. External ram stress or <mass> flow would require additional terms.

**<radiation budget of monatomic cloud collapse>.** For an <ideal gas> with fixed $\gamma$, the specific <internal energy> is $u=p/[\rho(\gamma-1)]$. Therefore the <pressure> <integral> is $3(\gamma-1)U$. Cold monatomic <hydrogen> has $\gamma=5/3$ when only translational degrees of freedom matter. With negligible final surface <pressure>, $2U+\Omega=0$, so $E_f=U+\Omega=\Omega/2$. If the initial cloud is sufficiently extended and cold that $E_i\simeq0$, the emitted <energy> is
$$
\boxed{E_{\rm rad}=E_i-E_f\simeq-\frac\Omega2>0}.
$$
Being initially at rest alone does not set its <binding energy> to zero. If the initial total <energy> is appreciable, retain $E_{\rm rad}=E_i-\Omega/2$; a different <equation of state> also changes the half-energy result.

**Inside-out <accretion>.** The combination of $a_T$ and $G$ with dimensions of <mass> per time is
$$
\boxed{\dot M=C\frac{a_T^3}{G}},
$$
where <dimensional analysis> leaves the dimensionless coefficient $C$ undetermined. An isothermal gas keeps $a_T$ constant, giving constant supply in the self-similar idealization. A front at radius $a_Tt$ and an isothermal <mass> scale $M(r)\sim a_T^2r/G$ give the same scaling. The front's swept <mass> is not automatically the accreted central <mass>; the coefficient requires a dynamical similarity solution.<Self-Similar Collapse of Isothermal Spheres and Star Formation by Frank H. Shu>[Inside-out similarity model; only the dimensional scaling is required here]{ref}

**<constant-radius accretion energy budget>.** If all available <accretion> power is radiated at the fixed radius, $L_{\rm acc}=GM\dot M/r_0$. Neglecting the seed <mass>,
$$
\int L_{\rm acc}\,dt=\int_0^M\frac{GM'}{r_0}\,dM'
=\frac{GM^2}{2r_0}.
$$
This equals $-\Omega/2$ precisely when
$$
\boxed{\Omega=-\frac{GM^2}{r_0}}.
$$
The positive sign in the final printed <energy> condition is inconsistent with the earlier use of negative <gravitational potential energy>. Equivalently its positive right side must denote the binding-energy magnitude $|\Omega|$. A general structural coefficient $\Omega=-qGM^2/r_0$ leaves a mismatch unless $q=1$ under these other idealizations. Radiation trapped in the object, outflows or release over a different radius also change the budget.

**Which <temperature>?** With $M=M_s+\dot M t$ and fixed $r_0$, $L_{\rm acc}\propto M$. If this power emerges thermally from area $4\pi r_0^2$, the <accretion-powered effective-temperature scaling> is
$$
\boxed{T_{\rm eff}=\left(\frac{GM\dot M}{4\pi\sigma_{\rm SB}r_0^3}\right)^{1/4}
\ \propto\ t^{1/4}}
$$
when the seed <mass> is negligible. This is the radiating surface's <effective temperature>, inferred from the preceding accretion-<luminosity> calculation. A homologous ideal-gas interior instead has <virial temperature> $T_c\sim\mu m_HGM/(k_Br_0)\propto t$. Thus **the surface-effective and interior-virial scalings are $t^{1/4}$ and $t$, respectively**, under their stated assumptions. Inferring a detailed interior <temperature> profile would additionally require a structural and transport model.

= Solution
{parent=3}

**Growing and decaying modes.** Neglect <pressure> and insert the Einstein-de Sitter background. A trial $\Delta\propto t^p$ gives
$$
p(p-1)+\frac43p-\frac23=0,\qquad p=\frac23,-1.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Delta=A t^{2/3}+B t^{-1},\qquad
\Delta_{\rm growing}\propto R\propto(1+z)^{-1}}.
$$
The quoted growth law selects the growing mode; arbitrary initial data can also contain the decaying one. In a static homogeneous Jeans model, $\ddot\Delta=(4\pi G\bar\rho-k^2c_s^2)\Delta$, so an unstable mode grows exponentially, $e^{\sqrt{4\pi G\bar\rho-k^2c_s^2}\,t}$. Expansion introduces the $2H\dot\Delta$ damping term and a falling background <density>, yielding power-law growth instead. If the <wavevector> in the supplied <pressure> term is comoving, that term would need $R^{-2}$; the pressureless result does not depend on this convention.

**Amplitude problem.** Using the stated <recombination> epoch, matter-era growth from then until today is only about $1501$. A baryonic <density> perturbation of order $10^{-5}$ would reach merely $1.5\times10^{-2}$, far below the order-unity regime needed for nonlinear collapse. <photon> coupling also prevents <baryons> from freely collapsing before <recombination>. The observed microwave <temperature> contrast is not an exact direct measurement of <baryon> <density contrast> at every scale: acoustic transfer and gravitational <redshift> matter. The estimate expresses the insufficient growth of tiny photon-coupled baryonic seeds, not an exact conversion between the two observables.

**Coupled gravitational response.** Both pressureless components respond to the same perturbed potential. The equations are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\ddot\Delta_B+2H\dot\Delta_B&=4\pi G(\bar\rho_B\Delta_B+\bar\rho_D\Delta_D),\\
\ddot\Delta_D+2H\dot\Delta_D&=4\pi G(\bar\rho_B\Delta_B+\bar\rho_D\Delta_D).
\end{aligned}}
$$
Their difference obeys $\ddot D+2H\dot D=0$, where $D=\Delta_D-\Delta_B$. Thus $\dot D\propto R^{-2}$ and, in Einstein-de Sitter, $D=C_0+C_1t^{-1/3}=C_0+\widetilde C_1R^{-1/2}$.

**Catch-up branch and its condition.** With <dark matter> dominating the source and in its growing mode, write $\Delta_D=AR$. The full <baryon> solution is
$$
\boxed{\Delta_B=AR-C_0-\widetilde C_1R^{-1/2}}.
$$
The requested <baryon catch-up approximation after recombination> selects the branch with negligible relative decaying mode, $\widetilde C_1=0$. Defining $C_0=AR(z_0)$ then gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{\Delta_B}{\Delta_D}=1-\frac{R(z_0)}{R(z)}
=1-\frac{1+z}{1+z_0}}.
$$
This branch applies at and after $z_0$ and has $\Delta_B(z_0)=0$ and matched <baryon>/dark-matter growth velocities at that epoch. It does not follow uniquely from vanishing initial <baryon> <density contrast> alone. If both $\Delta_B$ and its initial time <derivative> vanish at $R=R(z_0)$, the <zero-initial-baryon-velocity catch-up transient> instead gives
$$
\frac{\Delta_B}{\Delta_D}=1-3x^{-1}+2x^{-3/2},\qquad x=R/R(z_0).
$$
Both approach unity for $x\gg1$. The displayed target therefore needs its stated no-relative-decaying-mode assumption; a finite <baryon> fraction modifies the transient further.

**Physical consequence.** <cold dark matter> is not held to the radiation by <Thomson scattering>, and can build gravitational wells before <baryon> decoupling, with appreciable matter-era growth after equality. Its growing perturbations then attract <baryons> once <pressure> and radiation drag become unimportant. The relative offset stays constant or decays while the common mode grows, so <baryons> catch up. **Nonlinear structure need not grow solely from the tiny baryonic perturbation inferred at decoupling.** <cold dark matter> growth during <radiation domination> is slower than the matter-era law, and the stated formula is neither a radiation-era nor a nonlinear-collapse solution.
