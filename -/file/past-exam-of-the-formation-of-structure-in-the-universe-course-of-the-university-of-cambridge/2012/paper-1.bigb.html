= Solution
{parent=a}

<gas> heats when compression, <shocks> or absorbed radiation increase its <internal energy>. Important sources include <accretion> <shocks>, <stellar winds>, <supernovae>, <photoionization>, photoelectric emission from <interstellar dust>, <cosmic rays> and <energy> deposited by <active galactic nuclei>. <photoionization> heating uses the photoelectron <energy> above the <ionization> threshold; compression contributes mechanical work rather than <photon> <absorption>.

For specific <internal energy> $u$, an idealized thermal balance is
$$
\rho\frac{Du}{Dt}=-P\nabla\cdot\mathbf v+\mathcal H-\mathcal C,
\qquad
\mathcal C=n_en_H\Lambda(T,Z).
$$
Here $\mathcal H,\mathcal C$ are powers per volume and $Z$ describes abundances. The <electron-hydrogen normalization of the cooling function> must be specified: some conventions instead factor out $n_H^2$. For a monatomic <gas> with total particle <density> $n$,
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm cool}=\frac{3nk_BT}{2n_en_H\Lambda}}
$$
when heating is negligible. Compare this with the dynamical time: rapid cooling permits loss of <pressure> support, whereas long cooling times allow a hot atmosphere to persist.

<collisional excitation> followed by <spontaneous emission> cools partially ionized <gas>; <radiative recombination> also produces continuum cooling. <metal> lines enhance cooling over substantial intermediate-temperature ranges, while <thermal bremsstrahlung> gives a roughly $\Lambda\propto T^{1/2}$ high-temperature tail. Atomic <hydrogen> cooling becomes inefficient below roughly $10^4\,\mathrm K$, where <molecular line cooling> and <interstellar dust> can be important. <inverse Compton scattering> cools hot <electrons> against a colder radiation field. A tabulated cooling curve assumes a composition and <ionization> model; <photoionization> or nonequilibrium <ionization> can change it.<Thermal radiation processes by Jelle S. Kaastra and others>[2008, §§5 and 8: emission mechanisms, cooling normalization, cooling time and limits of collisional-ionization-equilibrium curves]{ref}

<X-ray> line ratios and continuum shape constrain <temperature> and <ionization>, while the <emission measure> constrains $\int n_en_H\,dV$. Turning that into a <density> and <radiative gas cooling time> also requires a geometry; unresolved clumping, foreground <absorption> and multiphase <gas> are important controls.

= Solution

<inside-out collapse of a singular isothermal sphere> starts from an idealized static envelope,
$$
\rho(r)=\frac{a_s^2}{2\pi Gr^2},\qquad
M(r)=\frac{2a_s^2r}{G},
$$
with constant <isothermal sound speed> $a_s$. Collapse begins centrally after formation of a central <mass>. An expansion wave moves outward through the initially static envelope at the <sound speed>:
$$
\boxed{r_{\rm ew}=a_st.}
$$
“Expansion” describes the outward-moving information front; the <gas> behind it moves inward. The exterior remains close to its original $r^{-2}$ equilibrium until the wave reaches it.

In the inner region, the central <mass> dominates and <pressure> becomes less important:
$$
v_r\simeq-\sqrt{\frac{2GM_*}{r}},\qquad
\rho\simeq\frac{\dot M_*}{4\pi r^2|v_r|}\propto r^{-3/2}.
$$
The self-similar <accretion> scale is **$\dot M_*=m_0a_s^3/G$**, where the <dimensionless> coefficient $m_0$ follows from the dynamical similarity solution, not from counting material swept by the front. Indeed, the initial <mass> enclosed by the front grows at $2a_s^3/G$, and some of that material remains in the infalling envelope. For fixed <mean particle mass>, $\dot M_*\propto T^{3/2}$. The original similarity calculation establishes the static outer and free-fall inner regimes.<Self-Similar Collapse of Isothermal Spheres and Star Formation by Frank H. Shu>[1977, pp. 488–497: expansion-wave collapse after central-core formation and the two radial density asymptotes]{ref}

The model assumes <spherical symmetry>, isothermality and negligible <rotation> or magnetic stresses. An asymmetric <optically thick> <spectral line> alone does not establish infall: an <optically thin> systemic-velocity tracer and spatial maps help distinguish inward motion from <rotation>, foreground <absorption> and outflow.

= Solution

<galactic feedback> couples <star formation> and <black-hole accretion> back to the <gas> supply. Stellar radiation, winds, <supernovae> and <cosmic rays> inject <energy> and <momentum>; <active-galactic-nucleus feedback> adds radiation, jets and winds powered by <accretion>. Heating, <turbulence> or <gas> ejection can suppress later <star formation>, while compression can sometimes promote it. Negative feedback permits self-regulation rather than unrestricted <gas> consumption.

Define the <mass-loading factor> $\eta=\dot M_{\rm out}/\dot M_*$. If the launch speed scales with the <escape speed> and the injected <energy> per stellar <mass> is fixed, <conservation of energy> suggests $\eta\propto v_{\rm esc}^{-2}$; fixed injected <momentum> instead suggests $\eta\propto v_{\rm esc}^{-1}$. Coupling efficiencies, radiative losses and recycling change these ideal scalings. Thermal <energy> injected into dense <gas> can radiate away before driving an outflow, so an <energy> budget alone does not demonstrate effective feedback.

Radio-filled <X-ray> cavities provide a mechanical diagnostic. For a cavity containing a relativistic fluid with $\gamma=4/3$, its <enthalpy> is $\gamma PV/(\gamma-1)\simeq4PV$; dividing by an estimated age gives a mechanical-power estimate. <Chandra X-ray Observatory> observations of the <Perseus cluster> revealed fronts and ripples associated with radio bubbles, interpreted as weak <shocks> and <pressure> waves that can transport jet <energy> into the cooling atmosphere.<A deep Chandra observation of the Perseus cluster: shocks and ripples by A. C. Fabian and others>[2003, abstract: radio bubbles, weak-shock fronts, ripples and the proposed heating/cooling balance]{ref}

The test requires ambient <density> and <temperature> estimates, radio/<X-ray> spatial comparison, projected cavity volumes and an age model. Brightness edges must be distinguished from <absorption> and cool rims; wave <energy> is not automatically thermalized locally. These controls determine whether inferred mechanical heating can actually offset <radiative cooling>.

= Solution
{parent=d}

<cold dark matter> is nonrelativistic and effectively collisionless on the scales of interest. Its small <velocity dispersion> limits <free streaming>, allowing small-scale gravitational wells to survive. <baryons> coupled to <photons> undergo acoustic motion and damping before <recombination>; <dark matter> does not share that <photon> <pressure>, and provides potential wells into which <baryons> can later fall.

For a subhorizon pressureless perturbation, once a Newtonian treatment is applicable,
$$
\ddot\delta+2H\dot\delta-4\pi G\bar\rho_m\delta=0.
$$
During <radiation domination> the matter contribution to the background gravity is small; neglecting it gives $\delta\simeq C_1+C_2\log t$. This slow subhorizon growth is the <Mészáros effect>. During <matter domination>,
$$
\boxed{\delta=C_+a+C_-a^{-3/2}}
$$
in an <Einstein-de Sitter universe>. Once $\delta$ approaches unity, linear theory fails and collapse, <virialization> and merging become important.

Modes entering the horizon during <radiation domination> therefore grow less before equality than longer modes entering later. This scale dependence is encoded in a <transfer function>, with a schematic linear <matter power spectrum> $P(k,a)=A k^{n_s}T^2(k)D^2(a)$. <cold dark matter> supports hierarchical assembly; a sufficiently large warm- or hot-dark-matter free-streaming length suppresses small-scale structure. Superhorizon <density contrasts> change under a <gauge transformation in cosmological perturbation theory>, so the subhorizon growth laws should not be applied indiscriminately outside the horizon. The original cold-dark-matter formation model explicitly connects weak <free streaming> to the spectrum that seeds galaxies and clusters.<Formation of galaxies and large-scale structure with cold dark matter by George R. Blumenthal and others>[1984, Nature 311, pp. 517–525, abstract: collisionless cold particles, retained fluctuations and the post-recombination spectrum]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=2}

For the <radial epicyclic frequency>, introduce the <effective potential> at fixed <specific angular momentum> $l$: $\Phi_{\rm eff}=\Phi+l^2/(2r^2)$. A <circular orbit> at $r_c$ satisfies $\Phi'(r_c)=l^2/r_c^3=r_c\Omega^2(r_c)$. A small radial displacement $\xi$ obeys $\ddot\xi=-\Phi_{\rm eff}''(r_c)\xi$. Differentiate at fixed $l$ before inserting the circular-orbit relation:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\kappa^2&=\frac{3l^2}{r_c^4}+\Phi''(r_c)
=3\Omega^2+\frac{d}{dr}(r\Omega^2)\\
&=\boxed{4\Omega^2+2r\Omega\Omega'
=4\Omega^2\left(1+\frac r{2\Omega}\Omega'\right)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
For a <flat rotation curve>, $\Omega\propto r^{-1}$, so **$\kappa=\sqrt2\,\Omega$**.

In a perturbation proportional to $\exp[i(\int k\,dr+n\phi-\omega t)]$, $n$ is the integer azimuthal mode number, giving $|n|$ arms for a nonaxisymmetric pattern. Its angular <pattern speed> is $\Omega_p=\omega/n$ for a real <frequency> and $n\ne0$; $a_0$ is the unperturbed <gas> <sound speed>, or the specified effective fluid pressure-response speed. For growing patterns the angular phase speed uses $\operatorname{Re}\omega/n$. Axisymmetric modes have $n=0$: their <pattern speed> is undefined, and one must use $(\omega-n\Omega)^2\to\omega^2$, not set the pattern-speed expression to zero.

Define the <Toomre parameter> and <Toomre wavenumber> by
$$
\boxed{k_T=\frac{\kappa^2}{2\pi G\sigma},\qquad
Q=\frac{\kappa a_0}{\pi G\sigma}.}
$$
Then $q=|k|/k_T$ gives
$$
\frac{\omega^2}{\kappa^2}
=1-q+\frac{Q^2q^2}{4}
=1-\frac1{Q^2}+\frac{Q^2}{4}
\left(q-\frac2{Q^2}\right)^2.
$$
For $a_0>0$, the minimum is negative precisely when **$Q<1$**. Thus this condition is necessary and sufficient for an exponentially growing axisymmetric mode within the given local razor-thin fluid model; $Q=1$ is marginal. It is not a guarantee against global or nonaxisymmetric instabilities, and the collisionless stellar-disk criterion has a different pressure-response normalization.

The <Toomre unstable band and fastest growth> follow by minimizing $\omega^2$:
$$
\boxed{|k|_*=\frac{\pi G\sigma}{a_0^2}
=\frac{2k_T}{Q^2},\qquad
\lambda_*=\frac{2a_0^2}{G\sigma},\qquad
\gamma_*=\kappa\sqrt{Q^{-2}-1}.}
$$
Here the growing branch has $\omega=i\gamma_*$ under the stated Fourier convention. Both signs of the radial <wave number> have the same <growth rate>. The unstable band is $q_-<q<q_+$, where $q_\pm=2(1\pm\sqrt{1-Q^2})/Q^2$.

For the <minimum Toomre parameter in an exponential flat-rotation disk> with constant $a_0$, put $x=r/r_0$:
$$
Q(r)=Q_0\frac{e^{x-1}}x,\qquad
Q_0=\frac{\sqrt2\,\Omega_0a_0}{\pi G\sigma_0}.
$$
The <logarithmic derivative> is $d\log Q/dx=1-1/x$, so the unique minimum occurs at $r=r_0$. Both $Q\to\infty$ as $r\downarrow0$ and $Q\to\infty$ as $r\to\infty$. Hence inner and outer regions are stable within this idealized <rotation> law, even when an intermediate annulus is unstable. The whole disk is strictly stable in this local axisymmetric test if
$$
\boxed{\Omega_0>\frac{\pi G\sigma_0}{\sqrt2\,a_0}.}
$$
Equality makes the ring at $r_0$ marginal. The central conclusion uses the assumed <flat rotation curve> all the way inward; real central <rotation> laws need not retain that limit.

= Solution
{parent=3}

The normalized <cycloid> has
$$
\frac{R_s}{R_m}=\frac{1-\cos\eta}{2},\qquad
\frac{t}{t_m}=\frac{\eta-\sin\eta}{\pi}.
$$
The initially expanding perturbation reaches maximum radius at $\eta=\pi$, $t=t_m$, then formally collapses to zero radius at $\eta=2\pi$, $t=2t_m$. A finite virialized object is assigned the conventional formation epoch **$t_f=2t_m$**; random motions replace the pressureless point-collapse singularity. The <cycloid> alone does not describe that relaxation.

\Image[paper-1-collapse-history.png]
{title=Spherical-collapse history}

For the standard top-hat <virialization> estimate, write $U=-C_GGM^2/R$ and retain the same gravitational structure factor $C_G$ at turnaround and final equilibrium. The coherent <kinetic energy> vanishes at turnaround, whereas the <virial theorem> gives $2K_v+U_v=0$ in the final isolated equilibrium. <conservation of energy> gives
$$
U_m=K_v+U_v=\frac{U_v}{2},
\qquad\boxed{R_v=R_m/2}.
$$
The final <density> is eight times the turnaround <density>. Meanwhile $\bar\rho\propto a^{-3}\propto t^{-2}$, so $\bar\rho(t_f)=\bar\rho(t_m)/4$. The <Einstein-de Sitter virial overdensity> follows by combining these factors with the supplied turnaround contrast:
$$
\boxed{\Delta_f=8\times4\left(\frac{3\pi}{4}\right)^2
=18\pi^2\simeq177.65\approx200,}
\qquad
\boxed{\rho_v=\Delta_f\rho_0(1+z_f)^3.}
$$
The value 200 is a convenient rounded overdensity convention, not exactly the Einstein-de Sitter result.

The <virial radius of a dark-matter halo> follows from $M_v=4\pi R_v^3\rho_v/3$:
$$
\boxed{R_v=
\left(\frac{3M_v}{4\pi\Delta_f\rho_0}\right)^{1/3}
\frac1{1+z_f}.}
$$
The <hydrostatic temperature of an inverse-square halo> follows from <hydrostatic equilibrium> and the <ideal gas law>:
$$
\frac{dP}{dr}=-\rho_g\frac{GM(r)}{r^2},\qquad
P=\frac{\rho_gk_BT_v}{\mu}.
$$
Since $\rho_g\propto r^{-2}$ and $M(r)=M_vr/R_v$, cancellation of $\rho_g/r$ gives $2k_BT_v/\mu=GM_v/R_v$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{T_v=\frac{GM_v\mu}{2k_BR_v}
=\frac{GM_v^{2/3}\mu}{2k_B}
\left(\frac{4\pi\Delta_f\rho_0}{3}\right)^{1/3}(1+z_f).}
$$
Here $\mu$ is a dimensionful <mean particle mass>, as specified in the paper; a <dimensionless> <mean molecular weight> would need multiplication by a particle-mass unit. The isothermal interior is a further halo idealization, not an exact continuation of the uniform top-hat profile used in the virial-overdensity estimate. Changing its gravitational structure factor in a literal <energy> comparison would change the turnaround-radius relation.

The <formation redshift from halo mean density> uses the cluster data, which imply $\rho_v=3M_v/(4\pi R_v^3)=5.37148\times10^{13}M_\odot\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-3}$. Thus, using the requested rounded convention,
$$
\boxed{1+z_f=\left(\frac{3M_v}{4\pi R_v^3\Delta_f\rho_0}\right)^{1/3}
=1.91908,\qquad z_f\simeq0.92\quad(\Delta_f=200).}
$$
Using $18\pi^2$ instead gives $z_f\simeq1.00$. In the Einstein-de Sitter time scaling the rounded-convention result is $t_f/t_0=(1+z_f)^{-3/2}\simeq0.376$. Directly from the <mass> and radius,
$$
\boxed{T_v=2.34478\times10^8
\left(\frac{\mu}{m_p}\right)\mathrm K.}
$$
For the estimate, take <mean particle mass> $\mu=0.6m_p$, appropriate approximately to a <hydrogen>-<helium> mixture with complete <ionization>. This gives **$T_v\simeq1.41\times10^8\,\mathrm K$**, or $k_BT_v\simeq12.1\,\mathrm{keV}$. The composition choice is additional input, not a value supplied by the examination; the boxed expression retains the result for any $\mu$.
