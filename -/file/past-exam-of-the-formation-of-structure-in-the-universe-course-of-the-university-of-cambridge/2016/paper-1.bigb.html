= Solution

Write $A=\lambda\beta\ell_E$, where $\ell_E=L_{\rm Edd}/M_{\rm BH}$. For ionized <hydrogen> with <Thomson scattering> opacity, radiation force on one electron-proton pair balances gravity when
$$
\frac{\sigma_T L}{4\pi r^2c}=\frac{GM_{\rm BH}m_p}{r^2},
\qquad
\ell_E=\frac{4\pi Gm_pc}{\sigma_T}.
$$
The mass-loading coefficient $A$ has units of inverse time. With the stated neglect of returned and directly accreted gas, <mass conservation> gives
$$
\dot M_g=\Phi-\epsilon M_g-A M_s,\qquad
\dot M_s=\epsilon M_g.
$$
At a specified stellar mass, the <gas-balance nullcline> is
$$
M_g^{\rm eq}(M_s)=\frac{\Phi-A M_s}{\epsilon}.
$$
A positive reservoir exists only below the limiting mass
$$
\boxed{M_{s,\max}=\frac{\Phi}{A}
=\frac{\Phi\sigma_T}{4\pi Gm_pc\,\lambda\beta}.}
$$
Equality has zero gas, so this is the supremum of masses with a positive instantaneous <equilibrium>. For $\Phi,\epsilon,A>0$, the corresponding gas fraction is
$$
\boxed{f_{\rm gas}(M_s)=
\frac{\Phi-A M_s}{\Phi+(\epsilon-A)M_s}
=\left[1+\frac{\epsilon M_s}{\Phi-A M_s}\right]^{-1},
\quad0\leq M_s<M_{s,\max}.}
$$
It decreases from one to zero: the derivative is $-\epsilon\Phi/[\Phi+(\epsilon-A)M_s]^2<0$. This is a <gas regulator> with an outflow increasing with the stellar mass through the assumed black-hole scaling.

There is an important time-dependence qualification. A positive gas mass cannot remain exactly constant forever while the stellar mass continues to increase and $M_{\rm BH}=\beta M_s$ is maintained: differentiating $\dot M_g=0$ would require $A\epsilon M_g=0$. The expression above is the instantaneous <equilibrium>, or a quasisteady approximation when gas relaxation is fast relative to stellar-mass evolution. The only exact <fixed point> of the coupled idealized equations is $(M_g,M_s)=(0,\Phi/A)$. It is not a sustained star-forming state.

Above the limiting mass, $\dot M_g<0$ for every nonnegative reservoir: the assumed Eddington-driven wind already demands more than the incoming supply, even before <star formation> consumes gas. The reservoir is depleted and <star formation> is quenched. The equations must cease to apply when gas runs out; one cannot continue to negative gas mass or power an indefinitely Eddington-luminous nucleus without fuel. At fixed $M_s>M_{s,\max}$ the depletion time from $M_{g,0}$ is
$$
t_{\rm dep}=\epsilon^{-1}\ln\left(1+
\frac{\epsilon M_{g,0}}{A M_s-\Phi}\right).
$$
Allowing $M_s$ to grow only strengthens the initial depletion tendency. If $\lambda\beta=0$, this particular feedback threshold is absent.

Distinguish the rest-mass supply $\dot M_{\rm in}$ from the actual black-hole mass growth. With the usual radiative-efficiency definition,
$$
L=\epsilon\dot M_{\rm in}c^2,\qquad
\dot M_{\rm BH}=(1-\epsilon)\dot M_{\rm in}.
$$
At the Eddington limit the growth is exponential:
$$
\frac{\dot M_{\rm BH}}{M_{\rm BH}}
=\frac{1-\epsilon}{\epsilon}
\frac{4\pi Gm_p}{\sigma_e c}
=\frac1{t_{\rm Sal}},\qquad
t_{\rm Sal}=\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}
\frac{\sigma_e c}{4\pi Gm_p}.
$$
The available time is the difference of the supplied ages, **$\Delta t=650\ {\rm Myr}$**; no new cosmological age-redshift relation is needed.

Using the printed $\sigma_e=6.6\times10^{-29}\ {\rm m^2}$, together with $G=6.67430\times10^{-11}\ {\rm m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}$, $m_p\simeq1.67262\times10^{-27}\ {\rm kg}$, $c=299792458\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}$ and a Julian year of $31557600\ {\rm s}$, gives
$$
t_E=\frac{\sigma_e c}{4\pi Gm_p}\simeq446.94\ {\rm Myr},
\qquad t_{\rm Sal}\simeq49.66\ {\rm Myr}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{M_{{\rm BH},0,\min}
=(2\times10^9M_\odot)e^{-650/49.66}
\simeq4.1\times10^3M_\odot.}
$$
This is about $13.09$ e-folds. The constants just listed are evaluation constants, not additional observational inputs asserted to be on the paper.

Sometimes an elementary growth convention identifies $\dot M_{\rm BH}$ directly with $L/(\epsilon c^2)$ and drops the radiated fraction of the incoming rest mass. That approximation instead gives $t_{\rm Sal}\simeq44.69\ {\rm Myr}$ and $M_0\simeq9.7\times10^2M_\odot$. The first result retains the physically distinct supplied and retained masses; the convention must be stated because exponential growth magnifies this difference.

The main implication is already severe in the optimistic calculation: a low-mass stellar-remnant seed has insufficient time under the specified efficiency and strictly Eddington-limited <accretion>. A heavier seed, earlier formation, a different efficiency, mergers or episodes beyond that idealized <luminosity> limit can relax the inference. Any <duty cycle> below unity or sub-Eddington episodes require a larger initial mass within the adopted model. The reported high-redshift quasar motivating this calculation was identified spectroscopically at $z=7.085$; its published age estimate need not replace the ages supplied here.<A luminous quasar at a redshift of z = 7.085 by Daniel J. Mortlock and collaborators>[2011 abstract, redshift and inferred black-hole mass]{ref}

Only the <photon> energy above the <hydrogen> <ionization> threshold initially becomes <photoelectron> <kinetic energy>. The stated mean therefore supplies
$$
E_{\rm heat}=\langle h\nu\rangle-h\nu_{\rm Lye}=k_BT_*.
$$
Let $\mathcal R$ be the local number of <ionizations> per volume per time. <Photoionization equilibrium> makes this equal to the recombination rate. If $\langle E_{\rm rec}(T)\rangle$ is the mean <kinetic energy> removed per recombination, thermal balance reduces to
$$
\mathcal R k_BT_*=\mathcal R\langle E_{\rm rec}(T)\rangle,
\qquad
\boxed{\langle E_{\rm rec}(T_{\rm eq})\rangle=k_BT_*.}
$$
Neither the <ionization> threshold nor the density remains in this event-by-event balance. The threshold energy is stored as atomic <ionization energy> and returned in recombination radiation; it is not a net thermal input. This separation between <photoelectron> heating and <kinetic energy> lost by recombining <electrons> is the relevant cooling balance.<Heating and Cooling of HII regions by Ian Czekala>[Heating by photoionization and Recombination radiation sections]{ref}

In the elementary mean-thermal-energy approximation, a captured <electron> removes $\tfrac32k_BT$. This gives the concise estimate
$$
\boxed{T_{\rm eq}\simeq\frac23T_*
\quad\text{if }\langle E_{\rm rec}\rangle=\tfrac32k_BT.}
$$
That coefficient is a stated approximation, not a universal recombination identity. Real recombination weights the <electron> distribution by its velocity-dependent capture rate. If the chosen cooling model instead writes $\langle E_{\rm rec}\rangle=\eta_{\rm rec}(T)k_BT$, the precise answer is $\eta_{\rm rec}(T_{\rm eq})T_{\rm eq}=T_*$. The source supplies no recombination cooling coefficient or capture kernel from which a unique more accurate coefficient can be computed. Its emitted-photon average is also being used as the average of the absorbed ionizing <photons>; spectral filtering would require a different weighted average.

Use the supplied flat radiation-era background equations. Write $\overline\rho R^4=C$, with $C$ constant. On the expanding branch,
$$
\dot R^2=\frac{8\pi GC}{3R^2},\qquad
R\,\dot R=\sqrt{\frac{8\pi GC}{3}}.
$$
Choosing the origin at the big bang gives
$$
R\propto t^{1/2},\qquad H=\frac{\dot R}{R}=\frac1{2t},
\qquad
\overline\rho=\frac{3}{32\pi Gt^2}.
$$
The supplied perturbation equation consequently becomes
$$
\ddot\Delta+\frac1t\dot\Delta=\frac{\Delta}{t^2}.
$$
A trial $\Delta=t^p$ gives $p(p-1)+p=1$, so $p=\pm1$. Thus **both independent modes are**
$$
\boxed{\Delta(t)=C_+t+C_-t^{-1}.}
$$
The growing mode is **$\Delta_{\rm grow}\propto t\propto R^2$**, and the other decays as $t^{-1}$. At sufficiently late times any nonzero growing-mode coefficient dominates while <linearization> remains valid.

The calculation follows the stated differential equation, including its coefficient $32\pi G/3$. The name Einstein-de Sitter ordinarily denotes a flat pressureless matter universe, whose growth law is different; here the explicit radiation scaling fixes the intended background. Nor does this scale-free equation describe every subhorizon radiation perturbation: including a pressure-gradient term introduces acoustic behavior. No wavelength-independent claim beyond the supplied model is needed.

For the <binding energy of a truncated singular isothermal sphere>, build the mass distribution from concentric shells. Its <enclosed mass> and mass element are
$$
M(r)=\int_0^r4\pi s^2\rho(s)\,\mathrm ds
=\frac{2k}{G}r,\qquad
\mathrm dM=\frac{2k}{G}\,\mathrm dr,
\qquad M_0=\frac{2kr_0}{G}.
$$
The interaction of a newly added shell with the interior contributes $-GM(r)\,\mathrm dM/r$. Counting each pair once,
$$
W=-\int_0^{r_0}\frac{GM(r)}r\,\mathrm dM(r)
=-\frac{4k^2}{G}\int_0^{r_0}\mathrm dr
=-\frac{4k^2r_0}{G}.
$$
Eliminating $k$ gives
$$
\boxed{W=-\frac{GM_0^2}{r_0}.}
$$
The central density is singular, but the mass and this energy integral are finite. The result assumes the stated truncation and no additional gravitating component.

The <gravitational potential of a truncated singular isothermal sphere> is a separate quantity from this shell-assembly energy allocation. With zero potential at infinity, both interior and exterior shells contribute:
$$
\Phi_{\rm grav}(r)
=-G\left[\frac{M(r)}r+\int_r^{r_0}\frac{\mathrm dM(s)}s\right]
=-2k\left[1+\ln\frac{r_0}r\right],\quad0<r\leq r_0.
$$
It matches $-GM_0/r_0=-2k$ at the edge. The equivalent total energy is $W=\tfrac12\int\rho\Phi_{\rm grav}\,\mathrm dV$; the half prevents double counting. By contrast, $\int\rho[-\Phi_{\rm grav}]\,\mathrm dV$ counts the escape work of test parcels in a fixed potential and equals $-2W$. Confusing these quantities would change a local feedback threshold.

Introduce the constant $\nu>0$ by $\psi(r,t)=\nu\rho_g(r,t)$, and set $q=\gamma E_{\rm SN}$, the injected energy per unit mass of newly formed <stars>. Before a local wind switches on, no return or matter exchange implies
$$
\rho_g(r,t)=\rho_0(r)e^{-\nu t},\qquad
\rho_s(r,t)=\rho_0(r)(1-e^{-\nu t}).
$$
In this <independent-volume supernova quenching> model, the accumulated injection per volume is
$$
u_{\rm SN}(r,t)=\int_0^t\gamma E_{\rm SN}\psi(r,t')\,\mathrm dt'
=q\rho_0(r)(1-e^{-\nu t}).
$$
For any specified positive <binding energy> per unit remaining gas mass $B(r)$, the local threshold is
$$
u_{\rm SN}=\rho_g B(r)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\boxed{Q(t)\equiv q(e^{\nu t}-1)=B(r).}
$$
Gas conversion must be included in this comparison; replacing the integrated <star formation> by its initial rate times $t$ is only a short-time approximation.

For a <quenching front in a fixed galactic potential>, take $B(r)=-\Phi_{\rm grav}(r)=2k[1+\ln(r_0/r)]$. This treats the gravitating profile as fixed: newly formed <stars> initially replace gas at the same radius, and later changes of the potential caused by expelled gas are neglected. Gas first becomes unbound at the outer edge, at
$$
t_{\rm start}=\nu^{-1}\ln\left(1+\frac{2k}{q}\right).
$$
Thereafter the inner edge of the quenched exterior region is
$$
\boxed{r_{\rm quench}(t)
=r_0\exp\left[1-\frac{q}{2k}(e^{\nu t}-1)\right],
\qquad t\geq t_{\rm start}.}
$$
There is no quenched radius inside the <galaxy> before this onset. The remaining star-forming region is $0<r<r_{\rm quench}$; the front moves inward. The ideal logarithmically divergent central potential delays complete removal to infinite time in this fixed-profile model. It should not be interpreted as a physical unresolved central core.

The <local binding-energy allocation in galactic feedback> also admits a different convention, because it calls $\phi$ an energy of a volume element without specifying how the nonlocal self-gravitating energy is assigned to that element. If the intended prescription is instead the shell-assembly contribution from part (a),
$$
\phi_{\rm shell}=-\frac{GM_0(r)}r\rho_g=-2k\rho_g,
$$
then $B_{\rm shell}=2k$ at every radius. The same time $t_{\rm start}$ applies everywhere: **all shells quench simultaneously**, and there is no continuously propagating radial front. This is a shell-energy toy model, not the escape potential including exterior shells.

Both results follow from explicit definitions. A convention assigning $\tfrac12\rho_g\Phi_{\rm grav}$ locally replaces $B$ by $B/2$ in $Q(t)=B(r)$; it is again an energy allocation rather than test-parcel escape. Updating the potential after gas loss or choosing a fixed initial-gas binding budget instead of the remaining-gas budget would require an additional closure. The stated independent-volume assumptions do not uniquely choose between these. The displayed escape-potential result and shell prescription must therefore not be presented as interchangeable universal laws.

For the shallower initial density profile,
$$
M_0(r)=\frac{k'}G r^2,\qquad
M_0=\frac{k'r_0^2}{G}.
$$
Including outer shells, the initial <gravitational potential> is
$$
\Phi_{\rm grav}(r)
=-G\left[\frac{M_0(r)}r+\int_r^{r_0}\frac{\mathrm dM_0(s)}s\right]
=-k'(2r_0-r).
$$
Its central value is finite. With the same remaining-gas escape criterion and fixed-potential approximation as above,
$$
\boxed{r_{\rm quench}(t)
=2r_0-\frac{q}{k'}(e^{\nu t}-1).}
$$
This front is inside the <galaxy> only when $k'r_0\leq Q(t)\leq2k'r_0$. Thus
$$
t_{\rm start}=\nu^{-1}\ln\left(1+\frac{k'r_0}{q}\right),\qquad
t_{\rm all}=\nu^{-1}\ln\left(1+\frac{2k'r_0}{q}\right).
$$
Quenching still moves **from the outside inward**, but now reaches the centre in a finite time. The contrast with the $r^{-2}$ escape model is the finite central potential, not a reversed gravitational force.

Under the alternative <shell-energy quenching prescription>, the conclusion is different:
$$
B_{\rm shell}(r)=\frac{GM_0(r)}r=k'r,\qquad
\boxed{r_{\rm quench}^{\rm shell}(t)=\frac q{k'}(e^{\nu t}-1).}
$$
Here the quenched region is inside the front and grows **from the centre outward**, unlike the simultaneous quenching of the isothermal shell-allocation model. Its edge reaches $r_0$ at $\nu^{-1}\ln(1+k'r_0/q)$. This change of direction is a consequence of the chosen local energy model: $GM(r)/r$ increases with radius for this profile, whereas the true binding potential including all outer shells always becomes shallower outward. The source does not identify which local allocation it intends; keeping the conventions explicit resolves the different conditional predictions without discarding the exterior-shell potential.

To test <radial stability of an axisymmetric circular orbit>, let $\Phi(r)$ be the time-independent <gravitational potential> per unit stellar mass in the midplane. Axial symmetry conserves the <specific angular momentum> $j=r^2\dot\varphi$. The radial equation is
$$
\ddot r=\frac{j^2}{r^3}-\Phi'(r)
=-\frac{\mathrm d\Phi_{\rm eff}}{\mathrm dr},
\qquad
\Phi_{\rm eff}(r)=\Phi(r)+\frac{j^2}{2r^2}.
$$
A <circular orbit> at $r_0$ obeys $j_0^2=r_0^3\Phi'(r_0)$. Choose the guiding <circular orbit> with the same conserved <angular momentum> as the perturbed <star>. Setting $r=r_0+x$ and expanding at fixed $j=j_0$ gives
$$
\ddot x=
\left[-\frac{3j_0^2}{r_0^4}-\Phi''(r_0)\right]x+O(x^2)
=-\kappa^2x+O(x^2),
$$
where the <radial epicyclic frequency> is
$$
\boxed{\kappa^2=\Phi''(r_0)+\frac3{r_0}\Phi'(r_0)
=4\Omega_0^2+
r_0\left.\frac{\mathrm d\Omega^2}{\mathrm dr}\right|_{r_0}
=\left.\frac1{r^3}\frac{\mathrm d j_c^2}{\mathrm dr}\right|_{r_0}.}
$$
Here $\Omega^2(r)=\Phi'(r)/r$ and $j_c=r^2\Omega$ refer to the family of <circular orbits>. They are differentiated as functions of radius only after the perturbation equation has been expanded at conserved stellar $j$.

For a radially stable <circular orbit>, $\kappa^2>0$, so to linear order
$$
\boxed{x(t)=C\cos(\kappa t)+D\sin(\kappa t).}
$$
Thus the orbit performs small simple harmonic radial oscillations about its guiding radius.

\Image[paper-1-epicycle.png]
{title=Original linear epicycle calculation for stable circular motion with a flat circular-speed curve}

Symmetry alone is insufficient to guarantee <stability>: $\kappa^2<0$ gives exponentially growing displacement, and $\kappa^2=0$ is marginal. The intended statement assumes a stable unperturbed orbit.

For a Kepler potential $\kappa=\Omega$; for a flat circular-speed curve $\kappa=\sqrt2\,\Omega$; for <solid-body rotation> $\kappa=2\Omega$. Conservation of $j$ also gives $\dot\varphi\simeq\Omega_0(1-2x/r_0)$, explaining the accompanying azimuthal epicycle rather than a purely radial motion in the inertial plane.

**A <protostar> forms when a self-gravitating cloud fragment can contract faster than pressure support and energy loss can arrest it.** A useful local scale is the <Jeans length>, $\lambda_J\sim c_s/\sqrt{G\rho}$, with characteristic <Jeans mass> $M_J\sim c_s^3/(G^{3/2}\rho^{1/2})$ and free-fall time
$$
t_{\rm ff}=\sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{32G\rho}}
$$
for an initially uniform sphere. Geometry, <turbulence> and magnetic support change the detailed threshold; none of these estimates asserts that every cloud contracts as a uniform sphere.

While cooling is efficient, compression is nearly isothermal. Then $M_J$ decreases as the density rises, allowing fragmentation. Rotation becomes increasingly important during contraction because a parcel conserving <specific angular momentum> $j$ encounters a centrifugal scale $r_c\sim j^2/(GM)$. The resulting <accretion disc> must transport <angular momentum> before matter can reach the central object. Infall, a dense accreting interior and an outer envelope can therefore coexist; a <protostar> is not initially an isolated fixed-mass <star>.

Increasing <optical depth> eventually slows <radiative cooling>. The central gas then becomes less compressible and can form a pressure-supported first core. <Molecular hydrogen> dissociation absorbs compressional energy and can trigger a second collapse before a compact stellar core is established. Subsequent <accretion> supplies a <luminosity> of order
$$
L_{\rm acc}\sim\frac{GM\dot M}{R}.
$$
The retained fraction depends on shock and disc radiation, so this expression is an energy scale rather than a guaranteed <luminosity> all emerging from the stellar surface.

A central accreting core can be nearly hydrostatic on its short sound-crossing time while its mass, <entropy> and radius evolve on longer times. A low-mass object is often largely convective because of its opacity and steep <radiative temperature gradient>; deuterium burning can temporarily contribute to support and moderate contraction. Neither <convection> nor the timing of deuterium ignition is universal across all masses and <accretion> histories.

When the main <accretion> phase ends, gravitational contraction remains an energy source. The <virial theorem> explains how contraction both heats the interior and supplies radiation: for an ideal-gas-supported object with negligible surface-pressure correction, $2U+W\simeq0$, so only part of the released <binding energy> appears as increased <thermal energy>. The corresponding Kelvin-Helmholtz time is
$$
t_{\rm KH}\sim\frac{GM^2}{RL}.
$$
Fully convective pre-main-sequence evolution approximately follows a <Hayashi track>, with decreasing radius and <luminosity> at comparatively weakly changing <effective temperature>. Objects developing radiative interiors can follow <Henyey tracks>. Eventually sustained core <hydrogen burning> balances the <luminosity> and the <star> reaches the <main sequence>. Massive objects can ignite <hydrogen> while substantial <accretion> continues, so “<accretion> ends, then <hydrogen> ignites” is not a universal ordering.

A useful structural distinction is therefore between an accretion-powered <protostar> and a largely fixed-mass pre-main-sequence contraction model. An infrared-bright envelope or disc traces obscured formation and reprocessing of radiation; a schematic evolutionary track should not replace the actual mass- and entropy-dependent stellar-structure problem.

**An encounter between <galaxies> or clusters mainly couples their collective gravitational fields; it need not involve physical impacts between individual <stars>.** Direct stellar collision rates depend on number density, relative speed and <gravitational focusing>. For two <stars> the <collision cross-section> is approximately
$$
\sigma_{\rm coll}=\pi(R_1+R_2)^2
\left[1+\frac{2G(m_1+m_2)}
{(R_1+R_2)v_\infty^2}\right],
\qquad
t_{\rm coll}\sim(n_\star\sigma_{\rm coll}v_\infty)^{-1}.
$$
In an ordinary <galaxy>, the small geometrical stellar filling factor makes such impacts rare even while the <galaxy> undergoes a major encounter. Dense stellar cores are an important exception, not a reason to treat all <galaxies> as collisional fluids.

<Tidal forces> depend on the gradient of the perturber's field. For a distant fast encounter of mass $M_p$, impact parameter $b$ and speed $V$, the impulse scale for a <star> at displacement $r$ is
$$
\Delta v\sim\frac{GM_p r}{b^2V}.
$$
A more specific result illustrates the geometry. Put the straight-line perturber trajectory in the $(x,y)$ plane along $y$, with closest approach at $x=b$. In the tidal and impulse limits,
$$
\Delta\mathbf v=\frac{2GM_p}{b^2V}(x,0,-z),
\qquad
\boxed{\Delta E=
\frac{4G^2M_p^2M\langle r^2\rangle}{3b^4V^2}}
$$
for an initially spherical, nonstreaming target with randomly sampled internal phases. The cross term averages to zero, and $\langle x^2+z^2\rangle=2\langle r^2\rangle/3$. Heating, expansion and <tidal stripping> preferentially affect weakly bound outer <stars>. A slow encounter can be adiabatically shielded rather than impulsive; a penetrating or strongly resonant encounter needs more than this distant straight-line approximation.

Disc rotation and orbital orientation matter. Prograde passages can drive stronger coherent distortions than corresponding retrograde passages. Tidal bridges, long tails, asymmetric arms and shells can record the encounter. Restricted gravitational calculations already reproduced prominent bridge and tail structures without requiring individual stellar collisions; full self-consistent models additionally follow the gravity of the responding discs and haloes.<Galactic Bridges and Tails by Alar Toomre and Juri Toomre>[1972 abstract and §II, close tidal encounters of initially rotating test-particle discs]{ref}

A first high-speed passage need not bind the pair. Capture or merging requires orbital energy and <angular momentum> to be redistributed into internal motions, escaping material, gas radiation or the surrounding halo. Dynamical friction transfers orbital energy to a gravitational wake and can bring a bound satellite inward. In a major merger, the potential changes on orbital times and <violent relaxation> redistributes stellar energies; <phase mixing> then smooths fine orbital structure. These are collective collisionless processes, distinct from slow <two-body relaxation>.

Gas behaves differently from <stars>: gas streams can shock, dissipate energy, lose <angular momentum> and fuel a central starburst or active nucleus. Gas-poor mergers are correspondingly more nearly dissipationless. Outcomes depend on mass ratio, orbital <angular momentum>, relative velocity, halo structure and gas fraction: they range from a modest flyby and tidal debris to disruption of a satellite or a merged remnant. Slow encounters in a group are generally more favorable to merging than otherwise comparable very fast unbound passages, but no single speed or mass-ratio threshold follows without an explicit structural model.

= Solution
{parent=c}

**<Galaxy> formation combines gravitational assembly of dark-matter haloes with dissipative baryonic physics.** Primordial density fluctuations supply a statistical seed distribution. In linear theory, each <Fourier mode> evolves through a growth factor and a scale-dependent <transfer function>; the <power spectrum> describes the variance by scale. Radiation pressure, <free streaming> and the time of horizon entry influence the small-scale transfer, so one should not replace the spectrum by a single universal growing power of time.

When fluctuations become nonlinear, overdense regions collapse, virialize and build larger structures through <accretion> and mergers. <Cold dark matter> permits a broad hierarchy of bound haloes. A spherical-collapse threshold and a mass-variance prescription, such as the <Press-Schechter formalism>, organize an approximate halo abundance; departures from <spherical collapse> and correlated environments limit its literal interpretation.

Gas falling into a halo is compressed and may be shock-heated toward a virial scale
$$
k_BT_{\rm vir}\sim\frac{\mu m_pV_c^2}{2}.
$$
Unlike collisionless dark matter, it can radiate away <thermal energy>. A rough cooling time is
$$
t_{\rm cool}\sim
\frac{\tfrac32n_{\rm tot}k_BT}{n_en_i\Lambda(T,Z)},
$$
where the denominator specifies a chosen optically thin <astrophysical cooling function> convention. Cooling is effective for condensation when this time is short compared with the relevant dynamical or supply time. Atomic transitions, molecular processes, <ionization> state, density and metal abundance matter. A stable virial shock is not obligatory for all <accretion>: cold dense inflows can supply gas in regimes where shock-heated gas would cool rapidly.

The distinction between a gravitationally assembled massive halo and a radiatively condensing luminous component is fundamental. In the classic two-stage picture, non-dissipative clustering builds potential wells while cooling allows gas to concentrate much further than the halo component.<Core condensation in heavy halos by Simon D. M. White and Martin J. Rees>[1978 abstract, gravitational clustering plus dissipative gas condensation]{ref}

<Angular momentum> inherited from tidal torques and later <accretion> helps gas settle into a rotationally supported disc after cooling; disc size depends on angular-momentum retention and transport. Locally self-gravitating gas fragments and forms <stars>. Stellar radiation, winds and supernovae then heat gas, drive <turbulence>, enrich it with heavy elements and can expel it from shallow potential wells. Metal enrichment can also accelerate later cooling, so feedback is more than a single positive or negative energy term.

<Galaxy> growth is usefully expressed as a gas-budget competition:
$$
\dot M_g=\dot M_{\rm in}-(1-\mathcal R)\psi-\dot M_{\rm wind}
+\dot M_{\rm reaccrete}.
$$
Here $\mathcal R$ is the promptly returned stellar mass fraction; unlike the simplification in Question1, a general evolutionary model need not set it to zero. The equation separates newly supplied, converted, expelled and recycled gas rather than assuming that halo mass uniquely fixes stellar mass.

Accreting central <black holes> can provide radiative and mechanical feedback. Heating a halo, ejecting gas or maintaining a hot atmosphere can reduce fresh fuel; removal of a satellite's gas supply and <ram pressure> in a surrounding medium provide additional environmental routes. <Quiescent galaxy assembly> can still add stellar mass through dry mergers, so cessation of <star formation> is not identical to cessation of all mass assembly.

Mergers and disc instabilities can rearrange morphology and trigger bursts, while smooth <accretion> can rebuild discs. Cosmological expansion changes the supply and merger environment; accelerated late-time expansion also slows linear growth. A predictive model must connect these mechanisms to the stellar mass function, gas fractions, star-formation histories, sizes, chemical abundances and clustering. Those observables test different pieces of the assembly-and-feedback picture; none alone implies a one-to-one instantaneous relation between <galaxy> <luminosity> and total halo mass.
