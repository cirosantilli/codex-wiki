= Solution
{parent=1}

**<virial pressure-radius curve with uniform-sphere binding energy>.** Put $c_s^2=k_BT/\mu$. Isothermality makes $\int(p/\rho)dM=c_s^2M_0$, so the <virial theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{p_0=\frac{A}{r_0^3}-\frac{B}{r_0^4},\qquad
A=\frac{3c_s^2M_0}{4\pi},\quad B=\frac{3GM_0^2}{20\pi}}.
$$
The curve crosses zero at $r_z=B/A=GM_0/(5c_s^2)$, rises to its maximum at
$$
\boxed{r_c=\frac{4B}{3A}=\frac{4GM_0}{15c_s^2}},
$$
then decreases toward zero at large radius. Since $p_0'=(4B-3Ar_0)/r_0^5$, a cloud displaced outward at fixed external <pressure> has a restoring <pressure> deficit only on the descending branch. Thus **the homologous, isothermal radial mode is stable for $r_0>r_c$**, unstable for $r_z<r_0<r_c$, and marginal at $r_c$. The negative-pressure branch is not a physical equilibrium with a positive confining <pressure>. This conclusion uses the stipulated gravitational-energy approximation, not the exact isothermal <density> profile.

\Image[paper-1-virial-pressure.png]
{title=Original pressure-radius curve and stable versus unstable branches of the stipulated virial cloud model}

**<critical mass from a virial pressure turning point>.** At the turning point, $B=3Ar_c/4$; hence
$$
\boxed{p_{\max}=\frac{A}{4r_c^3}
=\frac{10125}{1024\pi}\frac{c_s^8}{G^3M_0^2}
\propto\left(\frac{T^2}{M_0}\right)^2}.
$$
Using the cloud's <mean density> $\bar\rho=3M_0/(4\pi r_0^3)$, the condition $r_0>4GM_0/(15c_s^2)$ becomes
$$
\boxed{M_0<M_{\rm crit}=\left(\frac{15c_s^2}{4G}\right)^{3/2}
\left(\frac{3}{4\pi\bar\rho}\right)^{1/2}
\propto\left(\frac{T^3}{\bar\rho}\right)^{1/2}}.
$$
Using the boundary <density> instead changes the numerical coefficient, not this scaling. The coefficient should not be identified with the exact <Bonnor-Ebert sphere> threshold because the assumed <potential energy> is different.

**<mass and luminosity weighting of a stellar initial mass function>.** Impose finite birth-mass cutoffs $m_l,m_u$. The stellar birth <mass> and main-sequence <luminosity> scale as
$$
M_{\rm born}=\frac{\xi_0}{0.35}(m_l^{-0.35}-m_u^{-0.35}),\qquad
L_{\rm tot}\propto\frac{\xi_0}{2.65}(m_u^{2.65}-m_l^{2.65}).
$$
For a broad <mass> interval, the low-mass end controls the first <integral> and the high-mass end the second. Per logarithmic <mass> interval the weights are respectively $m^2\xi\propto m^{-0.35}$ and $mL\xi\propto m^{2.65}$. Finite lifetimes remove massive stars from an aging population, so the <luminosity> conclusion applies while those stars remain on the <main sequence>.

**<exponential closed-box gas consumption>.** The gross formation rate is $\Psi=\epsilon M_g$, but the permanent locking rate is $\alpha\Psi$. The <instantaneous recycling approximation> and <mass conservation> give $\dot M_g=-\alpha\epsilon M_g$, $\dot M_*=\alpha\epsilon M_g$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{M_g=M_0e^{-\alpha\epsilon t},\qquad
M_*=M_0(1-e^{-\alpha\epsilon t}),\qquad
f_g=e^{-\alpha\epsilon t}}.
$$
Here $M_*$ means surviving locked stellar <mass> in the <galactic gas fraction>, not the cumulative gross <mass> that has passed through <star formation>.

**<locked-mass and returned-mass yield conventions>.** Pre-existing metals are locked at rate $\alpha Z\Psi$; the stipulated newly produced metal source is $b(1-\alpha)\Psi$. Thus
$$
\frac{d(M_gZ)}{dt}=-\alpha Z\Psi+b(1-\alpha)\Psi,
\qquad M_g\dot Z=b(1-\alpha)\Psi.
$$
For $\alpha>0$ and initially pristine gas,
$$
\boxed{Z=y\ln\frac{M_0}{M_g}=y\ln\frac1{f_g},\qquad
y=\frac{b(1-\alpha)}\alpha,\qquad
Z(t)=b(1-\alpha)\epsilon t}.
$$
The effective yield $y$ is per unit permanently locked <mass>; it is not the source parameter $b$. For $\alpha=0$, $M_g=M_0$ and the direct time equation gives $Z=b\epsilon t$. For $\alpha=1$, the explicitly stipulated metal source vanishes, so this particular enrichment model gives $Z=0$. Constant yield is a trace-enrichment approximation; the formal logarithmic divergence as gas is exhausted must not be interpreted as a physical metal fraction exceeding one.

**<backward extrapolation of exponential closed-box star formation>.** Denote the present <age of the universe> by $t_{\rm now}$. With the last paragraph's $\alpha=1$,
$$
\boxed{\Psi(z\simeq3)=\left(1M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}\right)
\exp\left[0.5\left(\frac{t_{\rm now}}{\mathrm{Gyr}}-2\right)\right]}.
$$
Adopting a present <age of the universe> $t_{\rm now}=13.8\,\mathrm{Gyr}$ for the estimate gives about **$365M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$**, requiring $7.3\times10^{11}M_\odot$ of gas at that epoch in the same model. The boxed expression makes the dependence on the adopted present age explicit. This is an extreme history for a present Milky-Way-like system, although intense high-redshift <star formation> by itself is not forbidden.

The extrapolation forces a fixed gas reservoir to decline exponentially over many <gas-depletion times>. Gas <accretion>, outflows, evolving gas <density> and star-formation efficiency, delayed recycling, and galaxy assembly break that assumption. The empirical disk-averaged relation is not a universal constant-efficiency law for all gas: the original measurements combined <Balmer series> H-alpha, atomic gas, <carbon monoxide> and <infrared> data, with tracer calibration and dust corrections entering the inference.<The Global Schmidt Law in Star Forming Galaxies by Robert C. Kennicutt Jr.>[Abstract: observed tracers, sample, and fitted index]{ref} Cosmological calculations explicitly support continuing cold-gas supply to rapidly forming high-redshift galaxies, providing a physical alternative to this closed-box backward extrapolation.<Cold streams in early massive hot haloes as the main mode of galaxy formation by Avishai Dekel and others>[Abstract: stream-fed galaxies and comparison with observed abundance]{ref}

**Radial <epicyclic motion>.** At fixed <specific angular momentum>, use $\Phi_{\rm eff}=\Phi+\ell^2/(2r^2)$ and $\ddot r=-\Phi_{\rm eff}'$. A <circular orbit> at $r_c$ obeys $\Phi'(r_c)=\ell^2/r_c^3=r_c\Omega^2$. Expanding $r=r_c+\xi$ gives $\ddot\xi+\kappa^2\xi=0$, where
$$
\boxed{\kappa^2=\Phi''+\frac{3\ell^2}{r_c^4}
=4\Omega^2+2r_c\Omega\Omega'
=4\Omega^2\left(1+\frac{r_c\Omega'}{2\Omega}\right)}.
$$
For $\kappa^2>0$ the <radial epicyclic frequency> describes <simple harmonic motion>. The angular correction follows from <conservation of angular momentum>, $\dot\phi\simeq\Omega(1-2\xi/r_c)$, and describes an <epicyclic motion> around the guiding <circular orbit>.

**Stabilizing terms.** In the given local fluid <dispersion relation>, $\kappa^2$ is rotational support, important at long <wavelength>; $a_0^2k^2$ is random-motion or <pressure> support, important at short <wavelength>. The term $-2\pi G\Sigma|k|$ is destabilizing <self-gravity>. Although the wording refers to stellar dispersion, this algebra uses the supplied fluid approximation; a collisionless stellar treatment has a reduction factor and a different numerical normalization.<On the Gravitational Stability of a Disk of Stars by Alar Toomre>[Abstract and Sections II–V: collisionless stability threshold and random-motion support]{ref}

**Axisymmetric threshold.** For $n=0$, set $q=|k|\geq0$. Completing the square gives
$$
\omega^2=a_0^2(q-q_*)^2+\kappa^2(1-Q^{-2}),
\qquad q_*=\frac{\pi G\Sigma}{a_0^2},\quad
Q=\frac{\kappa a_0}{\pi G\Sigma}.
$$
Thus **$Q>1$ gives local axisymmetric oscillatory stability; $Q<1$ gives a growing band**, with
$$
q_-<q<q_+,\qquad
q_\pm=\frac{\pi G\Sigma}{a_0^2}\left(1\pm\sqrt{1-Q^2}\right).
$$
At $Q=1$, the minimizing mode has zero <frequency> and is marginal, not a strictly restoring oscillation. Local axisymmetric stability does not establish stability to every global nonaxisymmetric mode.

**<long-wave and short-wave branches at marginal Toomre stability>.** For $Q=1$ and $n\geq1$,
$$
n^2(\Omega_P-\Omega)^2=(a_0q-\kappa)^2,
\qquad
q=\frac{\kappa\pm n|\Omega_P-\Omega|}{a_0}.
$$
The long-wavelength branch, using the minus sign, requires
$$
\boxed{\Omega-\frac\kappa n\leq\Omega_P\leq\Omega+\frac\kappa n}.
$$
These endpoints are the inner and outer <Lindblad resonance> conditions. **The stated <dispersion relation> does not restrict every wave solution to that interval.** Its plus-sign short-wavelength branch has $q>0$ even outside it: for example, $\Omega_P=\Omega+2\kappa/n$ gives $q=3\kappa/a_0$ and satisfies the equation. Additional branch selection or resonant absorption physics would be needed for an absolute confinement claim. No such condition is supplied.

**<Toomre stability radius of a flat-rotation disk>.** With $\Omega=v_0/r$, $\kappa=\sqrt2v_0/r$. Using $G=4.3009\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{pc}\,(\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}})^2M_\odot^{-1}$,
$$
\boxed{Q(r)=\frac{\sqrt2v_0a_0}{\pi G\Sigma r}
\simeq\frac{4.19\ \mathrm{kpc}}r}.
$$
The supplied local model therefore predicts stable radii below about **$4.19\ \mathrm{kpc}$**, marginality at that radius, and axisymmetric instability farther out while its assumed constant <surface density> and thin-disk approximation remain applicable. An infinite constant-density, flat-rotation disk is an idealization, not a global self-consistent galaxy model.

= Solution
{parent=a}

For a monatomic <ideal gas>, write the thermal <energy> <density> as $u=3nk_BT/2$. At low <optical depth>, radiative loss uses an <astrophysical cooling function>, often written $\mathcal C=n_en_H\Lambda(T,Z)$, while $\mathcal H$ contains heating. At fixed <density> the net <radiative gas cooling time> is approximately $u/(\mathcal C-\mathcal H)$ when the denominator is positive; a cooling flow closer to constant <pressure> must include <enthalpy> and compressional work. Comparing this time with the <free-fall time of a uniform sphere> determines whether <pressure> can be lost during gravitational contraction.

Hot ionized gas cools by <bremsstrahlung> and <recombination>; <collisional excitation> followed by <photon> escape supplies atomic and metal-line cooling at lower <temperatures>. <molecular line cooling> through rotational or vibrational lines and <dust thermal emission> become important in cold dense gas. Abundances, <ionization> state, density-dependent collisional de-excitation, and <photon> trapping all change the cooling function. Optically thick gas requires <radiative transfer>, not a simple local density-squared loss law.

Heating comes from <photoionization>, <grain photoelectric heating> driven by ultraviolet light, <cosmic rays>, <shocks>, turbulent dissipation and compression. <photoionization heating> deposits the <photon> <energy> above the <ionization> threshold as electron <kinetic energy>. A <thermal equilibrium> satisfies $\mathcal H=\mathcal C$; its stability depends on how the net loss changes under the relevant <pressure> or <density> constraint, not merely on equality. Different stable balances permit a multiphase <interstellar medium>. Spectral-line ratios constrain <temperature> and <ionization>, but interpreting them requires <density> diagnostics, <interstellar extinction> corrections and a radiation-field model.

= Solution
{parent=b}

A cold self-gravitating core initially contracts on a dynamical scale $t_{\rm ff}\sim(G\rho)^{-1/2}$ if cooling prevents <pressure> from halting collapse. Increasing <optical depth> eventually traps <radiation> and forms a pressure-supported <first hydrostatic core>. Dissociation of <molecular hydrogen> absorbs compressional <energy>, softens the <equation of state> and triggers a <second protostellar collapse> to a compact <protostar>. Rotation produces a disk; angular-momentum transport permits continuing <accretion>, while outflows remove some <mass> and <angular momentum>.

An accreting object's <luminosity> includes a contribution of order $L_{\rm acc}\sim GM\dot M/R$, with a coefficient set by where <accretion> <energy> is radiated or retained. After <accretion> subsides, gravitational contraction supplies internal <luminosity> over the <Kelvin-Helmholtz cooling time> $t_{\rm KH}\sim GM^2/(RL_{\rm int})$. Hydrostatic contraction heats the interior even while <energy> is radiated, as follows from the <virial theorem> and the <negative heat capacity of a virialized gravitational system>. <accretion luminosity> must not be substituted for $L_{\rm int}$ in the contraction time without an energy-budget justification.

Low-mass, nearly fully convective <pre-main-sequence stars> contract down a <Hayashi track> at roughly constant <effective temperature> and decreasing radius and <luminosity>. A developing <radiative core> can lead onto a <Henyey track> toward higher <effective temperature>; the route depends on <mass> and opacity. The <deuterium-burning energy-generation rate> can temporarily moderate contraction, but sustained core <hydrogen burning> marks arrival on the <main sequence>. Massive objects may begin <hydrogen burning> while still accreting, and substellar objects never establish long-lived <hydrogen burning>. An <infrared> spectral class reflects envelope and disk emission as well as inclination and <interstellar extinction>, so it is not a unique stellar age or interior evolutionary track.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Stellar <radiation>, <stellar winds> and <supernovae> return <energy>, <momentum>, <mass> and metals to surrounding gas. Heating, <ionization>, turbulent support and gas removal can reduce subsequent <star formation>; swept-up shells can also compress gas locally. Whether thermal <energy> drives an outflow depends on its <radiative gas cooling time> relative to expansion: radiative losses can remove <energy> while leaving substantial <momentum> transfer. Thus deposited <energy> alone does not specify the dynamical effect.

The <galactic gas regulator> has <mass> budget $\dot M_g=\dot M_{\rm in}-(1-R+\eta)\Psi$, where $R$ is the returned fraction and $\eta=\dot M_{\rm out}/\Psi$ is the <mass-loading factor>. In a quasi-steady state, $\Psi\simeq\dot M_{\rm in}/(1-R+\eta)$. This connects feedback to the available gas supply. A wind's kinetic power $\dot M_{\rm out}v_w^2/2$ and <momentum> flux $\dot M_{\rm out}v_w$ must fit the source budget and gravitational binding cost. Gas that fails to escape may form a <galactic fountain> and later return; preferential metal loss changes chemical evolution.

Absorption against a galaxy's stellar continuum provides a concrete outflow test: compare interstellar sodium absorption with the systemic velocity and stellar absorption spectrum. A primary starburst survey found blueshifted interstellar absorption and an inclination dependence consistent with minor-axis outflows. Its saturated lines also illustrate why velocity detection is more direct than a unique column <density> or <mass> flux.<Absorption-Line Probes of Gas and Dust in Galactic Superwinds by Timothy M. Heckman and others>[Abstract: NaD spectra, systemic velocities, viewing angle and optical depth]{ref} Deprojection, covering fraction, <ionization>, radius and the unseen hot phase limit total outflow-rate estimates.

An accreting <supermassive black hole> adds <radiation>, winds and <astrophysical jets>. Coupling these to the interstellar or <circumgalactic medium> can suppress cooling or expel gas, especially in deep potentials where stellar driving alone may be inadequate. Jet cavities, line-emitting gas and multiphase outflows test different channels; coexisting activity and low <star formation> alone do not prove a causal feedback history.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Small primordial fluctuations seed later structure. For <adiabatic cosmological perturbations> on scales outside the horizon, the <uniform-density curvature perturbation> is conserved when the <non-adiabatic pressure perturbation> and the decaying mode can be neglected. A <density contrast> outside the horizon is gauge-dependent, so its apparent power-law growth should not be confused with directly measured local collapse.

For subhorizon, pressureless matter, the linearized <continuity equation>, <Euler equation> and <Poisson equation> combine into
$$
\boxed{\ddot\delta+2H\dot\delta-4\pi G\bar\rho_m\delta=0}.
$$
Hubble expansion damps peculiar motion; <self-gravity> amplifies overdensities. During <radiation domination>, <radiation> <pressure> drives acoustic oscillations and <cold dark matter> growth after horizon entry is only logarithmic. During <matter domination> the growing and decaying solutions are $\delta\propto a$ and $\delta\propto a^{-3/2}$. Dark-energy domination later suppresses growth relative to the matter-era law.

For a pressure-supported fluid the Fourier-mode equation also contains $+c_s^2k^2\delta/a^2$, giving a <Jeans length> separating acoustic support from gravitational growth. Before <recombination>, <baryons> are tightly coupled to <photons> and oscillate rather than freely following <dark matter>. <Silk damping> from photon diffusion damps small-scale photon-baryon fluctuations; after decoupling, <baryons> can fall into dark-matter potential wells. <free streaming> smooths species-dependent scales and distinguishes cold from warm or hot <dark matter>.

Schematically, the linear <matter power spectrum> can be written $P_\delta(k,a)=A k^{n_s}T^2(k)D^2(a)$ under the usual primordial-curvature normalization. The <cosmological transfer function> records horizon entry, matter-radiation equality, acoustic physics and damping; the <linear growth factor> records later amplification. <density contrasts> approaching unity require nonlinear collapse and halo formation beyond this approximation. <cosmic microwave background> anisotropies and <galaxy clustering> test the same initial perturbations through different transfer histories; <galaxy bias>, <redshift-space distortions> and observational selection enter the galaxy comparison.
