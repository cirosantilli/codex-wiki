= Solution
{parent=a}

For a <material volume> $V(t)$ moving with <velocity> $\mathbf u$, the <Reynolds transport theorem> is
$$
\frac d{dt}\int_{V(t)}f\,dV=\int_{V(t)}[\partial_tf+\nabla\cdot(f\mathbf u)]\,dV.
$$
It applies componentwise to vector quantities. <Cauchy's stress theorem> represents <traction> on a surface with outward unit normal $\mathbf n$ as $t_i=\sigma_{ij}n_j$. <Conservation of mass> gives $d\int_{V(t)}\rho\,dV/dt=0$ and hence $\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)=0$. Constant <density> reduces this to $\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0}$.

With no <body force>, <conservation of momentum> and the <divergence theorem> give
$$
\frac d{dt}\int_{V(t)}\rho u_i\,dV=\int_{\partial V}\sigma_{ij}n_j\,dS=\int_V\partial_j\sigma_{ij}\,dV.
$$
Applying transport and then <mass conservation> cancels the terms proportional to $u_i$, leaving $\rho(\partial_tu_i+u_j\partial_ju_i)=\partial_j\sigma_{ij}$. For the stated Newtonian stress, constant <viscosity> gives $\partial_j\sigma_{ij}=-\partial_ip+\mu\Delta u_i+\mu\partial_i(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)$. The last term vanishes. Since the <material volume> was arbitrary,
$$
\boxed{\rho[\mathbf u_t+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u]=-\nabla p+\mu\Delta\mathbf u.}
$$
A <body force>, if present, would have to be added to the momentum balance; it is absent from the specified model.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For $\mathbf u=u(y,t)\mathbf e_x$, <incompressibility> is automatic and the nonlinear advection term is zero. With no <pressure> gradient the streamwise momentum equation is $\boxed{\rho u_t=\mu u_{yy}}$. Seek its periodic response as $u=\operatorname{Re}[Uf(y)e^{i\Omega t}]$. The amplitude satisfies $f''=i\rho\Omega f/\mu$ with $f(\pm d)=1$. Since $[k(1+i)]^2=i\rho\Omega/\mu$ for $k=\sqrt{\rho\Omega/(2\mu)}$, even symmetry gives
$$
\boxed{f(y)=\frac{\cosh[k(1+i)y]}{\cosh[k(1+i)d]}.}
$$
Odd components vanish because the two boundary amplitudes are equal. This is the unique periodic solution. Without a specified initial profile, decaying zero-wall diffusion transients may be added, so the formula should not be read as a claim that every transient is absent.

As $kd\to\infty$, the wall motions penetrate only a distance $k^{-1}=\sqrt{2\mu/(\rho\Omega)}$. Near either wall, with inward distance $s=d-|y|$, the leading <velocity> is
$$
\boxed{u\sim Ue^{-ks}\cos(\Omega t-ks).}
$$
The two oscillatory viscous layers are separated by an almost motionless core; the phase lag increases inward. This is <in-phase oscillating parallel-plate flow>.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $\delta=Re^{-1/2}$ and use the inner variables $\boxed{Y=y/\delta=Re^{1/2}y,\quad V=v/\delta=Re^{1/2}v}$. Continuity balances $u_x$ with $V_Y$. In the tangential momentum balance, advection is order one while the normal viscous term is $Re^{-1}\delta^{-2}u_{YY}=u_{YY}$. Streamwise <viscosity> is smaller by $Re^{-1}$. The normal momentum balance forces the leading <pressure> to be independent of $Y$. Thus
$$
\boxed{u_x+V_Y=0,\qquad uu_x+Vu_Y=-p_x+u_{YY},\qquad p_Y=0.}
$$
On the stationary impermeable plate, <no-slip boundary condition> and <no-penetration boundary condition> give $u=V=0$ at $Y=0$, $x>0$. Matching to the uniform outer stream gives $u\to1$ as $Y\to\infty$, and the leading-edge/upstream data are $u\to1$ as $x\downarrow0$ at fixed $Y>0$. There is no requirement that the scaled transverse <velocity> $V$ vanish at infinity; its physical value $v=\delta V$ matches a smaller outer correction. The outer streamwise Euler equation with constant speed one gives $p_x=0$, and $p_Y=0$ carries this <pressure> gradient throughout the layer.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <von Mises boundary-layer transformation> uses $\Psi$ as transverse coordinate. With $u=U(x,\Psi)$ and $\Psi_x=-V$, $\Psi_Y=u=U$, the <chain rule> gives
$$
\boxed{u_x=U_x-VU_\Psi,\qquad u_Y=UU_\Psi,\qquad u_{YY}=U\partial_\Psi(UU_\Psi).}
$$
The streamwise advection terms reduce to $u u_x+Vu_Y=UU_x$, so, for $Y>0$ where $U>0$, division by $U$ gives
$$
\boxed{U_x=\partial_\Psi(UU_\Psi).}
$$
Continuity was already enforced by the <stream function>. Substitute $U=g(\eta)$ with $\eta=\Psi/\sqrt x$. Then $U_x=-\eta g'/(2x)$ and $\partial_\Psi(UU_\Psi)=(gg')'/x$, yielding
$$
\boxed{(gg')'+\tfrac12\eta g'=0,\qquad g(0)=0,\quad g(\infty)=1.}
$$
The wall coordinate is zero because $\Psi$ was set to zero there, and the far condition matches unit external speed. This is the transformed Blasius similarity problem. One must not additionally prescribe a finite $g'(0)$: the coordinate change degenerates at the no-slip wall, and the regular physical shear corresponds to $g\propto\sqrt\eta$ near zero. This similarity reduction is obtained for the attached monotone <boundary layer> assumed in the question.

Existence of a positive normalized profile can also be seen directly. Put $q=gg'$ and choose $q(0)=a>0$. In $g$ coordinates the regular initial-value system is $dq/dg=-\eta/2$, $d\eta/dg=g/q$, with $\eta(0)=0$. It starts as $g\sim\sqrt{2a\eta}$. For finite positive $\eta$, $q'/q=-\eta/(2g)$ keeps $q$ positive and $q\leq a$, while $(g^2)'=2q$ gives $g\leq\sqrt{2a\eta}$. Consequently $q'/q\leq-\sqrt\eta/(2\sqrt{2a})$, so $q$ is integrable at infinity. It follows that $g$ increases to a finite positive limit $M$. The rescaled profile $\widetilde g(\eta)=M^{-1}g(\sqrt M\eta)$ satisfies the same equation and has limit one. This supplies the requested similarity solution with both boundary conditions, rather than only its formal differential equation.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Scale $x,y$ by $L$, $z$ by $\delta L$, tangential velocities by $U$, normal <velocity> by $\delta U$, and time by $L/U$. Continuity balances all three derivatives. Tangential viscous forces scale as $\mu U/(\delta^2L^2)$, hence their <pressure> balance requires
$$
\boxed{p\sim\frac{\mu U}{\delta^2L},\qquad t\sim L/U.}
$$
The inertial/normal-viscous ratio is $\delta^2\rho LU/\mu$, so it is negligible under the given smallness condition. Tangential viscous derivatives are smaller by $\delta^2$; the normal <pressure> balance gives $p_z=0$ at leading order. In dimensional variables the remaining equations are continuity, $\mu u_{zz}=p_x$, $\mu v_{zz}=p_y$, $p_z=0$.

<No-slip boundary condition> at each plate imposes $u=v=0$, while their normal velocities are $w(0)=0$ and $w(h)=\dot h$. Integrating the two tangential equations twice gives
$$
(u,v)=\frac{z(z-h)}{2\mu}\nabla p,\qquad
\boxed{\overline{\mathbf u}=\frac1h\int_0^h(u,v)\,dz=-\frac{h^2}{12\mu}\nabla p.}
$$
Integrate continuity through the gap. Since $h$ is independent of in-plane position and the tangential <velocity> vanishes at the upper wall, the flux divergence is $\nabla\cdot(h\overline{\mathbf u})$ and the normal contribution is $\dot h$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\dot h+\nabla\cdot(h\overline{\mathbf u})=0.}
$$

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For spatially uniform $h(t)$, the gap equation gives $\Delta p=12\mu\dot h/h^3$. Axisymmetry makes this $r^{-1}(rp_r)_r=12\mu\dot h/h^3$. Its general integral contains a logarithmic term; bounded <pressure> at zero excludes that term. Enforcing zero <gauge pressure> at the outer edge gives the <axisymmetric squeeze-film pressure>
$$
\boxed{p(r,t)=\frac{3\mu\dot h}{h^3}(r^2-R^2).}
$$
The mean radial <velocity> is $\overline u_r=-h^2p_r/(12\mu)=-r\dot h/(2h)$. At the material edge this must equal $\dot R$, so $2h\dot R+R\dot h=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\pi R(t)^2h(t)=\pi R(0)^2h(0).}
$$
It is conservation of the incompressible liquid volume, with no liquid entering or leaving through the plates or material rim.

When the plates approach, $\dot h<0$ and $p>0$ inside: the liquid is squeezed outward. Separation requires $p<0$ relative to the surrounding air and inward motion of the edge. Negative <gauge pressure> is not intrinsically impossible, but substantial suction can cause <cavitation> or air invasion, and an advancing air/liquid interface can develop fingering. Thus the same mathematical lubrication solution exists for either sign, whereas its opening-plate physical validity needs these additional failure mechanisms to be absent; the closing case is the robust regime of the stated idealized model.

= Solution

The inviscid equations are $\rho_t+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)=0$ and $\rho[\mathbf u_t+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u]=-\nabla p$. Write $\rho=\rho_0+\rho'$, $p=P(\rho_0)+p'$ and retain only linear terms. With $c_0^2=P'(\rho_0)>0$, barotropy gives $p'=c_0^2\rho'$. Irrotational perturbations allow $\mathbf u'=\nabla\phi$. The momentum equation integrates to $p'=-\rho_0\phi_t$, after using the time-dependent gauge of $\phi$ to remove the spatially constant integration term. Thus
$$
\boxed{c_0^2=P'(\rho_0),\quad p'=-\rho_0\phi_t,\quad\rho'=-\frac{\rho_0}{c_0^2}\phi_t.}
$$
Substitution into $\rho'_t+\rho_0\Delta\phi=0$ gives the acoustic <wave equation> $\boxed{\phi_{tt}=c_0^2\Delta\phi}$.

{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Impermeability imposes homogeneous Neumann data: $\phi_x=0$ at $x=0,a$ and $\phi_y=0$ at $y=0,b$. Separation of variables therefore gives
$$
\phi=A\cos(n\pi x/a)\cos(m\pi y/b)e^{-i\omega t},\qquad
\boxed{\omega^2=\pi^2c_0^2(n^2/a^2+m^2/b^2).}
$$
One may take $n,m\geq0$ without duplication. For a nontrivial oscillation they are not both zero. A time-independent spatially constant potential is a gauge mode at zero frequency, rather than an additional sound oscillation.

= Solution
{parent=c}

At leading order the two wall conditions may be evaluated on the undeformed line $y=b$: shifting the evaluation point multiplies small perturbations and creates only second-order errors. The dynamic relation and the linear Bernoulli relation give $\eta=p'/(\rho_0k)=-\phi_t/k$. Differentiate this in time and use $\phi_y=\eta_t$:
$$
\boxed{k\phi_y+\phi_{tt}=0\quad\text{at }y=b.}
$$
The sign follows from the convention that positive <pressure> displaces the upper wall upward.

{parent=4}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Retain the rigid-wall cosine in $x$ and the lower-wall cosine in $y$:
$$
\phi=A\cos(n\pi x/a)\cos(\beta y/b)e^{-i\omega t}.
$$
The <wave equation> gives $\omega^2=c_0^2[(n\pi/a)^2+(\beta/b)^2]$. The upper-wall condition gives $-k\beta\sin\beta/b-\omega^2\cos\beta=0$, hence
$$
\boxed{\beta\tan\beta=-\omega^2b/k.}
$$
For nonzero real $\beta$, eliminating $\omega$ gives $-\tan\beta=M(\beta+\kappa_n^2/\beta)$ with $M=c_0^2/(kb)>0$, $\kappa_n=n\pi b/a$.

On every interval $((j+1/2)\pi,(j+1)\pi)$, the left side decreases from positive infinity to zero, while the right side is finite and positive. Continuity gives at least one intersection per interval for each $n$. The functions are analytic between their poles, so roots are isolated there; their union over countably many intervals is a countably infinite set. Reflection gives the redundant negative-$\beta$ solutions. For $n=0$, the limiting formal root $\beta=0$ is the zero-frequency gauge mode, not one of these positive-frequency branches.

\Image[2009-compliant-wall-spectrum.png]
{title=Real vertical-mode intersections for three horizontal mode numbers}

The graph uses representative dimensionless values $M=1$ and $b/a=1$; the interval argument applies for all positive values. For fixed $n$ and fixed branch $j$, take $M\to\infty$. The positive right side becomes large, forcing the intersection towards the tangent pole from the right:
$$
\beta_j=(j+\tfrac12)\pi+O(M^{-1}).
$$
More precisely, if $\beta_j^0=(j+1/2)\pi$, then $\beta_j-\beta_j^0\sim\beta_j^0/[M((\beta_j^0)^2+\kappa_n^2)]$. Therefore the requested real-branch frequencies satisfy
$$
\boxed{\omega_{n,j}\sim\pi c_0\sqrt{\frac{n^2}{a^2}+\frac{(j+1/2)^2}{b^2}},\qquad n,j=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
The limiting upper condition is effectively <pressure> release for these acoustic branches. No claim is made here about imaginary or other complex $\beta$ branches, which were excluded from the requested analysis.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Split $I$ into the two tails and the interval $|k-k_*|<\delta$. On each tail $\psi'$ has a fixed nonzero sign, so changing variable to $s=\psi(k)$ produces a nonstationary Fourier integral. Under the usual decay and regularity needed for <integration by parts>, these pieces are $O(t^{-1})$.

Near the nondegenerate stationary point, make the signed local change
$$
z=\operatorname{sgn}(k-k_*)\sqrt{2[\psi(k)-\psi(k_*)]/\psi''(k_*)}.
$$
It has $dk/dz=1$ at zero and makes the phase exactly $\psi(k_*)+\psi''(k_*)z^2/2$. The transformed amplitude tends to $f(k_*)$. Scale $z$ by $t^{-1/2}$ and use the <Fresnel integral> to obtain
$$
\boxed{I(t)=f(k_*)e^{it\psi(k_*)+i\pi/4}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{t\psi''(k_*)}}+o(t^{-1/2}).}
$$
The $O(t^{-1})$ tails are smaller than this leading stationary contribution. If $\psi''(k_*)<0$, complex conjugation of the Gaussian phase changes the phase correction to $-\pi/4$ and replaces the second derivative by its absolute value:
$$
\boxed{I(t)=f(k_*)e^{it\psi(k_*)-i\pi/4}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{t|\psi''(k_*)|}}+o(t^{-1/2}).}
$$
When the leading coefficient is nonzero this may be written as an <asymptotic equivalence>. For full rigor, continuity of an arbitrary amplitude alone is not sufficient for the stated nonstationary $O(t^{-1})$ rate, nor does it ensure convergence of an infinite integral. Piecewise differentiability with integrable derivative, bounded variation, or an equivalent localization/decay hypothesis supplies the usual missing assumption. The exponentially decaying beam amplitude below has the required piecewise regularity.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Use $\widehat\eta(k,t)=\int\eta(x,t)e^{-ikx}\,dx$ and the inverse factor $1/(2\pi)$. Fourier transformation gives $\widehat\eta_{tt}+\lambda^2k^4\widehat\eta=0$. The supplied Lorentzian transform and zero initial <velocity> therefore imply
$$
\boxed{\widehat\eta=ae^{-\epsilon|k|}\cos(\lambda k^2t),\qquad\eta(x,t)=\frac1{2\pi}\int\widehat\eta(k,t)e^{ikx}\,dk.}
$$
Along $x=Vt$, split the cosine into two exponentials. Their phases are $\psi_\pm=Vk\pm\lambda k^2$, with stationary points $k_\pm=\mp V/(2\lambda)$, phase values $\mp V^2/(4\lambda)$ and second derivatives $\pm2\lambda$. Both amplitudes equal $ae^{-\epsilon|V|/(2\lambda)}$. The two stationary-phase contributions are conjugate, giving
$$
\boxed{\eta(Vt,t)=\frac{ae^{-\epsilon|V|/(2\lambda)}}{2\sqrt{\pi\lambda t}}\cos\left(\frac{V^2t}{4\lambda}-\frac\pi4\right)+o(t^{-1/2}).}
$$
Thus the finite-width pulse retains a ray-dependent attenuation. In the further regime $\epsilon|V|/(2\lambda)\ll1$, it may be replaced by one, recovering
$$
\boxed{\eta(x,t)\sim\frac{a}{2\sqrt{\pi\lambda t}}\cos\left(\frac{x^2}{4\lambda t}-\frac\pi4\right).}
$$
The large-time ray limit means $V=x/t$ is fixed. Merely making both $x$ and $t$ large does not justify dropping the attenuation if $|x|/t$ becomes large compared with $\lambda/\epsilon$. The leading oscillation decays as $t^{-1/2}$ because each saddle samples a Fourier band of width $t^{-1/2}$.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Integrate each steady one-dimensional conservation law across a thin region enclosing the <shock>. <Mass conservation> gives constant <mass flux> $j=\rho u$. <Momentum conservation> includes both advected momentum and <pressure> force, so $p+\rho u^2$ is constant. The total energy flux is $u(\rho e+p)=j(e+p/\rho)$, where $e=c_vT+u^2/2$. For a genuine <shock> $j\ne0$, division by the <mass flux> and the equation of state $c_vT=p/[(\gamma-1)\rho]$ give the conserved stagnation enthalpy
$$
e+p/\rho=\frac{u^2}2+\frac{\gamma p}{(\gamma-1)\rho}.
$$
Thus the <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas> are
$$
\boxed{[\rho u]=0,\quad[p+\rho u^2]=0,\quad\left[\frac{u^2}2+\frac{\gamma p}{(\gamma-1)\rho}\right]=0.}
$$
The velocities here are measured in the stationary <shock frame>; a moving-<shock> problem must first be transformed to that frame.

{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let state zero be the undisturbed gas and state one the uniform gas between piston and <shock>. Their laboratory velocities are zero and $U$. In the <shock frame> their speed magnitudes are $V$ and $V-U$. Mass and momentum jumps give
$$
\rho_1=\rho_0\frac V{V-U},\qquad p_1-p_0=\rho_0VU.
$$
Energy conservation gives $\gamma[p_1/\rho_1-p_0/\rho_0]/(\gamma-1)=VU-U^2/2$. Substitute the preceding relations and $\gamma p_0/\rho_0=c_0^2$, then divide by the nonzero $U$. The result is
$$
\boxed{V^2-\frac{\gamma+1}{2}UV-c_0^2=0.}
$$
The right-moving root is
$$
\boxed{V=\frac{\gamma+1}{4}U+\sqrt{c_0^2+\left(\frac{\gamma+1}{4}U\right)^2}.}
$$
The other root is negative and does not describe the outgoing <shock>. For $U>0$, the quadratic is negative at $V=U$, hence its positive root obeys $V>U$: the <shock> stays ahead of the piston and the <density> ratio is positive.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The second law requires the <specific entropy> to increase along the fluid's passage through a nontrivial physical <shock>: $s_1>s_0$. For a <perfect gas> this selects a compressive <shock>, whose upstream <shock>-frame <Mach number> is greater than one and whose downstream <shock>-frame flow is subsonic.

Here the upstream <Mach number> is $M_0=V/c_0$. The quadratic gives $V^2-c_0^2=(\gamma+1)UV/2>0$, so $\boxed{M_0>1}$. <Density> and <pressure> both rise. With $c_1^2=\gamma p_1/\rho_1$, one also has $V-U<c_1$. For example, put $M=V/c_0$ and use the normal-<shock> identity
$$
\frac{(V-U)^2}{c_1^2}=\frac{(\gamma-1)M^2+2}{2\gamma M^2-(\gamma-1)}<1\quad(M>1).
$$
The characteristic geometry is therefore $c_0<V<U+c_1$. The plus-family characteristics from the gas ahead are overtaken by the <shock>, while those behind catch up with it; they converge into the discontinuity. The minus-family speeds are $-c_0$ ahead and $U-c_1$ behind. The piston is the line $x=Ut$ and the <shock> is the steeper-in-$x/t$ line $x=Vt$.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For $U<0$ the same positive algebraic root remains real and satisfies $0<V<c_0$, because $V^2-c_0^2=(\gamma+1)UV/2<0$. The jump relations then give a <density> decrease, $\rho_1/\rho_0=V/(V-U)<1$, and a <pressure> decrease. Where both states remain thermodynamically positive, this is an expansion <shock>: its upstream <shock>-frame flow is subsonic and its <entropy> change has the forbidden sign. At sufficiently large negative $U$ the formula even predicts nonpositive <pressure>, an additional reason it cannot represent a physical gas state.

The observed outgoing wave is instead a continuous isentropic <rarefaction wave>. Its plus-family rays spread from the initial piston corner rather than coalescing. The fan head enters the undisturbed gas at $x=c_0t$; for moderate recession the tail is $x=(U+c_1)t$, with the uniform gas next to the piston moving at $U$ and $c_1=c_0+(\gamma-1)U/2>0$. The appropriate <Riemann invariant> is $u-2c/(\gamma-1)=-2c_0/(\gamma-1)$, so inside the fan, with $\xi=x/t$,
$$
u=\frac{2(\xi-c_0)}{\gamma+1},\qquad c=\frac{2c_0+(\gamma-1)\xi}{\gamma+1}.
$$
These expressions show the spreading characteristics in the right panel of the preceding figure. If $U\leq-2c_0/(\gamma-1)$, the gas cannot follow the piston with positive <sound speed>; a <vacuum> gap forms, bounded by the limiting <rarefaction wave> edge. Thus the fan description also identifies the strong-recession limit.
