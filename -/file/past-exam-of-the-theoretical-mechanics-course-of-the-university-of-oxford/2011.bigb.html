= Solution
{parent=i}

For a material <volume> moving with the <fluid>, the <Reynolds transport theorem> gives, componentwise,
$$
\frac d{dt}\int_{V(t)}q\,dV=\int_{V(t)}\bigl(\partial_tq+\nabla\cdot(q\mathbf u)\bigr)\,dV.
$$
For a scalar $q$ this is also $\int_{V(t)}(Dq/Dt+q\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)dV$. Apply the component formula to <momentum> <density> $q=\rho u_i$. Constant <density> and <incompressibility> make the integrand $\rho Du_i/Dt$.

The <traction> $\mathbf t(\mathbf n)$ is the <force> per unit <area> exerted by the material outside a surface on the material inside, with outward <unit normal> $\mathbf n$. Reversing the normal exchanges the two sides of the same internal surface. Thus $\mathbf t(-\mathbf n)=-\mathbf t(\mathbf n)$ expresses equal and opposite internal contact <forces>, the local form of <Newton's third law>.

With no <body force>, <Newton's second law> equates the rate of change of the material <volume>'s total <momentum> to its surface <force>. Combining this balance with the transport theorem gives
$$
\boxed{\int_{V(t)}\rho\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}\,dV=\int_{\partial V(t)}\mathbf t(\mathbf n)\,dS.}
$$
This is a <vector> identity, since the component argument applies to each direction.

= Solution

Define $\sigma_{ij}=\mathbf e_i\cdot\mathbf t(\mathbf e_j)$: the second index labels the normal direction of a coordinate face, and the first labels the <force> component. These nine entries form the <Cauchy stress tensor>.

Take a small tetrahedron with oblique face of <area> $S$ and outward normal $\mathbf n$. First suppose $n_j>0$; its other three faces have normals $-\mathbf e_j$ and areas $S_j=n_jS$, by <orthogonal> projection of the oblique face. The total surface <force> is
$$
S\mathbf t(\mathbf n)+\sum_jS_j\mathbf t(-\mathbf e_j).
$$
If its linear size is $\ell$, its inertia is $O(\ell^3)$ while its surface <forces> are $O(\ell^2)$. Divide the <momentum> balance by $S$ and shrink the tetrahedron to the point. Smoothness of the stress and the odd-normal relation then give $\mathbf t(\mathbf n)=\sum_jn_j\mathbf t(\mathbf e_j)$. Choosing coordinate faces with the appropriate reversed normals gives the same identity in the other octants. In components,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf t(\mathbf n)=\sum_{i,j}\mathbf e_i\sigma_{ij}n_j=\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n.}
$$
The face-<area> relations used are exactly $S_j=|n_j|S$, with each coordinate face oriented opposite the corresponding component of the oblique normal.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Insert the <traction> formula into the material-<volume> balance and apply the <divergence theorem> to each row of the <stress tensor>:
$$
\int_{V(t)}\rho\frac{Du_i}{Dt}\,dV=\int_{\partial V(t)}\sigma_{ij}n_j\,dS=\int_{V(t)}\partial_j\sigma_{ij}\,dV.
$$
The <volume> is arbitrary, so continuity of the fields gives the local <Cauchy momentum equation>
$$
\boxed{\rho\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}=\mathbf e_i\frac{\partial\sigma_{ij}}{\partial x_j}.}
$$
For an incompressible <Newtonian fluid>, the constitutive law is
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{ij}=-p\delta_{ij}+\mu(\partial_ju_i+\partial_iu_j).}
$$
Here the <pressure> enforces <incompressibility>, and the viscous stress is twice the <viscosity> times the symmetric rate-of-strain <tensor>. With constant $\mu$,
$$
\partial_j\sigma_{ij}=-\partial_ip+\mu\nabla^2u_i+\mu\partial_i(\partial_ju_j)=-\partial_ip+\mu\nabla^2u_i.
$$
Using $D/Dt=\partial_t+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla$ proves the stated <Navier-Stokes equations>.

The only <velocity> component is axial, so <incompressibility> gives $\partial_zw=0$. Thus $w=w(x,y)$, and the steady convective <acceleration> is $w\partial_z(w\mathbf k)=0$. The transverse <momentum> equations give $p_x=p_y=0$, so $p=p(z)$. The axial <momentum> equation is therefore
$$
\boxed{\mu(w_{xx}+w_{yy})=p'(z).}
$$
Its left side depends only on $x,y$, while its right side depends only on $z$. Equality throughout the pipe <forces> both to be one constant, say $G$. Consequently **the axial <pressure gradient> is constant**, and $p(z)=Gz+p_*$. This is absence of dependence on a coordinate, rather than probabilistic independence.

For <equilateral triangular pipe flow>, the <polynomial> vanishes on all three walls, so it satisfies <no-slip boundary conditions>. Direct differentiation gives
$$
\nabla_\perp^2\bigl[(x^2-3y^2)(x-a)\bigr]=(6x-2a)+(-6x+6a)=4a.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{A=\frac{G}{4a\mu},\qquad G=\frac{dp}{dz}.}
$$
For $G<0$, $A<0$ and the <velocity> is positive in the triangular interior.

At the wall $x=a$, the outward normal of the <fluid> is $\mathbf e_x$. The axial <traction> exerted by the wall on the <fluid> is $\mu w_x$, so the <fluid> exerts the opposite axial <traction> on the wall. Since $w_x(a,y)=A(a^2-3y^2)$, the <force> per unit axial length is
$$
\frac{\mathbf F}{\text{axial length}}=-\mu A\mathbf k\int_{-a/\sqrt3}^{a/\sqrt3}(a^2-3y^2)\,dy
=-\mu A\frac{4a^3}{3\sqrt3}\mathbf k
=\boxed{-\frac{a^2}{3\sqrt3}\frac{dp}{dz}\,\mathbf k.}
$$
It points with the flow for a negative axial <pressure gradient>. As a check, the triangular <area> is $a^2/\sqrt3$, so the total <pressure> driving <force> per unit length is $-Ga^2/\sqrt3$; each of the three equivalent walls receives one third.

Take the tangential length and <velocity> scales as order one. In the <boundary layer>, continuity reads $u_x+(\delta_2/\delta_1)V_Y=0$, so a nontrivial balance requires $\delta_2\sim\delta_1$. Tangential inertia is order one, and the normal viscous term has size $\mathrm{Re}^{-1}\delta_1^{-2}$. Balancing them gives
$$
\boxed{\delta_1=\delta_2=\mathrm{Re}^{-1/2}}
$$
up to constants absorbable in the scaled variables. Streamwise diffusion is then smaller by $\mathrm{Re}^{-1}$. The normal <momentum> equation has a <pressure> term of size $\delta_1^{-1}p_Y$ and inertial/viscous terms of size $\delta_1$, hence $p_Y=0$ at leading order. The remaining <Prandtl boundary-layer equations> are
$$
\boxed{uu_x+Vu_Y=-p_x+u_{YY},\qquad p_Y=0,\qquad u_x+V_Y=0.}
$$
The <boundary conditions> are **no slip and no penetration at the plate, and matching to the outer tangential <velocity>**:
$$
\boxed{u(x,0)=0,\qquad V(x,0)=0,\qquad u(x,Y)\longrightarrow U_s(x)\quad(Y\to\infty).}
$$
The outer inviscid tangential <momentum> equation supplies $p_x=-U_sU_s'$; <pressure> is shared between the <boundary layer> and outer flow because it has no leading normal variation.

Continuity allows a <stream function> with $u=\Psi_Y$ and $V=-\Psi_x$. Substituting these into the tangential equation gives
$$
uu_x+Vu_Y=\Psi_Y\Psi_{xY}-\Psi_x\Psi_{YY}.

The wall no-penetration condition makes $\Psi$ constant along the wall; its additive freedom may be used to set that constant to zero.

For a <boundary layer with exponentially varying outer velocity>, the ansatz has $u=\Psi_Y=e^{(\beta+\gamma)x}f'(\eta)$. Matching it to $Ae^{\alpha x}$ requires $\beta+\gamma=\alpha$ and $f'(\infty)=A$. The nonlinear terms in the stream-<function> equation scale as $e^{2(\beta+\gamma)x}$, whereas $\Psi_{YYY}$ scales as $e^{(\beta+3\gamma)x}$. Matching exponents gives $\beta=\gamma$, hence
$$
\boxed{\beta=\gamma=\alpha/2.}
$$
More explicitly,
$$
\Psi_Y\Psi_{xY}-\Psi_x\Psi_{YY}
=e^{2\alpha x}\left[\alpha(f')^2-\frac\alpha2ff''\right],\qquad
\Psi_{YYY}=e^{2\alpha x}f''',\qquad U_sU_s'=\alpha A^2e^{2\alpha x}.
$$
Canceling the common factor gives
$$
\boxed{f'''+\frac\alpha2ff''+\alpha\bigl(A^2-(f')^2\bigr)=0.}
$$
Together with $f(0)=0$, the other two conditions are
$$
\boxed{f'(0)=0,\qquad f'(\eta)\longrightarrow A\quad(\eta\to\infty).}
$$
They represent wall no slip and outer-flow matching, respectively.

Use <autonomous third-order reduction using the dependent variable>. On a monotone branch where $f$ can serve as the independent variable, the <chain rule> gives
$$
f''=GG_f,\qquad f'''=G(G_f^2+GG_{ff}).
$$
Therefore the reduced second-order equation is
$$
\boxed{G^2G_{ff}+G(G_f)^2+\frac\alpha2 fGG_f+\alpha(A^2-G^2)=0.}
$$
The wall and matching conditions become
$$
\boxed{G(0)=0,\qquad G(f)\longrightarrow A\text{ as }f\to\operatorname{sgn}(A)\infty.}
$$
For the usual positive outer flow this is the limit $f\to+\infty$. The reduced equation is singular at $G=0$: if $f''(0)=b\ne0$, then $f\sim b\eta^2/2$ and $G\sim b\eta$, so $G$ has square-root behavior as a <function> of $f$ near the wall. A finite value for $G_f(0)$ should not be imposed as an extra <boundary condition>.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

An attached similarity profile must approach $f'\to A$ as $\eta\to\infty$. Linearizing about $f\sim A\eta+\text{constant}$ gives, for the small <velocity> correction $g=f'-A$,
$$
g''+\frac{\alpha A}{2}\eta g'-2\alpha A g\simeq0.
$$
When $\alpha A>0$, one independent far-field correction decays with a Gaussian factor; when $\alpha A<0$, the two nontrivial asymptotic modes grow, one algebraically and one exponentially, so a nonconstant wall profile cannot decay into the prescribed outer flow. Thus the attached exponential similarity construction requires
$$
\boxed{\alpha A>0.}
$$
Equivalently the outer speed increases along the direction of motion, giving a favorable <pressure gradient>. In the intended flow away from the leading edge along positive $x$, this means **$A>0$ and $\alpha>0$**. The sign-reversed choice $A<0,\alpha<0$ describes locally favorable flow in the opposite direction; it is not a layer developing downstream from the stated leading edge.

The asymptotic approximation also requires a geometrically thin layer, away from the leading-edge region. Comparing its thickness with the streamwise variation length $1/|\alpha|$ gives the local condition $\mathrm{Re}|A|e^{\alpha x}/|\alpha|\gg1$. An exponentially decaying outer speed eventually violates this condition on an infinite downstream interval. These scale and orientation requirements distinguish formal similarity reduction from a uniformly valid attached <boundary layer>.

Let $\mathrm{Re}=\rho UL/\mu$ and use the given anisotropic scales. Continuity makes the transverse <velocity> scale $\delta U$ consistent with the gap height $\delta L$. The streamwise viscous stress <divergence> has scale $\mu U/(\delta^2L^2)$, while the streamwise <pressure gradient> has scale $\Pi/L$. Their balance selects
$$
\boxed{\Pi=\frac{\mu U}{\delta^2L}.}
$$
The streamwise equation then has the dimensionless form
$$
\delta^2\mathrm{Re}(\widehat u\widehat u_{\widehat x}+\widehat w\widehat u_{\widehat z})
=-\widehat p_{\widehat x}+\widehat u_{\widehat z\widehat z}+\delta^2\widehat u_{\widehat x\widehat x}.
$$
The normal equation becomes
$$
\delta^4\mathrm{Re}(\widehat u\widehat w_{\widehat x}+\widehat w\widehat w_{\widehat z})
=-\widehat p_{\widehat z}+\delta^2\widehat w_{\widehat z\widehat z}+\delta^4\widehat w_{\widehat x\widehat x}.
$$
When $\delta\ll1$ and $\delta^2\mathrm{Re}\ll1$, neglecting the indicated smaller terms gives
$$
\boxed{\widehat u_{\widehat z\widehat z}=\widehat p_{\widehat x},\qquad\widehat p_{\widehat z}=0,\qquad
\widehat u_{\widehat x}+\widehat w_{\widehat z}=0.}
$$
The bottom conditions express **no slip on a plate moving at speed $U$ and no penetration**. The top conditions express **no slip and no penetration on the stationary wall**. The leading <pressure> is a <function> of the streamwise coordinate alone.

Suppress hats temporarily. Mass conservation between any two cross-sections, together with impermeability of both walls, makes their fluxes equal. Where the wall is differentiable, the same result follows locally by differentiating the <flux> with a variable upper limit and using continuity gives
$$
Q'(x)=\int_0^{h(x)}u_x\,dz+u(x,h)h'=-\int_0^hw_z\,dz=-(w(x,h)-w(x,0))=0.
$$
Both the upper-limit term and the boundary velocities vanish by the wall conditions. Thus **the <flux> is constant**. Since $p=p(x)$, integrate $u_{zz}=p'(x)$ twice and impose $u(0)=1$, $u(h)=0$:
$$
\boxed{u(x,z)=1-\frac zh+\frac{p'}2z(z-h).}
$$
Integrating across the gap gives $Q=h/2-p'h^3/12$. Restoring hats,
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\widehat p}{d\widehat x}=\frac{6(\widehat h-2Q)}{\widehat h^3},\qquad
\widehat p(-1)=\widehat p(1)=0.}
$$
The endpoint conditions are the specified equal reservoir pressures after subtracting $p_0$ and dividing by the <pressure> scale.

The <flux average for a translating lubrication gap> follows by integrating the <pressure gradient> between the endpoints:
$$
0=6\int_{-1}^1\frac{\widehat h-2Q}{\widehat h^3}\,d\widehat x.
$$
If $2Q$ were smaller than the minimum height, the integrand would be strictly positive everywhere, contradicting zero total <pressure> change. If it were larger than the maximum, the integrand would be strictly negative. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\min\widehat h\leq2Q\leq\max\widehat h.}
$$
Equivalently,
$$
2Q=\frac{\int_{-1}^1\widehat h^{-2}\,d\widehat x}{\int_{-1}^1\widehat h^{-3}\,d\widehat x}
$$
is a weighted average of the height with strictly positive weight $\widehat h^{-3}$. This also identifies the constant <flux> uniquely.

For <pressure lobes in a symmetric translating lubrication gap>, the height integrals are
$$
\int_{-1}^1\widehat h^{-2}\,d\widehat x=\int_{-1}^1\frac{d\widehat x}{1+\widehat x^2}=\frac\pi2,\qquad
\int_{-1}^1\widehat h^{-3}\,d\widehat x=\left[\frac{\widehat x}{\sqrt{1+\widehat x^2}}\right]_{-1}^1=\sqrt2.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{Q=\frac\pi{4\sqrt2}.}
$$
The denominator of the <pressure gradient> is positive. Its numerator changes sign when $\sqrt{1+\widehat x^2}=\pi/(2\sqrt2)$, that is at $\widehat x=\pm x_*$ with $x_*=(\pi^2/8-1)^{1/2}$. The <gradient> is positive for $|\widehat x|>x_*$, zero at those two points, and negative for $|\widehat x|<x_*$. Integration, with either endpoint condition, gives
$$
\boxed{\widehat p(\widehat x)=6\arctan\widehat x-\frac{3\pi}{\sqrt2}\frac{\widehat x}{\sqrt{1+\widehat x^2}}.}
$$
This <function> is odd and vanishes at $-1,0,1$. It has a positive maximum at $-x_*$ and a negative minimum at $x_*$. Thus the sketch has a positive <pressure> lobe in the converging left half of the gap and a negative gauge-<pressure> lobe in the diverging right half.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $R(t)$ rotate <vectors> from the rotating frame to the inertial frame. For any rotating-frame <vector> $\mathbf a$, differentiating $R\mathbf a$ adds $R(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf a)$ to $R\dot{\mathbf a}$. Thus an inertial particle <velocity> is $R(\mathbf u+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf x)$, and, for constant <angular velocity>, its <acceleration> is
$$
R\left[\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf u+\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf x)\right].
$$
Insert this into the inertial <Euler equations> and express <forces> in the rotating coordinates. This gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf u+\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf x)=-\rho^{-1}\nabla p+\mathbf g.}
$$
The additional terms are the <Coriolis acceleration> and the centripetal term associated with frame rotation. There is no angular-<acceleration> term because $\boldsymbol\Omega$ is constant.

Write the conservative <body force> as $\mathbf g=-\nabla\Phi_g$. The supplied <vector> identity gives $\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf x)=-\tfrac12\nabla|\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf x|^2$. For constant <density>, define
$$
\boxed{P=p+\rho\Phi_g-\frac\rho2|\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf x|^2.}
$$
Moving the centrifugal and conservative terms to the right then gives $\partial_t\mathbf u+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf u=-\rho^{-1}\nabla P$. Only the <gradient> of this reduced <pressure> matters, so an additive spatial constant is immaterial.

= Solution

Linearize about rest, with the equilibrium <forces> already absorbed in the reduced <pressure>. The quadratic convective <acceleration> drops out. Since $\mathbf k\times(u\mathbf i+v\mathbf j+w\mathbf k)=-v\mathbf i+u\mathbf j$, the component equations are
$$
\boxed{u_t-2\Omega v=-P_x/\rho,\qquad v_t+2\Omega u=-P_y/\rho,\qquad w_t=-P_z/\rho,\qquad u_x+v_y+w_z=0.}
$$
The scan prints $v_w$ in the <continuity equation>; **the <derivative> required by <incompressibility> is $v_y$**.

To eliminate <pressure>, put $\zeta=v_x-u_y$ and $\nabla_h^2=\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2$. Taking the vertical component of the <curl> of <momentum> gives
$$
\zeta_t+2\Omega(u_x+v_y)=0,\qquad\text{so}\qquad\zeta_t=2\Omega w_z.
$$
Taking horizontal <divergence> of <momentum> and substituting continuity gives
$$
\rho^{-1}\nabla_h^2P=w_{zt}+2\Omega\zeta.
$$
Differentiate this in $z$ and use $P_z/\rho=-w_t$:
$$
\partial_t(\nabla^2w)+2\Omega\zeta_z=0.
$$
One more time <derivative>, followed by the <vorticity> relation, yields the <Poincaré equation for inertial waves>
$$
\boxed{\partial_t^2(\nabla^2w)+4\Omega^2w_{zz}=0.}
$$
This derivation retains all three <velocity> components, including the transverse component coupled by rotation.

Write $\omega$ for the nonzero forcing <frequency>, to distinguish it from the vertical <velocity> $w$. Put $k_z=\pi/h$ and $Z(z)=\cos(\pi(2z+h)/(2h))=-\sin(k_zz)$. Then $Z(0)=Z(h)=0$ and $Z''=-k_z^2Z$, so it meets the rigid-wall conditions. Substituting $w=\operatorname{Re}(f(x)e^{-i\omega t}Z(z))$ into the scalar <wave> equation gives
$$
-\omega^2(f''-k_z^2f)-4\Omega^2k_z^2f=0,
$$
or
$$
\boxed{f''+k_z^2\left(\frac{4\Omega^2}{\omega^2}-1\right)f=0,\qquad f(0)=a.}
$$
The <radiation sign for an inertial wave> must exclude any disturbance arriving from $x=+\infty$. This means an <radiation condition> for propagating <waves>, or bounded decay for an evanescent <wave>.

For $0<|\omega|<2|\Omega|$, put $q=k_z\sqrt{4\Omega^2/\omega^2-1}$. The solutions are oscillatory. The <inertial-wave dispersion relation> is $\omega^2=4\Omega^2k_z^2/(k_x^2+k_z^2)$; its horizontal <group velocity> is $c_{g,x}=-\omega k_x/(k_x^2+k_z^2)$. Positive energy transport into the channel therefore requires $k_x=-\operatorname{sgn}(\omega)q$. The solution is
$$
\boxed{w(x,z,t)=\operatorname{Re}\left[a\,e^{-i\operatorname{sgn}(\omega)qx-i\omega t}\cos\left(\frac\pi{2h}(2z+h)\right)\right].}
$$
For a positive <frequency>, the phase travels towards negative $x$ while the <group velocity> travels towards positive $x$. Choosing the outgoing sign from the <phase velocity> alone would give the wrong result.

For $|\omega|>2|\Omega|$, set $\kappa=k_z\sqrt{1-4\Omega^2/\omega^2}$. Excluding the exponentially growing solution gives
$$
\boxed{w(x,z,t)=\operatorname{Re}\left[a\,e^{-\kappa x-i\omega t}\cos\left(\frac\pi{2h}(2z+h)\right)\right].}
$$
This disturbance decays away from the wavemaker and does not propagate horizontally. At the cutoff the ODE degenerates to $f''=0$; zero <frequency> is not a nonzero forced harmonic mode with this vertical shape.

There is a second printed inconsistency: the stated <velocity> $u\mathbf i+w\mathbf k$, if taken literally with no $y$ dependence and no transverse <velocity>, cannot support a nontrivial rotating <inertial wave>. Its transverse <momentum> equation would give $2\Omega u=0$, then continuity and the rigid walls would <force> $w=0$. The scalar solutions above are the intended inertial-<wave> solutions when two-dimensional means dependence on $x,z$ while **retaining the transverse component $v$**. For a mode, $-i\omega v+2\Omega u=0$ explicitly supplies the generally nonzero transverse <velocity>. Thus the scalar equation can be solved as requested, but the full <vector> field needs this correction to the printed two-component description.

Take the nondegenerate <stationary point> in the interior of the integration interval. Choose a smooth cutoff equal to one near $k_\star$. Away from its support, $\psi'$ is bounded away from zero. <Integration by parts> using $e^{it\psi}=(it\psi')^{-1}\partial_ke^{it\psi}$ makes that nonstationary contribution $O(t^{-1})$, under the usual smooth-phase assumptions.

Near the <stationary point> use the signed coordinate
$$
s=\operatorname{sgn}(k-k_\star)\sqrt{2(\psi(k)-\psi(k_\star))}.
$$
Since $\psi''(k_\star)>0$, this is locally invertible, with $ds/dk=\sqrt{\psi''(k_\star)}$ at the point. The local <integral> becomes
$$
e^{it\psi(k_\star)}\int a(s)e^{its^2/2}\,ds,\qquad a(0)=\frac{f(k_\star)}{\sqrt{\psi''(k_\star)}}.
$$
Scale $s=\sqrt{2/t}\,r$. The supplied <Fresnel integral> gives $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{ir^2}dr=e^{i\pi/4}\sqrt\pi$. Approximating the smooth <amplitude> by its value at zero, with a cutoff and a nonstationary estimate for the tails, therefore gives
$$
\boxed{I(t)=f(k_\star)e^{it\psi(k_\star)+i\pi/4}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{t\psi''(k_\star)}}+o(t^{-1/2}).}
$$
If $f(k_\star)\ne0$, this is the requested asymptotic equivalence. The phase shift $\pi/4$ comes from the positive quadratic curvature. A <stationary point> at an integration endpoint instead needs a one-sided formula.

The printed auxiliary estimate asserting an $O(\gamma^{-1})$ Fourier <integral> for every continuous <amplitude> is too strong without extra regularity. The argument here uses <integration by parts> only for the actual smooth, cutoff nonstationary amplitudes, where that estimate is justified; continuity alone generally guarantees convergence to zero, not this rate.

Irrotationality permits a <velocity potential> with $\mathbf u=(\phi_x,\phi_z)$. <Incompressibility> gives <Laplace's equation> in the moving domain $z<\eta(x,t)$. Neglecting surface tension and taking the free-surface <pressure> as constant atmospheric <pressure>, the full surface conditions are
$$
\boxed{\phi_{xx}+\phi_{zz}=0\quad(z<\eta),\qquad
\eta_t+\phi_x\eta_x=\phi_z\quad(z=\eta),\qquad
\phi_t+\tfrac12(\phi_x^2+\phi_z^2)+g\eta=0\quad(z=\eta).}
$$
The kinematic condition makes the <free surface> material; the dynamic condition is <Bernoulli's equation> on that surface. A time-dependent additive gauge in $\phi$ has absorbed the spatially uniform Bernoulli constant. Deep below the surface, impose $\nabla\phi\to0$ as $z\to-\infty$, together with suitable horizontal decay. Initially $\eta(x,0)=\eta_0(x)$ and the <fluid> is at rest, so $\nabla\phi(x,z,0)=0$; its initial constant may be chosen as zero.

= Solution

Linearize about a flat quiescent surface. The product $\phi_x\eta_x$ and the kinetic-energy term are quadratic, and the <boundary conditions> can be evaluated at $z=0$ to first order. Thus $\eta_t=\phi_z$ and $\phi_t+g\eta=0$ there. Fourier transformation in $x$ changes $\partial_x^2$ into multiplication by $-k^2$, giving
$$
\boxed{\widehat\phi_{zz}=k^2\widehat\phi\quad(z<0),\qquad\widehat\phi_z\to0\quad(z\to-\infty),\qquad
\widehat\eta_t=\widehat\phi_z\quad(z=0),\qquad\widehat\phi_t+g\widehat\eta=0\quad(z=0).}
$$
The transform uses $e^{-ikx}$ in its forward definition; the inverse transform therefore uses $e^{ikx}/(2\pi)$. The zero-<wavenumber> potential has an irrelevant spatially constant gauge freedom.

= Solution

For $k\ne0$, the solution decaying at depth is $\widehat\phi(k,z,t)=C(k,t)e^{|k|z}$. The surface conditions give $\widehat\eta_t=|k|C$ and $C_t=-g\widehat\eta$, hence
$$
\widehat\eta_{tt}+g|k|\widehat\eta=0.
$$
Initial rest implies $\widehat\eta_t(k,0)=0$. Define the transform of the initial elevation by $\widehat\eta_0(k)=\int_{\mathbb R}\eta_0(x)e^{-ikx}dx$. Solving this oscillator equation and inverting the transform gives
$$
\boxed{\eta(x,t)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}\widehat\eta_0(k)\cos(t\sqrt{g|k|})e^{ikx}\,dk.}
$$
The formula also includes the continuous zero-<frequency> limit. For real even initial data, the transform is real and even, ensuring a real even surface profile at every time.

= Solution

For <wavenumber selection by a moving observer of deep-water waves>, an observer with nonzero <velocity> $V$ follows $x=Vt$ up to a constant initial displacement. Splitting the cosine into exponentials gives phase <functions> $\psi_\pm(k)=kV\pm\sqrt{g|k|}$. Their stationary points satisfy
$$
V\pm\frac{\sqrt g\,\operatorname{sgn}k}{2\sqrt{|k|}}=0,
$$
so the selected wavenumbers are
$$
\boxed{k=\pm\frac g{4V^2}.}
$$
The two signs arise in the conjugate branches needed for a real <wave>. Equivalently the observer selects <wave> packets whose <group velocity> equals $V$. Stationary-phase contributions decay as $t^{-1/2}$, whereas smooth nonstationary contributions decay more rapidly, so these are the dominant wavenumbers when the initial spectrum is nonzero there.

For real even data, one may make this explicit. Set $k_*=g/(4V^2)$. The stationary curvature has magnitude $2|V|^3/g$, yielding
$$
\eta(Vt,t)\sim\widehat\eta_0(k_*)\sqrt{\frac{g}{4\pi|V|^3t}}\cos\left(\frac{gt}{4|V|}-\frac\pi4\right)
$$
when its leading coefficient is nonzero. If that spectral value vanishes, the leading stationary term vanishes too. At $V=0$ there is no finite nonzero stationary <wavenumber> selected by this condition.

A <weak solution> is a field $\mathbf u$ for which the composed densities $\mathbf P(x,t,\mathbf u)$ and fluxes $\mathbf Q(x,t,\mathbf u)$ are locally integrable and
$$
\boxed{\iint\bigl(\mathbf P\cdot\partial_t\boldsymbol\varphi+\mathbf Q\cdot\partial_x\boldsymbol\varphi\bigr)\,dx\,dt=0}
$$
for every smooth compactly supported <vector> <test function> $\boldsymbol\varphi$ in the spacetime domain. This is the distributional form of the <conservation law>, obtained by moving <derivatives> onto the <test function>. If tests are allowed to meet an initial boundary $t=0$, the specified initial <density> contributes the additional term $\int\mathbf P(x,0,\mathbf u_0)\cdot\boldsymbol\varphi(x,0)dx$ in the usual initial-value formulation.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

If the fields are continuously differentiable and obey the pointwise <conservation law>, <integration by parts> against a compactly supported <test function> gives the weak identity, with no boundary terms. Conversely, integrate the weak identity by parts for a continuously differentiable field:
$$
\iint(\partial_t\mathbf P+\partial_x\mathbf Q)\cdot\boldsymbol\varphi\,dx\,dt=0
$$
for every <test function>. The <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations>, applied to each component, makes the continuous integrand vanish at every point. Hence **the classical and weak formulations are equivalent for continuously differentiable fields**.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Write the shock as $x=s(t)$ and let the plus trace be on $x>s(t)$. Locally its discontinuous part can be represented with the Heaviside factor $H(x-s(t))$. Distributional differentiation gives $\partial_tH=-s'(t)\delta(x-s(t))$ and $\partial_xH=\delta(x-s(t))$. The singular part of the <conservation law> is therefore
$$
\bigl(-s'(t)[\mathbf P]_-^++[\mathbf Q]_-^+\bigr)\delta(x-s(t)).
$$
It must vanish, so the <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions> are
$$
\boxed{V[\mathbf P]_-^+=[\mathbf Q]_-^+,\qquad V=s'(t).}
$$
The smooth parts of the fields still satisfy the classical <conservation law> on each side. The jump condition ensures that the moving discontinuity creates no extra source of the conserved quantities.

= Solution

Put $v=u-V$, the <velocity> in the shock frame. The mass jump condition gives a common <mass flux> $j=\rho v$. Subtracting $V$ times the mass balance from the <momentum> balance gives
$$
\boxed{[\rho(u-V)]_-^+=0,\qquad[p+\rho(u-V)^2]_-^+=0.}
$$
For energy, transform the conserved energy to the shock frame by subtracting $V$ times <momentum> and adding $V^2/2$ times mass. The resulting energy <flux> is
$$
\rho v\left(\frac{v^2}{2}+\frac{\gamma p}{(\gamma-1)\rho}\right).
$$
For a genuine shock, $j\ne0$; dividing its continuous energy <flux> by the same nonzero $j$ on both sides gives
$$
\boxed{\left[\frac{(u-V)^2}{2}+\frac{\gamma p}{(\gamma-1)\rho}\right]_-^+=0.}
$$
The second term is the ideal-gas <specific enthalpy>. A <contact discontinuity> has zero relative <mass flux> and does not in general require equal specific enthalpies; the division here is appropriate for the shock specified in the question.

{parent=b}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let the undisturbed state ahead have $(\rho_0,p_0,u_0)=(\rho_0,p_0,0)$, and the uniform compressed state behind have <velocity> $u_1=U$. The piston boundary fixes that downstream <velocity>. Mass and <momentum> jumps give
$$
\rho_1(V-U)=\rho_0V,\qquad p_1-p_0=\rho_0UV.
$$
Thus $\rho_1/\rho_0=V/(V-U)$. Equality of specific stagnation <enthalpy> in the shock frame is
$$
\frac{V^2}{2}+\frac{\gamma p_0}{(\gamma-1)\rho_0}
=\frac{(V-U)^2}{2}+\frac{\gamma p_1}{(\gamma-1)\rho_1}.
$$
Substitute $p_1=p_0+\rho_0UV$ and $1/\rho_1=(V-U)/(\rho_0V)$, then multiply by $2(\gamma-1)V$. The resulting difference factors as $U[-2V^2+(\gamma+1)UV+2\gamma p_0/\rho_0]=0$. Since the piston moves into the gas with $U>0$, division gives
$$
\boxed{2V^2-(\gamma+1)UV-2c_0^2=0,\qquad c_0^2=\gamma p_0/\rho_0.}
$$
This determines the constant speed of the compression shock ahead of the piston.

= iii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For a <piston-driven normal shock> with $U>0$, $c_0>0$ and $\gamma>1$, the quadratic has one positive and one negative root. The forward-moving shock uses
$$
\boxed{V=\frac{(\gamma+1)U+\sqrt{(\gamma+1)^2U^2+16c_0^2}}4.}
$$
Since the square root is greater than $(\gamma+1)U$,
$$
V-U>\frac{\gamma-1}{2}U>0.
$$
Therefore **there is always a forward root with shock speed greater than the gas and piston speed** under the stated compression assumptions. This also makes the <density> ratio $V/(V-U)$ positive and greater than one. Evaluating the quadratic at $V=c_0$ gives a negative value, so the positive root additionally has $V>c_0$: it is <supersonic> relative to the undisturbed gas. The negative root does not describe the shock driven into the region $x>0$.
