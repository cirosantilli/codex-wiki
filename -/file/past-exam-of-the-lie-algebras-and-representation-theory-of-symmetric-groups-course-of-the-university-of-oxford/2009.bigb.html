= Solution
{parent=a}

First prove the unlettered common-<eigenvector> assertion, in the stronger form that some nonzero vector is killed by every operator in $W$. This is the linear form of <Engel's theorem>.

Induct on $\dim W$, with the zero algebra immediate. If $a^r=0$, the operator $\operatorname{ad}a$ on $\operatorname{End}V$ is nilpotent: left and right multiplication commute, and every term in $(L_a-R_a)^{2r-1}$ contains a power of $a$ at least $r$. This argument works in every characteristic.

For a proper <Lie subalgebra> $H<W$, its action on $W/H$ by brackets is therefore by nilpotent operators. The image of this action has <dimension> at most $\dim H<\dim W$, so induction gives a nonzero class $w+H$ killed by $H$. Thus the normalizer $N_W(H)=\{w:[w,H]\subseteq H\}$ strictly contains $H$. Choose $H$ maximal proper. Its normalizer must be $W$, so $H$ is an ideal. Moreover $W/H$ has <dimension> one, since otherwise any one-dimensional subalgebra of the quotient would lift to an intermediate proper subalgebra.

Induction applied to $H$ acting on $V$ makes $V_0=\{v:Hv=0\}$ nonzero. This space is $W$-invariant: for $h\in H,x\in W,v\in V_0$, $h(xv)=x(hv)+[h,x]v=0$. Choose $x$ complementing $H$ in $W$. Its restriction to the nonzero space $V_0$ is nilpotent and has a nonzero kernel. A vector in this kernel is killed by $x$ and $H$, hence by all of $W$. This proves the preliminary assertion, with every common <eigenvalue> equal to zero.

Now apply it to the adjoint action of $L$ on itself. If $L\ne0$, its common zero space is the center $Z(L)$, which is therefore nonzero. Every induced adjoint map on $L/Z(L)$ remains nilpotent. Induction on <dimension> makes that quotient nilpotent. A central extension of a <nilpotent Lie algebra> is nilpotent: if its lower central series vanishes after projection, the next term lies in the center and the following term is zero. Hence **$L$ is nilpotent**. The zero algebra is immediate.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Every subalgebra of a <nilpotent Lie algebra> is nilpotent, since its lower central series is contained term by term in the ambient lower central series.

Conversely, if $L$ were not nilpotent, part (a) would give $x$ for which $\operatorname{ad}x$ is not nilpotent. Over an <algebraically closed field>, such an endomorphism has a nonzero <eigenvalue> $\lambda$ and <eigenvector> $y$. The vectors $x,y$ are independent because $[x,x]=0$ while $[x,y]=\lambda y\ne0$. Their span is a two-dimensional subalgebra, and its lower central series is $Ky$ at every stage after the first. It is therefore not nilpotent, a contradiction. Thus
$$
\boxed{L\text{ nilpotent}\iff\text{every two-dimensional subalgebra is nilpotent}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=c}

Polarizing $[x,[x,z]]=0$ gives
$$
[x,[y,z]]+[y,[x,z]]=0.
$$
Let $T=[x,[y,z]]$. Antisymmetry and this identity give $[y,[z,x]]=T$ and $[z,[x,y]]=T$. The <Jacobi identity> therefore gives $3T=0$. Since the characteristic is not three, $T=0$ for every triple. Consequently
$$
\boxed{[L,[L,L]]=0,\qquad L\text{ has nilpotency class at most two}.}
$$
No division by two was used, so the proof also applies in characteristic two with the usual alternating definition of a <Lie bracket>.

The <Killing form> is $\kappa_L(x,y)=\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{ad}x\operatorname{ad}y)$. Write $X=\operatorname{ad}x$, $Y=\operatorname{ad}y$, $Z=\operatorname{ad}z$. Since $\operatorname{ad}[x,y]=[X,Y]$, cyclicity of <trace> gives
$$
\kappa_L([x,y],z)=\operatorname{Tr}((XY-YX)Z)=\operatorname{Tr}(X(YZ-ZY))=\kappa_L(x,[y,z]).
$$
If $x\in I^\perp$, $a\in L$ and $y\in I$, invariance gives $\kappa_L([a,x],y)=\kappa_L(x,[y,a])=0$, because $[y,a]\in I$. Thus **$I^\perp$ is an ideal**. For $x,y\in I$, the restrictions of their adjoint maps to $I$ are the adjoint maps of $I$, and their induced maps on $L/I$ vanish. Block traces therefore give $\kappa_L|_{I\times I}=\kappa_I$.

If $A$ is an Abelian ideal, $\operatorname{ad}a\operatorname{ad}x$ maps $L$ into $A$ and vanishes on $A$ for $a\in A$. In a <basis> starting with $A$ it has zero diagonal blocks, hence <trace> zero. Therefore **every Abelian ideal lies in $L^\perp$**.

<Cartan criterion for semisimplicity> says that a finite-dimensional complex <Lie algebra> is semisimple exactly when its <Killing form> is nondegenerate. To see why nondegeneracy implies semisimplicity, any nonzero solvable ideal has a last nonzero derived term which is an Abelian ideal of $L$. The previous paragraph would put it in the radical of the <Killing form>, a contradiction.

If $L$ is a semisimple ideal of $M$, the restriction $\kappa_M|_L=\kappa_L$ is nondegenerate. Put $J=L^{\perp_{\kappa_M}}$. This is an ideal of $M$ by the argument above, and nondegeneracy on $L$ gives $L\cap J=0$ and $M=L+J$. One way to check the latter is to restrict the pairing map $M\to L^*$: its restriction to $L$ is an isomorphism, so every vector of $M$ can be adjusted by an element of $L$ to lie in its kernel $J$. Since both are ideals, $[L,J]\subseteq L\cap J=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{M=L\oplus J\text{ as a direct sum of ideals}.}
$$
Apply this to the supplied extension by the derivation. If $\delta=0$, it is $\operatorname{ad}0$. Otherwise $D$ and $J$ are one-dimensional, and projection $J\to D$ is an isomorphism. Choose its vector with $D$-component $\delta$, writing it as $j=\delta-x$ for $x\in L$. Because $[J,L]=0$, for every $l\in L$ one has $0=[j,l]=\delta(l)-[x,l]$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\delta=\operatorname{ad}_Lx.}
$$
This also handles the sign in the extension bracket explicitly.

A <base of a root system> is a vector-space <basis> $\Delta$ of the ambient Euclidean space such that every root is an integer combination of its elements with all coefficients nonnegative or all nonpositive. The corresponding nonzero nonnegative combinations are the <positive roots>.

If distinct $\alpha,\beta\in\Delta$ had positive <inner product>, the allowed root-difference fact would make $\alpha-\beta$ a root. Its simple-root coefficients have opposite signs, contradicting the definition. Hence **distinct <simple roots> have nonpositive <inner product>**.

For a <positive root> $\gamma=\sum n_\alpha\alpha$ which is not simple, the identity $(\gamma,\gamma)=\sum n_\alpha(\gamma,\alpha)>0$ gives a <simple root> $\alpha$ with $n_\alpha>0$ and $(\gamma,\alpha)>0$. The allowed fact makes $\gamma-\alpha$ a root. Its coefficients remain nonnegative, and it is nonzero because $\gamma$ is not simple. It has smaller height $\sum n_\alpha$. Induction expresses it as a sum of <simple roots> with each partial sum a root; appending $\alpha$ gives the required expression for $\gamma$.

Finally, reflection in a <simple root> changes only that root's coefficient:
$$
\sigma_\alpha(\gamma)=\gamma-\frac{2(\gamma,\alpha)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}\alpha.
$$
For positive $\gamma\ne\alpha$, at least one other simple coefficient is positive: in a reduced <root system> the only <positive root> on the line of $\alpha$ is $\alpha$ itself. That other coefficient is unchanged. The reflected vector is a root, so its coefficients cannot all be nonpositive and must all be nonnegative. Thus **$\sigma_\alpha$ preserves all <positive roots> except $\alpha$**, which it sends to $-\alpha$.

= Solution

Use the column-coroot convention $C_{ij}=2(\alpha_i,\alpha_j)/(\alpha_j,\alpha_j)$, consistent with the printed <matrix> and the stated shorter first root. It gives
$$
\frac{2(\alpha,\beta)}{\|\beta\|^2}=-1,\qquad\frac{2(\beta,\alpha)}{\|\alpha\|^2}=-3,\qquad\|\beta\|^2=3\|\alpha\|^2.
$$
The <root string> through $\beta$ in the $\alpha$ direction contains $\beta,\beta+\alpha,\beta+2\alpha,\beta+3\alpha$. Reflecting $3\alpha+\beta$ in $\beta$ gives $3\alpha+2\beta$. Together with $\alpha$ and their negatives these give
$$
\boxed{\Phi=\pm\{\alpha,\ \beta,\ \alpha+\beta,\ 2\alpha+\beta,\ 3\alpha+\beta,\ 3\alpha+2\beta\}.}
$$
There are no further roots. Indeed the simple reflections act on coefficient pairs by $(a,b)\mapsto(-a+3b,b)$ and $(a,b)\mapsto(a,a-b)$; the displayed twelve-element set is closed under both. Every positive nonsimple root can be reflected to a <positive root> of smaller height: choose a <simple root> with positive <inner product> as in (a), and its positive integral Cartan pairing decreases height. Repetition reaches a <simple root>, so every root lies in an orbit already contained in the list. This is the <G2 root system>.

With $\alpha=e_1$, the <inner product> and length give the two reflected possibilities
$$
\boxed{\beta=-\frac32e_1+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}e_2\quad\text{or}\quad\beta=-\frac32e_1-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}e_2.}
$$

The <Young diagram> is $[\lambda]=\{(i,j):1\leq i\leq k,\ 1\leq j\leq\lambda_i\}$. The <hook length> of a box is
$$
h(i,j)=\lambda_i-j+\lambda'_j-i+1,
$$
counting the box itself, the boxes to its right in the same row, and the boxes below it in the same column. The <hook-length formula> is
$$
\boxed{f^\lambda=\frac{n!}{\prod_{(i,j)\in[\lambda]}h(i,j)}.}
$$
The first implication in the equivalence is immediate: **if every <hook length> is odd, no hook can have length two**.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Assume no hook has length two. The last row has length one, since otherwise its penultimate box has <hook length> two. If two adjacent row lengths are equal, take the last two rows in the maximal block of that length; the last box in the upper of those two rows has <hook length> two. Thus all row lengths are distinct. If a gap $\lambda_i-\lambda_{i+1}$ were at least two, the penultimate box in row $i$ would again have <hook length> two, since there would be no boxes below it. Therefore **every successive gap equals one and the final part equals one**, proving (2) implies (3).

= Solution
{parent=3}

The final condition makes $\lambda=(k,k-1,\ldots,1)$. Its column lengths are the same staircase, so
$$
h(i,j)=2k-2i-2j+3,
$$
which is odd. This proves (3) implies (1) and completes the equivalence. Such a partition exists exactly at the triangular numbers
$$
\boxed{n=k(k+1)/2\quad(k\geq1),\qquad\lambda=(k,k-1,\ldots,1).}
$$
The hook formula gives $v_2(f^\lambda)=v_2(n!)=a$, since its denominator is odd. Thus $2^a$ divides $f^\lambda$. Conversely $2^a\mid f^\gamma$ implies $v_2(\prod h_\gamma)=a-v_2(f^\gamma)\leq0$. The product is an integer, so its valuation is zero and all its hooks are odd. By the equivalence $\gamma$ is a staircase; its size uniquely determines its length, so **$\gamma=\lambda$**.

For the <character> assertion, every removable <rim hook> has length equal to some <hook length> of the diagram. Here is a direct justification. If a <border strip> $\lambda/\mu$ occupies rows $i$ through $j$, connectedness and absence of a two-by-two block make adjacent strips of boxes overlap in exactly one column. Hence $\mu_l=\lambda_{l+1}-1$ for $i\leq l<j$, so its size is $\lambda_i-\mu_j+j-i$. The column $c=\mu_j+1$ has height $j$, giving exactly $h_\lambda(i,c)=\lambda_i-\mu_j+j-i$. Therefore every removable <rim hook> of the staircase has odd length.

A fixed-point-free permutation of power-of-two order has only even cycles. Apply the <Murnaghan-Nakayama rule> starting with any cycle. It would require removal of a <rim hook> of that even length, but none exists. The sum is empty, so
$$
\boxed{\chi^\lambda(g)=0.}
$$
Finally suppose $\gamma$ has distinct parts and is not $\lambda$. It cannot be another staircase of the same size. Thus some gap between successive rows is at least two, or the last row has length at least two; in either case a horizontal rim domino is removable. No vertical rim domino is removable, since that would require two equal adjacent row lengths. Murnaghan-Nakayama on a <transposition> consequently gives a nonempty sum
$$
\chi^\gamma(\tau)=\sum_{\text{removable horizontal dominoes}}f^{\gamma\setminus\text{domino}}>0,
$$
all signs being positive and all tableau counts positive. Thus **the <character> is nonzero at every <transposition>**, proving the requested existence.

For a tableau $t$, let $C_t$ be its column stabilizer and $e_t=\sum_{\sigma\in C_t}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\{\sigma t\}$ its <polytabloid>. The <Specht module> is the span of all such <polytabloids> inside the <tabloid> permutation module.

A <tabloid> with second row of size $k$ is determined by that subset, so $\dim M^{(n-k,k)}=\binom nk$. The two-row hook formula gives
$$
\dim S^{(n-k,k)}=\frac{n!(n-2k+1)}{(n-k+1)!k!}=\binom nk-\binom n{k-1}.
$$
At $k=3$ this proves
$$
\boxed{\dim M^{(n-3,3)}=\dim M^{(n-2,2)}+\dim S^{(n-3,3)}.}
$$
By <Maschke's theorem>, characteristic-zero permutation modules are completely reducible, so their Specht submodules are summands. The nonzero sum of all three-subset tabloids is fixed by every permutation; its span is a copy of the trivial module $S^{(n)}$.

A permutation sends $v_a$ to $v_{ga}$, $w_{ab}$ to $w_{ga,gb}$ and $x_{abc}$ to $x_{ga,gb,gc}$. Relabeling the summation indices gives
$$
g\phi_1(v_a)=\sum_{b\ne a}w_{ga,gb}=\phi_1(v_{ga}),\qquad g\phi_2(w_{ab})=\sum_{c\notin\{a,b\}}x_{ga,gb,gc}=\phi_2(w_{ga,gb}).
$$
Linearity therefore proves **both maps are <module homomorphisms>**. The map denoted $\phi$ in the second displayed source formula is $\phi_2$.

For the standard tableau with second row $2,4$, the first entries in the top row are $1,3$, so its nontrivial columns are $(1,2)$ and $(3,4)$. Hence
$$
e_t=w_{24}-w_{14}-w_{23}+w_{13}.

For $S^{(n-1,1)}$, choose the standard tableau with lower entry two and top-left entry one. Its <polytabloid> is $v_2-v_1$. Each three-subset containing $a$ occurs twice in $\phi_2\phi_1(v_a)$, once for each choice of its other two entries as the intermediate $b$. Consequently the coefficient of $x_{23n}$ in $\phi_2\phi_1(v_2-v_1)$ is two. This is nonzero in characteristic zero. Simplicity again makes the restriction injective, giving **a copy of $S^{(n-1,1)}$**.

The four constructed submodules have the distinct shapes $(n-3,3)$, $(n-2,2)$, $(n-1,1)$ and $(n)$, so they are pairwise nonisomorphic irreducible characteristic-zero modules. Complete reducibility therefore makes their sum direct. The given decomposition of $M^{(n-2,2)}$ and the <dimension> identity in (a) show their dimensions sum to $\dim M^{(n-3,3)}$. Their <direct sum> is consequently the whole module:
$$
\boxed{M^{(n-3,3)}=S^{(n-3,3)}\oplus S^{(n-2,2)}\oplus S^{(n-1,1)}\oplus S^{(n)}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=6}

Use the standard invariant <tabloid bilinear form>, with the <tabloid> <basis> orthonormal. Each permutation acts by an orthogonal <matrix>, so $\langle gx,y\rangle=\langle x,g^{-1}y\rangle$. Inversion permutes $C_t$ and preserves its signs. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\langle\kappa_tx,y\rangle=\sum_{g\in C_t}\operatorname{sgn}(g)\langle x,g^{-1}y\rangle=\langle x,\kappa_ty\rangle.}
$$
The <James submodule theorem> states that for any submodule $U\leq M^\lambda$, either $S^\lambda\subseteq U$ or $U\subseteq(S^\lambda)^\perp$. Use the allowed fact $\kappa_tM^\lambda=Fe_t$. The coefficient of $\{t\}$ in $e_t$ is one, so the displayed adjoint identity gives $\kappa_tx=\langle x,e_t\rangle e_t$. If $U$ is not orthogonal to $S^\lambda$, some $x\in U$ and some tableau $t$ have $\langle x,e_t\rangle\ne0$. Hence $e_t\in U$, and its permutation translates span $S^\lambda$. This proves the theorem.

Let $R=S^\lambda\cap(S^\lambda)^\perp$. Every proper submodule of $S^\lambda$ lies in $R$ by the theorem. If the restricted form is nonzero, $R$ is proper, hence is the unique maximal submodule and
$$
\boxed{D^\lambda=S^\lambda/R\text{ is the unique simple quotient}.}
$$
In the stronger nondegenerate case specified in the question, $R=0$, so $S^\lambda$ itself is simple. The nonzero-form version also covers the possibly degenerate case needed in the final calculation.

For shape $(n-1,1)$, identify $M$ with $F^n$ with <basis> $v_1,\ldots,v_n$. Its <Specht module> is the augmentation subspace $S=\{\sum a_iv_i:\sum a_i=0\}$, spanned freely by $v_i-v_n$, $1\leq i<n$. Its <Gram matrix> is $G=I_{n-1}+J_{n-1}$, with diagonal entries two and off-diagonal entries one. Equivalently, $S^\perp$ in $M$ is the span of $v_1+\cdots+v_n$. This vector belongs to $S$ exactly when $p\mid n$. Thus the restricted radical has <dimension> one if $p\mid n$ and zero otherwise, giving
$$
\boxed{\dim D^{(n-1,1)}=\begin{cases}n-2,&p\mid n,\\n-1,&p\nmid n.\end{cases}}
$$
For $n\geq3$ this quotient is nonzero and simple by the theorem. In the small exceptional case $n=2,p=2$, the canonical radical quotient is zero, as the same <dimension> formula states; it is then not a nonzero <simple module>. This qualification distinguishes the numerical formula from the nonzero-form hypothesis.
