= Solution
{parent=2007/a/1/a}

On the space of <endomorphisms> of $V$, left and right multiplication by $x$ commute, and $\operatorname{ad}x$ is their difference. The <binomial theorem> therefore gives
$$
(\operatorname{ad}x)^m(y)=\sum_{j=0}^m(-1)^j\binom mj x^{m-j}yx^j.
$$
For $m=2n-1$, every term has either $j\ge n$ or $m-j\ge n$, so every term vanishes when $x^n=0$. Restriction to the invariant <Lie subalgebra> $L$ proves
$$
\boxed{(\operatorname{ad}_Lx)^{2n-1}=0.}
$$
The argument works over every <field>; no division by a binomial coefficient is used.

We prove the common-zero-vector form of <Engel's theorem> by strong <induction> on the dimension of the acting <Lie algebra> of operators, allowing its action on a nonzero finite-dimensional space to have a kernel. The zero-dimensional case is immediate.

First, every proper <Lie subalgebra> $H<L$ has a strictly larger <normalizer of a Lie subalgebra>. The action of $H$ on $L/H$ by $h\cdot(x+H)=[h,x]+H$ consists of nilpotent operators by part (a). Its image has dimension at most $\dim H<\dim L$. By <induction> it has a nonzero common-zero <vector> $x+H$, giving $[H,x]\subseteq H$ with $x\notin H$. Hence $N_L(H)>H$.

Choose a maximal proper <Lie subalgebra> $H$. The preceding paragraph forces $N_L(H)=L$, so $H$ is an <ideal of a Lie algebra>. Moreover $L/H$ has dimension one: otherwise any nonzero one-dimensional subspace of $L/H$, which is a <Lie subalgebra>, would lift to an intermediate proper subalgebra. By <induction> the common kernel
$$
W=\{v\in V:Hv=0\}
$$
is nonzero. It is $L$-invariant, since $h(xv)=x(hv)+[h,x]v=0$ for $v\in W$, $h\in H$, $x\in L$. Take $x\notin H$. Its restriction to $W$ is nilpotent and so has a nonzero <vector> $v$ in its kernel. As $L=H+Kx$, this <vector> is killed by every element of $L$:
$$
\boxed{Lv=0\quad\text{for some }v\ne0.}
$$
This completes the <induction>. The underlying space is understood to be nonzero, as required for the asserted <vector> to exist.

A <Cartan subalgebra> is a nilpotent <Lie subalgebra> equal to its <normalizer of a Lie subalgebra>. Write $H=L_{0,x}$ for a choice $x\in A$ attaining the minimum dimension, and let $h=\dim H$. For $y\in A$, the subalgebra $H$ is invariant under $\operatorname{ad}(x+ty)$ because $A\subseteq H$. On $L/H$, the operator induced by $\operatorname{ad}x$ is invertible: $H$ is its full generalized zero eigenspace, and the <finite-dimensional Fitting decomposition> separates that from the invertible part.

The determinant of the operator induced by $\operatorname{ad}(x+ty)$ on $L/H$ is a <polynomial> in $t$, nonzero at zero. Thus it is nonzero for all but finitely many $t\in K$. For these values, the generalized zero eigenspace $L_{0,x+ty}$ lies in $H$. Minimality of $\dim H$ among elements from $A$ forces equality. Hence $\operatorname{ad}(x+ty)|_H$ is nilpotent for all but finitely many $t$.

Each nonleading coefficient of its <characteristic polynomial> is a <polynomial> in $t$. Since $K$ is infinite, a <polynomial> vanishing at all but finitely many elements is identically zero. The coefficient of degree $i$ in the <matrix> entries is homogeneous of degree $i$; its highest possible $t^i$ coefficient is the corresponding characteristic coefficient of $\operatorname{ad}y|_H$. All these vanish, so that operator has <characteristic polynomial> $T^h$ and is nilpotent by the <Cayley-Hamilton theorem>. Thus
$$
\boxed{H\subseteq L_{0,y}\qquad(y\in A).}
$$
For existence, choose an $L_{0,x}=H$ of smallest dimension among all $x\in L$. Since $x\in H$, it also minimizes the same set with $x$ restricted to $A=H$. Applying the proved statement makes every $\operatorname{ad}y|_H$ nilpotent. <Engel's theorem> makes $H$ nilpotent, and the allowed self-idealizer property makes it self-normalizing. Therefore it is a <Cartan subalgebra>, illustrating <Cartan subalgebra existence from a minimal Engel subalgebra>.

For the three-dimensional example, direct use of the brackets gives $C_L(z)=Kz$ for every nonzero $z$: the equations $[u,z]=0$ are the vanishing of all two-by-two minors of the two coordinate <vectors>, so they are proportional. Every nonzero nilpotent <Lie algebra> has a nonzero center, as seen from its last nonzero lower-central term. A nilpotent subalgebra $H$ with a central nonzero element $z$ must therefore satisfy $H\subseteq C_L(z)=Kz$, and has dimension one. The zero subalgebra is not self-normalizing. Thus **all <Cartan subalgebras> have dimension one**. For example $Ka$ is nilpotent and its normalizer is $Ka$, so such subalgebras do occur.

Use the standard split <weight-space decomposition> in <characteristic zero>, in particular the usual complex setting: $L=\bigoplus_\varphi L_\varphi$, with generalized weights for the nilpotent algebra $H$. The characteristic-zero condition prevents nonzero weight shifts from wrapping around; the unrestricted positive-characteristic version would need additional hypotheses.

For fixed $h\in H$, let $D=\operatorname{ad}h$. On a bracket of <vectors> from $L_\varphi,L_\psi$, the <derivation> identity gives
$$
(D-(\varphi+\psi)(h))[u,v]=[(D-\varphi(h))u,v]+[u,(D-\psi(h))v].
$$
Iterating it gives the binomial expansion of these two commuting actions. At power $2n-1$, each term kills at least one factor, because generalized weight <vectors> are killed by the $n$th power of the corresponding shifted operator. Therefore the bracket lies in the generalized eigenspace for $(\varphi+\psi)(h)$ for every $h$. Stabilization of kernels by dimension $n$ gives
$$
\boxed{[L_\varphi,L_\psi]\subseteq L_{\varphi+\psi},}
$$
where $L_\eta=0$ when $\eta$ is not a weight.

Nilpotence of $H$ gives $H\subseteq L_0$. If $H$ is a <Cartan subalgebra> and $L_0/H\ne0$, the action of $H$ on this quotient consists of nilpotent operators. <Engel's theorem> supplies a nonzero coset $v+H$ killed by $H$, giving $[H,v]\subseteq H$ with $v\notin H$, a contradiction to self-normalization. Conversely, if $L_0=H$ and $v$ normalizes $H$, then $[h,v]\in H$ for each $h$. Applying further powers of $\operatorname{ad}h$ kills this <vector> because its restriction to $H$ is nilpotent. Hence $v\in L_0=H$. This proves the equivalence.

The operator $\operatorname{ad}e$ sends $L_\varphi$ into $L_{\varphi+\alpha}$. Repetition follows a consecutive $\alpha$-string; after $r$ steps it must leave the weight set, because $r$ is the largest number of weights in any such string. Thus $\boxed{(\operatorname{ad}e)^r=0}$ on every <weight space> and hence on $L$.

Finally put $E=\operatorname{ad}e$ and $F=\operatorname{ad}f$. Expand $(E+F)^n$ as the sum of all words of length $n$, without commuting $E$ and $F$. Acting on $L_\gamma$, each word follows weights $\gamma+i\alpha+j\beta$ with $i+j$ increasing by one at each step. <Linear independence> of $\alpha,\beta$ in <characteristic zero> makes the successive weights distinct. A nonzero length-$n$ path would require $n+1$ different <weight spaces>, but $\dim L=n$. Every word therefore vanishes, giving
$$
\boxed{(\operatorname{ad}(e+f))^n=0.}
$$

A reduced crystallographic <root system> in a real <Euclidean space> is a finite spanning subset $\Phi$ not containing zero, with $\Phi\cap\mathbb R\alpha=\{\alpha,-\alpha\}$, closed under $s_\alpha(v)=v-2(v,\alpha)\alpha/(\alpha,\alpha)$, and satisfying $2(\beta,\alpha)/(\alpha,\alpha)\in\mathbb Z$ for all roots. A <base of a root system> is a vector-space <basis> $\Delta\subseteq\Phi$ such that every root has <integer> coefficients of one sign in that <basis>.

Choose $h$ not orthogonal to any root and call roots with $(h,\alpha)>0$ positive. Let $\Delta$ be the positive roots that cannot be written as a sum of two positive roots. Repeated decomposition terminates because each summand has strictly smaller positive $h$-value in a finite set. Thus every positive root is a nonnegative <integer> combination of $\Delta$.

For distinct $\alpha,\beta\in\Delta$, $(\alpha,\beta)\le0$: otherwise the allowed difference-root lemma would make either $\alpha-\beta$ or $\beta-\alpha$ positive, decomposing one of them. In a real <linear relation> among $\Delta$, move negative coefficients to the other side, obtaining $u=v$ as positive combinations with disjoint supports. Then $\|u\|^2=(u,v)\le0$, so $u=0$. But a nonempty positive combination has $(h,u)>0$, a contradiction. Thus the elements are <linearly independent>. They span every root and hence $E$, proving existence of a <base of a root system>. The difference-root lemma is used only for distinct roots; that qualification excludes the zero difference.

For the displayed set, there are eight coordinate roots, twenty-four roots with two nonzero unit entries, and sixteen half-coordinate roots: forty-eight in total. It spans $\mathbb R^4$, is reduced, and has squared root lengths one and two. Signed coordinate <permutations> preserve it. <Reflections> in coordinate roots are sign changes, and <reflections> in roots $e_i\pm e_j$ are signed coordinate <permutations>. Every half-coordinate root is obtained by a signed <permutation> from $h=(1/2,1/2,1/2,1/2)$, so it remains to check its <reflection>
$$
s_h(v)=v-\frac{\sum_i v_i}{2}(1,1,1,1).
$$
It sends $e_i$ to $e_i-h$, a half-coordinate root; it fixes $e_i-e_j$ and sends $e_i+e_j$ to the negative sum of the other two coordinate <vectors>. For a half-coordinate root, the sum of its four signs is $0,\pm2$ or $\pm4$; the formula sends it, respectively, to itself, a coordinate root, or its negative when parallel to $h$. Thus <reflection> closure holds. For a long root, the required Cartan <integer> is an <integer> dot product; for a coordinate root it is twice a half-integer; for $h$ it is $\sum_i\beta_i\in\mathbb Z$. Symmetry gives the remaining cases. Therefore this is the <F4 root system>.

In the printed base order, the <Gram matrix> is
$$
G=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0&0\\-1&2&-1&0\\0&-1&1&-1/2\\0&0&-1/2&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Using the convention $a_{ij}=2(\alpha_i,\alpha_j)/(\alpha_j,\alpha_j)$, the <Cartan matrix> is
$$
\boxed{A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0&0\\-1&2&-2&0\\0&-1&2&-1\\0&0&-1&2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The row-coroot convention transposes this <matrix>. The <Dynkin diagram> is the chain $1-2\Rightarrow3-4$, with the double bond pointing toward the shorter third root:

\Image[2007-f4-dynkin.png]
{title=The <F4 Dynkin diagram> in the printed simple-root order; the arrow points toward the shorter root}

The associated symmetric positive definite form, normalized as a squared length, is
$$
\boxed{Q(x)=2x_1^2+2x_2^2+x_3^2+x_4^2-2x_1x_2-2x_2x_3-x_3x_4.}
$$
Indeed it equals
$$
(x_4/2)^2+(x_1-x_4/2)^2+(-x_1+x_2-x_4/2)^2+(-x_2+x_3-x_4/2)^2,
$$
which is strictly positive for nonzero $x$. The alternative Gram normalization $Q/2$ is the same positive definite form up to a positive scalar.

A <tabloid> records the sets of entries in each row of a tableau. Let $C_t$ be the subgroup permuting entries independently within each column. The <polytabloid> is
$$
e_t=\sum_{\pi\in C_t}\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\{\pi t\},
$$
and the <Specht module> $S^\lambda$ is spanned by these <vectors>. A <standard polytabloid> is one whose tableau increases along rows and down columns.

Order <tabloids> by first sorting each row increasingly, then comparing the resulting row-reading words lexicographically. For a standard tableau $t$, every nonidentity column <permutation> produces a strictly larger word. In the first row affected by that <permutation>, each replacement comes from farther down the same column and is larger than the entry it replaces; all earlier rows remain unchanged. Sorting the affected row therefore increases its word. Also no nontrivial column <permutation> fixes its row sets, since a cell is the unique intersection of its row and column. Hence the least <tabloid> in $e_t$ is $\{t\}$ with coefficient one.

Distinct standard tableaux have distinct row <tabloids>. In a nonzero <linear relation> among their <polytabloids>, choose the least leading <tabloid> with a nonzero coefficient. None of the <vectors> with larger leading <tabloids> can contribute to it, so its coefficient cannot cancel. This contradiction proves **<linear independence> of the <standard polytabloids>**.

The <hook length> of a cell is one plus the number of cells to its right in the same row and below it in the same column. The <hook-length formula> is
$$
f^\lambda=\frac{n!}{\prod_{c\in\lambda}h(c)}.
$$
For $\lambda=(n-k,k)$, with $0\le k\le n/2$, the second-row hook product is $k!$. In the first row, the hooks are $n-k-j+2$ for $1\le j\le k$ and $n-k-j+1$ for $k<j\le n-k$, so their product is $(n-k+1)!/(n-2k+1)$. Consequently
$$
f^{(n-k,k)}=\frac{n!(n-2k+1)}{k!(n-k+1)!}
=\boxed{\binom nk-\binom n{k-1}}.
$$
At $k=0$, interpret the second binomial coefficient as zero.

For a <cycle type> $\mu=(m,\nu)$, the <Murnaghan–Nakayama rule> is
$$
\chi^\lambda(m,\nu)=\sum_{\lambda/\rho\text{ a rim hook of size }m}
(-1)^{\operatorname{ht}(\lambda/\rho)}\chi^\rho(\nu).
$$
The sum is over partitions $\rho$ whose diagrams remain after removing the specified border strip; $\nu$ is the remaining <cycle type>, and the recursion ends with the empty <character> equal to one. At the identity, $\chi^\rho(1^{|\rho|})=f^\rho$. Incompatible total sizes contribute zero.

For shape $(2,2)$ and a transposition, a horizontal strip in the bottom row leaves $(2)$ with positive sign; a vertical strip in the right column leaves $(1,1)$ with negative sign. Both remaining dimensions are one. Thus
$$
\boxed{\chi^{(2,2)}(2,1,1)=1-1=0.}
$$

First prove preservation of the row and column inequalities for distinct ordered labels. If $b>c_l$, <row insertion> appends it to the first row, and the inequalities remain valid. Otherwise let $j$ be the first position with $c_j>b$. Replace $c_j$ by $b$ and insert the bumped $c_j$ in the second row. Its first larger entry is some $d_k$ with $k\le j$, because $d_j>c_j$. Replace $d_k$ by $c_j$ and put the bumped $d_k$ in the new third-row cell.

The first-larger-entry choices preserve strict row order. At column $k$, if $k<j$ the first-row entry is $c_k<c_j$, while if $k=j$ it is the new $b<c_j$. At column $j$ when $k<j$, the unchanged lower entry satisfies $d_j>c_j>b$. Every other old column keeps its previous inequalities. The new third-row entry is larger than the second-row first entry: for $k=1$ this is $d_1>c_j$, while for $k>1$ it follows from $d_k>d_1$. Thus the new shape $(l,l,1)$ is a <Young diagram> with strictly increasing rows and columns. This proves the insertion assertion for the ordered-label convention.

There is a numbering qualification in the literal standard convention of part (a). If $T$ uses exactly $1,\ldots,2l$, an arbitrary missing natural number $b$ need not be $2l+1$. For example, inserting six into rows $(1,2);(3,4)$ produces $(1,2,6);(3,4)$, which has the order inequalities but not the labels $1,\ldots,5$. Thus **standardness with consecutive labels requires $b=2l+1$, or increasing relabelling afterward**. The printed wording is retained in the private transcription; <row insertion> itself preserves the needed order structure.

The <symmetric group> acts on <tabloids> by permuting their entries; extending linearly gives the <permutation module> $M^\mu$. Its dimension is
$$
\boxed{\dim M^\mu=\frac{n!}{\prod_i\mu_i!}.}
$$
A tableau of type $\mu$ contains exactly $\mu_i$ copies of the label $i$. Fix a numbering of the $\lambda$-diagram's cells by $1,\ldots,n$. For a filling $T$, let the $i$th row set of an associated $\mu$-tabloid be the set of cell numbers whose labels in $T$ equal $i$. This is a bijection from type-$\mu$ fillings to $\mu$-tabloids.

Define $\sigma T$ by moving labels along the numbered positions: its label at cell $j$ is the old label at cell $\sigma^{-1}j$. The associated row set is then the image under $\sigma$ of the old row set. Thus the bijection is equivariant, and its linear extension makes $F[\mathcal T_{\lambda,\mu}]$ and $M^\mu$ <isomorphic representations>. Extend from group elements to the <group algebra> by linearity.

For shape and type both $\mu$, column strictness makes every entry in row $r$ at least $r$. All $\mu_1$ entries equal to one must therefore be in the first row, which has exactly that length; it is filled with ones. More generally, if a semistandard filling of shape $\lambda$ and type $\mu$ exists, its entries at most $k$ must lie in the first $k$ rows. Counting them gives
$$
\sum_{i=1}^k\mu_i\le\sum_{i=1}^k\lambda_i\qquad\text{for every }k.
$$
These are the inequalities defining the <dominance order on partitions>. Hence $K_{\lambda\mu}>0$ implies $\boxed{\lambda\ge\mu}$, and the asserted restriction on summands follows from <Young's rule>.

We calculate the exact multiplicity without using <Young's rule>, via a <two-subset incidence realization of a two-row Specht module>. Let $V_2$ be the <permutation> <representation> on unordered pairs from a six-element set, and $V_1$ the natural <permutation> <representation> on its singletons. The equivariant incidence map
$$
D:V_2\to V_1,\qquad e_{ij}\longmapsto e_i+e_j
$$
is surjective: the transpose map sends $\sum c_ie_i$ to the pair coefficients $c_i+c_j$, and all such sums can be zero only when every $c_i=0$ in <characteristic zero>. Thus $\dim\ker D=15-6=9$.

Identify the <tabloids> of shape $(4,2)$ with their two-element bottom rows. A <polytabloid> is a signed four-edge <vector> $e_{cd}-e_{ad}-e_{cb}+e_{ab}$, whose incidence at every vertex is zero. Hence $S^{(4,2)}\subseteq\ker D$. The <standard polytabloid> <basis> theorem and the <hook-length formula> give $\dim S^{(4,2)}=\binom62-\binom61=9$, so equality holds. By <Maschke's theorem>,
$$
V_2\cong S^{(4,2)}\oplus V_1.
$$
Take $H=\Sigma_3\times\Sigma_2\times\Sigma_1$, preserving blocks of sizes three, two and one. It has three orbits on singletons and five on pairs: pairs inside the first block, inside the second, and between each of the three distinct pairs of blocks. Thus $\dim V_1^H=3$ and $\dim V_2^H=5$. Averaging over $H$ makes taking fixed <vectors> exact in <characteristic zero>, so $\dim(S^{(4,2)})^H=2$.

Finally $M^{(3,2,1)}=\operatorname{Ind}_H^{\Sigma_6}1$. <Frobenius reciprocity>, and equality of invariant and coinvariant dimensions in <characteristic zero>, identify the required multiplicity with this two-dimensional fixed space. Therefore
$$
\boxed{[M^{(3,2,1)}:S^{(4,2)}]=2,}
$$
which in particular satisfies the requested lower and upper bounds.

= Solution

The simple <modules> are indexed by the <p-regular partitions> $\lambda$ of $n$, meaning no part is repeated $p$ or more times. Equip the <tabloid> <permutation module> with the <bilinear form> invariant under the <symmetric group> making its <tabloid> <basis> orthonormal. The construction is
$$
D^\lambda=S^\lambda/(S^\lambda\cap(S^\lambda)^\perp).
$$
The standard modular Specht theorem says this quotient is nonzero precisely for p-regular $\lambda$, is then simple, and these quotients give all simple <modules>.

To verify distinctness of labels, use the standard dominance triangularity theorem: if $D^\nu$ occurs as a composition factor of $S^\lambda$, then $\nu\ge\lambda$; for a p-regular label the diagonal multiplicity is one. If $D^\lambda\cong D^\mu$, then $D^\lambda$ occurs in $S^\mu$, so $\lambda\ge\mu$, and the symmetric argument gives $\mu\ge\lambda$. Antisymmetry of the <dominance order on partitions> yields $\boxed{\lambda=\mu}$. These are the general representation-theoretic results allowed in this part.

Realize $S^{(n-1,1)}$ as the augmentation <submodule>
$$
W=\{(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\in F^n:\sum_i x_i=0\}
$$
of the natural <permutation module>: its <polytabloids> are the differences $e_i-e_j$, which span exactly $W$. Let $U$ be a <submodule>. If it contains a <vector> $v$ with two unequal coordinates $v_i,v_j$, then
$$
v-(i\ j)v=(v_i-v_j)(e_i-e_j)\in U.
$$
The coefficient is nonzero, so $e_i-e_j\in U$; its symmetric-group orbit spans $W$, forcing $U=W$. Therefore every proper nonzero <submodule> consists only of constant-coordinate <vectors>. Those form at most a one-dimensional trivial <submodule>. If such a trivial <submodule> is absent, no proper nonzero <submodule> exists, so **the <Specht module> is simple**.

On a one-dimensional <representation>, $s_i$ acts by a nonzero scalar $\varepsilon_i$. Its square is one. The braid relation becomes $\varepsilon_i^2\varepsilon_{i+1}=\varepsilon_{i+1}^2\varepsilon_i$, and hence $\varepsilon_{i+1}=\varepsilon_i$. Thus all adjacent transpositions act by one common scalar $\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon^2=1$.

In characteristic different from two, $\varepsilon=1$ or $-1$. Since adjacent transpositions generate the group, these choices give exactly the <trivial representation> and <sign representation>. For $n\le1$ they coincide, leaving only the <trivial representation>.

The <standard polytabloid> <basis> is defined over the <integers> and remains a <basis> after reduction modulo three. Thus $\dim S^{(3,2)}=f^{(3,2)}=\binom52-\binom51=5$. The given <submodule> has dimension four by part (d), so the quotient is one-dimensional and part (c) leaves only the trivial or sign action.

A transposition has ordinary trace one on $S^{(3,2)}$: the <Murnaghan–Nakayama rule> has only the horizontal bottom-row two-strip, leaving shape $(3)$ with dimension one. Reduction of its integral action <matrix> preserves this trace in $F$. Its trace on $D^{(4,1)}=S^{(4,1)}$ is $3-1=2$, since the natural five-point <permutation> <representation> has three fixed coordinates and contains the trivial line. Therefore its trace on the one-dimensional quotient is $1-2=-1$, distinct from one in characteristic three. This gives the <characteristic-three sign quotient of the Specht module of shape three two>. The quotient is the <sign representation>:
$$
\boxed{\sigma\cdot\overline v=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\,\overline v
\quad\text{on }S^{(3,2)}/D^{(4,1)}.}
$$
