Define $L_1=L$ and $L_{n+1}=[L,L_n]$, where brackets of subspaces mean their linear span. Each term is an <ideal of a Lie algebra>[ideal]. Inductively, if $L_n$ is an <ideal of a Lie algebra>[ideal], the <Jacobi identity> gives
$$
[z,[x,y]]=[[z,x],y]+[x,[z,y]]\in[L,L_n]\quad(z,x\in L,\ y\in L_n),
$$
so $L_{n+1}$ is an <ideal of a Lie algebra>[ideal] as well. Also $L_{n+1}\subseteq L_n$. Nilpotence means that this descending series eventually reaches zero; class $c$ means $L_{c+1}=0$ with $L_c\ne0$ for nonzero $L$.

The idealizer here is the normalizer $\operatorname{Id}_L(A)=\{x\in L:[x,A]\subseteq A\}$. Since $A$ is a subalgebra it contains its own brackets, so $A\subseteq\operatorname{Id}_L(A)$.

Because $L_1\not\subseteq A$ and the lower central series terminates, choose $j$ with $L_j\not\subseteq A$ but $L_{j+1}\subseteq A$. Take $x\in L_j\setminus A$. Then $[x,A]\subseteq[L_j,L]=L_{j+1}\subseteq A$. Thus $x$ belongs to the idealizer and not to $A$, proving $\boxed{A<\operatorname{Id}_L(A)}$.

Induct on $i$, proving the claim for all $j$ simultaneously. The case $i=1$ is the definition $[L,L_j]=L_{j+1}$. If it holds for $i$, Jacobi gives
$$
[L_{i+1},L_j]=[[L,L_i],L_j]\subseteq[L,[L_i,L_j]]+[L_i,[L,L_j]]\subseteq[L,L_{i+j}]+[L_i,L_{j+1}]\subseteq L_{i+j+1}.

The <Killing form> is $\kappa_L(x,y)=\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{ad}_Lx\,\operatorname{ad}_Ly)$. Symmetry follows from $\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(BA)$. Since $\operatorname{ad}[z,x]=[\operatorname{ad}z,\operatorname{ad}x]$, cyclicity of <trace> gives the invariance identity
$$
\kappa_L([z,x],y)=-\kappa_L(x,[z,y]).
$$
If $x\in I^\perp$ and $y\in I$, then $[z,y]\in I$, so the right side is zero. Hence $[z,x]\in I^\perp$ for every $z$, proving that $\boxed{I^\perp\text{ is an ideal}}$.

For $x\in I$, use a <basis> of $I$ followed by a complementary <basis> of $L$. Because $[x,L]\subseteq I$, the <matrix> of $\operatorname{ad}_Lx$ has blocks $\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{ad}_Ix&B_x\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$. Multiplying two such <matrices> shows
$$
\boxed{\kappa_L(x,y)=\kappa_I(x,y)\quad(x,y\in I).}
$$
The restriction statement has both arguments in $I$; it does not identify the two forms for arbitrary elements of $L$ outside $I$.

Now use the ordered <basis> in the question for a central <ideal of a Lie algebra>[ideal] $J$. The images of the $u_j$ in the quotient have adjoint <matrix> $A$, so the $u$-coordinate block of their images is $A$; arbitrary $J$-components form $B$. Each $v_j$ is central, so its image under every adjoint map is zero. Thus the <matrix> is exactly $\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\B&0\end{pmatrix}$. Products have top-left block $A_xA_y$ and zero bottom-right block. Taking traces gives
$$
\boxed{\kappa_L(x,y)=\kappa_{L/J}(\bar x,\bar y).}
$$
Finally, a nonzero finite-dimensional <nilpotent Lie algebra> has nonzero <center of a Lie algebra>[centre] by (1)(b). Induct on its <dimension>: the <Killing form> descends to the quotient by its <center of a Lie algebra>[centre], which is again nilpotent of smaller <dimension>, and is therefore zero by induction. The zero algebra starts the induction. Apply this to the nilpotent <ideal of a Lie algebra>[ideal] $I$ and then use the restriction identity to obtain $\boxed{\kappa_L(x,y)=0\text{ for }x,y\in I}$. This avoids the false general claim that the product of two nilpotent linear maps must be nilpotent.

By <irreducible traceless matrix Lie algebra is semisimple>, let $R$ be the <radical of a Lie algebra>[solvable radical]. <Lie's theorem> triangularizes its action, with finitely many diagonal <characters> $\lambda_i:R\to\mathbb C$, and supplies a nonzero common <eigenspace> for one of them. For $x\in L$, conjugation by $e^{tx}$ preserves $R$ and gives an equivalent <representation> of it. Thus the multiset of <characters> $\lambda_i\circ e^{t\operatorname{ad}x}$ is constant. Each <character> varies continuously in $t$ and has values in that finite set of <characters>, so it is individually constant. Differentiating at zero gives $\lambda_i([x,r])=0$ for all $r\in R$.

Choose a common <eigenspace> $V_\lambda\ne0$. For $v\in V_\lambda$,
$$
r(xv)=x(rv)+[r,x]v=\lambda(r)xv+\lambda([r,x])v=\lambda(r)xv.
$$
Thus $V_\lambda$ is $L$-invariant, so irreducibility makes it all of $\mathbb C^n$. Every element of $R$ acts as a scalar. Its <trace> is zero because $L\subseteq\mathfrak{sl}_n$, so that scalar is zero. Faithfulness of the <matrix> inclusion gives $R=0$. Hence $\boxed{L\text{ is semisimple}}$.

One sufficient diagonal-<matrix> condition for simplicity is that $L$ contain **all traceless <diagonal matrices>**. Indeed invariance under their adjoint actions then decomposes $L$ into that diagonal algebra and some <matrix>-unit spaces $\mathbb Ce_{ij}$. Put a directed edge $j\to i$ when $e_{ij}\in L$. Irreducibility makes the directed graph strongly connected; otherwise a terminal component spans a proper invariant coordinate subspace. Bracketing <matrix units> along a simple directed path supplies the <matrix unit> between its endpoints. Strong connectivity therefore supplies every off-<diagonal matrix> unit, so $L=\mathfrak{sl}_n$, which is simple. The mere existence of a <diagonal matrix> with distinct diagonal entries would not be a sufficient condition.

For the specified construction, $H$ has <dimension> two. Its diagonal adjoint action and the twelve distinct nonzero <weights of a representation>[weights] give a <direct sum> $L=H\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{12}\mathbb Cg_i$, of <dimension> fourteen. The stated bracket condition and the <weight of a representation>[weight]-vector relations make it a <Lie algebra>. A subspace invariant under it is invariant under all the $g_i$ and hence their associative algebra, so it acts irreducibly on $\mathbb C^7$. The preceding result makes it semisimple.

Use <prime-dimensional faithful irreducible module forces simplicity>. To see this, decompose the algebra into simple <ideals of a Lie algebra>[ideals]. Complete reducibility for one <ideal of a Lie algebra>[ideal] and commutation with the others make its isotypic component invariant under the whole algebra, hence all of the <representation>. Writing that component as $W\otimes\operatorname{Hom}(W,V)$ and repeating produces a <tensor product> of irreducibles for the simple <ideals of a Lie algebra>[ideals]. Faithfulness makes every factor nontrivial, of <dimension> at least two. Their dimensions multiply to seven, so only one factor is possible. Thus the constructed $L$ is simple.

The diagonal algebra $H$ is toral, and its centralizer inside $L$ is just $H$, since no $g_i$ has zero <weight of a representation>[weight]. A maximal <toral subalgebra> containing $H$ lies in that centralizer, so equals $H$; it is a <Cartan subalgebra>. Thus the rank is two and the twelve <weights of a representation>[weights] are the <roots of a root system>[roots]. Pair them as $\alpha_{i+6}=-\alpha_i$. The opposite-<root of a root system>[root] brackets have <weight of a representation>[weight] zero and belong to $H$. For distinct $i,j\le6$, the sum cannot be zero, and the supplied <root of a root system>[root]-bracket result says their bracket is nonzero exactly when $\alpha_i+\alpha_j$ is a <root of a root system>[root]. This is equivalent to $-\alpha_i-\alpha_j$ being a <root of a root system>[root], proving the requested equivalence for the paired brackets.

Among rank-two <simple root> systems, $A_2,B_2,G_2$ have respectively six, eight and twelve <roots of a root system>[roots]. Therefore
$$
\boxed{L\text{ has type }G_2.}
$$
Choose <simple roots> $\alpha$ short and $\beta$ long. In $G_2$, $\beta+\alpha$ and $\beta+2\alpha$ are <roots of a root system>[roots]. Renumber with $g_1\in L_\alpha$, $g_3\in L_\beta$, $g_4\in L_{\beta+\alpha}$ and $g_5\in L_{\beta+2\alpha}$, retaining opposite partners in the other six positions. <Root spaces> are one dimensional, and the allowed bracket equality gives

Use the convention $A_{ij}=\langle\alpha_j,\alpha_i^\vee\rangle=2(\alpha_i,\alpha_j)/(\alpha_i,\alpha_i)$. For a finite reduced crystallographic <root system>, diagonal entries are two and off-diagonal entries belong to $\{0,-1,-2,-3\}$, with $A_{ij}A_{ji}\in\{0,1,2,3\}$. Transposing this convention merely transposes the <Cartan matrix>.

The $\alpha$-string through $\beta$ has the consecutive <roots of a root system>[roots] $\beta-p\alpha,\ldots,\beta+q\alpha$, where $p,q\ge0$ and $p-q=\langle\beta,\alpha^\vee\rangle$. For distinct <simple roots> $\alpha_i,\alpha_j$, the negative step is absent, so $p=0$ and $q=-A_{ij}$.

The <matrix> determines each simple <reflection> on the simple-<root of a root system>[root] <basis>:
$$
s_i(\alpha_j)=\alpha_j-A_{ij}\alpha_i.
$$
It therefore determines their generated <Weyl group> as linear transformations. Every <root of a root system>[root] is in the Weyl orbit of a <simple root>. For a positive nonsimple <root of a root system>[root] $\beta=\sum c_i\alpha_i$, some $(\beta,\alpha_i)$ is positive because $\sum c_i(\beta,\alpha_i)=(\beta,\beta)>0$. Its <reflection> subtracts a positive integer multiple of $\alpha_i$ and lowers height. It stays positive unless the <root of a root system>[root] is simple, since the other simple-<root of a root system>[root] coefficients are unchanged and at least one is positive. Induction on height reaches a <simple root>; negative <roots of a root system>[roots] follow by sign. Hence $\Phi=W\Delta$, which is completely reconstructed by those <reflection> <matrices>. This determines the system up to <root of a root system>[root]-system isomorphism, rather than fixing an absolute length scale; irreducible components may be rescaled.

For <positive roots for a chain Cartan matrix>, symmetry and adjacency imply all <simple roots> have equal length; normalize their squared lengths to two. Adjacent inner products are minus one and all others zero. Since every <root of a root system>[root] is Weyl-conjugate to a <simple root>, every <root of a root system>[root] also has squared length two. Write a <positive root> as $\beta=\sum_{i=1}^lc_i\omega_i$ with nonnegative integers $c_i$, and set $c_0=c_{l+1}=0$. Then
$$
2=(\beta,\beta)=2\sum_{i=1}^lc_i^2-2\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}c_ic_{i+1}=\sum_{i=0}^l(c_{i+1}-c_i)^2.
$$
There must be exactly two nonzero integer differences, one $+1$ and one $-1$. Nonnegativity and the two zero endpoint values imply that the coefficients are one on one consecutive interval and zero elsewhere.

Conversely a single <simple root> is a <root of a root system>[root]. If $\omega_i+\cdots+\omega_j$ is a <root of a root system>[root] and $j<l$, its pairing with $\omega_{j+1}^\vee$ is minus one. Reflecting in $\omega_{j+1}$ therefore adds that <simple root>, making the next longer interval a <root of a root system>[root]. Induction constructs every interval. Thus
$$
\boxed{\Phi^+=\left\{\sum_{k=i}^j\omega_k:1\le i\le j\le l\right\}.}
$$

Indeed the displayed second row lists the positions of one through seven in the original permutation.

Let $f^\lambda$ be the number of <standard tableaux> of shape $\lambda$. First the associated <polytabloids> are linearly independent. Order <tabloids> lexicographically by reading each row after sorting its entries. For a column-increasing tableau $t$, the term $\{t\}$ in $e_t$ has coefficient one; every other <tabloid> term has larger row word. To see this, take the first row changed by a nontrivial column permutation. Earlier rows are fixed entry by entry, since each entry belongs to one original column. The changed row can only receive entries from lower positions of those increasing columns, so after sorting it is lexicographically larger. In a linear relation between standard <polytabloids>, choose the smallest row word with a nonzero coefficient. Its own <tabloid> coefficient cannot be cancelled by a larger standard <polytabloid>, a contradiction. Thus $\dim S^\lambda\ge f^\lambda$.

Work first over $\mathbb C$. The <RSK> bijection between permutations and pairs of <standard tableaux> of the same shape gives $\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}(f^\lambda)^2=n!$. The assumed pairwise nonisomorphic irreducible <Specht modules>, and the decomposition of the <regular representation> with irreducible multiplicities equal to their dimensions, give $\sum_\lambda(\dim S^\lambda)^2\le n!$. Combining the two inequalities forces equality term by term: $\dim S^\lambda=f^\lambda$. Hence the independent standard <polytabloids> are a **<basis>**.

The same conclusion holds over every characteristic-zero <field>. All <polytabloid> coordinates in the <tabloid> <basis> are integers, so the ranks of their coordinate <matrices> are the same over any characteristic-zero <field> as over $\mathbb C$. In particular the <dimension> equality and independence transfer without requiring an embedding of the whole <field> into $\mathbb C$.

\boxed{0\longrightarrow\mathbf1\longrightarrow S^{(2,2)}\longrightarrow\operatorname{sgn}\longrightarrow0,}
$$
and the two <composition factors> are trivial and sign, each once. In the $D$ notation they are $D^{(4)}$ and $D^{(2,2)}$ respectively. Indeed the restricted <tabloid> form has <Gram matrix> $\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}$, whose characteristic-three radical is precisely the trivial line, so the simple quotient is $D^{(2,2)}$.

The extension does not split: the minus-one <eigenspace> of $\rho(12)$ is the line through $e_2$, but $\rho(23)$ sends that line to the line through $e_1$. Thus there is no sign <submodule> complementing the trivial line, consistent with the failure of semisimplicity.
