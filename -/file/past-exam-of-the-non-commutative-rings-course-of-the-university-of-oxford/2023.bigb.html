A <left-primitive ideal> is a two-sided <ideal> of the form
$$
P=\operatorname{Ann}_R(V)=\{r\in R:rV=0\}
$$
for a nonzero <simple module> $V$ on the left. Equivalently, the <quotient ring> $R/P$ has a <faithful module> that is a <simple module>, and is therefore a <primitive ring>.

To prove that $P$ is a <prime ideal of a noncommutative ring>, let $A,B$ be two-sided <ideals> with $AB\subseteq P$. Since $B$ is two-sided, $BV$ is a <submodule> of $V$: for $r\in R$, $b\in B$ and $v\in V$, one has $r(bv)=(rb)v\in BV$. If $B\not\subseteq P$, then $BV\ne0$, so simplicity gives $BV=V$. Consequently
$$
AV=A(BV)=(AB)V=0,
$$
and $A\subseteq P$. Thus $AB\subseteq P$ implies $A\subseteq P$ or $B\subseteq P$. Also $P\ne R$, since $1$ acts as the <identity element> on the nonzero <module> $V$. These are precisely the required <prime ideal of a noncommutative ring> conditions.

The <Jacobson radical> is
$$
J(R)=\bigcap_{L\text{ maximal left ideal}}L
=\bigcap_{V\text{ simple left module}}\operatorname{Ann}_R(V).
$$
Here the second intersection ranges over isomorphism classes of nonzero <simple modules>. For the equality, every nonzero $v$ in a <simple module> gives a <surjective> <module homomorphism> $R\to V$, $r\mapsto rv$, whose <kernel> is a <maximal left ideal>. Conversely, every <maximal left ideal> $L$ gives the <simple module> $R/L$. This description by <annihilators> shows that $J(R)$ is a two-sided <ideal>.

Let $j\in J(R)$ and put $a=1+j$. If the <left ideal> $Ra$ were proper, it would lie in a <maximal left ideal> $L$. Both $a$ and $j$ would belong to $L$, giving $1\in L$, a contradiction. Therefore there is $u\in R$ with $ua=1$.

A left inverse alone need not be a two-sided inverse in a general <ring>, so one more step is necessary. The equality $u(1+j)=1$ gives $u=1-uj\in1+J(R)$. Applying the same argument to $u$ supplies $v\in R$ with $vu=1$. Then
$$
v=v(ua)=(vu)a=a,
$$
and hence $au=vu=1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{1+J(R)\subseteq R^\times.}
$$
For the <zero ring> the conclusion holds directly; the <maximal left ideal> argument concerns the nonzero case.

Let $L$ be any <maximal left ideal>. If $I\not\subseteq L$, maximality gives $L+I=R$, because the two-sided <ideal> $I$ is in particular a <left ideal>. Thus $1=\ell+i$ for some $\ell\in L$ and $i\in I$. But
$$
\ell=1-i\in1+I
$$
is a <unit> by hypothesis. A proper <left ideal> cannot contain a <unit>, since left multiplication by its inverse would put $1$ in the <left ideal>. This contradiction proves $I\subseteq L$ for every <maximal left ideal> $L$, and therefore
$$
\boxed{I\subseteq J(R).}
$$
Together with <1/a/ii>, this is the ideal form of the <unit criterion for the Jacobson radical>.

Assume $A\ne0$, as is implicit in the claimed conclusion. Suppose that a <surjective> <ring homomorphism> $f:R\to M_2(A)$ exists. Choose $r_1,r_2\in R$ mapping to the <matrix units> $E_{12}$ and $E_{21}$. Applying $f$ to the assumed relation gives integers $c_1,\ldots,c_n$ such that
$$
E_{22}=E_{21}E_{12}
=\sum_{k=1}^n c_kE_{12}^{\,k}E_{21}.

But $E_{12}^2=0$ and $E_{12}E_{21}=E_{11}$, so the right side is $c_1E_{11}$. Its $(2,2)$ entry is zero, whereas the $(2,2)$ entry of $E_{22}$ is $1_A\ne0$. This contradiction proves that no such <surjective> <ring homomorphism> exists.

If the convention for <rings> allows $A$ to be the <zero ring>, that case must be excluded from the assertion: $M_2(0)=0$, and the zero <ring homomorphism> is <surjective>. This is a convention issue in the printed question.

Put $B=R/I$. By the definition of a <left-primitive ideal>, there is a <faithful module> $V$ that is a <simple module> on the left over $B$. The assumed relation passes to the <quotient ring> $B$, since any pair of elements of $B$ can be lifted to $R$.

By <Schur's lemma>, the <endomorphism ring> $\operatorname{End}_B(V)$ is a <division ring>: the <kernel> and <image> of a nonzero <module endomorphism> are <submodules>, so simplicity makes that <module endomorphism> both <injective> and <surjective>. Set
$$
\Delta=\operatorname{End}_B(V)^{\mathrm{op}}.
$$
Evaluation makes $V$ a right <vector space over a division ring> $\Delta$, and the $B$-action is $\Delta$-linear. The <opposite ring> in the definition ensures the correct order for this right action.

The <Jacobson density theorem> says that, for any finite $\Delta$-<linearly independent> family $v_1,\ldots,v_m$ and any $w_1,\ldots,w_m\in V$, some $b\in B$ satisfies $bv_i=w_i$ for every $i$.

Suppose that $V$ contains two $\Delta$-<linearly independent> vectors $v,w$. Applying the <Jacobson density theorem> twice, choose $a,b\in B$ satisfying
$$
av=0,\qquad aw=v,\qquad bv=w,\qquad bw=0.
$$
The inherited relation gives
$$
ba=\sum_{k=1}^n c_ka^kb
$$
with integers $c_k$. Acting on $w$, the left side gives $baw=bv=w$, but every term on the right gives $a^kbw=0$. This contradicts $w\ne0$. Thus $\dim_\Delta V=1$.

The <ring homomorphism>
$$
B\longrightarrow\operatorname{End}_\Delta(V)
$$
given by the action is <injective> because $V$ is a <faithful module>. It is <surjective> because the <Jacobson density theorem>, applied to a single nonzero <basis vector>, realizes every $\Delta$-linear <endomorphism> of this one-dimensional <vector space over a division ring>. Every nonzero such <endomorphism> is invertible. Hence
$$
\boxed{R/I\cong\operatorname{End}_\Delta(V)\text{ is a division ring}.}
$$
The use of the <Jacobson density theorem> here only prescribes actions on finitely many vectors; it does not assume that an arbitrary <primitive ring> is already a full <endomorphism ring>.

A <multiplicative subset> $S$ is a <left Ore set> if for every $s\in S$ and $r\in R$ there are $s'\in S$ and $r'\in R$ with
$$
\boxed{s'r=r's.}
$$
Equivalently, the subsets $Sr$ and $Rs$ intersect for each such pair. This is the common-left-multiple condition needed for <Ore localization> with denominators on the left. In a <noncommutative domain>, no extra condition concerning annihilation by denominators is needed, since all members of $S$ are <regular elements of a ring>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

We first prove that any two nonzero principal <left ideals> $Ra$ and $Rb$ intersect nontrivially. Suppose instead that $Ra\cap Rb=0$. The nonzero <left ideals>
$$
Ra,\quad Rab,\quad Rab^2,\quad\ldots
$$
then form a <direct sum>. Indeed, take a finite relation
$$
\sum_{i=0}^m u_iab^i=0.
$$
It implies
$$
u_0a=-\left(\sum_{i=1}^m u_iab^{i-1}\right)b\in Ra\cap Rb=0.
$$
As $R$ is a <noncommutative domain> and $a\ne0$, this gives $u_0=0$. Right cancellation of the nonzero element $b$ reduces the remaining relation to one of the same form with one fewer term. Repetition gives $u_1=\cdots=u_m=0$, proving the asserted <direct sum>.

Consequently its finite partial sums give a strictly ascending chain of <left ideals>, contradicting the <ascending chain condition> in a <left Noetherian ring>. Thus $Ra\cap Rb\ne0$ whenever $a,b\ne0$. This is the <left Noetherian domain Ore lemma>.

Now take $s\in S=R\setminus\{0\}$ and $r\in R$. If $r=0$, choose $s'=1$ and $r'=0$. Otherwise choose
$$
0\ne z\in Rr\cap Rs,\qquad z=s'r=r's.
$$
Since $z\ne0$, the coefficient $s'$ is nonzero and hence belongs to $S$. This proves the <left Ore set> condition in all cases.

Use $s=x_1$ and $r=x_2$. A putative <left Ore set> equality would be
$$
s'x_2=r'x_1,\qquad s'\in S,\quad r'\in R.
$$
The left side is a single <word> ending in $x_2$, with coefficient $1$. The right side is a finite <linear combination> of <words> ending in $x_1$. Distinct <words> are <linearly independent> in the <free associative algebra>, so equality is impossible. Therefore
$$
\boxed{S\text{ is not a left Ore set in }\mathbb Q\langle x_1,x_2\rangle.}
$$
The last letters matter because the denominators in the <left Ore set> equation occur on the right of the common left multiple.

View $\hat R$ as a <noncommutative formal power series ring>, and let $\varepsilon:\hat R\to\mathbb Q$ take the coefficient of the empty <word>. This is a <ring homomorphism>, since the empty <word> has only the factorization $1\cdot1$. In particular the two-sided <ideal> $I$ generated by $x_1,x_2$ lies in $\ker\varepsilon$.

In fact $I=\ker\varepsilon$. Every <noncommutative formal power series> with zero <constant term> can be partitioned according to the first letter of its <words> and written
$$
h=x_1h_1+x_2h_2,\qquad h_1,h_2\in\hat R.
$$
Both summands belong to the two-sided <ideal> $I$.

For $a=1+h\in1+I$, define the formal <geometric series>
$$
g=\sum_{k=0}^\infty(-h)^k.
$$
This defines an element of $\hat R$: since $h$ has zero <constant term>, every <word> in $h^k$ has length at least $k$. Thus the coefficient of a fixed <word> of length $d$ receives contributions only from $0\leq k\leq d$, each a finite sum.

For each finite partial sum $g_N=\sum_{k=0}^N(-h)^k$, <associativity> gives both identities
$$
(1+h)g_N=g_N(1+h)=1-(-h)^{N+1}.
$$
In every fixed <word> degree the last term vanishes once $N$ is large enough. It follows coefficient by coefficient that $ag=ga=1$. Hence
$$
\boxed{(1+h)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty(-h)^k\qquad(h\in I).}
$$
The argument is formal and uses no analytic convergence. More generally, every <noncommutative formal power series> with a nonzero rational <constant term> is a <unit>, after factoring out that scalar. This is the <unit criterion for noncommutative formal power series>.

Take $D=\hat R$. We first check that this <noncommutative formal power series ring> is a <noncommutative domain>. For nonzero $F,G\in D$, let $p,q$ be the smallest lengths of <words> with nonzero coefficients in $F,G$, respectively. Their degree-$p$ and degree-$q$ parts $F_p,G_q$ are nonzero <homogeneous polynomials> in the <free associative algebra>.

Choose <words> $u,v$ with nonzero coefficients in $F_p,G_q$. In $F_pG_q$, the coefficient of $uv$ is the product of those two coefficients: a <word> of length $p+q$ has a unique splitting into a prefix of length $p$ and a suffix of length $q$. This product is nonzero in $\mathbb Q$. The degree-$p+q$ part of $FG$ is exactly $F_pG_q$, since all lower parts of $F$ and $G$ vanish. Thus $FG\ne0$, proving that $D$ is a <noncommutative domain>.

By the defining property of the <free associative algebra>, there is a <ring homomorphism>
$$
f:R\longrightarrow D,\qquad f(x_1)=1+x_1,\qquad f(x_2)=2+x_2.
$$
The same substitution defines a <ring automorphism> of $R$, with inverse $x_1\mapsto x_1-1$, $x_2\mapsto x_2-2$. Composing this <ring automorphism> with the natural inclusion $R\hookrightarrow D$ proves that $f$ is <injective>.

Finally,
$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x_1+x_2)&=3+x_1+x_2,& f(x_1-x_2)&=-1+x_1-x_2,\\
f(x_1)&=1+x_1,&f(x_2)&=2+x_2.
\end{aligned}
$$
Their <constant terms> are $3,-1,1,2$, respectively, all nonzero. By the <unit criterion for noncommutative formal power series> proved in <2/c>, all four elements are <units>. Therefore $D$ and $f$ have every required property. The embedding changes the images of the generators, so the failure of the original monomial <left Ore set> in <2/b> presents no obstruction.

Use the <Weyl algebra> convention $[y_i,x_j]=\delta_{ij}$, with $[x_i,x_j]=[y_i,y_j]=0$, where the brackets are <commutators>. Its <order filtration of a Weyl algebra> is
$$
F_jR=\sum_{|\beta|\leq j}\mathbb C[x_1,\ldots,x_n]y^\beta\quad(j\geq0),\qquad F_jR=0\quad(j<0).
$$
Thus $x_i$ has degree zero and $y_i$ degree one. By the <ordered monomial basis of a Weyl algebra>, the <associated graded ring> is
$$
A=\operatorname{gr}R\cong\mathbb C[x_1,\ldots,x_n,\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n],\qquad \deg x_i=0,\quad\deg\xi_i=1,
$$
where $\xi_i$ is the symbol of $y_i$.

The <Rees ring of a filtered algebra> is the <graded ring>
$$
\boxed{\widetilde R=\bigoplus_{j\geq0}(F_jR)t^j\subseteq R[t],}
$$
where $t$ is a central indeterminate. Here it is generated by $x_i$, $Y_i=y_it$, and $t$, with degrees $0,1,1$, respectively, and relations
$$
[Y_i,x_j]=\delta_{ij}t,\qquad [x_i,x_j]=[Y_i,Y_j]=[t,x_i]=[t,Y_i]=0.
$$
It interpolates between the <associated graded ring> and the original <filtered ring>:
$$
\widetilde R/(t)\cong\operatorname{gr}R,\qquad \widetilde R/(t-1)\cong R.
$$

A <good filtration for the order filtration> on $M$ is an increasing exhaustive family of complex <vector subspaces> $(M_j)_{j\in\mathbb Z}$, zero for sufficiently negative $j$, with $F_iR\,M_j\subseteq M_{i+j}$, such that the <associated graded module>
$$
\operatorname{gr}M=\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb Z}M_j/M_{j-1}
$$
is a <finitely generated module> over $A$. Equivalently, there are finitely many $m_1,\ldots,m_s$ and integer shifts $d_1,\ldots,d_s$ such that $M_j=\sum_{\ell=1}^s(F_{j-d_\ell}R)m_\ell$. In particular, choose any finite generating family $m_1,\ldots,m_s$ of $M$ and put
$$
\boxed{M_j=\sum_{\ell=1}^s(F_jR)m_\ell.}
$$
This is a <good filtration for the order filtration>.

For $M\ne0$, its <dimension of a Weyl algebra module for the order filtration> is
$$
\boxed{d(M)=\dim\operatorname{Supp}_A(\operatorname{gr}M)
=\dim_{\mathrm{Krull}}\bigl(A/\operatorname{Ann}_A(\operatorname{gr}M)\bigr).}
$$
Here <support of a module>, <annihilator of a module>, and <Krull dimension> are taken over the commutative <polynomial ring> $A$. The corresponding <algebraic set> is the <order characteristic variety of a Weyl algebra module>. This dimension is independent of the chosen <good filtration for the order filtration>; one may set $d(0)=-\infty$.

The pieces $F_jR$ generally have infinite complex <dimension>, since they contain all polynomials in the $x_i$. Thus a polynomial-growth definition based directly on $\dim_{\mathbb C}M_j$ would be inappropriate for this <order filtration of a Weyl algebra>.

For the <submodule> $N\subseteq M$, define its <subspace filtration> and the <quotient filtration> on the <quotient module> $Q=M/N$ by
$$
N_j=N\cap M_j,\qquad Q_j=(M_j+N)/N.
$$
They are compatible <filtrations of a module>, since multiplication by $F_iR$ sends $N_j$ into $N_{i+j}$ and $Q_j$ into $Q_{i+j}$.

In each degree, inclusion and the quotient map induce
$$
\iota_j:N_j/N_{j-1}\longrightarrow M_j/M_{j-1},\qquad
\pi_j:M_j/M_{j-1}\longrightarrow Q_j/Q_{j-1}.
$$
The first map is <injective>: if $n\in N_j$ belongs to $M_{j-1}$, then $n\in N\cap M_{j-1}=N_{j-1}$. The second map is <surjective> by the definition of $Q_j$.

For exactness in the middle, a class $m+M_{j-1}$ lies in $\ker\pi_j$ precisely when $m\in M_j$ and $m+N\in Q_{j-1}$. This means $m=m'+n$ for $m'\in M_{j-1}$ and $n\in N$. Since $m,m'\in M_j$, one also has $n\in N_j$, and
$$
m+M_{j-1}=n+M_{j-1}\in\operatorname{im}\iota_j.
$$
Conversely, every class from $N_j$ maps to zero in $Q_j/Q_{j-1}$. Taking the <direct sum> over all degrees gives the <short exact sequence> of <graded modules>
$$
0\longrightarrow\operatorname{gr}N\longrightarrow\operatorname{gr}M
\longrightarrow\operatorname{gr}(M/N)\longrightarrow0.
$$
The maps respect the $\operatorname{gr}R$-action because they come from <module homomorphisms> compatible with the <filtrations of a module>. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{gr}M/\operatorname{gr}N\cong\operatorname{gr}(M/N)}
$$
as $\operatorname{gr}R$-<modules>. This proves <exactness of associated graded modules for induced filtrations>.

Furthermore $A=\operatorname{gr}R$ is a <Noetherian ring>, and $\operatorname{gr}M$ is a <finitely generated module>. Its <submodule> $\operatorname{gr}N$ and its <quotient module> $\operatorname{gr}(M/N)$ are therefore <finitely generated modules>. Hence the induced <subspace filtration> and <quotient filtration> are both <good filtrations for the order filtration>.

Use the <quotient filtration> induced from the <order filtration of a Weyl algebra>. The key step is to identify the entire <associated graded module> of the <left ideal> $I$, rather than merely take symbols of its displayed generators.

For every member $x^\alpha y^\beta$ of the <ordered monomial basis of a Weyl algebra>, right multiplication by the pure $y$-<monomials> gives
$$
(x^\alpha y^\beta)y_1^3y_2^2=x^\alpha y^{\beta+(3,2,0,\ldots,0)},\qquad
(x^\alpha y^\beta)y_1^2y_3^3=x^\alpha y^{\beta+(2,0,3,0,\ldots,0)}.
$$
No commuting past an $x_i$ is necessary. It follows that $I$ is exactly the complex <linear span> of the <basis> <monomials> satisfying either
$$
\beta_1\geq3,\ \beta_2\geq2,
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
\beta_1\geq2,\ \beta_3\geq3.
$$
Intersecting this <linear span> with each filtration piece and then taking symbols therefore yields the <monomial ideal>
$$
\operatorname{gr}I=K=(\xi_1^3\xi_2^2,\xi_1^2\xi_3^3)\subseteq A.
$$
By <3/b>,
$$
\operatorname{gr}(R/I)\cong A/K.
$$

To determine its <Krull dimension>, compute the <radical of an ideal>:
$$
\sqrt K=(\xi_1\xi_2,\xi_1\xi_3)
=(\xi_1)\cap(\xi_2,\xi_3).
$$
Indeed, $K$ lies in the displayed intersection, an intersection of <prime ideals>. Conversely, $(\xi_1\xi_2)^3\in K$ and $(\xi_1\xi_3)^3\in K$, so both generators of that intersection lie in $\sqrt K$. The equality of the intersection with $(\xi_1\xi_2,\xi_1\xi_3)$ follows by factoring out $\xi_1$ and reducing modulo $(\xi_2,\xi_3)$.

The <order characteristic variety of a Weyl algebra module> in this case has two <irreducible components>:
$$
V(K)=\{\xi_1=0\}\ \cup\ \{\xi_2=\xi_3=0\}.
$$
They are coordinate <affine spaces> of dimensions $2n-1$ and $2n-2$, respectively, in the $2n$ coordinates $x_1,\ldots,x_n,\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n$. The <Krull dimension> is the larger of these component dimensions. Thus
$$
\boxed{d(R/I)=2n-1.}
$$
In particular, two displayed generators do not force a drop of two in <dimension>: their common factor creates the component $\xi_1=0$ of dimension $2n-1$.

In fact $J=R$, so the <quotient module> is zero. This requires an argument using <left ideals>: a <left ideal> is not generally closed under multiplication on the right.

Write $x=x_1$, $y=y_1$, so that $yx-xy=1$. The <commutator product rule> gives
$$
yx^2=x^2y+2x,\qquad y^2x^2=x^2y^2+4xy+2.
$$
Both $y^2x^2$ and $x^2y^2$ lie in $J=Rx^2+Ry^2$, so their difference
$$
h=y^2x^2-x^2y^2=4xy+2
$$
belongs to $J$. Multiplying on the left and subtracting another known element of the <left ideal> gives
$$
yh-4xy^2=4(yx-xy)y+2y=6y\in J.
$$
Because the scalar $6$ is invertible in $\mathbb C$, it follows that $y\in J$, and hence $xy\in J$. Therefore $h-4xy=2\in J$, giving $1\in J$. We conclude
$$
\boxed{R/J=0,\qquad\dim_{\mathbb C}(R/J)=0.}
$$
Thus the answer is yes for every $n\geq1$. All ideal operations in the proof use left multiplication. In particular, simply replacing $J$ by the commutative <ideal> $(x_1^2,\xi_1^2)$ of the <associated graded ring> would miss the lower-order consequences that force $J=R$.
