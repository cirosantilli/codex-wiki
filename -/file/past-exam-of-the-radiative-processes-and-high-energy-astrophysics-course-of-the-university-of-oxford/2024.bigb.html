= Solution
{parent=a}

Use the <shock frame>, with upstream and downstream fluid <velocities> $u_1=u$ and $u_2=u/4$ pointing in the same direction. These are the <strong-shock Rankine-Hugoniot conditions> for a nonrelativistic <monatomic gas> with <adiabatic index> $5/3$. The two scattering media approach one another at relative <speed> $U=u_1-u_2=3u/4$, to first order in $u/c$. An energetic <electron> repeatedly crosses the <shock wave>, scatters from <magnetic turbulence> carried by the local <plasma>, and returns. Each scattering approximately conserves its <energy> in the local fluid rest frame while changing its direction; comparing successive fluid frames produces a systematic <energy> and <momentum> gain. The <energy> comes from the converging bulk flow, through the moving <magnetic field> irregularities and their associated <electric fields>. This is <diffusive shock acceleration>, a <first-order Fermi acceleration> process.

\Image[2024-shock-acceleration.png]
{title=Repeated shock crossings and the distinct upstream and downstream fluid speeds in the shock frame}

Let $\eta$ be the positive cosine of the crossing angle with the <shock wave>'s normal. The <Lorentz transformation> between fluid frames gives, for either crossing direction with the corresponding sign of the normal reversed,
$$
E'=E+Up\eta+O(U^2/c^2).
$$
For an almost <isotropic> population, the number crossing a unit area is weighted by its normal <velocity>. Thus the normalized crossing distribution is $2\eta\,d\eta$, rather than a uniform distribution in $\eta$, and
$$
\langle\eta\rangle=\frac{\int_0^1\eta^2\,d\eta}{\int_0^1\eta\,d\eta}=\frac23,
\qquad
\boxed{\langle\Delta E\rangle_{\rm crossing}=\frac23Up=\frac12up.}
$$
For an ultrarelativistic <electron>, $E\simeq pc$ and its <speed> $v\simeq c$, so
$$
\boxed{\frac{\langle\Delta E\rangle}{E}=\frac{u}{2c},\qquad
\frac{\langle\Delta p\rangle}{p}=\frac{u}{2c}.}
$$
More generally $dE/dp=v$ gives $\langle\Delta p\rangle/p=2U/(3v)$, while a nonrelativistic <kinetic energy> has $\langle\Delta K\rangle/K=4U/(3v)$. A complete upstream–downstream–upstream cycle contains **two** crossings and has ultrarelativistic fractional <energy> gain $u/c$.

The initially cold <electrons> need an injection stage: <shock wave> heating and small-scale <plasma> processes must first produce particles with <speed> much larger than $u$ that can return across the front. The $v\simeq c$ result is a description of that accelerated population; a cold <electron> cannot simply be assigned $E\simeq pc$ at its first encounter.

= Solution
{parent=b}

In the ultrarelativistic regime of <1/a>, retaining a constant mean fractional <energy> gain and ignoring losses gives, after $N$ individual crossings,
$$
\boxed{E_N\simeq E_0\left(1+\frac{u}{2c}\right)^N
\simeq E_0\exp\!\left(\frac{Nu}{2c}\right).}
$$
After $n$ complete return cycles, $N=2n$ and $E_n\simeq E_0(1+u/c)^n$, to the same first-order accuracy. These products approximate the mean amplification; individual <electron> crossing angles give a distribution around it.

The downstream <electron> distribution is nearly <isotropic>. Its crossing <particle flux> per unit area is $n_{\rm a}c/4$, where $n_{\rm a}$ is the accelerated-particle <number density>. The downstream flow permanently advects particles away at <particle flux> $n_{\rm a}u_2$. Their ratio estimates the escape probability after a downstream visit:
$$
P_{\rm esc}\simeq\frac{n_{\rm a}u_2}{n_{\rm a}c/4}
=\frac{4u_2}{c}=\frac uc,
\qquad \boxed{P_{\rm ret}\simeq1-\frac uc.}
$$
Upstream particles are swept back towards the <shock wave> in this idealized infinite-upstream model. Consequently this return probability applies once per **complete cycle**, not once per individual crossing or local scattering event.

Let $A\simeq1+u/c$ be the amplification per complete cycle and $P=P_{\rm ret}$. A steady injection of $\mathcal N_0$ particles leaves $\mathcal N_0P^n$ surviving to reach $E=E_0A^n$. Eliminating $n$ gives the cumulative <power law> and differential <energy> distribution
$$
\mathcal N(>E)\propto E^{\ln P/\ln A},\qquad
\frac{d\mathcal N}{dE}\propto E^{-s},\qquad
s=1-\frac{\ln P}{\ln A}\simeq2.
$$
The escape distribution has the same differential exponent when $P$ is independent of <energy>. Thus the standard <strong-shock Rankine-Hugoniot conditions> give
$$
\boxed{\mathcal N(>E)\propto E^{-1},\qquad d\mathcal N/dE\propto E^{-2}.}
$$
Equivalently the phase-space <one-particle distribution function> is $f(p)\propto p^{-4}$ for ultrarelativistic particles. Equal logarithmic intervals then contain equal particle <energy>. A finite source age, upstream escape, and radiative losses eventually introduce cutoffs or breaks.

A particle exponent is not automatically the <radiation spectral index>. For <optically thin> uncooled <synchrotron radiation> from $d\mathcal N/dE\propto E^{-s}$, the characteristic <frequency> scales as $\nu\propto E^2$ and the emitted <power> as $E^2$. Hence $L_\nu\propto E^{-s}E^2/(d\nu/dE)\propto\nu^{-(s-1)/2}$. With the convention $L_\nu\propto\nu^{-\alpha}$, this gives $\boxed{\alpha\simeq1/2}$ and $\nu L_\nu\propto\nu^{1/2}$. Strong <synchrotron cooling> steepens a continuously injected particle distribution by one exponent, giving $\alpha\simeq1$ above its cooling break. A <photon> number distribution $d\dot N_\gamma/d\varepsilon\propto\varepsilon^{-\Gamma}$ has $\Gamma=\alpha+1$, or $3/2$ in the uncooled case.

**Convention qualification.** If the printed $P=1-u/c$ is instead imposed independently at every individual crossing, its combination with $A_{\rm crossing}=1+u/(2c)$ gives $s\simeq3$ and uncooled $\alpha\simeq1$. For example $u/c=0.01$ gives $s=1-\ln(0.99)/\ln(1.005)\simeq3.015$, whereas the consistent cycle model gives $s\simeq2.010$. The standard $s=2$ estimate requires matching the probability and amplification to the same complete cycle.

= Solution

In a sufficiently tight <binary star>, the <main sequence> companion can fill its <Roche lobe>. <Roche-lobe overflow> then transfers gas through the <inner Lagrange point> towards the <neutron star>. The gas carries <angular momentum>, so it generally forms an <accretion disk> rather than falling radially onto the surface. Internal stresses carry <angular momentum> outwards and permit <accretion> inwards. Capture of a stellar wind is another possible supply mechanism if the companion does not fill its <Roche lobe>.

Near the <neutron star>, the rapidly increasing <magnetic dipole field>, with $B\propto r^{-3}$, becomes dynamically important when its <magnetic pressure> $B^2/(2\mu_0)$ is comparable to the inflow stresses. The <accretion disk> is truncated around a magnetospheric radius. Ionized material attaches to <magnetic field lines> and streams along them in <magnetically channelled accretion>, forming an <accretion column> above one or both magnetic poles. The <Lorentz force> impedes motion across the <magnetic field>, but does not forbid motion parallel to it. Gas can therefore reach the polar surface, with its gravitational <potential energy> released in a hot <shock wave> and radiation.

This description presumes the <neutron star> rotates slowly enough for material to reach it: sufficiently rapid rotation can centrifugally expel the inflow instead. A strong <magnetic field> by itself does not guarantee surface <accretion>.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Work in the locally planar <shock frame>, taking $v_u,v_d>0$ towards the surface. For a stationary <normal shock wave> with negligible radiative loss inside the discontinuity, the <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas> express <conservation of mass>, <conservation of momentum>, and <conservation of energy> per unit area as
$$
\boxed{\rho_uv_u=\rho_dv_d\equiv J,}
$$
$$
\boxed{P_u+\rho_uv_u^2=P_d+\rho_dv_d^2,}
$$
$$
\boxed{J\left(\frac{v_u^2}{2}+h_u\right)
=J\left(\frac{v_d^2}{2}+h_d\right),\qquad
h=\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\frac P\rho.}
$$
Here $h$ is <specific enthalpy>, including the <pressure> work; using only <internal energy> would omit that work. The <gravitational potential> is continuous across an infinitesimally thin front. For cold upstream gas, $P_u$ and $h_u$ are negligible, while a <monatomic gas> has $\gamma=5/3$ and $h=5P/(2\rho)$.

The hydrodynamic equations apply to flow parallel to the guiding <magnetic field> when its stress contributions cancel across the front. An oblique <magnetohydrodynamic shock> requires magnetic stresses and <Poynting flux>; a radiation-mediated front or appreciable escaping radiation requires the corresponding radiative <momentum> and <energy flux> terms. These are additional physics, not terms hidden in $h$ here.

= Solution
{parent=e}

For the stipulated <strong-shock Rankine-Hugoniot conditions>, $\rho_d=4\rho_u$ and $v_d=v_u/4$. The <ideal gas law> is $P_d=\rho_d k_BT_d/\mu$, with $\mu$ a dimensional <mean particle mass>. The <specific enthalpy> of a nonrelativistic <monatomic gas> is $5k_BT/(2\mu)$. Dividing the <energy flux> equation in <1/d> by the common <mass flux> gives
$$
\frac{v_u^2}{2}=\frac{v_u^2}{32}+\frac{5k_BT_d}{2\mu}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\frac{5k_BT_d}{2\mu}=\frac{15v_u^2}{32},\qquad
\boxed{k_BT_d=\frac{3}{16}\mu v_u^2.}
$$
As an independent check, <conservation of momentum> gives $P_d=\rho_uv_u^2-4\rho_u(v_u/4)^2=3\rho_uv_u^2/4$, and division by $\rho_d=4\rho_u$ gives exactly the same <temperature>. The numerical coefficient relies on $\gamma=5/3$ and the absence of dynamically important radiative or magnetic stresses.

= Solution
{parent=f}

Interpret the terminal infall <speed> as the free-fall <speed> from a radius much larger than the <neutron star> radius $R_*=12\,\mathrm{km}$. In the Newtonian estimate,
$$
v_u\simeq\sqrt{\frac{2GM_*}{R_*}}
=2.00\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\simeq0.666c,
\qquad M_*=1.8M_\odot.
$$
For fully ionized <hydrogen> with <electrons> and <protons> at a common <temperature>, there are two particles per atom and $\mu=(m_p+m_e)/2\simeq m_p/2$. The result of <1/e> then yields
$$
\boxed{k_BT_d=\frac{3GM_*m_p}{16R_*}\simeq39\,\mathrm{MeV},\qquad
T_d\simeq4.5\times10^{11}\,\mathrm K.}
$$
This is the thermal <energy> scale $k_BT_d$ per particle. In the same classical <monatomic gas> approximation, the mean random <kinetic energy> per particle is $3k_BT_d/2\simeq59\,\mathrm{MeV}$. Before the <shock wave>, the bulk <kinetic energy> per <proton> is $m_pv_u^2/2=GM_*m_p/R_*\simeq208\,\mathrm{MeV}$. Distinguishing these quantities avoids an ambiguity in “particle energy”. If only the ions support the downstream <pressure> because <electrons> have not equilibrated, $\mu\simeq m_p$ instead gives $k_BT_i\simeq78\,\mathrm{MeV}$; that is a different, two-temperature assumption.

These are estimates within the question's Newtonian, $\gamma=5/3$ model. Its self-consistency is poor here: $2GM_*/(R_*c^2)\simeq0.443$, and $39\,\mathrm{MeV}$ is much larger than the <electron> rest <energy> $0.511\,\mathrm{MeV}$. Relativistic <electrons>, electron–positron <pair production>, radiation, unequal species <temperatures>, and <general relativity> can alter the <equation of state> and <shock wave> structure. In particular, the numerical $39\,\mathrm{MeV}$ must not be presented as a precision surface <temperature> prediction.

= Solution

Write $x=ar=\pi r/R$. The <mass density> has a finite central value because $\sin x/x\to1$ as $x\to0$, is monotonically decreasing on $0<x<\pi$, and first vanishes at $r=R$. Near the centre, $\rho/\rho_c=1-\pi^2r^2/(6R^2)+O(r^4/R^4)$; near the surface, $\rho/\rho_c\simeq(R-r)/R$. It is zero outside. The enclosed <mass> is
$$
m(r)=4\pi\int_0^r s^2\rho(s)\,ds
=\frac{4\pi\rho_c}{a^3}(\sin x-x\cos x).
$$
Consequently $M=m(R)=4\pi^2\rho_c/a^3=4\rho_cR^3/\pi$, so
$$
\boxed{\rho_c=\frac{\pi M}{4R^3},\qquad
\frac{\rho_c}{\bar\rho}=\frac{\pi^2}{3},\qquad
\bar\rho=\frac{3M}{4\pi R^3}.}
$$
This is the <polytrope of index one> profile. Its derivative is negative because $\sin x-x\cos x>0$ for $0<x<\pi$: the latter function vanishes at zero and has derivative $x\sin x>0$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Charge neutrality in fully ionized <hydrogen> gives $n_e=n_p$ and $\rho=(m_p+m_e)n_e\simeq m_pn_e$. With only <Thomson scattering>, the <photon> <mean free path> is $\ell=(n_e\sigma_T)^{-1}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\ell(r)=L\frac{ar}{\sin ar},\qquad
L=\frac{m_p}{\sigma_T\rho_c}=\frac{4m_pR^3}{\pi\sigma_TM},\qquad
\frac LR=\frac{4m_pR^2}{\pi\sigma_TM}.}
$$
Here and below the tiny <electron mass> correction can be retained by replacing $m_p$ by $m_p+m_e$. $L$ is the central <mean free path>, not a <luminosity>. The divergence at $r=R$ reflects the vanishing <electron> <number density>.

Using $M_\odot=1.9885\times10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg}$, $R_\odot=6.957\times10^8\,\mathrm m$, $m_p=1.6726\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}$, and $\sigma_T=6.6525\times10^{-29}\,\mathrm{m^2}$ gives
$$
\rho_c\simeq4.64\times10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}},\qquad
\boxed{L_\odot\simeq5.42\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm m,\qquad L_\odot/R_\odot\simeq7.79\times10^{-12}.}
$$
These are this profile's values, rather than the <Sun>'s actual central <mass density> or opacity profile.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Put $\Delta=R-r\ll R$. The near-surface <mass density> and <mean free path> from <2/a> and <2/b> are
$$
\rho\simeq\rho_c\frac\Delta R,\qquad
\ell\simeq\frac{LR}{\Delta}.
$$
A simple local escape estimate sets the remaining distance equal to the <mean free path>, $\ell\simeq\Delta$. It gives
$$
\boxed{\Delta R_{\rm esc,local}\simeq\sqrt{LR},\qquad
\frac{\Delta R_{\rm esc,local}}R\simeq\sqrt{\frac LR}.}
$$
For the <Sun>'s parameters this is $\boxed{\Delta R_{\rm esc,local}\simeq1.94\,\mathrm{km},\quad\Delta R_{\rm esc,local}/R\simeq2.79\times10^{-6}}$.

A more precise operational definition uses the outward radial <optical depth>. In this varying <mass density>,
$$
\tau(r)=\int_r^R\frac{ds}{\ell(s)}
=\frac1{aL}\int_{ar}^{\pi}\frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx
\simeq\frac{\Delta^2}{2LR}.
$$
The chance of an outward radial <photon> escaping without another scattering is $e^{-\tau}$. Thus the conventional $\tau=1$ last-scattering depth gives
$$
\boxed{\Delta R_{\rm esc,\tau=1}\simeq\sqrt{2LR}
\simeq2.75\,\mathrm{km},\qquad
\Delta R_{\rm esc,\tau=1}/R\simeq3.95\times10^{-6}.}
$$
There is no sharp escape surface: $\tau\ll1$ gives nearly free propagation, and oblique rays have different <optical depths>. The local $\ell=\Delta$ estimate actually has $\tau\simeq1/2$ in this model, not $1$; the factor $\sqrt2$ is a convention-dependent order-one correction, not a discrepancy in the <Thomson cross-section>. Neither scattering criterion establishes a thermalization surface.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For a spherical <star> in Newtonian <hydrostatic equilibrium>, the total supporting <pressure> satisfies
$$
\frac{dP}{dr}=-\frac{Gm(r)\rho(r)}{r^2},\qquad P(R)=0,
$$
where zero external <pressure> is the idealized vacuum boundary condition. Substitute the enclosed <mass> from <2/a>, set $x=ar$, and integrate inwards:
$$
P(r)=\frac{4\pi G\rho_c^2}{a^2}
\int_{ar}^{\pi}\frac{\sin x(\sin x-x\cos x)}{x^3}\,dx.
$$
The supplied <integral> follows directly by differentiating $\tfrac12(\sin x/x)^2$. It yields
$$
\boxed{P(r)=\frac{2\pi G\rho_c^2}{a^2}
\left(\frac{\sin ar}{ar}\right)^2
=\frac{2GR^2}{\pi}\rho(r)^2.}
$$
Thus $P_c=\pi GM^2/(8R^4)$, about $4.42\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{Pa}$ for the <Sun>'s parameters. This is precisely the <equation of state> of a <polytrope of index one>.

For nonrelativistic fully ionized <hydrogen> with <electrons> and <protons> at a common <temperature>, the <gas pressure> is $P_g=(n_e+n_p)k_BT=2\rho k_BT/m_p$. If <gas pressure> dominates, the resulting <temperature> profile is
$$
\boxed{T_g(r)=\frac{m_pP(r)}{2k_B\rho(r)}
=T_c\frac{\sin ar}{ar},\qquad
T_c=\frac{GMm_p}{4Rk_B}.}
$$
Numerically $T_c\simeq5.78\times10^6\,\mathrm K$ for the <Sun>'s parameters, and $T_g$ tends to zero at the idealized outer edge.

If the <photons> are additionally assumed to be in <local thermodynamic equilibrium> with this gas, their <radiation pressure> is $a_{\rm rad}T^4/3$, with $a_{\rm rad}=4\sigma_{\rm SB}/c$. The consistent <temperature> is instead the unique nonnegative root of
$$
\boxed{\frac{2\rho(r)k_B}{m_p}T(r)+\frac{a_{\rm rad}}3T(r)^4=P(r).}
$$
The left side is strictly increasing in $T$ for $r<R$, so the root is unique. Evaluating the <radiation pressure> at $T_g$ gives at most $P_{\rm rad}/P\simeq6.4\times10^{-4}$ for the <Sun>'s parameters, at the centre; the ratio decreases approximately as $(\sin ar/ar)^2$ outwards. The gas-only profile is consequently an excellent approximation *within this imposed stellar model*.

<Hydrostatic equilibrium> by itself fixes total <pressure>, not how it is divided between gas and radiation. <Thomson scattering> alone does not enforce a common gas–radiation <temperature> or a <Planck photon distribution>. Without either neglecting <radiation pressure> or specifying the radiation field and its thermodynamic closure, a unique gas <temperature> cannot be inferred from the given <mass density> alone.

= Solution
{parent=e}

Under an additional thermalization assumption, an opaque <star> emits approximately <black-body radiation> characterized by the <temperature> near the layer from which thermal <photons> escape. <Planck's law> gives a <Rayleigh-Jeans law> low-<frequency> tail $B_\nu\propto T\nu^2$ and a <Wien approximation> high-<frequency> tail $B_\nu\propto\nu^3e^{-h\nu/(k_BT)}$.

Using the gas-dominated <temperature> profile in <2/d> and the local escape criterion in <2/c>, the model's putative emission <temperature> for the <Sun>'s parameters would be
$$
T_{\rm esc}\simeq T_c\frac{\Delta R_{\rm esc}}R
\simeq16.1\,\mathrm K.
$$
Using radial $\tau=1$ instead gives $22.8\,\mathrm K$. The <frequency form of Wien's displacement law> for the maximum of $B_\nu$ is $h\nu_{\max}/(k_BT)=2.82144$, so these two prescriptions predict
$$
\boxed{\nu_{\max}\simeq9.5\times10^{11}\,\mathrm{Hz}
\quad\hbox{or}\quad1.34\times10^{12}\,\mathrm{Hz},}
$$
respectively, in the far infrared/submillimetre region. These are conditional estimates for this model, not observed solar peaks. A realistic solar <effective temperature> near $5.8\times10^3\,\mathrm K$ gives $\nu_{\max}\simeq3.4\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$ in $B_\nu$. The maximum of $B_\lambda$ occurs near $0.50\,\mathrm{\mu m}$; it is not obtained by simply taking $c/\nu_{\max}$, because spectral densities transform with a <Jacobian determinant>.

The model is unsuitable for the solar <photosphere>: fully ionized <hydrogen> at $16$–$23\,\mathrm K$ is inconsistent with thermal ionization, and the assumed opacity and near-surface <temperature> gradient do not resemble the <Sun>'s. Even its central <temperature> is substantially below the actual solar value, about $1.6\times10^7\,\mathrm K$.

There is a further logical limitation: the question retains **only <Thomson scattering>**. In its coherent, cold-<electron> limit, this redistributes <photon> directions without generating a <Planck photon distribution>. For a concrete counterexample, monochromatic injected radiation remains monochromatic after any number of such elastic scatterings; large <optical depth> cannot turn it into <black-body radiation>. True absorption and emission, or a specified pre-existing thermal radiation field with appropriate energy exchange, are missing. Thus an approximately <blackbody> emergent spectrum is the usual additional physical assumption, while the stated scattering-only model does not uniquely predict an emergent spectrum or its peak.

= Solution

<Thermal bremsstrahlung> is radiation from the acceleration of <electrons> in the <Coulomb force> of ions. At fixed <temperature>, the encounter rate per <electron> is proportional to ion <number density> $n_i$, and the number of radiating <electrons> per unit <volume> is $n_e$. Therefore $j_\nu\propto n_en_i$ in a dilute, <optically thin> <plasma>. This is a binary-encounter rate, not a one-particle emission process.

For a fixed encounter geometry, the ionic <electric field> and hence the <electron> acceleration are proportional to ionic charge $Ze$. The <Larmor formula> is quadratic in acceleration, giving $j_\nu\propto Z^2$ at fixed species <number densities>. The dimensionless <Gaunt factor> $g(\nu,T)$ corrects the simple classical result for the detailed quantum scattering and radiation calculation, after averaging over the thermal <electron> distribution. It depends on <frequency> and <temperature> and, in a more complete notation, ionic charge.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For $h\nu\ll k_BT$, expand the exponential in the <thermal free-free emissivity>:
$$
e^{-h\nu/(k_BT)}=1-\frac{h\nu}{k_BT}+O\!\left(\frac{h^2\nu^2}{k_B^2T^2}\right).
$$
The <energy> emitted per unit <frequency> is therefore approximately flat, with the weak <Gaunt factor> dependence superposed. At these low <photon> <energies>, most of the thermal <electrons> can participate in emission; there is no exponentially small population threshold.

For $h\nu>k_BT$, the spectrum turns down approximately as $e^{-h\nu/(k_BT)}$, apart from the <Gaunt factor>. <Conservation of energy> requires an emitting <electron> to supply at least $h\nu$. The high-<energy> tail of its <Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution> is exponentially rare, which produces this cutoff.

“Flat” refers to $j_\nu$, an <energy> spectrum per unit <frequency>. The emitted <photon> number spectrum instead contains $1/(h\nu)$, while <energy> per logarithmic <frequency> interval is $\nu j_\nu$. At sufficiently low <frequency> the <plasma> can also become <optically thick> to <free-free absorption>; the given optically thin emissivity is then not the escaping spectrum.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Neglect <electron mass> and nuclear binding corrections when converting <mass density> $\rho$ into <hydrogen> and <helium> <number densities>. With $x=x_H$,
$$
n_H=\frac{x\rho}{m_p},\qquad n_{\rm He}=\frac{(1-x)\rho}{4m_p},\qquad
n_e=n_H+2n_{\rm He}=\frac{(1+x)\rho}{2m_p}.
$$
The <thermal free-free emissivity> adds over ion species with weights $Z_i^2$. Consequently
$$
\sum_i Z_i^2n_i=n_H+4n_{\rm He}=\frac\rho{m_p}=\frac{2n_e}{1+x}.
$$
With each <Gaunt factor> set to one, the mixture has
$$
\boxed{j_\nu=j_0\frac{2}{1+x_H}
\left(\frac{n_e}{10^{-3}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-3}}\right)^2
\sqrt{\frac{T_0}{T}}e^{-h\nu/(k_BT)}.}
$$
At fixed $n_e$, pure <hydrogen> gives the composition factor $1$, and pure <helium> gives $2$. The latter check follows independently from $n_{\rm He}=n_e/2$ and $Z^2=4$; simply replacing $Z$ by an average charge would lose this weighting.

= Solution
{parent=d}

The coefficient $j_\nu$ is <power> per unit <volume>, <frequency> and <solid angle>. For uniform, <optically thin>, <isotropic> emission from $V=4\pi R^3/3$, the <spectral luminosity> is $L_\nu=4\pi Vj_\nu$. With <photon> <energy> $\varepsilon=h\nu$, <conservation of energy> and the change of spectral variable give
$$
L_\nu\,d\nu=\varepsilon L_\varepsilon^X\,d\varepsilon,
\qquad d\nu=\frac{d\varepsilon}{h}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{L_\varepsilon^X=\frac{4\pi}{h\varepsilon}\frac{4\pi R^3}{3}
 j_0\frac{2}{1+x_H}
\left(\frac{n_e}{10^{-3}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-3}}\right)^2
\sqrt{\frac{T_0}{T}}e^{-\varepsilon/(k_BT)}.}
$$
The $4\pi$ outside $V$ integrates over emitted <solid angle>; $1/\varepsilon$ changes radiated <energy> to a <photon> count, and $1/h$ is the <Jacobian determinant> from <frequency> to <energy>.

With $h$ in $\mathrm{J\,s}$ and $\varepsilon$ in joules, this formula produces $\mathrm{J^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$. To compare with counts per $\mathrm{keV}$, multiply by $1.602176634\times10^{-16}\,\mathrm{J/keV}$. Equivalently, the conversion cancels the same joule-per-$\mathrm{keV}$ factor in $\varepsilon$: one can use the numerical $\varepsilon$ in $\mathrm{keV}$ in the $1/\varepsilon$ factor, retain $h$ in $\mathrm{J\,s}$, and express $k_BT$ in $\mathrm{keV}$ in the exponential. The radius in the <volume> must still be converted to metres because $j_0$ is per $\mathrm{m^3}$.

= Solution
{parent=e}

In the <thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect>, hot intracluster <electrons> transfer <energy> to <cosmic microwave background> <photons> by <inverse Compton scattering>. Their thermal <energy> is much larger than the <CMB> <photon> <energy>; in the nonrelativistic limit the mean fractional <photon> <energy> gain per scattering is about $4k_BT/(m_ec^2)$. Multiplication by the incremental <Thomson scattering> <optical depth> $d\tau=\sigma_Tn_e\,dx$ explains why $y=\int[k_BT/(m_ec^2)]\,d\tau$ measures the electron <pressure> integrated along the line of sight. The result is a spectral distortion, with a low-<frequency> decrement and a high-<frequency> increment, rather than a positive additional emission component at every <frequency>.

For a uniform sphere, a line of sight at impact parameter $b$ has path length $2\sqrt{R^2-b^2}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{y(b)=\frac{2\sigma_T}{m_ec^2}n_ek_BT\sqrt{R^2-b^2},\qquad 0\le b\le R.}
$$
On the central line of sight this reduces to the requested expression
$$
\boxed{y(0)=\frac{2\sigma_T}{m_ec^2}Rn_ek_BT.}
$$
The central-ray qualification matters: the average over the projected disk is $\bar y=(2/3)y(0)$, while the tangent ray $b=R$ has $y(R)=0$. Thus the printed formula does not hold for an arbitrary sightline. Part <3/f> uses the specified $y$ as this central value, as required by the preceding formula.

= Solution
{parent=f}

Write $\Theta=k_BT$ in $\mathrm{keV}$. The <photon> count ratio in <3/d> eliminates <radius>, <electron> <number density> and the common normalization:
$$
\frac{L_{\varepsilon_1}^X}{L_{\varepsilon_2}^X}
=\frac{\varepsilon_2}{\varepsilon_1}
\exp\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon_2-\varepsilon_1}{\Theta}\right).
$$
The extra $\varepsilon_2/\varepsilon_1$ is essential for a <photon> number spectrum. Hence
$$
\boxed{\Theta=\frac{5-0.2}{\ln\!\left[\frac{(2.88\times10^{54})(0.2)}{(4.40\times10^{50})(5)}\right]}
=0.86212\,\mathrm{keV},\qquad
T=1.00045\times10^7\,\mathrm K.}
$$
The measured central <Compton y parameter> and the supplied conversion coefficient then determine a column combination:
$$
Rn_e=\frac{y}{2(0.004)\Theta}
=2.00087\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Mpc\,cm^{-3}}\equiv Y.
$$
To determine the two remaining quantities separately, define $\widehat R=R/(1\,\mathrm{Mpc})$ and $\widehat n=n_e/(1\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}})$. Use $1\,\mathrm{Mpc}=3.08567758\times10^{22}\,\mathrm m$, $h=6.62607015\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$, $j_0=4.885\times10^{-51}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-3}\,Hz^{-1}\,sr^{-1}}$, $T_0=1.977\times10^9\,\mathrm K$, and $x_H=0.76$ in <3/d>. With the spectral unit conversion explained there, the $0.2\,\mathrm{keV}$ count <luminosity> is
$$
L_{0.2}^X=\left(7.22106\times10^{59}\,\mathrm{keV^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\right)
\widehat R^3\widehat n^2.
$$
Explicitly the numerical coefficient is
$$
\frac{4\pi}{h(0.2)}\frac{4\pi}{3}
(3.08567758\times10^{22})^3j_0\frac{2}{1.76}
10^6\sqrt{\frac{T_0}{T}}e^{-0.2/\Theta}.
$$
Since $\widehat R\widehat n=0.00200087$, substitution gives $\widehat R^3\widehat n^2=\widehat R(0.00200087)^2$, so
$$
\boxed{R=0.9962\,\mathrm{Mpc}\simeq1.00\,\mathrm{Mpc},\qquad
n_e=2.0085\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}\simeq2.00\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}.}
$$
Rounding consistently with the supplied coefficient $0.004$, the cluster has $T\simeq10^7\,\mathrm K$, $R\simeq1\,\mathrm{Mpc}$ and $n_e\simeq2\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. Substituting the unrounded values into both <X-ray> count <luminosities> gives $2.8800\times10^{54}$ and $4.4000\times10^{50}\,\mathrm{keV^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$, and the central <Compton y parameter> returns $1.3800\times10^{-5}$. If the observed $y$ were a projected-disk average, its central value would be $3/2$ times larger and this same inversion would give $R$ smaller by $4/9$ and $n_e$ larger by $27/8$; specifying the sightline is therefore part of the inference.
