Both terms in the <graph norm> are finite and nonnegative. Moreover, $\|x\|_T=0$ forces $\|x\|_X=0$, hence $x=0$; conversely $T0=0$ gives $\|0\|_T=0$. This proves <definiteness of a norm>.

The <linearity> of $T$ and <absolute homogeneity of a norm> give, for each real $a$,
$$
\|ax\|_T=|a|\|x\|_X+\|aTx\|_Y=|a|\|x\|_T.
$$
Applying the <triangle inequality> in both <normed spaces> gives
$$
\|x+z\|_T=\|x+z\|_X+\|Tx+Tz\|_Y\leq\|x\|_T+\|z\|_T.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\|\cdot\|_T\text{ is a norm on }X.}
$$

Since $W$ is proper, $T$ is a nonzero <linear functional>. Choose $e\in X$ with $Te=1$, by rescaling any <vector> outside its <kernel>. For every $x\in X$,
$$
x=\underbrace{(x-(Tx)e)}_{\in W}+(Tx)e.
$$
If $ae\in W$, applying $T$ gives $a=0$. Consequently this is a <direct sum>, and the <quotient vector space> $X/W$ has <dimension> one. This computes the <codimension of a vector subspace>:
$$
\boxed{X=W\oplus\langle e\rangle,\qquad\operatorname{codim}_XW=1.}
$$

To prove boundedness without assuming <completeness>, use the <linear functional with closed kernel> criterion directly. Since $W$ is a <closed set> and $e\notin W$, the distance
$$
d=\inf_{w\in W}\|e-w\|_X
$$
is positive. If $Tx\ne0$, then $e-x/(Tx)\in W$, so
$$
d\leq\left\|\frac{x}{Tx}\right\|_X=\frac{\|x\|_X}{|Tx|}.
$$
For $Tx=0$ the resulting estimate is immediate. Therefore
$$
\boxed{|Tx|\leq d^{-1}\|x\|_X\quad(x\in X),\qquad T\in X^*.}
$$
Here $X^*$ denotes the <continuous dual space>.

= Solution

The <bounded linear functional> from part (i) satisfies $|Tx|\leq\|T\|\|x\|_X$, where $\|T\|$ is its <operator norm>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\|x\|_X\leq\|x\|_T\leq(1+\|T\|)\|x\|_X.}
$$
These are <equivalent norms>. Applied to $x_n-x_m$, the inequalities show that a <sequence> is a <Cauchy sequence> for either <norm> exactly when it is one for the other. Applied to $x_n-x$, they show that the two <norms> give the same limits in $X$. Thus every <Cauchy sequence> has a limit in one <norm> precisely when it does in the other:
$$
\boxed{(X,\|\cdot\|_T)\text{ is complete}\iff(X,\|\cdot\|_X)\text{ is complete}.}
$$

= Solution

Let $(x_n)$ be a <Cauchy sequence> in the <graph norm>, and set $y_n=Tx_n$. Since
$$
\|y_n-y_m\|_Y\leq\|x_n-x_m\|_T,
$$
$(y_n)$ is a <Cauchy sequence> in the <Banach space> $Y$. Write $y_n\to y\in Y$.

Use the bounded <right inverse> to remove the part seen by $T$:
$$
w_n=x_n-Sy_n,\qquad Tw_n=Tx_n-TSy_n=y_n-y_n=0.
$$
Thus $w_n\in W$, and the <operator norm> bound gives
$$
\|w_n-w_m\|_X\leq\|x_n-x_m\|_X+\|S\|\|y_n-y_m\|_Y
\leq(1+\|S\|)\|x_n-x_m\|_T.
$$
The assumed <completeness> of $W$ supplies $w\in W$ with $w_n\to w$ in $\|\cdot\|_X$. Put $x=w+Sy\in X$. The <linearity> of $T$ and the <right inverse> identity give $Tx=y$. Therefore
$$
\|x_n-x\|_T\leq\|w_n-w\|_X+(1+\|S\|)\|y_n-y\|_Y\longrightarrow0.
$$
We have proved the <completeness of a graph norm from a bounded right inverse>:
$$
\boxed{(X,\|\cdot\|_T)\text{ is a Banach space}.}
$$

By the <mean value theorem>, a <continuously differentiable function> with zero <derivative> on $[-1,1]$ is constant. Thus the <kernel> of the <differentiation operator> is
$$
\boxed{W=\{x\mapsto a:a\in\mathbb R\}=\langle1\rangle.}
$$
It is a <closed set> by the <finite-dimensional subspace is closed> theorem, applied in the <normed space> $(C^1([-1,1]),\|\cdot\|_\infty)$. It is proper because the <function> $x\mapsto x$ has nonzero <derivative>.

For $n\geq1$, take $u_n(x)=\sin(nx)$. Its <supremum norm> is at most one, whereas $u_n'(0)=n$ and $|u_n'(x)|\leq n$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\|Tu_n\|_\infty=n,\quad\|u_n\|_\infty\leq1;\qquad T\text{ is unbounded}.}
$$

Choose the <continuously differentiable functions>
$$
f_n(x)=\sqrt{x^2+n^{-2}}.
$$
The estimate $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\leq a+b$ for $a,b\geq0$ gives
$$
0\leq f_n(x)-|x|\leq\frac1n,\qquad
\|f_n-|\cdot|\|_\infty=\frac1n.

Thus $f_n\to|\cdot|$ by <uniform convergence>, and the <triangle inequality> gives $\|f_n-f_m\|_\infty\leq n^{-1}+m^{-1}$. The <sequence> is a <Cauchy sequence> in $X$, but its unique possible <supremum norm> limit is $x\mapsto|x|$, which is not <differentiable> at zero. That limit is outside $X$, so
$$
\boxed{(C^1([-1,1]),\|\cdot\|_\infty)\text{ is incomplete}.}
$$

The <kernel> $W=\langle1\rangle$ is <complete>: the map $a\mapsto a1$ is an <isometry> from $\mathbb R$ with its absolute-value <norm> onto $W$ with the <supremum norm>.

Define a <linear map> $S:Y\to X$ by

(Sg)(x)=\int_0^x g(t)\,dt.

The <fundamental theorem of calculus> gives $Sg\in C^1([-1,1])$ and $TSg=g$. Also,

|(Sg)(x)|\leq|x|\|g\|_\infty\leq\|g\|_\infty,
\qquad\|Sg\|_X\leq\|g\|_Y.

Thus $S$ is a bounded <right inverse>, and $Y=C([-1,1])$ is a <Banach space>. Applying part (c),

For a <normed space> $X$, let $X^*$ be its <continuous dual space> and $X^{**}=(X^*)^*$ its <bidual space>. Its <canonical embedding into the bidual> is
$$
J_X:X\to X^{**},\qquad(J_Xx)(\ell)=\ell(x).
$$
The definition of a <reflexive space> in this setting is
$$
\boxed{J_X(X)=X^{**}.}
$$
The <Hahn-Banach theorem> makes $J_X$ a <linear map> that is an <isometry>. For any <normed space> $Z$, its <continuous dual space> is a <Banach space>: a <Cauchy sequence> $(\ell_n)$ in the <operator norm> has scalar limits $\ell(z)=\lim_n\ell_n(z)$, and the uniform Cauchy bound proves both boundedness of $\ell$ and $\|\ell_n-\ell\|\to0$. Apply this with $Z=X^*$ to see that $X^{**}$ is <complete>. If $J_X$ is onto, the <isometry> transfers this <completeness> to $X$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{X\text{ reflexive}\ \Longrightarrow\ X\text{ complete}.}
$$

The answer is
$$
\boxed{p=1,\qquad p'=\infty.}
$$
Indeed, <Lebesgue measure> on $E\subset\mathbb R^n$ is a <sigma-finite measure>, and <duality of Lp spaces> gives $(L^1(E))^*=L^\infty(E)$. The restricted <Lebesgue measure> is a <non-atomic measure> and $E$ has positive measure, so the <dual of L infinity is larger than L1>: the canonical integral representation does not exhaust $(L^\infty(E))^*$.

For $1<p<\infty$, the <Holder conjugate exponent> also lies strictly between one and infinity. <Duality of Lp spaces> then holds in both directions, in agreement with <reflexivity of Lp spaces>. For $p=\infty$, the first required identification already fails.

Let $p'$ be the <Holder conjugate exponent> of $p$, with $1/\infty=0$. The <indicator function> $\mathbf1_{(-h,h)}$ has <Lp norm> $(2h)^{1/p'}$ in $L^{p'}$, including the value one when $p'=\infty$. The <Holder inequality> therefore gives
$$
|T_hf|\leq h^{-1/p'}\|f\|_p\|\mathbf1_{(-h,h)}\|_{p'}
=2^{1/p'}\|f\|_p.
$$
The <Lebesgue integral> is unchanged by equality <almost everywhere>, so $T_h$ is well-defined on the <Lp space>; its <linearity> and the bound put it in the <continuous dual space> $X^*$.

For $p<\infty$, the <function> $f_h=(2h)^{-1/p}\mathbf1_{(-h,h)}$ has <Lp norm> one and satisfies
$$
T_hf_h=h^{-1/p'}(2h)^{1-1/p}=2^{1/p'}.
$$
For $p=\infty$, use $f_h=\mathbf1_{(-h,h)}$, whose <L-infinity norm> is one and whose image is $2$. Thus all endpoints are included in
$$
\boxed{T_h\in X^*,\qquad\|T_h\|=2^{1/p'}\quad(0<h<1).}
$$

Put $M=2^{1/p'}$, the common <operator norm> from part (b). Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ with $\|f-f_N\|_p<\varepsilon/(3M)$. Since $f_N\in Y$, the scalar limit of $T_hf_N$ exists. The <Cauchy criterion for a function limit> gives $\delta>0$ such that
$$
|T_hf_N-T_{\widetilde h}f_N|<\frac\varepsilon3
\qquad(0<h,\widetilde h<\delta).
$$
Using the <triangle inequality> and the uniform <operator norm> bound,
$$
\begin{aligned}
|T_hf-T_{\widetilde h}f|
&\leq|T_h(f-f_N)|+|T_hf_N-T_{\widetilde h}f_N|+|T_{\widetilde h}(f_N-f)|\\
&\leq2M\|f-f_N\|_p+|T_hf_N-T_{\widetilde h}f_N|<\varepsilon.
\end{aligned}
$$
This is the requested estimate. The <completeness> of $\mathbb R$ makes the scalar <Cauchy criterion for a function limit> sufficient, hence $\lim_{h\to0^+}T_hf$ exists and $f\in Y$.

For $u,v\in Y$ and real $a,b$, the <linearity> of $T_h$ gives $T_h(au+bv)=aT_hu+bT_hv$, which also has a limit. Thus $Y$ is a <linear subspace>. The preceding argument shows it contains every <norm convergence> limit of its <sequences>, so the sequential criterion for <closed sets> in a <metric space> gives
$$
\boxed{Y\text{ is a closed linear subspace of }X.}
$$

Use $D=C([-1,1])$, restricted to $(-1,1)$ and regarded as a <linear subspace> of $L^p((-1,1))$. The <density of continuous functions in Lp on an interval> says that $D$ is a <dense subspace> whenever $1\leq p<\infty$.

For $g\in D$, the <supremum norm> gives
$$
|T_hg|\leq h^{-1/p'}\int_{-h}^h|g(x)|\,dx
\leq2\|g\|_\infty h^{1-1/p'}=2\|g\|_\infty h^{1/p}\longrightarrow0.
$$
In particular $D\subseteq Y$, with zero limit. Part (c), together with $D$ being a <dense subspace>, implies $Y=X$. To identify the limit throughout $X$, for $f\in X$ and $g\in D$ use
$$
|T_hf|\leq M\|f-g\|_p+2\|g\|_\infty h^{1/p},\qquad M=2^{1/p'}.
$$
First choose $g$ in the <dense subspace> to make the first term arbitrarily small, then let $h\to0^+$. This is <convergence on a dense subspace under a uniform operator bound>, and proves
$$
\boxed{Tf=\lim_{h\to0^+}T_hf=0\quad(f\in L^p((-1,1))),\qquad\|T\|=0.}
$$
The zero <linear functional> belongs to the <continuous dual space> $X^*$.

Writing $\mathcal B(X)$ for the <Banach space of bounded linear operators> on $X$, the <spectrum> is
$$
\boxed{\sigma(T)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb C:\lambda I-T\text{ has no bounded two-sided inverse in }\mathcal B(X)\}.}
$$
Here $I$ is the <identity operator>. Since $X$ is a <Banach space>, the <bounded inverse theorem> makes this equivalent to failure of bijectivity of $\lambda I-T$.

The <spectral radius> is
$$
\boxed{r(T)=\sup\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\sigma(T)\}.}
$$
For a nonzero complex <Banach space>, the <spectrum> of a <bounded linear operator> is nonempty and <compact>, so the <supremum> is a maximum. If the zero space is allowed, its empty <spectrum> is assigned <spectral radius> zero.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <Neumann series> criterion states that $\|A\|<1$ for a <bounded linear operator> on a <Banach space> implies
$$
(I-A)^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A^n,
$$
with convergence in the <operator norm>. In particular, $\|T\|<1$ suffices for invertibility of $I-T$.

If $|\lambda|>\|T\|$, apply this criterion to $A=T/\lambda$. It gives
$$
(\lambda I-T)^{-1}=\lambda^{-1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(T/\lambda)^n.
$$
Thus no such $\lambda$ belongs to the <spectrum>, and
$$
\boxed{r(T)\leq\|T\|.}
$$

For each integer $j\geq1$, if $|\lambda|^j>\|T^j\|$, then $\lambda^jI-T^j$ is invertible by the same argument. The commuting factorization
$$
\lambda^jI-T^j=(\lambda I-T)\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\lambda^{j-1-k}T^k
$$
then gives a bounded two-sided inverse for $\lambda I-T$, namely the displayed sum multiplied by $(\lambda^jI-T^j)^{-1}$. Hence every $\lambda\in\sigma(T)$ satisfies $|\lambda|^j\leq\|T^j\|$. Taking the <supremum> over the <spectrum> and then the <infimum> over $j$ proves
$$
\boxed{r(T)\leq\inf_{j\geq1}\|T^j\|^{1/j}.}
$$

For a <continuous function> $g$ on $[0,1]$, the <L-infinity norm> of its restriction to $(0,1)$ equals its <supremum norm> on $[0,1]$. Indeed, values arbitrarily close to a maximum occur on an interval of positive <Lebesgue measure>, even if the maximum lies at an endpoint. Also, <continuous functions> equal <almost everywhere> on $(0,1)$ are equal everywhere on $[0,1]$. Thus restriction embeds $C([0,1])$ isometrically as a <linear subspace> of $X$.

The <point evaluation functional> $E_{x_0}(g)=g(x_0)$ is complex-linear and satisfies $|E_{x_0}(g)|\leq\|g\|_\infty$. The constant <function> one attains equality, so $\|E_{x_0}\|=1$. The <complex Hahn-Banach theorem> extends this <bounded linear functional> from that <linear subspace> to $X$, preserving its <operator norm>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\ell_{x_0}\in X^*,\qquad\|\ell_{x_0}\|=1,\qquad\ell_{x_0}(g)=g(x_0)\quad(g\in C([0,1])).}
$$

Write $\psi=\psi_{x_0}$; its value at $x_0$, if that point is in $(0,1)$, may be assigned arbitrarily because <Lp spaces> identify <functions> equal <almost everywhere>. Its <L-infinity norm> is one.

The supplied estimate shows that $\psi$ cannot agree <almost everywhere> with any <continuous function> $q$: choosing $g=-q$ would give $0\geq|q(x_0)|+1$. Hence
$$
V=C([0,1])\oplus\langle\psi\rangle
$$
is a <direct sum>, so every element has a unique expression $g+c\psi$.

For any complex $a$ with $|a|\leq1$, define the <linear functional>
$$
L_a(g+c\psi)=g(x_0)+ca.
$$
If $c\ne0$, the supplied estimate, applied to $g/c$, and <absolute homogeneity of a norm> give
$$
\|g+c\psi\|_\infty=|c|\|g/c+\psi\|_\infty
\geq|g(x_0)|+|c|
\geq|g(x_0)+ca|.
$$
For $c=0$, the same bound follows from <point evaluation>. Thus $\|L_a\|\leq1$, and $L_a(1)=1$ makes its <operator norm> exactly one. The <complex Hahn-Banach theorem> extends $L_a$ to a norm-one <linear functional> on all of $X$, with value $a$ at $\psi$.

Conversely every norm-one extension satisfies $|\ell_{x_0}(\psi)|\leq\|\psi\|_\infty=1$. We have therefore determined all possible values:
$$
\boxed{\{\ell_{x_0}(\psi_{x_0}):\ell_{x_0}\text{ is a norm-one extension of }E_{x_0}\}
=\{a\in\mathbb C:|a|\leq1\}.}
$$
In particular, taking $a=0$ and $a=1$ proves <nonuniqueness of norm-preserving point evaluation extensions>.

Because the <linear functionals> $\ell_0,\ell_1$ agree with endpoint <point evaluations> on <continuous functions>,

\boxed{\operatorname{Im}T=\langle e_0,e_1\rangle,\qquad\operatorname{rank}T=2.}

= Solution

Let $M=\|\,|e_0|+|e_1|\,\|_\infty$. Since both <linear functionals> have <operator norm> one, for every $f\in X$ the <triangle inequality> gives
$$
|(Tf)(x)|\leq|\ell_0(f)||e_0(x)|+|\ell_1(f)||e_1(x)|
\leq\|f\|_\infty\bigl(|e_0(x)|+|e_1(x)|\bigr).
$$
Taking the <essential supremum> proves $\|T\|\leq M$.

For the reverse bound, <continuity> and the <extreme value theorem> give $x_*\in[0,1]$ with $|e_0(x_*)|+|e_1(x_*)|=M$. Choose complex $a_0,a_1$ of modulus one so that $a_je_j(x_*)=|e_j(x_*)|$, taking $a_j=1$ if $e_j(x_*)=0$. The <continuous function>
$$
f_*(x)=(1-x)a_0+xa_1
$$
has <supremum norm> one by the <triangle inequality> and its endpoint values. Since $\ell_j(f_*)=a_j$,
$$
(Tf_*)(x_*)=a_0e_0(x_*)+a_1e_1(x_*)=M.
$$
The output is continuous, so its <L-infinity norm> equals its <supremum norm>, including when $x_*$ is an endpoint. Therefore $\|T\|\geq M$, and the exact <operator norm> is
$$
\boxed{\|T\|=\|\,|e_0|+|e_1|\,\|_\infty.}
$$
This is the <norm of a two-endpoint interpolation projection>.

= Solution

From $Te_0=e_0\ne0$, one is an <eigenvalue>. Zero is also an <eigenvalue>: the nonzero <continuous function> $q(x)=x(1-x)$ has both endpoint values zero, so $Tq=0$.

For $\lambda\notin\{0,1\}$, the <linear projection> identity $T^2=T$ gives an explicit bounded inverse:
$$
(\lambda I-T)^{-1}=\frac1\lambda(I-T)+\frac1{\lambda-1}T.
$$
Multiplying on either side by $\lambda I-T$ yields $(I-T)+T=I$. There are no other points in the <spectrum>, and thus the <spectrum of a nontrivial bounded projection> here is
$$
\boxed{\sigma(T)=\{0,1\},\qquad r(T)=1.}
$$

Let $M=\|T\|$, as calculated in part (ii). Its endpoint values ensure $1\leq M<\infty$. Since $T^j=T$ for all $j\geq1$,
$$
\|T^j\|^{1/j}=M^{1/j}\longrightarrow1,
\qquad\inf_{j\geq1}\|T^j\|^{1/j}=1.
$$
This verifies the <spectral radius formula> explicitly:
$$
\boxed{r(T)=\inf_{j\geq1}\|T^j\|^{1/j}=\lim_{j\to\infty}\|T^j\|^{1/j}=1.}
$$
