= Solution
{parent=1}

A real square <orthogonal matrix> satisfies $Q^TQ=QQ^T=I$, so it preserves the <Euclidean norm>. The columns of $Z_h$ are orthonormal, hence $\|Z_hv\|_2=\|v\|_2$. The <induced matrix norm> therefore gives
$$
\|AZ_h\|_2=\sup_{\|v\|_2=1}\|AZ_hv\|_2\leq\boxed{\|A\|_2}.
$$
For the left truncation, $\|Q_\ell^TA\|_2\leq\|A\|_2$, and equality is guaranteed for arbitrary $A$ only when $\boxed{\ell=m}$. Indeed, if $\ell<m$, take $A=q_{\ell+1}e_1^T$, where $q_{\ell+1}$ is an omitted column of $Q$. Then $\|A\|_2=1$ but $Q_\ell^TA=0$. Merely taking $\ell\geq n$ does not guarantee preservation for an arbitrary $A$.

A full <QR decomposition> of a real $m\times n$ matrix is $A=QR$, with $Q$ orthogonal and $R$ upper trapezoidal; for a square matrix, $R$ is upper triangular. Fix the following sign convention for a <Givens rotation>: it is the identity except for its $(i,k)$ block
$$
\begin{pmatrix}c&s\\-s&c\end{pmatrix},\qquad c=\cos\theta,\ s=\sin\theta.
$$
The block transpose times the block is $I_2$ because $c^2+s^2=1$, proving orthogonality. Writing $r=\sqrt{x_i^2+x_k^2}$, the choice $\boxed{c=x_i/r,\ s=x_k/r}$ gives $(Jx)_k=0$ and $(Jx)_i=r$. To zero the other coordinate take $\boxed{c=x_k/r,\ s=-x_i/r}$, giving $(Jx)_i=0$. If $r=0$, both entries are already zero and the identity suffices.

For a <tridiagonal matrix>, successively rotate adjacent row pairs $(1,2),(2,3),\ldots,(n-1,n)$, each time zeroing the current entry $(k+1,k)$. Earlier columns remain triangular because both affected rows are already zero there. The result $R=J_{n-1}\cdots J_1A$ is upper triangular, so $Q=J_1^T\cdots J_{n-1}^T$ gives its <QR decomposition>. Only $n-1$ rotations are required; the resulting $R$ has at most two superdiagonals.

For a full square matrix, process columns $j=1,\ldots,n-1$. Within column $j$, for $i=n,n-1,\ldots,j+1$, rotate rows $(i-1,i)$ to eliminate the current $(i,j)$ entry. Previously cleared columns stay clear. With $J_1,\ldots,J_s$ in application order, $s=n(n-1)/2$, one has $\boxed{R=J_s\cdots J_1A,\quad Q=J_1^T\cdots J_s^T}$. This is <QR decomposition by Givens rotations>.

= Solution

The <Gauss-Seidel method> updates coordinates successively using the newest available entries:
$$
\boxed{x_i^{(k+1)}=\frac{b_i-\sum_{j<i}a_{ij}x_j^{(k+1)}-\sum_{j>i}a_{ij}x_j^{(k)}}{a_{ii}}.}
$$
It is well-defined for every initial vector exactly when every $a_{ii}\ne0$, equivalently when the triangular matrix $D+L$ is invertible. Nonsingularity of $A$ alone does not ensure this. The matrix form is
$$
\boxed{(D+L)x^{(k+1)}=b-Ux^{(k)}.}
$$
If the iterates converge to $z$, passing to the limit gives $(D+L)z=b-Uz$, hence $Az=b$. Nonsingularity identifies $z$ as the unique solution $x$.

Now assume $A$ is a <symmetric positive-definite matrix>. Testing with the coordinate vector gives $a_{ii}=e_i^TAe_i>0$, so $D$ is positive definite. The form $\langle v,w\rangle_A=v^TAw$ is an <inner product>: bilinearity and symmetry are immediate and positivity is the hypothesis. Its <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives
$$
\|v+w\|_A^2\leq\|v\|_A^2+2\|v\|_A\|w\|_A+\|w\|_A^2,
$$
proving the triangle inequality; definiteness and absolute homogeneity are immediate. Thus $\|v\|_A=\sqrt{v^TAv}$ is the <energy norm>.

Subtract the iteration from the exact equation to obtain $(D+L)e_{k+1}=-L^Te_k$. Hence
$$
Ae_k=(D+L)(e_k-e_{k+1}).
$$
Using the preceding iteration also gives $(D+L)e_k=-L^Te_{k-1}$ and therefore $Ae_k=L^T(e_k-e_{k-1})$ for $k\geq1$, as required.

Put $d=e_k-e_{k+1}$. Expanding the two quadratic forms and using the first identity gives the exact <Gauss-Seidel energy decrease identity>:
$$
\boxed{\|e_k\|_A^2-\|e_{k+1}\|_A^2=2d^TAe_k-d^TAd=d^TDd.}
$$
Here $2d^TLd=d^T(L+L^T)d$. If $d=0$, then $Ae_k=0$, so $e_k=0$. Otherwise $d^TDd>0$. Thus $\boxed{\|e_k\|_A>\|e_{k+1}\|_A}$ unless the exact solution has already been reached.

= Solution
{parent=3}

The <Krylov subspace> is $\boxed{K_k(A,r)=\operatorname{span}\{r,Ar,\ldots,A^{k-1}r\}}$. If $y-z=q(A)r$ for a polynomial $q$ of degree at most $k-1$, then
$$
b-Ay=r-Aq(A)r=p(A)r,\qquad p(t)=1-tq(t),
$$
so $\deg p\leq k$ and $p(0)=1$. Conversely, this residual identity implies
$$
A\bigl(y-z-q(A)r\bigr)=0,\qquad q(t)=\frac{1-p(t)}t\in\Pi_{k-1}.
$$
Thus the converse holds whenever $A$ is invertible. More precisely, for fixed $A,r,k$ it holds for every such $y$ exactly when $\ker A\subseteq K_k(A,r)$: the identity determines $y-z$ only up to an arbitrary <nullspace> vector. Without this qualification the singular-matrix converse is false, for example when $A=0$, $r=e_1$ and $y-z=e_2$.

Assume $r\ne0$ and exact arithmetic for the <Lanczos algorithm>. At step $j$ put
$$
w=Av_j-\delta_jv_j-\gamma_jv_{j-1},\quad\delta_j=v_j^TAv_j,\quad\gamma_{j+1}=\|w\|_2,\quad v_{j+1}=w/\gamma_{j+1},
$$
provided $w\ne0$. The initial coefficient of $v_0=0$ is immaterial. Inductively suppose $v_1,\ldots,v_j$ are orthonormal. Then $v_j^Tw=0$ by the choice of $\delta_j$. For $j>1$, symmetry and the preceding recurrence give $v_{j-1}^TAv_j=\gamma_j$, so $v_{j-1}^Tw=0$. If $i\leq j-2$, the recurrence gives $Av_i\in\operatorname{span}(v_1,\ldots,v_{i+1})$, and therefore $v_i^TAv_j=(Av_i)^Tv_j=0$. Hence $w$ is perpendicular to every preceding basis vector. Its normalization supplies the next orthonormal vector.

Every new $v_{j+1}$ belongs to $K_{j+1}(A,r)$. The $j+1$ orthonormal vectors are independent, whereas that <Krylov subspace> has dimension at most $j+1$; they therefore form its basis. Continuing the recurrence one more step gives
$$
\boxed{AV_k=V_kT_k+\gamma_{k+1}v_{k+1}e_k^T,\quad
T_k=\begin{pmatrix}
\delta_1&\gamma_2&&\\
\gamma_2&\delta_2&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\gamma_k\\
&&\gamma_k&\delta_k
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
In particular $T_k=V_k^TAV_k$. If the next residual is zero, the last term is zero and the span is an invariant subspace; no undefined normalized vector is needed. If $v_1$ is an <eigenvector>, $Av_1=\delta_1v_1$, so $\gamma_2=0$ immediately. The algorithm terminates with the exact one-dimensional invariant subspace, rather than generating further vectors. If $r=0$, the initial residual has already vanished and the normalization defining $v_1$ is unnecessary.

= Solution
{parent=a}

A <descent direction> $s$ satisfies $g^Ts<0$, where $g=\nabla f(x)$; the first-order <Taylor expansion> then gives $f(x+\alpha s)<f(x)$ for sufficiently small $\alpha>0$. To compare directions one must fix their scale. Among Euclidean unit vectors, the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives
$$
g^Td\geq-\|g\|_2,\qquad\boxed{d=-g/\|g\|_2}\quad(g\ne0).
$$
This is the unit <steepest descent direction>. The conventional unnormalized direction used in the algorithm is the same ray, $\boxed{s=-g}$. Equivalently it is the unique minimizer of $g^Ts+\tfrac12\|s\|_2^2=\tfrac12\|s+g\|_2^2-\tfrac12\|g\|_2^2$. If $g=0$ there is no strict first-order <descent direction>, and the conventional update is zero.

= b
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

A <line search> chooses a positive <step size> giving useful decrease along the chosen direction. Fix $\sigma,\beta\in(0,1)$ and a trial step $\bar\alpha>0$. The <Armijo condition> is
$$
\boxed{f(x+\alpha s)\leq f(x)+\sigma\alpha\nabla f(x)^Ts.}
$$
The <backtracking line search> tests $\bar\alpha,\beta\bar\alpha,\beta^2\bar\alpha,\ldots$ and takes the first accepted value. A <Lipschitz gradient> has $\|\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)\|_2\leq L\|y-x\|_2$ with a constant $L$ independent of $x,y$.

Integrating the <gradient> along a segment gives the <descent lemma>
$$
f(x+\alpha s)\leq f(x)+\alpha g^Ts+\frac L2\alpha^2\|s\|_2^2.
$$
For $s=-g$, every $0<\alpha\leq\tau=2(1-\sigma)/L$ satisfies the <Armijo condition>. Thus backtracking terminates. If the trial step is accepted, $\alpha=\bar\alpha$; otherwise the immediately preceding step $\alpha/\beta$ failed, so it exceeded $\tau$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\alpha\geq c=\min\{\bar\alpha,\,2\beta(1-\sigma)/L\}>0.}
$$
The same global Lipschitz constant and fixed search parameters make $c$ independent of $x$. If $g=0$, the zero direction passes the first test. If $L=0$, the gradient is constant and the first test passes, so take $c=\bar\alpha$.

= c
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Choose $x_0$, fixed $\bar\alpha>0$, $\sigma,\beta\in(0,1)$, and repeat for every $n\geq0$: compute $g_n=\nabla f(x_n)$, choose $\alpha_n$ by the <backtracking line search> in part (b) with $s_n=-g_n$, and set $x_{n+1}=x_n-\alpha_ng_n$. At a stationary point keep the sequence constant.

Assume $f\in C^1$ has a globally <Lipschitz gradient> and is bounded below on its initial <sublevel set> $\{x:f(x)\leq f(x_0)\}$. Then the <steepest descent method> satisfies $\boxed{\nabla f(x_n)\to0}$. Indeed, the <Armijo condition> and the uniform lower step bound give
$$
f(x_n)-f(x_{n+1})\geq\sigma\alpha_n\|g_n\|_2^2\geq\sigma c\|g_n\|_2^2.
$$
The objective values decrease and stay in the stated sublevel set. Summing the inequality and using its lower bound $f_{\inf}$ yields
$$
\sum_{n=0}^\infty\|g_n\|_2^2\leq\frac{f(x_0)-f_{\inf}}{\sigma c}<\infty,
$$
which proves the limit. Every cluster point is a <stationary point> by continuity of the <gradient>. If the initial sublevel set is compact, cluster points exist. This global convergence result guarantees stationarity from arbitrary initial points under these assumptions; it does not, for a general nonconvex objective, guarantee convergence of the whole iterate sequence or a global minimum.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For any vectors $a,b$,
$$
|a^Tb|\leq\sum_j|a_j||b_j|\leq\|a\|_\infty\sum_j|b_j|=\boxed{\|a\|_\infty\|b\|_1}.
$$
Thus, among directions with $\|d\|_\infty\leq1$, $g^Td\geq-\|g\|_1$. Equality is achieved by
$$
\boxed{d_j=-\operatorname{sgn}(g_j)\quad(g_j\ne0),\qquad d_j\in[-1,1]\quad(g_j=0).}
$$
For $g\ne0$ these minimizing directions have infinity norm one. The strongest decrease per unit infinity-norm step is $-\|g\|_1$, illustrating the <dual norm> pairing between the infinity norm and the one-norm.

To match the unnormalized convention in part (a), minimize $g^Ts+\tfrac12\|s\|_\infty^2$. At fixed $t=\|s\|_\infty$, its minimum is $-t\|g\|_1+\tfrac12t^2$, attained at $t=\|g\|_1$. One conventional choice is therefore $\boxed{s=-\|g\|_1\operatorname{sgn}(g)}$, with zero entries where $g_j=0$. Its ray agrees with the unit direction just computed.

= Solution
{parent=a}

A local minimizer of a <differentiable function> on $\mathbb R^N$ necessarily satisfies $\boxed{\nabla f(x_*)=0}$. A sufficient condition for a strict local minimum is $\boxed{\nabla f(x_*)=0,\quad\nabla^2f(x_*)\text{ positive definite}}$. Indeed, the second-order <Taylor expansion> has a strictly positive quadratic term that dominates its $o(\|x-x_*\|^2)$ remainder near $x_*$.

For <Newton's method>, solve $H_ns_n=-g_n$ with $H_n=\nabla^2f(x_n)$, $g_n=\nabla f(x_n)$, and set $x_{n+1}=x_n+s_n$. A positive-definite Hessian makes this a <descent direction>; general Hessians may need modification or a separate <line search> for a globally useful method.

The sequence is q-quadratically convergent if there is $C<\infty$ such that $\|x_{n+1}-x_*\|\leq C\|x_n-x_*\|^2$ eventually. Sufficient local hypotheses are $\nabla f(x_*)=0$, a nonsingular $H_*=\nabla^2f(x_*)$, a <Lipschitz continuous> Hessian near $x_*$, and $x_0$ sufficiently close. For convergence to a local minimum take $H_*$ positive definite. Near $x_*$, inverse Hessians are uniformly bounded, and integration along the error segment gives
$$
x_{n+1}-x_*=H_n^{-1}\int_0^1[H_n-\nabla^2f(x_*+t(x_n-x_*))](x_n-x_*)\,dt.
$$
A Hessian Lipschitz constant $L_H$ yields $\|x_{n+1}-x_*\|\leq\tfrac12L_H\|H_n^{-1}\|\|x_n-x_*\|^2$. Choosing a sufficiently small invariant neighborhood proves <quadratic convergence>. Continuous second derivatives alone do not guarantee this quadratic rate; the Lipschitz hypothesis supplies the needed remainder bound.

{parent=5}

= Solution
{parent=b}

A <quasi-Newton method> replaces the Hessian in the Newton system by an efficiently updated approximation $B_n$:
$$
\boxed{B_ns_n=-\nabla f(x_n),\qquad x_{n+1}=x_n+s_n.}
$$
The matrices must be nonsingular for the step to exist. Symmetry reflects the true Hessian, and positive definiteness makes every nonzero step a <descent direction>, since $\nabla f(x_n)^Ts_n=-s_n^TB_ns_n<0$. Secant updates often enforce the <quasi-Newton secant equation> $B_{n+1}s_n=\nabla f(x_{n+1})-\nabla f(x_n)$.

Q-<superlinear convergence> means $\|x_{n+1}-x_*\|/\|x_n-x_*\|\to0$, unless the sequence terminates exactly. A sufficient <Dennis-Moré condition> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{\|(B_n-H_*)s_n\|}{\|s_n\|}\to0,\qquad H_*=\nabla^2f(x_*),}
$$
assuming $x_n\to x_*$, $\nabla f(x_*)=0$, continuous Hessian near $x_*$ and nonsingular $H_*$. Only accuracy on the actual step direction is required; full matrix convergence $B_n\to H_*$ is sufficient but stronger.

To prove the assertion, continuity of the Hessian along the short segment from $x_n$ to $x_{n+1}$ gives
$$
\nabla f(x_{n+1})=\nabla f(x_n)+H_*s_n+o(\|s_n\|)=-(B_n-H_*)s_n+o(\|s_n\|)=o(\|s_n\|).
$$
Nonsingularity of $H_*$ and $\nabla f(x_*+e)=H_*e+o(\|e\|)$ give a constant $C$ with $\|e\|\leq C\|\nabla f(x_*+e)\|$ near zero. Writing $e_n=x_n-x_*$, we obtain
$$
\|e_{n+1}\|\leq\varepsilon_n\|s_n\|\leq\varepsilon_n(\|e_n\|+\|e_{n+1}\|),\qquad\varepsilon_n\to0.
$$
Thus $\|e_{n+1}\|/\|e_n\|\leq\varepsilon_n/(1-\varepsilon_n)\to0$. This proves sufficiency. It is a local rate result for an already convergent sequence, not a claim that arbitrary unmodified quasi-Newton iterations converge from every starting point.

{parent=5}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Put $J(x)=DR(x)$, with one residual gradient per row. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\boxed{\nabla f(x)=\sum_jR^{(j)}(x)\nabla R^{(j)}(x)=J(x)^TR(x).}
$$
Differentiating each product gives
$$
\boxed{\nabla^2f(x)=J(x)^TJ(x)+\sum_jR^{(j)}(x)\nabla^2R^{(j)}(x).}
$$
The approximation $B_n=J(x_n)^TJ(x_n)$ defines the <Gauss-Newton method>. It is symmetric, and full column rank gives $v^TB_nv=\|J(x_n)v\|_2^2>0$ for $v\ne0$, so the step is well-defined.

If $x_n\to x_*$, $R(x_*)=0$ and $J(x_*)$ has full column rank, then $\nabla f(x_*)=0$ and $H_*=\nabla^2f(x_*)=J(x_*)^TJ(x_*)$ is positive definite. Continuity of $J$ gives $B_n\to H_*$. Therefore
$$
\frac{\|(B_n-H_*)s_n\|}{\|s_n\|}\leq\|B_n-H_*\|\to0.
$$
The <Dennis-Moré condition> applies, proving $\boxed{x_n\to x_*\text{ q-superlinearly}}$, or finite termination. The zero-residual hypothesis is what makes the discarded second-derivative term vanish at the limit.

The <contingent tangent cone> consists of feasible first-order velocities:
$$
\boxed{T_\Omega(x)=\{d:\exists t_n\downarrow0,\ x_n\in\Omega,\ (x_n-x)/t_n\to d\}.}
$$
If $x_*$ is a local minimizer, then $\boxed{\nabla f(x_*)^Td\geq0\text{ for every }d\in T_\Omega(x_*)}$. Indeed, $x_n\to x_*$ along the defining sequence, so eventually $f(x_n)-f(x_*)\geq0$. Differentiability gives
$$
\frac{f(x_n)-f(x_*)}{t_n}=\nabla f(x_*)^T\frac{x_n-x_*}{t_n}+o(1)\longrightarrow\nabla f(x_*)^Td.
$$
This proves the inequality. Using feasible limiting velocities avoids assuming that every tangent direction is realized by a straight segment staying in the feasible set.

{parent=6}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <active constraint set> is $\boxed{\mathcal A(x)=\{j:c_j(x)=0\}}$, and the <linearized tangent cone> is
$$
\boxed{F(x)=\{d:\nabla c_j(x)^Td\geq0\text{ for }j\in\mathcal A(x)\}.}
$$
Differentiating each active feasibility inequality along a tangent sequence gives $T_\Omega(x)\subseteq F(x)$. A sufficient condition for equality is the <linear independence constraint qualification>: the active constraint gradients are linearly independent. The weaker <Mangasarian-Fromovitz constraint qualification> also suffices in this inequality-only setting.

Under the stated independence condition, the necessary first-order conditions become the <Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions>: there are multipliers $\lambda_j$ with
$$
\boxed{\nabla f(x_*)-\sum_j\lambda_j\nabla c_j(x_*)=0,\quad c_j(x_*)\geq0,\quad\lambda_j\geq0,\quad\lambda_jc_j(x_*)=0.}
$$
Use the allowed span assertion to write $\nabla f(x_*)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal A}\lambda_j\nabla c_j(x_*)$. Independence gives vectors $\varphi_i$ with $\nabla c_j(x_*)^T\varphi_i=\delta_{ji}$ for the active indices. Each $\varphi_i$ lies in $F=T_\Omega$, so part (a) gives $\lambda_i=\nabla f(x_*)^T\varphi_i\geq0$. Assign zero multipliers to inactive constraints. This proves all displayed conditions, including complementarity. Conversely, their stationarity and sign conditions imply $\nabla f(x_*)^Td=\sum_{j\in\mathcal A}\lambda_j\nabla c_j(x_*)^Td\geq0$ for every $d\in F$. Thus they reformulate the first-order inequality; they do not alone prove a minimum for a nonconvex problem.

= Solution
{parent=c}

On the strictly feasible set $\Omega^\circ_c=\{x:c_j(x)>0\text{ for all }j\}$, use the <logarithmic barrier>
$$
\boxed{\Phi_\mu(x)=f(x)-\mu\sum_{j=1}^M\log c_j(x),\qquad\mu>0.}
$$
Choose a strictly feasible starting point and parameters $\mu_n\downarrow0$. For each parameter minimize the barrier functional by an unconstrained method confined to $\Omega^\circ_c$, warm-starting from the preceding point. In the exact version take a stationary point $x_n$ of the barrier subproblem. The stationarity equation is
$$
\nabla f(x_n)-\sum_j\lambda_{n,j}\nabla c_j(x_n)=0,\qquad\boxed{\lambda_{n,j}=\mu_n/c_j(x_n)>0,\quad\lambda_{n,j}c_j(x_n)=\mu_n.}
$$
These are the natural <Lagrange multiplier> estimates; the basic method need not perform a separate primal-dual correction.

The <barrier method KKT convergence theorem> is a global stationarity result: if the exact barrier stationary points form a bounded sequence, then every cluster point satisfying the <linear independence constraint qualification> is a <KKT point>. To prove it, take any subsequence $x_n\to x_*$. Continuity gives feasibility of $x_*$. For an inactive index, $c_j(x_*)>0$ and therefore $\lambda_{n,j}=\mu_n/c_j(x_n)\to0$.

The active gradients at $x_*$ are independent. The supplied dual-basis result gives bounded vectors $\varphi_{n,i}$, converging as $n\to\infty$, with $\nabla c_j(x_n)^T\varphi_{n,i}=\delta_{ji}$ for active $i,j$. Pair stationarity with these vectors:
$$
\lambda_{n,i}=\varphi_{n,i}^T\left(\nabla f(x_n)-\sum_{j\notin\mathcal A(x_*)}\lambda_{n,j}\nabla c_j(x_n)\right).
$$
Thus all active multipliers are bounded as well. Extract a convergent multiplier subsequence. Its limit is nonnegative, satisfies stationarity by continuity, and satisfies complementarity because $\lambda_{n,j}c_j(x_n)=\mu_n\to0$. This proves the claimed KKT conclusion. The same proof permits approximate barrier stationary points whose stationarity residual tends to zero. Bounded iterates and the constraint qualification are substantive hypotheses, not automatic consequences of writing a barrier functional.

For a global-minimum conclusion one needs global minimization of the barrier subproblems and appropriate access to every feasible point. Specifically, suppose $x_n$ globally minimizes $\Phi_{\mu_n}$, has a cluster point $x_*$, and $\overline{\Omega^\circ_c}=\Omega$. For any fixed strictly feasible $z$,
$$
f(x_n)\leq f(z)+\mu_n\sum_j\bigl(\log c_j(x_n)-\log c_j(z)\bigr).
$$
Along the convergent subsequence the $c_j(x_n)$ are bounded above, so taking the upper limit gives $f(x_*)\leq f(z)$. Density of strictly feasible points and continuity of $f$ extend the inequality to every feasible $z$. Hence $x_*$ is a global minimizer under these stronger hypotheses.
