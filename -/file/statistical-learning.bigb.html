= Statistical learning

Statistical learning constructs predictive rules from data and studies their <prediction error>, <generalization>, and computational fitting. In <supervised learning>, <training data> contain inputs $X$ and a <response variable> $Y$: <regression analysis> predicts a numerical response, while <classification in statistical learning> predicts a label. <Unsupervised learning> instead seeks structure without supplied responses, as in <cluster analysis> and <principal component analysis>. These tasks connect learning to <probability> and <mathematical optimization>. https://web.stanford.edu/~hastie/ISLR2/Slides/Ch2_Statistical_Learning.pdf[James, Witten, Hastie and Tibshirani, ISLR chapter 2 slides]{ref}

For a fitted rule $\widehat f$ and <loss function> $L$, the predictive goal is
$$
R(\widehat f)=\mathbb E\bigl[L(Y^*,\widehat f(X^*))\mid\mathcal D_n\bigr],
$$
where $(X^*,Y^*)$ is a fresh observation, <independent> of the <training data> $\mathcal D_n$, from the intended target <probability distribution>. This <prediction error> fixes the fitted rule and averages over future observations; averaging again over <training data> gives the learning procedure's average performance. Its practical beauty is that a rule compresses past observations into useful future predictions. Its central difficulty is that excellent <training error> need not imply good <generalization>: <cross-validation> supports <model selection>, and a reserved <test set> assesses the resulting choice.

= Dimensionality reduction
{parent=Statistical learning}

Representing data using fewer coordinates. A method must specify which properties it tries to preserve, such as reconstruction, local distances, prediction or temporal structure. Preserving most total <variance> need not preserve the information required by a task.

= Stochastic approximation
{parent=Statistical learning}

Estimating a solution of a deterministic equation through repeated noisy samples of its residual or update direction. Temporal-difference policy evaluation is an example. Convergence depends on stability, sampling, noise and step sizes, rather than merely on the existence of an unbiased sample.

= Stochastic-approximation step-size conditions
{parent=Stochastic approximation}
{title2=$\sum_n\alpha_n=\infty,\quad\sum_n\alpha_n^2<\infty$}

A common condition along each repeatedly sampled component is $\sum_n\alpha_n=\infty$ and $\sum_n\alpha_n^2<\infty$. The first permits continuing correction; the second limits accumulated noise under the relevant stochastic-approximation hypotheses. The example $\alpha_n=1/n$ has a divergent harmonic sum and a convergent sum of squares. These conditions are not sufficient without suitable assumptions about the update dynamics and sampling.

= Self-supervised learning
{parent=Statistical learning}

Learning from targets or constraints derived from the data or their transformations rather than externally supplied semantic labels. The supervision source and permitted transformations are part of the hypothesis. <Contrastive representation learning> is one family of such methods.

= Contrastive representation learning
{parent=Self-supervised learning}

Learning a representation by relating selected matching examples or views and contrasting them with other examples. The augmentation, matching and sampling rules determine which distinctions are retained. Brain-like prediction in a trained model does not establish the exact objective or plasticity mechanism in cortex.<Unsupervised neural network models of the ventral visual stream by Chengxu Zhuang and others>[Abstract and Results; PNAS 118, e2014196118: contrastive learning, multi-area prediction and child-view <training data>]{ref}

= Noise ceiling
{parent=Statistical learning}

An estimate of the agreement achievable with a measured dataset given its reliability, variation across observations or participants, and the chosen comparison metric. Reaching it does not mean that a model captures every biological property; a more informative dataset can expose differences that the original data could not resolve.

= Stability-plasticity dilemma
{parent=Statistical learning}

The need to adapt to new evidence while preserving useful information from prior experience. Freezing everything prevents learning; changing shared representations without appropriate constraints can damage retention. <Complementary learning systems> and replay address aspects of this trade-off.

= Catastrophic interference
{parent=Statistical learning}

= Catastrophic forgetting
{synonym}

A severe loss of previously learned performance when updating a shared representation on new material. Overlapping parameters allow transfer but also let a new objective change computations needed for older examples. Interleaving and representation design can reduce the problem; the mere presence of a <neural network> does not imply inevitable catastrophic forgetting.

= Inductive bias
{parent=Statistical learning}

= Inductive biases
{synonym}

Constraints or preferences that guide <generalization> beyond observed training examples. Local <convolution>, <weight sharing>, a compositional grammar and a prior over programs supply different inductive biases. Their usefulness depends on the structure of the target problem.

= Reinforcement learning
{parent=Statistical learning}

Learning to choose actions from their consequences in an environment, with the objective of increasing expected reward or return. Reward does not normally specify a correct action label. State representation, exploration and temporal credit assignment are central computational requirements.

= Distributional reinforcement learning
{parent=Reinforcement learning}

<Learning> about a distribution of possible future returns rather than only its mean. Multiple prediction channels can vary in optimism and sensitivity to positive and negative errors. Such population heterogeneity can refine a <reward prediction error> account without implying that every <dopamine> signal performs the same computation.

= Experience replay
{parent=Reinforcement learning}

Using stored or regenerated experience for further learning, often interleaved with new observations. It can improve data reuse and retention, but its sampling distribution and fidelity affect what objective is learned. A software replay buffer is not a complete biological model of <hippocampal> reactivation.

= Model-based reinforcement learning
{parent=Reinforcement learning}

Learning or using a model of transitions and outcomes to evaluate possible actions. It can support flexible replanning when the value of an outcome changes, but depends on the learned model and computational resources. Behavioural experiments must distinguish it from alternative strategies with similar observable predictions.

= Model-free reinforcement learning
{parent=Reinforcement learning}

Learning cached values or action preferences without explicitly planning through a model of state transitions and outcomes. A basic <actor-critic> is an example. The absence of a transition model can limit rapid revaluation of an unvisited action after its outcome changes.

= Temporal-difference learning
{parent=Reinforcement learning}
{title2=$\delta_t=r_{t+1}+\gamma V(s_{t+1})-V(s_t)$}

A value prediction is updated using a reward and a subsequent prediction: $\delta_t=r_{t+1}+\gamma V(s_{t+1})-V(s_t)$. The linear semi-gradient rule is $\Delta w=\alpha\delta_t x(s_t)$ for $V_w(s)=w^{\mathsf T}x(s)$; the target is held fixed for this update. Terminal-state value is zero in an episodic task.<Learning to predict by the methods of temporal differences by Richard S. Sutton>[Abstract and sections 2–3; Machine Learning 3, pp. 9–44: successive predictions and temporal credit assignment]{ref}

= Tabular temporal-difference policy evaluation
{parent=Temporal-difference learning}
{title2=$V(S_t)\leftarrow V(S_t)+\alpha_t\delta_t$}

A sampled transition updates only the visited value: $V(S_t)\leftarrow V(S_t)+\alpha_t\delta_t$, where $\delta_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma V(S_{t+1})-V(S_t)$. Under the fixed policy, its conditional mean is $(T^\pi V)(s)-V(s)$. Standard tabular convergence requires adequate state visitation and step-size/noise conditions; this mean identity alone does not prove convergence for arbitrary function approximation.

= Reward prediction error
{parent=Temporal-difference learning}

A discrepancy between obtained or newly predicted reward and the reward previously expected. In a temporal-difference model it includes the next-state prediction, rather than being simply the immediate reward. A positive value signals a better-than-predicted outcome; a negative value signals a worse-than-predicted one.

= Actor-critic architecture
{parent=Reinforcement learning}

= Actor-critic
{synonym}

An actor learns an action-selection policy while a critic learns a value prediction used to evaluate outcomes. A temporal-difference error can update both. With an accurate on-policy state-value function, its conditional mean given a state and action is that action’s advantage, supplying a useful policy-learning direction without a labelled correct action.

= Multivariate pattern analysis
{parent=Statistical learning}

= MVPA
{c}
{synonym}

Analysis using patterns across multiple measured features rather than a single averaged response. In neuroimaging, classification or related methods can distinguish remembered content from spatial activity patterns. Independent testing and control of nuisance structure are essential to interpreting the result.

= Cross-modal decoding
{parent=Multivariate pattern analysis}

Training a statistical predictor on one modality and evaluating it on another to test information that generalises across input or output forms. Stimulus identity, response mapping, noise and held-out evaluation affect what a successful or failed result means.

= Representational similarity analysis
{parent=Multivariate pattern analysis}

Comparison of the pairwise relationships among measured or model response patterns. The same stimuli are presented to the systems being compared, and their representational dissimilarity matrices are related. A match can constrain representation without requiring one-to-one unit correspondence or establishing the underlying learning rule.

= Representational dissimilarity matrix
{parent=Representational similarity analysis}
{title2=$D_{ij}=1-\operatorname{corr}(z_i,z_j)$}

= RDM
{c}
{synonym}

A <matrix> whose entry $D_{ij}$ describes how differently a system responds to stimuli $i$ and $j$. One convention is $D_{ij}=1-\operatorname{corr}(z_i,z_j)$ when the response <vectors> have nonzero variance. Such a dissimilarity need not satisfy every metric axiom. Model comparison must control fitted transformations and use held-out data.

= Online learning
{parent=Statistical learning}

Learning through a sequence of decisions and observations, assessing performance by comparison with a benchmark decision or rule. A <multi-armed bandit> is a model in which only the reward of the selected action is observed.

= Optimism in the face of uncertainty
{parent=Online learning}

A decision principle that maximizes possible reward jointly over an action and a parameter in a <confidence set>. On the event that the set contains the true parameter, the reward predicted for this selected pair is at least the true optimal reward. This inequality bounds <pseudoregret> by the width of the <confidence set> in the selected action's direction. For an $L$-<Lipschitz continuous> reward link and a radius-$\beta$ <confidence ellipsoid>, <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> and the <triangle inequality> bound that width by $2L\beta\sqrt{x^TV^{-1}x}$.

= Multi-armed bandit
{parent=Online learning}

A sequential decision model in which each round selects an action and observes its reward, balancing information gathering against immediate reward. For fixed action means $m(x)$, the <pseudoregret> compares selected actions with an action maximizing $m$. In models with a <link function> and parameter $\theta$, rewards may have means $m_\theta(x)=\mu(\langle x,\theta\rangle)$.

= Elliptical potential bound
{parent=Multi-armed bandit}
{title2=$\sum_{t=1}^n x_t^TV_{t-1}^{-1}x_t\leq2\log(\det V_n/\det V_0)$}

Let $V_t=V_{t-1}+x_tx_t^T$ with <positive-definite matrix> $V_0$, and suppose $u_t=x_t^TV_{t-1}^{-1}x_t\leq1$. The <matrix determinant lemma> gives $\det V_t/\det V_{t-1}=1+u_t$. Since $2\log(1+u)-u$ has nonnegative derivative $(1-u)/(1+u)$ for $0\leq u\leq1$, summation proves the displayed bound. In particular, $V_0=\lambda I$, $\lambda\geq1$, and $\|x_t\|_2\leq1$ ensure $u_t\leq1$. Without that restriction a general form is $\sum_t\min\{1,u_t\}\leq2\log(\det V_n/\det V_0)$: for $u\geq1$, $2\log(1+u)\geq2\log2\geq1$. The bound connects widths of <confidence ellipsoids> to growth of a <determinant>.

= Pseudoregret
{parent=Multi-armed bandit}
{title2=$R_n=\sum_{t=1}^n[m(x_\star)-m(X_t)]$}

The cumulative difference between the mean reward of an optimal fixed action and the mean rewards of the selected actions. For adaptive random actions, this sum is itself a <random variable>; taking its <expected value> gives expected pseudoregret. Observation noise is absent from the sum, although earlier noise can influence later actions.

= Feature interaction
{parent=Statistical learning}

A predictive dependence in which the effect of one input feature changes with another. A product term is a simple example that cannot be represented by a purely additive linear predictor.

= Multilabel classification
{parent=Statistical learning}

Prediction of several labels that can occur together. Independent sigmoid outputs do not impose the sum-to-one constraint used for mutually exclusive classes.

= Multiclass classification
{parent=Statistical learning}

Classification with more than two mutually exclusive outcome classes. A softmax output can represent a normalized distribution over the classes.

= One-hot encoding
{parent=Statistical learning}

Representation of one categorical outcome by a vector with one component equal to one and all other components zero.

A learned method for predicting certain cellular perturbation responses from <single-cell RNA sequencing> data. Generalization to evaluated held-out cell types or conditions does not establish predictive validity for all interventions.<scGen predicts single-cell perturbation responses by Mohammad Lotfollahi and others>[Abstract and out-of-sample evaluations]{ref}

= Supervised learning
{parent=Statistical learning}
{wiki}

Supervised learning fits a prediction rule from <training data> containing both inputs and associated target responses. <Regression analysis> predicts numerical responses; <classification in statistical learning> predicts class labels. A <loss function> specifies which prediction errors matter, and <generalization> concerns performance beyond the observations used to fit the rule.

= Error-correction learning
{parent=Supervised learning}

Learning driven by a discrepancy between a target and the network’s actual output. The architecture must supply both a meaningful teaching signal and a mechanism for assigning that signal to the connections responsible for the error. This is distinct from error correction during retrieval in a fixed associative memory.

= Delta rule
{parent=Error-correction learning}
{title2=$\Delta W=\eta(t-y)x^{\mathsf T}$}

For a linear output $y=Wx$ and squared loss $L=\|t-y\|^2/2$, <gradient descent> gives $\Delta W=\eta(t-y)x^{\mathsf T}$. Indeed $\partial L/\partial W_{ij}=-(t_i-y_i)x_j$. On the same example, $e'=(1-\eta\|x\|^2)e$, proving strict error reduction when $e\ne0$ and $0<\eta\|x\|^2<2$.

= Generalization in statistical learning
{parent=Statistical learning}

= Generalization
{synonym}

Generalization is the ability of a fitted prediction rule to perform well on new observations under a specified target <probability distribution>. It is assessed through <prediction error> rather than <training error>. The target distribution and use of data are part of the question: <dataset shift> can change performance, and <data leakage> can make an apparent assessment misleading.

= Compositional generalization
{parent=Generalization in statistical learning}

Generalizing known components or operations to combinations not encountered during training. A held-out combination split tests a different ability from random train–test splitting when both sets contain nearly identical constructions. A failure of a particular neural training setup does not prove a limitation of every network architecture.<Generalization without Systematicity: On the Compositional Skills of Sequence-to-Sequence Recurrent Networks by Brenden M. Lake and others>[Abstract and experiments; PMLR 80, pp. 2873–2882, systematic versus ordinary SCAN <generalization>]{ref}

= Unsupervised learning
{parent=Statistical learning}
{wiki}

Learning structure from data without supplied target labels. Examples include extracting <principal components>, clustering and learning representations that efficiently reconstruct inputs.

= Continuous transformation learning
{parent=Unsupervised learning}

Learning transformation-tolerant representations by combining input-feature overlap, current-activity <Hebbian learning> and <synaptic competition>. A new view partly activates an output already learned for an overlapping view, then strengthens its new features onto that output. Chains of overlapping views can link more distant transformations without an explicit temporal trace; dense enough overlap and suitable competition remain conditions.<Learning invariant object recognition in the visual system with continuous transformations by Simon M. Stringer and others>[Abstract and Introduction, pp. 128–131; experiments 3–4 distinguish spatial overlap from temporal ordering]{ref}

= Competitive learning
{parent=Unsupervised learning}
{title2=$\Delta w_k=\eta(x-w_k)$}

Units compete to respond to an input, and the winning unit or sparse winning set strengthens its representation of that input. A prototype update is $w_k\leftarrow w_k+\eta(x-w_k)$ for the winner $k$, with $0<\eta<1$. The winner moves toward the sample: $w_k'-x=(1-\eta)(w_k-x)$. Competition and constraints prevent all units from learning the same unrestricted average.

= Statistical separation of objects in clutter
{parent=Competitive learning}

Independently varying companions can make an individual object’s features more consistently related than the features of any particular multi-object conjunction. Competitive networks can exploit this difference to learn separate object representations without being shown each object in isolation. The result depends on the training distribution; a fixed co-occurring group can instead be learned as a conjunction.<Invariant object recognition with trace learning and multiple stimuli present during training by Simon M. Stringer and others>[Abstract; Network 18, pp. 161–187, changing object repertoire and <trace learning> with multiple objects]{ref}<Learning transform invariant object recognition in the visual system with multiple stimuli present during training by Simon M. Stringer and others>[Abstract; <Neural Networks> 21, pp. 888–903, competitive networks and rotating 3D objects]{ref}

= Multidimensional scaling
{parent=Unsupervised learning}

Construct low-dimensional coordinates representing a <dissimilarity matrix>. Different methods preserve different features: <classical multidimensional scaling> approximates a centered <Gram matrix>, while other methods minimize discrepancies between fitted and supplied distances or preserve distance ordering. The method and dimension determine what geometric claims the resulting display supports.

= Classical multidimensional scaling
{parent=Multidimensional scaling}
{title2=$G=-\tfrac12HD^{(2)}H,\quad X_k=U_k\Lambda_k^{1/2}$}

= Classical scaling
{synonym}

= Principal coordinates analysis
{synonym}

For a symmetric zero-diagonal <dissimilarity matrix> $D$, square each entry to obtain $D^{(2)}$ and put $H=I-\mathbf1\mathbf1^T/n$. Take the <eigenvectors> of $G=-HD^{(2)}H/2$ corresponding to its largest positive <eigenvalues>. Multiplying them by square roots of these <eigenvalues> gives coordinates. If $D$ contains <Euclidean distances>, $G$ is a centered <Gram matrix>, so retaining all positive <eigenvalues> reconstructs those distances exactly. Retaining fewer approximates the <Gram matrix>, not generally the sum of squared errors in raw distances. Negative <eigenvalues> signal failure of an exact Euclidean representation. Coordinates are unchanged in meaning by <orthogonal transformations>.

= Euclidean distance matrix criterion
{c}
{parent=Classical multidimensional scaling}
{title2=$D\text{ is Euclidean}\iff-\tfrac12HD^{(2)}H\succeq0$}

For symmetric nonnegative $D$ with zero diagonal, a centered Euclidean realization implies $D_{ij}^2=G_{ii}+G_{jj}-2G_{ij}$ and hence the displayed double-centering identity. Conversely, if the double-centered matrix is <positive semidefinite>, factor it as $XX^T$. Its reconstructed squared distances equal the original squared distances: their difference has zero double-centering, so is of the form $\mathbf1 a^T+a\mathbf1^T$; the zero diagonal forces $a=0$. The minimum exact embedding dimension is $\operatorname{rank}G$.

= Sparse coding
{parent=Unsupervised learning}

Represent inputs accurately using few strongly active coefficients, for example by minimizing $\|x-Az\|^2/2+\lambda\sum_i|z_i|$. Learning basis functions on natural images can yield localized oriented filters resembling simple-cell receptive fields. This demonstrates one possible functional account of selectivity, not a proof that cortex implements that exact objective or learning algorithm.

= Hebbian learning
{c}
{parent=Unsupervised learning}
{wiki}

Activity-dependent strengthening associated with coactivation of an input and its postsynaptic output. The idealized update $\Delta w_i\propto yx_i$ requires normalization, competition or other constraints to prevent indefinite weight growth.

In <Hebbian memory weights for a Hopfield network>, the local update $\Delta w_{ij}=\eta\xi_i\xi_j$ records a pairwise association, where $\eta>0$ and $\xi_i,\xi_j\in\{-1,1\}$. Equal signs strengthen a positive coupling; opposite signs favor a negative coupling. Summing such updates gives a <symmetric matrix> of pairwise associations. This is a storage prescription: it does not by itself compare the generated <probability distribution> with the <training data>, as <Boltzmann machine> learning does. Bipolar activity also makes joint inactivity contribute positively; this encoding is a mathematical idealization, not a literal statement about inactive biological cells.

= Trace learning
{parent=Hebbian learning}
{title2=$\Delta w_{ij}=\eta\bar y_i(t-1)x_j(t)$}

A local association rule uses a decaying activity trace to connect representations encountered at nearby times. One implementation has $\bar y_i(t)=\rho\bar y_i(t-1)+(1-\rho)y_i(t)$ and $\Delta w_{ij}=\eta\bar y_i(t-1)x_j(t)$, followed by <synaptic normalization>. Temporal continuity can link distinct views of one object, but a trace spanning an identity change can create a false association.<Learning invariance from transformation sequences by Peter Földiák>[Abstract; Neural Computation 3, pp. 194–200, transformation-sequence learning]{ref}

= Quartic norm stabilization of Hebbian learning
{parent=Hebbian learning}
{title2=$\tau\dot w=yx-\alpha y^2w|w|^2$}

With $y=w\cdot x$, the squared weight norm $s=|w|^2$ satisfies $\tau\dot s=2y^2(1-\alpha s^2)$. It moves monotonically toward $s_*=\alpha^{-1/2}$ without crossing it. Explicitly $(s-s_*)/(s+s_*)$ equals its initial value times $\exp[-(4\sqrt\alpha/\tau)\int_0^ty(u)^2\,du]$. Persistent excitation therefore drives $|w|$ to $\alpha^{-1/4}$; without excitation it can remain stationary. This nonlinear normalization avoids the unbounded amplification of unmodified <Hebbian learning>.

= Oja's rule
{c}
{parent=Hebbian learning}
{title2=$\tau\dot w=yx-\alpha y^2w$}
{wiki}

For a linear output $y=w^Tx$, the second term limits the growth produced by the Hebbian term. On the averaged dynamics with $C=\mathbb E[xx^T]$, $\tau\dot w=Cw-\alpha(w^TCw)w$. For $\alpha>0$, positive-eigenvalue equilibria have norm $1/\sqrt\alpha$ and align with eigenvectors of $C$. If inputs are centered, $C$ is their <covariance matrix>.

= Stability and normalization of averaged Oja learning
{parent=Oja's rule}
{title2=$\tau\dot u_j=(\lambda_j-\lambda_i)u_j,\quad\tau\dot u_i=-2\lambda_i u_i$}

At the equilibrium $v_i/\sqrt\alpha$, linearization along orthonormal eigenvectors of $C$ gives the displayed rates, with the first equation applying to $j\ne i$. A simple positive largest eigenvalue makes its two normalized eigenvectors locally asymptotically stable; smaller eigenvalues have an unstable direction. Repeated largest eigenvalues give a manifold of equilibria with neutral directions. Generic convergence also needs a nonzero initial component in the largest eigenspace. The squared norm satisfies $\tau\,d\|w\|^2/dt=2(w^TCw)(1-\alpha\|w\|^2)$.

= Cluster analysis
{parent=Statistical learning}

Cluster analysis groups observations using similarities or dissimilarities without supplied class labels. A distance choice specifies which features and scales matter. <Agglomerative hierarchical clustering> merges groups recursively, producing partitions at different resolutions rather than one intrinsically determined number of groups.

= Cluster in cluster analysis
{parent=Cluster analysis}

= Clusters in cluster analysis
{synonym}

A cluster in <cluster analysis> is a nonempty group of observations treated as similar under the chosen representation and dissimilarity. A partition assigns each observation to exactly one cluster; <agglomerative hierarchical clustering> supplies a nested sequence of such partitions. An algorithmic cluster need not correspond to a distinct probabilistic population.

= K-means clustering
{parent=Cluster analysis}
{title2=$\min_{C_1,\ldots,C_K}\sum_{k=1}^K\sum_{i\in C_k}\|x_i-m_k\|^2$}
{wiki}

For a prescribed number of groups, minimize within-cluster squared Euclidean distances. At fixed assignments, differentiating the objective gives the mean as the optimal centroid. At fixed centroids, assigning each point to a nearest centroid minimizes its contribution. Alternating these steps cannot increase the objective but can converge to a local optimum, so multiple starts are useful. Empty clusters and ties need an explicit handling rule. The method favors roughly spherical groups and depends on variable scaling.

= One-dimensional K-means iteration bound
{parent=K-means clustering}
{title2=$N_{\mathrm{steps}}\le\binom{n-1}{K-1}\le n^{K-1}$}

For $n$ distinct observations on the real line and $1\le K\le n$, nearest-centre assignment produces contiguous blocks of the sorted observations because each <Voronoi cell> is an <interval>. The <arithmetic means> of distinct ordered blocks are strictly ordered, preserving this property at subsequent assignments. Initialize at $K$ distinct observed values so that the initial assignment has no empty cluster.

The empty-cluster repair in <finite termination of k-means clustering> also preserves contiguous blocks: an observation farthest from a centre over its assigned block lies at one of that block's endpoints, so transferring it to a new singleton splits the block into two contiguous blocks.

There are $\binom{n-1}{K-1}$ possible partitions into $K$ nonempty blocks: choose $K-1$ cuts among the $n-1$ gaps. Under the stable tie rule in <finite termination of k-means clustering>, every round after the first strictly reduces the objective until stopping, so a block partition cannot recur. The number of assignment, repair, and centre-update rounds is therefore at most $\binom{n-1}{K-1}\le n^{K-1}$. Check stability after each update without an additional update round; for $K=1$, one update suffices.

= Finite termination of K-means clustering
{parent=K-means clustering}

For a finite dataset, alternate nearest-centre assignment and replacement of each centre by the <centroid> of its assigned observations. Keep the current assignment whenever an observation is tied between nearest centres. A changed assignment then strictly decreases the within-cluster sum of squared <Euclidean distances>; updating centres cannot increase it by the <centroid sum-of-squares identity>.

After each centre update, the centres are determined by the current <partition of a set> of observation indices. There are only finitely many such partitions, so a strictly improving sequence cannot revisit one and must stop. A convenient exact stopping test is to check after each update whether every observation is still assigned to a nearest centre. This proof is for <algorithm termination>; it does not guarantee a <global minimum>.

Empty clusters need an explicit convention. To retain exactly $K\le n$ nonempty clusters, transfer an observation from a cluster with at least two members to each empty cluster, making it a singleton centred at that observation. This cannot increase the objective: the transferred observation now has squared distance zero, and no other contribution changes before centre updating. A deterministic choice is an eligible observation farthest from its currently assigned centre, with ties resolved by index. After the first round, an empty cluster can arise only during a strictly improving reassignment, so these repairs preserve the finite-termination argument.

= Nearest-centroid decision hyperplane
{parent=K-means clustering}
{title2=$2(z_1-z_2)^{\mathsf T}x+\|z_2\|^2-\|z_1\|^2=0$}

For two distinct <centroids> in a <Euclidean space>, equal squared <Euclidean distances> expand to the displayed affine <hyperplane>. It is their <perpendicular bisector>; each closed <half-space> consists of points at least as close to the centroid on that side. This proves weak <linear separability> after a two-centroid nearest-assignment step, allowing ties on the boundary. Coincident <centroids> are an essential exception: every point ties and the distance comparison supplies no nonzero normal vector.

= Centroid sum-of-squares identity
{parent=K-means clustering}
{title2=$\sum_i\|x_i-z\|^2=\sum_i\|x_i-\bar x\|^2+n\|z-\bar x\|^2$}

For a nonempty finite collection of <Euclidean vectors>, its <sample mean> $\bar x=n^{-1}\sum_i x_i$ uniquely minimizes the sum of squared <Euclidean distances> to a proposed <centroid> $z$. Expand each squared <Euclidean norm> around $\bar x$ and use $\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)=0$ to cancel the cross term. The displayed identity leaves a nonnegative term $n\|z-\bar x\|^2$, equal to zero exactly at $z=\bar x$.

= Jaccard coefficient
{c}
{synonym}

The index measures similarity of finite sets by shared presence relative to total presence. For binary profiles it ignores joint absences, unlike the <simple matching coefficient>. When both sets are empty, define the similarity to be one. The complementary <Jaccard distance> measures dissimilarity.

= Jaccard distance
{c}
{parent=Jaccard index}
{title2=$d_J(A,B)=1-J(A,B)$}

The dissimilarity is the number of mismatched presences divided by the number of coordinates present in at least one profile. It differs from <normalized Hamming distance> because coordinates absent from both profiles do not enter its denominator. Define the distance of two empty sets to be zero.

= Dissimilarity matrix
{parent=Cluster analysis}
{title2=$D_{rt}=d(x_r,x_t)$}

A dissimilarity matrix records pairwise separation of observations or profiles. Usually it is symmetric, nonnegative and zero on the diagonal. A <metric space> additionally requires the triangle inequality and separation of distinct profiles. <Agglomerative hierarchical clustering> can be applied to many dissimilarity matrices that are not Euclidean distance matrices; the claimed geometry must be checked separately.

= Simple matching coefficient
{parent=Cluster analysis}
{title2=$S_1(x,y)=\#\{j:x_j=y_j\}/p$}

For binary vectors, this similarity counts agreements on both presences and absences. Its complement is the <normalized Hamming distance>. Whether joint absences should count as similarity depends on the application; rare-presence data may call for a different coefficient.

= Metric property of simple matching dissimilarity
{parent=Simple matching coefficient}
{title2=$1-S(x,y)=p^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^p\mathbf1_{\{x_j\ne y_j\}}$}

The displayed distance is the <normalized Hamming distance>. Each coordinate mismatch between $x,z$ implies a mismatch between $x,y$ or $y,z$, so summing the indicator inequalities proves the triangle inequality. Nonnegativity, symmetry and separation follow immediately. It is a metric on profiles; on separately labelled individuals with identical profiles it is a <pseudometric>.

= Agglomerative hierarchical clustering
{parent=Cluster analysis}

Start with singleton clusters and repeatedly merge the two clusters with the smallest chosen intercluster dissimilarity. <Single-linkage clustering> uses the smallest cross-pair distance; <complete-linkage clustering> uses the largest. Merge heights and sensitivity to ties help assess a proposed number of groups.

= Ward minimum-variance clustering
{c}
{parent=Agglomerative hierarchical clustering}
{title2=$\Delta(A,B)=\frac{|A||B|}{|A|+|B|}\|\bar x_A-\bar x_B\|^2$}

= Ward clustering
{c}
{synonym}

Ward clustering merges the pair causing the smallest increase in within-cluster sum of squares. Expanding each cluster's squared deviations about the merged mean gives the displayed increase. Its Euclidean sum-of-squares interpretation must not be asserted for arbitrary non-Euclidean dissimilarities.

= Average-linkage clustering
{parent=Agglomerative hierarchical clustering}
{title2=$d(A,B)=\frac1{|A||B|}\sum_{x\in A,y\in B}d(x,y)$}

Average linkage merges the pair with the smallest mean cross-pair dissimilarity. This differs from the minimum used by <single-linkage clustering> and the maximum used by <complete-linkage clustering>. The convention here weights individual cross-pairs equally; weighting already formed clusters equally is a different algorithm.

= Agglomerative clustering of nonmetric dissimilarities
{parent=Agglomerative hierarchical clustering}

The min and max cross-pair definitions of <single-linkage clustering> and <complete-linkage clustering> remain meaningful for a symmetric nonnegative <dissimilarity matrix> that violates the triangle inequality. Such a calculation gives a valid algorithmic dendrogram, but does not turn the original matrix into Euclidean distances. A triangle-inequality counterexample identifies that source defect without requiring invented replacement data.

= Dendrogram
{parent=Agglomerative hierarchical clustering}
{wiki}

A dendrogram displays successive cluster merges, with the height of each merge equal to its intercluster dissimilarity. Cutting at a height gives a partition. <Single-linkage clustering> measures the closest cross-pair and can form chains, while <complete-linkage clustering> measures the farthest cross-pair and favors smaller within-cluster diameters.

= Complete-linkage clustering
{parent=Agglomerative hierarchical clustering}
{title2=$d(C,D)=\max_{x\in C,y\in D}d(x,y)$}

= Complete link
{synonym}

The farthest cross-pair distance determines the next merge. It favors groups with small diameter and reduces the chaining effect of <single-linkage clustering>, but can be sensitive to isolated extreme observations. Ties can affect intermediate partitions.

= Single-linkage clustering
{parent=Agglomerative hierarchical clustering}
{title2=$d(C,D)=\min_{x\in C,y\in D}d(x,y)$}

= Single link
{synonym}

The nearest cross-pair distance determines the next merge. At a threshold, clusters are connected components of the graph joining pairs below that threshold. This handles nonconvex shapes but can join otherwise separate groups through a chain of intermediate observations.

= Validation set
{parent=Statistical learning}

A validation set is held out from parameter fitting and used to choose models or tuning parameters. A separate test set is then needed for an unbiased final evaluation after selection.

= Test set
{parent=Validation set}
{title2=$\mathcal D_{\mathrm{test}}$}

A test set is held out from both parameter fitting and <model selection> and is used to estimate the final selected procedure's <prediction error>. A <validation set> influences the choice; a <test set> assesses that choice after it is fixed. The distinction concerns how observations are used, not their file names.

For a fixed rule and an <independent> <test set> of size $m$ from the target <probability distribution>,
$$
\widehat R_{\mathrm{test}}=\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m L(Y_j^*,\widehat f(X_j^*)),\qquad
\mathbb E[\widehat R_{\mathrm{test}}\mid\mathcal D_n]=R(\widehat f).
$$
This <test error> is an estimate, with sampling uncertainty; it is not a guarantee for each future prediction. <Dataset shift> changes what performance the estimate represents.

In the <overfitting> example, all degree choices are fixed in advance, <cross-validation> uses only <training data>, and the selected degree is locked before evaluating 400 separate points. Displaying the other curves afterward explains their behavior; it does not authorize selecting by those scores. Repeatedly inspecting a <test set> and changing the model makes it part of <model selection>, introducing <data leakage> and potentially optimistic assessment. https://jmlr.org/papers/v11/cawley10a.html[Cawley and Talbot, over-fitting in model selection, 2010]{ref}

= Prediction error
{parent=Statistical learning}

Prediction error is the expected loss of a fitted rule on a new observation from the target distribution.

= Test error
{parent=Prediction error}

Test error is the average loss on an independent test set and estimates prediction error when the test set has not influenced model fitting or selection.

= Mean squared prediction error
{title2=$\operatorname{MSPE}$}
{parent=Prediction error}

For a predictor $\widehat f$ and an independent response $Y^*$ at covariate $X^*$, mean squared prediction error is $\mathbb E[(Y^*-\widehat f(X^*))^2]$.

= Training error
{parent=Statistical learning}
{title2=$\widehat R_{\mathrm{train}}=n^{-1}\sum_i L(Y_i,\widehat f(X_i))$}

= In-sample error
{synonym}

Training error is the average <loss function> evaluated on the observations used to fit the prediction rule. It is the <empirical risk> of the fitted rule on its own <training data>. With <squared-error loss>, it is the average squared <regression residual>, measuring fit to observed responses rather than performance on fresh responses. Flexible fitting usually makes it optimistically biased for <prediction error>, as quantified by <prediction optimism>.

For a reproducible <polynomial regression> example, take $x_i=-1+2i/11$, $i=0,\ldots,11$, and the known noiseless target $f(x)=1+x/2+2x^2$. Construct responses $y_i=f(x_i)+e_i$ with fixed disturbances
$$
(e_i)=(0.12,-0.17,0.10,0.18,-0.13,0.08,-0.16,0.11,-0.08,0.15,-0.12,0.06).
$$
<Ordinary least squares> over <polynomials> of <polynomial degree> at most 1, 2, and 7 gives <training errors> $0.518937$, $0.015784$, and $0.009257$. The monotone improvement follows because these candidate families are nested: every smaller-degree fit remains available in the larger family. This argument concerns minimum <training error>, not <generalization>, and assumes the fitting <mathematical optimization> is solved. Degrees 2 and 7 fitted instead to the exact noiseless responses $f(x_i)$ have mathematically zero <training error>; the script obtains approximately $5.31\times10^{-31}$ and $7.82\times10^{-31}$ from rounding. Extra flexibility alone does not force <overfitting>.

= Prediction optimism
{parent=Training error}
{title2=$\operatorname{Opt}=\operatorname{Err}_{\mathrm{in}}-\mathbb E\operatorname{err}$}

Prediction optimism is the difference between expected squared prediction error on new independent responses at the training covariates and expected <training error>. Reusing responses to fit and assess a model tends to make training error too favorable. The <covariance formula for prediction optimism> measures this reuse without assuming a linear fitting rule.

= Covariance formula for prediction optimism
{parent=Prediction optimism}
{title2=$\operatorname{Opt}=\frac2n\sum_i\operatorname{Cov}(Y_i,\widehat Y_i)$}

With fixed covariates and independent new responses having the same marginal distributions as the training responses, expanding squared losses cancels their second moments. Independence makes $\mathbb E(Y_i^{\mathrm{new}}\widehat Y_i)=\mathbb EY_i\mathbb E\widehat Y_i$, yielding the displayed identity. For a fixed <linear smoother> $\widehat Y=HY$ with covariance $\sigma^2I$, it reduces to $2\sigma^2\operatorname{tr}(H)/n$, linking prediction optimism to <effective degrees of freedom>.

= Principal component analysis
{parent=Statistical learning}
{wiki}

= PCA
{c}
{synonym}

Principal component analysis finds orthogonal directions of greatest sample variance. Its first loading vector is a unit eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue of the sample covariance matrix.

= High explained variance need not preserve class information under PCA
{parent=Principal component analysis}

Let $W,S$ be independent balanced variables with values $-1,1$, and let $X=(aW,bS)^T$ with $a>b>0$ and class $Y=S$. Then $E[X]=0$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(X)=\operatorname{diag}(a^2,b^2)$. The first <principal component> is the first coordinate and explains $a^2/(a^2+b^2)$ of total <variance>. Yet it is independent of $Y$, so even the best <classifier> using only that coordinate has accuracy $1/2$. The second coordinate determines $Y$ exactly. Taking $a/b$ arbitrarily large makes the retained <variance> approach one without improving class accuracy. Adding a constant baseline to each coordinate preserves the centered result and permits positive neural rates.

= Principal component regression
{parent=Principal component analysis}
{wiki}

= PCR
{c}
{synonym}

Regress a response on selected <principal component scores> of centered predictors. If $X=UDV^T$ is a <singular value decomposition> and the first $q$ positive-singular-value components are retained, the retained score matrix is $U_qD_q$ and the fitted coefficient in predictor coordinates is $V_qD_q^{-1}U_q^Ty$. The fitted centered response is $U_qU_q^Ty$: this follows by applying the <least-squares normal equations> to the orthogonal score columns. Truncating small singular directions limits variance amplification, but directions of large predictor variance need not be those best predicting the response; select $q$ using prediction assessment rather than explained predictor variance alone.

= Scree plot
{parent=Principal component analysis}
{wiki}

A scree plot graphs ordered covariance or correlation <eigenvalues> against component number. A transition from a steep decline to a flatter tail suggests a dimension, but this is a diagnostic rather than a proof of the number of latent factors. In <factor analysis>, it should be combined with model fit, substantive interpretation and uncertainty assessment.

= Principal component loading
{parent=Principal component analysis}
{title2=$r_j=\sqrt{\lambda_j}\ell_j$}

Two conventions occur for principal-component loadings: the unit <eigenvector> $\ell_j$ defining a <principal component>, and the scaled vector $r_j=\sqrt{\lambda_j}\ell_j$. For <principal component analysis on a correlation matrix>, the entries of the latter are the correlations between each standardized variable and the component scaled to variance one. Indeed, $\operatorname{Cov}(Z,\ell_j^TZ)=C\ell_j=\lambda_j\ell_j$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\ell_j^TZ)=\lambda_j$. Thus $\|r_j\|^2=\lambda_j$, whereas $\|\ell_j\|=1$; reporting the convention avoids confusing component directions with correlations.

= Loadings matrix
{parent=Principal component loading}
{title2=$V=(v_1,\ldots,v_p)$}

In <principal component analysis>, the <matrix> whose columns are the chosen orthonormal <eigenvectors> of a <covariance matrix>, ordered by decreasing <eigenvalues>. Under this convention the <principal component scores> of centered data are $Z=X_cV$. Some literature instead scales the vectors by square roots of eigenvalues; the convention should be stated explicitly.

= Principal component
{parent=Principal component analysis}

A principal component is the projection of centered data onto a unit <eigenvector> of its <covariance matrix>. Ordering the components by their <eigenvalues> orders their variances. Repeated <eigenvalues> allow any <orthonormal basis> of the corresponding <eigenspace>.

= Principal component score
{parent=Principal component}
{title2=$z_{ij}=q_j^T(x_i-\bar x)$}

A score is an observation's coordinate along a <principal component> direction. Center an observation by the fitted <sample mean>, apply the fitted variable scaling when using <principal component analysis on a correlation matrix>, then take its <inner product> with the unit <principal component loading> vector. New observations must use the training center and scaling. The score <variance> equals the corresponding <eigenvalue> when the covariance normalization is consistent.

= Explained variance of a principal component
{parent=Principal component analysis}
{title2=$\lambda_j/\operatorname{tr}\Sigma$}

A <population principal component> explains the displayed fraction of total <variance>. The first $q$ components explain $\sum_{j\le q}\lambda_j/\sum_j\lambda_j$. Comparing covariance and correlation analyses requires different denominators: the original <variance> sum for the former and the number of nonconstant standardized variables for the latter.

= Principal component analysis on a correlation matrix
{parent=Principal component analysis}
{title2=$Z=D^{-1/2}(X-\mu),\quad\operatorname{Cov}(Z)=D^{-1/2}\Sigma D^{-1/2}$}

For positive marginal <variances>, standardizing each variable to <variance> one converts covariance-based <PCA> on $Z$ into correlation-based PCA on $X$. This makes the analysis insensitive to the original measurement units but changes the optimization criterion. Standardization gives small-variance variables the same marginal weight as large-variance variables; it is a modelling choice, not an automatic improvement.

= Population principal component
{parent=Principal component analysis}
{title2=$Y_j=q_j^T(X-\mu),\quad\Sigma q_j=\lambda_jq_j$}

The <population principal components> are centered linear compounds along an orthonormal eigenbasis of the <covariance matrix>, ordered by decreasing <eigenvalue>. They are uncorrelated and have <variances> $\lambda_j$. Each successive direction maximizes <variance> subject to unit length and orthogonality to earlier directions. Uncorrelatedness does not imply <independence> unless additional distributional assumptions hold.

= Sample principal component
{parent=Principal component analysis}

Let the columns of a centered <design matrix> $X\in\mathbb R^{n\times p}$ be variables and write $X^TX=V\Lambda V^T$ by the <spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices>. The columns $u_i$ of $U=XV$ are the sample principal-component score vectors. They satisfy $u_i^Tu_j=\Lambda_{ij}$, so distinct score vectors have zero <sample covariance> and $u_i$ has <sample variance> $\Lambda_{ii}/n$ under the divisor-$n$ convention.

= Normalized sample principal component
{parent=Sample principal component}

When $\Lambda_{ii}>0$, the normalized sample principal component $w_i=u_i/\sqrt{\Lambda_{ii}}$ has <Euclidean norm> one. The vectors $w_i$ form an <orthonormal set> spanning the <column space> of a full-column-rank centered design matrix.

= Classification in statistical learning
{parent=Statistical learning}

Classification predicts a discrete label from observed covariates.

= Classification accuracy
{parent=Classification in statistical learning}

The fraction of predicted class labels that agree with the observed labels.

= Misclassification rate
{parent=Classification in statistical learning}
{title2=$\mathbb P(\widehat g(X)\ne Y)$}

= Misclassification error
{synonym}

The misclassification rate is the expected <zero-one loss> of a <classification in statistical learning> rule. Its empirical version is the fraction of incorrectly labeled observations. Evaluation on the fitting sample gives a <training error>; evaluation on an independent <test set> estimates <prediction error>. For a majority-class leaf of a <classification tree>, the training error count is $n_t-\max_kn_{tk}$, unlike <classification-tree deviance>, which also uses the fitted class <probabilities>.

= Classification tree
{parent=Classification in statistical learning}

A classification tree partitions predictor space recursively and attaches a class probability vector to each terminal region. For a categorical response, a fitted leaf commonly uses its observed class proportions and predicts their largest entry. Binary numerical splits compare one coordinate with a threshold. Growing a tree greedily by reducing <classification-tree deviance> gives an interpretable rule but does not globally optimize the tree. <Cost-complexity tree pruning> and <cross-validation> control complexity and assess <prediction error>.

= Cost-complexity tree pruning
{parent=Classification tree}
{title2=$R_\alpha(T)=R(T)+\alpha|\mathcal L(T)|$}

Pruning balances a tree's empirical loss $R(T)$ against the number of leaves. Increasing $\alpha$ favors a nested sequence of smaller subtrees, obtained by removing splits with the smallest effective cost per removed leaf. Choose complexity using <cross-validation> or a <validation set>, and assess the selected tree on a separate <test set>. The loss can be deviance or misclassification loss; the chosen convention must be specified.

= Classification-tree deviance
{parent=Classification tree}
{title2=$D(t)=-2\sum_kn_{tk}\log(n_{tk}/n_t)$}

Maximizing a node's multinomial likelihood gives class probabilities $\widehat p_{tk}=n_{tk}/n_t$. Twice the negative maximized log-likelihood relative to perfect classification is the displayed deviance, with $0\log0=0$. It equals twice the node size times its <Shannon entropy>. A split is chosen by maximizing $D(t)-D(t_L)-D(t_R)$, and tree deviance is the sum over terminal nodes. The training misclassification count is instead $\sum_t(n_t-\max_kn_{tk})$; the two criteria are different.

= Binary classification
{parent=Classification in statistical learning}
{title2=$Y\in\{0,1\}$}
{wiki}

<Classification in statistical learning> with two possible labels. Given the <conditional probability> $\eta(x)=\mathbb P(Y=1\mid X=x)$, a <Bayes classifier> under <zero-one loss> predicts one exactly when $\eta(x)\geq1/2$, with arbitrary decisions at ties.

A plot of <true positive rate> against <false positive rate> while a decision threshold varies over a fixed evaluation population. A random ranking has an expected diagonal curve; a perfect ranking reaches the upper-left corner.

= Area under the ROC curve

{c}
{synonym}

The integral of the <receiver operating characteristic>'s true positive rate with respect to its false positive rate. Equivalently, the probability that a randomly selected positive is ranked above a randomly selected negative, with half weight for ties. It measures discrimination rather than probability calibration or utility at a particular threshold.

= Linear separability
{parent=Binary classification}

= Linearly separable
{synonym}

Two subsets of a <Euclidean space> are weakly linearly separable when a <hyperplane> with nonzero normal vector places them in opposite closed <half-spaces>. Points on the separating <hyperplane> may belong to either subset. Strict separation instead requires strictly opposite signs, excluding all boundary ties.

= Diagnostic specificity
{parent=Binary classification}
{title2=$Sp=P(-\mid\text{condition absent})$}

<Diagnostic specificity> is the probability that a test is negative in someone without the target condition. For an antibody test, relevant negative controls can include samples with antibodies to related pathogens to assess cross-reactivity. With true positive fraction $p$, observed positivity is $Se\,p+(1-Sp)(1-p)$.

= Diagnostic sensitivity
{parent=Binary classification}
{title2=$Se=P(+\mid\text{condition present})$}

<Diagnostic sensitivity> is the probability that a test is positive in a person who has the target condition. For past-infection assays, the target is prior infection rather than current disease. Estimate it from suitable independently characterized positive samples; using the same assay to define truth would make the assessment circular.

= False positive rate

The <false-positive rate> is $\mathbb P(\text{positive result}\mid\text{truly negative class})$. In screening, it measures erroneous positives among genuinely disease-free individuals.

= Surrogate split

A surrogate split uses another predictor to approximate the left-versus-right assignments made by a tree node's primary split. It can route observations whose primary predictor is missing. The K-nearest neighbors algorithm predicts from the labels or responses of the $K$ training observations closest to the query under a chosen distance.

= Bias and variance of a three-neighbour weighted smoother
{parent=K-nearest neighbors algorithm}
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}(\widehat m_w)=\sigma^2[w^2+(1-w)^2/2]$}

With fixed neighbor positions, independent equal-variance errors and weights $w,(1-w)/2,(1-w)/2$, the displayed <variance> is minimized at $w=1/3$, with value $\sigma^2/3$. Its <bias> is $w m_1+(1-w)(m_2+m_3)/2-m(x)$. Larger nearest-point weight often reduces smoothing bias, but neither the bias nor the variance is universally monotone over $0<w<1$. If the nearest point is the target, the squared bias is $(1-w)^2[(m_2+m_3)/2-m(x)]^2$.

= One-nearest-neighbour classifier
{parent=K-nearest neighbors algorithm}
{title2=$\widehat C_n^{1{\rm nn}}(x)=Y_{I_n(x)}$}

= 1-nearest neighbour classifier
{synonym}

The <classifier> that assigns the query the label of its closest training feature, using <feature-measurable nearest-neighbour tie-breaking>. Its <misclassification risk> need not converge to <Bayes risk>, even with infinitely many <independent and identically distributed random variables> as <training data>.

= One-nearest-neighbour asymptotic risk
{parent=One-nearest-neighbour classifier}
{title2=$R_\infty=\mathbb E[2\eta(X)(1-\eta(X))]$}

For <binary classification>, write $\eta(x)=\mathbb P(Y=1\mid X=x)$ and assume <feature-measurable nearest-neighbour tie-breaking>. If $\mathbb E|\eta(X_{(1)}(X))-\eta(X)|\to0$, then the expected <conditional misclassification risk> of the <one-nearest-neighbour classifier> tends to $\mathbb E[2\eta(X)(1-\eta(X))]$. Couple each label and an oracle label using one uniform variable. Their disagreement has the preceding expected absolute difference. The oracle and the test label are conditionally <independent> <Bernoulli random variables> of parameter $\eta(X)$, so their disagreement <probability> is exactly $2\eta(X)(1-\eta(X))$. The difference of <classification in statistical learning> error probabilities is at most the coupling disagreement.

= Bayes risk bound for one-nearest-neighbour classification
{c}
{parent=One-nearest-neighbour asymptotic risk}
{title2=$R^*\leq R_\infty\leq2R^*$}

Under the hypotheses for the <one-nearest-neighbour asymptotic risk>, the limiting risk satisfies $R^*\leq R_\infty\leq2R^*$. Put $a=\min(\eta,1-\eta)$: then $a\leq2a(1-a)\leq2a$ since $0\leq a\leq1/2$. Integrate, using the definition of <Bayes risk>. Constant $\eta=1/4$ gives limiting risk $3/8$ and <Bayes risk> $1/4$, so the lower comparison is not generally equality.

= Feature-measurable nearest-neighbour tie-breaking
{parent=K-nearest neighbors algorithm}
{title2=$I(x)=\min\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{i}\|X_i-x\|$}

For a fixed query, order training features by their distances and break equal distances by a rule depending only on features, such as original index. If auxiliary uniforms are <independent> of the features, the uniform selected by the resulting index remains uniform conditional on all features: sum over the possible selected indices. Label-dependent tie-breaking can invalidate <Bernoulli coupling by a shared uniform random variable> and the usual <one-nearest-neighbour asymptotic risk>.

= Support vector machine
{parent=Classification in statistical learning}
{wiki}

A support vector machine chooses a separating decision function by trading a wide geometric margin against hinge-loss violations.

= Kernel support vector machine
{parent=Support vector machine}

A kernel support vector machine trains a <support vector machine> using a <positive-definite kernel> instead of explicit feature coordinates. The <kernel trick> evaluates all required feature inner products through the training <Gram matrix>; prediction is $\sum_i\alpha_i y_i k(x_i,x)+b$. Nonzero dual coefficients identify <support vectors>. An unpenalized intercept supplies the dual equality $\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0$. The <reproducing-kernel Hilbert space> construction explains why a positive-semidefinite kernel defines valid feature geometry.

= Soft-margin support vector machine
{parent=Support vector machine}

For signed training labels, a linear soft-margin <support vector machine> minimizes
$$
\frac12\lVert w\rVert^2+C\sum_i\xi_i,\qquad y_i(w^Tx_i+b)\geq1-\xi_i,\quad\xi_i\geq0,
$$
with $C>0$. Its <Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions> give $w=\sum_i\alpha_i y_ix_i$, $\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0$, and $0\leq\alpha_i\leq C$.

= Squared-slack support vector machine
{parent=Soft-margin support vector machine}
{title2=$\min_{w,b,\xi\geq0}\tfrac12\|w\|^2+C\sum_i\xi_i^2$}

The squared-slack <support vector machine> uses constraints $y_i(w\cdot x_i+b)\geq1-\xi_i$. With an unpenalized intercept, its <Lagrangian dual problem> maximizes $\sum_i\alpha_i-\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_jK(x_i,x_j)-\tfrac1{4C}\sum_i\alpha_i^2$ subject to $\alpha_i\geq0$ and $\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0$. The square adds a diagonal penalty in the dual rather than a finite upper box constraint. The <Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions> give $w=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i\phi(x_i)$ and $\xi_i=\alpha_i/(2C)$ for a <feature map> realizing the <positive-semidefinite kernel> $K$.

= Kernel support-vector coefficient from hinge activity
{parent=Soft-margin support vector machine}

For the kernel <support vector machine> objective $n^{-1}\sum_i(1-Y_i(\mu+K_i^T\alpha))_++\lambda\alpha^TK\alpha$, assume $Y_i\in\{-1,1\}$, $\lambda>0$, and that the <kernel matrix> $K$ is invertible. The <subdifferential> optimality equation gives
$$
\alpha_i=Y_it_i/(2n\lambda),\qquad t_i=\begin{cases}1&Y_i(\mu+K_i^T\alpha)<1,\\{}[0,1]&Y_i(\mu+K_i^T\alpha)=1,\\0&Y_i(\mu+K_i^T\alpha)>1.\end{cases}
$$
Thus strict margins greater than one force zero coefficients, while misclassified observations have nonzero coefficients. Invertibility matters: a singular <kernel matrix> allows coefficient changes in its <null space> without changing the fitted <function> or objective.

= Support-vector leave-one-out error bound
{parent=Soft-margin support vector machine}
{title2=$\operatorname{err}_{CV}\leq s/n$}

For a fixed $C$ in the unnormalized sum-of-slacks objective and unique training optimizers, deleting an observation with $\alpha_i=0$ preserves the <Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions> and the fitted decision function. It is correctly classified when held out. Consequently <leave-one-out cross-validation> makes at most $s$ errors, where $s$ counts positive dual coefficients. With geometric support vectors, counting all points of signed margin at most one also gives the bound, without a nondegeneracy assumption.

= Cross-validation
{parent=Statistical learning}
{title2=$\operatorname{CV}=n^{-1}\sum_i L(Y_i,\widehat f^{(-k(i))}(X_i))$}
{wiki=Cross-validation_(statistics)}

Cross-validation estimates predictive performance by repeatedly fitting on one part of the data and evaluating on held-out observations. In <K-fold cross-validation>, divide <training data> into disjoint folds $I_1,\ldots,I_K$; $k(i)$ identifies observation $i$'s fold, and $\widehat f^{(-k)}$ is fitted without fold $I_k$. The displayed average scores each observation using a rule that did not train on it. Weight by observation count when fold sizes differ. <Leave-one-out cross-validation> uses one observation per fold. https://web.stanford.edu/~hastie/ISLR2/Slides/Ch5_Resampling_Methods.pdf[ISLR chapter 5 slides, resampling methods]{ref}

For the <training error> example, three folds contain indices $i$ with equal remainder modulo 3. Each fit uses eight observations. The <squared-error loss> averages for degrees 1, 2, and 7 are $0.724132$, $0.027192$, and $1.532790$ respectively. Select degree 2 and refit on all twelve <training data> observations. The folds reuse scarce data while challenging each candidate to predict unseen responses; they do not touch the final <test set>.

Any learned preprocessing, including variable selection, must also be fitted within each training fold to avoid <data leakage>. https://web.stanford.edu/~hastie/ISLR2/Slides/Ch5_Resampling_Methods.pdf[ISLR chapter 5 slides, the wrong and right way]{ref} Repeated tuning can itself overfit the <cross-validation> score; <nested cross-validation> uses inner folds for <model selection> and outer folds for assessment of that entire procedure. <On Over-fitting in Model Selection and Subsequent Selection Bias in Performance Evaluation by Gavin C. Cawley and others>{ref}

Folds use fewer training observations than the final refit, and their scores are dependent because training observations overlap. https://web.stanford.edu/~hastie/ISLR2/Slides/Ch5_Resampling_Methods.pdf[ISLR chapter 5 slides, correlated fold estimates]{ref} Splitting must match deployment: for <time series> forecasting, rolling-origin <cross-validation> trains on the past and evaluates later observations, preventing future information from entering a forecast. Ordinary random folds need not answer that forecasting question. https://otexts.com/fpp3/tscv.html[Hyndman and Athanasopoulos, time series cross-validation]{ref}

= Nested cross-validation
{parent=Cross-validation}

Nested cross-validation separates selection from assessment using two levels of held-out data. Within each outer training fold, inner <cross-validation> chooses a model or tuning parameters; the complete selected fitting procedure is then applied to that outer training fold and scored on its untouched outer evaluation fold. This estimates the performance of the <model selection> procedure, including its choices, rather than reusing its best inner score as an assessment. A final model can then be selected and fitted using all available <training data>. https://jmlr.org/papers/v11/cawley10a.html[Cawley and Talbot, selection bias and performance evaluation]{ref}

A neural network composes affine maps with nonlinear activation functions and learns their weights from data.

= Winner-take-all network

A network in which competition allows the strongest responding unit to dominate while suppressing alternatives. A sparse winning population is a softer variant; inhibition and normalization specify the mechanism rather than following automatically from a diagram.

= Exploding gradient problem
{parent=Neural network}

= Exploding gradient
{synonym}

Repeated differentiation through a deep or recurrent <neural network> can make some <gradient> components extremely large. In the scalar recurrence $h_t=ah_{t-1}$, the derivative with respect to $h_0$ is $a^t$, which grows exponentially if $|a|>1$. Large individual matrix norms alone do not imply such growth, because directions can change or cancel; the full product of step <Jacobian matrices> determines the effect.

= Vanishing gradient problem
{parent=Neural network}

= Vanishing gradient
{synonym}

Repeated differentiation through a deep or recurrent <neural network> can make <gradients> associated with distant layers or earlier sequence positions extremely small. A simple mechanism is a product of step <Jacobian matrices> whose <operator norms> are all bounded above by some $a<1$: the product <operator norm> is then at most $a^L$ over $L$ steps by submultiplicativity. This can impede learning long-range dependencies in a <recurrent neural network>.

= Recurrent neural network
{parent=Neural network}
{title2=RNN}

A <neural network> whose hidden state follows a recurrence $h_t=F(h_{t-1},x_t)$ with shared parameters. It represents dependence across sequence positions through its hidden state. The standard recurrence is sequential across positions, and differentiation through many steps multiplies step <Jacobian matrices>, potentially causing a <vanishing gradient> or an <exploding gradient>.

= Gated recurrent neural network
{parent=Recurrent neural network}

A recurrent architecture that uses learned gates to regulate state preservation, replacement or readout. Gating can improve useful memory and learning over long intervals, but does not by itself establish biological implementation or solve arbitrary temporal credit assignment.

= Continuous attractor network
{parent=Recurrent neural network}

A recurrent network with a continuous family of stable or approximately stable activity patterns, such as a localized bump at any angle on a ring. Recurrent dynamics maintain the represented variable while motion-related input updates it and sensory input anchors it. Postulating the attractor family does not by itself explain its developmental origin.

= Dropout
{parent=Neural network}

A training regularization method that randomly removes selected activations or connections. In inverted dropout with keep probability $p$, multiply each retained activation by $1/p$, so its conditional <expectation> equals the original activation; inference then uses the unmasked activations. The random masks discourage dependence on a particular small collection of activations.

= Residual connection
{parent=Neural network}
{title2=$z\mapsto z+F(z)$}

A connection that adds a sublayer's input to its output, using a projection when dimensions require it. Its derivative is $I+DF(z)$, so the derivative has a direct identity contribution in addition to the sublayer's contribution. This supports propagation through a deep network but does not itself guarantee bounded <gradients>.

= Layer normalization
{parent=Neural network}
{title2=$\mathrm{LN}(z)$}

For $z\in\mathbb R^d$, define $\bar z=d^{-1}\sum_jz_j$ and $s_z^2=d^{-1}\sum_j(z_j-\bar z)^2$. Layer normalization returns $\gamma\odot(z-\bar z\mathbf1)/\sqrt{s_z^2+\varepsilon}+\beta$, with learned coordinatewise scale $\gamma$, shift $\beta$, and fixed $\varepsilon>0$. In a <transformer block>, this normalization is applied across feature coordinates separately within each example and sequence position; it does not average over a batch.

= Transformer model
{parent=Neural network}

A sequence model built from stacked <transformer blocks>, using <self-attention> to mix information between positions and positionwise <feedforward neural networks> to transform their representations. <Positional encoding> supplies sequence-order information. An autoregressive decoder uses <causal self-attention>; generation remains sequential even when training computations over known sequence positions are parallel.

= Positional encoding
{parent=Transformer model}

Information representing sequence position or relative position, supplied to input representations or to attention scores. It can be fixed or learned. Without order information or a position-dependent mask, a <self-attention> layer using shared projections treats a simultaneous permutation of sequence positions equivariantly.

= Self-attention
{parent=Transformer model}

An attention operation in which queries, keys, and values are projections of one sequence's representations. At each position, learned similarities between its query and permitted keys determine a weighted sum of the corresponding values. <Scaled dot-product attention> and <multi-head attention> are standard implementations; <causal self-attention> restricts which positions can be accessed.

= Causal self-attention
{parent=Self-attention}

<Self-attention> whose output at sequence position $t$ may depend only on positions at or before $t$. In a score <matrix>, set forbidden future-position scores to negative infinity before applying the <softmax function>; the corresponding weights become zero. This permits parallel computation over a known training sequence without allowing future tokens to influence earlier predictions.

= Multi-head attention
{parent=Self-attention}

Several independently learned query, key, and value projections apply <scaled dot-product attention> in parallel. Concatenating their outputs and applying a learned output projection allows different heads to mix sequence information using different learned representations.

= Scaled dot-product attention
{parent=Self-attention}

With query and key dimension $d_k$, form the score <matrix> $QK^T/\sqrt{d_k}$, apply the <softmax function> row by row, and multiply by the values $V$. If query and key coordinates have independent unit <variance>, their dot product has <variance> $d_k$; division by $\sqrt{d_k}$ keeps that score scale of order one. Optional additive masks exclude forbidden keys before the <softmax function>.

= Transformer block

A standard transformer block combines <multi-head attention> with a positionwise <feedforward neural network>, wrapping each sublayer in a <residual connection> and <layer normalization>. An encoder-decoder transformer's decoder also has a cross-attention sublayer. Pre-normalization and post-normalization variants differ in where <layer normalization> occurs relative to the sublayer and <residual connection>.

= Hopfield network
{c}
{parent=Neural network}
{wiki}

A recurrent network with symmetric interactions and an energy function. In the binary version, units take values $\pm1$, self-couplings are zero, and asynchronous threshold updates can retrieve stored patterns from incomplete or corrupted cues. Fixed points are local energy minima, which need not be the intended memories or global minima.

For a <symmetric matrix> of couplings $W$ with $w_{ii}=0$, define the <energy landscape>
$$
E(x)=-\frac12\sum_{i,j}w_{ij}x_ix_j+\sum_i\theta_ix_i,\qquad x_i\in\{-1,1\}.
$$
The factor $1/2$ removes double counting. The local input is $h_k=\sum_{j\ne k}w_{kj}x_j-\theta_k$. Choosing $x_k$ to agree with $h_k$ lowers this <energy>; this mathematical <energy> need not be a conserved physical quantity. Its pairwise form connects the model to the <Ising model>; competing couplings also motivate the comparison with a <spin glass>. Here retrieval seeks a useful nearby <local minimum>, so reaching the global minimum is not the goal.

<John Hopfield>'s 1982 computer study stored random <binary strings> in <neural networks> of 30 and 100 units. Couplings were prepared from the stored patterns; asynchronous updates supplied the computational apparatus. Starting at each nominal memory, the readout counted <Hamming distances> after settling as storage load varied. Corrupted starting patterns separately tested recovery. Varying the load and cue damage exposed interference between memories. These simulations supported collective retrieval, not a measurement of biological memory capacity.<Neural networks and physical systems with emergent collective computational abilities by John Joseph Hopfield>[1982, pp. 2555–2557, storage Eq. 2, Fig. 2 and corrupted-start tests]{ref}

= Asynchronous Hopfield energy descent
{parent=Hopfield network}
{title2=$\Delta E=-(x_k'-x_k)\sum_{j\ne k}w_{kj}x_j\leq0$}

For symmetric zero-diagonal weights and zero thresholds, update one binary unit to the sign of its current field, retaining it at a zero field. The displayed energy change is negative on every actual flip. There are finitely many states, so a fair asynchronous update schedule eventually reaches a fixed point that is a minimum against single-unit flips. Synchronous updates can instead cycle, and local minima need not be global minima.

To obtain the displayed identity, subtract the two <energies>. Terms not involving $x_k$ cancel, and symmetry combines the two copies of each pair. With thresholds included the result is $\Delta E=-(x_k'-x_k)h_k$. The zero-threshold expression above is its specialization. At $h_k=0$, keeping the old state rules out motion along equal-<energy> states. Finiteness then excludes infinitely many strict decreases, while fairness ensures that an unstable unit cannot be ignored forever. A <local minimum> here means no single flip lowers <energy>; equality directions can remain.

The asynchronous condition matters even for two units: with $w_{12}=w_{21}=1$ and zero thresholds, simultaneous updates alternate $(1,-1)$ and $(-1,1)$ forever. Either single-unit update instead reaches an aligned stable state.

= Hebbian memory weights for a Hopfield network
{c}
{parent=Hopfield network}
{title2=$w_{ij}=M^{-1}\sum_\mu\xi_i^\mu\xi_j^\mu,\quad i\ne j$}

For $M$ binary units and desired patterns $\xi^\mu$, use these symmetric weights and set $w_{ii}=0$. For one pattern, the field at that pattern is $(M-1)\xi_i/M$, so it is stable. Several patterns add cross-talk terms; storage by this rule alone does not guarantee stability of every arbitrary collection of patterns.

Writing $m_\mu=M^{-1}\sum_j\xi_j^\mu x_j$ gives
$$
\sum_{j\ne i}w_{ij}x_j=\sum_\mu\xi_i^\mu m_\mu-\frac{P}{M}x_i,
$$
for $P$ stored patterns. The subtraction removes the forbidden self-couplings. It makes explicit how the desired association competes with all other stored patterns. An unintended stable state can therefore arise even though every update obeys <asynchronous Hopfield energy descent>.

= Hopfield recall from a corrupted cue
{c}
{parent=Hebbian memory weights for a Hopfield network}
{title2=$E(d)=-((M-2d)^2-M)/(2M)$}

Store only $\xi=(1,-1,1,-1,1)$ with $M=5$, zero thresholds, and <Hebbian memory weights for a Hopfield network>. If $d$ bits differ from $\xi$, their <Hamming distance> is $d$ and $\sum_i\xi_ix_i=M-2d$. Squaring this sum and subtracting the $M$ diagonal terms yields the displayed <energy>. Thus $d=0,1,2,3,4,5$ gives $E=-2,-2/5,2/5,2/5,-2/5,-2$.

For the cue $x=(-1,1,1,-1,1)$, the first unit has input $h_1=2/5$ and flips to $1$. The second then has $h_2=-4/5$ and flips to $-1$, completing recall. The <energy> drops $2/5\to-2/5\to-2$. Every cue with at most two wrong bits is corrected under a fair asynchronous schedule: a wrong bit sees the correct majority among the other four, while a correct bit either sees agreement or retains its value at a tie. The opposite pattern $-\xi$ is also stored implicitly, so cues with at least three errors retrieve it. This is a limitation of this symmetric one-pattern construction.

= Boltzmann machine
{c}
{parent=Neural network}
{wiki}
{title2=$p_\beta(x)=Z^{-1}e^{-\beta E(x)}$}

A <Boltzmann machine> is a stochastic <neural network> that learns a <probability distribution> specified by pairwise <energies>. To compare it directly with a <Hopfield network>, use the bipolar <energy> $E(x)=-\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}x_ix_j+\sum_i\theta_ix_i$. Here $\beta>0$ controls the preference for low <energy>, and the <partition function> $Z=\sum_xe^{-\beta E(x)}$ normalizes all states. In a computational model, $\beta$ is an algorithmic inverse <temperature>; interpreting it as $1/(k_BT)$ additionally requires physical <energy> units.

A random single-unit <Gibbs sampling> update has <conditional probability>
$$
p_\beta(x_k=1\mid x_{-k})=\frac1{1+e^{-2\beta h_k}},\qquad h_k=\sum_{j\ne k}w_{kj}x_j-\theta_k.
$$
Indeed, the two possible <energies> differ by $-2h_k$; their normalized <Boltzmann weights> give this <logistic function>. Random selection of one unit at a time satisfies <detailed balance>. For finite weights and finite positive $\beta$, all conditional probabilities lie strictly between zero and one, so the finite <Markov chain> can reach every state and can remain in place. Its unique <stationary distribution> is $p_\beta$. A short run need not yet sample that distribution accurately.

As $\beta\to\infty$, a nonzero field selects its sign, approaching <asynchronous Hopfield energy descent>. At zero field the stochastic rule remains a fair coin, whereas the deterministic convention above retains the old value. At finite inverse <temperature>, uphill moves permit exploration beyond one <local minimum>. Recall and learning are different operations: sampling changes the units at fixed weights; learning changes the weights to fit the <training data>.

= Positive and negative phases of Boltzmann learning
{parent=Boltzmann machine}
{title2=$\partial_{w_{ij}}\ell=\beta(\langle x_ix_j\rangle_+-\langle x_ix_j\rangle_-)$}

Split the units into <visible units> $v$, whose patterns occur in the <training data>, and <hidden units> $z$. Let $q(v)$ be the empirical <probability distribution> and maximize the average <log-likelihood> $\ell=\sum_vq(v)\log p_\beta(v)$, where $p_\beta(v)=\sum_zp_\beta(v,z)$. The positive phase clamps $v$ to data and averages over $q(v)p_\beta(z\mid v)$; the negative phase releases all units and averages over $p_\beta(v,z)$. The displayed difference uses these two <expectations>. When both units are visible, the positive pair product is supplied directly by the data.

For a proof, write $\log p_\beta(v)=\log\sum_z e^{-\beta E(v,z)}-\log Z$. Differentiating each finite sum gives, respectively, $-\beta\langle\partial E\rangle_{z\mid v}$ and $-\beta\langle\partial E\rangle_{v,z}$. Since $\partial E/\partial w_{ij}=-x_ix_j$, subtraction proves the rule. Similarly $\partial_{\theta_i}\ell=-\beta(\langle x_i\rangle_+-\langle x_i\rangle_-)$. A small positive step along these <gradients> increases <log-likelihood>. The negative term suppresses associations overproduced by the model; strengthening observed pairs alone misses this correction. The identity requires equilibrium <expectations>; estimates from insufficiently mixed sampling can give a biased update.

= Visible unit
{parent=Positive and negative phases of Boltzmann learning}

A <visible unit> represents an observed coordinate of the data. Clamping it means holding that coordinate fixed while other units update. In a <Boltzmann machine>, <hidden units> are unobserved coordinates summed out to obtain the visible <marginal distribution>.

= Two-spin Boltzmann learning
{parent=Boltzmann machine}
{title2=$Z(J)=4\cosh J,\quad\ell'(J)=1/2-\tanh J$}

Take two visible bipolar units, no <hidden units>, zero thresholds, $\beta=1$, and $E_J(x)=-Jx_1x_2$. As original <training data>, assign probabilities $3/8$ each to $(+,+)$ and $(-,-)$ and $1/8$ each to $(+,-)$ and $(-,+)$. The data pair product averages to $1/2$. Enumeration of all four states gives two weights $e^J$ and two $e^{-J}$, hence the <partition function> $Z(J)=4\cosh J$ and
$$
\langle x_1x_2\rangle_-=\tanh J,\qquad
\ell(J)=\frac J2-\log(4\cosh J).
$$
Starting at $J=0$, the positive-minus-negative difference is $1/2-0$. A <gradient ascent> step of size $1/2$ gives $J=1/4$. Each aligned state now has <probability> $0.311230$ and each opposed state $0.188770$, bringing the model toward the data. The <log-likelihood> increases from $-1.386294$ to $-1.292224$. Solving $\ell'(J)=0$ gives $J_*=(\log3)/2$, which reproduces the data exactly. Since $\ell''(J)=-1/\cosh^2J<0$, this is the unique maximum. These are exact enumerations and their decimal evaluations, with no sampling or laboratory measurements.

= Boltzmann encoder demonstration
{c}
{parent=Boltzmann machine}

<David H. Ackley>, <Geoffrey Hinton> and <Terrence J. Sejnowski> tested their 1985 algorithm on a simulated 4–2–4 encoder. Two four-unit visible groups communicated through two <hidden units>, with no direct connections between the groups. Preparation set weights to zero and supplied corresponding one-active-unit patterns. Noisy clamping and annealed settling generated pair-coactivation readouts, compared with freely running readouts to choose weight changes. Learned hidden codes were the outcome. Equal sampling effort in clamped and free conditions supplied the comparison; noise limited weight growth. Finite settling was assumed to reach equilibrium, limiting the evidence for exact <likelihood> learning.<A Learning Algorithm for Boltzmann Machines by David H. Ackley and others>[1985, sec. 4 and sec. 4.1, pp. 157–160, Figs. 2–3; finite settling assumption p. 159]{ref}

= Feedforward neural network
{parent=Neural network}
{wiki}

A feedforward neural network composes layers without directed cycles, passing each layer's activations only to later layers.

= Multilayer perceptron
{parent=Feedforward neural network}

= MLP
{c}
{synonym}

A layered <neural network> with <affine maps> and <activation functions>, usually with dense connections between successive layers. A dense layer with $n$ inputs and $m$ outputs has $m(n+1)$ parameters including biases.

= Four-input parity network
{parent=Feedforward neural network}

Let $S$ be the number of active inputs. Four <hidden units> $h_m=H(S-m+1/2)$, $m=1,2,3,4$, detect successively larger counts. A <binary threshold unit> with weights $(1,-1,1,-1)$ on these features and threshold $1/2$ returns one precisely for $S=1,3$. Each hidden input weight is one. This computes parity with a single hidden layer, but representability does not guarantee easy learning by <backpropagation>.

= Hidden unit
{parent=Feedforward neural network}
{title2=$z_j=g_j(\sum_iw_{ij}z_i)$}

A <hidden unit> transforms input information before the final output rather than directly representing a supplied target. Nonlinear hidden features can make a classification linearly separable in feature space even when it is not separable in the original input coordinates.

= Backpropagation
{parent=Feedforward neural network}
{wiki}

Backpropagation applies the <chain rule> from a neural network's output toward its input to compute the gradient of a loss with respect to every parameter.

= Backpropagation through time
{parent=Backpropagation}
{title2=$\partial h_t/\partial h_{t-1}$}

= BPTT
{c}
{synonym}

Apply the chain rule to a recurrent network unrolled over its input sequence, adding contributions to parameters reused at multiple time steps. Products of state Jacobians convey effects from earlier states to later losses. This can compute exact <gradients> for the chosen finite unrolling, while physiological availability of the same history and error signals is a separate question.

= Feedback alignment
{parent=Backpropagation}
{title2=$\delta^{h}=(B\delta^{o})\odot\sigma^{\prime}$}

A learning scheme replacing a transposed forward-weight matrix in the teaching signal with a separate fixed random matrix. Simulations show that adaptation of the forward weights can make this feedback useful on tested tasks. It need not equal the exact loss <gradient>, and successful simulations alone do not establish the mechanism in the brain.<Random synaptic feedback weights support error backpropagation for deep learning by Timothy P. Lillicrap and others>[Results, Figure 1 and Figure 2; Discussion: random feedback, learning performance and biological limitations]{ref}

= Weight transport problem
{parent=Backpropagation}

Exact <backpropagation> computes hidden teaching signals using the transpose of the current forward weight matrix. Explaining how a biological feedback pathway could know or maintain those weights is the <weight transport problem>. Useful approximate error correction need not satisfy exact reciprocal weight equality.<Random synaptic feedback weights support error backpropagation for deep learning by Timothy P. Lillicrap and others>[Results, Figure 1 and Figure 2; Discussion: random feedback, learning performance and biological limitations]{ref}

= Squared-error backpropagation
{parent=Backpropagation}

For squared loss $E=\sum_k(t_k-z_k)^2/2$, let $e_k=(t_k-z_k)g_k'(x_k)$ and $e_j=g_j'(x_j)\sum_kw_{jk}e_k$. The <chain rule> gives $\partial E/\partial w_{jk}=-e_kz_j$ and $\partial E/\partial w_{ij}=-e_jz_i$. A <gradient descent> step of size $\eta$ uses $\Delta w_{jk}=\eta e_kz_j$ and $\Delta w_{ij}=\eta e_jz_i$, with every derivative evaluated at the same pre-update weights.

= Sigmoid-softmax network gradients
{parent=Backpropagation}

For hidden activations $h_j=\sigma(b_j+\sum_rW_{jr}x_r)$ and class probabilities $p_c=\operatorname{softmax}_c(a+Vh)$, a one-hot label $t$ has <log-likelihood> $\ell=\sum_ct_c\log p_c$. Put $\delta_c=t_c-p_c$ and $\Delta_j=h_j(1-h_j)\sum_cV_{cj}\delta_c$. Then
$$
\partial_{a_c}\ell=\delta_c,\quad\partial_{V_{cj}}\ell=\delta_ch_j,\quad\partial_{b_j}\ell=\Delta_j,\quad\partial_{W_{jr}}\ell=\Delta_jx_r.
$$
These follow from the <chain rule> and give single-observation <stochastic gradient descent> updates for the negative <log-likelihood>.

= Sigmoid function
{parent=Neural network}
{wiki}

The sigmoid function
$$
\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}
$$
is a smooth activation function with derivative $\sigma'(x)=\sigma(x)(1-\sigma(x))$.

= Logistic function
{parent=Sigmoid function}
{title2=$s(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$}

The standard logistic function maps a <log odds> $z$ to probability $1/(1+e^{-z})$. More generally, its translated and scaled form is $L/(1+e^{-k(x-x_0)})$.

The rectified linear unit activation is $\operatorname{ReLU}(x)=\max(x,0)$.

The Gaussian error linear unit activation is $\operatorname{GELU}(x)=x\Phi(x)$, where $\Phi$ is the <standard normal distribution function>.

= Softmax function
{parent=Neural network}
{wiki}

For logits $z_1,\ldots,z_K$, the softmax probabilities are $p_k=e^{z_k}/\sum_je^{z_j}$.

= Choice-value scale non-identifiability
{parent=Softmax function}

If $P(a)=\exp(\beta Q(a))/\sum_b\exp(\beta Q(b))$, then for every $c>0$, replacing $Q$ by $cQ$ and $\beta$ by $\beta/c$ leaves each exponent unchanged. The same choices therefore cannot identify both an unrestricted value scale and inverse temperature. Fixing a value scale or adding independent measurements is needed to break this equivalence.

= Softmax non-identifiability
{parent=Softmax function}

Adding the same scalar-valued function of the input to every class logit leaves all softmax probabilities unchanged. A reference-class constraint removes this common-shift non-identifiability.

= Categorical cross-entropy loss
{parent=Softmax function}

For one-hot label $y$ and predicted class probabilities $p$, categorical cross-entropy is $-\sum_ky_k\log p_k$, the negative categorical log-likelihood.

= Overfitting
{parent=Statistical learning}
{wiki}

Overfitting occurs when further adaptation improves <training error> while degrading performance on new data, for example by fitting incidental disturbances instead of a stable <regression function>. A gap between <training error> and <test error> alone does not prove that a simpler rule would generalize better. Compare candidates using <cross-validation> or a <validation set>; reserve the <test set> for assessment. <Regularization> and <early stopping> can limit adaptation. https://web.stanford.edu/~hastie/ISLR2/Slides/Ch2_Statistical_Learning.pdf[ISLR chapter 2 slides, assessing model accuracy]{ref}

Evaluate the <training error> example at $z_j=-1+2(j+1/2)/400$, $j=0,\ldots,399$, none coinciding with a training input. The mean squared discrepancy $400^{-1}\sum_j(\widehat f(z_j)-f(z_j))^2$ is $0.373259$, $0.000255$, and $0.006319$ for degrees 1, 2, and 7. Thus degree 7 improves <training error> but worsens recovery of the target relative to degree 2. Degree 1 misses the target's curvature, illustrating <underfitting>.

These are errors against a known noiseless target, not <test errors> against fresh noisy responses. In a separate noiseless control, degrees 2 and 7 both recover this target exactly; their numerical held-out errors are approximately $3.77\times10^{-31}$ and $3.72\times10^{-31}$. The fixed-disturbance simulation illustrates a mechanism, not a universal numerical benchmark or a claim that higher <polynomial degree> always harms <generalization>.

= Underfitting
{parent=Overfitting}

Underfitting occurs when a prediction rule fails to capture useful structure in the target relation. A straight-line <polynomial regression> fitted to a genuinely quadratic noiseless target illustrates a representational limitation. Poor prediction alone does not identify the cause: compare candidate rules using <cross-validation> or a <validation set>, while keeping the final <test set> reserved for assessment.

= Regularization
{parent=Statistical learning}
{wiki}

Regularization restricts or penalizes model fitting to improve generalization.

= Penalized least squares
{parent=Regularization}

= Penalised least squares
{synonym}

For $\lambda\geq0$, a full-column-rank <design matrix> $B$, and a <positive semidefinite matrix> $\Omega$, minimize $\|y-B\beta\|^2+\lambda\beta^T\Omega\beta$. Differentiation gives $(B^TB+\lambda\Omega)\widehat\beta=B^Ty$. The fitted-value matrix is $B(B^TB+\lambda\Omega)^{-1}B^T$, whose trace measures the <effective degrees of freedom>.

= Penalized least-squares estimator
{parent=Penalized least squares}
{title2=$\widehat\theta\in\arg\min_\theta\{\|y-X\theta\|_2^2/n+\lambda J(\theta)\}$}

A penalized least-squares estimator balances squared error with a nonnegative penalty $J$. The <Lasso> uses the coefficient $\ell_1$ <norm>; <graph total variation denoising> uses the $\ell_1$ <norm> of endpoint differences. The choice of normalization of the squared loss changes the numerical tuning parameter.

= Basic inequality for a penalized least-squares estimator
{parent=Penalized least-squares estimator}
{title2=$\|h\|_2^2/n\leq2\langle z,h\rangle/n+\lambda J(\theta^*)-\lambda J(\widehat\theta)$}

For the identity design and $y=\theta^*+z$, comparing the minimizing objective at $\widehat\theta$ with its value at $\theta^*$ and expanding the squared <Euclidean norm> proves the displayed inequality, with $h=\widehat\theta-\theta^*$. This deterministic inequality does not assume any <probability distribution> for the noise. A bound on the noise inner product turns it into a statistical error estimate.

= Roughness penalty
{parent=Regularization}

A <roughness penalty> penalizes variation of derivatives, such as $\int(m^{\prime\prime})^2$. The second-derivative penalty vanishes precisely on <affine functions> and is represented in a chosen basis by a <roughness penalty matrix>.

= Early stopping
{parent=Regularization}
{wiki}

Early stopping halts iterative fitting near the best validation performance, limiting adaptation to training noise.
