= Nuclear magnetic resonance
{wiki}

= NMR
{c}
{synonym}

= NMR spectroscopy
{c}
{synonym}

<Nuclear magnetic resonance> detects the response of <atomic nuclei> with a <magnetic moment> to a <radio frequency> <magnetic field> in a steady <magnetic field>. Its striking possibility is to measure a nuclear property through an ordinary electrical <signal> from a bulk sample: an enormous number of tiny <magnetic moments> can produce a measurable change in a receiving circuit.

For a nucleus with <spin angular momentum> $\mathbf J$, define its <gyromagnetic ratio> by $\boldsymbol\mu=\gamma\mathbf J$. Throughout this article $\gamma$ is angular, measured in radians per second per tesla; $\gamma/(2\pi)$ is the corresponding value in cycles per second per tesla. With $\mathbf B_0=B_0\hat{\mathbf z}$, the <Hamiltonian> is $H=-\gamma B_0J_z$. Adjacent <energy levels> differ by
$$
\Delta E=\hbar|\gamma|B_0,\qquad \omega_0=|\gamma|B_0,\qquad f_0=\frac{|\gamma|B_0}{2\pi}.
$$
Thus a measured resonance <frequency> and a calibrated <magnetic field> determine $|\gamma|$. Converting that into a <nuclear magnetic moment> also requires the <spin> quantum number: the maximum projection has magnitude $|\gamma|\hbar I$. This simple experiment does not by itself determine the sign of $\gamma$.

The <Rabi molecular beam magnetic resonance experiment> detects transitions through changes in a selected <molecular beam>. The bulk experiments below instead detect electrical consequences of nuclear <magnetization> in matter at ordinary density. A small <Boltzmann distribution> population imbalance is multiplied by a very large number of nuclei. For noninteracting <spin one-half> nuclei of number density $n$ and positive $\gamma$, their equilibrium <magnetization> is
$$
M_0=n\frac{\gamma\hbar}{2}\tanh\!\left(\frac{\gamma\hbar B_0}{2k_{\rm B}T}\right).
$$
This follows by weighting the two projections $\pm\gamma\hbar/2$ with their <Boltzmann distribution> probabilities. It explains both the smallness of the bulk polarization at ordinary <temperature> and the value of many nuclei contributing to one receiving circuit.

<NMR spectroscopy> measures resonant responses of <atomic nuclei> to applied <electromagnetic radiation> in a <magnetic field>. In <residual-group monitoring of polyurethane formation>, distinguishable signal <integrals> supply a relative residual-group readout; overlap or very weak signals can limit that inference.

= Weak coupling condition for two NMR spins
{parent=NMR scalar coupling}
{title2=$|\omega_1-\omega_2|\gg|J|$}

For the two-<spin> <Hamiltonian operator> $H=(\omega_1ZI+\omega_2IZ+J(XX+YY+ZZ))/2$, juxtaposition of two <Pauli matrices> denotes their <tensor product>. Put $\Delta=\omega_1-\omega_2$. The exchange matrix element between $|01\rangle$ and $|10\rangle$ is $J$, whereas their diagonal separation is $\Delta$. <Nondegenerate perturbation theory> therefore gives admixtures $\pm J/\Delta$ and second-order frequency shifts $\pm J^2/\Delta$. Neglecting the exchange terms is justified to leading order when $|J/\Delta|\ll1$. A large common <Zeeman energy> is insufficient. To approximate accumulated phases by this leading-order <Hamiltonian operator>, one also needs $J^2t/|\Delta|\ll1$ over the observation time, or must include the second-order shifts.

For fixed nonidentical <chemical shifts>, increasing the static <magnetic field> increases $|\Delta|$ in angular-frequency units while the <NMR scalar coupling> changes little. If $\Delta=0$ remains exact, the approximation fails at every nonzero $J$.

= Secular approximation for scalar-coupled spins
{parent=Weak coupling condition for two NMR spins}
{title2=$H_{\mathrm{eff}}=(\omega_1ZI+\omega_2IZ+JZZ)/2$}

The leading-order averaging of rapidly oscillating exchange terms in the <interaction picture> of two nuclear <Zeeman energy> terms. For $\Delta=\omega_1-\omega_2$,
$$
V_{\mathrm{int}}(t)=J\left(e^{i\Delta t}|01\rangle\langle10|+e^{-i\Delta t}|10\rangle\langle01|\right).
$$
Their first-order time integrals are bounded by $2|J/\Delta|$, so they contribute little when $|\Delta|\gg|J|$, while the stationary <Ising coupling> $JZZ/2$ survives. Higher orders give frequency shifts of order $J^2/\Delta$. This approximation is controlled by the difference of resonance frequencies, not their common magnitude.

= Inversion recovery

An ideal resonant inversion pulse reverses the longitudinal magnetization. Subsequent <spin-lattice relaxation> gives the displayed recovery law. <Magnetic resonance imaging> with a prepared nuclear population imbalance exceeding ordinary thermal polarization under the imaging conditions. <Dynamic nuclear polarization>, optical spin transfer and spin-order transfer offer different preparation routes. Preparation and acquisition must account for <spin-lattice relaxation>, which erases the excess polarization.

= Residual dipolar coupling

= Chemical exchange in NMR
{parent=Nuclear magnetic resonance}
{c}

<Chemical exchange in NMR> interconverts environments during the observation. Fast exchange relative to their frequency difference produces averaged shifts; slow exchange resolves separate sites. A single peak alone does not prove a single static geometry.

= Fast exchange limit in NMR
{parent=Chemical exchange in NMR}

Exchange much faster than the resonance frequency difference gives one population-weighted resonance. The averaging does not prove a single static geometry.

= Boron-11 nuclear magnetic resonance
{parent=Nuclear magnetic resonance}
{title2=$^{11}\mathrm B\ \mathrm{NMR}$}

<Boron-11 nuclear magnetic resonance> probes boron environments. Rapid exchange can average inequivalent sites into one resonance; slow exchange reveals distinct sites. Quadrupolar relaxation and line broadening can complicate integration.

= Phosphorus-31 nuclear magnetic resonance
{parent=Nuclear magnetic resonance}
{title2=$^{31}\mathrm P\ \mathrm{NMR}$}

<Phosphorus-31 nuclear magnetic resonance> observes spin-one-half phosphorus-31. An $A_2X$ system can show a two-phosphorus doublet and a one-phosphorus triplet; three inequivalent mutually coupled phosphorus sites can each give a doublet of doublets in the first-order limit.

= Proton nuclear magnetic resonance
{parent=Nuclear magnetic resonance}
{title2=$^1\mathrm H\ \mathrm{NMR}$}

<Proton nuclear magnetic resonance> measures resonances of hydrogen-1 nuclei. Structural signal counts require an explicit <symmetry> and exchange-timescale model; exchangeable N–H or O–H signals can be broadened or lost.

= Fluorine-19 NMR
{parent=Nuclear magnetic resonance}

<NMR> observation of spin-$\tfrac12$ fluorine-19 nuclei. Coupling to nearby <protons> can split a resonance unless <proton decoupling> is applied.

= Carbon-13 NMR
{parent=Nuclear magnetic resonance}

<NMR> observation of spin-$\tfrac12$ <carbon-13> nuclei. Routine spectra often use <proton decoupling>, but this does not automatically decouple other spin-active nuclei.

= Attached proton test
{parent=Carbon-13 NMR}

An NMR multiplicity-editing experiment in which carbon signals are separated by the parity of their attached proton count: CH and CH3 have one relative phase, while CH2 and nonprotonated carbons have the other. The absolute up/down convention depends on processing and is not a universal assignment.

= Proton decoupling
{parent=Nuclear magnetic resonance}

Irradiation that removes resolved <scalar spin-spin coupling> to <protons> from another nucleus’s spectrum. Other couplings, such as carbon–fluorine coupling in a proton-decoupled <carbon-13 NMR> spectrum, can remain.

= NMR signal integral
{parent=Nuclear magnetic resonance}
{c}

The area of a resonance or multiplet, proportional to the contributing nuclear population under suitable excitation, relaxation and acquisition conditions. Relative proton integrals can count chemically distinct <protons>.

= Scalar spin-spin coupling
{parent=Nuclear magnetic resonance}

Through-bond interaction between nuclear <spins> that splits <NMR> resonances. In the weak-coupling limit, $n$ equivalent spin-$\tfrac12$ neighbours produce $n+1$ lines with <binomial coefficient> intensities. Equivalent nuclei do not split each other.

= Acetal-alkyne long-range coupling
{parent=Scalar spin-spin coupling}

<Proton> coupling across H-C-C≡C-H can resolve a small four-bond splitting. In a propargyl <acetal> this connects the <acetal> methine and terminal <alkyne> <proton>.

= Vinylic scalar coupling
{parent=Scalar spin-spin coupling}

Through-bond spin coupling involving vinylic nuclei. The geometry of a vicinal pathway across an alkene distinguishes cis and trans coupling.

= Cis vinylic coupling
{parent=Vinylic scalar coupling}

<vicinal coupling> between <protons> on the same side of an alkene. A measured first-order splitting is an approximation if the spin system is appreciably strongly coupled.

= Trans vinylic coupling
{parent=Vinylic scalar coupling}

<vicinal coupling> between <protons> on opposite sides of an alkene. In many ordinary terminal alkenes its magnitude exceeds the corresponding cis coupling.

= NMR multiplet
{parent=Scalar spin-spin coupling}
{c}

A set of <resonance> lines arising from resolved spin coupling. Weak-coupling patterns can be assigned through coupling paths and line intensities; line count alone can be ambiguous.

= NMR sextet
{parent=NMR multiplet}
{c}

An approximately six-line scalar-coupling pattern. Similar coupling to five spin-one-half partners can mimic a sextet even when those partners are not all chemically equivalent.

= Quartet of quartets
{parent=NMR multiplet}

A first-order <NMR multiplet> produced by coupling to two distinct sets of three equivalent spin-one-half nuclei. Sixteen lines are possible; equal couplings can collapse the pattern to a septet.

= Doublet of quartets
{parent=NMR multiplet}

A first-order <NMR multiplet> split into a doublet by one spin-one-half nucleus and into quartets by three equivalent spin-one-half nuclei. Ideally it has eight lines; overlap and second-order behavior can change its appearance.

= Doublet of doublets of triplets
{parent=NMR multiplet}

A first-order <NMR multiplet> split successively by two distinct spin-one-half couplings and by two equivalent spin-one-half nuclei. Ideally it has twelve resolved lines before coincidences or further unresolved couplings.

= NMR septet
{parent=NMR multiplet}
{c}

A seven-line <first-order NMR multiplet>. Coupling to six equivalent spin-one-half neighbours gives the intensity ratio $1:6:15:20:15:6:1$.

= NMR quartet
{parent=NMR multiplet}
{c}

A four-line first-order pattern, often from three equivalent spin-one-half neighbours, with intensities 1:3:3:1. Equal couplings to different groups can mimic it.

= NMR triplet
{parent=NMR multiplet}
{c}

A three-line first-order pattern, often from two equivalent spin-one-half neighbours, with intensities 1:2:1.

= NMR doublet
{parent=NMR multiplet}
{c}

A pair of lines with the first-order splitting expected for one spin-one-half neighbour. Additional unresolved or coincident interactions can complicate the pattern.

= NMR singlet
{parent=NMR multiplet}
{c}

A <resonance> with no resolved scalar splitting under the acquisition conditions. This does not prove that every coupling is exactly zero.

= Long-range scalar coupling
{parent=Scalar spin-spin coupling}

Through-bond coupling over a longer path, often four or more bonds. Small resolved allylic couplings can split alkene and oxygen-bound methylene signals and should not be discarded merely because vicinal couplings are larger.

= Allylic scalar coupling
{parent=Long-range scalar coupling}

Coupling across an allylic four-bond path, such as between an oxygen-bound CH2 group and terminal vinyl hydrogens in an allyl ester.

= First-order NMR multiplet
{parent=Scalar spin-spin coupling}
{c}

The line pattern obtained when chemically distinguishable spin groups can be treated in the weak-coupling approximation. For an observed spin, each coupled neighbour contributes a frequency displacement of plus or minus half the relevant coupling; coincident lines add intensities.

= Doublet of triplets
{parent=First-order NMR multiplet}

A first-order pattern combining a doublet splitting with a triplet splitting, commonly from one neighbour and a pair of equivalent neighbours.

= Doublet of doublets
{parent=First-order NMR multiplet}

Splitting by two distinguishable spin-one-half neighbours with different resolved coupling magnitudes. The four line positions are displaced from the centre by every choice of plus or minus half of each coupling.

= Apparent NMR quartet
{parent=First-order NMR multiplet}

Four equally spaced lines can arise from three equivalent neighbours or from coincident couplings to different groups. Combining a doublet with a triplet of the same spacing gives intensities 1:3:3:1; line count alone does not identify a unique topology.

A <Karplus equation> relates a vicinal scalar coupling to molecular torsion, often represented as $J=A\cos^2\phi+B\cos\phi+C$. Parameters depend on the chemical environment. A mobile molecule gives a population average of couplings, which is generally not the coupling evaluated at its mean angle.

<NMR relaxation dispersion> measures how an observed nuclear-spin relaxation rate changes with pulse timing or effective field conditions. = Chemical shift anisotropy

= Bloch nuclear induction experiment
{c}
{parent=Nuclear magnetic resonance}

In their 1946 letter, <Felix Bloch>, <William Webster Hansen>, and <Martin Packard> reported <water> at room <temperature>. A steady <magnetic field> along $z$ and an oscillating field along $x$ produced a <signal> in a pickup coil along $y$, displayed on an <oscilloscope>. Some samples included paramagnetic additives to accelerate <thermal equilibrium>. Resonance fields at two <frequencies> gave the same field-to-frequency ratio, consistent with the <proton> value.<Nuclear Induction by Felix Bloch and others>[p. 127, Nuclear Induction, entire letter; geometry, water, additives, readout and two-frequency check]{ref}

The detailed paper describes glass <water> bulbs inside tuned receiving coils. Transmitter coils, shields and a conducting paddle controlled leakage. Field modulation at 60 Hz drove the horizontal <oscilloscope> sweep. A <diode> and audio amplification supplied the vertical trace. Vibration changed coupling: their largest difficulty. Coil tuning capacitors formed tank circuits. The actual receiver omitted the conceptual diagram's radiofrequency <amplifier>.<The Nuclear Induction Experiment by Felix Bloch and others>[pp. 475–480, Figs. 1–4, APPARATUS and opening RESULTS; actual diode receiver versus conceptual amplifier]{ref}

The orthogonal coil axes suppress direct transmitter pickup, but do not eliminate it. A field-dependent <signal> at the nuclear resonance must be distinguished from leakage and <noise>; merely detecting a radiofrequency voltage is insufficient. This is why the field sweep and the independently checked field-to-frequency ratio matter.

= Purcell nuclear resonance absorption experiment
{c}
{parent=Nuclear magnetic resonance}

<Edward Mills Purcell>, <Henry Cutler Torrey>, and <Robert Vivian Pound> placed <paraffin wax> in a capacitively loaded coaxial cavity near 30 MHz between magnet poles. Coupling loops supplied and extracted power; oscillating and steady fields were perpendicular. Output balanced against a generator reference fed amplification, detection and a microammeter. A slow field sweep revealed absorption above fluctuations at room <temperature>. Weak drive avoided population equalization. Calibration was rough; field inhomogeneity could contribute to linewidth.<Resonance Absorption by Nuclear Magnetic Moments in a Solid by Edward Mills Purcell and others>[pp. 37–38, apparatus and results; weak drive, balanced cavity output, microammeter, field sweep, calibration and linewidth limitations]{ref}

These are driven experiments with continuous radiofrequency excitation and changing static field, not demonstrations of modern pulsed <Fourier transform> <nuclear magnetic resonance>. Bloch's theoretical paper explicitly distinguishes proposed free precession after a pulse from the forced precession actually studied.<Nuclear Induction by Felix Bloch>[pp. 460–461, introduction: proposed pulse/free-precession experiment versus performed forced-precession experiment]{ref}

An experimental failure need not mean absent <nuclear magnetic moments>. In the model below, excessive drive reduces the population imbalance through <nuclear magnetic resonance saturation>; waiting too briefly can leave insufficient polarization. Separately, ordinary electrical losses can cause heating without identifying a nuclear resonance. The absorption experiment measured a balanced electrical <signal>, rather than using heating as its readout. Useful diagnostic checks include moving off resonance, changing the frequency, checking field uniformity and reducing drive. These are general checks; the historical operations actually documented are specified above.

= Bloch equations
{c}
{parent=Nuclear magnetic resonance}
{wiki}
{title2=$\dot{\mathbf M}=\gamma\mathbf M\times\mathbf B-\mathbf M_\perp/T_2-(M_z-M_0)\hat{\mathbf z}/T_1$}

The <Bloch equations> describe an ensemble <magnetization> $\mathbf M$ with <Larmor precession> plus phenomenological recovery and decay:
$$
\frac{d\mathbf M}{dt}=\gamma\mathbf M\times\mathbf B
-\frac{M_x\hat{\mathbf x}+M_y\hat{\mathbf y}}{T_2}
-\frac{M_z-M_0}{T_1}\hat{\mathbf z}.
$$
Here $T_1,T_2>0$ are model parameters, not a microscopic explanation of relaxation. The precession term follows from the torque on a <magnetic moment>; the two dissipative terms prescribe <exponential decay>. Bloch explicitly introduced this macroscopic relaxation description as a simplifying approximation.<Nuclear Induction by Felix Bloch>[§4, pp. 467–468, equations (32), (37) and (38): longitudinal recovery and assumed exponential transverse decay]{ref}

For positive $\gamma$ and no transverse drive, $M_x+iM_y$ rotates as $e^{-i\gamma B_0t}$: its signed rotation is negative about $z$, although the resonance <angular frequency> $\omega_0=\gamma B_0$ is positive. This distinction prevents a hidden sign change when choosing a <rotating frame>.

= Longitudinal nuclear spin relaxation
{parent=Bloch equations}
{title2=$T_1$}

With no transverse drive, the <Bloch equations> give
$$
M_z(t)=M_0+[M_z(0)-M_0]e^{-t/T_1}.
$$
<Longitudinal nuclear spin relaxation> restores the population imbalance and thus the <magnetization> parallel to the static <magnetic field>. It corresponds to <population relaxation> for the two-level model. Starting from $M_z(0)=0$, waiting $T_1$ recovers $1-e^{-1}$ of $M_0$, whereas waiting $5T_1$ recovers about $99.3\%$. This is why repeated measurements require a recovery-time decision.

= Transverse nuclear magnetization decay
{parent=Bloch equations}
{title2=$T_2$}

<Transverse relaxation> describes loss of ensemble transverse <magnetization>. It must not automatically be identified with pure <dephasing>. In a simple completely positive two-level relaxation model,
$$
\frac1{T_2}=\frac1{2T_1}+\frac1{T_\phi},
$$
where $T_\phi$ describes additional pure <dephasing>. Thus population recovery itself contributes to transverse decay. A spread of static <magnetic fields> can further cancel ensemble phases without the same microscopic relaxation; an observed free signal may have an effective $T_2^*$ rather than homogeneous $T_2$. The simple exponential examples below assume a uniform field and a single homogeneous $T_2$.

= Free induction decay
{parent=Nuclear magnetic resonance}
{wiki}
{title2=$S(t)=S_0e^{-t/T_2}e^{-i\Delta t}$}

<Free induction decay> is a receiving <signal> from freely precessing transverse <magnetization> after excitation ends. <Faraday's law of induction> gives coil voltage proportional to the time derivative of linked <magnetic flux>, with sign set by the winding. At $\omega_0T_2\gg1$ its envelope follows the transverse <magnetization>; a fixed receiver phase can absorb the derivative's near-quadrature shift. We define the calibrated complex <signal> below to represent <magnetization>, not raw coil voltage.

For an original teaching model, take positive $\gamma$, a circular drive $B_1(\cos\omega t\,\hat{\mathbf x}-\sin\omega t\,\hat{\mathbf y})$, and axes rotating at $-\omega$ about $z$. Define $u+iv=e^{i\omega t}(M_x+iM_y)$, $w=M_z$, $\Delta=\omega_0-\omega$ and $\omega_1=\gamma B_1$. The <Bloch equations> become
$$
\dot u=-u/T_2+\Delta v,\qquad
\dot v=-\Delta u-v/T_2+\omega_1w,\qquad
\dot w=-\omega_1v-(w-M_0)/T_1.
$$
A linearly oscillating field of peak amplitude $2B_1$ has a co-rotating component of amplitude $B_1$; neglecting its counter-rotating component requires $\omega_1\ll\omega_0$ near resonance.

At resonance, a short pulse starting from $(0,0,M_0)$ gives $(u,v,w)=(0,M_0\sin\theta,M_0\cos\theta)$ with $\theta=\gamma B_1\tau$, neglecting relaxation during duration $\tau$. A receiver phase rotation can make a $\pi/2$ pulse's initial transverse <signal> real. After the pulse, direct integration gives
$$
S(t)=S_0e^{-t/T_2}e^{-i\Delta t},\qquad
I(t)=\operatorname{Re}S(t),\qquad Q(t)=\operatorname{Im}S(t).
$$
These are the two components extracted by <quadrature demodulation>. Differentiating verifies $\dot S=-(T_2^{-1}+i\Delta)S$; taking its magnitude separates decay from detuning.

Choose rounded illustrative $\gamma/(2\pi)=42.58\,\mathrm{MHz/T}$, $B_0=0.200\,\mathrm T$, $B_1=10\,\mathrm{\mu T}$, $T_1=0.800\,\mathrm s$ and $T_2=0.120\,\mathrm s$. Then $f_0=8.516\,\mathrm{MHz}$ and the $\pi/2$ duration is $\tau=1/(4(\gamma/(2\pi))B_1)=0.587\,\mathrm{ms}$, short relative to both relaxation times. For receiver detuning $\Delta/(2\pi)=5\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and $S_0=1$, $S(0.050\,\mathrm s)=-0.65924i$ and $S(0.100\,\mathrm s)=-0.43460$. These are calculated examples, not 1946 measurements.

= Continuous-wave nuclear magnetic resonance
{parent=Nuclear magnetic resonance}

For constant drive, set the three <rotating frame> derivatives to zero. Substitution first gives $u=\Delta T_2v$, then $w=M_0-\omega_1T_1v$, hence
$$
v=\frac{\omega_1T_2M_0}{1+\Delta^2T_2^2+s},\qquad
s=\omega_1^2T_1T_2.
$$
The absorption is proportional to $\omega_1v$. With $s\ll1$, its frequency dependence is a <Cauchy distribution> profile, often called a Lorentzian. With appreciable $s$, the response broadens and $w=M_0/(1+s)$ on resonance: <nuclear magnetic resonance saturation> limits the response. The short-pulse amplitude in the previous example is not a weak continuous drive; these are different operating regimes of the same equations.

= Nuclear magnetic resonance saturation
{parent=Continuous-wave nuclear magnetic resonance}
{title2=$s=\gamma^2B_1^2T_1T_2$}

Drive reduces the longitudinal population imbalance when excitation competes with recovery. The dimensionless $s$ in the steady <Bloch equations> measures that competition.

= Nuclear resonance linewidth
{parent=Continuous-wave nuclear magnetic resonance}
{title2=$\Delta f_{\rm FWHM}=1/(\pi T_2)$}

For weak drive in this homogeneous model, half maximum occurs at $|\Delta|=1/T_2$. The absorption <full width at half maximum> is therefore
$$
\Delta\omega_{\rm FWHM}=\frac2{T_2},\qquad
\Delta f_{\rm FWHM}=\frac1{\pi T_2},\qquad
\Delta B_{\rm FWHM}=\frac2{|\gamma|T_2}.
$$
The example gives $2.653\,\mathrm{Hz}$ or $0.06230\,\mathrm{\mu T}$. At finite drive the model width is multiplied by $\sqrt{1+s}$. Field inhomogeneity and unresolved components can also broaden an actual line, so its width alone does not determine pure <dephasing>.

The same width appears by an original <Fourier transform> calculation. For $a=1/T_2$ and a free homogeneous <signal> demodulated exactly at resonance,
$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-at}e^{i\Omega t}\,dt=\frac1{a-i\Omega},\qquad
\operatorname{Re}\frac1{a-i\Omega}=\frac{a}{a^2+\Omega^2}.
$$
This connects exponential memory to a Lorentzian absorptive line. It is a mathematical connection, not evidence that the 1946 teams performed pulsed <Fourier transform> spectroscopy. Instrumental <signal bandwidth> is a separate choice: narrowing a receiver band can reject <thermal electrical noise>, but too narrow a band distorts a changing <signal>. A sample linewidth and a receiver bandwidth are not interchangeable.

= Spin echo
{parent=Nuclear magnetic resonance}
{wiki}
{title2=$90^\circ-\tau-180^\circ-\tau$}

= Hahn echo
{c}
{synonym}

A <spin echo> is a reappearance of transverse <magnetization> after a pulse sequence reverses an earlier spread of <phases>. An ensemble can have almost no net transverse <magnetization> while its components retain enough individual phase memory to reassemble. In the ideal <Hahn echo> below, a short 90° pulse prepares transverse <magnetization>, a 180° pulse follows after time $\tau$, and the <signal> refocuses at $t=2\tau$. The receiver observes freely precessing <magnetization>, as in <free induction decay>; no third driving pulse is needed at the echo time.

The useful distinction is between cancellation in an ensemble average and loss of recoverable phase memory. A static spread of <Larmor precession> <frequencies> can make <free induction decay> much faster than <transverse nuclear magnetization decay>. A <spin echo> can remove that static spread from the echo amplitude, allowing a more selective measurement of <transverse relaxation>. It does not in general reverse <noise>, <population relaxation>, or every interaction between <spins>.

= Static inhomogeneity refocusing
{parent=Spin echo}
{title2=$S(2\tau)=e^{-2\tau/T_2}$}

Use the <rotating frame> and positive <gyromagnetic ratio> convention of <free induction decay>. Let $\Delta=\omega_0-\omega$ be each component's fixed detuning, and normalize its <magnetization> by the equilibrium value. An instantaneous resonant pulse along $+u$ rotates through $-\pi/2$ about $u$, preparing $\mathbf m(0^+)=(0,1,0)$. A second pulse along $+v$ rotates through $-\pi$ about $v$. These ideal pulses require a drive much stronger than the relevant detunings and negligible relaxation during either pulse. The corresponding <rotation matrices>, acting on column <vectors>, are
$$
R_u(-\pi/2)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0&1\\0&-1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
P=R_v(-\pi)=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
F_\Delta(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\Delta t&\sin\Delta t&0\\-\sin\Delta t&\cos\Delta t&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Here $F_\Delta(t)$ is free <Larmor precession> in the <rotating frame>, before including <transverse relaxation>. Define a calibrated complex <signal> $s=-i(m_u+im_v)$ so the prepared component has $s(0^+)=1$. The pulse $P$ sends $m_u+im_v$ to its negative <complex conjugate>, hence sends $s$ to $s^*$. Direct multiplication gives
$$
s_\Delta(t)=\begin{cases}
e^{-t/T_2}e^{-i\Delta t},&0\leq t<\tau,\\
e^{-t/T_2}e^{-i\Delta(t-2\tau)},&t>\tau.
\end{cases}
$$
The <phase> accumulated before the pulse is reflected; the <angular velocity> during subsequent free evolution keeps its sign. At $2\tau$ every fixed detuning has zero residual <phase>. Equivalently, $PF_\Delta(t)P=F_\Delta(-t)$, so $F_\Delta(\tau)PF_\Delta(\tau)=P$. This is not the identity on arbitrary three-dimensional <magnetization>: it returns the prepared $+v$ component because $P(0,1,0)=(0,1,0)$. The two transverse pulse axes specified here also fix the receiver sign of the echo.

Their subsequent free <angular velocities> are unchanged.}

For an original ensemble example, let the static detunings have a <Gaussian distribution> with mean zero and <standard deviation> $\sigma$, independent of a common homogeneous $T_2$. Averaging $s_\Delta$ uses the <characteristic function> of that <Gaussian distribution>:
$$
p(\Delta)=\frac{e^{-\Delta^2/(2\sigma^2)}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma},\qquad
S(t)=e^{-t/T_2}\begin{cases}
e^{-\sigma^2t^2/2},&0\leq t<\tau,\\
e^{-\sigma^2(t-2\tau)^2/2},&t>\tau.
\end{cases}
$$
Choose $\sigma/(2\pi)=25\,\mathrm{Hz}$, $\tau=40\,\mathrm{ms}$ and $T_2=160\,\mathrm{ms}$. Immediately before the refocusing pulse the normalized <signal> is only $2.08\times10^{-9}$, yet $S(80\,\mathrm{ms})=e^{-1/2}=0.60653$. A one-pulse <free induction decay> at 80 ms would instead be about $3.11\times10^{-35}$. These chosen numbers describe a calculation, not a measured trace. Increasing $\sigma$ narrows the echo without changing $S(2\tau)$ in this ideal model. Because this <Gaussian distribution> produces a Gaussian factor rather than an <exponential decay>, its inhomogeneous decay is not characterized by a unique exponential $T_2^*$.

\Image[hahn-echo-gaussian-ensemble.png]
{title=Synthetic <Gaussian distribution> ensemble: a vanished <free induction decay> and a recovered <spin echo>}
{description=Left: the stated instantaneous $90_u^\circ$ and $180_v^\circ$ sequence, ensemble magnitude, one-pulse comparison and homogeneous $T_2$ envelope. Direct sums over weighted detunings agree with the analytic curve. Right: samples at exact refocusing times for a series of separate sequences, with and without the <diffusion> model below. No panel reproduces an experimental trace.}

The exact phase-refocusing time is $2\tau$. A slowly falling <transverse relaxation> envelope moves the maximum of this particular echo shape slightly earlier: differentiating $\log S$ after the pulse gives $t_{\rm max}=2\tau-1/(\sigma^2T_2)$, or $79.747\,\mathrm{ms}$ here. Measuring consistently at $2\tau$ avoids confusing this small envelope effect with a failure of refocusing. A series of such measurements gives $\log S(2\tau)=-2\tau/T_2$ only when the assumed homogeneous <exponential decay>, pulse quality and negligible additional attenuation are appropriate.

= Hahn spin echo experiment
{c}
{parent=Spin echo}

<Erwin Louis Hahn>'s 1950 apparatus enclosed <liquid> samples in the coil of a tuned <LC circuit>, coupled to a gated <radio transmitter> and <radio receiver>. One transmitter quadrupled a crystal <oscillator>'s 7.5 MHz to 30 MHz. A separate <proton> resonance regulator stabilized the static <magnetic field>.<Spin Echoes by Erwin Louis Hahn>[§IV, Figs. 12–13: transmitter, coil, receiver and regulator]{ref}

Waiting for <thermal equilibrium> prepared <nuclear polarization>. <Oscilloscope> photographs compared <glycerol> <proton> echoes at different field homogeneity: echo width changed; echo time followed twice the pulse separation. Receiver saturation obscured early <free induction decay>; transmitter leakage required care. The delayed <signal> tested refocusing; <diffusion> could still attenuate it.<Spin Echoes by Erwin Louis Hahn>[§II(B), Fig. 2, §IV: preparation, timing, homogeneity comparison and readout limits]{ref}

Hahn analyzed equal pulses as well as noting improved recovery with a second pulse twice as long; the ideal sequence above isolates that latter case.<Spin Echoes by Erwin Louis Hahn>[§III(B), Fig. 1; footnote discussing a doubled second pulse and drive inhomogeneity]{ref}

= Diffusion attenuation of a spin echo
{parent=Spin echo}
{title2=$S(2\tau)=\exp[-2\tau/T_2-2D\gamma^2G^2\tau^3/3]$}

A <spin echo> refocuses a nucleus's fixed detuning. A nucleus that moves through a <magnetic field gradient> can have different detunings during the two intervals. To see the distinction without assuming that all <noise> is reversible, let $\Delta(t)$ vary during free evolution. With the same pulse convention, its residual <phase> at $2\tau$ is
$$
\Phi=\int_0^\tau\Delta(t)\,dt-\int_\tau^{2\tau}\Delta(t)\,dt
=\int_0^{2\tau}y(t)\Delta(t)\,dt,\qquad
y(t)=\begin{cases}+1,&t<\tau,\\-1,&t>\tau.\end{cases}
$$
Static detuning cancels because $\int y(t)\,dt=0$. A detuning $\Delta_a$ during the first interval and $\Delta_b$ during the second leaves $\Phi=\tau(\Delta_a-\Delta_b)$, which need not vanish. The final component <signal> is $e^{i\Phi}$ before relaxation; changing the overall receiver convention changes its conjugation but not its magnitude.

For an original quantitative model, take a constant <magnetic field gradient> $G=dB_z/dx$ and unrestricted one-dimensional <Brownian motion> $x(t)=x_0+\sqrt{2D}W_t$, where $D$ is the <diffusion coefficient> and $W_t$ is standard <Brownian motion>. Then $\Delta(t)=\Delta_0+\gamma Gx(t)$. The constant terms cancel, and the <Gaussian distribution> of the remaining <phase> follows from the <Brownian covariance kernel> $\mathbb E[W_sW_t]=\min(s,t)$. Writing $Q(r)=\int_r^{2\tau}y(t)\,dt$ gives
$$
Q(r)=\begin{cases}-r,&0\leq r\leq\tau,\\r-2\tau,&\tau\leq r\leq2\tau,\end{cases}\qquad
\operatorname{Var}\Phi=2D\gamma^2G^2\int_0^{2\tau}Q(r)^2\,dr
=\frac43D\gamma^2G^2\tau^3.
$$
The <characteristic function> of a zero-mean <Gaussian distribution> now supplies the extra factor $\mathbb E[e^{i\Phi}]=e^{-\operatorname{Var}\Phi/2}$, so
$$
S(2\tau)=\exp\!\left[-\frac{2\tau}{T_2}-\frac23D\gamma^2G^2\tau^3\right].
$$
Here $\gamma$ is in radians per second per tesla, $G$ in tesla per metre, and $D$ in square metres per second: the extra exponent is <dimensionless>. The figure chooses $\gamma/(2\pi)=42.58\,\mathrm{MHz/T}$, $G=0.010\,\mathrm{T/m}$ and $D=2.0\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$. With the earlier $\tau$ and $T_2$, diffusion reduces the echo from $0.60653$ to about $0.32930$. These are illustrative model parameters, not values extracted from Hahn's experiment.

This result assumes instantaneous perfect pulses, a constant <magnetic field gradient>, unrestricted <diffusion> with constant <diffusion coefficient>, and independent homogeneous <transverse relaxation>. Restricted motion, changing gradients, finite pulses and interactions between <spins> can require different attenuation laws. Even a fixed <Hamiltonian> is refocused only for the terms whose sign the pulse reverses: a simultaneous 180° rotation of two <spins> leaves their $J_{z,1}J_{z,2}$ product unchanged, because both factors change sign. A recovered echo therefore demonstrates a particular recoverable phase history, rather than reversal of every microscopic process.
