= Modular arithmetic
{wiki}
{title2=$\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z$}

Modular arithmetic performs <addition> and <multiplication> on <integers> while remembering only their <remainders> for a fixed positive <integer modulus> $m$. The relation $a\equiv b\pmod m$ means that $m$ divides $a-b$: the two <integers> lie in the same <congruence class>. For example, $-3$, $2$ and $7$ represent one class modulo $5$. Choosing the <least nonnegative residue> gives exactly $m$ representatives, $0,1,\ldots,m-1$, even though each <congruence class> contains infinitely many <integers>.

A clock supplies a familiar example. Represent the twelve positions by $0,1,\ldots,11$, with $0$ at the position usually labelled twelve. Starting at ten and moving seven positions gives
$$
10+7=17\equiv5\pmod{12}.
$$
The <integer congruence> records the final position; it discards the number of complete revolutions. Adding another twelve changes the elapsed count while preserving the position. The picture also shows a single <congruence class> modulo five spread along the <number line>.

\Image[modular-arithmetic-clock-and-classes.png]
{title=Clock addition wraps modulo twelve, while integers separated by five represent one congruence class}

The useful surprise is that discarding this information still preserves the arithmetic. If $a\equiv a'$ and $b\equiv b'$ modulo $m$, then $a+b\equiv a'+b'$ and $ab\equiv a'b'$ modulo $m$. Thus a large calculation can reduce its intermediate <integers> after every step. For instance,
$$
10+7\equiv5\pmod{12},\qquad 10\cdot7\equiv10\pmod{12},\qquad 10^2\equiv4\pmod{12}.
$$
Replacing $10$ by $-2$ gives the same answers with smaller intermediate values. The <congruence classes> with these operations form the <quotient ring> $\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z$; their <addition> alone forms a <cyclic group>. This passage from <integers> to a finite arithmetic system is the setting for <modular exponentiation>, <primality testing> and the <RSA cryptosystem>. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-200-principles-of-discrete-applied-mathematics-spring-2024/mit18_200_s24_lec15.pdf[Ankur Moitra, MIT 18.200, Lecture 15, modular arithmetic and its applications]{ref}

<Multiplicative cancellation> needs extra care. Modulo six, $2\cdot1\equiv2\cdot4$, but $1\not\equiv4$: multiplication by two has merged distinct <congruence classes>. Cancelling a factor while retaining the <integer modulus> is justified when that factor has a <modular multiplicative inverse>, equivalently when it is <coprime> to the <integer modulus>. A <linear congruence> can consequently have no solutions, one solution class, or several solution classes. The <Chinese remainder theorem> supplies a complementary operation: splitting a calculation across <coprime> <integer moduli> and reconstructing its class modulo their product.

Small remainders can also certify impossibility. A <perfect square> has residue zero or one modulo four, as checking the four <congruence classes> shows. Therefore an <integer> equation $x^2=4k+2$ has no solution. The converse direction needs care: a permitted residue does not guarantee an <integer> solution, since $5\equiv1\pmod4$ but five is not a <perfect square>. Such local tests provide necessary conditions for <Diophantine equations> without replacing the full problem.

In *Disquisitiones arithmeticae* (1801), <Carl Friedrich Gauss> defines <integer congruence> in article 1 and adopts the symbol $\equiv$ in article 2. Article 12 derives <divisibility> tests from powers of ten and explains checking arithmetic by its remainders. These passages show a systematic treatment of the same idea; they are not a claim that remainders were first used in 1801. https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Disquisitiones_arithmeticae/Sectio_prima[Gauss, Disquisitiones arithmeticae, section I, articles 1–2 and 12; text from the 1870 collected edition]{ref}

= Modulo one
{synonym}

<Reduction modulo one> sends a real number to its unique representative in $[0,1)$ by subtracting its integer part. Applied coordinatewise, it represents points of a torus by a unit cube.

= Modulo-counter elapsed-time identity
{parent=Modular arithmetic}
{title2=$((p+d)\bmod M-p)\bmod M=d\quad(0\leq d<M)$}

A free-running <digital counter> with modulus $M$ shows only the residue of elapsed ticks. If two observations differ by a true interval $d<M$, their modular difference is congruent to $d$ and is the unique representative in $\{0,\ldots,M-1\}$, proving the displayed identity. It handles a wrap between observations without a conditional branch. A complete elapsed modulus is indistinguishable from zero, so the interval bound is essential. For a $w$-bit timer at frequency $f$, the wrap period is $2^w/f$, and <unsigned integer arithmetic in C> can implement the difference directly on an appropriate type.

= Biquadratic residue
{parent=Modular arithmetic}
{title2=$x^4\equiv a\pmod p$}

A nonzero residue class $a$ modulo an odd <prime number> $p$ is a <biquadratic residue> if $x^4\equiv a\pmod p$ for some <integer> $x$.

= Modulo

= Mod
{synonym}

For an <integer> $a$ and a positive <integer modulus> $m$, the modulo operation returns the unique <least nonnegative residue> $r$ satisfying $a=qm+r$ for an <integer> $q$ and $0\leq r<m$. Equivalently, $r=a-m\lfloor a/m\rfloor$. For example, $-3\bmod5=2$. Some programming languages instead use a remainder with the sign of the dividend, so their negative-input convention must be checked before identifying an operator with this mathematical definition.

= Modular square root
{parent=Modular arithmetic}
{title2=$x^2\equiv a\pmod m$}

= Modular multiplication
{parent=Modular arithmetic}

Computing the <remainder> $ab\bmod m$ while working with bounded <integer> representatives. Repeated doubling and addition can avoid forming an overflowing product: process the <binary digits> of $b$, double the current contribution <modulo> $m$, and add it to the result whenever the current <binary digit> is 1. The additions themselves must also use an overflow-safe implementation.

= Addition modulo an integer
{parent=Modular arithmetic}

= Modular addition
{synonym}

For an integer $n\ge1$, add residue classes by adding their representatives and taking the class modulo $n$. This is well-defined because replacing either representative by a multiple-of-$n$ translate changes the sum by a multiple of $n$. The <associativity>, identity class $[0]_n$ and inverse $[-a]_n$ are inherited from integer <addition>. The result is the additive <cyclic group> $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$.

= Modulus in modular arithmetic

= Integer modulus
{synonym}

= Integer moduli
{synonym}

The positive <integer> $m$ in $a\equiv b\pmod m$ is the modulus. It specifies which differences are ignored: exactly the <integer> multiples of $m$. It is distinct from the <modulus of a complex number>, which measures magnitude.

= Least nonnegative residue
{parent=Modular arithmetic}
{title2=$r=a\bmod m,\quad0\leq r<m$}

The least nonnegative residue of an <integer> $a$ modulo a positive <integer modulus> $m$ is the unique representative $r$ of its <congruence class> in $\{0,\ldots,m-1\}$. <Euclidean division> gives $a=qm+r$. For example, $-3=(-1)\cdot5+2$, so $-3\bmod5=2$; the <integer congruence> $-3\equiv2\pmod5$ does not assert equality of those two <integers>.

= Integer congruence
{parent=Modular arithmetic}
{title2=$a\equiv b\pmod m\iff m\mid(a-b)$}

For a positive modulus $m$, integers $a,b$ are congruent when their difference is divisible by $m$. This is an <equivalence relation>: zero is divisible by $m$, changing sign preserves <divisibility>, and adding two divisible differences proves transitivity. Addition and multiplication respect it, because $(a+c)-(b+d)=(a-b)+(c-d)$ and $ac-bd=c(a-b)+b(c-d)$. Its <equivalence classes> are the residue classes used in <modular arithmetic>.

= Wolstenholme's theorem
{parent=Integer congruence}
{c}

For every <prime> $p\ge5$, $\binom{2p-1}{p-1}\equiv1\pmod{p^3}$. Equivalently, $\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}1/k\equiv0\pmod{p^2}$, with denominators interpreted through <modular inverses>.

= Wilson's theorem
{parent=Modular arithmetic}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$(n-1)!\equiv-1\pmod n$}

= Wilson theorem
{c}
{synonym}

For an <integer> $n>1$, <Wilson's theorem> characterizes <prime numbers> by a <factorial>:
$$
n\text{ is prime}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(n-1)!\equiv-1\pmod n.
$$
The right side is an <integer congruence>, so the least nonnegative <remainder> is $n-1$. For example, $4!=24\equiv4\pmod5$ certifies that five is a <prime number>, whereas $5!=120\equiv0\pmod6$ rules out six. <Wilson's Theorem and Fermat's Theorem by Bruce Ikenaga>{ref}

\Image[wilson-factorial-residue-primality.png]
{title=Factorial residues distinguish primes from composites, with inverse pairs explaining the prime case}

For an odd <prime number> $p$, each nonzero <congruence class> has a unique <modular multiplicative inverse>. Pair each class with its inverse: each distinct pair contributes one. A class is its own inverse exactly when $a^2\equiv1\pmod p$; since $p\mid(a-1)(a+1)$, <Euclid's lemma> leaves only $a\equiv\pm1$. Their product is $-1$. The <prime number> two needs a separate check: $1!\equiv-1\pmod2$. Conversely, a proper <divisor> $1<d<n$ divides both $n$ and $(n-1)!$, so the displayed <integer congruence> would force $d\mid1$, a contradiction. <Wilson's Theorem and Fermat's Theorem by Bruce Ikenaga>{ref}

The <composite numbers> have an even sharper pattern: $(n-1)!\equiv0\pmod n$ for every <composite number> $n>4$, but $3!=6\equiv2\pmod4$. To check the zero claim, write $n=ab$ with $1<a\leq b<n$. If $a<b$, both occur as different factors in the <factorial>. If $a=b\geq3$, the different factors $a$ and $2a$ both occur since $2a<a^2=n$, and their product is divisible by $n$. The only excluded case is $a=b=2$. Thus four is an exception to the zero pattern, but still fails <Wilson's theorem>: its required <remainder> would be three.

This is an exact <primality testing> criterion, but direct evaluation is expensive: even reducing modulo $n$ after every multiplication still takes a number of multiplications proportional to $n$, which grows exponentially with the number of <binary digits> of $n$. The problem is not merely storing a huge <factorial>. The more useful role of <Wilson's theorem> is simplifying <factorial> expressions in <modular arithmetic>. For instance, if $p>2$ is a <prime number>, then $(p-1)(p-2)!\equiv-1\pmod p$ gives $(p-2)!\equiv1\pmod p$ by a <modular multiplicative inverse>.

= Wilson factorial congruence modulo a triangular number
{c}
{parent=Wilson's theorem}
{title2=$(p-1)!\equiv p-1\pmod{p(p-1)/2}$}

For an odd <prime number> $p$, put $m=(p-1)/2$. Both $(p-1)!$ and $p-1$ are divisible by $m$. By <Wilson's theorem>, they are also congruent modulo $p$, because each is $-1$ modulo $p$. The <coprime integers> $m,p$ therefore both divide their difference, giving the displayed <integer congruence>. This combines the prime-modulus factorial identity with an elementary divisibility constraint.

= Odd-residue squared product modulo a prime
{parent=Wilson's theorem}
{title2=$\prod_{j=1}^{(p-3)/2}(2j+1)^2\equiv(-1)^{(p+1)/2}\pmod p$}

For an odd prime $p$, the even residues are the negatives of the odd residues. If $O=1\cdot3\cdots(p-2)$, then $(p-1)!\equiv(-1)^{(p-1)/2}O^2$. Apply <Wilson theorem> to obtain the displayed congruence. The empty product for $p=3$ is one.

= Complementary factorial congruence
{parent=Wilson's theorem}
{title2=$h!k!\equiv(-1)^{h+1}\pmod p$}

For a <prime number> $p$ and nonnegative <integers> $h+k=p-1$, <Wilson theorem> gives $(-1)^k h!k!\equiv-1\pmod p$ by replacing the last $k$ factors of $(p-1)!$ with $-1,-2,\ldots,-k$. For odd $p$, $h,k$ have the same parity; for $p=2$, both signs have the same <residue class>. Thus $h!k!+(-1)^h\equiv0\pmod p$, including the endpoint cases $0!=1$.

= Congruence class
{parent=Modular arithmetic}
{title2=$[a]_N$}

The <congruence class> of $a$ modulo a positive integer $N$ is $\{a+Nk:k\in\mathbb Z\}$. Two integers have the same class exactly when their difference is divisible by $N$. Addition and multiplication of representatives descend to well-defined operations on $\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z$, because replacing a representative by a multiple-of-$N$ translate changes the result by a multiple of $N$.

= Complete residue system
{parent=Congruence class}
{title2=$\{a_1,\ldots,a_m\}\longrightarrow\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z\text{ bijectively}$}

A complete residue system modulo a positive <integer> $m$ contains exactly one representative of every <congruence class> modulo $m$. Both $\{0,\ldots,m-1\}$ and $\{1,\ldots,m\}$ are examples. Counting the representatives that are <coprime> to $m$ gives <Euler's totient function>; those representatives form a reduced residue system.

= Modular congruence
{title2=$\equiv$}
{parent=Modular arithmetic}

= Congruence modulo an integer
{synonym}

For <integers> $a,b$ and a positive <integer modulus> $n$, modular congruence means
$$
a\equiv b\pmod n\iff n\mid(a-b).
$$
Thus $a-b=kn$ for some <integer> $k$: the two <integers> differ by complete cycles of length $n$. For example, $14\equiv2\pmod{12}$ because $14-2=12$, and $-1\equiv11\pmod{12}$ because $-1-11=-12$. A <modular congruence> need not be an equality of <integers>.

On a twelve-position clock labelled $0,1,\ldots,11$, adding five to nine follows $9\to10\to11\to0\to1\to2$. Hence $9+5=14\equiv2\pmod{12}$: the final position remembers the <remainder> while forgetting a complete revolution.

\Image[modular-clock-addition.png]
{title=Adding five to nine takes five clockwise steps and ends at two modulo twelve}

The same idea tracks any count repeating every $n$ steps. It also lets us compute sums and products with small <residue classes>, reducing intermediate results without changing the final class. The justification comes from <addition> and <multiplication> being well-defined on <residue classes>.

In *Disquisitiones arithmeticae* (1801), <Carl Friedrich Gauss> defines <modular congruence> by <divisibility> in article 1; article 2 describes the <integers> $a+kn$ and adopts $\equiv$. These passages document his presentation and notation, without claiming that repeating remainders began in 1801. https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Disquisitiones_arithmeticae/Sectio_prima[Gauss, Disquisitiones arithmeticae, section I, articles 1–2; original text in the 1870 collected edition]{ref}

= Quadratic congruence
{parent=Modular congruence}
{title2=$ax^2+bx+c\equiv0\pmod n$}

A quadratic congruence is a <modular congruence> defined by a degree-two <polynomial>, with solutions sought among <residue classes>. For odd $n$ and $a$ invertible modulo $n$, completing the square gives $(2ax+b)^2\equiv b^2-4ac\pmod n$. A <Jacobi symbol> of $-1$ for a <coprime> proposed square rules out solutions, whereas a <Jacobi symbol> of $1$ alone does not establish solubility for a composite modulus.

= Prime-power contraction under pth powers
{parent=Modular congruence}
{title2=$a\equiv b\pmod{p^k}\Rightarrow a^{p^r}\equiv b^{p^r}\pmod{p^{k+r}}$}

For a prime $p$ and $k\geq1$, write $a=b+p^kh$. The <binomial theorem> shows $a^p-b^p$ is divisible by $p^{k+1}$: the linear term contains the extra factor $p$ and every higher term contains at least $p^{2k}$. Iteration gives the displayed <modular congruence> for every $r\geq0$, including $p=2$. Applied to <Fermat's little theorem>, it yields $a^{p^n}\equiv a^{p^{n-1}}\pmod{p^n}$.

= Reduced residue system
{parent=Modular congruence}
{title2=$r_1,\ldots,r_{\varphi(n)}$}
{wiki}

For a positive modulus $n$, a reduced residue system contains exactly one representative of each <residue class> coprime to $n$. Its size is the <Euler totient function> $\varphi(n)$. Multiplication by any <unit modulo n> permutes these classes, which proves the <Fermat-Euler theorem> after multiplying and cancelling their unit product.

= Linear congruence
{parent=Modular congruence}
{title2=$ax\equiv b\pmod n$}

A linear congruence asks for the <integers> $x$ satisfying $ax\equiv b\pmod n$, where $a,b$ are <integers> and $n>1$. Let $d=\gcd(a,n)$ be their <greatest common divisor>. There are solutions exactly when $d$ divides $b$. When solutions exist, there are **exactly $d$ solution classes modulo $n$**, obtained from one solution $x_0$ by
$$
x\equiv x_0+j\frac nd\pmod n,\qquad j=0,\ldots,d-1.
$$
The <divisibility> condition follows immediately from $ax-b=kn$: every common <divisor> of $a,n$ must divide $b$. Conversely, <Bézout's identity> constructs a solution when $d\mid b$. Differences between any two solutions obey $n\mid a(x-x_0)$, equivalently $n/d\mid x-x_0$, since $a/d$ and $n/d$ are <coprime>. This explains both the spacing and the number of solution classes.

For a complete example, solve $6x\equiv9\pmod{15}$. Here $d=3$ divides nine. Dividing the corresponding <divisibility> statement by three changes the <integer modulus> too:
$$
6x\equiv9\pmod{15}\iff2x\equiv3\pmod5.
$$
Because $2\cdot3\equiv1\pmod5$, multiplying by the <modular inverse> three gives $x\equiv9\equiv4\pmod5$. Lifting this class back to modulus fifteen yields **$x\equiv4,9,14\pmod{15}$**. Direct substitution gives $24$, $54$ and $84$, each with <remainder> nine after <division with remainder> by fifteen. By contrast, $6x\equiv10\pmod{15}$ has no solutions, because three does not divide ten.

\Image[linear-congruence-solution-classes.png]
{title=Multiplication by six modulo fifteen hits residue nine three times and never hits residue ten}

This is why ordinary-looking <division> cannot be performed blindly in <modular arithmetic>. Reducing the equation and its <integer modulus> together keeps all solutions; retaining modulus fifteen after dividing by three would lose valid classes. When $d=1$, the <modular inverse> of $a$ exists and gives a unique class $x\equiv a^{-1}b\pmod n$. The <Euclidean algorithm> computes the <Bézout coefficients> needed for this construction. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-200-principles-of-discrete-applied-mathematics-spring-2024/mit18_200_s24_lec15.pdf[Moitra, MIT 18.200, Lecture 15, modular inverses and the extended Euclidean algorithm]{ref}

= Cancellation of a common factor in a congruence
{parent=Linear congruence}
{title2=$ac\equiv bc\pmod n\iff a\equiv b\pmod{n/\gcd(c,n)}$}

For <integers> $a,b$ and positive <integers> $c,n$, a common factor may be cancelled from a <integer congruence> provided the modulus is divided by the corresponding <greatest common divisor>. Let $d=\gcd(c,n)$, $c=dc'$, and $n=dn'$. The <integers> $c',n'$ are coprime: any common divisor greater than one would give a common divisor of $c,n$ larger than $d$.

By <divisibility> and <Euclid's lemma>,
$$
ac\equiv bc\pmod n
\iff n'\mid c'(a-b)
\iff n'\mid a-b.
$$
This proves the displayed equivalence. If $c$ is coprime to $n$, the modulus stays unchanged and this is ordinary cancellation by a <modular multiplicative inverse>. If $n\mid c$, the reduced modulus is $1$, so every pair $a,b$ satisfies the reduced <integer congruence>.

= Residue class
{title2=$[a]_m$}
{parent=Modular congruence}
{wiki}

For a positive <integer modulus> $m$, the residue class of an <integer> $a$ is the <equivalence class>
$$
[a]_m=\{a+km:k\in\mathbb Z\}=\{b\in\mathbb Z:b\equiv a\pmod m\}.
$$
It contains all <integers> in the same position of the repeating cycle. For example,
$$
[-1]_{12}=[11]_{12}=\{\ldots,-13,-1,11,23,35,\ldots\}.
$$
The <residue class> is this whole infinite collection; its canonical representative is the <least nonnegative residue>. <Euclidean division> gives exactly one such representative in $0,1,\ldots,m-1$, so there are exactly $m$ <residue classes>. When $m=1$, there is just one, $[0]_1=\mathbb Z$.

Define <addition> and <multiplication> of <residue classes> by
$$
[a]_m+[b]_m=[a+b]_m,\qquad [a]_m[b]_m=[ab]_m.
$$
These definitions must give the same answer whichever <integers> represent the classes. If $a'\equiv a$ and $b'\equiv b$ modulo $m$, then
$$
(a'+b')-(a+b)=(a'-a)+(b'-b),\qquad a'b'-ab=b'(a'-a)+a(b'-b).
$$
Both differences are divisible by $m$, proving that the operations are well-defined. For example, $[9]_{12}+[5]_{12}=[2]_{12}$ and $[9]_{12}[5]_{12}=[9]_{12}$, since $45=3\cdot12+9$. Replacing nine by $-3$ gives the same answers with smaller intermediate <integers>.

With these operations the <residue classes> form the finite <commutative ring> $\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z$, a <quotient ring>. It need not be a <field>: $[6]_{12}[2]_{12}=[0]_{12}$ although neither factor is the zero class. These <zero divisors> explain why <division> requires a <unit modulo n>. Reducing after each <multiplication> is especially useful in <modular exponentiation>, where the unreduced powers can become enormous.

= Unit modulo n
{parent=Modular congruence}

For a positive <integer modulus> $n$, a <residue class> $[a]_n$ is a <unit> when it has a <modular multiplicative inverse>: some <integer> $u$ satisfies $au\equiv1\pmod n$. Modulo twelve, the <units> are exactly
$$
[1]_{12},\quad[5]_{12},\quad[7]_{12},\quad[11]_{12}.
$$
In particular, five is its own <modular inverse>, since $5\cdot5=25\equiv1\pmod{12}$. Multiplying all twelve <residue classes> by five gives a <permutation>; multiplying the outputs by five recovers the inputs. By contrast, multiplying by six gives only zero and six, with six inputs for each output. It cannot be reversed.

\Image[modular-multiplication-units-and-zero-divisors.png]
{title=Multiplication by five permutes all twelve residue classes, whereas multiplication by six merges distinct inputs}

In general, the exact criterion is
$$
[a]_n\text{ is a unit}\iff\gcd(a,n)=1.
$$
For the forward direction, $au-kn=1$ implies every common <divisor> of $a,n$ divides one. Conversely, if their <greatest common divisor> is one, <Bézout's identity> supplies $ua+vn=1$, so $u$ is a <modular inverse>. The <extended Euclidean algorithm> computes these coefficients. The <units> form the <unit group> $(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^\times$ under <multiplication>. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-200-principles-of-discrete-applied-mathematics-spring-2024/mit18_200_s24_lec15.pdf[Ankur Moitra, MIT 18.200, Lecture 15 (2024), pp. 3–4: inverses, coprimality and the unit group]{ref}

<Multiplicative cancellation> while keeping the same <integer modulus> is valid for a <unit modulo n>: from $ca\equiv cb\pmod n$, multiply by a <modular inverse> of $c$ to get $a\equiv b\pmod n$. A nonunit cannot be cancelled as a general rule. For example, $6\cdot0\equiv6\cdot2\pmod{12}$, yet $0\not\equiv2\pmod{12}$. Thus <division> by six cannot undo this <multiplication>; solving a <linear congruence> instead keeps track of all possible classes.

This distinction marks the boundary between <commutative rings> and <fields>. Modulo a <prime number> $p>1$, every nonzero <residue class> is a <unit>, so $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$ is a <finite field>. Modulo twelve it is a <commutative ring> with <zero divisors>, and is not a <field>. For $n=1$, the sole class satisfies $[0]_1=[1]_1$ and is a <unit>; this one-element <zero ring> is not a <field>. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-200-principles-of-discrete-applied-mathematics-spring-2024/mit18_200_s24_lec15.pdf[Moitra, MIT 18.200, Lecture 15, pp. 8–9: the prime-modulus field criterion]{ref}
