= Continuous probability distribution
{wiki}

A real <probability distribution> with a continuous <cumulative distribution function> assigns probability zero to each individual value. Here this is the broad meaning of <continuous probability distribution>. Many elementary examples are more specifically <absolutely continuous probability distributions>, described by a <probability density function> with respect to <Lebesgue measure>. Some texts use "continuous" for that narrower class. The <Cantor distribution> shows why the distinction matters: its <cumulative distribution function> is continuous, yet no <probability density function> with respect to <Lebesgue measure> represents its whole law. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-436j-fundamentals-of-probability-fall-2018/9797310bed4c7f5b5d40d007783eec8d_MIT6_436JF18_lec04.pdf[MIT Lecture 4: continuous CDFs and the singular Cantor example]{ref}.

= Log-logistic distribution
{parent=Continuous probability distribution}
{title2=$F(x)=\frac{(x/a)^\kappa}{1+(x/a)^\kappa}$}

For scale $a>0$ and shape $\kappa>0$, the log-logistic distribution has the displayed cumulative distribution function for $x\geq0$, with density $(\kappa/a)(x/a)^{\kappa-1}/[1+(x/a)^\kappa]^2$ for $x>0$. Its log-odds are affine in $\log x$, giving the name and motivating a <log-logistic probability plot>. In the exam's parameterization, $a=\alpha$ and $\kappa=\beta-1$.

= Log-logistic probability plot
{parent=Log-logistic distribution}
{title2=$\log\frac{F(x)}{1-F(x)}=(\beta-1)\log x-(\beta-1)\log\alpha$}

For $F(x)=1-[1+(x/\alpha)^{\beta-1}]^{-1}$, plotting the logits of $p_i=i/(n+1)$ against sorted log observations gives an approximately straight line under the model. The <probability integral transform> makes $F(X_{(i)})$ a uniform order statistic with expectation $i/(n+1)$, motivating these plotting positions. With those axes the slope is $\beta-1$ and the intercept is $-(\beta-1)\log\alpha$.

= Wigner semicircle distribution
{parent=Continuous probability distribution}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$f(x)=\frac2\pi\sqrt{1-x^2}\,\mathbf1_{(-1,1)}(x)$}

The unit-radius <Wigner semicircle distribution> has the displayed <probability density function>. Its graph is a rescaled upper semicircle. The area of the unit semicircle is $\pi/2$, so the factor $2/\pi$ normalizes it. Symmetry gives mean zero, and the substitution $x=\sin\theta$ gives variance $1/4$.

A scaled radius-$R$ version has density $2\sqrt{R^2-x^2}/(\pi R^2)$ on $(-R,R)$ and variance $R^2/4$. <Rejection sampling> from a uniform proposal on $(-1,1)$ accepts a proposed $x$ with probability $\sqrt{1-x^2}$; its average acceptance probability is $\pi/4$. Thus the number of proposals for one output has <geometric distribution> with expectation $4/\pi$. This law also describes the limiting empirical eigenvalue density in standard ensembles of large symmetric <random matrices>.

= Singular continuous distribution
{parent=Continuous probability distribution}

A <probability distribution> is singular continuous if it has no point masses and assigns probability one to a <set> of zero <Lebesgue measure>. Its <cumulative distribution function> is continuous, but the distribution has no <probability density function> with respect to <Lebesgue measure>. This distinguishes continuity of the <cumulative distribution function> from an <absolutely continuous probability distribution>, whose probabilities are <integrals> of a <probability density function>.

= Exponential-logarithmic distribution
{parent=Continuous probability distribution}
{title2=$f_{p,\beta}(x)=-\frac{\beta}{\log p}\frac{(1-p)e^{-\beta x}}{1-(1-p)e^{-\beta x}}$}

For $0<p<1$, $\beta>0$ and $x>0$, this <probability density function> has <cumulative distribution function> $F(x)=1-\log(1-(1-p)e^{-\beta x})/\log p$. Differentiating gives the density, while the endpoint limits are zero and one. Its <quantile function> is $F^{-1}(u)=\beta^{-1}[\log(1-p)-\log(1-p^{1-u})]$ for $0<u<1$, enabling <inverse transform sampling>.

Expanding the denominator as a <geometric series> gives
$$
f_{p,\beta}(x)=-\frac{\beta}{\log p}\sum_{k\geq1}(1-p)^ke^{-k\beta x}.
$$
Termwise integration, justified by the <monotone convergence theorem>, and the <gamma function> integral yield <raw moments> $\mathbb E[X^j]=-j!\operatorname{Li}_{j+1}(1-p)/(\beta^j\log p)$. Thus the <polylogarithm> expresses its <mean>, <variance> and higher <moments>. Here $p$ is the complement of the series parameter $1-p$; parameter conventions must be checked when comparing formulas.

= Cantor distribution
{c}
{parent=Continuous probability distribution}
{wiki}

The <Cantor distribution> is the <law of a random variable>
$$
X=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2B_n}{3^n},
$$
where the $B_n$ are <independent> <Bernoulli random variables> taking values zero and one with equal probability. Its values lie in the <Cantor set>, and its <cumulative distribution function> is the <Cantor function>. Every individual value has probability zero, but the <Cantor set> has probability one despite having zero <Lebesgue measure>. A <probability density function> on that set would integrate to zero, so it cannot describe this law. The example motivates the distinction between a <continuous probability distribution> and an <absolutely continuous probability distribution>.

= Distance between two uniform points in a three-dimensional ball
{parent=Continuous probability distribution}
{title2=$f_R(r)=3r^2/R^3-9r^3/(4R^4)+3r^5/(16R^6)$}

For two <independent random variables> taking values in a three-dimensional <ball> with constant volume <probability density>, the density of their distance is the displayed expression for $0\leq r\leq2R$, and zero otherwise. Both <independence> and the common radius $R>0$ are essential. To derive it, put $u=|\mathbf r_1|$, $v=|\mathbf r_2|$ and integrate the product volume <differential form> over orientations. The <law of cosines> gives the angular <Jacobian determinant> $v/(ur)$, leaving $f_R(r)=9r\int uv\,du\,dv/(2R^6)$ over $0\leq u,v\leq R$, $|u-v|\leq r\leq u+v$. With $x=u+v$, $y=u-v$, the integrand becomes $(x^2-y^2)\,dx\,dy/8$. Direct integration gives $\int uv\,du\,dv=2R^3r/3-R^2r^2/2+r^4/24$. An independent geometric derivation uses the overlap volume of two radius-$R$ balls displaced by $r$, namely $\pi(4R+r)(2R-r)^2/12$, multiplied by $4\pi r^2$ and divided by the square of $4\pi R^3/3$. The density integrates to one and has mean $36R/35$.

= Beta-prime distribution
{parent=Continuous probability distribution}
{title2=$f(x)=\frac{x^{a-1}}{B(a,b)(1+x)^{a+b}},\quad x>0$}

For $a,b>0$, this <probability density function> is obtained from a <beta distribution> variable $U$ by $X=U/(1-U)$. Indeed $u=x/(1+x)$ and $du/dx=(1+x)^{-2}$, and the <change of variables formula> gives the displayed density. In particular, the ratio of two <independent> equal-rate <exponential distributions> has $a=b=1$, density $(1+x)^{-2}$ and <cumulative distribution function> $x/(1+x)$.

= Triangular distribution
{parent=Continuous probability distribution}
{wiki}
{title2=$\operatorname{Triangular}(a,c,b),\quad a<c<b$}

A triangular <probability density function> rises linearly from zero at $a$ to its mode $c$, then falls linearly to zero at $b$. Its peak is $2/(b-a)$. Its <inverse transform sampling> rule is $a+\sqrt{u(b-a)(c-a)}$ for $u\le(c-a)/(b-a)$, and $b-\sqrt{(1-u)(b-a)(b-c)}$ otherwise.

= Difference of two independent uniform random variables
{parent=Triangular distribution}
{title2=$U-V\sim q(t)=(a-|t|)/a^2,\quad |t|<a$}

If $U,V$ are <independent random variables>, each with the <uniform distribution> on $[0,a]$ for $a>0$, then $T=U-V$ has the symmetric <triangular distribution>
$$
q(t)=\begin{cases}(a-|t|)/a^2,&|t|<a,\\0,&|t|\geq a.\end{cases}
$$
The <convolution of probability densities> gives $q(t)=a^{-2}\int\mathbf1_{[0,a]}(s)\mathbf1_{[0,a]}(s+t)\,ds$. The overlap of these two <intervals> has length $a-|t|$ for $|t|<a$ and is empty otherwise, proving the formula. By <linearity of expectation> and the <variance of a sum>,
$$
\mathbb E[T]=0,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(T)=a^2/6.
$$
For $0\leq b\leq a$, its upper-tail <probability> is $(a-b)^2/(2a^2)$.

A useful model for elapsed time measured from two independently truncated clock readings uses $a$ as the display resolution and $T$ as the endpoint-offset difference. Independence is essential: uniform marginal <probability distributions> alone do not imply this <triangular distribution>. For example $U=V$ gives $T=0$ with certainty. In general, with the same uniform marginals, $\operatorname{Var}(U-V)=a^2/6-2\operatorname{Cov}(U,V)$.

= Arcsine distribution
{parent=Continuous probability distribution}
{title2=$\operatorname{Beta}(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$}
{wiki}

The standard <arcsine distribution> is a <continuous probability distribution> on $[0,1]$ with <probability density function>
$$
f(x)=\frac1{\pi\sqrt{x(1-x)}},\qquad0<x<1.
$$
It is the <beta distribution> with both parameters equal to $1/2$. The density grows without bound toward both endpoints, yet neither endpoint has any <probability mass>. Its name comes from the <inverse sine> in its <cumulative distribution function>.<Random: Probability, Mathematical Statistics, Stochastic Processes by Kyle Siegrist/The Arcsine Distribution>[Random: Probability, Mathematical Statistics, Stochastic Processes by Kyle Siegrist; The Standard Arcsine Distribution, Distribution Functions and Related Distributions: density, normalization, beta representation and CDF]{ref}

A useful way to see the shape is to choose a point uniformly around a <circle> and project it onto a diameter. Equal angular intervals do not give equal horizontal distances: the projection moves slowly near either end. An ideal <harmonic oscillator> sampled at a uniformly chosen time in one complete period has the same endpoint concentration after rescaling its position. The distribution translates a simple geometric slowdown into a precise <probability> calculation.

{title=Uniform angles project unevenly onto a diameter}
{description=Original calculated geometry. The circle marks an equally spaced angle grid to illustrate the uniform-angle model; these are not experimental observations. Horizontal projection gives $Y=\cos\Phi$ on $[-1,1]$. The right curve is its exact <probability density function> $1/(\pi\sqrt{1-y^2})$; the standard variable is $X=(1+Y)/2$.}

For an original comparison, take equal-length regions: the middle half $[1/4,3/4]$, and the two outside quarters together. Then
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(1/4\leq X\leq3/4)=\frac13,\qquad
\mathbb P(X<1/4\text{ or }X>3/4)=\frac23.}
$$
A <uniform distribution> on $[0,1]$ instead assigns each region <probability> $1/2$. Both distributions have <mean> $1/2$; the <mean> alone hides their different concentration.

= Arcsine distribution from a uniform angle
{parent=Arcsine distribution}
{title2=$X=\sin^2\Theta,\quad\Theta\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,\pi/2)$}

Choose $\Theta$ with <uniform distribution> on $(0,\pi/2)$ and set $X=\sin^2\Theta$. Since the <sine> is increasing on this interval,
$$
F(x)=\mathbb P(X\leq x)=\frac2\pi\arcsin\sqrt{x},\qquad0\leq x\leq1.
$$
Differentiation gives $f(x)=1/(\pi\sqrt{x(1-x)})$, and $F(0)=0$, $F(1)=1$ prove normalization. This also evaluates the density's <improper integral> without treating its divergent endpoint values as infinite <probability>.

For the <circle> construction, instead choose $\Phi$ with <uniform distribution> on $[0,2\pi)$ and put $Y=\cos\Phi$. Each $-1<y<1$ has two preimages in one revolution. The <change of variables> formula adds both contributions:
$$
f_Y(y)=\frac1{2\pi}\frac1{\sqrt{1-y^2}}+
\frac1{2\pi}\frac1{\sqrt{1-y^2}}
=\frac1{\pi\sqrt{1-y^2}}.
$$
The affine <change of variables> $X=(1+Y)/2$ gives the standard <arcsine distribution>. A single monotone-branch calculation without the second preimage would give only half the total <probability>.

The same geometry explains the ideal <harmonic oscillator> $y(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$, with amplitude $A>0$ and <angular frequency> $\omega>0$. Choose $T$ uniformly in any complete period of duration $2\pi/\omega$. Its <oscillation phase> modulo $2\pi$ is uniform, and $Y=y(T)$ has density
$$
f_Y(y)=\frac1{\pi\sqrt{A^2-y^2}},\qquad-A<y<A.
$$
Near a <turning point>, $|dy/dt|=\omega\sqrt{A^2-y^2}$ is small, so a fixed position interval takes more time to cross. For the chosen amplitude $A=2\,\mathrm{cm}$, exactly one third of the period is spent in $|y|\leq1\,\mathrm{cm}$, and two thirds outside that central interval. These are calculations for an ideal sinusoid, rather than measured oscillator data.

The sampling assumption matters. Taking one observation every full period returns the same position each time. It does not produce this <probability distribution>. Uniformly random observation times and a fixed regularly spaced sampling scheme are different models; <aliasing> can conceal the motion altogether.

<Paul Lévy> used a related geometric construction in his work on <stochastic processes>: project a uniformly chosen point on a <circle> onto its diameter to divide a segment. The projection description appears explicitly in §5 of his paper, following its arcsine density calculation.<Sur certains processus stochastiques homogènes by Paul Lévy>[§5, printed pp. 301–302, Theorem 3 and the projection paragraph on p. 302; Numdam catalogues volume 7 as 1940, while the paper records receipt on 29 March 1939]{ref}

= Endpoint concentration in the arcsine distribution
{parent=Arcsine distribution}

An unbounded <probability density function> does not imply an atom. Here
$$
\mathbb P(X=0)=\mathbb P(X=1)=0,\qquad
\mathbb P(0<X<\varepsilon)=\frac2\pi\arcsin\sqrt\varepsilon
\sim\frac2\pi\sqrt\varepsilon\quad(\varepsilon\downarrow0).
$$
The last equivalence follows from $\arcsin u/u\to1$. The endpoint interval has much more <probability> than the same interval under a <uniform distribution>, whose value is $\varepsilon$, but its <probability> still tends to zero. This is an <integrable singularity>.

For example, the first 1% of the unit interval contains about $6.377\%$ of the <probability>; the last 1% contains the same amount because the <arcsine distribution> is a <symmetric probability distribution>. Each exact endpoint still has zero <probability>. Density and interval <probability> answer different questions.

\Image[arcsine-cdf-and-equal-bin-probabilities.png]
{title=Endpoint density diverges, but every finite bin has finite probability}
{description=Original exact curves and bin probabilities. Left: the arcsine <cumulative distribution function> compared with the uniform one. Right: ten equal-width bins have probabilities $F((j+1)/10)-F(j/10)$, not heights sampled from the divergent density. Their total is one.}

A numerical integration routine must respect these endpoint <integrable singularities>. The substitution $x=\sin^2\theta$ gives
$$
f(x)\,dx=\frac2\pi\,d\theta,\qquad0<\theta<\pi/2.
$$
It turns a difficult-looking density integral into a constant measure in angle. Integrating finite bins is also a faithful way to draw the distribution near its endpoints; sampling $f(0)$ to make a bar height is not.

= Arcsine quantiles and moments
{parent=Arcsine distribution}
{title2=$Q(p)=\sin^2(\pi p/2)$}

Inverting $F(x)=p$ gives the <quantile function>
$$
Q(p)=\sin^2\left(\frac{\pi p}{2}\right),\qquad0\leq p\leq1.
$$
Consequently, <inverse transform sampling> uses $X=\sin^2(\pi U/2)$ with $U$ uniform on $(0,1)$. The <quartiles> are $(2-\sqrt2)/4$, $1/2$ and $(2+\sqrt2)/4$.<Random: Probability, Mathematical Statistics, Stochastic Processes by Kyle Siegrist/The Arcsine Distribution>[Random: Probability, Mathematical Statistics, Stochastic Processes by Kyle Siegrist; The Standard Arcsine Distribution, Distribution Functions and Related Distributions: quantiles, quartiles and random quantile construction]{ref}

The first and third <quartiles> are about $0.1464$ and $0.8536$, so the central half of the <probability> spans about $0.7071$ of the unit interval. Under a <uniform distribution>, its width would be $0.5$.

The <symmetric probability distribution> gives $\mathbb E[X]=1/2$. The angle representation gives a short calculation of the second <moment>:
$$
\mathbb E[X^2]=\frac2\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^4\theta\,d\theta=\frac38,
\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac38-\frac14=\frac18.
$$
Use $\sin^4\theta=(3-4\cos2\theta+\cos4\theta)/8$ to evaluate the integral. A <uniform distribution> on $[0,1]$ has <variance> $1/12$; the larger <variance> $1/8$ quantifies the greater spread toward the ends.

More generally, for a nonnegative <integer> $n$, the <beta function> yields
$$
\mathbb E[X^n]=\frac{B(n+1/2,1/2)}{B(1/2,1/2)}
=\frac{\binom{2n}{n}}{4^n}.
$$
For $n=0$ this is one; for $n=1,2$ it reproduces the values above. The <central binomial coefficient> thus appears in an exact moment calculation for a continuous <probability distribution>.

= Arcsine distribution on a bounded interval
{parent=Arcsine distribution}
{title2=$Y=a+(b-a)X,\quad a<b$}

Specify whether two parameters mean endpoints or location and width. With endpoints $a<b$, define $Y=a+(b-a)X$ for a standard <arcsine distribution> variable $X$. Then
$$
f_Y(y)=\frac1{\pi\sqrt{(y-a)(b-y)}},\quad a<y<b,
\qquad F_Y(y)=\frac2\pi\arcsin\sqrt{\frac{y-a}{b-a}}.
$$
This is a <location-scale family> with location $a$ and width $b-a$. Its <mean> is $(a+b)/2$ and its <variance> is $(b-a)^2/8$. A convention using parameters $a,w$ for location and width instead has right endpoint $a+w$, so it must not be read as having endpoints $a,w$.<Random: Probability, Mathematical Statistics, Stochastic Processes by Kyle Siegrist/The Arcsine Distribution>[Random: Probability, Mathematical Statistics, Stochastic Processes by Kyle Siegrist; The General Arcsine Distribution, Definition, Distribution Functions and Moments: location-plus-width convention and alternative endpoint parametrization]{ref}

For example, a chosen interval $[-2,6]$ gives <mean> $2$ and <variance> $8$, while its density still concentrates toward both endpoints. It is a bounded <probability distribution>; its endpoint singularities do not make its support infinite.

= Pareto distribution
{c}
{parent=Continuous probability distribution}
{title2=$\operatorname{Pareto}(\alpha,\beta)$}
{wiki}

The <Pareto distribution> describes a positive <random variable> whose <tail probability> falls as a <power law>. In the shape–minimum convention used here, $X\sim\operatorname{Pareto}(\alpha,\beta)$ has <shape parameter> $\alpha>0$ and minimum $\beta>0$, with <probability support> $[\beta,\infty)$. Its <probability density function> is
$$
f(x)=\begin{cases}\alpha\beta^\alpha x^{-\alpha-1},&x\geq\beta,\\0,&x<\beta.\end{cases}
$$
The minimum $\beta$ has the same units as $X$; the <shape parameter> $\alpha$ is dimensionless. At fixed $\alpha$, changing $\beta$ gives a <scale family>, since $X/\beta\sim\operatorname{Pareto}(\alpha,1)$. This convention is also used in <uniform-Pareto conjugacy>.<NIST Dataplot Reference Manual/Pareto Probability Density Function>[NIST Dataplot Reference Manual; Description: standard Pareto density and lower bound; source gamma,a correspond to alpha,beta here]{ref}

The <survival function> and <cumulative distribution function> are
$$
\overline F(x)=\mathbb P(X>x)=\begin{cases}1,&x<\beta,\\(\beta/x)^\alpha,&x\geq\beta,\end{cases}
\qquad F(x)=1-\overline F(x).
$$
For $0<p<1$, solving $F(x)=p$ gives the <quantile function>
$$
Q(p)=\beta(1-p)^{-1/\alpha}.
$$
Its right-hand limit at $p=0$ is $\beta$; it tends to infinity as $p\uparrow1$.<Random: Probability, Mathematical Statistics, Stochastic Processes by Kyle Siegrist/The Pareto Distribution>[Random: Probability, Mathematical Statistics, Stochastic Processes by Kyle Siegrist; The General Pareto Distribution, Distribution Functions: density, CDF and quantiles; source a,b correspond to alpha,beta here]{ref} There is no <probability mass> at $\beta$: changing a <probability density function>'s value at that single point changes no <probabilities>.

Choose $\alpha=2$ and $\beta=10$ as an original theoretical example. Then $\mathbb P(X>20)=1/4$, $\mathbb P(X>100)=1/100$, the <median> is $10\sqrt2\simeq14.142$, and the 0.99 <quantile> is $Q(0.99)=100$. These chosen values and all figures below describe mathematical <probability distributions>, not actual wealth observations.

\Image[pareto-survival-loglog.png]
{title=Pareto survival curves: smaller alpha gives a slower power-law tail}

On this <log-log plot>, write $r=x/\beta$ to obtain $\log\overline F(\beta r)=-\alpha\log r$. The <slope> is $-\alpha$, whereas the <probability density function> has power exponent $\alpha+1$. Multiplying a threshold by ten divides its <tail probability> by $10^\alpha$. A larger $\alpha$ therefore concentrates the <Pareto distribution> nearer $\beta$; a smaller $\alpha$ makes extreme values more plausible. This exact scaling is useful when modelling large positive losses or sizes over several orders of magnitude.

The <Pareto distribution> is a <heavy-tailed distribution>: for every $t>0$, the <exponential moment> $\mathbb E[e^{tX}]$ diverges because $e^{tx}x^{-\alpha-1}$ eventually grows without bound. In contrast, the <Laplace distribution> has exponentially decreasing tails and some finite positive <exponential moments>. The <Student's t-distribution> also has power tails, but on both sides of the <real line>, with a smooth central region rather than a positive minimum.

= Logarithm of a Pareto random variable
{parent=Pareto distribution}
{title2=$\log(X/x_m)\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\alpha)$}

If $X$ has a <Pareto distribution> with positive scale $x_m$ and positive shape $\alpha$, then for $z\geq0$,
$$
\mathbb P(\log(X/x_m)>z)=\mathbb P(X>x_m e^z)=e^{-\alpha z}.
$$
Thus the rescaled <logarithm> has an <exponential distribution> with rate $\alpha$. The <tail probability> calculation also identifies the support as $z\geq0$.

= Zeta bounds from rounded Pareto variables
{parent=Pareto distribution}
{title2=$1/(s-1)\le\zeta(s)\le s/(s-1)$}

Let $X$ have <survival function> $\mathbb P(X\ge x)=x^{-s}$ for $x\ge1$, with $s>1$. Its <expected value> is $s/(s-1)$. The <tail-sum formula for expectation> gives
$$
\mathbb E[\lfloor X\rfloor]=\sum_{k\ge1}k^{-s}=\zeta(s).
$$
The <absolutely continuous probability distribution> gives zero mass to the <integers>, so $\lceil X\rceil=\lfloor X\rfloor+1$ almost surely and $\mathbb E[\lceil X\rceil]=\zeta(s)+1$. Taking expectations of $\lfloor X\rfloor\le X\le\lceil X\rceil$ gives $\zeta(s)\le s/(s-1)\le\zeta(s)+1$, which rearranges to the displayed bounds on the <Riemann zeta function>. Finiteness is justified by $\lfloor X\rfloor\le X$ and $\lceil X\rceil\le X+1$.

= Pareto highest density interval
{c}
{parent=Pareto distribution}
{title2=$[b,b\alpha^{-1/a}]$}

For the <Pareto distribution> of shape $a>0$ and lower bound $b>0$, the density $ab^ax^{-a-1}$ is strictly decreasing on $[b,\infty)$. Its highest-density interval of probability $1-\alpha$, $0<\alpha<1$, must begin at $b$. The tail probability $\mathbb P(X>u)=(b/u)^a$ makes the upper endpoint $u=b\alpha^{-1/a}$. If this is a <posterior distribution>, the result is its <highest posterior density interval>. In particular, <Uniform-Pareto conjugacy> supplies posterior shape $a+n$ and lower bound $\max(b,\max_i y_i)$.

= One-big-jump polynomial lower bound
{parent=Pareto distribution}
{title2=$\mathbb P(S_n\geq na)\geq\mathbb P(X_1\geq na)$}

Let $S_n=\sum_{i=1}^nX_i$, where the summands are <independent and identically distributed> with <Pareto distribution> $\operatorname{Pareto}(\alpha,\beta)$. Their positivity implies $\{X_1\geq na\}\subseteq\{S_n\geq na\}$, so for fixed $a>0$ and $na\geq\beta$,
$$
\mathbb P(S_n\geq na)\geq\left(\frac{\beta}{na}\right)^\alpha,\qquad
\frac{\alpha}{n}\log\frac{\beta}{na}\leq\frac1n\log\mathbb P(S_n\geq na)\leq0.
$$
Both outer expressions tend to zero. A single large summand already prevents a strictly negative exponential rate. No positive <exponential moment> exists, so the exponential-moment form of the <Cramér theorem> cannot be applied. The bound proves this logarithmic rate and does not assert an exact tail asymptotic. In particular, it remains true when $a$ lies below a finite <mean>, where the event need not even be rare. The stronger fixed-summand-count asymptotic in a <heavy-tailed subexponential distribution> is a separate statement.

= Pareto moment threshold
{c}
{parent=Pareto distribution}
{title2=$\mathbb E[X^q]=\alpha\beta^q/(\alpha-q),\quad 0\leq q<\alpha$}

A <Pareto distribution> is a proper <probability distribution> for every $\alpha,\beta>0$, even when its <mean> is infinite. To see both facts, integrate the <probability density function> against $x^q$. For real $q\geq0$,
$$
\mathbb E[X^q]=\alpha\beta^\alpha\int_\beta^\infty x^{q-\alpha-1}\,dx
=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\alpha\beta^q}{\alpha-q},&0\leq q<\alpha,\\+\infty,&q\geq\alpha.\end{cases}
$$
The <improper integral> converges exactly when $q-\alpha-1<-1$. At $q=\alpha$ it diverges logarithmically; above that it diverges as a positive power of the upper endpoint. Setting $q=0$ gives one, proving normalization regardless of whether higher <moments> exist.

Taking $q=1,2$ and using $\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E[X^2]-(\mathbb E[X])^2$ when both terms are finite gives
$$
\mathbb E[X]=\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha-1}\quad(\alpha>1),\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{\alpha\beta^2}{(\alpha-1)^2(\alpha-2)}\quad(\alpha>2).
$$
For $0<\alpha\leq1$, the nonnegative <random variable> has <expected value> $+\infty$; a centered <variance> about a finite <mean> is unavailable. For $1<\alpha\leq2$, the <mean> is finite and the <variance> is infinite.<Random: Probability, Mathematical Statistics, Stochastic Processes by Kyle Siegrist/The Pareto Distribution>[Random: Probability, Mathematical Statistics, Stochastic Processes by Kyle Siegrist; The General Pareto Distribution, Moments: existence threshold, mean and variance]{ref} A finite total <probability> and a finite <expected value> answer different questions.

At the chosen minimum $\beta=10$, $\alpha=3$ gives $\mathbb E[X]=15$, $\mathbb E[X^2]=300$ and <variance> $300-15^2=75$, but an infinite third <moment>. At $\alpha=3/2$, the <mean> is 30 and the <variance> is infinite; at $\alpha=3/4$, even the <mean> is infinite, while the <median> remains the finite number $10\,2^{4/3}\simeq25.198$. At the boundary $\alpha=2$, the earlier example has <mean> 20 but infinite <variance>. A finite sample contains finite values almost surely; that fact cannot establish that the underlying <mean> or <variance> is finite.

= Pareto tail conditional scaling
{c}
{parent=Pareto distribution}
{title2=$X\mid X\geq u\sim\operatorname{Pareto}(\alpha,u),\quad u\geq\beta$}

Conditioning a <Pareto distribution> on exceeding a threshold changes its minimum while preserving its <shape parameter>. For $u\geq\beta$ and $x\geq u$, the definition of <conditional probability> gives
$$
\mathbb P(X>x\mid X\geq u)=\frac{(\beta/x)^\alpha}{(\beta/u)^\alpha}=\left(\frac{u}{x}\right)^\alpha.
$$
Equivalently, for $r\geq1$,
$$
\mathbb P(X/u>r\mid X\geq u)=r^{-\alpha}.
$$
The ratio to the threshold has the same <Pareto distribution> at every admissible threshold.<Random: Probability, Mathematical Statistics, Stochastic Processes by Kyle Siegrist/The Pareto Distribution>[Random: Probability, Mathematical Statistics, Stochastic Processes by Kyle Siegrist; The General Pareto Distribution, Related Distributions: closure under right-tail conditioning]{ref} For the chosen $\alpha=2$, $\beta=10$, only $1/4$ of outcomes exceed 20, but among those outcomes the <probability> of exceeding 40 is again $1/4$. When $\alpha>1$, the same identity gives <conditional expectation> $\mathbb E[X\mid X\geq u]=\alpha u/(\alpha-1)$.

This multiplicative scaling differs from the additive <memoryless property> of an <exponential distribution>. Indeed $Y=\log(X/\beta)$ has <exponential distribution> of rate $\alpha$, because $\mathbb P(Y>y)=e^{-\alpha y}$ for $y\geq0$. Thus logarithms turn multiplication into addition and explain the scaling property. Conversely, if $U$ has <uniform distribution> on $(0,1)$, <inverse transform sampling> gives $X=\beta(1-U)^{-1/\alpha}$.

The excess $Z=X-u$ conditional on $X\geq u$ has <survival function> $(1+z/u)^{-\alpha}$ for $z\geq0$. In the <generalized Pareto distribution> convention $\mathbb P(Z>z)=(1+\xi z/\sigma)^{-1/\xi}$, this means $\xi=1/\alpha$ and $\sigma=u/\alpha$. Here the excess starts at zero; the original <Pareto distribution> starts at $\beta>0$.

= Pareto top shares and the 80/20 rule
{c}
{parent=Pareto distribution}
{title2=$T(q)=q^{1-1/\alpha},\quad\alpha>1$}

For a nonnegative <random variable> with finite positive <mean>, the share of its total amount carried by the largest fraction $q$ of outcomes can be defined by dividing their contribution to the <expected value> by the whole <expected value>. For a <Pareto distribution>, let $0<q\leq1$ and choose $x_q=\beta q^{-1/\alpha}$, so that $\mathbb P(X\geq x_q)=q$. If $\alpha>1$, direct integration gives
$$
T(q)=\frac{\mathbb E[X\mathbf1_{\{X\geq x_q\}}]}{\mathbb E[X]}
=\frac{\alpha\beta^\alpha x_q^{1-\alpha}/(\alpha-1)}{\alpha\beta/(\alpha-1)}
=q^{1-1/\alpha}.
$$
This is a share of amount, distinct from the fraction of outcomes. It depends on $\alpha$, but not on the unit of measurement $\beta$.<Power laws, Pareto distributions and Zipf's law by Mark Newman>[Section III.D, pp. 10–11, Eqs. (27)–(28): upper-population share; Newman's density exponent equals alpha+1 in the present tail-exponent convention]{ref}

For the chosen $\alpha=2$, the top 20% starts at $x_{0.2}=10\sqrt5\simeq22.361$ and carries $T(0.2)=\sqrt{0.2}\simeq44.72\%$ of the amount. To make the top 20% carry exactly 80%, solve
$$
(1/5)^{1-1/\alpha}=4/5
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\alpha=\frac{\log5}{\log4}\simeq1.160964.
$$
The resulting <Pareto distribution> has a finite <mean> and infinite <variance>. The 80/20 rule selects one <shape parameter>; it is not a universal law, or even a property of every <Pareto distribution>. For $\alpha\leq1$, the population-share definition above is unavailable because its denominator is infinite; substituting such an $\alpha$ into the finite-mean formula is invalid.

= Rayleigh distribution
{parent=Continuous probability distribution}
{c}
{title2=$R\sim\operatorname{Rayleigh}(\sigma),\quad\sigma>0$}
{wiki}

The <Rayleigh distribution> is the <probability distribution> of the <Euclidean norm> of a two-dimensional <random vector> with <independent> zero-<mean> <normal random variables> of equal <variance> as components. If $X,Y\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$ are <independent>, then
$$
R=\sqrt{X^2+Y^2}\sim\operatorname{Rayleigh}(\sigma),\qquad
f_R(r)=\begin{cases}\dfrac r{\sigma^2}e^{-r^2/(2\sigma^2)},&r\geq0,\\0,&r<0.\end{cases}
$$
Here $\sigma$ is the <standard deviation> of each component, not of the <radius> $R$. At $\sigma=1$ this gives the unit-scale <probability density function> $r e^{-r^2/2}$.<Random: Probability, Mathematical Statistics, Stochastic Processes by Kyle Siegrist/The Rayleigh Distribution>[Random: Probability, Mathematical Statistics, Stochastic Processes by Kyle Siegrist; The General Rayleigh Distribution, Definition and Distribution Functions: independent equal-scale centered normal components and radial density]{ref}

This geometry turns two signed errors into one nonnegative distance. For example, suppose horizontal and vertical positioning errors are <independent> $N(0,4\,\mathrm{cm}^2)$ <random variables>. Their <means> both vanish, but the distance from the intended point is usually positive. The <Rayleigh distribution> with $\sigma=2\,\mathrm{cm}$ gives the <probability> of landing inside any centered <disk>. These are chosen model assumptions, rather than measurements of a particular instrument.

\Image[rayleigh-gaussian-area-and-radius.png]
{title=The center maximizes planar density, while a positive <radius> maximizes radial density}

Right: <probability density function> per unit <radius>; the shaded area is the probability of that same <annulus>. A single point and a single <circle> both have probability zero.}

An apparently paradoxical feature is that the most likely location per unit <area> is the origin, while the radial <mode> is $\sigma$. A narrow <annulus> farther out has more <area>. Near zero that growing <circumference> outweighs the falling planar density; farther out the <exponential function> wins. Distinguishing densities measured against different elements of <area> or length resolves the paradox.

= Rayleigh radius from Gaussian components
{c}
{parent=Rayleigh distribution}

The <joint probability density> of $X,Y$ is
$$
p(x,y)=\frac1{2\pi\sigma^2}e^{-(x^2+y^2)/(2\sigma^2)}.
$$
In <polar coordinates>, $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$, and the absolute <Jacobian determinant> is $r$. Thus the joint density of <radius> and angle factors as
$$
p_{R,\Theta}(r,\theta)=\frac r{\sigma^2}e^{-r^2/(2\sigma^2)}\frac1{2\pi},\qquad r>0,\quad0\leq\theta<2\pi.
$$
Integrating over angle gives the <Rayleigh distribution>. The same factorization shows that the angle has <uniform distribution> and is <independent> of the <radius>. This is a short <change of variables> argument: an <annulus> of width $dr$ has <area> $2\pi r\,dr$ to first order.

The assumptions are substantive. Unequal component <variances> give elliptical rather than circular density contours; a nonzero mean displaces the center. Even zero <covariance> and equal <normal distributions> do not suffice without <independence>. For an original counterexample, let $X\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$ and let $S$ be an <independent> <Rademacher random variable>. Put $Y=SX$. Then $Y$ has the same <normal distribution> as $X$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb E[SX^2]=0$. Nevertheless, all points lie on $y=x$ or $y=-x$, and $R=\sqrt2|X|$ has density
$$
f_R(r)=\frac1{\sigma\sqrt\pi}e^{-r^2/(4\sigma^2)},\qquad r\geq0,
$$
which is positive at zero, unlike the <Rayleigh distribution>. This pair is not a <Gaussian random vector>.

= Rayleigh radial thresholds and quantiles
{c}
{parent=Rayleigh distribution}
{title2=$\mathbb P(R>r)=e^{-r^2/(2\sigma^2)}$}

For $r\geq0$, integration gives the <survival function> and <cumulative distribution function>
$$
\mathbb P(R>r)=e^{-r^2/(2\sigma^2)},\qquad
F_R(r)=1-e^{-r^2/(2\sigma^2)}.
$$
Solving $F_R(r_p)=p$ gives the <quantile function>
$$
\boxed{r_p=\sigma\sqrt{-2\log(1-p)},\qquad0<p<1.}
$$
<Random: Probability, Mathematical Statistics, Stochastic Processes by Kyle Siegrist/The Rayleigh Distribution>[Random: Probability, Mathematical Statistics, Stochastic Processes by Kyle Siegrist; The General Rayleigh Distribution, Distribution Functions: CDF, quantile and survival functions]{ref}

For the chosen positioning model $\sigma=2\,\mathrm{cm}$,
$$
\mathbb P(R\leq2\,\mathrm{cm})=1-e^{-1/2}\simeq0.3935,\qquad
\mathbb P(R\leq4\,\mathrm{cm})=1-e^{-2}\simeq0.8647.
$$
The <disk> of <radius> one component <standard deviation> therefore contains about 39.35% of outcomes; the familiar one-dimensional normal interval percentage does not apply to a two-dimensional <disk>. The 95% and 99% radial thresholds are
$$
\boxed{r_{0.95}\simeq4.8955\,\mathrm{cm},\qquad r_{0.99}\simeq6.0697\,\mathrm{cm}.}
$$
A <circle> defined by $R=r_p$ itself still has <probability> zero; its enclosed <disk> carries the stated <probability>.

= Squared Rayleigh radius
{parent=Rayleigh distribution}
{title2=$R^2/(2\sigma^2)\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1)$}

Squaring a <Rayleigh distribution> variable produces an <exponential distribution>. For $w\geq0$,
$$
\mathbb P(R^2>w)=e^{-w/(2\sigma^2)},\qquad
R^2\sim\operatorname{Exp}\left(\frac1{2\sigma^2}\right),
$$
where the <exponential distribution> parameter is a rate. Equivalently, $R^2/\sigma^2$ has <chi-squared distribution> with two <degrees of freedom (statistics)>. An amplitude and its square therefore have different <probability density functions>, even though one determines the other.

This makes the <Rayleigh-normal scale mixture> transparent: at unit scale, $R^2$ is exponential, allowing a random normal scale to produce a <Laplace distribution>. Another equivalent parametrization uses a <Weibull distribution> with shape two and scale $\sqrt2\sigma$. Conventions that call this latter scale the Rayleigh scale differ by a factor of $\sqrt2$; check the <probability density function> before comparing numerical parameter values.<NIST Dataplot Reference Manual/Rayleigh Probability Density Function>[NIST Dataplot Reference Manual; Description: chi distribution with two degrees of freedom; Weibull shape two and scale sqrt(2) times sigma; warning about Rayleigh scale conventions]{ref}

= Rayleigh moments
{c}
{parent=Rayleigh distribution}
{title2=$\mathbb E[R^q]=\sigma^q2^{q/2}\Gamma(1+q/2),\quad q>-2$}

Using $u=r^2/(2\sigma^2)$ in the defining <expected value> integral gives
$$
\mathbb E[R^q]
=\int_0^\infty r^q\frac r{\sigma^2}e^{-r^2/(2\sigma^2)}\,dr
=\sigma^q2^{q/2}\int_0^\infty u^{q/2}e^{-u}\,du
=\sigma^q2^{q/2}\Gamma(1+q/2),\qquad q>-2.
$$
The last integral is the <gamma function>. Near zero, the integrand before substitution behaves as $r^{q+1}$, explaining the threshold $q>-2$; at infinity the Gaussian tail controls every power. In particular,
$$
\mathbb E[R]=\sigma\sqrt{\frac\pi2},\quad
\mathbb E[R^2]=2\sigma^2,\quad
\operatorname{Var}(R)=\left(2-\frac\pi2\right)\sigma^2.
$$

For the chosen $\sigma=2\,\mathrm{cm}$, the <mean> <radius> is about $2.5066\,\mathrm{cm}$, its <median> is $2\sqrt{2\log2}\simeq2.3548\,\mathrm{cm}$, and its <mode> is $2\,\mathrm{cm}$. Its <root mean square> is $2\sqrt2\simeq2.8284\,\mathrm{cm}$. Different summaries answer different questions; the component scale $\sigma$ is neither the radial <mean> nor the radial <standard deviation>.

= Random-phase amplitude and the Rayleigh distribution
{parent=Rayleigh distribution}

In an 1880 paper, <John William Strutt>, known as <Lord Rayleigh>, studied the resultant of many <oscillations> with a common <frequency> and arbitrary <oscillation phases>. His large-number calculation distinguished a mean intensity from a definite resultant <amplitude> and used planar coordinates followed by a polar integral. The resulting <amplitude> density is the <Rayleigh distribution> in a different scale convention. This is a large-number description, not an exact law for every finite collection of <oscillations>.<On the resultant of a large number of vibrations of the same pitch and of arbitrary phase by John William Strutt, 3rd Baron Rayleigh>[Philosophical Magazine, Series 5, volume 10 (August 1880), pp. 73–74: expected intensity versus definite resultant; pp. 75–77: planar construction and polar integration; primary-paper transcription inspected on Scribd]{ref}

To see the connection in a chosen model, take <independent> <oscillation phases> $\Theta_j$ with <uniform distribution> on $[0,2\pi)$ and equal component <amplitude> $a$. The <phasor> sum has coordinates
$$
X_n=a\sum_{j=1}^n\cos\Theta_j,\qquad Y_n=a\sum_{j=1}^n\sin\Theta_j.
$$
Both have <mean> zero, <variance> $na^2/2$ and zero <covariance>. The <multivariate central limit theorem> gives an approximately circular <Gaussian random vector> for large $n$, with $\sigma=a\sqrt{n/2}$. Its <amplitude> $\sqrt{X_n^2+Y_n^2}$ is therefore approximately Rayleigh. For every finite $n$, that <amplitude> is bounded by $na$, whereas a <Rayleigh distribution> has an unbounded tail. At $n=1$ it is exactly $a$. This simple endpoint check prevents the approximation from becoming a false exact identity.

= Hypoexponential distribution
{parent=Continuous probability distribution}
{wiki}

A hypoexponential distribution is the distribution of a sum of <independent> variables with <exponential distributions>. For two positive unequal rates $\lambda,\mu$, <convolution of independent random variables> gives the <probability density function>
$$
f(s)=\frac{\lambda\mu}{\mu-\lambda}(e^{-\lambda s}-e^{-\mu s}),\qquad s\geq0.
$$
It is zero for negative $s$. Its equal-rate limit is the shape-two <gamma distribution> with rate $\lambda$.

= Serial reaction dwell-time density
{parent=Hypoexponential distribution}
{title2=$p(t)=k_1k_2(e^{-k_1t}-e^{-k_2t})/(k_2-k_1)$}

Two sequential independent memoryless reaction waits have a <hypoexponential distribution>. Convolution gives $p(t)=k_1k_2(e^{-k_1t}-e^{-k_2t})/(k_2-k_1)$. Equal rates give the <Erlang distribution> $k^2te^{-kt}$. A nonzero mode distinguishes this ideal two-event model from a single exponential wait, but does not chemically identify the events.

= Sum of exponential variables with linearly increasing rates
{parent=Hypoexponential distribution}
{title2=$\sum_{i=1}^N T_i\overset d=\max_{i\leq N}E_i$}

If the <independent> <exponential random variables> $T_i$ have rates $i\lambda$, then
$$
\sum_{i=1}^N T_i\ \overset{d}{=}\ \max_{1\leq i\leq N}E_i,\qquad
\mathbb P\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^NT_i\leq t\right)=(1-e^{-\lambda t})^N\quad(t\geq0),
$$
where the $E_i$ are <independent> <exponential random variables> of rate $\lambda$. To see this, the first of $N$ independent exponential clocks rings at rate $N\lambda$; after it rings, the <memoryless property> leaves $N-1$ independent rate-$\lambda$ clocks. The successive <order statistic> gaps are therefore independent with rates $N\lambda,(N-1)\lambda,\ldots,\lambda$. Their sum has the same distribution as $\sum T_i$. In particular, for $x>0$, the tail at $Nx$ has logarithm $-\lambda Nx+o(N)$, since $1-(1-e^{-\lambda Nx})^N\sim N e^{-\lambda Nx}$. The sum divided by $N$ has a <large deviation principle> with speed $N$ and <rate function> $\lambda x$ on $x\geq0$, infinity otherwise.

= Conditional split of two exponential times
{parent=Hypoexponential distribution}
{title2=$E[S_1\mid S_1+S_2=t]=\frac1{\lambda_1-\lambda_2}-\frac t{e^{(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)t}-1}$}

For <independent> <exponential distributions> with rates $\lambda_1>\lambda_2>0$, let $T=S_1+S_2$. Dividing the joint density of $(S_1,T)$ by the <hypoexponential distribution> density of $T$ gives a <truncated exponential distribution> on $(0,t)$ with rate $\lambda_1-\lambda_2$. Its displayed <conditional expectation> supplies an E-step when only pair totals are observed. At equal rates the conditional split is uniform, with mean $t/2$.

= Student t-distribution
{c}
{synonym}

The <Student's t-distribution> describes a <standard normal random variable> divided by an <independent> estimate of its scale. For any real $\nu>0$, let $Z\sim N(0,1)$ have the <standard normal distribution> and let $V\sim\chi^2_\nu$ have the <chi-squared distribution>, with $Z$ and $V$ <independent>. Then
$$
T=\frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/\nu}}\sim t_\nu.
$$
The parameter $\nu$ is called the number of <statistical degrees of freedom>. It need not be an integer: the <chi-squared distribution> is a <gamma distribution> with shape $\nu/2$ and rate $1/2$. The <independence> assumption is essential; specifying only the two <marginal distributions> does not determine the <probability distribution> of their ratio.

Its <probability density function> on the whole <real line> is
$$
f_\nu(t)=\frac{\Gamma((\nu+1)/2)}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\,\Gamma(\nu/2)}\left(1+\frac{t^2}{\nu}\right)^{-(\nu+1)/2},\qquad -\infty<t<\infty,
$$
where $\Gamma$ is the <gamma function>. Every member is a <symmetric distribution> about zero. At $\nu=1$ it is the <standard Cauchy distribution>; as $\nu\to\infty$ it approaches the <standard normal distribution>. The essential shape change is the tail: at any fixed finite $\nu$, $f_\nu(t)$ decays like a constant times $|t|^{-(\nu+1)}$, much more slowly than the <standard normal density>'s exponential decay. Thus it is a <heavy-tailed distribution>. <NIST and SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods/1.3.6.6.4. t Distribution>[NIST and SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods; NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook, Section 1.3.6.6.4: density, Cauchy special case and normal limit; the ratio derivation here also permits noninteger degrees of freedom]{ref}

\Image[student-t-density-families.png]
{title=Student t densities with 1, 3 and 10 degrees of freedom compared with the standard normal density}

The left panel shows the centers; the right panel uses a <logarithmic scale> to reveal the tails that a linear plot hides. All four curves have location zero and scale one. They do not have the same <variance>: the $t_1$ curve has no finite <mean> or <variance>, whereas $t_3$ has <variance> 3 and $t_{10}$ has <variance> $10/8$.

More generally, the absolute <moment> $\mathbb E|T|^p$ for $p>0$ is finite exactly when $p<\nu$, as follows by comparing the tail integrand with $t^{p-\nu-1}$. Consequently
$$
\mathbb E[T]=0\quad(\nu>1),\qquad \operatorname{Var}(T)=\frac{\nu}{\nu-2}\quad(\nu>2).
$$
For $0<\nu\leq1$ there is no finite <mean>; symmetry alone cannot supply one. For $1<\nu\leq2$ the <mean> exists but the <variance> is infinite. For $0<\nu\leq1$ even the centered definition of <variance> is unavailable because the <mean> does not exist.

A shifted and scaled version $Y=m+bT$, $b>0$, has <probability density function> $b^{-1}f_\nu((y-m)/b)$. The scale $b$ is not its <standard deviation>: for $\nu>2$,
$$
\operatorname{SD}(Y)=b\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{\nu-2}}.
$$
For example, $\nu=3$ and scale $b=2$ give <standard deviation> $2\sqrt3$, rather than 2. The distinction matters when using a <Student's t-distribution> to model data with occasional extreme values, as well as when interpreting a <Student t confidence interval>.

= Normal scale mixture representation of Student t
{parent=Student's t-distribution}
{title2=$T=Z/\sqrt{\Lambda/\nu},\quad\Lambda\sim\chi_\nu^2$}

For <independent> $Z\sim N(0,1)$ and $\Lambda\sim\chi_\nu^2$, the displayed ratio has the <Student t-distribution> with $\nu>0$ <statistical degrees of freedom>. Equivalently, conditional on $\Lambda=\lambda>0$, $T$ has <normal distribution> $N(0,\nu/\lambda)$. This is a <normal scale mixture representation of Student t>: a small latent denominator occasionally produces a much larger spread. The result is a <heavy-tailed distribution> even though every conditional <probability distribution> is a <normal distribution>.

For a short derivation, <independence> lets us multiply the conditional <normal density> and the <chi-squared distribution>'s <probability density function>, then integrate over the unobserved $\lambda$:
$$
f_\nu(t)=\int_0^\infty \sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\nu}}e^{-\lambda t^2/(2\nu)}\frac{\lambda^{\nu/2-1}e^{-\lambda/2}}{2^{\nu/2}\Gamma(\nu/2)}\,d\lambda.
$$
The resulting <gamma function> integral has shape $(\nu+1)/2$ and rate $(1+t^2/\nu)/2$, yielding the normalized <probability density function> above. Also, for $\nu>2$, <conditional expectation> gives $\mathbb E[T^2]=\nu\mathbb E[\Lambda^{-1}]=\nu/(\nu-2)$. Thus unit scale does not mean unit <variance>. Finally, $\mathbb E[\Lambda/\nu]=1$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\Lambda/\nu)=2/\nu$ imply <convergence in probability> to 1 by <Chebyshev's inequality>; <Slutsky's theorem> then gives the <standard normal distribution> limit.

= Noncentral t-distribution
{parent=Student's t-distribution}
{title2=$t_\nu(\delta)$}
{wiki}

If $Z\sim N(0,1)$ and $U\sim\chi^2_\nu$ are <independent>, then $(Z+\delta)/\sqrt{U/\nu}$ has the <noncentral t-distribution> with $\nu>0$ <statistical degrees of freedom> and noncentrality $\delta$. It describes the <test statistic> of a <Student's t-test> under a shifted <alternative hypothesis>. At $\delta=0$ it reduces to the <Student's t-distribution>. This is different from adding a constant to a central <Student t-distribution>: here the shift is inside the random denominator. For a <normal distribution> sample tested against <null hypothesis> $\mu=\mu_0$, the noncentrality is $\delta=\sqrt n(\mu-\mu_0)/\sigma$.

= Studentized normal sample mean
{parent=Student's t-distribution}
{title2=$T=(\bar X-\mu)/(S/\sqrt n)\sim t_{n-1}$}

Suppose $X_1,\ldots,X_n$, $n\geq2$, are <independent and identically distributed> with <normal distribution> $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, where the <mean> $\mu$ and <standard deviation> $\sigma>0$ are unknown. Define the <sample mean> and <sample variance> by
$$
\bar X=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_i,\qquad S^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X)^2.
$$
Under these assumptions,
$$
Z=\frac{\sqrt n(\bar X-\mu)}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1),\qquad V=\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1},\qquad Z\text{ and }V\text{ are independent}.
$$
Substitution gives the exact ratio
$$
\frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/(n-1)}}=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1}.
$$
The unknown $\sigma$ cancels. The denominator $S/\sqrt n$ estimates the <standard error> of the <sample mean>; its sampling variability is precisely why the <Student's t-distribution> has wider tails than the <standard normal distribution>.

The crucial <independence> has a geometric explanation. Apply an <orthogonal transformation> to the standardized observations $(X_i-\mu)/\sigma$, choosing its first coordinate along $(1,\ldots,1)/\sqrt n$. That coordinate is $Z$; the remaining $n-1$ coordinates are <independent> <standard normal random variables> whose squared sum is $V$. Their <joint probability density> factorizes because the transformation preserves the squared length. This also explains the loss of one <statistical degree of freedom> when estimating the <mean>. Uncorrelatedness alone would not establish the required <independence> for arbitrary data.

The result is exact for every $n\geq2$ under the stated <normal distribution> model. A small <sample size> by itself does not justify it: a sample from an arbitrary <probability distribution> need not have the displayed <chi-squared distribution> or the necessary <independence>.

= Small normal sample mean confidence interval
{parent=Studentized normal sample mean}

Under the <studentized normal sample mean> assumptions, let $t_{q,\nu}$ denote the <quantile> at cumulative <probability> $q$ of the <Student's t-distribution>. For $0<\alpha<1$, rearranging the event $|T|\leq t_{1-\alpha/2,n-1}$ gives a <Student t confidence interval> for the unknown <mean> $\mu$ with exact <coverage probability> $1-\alpha$. <NIST and SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods/1.3.5.2. Confidence Limits for the Mean>[NIST and SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods; NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook, Section 1.3.5.2: confidence limits for the mean, interval formula and repeated-sampling interpretation]{ref}

Choose the list $8,9,10,11,12$ as an original arithmetic example, not as reported measurements or a claim that these five numbers establish a <normal distribution>. If such values were obtained under the assumed <independent and identically distributed> <normal distribution> model with unknown $\sigma$, then
$$
n=5,\qquad\bar x=10,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^5(x_i-\bar x)^2=10,\qquad s^2=\frac{10}{4}=2.5,\qquad\frac{s}{\sqrt5}=\sqrt{0.5}.
$$
There are four <statistical degrees of freedom>. For a 95% <confidence interval>, $t_{0.975,4}=2.776445105\ldots$, so
$$
\bar x\pm t_{0.975,4}\frac{s}{\sqrt5}=10\pm1.963243161\ldots=[8.036756839\ldots,\ 11.963243161\ldots].
$$
Using the <standard normal quantile> $z_{0.975}=1.959963985\ldots$ with the same estimated <standard error> would give only $10\pm1.385903824\ldots$. That underestimates the correct margin by about 29.4%. Indeed its actual <coverage probability> under this <normal distribution> model is $\mathbb P(|t_4|\leq z_{0.975})\simeq0.8784$, rather than 0.95.

The observations, the <sample mean> and the <confidence interval> all have different meanings. The bar estimates the population <mean>; it is not an interval intended to contain 95% of individual observations. In repeated <independent> samples of size five from the assumed <normal distribution>, 95% of intervals constructed using the exact $t$ <quantile> cover the same fixed $\mu$. This is not a 95% <posterior probability> that the fixed <statistical parameter> lies in this particular realized interval. Such a probability requires a separate <Bayesian inference> model.

For this four-degree case, the <cumulative distribution function> has an especially simple form:
$$
F_4(t)=\frac12+\frac34a-\frac14a^3,\qquad a=\frac{t}{\sqrt{4+t^2}}.
$$
One can check the formula by <differentiation>: $F_4'(t)=\frac38(1+t^2/4)^{-5/2}=f_4(t)$, with limits zero and one at the two ends of the <real line>. Solving the increasing equation $F_4(t)=0.975$ by the <bisection method> gives the <quantile> used above.

= Width distribution of a Student confidence interval

= History of Student's t-distribution
{parent=Student's t-distribution}

<William Sealy Gosset> worked at Guinness as a chemist and brewer and developed methods of <statistical inference> for small samples. He published under the pseudonym "Student". His employment as a chemist is independently recorded by MacTutor; the Dictionary of Irish Biography identifies him as an applied statistician and brewer and explains the pseudonym policy. <William Sealy Gosset by John Joseph O'Connor and Edmund Frederick Robertson>[Biography, opening paragraphs and discussion of the name Student]{ref} <Gosset, William Sealy (‘Student’) by Philip J. The 1908 paper https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Probable_Error_of_a_Mean[The Probable Error of a Mean] appeared in Biometrika 6(1), pages 1–25, with the byline "Student". Its introduction identifies the uncertainty introduced by estimating a <standard deviation> from few observations and explicitly assumes a <normal distribution>. Sections II and III develop the relation between sample spread and the standardized <sample mean>. Its notation uses a sample spread with divisor $n$ and calls the ratio $z$; with that convention, the modern $T$ equals $\sqrt{n-1}\,z$. The modern exact <independence> argument is given under <studentized normal sample mean>. <The Probable Error of a Mean by William Sealy Gosset>[1908 original text: byline, Introduction, Section I definition of sample spread, and Sections II–III]{ref}

Section VI describes a physical sampling check: 3,000 cards carrying existing paired measurements were shuffled, then divided into 750 groups of four. He compared observed frequencies of sample spread and standardized <sample mean> with theoretical frequencies. This was a check using finite, grouped, approximately <normal distribution> data; it did not prove exact calibration for arbitrary populations. <The Probable Error of a Mean by William Sealy Gosset>[Section VI, Practical Test of the foregoing Equations: cards, groups of four and limitations from nonnormality and coarse grouping]{ref}

= Countable sets have zero probability under a density
{parent=Absolutely continuous probability distribution}

If a <random variable> has a <probability density function>, each singleton has probability zero because its density has zero <integral> there. For a <countable set> $D=\{d_1,d_2,\ldots\}$, remove duplicates to make the events $\{X=d_i\}$ disjoint. <Countable additivity> gives $\mathbb P(X\in D)=\sum_i\mathbb P(X=d_i)=0$. = Probability density function
{title2=$f_X(x)$}
{parent=Absolutely continuous probability distribution}
{wiki}

= Statistical probability density
{synonym}

A <probability density function> represents probabilities as areas under a nonnegative <measurable function>. For a real <random variable> with an <absolutely continuous probability distribution>, it satisfies
$$
\mathbb P(X\in A)=\int_A f_X(x)\,dx,\qquad
f_X(x)\geq0,\qquad \int_{\mathbb R}f_X(x)\,dx=1
$$
for every <Borel set> $A$. Its height is probability per unit of the outcome variable, not the probability of that exact outcome. Thus a <probability density function> may exceed one, while the probability of a <singleton> is zero. NIST describes the interval-probability interpretation through integration. https://itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda362.htm[NIST: probability density functions and interval probabilities]{ref}.

For the example $f_Y(y)=2y$ on $[0,1]$, $f_Y(0.8)=1.6$, but $\mathbb P(Y=0.8)=0$ and $\mathbb P(0.7<Y<0.9)=0.9^2-0.7^2=0.32$. The <cumulative distribution function> is $F_Y(y)=y^2$ within that <interval>, connecting density height to the local accumulation rate.

Changing the scale changes the density height. If $Z=100Y$, then $f_Z(z)=f_Y(z/100)/100=z/5000$ on $[0,100]$. At the corresponding value $z=80$, its height is $0.016$, while $\mathbb P(70<Z<90)=0.32$ is unchanged. The factor $1/100$ compensates for the wider integration variable. This is a <change of variables>, not a change in the underlying probabilities.

A <probability density function> is determined only <almost everywhere>: changing its values on a <Lebesgue measure> zero set changes no probabilities. The <Cantor distribution> has no such representation, and a <mixed probability distribution> can have both a point mass and a density component, so differentiating its <cumulative distribution function> does not recover all its mass.

= Change of variables for probability densities
{parent=Probability density function}
{title2=$f_Y(y)=f_X(h^{-1}(y))\left|\frac{d h^{-1}(y)}{dy}\right|$}

For a <random variable> $X$ with <probability density function> $f_X$ on an <interval>, let $Y=h(X)$, where $h$ is a <diffeomorphism> onto another <interval>. The <change of variables formula> gives
$$
f_Y(y)=f_X(h^{-1}(y))\left|\frac{d h^{-1}(y)}{dy}\right|.
$$
Indeed, integrating the right-hand side over an outcome interval gives the <probability> that $X$ lies in its inverse image. The absolute <derivative> preserves positive probability when $h$ reverses orientation. A <gravity deflection of an atomic beam> is a physical example: its speed-to-height transformation changes the <probability density> and its <mode>.

= Probability density of a product of random variables

The line $x=0$ has two-dimensional <Lebesgue measure> zero, so its omission creates no <probability mass>. A density may be infinite at exceptional points without creating an atom.

= Connectivity of three acute-angle sphere points
{parent=Central angle between independent uniform sphere points}
{title2=$P(\text{connected})=(\pi+2)/(4\pi)$}

Join three independent uniform points on a <sphere> when their central angle is acute. Given angle $\gamma$ between the first pair, the third point's two favorable <hemispheres> overlap in a <spherical lune> of area fraction $(\pi-\gamma)/(2\pi)$. = Pairwise acute central angles of three uniform sphere points

= Slow exponential plus an exponential sample mean
{parent=Exponential distribution}
{title2=$I(x)=\inf_{0<z\leq x}\{\lambda(x-z)+K_r(z)\}$}

Let $S$ be a fixed sum of <independent> <exponential random variables> with smallest rate $\lambda>0$, and let $Z_N$ be the sum of $N-k$ independent rate-$r$ <exponential random variables>, independent of $S$, with fixed $k$ and $r>\lambda$. $$
I(x)=\inf_{0<z\leq x}\{\lambda(x-z)+rz-1-\log(rz)\}
=\begin{cases}
rx-1-\log(rx),&0<x\leq(r-\lambda)^{-1},\\
\lambda x+\log((r-\lambda)/r),&x\geq(r-\lambda)^{-1},\\
\infty,&x\leq0.
\end{cases}
$$
Indeed the derivative of the quantity minimized with respect to $z$ is $r-\lambda-1/z$. Its minimizer is $\min(x,(r-\lambda)^{-1})$. Beyond that transition, a single slow <exponential random variable> carries the extra excursion; the <rate function> becomes affine.

Differentiating gives density $z^{1/a-1}/a$ on $(0,1)$, which is the <beta distribution> <probability density function> with parameters $(1/a,1)$. This also gives a simple instance of <inverse transform sampling>: to sample a density $\alpha z^{\alpha-1}$ on $(0,1)$, raise a uniform draw to the power $1/\alpha$.

= Product of independent uniform random variables
{parent=Continuous uniform distribution}
{title2=$f_{XY}(z)=b^{-2}\log(b^2/z),\quad0<z<b^2$}

For <independent random variables> $X,Y$ with the <uniform distribution> on $(0,b)$, use the <probability density of a product of random variables> and integrate $1/(b^2x)$ over $z/b<x<b$. The displayed <probability density function> is zero off $(0,b^2)$ and has <integral> one. More generally, for <independent and identically distributed> factors $U_j$ with this <uniform distribution>, $W_n=\prod_{j=1}^nU_j$ satisfies
$$
\mathbb E[W_n^\alpha]=\left(\frac{b^\alpha}{1+\alpha}\right)^n\quad(\alpha>-1),\qquad \mathbb E W_n=(b/2)^n,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(W_n)=(b^2/3)^n-(b^2/4)^n.
$$
The <expectation of a product of independent random variables> proves these identities directly.

= Coverage of antipodal points by a randomly oriented sector
{parent=Continuous uniform distribution}
{title2=$\mathbb P(\text{both covered}\mid\Phi=\phi)=\max(\phi-\pi,0)/\pi$}

For a sector of width $\phi$ and an independent uniformly distributed central bearing, a specified bearing is covered with probability $\phi/(2\pi)$. Two antipodal bearings cannot both be covered when $\phi<\pi$. For $\phi\ge\pi$, the allowable centre arcs overlap in two components of length $\phi-\pi$, proving the displayed <conditional probability>. Averaging over the width's <probability density function> gives unconditional coverage probabilities.

= Random quadratic with uniform coefficients
{parent=Continuous uniform distribution}
{title2=$r^2+2Vr+U=0$}

For independent $U,V$ uniform on $[0,1]$, real roots occur on $U\leq V^2$, a region of <probability> $1/3$. On that region both roots are nonpositive. Both lie in $[-1,0]$ exactly when $\max(0,2V-1)\leq U\leq V^2$. Its area is $1/12$, so the conditional <probability> of both absolute values being at most one is $1/4$. <Joint probability density> integration turns algebraic <discriminant> and root constraints into simple planar areas.

= Steinhaus first-moment lower bound

{c}

For independent <Steinhaus random variables>, condition on their acute angles and use the <sharp Rademacher second-moment inequality> on the $2d$ sign coefficients $a_i\cos\theta_i,ia_i\sin\theta_i$. = Sum of two independent uniform variables
{parent=Continuous uniform distribution}

For independent $U_1,U_2\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1)$, the sum has triangular density
$$
f(s)=
\begin{cases}
s,&0\leq s\leq1,\\
2-s,&1\leq s\leq2,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$
Its upper tail for $1\leq c\leq2$ is $(2-c)^2/2$.

= Uniform split of a random total
{parent=Continuous uniform distribution}

Let $X\geq0$ have density $g$, let $U$ be independent and uniform on $[0,1]$, and set
$$
Y=XU,\qquad Z=X(1-U).
$$
Then
$$
f_{Y,Z}(y,z)=\frac{g(y+z)}{y+z}\mathbf 1_{\{y,z\geq0\}},
\qquad
f_Y(y)=f_Z(y)=\int_y^\infty\frac{g(t)}t\,dt.
$$

= Exponential characterization by a uniform random split
{parent=Uniform split of a random total}

Write $h(y)=\int_y^\infty g(t)/t\,dt$. The two pieces in a uniform split are independent exactly when
$$
-h'(y+z)=h(y)h(z).
$$
Setting $z=0$ shows that $h(y)=ce^{-cy}$ for some $c>0$. Thus the pieces are independent exactly when they are independent exponential variables of the same rate; equivalently, the total has gamma density $c^2xe^{-cx}$.

= Double exponential distribution
{synonym}

The <Laplace distribution>, also called the <double exponential distribution>, is a <location-scale family> with <probability density function>
$$
f(x;\mu,b)=\frac1{2b}\exp\!\left(-\frac{|x-\mu|}{b}\right),\qquad x\in\mathbb R,\quad\mu\in\mathbb R,\quad b>0.
$$
The location $\mu$ is its <mean>, <median> and <mode>; $b$ controls its spread. In the centred rate convention, the same law has $\mu=0$ and $\theta=1/b>0$, giving $f(x)=\theta e^{-\theta|x|}/2$. Keeping track of whether a parameter is a scale or its reciprocal prevents reversing the effect of increasing it. <NIST and SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods/1.3.6.6.12. Double Exponential Distribution>[NIST and SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods; NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook, Section 1.3.6.6.12, Probability Density Function and Common Statistics; NIST denotes the scale by beta]{ref}

The two halves of the <probability density function> are reflected <exponential decay> curves, each with total <probability> $1/2$. Their meeting point has a sharp corner: the left and right <derivatives> are $1/(2b^2)$ and $-1/(2b^2)$. This finite peak is not a positive <probability mass> at $\mu$. Indeed
$$
\mathbb P(|X-\mu|\leq\varepsilon)=1-e^{-\varepsilon/b}\longrightarrow0\qquad(\varepsilon\downarrow0),
$$
so $\mathbb P(X=\mu)=0$, just as for any <absolutely continuous probability distribution>.

\Image[laplace-density-and-log-tail-comparison.png]
{title=Laplace and normal densities and two-sided tails with the same mean and variance}

The figure compares a <Laplace distribution> with $\mu=0$, $b=1$ against a <normal distribution> with <mean> zero and <standard deviation> $\sigma=\sqrt2$: both have <variance> 2. The right panel uses a <logarithmic scale> for the two-sided tail <probability> $\mathbb P(|X|>r)$. Its straight <Laplace distribution> curve comes from $e^{-r}$; the <normal distribution> tail falls faster. Thus values far from the center can be more plausible under a <Laplace distribution> even after matching the <variance>. These are theoretical curves, not measured frequencies. The tails still have <exponential decay>; this is not a <heavy-tailed distribution> in the convention requiring every positive <exponential moment> to diverge.

Integrating either half of the <probability density function> gives the <cumulative distribution function>
$$
F(x)=\begin{cases}
\tfrac12e^{(x-\mu)/b},&x<\mu,\\
1-\tfrac12e^{-(x-\mu)/b},&x\geq\mu.
\end{cases}
$$
In particular $F(\mu)=1/2$. The basic <moments> are
$$
\mathbb E[X]=\mu,\qquad \mathbb E|X-\mu|=b,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=2b^2,\qquad \operatorname{SD}(X)=\sqrt2\,b.
$$
Thus the scale is the <expected value> of the absolute distance from the center, and is not the <standard deviation>. <NIST and SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods/1.3.6.6.12. Double Exponential Distribution>[NIST and SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods; NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook, Section 1.3.6.6.12, Cumulative Distribution Function and Common Statistics; rescale the standard form by x=mu+bz]{ref} A short way to obtain the <moments> is to note that $R=|X-\mu|$ has an <exponential distribution> with rate $1/b$: $\mathbb P(R>r)=e^{-r/b}$ for $r\geq0$. Integrating gives $\mathbb E R=b$ and $\mathbb E R^2=2b^2$; the <symmetric distribution> gives the centered first <moment> zero.

= Square of a standard Laplace variable
{parent=Laplace distribution}
{title2=$F_{X^2}(y)=1-e^{-\sqrt y}\quad(y\geq0)$}

For a <random variable> $X$ with <probability density function> $e^{-|x|}/2$, the event $X^2\leq y$ is $-\sqrt y\leq X\leq\sqrt y$ for $y\geq0$. Integrating the symmetric density gives the displayed <cumulative distribution function>; it is zero for negative $y$. The density on $y>0$ is $e^{-\sqrt y}/(2\sqrt y)$. Its integrable singularity at zero does not create an atom, because the <cumulative distribution function> is continuous there.

= Known-rate Laplace location confidence interval
{parent=Laplace distribution}
{title2=$\overline X\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt2/(\lambda\sqrt n)$}

For an <independent and identically distributed> <Laplace distribution> sample with density $\lambda e^{-\lambda|x-\mu|}/2$ and known rate $\lambda>0$, the <sample mean> has <mean> $\mu$ and <variance> $2/(n\lambda^2)$. The <central limit theorem> gives the displayed asymptotic <confidence interval> for $\mu$ at confidence level $1-\alpha$. The <sample mean> need not be a <maximum likelihood estimator> for this construction to be valid. The <Laplace location and scale maximum likelihood> location is a <sample median>; that is a different choice of statistic.

= Laplace location and scale maximum likelihood
{c}
{parent=Laplace distribution}
{title2=$\widehat\mu\in\arg\min_a\sum_i|x_i-a|,\quad\widehat b=\frac1n\sum_i|x_i-\widehat\mu|$}

Suppose $X_1,\ldots,X_n$, $n\geq1$, are <independent and identically distributed> with <Laplace distribution> $\operatorname{Laplace}(\mu,b)$, where $b>0$. For realized values $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, the <likelihood function> and <log-likelihood function> are
$$
L(\mu,b)=(2b)^{-n}e^{-S(\mu)/b},\qquad \ell(\mu,b)=-n\log(2b)-\frac{S(\mu)}b,\qquad S(a)=\sum_{i=1}^n|x_i-a|.
$$
For fixed $b$, <maximum likelihood estimation> of the location therefore minimizes the sum of <absolute losses>. This connects the <Laplace distribution> to <robust statistics>: each observation contributes a cost growing linearly with its distance, whereas <squared-error loss> grows quadratically.

Why does a <sample median> minimize $S$? Between successive <order statistics>, moving $a$ a little to the right increases each distance to a value on the left and decreases each distance to a value on the right. Hence the slope is the number on the left minus the number on the right. It changes from negative to positive at the middle. More precisely, if $x_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq x_{(n)}$, all minimizing locations are
$$
\arg\min_a S(a)=\begin{cases}
\{x_{(m+1)}\},&n=2m+1,\\
[x_{(m)},x_{(m+1)}],&n=2m.
\end{cases}
$$
For an even <sample size>, the objective is flat throughout the interval between the two central <order statistics>. The midpoint convention for the <sample median> selects one solution; it does not prove uniqueness. <SciPy 1.18.0 documentation/Laplace (Double Exponential, Bilateral Exponential) Distribution>[SciPy 1.18.0 documentation; SciPy reference guide, Laplace distribution, ML location estimator and the even-N interval between the two central order statistics]{ref} This is the finite-sample counterpart of <median minimizes expected absolute loss>.

If $S(\widehat\mu)>0$, maximizing over $b>0$ gives
$$
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial b}=-\frac nb+\frac{S(\widehat\mu)}{b^2}=0,\qquad \widehat b=\frac{S(\widehat\mu)}n.
$$
The <derivative> is positive below this value and negative above it. Moreover the maximized <likelihood function> is proportional to $S(\mu)^{-n}$ wherever $S(\mu)>0$, so the same minimizing locations give the joint <maximum likelihood estimates>. <NIST and SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods/1.3.6.6.12. Double Exponential Distribution>[NIST and SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods; NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook, Section 1.3.6.6.12, Parameter Estimation; median and average absolute deviation formulas]{ref} The scale estimate divides by $n$; this formula is not an assertion that it is an <unbiased estimator>.

The boundary case matters: if all observations equal $c$, choose $\mu=c$. Then $S(c)=0$ and $L(c,b)=(2b)^{-n}\to\infty$ as $b\downarrow0$. There is no finite joint <maximum likelihood estimate> in the parameter space $b>0$. In particular, this always occurs for $n=1$. Writing $\widehat b=0$ does not produce an admissible <Laplace distribution>.

= Laplace median robustness example
{c}
{parent=Laplace location and scale maximum likelihood}

Choose the five numbers $1,2,2,5,10$ as an original illustration. Their <sample median> is 2 and their <sample mean> is 4. The sum of <absolute losses> at the <sample median> is
$$
S(2)=|1-2|+|2-2|+|2-2|+|5-2|+|10-2|=1+0+0+3+8=12.
$$
If these numbers were modeled as an <independent and identically distributed> <Laplace distribution> sample, the joint <maximum likelihood estimates> would therefore be $\widehat\mu=2$ and $\widehat b=12/5=2.4$. Using the <sample mean> instead gives $S(4)=14$, so it does not maximize this <likelihood function>.

Now replace only 10 by 100. The <sample median> stays at 2, while the <sample mean> becomes $(1+2+2+5+100)/5=22$. The minimized sum of <absolute losses> rises to $S(2)=102$, and the fitted scale rises to $\widehat b=102/5=20.4$. Stability of the fitted location therefore does not mean stability of every fitted parameter.

{title=Chosen five-value example: moving the largest value leaves the median fixed but moves the mean}

The left panel subtracts each objective's minimum so the two <absolute loss> curves can be compared despite the different totals. The right panel tracks the <sample mean> and <sample median> as the largest chosen value changes. This is an <outlier> sensitivity illustration, not actual observations or a check that a <Laplace distribution> fits any population. In applications, <regression residuals> and the data-generating assumptions need separate examination; a stable <sample median> alone does not validate a <statistical model>.

= Laplace prior for sparse images
{c}
{parent=Laplace distribution}
{title2=$\pi_0(u)\propto e^{-\lambda\|u\|_1}$}

Independent centered <Laplace distributions> for pixel intensities give the <prior distribution>
$$
\pi_0(u)=(\lambda/2)^d e^{-\lambda\sum_{j=1}^d|u_j|},\qquad\lambda>0.
$$
This prior favors small intensities through its sharp peak at zero, while its tails permit isolated appreciable pixels. Its negative log density is an <L1 norm> penalty, so with a <Gaussian likelihood> a <maximum a posteriori estimate> minimizes a sum of squared residuals plus that penalty. A draw from this continuous prior has no exactly zero coordinate with positive probability; sparsity of a maximizing estimate and concentration of prior mass near zero are different statements. The product prior also does not encode spatial contiguity. <Inverse transform sampling> produces each coordinate from an independent <uniform distribution> by $\lambda^{-1}\log(2U)$ for $U\leq1/2$ and $-\lambda^{-1}\log(2(1-U))$ otherwise, ignoring the probability-zero endpoints.

= Least absolute deviations
{synonym}

For <linear regression> with fixed predictor vectors $X_i$, assume conditionally <independent> responses with a common scale $b>0$:
$$
Y_i\mid X_i\sim\operatorname{Laplace}(X_i^T\beta,b).
$$
Each conditional <median> and <mean> is $X_i^T\beta$. The <log-likelihood function> is $-n\log(2b)-S(\beta)/b$, so <maximum likelihood estimation> minimizes the sum of absolute <regression residuals>:
$$
S(\beta)=\sum_i|Y_i-X_i^T\beta|.
$$
This is <least absolute deviations>. With only an intercept, it reduces to the <Laplace location and scale maximum likelihood> problem, so a <sample median> is a fitted intercept. Provided the minimum $S(\widehat\beta)>0$, the scale estimate is $\widehat b=S(\widehat\beta)/n$; the conditional <standard deviation> is $\sqrt2\,b$. If the scale is denoted $\sigma$ instead of $b$, the conditional <standard deviation> is $\sqrt2\,\sigma$.

Minimizing a sum of <absolute losses> does not automatically give a unique coefficient vector. Even an intercept-only fit to an even <sample size> can have an interval of solutions, and redundant predictor columns can introduce further nonuniqueness. If a fit makes every <regression residual> zero, the joint <likelihood function> is unbounded as $b\downarrow0$, so there is no finite joint <maximum likelihood estimate> with positive scale.

The linear growth of <absolute loss> limits sensitivity to an extreme response at fixed predictors compared with <squared-error loss>. It does not protect against every <outlier>: an extreme predictor can have large <regression leverage>. The numerical example above changes a response in an intercept-only problem, and makes no general claim of immunity to such predictor changes.
