= Harmonic analysis
{wiki}

Harmonic analysis decomposes functions into elementary oscillations, generalizing <Fourier series> and the <Fourier transform>. On a locally compact Abelian group the elementary oscillations are <continuous unitary characters>, and <Haar measure> supplies translation-invariant integration.

= Carleson measure
{c}
{parent=Harmonic analysis}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mu(S(I))\le C m(I)$}

A positive <Borel measure> near a domain boundary is a Carleson measure when its mass in boundary neighborhoods is bounded by a constant times their boundary measure. On the <unit disc>, this becomes the displayed bound for every <Carleson box>. The equivalent <hyperbolic geodesic cap> version follows from the <geodesic-cap and Carleson-box comparison>. The <Carleson embedding theorem on the disk> explains why this geometric growth condition controls integration of analytic <Hardy spaces>.

= Carleson embedding theorem on the disk
{c}
{parent=Carleson measure}
{title2=$H^1(\mathbb D)\hookrightarrow L^1(\mu)\iff\mu\text{ is Carleson}$}

A positive <Borel measure> on the <unit disc> satisfies $\int|f|\,d\mu\le N\|f\|_1$ for every <H1 space on the unit disk> function exactly when it is a <Carleson measure>, with comparable optimal constants. The <normalized squared Cauchy kernel> tests necessity. For sufficiency, the boundary shadow of every point with $|f(z)|>t$ lies inside a component arc of $\{Nf>t\}$. The <geodesic-cap and Carleson-box comparison> bounds the measure of each shadow region by its component length, giving $\mu\{|f|>t\}\le C\|\mu\|_{\rm Carl}m\{Nf>t\}$. The <layer-cake representation> and <H1 non-tangential maximal inequality on the disk> give the embedding. The broader kernel and covering mechanism is discussed in https://www.math.ucla.edu/~tao/247b.1.07w/notes6.pdf[Terence Tao's harmonic-analysis notes, Exercise 2 after Lemma 4.1]; the analytic endpoint here uses the fractional-power argument just given.

= Carleson box
{c}
{parent=Carleson measure}
{title2=$S(I)=\{r\xi:\xi\in I,\ 1-m(I)<r<1\}$}

A boundary neighborhood over an arc $I$ of the <unit circle>, with radial depth equal to its normalized angular length $m(I)$. Changing that depth by any fixed positive factor gives an equivalent <Carleson measure> condition. Large arcs and central mass can be handled separately by finitely many fixed neighborhoods covering the <unit disc>.

= Non-tangential maximal function
{parent=Harmonic analysis}
{title2=$F^*_a(x)=\sup_{y>0,\ |x-x'|\leq ay}|F(x',y)|$}

This supremum function takes a supremum over a cone approaching the boundary point $x$; the positive number $a$ is its aperture. For an analytic upper-half-plane $H^1$ function, it belongs to $L^1$. To prove this without first assuming boundary absolute continuity, shift the function upwards, apply subharmonic Poisson comparison to its square root, and use the strong $L^2$ bound for the <Hardy-Littlewood maximal function>. <Fatou lemma> removes the shift.

= Poisson maximal function is dominated by the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function
{c}
{parent=Non-tangential maximal function}
{title2=$\sup_{z\in\Gamma_A(\xi)}|P[g](z)|\le C_A Mg(\xi)$}

For integrable boundary data $g$ on the <unit circle>, this follows from the <Poisson kernel> bound $C_A\delta/(\delta^2+d^2)$ in a fixed <Stolz region>. The arc of radius $\delta$ and successive dyadic annuli contribute at most $C_A2^{-k}Mg(\xi)$, which sum to the displayed estimate. This links harmonic extension to the <Hardy-Littlewood maximal function> and its strong $L^p$ bounds for $p>1$.

= Hardy space
{c}
{parent=Harmonic analysis}
{wiki}
{title2=$H^p$}

For $0<p<∞$, an analytic <Hardy space> controls the integral means of a <holomorphic function> as it approaches a domain boundary. On the disk, $H^p$ requires $\sup_{0<r<1}\int|F(re^{i\theta})|^p\,d\theta<\infty$; on the upper half-plane it requires $\sup_{y>0}\int_{\mathbb R}|f(x+iy)|^p\,dx<\infty$. For $p=\infty$ use a supremum norm. These analytic spaces differ from the <harmonic Hardy space of the upper half-space>; the <Hardy space of the circle> is the boundary Hilbert-space realization of the disk $H^2$ space.

= H1 space on the unit disk
{c}
{parent=Hardy space}
{title2=$H^1(\mathbb D)$}

This <Hardy space> consists of <holomorphic functions> with $\|f\|_1=\sup_{r<1}\int_{\partial\mathbb D}|f(r\xi)|\,dm(\xi)<\infty$, where $m$ is normalized angular measure. The <H1 non-tangential maximal inequality on the disk> controls their approach to the boundary, and the <Carleson embedding theorem on the disk> characterizes measures against which all these functions remain integrable.

= H1 non-tangential maximal inequality on the disk
{c}
{parent=H1 space on the unit disk}
{title2=$\|N_Af\|_{L^1}\le C_A\|f\|_{H^1}$}

For an analytic <H1 space on the unit disk> function, its fixed-aperture <non-tangential maximal function> obeys the displayed strong $L^1$ bound. First replace $f$ by $f_R(z)=f(Rz)$, $R<1$. The fact that <modulus powers of holomorphic functions are subharmonic> gives $|f_R|^{1/2}\le P[|f(R\xi)|^{1/2}]$. Since a <Poisson maximal function is dominated by the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function>, the strong $L^2$ <Hardy-Littlewood maximal inequality> yields $\int N_Af_R\le C_A\int|f(R\xi)|\le C_A\|f\|_1$. Pointwise interior convergence and the <Fatou lemma> remove $R$. Analyticity permits this endpoint estimate; general $L^1$ boundary data have only a weak $L^1$ maximal bound.

= Normalized squared Cauchy kernel
{parent=H1 space on the unit disk}
{title2=$f_a(z)=(1-|a|^2)/(1-\overline a z)^2$}

For $a\in\mathbb D$, this <holomorphic function> has <H1 space on the unit disk> norm one: its boundary modulus is the unit-mass <Poisson kernel on the circle>. The radial integrals are bounded by that boundary integral by <subharmonic> comparison and tend to it by continuity. Choosing $a$ at depth comparable to an arc's length makes $|f_a|$ bounded below by the reciprocal length throughout its <hyperbolic geodesic cap>. It tests necessity in the <Carleson embedding theorem on the disk>.

= H-infinity space
{c}
{parent=Hardy space}
{title2=$H^\infty(\mathbb D)$}

This <Hardy space> consists of bounded <holomorphic functions> on the <unit disc>, with norm $\|f\|_\infty=\sup_{z\in\mathbb D}|f(z)|$. Its almost-everywhere boundary realization follows from the <Fatou theorem for bounded holomorphic functions>.

= Fatou theorem for bounded holomorphic functions
{c}
{parent=H-infinity space}

A bounded <holomorphic function> on the <unit disc> has finite <nontangential limits> almost everywhere on its boundary. To outline a proof, bounded real and imaginary <harmonic> parts have $L^\infty$ boundary representations: apply the <Banach-Alaoglu theorem> to their circular traces and pass to the limit in the <Poisson integral on the unit disk>. Almost every boundary point is a <Lebesgue point>. The fact that a <Poisson integral converges nontangentially at Lebesgue points> gives the desired limits. Their $L^\infty$ norm equals the interior supremum norm, and the <Poisson integral> recovers the original function. Exceptional points can lack even radial limits; $\exp(i\operatorname{Log}(1-z))$ is a bounded example at the boundary point one.

= F. and M. Riesz theorem
{c}
{parent=Hardy space}
{wiki}

If a finite complex <Borel measure> $\mu$ on the circle has <Fourier coefficients> $\widehat\mu(n)=0$ for all $n<0$, then it is absolutely continuous with respect to arc length. The reflected Fourier convention gives the equivalent theorem with positive coefficients vanishing. The <Poisson integral on the unit disk> is analytic because of the one-sided Fourier spectrum. Subharmonic comparison for its square root and an $L^2$ maximal estimate produce an integrable radial maximum. This maximum dominates the weak-star boundary measure, forcing <absolute continuity of measures>. The same proof on the real line says that a finite measure with <Fourier transform of a finite measure> supported in $[0,\infty)$ has a Lebesgue density.

= Calderón–Zygmund decomposition
{c}
{parent=Harmonic analysis}
{title2=$f=g+\sum_Qb_Q$}

For $f\in L^1(\mathbb R^d)$ and a positive threshold $\lambda$, take maximal dyadic cubes with mean of $|f|$ exceeding $\lambda$. They are disjoint, have total volume at most $\|f\|_1/\lambda$, and their means are at most $2^d\lambda$. Replace $f$ inside each selected cube by its mean to obtain $g$; write $b_Q=(f-f_Q)\mathbf1_Q$. Then $\|g\|_\infty\leq2^d\lambda$, $\|g\|_1\leq\|f\|_1$, $\int b_Q=0$, and $\sum_Q\|b_Q\|_1\leq2\|f\|_1$. The outside bound follows from the <Lebesgue differentiation theorem>. This separates a bounded part from localized cancellation errors.

= Positive-definite function
{parent=Harmonic analysis}
{title2=$\sum_{i,j}z_i\overline{z_j}p(x_i x_j^{-1})\geq0$}
{wiki}

A complex-valued function on a group is positive-definite when the displayed inequality holds for every finite choice of points and coefficients. This includes positive semidefinite, rather than necessarily strictly positive definite, Gram matrices. In particular $p(e)\geq0$ and $|p(x)|\leq p(e)$. On an Abelian group, every convolution square $u*u^*$ with $u\in C_c(G)$ is continuous and positive-definite: its quadratic form is the integral of $|\sum_i z_i u(t+x_i)|^2$.

= Cyclic unitary representation of a positive-definite function
{parent=Positive-definite function}
{title2=$p(x)=\langle U_xv,v\rangle$}

For an Abelian group, on finite formal sums of group points, define $\langle[x],[y]\rangle=p(xy^{-1})$, with the first variable linear. Quotient by the null space of this <positive semidefinite> form and complete to a <Hilbert space>. Left translation is unitary, and $v=[e]$ generates a dense span of translated vectors. For continuous $p$, the equality $\|U_x[y]-[y]\|^2=2(p(e)-\operatorname{Re}p(x))$ gives <strong continuity>. This realizes $p$ as a <matrix coefficient> without assuming its integral representation.

= Bochner theorem
{c}
{synonym}

For a locally compact Hausdorff Abelian group, a continuous <positive-definite function> $p$ is uniquely representable as $p(x)=\int_{\widehat G}\chi(x)\,d\mu_p(\chi)$ for a finite positive <Radon measure> on the <Pontryagin dual group>. Its total mass is $p(e)$. Conversely every such finite positive measure defines a continuous positive-definite function.

= Bochner-Herglotz spectral theorem
{c}
{synonym}

A <positive-definite function> $r$ on the discrete group $\mathbb Z$ is the sequence of <Fourier coefficients> of a unique finite positive <Borel measure> on the circle, with total mass $r(0)$. Positivity makes the finite sums $\sum_{|n|<N}(1-|n|/N)r(n)e^{-2\pi int}$ nonnegative. Their measures converge weakly to the required measure; uniqueness follows from density of <trigonometric polynomials> in continuous functions.

= Wiener Fourier coefficient lemma
{c}
{parent=Bochner–Herglotz theorem}
{title2=$\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\sum_{n<N}|\widehat\sigma(n)|^2=\sum_t\sigma(\{t\})^2$}

For a finite positive <Borel measure> on the circle, averaged squared <Fourier coefficients> tend to the sum of squares of its atomic masses. Express each squared coefficient as a double integral and average the circle character. The averaged character is bounded by one, tends to one on the diagonal and to zero elsewhere, so the <dominated convergence theorem> gives the diagonal mass. In particular an atomless measure has vanishing averaged absolute coefficients by the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>.

= Bochner consistency identity for convolution squares
{c}
{parent=Bochner's theorem}
{title2=$\widehat p\,\mu_q=\widehat q\,\mu_p=\mu_{p*q}$}

For finite sums of compactly supported convolution squares, $\widehat p,\widehat q\geq0$. Insert the <Bochner theorem> representation of $q$ into $p*q$ and apply the <Fubini theorem>: its representing measure is $\widehat p\,\mu_q$. Reversing $p,q$ and using uniqueness gives the displayed identity. It makes the local quotients $\mu_p/\widehat p$ consistent wherever $\widehat p>0$.
