= Kernel of a linear map
{wiki}
{title2=$\ker T=\{v:T(v)=0\}$}

= Nullspace
{synonym}

= Kernel
{synonym}

= Null space
{synonym}

The <kernel of a linear map> $T:V\to W$ is the <vector subspace> of inputs sent to zero. <Linearity> makes it a <vector subspace>: if $T(v)=T(w)=0$, then $T(av+bw)=0$ for all <scalars> $a,b$. A <vector> in the <kernel> represents a change that the map cannot detect, since $T(x+v)=T(x)$ for every input $x$.

For $T:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^2$ given by $T(x,y,z)=(x+y,z)$, the zero-output equations are $x+y=0$ and $z=0$. Thus
$$
\ker T=\operatorname{span}\{(1,-1,0)\}.
$$
Increasing the first input while decreasing the second by the same amount leaves the output unchanged. For the <linear functional> $\varphi(x,y)=2x-y$, the <kernel> is the <straight line> $y=2x$ shown in its diagram. A <linear map> is <injective> exactly when its <kernel> is the zero <vector subspace>, because $T(v)=T(w)$ is equivalent to $T(v-w)=0$.

The <kernel> describes invisible changes, rather than all inputs that produce a specified nonzero output. If $T(x_0)=b$, the corresponding <fiber of a function> is the <affine solution space of a linear equation> $x_0+\ker T$. The difference of two inputs in that <fiber> belongs to the <kernel>; an input in that <fiber> need not. In particular, for $b\ne0$ the <fiber> cannot be a <vector subspace>, since it does not contain the <zero vector>.<Interactive Linear Algebra by Dan Margalit and others/Solution Sets>[Margalit and Rabinoff, Interactive Linear Algebra, §2.4.2, Key Observation and Remark: translate the homogeneous solution set by any particular solution]{ref}

For the <linear functional> $S:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R$, $S(x,y)=x+2y$, the <kernel> is $\operatorname{span}\{(2,-1)\}$. Each <fiber> $S^{-1}(\{c\})$ is the parallel <straight line> $(c,0)+\operatorname{span}\{(2,-1)\}$. The input <vector space> is two-dimensional, but the output is one <scalar>: a whole <straight line> of inputs collapses to one point of the output <real line>.

= Kernel of a Gram product
{parent=Kernel of a linear map}
{title2=$\ker(TT^\top)=\ker T^\top$}

For a real <matrix> $T$, a <Gram product> has the displayed <null space>. Here <Gram product> means a product $TT^\top$ or $T^\top T$.

**Proof.** The inclusion from right to left is immediate. Conversely, if $TT^\top x=0$, then
$$
0=x^\top TT^\top x=\|T^\top x\|^2,
$$
so $T^\top x=0$. Similarly $\ker(T^\top T)=\ker T$. Thus these <positive semidefinite matrices> retain the relevant <matrix rank> of $T$. The argument uses the positive <Euclidean norm>; a corresponding statement for an arbitrary indefinite <bilinear form> need not hold.

= Left null space
{parent=Kernel of a linear map}
{title2=$\ker A^T$}

For a real <matrix> $A$, its <left null space> consists of the <vectors> $w$ with $w^TA=0$, or equivalently $A^Tw=0$. It is the <null space> of the <matrix transpose>, and is the <orthogonal complement> of the <column space>. The <linear system> $Ax=b$ is solvable exactly when $w^Tb=0$ for every such $w$.

= Conserved inventory from a left null vector
{parent=Left null space}
{title2=$w^TA=0\ \Longrightarrow\ (w^Tc)'=-w^Tb$}

For the <linear ordinary differential equation> $c'=Ac-b$, a fixed <left null space> <vector> gives $(w^Tc)'=-w^Tb$. Thus $w^Tc$ is a <conserved quantity> when $w^Tb=0$. In a <box model> written for <concentrations> per unit <volume>, physical tracer inventory uses the box <volumes> as weights. Zero column sums make $w=\mathbf1$ possible, but that property must be checked rather than assumed.

= Injections between successive kernel quotients
{parent=Kernel of a linear map}
{title2=$\ker T^{i+1}/\ker T^i\hookrightarrow\ker T^i/\ker T^{i-1}$}

For a <linear map> $T:V\to V$, set $K_i=\ker T^i$ and $K_0=0$. Since $T(K_{i+1})\subseteq K_i$ and $T(K_i)\subseteq K_{i-1}$, the rule $v+K_i\mapsto Tv+K_{i-1}$ defines a <linear map> $K_{i+1}/K_i\to K_i/K_{i-1}$. It is <injective>: if $Tv\in K_{i-1}$, then $T^iv=0$, so $v\in K_i$. In finite <dimension>, this proves
$$
\dim K_{i+1}-\dim K_i\leq\dim K_i-\dim K_{i-1}.
$$
The <linear map> need not be <surjective>: for $T=0$ on a nonzero <vector space>, its $i=1$ instance is $0\to V$. For a <nilpotent linear map>, these <dimensions> are the increments in the <kernel dimensions of powers of a nilpotent map>.

= Null direction of a linear map
{parent=Kernel of a linear map}
{title2=$v\ne0,\quad T(v)=0$}

= Null directions of a linear map
{synonym}

A <null direction of a linear map> $T$ is represented by a nonzero <vector> $v$ in its <kernel>. Every <scalar multiple> $tv$ is an undetectable displacement: $T(x+tv)=T(x)$. The <vector subspace> $\operatorname{span}\{v\}$ describes the direction independently of the chosen nonzero representative. More generally, a <basis> of the <kernel> supplies all independent <null directions of a linear map>; their number is the <nullity>. The <zero vector> belongs to the <kernel> but supplies no direction.

= Rectangular kernel and solution-fibre example
{parent=Kernel of a linear map}

Consider the <linear map> $A:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^2$ represented by the rectangular <matrix>
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
A\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x+2y\\y+z\end{pmatrix}.
$$
To compute its <kernel>, solve the <homogeneous linear system> $x+2y=0$, $y+z=0$. Taking $y=t$ gives $x=-2t$, $z=-t$, so
$$
\ker A=\left\{t\begin{pmatrix}-2\\1\\-1\end{pmatrix}:t\in\mathbb R\right\},\qquad
v=\begin{pmatrix}-2\\1\\-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad Av=\begin{pmatrix}-2+2\\1-1\end{pmatrix}=0.
$$
Thus $v$ represents a <null direction of a linear map>: decreasing the first input by two, increasing the second by one, and decreasing the third by one changes neither output. Solving the <homogeneous linear system> is the concrete computation behind a <kernel>; the <column space> instead decides which outputs are attainable.<Linear Algebra, MIT 18.06, Spring 2010/Lecture 6: Column space and nullspace>[Gilbert Strang, Lecture 6: Column space and nullspace, opening lecture description: the distinct roles of Ax=0 and Ax=b]{ref}

Now prescribe the nonzero output $b=(3,2)^T$. The <linear system> $Au=b$ becomes $x+2y=3$, $y+z=2$. Again take $y=t$:
$$
u=\begin{pmatrix}3-2t\\t\\2-t\end{pmatrix}
=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}3\\0\\2\end{pmatrix}}_{u_0}
+t\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}-2\\1\\-1\end{pmatrix}}_{v},\qquad t\in\mathbb R.
$$
Direct substitution gives $Au_0=(3,2)^T$, and $A(u_0+tv)=Au_0+tAv=b$. Conversely, the two <linear equations> force exactly this form, so no solutions have been omitted. This <fiber> is an <affine subspace> of <dimension> one in the three-dimensional input <vector space>. It is parallel to the <kernel>, but does not pass through the origin. For instance, $u_0=(3,0,2)^T$ and $u_1=(1,1,1)^T$ both give $b$, while their difference $u_1-u_0=v$ gives zero. Adding $u_0$ and $u_1$ gives output $2b$, which is another way to see that this nonzero-output <fiber> is not a <vector subspace>.

\Image[kernel-rectangular-input-output-ambiguity.png]
{title=Three-dimensional inputs along one null direction give the same two-dimensional output}
{description=The blue <straight line> is the <kernel> in the actual three-dimensional input <vector space>. The orange solution <straight line> is its <translation> by $u_0$. Three marked inputs map to the single orange output $(3,2)$ in the two-dimensional output <vector space>; the blue <kernel> maps to zero. Finite plotted segments represent infinite <straight lines>.}

The first and third columns of $A$ are $(1,0)^T$ and $(0,1)^T$, a <basis> of the output <vector space>. Hence $A$ has <matrix rank> two and reaches every output. The <rank-nullity theorem> predicts <nullity> $3-2=1$ before we solve any <linear system>. It provides a useful completeness check: one nonzero <kernel> <vector> already forms a <basis> here, so a second independent <null direction of a linear map> would signal a calculation error. In general, <matrix rank> $r$ for an $m\times n$ <matrix> leaves $n-r$ independent <kernel> directions, rather than $m-r$.<Interactive Linear Algebra by Dan Margalit and others/The Rank Theorem>[Margalit and Rabinoff, Interactive Linear Algebra, §2.9, Rank Theorem: column count equals rank plus nullity]{ref}

= Noninjective linear measurement
{parent=Kernel of a linear map}
{title2=$T(x)=T(x')\iff x-x'\in\ker T$}

When a measurement model is a <linear map> $T$, its <kernel> records changes that its readout cannot distinguish. For example, the <rectangular kernel and solution-fibre example> measures only $x+2y$ and $y+z$. Even exact readouts $(3,2)$ leave the whole <fiber> $u_0+\ker A$ possible. Repeating either readout with greater precision cannot distinguish those inputs: their outputs agree exactly. This ambiguity follows from a nontrivial <kernel>, rather than from measurement error.

Additional information can remove a <null direction of a linear map>. In that example, adding a third <linear functional> that reads $y$ distinguishes $u_0+tv$, because its third readout is $t$. More generally, additional <linear functionals> $c_1,\ldots,c_k$ give unique recovery of all inputs exactly when
$$
\ker T\cap\ker c_1\cap\cdots\cap\ker c_k=\{0\}.
$$
This follows by applying the <trivial kernel> criterion to the combined <linear map> $x\mapsto(Tx,c_1(x),\ldots,c_k(x))$. Merely adding repeated <linear equations> does not meet this condition. On a finite-dimensional input <vector space>, each added <scalar> readout can increase the <matrix rank> by at most one, so at least $\dim\ker T$ extra independent readouts are needed for recovery of unrestricted inputs.

Restrictions on the possible inputs can also make recovery unique. If the allowed inputs form a <set> $D$, two allowed inputs are indistinguishable exactly when their difference is in the <kernel>. Thus $T$ is <injective> on $D$ precisely when no difference of distinct points of $D$ is a <kernel> <vector>. Such a restriction is extra information about the input; the original readout alone determines only its <fiber>.

= Nullity of a linear map
{parent=Kernel of a linear map}
{title2=$n(T)=\dim\ker T$}

= Nullity
{synonym}

The <nullity of a linear map> $T:V\to W$ is $n(T)=\dim\ker T$, the <dimension> of its <kernel>. It counts independent input directions that can change without changing the output. For a <finite-dimensional vector space> $V$, the <rank-nullity theorem> gives
$$
n(T)=\dim V-\operatorname{rank}T.
$$
For an $m\times n$ <matrix> $A$ of <matrix rank> $r$, this is $n-r$, using the number of columns, not the number of rows. This dimension identity is <Linear Algebra Done Right by Sheldon Axler (4th edition)>[Sheldon Axler, Linear Algebra Done Right, fourth edition, §3B, theorem 3.21]{ref}.

The <nullity> answers a practical question about a <linear system>: once one input is known to produce the requested output, how much freedom remains? If $T(x_0)=b$, its <fiber of a function> over $b$ is
$$
\boxed{T^{-1}(\{b\})=x_0+\ker T.}
$$
Indeed, $T(x)=T(x_0)$ exactly when $T(x-x_0)=0$. Thus every nonempty <fiber> is an <affine subspace> of <dimension> $n(T)$, with the same direction space as the <kernel>. The <kernel> itself is the <fiber> over zero. Outputs outside the <image of a linear map> have empty <fibers>; the <nullity> does not guarantee existence, and we assign no solution-space <dimension> to an empty <fiber> here.

\Image[singularity-nullity-fibers.png]
{title=Nullity one gives parallel solution lines for $F(x,y)=(x+y,0)$}
{description=Each colored input <straight line> $x+y=c$ is the <fiber> over the corresponding output $(c,0)$. The blue line through the origin is the <kernel>. The orange inputs $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ give the same output; their difference belongs to the <kernel>. The output $(1,1)$ lies outside the <image of a linear map> and has no input. Exact toy geometry, not measured data.}

In the diagram, $F:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^2$ has <matrix> $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, <matrix rank> one and <nullity> one. Its <kernel> has <basis> $(1,-1)$, and the full solution of $F(x,y)=(c,0)$ is $(x,y)=(c,0)+s(1,-1)$. One free <scalar> moves the solution along a <straight line>; the output $(c,1)$ is impossible.

The existing three-input example $T(x,y,z)=(x+y,z)$ instead has <rank> two and <nullity> one. Its <image of a linear map> is all of $\mathbb R^2$, and
$$
T^{-1}(\{(a,b)\})=(a,0,b)+\operatorname{span}\{(1,-1,0)\}.
$$
Every output has a solution <straight line>. In contrast, $U(x,y,z)=(x+y,0)$ has <rank> one and <nullity> two, with <kernel> <basis> $(1,-1,0),(0,0,1)$. Its reachable output $(a,0)$ has the solution <affine subspace>
$$
(x,y,z)=(a,0,0)+s(1,-1,0)+u(0,0,1),\qquad s,u\in\mathbb R,
$$
a plane, while $(a,b)$ with $b\ne0$ has no solution. These two <linear maps> have the same input and output dimensions but different freedom and reachability. Computing the <kernel> separates what an output determines from what an extra constraint would have to fix. For either example, a <basis> of the <kernel> gives one independent free <scalar> per <basis vector>, which is why <nullity> measures freedom rather than the number of solutions.

= Trivial kernel
{parent=Kernel of a linear map}

A <linear map> $T:V\to W$ has a trivial <kernel> when $\ker T=\{0_V\}$: the <zero vector> is the only input producing the zero output. The <kernel> is a <singleton set>, not the <empty set>, because every <linear map> sends zero to zero. Equivalently, its <nullity> is zero and $T$ is <injective>. This holds even without a finite-dimensional hypothesis; see <Linear Algebra Done Right by Sheldon Axler (4th edition)>.

The reason this zero-output test controls all outputs is <linearity>:
$$
T(v)=T(w)\iff T(v-w)=0\iff v-w\in\ker T.
$$
With a <trivial kernel>, equal outputs force equal inputs. Therefore every equation $T(x)=b$ has at most one solution. Existence is a separate question: $b$ must belong to the <image of a linear map>. In the <fiber> description under <nullity>, $x_0+\{0\}$ is just the single point $x_0$.

For example, the <linear map> $J:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^3$, $J(x,y)=(x,y,0)$, has <trivial kernel> and <full column rank>:
$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\ker J=\{0\},\qquad\operatorname{rank}J=2.
$$
The output $(3,4,0)$ comes from exactly $(3,4)$, while $(3,4,1)$ is unreachable. Conversely, $P:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^2$, $P(x,y,z)=(x,y)$, is <surjective> and has <full row rank>, but its <kernel> is $\operatorname{span}\{(0,0,1)\}$: every output $(a,b)$ comes from the solution <straight line> $(a,b,s)$.

For an $m\times n$ <matrix>, a <trivial kernel> is equivalent to <matrix rank> $n$ and <linear independence> of its columns. It can therefore occur only if $n\leq m$. If $m=n$, the <rank-nullity theorem> also forces <surjectivity>, giving an <invertible matrix>; if $m>n$, the example $J$ shows why unique recovery of reachable inputs does not imply that every output is reachable. Finally, a <trivial kernel> guarantees exact uniqueness without guaranteeing numerical stability: the <singular matrix> example $A(t)$ has <trivial kernel> for every $t\ne0$, even as its <condition number> grows without bound when $t$ approaches zero.

= Rank-nullity theorem
{parent=Kernel of a linear map}
{wiki}
{title2=$\dim V=\dim\ker T+\dim\operatorname{im}T$}

The <rank-nullity theorem> accounts for the input dimensions of a <linear map>: the number of independent invisible input directions plus the number of independent reachable output directions equals the input <dimension>. For $T:V\to W$ over a <field>, with finite-dimensional <domain of a function> $V$,
$$
\boxed{\dim V=\dim\ker T+\dim\operatorname{im}T.}
$$
Here $\dim\ker T$ is the <nullity>, and $\dim\operatorname{im}T$ is the <rank of a linear map>. The <kernel> lives in the input <vector space>, while the <image of a linear map> lives in the output <vector space>. For an $m$-by-$n$ <matrix> of <matrix rank> $r$, the formula is $n=(n-r)+r$: the number of columns counts input coordinates. <Linear Algebra Done Right by Sheldon Axler (4th edition)>[Sheldon Axler, Linear Algebra Done Right, fourth edition, §3B: fundamental theorem of linear maps]{ref}

For a computed example over the <real numbers>, take
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
T(x,y)=A\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}=(x+2y,0).
$$
The <kernel> is $\operatorname{span}\{(-2,1)\}$ and the <image of a linear map> is $\operatorname{span}\{(1,0)\}$. Each has <dimension> one, so the two input dimensions split as $2=1+1$. The <vector> $(3,1)$ can be written
$$
(3,1)=(1,2)+(2,-1),\qquad
T(1,2)=(5,0),\qquad T(2,-1)=(0,0).
$$
The second summand is undetectable: $T(3,1)=T(1,2)$. The picture uses the standard <dot product> to choose a visible direction perpendicular to the <kernel>; this is a convenient geometric choice, not an extra hypothesis of the <rank-nullity theorem>.

\Image[rank-nullity-domain-decomposition.png]
{title=Two input dimensions split into one visible direction and one kernel direction}
{description=For $T(x,y)=(x+2y,0)$, the input $(3,1)$ splits into $(1,2)$ and $(2,-1)$. Both $(3,1)$ and $(1,2)$ map to $(5,0)$, while the <kernel> component maps to zero. The horizontal output line is the entire <image of a linear map>.}

This explains a practical tradeoff in a <linear system>. If $T(x_0)=b$, every other input with output $b$ is $x_0+k$ for $k\in\ker T$. The <nullity> therefore counts independent freedom in an existing solution; the <rank> counts independent reachable output directions. In the example, $T(x,y)=(3,0)$ has all solutions $(x,y)=(3-2t,t)$, while $T(x,y)=(3,1)$ has none. The <cokernel> records this failure of reachability.

For an $m$-by-$n$ <matrix>, <injectivity> requires <matrix rank> $n$, and <surjectivity> requires <matrix rank> $m$. When $m\ne n$, these conditions differ. For example, $B:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^2$ with $B(x,y,z)=(x+2y,0)$ has <rank> one and <nullity> two, because $\ker B=\operatorname{span}\{(-2,1,0),(0,0,1)\}$. The input accounting is $3=2+1$, although only one of two output directions is reached.

Proof outline: extend a <basis> $k_1,\ldots,k_q$ of the <kernel> to a <basis> $k_1,\ldots,k_q,v_1,\ldots,v_r$ of $V$. Every output is a <linear combination> of $T(v_1),\ldots,T(v_r)$ because the $k_i$ vanish under $T$. If a <linear combination> of the $T(v_j)$ is zero, the corresponding combination of the $v_j$ lies in the <kernel>; <linear independence> of the full <basis> forces all its coefficients to vanish. Thus the added <basis vectors> give a <basis> of the <image of a linear map>. Counting them proves the formula.

= Nullity of a composition
{parent=Rank-nullity theorem}
{title2=$n(ST)=n(T)+\dim(\operatorname{im}T\cap\ker S)$}

For finite-dimensional <linear maps> $T:U\to V$ and $S:V\to W$, the restriction of $T$ to $\ker(ST)$ maps onto $\operatorname{im}T\cap\ker S$ and has kernel $\ker T$. The <rank-nullity theorem> on this restriction proves the title identity. Since the intersection has dimension at most $\dim\ker S$, it implies $n(ST)\leq n(T)+n(S)$. Equivalently, $r(ST)\geq r(T)+r(S)-\dim V$, the <rank inequality for a composition>. Also $\operatorname{im}(ST)=S(\operatorname{im}T)$ directly gives the upper bounds $r(ST)\leq\min(r(S),r(T))$.

= Equality in the rank-sum and nullity-product inequalities
{parent=Rank-nullity theorem}

For <endomorphisms> $S,T$ of a finite-dimensional space of <dimension> $d$, $r(S+T)\le r(S)+r(T)$ and $n(ST)\le n(S)+n(T)$. If both equalities hold, the first gives $r(S)+r(T)\le d$, while the second gives $2d-r(S)-r(T)\le d$. Thus the <rank> sum is $d$, $S+T$ is an <isomorphism>, and $ST=0$. Conversely, $ST=0$ implies $\operatorname{im}T\subseteq\ker S$ and hence <rank> sum at most $d$, whereas invertibility of $S+T$ gives <rank> sum at least $d$. This proves both equalities.

= Kernel jump in a parameter-dependent linear map
{parent=Rank-nullity theorem}

For a matrix whose entries depend on a parameter, a nonzero maximal minor certifies a fixed <matrix rank> away from its zeros. At a zero, other minors must still be checked: one vanishing minor does not itself imply a rank drop. Once the <matrix rank> is determined, the <rank-nullity theorem> gives the dimension of the <kernel of a linear map>. Exceptional parameters can then be solved separately to exhibit the additional <kernel of a linear map> directions.
