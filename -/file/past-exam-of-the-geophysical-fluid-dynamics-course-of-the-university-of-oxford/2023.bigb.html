= Solution
{parent=a}

Choose $y$ northward and $z$ upward in the Northern Hemisphere. In a locally linear <Boussinesq approximation>, write the background <buoyancy> as $\bar b=b_*+By+N^2z$, with horizontal <buoyancy gradient> $B<0$ and positive squared <buoyancy frequency> $N^2$. The <stable density stratification> resists a purely vertical <displacement>, but the <isopycnals> slope upward poleward:
$$
s_\rho=\left.\frac{dz}{dy}\right|_{\bar b}=-\frac{B}{N^2}>0.
$$
The <geopotential surfaces> are horizontal in this local approximation. Their inclination differs from that of the <isopycnals>, which is the relevant <baroclinicity>.

Exchange two equal small <fluid elements>, each of volume $V$, between positions separated by $(\delta y,\delta z)$. The <adiabatic parcel exchange> has no thermal diffusion, so each parcel retains its <buoyancy>. The background <buoyancy> difference is $\Delta\bar b=B\delta y+N^2\delta z$. Because the variable-density contribution to <gravitational potential energy> is $-\rho_0 Vbz$, the change in the pair's <gravitational potential energy> is
$$
\Delta E_p=\rho_0V\,\Delta\bar b\,\delta z
=\rho_0V\,\delta z(B\delta y+N^2\delta z).
$$
For a poleward-rising exchange, set $\delta z=s\delta y$ with $s>0$. Then
$$
\boxed{\Delta E_p<0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 0<s<s_\rho.}
$$
The trajectory lies between a <geopotential surface> and an <isopycnal>. The poleward-rising parcel starts more buoyant than the parcel it replaces, and the equatorward-sinking parcel starts less buoyant: the <buoyancy> does positive work on both. This releases <available potential energy> into <kinetic energy>, allowing <eddies> to amplify if the full rotating dynamics supports the displacement and dissipation does not remove the gain.

A trajectory steeper than the <isopycnals>, $s>s_\rho$, instead costs <gravitational potential energy>; an equatorward-rising trajectory also costs it. Motion exactly along a <geopotential surface> or an <isopycnal> gives zero change in this ideal exchange. At fixed $\delta y$, the largest release occurs at $s=s_\rho/2$, since $N^2s(s-s_\rho)$ is minimized there.

\Image[2023-sloping-parcel-exchange.png]
{title=Two <adiabatic parcel exchanges>: a shallow poleward rise releases <potential energy>, whereas a rise steeper than the <isopycnals> costs it. Slopes are exaggerated.}

This is an energetic possibility argument for <baroclinic instability>, not a sufficient <normal-mode> instability criterion. In particular, unrestricted parcel motion must not be assumed in a rotating fluid: <conservation of angular momentum> can impose an additional restoring effect. <Symmetric instability> has a separate <Ertel potential vorticity> criterion; ordinary <Eady instability> can occur without violating that criterion.

Let $\mathbf U_h=\Gamma z\,\mathbf u_h(x,y)$ denote the actual horizontal <velocity field>. With constant nonzero <Coriolis parameter> $f_0$, <geostrophic balance> and <hydrostatic balance>, using <pressure> per reference <density> $\pi$, give
$$
f_0\mathbf e_z\mathbin\times\mathbf U_h=-\nabla_h\pi,
\qquad \pi_z=b.
$$
Differentiate the first equation in $z$ to obtain <thermal-wind balance>:
$$
f_0\Gamma\mathbf e_z\mathbin\times\mathbf u_h=-\nabla_h b.
$$
Taking the <dot product> with $\mathbf U_h$ proves
$$
\boxed{\mathbf U_h\cdot\nabla_h b=0.}
$$
Thus the horizontal <velocity> is tangent to the horizontal <buoyancy> contours at every height. For a fluid whose thermal <equation of state> makes horizontal <buoyancy> contours coincide with horizontal <temperature> contours, there is no horizontal <advection> across those contours. In a dry <terrestrial atmosphere>, use <potential temperature> as the materially conserved thermal variable. A salinity contribution to <buoyancy> would require separate treatment before interpreting the result as a statement about <temperature>.

The usual statement about net meridional <heat transport> also follows explicitly. Introduce a horizontal <streamfunction> $\Psi$ such that $\mathbf u_h=(-\Psi_y,\Psi_x)$. Integration of <thermal-wind balance> gives
$$
b=f_0\Gamma\Psi+b_0(z),\qquad
v=\Gamma z\Psi_x,
$$
and hence
$$
vb=\partial_x\left[\Gamma z\left(\frac{f_0\Gamma}{2}\Psi^2+b_0\Psi\right)\right].
$$
For a zonally <periodic function> $\Psi$, or vanishing endpoint contributions, the zonal integral is zero. Likewise, the anomalous <eddy buoyancy flux> is
$$
\boxed{\overline{v'b'}=f_0\Gamma^2z\,\overline{\Psi'_x\Psi'}=0.}
$$
The required correlation between meridional <velocity> and thermal anomaly is absent. A nonzero vertical <phase> tilt, as in the next part, permits that correlation.

**Qualification of the wording.** The result does not say that the local <heat flux> vector $b\mathbf U_h$ vanishes, or that an arbitrary open-domain integral vanishes without <boundary conditions>. For a concrete counterexample to that literal reading, take $\mathbf u_h=(0,1)$ and $b=f_0\Gamma x+N^2z$. These satisfy the displayed <thermal-wind balance>, but $vb=\Gamma z(f_0\Gamma x+N^2z)$ is generally nonzero. What the hypotheses alone guarantee is zero transport across horizontal <buoyancy> contours; the vanishing zonally integrated <heat transport> uses the stated endpoint condition.

Take eastward $x$, upward $z$, positive <wavenumbers> $k,K$, and a real instantaneous amplitude $A$. A common growing factor does not affect the <phase> geometry. Put $C=\cosh(Kz)$ and $S=\sinh(Kz)$, using the <hyperbolic cosine> and <hyperbolic sine>. The prescribed <streamfunction> is
$$
\psi'=A[C\cos(kx)-\alpha S\sin(kx)]
=AR_\psi\cos[kx+\delta_\psi(z)],
$$
where
$$
R_\psi^2=C^2+\alpha^2S^2,\qquad
\delta_\psi=\arctan[\alpha\tanh(Kz)].
$$
Following a fixed <phase> gives
$$
\frac{dx_\psi}{dz}=-\frac{\delta_{\psi,z}}k
=-\frac{\alpha K}{k(C^2+\alpha^2S^2)}<0.
$$
Therefore the <streamfunction> pattern **tilts westward with height**. The lower boundary leads the upper boundary eastward.

In the conventional <quasi-geostrophic streamfunction> normalization, <hydrostatic balance> gives $b'=f_0\psi'_z$. Therefore
$$
b'=f_0AK[S\cos(kx)-\alpha C\sin(kx)].
$$
For the <phase> calculation, multiplication by the constant $f_0$ is immaterial. Define a continuous <phase> $\delta_b=\operatorname{atan2}(\alpha C,S)$; this avoids a spurious discontinuity at $z=0$. Then
$$
R_b^2=S^2+\alpha^2C^2,\qquad
\delta_{b,z}=-\frac{\alpha K}{S^2+\alpha^2C^2},
\qquad
\boxed{\frac{dx_b}{dz}=\frac{\alpha K}{k(S^2+\alpha^2C^2)}>0.}
$$
The <buoyancy perturbation> pattern **tilts eastward with height**, opposite to the <streamfunction> pattern. At $z=0$, $\psi'=A\cos(kx)$ while $b'=-f_0AK\alpha\sin(kx)$, so the two fields are a quarter <wavelength> apart there.

Both perturbation amplitudes are largest at the boundaries. Indeed,
$$
R_\psi^2=1+(1+\alpha^2)\sinh^2(Kz),\qquad
R_b^2=\alpha^2+(1+\alpha^2)\sinh^2(Kz),
$$
which increase with $|z|$. The physical reason is particularly clear through <stratified quasi-geostrophic inversion>: the <Eady model> has no interior background <potential-vorticity gradient>, and a growing <normal mode> has zero interior <potential vorticity> anomaly. The independent thermal anomalies reside at the rigid upper and lower boundaries, where meridional motion advects the basic horizontal <buoyancy gradient>. Each <Eady edge wave> induces an interior balanced flow that decays away from its boundary on scale $K^{-1}$. Their interacting, phase-locked combination produces the growing <baroclinic instability>. Boundary confinement of the thermal sources, rather than a rigid-wall requirement $\psi'=0$, explains the amplitude pattern.

For a sketch with a quarter-<wavelength> difference between the boundary <streamfunction> patterns, choose
$$
2\arctan[\alpha\tanh(KD)]=\frac\pi2,
\qquad \alpha\tanh(KD)=1.
$$
An upper positive <streamfunction> crest is then $\lambda_x/4$ west of its lower counterpart, where $\lambda_x=2\pi/k$. The <buoyancy perturbation> crests slope the other way. The quarter-<wavelength> boundary assumption is used for the <streamfunction>; differentiating in $z$ fixes the <buoyancy perturbation> and does not generally give it the same boundary <phase> separation.

\Image[2023-baroclinic-wave-structure.png]
{title=Original <Eady model> sketch: <streamfunction> crests tilt westward and <buoyancy perturbation> crests eastward. Dashed curves follow positive maxima; the <streamfunction> boundary <phase> difference is a quarter <wavelength>.}

The connection to poleward <heat transport> can be checked without relying on the sketch. The meridional <geostrophic flow> and <buoyancy perturbation> are
$$
v'=\psi'_x=-Ak[C\sin(kx)+\alpha S\cos(kx)],
\qquad
b'=f_0AK[S\cos(kx)-\alpha C\sin(kx)].
$$
Average over one zonal <wavelength>, using equal averages $1/2$ for squared <sine> and <cosine> and zero for their product:
$$
\boxed{\overline{v'b'}=\frac{f_0A^2kK\alpha}{2}(C^2-S^2)
=\frac{f_0A^2kK\alpha}{2}.}
$$
In the Northern Hemisphere this is positive: warm anomalies preferentially move northward and cold anomalies southward. For the same eastward-increasing basic <thermal wind> in the Southern Hemisphere, $f_0<0$ and the basic <buoyancy gradient> reverses; the negative northward <eddy buoyancy flux> is again poleward. For <thermal expansion coefficient> $\alpha_T>0$ and <specific heat capacity at constant pressure> $c_p$, the corresponding <sensible heat> flux in a thermal <Boussinesq approximation> is $\rho_0c_p\overline{v'T'}=\rho_0c_p\overline{v'b'}/(g\alpha_T)$. The growing amplitude makes this flux grow as $A^2$. The tilt supplies the correlation absent in an untilted disturbance and reduces the background <available potential energy>.

The low-latitude regions of <Earth> absorb more incoming solar <radiation> than they emit as outgoing <thermal radiation>, whereas high latitudes have a net annual <radiation> deficit. The obliquity of solar incidence and the distribution of daylight create a strong latitude dependence of absorbed solar <energy>; emitted <thermal radiation> varies less strongly because terrestrial <temperature> contrasts are smaller. The <terrestrial atmosphere> and <ocean> therefore carry <energy> from the tropical surplus toward the polar deficits. Otherwise the low latitudes would keep warming and the high latitudes cooling until a different local <radiative equilibrium> was reached.

For a steady, zonally averaged <energy balance>, let $R(\phi)$ be net downward radiation at the top of the <terrestrial atmosphere> and $H(\phi)$ the total northward <energy> transport across latitude $\phi$. A narrow spherical band and <conservation of energy> give
$$
\frac{dH}{d\phi}=2\pi a^2\cos\phi\,R(\phi),\qquad
H(\phi)=2\pi a^2\int_{-\pi/2}^{\phi}R(\varphi)\cos\varphi\,d\varphi.
$$
The poleward direction is northward in the Northern Hemisphere and southward in the Southern Hemisphere. Seasonal <internal energy> storage modifies the instantaneous balance, but not the annual need for poleward redistribution.

Several circulation structures contribute in addition to the transient <eddies> produced by <baroclinic instability> of the middle latitudes:

* The <Hadley cells>, mainly between the equator and about $30^\circ$ latitude, export <energy> in their upper poleward branches and return air equatorward near the surface. The transported quantity includes <enthalpy>, <gravitational potential energy> and <latent heat>; the upper and lower branches have different thermal properties. The cross-equatorial <Hadley cell> is especially important in the seasonal cycle.
* Quasi-stationary <Rossby waves>, associated with mountains and continental heating contrasts, carry <heat> in the extratropical <terrestrial atmosphere>. Their meridional <velocity> and <temperature> anomalies can have a poleward correlation. These are an additional time-mean contribution alongside travelling weather disturbances.
* The <ocean gyres> in tropical and subtropical basins redistribute warm water poleward in strong <western boundary currents> and return cooler water equatorward over broader regions. The thermal difference between the branches produces net <heat transport> despite little net meridional volume transport.
* The <oceanic overturning circulation>, including the <Atlantic meridional overturning circulation>, combines warm upper water moving poleward with colder deep return water. It transports substantial <heat> through the tropical and subtropical Atlantic toward the northern high latitudes. Wind forcing, <buoyancy> exchange and mixing all help maintain this global circulation.

The <Brewer-Dobson circulation> additionally redistributes <energy> in the <stratosphere>, with tropical ascent and extratropical descent, although most total atmospheric <heat transport> occurs lower down. An <Eulerian mean> middle-latitude <Ferrel cell> is thermally indirect and can oppose the poleward transport by <eddies>; listing every meridional circulation as a direct poleward heat carrier would be incorrect.

Use eastward $x$, northward $y$, <pressure coordinate> $p$ increasing downward, and <geopotential> $\Phi=gz$. Horizontal derivatives are taken on an <isobaric surface>. The horizontal <geostrophic balance> and vertical <hydrostatic balance> are
$$
f u_g=-\Phi_y,\qquad f v_g=\Phi_x,\qquad
\Phi_p=-\frac1\rho=-\frac{RT}{p},
$$
where $R$ is the <specific gas constant> of dry air and the final equality uses the <ideal gas law>. At fixed latitude $f$ is independent of $p$. Cross-differentiating gives
$$
f\,\partial_pu_g=-\partial_y\Phi_p=\frac R p\,T_y,
\qquad
f\,\partial_pv_g=\partial_x\Phi_p=-\frac R p\,T_x.
$$
Consequently the pressure-coordinate <thermal wind> equations are
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial u_g}{\partial\ln p}=\frac Rf\frac{\partial T}{\partial y},
\qquad
\frac{\partial v_g}{\partial\ln p}=-\frac Rf\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}.}
$$
Equivalently, $f\mathbf e_z\mathbin\times\partial_{\ln p}\mathbf u_g=R\nabla_pT$. Notice the sign: increasing eastward <velocity> upward means decreasing eastward <velocity> with $\ln p$, and hence poleward-decreasing <temperature> in the Northern Hemisphere.

For the <stratospheric polar vortex> estimate, the original diagram must be read on its logarithmic pressure axis. Near $60^\circ$ in the winter hemisphere, adjacent $10\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ wind contours suggest an increase of roughly $20\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ through a pressure decade surrounding $10\ \mathrm{mb}$: for example, about $10\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ near $30\ \mathrm{mb}$ and $30\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ near $3\ \mathrm{mb}$. These are coarse graphical readings, not a sounding. Thus
$$
\frac{\partial u_g}{\partial\ln p}\simeq
\frac{30-10}{\ln(3/30)}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}
\simeq-8.7\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.
$$
With $|f|=2\Omega\sin60^\circ\simeq1.26\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $R\simeq287\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, the poleward <temperature gradient> is approximately
$$
\frac{\partial T}{\partial y_{\rm pole}}
\simeq\frac{|f|}{R}\frac{\partial u_g}{\partial\ln p}
\simeq-3.8\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{K\,m^{-1}}
\simeq-0.42\ \mathrm{K\ per\ degree\ latitude}.
$$
If the vortex edge is taken to span roughly $50^\circ$ to $70^\circ$, its width is $a(20\pi/180)\simeq2.2\times10^6\ \mathrm m$, giving
$$
\boxed{T(70^\circ)-T(50^\circ)\simeq-8.5\ \mathrm K,
\quad\text{a poleward drop of order }10\ \mathrm K.}
$$
The estimate depends on the width assigned to the edge and the graphical <vertical shear>; a range of several to roughly fifteen kelvin is more justified than a precise value. The sign remains a cold winter pole in either hemisphere. The diagram does not by itself prescribe a unique pair of edge latitudes.

At low latitudes, horizontal free-atmospheric <temperature gradients> are usually much smaller than at the winter vortex edge. For a comparable finite wind <vertical shear>, the rearranged <thermal-wind balance>, $T_y=(f/R)u_{\ln p}$, is consistent with weaker <temperature gradients> as $|f|$ decreases. However, <geostrophic balance> loses validity near the equator because the <Coriolis parameter> vanishes; division by $f$ cannot be used there to derive a prediction. The <weak temperature gradient approximation> is better motivated by rapid horizontal <internal gravity wave> adjustment, together with tropical <convection> and the <Hadley cell>. <Thermal-wind balance> describes the balanced extratropical relation and its tendency approaching the tropics; it is not a complete causal explanation of equatorial thermal uniformity.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <quasi-geostrophic potential-vorticity equation> is the leading balanced form of <potential vorticity conservation> for an inviscid rotating fluid with <stable density stratification>, small <Rossby number> and an <adiabatic process> for each <fluid element>. Its parent conservation law is <Ertel potential vorticity> conservation. Horizontal <geostrophic balance>, vertical <hydrostatic balance>, and the <beta-plane approximation> reduce it to advection of one scalar by the leading horizontal <geostrophic flow>.

One can see its origin by combining the vertical <vorticity equation> with the thermal equation. Set $D_g=\partial_t+\mathbf u_g\cdot\nabla_h$, $\mathbf u_g=(-\psi_y,\psi_x)$, and $\zeta_g=\nabla_h^2\psi$. The <quasi-geostrophic approximation> gives
$$
D_g(\zeta_g+f_0+\beta y)=f_0w_z,
\qquad
D_gb+N^2w=0,\qquad b=f_0\psi_z.
$$
Here $b$ is the <buoyancy perturbation> relative to the reference <stable density stratification>, $N=N(z)$ its <buoyancy frequency>, and $w$ the small vertical <velocity>. The stretching of absolute <vorticity> in the first equation is offset by the change of <stratification> represented by the second. More precisely,
$$
D_g\partial_z\left(\frac{f_0b}{N^2}\right)
=\partial_z\left(\frac{f_0D_gb}{N^2}\right)
-\frac{f_0}{N^2}(\partial_z\mathbf u_g)\cdot\nabla_hb
=-f_0w_z.
$$
The last <dot product> is zero because <thermal-wind balance> makes $\partial_z\mathbf u_g$ perpendicular to $\nabla_hb$. Adding therefore yields
$$
\boxed{D_gq=0,\qquad
q=\underbrace{\nabla_h^2\psi}_{\text{relative vorticity}}
+\underbrace{f_0+\beta y}_{\text{planetary vorticity}}
+\underbrace{\partial_z\left(\frac{f_0^2}{N^2}\psi_z\right)}_{\text{stratification/stretching contribution}}.}
$$
The constant $f_0$ has no effect on the tendency, but its inclusion makes the connection with absolute <vorticity> clear. Vertical <advection> of $q$ is beyond the retained order; the vertical <velocity> has already supplied the stretching and thermodynamic terms.

For a simple balanced weather forecast, the essential cycle is **diagnose, advect, invert**:

* Use observed <pressure> or <geopotential> and <temperature> to construct a balanced initial <quasi-geostrophic streamfunction>, its interior <potential vorticity>, and the upper and lower boundary <buoyancy>.
* Evolve $q$ with $q_t=-\mathbf u_g\cdot\nabla_hq$ for a small time step. For flat rigid boundaries, also evolve their thermal data with $D_gb=0$ there. Appropriate lateral <boundary conditions> and any known large-scale circulation must accompany these fields.
* Recover the new <streamfunction> by <stratified quasi-geostrophic inversion>:
$$
\left[\nabla_h^2+\partial_z\left(\frac{f_0^2}{N^2}\partial_z\right)\right]\psi
=q-f_0-\beta y.
$$
This is an <elliptic boundary-value problem>, not a local algebraic diagnosis. Its solution supplies the next <geostrophic flow> and, through $b=f_0\psi_z$, the next thermal field. Repeat the cycle to move the balanced weather systems forward.

Interior <potential vorticity> alone is insufficient: boundary thermal anomalies can induce flow even with zero interior anomaly, as in the <Eady model>. The method filters fast <inertia-gravity waves> and is useful for large-scale extratropical weather, while tropical dynamics, <convection>, moisture, friction and diabatic heating require additional physics. If vertical motion is wanted, the <quasi-geostrophic omega equation> supplies a further diagnostic calculation.

For the proposed <plane wave>, define the positive squared effective <wavenumber>
$$
\mathcal K^2=k^2+l^2+\frac{f_0^2}{N^2}m^2.
$$
The horizontal <Laplacian> contributes $-(k^2+l^2)\psi'$ and the vertical stretching operator contributes $-(f_0^2/N^2)m^2\psi'$. Substitution in the <linear Rossby-wave equation> therefore gives
$$
[-i(\omega-Uk)](-\mathcal K^2)+i\beta k=0,
\qquad
\boxed{\omega=Uk-\frac{\beta k}{k^2+l^2+(f_0^2/N^2)m^2}.}
$$
The intrinsic <phase velocity> is westward for positive $\beta$. A stationary disturbance with $\omega=0$ and $k\ne0$ must have its westward intrinsic <phase velocity> exactly cancelled by the eastward background current. Solving for the squared vertical <wavenumber> yields
$$
m^2=\frac{N^2}{f_0^2}\left(\frac\beta U-k^2-l^2\right).
$$
For <Earth>, with northward $y$, $\beta=2\Omega\cos\phi/a>0$ in both hemispheres away from the poles. Thus an oscillatory vertical structure requires
$$
\boxed{0<U<\frac\beta{k^2+l^2},\qquad N^2>0,\quad f_0\ne0,\quad k\ne0.}
$$
The background flow must be eastward but not too strong for the chosen horizontal scale. Long horizontal <wavelengths> have the largest allowed speed. An easterly current, $U<0$, gives $m^2<0$ and an <evanescent wave>; so does an eastward current exceeding the upper bound. At equality $m=0$ and the vertical <group velocity> vanishes. A zonally uniform disturbance, $k=0$, is a degenerate stationary pattern with no vertical <Rossby-wave> energy propagation, so it cannot be obtained by cancelling $k$ in the preceding calculation.

A real $m$ alone does not select *upward* propagation. Differentiate the <dispersion relation> at fixed $k,l$ to obtain the vertical <group velocity>:
$$
c_{g,z}=\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial m}
=\frac{2\beta k(f_0^2/N^2)m}
{[k^2+l^2+(f_0^2/N^2)m^2]^2}.
$$
The outgoing <radiation condition> for a surface source requires
$$
\boxed{c_{g,z}>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad km>0\quad(\beta>0).}
$$
For the convention $k>0$, take $m>0$. The stationary <phase> lines then obey $dx/dz=-m/k<0$: they tilt westward with height while <wave energy> travels upward. The real conjugate component $(-k,-l,-m)$ describes the same physical upward-propagating wave.

The figure suggests a winter extratropical path from the lower <terrestrial atmosphere> into the <stratosphere>, where the mean <zonal flow> is eastward. The summer <stratosphere> has westward flow and blocks such stationary <Rossby waves> in this idealization. A zero-wind surface is a stationary-wave <critical layer in a shear flow>; it also bounds the winter westerlies aloft near the upper part of the diagram. For a slowly varying real wind profile, local propagation needs $m^2(z)>0$ along the path, sufficient surface forcing, and no intervening absorbing <critical layer in a shear flow>. A layer where the eastward wind becomes too strong is a turning barrier for the given horizontal <wavenumbers>. The uniform-current result is a local guide, not a complete prediction through a sheared spherical <terrestrial atmosphere>.

For scale, at $60^\circ$ latitude $\beta\simeq1.14\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$. A planetary <zonal wavenumber> $s$ has local $k=s/(a\cos\phi)$. Taking $l=0$ gives the most permissive limit
$$
U_{\max}=\frac{\beta a^2\cos^2\phi}{s^2}
\simeq\frac{116}{s^2}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.
$$
The resulting limits are approximately $116$, $29$ and $13\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ for $s=1,2,3$. Therefore the long planetary waves are favored; the strong core of the winter vortex can exclude shorter ones even though it is westerly. Nonzero meridional <wavenumber> lowers these limits further. A compressible <log-pressure coordinate> model would also contain a density-scale-height correction; it is absent from the equation supplied here and should not be silently inserted.

Interchanging the Northern and Southern Hemispheres changes the sign of $f_0$, but only $f_0^2$ enters the <dispersion relation> and vertical <group velocity>. With the same eastward $x$ and northward $y$, $\beta$ stays positive. Thus the **propagation criterion is the same in both hemispheres**: it selects the winter westerlies, irrespective of which hemisphere is winter. Actual amplitudes can differ because mountains, land-sea contrasts, forcing spectra and mean winds differ between hemispheres; those source differences do not reverse this propagation criterion.
