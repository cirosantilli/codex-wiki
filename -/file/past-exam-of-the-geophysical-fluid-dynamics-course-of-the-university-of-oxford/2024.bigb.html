= Solution
{parent=a}

Take $x$ eastward, $y$ northward and $z$ upward. In a <rotating reference frame>, the horizontal <Cauchy momentum equation> is
$$
\frac{D\mathbf u_h}{Dt}+f\widehat{\mathbf z}\times\mathbf u_h=-\frac1\rho\nabla_h p+\mathbf F_h,
\qquad f=2\Omega\sin\phi.
$$
Here $\mathbf F_h$ represents other accelerations, such as <friction>, and $f$ is the <Coriolis parameter>. When the <Rossby number> $U/(|f|L)$ and the ratio $1/(|f|\tau)$ are small, and <friction> is weak, both advective and local <acceleration> can be neglected. The <Coriolis force> then balances the <pressure gradient force>: this is <geostrophic balance>, with $fv=p_x/\rho$ and $fu=-p_y/\rho$. The <geostrophic wind> runs along <atmospheric isobars>, with low <pressure> on its left in the northern hemisphere.

A rapid change of <pressure> need not bring the corresponding <geostrophic wind> into existence at once. An initially weak wind therefore leaves an inward <pressure gradient force> around the new low. For a smoothed anomaly of horizontal scale $L=5\times10^5\ \mathrm m$, <geostrophic balance> would require
$$
U_g\sim\frac{|p\prime|}{\rho |f|L}
\simeq33\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}
\left(\frac{1.2\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}}{\rho}\right)
\left(\frac{10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}}{|f|}\right).
$$
For the illustrative midlatitude value $|f|=10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, the implied <Rossby number> is already about $0.67$, so curved-flow <acceleration> matters. With a small tropical value $|f|=10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, the inferred speed is $333\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and the <Rossby number> about $67$: the assumed <geostrophic balance> is untenable. <Geostrophic adjustment> redistributes the anomaly and emits <internal gravity waves>, leaving a weaker balanced component.

For a target speed $U_*=10\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ on the same scale, the required scale estimate is
$$
\boxed{|p\prime_{\rm adj}|\sim\rho |f|LU_*
=6\ \mathrm{hPa}\left(\frac{\rho}{1.2\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}}\right)
\left(\frac{|f|}{10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}}\right).}
$$
Thus $6\ \mathrm{hPa}$, or $0.30$ of the initial magnitude, is the midlatitude estimate; at the illustrative tropical $|f|=10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ it is $0.6\ \mathrm{hPa}$, or $0.030$ of the initial magnitude. **The paper gives no latitude, so there is no unique numerical reduction.** At the equator itself one must use equatorial dynamics rather than divide by $f=0$. Even the tropical $10\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ estimate has <Rossby number> $2$, so it describes the scale permitted by the stated balance, rather than a controlled tropical <geostrophic balance>.

There are two qualifications to “unstable”. A literal top-hat <pressure> anomaly has a discontinuous edge, where no finite classical <geostrophic wind> can balance its distributional <pressure gradient>. The $500\ \mathrm{km}$ estimate assumes a smoothed edge; a thinner edge requires stronger winds. More generally a large low is not, by its amplitude alone, proved to have an exponentially growing instability. For a concrete counterexample to the claim that such an amplitude cannot be stationary, set $p\prime(s)=-P\exp(-s^2/a^2)$, with cylindrical radius $s$, constant $\rho$ and $f>0$. The cyclonic <gradient wind>
$$
v_\theta(s)=\frac{-fs+\sqrt{f^2s^2+4s p_s/\rho}}2
$$
is real and satisfies $v_\theta^2/s+fv_\theta=p_s/\rho$ for every $P>0$, with a regular limit at the centre. This is a stationary <axisymmetric flow>; no claim about its perturbation stability is needed. Here the physically justified conclusion is that a rapidly imposed anomaly without its balancing wind is **unbalanced**.

Small tropical <Coriolis parameter> allows only weak horizontal <pressure gradients> to coexist with ordinary large-scale winds. <Hydrostatic balance> connects the vertical variation of these <pressure gradients> to horizontal <temperature gradients>; equivalently the approximate <thermal wind> relation gives $f u_z\simeq-(g/T)T_y$. For comparable vertical wind shear, small $|f|$ means smaller $|T_y|$. <Internal gravity waves> can spread heating-induced <buoyancy> anomalies efficiently before rotation arrests the adjustment. This motivates the <weak temperature gradient approximation> for the tropical free troposphere; it does not require the land surface or atmospheric boundary layer to have uniform <temperature>.

= Solution

Use a <plane wave> with phase $kx+mz-\omega t$, and take $N>0$, $k,m\ne0$. The positive-<frequency> branch of the given hydrostatic <dispersion relation> is $\omega=N|k|/|m|$. The <wavevector> is $\mathbf K=(k,m)$. Differentiating with respect to its components gives the <group velocity>, which carries the <wave packet> and its <energy>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf c_g=\nabla_{\mathbf K}\omega
=\left(\frac\omega k,-\frac\omega m\right).}
$$
The normal <phase velocity>, which follows a crest in its normal direction, is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf c_p=\frac\omega{k^2+m^2}(k,m).}
$$
Consequently,
$$
\mathbf K\cdot\mathbf c_g=0,\qquad
\mathbf c_p\cdot\mathbf c_g=0,\qquad
\frac{c_{g,z}}{c_{g,x}}=-\frac{k}{m}.
$$
Thus the <group velocity> lies along the constant-phase lines $z=-(k/m)x+\mathrm{constant}$, perpendicular to the <phase velocity>. For $k,m>0$, crests move upward and rightward, whereas <energy> moves downward and rightward. Choosing $m<0$ gives upward and rightward <energy> propagation and downward and rightward normal phase propagation. Conjugate or oppositely directed components supply the other outgoing directions from a localized source.

\Image[2024-internal-wave-propagation.png]
{title=Internal-wave crests, normal phase propagation, and perpendicular energy propagation for positive horizontal and vertical wavenumbers}

The speeds are
$$
\boxed{|\mathbf c_g|=\frac N{|m|}\sqrt{1+\frac{k^2}{m^2}},\qquad
|\mathbf c_p|=\frac{N|k|}{|m|\sqrt{k^2+m^2}}.}
$$
The hydrostatic limit is $|k/m|\ll1$. In that limit <energy> propagates almost horizontally, with $|c_{g,x}|=N/|m|$ and $|c_{g,z}|=N|k|/m^2$, while normal phase propagation is almost vertical and has speed approximately $N|k|/m^2$. The horizontal speed at which a crest intersects a fixed height is $\omega/k=c_{g,x}$; this is not the horizontal component of the normal <phase velocity> vector. For example, $N=10^{-2}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, vertical <wavelength> $2\pi/|m|=10\ \mathrm{km}$ and horizontal <wavelength> $2\pi/|k|=1000\ \mathrm{km}$ give $|c_{g,x}|\simeq15.9\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and $|c_{g,z}|\simeq0.159\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

These are local speeds in a uniform medium, without a background current. The non-rotating approximation requires $|f|\ll\omega$, while the <hydrostatic approximation> requires $\omega\ll N$. At lower <frequencies>, rotation changes the <dispersion relation> to that of <inertia-gravity waves>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The equations describe the linear <reduced gravity> <shallow water equations> for a uniform upper layer over a deep, resting lower layer, or equivalently one separated internal vertical mode. The layer is shallow compared with its horizontal scales, permitting <hydrostatic balance>, and its horizontal <velocity> is independent of depth. The <Boussinesq approximation> retains the small <density> contrast through $g\prime=g(\rho_2-\rho_1)/\rho_0>0$. We neglect <friction>, external forcing, background currents, and quadratic perturbation terms. On an equatorial <beta plane>, $f=\beta y$ with $\beta>0$, and the <equatorial Kelvin wave> branch imposes $v=0$. The meridional <Cauchy momentum equation> then reduces exactly within this linear model to a balance between the <Coriolis force> and <pressure gradient force>.

Strictly, the printed $h$ must denote the **thickness perturbation** $\eta$, with total thickness $h_0+\eta$ and $|\eta|\ll h_0$. A signed oscillation around zero cannot represent the total positive layer thickness. Define $c=\sqrt{g\prime h_0}$. For a nonzero zonal <wavenumber> $k$, the zonal <Cauchy momentum equation> and <conservation of mass> give
$$
\omega\widehat u=kg\prime\widehat\eta,\qquad
\omega\widehat\eta=kh_0\widehat u,
\qquad \omega^2=c^2k^2.
$$
On the eastward branch $\omega=ck$, the <wave polarization> is $u=(c/h_0)\eta$. Meridional <geostrophic balance> gives
$$
g\prime\eta_y=-\beta y\frac c{h_0}\eta,
\qquad \frac{\eta_y}{\eta}=-\frac{\beta y}{c},
\qquad \eta\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\beta y^2}{2c}\right).
$$
The branch $\omega=-ck$ instead grows as $\exp(+\beta y^2/(2c))$ and is excluded by decay away from the equator. Matching the paper's <Gaussian function> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{L_{\rm eq}^2=\frac{2c}{\beta},\qquad
\eta=A_0e^{-y^2/L_{\rm eq}^2}\cos(kx-ckt),\qquad
u=\frac c{h_0}\eta,\quad v=0.}
$$
The usual <equatorial deformation radius> is $R_{\rm eq}=\sqrt{c/\beta}$, with the envelope written $e^{-y^2/(2R_{\rm eq}^2)}$. Thus the printed exponent uses $L_{\rm eq}=\sqrt2R_{\rm eq}$. Calling that width a <deformation radius> is a convention; using the standard radius without the factor $1/2$ would fail the printed meridional equation.

\Image[2024-equatorial-kelvin-wave.png]
{title=Equatorial Kelvin-wave thickness anomaly and zonal velocity at one instant, using the paper's Gaussian-width convention}

At an instant, alternating positive and negative thickness anomalies have their largest magnitudes on the equator and decay symmetrically away from it. Eastward arrows occupy the positive anomalies, westward arrows the negative anomalies; the <velocity> vanishes at the nodes and weakens away from the equator. The whole pattern travels eastward at speed $c$. **Meridionally**, $fu=-g\prime\eta_y$ is <geostrophic balance>. For a positive anomaly north of the equator, the northward <pressure gradient force> balances the southward <Coriolis force>; both directions reverse south of the equator. **Zonally**, $fv=0$ and $u_t=-g\prime\eta_x$: the zonal <pressure gradient force> accelerates the flow, so it is not in zonal <geostrophic balance>. On the equator the meridional terms both vanish smoothly.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Hold $l,m,f_0,N$ fixed when differentiating the <dispersion relation> of <Rossby waves>, so that $\alpha^2\geq0$ is independent of $k$. For $k\ne0$, stationary zonal phase requires
$$
0=\frac\omega k=U-\frac\beta{k^2+\alpha^2},
\qquad U=\frac\beta{k^2+\alpha^2}.
$$
The zonal <group velocity> measured relative to the ground is
$$
c_{g,x}=\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial k}
=U+\frac{\beta(k^2-\alpha^2)}{(k^2+\alpha^2)^2}.
$$
Using the stationarity condition,
$$
\boxed{c_{g,x}=\frac{2\beta k^2}{(k^2+\alpha^2)^2}
=\frac{2Uk^2}{k^2+\alpha^2}>0.}
$$
Here $\beta=2\Omega\cos\phi/a>0$ on the ordinary midlatitude <beta plane>, so the stationary <Rossby wave> requires an eastward background current and carries its <wave packet> eastward. If $\alpha>0$, a real nonzero stationary $k$ exists only for $0<U<\beta/\alpha^2$. The restriction $k\ne0$ matters: a zonally uniform disturbance with $k=0$ has $\omega=0$ identically, but its zonal <phase velocity> is undefined, so it is not a counterexample within the stated meaning of stationary waves.

= Solution
{parent=e}

**Kelvin waves:** weakened <trade winds>, or a westerly wind burst, excite a downwelling <equatorial Kelvin wave>. Its positive upper-layer thickness anomaly travels eastward and deepens the eastern Pacific <thermocline>. <Upwelling> then entrains warmer water, favouring eastern Pacific surface warming and the development of <El Niño>. This is a propagating adjustment of the <thermocline>, rather than transport of an unchanged parcel of warm water across the entire basin.<El Niño and La Niña: Causes and Global Consequences by Michael J. McPhaden>[Box 2, “Equatorial Waves”, and the adjoining discussion of eastern thermocline depression, printed page 358]{ref}

**Gravity waves:** changed tropical <convection> and its <latent heat> release force atmospheric <internal gravity waves>. Their rapid propagation spreads the associated <pressure> and <buoyancy> disturbances, allowing the tropical <terrestrial atmosphere> to adjust to the shifted heating and contributing to the divergent circulation and surface wind response coupled to the ocean. This is one atmospheric adjustment mechanism; it does not assert that all wind variability during a particular <El Niño> is caused by this wave family.

**Rossby waves:** wind forcing during <El Niño> also launches westward <equatorial Rossby waves> with a shoaling <thermocline> signal. Reflection at the western Pacific boundary can produce an eastward upwelling <equatorial Kelvin wave>, which shoals the eastern <thermocline> and promotes cooling. This supplies a delayed negative feedback that can help terminate the event.<El Niño and La Niña: Causes and Global Consequences by Michael J. McPhaden>[Printed pages 358–359, reflection of the western thermocline-elevation signal and the return of surface cooling]{ref} These examples describe mechanisms relevant to events such as 2023/24, rather than claiming an event-specific attribution from the exam data.

= Solution

Use eastward $x$, northward $y$, constant layer depth $H$, constant reference <density> $\rho_0$, and a depth-independent horizontal <velocity field> $(u,v)$. Assume a flat bottom and a rigid upper boundary, giving the two-dimensional <incompressibility condition> $u_x+v_y=0$. The <traditional approximation> retains only the vertical component of planetary rotation in the horizontal equations. Take the <Coriolis parameter> $f=f(y)$, a constant <linear drag> rate $r$, and no other torques. The component <Cauchy momentum equations> are
$$
Du-fv=-\frac{p_x}{\rho_0}+\frac{\tau_s}{\rho_0H}-ru,
\qquad Dv+fu=-\frac{p_y}{\rho_0}-rv,
\qquad D=\partial_t+u\partial_x+v\partial_y.
$$
Define <relative vorticity> $\xi=v_x-u_y$. Apply $\partial_x$ to the second equation and subtract $\partial_y$ of the first. The <pressure gradient force> has zero <curl> because $\rho_0$ is constant and mixed <partial derivatives> commute. Direct expansion of the material-acceleration terms gives
$$
\partial_x(Dv)-\partial_y(Du)
=D\xi+(u_x+v_y)\xi=D\xi.
$$
The <curl> of the rotation terms is
$$
\partial_x(fu)+\partial_y(fv)
=f(u_x+v_y)+vf_y=vf_y.
$$
The imposed zonal <wind stress> contributes $-\tau_{s,y}/(\rho_0H)$, and constant <linear drag> contributes $-r\xi$. Hence
$$
D\xi+vf_y=-\frac{\tau_{s,y}}{\rho_0H}-r\xi.
$$
Since $Df=vf_y$, defining <absolute vorticity> $\zeta=\xi+f$ gives
$$
\boxed{\left(\partial_t+u\partial_x+v\partial_y\right)\zeta
=-\frac1{\rho_0H}\frac{\partial\tau_s}{\partial y}-r\xi.}
$$
Without the <incompressibility condition>, the left-hand side would contain the stretching term $\zeta\nabla_h\cdot\mathbf u$. Variable layer depth, variable <density> or spatially varying <linear drag> would introduce further terms. No small-<Rossby number> assumption was needed to obtain this constant-depth <vorticity equation>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

On a <beta plane>, $f=f_0+\beta y$, the preceding <vorticity equation> is
$$
\xi_t+u\xi_x+v\xi_y+\beta v
=-\frac{\tau_{s,y}}{\rho_0H}-r\xi.
$$
In the broad ocean interior, assume a statistically steady flow, weak <advection> of <relative vorticity>, and negligible <friction> compared with planetary-<vorticity> advection. The constant-depth, constant-<density> assumptions of 2(a) exclude topographic and <baroclinic vorticity generation> contributions. More precisely, require $|\xi_t|,|\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\xi|,|r\xi|\ll|\beta v|$. Then <Sverdrup balance> is
$$
\boxed{\beta v=\frac{(\nabla_h\times\boldsymbol\tau)_z}{\rho_0H}
=-\frac1{\rho_0H}\frac{d\tau_s}{dy}.}
$$
Small <Rossby number> and slow forcing help justify these conditions in the interior; they do not justify them inside an intense <western boundary current>. For fluctuations, the mean <eddy> contribution to relative-<vorticity> advection must also be negligible.

The depth-integrated northward <volume flux> per unit zonal distance is $V=Hv$. For a basin $0<x<L$ and <wind stress> independent of $x$, the northward **interior** <volume flux> is
$$
\boxed{T(y)=\int_0^L Hv\,dx=-\frac{L}{\beta\rho_0}\frac{d\tau_s}{dy}.}
$$
A negative value denotes southward transport. The cancellation of $H$ is why this interior <volume flux> is insensitive to the chosen constant depth. In a closed, steady basin the net <volume flux> across a full latitude section is zero: a boundary return must compensate $T$. Thus the displayed formula cannot represent the total closed-basin transport including that return.

For a concrete circulation sketch, use a northern-hemisphere subtropical basin $0<y<B$ and
$$
\tau_s(y)=-\tau_0\cos(\pi y/B),\qquad
V(y)=-\frac{\pi\tau_0}{\beta\rho_0B}\sin(\pi y/B).
$$
This represents westward <trade winds> at the southern edge and eastward westerlies at the northern edge. The negative <wind stress curl> drives southward interior flow. Define an <ocean transport streamfunction> by $Hu=-\Psi_y$, $Hv=\Psi_x$ and impose no normal flow at the eastern wall through $\Psi(L,y)=0$. Integrating <Sverdrup balance> westward gives
$$
\Psi_I=(x-L)V(y),\qquad
Hu_I=(L-x)V_y(y),\qquad Hv_I=V(y).
$$
Since $V=0$ at $y=0,B$, this also gives no normal flow at the north and south walls. Interior water moves westward in the southern part, southward across the basin, and eastward in the northern part. A northward <western boundary current> completes the clockwise <ocean gyre>.

\Image[2024-sverdrup-gyre.png]
{title=Northern-hemisphere subtropical Sverdrup interior and a schematic western return, with arrows indicating volume transport}

Pure <Sverdrup balance> cannot satisfy the western impermeability condition as well: $\Psi_I(0,y)=-LV(y)$ varies along that wall. Indeed imposing no normal flow on both east and west walls would require their constant <streamfunction> values to agree in this simply connected closed basin, whereas their difference here is the nonzero $T(y)$. The shaded return in the sketch represents a boundary region where the interior approximation breaks down. It is supplied by <friction> in 2(c), rather than inferred from <Sverdrup balance> alone. A southern-hemisphere subtropical <ocean gyre> has the reflected, anticlockwise circulation.

For an order-of-magnitude estimate, use $\tau_0=0.1\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}$, $\rho_0=10^3\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, $\beta=2\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$, $L=5\times10^6\ \mathrm m$ and a <wind stress> variation scale $\ell_\tau=10^6\ \mathrm m$. Then
$$
\boxed{|T|\sim\frac{L\tau_0}{\beta\rho_0\ell_\tau}
=2.5\times10^7\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}}=25\ \mathrm{Sv}.}
$$
Here $1\ \mathrm{Sv}=10^6\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}}$ is a <Sverdrup>. The sinusoidal example has maximum $|\tau_{s,y}|=\pi\tau_0/B$; taking $B=\pi\times10^6\ \mathrm m$ makes it agree with the chosen variation scale. Other reasonable basin widths and wind profiles change the coefficient, but retain a transport of order tens of <Sverdrups>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Let $V_b$ be the northward speed in the <western boundary current>, whose zonal width $\delta$ is much less than its meridional scale. Its dominant <relative vorticity> is $\xi\simeq v_x\sim V_b/\delta$. Neglecting local <wind stress curl> and relative-<vorticity> advection in the narrow return region, the linear stationary <vorticity equation> becomes
$$
\beta v\simeq-r\xi\simeq-rv_x.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\delta\sim\frac r\beta,\qquad v(x,y)\propto e^{-x/\delta}.}
$$
This decay into the interior from the western wall explains why the <Stommel boundary layer> lies on the west. The sign also matters: a northward return has $v_x<0$, so its negative <relative vorticity> supplies a positive drag contribution $-r\xi$ to balance $\beta v$. Here $r$ has units $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$; no extra factor of $f_0$ belongs in $r/\beta$ with the paper's definition of <linear drag>.

For example, at fixed $y$ the leading correction to the interior <ocean transport streamfunction> is
$$
\Psi_B=LV(y)e^{-x/\delta},\qquad
Hv_B=\partial_x\Psi_B=-\frac{LV(y)}\delta e^{-x/\delta}.
$$
It cancels the western value of $\Psi_I$ and carries $\int_0^\infty Hv_B\,dx=-LV=-T$. Exponentially small eastern-wall corrections and the turning regions near the northern and southern walls do not affect the leading width estimate.

Using one year $=3.156\times10^7\ \mathrm s$, the representative $\beta$ of 2(b), and $H=10^3\ \mathrm m$ gives
$$
r=3.17\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},\qquad
\boxed{\delta\simeq1.58\times10^3\ \mathrm m=1.6\ \mathrm{km}.}
$$
To return the $25\ \mathrm{Sv}$ interior <volume flux>, <conservation of mass> requires
$$
\boxed{V_b\sim\frac{|T|}{H\delta}
\simeq15.8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
For the exponential profile this is the boundary speed associated with its effective transport width $\delta$. It is much narrower and faster than the familiar large-scale <western boundary currents>, whose energetic cores span tens of kilometres and have speeds of order $1\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The simplified constant-depth <linear drag> model with a one-year decay time therefore does not give a realistic quantitative boundary structure. <Lateral viscosity>, <eddies>, <stratification> and inertia can all affect a real current.

The corresponding <relative vorticity> scale is
$$
\boxed{|\xi|\sim\frac{V_b}\delta\simeq9.96\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},\qquad
\frac{|\xi|}{|f_0|}\sim100\quad\text{for }|f_0|\sim10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}.}
$$
Thus <relative vorticity> cannot be discarded relative to <planetary vorticity> in this predicted return. Even a more realistic $1\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ across $50\ \mathrm{km}$ gives $|\xi|\sim2\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, an appreciable fraction of $|f_0|$.

Magnitude alone does not prove that its material <advection> is large: a perfectly parallel current $v=v(x)$, $u=0$ has $\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\xi=0$ exactly. For a gyre return varying over meridional scale $B$, however, <incompressibility> gives $u\sim\delta V_b/B$, and both generic contributions to $\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\xi$ scale as $V_b^2/(B\delta)$. Relative to $\beta V_b$, this is
$$
\frac{|\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\xi|}{\beta V_b}
\sim\frac{V_b}{\beta B\delta}
\simeq1.6\times10^2\qquad(B=\pi\times10^6\ \mathrm m).
$$
Absent special cancellations, the assumed neglect of relative-<vorticity> advection is therefore not self-consistent for these parameters. Its natural inertial width is instead $\delta_I\sim\sqrt{V_I/\beta}$, where $V_I=|T|/(HL)\simeq5\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$; this gives about $16\ \mathrm{km}$. That comparison diagnoses the missing inertia rather than supplying a complete nonlinear boundary-current solution.
