= Solution
{parent=a}

The annual, zonal-mean absorbed sunlight falls from the tropics towards the poles much more strongly than outgoing <thermal radiation>. Thus <Earth>'s top-of-atmosphere <radiation budget> has a tropical surplus and a polar deficit, with changes of sign around $35^\circ$ latitude in a basic sketch. The curves below are illustrative, rather than measured data; their area-weighted global means agree.

\Image[2025-radiation-balance.png]
{title=An illustrative latitude-dependent radiation budget and the required poleward energy transport}

In a statistically stationary <climate>, local <conservation of energy> requires the surplus to be exported. If $R(\phi)$ is the net downward radiative <heat flux> and $\mathcal H(\phi)$ the northward total <power> carried across the latitude circle, then
$$
\frac{d\mathcal H}{d\phi}=2\pi a^2R(\phi)\cos\phi,\qquad
\mathcal H(\phi)=2\pi a^2\int_{-\pi/2}^{\phi}R(\theta)\cos\theta\,d\theta.
$$
Here $a$ is <Earth>'s radius. The <terrestrial atmosphere> and <ocean circulation> carry this <energy> poleward by moving warm and cold fluid, including atmospheric transport of <latent heat>. Without this transport the assumed steady <radiation budget> could not persist: the tropics would warm and polar regions cool until the budget changed. The tropical atmospheric contribution includes the <Hadley cell>: rising warm air near the equator, poleward flow aloft, subtropical descent, and an equatorward lower return.

For a parcel moving without an axial <torque>, <conservation of angular momentum> applies in an inertial frame. The specific axial <angular momentum> at latitude $\phi$ is
$$
M=(\Omega a\cos\phi+u)a\cos\phi.
$$
“Initially at rest” means $u=0$ relative to the rotating surface at the equator, so $M=\Omega a^2$. Neglecting atmospheric height compared with $a$ gives
$$
\boxed{u(\phi)=\Omega a\left(\frac1{\cos\phi}-\cos\phi\right)
=\frac{\Omega a\sin^2\phi}{\cos\phi}.}
$$
With $\Omega=7.292\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $a=6.371\times10^6\ \mathrm m$, the eastward <velocity> at $30^\circ\mathrm N$ is
$$
\boxed{u(30^\circ)=134\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
This is the ideal angular-momentum-conserving upper branch of the <Hadley cell>. Real <jet streams> exchange <angular momentum> through <eddies> and <friction>, so this estimate is not an observational claim about the actual wind speed.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Take $x$ eastward, $y$ northward and $z$ upward, and let $u$ be the eastward <geostrophic wind>. With <Coriolis parameter> $f$ independent of $z$, <geostrophic balance>, <hydrostatic balance>, and the <ideal gas law> give
$$
fu=-\frac{p_y}{\rho},\qquad p_z=-\rho g,\qquad p=\rho R_dT.
$$
Here $R_d$ is the specific gas constant for dry air. Taking a vertical <derivative> of the first equation and interchanging mixed <partial derivatives> gives
$$
f u_z=g\frac{\rho_y}{\rho}-fu\frac{\rho_z}{\rho}.
$$
It matters that $\rho$ is not constant in the <pressure gradient force>. Substituting
$$
\frac{\rho_y}{\rho}=-\frac{fu}{R_dT}-\frac{T_y}{T},\qquad
\frac{\rho_z}{\rho}=-\frac g{R_dT}-\frac{T_z}{T}
$$
cancels the two terms proportional to $gfu/(R_dT)$ and yields the height-coordinate <thermal wind> relation
$$
f u_z=-\frac gT T_y+\frac{fu}{T}T_z.
$$
If $|uT_z|\ll|Tu_z|$, this reduces to
$$
\boxed{f\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}\simeq-\frac gT\frac{\partial T}{\partial y}.}
$$
The discarded term has relative scale $H|T_z|/T$ when $u_z\sim u/H$. Taking $H=10\ \mathrm{km}$, $T=250\ \mathrm K$ and a <atmospheric lapse rate> of $6.5\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$ gives about $0.26$. Thus this height-coordinate formula is a useful estimate, with potentially tens-of-percent compressibility-related correction through the vertical <temperature gradient>; it is not an exact consequence of the two balances. If the <temperature gradient> vanishes vertically, the displayed approximation is exact within those balances.

The <hydrostatic approximation> is very good on the broad, shallow atmospheric scales considered. The <geostrophic balance> is normally justified by a small <Rossby number> and fails as a general approximation near the equator. At $30^\circ\mathrm N$, $f=7.292\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. With the ideal $134\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ wind and a $1000$--$2000\ \mathrm{km}$ horizontal scale, $\mathrm{Ro}=U/(fL)\simeq0.9$--$1.8$: quantitative accuracy is limited for this especially strong ideal jet. Small vertical aspect ratio alone does not ensure <geostrophic balance>, although a straight, steady zonal flow can satisfy it even when this scale estimate of the <Rossby number> is not small.

For the requested estimate, suppose the upper <jet stream> is $H=10\ \mathrm{km}$ above a weak surface wind, has <temperature> $T\simeq250\ \mathrm K$, and spans a meridional width $L$. Integrating the approximate <thermal wind> relation in scale form gives
$$
\boxed{T_{\rm south}-T_{\rm north}\simeq\frac{fTUL}{gH}
\simeq25\ \mathrm K\left(\frac{L}{1000\ \mathrm{km}}\right)
\left(\frac{10\ \mathrm{km}}H\right)
\left(\frac{T}{250\ \mathrm K}\right).}
$$
The northward <temperature gradient> is negative because the eastward wind strengthens upward. A $1000$--$2000\ \mathrm{km}$ jet width gives roughly $25$--$50\ \mathrm K$. No unique numerical difference follows without specifying the jet width, height and representative <temperature>; these are explicitly estimated quantities.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Write the <velocity field> as $u=U+u\prime$, $v=v\prime$. The uniform background has zero <relative vorticity>, and the perturbation has $\xi\prime=v\prime_x-u\prime_y$. Keeping only terms linear in the perturbations gives
$$
\boxed{(\partial_t+U\partial_x)\xi\prime+\beta v\prime=0.}
$$
The <incompressibility condition> permits a <streamfunction> $\psi$ with $u\prime=-\psi_y$, $v\prime=\psi_x$, hence $\xi\prime=\nabla_h^2\psi$. Thus the <linear Rossby-wave equation> here is
$$
(\partial_t+U\partial_x)\nabla_h^2\psi+\beta\psi_x=0.
$$
Choose a <plane wave> $\psi=\operatorname{Re}\{\widehat\psi e^{i(kx+ly-\omega t)}\}$, with $K^2=k^2+l^2>0$. Substitution gives
$$
i(\omega-Uk)K^2\widehat\psi+i\beta k\widehat\psi=0,
$$
and therefore the <local Rossby-wave dispersion relation in a zonal jet>
$$
\boxed{\omega=Uk-\frac{\beta k}{k^2+l^2}.}
$$
The $Uk$ term is the background <Doppler shift>; the intrinsic <phase velocity> is westward. A real physical <wave> can always be represented using the conjugate <wavevector> if necessary to choose $\omega\geq0$.

The relevant ground-frame zonal <phase velocity> and <group velocity> are
$$
c_{p,x}=\frac\omega k=U-\frac\beta{K^2},\qquad
c_{g,x}=\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial k}
=U+\frac{\beta(k^2-l^2)}{K^4},\qquad
c_{g,y}=\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial l}=\frac{2\beta kl}{K^4}.
$$
**Time increases downward in the source diagram.** The individual phase contours lean towards increasing longitude as time increases, so their <phase velocity> is eastward. The envelope moves eastward faster: successive crests grow at the downstream edge and decay upstream. This illustrates <Rossby-wave downstream development>.

For the intended purely zonal <wave packet>, $l=0$, and
$$
c_p=U-\frac\beta{k^2}>0,\qquad c_g=U+\frac\beta{k^2}>U.
$$
Consequently, with $U,\beta>0$ and the positive-frequency choice $k>0$,
$$
\boxed{k>\sqrt{\beta/U}.}
$$
There is a small interpretation qualification: if “zonally propagating” specified only the plotted direction while allowing an unknown nonzero meridional <wavenumber>, eastward <phase velocity> alone would imply $k^2+l^2>\beta/U$, not the stronger bound on $k$ alone. In this uniform model a strictly zonal <group velocity> with $\beta k\ne0$ does imply $l=0$, which supplies the intended interpretation.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For a zonal background $U(y)$, its <relative vorticity> is $\overline\xi=-U_y$. The meridional <absolute vorticity> gradient is therefore
$$
Q_y=\beta-U_{yy},\qquad
\beta=\frac{2\Omega\cos\phi}{a}.
$$
At $30^\circ\mathrm N$,
$$
\boxed{\beta\simeq1.98\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
A localized <jet stream> of <velocity> scale $U=134\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and width $L$ has
$$
\boxed{|\overline\xi_y|=|U_{yy}|\sim\frac U{L^2}
=1.34\times10^{-10}\left(\frac{1000\ \mathrm{km}}L\right)^2
\mathrm{m^{-1}\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
For $L=1000$--$2000\ \mathrm{km}$ this is about $1.7$--$6.8$ times the planetary contribution, so wind curvature cannot generally be neglected. The coefficient in this scaling depends on the chosen jet profile and width convention.

Linearizing the <vorticity equation> now gives the <Rossby-wave equation for a sheared zonal current>
$$
(\partial_t+U(y)\partial_x)\nabla_h^2\psi+(\beta-U_{yy})\psi_x=0.
$$
For a qualitative estimate at the jet core, freeze the local coefficients. At a smooth maximum $U_{yy}<0$, so $Q_y>\beta$: the restoring <absolute vorticity> gradient is stronger. The <local Rossby-wave dispersion relation in a zonal jet> gives, for the same local $U$ and zonal <wavenumber> $k$ with $l=0$,
$$
c_p=U-\frac{Q_y}{k^2},\qquad c_g=U+\frac{Q_y}{k^2},
$$
so
$$
\boxed{\Delta c_p=\frac{U_{yy}}{k^2}<0,\qquad
\Delta c_g=-\frac{U_{yy}}{k^2}>0.}
$$
The envelope in the diagram would advance eastward faster, while individual phase contours would advance more slowly eastward, become stationary if $Q_y=Uk^2$, or move westward if $Q_y>Uk^2$. The separation between envelope and phase speeds increases. On a plot with time downward, compare horizontal displacement at fixed time: the envelope moves farther right, while a given phase moves less far right, or left after reversal. If the <frequency> changes sign for a fixed signed $k$, conjugate the mode to restore the convention $\omega\geq0$; the physical propagation is unchanged.

This conclusion concerns the concave-down jet core. On a flank with $U_{yy}>0$ the correction has the opposite sign; the value at the edge of the <Hadley cell> alone does not specify the curvature everywhere. Indeed the angular-momentum-conserving profile in 1(a) is a rising inner branch, not a complete smooth jet maximum. A global sheared-current <normal mode> requires its meridional structure and boundary conditions; the local calculation is the qualitative estimate requested.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Define the <salinity> contrast $\Delta S=S_1-S_2$, positive when the warm box is saltier. The linear <equation of state> gives the cold-minus-warm <density> contrast and signed <volume flux>
$$
\frac{\rho_2-\rho_1}{\rho_0}=\alpha\Delta T-\beta\Delta S,\qquad
q=k(\alpha\Delta T-\beta\Delta S).
$$
Here $q>0$ represents warm upper water travelling poleward and cold deep water returning equatorward. The $\beta$ in this question is the haline <density> coefficient, unrelated to the planetary <vorticity> gradient in Question 1. The imposed heating and cooling are taken to maintain $\Delta T$.

For the positive circulation shown in the source diagram, the exact stationary <salt budget> of the cold box is
$$
qS_1-(q+E)S_2=0,
\qquad q\Delta S=ES_2.
$$
The precipitation adds no salt. Replacing the salinity multiplying the small compensating <volume flux> by a reference $S_0$ gives $q\Delta S\simeq ES_0$. When circulation reverses, the upstream box for the exchange reverses too: the homogenizing exchange is $|q|$, not $q$. In the same reference-salinity approximation the two stationary <salt budgets> reduce to
$$
\boxed{|q|\Delta S=ES_0.}
$$
For example, when $q=-r<0$ and $r>E$, the exact cold-box balance is $(r-E)S_1-rS_2=0$, giving $r\Delta S=ES_1$ and the same leading approximation.

Substitute $\Delta S=ES_0/|q|$ into the <density>-driven <volume flux> and multiply by $|q|$:
$$
\boxed{q|q|-A|q|+BE=0,\qquad
A=k\alpha\Delta T,\qquad B=k\beta S_0.}
$$
Both $A$ and $B$ have the units of <volume flux>, so every term has units of squared <volume flux>. The absolute value expresses the fact that transport in either direction reduces the <salinity> contrast, while the <density> contrast sets its direction.

The requested equation is thus the reference-salinity, or <virtual salt flux>, version of the <two-box overturning model>. If the finite compensating flow is retained exactly with unequal $S_1,S_2$, one has the appropriate upstream salinity on the right-hand side instead of exactly $S_0$. The approximation assumes $|\Delta S|/S_0\ll1$ and $E/|q|\ll1$. Also, for $E>0$, $q=0$ cannot be a steady solution: the imposed freshwater transport would build an uncompensated <salinity> contrast.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Using the constants **as printed**, with <volume flux> measured in <Sverdrups>, gives
$$
\boxed{A=(7\times10^6)(2\times10^{-7})(15)=21\ \mathrm{Sv},\qquad
B=(7\times10^6)(8\times10^{-7})(35)=196\ \mathrm{Sv}.}
$$
In particular the two small expansion coefficients should not be silently replaced by different oceanographic values.

For a positive steady <volume flux>, the equation in 2(a) is $q^2-Aq+BE=0$. For a negative steady <volume flux>, it is $q^2-Aq-BE=0$, with only its negative root admissible. Therefore for $E>0$ the branches are
$$
\boxed{q_\pm(E)=\frac{A\pm\sqrt{A^2-4BE}}2
\quad\left(0<E\leq\frac{A^2}{4B}\right),\qquad
q_{\rm rev}(E)=\frac{A-\sqrt{A^2+4BE}}2<0.}
$$
The upper positive branch $q_+$ is stable, the lower positive branch $q_-$ is unstable, and the reversed branch $q_{\rm rev}$ is stable under the usual <salt budget> dynamics with fixed $\Delta T$. Thus there is <bistability> below the positive-branch fold. At the fold the two positive <equilibria> merge with a zero restoring rate; the point is not an ordinary asymptotically stable interior point of either branch. The negative algebraic root of the positive-$q$ quadratic, or positive root of the negative-$q$ quadratic, must never be added as extra solutions.

\Image[2025-overturning-bifurcation.png]
{title=Steady overturning branches, their stability, and the effect of reducing the density-to-transport coefficient}

At $E=0.3\ \mathrm{Sv}$, the three steady <volume fluxes> are
$$
\boxed{q_+=17.67\ \mathrm{Sv},\qquad q_-=3.327\ \mathrm{Sv},\qquad
q_{\rm rev}=-2.502\ \mathrm{Sv}.}
$$
The present thermally dominated, northward-upper-flow <Atlantic meridional overturning circulation> corresponds to $q_+\simeq17.7\ \mathrm{Sv}$. These algebraic data alone also allow a stable reversed state, so selecting the present branch uses the stated circulation direction. On the large positive branch, the predicted <salinity> contrast is $\Delta S=ES_0/q_+\simeq0.594\ \mathrm{g\,kg^{-1}}$, consistent with the reference-salinity approximation. The weaker branches give larger contrasts, approximately $3.16$ and $4.20\ \mathrm{g\,kg^{-1}}$, so the approximation is less accurate there.

The <saddle-node bifurcation> occurs at
$$
\boxed{E_c=\frac{A^2}{4B}=0.5625\ \mathrm{Sv},\qquad q_c=\frac A2=10.5\ \mathrm{Sv}.}
$$
For $E>E_c$, only the reversed steady <volume flux> remains. At $E=E_c$ there are still two distinct values, the double positive root and the negative root; “single-valued” holds strictly above this threshold.

Slowly increasing $E$ from the present value causes the stable positive <volume flux> to weaken. Its <salinity> contrast then increases because salt exchange is weaker, reducing the cold-box <density> advantage further: this is the <salt-advection feedback>. At the fold the positive steady state disappears, producing an abrupt transition to the reversed branch in the quasistatic limit. At the same $E_c$ that branch has
$$
q_{\rm rev}(E_c)=\frac A2(1-\sqrt2)\simeq-4.35\ \mathrm{Sv}.
$$
For a finite slow ramp there can be a small dynamical delay near the fold. In this model subsequently reducing $E$ while keeping it positive follows the stable reversed branch instead of automatically restoring the positive branch, an example of <hysteresis>. The two small branches approach $q=0$ as $E\to0^+$. At $E=0$ this is an equal-density, no-exchange equilibrium with $\Delta S=\alpha\Delta T/\beta=3.75\ \mathrm{g\,kg^{-1}}$, whereas the positive branch tends to $q=A$ with $\Delta S=0$. One must use the original <density> relation at this endpoint, rather than divide by $|q|=0$.

The reversal is thermally opposed but salinity driven: freshwater makes the cold box sufficiently fresh that the warm, salty box becomes denser. A real weakening would reduce the northward <ocean heat flux> and alter regional <climate> and ocean ventilation. The fold and full reversal here describe the ideal <two-box overturning model>; its numerical threshold is not a forecast for the real ocean, whose other controls appear in 2(c).

= Solution
{parent=i}

Northern high-latitude cooling removes <heat> from surface water and raises its <density>. If the surface water becomes denser than the water below, <convection> mixes it downward and forms dense water that can be exported in the deep southward branch of the <Atlantic meridional overturning circulation>. This <water-mass transformation> helps maintain the contrast represented by $\alpha\Delta T$ in 2(a). Freshening can oppose it by reducing <density> and increasing stable <stratification>. Local <convection> and the basin-integrated sinking <volume flux> are related but are not the same process: export and the return path also depend on the large-scale dynamics.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Dense water formed in marginal seas spills over sills and descends the continental slope as a <marginal-sea overflow>. Strong <shear flow> generates <turbulent mixing> and <entrainment> of surrounding, lighter water. The descending current's <volume flux> grows while its <density> anomaly is diluted, modifying the depth at which it joins the deep circulation and the <temperature> and <salinity> of the exported water. Thus the deep <volume flux> can substantially exceed the source overflow <volume flux>; it cannot be inferred from northern surface cooling alone.https://www2.whoi.edu/staff/lpratt/wp-content/uploads/sites/120/2019/07/PrattSec2.11_11823.pdf[Anatomy of an overflow, section 2.11: observed downstream entrainment and dilution of the Faroe Bank Channel overflow]{ref}

{parent=c}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Southern Ocean westerly <wind stress> drives northward surface <Ekman transport> in the Southern Hemisphere. Its divergence and the circumpolar geometry allow deep water to rise towards the surface along tilted <isopycnals>, providing an important return route for the deep branch of the <Atlantic meridional overturning circulation>. Surface <heat fluxes> and freshwater fluxes then transform the upwelled water before it enters shallower return pathways. The winds supply mechanical <energy>; the overturning cannot be represented entirely by a local northern <density> difference. <Eddy-induced overturning> opposes part of the wind-driven contribution, so the net <water-mass transformation> and transport need not increase in direct proportion to the <wind stress>.https://www.awi.de/en/science/climate-sciences/physical-oceanography/main-research-focus/southern-ocean-overturning-circulation.html[Alfred Wegener Institute, Southern Ocean overturning circulation: wind-driven upwelling and its opposition by mesoscale eddies]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Deep currents and <tides> moving across rough topography, including the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, generate <internal waves>. Their breaking produces <turbulent mixing> across <isopycnals>, called <diapycnal mixing>. This transfers <buoyancy> between deep and overlying waters, transforms dense water into lighter water, and contributes to the upward return needed to close the overturning. The required mechanical <energy> ultimately comes from winds and <tides>; roughness is a mechanism for converting this input into mixing, rather than an independent source of <energy>. Mixing is spatially concentrated, and local vertical motion depends on the divergence of the turbulent <buoyancy flux>, so enhanced mixing does not imply uniform local upwelling everywhere over a ridge.https://www.whoi.edu/cms/files/vmix_38943.pdf[The Impact of Abyssal Mixing Parameterizations in an Ocean General Circulation Model, abstract and introduction: tidal internal-wave generation over rough topography and its role in overturning]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=d}

With the same prescribed $\Delta T$, $S_0$, $\alpha$ and $\beta$, replacing $k$ by $\lambda k$ changes both transport coefficients:
$$
A_\lambda=\lambda A,\qquad B_\lambda=\lambda B.
$$
Hence the positive steady <volume fluxes> become
$$
q_{\pm,\lambda}(E)=\frac{\lambda A\pm\sqrt{\lambda^2A^2-4\lambda BE}}2,
$$
and the negative steady <volume flux> is
$$
q_{{\rm rev},\lambda}(E)=\frac{\lambda A-\sqrt{\lambda^2A^2+4\lambda BE}}2.
$$
The <bifurcation diagram> undergoes a particularly simple rescaling:
$$
\boxed{q_\lambda(E)=\lambda q_1(E/\lambda),\qquad
E_{c,\lambda}=\lambda E_c,\qquad q_{c,\lambda}=\lambda q_c.}
$$
Thus it contracts horizontally and vertically by the same factor. Its branch stability and <saddle-node bifurcation> structure persist, but the range of freshwater forcing that admits the stable positive circulation shrinks. At a fixed subcritical $E$, the upper stable positive branch weakens, the lower unstable positive branch moves upward, and the reversed branch decreases in magnitude. These changes reduce the separation of the positive <equilibria> and the margin against an overturning transition. The right panel of the figure in 2(b) shows this for $\lambda=0.7$ and $0.45$.

For the present $E=0.3\ \mathrm{Sv}$, a positive steady state exists only if
$$
0.3\leq0.5625\lambda,
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
\boxed{\lambda\geq\frac8{15}\simeq0.5333.}
$$
At equality the positive branches merge at $q=\lambda A/2=5.6\ \mathrm{Sv}$; below it only the reversed steady state remains. If a discovery merely revises our estimate of $k$, it revises the inferred branch strengths and threshold, rather than itself causing an instantaneous physical transition. If the real effective $k$ were to decrease slowly through the critical value while $E$ stayed fixed, this model predicts loss of the positive steady circulation and transition to the reversed branch. Uncertainty in $k$ therefore translates directly into uncertainty in the model's freshwater threshold and its inferred proximity to a transition.
