== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

**For an <intelligent agent> that must perceive a changing world and reason about new combinations of objects and goals, a hybrid of learned representations and structured computation is the most defensible choice.** The distinction concerns representation and operations, not whether the implementation is a computer or a biological <neural network>.

In <symbolic computation>, identifiable tokens stand for entities, properties or relations, and explicit procedures manipulate structured expressions. A planner might represent $\mathrm{At}(\text{agent},A)$ and apply an action rule with preconditions and consequences. The same rule can apply to different objects by substituting their tokens. <Subsymbolic computation> instead operates on quantities such as distributed activity <vectors> and learned connection weights; a concept need not correspond to one independently inspectable token. A <neural network> can transform an image into an action tendency through many interacting units without an explicit list of logical propositions.

The <physical symbol system hypothesis> made structured symbolic processing a proposed basis for general intelligence. Its accompanying account of search explains how a system can construct and test candidate solutions. This is a substantive empirical hypothesis about intelligent computation, not a theorem that all intelligent behaviour must have one implementation.<Computer science as empirical inquiry: symbols and search by Allen Newell and others>[Communications of the ACM 19, pp. 113–126, physical symbol systems and search]{ref}

<Symbolic computation> has strong advantages when a task already has a reliable relational description. A route planner can combine a newly learned destination with familiar movement rules, enforce a safety constraint explicitly, and revise a plan after a route is blocked. Its intermediate states can expose why an action is permitted. However, perceptual input does not arrive as a clean set of symbols. Identifying objects, deciding which relations matter and connecting tokens to sensory consequences constitute a <symbol grounding> problem. Large rule sets also face combinatorial search and brittle assumptions about uncertain or changing situations. Explicit representation does not guarantee that the rules are correct or that the search is tractable.

<Subsymbolic computation> is attractive for perception and motor coordination because <statistical learning> can exploit regularities in noisy, high-dimensional observations. Shared features allow a learned representation to generalize across similar inputs, and differentiable models can be optimized jointly with a task objective. Yet good interpolation is not the same as <compositional generalization>. A system can recognize familiar combinations while failing to recombine a familiar operation with a newly learned primitive. A plausible-looking output also need not satisfy a constraint or reflect calibrated uncertainty.

A useful computational test used artificial navigation commands paired with action sequences. Recurrent sequence models generalized well on ordinary splits but much less reliably on splits requiring systematic recombination of primitives and operators. The controlled split is crucial: high accuracy when train and test combinations resemble one another does not test the same claim as transfer to held-out constructions. This result constrains the tested training procedures and <inductive biases>; it does not prove that every <recurrent neural network> is incapable of compositional computation.<Generalization without Systematicity: On the Compositional Skills of Sequence-to-Sequence Recurrent Networks by Brenden M. Lake and others>[Abstract and experiments; PMLR 80, pp. 2873–2882, systematic versus ordinary SCAN <generalization>]{ref}

Evidence also argues against equating useful symbolic structure with a hand-written, deterministic rule book. In one-shot character learning, a probabilistic model represented a character through a generative drawing program and inferred that program from a small amount of evidence. It was tested on recognizing new examples and generating plausible variations, with comparisons to human performance. The study shows how <Bayesian inference>, reusable parts and structured generative programs can work together; it does not establish a universal advantage over every future deep learner or solve general agency.<Human-level concept learning through probabilistic program induction by Brenden M. Lake and others>[Abstract; Science 350, pp. 1332–1338: one-shot handwritten-character learning and generative tests]{ref}

The two approaches can therefore interact. A learned visual representation can propose objects and uncertain relations; a structured <world model> can evaluate actions and enforce constraints; sensory <prediction errors> can revise the representation. Conversely, a <neural network> can implement operations over structured variables, and a symbolic program can manipulate probabilities and call a learned perceptual module. A binary division into “rules” and “learning” misses these possibilities.

For viability, I would compare <intelligent agents> under the same sensory inputs, experience budget and action constraints. Tests should include novel combinations, changing goals, noisy observations, transfer after an intervention, and the computational cost of <planning>. The <intelligent agent> must learn which aspects of the environment can be trusted and detect when its abstractions fail. **Learned distributed computation is particularly useful for acquiring representations; structured, uncertainty-aware computation is particularly useful for flexible recombination and <planning>. Their integration is a stronger proposal for broad agency than an unsupported claim of universal superiority for either alone.**

{scope}

=== Solution

**<Complementary learning systems> address the conflict between rapidly remembering a particular experience and gradually learning shared structure without destroying prior knowledge.** In the 1995 framework, the <hippocampal> system rapidly stores relatively separated event representations, while the <neocortex> learns more slowly through overlapping representations. Separation reduces interference between episodes; overlap allows related experiences to influence common features and support <generalization>. The <hippocampal> system can reinstate experiences for interleaved cortical learning. Thus the distinction is not simply a short-lived store versus a long-lived store, nor two copies differing only in learning rate. Representation, learning dynamics and communication between systems are complementary.<Why there are complementary learning systems in the hippocampus and neocortex: insights from the successes and failures of connectionist models of learning and memory by James L. McClelland and others>[Psychological Review 102, pp. 419–457; pp. 432–435 and Figures 9–11, interference and interleaved learning; later sections on consolidation]{ref}

The computational difficulty is <catastrophic interference>. When many memories share parameters, updates that improve a new task can impair previous tasks. Completely isolating every experience would reduce this problem, but would also prevent useful transfer through shared features. The <intelligent agent> therefore faces a <stability-plasticity dilemma>: it needs to remain responsive to new evidence while retaining useful old structure.

An original linear example exposes the issue without assuming contradictory facts. Let $f_w(x)=w^{\mathsf T}x$. An old example is $x_A=(1,0)$ with target $1$, already fitted by $w=(1,0)$. A new example is $x_B=(1,1)$ with target $0$. One <gradient descent> step on its squared error, with learning rate $1/2$, gives
$$
w'=(1,0)-\frac12(1-0)(1,1)=(1/2,-1/2).
$$
The new prediction is now correct, but the old prediction falls from $1$ to $1/2$. Both examples could have been fitted exactly by $w_*=(1,-1)$, so the loss is due to the training history, not an impossible specification. Interleaving old and new examples instead estimates the <gradient> of their joint <loss function>,
$$
L(w)=\frac12\left[(w_1-1)^2+(w_1+w_2)^2\right],
$$
whose unique minimum is $w_*$. This small calculation illustrates interference and the benefit of retaining old constraints; it is not a reproduction of the published CLS simulations or measured forgetting.

A fast episodic store gives the <intelligent agent> immediate access to the new observation even while its slower predictive system is still learning. Subsequent retrieval supplies examples to be mixed with new experience. Over many updates, the slower system can extract regularities while accommodating exceptions. The mathematical target is a suitably weighted mixture of previous and current experience, not necessarily an exact photographic copy of every past event. Biased or incomplete replay can still distort learning, and finite storage does not eliminate the stability problem indefinitely.

The anatomical proposal fits previously established computations: <dentate gyrus> sparsification and <pattern separation>, recurrent <hippocampal CA3> association, and <hippocampal CA1> and cortical output pathways for reinstatement. These functions give a plausible route from a partial cue to a reactivated episode. They do not require every <neuron> or every memory to obey one perfectly separated code. A complementary architecture also need not imply that cortex never learns rapidly.

A motivating recording experiment compared <hippocampal> place-cell coactivity during spatial behaviour with sleep before and after it. Cell pairs coactive during behaviour were more likely to coactivate in subsequent sleep; inactive or spatially nonoverlapping pairs provided controls. This supports experience-related reactivation, rather than merely elevated firing during sleep. The 1994 result concerns ensemble coactivation: it should not be inflated into direct proof that a complete temporally ordered memory was transferred into cortex.<Reactivation of hippocampal ensemble memories during sleep by Matthew A. Wilson and others>[Abstract; Science 265, pp. 676–679, place-cell coactivation before and after spatial experience]{ref}

Intervention adds a different kind of evidence. Selectively suppressing <hippocampal> <sharp-wave ripples> during post-training consolidation periods impaired a spatial memory task in rats. This supports a functional role for ripple-associated processing, but does not reveal which information was replayed or establish that cortical weight changes followed the exact interleaving algorithm.<Selective suppression of hippocampal ripples impairs spatial memory by Gabrielle Girardeau and others>[Abstract; Nature Neuroscience 12, pp. 1222–1223, post-training ripple suppression]{ref}

An important qualification comes from flavour–place learning. After rats had acquired a structured set of associations, new information fitting that schema could be consolidated much more rapidly. This challenges an account in which cortical integration always requires the same long delay. It is compatible with the broader computational idea that information consistent with an established representation can be incorporated with less disruptive change.<Schemas and memory consolidation by Dorothy Tse and others>[Abstract; Science 316, pp. 76–82, flavour–place paired associates and schema-dependent rapid consolidation]{ref}

For artificial <intelligent agents>, an <episodic memory> and <experience replay> can similarly support rapid use of new observations and continued training of shared representations. This is a functional analogy, not proof that a replay buffer reproduces the <hippocampus>. It needs appropriate retrieval, protection against sampling bias and a representation that preserves relevant context. Exact episode details, semantic regularities and current goals may require different readouts.

**The central contribution of <complementary learning systems> is a computational explanation of why rapid episodic storage and overlapping <generalization> should cooperate.** The theory predicts distinctive effects of learning history, replay disruption and prior knowledge, while its biological implementation and the eventual <hippocampal> dependence of detailed memories remain matters for further testing.

{scope}

=== Solution

**Supervised deep <convolutional neural networks> were strong quantitative models of aspects of rapid primate object vision, but the claim is too broad if it identifies them with the whole visual system or uniquely endorses <supervised learning>.** Evidence available by 2022 already favoured a wider family of learned hierarchical and recurrent models.

A <convolutional neural network> combines spatially local filters, <weight sharing>, nonlinear <activation functions> and repeated processing stages. Later units integrate over larger effective <receptive fields>. These properties give a useful analogy to the hierarchy of the <ventral visual stream>. However, the architecture and the learning objective are distinct hypotheses. Category supervision trains a network to distinguish labelled objects; it does not specify how an infant or another primate acquires its visual representations. Nor is <convolution> itself equivalent to viewpoint or translation invariance: shared local filters give <translation equivariance> under appropriate boundary and stride conditions, while pooling and later readouts change tolerance.

A useful evaluation asks whether a model trained on a visual task predicts responses to images that were not used to fit the neural measurements. Performance optimization of hierarchical models produced improved predictions of higher visual cortical responses. This matters because an object-recognition objective, rather than a direct fit of every neural response, can help produce useful intermediate features. Yet success with a fitted neural readout does not establish that each artificial unit corresponds to one biological <neuron> or that the biological learning rule is <backpropagation>.<Performance-optimized hierarchical models predict neural responses in higher visual cortex by Daniel L. K. Yamins and others>[Abstract and Results; PNAS 111, pp. 8619–8624, object-recognition optimization and held-out neural prediction]{ref}

<Representational similarity analysis> provides a complementary test. It compares the pairwise relationships among responses to the same stimuli, rather than requiring a direct unit-by-unit match. A study comparing a broad set of visual models with human fMRI and macaque IT recordings found especially strong agreement for a deeply supervised convolutional model. Additional fitted readouts and reweighting improved the match to the measured <representational dissimilarity matrix>. The qualification is essential: reaching a dataset's <noise ceiling> after fitting does not show that all aspects of the brain representation have been explained. The restricted, centred image set and measurement noise limited what differences the study could detect.<Deep Supervised, but Not Unsupervised, Models May Explain IT Cortical Representation by Seyed-Mahdi Khaligh-Razavi and others>[Abstract; Results, remixing and reweighting; Discussion, stimulus-set and noise-ceiling limitations]{ref}

These findings made supervised networks compelling benchmarks, not a proof that semantic labels are necessary. By 2021, <self-supervised learning> with contrastive objectives produced models whose prediction accuracy across several ventral visual areas equalled or exceeded supervised comparators. Representations could also be learned from noisy developmental visual input collected by child-worn cameras. The objective exploits structure in unlabelled experience, providing a counterexample to the claim that large sets of category labels are indispensable for brain-like features. Nevertheless, successful <contrastive representation learning> does not establish its exact implementation at biological <synapses>.<Unsupervised neural network models of the ventral visual stream by Chengxu Zhuang and others>[Abstract and Results; PNAS 118, e2014196118: contrastive learning, multi-area prediction and child-view <training data>]{ref}

Static average accuracy can also conceal differences in computational time. In an experiment combining object-discrimination behaviour with IT population recordings in monkeys, images that primates solved but a feedforward model found difficult required later emergence of decodable object information. Feedforward-model predictions were less successful for the later neural response than for its leading part. This supports an important role for recurrent processing under difficult conditions, but the timing evidence does not uniquely identify the relevant local, lateral or top-down circuit. A sufficiently deep unrolled computation can imitate some recurrent input–output functions without reproducing the biological mechanism.<Evidence that recurrent circuits are critical to the ventral stream's execution of core object recognition behavior by Kohitij Kar and others>[Introduction and Results; Nature Neuroscience 22, pp. 974–983: challenge images and IT population response timing]{ref}

Behavioural error structure supplies another constraint. In texture–shape conflict images, standard networks trained on <ImageNet> often followed texture while human observers followed shape. Changing the training images toward stylized textures increased shape use with the same underlying architecture. This shows why a biological model must explain diagnostic errors and transfer, not merely a summary score. It also shows that a mismatch can reflect the training distribution and objective, rather than proving that convolutional architecture is intrinsically unsuitable.<ImageNet-trained CNNs are biased towards texture; increasing shape bias improves accuracy and robustness by Robert Geirhos and others>[Abstract and cue-conflict experiments; ICLR 2019, texture–shape conflict and Stylized-ImageNet training]{ref}

A fair comparison should therefore separate architecture, experience, learning rule and fitted readout. Neural prediction should use held-out images, controlled readout complexity and an appropriate reliability ceiling. Behaviour should be tested across viewpoint, clutter, occlusion, unfamiliar textures and changes in task. Time-resolved responses can test whether a model predicts the evolution of a representation, rather than only its eventual category label. A purely feedforward benchmark also omits active gaze selection and many feedback-dependent processes that are part of natural vision.

Biological plausibility is consequently graded. Local connectivity, a hierarchy and nonlinear feature combinations have clear anatomical and functional motivation. Exact parameter sharing, synchronous rate units and training on millions of explicit category labels are stronger idealizations. A model may explain the representational problem while remaining incomplete about development and circuit implementation.

**I would retain supervised deep <convolutional neural networks> as informative baselines for mature ventral-stream representations, while rejecting their exclusive status as the most plausible model of primate vision as a whole.** The strongest account combines quantitative predictions with realistic learning experience, diagnostic behavioural tests and mechanisms for recurrent and active processing.

=== Solution

**<Temporal-difference learning> uses sampled reward-plus-continuation predictions to approach a <Bellman expectation equation>. Policy evaluation must be distinguished from finding an optimal policy.** Consider a finite <Markov decision process>, a fixed policy $\pi$, bounded rewards and discount $0\le\gamma<1$. Define
$$
V^\pi(s)=\mathbb E_\pi\!\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^kR_{t+k+1}\,\middle|\,S_t=s\right].
$$
Separating the first reward from the remaining return gives
$$
\boxed{V^\pi(s)=r^\pi(s)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P^\pi(s,s')V^\pi(s').}
$$
Here $r^\pi$ is expected immediate reward and $P^\pi$ is the transition <matrix> under the fixed policy. In <matrix> form, $V^\pi=(I-\gamma P^\pi)^{-1}r^\pi$. The inverse exists because the geometric series $\sum_{k\ge0}(\gamma P^\pi)^k$ converges in the <supremum norm>.

Define the <Bellman expectation operator> $T^\pi V=r^\pi+\gamma P^\pi V$. A <stochastic matrix> averages component differences, so
$$
\|T^\pi V-T^\pi U\|_\infty\le\gamma\|V-U\|_\infty.
$$
The <Banach fixed-point theorem> gives a unique <fixed point> and convergence of exact repeated backups. This establishes why the equation has a well-defined solution in the stated discounted setting; it does not require the <intelligent agent> to know its transition <matrix>.

The model-free alternative observes $(S_t,R_{t+1},S_{t+1})$ and forms
$$
\delta_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma V_t(S_{t+1})-V_t(S_t),\qquad V_{t+1}(S_t)=V_t(S_t)+\alpha_t\delta_t.
$$
Other tabular entries remain unchanged. Under the same policy and Markov assumptions, conditioning on the current value estimates gives
$$
\mathbb E[\delta_t\mid S_t=s,V_t]=(T^\pi V_t)(s)-V_t(s).
$$
Thus the sample error has the correct mean direction for correcting the current Bellman mismatch. The defining idea is bootstrapping: a later prediction supplies part of the earlier prediction's target, so learning need not wait for a final outcome.<Learning to predict by the methods of temporal differences by Richard S. Sutton>[Abstract and sections 2–3; successive predictions and temporal credit assignment]{ref}

In <tabular temporal-difference policy evaluation>, convergence requires appropriate sampling and step sizes, not merely writing the update. Standard sufficient conditions include bounded rewards, infinitely many visits to each relevant state and, along visits to each state, $\sum_t\alpha_t(s)=\infty$ with $\sum_t\alpha_t(s)^2<\infty$. Diminishing steps allow continuing correction while limiting accumulated sampling noise. Constant steps generally track a noisy estimate instead of converging exactly. The target's dependence on the present values also means that this is not ordinary <gradient descent> on an independent, fixed supervised label. These tabular guarantees should not be transferred automatically to nonlinear function approximation or off-policy training.

For an exact illustrative problem, alternate deterministically between states $A$ and $B$, receive reward $1$ on leaving $A$ and $0$ on leaving $B$, and take $\gamma=1/2$. Then
$$
V_A=1+\frac12V_B,\qquad V_B=\frac12V_A,\qquad\boxed{V_A=\frac43,\quad V_B=\frac23.}
$$
Starting from zero with step size $1/2$, the first transition updates $V_A$ to $1/2$; the next updates $V_B$ to $1/8$. At the exact solution, both transition errors are zero. These values are a model calculation, not neural measurements.

This solves evaluation of the specified policy. The optimal <Bellman equation> instead maximizes over possible actions. An <intelligent agent> can combine policy evaluation with <policy iteration> or an <actor-critic> improvement mechanism. Estimating the value of a poor fixed policy does not by itself make that policy optimal. Episodic tasks use terminal-state value zero; undiscounted continuing tasks require additional conditions or a different formulation.

A proposed neural implementation uses a representation of the situation to drive a value-predicting population, with a <reward prediction error> modulating recently active <synapses>. An <eligibility trace> can preserve a record of prior activity so delayed errors influence earlier states. Temporal representation is indispensable: if the population does not distinguish when a reward is expected, it cannot generate the appropriate omission signal at that time.

Primate recordings during acquisition of successive delayed-response tasks found that dopamine-neuron reward responses were more prominent during learning than after performance was established, while significant cues elicited phasic responses. This supports prediction-sensitive signalling but also allows attentional and motivational contributions.<Responses of monkey dopamine neurons to reward and conditioned stimuli during successive steps of learning a delayed response task by Wolfram Schultz and others>[Abstract; Journal of Neuroscience 13, pp. 900–913, learning versus established task performance]{ref} The temporal-difference interpretation explains how a reward response can diminish as reward becomes predicted and a response can develop at an informative cue; a worse-than-expected omission gives a negative error. The physiological interpretation is a quantitative hypothesis, not an identification of every <dopamine> signal with a single critic variable.<A neural substrate of prediction and reward by Wolfram Schultz and others>[Science 275, pp. 1593–1599, temporal-difference interpretation of reward and cue responses]{ref}

Causal evidence comes from reward-timed stimulation of <dopamine> <neurons> in rat blocking and extinction tasks. Artificial activation, intended to supply a positive prediction-error-like event, increased subsequent cue-driven reward seeking. This strengthens the teaching-signal interpretation beyond response correlation. It does not show that all components of the Bellman computation reside in <dopamine> <neurons> or that the entire algorithm is unique.<A causal link between prediction errors, dopamine neurons and learning by Elizabeth E. Steinberg and others>[Abstract and Figures 1–4, reward-timed <dopamine> stimulation in blocking and extinction]{ref}

**The mathematical bridge is precise: the mean TD error is the sampled Bellman mismatch, and appropriate repeated updates solve tabular policy evaluation. Neural evidence supports prediction-sensitive plasticity signals, while state representation, timing, function approximation and action improvement remain distinct parts of the implementation.**

=== Solution

**A <world model> supports intelligent behaviour by predicting the consequences of actions that need not be executed first. The <prefrontal cortex> can contribute task-state and goal control, while the <hippocampus> can supply relational and episodic content for constructing possible situations and trajectories.** This is a division of computational requirements, not a rigid one-region-per-function scheme.

In a <Markov decision process>, a model can represent transitions $\widehat P(s'\mid s,a)$ and expected rewards $\widehat r(s,a)$. <Planning> uses them to evaluate an action through a backup such as
$$
\widehat Q(s,a)=\widehat r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}\widehat P(s'\mid s,a)\widehat V(s').
$$
Longer searches apply the same principle across alternative trajectories. A <world model> can be symbolic, distributed, probabilistic or a combination; it need only preserve information relevant to the decision. In a partially observed situation, the <intelligent agent> must infer a state or maintain uncertainty over states rather than treating the latest image as a complete description of the world.

For a simple constructed example, suppose one action leads to food and another to water, with current outcome values $2$ and $1$. After the food is devalued to $0$, an <intelligent agent> that knows the action–outcome relations can immediately prefer water without experiencing the food-producing action again. A purely cached action value does not necessarily change at that moment. This demonstrates the usefulness of an internal model; it does not imply that every devaluation effect uniquely identifies one algorithm, because inferred state changes or offline value updates can also alter cached decisions.

<Mental simulation> can recombine remembered components into a possible future, evaluate an untried route and compare goals. It differs from simply replaying a previous trajectory verbatim. It also differs from <episodic memory> retrieval: simulated content may describe something that never happened. Both operations can use shared representations, so a new imagined situation need not be generated independently of memory.

The <prefrontal cortex> is well placed to contribute the control side of this process: maintaining the current task, selecting relevant information, representing goals and using prospective outcomes to guide action. It is heterogeneous, and activity in one subregion is not evidence that all of <prefrontal cortex> carries a complete internal simulator. A particularly relevant hypothesis concerns the <orbitofrontal cortex>: a useful decision state may depend on previous events and task rules even when current sensory input is unchanged.

A human fMRI study used superimposed face and house images with a rule determining which category to judge from the preceding task history. Different hidden task states could therefore accompany similar current inputs. Cross-validated multivariate decoding recovered task-state information from <orbitofrontal cortex>, and decoding fidelity related to behavioural accuracy. This supports a role in representing the situation in which values and actions are evaluated. It does not demonstrate that the decoded patterns themselves contain a complete future trajectory, and noninvasive imaging cannot identify the synaptic algorithm.<Human Orbitofrontal Cortex Represents a Cognitive Map of State Space by Nicolas W. Schuck and others>[Abstract, Figures 1–5 and Experimental Procedures: hidden task states, face/house judgements and cross-validated fMRI decoding]{ref}

The <hippocampus> provides a complementary source of spatial and relational content. A <cognitive map> describes relationships among locations or task states rather than only a sequence of rewarded responses. Such a representation can support a novel route when goals change, and event representations can contribute objects, locations and contextual relations to a simulated scene. Whether an internally generated sequence represents a possible future, a remembered past or a training signal must be tested rather than inferred from sequence-like firing alone.

In freely moving rats choosing journeys between food locations, <hippocampal> place-cell ensembles sometimes produced internally generated sequences before travel. Decoded sequences described trajectories related to remembered goals and predicted upcoming behaviour. The ordering and timing make the result relevant to prospective computation, but the recording is correlational: a sequence could reflect a prepared choice or goal-related retrieval rather than perform the decisive search itself.<Hippocampal place-cell sequences depict future paths to remembered goals by Brad E. Pfeiffer and others>[Abstract and Results; Nature 497, pp. 74–79: awake ensemble sequences before goal-directed journeys]{ref}

A separate intervention interrupted awake <sharp-wave ripples> during a spatial alternation task and impaired learning and performance. Place fields and post-experience reactivation were relatively preserved, helping distinguish the effect from wholesale destruction of spatial coding or later replay. This supports a causal contribution of awake ripple-associated processing to memory-guided decisions, without identifying every disrupted sequence as a <planning> calculation.<Awake hippocampal sharp-wave ripples support spatial memory by Shantanu P. Jadhav and others>[Abstract; Science 336, pp. 1454–1458: awake ripple interruption during spatial alternation]{ref}

Human lesion evidence extends the discussion beyond navigation. Patients with predominantly bilateral <hippocampal> damage were asked to construct new experiences from short scenario cues. Their descriptions were generally less spatially coherent than those of matched controls, despite preserved semantic retrieval; one patient performed well. The task therefore supports a <hippocampal> contribution to binding imagined scene elements, while the spared case, lesion selectivity and the difference between scene construction and action <planning> limit a universal necessity claim.<Patients with hippocampal amnesia cannot imagine new experiences by Demis Hassabis and others>[Results, Discussion and Methods: scenario construction, spatial coherence, preserved semantic retrieval and the spared patient]{ref}

A plausible integrated model lets prefrontal representations specify the task and current goal, <hippocampal> and cortical representations generate or retrieve candidate situations, and valuation and action-selection circuits compare their consequences. Communication can be iterative: candidate outcomes change what information is sought, and new observations update the inferred state. This is more specific than assigning “imagination” to the <hippocampus> and “intelligence” to <prefrontal cortex>, but remains a hypothesis about their interaction.

<Planning> also has costs. Long simulations can amplify model errors, and unlimited search is computationally expensive. An <intelligent agent> should select a useful abstraction, prioritize relevant branches and decide when a cached policy is adequate. Tests should distinguish sensitivity to reward changes from sensitivity to transition changes, and compare neural predictions before a decision rather than only after a successful action. **<World models> enable flexible prospective control; the evidence implicates interacting state, memory and valuation processes, rather than a single anatomical seat of <mental simulation>.**

{scope}

=== Solution

**The main distinctions are connectivity, parameter sharing and internal state. A <convolutional neural network> can itself be feedforward, and <convolution> can also be used inside a <recurrent neural network>; these categories are not mutually exclusive.** Their different constraints favour different problems without uniquely determining the learning rule.

A <feedforward neural network> passes activity through a directed acyclic sequence of layers,
$$
h^{(0)}=x,\qquad h^{(\ell+1)}=\phi_\ell(W_\ell h^{(\ell)}+b_\ell).
$$
An input produces an output without a persistent state feeding back into the computation. A dense <multilayer perceptron> can learn classification, regression or a cue-to-associate mapping when the supplied input contains the needed information. Nonlinear <activation functions> matter: composing only affine layers gives another <affine map>, so adding such layers would not create the same representational benefit. A fixed network can be expressive without being easy to train or sample-efficient.

Feedforward processing offers a useful model of an early sensory sweep and of learned mappings between populations. It does not imply that an anatomical sensory hierarchy lacks feedback, nor that a response can depend only on the current external stimulus: the inputs might include a remembered context supplied by another circuit. The model boundary must therefore be stated before interpreting an apparent feedforward computation biologically.

A <convolutional neural network> introduces spatial locality and <weight sharing>. For a stride-one layer on an appropriate grid,
$$
h_o(p)=\phi\!\left(b_o+\sum_{c,r}K_{o,c}(r)x_c(p-r)\right).
$$
The same filter $K$ is applied at many positions. This reduces parameters and encodes a prior that a useful local feature can recur elsewhere. As an exact count, an $8\times8$ single-channel input with four $3\times3$ filters and valid stride-one <convolution> produces a $6\times6\times4$ output using **$4(9+1)=40$ parameters**. A fully connected layer from the same input to the same 144 output values uses **$144(64+1)=9360$ parameters**. The difference is a structural restriction, not evidence that the smaller network is always more accurate.

Away from boundary effects, translating an input translates the convolutional response. This is <translation equivariance>, not complete invariance. Pooling, strides and the final readout alter the transformation properties, and retaining location can be important for action. Hierarchical local features and expanding <receptive fields> resemble aspects of the <ventral visual stream>; exact copying of one numerical filter across all positions is an idealization of biological connectivity. Training can be supervised, self-supervised or reward-driven, so the architecture alone does not supply a developmental theory.

A <recurrent neural network> updates a state as new input arrives,
$$
h_t=\phi(Ux_t+Wh_{t-1}+b),\qquad y_t=\psi(Vh_t+c).
$$
The same current observation can then produce different outputs depending on history. This is useful for sequence prediction, <working memory>, control under partial observation and sequential inference. For an original scalar example, let $h_t=h_{t-1}/2+x_t$ with $h_0=0$. Sequences $(1,0)$ and $(0,0)$ have the same final input but final states $1/2$ and $0$. A feedforward map of only that final input cannot distinguish them. A feedforward map supplied with the complete two-step history could do so; recurrence supplies an economical state-update mechanism rather than a magical representational capability.

The classic context-copying recurrent simulations learned tasks including temporal XOR and prediction in artificial word sequences. Hidden representations reflected both past state and current task demands. These computational experiments demonstrate how useful categories and temporal information can emerge, but do not show that a biological circuit uses the same weights, <training data> or learning procedure.<Finding structure in time by Jeffrey L. Elman>[Abstract; Cognitive Science 14, pp. 179–211, recurrent context, temporal XOR and sequence-learning simulations]{ref}

Recurrent learning is also harder to explain biologically. <Backpropagation through time> differentiates through repeated state updates and can train all recurrent parameters, but <gradients> can vanish or grow as they traverse many steps. Gating can help preserve useful information and regulate updates; it does not remove every credit-assignment problem. Activity and stability depend on the operating regime. Even a linear recurrence can fade when its gain is below one or grow without bound when its gain exceeds one, whereas nonlinear circuits can have more varied attractor dynamics.

Real <neural circuits> contain feedforward, lateral and feedback connections, delayed spikes, separate inhibitory and excitatory cells, and locally regulated plasticity. A feedforward model can approximate a computation over a chosen time interval; a recurrent model can account for refinement and state dependence over longer intervals. Neither is a complete cell-level description. Biological training signals must also be distinguished from an engineering method that produces useful responses.

**Feedforward networks favour direct mappings, convolutional constraints favour reusable spatial features, and recurrence favours state-dependent computation over time.** Their scientific value is assessed by predictions about representations, timing, behaviour and learning, with the architecture and its training assumptions tested separately.
