= Acid-base chemistry

<Acid-base chemistry> studies how <chemical acids> and <chemical bases> exchange <protons> or <electron pairs>. Here the focus is transfer of <protons> in <water>: a <chemical acid> donates a <proton>, and a <chemical base> accepts it. The resulting <chemical equilibrium> determines <pH>, while a <buffer solution> can resist changes in <pH>. This connects microscopic <chemical reactions> with a compact, measurable description of a <chemical solution>.

The important distinction is between how much <chemical acid> is present and how readily it transfers a <proton>. A <weak acid> and a <strong acid> at the same total <concentration> need not have the same <pH>. Conversely, two <buffer solutions> can have the same <pH> while containing very different total quantities of their <chemical acids> and <chemical bases>.

= Isoelectric point
{parent=Acid-base chemistry}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mathrm{pI}$}

The <isoelectric point> is the <pH> at which a specified <molecule>'s mean net <electric charge> is zero. Different <protonation> states can still carry nonzero <electric charges>; their contributions cancel in the average. For the two-step <glycine speciation model>, this gives $\mathrm{pI}=(\mathrm pK_{a1}+\mathrm pK_{a2})/2$. Additional ionizable <amino acid side chains> require their own <chemical equilibrium> calculations; averaging every <pKa> is not a general rule. <Organic Chemistry by John McMurry>[§26.2, discussion following Figure 26.2: isoelectric points and the dissociations surrounding the neutral form]{ref}

Below its <isoelectric point>, free <glycine> has positive mean <electric charge> and an <electric field> drives it toward the negative <electrode>; above its <isoelectric point>, the mean <electric charge> is negative and the direction reverses. At its <isoelectric point> the charge-driven contribution vanishes. <Diffusion> and bulk movement of the <chemical solution> can still move the sample. <Gel electrophoresis> applies this charge dependence to separation, although <protein> size, shape and interactions also affect migration. The <pKa> values of free <glycine> do not determine a <protein>'s <isoelectric point>: its free <N-terminus>, <C-terminus> and ionizable <amino acid side chains> supply a different inventory. <Organic Chemistry by John McMurry>[§26.2, Figure 26.3 and accompanying electrophoresis discussion]{ref}

= Strong acid
{parent=Acid (chemistry)}

In <water>, a <strong acid> transfers its available <proton> essentially completely to <water> under the dilute conditions considered here. For the <pH> example, each initial <chemical acid> unit supplies one <hydronium> <ion>. “Strong” describes this conversion, rather than a high <concentration>. See <Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/14.3 Relative Strengths of Acids and Bases>.

= Hydrochloric acid
{parent=Strong acid}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mathrm{HCl}(\mathrm{aq})$}

<Hydrochloric acid> is an aqueous <chemical solution> of <hydrogen chloride>. The <glycine titration> model treats its dissociation into <hydronium> and <chloride> as complete, retaining both <ions> in the <electric charge> balance.

= Weak acid
{parent=Acid (chemistry)}

A <weak acid> establishes a <chemical equilibrium> between its proton-donating form and its <conjugate base> in <water>. For a neutral <monoprotic acid> $\mathrm{HA}$, the aqueous <chemical equation> is
$$
\mathrm{HA(aq)}+\mathrm{H_2O(l)}\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{H_3O^+(aq)}+\mathrm{A^-(aq)}.
$$
<Water> accepts a <proton> to become <hydronium>; $\mathrm A^-$ is the <conjugate base>. The double arrow denotes reversible exchange at <chemical equilibrium>, not equal populations. The <acid dissociation constant> and composition determine the <hydronium> <concentration>; substituting total <chemical acid> <concentration> into the <pH> formula is generally incorrect.

“Weak” describes the tendency to transfer a <proton> in a specified <solvent>, rather than how much <chemical acid> was dissolved. A <weak acid> can be concentrated, and a <strong acid> can be dilute. Even the same <weak acid> can have a large <weak acid dissociation fraction> after sufficient <dilution>, while its <acid dissociation constant> stays fixed at the same <temperature> and <standard state> conventions.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/14.3 Relative Strengths of Acids and Bases>[Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others; §14.3, acid ionization and concentration-dependent percent ionization]{ref}

= Monoprotic acid
{parent=Weak acid}

A <monoprotic acid> supplies one <proton> per acid unit in the <acid-base chemistry> model under consideration. <Acetic acid> is an example. Several successive proton transfers require several <acid dissociation constants> and a larger set of <chemical species>.

= Weak acid dissociation fraction
{parent=Weak acid}
{title2=$\alpha=[\mathrm A^-]/C_T$}

For a <monoprotic acid> with <analytical concentration> $C_T=[\mathrm{HA}]+[\mathrm A^-]$, define the <dimensionless> <weak acid dissociation fraction>
$$
\alpha=\frac{[\mathrm A^-]}{C_T},\qquad 1-\alpha=\frac{[\mathrm{HA}]}{C_T}.
$$
This counts acid units in the <conjugate base> form. Identifying it with $[\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^+]/C_T$ additionally requires negligible <self-ionization of water> and no other sources of <electric charge>. In the ideal dilute <chemical solution> model, with $h=[\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^+]/c^\circ$, the <acid dissociation constant> gives $\alpha=K_a/(K_a+h)$. Thus lowering <hydronium> <thermodynamic activity> favors the <conjugate base>.

The diagram uses stipulated <dimensionless> <acid dissociation constant> $K_a=10^{-5}$ and <self-ionization of water> ion product $K_w=10^{-14}$ at fixed <temperature>; these are model inputs, not measurements of a named substance. It solves the <weak acid mass and charge balances>. More acid units are dissociated as a fraction of the total, yet fewer <hydronium> <ions> occur per <volume (thermodynamics)>.

At extreme <dilution>, <self-ionization of water> makes $h$ approach $\sqrt{K_w}$, so <pH> approaches $7$ for the chosen model. The limiting <weak acid dissociation fraction> is $K_a/(K_a+\sqrt{K_w})\approx0.9901$, rather than exactly one. The calculation assumes pure added <water>, a neutral <monoprotic acid>, and no competing <chemical reactions>; actual contamination becomes increasingly important as $C_T$ falls.

= Weak acid mass and charge balances
{parent=Weak acid}

Consider only neutral $\mathrm{HA}$ added to pure <water>, at fixed <temperature>, with ideal dilute <thermodynamic activities>, <water> <thermodynamic activity> approximated by one, and no added salts or other <chemical species>. Normalize every <concentration> by $c^\circ=1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$:
$$
c=C_T/c^\circ,\qquad h=[\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^+]/c^\circ,\qquad x=[\mathrm A^-]/c^\circ,\qquad y=[\mathrm{HA}]/c^\circ,\qquad o=[\mathrm{OH}^-]/c^\circ.
$$
Conservation of acid units and zero net <electric charge>, respectively, require
$$
x+y=c,\qquad h=x+o.
$$
The second equation balances positive <hydronium> against negative <conjugate base> and <hydroxide>. It would need all additional <ions> if salts or a <chemical base> were added. Together with <acid dissociation constant> $K_a=hx/y$ and the <self-ionization of water> relation $K_w=ho$, these give
$$
x=\frac{K_ac}{K_a+h},\qquad y=\frac{hc}{K_a+h},\qquad o=\frac{K_w}{h},\qquad
h-\frac{K_w}{h}-\frac{K_ac}{K_a+h}=0.
$$
This is exact within the stated ideal model, including depletion and <self-ionization of water>. Multiplication by $h(K_a+h)$ yields the <cubic equation>
$$
h^3+K_ah^2-(K_w+K_ac)h-K_aK_w=0.
$$
For positive $K_a,K_w,c$, the unmultiplied left-hand side increases strictly from negative to positive values as $h$ increases from zero to infinity; its <derivative> is $1+K_w/h^2+K_ac/(K_a+h)^2>0$. There is therefore exactly one positive <root of a polynomial>. A <bisection method> can find it without choosing an unphysical <root of a polynomial>.

If $K_w/h^2\ll1$, neglect <hydroxide>, so $h\approx x$ and the <quadratic equation> retaining acid depletion gives
$$
\frac{x^2}{c-x}=K_a,\qquad
x=\frac{\sqrt{K_a^2+4K_ac}-K_a}{2}
=\frac{2K_ac}{\sqrt{K_a^2+4K_ac}+K_a}.
$$
The last form avoids subtracting nearly equal numbers. If also $x/c\ll1$, neglect depletion to obtain
$$
h\approx x\approx\sqrt{K_ac},\qquad
[\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^+]\approx\sqrt{K_a c^\circ C_T}.
$$
Check both conditions after computing: $x/c$ measures the fraction lost from the initial undissociated <weak acid> inventory, while $K_w/h^2$ measures the omitted <hydroxide> contribution to <electric charge> balance. A $5\%$ depletion criterion is a chosen tolerance, not a universal boundary. Keeping depletion does not cure the neglect of <self-ionization of water>. These <weak acid mass and charge balances> also explain why the <square root> estimate cannot be extended to arbitrarily dilute <chemical solutions>.

= Analytical concentration
{parent=Weak acid mass and charge balances}
{title2=$C_T$}

An <analytical concentration> records the total conserved inventory of a specified component per <volume (thermodynamics)> of <chemical solution>, summed over its relevant <chemical species>. Here $C_T=[\mathrm{HA}]+[\mathrm A^-]$; it is not generally the <chemical equilibrium> <concentration> $[\mathrm{HA}]$ of the undissociated <weak acid>.

= Weak acid dissociation measurement
{parent=Weak acid}

A <pH> measurement becomes evidence for a <weak acid> <chemical equilibrium> when paired with a known <analytical concentration> and a calibrated <glass electrode>. For the pure-<weak acid> model, measured <pH> estimates $h=10^{-\mathrm{pH}}$ only in the ideal <thermodynamic activity> approximation. Then $o=K_w/h$, $x=h-o$, $y=c-x$, and $K_a=hx/y$. Require $0<x<c$; values outside that range signal inconsistent measurements or an inadequate model. Repeating this reconstruction across a <dilution> series tests whether one <acid dissociation constant> describes the data, rather than merely fitting one point.

The Analytical Sciences Digital Library's <Analytical Electrochemistry: Potentiometry/Potentiometric Titration of an Unknown Weak Monoprotic Acid> provides a complementary <acid-base titration> experiment. Its apparatus includes a <glass electrode> and meter, a stirring magnet, a <volumetric flask>, <pipettes>, and a <burette> containing standardized <sodium hydroxide>. Prepare an aqueous acid stock of known <analytical concentration>, transfer a measured portion, and stir gently with the <glass electrode> clear of the magnet. Record added <volume (thermodynamics)> and stable <pH> after each addition; use a preliminary run to locate the steep region and smaller additions there on a fresh portion. The readout is the <pH> versus added <volume (thermodynamics)> curve, not a visual count of dissociated <molecules>.

Calibrate with fresh standard <buffer solutions> bracketing the <pH> range being interpreted. Use their assigned values at the actual <temperature>; a pair near <pH> $4$ and $7$ can cover the midpoint of many <weak acid> examples, but does not cover an entire alkaline endpoint region. Rinse between standards and samples to limit carryover, keep the sensing bulb and <reference electrode> junction immersed, and wait for a stable reading. These instrument practices are documented in <HI5221 & HI5222 Instruction Manual: pH and mV and ISE and Temperature Bench Meters by Hanna Instruments> (pp. 45–47, 49–50 and 73–74: calibration, measurement, rinsing and electrode conditioning).

Locate the <acid-base titration> equivalence region from the steepest change of <pH> with added <volume (thermodynamics)>. For one-to-one <neutralization>, standardized <sodium hydroxide> <concentration> times equivalence <volume (thermodynamics)> estimates the initial <amount of substance> of acid. Half that <volume (thermodynamics)> gives approximately equal $[\mathrm{HA}]$ and $[\mathrm A^-]$ only when subsequent <chemical equilibrium> redistribution is small. The <Henderson-Hasselbalch equation> then gives $\mathrm{pH}\approx\mathrm pK_a$. Unequal <activity coefficients> or appreciable <hydronium> or <hydroxide> inventories can shift this estimate. The full <chemical equilibrium> curve supplies a stronger consistency check than its midpoint alone.

Useful controls for this model test are a fresh independently prepared acid portion, a <water> blank, a check standard after measurement to detect instrument drift, and fixed recorded <temperature>. A blank diagnoses contamination but should not be subtracted directly from <pH>, which is a <logarithm>. Keep exposure to atmospheric <carbon dioxide> comparable, especially at high <dilution>, and record any background salt that changes <ionic strength>. These are experimental-design controls suggested by the model's assumptions. The observed curve and its uncertainty determine whether the model is adequate; the diagrams here contain no experimental readouts. An aqueous <acid dissociation constant> cannot be inferred unchanged from a sample requiring a different <solvent> mixture, and a nearly neutral dilute sample makes the difference $h-K_w/h$ particularly sensitive to <pH> error.

= Acid-base titration
{parent=Weak acid dissociation measurement}

An <acid-base titration> adds measured quantities of a standardized <chemical acid> or <chemical base> to determine an initially unknown acid or base inventory through <neutralization>. Monitoring <pH> also constrains <chemical equilibrium> parameters such as the <acid dissociation constant>.

The added reagent is the <titrant>; a <pipette> selects an <aliquot> of the sample and a <burette> measures the added <volume (thermodynamics)>. Known <stoichiometry> converts this <volume (thermodynamics)> into <amount of substance>. This separates two questions that a single <pH> reading cannot answer: how much <chemical acid> is present, and how readily it donates a <proton>. A large inventory of <weak acid> can consume more <titrant> than a small inventory of <strong acid> despite having a higher initial <pH>.

= Equivalence point
{parent=Acid-base titration}
{wiki}
{title2=$V_{\mathrm{eq}}$}

The <equivalence point> is the composition at which added <titrant> and the initial sample inventory match the specified <chemical reaction>'s <stoichiometry>. It is a theoretical inventory condition; the <titration endpoint> is an observed stopping criterion. For a <monoprotic acid> consuming one <hydroxide> per acid unit, initial acid <molar concentration> $c_A$, sample <volume (thermodynamics)> $V_A$, and base <titrant> <molar concentration> $c_B$ give
$$
n_A=c_AV_A=c_BV_{\mathrm{eq}},\qquad V_{\mathrm{eq}}=\frac{c_AV_A}{c_B}.
$$
The proof is <amount of substance> conservation with one-to-one <neutralization>; a different <stoichiometry> requires its coefficients. All <volumes> must use compatible units. Since $1\,\mathrm{mL}=10^{-3}\,\mathrm L$, $0.100\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\times10.00\,\mathrm{mL}=0.001000\,\mathrm{mol}$, not $1\,\mathrm{mol}$.

An <equivalence point> does not prescribe <pH> $7$. A <weak acid> titrated with a <strong base> leaves its <conjugate base>, which reacts with <water>; the corresponding <chemical equilibrium> can make the <chemical solution> alkaline. The neutral <strong acid>/<strong base> case additionally depends on <temperature>, through <self-ionization of water>.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/14.7 Acid-Base Titrations>[Chemistry 2e, §14.7, Example 14.21(c), Example 14.22(b), and Figure 14.18: different equivalence-point pH values]{ref}

= Titration endpoint
{parent=Acid-base titration}

A <titration endpoint> is the experimentally assigned point indicated by a specified signal: for example, a persistent color of a <pH indicator>, or a feature extracted from a <potentiometric titration>. An <equivalence point> is defined by <stoichiometry>; an endpoint estimates its location. State the signal rule and its repeatability. A fleeting local color where a drop enters an unmixed sample need not satisfy the rule for the mixed sample.

Define the endpoint offset $\Delta V=V_{\mathrm{end}}-V_{\mathrm{eq}}$. For one-to-one <neutralization>, using the endpoint without correction gives inferred acid inventory $\widehat n_A=c_BV_{\mathrm{end}}$ and
$$
\frac{\widehat n_A-n_A}{n_A}=\frac{\Delta V}{V_{\mathrm{eq}}}.
$$
Thus an illustrative $0.05\,\mathrm{mL}$ overshoot at $V_{\mathrm{eq}}=10.00\,\mathrm{mL}$ overestimates the inventory by $0.5\%$. Repeatedly reaching the same color can reduce scatter while retaining this systematic offset. This example stipulates an offset; it is not a measured drop size.

= Titration curve
{parent=Acid-base titration}

A <titration curve> plots a measured or calculated property against added <titrant> <volume (thermodynamics)> or <amount of substance>. = Strong acid titration model
{parent=Acid-base titration}

Consider an original ideal model: $V_A=10.00\,\mathrm{mL}$ of <hydrochloric acid> at $c_A=0.100\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, titrated with <sodium hydroxide> at $c_B=0.100\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$. Treat these numbers as exact model inputs. Assume complete <dissociation of an ionic compound>, complete <strong acid> ionization, additive <volumes>, uniform mixing, and <activity coefficients> equal to one. Use stipulated $K_w=10^{-14}$, approximately appropriate near $25\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. These are calculated <pH> values, not measurements of these materials.

The <chemical equation> for <neutralization> is
$$
\mathrm{H_3O^+}+\mathrm{OH^-}\longrightarrow2\mathrm{H_2O}.
$$
Initial acid inventory is $n_A=1.000\,\mathrm{mmol}$, so the <equivalence point> is $v=10.00\,\mathrm{mL}$. Added <water> does not change this inventory. But the final <volume (thermodynamics)> $V_A+v$, including the <titrant>, determines all <concentrations>.

Keep <self-ionization of water> even at the <equivalence point>. With $c^\circ=1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, define the <dimensionless> acid excess $d$, <hydronium> level $h$, and <hydroxide> level $o$ by
$$
d=\frac{c_AV_A-c_Bv}{c^\circ(V_A+v)},\qquad h=\frac{[\mathrm{H_3O^+}]}{c^\circ},\qquad o=\frac{[\mathrm{OH^-}]}{c^\circ}.
$$
The <sodium> <cation> and <chloride> <anion> inventories fix the <electric charge> balance $h-o=d$, while <chemical equilibrium> gives $ho=K_w$. Eliminating $o$ yields a <quadratic equation> $h^2-dh-K_w=0$ with exactly one positive <root>. For stable calculation on either side, write
$$
s=\sqrt{d^2+4K_w},\qquad
h=\begin{cases}(d+s)/2,&d\geq0,\\2K_w/(s-d),&d<0,\end{cases}
\qquad o=\frac{K_w}{h},\qquad \mathrm{pH}=-\log_{10}h.
$$
The second expression follows by multiplying by $s-d$; it avoids subtracting two nearly equal large quantities on the alkaline side. A <logarithm> acts on the <dimensionless> $h$, as required by the <pH> definition.

At $v=5.00\,\mathrm{mL}$, the excess is $0.500\,\mathrm{mmol}$ in $15.00\,\mathrm{mL}$, giving $d=1/30$ and <pH> $1.477121\ldots$. At $v=15.00\,\mathrm{mL}$, excess <hydroxide> is $0.500\,\mathrm{mmol}$ in $25.00\,\mathrm{mL}$, giving $d=-1/50$ and <pH> $12.301030\ldots$. Neglecting <dilution> would give the wrong values even far from the <equivalence point>.

Exactly at $v=10.00\,\mathrm{mL}$, $d=0$ and $h=o=\sqrt{K_w}=10^{-7}$, giving <pH> $7$ in this model. At $9.999\,\mathrm{mL}$ and $10.001\,\mathrm{mL}$, the same smooth expression gives <pH> $5.300835\ldots$ and $8.699121\ldots$. The familiar excess-only formulas would predict zero <hydronium> or zero <hydroxide> at the <equivalence point>; retaining <self-ionization of water> removes that failure. A steep curve is still continuous.

The sensitivity is especially revealing. Differentiating $h-K_w/h=d$ gives
$$
\frac{\mathrm d(\mathrm{pH})}{\mathrm d d}=-\frac{1}{\ln(10)\sqrt{d^2+4K_w}},\qquad
\left.\frac{\mathrm d d}{\mathrm d v}\right|_{v=V_A}=-\frac{c_A}{2c^\circ V_A}
$$
when $c_A=c_B$. At this model's <equivalence point>, the <derivative> is about $1.086\times10^4$ <pH> units per millilitre. This is a local <derivative>, not a linear prediction over a whole drop. Real endpoint accuracy also depends on dosing, mixing, <measurement uncertainty>, <temperature>, extra reacting <chemical species>, and nonideal <thermodynamic activities>.

= Acid-base titration measurement
{parent=Acid-base titration}

The institutional experiment The Potentiometric Titration of an Acid Mixture, from Massachusetts Institute of Technology's 5.310 Laboratory Chemistry, determines <hydrochloric acid> and <phosphoric acid> inventories. Dried <potassium hydrogen phthalate> standardizes <sodium hydroxide>. A <volumetric pipette> selects an acid <aliquot>, diluted with <water> in a beaker. A <burette> feeds a stirred sample beside a <glass electrode>; the apparatus isolates the sample from stirrer warming. The <burette> is conditioned with <titrant> and its tip freed of bubbles. Stable <pH> and added <volume (thermodynamics)> are recorded, with smaller additions near the transition. Controls include replicate standardizations, a consistent pale-pink <phenolphthalein> endpoint, and a standard <buffer solution> check between sample runs. The readout supports inventory estimates through known <stoichiometry>; it does not directly count <molecules>.<The Potentiometric Titration of an Acid Mixture (MIT 5.310 laboratory handout)>[The Potentiometric Titration of an Acid Mixture, printed Titration-5–8, §IV.2 and Day \#2; Figure 3; standardization, aliquot, bubbles, stable readings, and buffer check]{ref}

The same protocol requires drainage time for <burette> readings and warns that an undelivered hanging drop spoils the association between <volume (thermodynamics)> and <pH>. = Titrant standardization
{parent=Acid-base titration}

<Titrant standardization> determines the actual <titrant> <concentration> by reaction with a known inventory, often a <primary standard>. It is distinct from calibrating the delivered <volume (thermodynamics)> or a <glass electrode>'s response. For a known <monoprotic acid> inventory $n_s$ and a suitable blank-corrected endpoint,
$$
c_B=\frac{n_s}{V_{\mathrm{end}}-V_{\mathrm{blank}}}.
$$
An original illustrative standard of $0.980\,\mathrm{mmol}$ requiring $9.85\,\mathrm{mL}$, with matched blank $0.05\,\mathrm{mL}$, gives $c_B=0.100\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$. The measured quantities here are stipulated examples. Independent <solution preparation and volumetric calibration> tests the <pipette>, <burette>, and <volumetric flask>; <titrant standardization> tests the reacting inventory per delivered <volume (thermodynamics)>.

The useful chain is known <mass>, known composition, known <amount of substance>, and then a measured <titre> through a specified <stoichiometry>. If each standard unit consumes $r$ units of <titrant>, the general inventory equation is $c_BV=r n_s$, where $V$ is the corrected <titre>. The one-to-one formula above has $r=1$. A <titration endpoint> supplies an observable stopping rule; whether it represents the <equivalence point> accurately remains a separate question. <Standardization endpoint and blank bias> makes the direction of the resulting error explicit.

= Primary standard
{parent=Titrant standardization}
{wiki}

A <primary standard> supplies a reliably known reacting inventory from which a <titrant> <concentration> can be determined.

The defining advantage is known high purity: the weighed portion can be assigned a known <amount of substance>. A <titrant> whose <concentration> is established against that inventory is a secondary standard solution in IUPAC terminology.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology, fifth edition (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry/standard solution>[IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology, fifth edition (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry; Gold Book, standard solution, S05924; definitions of primary standard and secondary standard solution; underlying source Orange Book, second edition, p. 48]{ref}

An accurate balance determines total <mass>, including any unwanted <water> or other material. To infer reacting inventory, one also needs known composition, stability during storage and weighing, complete transfer and dissolution, and a sufficiently complete <chemical reaction> of known <stoichiometry>. A finely resolved balance cannot establish these chemical facts. <Potassium hydrogen phthalate> is useful because a suitably prepared portion provides a known acid inventory for <sodium hydroxide> <titrant standardization>; the <KHP standardization protocol> below shows how that claim is put into practice.

Preparation belongs to the particular material and method. For example, the MIT handout specifies drying <potassium hydrogen phthalate> at $110\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ for $1.5$ hours or overnight, cooling in a desiccator, and weighing by difference; its footnote identifies removal of superficial moisture as the purpose. This is a condition of that <potassium hydrogen phthalate> procedure, not a universal instruction to heat every <primary standard> in the same way.<The Potentiometric Titration of an Acid Mixture (MIT 5.310 laboratory handout)>[Printed Titration-5, §IV.2 and footnote 5: drying, cooling, weighing by difference, and superficial moisture]{ref}

= Primary standard inventory
{parent=Primary standard}
{title2=$n_s=mp/M$}

Let $m$ be the transferred sample <mass>, $p$ the known <mass fraction> of the intended <chemical compound>, and $M$ its <molar mass>. In a model with complete dissolution and chemically inactive impurities,
$$
n_s=\frac{mp}{M},\qquad c_B=\frac{rmp}{MV}.
$$
The purity fraction is <dimensionless>: $99.95\%$ would mean $p=0.9995$, not $99.95$. These equations assume that the impurity contributes no <titrant> demand; an impurity that also reacts needs its own inventory equation. The value of $p$ must refer to the same preparation state as $m$, for example the specified dry material. A <mass fraction> assigned before unaccounted uptake of <water> cannot simply be used after it.

For an original worked example, stipulate pure <potassium hydrogen phthalate>, abbreviated KHP, so $p=1$, a selected <mass> $m=0.51055\,\mathrm g$, and rounded <molar mass> $M=204.22\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$. The NIST Chemistry WebBook lists formula $\mathrm{C_8H_5KO_4}$ and molecular weight $204.2212$, consistent with the rounded <molar mass> used here.<NIST Chemistry WebBook, SRD 69/potassium hydrogen phthalate>[NIST Chemistry WebBook, SRD 69, potassium hydrogen phthalate, CAS 877-24-7; Formula and Molecular weight fields]{ref} The selected <mass>, purity assumption and <volumes> in this example are chosen inputs, not measured results or a claim about a real batch's purity.

The acid-bearing <anion> of <potassium hydrogen phthalate> loses one <proton> to <hydroxide>. The net <chemical equation> is
$$
\mathrm{HC_8H_4O_4^-}+\mathrm{OH^-}\longrightarrow\mathrm{C_8H_4O_4^{2-}}+\mathrm{H_2O}.
$$
Thus <sodium hydroxide> has one-to-one <stoichiometry> with <potassium hydrogen phthalate> in this <neutralization>, giving
$$
n_s=\frac{0.51055\,\mathrm g}{204.22\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}}=0.002500\,\mathrm{mol}.
$$
Choose a delivered endpoint <titre> of $24.85\,\mathrm{mL}$ and matched <titration blank> of $0.05\,\mathrm{mL}$. Then
$$
V=(24.85-0.05)\,\mathrm{mL}=0.02480\,\mathrm L,\qquad
c_B=\frac{0.002500\,\mathrm{mol}}{0.02480\,\mathrm L}
=0.1008064516\ldots\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}.
$$
The extra displayed digits show the arithmetic of the stipulated model; they do not establish experimental accuracy. A compact rounded result is $0.1008\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$.

= Standardization endpoint and blank bias
{parent=Titrant standardization}

Hold the known <primary standard inventory> fixed, and let $V$ be the correct blank-subtracted <titre>. In the following model, any remaining endpoint offset has been removed from this reference. If a <titration endpoint> overshoot adds $\delta V>0$, the inferred <titrant> <concentration> is too small:
$$
c=\frac{n_s}{V},\qquad \widehat c=\frac{n_s}{V+\delta V},\qquad
\frac{\widehat c-c}{c}=-\frac{\delta V}{V+\delta V}.
$$
The denominator grows while the numerator stays fixed. For $\delta V\ll V$, the relative effect is approximately $-\delta V/V$, but the displayed fraction is exact for this additive model. An unremoved positive <titration blank> $b$ has the same mathematical effect: replace $\delta V$ by $b$. If both remain, replace it by $\delta V+b$. This analogy concerns their effects on the denominator; it does not mean a blank measures endpoint overshoot.

For the chosen $24.80\,\mathrm{mL}$ reference <titre>, an extra $0.05\,\mathrm{mL}$ gives $\widehat c=0.1006036217\ldots\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ and relative error $-0.05/24.85=-0.201207\ldots\%$. Leaving both a $0.05\,\mathrm{mL}$ blank and a $0.05\,\mathrm{mL}$ overshoot gives denominator $24.90\,\mathrm{mL}$ and relative error $-0.10/24.90=-0.401606\ldots\%$.

= Acid dissociation constant
{parent=Acid (chemistry)}
{wiki}
{title2=$K_a$}

The <acid dissociation constant> quantifies the <chemical equilibrium> for loss of one <proton> from a <chemical acid>. In <water>, write the actual proton transfer as $\mathrm{HA}+\mathrm H_2\mathrm O\rightleftharpoons\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^++\mathrm A^-$. Its full <dimensionless> <equilibrium constant> is
$$
K_{\mathrm{rxn}}=\frac{a_{\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^+}a_{\mathrm A^-}}{a_{\mathrm{HA}}a_{\mathrm H_2\mathrm O}}.
$$
The usual aqueous <acid dissociation constant> uses the <solvent> convention with <water> <thermodynamic activity> approximated by one:
$$
K_a=\frac{a_{\mathrm H^+}a_{\mathrm A^-}}{a_{\mathrm{HA}}},\qquad \mathrm pK_a=-\log_{10}K_a.
$$
Here hydrated <hydrogen ions> are understood; $\mathrm H^+$ is shorthand, not an isolated bare <proton> in the <chemical solution>. All <thermodynamic activities> and $K_a$ are <dimensionless quantities>, using consistent <standard states>. If <water> <thermodynamic activity> differs appreciably from one, retain it in the full <equilibrium constant> instead of silently mixing conventions.

With <activity coefficients> $\gamma_i$ and reference <concentration> $c^\circ=1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, put $a_i=\gamma_i[i]/c^\circ$ for each <solute>. Then
$$
K_a=\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm H^+}\gamma_{\mathrm A^-}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{HA}}}
\frac{[\mathrm H^+][\mathrm A^-]}{c^\circ[\mathrm{HA}]}
\approx\frac{[\mathrm H^+][\mathrm A^-]}{c^\circ[\mathrm{HA}]}.
$$
The last expression is the dilute ideal <concentration> approximation, not the definition in a nonideal <chemical solution>. Omitting $c^\circ$ instead produces a quotient with units of <concentration>; its numerical value depends on the chosen units. Do not take a <logarithm> of that dimensional quotient. Conditional <concentration> quotients can change with <ionic strength> even at fixed <temperature>, whereas the thermodynamic <acid dissociation constant> retains the <activity coefficient> corrections. The <IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology, fifth edition (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry/acid dissociation constant> entry distinguishes these conventions.

At the same <temperature>, <solvent> and <standard states>, a larger <acid dissociation constant>, equivalently a smaller <pKa>, indicates a stronger tendency to donate a <proton>. It does not specify an <analytical concentration>, a <pH>, or a <reaction rate>. Composition and the <weak acid mass and charge balances> are needed to predict <pH>; <chemical kinetics> addresses how quickly the <chemical equilibrium> is approached.

= Conjugate base
{parent=Base (chemistry)}

A <conjugate base> is what remains after a <chemical acid> donates one <proton>. In the <conjugate acid-base pair> $\mathrm{HA}/\mathrm A^-$, $\mathrm{HA}$ is the <conjugate acid> of $\mathrm A^-$; accepting a <proton> reverses the conversion. For example, <acetic acid> $\mathrm{CH_3COOH}$ loses a <proton> to give its <conjugate base> $\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}$. At a prescribed <pH>, the ideal dilute <acid dissociation constant> determines the partition of a <monoprotic acid> inventory between its <chemical species>:
$$
\alpha_{\mathrm A^-}=\frac{K_a}{K_a+10^{-\mathrm{pH}}},\qquad
\alpha_{\mathrm{HA}}=\frac{10^{-\mathrm{pH}}}{K_a+10^{-\mathrm{pH}}}.
$$
These <dimensionless> fractions sum to one. They are the <weak acid dissociation fraction> and its complement when the only acid-bearing <chemical species> are $\mathrm{HA}$ and $\mathrm A^-$. Equal fractions occur at $\mathrm{pH}=\mathrm pK_a$ in this ideal approximation. For nonideal <chemical solutions>, equality of <thermodynamic activities> need not mean equality of <concentrations>.

= Base dissociation constant
{parent=Conjugate base}
{title2=$K_b$}

For the <conjugate base> $\mathrm A^-$ accepting a <proton> from <water>,
$$
\mathrm A^-+\mathrm H_2\mathrm O\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{HA}+\mathrm{OH}^-,\qquad
K_b=\frac{a_{\mathrm{HA}}a_{\mathrm{OH}^-}}{a_{\mathrm A^-}}.
$$
This <base dissociation constant> uses the same <water> <thermodynamic activity> approximation and <standard state> conventions as the <acid dissociation constant>. Multiplying the two relations cancels the partner <thermodynamic activities>:
$$
K_aK_b=a_{\mathrm H^+}a_{\mathrm{OH}^-}=K_w,\qquad
\mathrm pK_a+\mathrm pK_b=\mathrm pK_w,\qquad
\mathrm pK_b=-\log_{10}K_b,\quad \mathrm pK_w=-\log_{10}K_w.
$$
Here $K_w$ is the aqueous ion product associated with <self-ionization of water>. At fixed <temperature>, a larger <acid dissociation constant> gives a smaller <base dissociation constant> for its own <conjugate base>; this reciprocal rule compares matching <conjugate acid-base pairs>. A <weak acid> does not necessarily have a <conjugate base> that reacts almost completely with <water>. For example, stipulated $K_a=10^{-5}$ and $K_w=10^{-14}$ give $K_b=10^{-9}$.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/14.3 Relative Strengths of Acids and Bases>[Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others; §14.3, Relative Strengths of Conjugate Acid-Base Pairs, cancellation giving Ka Kb = Kw]{ref}

The value of $K_w$ changes with <temperature>; $10^{-14}$ is the familiar approximate value near $25\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, not a universal constant. If <water> is retained explicitly in both full <equilibrium constants>, their product instead equals $a_{\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^+}a_{\mathrm{OH}^-}/a_{\mathrm H_2\mathrm O}^2$, the full <equilibrium constant> for $2\mathrm H_2\mathrm O\rightleftharpoons\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^++\mathrm{OH}^-$. The <acid dissociation constant>, <base dissociation constant> and <self-ionization of water> convention must agree.

\mathrm{pH}=-\log_{10}a_{\mathrm H^+},\qquad a_{\mathrm H^+}=10^{-\mathrm{pH}}.
$$
The <thermodynamic activity> is a <dimensionless quantity>, so the <logarithm> has a <dimensionless> argument. The definition uses <thermodynamic activity>, rather than total <chemical acid> <concentration>. Single-<ion> <thermodynamic activities> require a convention; practical <pH> measurements are tied to calibrated standards. See https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/P04524[IUPAC Gold Book, pH].

In a dilute ideal <chemical solution> in <water>, a useful approximation is
$$
a_{\mathrm H^+}\approx\frac{[\mathrm H^+]}{c^\circ},\qquad c^\circ=1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}},\qquad
\mathrm{pH}\approx-\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{[\mathrm H^+]}{1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}}\right).
$$
Square brackets denote <concentration> in <moles> per litre. In <water>, $[\mathrm H^+]$ is shorthand for the <hydronium> <concentration>, often written $[\mathrm H_3\mathrm O^+]$; it does not describe isolated bare <protons>. Departures from ideal behavior need <activity coefficients>. The <concentration> formula approximates the <thermodynamic activity> definition.

For an ideal <strong acid> supplying one <proton> per initial unit, stipulate a total <concentration> of $0.001\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ and negligible <self-ionization of water>. Then $[\mathrm H^+]\approx10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, so
$$
\mathrm{pH}\approx-\log_{10}(10^{-3})=3.
$$
Reducing that <concentration> tenfold by <dilution> gives $10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ and <pH> $4$, under the same assumptions. More generally, dividing the <hydrogen ion> <thermodynamic activity> by ten raises <pH> by one. This is why the scale compresses an enormous range: equal steps of <pH> represent multiplicative changes, rather than equal differences in <concentration>. These are model calculations, rather than measured samples.

Neutrality depends on <temperature>. In the dilute ideal <water> model, define the <dimensionless> ion product $K_w=([\mathrm H^+]/c^\circ)([\mathrm{OH}^-]/c^\circ)$ and $\mathrm pK_w=-\log_{10}K_w$. Equal <hydronium> and <hydroxide> <concentrations> imply
$$
\mathrm{pH}_{\mathrm{neutral}}=\frac{\mathrm pK_w}{2}.
$$
At $25\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, the textbook approximation $K_w\approx10^{-14}$ gives neutral <pH> $7$. Since $K_w$ varies with <temperature>, neutrality need not mean <pH> $7$. Nor is <pH> restricted to $0$–$14$: the activity definition permits negative <pH> when $a_{\mathrm H^+}>1$, and <pH> above $14$ when $a_{\mathrm H^+}<10^{-14}$. High <concentrations> can invalidate the dilute ideal approximation. See https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/14-2-ph-and-poh[Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, pH and pOH].

<S. P. L. Sørensen> introduced <pH> at the Carlsberg Laboratory in 1909, as recorded in the https://carlsbergfondet.dk/en/about-the-foundation/our-history/timeline-of-the-foundation-s-history/[Carlsberg Foundation historical timeline].

= Buffer solution
{parent=Acid-base chemistry}
{wiki}

A <buffer solution> contains appreciable quantities of a <weak acid> and its <conjugate base>, and resists changes in <pH> caused by small quantities of added <chemical acid> or <chemical base>. The <conjugate base> accepts added <protons>; the <weak acid> supplies <protons> when a <chemical base> consumes them. Consuming most of either partner undermines the buffering action. See https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/14-6-buffers[Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, buffers].

For a separate toy calculation, stipulate <pKa> $5$ for a <weak acid> $\mathrm{HA}$; this is an input, not measured material data. Initially $1\,\mathrm L$ of <buffer solution> contains $0.10\,\mathrm{mol}$ of $\mathrm{HA}$ and $0.10\,\mathrm{mol}$ of its <conjugate base> $\mathrm A^-$. In the ideal <concentration> approximation, the <Henderson-Hasselbalch equation> gives initial <pH> $5$. Add $0.010\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <hydroxide> with negligible volume change. The dominant <neutralization> has one-to-one <stoichiometry>:
$$
\mathrm{HA}+\mathrm{OH}^-\longrightarrow\mathrm A^-+\mathrm H_2\mathrm O.
$$
Each <mole> of consumed <hydroxide> removes one <mole> of <weak acid> and forms one <mole> of <conjugate base> and <water>. Thus the inventories after <neutralization> are
$$
n_{\mathrm{HA}}\approx(0.10-0.01)\,\mathrm{mol}=0.09\,\mathrm{mol},\qquad
n_{\mathrm A^-}\approx(0.10+0.01)\,\mathrm{mol}=0.11\,\mathrm{mol}.
$$
The shared volume cancels from the <concentration> ratio. The <Henderson-Hasselbalch equation> therefore predicts
$$
\mathrm{pH}\approx5+\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{0.11}{0.09}\right)=5+\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{11}{9}\right)=5.0871501757\ldots.
$$
This small increase illustrates how conversion between abundant partners absorbs added <chemical base>. The calculation neglects the tiny free <hydronium> and <hydroxide> inventories, changes during re-establishment of <chemical equilibrium>, <self-ionization of water>, and <activity coefficient> corrections. The displayed inventories approximate the final <chemical species> distribution; they are not an exact solution of all <chemical equilibrium> conditions.

A <buffer solution> has a finite <buffer capacity>: its <weak acid> inventory supplies protection against added <chemical base>, and its <conjugate base> inventory supplies protection against added <chemical acid>. Here an addition approaching $0.10\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <hydroxide> uses up the available $\mathrm{HA}$ in the dominant <stoichiometry> calculation. Close to that limit, the neglected <chemical equilibrium> contributions matter; once $\mathrm{HA}$ is exhausted, inserting a zero denominator into the <Henderson-Hasselbalch equation> cannot predict the actual <pH>. Excess <hydroxide> and the remaining <chemical species> require a fresh <chemical equilibrium> calculation. This is why a <buffer solution> stabilizes conditions for <chemical reactions> over a finite range of additions. See https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/14-6-buffers[Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, buffer capacity and added base].

For the pair $\mathrm{HA}/\mathrm A^-$, rearranging the <acid dissociation constant> gives the activity relation
$$
\mathrm{pH}=\mathrm pK_a+\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{a_{\mathrm A^-}}{a_{\mathrm{HA}}}\right).
$$
With <thermodynamic activity> of <water> approximated by one and ideal dilute <thermodynamic activities> for both partners, this yields the familiar <Henderson-Hasselbalch equation>:
$$
\mathrm{pH}\approx\mathrm pK_a+\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{[\mathrm A^-]}{[\mathrm{HA}]}\right).
$$
The <concentrations> are at <chemical equilibrium>; using initial values requires negligible equilibration changes. Below, also neglect <self-ionization of water> and competing <chemical reactions>.

Stipulate $\mathrm pK_a=4.76$ for an illustrative <weak acid>, without identifying it with a measured substance. A <conjugate base> to <weak acid> <concentration> ratio of two gives
$$
\mathrm{pH}\approx4.76+\log_{10}2=5.061029995\ldots\approx5.06103.
$$
Equal <concentrations> give a ratio of one, whose <logarithm> is zero, hence <pH> $4.76$. Multiplying the ratio by ten raises <pH> by one: ratios $0.1$, $1$, and $10$ give <pH> values $3.76$, $4.76$, and $5.76$. The ratio controls the ideal prediction, while the absolute quantities control how much added <chemical acid> or <chemical base> the <buffer solution> can accommodate.

= Henderson-Hasselbalch equation
{c}
{parent=Buffer solution}
{wiki=Henderson–Hasselbalch_equation}
{title2=$\mathrm{pH}\approx\mathrm pK_a+\log_{10}([\mathrm A^-]/[\mathrm{HA}])$}

The <Henderson-Hasselbalch equation> estimates <buffer solution> <pH> using a <weak acid>'s <acid dissociation constant> and the <conjugate base> to <weak acid> <concentration> ratio.

Its activity form is an exact rearrangement of the chosen <chemical equilibrium> relation. Write the acid dissociation step as $\mathrm{HA}\rightleftharpoons\mathrm H^++\mathrm A^-$, with hydrated <hydrogen ions> understood in <water> and the <solvent> convention incorporated into the <acid dissociation constant>. For consistent <standard states>,
$$
K_a=\frac{a_{\mathrm H^+}a_{\mathrm A^-}}{a_{\mathrm{HA}}},\qquad
\mathrm pK_a=-\log_{10}K_a,\qquad
\mathrm{pH}=\mathrm pK_a+\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{a_{\mathrm A^-}}{a_{\mathrm{HA}}}\right).
$$
Here each $a_i$ is a <dimensionless> <thermodynamic activity> at <chemical equilibrium>. Exactness refers to this specified <acid dissociation constant> relation and the chosen single-<ion> <thermodynamic activity> convention, not to identifying the ratio with quantities initially mixed. If <water> is instead written explicitly as a <reactant>, its <thermodynamic activity> must be treated consistently. See https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/167121/1/BM_5_2020_Unterricht_Hippler_Metcalfe.pdf[Hippler and Metcalfe, Using activities to correct the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation (2020), equations 3–4].

For a toy <weak acid> with stipulated <pKa> $5$, use the ideal dilute <concentration> approximation and put $r=[\mathrm A^-]/[\mathrm{HA}]$. The <Henderson-Hasselbalch equation> becomes $\mathrm{pH}\approx5+\log_{10}r$:
$$
r=0.1\ \Rightarrow\ \mathrm{pH}\approx4,\qquad
r=1\ \Rightarrow\ \mathrm{pH}\approx5,\qquad
r=10\ \Rightarrow\ \mathrm{pH}\approx6.
$$
More <conjugate base> relative to <weak acid> means higher <pH>. Each tenfold increase in the ratio contributes exactly one to the calculated <pH>. The stipulated <pKa> is not a tabulated value for an identified substance.

{title=Toy <pKa> $5$: ratios $0.1$, $1$, and $10$ give calculated <pH> $4$, $5$, and $6$}
{description=Both panels show the same <Henderson-Hasselbalch equation> in the ideal <concentration> approximation, with linear and <logarithmic scales> for the <conjugate base> to <weak acid> ratio. The <logarithmic scale> turns the curve into a straight line. The plotted range is illustrative, rather than a guarantee of useful <buffer capacity>.}

The distinction between <thermodynamic activity> and <concentration> is explicit when $a_i=\gamma_i[i]/c^\circ$, where $\gamma_i$ is an <activity coefficient> and $c^\circ$ is the reference <concentration>:
$$
\mathrm{pH}=\mathrm pK_a+\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{[\mathrm A^-]}{[\mathrm{HA}]}\right)+\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm A^-}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{HA}}}\right).
$$
Neglecting the final term gives the usual approximate <Henderson-Hasselbalch equation>. It is justified when the ratio of <activity coefficients> is sufficiently close to one, as in a suitable dilute ideal approximation. <Ionic strength>, including contributions from other dissolved <ions>, affects <activity coefficients>; a nominally dilute <weak acid> alone does not establish that all corrections are small. The ratio must use <chemical equilibrium> <concentrations>. Replacing these by prepared amounts or post-<neutralization> inventories is a further approximation, suitable only when subsequent redistribution is small. See https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/167121/1/BM_5_2020_Unterricht_Hippler_Metcalfe.pdf[Hippler and Metcalfe, concentration, activity, and analytical-concentration approximations].

The short derivation is to solve the <acid dissociation constant> relation for $a_{\mathrm H^+}$, take a negative base-ten <logarithm>, and use the definitions of <pH> and <pKa>. Its useful beauty is that a <chemical equilibrium> becomes a simple rule about a ratio: select a <weak acid> with <pKa> near the desired <pH>, then adjust the partner ratio. Scaling both partner <concentrations> equally leaves the ideal <pH> prediction unchanged, while changing <buffer capacity>. In practical <buffer solutions>, <temperature>, <ionic strength>, and the available inventories still matter.

= Buffer capacity
{parent=Buffer solution}
{title2=$\beta$}

<Buffer capacity> measures resistance to a change in <pH>: how much additional <chemical acid> or <chemical base> a <chemical solution> accommodates per small change in <pH>. Its differential form is a local <derivative>, rather than the total inventory available before a <buffer solution> is exhausted. Two <buffer solutions> can have identical <pH> and very different <buffer capacities>.

Choose a constant <volume (thermodynamics)> $V$ and fixed <temperature>. Let $n_B$ count added <moles> of fully dissociated, monovalent <chemical base> supplying <hydroxide>, and $n_A$ added <moles> of fully dissociated, monovalent <strong acid> supplying <hydrogen ions>. Neither addition supplies the <weak acid> component itself. The signed addition is $b=(n_B-n_A)/V$, with units $\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, and
$$
\beta=\frac{\mathrm db}{\mathrm d\mathrm{pH}}=\frac1V\frac{\mathrm dn_B}{\mathrm d\mathrm{pH}}=-\frac1V\frac{\mathrm dn_A}{\mathrm d\mathrm{pH}}.
$$
The last two forms describe separate base-only and acid-only additions. The <buffer capacity> is positive for the stable <chemical equilibrium> model below and is reported in $\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ per unit of <pH>. Since <pH> is <dimensionless>, the dimensional unit is just $\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$; stating “per <pH>” makes the measurement convention clear. Here constant <volume (thermodynamics)> is an idealization, realized approximately by small additions of concentrated reagent.

For a sufficiently small addition, $\Delta\mathrm{pH}\approx\Delta b/\beta$. A large <buffer capacity> means a small local change of <pH> for a given addition. Over a finite interval, use the changing <buffer capacity> instead:
$$
\Delta b=\int_{\mathrm{pH}_0}^{\mathrm{pH}_1}\beta(p)\,\mathrm dp.
$$
This <integral> gives a finite dose in $\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$; it need not equal the initial <buffer capacity> times the eventual change in <pH>.

= Buffer capacity of a monoprotic acid
{parent=Buffer capacity}

Assume an ideal dilute aqueous <chemical solution> with just one <monoprotic acid> pair $\mathrm{HA}/\mathrm A^-$, <self-ionization of water>, and other <ions> that balance <electric charge> without undergoing further <chemical reactions>. Hold the <analytical concentration> $C=[\mathrm{HA}]+[\mathrm A^-]$ constant. Keep the <acid dissociation constant> $K_a$ and the <self-ionization of water> constant $K_w$ <dimensionless>, consistently with <pH>:
$$
c^\circ=1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}},\qquad H=10^{-\mathrm{pH}}=\frac{[\mathrm H^+]}{c^\circ},\qquad K_w=\frac{[\mathrm H^+][\mathrm{OH}^-]}{(c^\circ)^2}.
$$
Thus $H$ denotes normalized <hydrogen ion> <concentration>, not a dimensional <concentration>. The <chemical equilibrium> and conserved <analytical concentration> give
$$
[\mathrm A^-]=\frac{C K_a}{K_a+H},\qquad [\mathrm{OH}^-]=\frac{c^\circ K_w}{H},\qquad [\mathrm H^+]=c^\circ H.
$$
Adding <chemical base> converts <weak acid> to <conjugate base>, increases free <hydroxide>, and decreases free <hydrogen ion> <concentration>. Balancing <electric charge>, including the added reagent's accompanying <ions>, gives the signed dose relative to any initial <chemical equilibrium> at $p_0$:
$$
b(p)-b(p_0)=Q(p)-Q(p_0),\qquad Q(p)=[\mathrm A^-]+[\mathrm{OH}^-]-[\mathrm H^+].
$$
Differentiate with respect to <pH>, using $\mathrm dH/\mathrm dp=-(\ln10)H$. The result is
$$
\boxed{\beta=(\ln10)\left(\frac{C K_a H}{(K_a+H)^2}+c^\circ H+\frac{c^\circ K_w}{H}\right).}
$$
The first term is the <monoprotic acid> pair's contribution, $\beta_{\mathrm{pair}}$; the remaining terms are the free <hydrogen ion> and <hydroxide> contributions, $\beta_{\mathrm{water}}$. Each has units $\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ per <pH>. Equivalently, with dimensional $H_c=[\mathrm H^+]$, $K_{a,c}=c^\circ K_a$ and $K_{w,c}=(c^\circ)^2K_w$, the expression is $(\ln10)(C K_{a,c}H_c/(K_{a,c}+H_c)^2+H_c+K_{w,c}/H_c)$. Mixing the dimensional and <dimensionless> conventions would give incorrect units.

For $r=K_a/H=10^{\mathrm{pH}-\mathrm pK_a}$,
$$
\beta_{\mathrm{pair}}=(\ln10)C\frac{r}{(1+r)^2}\leq\frac{\ln10}{4}C,
$$
because $(1+r)^2-4r=(1-r)^2\geq0$. Equality occurs at $r=1$: equal <weak acid> and <conjugate base> <concentrations>, or <pH> equal to <pKa>. This is the exact maximum of the pair contribution in this model. The full <buffer capacity> need not have its maximum there: free <hydrogen ions> and <hydroxide> make it grow toward the acidic and alkaline extremes. When these contributions are small, the central peak lies close to <pKa>.

= Buffer capacity and concentration
{parent=Buffer capacity}

Stipulate <pKa> $5$ and $K_w=10^{-14}$; these are model inputs, not measurements of an identified substance. At <pH> $5$ the pair contribution is $0.575646\ldots C$. For <analytical concentrations> $C=0.010$ and $0.100\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, respectively, it is
$$
\beta_{\mathrm{pair}}=0.00575646\ldots\quad\text{and}\quad0.0575646\ldots\;\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\ per\ pH}.
$$
The free <hydrogen ion> and <hydroxide> contribution is the same at this <pH>, $\beta_{\mathrm{water}}=(\ln10)(10^{-5}+10^{-9})\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}=0.0000230282\ldots\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ per <pH>. The full <buffer capacities> are therefore approximately $0.00577949$ and $0.0575877\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ per <pH>. Identical <pH> fixes the free <hydrogen ion> <thermodynamic activity>; it does not fix the inventory of <weak acid> and <conjugate base> available for <neutralization>.

To predict the new <pH> after a finite signed addition $\Delta b$, solve $Q(\mathrm{pH}_1)-Q(5)=\Delta b$ using the <chemical equilibrium> expression above. For $\Delta b=0.002\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ of added <chemical base>, the calculated final <pH> is $5.366798$ at $C=0.010\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, but $5.034749$ at $C=0.100\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$. The initial local approximation gives changes of about $0.34605$ and $0.034730$, respectively; its accuracy decreases as the partner balance changes appreciably.

= Buffer capacity measurement
{parent=Buffer capacity}

A proposed modern measurement extends the <weak acid dissociation measurement> by estimating the local slope of an <acid-base titration>. Transfer a measured sample with a <pipette> into a stirred vessel; clamp a combination <glass electrode>/<reference electrode> clear of the stirring magnet and place a <temperature> probe nearby. A calibrated <burette> delivers a <chemical base> of independently standardized <concentration> $c_T$, such as <sodium hydroxide>. Record the initial sample <volume (thermodynamics)> $V_0$, cumulative delivered <volume (thermodynamics)> $v$, stable <pH>, and <temperature> after each small addition. Use a separate fresh portion for an acidward series. These are proposed measurement conditions, not reported experimental results.

For the instrument calibration, use fresh standard <buffer solutions> bracketing the measured <pH> range, with values appropriate to the actual <temperature>. The Hanna manual specifies separate rinse and calibration portions, gentle stirring, and approximately $4\,\mathrm{cm}$ immersion of the probe tip. It calls for stable readings; the sensing bulb and <reference electrode> junction must remain immersed. Rinse between standards and samples and avoid carrying rinse <water> into the measured portion.<HI5221 & HI5222 Instruction Manual: pH and mV and ISE and Temperature Bench Meters by Hanna Instruments>[pp. 45–47: calibration portions, bracketing standards, temperature and immersion; pp. 49–50 and 74–75: stable readings, rinsing, junction immersion and drift]{ref}

The readout pairs each added <amount of substance> with the measured <pH>. With negligible added <volume (thermodynamics)>, neighboring readings give the <finite difference> estimate
$$
\beta\approx\frac{c_T(v_{i+1}-v_i)}{V_0(\mathrm{pH}_{i+1}-\mathrm{pH}_i)},
$$
assigned to the interval's midpoint. High <buffer capacity> appears as a small change of <pH> for a measured reagent dose. Choose increments small enough to resolve changing <buffer capacity>, but large enough that the change of <pH> exceeds measurement noise. Repeat with a fresh portion and different increment sizes; remeasure a standard afterward to check drift. A <water> blank can reveal contamination, but its <pH> must not be subtracted from the sample <pH>. These controls are proposed tests of the measurement and model assumptions.

If additions appreciably increase <volume (thermodynamics)>, even the <analytical concentration> changes: $C(v)=C_0V_0/(V_0+v)$. Merely dividing the added <moles> by the current <volume (thermodynamics)> does not restore the constant-<volume (thermodynamics)> <derivative>. One alternative is explicitly to report a changing-volume convention. For $c(v)=c_Tv/(V_0+v)$, define
$$
\beta_V=\left|\frac{\mathrm dc}{\mathrm d\mathrm{pH}}\right|=\frac{c_TV_0}{(V_0+v)^2}\left|\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm d\mathrm{pH}}\right|.
$$
This definition includes the reagent's changing <concentration> and differs from the constant-<volume (thermodynamics)> <buffer capacity> defined above.<Dynamic Buffer Capacity in Acid–Base Systems by Anna M. Michałowska-Kaczmarczyk and Tadeusz Michałowski>[§2, Definition of Dynamic Buffer Capacity, equations (1)–(3): reagent concentration in the combined volume and its derivative]{ref}

To compare with the constant-<volume (thermodynamics)> theory, fit the changing-<volume (thermodynamics)> <chemical equilibrium> model instead of treating the observed slope as $\beta$. In the same ideal <monoprotic acid> system, define $S_0=[\mathrm A^-]_0+[\mathrm{OH}^-]_0-[\mathrm H^+]_0$. For baseward additions, the <electric charge> balance at each reading is
$$
\frac{S_0V_0+c_Tv}{V_0+v}=\frac{C(v)K_a}{K_a+H}+c^\circ\left(\frac{K_w}{H}-H\right).
$$
Use a minus sign before $c_Tv$ for the corresponding acidward series. From the model parameters, evaluate the constant-<volume (thermodynamics)> <buffer capacity> at the chosen <analytical concentration> and <pH>. The fit also tests whether one <acid dissociation constant> describes the observations.

Limitations include incomplete mixing, slow <glass electrode> response, uncertain reagent <concentration>, and changes in <temperature> or <ionic strength>. Dissolved atmospheric <carbon dioxide> introduces an additional <chemical acid> system; comparable exposure and freshly prepared samples help distinguish it from the intended <monoprotic acid> response. Additional reacting <chemical species> and nonideal <activity coefficients> invalidate the simple formula. Especially when <pH> increments are tiny, measurement noise can dominate the inferred <derivative>; reporting the dose, <pH> interval, <volume (thermodynamics)> convention, and repeatability is part of reporting a meaningful <buffer capacity>.
