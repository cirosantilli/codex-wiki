= Thermal equilibrium
{wiki}

Two internally equilibrated systems are in thermal equilibrium if bringing them into contact that permits only <heat> exchange causes no net <heat transfer>. In ordinary nonrelativistic <thermodynamics> without appreciable gravitational temperature shifts, their <thermodynamic temperatures> are equal. Merely preventing <heat transfer> with insulation does not establish <thermal equilibrium>: two insulated bodies can have different <temperatures>. <University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium>[Samuel J. Ling, William Moebs and Jeff Sanny, OpenStax University Physics Volume 2, section 1.1]{ref}

A <thermometer> illustrates the definition. Its own <temperature> settles to the object's <temperature> after thermal contact, provided its effect on the object is negligible. The existing <zeroth law of thermodynamics> makes this comparison transitive: two objects separately in <thermal equilibrium> with the same <thermometer> are in <thermal equilibrium> with each other. This lets one calibrated scale compare different materials.

Equal <temperatures> do not imply equal <internal energies> or equal <heat capacities>. Nor does <thermal equilibrium> by itself imply <mechanical equilibrium> or <chemical equilibrium>; these require additional allowed exchanges. There is also a distinction between no net exchange in <thermal equilibrium> and a constant <temperature> maintained by equal incoming and outgoing <heat>. The latter can be a <steady state> with ongoing <entropy production>. Microscopic exchanges can continue at <thermal equilibrium>, but their opposing contributions balance on average.

Historically, OpenStax credits the name “zeroth law” to Ralph Fowler in the 1930s: the already numbered laws needed a logically earlier statement about <thermal equilibrium>. <University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium>[Ling, Moebs and Sanny, section 1.1: naming the zeroth law]{ref}

= Thermostat
{parent=Thermal equilibrium}

A <thermostat> senses <temperature> and adjusts a heating or cooling actuator to approach a reference <temperature>. For precise regulation, sensor placement, thermal delay and feedback stability matter as well as the nominal reference. Thermal shielding can reduce rapid environmental disturbances while feedback corrects slower ones.

= Thermal equilibration of two finite bodies
{parent=Thermal equilibrium}
{title2=$T_f=(C_1T_{1,0}+C_2T_{2,0})/(C_1+C_2)$}

Consider two rigid bodies forming an <isolated system>, with fixed composition and constant whole-body <heat capacity at constant volume> values $C_1=1\,\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}}$ and $C_2=3\,\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}}$. Their initial <temperatures> are $T_{1,0}=300\,\mathrm K$ and $T_{2,0}=400\,\mathrm K$. Assume each body remains internally uniform, their contact stores negligible <energy>, no <phase transition> occurs and no <work> is done or extracted. These are stipulated model values, not material measurements.

Because $dU_i=C_i\,dT_i$, write $U_i=a_i+C_iT_i$ with arbitrary fixed reference constants $a_i$. The conserved relative <internal energy> is
$$
E=U_1+U_2-a_1-a_2=C_1T_1+C_2T_2=1500\,\mathrm{kJ}.
$$
This is an <energy> reference convention, not a claim about actual <heat capacities> down to zero <temperature>. At <thermal equilibrium> the two <temperatures> equal $T_f$, so the <first law of thermodynamics> gives
$$
T_f=\frac{C_1T_{1,0}+C_2T_{2,0}}{C_1+C_2}=375\,\mathrm K,\qquad
\Delta U_1=75\,\mathrm{kJ},\qquad \Delta U_2=-75\,\mathrm{kJ}.
$$
The larger <heat capacity> pulls the common <temperature> closer to its initial value: body 1 warms by $75\,\mathrm K$, while body 2 cools by $25\,\mathrm K$.

\Image[thermal-equilibrium-two-bodies.png]
{title=Two finite bodies approach <thermal equilibrium> at $375\,\mathrm K$}
{description=Ideal model with $C_1=1$ and $C_2=3\,\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}}$ and contact conductance $G=0.1\,\mathrm{kJ\,s^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$. The weighted <temperature> stays at $375\,\mathrm K$ and the relative <internal energy> stays at $1500\,\mathrm{kJ}$; the plotted curves are model predictions.}

To predict a timescale, stipulate a constant contact conductance $G=1/R_{\rm th}=0.1\,\mathrm{kJ\,s^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, the reciprocal of the contact's <thermal resistance>. The rate of <heat transfer> into body 1 is $G(T_2-T_1)$, giving
$$
C_1\dot T_1=G(T_2-T_1),\qquad C_2\dot T_2=-G(T_2-T_1).
$$
Adding proves conservation of $E$. Subtracting gives <exponential decay> of the <temperature> difference, with <time constant>
$$
\tau=\frac{C_1C_2}{G(C_1+C_2)}=7.5\,\mathrm s,\qquad
T_2-T_1=100\,\mathrm K\,e^{-t/\tau},
$$
and therefore
$$
T_1(t)=375\,\mathrm K-75\,\mathrm K\,e^{-t/\tau},\qquad
T_2(t)=375\,\mathrm K+25\,\mathrm K\,e^{-t/\tau}.
$$
Exact equality is approached as $t\to\infty$ in this ideal model. Changing $G$ changes the rate but not $T_f$: equilibrium and the speed of reaching it are separate predictions. Real bodies can have internal <temperature gradients>, changing <heat capacities> and contact losses, so the plotted $7.5\,\mathrm s$ is an assumed model timescale.

= Tolman temperature law
{c}
{synonym}

In static <thermal equilibrium>, the local temperature times the stationary lapse is constant. Energy measured locally redshifts to conserved Killing energy by that same lapse; requiring a single equilibrium Boltzmann exponent gives the relation. The <Killing vector field> normalization fixes the constant but not the ratio of temperatures at different locations.

= Boltzmann suppression
{parent=Thermal equilibrium}
{c}
{title2=$e^{-m/T}$}

<Boltzmann suppression> is the exponential reduction of an equilibrium population when creating it costs energy much greater than the temperature. For a nonrelativistic species with negligible <chemical potential>, the leading factor is $e^{-m/T}$, multiplied by its momentum phase-space volume. It concerns the equilibrium abundance; freeze-out can preserve a larger population than the instantaneous equilibrium value at later times.

= Boltzmann suppression of a nonrelativistic relic
{parent=Boltzmann suppression}
{c}
{title2=$n_{\rm eq}\simeq g(mT/2\pi)^{3/2}e^{-m/T}$}

A nonrelativistic species with $g$ states and negligible <chemical potential> has equilibrium density $g(mT/(2\pi))^{3/2}e^{-m/T}$. If particle-antiparticle annihilation maintains equilibrium after the species becomes massive, its eventual freeze-out population can be much smaller than a relativistically decoupled population. The nonrelativistic annihilation rate and any asymmetry determine the final abundance.

= Kinetic equilibrium
{parent=Thermal equilibrium}

Momentum-exchange collisions maintain a common <temperature> and an equilibrium-shaped momentum <probability distribution>. Particle abundances and <chemical potentials> need not have their <chemical equilibrium> values: elastic scattering alone conserves particle number. Thus kinetic equilibrium does not by itself imply either zero <chemical potential> or conserved comoving <entropy>.

= Heat bath
{parent=Thermal equilibrium}
{wiki}

= Thermal reservoir
{synonym}

A heat bath, or thermal reservoir, is an idealized system so large that exchanging a finite amount of <heat> does not appreciably change its <temperature>.

The ideal <heat bath> supplies a fixed absolute <temperature> $T_b$ while its <internal energy> changes through <heat transfer>. A real body's suitability as a <heat bath> depends on its <heat capacity> relative to the exchange being studied, and on how quickly its internal <temperature gradients> disappear. Large physical size alone supplies neither condition. A <finite heat bath> makes the approximation quantitative; a <regulated calibration bath> instead uses <feedback control> to maintain its <temperature> by exchanging <energy> with external equipment.

For a weakly coupled system that exchanges <energy> but no matter with an ideal <heat bath>, the <canonical ensemble> assigns the <Boltzmann distribution>
$$
p_n=\frac{e^{-E_n/(k_BT_b)}}{Z},\qquad Z=\sum_m e^{-E_m/(k_BT_b)}.
$$
Here $E_n$ is a <microstate>'s <energy>, $k_B$ the <Boltzmann constant> and $Z$ the <partition function>. The fixed <temperature> removes the need to track every exchange with the environment. A substantially changing bath <temperature> no longer gives this one fixed <Boltzmann distribution>.<Statistical Physics by David Tong/1 The Fundamentals of Statistical Mechanics>[Statistical Physics, §1.3, printed pp. 17–18, equation (1.19): reservoir assumption and canonical probabilities]{ref}

= Finite heat bath
{parent=Heat bath}
{title2=$\Delta T_b=Q_b/C_b$}

A <finite heat bath> has a finite whole-body <heat capacity> $C_b$ and therefore changes <temperature> when it absorbs <heat> $Q_b$. For a rigid <closed system> with no <work>, fixed composition and no <phase transition>, the <first law of thermodynamics> gives
$$
Q_b=\Delta U_b=\int_{T_{b,0}}^{T_{b,f}}C_b(T)\,dT.
$$
If the <heat capacity at constant volume> $C_b$ is constant throughout this <temperature> <interval>, the <integral> becomes $Q_b=C_b(T_{b,f}-T_{b,0})$. This statement concerns an <internal energy> difference; $C_bT_b$ need not be the body's absolute <internal energy>.

For an original example, put a rigid body 1 and a finite bath 2 into thermal contact inside an <isolated system>. Stipulate constant whole-body <heat capacities> $C_1=100\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$ and $C_2=900\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$, with initial <temperatures> $T_{1,0}=350\,\mathrm K$ and $T_{2,0}=290\,\mathrm K$. Each body remains internally uniform; the connection stores negligible <energy>; no external <work>, matter exchange or <phase transition> occurs. These are constructed model values. The argument in <thermal equilibration of two finite bodies> applies directly: equal final <temperatures> and conserved total <internal energy> give
$$
C_1(T_f-T_{1,0})+C_2(T_f-T_{2,0})=0,\qquad
T_f=\frac{C_1T_{1,0}+C_2T_{2,0}}{C_1+C_2}=296\,\mathrm K.
$$
Thus the <heat> $Q$ transferred from body 1 to bath 2 is
$$
Q=C_1(350-296)\,\mathrm K=C_2(296-290)\,\mathrm K=5400\,\mathrm J.
$$
Body 1 loses $5400\,\mathrm J$ of <internal energy> and bath 2 gains exactly that amount. Body 1 cools by $54\,\mathrm K$, while bath 2 warms by $6\,\mathrm K$. Equal <heat> transfers produce unequal <temperature> changes because the <heat capacities> differ.

\Image[finite-heat-bath-temperature-and-energy.png]
{title=Finite bath <temperature> changes and the integrated <heat> ledger}
{description=Original constant-<heat capacity> model, not measured data. Left: accumulated <heat> $Q$ leaving body 1 lowers its <temperature> and raises bath 2's <temperature>; the lines meet at $Q=5400\,\mathrm J$ and $T_f=296\,\mathrm K$. Right: the shaded <integrals> $\int C_i\,dT$ have equal area $5400\,\mathrm J$, although their widths are $54\,\mathrm K$ and $6\,\mathrm K$. The outgoing <heat> magnitude is shown positive; body 1's signed <internal energy> change is negative.}

The <heat bath> approximation can now be assessed against a chosen allowed <temperature> change $\delta T>0$. Constant $C_b$ requires $|Q_b|/C_b\le\delta T$, rather than merely calling the bath large. For this example, an allowed change of $0.1\,\mathrm K$ would fail because the computed change is $6\,\mathrm K$. The exact bath shift and its ideal-reservoir <limit> are
$$
T_f-T_{2,0}=\frac{C_1}{C_1+C_2}(T_{1,0}-T_{2,0}),\qquad
\lim_{C_2\to\infty}T_f=T_{2,0}=290\,\mathrm K.
$$
Hold $C_1$ and both initial <temperatures> fixed in this <limit>. The transferred <heat> then approaches $C_1(T_{1,0}-T_{2,0})=6000\,\mathrm J$. The finite bath receives less <heat> because its warming reduces the available <temperature> difference. For <heat capacities> that vary, use the two <integrals> in the <energy> balance instead of this weighted average.

Conservation of <energy> also permits imagining the reverse transfer, so it cannot explain the direction of equilibration by itself. <Entropy from heat capacity> gives the additional test, using reversible comparison paths between equilibrium endpoints:
$$
\Delta S_1=C_1\ln\frac{296}{350}\simeq-16.75737\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}},\qquad
\Delta S_2=C_2\ln\frac{296}{290}\simeq18.43068\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}},
$$
$$
\Delta S_{\rm tot}=C_1\ln\frac{T_f}{T_{1,0}}+C_2\ln\frac{T_f}{T_{2,0}}
\simeq1.673308\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}>0.
$$
All ratios inside the <natural logarithms> use absolute <temperatures>. The actual exchange across unequal <temperatures> is irreversible, even though reversible paths calculate the endpoint <entropy> differences.

There is a short general proof of positivity. Put $w_i=C_i/(C_1+C_2)$ for positive constant <heat capacities> and positive initial <temperatures>. The strict <concavity> of the <natural logarithm> gives
$$
\frac{\Delta S_{\rm tot}}{C_1+C_2}
=\ln(w_1T_{1,0}+w_2T_{2,0})-w_1\ln T_{1,0}-w_2\ln T_{2,0}\ge0,
$$
with equality only for equal initial <temperatures>. In this proof all <temperatures> inside the <logarithms> mean their numerical values in one common absolute unit. This is <Jensen's inequality> applied to the same weighted average that <conservation of energy> selected. The direction of <heat transfer> and the stability of its endpoint also follow from <entropy maximization under thermal contact>.

= Heat bath relaxation with finite capacity
{parent=Finite heat bath}
{title2=$\tau=C_1C_2/[G(C_1+C_2)]$}

The equilibrium calculation determines where the two <temperatures> end, without determining how long they take. Stipulate a constant <thermal conductance> $G>0$ and the linear <heat transfer> law underlying <Newton's law of cooling>. With $Q$ positive from body 1 to bath 2, the <ordinary differential equations> are
$$
\dot Q=G(T_1-T_2),\qquad C_1\dot T_1=-G(T_1-T_2),\qquad C_2\dot T_2=G(T_1-T_2).
$$
The dot denotes a <derivative> with respect to time. This model assumes internal equilibration is fast compared with exchange between the bodies. Adding the two <energy> rates gives zero; subtracting the <temperature> rates gives
$$
\frac{d}{dt}(T_1-T_2)=-G\left(\frac1{C_1}+\frac1{C_2}\right)(T_1-T_2),\qquad
\tau=\frac{C_1C_2}{G(C_1+C_2)}.
$$
The <temperature> difference has <exponential decay> with <time constant> $\tau$. For the <finite heat bath> example and the stipulated $G=2\,\mathrm{W\,K^{-1}}=2\,\mathrm{J\,s^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, $\tau=45\,\mathrm s$, and solving the <ordinary differential equations> gives
$$
T_1(t)=296\,\mathrm K+54\,\mathrm K\,e^{-t/(45\,\mathrm s)},\qquad
T_2(t)=296\,\mathrm K-6\,\mathrm K\,e^{-t/(45\,\mathrm s)},
$$
$$
Q(t)=5400\,\mathrm J\left(1-e^{-t/(45\,\mathrm s)}\right).
$$
At one <time constant>, $T_1\simeq315.865\,\mathrm K$, $T_2\simeq293.793\,\mathrm K$ and $Q\simeq3413.451\,\mathrm J$. Equal <temperatures> are approached as a <limit> at infinite time. The finite bath causes the <temperature> difference to shrink faster than the fixed-bath model, because both bodies change <temperature>.

{title=Finite bath and fixed bath <Newton's law of cooling> histories}

Solid lines show the two finite bodies approaching $296\,\mathrm K$ with <time constant> $45\,\mathrm s$. For an ideal <heat bath> with the same $G$, set $T_2=T_b=290\,\mathrm K$ rather than evolving its <temperature>. <Newton's law of cooling> then yields
$$
T_1(t)=290\,\mathrm K+60\,\mathrm K\,e^{-t/(50\,\mathrm s)},\qquad
\tau_\infty=C_1/G=50\,\mathrm s.
$$
Changing $G$ changes these <time constants>, while the final <temperature> is fixed by the capacities and initial conditions. The dynamical model independently checks the <second law of thermodynamics>:
$$
\dot S_{\rm tot}=\frac{-\dot Q}{T_1}+\frac{\dot Q}{T_2}
=\frac{G(T_1-T_2)^2}{T_1T_2}\ge0.
$$
Its <integral> over the relaxation reproduces the endpoint <entropy> change. The useful separation is between capacity, which governs how much the bath <temperature> shifts, and <thermal conductance>, which governs the exchange rate. Neither alone establishes a reliable <heat bath>.

= Regulated calibration bath
{parent=Heat bath}

A regulated bath holds its <temperature> near a chosen setting through <feedback control>, while stirring reduces internal <temperature gradients>. Its finite <heat capacity> buffers disturbances; external heating and cooling replace the ideal <heat bath>'s unlimited capacity. This is useful for <thermometer calibration> and for maintaining a <blackbody> calibration source at a known <temperature>.

<Joel B. Fowler>'s A Third Generation Water Bath Based Blackbody Source reports a NIST test of a stirred water bath containing a copper <blackbody> cavity. A bottom plate supplied heating and cooling; a double stirrer circulated water. Electronic <feedback control> regulated heating. An electronic <thermometer> with a <thermistor> probe provided the readout. The preparation was to approach a selected setting, wait for stabilization, adjust against the measured <temperature> and then log continuously.<A Third Generation Water Bath Based Blackbody Source by Joel B. Fowler>[A Third Generation Water Bath Based Blackbody Source, §§2–3, printed pp. 592–593: bath construction, controller and setpoint procedure]{ref}

To test spatial uniformity, one <thermistor> stayed near the cavity tip while another visited ten locations at three heights. The simultaneous reference distinguished spatial differences from temporal drift. An insulated dome reduced <thermal conduction> along probe casings. Table 1 reports nonzero departures at several settings: circulation did not make every location identical. The <measurement uncertainty> analysis separately included uniformity, stability, setting, immersion and readout contributions. These tests supported a bounded <temperature> assignment around the cavity, rather than infinite <heat capacity> or perfect <thermal equilibrium> throughout the apparatus.<A Third Generation Water Bath Based Blackbody Source by Joel B. Fowler>[A Third Generation Water Bath Based Blackbody Source, §4.1 and Tables 1–2, printed pp. 594–595: fixed and movable probes, immersion control, measured nonuniformity and uncertainty components]{ref}

= Entropy maximization under thermal contact
{parent=Thermal equilibrium}
{title2=$\partial S_{\rm tot}/\partial E_1=1/T_1-1/T_2$}

At fixed <volumes> and material inventories, two internally equilibrated bodies that exchange only <heat> within an <isolated system> have fixed total <internal energy> $E=E_1+E_2$. Their total <entropy> is
$$
S_{\mathrm{tot}}(E_1)=S_1(E_1)+S_2(E-E_1).
$$
Using $1/T_i=(\partial S_i/\partial E_i)_{V_i,N_i}$, an interior stationary point obeys
$$
\frac{dS_{\rm tot}}{dE_1}=\frac1{T_1}-\frac1{T_2}=0.
$$
Thus <entropy> stationarity requires matching <thermodynamic temperatures>. Positive values of <heat capacity at constant volume> make this a strict local maximum because
$$
\frac{d^2S_{\rm tot}}{dE_1^2}=-\frac1{C_1T_1^2}-\frac1{C_2T_2^2}<0.
$$
For bodies with positive <heat capacities> throughout the accessible range, this <concavity> gives at most one interior maximum. This is the stability check behind the equal-<temperature> condition; see <entropy concavity and positive heat capacity>. <Statistical Physics by David Tong/1 The Fundamentals of Statistical Mechanics>[David Tong, Statistical Physics, sections 1.2.1–1.2.2: entropy maximization, temperature and heat capacity]{ref}

For <thermal equilibration of two finite bodies>, <entropy from heat capacity> gives
$$
\Delta S_{\rm tot}=C_1\ln\frac{T_f}{T_{1,0}}+C_2\ln\frac{T_f}{T_{2,0}}
=\left(\ln\frac{375}{300}+3\ln\frac{375}{400}\right)\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}}
\simeq0.02952799\,\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}}>0.
$$
Body 1 gains about $0.22314355\,\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}}$ of <entropy>, while body 2 loses about $0.19361556\,\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}}$. Total <energy> is conserved even though total <entropy> increases. The <entropy> calculation uses reversible comparison paths between the endpoint states; actual exchange across a finite <temperature> difference is irreversible.

\Image[thermal-equilibrium-entropy-maximum.png]
{title=At fixed total <energy>, matching <temperatures> maximize total <entropy>}
{description=The curve is total <entropy> relative to the initial $300\,\mathrm K$, $400\,\mathrm K$ state. Every point satisfies $C_1T_1+C_2T_2=1500\,\mathrm{kJ}$. Only $250\le T_1/\mathrm K\le500$ is shown; the mathematical model's positive-<temperature> domain is $0<T_1/\mathrm K<1500$.}

To construct the plot, vary $T_1$ while enforcing the <energy> constraint, rather than holding $T_2$ fixed. In numerical units of <kelvin>, $T_2/\mathrm K=(1500-T_1/\mathrm K)/3$, and the plotted function is
$$
\mathcal S(T_1)=C_1\ln\frac{T_1}{300\,\mathrm K}+C_2\ln\frac{T_2(T_1)}{400\,\mathrm K},\qquad
\frac{dT_2}{dT_1}=-\frac{C_1}{C_2}.
$$
Here $\mathcal S$ means $S_{\rm tot}-S_{\rm tot,initial}$, so its zero is a reference choice. The <chain rule> yields
$$
\mathcal S'(T_1)=\frac{C_1}{T_1}-\frac{C_1}{T_2},\qquad
\mathcal S''(T_1)=-\frac{C_1}{T_1^2}-\frac{C_1^2}{C_2T_2^2}<0.
$$
The unique maximum is at $T_1=T_2=375\,\mathrm K$. The physical trajectory starts at $T_1=300\,\mathrm K$ and moves toward that maximum; the rest of the curve compares other possible <energy> partitions, not later times in the trajectory.

The contact model also proves the direction of change directly:
$$
\dot S_{\rm tot}=G(T_2-T_1)\left(\frac1{T_1}-\frac1{T_2}\right)
=\frac{G(T_2-T_1)^2}{T_1T_2}\ge0.
$$
For $G>0$ and positive <temperatures>, equality holds exactly at <thermal equilibrium>. The appealing link is that a dynamical <heat transfer> law moves the system uphill on the constrained <entropy> curve, while conservation of <energy> keeps it on that curve. This predicts both why the endpoint is stable and why the reverse separation into hot and cold bodies cannot happen spontaneously in the <isolated system>.
