= Rough path
{parent=Analysis}

A rough path augments a path with compatible higher-order iterated-integral data. The extra data records effects, such as signed area, that are not continuously determined by the first-level path in the uniform topology. A <rough differential equation> uses this enhancement to recover a continuous solution map for irregular drivers.

= Control function for rough paths
{parent=Rough path}
{title2=$\omega(s,u)+\omega(u,t)\leq\omega(s,t)$}

A control is a continuous nonnegative function on ordered pairs of times, zero on the diagonal and superadditive as displayed. It measures the size of increments of a <rough path>, typically through $|X^{(k)}_{s,t}|\leq\omega(s,t)^{k/p}$. Continuous finite variation gives suitable controls, which let local estimates be stated without tying them to a particular time parametrization.

= Rough differential equation
{parent=Rough path}
{title2=$dY=V(Y)\,d\mathbf X$}

A rough differential equation interprets a differential equation using the higher-order data of its driving <rough path>. For $2<p<3$, its local expansion includes $V_i(Y_s)X^i_{s,t}+DV_j(Y_s)V_i(Y_s)\mathbb X^{ij}_{s,t}$, with a remainder controlled at order greater than one. For a smooth driver it is the usual controlled <ordinary differential equation>; for <enhanced Brownian motion> it is the corresponding Stratonovich equation.

= Pathlevel solution of a rough differential equation
{parent=Rough differential equation}
{title2=$y_t-y_s=\sum_{1\leq|I|\leq\lfloor p\rfloor}(V_I\operatorname{id})(y_s)X^I_{s,t}+O(\omega(s,t)^\theta)$}

A pathlevel solution is the ordinary state path satisfying a local rough Taylor expansion with a remainder of order $\theta>1$ in a <control function for rough paths>. It specifies the first level of the solution, rather than requiring the full lifted solution as part of the definition. With vector fields of regularity $\operatorname{Lip}^\gamma$, $\gamma>p$, the <Davie lemma> bounds discrete Euler errors uniformly; compactness and passage to the limit give such a path.

= Davie lemma
{c}
{synonym}

For a finite time grid, suppose a nonnegative error is zero on neighboring points and obeys $a_{s,t}\leq a_{s,u}+a_{u,t}+L\omega(s,t)^{1/p}a_{s,u}+A\omega(s,t)^\theta$. For a <control function for rough paths> and $\theta>1$, sufficiently small control intervals have the displayed bound with constants independent of the grid. Splitting near half the control size makes the higher-order errors contract, and handles a crossing single grid interval by its zero error. Applying this to local Euler composition errors yields compactness of approximate pathlevel solutions.

= Universal limit theorem
{parent=Rough differential equation}

For sufficiently regular vector fields, for example bounded fields with bounded derivatives through order three when $2<p<3$, the solution map of a <rough differential equation> exists uniquely and is locally continuous in the <rough path metric>. <Ordinary differential equations> driven by smooth approximations therefore converge whenever their enhanced drivers converge. The theorem makes the enhancement, rather than just the original path, the correct input for a continuous solution map.

= Enhanced Brownian motion
{parent=Rough path}
{title2=$\mathbf B_{s,t}=(1,B_t-B_s,\int_s^t(B_r-B_s)\otimes\circ dB_r)$}

The Stratonovich enhancement of <Brownian motion> is a geometric $p$-rough path for every $p>2$. Its second level consists of <Lévy area> and the prescribed symmetric tensor. Its increments satisfy the <Chen identity> and have finite Hölder rough-path norms at every exponent below $1/2$.

= Brownian rough path small-ball positivity
{c}
{parent=Enhanced Brownian motion}
{title2=$\mathbb P(d_p(\mathbf B,e)<\varepsilon)>0$}

For every $2<p<3$ and every positive radius, the canonical Brownian enhancement has positive probability of lying in the displayed <rough path metric> ball. This controls both displacement and area. Together with <Cameron-Martin quasi-invariance of enhanced Brownian motion>, it puts every lifted Cameron-Martin control in the <support of enhanced Brownian motion>.

= Support of enhanced Brownian motion
{parent=Enhanced Brownian motion}
{title2=$\operatorname{supp}\mathcal L(\mathbf B)=\overline{\{S_2(h):h\in H_0^1\}}^{d_p}$}

For $2<p<3$, the <support of a measure> of <enhanced Brownian motion> is the rough-path closure of <path signatures> of paths in the <Cameron-Martin space of Wiener measure>. Polygonal convergence gives one inclusion. For the reverse inclusion, decompose <Brownian motion> into its polygonal interpolation and independent pinned bridges. The bridge enhancement becomes small in probability as the mesh decreases, while every finite-dimensional neighborhood of the polygonal vertices has positive Gaussian probability. <Rough path translation> then gives positive probability in every ball around a prescribed control <path signature>.

= Nested polygonal approximation of Brownian rough paths
{parent=Enhanced Brownian motion}

For deterministic nested partitions with mesh tending to zero, lift the polygonal interpolation of <Brownian motion> by its ordinary iterated integrals. These <path signatures> converge almost surely to <enhanced Brownian motion> in every Hölder rough-path metric of exponent below $1/2$, hence in $p$-variation for $p>2$. Conditioning on the partition values gives a nested <martingale> for the first level and the off-diagonal second levels. Moment bounds and interpolation upgrade pointwise convergence to convergence in the <rough path metric>.

= Brownian rough path martingale Hölder bound
{c}
{parent=Nested polygonal approximation of Brownian rough paths}
{title2=$\|S_2(B^{(n)})\|_\alpha\leq C\sqrt{\mathbb E[\|\mathbf B\|_\alpha^2\mid\mathcal F_n]}$}

Condition on the Brownian values at a dyadic mesh. First-level increments and antisymmetric area increments of its polygonal lift are conditional expectations of the corresponding Brownian increments. Conditional Jensen inequalities and the homogeneous degree-two norm give the displayed bound. The <Doob maximal inequality> makes the right-hand side uniformly finite almost surely when the Brownian Hölder norm has sufficiently many moments. Dense-time martingale convergence and equicontinuity then yield simultaneous convergence at every time.

= Signatures of bounded variation paths
{synonym}

The <path signature> collects the ordered iterated <Riemann-Stieltjes integrals> of a continuous path of <bounded variation>. Its truncation belongs to the <free step-N nilpotent Lie group>. It multiplies under concatenation by the <Chen identity>, is independent of orientation-preserving reparametrisation of the whole path, and records more than its endpoint displacement.

= Iterated integral of a path

= Iterated integrals of a path
{synonym}

For a continuous path of <bounded variation>, integrate successively over the ordered simplex using <Riemann-Stieltjes integrals>. These integrals form the levels of its <path signature>. Appending the final integration variable gives the differential recursion $dS^{(k)}_t=S^{(k-1)}_t\otimes dx_t$.

= Shuffle identity for iterated integrals
{parent=Iterated integral of a path}

The product of two coordinate <iterated integrals of a path> is the sum over order-preserving interleavings of their integration variables. Split the product of the two ordered simplices according to the joint order of all variables. This identity explains the algebraic constraints on a geometric <rough path> and the cancellation of low-degree terms in the composition of its local Euler maps.

= Chen identity
{c}
{parent=Signature of a bounded variation path}
{title2=$S_N(x)_{s,u}=S_N(x)_{s,t}\otimes S_N(x)_{t,u}$}

Partition each ordered integration simplex according to the number of times before the concatenation point. The integral over each resulting region factors into an initial iterated integral and a final iterated integral. Summing these factorizations proves the identity in the <truncated tensor algebra>.

= Rough path metric
{parent=Rough path}
{title2=$d_p(\mathbf X,\mathbf Y)=\max_{1\le k\le\lfloor p\rfloor}\left(\sup_D\sum_{[u,v]\in D}|X^{(k)}_{u,v}-Y^{(k)}_{u,v}|^{p/k}\right)^{k/p}$}

This inhomogeneous variation metric compares the tensor increments level by level. A common alternative is the Hölder metric $\rho_\alpha=\max_k\sup_{s<t}|X^{(k)}_{s,t}-Y^{(k)}_{s,t}|/|t-s|^{k\alpha}$. On a finite interval convergence in this metric implies $p$-variation convergence whenever $p\alpha\ge1$. Both topologies control the iterated integrals needed by the <universal limit theorem>.

= Geometric p-rough path
{parent=Rough path}

A geometric $p$-rough path is a limit, in the <rough path metric>, of <path signatures> of sufficiently regular paths. It is a <weak geometric p-rough path>. At the same exponent these definitions need not coincide; weak geometric paths can be approximated after slightly weakening the variation exponent. The Brownian enhancement is geometric for every $p>2$.

= Rough path translation
{parent=Geometric p-rough path}
{title2=$T_h\mathbf X$}

For $2<p<3$ and a continuous path $h$ of <bounded variation>, translate the first level by $h$ and add the mixed integrals $\int X_{s,r}\otimes dh_r$, $\int h_{s,r}\otimes dX_r$ and $\int h_{s,r}\otimes dh_r$ to the second level. <Integration by parts> defines the second mixed integral. The operation is continuous in the <rough path metric> and the variation norm of $h$. For canonical lifts it is precisely the enhancement of the sum of the paths.

= Cameron-Martin quasi-invariance of enhanced Brownian motion
{c}
{parent=Rough path translation}

For a deterministic path $h$ in the <Cameron-Martin space of Wiener measure>, the law of $T_h\mathbf B$ is equivalent to the law of <enhanced Brownian motion>. The <Cameron-Martin theorem> gives equivalence for the underlying Brownian paths; the canonical enhancement of the translated path is exactly the rough-path translation, including its mixed area terms. Continuity of this translation transfers small-ball positivity at the identity to every control signature.

= Weak geometric p-rough path
{parent=Rough path}
{title2=$\mathbf X:[0,T]\to G^{\lfloor p\rfloor}(\mathbb R^d)$}

A weak geometric $p$-rough path is a continuous path in the <free step-N nilpotent Lie group>, with $N=\lfloor p\rfloor$, whose increments have finite $p$-variation for the <Carnot-Carathéodory distance>. Its multiplicative increments are $\mathbf X_{s,t}=\mathbf X_s^{-1}\otimes\mathbf X_t$. They obey the <Chen identity> and the algebraic symmetries of <path signatures>.

= Pure-area rough path
{parent=Weak geometric p-rough path}
{title2=$\mathbf y_t=\exp(t[e_1,e_2])$}

The first level of this group path is zero but its second level grows linearly. Its <Carnot-Carathéodory distance> increments are a constant times $|t-s|^{1/2}$. It therefore has finite $p$-variation precisely when $p\geq2$, and is a step-two weak geometric rough path at $p=2$, even though its first-level trace is constant. For higher truncations the same Lie exponential defines a compatible lift.

= Harmonic analysis
{parent=Analysis}
{wiki}

Harmonic analysis decomposes functions into elementary oscillations, generalizing <Fourier series> and the <Fourier transform>. On a locally compact Abelian group the elementary oscillations are <continuous unitary characters>, and <Haar measure> supplies translation-invariant integration.

= Carleson measure
{c}
{parent=Harmonic analysis}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mu(S(I))\le C m(I)$}

A positive <Borel measure> near a domain boundary is a Carleson measure when its mass in boundary neighborhoods is bounded by a constant times their boundary measure. On the <unit disc>, this becomes the displayed bound for every <Carleson box>. The equivalent <hyperbolic geodesic cap> version follows from the <geodesic-cap and Carleson-box comparison>. The <Carleson embedding theorem on the disk> explains why this geometric growth condition controls integration of analytic <Hardy spaces>.

= Carleson embedding theorem on the disk
{c}
{parent=Carleson measure}
{title2=$H^1(\mathbb D)\hookrightarrow L^1(\mu)\iff\mu\text{ is Carleson}$}

A positive <Borel measure> on the <unit disc> satisfies $\int|f|\,d\mu\le N\|f\|_1$ for every <H1 space on the unit disk> function exactly when it is a <Carleson measure>, with comparable optimal constants. The <normalized squared Cauchy kernel> tests necessity. For sufficiency, the boundary shadow of every point with $|f(z)|>t$ lies inside a component arc of $\{Nf>t\}$. The <geodesic-cap and Carleson-box comparison> bounds the measure of each shadow region by its component length, giving $\mu\{|f|>t\}\le C\|\mu\|_{\rm Carl}m\{Nf>t\}$. The <layer-cake representation> and <H1 non-tangential maximal inequality on the disk> give the embedding. The broader kernel and covering mechanism is discussed in \a[https://www.math.ucla.edu/~tao/247b.1.07w/notes6.pdf][Terence Tao's harmonic-analysis notes, Exercise 2 after Lemma 4.1]; the analytic endpoint here uses the fractional-power argument just given.

= Carleson box
{c}
{parent=Carleson measure}
{title2=$S(I)=\{r\xi:\xi\in I,\ 1-m(I)<r<1\}$}

A boundary neighborhood over an arc $I$ of the <unit circle>, with radial depth equal to its normalized angular length $m(I)$. Changing that depth by any fixed positive factor gives an equivalent <Carleson measure> condition. Large arcs and central mass can be handled separately by finitely many fixed neighborhoods covering the <unit disc>.

= Non-tangential maximal function
{parent=Harmonic analysis}
{title2=$F^*_a(x)=\sup_{y>0,\ |x-x'|\leq ay}|F(x',y)|$}

This supremum function takes a supremum over a cone approaching the boundary point $x$; the positive number $a$ is its aperture. For an analytic upper-half-plane $H^1$ function, it belongs to $L^1$. To prove this without first assuming boundary absolute continuity, shift the function upwards, apply subharmonic Poisson comparison to its square root, and use the strong $L^2$ bound for the <Hardy-Littlewood maximal function>. <Fatou lemma> removes the shift.

= Poisson maximal function is dominated by the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function
{c}
{parent=Non-tangential maximal function}
{title2=$\sup_{z\in\Gamma_A(\xi)}|P[g](z)|\le C_A Mg(\xi)$}

For integrable boundary data $g$ on the <unit circle>, this follows from the <Poisson kernel> bound $C_A\delta/(\delta^2+d^2)$ in a fixed <Stolz region>. The arc of radius $\delta$ and successive dyadic annuli contribute at most $C_A2^{-k}Mg(\xi)$, which sum to the displayed estimate. This links harmonic extension to the <Hardy-Littlewood maximal function> and its strong $L^p$ bounds for $p>1$.

= Hardy space
{c}
{parent=Harmonic analysis}
{wiki}
{title2=$H^p$}

For $0<p<∞$, an analytic <Hardy space> controls the integral means of a <holomorphic function> as it approaches a domain boundary. On the disk, $H^p$ requires $\sup_{0<r<1}\int|F(re^{i\theta})|^p\,d\theta<\infty$; on the upper half-plane it requires $\sup_{y>0}\int_{\mathbb R}|f(x+iy)|^p\,dx<\infty$. For $p=\infty$ use a supremum norm. These analytic spaces differ from the <harmonic Hardy space of the upper half-space>; the <Hardy space of the circle> is the boundary Hilbert-space realization of the disk $H^2$ space.

= H1 space on the unit disk
{c}
{parent=Hardy space}
{title2=$H^1(\mathbb D)$}

This <Hardy space> consists of <holomorphic functions> with $\|f\|_1=\sup_{r<1}\int_{\partial\mathbb D}|f(r\xi)|\,dm(\xi)<\infty$, where $m$ is normalized angular measure. The <H1 non-tangential maximal inequality on the disk> controls their approach to the boundary, and the <Carleson embedding theorem on the disk> characterizes measures against which all these functions remain integrable.

= H1 non-tangential maximal inequality on the disk
{c}
{parent=H1 space on the unit disk}
{title2=$\|N_Af\|_{L^1}\le C_A\|f\|_{H^1}$}

For an analytic <H1 space on the unit disk> function, its fixed-aperture <non-tangential maximal function> obeys the displayed strong $L^1$ bound. First replace $f$ by $f_R(z)=f(Rz)$, $R<1$. The fact that <modulus powers of holomorphic functions are subharmonic> gives $|f_R|^{1/2}\le P[|f(R\xi)|^{1/2}]$. Since a <Poisson maximal function is dominated by the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function>, the strong $L^2$ <Hardy-Littlewood maximal inequality> yields $\int N_Af_R\le C_A\int|f(R\xi)|\le C_A\|f\|_1$. Pointwise interior convergence and the <Fatou lemma> remove $R$. Analyticity permits this endpoint estimate; general $L^1$ boundary data have only a weak $L^1$ maximal bound.

= Normalized squared Cauchy kernel
{parent=H1 space on the unit disk}
{title2=$f_a(z)=(1-|a|^2)/(1-\overline a z)^2$}

For $a\in\mathbb D$, this <holomorphic function> has <H1 space on the unit disk> norm one: its boundary modulus is the unit-mass <Poisson kernel on the circle>. The radial integrals are bounded by that boundary integral by <subharmonic> comparison and tend to it by continuity. Choosing $a$ at depth comparable to an arc's length makes $|f_a|$ bounded below by the reciprocal length throughout its <hyperbolic geodesic cap>. It tests necessity in the <Carleson embedding theorem on the disk>.

= H-infinity space
{c}
{parent=Hardy space}
{title2=$H^\infty(\mathbb D)$}

This <Hardy space> consists of bounded <holomorphic functions> on the <unit disc>, with norm $\|f\|_\infty=\sup_{z\in\mathbb D}|f(z)|$. Its almost-everywhere boundary realization follows from the <Fatou theorem for bounded holomorphic functions>.

= Fatou theorem for bounded holomorphic functions
{c}
{parent=H-infinity space}

A bounded <holomorphic function> on the <unit disc> has finite <nontangential limits> almost everywhere on its boundary. To outline a proof, bounded real and imaginary <harmonic> parts have $L^\infty$ boundary representations: apply the <Banach-Alaoglu theorem> to their circular traces and pass to the limit in the <Poisson integral on the unit disk>. Almost every boundary point is a <Lebesgue point>. The fact that a <Poisson integral converges nontangentially at Lebesgue points> gives the desired limits. Their $L^\infty$ norm equals the interior supremum norm, and the <Poisson integral> recovers the original function. Exceptional points can lack even radial limits; $\exp(i\operatorname{Log}(1-z))$ is a bounded example at the boundary point one.

= F. and M. Riesz theorem
{c}
{parent=Hardy space}
{wiki}

If a finite complex <Borel measure> $\mu$ on the circle has <Fourier coefficients> $\widehat\mu(n)=0$ for all $n<0$, then it is absolutely continuous with respect to arc length. The reflected Fourier convention gives the equivalent theorem with positive coefficients vanishing. The <Poisson integral on the unit disk> is analytic because of the one-sided Fourier spectrum. Subharmonic comparison for its square root and an $L^2$ maximal estimate produce an integrable radial maximum. This maximum dominates the weak-star boundary measure, forcing <absolute continuity of measures>. The same proof on the real line says that a finite measure with <Fourier transform of a finite measure> supported in $[0,\infty)$ has a Lebesgue density.

= Calderón–Zygmund decomposition
{c}
{parent=Harmonic analysis}
{title2=$f=g+\sum_Qb_Q$}

For $f\in L^1(\mathbb R^d)$ and a positive threshold $\lambda$, take maximal dyadic cubes with mean of $|f|$ exceeding $\lambda$. They are disjoint, have total volume at most $\|f\|_1/\lambda$, and their means are at most $2^d\lambda$. Replace $f$ inside each selected cube by its mean to obtain $g$; write $b_Q=(f-f_Q)\mathbf1_Q$. Then $\|g\|_\infty\leq2^d\lambda$, $\|g\|_1\leq\|f\|_1$, $\int b_Q=0$, and $\sum_Q\|b_Q\|_1\leq2\|f\|_1$. The outside bound follows from the <Lebesgue differentiation theorem>. This separates a bounded part from localized cancellation errors.

= Positive-definite function
{parent=Harmonic analysis}
{title2=$\sum_{i,j}z_i\overline{z_j}p(x_i x_j^{-1})\geq0$}
{wiki}

A complex-valued function on a group is positive-definite when the displayed inequality holds for every finite choice of points and coefficients. This includes positive semidefinite, rather than necessarily strictly positive definite, Gram matrices. In particular $p(e)\geq0$ and $|p(x)|\leq p(e)$. On an Abelian group, every convolution square $u*u^*$ with $u\in C_c(G)$ is continuous and positive-definite: its quadratic form is the integral of $|\sum_i z_i u(t+x_i)|^2$.

= Cyclic unitary representation of a positive-definite function
{parent=Positive-definite function}
{title2=$p(x)=\langle U_xv,v\rangle$}

For an Abelian group, on finite formal sums of group points, define $\langle[x],[y]\rangle=p(xy^{-1})$, with the first variable linear. Quotient by the null space of this <positive semidefinite> form and complete to a <Hilbert space>. Left translation is unitary, and $v=[e]$ generates a dense span of translated vectors. For continuous $p$, the equality $\|U_x[y]-[y]\|^2=2(p(e)-\operatorname{Re}p(x))$ gives <strong continuity>. This realizes $p$ as a <matrix coefficient> without assuming its integral representation.

= Bochner theorem
{c}
{synonym}

For a locally compact Hausdorff Abelian group, a continuous <positive-definite function> $p$ is uniquely representable as $p(x)=\int_{\widehat G}\chi(x)\,d\mu_p(\chi)$ for a finite positive <Radon measure> on the <Pontryagin dual group>. = Bochner-Herglotz spectral theorem
{c}
{synonym}

A <positive-definite function> $r$ on the discrete group $\mathbb Z$ is the sequence of <Fourier coefficients> of a unique finite positive <Borel measure> on the circle, with total mass $r(0)$. Positivity makes the finite sums $\sum_{|n|<N}(1-|n|/N)r(n)e^{-2\pi int}$ nonnegative. Their measures converge weakly to the required measure; uniqueness follows from density of <trigonometric polynomials> in continuous functions.

= Wiener Fourier coefficient lemma
{c}
{parent=Bochner–Herglotz theorem}
{title2=$\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\sum_{n<N}|\widehat\sigma(n)|^2=\sum_t\sigma(\{t\})^2$}

For a finite positive <Borel measure> on the circle, averaged squared <Fourier coefficients> tend to the sum of squares of its atomic masses. Express each squared coefficient as a double integral and average the circle character. The averaged character is bounded by one, tends to one on the diagonal and to zero elsewhere, so the <dominated convergence theorem> gives the diagonal mass. In particular an atomless measure has vanishing averaged absolute coefficients by the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>.

= Bochner consistency identity for convolution squares
{c}
{parent=Bochner's theorem}
{title2=$\widehat p\,\mu_q=\widehat q\,\mu_p=\mu_{p*q}$}

For finite sums of compactly supported convolution squares, $\widehat p,\widehat q\geq0$. Insert the <Bochner theorem> representation of $q$ into $p*q$ and apply the <Fubini theorem>: its representing measure is $\widehat p\,\mu_q$. Reversing $p,q$ and using uniqueness gives the displayed identity. It makes the local quotients $\mu_p/\widehat p$ consistent wherever $\widehat p>0$.

= Dirichlet form

A Dirichlet form on a real $L^2$ space is a densely defined closed nonnegative symmetric <bilinear form> with the Markov contraction property. = Integral equation
{parent=Analysis}
{wiki}

An integral equation asks for an unknown function that occurs under an integral. Integral equations with a variable upper limit have a triangular dependence structure that often permits successive approximation.

= Abel integral equation
{c}
{parent=Integral equation}
{title2=$g(x)=\int_x^b f(y)(y-x)^{-1/2}\,dy$}

This square-root-kernel <integral equation> is inverted by integrating $g(x)/\sqrt{x-z}$ over $z<x<b$ and interchanging integrations. The inner integral over $z<x<y$ equals $\pi$, so $\int_z^bg(x)/\sqrt{x-z}\,dx=\pi\int_z^bf(y)\,dy$. Differentiating recovers $f(z)=-\pi^{-1}d[\int_z^bg(x)/\sqrt{x-z}\,dx]/dz$. The changes of variables $x=p^2$, $y=\eta^2$ put the angular-ray equation into this form.

= Linear integral equation
{parent=Integral equation}
{title2=$(I+K)f=g$}

A <linear integral equation> depends linearly on its unknown function. For example, $(I+K)f=g$ with $(Kf)(x)=\int K(x,y)f(y)dy$ has a trivial homogeneous null space whenever its inhomogeneous problem has a unique solution: otherwise a nonzero homogeneous solution could be added to that solution. This is the uniqueness step used in dressing constructions of a <Lax pair>.

= Volterra integral equation
{c}
{parent=Integral equation}
{wiki}

A Volterra integral equation has a variable integration limit, so its value at a point depends only on earlier values. Repeated integration produces factorial decay, which often makes its Neumann series converge without a smallness assumption.

= Volterra iteration for a singular power-series problem
{parent=Volterra integral equation}
{title2=$y_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^kx^{k+1}/[k!(k+1)!]$}

The iteration $y_0=x$, $y_n=x-\int_0^x(x-s)y_{n-1}(s)/s\,ds$ successively appends terms of the displayed series. Induction verifies the iterates, and factorial denominator bounds give convergence with derivatives on compact sets. Its limit is the <power series> solution of $xy''+y=0$, $y(0)=0$, $y'(0)=1$.

= Volterra convolution of kernels
{c}
{parent=Volterra integral equation}
{title2=$(A*B)(t)=\int_0^t A(t-s)B(s)\,ds$}

For time-dependent integral kernels on a <Riemannian manifold>, composition also integrates over the intermediate point: $(A*B)(t,x,y)=\int_0^t\int_M A(t-s,x,z)B(s,z,y)\,dV_z\,ds$. It is associative under the usual absolute-integrability hypotheses. The integration simplex gives factorial bounds for iterated convolutions of bounded kernels.

= Iterated Cauchy-Schwarz bound for a Volterra operator
{parent=Volterra integral equation}
{title2=$\|\tau^nf(t)\|\leq\frac{C^nt^n}{\sqrt{(2n-1)!!}}\sup_{s\leq t}\|f(s)\|$}

Starting from $\|\tau g(t)\|^2\leq C^2t\int_0^t\|g(s)\|^2ds$, induction integrates $s^{2n-2}$ at the $n$th step. Its denominator accumulates the odd integers $1,3,\ldots,2n-1$. The <factorial bound for a Volterra iterate> is stronger because $(n!)^2\geq(2n-1)!!$.

= Factorial bound for a Volterra iterate
{parent=Volterra integral equation}
{title2=$\|\tau^nf(t)\|\leq\frac{C^nt^n}{n!}\sup_{s\leq t}\|f(s)\|$}

If $\|\tau f(t)\|\leq C\int_0^t\|f(s)\|ds$, repeated integration produces the time-ordered simplex of volume $t^n/n!$. Thus the <Neumann series> $\sum_{n\geq0}\tau^n$ converges on every finite interval, without requiring the single-step <operator norm> to be less than one.

= Angular dipole

The angular dipole is the degree-one <spherical harmonic> component. A real angular dipole is a function $\mathbf d\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}$ for a fixed vector $\mathbf d$. Direction-dependent observer velocity and gravitational endpoint terms can therefore be discarded from higher-multipole calculations when they have this form.

= Angular monopole
{parent=Spherical harmonic}
{title2=$\ell=0$}

The angular monopole of a function on the sphere is its constant spherical-harmonic component, equal to its angular average. Subtracting it removes the mean temperature of a cosmic microwave background sky map without altering higher angular multipoles.

= Vector spherical harmonic
{parent=Spherical harmonic}
{wiki}

For an angular <spherical harmonic> $Y$ of degree $n\geq1$, the radial, tangential gradient and tangential curl families can be normalized as $\mathbf P=\widehat{\mathbf r}Y$, $\mathbf B=r\nabla Y/\sqrt{n(n+1)}$ and $\mathbf C=-r\widehat{\mathbf r}\times\nabla Y/\sqrt{n(n+1)}$. They depend only on direction. At $n=0$ only the radial family is defined by these formulas.

= Solid harmonic
{parent=Spherical harmonic}
{title2=$r^nY_{nm},\quad r^{-n-1}Y_{nm}$}
{wiki}

A <solid harmonic> extends a <spherical harmonic> to a <harmonic function> in space with either the regular radial power $r^n$ or the irregular radial power $r^{-n-1}$. In three dimensions these are the two separated radial solutions of the <Laplace equation> at angular degree $n$.

= Irregular solid harmonic
{parent=Solid harmonic}
{title2=$r^{-n-1}Y_{nm}=r^{-(2n+1)}H_n$}

An <irregular solid harmonic> is harmonic on punctured three-dimensional <Euclidean space> and generally singular at the origin. The <Kelvin transform> maps a degree-$n$ <regular solid harmonic> to a constant multiple of its irregular counterpart.

= Homogeneous harmonic inversion
{parent=Irregular solid harmonic}
{title2=$\Delta(r^{-(2n+1)}H_n)=r^{-(2n+1)}\Delta H_n$}

A degree-$n$ <homogeneous polynomial> satisfies <Euler homogeneous function theorem> $\mathbf r\cdot\nabla H_n=nH_n$. The <product rule> gives $\Delta(r^sH_n)=r^s\Delta H_n+s(2n+s+1)r^{s-2}H_n$ in three dimensions. Taking $s=-(2n+1)$ gives the displayed identity and the equivalence of harmonicity on $r>0$. The origin is excluded; even $1/r$ has a distributional <Dirac delta function> source there.

= Spectrum of the round sphere
{parent=Spherical harmonic}
{title2=$\lambda_n=n(n+d-1)$}

For the positive <Laplace-Beltrami operator> on the unit <sphere> $S^d$, with $d\geq1$, the <eigenvalues> are $n(n+d-1)$ for integers $n\geq0$. Their <eigenspaces> consist of restrictions of degree-$n$ <harmonic polynomials> and have dimensions $\binom{n+d}{d}-\binom{n+d-2}{d}$, with the second term zero for $n<2$. The <Laplacian in polar coordinates> gives the eigenvalue; <Fischer decomposition> and the <Stone-Weierstrass theorem> prove completeness.

= Gaunt coefficient
{c}
{synonym}

The integral of three <spherical harmonics> on the sphere. In the conventional complex basis it is real and equals a product of two <Wigner 3j symbols> and $\sqrt{(2\ell_1+1)(2\ell_2+1)(2\ell_3+1)/(4\pi)}$. It vanishes unless the multipoles satisfy a triangle condition, $\ell_1+\ell_2+\ell_3$ is even, and $m_1+m_2+m_3=0$. It carries the angular geometry of the <reduced CMB bispectrum>.

= Regular solid harmonic
{parent=Spherical harmonic}
{title2=$r^lY_l^m$}

A <regular solid harmonic> is $r^lY_l^m(\theta,\phi)$: it is homogeneous of degree $l$ and solves the <Laplace equation>. Thus $\mathbf r\cdot\nabla F=lF$ and $\nabla^2F=0$. These identities turn angular <spherical harmonic> expansions of displacement, density and <Newtonian gravitational potential> into radial equations.

= Spherical harmonic addition theorem
{parent=Spherical harmonic}
{title2=$P_\ell(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\hat{\mathbf m})=\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}\sum_mY_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf n})Y_{\ell m}^*(\hat{\mathbf m})$}

For orthonormal complex <spherical harmonics>, $P_\ell(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\hat{\mathbf m})=4\pi\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf n})Y_{\ell m}^*(\hat{\mathbf m})/(2\ell+1)$. This converts an angle between two directions into a separable sum. Together with <orthogonality of Legendre polynomials>, it projects rotationally invariant scattering kernels onto angular multipoles.

= Spin weight
{parent=Spherical harmonic}
{title2=$s$}

A spin-weight-$s$ field on an oriented sphere acquires a phase under rotation of its local tangent-frame basis. <Spin-weighted spherical harmonics> form the angular basis for such fields. The polarization combinations $Q\pm iU$ carry opposite spin weights of magnitude two; ordinary scalar functions have spin weight zero.

= Spin-weighted spherical harmonic
{parent=Spin weight}
{title2=${}_sY_{\ell m}$}

The angular basis ${}_sY_{\ell m}$ for a spin-weight-$s$ field generalizes ordinary <spherical harmonics>. It has $\ell\ge|s|$. For scalar-source <CMB polarization> about a wavevector, only $m=0$ survives and ${}_{\pm2}Y_{20}\propto\sin^2\theta$.

= Spin-raising and lowering operators
{parent=Spin-weighted spherical harmonic}
{title2=$\eth,\ \overline{\eth}$}

On a spin-$s$ field $f$, the spin-raising and lowering operations are $\eth f=-\sin^s\theta(\partial_\theta+i\csc\theta\partial_\varphi)(\sin^{-s}\theta f)$ and $\overline{\eth}f=-\sin^{-s}\theta(\partial_\theta-i\csc\theta\partial_\varphi)(\sin^s\theta f)$. The intermediate spin changes after each application. For an axisymmetric scalar $f(\mu)$, either operation applied twice yields $(1-\mu^2)f''(\mu)$.

= Spherical-harmonic streaming recurrence
{parent=Spherical harmonic}
{title2=$\mu Y_{\ell m}=a_{\ell+1,m}Y_{\ell+1,m}+a_{\ell,m}Y_{\ell-1,m}$}

Here $\mu=\cos\theta$ and $a_{\ell,m}=\sqrt{(\ell^2-m^2)/[(2\ell+1)(2\ell-1)]}$. This follows by combining the <associated Legendre function> recurrence $(2\ell+1)\mu P_\ell^m=(\ell-m+1)P_{\ell+1}^m+(\ell+m)P_{\ell-1}^m$ with the normalized <spherical harmonic> constants. It gives the adjacent-multipole couplings in a <photon Boltzmann hierarchy>.

The Rayleigh plane-wave expansion is

=4\pi\sum_{\ell m}i^\ell j_\ell(kr)

= Power law
{parent=Analysis}
{wiki}

A power law is a relation of the form $f(x)=Cx^p$ for constants $C$ and $p$. Under rescaling of its argument it satisfies $f(\lambda x)=\lambda^pf(x)$.

= Cored power-law density profile
{parent=Power law}

A cored power-law density profile is finite at its centre and approaches a <power law> outside a core radius. A simple continuous example is $n(r)=n_0$ for $r\leq r_c$ and $n(r)=n_0(r/r_c)^{-\beta}$ for $r>r_c$. The normalization $n_0$ is the core density. Smoothing the transition changes integrated coefficients but not convergence thresholds fixed by the outer slope.

= Polynomial growth

A function has polynomial growth when $|f(x)|\leq C(1+|x|)^N$ for some constants $C,N$. Such growth is slow enough for a <locally integrable function> to define a <tempered distribution>.

= Continuous exponential functional equation
{parent=Functional equation}
{title2=$g(x+y)=g(x)g(y)$}

A continuous real <function> satisfying $g(x+y)=g(x)g(y)$ is either identically zero or $g(x)=e^{cx}$ for a real constant $c$. If it is nonzero somewhere, $g(0)=1$ and $g(x)g(-x)=1$, so it never vanishes. The identity $g(x)=g(x/2)^2$ then makes it everywhere positive. Its real logarithm is a continuous <additive function>, hence linear by <additive function continuous at one point is linear>. Exponentiating proves the classification, and each listed <function> satisfies the equation.

= Additive function
{parent=Cauchy's functional equation}

An additive function on an <additive semigroup> satisfies $f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)$ whenever $x$, $y$, and $x+y$ belong to its domain.

= Additive function continuous at one point is linear
{parent=Additive function}
{title2=$f(x)=xf(1)$}

A real <additive function> $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ that is <continuous> at one point $x_0$ is <continuous> everywhere and has form $f(x)=xf(1)$. Indeed $f(0)=0$, and $f(h)=f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)\to0$ as $h\to0$. Then $f(x+h)-f(x)=f(h)\to0$ for every $x$. Additivity gives $f(q)=qf(1)$ for every <rational number>; continuity and the <density of the rational numbers> extend this identity to every real argument.

= Measurable additive function is continuous
{parent=Additive function}

Every Lebesgue-measurable additive function $f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$ is continuous. Small-value preimages are measurable; one of their integer dilates has positive measure, so the <Steinhaus theorem> puts a neighbourhood of zero in their difference set. Additivity then makes $f$ small on that neighbourhood.

= Continuous additive function on the nonnegative real numbers
{parent=Additive function}

If $f:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ is <continuous> and is an <additive function>, then $f(t)=tf(1)$. Additivity proves this first for nonnegative <rational numbers>; because $f$ is <continuous>, the identity extends to every nonnegative <real number>.

= Filtered backprojection
{parent=Radon transform}
{title2=$f(x)=(4\pi)^{-1}\int_0^{2\pi}(\partial_\rho H\mathcal Rf)(x\cdot n_\theta,\theta)d\theta$}

The <Radon transform> records line integrals of a planar density. Apply the transverse <Hilbert transform> and a derivative to each projection, then average the filtered values over all lines through the reconstruction point. With $Hh(\rho)=\pi^{-1}\operatorname{PV}\int h(r)/(\rho-r)dr$, the filter has <Fourier multiplier> $|\kappa|$. The <Fourier inversion theorem>, or the jump of the <complexified transport Cauchy kernel>, proves the displayed formula. The factor is $1/(2\pi)$ when directions are integrated over a half-circle. The growing <Fourier multiplier> explains amplification of measurement noise and the need for <regularization of an inverse problem>.

= Attenuated Radon transform
{parent=Radon transform}
{title2=$\mathcal R_\mu f$}

The integral of a source over a directed line, weighted by exponential absorption between each source point and a detector. For line coordinates $x=p\boldsymbol n+s\boldsymbol d$ and a detector at the negative end, it is $\int f(p,s)\exp[-\int_{-\infty}^s\mu(p,r)dr]ds$. The <integrating factor> of a <transport equation> derives the weight. Reversing the detector direction changes the weight for spatially varying attenuation, so the two directed measurements need not coincide. Setting $\mu=0$ recovers the <Radon transform>.

= Spectral reconstruction of an attenuated Radon transform
{parent=Attenuated Radon transform}
{title2=$L_k=k\partial_z+k^{-1}\partial_{\bar z}$}

Complexifying the directional <transport equation> yields spectral solutions analytic inside and outside the unit circle. An <integrating factor> built from known attenuation removes its multiplication term. The limiting Green kernels encode weighted line measurements and transverse <Hilbert transforms>. Their jump gives a <Riemann-Hilbert problem>; its solution recovers the source from an asymptotic coefficient. A general primary treatment of this spectral construction is https://www.stat.uchicago.edu/~guillaumebal/PAPERS/AttRadTh.pdf .

= Projection formula for inverse attenuated Radon transform
{parent=Spectral reconstruction of an attenuated Radon transform}
{title2=$g=(4\pi)^{-1}(\partial_{x_1}-i\partial_{x_2})\int_0^{2\pi}e^{i\theta}J\,d\theta$}

Let $F(\rho,\tau,\theta)=\int_\tau^\infty f(s\boldsymbol d+\rho\boldsymbol n)ds$, $S=\mathcal Rf$, and $A=\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-F}g\,d\tau$ be the directed <attenuated Radon transform>. With the <signed Cauchy boundary operators>, set $J=e^F[e^{P^-S}P^-(e^{-P^-S}A)+e^{-P^+S}P^+(e^{P^+S}A)]$. An <integrating factor> turns the attenuated equation into unattenuated directional transport; its spectral solution has jump $-J$ on the unit circle. A normalized <Cauchy integral formula> and the coefficient at spectral infinity then prove the reconstruction formula. Known attenuation and full directed angular data determine the source; attenuation itself is not reconstructed by this pair.

= Complexified transport Cauchy kernel
{parent=Spectral reconstruction of an attenuated Radon transform}
{title2=$E_k(w)=\operatorname{sgn}(1-|k|^2)\,k/[\pi(w-k^2\bar w)]$}

For $w=x_1+ix_2$ and $0<|k|\ne1$, the operator $D_k=(k+k^{-1})\partial_{x_1}/2+(k-k^{-1})\partial_{x_2}/(2i)$ has the displayed decaying <fundamental solution>. A rotated real-linear coordinate turns it into a multiple of a <Wirtinger derivative>, reducing the kernel identity to $\partial_{\bar z}(1/(\pi z))=\delta$. The kernel is analytic in $k$ separately inside and outside the unit circle, is $O(k)$ at zero and $O(k^{-1})$ at infinity, and develops a jump at the real directional-transport limit.

= Mellin transformation
{c}
{synonym}

The Mellin transform of $f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb C$ is
$$
\mathcal M f(s)=\int_0^\infty f(t)t^{s-1}\,dt
$$
where the integral converges.

= Imaginary-part regularization at a zero Mellin exponent
{parent=Mellin transform}
{title2=$\operatorname{Im}\!\left[-\frac1a\frac1{ia+\log x}\right]=\frac1{a^2+\log^2x}$}

At exponent zero the complex <integral> $\int_0 x^{-1}e^{-tx}/(ia+\log x)\,dx$ fails absolute and ordinary convergence through its real part, proportional to $dx/(x\log x)$. The imaginary-part kernel displayed above instead decays as $dx/(x\log^2x)$ and is integrable. Thus a positive-exponent family can have a well-defined imaginary-part boundary limit even when its full complex limit is invalid. Endpoint expansion coefficients need not be uniform in that limiting exponent: a sine zero can cancel a gamma-function pole and introduce a new nonzero constant term.

= Mellin transform of call prices
{c}
{parent=Mellin transform}

For $C(k)=\mathbb E(e^\xi-e^k)^+$ and $\operatorname{Re}z>1$ in the <exponential moment> domain, the <moment-generating function> satisfies $\mathbb Ee^{z\xi}=\int_{\mathbb R}C(k)z(z-1)e^{(z-1)k}dk$.

= Meromorphic continuation of a Mellin transform from an asymptotic expansion
{parent=Mellin transform}
{title2=$\operatorname{Res}_{s=-\sigma_j}\mathcal Mf(s)=c_j$}

Suppose $f$ decays faster than every power at infinity and, near zero, $f(y)=\sum_{j=1}^r c_jy^{\sigma_j}+O(y^{\sigma_{r+1}})$ for every $r$, with $\sigma_j$ strictly increasing and tending to infinity. Subtracting finitely many terms in the integral over $(0,1)$ gives
$$
\mathcal Mf(s)=\int_1^\infty f(y)y^{s-1}\,dy+\sum_{j=1}^r\frac{c_j}{s+\sigma_j}+\int_0^1\left(f(y)-\sum_{j=1}^r c_jy^{\sigma_j}\right)y^{s-1}\,dy.
$$
The last integral is <holomorphic> on $\operatorname{Re}s>-\sigma_{r+1}$. These expressions continue the <Mellin transform> meromorphically to the whole plane, with simple poles precisely at $-\sigma_j$ for nonzero $c_j$.

= Accumulating asymptotic exponents obstruct Mellin continuation
{parent=Meromorphic continuation of a Mellin transform from an asymptotic expansion}

Strict increase alone does not suffice in the preceding theorem. Let $\sigma_j=1-1/j$, $c_j=2^{-j}$ and $f(y)=\eta(y)\sum_{j\geq1}2^{-j}y^{1-1/j}$, where $\eta$ is continuous, equals one on $(0,1]$ and vanishes on $[2,\infty)$. After division by the next power, every finite remainder extends continuously to zero. Nevertheless the <Mellin transform> has genuine poles at $-\sigma_j$ accumulating at $-1$, so it cannot be a <meromorphic function> on the whole plane.

{c}
{synonym}

Under standard decay and regularity hypotheses,

f(x)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}

= Ramanujan's function
{parent=Laplace transform}
{c}
{title2=$N(t)=\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-tx}}{x(\pi^2+\log^2x)}dx$}

For $t>0$, this <Laplace transform> converges at zero because $dx/[x\log^2x]$ is integrable, and at infinity because of exponential damping. Its large-$t$ behavior is governed by a logarithmic zero-endpoint singularity and begins with $1/\log t$, rather than a power of $t$.

= Logarithmic expansion of Ramanujan's function
{parent=Ramanujan's function}
{title2=$N(t)\sim\sum_{n\ge0}c_n(\log t)^{-n-1},\quad\sum_{n\ge0}c_nz^n/n!=1/\Gamma(1-z)$}

The <Gamma reflection formula> gives $(\sin\pi z/\pi)\Gamma(z)=1/\Gamma(1-z)$, analytic at zero with value one. A finite-parameter <Watson's lemma> representation of <Ramanujan's function> therefore gives the displayed coefficients. In particular $c_0=1$, $c_1=-\gamma$ and $c_2=\gamma^2-\pi^2/6$, with $\gamma$ <Euler's constant>. Obtaining this representation requires taking an imaginary part before sending its Mellin exponent to zero; the original complex <integral> is divergent at that boundary.

= Newman Tauberian theorem
{c}
{parent=Laplace transform}
{title2=$\int_0^\infty f(t)\,dt=F(0)$}

For bounded locally integrable $f$ on the nonnegative real line, suppose its <Laplace transform> $F$ extends holomorphically to an open neighborhood of the closed right half-plane. Then the ordinary improper integral converges to $F(0)$. <Contour damping for bounded Laplace transforms> bounds the difference from the finite-interval transform by $2\|f\|_\infty/R$ in the limit for every radius $R$. Extension merely to the open right half-plane is insufficient, as the constant function and transform $1/z$ show.

= Contour damping for bounded Laplace transforms
{parent=Newman Tauberian theorem}
{title2=$e^{Tz}(1+z^2/R^2)/z$}

The displayed kernel has residue one at zero and vanishes at the endpoints of a radius-$R$ imaginary-axis segment. On either radius-$R$ semicircle, $|1+z^2/R^2|=2|\operatorname{Re}z|/R$, cancelling the inverse real-part bound on a bounded function's <Laplace transform> tail. Use the full transform only on a right semicircle and a thin analytic leftward path; deform the finite-interval entire transform to the large left semicircle. This proves the <Newman Tauberian theorem> without assuming analytic continuation across an entire left half-disk.

= Convolution theorem for Laplace transforms
{parent=Laplace transform}
{title2=$\mathcal L(f*g)=\mathcal Lf\,\mathcal Lg$}

For functions whose <Laplace transforms> converge on a common right half-plane, the causal <convolution> $(f*g)(t)=\int_0^tf(s)g(t-s)ds$ satisfies $\mathcal L(f*g)=\mathcal L(f)\mathcal L(g)$. This converts a transformed boundary forcing into a known time-domain <integral representation>.

= Bromwich inversion formula
{c}
{parent=Laplace transform}

Under suitable regularity and growth conditions, a function with <Laplace transform> $F$ is recovered by the inverse contour integral
$$
f(t)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma-i\infty}^{\gamma+i\infty}e^{st}F(s)\,ds,
$$
where the vertical line lies to the right of every singularity of $F$.

= Causality in finite-time Laplace contour inversion
{parent=Bromwich inversion formula}
{title2=$e^{pt}G_T(p)$}

A <Laplace transform> $G_T(p)=\int_0^Te^{-ps}g(s)ds$ can grow exponentially as $\operatorname{Re}p\to-\infty$. At an evaluation time $t<T$, multiplying by $e^{pt}$ does not automatically permit closing the inverse contour to the left. Either retain the growth-bearing contour pieces, use a causal time convolution, or justify a deformation with the time-truncated transform. Resolvent-pole information alone is insufficient.

= Long-time asymptotics from a Laplace transform
{parent=Laplace transform}

Terms singular at $s=0$ in a <Laplace transform> encode persistent late-time behaviour. In particular,

F(s)=\frac{A}{s^2}+\frac{B}{s}+O(1)
$$
contributes $At+B$ to the inverse transform; singularities farther into the left half-plane contribute decaying terms under the usual analyticity and growth assumptions.

= Hardy-Littlewood maximal function

= Radial decreasing kernel domination by the maximal function
{parent=Hardy-Littlewood maximal function}

If $k(x)=\phi(|x|)\geq0$ is decreasing in radius and has integral one, then $|k_t*f(x)|\leq M f(x)$, where $k_t(x)=t^{-d}k(x/t)$ and $M$ is the centered <Hardy-Littlewood maximal function> of $|f|\,dx$. The <layer-cake representation> expresses $k_t$ as a positive mixture of ball indicators; each corresponding integral is bounded by its ball volume times $Mf(x)$. Integrating these volumes gives exactly one. This applies to the <Poisson kernel for the upper half-space> and supplies a <weak type (1,1)> domination for its boundary convergence.

= Uncentered maximal function of a finite measure
{parent=Hardy-Littlewood maximal function}
{title2=$M_u\mu(x)=\sup_{x\in B}\mu(B)/|B|$}

= Uncentered maximal function
{synonym}

The uncentered maximal function takes the supremum over all open Euclidean balls containing $x$, not only balls centred at $x$. It is an extended nonnegative <lower semicontinuous> function: each strict superlevel set is the union of the balls whose average density exceeds that level. For finite positive measures it satisfies the <uncentered maximal weak-type inequality>.

= One-sided interval averaging operator
{parent=Uncentered maximal function of a finite measure}
{title2=$A_r^+,A_r^-$}

For $r>0$, the forward average is $r^{-1}\int_x^{x+r}f(t)\,dt$ and the backward average is $r^{-1}\int_{x-r}^xf(t)\,dt$, both with positive interval length in the denominator. They are <linear operators>. Their absolute values are bounded by the <uncentered maximal function>: enlarge the interval to put $x$ in its interior and pass to the limit. The same boundary-enlargement argument works regardless of the value of the chosen representative at $x$.

= Uncentered maximal weak-type inequality
{parent=Uncentered maximal function of a finite measure}
{title2=$|\{M_u\mu>\alpha\}|\leq3^d\mu(\mathbb R^d)/\alpha$}

For a finite positive measure on Euclidean space, cover a compact subset of the maximal superlevel set by finitely many high-density balls. <Wiener covering lemma> selects disjoint balls whose triples cover it. Their total measure mass is at most the mass of the original measure. <Inner regularity of Lebesgue measure> yields the displayed bound. Applied to approximation errors, it proves the <Lebesgue differentiation theorem>.

= Localized uncentered maximal weak-type inequality

{parent=Analysis}

= Watson lemma
{c}
{synonym}

Suppose that, as $t\downarrow0$,
$$
f(t)\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nt^{\lambda_n-1},
\qquad
0<\lambda_0<\lambda_1<\cdots,
$$
and that the corresponding Laplace integral has suitable integrability and growth control away from zero. Watson lemma gives the endpoint expansion
$$
\int_0^b e^{-xt}f(t)\,dt
\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n\Gamma(\lambda_n)x^{-\lambda_n}
$$
as $x\to+\infty$. Each finite truncation of the local amplitude expansion produces the corresponding finite truncation and remainder of the integral expansion.

= Parameter-shift remainder bound for a logarithmic Laplace integral
{parent=Watson's lemma}
{title2=$|g(s;t,a,\sigma)|\le\Gamma(s+\sigma)/(a^2t^{s+\sigma})$}

For $g(s)=-(1/a)e^{ias}\int_0^\infty x^{s+\sigma-1}e^{-tx}/(ia+\log x)\,dx$ with positive $a,t,\sigma$ and $s\ge0$, differentiation cancels the logarithmic denominator and gives $g_s=-(1/a)e^{ias}\Gamma(s+\sigma)t^{-s-\sigma}$. Integrating this <derivative> expresses $g(0)$ as a finite parameter-Laplace <integral> plus $g(s)$. The elementary lower bound $|ia+\log x|\ge a$ gives the displayed remainder, smaller than every logarithmic term at fixed $s>0$. <Watson's lemma> then determines all coefficients from the finite <integral>'s endpoint Taylor series.

= Laplace endpoint expansion with a logarithmic denominator
{parent=Watson's lemma}
{c}
{title2=$I_0(x)\sim[x(\log x)^2]^{-1}[1-2\gamma_E/\log x+\cdots]$}

A cut-off endpoint integral with amplitude $(\log u)^{-2}$ has a logarithmic rather than a Taylor endpoint expansion. Set $v=xu$ and expand $(\log x-\log v)^{-2}$ against $e^{-v}dv/x$. The <derivatives of the gamma function> at one supply the logarithmic moments: $\int_0^\infty e^{-v}\log v\,dv=-\gamma_E$ and $\int_0^\infty e^{-v}(\log v)^2dv=\gamma_E^2+\pi^2/6$. Other peaks or singularities must be treated separately before applying this endpoint result to a whole integral.

= Logarithmic endpoint Fourier asymptotics
{parent=Watson's lemma}
{title2=$\int_0^1\log t\,e^{ixt}dt\sim[-\pi/2-i(\log x+\gamma)]/x-e^{ix}\sum_{k\ge0}k!/(ix)^{k+2}$}

For real $x\to+\infty$, deformation into the upper half-plane separates a logarithmically singular endpoint at zero from the smooth endpoint at one. The first contributes the exact logarithmic term displayed. The second yields its factorial <asymptotic expansion> by <Watson's lemma>. The two endpoints must both be retained; repeated <integration by parts> without treating the logarithmic singularity loses its leading contribution.

= Complex Watson lemma

Under the corresponding holomorphic and uniform remainder hypotheses, the expansion in <Watson lemma> remains valid for a complex large parameter $z$, uniformly in every closed subsector

with $0<\delta<\pi/2$. A rotation of an endpoint contour reduces oscillatory factors to this decaying Laplace form.

= Reciprocal-amplitude Laplace integral

Expanding $(1+t)^{-1}$ at the endpoint and applying Watson's lemma gives
$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-xt}}{1+t}\,dt
\sim\sum_{n=0}^\infty(-1)^n n!x^{-n-1}
\qquad(x\to+\infty).
$$

= Stationary phase method

= Stationary phase
{synonym}

Oscillatory integrals are led by points where the phase derivative vanishes, with a phase shift determined by the Hessian signature.

= Cubic stationary endpoint
{parent=Stationary phase method}
{title2=$\int_0^\infty\cos(Na t^3)\,dt\sim\Gamma(1/3)\cos(\pi/6)/[3(Na)^{1/3}]$}

If the first two phase <derivatives> vanish at an endpoint but the third does not, an oscillatory contribution is concentrated on a width proportional to $N^{-1/3}$. For a smooth nonzero endpoint <oscillatory integral amplitude>, rescaling reduces the leading contribution to a cubic oscillatory integral. Its constant follows from the <Gamma function> Fourier integral. This is a degenerate form of the <stationary phase method>, and a quadratic formula is insufficient.

= Bessel turning-point asymptotic
{c}
{parent=Cubic stationary endpoint}
{title2=$J_n(n)\sim 2^{1/3}n^{-1/3}/[3^{2/3}\Gamma(2/3)]$}

At equal argument and large integer order, the <Bessel function of the first kind> has phase $n(\sin t-t)=-nt^3/6+\cdots$ near the endpoint. The <cubic stationary endpoint> yields the displayed positive constant. It is also the zero-argument value of the <Airy function> in the uniform Bessel turning-point approximation.

= Oscillatory integral phase
{parent=Stationary phase method}
{title2=$\beta(q)$}

= Oscillatory phase
{synonym}

In an integral $\int a(q)e^{i\lambda\beta(q)}dq$, the real-valued function $\beta$ is the phase. Its stationary points satisfy $\nabla\beta=0$; its <Hessian matrix> determines the local quadratic oscillation and the signature factor in <stationary phase method>. Degenerate stationary points and boundaries require separate analysis.

= Two-dimensional stationary-phase formula
{parent=Stationary phase method}
{title2=$\frac{2\pi a(q)e^{i\lambda\phi(q)+i\pi\operatorname{sgn}(H)/4}}{\lambda\sqrt{|\det H|}}$}

For a smooth real phase with an isolated nondegenerate interior critical point $q$, smooth amplitude $a$, and no competing boundary contribution, the leading contribution is $2\pi\lambda^{-1}a(q)|\det H|^{-1/2}e^{i\lambda\phi(q)+i\pi\operatorname{sgn}(H)/4}$, where $H$ is the <Hessian matrix>. Orthogonal diagonalization gives two one-dimensional <Fresnel integrals>. A degenerate critical point or a stationary boundary needs its own local analysis.

= Stationary phase endpoint cancellation in a half-strip contour
{parent=Stationary phase method}

For fixed $\nu$, the vertical sides of the half-strip integral of $e^{-ix\sin z+i\nu z}$ contribute $-\sin(\pi\nu)/(\pi x)+O(x^{-2})$. <Integration by parts> on the nonstationary horizontal pieces contributes the opposite endpoint term. After cancellation, the two stationary points give $\sqrt{2/(\pi x)}\cos(x-\nu\pi/2-\pi/4)+O(x^{-3/2})$.

= Nondegenerate stationary point of a phase
{parent=Stationary phase method}

= Nondegenerate stationary points of a phase
{synonym}

A <stationary point> $x_0$ of a <smooth function>[smooth] phase $\phi$ is nondegenerate when its <Hessian matrix> at $x_0$ is an <invertible matrix>. In one dimension, this condition is $\phi''(x_0)\ne0$.

= One-dimensional stationary-phase formula
{parent=Stationary phase method}

If $\psi'(k_0)=0$, $\psi''(k_0)\ne0$, and $A$ is smooth near $k_0$, then
$$
\int A(k)e^{it\psi(k)}\,dk
\sim A(k_0)e^{it\psi(k_0)+i\pi\operatorname{sgn}(\psi''(k_0))/4}
\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{t|\psi''(k_0)|}}.
$$
Contributions from distinct stationary points add.

= Cubic endpoint stationary-phase scaling
{parent=Stationary phase method}

If a phase at an endpoint satisfies $\phi(u)=\phi(0)-cu^3+O(u^5)$ with $c>0$, the leading region has width $O(t^{-1/3})$. Rescaling $u=(ct)^{-1/3}v$ reduces the leading term to a cubic oscillatory integral and produces decay $t^{-1/3}$.

= Stationary-phase asymptotic for the generalized Bessel cosine integral
{title2=$Q_\alpha(x)$}
{parent=Stationary phase method}

For
$$
Q_\alpha(x)=\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi
\cos(x\sin\theta-\alpha\theta)d\theta,
$$
fixed $\alpha$ and $x\to+\infty$, the stationary point near $\pi/2$ gives
$$
Q_\alpha(x)\sim\sqrt{\frac2{\pi x}}
\cos\left(x-\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}-\frac\pi4\right).
$$
When $\alpha=x$, the stationary point coalesces cubically with the endpoint at zero and the leading order changes to $x^{-1/3}$.

= Nonstationary oscillatory integral
{parent=Stationary phase method}

If the phase derivative has no zero on the support of a smooth amplitude, repeated <integration by parts> makes the oscillatory integral decay faster than any prescribed inverse power, subject to corresponding decay and regularity of the amplitude.

= WKB approximation

= Liouville–Green approximation

{synonym}

For a large-parameter second-order ODE, WKB separates a rapidly varying exponential phase from a slowly varying transport amplitude.

= Localized forcing determines WKB phase constants
{parent=WKB approximation}
{title2=$u=u_p+u_h$}

For a slowly varying oscillator with rapidly decaying forcing, first construct the localized particular solution $u_p$. Apply the <initial conditions> to the residual $u_h$ to determine its <WKB approximation> amplitude and phase constants. Ignoring the early forcing because it later becomes small loses the permanent free oscillations it excites. In $u''+4e^{-2\varepsilon t}u=8e^{-2t}$, the particular expansion is $u_p=e^{-2t}[1+\varepsilon(t+1/2)]+O(\varepsilon^2)$, whose initial value shifts the first homogeneous cosine coefficient by $-\varepsilon/2$.

= Slowly varying triangular-jet envelope
{parent=WKB approximation}
{title2=$B\propto a^{-q/(1-2q)},\quad q=(1-\sqrt{1-4\Omega})/2$}

For $A_t=-a(\epsilon x)A_x-ia(\epsilon x)^2A_{xx}$, a downstream <WKB approximation> at fixed frequency $\Omega<1/4$ has local <wavenumber> $k=q/a$. The next-order <amplitude> equation is $(a-2a^2k)B_X=a^2k_XB$, giving the stated envelope. For small $\Omega$, it reduces to $B\propto a^{-\Omega}$ to first order in frequency. The approximation requires positive slowly varying width and separation from the double root at $\Omega=1/4$.

= WKB correction term
{c}
{parent=WKB approximation}
{title2=$F=f\prime\prime/(4f^2)-5(f\prime)^2/(16f^3)$}

The <Liouville transformation of a second-order equation> with $t=\int^x\sqrt f\,dx$ gives $\ddot U-(1+F)U=0$. Neglecting this correction yields amplitude $f^{-1/4}$ and phase or exponent $\int\sqrt f$. Its smallness measures a local slowly-varying-coefficient approximation, away from zeros of $f$.

= WKB approximation for a slowly varying oscillator
{parent=WKB approximation}

For $\epsilon^2y_{XX}+k^2(X)y=0$ with nonzero slowly varying $k$,
$$
y\sim k(X)^{-1/2}\left[
C_+e^{i\epsilon^{-1}\int^Xk(s)ds}
+C_-e^{-i\epsilon^{-1}\int^Xk(s)ds}
\right].
$$
It fails near turning points and where $\epsilon|k'|/|k|^2$ is not small.

= WKB phase correction for a slowly varying oscillator
{c}
{parent=WKB approximation for a slowly varying oscillator}
{title2=$\sigma_1=3(K')^2/(8K^3)-K''/(4K^2)$}

For smooth $K=|k|>0$ and fixed amplitude flux, use $\sigma=K+\epsilon^2\sigma_1+\cdots$ and $A_0\propto K^{-1/2}$. The <exact amplitude-phase equation> gives $\sigma_1=A_0''/(2KA_0)$, which is the displayed formula. The first phase correction is order $\epsilon^2$ in the local wavenumber, hence order $\epsilon$ in its integrated exponential phase.

= Bessel transition for an exponentially decaying oscillator
{parent=WKB approximation for a slowly varying oscillator}
{c}
{title2=$T=\varepsilon t-\log(\varepsilon^{-1})$}

An oscillator with frequency $\omega(t)=e^{-\varepsilon t}$ leaves the <WKB approximation> when $|\omega_t|/\omega^2=\varepsilon e^{\varepsilon t}$ becomes order one. The shifted variable $T=\varepsilon t-\log(\varepsilon^{-1})$ changes its leading undamped equation to $Y_{TT}+e^{-2T}Y=0$. With $Z=e^{-T}$ this is the order-zero <Bessel differential equation>. Large-$Z$ <Bessel functions> match the earlier oscillations, while the small-$Z$ constant and logarithmic solutions become a constant and a linear function of late time.

= Exact late slope of an exponentially damped oscillator
{parent=Bessel transition for an exponentially decaying oscillator}
{title2=$\lim_{t\to\infty}y_t=e^{-1/2}M(-1/(2\varepsilon^2),1,1/2)$}

For $y_{tt}+e^{-2\varepsilon t}(\varepsilon y_t+y)=0$, $y(0)=0$, $y_t(0)=1$, set $r=e^{-2\varepsilon t}/2$. The exact equation becomes $ry_{rr}+(1-r)y_r+y/(2\varepsilon^2)=0$. Let $m(t)=M(-1/(2\varepsilon^2),1,r)$, using the <Kummer function>. It is regular at $r=0$ and tends to one. The <Wronskian> $W=my_t-m_ty$ obeys $W_t=-\varepsilon e^{-2\varepsilon t}W$, with $W(0)=M(-1/(2\varepsilon^2),1,1/2)$. Integration gives the displayed exact late slope. The other local solution grows at most logarithmically in $r$, so $m_ty\to0$ and $W\to y_t$. This provides a check on a <WKB approximation> even near phases where its leading predicted slope vanishes.

= WKB amplitude in a slowly varying duct
{parent=WKB approximation}

For a separated duct mode with local width $h(X)$ and nonzero axial wavenumber $k(X)$, conservation of modal flux gives amplitude proportional to $[h(X)k(X)]^{-1/2}$. The phase is $\epsilon^{-1}\int^Xk(\xi)d\xi$.

= Variable-sound-speed WKB duct amplitude
{parent=WKB amplitude in a slowly varying duct}
{title2=$A=Ck_0/\sqrt{kN},\quad N=\int_{-R}^{R}F^2dy$}

For $\nabla\cdot(k_0(X)^{-2}\nabla p)+p=0$ in a slowly varying rigid duct, a real local transverse <normal mode> $F$ with squared norm $N(X)$ has longitudinal <wavenumber> $k(X)$. The <WKB approximation> is $p=A(X)F(y,X)\exp[-i\epsilon^{-1}\int^Xk(\xi)d\xi]$. The moving-wall <Neumann boundary condition> and next-order solvability give $2kN k_0^{-2}A_X+(kN k_0^{-2})_X A=0$, hence the displayed amplitude. For $F=\cos(n\pi y/R)$, $N=R$ for $n\ge1$ and $N=2R$ for $n=0$. The factor $k_0$ is essential when the coefficient in the divergence-form equation varies. This approximation requires a mode separated from cutoff and from other modes.

= Airy turning-point connection formula
{parent=WKB approximation}
{c}

Near a simple turning point, rescaling the independent variable reduces a second-order equation to the Airy equation. The decaying Airy solution on the forbidden side matches an oscillatory solution with phase shift $\pi/4$ on the allowed side.

For a one-dimensional energy $E$ and potential $V(x)$, a classical turning point satisfies $V(x)=E$. The classically allowed region has $E>V(x)$ and supports an oscillatory leading-order WKB solution.

For an oscillatory interval terminated by a hard boundary and a simple turning point, matching the boundary condition to the decaying solution gives an action integral equal to an integer multiple of $\pi$ with the turning-point phase correction.

= Two-term Dirichlet WKB quantization
{parent=WKB quantization condition}
{title2=$\epsilon^{-1}\int_a^b K\,dx+\epsilon\int_a^b\sigma_1\,dx=n\pi+O(\epsilon^3)$}

For smooth positive $K$ on an interval with regular Dirichlet <boundary conditions>, there is no turning-point phase shift. Combining conjugate <WKB approximation> branches to vanish at the first endpoint makes the total phase at the second an integer multiple of $\pi$. The first correction is the integrated <WKB phase correction for a slowly varying oscillator>. For $K=x+1$ on $[0,1]$ the resulting <eigenvalues> are $4\pi^2n^2/9-3/16+O(n^{-2})$.

=\left(n+\frac12\right)\pi,
\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.
$$

The semiclassical action integral

f(E)=\int_{a(E)}^{b(E)}\sqrt{E-V(x)}\,dx

For the even potential

Three distinct particular solutions $g,h,j$ of a <Riccati equation> determine every solution by one constant <cross-ratio>. Indeed, for $f'=af^2+bf+c$, differences satisfy $(f-g)'=(a(f+g)+b)(f-g)$. = Linearization of a Riccati equation

The <logarithmic derivative> is defined on intervals where $x\ne0$, and multiplying $x$ by a nonzero scalar does not change $y$. For variable $c(t)\ne0$, the derivative coefficient becomes $a-c'/c$.

= Poles of a Riccati solution from zeros of its linearizing solution

Under the <linearization of a Riccati equation>, such a zero $t_*$ produces $y=x'/(cx)\sim1/[c(t-t_*)]$. Zeros separate the intervals on which the <Riccati equation> solution is finite.

= Uniform asymptotic approximation
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}

An approximation is uniform on a specified domain when its error bound holds throughout that domain as the small parameter tends to zero. In a <matched asymptotic expansion>, adding the inner and outer approximations and subtracting their common overlap produces a composite approximation. Checking only a fixed point away from a <boundary layer> does not establish uniformity.

= Lorentzian peak approaching an integration endpoint
{c}
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}
{title2=$\int_0^1[(\lambda x+1-\lambda)^2+\epsilon^2c(x)^2]^{-1}\,dx$}

When $c(0)>0$ and $\lambda-1=O(\epsilon)$, the change $x=\epsilon X$ resolves a truncated <Cauchy distribution>. Its leading contribution is $[\epsilon\lambda c(0)]^{-1}[\pi/2+\arctan((\lambda-1)/(\epsilon c(0)))]$. It matches the nonresonant endpoint integral on one side and the full interior peak on the other. An interior peak at $x_*$ has width $\epsilon c(x_*)/\lambda$; proximity to an endpoint must be measured against this width, not against $\epsilon$ alone.

= Asymptotic power series
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}
{title2=$f(x)-\sum_{k=0}^Nc_k(x-a)^k=o(|x-a|^N)$}

The displayed condition for every fixed $N$ defines an <asymptotic expansion> in powers about $a$ along a specified domain. It need not converge away from $a$. The coefficients are uniquely recovered by successive limits after subtracting all lower-order powers. A convergent <power series> is asymptotic because each fixed finite truncation has a higher-order tail, but a factorially growing formal series can be asymptotic while having zero radius of convergence.

= Compactified approximation from endpoint asymptotics
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}

Suppose a continuous function $F(\lambda)$ on $[0,\infty)$ has a known power-law tail $F(\lambda)\sim a\lambda^{-p}$. Put $u=\lambda/(1+\lambda)$ and $h(u)=(1+\lambda)^pF(\lambda)$. When $h$ extends continuously to $u=1$, <polynomial approximation> on $[0,1]$ gives a global approximation $F(\lambda)\approx(1+\lambda)^{-p}h_N(u)$ using only elementary operations. <Taylor series> at zero and the tail <asymptotic expansion> can constrain endpoint coefficients. Agreement at the two endpoints alone does not guarantee accuracy in the intervening interval; uniform error control or intermediate calibration is needed.

= Perturbation theory
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}
{title2=$u(\epsilon)\sim u_0+\epsilon u_1+\cdots$}

Perturbation theory develops approximate solutions in a small parameter around a simpler reference problem. A power-series <asymptotic expansion> gives equations for successive correction coefficients; a <matched asymptotic expansion> handles cases where one expansion is not uniformly valid.

= Perturbation
{parent=Perturbation theory}
{title2=$F_\varepsilon=F_0+\varepsilon G+o(\varepsilon)$}

A perturbation is a small change in a model, operator, state or parameter. It can be organized as a family $F_\varepsilon=F_0+\varepsilon G+o(\varepsilon)$ in a specified norm. If $F_\varepsilon(z_\varepsilon)=0$ and $DF_0(z_0)$ is invertible, differentiating at zero gives $z'_0=-[DF_0(z_0)]^{-1}G(z_0)$. The <implicit function theorem> justifies this continued solution locally. Singular derivatives at a bifurcation instead require an unfolding or reduced equation; smallness of the change alone does not preserve a degenerate bifurcation.

= Integer-frequency endpoint expansion of cosine integrals
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}
{title2=$a_k=(-1)^{k-1}[h^{(2k-1)}(2\pi)-h^{(2k-1)}(0)]$}

For smooth $h$ on $[0,2\pi]$ and positive integer $n$, repeatedly integrating $\int h(t)\cos(nt)\,dt$ twice eliminates the sine endpoint terms and yields only even inverse powers of $n$. The exact remainder after $M$ pairs is $(-1)^Mn^{-2M}\int h^{(2M)}\cos(nt)$, which is $o(n^{-2M})$ by the <Riemann-Lebesgue lemma>. Without integer frequencies an $n^{-1}$ sine endpoint term generally remains.

= Dominant balance
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}

Rescale variables so that the terms responsible for the leading behaviour have comparable size. The resulting <distinguished limit> exposes a governing equation that may differ from the naive zero-parameter equation. Examples are <dominant balance for algebraic roots> and the derivative balance in a <square-root boundary layer at a vanishing drift>.

= Regular perturbation
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}

A regular perturbation admits an <asymptotic expansion> about the limiting problem without changing its relevant scale or losing solutions or conditions. A <regular perturbation of a simple algebraic root> follows from the <implicit function theorem>. A full problem can still be a <singular perturbation> even when some individual solution branches are regular.

= Large roots near tangent poles
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}
{title2=$x_n\sim(n+\tfrac12)\pi-\big[\mu(n+\tfrac12)\pi\big]^{-1}$}

For fixed real $\mu\ne0$, roots of $\tan x=\mu x$ tending to positive infinity approach tangent <poles> $a_n=(n+1/2)\pi$. Expanding $-\cot(x-a_n)$ gives
$$
x_n=a_n-\frac1{\mu a_n}+\frac{1/(3\mu^3)-1/\mu^2}{a_n^3}+O(a_n^{-5}).
$$
The expansion works for either sign of $\mu$ when $|\mu|a_n\gg1$, but is nonuniform at $\mu=0$, where the roots lie at $n\pi$ instead. The <pole> labeling need not equal the ordered root index at small $n$.

= Uniform error bound for nearby decaying exponentials
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}
{title2=$\sup_{x\geq0}|e^{-ax}-e^{-bx}|\leq |a-b|/[e\min(a,b)]$}

For positive rates $a,b$, the <mean value theorem> in the rate gives a bound by $|a-b|x e^{-\min(a,b)x}$. Its maximum is $|a-b|/[e\min(a,b)]$. Combining this estimate with coefficient errors bounds a finite spectral sum uniformly on a half-line, including slow scales much longer than one. Positivity of both rates is essential: unstable exponentials need a different time-window or relative-error analysis.

= Borel summation
{c}
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}
{title2=$\int_0^\infty e^{-zt}\mathcal B(t)\,dt$}

For a formal inverse-power series $\sum_{n\geq0}a_nz^{-n-1}$, divide its coefficients by $n!$ to form a Borel transform $\sum a_nt^n/n!$ and integrate it against $e^{-zt}$. Singularities on the integration ray require a specified contour prescription. Different lateral contours can produce exponentially small differences and the <Stokes phenomenon>.

= Borel transform of a factorially divergent series
{c}
{parent=Borel summation}
{title2=$\mathcal B(t)=\sum_{r\ge1}a_rt^{r-1}/\Gamma(r)$}

= Borel transform
{c}
{synonym}

Removing factorial growth from the coefficients of a formal inverse-power series produces its Borel transform. When continuation and exponential growth bounds allow it, $\int_{\mathcal C}e^{-zt}\mathcal B(t)\,dt$ reconstructs the series by <Laplace transform>. A singularity on the integration direction requires a lateral contour prescription. A <simple pole> contributes a <residue> when that prescription is changed, explaining how <Stokes phenomenon> arises from otherwise identical algebraic expansions.

= Borel integral with a simple pole
{parent=Borel summation}

If a <Borel summation> contour meets a simple pole, an upper or lower indentation fixes the exponentially small contribution. For the transform $(1-t^2)^{-1}$ on the positive ray, the relevant pole is $t=1$. A lower indentation gives the <Cauchy principal value> minus $i\pi e^{-z}/2$. Finite geometric expansion gives an exact remainder integral, which enables <optimal truncation> and <error-function smoothing of a Stokes multiplier>.

= Optimal truncation
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}

A divergent <asymptotic expansion> should usually be stopped near its smallest term rather than summed indefinitely. For factorial terms $n!z^{-n}$ the cutoff has $n=|z|+O(1)$. This exposes exponentially small contributions and the <Stokes phenomenon>. The smallest term alone does not always bound the remainder, especially when a saddle meets a pole.

= Dominant balance for algebraic roots
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}
{title2=$t=\epsilon^p s$}

For a polynomial depending on a small parameter, substitute $t=\epsilon^p s$ and compare the exponents of the parameter. Candidate root scales arise when two or more lowest exponents balance. Solve the leading scaled equation and expand about each of its nonzero roots. Different balances can reveal different families of roots; real-root counts must be checked separately.

= Divergent algebraic root in a singular perturbation
{parent=Dominant balance for algebraic roots}
{title2=$\lambda\sim\varepsilon^{-p}\mu$}

= Divergent perturbation root
{synonym}

A polynomial whose degree decreases when its parameter vanishes may have roots escaping to infinity. Such a <singular perturbation> root is found by balancing powers after rescaling $\lambda=\varepsilon^{-p}\mu$. If the root enters an exponential, even a lower-order additive root correction can matter on a fixed interval when its leading exponential grows. This phenomenon is different from a repeated root of a fixed polynomial.

= Regular perturbation of a simple algebraic root
{parent=Dominant balance for algebraic roots}
{title2=$\lambda(\varepsilon)=\lambda_0+\lambda_1\varepsilon+\cdots$}

= Regular perturbation root
{synonym}

If $P(\lambda_0,0)=0$ and $P_\lambda(\lambda_0,0)\ne0$, the <implicit function theorem> gives a local root branch. For analytic coefficients it has an ordinary power series in the perturbation parameter. Its corrections follow by substituting that series into the polynomial and comparing coefficients. A vanishing leading root may require its first nonzero correction to capture a slow exponential mode.

= Endpoint transition of a regularized interior pole
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}
{title2=$I\sim[\pi/2+\arctan(d/(\eta L_0))]/(a\eta L_0)$}

Suppose $a,b,L_0>0$, $L(x)$ is smooth and positive near zero with $L(0)=L_0$, and $d=O(\eta)$ as $\eta\to0^+$. The leading contribution to
$$
I=\int_0^b\frac{dx}{(ax-d)^2+\eta^2L(x)^2}
$$
is obtained by scaling $x=\eta s$ and replacing $L$ by $L_0$. Integration of the resulting reciprocal quadratic gives the displayed formula. For constant $L$ the exact expression is
$$
I=\frac1{a\eta L_0}\left[\arctan\frac{ab-d}{\eta L_0}+\arctan\frac d{\eta L_0}\right].
$$
This proves the half-peak value at $d=0$ and the transition between an interior peak and a pole outside the interval. If $L$ varies, smoothness and positivity on the contributing neighborhood justify its leading replacement; other zeros or singular contributions elsewhere must be excluded.

= Lorentzian contribution from shrinking regularization
{parent=Endpoint transition of a regularized interior pole}
{c}
{title2=$\int a\,[h(u)^2+a^2]^{-1}du\to\pi/|h'(u_0)|$}

If $h$ has a simple interior zero $u_0$ and $a\to0^+$, the <inner variable> $v=h'(u_0)(u-u_0)/a$ turns the local integral into $(1/|h'(u_0)|)\int_{\mathbb R}(1+v^2)^{-1}dv$. Thus a peak with vanishing width can retain finite area. Slowly varying positive regularization factors cancel between peak height and width when the numerator has the matching factor. An ordinary <outer expansion> can miss this contribution even though the peak is exponentially narrow.

= Stokes phenomenon
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}
{c}
{wiki}

Exponentially small contributions can change their asymptotic representation when a complex parameter crosses a Stokes direction. An all-orders <asymptotic expansion> in inverse powers alone does not resolve these terms.

= Sectorial fundamental solution
{parent=Stokes phenomenon}

At an unramified <irregular singular point> with distinct leading <eigenvalues>, a sectorial fundamental solution is a <fundamental matrix> whose entries are <holomorphic functions> asymptotic to a prescribed formal <matrix> on a sector of the <logarithm> cover. For competing cubic exponentials, the usual construction uses six overlapping sectors with basic angle $\pi/3$. A <formal power series> need not converge, and two sectorial realizations may differ by exponentially small terms across a sector boundary.

= Stokes matrix
{parent=Stokes phenomenon}
{c}
{title2=$Y_{j+1}=Y_jS_j$}

Two <sectorial fundamental solutions> of the same linear system differ on their overlap by a constant invertible right factor, since $(Y_j^{-1}Y_{j+1})'=0$. With a common formal normalization these factors encode the exponentially small ambiguities invisible to the <asymptotic expansion>. They are called <Stokes matrices>. Equivalently their inverses act on column vectors of solution coefficients; the convention must be specified when ordering products.

= Cubic exponential integral Stokes sectors
{parent=Stokes phenomenon}
{title2=$G(z)=\int_0^1e^{-z^3t^3}dt$}

The saddle and endpoint <asymptotic expansions> of this <entire function> have algebraic coefficients $C\omega^j/z$ in sectors $(2j-1)\pi/3<\arg z<(2j+1)\pi/3$, where $C=\Gamma(1/3)/3$ and $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$. Deforming the contour onto the decay ray $\arg(zt)=2j\pi/3$ gives its complete integral $C\omega^j$. The endpoint contribution is $-e^{-z^3}(3z^3)^{-1}\sum_{r\geq0}(-1)^r(2/3)_r/z^{3r}$. The algebraic coefficient switches at $\arg z=\pi/3,\pi,5\pi/3$; the equal-exponential-magnitude <anti-Stokes lines> are the intervening rays $\pi/6+j\pi/3$.

= Borel remainder for a cubic exponential integral
{parent=Cubic exponential integral Stokes sectors}
{c}
{title2=$R_N=\frac C z+\frac{e^{i\pi/3}}{3\Gamma(2/3)z}I(-z^3,N+2/3)$}

Near $\arg z=\pi/3$, set $\lambda=-z^3$ on the branch with argument near zero and truncate the endpoint expansion at $N$ terms. Its remaining factorial series, indexed by $r=N+p$, is $\lambda^{-1/3}/[3\Gamma(2/3)]$ times the <Borel summation> of $\sum_{p\geq0}\Gamma(N+2/3+p)e^\lambda/\lambda^{N+2/3+p}$. The upper-pole contour fixes the stated remainder and its continuation from the sector below the switching line. With $N+2/3\sim|z|^3$, the <Gaussian pole transition for a large gamma parameter> smooths the algebraic coefficient from $C$ to $Ce^{2\pi i/3}$.

= Gaussian Cauchy transform and its Stokes jump
{parent=Stokes phenomenon}
{title2=$I(z)=i\pi e^{-z^2}\operatorname{erfc}(iz)$}

The integral $\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-t^2}(z-t)^{-1}dt$ in the lower half-plane extends to the displayed entire function. In the upper half-plane the continued function differs from the directly evaluated real-contour integral by $2\pi i e^{-z^2}$. Its algebraic expansion is $\sqrt\pi z^{-1}\sum_{m\geq0}(2m-1)!!/(2z^2)^m$. The real axes are switching lines for the exponentially small term under the Stokes-multiplier convention; the diagonal rays $\arg z=\pi/4+j\pi/2$ are equal-magnitude boundaries for the exponential and algebraic mechanisms. Stating both the residue jump and the sector domains prevents confusion between switching and dominance conventions.

= Gaussian saddle coalescing with a simple pole
{parent=Stokes phenomenon}
{title2=$t=1+u/\sqrt n$}

For $z=2n+i\mu\sqrt n+O(1)$, the factor $t^{2n}e^{z(1-t)}$ has saddle scale $t=1+u/\sqrt n$ and becomes $e^{-u^2-i\mu u}$. If the amplitude has a simple pole there, its leading local integral contains $e^{-u^2-i\mu u}/u$. Differentiating its <Cauchy principal value> with respect to $\mu$ reduces it to a <Gaussian integral>, producing an <error function>. The nonsingular amplitude $(1+t)^{-1}$ gives $\frac12\sqrt{\pi/n}e^{-\mu^2/4}$.

= Error-function smoothing of a Stokes multiplier
{parent=Gaussian saddle coalescing with a simple pole}
{title2=$\operatorname{erfc}(\mu/2)$}

The pole contour in a <Gaussian saddle coalescing with a simple pole> changes the multiplier smoothly. For the lower contour, $\int_{\mathcal C_-}e^{-u^2-i\mu u}\,du/u=i\pi\operatorname{erfc}(\mu/2)$. This follows by integrating $-i\sqrt\pi e^{-\mu^2/4}$ and adding the indentation contribution $i\pi$. The transition width is inverse square root of the large parameter; on a <Stokes line> the multiplier takes half its switched-on value.

= Gaussian pole transition for a large gamma parameter
{parent=Error-function smoothing of a Stokes multiplier}
{c}
{title2=$I(\lambda,\gamma)\sim i\pi[1+\operatorname{erf}(\mu/\sqrt2)]$}

For the upper-pole integral $I=\int_{\mathcal C_+}t^{\gamma-1}e^{\lambda(1-t)}(1-t)^{-1}dt$, let $\gamma\to\infty$ and $\lambda=\gamma+i\mu\sqrt\gamma+O(1)$. Scaling $t=1+s/\sqrt\gamma$ gives $-\int_{\mathcal C_+}e^{-s^2/2-i\mu s}ds/s$ at leading order. At $\mu=0$ its indentation is $i\pi$. Differentiation with respect to $\mu$ gives $i\sqrt{2\pi}e^{-\mu^2/2}$, so integration gives the displayed <error function>. This is an <optimal truncation> transition; the underlying analytic integral has no literal discontinuity. The general mechanism is discussed in https://dlmf.nist.gov/2.11#iv[the DLMF account of Stokes smoothing].

= anti-Stokes line
{c}
{parent=Stokes phenomenon}
{title2=$\operatorname{Re}(S_1-S_2)=0$}

With the equal-magnitude convention, an anti-Stokes line has $\operatorname{Re}(S_1-S_2)=0$ for competing exponentials. For $e^{\pm x^2/\varepsilon}$, its rays are $\arg x=\pi/4+m\pi/2$. Crossing one interchanges which exponential grows. Some literature interchanges the names, so the action-based convention should always be specified.

= Stokes line
{c}
{parent=Stokes phenomenon}
{title2=$\operatorname{Im}(S_1-S_2)=0$}

With the convention used here, a Stokes line is a ray where competing exponential actions have the same imaginary part. A subdominant exponential can acquire a rapidly changing multiplier there. For $e^{\pm x^2/\varepsilon}$, these rays have $\arg x=m\pi/2$. The <anti-Stokes lines> instead have equal exponential magnitudes.

= Series reversion
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}
{wiki}

Series reversion constructs the local inverse of a power series. Substituting an ansatz for the inverse and matching successive powers determines its coefficients recursively.

= Divide-and-conquer asymptotic expansion
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}

The divide-and-conquer method splits an integral at an intermediate scale, expands each part in the regime where its approximation is uniform, and cancels the artificial splitting parameter after the pieces are recombined.

= Asymptotic sequence
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}

Functions $(\phi_n)$ form an asymptotic sequence at $x_0$ when $\phi_{n+1}=o(\phi_n)$. The notation
$$
f\sim\sum_{n\geq0}a_n\phi_n
$$
means that for every $N\geq0$,
$$
f-\sum_{n=0}^Na_n\phi_n=o(\phi_N).
$$
Successive division of the remainder by $\phi_N$ uniquely recovers every coefficient.

= Harmonic-mean refinement of an asymptotic sequence
{parent=Asymptotic sequence}

For a positive asymptotic sequence $(\phi_n)$, define $\psi_0=\phi_0$ and
$$
\psi_n=\frac{\phi_{n-1}\phi_n}{\phi_{n-1}+\phi_n}.
$$
Then $\psi_n\sim\phi_n$ term by term and $(\psi_n)$ is again an asymptotic sequence.

= Geometric-mean refinement of an asymptotic sequence
{parent=Asymptotic sequence}

For $\chi_0=\phi_0$ and $\chi_n=\sqrt{\phi_{n-1}\phi_n}$, the sequence $(\chi_n)$ is asymptotic, but $\chi_n/\phi_n\to\infty$ for every $n\geq1$.

= Termwise equivalent asymptotic scales need not preserve expansions
{parent=Asymptotic sequence}

Even if $\phi_n\sim\psi_n$ for every $n$, an expansion in the scale $(\phi_n)$ need not induce one in $(\psi_n)$. A perturbation of $\psi_0$ that is smaller than $\phi_0$ but larger than $\phi_1$ supplies a counterexample.

= Laplace's method
{c}
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}
{wiki}

= Laplace method
{c}
{synonym}

If a smooth real phase $\phi$ has a unique nondegenerate interior maximum at $t_0$, then
$$
\int a(t)e^{x\phi(t)}dt
\sim a(t_0)e^{x\phi(t_0)}
\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{x|\phi''(t_0)|}}.
$$
For a maximum at an endpoint $b$ with $\phi'(b)>0$, the leading factor is $a(b)e^{x\phi(b)}/(x\phi'(b))$.

= Mixed cubic and quadratic Laplace endpoints
{parent=Laplace's method}
{title2=$I(\lambda)=A\lambda^{-1/3}+B\lambda^{-1/2}+O(\lambda^{-1})$}

If a nonnegative smooth phase has an isolated cubic zero at one endpoint and an isolated quadratic zero at the other, their leading <Laplace's method> contributions scale respectively as $\lambda^{-1/3}$ and $\lambda^{-1/2}$. The first two global terms therefore come from different endpoints, rather than from the first two terms at the dominant endpoint. When the cubic endpoint has no quartic phase term and constant amplitude, its next local term starts at $\lambda^{-1}$; cubic corrections at the quadratic endpoint also start at that order.

= Laplace approximation
{parent=Laplace's method}
{c}
{title2=$\int e^{nh(\theta)}a(\theta)d\theta\sim e^{nh(\widehat\theta)}a(\widehat\theta)(2\pi/n)^{p/2}|-h''(\widehat\theta)|^{-1/2}$}

At a unique interior maximum with positive-definite negative <Hessian matrix>, a quadratic expansion and rescaling by $\sqrt n$ give the displayed Gaussian integral, provided the tails are negligible. In a regular <Bayesian inference> it approximates the integrated <likelihood> by the <likelihood> at its dominant point times the prior density and inverse square root of local curvature. Boundary modes, singular Hessians, and multiple equally dominant modes need modified formulas.

= Localization condition for Laplace's method
{parent=Laplace's method}

A unique nondegenerate interior minimum does not by itself ensure that a integral treated by <Laplace's method> is localized there. A sufficient condition is a positive gap above the minimum outside every sufficiently small fixed neighbourhood, together with finiteness of the integral for some positive exponent. The exterior contribution is then exponentially negligible. Without localization, $g(y)=y^2(1-y)^4$ on $(-1,1)$ has its unique interior minimum at zero with $g''(0)=2$, but its boundary contribution is at least $e^{-1}n^{-1/4}$ and dominates the nominal $n^{-1/2}$ interior Gaussian contribution.

= Two-term monotone-endpoint Laplace expansion
{parent=Laplace's method}
{title2=$e^{\lambda\phi(a)}[-g(a)/(\lambda\phi'(a))+(g'(a)/\phi'(a)^2-g(a)\phi''(a)/\phi'(a)^3)/\lambda^2]$}

For a smooth real phase decreasing away from its endpoint maximum, put $t=\phi(a)-\phi(x)$ and write the transformed amplitude as $G(t)=-g(x(t))/\phi'(x(t))$. Its first two Taylor coefficients are $G(0)=-g(a)/\phi'(a)$ and $G'(0)=g'(a)/\phi'(a)^2-g(a)\phi''(a)/\phi'(a)^3$. Applying <Watson lemma> gives the displayed two-term <Laplace integral> expansion. Localization and a Taylor remainder estimate justify termwise integration.

= Endpoint asymptotics after odd Gaussian cancellation
{parent=Laplace's method}

If an odd amplitude multiplies an even Gaussian phase on an interval asymmetric about its interior minimum, the symmetric central integral cancels exactly. The surviving leading term can be exponentially small and comes from the nearer unmatched endpoint. For example,
$$
\int_{-2}^1\sin t\,e^{-\lambda t^2}\,dt
=-\int_1^2\sin t\,e^{-\lambda t^2}\,dt
\sim-\frac{\sin1}{2\lambda}e^{-\lambda}.
$$
An interior <Laplace method> expansion alone yields zero to every algebraic order and does not capture this term.

= Laplace endpoint estimate
{c}
{parent=Laplace's method}
{title2=$\frac{a(b)e^{\lambda\phi(b)}}{\lambda\phi\prime(b)}$}

If a real smooth phase has its strict maximum at the upper endpoint $b$ and $\phi\prime(b)>0$, then $\int_a^b a(t)e^{\lambda\phi(t)}dt\sim a(b)e^{\lambda\phi(b)}/(\lambda\phi\prime(b))$. The endpoint coordinate $b-t$ has scale $1/\lambda$. At a nondegenerate stationary endpoint the scale is instead $\lambda^{-1/2}$ and the leading contribution is half a <Gaussian integral>.

= Laplace integral
{parent=Laplace's method}

A Laplace integral contains a large parameter in an exponential weight, typically in the form $\int a(t)e^{x\phi(t)}\,dt$ or $\int_0^b e^{-xt}f(t)\,dt$. Its asymptotic behavior is controlled by the points where the real part of the phase is largest.

= Laplace integral solution
{parent=Laplace integral}

A linear differential-equation solution may be sought as $\int_Ce^{xt}f(t)dt$. Integration by parts transfers the factor $x$ to <derivatives> of $f$, giving an equation for the amplitude and boundary terms. Admissible contours must cancel those terms and make the integral converge.

= Laplace's method at a nondegenerate stationary endpoint
{c}
{parent=Laplace's method}

If the maximum $t_0$ of the smooth real phase $\phi$ is an endpoint, $\phi'(t_0)=0$, and $\phi''(t_0)<0$, the local Gaussian is integrated over only one side of its peak. Consequently
$$
\int_{t_0}^{b}a(t)e^{x\phi(t)}\,dt
\sim\frac12a(t_0)e^{x\phi(t_0)}
\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{x|\phi''(t_0)|}},
$$
with the analogous formula for a right endpoint.

= Laplace's method with multiple global maxima
{c}
{parent=Laplace's method}

If a <Laplace's method>[Laplace integral] has finitely many separated nondegenerate global maxima with the same phase value, its leading term is the sum of their local Gaussian contributions. An interior maximum contributes a full Gaussian integral, while a <Laplace's method at a nondegenerate stationary endpoint>[stationary endpoint maximum] contributes half of one.

= Degenerate endpoint in Laplace's method
{parent=Laplace's method}

Suppose a real phase has its unique minimum at the left endpoint and $\phi(t)-\phi(0)\sim ct^m$, with $c>0$ and integer $m\geq1$. For a continuous amplitude $a$ with $a(0)\ne0$, localization followed by $s=(\nu c)^{1/m}t$ gives
$$
\int_0^\infty a(t)e^{-\nu\phi(t)}\,dt\sim a(0)e^{-\nu\phi(0)}\frac{\Gamma(1/m)}{m(\nu c)^{1/m}}.
$$
This uses the <Gamma integral>. It assumes sufficient decay away from the minimum to make the remaining integral negligible. A cubic endpoint has width $\nu^{-1/3}$; an ordinary quadratic endpoint has width $\nu^{-1/2}$.

= Cubic endpoint-to-saddle transition
{parent=Degenerate endpoint in Laplace's method}
{title2=$\delta=O(\nu^{-2/3})$}

For a <Laplace integral> with local phase $\phi(t;\delta)=\delta t+bt^3/3+\cdots$, $b>0$, an interior minimum approaches the endpoint as $\delta\uparrow0$. The <distinguished limit> is $t=O(\nu^{-1/3})$, $\delta=O(\nu^{-2/3})$. With $s=(\nu b)^{1/3}t$, its leading local integral is
$$
(\nu b)^{-1/3}I(-\delta\nu^{2/3}b^{-1/3}),\qquad I(x)=\int_0^\infty e^{xs-s^3/3}\,ds.
$$
The <cubic Laplace transition integral> joins the ordinary endpoint estimate for positive $\delta$, the cubic endpoint estimate at zero, and the interior <Laplace's method> estimate for negative $\delta$. Separate fixed-parameter formulas fail to be uniform when the minimum is within its own width of the endpoint.

= Discrete Laplace method
{c}
{parent=Laplace's method}

The discrete Laplace method estimates a sum whose positive terms concentrate near the maximum of a smooth exponent. When the Gaussian concentration width contains asymptotically many lattice points, the sum is a Riemann-sum version of <Laplace's method> with the corresponding lattice-spacing factor.

= Laplace asymptotic for the derivative of the Gamma function
{parent=Laplace's method}

After $t=zs$, the phase in the integral for $\Gamma'(z)$ becomes $\log s-s$, with maximum $-1$ and second derivative $-1$ at $s=1$. Hence
$$
\Gamma'(z)\sim\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{z}}e^{z\log z-z}\log z.
$$

= Method of steepest descent
{parent=Asymptotic expansion}
{wiki}

= Steepest descent
{synonym}

A contour integral with a large exponential parameter is deformed through saddle points along curves on which the real part of the phase decreases most rapidly and the imaginary part is locally constant.

= Steepest descent contour
{parent=Method of steepest descent}

For an integrand $e^{kf(z)}$, a contour along which $\operatorname{Im}f$ is constant and $\operatorname{Re}f$ decreases away from the contributing endpoint or saddle. This suppresses oscillation while localizing the large-parameter integral.

= Quadratic complex endpoint descent path
{parent=Method of steepest descent}
{title2=$z(s)^2=b^2+s$}

For $e^{-\lambda z^2}$, the <saddle point> descent is the real axis and ascent is the imaginary axis. A descent path from a nonzero endpoint $b$ has $z(s)^2=b^2+s$, $s\geq0$, with the continuous square-root branch from $b$. It ends at positive or negative real infinity according to the sign of $\operatorname{Re}b$. The oriented outward <endpoint contribution in steepest descent> is $e^{-\lambda b^2}/(2\lambda b)$.

= Gaussian saddle and endpoint dominance by angle
{c}
{parent=Quadratic complex endpoint descent path}
{title2=$f(\theta,\lambda)$}

= Gaussian complex-endpoint dominance
{c}
{synonym}

For a <Gaussian integral> from $-1/\sqrt2$ to $e^{i\theta}$, a <saddle point> contribution dominates for $0\leq\theta\leq\pi/4$. The moving <endpoint contribution in steepest descent> dominates for $\pi/4<\theta<5\pi/6$. At $5\pi/6$ the two endpoints have equal exponential rates and both are needed; for larger angles the fixed endpoint dominates. Saddle connectivity switches at $\pi/2$, but that change is subdominant in the leading endpoint regime.

= Simple-saddle contribution in steepest descent
{parent=Method of steepest descent}

If $\phi'(z_0)=0$, $\phi''(z_0)\ne0$, and the oriented steepest-descent tangent has angle $\alpha$, then
$$
\int_C f(z)e^{x\phi(z)}\,dz
\sim
f(z_0)e^{x\phi(z_0)+i\alpha}
\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{x|\phi''(z_0)|}}.
$$
The tangent condition is
$$
\arg\phi''(z_0)+2\alpha=\pi\pmod{2\pi}.
$$

= Endpoint contribution in steepest descent
{parent=Method of steepest descent}

If a deformed contour starts at an endpoint $z_0$ with $\phi'(z_0)\ne0$ and initially enters a decaying sector, local integration gives the leading contribution $-a(z_0)e^{\lambda\phi(z_0)}/[\lambda\phi'(z_0)]$, with the sign adjusted to the contour orientation.

= Endpoint at infinity
{parent=Endpoint contribution in steepest descent}

An endpoint at infinity contributes when the region controlling an asymptotic integral recedes without bound as the large parameter changes. Expanding the phase and amplitude at infinity reveals the rescaling that converts the distant tail into a finite limiting integral.

= Saddle point
{parent=Analysis}
{wiki}

A saddle point of a differentiable real-valued function is a <critical point> which is neither a <local maximum> nor a <local minimum>. For example $f(x,y)=x^2-y^2$ has a saddle at the origin. For a nonconstant <holomorphic function>, critical points of its real part supply the complex-phase application used by the <method of steepest descent>.

= Monkey saddle
{parent=Saddle point}
{wiki}
{title2=$x^3-3xy^2$}

This cubic <smooth function> has one isolated degenerate <critical point> at the origin, with three ascending and three descending sectors. Adding $-tx$ with $t>0$ replaces it by two nondegenerate saddle points at $(\pm\sqrt{t/3},0)$. The <Hessians> there have opposite diagonal entries $6x$ and $-6x$. It illustrates why the number of smooth <critical points> can be smaller than that required for a <Morse function>.

= Saddle-point approximation
{parent=Saddle point}

A saddle-point approximation expands an exponent around a stationary configuration and integrates its leading Gaussian fluctuations. A large parameter multiplying the exponent suppresses higher fluctuations.

= Cubic saddle-point approximation
{parent=Saddle-point approximation}

When two quadratic saddles coalesce and the first nonzero phase derivative is cubic, the contributing neighborhood has width $O(\lambda^{-1/3})$ rather than $O(\lambda^{-1/2})$. The leading contour integral reduces to a Gamma-function or Airy-function constant.

= Saddle-point approximation with a nearby pole
{parent=Saddle-point approximation}

When a simple pole approaches a saddle within its Gaussian width, separate residue and saddle approximations cease to be uniform. A local change of variable reduces the transition to a complementary error function whose multiplier smoothly switches the pole residue across the deformed contour.

= Simple saddle coinciding with a simple pole
{parent=Saddle-point approximation with a nearby pole}
{title2=$J=e^{-i\lambda G_0}[-i\pi F_0+O(\lambda^{-1/2})]$}

Let $G'(z_0)=0$, $G''(z_0)\ne0$, and let $F,G$ be analytic. Assume a contributing contour can be deformed onto two descent arms of the <method of steepest descent> with one clockwise semicircular avoidance of the pole at $z_0$, and that its other portions are negligible. For the integrand $F(z)e^{-i\lambda G(z)}/(z-z_0)$, a local coordinate with $G-G_0=-iw^2/2$ gives
$$
z-z_0=cw+dw^2+\cdots,\qquad c^2=-i/G''(z_0),\qquad
\frac{F(z)}{z-z_0}\frac{dz}{dw}=\frac{F_0}w+c\left(F'_0-\frac{F_0G'''_0}{6G''_0}\right)+O(w).
$$
The odd pole term has zero <Cauchy principal value> on the real arms and integral $-i\pi F_0$ on the clockwise indentation. The constant regular term integrates to $\sqrt{2\pi/\lambda}$ times its coefficient, proving the expansion and generic correction order. A degenerate saddle has a different local width and possibly a different indentation angle; analyticity and a single critical point alone do not imply this result. If $F_0=0$, retain the additive expansion rather than writing asymptotic equivalence to zero.

= Lambert W function

The Lambert W function is the multivalued inverse of $w\mapsto we^w$, so $W(z)e^{W(z)}=z$. Its principal real branch satisfies $W(z)=\log z-\log\log z+o(1)$ as $z\to+\infty$.

= Real branches of Lambert W
{parent=Lambert W function}
{title2=$W_0,\ W_{-1}$}

On $-e^{-1}<z<0$, the <Lambert W function> has two real branches: $-1<W_0(z)<0$ and $W_{-1}(z)<-1$. The <derivative> of $we^w$ is $e^w(1+w)$, so this function decreases on $(-\infty,-1)$ and increases on $(-1,\infty)$, proving the two-branch structure. As $u\to\infty$, <series reversion> gives
$$
W_0(-e^{-u})=-e^{-u}-e^{-2u}+O(e^{-3u}),\qquad W_{-1}(-e^{-u})=-u-\log u+O(\log u/u).
$$
Consequently the two positive roots of $xe^{1/x}=e^u$ are $-1/W_0(-e^{-u})$ and $-1/W_{-1}(-e^{-u})$.

= Logarithmic spherical Bessel square integral
{parent=Spherical Bessel function}
{title2=$\int_0^\infty j_\ell(x)^2\,dx/x=1/[2\ell(\ell+1)]$}

For integer $\ell\geq1$, put $I_\ell=\int_0^\infty j_\ell(x)^2\,dx/x$ and $J_\ell=\int_0^\infty j_\ell(x)j_{\ell-1}(x)\,dx$. Integrating $j_\ell'=j_{\ell-1}-(\ell+1)j_\ell/x$ gives $J_\ell=(\ell+1)I_\ell$. For $\ell>1$, integrating $j_{\ell-1}'=(\ell-1)j_{\ell-1}/x-j_\ell$ also gives $J_\ell=(\ell-1)I_{\ell-1}$. For $\ell=1$, the boundary values $j_0(0)=1$ and $j_0(\infty)=0$ give $J_1=1/2$, hence $I_1=1/4$. The recurrence proves the displayed identity. The integral without the square is different; for example $\int_0^\infty j_1(x)\,dx/x=\pi/4$.

= Spherical Hankel function
{parent=Spherical Bessel function}
{title2=$h_n^{(1)}=j_n+iy_n,\quad h_n^{(2)}=j_n-iy_n$}

These combinations of <spherical Bessel functions> solve the radial <Helmholtz equation>. For time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$, $h_n^{(1)}(kr)$ is outgoing and $h_n^{(2)}(kr)$ incoming. = Fixed-argument growth of spherical Hankel functions
{parent=Spherical Hankel function}
{title2=$h_n^{(1)}(t)\sim-i\sqrt2\,t^{-1}(2n/(et))^n$}

For fixed $t>0$ and large integer order, $y_n(t)\sim-(2n-1)!!/t^{n+1}$ while $j_n(t)$ is negligible. Stirling's formula gives $(2n-1)!!=(2n)!/(2^nn!)\sim\sqrt2(2n/e)^n$, proving the displayed asymptotic. In particular its magnitude is bounded above and below by constant multiples of $(2n/(et))^n$. This two-sided comparison is stronger than a one-sided big-O estimate and is what converts a finite spherical trace norm into a weighted condition on far-field coefficients.

= Spherical Bessel product integral
{parent=Spherical Bessel function}

= Laplace integral method for a differential equation
{parent=Modified Bessel function}

Laplace's integral method seeks $y(z)=\int_Ce^{zt}f(t)\,dt$. Substitution into the differential equation and integration by parts transfer multiplication by $z$ into differentiation with respect to $t$, producing a first-order equation for $f$ and endpoint conditions for $C$.

= Laplace integral solution of a singular third-order equation
{parent=Laplace integral method for a differential equation}

For $x>0$, substituting $y(x)=\int_\gamma e^{xt}f(t)\,dt$ into $xy^{(3)}+2y=0$ and integrating by parts gives
$$
[e^{xt}t^3f(t)]_{\partial\gamma}
+\int_\gamma e^{xt}\{2f-(t^3f)'\}\,dt=0.
$$
The amplitude equation $(t^3f)'=2f$ has solution $f(t)=Ct^{-3}e^{-1/t^2}$. On $\gamma=(-\infty,0)$ both endpoint terms vanish, and hence
$$
y(x)=C\int_{-\infty}^0e^{xt-t^{-2}}t^{-3}\,dt
=C_1\int_0^\infty u e^{-u^2-x/u}\,du.
$$

{c}
{synonym}

For a continuously differentiable function on a bounded planar domain, the <Cauchy-Pompeiu formula> supplements the <Cauchy integral formula> with an area integral of its <Wirtinger derivative>. = Half-plane decomposition by Cauchy integrals
{parent=Cauchy integral formula}

Let $g$ be a <meromorphic function> with simple poles $\alpha_+$ above $c$ and $\alpha_-$ below $-c$, and $zg(z)\to0$ uniformly at infinity in $\operatorname{Im}z\ge-c$. The left-to-right line integrals $H(\lambda)=(2\pi i)^{-1}\int_{\mathbb R-ic}g(z)/(z-\lambda)\,dz$ and $J(\lambda)=-(2\pi i)^{-1}\int_{\mathbb R+ic}g(z)/(z-\lambda)\,dz$ satisfy $H=g-r_+/(\lambda-\alpha_+)$ above $-c$ and $J=r_+/(\lambda-\alpha_+)$ below $c$, where $r_+$ is the upper <residue>. Upper semicircle closure proves both formulas by the <residue theorem>; subtracting the principal part makes $H$ <holomorphic> at $\alpha_+$ too. Consequently $H+J=g$ on the intervening strip. No lower-half-plane decay is needed.

= Equality in the Cauchy coefficient estimate
{parent=Cauchy estimate}

For an <entire function> $f(z)=\sum_jc_jz^j$, equality $|c_n|=\max_{|z|=r}|f(z)|/r^n$ at a fixed $r>0$ holds exactly when $f(z)=cz^n$. = Smooth-data instability of the Cauchy-Riemann Cauchy problem
{parent=First-order Cauchy-Riemann operator}
{title2=$\phi_n(x,t)=e^{-\sqrt n}\cos(n(x+it))$}

For the <first-order Cauchy-Riemann operator>, the equation $\phi_t-i\phi_x=0$ admits the <entire functions> $\phi_n(x,t)=e^{-\sqrt n}\cos(n(x+it))$. = Parabolic quartic critical-point classification
{parent=Critical point}

For $F(x,y)=(y-cx^2)^2+\alpha x^2-\beta x^4$ with $\beta>0$, its stationary points are the origin and, when $\alpha>0$, $x=\pm\sqrt{\alpha/(2\beta)}$, $y=cx^2$. The other stationary points have Hessian determinant $-8\alpha$ and are saddles.

= Quartic potential with fourfold symmetry
{parent=Critical point}
{title2=$f=x^2+y^2-\tfrac12(x^4+y^4)-b x^2y^2$}

For $b>0$, the origin is a strict <local minimum>. The four axis points at distance one have <Hessian> eigenvalues $-4$ and $2(1-b)$. The four diagonal points with $x^2=y^2=1/(1+b)$ have <Hessian> eigenvalues $-4$ and $-4(1-b)/(1+b)$. Thus for $b<1$ the diagonal points are the global maxima and the axis points are <saddle points of a scalar function>; for $b>1$ these roles reverse. In <polar coordinates>, $f=r^2-\tfrac12q(\theta)r^4$, where $q=1+\tfrac12(b-1)\sin^2(2\theta)>0$. Maximizing over $r$ gives $1/(2q)$ and proves the global statements. At $b=1$, $f=\tfrac12-\tfrac12(r^2-1)^2$, so the entire unit circle is the maximum set.

= Higher-order saddle test
{parent=Saddle point of a scalar function}

= Two-time expansion of a weakly damped linear oscillator
{parent=Damped harmonic oscillator}
{title2=$x=e^{-\epsilon t}\sin(\sqrt{1-\epsilon^2}\,t)/\sqrt{1-\epsilon^2}$}

= Causal Green function of a damped oscillator
{parent=Damped harmonic oscillator}

{synonym}

For $L=D_t^2+2kD_t+k^2+\omega^2$, the causal <Green function> satisfies $LG=\delta(t)$. It is continuous at zero, with a unit jump in its first derivative. = Step response of a damped oscillator
{parent=Causal Green function of a damped oscillator}
{title2=$H(t)K(t),\quad K(t)=\int_0^t g(s)ds$}

Integrating the smooth positive-time impulse kernel gives the zero-data response to the <Heaviside step function>. For nonzero $\omega$,
$$
K(t)=\frac{1-e^{-kt}[\cos\omega t+(k/\omega)\sin\omega t]}{k^2+\omega^2}.
$$
At zero frequency and nonzero $k$ the limit is $[1-e^{-kt}(1+kt)]/k^2$; when both parameters vanish it is $t^2/2$. The integral representation avoids ambiguous divisions in these limiting cases.

= Shift derivative of a step response

{title2=$-\partial_b[H(t-b)K(t-b)]=H(t-b)K'(t-b)$}

For a step-response kernel with $K(0)=0$, differentiating the start time produces no delta term from the response itself, because its coefficient is zero. The negative shift derivative is the impulse response. More generally an additional $\delta(t-b)K(0)$ term appears if the kernel has a nonzero initial value. The identity holds distributionally as well as for the ordinary smooth pieces.

= Differentiability does not imply continuity of the derivative
{parent=Differentiable function}
{title2=$f(x)=x^2\sin(1/x),\quad f'(0)=0$}

Extend the displayed function by $f(0)=0$. Its <difference quotient> at zero is $h\sin(1/h)$, which tends to zero. Away from zero its <derivative> is $2x\sin(1/x)-\cos(1/x)$. At $x_k=1/(2\pi k)$ this derivative equals $-1$, whereas at $y_k=1/((2k+1)\pi)$ it equals $+1$. Thus <differentiability implies continuity> of the function but does not imply <continuity> of its derivative.

= Quadratic damping restores differentiability at the origin
{parent=Differentiable function}
{title2=$|f(x)|\leq C|x|^2\ \Longrightarrow\ Df(0)=0$}

A function with $f(0)=0$ and the displayed bound has zero <Frechet derivative> at zero, since $|f(x)|/|x|\to0$. In particular, a bounded oscillatory factor multiplied by a quadratic expression is differentiable at the origin even if the oscillations have no limit themselves. For $f(x,y)=(x^2-y^2)\sin[1/(x^2-y^2)]$, extended by zero on the two diagonal lines, this damping works at the origin. At a nonzero point of either line, the quadratic factor instead vanishes to first order along a transverse direction; the difference quotient oscillates without a limit. Thus the differentiability set is the complement of those two lines together with the origin.

= Secants straddling a differentiability point
{parent=Differentiable function}
{title2=$\frac{g(v_n)-g(u_n)}{v_n-u_n}\to g'(x)$}

If $u_n\le x\le v_n$, $u_n<v_n$ and both endpoints tend to $x$, differentiability gives $g(x+h)=g(x)+g'(x)h+r(h)$ with $r(h)=o(|h|)$. The error in the secant is bounded by $\varepsilon(|v_n-x|+|u_n-x|)/(v_n-u_n)=\varepsilon$ for large $n$. Thus these secants converge even when either endpoint equals $x$.

= Differentiability implies continuity
{parent=Differentiable function}
{title2=$f(x+h)-f(x)=h[f'(x)+o(1)]$}

If a real <function> has a finite <derivative> at $x$, its <difference quotient> tends to $f'(x)$ and is bounded for sufficiently small nonzero increments. Multiplying it by the increment shows $f(x+h)-f(x)\to0$, proving <continuity> at $x$. The converse fails: the <absolute value function> is continuous but not differentiable at zero.

= Differentiability
{parent=Differentiable function}

Differentiability is the existence of a <derivative> at a point. For maps between finite-dimensional <normed vector spaces>, the existence of a <Frechet derivative> requires a single <linear map> approximating the increment with error $o(\|h\|)$. Merely having <partial derivatives> need not provide this approximation; <continuous> <partial derivatives> on a neighbourhood do suffice.

= Discontinuous derivative of a differentiable plane curve
{parent=Differentiability}
{title2=$f(t)=t^2(\sin(1/t),\cos(1/t))$}

Set $f(0)=0$. The difference quotient has <Euclidean norm> $|t|$, so $f'(0)=0$. For $t\ne0$, differentiation gives $\|f'(t)\|^2=1+4t^2$. Thus this <differentiable> curve has a <derivative> discontinuous at zero, despite each scalar component satisfying the usual one-dimensional derivative properties.

= Differentiability does not imply continuous derivatives
{parent=Differentiability}

The function $f(x)=x^2\sin(1/x^2)$ for $x\ne0$, extended by $f(0)=0$, is <differentiable> everywhere. At zero its <derivative> is zero by the difference quotient, while away from zero $f'(x)=2x\sin(1/x^2)-2x^{-1}\cos(1/x^2)$ is unbounded near zero. Differentiability of a function is therefore weaker than continuity of its derivative.

= Differentiability from continuous partial derivatives
{parent=Differentiability}
{title2=$Df(a)h=\sum_j\partial_jf(a)h_j$}

If all <partial derivatives> exist near a point and are continuous there, successive coordinate increments and the one-variable <mean value theorem> express $f(a+h)-f(a)$ as $\sum_j h_j\partial_jf(a+\eta_j(h))$ with $\eta_j(h)\to0$. Subtracting the displayed <linear map> leaves $o(\|h\|)$, proving <Frechet differentiability>. In two dimensions the absolute remainder is at most $\epsilon(|h_1|+|h_2|)\le\sqrt2\epsilon\|h\|$ for small enough increments.

= Differentiability of the square of a continuous function
{parent=Differentiability}
{title2=$f^2\text{ differentiable at }x\iff f\text{ differentiable at }x\text{ or }f(x)=0,\ f(x+h)=o(\sqrt{|h|})$}

At a point with $f(x)\ne0$, factor the difference of squares and divide by $f(x+h)+f(x)\to2f(x)$ to show that <differentiability> of $f^2$ is equivalent to that of $f$. At a zero of $f$, its square has a local minimum; if <differentiable>, its derivative must vanish. The difference quotient then vanishes exactly when $f(x+h)^2/|h|\to0$, equivalently $f(x+h)/\sqrt{|h|}\to0$. For example, $f(t)=|t|^\alpha$ with $1/2<\alpha<1$ is not <differentiable> at zero, but its square is.

= Higher-order differentiability at a point
{parent=Differentiability}
{title2=$f^{(n)}(a)$}

For $n=1$, <differentiability> at $a$ means that the <difference quotient> has a finite two-sided <limit of a function>. For $n\geq2$, require <derivatives> through order $n-1$ throughout some open <neighbourhood> of $a$, and require the final <difference quotient>
$$
f^{(n)}(a)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f^{(n-1)}(a+h)-f^{(n-1)}(a)}h
$$
to exist as a finite real number. The last <derivative> need not exist elsewhere or be continuous at $a$. In particular, existence of $f'(a)$ by itself does not justify taking a second <derivative>: the function $f'$ must first be defined in a <neighbourhood> of $a$.

= Zero-boundary harmonic uniqueness
{parent=Dirichlet problem}
{title2=$\Delta u=0,\quad u|_{\partial V}=0\ \Longrightarrow\ u=0$}

For a bounded regular region and a sufficiently smooth real <harmonic function>, apply the <divergence theorem> to $u\nabla u$. Its divergence is $|\nabla u|^2+u\Delta u$. The boundary term vanishes, hence the integral of the nonnegative gradient square is zero. The gradient vanishes and the boundary values fix each component's constant as zero. Taking the difference of two solutions gives uniqueness for the <Poisson equation> with <Dirichlet boundary data>. Unbounded regions require an additional condition at infinity.

= Nonuniqueness of Dirichlet data without control at infinity
{parent=Dirichlet problem}

Unbounded-domain <Dirichlet problems> can need a condition at infinity. On all of $\mathbb R^n$, constant functions have identical empty boundary data and are distinct finite-energy stationary points of $\frac12\int|Du|^2$. With nonempty boundary, on the exterior of the unit ball in dimension at least three, $1-|x|^{2-n}$ is a nonzero harmonic function vanishing on the sphere, with finite <Dirichlet energy> and a nonzero limit at infinity. It is stationary for compactly supported variations. The permitted class of variations and the behaviour at infinity must be specified for a global variational uniqueness claim.

= Uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem

= Brownian representation of the Dirichlet problem
{c}
{parent=Dirichlet problem}

Let $D$ be bounded, let $u$ be harmonic on $D$ and continuous on $\overline D$, and let $\tau_D$ be the first exit time of Brownian motion. The <Itô formula> makes $u(B_{t\wedge\tau_D})$ a bounded martingale, so
$$
u(x)=\mathbb E_x[u(B_{\tau_D})].
$$
If $u=f$ on the boundary, this becomes $u(x)=\mathbb E_x[f(B_{\tau_D})]$.

= Kakutani solution of the Dirichlet problem
{parent=Brownian representation of the Dirichlet problem}
{c}
{title2=$u(x)=\mathbb E_x[f(B_{\tau_D})]$}

For a bounded <domain> whose boundary points are all <regular boundary points>, continuous <Dirichlet boundary data> $f$ have a unique solution
$$
u(x)=\mathbb E_x[f(B_{\tau_D})],\qquad\Delta u=0\text{ in }D,\quad u|_{\partial D}=f.
$$
Here $\tau_D$ is the <Brownian exit time>. The <strong Markov property> gives the <mean value property for harmonic functions>; a <barrier for the Dirichlet problem> gives continuity at the boundary. This is an existence theorem as well as the <Brownian representation of the Dirichlet problem> for an already known solution.

= Bounded harmonic representation with almost sure Brownian exit

If $u$ is a bounded <harmonic function> on a domain $D$, continuous on its closure, and the <Brownian exit time> $T_D$ is almost surely finite from $z$, then $u(z)=\mathbb E_z[u(B_{T_D})]$. = Harmonic limit along an infinite strip arm
{parent=Bounded harmonic representation with almost sure Brownian exit}
{title2=$u(x,y)\to(a+b)/2+(a-b)x/2$}

Suppose an unbounded domain contains the upper strip $(-1,1)\times(1,\infty)$ with its two vertical sides on the boundary. A bounded <harmonic function> with constant values $a$ on the right side and $b$ on the left side, and with valid <bounded harmonic representation with almost sure Brownian exit>, satisfies $u(x,y)\to a(1+x)/2+b(1-x)/2$ as $y\to\infty$. The horizontal <Brownian exit time> is finite; on that finite random interval the vertical Brownian path has a finite minimum. Consequently reaching the bottom before a side has probability tending to zero. The remaining two exit probabilities are the one-dimensional interval probabilities.

= Failure of the Brownian Dirichlet representation on an unbounded domain
{c}
{parent=Brownian representation of the Dirichlet problem}

Almost-sure finiteness of the exit time does not replace boundedness of the domain. In the upper half-plane, $u(x,y)=y$ is harmonic with zero boundary values and Brownian motion reaches the boundary almost surely, but $u$ is positive inside. The stopped martingale fails to be uniformly integrable.

= Perron method
{parent=Dirichlet problem}
{wiki}
{c}

= Perron method for the Dirichlet problem
{c}
{synonym}

The Perron method constructs a harmonic solution with prescribed boundary data as the pointwise supremum of all subharmonic functions lying below those data. Harmonic lifting proves interior harmonicity; barriers determine whether the supremum attains the boundary values.

= Perron family of subharmonic functions
{c}
{parent=Perron method}

A nonempty collection of real continuous <subharmonic functions> on a connected <Riemann surface> is a Perron family if it is closed under pairwise maxima and <harmonic lifting of a continuous subharmonic function> on every relatively compact coordinate disk. Its pointwise supremum is either everywhere infinite or a <harmonic function on a Riemann surface>. At a point where the supremum is finite, take increasing disk lifts approaching that value. The <Harnack inequality for harmonic functions> gives a finite harmonic limit on the disk. Lifting their maxima with any other member gives a larger harmonic limit with the same value at the chosen point, hence the same limit by the <strong maximum principle for harmonic functions>. The supremum is therefore harmonic on that disk; connectedness propagates finiteness to the whole surface.

= Perron subfunction for the Poisson equation
{c}
{parent=Perron method}
{title2=$\Delta v\geq f$}

= Perron subfunctions for the Poisson equation
{c}
{synonym}

A continuous function $v$ is a Perron subfunction for $\Delta u=f$ when it lies below every <classical solution> of that <Poisson equation> on a relatively compact <open ball> whenever it lies below that solution on the ball's boundary. For $C^2$ functions, this is equivalent to $\Delta v\geq f$. A superfunction reverses the comparison and has $\Delta v\leq f$ when $C^2$. Adding a boundary upper bound gives the family whose supremum defines the <Perron method for the Dirichlet problem>.

= Barrier for the Dirichlet problem
{parent=Perron method}

A barrier at $z\in\partial\Omega$ is a continuous superharmonic function that vanishes at $z$ and is strictly positive at every other point of the closure. It forces the Perron solution to converge to its prescribed boundary value at $z$.

= Regular boundary point
{parent=Barrier for the Dirichlet problem}

A boundary point is regular for the Dirichlet problem when the Perron solution approaches the prescribed continuous boundary value there. The existence of a barrier implies regularity.

= Exterior cone condition
{parent=Regular boundary point}

An open set satisfies an exterior cone condition at $z$ if a solid cone with vertex $z$ lies outside the set in some neighbourhood of $z$. The stronger global version requires the closures of the cone and the set to meet only at $z$. In two dimensions, a <power barrier for an exterior cone> makes such a point a <regular boundary point> for the <Dirichlet problem>. Local cone conditions suffice for local boundary regularity; a global cone is an additional geometric assumption.

= Power barrier for an exterior cone
{parent=Exterior cone condition}
{title2=$g=-r^\nu\cos(\beta\psi)$}

For a planar set outside a global solid cone of half-angle $\alpha$, choose $0<\alpha_0<\min(\alpha,\pi/2)$, $\beta=\pi/[2(\pi-\alpha_0)]$, and $0<\nu<\beta$. If $\psi$ is the angle from the opposite axis, the <Laplacian in polar coordinates> gives
$$
g=-r^\nu\cos(\beta\psi)<0,\qquad
\Delta g=(\beta^2-\nu^2)r^{\nu-2}\cos(\beta\psi)>0.
$$
The cosine has a positive uniform lower bound outside the original cone. On a bounded set, $\Delta g$ therefore has a positive uniform lower bound as well. The positive function $-g$ is a <barrier for the Dirichlet problem>. An unbounded set does not receive the same global lower bound from this construction.

= Exterior sphere condition
{parent=Regular boundary point}

A domain satisfies the exterior sphere condition at a boundary point when a ball contained in the complement is tangent to the boundary only at that point. Fundamental solutions of the Laplace equation centred at the ball's centre provide a harmonic barrier, so such a point is regular.

= Logarithmic exterior-sphere barrier in two dimensions
{parent=Exterior sphere condition}

If $B(y,R)$ is an exterior ball tangent to a planar domain at $x_0$, then
$$
w(x)=\log\frac{|x-y|}{R}
$$
is a <harmonic function> in the domain, vanishes at $x_0$, and is positive at every other point of its closure. It is therefore a <barrier for the Dirichlet problem> and proves that $x_0$ is a <regular boundary point>.

= Fixed-length stationary surface of revolution
{parent=Isoperimetric constraint}
{title2=$y(x)=k^{-1}(\cosh kx-\cosh ka),\quad l=2\sinh(ka)/k$}

= Natural boundary conditions for a free endpoint

= Nonattainment under an initial derivative constraint
{parent=Natural boundary conditions for a free endpoint}

A pointwise constraint on the initial derivative need not be closed in the norm controlling the value and first-derivative square integrals. For example, minimize $b\int_0^T\dot V^2\,dt$ with $b,L,T>0$, $V(0)=0$ and $\int_0^TV\,dt=L$. The attained minimum is $3bL^2/T^3$, uniquely achieved by $V_*=3Lt(2T-t)/(2T^3)$, with natural condition $\dot V_*(T)=0$. Adding $\dot V(0)=0$ excludes this minimizer but leaves the same infimum. Multiplying $V_*$ by $1-e^{-t/\varepsilon}$ and rescaling to restore the integral gives smooth admissible approximations converging in energy. The formal quadratic from the interior <Euler-Lagrange equation> and two initial conditions alone is therefore not a true minimizer.

= Uncentred Fourier uncertainty principle
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$\|xf\|_2\|f'\|_2\geq\|f\|_2^2/2$}

For nonzero $f$ with $f,xf,f'\in L^2(\mathbb R)$, integration by parts gives $\|f\|_2^2=-2\operatorname{Re}\langle f',xf\rangle$. = Fourier transform on a locally compact abelian group
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$\widehat f(\chi)=\int_G f(x)\overline{\chi(x)}\,dm_G(x)$}

For $f\in L^1(G)$ the displayed integral defines a bounded continuous function on the <Pontryagin dual group>. It takes <convolution> to pointwise multiplication and left translation $T_x$ to multiplication by $\overline{\chi(x)}$. The transform depends on the scale of <Haar measure> on $G$; the compatible <dual Haar measure> scales reciprocally.

= Fourier inversion on a locally compact abelian group
{c}
{parent=Fourier transform on a locally compact abelian group}
{title2=$f(x)=\int_{\widehat G}\widehat f(\chi)\chi(x)\,dm_{\widehat G}(\chi)$}

For the <dual Haar measure>, the formula first follows for continuous compactly supported convolution squares from the <Bochner theorem>. More generally, if $f\in L^1(G)$ and $\widehat f\in L^1(\widehat G)$, the right-hand side is a continuous representative of $f$; equality holds everywhere if $f$ is continuous, and otherwise at every point where the chosen representative is continuous. Convolution with normalized small-support positive convolution squares proves the extension: the spatial convolutions approach $f$ in $L^1$, while their transforms approach $\widehat f$ in $L^1$ by <equicontinuity of compact families of characters> and an integrable-tail split.

= Fourier decay from a derivative jump
{parent=Fourier transform}
{title2=$\widehat f(k)\supset\frac{[f^{(j)}]_a e^{-ika}}{(ik)^{j+1}}$}

For a piecewise smooth decaying function, a jump in its $j$th derivative at $a$ contributes the displayed algebraic term to its high-frequency <Fourier transform>. Distributionally, $D^{j+1}f$ contains $[f^{(j)}]_a\delta(x-a)$, and <integration by parts> or the derivative-transform identity supplies the factor $(ik)^{-j-1}$. Adequate integrability of the subsequent derivatives controls the remainder. Smooth localized polynomial terms give no algebraic cusp contribution.

= Fourier transform of an algebraic cusp
{parent=Fourier decay from a derivative jump}
{title2=$\mathcal F(|x|^p)=2\Gamma(p+1)\cos[\pi(p+1)/2]\,|k|^{-p-1}$}

For $p>-1$ this formula is interpreted away from $k=0$ as a <tempered distribution> or by exponential regularization. = Fresnel tail expansion
{c}
{parent=Finite Fresnel integrals}
{title2=$C=A+P\sin\lambda^2-Q\cos\lambda^2$}

For $A=\sqrt{\pi/8}$, define real tail functions by $Q+iP=e^{-i\lambda^2}\int_\lambda^\infty e^{it^2}dt$. Then $C=A+P\sin\lambda^2-Q\cos\lambda^2$ and $S=A-P\cos\lambda^2-Q\sin\lambda^2$. Repeated <integration by parts> gives
$$

= Gradient flow of the periodic screened Poisson energy
{parent=Gradient flow}

For smooth $2\pi$-periodic $f$, the equation $u_t-u_{xx}+u=f$ is the negative $L^2$ <gradient flow> of $I_f[u]=\frac12\int(u_x^2+u^2-2fu)\,dx$. Periodic <integration by parts> gives $dI_f/dt=-\int u_t^2\,dx$. Its unique equilibrium has <Fourier coefficients> $f_n/(1+n^2)$, and every transient coefficient decays as $e^{-(1+n^2)t}$. Thus the flow converges to the unique minimizer of the energy.

= Sheared quartic double-well gradient flow
{parent=Gradient flow}
{title2=$V=(x^2-1)^2+(x+y)^2-1$}

The completed-square form gives global minima at $(1,-1)$ and $(-1,1)$, both with value $-1$, and a <saddle point> at $(0,0)$ with value zero. Negative levels split into two closed components; the zero contour joins their lobes at the saddle. The <gradient flow> $\dot x=-V_x,\dot y=-V_y$ decreases $V$ by the <gradient-flow dissipation identity>. A negative initial level traps each trajectory on its original side of $x=0$, because $V(0,y)\geq0$.

= Diffusion from a gradient energy

= Differential operator
{parent=Analysis}
{wiki}

A differential operator maps a <function> to an expression depending on finitely many of its <derivatives>. A linear differential operator has the form $P=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}a_\alpha(x)\partial^\alpha$; its order is the largest derivative order with nonzero coefficient. The <Laplacian> is a second-order example, and <boundary conditions> specify the domain when defining an operator on a <function space>.

= Differential operator relative to a base ring
{parent=Differential operator}
{title2=$D_k^m(R)$}

For a commutative $k$-algebra $R$, a relative differential operator is a $k$-linear endomorphism. Set $D_k^{-1}(R)=0$, and define order at most $m$ by $[P,m_a]\in D_k^{m-1}(R)$ for every $a\in R$, where $m_a$ means multiplication by $a$. Order zero is multiplication by an element of $R$. The finite-order operators form the ring $D_k(R)$ under composition. Specifying the base is essential: absolute operators on a complex polynomial ring include derivations of the coefficient field and need not be complex-linear.

= Formal pseudodifferential operator
{parent=Differential operator}
{title2=$P=\sum_{j\leq N}p_j(x)\partial^j$}

A <formal pseudodifferential operator> is a series in <integer> powers of $\partial=d/dx$, bounded above but possibly infinite towards negative powers. Its <coefficients> are <smooth functions>. Multiplication uses the <formal pseudodifferential composition rule>. Negative powers are formal inverses, not globally defined integration operators on all <smooth functions>. Every <coefficient> in a product is a finite sum, so multiplication is defined without analytic convergence.

= Formal pseudodifferential wave function
{parent=Formal pseudodifferential operator}
{title2=$w=k^\beta e^{kx}\sum_{i\geq0}w_i(x)k^{-i}$}

Treat $k$ as a formal spectral parameter near infinity. If $P=\sum_{j\geq0}g_j\partial^{\alpha-j}$, the <formal pseudodifferential composition rule> gives $Pw=k^{\alpha+\beta}e^{kx}\sum_{s\geq0}k^{-s}\sum_{i+j+r=s}g_j\binom{\alpha-j}{r}w_i^{(r)}$. Each <coefficient> contains finitely many terms. This describes a formal module action, not a claim that the infinite series converges at finite $k$.

= Order filtration of formal pseudodifferential operators
{parent=Formal pseudodifferential operator}
{title2=$\operatorname{ord}[P,Q]\leq\operatorname{ord}P+\operatorname{ord}Q-1$}

For scalar <coefficients>, leading symbols commute. If $P=a\partial^p+\cdots$ and $Q=b\partial^q+\cdots$, their product has leading <coefficient> $ab$, and their <commutator> has <coefficient> $pa b'-qb a'$ at order $p+q-1$. Thus product order is at most $p+q$, with equality when $ab$ is not identically zero; <commutator> order is at most $p+q-1$, with equality when its displayed <coefficient> is not identically zero. Constant-coefficient operators commute. Nonzero <smooth functions> with disjoint supports show why product orders need not add over $C^\infty(\mathbb R)$.

= Formal pseudodifferential residue
{parent=Formal pseudodifferential operator}
{title2=$\operatorname{res}P=p_{-1}(x)$}

The formal residue is the <coefficient> of $\partial^{-1}$ after <coefficients> have been moved to the left by the <formal pseudodifferential composition rule>. It is not an analytic <residue> at a point. For scalar <coefficients>, the residue of a <commutator> is a total <derivative>; integrating it with vanishing boundary terms produces the cyclic <Adler trace>.

= Adler trace
{parent=Formal pseudodifferential residue}
{c}
{title2=$\operatorname{Tr}P=\int\operatorname{res}P\,dx$}

Integrate the <formal pseudodifferential residue> over a period, or over the real line when the integral converges and the relevant boundary terms vanish. More algebraically, apply a linear <functional> on the <coefficient> algebra that annihilates <derivatives>. For $A=a\partial^p$, $B=b\partial^q$ and $r=p+q+1\geq0$, the two residues are $\binom pr ab^{(r)}$ and $\binom qr ba^{(r)}$. Since $\binom qr=(-1)^r\binom pr$, <integration by parts> proves $\operatorname{Tr}(AB)=\operatorname{Tr}(BA)$. For $r<0$ both residues vanish. Bounded orders ensure only finitely many monomial pairs contribute to the residue of a product.

= Hamiltonian trace functional on monic differential operators
{parent=Adler trace}
{c}
{title2=$l_P(L)=c+\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\int a_lu_l\,dx$}

For a fixed <formal pseudodifferential operator> $P=\sum_j p_j\partial^j$ and a monic <differential operator> $L=\partial^m+\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}u_l\partial^l$, cyclicity of the <Adler trace> gives $l_P(L)=\operatorname{Tr}(PL)$ with $c=\int p_{-m-1}\,dx$ and $a_l=\operatorname{res}(\partial^lP)=\sum_{k=0}^l\binom lk p_{k-l-1}^{(k)}$. The <function> is affine in the variable <coefficients>; its <variational derivative> with respect to $u_l$ is $a_l$.

= Formal pseudodifferential composition rule
{parent=Formal pseudodifferential operator}
{title2=$\partial^n f=\sum_{r\geq0}\binom nr f^{(r)}\partial^{n-r}$}

The generalized <Leibniz rule> extends composition of <differential operators> to all <integer> $n$. Define $\binom nr=n(n-1)\cdots(n-r+1)/r!$. For nonnegative $n$ the sum stops at $r=n$. For $n=-s<0$, its <coefficients> are $(-1)^r\binom{s+r-1}{r}$; in particular $\partial^{-1}f=f\partial^{-1}-f'\partial^{-2}+f''\partial^{-3}-\cdots$. Applying $\partial$ on the left cancels consecutive <derivative> terms and recovers multiplication by $f$, which explains the inverse expansion.

= Dirichlet Green function in a quadrant

{parent=Dirichlet Green function}
{title2=$G_Q(\mathbf r,\mathbf r_0)$}

For the positive Laplacian convention $\Delta G=\delta$, the <method of images> in the first quadrant reflects an interior pole $(a,b)$ across each axis with negative sign and across both with positive sign:
$$
G_Q(x,y;a,b)=\frac1{2\pi}\left[\log|(x,y)-(a,b)|-\log|(x,y)-(-a,b)|-\log|(x,y)-(a,-b)|+\log|(x,y)-(-a,-b)|\right].
$$
The <Dirichlet Green function> vanishes on both axes. Its outward normal derivative on the horizontal axis is
$$
\partial_nG_Q(t,0;a,b)=\frac{4abt}{\pi[(t-a)^2+b^2][(t+a)^2+b^2]},
$$
and the vertical-axis kernel follows by exchange of coordinates. <Green second identity> converts these derivatives into the quadrant <Poisson integral> for boundary data.

= Regular part of the planar Dirichlet Green function
{parent=Dirichlet Green function}
{title2=$G_D(z,y)=-\log|z-y|-G_z(y)$}

For the canonical <Dirichlet Green function> with logarithmic singularity, its harmonic correction satisfies $G_z(z)=-\log\operatorname{crad}_D(z)$. If $\phi$ maps the unit disc to $D$ with $\phi(0)=z$, then
$$
G_z(\phi(w))=-\log\left|\frac{\phi(w)-\phi(0)}w\right|.
$$
The quotient extends at zero to $\phi'(0)$. On unbounded domains the canonical Green function, rather than boundary values alone, fixes the harmonic correction.

= Green-function representation

If $G(x;\xi)$ satisfies the boundary conditions and $L_xG(x;\xi)=\delta(x-\xi)$, then a solution of $Ly=f$ is

y(x)=\int G(x;\xi)f(\xi)\,d\xi,

up to any homogeneous contribution required by nonhomogeneous boundary data.

= One-dimensional modified Helmholtz Green function

For $\operatorname{Re}m>0$, the decaying solution of

is

= Fourier decay from a Green-function derivative jump
{parent=One-dimensional modified Helmholtz Green function}
{title2=$\int H(s)e^{-iqs}ds\sim-[H']e^{-iqy}/q^2$}

If $H$ is continuous, piecewise twice differentiable, and has derivative jump $[H']=H'(y+)-H'(y-)$ at $y$, then its distributional second <derivative> is $H''_{\mathrm{reg}}+[H']\delta_y$. If the regular part is integrable and the boundary terms vanish, two <integrations by parts> give the displayed <asymptotic expansion> by the <Riemann-Lebesgue lemma>. A jump $[H]$ in $H$ itself instead produces $[H]e^{-iqy}/(iq)$ at leading order. This distinguishes the different decay rates of the <streamfunction> and streamwise <velocity> in <linear inviscid damping in unbounded Couette flow>.

= Neumann Green function for a second-order ordinary differential equation

{c}

Let $y_1,y_2$ solve $y''+\alpha y'+\beta y=0$ with $y_1'(0)=0$ and $y_2'(1)=0$. For Neumann boundary conditions, the Green function is
$$
G(x,\xi)=\frac1{W(\xi)}
\begin{cases}
y_1(x)y_2(\xi),&x<\xi,\\
y_2(x)y_1(\xi),&x>\xi,
\end{cases}
$$
where $W=y_1y_2'-y_1'y_2$. The derivative jump is one. If $\alpha=0$, the Abel identity makes $W$ constant and $G$ symmetric.

= Jump conditions for a third-order Green function
{parent=Causal Green function}

For $\mathcal L=D^3+a_2D^2+a_1D+a_0$ with smooth coefficients and leading coefficient one, a <Green function> solving $\mathcal L G=\delta_\xi$ is continuous together with its first derivative at an interior source, and its second derivative jumps by one. For a causal initial-value Green function, take the left branch zero and choose the right branch with initial values $0,0,1$ at the source.

= Hermite oscillator quantization
{parent=Hermite differential equation}
{c}
{title2=$\Lambda=2n+1,\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots$}

A solution of $b''+(\Lambda-\xi^2)b=0$ decaying at both real infinities has $b=e^{-\xi^2/2}H_n(\xi)$ with a <Hermite polynomial>. Substitution gives $H''-2\xi H'+(\Lambda-1)H=0$; its power-series recurrence terminates at degree $n$ when $\Lambda=2n+1$. The square-integrable <Hermite functions> give the complete oscillator spectrum, including the ground state $n=0$. This quantization also determines <global modes of a quadratically confined Ginzburg-Landau equation> after removing drift.

= Odd polynomial solutions of the Hermite differential equation
{parent=Hermite differential equation}
{title2=$E=4j+2,\quad j\geq0$}

For $-W''+2xW'=EW$, the <power series> coefficients obey $(n+2)(n+1)a_{n+2}=(2n-E)a_n$. The condition $W(0)=0$ makes every even coefficient zero. A nonzero odd series terminates precisely when $E=2d$ with $d$ an odd positive integer. A polynomial of degree $d$ must have this value of $E$ by comparison of leading coefficients, so these are all possibilities. With $W'(0)=1$, the first three <polynomial> solutions are $x$, $x-2x^3/3$, and $x-4x^3/3+4x^5/15$, with $E=2,6,10$. They are scalar multiples of the odd <Hermite polynomials>.

= Singular endpoints in higher-order variational problems
{parent=Higher-order Euler-Lagrange equation}

The Euler-Lagrange equation on an open interval can be derived using compactly supported variations even when an endpoint is singular. A formal solution with singular endpoint data need not make the improper action finite. For example, $y=x+x^{-1}$ solves $(x^4y'')''=0$, but substituting into $x^4(y'')^2+4y^2y'$ gives $4x^2+4-4x^{-4}$, whose <integral> down to zero diverges. Stationarity of a finite functional then requires an additional regularization or admissible-space specification.

= Clamped second-variation inequality
{parent=Higher-order Euler-Lagrange equation}
{title2=$\int_0^\pi\eta^2\le\int_0^\pi(\eta\prime)^2\le\int_0^\pi(\eta\prime\prime)^2$}

A perturbation preserving both values and slopes has $\eta=\eta\prime=0$ at the endpoints. Apply the <Poincare inequality> to $\eta$ and then to $\eta\prime$. The resulting inequality makes a quadratic <second variation> proportional to $\int(\eta^2-(\eta\prime\prime)^2)$ nonpositive. If a functional is quadratic, its exact expansion about a stationary function then proves a global maximum.

= Clamped-free beam under uniform load and endpoint force
{parent=Higher-order Euler-Lagrange equation}

For
$$
E[y]=\int_0^L\left(\frac A2(y'')^2+\rho gy\right)\,dx
$$
with the <boundary conditions> $y(0)=y'(0)=0$, $y''(L)=0$, and $-Ay'''(L)=F$, the <higher-order Euler-Lagrange equation> is
$$
Ay''''+\rho g=0.
$$
Its solution splits as
$$
y=y_0+y_F,
\qquad
y_0=-\frac{\rho g}{24A}x^2(6L^2-4Lx+x^2),
\qquad
y_F=\frac{F}{6A}x^2(3L-x).
$$

= Endpoint force derivative of clamped-free beam energy

For the <clamped-free beam under uniform load and endpoint force>, the additional minimized internal energy is
$$
\mathcal E(F)=\frac{F^2L^3}{6A}.
$$
Its <derivative> is the endpoint <displacement> caused by the endpoint <force>:
$$
\frac{d\mathcal E}{dF}=\frac{FL^3}{3A}=h.

= Laurent coefficient

{title2=$a_n=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z-z_0|=r}\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^{n+1}}\,dz$}

A Laurent coefficient is a coefficient $a_n$ in the <Laurent series> $f(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}a_n(z-z_0)^n$ of a <holomorphic function> on an annulus. Integration around a circle in the annulus gives the displayed formula, including negative indices. The coefficient $a_{-1}$ is the <residue>.

= Basel sum from exponential poles
{parent=Laurent series}
{c}
{title2=$\sum_{r\ge1}r^{-2}=\pi^2/6$}

The meromorphic function $(e^z-1)^{-1}$ has simple poles of residue one at $2\pi i\mathbb Z$. Subtracting $1/z+\sum_{r=1}^n2z/(z^2+4\pi^2r^2)$ removes all poles inside radius $2(n+1)\pi$. Comparing its inner Taylor coefficients with the outer Laurent expansion gives annular coefficient $a_1=1/12-\sum_{r=1}^n1/(2\pi^2r^2)$. On circles of radius $(2n+1)\pi$, the function is uniformly bounded: outside the strip $|\operatorname{Re}z|\le1$ use the exponential modulus, and inside it the imaginary part stays near an odd multiple of $\pi$. The Laurent coefficient integral bounds $|a_1|\le C/[(2n+1)\pi]\to0$, proving the sum.

= Puiseux series
{c}
{parent=Laurent series}
{title2=$\sum_{n\geq n_0}a_nz^{n/q}$}

A Puiseux series permits fractional exponents with a fixed common denominator: $\sum_{n\geq n_0}a_nz^{n/q}$. These are natural local expansions of branches of algebraic functions. A <fractional-power root expansion> uses such powers to resolve roots that coalesce as a parameter tends to zero.

= Square-root splitting of a double polynomial root
{parent=Puiseux series}
{title2=$x-x_0=O(\epsilon^{1/2})$}

Suppose a perturbed <polynomial> has the local form $A(x-x_0)^2+\epsilon B+O((x-x_0)^3,\epsilon(x-x_0),\epsilon^2)$ with $AB\ne0$. <Dominant balance> gives $x-x_0=\epsilon^{1/2}z$ and $Az^2+B=0$. The two resulting <Puiseux series> split a <double root>; their leading displacements can be real or a <complex conjugate> pair. Ordinary <Taylor series> in $\epsilon$ cannot describe this splitting.

= Fractional-power root expansion
{parent=Puiseux series}

An algebraic root can depend on a parameter through fractional powers even when the polynomial coefficients have ordinary power series. For example, the roots of $t^3=\epsilon^2$ are on the scale $\epsilon^{2/3}$. <Dominant balance for algebraic roots> identifies the exponent, and local expansion of the scaled root supplies successive coefficients.

= Laurent theorem
{parent=Laurent series}
{c}

An analytic function on an annulus $r<|z-a|<R$ has a unique locally uniformly convergent expansion
$$
f(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_n(z-a)^n,
\qquad
c_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(\zeta)}{(\zeta-a)^{n+1}}\,d\zeta,

where $C$ is any positively oriented circle around $a$ in the annulus.

= Real analytic

A function $f$ is real analytic at $y$ when its <Taylor series>

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb N^n}\frac{D^\alpha f(y)}{\alpha!}(x-y)^\alpha

= Factorial derivative criterion for real analyticity
{parent=Real analytic function}
{title2=$|f^{(n)}|\leq CA^n n!$}

A <smooth function> on a real open interval is <real analytic> exactly when, near each point, there are constants $C,A>0$ such that $|f^{(n)}(x)|\leq CA^n n!$ for every $n$ and every $x$ in that neighborhood. The bound must hold on a neighborhood, not only at its center. The <Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder> proves sufficiency; differentiating a convergent <power series> on a smaller interval proves necessity. A <flat function> shows why pointwise bounds alone are insufficient.

= Uniform analyticity under polynomial coordinate changes
{parent=Real analytic function}
{title2=$\|\partial^\beta f_\varepsilon\|_\infty\le A\beta!R^{-|\beta|}$}

Suppose a family of <polynomial> coordinate maps has a fixed bound on its degrees and coefficients depending continuously on a compact parameter set, and sends a fixed real compact set into a compact subset of the domain of a <real analytic function>. A common complex neighborhood can then be chosen so the compositions are holomorphic and uniformly bounded there. The multivariable <Cauchy estimate> gives uniform factorial <derivative> bounds. For the change $\tilde x=x+y^2-\varepsilon$, the coefficient pullback uses its inverse: $f_\varepsilon(\tilde x,\tilde y)=f(\tilde x-\tilde y^2+\varepsilon,\tilde y)$.

= Residue

The residue of a <meromorphic function> $f$ at an <isolated singularity> $a$ is the coefficient of $(z-a)^{-1}$ in its <Laurent series>. At a simple <pole>,
$$
\operatorname{Res}(f,a)=\lim_{z\to a}(z-a)f(z).
$$

with $g$ holomorphic at $a$, then

\operatorname{Res}(f,a)
=\frac{g^{(n-1)}(a)}{(n-1)!}.

= Residue theorem
{parent=Analysis}
{wiki}

If $f$ is <holomorphic function>[holomorphic] on and inside a positively oriented closed contour except for finitely many isolated singularities $a_j$ inside it, then
$$
\oint f(z)\,dz=2\pi i\sum_j\operatorname{Res}(f,a_j).
$$

= Fourier integral of a rational function with simple nonreal poles
{parent=Residue theorem}
{title2=$\int_{\mathbb R}\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}e^{ix}\,dx=2\pi i\sum_{\operatorname{Im}\alpha>0}\frac{p(\alpha)e^{i\alpha}}{q'(\alpha)}$}

For monic $q$ with distinct nonreal zeros and $\deg p\leq\deg q-1$, close the contour in the upper half-plane. The rational factor is $O(R^{-1})$ on the large semicircle, whose exponential damping makes the arc integral $O(R^{-1})$. The two one-sided real integrals also converge by <integration by parts>, since the rational factor tends to zero and its derivative is $O(x^{-2})$. Thus the displayed formula is an ordinary improper integral, not merely a principal value.

= Rectangular contour for an exponential beta integral
{parent=Residue theorem}
{title2=$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ax}}{1+e^x}\,dx=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi a)}$}

For $0<a<1$, integrate $e^{az}/(1+e^z)$ around a rectangle of imaginary height $2\pi$. Its single pole at $\pi i$ has residue $-e^{\pi ia}$, the top-edge integral is $-e^{2\pi ia}$ times the bottom-edge integral, and both vertical sides vanish at large real width. The <residue theorem> gives $\int_{\mathbb R}e^{ax}/(1+e^x)\,dx=\pi/\sin(\pi a)$. Substitution $t=e^x$ gives the equivalent integral $\int_0^\infty t^{a-1}/(1+t)\,dt$.

= Contour integral for a sine-squared trigonometric quotient
{parent=Residue theorem}
{title2=$J(a,b)=2\pi(a-\sqrt{a^2-b^2})/b^2$}

For $a>b>0$, substitution $z=e^{i\theta}$ gives poles at zero and the two roots of $bz^2+2az+b$. The inside-pole <residues> sum to $(a-\sqrt{a^2-b^2})/(ib^2)$, so
$$
\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\sin^2\theta}{a+b\cos\theta}\,d\theta=\frac{2\pi}{b^2}(a-\sqrt{a^2-b^2}).
$$
The double pole at zero is essential; omitting it gives the wrong sign and value.

= Upper-half-plane contour for square-root logarithmic integrals
{parent=Residue theorem}
{title2=$I=\pi/\sqrt2,\quad J=\pi^2/(2\sqrt2)$}

Set $I=\int_0^\infty x^{1/2}/(1+x^2)\,dx$ and $J=\int_0^\infty x^{1/2}\log x/(1+x^2)\,dx$. In an indented upper semicircle use the <principal complex logarithm> and $z^{1/2}=e^{\log z/2}$. The negative real boundary contributes $iJ-\pi I$, the positive boundary contributes $J$, and both circular arcs vanish. The pole at $i$ has <residue> $\pi e^{i\pi/4}/4$. Thus $(1+i)J-\pi I=(\pi^2/(2\sqrt2))(-1+i)$; comparing real and imaginary parts yields the displayed integrals.

= Upper-half-plane indentation rule
{parent=Residue theorem}

Suppose a contour follows the <real line>[real axis], closes in the upper half-plane, and avoids each simple real pole $a_j$ by a small semicircle above it. Each indentation is clockwise and tends to
$$
-i\pi\operatorname{Res}(f,a_j).
$$
If the large arc vanishes and there are no enclosed poles away from the real axis, the <Cauchy principal value> is therefore
$$
\operatorname{PV}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx
=i\pi\sum_j\operatorname{Res}(f,a_j).
$$

= Zero-free Schwarz lemma
{parent=Schwarz lemma}
{title2=$|f'(0)|\le2|f(0)|\log(1/|f(0)|)$}

For a zero-free <holomorphic function> $f:\mathbb D\to\mathbb D$, put $c=f(0)$ and $\alpha=-\log|c|$. A <holomorphic logarithm> makes $-\log f$ have positive real part. The <Carathéodory derivative bound for the right half-plane> yields the displayed sharp bound. Equality functions are $f(z)=c\exp[-2\alpha\omega z/(1-\omega z)]$, $|\omega|=1$. The <Harnack inequality on the unit disk> also yields the sharp pointwise bounds $|c|^{(1+r)/(1-r)}\le|f(w)|\le|c|^{(1-r)/(1+r)}$ for $r=|w|$. Aligning $\omega w=\pm r$ attains the two endpoints.

= Carathéodory derivative bound for the right half-plane
{c}
{parent=Schwarz lemma}
{title2=$|F'(0)|\le2\operatorname{Re}F(0)$}

If a <holomorphic function> $F$ on the <unit disc> has positive real part, the <Cayley transform between the half-plane and disk> $\phi=(F-F(0))/(F+\overline{F(0)})$ maps the disc into itself and vanishes at zero. The <Schwarz lemma> gives the displayed derivative bound. Equality holds exactly when $\phi(z)=\omega z$ with $|\omega|=1$, equivalently $F(z)=i\operatorname{Im}F(0)+\operatorname{Re}F(0)(1+\omega z)/(1-\omega z)$.

= Schwarz-Pick theorem
{c}
{parent=Schwarz lemma}
{title2=$\rho(f(z),f(w))\leq\rho(z,w)$}

= Schwarz-Pick lemma
{c}
{synonym}

A holomorphic self-map of the <unit disc> contracts its pseudohyperbolic distance. Conjugating by <Möbius transformations> that send one chosen source point and its image to zero reduces the statement to the <Schwarz lemma>. In the <complex upper half-plane> this gives
$$
\left|\frac{f(z)-f(w)}{f(z)-\overline{f(w)}}\right|\leq\left|\frac{z-w}{z-\overline w}\right|.
$$
If $f(iY)=iY+o(Y)$ at infinity, multiply the equivalent inequality between one minus the squared distances by $Y$ and let $Y\to\infty$. It yields $\operatorname{Im}f(z)\geq\operatorname{Im}z$. Applied to the inverse <mapping-out function> of a <compact H-hull>, this proves the nonnegativity of its <half-plane capacity>.

= Zero location bound from Schwarz-Pick
{parent=Schwarz-Pick theorem}
{title2=$|a|\geq|f(0)|/M$}

If a <holomorphic function> $f$ on the <unit disc> satisfies $|f|\leq M$ and $f(a)=0$, the <Schwarz-Pick lemma> applied to $f/M$ gives $|f(0)|/M\leq|a|$. Equality for a nonzero central value forces a scaled <automorphism of the unit disk>. If $f$ extends past the unit circle and $|f(0)|$ is smaller than its minimum boundary modulus, the <maximum modulus principle> applied to $1/f$ proves that a zero occurs inside the circle.

= One-point value region of a holomorphic disc map
{parent=Schwarz-Pick theorem}
{title2=$|\psi_{w_0}(w)|\le|\psi_{z_0}(z)|$}

For <holomorphic functions> $f:\mathbb D\to\mathbb D$ with $f(z_0)=w_0$, put $r=|(z-z_0)/(1-\overline z_0z)|$. Their possible values at $z$ form the closed disc with centre $(1-r^2)w_0/(1-r^2|w_0|^2)$ and radius $r(1-|w_0|^2)/(1-r^2|w_0|^2)$. Indeed $\psi_{w_0}\circ f=\psi_{z_0}h$ with <holomorphic> $|h|\le1$; all constant choices of $h$ in the closed <unit disc> are admissible. Completing the square in the displayed pseudohyperbolic inequality gives the centre and radius.

= Equality in hyperbolic Schwarz-Pick contraction
{parent=Schwarz-Pick theorem}

A <holomorphic function> between hyperbolic <Riemann surfaces> lifts to a disk map. If the lift is not an automorphism, strict <Schwarz-Pick lemma> derivative inequality makes every nonconstant geodesic shorter. Equality of distance for one distinct pair therefore forces the map to be a <covering map> and a local <isometry>. Covering maps attain equality for sufficiently close pairs in a single lift of a small convex metric ball; they need not preserve all global distances. A punctured disk covers a thrice-punctured sphere by including a cyclic parabolic subgroup into $\Gamma(2)$, so equality is possible in that example.

= Extremal derivative of a disc map into a symmetric annulus
{parent=Schwarz-Pick theorem}
{title2=$\max|f\prime(0)|=4L/\pi$}

Let $L>0$ and consider <holomorphic functions> $f$ from the <unit disc> into $e^{-L}<|w|<e^L$, normalized by $f(0)=1$. Their largest possible <derivative> modulus is $4L/\pi$. Lift $f$ to its <holomorphic logarithm> $h$ with $h(0)=0$, so $|\operatorname{Re}h|<L$. The function $\tan(\pi h/(4L))$ maps the disc into itself and fixes zero. The <Schwarz lemma> gives $|h'(0)|\le4L/\pi$. Equality holds precisely for
$$
f_\theta(z)=\exp\left(\frac{4L}{\pi}\arctan(e^{i\theta}z)\right),\qquad\theta\in\mathbb R.
$$
Here $\arctan$ is its <holomorphic> branch vanishing at zero; $(4L/\pi)\arctan z$ is a <conformal map> from the disc onto the vertical strip. Thus the maximal <derivative> modulus is attained, with a circle of distinct extremizers rather than a unique map.

= Spectral radius
{title2=$\rho(A)$}
{parent=Analysis}
{wiki}

The spectral radius is the largest modulus of an operator's or matrix's eigenvalues:
$$
\rho(A)=\max_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}|\lambda|.
$$

= Spectral-radius three-circle inequality
{parent=Spectral radius}
{title2=$M_r(f)\leq M_{r_0}(f)^{1-\theta}M_{r_1}(f)^\theta$}

For a holomorphic <Banach algebra>-valued function, put $M_r(f)=\sup_{|z|=r}r(f(z))$. Apply the <Hadamard three-circle theorem> to the holomorphic powers $f^{2^n}$. The continuous functions $\|f(z)^{2^n}\|^{1/2^n}$ decrease to the <spectral radius>. <Supremum convergence for decreasing continuous functions> passes the boundary suprema to the limit, proving the inequality with the same logarithmic interpolation parameter. In particular, at the geometric-mean radius, the square of the middle spectral radius is at most the product of the two boundary suprema.

= Spectral-radius Liouville theorem
{parent=Spectral-radius three-circle inequality}

If an entire <Banach algebra>-valued function has bounded <spectral radius>, that spectral radius is constant. The <spectral-radius three-circle inequality> on circles about an arbitrary point, with the outer radius tending to infinity, bounds every interior value by the maximum on any smaller circle. Upper semicontinuity of the spectral radius then lets the smaller radius tend to zero. Every value is bounded by the value at the arbitrary center; exchanging centers gives equality. The algebra-valued function itself can be nonconstant, for example $zN$ for a fixed nonzero square-zero matrix $N$.

= Joint spectral radius
{parent=Spectral radius}
{title2=$\widehat\rho(\mathcal A)=\lim_{p\to\infty}\max_{A_i\in\mathcal A}\|A_p\cdots A_1\|^{1/p}$}
{wiki}

The <joint spectral radius> measures the worst exponential growth under arbitrary products from a finite family of <matrices>. Submultiplicativity gives its existence and invariance under changes of the chosen norm. Every fixed-length product $B$ satisfies $\rho(B)^{1/p}\le\widehat\rho(\mathcal A)$, while the maximum norm of all length-$p$ products to the power $1/p$ is an <upper bound>. A repeated product gives the first inequality; grouping an arbitrary long word into blocks proves the second. The <spectral radius> of each individual <matrix> does not control growth of all switched products.

= Power-decay characterization of the spectral radius
{parent=Spectral radius formula}
{title2=$(x/t)^n\to0\iff t>r(x),\quad t>0$}

In a complex unital <Banach algebra>, the <spectral radius formula> makes the root norms of $(x/t)^n$ tend to $r(x)/t$. If $t>r(x)$, those norms are eventually bounded above by a geometric sequence and the powers vanish. Conversely, vanishing powers eventually have norm less than one; the infimum version of the radius formula then forces $r(x)/t<1$. Consequently $r(x)=\inf\{t>0:(x/t)^n\to0\}$, including the case $r(x)=0$.

= Sturm oscillation theorem
{c}
{parent=Sturm-Liouville theory}

For a real one-dimensional <Sturm-Liouville problem> with separated self-adjoint boundary conditions and discrete simple spectrum, the eigenfunction for the $n$th eigenvalue has exactly $n$ interior zeros, numbering from zero. For a confining real <Schrödinger operator> on the line, the same ordering makes the ground state nodeless and the first excited state have one node. If the potential is even, uniqueness and reflection symmetry imply that these states are respectively even and odd.

= Drift-removing substitution for a Dirichlet Sturm-Liouville problem
{parent=Sturm-Liouville theory}

For $\mathcal Ly=-y''-2ay'$, setting $y=e^{-ax}v$ gives $\mathcal Ly=e^{-ax}(-v''+a^2v)$. Dirichlet conditions are unchanged. The weighted self-adjoint form is $-e^{-2ax}(e^{2ax}y')'$, with weight $e^{2ax}$. On an interval of length $L$, <eigenvalues> are $(n\pi/L)^2+a^2$ and <eigenfunctions> are $e^{-ax}\sin(n\pi x/L)$.

= Orthogonality of Sturm-Liouville eigenfunctions
{parent=Sturm-Liouville theory}
{title2=$\int_a^b w\overline{y_m}y_n\,dx=0$}

For a regular <self-adjoint> <Sturm-Liouville problem> with positive weight $w$ and self-adjoint boundary conditions, <eigenfunctions> with distinct <eigenvalues> are <orthogonal> in the weighted <inner product> $\int w\overline uv$. <Integration by parts> proves this by equating $\int\overline u Lv$ and $\int\overline{Lu}v$.

= Gegenbauer polynomial

{parent=Sturm-Liouville theory}

For $\alpha>0$, the Gegenbauer polynomial $C_n^\alpha$ is the degree-$n$ polynomial solution of
$$
(1-x^2)y''-(2\alpha+1)xy'+n(n+2\alpha)y=0.
$$
These polynomials are orthogonal on $(-1,1)$ with weight $(1-x^2)^{\alpha-1/2}$.

= Gegenbauer differential equation

The Gegenbauer differential equation is the displayed Sturm–Liouville eigenvalue equation defining the <Gegenbauer polynomial>. Multiplication by $(1-x^2)^{\alpha-1/2}$ puts it in self-adjoint form.

= Roots of a Gegenbauer polynomial
{parent=Gegenbauer polynomial}
{c}

For $\alpha>0$, all $n$ roots of $C_n^\alpha$ are simple and lie in $(-1,1)$. If the product $q$ of its interior linear factors had degree below $n$, orthogonality would make $\int C_n^\alpha q w=0$, while $C_n^\alpha q=q^2(C_n^\alpha/q)$ has a constant strict sign away from finitely many points.

= Sturm-Liouville form
{c}
{parent=Sturm-Liouville theory}

A scalar second-order differential operator is in Sturm–Liouville form when it is written as

Lu=-(p(x)u')'+q(x)u,
$$
possibly together with a positive weight in an eigenvalue problem.

= Sturm-Liouville operator
{c}
{parent=Sturm-Liouville form}
{title2=$L\xi=W^{-1}[-(P\xi\prime)\prime+Q\xi]$}

The weighted operator $L=W^{-1}[-(P\xi\prime)\prime+Q\xi]$, with real coefficients and $P,W>0$ in the interior, defines a <Sturm-Liouville problem>. Its realization as a <self-adjoint differential operator> requires a domain whose endpoint conditions annihilate the boundary form $[P(\eta\prime^*\xi-\eta^*\xi\prime)]$. = Flux-domain realization of a one-dimensional elliptic operator
{parent=Sturm-Liouville operator}
{title2=$D(L)=\{v\in H_0^1(0,1):pv'\in H^1(0,1)\}$}

For continuous $p>0$ and bounded real $q$, the form $a(u,v)=\int(pu'v'+quv)$ on the <zero-trace Sobolev space> defines the weak operator $L=-(pu')'+qu$. Its literal $L^2$ operator domain is the displayed flux domain. If a weak solution has right-hand side $f\in L^2$, then $(pu')'=qu-f\in L^2$, so it belongs to that domain. This avoids assuming that a merely differentiable coefficient has bounded derivative. With $p\in C^1$ bounded away from zero, the domain reduces to $H^2\cap H_0^1$.

= Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator
{c}
{parent=Sturm-Liouville operator}
{title2=$uL(v)-vL(u)=[p(uv'-vu')]'$}

For $L(v)=(pv')'+av$, direct differentiation gives $uL(v)-vL(u)=[p(uv'-vu')]'$. The potential terms cancel. Integrating with common homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions> eliminates the endpoint term. If $L(u)=0$ and $L(v)=f$, this gives $\int uf=0$, a necessary <solvability condition> for the inhomogeneous equation.

= Sturm-Liouville problem
{parent=Sturm-Liouville theory}
{c}

A regular Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue problem has the form
$$
+(p y')'+q y=-\lambda w y
$$
on a bounded interval with self-adjoint boundary conditions and positive weight $w$.

= Inverse-square weighted Dirichlet spectrum
{parent=Sturm-Liouville problem}
{title2=$\lambda_n=1/4+[n\pi/\log(b/a)]^2$}

For $y''+\lambda x^{-2}y=0$ on $0<a<x<b$ with zero endpoint values, write $t=\log(x/a)$ and $y=\sqrt{x}\,w(t)$. The <Euler-Cauchy equation> becomes $w''+(\lambda-1/4)w=0$. Thus $y_n=\sqrt{x}\sin[n\pi\log(x/a)/\log(b/a)]$ and the exact spectrum is displayed above. Leading <WKB quantization> omits the constant $1/4$, while producing the same leading eigenfunction shape after quantization.

= Dirichlet eigenvalue

A Dirichlet eigenvalue is an eigenvalue for an eigenfunction constrained to vanish at the endpoints of its interval or on the boundary of its domain.

For a bounded domain $\Omega$, the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of $-\Delta$ has the variational characterization

\lambda_1=\inf_{0\ne u\in H_0^1(\Omega)}
\frac{\int_\Omega|\nabla u|^2}{\int_\Omega|u|^2}.

= Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion
{parent=Sturm-Liouville problem}
{c}

For a regular self-adjoint Sturm-Liouville problem, eigenfunctions are orthogonal in the weighted inner product
$$
\langle f,g\rangle_w=\int fgw,
$$
and the coefficient of $y_n$ is $\langle f,y_n\rangle_w/\langle y_n,y_n\rangle_w$.

= Spectral Green function of a regular Sturm-Liouville problem
{parent=Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion}
{title2=$G_\mu(x,\xi)=\sum_n y_n(x)y_n(\xi)/(\lambda_n-\mu)$}

Normalize the real <eigenfunctions> using $\int r y_my_n=\delta_{mn}$. For $\mu$ outside the <spectrum>, applying the operator to the displayed <Green function> expansion gives $r(x)\sum_n y_n(x)y_n(\xi)=\delta(x-\xi)$ by weighted completeness. This is the kernel with respect to ordinary source integration $d\xi$; using the weighted measure would change its normalization.

= Odd reciprocal-square sum from a Dirichlet Green function
{parent=Spectral Green function of a regular Sturm-Liouville problem}
{title2=$\sum_{j\ge0}(2j+1)^{-2}=\pi^2/8$}

For $L=d^2/dx^2$ on $(0,\pi)$ with zero endpoints, the zero-parameter <Green function> is $-x_<(\pi-x_>)/\pi$. Its <eigenfunctions> are $\sqrt{2/\pi}\sin(nx)$ with eigenvalues $-n^2$. At $x=\xi=\pi/2$, the kernel is $-\pi/4$ and its expansion contains precisely the odd reciprocal squares, giving the displayed identity.

= Self-adjoint differential operator
{parent=Sturm-Liouville theory}
{wiki}

A differential operator is self-adjoint when integration by parts makes its inner products symmetric on its domain.

= Self-adjoint fourth-order scalar differential operator
{title2=$D^4+pD^2+p'D+r$}
{parent=Self-adjoint differential operator}

For real coefficient functions, the fourth-order scalar operator
$$
L=D^4+pD^2+qD+r
$$
is formally self-adjoint exactly when $q=p'$. Indeed, $(pD^2)^*=pD^2+2p'D+p''$ and $(qD)^*=-qD-q'$. Under <clamped boundary condition>[clamped boundary conditions], its quadratic form is
$$
\langle y,Ly\rangle=\int\left[(y'')^2-p(y')^2+ry^2\right]dx.
$$

= Self-adjoint differential equation
{parent=Self-adjoint differential operator}

A second-order scalar differential equation is in self-adjoint form when it can be written
$$
(p(x)y')'+q(x)y=0.
$$
Multiplication by $y$ and integration by parts then gives an energy identity.

= Solvability condition at a Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue
{parent=Self-adjoint differential operator}

If $L(y_0;\lambda_0)=0$ and $y_0,y$ satisfy homogeneous separated boundary conditions, the Lagrange identity gives
$$
\int y_0L(y;\lambda_0)\,dx
=\int yL(y_0;\lambda_0)\,dx=0.
$$
Thus $L(y;\lambda_0)=f$ can be solved only if $f$ is orthogonal to the null mode $y_0$.

= Fredholm solvability condition
{parent=Solvability condition at a Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue}

At a simple eigenvalue of a self-adjoint linear operator, an inhomogeneous equation is solvable only when its forcing is orthogonal to the null eigenfunction. In weakly nonlinear expansions, this condition determines the amplitude equation.

= Translation-mode solvability for a bistable front
{parent=Fredholm solvability condition}
{title2=$c_1=-\int U_0\prime h(U_0)/\int(U_0\prime)^2$}

Suppose $u_t=Du_{xx}+f_0(u)+\varepsilon h(u)$ has a balanced stationary front $U_0(z)$, where $z=(x-q)/\sqrt D$ and $U_0''+f_0(U_0)=0$. Take $\dot q/\sqrt D=\varepsilon c_1+\cdots$ and $u=U_0+\varepsilon U_1+\cdots$. At first order, $LU_1=-c_1U_0'-h(U_0)$ with $L=d^2/dz^2+f_0'(U_0)$. Differentiating the front equation proves $LU_0'=0$. Integration by parts against the decaying <translation> mode gives
$$
c_1=-\frac{\int_{-\infty}^\infty U_0'h(U_0)\,dz}{\int_{-\infty}^\infty(U_0')^2\,dz}.
$$
This is the <Fredholm solvability condition> determining the velocity, derived from the <self-adjoint differential operator> rather than assumed. An orthogonality condition or a fixed midpoint removes the freedom to add a multiple of $U_0'$ to $U_1$. For the <bistable cubic reaction-diffusion equation>, $h=-aU_0(1-U_0)$ gives $c_1=-\sqrt2a$, and its first-order forcing vanishes pointwise, so the phase-fixed shape correction is zero.

= Moving-defect locking of a cubic-quintic front
{parent=Translation-mode solvability for a bistable front}

At the <Maxwell balance for a scalar reaction-diffusion front>, let $q=3\alpha/4$, $\beta=\sqrt3\alpha/2$, and perturb the linear growth rate by $\varepsilon K\delta(x+cT)$, $T=\varepsilon t$. Projection onto the front's translation mode gives $X_T=-K\operatorname{sech}^2(\beta Z/2)$, where $Z=X+cT+\beta^{-1}\log q$. Hence $Z_T=c-K\operatorname{sech}^2(\beta Z/2)$. Finite locked positions exist for $0<c/K\le1$. For a strict inequality, the root with $KZ<0$ is stable and the other unstable; they meet in a <saddle-node bifurcation> at $c=K$. This is a leading-order <solvability condition>, not an exact finite-perturbation solution.

= Leading nonlinear eigenvalue shift in a Sturm-Liouville problem
{parent=Solvability condition at a Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue}

For $L(y;\lambda)=y^{m+1}$ near a normalized simple null mode $y_0$, put $y=\varepsilon y_0+O(\varepsilon^2)$ and $\lambda-\lambda_0=\varepsilon^m\mu$. Orthogonality to $y_0$ yields
$$
\mu=\int y_0^{m+2}\,dx+O(\varepsilon).
$$

= Front
{synonym}

A front is a spatial profile connecting different asymptotic states on its two sides. A <travelling wave> front moves with a constant speed; a stationary front has zero speed. In a one-dimensional stationary reduction it is a <heteroclinic orbit> between the corresponding homogeneous <equilibria>. Equal first-integral values are necessary for a stationary front in a scalar conservative spatial equation.

= Maxwell balance for a scalar reaction-diffusion front
{parent=Front solution}
{c}

A stationary <front solution> of $u_t=u_{xx}+f(u)$ connecting equilibria $u_-$ and $u_+$ requires $\int_{u_-}^{u_+}f(s)\,ds=0$. Multiply the stationary equation by $u_x$ and integrate: the derivative term has zero endpoint contribution. This equal-potential condition is the Maxwell balance. For $f(u)=\mu u+\alpha u^3-u^5$, a front from zero to a nonzero constant requires $u_+^2=3\alpha/4$ and $\mu=-3\alpha^2/16$.

= Mesa solution
{parent=Front solution}

A mesa is a localized broad plateau joined to its surrounding state by two separated <fronts>. Its leading spatial average equals the plateau fraction times the plateau value; the transition layers give corrections proportional to their width divided by the domain length. A conserved mean can determine the fraction occupied by the plateau, as in the <coexistence fraction of a conserved-field amplitude mesa>.

= Fisher's equation
{c}
{parent=Travelling wave}
{wiki}
{title2=$u_t=D\Delta u+ru(1-u/K)$}

<Fisher's equation> is a <reaction-diffusion equation> combining diffusion with logistic growth. For positive $D,r,K$, one-dimensional fronts joining $K$ to zero have minimum speed $2\sqrt{Dr}$. The <phase-plane construction of a Fisher travelling front> and the linearized leading edge explain this threshold.

= Phase-plane construction of a Fisher travelling front
{parent=Travelling wave}
{title2=$c\geq2\sqrt{k}$}

For $u_t=u_{xx}+u(k-u)$ with $k>0$, a decreasing <travelling wave> connecting $k$ to $0$ satisfies $w'=p$, $p'=-cp-w(k-w)$. If $c\geq2\sqrt{k}$, choose $a>0$ with $a(c-a)\geq k$. The triangle $0\leq w\leq k$, $-aw\leq p\leq0$ is forward invariant: on its sloping side, $(p+aw)'=w[a(c-a)-k+w]\geq0$. The descending <unstable manifold> of the <saddle equilibrium> $(k,0)$ stays in this triangle and tends to $(0,0)$, producing a positive <heteroclinic orbit>. Below the threshold the origin is a <stable spiral> for positive $c$, forcing sign changes; at $c=0$ conserved energy also excludes the connection.

= Travelling-wave reduction of a reaction-diffusion system
{parent=Travelling wave}

The substitution $U(x,t)=U(x-ct)$ turns time derivatives into $-cU'$ and spatial diffusion into $U''$, reducing a reaction-diffusion PDE to an ODE system.

= Fixed-point theorem
{parent=Analysis}
{wiki}

A fixed-point theorem gives conditions under which a map $T$ has a point satisfying $Tx=x$.

= Strict distance decrease on a compact metric space
{parent=Fixed-point theorem}
{title2=$d(fx,fy)<d(x,y)\quad(x\ne y)$}

A self-map $f$ of a nonempty <compact metric space> satisfying the displayed strict distance inequality has exactly one <fixed point>, although a uniform <contraction> constant need not exist. The inequality makes $f$ <Lipschitz continuous> with constant at most one, so $g(x)=d(fx,x)$ is continuous and attains a minimum at $p$. If $fp\ne p$, then $g(fp)<g(p)$, a contradiction. Two different <fixed points> contradict the same distance inequality.

= Bounded-orbit fixed point for a strictly distance-decreasing Euclidean map
{parent=Strict distance decrease on a compact metric space}

For a strictly distance-decreasing map of a finite-dimensional <Euclidean space>, one bounded forward <orbit> suffices for existence of a <fixed point>. Its closure is compact by the <Heine-Borel theorem> and invariant by continuity, so <strict distance decrease on a compact metric space> applies. Strict distance decrease gives uniqueness throughout the whole space, not merely the orbit closure.

= Kakutani fixed-point theorem
{parent=Fixed-point theorem}
{c}
{wiki}

A nonempty <compact convex set> $K$ in a finite-dimensional space and an <upper hemicontinuous> <set-valued mapping> $F:K\rightrightarrows K$ with nonempty closed <convex> values admit $x\in F(x)$. A closed graph suffices when the whole output space is <compact>.

= Schauder-Tychonoff fixed-point theorem
{parent=Fixed-point theorem}
{c}
{title2=$T(K)\subseteq K\Longrightarrow\exists x\in K:T(x)=x$}

Every continuous self-map of a nonempty compact convex subset of a <locally convex space> has a <fixed point>. Compactness is in the locally convex topology, which may be weaker than a norm topology. This version permits fixed-point arguments on a <weak-star topology> compact set of <linear functionals>. See \a[https://www.math.purdue.edu/~yipn/543/hartmanODE12-ImpFctFixedPts.pdf][Hartman's fixed-point chapter, Theorem 0.2].

= Leray-Schauder fixed point theorem
{c}
{parent=Fixed-point theorem}
{wiki}

Let $T$ be a compact continuous self-map of a Banach space. If the set of solutions of $u=tT(u)$ for $0\leq t\leq1$ is bounded, then $T$ has a fixed point. The theorem converts a priori estimates for a homotopy of equations into existence.

= Knaster-Tarski theorem
{c}
{parent=Fixed-point theorem}
{wiki=Knaster–Tarski_theorem}

Every monotone self-map of a complete lattice has a complete lattice of fixed points. Its least fixed point is the meet of its prefixed points, and its greatest fixed point is the join of its postfixed points.

= Fixed-point property
{parent=Fixed-point theorem}
{wiki}

A topological space has the fixed-point property when every continuous self-map has a fixed point.

= Homeomorphism invariance of the fixed-point property
{parent=Fixed-point property}

If $h:A\to B$ is a <homeomorphism> and $A$ has the fixed-point property, then a continuous $f:B\to B$ induces $h^{-1}fh:A\to A$. A fixed point of the conjugate map is carried by $h$ to a fixed point of $f$.

= Fixed-point property of a closed interval
{parent=Fixed-point property}

For continuous $f:[0,1]\to[0,1]$, the function $f(x)-x$ is nonnegative at zero and nonpositive at one, so the <intermediate value theorem> gives a fixed point.

= Contraction mapping theorem
{parent=Fixed-point theorem}

= Banach fixed-point theorem
{c}
{synonym}

A contraction of a nonempty complete metric space has one fixed point, and every orbit converges to it geometrically.

= A posteriori contraction ball
{parent=Contraction mapping theorem}

Let $A$ be differentiable on a neighbourhood of a closed radius-$r$ ball in a <Banach space>, with $\|DA\|\leq\kappa<1$ on that ball. If $\|A(p_0)-p_0\|\leq(1-\kappa)r$, the mean-value estimate gives $A(B)\subseteq B$ and $\|A(p)-A(q)\|\leq\kappa\|p-q\|$. The <contraction mapping theorem> supplies exactly one <fixed point> in $B$, and its distance from $p_0$ is at most $\|A(p_0)-p_0\|/(1-\kappa)$.

= Fixed-point stability in the uniform metric
{parent=Contraction mapping theorem}
{title2=$d(x_f,x_g)\leq d_\infty(f,g)/(1-q_f)$}

For two <contraction mappings> of a nonempty <complete metric space>, their <fixed points> obey $d(x_f,x_g)\leq q_fd(x_f,x_g)+d_\infty(f,g)$. Rearranging proves the displayed bound whenever the <uniform metric> is finite. It establishes continuity of the fixed-point map at each $f$ without assuming a contraction constant shared by all maps near $f$.

= Continuity of the fixed-point assignment
{parent=Contraction mapping theorem}
{title2=$d(\tau(f),\tau(g))\le d_\infty(f,g)/(1-q_f)$}

For <contraction mappings> $f,g$, compare their <fixed points> using $f$ on both arguments, then bound the change from $f$ to $g$ by the <uniform metric>. The resulting inequality gives the displayed estimate. Continuity at $f$ only needs its own contraction constant $q_f<1$, even if nearby maps have constants approaching one.

= Contraction property of closed subsets characterizes completeness
{parent=Contraction mapping theorem}

A <metric space> is <complete> if and only if every <contraction mapping> of every nonempty <closed subset> into itself has a <fixed point>. For the converse, a nonconvergent <Cauchy sequence> contains a rapidly approaching subsequence whose point set is closed and whose shift is a contraction without a fixed point. Requiring the property only on the whole space is a different, weaker condition.

= Contraction mapping
{parent=Contraction mapping theorem}

= Contraction
{synonym}

A contraction mapping $T$ on a metric space satisfies $d(Tx,Ty)\leq qd(x,y)$ for every $x,y$ and some constant $0\leq q<1$.

= Contraction property under equivalent metrics
{parent=Contraction mapping}

The same self-map can be a <contraction mapping> for one metric but not for a <bilipschitz equivalent> metric. On three points with distances $d'(x,y)=d'(x,z)=2$, $d'(y,z)=1$, the map $f(x)=y$, $f(y)=f(z)=z$ has Lipschitz constant $1/2$. For the <discrete metric>, its Lipschitz constant is one. The metrics satisfy $d\le d'\le2d$, so they preserve topology and completeness but not the strict numerical contraction condition. A <contraction mapping theorem> fixed-point proof therefore needs the specified metric and its contraction constant.

= Continuous dependence of the fixed point of a uniform contraction
{parent=Contraction mapping theorem}

Suppose $T_\lambda$ are contractions of one complete metric space with a common constant $k<1$, and $T_\lambda x$ depends continuously on $\lambda$ for each fixed $x$. Their fixed points $x_\lambda$ depend continuously on $\lambda$, because for a fixed parameter $\lambda_0$,
$$
d(x_\lambda,x_{\lambda_0})
\leq\frac{d(T_\lambda x_{\lambda_0},T_{\lambda_0}x_{\lambda_0})}{1-k}.
$$

= Iterated contraction
{parent=Contraction mapping theorem}

= Fixed point from a contracting iterate
{synonym}

If a self-map $T$ of a nonempty <complete metric space> has an iterate $T^n$ that is a <contraction mapping>, the <contraction mapping theorem> gives a unique <fixed point> $a$ of $T^n$. The maps commute, so $T^n(Ta)=T(T^na)=Ta$, and uniqueness forces $Ta=a$. Conversely every <fixed point> of $T$ is fixed by $T^n$. Thus $a$ is the unique <fixed point> of $T$.

= Local contraction proof for Newton iteration
{parent=Contraction mapping theorem}

For the Newton map $g(x)=x-f(x)/f'(x)$ near a simple root $r$,
$$
g'(x)=\frac{f(x)f''(x)}{f'(x)^2},
\qquad g'(r)=0.
$$
Bounds $|f'|\geq\delta$ and $|f''|\leq M$ make $|g'|<1$ on a sufficiently small closed interval around $r$. The contraction mapping theorem then gives the unique local fixed point.
