= Biology
{wiki}

<Biology> studies living systems: how <biological cells> maintain themselves, how <organisms> develop and reproduce, and how <evolution> generates diversity. Its scales connect <molecules>, <genes>, <biological cells>, <organisms> and the interactions studied by <ecology>. These are complementary viewpoints: a <protein>'s chemistry can explain a cellular mechanism, while <evolution> explains why inherited variants of that mechanism persist. See \a[https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/1-1-the-science-of-biology][Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: the science of biology]{ref}.

Two organizing ideas make this immense subject navigable: shared cellular organization and descent with inherited modification. A <neuron> and a <gliding bacterium> differ dramatically in shape and behavior, yet both depend on a <cell membrane> and <metabolism>. Their differences become intelligible through specialization and <evolution>.

Keep observations separate from the <mathematical models> used to explain them. A measured <membrane potential> is evidence; an idealized <two-state ion-channel gating> equation proposes a mechanism. A <mathematical model> earns trust through predictions and comparisons with measurements. Its assumptions delimit its usefulness, and disagreement can reveal missing biology rather than a faulty measurement.

= Parasitism
{parent=Ecology}

<Parasitism> is an interaction in which a <parasite> obtains resources from a <host>, reducing some component of the <host>'s performance. Its demographic effects depend on exposure, within-host processes and transmission opportunities.

= Parasitoid
{parent=Parasitism}

A <parasitoid> develops on or in a <host> and eventually kills it. Host-parasitoid transmission therefore has different offspring accounting from ordinary <predation> or a nonlethal parasite-burden model.

= Host
{synonym}

A biological <host> provides the environment or resources used by a <parasite> or other associated organism.

= Parasite
{parent=Parasitism}

A <parasite> is an organism benefiting from <parasitism>. <Microparasite> and <macroparasite> distinguish useful transmission-model descriptions of within-host multiplication and distributed <parasite> burden.

= Macroparasite
{parent=Parasite}

A <macroparasite> is typically modelled using the number of <parasites> carried by each <host>. Adult parasitic worms illustrate why <within-host parasite burden>, developmental stages, mating and <host> exposure heterogeneity matter beyond an infected/not-infected classification.

= Within-host parasite burden
{parent=Macroparasite}
{title2=$j$}

The <within-host parasite burden> is the number of <parasites>, or a specified parasite-load measure, carried by one <host>. A population's <mean> burden and its infected-host <prevalence> are different summaries of the burden distribution.

= Microparasite
{parent=Parasite}

A <microparasite> is typically modelled as multiplying rapidly within a <host>, so infection status rather than the exact organism count is the first between-host state variable. <SIS model>, <SIR model> and <SEIR model> descriptions are examples. Additional within-host structure can still be required.

= Protein
{parent=Biology}

A <protein> is a chain of amino-acid residues whose sequence and conformation determine its biological functions. Some proteins assemble into filaments whose mechanical properties can be described by <biopolymer mechanics>.

= Actin
{parent=Protein}

<Actin> is a <protein> whose monomers assemble into filaments. An idealized actin filament has a bending modulus and persistence length; inferring its effective elastic modulus from those measurements additionally requires a cross-sectional model.

= Morphogen
{parent=Biology}
{wiki}

A <morphogen> is a developmental signal whose spatial concentration influences different <biological cell> fates or behaviors. Transport, production and degradation can establish a concentration gradient; a <morphogen reaction-diffusion equation> is one model of that gradient, not the signal itself.

= Twin
{parent=Biology}
{wiki}

Twins are two offspring from the same gestation. <Monozygotic twins> arise from one fertilized egg, while <dizygotic twins> arise from two. The distinction supplies the biological premise for <twin studies>.

= Biosignature
{parent=Biology}
{wiki}

A <biosignature> is observable chemical, morphological or other evidence attributable to biological activity after assessing plausible abiotic explanations. It can concern present or past life on Earth or elsewhere. An <exoplanet biosignature> is the remote planetary specialization, requiring atmospheric and environmental context.

= Mortality
{parent=Biology}

Mortality describes deaths in a defined population during a specified observation window. A cumulative death <probability> is distinct from a death rate per person-time. Comparing treatments requires the same endpoint definition, time origin and ascertainment in both arms.

= Neuroscience
{parent=Biology}
{wiki}

The study of nervous systems, from the properties of individual <neurons> to circuits, development, behavior and information processing.

= Primary visual cortex
{parent=Neuroscience}
{title2=V1}

An early cortical stage of visual processing containing neurons selective for such properties as position and orientation. Its organization combines developmental constraints and activity-dependent mechanisms.

= Correlation-based development of visual cortical maps
{parent=Primary visual cortex}

Consider afferent activities $z_i$, cortical activity $y_a$ and a constrained <Hebbian learning> rule $\dot w_{ai}=\epsilon\langle y_az_i\rangle-\Lambda_a$. If $y_a=\sum_{b,j}K_{ab}w_{bj}z_j$, then $\langle y_az_i\rangle=\sum_{b,j}K_{ab}w_{bj}C_{ji}$, where $C$ is the input correlation matrix. Thus input correlations and lateral interactions select growing weight modes, while <synaptic normalization> and bounds stop unlimited growth. The model tests how organization can emerge without assigning every final cortical feature in advance.

= Complex cell
{parent=Primary visual cortex}
{wiki}

A <complex cell> typically responds selectively to an oriented edge or bar over a finite spatial region with less dependence on spatial phase and contrast polarity than a <simple cell>. It need not have the simple cell's separated linear ON and OFF subregions. Phase tolerance is local, not invariance to arbitrary displacement outside its <receptive field>.

= Quadrature energy model of a complex cell
{parent=Complex cell}
{title2=$E=(k_e*I)^2+(k_o*I)^2$}

Two oriented linear filters in quadrature have responses $A\cos\varphi$ and $A\sin\varphi$ to a suitably matched narrowband grating. Their squared sum is $A^2$, independent of phase $\varphi$, while orientation and spatial-frequency selectivity remain. A nonlinear output function or normalization can transform this energy into firing rate. Ideal quadrature gives an exact phase identity for the matched stimulus; finite approximate filter pairs need not have perfect invariance for arbitrary images.

= Pooling model of a complex cell
{parent=Complex cell}

Rectify outputs of <simple cells> with similar orientations and varying phases or positions, then sum or pool them. Opposite-polarity subunits allow response to both light and dark features. Pooling gives approximate phase and position tolerance over the subunit coverage; its extent and contrast dependence depend on the pooling rule.

= Ocular dominance column
{parent=Primary visual cortex}
{wiki}

A cortical region whose visual responses preferentially reflect input from one eye. Competitive activity-dependent models can explain segregation of correlated inputs, while anatomical constraints and species differences also matter.

= Activity-dependent ocular dominance segregation
{parent=Ocular dominance column}

Two eye-specific afferent populations compete under <Hebbian learning> and <synaptic normalization>. In a simplified symmetric two-eye model, the eye-difference mode $d_a=w_{aL}-w_{aR}$ evolves with a linear operator containing $K_{ab}(C_{\rm same}-C_{\rm cross})_{ab}$. Dominant spatial modes can produce alternating eye preference, while saturation sets finite contrast. The interaction width, arbor constraints and correlation structure influence the column scale. This is a mechanistic hypothesis whose predictions can be tested, not a unique inference from the existence of columns.

= Orientation selectivity
{parent=Primary visual cortex}
{wiki}

Dependence of a visual neuron's response on stimulus orientation. Elongated ON/OFF receptive-field organization can make aligned bars or edges produce stronger drive than orthogonal ones.

= Orientation-map self-organization
{parent=Orientation selectivity}

Correlation-based learning among spatially arranged ON and OFF inputs, with <synaptic competition> and lateral interactions, can select elongated <simple cell> receptive fields. Neighbor interactions coordinate preferred orientations across cortex, while competition allows different orientations to persist. Selectivity within one cell and a spatial map across many cells are separate predictions, and neither follows from unbounded <Hebbian learning> alone.

= Simple cell
{parent=Primary visual cortex}
{wiki}

A visual cortical neuron with spatially distinct ON and OFF subregions, commonly elongated along a preferred orientation. A signed oriented filter followed by a nonlinearity gives a useful simplified model of its response to bars or gratings.

= Neuron
{parent=Neuroscience}
{wiki}

A specialized <biological cell> that receives and transmits signals. Many neurons integrate graded inputs and communicate through <action potentials> and <synapses>, although neuronal signaling is not limited to spikes.

= Neural adaptation
{parent=Neuron}
{wiki}

Changes in responsiveness during sustained or repeated input. Adaptation makes activity depend on recent stimulation and can invalidate a model whose rate depends only on the present stimulus.

= Synapse
{parent=Neuron}
{wiki}

A signaling junction between a neuron and another cell. Chemical synapses release <neurotransmitters>, which alter the receiving cell's membrane currents; electrical synapses couple cells more directly. Synaptic inputs can favor or oppose subsequent spike generation.

= Synaptic plasticity
{parent=Synapse}
{wiki}

Changes in synaptic efficacy over time. Activity-dependent plasticity can support learning and development, but also requires constraints to prevent unbounded growth or competition in simplified models.

= Synaptic competition
{parent=Synaptic plasticity}

Constraints or interactions make the strengthening of some inputs limit others. In developmental models, competition together with input correlations can segregate ON and OFF subregions or inputs from the two eyes; correlations alone do not specify the full biological developmental mechanism.

= Synaptic normalization
{parent=Synaptic competition}

A learning rule can constrain the sum or norm of incoming synaptic weights to prevent unbounded <Hebbian learning>. For $\sum_iw_{ai}=S$, subtracting the mean unconstrained update from each update preserves the sum. Bounds and renormalization may also be needed to keep excitatory weights nonnegative. Normalization of learning weights is distinct from divisive gain control in instantaneous neural responses.

= Neurotransmitter
{parent=Synapse}
{wiki}

A chemical signal released by a neuron that changes a target cell's activity through its receptors. Its effect depends on the receptor and ionic mechanism rather than being intrinsically excitatory or inhibitory in every context.

= Action potential
{parent=Neuron}
{wiki}

A regenerative transient of <membrane potential> caused by changing membrane conductances. Its roughly stereotyped waveform permits signals to propagate along an <axon>; information can depend on the number and timing of these events rather than their amplitude.

= Neuronal refractory period
{parent=Action potential}

After an <action potential>, channel inactivation and recovery temporarily suppress subsequent firing. An absolute refractory interval forbids another spike; a relative interval requires stronger excitation. A memoryless Poisson spike model omits this dependence on recent spikes.

= Dendrite
{parent=Neuron}
{wiki}

A neuronal process that receives and integrates many inputs. Its geometry and membrane properties can make integration spatially and temporally selective.

= Axon
{parent=Neuron}
{wiki}

A neuronal process that carries signals toward output sites, often by propagation of <action potentials> to <synapses>.

= Computational neuroscience
{parent=Neuroscience}
{wiki}

Mathematical and computational models connect neural mechanisms, measured responses and proposed information-processing functions. Their predictions require comparison with experiments; matching observations does not uniquely identify the biological mechanism.

= Dynamic clamp
{parent=Computational neuroscience}
{wiki}

A <dynamic clamp> measures a real neuron's voltage, updates a conductance model in real time and injects the resulting current, for example $I=g(t)(E-V)$. It implements a virtual conductance without holding the voltage fixed. It can add artificial synapses, couple real neurons into a hybrid network or test how a voltage-dependent conductance affects excitability. Sampling delay and the injection site limit fidelity to spatially distributed biological channels.

= Three-neuron ring stability
{parent=Computational neuroscience}
{title2=$(1+\tau\lambda)^3=\Gamma$}

For a directed three-neuron rate ring $\tau\dot x_i=-x_i+W_{i,i-1}\tanh(\beta x_{i-1})$, the linearization has characteristic equation $(1+\tau\lambda)^3=\Gamma$, where $\Gamma=\beta^3W_{13}W_{32}W_{21}$. Positive loop gain has strict linear asymptotic stability for $\Gamma<1$. Negative loop gain has it for $-8<\Gamma<0$; at $\Gamma=-8$ a complex pair crosses the imaginary axis at frequency $\sqrt3/\tau$. The saturating cubic gives a <supercritical Hopf bifurcation> under an ordinary loop-gain variation. This directed system does not inherit the energy-descent property of a symmetric <Hopfield network>.

= Leaky integrate-and-fire model
{parent=Computational neuroscience}
{title2=$\tau_m\dot V=-(V-V_0)+RI(t)$}

Between spikes, voltage follows a leaky integration equation. Reaching a threshold registers a spike and resets the voltage, optionally followed by a refractory interval. This simplifies large-network simulations while omitting the detailed spike waveform and channel kinetics.

= Constant-input firing rate of a leaky integrate-and-fire neuron
{parent=Leaky integrate-and-fire model}

For $C_m\dot V=-g_L(V-V_L)+I$, reset $V_0<V_\theta$ and no explicit refractory interval, define $I_c=g_L(V_\theta-V_L)$ and $\tau_m=C_m/g_L$. Constant current gives
$$
r(I)=\begin{cases}0,&I\leq I_c,\\\bigl[\tau_m\log((I-g_L(V_0-V_L))/(I-I_c))\bigr]^{-1},&I>I_c.\end{cases}
$$
The threshold is approached only asymptotically at $I=I_c$. At large current, $r\sim I/[C_m(V_\theta-V_0)]$. Adding an absolute <neuronal refractory period> changes the denominator to $T(I)+t_{\rm ref}$ and makes the firing rate saturate rather than grow without bound.

= Hodgkin-Huxley model
{c}
{parent=Computational neuroscience}
{wiki}

A conductance-based model couples <membrane potential> to voltage-dependent channel-gating variables. It explains generation of <action potentials> through regenerative and opposing ionic currents, while its parameters and channel assumptions determine its biological domain of validity.

= Mutually excitatory saturating rate network
{parent=Computational neuroscience}
{title2=$\tau\dot x=-x+g(y),\quad g(z)=ma^2z^2/(\sigma^2+a^2z^2)$}

The symmetric two-neuron rate model has nonnegative activity and saturating recurrent input. Every equilibrium has $x=y$: otherwise monotonicity of $g$ gives a contradiction. With positive parameters it has three distinct equilibria exactly when $am>2\sigma$, namely zero and $z_\pm=(m\pm\sqrt{m^2-4\sigma^2/a^2})/2$. The lower positive equilibrium is a saddle and the origin and upper equilibrium are stable nodes. The resulting two attracting activity levels model a persistent switch rather than a spiking waveform.

= Linear-nonlinear-Poisson cascade model
{parent=Computational neuroscience}
{title2=$r(t)=g(b+(k*s)(t))$}
{wiki}

= LNP model
{c}
{synonym}

A stimulus passes through a linear temporal or spatial filter, then a nonnegative nonlinear rate function. A conditionally <inhomogeneous Poisson process> generates spikes at that rate. The filter and nonlinearity can be fitted from data and tested on held-out trials. Refractoriness and spike-history correlations require extensions beyond this basic cascade.

= Poisson spike-train likelihood fitting
{parent=Linear-nonlinear-Poisson cascade model}
{title2=$\ell=\sum_i\log\lambda(t_i)-\int\lambda(t)\,dt$}

For a stimulus-conditioned <inhomogeneous Poisson process>, the event log-likelihood is the displayed expression. A causal filter expansion $\lambda(t)=\exp[b+\sum_aw_a(s*b_a)(t)]$ can be fitted by <maximum likelihood estimation>, with a smoothness or size penalty and held-out validation. The integral term penalizes predicted spikes that did not occur; fitting only stimulus values at observed spikes omits this essential part.

= Spike-triggered average
{parent=Computational neuroscience}
{title2=$\widehat k(u)=K^{-1}\sum_i s(t_i-u)$}
{wiki}

= STA
{c}
{synonym}

Average the stimulus history preceding each observed spike, after removing the stimulus mean. With white Gaussian stimulation and a suitable one-filter encoding model, the result is proportional to the relevant linear filter. An even nonlinearity can make the average vanish despite strong stimulus dependence, so averaging alone is not universally sufficient.

= Stimulus-correlated spike-triggered averaging
{parent=Spike-triggered average}

For a single linear filter followed by a rate nonlinearity and a Gaussian stimulus-history vector $s$ with covariance $C$, Gaussian integration by parts gives $\mathbb E[s\,g(k^Ts)]\propto Ck$. Thus a <spike-triggered average> estimates $Ck$, rather than $k$ directly, when the stimulus is correlated. Covariance correction, with regularization if necessary, can recover the filter direction. For an even nonlinearity the proportionality factor can vanish; then a one-filter average is insufficient.

= Neural coding
{parent=Computational neuroscience}
{wiki}

How neural responses represent variables relevant to an organism. A coding claim specifies both which response features carry information and how a downstream system can use them.

= Receptive field
{parent=Neural coding}
{wiki}

The region and stimulus features whose variation influences a sensory neuron's response. A visual receptive field may include spatially distinct ON and OFF regions; a linear receptive-field kernel is a signed weighting map, while the full response can be nonlinear and depend on stimulus history.

= Temporal coding
{parent=Neural coding}

Representation by spike timing, latency, phase or relative timing across neurons, beyond a chosen count summary. Its interpretation requires a timing reference and a decoder capable of resolving the relevant precision.

= Rate coding
{parent=Neural coding}

Representation by a neuron's firing frequency or the expected spike count in a chosen time window. The window length and averaging convention matter; equal counts do not imply equal temporal patterns.

= Spike train
{parent=Neural coding}
{title2=$n(t)=\sum_i\delta(t-t_i)$}

A sequence of <action potential> times, represented mathematically as a point process or a sum of <Dirac deltas>. Its event counts, intervals and correlations summarize different aspects of neural activity.

= Time-rescaled spike-train diagnostics
{parent=Spike train}
{title2=$z_i=\int_{t_i}^{t_{i+1}}\lambda(t\mid\mathcal H_t)\,dt$}

For a correctly specified conditional rate, transformed interspike intervals have independent unit-exponential distributions, apart from observation-boundary censoring. Their transforms $1-e^{-z_i}$ should be uniform. Distributional checks and serial-correlation checks assess a fitted spike model on held-out data. A history-dependent model must include past spikes in its conditional rate; stimulus-only <Poisson spike-train likelihood fitting> cannot automatically capture refractoriness.

= Biological cell
{parent=Biology}
{wiki=Cell_(biology)}

A <biological cell> is a membrane-bounded basic unit of living organization. In a cell-level experiment it can be an <experimental unit>, provided that treatment assignment and relevant interactions are handled at that level.

The shared architecture of cellular life combines a selective <cell membrane>, internal <cytoplasm>, inherited information in <DNA>, and <ribosomes> that assemble <proteins>. <Metabolism> supplies transformations of matter and <energy> needed for maintenance and growth. These components form a coupled system: a boundary retains useful <molecules>, <enzymes> organize <chemical reactions>, and inherited information supports rebuilding the machinery. See \a[https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/4-2-prokaryotic-cells][Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: prokaryotic cells]{ref}.

A worked structural comparison: in a typical <prokaryote>, <DNA> occupies a region without a surrounding <cell nucleus>; in a typical nucleated <eukaryote>, much of the <DNA> lies inside a membrane-bounded <cell nucleus>. Both still have a <cell membrane>, <cytoplasm> and <ribosomes>. The extra boundary compartmentalizes processes; it does not create a different basic principle of living organization. See \a[https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/4-3-eukaryotic-cells][Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: eukaryotic cells]{ref}.

\Image[cell-compartments.png]
{title=Shared cellular architecture with different DNA compartmentalization}
{description=Original simplified schematics of a <prokaryote> and a nucleated <eukaryote>. Colors identify corresponding components; sizes and component counts are illustrative, and additional structures are omitted.}

Shared architecture describes cellular life and its developmental machinery, rather than requiring every specialized <biological cell> to retain every component. A mature mammalian <red blood cell>, for example, lacks a <cell nucleus>, although it develops from nucleated precursors. See \a[https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/40-2-components-of-the-blood][Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: components of blood]{ref}. This distinction makes cellular unity compatible with striking specialization.

= Gliding bacterium
{parent=Biological cell}

A <gliding bacterium> moves along a surface without swimming through the bulk fluid. In a thin-fluid-film model, its relative motion dissipates energy through <Couette flow>, although the actuation mechanism can involve additional dissipation.

= Neutrophil
{parent=Biological cell}

A <neutrophil> is an immune <biological cell>. Its adhesive rolling on a surface involves contact mechanics, fluid stresses, and binding and unbinding of adhesive bonds.

= Red blood cell
{parent=Biological cell}

A red blood cell is a specialized <biological cell> that transports <oxygen> in blood. Its deformability lets it pass through vessels narrower than its unstressed size. A compliant-body <lubrication> model isolates the coupling between its deformation, the surrounding viscous film and a driving <pressure> difference; it is an idealization rather than a complete membrane or cytoplasm model.

= Voltage-gated ion channel

A membrane channel whose opening depends on <membrane potential>. Regenerative activation and subsequent inactivation or opposing currents help produce an <action potential>.

= Two-state ion-channel gating
{parent=Voltage-gated ion channel}
{title2=$\dot X=\alpha_X(V)(1-X)-\beta_X(V)X$}

For a gate that switches independently between closed and open states, its open probability obeys the displayed balance equation. At fixed voltage, $X_\infty=\alpha_X/(\alpha_X+\beta_X)$ and $\tau_X=1/(\alpha_X+\beta_X)$. A channel requiring several independent permissive gates to be open has an open probability equal to their product. In the <Hodgkin-Huxley model>, sodium and potassium conductances are $\bar g_{\rm Na}m^3h$ and $\bar g_{\rm K}n^4$ respectively.

= Metabolism
{parent=Biology}
{wiki}

The network of <chemical reactions> that maintains a living system, supplies energy and builds or processes its material. Gas emissions can arise as <primary metabolic byproducts> or <secondary metabolic byproducts>, with different implications for an <atmospheric biosignature gas>.

= Photosynthesis
{parent=Metabolism}
{wiki}

Conversion of light energy into chemical energy used to build biological material. Oxygenic photosynthesis uses <water> as an electron donor and releases <molecular oxygen>; other forms do not necessarily produce oxygen. Its atmospheric oxygen source is a major component of the modern terrestrial <atmospheric biosignature gas> interpretation.

= Secondary metabolic byproduct
{parent=Metabolism}

A compound released through specialized functions such as signaling, defense or stress adaptation rather than the core reactions needed for energy and biomass. <Dimethyl sulfide> and <chloromethane> are examples discussed as atmospheric biosignatures. Their specialized origins may improve specificity while lowering the global emission flux.

= Primary metabolic byproduct
{parent=Metabolism}

A product released by reactions needed for energy acquisition, growth or biomass construction. <Methane> from energy-yielding microbial reactions and <molecular oxygen> from oxygenic <photosynthesis> are examples. Abiotic chemistry can make some of the same molecules, so production mechanism and environmental abundance matter.

= Evolution
{parent=Biology}
{wiki}

<Evolution> is descent with inherited modification. Within a population, changes in <allele frequencies> provide a precise genetic description; over longer histories, branching descent connects populations through <common descent>. An individual growing larger is <biological development>, whereas an inherited variant becoming more frequent across generations is <evolution>. See \a[https://evolution.berkeley.edu/evolution-101/an-introduction-to-evolution/][UC Museum of Paleontology: the definition of evolution]{ref}.

<Mutation> supplies new <alleles>, <genetic recombination> reshuffles inherited material, <natural selection> favors variants with greater reproductive contribution in a specified environment, and <genetic drift> changes frequencies through chance. <Gene flow> moves variants between populations. Consequently, an increasing <allele frequency> alone does not identify <natural selection>. See \a[https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/19-2-population-genetics][Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: population genetics]{ref}.

Evidence includes ordered <fossils>, shared inherited structures and similarities in <DNA>; <phylogenetics> uses such observations to infer relationships. A <phylogenetic tree> is an inferred historical account, with uncertainty about particular branches. See \a[https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/18-1-understanding-evolution][Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: evidence of evolution]{ref}. Evidence also comes from experiments: Blount, Borland and Lenski reported an <Escherichia coli> lineage able to use <citrate> with <oxygen> present by 31,500 generations, and tested historical dependence by replaying <evolution> from stored ancestors. See \a[https://www.blount-lab.org/uploads/2/3/0/3/23037222/2008_pnas_blount_et_al.pdf][Blount, Borland and Lenski, 2008: original experimental report]{ref}. Such observations constrain explanations; no single frequency equation captures every evolutionary process.

= Natural selection
{parent=Evolution}
{wiki}

<Natural selection> involves systematic differences in expected <reproductive success> associated with variation in <phenotype>. For a continuing evolutionary response, the relevant variation must be inherited: offspring must tend to preserve the differences being favored. Actual offspring counts also fluctuate by chance, producing <genetic drift>. Survival matters insofar as it affects contribution to future generations. <Genotype fitness> is therefore contextual, depending on environment and the life cycle being counted, rather than simply measuring strength or longevity. See \a[https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/19-1-population-evolution][Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: population evolution]{ref}.

In chapter IV of his 1859 book, Darwin explained <natural selection> through preservation of favorable variations and greater chances of survival and reproduction. See \a[https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1859_Origin_F373/1859_Origin_F373.html][Darwin, On the Origin of Species, first edition, chapter IV]{ref}. The beautiful consequence is cumulative change without foresight: inherited differences are filtered repeatedly by present conditions. <Natural selection> neither generates a needed <mutation> on demand nor guarantees progress toward a predetermined goal.

= Haploid selection recurrence
{parent=Natural selection}
{title2=$p'=\frac{wp}{wp+(1-p)}$}

Consider an explicitly idealized <haploid> population with two inherited types $A,B$, one <allele> per individual at the tracked <genetic locus>, and discrete, nonoverlapping generations. Assume faithful type transmission, no <mutation> or <gene flow>, a constant environment, and reproductive contributions independent of type frequency. Let the <expected values> of offspring surviving to the next census be $m_A,m_B>0$, with relative <genotype fitness> $w=m_A/m_B$.

For $A$ frequency $p$, expected offspring contributions are proportional to $pm_A$ and $(1-p)m_B$. Normalizing gives the deterministic large-population <mathematical model>
$$
p'=\frac{pm_A}{pm_A+(1-p)m_B}=\frac{wp}{wp+(1-p)}.
$$
The denominator is mean relative <genotype fitness>. For $w=1.2$ and $p_0=0.2$, $p_1=0.24/1.04=3/13\approx0.23077$. Equivalently, 200 $A$ and 800 $B$ parents with $m_A=1.2,m_B=1$ contribute expected counts 240 and 800. Fractional per-parent <expected values> are averages, not literal fractional offspring. The ratio of these expectations is a deterministic approximation, not generally the expectation of a random ratio.

The odds multiply particularly simply:
$$
\frac{p_{t+1}}{1-p_{t+1}}=w\frac{p_t}{1-p_t},\qquad p_t=\frac{w^tp_0}{1-p_0+w^tp_0}.
$$
Thus $p_{10}\approx0.60753$: repeated modest advantage can substantially change composition. For $w>1$ and $0<p<1$, $p'-p=(w-1)p(1-p)/(1+(w-1)p)>0$. The deterministic boundary $p=0$ remains unchanged: <natural selection> needs existing variation.

= Identical by descent
{synonym}

Allele copies are identical by descent if they derive from the same specified ancestral copy. Identical observed states need not imply this ancestry. = Sibling IBD ascertainment under multiplicative penetrance
{parent=Identity by descent}
{title2=$(z_0,z_1,z_2)=\dfrac{(\alpha^4,2\alpha^2\beta,\beta^2)}{(\alpha^2+\beta)^2}$}

For <full siblings> in an <outbred pedigree>, condition on both being affected under <multiplicative diallelic penetrance> and on residual disease outcomes being independent given their <genotypes>. The usual <Mendelian IBD sharing of full siblings> probabilities $(1/4,1/2,1/4)$ are weighted by $p^2\alpha^4,p^2\alpha^2\beta,p^2\beta^2$, respectively, giving the displayed conditional sharing probabilities. Since $\beta-\alpha^2=\phi(1-\phi)(\theta-1)^2\geq0$, $z_1\leq1/2$, strictly for a polymorphic locus with a nontrivial effect.

= Jacquard identity coefficients
{parent=Identity by descent}
{c}
{title2=$\Delta_1,\ldots,\Delta_9$}

For two individuals carrying labelled copies $(a,b)$ and $(c,d)$, condensed identity coefficients give the <probabilities> of nine <identity by descent> patterns, identifying states that differ only by exchanging the two homologues within either individual. They retain within-individual <autozygosity>, which three ordinary outbred sharing coefficients omit. One convention is
$$
\begin{array}{c|l}
1&abcd\\2&ab\mid cd\\3&abc\mid d\text{ or }abd\mid c\\4&ab\mid c\mid d\\5&acd\mid b\text{ or }bcd\mid a\\6&cd\mid a\mid b\\7&ac\mid bd\text{ or }ad\mid bc\\8&\text{one cross-individual pair, two singletons}\\9&a\mid b\mid c\mid d
\end{array}
$$
Each block consists of copies identical by descent, and different blocks have distinct ancestral origins. For siblings whose parents are otherwise outbred <first cousins>, these coefficients are $(1,0,2,1,2,1,15,30,12)/64$. This follows by conditioning on whether the parents share one ancestral copy and enumerating the four independent <Mendelian segregation> choices to their two children. The <kinship coefficient> is $\Delta_1+(\Delta_3+\Delta_5+\Delta_7)/2+\Delta_8/4$, here $9/32$. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2585748/[Weir's discussion of identity states] uses this convention.

= Identity-by-descent sharing of inbred full siblings
{parent=Jacquard identity coefficients}
{title2=$(P(J=0),P(J=1),P(J=2))=(7/32,17/32,1/4)$}

Let $J$ be the largest number of chromosome copies that can be paired across the two <full siblings> as <identical by descent>, counting repeated copies when a child is <autozygous>. For offspring of otherwise outbred <first cousins>, with probability $3/4$ the parental copies have no common ancestral origin, giving ordinary sibling sharing $(1/4,1/2,1/4)$. With probability $1/4$, write the parental copies as $(A,B)$ and $(A,C)$ with three distinct origins. Each child independently has one of $AA,AB,AC,BC$, equally likely. Among sixteen ordered child pairs, two share no copy, ten share one, and four share two. The mixture gives the displayed probabilities. These three numbers do not determine all <Jacquard identity coefficients>; the formula $\phi=\mathbb E[J]/4$ is restricted to noninbred individuals and fails here.

= First-cousin identity-by-descent probabilities
{parent=Identity by descent}
{title2=$(k_0,k_1,k_2)=(3/4,1/4,0)$}

For <first cousins> in an otherwise <outbred pedigree>, compare the copies transmitted from their related parents, who are <full siblings>. These transmissions trace to the same shared grandparent with <probability> $1/2$ and then to the same homologous copy of that grandparent with probability $1/2$, giving $k_1=1/4$. The unrelated parental contributions cannot match, so two shared copies have probability zero. Selecting one random copy from each cousin chooses those two potentially shared copies with probability $1/4$, giving <kinship coefficient> $k_1/4=1/16$.

= Identical-by-descent segment
{parent=Identity by descent}

= IBD segment
{synonym}
{c}

An identical-by-descent segment is a chromosomal tract inherited from the same ancestral chromosome without an intervening breakpoint in the tracked ancestry. With a specified total number $m$ of independent <meioses> and a uniform recombination map, the probability that a fixed tract of genetic length $d$ survives without <genetic recombination> is approximately $e^{-md}$. Recent common ancestry therefore tends to give longer shared tracts. Lengths of tracts selected by covering a random point are length-biased relative to tracts sampled by their boundaries.

= Kinship coefficient
{parent=Identity by descent}
{title2=$\phi_{XY}$}

The kinship coefficient $\phi_{XY}$ is the <probability> that one randomly selected <allele> copy from each of $X,Y$ is <identical by descent>. For a noninbred individual, $\phi_{XX}=1/2$, and more generally $\phi_{XX}=(1+F_X)/2$. For distinct individuals with parents $M_X,P_X$, <Mendelian segregation> gives $\phi_{XY}=[\phi_{M_XY}+\phi_{P_XY}]/2$. The offspring's <inbreeding coefficient> equals the parents' kinship coefficient.

= Finite-population chromosomal coancestry
{parent=Kinship coefficient}
{title2=$\Phi_{G+1}=\Phi_G+\dfrac{1+F_G-2\Phi_{m,G}}{4(N-1)}$}

For $N$ individuals in monogamous families of exactly two offspring, let $\Phi_G$ be <mean> pairwise <kinship coefficient>, $F_G$ <mean> inbreeding and $\Phi_{m,G}$ <mean> kinship of mates. A random distinct offspring pair are siblings with <probability> $1/(N-1)$. Accounting separately for within-family and cross-family pairs gives the displayed recurrence, while $F_{G+1}=\Phi_{m,G}$. Random mating makes $\Phi_{m,G}=\Phi_G$. Sibling avoidance changes the mating-pair <mean>; it does not create unrelated founders or stop eventual drift to ancestral identity in a closed finite population.

= Sex-linked cousin kinship
{parent=Kinship coefficient}
{title2=$F_X\in\{0,1/8,3/16\}$}

For otherwise outbred opposite-sex <first cousins>, the <probability> that the mother's randomly transmitted X copy matches the father's X by descent depends on which of their parents are siblings. It is zero when the male cousin is related through his father, $1/8$ when the female cousin is related through her father and the male through his mother, and $3/16$ when their mothers are sisters. Daughters can be X-autozygous; sons have one X and a Y and are not homozygous for a paired X chromosome. Recombination and pseudoautosomal regions require a locus-based formulation instead of whole-chromosome blocks.

= Mendelian IBD sharing of full siblings
{parent=Identity by descent}
{c}
{title2=$J\sim\operatorname{Bin}(2,1/2)$}

In an <outbred pedigree>, the two <full siblings> receive the same paternal copy with probability $1/2$, and independently the same maternal copy with probability $1/2$. Thus the number $J$ of shared copies has a <binomial distribution> with probabilities $(1/4,1/2,1/4)$, and $E[J/2]=1/2$.

<Autozygosity> is <homozygosity> by descent: the maternal and paternal copies in one individual derive from the same ancestral copy. Unlike ordinary <homozygosity>, it concerns the ancestry of chromosome copies rather than only their observed states. In a <pedigree> with one ancestral disease-allele copy and fully penetrant <recessive inheritance>, every affected individual is <autozygous> for that copy.

= Genetic association
{parent=Genetics}
{wiki}

A genetic association is a statistical dependence between a <genetic variant> and a <phenotype>. An associated marker need not be causal: <linkage disequilibrium> can correlate it with a causal locus, and <population stratification> can produce an association without such a link.

= Genotype relative risk
{parent=Genetic association}
{title2=$R_g=P(D\mid g)/P(D\mid g_0)$}

= Genotypic relative risk
{synonym}

The genotype relative risk compares the <penetrance> for <genotype> $g$ with that of a chosen baseline $g_0$. The common baseline factor cancels when computing <conditional probabilities> of transmitted genotypes given an affected offspring. It is a ratio of disease probabilities, unlike an <odds ratio>.

= Disease-ascertained transmission probability
{parent=Genotype relative risk}
{title2=$P(g\mid g_f,g_m,D)=\frac{q_gR_g}{\sum_hq_hR_h}$}

Let $q_g=P(g\mid g_f,g_m)$ be the <Mendelian segregation> probability, and assume the child's disease status depends on the parental <genotypes> only through its own genotype, with <penetrance> proportional to $R_g$. <Bayes theorem> gives the displayed conditional law. For a diallelic parent pair there are four equally likely labelled transmission outcomes. If an unordered genotype occurs in $m_g$ of them, $q_g=m_g/4$, so its probability is $m_gR_g/\sum_hm_hR_h$. Dropping multiplicities would, for example, misweight a heterozygous child of two heterozygotes.

= Haplotype-relative-risk
{parent=Genetic association}
{wiki}
{title2=$\widehat{\mathrm{OR}}=\frac{wz}{xy}$}

The haplotype-relative-risk design compares transmitted <alleles> to the corresponding untransmitted parental copies, used as internal controls for an affected offspring. If allele-1/allele-2 transmitted counts are $w,x$ and untransmitted counts are $y,z$, the allele-1 <odds ratio> estimate is $wz/(xy)$. An unmatched <Pearson chi-squared test of independence> treats this as a two-by-two table. The <transmission disequilibrium test> instead keeps parent-specific pairing and uses only discordant transmissions. The sampling assumptions of the unmatched calculation and any missing-parent reconstruction must be justified separately.

= Association mapping
{parent=Genetic association}
{wiki}

Association mapping localizes genetic influences on a <phenotype> by testing population <genetic association> with markers. <Linkage disequilibrium> allows a typed marker to tag a nearby causal variant; historical <genetic recombination> can narrow the associated interval. <Population stratification> can produce association without a local causal relation.

= Linkage-disequilibrium marker tagging
{parent=Association mapping}

An observed <genetic marker> tags an unobserved causal <genetic variant> when <linkage disequilibrium> correlates their allele indicators. Their squared <allelic correlation> $r^2$ measures prediction strength. Under standard local-alternative score-test approximations, using the marker rather than the causal allele reduces the noncentrality parameter by roughly $r^2$, suggesting a sample-size multiplier about $1/r^2$. This is an approximation requiring the same disease model and sampling design, not a universal power identity.

= Transmission disequilibrium test
{parent=Genetic association}
{wiki}
{title2=$\chi^2_{\mathrm{TDT}}=\frac{(b-c)^2}{b+c}$}

= TDT
{synonym}
{c}

For affected offspring and heterozygous parents at a diallelic locus, let $b$ count transmissions of allele 1 with allele 2 untransmitted and $c$ count the reverse. Under the fair-transmission null, conditional on $b+c$, $b$ is binomial with probability $1/2$. The asymptotic statistic is $(b-c)^2/(b+c)$, with a one-degree-of-freedom chi-squared null distribution. Homozygous-parent transmissions give no contrast. Conditioning within families protects this test from ordinary <population stratification>.

= Incomplete-trio transmission-selection bias
{parent=Transmission disequilibrium test}

If one parent is <heterozygous> and the other parent's <genotype> is missing, a homozygous child reveals the observed parent's transmitted <allele>, whereas a heterozygous child need not. Selecting only revealed transmissions can therefore bias the <transmission disequilibrium test>. For allele-1 frequency $p$ under random mating and fair transmission, the probabilities of an allele-1 or allele-2 homozygous child from an observed heterozygous parent are $p/2$ and $(1-p)/2$. Among selected homozygous children, the observed allele-1 transmission probability is $p$, not generally $1/2$. A complete-trio analysis avoids this reconstruction rule; methods retaining missing-parent families need an explicit <likelihood function> or a valid conditional adjustment. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3297829/[Family-based association tests using genotype data with uncertainty] discusses this bias.

= Family pseudo-control genotype
{parent=Transmission disequilibrium test}

Combine the untransmitted maternal and paternal alleles into a hypothetical control <genotype>, paired with the child's transmitted genotype. Even when two heterozygous parents have a heterozygous child and parent-specific phase is ambiguous, the combined untransmitted genotype is unambiguously heterozygous. Such pseudo-controls are matched family controls, not independent random individuals; <Hardy-Weinberg principle> is therefore not guaranteed merely by this construction.

= Transmitted and untransmitted alleles under multiplicative penetrance
{parent=Family pseudo-control genotype}

Under random mating and <Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium>, label each parental allele by whether it is transmitted. Let $(A,B)$ and $(C,D)$ be the transmitted/untransmitted pairs of two parents. Before ascertainment all four draws are independent with population frequencies $\pi$. If offspring disease weights the transmitted pair by $\psi_A\psi_C$, then
$$
\mathbb P(A=a,B=b,C=c,D=d\mid\text{offspring disease})=\pi_a^*\pi_c^*\pi_b\pi_d.
$$
The transmitted pair is an affected-case genotype and the untransmitted pair a population <family pseudo-control genotype> under this homogeneous model. For unordered genotypes, compatible labelled transmissions must be summed. Conditioning on parental genotypes instead removes nuisance population frequencies and gives the usual <transmission disequilibrium test>.

= Genetic linkage
{parent=Genetics}
{wiki}

Genetic linkage is dependence of transmission at loci on the same chromosome, measured in a two-locus model by a <recombination fraction> below $1/2$. Linkage analysis uses co-segregation within <pedigrees>; <genetic association> instead compares allele or genotype frequencies with phenotype.

= Genetic map
{parent=Genetic linkage}

A genetic map orders <genetic loci> by <genetic linkage> and describes distances inferred from <genetic recombination>. A <recombination fraction> is a probability of a recombinant transmission; map distances count expected exchanges and can be added along a chromosome, whereas recombination fractions generally cannot. Whole-chromosome identity requires the linkage and recombination model, whereas single-locus <kinship coefficients> depend only on descent at that locus.

= Nonparametric linkage analysis
{parent=Genetic linkage}

Nonparametric linkage analysis tests excess <identity by descent> among relatives with a shared <phenotype>, without specifying its complete <penetrance> model or disease-allele frequency. Its null still uses <Mendelian segregation> and the specified <pedigree>.

= Mean allele-sharing test for affected siblings
{parent=Nonparametric linkage analysis}
{title2=$Z=(\bar J-1)/\sqrt{1/(2n)}$}

For independent pairs of affected <full siblings> with unrelated, noninbred parents and a fully informative <genetic marker>, the no-linkage null gives shared <identity by descent> count $J\sim\operatorname{Bin}(2,1/2)$. Therefore $EJ=1$ and $\operatorname{Var}J=1/2$. An excess-sharing test uses the displayed approximately <standard normal> statistic and an upper-tail critical value. Pairs sharing family members are dependent, so their variance needs separate treatment. This targeted test differs from an omnibus test of all three sharing probabilities.

= Affected-sib-pair analysis
{parent=Nonparametric linkage analysis}

An affected-sib-pair analysis examines marker <identity by descent> between affected <full siblings>. Without <genetic linkage> to the disease locus, the probabilities of sharing zero, one and two copies are $1/4,1/2,1/4$. Excess sharing supplies evidence for linkage under disease models that preferentially transmit a common risk allele to both siblings.

= Recessive affected-sib-pair likelihood
{parent=Affected-sib-pair analysis}
{title2=$L_0=u^2,\ L_1=uv,\ L_2=v^2$}

For unaffected <genetic carriers> of a disease with complete <penetrance> and <recessive inheritance>, suppose parental marker-disease <haplotype> phases are equally likely and the marker is fully informative. Write $u=\theta(1-\theta)$ and $v=1/2-u$, where $\theta$ is the <recombination fraction>. Conditional on sharing a parental marker copy, the probability that both siblings inherit that parent's disease allele is $[(1-\theta)^2+\theta^2]/2=v$; conditional on inheriting different marker copies it is $\theta(1-\theta)=u$. Independent paternal and maternal transmissions therefore give $L_0=u^2,L_1=uv,L_2=v^2$. Conditional instead on both siblings being affected, the IBD probabilities are $s^2,2s(1-s),(1-s)^2$, where $s=2u\in[0,1/2]$.

// Target: biology.bigb

= Allele-sharing sufficiency for recessive linkage
{parent=Recessive affected-sib-pair likelihood}

For independent affected sib pairs with IBD counts $n_0,n_1,n_2$, the <likelihood function> of the <recombination fraction> is proportional to $s^{2n_0+n_1}(1-s)^{2n_2+n_1}$, with $s=2\theta(1-\theta)$. At fixed sample size, the total shared-copy count $2n_2+n_1$ is a <sufficient statistic> by the <Fisher-Neyman factorization theorem>. Thus a one-sided <likelihood-ratio test> has the same ordering as a test for excess mean IBD sharing. This equivalence depends on the stated fully penetrant recessive model and does not extend to arbitrary disease models.

= Parametric linkage analysis
{parent=Genetic linkage}

Parametric linkage analysis forms a <pedigree likelihood> from a specified disease model, including <penetrance>, <allele> frequencies and <recessive inheritance> or another transmission model. It estimates or tests the <recombination fraction> between a disease locus and a <genetic marker>.

= Complete genetic linkage
{parent=Genetic linkage}
{title2=$\theta=0$}

= Complete linkage
{synonym}

<Complete linkage> in a two-locus model means <recombination fraction> zero: only parental <haplotype> combinations are transmitted. Multiple <genetic markers> in a nonrecombining block represent one <haplotype> segregation observation.

= Homozygosity mapping
{parent=Genetic linkage}

Homozygosity mapping searches for shared homozygous marker regions in individuals with a rare recessive condition, often in an <inbred> pedigree. Under a single ancestral disease copy, affected individuals inherit two copies identical by descent. Marker homozygosity alone gives <identity by state>, so founder haplotype frequencies matter. If a particular haplotype has founder frequency $p$ and $K$ distinct founder chromosome copies underlie all selected descendant copies, their marker-pattern probability is $E[p^K]$. For complete linkage and a single disease copy, the affected-only numerator is $p$, yielding a <LOD score> $\log_{10}(p/E[p^K])$ under linkage-equilibrium founder assumptions.

= LOD score
{parent=Genetic linkage}
{c}
{title2=$Z(\theta)=\log_{10}[L(\theta)/L(1/2)]$}

The LOD score compares the likelihood at recombination fraction $\theta$ with independent assortment:
$$
Z(\theta)=\log_{10}\frac{L(\theta)}{L(1/2)}.
$$
When phenotype likelihood is independent of $\theta$, it can be computed from marker probabilities conditional on phenotype and ascertainment. Positive values support linkage; the observed marker pattern must be specified precisely because affected-only and affected-plus-unaffected observations give different likelihoods.

= Genetic recombination
{parent=Genetics}
{wiki}

Genetic recombination produces a chromosome whose inherited segments come from different parental homologues. In a two-locus meiosis, the <recombination fraction> is the probability that the transmitted combination is recombinant relative to the parental phase.

= Four-gamete test
{parent=Genetic recombination}
{wiki}

Under an <infinite sites mutation model>, two variable sites on one unrecombined rooted tree have nested or disjoint derived-allele clades. At most three of the four biallelic <haplotypes> can then occur. Observation of all four haplotypes proves that the single-tree, single-mutation-per-site model cannot hold: some ancestral <genetic recombination> is necessary if recurrent mutation is excluded. The test gives no proof of absence of recombination when only three haplotypes occur.

= Recombination hotspot
{parent=Genetic recombination}
{wiki}

A recombination hotspot is a short region with an elevated local rate of <genetic recombination>. Repeated ancestral breakpoints there can separate neighboring <haplotype blocks> of strong <linkage disequilibrium>.

// Target: biology.bigb

= Recombination fraction
{parent=Genetic recombination}
{title2=$0\le\theta\le\tfrac12$}

The recombination fraction $\theta$ is the probability of a recombinant transmission between two loci in an ordinary two-locus linkage model. Complete linkage has $\theta=0$; independent assortment has $\theta=1/2$. Several physical crossovers can restore parental phase, so recombination fraction is not the expected number of crossovers.

= Pedigree
{parent=Genetics}

A pedigree records parent–offspring relationships. A <founder in a pedigree> has no recorded parents; all other individuals have a genotype conditional on their parental genotypes. Inbreeding can produce loops in the undirected diagram while the directed ancestry remains acyclic.

= Outbred pedigree
{parent=Pedigree}

For calculations of <identity by descent>, an outbred pedigree has unrelated, noninbred <founders in a pedigree>, and no additional ancestral relationships beyond those explicitly specified. Founder gene copies are treated as distinct ancestral copies even if they have <identity by state>.

= First cousin
{parent=Pedigree}

First cousins are children of <full siblings>. An otherwise outbred first-cousin pair shares two noninbred grandparents and has <kinship coefficient> $1/16$.

// Target: biology.bigb

= Full sibling
{parent=Pedigree}

Full siblings share both parents. With unrelated, noninbred <founders in a pedigree>, the two parental contributions give independent opportunities for <identity by descent>.

= Pedigree likelihood
{parent=Pedigree}

For latent <genotypes> $G_i$ and observed <phenotypes> $X_i$, assume conditional phenotype independence, independent founder genotypes, and conditionally independent Mendelian transmissions. The <likelihood function> is
$$
\sum_{(G_i)}\prod_i P(X_i\mid G_i)\prod_{i\in F}P(G_i)\prod_{i\notin F}P(G_i\mid G_{m(i)},G_{f(i)}).
$$
This follows by the chain rule applied to the acyclic ancestry graph, then the law of total probability over all latent genotypes. <Penetrance> supplies the phenotype factors and <genetic recombination> supplies multilocus transmission factors.

= Phase averaging in a linkage likelihood
{parent=Pedigree likelihood}
{title2=$L(\theta)=\sum_s\pi_sL_s(\theta)$}

An unphased multilocus <genotype> can have several pairs of <haplotypes>. Its <pedigree likelihood> must average over these possible phases with prior weights $\pi_s$, rather than maximize over phases after observing the offspring. Under <linkage disequilibrium>, the weights need not be equal. They can be obtained by conditioning population haplotype frequencies on the observed parental <genotype>; different choices of what is conditioned on change only parameter-independent factors when those frequencies are held fixed.

= Score equation for a phase-averaged linkage likelihood
{parent=Phase averaging in a linkage likelihood}

For a known-phase contribution $L_1(\theta)\propto\theta^{r_1}(1-\theta)^{m_1-r_1}$ and an independent phase-averaged contribution $L_2(\theta)$, an interior maximum satisfies
$$
[r_1-m_1\theta]L_2(\theta)+\theta(1-\theta)L_2'(\theta)=0.
$$
This follows by differentiating $\log L_1+\log L_2$ and multiplying by $\theta(1-\theta)L_2$. The constrained <maximum likelihood estimate> on $[0,1/2]$ must also be compared with the endpoints. An interior root cannot be asserted without the observed pedigree counts; complete nonrecombinant data, for example, have their maximum at zero.

= Pedigree graphical model
{parent=Pedigree likelihood}

Assign one <genotype> <random variable> to each person in a <pedigree>. Independent <founders in a pedigree> carry population genotype laws, and each other genotype has its <conditional distribution> given its two parents from <Mendelian segregation>. The ancestry <directed acyclic graph> is a <Bayesian network> for the joint genotype law. Its <moral graph> contains each parent-child edge and each coparent edge. Each founder factor and each child-with-parents factor belongs to a <clique>, giving an undirected graphical representation. The graph is an independence representation; equality with the minimal <conditional independence graph> needs additional faithfulness assumptions, particularly because inheritance can produce zero probabilities.

= Elston-Stewart algorithm
{parent=Pedigree likelihood}
{c}

Pedigree peeling eliminates latent <genotypes> by moving sums inside products whenever the other factors do not depend on the eliminated genotype. For one parent genotype $g$ and one child genotype $h$, the likelihood becomes
$$
\sum_g P(g)P(X_p\mid g)\left[\sum_hP(h\mid g)P(X_c\mid h)\right].
$$
The inner bracket is the child's message to its parent. With two states each, it needs four multiplications and two additions; multiplying the two parent weights and messages needs four more multiplications and the final sum one addition. The unrearranged four terms instead use twelve multiplications. Larger pedigrees use the same variable-elimination identity, with additional handling of pedigree loops.

= Pedigree founder
{synonym}

A founder is an individual whose parents are outside the recorded <pedigree>. The standard <pedigree likelihood> assigns unrelated founders independent genotype distributions based on population allele or haplotype frequencies. = Recessive inheritance
{parent=Genetics}

In a fully penetrant autosomal recessive model with normal allele $A$ and disease allele $a$, $aa$ is affected and $AA,Aa$ are unaffected. A carrier cross $Aa\times Aa$ has offspring probabilities $1/4,1/2,1/4$ for $AA,Aa,aa$, respectively, from the two independent parental meioses.

= Sibling recurrence risk
{parent=Recessive inheritance}

Sibling recurrence risk is the <conditional probability> that another child has the disease, given an affected sibling. For unaffected parents, complete <penetrance> and a single recessive disease allele, the affected child identifies both parents as <genetic carriers>. Independent <Mendelian segregation> then gives recurrence probability $1/4$, whether or not the parents are related. Incomplete penetrance, phenocopies, different causal loci or affected parents change this calculation.

= Genetic carrier
{parent=Recessive inheritance}

A genetic <genetic carrier> has one disease-associated recessive <allele> and one normal <allele> at a <genetic locus>, and is unaffected under a fully penetrant recessive model. Two <genetic carriers> have affected-offspring <probability> $1/4$ under <Mendelian segregation>; a <genetic carrier> and an individual with no disease <allele> have affected-offspring <probability> zero under that same mutation-free model.

= Phenotype
{parent=Genetics}
{wiki}

A phenotype is an observable characteristic of an individual, arising from its <genotype>, environment and other influences. In a <pedigree likelihood>, the conditional phenotype probability is specified given the individual's underlying genotype.

= Penetrance
{parent=Phenotype}
{wiki}

Penetrance is the probability of an expressed <phenotype> conditional on a specified <genotype>. It can depend on age, sex and environment. A fully penetrant recessive model assigns disease probability one to the recessive homozygote and zero to other genotypes; incomplete penetrance or phenocopies replace these indicators by intermediate probabilities.

= Genetic variance of penetrance
{parent=Penetrance}
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}\{\mathbb E(Y\mid G)\}$}

For a binary disease trait $Y$ with <genotype> $G$, the genetic variance of <penetrance> is the between-genotype term in the <law of total variance>. The averaged within-genotype term is $\mathbb E[q_G(1-q_G)]$. Under <multiplicative diallelic penetrance>, the genetic term is $p^2(\beta^2-\alpha^4)$. This decomposition attributes variance to the modeled locus, not automatically to all hereditary causes.

= Multiplicative diallelic penetrance
{parent=Penetrance}
{title2=$q_G=p\theta^G,\quad G\sim\operatorname{Bin}(2,\phi)$}

With <Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium>, let $G$ count copies of the risk <allele>. The <penetrance> $p\theta^G$ must lie in $[0,1]$ for every possible <genotype>. Writing $\alpha=1-\phi+\phi\theta$ and $\beta=1-\phi+\phi\theta^2$ gives $\mathbb E(q_G)=p\alpha^2$ and $\mathbb E(q_G^2)=p^2\beta^2$.

= Genotype
{parent=Genetics}
{wiki}

A genotype records the inherited <alleles> carried by an individual at one or more <genetic loci>. For a diploid autosomal locus it is an unordered allele pair unless parental origin or chromosome phase is specified.

= Genotyping error
{parent=Genotype}

A <genotyping error> is an incorrect observed <allele> or <genotype> call. A probabilistic observation model replaces the deterministic <genetic marker> factor by $P(X=x\mid G=g)$, allowing nonzero <probability> of an observed call inconsistent with the latent <genotype>. Such error can bias family transmission tests and linkage <likelihoods>.

= Heterozygosity
{parent=Genotype}

= Heterozygous
{synonym}

<Heterozygosity> means that the two <allele> states at a diploid <genetic locus> differ; an individual with that property is a <heterozygote>. <Heterozygous> parental <genotypes> provide alternative <alleles> for a <transmission disequilibrium test>.

= Homozygosity
{parent=Genotype}

= Homozygous
{synonym}

<Homozygosity> means that the two <allele> states at a diploid <genetic locus> are the same; an individual with that property is a <homozygote>. Observed <homozygosity> does not by itself establish <identity by descent>.

= Heterozygote
{parent=Genotype}

A heterozygote carries different <alleles> at a diploid locus. Under ordinary Mendelian segregation each is transmitted to an offspring with probability $1/2$. Only heterozygous parents provide a comparison between alternative transmissions in the <transmission disequilibrium test>.

= Homozygote
{parent=Genotype}

A homozygote carries two identical <alleles> at a locus; a <heterozygote> carries different alleles. Homozygosity of an observed <haplotype> is identity by state, and need not be <identity by descent>.

= Haplotype
{parent=Genotype}
{wiki}

A haplotype is the set of <alleles> on one chromosome across specified <genetic loci>. Closely linked markers without intervening <genetic recombination> are transmitted as one haplotype; they are not independent observations of inheritance.

= Haplotype block
{parent=Haplotype}
{wiki}

A haplotype block is a chromosomal region with limited historical <genetic recombination>, strong local <linkage disequilibrium> and comparatively few common <haplotypes>. Its definition is operational and population-dependent; it does not mean that recombination never occurs within it.

= Phase-known genotype
{parent=Genotype}

At several <genetic loci>, a phase-known genotype specifies which <alleles> occur together on each chromosome, hence its two <haplotypes>. An unphased multilocus <genotype> may be compatible with several haplotype pairs. For two heterozygous loci, $AB/ab$ and $Ab/aB$ have identical unphased genotypes but different transmission probabilities when <genetic linkage> is present.

= Genetic locus
{parent=Genetics}

= Locus (genetics)
{synonym}

A locus is a position or region of a genome. An <allele> specifies an inherited state there; a <haplotype> specifies states at several loci on one chromosome.

= Allele
{parent=Genetics}
{wiki}

An allele is one of the alternative inherited states at a <genetic locus>. A diploid individual normally carries two copies at an autosomal locus, one inherited from each parent.

= Allele frequency
{parent=Allele}
{title2=$p_A$}

The allele frequency is the proportion of gene copies at a specified <genetic locus> carrying an <allele>. Two unrelated copies can have the same observed allele without being <identical by descent>; under independent sampling their matching probability is the sum of squared allele frequencies. Pedigree <autozygosity> instead traces copies to the same ancestral copy.

= Allele fixation
{parent=Allele frequency}
{title2=$u=0\text{ or }u=1$}

An <allele> fixes when all copies at the relevant locus in the population are that <allele>. Fixation can occur through selection or <genetic drift>. Spatial polymorphism means that fixation within an individual habitat need not imply fixation across a <metapopulation>.

= Population genetics
{parent=Genetics}
{wiki}

Population genetics studies the frequencies of <alleles> and <genotypes> in populations, and their changes through inheritance, mutation, selection, migration and random sampling. <Kingman's coalescent> describes a neutral sample's ancestry backward in time.

= Heterozygote advantage
{parent=Population genetics}

A <heterozygote> has greater <genotype fitness> than either corresponding <homozygote>. In the deterministic random-mating two-allele viability-selection model this gives an attracting interior <allele frequency>, unlike the repelling interior frequency produced by <underdominance>.

= Underdominance
{parent=Population genetics}
{wiki}

= Heterozygote disadvantage
{synonym}

<Underdominance> means that the <heterozygote> has lower <genotype fitness> than both corresponding <homozygotes>. In a deterministic randomly mating biallelic population, it produces an unstable interior allele-frequency threshold separating the two fixation outcomes, unlike <heterozygote advantage>.

= Underdominant allele-frequency dynamics
{parent=Underdominance}
{title2=$\dot u=u(1-u)[(a+b)u-b]$}

With <homozygote> fitness advantages $a,b>0$ over the <heterozygote>, the <allele frequency> $u$ of the first <allele> has an unstable threshold $b/(a+b)$. Below it that <allele> decreases; above it it increases. In a discrete viability-selection generation the same numerator gives $u'-u$ after division by <mean> <genotype fitness>. Equal <homozygote> advantages give $\dot u=su(1-u)(2u-1)$, with stable endpoints and unstable midpoint.

= Genotype fitness
{parent=Population genetics}
{title2=$w$}

<Genotype fitness> measures the reproductive or viability success assigned to a <genotype> in a specified selection model. Relative fitnesses weight <Hardy-Weinberg proportions> after viability selection; continuous-time fitness differences instead act as selection-rate differences. The convention and time units must be specified before comparing selection with migration.

= Ancestral recombination graph
{parent=Population genetics}
{wiki}

= ARG
{synonym}
{c}

An ancestral recombination graph records ancestral coalescences and <genetic recombination> events for sampled chromosomal material. Backwards in time, each pair of ancestral lineages merges at rate one in standard diploid coalescent units, while a lineage splits at a recombination breakpoint at rate $\rho/2$ times the genetic span of the material it carries. The breakpoint splits that lineage's ancestral material into two parental contributions. Waiting times are exponential with the sum of the current event rates. At each genomic position, retaining the ancestry at that position gives a marginal <phylogenetic tree> with the neutral <Kingman's coalescent> distribution. Different positions have dependent trees because they share parts of the graph.

A population bottleneck is a temporary large reduction in population size. It changes ancestral coalescence rates and can leave fewer ancestral <haplotypes> and increased <linkage disequilibrium> after the population expands.

Genetic drift is random variation of <allele> frequencies due to finite-population reproduction. In the neutral ancestry description, randomly sampled gene copies merge into common ancestors according to <Kingman's coalescent>.

Effective population size is the size of an idealized population giving the relevant rate of <genetic drift> or ancestral coalescence in the population being modeled. = Segregating site
{parent=Population genetics}
{title2=$S$}

A segregating site is a position at which at least two states occur in the sampled chromosomes. Under an <infinite sites mutation model>, mutations below the sample's common ancestor correspond one-to-one to segregating sites. Their number is commonly denoted $S$.

= Watterson estimator
{parent=Segregating site}
{c}
{title2=$\widehat\theta_W=\frac{S}{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}1/i}$}

For a neutral sample of size $n\ge2$ under the <infinite sites mutation model>, the Watterson estimator is $\widehat\theta_W=S/a_{n-1}$, where $a_{n-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}1/i$. Since the coalescent has $ES=\theta a_{n-1}$, this estimator is unbiased. It matches the observed segregating-site count to its expected value, but need not maximize the exact <segregating-site likelihood under the neutral coalescent>.

= Asymptotic normality of the Watterson estimator
{parent=Watterson estimator}
{title2=$\sqrt{\log n}(\widehat\theta_W-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\theta)$}

For fixed $\theta>0$, a <geometric mutation count in a coalescent epoch> with mean $\mu_i=\theta/i$ has third <moment> $\mu_i+6\mu_i^2+6\mu_i^3$. The centered absolute third moment is at most $4\mathbb E[Y_i^3]+4\mu_i^3$, so its sum is $O(\log n)$. The total <variance> is asymptotic to $\theta\log n$, making the <Lyapunov condition> with $\delta=1$ hold. Rescaling by the <harmonic sum> gives the displayed limit. At $\theta=0$ the estimator is identically zero and the limit is degenerate. The <variance of the Watterson estimator> decreases only logarithmically with sample size at a fixed nonrecombining locus.

= Variance of the Watterson estimator
{parent=Watterson estimator}
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\theta_W)=\frac{\theta}{H_{n-1}}+\frac{\theta^2H_{n-1}^{(2)}}{H_{n-1}^2}$}

The <moments of the mutation count on a neutral coalescent tree> give this <variance> for $\widehat\theta_W=S/H_{n-1}$. Since $H_{n-1}\sim\log n$ and the sum of inverse squares is bounded, it tends to zero. Together with unbiasedness, this gives <mean-square convergence> to the mutation parameter, so it is a <consistent estimator>.

= Infinite sites mutation model
{parent=Population genetics}
{wiki}

= Infinitely many sites mutation model
{synonym}

The infinite-sites model assumes that every mutation occurs at a previously unmutated site, so each mutation on the sample genealogy produces a distinct segregating site. If mutation rate per lineage is $\theta/2$ in standard coalescent time units, a tree of total length $L$ has a <Poisson distribution> of mutation count with mean $\theta L/2$.

= Segregating-site likelihood under the neutral coalescent
{parent=Infinite sites mutation model}

For $n\ge2$, the <probability generating function> of the segregating-site count conditional on mutation parameter $\theta$ is
$$
E[z^S\mid\theta]=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{i}{i+\theta(1-z)}.
$$
This follows by integrating the Poisson generating function over the independent exponential epoch lengths. With $W=L/2$, its density is $(n-1)e^{-w}(1-e^{-w})^{n-2}$. Expanding the binomial factor and integrating $w^k e^{-(\theta+r+1)w}$ gives
$$
P(S=k\mid\theta)=(n-1)\theta^k\sum_{r=0}^{n-2}\frac{(-1)^r\binom{n-2}{r}}{(\theta+r+1)^{k+1}}.
$$
For $k=0$ use $\theta^0=1$, including at $\theta=0$. A posterior density divided by a positive prior density is proportional to this likelihood, allowing posterior simulation output to estimate its maximizer.

= Inbreeding
{parent=Population genetics}
{wiki}

= Inbred
{synonym}

Inbreeding is reproduction between genetically related individuals. It increases the chance that an offspring's maternal and paternal copies at a locus are <identical by descent>. Such homozygosity by descent is autozygosity.

= Inbreeding coefficient
{parent=Inbreeding}
{title2=$F$}

The inbreeding coefficient is the <probability> that the two <allele> copies at a locus within an individual are <identical by descent>. It equals the <kinship coefficient> of the individual's parents. It concerns ancestral identity, rather than merely <homozygosity> of observed allelic states.

// Target: biology.bigb

= Inbreeding genotype frequencies
{parent=Inbreeding coefficient}

For a biallelic locus with frequencies $p=1-q$ and $q$, and <inbreeding coefficient> $F$, ancestral-copy identity with probability $F$ gives <genotype> probabilities $P(AA)=p^2+Fpq$, $P(Aa)=2pq(1-F)$ and $P(aa)=q^2+Fpq$. In the IBD component both copies equal a single ancestral draw; in the remaining component the two ancestral alleles are independent population draws. A fully penetrant recessive disease therefore has risk $q^2+Fq(1-q)$.

= First-cousin inbreeding coefficient
{parent=Inbreeding coefficient}
{title2=$F=1/16$}

For an otherwise <outbred pedigree>, each of the two shared grandparents contributes $(1/2)^{2+2+1}=1/32$ to the <kinship coefficient> of first cousins. Thus their child has <inbreeding coefficient> $1/16$. Equivalently, cousin kinship is one quarter of the $1/4$ kinship of their sibling parents.

= Linkage disequilibrium
{parent=Population genetics}
{wiki}
{title2=$D=P(AB)-P(A)P(B)$}

= LD
{c}
{synonym}

Linkage disequilibrium is nonindependence of allelic states at different loci in population <haplotypes>. For alleles $A,B$, $D=P(AB)-P(A)P(B)$. It is a population-frequency property, distinct from <genetic linkage>, which concerns transmission within meioses.

= Standardized linkage disequilibrium
{parent=Linkage disequilibrium}
{title2=$D'=D/D_{\max}$}

For positive <linkage disequilibrium> $D$, take $D_{\max}=\min(p_A(1-p_B),(1-p_A)p_B)$; for negative $D$, take $D_{\max}=\min(p_Ap_B,(1-p_A)(1-p_B))$ and retain its negative sign in $D'$. Thus $|D'|\le1$. Its magnitude measures the fraction of the largest disequilibrium possible at the specified allele frequencies. High $|D'|$ need not imply high <allelic correlation>, so it does not alone guarantee good marker prediction.

= Linkage-disequilibrium decay under recombination
{parent=Linkage disequilibrium}
{title2=$D_{t+1}=(1-r)D_t$}

In a random-mating two-locus population with recombination fraction $r$, and no <mutation>, selection, migration or <genetic drift>, the next-generation frequency of haplotype $AB$ is $(1-r)p_{AB}+rp_Ap_B$. The marginal allele frequencies do not change, so subtracting $p_Ap_B$ gives the displayed recursion for <linkage disequilibrium>. Observed disequilibrium also reflects population history and sampling; it is not a deterministic function of physical distance.

= Allelic correlation
{parent=Linkage disequilibrium}
{title2=$r^2=D^2/[p_A(1-p_A)p_B(1-p_B)]$}

For two segregating biallelic loci, encode the allele on a random population <haplotype> by indicator variables $X,Y$. Their <covariance> is the <linkage disequilibrium> coefficient $D$. The squared <correlation> is $r^2=D^2/[p_A(1-p_A)p_B(1-p_B)]$, between zero and one. It measures how well one marker predicts the other and is undefined if either marker is monomorphic.

= Population stratification
{parent=Genetics}
{wiki}

Population stratification is systematic variation in allele frequencies between subpopulations. If those subpopulations also differ in outcome causes, <genetic variant> associations can exhibit <confounding>, violating <instrumental-variable independence> in <Mendelian randomization>.

= Hardy-Weinberg principle
{c}
{parent=Genetics}
{wiki=Hardy–Weinberg_principle}

= Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium
{c}
{synonym}

= HWE
{c}
{synonym}

= Hardy-Weinberg proportions
{c}
{synonym}

For two alleles $A,B$ sampled independently with frequencies $1-\theta$ and $\theta$, the genotype probabilities under Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium are
$$
P(AA)=(1-\theta)^2,
\qquad P(AB)=2\theta(1-\theta),
\qquad P(BB)=\theta^2.
$$

= Multiplicative penetrance preserves Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium
{parent=Hardy-Weinberg principle}
{title2=$\pi_i^*=\pi_i\psi_i/\sum_u\pi_u\psi_u$}

For ordered alleles $I,J$ independently drawn with probabilities $\pi_i$, suppose disease <penetrance> is $c\psi_i\psi_j$. Conditional on disease, the allele draws remain independent with probabilities $\pi_i^*=\pi_i\psi_i/\sum_u\pi_u\psi_u$. This follows by normalizing the product $c\pi_i\pi_j\psi_i\psi_j$. Unordered homozygote probabilities are $(\pi_i^*)^2$ and heterozygote probabilities are $2\pi_i^*\pi_j^*$. Unaffected controls do not generally share this factorization: their genotype weights contain $1-c\psi_i\psi_j$.

// Target: biology.bigb

= One-generation random-mating equilibrium
{parent=Hardy-Weinberg principle}
{title2=$p=u+v/2,\quad(p^2,2p(1-p),(1-p)^2)$}

If parental <genotype> frequencies are $(u,v,w)$ and the two parental gametes are independently sampled from the same <allele> pool, <Mendelian segregation> gives allele frequency $p=u+v/2$. Their independent union has the displayed <Hardy-Weinberg proportions>, irrespective of parental genotype proportions. Equal allele frequencies in the two sexes and no transmission or viability distortion are part of this conclusion.

= Allele-frequency estimator under Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium
{parent=Hardy-Weinberg principle}

From genotype counts in $n$ independent diploid individuals, the unbiased and maximum-likelihood estimator of the frequency of allele $B$ is
$$
\widehat\theta=\frac{2n_{BB}+n_{AB}}{2n}.
$$
It is the sample proportion among the $2n$ sampled alleles, so it is consistent and
$$
\sqrt n(\widehat\theta-\theta)
\xrightarrow{d}N\left(0,\frac{\theta(1-\theta)}2\right).
$$

= Informed consent
{parent=Medicine}
{wiki}

Informed consent is voluntary agreement after receiving and understanding relevant information about an intervention or participation in research. In a <clinical trial>, the participant is informed about the study procedures, potential benefits and risks, alternatives and the option to decline or withdraw. Consent precedes <randomization>.

= Migraine

Migraine is a neurological disorder that can be an outcome in a <clinical trial> or an <observational study>. Its statistical association with an <exposure> does not alone establish a <causal effect>.

= Prion disease
{parent=Disease}

A prion disease is associated with self-propagating abnormal folding of a <protein>. Its epidemiological interpretation requires distinguishing exposure, infection, clinical onset and diagnostic detection.

= Variant Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease
{parent=Prion disease}

= vCJD
{c}
{synonym}

A human <prion disease> associated with exposure to the <BSE> agent. Exposure totals and clinical-onset totals measure different stages of the disease process; <incubation periods> and ascertainment affect their relationship.

= Scrapie
{parent=Prion disease}

A <prion disease> of sheep and goats. A <scrapie> test result and a <BSE> diagnosis are distinct outcomes, even when the clinical appearances overlap.

= Bovine spongiform encephalopathy
{parent=Prion disease}

= BSE
{c}
{synonym}

A <prion disease> of cattle. A positive surveillance test is an observed diagnostic outcome, whose frequency need not equal true infection <prevalence> unless test performance and sampling are accounted for.

= Chronic disease
{parent=Disease}

A chronic disease persists over an extended period. A <continuous-time multi-state model> can represent its progression through severity states, but long duration alone does not imply irreversible progression or justify ignoring state misclassification.
