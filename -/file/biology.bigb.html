= Biology
{wiki}

<Biology> studies living systems: how <biological cells> maintain themselves, how <organisms> develop and reproduce, and how <evolution> generates diversity. Its scales connect <molecules>, <genes>, <biological cells>, <organisms> and the interactions studied by <ecology>. These are complementary viewpoints: a <protein>'s chemistry can explain a cellular mechanism, while <evolution> explains why inherited variants of that mechanism persist. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/1-1-the-science-of-biology[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: the science of biology].

Two organizing ideas make this immense subject navigable: shared cellular organization and descent with inherited modification. A <neuron> and a <gliding bacterium> differ dramatically in shape and behavior, yet both depend on a <cell membrane> and <metabolism>. Their differences become intelligible through specialization and <evolution>.

Keep observations separate from the <mathematical models> used to explain them. A measured <membrane potential> is evidence; an idealized <two-state ion-channel gating> equation proposes a mechanism. A <mathematical model> earns trust through predictions and comparisons with measurements. Its assumptions delimit its usefulness, and disagreement can reveal missing biology rather than a faulty measurement.

= Facilitated target search
{parent=Diffusion and capture}

A facilitated target search combines bulk <diffusion> with lower-dimensional motion on a substrate, such as sliding on <DNA>. In the scaling model $\tau=A/\lambda+B\lambda$, positive constants $A,B$ balance relocation and local exploration. Differentiation gives $\lambda_*=\sqrt{A/B}$ and $\tau_{\min}=2\sqrt{AB}$. These expressions require an accessible optimum within the physically allowed range of sliding lengths.

= Sliding diffusivity with per-step dissociation
{parent=Facilitated target search}
{title2=$\tau_b\simeq a^2/(2DP),\quad\ell_{rms}\simeq a/\sqrt P$}

For an unbiased one-dimensional walk with step size $a$, <diffusion coefficient> $D$ fixes step interval $a^2/(2D)$. A small dissociation probability $P$ per step gives about $1/P$ steps before release. Thus lifetime depends on $D$, while the root-mean-square net displacement before release is $a/\sqrt P$. Net displacement is not the cumulative distance along all backtracking steps. Physically impossible printed diffusivities should not be silently replaced by a guessed exponent.

= Smoluchowski association rate
{parent=Diffusion and capture}
{c}
{title2=$k=4\pi D_{\rm rel}a$}

For an absorbing spherical target of radius $a$ in an infinite uniform reservoir, steady <diffusion> gives $c(r)=c_\infty(1-a/r)$. Integrating the inward flux yields $k=4\pi D_{\rm rel}a$, where $D_{\rm rel}=D_A+D_B$ if both partners diffuse. Here $k$ multiplies <number densities>; its units are volume per time. Molecular orientation, finite-domain transients and imperfect capture require corrections.

= Earliest diffusive target encounter among independent searchers
{parent=Smoluchowski association rate}
{title2=$\tau\simeq V/(Nk_a)$}

If $N$ independent well-mixed searchers each encounter a small absorbing target with approximate exponential hazard $k_a/V$, their minimum waiting time has hazard $Nk_a/V$. The <Smoluchowski association rate> is $k_a=4\pi D_{\rm rel}r_0$. Cell geometry, orientational capture and nonequilibrium spatial distributions can change this idealized estimate; an arbitrary nonexponential first-passage mean does not in general divide by $N$ exactly.

= Force-extension relation
{parent=Biophysics}
{title2=$F(x)$}

The dependence of stretching <force> on extension of a molecule or polymer. Measurements with <optical tweezers> probe conformational entropy, stiffness and structural transitions.

= Electrocardiography
{parent=Biophysics}
{wiki}

Measurement and interpretation of cardiac electrical activity using body-surface electrodes. The resulting trace is an <electrocardiogram>; waveform timing reflects the coordinated activation and recovery of cardiac tissue.

= Electrocardiogram
{parent=Electrocardiography}

A recording of time-varying electrical potential differences at the body surface due to cardiac electrical activity. Its QT interval spans ventricular depolarization and repolarization; a prolonged QT interval need not specify a unique underlying channel defect.

= Ion-channel disease
{parent=Biophysics}

A disease in which altered <ion channel> number, transport or gating contributes to abnormal cell function. A causal account connects measured channel behavior to cellular activity, rather than inferring mechanism from a sequence variant alone.

= Myotonia congenita
{parent=Ion-channel disease}
{wiki}

A skeletal-muscle excitability disorder associated with variants of CLCN1, encoding the ClC-1 <chloride channel>. Reduced stabilizing resting conductance can permit repetitive <action potentials> and delayed relaxation after contraction.

= Long QT syndrome
{parent=Ion-channel disease}
{wiki}

A disorder characterized by delayed ventricular repolarization and susceptibility to dangerous cardiac rhythms. Different molecular subtypes affect different <ion channels>; type 3 can involve persistent inward sodium current caused by impaired fast <ion-channel inactivation>.

= Biomolecular condensate
{parent=Biophysics}

A compositionally enriched biomolecular assembly. Calling an assembly a condensate does not alone establish equilibrium liquid–liquid phase separation or a particular biological function.

= Stress granule
{parent=Biomolecular condensate}

A stress-associated RNA–protein assembly whose formation and composition depend on context. <RNA> enrichment does not establish obligatory translational silence.<Single-Molecule Imaging Reveals Translation of mRNAs Localized to Stress Granules by Daniel Mateju and others>[Translation of granule-associated mRNAs]{ref}

A <molecular motor> couples an <energy> supply to directed mechanical motion. <ATP synthase> provides a rotary example.

= Inchworm motion of a two-headed motor
{parent=Molecular motor}

In inchworm motion the same head remains in front as the two heads advance without exchanging order. Ideal fixed label positions then advance by one centre-of-mass step per cycle, contrasting with the unequal alternating displacements possible in <hand-over-hand motion of a two-headed motor>.

= Hand-over-hand motion of a two-headed motor
{parent=Molecular motor}

In hand-over-hand motion, two attached heads exchange their leading and trailing roles. For a centre-of-mass advance $d$, one head can move about $2d$ while the other remains fixed. The motion of a fluorescent label also depends on its position on the motor.<Myosin V Walks Hand-Over-Hand: Single Fluorophore Imaging with 1.5-nm Localization by Ahmet Yildiz and others>[Alternating light-chain label displacements support head exchange]{ref}

= Model organism
{parent=Biology}
{wiki}

A <model organism> is an <organism> used to investigate biological processes through accessible, repeatable experiments. Its value combines <experimental tractability> with a carefully delimited relationship to other <organisms>. For example, a <gene deletion> in <Saccharomyces cerevisiae> can test whether a <gene> is required for growth under specified conditions; adding another <gene> can test <functional complementation>. Such interventions support <causal inference> more directly than merely noticing that a <gene> and a <phenotype> occur together. <Budding Yeast for Budding Geneticists: A Primer on the Saccharomyces cerevisiae Model System by Andrea A. Duina and others>[Duina, Miller and Keeney (2014), introduction and Experimental Toolkit and Related Resources: manipulation of genes and phenotypic effects]{ref}

The striking opportunity is to ask two experimentally separate questions: what does this component do here, and which part of that function survives a change of <species> or conditions? The <yeast cross-species complementation experiment> makes both questions concrete. <Model organism evidence transfer> requires specifying the function being compared; a useful <model organism> need not reproduce every feature of another <organism>.

{parent=Model organism}
{c}

A flowering plant used to investigate <genetics>, <chloroplast> function and development.

= Experimental tractability
{parent=Model organism}

<Experimental tractability> is the practical ability to cultivate a system, manipulate the variables of interest, and obtain an interpretable readout with appropriate <scientific controls>. In <Saccharomyces cerevisiae>, inexpensive cultivation, accessible <haploid> and <diploid> states, and tools for introducing <DNA> make controlled changes of <genotype> feasible. A <haploid> <biological strain> exposes a changed <allele> without a second copy at that <genetic locus>, while crosses and <meiosis> permit tests of inheritance. <Budding Yeast for Budding Geneticists: A Primer on the Saccharomyces cerevisiae Model System by Andrea A. Duina and others>[Duina, Miller and Keeney (2014), introduction, Figure 2 and Experimental Toolkit and Related Resources]{ref}

An accessible readout is not necessarily a direct measurement of the mechanism. Growth of a <biological colony> integrates many cellular functions. Failure to grow could reflect the intended <gene deletion>, unsuitable conditions, failed <genetic transformation>, or another <mutation>. Matching the <genetic background> and using <scientific controls> narrows these alternatives. Ease of doing an experiment and strength of its inference are distinct advantages.

= Yeast cross-species complementation experiment
{parent=Model organism}

Can a <gene> from <Schizosaccharomyces pombe> replace a missing function in <Saccharomyces cerevisiae>? A modern laboratory project tested <functional complementation> of <methionine> synthesis. For each tested <gene deletion>, the investigators compared <plasmids> carrying the corresponding <Saccharomyces cerevisiae> <gene>, a candidate <Schizosaccharomyces pombe> <gene>, and an unrelated <gene>. The first was a positive <scientific control>; the last was a negative <scientific control>. The paper describes this design rather than publishing a complete set of individual growth measurements. <Pathways over Time: Functional Genomics Research in an Introductory Laboratory Course by Todd D. Reeves and others>[Reeves, Warner, Ludlow and O’Connor (2018), Course Activities, pp. 5–6 and Table 1: three plasmids, replica plating and protein-expression checks]{ref}

The recipient <biological strains> also lacked the ability to make <uracil>. The pYES2.1/V5-His <plasmids> carried URA3, a <gene> restoring that ability, and used the GAL1 <gene promoter> to control the inserted <gene>. Initial plates lacked <uracil> but supplied <methionine>: this selected <biological colonies> containing <plasmids> without requiring successful <methionine> synthesis. Subsequent plates lacking <methionine> tested the missing function, comparing <galactose induction> with <glucose> conditions. Thus selecting for a <plasmid> and testing its functional contribution were separate operations. <Pathways Over Time Plasmids Engage Students in Functional Genomics Research by Clare O’Connor>[O’Connor (2018), Plasmid features enable multiple student experiments and Complementation reveals conservation of function]{ref}

The apparatus can be simple and informative. In the associated protocol, <replica plating> uses a block covered with velveteen to transfer small samples of <biological colonies> from a master plate onto plates with different nutrient conditions. Orientation marks preserve correspondence between locations; gentle contact limits the transferred material. The specified replicas omit <uracil> and <methionine> with either inducing <sugar> or <glucose>, and include a fresh <glucose> plate supplying <methionine>. The readout is growth at corresponding locations, not a direct measurement of <enzyme> activity. <Investigations in Molecular Cell Biology by Clare M. O’Connor/12.6: Exercise 2 - Replica plating and complementation>[O’Connor, Investigations in Molecular Cell Biology, §12.6, introductory paragraphs and steps 1–3: velveteen block, orientation marks and three replica media]{ref}

The <scientific controls> answer different objections. Growth on the <methionine>-supplied replica checks survival and transfer. Rescue by the native <gene> checks that this <biological strain> and assay can reveal <functional complementation>. The unrelated <gene> tests whether carrying a <plasmid> alone is sufficient. Comparing induction conditions checks dependence on <gene expression>, although changing the <sugar> also changes <metabolism>: it is not an isolated change of <gene expression> in every other respect.

If the candidate <gene> restores growth while those comparisons behave as intended, it supplies a function sufficient for the tested growth requirement in this recipient. Failure to rescue is less decisive: the candidate <protein> might not be produced, might be altered by its attached tag, or might not work in this cellular context. = Model organism evidence transfer
{parent=Model organism}

Evidence transfers at a stated level. <Sequence alignment> can suggest a candidate relationship between <genes>; <functional complementation> tests whether a candidate can supply a particular function in a chosen recipient. Neither alone establishes identical native <gene expression>, identical <signal transduction>, or identical consequences in every <species>. The <yeast cross-species complementation experiment> is especially useful because it tests a conserved function by intervention rather than assuming it from resemblance. <Pathways over Time: Functional Genomics Research in an Introductory Laboratory Course by Todd D. Reeves and others>[Reeves et al. (2018), project design, pp. 3–4, and Course Activities, pp. 5–6]{ref}

Even successful <functional complementation> occurs within a specific <genetic background>, nutrient supply and imposed <gene expression>. The <enzyme> may work with the recipient’s available <substrates> and partner <proteins> at an experimentally imposed abundance. Establishing the same role in the donor <species> calls for experiments there; establishing behavior under another condition calls for experiments under that condition. = Taxonomy
{synonym}

<Taxonomy> names and classifies <organisms>. Classification can organize known diversity and make comparisons useful, but ranks and names are not themselves evidence of ancestry. <Phylogenetics> infers that ancestry; a <clade> includes an ancestor and all its descendants. = Domain
{disambiguate=biology}
{parent=Taxonomy (biology)}

A biological <domain (biology)> is a high-level rank used to classify <organisms> in the <three-domain system>. Distinguish a taxonomic rank from a primary branch in an inferred <phylogenetic tree>: retaining familiar names does not settle the branching relationships.

= Three-domain system
{parent=Domain (biology)}
{wiki}

The <three-domain system> recognizes <bacteria>, <archaea> and <eukaryotes> as three domains. In 1990, Woese, Kandler and Wheelis proposed the domain rank and the names Bacteria, Archaea and Eucarya, using molecular comparisons to argue that the older <prokaryote>/<eukaryote> division hid important differences. See https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2112744/[Woese, Kandler and Wheelis, 1990: Towards a natural system of organisms].

The associated three-domain ancestry model places the nuclear lineage of <eukaryotes> alongside <archaea>, rather than within them. This historically influential interpretation should be distinguished from the <two-domain system>; listing three familiar groups does not establish three independent primary branches.

= Two-domain system
{parent=Domain (biology)}
{wiki}

The <two-domain system> recognizes <bacteria> and <archaea> as the two primary lineages, placing the nuclear ancestry of <eukaryotes> within <archaea>. In this model, <archaea> as a complete <clade> includes that <eukaryotic> lineage; restricting the name to <prokaryotic> members describes a different grouping. Williams and colleagues' study, published online in 2019 and in a 2020 journal issue, found support for this model and a close relationship to <Asgard archaea>. See https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-019-1040-x[Williams et al., 2020: Phylogenomics provides robust support for a two-domains tree of life].

This updates the interpretation of the 1977 and 1990 classifications rather than undoing their discovery of deep differences between <bacteria> and <archaea>. Deep <phylogenetic trees> depend on the sampled <genes>, <organisms> and inference model; the precise closest archaeal relatives and early sequence of events require further evidence. Bacterial ancestry of <mitochondria> through <endosymbiosis> is a separate part of <eukaryotic> history.

= Species
{parent=Taxonomy (biology)}
{wiki}

A <species> is a named unit of biological classification. The useful criteria vary among groups: reproductive compatibility can inform classification in many <animals>, while microbial comparisons also use <genomes> and other characters. <Escherichia coli> is a <species>; K-12 MG1655 identifies a particular strain within it. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/18-2-formation-of-new-species[OpenStax Biology 2e, 18.2: species concepts].

= Species concept
{parent=Species}

A <species concept> states what should make <organisms> members of the same <species>; an operational criterion states what observations will test that claim. <Reproductive isolation>, distinguishable <morphology> and ancestry inferred through <phylogenetics> answer different questions. In a lineage-based interpretation, they are evidence for separation of evolving <biological populations>, and need not appear together. <Species Concepts and Species Delimitation by Kevin de Queiroz>[pp. 880–883, Table 1, Figure 1 and “Conceptualization versus Delimitation”: distinguish defining properties from operational evidence]{ref}

= Phenetic species concept
{parent=Species concept}

A <species concept> based on overall measured similarity among organisms, with explicit or implicit choices of characters, weights and grouping thresholds. A <morphological species concept> emphasizes anatomical similarity, while phenetic comparisons can use a wider range of <phenotypic traits>. <Cryptic species>, <phenotypic plasticity>, <sexual dimorphism> and <convergent evolution> explain why similarity need not coincide with <reproductive isolation> or a unique evolutionary lineage.

= Biological species concept
{parent=Species concept}

The <biological species concept> groups naturally interbreeding <biological populations> with viable, fertile offspring, distinguished from other such groups by <reproductive isolation>. An operational test examines mating, <fertilization>, offspring survival and offspring <reproduction>, rather than only whether two adults can be paired. <Sexual reproduction> is its scope: <fossils> and <organisms> studied only through <asexual reproduction> cannot supply the same mating evidence. <Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[§18.2, “Species and the Ability to Reproduce”, “Speciation” and “Reproductive Isolation”]{ref}

For an original hypothetical example, imagine two separated <biological populations> that reproduce successfully after contact. The separation alone did not demonstrate <reproductive isolation>. Conversely, failure to mate in one unfamiliar chamber could reflect the assay conditions. Field observations of <gene flow> and tests under suitable conditions address the biological question more directly. A successful laboratory pairing does not establish frequent interbreeding in nature.

= Morphological species concept
{parent=Species concept}

A <morphological species concept> distinguishes <species> using <morphology>: measurable differences in form or structure. It can be applied to preserved specimens and <fossils> when mating cannot be observed. Similar appearance need not imply the same <species>, and different appearance need not imply different <species>. <Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[§18.2, “Species and the Ability to Reproduce”, paragraphs accompanying Figures 18.9–18.10: appearance can mislead]{ref}

An original operational example is to compare a chosen structure in specimens of comparable age, sex and rearing conditions. Suppose two apparent groups differ in size, but one group contains only juveniles. The observed difference does not yet diagnose two <species>. Specify the measured <phenotype>, variation within each sample, and whether the distinction persists across comparable specimens. A single striking specimen is a poor substitute for that comparison.

= Phylogenetic species concept
{parent=Species concept}

<Phylogenetic species concepts> use ancestry and diagnostic inherited differences; versions differ over requiring a <clade>, an exclusive ancestry pattern at <genetic loci>, or a distinguishable character combination. State which criterion is intended. <Species Concepts and Species Delimitation by Kevin de Queiroz>[p. 880, Table 1: distinct monophyletic, genealogical and diagnosable versions of phylogenetic criteria]{ref}

For an original operational example, suppose a <phylogenetic tree> inferred from one <genetic locus> separates two sampled <biological populations>. Compare further <genetic loci> and additional local samples before interpreting that branch as a <species> boundary. Otherwise the test may merely identify the particular ancestry of the sampled <alleles>. Adding more sites from the same <genetic locus> improves that locus's evidence without creating an independent ancestry test.

= Species delimitation
{parent=Species}

<Species delimitation> infers the boundaries and number of <species> from evidence. No single arbitrary percentage of <nucleotide sequence> difference is a universal boundary. Divergence within an old, widespread <species> can exceed divergence between recently separated <species>; geography and multiple kinds of evidence matter. <Species Concepts and Species Delimitation by Kevin de Queiroz>[pp. 884–885, “Alternatives to the Traditional Properties”: divergence cutoffs and geographic differentiation]{ref}

An original counterexample to a universal percentage rule: an assay could examine a strongly conserved <gene> in one comparison and a rapidly changing <nucleotide sequence> in another. Equal percentages then answer different historical questions. Even the denominator needs specifying: all sites, aligned sites, or only a filtered region? A threshold is an operational rule for a stated assay and group of <organisms>, whose errors need evaluating. It is not a definition that automatically transfers to every <species>.

= Cryptic species
{parent=Species delimitation}

Distinct evolutionary lineages or reproductively isolated <species> that are difficult to distinguish using the morphological characters traditionally examined. Similar appearance can therefore conceal <species> boundaries. Genetic evidence alone also requires interpretation, because population structure need not imply complete <reproductive isolation>.

= Species, populations and strains
{parent=Species}

A <species> can encompass many <biological populations>. A <biological population> specifies a set of <organisms> in a place and time; a <biological strain> identifies a maintained variant or lineage, often in a laboratory. A <species> name, a local census and a <biological strain> designation therefore describe different things. Multiple independently maintained cultures can also share the same <biological strain> designation.

For example, a culture identified as <Escherichia coli> K-12 MG1655 specifies more than the <species> alone. Two flasks inoculated from that culture are separate experimental <biological populations>; the separate containers do not establish two <species> or two different <biological strains>. This is an original experimental bookkeeping example, not a claim that every <biological strain> name encodes a complete <genotype>.

= Flow cytometric DNA-content assay
{parent=Flow cytometry}

Under controlled staining and detector settings, integrated <fluorescence> can be approximately proportional to nuclear <DNA> amount. A <histogram> of accepted single-cell events then separates a $2C$ region, an intermediate region and a $4C$ region. These are ordinarily assigned to <G0 phase>/<G1 phase>, <S phase>, and <G2 phase>/<M phase> respectively in the stated <diploid> model.

= Calcium in biology
{parent=Biology}

Calcium has roles in cell signalling and skeletal structure. Dietary <calcium intake> and <serum calcium> are distinct quantities: physiological regulation means a change in one need not correspond to an equivalent change in the other.

= Calcium wave
{parent=Calcium in biology}

A propagating spatial variation in cellular calcium concentration. Coupling local release and recovery to transport can produce pulses with rapid fronts and slower return to a resting state.

= Fast-slow matching for a calcium pulse
{parent=Calcium wave}
{title2=$U_X=s[U-U_B+\gamma(V-V_B)],\quad sV_X=f(U,V)$}

In a two-variable calcium-wave model, the fast front scale can reduce to the displayed <dynamical system>. Its equilibria are intersections of the kinetic <nullcline> $V=g(U)$ and a line of slope $-1/\gamma$. At a simple intersection with $f_V<0$, the <Jacobian determinant> is $f_V(1+\gamma g')$; a negative determinant identifies a <saddle point>. Matching a rising front requires a <heteroclinic orbit> from one saddle to another, selected by a <shooting method> in the speed $s$. A falling connection must use the same speed. Between fronts the slow equation is $s(1+\gamma g')U'=\mu-U$. Recovery on the two outer rising branches requires the resting value $\mu$ to lie between the low landing value and the high takeoff value. The nullcline shape supplies this geometry but does not by itself prove existence of both connections.

= Serum calcium
{parent=Calcium in biology}

Serum calcium is the concentration of calcium in blood serum. It can be an <exposure> in an <observational study> or <Mendelian randomization>, but genetically changing it need not reproduce the effect of changing dietary <calcium intake>.

= Gel electrophoresis
{parent=Biology}
{wiki}

Separation of charged <molecules> through a gel in an <electric field>. Denaturing conditions suppress folded structures, helping compare <RNA> lengths; matching migration alone does not establish an identical <nucleotide sequence> or identical <covalent bonds>. In the <ribozyme> experiments, labelled <RNA> was detected after separation by exposing photographic film.

In <DNA> <agarose gel electrophoresis>, the backbone <phosphate> groups carry negative <electric charge> under the usual mildly alkaline <buffer solution> conditions. <DNA> therefore moves toward the positive <anode>, away from the negative <cathode>.<Agarose Gel Electrophoresis by Addgene>[Introduction; Loading Samples and Running an Agarose Gel, step 6: DNA migration toward the positive electrode]{ref}

The <electric field> points the other way: the <electric force> $\mathbf F=q\mathbf E$ reverses direction when $q<0$. The apparatus is driven by an external source of <electric potential difference>; the positive <anode> convention here belongs to that operating mode. Direction follows <electric charge>, so it is not a rule that all <molecules> move toward the same <electrode>.

The useful result is a spatial comparison: a mixture becomes separated <gel bands>, which can be compared with a <DNA ladder> or recovered for subsequent analysis. <Agarose gel electrophoresis> of linear double-stranded <DNA>, <gel electrophoresis> of denatured <RNA>, and <SDS-PAGE> of <proteins> use different conditions to make migration informative about chain size. An <electric field> supplies motion; the gel supplies a porous obstacle course.

= Gel band
{parent=Gel electrophoresis}

A <gel band> is a localized population of stained or otherwise detected <molecules> with sufficiently similar migration in one sample lane. One visible <gel band> need not contain only one <nucleotide sequence>. Two different linear <DNA> fragments of 375 <base pairs> can occupy the same unresolved <gel band>; equal migration supplies size evidence under suitable conditions, not a sequence determination.

<Fluorescence> from a bound stain can support an estimate of <DNA> quantity, but suitable staining and detector response matter.<Five Main Nucleic Acid Gel Electrophoresis Steps by Thermo Fisher Scientific>[Step 4, Sample visualization, Fluorescent dyes: relation between fluorescence and bound nucleic acid]{ref}

For a quantitative model with equal staining response per unit <mass>, background-subtracted, integrated <fluorescence> $I$ satisfies $I=km$. This requires an unsaturated detector and a common proportionality constant $k$; visual peak brightness alone does not supply $I$.

The <amount of substance> is $n=m/M$, where $M$ is <molar mass>. For double-stranded <DNA> with approximately equal average <mass> per <base pair>, $M$ is proportional to length $L$, so $n$ is proportional to $I/L$. In a constructed example, equal integrated signals from 250-<base-pair> and 1000-<base-pair> <gel bands> imply approximately equal <masses> but four times as many short <DNA> <molecules>. Conversely, equal numbers of those <DNA> <molecules> would give about four times the signal for the longer fragments in this model. Neither conclusion follows from darkness in a schematic drawing.

= Agarose gel electrophoresis
{parent=Gel electrophoresis}

<Agarose gel electrophoresis> uses a porous <agarose> <polymer> network. For linear double-stranded <DNA> within a suitable separation range, shorter fragments generally move farther in a fixed run. Raising the <agarose> concentration changes the mesh and the range of sizes that can be resolved.<Five Main Nucleic Acid Gel Electrophoresis Steps by Thermo Fisher Scientific>[Step 1, Selection and gel preparation; Recommended percentage of agarose gels for separation of DNA fragments]{ref} A compact circular <DNA> <molecule> need not migrate like linear <DNA> of the same length: geometry affects passage through the mesh.<Five Considerations for the Nucleic Acid Gel Electrophoresis Process by Thermo Fisher Scientific>[Effects of conformation, Figure 1: nicked circular, linear and supercoiled forms of the same DNA]{ref}

A concrete laboratory example is Addgene's documented <DNA> separation protocol. Its standard 1% gel contains 1 g of <agarose> in 100 mL of <TAE buffer>. Dissolved <agarose> sets in a casting tray around a comb; removing the comb leaves sample wells. The gel is covered with the same <buffer solution> in a box connected to a voltage source. Loading buffer contains <glycerol> to increase sample <density> and a tracking dye to show run progress. A <DNA ladder> occupies a separate lane beside the samples. With wells near the negative <cathode>, <DNA> migrates toward the positive <anode>. After separation, bound stain makes the <DNA> visible by <fluorescence> under a <UV transilluminator>. The <DNA ladder> supplies the size reference; the tracking dye monitors migration but does not identify sample <DNA>.<Agarose Gel Electrophoresis by Addgene>[Equipment; Pouring a Standard 1% Agarose Gel, steps 1–7; Loading Samples and Running an Agarose Gel, steps 1–9; Analyzing Your Gel]{ref}

In the manufacturer's controlled comparison, the same <DNA ladder> runs in 1%, 2%, and 3% <agarose> gels with the same run time and other stated conditions. The changed <gel band> spacing shows why a calibration belongs to its particular gel conditions, rather than establishing a universal distance law.<Five Main Nucleic Acid Gel Electrophoresis Steps by Thermo Fisher Scientific>[Step 1, Figure 3 and caption: same DNA ladder at three agarose percentages under the same conditions, including run time]{ref}

In interpreting an unknown sample, a blank lane tests for detectable background or carryover in that blank, while the <DNA ladder> checks migration and detection of its known fragments. An empty sample lane cannot by itself distinguish absent <DNA> from unsuccessful loading or insufficient detection. These controls answer different questions; neither makes matching migration a proof of matching <nucleotide sequence>.

= Gel electrophoresis size calibration
{parent=Agarose gel electrophoresis}
{title2=$d=a-b\log_{10}(L/L_0)$}

A <gel electrophoresis size calibration> relates migration distance to known fragment lengths on the same gel. Here is an explicitly chosen model for linear double-stranded <DNA>, not measured data or a universal law. Let $L$ be the numerical length in <base pairs> and $d$ the numerical migration distance in millimetres from the wells:
$$
d=55-12\log_{10}(L/100).
$$
The <logarithm> has a dimensionless argument: $L/100$ is the length divided by 100 <base pairs>. The chosen <DNA ladder> fragments $L=100,250,500,1000$ give $d=55.00,50.22,46.61,43.00$, respectively. The <gel bands> therefore appear in the reverse order of length along the direction of migration.

\Image[gel-electrophoresis-ladder-calibration.png]
{title=Constructed <DNA ladder>, unknown, mixture and blank lanes with a <logarithm> calibration. All positions follow the chosen model; the unknown is 375 <base pairs> at 48.11 mm, and darkness does not represent <DNA> quantity}

To invert the calibration, rearrange the <logarithm>:
$$
L=100\,10^{(55-d)/12}.
$$
Our chosen unknown $L=375$ gives $d=48.1116248\ldots$, between the 250-<base-pair> and 500-<base-pair> references. Inserting that distance into the inverse recovers 375; retaining only $d=48.11$ gives about $375.12$ <base pairs>. The extra digits reflect rounding in this model, not the resolving power of a real gel.

For <linear interpolation> between those two reference <gel bands>, use the <logarithm> of length. Here the fraction of the distance from the 500-<base-pair> reference toward the 250-<base-pair> reference is $t=\log_{10}(500/375)/\log_{10}2\approx0.4150$, giving $L=500(250/500)^t=375$. Direct <linear interpolation> of lengths instead gives approximately $396.24$ <base pairs>. This difference explains why the choice of calibration coordinates matters. A real calibration must be checked over its usable range; folded <RNA> or circular <DNA> does not automatically share the linear double-stranded <DNA> calibration.

= Protective factor
{parent=Epidemiology}

A characteristic associated with lower risk or a weaker effect of a risk exposure under specified conditions. Causal protection requires stronger evidence than an unadjusted association.

= Stress-buffering interaction
{parent=Protective factor}
{title2=$\partial\mathbb E[D\mid S,P]/\partial S=\beta_1+\beta_3P$}

A pattern in which a resource modifies the relationship between <adversity> and an adverse outcome. In the model $\mathbb E[D\mid S,P]=\beta_0+\beta_1S+\beta_2P+\beta_3SP$, a negative $\beta_3$ attenuates the <adversity> slope as the resource increases. Its interpretation is scale dependent, and an observational interaction is not automatically a causal buffering effect.

= Risk factor
{parent=Epidemiology}

A characteristic associated with increased probability of an outcome. Association can arise from causal influence, <confounding> or selection; being predictive does not automatically make a factor a modifiable causal target.

= Risk difference
{parent=Epidemiology}
{title2=$RD=p_1-p_0$}

The difference between two outcome <probabilities> over specified populations and follow-up periods. With intervention risk $p_1$ and comparison risk $p_0$, a beneficial reduction in an adverse outcome has $RD<0$ and absolute risk reduction $p_0-p_1$. A causal interpretation requires a suitable design or identification assumptions; a simple difference between selected observational groups is not automatically a treatment effect.

= Tumour clonality

The organization of a <tumour> into related lineages sharing inherited changes. A clonal alteration need not be a causal driver because passengers can hitchhike during expansion.

= Tumour purity

= Cancer dependency

= Antibiotic tolerance
{parent=Antibiotic}

Reduced killing under antibiotic exposure without a corresponding increase in the concentration inhibiting growth. It differs from growth-based <antibiotic resistance>.

= Bacterial persistence
{parent=Antibiotic tolerance}

Survival of a subpopulation during bactericidal exposure while the broader population remains susceptible. Regrown survivors can recover the original susceptibility; persistence is not synonymous with every persistent infection.

= Minimum inhibitory concentration
{parent=Antibiotic}

The lowest tested antimicrobial concentration preventing defined visible growth under specified conditions. It measures an organism-level response, not just the catalytic efficiency of an isolated resistance <enzyme>.
