= Solution
{parent=1}

**Cosmological variables and conventions.** The <scale factor> $R(t)$ describes the relative separation of <comoving observers>; dots denote <derivatives> with respect to <cosmic time> $t$. The <Hubble parameter> is $H(t)=\dot R/R$, usually time dependent; “<Hubble constant>” conventionally means its present value. Here $\rho$ is mass-equivalent density, so $\epsilon=\rho c^2$ is <energy density>, while $P$ is isotropic <pressure>. $G$ is the <gravitational constant> and $c$ the <speed of light>. The constant $K$ specifies <cosmological spatial curvature>: with $R$ a length, $K=kc^2$ and $k=+1,0,-1$ describe closed, flat and open spatial geometries. Alternative normalizations of $R$ move a constant factor into $K$. In the printed equations $\Lambda$ has units $\mathrm{s}^{-2}$: it is $c^2$ times a <cosmological constant> defined in inverse square metres. This <time-unit convention for the cosmological constant> prevents a missing factor of $c^2$.

**Continuity and the first law.** Differentiating the first <Friedmann equation> with constant $K$ and $\Lambda$, and then eliminating $\ddot R/R$ with the second, gives
$$
2H\left(\dot H-\frac{K}{R^2}\right)=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\dot\rho,\qquad
\dot H-\frac{K}{R^2}=-4\pi G\left(\rho+\frac{P}{c^2}\right).
$$
Thus, on an expanding or contracting branch,
$$
\boxed{\dot\rho=-3H\left(\rho+\frac{P}{c^2}\right).}
$$
The relation extends continuously to a regular turning point. For a physical volume $V(t)=V_*R^3(t)$ bounded by fixed <comoving coordinates>, $\dot V=3HV$. Multiplication by $c^2V$ gives
$$
\frac{d}{dt}(\rho c^2V)=-P\dot V,\qquad
\boxed{dU+P\,dV=0,\quad U=\rho c^2V.}
$$
This <adiabatic work in a comoving volume> is the <first law of thermodynamics> for adiabatic evolution with fixed <entropy> and no additional chemical work. It expresses local <energy> conservation, including <pressure> work. **It does not assert that $U$ is constant:** <radiation> loses <internal energy> as it does work; negative <pressure> makes $U$ grow during expansion. A generic expanding spacetime has no global time-translation <symmetry> from which a conserved total <energy> follows.

**Constant scalar-field <energy density>.** Setting $\dot\rho=0$ in the continuity equation, with $H\ne0$, gives
$$
\boxed{P=-\rho c^2,\qquad w=\frac{P}{\rho c^2}=-1.}
$$
For a homogeneous canonical <scalar field>, in units $c=1$, $\epsilon=\dot\phi^2/2+V(\phi)$ and $P=\dot\phi^2/2-V(\phi)$. A stationary potential-dominated field has precisely this <equation of state>. A general evolving <scalar field> need not have $w=-1$; the constant-density assumption is essential.

**Expansion at the stipulated Higgs <energy density>.** With zero explicit $\Lambda$ and zero $K$, constant $\epsilon_H$ gives
$$
H_H=\sqrt{\frac{8\pi G\epsilon_H}{3c^2}},\qquad
\boxed{R(t)=R_i e^{H_H(t-t_i)}}.
$$
The plus sign selects the expanding branch; there is also a contracting solution. The scalar-field <energy> is already included in $\rho$, so adding it again as an explicit <cosmological constant> would double count it. Using $1\ \mathrm{GeV}=1.602176634\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{J}$ gives $\epsilon_H=2.4033\times10^{45}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$, $\rho_H=2.6740\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and $H_H\simeq3.87\times10^9\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm double}=\frac{\log2}{H_H}\simeq1.79\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{s}.}
$$
This <vacuum-dominated doubling time> describes accelerated expansion in <de Sitter spacetime>, with $\ddot R/R=H_H^2>0$. The stipulated <energy density> exceeds the <critical density> in <energy> units used in Question 2 by $3\times10^{54}$, so it cannot persist as the dominant component of the present universe. The calculation exhibits the <cosmological constant problem>: the absolute gravitational <vacuum energy> must be enormously smaller than such a particle-physics scale today. It does not establish that the <Standard Model> <Higgs field> actually remains constant or supplies a viable inflationary epoch.

**The acoustic ruler and accelerated expansion.** Before <baryon> drag becomes ineffective, <pressure> in the <photon-baryon fluid> drives acoustic waves around initial <linear cosmological density perturbations>. Their comoving <sound horizon> at the <baryon drag epoch> is
$$
r_d=\int_{z_d}^{\infty}\frac{c_s(z)}{H(z)}\,dz.
$$
After decoupling, this preferred separation survives in <galaxy> clustering as <baryon acoustic oscillations>: a peak in the <two-point correlation function> and oscillations in the <matter power spectrum>. Its value is calibrated by early-universe physics, commonly constrained by the <cosmic microwave background>. The SDSS luminous-red-galaxy measurement established a peak near $100h^{-1}\ \mathrm{Mpc}$; its use as a distance indicator depends on the ruler <calibration> rather than the length of an individual <galaxy>.<Detection of the Baryon Acoustic Peak in the Large-Scale Correlation Function of SDSS Luminous Red Galaxies by Daniel Eisenstein and others>[Abstract and Sections 2–5, correlation-function peak and distance-scale interpretation]{ref}

For a ruler perpendicular to the line of sight and one parallel to it,
$$
\boxed{\Delta\theta=\frac{r_d}{D_M(z)},\qquad
\Delta z=\frac{H(z)r_d}{c}},
$$
where $D_M=(1+z)D_A$ is the <transverse comoving distance> and $D_A$ the <angular diameter distance>. These measure $D_M/r_d$ and $H r_d$, respectively. Comparing multiple redshifts tests the distance–redshift relation and expansion history. In particular,
$$
q(z)=-\frac{\ddot R R}{\dot R^2}
=\frac{1+z}{H(z)}\frac{dH}{dz}-1,
$$
so sufficiently precise measurements of the shape of $H(z)$ can test $q<0$. In <general relativity>, accelerated expansion requires a sufficiently negative total $\rho+3P/c^2$; a positive <cosmological constant> supplies it.

A single acoustic angular scale does not independently determine acceleration, <cosmological spatial curvature>, the absolute <sound horizon> and the dark-energy <equation of state>. One combines redshift coverage with early-universe <calibration> and independent matter-density or distance information, comparing accelerating and nonaccelerating models. The <galaxy> selection function and redshift completeness must be modeled with random catalogues, survey covariance estimated with mock catalogues, and <redshift-space distortions>, <galaxy bias> and nonlinear broadening controlled. These tests distinguish a cosmological feature from a survey artifact. **The evidence is the measured expansion history within an explicit cosmological framework, rather than the existence of an acoustic peak alone.**

= Solution

The primordial-element abundances from <Big Bang nucleosynthesis>, together with the <cosmic microwave background>, constrain the total baryonic density. <massive compact halo objects> such as ordinary faint stars, <brown dwarfs> and <stellar remnants> are baryonic whether or not they shine. With the stipulated density fractions, **the entire baryonic budget is only one fifth of the required dark-matter density**, and much of it is already accounted for by ordinary gas and stars.

A second test does not require a compact object to emit light: <gravitational microlensing> temporarily magnifies a background star as the object crosses the line of sight. Monitoring many Magellanic-Cloud stars constrains the <microlensing optical depth> and event-duration distribution. Surveys such as EROS-2 found too few suitable events for a standard halo predominantly composed of compact objects over their tested <mass> range.<Limits on the Macho Content of the Galactic Halo from the EROS-2 Survey of the Magellanic Clouds by the EROS-2 Collaboration>[Abstract and halo-fraction limits; mass-dependent detection efficiency]{ref} Artificial-event injection, cadence and blending corrections are needed because very short or long events are easily missed; lensing by stars within the Clouds must also be separated from halo lensing. **These are mass-range and halo-model constraints, not a proof excluding every compact-object <mass>.** Primordial <black holes> are not limited by the ordinary-baryon budget in the same way as <stellar remnants>; their viability requires their own observational bounds.

= Solution

<Standard Model> active <neutrinos> decouple while relativistic and retain a thermal momentum distribution. They therefore constitute <hot dark matter> for the light masses compatible with the standard cosmological picture. Their <collisionless free streaming> erases <linear cosmological density perturbations> below a characteristic scale. Making them the dominant gravitating matter would delay small-scale structure formation and suppress the observed <matter power spectrum>.

For standard thermal abundances and nonrelativistic <neutrinos> today,
$$
\Omega_\nu h^2\simeq\frac{\sum_i m_{\nu_i}}{93\ \mathrm{eV}/c^2},
\qquad
h=\frac{H_0}{100\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}}.
$$
Increasing the <mass> sum enough to provide all the stipulated <dark matter> conflicts with the successful <cold dark matter> description of cosmological structure. Independently, the <Tremaine-Gunn bound> on the fermionic phase-space occupation limits the density light <neutrinos> can supply in small galactic haloes.<Dynamical Role of Light Neutral Leptons in Cosmology by Scott Tremaine and James Edward Gunn>[Pages 407–410, collisionless phase-space bound]{ref} Oscillations establish nonzero <neutrino mass> differences, but do not make the relic <neutrinos> cold. **Active <neutrinos> can contribute some matter; they cannot supply the dominant cold component.** A heavier <sterile neutrino> candidate would be a different hypothesis, not an ordinary <Standard Model> relic.

{parent=2}

**Particle candidate.** A stable, electrically neutral <weakly interacting massive particle> can become nonrelativistic before <cosmological particle freeze-out>. Its <annihilation> rate per particle $\Gamma=n\langle\sigma v\rangle$ eventually falls below the <Hubble parameter>; the surviving comoving number then remains approximately fixed. A weak-scale <annihilation> <cross section> can give a cosmologically significant <thermal relic>. This is a mechanism and a parameter-scale motivation, not a guarantee that every weakly interacting particle has the right abundance.

<supersymmetry> supplies <neutralinos>, mixtures of neutral electroweak <gauginos> and <higgsinos>. If a <neutralino> is the <lightest supersymmetric particle> and <R-parity> is conserved, it cannot decay solely into <Standard Model> particles and is stable. Its <electroweak interactions> can make it a <WIMP>. Conservation of <R-parity> and a neutral lightest state are additional assumptions; <R-parity violation>, <coannihilation>, composition and resonant <annihilation> can substantially change the conclusion.

**An underground test.** <XENON100> at Gran Sasso used a liquid-xenon target in a two-phase <time projection chamber>. A <WIMP> scattering elastically from a <xenon> nucleus could produce a low-energy <nuclear recoil>. The interaction makes prompt <particle scintillation>, S1, and <ionization> electrons. An <electric field> drifts the electrons upward; their extraction into <xenon> gas produces delayed <electroluminescence>, S2. The delay measures depth, while the S2 light pattern determines the transverse position. An inner <fiducial volume> suppresses surface backgrounds through <xenon> self-shielding; underground overburden reduces cosmic-ray <muons>, and shielding and material selection reduce <radioactivity>.<The XENON100 Dark Matter Experiment by the XENON100 Collaboration>[Detector design, signal formation and background-rejection principles]{ref}

The S2/S1 ratio separates most <electron recoils> from <nuclear recoils>. Gamma-source <calibration> maps the electron-recoil leakage; an americium–beryllium <neutron> source calibrates nuclear-recoil response and cut acceptance. Purity and position corrections must be measured, and selection fixed before unblinding the search region. <neutrons> can themselves produce single <nuclear recoils>, so discrimination is not perfect; material assays and transport simulations constrain this background. In the 224.6-live-day, 34-kg analysis, two candidate events were compatible with the expected $1.0\pm0.2$ background events. The result constrained scattering <cross sections> rather than establishing a discovery.<Dark Matter Results from 225 Live Days of XENON100 Data by the XENON100 Collaboration>[Pages 2–4, calibration, blinded selection, neutron background and candidate counts]{ref}

A predicted count also depends on the local <dark matter halo> density, distribution of <velocities>, recoil threshold and nuclear response. Thus a null result constrains a particle model under stated astrophysical assumptions; it does not exclude all <dark matter>. A collider missing-momentum signal would likewise need independent evidence for a cosmological lifetime.

**Relic <energy density> in the stipulated freeze-out approximation.** Let $M=mc^2=1900\ \mathrm{GeV}$ and $\theta=k_BT$. Writing the given distribution in <energy> units gives
$$
n_f=6\left(\frac{M\theta_f}{(\hbar c)^2}\right)^{3/2}e^{-P},
\qquad
\theta_f=\frac{M}{P}=52.78\ \mathrm{GeV}.
$$
By <sharp-freeze-out relic dilution>, after <cosmological particle freeze-out> conserve <particle number>, $n a^3=\mathrm{constant}$. In the approximation $aT=\mathrm{constant}$ for the background bath,
$$
n_0=n_f\left(\frac{\theta_0}{\theta_f}\right)^3
=6P^{3/2}e^{-P}\left(\frac{\theta_0}{\hbar c}\right)^3.
$$
Inserting the printed inputs yields
$$
n_f=5.5242\times10^{39}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}},\qquad
n_0=4.8766\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}},
$$
and hence
$$
\boxed{\rho_0c^2=M n_0=0.927\ \mathrm{GeV\,m^{-3}},
\qquad\Omega_\chi=\frac{0.927}{5}=0.185.}
$$
This is approximately $93\%$ of the stipulated dark-matter <energy density>, $0.2\times5=1\ \mathrm{GeV\,m^{-3}}$, so the toy calculation gives the right order and is close to the target. At fixed $P$ the cancellation of $M$ in $n_0$ is exact for the given prefactor, while the present <energy density> remains proportional to $M$.

The equilibrium exponential must be evaluated at freeze-out, **not at today's <temperature>**: equilibrium no longer maintains the <particle number> afterward. More accurately, <comoving entropy conservation> gives $a^3g_{*s}T^3=\mathrm{constant}$ and multiplies the displayed $n_0$ by $g_{*s,0}/g_{*s,f}$, when both counts use the same bath-temperature convention. Thus $0.185$ is the constant-$g_{*s}$ estimate supported by the supplied toy model. A refined abundance would require additional <entropy degrees of freedom> and <annihilation> history: realistic <entropy> release, gradual freeze-out and the model's <annihilation> channels matter.

= Solution

When a cosmological <phase transition> breaks a <symmetry> $G$ to a <subgroup> $H$, the degenerate vacuum configurations form a <vacuum manifold> $\mathcal M=G/H$. Causally separated regions need not choose the same vacuum orientation. The <Kibble mechanism> explains how joining those choices can leave a field configuration that cannot be continuously deformed to a uniform vacuum without leaving $\mathcal M$. The field then stores <energy> in a defect core, gradients or <gauge fields>.<Topology of cosmic domains and strings by Thomas Walter Bannerman Kibble>[Sections on vacuum topology, domains and strings]{ref}

The relevant <homotopy classification of cosmological defects> uses the vacuum map on a surface surrounding a proposed defect:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{nontrivial topology}&\text{defect in three spatial dimensions}&\text{example}\\
\hline
\pi_0(\mathcal M)&\text{domain wall}&\mathcal M=\{+v,-v\}\\
\pi_1(\mathcal M)&\text{string}&\mathcal M=S^1\\
\pi_2(\mathcal M)&\text{monopole}&\mathcal M=S^2
\end{array}
$$
A <domain wall> separates different disconnected vacua. A <cosmic string> has nonzero <winding number> on a loop around its core. A <magnetic monopole> has a nontrivial vacuum map on an enclosing sphere. Nontrivial $\pi_3(\mathcal M)$ can instead support <cosmological texture> configurations extending over a volume; <cosmological textures> can unwind dynamically by leaving the <vacuum manifold> and need not possess a permanently localized stable core. Nontrivial topology permits a defect; formation efficiency, interactions and the cosmological history still determine its abundance.

For example, a complex scalar with a circular set of minima can wind by $2\pi n$ around a <cosmic string>. Continuity forces the field away from its vacuum value near the core. A local <gauge symmetry> allows <gauge fields> to screen much of the exterior gradient and trap flux, whereas a global <symmetry> leaves long-range Goldstone-field gradients. Thus local and global <cosmic strings> have different energy-loss channels; they should not be treated as identical networks.

Relic defects can be tightly constrained because their <energy> dilutes differently from <radiation>. Nonrelativistic <magnetic monopoles> approximately dilute as $a^{-3}$, so excessive production causes the <cosmological monopole problem>. A roughly frozen wall network has <energy density> proportional to $a^{-1}$, even slower dilution. An evolving <cosmic string network> can approach a scaling regime with characteristic separation proportional to <cosmic time> and density of order $\mu/t^2$ in $c=1$ units, losing <energy> into loops and <radiation>. That is a dynamical network statement, not the dilution law of a fixed collection of <cosmic strings>.

<cosmic inflation> can dilute defects made before it; <reheating> and later <symmetry> breaking must not regenerate an unacceptable abundance. Observational tests include microwave-background anisotropy, <gravitational lensing> by <cosmic strings>, <gravitational radiation> from string loops, and searches for <magnetic monopoles>. Each is sensitive to model-dependent tensions, couplings and evolution. **Defects are possible relics of <symmetry> breaking, not an inevitable dominant dark-matter population.** Their absence constrains a transition and its subsequent history rather than every theory possessing a nontrivial vacuum topology.

= Solution
{parent=b}

<supersymmetry> predicts partners with <spins> differing by one half. At a proton collider, strongly interacting <squarks> and <gluinos> can be pair produced if kinematically accessible. With conserved <R-parity>, their cascade decays end in a stable <lightest supersymmetric particle>, often a <neutralino>. The generic signature is energetic <particle jets>, possibly <leptons> or heavy-flavour jets, together with <missing transverse momentum> from invisible particles. Longitudinal missing momentum is not an equally clean observable because the initial <parton> momentum fractions are unknown.

A practical inclusive search defines <collider signal regions> using jet multiplicity, <transverse momenta>, <missing transverse momentum> and an event scale such as
$$
m_{\rm eff}=|\mathbf p_T^{\rm miss}|+\sum_{\rm selected\ jets}p_{T,j}.
$$
Leptonic and heavy-flavour selections probe other decay chains. For a <compressed supersymmetric spectrum>, the daughter particles can be soft, making a hard <initial-state radiation> jet useful for triggering and recoil. Direct <chargino> or <neutralino> production instead motivates multilepton searches. Decays with <R-parity violation> can yield high jet or <lepton> multiplicities with little missing momentum, and long-lived particles motivate displaced vertices, disappearing tracks or anomalous <ionization>. No single selection covers all these cases.

The difficult issue is background inference. Invisible $Z$ decay with jets is a real missing-momentum background: a photon-plus-jets sample or reconstructed $Z$ decays to charged <leptons> constrains its normalization and recoil shape through modeled transfer factors. A single-lepton <collider control region> constrains $W$ and top backgrounds whose charged <lepton> is missed in the <collider signal region>. Jet mismeasurement in ordinary multijet events gives instrumental missing momentum; angular cuts, detector-quality checks and control samples test that tail. Validation regions check these extrapolations without tuning on the final signal count.

<detector acceptance>, trigger efficiency, jet <calibration>, <collider luminosity>, theoretical production rates and transfer factors enter a likelihood as <nuisance parameters>. One compares the observed signal-region distribution with background-only and signal-plus-background hypotheses, using a <profile likelihood> and reporting exclusions for specified masses, <branching fractions> and spectra. As a pre-examination example, the 2014 ATLAS zero-lepton jets-plus-missing-momentum search used $20.3\ \mathrm{fb^{-1}}$ at $8\ \mathrm{TeV}$ and found no significant excess. Its interpretation depended on <R-parity> conservation and a <neutralino> lightest state; different simplified models gave different <mass> exclusions.<Search for squarks and gluinos with the ATLAS detector in final states with jets and missing transverse momentum using 8 TeV proton-proton collision data by the ATLAS Collaboration>[Sections 4–8, event selection, background control, statistical analysis and model-dependent interpretation]{ref}

**A null search excludes the tested parameter region, not <supersymmetry> as a whole.** Conversely, a missing-momentum excess alone would not establish <supersymmetry>: one would need consistent <masses>, decay chains, couplings and eventually <spin> information. A stable particle on detector flight-time scales is not thereby proved stable for the age of the universe.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The inference of <dark matter> uses a gravitational law to turn observed motions and lensing into a <mass> distribution. <modified gravity> asks whether some discrepancies instead reveal a breakdown of that law. The strongest motivation at galactic scales is the regular relation between baryonic structure and <galaxy rotation curves>, including approximately flat outer rotation speeds and the <baryonic Tully-Fisher relation>.

In <modified Newtonian dynamics>, the low-acceleration spherical relation is $g\,\mu(g/a_0)=g_N$, with $\mu(x)\to1$ at high acceleration and $\mu(x)\to x$ at low acceleration. For an isolated baryonic <mass> $M_b$ in the latter regime,
$$
\frac{v_c^4}{r^2a_0}=\frac{GM_b}{r^2},
\qquad
\boxed{v_c^4=GM_ba_0}.
$$
Thus an asymptotically flat rotation curve and a fourth-power <mass>–speed relation arise from a common acceleration scale, without assigning an independent halo profile to every <galaxy>. This is a substantive phenomenological motivation. Distances, stellar <mass-to-light ratios>, gas distributions and the external gravitational environment must still be controlled.<A modification of the Newtonian dynamics as a possible alternative to the hidden mass hypothesis by Mordehai Milgrom>[Astrophysical Journal 270, pp. 365–370, low-acceleration law and asymptotic rotation-speed prediction]{ref}

A nonrelativistic acceleration prescription does not itself specify light bending or cosmology. A relativistic completion must determine the <metric> followed by photons and matter. <tensor-vector-scalar gravity> illustrates the need for additional dynamical fields and a specified physical <metric>; lensing predictions then have to be calculated within that theory rather than imported unchanged from <general relativity>.<Relativistic gravitation theory for the MOND paradigm by Jacob Bekenstein>[Abstract and relativistic field construction, physical metric and lensing]{ref}

Several observations challenge a simple replacement of <dark matter> by a single galactic acceleration law. <galaxy clusters> retain substantial <mass> discrepancies even after such a modification. In the <bullet Cluster dark-matter separation>, the dominant X-ray-emitting baryonic gas is displaced from the galaxies, whereas weak-lensing <mass> concentrations lie nearer the galaxies. Collisionless <dark matter> accompanying the galaxies provides a natural account of this separation. The inference uses a lensing model and does not constitute a theorem excluding every conceivable modified-gravity theory, but a successful alternative must reproduce the displaced lensing signal quantitatively.<A direct empirical proof of the existence of dark matter by Douglas Clowe and others>[L109–L113, comparison of optical galaxies, X-ray gas and weak-lensing mass map]{ref}

The <cosmic microwave background>, acoustic structure, expansion history and growth of cosmic structure provide additional simultaneous tests. A theory that fits <galaxy rotation curves> must also supply the appropriate cosmological perturbation evolution and lensing. Adding extra clustering fields can help, but may reintroduce an effective unseen gravitating component. Solar-system and binary-pulsar agreement with <general relativity> constrain alternatives in their tested regimes, although a modification designed to act only at very low acceleration is not automatically ruled out by a high-acceleration test.

A discriminating programme compares dynamics, which probes the potential accelerating nonrelativistic matter, with lensing, which probes the sum of the two scalar <metric> potentials. Scale-dependent discrepancies, <gravitational slip> and environmental screening can then be tested with controlled <galaxy bias> and selection effects. Galaxy-scale tensions in a dark-matter model must likewise be separated from uncertain baryonic astrophysics. **Regular galactic scaling relations motivate <modified gravity>; clusters and the combined cosmological evidence strongly challenge its simplest dark-matter-free forms.** Failure to detect a particular <WIMP> is not positive evidence for a particular modified-gravity theory.
