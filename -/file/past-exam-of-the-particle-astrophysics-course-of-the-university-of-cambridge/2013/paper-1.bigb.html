For an isolated approximately spherical <mass> distribution outside its luminous extent, retain the gravitational <force> $GMm/r^2$ and use the specified low-acceleration inertial law. <circular motion> then gives
$$
\frac{GMm}{r^2}=\frac m{a_0}\left(\frac{v^2}{r}\right)^2,
\qquad\boxed{v^4=GMa_0}.
$$
The <radius> cancels when enclosed luminous <mass> is effectively constant. This is the asymptotic <modified Newtonian dynamics> result associated with the <baryonic Tully-Fisher relation>; an arbitrary extended disk cannot everywhere be replaced exactly by a point <mass>.

At the disk edge,
$$
a(R)=\frac{(9\times10^4)^2}{3\times10^{20}}
=\boxed{2.7\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}=0.27a_0}.
$$
Using $G=6.67\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}$ gives
$$
M_{\rm MOND}=\frac{v^4}{Ga_0}=9.84\times10^{39}\,\mathrm{kg}
=\boxed{4.9\times10^9M_\odot}.
$$
This is an order-of-magnitude isolated, low-acceleration estimate. The edge <acceleration> is below $a_0$, but not extremely small compared with it; a full interpolation law, disk geometry and an external gravitational field can affect a more precise <mass> fit.

For the <spherical mass profile for a flat rotation curve>, assume spherical symmetry where the halo dominates. A <flat rotation curve> gives $M(<r)=v^2r/G$, and differentiating $M(<r)=4\pi\int_0^r\rho(s)s^2\,ds$ yields
$$
\boxed{\rho(r)=\frac{v^2}{4\pi Gr^2}}.
$$
Here $\rho$ is the density of the spherical gravitating model; it is not an exact deprojection of a thin luminous disk or a proof that the central density must diverge. At the outermost stated measured <radius>,
$$
M(<2R)=\frac{v^2(2R)}G=7.29\times10^{40}\,\mathrm{kg}
=\boxed{3.6\times10^{10}M_\odot}.
$$
This gives the requested galaxy-plus-halo <mass> estimate if the model is truncated at the outermost observed <radius> $2R$. **The observation itself constrains enclosed <mass> within $2R$**; any additional outer halo remains unconstrained. Extending $\rho\propto r^{-2}$ to infinity would give divergent <mass>. Subtracting the MOND-inferred luminous <mass> to obtain a Newtonian halo <mass> would mix independent model-dependent estimates without an independently measured luminous <mass>.

In the <Bullet Cluster> collision, most observed baryonic <mass> is hot gas. <ram pressure> slows and <shocks> the gas, while the galaxies pass through much more freely. <weak gravitational lensing> maps the projected gravitational <mass> without requiring that the gas be in <hydrostatic equilibrium>. The dominant lensing peaks lie closer to the galaxies than to the displaced X-ray gas. This supports a dominant weakly collisional <dark matter> component under the usual gravity model. It is stronger evidence than a mass-to-light discrepancy alone because distinct components separate during the same encounter. The inference needs background-source shape/redshift <calibration>, projection control and uncertainty in the lensing reconstruction; it does not identify a <WIMP> species or by itself exclude every modified-gravity theory.<A direct empirical proof of the existence of dark matter by Douglas Clowe and others>[Abstract and comparison of lensing mass, optical galaxies and X-ray gas]{ref}

For <dark-matter direct detection>, consider <XENON100> at Gran Sasso. A halo <WIMP> can undergo <elastic scattering> from a <xenon> <atomic nucleus>, leaving a recoil of only a few to tens of keV. The expected rate is very low, so a large target, low threshold and long exposure must be combined with background rejection. The <nuclear-recoil kinematics of WIMP scattering> gives $E_{R,\max}=2\mu_{\chi N}^2v^2/m_N$ in the nonrelativistic limit, where $\mu_{\chi N}$ is the <reduced mass> and <masses> and <velocities> use consistent units. This explains why low-energy response <calibration> matters.

The dual-phase <xenon> detector measures prompt <particle scintillation> S1 and delayed <electroluminescence> S2 from <ionization> electrons extracted into gas. Their time separation gives interaction depth; the S2 light pattern supplies transverse position. An inner <fiducial volume> uses <xenon>'s self-shielding to suppress external backgrounds. The S2/S1 distribution statistically separates <nuclear recoils> from <electron recoils>; it is not perfect event-by-event identification. Underground siting reduces cosmic-ray backgrounds, while passive shielding, low-radioactivity components, <xenon> purification and a surrounding <active detector veto> reduce other events.<The XENON100 Dark Matter Experiment by the XENON100 Collaboration>[Sections 2–3, two-phase detection, detector design and background control]{ref}

Neutrons can also make <nuclear recoils>, so charge-to-light discrimination alone cannot eliminate them. Multiple-scatter rejection, shielding and background modelling remain necessary. Gamma-source and neutron-source <calibrations> map electron- and nuclear-recoil distributions, <energy> scale and selection acceptance. Position-dependent light collection and charge losses need correction; photon-counting fluctuations and uncertain low-energy yields limit threshold sensitivity. Blind event selection and independent sidebands guard against tuning cuts to apparent candidates. The 224.6-live-day, 34 kg search found two benchmark candidates against about one expected background event, consistent with background. Its interaction limit therefore depends on the assumed halo distribution, <WIMP> <mass> and nuclear response; it is not a detection or an exclusion of all <dark matter>.<Dark Matter Results from 225 Live Days of XENON100 Data by the XENON100 Collaboration>[Printed pp. 2–4, calibrations, blind selection and backgrounds; abstract for exposure and result]{ref}

A <grand unified theory> embeds the strong and electroweak <gauge groups> in a larger group, for example $SU(3)_c\times SU(2)_L\times U(1)_Y\subset SU(5)$. <quarks> and <leptons> can belong to common representations, while heavy extra gauge or scalar <bosons> connect them. The resulting interactions can violate <baryon number>, unlike ordinary perturbative Standard Model reactions. Running gauge couplings and proton-decay searches test such ideas; unification is a theoretical possibility, not an established observation.<Unified Gauge Theories and the Baryon Number of the Universe by Motohiko Yoshimura>[Abstract, early-universe baryon-number-violating reactions in unified theories; later erratum noted in source record]{ref}

<baryogenesis> must explain why a nearly matter-antimatter-symmetric early state evolves into a surviving <baryon asymmetry>. In the usual local CPT-invariant framework, the <Sakharov conditions> require baryon-number violation, C and <CP violation>, and departure from <thermal equilibrium>. <baryon> conservation would forbid creating a net excess; exact conjugation symmetries would pair baryon-producing processes with equal antibaryon production; equilibrium with no imposed asymmetry would erase a source term through <detailed balance>.

For heavy particles $X$ and $\bar X$, let decay channels carry different <baryon numbers>. CP-violating interference can make their <branching fractions> differ, even though <CPT symmetry> requires equal total widths. Defining the mean <baryon number> produced per decay as $\epsilon$, an out-of-equilibrium abundance can generate $n_B/s$ of order $\epsilon$ times its initial number per <entropy>, reduced by inefficient production and washout. A rough condition is that decays become effective after inverse production has ceased, with the relevant reaction rate compared with the <Hubble parameter>. Complex couplings alone do not guarantee a nonzero rate asymmetry: a relative <absorptive phase> is also needed.

Survival matters as much as production. Electroweak <sphalerons> violate $B+L$ while conserving <baryon-minus-lepton number> $B-L$, so a minimal GUT-generated asymmetry with $B-L=0$ can be washed out if <sphalerons> and the other relevant reactions equilibrate. Generating nonzero $B-L$, as in suitable heavy-Majorana-neutrino <leptogenesis>, can avoid this problem. **A viable GUT <baryogenesis> model must generate an asymmetry and demonstrate that subsequent reactions do not erase it.** The <Sakharov conditions> are necessary ingredients under these assumptions, not a numerical prediction of the observed abundance.

<supersymmetry> pairs bosonic and fermionic degrees of freedom; broken <supersymmetry> permits partners heavier than known particles. At the <Large Hadron Collider>, strong production of <squarks> and <gluinos> is an important channel. If <R-parity> is conserved, partners are produced in pairs and their cascades end in a stable <lightest supersymmetric particle>. A neutral weakly interacting <lightest supersymmetric particle> escapes, giving <missing transverse momentum> together with jets and possibly <leptons>. Conservation is tested transversely because the initial <parton> longitudinal momenta are unknown.

The experimental observable is
$$
\boldsymbol p_T^{\rm miss}=-\sum_{\rm reconstructed}\boldsymbol p_T.
$$
<neutrinos> produce genuine <missing transverse momentum>, and <calorimeter> mismeasurement or detector defects can imitate it. Searches use jet multiplicity, transverse momenta, missing <momentum>, angular separation cuts and a <collider effective mass> such as $m_{\rm eff}=p_T^{\rm miss}+\sum p_T^{\rm jet}$. Isolated-lepton selections explore complementary cascades. Trigger efficiency, jet <energy> scale, <collision pileup> and event cleaning affect acceptance.

A credible search estimates backgrounds from <particle-physics control regions> before inspecting the <signal region>. Invisible $Z\to\nu\bar\nu$ plus jets can be constrained using photon-plus-jets or visible $Z\to\ell^+\ell^-$ samples with <control-region transfer factors>. Lepton-enriched samples constrain $W$ and top backgrounds with lost <leptons>; multijet samples constrain fake missing <momentum>. A joint <likelihood> includes these observations and <nuisance parameters> for <systematic uncertainties>. Agreement with background gives upper bounds on signal rate and conditional <mass> exclusions. The pre-examination ATLAS 7 TeV analysis is a concrete example of this strategy and reported no significant excess.<Search for squarks and gluinos with the ATLAS detector in final states with jets and missing transverse momentum using 4.7 fb^-1 of sqrt(s) = 7 TeV proton-proton collision data by ATLAS Collaboration>[Sections III, IX and XI, control-region likelihood and background estimates; abstract for the null result]{ref}

Electroweak production motivates multilepton searches; compressed spectra can give soft visible decay products and require special selections such as recoil against <initial-state radiation>. <R-parity> violation can instead remove the stable missing-particle signature, requiring other searches. **An exclusion always depends on <masses>, <branching fractions>, production rates and decay assumptions; a null jets-plus-missing-momentum result does not exclude <supersymmetry> as a whole.**

A <gravitational wave> produces a time-dependent tidal distortion. For a normally incident plus-polarized wave in the long-wavelength limit, two perpendicular freely suspended arms change by $\delta L_x/L=h_+/2$ and $\delta L_y/L=-h_+/2$. Their differential length is therefore
$$
\boxed{\Delta L=Lh_+},\qquad
\delta\Phi=\frac{4\pi\Delta L}{\lambda}
$$
for a simple round-trip <Michelson interferometer>. <LIGO> uses kilometre-scale orthogonal arms with <optical cavities> that enhance the phase response, a stabilized <laser> and suspended mirrors in <vacuum>. As an illustrative scale, $h=10^{-21}$ across a 4 km arm corresponds to differential length $4\times10^{-18}\,\mathrm m$. The measured quantity is returning light phase, not mirror displacement against a rigid external ruler. Optical storage and control loops give a frequency-dependent calibrated response.

Seismic and terrestrial disturbances dominate low <frequencies>, thermal motion of suspensions and mirror coatings contributes at intermediate <frequencies>, and <photon> <shot noise> limits high-frequency readout. Isolation and suspension systems reduce ground coupling; low-loss materials reduce <thermal noise>; <vacuum> suppresses gas scattering and <forces>. Greater optical <power> reduces <shot noise> but increases radiation-pressure fluctuations and thermal demands. Stable optical alignment and feedback are essential, yet their own sensor and actuator noise must be controlled. These are engineering requirements because the desired <strain> is extraordinarily small.<LIGO: The Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory by B. P. Abbott and others>[Instrument configuration and detector-noise discussion; primary initial-LIGO account]{ref}

A candidate should appear coherently at separated observatories with consistent propagation delay and antenna response. Auxiliary environmental monitors, <calibration> injections and time-shifted coincidence backgrounds test instrumental explanations. Matched filtering is powerful for modelled compact-binary chirps; coherent burst searches allow less predictable signals. Binary-pulsar orbital <energy> loss already provided indirect evidence for <gravitational radiation> before 2013, but it is distinct from directly measuring passing waves.<Binary Pulsars and Relativistic Gravity by Joseph Hooton Taylor Jr.>[Binary-pulsar orbital-decay test of relativistic gravity]{ref} At the examination date, direct detection remained a search objective. **Reliable detection requires both sufficient <strain> sensitivity and a demonstrated astrophysical signal rather than correlated or accidental noise.**

= Solution

The <cosmic microwave background> is relic thermal <radiation> from the hot early universe. Its <temperature> anisotropy is expanded in <spherical harmonics>, with angular <power> spectrum $C_\ell^{TT}$. <Thomson scattering> of a local <radiation quadrupole> generates <linear polarization> around last scattering and again during <reionization>. Polarization maps are decomposed into <E-mode polarization> and <B-mode polarization>, giving spectra such as $EE$, $TE$ and $BB$ in addition to <temperature> information.

<Cosmic microwave background acoustic peak> positions compare the <sound horizon> with the angular-diameter distance to last scattering, constraining geometry and expansion history. Relative peak heights constrain <baryon> loading and <matter-radiation equality>; the damping tail constrains diffusion and other early-universe physics. The <scalar spectral index> and <primordial scalar amplitude> constrain inflationary perturbations. Coherent acoustic structure and temperature-polarization correlations support predominantly adiabatic initial perturbations. Large-angle <E-mode polarization> measures the <reionization> <optical depth>, helping break its degeneracy with the scalar amplitude inferred from small-scale <temperature> anisotropy.

Under a specified cosmological model, these data constrain ordinary and <cold dark matter> densities, the <Hubble constant>, spatial curvature and cosmic age. Complementary distance information helps break the geometrical degeneracy: <temperature> peaks alone do not uniquely establish a <cosmological constant>. The Planck 2013 parameter analysis used Planck <temperature> data and, in important combinations, <WMAP> low-multipole polarization; it should not be described as a full Planck polarization release.<Planck 2013 results. XVI. Cosmological parameters by Planck Collaboration>[Abstract and data-combination definitions, including WMAP polarization]{ref}

Linear <scalar cosmological perturbations> generate <E-mode polarization> but no primordial <B-mode polarization> in the simplest treatment. <tensor cosmological perturbations> can generate <B-mode polarization>, making them a possible probe of inflationary <gravitational waves>. <gravitational lensing> converts some E-mode <power> into <B-mode polarization>, however, and Galactic dust and <synchrotron radiation> also contribute polarized foregrounds. Multifrequency observations, foreground modelling, beam <calibration> and control of <temperature-to-polarization leakage> are therefore necessary. Polarization does not by itself establish a primordial tensor signal. **<temperature> and polarization jointly test the expansion history and primordial perturbations, while complementary data and foreground control determine how uniquely parameters can be inferred.**

For a homogeneous canonical <scalar field>, $\rho_\phi=\dot\phi^2/2+V$ and $P_\phi=\dot\phi^2/2-V$. Potential-energy domination gives $P_\phi\simeq-\rho_\phi$, accelerated expansion and a decreasing <comoving Hubble radius>. <Hubble friction> can permit slow roll down a sufficiently flat potential. Enough inflation stretches an initially small causally connected region to encompass our observable universe, suppresses the relative spatial-curvature term and dilutes unwanted massive relics. <quantum fluctuations> supply possible seeds for cosmological structure; a successful model must also end inflation and create the hot post-inflationary universe. These statements do not imply that arbitrary initial conditions inevitably inflate.

Use the stated reduced-Planck units throughout the field calculation. Differentiating the Friedmann constraint and substituting the field equation gives
$$
2H\dot H=\frac13\dot\phi(\ddot\phi+m^2\phi)
=-H\dot\phi^2,\qquad
\boxed{\dot H=-\frac12\dot\phi^2}.
$$
Division assumes the nontrivial expanding branch $H>0$; the <vacuum> endpoint follows continuously.

During rapid <oscillations>, require $m\gg H$ and $|\dot A|\ll mA$. To leading order, $\dot\phi\simeq-mA\sin mt$, while the Friedmann constraint gives $H_m^2\simeq m^2A^2/6$. Thus
$$
\dot H\simeq-\frac{m^2A^2}{2}\sin^2mt
=-\frac32H_m^2(1-\cos2mt).
$$
Averaging over many <oscillations> on a timescale still short compared with $H^{-1}$ gives
$$
\boxed{\dot H_m=-\frac32H_m^2}.
$$
The <quadratic-inflaton Hubble oscillations> obey, to leading order in $H_m/m$,
$$
H_{\rm fluct}\simeq\frac{3H_m^2}{4m}\sin2mt,
\qquad \frac{|H_{\rm fluct}|}{H_m}=O(H_m/m)\ll1.
$$
Differentiating this expression reproduces the cosine term, with smaller corrections from the slowly varying amplitude. Instantaneously $H$ remains nonincreasing because its exact <derivative> is $-\dot\phi^2/2$; an oscillatory correction does not mean oscillatory growth of the total <Hubble parameter>.

The mean equation integrates to
$$
\frac1{H_m(t)}=\frac1{H_m(t_i)}+\frac32(t-t_i),\qquad
\boxed{H_m(t)=\frac{2}{3(t-t_*)}},\qquad
\boxed{a(t)\propto(t-t_*)^{2/3}}.
$$
The shift $t_*$ is set by matching to the earlier evolution, not necessarily the Big Bang origin of the full solution. Averaged kinetic and <potential energies> are equal, so $\langle P_\phi\rangle\simeq0$, $\langle\rho_\phi\rangle\propto a^{-3}$ and $A\propto a^{-3/2}$. **The coherent quadratic <oscillations> behave as <pressureless matter>, not <radiation>**, even though they are a <scalar field> rather than a gas of cold particles. <radiation> would instead give $a\propto t^{1/2}$. The averaged approximation is controlled only after <oscillations> are fast compared with expansion.

<reheating> requires interactions beyond the isolated field equations above. The oscillating <inflaton> can decay into other particles, whose scattering and further decays establish a thermal bath. The <perturbative inflaton reheating equations> are
$$
\dot\rho_\phi+3H\rho_\phi=-\Gamma\rho_\phi,\qquad
\dot\rho_r+4H\rho_r=\Gamma\rho_\phi.
$$
Expansion redshifts the components differently; transfer becomes efficient around $H\sim\Gamma$, and <radiation> domination follows when the transferred relativistic <energy> dominates. For suitable couplings, nonperturbative <preheating> by <parametric resonance> can precede thermalization, with backreaction and rescattering affecting its completion. Neither the decay rate nor thermalization is specified by $m$ and $V$ alone. Instantaneous <reheating> idealizes all this as immediate conversion of the end-of-inflation <energy> to <radiation>.<Towards the Theory of Reheating After Inflation by Lev Kofman and others>[Abstract and Introduction: perturbative decay, resonant preheating, backreaction and thermalization]{ref}

For <instantaneous-reheating horizon matching>, specify the comparison: take the initial patch to be of order $H_i^{-1}$, compare the same-shaped inflationary history with approximately fixed $H_i/H_{\rm end}$, and assume instantaneous <reheating> followed by <adiabatic expansion>. Ignoring changes in relativistic degrees of freedom, $a_0/a_{\rm reh}\propto T_{\rm reh}$, while <energy> conservation and the <Friedmann equation> give $H_{\rm end}\propto T_{\rm reh}^2$. The present size of this inflated patch therefore scales as
$$
\ell_0\sim H_i^{-1}e^N\frac{a_0}{a_{\rm reh}}
\propto\frac{e^N}{T_{\rm reh}}.
$$
At fixed present <Hubble radius>, **a higher instantaneous <reheating> scale requires more e-folds** in this standard comparison, approximately $\Delta N\simeq\ln(T_2/T_1)$, up to slow scale and entropy-degree corrections. Hotter <reheating> gives more subsequent expansion, but the associated initial Hubble patch is smaller by a stronger <power> of <temperature>. If instead one artificially holds the initial physical patch or $H_i$ fixed while varying the end <energy> independently, the post-inflationary geometric effect alone would give the opposite trend. <temperature> without an inflation-scale comparison convention does not fix that alternative problem; this is why the matching assumptions must be stated.<How long before the end of inflation were observable perturbations produced? by Andrew R. Liddle and Samuel M. Leach>[Section II, equations 2, 6–8: horizon-scale matching and its dependence on inflation and reheating energy scales]{ref}
