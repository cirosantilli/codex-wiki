= Solution
{parent=1}

The metric source is a gravitational frequency shift. <photons> following <null geodesics> through a time-dependent spatial metric change <momentum> relative to the background comoving frame. The directional projection of that metric change gives the $\mu^2$ factor. It is not a collision term or continuing <photon> scattering after recombination. The normalization and sign belong to the specified definition of $h$ and $\Delta$; a distribution perturbation should not silently be identified with <temperature> without fixing that normalization.

Define $\mathcal H=\dot R/R$, where dots here mean conformal-time derivatives. In an Einstein-de Sitter background,
$$
\mathcal H=\frac2\tau,\qquad
\mathcal H^2=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\bar\rho_mR^2,
\qquad4\pi G\bar\rho_mR^2=\frac6{\tau^2}.
$$
A <power law> $h\propto\tau^p$ therefore satisfies $p(p-1)+2p=6$, or $(p-2)(p+3)=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{h=A\tau^2+B\tau^{-3}},
$$
with the required growing mode $h=A\tau^2$. This is a solution in the perturbation convention used in the question, rather than a statement that every gauge-invariant gravitational potential grows as $\tau^2$.

Use an <integrating factor> for the <photon> equation:
$$
\Delta(\tau)=\Delta_R e^{-ik\mu(\tau-\tau_R)}
+2\mu^2\int_{\tau_R}^{\tau}\dot h(t)e^{-ik\mu(\tau-t)}dt.
$$
For the growing mode $\dddot h=0$. For the <polynomial-source photon transport solution>, a particular solution is $B_h=-2i\mu\dot h/k+2\ddot h/k^2$: direct substitution gives $\dot B_h+ik\mu B_h=2\mu^2\dot h$. The initial-value solution is consequently
$$
\boxed{\Delta(\tau)=\left[\Delta_R+\frac{2i\mu\dot h_R}{k}-\frac{2\ddot h_R}{k^2}\right]e^{-ik\mu(\tau-\tau_R)}
-\frac{2i\mu\dot h(\tau)}{k}+\frac{2\ddot h(\tau)}{k^2}}.
$$
This also follows by integrating the source twice by parts. The apparent inverse powers of $k$ cancel in the complete long-wavelength limit; the integral form remains regular. At $\mu=0$, the source vanishes and the complete expression reduces to $\Delta=\Delta_R$.

Physically, the exponential transports the recombination pattern along <photon> trajectories; its direction-dependent phase projects a Fourier perturbation onto different angular multipoles. The extra terms are the accumulated gravitational contribution, here expressible as endpoint terms because the source has this special time dependence. For <endpoint monopole and dipole in photon transport>, at the observer the last two terms are respectively a directional dipole and an angular monopole for each mode; they do not supply a new sky pattern at multipoles $\ell\geq2$. The propagating recombination combination contains the intrinsic perturbation and gravitational/velocity endpoint contributions. Individual terms depend on the perturbation gauge; observable <temperature> anisotropy requires the consistent emitter and observer contributions.

In pure matter domination the gauge-invariant scalar potentials are constant for a growing linear mode: the matter contrast grows as $R$ while the potential scales as its contrast divided by $R$. Thus **growth of this $h$ is compatible with no late <Integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect> in Einstein-de Sitter**. When <dark energy> suppresses matter growth, the potentials generally evolve; <photons> gain and lose unequal energies entering and leaving a potential well. In a convention where $\Phi,\Psi$ are the dimensionless Newtonian-gauge potentials, the integrated <temperature> source is $\int(\dot\Phi+\dot\Psi)d\tau$ along the light path.

Large-angle cosmic microwave background data can constrain that late contribution, but low-multipole power alone has large <cosmic variance> and depends on the primordial <radiation spectrum>, geometry and foreground treatment. Cross-correlating the <temperature> map with low-redshift galaxies or other matter tracers isolates a late-time connection; decaying wells typically correlate overdensities with hotter <cosmic microwave background> directions. Boughn and Crittenden compared WMAP <temperature> data with the HEAO1 hard X-ray background and examined possible foreground origins of the correlation. This tests potential evolution under a cosmological model, not <dark energy> independently of all gravity and <curvature> assumptions.<Investigating the origins of the CMB-XRB cross correlation by Stephen Boughn and Robert Crittenden>[abstract and foreground-correlation tests]{ref}

= Solution

Gravitational evidence requires much more nonbaryonic matter than can be supplied by ordinary luminous material or by the baryon abundance permitted by <nucleosynthesis>. A stable, electrically neutral massive particle is a plausible cold component. Thermal production followed by <cosmological particle freeze-out> gives an additional motivation: an <annihilation> rate near a weak-interaction scale can leave an appreciable relic abundance. This is a mechanism and search hypothesis, not proof that the <dark matter> must be a <WIMP> or have any particular elastic-scattering <cross section>.

The plotted regions are conditional <WIMP> interpretations of different measurements. The experiments use different targets and distinguish signal from background differently:

* <DAMA experiment> uses NaI(Tl) <particle scintillation> and seeks an annual variation of low-energy single-hit counts as the Earth's velocity through the halo changes. Multiple-hit events and other <energy> ranges provide controls, but <particle scintillation> alone does not identify every event as an <electron> or <nuclear recoil>. Converting the modulation into <mass> and <cross section> requires a <halo model> and <nuclear-recoil quenching factor> assumptions.<new-results-from-dama-libra-by-r-bernabei-and-others>[abstract and single-hit annual-modulation analysis]{ref}
* <CoGeNT experiment> uses a low-capacitance p-type point-contact <germanium> diode to reach low <ionization> thresholds. Low-energy event <radiation spectrum> and a possible annual modulation can suggest light <dark matter>, but surface charge collection, internal x-ray lines and threshold efficiency must be distinguished from bulk recoil events. Its 2011 modulation paper described a modulated component of unknown origin rather than an unambiguous particle identification.<Search for an Annual Modulation in a P-type Point Contact Germanium Dark Matter Detector by C. E. Aalseth and others>[abstract and pulse/energy-event analysis]{ref}
* <CDMS experiment> measures <phonons> and <ionization> in cryogenic <germanium> or <silicon>. The smaller <ionization> yield of <nuclear recoils> and <phonon> timing help reject <electron> and surface events. The lowered-threshold <germanium> analysis improves low-mass sensitivity, at the cost of more difficult background discrimination near threshold; this explains why distinct 2010 and 2011 curves appear.<Results from a Low-Energy Analysis of the CDMS II Germanium Data by CDMS Collaboration>[abstract; low-threshold analysis and background discussion]{ref}
* <XENON100> uses a <dual-phase xenon detector>. A recoil produces prompt liquid <particle scintillation>, S1, and <ionization> <electrons>. A drift field transports the charge to the surface; extracted <electrons> produce gas <electroluminescence>, S2. Drift delay and the S2 light pattern reconstruct position, while S2/S1 statistically separates <electron> and <nuclear recoils>. An inner <fiducial volume> suppresses external radiation. Neutron and gamma <calibrations> establish recoil response and leakage, and low-light-yield <calibration> is particularly important for light <WIMPS>. Neutrons can themselves mimic <nuclear recoils>.<The XENON100 Dark Matter Experiment by the XENON100 Collaboration>[detector principle, signal reconstruction and calibration sections]{ref}

The plot also includes <CRESST experiment>, whose cryogenic calcium-tungstate modules combine <phonon> and scintillation-light signals. Different nuclei contribute to its recoil interpretation. Its excess region is another conditional candidate region, not an extra confirmed detection.<Results from 730 kg days of the CRESST-II Dark Matter Search by G. Angloher and others>[abstract and detector/recoil-population analysis]{ref}

Around ten GeV, the shaded regions favour <cross sections> roughly $10^{-41}$–$10^{-40}\,\mathrm{cm^2}$, while the <XENON100> solid curve and the improved low-mass CDMS curve cut into or exclude much of those regions under the common elastic, <spin-independent WIMP-nucleus scattering> interpretation expressed per <nucleon>. **There is tension within that shared model; the plot does not show mutually confirmed discoveries.** <electron> backgrounds, threshold response, <nuclear-recoil quenching factors>, channeling assumptions and the halo velocity distribution can shift interpretations. Changing the interaction model can also alter target comparisons, but cannot establish a <WIMP> by assertion. The 100-live-day <XENON100> result itself reported candidates compatible with estimated background.<Dark Matter Results from 100 Live Days of XENON100 Data by XENON100 Collaboration>[abstract; background-compatible candidates and elastic spin-independent limit]{ref}

For a rough dimensional event estimate, $1\,\mathrm{cm^2}=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$ gives
$$
10^{-41}\text{--}10^{-40}\,\mathrm{cm^2}=0.01\text{--}0.1\,\mathrm{fb},
\qquad \mathcal L\sigma\sim20(0.01\text{--}0.1)=\boxed{0.2\text{--}2\ \text{events}}.
$$
If a collider production <cross section> had this value, that small yield, before acceptance and backgrounds, would not support a likely discovery. **The plotted nuclear-scattering <cross section> is not the LHC production <cross section>**, however. Converting between them needs a mediator, couplings, parton distributions and production kinematics; a light stable <WIMP> would normally be inferred from <missing transverse momentum> with a visible recoil object, not a visible <WIMP> track. The <mass> makes <pair production> kinematically accessible, but the plot and <integrated collider luminosity> alone do not decide discovery probability.

For the relic estimate, define $M=mc^2$ and $\theta=k_BT$. The <sharp-freeze-out relic dilution> model gives
$$
\theta_f=\frac{M}{P}=\frac{10}{30}\,\mathrm{GeV},\qquad
n_f=6\left[\frac{M\theta_f}{(\hbar c)^2}\right]^{3/2}e^{-P}
=4.27\times10^{35}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}.
$$
Assume stable particles, no subsequent <annihilation>, and $RT=\mathrm{constant}$. Conserved comoving particle number then gives
$$
n_0=n_f\left(\frac{T_0}{T_f}\right)^3
=6P^{3/2}\left(\frac{k_BT_0}{\hbar c}\right)^3e^{-P}
=\boxed{0.1497\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}}.
$$
The present rest-energy density and its critical fraction are
$$
\boxed{\rho_{\chi,0}=Mn_0\simeq1.50\,\mathrm{GeV\,m^{-3}},\qquad
\Omega_\chi\simeq\frac{1.50}{5}=0.30}.
$$
Thus the supplied toy model yields a cosmologically substantial density, of the right broad scale to motivate a dark-matter candidate. It is not an independent precision prediction: $P$ is imposed and the abundance depends exponentially on it. If <entropy degrees of freedom> change, <entropy> conservation instead gives an extra factor $g_{*s,0}/g_{*s,f}$ in $n_0$ when using the <photon> <temperature>. The species content needed to evaluate that correction is not supplied. A realistic calculation also follows residual <annihilations> with the abundance <Boltzmann equation> rather than stopping exactly at equilibrium freeze-out.

= Solution
{parent=a}

<annihilation> can produce <gamma rays>, charged <antiparticles> and <neutrinos>, directly or through decay and <hadronization> of the primary products. For self-conjugate <dark matter> of <mass> $m_\chi$, the <annihilation> rate per unit volume is $\langle\sigma v\rangle\rho_\chi^2/(2m_\chi^2)$. For a <photon> yield $dN_\gamma/dE$, the optically thin flux from a chosen solid angle is
$$
\frac{d\Phi_\gamma}{dE}=\frac{\langle\sigma v\rangle}{8\pi m_\chi^2}\frac{dN_\gamma}{dE}\,
\underbrace{\int_{\Delta\Omega}d\Omega\int_{\rm los}\rho_\chi^2\,dl}_{J}.
$$
Use consistent mass-energy units. The factor $1/2$ assumes identical self-conjugate particles; distinct particle-antiparticle populations require their separate <number densities>. A gamma-ray continuum with an endpoint near the particle <mass> is generic for many channels, whereas a narrow line from $\chi\chi\to\gamma\gamma$ or $\gamma Z$ would be especially distinctive but often loop suppressed. <dwarf spheroidal galaxies> offer high dark-matter content with limited conventional emission; the <Galactic centre> has a potentially larger signal and much more complicated foregrounds.

Charged <Positrons> and <antiprotons> are redistributed by <magnetic fields> and lose <energy>, so their <radiation spectrum> depend on propagation and cannot usually point directly to the source. <annihilation> <electrons> can also produce synchrotron and inverse-Compton radiation. <dark matter> captured in the Sun could produce escaping high-energy <neutrinos>, with the signal depending on capture, <annihilation> equilibrium and particle interactions. Early <annihilation> <energy> injection can alter recombination and <cosmic microwave background> anisotropies.

**A persuasive interpretation joins particle-like spectral structure with a consistent density-dependent morphology and other channels.** Pulsars, cosmic-ray interactions and unresolved sources can imitate continuum excesses. Density-profile uncertainties, substructure and velocity-dependent <annihilation> modify the predicted signal; a lack of a detectable line does not exclude all annihilating <dark matter>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

A <cosmological constant> has fixed density and <equation of state> $w=p/(\rho c^2)=-1$. A separately conserved dynamical component obeys
$$
\rho_{\rm de}(z)=\rho_{{\rm de},0}\exp\!\left[3\int_0^z\frac{1+w(z')}{1+z'}\,dz'\right].
$$
If $w$ departs from $-1$, its history changes the <Hubble parameter>, <luminosity distances> and angular-diameter distances. Type Ia supernovae probe a relative distance-redshift relation, <baryon acoustic oscillations> provide standard-ruler distances and expansion information, and the <cosmic microwave background> fixes early-time scales and geometric combinations. Combining these breaks some degeneracies with <matter density>, <curvature> and <calibration>. Distance observables integrate the expansion history, so they smooth rapid variations in $w$ and cannot determine an arbitrary function exactly.

Structure-growth measurements add a dynamical test. Galaxy clustering, <redshift-space distortions> and <weak gravitational lensing> constrain the growth of matter perturbations and metric potentials. The late <Integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect> probes potential evolution through CMB-matter correlations. A scalar-field dark-energy model can have perturbations and an effective <sound speed> different from other models with a similar background expansion. Comparisons of geometry and growth can therefore distinguish some models and test gravity assumptions.

**A statistically significant, robust departure from $w=-1$ or from constant dark-energy density would disfavor a pure <cosmological constant> within the stated framework.** Consistency with $w=-1$ over a finite redshift range does not prove exact constancy: a slowly rolling field can mimic it. Supernova <calibration>/evolution, <galaxy bias>, <photometric redshift> errors, <intrinsic galaxy alignments> and foregrounds must be controlled; modified gravity can also change growth. Parameterizations such as $w(a)=w_0+w_a(1-a)$ are useful finite-dimensional tests rather than a complete classification of all dark-energy histories.

= Solution

The baryon excess requires a mechanism that produces different net <baryon numbers> from an initially symmetric ensemble. In the usual local, CPT-invariant framework, the <Sakharov conditions> are baryon-number violation, C and <CP violation>, and departure from <thermal equilibrium>. Baryon conservation would preserve zero net <baryon number>; exact conjugation symmetries equate the conjugate production processes; equilibrium inverse reactions and <detailed balance> erase a would-be asymmetry. These necessary conditions do not determine its magnitude.<Violation of CP invariance, C asymmetry, and baryon asymmetry of the universe by Andrei Sakharov>[1967 original statement of the conditions]{ref}

For example, let equal numbers of a heavy particle $X$ and its <antiparticle> decay into channels $i$ with <baryon numbers> $B_i$. Their mean net yield per pair is
$$
\epsilon_{\rm pair}=\sum_i B_i\left[\operatorname{Br}(X\to i)-\operatorname{Br}(\bar X\to\bar i)\right].
$$
It can be nonzero even though CPT requires equal total widths: the <branching fractions> differ among channels carrying different <baryon numbers>. A nonzero decay-rate asymmetry requires interference with both a CP-odd <weak phase> and an <absorptive phase>. If the expansion rate overtakes the inverse-reaction rate, decays can occur out of equilibrium; later baryon-violating washout must then be weak enough for the asymmetry to survive.

In electroweak <baryogenesis>, CP-asymmetric transport near a first-order phase-transition boundary can bias baryon-violating <sphaleron> transitions. <CP violation> alone is insufficient: the transition and washout dynamics also matter. In <leptogenesis>, out-of-equilibrium decays of heavy <Majorana neutrinos> can generate <lepton> asymmetry, part of which <sphalerons> convert into baryon asymmetry while conserving $B-L$. The CKM phase demonstrates <CP violation> in the <Standard Model> but does not by itself supply a successful cosmological mechanism. **The key quantity is a surviving net charge yield, not merely the existence of a CP-violating reaction.**

= Solution
{parent=d}

A <Majorana neutrino> is its own <antiparticle>, with a <mass> term that can violate <lepton number> by two units. It is not thereby a state of both readily observable <helicities> with equal weak rates: charged-current vertices still select <chirality>. In light-neutrino exchange, the needed <helicity> connection between two beta vertices supplies a neutrino-mass factor.

Ordinary two-neutrino <double beta decay> is $(A,Z)\to(A,Z+2)+2e^-+2\bar\nu_e$ and conserves <lepton number>. It can occur when the direct single-beta route is energetically forbidden or highly suppressed but the double change is allowed. <neutrinoless double beta decay>, $(A,Z)\to(A,Z+2)+2e^-$, violates <lepton number> by two. For the conventional <light-Majorana exchange in neutrinoless double beta decay> mechanism,
$$
[T_{1/2}^{0\nu}]^{-1}=G_{0\nu}|\mathcal M_{0\nu}|^2
\left|\frac{m_{\beta\beta}}{m_e}\right|^2,\qquad
m_{\beta\beta}=\left|\sum_iU_{ei}^2m_i\right|.
$$
Here $U$ is the <lepton mixing matrix>, $G_{0\nu}$ the phase-space factor in this convention and $\mathcal M_{0\nu}$ a <nuclear matrix element>. Complex phases can cancel contributions to the <effective Majorana mass>; oscillation <mass> differences alone do not fix it. Other lepton-number-violating mechanisms can also cause the process, so a rate is not a model-independent measurement of $m_{\beta\beta}$.

The experimental signature is a peak in the summed <electron> <energy> at the nuclear Q-value, apart from small recoil and <energy> losses. Two-neutrino decay instead gives a continuous sum-energy <radiation spectrum> because <neutrinos> carry away variable <energy>. Isotope enrichment, large exposure, good <energy resolution> and radiopure shielding improve sensitivity; gamma backgrounds, <radon>, surface events and the two-neutrino tail require <calibration> and sideband controls. Coincidence or track information can test whether the topology is compatible with two <electrons>. A later concrete example is GERDA's 2013 germanium-76 source-detector search, which used pulse-shape discrimination and background controls rather than identifying any isolated <energy> deposit as a decay.<Results on neutrinoless double beta decay of 76Ge from GERDA Phase I by the GERDA Collaboration>[experimental method and pulse-shape/background analysis; published after this exam]{ref}

**Observation would establish lepton-number violation and support a Majorana <mass> component; a null search constrains mechanisms and does not prove <neutrinos> are Dirac particles.**
