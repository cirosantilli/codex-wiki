= Solution

Use the angular coordinate $\theta$ for the position on the ring. The <kinetic energy> is $T=ma^2\dot\theta^2/2$. The <gravitational potential energy> is constant because the ring is horizontal, and the ideal constraint force has no tangential component. Thus the <Lagrangian> has no dependence on $\theta$ itself: it is a <cyclic coordinate>.

The <Euler-Lagrange equation> gives
$$
\frac d{dt}(ma^2\dot\theta)=0,\qquad\boxed{\dot\theta=\text{constant}.}
$$
Equivalently, the conserved <conjugate momentum> $p_\theta=ma^2\dot\theta$ is the vertical <angular momentum>, as required by <rotational symmetry>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let $\theta$ and $\varphi$ be the lower and upper azimuthal angles, measured from a common fixed direction. The squared spring length is
$$
d^2=(a\cos\theta-A\cos\varphi)^2+(a\sin\theta-A\sin\varphi)^2+h^2=a^2+A^2+h^2-2aA\cos(\theta-\varphi).
$$
By <Hooke's law>, a spring with zero natural length has <potential energy> $kd^2/2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{L=\frac12ma^2\dot\theta^2+\frac12MA^2\dot\varphi^2-\frac k2[a^2+A^2+h^2-2aA\cos(\theta-\varphi)].}
$$
Constant gravitational energies can be omitted. It is useful to define the <moments of inertia> $I_1=ma^2$, $I_2=MA^2$ and the angular coupling $\kappa=kaA$. The part of the potential that affects motion is simply $-\kappa\cos(\theta-\varphi)$. In particular, $h$ changes the constant spring energy but not the angular equations, because the spring has zero natural length.

= Solution

The two continuous, time-independent <symmetries> are time translation and a common <rotation> of both angles. Their <Noether charges> are respectively the total <energy> and vertical <angular momentum>:
$$
\boxed{E=\frac12I_1\dot\theta^2+\frac12I_2\dot\varphi^2+\frac k2[a^2+A^2+h^2-2aA\cos(\theta-\varphi)],}
$$
$$
\boxed{J=I_1\dot\theta+I_2\dot\varphi.}
$$
For the second symmetry, $\theta\mapsto\theta+\epsilon$ and $\varphi\mapsto\varphi+\epsilon$, so the <Noether theorem> gives the sum of their conjugate momenta. Independent <rotations> are not <symmetries> of the spring coupling.

There are also <discrete symmetries>: simultaneous reflection $(\theta,\varphi)\mapsto(-\theta,-\varphi)$, and <time reversal in classical mechanics>, which reverses both angular velocities. These leave the dynamics invariant but provide no additional infinitesimal <Noether charges>. Combined with common rotations, the reflections give the full planar orthogonal symmetry of the angular geometry. If $I_1=I_2$, interchange of the two angular coordinates is an additional <discrete symmetry> of the reduced dynamics.

There is a further useful time-dependent <symmetry>, up to a <total derivative>, on a continuous lift of the angles: adding the same constant <angular velocity>, $(\theta,\varphi)\mapsto(\theta+vt,\varphi+vt)$. It changes $L$ by
$$
\frac d{dt}\left[v(I_1\theta+I_2\varphi)+\frac12(I_1+I_2)v^2t\right].
$$
Its conserved quantity is $\boxed{I_1\theta+I_2\varphi-tJ}$. It is the integration constant for free common rotation, rather than a new globally single-valued angle charge on the configuration torus. The globally defined continuous charges are $E$ and $J$ above.

The angular <Euler-Lagrange equations> are
$$
I_1\ddot\theta=-\kappa\sin(\theta-\varphi),\qquad I_2\ddot\varphi=\kappa\sin(\theta-\varphi).
$$
A static <equilibrium point> has both angular velocities zero and $\sin(\theta-\varphi)=0$. Hence the complete equilibrium families, with angles taken modulo $2\pi$, are
$$
\boxed{(\theta,\varphi)=(q,q)\quad\text{or}\quad(\theta,\varphi)=(q+\pi,q),\qquad q\in\mathbb R/(2\pi\mathbb Z).}
$$
The first family is aligned in azimuth and minimizes the spring length; the second is antipodal and maximizes it. Their potentials are $k[(a-A)^2+h^2]/2$ and $k[(a+A)^2+h^2]/2$, respectively. Common rotations generate a whole family rather than isolated equilibria.

Let $\delta_0=\theta_0-\varphi_0$ be $0$ or $\pi$, and write the angular displacements as $\eta_1,\eta_2$. To quadratic order the restoring <potential energy> is $\kappa\cos\delta_0(\eta_1-\eta_2)^2/2$. The linearized <normal mode> equation is
$$
\left[\kappa\cos\delta_0\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}-\omega^2\begin{pmatrix}I_1&0\\0&I_2\end{pmatrix}\right]\begin{pmatrix}\eta_1\\\eta_2\end{pmatrix}=0.
$$
The <determinant> gives $\omega^2[\omega^2I_1I_2-\kappa\cos\delta_0(I_1+I_2)]=0$. Thus the <normal modes of two spring-coupled coaxial rotors> are
$$
\boxed{\omega_0^2=0,\qquad(\eta_1,\eta_2)\propto(1,1),}
$$
$$
\boxed{\omega_{\mathrm{rel}}^2=\kappa\left(\frac1{I_1}+\frac1{I_2}\right)\cos\delta_0,\qquad(\eta_1,\eta_2)\propto(I_2,-I_1).}
$$
For the aligned family, the relative mode oscillates with positive frequency
$$
\Omega=\sqrt{kaA\left(\frac1{ma^2}+\frac1{MA^2}\right)}.
$$
For the antipodal family, $\omega_{\mathrm{rel}}^2=-\Omega^2$, so that displacement has solutions $e^{\pm\Omega t}$ and the equilibrium is unstable.

The common <zero mode> has $\eta_1=\eta_2=C+Dt$. It describes a neutral displacement or uniform common <rotation>, not a restoring oscillation. Thus the aligned family is stable with respect to relative motion and has <orbital stability> modulo common rotations. A particular static point is not strictly <Lyapunov stable> against unrestricted small velocity perturbations: arbitrarily small nonzero $J$ eventually moves the common angle far from that point. With $J=0$, the common angle is fixed and the aligned relative <equilibrium point> is stable. This distinguishes the symmetry-induced drift from the exponential instability of the antipodal family.

= Solution
{parent=f}

A common rotation changes neither the separation nor the spring energy. Its infinitesimal direction is $(1,1)$, exactly the zero-frequency <normal mode>. Thus the continuum of rotated <equilibrium points> requires that zero mode; a nonzero restoring frequency in that direction would contradict <rotational symmetry>.

The second mode has $I_1\eta_1+I_2\eta_2=0$, so it changes the relative angle while leaving the inertia-weighted common angle unchanged. It therefore carries zero perturbation in the conserved <angular momentum> associated with common motion. Indeed the coordinates
$$
q=\frac{I_1\theta+I_2\varphi}{I_1+I_2},\qquad\delta=\theta-\varphi
$$
separate the <Lagrangian> exactly:
$$
L=\frac12(I_1+I_2)\dot q^2+\frac12\frac{I_1I_2}{I_1+I_2}\dot\delta^2+\kappa\cos\delta-\text{constant}.
$$
The <cyclic coordinate> $q$ is free, while $\delta$ has restoring or inverted curvature. This also explains why all members of either equilibrium family have the same frequencies. Reflection symmetry makes the potential even in $\delta$, and the stable oscillatory mode is consistent with <time reversal in classical mechanics> through its two opposite frequency signs.

Vary the <action> $S=\int dt\,dx\,\mathcal L$ with compactly supported $\delta\phi$, or impose vanishing $\delta\phi$ and its first derivatives on the spacetime boundary. The <first variation> is
$$
\delta S=\int dt\,dx\left[\mathcal L_\phi\delta\phi+\mathcal L_{\phi_{,\mu}}\partial_\mu\delta\phi+\mathcal L_{\phi_{,\mu\nu}}\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\delta\phi\right].
$$
One <integration by parts> on the first-derivative term and two on the second-derivative term remove all <derivatives> from $\delta\phi$. The boundary terms vanish, leaving
$$
\delta S=\int dt\,dx\left[\mathcal L_\phi-\partial_\mu\mathcal L_{\phi_{,\mu}}+\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\mathcal L_{\phi_{,\mu\nu}}\right]\delta\phi.
$$
The <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> gives the <Euler-Lagrange field equation with second derivatives>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi}-\partial_\mu\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}+\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\phi)}=0.}
$$
Repeated indices run over time and space. The coefficient of the symmetric second derivative is defined by the displayed variation; if $\phi_{tx}$ is instead treated as a single independent mixed derivative variable, it is counted once rather than twice.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Interpret $\phi$ as the small transverse displacement of the <elastic beam>. The first term is the positive <kinetic energy> density $\rho\phi_t^2/2$, with $\rho$ the mass per unit length. The <Lagrangian density> is kinetic minus potential energy, so the potential density encoded by the other two terms is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal V=-\frac\alpha2\phi_x^2+\frac\beta2\phi_{xx}^2.}
$$
For a compressed rod, transverse deflection releases compression energy: the small-slope geometric change in length is proportional to $\phi_x^2/2$, so the applied compressive load lowers the potential by $\alpha\phi_x^2/2$. This explains the positive sign of the $\alpha$ term in the Lagrangian. Tension would give the opposite sign and a restoring gradient term.

Bending costs <elastic energy>. At small slopes the <curvature> is $\phi_{xx}$, so positive <bending stiffness> $\beta$ gives potential $\beta\phi_{xx}^2/2$, and hence a negative term in the Lagrangian. For a uniform slender beam $\beta$ is its <flexural rigidity>. The competition between destabilizing compression and stabilizing bending is responsible for <Euler buckling of an elastic filament>.

A constant shift $\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon$ leaves every derivative unchanged, and the density has no undifferentiated $\phi$. Thus it is exactly invariant under this <scalar-field shift symmetry>. Localize the shift parameter temporarily to $\epsilon(x,t)$:
$$
\delta\mathcal L=\rho\phi_t\epsilon_t+\alpha\phi_x\epsilon_x-\beta\phi_{xx}\epsilon_{xx}.
$$
Integrating the last term by parts once identifies the <Noether current for a constant shift with second derivatives>:
$$
\boxed{j^0=\rho\phi_t,\qquad j^x=\alpha\phi_x+\beta\phi_{xxx}.}
$$
The <Euler-Lagrange field equation> is $\rho\phi_{tt}+\alpha\phi_{xx}+\beta\phi_{xxxx}=0$, precisely the <continuity equation> $\partial_tj^0+\partial_xj^x=0$. Therefore the conserved quantity, when the endpoint current vanishes or the field has suitable periodic/decaying conditions, is
$$
\boxed{Q=\int\rho\phi_t\,dx,}
$$
the total transverse <momentum>. More explicitly $dQ/dt=-[\alpha\phi_x+\beta\phi_{xxx}]_{\rm ends}$. The fixed endpoints introduced later can exert transverse forces and break the global shift symmetry of the boundary problem, so vanishing endpoint current must not be inferred merely from fixed displacement.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For this density the <Euler-Lagrange field equation with second derivatives> gives
$$
\boxed{\rho\phi_{tt}+\alpha\phi_{xx}+\beta\phi_{xxxx}=0.}
$$
Insert a <plane wave> $\phi=Ae^{i(kx-\omega t)}$. The second and fourth spatial <derivatives> give $-k^2$ and $k^4$, respectively, so
$$
-\rho\omega^2-\alpha k^2+\beta k^4=0,\qquad\boxed{\omega^2=\frac{\beta k^4-\alpha k^2}{\rho}.}
$$
For propagating modes, $k^2>\alpha/\beta$ and the <phase velocity> has magnitude $|\omega/k|=\sqrt{(\beta k^2-\alpha)/\rho}$, which depends on the <wave number>. Thus the waves are dispersive. For $0<k^2<\alpha/\beta$, the squared frequency is negative: the corresponding solutions grow or decay exponentially rather than propagate with a real frequency. The uniform $k=0$ displacement is a zero-frequency shift mode.

= Solution
{parent=e}

The <dispersion relation> shows that an infinite rod has unstable long-wave modes. A finite rod excludes sufficiently long wavelengths, so stability depends on its length and the precise end conditions. The fourth spatial <derivative> requires two conditions at each end; fixing transverse positions alone does not specify whether slopes are constrained.

First take displacement-fixed ends with free rotation. Setting $\phi(0)=\phi(l)=0$ leaves slope variations free; the bending boundary term in the <first variation> then gives the corresponding <natural boundary conditions on the slope>, $\phi_{xx}(0)=\phi_{xx}(l)=0$. The spatial <normal modes> are $\sin(n\pi x/l)$, and
$$
\boxed{\omega_n^2=\frac\beta\rho\left(\frac{n\pi}{l}\right)^4-\frac\alpha\rho\left(\frac{n\pi}{l}\right)^2.}
$$
The first mode destabilizes first. Consequently
$$
\boxed{l<\pi\sqrt{\frac\beta\alpha}:\ \text{stable oscillatory modes};\qquad l>\pi\sqrt{\frac\beta\alpha}:\ \text{unstable straight rod}.}
$$
At equality, the first mode has zero restoring force and is marginal, with a static component and a possible linear-in-time component in the linear theory. Every integer $n<l\sqrt{\alpha/\beta}/\pi$ gives an exponentially growing mode.

If "fixed" instead means clamped, the <clamped boundary condition> is $\phi=\phi_x=0$ at both ends. The critical compression is then
$$
\boxed{\alpha_c=\frac{4\pi^2\beta}{l^2},\qquad l_c=2\pi\sqrt{\frac\beta\alpha}.}
$$
For example, the static critical shape $1-\cos(2\pi x/l)$ obeys the four clamp conditions and the zero-frequency equation at this load. To establish that it is the first threshold, write $v=\phi_x$: it has zero endpoints and zero mean, and periodic extension gives $\int_0^lv_x^2dx\geq(2\pi/l)^2\int_0^lv^2dx$ by the zero-mean periodic <Poincare inequality>, with equality at $v\propto\sin(2\pi x/l)$. This proves the critical <Rayleigh quotient>. Both cases are instances of the <compressed Euler-Bernoulli beam and its endpoint-dependent buckling threshold>. The source does not specify the endpoint rotation, so both interpretations are stated explicitly.

The <mean-field approximation> replaces the fluctuating environment of each microscopic variable by a uniform average field. For spins, write $S_i=S+\delta S_i$ and drop products $\delta S_i\delta S_j$ between different sites. The resulting single-site problem can be solved independently, and its average spin must reproduce the assumed <order parameter> $S$: this is the <self-consistency equation>.

It is reliable when correlated <thermal fluctuations> about the mean are small, for example with many interacting neighbours, long-range interactions or high spatial dimension. Near a <thermodynamic critical point> the quantitative test is the <Ginzburg criterion>. For short-range scalar ordering the <upper critical dimension> is four, with logarithmic qualifications at four; a two-dimensional nearest-neighbour lattice is below this and its asymptotic <critical region of a phase transition> is not described accurately by simple <mean-field theory>.<Statistical Field Theory by David Tong>[§2.2.4, The Upper Critical Dimension: correlation-volume fluctuations and the Ginzburg criterion]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <square lattice> has <coordination number of a lattice> $z=4$. The mean-field decoupling is
$$
S_iS_j\simeq S(S_i+S_j)-S^2.
$$
Accounting for the factor $1/2$ in the directed-neighbour sum gives the <Hamiltonian> in the <mean-field approximation>:
$$
H_{\mathrm{MF}}=2JNS^2-(4JS+B)\sum_iS_i.
$$
The constant term is necessary to correct the double counting of the interaction. Let $h=4JS+B$. The one-site <partition function> is
$$
Z_1=\sum_{s=-1,0,1}e^{\beta hs}=1+2\cosh(\beta h),
$$
so $Z_{\rm MF}=e^{-2\beta JNS^2}Z_1^N$. The <Helmholtz free energy> is therefore
$$
\boxed{F_{\mathrm{MF}}(S,T,B)=N\left[2JS^2-\frac1\beta\log\{1+2\cosh[\beta(4JS+B)]\}\right].}
$$
The equilibrium <mean-field approximation> is obtained by minimizing this expression over the allowed $S$. The $s=0$ state supplies the extra $1$ in $Z_1$; omitting it would instead describe the two-state <Ising model>. This is the <Blume-Capel mean-field transition at zero single-ion anisotropy>.

= Solution

The <self-consistency equation> requires that the mean spin computed using the single-site <Boltzmann distribution> equals the spin $S$ used to form the effective field. With $h=4JS+B$,
$$
\langle s\rangle_h=\frac{\sum_{s=-1,0,1}s e^{\beta hs}}{\sum_{s=-1,0,1}e^{\beta hs}}=\frac{e^{\beta h}-e^{-\beta h}}{1+e^{\beta h}+e^{-\beta h}}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{S=\frac{2\sinh[\beta(4JS+B)]}{1+2\cosh[\beta(4JS+B)]}.}
$$
Equivalently, differentiating part (b) gives $N^{-1}\partial F_{\rm MF}/\partial S=4J[S-\langle s\rangle_h]$, so stationarity of the <Helmholtz free energy> in the <mean-field approximation> is exactly this condition. Among multiple <stationary points> one must select the stable <minimum>.

= Solution

At zero field, write the right-hand side of the <self-consistency equation> as $G_T(S)=g(4\beta JS)$, with $g(x)=2\sinh x/[1+2\cosh x]$. The origin is always a solution, and the slope there is
$$
G_T'(0)=4\beta J\,g'(0)=\frac{8\beta J}{3}.
$$
The critical crossing occurs when this equals one, giving
$$
\boxed{k_BT_c=\frac{8J}{3},\qquad T_c=\frac{8J}{3k_B}.}
$$
This is a <continuous phase transition> in the <mean-field approximation>. For $x>0$,
$$
g''(x)=-\frac{2(7+2\cosh x)\sinh x}{(1+2\cosh x)^3}<0,
$$
so the positive branch is a <strictly concave> function, while $g$ is odd and bounded by one. For $T\geq T_c$, the slope at zero is at most one and <strict concavity> puts $G_T(S)$ below $S$ for every $S>0$, leaving only the unmagnetized solution. For $T<T_c$, it initially lies above $S$ but has $G_T(1)<1$; hence there is one positive intersection and, by <spin inversion symmetry>, one negative intersection.

The curvature of the <Helmholtz free energy> in the <mean-field approximation> is $4J[1-G_T'(S)]$ per site. It is negative at $S=0$ below $T_c$ and positive at the nonzero intersections, identifying the ordered minima and the unstable zero solution.

The small-argument <Taylor series> of the single-site response is
$$
\frac{2\sinh x}{1+2\cosh x}=\frac23x-\frac19x^3+O(x^5).

Let $K=4\beta J$. For a small nonzero <order parameter> at zero field, the <self-consistency equation> becomes

Since $K_c=3/2$ and $K=K_c/(1+t)$, where $t=T/T_c-1$, this gives

Consequently

\boxed{S_\pm=\pm\sqrt{\frac83}\,(-t)^{1/2}+O((-t)^{3/2}),\qquad t\to0^-.}
$$
The positive branch is the expression printed in the question, while its negative partner is required by <spin inversion symmetry>. The square-root behavior gives the <mean-field critical exponent> $1/2$ for the <spontaneous magnetization>.

The coarse-grained <order parameter> is a <real scalar field> because the microscopic spin has one component. At $B=0$, <spin inversion symmetry> takes $m\mapsto-m$, so an analytic local <Landau free energy> contains only even powers. Near a <continuous phase transition>, the quadratic coefficient changes sign linearly, represented by $a(T-T_c)$ with $a>0$. A positive quartic coefficient stabilizes the density when the quadratic term becomes negative, giving the two <minima> $m=\pm\sqrt{a(T_c-T)/b}$ below $T_c$.

The ferromagnetic interaction favours neighbouring spins pointing alike, so spatial variation costs energy; its leading long-wavelength form is the positive <gradient> term $c(\partial_xm)^2$. The source term $-Bm$ favours alignment with the applied <magnetic field> and explicitly breaks <spin inversion symmetry> at fixed nonzero $B$. Finally $f_0(T)$ is the spin-independent background <free-energy density>.

The quartic sign can also be checked from part (b): its expansion contains
$$
\frac{F_{\rm MF}}N=-\frac{\log3}{\beta}+\left(2J-\frac{16\beta J^2}{3}\right)S^2+\frac{64\beta^3J^4}{9}S^4+O(S^6),
$$
whose quartic coefficient is positive. This is consistent with the proposed <Landau-Ginzburg theory>, not a tricritical truncation requiring a stabilizing sixth-order term. The microscopic <square lattice> is two-dimensional; the written gradient describes a profile depending on one coordinate. For general profiles, replace it by $c[(\partial_xm)^2+(\partial_ym)^2]$.

For a homogeneous <saddle-point approximation> define $r=a(T-T_c)>0$. With the cubic source coupling, minimizing the <Landau free energy> gives
$$
2rm+2bm^3-B^3=0.
$$
Its curvature $2r+6bm^2$ is positive, so there is a unique <local minimum> and <global minimum>. The solution near $B=0$ has
$$
m(B)=\frac{B^3}{2r}+O(B^9),
$$
because substituting this leading term makes the cubic self-interaction of order $B^9$. Therefore the <cubic source coupling and third-order susceptibility> give
$$
\boxed{\chi^{(1)}=\chi^{(2)}=0,\qquad\chi^{(3)}=\frac{3!}{2r}=\frac3{a(T-T_c)}.}
$$
Thus the first nonzero response diverges like $(T-T_c)^{-1}$ from above. The factor $3!$ is essential: the coefficient of $B^3$ is not itself the third <derivative>. This is susceptibility to the specified cubic source variable, rather than the usual third-order response to a linearly coupled field.

Apply the linear transformation of the <scalar fields> to either time- or space-derivative product. Its coefficients become $\alpha\gamma$ multiplying the square of the first derivative, $\beta\delta$ multiplying the square of the second, and $\alpha\delta+\beta\gamma$ multiplying their cross product. Invariance for arbitrary fields therefore requires
$$
\alpha\gamma=0,\qquad\beta\delta=0,\qquad\alpha\delta+\beta\gamma=1.
$$
The complete real solution is
$$
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}r&0\\0&r^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{or}\quad\begin{pmatrix}0&r\\r^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad r\in\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}.}
$$
There are four <connected components>, distinguished by the diagonal/antidiagonal choice and the sign of $r$. Only the positive diagonal family is in the <identity component>; write $r=e^s$ to obtain $\phi_1\mapsto e^s\phi_1$, $\phi_2\mapsto e^{-s}\phi_2$. This is the <reciprocal rescaling symmetry of two cross-coupled scalar fields>. The antidiagonal transformations exchange the fields as well as rescale them.

The infinitesimal connected <internal symmetry of a classical field theory> is $\delta\phi_1=\epsilon\phi_1$, $\delta\phi_2=-\epsilon\phi_2$. For this exact invariance of the <Lagrangian density>, the <Noether current> is $J^\mu=\sum_j\mathcal L_{\partial_\mu\phi_j}\delta\phi_j/\epsilon$. In coordinates $(t,x)$ it is
$$
\boxed{J^0=\phi_1\phi_{2,t}-\phi_2\phi_{1,t},\qquad J^x=c^2(\phi_2\phi_{1,x}-\phi_1\phi_{2,x}).}
$$
Varying the two fields gives the two <wave equations> $\phi_{1,tt}-c^2\phi_{1,xx}=0$ and $\phi_{2,tt}-c^2\phi_{2,xx}=0$. Direct differentiation cancels the first-derivative cross terms:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_tJ^0+\partial_xJ^x
&=\phi_1(\phi_{2,tt}-c^2\phi_{2,xx})-\phi_2(\phi_{1,tt}-c^2\phi_{1,xx})\\
&=\boxed{0}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The <Noether charge> $Q=\int J^0dx$ is constant when the spatial boundary flux vanishes. The disconnected transformations from part (a) do not have independent infinitesimal generators of this type.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Use coordinates $x^0=t$, $x^1=x$ and first compute the mixed <canonical stress-energy tensor> supplied by spacetime translations:
$$
T^\mu{}_{\nu}=\sum_{j=1}^2\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_j)}\partial_\nu\phi_j-\delta^\mu{}_{\nu}\mathcal L.
$$
Define the energy density $\mathcal E=\phi_{1,t}\phi_{2,t}+c^2\phi_{1,x}\phi_{2,x}$ and $\mathcal P=\phi_{1,t}\phi_{2,x}+\phi_{2,t}\phi_{1,x}$. Direct substitution gives
$$
\boxed{T^\mu{}_{\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal E&\mathcal P\\-c^2\mathcal P&-\mathcal E\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The two wave equations imply
$$
\partial_t\mathcal E=c^2\partial_x\mathcal P,\qquad\partial_t\mathcal P=\partial_x\mathcal E.
$$
For example, differentiating $\mathcal E$ and replacing each $\phi_{j,tt}$ by $c^2\phi_{j,xx}$ gives exactly $c^2\partial_x\mathcal P$ by the <product rule>. These are respectively the two component equations $\boxed{\partial_\mu T^\mu{}_{\nu}=0}$, establishing <stress-energy conservation> explicitly.

If the given metric $g^{\mu\nu}=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$ is used to raise the second index in these unrescaled coordinates, the result is $T^{\mu\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal E&-\mathcal P\\-c^2\mathcal P&\mathcal E\end{pmatrix}$, still conserved. To display the symmetric relativistic form with light speed $c$, choose $X^0=ct$, $X^1=x$ instead. With $\partial_{X^0}=c^{-1}\partial_t$, the corresponding tensor is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal T^{\mu\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal E&-c\mathcal P\\-c\mathcal P&\mathcal E\end{pmatrix},\qquad\partial_{X^\mu}\mathcal T^{\mu\nu}=0.}
$$
This states the coordinate convention explicitly rather than setting $c=1$ in some components but not others.

= Solution
{parent=d}

The velocity coupling changes the momenta and the undifferentiated field terms. Variation with respect to $\phi_2$ gives the equation for $\phi_1$:
$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi_2}=-\frac{\phi_{1,t}}{2\tau},\qquad\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi_{2,t}}=\phi_{1,t}+\frac{\phi_1}{2\tau},
$$
so the two <Euler-Lagrange field equations> are
$$
\boxed{\phi_{1,tt}-c^2\phi_{1,xx}+\frac1\tau\phi_{1,t}=0,\qquad\phi_{2,tt}-c^2\phi_{2,xx}-\frac1\tau\phi_{2,t}=0.}
$$
They describe <damped and antidamped fields from an antisymmetric velocity coupling>. With the convention $\phi_j=A_je^{i(k_jx-\omega_jt)}$, their characteristic equations are
$$
\omega_1^2+\frac i\tau\omega_1-c^2k_1^2=0,\qquad\omega_2^2-\frac i\tau\omega_2-c^2k_2^2=0.
$$
Thus the <dispersion relations> and modes are
$$
\boxed{\omega_{1,\pm}=-\frac i{2\tau}\pm\sqrt{c^2k_1^2-\frac1{4\tau^2}},\qquad\phi_{1,\pm}=A_{1,\pm}e^{i(k_1x-\omega_{1,\pm}t)},}
$$
$$
\boxed{\omega_{2,\pm}=+\frac i{2\tau}\pm\sqrt{c^2k_2^2-\frac1{4\tau^2}},\qquad\phi_{2,\pm}=A_{2,\pm}e^{i(k_2x-\omega_{2,\pm}t)}.}
$$
For each spatial <Fourier mode>, the general solution is the <linear combination> of its two temporal branches; spatial superposition gives the general field. Take <real parts> or suitable <complex conjugate> pairs to obtain the required real fields. At the repeated-root threshold, the independent second temporal solution contains an extra factor $t$, as detailed next.

Above the threshold, $c^2k_j^2>1/(4\tau^2)$, the square root in part (d) is real. Both fields oscillate, but the first has envelope $\boxed{e^{-t/(2\tau)}}$ and the second $\boxed{e^{+t/(2\tau)}}$. Thus the first is damped and the second amplified.

Below the threshold, set $\sigma_j=\sqrt{1/(4\tau^2)-c^2k_j^2}$. The temporal factors are
$$
\boxed{\phi_1:\ e^{[-1/(2\tau)\pm\sigma_1]t},\qquad\phi_2:\ e^{[+1/(2\tau)\pm\sigma_2]t}.}
$$
They are nonoscillatory. For nonzero $k_j$, $0<\sigma_j<1/(2\tau)$, so both first-field branches decay and both second-field branches grow. At $k_j=0$, one branch is constant; the other is $e^{-t/\tau}$ for $\phi_1$ or $e^{t/\tau}$ for $\phi_2$.

At equality, the repeated temporal root gives $\boxed{(A+Bt)e^{-t/(2\tau)}}$ for the first field and $\boxed{(C+Dt)e^{t/(2\tau)}}$ for the second. The full two-field system is generically unstable on both sides because of the amplified sector. This is compatible with its autonomous Lagrangian: the total conserved <energy> is an <indefinite quadratic form>, rather than a positive energy forcing every component to remain bounded.
