= Solution
{parent=a}

Use the figure's upward-positive vertical coordinate. The support is at $(x,0)$ and the bob at $(x+l\sin\phi,-l\cos\phi)$. Thus
$$
v_2^2=(\dot x+l\dot\phi\cos\phi)^2+(l\dot\phi\sin\phi)^2
=\dot x^2+2l\dot x\dot\phi\cos\phi+l^2\dot\phi^2.
$$
The potential is $V=-m_2gl\cos\phi$, up to an irrelevant constant. Combining both <kinetic energies> gives
$$
\boxed{L=\frac12(m_1+m_2)\dot x^2+
\frac12m_2l^2\dot\phi^2+m_2l\dot x\dot\phi\cos\phi+m_2gl\cos\phi.}
$$
The cross term expresses the moving support's contribution to the bob <velocity>; it would be lost by treating the pivot as fixed.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Differentiate with respect to the generalized <velocities>:
$$
\boxed{p_x=(m_1+m_2)\dot x+m_2l\dot\phi\cos\phi,\qquad
p_\phi=m_2l^2\dot\phi+m_2l\dot x\cos\phi.}
$$
The <Lagrangian> has no explicit $x$, so $\dot p_x=\partial L/\partial x=0$. This <canonical momentum> is also the system's total horizontal mechanical <momentum>. **Horizontal <translation symmetry> conserves $p_x$** even though the support and bob separately accelerate.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Write $M=m_1+m_2$. Integrating the zero-momentum condition gives $Mx+m_2l\sin\phi=MX_c$, where $X_c$ is the constant horizontal centre-of-mass position. Therefore the bob coordinates are
$$
X=x+l\sin\phi=X_c+\frac{m_1l}{M}\sin\phi,\qquad Y=-l\cos\phi.
$$
Eliminating the angle gives the <elliptic bob path at zero horizontal momentum>,
$$
\boxed{\frac{(X-X_c)^2}{(m_1l/M)^2}+\frac{Y^2}{l^2}=1.}
$$
The semiaxes are $m_1l/M$ horizontally and $l$ vertically. An <oscillation> traverses only the allowed arc, reversing at its <turning points>. The support counter-moves to keep the horizontal <centre of mass> fixed. The heavy-support limit tends to the familiar fixed-pivot circle.

= Solution
{parent=d}

The time-independent <Lagrangian> conserves $E=T+V$. Substitute $\dot x=-m_2l\dot\phi\cos\phi/M$ into the <kinetic energy> to obtain
$$
\boxed{E=\frac{m_2l^2}{2M}(m_1+m_2\sin^2\phi)\dot\phi^2-m_2gl\cos\phi.}
$$
This is the angle-dependent effective inertia of a <pendulum with a freely sliding support>. Solving for the speed gives
$$
|\dot\phi|=\sqrt{\frac{2M(E+m_2gl\cos\phi)}{m_2l^2(m_1+m_2\sin^2\phi)}}.
$$
On a monotone increasing segment, the travel time is therefore
$$
\boxed{t=l\sqrt{\frac{m_2}{2M}}\int_{\phi_1}^{\phi_2}
\sqrt{\frac{m_1+m_2\sin^2\phi}{E+m_2gl\cos\phi}}\,d\phi.}
$$
The printed oriented <integral> assumes that increasing-angle branch. For decreasing motion use $|d\phi|$ or interchange the limits; if a <turning point> lies between the two events, split the trajectory into monotone segments and add their positive times. The numerator remains positive for positive masses. At an ordinary <turning point> the inverse-square-root singularity is integrable; reaching the unstable upright <separatrix> takes infinite time.

= Solution

A one-degree-of-freedom <canonical transformation> requires the <Poisson bracket> $\{x,p\}_{X,P}=1$. To first order,
$$
\{x,p\}_{X,P}
=(1+2\alpha_1X+2\alpha_2P)(1+2\beta_2X+2\beta_3P)+O(\alpha\beta)
=1+2(\alpha_1+\beta_2)X+2(\alpha_2+\beta_3)P+O(2).
$$
The product of the off-diagonal <derivatives> contributes only at second order. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\beta_2=-\alpha_1,\qquad\beta_3=-\alpha_2,}
$$
with $\alpha_3$ and $\beta_1$ unconstrained at this order. The self-brackets vanish identically. This is perturbative preservation of the <symplectic form>, not exact canonicity of the truncated polynomial map.

{parent=2}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Treat all transformation coefficients as $O(\lambda)$, so products of them with $\lambda$ are second order. Substitution into the <Hamiltonian> gives its harmonic part plus
$$
(m\omega^2\alpha_1+\lambda)X^3+
(2m\omega^2\alpha_2+\beta_1/m)X^2P+
(m\omega^2\alpha_3+2\beta_2/m)XP^2+
(\beta_3/m)P^3.
$$
Set these four coefficients to zero and impose the canonical constraints. The result is
$$
\boxed{\alpha_1=-\frac\lambda{m\omega^2},\quad
\alpha_2=0,\quad\alpha_3=-\frac{2\lambda}{m^3\omega^4},\quad
\beta_1=0,\quad\beta_2=\frac\lambda{m\omega^2},\quad\beta_3=0.}
$$
Thus the <first-order canonical removal of a cubic oscillator perturbation> is
$$
\boxed{x=X-\frac\lambda{m\omega^2}X^2-\frac{2\lambda}{m^3\omega^4}P^2,
\qquad p=P+\frac{2\lambda}{m\omega^2}XP,}
$$
up to $O(\lambda^2)$. It yields $K=P^2/(2m)+m\omega^2X^2/2+O(\lambda^2)$. The inverse map, if old <initial data> are specified, is $X=x+\lambda x^2/(m\omega^2)+2\lambda p^2/(m^3\omega^4)$ and $P=p-2\lambda xp/(m\omega^2)$ to the same order. There is no time-dependent generating term because the map is autonomous.

= Solution
{parent=c}

<Hamilton's equations> give $\dot X=P/m+O(\lambda^2)$ and $\dot P=-m\omega^2X+O(\lambda^2)$. Hence, with constants $A\geq0$ and $\delta$ fixed by transformed <initial data>,
$$
\boxed{X(t)=A\cos\vartheta+O(\lambda^2),\qquad
P(t)=-m\omega A\sin\vartheta+O(\lambda^2),\qquad
\vartheta=\omega t+\delta.}
$$
An equivalent form uses $X(0)\cos\omega t+[P(0)/(m\omega)]\sin\omega t$ for $X(t)$. The indicated errors are a fixed-time perturbative statement; a higher-order <frequency> correction can accumulate <phase> on longer times.

= d
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Insert the harmonic variables into the forward map and reduce the squared trigonometric functions. This gives
$$
\boxed{x(t)=A\cos\vartheta-
\frac{3\lambda A^2}{2m\omega^2}+
\frac{\lambda A^2}{2m\omega^2}\cos2\vartheta+O(\lambda^2),}
$$
$$
\boxed{p(t)=-m\omega A\sin\vartheta-
\frac{\lambda A^2}{\omega}\sin2\vartheta+O(\lambda^2).}
$$
Indeed $p=m\dot x$ at this order. The <second harmonic and mean displacement of a weakly cubic oscillator> show asymmetry of the motion: for positive $\lambda$ the mean shifts towards negative $x$. There is **no first-order <frequency> shift**, although the waveform is no longer purely sinusoidal.

The small parameter is $|\lambda|A/(m\omega^2)\ll1$. A purely cubic perturbation is unbounded in one direction, so these formulas describe small local <oscillations> around the stable origin, not globally confined motion at arbitrary <energy>. Amplitude $A$ labels the transformed orbit and must be inferred using the inverse map if physical initial position and <momentum> are supplied.

= Solution
{parent=3}

In Cartesian components, $\partial L/\partial v_i=mv_i+eA_i$ and $\partial L/\partial x_i=ev_j\partial_iA_j-e\partial_i\phi$. The <Euler-Lagrange equation> therefore gives
$$
m\dot v_i=e[-\partial_i\phi-\partial_tA_i+v_j(\partial_iA_j-\partial_jA_i)]
=e[E_i+(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)_i].
$$
Thus **the minimally coupled particle <Lagrangian> yields the <Lorentz force>**.

For an arbitrary smooth gauge function $\chi$, take $\mathbf A\to\mathbf A+\nabla\chi$ and $\phi\to\phi-\partial_t\chi$. Both fields are unchanged, while
$$
L\to L+e\left(\partial_t\chi+\mathbf v\cdot\nabla\chi\right)
=L+e\frac{d\chi}{dt}.
$$
The <action> changes only by an endpoint term, so fixed-endpoint variation gives the same motion. <Canonical momentum> shifts by $e\nabla\chi$, whereas <kinetic momentum> $m\mathbf v$ and the <Lorentz force> are gauge invariant. This is the relevant <gauge invariance> of the classical particle dynamics.

= Solution

The cylindrical entries of $\mathbf A$ are physical <vector> components, so $\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf A=\mu r^2\dot\theta$, not $\mu r\dot\theta$. The <Lagrangian> is
$$
L=\frac m2(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2+\dot z^2)+e\mu r^2\dot\theta-e\lambda z^2.
$$
Its three equations are
$$
\boxed{m\ddot r=mr\dot\theta^2+2e\mu r\dot\theta,\qquad
\frac d{dt}(mr^2\dot\theta+e\mu r^2)=0,\qquad
m\ddot z=-2e\lambda z.}
$$
For $r>0$, the angular equation can be expanded as $r\ddot\theta+2\dot r\dot\theta+(2e\mu/m)\dot r=0$. Directly computing the fields gives $\mathbf B=2\mu\hat{\mathbf z}$ and $\mathbf E=-2\lambda z\hat{\mathbf z}$, agreeing with the equations above.

= Solution

The <energy> function $E=\sum_i\dot q_i\partial L/\partial\dot q_i-L$ is
$$
\boxed{E=\frac m2(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2+\dot z^2)+e\lambda z^2.}
$$
The velocity-linear magnetic term cancels. Since $L$ has no explicit time dependence, $dE/dt=-\partial_tL=0$. Equivalently, magnetic <force> does no <work>, and electric <work> is exactly minus the change of $e\lambda z^2$. <Energy> here is defined in the supplied time-independent gauge and equals physical kinetic plus <electrostatic potential> <energy>.

The coordinate $\theta$ is cyclic. Its conjugate <momentum> is therefore a second <conserved quantity>,
$$
\boxed{p_\theta=mr^2\dot\theta+e\mu r^2=\text{constant}.}
$$
It differs from mechanical <angular momentum> $mr^2\dot\theta$. The magnetic contribution is essential to the rotational <Noether charge> in this axisymmetric gauge.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For constant $r=R>0$, the radial equation requires $\dot\theta(\dot\theta+2e\mu/m)=0$. Select the stipulated nonzero <angular velocity> and define the positive <cyclotron frequency> $\Omega_c=2e\mu/m$. Then
$$
\boxed{\theta(t)=\theta_0-\Omega_ct,\qquad
z(t)=Z\cos(\Omega_zt+\delta),\qquad
\Omega_z=\sqrt{2e\lambda/m}.}
$$
This is a circle in the transverse plane combined with an independent axial <oscillation>, as in <uniform magnetic field with axial harmonic confinement>. Viewed from positive $z$, positive charge rotates clockwise. Its three-dimensional path lies on the cylinder $r=R$ and reverses its vertical motion; **it is generally not a constant-pitch helix**. For $Z=0$ it is a horizontal circle. This orbit has $p_\theta=-e\mu R^2$.

= Solution
{parent=e}

For the specified parameter relation,
$$
\boxed{\Omega_z^2=\frac{2e\lambda}{m}
=\left(\frac{2e\mu}{m}\right)^2n^2,
\qquad\Omega_z=|n|\Omega_c.}
$$
Thus there are $|n|$ axial <oscillations> per transverse revolution, and the nondegenerate three-dimensional trajectory closes after $2\pi/\Omega_c$. Since $\lambda>0$, the integer $n=0$ is excluded; changing its sign changes nothing. More generally <commensurate cyclotron and axial oscillations> close for any rational <frequency> ratio, whereas irrational ratios give <quasiperiodic motion> when both components are present. An integer ratio is sufficient, not the only closure condition. The same <frequency> argument also applies to transverse cyclotron circles centred away from the chosen cylindrical origin.

= Solution
{parent=4}

Use <metric signature> $(+,-,-,-)$ and units with propagation speed one. The scalar <Euler-Lagrange equation> gives
$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal L_{\rm KG}}{\partial\phi}=-m^2\phi,\qquad
\frac{\partial\mathcal L_{\rm KG}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}=\partial^\mu\phi,
\qquad
\boxed{(\partial_t^2-\nabla^2+m^2)\phi=0.}
$$
With the stated spatial Fourier convention, $-\nabla^2$ becomes $k^2$. Each transformed mode obeys
$$
\ddot{\tilde\phi}+\omega_k^2\tilde\phi=0,\qquad
\omega_k=\sqrt{k^2+m^2},\qquad
\boxed{\tilde\phi(\mathbf k,t)=F(\mathbf k)\cos\omega_kt+
\frac{V(\mathbf k)}{\omega_k}\sin\omega_kt.}
$$
Here $F$ and $V$ are the initial transformed field and <velocity>. Reality requires $F(-\mathbf k)=F(\mathbf k)^*$ and the same for $V$; the Fourier convention places no extra $(2\pi)^3$ in these evolution formulas.

Including the interaction gives the <two coupled real Klein-Gordon fields>,
$$
\boxed{(\Box+m^2)\phi_1-g\phi_2=0,\qquad
(\Box+m^2)\phi_2-g\phi_1=0.}
$$
Adding and subtracting diagonalizes them:
$$
(\Box+m^2-g)\phi_+=0,\qquad
(\Box+m^2+g)\phi_-=0,\qquad
\omega_\pm(k)=\sqrt{k^2+m^2\mp g}.
$$
Both squared masses are positive for $0<g<m^2$. For arbitrary <initial data> $F_i=\tilde\phi_i(0)$ and $V_i=\dot{\tilde\phi}_i(0)$, define
$$
U_\pm(\mathbf k,t)=(F_1\pm F_2)\cos\omega_\pm t+
\frac{V_1\pm V_2}{\omega_\pm}\sin\omega_\pm t.
$$
Then the general solution is $\tilde\phi_1=(U_++U_-)/2$, $\tilde\phi_2=(U_+-U_-)/2$, with the corresponding reality conditions on <initial data>.

For the specified sinusoidal initial <displacement>, put $\Omega_\pm=\sqrt{q^2+m^2\mp g}$. Both normal fields initially have amplitude $A\sin(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x)$ and zero time <derivative>. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\phi_1=\frac A2\sin(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x)
[\cos\Omega_+t+\cos\Omega_-t],\qquad
\phi_2=\frac A2\sin(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x)
[\cos\Omega_+t-\cos\Omega_-t].}
$$
Writing $\bar\Omega=(\Omega_-+\Omega_+)/2$ and $\delta\Omega=(\Omega_--\Omega_+)/2$ gives $\phi_1=A\sin(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x)\cos\bar\Omega t\cos\delta\Omega t$ and $\phi_2=A\sin(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x)\sin\bar\Omega t\sin\delta\Omega t$. This is <field-amplitude beating of coupled Klein-Gordon modes>. The conserved <energy> includes the interaction term $-g\phi_1\phi_2$; separate uncoupled-field <energies> need not be conserved, and an envelope zero is not by itself exact complete <energy> transfer.

= Solution
{parent=5}

<Noether's theorem> associates a continuous one-parameter differentiable symmetry of the <action> with an on-shell conserved current. If a first-derivative <Lagrangian density> varies by $\delta\mathcal L=\partial_\mu B^\mu$, the current is $J^\mu=\mathcal L_{\partial_\mu\phi}\delta\phi-B^\mu$. The <integral> of $J^0$ is conserved when the spatial boundary flux vanishes. This connects symmetries to <conservation laws>; time translation, spatial translation and internal <phase> symmetry furnish familiar examples.

For the strict fixed-coordinate invariance in the question, $B^\mu=0$. In one space dimension, <integration by parts> at the <density> level gives
$$
\delta\mathcal L=
\left[\mathcal L_\phi-\partial_t\mathcal L_{\dot\phi}-\partial_x\mathcal L_{\phi'}\right]\delta\phi
+\partial_t(\mathcal L_{\dot\phi}\delta\phi)
+\partial_x(\mathcal L_{\phi'}\delta\phi).
$$
The bracket vanishes by the field equation. Thus $\delta\mathcal L=0$ yields
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\rho+\partial_xJ_x=0,\qquad
\rho=\mathcal L_{\dot\phi}\delta\phi,\qquad
J_x=\mathcal L_{\phi'}\delta\phi.}
$$
In four <spacetime> dimensions the same identity gives $\partial_\mu J^\mu=0$ with $J^\mu=\mathcal L_{\partial_\mu\phi}\delta\phi$. In particular, for the usual scalar kinetic term the spatial current has the minus sign inherited from $\partial^i=-\partial_i$.

For the quartic <density> in $n$ dimensions,
$$
\mathcal L_\phi=-4\lambda\phi^3,\qquad
\mathcal L_{\partial_\mu\phi}=\partial^\mu\phi,
\qquad\boxed{\Box\phi+4\lambda\phi^3=0.}
$$
To evaluate the displayed dilation current, abbreviate $S=(\partial_\mu\phi)(\partial^\mu\phi)$ and $D=x^\nu\partial_\nu$. Product differentiation gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_\mu J^\mu
&=2S+(D\partial_\mu\phi)\partial^\mu\phi
+(\phi+D\phi)\Box\phi-n\mathcal L-D\mathcal L\\
&=2S-4\lambda\phi^4-n\mathcal L
+(\phi+D\phi)(\Box\phi+4\lambda\phi^3)\\
&=(4-n)\mathcal L+(\phi+D\phi)(\Box\phi+4\lambda\phi^3).
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $D(S/2)=(D\partial_\mu\phi)\partial^\mu\phi$ makes the differentiated kinetic terms cancel, and $D(\lambda\phi^4)=4\lambda\phi^3D\phi$. <On shell>, the <on-shell divergence of a quartic scalar dilation current> reduces to
$$
\boxed{\partial_\mu J^\mu=(4-n)\mathcal L.}
$$
Hence this current is conserved for generic solutions in **four <spacetime> dimensions**. Special field configurations with vanishing <density> do not alter the generic dimensional statement.

The subtraction $-x^\mu\mathcal L$ has a symmetry explanation: for $\delta\phi=\phi+D\phi$, direct variation gives $\delta\mathcal L=D\mathcal L+4\mathcal L=\partial_\mu(x^\mu\mathcal L)+(4-n)\mathcal L$. At $n=4$ the <action> is invariant up to a <boundary term>, so $B^\mu=x^\mu\mathcal L$. Strict invariance of the <density> is not required. This is a classical result; no claim about quantum <scale invariance> is needed.

Use the forward spatial transform $\hat f(k)=\int e^{-ikx}f(x)\,dx$ and inverse $f(x)=\int e^{ikx}\hat f(k)\,dk/(2\pi)$. The retarded transformed <Green function> obeys
$$
\left[\frac1{c^2}\frac{d^2}{d\tau^2}+2\alpha\frac d{d\tau}+k^2\right]G_k(\tau)=\delta(\tau),\qquad \tau=t-t'.
$$
It vanishes for negative $\tau$. Continuity at zero excludes an unwanted $\delta'$ term; integrating across zero then fixes $G_k'(0^+)=c^2$. Define $\gamma=\alpha c^2$ and $d_k=\sqrt{\gamma^2-c^2k^2}$. The two homogeneous exponents are $-\gamma\pm d_k$. Imposing the two matching conditions gives the <Fourier-mode Green function of the telegraph equation>,
$$
\boxed{G_k(\tau)=\Theta(\tau)\frac{c^2}{d_k}e^{-\gamma\tau}\sinh(d_k\tau)
=\Theta(\tau)\frac{e^{-\alpha c^2\tau}}{\sqrt{\alpha^2-k^2/c^2}}
\sinh\!\left(\sqrt{\alpha^2c^4-k^2c^2}\,\tau\right).}
$$
For an explicit time-Fourier route, use $G_k(\tau)=\int e^{-i\omega\tau}\mathcal G_k(\omega)\,d\omega/(2\pi)$. Then
$$
\mathcal G_k(\omega)=\frac1{k^2-2i\alpha\omega-\omega^2/c^2},\qquad
\omega_\pm=-i\gamma\pm\sqrt{c^2k^2-\gamma^2}.
$$
For $k\ne0$, both <poles> lie below the real axis. Closing above for $\tau<0$ gives zero; closing below for $\tau>0$ is clockwise, giving $-i$ times the <residue> sum. Since $\mathcal G_k=-c^2/[(\omega-\omega_+)(\omega-\omega_-)]$, this is
$$
G_k(\tau)=ic^2\frac{e^{-i\omega_+\tau}-e^{-i\omega_-\tau}}{\omega_+-\omega_-}
\quad(\tau>0),
$$
which reduces to the same damped sine or hyperbolic sine above. The $k=0$ <pole> on the real axis is interpreted by the retarded limiting prescription, or directly by the initial-value matching above.

For $|k|<\alpha c$ the response is overdamped and nonoscillatory. For $|k|>\alpha c$, put $\Omega_k=\sqrt{c^2k^2-\gamma^2}$; analytic continuation gives $G_k=\Theta(\tau)c^2e^{-\gamma\tau}\sin(\Omega_k\tau)/\Omega_k$, a real damped <oscillation>. At equality the finite limit is $\Theta(\tau)c^2\tau e^{-\gamma\tau}$.

All nonzero <Fourier modes> decay. The uniform mode instead has $G_0=(1-e^{-2\gamma\tau})/(2\alpha)$ for positive time, leaving a constant <temperature> increment. At small wavenumber the slow decay exponent is $-k^2/(2\alpha)+O(k^4)$, so the <diffusion limit of the damped heat-transmission equation> has <diffusivity> $1/(2\alpha)$. The full hyperbolic equation has <finite propagation speed> $c$, unlike the limiting parabolic <heat equation>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The spatial transform of $\cos(px)$ is $\pi[\delta(k-p)+\delta(k+p)]$. <Convolution> with the impulsive source and the inverse transform therefore give simply $T(x,t)=\cos(px)G_p(t-t_0)$. Take $p\geq0$ without loss of generality and set $\tau=t-t_0$.

For $\alpha>p/c$, let $d_p=\sqrt{\alpha^2c^4-p^2c^2}$. Then
$$
\boxed{T(x,t)=\Theta(\tau)\cos(px)\frac{c^2}{d_p}e^{-\alpha c^2\tau}\sinh(d_p\tau).}
$$
For $\alpha<p/c$, let $\Omega_p=\sqrt{p^2c^2-\alpha^2c^4}$. Then
$$
\boxed{T(x,t)=\Theta(\tau)\cos(px)\frac{c^2}{\Omega_p}e^{-\alpha c^2\tau}\sin(\Omega_p\tau).}
$$
Both satisfy $T(t_0^+)=0$ and $\partial_tT(t_0^+)=c^2\cos(px)$, so the impulse changes the time <derivative> rather than discontinuously changing <temperature>. The first case rises and then relaxes without sign <oscillations> for a nonzero mode; the second oscillates as its amplitude decays. The limiting equality gives $T=\Theta(\tau)c^2\tau e^{-\alpha c^2\tau}\cos(px)$, and $p=0$ gives the nondecaying uniform increment discussed above.

A cosine source has both positive and negative regions, so this linear solution can be understood as a <temperature> perturbation about a background; its negative values are not assertions of negative absolute <temperature>. The temporal <oscillations> arise from the second-time-derivative term. Dropping that term changes the initial-jump structure as well as removing the underdamped regime.
