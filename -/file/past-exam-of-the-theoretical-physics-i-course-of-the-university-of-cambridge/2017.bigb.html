= Solution
{parent=a}

Write $A=\pi a^2$ for the cross-sectional area, and use $z$ consistently for the longitudinal coordinate: the paper's later $x$ is the same coordinate. The <canonical momentum> is a density per reference length,
$$
\boxed{\Pi(z,t)=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\psi}=A\rho\dot\psi}.
$$
The <Legendre transform> gives the <Hamiltonian density> in its proper canonical variables:
$$
\boxed{\mathcal H(\psi,\Pi)=\Pi\dot\psi-\mathcal L=\frac{\Pi^2}{2A\rho}+\frac{AK}{2}(\psi')^2}.
$$
The <Euler-Lagrange equation> is
$$
\partial_t(A\rho\dot\psi)+\partial_z(-AK\psi')=0,
\qquad \boxed{\ddot\psi=\frac K\rho\psi''}.
$$
For a twice-differentiable <wave packet> $\psi=f(z-ct)$, the derivatives are $\ddot\psi=c^2f''$ and $\psi''=f''$. Thus the nontrivial packet solves the <wave equation> when
$$
\boxed{c=\sqrt{K/\rho}}.
$$
The negative sign gives the opposite propagation direction. Here $\Pi$ has units $\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$, $\mathcal H$ has units $\mathrm{J\,m^{-1}}$, and $c$ has units $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The result follows from the supplied axial stiffness in the <Lagrangian>. For a freely contracting thin elastic rod the relevant longitudinal modulus is normally <Young's modulus>, rather than the <bulk modulus>; the question's given <Lagrangian> fixes which coefficient is being used.

Both <Noether charges> require boundary conditions that admit the stated transformations and no net boundary-current flux. Take a sufficiently localized field on the whole rod, with the weighted integrals below finite. Fixed endpoints at finite positions generally spoil the waveform-dilation <symmetry>.

= Uniform field shift
{parent=Solution}

The transformation $\psi\mapsto\psi+b$ leaves both derivatives unchanged, so the <Lagrangian density> and <action> are invariant. For infinitesimal unit field variation, the <Noether current for a first-derivative scalar field> is the <shift current of an elastic rod>,
$$
j_1^0=A\rho\dot\psi=\Pi,\qquad j_1^z=-AK\psi',\qquad
\boxed{Q_1=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Pi\,dz}.
$$
Its divergence vanishes by the <equation of motion>. The charge is the total longitudinal mechanical <momentum> to this order in the displacement field.

= Waveform dilation
{parent=Solution}

For $b>0$, put $\psi_b(z,t)=\psi(bz,bt)$. Each derivative gains a factor $b$, so $\mathcal L_b(z,t)=b^2\mathcal L(bz,bt)$. Changing integration variables to $(Z,T)=(bz,bt)$ contributes $b^{-2}$, proving exact invariance of the full-space <action>.

Set $b=1+\eta$. At fixed coordinates, $\delta\psi=\eta X$ with $X=z\psi'+t\dot\psi$. Since the density is quadratic in derivatives,
$$
\frac{\delta\mathcal L}{\eta}=2\mathcal L+z\partial_z\mathcal L+t\partial_t\mathcal L
=\partial_t(t\mathcal L)+\partial_z(z\mathcal L).
$$
The variation is a divergence, so subtract its boundary term in the <Noether current>:
$$
j_2^0=\Pi X-t\mathcal L=t\mathcal H+z\Pi\psi',\qquad
j_2^z=-AK\psi'X-z\mathcal L=-tAK\dot\psi\psi'-z\mathcal H.
$$
Thus the <dilatation charge of a one-dimensional elastic wave> is
$$
\boxed{Q_2=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(t\mathcal H+z\Pi\psi'\right)dz}.
$$
An overall sign change would give the same <conservation law>. Unlike $Q_1$, this charge has units of <action>, $\mathrm{J\,s}$. Its explicit factor of $t$ is balanced by transport of the weighted <momentum> density; an explicitly time-dependent charge can still be conserved.

= Solution

For a rightgoing <wave packet>, put $\xi=z-ct$ and assume $f'$ decays sufficiently rapidly. Then
$$
\Pi=-A\rho c f'(\xi),\qquad \mathcal H=A\rho c^2[f'(\xi)]^2.
$$
The shift charge becomes
$$
\boxed{Q_1=-A\rho c\int f'(\xi)d\xi=-A\rho c[f(+\infty)-f(-\infty)]}.
$$
It is time independent. It vanishes for a localized displacement packet that returns to the same displacement at both ends, but need not vanish for a localized strain pulse joining different asymptotic displacements.

For the dilation charge, substitute $z=\xi+ct$:
$$
Q_2=A\rho c\int(ct-z)[f'(\xi)]^2dz
=\boxed{-A\rho c\int\xi[f'(\xi)]^2d\xi}.
$$
The change of variables removes all time dependence. This charge also need not vanish; choosing an origin at the packet's <energy> centroid can make it zero. Conservation assumes the integral exists and there is no boundary flux, rather than merely assuming that the <energy> is finite.

**Position-dependent density.** The density $\mathcal L_1$ remains independent of $\psi$ itself, so the shift <Noether charge> survives with its altered <canonical momentum>:
$$
\boxed{Q_{1,1}=A\int\rho(z)\dot\psi\,dz}.
$$
A prescribed generic function $\rho(z)$ supplies a fixed spatial scale and breaks <scale invariance>. For the dilation-current candidate with the corresponding $\mathcal H_1$ and $\Pi_1$, the <scale-breaking sources in derivative field theories> give
$$
\partial_t j_{D,1}^0+\partial_z j_{D,1}^z=-\frac A2z\rho'(z)\dot\psi^2.
$$
Thus **$Q_2$ is generally not conserved**. Constant density recovers the earlier case; specially scale-invariant profiles and suitably invariant domains would require separate treatment.

**Quartic strain.** The extra term $-\alpha(\psi')^4$ in $\mathcal L_2$ again preserves a uniform field shift. Its time <momentum> is unchanged, while its spatial current becomes $-AK\psi'-4\alpha(\psi')^3$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{Q_{1,2}=A\rho\int\dot\psi\,dz=Q_1}.
$$
The new <Hamiltonian density> is $\mathcal H_2=\mathcal H_0+\alpha(\psi')^4$. Under waveform dilation, the quartic term scales with $b^4$ rather than $b^2$, and
$$
\partial_\mu j_{D,2}^\mu=-2\alpha(\psi')^4.
$$
Hence **there is no corresponding conserved $Q_2$ for nonzero generic $\alpha$**, even if the <energy> in its candidate expression is updated.

**Field-dependent quadratic stiffness.** In $\mathcal L_3$, the term $-\beta\psi^2(\psi')^2$ breaks the uniform field shift:
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int\Pi\,dz=-2\beta\int\psi(\psi')^2dz
$$
when spatial flux vanishes. Thus **$Q_1$ is not generally conserved**. However all terms still contain exactly two derivatives in total, with a field of zero dilation weight. The factor $\psi^2$ gains no extra power of $b$, so waveform dilation remains an <action> <symmetry>. Its charge is
$$
\boxed{Q_{2,3}=\int\left[t\left(\mathcal H_0+\beta\psi^2(\psi')^2\right)+z\Pi\psi'\right]dz}.
$$
Here $\Pi=A\rho\dot\psi$ as before. Relative to the original charge, the <energy> term changes; the <symmetry> survives despite the nonlinear field dependence. These statements concern <symmetry> conservation, separately from any restrictions on parameters needed for positive elastic <energy>.

Write $\rho$ for cylindrical radius in this question, not the rod's <mass density>. A charged relativistic particle in a time-independent <magnetic field> has the <charged-particle electromagnetic Lagrangian>
$$
L=-mc^2\sqrt{1-\mathbf v^2/c^2}+q\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf v,
$$
with zero electrostatic potential. The cylindrical speed is $\mathbf v^2=\dot\rho^2+\rho^2\dot\theta^2+\dot z^2$. Inside a uniform axial field, choose the symmetric <magnetic vector potential> $A_\theta=B\rho/2$, $A_\rho=A_z=0$. Its cylindrical curl gives $B_z=\rho^{-1}\partial_\rho(\rho A_\theta)=B$. Hence
$$
\boxed{L=-mc^2\sqrt{1-\frac{\dot z^2+\dot\rho^2+\rho^2\dot\theta^2}{c^2}}+\frac{qB\rho^2\dot\theta}{2}}.
$$
In a field-free region far from either end one can use $A_\theta=0$, recovering the requested outside expression. The question's $V=-qB\rho^2\dot\theta/2$ is a velocity-dependent generalized potential, not electrostatic potential <energy>.

There is an important end-field qualification. A finite axial field that jumps to zero with no radial component would violate <Gauss's law for magnetism>. The consistent near-axis idealisation is the <solenoid fringe field from an axisymmetric vector potential>, $A_\theta=\rho B(z)/2$, giving $B_\rho=-\rho B'(z)/2$ and $B_z=B(z)$. The piecewise <Lagrangian> is its hard-edge limit and retains fringe impulses through the jump in $A_\theta$. Those impulses are essential to the focusing calculation.

The azimuth is a <cyclic coordinate>, so its <canonical momentum> is conserved:
$$
\boxed{p_\theta=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\theta}=\gamma m\rho^2\dot\theta+q\rho A_\theta},\qquad
\gamma=\frac1{\sqrt{1-\mathbf v^2/c^2}}.
$$
Initially the <velocity> is parallel to the axis in a field-free region, so $p_\theta=0$. Conservation of <canonical angular momentum across a solenoid fringe> therefore gives, inside the uniform field,
$$
\boxed{\dot\theta=-\frac{qB}{2\gamma m}\equiv-\Omega}.
$$
This holds away from the coordinate singularity $\rho=0$; the Cartesian orbit will be smooth there.

Time independence also conserves the <energy> <Hamiltonian>,
$$
E=\sum_i\dot q_i\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}-L
=\boxed{\gamma mc^2}.
$$
The velocity-linear magnetic term cancels in this <Legendre transform>. Thus $\gamma=\gamma_v=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$ is constant, as expected because a static magnetic <Lorentz force> does no work. Mechanical angular <momentum> can change even though $p_\theta$ and the total speed are conserved.

= Solution
{parent=c}

In a uniform-field region, $z$ is cyclic and $\gamma$ is constant. Its equation is $d(\gamma m\dot z)/dt=0$. The radial <Euler-Lagrange equation> is
$$
\frac{d}{dt}(\gamma m\dot\rho)=\gamma m\rho\dot\theta^2+qB\rho\dot\theta,
$$
so with $\dot\theta=-\Omega$ and $qB/(\gamma m)=2\Omega$,
$$
\boxed{\ddot z=0,\qquad\ddot\rho+\Omega^2\rho=0\quad\text{inside}}.
$$
The radial equation applies on intervals with a regular signed cylindrical-coordinate description; the Cartesian solution handles axis crossings.

In a field-free region, the equations are
$$
\boxed{\ddot z=0,\qquad\ddot\rho=\rho\dot\theta^2,\qquad
\frac{d}{dt}(\gamma m\rho^2\dot\theta)=0}.
$$
For this particle $p_\theta=0$, so $\dot\theta=0$ and $\ddot\rho=0$ outside, until a coordinate change is needed at an axis crossing.

**Axial <velocity> is constant within each uniform region, but not across the ends.** In a smooth fringe,
$$
\frac{d}{dt}(\gamma m\dot z)=\frac q2\rho^2\dot\theta B'(z).
$$
The entrance develops an azimuthal speed $|\Omega|\rho_0$ while radial <velocity> remains zero. Conservation of total speed gives
$$
\boxed{\dot z_{\rm in}=w=\sqrt{v^2-\Omega^2\rho_0^2}}.
$$
Forward passage presumes the radicand is positive. The exit fringe changes axial <velocity> again. Neglecting these small changes is a <paraxial approximation>, requiring $|\Omega|\rho_0\ll v$, rather than an exact consequence of cylindrical <symmetry>.

= Solution

Set $t=0$ just inside the entrance, $z=0$, $\rho=\rho_0$ and $\theta=\theta_0$. The stated continuity gives $\dot\rho(0)=0$. Until the first axis crossing,
$$
\boxed{\rho(t)=\rho_0\cos(\Omega t),\quad\theta(t)=\theta_0-\Omega t,\quad z(t)=wt},\qquad
w=\sqrt{v^2-\Omega^2\rho_0^2}.
$$
For the full orbit, including any crossings, use the transverse complex coordinate $u=x+iy$:
$$
\boxed{u(t)=\rho_0e^{i\theta_0}\cos(\Omega t)e^{-i\Omega t}
=\frac{\rho_0e^{i\theta_0}}2(1+e^{-2i\Omega t})},\qquad 0<t<l/w.
$$
This <helical solenoid trajectory with zero incident angular momentum> is a circle of radius $\rho_0/2$ about transverse guiding centre $u_0/2$, combined with uniform axial motion. Its cyclotron <angular frequency> is $|qB|/(\gamma m)=2|\Omega|$, and its axial pitch per turn is $\pi w/|\Omega|$. The speed check is $|\dot u|^2+w^2=\Omega^2\rho_0^2+w^2=v^2$.

The physical radius is $|u|=\rho_0|\cos\Omega t|$. When the cosine changes sign, a nonnegative-radius coordinate adds $\pi$ to $\theta$; this is a coordinate change, not a discontinuous turn of the particle. For $q=0$ or $B=0$, the limit is simply $u=u_0$, $z=vt$.

= Solution
{parent=e}

Define the positive focusing <frequency>
$$
\boxed{\omega=|\Omega|=\frac{|qB|}{2\gamma_v m}}.
$$
In the <paraxial approximation> $w\simeq v$, traversal time is $T\simeq l/v$. The exit values are
$$
\rho_e=\rho_0\cos(\omega l/v),\qquad
\dot\rho_e=-\omega\rho_0\sin(\omega l/v).
$$
Across the exit fringe, $p_\theta=0$ and $A_\theta\to0$ remove the azimuthal <velocity>. The radial <velocity> remains continuous in the thin-fringe model. Outside, the transverse trajectory is therefore straight along a fixed azimuth, not a continuing helix. Taking the outgoing axial speed also as $v$, the distance to the axis is
$$
f=v\frac{\rho_e}{-\dot\rho_e}
=\boxed{\frac v\omega\cot\left(\frac{\omega l}v\right)}.
$$
It is independent of $\rho_0$, so all equal-speed paraxial rays focus to a common point. The condition $0<\omega l/v<\pi/2$ ensures the first focus lies beyond the exit. For a weak lens, $\boxed{f\simeq4(\gamma_vmv)^2/(q^2B^2l)}$.

The <relativistic solenoid magnetic lens> focuses either charge sign, while its rotation reverses with $qB$. Keeping the exact radius-dependent $w$, or the exit axial-speed change, produces radius-dependent corrections and spoils an exact common focus. Thus the requested focus is appropriately a paraxial result, with the end fields included even when their axial-speed changes are neglected.

Use metric signature $(+,-,-,-)$ and the model's rescaled units $c=\hbar=1$. Its exponential implicitly uses dimensionless rescaled field and coupling combinations; the numerical coefficients of the potential are those supplied. Treat $\phi$ and $\phi^*$ as independent variables in taking canonical derivatives:
$$
\pi_\phi=\dot\phi^*,\qquad\pi_{\phi^*}=\dot\phi.
$$
The <Hamiltonian density> is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal H=|\dot\phi|^2+|\nabla\phi|^2+V(\phi)
=\pi_\phi\pi_{\phi^*}+|\nabla\phi|^2-|\phi|^2+e^{\lambda|\phi|^2}}.
$$
The kinetic and gradient terms are nonnegative, so a static homogeneous minimum is obtained by minimizing the potential. Put $s=|\phi|^2\geq0$. For $\lambda=0$, $V=1-s$ is unbounded below. For $\lambda<0$, the exponential decays while $-s$ tends to minus infinity. Thus **a stable vacuum requires $\boxed{\lambda>0}$**.

For positive $\lambda$,
$$
\frac{dV}{ds}=-1+\lambda e^{\lambda s},\qquad
\frac{d^2V}{ds^2}=\lambda^2e^{\lambda s}>0.
$$
The <exponential complex-scalar potential> consequently has
$$
\boxed{\begin{cases}
|\phi|^2=-\ln\lambda/\lambda,&0<\lambda<1,\\
\phi=0,&\lambda\geq1.
\end{cases}}
$$
For $0<\lambda<1$, the minima form a circle $\phi=ve^{i\theta}$, with $v^2=-\ln\lambda/\lambda$ and $V_{\min}=(1+\ln\lambda)/\lambda$. For $\lambda\geq1$ the minimum has $V_{\min}=1$. The <symmetry> remains global <phase> rotation in either <phase>, but a chosen nonzero vacuum breaks it spontaneously. At $\lambda=1$ the origin is a unique minimum with vanishing quadratic curvature and a positive leading quartic term; this critical masslessness should not be confused with a Goldstone mode.

Let $v=\sqrt{-\ln\lambda/\lambda}>0$ and write $\phi=v+\chi$. Then
$$
|\phi|^2=v^2+v(\chi+\chi^*)+\chi^*\chi.
$$
Since $V'(v^2)=0$ and $V''(v^2)=\lambda$, the quadratic potential is
$$
V=V_{\min}+\frac\lambda2v^2(\chi+\chi^*)^2+O(\chi^3).
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L^{(2)}=\partial_\mu\chi^*\partial^\mu\chi-\frac{\lambda v^2}{2}(\chi+\chi^*)^2-V_{\min}}.
$$
The constant can be dropped from the fluctuation equations. For canonical real fields, use $\chi=(h+i\pi)/\sqrt2$:
$$
\mathcal L^{(2)}=\frac12(\partial h)^2+\frac12(\partial\pi)^2-\frac12m_h^2h^2,
\qquad
\boxed{m_h^2=2\lambda v^2=-2\ln\lambda>0,\quad m_\pi^2=0}.
$$
These are the <radial and angular masses of a phase-invariant scalar potential>. The radial <Higgs mode> changes the field magnitude and costs <energy> to second order. The angular <Goldstone boson> changes the <phase> along the vacuum circle without a quadratic restoring <force>. Its linear dispersion is $\omega^2=|\mathbf k|^2$, whereas the radial mode has $\omega^2=|\mathbf k|^2+m_h^2$. The zero angular <mass> follows the continuous broken global <symmetry>, not from an accidental cancellation in a particular coordinate choice.

Write $\phi=x+iy$. The added term is $-(x^2-y^2)$, so the full potential simplifies to
$$
V(x,y)=-2x^2+e^{\lambda(x^2+y^2)}.
$$
The phase-rotation subgroup is reduced from <U(1)> to the two-element <symmetry> $\phi\mapsto-\phi$. More fully, the kinetic term and potential are invariant under independent reflections $x\mapsto-x$ and $y\mapsto-y$, a $\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_2$ internal <symmetry> generated by $\phi\mapsto-\phi$ and <complex conjugation>. There is no continuous internal rotation <symmetry> for the <explicit twofold anisotropy of a complex scalar field>.

For fixed $x$, positive $\lambda$ makes the exponential grow with $y^2$, so every global minimum has $y=0$. On the real axis,
$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}=2x[-2+\lambda e^{\lambda x^2}].
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\begin{cases}
\phi=\pm v,\quad v^2=\ln(2/\lambda)/\lambda,&0<\lambda<2,\\
\phi=0,&\lambda\geq2.
\end{cases}}
$$
For $0<\lambda<2$, the nonzero stationary points are the two global minima; the origin is unstable along the real direction. For $\lambda\geq2$ the origin is the unique minimum, with zero real-direction quadratic curvature at the critical value $2$. Selecting $+v$ breaks the sign reversal that exchanges the two vacua, while <complex conjugation> remains unbroken.

Set $\phi=v+\chi$, $\chi=u+iw$. Since $e^{\lambda v^2}=2/\lambda$, expansion gives
$$
\boxed{V=V_{\min}+4\lambda v^2u^2+2w^2+O(\chi^3)},\qquad
V_{\min}=\frac2\lambda\left[1-\ln\frac2\lambda\right].
$$
Equivalently, in canonical fields $h=\sqrt2u$, $\pi=\sqrt2w$,
$$
V=V_{\min}+\frac12m_h^2h^2+\frac12m_\pi^2\pi^2+\cdots,\qquad
\boxed{m_h^2=4\lambda v^2=4\ln(2/\lambda)>0,\quad m_\pi^2=2>0}.

Both masses are positive throughout the broken <phase> $0<\lambda<2$. A broken discrete <symmetry> does not demand a massless <Goldstone boson>. At $\lambda=2$ the radial <mass> tends to zero at a <phase> boundary, so a claim of strictly positive <mass> there would be incorrect. For completeness, expanding around the symmetric origin gives squared masses $\lambda-2$ and $\lambda$ for the canonical real and imaginary fields.

= Frequency-space inverse
{parent=Solution}

Use exactly the supplied inverse <Fourier transform> convention $e^{-i\omega\tau}$, with $\tau=t-t'$. Then $\partial_t\mapsto-i\omega$ and $\partial_t^3\mapsto i\omega^3$. The transformed equation is
$$
(1-\omega^2-i\varepsilon\omega^3)x(\omega)=f(\omega),\qquad
\boxed{G(\omega)=\frac1{1-\omega^2-i\varepsilon\omega^3}}.
$$
This algebraic inverse still needs a <pole> prescription to select the time-domain solution, particularly when a <pole> is on the real integration contour.

= Causal support and contour conditions
{parent=Solution}

A <retarded Green function> obeys $G(\tau)=0$ for $\tau<0$ and
$$
(\partial_\tau^2+1-\varepsilon\partial_\tau^3)G(\tau)=\delta(\tau).
$$
For a bounded or suitably tempered response represented by the real-axis Fourier integral, analyticity in the upper <frequency> half-plane is required, with suitable behaviour at infinity. For negative $\tau$, $e^{-i\omega\tau}$ decays on a large upper semicircle. If that half-plane has no singularities, contour closure gives zero response before the source. Lower-half-plane poles give factors decaying for positive time. Real poles are approached from below for a retarded prescription; at $\varepsilon=0$, for example, $G_R(\tau)=\Theta(\tau)\sin\tau$.

The <causality versus stability of a Green function> distinction matters: an unstable initial-value problem can still have a strictly causal response that grows exponentially. Such a response has no ordinary real-axis <Fourier transform>. Its inverse <Laplace transform> must run on a <frequency> contour above its growth poles. Thus “upper poles violate causality” is correct only with the stable/tempered real-axis Fourier assumptions, not as an unconditional theorem about differential equations.

= Perturbation of the oscillatory poles
{parent=Solution}

At $\varepsilon=0$ the algebraic poles are $\boxed{\omega_\pm^{(0)}=\pm1}$; a retarded limiting prescription displaces them infinitesimally below the real axis. Set $\omega_s=s+\delta_s$, with $s=\pm1$ and $\delta_s=O(\varepsilon)$. The denominator becomes
$$
1-(s+\delta_s)^2-i\varepsilon(s+\delta_s)^3
=-2s\delta_s-i\varepsilon s^3+O(\varepsilon^2).
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\omega_\pm=\pm1-\frac{i\varepsilon}{2}+O(\varepsilon^2)}.
$$
For positive $\varepsilon$ these two poles move below the real axis, giving slowly damped oscillations. For negative $\varepsilon$ they move above it and the slow oscillations grow. This is only the slow part of the <radiation-reaction oscillator pole structure>; the third <pole> must still be examined.

= Sign of radiation damping and comparison with velocity damping
{parent=Solution}

The sign can be checked independently by multiplying the equation by $\dot x$. With $E=(\dot x^2+x^2)/2$,
$$
\frac{d}{dt}(E-\varepsilon\ddot x\dot x)
=f\dot x-\varepsilon\ddot x^2.
$$
For periodic motion the total-derivative near-field term averages to zero, so positive $\varepsilon$ gives a radiative <energy> loss, consistent with the slow poles' damping. Negative $\varepsilon$ acts as <energy> gain in this model.

For the comparison equation **as printed**, $\ddot x+x-\varepsilon\dot x=f$, the response denominator instead is $1-\omega^2+i\varepsilon\omega$, with exact poles
$$
\omega_\pm=\frac{i\varepsilon}{2}\pm\sqrt{1-\varepsilon^2/4}
=\pm1+\frac{i\varepsilon}{2}+O(\varepsilon^2).
$$
Thus ordinary positive damping in that sign convention requires $\boxed{\varepsilon<0}$, the opposite sign from slow radiation damping in the third-derivative equation. The word “damped” does not override the explicitly printed minus sign. With the usual equation $\ddot x+x+\gamma\dot x=f$, positive $\gamma$ gives the familiar lower-half-plane poles.

Substitute $\omega=\alpha_3/\varepsilon+\cdots$ into the cubic denominator. Its leading terms are
$$
1-\omega^2-i\varepsilon\omega^3
=-\frac{\alpha_3^2+i\alpha_3^3}{\varepsilon^2}+O(1).
$$
The nonzero large root obeys $1+i\alpha_3=0$, so
$$
\boxed{\alpha_3=i,\qquad\omega_3\simeq\frac i\varepsilon}.
$$
The sum of the three roots is exactly $i/\varepsilon$. Combining this with the two slow-root expansions gives the refinement $\omega_3=i/\varepsilon+i\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^3)$.

For physical radiative sign $\varepsilon>0$, the fast <pole> lies high in the upper half-plane. Its homogeneous mode is $e^{-i\omega_3t}\simeq e^{t/\varepsilon}$, a runaway. It prevents a **bounded retarded response with the real-axis Fourier prescription** even though the two slow poles are damped. If $\varepsilon<0$, the fast <pole> decays but the slow pair grow. No nonzero real sign puts every <pole> in the lower half-plane.

There are two distinct boundary choices. A strictly causal initial-value <Green function> exists and includes the growing mode for $\varepsilon>0$; it must be represented by a Laplace/<Bromwich contour> above that <pole> rather than the stated ordinary Fourier integral. Alternatively the real-axis inverse gives a bounded <stationary no-runaway response and preacceleration>: for $\tau<0$, upper closure encloses the fast <pole> and yields a nonzero response before the <force>. Its leading contribution is
$$
G(\tau)\simeq\varepsilon e^{\tau/\varepsilon},\qquad \tau<0,\quad\varepsilon>0.
$$
This follows from the upper-pole <residue> $\operatorname{Res}G\simeq-i\varepsilon$ and the factor $i$ from upper contour closure. Eliminating runaway growth by a future <boundary condition> therefore sacrifices strict retardation on the short scale $\varepsilon$.

The singular root also shows why a perturbative expansion around only $\omega=\pm1$ misses the problem. For a low-frequency effective description, <order reduction of a radiation-reaction oscillator> uses $\dddot x\simeq\dot f-\dot x$ in the small correction and gives $\ddot x+\varepsilon\dot x+x=f+\varepsilon\dot f$. For positive $\varepsilon$ this approximate second-order equation has a causal damped response and discards the fast mode outside its intended regime; it does not make the original third-order equation simultaneously bounded and retarded.
