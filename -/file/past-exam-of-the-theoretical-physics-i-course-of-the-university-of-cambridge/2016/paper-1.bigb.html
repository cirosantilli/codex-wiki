= Solution
{parent=a}

Write the bead position in <Cartesian coordinates> as $\mathbf x_B=(a\cos\theta_B,a\sin\theta_B,b\theta_B)$. Differentiation gives $|\dot{\mathbf x}_B|^2=(a^2+b^2)\dot\theta_B^2$, while the free particle has $|\dot{\mathbf x}|^2=\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2+\dot z^2$. Thus the <kinetic energy> is the sum of these squared <speeds> weighted by half the respective <mass>. The squared <spring> length is
$$
\ell^2=|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_B|^2=r^2+a^2-2ar\cos(\theta-\theta_B)+(z-b\theta_B)^2.
$$
A <spring> obeying <Hooke's law> with zero natural length has <potential energy> $k\ell^2/2$. Consequently **$L=T-k\ell^2/2$**, and deleting the additive constant $-ka^2/2$ gives
$$
L=\frac m2(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2+\dot z^2)+\frac{M(a^2+b^2)}2\dot\theta_B^2-\frac k2\left[r^2-2ar\cos(\theta-\theta_B)+(z-b\theta_B)^2\right].
$$
The <spring> length is the straight separation of its endpoints, not distance measured along the <helix>. The fixed frictionless constraint is already incorporated by using the <generalized coordinate> $\theta_B$; it supplies no extra <potential energy>.

= Solution

Set $\Delta=\theta-\theta_B$ and $u=z-b\theta_B$. Applying the <Euler-Lagrange equations> separately to $r,\theta,z,\theta_B$ gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
m(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2)&=-k(r-a\cos\Delta),\\
\frac{d}{dt}(mr^2\dot\theta)&=-kar\sin\Delta,\\
m\ddot z&=-ku,\\
M(a^2+b^2)\ddot\theta_B&=k(ar\sin\Delta+bu).
\end{aligned}}
$$
For example, $\partial L/\partial\theta=-kar\sin\Delta$ whereas $\partial L/\partial\theta_B=kar\sin\Delta+kb u$; these signs also follow from equal and opposite <spring> <forces> projected on the permitted bead motion. The angular particle equation can equivalently be written $m(r^2\ddot\theta+2r\dot r\dot\theta)=-kar\sin\Delta$. The factor $M(a^2+b^2)$ is the bead's inertia with respect to the helical coordinate, rather than its <moment of inertia> about the axis alone.

= Solution

The continuous transformation $\theta\mapsto\theta+\epsilon$, $\theta_B\mapsto\theta_B+\epsilon$, $z\mapsto z+b\epsilon$, $r\mapsto r$ moves both endpoints by the same screw motion and preserves the fixed <helix>. Both $\Delta$ and $u$ are unchanged, so $\delta L=0$. <Noether theorem> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal Q=p_\theta+p_{\theta_B}+bp_z=mr^2\dot\theta+M(a^2+b^2)\dot\theta_B+bm\dot z=\text{constant}.}
$$
This is the <Noether conserved quantity for a mechanical point symmetry> applied to <helical symmetry>. In physical Cartesian terms it equals **total $z$ <angular momentum> plus $b$ times total $z$ <linear momentum>**: the bead contributes $Ma^2\dot\theta_B+b(Mb\dot\theta_B)$. Neither pure axial <translation> nor pure axial <rotation> preserves the fixed coil separately. Their conserved combination is a consequence of the <helical symmetry>, not two independently conserved momenta. Substitution of the four equations of motion verifies $\dot{\mathcal Q}=-kar\sin\Delta+k(ar\sin\Delta+bu)-bku=0$.<Classical Dynamics by David Tong>[online chapter 2, §2.4.1, equations (2.76)–(2.79): continuous coordinate symmetry and its conserved momentum]{ref}

In the negligible bead-mass limit the bead contribution to $\mathcal Q$ vanishes. Since the particle starts at rest, $\mathcal Q=0$, and hence $r^2\dot\theta+b\dot z=0$. Assume $b\ne0$ and a regular massless limit. Integrating along the trajectory gives
$$
\Delta z=-\frac1b\int_0^T r^2\dot\theta\,dt=-\frac1b\oint_C(x\,dy-y\,dx),
$$
where $C$ is the closed projection onto the plane perpendicular to the coil axis. The given final $r$ and $\theta$ ensure that this projection closes. <Green's theorem> identifies
$$
A=\frac12\oint_C(x\,dy-y\,dx)=\frac12\oint_C r^2\,d\theta,\qquad \boxed{\Delta z=-\frac{2A}{b}}.
$$
Thus **$A$ is the <signed area> enclosed by the transverse projection**, positive for counterclockwise traversal viewed from positive $z$. Self-intersecting projections contribute their winding-weighted areas; it is neither an unsigned area nor an area of a surface in three dimensions. For $b>0$, a positive <signed area> gives negative axial displacement. This is <signed-area displacement under a helical constraint>: returning to the same projected coordinates can leave a nonzero axial displacement.

Use <natural units> and the <Minkowski metric> $g_{\mu\nu}=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$, with $A^\mu=(\Phi,\mathbf A)$ and $A_\mu=(\Phi,-\mathbf A)$. Then $F_{0i}=E_i$, $F^{i0}=E_i$, and $F^{ij}=-\epsilon_{ijk}B_k$. The <electromagnetic field tensor> is an <antisymmetric tensor>, which gives gives
$$
\delta\mathcal L=-\tfrac12F^{\mu\nu}\delta F_{\mu\nu}=-F^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\delta A_\nu.
$$
<integration by parts> in the <action>, with the <variation> vanishing on its boundary, yields $\delta S=\int d^4x\,(\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu})\delta A_\nu$. Independent <variations> of the four components therefore give **$\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=0$**. The time component is $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0$; the spatial components are $\nabla\times\mathbf B-\partial_t\mathbf E=0$. These are the two source-free <Maxwell equations> obtained dynamically. The other two, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$ and $\partial_t\mathbf B+\nabla\times\mathbf E=0$, follow identically from $F=dA$, through the <Electromagnetic Bianchi identity>. They are not additional <Euler-Lagrange field equations>.<Quantum Field Theory by David Tong>[chapter 1, §§1.1 and 1.1.3: potential conventions and the Maxwell variation]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=b}

In the same <Minkowski metric> and electromagnetic units, $T^{\mu\nu}=-F^\mu{}_{\lambda}F^{\nu\lambda}+\tfrac14g^{\mu\nu}F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta}$. Differentiating gives
$$
\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=-(\partial_\mu F^\mu{}_{\lambda})F^{\nu\lambda}-F^\mu{}_{\lambda}\partial_\mu F^{\nu\lambda}+\frac14\partial^\nu(F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta}).
$$
To simplify the last two terms, contract $\partial_\mu F_{\nu\lambda}+\partial_\nu F_{\lambda\mu}+\partial_\lambda F_{\mu\nu}=0$ with $F^{\mu\lambda}$. Its first and third terms are equal, and its middle term is $-\tfrac12\partial_\nu(F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta})$. Raising $\nu$ therefore gives $F^\mu{}_{\lambda}\partial_\mu F^{\nu\lambda}=\tfrac14\partial^\nu(F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta})$. The cancellation leaves
$$
\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=-F^{\nu\lambda}\partial_\mu F^\mu{}_{\lambda},\qquad \boxed{\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=0\quad\text{in vacuum}.}
$$
This is the <electromagnetic stress-energy divergence identity> in rationalized units. As a sign check, $T^{00}=(E^2+B^2)/2$ is positive and $T^{0i}=(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)_i$ is the <Poynting vector>. The integrated <four-momentum> $P^\nu=\int T^{0\nu}d^3x$ is conserved if the boundary flux vanishes. With matter <sources>, the <electromagnetic field> alone instead obeys $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=-F^{\nu\lambda}J_\lambda$: <energy> and momentum transfer to matter restore conservation of the total <stress-energy tensor>.

Hold $\phi,\phi^*$ fixed while varying $A_\nu$. With the specified convention $D_\mu=\partial_\mu+iqA_\mu$, the two matter <variations> are $\delta(D_\mu\phi)=iq\phi\,\delta A_\mu$ and $\delta(D_\mu\phi)^*=-iq\phi^*\delta A_\mu$. Thus
$$
\delta\mathcal L_{\rm matter}=iq\left[\phi(D^\nu\phi)^*-\phi^*D^\nu\phi\right]\delta A_\nu=-J^\nu\delta A_\nu.
$$
Adding the Maxwell <variation> from part (a) gives $\delta S=\int d^4x\,[\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}-J^\nu]\delta A_\nu$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=J^\nu,\qquad J^\mu=iq\left[\phi^*D^\mu\phi-\phi(D^\mu\phi)^*\right].}
$$
Expanding the <gauge covariant derivatives> supplies a useful convention check:
$$
J^\mu=iq(\phi^*\partial^\mu\phi-\phi\partial^\mu\phi^*)-2q^2A^\mu|\phi|^2.
$$
In particular, the last sign is negative for the specified plus sign in $D_\mu$. Reversing that convention requires consistently changing the phase transformation and current conventions as well. This current is the matter <source> in <scalar electrodynamics>; its $A$ dependence must not be dropped.

Take the constant-parameter subgroup of the local transformation: $\delta\phi=-iq\epsilon\phi$, $\delta\phi^*=iq\epsilon\phi^*$, $\delta A_\mu=0$. The momenta conjugate to the <complex scalar field> <gradients> are $\Pi_\phi^\mu=(D^\mu\phi)^*$ and $\Pi_{\phi^*}^\mu=D^\mu\phi$. <Noether theorem> gives
$$
j^\mu=\Pi_\phi^\mu\frac{\delta\phi}{\epsilon}+\Pi_{\phi^*}^\mu\frac{\delta\phi^*}{\epsilon}=iq[\phi^*D^\mu\phi-\phi(D^\mu\phi)^*]=J^\mu.
$$
The <scalar field> equations are $D_\mu D^\mu\phi+m^2\phi=0$ and their <complex conjugate>. Applying the <product rule>, the gauge-potential terms cancel to give
$$
\partial_\mu J^\mu=iq\left[\phi^*D_\mu D^\mu\phi-\phi(D_\mu D^\mu\phi)^*\right]=iq[-m^2|\phi|^2+m^2|\phi|^2]=\boxed{0}.
$$
This establishes conservation of the <Noether current of scalar electrodynamics> <on shell>, and conservation of its integrated <electric charge> when there is no boundary flux. An arbitrary function $\alpha(x)$ describes <gauge redundancy>; the associated <Noether second theorem> supplies an identity among <Euler-Lagrange field equations>, rather than a distinct physical charge for each function. Indeed its on-shell current can be written $j^\mu[\alpha]=\alpha J^\mu-F^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu\alpha=-\partial_\nu(\alpha F^{\mu\nu})$. The <antisymmetric tensor> makes its <divergence> vanish. Independently, differentiating the Maxwell equation also gives $\partial_\nu J^\nu=\partial_\nu\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=0$, confirming compatibility with the matter derivation.<Quantum Field Theory by David Tong>[§1.3.4, internal phase symmetry; §6.1, gauge symmetry and its constant subgroup]{ref}

Treat $\phi$ and $\phi^*$ as independent variables during <variation>. With <Minkowski metric> $(+---)$, differentiation with respect to $\phi^*$ gives
$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi^*)}=\partial^\mu\phi+ieA^\mu\phi,\qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi^*}=-m^2\phi-ieA^\mu\partial_\mu\phi+f(t).
$$
The <Euler-Lagrange field equation> is therefore $\Box\phi+2ieA^\mu\partial_\mu\phi+ie(\partial_\mu A^\mu)\phi+m^2\phi=f$. The static purely temporal potential has $\partial_\mu A^\mu=\partial_tV=0$, giving
$$
\boxed{(\partial_t^2-\nabla^2+2ieV(\mathbf r)\partial_t+m^2)\phi=f(t).}
$$
<variation> with respect to $\phi$ gives $(\Box-2ieA^\mu\partial_\mu+m^2)\phi^*=f(t)$. This is the <complex conjugate> equation when the driving <force> is real, as is required for the supplied <lagrangian density> to be real. For a complex driving amplitude a real <source> term would instead require conjugate coefficients. The interaction supplied here is linear in the external potential: adding the $e^2A_\mu A^\mu|\phi|^2$ term from full <minimal coupling> would change the problem and its <equation of motion>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Time independence of $V$ makes the <Green function> depend on $t,t'$ through $\tau=t-t'$. A spatially varying potential does not imply spatial <translation> invariance. Fix $\mathbf r'$ and set $\mathbf x=\mathbf r-\mathbf r'$; write the Fourier coefficient more fully as $G_{\mathbf r'}(\mathbf k;\omega)$. The <source> position is an implicit parameter in the printed notation. Define
$$
V_{\mathbf r'}(\mathbf q)=\int d^3x\,V(\mathbf r'+\mathbf x)e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x}=e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r'}\widetilde V(\mathbf q).
$$
The ordinary derivative terms transform into $(-\omega^2+k^2+m^2)G_{\mathbf r'}$. The product $V\partial_tG$ becomes a <convolution>, and $2ie(-i\omega)=2e\omega$. More explicitly, insert both inverse spatial <Fourier transforms> into $VG$: the integral over $\mathbf x$ enforces $\mathbf q+\mathbf k'=\mathbf k$. The transformed delta <source> is one, leaving
$$
\boxed{(-\omega^2+k^2+m^2)G_{\mathbf r'}(\mathbf k;\omega)+2e\omega\int\frac{d^3k'}{(2\pi)^3}V_{\mathbf r'}(\mathbf k-\mathbf k')G_{\mathbf r'}(\mathbf k';\omega)=1.}
$$
This is a <source-position-dependent Fourier Green function>. Dropping the explicit $\mathbf r'$ subscripts recovers the requested notation with exactly the potential transform defined in the source. No assumption of a homogeneous potential is needed. The factor $(2\pi)^{-3}$ and the source-position phase are necessary for the stated Fourier convention.

The delta function in the specified <convolution> kernel collapses the $\mathbf k'$ integral to $-i\gamma G$. Set $\lambda=e\gamma$ and $\omega_0^2=k^2+m^2$. Then
$$
G(\mathbf k;\omega)=\frac1{-\omega^2-2i\lambda\omega+\omega_0^2},\qquad G(\mathbf k;\tau)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{e^{-i\omega\tau}}{-\omega^2-2i\lambda\omega+\omega_0^2}.
$$
There is a change of assumptions here: the specified kernel is the transform of **$V=-i\gamma$**, which is not real. These later parts define a formal <analytic continuation> to an effective damped equation, $[\partial_\tau^2+2\lambda\partial_\tau+\omega_0^2]G=\delta(\tau)$; they cannot simultaneously describe the earlier real external potential. The following <pole> analysis uses that continued operator.

Completing the square gives $D(\omega)=-(\omega+i\lambda)^2+(\omega_0^2-\lambda^2)$, so
$$
\boxed{\omega_\pm=-i\lambda\pm\sqrt{k^2+m^2-\lambda^2}.}
$$
For $e>0$, hence $\lambda>0$, the underdamped regime has two <poles> at $\pm\Omega-i\lambda$, with $\Omega=\sqrt{k^2+m^2-\lambda^2}>0$. Both lie below the <real axis>. In the overdamped regime they lie on the <imaginary axis> at $-i(\lambda\mp\kappa)$, where $\kappa=\sqrt{\lambda^2-k^2-m^2}<\lambda$ for $k^2+m^2>0$; both are again below the <real axis>. At equality they coalesce into a <double pole> at $-i\lambda$. If $m<|\lambda|$, the crossover occurs at $k_c=\sqrt{\lambda^2-m^2}$; increasing $k$ moves the two imaginary <poles> together and then apart horizontally. If $m>|\lambda|$, every $k$ is underdamped; if equal, only $k=0$ is critical.

The sign of $e$ is not specified: for $e<0$ all nonzero-frequency <poles> are instead in the <upper half-plane>, describing <antidamping>; for $e=0$ they lie on the <real axis> and a causal prescription is required. When $m=k=0$, one <pole> is at zero even for nonzero $\lambda$ and a real-axis prescription is again needed. The figure uses illustrative $\lambda=1$, not supplied numerical data.

= Solution

Let $\tau=t-t'$ and $\Omega=\sqrt{k^2+m^2-\lambda^2}>0$. First take $\lambda=e\gamma>0$. Since $|e^{-i\omega\tau}|=e^{\tau\operatorname{Im}\omega}$, close the <contour> below for $\tau>0$, and above for $\tau<0$. On the chosen semicircle the exponential is bounded and the denominator grows as $R^2$, so the arc integral tends to zero. The upper <contour> encloses no <pole>, giving **$G=0$ for $t<t'$**. The lower <contour> is clockwise, hence the real-axis integral divided by $2\pi$ is $-i$ times the sum of <residues>. Since $D'(\omega_+)=-2\Omega$ and $D'(\omega_-)=2\Omega$, that sum is
$$
-\frac{e^{-i(\Omega-i\lambda)\tau}}{2\Omega}+\frac{e^{-i(-\Omega-i\lambda)\tau}}{2\Omega}=i e^{-\lambda\tau}\frac{\sin(\Omega\tau)}{\Omega}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{G(\mathbf k;t,t')=H(t-t')\,e^{-\lambda(t-t')}\frac{\sin[\Omega(t-t')]}{\Omega}\quad(\lambda>0).}
$$
This is the <causal Green function of a damped oscillator>. Its value is continuous through $\tau=0$, and its derivative jumps from zero to one; integrating the <differential equation> through zero verifies the delta-source normalization and the <residue> sign. The limits are $H(\tau)\tau e^{-\lambda\tau}$ at <critical damping> and $H(\tau)e^{-\lambda\tau}\sinh(\kappa\tau)/\kappa$ in the overdamped regime.

For completeness, if the unspecified charge has $e<0$, the same real-axis integral instead encloses upper <poles> for $\tau<0$ and gives $-H(-\tau)e^{-\lambda\tau}\sin(\Omega\tau)/\Omega$, an advanced <Green function>. A causal growing response would require an inverse-Laplace <contour> above those <poles>, not the stated real-axis Fourier integral. At $e=0$ the real-axis integral needs a <pole> prescription; shifting both <poles> below it gives the retarded zero-damping limit. These distinctions are necessary because $\gamma>0$ alone does not fix the sign of damping.

For the isotropic zero-field <classical Heisenberg model> of a <ferromagnet>, the <analytic vector Landau expansion> uses the Cartesian components of the <vector order parameter> near $\mathbf m=0$. <rotational symmetry> makes its scalar terms functions of $\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf m$. Global <spin inversion symmetry> also requires $f(\mathbf m)=f(-\mathbf m)$. Through fourth order this gives
$$
\boxed{a=c=0,\qquad f=bm^2+dm^4,\qquad d>0.}
$$
A positive quartic coefficient stabilizes the unconstrained truncated <free energy>. The disordered phase has $b>0$ and its minimum at $m=0$; the aligned phase has $b<0$ and a minimum at nonzero $m$. At the continuous transition $b=0$. The attractive alignment interaction supplies an energetic tendency toward negative quadratic curvature; orientational <entropy> favors the disordered phase at high <temperature>, so the sign of $b$ is a free-energy question rather than a consequence of the pair <energy> alone.

The analyticity assumption matters: the variable printed as $m$ is a nonnegative magnitude, so reversing $\mathbf m$ does not send that scalar to $-m$. Terms proportional to $|\mathbf m|$ or $|\mathbf m|^3$ would be rotationally invariant but nonanalytic at the <vector> origin. Standard analytic <Landau theory> excludes them. Without that assumption, the stated pair <symmetry> alone would not exclude odd powers of the magnitude.<Statistical Field Theory by David Tong>[§1.2, small-order-parameter expansion; §4.2, rotationally invariant vector free energy]{ref}

Differentiation gives $f'(m)=2m(b+2dm^2)$. For $b>0$ the origin is the <global minimum>; for $b<0$, $m=\sqrt{-b/(2d)}$ has $f''=-4b>0$ and $f=-b^2/(4d)<f(0)$. Therefore, close enough to the transition for the quartic expansion to apply,
$$
\boxed{m_{\rm eq}(T)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{(T_c-T)/(2dT_c)},&T<T_c,\\0,&T\geq T_c.\end{cases}}
$$
The <order parameter> approaches zero continuously with <mean-field critical exponent> $\beta=1/2$: **the transition is continuous**. In the aligned phase a particular direction is chosen. The vector-spin <rotation> <symmetry> is spontaneously broken from $SO(3)$ to $SO(2)$, or from $O(3)$ to $O(2)$ including reflections. The manifold of <vector order parameter> directions is a <sphere>, so this is not just a two-state sign choice.<Statistical Field Theory by David Tong>[§4.2, $O(3)$ Heisenberg model and the continuous set of ordered directions]{ref}

The <independent head-tail reversal symmetry> means that the squared <dot product> interaction <energy> is unchanged when any individual $\mathbf s_i$ is replaced by $-\mathbf s_i$. All configurations $\mathbf s_i=\sigma_i\mathbf n$, with $\sigma_i=\pm1$ independently, attain the aligned ground-state pair <energy>. There is consequently no energetic preference for either end of a molecular axis. Averaging over these equally weighted signs gives $\langle\mathbf s_i\rangle=0$ and **$\langle\mathbf m\rangle=0$ even in the nematically ordered phase**. The surviving order is the unoriented axis, represented by a <nematic director> with $\mathbf n\sim-\mathbf n$. A second-moment tensor can detect this order while a polar <vector> cannot.<Lectures on Landau Theory of Phase Transitions by Peter D. Olmsted>[§4.3, printed pp.10–11, head-tail equivalence and the tensor order parameter]{ref}

The word “always” needs the <statistical ensemble> or apolar interpretation: it is not an identity for each finite configuration. For example, choosing all $\sigma_i=+1$ is also a ground-state representative and gives $\mathbf m=\mathbf n$. In an unbiased sign <statistical ensemble> the typical residual polar <vector> has size of order $N^{-1/2}$, because $\langle|\mathbf m|^2\rangle=1/N$ for these independent ground-state signs. This does not make polar order an equilibrium <nematic order parameter>. The tensor below is unchanged by each sign flip and thus removes the arbitrary labeling of rod ends.

Choose a basis whose first axis is $\mathbf n$. The tensor with <uniaxial nematic order> then has <eigenvalues> $2Q,-Q,-Q$. Hence $\operatorname{Tr}S^2=6Q^2$, $\operatorname{Tr}S^3=6Q^3$ and $\operatorname{Tr}S^4=18Q^4$. Substitution gives
$$
\boxed{f(Q)=6aQ^2+6bQ^3+18cQ^4.}
$$
For $a,c>0$, $Q=0$ remains a <local minimum> with curvature $12a$. Its nonzero <stationary points> satisfy $2a+3bQ+12cQ^2=0$, so
$$
Q_\pm=\frac{-3b\pm\sqrt{9b^2-96ac}}{24c}.
$$
They first appear at $b_s=-\sqrt{32ac/3}$ as a stationary inflection point; for more negative $b$, $Q_-$ is a <local maximum> and $Q_+$ a <local minimum>. The latter is initially metastable, becomes degenerate with $Q=0$ at $b_t=-\sqrt{12ac}$, and then becomes the <global minimum> as $b$ decreases further. This is a <first-order transition in a cubic-quartic Landau potential>: **the order changes discontinuously**. The <saddle-node bifurcation> creating a <local minimum> is not the coexistence condition. The isotropic minimum never loses local stability in this fixed positive-$a$ scan.

At coexistence a nonzero $Q_t$ must be stationary and have the same <free energy> as $Q=0$. Dividing $f(Q_t)=0$ by $6Q_t^2$ and $f'(Q_t)=0$ by $6Q_t$ gives

a+bQ_t+3cQ_t^2=0,\qquad 2a+3bQ_t+12cQ_t^2=0.
$$
Eliminating $a$ yields $Q_t=-b/(6c)$ and then $b^2=12ac$. The allowed nonpositive root therefore gives
$$
\boxed{b_t=-\sqrt{12ac}=-2\sqrt{3ac},\qquad Q_t=\sqrt{\frac{a}{3c}}.}
$$
As $b$ is decreased through $b_t$, the equilibrium scalar order jumps **from $0$ for $b>b_t$ to $Q_t$ just on the $b<b_t$ side**. At $b=b_t$ both values coexist; the stable aligned branch below it is $Q_+$ from part (e). As a global-minimum check, at coexistence
$$
f(Q)=18cQ^2(Q-Q_t)^2\geq0.
$$
There are two equal minima and a barrier at $Q_t/2$, of height $a^2/(8c)$. Comparing these minima, rather than finding only the stationary-point discriminant, determines the transition.

This is the unrestricted Landau answer, compatible with the <physical range of a uniaxial nematic order parameter> when $a\leq3c$ so that $Q_t\leq1$. If one instead enforces $Q\in[-1/2,1]$ for arbitrary positive coefficients, negative $Q$ cannot win for $b\leq0$, and <bounded quartic nematic coexistence> minimizes $a/Q+3cQ$ over $0<Q\leq1$. For $a>3c$ its minimum is at the boundary, giving **$b_t=-(a+3c)$ and a jump $0\to1$**. No coefficients were supplied to choose this boundary case; the near-transition Landau approximation normally uses the interior result.
