= Galton-Watson process
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$Z_{n+1}=\sum_{j=1}^{Z_n}\xi_{n,j}$}

A <Galton-Watson process> is a <branching process> that counts individuals by generation. Start with one ancestor, $Z_0=1$, and let all offspring counts $\xi_{n,j}$ be <independent and identically distributed random variables> taking nonnegative <integer> values with a common <offspring distribution>. Then
$$
Z_{n+1}=\sum_{j=1}^{Z_n}\xi_{n,j},\qquad \mathbb P(\xi_{n,j}=k)=p_k,\quad k\geq0.
$$
The <empty sum> is zero: zero is an <absorbing state>, so once a generation is empty every later generation is empty. The same <offspring distribution> applies to every individual in every generation. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-445-introduction-to-stochastic-processes-spring-2015/27aaeb9eb9a9cecce93d9ad74413ddf5_MIT18_445S15_lecture19.pdf[Hao Wu, MIT 18.445, Lecture 19, slides 3–4]{ref}

For a concrete <offspring distribution>, take $p_0=1/4$, $p_1=1/4$, $p_2=1/2$, and $p_k=0$ for $k\geq3$. An individual has no children with <probability> $1/4$, one with <probability> $1/4$, and two with <probability> $1/2$. The offspring <expected value> is $\mu=5/4$. A possible sequence of generation sizes is $1,2,1,0,0,\ldots$: two children need not lead to a permanently growing family.

The <probability generating function> $F(s)=1/4+s/4+s^2/2$ gives the exact generation <probability distributions> through <branching-process generating-function iteration>. With $G_0(s)=s$ and $G_{n+1}(s)=F(G_n(s))$, the second generation has
$$
G_2(s)=\frac{11}{32}+\frac18s+\frac9{32}s^2+\frac18s^3+\frac18s^4.
$$
Thus $\mathbb P(Z_2=0)=11/32$ and $\mathbb E[Z_2]=25/16$. The figure shows full generation <probability mass functions>, computed by <polynomial> <function composition>, through generation six; no simulated frequencies are used.

\Image[branching-generation-distributions.png]
{title=Exact Galton-Watson generation distributions: growing means and increasing extinction mass}

The contrast is striking: the <expected generation size in a Galton-Watson process> grows as $(5/4)^n$, while the <branching extinction probability> is $1/2$. By generation six, $\mathbb P(Z_6=0)\approx0.462186$ although $\mathbb E[Z_6]\approx3.814697$. Larger surviving families carry the rising <expected value>; that <mean> is an average over possible families, not a forecast of one family's path. The <Galton-Watson extinction fixed point> turns an unbounded number of future generations into a single equation for this <probability>.

The historical question was the disappearance of surnames passed through male descendants. In https://math.uchicago.edu/~shmuel/Modeling/Galton%20Watson.pdf[Watson and Galton, On the Probability of the Extinction of Families, pages 139–140], <Francis Galton> formulates a constant-law model counting sons who reach adulthood, and Watson computes later generation <probability distributions> by composing <generating functions>. Those are assumptions of their model, rather than a claim that actual human reproduction follows this <offspring distribution>.

= Offspring distribution of a branching process

{title2=$p_k=\mathbb P(\xi=k),\quad F(s)=\sum_{k\geq0}p_ks^k$}

= Offspring distribution
{synonym}

The offspring <probability distribution> of a <Galton-Watson process> is the law of one individual's number of children: $p_k=\mathbb P(\xi=k)$ for $k\geq0$, with $p_k\geq0$ and $\sum_{k\geq0}p_k=1$. Its <probability generating function> and, when finite, its <expected value> are
$$
F(s)=\mathbb E[s^\xi]=\sum_{k\geq0}p_ks^k,\quad 0\leq s\leq1,\qquad \mu=\mathbb E[\xi]=\sum_{k\geq0}kp_k=F'(1-).
$$
Use $0^0=1$ when evaluating the <probability generating function> at zero: $F(0)=p_0$ and $F(1)=1$.

For $p_0=p_1=1/4$ and $p_2=1/2$, $F(s)=1/4+s/4+s^2/2$, $\mu=5/4$, and the offspring <variance> is $11/16$: $\mathbb E[\xi^2]=9/4$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\xi)=9/4-(5/4)^2$. The generation <probability mass functions> pictured under <Galton-Watson process> come from repeatedly composing this <probability generating function>. They describe the whole generation, whereas $(p_k)$ describes one individual's reproduction. https://www.uni-muenster.de/Stochastik/lehre/WS1011/SpezielleStochastischeProzesse/Ch_1.pdf[University of Münster branching-process notes, Section 1.3, Proposition 1.4]{ref}

The whole <offspring distribution> matters for extinction. Another law, $p_0=3/8$ and $p_2=5/8$, has the same offspring <mean> $5/4$ but <branching extinction probability> $3/5$, since $3/8+5s^2/8=s$ has <roots of a polynomial> $3/5$ and $1$. Consequently the two laws have the same <expected generation size in a Galton-Watson process> at each generation but different survival chances. A <binomial branching process> and a <Poisson branching process> likewise specify different <offspring distributions>; a common <mean> does not make their <probability generating functions> identical.

= Fractional-power offspring generating function
{parent=Offspring distribution of a branching process}
{title2=$G(s)=1-\alpha(1-s)^\beta$}

For $0<\alpha,\beta<1$, this <probability generating function> has probabilities $p_0=1-\alpha$ and $p_k=\alpha\beta(1-\beta)\cdots(k-1-\beta)/k!$ for $k\ge1$, where the product after $\beta$ is empty for $k=1$. All probabilities are positive and sum to one, while the <expected value> is infinite. In a <Galton-Watson process> starting with one individual, the generation-$n$ <probability generating function> is $G_n(s)=1-\alpha^{(1-\beta^n)/(1-\beta)}(1-s)^{\beta^n}$. The <branching extinction probability> is $1-\alpha^{1/(1-\beta)}$, obtained by taking the limit of $G_n(0)$ and using that extinction by generation $n$ is an increasing sequence of <events>.

= Branching process conditioned on extinction
{parent=Galton-Watson process}
{title2=$f_q(s)=f(qs)/q$}

Let $q\in(0,1]$ be the <branching extinction probability> of a <Galton-Watson process> with offspring <probability generating function> $f$. Conditional on extinction, the probability of $j$ children is multiplied by $q^j/q$: every child's descendants must also become extinct. Hence the new <probability generating function> is $f_q$ and its mean is $f'(q)$. If $f'(q)<1$, the <total progeny of a branching process> has conditional <expected value> $1/(1-f'(q))$, and unconditionally $\mathbb E[T;T<\infty]=q/(1-f'(q))$. The <Markov inequality> gives a finite-total-progeny tail bound without discarding the survival mass.

= Poisson branching process
{c}
{parent=Galton-Watson process}
{title2=$f(s)=e^{\mu(s-1)}$}

A <Galton-Watson process> with a <Poisson distribution> of offspring of mean $\mu$ has this <probability generating function>. For $\mu=1+\varepsilon>1$ its <branching survival probability> $r$ satisfies $-\log(1-r)=(1+\varepsilon)r$, and $2\varepsilon/(1+2\varepsilon)\leq r\leq2\varepsilon$. Indeed $\varepsilon=\sum_{j\geq1}r^j/(j+1)$ lies between $r/2$ and $r/[2(1-r)]$. These exact inequalities belong to the <Poisson branching process>, not to every finite <binomial branching process>.

= Bounded information in a subcritical Poisson branching process
{parent=Poisson branching process}
{title2=$i_n(\theta)=\frac{1-\theta^{n+1}}{\theta(1-\theta)},\quad0<\theta<1$}

Start with $Y_0\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\theta)$ and conditionally let $Y_i\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\theta Y_{i-1})$. The mean generation size is $\theta^{i+1}$. The log-likelihood is $S_n\log\theta-\theta T_n$ plus a parameter-free term, where $S_n=\sum_{i=0}^nY_i$ and $T_n=1+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}Y_i$. Expected negative curvature gives the displayed <Fisher information>, bounded as the horizon grows. Extinction makes the observations eventually all zero, so one longer observed family does not supply indefinitely increasing information.

= Binomial branching process
{parent=Galton-Watson process}
{title2=$f(s)=(1-p+ps)^n$}

A <Galton-Watson process> whose offspring law is a <binomial distribution> $\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)$ has mean $np$ and the displayed offspring <probability generating function>. Its <branching extinction probability> is the smallest root of $q=f(q)$ in $[0,1]$. It dominates a <breadth-first exploration of a binomial random graph> by allowing each explored <vertex> up to $n$ independent possible children, including previously exposed or already discovered <vertices> as phantom children.

= Binomial branching survival correction
{parent=Binomial branching process}
{title2=$\rho=2\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^2+\varepsilon/n)$}

For $p=(1+\varepsilon)/n$, the <branching survival probability> satisfies $1-\rho=(1-p\rho)^n$. If $(1+\varepsilon)^2<n$, comparison with a <Poisson branching process> and expansion of the <logarithm> give
$$
\frac{2\varepsilon}{1+2\varepsilon}\leq\rho\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{1-(1+\varepsilon)^2/n}.
$$
For the upper bound use $\varepsilon=\sum_{j\geq1}(1-np^{j+1})\rho^j/(j+1)$ and retain its first nonnegative term. For fixed $n>1$, the leading term is instead $2n\varepsilon/(n-1)$. In particular $n=2$ gives $\rho=4\varepsilon/(1+\varepsilon)^2$, showing that the uncorrected exact bound $\rho\leq2\varepsilon$ is false for small positive $\varepsilon$.

For $0<\varepsilon\leq1/4$ and $n\varepsilon\geq2$, the uncorrected exact upper bound does hold for the <binomial branching process>. Here $np^2\leq\varepsilon$ and $np^3\leq1/4$, so at $x=2\varepsilon$ the first two terms of the nonnegative series give $\varepsilon(1-np^2)+(4/3)\varepsilon^2(1-np^3)\geq\varepsilon$. Monotonicity of that series yields $\rho\leq2\varepsilon$. This recovers the intended large-$n$ bracket without claiming it for every finite reproduction law.

= Total progeny
{synonym}

The total progeny counts all individuals, including the initial ancestor, in a <Galton-Watson process>. It is infinite on survival. If the offspring mean is $m<1$, the <expected generation size in a Galton-Watson process> and the <monotone convergence theorem> give $\mathbb ET=1/(1-m)$.

= Finite-horizon total progeny generating function
{parent=Total progeny of a branching process}
{title2=$H_{n+1}(s)=sG(H_n(s)),\quad H_0(s)=1$}

Count the ancestor and the next $n-1$ generations of a <Galton-Watson process>. Conditioning on the ancestor's offspring, their independent descendant trees each have the preceding finite-horizon <probability generating function>, proving the displayed recursion. If the count instead excludes the ancestor, the recursion is $\widehat H_{n+1}(s)=G(s\widehat H_n(s))$ with $\widehat H_0=1$. The indexing convention must be specified.

= Geometric-offspring critical branching process
{parent=Galton-Watson process}
{title2=$G(s)=1/(2-s)$}

The offspring <probabilities> $P(K=j)=2^{-j-1}$ for $j\geq0$ have <expected value> one. For one initial individual, the <branching-process generating-function iteration> gives
$$
G_n(s)=\frac{n-(n-1)s}{n+1-ns},\quad P(X_n=0)=\frac n{n+1},\quad
P(X_n=j)=\frac1{(n+1)^2}\left(\frac n{n+1}\right)^{j-1}\ (j\geq1).
$$
For $n\geq1$ the <PGF> disk is $|s|<1+1/n$; $G_0(s)=s$ separately. Conditional on survival, the population is positive geometric with success <probability> $1/(n+1)$, leading to the <exponential limit of surviving geometric branching>.

= Exponential limit of surviving geometric branching
{parent=Geometric-offspring critical branching process}
{title2=$P(X_n/n>x\mid X_n>0)\to e^{-x}$}

For the stated critical geometric offspring law, the conditional upper tail is exactly $[n/(n+1)]^{\lfloor nx\rfloor}$ for $x\geq0$. Its logarithm tends to $-x$, proving an <exponential distribution> limit of rate one without a transform continuity theorem. The unconditional mass at zero tends to one, so survival conditioning changes the relevant limiting population scale.

= Branching-process generating-function iteration
{parent=Galton-Watson process}
{title2=$G_{n+1}=G\circ G_n,\quad G_0(s)=s$}

Conditioning on the initial individual's children gives independent copies of the population $n$ generations later. Their <PGFs> multiply, and averaging the product over the offspring number yields $G(G_n(s))$. Conditioning on the last generation gives $G_n(G(s))$ as well; both agree because $G_n$ is the $n$fold iterate of $G$.

= Mean and variance of a Galton-Watson generation
{parent=Galton-Watson process}
{title2=$\mathbb E[X_n]=\mu^n,\quad v_{n+1}=\mu^2v_n+\sigma^2\mu^n$}

= Branching-process generation moments
{synonym}

With one initial ancestor and independent offspring counts of finite mean and <variance>, conditioning on the current generation gives the displayed recurrences. For $\mu\ne1$ the <variance> is $\sigma^2\mu^{n-1}(\mu^n-1)/(\mu-1)$ for positive generation index. At $\mu=1$ it is $n\sigma^2$. At zero offspring mean the distribution is concentrated at zero and later generations vanish. These formulas are for the <Galton-Watson process>, not arbitrary dependent reproduction.

= Galton-Watson extinction fixed point
{parent=Galton-Watson process}
{c}
{title2=$q=\min\{s\in[0,1]:F(s)=s\}$}

For a <Galton-Watson process> with one ancestor and offspring <probability generating function> $F$, the <branching extinction probability> $q$ is the smallest <fixed point> of $F$ in $[0,1]$. A direct way to find it is to compute extinction by generation $n$:
$$
q_0=0,\qquad q_n=\mathbb P(Z_n=0),\qquad q_{n+1}=F(q_n),\qquad q_n\uparrow q.
$$
Zero is an <absorbing state>, so extinction by generation $n$ is exactly the <event> $Z_n=0$. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-445-introduction-to-stochastic-processes-spring-2015/27aaeb9eb9a9cecce93d9ad74413ddf5_MIT18_445S15_lecture19.pdf[Hao Wu, MIT 18.445, Lecture 19, slide 4]{ref}

For $F(s)=1/4+s/4+s^2/2$, the equation is
$$
F(s)-s=\frac12\left(s-\frac12\right)(s-1)=0.
$$
Both $1/2$ and $1$ are <fixed points>, but iteration from zero selects $q=1/2$. The first values are $q_1=1/4$, $q_2=11/32$, $q_3=809/2048\approx0.395020$, and $q_6\approx0.462186$. Iteration started at $1$ would stay at $1$ forever and would give the wrong extinction answer for this <offspring distribution>.

\Image[branching-extinction-fixed-point.png]
{title=The smallest extinction fixed point selected by iteration from zero}

The vertical steps in the figure apply the offspring <probability generating function>; horizontal steps return to the diagonal to start the next iteration. This gives a useful numerical calculation even when solving $F(s)=s$ explicitly is difficult. For the displayed law, every finite iteration is a lower bound for the eventual <branching extinction probability>; at generation six, about $0.037814$ additional extinction <probability> remains beyond the current empty families.

Why the smallest <fixed point>? The ancestor's children's descendant families are <independent>; given $k$ children, all are empty $n$ generations later with <probability> $q_n^k$. Averaging gives $q_{n+1}=\sum_kp_kq_n^k=F(q_n)$. The increasing extinction <events> give the <limit> $q$, and <continuity> of the <probability generating function> yields $F(q)=q$. For any other <fixed point> $r\in[0,1]$, $q_0\leq r$ and <monotonicity> of $F$ imply $q_n\leq r$ at every step; hence $q\leq r$. https://www.uni-muenster.de/Stochastik/lehre/WS1011/SpezielleStochastischeProzesse/Ch_1.pdf[University of Münster branching-process notes, Section 1.4, Theorem 1.7]{ref}

= Laziness invariance of branching extinction
{parent=Galton-Watson extinction fixed point}
{title2=$G_q(z)=qz+(1-q)G(z),\quad q<1$}

For a <Galton-Watson process>, replacing an individual's reproduction by a one-descendant uneventful step with probability $q<1$ changes its offspring <probability generating function> to the displayed mixture. Its fixed-point equation reduces to $G(z)=z$, so the smallest fixed point and hence the <extinction probability of a branching process> are unchanged. The mean is $q+(1-q)G'(1)$, which also preserves the subcritical/critical/supercritical classification. The excluded case $q=1$ never reproduces or dies and need not share the extinction probability.

= Extinction probability of a branching process
{parent=Galton-Watson extinction fixed point}
{title2=$q=\mathbb P(\exists j:Z_j=0)$}

= Branching extinction probability
{synonym}

For a <Galton-Watson process> with one ancestor, the <branching extinction probability> is the <probability> that some generation is empty:
$$
q=\mathbb P\left(\bigcup_{n\geq0}\{Z_n=0\}\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb P(Z_n=0).
$$
The equality uses the <absorbing state> at zero. The complementary <branching survival probability> $1-q$ is the <probability> that every generation is nonempty. The <Galton-Watson extinction fixed point> computes $q$ from the <offspring distribution>.

For $p_0=p_1=1/4$, $p_2=1/2$, one ancestor has $q=1/2$. Starting instead from $m$ ancestors with <independent> descendant families gives
$$
\mathbb P(\text{eventual extinction}\mid Z_0=m)=q^m,\qquad \mathbb P(Z_n=0\mid Z_0=m)=q_n^m.
$$
All $m$ families must die out. With three ancestors, eventual extinction has <probability> $1/8$ and survival has <probability> $7/8$; by generation two, extinction has <probability> $(11/32)^3=1331/32768\approx0.040619$. Extra ancestors change the extinction <probability> without changing the <offspring distribution>. https://www.uni-muenster.de/Stochastik/lehre/WS1011/SpezielleStochastischeProzesse/Ch_1.pdf[University of Münster branching-process notes, Lemma 1.2 and equation (1.10)]{ref}

For finite offspring <mean> $\mu$, the extinction classification is:
* If $\mu<1$, extinction has <probability> $1$. For example $p_0=3/4$, $p_2=1/4$ gives $\mu=1/2$ and $F(s)=3/4+s^2/4$; its only <fixed point> in $[0,1]$ is $1$.
* If $\mu=1$ and $p_1\ne1$, extinction also has <probability> $1$. For example $p_0=p_2=1/2$ gives $F(s)-s=(s-1)^2/2$ and $q=1$, despite <expected generation size in a Galton-Watson process> remaining one.
* If $1<\mu<\infty$, extinction has <probability> $q<1$. The value can be zero: if $p_0=0$, every individual has at least one child, so an initially nonempty family never becomes empty. In particular $p_2=1$ gives $Z_n=2^n$ and $q=0$.

The critical exception is $p_1=1$: every individual has exactly one child, $F(s)=s$, and $Z_n=1$ forever from one ancestor. Thus $\mu=1$ but $q=0$; a blanket claim that every critical <Galton-Watson process> becomes extinct would be false. These cases concern the <Galton-Watson process> with its common <offspring distribution> and <independence> assumptions. https://www.uni-muenster.de/Stochastik/lehre/WS1011/SpezielleStochastischeProzesse/Ch_1.pdf[University of Münster branching-process notes, Section 1.4, trivial cases and Theorem 1.7]{ref}

This <probability> answers whether any lineage persists, which the offspring <mean> alone cannot quantify in the supercritical case. A <branching process conditioned on extinction> uses the same <fixed point> to reweight reproduction and describe families known eventually to die out.

= Branching survival probability
{synonym}

For a <Galton-Watson process> started from one ancestor, this is the <probability> that every generation is nonempty. It is the complement of the <branching extinction probability>. The <binomial branching survival correction> distinguishes finite-binomial reproduction from the <Poisson branching process> near mean one.

= Expected generation size in a Galton-Watson process
{parent=Galton-Watson process}
{title2=$\mathbb E[Z_n]=\mu^n$}

For a <Galton-Watson process> starting with one ancestor and finite offspring <mean> $\mu$, the <expected value> of the generation size is $\mathbb E[Z_n]=\mu^n$. At $n=0$ it is one, including when $\mu=0$; zero offspring <mean> forces every later generation to be empty. With $m$ initial ancestors, the <expected value> is $m\mu^n$.

For $p_0=p_1=1/4$, $p_2=1/2$, the offspring <mean> is $5/4$. The exact calculations used in the generation <probability mass function> figure under <Galton-Watson process> give:

Nearly half the families are already empty at generation six, yet the <expected value> exceeds three. Since empty families contribute zero,
$$
\mathbb E[Z_n\mid Z_n>0]=\frac{\mu^n}{1-q_n}
$$
whenever $q_n<1$. At generation six this <conditional expectation> is about $7.092969$. Conditioning on being nonempty now differs from conditioning on eventual survival: some presently nonempty families will still die out. The eventual <branching extinction probability> is $1/2$, even as the unconditional <expected value> grows without bound.

The <expected value> is useful for computing average population counts and, in the subcritical case, the <expected value> of the <total progeny of a branching process>. Its simple formula comes from one local calculation: <linearity of expectation> and the <independent and identically distributed> offspring counts give
$$
\mathbb E[Z_{n+1}\mid Z_n]=\mu Z_n,\qquad \mathbb E[Z_{n+1}]=\mu\mathbb E[Z_n].
$$
The <tower property of conditional expectation> gives the second equality; starting from $\mathbb E[Z_0]=1$ gives the formula. It describes an average over the generation <probability distribution>, not deterministic growth of an individual family. https://www.uni-muenster.de/Stochastik/lehre/WS1011/SpezielleStochastischeProzesse/Ch_1.pdf[University of Münster branching-process notes, Section 1.3, Proposition 1.4]{ref}

= Quadratic Galton-Watson extinction bound
{parent=Galton-Watson process}

For offspring mean $\mu>1$ and variance $\sigma^2$, Taylor expansion at one gives
$$
d\leq1-\frac{2(\mu-1)}{\sigma^2+\mu^2-\mu}
$$
for the extinction probability $d$.
