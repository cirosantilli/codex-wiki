= Solution
{parent=a}

An initially occupied, isolated instantaneous <energy eigenstate> follows its changing eigenspace, acquiring a <dynamical phase> and generally a <Berry phase>. For smooth nondegenerate evolution, a useful local smallness condition is
$$
\boxed{\frac{\hbar|\langle m(t)|\dot H(t)|n(t)\rangle|}{|E_n(t)-E_m(t)|^2}\ll1\quad(m\ne n)}.
$$
A nonvanishing <spectral gap> and suitably smooth, slow driving throughout the evolution are needed; the local inequality alone is not a universal long-time error bound. Gap closure invalidates the simple nondegenerate approximation. A separated degenerate eigenspace may mix internally even when transitions out of it are suppressed.

= Solution

Set $\vartheta_\alpha(t)=\hbar^{-1}\int^t E_\alpha(s)ds$. Differentiating the expansion produces $\dot a_\beta|\varphi_\beta\rangle+a_\beta|\dot\varphi_\beta\rangle-iE_\beta a_\beta|\varphi_\beta\rangle/\hbar$ for each term. The <energy> term cancels $H|\Psi\rangle$ in the <Schrödinger equation>. Projecting the remainder with $\langle\varphi_\alpha|$ and using <orthonormality> yields
$$
\boxed{\dot a_\alpha=-\sum_\beta\langle\varphi_\alpha|\dot\varphi_\beta\rangle a_\beta e^{i(\vartheta_\alpha-\vartheta_\beta)}}.
$$
The diagonal term is purely imaginary and supplies the <geometric phase>. Off-diagonal <instantaneous eigenstate couplings> transfer population; differentiating the <eigenvalue> equation gives $\langle m|\dot n\rangle=\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle/(E_n-E_m)$ when the levels are distinct.

{parent=1}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Use normalized instantaneous modes $\varphi_p(x,t)=\sqrt{2/L}\sin(p\pi x/L)$ and expand $\Psi=\sum_p a_pe^{-i\vartheta_p}\varphi_p$. Differentiating at fixed $x$ gives
$$
\partial_t\varphi_p=-\frac{\dot L}{2L}\varphi_p-\sqrt{\frac2L}\frac{p\pi x\dot L}{L^2}\cos(p\pi x/L).
$$
For $n\ne p$, <integration> uses
$$
\int_0^1 u\sin(n\pi u)\cos(p\pi u)\,du
=-\frac{(-1)^{n+p}n}{\pi(n^2-p^2)},
$$
so the <moving-wall derivative coupling> is
$$
\langle\varphi_n|\dot\varphi_p\rangle
=\frac{\dot L}{L}(-1)^{n+p}\frac{2np}{n^2-p^2}.
$$
For $n=p$ the normalization <derivative> and the remaining <integral> cancel exactly, giving zero. The moving <integration> endpoint contributes no boundary term because every mode vanishes there. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\dot a_n=\frac{\dot L}{L}\sum_{p\ne n}(-1)^{n+p}\frac{2np}{p^2-n^2}\,a_p e^{i(\vartheta_n-\vartheta_p)}}.
$$
The real coupling <matrix> is antisymmetric, as required by conservation of total <probability>.

= Solution

For <first-order excitation in a linearly expanding infinite well>, retain only the ground-state amplitude on the right. For the second level, the coupling coefficient is $4\dot L/(3L)$. With $L=vt$,
$$
\frac{E_2-E_1}{\hbar}=\frac{\alpha}{t^2},\qquad
\alpha=\frac{3\pi^2\hbar}{2mv^2}.
$$
The relative <dynamical phase> is $-\alpha/t$ plus a constant. Up to an irrelevant phase,
$$
a_2(t)\simeq\frac43\int_0^t\frac{e^{-i\alpha/s}}{s}\,ds,
\qquad
\boxed{P_{1\to2}\simeq\frac{16}{9}\left|\int_0^t\frac{e^{-i\alpha/s}}s\,ds\right|^2}.
$$
The lower endpoint is an oscillatory improper limit: substituting $u=\alpha/s$ gives $\int_{\alpha/t}^\infty e^{-iu}du/u$. A physical initial state cannot occupy a zero-width well. Prepare the ground state at $t_0>0$, integrate from $t_0$, and then take the formal $t_0\downarrow0$ limit of the <probability>. For $t/\alpha\ll1$, endpoint asymptotics give $P\sim16t^2/(9\alpha^2)$; the first-order expression is reliable only while total excitation remains small.

The <quantum propagator> is the position-space kernel of the <time-evolution operator>: $\Psi(x,t)=\int K(x,t|x',t')\Psi(x',t')dx'$. For a time-independent <Quantum Hamiltonian>, $U=e^{-iH(t-t')/\hbar}$. Inserting a complete orthonormal <energy> eigenbasis gives
$$
\boxed{K(x,t|x',t')=\sum_\alpha\varphi_\alpha(x)\varphi_\alpha^*(x')e^{-iE_\alpha(t-t')/\hbar}}.
$$
A <continuous spectrum> requires the corresponding <integral>. At equal times, completeness makes this kernel a <Dirac delta function>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let $I=mR^2$, $T=t-t'>0$, and $\Delta=\theta-\theta'$. Periodic angular <wave functions> have modes $e^{il\theta}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ with integer $l$ and <energies> $\hbar^2l^2/(2I)$. Normalizing the kernel with respect to $d\theta'$ gives
$$
\boxed{K_\theta(\Delta,T)=\frac1{2\pi}\sum_{l\in\mathbb Z}e^{il\Delta-i\hbar l^2T/(2I)}}.
$$
If the <integration> measure is arc length $R\,d\theta'$, the corresponding kernel is $K_\theta/R$. This measure choice accounts for the otherwise ambiguous <radius> prefactor.

= c
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Lift the circle to its real-valued angular covering coordinate. The free classical equation is $\ddot\theta=0$. For each <winding number> $p\in\mathbb Z$, the endpoint lift is $\theta+2\pi p$, and
$$
\boxed{\theta_p(s)=\theta'+\frac{\Delta+2\pi p}{T}(s-t'),\qquad
S_p=\frac{I(\Delta+2\pi p)^2}{2T}}.
$$
Positive and negative windings are distinct <classical paths> with the same physical endpoints. Reducing the angle modulo $2\pi$ returns their trajectories on the ring.

= d
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

The angular <action> is $S=\int I\dot\theta^2ds/2$. In each winding sector, write $\theta=\theta_p+\xi$ with $\xi$ zero at both endpoints. The cross term vanishes and the Gaussian fluctuation <integral> is the free-particle prefactor with <mass> replaced by $I$. Thus
$$
\boxed{K_\theta(\Delta,T)=\sqrt{\frac{I}{2\pi i\hbar T}}\sum_{p\in\mathbb Z}\exp\left[\frac{iI(\Delta+2\pi p)^2}{2\hbar T}\right]}.
$$
Equivalently, multiply the line kernel for displacement $R(\Delta+2\pi p)$ by $R$ to change its measure from arc length to angle. The quadratic <action> makes the classical-path sum exact, rather than merely a <stationary phase> approximation. Real-time sums are understood with the usual negative imaginary part of $T$ and its boundary limit.

= e
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=e}

Take $\alpha=I/(i\hbar T)$ in the supplied Poisson summation identity, initially with a convergent regulator. Its left side is the winding sum. Multiplying its right side by the prefactor in part d gives
$$
\sqrt{\frac{I}{2\pi i\hbar T}}\sqrt{\frac1{2\pi\alpha}}=\frac1{2\pi},
\qquad
-\frac{q^2}{2\alpha}=-\frac{i\hbar Tq^2}{2I}.
$$
Consequently
$$
K_\theta=\frac1{2\pi}\sum_qe^{-iq\Delta-i\hbar q^2T/(2I)}.
$$
Relabelling $q=-l$ gives exactly the spectral answer. The square-root branch is fixed by continuation from positive imaginary-time damping; the limiting kernel has the required periodic delta-function normalization.

For an interaction of finite range $b$, higher <partial waves> are suppressed at $kb\ll1$ by the <centrifugal barrier>, away from exceptional higher-wave resonances. Outside the potential, the zero-energy radial <S-wave scattering> equation is $u''=0$, where $\psi=u/r$. Its solution is proportional to $r-a$, so normalization of the constant term gives
$$
\boxed{\psi\simeq1-\frac ar}.
$$
The <scattering length> $a$ is the extrapolated zero of $u(r)$. At nonzero small $k$, this form applies in the overlap region $b\ll r\ll k^{-1}$; it is not the far-field limit at fixed $k$. Equivalently, $k\cot\delta_0\to-1/a$ and $f_0\to-a$.

= b
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Put $d=|\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2|$, $g=e^{ikd}/d$ and $s_j=e^{i\mathbf k_i\cdot\mathbf r_j}$. In the range-to-wavelength overlap region around centre $j$, the outgoing wave's singular and constant terms are $c_j/(k\rho_j)+ic_j$. Matching their ratio to the <Bethe-Peierls boundary condition> yields
$$
(1+ika_1)c_1+a_1g c_2=-ka_1s_1,\qquad
(1+ika_2)c_2+a_2g c_1=-ka_2s_2.
$$
Define $A_j=1+ika_j$ and $D=A_1A_2-a_1a_2g^2$. Solving the coupled system gives
$$
\boxed{c_1=-ka_1\frac{A_2s_1-a_2gs_2}{D},\qquad
c_2=-ka_2\frac{A_1s_2-a_1gs_1}{D}}.
$$
This is the <two-centre scattering-length model> result, retaining the outgoing-wave $ik$ term and all repeated exchange between the two centres. If terms of order $ka_j$ are neglected in the local matching, set $A_1=A_2=1$ in these formulae; if also $kd\ll1$, set $g\simeq1/d$ and $s_j\simeq1$. Away from a vanishing denominator this gives $c_1\simeq-ka_1(1-a_2/d)/(1-a_1a_2/d^2)$ and its exchanged partner. The isolated-centre limit is $c_j=-ka_js_j/(1+ika_j)$. Finite-range corrections require additional effective-range data, not supplied here.

= Solution

At large $r$, $|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_j|=r-\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf r_j+O(r^{-1})$. Retain the displacement in the phase, but replace the denominator by $r$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{f(\hat{\mathbf r})=\frac1k\left[c_1e^{-ik\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf r_1}+c_2e^{-ik\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf r_2}\right]}.
$$
The <differential scattering cross-section> is $|f|^2$ for <elastic scattering> with this unit incident plane-wave normalization. <interference> depends on outgoing direction and centre separation as well as the incoming phases already contained in $c_j$.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Choose the polar axis along $\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2$. Then
$$
\int e^{-ik\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot(\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2)}d\Omega
=2\pi\int_{-1}^1e^{-ikdu}du=4\pi\frac{\sin(kd)}{kd}.
$$
Using the <angular interference integral for two point scatterers>, integrating the two self terms and the <interference> terms gives
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{\rm tot}=\frac{4\pi}{k^2}\left[|c_1|^2+|c_2|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(c_1c_2^*)\frac{\sin(kd)}{kd}\right]}.
$$
For $kd\ll1$, the amplitudes add coherently and $\sigma\to4\pi|c_1+c_2|^2/k^2$; when $kd\gg1$ the angle-integrated <interference> is suppressed. These limits hold for fixed incoming direction; no incoming-orientation average has been silently imposed.

With $\beta=(k_BT)^{-1}$, the normalized canonical <density operator> is
$$
\boxed{\rho=\frac{e^{-\beta H}}Z,\qquad Z=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H}}.
$$
Its <eigenvalues> are the Boltzmann probabilities and its trace is one.

= b
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <density operator> is diagonal in the oscillator number basis, so $\langle a^2\rangle=\langle a^{\dagger2}\rangle=0$. With $\bar n=\langle a^\dagger a\rangle$ and $aa^\dagger=a^\dagger a+1$,
$$
\langle x^2\rangle=\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}(2\bar n+1),\qquad
\langle p^2\rangle=\frac{m\hbar\omega}{2}(2\bar n+1).
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\left\langle\frac{p^2}{2m}\right\rangle
=\left\langle\frac{m\omega^2x^2}{2}\right\rangle
=\frac{\hbar\omega}{4}(2\bar n+1)}.
$$
Equality holds even at zero <temperature>, when each contribution is $\hbar\omega/4$. Classical equipartition alone would miss this <zero-point energy>.

{parent=4}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Set $q=e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}$. The oscillator populations are $(1-q)q^n$, and differentiating the <geometric series> gives $\bar n=q/(1-q)$. The <canonical oscillator coordinate variance> follows from $2\bar n+1=(1+q)/(1-q)=\coth(\beta\hbar\omega/2)$, and
$$
\boxed{\langle x^2\rangle_T=\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}\coth\left(\frac{\hbar\omega}{2k_BT}\right)}.
$$
It approaches $\hbar/(2m\omega)$ at zero <temperature> and $k_BT/(m\omega^2)$ at high <temperature>, agreeing respectively with ground-state fluctuations and classical equipartition.

{parent=4}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Write $T_p=p^2/(2m)$ and $V=m\omega^2x^2/2$. Dropping <commutators> of order $\beta^2$ permits $e^{-\beta H}\simeq e^{-\beta V}e^{-\beta T_p}$. With $\langle x|p\rangle=e^{ipx/\hbar}/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}$, the <momentum> Gaussian gives
$$
\langle x|e^{-\beta H}|x'\rangle\simeq
\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi\beta\hbar^2}}\exp\left[-\frac{m(x-x')^2}{2\beta\hbar^2}-\frac{\beta m\omega^2x^2}{2}\right].
$$
Integrating the diagonal yields $Z\simeq1/(\beta\hbar\omega)$, so the <leading high-temperature density kernel> is
$$
\boxed{\langle x|\rho|x'\rangle\simeq
\omega\sqrt{\frac{\beta m}{2\pi}}\exp\left[-\frac{m(x-x')^2}{2\beta\hbar^2}-\frac{\beta m\omega^2x^2}{2}\right]}.
$$
This unsymmetrized product has an apparent off-diagonal asymmetry at the omitted order; reversing the product puts $x'$ in the potential factor. A manifestly <Hermitian> approximation uses $e^{-\beta V/2}e^{-\beta T_p}e^{-\beta V/2}$, replacing $\beta m\omega^2x^2/2$ by $\beta m\omega^2(x^2+x'^2)/4$ with the same leading normalization. The condition is $\beta\hbar\omega\ll1$; the Gaussian off-diagonal width is of order $\hbar\sqrt{\beta/m}$.

<orthogonality> of angular modes makes the one-body term $\mathcal E a_1^\dagger a_1$, where
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E=\frac{\hbar^2}{2mR^2}}.
$$
The contact-interaction <integral> supplies $(U/4\pi)\delta_{l_1+l_2,l_3+l_4}$, expressing <angular momentum conservation>. Within modes zero and one, the four mixed permutations each reduce to $a_1^\dagger a_0^\dagger a_0a_1$. The two remaining terms have all four indices equal. Thus
$$
H=\mathcal E N_1+\frac U{4\pi}\left[N_0(N_0-1)+N_1(N_1-1)+4N_0N_1\right].
$$
This is the projected two-mode <Quantum Hamiltonian>; the full interaction could scatter into excluded modes, so the truncation is an assumed approximation rather than an exact invariant subspace of the complete ring problem.

= Solution

The occupation state is an <eigenstate> of the projected <Quantum Hamiltonian>. Since $a_j^\dagger a_j^\dagger a_ja_j=N_j(N_j-1)$,
$$
\boxed{E_{N_0,N_1}=\mathcal E N_1+\frac U{4\pi}\left[N_0(N_0-1)+N_1(N_1-1)+4N_0N_1\right]}.
$$
Equivalently, the bracket is $N(N-1)+2N_0N_1$, where $N=N_0+N_1$; there is no self-interaction for a single particle.

Put $C=\cos(\chi/2)$ and $S=\sin(\chi/2)$. Commutativity of the two <creation operators> and the <binomial theorem> give
$$
|\chi\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^N\sqrt{\binom Nj}\,C^{N-j}S^j|N-j,j\rangle.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{P(N_1=j)=\binom Nj(1-p)^{N-j}p^j,\quad p=S^2},
\qquad
\boxed{\overline N_1=Np,\quad\overline N_0=N(1-p)}.
$$
The state is a <fixed-number two-mode condensate> with binomial mode occupations; it is not a product of two independent Poisson-distributed Glauber <coherent states>.

Binomial <factorial moments> give $\langle N_1(N_1-1)\rangle=N(N-1)p^2$, its mode-zero counterpart $N(N-1)(1-p)^2$, and $\langle N_0N_1\rangle=N(N-1)p(1-p)$. Consequently the exact projected expectation is
$$
\frac{E(\chi)}N=\mathcal E p+\frac{U(N-1)}{4\pi}[1+2p(1-p)].
$$
Since $p(1-p)=\sin^2\chi/4$ and $n=N/(2\pi)$,
$$
\boxed{\frac{E(\chi)}N\simeq\mathcal E\sin^2(\chi/2)+\frac{nU}{2}\left[1+\frac12\sin^2\chi\right]}.
$$
The finite-$N$ correction is simply the replacement $N\mapsto N-1$ in the interaction term. Number correlations must be retained to obtain that correction.

With $J=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$ these matrices obey $M^\dagger JM=J$ and $\det M=1$, identifying the group as <SU(1,1)>. Products preserve the displayed conjugate-entry pattern and <determinant>; the identity belongs, and
$$
M^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha^*&-\beta\\-\beta^*&\alpha\end{pmatrix}
$$
also belongs. Multiplication and inversion are smooth. The real constraint $|\alpha|^2-|\beta|^2=1$ has nonzero gradient on its level set, so the matrices form a smooth real three-dimensional manifold. Associativity is inherited from <matrix multiplication>. These facts establish a <Lie group>, which is noncompact because $|\beta|$ is unbounded.

At first order write $\alpha=1+iz$ and $\beta=x+iy$ with real $x,y,z$. The constraint forces the real part of the change in $\alpha$ to vanish. Then
$$
m=\begin{pmatrix}iz&x+iy\\x-iy&-iz\end{pmatrix}
=2x\,m_1-2y\,m_2+2z\,m_3.
$$
Thus the three supplied generators span the <tangent space> over the reals. Multiplying their matrices gives
$$
\boxed{[m_1,m_2]=m_3,\qquad[m_2,m_3]=-m_1,\qquad[m_3,m_1]=-m_2}.
$$
For example, $m_1=\sigma_x/2$, $m_2=\sigma_y/2$, $m_3=i\sigma_z/2$ makes the noncompact signs explicit.

Set $P=a^\dagger b^\dagger$ and $Q=ab$. Independent <bosonic> <canonical commutation relations> give $[Q,P]=N_a+N_b+1=2K_3$, while $[K_3,P]=P$ and $[K_3,Q]=-Q$. Substituting $K_1=(P+Q)/2$ and $K_2=(P-Q)/(2i)$ yields
$$
[K_1,K_2]=-iK_3,\qquad[K_2,K_3]=iK_1,\qquad[K_3,K_1]=iK_2.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{[iK_1,iK_2]=iK_3,\quad[iK_2,iK_3]=-iK_1,\quad[iK_3,iK_1]=-iK_2},
$$
which matches the <matrix> <Lie algebra>. This <boson-pair realization of SU(1,1)> differs from the number-conserving <Schwinger boson representation> of $SU(2)$.

= Solution

The pair products are $PQ=N_aN_b$ and $QP=(N_a+1)(N_b+1)$. Hence
$$
K_1^2+K_2^2=\frac12(PQ+QP)
=N_aN_b+\frac12(N_a+N_b+1),
$$
whereas $K_3=(N_a+N_b+1)/2$. Subtraction gives
$$
\boxed{C=\frac{(N_a-N_b)^2-1}{4}}.
$$
The number difference commutes with pair creation, pair annihilation and $K_3$, since each pair operation changes both occupations equally. Its <boson-pair Casimir operator> $C$ therefore commutes with every generator. In a fixed-difference sector $N_a-N_b=d$, the Casimir <eigenvalue> is $(d^2-1)/4$, including $-1/4$ for equal occupations.
