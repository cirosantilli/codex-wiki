= Solution

Let $\Delta=\Omega_0-\omega$ and $R(t)=e^{-i\omega tS_z/\hbar}$. Differentiating $|\Psi\rangle=R|\Psi_R\rangle$ gives the rotating-frame <Hamiltonian operator> $R^\dagger HR-i\hbar R^\dagger\dot R$. The <angular momentum> <commutator> yields $R^\dagger S_\pm R=e^{\pm i\omega t}S_\pm$, while $-i\hbar R^\dagger\dot R=-\omega S_z$. Thus the <rotating-frame Rabi Hamiltonian> is
$$
\boxed{H_R=\Delta S_z+\Omega_RS_x
=\frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}\Delta&\Omega_R\\\Omega_R&-\Delta\end{pmatrix}},\qquad
i\hbar\partial_t|\Psi_R\rangle=H_R|\Psi_R\rangle.
$$
This transformation is exact for the circular drive; no <rotating-wave approximation> has been used.

Define $\Omega=\sqrt{\Delta^2+\Omega_R^2}$. Squaring the two-by-two matrix gives $H_R^2=\hbar^2\Omega^2 I/4$, so
$$
\boxed{E_\pm=\pm\frac{\hbar\Omega}{2},\qquad
|\Psi_\pm(t)\rangle=e^{\mp i\Omega t/2}e^{-i\omega tS_z/\hbar}|\pm\rangle_R}.
$$
Here $|\pm\rangle_R$ is the corresponding normalized <eigenstate> at $t=0$. In the rotating frame only its <quantum phase> changes. In the laboratory frame its <spin> direction also rotates about $z$ at <angular velocity> $\omega$: $\langle\mathbf S\rangle_\pm=\pm\hbar(\Omega_R\cos\omega t,\Omega_R\sin\omega t,\Delta)/(2\Omega)$. An arbitrary initial state is the same fixed linear combination of these two solutions. If $\Omega=0$, the rotating-frame <Hamiltonian operator> vanishes and the entire doublet is degenerate; evolution is just $R(t)$.

Take $\omega>0$ and $T=2\pi/\omega$. A full <spin one-half> rotation gives $R(T)=-I$, an essential sign. The <cyclic phase of a spin in a rotating field> has factors and total <quantum phases>
$$
\boxed{e^{i\Phi_\pm}=-e^{\mp i\pi\Omega/\omega},\qquad
\Phi_\pm=\pi\mp\frac{\pi\Omega}{\omega}\pmod{2\pi}}.
$$
To separate the adiabatic <dynamical phase>, set $B=\sqrt{\Omega_0^2+\Omega_R^2}>0$ and $\cos\theta=\Omega_0/B$. Then $\Omega=B-\omega\cos\theta+O(\omega^2/B)$. The instantaneous laboratory energies are $\pm\hbar B/2$, so the adiabatic <dynamical phases> are $\mp BT/2$. Subtracting them from $\Phi_\pm$ gives
$$
\boxed{\gamma_\pm=\mp\pi(1-\cos\theta)=\mp\frac{\mathcal A}{2}\pmod{2\pi},\qquad
\mathcal A=2\pi(1-\cos\theta)}.
$$
The oriented <solid angle> $\mathcal A$ is swept out by the field direction. These are the <Berry phases> for the <eigenstates> parallel and antiparallel to that direction. Retaining the <spinor> sign makes the <quantum phase> vanish modulo $2\pi$ when the field does not trace a nontrivial loop. The assumption $\omega\ll B$ suffices for adiabatic following; near degeneracy it must not be silently dropped.<Quantal phase factors accompanying adiabatic changes by Michael Victor Berry>[Section 4, spinning particles in slowly changing magnetic fields]{ref}

The <angular momentum> <commutators> used in the frame transformation are representation-independent. Therefore $H_R=\Delta S_z+\Omega_RS_x$ still holds. Rotating its axis onto $z$ gives $2s+1$ <eigenvalues> and exact laboratory solutions
$$
\boxed{E_\mu=\hbar\mu\Omega,\quad
|\Psi_\mu(t)\rangle=e^{-i\mu\Omega t}R(t)|\mu\rangle_R,\quad
\mu=-s,-s+1,\ldots,s}.
$$
Now $R(T)=(-1)^{2s}I$, so $e^{i\Phi_\mu}=(-1)^{2s}e^{-i\mu\Omega T}$. In the adiabatic limit the <dynamical phase> is $-\mu BT$ and
$$
\boxed{\gamma_\mu=-\mu\mathcal A\pmod{2\pi}}.
$$
Indeed $2\pi s+2\pi\mu\cos\theta$ and $-2\pi\mu(1-\cos\theta)$ differ by $2\pi(s+\mu)$, an integer multiple of $2\pi$. Integer <spins> return with a positive rotation sign and half-integer <spins> with a negative one; the <geometric phase> need not vanish for either.

Put $T=t-t'$. <momentum> <eigenstates> evolve with <energy> $p^2/(2m)$, so the retarded <momentum> kernel is $\theta(T)\delta(p-p')e^{-ip^2T/(2m\hbar)}$. Its <Fourier transform> reduces to
$$
K_0=\frac{\theta(T)}{2\pi\hbar}\int dp\,
\exp\left[\frac i\hbar\left(p(x-x')-\frac{p^2T}{2m}\right)\right].
$$
Completing the square and defining the oscillatory <Gaussian integral> by a convergent limiting prescription gives
$$
\boxed{K_0=\theta(T)\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar T}}
\exp\left[\frac{im(x-x')^2}{2\hbar T}\right]}.
$$
For $T>0$ the <square root> has <quantum phase> $e^{-i\pi/4}$. As $T\downarrow0$, this kernel tends to $\delta(x-x')$ in the distributional sense. Differentiating the <Heaviside step function> consequently supplies the required $i\hbar\delta(T)\delta(x-x')$ source; an extra $-i/\hbar$ factor would correspond to a different Green-function convention.

For the <momentum-space propagator in a uniform force>, the stated Fourier convention makes $x$ act on <momentum> <wavefunctions> as $i\hbar\partial_p$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\left[i\hbar\partial_t-\frac{p^2}{2m}-i\alpha\hbar\partial_p\right]K_p
=i\hbar\delta(T)\delta(p-p')}.
$$
For $T>0$, the characteristic has $p(s)=p'-\alpha(s-t')$. Along it the amplitude acquires <quantum phase> $-\int_{t'}^t p(s)^2ds/(2m\hbar)$. <momentum> is translated without a Jacobian, giving
$$
\boxed{K_p=\theta(T)\delta(p-p'+\alpha T)
\exp\left[\frac{i(p^3-p'^3)}{6\alpha m\hbar}\right]}.
$$
The cubic expression is evaluated on the delta-function support. There it equals $-i[p'^2T-\alpha p'T^2+\alpha^2T^3/3]/(2m\hbar)$, making both its sign and its continuous $\alpha\to0$ limit clear. The jump at $T=0$ is the identity <momentum> kernel.

Use the <Dirac delta function> to set $p'=p+\alpha T$, and write $X=x-x'$. The remaining momentum-dependent <quantum phase> is
$$
pX-\alpha Tx'-\frac{T}{2m}\left(p+\frac{\alpha T}{2}\right)^2
-\frac{\alpha^2T^3}{24m}.
$$
Shifting the integration variable by $\alpha T/2$ reduces the integral to the free <Gaussian integral>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{K_\alpha=\theta(T)\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar T}}
\exp\left\{\frac i\hbar\left[
\frac{mX^2}{2T}-\frac{\alpha T(x+x')}{2}-\frac{\alpha^2T^3}{24m}
\right]\right\}}.
$$
The <force> is $-\alpha$, not $+\alpha$. Both the linear endpoint term and the cubic-time term disappear in the zero-force limit. The prefactor remains the free one.

Measure intermediate time $u$ from $t'$. The <classical path> with the fixed endpoints is
$$
x_c(u)=x'+\frac{X}{T}u+\frac{\alpha}{2m}u(T-u),\qquad 0\leq u\leq T.
$$
It satisfies $m\ddot x_c=-\alpha$. Direct integration of $m\dot x_c^2/2-\alpha x_c$ gives
$$
\boxed{S_c=\frac{mX^2}{2T}-\frac{\alpha T(x+x')}{2}-\frac{\alpha^2T^3}{24m}}.
$$
For an arbitrary path write $x=x_c+\eta$ with $\eta(0)=\eta(T)=0$. Integrating the cross term by parts gives $-\int(m\ddot x_c+\alpha)\eta\,du=0$. Thus $S[x]=S_c+\int m\dot\eta^2du/2$ exactly. The fluctuation <path integral> is the free endpoint-fixed one, so its <determinant> supplies $\sqrt{m/(2\pi i\hbar T)}$. Multiplication by $\theta(T)e^{iS_c/\hbar}$ reproduces the previous kernel. This exact <classical-path factorization of a quadratic path integral> is possible because a linear potential introduces no additional quadratic fluctuation term.

For <elastic scattering> by a real short-range scalar potential, write $C=m/(2\pi\hbar^2)$, $\mathbf k_i=\mathbf k$ and $|\mathbf k_f|=k$. In the far field, $|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|=r-\widehat{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf r'+O(r^{-1})$ and $\mathbf k_f=k\widehat{\mathbf r}$. The outgoing spherical-wave coefficient is
$$
f(\mathbf k_f,\mathbf k_i)=-C\int d^3r'\,
e^{-i\mathbf k_f\cdot\mathbf r'}V(\mathbf r')\psi_{\mathbf k_i}(\mathbf r').
$$
Substitution of the incident <plane wave> inside this integral gives
$$
\boxed{f^{(1)}=-C\widetilde V(\mathbf q),\qquad
\frac{d\sigma^{(1)}}{d\Omega}=C^2|\widetilde V(\mathbf q)|^2},\qquad
\widetilde V(\mathbf q)=\int e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}V(\mathbf r)\,d^3r.
$$
The incoming and outgoing <flux> factors cancel because their speeds agree. Here $\mathbf q=\mathbf k_f-\mathbf k_i$ is a <wavevector> transfer; the actual <momentum transfer> is $\hbar\mathbf q$. This distinction preserves dimensions. The approximation requires the scattered correction to the interior wave to be small, and does not in general apply to an unscreened long-range potential.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Expanding the modulus squared yields an outgoing-angle factor $e^{-i\mathbf k_f\cdot\mathbf d}$ and an incoming factor $e^{i\mathbf k_i\cdot\mathbf d}$, where $\mathbf d=\mathbf r-\mathbf r'$. Choosing a polar axis along $\mathbf d$ gives
$$
\int d\Omega_f\,e^{-ik\widehat{\mathbf k}_f\cdot\mathbf d}
=2\pi\int_{-1}^1e^{-ikdu}\,du=4\pi\frac{\sin kd}{kd}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\sigma^{(1)}_{\rm total}=\frac{m^2}{\pi\hbar^4}
\int d^3r\,d^3r'\,V(\mathbf r)V(\mathbf r')
\frac{\sin kd}{kd}\,e^{i\mathbf k_i\cdot\mathbf d}}.
$$
The apparent $d=0$ singularity is removable: $\sin(kd)/(kd)\to1$. Exchanging $\mathbf r$ and $\mathbf r'$ cancels the imaginary part, while nonnegativity follows directly from its origin as an integral of $|f^{(1)}|^2$. A complex absorbing potential would require $V(\mathbf r')^*$ instead; the lossless <optical theorem> comparison below uses real $V$.

The first scattered correction to the incident wave is
$$
\psi^{(1)}(\mathbf r)=-C\int d^3r'\,
\frac{e^{ik|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}
V(\mathbf r')e^{i\mathbf k_i\cdot\mathbf r'}.
$$
Inserting it into the amplitude gives a second, positive contribution because the two minus signs multiply:
$$
\boxed{f^{(2)}=C^2\int d^3r\,d^3r'\,
e^{-i\mathbf k_f\cdot\mathbf r}V(\mathbf r)
\frac{e^{ikd}}dV(\mathbf r')e^{i\mathbf k_i\cdot\mathbf r'}}.
$$
Thus the amplitude through second Born order is $f^{(1)}+f^{(2)}+O(V^3)$. The intervening <outgoing Green function for the three-dimensional Helmholtz equation> describes propagation between the two interactions; replacing it by a real standing-wave kernel would discard the imaginary part needed for <unitarity>.

= Solution

In the forward direction $\mathbf k_f=\mathbf k_i$, the first Born amplitude $-C\int V$ is real. Symmetrize the second-order double integral under $\mathbf r\leftrightarrow\mathbf r'$ before taking its imaginary part. This replaces $e^{-i\mathbf k_i\cdot\mathbf d}$ by $\cos(\mathbf k_i\cdot\mathbf d)$, and gives
$$
\operatorname{Im}f^{(2)}(0)=C^2\int d^3r\,d^3r'\,
V(\mathbf r)V(\mathbf r')\frac{\sin kd}{d}
\cos(\mathbf k_i\cdot\mathbf d).
$$
The preceding total-cross-section integral equals the same real integral multiplied by $4\pi/k$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Im}[f^{(1)}(0)+f^{(2)}(0)]
=\frac{k\sigma^{(1)}_{\rm total}}{4\pi}+O(V^3)}.
$$
This is the <optical theorem at second Born order>. Comparing only the real first-order forward amplitude with a nonzero second-order <scattering cross-section> would mix perturbative orders. Terms of order $V^3$ require both the next amplitude order and interference in the <scattering cross-section>.

There is a state-assumption issue. For a <number-conserving Gaussian quantum state> with no anomalous contractions, put $\psi_i=\psi(\mathbf r_i)$, $n_i=\langle\psi_i^\dagger\psi_i\rangle$ and the <one-body density matrix> $g_{ij}=\langle\psi_i^\dagger\psi_j\rangle$. <Wick's theorem> pairs each creation operator with an annihilation operator. The direct pairing gives $n_1n_2$; the exchanged pairing gives $\epsilon g_{12}g_{21}$, with $\epsilon=+1$ for <bosons> and $-1$ for <fermions>. Hence
$$
\boxed{C_{12}=n_1n_2+\epsilon|g_{12}|^2}.
$$
<normal ordering> removes the equal-position delta-function self-counting term. Fermionic occupation-number product states are <Slater determinants> and obey this <Wick theorem>. Independent thermal bosonic modes also obey it, with $N_\alpha$ understood as mean occupations.

**A bosonic Fock product state does not generally obey the asserted identity.** The <bosonic number-state corrections to Gaussian density correlations> follow from $\langle a_\alpha^{\dagger2}a_\alpha^2\rangle=N_\alpha(N_\alpha-1)$ and give
$$
\boxed{C_{12}^{\rm Fock}=n_1n_2+|g_{12}|^2
-\sum_\alpha N_\alpha(N_\alpha+1)|\varphi_\alpha(\mathbf r_1)|^2
|\varphi_\alpha(\mathbf r_2)|^2}.
$$
For one occupied mode this is $N(N-1)|\varphi_1|^2|\varphi_2|^2$, whereas the uncorrected Gaussian expression gives $2N^2|\varphi_1|^2|\varphi_2|^2$. **This counterexample disproves the asserted identity for arbitrary bosonic number-product states.** The formulas below give both the Gaussian result, with its additional state assumption, and the exact bosonic number-state alternative. The ensemble distinction is also explicit in a later primary treatment of ideal-gas correlations.<Quantum Correlations of Ideal Bose and Fermi Gases in the Canonical Ensemble by Kazumasa Tsutsui and Takafumi Kita>[Abstract: canonical reduced density matrices versus grand-canonical Wick factorization; 2016]{ref}

= Solution

Under the Gaussian assumption, $g(\mathbf r,\mathbf r)=n(\mathbf r)$, so the coincident <normally ordered density correlation> is $(1+\epsilon)n(\mathbf r)^2$. For a homogeneous nonzero density $n$, the large-separation limit is $n^2$, giving
$$
\boxed{\text{bosons: }2,\qquad\text{fermions: }0}.
$$
These <coincident-point bunching factors> describe enhanced bosonic coincidence and excluded same-component fermionic coincidence. More generally the density-normalized ratio $C_{12}/(n_1n_2)$ tends to one at large separation. **The unnormalized ratio printed in the paper additionally needs matching nonzero local and remote densities**; $g_{12}\to0$ alone does not ensure this. For a bosonic Fock product state, the local normalized value instead is $2-\sum_\alpha N_\alpha(N_\alpha+1)|\varphi_\alpha(\mathbf r)|^4/n(\mathbf r)^2$, and even a large-separation factorization must be checked separately.

The <three-point density correlation of an ideal quantum gas> follows first for a <number-conserving Gaussian quantum state>. There are six pairings, one for each <permutation> of three annihilation operators. The identity gives the product of densities; each <transposition> has sign $\epsilon$; both three-cycles have positive sign. Thus
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
C^{(3)}_{123}={}&n_1n_2n_3
+\epsilon\left[n_1|g_{23}|^2+n_2|g_{13}|^2+n_3|g_{12}|^2\right]\\
&+g_{12}g_{23}g_{31}+g_{13}g_{32}g_{21}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The last two terms are <complex conjugates>. They equal $2\operatorname{Re}(g_{12}g_{23}g_{31})$, not an additional signed pair term. This is the <permanent of a matrix> $(g_{ij})$ for <bosons> and its <determinant> for <fermions>. <Wick's theorem> supplies the six terms only with the state assumption stated in part (a).

For completeness an exact bosonic Fock-state alternative can be given without that assumption. Let $\boldsymbol\ell$ be a mode-occupation decrement with $\sum_\alpha\ell_\alpha=3$. Define $A_{\boldsymbol\ell}(1,2,3)$ as the sum of $\prod_{j=1}^3\varphi_{\alpha_j}(\mathbf r_j)$ over all distinct ordered triples having these mode multiplicities. Acting three annihilation fields on the <number state> leaves mutually orthogonal states for different $\boldsymbol\ell$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{C^{(3),\rm Fock}_{123}=\sum_{\boldsymbol\ell:\,\sum\ell_\alpha=3}
\left[\prod_\alpha N_\alpha^{\underline{\ell_\alpha}}\right]
|A_{\boldsymbol\ell}(1,2,3)|^2}.
$$
Here $N^{\underline\ell}=N!/(N-\ell)!$, with zero when $\ell>N$. This expression includes repeated-mode occupation corrections absent from the Gaussian permanent; for a single mode it reduces to $N(N-1)(N-2)\prod_j|\varphi(\mathbf r_j)|^2$.

Use $[a_i,a_j^\dagger]=\delta_{ij}$ and define $E_{ij}=a_i^\dagger a_j$. A direct multiplication gives
$$
[E_{ij},E_{kl}]=\delta_{jk}E_{il}-\delta_{il}E_{kj}.
$$
With $S_+=\hbar a_\uparrow^\dagger a_\downarrow$, $S_-=\hbar a_\downarrow^\dagger a_\uparrow$, this implies $[S_+,S_-]=2\hbar S_z$ and $[S_z,S_\pm]=\pm\hbar S_\pm$. Resolving the raising and lowering combinations gives
$$
\boxed{[S_i,S_j]=i\hbar\varepsilon_{ijk}S_k}.
$$
These operators realize the <Schwinger boson representation> of <angular momentum>. Each commutes with $\widehat N=n_\uparrow+n_\downarrow$, so fixed total occupation is an invariant subspace.

The bosonic <commutator> is important in $S_+S_-=\hbar^2n_\uparrow(n_\downarrow+1)$ and $S_-S_+=\hbar^2n_\downarrow(n_\uparrow+1)$. Therefore

For example $N=0,1,2$ gives <spin> $0,\tfrac12,1$. The total <number operator> can be retained in the same identity before restricting to a sector.

**The literal printed difference is zero**, because the same spin-index product appears twice, and zero is invariant under every transformation. The intended <SU(2)-invariant antisymmetric spinor contraction> uses opposite indices in the second term. With $\varepsilon=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$, define $I=\phi^T\varepsilon\chi$. For any two-by-two matrix,
$$
U^T\varepsilon U=(\det U)\varepsilon.
$$
Thus for $U\in SU(2)$,
$$
\boxed{I'=(U\phi)^T\varepsilon(U\chi)=I}.
$$
Expanding the <determinant> identity proves the result without complex conjugating either <spinor>. This is the invariant alternating tensor behind the <spin singlet state>. If the two <spinors> are identical, the contraction vanishes. A constant electrostatic <energy> offset is removed locally by the <quantum phase> substitution $\psi=e^{-iV_0t/\hbar}\psi_0$, since $(i\hbar\partial_t-V_0)\psi=e^{-iV_0t/\hbar}i\hbar\partial_t\psi_0$. Therefore the two <frequency> branches become
$$
\boxed{\psi=\sum_k\frac1{\sqrt{2\omega_k}}\left[
a_ke^{ikx-i(\omega_k+V_0/\hbar)t}
+b_k^\dagger e^{-ikx+i(\omega_k-V_0/\hbar)t}\right]}.
$$
The normalization uses the kinetic <frequency> $\omega_k$, while the canonical <energy> <eigenvalues> are $E=V_0\pm\hbar\omega_k$. The opposite-frequency branch is not obtained by replacing $\omega_k$ by the same shifted positive <frequency> in both terms. A globally constant offset produces no <electric field>, whereas different offsets on opposite sides of a step cannot be removed by a single smooth <global phase>.

= Solution

Assume a positive-energy incoming particle, $E>mc^2$, and a nonzero upward step $V_0>0$. Set $M=mc^2$ and $k=\sqrt{E^2-M^2}/(\hbar c)>0$. With the common time factor $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ removed, take $\phi_<(x)=e^{ikx}+r e^{-ikx}$ and $\phi_>(x)=t e^{iqx}$. The right-hand wavenumber satisfies $q^2=[(E-V_0)^2-M^2]/(\hbar c)^2$; its physical branch is specified in part (c). Integrating the stationary equation across the finite step requires continuity of $\phi$ and $\phi'$, giving $1+r=t$ and $k(1-r)=qt$. Thus
$$
\boxed{r=\frac{k-q}{k+q},\qquad t=\frac{2k}{k+q}}.
$$
These are wave amplitudes, not <flux> probabilities. The <Noether current of scalar electrodynamics>, in a convenient common normalization for a stationary mode, is $\rho_Q=(E-V)|\phi|^2/(mc^2)$ and $j_Q=(\hbar/m)\operatorname{Im}(\phi^*\partial_x\phi)$. For a real $q$, its charge-current ratios are $\mathcal R=|r|^2$ and $\mathcal T=(q/k)|t|^2$, with $\mathcal R+\mathcal T=1$. The same statement uses $\operatorname{Re}q$ for a decaying solution. The <electrostatic potential> couples through the time derivative; it is not a Lorentz-scalar <mass> shift. A zero step is recovered separately with $q=k$, $r=0$ and $t=1$.

Let $z=E-V_0+i0$. The <outgoing analytic branch of Klein-Gordon step momentum> is
$$
\boxed{q(z)=\frac{\sqrt{z-M}\sqrt{z+M}}{\hbar c}},
$$
where each <square root> is continued from the <upper half-plane>. It behaves as $z/(\hbar c)$ at large positive or negative real $z$. The <branch points> are $E=V_0\pm M$; replacing this product by a principal <square root> of $z^2-M^2$ can choose the wrong sign below the lower threshold.

**I.** For $E-V_0>M$, $q>0$ and both transmitted <kinetic energy> and <group velocity> are positive. Thus $0\leq\mathcal R<1$ and $\mathcal T=4kq/(k+q)^2>0$; the transmission decreases to zero on approaching the upper threshold from above.

**II.** For $|E-V_0|<M$, write $q=i\kappa$ with $\kappa=\sqrt{M^2-(E-V_0)^2}/(\hbar c)>0$. The right wave decays, $r=(k-i\kappa)/(k+i\kappa)$ has modulus one, and $\mathcal T=0$. There is an evanescent tail but no <flux> transmitted to infinity. Its reflection <quantum phase> changes continuously between the two thresholds.

**III.** For $E-V_0<-M$, the <analytic continuation> gives $q=-Q$, where $Q=\sqrt{(E-V_0)^2-M^2}/(\hbar c)>0$. The <group velocity> $c^2\hbar q/(E-V_0)$ is again rightward, while the charge density and transmitted charge current are negative. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathcal R=\frac{(k+Q)^2}{(k-Q)^2}>1,\qquad
\mathcal T=-\frac{4kQ}{(k-Q)^2}<0,\qquad
\mathcal R+\mathcal T=1}.
$$
Choosing $q=+Q$ would describe a wavepacket arriving from the right, inconsistent with the chosen incoming channel. As the lower threshold is approached, $q\to0^-$ and $\mathcal R\to1$ from above. This <Klein-Gordon step amplification> has a real denominator zero at $E=V_0/2$ when $V_0>2M$: there $Q=k$, and no finite stationary solution with unit incoming amplitude satisfies both matching equations. The resulting formal pole must not be described as an infinite single-particle <probability>. Finite switching, wavepackets and the treatment of the external source require a multiparticle analysis.<Klein Paradox for Spin-0 Particles by Douglas M. The negative-kinetic-energy branch is interpreted as an <antiparticle> channel. In the overlap $mc^2<E<V_0-mc^2$, particles can escape to the left and oppositely charged <antiparticles> to the right. This interval exists only when $V_0>2mc^2$. The external electrostatic source supplies the <energy> for pair creation; the reflected particle <flux> can exceed the incident one because the outgoing state need not contain only the incident particle.

**The conserved quantity is signed charge current, not a positive one-particle <probability> in both regions.** Negative $\mathcal T$ denotes rightward transport of opposite charge, so it is consistent with $\mathcal R>1$ and $\mathcal R+\mathcal T=1$. A quantized charged field with particle and <antiparticle> creation operators gives the appropriate interpretation of this <Klein paradox>.
