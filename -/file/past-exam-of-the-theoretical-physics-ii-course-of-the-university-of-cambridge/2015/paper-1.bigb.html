= Solution
{parent=a}

The <spin> operators here are <dimensionless>: $S^j=\sigma_j/2$, so $\Omega$ has units of inverse <time> and $\Phi$ is a <dimensionless> pulse area. Integrating the Schrödinger evolution across an infinitesimally short pulse gives $U_\Phi=e^{-i\Phi S^x}$. The finite free <Hamiltonian> contributes a vanishing integrated phase within that pulse. Between pulses the free evolution is $U_\Omega=e^{-i\Omega TS^z}$.

In the $S^z$ eigenbasis, $S^z=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)/2$ and $(\sigma_x)^2=I$. Consequently
$$
U_\Omega=\operatorname{diag}(e^{-i\Omega T/2},e^{i\Omega T/2}),\qquad
U_\Phi=I\cos(\Phi/2)-i\sigma_x\sin(\Phi/2).
$$
Starting just before a pulse, the kick acts first and the free interval second. Operators act on states right to left, hence
$$
\boxed{U(NT-0^+,-0^+)=(U_\Omega U_\Phi)^N.}
$$
There are exactly $N$ kicks, at <times> $0,T,\ldots,(N-1)T$; the pulse at $NT$ is excluded. Sampling just after kicks would change the displayed product order. This endpoint convention specifies the <delta-kicked spin Floquet operator>.

= Solution

The <Pauli matrix multiplication law> gives
$$
(\mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)(\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)
=(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)I+i(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma.
$$
For a <unit vector> $\hat{\mathbf n}$, it follows that $(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)^2=I$. In the exponential series the even powers therefore multiply $I$ and the odd powers multiply $\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$. Summing them separately,
$$
\boxed{e^{-i\theta\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf S}
=I\cos(\theta/2)-i\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\sin(\theta/2).}
$$
Equivalently, the <eigenvalues> of $\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ are $\pm1$, so its two <spectral projectors> acquire phases $e^{\mp i\theta/2}$. The half-angle belongs to a <spinor> <rotation>, while the associated physical <spin> <vector> rotates through $\theta$.

= Solution
{parent=c}

For the <axis-angle composition of orthogonal spin rotations>, set $A=\Omega T/2$ and $B=\Phi/2$. Multiplication, including $\sigma_z\sigma_x=i\sigma_y$, gives
$$
U_\Omega U_\Phi=C I-i\mathbf v\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma,
\quad C=\cos A\cos B,
\quad\mathbf v=(\cos A\sin B,\ \sin A\sin B,\ \sin A\cos B).
$$
One checks that $C^2+|\mathbf v|^2=1$. For $s=|\mathbf v|\ne0$, choose
$$
\boxed{\vartheta=2\operatorname{atan2}(s,C),\qquad
\hat{\mathbf n}=\frac1s(\cos A\sin B,\ \sin A\sin B,\ \sin A\cos B).}
$$
This choice retains the full <spinor> sign and gives $U_\Omega U_\Phi=e^{-i\vartheta\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf S}$. Repeated periods have the same axis:
$$
\boxed{U(NT-0^+,-0^+)=e^{-iN\vartheta\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf S}.}
$$
Thus the <spin> expectation <vector> rotates through $N\vartheta$, interpreted modulo $2\pi$ as a spatial <rotation>. The positive $y$ component comes from the stated ordering; reversing the two <rotations> changes that component's sign.

When $s=0$, the one-period operator is $+I$ or $-I$, and no unique <rotation> axis exists. The latter is a $2\pi$ spatial <rotation> with a minus sign on a spin-$1/2$ state. It is safer to retain this central group element than to replace the angle by zero in a <spinor> calculation.

= Solution

For general <spin> $S$, use <dimensionless> generators $S^j$ obeying $[S^i,S^j]=i\epsilon_{ijk}S^k$. The two exponentials represent the same group elements as for <spin> $1/2$, now in the $(2S+1)$-dimensional <representation> of $SU(2)$. A <representation> preserves products, so
$$
\boxed{e^{-i\Omega TS^z}e^{-i\Phi S^x}
=e^{-i\vartheta\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf S}}
$$
with exactly the same axis and angle found in (c); after $N$ periods the angle is $N\vartheta$. One must not substitute higher-spin <matrices> into the two-term Pauli exponential in (b), which used a property special to <spin> $1/2$.

The dimension and <action> on <spin> states change, but the <spin-independent axis-angle composition> does not. In particular,
$$
\boxed{e^{-i2\pi\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf S}=(-1)^{2S}I.}
$$
Integer-spin states return with positive sign and half-integer-spin states with negative sign. <spin> expectation <vectors> transform as spatial <vectors> in either case, while a general higher-spin state also carries higher multipole information.

= Solution

Write $\tau=t-t'$, $y=x-x'$ and $G(y,\tau)=(4\pi D\tau)^{-1/2}e^{-y^2/(4D\tau)}$ for $\tau>0$, with $D>0$. Direct differentiation gives
$$
\partial_\tau G=\left(-\frac1{2\tau}+\frac{y^2}{4D\tau^2}\right)G
=D\partial_y^2G.
$$
Also $\int_{\mathbb R}G\,dy=1$ and, for a smooth bounded <test function> $f$,
$$
\int_{\mathbb R}G(y,\tau)f(y)\,dy
=\frac1{\sqrt\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-u^2}f(\sqrt{4D\tau}\,u)\,du
\longrightarrow f(0).
$$
Thus $G(\cdot,\tau)$ tends to a <Dirac delta distribution> as $\tau\downarrow0$. Defining the retarded kernel $K=\Theta(\tau)G$ supplies the initial impulse:
$$
\boxed{(\partial_t-D\partial_x^2)K
=\delta(t-t')\delta(x-x'),\qquad K=0\text{ for }t<t'.}
$$
The source term arises from the jump at $\tau=0$ in the distributional sense; differentiating only the smooth positive-time Gaussian would miss it.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Take $\tau=t_f-t_i>0$ and split it into $M$ intervals of width $\Delta t=\tau/M$. The <markov property> and Gaussian <convolution> give
$$
K=\int_{\mathbb R^{M-1}}\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}dx_j
\prod_{j=0}^{M-1}\frac{\exp[-(x_{j+1}-x_j)^2/(4D\Delta t)]}
{\sqrt{4\pi D\Delta t}},
\qquad x_0=x_i,\ x_M=x_f.
$$
This finite-dimensional identity is exact for every positive $M$. Define the path-integral measure by these normalized <time> slices:
$$
\int\mathcal D x\,(\cdots)
=\lim_{M\to\infty}(4\pi D\Delta t)^{-M/2}
\int\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}dx_j\,(\cdots).
$$
In the <time-sliced diffusion path integral>, the discrete exponent is $\sum_j[(x_{j+1}-x_j)/\Delta t]^2\Delta t/(4D)$. Its conventional continuum notation is therefore
$$
\boxed{K(x_f,t_f|x_i,t_i)
=\int_{x_i}^{x_f}\mathcal D x\,
\exp\!\left[-\int_{t_i}^{t_f}\frac{\dot x^2}{4D}\,dt\right].}
$$
The time-slice normalization is essential, not an arbitrary multiplicative constant. Brownian paths are almost surely nondifferentiable, so the displayed <action> is shorthand for the Gaussian limiting measure rather than an ordinary integral of the derivative of each typical path.

{parent=2}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Multiply each time-sliced path weight by $\exp[-\sum_jV(x_j)\Delta t]$. A nonnegative finite $V$ is a local killing rate: the Feynman-Kac weight attenuates paths in proportion to the <time> spent in regions where $V>0$. Let that rate tend to infinity outside the allowed interval. Paths that exit are removed, leaving <Brownian motion> killed at its first boundary hit.

Consequently the required kernel is the Gaussian path measure restricted to surviving paths, equivalently
$$
\boxed{K_{|x|<L}
=\int_{x_i}^{x_f}\mathcal D x\,
e^{-\int[\dot x^2/(4D)+V(x)]dt},}
$$
with the infinite-barrier interpretation stated above. It solves $\partial_tK=D\partial_x^2K$ in the interior with absorbing <Dirichlet boundary conditions> at $\pm L$. A <reflecting boundary condition for a diffusion> would describe a different process.

Strictly, this is a <transition probability> density in the final <position>, jointly with survival; a <probability> for an interval of final <positions> includes an integral over $x_f$. Its integral over the whole allowed interval is at most one. It is not the normalized endpoint density conditional on survival, which would require division by the <survival probability>. In these <diffusion> units $V$ and the <eigenvalues> of $H$ have units of inverse <time>.

= Solution

The normalized Dirichlet <eigenfunctions> on $(-L,L)$ and their decay rates are
$$
\varphi_n(x)=\frac1{\sqrt L}\sin\frac{n\pi(x+L)}{2L},\qquad
E_n=D\left(\frac{n\pi}{2L}\right)^2,\quad n=1,2,\ldots.
$$
Their norms are one because the sine-squared integral is $L$. At the initial <position> zero, $\varphi_n(0)=L^{-1/2}\sin(n\pi/2)$, so all even $n$ disappear. For $n=2j+1$, the two signs from $\varphi_n(x)$ and $\varphi_n(0)$ cancel, yielding
$$
\boxed{K_{|x|<L}(x,t|0,0)=\frac{\Theta(t)}L
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}
\cos\frac{(2j+1)\pi x}{2L}
\exp\!\left[-\frac{(2j+1)^2\pi^2Dt}{4L^2}\right].}
$$
This holds for $|x|<L$ and is zero outside. The kernel is even in $x$, vanishes at both boundaries and tends weakly to the initial delta. The <eigenvalues> are decay rates rather than quantum energies unless one makes an additional unit convention.

= Solution
{parent=e}

The <survival probability from an absorbing transition density> follows by integrating over final <position>. Since
$$
\int_{-L}^{L}\cos\frac{(2j+1)\pi x}{2L}\,dx
=\frac{4L(-1)^j}{(2j+1)\pi},
$$
the exact <survival probability> for $t>0$ is
$$
S(t)=\frac4\pi\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^j}{2j+1}
e^{-(2j+1)^2u},\qquad u=\frac{\pi^2Dt}{4L^2}.
$$
The slowest Dirichlet mode dominates at large <time>:
$$
\boxed{S(t)\sim\frac4\pi\exp\!\left(-\frac{\pi^2Dt}{4L^2}\right).}
$$
The first relative correction is $-\tfrac13e^{-8u}$. The approximation is a late-time result; extrapolating its prefactor to $t=0$ would incorrectly give a <probability> larger than one. The full alternating series tends to one there.

Outside a finite-range <scattering potential>, the stationary <Schrödinger equation> becomes the free <Helmholtz equation>. The specified incident state is a <plane wave> along $+z$. The scattered disturbance must be outgoing: the scattering experiment has an incoming incident beam but no independently imposed spherical wave arriving from infinity. At large radius its angular pattern is encoded by a <scattering amplitude> $f_k(\theta,\phi)$:
$$
\boxed{\psi_k(\mathbf r)\sim e^{ikz}+f_k(\theta,\phi)\frac{e^{ikr}}r.}
$$
An outgoing three-dimensional wave has amplitude proportional to $1/r$ because its <probability current> spreads over area proportional to $r^2$. Indeed its radial current is $(\hbar k/m)|f_k|^2/r^2$ to leading order, while the incident current density is $\hbar k/m$. Therefore $d\sigma/d\Omega=|f_k|^2$. The sign $e^{+ikr}$ assumes the stationary <time> factor $e^{-iE_kt/\hbar}$.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Rearrange the continuum equation as
$$
(\nabla^2+k^2)\psi_k=\frac{2mg}{\hbar^2}d_k\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf r).
$$
The incident <plane wave> contributes zero to the left side. The outgoing <Green function> identity gives $-4\pi f_k\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf r)$ for the scattered term, hence
$$
\boxed{f_k=-\frac{mg}{2\pi\hbar^2}\,d_k.}
$$
This fixes both the sign and the three-dimensional $4\pi$ factor. The model is an isotropic point coupling, so the scattered amplitude has no angular dependence. Take $g$ real, as required by the displayed Hermitian pair of coupling equations; an arbitrary phase of the discrete state can be chosen accordingly.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The outgoing wave is singular at the origin, so its value there cannot be inserted without <regularization>. At a short radial distance $\delta>0$, with $k\delta\ll1$,
$$
\psi_k(\delta)=1+\frac{f_ke^{ik\delta}}{\delta}+O(k\delta)
=1+f_k\left(\frac1\delta+ik\right)
+O(k\delta)+O(f_kk^2\delta).
$$
The incident factor tends to one independently of the chosen approach direction; the singular part is isotropic. The discrete-state equation becomes, to the retained short-distance order,
$$
\boxed{(E_k-\epsilon_0)d_k
=g\left[1+f_k\left(\frac1\delta+ik\right)\right].}
$$
Keeping only $1/\delta$ and discarding $ik$ would lose the outgoing-wave imaginary part and produce an amplitude inconsistent with scattering <unitarity>.

Define $A=mg^2/(2\pi\hbar^2)$, which has units of <energy> <times> <length>. Substituting the result of (b) into (c) gives
$$
\left(E_k-\epsilon_0+\frac A\delta+iAk\right)d_k=g.
$$
Absorb the real cutoff shift into the physical detuning,
$$
\widetilde\epsilon_0=\epsilon_0-\frac A\delta,\qquad
d_k=\frac g{E_k-\widetilde\epsilon_0+iAk}.
$$
It follows that
$$
f_k=-\frac A{E_k-\widetilde\epsilon_0+iAk}.
$$
Using $k=\sqrt{2mE_k}/\hbar$, identify
$$
\boxed{\gamma=\frac{\sqrt{2m}}\hbar A
=\frac{m^{3/2}g^2}{\sqrt2\,\pi\hbar^3},\qquad
f_k=-\frac{\hbar\gamma}{\sqrt{2m}}\,
\frac1{E_k-\widetilde\epsilon_0+i\gamma\sqrt{E_k}}.}
$$
Here $\gamma$ has units of square root of <energy>. This is the small-cutoff, renormalized resonant-level amplitude; terms vanishing with $k\delta$ have been omitted. Taking $\delta\to0$ at fixed measured detuning requires the bare $\epsilon_0$ to depend on the cutoff.

The sign is checked by $f_k^{-1}=-(E_k-\widetilde\epsilon_0)/A-ik$, which has the elastic $s$-wave <unitarity> form. Thus $\operatorname{Im}f_k=k|f_k|^2$, consistent with the <optical theorem> and $\sigma_{\rm tot}=4\pi|f_k|^2$. If the renormalized level is positive, at $E_k=\widetilde\epsilon_0$ the amplitude is $i/k$ and the <scattering cross-section> reaches $4\pi/k^2$.

For the canonical <quantum Gibbs state>,
$$
\boxed{\rho=\frac{e^{-\beta H}}Z,\qquad
Z=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H},\qquad
\beta=\frac1{k_BT}.}
$$
If $H|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle$, its spectral form is $\rho=Z^{-1}\sum_ne^{-\beta E_n}|n\rangle\langle n|$, with a sum over any degeneracy and an integral where a continuous spectrum needs one. It is Hermitian, positive and has unit <trace>, and it commutes with $H$. The formula presumes a well-defined finite <partition function>. At zero <temperature> a nondegenerate stable system approaches its ground-state projector; a degenerate ground space requires specifying the ensemble or preparation.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Put $g=k\hbar/(2m\omega)$, which has units of <energy>. In the original oscillator basis,
$$
H=\hbar\omega(a^\dagger a+b^\dagger b+1)
+g(ab+a^\dagger b^\dagger+a^\dagger b+ab^\dagger).
$$
Write $A=\widetilde a$, $B=\widetilde b$, $C_2=\cosh2\theta$ and $S_2=\sinh2\theta$. The transformed number and pair terms obey
$$
a^\dagger a+b^\dagger b+1
=C_2(A^\dagger A+B^\dagger B+1)-S_2(AB+A^\dagger B^\dagger),
$$
$$
ab+a^\dagger b^\dagger
=C_2(AB+A^\dagger B^\dagger)-S_2(A^\dagger A+B^\dagger B+1).
$$
Hence the coefficient of $AB+A^\dagger B^\dagger$ is $gC_2-\hbar\omega S_2$, which vanishes for
$$
\boxed{\tanh2\theta=\frac g{\hbar\omega}=\frac{k}{2m\omega^2},
\qquad\theta=\frac12\operatorname{artanh}\frac{k}{2m\omega^2}.}
$$

**This cancels the requested pair terms but does not diagonalize the printed <Hamiltonian>.** Its number-conserving coupling also transforms:
$$
a^\dagger b+ab^\dagger
=C_2(A^\dagger B+AB^\dagger)
-\frac{S_2}{2}(A^2+B^2+A^{\dagger2}+B^{\dagger2}).
$$
After the pair cancellation, the last creation terms have coefficient $-gS_2/2$, nonzero for $k\ne0$. They create two quanta from the $A,B$ vacuum, so that vacuum is not an <eigenstate> of the literal <Hamiltonian>. This is an inconsistency in the ground-state assertion used by the next part, present in the printed PDF.

For the actual coordinate-coupled system, the <normal coordinates> $q_\pm=(x\pm y)/\sqrt2$ and their conjugate momenta diagonalize $H$ into independent oscillators with
$$
\boxed{\omega_\pm=\sqrt{\omega^2\pm k/m}.}
$$
A normalizable <ground state> requires $|k|<m\omega^2$. Eliminating just the two indicated cross-pair terms is not sufficient to establish that <ground state>.

The stated annihilation conditions can be solved algebraically, even though (b) shows they are not ground-state conditions for the full printed <Hamiltonian>. Invert the <canonical transformation>:
$$
A=a\cosh\theta+b^\dagger\sinh\theta,\qquad
B=b\cosh\theta+a^\dagger\sinh\theta.
$$
For $|\psi_\lambda\rangle=\mathcal N e^{\lambda a^\dagger b^\dagger}|0,0\rangle$, the <commutators> give
$$
a|\psi_\lambda\rangle=\lambda b^\dagger|\psi_\lambda\rangle,\qquad
b|\psi_\lambda\rangle=\lambda a^\dagger|\psi_\lambda\rangle.
$$
Thus both $A$ and $B$ annihilate it precisely when
$$
\boxed{\lambda=-\tanh\theta.}
$$
The normalized <two-mode squeezed vacuum> is
$$
|\psi_\lambda\rangle=\sqrt{1-|\lambda|^2}
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^n|n\rangle_x|n\rangle_y,
\qquad |\lambda|<1.
$$
The factorial from the exponential cancels those generated by the two <creation operators>. Normalization follows from a <geometric series>; the problem does not require its value, but it makes the subsequent <partial trace> explicit.

For comparison, the actual ground wave function of the printed stable <Hamiltonian> is
$$
\psi_{\rm true}(x,y)=
\left(\frac{m^2\omega_+\omega_-}{\pi^2\hbar^2}\right)^{1/4}
\exp\!\left[-\frac m{4\hbar}
\left((\omega_++\omega_-)(x^2+y^2)+2(\omega_+-\omega_-)xy\right)\right].
$$
It follows directly from the two normal-mode Gaussian vacua. The proposed $|\psi_\lambda\rangle$ is instead the exact <ground state> of the distinct pair-coupling <Hamiltonian> $\hbar\omega(a^\dagger a+b^\dagger b+1)+g(ab+a^\dagger b^\dagger)$ under its stability condition. Omitting the remaining terms of $kxy$ silently would change the problem.

First <trace> the state explicitly specified in (c). <orthogonality> in the $y$ oscillator removes terms with different occupation numbers:
$$
\boxed{\rho_x=(1-q)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^n|n\rangle_x\langle n|,
\qquad q=|\lambda|^2=\tanh^2\theta.}
$$
For a thermal oscillator of <frequency> $\omega$, the ratio of successive <Boltzmann weights> is $e^{-\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}$. Consequently the conditional answer for that two-mode squeezed state is
$$
\boxed{T_{\rm sq}=
\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_B\ln(1/\tanh^2\theta)}.}
$$
At $\theta=0$ this is interpreted by the limit $T_{\rm sq}=0$. The reduced state is mixed for nonzero squeezing although the joint state is pure; its effective thermal statistics arise from entanglement, not from heating by a bath.

The ground-state identification must still be qualified. For the true coordinate-coupled <ground state>, direct normal-mode averages give
$$
\langle x^2\rangle=\frac{\hbar}{4m}\left(\frac1{\omega_+}+\frac1{\omega_-}\right),
\quad
\langle p_x^2\rangle=\frac{\hbar m}{4}(\omega_++\omega_-),
\quad
\langle xp_x+p_xx\rangle=0.
$$
Their ratio is $m^2\omega_+\omega_-$, rather than $m^2\omega^2$. Therefore this reduced state is not thermal with respect to the original bare <frequency> $\omega$ for $k\ne0$. It does have an exact thermal <representation> for an effective oscillator of <mass> $m$ and <frequency>
$$
\nu=\sqrt{\omega_+\omega_-},\qquad
q_{\rm true}=
\left(\frac{\sqrt{\omega_+}-\sqrt{\omega_-}}
{\sqrt{\omega_+}+\sqrt{\omega_-}}\right)^2,
\qquad
\boxed{T_{\rm true}=\frac{\hbar\nu}{k_B\ln(1/q_{\rm true})}.}
$$
The corresponding occupation basis is that of $\nu$, not that of the original $a$. This follows by matching the Gaussian <variances> to $\langle x^2\rangle=\hbar\coth[\hbar\nu/(2k_BT)]/(2m\nu)$ and its <momentum> counterpart. A direct reduced-Gaussian <eigenfunction> calculation gives the same effective <frequency> and geometric <eigenvalues>.<Entropy and Area by Mark Srednicki>[Equations (1)–(5), printed pp. 2–3 of the preprint: two coupled oscillators, partial trace and the effective thermal oscillator frequency]{ref}

Choose orthonormal, flux-normalized incoming and outgoing channels and include all channels of this lossless scattering problem. Conservation of <probability> requires $\|\beta\|^2=\|\alpha\|^2$ for every input <vector>. With $\beta=S\alpha$,
$$
\alpha^\dagger(S^\dagger S-I)\alpha=0\quad\text{for every }\alpha,
\qquad\boxed{S^\dagger S=I.}
$$
For a square channel <matrix> this also gives $SS^\dagger=I$. A submatrix obtained by omitting loss channels need not be unitary, but that is not the stated two-channel model.

The given real <Hadamard matrix> is symmetric, unitary and its own inverse. Hence
$$
a_1^\dagger=\frac{b_1^\dagger+b_2^\dagger}{\sqrt2},\qquad
a_2^\dagger=\frac{b_1^\dagger-b_2^\dagger}{\sqrt2}.
$$
This same transformation preserves the <canonical commutation relations> for <bosons> and anticommutation relations for <fermions>. To extract <probabilities> one must use normalized <number states>, including their factorial factors, even when the supplied input expression is unnormalized.

For identical <fermions>, $(b_j^\dagger)^2=0$ and $b_2^\dagger b_1^\dagger=-b_1^\dagger b_2^\dagger$. Therefore
$$
a_1^\dagger a_2^\dagger|0\rangle
=\frac12(b_1^\dagger+b_2^\dagger)(b_1^\dagger-b_2^\dagger)|0\rangle
=-b_1^\dagger b_2^\dagger|0\rangle.
$$
The minus sign is a <global phase> for this output. **The only occupancy is $(n_1,n_2)=(1,1)$, with <probability> one.** Double occupancy of either specified single-particle mode is excluded; no extra internal states are present in this model.

Bosonic <creation operators> commute, so the cross terms cancel:
$$
a_1^\dagger a_2^\dagger|0\rangle
=\frac12[(b_1^\dagger)^2-(b_2^\dagger)^2]|0\rangle
=\frac{|2,0\rangle-|0,2\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{P(2,0)=P(0,2)=\frac12,\qquad P(1,1)=0.}
$$
The square root of two in $(b^\dagger)^2|0\rangle=\sqrt2|2\rangle$ is essential. Destructive <interference> removes the one-particle-per-output amplitude; the particles do not independently choose ports.

For <photons> this is the ideal <Hong-Ou-Mandel effect>. Its laboratory test combines one <photon> from each input at a balanced <beam splitter> and scans relative arrival delay while measuring output coincidences. Spatial, <polarization> and spectral indistinguishability must be controlled; distinguishable <photons> restore a nonzero coincidence baseline. The original experiment used down-converted <photons>, a translated <beam splitter> and coincidence counting, with an independently estimated accidental-coincidence background.<Measurement of Subpicosecond Time Intervals between Two Photons by Interference by C. The input squared norm is $(N!)^2$, so divide its stated expression by $N!$. Commutativity and the inverse channel transformation give the normalized output
$$
|\psi_{\rm out}\rangle=
\frac1{2^NN!}\left[(b_1^\dagger)^2-(b_2^\dagger)^2\right]^N|0\rangle.
$$
After the binomial expansion, the coefficient of $|2j,2N-2j\rangle$ is
$$
c_j=\frac{(-1)^{N-j}}{2^NN!}\binom Nj
\sqrt{(2j)!(2N-2j)!},\qquad 0\le j\le N.
$$
Hence every possible occupancy has even particle number in each port:
$$
\boxed{P(2j,2N-2j)=\frac1{4^N}\binom{2j}{j}
\binom{2N-2j}{N-j},\quad j=0,\ldots,N.}
$$
All other occupancies have zero <probability>. The normalization identity
$$
\sum_{j=0}^N\binom{2j}{j}\binom{2N-2j}{N-j}=4^N
$$
follows by taking the coefficient of $z^N$ in $(1-4z)^{-1/2}(1-4z)^{-1/2}=(1-4z)^{-1}$. For $N=1$ it reduces to (c), and <symmetry> under $j\leftrightarrow N-j$ gives equal mean occupancies in both outputs.

The exponential input has norm squared $\exp(|\alpha_1|^2+|\alpha_2|^2)$. Its normalized form is a product of <coherent states>. Substitution of the inverse transformation gives
$$
\alpha_1a_1^\dagger+\alpha_2a_2^\dagger
=\beta_1b_1^\dagger+\beta_2b_2^\dagger,\qquad
\beta_1=\frac{\alpha_1+\alpha_2}{\sqrt2},\quad
\beta_2=\frac{\alpha_1-\alpha_2}{\sqrt2}.
$$
Since the output <creation operators> commute, the normalized output factorizes as $|\beta_1\rangle|\beta_2\rangle$. For all nonnegative integers $n_1,n_2$,
$$
\boxed{P(n_1,n_2)=
e^{-|\beta_1|^2-|\beta_2|^2}
\frac{|\beta_1|^{2n_1}}{n_1!}
\frac{|\beta_2|^{2n_2}}{n_2!}.}
$$
These are independent <Poisson distributions>, not a fixed-total-particle-number <distribution>. <unitarity> gives $|\beta_1|^2+|\beta_2|^2=|\alpha_1|^2+|\alpha_2|^2$. Equal complex inputs make $\beta_2=0$, so that port is certainly empty; opposite inputs empty port 1. This is amplitude <interference> of coherent inputs, distinct from the fixed-number two-particle <interference> in (c).

The defining condition is

The entries are complex and the <matrices> are invertible; products and inverses retain <determinant> one. There is no <unitarity> condition $S^\dagger S=I$ in general. Four complex entries subject to one complex <determinant> equation give three complex, or six real, local group parameters.

= Solution

The association between four real coordinates and Hermitian two-by-two <matrices> is a real-linear bijection. Congruence by $S$ preserves Hermiticity, so $X'=SXS^\dagger$ defines another real <four-vector> depending linearly on the original. Its <determinant> is
$$
\det X=(ct)^2-x^2-y^2-z^2,\qquad
\det X'=\det S\,\det X\,\det S^\dagger=\det X.
$$
Thus the induced invertible linear map preserves the Minkowski quadratic form:
$$
\boxed{\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda=\eta,\qquad
\eta=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1).}
$$
The maps obtained continuously from the identity are proper and orthochronous. For example, a future timelike <vector> gives a positive-definite $X$, which remains positive definite under invertible congruence; its <time> direction is preserved. The <matrices> $S$ and $-S$ induce the same <four-vector> transformation, illustrating the <Lorentz spinor double cover>.

The source uses $x+iy$ in the upper-right entry, corresponding to $X=ctI+x\sigma_x-y\sigma_y+z\sigma_z$ for the standard <Pauli matrices>. This is a permissible basis convention, but its minus sign in the $y$ basis must also be retained in the <spinor> identification.

= Solution

For nonzero $\chi$, the outer product $X=\chi\chi^\dagger$ is rank-one Hermitian positive semidefinite, so $\det X=0$ and $ct=\chi^\dagger\chi/2>0$. It therefore represents a future-directed <null vector>. Conversely any nonzero future-null Hermitian $X$ has <eigenvalues> $2ct$ and zero, so its positive <eigenvector> multiplied by $\sqrt{2ct}$ supplies $\chi$. The phase $\chi\mapsto e^{i\zeta}\chi$ leaves $X$ unchanged. A past-directed <null vector> needs $X=-\chi\chi^\dagger$, rather than the positive outer product alone.

For the printed normalized <spinor>, direct multiplication yields
$$
X=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}
1+\cos\theta&\sin\theta\,e^{-i\phi}\\
\sin\theta\,e^{i\phi}&1-\cos\theta
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Comparing with the printed $x+iy$ convention gives
$$
\boxed{(ct,x,y,z)=\frac12
(1,\ \sin\theta\cos\phi,\ -\sin\theta\sin\phi,\ \cos\theta).}
$$
**Its usual spatial <azimuth> is $-\phi$, not $\phi$.** The source's asserted usual-angle identification has a sign inconsistency. Either conjugating the two <spinor> phases or replacing the upper-right <matrix> entry by $x-iy$ restores the usual <azimuth> $\phi$. Neither alteration is made silently here. A further positive scale factor sets the magnitude of an arbitrary future-null <vector>; the displayed normalized <spinor> fixes $ct=1/2$ in the chosen coordinate units.

The <spinor> transformation is $\chi'=S\chi$, with $S=\operatorname{diag}(e^{\kappa/2},e^{-\kappa/2})$. Its squared norm becomes
$$
\mathcal R=e^\kappa\cos^2(\theta/2)+e^{-\kappa}\sin^2(\theta/2)
=\cosh\kappa+\sinh\kappa\cos\theta.
$$
Dividing by $\sqrt{\mathcal R}$ isolates the new direction without discarding the physical scale factor in $X'$. The lower-to-upper component ratio gives
$$
\boxed{\tan\frac{\theta'}2=e^{-\kappa}\tan\frac\theta2,\qquad
\phi'=\phi\pmod{2\pi}.}
$$
Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{\cos\theta'=\frac{\cos\theta+\tanh\kappa}
{1+\tanh\kappa\cos\theta},\qquad
\sin\theta'=\frac{\sin\theta}{\mathcal R}.}
$$
This is <relativistic aberration> for the active positive-$z$ boost specified in the question. At the poles, <azimuth> is undefined rather than a measurable transformed variable. Under the literal <matrix> convention from (c), the actual <azimuth> $-\phi$ is likewise unchanged. The <time> coordinate is multiplied by $\mathcal R$; normalizing a <spinor> for its direction must not erase that boost-induced change of null-vector scale.

The generator acts only in the $ct,z$ plane, with its square equal to the identity on that two-dimensional subspace. Its exponential is
$$
\boxed{\Lambda=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cosh\kappa&0&0&\sinh\kappa\\
0&1&0&0\\
0&0&1&0\\
\sinh\kappa&0&0&\cosh\kappa
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
For the <vector> in (c), this gives
$$
ct'=\tfrac12(\cosh\kappa+\sinh\kappa\cos\theta),\qquad
z'=\tfrac12(\sinh\kappa+\cosh\kappa\cos\theta),
$$
while $x'=x$ and $y'=y$. Since the <vector> is future-null, its spatial magnitude is $ct'$. Therefore
$$
\frac{z'}{ct'}=\frac{\cos\theta+\tanh\kappa}
{1+\tanh\kappa\cos\theta}=\cos\theta',
\qquad
\frac{\sqrt{x'^2+y'^2}}{ct'}=\frac{\sin\theta}{\mathcal R}.
$$
The transverse <azimuth> does not change. These are exactly the direction and scale obtained from the <spinor> congruence. In particular $ct'= \mathcal R\,ct$ and $\tan(\theta'/2)=e^{-\kappa}\tan(\theta/2)$. The azimuth-sign qualification in (c) is compatible with this check because a boost along $z$ leaves both transverse coordinates unchanged.
