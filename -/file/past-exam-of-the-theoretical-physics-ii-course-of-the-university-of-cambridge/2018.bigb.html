= Solution
{parent=a}

For independent bosonic modes, the <canonical commutation relation> is
$$
[b_i,b_j^\dagger]=\delta_{ij},\qquad [b_i,b_j]=[b_i^\dagger,b_j^\dagger]=0.
$$
For independent fermionic modes, the <canonical anticommutation relations> are
$$
\{c_i,c_j^\dagger\}=\delta_{ij},\qquad \{c_i,c_j\}=\{c_i^\dagger,c_j^\dagger\}=0,
$$
where $[A,B]=AB-BA$ and $\{A,B\}=AB+BA$. The <creation operator> is the adjoint of the <annihilation operator>. In either case the mode's <number operator> is
$$
\boxed{n_i=a_i^\dagger a_i},
$$
with $a_i=b_i$ or $c_i$ as appropriate. It measures the mode's <occupation number>. Its possible <eigenvalues> are
$$
\boxed{n_i=0,1,2,\ldots\ \text{for bosons};\qquad n_i=0,1\ \text{for fermions}}.
$$
For <fermions>, $c_i^2=0$ implies $n_i^2=c_i^\dagger(1-c_i^\dagger c_i)c_i=n_i$, so the <number operator> is a projection. This expresses the exclusion of double occupation of one fermionic mode. The total particle number is $N=\sum_i n_i$.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Expand the <anticommutators> using the relations in part (a). The terms with two creation or two <annihilation operators> vanish, leaving
$$
\boxed{\{\gamma_i^A,\gamma_j^A\}=2\delta_{ij},\qquad
\{\gamma_i^B,\gamma_j^B\}=2\delta_{ij},\qquad
\{\gamma_i^A,\gamma_j^B\}=0}.
$$
Each <Majorana fermion operator> is <Hermitian> and squares to the identity. In particular, $(\gamma_i^A)^\dagger\gamma_i^A=1$, so this expression cannot distinguish an empty mode from an occupied one. A single Majorana operator is its own adjoint and does not supply distinct creation and <annihilation operators> for such an occupation.

An occupation can instead be assigned after <pairing Majorana operators into a fermionic mode>. For two distinct Majorana operators $\gamma_a,\gamma_b$,
$$
f=\frac{\gamma_a+i\gamma_b}{2},\qquad
\boxed{n_f=f^\dagger f=\frac{1+i\gamma_a\gamma_b}{2}}.
$$
This is a nontrivial <projection operator> with <eigenvalues> zero and one. Thus **occupation belongs to a chosen pair of Majorana operators, not to an individual Majorana operator**.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Use $c_j=(\gamma_j^A+i\gamma_j^B)/2$ and $c_j^\dagger=(\gamma_j^A-i\gamma_j^B)/2$. Moving operators from different modes past one another introduces a minus sign. Direct expansion gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
c_1^\dagger c_2+c_2^\dagger c_1
&=\frac i2(\gamma_1^A\gamma_2^B-\gamma_1^B\gamma_2^A),\\
c_1^\dagger c_2^\dagger+c_2c_1
&=-\frac i2(\gamma_1^A\gamma_2^B+\gamma_1^B\gamma_2^A).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus, for real hopping and pairing parameters,
$$
H=\frac i2\left[(\Delta-t)\gamma_1^A\gamma_2^B+(t+\Delta)\gamma_1^B\gamma_2^A\right].
$$
At $\Delta=t\ne0$ this becomes
$$
\boxed{H=it\gamma_1^B\gamma_2^A}.
$$
Use $\tau$ for time to distinguish it from the hopping amplitude $t$. Each unused Majorana operator anticommutes with both factors in $H$, so commuting it through their product gives two minus signs. Hence $[H,\gamma_1^A]=[H,\gamma_2^B]=0$. The <Heisenberg picture> equation $dO/d\tau=(i/\hbar)[H,O]$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\gamma_1^A(\tau)=\gamma_1^A(0),\qquad \gamma_2^B(\tau)=\gamma_2^B(0)}.
$$
These are the two uncoupled <Majorana zero modes>.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For any two distinct normalized <Majorana fermion operators> $\eta,\chi$, let $f=(\eta+i\chi)/2$. Then
$$
\{f,f^\dagger\}=\frac14\big(\{\eta,\eta\}+\{\chi,\chi\}\big)=1,
$$
and
$$
\{f,f\}=\frac14\big(\{\eta,\eta\}-\{\chi,\chi\}+2i\{\eta,\chi\}\big)=0.
$$
The same applies to $f^\dagger$. Both specified pairs have this form. Moreover, the Majorana operators entering $f_1$ are all distinct from those entering $f_2$, so every cross <anticommutator> is zero. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\{f_i,f_j^\dagger\}=\delta_{ij},\qquad
\{f_i,f_j\}=\{f_i^\dagger,f_j^\dagger\}=0}.
$$
Thus $f_1,f_2$ are ordinary <fermionic annihilation operators>, constructed by a different pairing of the four Majorana operators.

= Solution

The occupation formula from part (b) gives
$$
n_2=f_2^\dagger f_2=\frac{1+i\gamma_1^B\gamma_2^A}{2}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{H=t(2f_2^\dagger f_2-1)=t(2n_2-1)}.
$$
The independent <occupation numbers> $n_1,n_2$ are each zero or one. The <Hamiltonian> is independent of $n_1$, so its four states have
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
n_2&E&\text{degeneracy}\\\hline
0&-t&2\\
1&+t&2
\end{array}}.
$$
Each energy admits $n_1=0$ or $n_1=1$; these states differ by occupancy of the zero-energy mode. The ground energy is $-|t|$ for either sign of $t$. These degeneracies refer to the full two-mode <fermionic Fock space>. If one separately restricts to a fixed global <fermion parity>, only one state at each energy remains in that parity sector.

= Solution
{parent=a}

For a short-range potential, an incident <plane wave> of unit amplitude and wave vector $k\mathbf e_z$ has the large-distance <scattering wavefunction>
$$
\boxed{\psi(\mathbf r)=e^{ikz}+f(\theta,\phi)\frac{e^{ikr}}r+O(r^{-2})\qquad(r\to\infty)}.
$$
The coefficient $f$ is the <scattering amplitude>. For a central potential it depends only on the scattering angle $\theta$. The incident <probability current> is $\hbar k/m$, while the scattered radial current is $(\hbar k/m)|f|^2/r^2$ to leading order. Their ratio gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=|f(\theta,\phi)|^2,\qquad
\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}=\int |f(\theta,\phi)|^2\,d\Omega}.
$$
These are the <differential scattering cross-section> and total <cross section> for elastic scattering with the same incident and outgoing wave number.

The expression is *asymptotic*: it specifies the outgoing wave's leading $1/r$ term, not its finite-distance radial corrections or its form inside the interaction region. In particular, for angle-dependent $f$, the spherical term by itself has an angular-Laplacian contribution of order $r^{-3}$ to the free wave equation, which is cancelled by subleading terms in the exact solution.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

In the <first Born approximation>, with incident and outgoing wave vectors $\mathbf k,\mathbf k'$, put $\mathbf q=\mathbf k'-\mathbf k$. For elastic scattering,
$$
q=2k\sin\frac\theta2,
\qquad f_B=-\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}V(r)\,d^3r.
$$
Choose the polar axis along $\mathbf q$. The angular integral is
$$
\int e^{-iqr\cos\vartheta}\,d\Omega
=2\pi\int_{-1}^1e^{-iqru}\,du=4\pi\frac{\sin(qr)}{qr}.
$$
The radial <Dirac delta distribution> then gives
$$
f_B=-\frac{2mV_0}{\hbar^2}\int_0^\infty r^2\delta(r-R)\frac{\sin(qr)}{qr}\,dr.
$$
Thus, introducing the <dimensionless> shell strength $g=2mV_0R/\hbar^2$,
$$
\boxed{f_B(\theta)=-\frac{2mV_0R}{\hbar^2q}\sin(qR)
=-gR\frac{\sin(qR)}{qR}}.
$$
The forward value is the continuous limit $f_B(0)=-gR$. This is the <delta-shell Born amplitude>, with its angular dependence expressed through the <momentum transfer>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Away from the shell, the reduced radial equation is free. Regularity at the origin and the definition of the S-wave <phase shift> give
$$
u_0(r)=\begin{cases}A\sin kr,&r<R,\\B\sin(kr+\delta_0),&r>R.\end{cases}
$$
The reduced radial <wave function> is continuous at $R$; otherwise its second derivative would contain a delta derivative not present in the equation. Integrating the equation across the shell gives the jump
$$
u_0'(R^+)-u_0'(R^-)=\frac{2mV_0}{\hbar^2}u_0(R).
$$
Divide by the common shell value and substitute the two free solutions. This yields
$$
\boxed{k\cot(kR+\delta_0)-k\cot(kR)=\frac{2mV_0}{\hbar^2}}.
$$
If the shell value vanishes, use the undivided matching equations or a continuous limit rather than this logarithmic-derivative division. For an explicit <phase shift of a delta-shell potential>, write $x=kR$ and $g=2mV_0R/\hbar^2$. The cotangent addition formula gives
$$
\boxed{\tan\delta_0(k)=-\frac{g\sin^2x}{x+g\sin x\cos x}}.
$$
The phase is defined modulo $\pi$; choose its branch continuously as energy or coupling varies. At zero coupling the physical <phase shift> is zero.

= Solution
{parent=d}

The <scattering length> is $a_0=-\lim_{k\to0}\tan\delta_0/k$. Since $\sin(kR)\sim kR$ and $\cos(kR)\sim1$, the exact <phase shift> gives, away from the pole,
$$
\boxed{a_0=R\frac{g}{1+g},\qquad g=\frac{2mV_0R}{\hbar^2}}.
$$
Thus plot $a_0/R$ against $g$, so that both axes are <dimensionless>.

\Image[2018-delta-shell-scattering-length.png]
{title=Delta-shell scattering length versus dimensionless coupling, with the bound-state threshold at g = −1}

For repulsion, $g>0$, the curve rises from zero to the horizontal asymptote $a_0/R=1$, staying between zero and one. For weak attraction, $-1<g<0$, the <scattering length> is negative and tends to $-\infty$ as $g\to-1^+$. For stronger attraction, $g<-1$, it is positive and greater than $R$, tending to $+\infty$ as $g\to-1^-$ and to $R$ as $g\to-\infty$. Its only zero is $g=0$.

The pole at $g=-1$ is the zero-energy threshold of an S-wave <bound state>. To see this directly, use $u\propto\sinh(\kappa r)$ inside and $u\propto e^{-\kappa r}$ outside for energy $-\hbar^2\kappa^2/(2m)$. The same derivative jump gives
$$
g=-\kappa R[1+\coth(\kappa R)]
=-\frac{2\kappa R}{1-e^{-2\kappa R}}.
$$
This approaches $-1$ as $\kappa\downarrow0$ and admits a positive $\kappa$ for $g<-1$. At the threshold the <scattering length> diverges; this is a <zero-energy scattering resonance>, not a divergence of the finite-energy cross-section beyond its <partial-wave unitarity> bound. These are the main features of the <scattering length of a delta-shell potential>.

= Solution
{parent=e}

The <delta-shell Born amplitude> is first order in the potential strength:
$$
f_B=-gR\frac{\sin(qR)}{qR}.
$$
It retains finite-energy angular dependence and includes the leading weak-coupling contributions of all <partial waves>. By contrast, the supplied $-gR/(1+g)=-a_0$ is the *zero-energy limit* of the exact <S-wave scattering> amplitude. It is isotropic, depends nonlinearly on $g$, and captures the scattering-length pole missed by first-order perturbation theory.

At finite energy the exact S-wave amplitude is instead
$$
f_0(k)=\frac{e^{2i\delta_0}-1}{2ik}
=\frac1{k\cot\delta_0-ik},
$$
which is generally complex and obeys elastic unitarity. Its zero-energy limit is $-a_0$ for finite <scattering length>. Thus the two expressions printed in the question represent different approximations; the energy-independent S-wave expression is not the full finite-$k$ result of part (c).

Their common regime requires **both weak coupling and low energy**:
$$
\boxed{|g|\ll1,\qquad kR\ll1}.
$$
Then $qR\leq2kR\ll1$, so
$$
f_B=-gR\left[1-\frac{(qR)^2}{6}+O((qR)^4)\right],\qquad
-a_0=-\frac{gR}{1+g}=-gR+g^2R+O(g^3R).
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{f_B\sim f_0\sim-gR=-\frac{2mV_0R^2}{\hbar^2}}
$$
in that joint regime. Low energy alone does not remove the nonperturbative denominator $1+g$, while weak coupling alone does not remove higher-partial-wave angular dependence.

= Solution

An ensemble with normalized quantum states $|\psi_a\rangle$ and probabilities $p_a\geq0$, $\sum_ap_a=1$, has <density matrix>
$$
\boxed{\rho=\sum_a p_a|\psi_a\rangle\langle\psi_a|}.
$$
Each rank-one projector is <Hermitian>, so $\rho^\dagger=\rho$. Taking the <trace> in any <orthonormal basis> gives
$$
\operatorname{Tr}\rho=\sum_a p_a\langle\psi_a|\psi_a\rangle=1.
$$
It is also positive: $\langle v|\rho|v\rangle=\sum_ap_a|\langle\psi_a|v\rangle|^2\geq0$. Its <eigenvalues> $\lambda_a$ are therefore nonnegative and sum to one. Hence
$$
\operatorname{Tr}\rho^2=\sum_a\lambda_a^2\leq\left(\sum_a\lambda_a\right)^2=1,
$$
with equality exactly when one <eigenvalue> is one and the others are zero. Thus the <purity of a density operator> distinguishes
$$
\boxed{\text{pure state: }\operatorname{Tr}\rho^2=1,\qquad
\text{mixed state: }\operatorname{Tr}\rho^2<1}.
$$
Equivalently, a <pure state> has $\rho^2=\rho$ and rank one. In a $d$-dimensional <Hilbert space>, purity is at least $1/d$, attained by the <maximally mixed state> $I/d$.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The fixed-particle-number sector is spanned by $|1\rangle=c_1^\dagger|0\rangle$ and $|2\rangle=c_2^\dagger|0\rangle$. In this basis,
$$
H=-t\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}=-t\sigma_x.
$$
The relevant <quantum Gibbs state> is normalized within this two-dimensional sector, not over all particle numbers. With $\beta=(k_BT)^{-1}$ and $x=\beta t$, the <exponential of a Pauli vector> gives
$$
e^{-\beta H}=e^{x\sigma_x}=I\cosh x+\sigma_x\sinh x,\qquad
Z=2\cosh x.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\rho_\beta=\frac12(I+\tanh x\,\sigma_x)
=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&\tanh x\\\tanh x&1\end{pmatrix}}.
$$
Its <eigenstates> are $|\pm\rangle=(|1\rangle\pm|2\rangle)/\sqrt2$, with energies $\mp t$ and probabilities $p_\pm=e^{\pm x}/(2\cosh x)$. These are nonnegative and sum to one, so the <matrix> is <Hermitian> and has <trace> one. Its <purity of a density operator> is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Tr}\rho_\beta^2=\frac12(1+\tanh^2x)}.
$$
For $t\ne0$, the zero-temperature limit is the pure ground-state projector,
$$
\rho_{T\to0}=\frac12[I+\operatorname{sgn}(t)\sigma_x],
$$
with purity one. As $T\to\infty$, $x\to0$ and $\rho\to I/2$, with purity $1/2$. At every strictly positive finite <temperature> and finite $t$, both <eigenvalues> are nonzero, so the state is mixed. If $t=0$, the two levels are degenerate and the Gibbs state is $I/2$ at all temperatures, including its zero-temperature limit.

Using <dimensionless> <von Neumann entropy>, $S=-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\ln\rho)$, gives
$$
\boxed{S(T)=-p_+\ln p_+-p_-\ln p_-
=\ln(2\cosh x)-x\tanh x}.
$$
For nonzero $t$, $S\to0$ as $T\to0$, while $S\to\ln2$ as $T\to\infty$. More precisely, the high-temperature expansion is $S=\ln2-x^2/2+O(x^4)$, and the low-temperature form is $S\sim(1+2|x|)e^{-2|x|}$. The thermodynamic entropy includes an overall factor $k_B$.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Fix the mode ordering with the $b^\dagger$ operator before the $c^\dagger$ operator, as in the given state. In the sector with one <fermion> in each subsystem, use the ordered basis
$$
(|B_1C_1\rangle,|B_1C_2\rangle,|B_2C_1\rangle,|B_2C_2\rangle).
$$
The <state vector> is $v=(1/2,1/\sqrt2,0,1/2)^T$, whose squared norm is one. Thus the global <density matrix> is the rank-one projector
$$
\boxed{\rho_{BC}=vv^\dagger=
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac14&\frac1{2\sqrt2}&0&\frac14\\
\frac1{2\sqrt2}&\frac12&0&\frac1{2\sqrt2}\\
0&0&0&0\\
\frac14&\frac1{2\sqrt2}&0&\frac14
\end{pmatrix}}.
$$
It has <trace> one and satisfies $\rho_{BC}^2=\rho_{BC}$, so the global state is pure.

Take the <partial trace> over $B$. The conditional vectors in $C$ are $v_1=(1/2,1/\sqrt2)^T$ for $B_1$ and $v_2=(0,1/2)^T$ for $B_2$. Therefore the <reduced density matrix> is
$$
\boxed{\rho_C=v_1v_1^\dagger+v_2v_2^\dagger
=\begin{pmatrix}\frac14&\frac1{2\sqrt2}\\\frac1{2\sqrt2}&\frac34\end{pmatrix}}.
$$
Its determinant is $1/16$, giving <eigenvalues>
$$
\lambda_\pm=\frac{2\pm\sqrt3}{4}.
$$
Both are positive; in particular, $\operatorname{Tr}\rho_C^2=7/8<1$, so the reduced state is mixed despite global purity. The <entanglement entropy> is obtained by diagonalizing this reduced <matrix> and evaluating
$$
\boxed{S_{\mathrm{ent}}=-\lambda_+\ln\lambda_+-\lambda_-\ln\lambda_-}.
$$
For a globally pure bipartite state this equals the entropy of either reduced subsystem. No numerical evaluation is needed.

Using $[x,p]=i\hbar$, the <Heisenberg picture> equations for the <quantum harmonic oscillator> are
$$
\boxed{\dot x=\frac{i}{\hbar}[H_0,x]=\frac pm,\qquad
\dot p=\frac{i}{\hbar}[H_0,p]=-m\omega^2x}.
$$
Solving these coupled linear equations gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
x(t)&=x(0)\cos\omega t+\frac{p(0)}{m\omega}\sin\omega t,\\
p(t)&=p(0)\cos\omega t-m\omega x(0)\sin\omega t.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The oscillator <annihilation operator> and <creation operator> are
$$
a=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}x+\frac{i}{\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}}p,\qquad
a^\dagger=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}x-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}}p.
$$
They obey $[a,a^\dagger]=1$ and $H_0=\hbar\omega(a^\dagger a+1/2)$. Substitution of the position and momentum solutions, or the corresponding <commutators> with $H_0$, yields
$$
\boxed{a(t)=a(0)e^{-i\omega t},\qquad a^\dagger(t)=a^\dagger(0)e^{i\omega t}}.
$$

= Solution
{parent=b}

Complete the square, keeping the equilibrium displacement signed:
$$
x_0=-\frac\gamma{m\omega^2},\qquad
U_{\min}=-\frac{\gamma^2}{2m\omega^2},\qquad
H=\frac{p^2}{2m}+\frac12m\omega^2(x-x_0)^2+U_{\min}.
$$
Write $X=x-x_0$ for position relative to the new minimum. By the <sudden approximation>, the state immediately after the <quantum quench> is still the old vacuum. Its initial means are
$$
\boxed{\langle X(0^+)\rangle=-x_0=\frac\gamma{m\omega^2},\qquad
\langle p(0^+)\rangle=0}.
$$
The old <ground state> has $\langle H_0\rangle=\hbar\omega/2$ and $\langle x\rangle=0$. Thus $\langle H\rangle=\hbar\omega/2$, but the energy measured above the *new potential minimum* is
$$
\boxed{\langle H-U_{\min}\rangle=\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}+\frac{\gamma^2}{2m\omega^2}
=\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}+\frac12m\omega^2x_0^2}.
$$
The extra term is the excitation energy above the new <ground state>; the zero-point term remains part of the energy above the potential minimum.

The variables $X,p$ obey the oscillator equations in part (a). Using the above initial means gives
$$
\boxed{\langle X(t)\rangle=-x_0\cos\omega t,\qquad
\langle x(t)\rangle=x_0(1-\cos\omega t),\qquad
\langle p(t)\rangle=m\omega x_0\sin\omega t}.
$$
The wavepacket's centre therefore oscillates about the new equilibrium, beginning at the old origin with zero mean momentum.

{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Define the signed <dimensionless> displacement
$$
\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}x_0.
$$
The <spatial translation operator> is $T(x_0)=e^{-ipx_0/\hbar}$, and the phases of the new <energy eigenstates> are fixed by $|n'\rangle=T(x_0)|n\rangle$. Their expansion coefficients are therefore
$$
\langle n'|0\rangle=\langle n|T(-x_0)|0\rangle.
$$
Since $p=i\sqrt{m\hbar\omega/2}(a^\dagger-a)$,
$$
T(-x_0)=e^{-\lambda(a^\dagger-a)}.
$$
Set $A=-\lambda a^\dagger$ and $B=\lambda a$. Their <commutator> is $[A,B]=\lambda^2I$, which commutes with both operators. Thus the stated special case of the <Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula> is applicable and gives
$$
e^{A+B}=e^{-\lambda^2/2}e^Ae^B.
$$
Because $a|0\rangle=0$,
$$
T(-x_0)|0\rangle=e^{-\lambda^2/2}e^{-\lambda a^\dagger}|0\rangle
=e^{-\lambda^2/2}\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-\lambda)^n}{\sqrt{n!}}|n\rangle.
$$
Taking the overlaps with $\langle n|$ therefore proves
$$
\boxed{|0\rangle=e^{-\lambda^2/2}\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-\lambda)^n}{\sqrt{n!}}|n'\rangle}.
$$
This is the <coherent state after shifting an oscillator equilibrium>. Its normalization follows from $e^{-\lambda^2}\sum_n\lambda^{2n}/n!=1$.

There is a sign qualification in the printed expansion: $(m\omega x_0^2/(2\hbar))^{n/2}=|\lambda|^n$ agrees with $(-\lambda)^n$ when $x_0\leq0$, equivalently $\gamma\geq0$. For a general signed displacement $x_0>0$, the odd coefficients have the opposite sign in the specified translated basis. The squared displacement alone fixes the occupation probabilities, not these coherent relative phases. The two-exponential identity also requires the central-commutator condition used above; it is not valid in that simple form for arbitrary operators.

= Solution
{parent=d}

The <annihilation operator> associated with the new coordinate $X=x-x_0$ is
$$
a'=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}(x-x_0)+\frac{i}{\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}}p=a-\lambda.
$$
As the initial state is the old vacuum,
$$
\boxed{a'|\Psi(0)\rangle=-\lambda|\Psi(0)\rangle,\qquad
\alpha_0=-\lambda=-\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}x_0}.
$$
Equivalently, applying $a'$ to the number-state expansion in part (c) and shifting its summation index returns $-\lambda$ times the same state. It is therefore a <coherent state> of the displaced oscillator.

The new <Hamiltonian> is $H=U_{\min}+\hbar\omega(a'^\dagger a'+1/2)$. In the <Schrödinger picture> its evolution gives
$$
|\Psi(t)\rangle=e^{-i(U_{\min}/\hbar+\omega/2)t}
 e^{-|\alpha_0|^2/2}\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(\alpha_0e^{-i\omega t})^n}{\sqrt{n!}}|n'\rangle.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{a'|\Psi(t)\rangle=\alpha(t)|\Psi(t)\rangle,\qquad
\alpha(t)=\alpha_0e^{-i\omega t}}.
$$
The displayed prefactor is only a global phase. Equivalently, in the <Heisenberg picture> $a'(t)=a'(0)e^{-i\omega t}$. The mean occupation $|\alpha(t)|^2=\lambda^2$ is constant, and $\hbar\omega(\lambda^2+1/2)$ reproduces the energy above the new minimum found in part (b). Its mean position and momentum likewise reproduce that part's oscillating wavepacket centre.
