= Solution
{parent=a}

For a differentiable family of invertible <linear operators>, differentiate the identity without changing the factor order:
$$
\frac{dA^{-1}}{d\lambda}A+A^{-1}\frac{dA}{d\lambda}=0.
$$
Multiplication on the right by $A^{-1}$ gives the <derivative of matrix inversion>,
$$
\boxed{\frac{dA^{-1}}{d\lambda}=-A^{-1}\frac{dA}{d\lambda}A^{-1}.}
$$
Commuting $A^{-1}$ past the <derivative> would generally be invalid.

= b
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Take an <orthonormal basis> $\{|j\rangle\}$ and insert its <completeness relation>:
$$
\operatorname{Tr}(|u\rangle\langle v|)
=\sum_j\langle j|u\rangle\langle v|j\rangle
=\langle v|\left(\sum_j|j\rangle\langle j|\right)|u\rangle
=\boxed{\langle v|u\rangle}.
$$
No normalization or <orthogonality> of $u,v$ is required. This is the <trace> of a <rank-one operator>; in a <Hilbert space> the sum converges by the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>.

= c
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

In a finite-dimensional <basis>, interchange the two finite sums:
$$
\operatorname{Tr}(AB)=\sum_{j,k}A_{jk}B_{kj}
=\sum_{k,j}B_{kj}A_{jk}
=\boxed{\operatorname{Tr}(BA)}.
$$
This <cyclic property of the trace> does not assert $AB=BA$ or permit arbitrary reordering of three factors. For infinite-dimensional <linear operators>, the <traces> and rearrangements need hypotheses, for example one <trace-class operator> and one <bounded operator>; an arbitrary pair of unbounded <linear operators> is not covered.

{parent=1}

= Solution
{parent=d}

For real $\epsilon$, <unitarity> to first order gives
$$
I=U^\dagger U=(I-i\epsilon F^\dagger)(I+i\epsilon F)
=I+i\epsilon(F-F^\dagger)+O(\epsilon^2).
$$
Hence the generator of an <infinitesimal unitary transformation> obeys **$F=F^\dagger$**: it is <Hermitian>. More precisely, $U(\epsilon)=I+i\epsilon F+O(\epsilon^2)$ is a differentiable unitary family at the identity. The omitted second-order terms matter if $\epsilon$ is finite.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=e}

Set $C(\beta)=[A,e^{-\beta B}]$. The <product rule> gives
$$
C'+BC=[B,A]e^{-\beta B},\qquad C(0)=0.
$$
Multiplying by the <integrating factor> $e^{\beta B}$ and integrating yields the <commutator of an operator with an exponential>:
$$
\boxed{[A,e^{-\beta B}]
=e^{-\beta B}\int_0^\beta
e^{\lambda B}[B,A]e^{-\lambda B}\,d\lambda.}
$$
Equivalently, differentiate $e^{\beta B}Ae^{-\beta B}-A$ directly; its <derivative> is the integrand and it vanishes at zero. The argument is exact for finite <matrices>, and for <bounded operators> with their norm-convergent exponentials; unbounded <linear operators> require an appropriate common domain.

An ensemble of normalized <quantum states> with <probabilities> $p_j$ is represented by the <density operator>
$$
\boxed{\rho=\sum_jp_j|\psi_j\rangle\langle\psi_j|,
\qquad p_j\ge0,\quad\sum_jp_j=1.}
$$
It determines every <observable> <expectation value> through $\langle A\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho A)$. The ensemble decomposition need not be unique; the operator specifies the measurement statistics.

= b
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Each term is a <Hermitian> rank-one <projection> and $p_j$ is real, so $\rho^\dagger=\rho$. Using the rank-one <trace>,
$$
\operatorname{Tr}\rho=\sum_jp_j\langle\psi_j|\psi_j\rangle
=\sum_jp_j=\boxed{1}.
$$
The remaining admissibility condition follows from $\langle v|\rho|v\rangle=\sum_jp_j|\langle\psi_j|v\rangle|^2\ge0$: **$\rho$ is <positive semidefinite>**.

= c
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

A normalized <pure state> has $\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$, and therefore
$$
\boxed{\rho^2=\rho,\qquad\operatorname{Tr}\rho^2=1.}
$$
Conversely, diagonalize a permissible <density operator>. Its <eigenvalues> obey $0\le\lambda_j\le1$ and $\sum_j\lambda_j=1$, so
$$
1-\operatorname{Tr}\rho^2
=\sum_j\lambda_j(1-\lambda_j)\ge0.
$$
Equality requires every <eigenvalue> to be zero or one; exactly one is one. Thus the <idempotent matrix> condition and <purity> one both characterize a <pure state>. A <mixed state> has at least two positive <eigenvalues>, **$\rho^2\ne\rho$ and $\operatorname{Tr}\rho^2<1$**. In <dimension> $d$ its <purity> is at least $1/d$.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

The given real symmetric <matrix> is <Hermitian> and has <trace> one. A <spectral decomposition> supplies its positivity without relying on diagonal entries alone:
$$
\rho=\frac34|v_1\rangle\langle v_1|
+\frac14|v_2\rangle\langle v_2|,\qquad
v_1=\frac{(2,1,1)^{\mathsf T}}{\sqrt6},\quad
v_2=\frac{(0,1,-1)^{\mathsf T}}{\sqrt2}.
$$
The remaining normalized <eigenvector> is $(-1,1,1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt3$ with <eigenvalue> zero. Consequently the spectrum is **$\{3/4,1/4,0\}$**, every <eigenvalue> is nonnegative, and the <matrix> is permissible. Its <purity> is $(3/4)^2+(1/4)^2=\boxed{5/8}$, so it is **mixed**.

= e
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=e}

The original <Von Neumann entropy> depends on its <eigenvalues>, with $0\log0$ defined by continuity:
$$
\boxed{\frac{S_{\rm before}}{k_B}
=-\frac34\log\frac34-\frac14\log\frac14
=2\log2-\frac34\log3
\simeq0.562335.}
$$
After <rank-one dephasing>, the <eigenvalues> are the retained diagonal populations $1/2,1/4,1/4$, giving
$$
\boxed{\frac{S_{\rm after}}{k_B}=\frac32\log2\simeq1.039721,\qquad
\frac{\Delta S}{k_B}=\frac34\log3-\frac12\log2\simeq0.477386>0.}
$$
<dephasing> removes coherences in the specified <basis>, increasing the system's mixedness while preserving those measurement <probabilities>. It cannot be a closed-system <unitary transformation>, since the <eigenvalues> change. The subsystem <entropy> increase does not by itself assert an increase in the <entropy> of a closed system plus its environment.

= Solution
{parent=a}

The <canonical commutation relation> $[x',p']=i\hbar I$ and <hamiltonian> $H'=p'^2/(2m)+m\omega^2x'^2/2$ give
$$
[H',x']=-\frac{i\hbar}{m}p',\qquad
[H',p']=i\hbar m\omega^2x'.
$$
Substitution in the <Heisenberg equation of motion> yields $\dot x'=p'/m$ and $\dot p'=-m\omega^2x'$. Differentiating once more gives the harmonic-oscillator equation for each operator. Its initial-value solution is
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
x'(t)&=x'(0)\cos\omega t+\frac{p'(0)}{m\omega}\sin\omega t,\\
p'(t)&=p'(0)\cos\omega t-m\omega x'(0)\sin\omega t.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Differentiating these expressions at zero verifies both prescribed initial <derivatives>. They also preserve $[x'(t),p'(t)]=i\hbar I$.

= b
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The normalized initial <Gaussian wave packet> is real and even, so the integrands for $\langle x'\rangle_0$ and $\langle p'\rangle_0$ are odd. Its second <position> moment is the elementary <Gaussian integral> $\langle x'^2\rangle_0=1/(2\alpha)$. Since $\partial_x\Psi=-\alpha x\Psi$ and $\partial_x^2\Psi=(\alpha^2x^2-\alpha)\Psi$,
$$
\langle p'^2\rangle_0
=\hbar^2\left(\alpha-\alpha^2\langle x'^2\rangle_0\right)
=\frac{\hbar^2\alpha}{2}.
$$
Moreover $\langle x'p'\rangle_0=i\hbar\alpha\langle x'^2\rangle_0=i\hbar/2$ and $\langle p'x'\rangle_0=-i\hbar/2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\langle x'\rangle_0=\langle p'\rangle_0
=\langle x'p'+p'x'\rangle_0=0,\qquad
\langle x'^2\rangle_0=\frac1{2\alpha},\quad
\langle p'^2\rangle_0=\frac{\hbar^2\alpha}{2}.}
$$

= c
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Square the operator solutions, retaining the symmetrized cross term rather than treating $x'(0)$ and $p'(0)$ as commuting variables. The initial symmetrized expectation vanishes, so
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\langle x'^2\rangle_t
&=\frac{\cos^2\omega t}{2\alpha}
+\frac{\hbar^2\alpha}{2m^2\omega^2}\sin^2\omega t,\\
\langle p'^2\rangle_t
&=\frac{\hbar^2\alpha}{2}\cos^2\omega t
+\frac{m^2\omega^2}{2\alpha}\sin^2\omega t.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The means remain zero. These are also the <variances>. Unless $\alpha=m\omega/\hbar$, the <Gaussian> state breathes: its <position> and <momentum> widths exchange periodically while the mean <energy> remains constant.

= d
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Introduce the <dimensionless> width $s=\hbar\alpha/(m\omega)>0$. Multiplying the <variances> gives
$$
\boxed{(\Delta x')_t^2(\Delta p')_t^2
=\frac{\hbar^2}{4}\left[
1+\frac14(s-s^{-1})^2\sin^2(2\omega t)\right]
\ge\frac{\hbar^2}{4}.}
$$
The minimum holds **at all times precisely when $\alpha=m\omega/\hbar$**, the oscillator ground-state width. For any other $\alpha$, it is still attained at the isolated quarter-period times $t=n\pi/(2\omega)$, including zero. At a fixed generic time, minimizing over $\alpha$ again selects $s=1$.

The excess product has a <covariance> interpretation:
$$
C_{xp}(t)=\frac12\langle x'p'+p'x'\rangle_t
=\frac{\hbar}{4}(s-s^{-1})\sin2\omega t,\qquad
(\Delta x')^2(\Delta p')^2-C_{xp}^2=\frac{\hbar^2}{4}.
$$
Thus the <breathing Gaussian state in a harmonic oscillator> saturates the <Robertson-Schrödinger uncertainty relation> throughout, even when its position-momentum product exceeds the lower bound in the <Heisenberg uncertainty principle>.

= Solution

The <half-line square-root potential well> is confining for $a>0$, which is assumed here. The hard wall imposes $\psi(0)=0$ and the smooth <turning point> is $x_{\rm t}=(E/a)^2$. With $u=a\sqrt x/E$, the WKB <action> becomes
$$
\int_0^{x_{\rm t}}k(x)\,dx
=\frac{2\sqrt{2m}E^{5/2}}{\hbar a^2}
\int_0^1u(1-u)^{1/2}\,du
=\frac{8\sqrt{2m}E^{5/2}}{15\hbar a^2}.
$$
The <hard-wall and soft-turning-point WKB quantization> condition therefore gives
$$
\boxed{E_n^{\rm WKB}
=\left[\frac{15\pi\hbar a^2}{8\sqrt{2m}}
\left(n+\frac34\right)\right]^{2/5},\qquad n=0,1,\ldots.}
$$
The coefficient $a$ here denotes the potential strength, not the <integration> limit in the generic quantization formula.

The <WKB approximation> is reliable in the interior where $|k'|/k^2\ll1$, but fails near the soft <turning point> where $k\to0$; a connection formula supplies its phase. The hard wall also requires its <boundary condition>, and $V'=a/(2\sqrt x)$ makes the local slow-variation condition fail sufficiently close to zero. Higher-energy states have a larger <action> relative to $\hbar$, with smaller relative endpoint regions, so the leading WKB spectrum is generally more reliable at large $n$ than for the <ground state>.

= b
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For $b>0$, the <exponential variational trial for a square-root half-line well> satisfies the wall <boundary condition> and has norm $\int_0^\infty x^2e^{-2bx}dx=1/(4b^3)$. <integration by parts> has no boundary term and gives
$$
\langle T\rangle
=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}
\frac{\int_0^\infty(1-bx)^2e^{-2bx}dx}{1/(4b^3)}
=\frac{\hbar^2b^2}{2m}.
$$
The supplied <gamma integral> gives
$$
\langle V\rangle=\frac{15a}{16}\sqrt{\frac\pi2}\,b^{-1/2},\qquad
E(b)=\frac{\hbar^2b^2}{2m}
+\frac{15a}{16}\sqrt{\frac\pi2}\,b^{-1/2}.
$$
Setting $E'(b)=0$ yields the unique minimum,
$$
\boxed{b_*=\left(\frac{ma}{\hbar^2}\frac{15}{32}
\sqrt{\frac\pi2}\right)^{2/5}.}
$$
At the minimum $\langle V\rangle=4\langle T\rangle$, so the variational ground-state estimate is
$$
\boxed{E_{\rm var}=\frac{5\hbar^2b_*^2}{2m}
=1.876493\left(\frac{\hbar^2a^4}{2m}\right)^{1/5}.}
$$
The <energy> diverges as $b\to0$ or $b\to\infty$, confirming that the stationary point minimizes the trial <energy>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Write $E_*=(\hbar^2a^4/(2m))^{1/5}$. Then **$E_0^{\rm WKB}=1.811694E_*$ and $E_{\rm var}=1.876493E_*$**. The <variational method> guarantees $E_0\le E_{\rm var}$, since the trial satisfies the wall condition and has finite <energy>. WKB gives no comparable upper-bound guarantee and is least controlled for the lowest state. The two estimates differ by about $3.6\%$ of the WKB value; their closeness is encouraging but does not prove that the exact <energy> lies between them.

Split the <time-evolution operator> into $N$ factors with $\epsilon=T/N$, and insert $N-1$ <position> <completeness relations> between them. Each factor is evaluated by inserting <momentum> states normalized by $\langle x|p\rangle=(2\pi\hbar)^{-1/2}e^{ipx/\hbar}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle x_{j+1}|e^{-i\epsilon p^2/(2m\hbar)}|x_j\rangle
&=\int_{\mathbb R}\frac{dp}{2\pi\hbar}
\exp\left[\frac{ip(x_{j+1}-x_j)}{\hbar}
-\frac{i\epsilon p^2}{2m\hbar}\right]\\
&=\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar\epsilon}}\,
\exp\left[\frac{im(x_{j+1}-x_j)^2}{2\hbar\epsilon}\right].
\end{aligned}
$$
Multiplying the $N$ kernels and integrating the intermediate <positions> produces the stated time-sliced <path integral>, including its $N/2$ normalization power. For a <free particle> this <Gaussian> short-time kernel is exact, so the convolution identity already holds at finite $N$. Oscillatory <integrals> are defined with a vanishing convergence regulator, consistently fixing the square-root phase.

= b
{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The exponent is $iS_N/\hbar$, where
$$
S_N=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\epsilon\,\frac m2
\left(\frac{x_{j+1}-x_j}{\epsilon}\right)^2
$$
is the classical <action> of the piecewise-linear interpolating path. For smooth paths its limit is $S=\int_0^T m\dot x^2\,dt/2$. The discretization defines the quantum <integral>; it does not assert that typical quantum paths are differentiable. Weighting histories by their <action> phase is the central formulation of the original path-integral paper.<Space-Time Approach to Non-Relativistic Quantum Mechanics by Richard Feynman>[1948, abstract: amplitudes from histories weighted by the exponential of classical action in units of reduced Planck's constant]{ref}

When <action> differences on the experimental scales are large compared with $\hbar$, nearby nonstationary phases cancel. <stationary phase> selects $\delta S=0$ at fixed endpoints, hence $m\ddot x=0$. The surviving free classical trajectory is $x_{\rm cl}(t)=x_0+(x_T-x_0)t/T$, with <action> $m(x_T-x_0)^2/(2T)$. This is the semiclassical mechanism; it does not mean each individual path becomes a classical path.

{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The <Fresnel convolution of free-particle time slices> follows by completing the square:
$$
\frac{(x_k-x_0)^2}{k}+(x_{k+1}-x_k)^2
=\frac{k+1}{k}\left(x_k-\frac{x_0+kx_{k+1}}{k+1}\right)^2
+\frac{(x_{k+1}-x_0)^2}{k+1}.
$$
The second term is independent of $x_k$. The shifted <Fresnel integral> in the first term gives
$$
\boxed{\sqrt{\frac{2\pi i\hbar\epsilon}{m}\frac{k}{k+1}}\,
\exp\left[\frac{im(x_{k+1}-x_0)^2}
{2\hbar\epsilon(k+1)}\right].}
$$
Here $k$ is the positive slice index, not a <wave number>. The phase is fixed by the same convergence prescription as the short-time kernel.

{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

After integrating $x_1,\ldots,x_{N-1}$, the remaining exponent contains $(x_T-x_0)^2/N$. The square-root factors telescope:
$$
\begin{aligned}
&\left(\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar\epsilon}\right)^{N/2}
\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}
\left(\frac{2\pi i\hbar\epsilon}{m}\frac{k}{k+1}\right)^{1/2}\\
&\hspace{2em}
=\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar\epsilon}}\,
\sqrt{\frac1N}
=\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar T}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $N\epsilon=T$, the <free-particle propagator> is
$$
\boxed{K(x_T,x_0;T)=
\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar T}}\,
\exp\left[\frac{im(x_T-x_0)^2}{2\hbar T}\right].}
$$
For $T>0$, the square root has phase $e^{-i\pi/4}$. Its short-time limit is the <position> delta function in the distributional sense, consistent with unitary evolution.

A <scattering phase shift> measures the change of a <partial wave>'s outgoing phase relative to the free radial solution. For a real <central potential> and <elastic scattering>, <probability> conservation gives a unit-modulus <Partial-wave S-matrix> $S_l=e^{2i\delta_l}$. Indeed
$$
e^{i\delta_l}\sin(z+\delta_l)
=\frac{e^{2i\delta_l}e^{iz}-e^{-iz}}{2i},
\qquad z=kr-l\pi/2,
$$
so the incoming amplitude is unchanged and the outgoing one acquires $S_l$.

Expanding an incident <plane wave> in <Legendre polynomials> and comparing the outgoing <spherical wave> gives
$$
f(\theta)=\frac1k\sum_{l=0}^\infty
(2l+1)e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l\,P_l(\cos\theta).
$$
Use $d\sigma/d\Omega=|f|^2$ and $\int d\Omega\,P_lP_{l'}=4\pi\delta_{ll'}/(2l+1)$ to remove cross terms:
$$
\boxed{\sigma=\frac{4\pi}{k^2}
\sum_{l=0}^\infty(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l.}
$$
This is the elastic total <scattering cross-section> for the one-particle central-potential problem, with no identical-particle symmetrization factor.

= Solution

For $l=0$, put $u(r)=rR_0(r)$. The radial <Schrödinger equation> reduces to $u''+k'^2u=0$ inside and $u''+k^2u=0$ outside, where $k'^2=2m(E+V_0)/\hbar^2$ and $k^2=2mE/\hbar^2$. Regularity at the origin requires $u(0)=0$, excluding the inner cosine. A real outer sine-cosine combination can be written with an amplitude and a <phase shift>:
$$
\boxed{R_0(r)=
\begin{cases}
A\,\sin(k'r)/r,&0r\le a,\\
B\,\sin(kr+\delta_0)/r,&ra.
\end{cases}}
$$
The inner expression has the finite limiting value $Ak'$ at the origin. An overall complex normalization can carry the asymptotic phase convention of part a.

{parent=6}

= Solution

At a finite potential step, both $u$ and $u'$ are continuous:
$$
A\sin(k'a)=B\sin(ka+\delta_0),\qquad
Ak'\cos(k'a)=Bk\cos(ka+\delta_0).
$$
Taking the <logarithmic derivative> eliminates the amplitudes:
$$
\boxed{k'a\cot(k'a)=ka\cot(ka+\delta_0).}
$$
At a zero of a denominator, the original two matching equations provide the continuous limiting condition.

= Solution

Write $\kappa=\sqrt{2mV_0}/\hbar$ and $q=\kappa a$. At low <energy>, the $S$ wave normally dominates. Under the additional stated near-resonance assumption $ka\ll|\delta_0|$, neglect $ka$ inside the cotangent of the matching condition and set $k'\simeq\kappa$:
$$
k\cot\delta_0\simeq\kappa\cot q,\qquad
\sin^2\delta_0\simeq
\frac{k^2}{k^2+\kappa^2\cot^2q}.
$$
Substitution in $\sigma_0=4\pi\sin^2\delta_0/k^2$ gives
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{\rm res}\simeq
\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{m}\,
\frac1{E+V_0\cot^2(\sqrt{2mV_0}\,a/\hbar)}.}
$$
This is a <near-resonance square-well approximation>, not a uniform formula for every well radius. A <phase shift> is defined modulo $\pi$; its representative must be chosen consistently when applying the comparison with $ka$.

= Solution
{parent=e}

For small fixed positive $E$, the stated approximate curve has maxima at
$$
\boxed{\kappa a=\left(n+\frac12\right)\pi,\qquad
\sigma_{\rm res,max}=\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{mE}=\frac{4\pi}{k^2},
\quad n=0,1,\ldots.}
$$
Its formal minima are zero at $\kappa a=n\pi$. The peak height is the $S$-wave <unitarity> limit, and the peaks narrow as $E/V_0$ decreases.

\Image[paper-1-square-well-resonances.png]
{title=Square-well resonance limits}

To identify the bound-state threshold, let the bound-state <energy> be $-\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon>0$. The reduced radial wave is proportional to $\sin(Kr)$ inside and $e^{-\chi r}$ outside, with $K=\sqrt{2m(V_0-\varepsilon)}/\hbar$ and $\chi=\sqrt{2m\varepsilon}/\hbar$. Matching gives $K\cot(Ka)=-\chi$. As a new <bound state> emerges at $\varepsilon\downarrow0$,
$$
\boxed{\kappa\cot(\kappa a)=0
\ \Longrightarrow\
\kappa a=\left(n+\frac12\right)\pi.}
$$
Thus the limiting peak locations coincide with the appearance of successive **$S$-wave <bound states>**. At the exact threshold the zero-energy radial solution is not <square-integrable>; a <normalizable> <bound state> appears just beyond it. Finite-energy maxima shift slightly from the zero-energy <positions>.

The formal minima require care because the assumption used in part d fails there. Retaining $ka$ in the exact matching condition gives the <spherical square-well scattering length>
$$
a_s=a-\frac{\tan(\kappa a)}{\kappa},\qquad
\sigma_0\longrightarrow4\pi a_s^2
$$
away from a threshold. At $\kappa a=n\pi$, $a_s=a$, so the true low-energy <S-wave scattering> contribution is $4\pi a^2$, not zero. Genuine leading-order zeros of that contribution instead satisfy $\tan q=q$, for example $q\simeq4.493409$ and $7.725252$. The original sketch compares the exact finite-energy $S$ wave with the requested approximation, making this nonuniformity visible. Dominance by the $S$ wave must be checked separately near a higher-partial-wave threshold. The scattering-length divergence and its bound-state interpretation are also derived in institutional lecture notes.<Topics in Quantum Mechanics by David Tong>[2017, §6.2.6, printed pp. 219–222: square-well matching, scattering length and threshold bound states]{ref}
