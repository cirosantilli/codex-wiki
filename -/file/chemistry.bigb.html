= Chemistry
{wiki}

Chemistry connects the composition and structure of <chemical substances> to their properties and transformations. Its central surprise is that rearranging the same <chemical atoms> can produce a different substance: <molecular hydrogen> and <molecular oxygen> can become <water> without changing which <chemical elements> are present. <Chemical bonds> explain the connections; <chemical reactions> describe the transformations; <stoichiometry> makes quantitative predictions from the bookkeeping.

This gives a useful route from an invisible world to measurable quantities. A <molecule> has a definite composition, such as two <hydrogen> <chemical atoms> and one <oxygen> <chemical atom> in <water>. A sample contains an enormous number of such <molecules>. Counting them through <amount of substance>, measured in <moles>, lets the same small-number ratios describe a macroscopic sample. The beauty is that the bookkeeping survives this huge change of scale.

= Molecule
{parent=Chemistry}
{wiki}

A molecule consists of two or more <chemical atoms> joined by <chemical bonds>, with zero net <electric charge>. This follows the terminology of the \a[https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/M04002][IUPAC Gold Book, molecule]{ref}. The <continuum approximation> uses bulk fields in place of individual molecular positions and motions.

= Chemical bond
{parent=Chemistry}
{wiki}

A <chemical bond> associates <chemical atoms> or groups of <chemical atoms> into a sufficiently stable structure to treat it as a distinct entity. The concept covers connections within <molecules> and in extended solids; see the \a[https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/CT07009][IUPAC Gold Book, chemical bond]{ref}. It is useful because composition alone does not specify how the <chemical atoms> are connected, and those connections help determine a <chemical substance>'s properties and possible <chemical reactions>.

In a <covalent bond>, <electrons> are shared between <chemical atoms>. Two <hydrogen> <chemical atoms> can form a <molecular hydrogen> <molecule>, with its <electrons> interacting with both <atomic nuclei>. In the usual description of this diatomic <molecule>, the <potential energy> as a function of nuclear separation has a minimum: moving the nuclei too close or too far raises the <energy>. See \a[https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/7-2-covalent-bonding][Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, covalent bonding]{ref}.

Separating bonded <chemical atoms> requires <energy>; forming the corresponding <chemical bonds> releases <energy>. Whether an overall <chemical reaction> releases <energy> depends on both the <chemical bonds> broken and those formed. The lines in the <stoichiometry> diagram indicate connections schematically, rather than literal rods or a sequence of microscopic events.

= Polymer
{parent=Chemistry}
{wiki}

A <polymer> is a large molecular chain or network built from many chemically linked repeating units. Statistical chain models idealize its microscopic structure by segment vectors and describe its conformation through distributions, correlations and scattering. A <freely jointed chain> fixes each segment length while allowing independent orientations; a <Gaussian chain> replaces the segment or coarse-grained displacement distribution by a <Gaussian distribution>. These ideal models neglect excluded-volume and other segment interactions.

= Flory approximation
{c}
{parent=Polymer}
{title2=$\mathcal F/(k_BT)\sim R^2/(Nb^2)+vN^2/R^d$}

The <Flory approximation> balances the entropic cost of expanding an <ideal chain> against a mean-field <excluded volume> interaction. For $N$ segments of length $b$ in $d$ spatial dimensions, minimizing $R^2/(Nb^2)+vN^2/R^d$ gives $R^{d+2}\sim vN^3b^2$. This yields the scaling exponents $3/5$ in three dimensions and $3/4$ in two dimensions. It is a scaling approximation, not an exact numerical free energy or proof of critical exponents; confinement replaces $R^d$ by the accessible coil volume.

= Polymerization
{parent=Polymer}

<Polymerization> joins molecular units into a <polymer>. For a filament end exchanging units with a solution, addition and removal rates determine growth, while steric constraints and an applied force can modify those rates.

= Monomer
{parent=Polymer}

A <monomer> is a molecular unit that can join other units through <polymerization>. A statistical link in a coarse-grained chain model need not coincide with one chemical monomer.

= Radius of gyration
{parent=Polymer}
{wiki}
{title2=$R_g^2=\frac1{2N^2}\sum_{m,n}\langle|\mathbf R_m-\mathbf R_n|^2\rangle$}

For $N$ equally weighted positions, the squared <radius of gyration> is the average squared distance from the <center of mass>: $R_g^2=N^{-1}\sum_n\langle|\mathbf R_n-\mathbf R_{\rm cm}|^2\rangle$, where $\mathbf R_{\rm cm}=N^{-1}\sum_n\mathbf R_n$. Expanding pair differences gives $\sum_{m,n}|\mathbf R_m-\mathbf R_n|^2=2N\sum_n|\mathbf R_n-\mathbf R_{\rm cm}|^2$, and therefore
$$
R_g^2=\frac1{2N^2}\sum_{m,n}\langle|\mathbf R_m-\mathbf R_n|^2\rangle.
$$
For a <Gaussian chain> or <freely jointed chain> with these $N$ positions and mean squared link length $b^2$, each pair <variance> is $b^2|m-n|$. Summing separations yields $R_g^2=b^2(N^2-1)/(6N)\sim Nb^2/6$. Counting all endpoints of $N$ links instead gives $N+1$ positions and the corresponding finite-size formula.

= Ion
{parent=Chemistry}

A <chemical atom> or molecular entity with nonzero net <electric charge>. An <ion> opposing a fixed charge is called a <counterion>.

= Counterion
{parent=Ion}

A mobile <ion> whose charge has the opposite sign to a charged macromolecule, surface or other fixed species. Its local <concentration> can increase close to that fixed charge; <counterion condensation> distinguishes a bound fraction that remains localized in an infinite-dilution limit.

= Electrochemistry
{parent=Chemistry}

Chemical systems whose reactions, transport or equilibrium couple to <electric charges> and <electric potential>. An <electrolyte> carries mobile <ions>; a <Poisson-Boltzmann equation> can approximate their equilibrium distributions.

= Electrolyte
{parent=Electrochemistry}

A medium with mobile <ions> capable of carrying <electric charge>. Dissolved salts typically supply both positive and negative species; a counterion-only model keeps only the mobile species required to oppose a fixed charge. <Poisson-Boltzmann equations> use ideal <Boltzmann distributions> for those species.

= Chemical reaction
{parent=Chemistry}
{wiki}

A <chemical reaction> interconverts <chemical species>, as defined by the \a[https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/C01033][IUPAC Gold Book, chemical reaction]{ref}. The consumed species are <reactants>; the formed species are <reaction products>. In ordinary <chemical reactions>, <chemical atoms> are rearranged while each <chemical element>'s total atom count is conserved. For a complete <chemical equation>, net <electric charge> is conserved too.

For example, the balanced <chemical equation>
$$
2\mathrm{H}_2+\mathrm{O}_2\longrightarrow2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}
$$
describes conversion of <molecular hydrogen> and <molecular oxygen> into <water>. Both sides contain four <hydrogen> <chemical atoms> and two <oxygen> <chemical atoms>. Three <reactant> <molecules> become two <reaction product> <molecules>: atom counts are conserved, but molecule counts need not be. See \a[https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/4-1-writing-and-balancing-chemical-equations][Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, balancing chemical equations]{ref}.

The coefficients describe net consumption and formation, without asserting that three <molecules> collide in one step. A balanced <chemical equation> supplies quantitative constraints; it does not determine how quickly the <chemical reaction> occurs, or whether <chemical equilibrium> prevents complete conversion. Those questions need further information. Keeping these roles distinct makes <stoichiometry> useful even when the microscopic details are complicated.

= Thermal dissociation
{parent=Chemical reaction}

Thermal dissociation is temperature-driven breaking of a molecule into simpler fragments. The <chemical equilibrium> abundance depends on pressure as well as temperature: reactions increasing particle number are generally favored at low pressure. In an <exoplanet atmosphere>, reduced <water> on a very hot dayside does not by itself establish a low planetary oxygen abundance.

= Stoichiometry
{parent=Chemical reaction}
{wiki}
{title2=$\nu_i$}

<Stoichiometry> relates the quantities consumed and formed in a <chemical reaction>. Its coefficients are ratios of entity counts, or equivalently of <amount of substance>, rather than ratios of <masses>. For the <water> <chemical equation>, consuming two <molecular hydrogen> <molecules> per <molecular oxygen> <molecule> gives the same $2:1$ ratio as consuming two <moles> of <molecular hydrogen> per <mole> of <molecular oxygen>. Multiplying every entity count by the same factor preserves the ratio. See \a[https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/4-3-reaction-stoichiometry][Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, reaction stoichiometry]{ref}.

Coefficients and subscripts do different jobs. The coefficient in $2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}$ counts two <water> <molecules>; the subscript says that each contains two <hydrogen> <chemical atoms>. Changing coefficients scales quantities. Changing a subscript changes the specified <chemical substance>.

\Image[stoichiometry-particle-counts.png]
{title=The same stoichiometric ratio at molecular and molar scales}
{description=The upper schematic counts individual <molecules>; the lower line multiplies each count by the <Avogadro constant> times one <mole>. Circle sizes and positions are illustrative.}

This distinction catches a common counting error: one <mole> of <water> <molecules> contains two <moles> of <hydrogen> <chemical atoms> and one <mole> of <oxygen> <chemical atoms>. Always specify what is counted.

<Stoichiometry> records reactant and product multiplicities in a <chemical reaction> or a <chemical reaction network>. The change caused by one reaction is its <stoichiometric vector>; multiplying by the reaction rate gives its contribution to the concentration equations. For two identical reactants the rate-constant convention absorbs any combinatorial factor.

= Molecular count and amount of substance
{parent=Stoichiometry}
{title2=$N=nN_{\mathrm A}$}

The molecular count $N$ is a dimensionless integer; the <amount of substance> $n$ has unit <mole>, symbol $\mathrm{mol}$. They satisfy
$$
N=nN_{\mathrm A},\qquad N_{\mathrm A}=6.02214076\times10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol}^{-1}.
$$
The <Avogadro constant> is exact by the modern definition of the <mole>: see \a[https://www.bipm.org/en/si-base-units/mole][BIPM, SI base unit: mole]{ref}. Thus two <water> <molecules> correspond to $2/N_{\mathrm A}$ <moles>, whereas two <moles> of <water> contain $1.204428152\times10^{24}$ <molecules>. A <mole> is a unit for counting specified entities, which may also be <chemical atoms>, <ions> or <electrons>.

= Extent of reaction
{parent=Stoichiometry}
{title2=$\xi$}
{wiki}

For a closed system with one specified <chemical reaction>, the <extent of reaction> $\xi$ records progress from $\xi=0$ through $n_i=n_{i,0}+\nu_i\xi$. Here $n_i$ is the <amount of substance> of species $i$, and the signed <stoichiometric vector> has negative entries for <reactants> and positive entries for <reaction products>. For the <water> <chemical equation> in the order $\mathrm{H}_2,\mathrm{O}_2,\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}$, it is $(-2,-1,2)$. The normalization of $\xi$ depends on the chosen coefficients. See \a[https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/E02283][IUPAC Gold Book, extent of reaction]{ref}.

= Limiting reagent
{parent=Stoichiometry}
{wiki}

= Limiting reactant
{synonym}

The <limiting reagent> is the <reactant> exhausted first if the specified <chemical reaction> proceeds to the maximum conversion permitted by <stoichiometry>. With initial values of <amount of substance> $n_{i,0}$ for the <reactants> and positive consumption coefficients $a_i$, the largest forward <extent of reaction> is $\xi_{\max}=\min_i(n_{i,0}/a_i)$. Compare these quotients, rather than simply choosing the smallest initial quantity. This calculation predicts the maximum <reaction product> quantity and identifies which <reactant> supply must increase to raise it. See \a[https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/4-4-reaction-yields][Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, limiting reactants]{ref}.

Start with $3\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <molecular hydrogen>, $1\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <molecular oxygen> and no <water>. Assume complete conversion by the displayed <chemical reaction>, without competing reactions. Then
$$
\xi_{\max}=\min\left(\frac{3\,\mathrm{mol}}2,\frac{1\,\mathrm{mol}}1\right)=1\,\mathrm{mol}.
$$
The <molecular oxygen> is the <limiting reagent>. Consumption of $2\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <molecular hydrogen> and $1\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <molecular oxygen> forms $2\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <water>, leaving $1\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <molecular hydrogen>. Initially there are $1.806642228\times10^{24}$ <molecular hydrogen> <molecules> and $6.02214076\times10^{23}$ <molecular oxygen> <molecules>; after conversion there are $1.204428152\times10^{24}$ <water> <molecules> and $6.02214076\times10^{23}$ leftover <molecular hydrogen> <molecules>.

\Image[limiting-reagent-amounts.png]
{title=Oxygen runs out while one mole of molecular hydrogen remains}
{description=The horizontal axis is <extent of reaction>, rather than time. The endpoint assumes complete conversion by one <chemical reaction>; the curves express <stoichiometry>.}

As a check, the initial inventory has $6\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <hydrogen> <chemical atoms> and $2\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <oxygen> <chemical atoms>. The final <water> contains $4\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <hydrogen> <chemical atoms>, and leftover <molecular hydrogen> supplies the remaining $2\,\mathrm{mol}$. The atom totals balance even though the total <amount of substance> of <molecules> falls from four to three <moles>.

= Beryllium-7
{parent=Beryllium}
{title2=$^7\mathrm{Be}$}

Beryllium-7 contains four <protons> and three <neutrons>. It forms in reactions including $^3\mathrm{He}+{}^4\mathrm{He}\to{}^7\mathrm{Be}+\gamma$ and becomes <lithium-7> through <electron capture>. In the early universe its ionization state affects when capture occurs, so the final primordial lithium inventory includes the earlier beryllium inventory rather than just lithium present when nuclear burning ends.

= Lithium
{parent=Chemical element}
{title2=$\mathrm{Li}$}
{wiki}

Lithium is the <chemical element> with atomic number three. Its stable isotopes include lithium-6 and <lithium-7>. Lithium observed in old stellar atmospheres is an important but processing-sensitive test of <Big Bang nucleosynthesis>.

= Lithium-7
{parent=Lithium}
{title2=$^7\mathrm{Li}$}

Lithium-7 has three <protons> and four <neutrons>. In <Big Bang nucleosynthesis> it can form directly or through <beryllium-7>, which later undergoes <electron capture>. Competition between direct lithium production, lithium destruction and beryllium production produces a valley in the primordial lithium abundance versus <baryon-to-photon ratio>. Its measured stellar surface abundance need not equal its birth abundance because stellar transport and nuclear burning can remove lithium.

= Nickel-56 decay chain
{parent=Nickel}
{title2=$^{56}\mathrm{Ni}\longrightarrow{}^{56}\mathrm{Co}\longrightarrow{}^{56}\mathrm{Fe}$}

The radioactive chain converts nickel-56 through cobalt-56 into iron-56, releasing energy that can power supernova light curves. <Electron capture> and, where allowed, <beta-plus decay> convert protons into neutrons; gamma rays and charged decay products deposit some of the released energy. Ionization and escape of decay radiation affect the observed heating.

= Snowflake
{parent=Ice}
{wiki}

A snowflake is an <ice> crystal or an aggregate of <ice> crystals grown in atmospheric conditions. Its morphology depends on vapour supersaturation, transport, <temperature> and crystal anisotropy. Not every snowflake is dendritic.

= Dendritic growth of a snowflake
{parent=Snowflake}

An exposed tip receives vapour more efficiently than a sheltered surface and can amplify its lead, producing <crystal dendrites> and side branches. The planar thermal <solidification> mechanism is an analogy, not a complete vapour-growth model. The <hexagonal ice crystal anisotropy> selects six preferred directions; an isotropic linear instability alone does not select six arms.

= Steady state
{disambiguate=chemistry}
{parent=Chemistry}
{wiki}

A <steady state (chemistry)> has time-independent concentrations because formation and removal rates balance for the species considered. Nonzero reaction fluxes can continue, so a steady state need not be <chemical equilibrium>. A <quasi-steady-state approximation> imposes this balance approximately for a fast intermediate while slower species evolve.
