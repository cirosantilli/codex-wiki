= Chemistry
{wiki}

Chemistry connects the composition and structure of <chemical substances> to their properties and transformations. Its central surprise is that rearranging the same <chemical atoms> can produce a different substance: <molecular hydrogen> and <molecular oxygen> can become <water> without changing which <chemical elements> are present. <Chemical bonds> explain the connections; <chemical reactions> describe the transformations; <stoichiometry> makes quantitative predictions from the bookkeeping.

This gives a useful route from an invisible world to measurable quantities. A <molecule> has a definite composition, such as two <hydrogen> <chemical atoms> and one <oxygen> <chemical atom> in <water>. A sample contains an enormous number of such <molecules>. Counting them through <amount of substance>, measured in <moles>, lets the same small-number ratios describe a macroscopic sample. The beauty is that the bookkeeping survives this huge change of scale.

= Atmospheric chemistry
{parent=Chemistry}
{wiki}

The chemistry of gases, particles and droplets in planetary atmospheres. Chemical conversion competes with radiation, transport, mixing and removal to surfaces, so local equilibrium is not a general assumption.

= Linear three-reservoir carbon model
{parent=Atmospheric chemistry}
{title2=$D\dot{\mathbf C}=E\mathbf e_a-L\mathbf C$}

A capacity-weighted exchange model for atmospheric, fast surface and deep <ocean> <carbon dioxide> anomalies. Positive diagonal capacities form $D$, while $L$ is the weighted path-graph <Laplacian matrix> with exchange conductances $r,s$. Then $\mathbf C^TL\mathbf C=r(C_a-C_b)^2+s(C_b-C_d)^2\geq0$. The homogeneous generator $-D^{-1}L$ is similar to a symmetric negative-semidefinite <matrix>, with two decaying <normal modes> and one zero <normal mode> for equal concentrations. Summing all budgets proves that the capacity-weighted total is conserved after emissions stop. The long-time concentration from total input $M$ is $M/(R_a+R_b+R_d)$, rather than zero.

= Climate-methane chemical-lifetime feedback
{parent=Atmospheric chemistry}

Changes in <temperature>, <water vapor> and <radical> chemistry alter <methane> removal. Warming can increase its <hydroxyl radical> reaction coefficient and the <hydroxyl radical> source from <oxygen singlet D atom> plus <water>, tending to reduce chemical lifetime at fixed precursor conditions. Changed emissions and competing <radical> sinks can modify or reverse that conditional response.

= Tropospheric OH budget
{parent=Atmospheric chemistry}
{title2=$[\mathrm{OH}]=P/K$}

A local or box-model balance between hydroxyl-radical production and effective losses. For fixed production $P$ and pseudo-first-order total loss frequency $K$, $[\mathrm{OH}]=P/K$. Additional hydrogen, <carbon monoxide> or <methane> consumption can lower <hydroxyl radical> if production and other terms stay fixed; <radical> recycling and spatial transport can alter that simple result.

= Hydrogen-induced methane lifetime response
{parent=Tropospheric OH budget}

In a fixed-production <hydroxyl radical> box, adding hydrogen by $\Delta[\mathrm H_2]$ changes $K$ to $K+k_{\mathrm H_2+OH}\Delta[\mathrm H_2]$. <Methane> lifetime scales as $K/P$ when its <hydroxyl radical> reaction coefficient is held fixed. The pre-existing hydrogen contribution is already in $K$ and must not be counted again.

= Humidity-temperature response of OH production
{parent=Atmospheric chemistry}

At fixed <relative humidity> and fixed excited-oxygen <concentration>, a water-reaction <hydroxyl radical> source scales as $e_s(T)/T$ when <concentrations> are number densities. Thus $d\ln P_{\rm OH}/dT=\Delta H_{\rm vap}/(RT^2)-1/T$ under constant <latent heat> and rate coefficient. Using only $e_s$ instead gives a <vapor pressure> or fixed-pressure mole-fraction approximation. Neither derivative follows without a <humidity> condition.

= Mixed chlorine-bromine ozone-loss cycle
{parent=Atmospheric chemistry}

In the productive mixed-radical pathway, <chlorine monoxide> and <BrO> yield <chlorine> and <bromine> atoms; subsequent atom reactions with <ozone> regenerate the oxides. The net cycle consumes two <ozone> <molecules> per radical-pair event, giving $L=2k[\mathrm{ClO}][\mathrm{BrO}]$ when the subsequent steps are fast. The branching of a real <chlorine monoxide>–<BrO> reaction must be included if not already absorbed into its specified effective rate coefficient.

= Chlorine nitrate
{parent=Atmospheric chemistry}
{title2=$\mathrm{ClONO_2}$}

The reservoir species $\mathrm{ClONO_2}$, formed from ClO and NO2 with third-body stabilization. Heterogeneous reaction with <hydrogen chloride> can produce molecular <chlorine> and <nitric acid>, activating <chlorine> while removing reactive <nitrogen> from the gas phase. It belongs to the wider reactive <nitrogen> family, not to the narrow NO-plus-NO2 definition of NOx.

= Air-sea gas exchange
{parent=Atmospheric chemistry}

Transfer of a gas across the atmosphere–ocean interface, driven by a departure from the gas's interfacial equilibrium and controlled by transfer resistances and mixing. This can move an added atmospheric carbon-dioxide perturbation into the ocean without immediately returning all of that carbon to a permanent geological store.

= Chlorine monoxide dimer cycle
{parent=Atmospheric chemistry}
{title2=$\mathrm{ClO+ClO+M\rightleftharpoons ClOOCl+M}$}

A low-temperature catalytic <ozone> destruction pathway involving ClO association to ClOOCl, <photodissociation> of the dimer and regeneration of <chlorine> atoms. Combined with two Cl + O3 reactions its net effect is $2\mathrm O_3\to3\mathrm O_2$. Low <temperature> suppresses thermal dimer dissociation, while sunlight is needed for the photolytic step. <Polar stratospheric clouds> can activate reservoir <chlorine> before the sunlit cycle becomes rapid.

= Photolytic branching in the chlorine dimer cycle
{parent=Chlorine monoxide dimer cycle}
{title2=$f=J/(J+k_d[M])$}

For dimer <photodissociation> frequency $J$ and competing <thermal dissociation> frequency $k_d[M]$, the successful branch fraction is $f=J/(J+k_d[M])$. With steady dimer abundance and rapid subsequent recycling, <ozone> destruction is $2f k_a[M][\mathrm{ClO}]^2$. The factor two counts <ozone> <molecules> consumed per productive dimer event.

= Chlorine peroxide
{parent=Chlorine monoxide dimer cycle}
{title2=$\mathrm{ClOOCl}$}

The peroxy-linked <chlorine> oxide $\mathrm{ClOOCl}$, often written $\mathrm{Cl_2O_2}$ in a <chlorine monoxide dimer cycle>. Thermal return to two <chlorine monoxide> <radicals> competes with a photolytic pathway that ultimately yields two <chlorine> atoms. The formula alone should not be taken as specifying every possible dichlorine-dioxide isomer.

= Dry deposition
{parent=Atmospheric chemistry}

Removal of a gas or particle to a surface without precipitation. A simple parameterization uses downward flux $F=v_d C$, where $C$ is the <concentration> near the surface and $v_d$ the <deposition velocity>. For a well-mixed layer of depth $H$, the loss is first order with coefficient $v_d/H$.

= Deposition velocity
{parent=Dry deposition}
{title2=$v_d=F/C$}

The ratio of a specified downward <dry deposition> flux to its reference near-surface <concentration>. It has units of speed but need not be an actual particle settling speed: transfer resistance and surface uptake can both control gas <dry deposition>.

= Ozone-depletion potential
{parent=Atmospheric chemistry}
{title2=$\mathrm{ODP}$}

= ODP
{synonym}
{c}

A relative measure of stratospheric <ozone> destruction associated with emissions of a substance, conventionally referenced to CFC-11. It depends on atmospheric survival, transport, halogen delivery and catalytic effectiveness, not only the number of <chlorine> atoms in the formula. Fluorine-only replacements can have negligible direct <ozone-depletion potential> while remaining strong <greenhouse gases>.

= Atmospheric lifetime
{parent=Atmospheric chemistry}
{title2=$\tau$}

For a well-mixed atmospheric burden $B$ removed by a single first-order sink, $dB/dt=-B/\tau$, the <atmospheric lifetime> is the <exponential decay> time $\tau$. At steady emission rate $E$, this model gives $B=E\tau$. Independent sinks acting on the same burden have additive inverse lifetimes. Local removal times in separate compartments cannot be combined this way without considering exchange and partitioning. A multi-reservoir perturbation, especially atmospheric <carbon dioxide>, generally needs more than one timescale.

= Methane perturbation lifetime
{parent=Atmospheric lifetime}

The e-folding relaxation time of an added <methane> burden after accounting for chemical feedbacks. It need not equal burden divided by the contemporaneous <methane> loss rate: extra <methane> can consume <hydroxyl radical> and reduce its own removal. A fixed-OH exponential estimate omits that coupled adjustment.

= Aerosol
{parent=Atmospheric chemistry}

= Aerosol optical depth
{parent=Aerosol}
{title2=$\tau_a=\int\beta_{\rm ext}(z)\,dz$}

The dimensionless column <optical depth> from <aerosol> extinction measures attenuation by absorption and scattering at a stated wavelength. It depends on particle size, composition, humidity and vertical distribution; it is not a direct count of <cloud condensation nuclei>. Using it as a proxy for droplet-number forcing requires control of meteorological covariates and cloud-contaminated retrievals.

= Number size distribution
{parent=Aerosol}
{title2=$n(D)=dN/d\ln D$}

A particle count per stated volume and logarithmic size interval. Its <integral> over $d\ln D$ gives a <number density>, unlike an <aerosol> mass distribution. In a <cloud-droplet activation> estimate, integrating the dry-size distribution above the size-dependent <Köhler theory> threshold gives activated number for a specified peak <atmospheric supersaturation> and composition.

= Aerosol coagulation
{parent=Aerosol}

Growth by collision and attachment of existing particles. Coagulation reduces particle number while conserving particulate material in a closed system without other sources or sinks. Larger particles can have a different scattering-per-mass efficiency and faster <sedimentation>, so increasing precursor injection need not increase <albedo> proportionally.

= Sulfate aerosol
{parent=Aerosol}

Atmospheric particles containing <sulfate>, commonly in sulfuric-acid/<water> or neutralized-salt mixtures. Particle size, <water> uptake, acidity and accompanying material determine scattering, absorption, <cloud> activation and heterogeneous chemistry; <sulfate> <mass> alone does not specify <radiative forcing>.

= Aerosol hygroscopicity
{parent=Aerosol}
{title2=$\kappa$}

A particle's tendency to take up <water vapor>, which depends on composition, solubility and ambient humidity. In a common parameterization, a dimensionless $\kappa$ summarizes soluble-water uptake for use with dry diameter in <Köhler theory>. A single composition parameter does not remove the importance of particle size or parcel supersaturation.

= Aerosol-cloud interaction
{parent=Aerosol}

A change in <cloud> microphysics or <cloud> properties caused by <aerosol>, including droplet activation, precipitation changes and adjustments of <water> content or coverage. The <Twomey effect> isolates a fixed-water-content optical response; it does not by itself establish the sign or magnitude of every <cloud> adjustment.

= Marine cloud brightening
{parent=Aerosol-cloud interaction}

<Marine cloud brightening> proposes increasing suitable <cloud condensation nuclei> over the ocean to increase reflected sunlight. At fixed cloud liquid-water content and thickness, the <Twomey effect> makes more, smaller droplets increase <cloud albedo>. Changes in cloud water, cloud fraction, entrainment and precipitation can reinforce or oppose this fixed-cloud response.

= Semi-direct aerosol effect
{parent=Aerosol-cloud interaction}

<Cloud> changes caused by <aerosol> absorption of radiation and the resulting heating. Heating can evaporate <clouds> and reduce shortwave reflection, but the effect can reverse when heating changes stability and <cloud> maintenance differently. The forcing sign depends on the relative positions of <cloud> and absorbing <aerosol> and on dynamical adjustment.

= Twomey effect
{parent=Aerosol-cloud interaction}
{c}

Increasing <cloud droplet> number at fixed liquid-water content and <cloud> thickness reduces mean droplet size and increases total extinction area, raising shortwave <optical depth> and <cloud albedo>. This idealized response produces negative shortwave <radiative forcing>. Changes in <cloud> amount, <water> content and dynamics are separate adjustments.<The Influence of Pollution on the Shortwave Albedo of Clouds by S. Twomey>[Fixed-water-content droplet-number and <albedo> response]{ref}

= Cloud albedo susceptibility
{parent=Twomey effect}
{title2=$dA/dN_d=A(1-A)/(3N_d)$}

For $\tau\propto N_d^{1/3}$ at fixed liquid-water content and thickness, and $A=\tau/(\tau+7)$, the derivative is $dA/dN_d=A(1-A)/(3N_d)$. For a fixed fractional number increase, the initial response is greatest at $A=1/2$. The exact finite-change expression is $A'=sA/(1-A+sA)$ with $s=(N_d'/N_d)^{1/3}$. Uncertain activation of additional particles and changes in <water> content limit direct application to real <clouds>.

= Cloud condensation nucleus
{parent=Aerosol}

= CCN
{synonym}
{c}

An <aerosol> particle able to activate into a growing <cloud droplet> at the supersaturation attained by a <cloud> parcel. Activity depends on particle dry size, hygroscopicity and interfacial properties. The number active at a specified supersaturation differs from total <aerosol> number.

= Cloud-droplet activation
{parent=Cloud condensation nucleus}

An <aerosol> particle becomes a freely growing <cloud droplet> after crossing its <Köhler theory> growth barrier under the parcel's <atmospheric supersaturation> history. Counting particles whose <critical supersaturation> is below a specified peak gives an equilibrium-threshold estimate; calculating that peak requires cooling, <water vapor> depletion and condensational-growth kinetics. Neither total <aerosol> number nor dry size alone determines activation.

= Köhler theory
{parent=Cloud condensation nucleus}
{c}

The equilibrium water-vapor saturation ratio above a solution droplet combines a curvature effect, which raises <vapor pressure>, with dissolved-solute reduction of <water> activity. Its maximum defines a <critical supersaturation> for activation. In a dilute hygroscopicity approximation using droplet diameter $D$ and dry diameter $D_d$, $s(D)\simeq A_K/D-\kappa D_d^3/D^3$, where $A_K=4\sigma M_w/(RT\rho_w)$. Then $s_c\simeq\sqrt{4A_K^3/(27\kappa D_d^3)}$. These approximate forms assume the stated diameter convention and dilute solution; composition and <surface tension> must be consistent.<A single parameter representation of hygroscopic growth and cloud condensation nucleus activity by M. D. Petters and S. M. Kreidenweis>[Equations describing hygroscopic growth and critical activation supersaturation]{ref}

= Köhler curve
{parent=Köhler theory}
{c}
{title2=$S_{\rm eq}(r)$}

The equilibrium <saturation ratio> over a <chemical solution> droplet as a function of wet radius for fixed solute content. In the dilute radius approximation $S_{\rm eq}\simeq1+A/r-B/r^3$, its maximum occurs at $r_c=\sqrt{3B/A}$. The corresponding <critical supersaturation> is $2A/(3r_c)$. Diameter and radius coefficients differ and must not be mixed.

= Dilute Köhler curve
{parent=Köhler theory}
{title2=$S_{\rm eq}(r)=1+A/r-B/r^3$}

The first-order combination of the <Kelvin equation> and <Raoult's law> for a <chemical solution> droplet with $A/r\ll1$ and $B/r^3\ll1$. Its maximum is at $r_c=\sqrt{3B/A}$, with $S_c-1=2A/(3r_c)$. For ambient <saturation ratio> $1<S<S_c$, the smaller equilibrium radius is stable and the larger is unstable: the sign of condensational growth is the sign of $S-S_{\rm eq}(r)$. In particular, exceeding $r_c$ alone need not activate continued growth at subcritical <atmospheric supersaturation>.

= Radius and diameter conventions in Köhler activation
{parent=Köhler theory}

Writing the same curve using diameter $D=2r$ changes its coefficients: $A_D=2A_r$ and $B_D=8B_r$ in $A_D/D-B_D/D^3$. The critical saturation ratio is unchanged, while $D_c=2r_c$. Mixing a diameter curvature coefficient with a radius solute term gives a spurious activation threshold.

= Critical supersaturation
{parent=Köhler theory}
{title2=$s_c$}

The maximum equilibrium supersaturation along a solution-droplet growth curve. Above this threshold, an <aerosol> particle can grow into an activated <cloud droplet> under the conditions of the parcel. In <Köhler theory>, larger dry diameter or greater hygroscopicity generally lowers the threshold; <aerosol> number alone does not specify it.

= Köhler activation radius
{parent=Critical supersaturation}
{c}
{title2=$r_c=\sqrt{3B/A},\quad\ln S_c=2A/(3r_c)$}

In the dilute radius form $\ln S_{\rm eq}=A/r-B/r^3$, the curvature coefficient A has length units and B has volume units. Setting its derivative to zero gives the displayed maximum. Ambient supersaturation above it permits activation into a growing <cloud droplet>. Below it, a sufficiently small wet equilibrium can be stable, while the larger-radius intersection is unstable. The approximation should not be extrapolated into the dry core.

= Odd oxygen
{parent=Atmospheric chemistry}
{title2=$O_x=\mathrm O+\mathrm O_3$}

A chemical-family bookkeeping variable in a simple stratospheric model. = Chapman cycle
{c}
{parent=Odd oxygen}

A basic oxygen–ozone mechanism combining oxygen photodissociation, three-body ozone formation, ozone photodissociation and $\mathrm O+\mathrm O_3\to2\mathrm O_2$. The last reaction removes two units of <odd oxygen>; a catalytic cycle can add another loss pathway.

= Nighttime Chapman ozone
{parent=Chapman cycle}

With photolysis switched off, constant molecular oxygen and collider reservoirs give $\dot x=-x(a+by)$ and $\dot y=x(a-by)$ for atomic oxygen $x$, ozone $y$, $a=k_1[\mathrm O_2][M]$ and $b=k_3$. Dividing the equations and integrating gives
$$
x-x_0=y-y_0+\frac{2a}{b}\ln\frac{a-by}{a-by_0}.
$$
For $y_0<a/b$, ozone grows toward a finite-reservoir endpoint below $a/b$. Its value at sunrise depends on the dark-interval duration; $a/b$ is an approximate quasi-steady balance while atomic oxygen remains available.

= Chapman family lifetime versus relaxation time
{c}
{parent=Chapman cycle}

Under fast <Chapman cycle> partitioning, an <odd oxygen> family abundance $X$ can satisfy $\dot X=P-kX^2$. At equilibrium $P=kX_e^2$, its turnover lifetime is $X_e/P=1/(kX_e)$, while linearizing gives $\delta\dot X=-2kX_e\delta X$ and relaxation time $1/(2kX_e)$. A chemical lifetime is therefore not automatically an eigenmode relaxation time. The often-used expression $1/(2k_3[\mathrm O])$ also assumes ozone dominates the family abundance.

= Temperature sensitivity of a catalytic ozone balance
{parent=Chapman cycle}
{title2=$d\ln[\mathrm O_3]/d(T^{-1})$}

Suppose a reduced steady balance is $P=AX^2+BX$, where $X=[\mathrm O_3]$, and hold $P$ and all specified background <concentrations> fixed. With $u=1/T$, $d\ln A/du=-a$ and $d\ln B/du=-b$, implicit differentiation gives $d\ln X/du=(aAX^2+bBX)/(2AX^2+BX)$. The quadratic loss term includes temperature-dependent O/O3 partitioning as well as the direct reaction coefficient. Holding the loss ratio fixed over a finite change is an additional approximation; solving the new quadratic provides a check.

= Dewar-Chatt-Duncanson model

The <Dewar-Chatt-Duncanson model> describes an <alkene>–<metal> bond through donation from the filled <alkene> $\pi$ orbital to the <metal> and <pi backbonding> from the <metal> into <alkene> $\pi^*$. = Chelation

<Chelation> binds multiple donor sites of one ligand to the same central atom, forming a <chelate ring>. It can retain a donor near a metal even when one attachment temporarily opens.

= Tridentate ligand
{parent=Chelation}

A <coordination ligand> that coordinates one <metal> through three donor atoms. A facial tridentate donor set occupies the vertices of one face of an octahedron.

= Macrocyclic effect

Enhanced complex stability associated with donor sites being held in a <macrocycle>, relative to comparable open-chain ligands. The comparison depends on donor geometry, solvation and strain.

= Bite angle

The donor–metal–donor angle imposed by a chelating ligand. Tether length, ring conformation, donor distances and substituent crowding all influence it.

= Chelate effect
{parent=Chelation}

The <chelate effect> is the enhanced stability often associated with binding several donor sites from one ligand compared with comparable independent donors. Solvent release, effective local concentration and enthalpic contributions all matter; it is not simply a consequence of satisfying an electron-count rule.

= Chelate ring
{parent=Chelation}

A <chelate ring> is a closed bonded path containing the central atom and a ligand with multiple attachments to it. A tethered Cp–pyridyl ligand can close such a path when the pyridyl N coordinates.

= Trans effect

The <trans effect> is a kinetic influence whereby a ligand accelerates substitution opposite itself, classically in square-planar complexes. It must be distinguished from ground-state bond weakening and from the carbonyl <cis effect>.

= Electron counting
{parent=Coordination chemistry}

<Electron counting> records metal valence <electrons> plus ligand donations using a declared convention. In ionic counting, subtract metal <oxidation state> from its group number, then add donor <electrons>; in neutral counting, combine neutral metal and ligand fragments consistently. An Fe–Fe single bond contributes one <electron> to the count at each metal.

= 18-electron rule
{parent=Electron counting}

The <18-electron rule> is a useful closed-shell guideline for many low-valent transition-metal complexes, filling nine valence orbitals. It is not universal: many early-metal $d^0$ complexes have fewer <electrons>, while stable square-planar $d^8$ complexes are often 16-electron.

= Ligand field
{parent=Coordination chemistry}

A <ligand field> describes how ligands split and mix the central metal orbitals. <Sigma donation>, pi donation and <pi backbonding> explain trends that a purely electrostatic picture misses.

= Racah parameter

A parameter in a compact description of intra-atomic <electron> repulsion within a configuration. $B$ and $C$ control relative d-shell multiplet energies; $B$ is not the <octahedral splitting> or a <rotational constant>.

= Tanabe-Sugano diagram

{c}

A plot of many-electron ligand-field term energies relative to the <ground state> against a normalized ligand-field strength. = Sigma-only octahedral molecular orbital model

A model in which six donor orbitals of an octahedral complex form $a_{1g}+e_g+t_{1u}$ symmetry-adapted combinations. <metal> s, p and two <d orbitals> supply matching symmetry partners; the other three <d orbitals> remain nonbonding $t_{2g}$ orbitals. The model neglects ligand <pi donation>, backbonding and departures from octahedral symmetry.

= Orbital energy barycentre

The degeneracy-weighted mean energy of a specified group of orbitals, $\overline\varepsilon=\sum_i g_i\varepsilon_i/\sum_i g_i$. Keeping it fixed separates level splitting from a common energy shift.

= Square-planar splitting
{parent=Ligand field}

A qualitative d-orbital splitting in a square-planar environment, with the strongly in-plane antibonding $d_{x^2-y^2}$ orbital highest. Ordering among the remaining orbitals can depend on the ligand set.

= Pairing energy
{parent=Ligand field}
{title2=$P$}

The energetic cost assigned to pairing electrons in an orbital rather than placing them in distinct orbitals within a specified ligand-field model.

= Spin crossover
{parent=Ligand field}

<Spin crossover> interconverts electronic states with different total <spin>, often in response to <temperature>. For octahedral <iron>(II), $t_{2g}^4e_g^2$ and $t_{2g}^6$ correspond to $S=2$ and $S=0$. Removing <electrons> from <metal>–ligand antibonding $e_g$ orbitals shortens bonds in the low-<spin> state. Crystal packing and <hydrogen bonds> can affect transition temperatures.

= Electron pairing energy
{parent=Ligand field}
{title2=$P$}

The <electron pairing energy> represents the energetic penalty for putting <electrons> into already occupied orbitals rather than suitable separate orbitals. A simple comparison with <octahedral splitting> explains high-<spin> and low-<spin> alternatives.

= High-spin complex
{parent=Ligand field}

A <high-spin complex> retains more <unpaired electrons> when the energy required for pairing exceeds the relevant orbital splitting. An octahedral high-<spin> $d^6$ center has $t_{2g}^4e_g^2$ and $S=2$.

= Jahn-Teller effect
{parent=Ligand field}
{c}

The <Jahn-Teller effect> is an instability of a nonlinear molecular arrangement with an orbitally degenerate electronic ground state toward a symmetry-lowering distortion. An octahedral high-<spin> $d^4$ ion has unequal occupation of its $e_g$ orbitals and often undergoes tetragonal elongation.

= Tetrahedral doublet distortion-sign ambiguity
{parent=Jahn-Teller effect}
{title2=$E(Q)=KQ^2/2-g|Q|$}

For a linearly split orbital doublet occupied by one <electron> or one <hole>, the lower adiabatic branch has a cusp $-g|Q|$. A harmonic elastic cost produces equal minima at $Q=\pm g/K$. Orbital degeneracy therefore establishes instability of the regular geometry, but cannot alone distinguish opposite tetragonal distortions. A symmetry-allowed cubic elastic invariant in the two-component distortion can select one sign.

= Jahn-Teller stabilization energy
{parent=Jahn-Teller effect}
{c}
{title2=$E_{\rm JT}=\alpha^2/(2\beta)$}

The positive energy reduction from a symmetry-lowering <Jahn-Teller effect>, measured from the undistorted reference configuration. In the harmonic linear-coupling model $E(Q)=-\alpha Q+\beta Q^2/2$, with $\alpha,\beta>0$, completing the square gives $Q_0=\alpha/\beta$ and $E(Q_0)=-\alpha^2/(2\beta)$. Relating this local energy directly to a cooperative transition temperature is an extra approximation.

= Linear Jahn-Teller selection rule
{parent=Jahn-Teller effect}
{title2=$\Gamma_Q\subset\operatorname{Sym}^2\Gamma_{\rm el}$}

For a real nonmagnetic electronic multiplet, a vibrational coordinate can couple linearly through a real symmetric electronic operator only if its representation occurs in the symmetric square. Remove the fully symmetric component when asking about symmetry-lowering distortion. In $T_d$, both $\operatorname{Sym}^2T_1$ and $\operatorname{Sym}^2T_2$ equal $A_1+E+T_2$, so $E$ and $T_2$ modes are symmetry-eligible for either triplet; coupling strengths are not determined by this decomposition.

= Tetragonal Jahn-Teller distortion
{parent=Jahn-Teller effect}

A symmetry-lowering change separating two axial <metal>–ligand distances from four equatorial ones. Occupancy of the split orbitals determines the electronic energy gain, while elastic cost and pre-existing ligand asymmetry affect the observed structure.

= Crystal field theory
{parent=Ligand field}

<Crystal field theory> models the effect of surrounding ligand charges or dipoles on <metal> <atomic orbitals>. Directional repulsion splits the $d$ levels. Unlike <molecular orbital theory>, the simplest model does not explicitly construct covalent <metal>–ligand bonds.

= Orbital quenching
{parent=Crystal field theory}

Suppression of a ground state's orbital <magnetic moment> by the splitting and mixing of free-ion <atomic orbitals> in a crystal. A nondegenerate orbital state can generally be chosen real in zero field, giving zero expectation of the imaginary <orbital angular momentum> operator. <Spin-orbit coupling> to excited states still produces residual <g-factor> shifts and anisotropy, so quenching is not absence of every orbital response.

= Crystal field splitting
{parent=Crystal field theory}
{title2=$\Delta_o$}

Separation of <metal> d-orbital energies by the electrostatic field of surrounding <coordination ligands>. An octahedral field puts the three $t_{2g}$ orbitals below the two $e_g$ orbitals.

= Tetrahedral crystal field splitting
{parent=Crystal field theory}
{title2=$\Delta_t$}

In the ideal tetrahedral point-charge model, the five metal <d orbitals> split into a lower twofold $e$ set and an upper threefold $t_2$ set. Relative to their weighted barycentre their energies are $-3\Delta_t/5$ and $+2\Delta_t/5$, where $\Delta_t>0$ is the separation. For occupancies $e^{n_e}t_2^{n_t}$, the <crystal field stabilization energy> is $(-0.6n_e+0.4n_t)\Delta_t$. Under weak-field high-spin filling it is $-0.6\Delta_t$, $-1.2\Delta_t$, $-0.8\Delta_t$ and zero for $d^6,d^7,d^8,d^{10}$ respectively. Shorter metal–ligand distances generally increase the splitting, so orbital filling can superimpose nonmonotonic bond-length changes on an ionic-size trend. The model alone does not determine an absolute <bond length>.

= Crystal field stabilization energy

{title2=$\mathrm{CFSE}$}

The change in d-electron energy relative to the unsplit barycentre in a crystal-field model, before separately accounting for pairing and other bonding contributions.

= Low-spin complex
{parent=Ligand field}

A <low-spin complex> has the smaller-spin occupancy favored when ligand-field splitting outweighs the cost of pairing <electrons> in the lower orbitals. An octahedral low-spin $d^6$ center has $t_{2g}^6e_g^0$ and total spin zero.

= Spectrochemical series
{parent=Ligand field}

The <spectrochemical series> orders ligands empirically by the splitting they produce in comparable complexes. <Pi-donor ligands> tend toward smaller splittings and <pi-acceptor ligands> toward larger ones. Metal, geometry and <oxidation state> still matter; a sigma-basicity scale is not an interchangeable substitute.

= Octahedral splitting
{parent=Ligand field}
{title2=$\Delta_o$}

In an ideal <octahedral molecular geometry>, the metal $d$ orbitals divide into lower $t_{2g}$ and higher $e_g$ sets. Their separation is $\Delta_o$. Whether <electrons> pair in the lower set depends on that separation relative to pairing costs; observed many-electron transition energies need not equal $\Delta_o$ exactly.

= Linkage isomerism
{parent=Coordination chemistry}

<Linkage isomerism> occurs when complexes have the same composition but an <ambidentate ligand> attaches through different atoms. N-bound and O-bound <nitrite> provide the classical example. This is a difference in attachment connectivity, rather than the different spatial arrangements of unchanged connectivity in <stereoisomers>.

= Linkage isomerization
{parent=Linkage isomerism}

<Linkage isomerization> changes which atom of an <ambidentate ligand> is bound to a central atom. Its rate depends on a reaction barrier, whereas the final equilibrium depends on relative free energies.

= Linkage isomer
{parent=Linkage isomerism}

A <linkage isomer> is one member of a set exhibiting <linkage isomerism>, such as the N-bound or O-bound form of a coordinated <nitrite>.

= Radical
{synonym}

A <radical (chemistry)> has one or more unpaired <electrons>. In a simple <atomic orbital> or <molecular orbital> occupancy picture, an unpaired <electron> occupies a spatial orbital without an opposite-<spin> partner in that orbital. A dot such as $\mathrm H^{\bullet}$ marks this electronic property; it does not mean a missing <electron> or positive <electric charge>. <Free radicals> can be neutral or charged. Terminology also has a boundary: metal <ions> exhibiting <paramagnetism> are not normally called <free radicals> just because they have unpaired <electrons>.<Glossary of Terms Used in Physical Organic Chemistry (IUPAC Recommendations 1994): R entries by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry>[Glossary of Terms Used in Physical Organic Chemistry, IUPAC Recommendations 1994: radical (or free radical), opening definition and metal-ion qualification]{ref}

Compare three species containing the same single <proton>. The <hydrogen atom> $\mathrm H^{\bullet}$ has one <electron> in its $1s$ <atomic orbital>, and is a neutral <radical (chemistry)>. The <hydrogen> <cation> $\mathrm H^+$ has no <electrons>, so no unpaired <electron>. The <hydrogen anion> $\mathrm H^-$ has two <electrons> paired in its $1s$ <atomic orbital>. It is charged but is not a <radical (chemistry)> in its <ground state>. With $N_p$ <protons>, $N_e$ <electrons>, and positive <elementary charge> $e$, the independent <electric charge> count is
$$
q=(N_p-N_e)e;\qquad
\mathrm H^+:q=+e,\quad \mathrm H^{\bullet}:q=0,\quad \mathrm H^-:q=-e.
$$
The <electron pair> and <electric charge> questions require different information. An odd $N_e$ ensures that not all <electrons> can be paired; an even $N_e$ alone does not ensure pairing, as the <molecular orbital> description of <molecular oxygen> illustrates.

A <radical chain reaction> can pass an unpaired <electron> from one species to another without generating net <electric charge>. Detection then exploits the unpaired <electron>'s <spin> and <magnetic moment>, rather than merely measuring whether the sample carries an <electric charge>.

= Radical chain reaction
{parent=Radical (chemistry)}

A <radical chain reaction> regenerates a <radical (chemistry)> during propagation. This explains why one initiating event can lead to many product-forming <chemical reactions>. The <chain-growth polymerization> article develops the corresponding growth of <polymers>.

= Methane chlorination chain
{parent=Radical chain reaction}

Light initiates <methane> chlorination by <photodissociation> of $\mathrm{Cl_2}$ through <homolysis>. The elementary bookkeeping is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{initiation:}\quad &\mathrm{Cl_2}\xrightarrow{h\nu}2\mathrm{Cl}^{\bullet},\\
\text{propagation:}\quad &\mathrm{Cl}^{\bullet}+\mathrm{CH_4}\longrightarrow\mathrm{HCl}+\mathrm{CH_3}^{\bullet},\\
&\mathrm{CH_3}^{\bullet}+\mathrm{Cl_2}\longrightarrow\mathrm{CH_3Cl}+\mathrm{Cl}^{\bullet}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $h\nu$ denotes the <photon> <energy>. The first propagation step replaces a <chlorine> <radical (chemistry)> with a <methyl radical>; the second regenerates a <chlorine> <radical (chemistry)>. Adding the two propagation equations cancels both <free radicals> and gives $\mathrm{CH_4+Cl_2\longrightarrow CH_3Cl+HCl}$: <chloromethane> and <hydrogen chloride> are made while the chain carrier is regenerated.<Organic Chemistry by John McMurry/6.6 Radical Reactions>[Organic Chemistry by John McMurry, §6.6: chlorine photolysis and the two-step methane chlorination cycle]{ref}

= Stable radical
{parent=Radical (chemistry)}

An unpaired <electron> does not set a universal <reaction rate>. A <stable radical> can retain radical character during isolation and storage under specified conditions; a persistent <radical (chemistry)> survives long enough under specified conditions to be observed. These descriptions concern lifetime and handling, rather than immunity to every possible <chemical reaction>. <DPPH> is a practical counterexample to the claim that all <free radicals> react immediately.

Two mechanisms help explain persistence. Spreading electronic character over several <chemical atoms>, represented by <chemical resonance>, can lower the <energy> relative to a localized description. Bulky surrounding groups can impede approach to a reactive site and slow recombination. Lower <energy> and slower <chemical kinetics> are different advantages: neither alone guarantees a long lifetime. A primary computational study explicitly separates these effects, using electronic and geometric descriptors; it does not directly measure every predicted lifetime.<A quantitative metric for organic radical stability and persistence using thermodynamic and kinetic features by Shree Sowndarya S. V. and others>[A quantitative metric for organic radical stability and persistence using thermodynamic and kinetic features, Shree Sowndarya S. V., Peter C. St. John and Robert S. Paton, 2021: Abstract and Introduction, thermodynamic stabilization versus kinetic persistence]{ref}

= 2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl
{parent=Stable radical}
{wiki}

= DPPH
{c}
{synonym}

<DPPH> is a neutral organic <radical (chemistry)> used as a <spin> reference in electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR), also called electron spin resonance (ESR). Its unpaired <electron> remains bound within the <chemical species>: “free radical” does not mean a freely moving <electron>. The <DPPH electron spin resonance experiment> makes its <magnetic moment> observable.

= DPPH electron spin resonance experiment
{c}
{parent=2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl}

The University of Iowa's <Electron Spin Resonance, University of Iowa laboratory protocol> (revised July 2007, Experiment and Resonance Data Analysis, printed pp. 2–6) specifies <DPPH> on cotton in a tube inside an RF coil, between <Helmholtz coils>. A bridge monitors RF absorption; an <oscilloscope> records this output and the voltage across a $1\,\Omega$ current-monitor resistor. Sweeping the coil current passes through the resonant <magnetic field>. The protocol describes two dips when the sweep revisits resonance; these are repeated crossings, not two distinct spectral lines.

Its calibration replaces the sample with a <Hall effect> probe, maps the <magnetic field> in $2\,\mathrm{mm}$ steps, and accounts for sample dimensions. At least four RF <frequencies> test the resonance relation. Sweep speed is adjusted to obtain comparable passages. Only a local peak-plus-background fit is prescribed, because the full shape is not Gaussian. The protocol also flags unexplained trailing spikes and possible electronics contributions. These controls constrain field and sweep errors; an empty-tube/cotton blank would be an additional background control, not a reported measurement in this protocol.<Electron Spin Resonance, University of Iowa laboratory protocol>[Electron Spin Resonance, revised July 2007: printed pp. 4–7, sweep behavior, field mapping, local fitting and anomalous spikes]{ref}

For an isolated effective <spin one-half> with dimensionless positive factor $g$, the basic resonance model is
$$
\Delta E=g\mu_{\rm B}|B|=h\nu,\qquad
g=\frac{h\nu}{\mu_{\rm B}|B_{\rm res}|}.
$$
Here $\mu_{\rm B}$ is the <Bohr magneton>, $h$ is the <Planck constant>, $\nu$ is the applied <frequency>, and $B_{\rm res}$ is the calibrated resonant <magnetic field>. A change in <frequency> should shift the resonance field proportionally in this model. The factor $g$ describes the electronic <magnetic moment> coupling, not the species' net <electric charge>.

A resonance supports the presence of detectable <paramagnetism>. It does not, by itself, identify an unknown <radical (chemistry)>: other species exhibiting <paramagnetism>, impurities, overlapping responses, and interactions with <atomic nuclei> can complicate interpretation. Conversely, absence of a detectable resonance need not mean absence of <free radicals>; a low <concentration>, a short lifetime, or an unsuitable measurement range can conceal them. The occupancy and chain diagrams are original bookkeeping illustrations; neither is a measured or simulated <DPPH> spectrum.

= Density functional theory
{parent=Quantum chemistry}

A framework describing electronic ground-state properties using the <electron density>. Practical approximations require an exchange-correlation functional; this does not mean that explicit <electrons> have been replaced by a classical atom-only force field.

= Exchange-correlation functional
{parent=Density functional theory}
{title2=$E_{\rm xc}[n]=F[n]-T_s[n]-E_H[n]$}

The part of the exact density functional left after subtracting the auxiliary noninteracting kinetic energy and classical <Hartree electrostatic energy>. It includes fermionic exchange, correlation and the difference between interacting and noninteracting kinetic energies. Its functional derivative enters the <Kohn-Sham equations>. Practical approximations to this functional, rather than the existence of a density description itself, are a central source of error.

= Density functional variational principle
{parent=Density functional theory}
{title2=$E_0=\min_n\{F[n]+\int vn\}$}

The constrained-search functional $F[n]=\min_{\Psi\to n}\langle\Psi|T+W|\Psi\rangle$ is universal for a fixed interaction. Minimization of $F[n]+\int vn$ over admissible densities with the correct electron number gives the <ground-state energy>. Each trial density represents a constrained set of antisymmetric states, so minimizing over densities and their representing states recovers the ordinary <variational principle>. The admissible domain and possible degeneracy must be handled explicitly.

= Kohn-Sham equations
{parent=Density functional theory}
{c}
{title2=$[-\tfrac12\nabla^2+v_{\rm eff}[n]]\phi_i=\epsilon_i\phi_i$}

Effective one-electron equations for orbitals whose density represents that of the interacting electronic system. The effective potential contains nuclear attraction, Hartree repulsion and an exchange-correlation contribution.

= Kohn-Sham energy double-counting correction
{parent=Kohn-Sham equations}
{c}
{title2=$E=\sum_i f_i\epsilon_i-E_H+E_{\rm xc}-\int nv_{\rm xc}$}

Summing the <Kohn-Sham equations> over occupied normalized orbitals yields $\sum_i f_i\epsilon_i=T_s+\int nv+2E_H+\int nv_{\rm xc}$. The exact ground-state energy functional is $T_s+\int nv+E_H+E_{\rm xc}$, so subtraction proves the correction in the title. Nuclear or lattice source self-energies must be added consistently. This accounting does not turn all auxiliary orbital eigenvalues into physical excitation energies.

= Configuration interaction
{parent=Quantum chemistry}
{title2=$\Psi=\sum_I c_I\Phi_I$}

= CI
{c}
{synonym}

A variational expansion $\Psi=\sum_I c_I\Phi_I$ over <Slater determinants> or spin-adapted configuration functions in a fixed orbital basis. Truncating the configurations restricts the correlation described.

= Configuration interaction with singles and doubles
{parent=Configuration interaction}

= CISD
{c}
{synonym}

<configuration interaction> retaining a reference <determinant> and its single and double excitations. Conventional matrix-vector iterations have leading $O(o^2v^4)$ work for occupied and virtual dimensions $o,v$, hence $O(N^6)$ when both grow with system size. Truncated <CI> is not generally size extensive.

= Self-consistent field
{parent=Quantum chemistry}

= SCF
{c}
{synonym}

An iterative orbital calculation in which the effective one-electron operator depends on the density built from its own occupied orbitals. Self-consistency is a stationarity condition, not a guarantee that the solution is the lowest-energy one.

= Hartree-Fock method
{parent=Quantum chemistry}
{c}

= HF
{c}
{synonym}

A variational approximation restricting a many-electron <wavefunction> to one <Slater determinant>. Minimizing its energy gives self-consistent one-electron equations with Coulomb and exchange operators. It includes exchange from antisymmetry but omits correlation beyond the <determinant>.

= Fock operator
{parent=Hartree-Fock method}
{c}
{title2=$\hat f$}

The self-consistent one-electron operator obtained by varying a <Slater determinant>. It contains a local <hartree potential> and a nonlocal exchange action. Its occupied <eigenfunctions> define a stationary determinant; its <eigenvalues> are Lagrange multipliers and are not by themselves the total many-electron <energy>.

= Hartree-Fock electron gas
{parent=Hartree-Fock method}
{c}

A variational single-Slater-determinant treatment of interacting <electrons> and a neutralizing positive background. For uniform plane-wave occupations the direct background and <electron> terms cancel, leaving kinetic and same-spin exchange <energy>. Allowing <spin> <polarization> or spatially modulated orbitals enlarges the variational class.

= Contact-interaction Hartree-Fock spin energy
{parent=Hartree-Fock electron gas}
{title2=$\mathcal E=A(n_\uparrow^{5/3}+n_\downarrow^{5/3})-\gamma(n_\uparrow^2+n_\downarrow^2)/2$}

In a uniform three-dimensional <Hartree-Fock electron gas> with repulsive contact coupling $\gamma$, a spin-independent neutralizing background can cancel the <Hartree energy>. The remaining same-spin exchange term is negative and quadratic in each <spin> <density>. Integrating the filled single-spin <Fermi sphere> gives $A=3\hbar^2(6\pi^2)^{2/3}/(10m)$. At fixed total <density> the exchange term favors <spin polarization>, whereas the kinetic term opposes it.

= Endpoint and continuous polarization thresholds of a contact electron gas
{parent=Contact-interaction Hartree-Fock spin energy}
{title2=$r_{\rm end}=2^{5/3}-2,\quad r_{\rm onset}=10/9$}

Let $r=(\gamma/A)(n/2)^{1/3}$ and $P=(n_\uparrow-n_\downarrow)/n$. In units $2A(n/2)^{5/3}$ the spin-dependent <energy> is $[(1+P)^{5/3}+(1-P)^{5/3}]/2-r(1+P^2)/2$. Comparing only $P=0$ and $P=1$ gives the endpoint crossing $r=2^{5/3}-2$. Expanding near $P=0$ gives a quadratic coefficient $5/9-r/2$, so the unpolarized state first loses local stability at $r=10/9$. The endpoint crossing is therefore not the actual continuous onset; intermediate polarizations matter.

= Overhauser instability
{parent=Hartree-Fock electron gas}
{c}

The uniform paramagnetic three-dimensional electron-gas determinant is unstable within Hartree-Fock theory to a spin-density-wave deformation, even at high density. Comparing only spatially uniform polarized and unpolarized determinants therefore does not find the unrestricted Hartree-Fock <ground state>.<Spin Density Waves in an Electron Gas by A. W. Overhauser>[abstract and p. 1437]{ref}

= Uniform electron-gas exchange energy
{parent=Hartree-Fock electron gas}

In a three-dimensional unpolarized gas, exchange <energy> per particle is $-3e^2k_F/(16\pi^2\varepsilon)$, with Coulomb convention $e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon r)$. It scales as minus the inverse dimensionless spacing radius, while kinetic <energy> scales as its inverse square. Exchange acts only between equal-spin occupied orbitals.

= Electron-gas spin polarization
{parent=Hartree-Fock electron gas}
{title2=$\zeta$}

For number densities $n_\uparrow,n_\downarrow$, $\zeta=(n_\uparrow-n_\downarrow)/(n_\uparrow+n_\downarrow)$. Uniform <spin> <polarization> raises kinetic <energy> by concentrating occupation in one Fermi sphere, while lowering exchange <energy>.

= Two-electron exchange integral
{parent=Hartree-Fock method}
{title2=$K_{pq}$}

For real <spatial orbitals>, $K_{pq}=\iint\phi_p(1)\phi_q(1)r_{12}^{-1}\phi_q(2)\phi_p(2)\,d1\,d2$ in <atomic units>. It enters the energy of a <Slater determinant> through antisymmetry and becomes small for well-separated localized orbitals.

= Two-electron Coulomb integral
{parent=Hartree-Fock method}
{title2=$J_{pq}$}

The electrostatic repulsion integral $J_{pq}=\iint|\phi_p(1)|^2|\phi_q(2)|^2r_{12}^{-1}\,d1\,d2$ in <atomic units>. For a normalized hydrogenic 1s orbital of exponent $\zeta$, the same-orbital value is $5\zeta/8$. It is a two-electron integral, unlike a diagonal Hückel one-electron parameter.

= Koopmans' theorem
{parent=Hartree-Fock method}
{c}
{title2=$I_i\simeq-\epsilon_i$}

In frozen-orbital Hartree-Fock theory, removing an <electron> from occupied orbital $i$ changes the energy by approximately $-\epsilon_i$. Orbital relaxation and correlation are omitted, so a measured <ionization energy> is not an exact orbital-energy measurement.

= Unrestricted Hartree-Fock method
{parent=Hartree-Fock method}
{c}

= UHF
{c}
{synonym}

A Hartree-Fock ansatz allowing different <spatial orbitals> for alpha and beta spin. This can lower the energy of a stretched bond, but the <determinant> need not be an eigenstate of total spin squared.

= Restricted Hartree-Fock method
{parent=Hartree-Fock method}
{c}

= RHF
{c}
{synonym}

A Hartree-Fock ansatz in which opposite-spin <electrons> in a doubly occupied orbital share the same spatial function. The closed-shell <determinant> is spin-pure but can dissociate incorrectly when a bond requires several <determinants>.

= Exchange integral
{parent=Quantum chemistry}
{title2=$K_{ab}=\iint a^*(1)b(1)b^*(2)a(2)r_{12}^{-1}\,d^3r_1d^3r_2$}

For two orthonormal spatial <atomic orbitals>, the normalized symmetric and antisymmetric two-particle factors have Coulomb energies $J_{ab}+K_{ab}$ and $J_{ab}-K_{ab}$. With common orbitals, the fermionic <singlet state> and <triplet state> therefore differ by $2K_{ab}$. This is an <exchange interaction> resulting from the state symmetry and the Coulomb operator, rather than a separate classical force.

= Coulomb integral
{c}
{parent=Quantum chemistry}
{title2=$J_{ab}=\iint |a(1)|^2|b(2)|^2r_{12}^{-1}\,d^3r_1d^3r_2$}

In <atomic units>, the displayed integral is the direct electrostatic contribution between two normalized <atomic orbitals> $a,b$. It enters a two-electron <energy> expectation together with the <exchange integral>.

= Electron density
{parent=Quantum chemistry}
{title2=$n(\mathbf r)$}

The electron density describes the expected number of <electrons> per unit <volume> at position $\mathbf r$. For a system with a fixed number $N$ of <electrons>, $\int n(\mathbf r)\,d^3\mathbf r=N$. A <Lewis structure> allocates electron counts without specifying this spatial density.

= Electron configuration
{parent=Quantum chemistry}
{wiki}

An <electron configuration> records the occupation of <electron shells> and <electron subshells> by <electrons>. For neutral <sodium> in its <ground state>, $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^1$ gives $2+2+6+1=11$ <electrons>; adding the occupations within each <electron shell> gives $(2,8,1)$. A superscript counts <electrons>, rather than multiplying the preceding labels. Removing the outer <electron> gives the <sodium> <ion> $\mathrm{Na}^{+}$ with $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6$, or $(2,8)$. See https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/6-4-electronic-structure-of-atoms-electron-configurations[Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, electron configurations of atoms and ions].

= Closed electron shell
{parent=Electron configuration}

A filled electronic shell has all its orbital and spin states occupied. Its total orbital and spin angular momenta cancel, so a closed core does not supply the valence angular momentum in an elementary alkali-atom term assignment.

= Aufbau principle
{parent=Electron configuration}
{c}
{wiki}

An approximate scheme for constructing an atomic <electron configuration> by filling lower-energy <atomic orbitals> before higher-energy ones, subject to the <Pauli exclusion principle>. Orbital ordering can change on ionization and has exceptions in interacting <chemical atoms>. Selecting the free-ion ground term within a specified open-shell configuration additionally requires <Hund's rules> or a more detailed interaction calculation.

= Isoelectronic species
{parent=Electron configuration}

Atoms, molecules or ions with the same <electron> count, often compared because their orbital occupations or bonding patterns are related. Equal <electron> counts do not guarantee identical energies or geometry.

= Effective nuclear charge

m eff}$}

An orbital- and model-dependent parameter representing nuclear attraction after electronic screening. A screening construction writes $Z_{\rm eff}=Z-S$; a binding-energy construction requires an additional orbital-energy model.

= Atomic-orbital penetration
{parent=Effective nuclear charge}

Probability density within the inner <electron> shells. Greater penetration can reduce screening and lower an orbital energy; this helps distinguish many-electron s and p levels with the same principal quantum number.

= Shielding constant
{parent=Effective nuclear charge}

The screening parameter $S$ in $Z_{\rm eff}=Z-S$. Different atomic orbitals can have different shielding parameters.

= Slater's rules
{parent=Shielding constant}

An empirical orbital-screening prescription. Other electrons in an $ns,np$ group count $0.35$ each; electrons in shell $n-1$ count $0.85$, deeper electrons $1.00$, and outer electrons zero. = Hund's rules
{parent=Electron configuration}
{c}
{wiki}

<Friedrich Hund>'s rules describe the usual ground-term ordering within an atomic <electron configuration> in the <LS coupling> regime. First maximize the total <spin> $S$ subject to the <Pauli exclusion principle>. Among the terms with that spin, maximize total <orbital angular momentum> $L$. Within the resulting term, the lowest level has $J=|L-S|$ for a less-than-half-filled open subshell and $J=L+S$ for a more-than-half-filled one. These are ordering rules, not universal replacements for diagonalizing an arbitrary atomic <Hamiltonian operator>.

For two equivalent $p$ electrons, parallel spins give $S=1$. Their spatial <wavefunction> must be antisymmetric; the highest allowed orbital projection with parallel spins is $1+0=1$, giving $L=1$. A $p^2$ subshell is less than half filled, so its lowest <term symbol> is $^3P_0$, followed within that term by $^3P_1$ and $^3P_2$. Closed subshells contribute zero total $L$ and $S$.

= Hund's coupling
{c}
{parent=Hund's rules}
{title2=$H_H=-J_H\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2,\quad J_H>0$}

An effective local exchange coupling can favor parallel <electron spins> in different orbitals on the same atom. The displayed sign convention represents that preference. It underlies the high-spin tendency in <Hund's rules> and the alignment of itinerant and core spins used in <double exchange>. The coupling is an effective description of intra-atomic Coulomb and exchange energies, rather than a separate classical magnetic force.

= Atomic orbital
{parent=Quantum chemistry}
{wiki}
{title2=$\phi_{n\ell m}(\mathbf r)$}

An atomic orbital is a one-<electron> spatial <wavefunction> used to describe an <electron>'s state in a <chemical atom>. For a one-electron <hydrogen atom>, orbitals can be exact <energy eigenstates>; descriptions of many-electron atoms instead use an orbital approximation. A normalized atomic orbital has $\int|\phi|^2\,d^3\mathbf r=1$, and the <Born rule> gives the position <probability density> $|\phi|^2$. It is a function extending through space, not a prescribed classical orbit. https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/6-3-development-of-quantum-theory[Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, 6.3: atomic orbitals and the wavefunction description]{ref}

The <linear combination of atomic orbitals> combines these single-centre functions to approximate a <molecular orbital>. The <orbital overlap> measures their <inner product>, and their relative <quantum phase> controls the <interference> shown in <molecular orbital theory>.

= Mean-square orbital radius

For a normalized spatial <wavefunction>, $\langle r^2\rangle=\int r^2|\psi(\mathbf r)|^2d^3r$. Its square root is the root-mean-square radius, which differs from the exponential decay length. A spherical <hydrogenic donor state> with $\psi\propto e^{-r/a}$ has $\langle r^2\rangle=3a^2$, obtained by dividing the radial integrals of $r^4e^{-2r/a}$ and $r^2e^{-2r/a}$.

= Molecular orbital theory
{parent=Quantum chemistry}
{wiki}

<Molecular orbital theory> describes the electronic arrangement of a <molecule> using <molecular orbitals>: one-<electron> spatial <wavefunctions> extending over the combined <atomic nuclei>. Filling <bonding molecular orbitals> can stabilize a <chemical bond>; filling <antibonding molecular orbitals> opposes that stabilization. A <molecular orbital> is a building block of a many-<electron> description, rather than the entire many-<electron> <wavefunction>. See https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-61-physical-chemistry-fall-2017/ef9da1132ff6233d732a1d6115099467_MIT5_61F17_lec24.pdf[Robert W. Field, MIT 5.61, lecture 24, pp. 1–4: molecular orbitals and the LCAO approximation].

First make the <interference> visible with an exactly normalized one-dimensional toy. Replace the three-dimensional $1s$ <atomic orbitals> of the <hydrogen atom> by two <Gaussian functions> centred at $x=\pm a$, with <dimensionless> $x$ and $a>0$:
$$
\phi_A(x)=\pi^{-1/4}e^{-(x+a)^2/2},\qquad
\phi_B(x)=\pi^{-1/4}e^{-(x-a)^2/2},\qquad
S=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi_A\phi_B\,dx=e^{-a^2}.
$$
The normalized sum and difference are $\psi_\pm=(\phi_A\pm\phi_B)/\sqrt{2(1\pm S)}$, following the same <linear combination of atomic orbitals> algebra. At $a=1$, the <orbital overlap> is $S=e^{-1}\approx0.367879$. At the midpoint, $\phi_A(0)=\phi_B(0)\approx0.455581$, giving
$$
\psi_+(0)\approx0.550879,\qquad |\psi_+(0)|^2\approx0.303468,\qquad
\psi_-(0)=|\psi_-(0)|^2=0.
$$
Both <probability densities> have total <integral> one: <interference> redistributes <probability>, rather than creating an extra <electron>. These are toy <wavefunctions>, not actual <hydrogen atom> orbitals or predictions of <molecular hydrogen> <energies>.

Now fill the two $1s$-derived <molecular orbitals> in the minimal model. The lower <bonding molecular orbital> is labelled $\sigma_g$; the upper <antibonding molecular orbital> is $\sigma_u^*$. The <Pauli exclusion principle> permits at most two <electrons> in each spatial <molecular orbital>, with opposite <spin> projections along a chosen axis. For the lowest <electron configuration> in this model, fill $\sigma_g$ first. The subscripts $g$ and $u$ denote <even> and <odd> behaviour under inversion through the midpoint; the star marks an <antibonding molecular orbital>. See https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/8-4-molecular-orbital-theory[Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, 8.4: occupancy and bond order] and https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-61-physical-chemistry-fall-2017/e9f9413400be87f6a0a512e757d52e3c_MIT5_61F17_lec25.pdf[Field, MIT 5.61, lecture 25, pp. 1–3: bonding, antibonding, and symmetry labels].

With $N_b$ <electrons> in <bonding molecular orbitals> and $N_a$ in <antibonding molecular orbitals>, the model's <bond order> is $b=(N_b-N_a)/2$. The three elementary counts are:
* <Molecular hydrogen>, $\mathrm H_2$: two <electrons> give $(\sigma_g)^2$ and $b=(2-0)/2=1$.
* The <hydrogen> molecular <ion> $\mathrm H_2^+$: one <electron> gives $(\sigma_g)^1$ and $b=(1-0)/2=1/2$.
* Two neutral <helium> <chemical atoms>, $\mathrm{He}_2$: four <electrons> give $(\sigma_g)^2(\sigma_u^*)^2$ and $b=(2-2)/2=0$ in this minimal $1s$ model.

The zero <bond order> says this minimal model gives no net <covalent bond> for $\mathrm{He}_2$. It does not rule out every bound pair of <helium> <chemical atoms>: a very weak <van der Waals force> can bind a neutral <helium> dimer, which has been observed experimentally. See https://www.atom.uni-frankfurt.de/publications/files/ZellerPNAS-2016.pdf[Zeller et al., Imaging the He2 quantum halo state using a free electron laser, PNAS 113, 14651–14655 (2016), p. 14651: van der Waals binding] and https://scholarworks.gvsu.edu/chm_articles/6/[Luo et al., The weakest bond: Experimental observation of helium dimer, Journal of Chemical Physics 98, 3564–3567 (1993)]. The <bond order> count does not include that weak binding mechanism.

The useful surprise is that sharing alone does not determine a <chemical bond>: the relative <quantum phase> and the occupancy matter together. The same two shapes explain why adding an <electron> to a <bonding molecular orbital> increases the model's <bond order>, while adding one to an <antibonding molecular orbital> decreases it. <Molecular orbital theory> also accounts for the unpaired <electrons> and <paramagnetism> of <molecular oxygen>, which its all-paired <Lewis structure> misses. See https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/8-4-molecular-orbital-theory[Chemistry 2e, 8.4: the molecular orbital description of oxygen].

A diagram linking reactant and product orbitals by a <symmetry> conserved along a stipulated reaction path. = Hückel method
{c}
{parent=Molecular orbital theory}
{wiki}

A model of conjugated $\pi$ <molecular orbitals> with an orthonormal atomic basis, one-centre energies $\alpha_i$, and resonance matrix elements $\beta_{ij}$ between selected neighbouring sites. Solving $H\mathbf c=E\mathbf c$ gives orbital <energies> and coefficients. Its simplicity makes symmetry and electron filling explicit, but quantitative charge predictions can depend strongly on the chosen parameters and omitted orbital relaxation.

= Cube nearest-neighbour Hückel model
{parent=Hückel method}
{title2=$E-\alpha\in\{3\beta,\beta,-\beta,-3\beta\}$}

With one orthonormal site orbital per cube vertex, common site energy $\alpha$, and coupling $\beta$ along every edge, the one-electron <Hamiltonian> is $\alpha I+\beta A$, where $A$ is the cube <adjacency matrix>. Its <eigenvalues> are $\alpha+3\beta,\alpha+\beta,\alpha-\beta,\alpha-3\beta$ with <multiplicities> $1,3,3,1$.

One proof labels each vertex by three signs. The adjacency operator flips one sign at a time, so a product of $k$ coordinate signs is an <eigenfunction> of $A$ with <eigenvalue> $3-2k$. There are $\binom3k$ such products. For $\beta<0$, eight <electrons> fill the lowest four spatial <molecular orbitals>, yielding total one-electron energy $8\alpha+12\beta$.

= Hückel square-to-trapezium distortion
{parent=Hückel method}
{c}

For a four-site cycle with site <energy> $\alpha$, three resonance integrals $\beta<0$ and one weakened integral $n\beta$, $0<n<1$, put $D=\sqrt{(n-1)^2+4}$. In the independent-electron <Hückel method>, filling the two or four lowest spin orbitals gives total electronic energies $E_2=2\alpha+\beta(n+1+D)$ and $E_4=4\alpha+2\beta D$. At the square limit $n=1$, these become $2\alpha+4\beta$ and $4\alpha+4\beta$. The weakened-edge distortion raises the two-electron sum but lowers the four-electron sum. Near $n=1$ the latter change is $\beta(1-n)^2/2+O((1-n)^4)$, so its first-order term cancels. This electronic comparison omits structural repulsion and other restoring forces, and does not determine the open-shell spin at the square limit.

= Coulson-Rushbrooke pairing theorem
{parent=Hückel method}
{c}
{title2=$PKP=-K$}

For a bipartite pi system with a common diagonal energy $\alpha$, the orbital spectrum is paired about $\alpha$. If $H=\alpha I+K$ and $P$ changes the sign on one sublattice, then $PKP=-K$; an <eigenvector> of energy $\alpha+\epsilon$ is mapped to one of energy $\alpha-\epsilon$. Their site-probability magnitudes are equal.

= Hückel pi bond order
{parent=Hückel method}
{c}
{title2=$p_{ij}=\sum_k f_kc_{ik}c_{jk}$}

For real <orthonormal> site functions, $p_{ij}=\sum_k f_k c_{ik}c_{jk}$, where $f_k$ is orbital occupation. It measures the off-diagonal pi density <matrix>; a complete C–C bond also contains a sigma contribution.

= Hückel Coulomb parameter
{parent=Hückel method}
{c}
{title2=$\alpha_i=H_{ii}$}

The diagonal one-centre energy $\alpha_i$ assigned to a pi-basis orbital. This one-electron parameter is distinct from a two-electron Coulomb repulsion integral in Hartree-Fock theory.

= Hückel resonance integral
{parent=Hückel method}
{c}
{title2=$\beta_{ij}=\langle\phi_i|\hat H|\phi_j\rangle$}

An off-diagonal <Hamiltonian> <matrix> element between selected neighbouring pi-basis functions. The usual bonding convention has $\beta<0$; non-neighbour <matrix> elements are neglected.

= Triangular three-site orbital model
{parent=Hückel method}

With equal on-site energy $\alpha$ and equal pair interaction $\beta<0$, the three-site Hamiltonian has one eigenvalue $\alpha+2\beta$ and a degenerate pair $\alpha-\beta$. This model explains symmetry and filling but omits ingredients needed for a quantitative dissociation energy.

= Delocalization energy
{parent=Hückel method}
{title2=$\Delta E=E_\pi-E_{\rm localized}$}

= Delocalisation energy
{synonym}

The change in <energy> on allowing a specified localized orbital reference to delocalize across a <conjugated system>. In a <Hückel method> calculation, the reference must use the same one-centre parameters and bond types as the delocalized <molecule>. With the signed convention $E_\pi-E_{\rm localized}$, stabilization is negative; its positive magnitude is often also called the <delocalization energy>, so the sign convention must be stated.

= Hückel orbital charge density
{c}
{parent=Hückel method}
{title2=$n_i=\sum_k f_k|c_{ik}|^2$}

In an orthonormal <Hückel method> basis, an occupied <molecular orbital> with occupancy $f_k$ contributes $f_k|c_{ik}|^2$ electrons to site $i$. Adding one electron to a fixed orbital changes the site population by $|c_i|^2$. A coefficient sign indicates orbital phase, not charge sign. A net atomic charge also needs a reference population and treatment of the electrons outside the model.

= Molecular orbital
{parent=Molecular orbital theory}
{title2=$\psi(\mathbf r)$}
{wiki}

A <molecular orbital> is a one-<electron> spatial <wavefunction> for the combined <atomic nuclei> of a <molecule>. For a normalized <molecular orbital>, the <Born rule> gives the position <probability density> $|\psi(\mathbf r)|^2$, with $\int_{\mathbb R^3}|\psi|^2\,d^3\mathbf r=1$. The <molecular orbital> is the amplitude, rather than its squared <modulus> or a classical trajectory.

In the independent-orbital approximation, a doubly occupied <molecular orbital> contributes $2|\psi|^2$ to the <electron> number density, whose <integral> is two. An empty <molecular orbital> contributes zero, even though its shape is still part of the model. The <molecular hydrogen> example therefore uses one occupied spatial <molecular orbital> for two <electrons>, distinguished by their <spins>. A negative real <probability amplitude> means a relative <quantum phase> of $\pi$, not positive <electric charge>; multiplying the whole <wavefunction> by $-1$ changes only its <global phase>.

= Frontier molecular orbital
{parent=Molecular orbital}

A <molecular orbital> near the boundary between occupied and unoccupied levels. The <highest occupied molecular orbital> and <LUMO> are central examples. Their energies, occupancies, spatial form and symmetry help predict which new <chemical bonds> can form when fragments approach.

= Nonbonding molecular orbital
{parent=Molecular orbital}

A <molecular orbital> whose occupation changes bonding between the selected centres relatively little. In a real complex, weak bonding or antibonding contributions may replace exact nonbonding character.

= Open-shell frontier orbital convention
{parent=Molecular orbital}

For an open-shell species, distinguish a singly occupied spatial orbital from a vacant <spin> orbital. Adding an <electron> to an <oxygen> molecule can fill the opposite-spin vacancy in a pi-star <singly occupied molecular orbital> even though the next completely empty spatial orbital is sigma-star.

= Singly occupied molecular orbital
{parent=Molecular orbital}

A spatial <molecular orbital> occupied by one <electron>. In an open-shell molecule, its empty opposite-spin slot can accept another <electron> even though the spatial orbital is occupied.

= Nonbonding orbital
{parent=Molecular orbital}

A <molecular orbital> with little or no net bonding interaction between the atoms in the stated orbital model. A symmetry-isolated <atomic orbital> can be exactly nonbonding in a minimal basis: the out-of-plane nitrogen $2p_z$ orbital in planar <ammonia> has no matching hydrogen <symmetry orbital>. A mainly localized <lone pair> can acquire bonding admixture when a distortion permits additional mixing.

= Molecular orbital diagram
{parent=Molecular orbital}

An energy-level scheme showing the occupied molecular orbitals and their relation to atomic orbitals. Occupancies must account for degeneracy and spin.

= Ammonia molecular orbital diagram
{parent=Molecular orbital diagram}

In pyramidal <ammonia>, the hydrogen <symmetry orbitals> span $A_1\oplus E$ of the <C3v molecular point group>. Nitrogen $2s$ and $2p_z$ both have $A_1$ symmetry and $(2p_x,2p_y)$ span $E$. In the planar <D3h molecular point group>, these become $A_1'$, $A_2''$ and $E'$, respectively, while the hydrogen combinations span $A_1'\oplus E'$. = Highest occupied molecular orbital
{parent=Molecular orbital}

The occupied <molecular orbital> of greatest <energy> in a specified reference <electron configuration>. A fixed-orbital model removes an <electron> from this level first; relaxation and <electron correlation> can modify the resulting energy.

= Orbital degeneracy
{parent=Molecular orbital}

<Orbital degeneracy> means distinct spatial orbitals have equal energy within the specified model or symmetry. Two <electrons> in degenerate orbitals need not pair; exchange can favor a <triplet state>.

= Lowest unoccupied molecular orbital
{parent=Molecular orbital}

= LUMO
{c}
{synonym}

The lowest-energy unoccupied orbital of a specified reference <electron configuration>. In a fixed-orbital approximation, adding one electron occupies this orbital; orbital relaxation and electron correlation can alter that picture.

= Bonding orbital
{parent=Molecular orbital}
{title2=$\sigma_g$}
{wiki}

= Bonding molecular orbital
{synonym}

Occupying a <bonding molecular orbital> favours a <chemical bond>. For the two equivalent $1s$ <atomic orbitals> of the <hydrogen atom>, the sum $\sigma_g=\sigma_+$ has constructive <interference> between the <protons>. The resulting <electron> density in that region helps stabilize the combined system. Its lower <energy> in this model follows from the <quantum Hamiltonian>, not from the plus sign alone; see https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-61-physical-chemistry-fall-2017/e9f9413400be87f6a0a512e757d52e3c_MIT5_61F17_lec25.pdf[Field, MIT 5.61, lecture 25, pp. 1–2].

For example, $(\sigma_g)^2$ in <molecular hydrogen> supplies $N_b=2$, hence <bond order> one when $N_a=0$. The sum in the <molecular orbital theory> toy has midpoint <probability density> $0.303468$, whereas the difference has zero there. The normalization factors must be included in this comparison.

= Antibonding orbital
{parent=Molecular orbital}
{title2=$\sigma_u^*$}
{wiki}

= Antibonding molecular orbital
{synonym}

Occupying an <antibonding molecular orbital> opposes a <chemical bond>. For equivalent $1s$ <atomic orbitals> of the <hydrogen atom>, the difference $\sigma_u^*=\sigma_-$ vanishes on the plane halfway between the <protons>: every point there is equally far from both centres, so the amplitudes cancel. This nodal plane removes the contribution of this <molecular orbital> to <electron> density on that plane; it does not make an entire finite region empty. The $1s$-derived <antibonding molecular orbital> lies above its <bonding molecular orbital> partner in <energy>. See https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-61-physical-chemistry-fall-2017/e9f9413400be87f6a0a512e757d52e3c_MIT5_61F17_lec25.pdf[Field, MIT 5.61, lecture 25, pp. 1–2].

In the $\mathrm{He}_2$ minimal model, $(\sigma_u^*)^2$ adds $N_a=2$ and cancels the <bond order> contribution of $(\sigma_g)^2$. Opposite signs on the two lobes describe <probability amplitudes>, while both lobes have nonnegative <probability density> after squaring. The star is an orbital label, not an extra <electron> or an <electric charge>.

= Linear combination of atomic orbitals
{parent=Molecular orbital theory}
{wiki}

= LCAO
{c}
{synonym}

The <LCAO> approximation builds a <molecular orbital> as a <linear combination> of single-centre <atomic orbitals>. For two equivalent normalized real $1s$ functions $\phi_A$ and $\phi_B$, choose their signs positive and define their <orbital overlap> $S$. At nonzero separation, $0<S<1$, and the normalized combinations are
$$
\sigma_\pm(\mathbf r)=\frac{\phi_A(\mathbf r)\pm\phi_B(\mathbf r)}{\sqrt{2(1\pm S)}},\qquad
S=\int_{\mathbb R^3}\phi_A(\mathbf r)\phi_B(\mathbf r)\,d^3\mathbf r.
$$
Here $\sigma_+=\sigma_g$ and $\sigma_-=\sigma_u^*$. Equal coefficients use the equivalence of the two centres; a general <LCAO> need not have equal coefficients. See https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-61-physical-chemistry-fall-2017/ef9da1132ff6233d732a1d6115099467_MIT5_61F17_lec24.pdf[Field, MIT 5.61, lecture 24, pp. 3–6: LCAO coefficients and overlap integrals].

For the <Gaussian function> toy's $a=1$, the two denominators are $\sqrt{2(1+S)}\approx1.654013$ and $\sqrt{2(1-S)}\approx1.124385$. To check <wavefunction normalization>, expand the square: $\int(\phi_A\pm\phi_B)^2=1+1\pm2S$. Dividing by $2(1\pm S)$ gives one. The local <probability density> is
$$
|\sigma_\pm|^2=\frac{\phi_A^2+\phi_B^2\pm2\phi_A\phi_B}{2(1\pm S)}.
$$
The cross term is the <interference>; adding the separate densities first would lose it. These formulas normalize the chosen <linear combination> exactly, while the restricted choice of <atomic orbitals> remains an approximation to an actual <molecular orbital>.

= Symmetry-adapted linear combination
{parent=Linear combination of atomic orbitals}
{title2=$\Phi_\Gamma=\sum_i c_i\phi_i$}

= Symmetry orbital
{synonym}

A <linear combination of atomic orbitals> chosen to transform under an <irreducible representation> of the molecular <point group>. Relative orbital signs must be transformed as well as the positions of their centres. Such combinations organize a <Hamiltonian operator> into symmetry blocks; combinations of the same symmetry generally still mix and need not themselves be <molecular orbitals>.

= Tetrahedral s-orbital symmetry combinations
{parent=Symmetry-adapted linear combination}

Four equivalent $s$ <atomic orbitals> at the vertices of a regular <tetrahedron> carry the <tetrahedral symmetry> representation $A_1\oplus T_2$. The normalized equal sum is $A_1$; three coordinate-sign combinations span $T_2$. Every normalized coefficient vector has entries $\pm1/2$ in a suitable orthonormal real basis. These functions simplify a <Hamiltonian> invariant under the tetrahedral operations, but equivalent symmetry copies on other sites may still mix.

= Orbital overlap
{parent=Linear combination of atomic orbitals}
{title2=$S$}

The <orbital overlap> of normalized real <atomic orbitals> is their <inner product> $S=\int\phi_A\phi_B$. It is a <dimensionless> measure of their common amplitude, not an <electron> count. For the <Gaussian function> toy, increasing $a$ from $1$ to $2$ reduces $S$ from $e^{-1}\approx0.367879$ to $e^{-4}\approx0.018316$. As $S$ tends to zero, both <LCAO> normalization denominators tend to $\sqrt2$.

= Bond order
{parent=Molecular orbital theory}
{title2=$b=(N_b-N_a)/2$}
{wiki}

In the elementary <molecular orbital theory> count, <bond order> is half the excess of <electrons> in <bonding molecular orbitals> over those in <antibonding molecular orbitals>. Thus adding one <electron> to a <bonding molecular orbital> raises $b$ by $1/2$, while adding one to an <antibonding molecular orbital> lowers it by $1/2$. The configurations drawn above give $1$, $1/2$, and $0$. This count summarizes net <covalent bond> occupancy; it is neither a measured <energy> nor a universal test for every kind of binding. See https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/8-4-molecular-orbital-theory[Chemistry 2e, 8.4: molecular orbital bond order].

= Molar mass
{parent=Chemistry}
{wiki}
{title2=$M$}

<Molar mass> turns a weighed sample into a count of specified particles. For a pure <chemical substance> with specified <chemical formula> and <isotopic abundances>, its <molar mass> is
$$
M=\frac{m}{n},\qquad n=\frac{m}{M},
$$
where $m$ is the sample <mass> and $n$ its <amount of substance>. The <SI> unit is $\mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}$; the common laboratory unit is $\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$, with $1\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}=10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}$. Equal values of <amount of substance> correspond to equal counts of specified entities, but heavier entities give greater <masses>.

Combining this definition with <molecular count and amount of substance> gives
$$
N=\frac{mN_{\mathrm A}}{M},\qquad \bar m_{\rm particle}=\frac{m}{N}=\frac{M}{N_{\mathrm A}},\qquad N_{\mathrm A}=6.02214076\times10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}.
$$
The <Avogadro constant> is exact under the modern definition of the <mole>. The <mass> $M/N_{\mathrm A}$ is the mean <mass> per specified entity; if <isotopes> differ within the sample, individual <molecules> need not all have that mean <mass>.<The International System of Units (SI), ninth edition, SI Brochure>[§2.3.1, The mole, printed p. 131: exact Avogadro constant, specified entities, and M(X) = N_A m(X)]{ref}

Specify what $n$ counts: <water> <molecules>, <hydrogen> <chemical atoms>, and <oxygen> <chemical atoms> give different values of <amount of substance> for the same sample. For a <mixture>, dividing total <mass> by total <amount of substance> of its chosen entities gives a composition-dependent mean. A <polymer> sample can contain many chain <molar masses>; its <number-average molar mass> and <weight-average molar mass> answer different averaging questions. Relating either to <degree of polymerization> also requires a unit-mass convention and end-group accounting.

= Number-average molar mass
{parent=Molar mass}
{title2=$M_n$}

The <number-average molar mass> weights each <molecule> equally. For counts $n_i$ with <molar masses> $M_i$,
$$
M_n=\frac{\sum_i n_iM_i}{\sum_i n_i}.
$$
A mass-proportional <SEC detector response> requires conversion to counts before determining this average.

= Weight-average molar mass
{parent=Molar mass}
{title2=$M_w$}

The <weight-average molar mass> weights each population by its contribution to total <mass>:
$$
M_w=\frac{\sum_i n_iM_i^2}{\sum_i n_iM_i}.
$$
Here $n_i$ counts <molecules> with <molar mass> $M_i$. <SEC light-scattering detection> can infer this average for an eluting slice under its optical and <concentration> assumptions; overlapping species need not have the same individual <molar mass>.

= Relative molecular mass
{parent=Molar mass}
{title2=$M_{\rm r}$}

The <relative molecular mass> is the <dimensionless> ratio of a specified mean <molecule> <mass> to the <unified atomic mass unit>, written $m_{\rm u}$ when treated as a <physical quantity>:
$$
M_{\rm r}=\frac{\bar m_{\rm molecule}}{m_{\rm u}},\qquad M=M_{\rm r}M_{\rm u}.
$$
Thus a <relative molecular mass> of approximately $18.015$ and a <molar mass> of approximately $18.015\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$ describe related quantities with different units. The conversion uses the <molar mass constant>, rather than identifying a <dimensionless> number with a <mass> per <mole>.

= Molar mass constant
{parent=Molar mass}
{title2=$M_{\rm u}=N_{\mathrm A}m_{\rm u}$}

The <molar mass constant> converts <relative atomic mass> or <relative molecular mass> into <molar mass>. It is approximately $1\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$. Its <SI> numerical value is determined experimentally: the exact <Avogadro constant> multiplies an experimentally determined <unified atomic mass unit> in kilograms. Treating $M_{\rm u}$ as exactly $1\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$ is therefore an excellent elementary-calculation approximation, rather than a defining equality of the modern <SI>.<The International System of Units (SI), ninth edition, SI Brochure>[§2.3.1, The mole, printed p. 131: carbon-12 molar mass no longer exact; M_u = N_A m_u and M(X) = A_r(X) M_u]{ref}

= Water mass to molecular count
{parent=Molar mass}

Choose approximate <atomic weights> $A_{\rm r}(\mathrm H)=1.008$ and $A_{\rm r}(\mathrm O)=15.999$, and use $M_{\rm u}\approx1\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$. The <chemical formula> $\mathrm{H_2O}$ then gives <water> <molar mass>
$$
M(\mathrm{H_2O})\approx\bigl(2(1.008)+15.999\bigr)\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}=18.015\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}.
$$
These are deliberately chosen rounded inputs, not exact <atomic weights> or a universal value for every <water> sample. Different <isotopic abundances>, notably those in <heavy water>, change the mean <molar mass>; <chemical formula> alone does not specify them. Adding <atomic weights> also neglects the tiny <mass> change associated with <chemical bond> <energies>, immaterial at this rounding precision.

For an illustrative pure <water> sample of <mass> $4.50375\,\mathrm g$, this convention gives
$$
n\approx\frac{4.50375\,\mathrm g}{18.015\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}}=0.250000\,\mathrm{mol},\qquad N\approx1.50553519\times10^{23}.
$$
The count refers to <water> <molecules>. Their <hydrogen> <chemical atom> count is approximately $2N=3.01107038\times10^{23}$, and their <oxygen> <chemical atom> count is approximately $N$. The mean <mass> per <water> <molecule> is $M/N_{\mathrm A}\approx2.99146\times10^{-26}\,\mathrm{kg}$. Extra displayed digits expose the arithmetic; they do not imply that the sample composition or <mass> was measured that precisely.

The same conversion makes <stoichiometry> a <mass> calculation. For the <chemical equation> $2\mathrm{H_2}+\mathrm{O_2}\to2\mathrm{H_2O}$, complete conversion of $0.250000\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <molecular hydrogen> and $0.125000\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <molecular oxygen> gives $0.250000\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <water>. With the chosen approximate <atomic weights>, the reactant <masses> are $0.50400\,\mathrm g$ and $3.99975\,\mathrm g$, summing to $4.50375\,\mathrm g$. The $2:1:2$ ratio belongs to <molecule> counts and <amount of substance>, not to <masses>; this bookkeeping neglects the much smaller reaction <energy> contribution to <mass>.

= Two-population molar mass example
{parent=Molar mass}

Suppose a synthetic <polymer> population contains three chains of <molar mass> $10000\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$ for every one chain of <molar mass> $40000\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$. Multiplying both chain counts by any common factor leaves the averages unchanged. Substitution into the existing <number-average molar mass> and <weight-average molar mass> definitions gives
$$
M_n=\frac{3(10000)+40000}{4}\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}=17500\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}},\qquad M_w=\frac{3(10000)^2+(40000)^2}{3(10000)+40000}\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\approx27143\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}.
$$
The larger chains are only one quarter of the chain count but supply $40000/70000=4/7$ of the <mass>. Thus a <weighted mean> that emphasizes <mass> gives a larger result than one that weights each <molecule> equally. Total <mass> divided by total chain <amount of substance> gives $M_n$, while $M_w/N_{\mathrm A}$ is not the mean <mass> of a randomly chosen chain. Inferring chain counts from <size-exclusion chromatography> still requires the existing <SEC detector response> and <SEC molar mass calibration> assumptions.

= Vapor-density molar mass measurement
{parent=Molar mass}

An <ideal gas law> measurement obtains <amount of substance> without knowing the <chemical formula> in advance:
$$
n=\frac{pV}{RT},\qquad M_{\rm inferred}=\frac{mRT}{pV}.
$$
Here $p$ is vapor <pressure>, $V$ is its occupied <volume (thermodynamics)>, $T$ is absolute <temperature>, $R$ is the <molar gas constant>, and $m$ is the <mass> of vapor occupying that <volume (thermodynamics)> at those conditions.

Santa Monica College's teaching experiment uses a $125\,\mathrm{mL}$ <Erlenmeyer flask> with a pinhole foil cap, a $600\,\mathrm{mL}$ water bath on a hot plate, a <thermometer>, and a barometer. Excess volatile liquid displaces air. A dissolved dye loses its colour and deposits when the liquid evaporates; three more minutes of heating follow. Cooling condenses retained vapor. Subtracting the dry assembly <mass> from the cooled assembly <mass> supplies $m$. The flask <volume (thermodynamics)> comes from brim-full <water> <mass> and <mass density> at its measured <temperature>. Controls include a dry exterior, excluding bath <water>, and three trials.<11B: The Dumas Method (Experiment) by Santa Monica College>[Santa Monica College, 11B, Experimental Set-up steps 1–6; Trials 1–3 steps 1–4: apparatus, dye endpoint, heating, drying and repetition]{ref}

The inference assumes vapor <pressure> equals atmospheric <pressure>, vapor and bath reach <thermal equilibrium>, the retained vapor <mass> is recovered on cooling, and the vapor obeys the <ideal gas law>. Residual air, <thermal expansion> of the flask, and vapor <temperature> below bath <temperature> are explicit error questions in the teaching experiment. Repeating trials assesses repeatability; it does not remove a shared bias.<11B: The Dumas Method (Experiment) by Santa Monica College>[Lab Report, questions 1–4: residual air, glass expansion, nonideal vapor and incomplete thermal equilibration]{ref}

= Vapor density
{parent=Vapor-density molar mass measurement}

The <density> of a <vapor> relative to a specified reference <gas> at the same <temperature> and <pressure>.

= Molar mass measurement model
{parent=Vapor-density molar mass measurement}

For a synthetic illustration, choose $m=0.3000\,\mathrm g$, $V=125.0\,\mathrm{mL}$, $p=101325\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and $T=373.15\,\mathrm K$. Using <molar gas constant> $R\approx8.314462618\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ gives $n\approx0.00408234\,\mathrm{mol}$ and $M_{\rm inferred}\approx73.4873\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$ under the <ideal gas law>. These are chosen model inputs, not observations of a particular <chemical substance>.

An error's direction follows the model. Extra exterior <water> raises the apparent $m$ and hence $M_{\rm inferred}$. If a fraction $f$ of the hot <gas> particles is residual air, and only the unknown vapor contributes to recovered $m$, an otherwise <ideal gas> calculation gives $M_{\rm inferred}=(1-f)M_{\rm true}$. For a pure nonideal vapor, define $Z=pV/(nRT)$; then $M_{\rm inferred}=M_{\rm true}/Z$, so the direction depends on $Z$. The dye endpoint alone cannot establish gas purity, <thermal equilibrium>, or ideality. These conditional checks explain why the same apparatus cannot provide a universal <molar mass> measurement for arbitrary <mixtures> or substances that decompose on heating.

= Ion
{parent=Chemistry}
{title2=$q=ze$}

An <ion> is a <chemical atom> or a group of <chemical atoms> with a nonzero net <electric charge>. A positive <ion> is a <cation>; a negative <ion> is an <anion>. The whole entity carries the <electric charge>: a <hydronium> <ion> is a group of four <chemical atoms> with net charge $+e$. This definition agrees with https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/I03158[IUPAC Gold Book: ion]{ref}.

For an ordinary <ion> containing only <atomic nuclei> and <electrons>, let $N_p$ count all its <protons> and $N_e$ all its <electrons>. Each <proton> contributes the <elementary charge> $+e$, each <electron> contributes $-e$, and <neutrons> contribute zero. Therefore
$$
q=(N_p-N_e)e=ze,\qquad z=N_p-N_e.
$$
The signed integer $z$ is the net <electric charge> number. Removing an <electron> increases $z$ by one; adding an <electron> decreases $z$ by one. Changing the <electron> count leaves the <atomic number>, and therefore the identity of the <chemical element>, unchanged.

For example, a neutral <sodium> <chemical atom> has $11$ <protons> and $11$ <electrons>. Its <cation> $\mathrm{Na}^+$ still has $11$ <protons>, but only $10$ <electrons>, so $q=(11-10)e=+e$. A neutral <chlorine> <chemical atom> has $17$ <protons> and $17$ <electrons>; its <anion> $\mathrm{Cl}^-$ has $17$ <protons> and $18$ <electrons>, so $q=(17-18)e=-e$. These are particle inventories, not pictures of <atomic orbitals>. See <Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/2.6 Ionic and Molecular Compounds> (§2.6: ions and ionic compounds).

\Image[ion-charge-bookkeeping.png]
{title=<Ion> <electric charge> bookkeeping: neutral <sodium> and <chlorine> <chemical atoms> compared with their <cation> and <anion>}
{description=Every colored dot counts one <proton> or one <electron>. Rows are counting arrays, not spatial models of <atomic nuclei> or <electron shells>. The net <electric charges> are $0,+e,0,-e$.}

One <sodium chloride> <formula unit> accounts for one $\mathrm{Na}^+$ <cation> and one $\mathrm{Cl}^-$ <anion>, whose net <electric charges> sum to zero. Ordinary solid <sodium chloride> is an extended arrangement of <ions>, rather than discrete $\mathrm{NaCl}$ <molecules>. This is a useful distinction: a neutral material can contain charged constituents, and its <chemical formula> tells us their ratio without claiming that each pair is a separate <molecule>.

<Ion> bookkeeping connects <chemistry> to <electric current>: moving <ions> can transport <electric charge>, while stationary <ions> cannot supply a transport <current density>. In <electrochemistry>, both signs of <ions> can contribute to the same <conventional current>. An <ion> opposing a fixed <electric charge> is called a <counterion>.

= Kirchhoff's law of thermochemistry
{parent=Thermochemistry}
{c}
{title2=$(\partial\Delta H/\partial T)_p=\Delta C_p$}

At fixed <pressure>, the <temperature> <derivative> of a reaction or transformation <enthalpy> is the corresponding difference in <heat capacity at constant pressure>:
$$
\left(\frac{\partial\Delta H}{\partial T}\right)_p=\Delta C_p,\qquad
\Delta H(T_2)=\Delta H(T_1)+\int_{T_1}^{T_2}\Delta C_p(T)\,dT.
$$
Use a consistent molar or mass basis and retain each phase's range of stability when applying the relation. A latent enthalpy evaluated along a pressure-dependent <phase coexistence curve> can also have a pressure-variation contribution; the fixed-pressure <derivative> must not automatically be identified with that full <derivative>.

= Hess's law
{c}
{parent=Thermochemistry}
{wiki}

<Hess's law> states that compatible <chemical reaction> <enthalpies> add when their equations add. Reversing a <chemical reaction> reverses its <enthalpy> change; multiplying the entire <chemical reaction> equation by a factor multiplies that change by the same factor. The reason is that <enthalpy> is a <state function>: intermediate states cancel from the differences. A cycle supplies one independent balance relation; it cannot determine two unknown inputs without further information.

The law is named after <Germain Henri Hess>.

For specified initial and final <thermodynamic states>, the change is $\Delta H=H_{\rm final}-H_{\rm initial}$. Because <enthalpy> is an <extensive variable>, a quoted reaction $\Delta H$ belongs to the <amount of substance> specified for each species in its <chemical equation>. Reversing the equation changes the sign; scaling every coefficient scales $\Delta H$; adding compatible equations adds their $\Delta H$ values. A per-<mole> value must say which <chemical equation> and normalization it uses. Match <temperature>, <pressure>, <thermodynamic phases>, <chemical solution> compositions and reference states before cancellation; the same formula in a <gas> and a <liquid> represents different states.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/5.3 Enthalpy>[Chemistry 2e, §5.3: enthalpy conventions before Example 5.8; Hess’s Law, equation scaling and reversal]{ref}

The value of <Hess's law> is that an inaccessible reaction <enthalpy> can be obtained from measurable transformations. The algebra does not require those transformations to be steps of the actual <reaction mechanism>. Neither an <activation energy> nor a <reaction rate> follows from the endpoint difference: a <catalyst> may change the <reaction rate> while preserving the compatible initial and final <enthalpies>.

= Enthalpy cycle
{parent=Hess's law}
{title2=$\sum_{\rm cycle}\Delta H=0$}

An <enthalpy cycle> is a collection of compatible <thermodynamic processes> that returns the entire material inventory to its initial <thermodynamic state>. Its signed <enthalpy> changes sum to zero. This supplies a consistency check and lets a missing change be recovered from the others.

Consider three abstract <thermodynamic states> $A,B,C$ of the same conserved material inventory at common <temperature> and <pressure>. Choose their <molar enthalpies>, on the same one-<mole> basis, as
$$
h_A=0,\qquad h_B=18\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},\qquad h_C=-7\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.
$$
These are constructed values, not material measurements. Endpoint subtraction gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta h_{AB}&=h_B-h_A=+18\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},\\
\Delta h_{BC}&=h_C-h_B=-25\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},\\
\Delta h_{AC}&=h_C-h_A=-7\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus $+18-25=-7$: the two-step route and the direct route have the same <molar enthalpy> change. Closing the route with $C\to A$ gives $+18-25+7=0$. Shifting every $h$ by the same arbitrary reference constant leaves all these differences unchanged.

{id=image-hess-law-enthalpy-state-paths}
{title=Two routes between the same <thermodynamic states> give the same <enthalpy> change}
{description=Original abstract states with <molar enthalpies> $0$, $18$, and $-7\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ on one common basis. Arrows show signed endpoint differences, not an <activation energy> curve, a <reaction mechanism>, or elapsed time.}

The short proof is cancellation:
$$
(H_B-H_A)+(H_C-H_B)=H_C-H_A.
$$
Adding any finite number of consecutive endpoint differences works the same way. For a closed <enthalpy cycle> the final $H_A$ cancels the initial $H_A$. A nonzero experimental residual can therefore expose mismatched states or measurement error; it does not identify which individual input is responsible.

= Hess's law equation cancellation
{c}
{parent=Hess's law}

Keep the <stoichiometry> attached to the <enthalpy>. Use the same abstract $A,B,C$ and suppose each of two entries fixes an <amount of substance> for every species:
$$
\begin{aligned}
R_1:&\quad 2A\longrightarrow2B, &&\Delta H_1=+36\,\mathrm{kJ},\\
R_2:&\quad C\longrightarrow B, &&\Delta H_2=+25\,\mathrm{kJ}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here each unit coefficient represents one <mole> on the common basis. To obtain $A\to C$, halve $R_1$ and reverse $R_2$. Adding those transformed <chemical equations> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\tfrac12R_1:&\quad A\longrightarrow B, &&\tfrac12\Delta H_1=+18\,\mathrm{kJ},\\
-R_2:&\quad B\longrightarrow C, &&-\Delta H_2=-25\,\mathrm{kJ},\\
\text{sum}:&\quad A+B\longrightarrow B+C, &&\Delta H=-7\,\mathrm{kJ}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The equal amounts of $B$ on opposite sides cancel, leaving $A\to C$. Doubling this final <chemical equation> would give $2A\to2C$ and $\Delta H=-14\,\mathrm{kJ}$; its change per <mole> of $A$ would remain $-7\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$.

More generally, write each <chemical equation> as a <stoichiometric vector> $\boldsymbol\nu_j$, with negative entries for <reactants> and positive entries for <reaction products>. Finding coefficients $a_j$ with $\boldsymbol\nu_{\rm target}=\sum_j a_j\boldsymbol\nu_j$ also gives $\Delta H_{\rm target}=\sum_j a_j\Delta H_j$, using compatible state and amount conventions. An identical <chemical species> cancels only with the same <thermodynamic phase> and composition conditions. A leftover <water> or <dilution> step is a real part of the balance.

= Hess's law calorimetry experiment
{c}
{parent=Hess's law}

A practical test compares three transformations involving <sodium hydroxide>: <dissolution> of the <solid>, <neutralization> of its <chemical solution> by <hydrochloric acid>, and reaction of the <solid> directly with <hydrochloric acid>. In the ideal compatible-state notation, the <hydrogen ion> and <hydroxide> give
$$
\begin{aligned}
D:&\quad\mathrm{NaOH(s)\longrightarrow Na^+(aq)+OH^-(aq)},\\
N:&\quad\mathrm{H^+(aq)+OH^-(aq)\longrightarrow H_2O(l)},\\
D+N:&\quad\mathrm{NaOH(s)+H^+(aq)\longrightarrow Na^+(aq)+H_2O(l)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The <hydroxide> cancels, whereas the newly formed <water> remains. <Sodium> and <chloride> <spectator ions> must still be included in the full material inventory when matching actual <chemical solutions>.

The teaching protocol https://www.pc.maricopa.edu/chemistry/151LLWeb/caloimetry.htm[Calorimetry: Heat of Neutralization Reaction & Hess's Law], Procedure A–C, uses nested insulated cups, a cardboard lid and a <thermometer>. It mixes $75\,\mathrm{mL}$ each of $2.0\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ <hydrochloric acid> and <sodium hydroxide> <chemical solutions>; separately it adds $6.00\,\mathrm g$ <solid> <sodium hydroxide> to diluted <hydrochloric acid>, or to $150\,\mathrm{mL}$ <water>. It records initial <temperatures>, stirs, reads <temperature> every $30\,\mathrm s$, repeats runs, and extrapolates cooling trends to mixing time. Rapid weighing limits moisture uptake. The Introduction neglects vessel <heat capacity> and room losses; these are approximations, not a measured calibration. The readout tests whether the inferred <enthalpies> add, rather than directly measuring a <state function>.<Calorimetry: Heat of Neutralization Reaction & Hess's Law, Chemistry 151LL>[Introduction; Procedure A.1–8, B.1–5 and C.1–6; Additional Data Treatment: cup approximation, preparation, repeat runs and mixing-time extrapolation]{ref}

Matching the <chemical equations> alone is insufficient. The direct-acid recipe uses $0.160\,\mathrm{mol}$ <hydrochloric acid>, whereas the solution-mixing recipe uses $0.150\,\mathrm{mol}$. The <dissolution> run also does not automatically reproduce the <water> inventory of the mixed-solution run. Exact closure requires matching initial and final <chemical solution> compositions and adding any necessary mixing or <dilution> <enthalpies>. Agreement after approximate corrections is a limited test, rather than proof of exact cancellation for those recipes.

To improve the <calorimetry>, use <calorimeter calibration> for the participating hardware, and estimate <calorimeter heat-loss correction> under comparable conditions. With an approximately constant total receiving <heat capacity> $C_{\rm tot}=mc_p+C_{\rm cal}$, common initial <temperature>, constant external <pressure>, only expansion <work>, negligible escaping material and negligible bulk <kinetic energy> and <potential energy> changes, the balance is
$$
q_{\rm rxn}+C_{\rm tot}\Delta T+Q_{\rm loss,out}=0,\qquad
\Delta H_{\rm rxn}=q_{\rm rxn}.
$$
Here $m$ is the <chemical solution> <mass>, $c_p$ its <specific heat capacity at constant pressure>, $C_{\rm cal}$ the hardware <heat capacity>, and $Q_{\rm loss,out}$ the signed outward <heat> loss. Different initial <temperatures> require separate warming terms. <Calorimeter calibration> and matching <chemical solution> compositions address different errors.

For an original constructed model, choose $C_{\rm tot}=120\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$ and $\Delta T=3\,\mathrm K$. Neglect outward <heat> loss and other corrections. The receiving <chemical solution> and hardware gain $q_{\rm receiver}=360\,\mathrm J$, so $q_{\rm rxn}=-360\,\mathrm J$. If the completed <extent of reaction> is $\xi=0.020\,\mathrm{mol}$ for a unit-coefficient <chemical equation>, the inferred reaction <enthalpy> per <mole> of that reaction is
$$
\frac{\Delta H_{\rm rxn}}{\xi}
=\frac{-360\,\mathrm J}{0.020\,\mathrm{mol}}
=-18\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.
$$
These chosen numbers illustrate the signs and normalization; they are not measured <sodium hydroxide> data and are independent of the abstract $A,B,C$ example.

= Mass spectrometry
{parent=Chemistry}
{wiki}

<Mass spectrometry> separates and detects <ions> according to <mass-to-charge ratio>. It can distinguish <isotopes> which belong to the same <chemical element> but have different <atomic masses>. The instrument measures <ions>: interpreting the result as a neutral <atomic mass> requires accounting for <electron> removal and its <ionization energy>. For a singly positive <ion> in its <ground state>, $m_+=m_{\rm a}-m_e+I/c^2$, where $m_e$ is the <electron> <rest mass>, $I$ is the <ionization energy>, and $c$ is the <speed of light>.

For a simple illustrative analyzer, positive <ions> of <mass> $m$ and <electric charge> $q=ze$ gain <kinetic energy> $qV=mv^2/2$ across an <electric potential difference> $V$ before entering a transverse uniform <magnetic field> $B$. The <Lorentz force> supplies <centripetal acceleration>, so
$$
qvB=\frac{mv^2}{r},\qquad\frac{m}{q}=\frac{B^2r^2}{2V}.
$$
At fixed $V$ and $B$, the measured bending radius $r$ separates <mass-to-charge ratios>, not <masses> independently of <electric charge>. This ideal model assumes negligible initial <kinetic energy> and speeds small compared with the <speed of light>; the historical focusing <mass spectrograph> used the geometry described below.

The experimental achievement is to turn invisible differences between individual <ions> into separated, calibrated positions. <Aston velocity focusing> explains how an early <mass spectrograph> sharpened its images; the <velocity-selected magnetic mass analyzer> provides an independent model whose equations can be checked directly. The <magnesium isotope mass spectrum> shows why position, width, and intensity carry different information.

= Mass spectrograph
{parent=Mass spectrometry}

A <mass spectrometry> instrument with a photographic readout. In the 1919 apparatus described in <Isotopes by Francis William Aston/Chapter V: The Mass-Spectrograph>, a <gas> discharge produced positive <ions>. Two narrow slits formed a ribbon beam in a <vacuum>; plates applied an <electric field> to spread it, an aperture selected part of it, and a <magnetic field> bent it oppositely. Focusing reduced the spread caused by differing <ion> <speeds>, concentrating a given <mass-to-charge ratio> into a line on a photographic plate.

The observed quantity was line displacement from a fiducial spot. Known reference lines calibrated displacement against <mass-to-charge ratio>; repeated exposures with changed <magnetic field> tested the calibration. Evacuation limited collisions. Comparing line shapes with narrow and wide apertures helped distinguish geometric broadening from photographic spreading. Finite slit widths, plate response, and overlapping molecular or multiply charged <ions> limited interpretation. See <Isotopes by Francis William Aston/Chapter V: The Mass-Spectrograph> (§§33–40: apparatus and photographic procedure; §§43 and 45–46: calibration, aperture comparisons, and accuracy).

In <Mass spectra and isotopes by Francis William Aston> (Nobel lecture, 12 December 1922), the reported <neon> lines at 20 and 22 have companions at 10 and 11 from doubly charged <ions>. That paired pattern and comparison with reference lines support different <atomic masses> within one <chemical element>, rather than an <atomic mass> concentrated at the bulk average. The lecture also describes an earlier failed separation by distillation and partial separation by <diffusion>, showing why the focused <mass spectrum> supplied stronger evidence. These numbers used a historical <oxygen>-based reference scale. The lecture's whole-number rule was an empirical approximation at its stated accuracy, not the modern exact definition $A=Z+N$.<Mass spectra and isotopes by Francis William Aston>[pp. 9–13: separation attempts, focusing, reference lines, charge orders, and neon; pp. 15–17: whole-number rule and historical nuclear interpretation]{ref}

= Aston velocity focusing
{c}
{parent=Mass spectrograph}

In <Mass spectra and isotopes by Francis William Aston>, printed pp. 10–12, Fig. 1, <Francis William Aston> describes successive electric and magnetic deflections in one plane, with opposite senses. This geometry focuses a range of <ion> <speeds>; it does not require the straight-through cancellation of a <velocity selector>.

The reason compensation is possible follows from the <Lorentz force>. In a short region of length $\ell_E$, a small electric deflection angle has magnitude $\theta_E\simeq qE\ell_E/(mv^2)$: the transverse <acceleration> acts for a time $\ell_E/v$, and the deflection angle is the acquired transverse <velocity> divided by $v$. A short magnetic region of length $\ell_B$ gives $\theta_B\simeq qB\ell_B/(mv)$, since the magnetic <force> already contains one factor of <speed>. Slower <ions> therefore receive a disproportionately larger electric deflection.

An original local approximation makes the focusing idea explicit. At fixed <mass-to-charge ratio>, replace each region by a small angular kick and include the drift distances in two positive constants $a$ and $b$. With $t=v/v_0$, write the detector coordinate as
$$
X(t)=\frac{a}{t^2}-\frac{b}{t},\qquad
\left.\frac{dX}{dt}\right|_{t=1}=-2a+b.
$$
Choosing the geometry and fields so that $b=2a$ eliminates the first-order <speed> sensitivity. Indeed,
$$
X(t)-X(1)=a\frac{(t-1)^2}{t^2}.
$$
A fractional <speed> error $\varepsilon=t-1$ then contributes only at order $\varepsilon^2$ in this approximation. This is a local thin-kick explanation, not Aston's exact pole geometry, focal-plane equation, or a prediction of its measured <mass resolution>. Finite slit widths, initial directions, and field boundaries still matter. Unlike a <velocity selector>, focusing can collect nearby <speeds> into a narrow image instead of discarding all but a narrow accepted range.

= Velocity-selected magnetic mass analyzer
{parent=Mass spectrometry}

Reuse the <velocity selector> analysis in the <Thomson charge-to-mass experiment>, but now send positive <ions> with $q=ze$ into a separate magnetic analyzer. The selector fields $E_s$ and $B_s$ give $v_0=E_s/B_s$. If the analyzer field $B_a$ is perpendicular to the selected <velocity>, its <gyroradius> gives
$$
\frac{m}{q}=\frac{B_a r}{v_0}=\frac{B_aB_s r}{E_s},\qquad D=2r
$$
for a semicircular return to the entrance plane. The displayed impact separation $D$ is a diameter. This formula is linear in $r$ at fixed selected <speed>; the fixed-<electric potential difference> model above instead gives $m/q\propto r^2$. They impose different experimental conditions.

For a chosen example, take $E_s=2.00\times10^4\,\mathrm{V/m}$, $B_s=0.200\,\mathrm T$, and $B_a=0.500\,\mathrm T$. Thus $v_0=1.00\times10^5\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Approximating an <ion> <mass> by $24\,\mathrm u$ and setting $q=e$ gives $r\approx49.75\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $D\approx99.50\,\mathrm{mm}$. Here $\mathrm u$ denotes the <unified atomic mass unit>. These are stipulated teaching inputs, not a reported Aston measurement or an exact <magnesium> <atomic mass>.

A finite accepted <speed> range broadens the radius distribution. At fixed $m/q$ and $B_a$, $\delta r/r=\delta v/v$. At fixed <electric charge> and <mass>, $K=mv^2/2$ gives $\delta v/v\simeq\delta K/(2K)$ for a small <kinetic energy> spread. A one-percent <kinetic energy> spread therefore produces roughly a half-percent radius spread before other broadening is included. Its width convention must be specified before converting that statement into a <mass resolving power>.

{description=All curves are stipulated sums of unit-area <Gaussian functions>, not measured data. The first two panels use equal isotope responses and selected <full width at half maximum> values $0.12$ and $1.20$ in $x$. The last panel keeps the reference <isotopic abundances> fixed but multiplies the mass-25 response by two, then normalizes the areas. Neutral <atomic masses> approximate <ion> <masses>; charge-state yields are chosen independently, and no historical intensity or resolving-power claim is made.}

For the narrow singly charged peak at $x\approx24.98584$, the chosen <full width at half maximum> $\Delta x=0.12$ gives <mass resolving power> $R\approx208.2$; widening to $\Delta x=1.20$ gives $R\approx20.82$. Adjacent isotope spacings here are approximately one unit in $x$. Peaks are visibly separated in the first chosen profile and overlap strongly in the second. A small uncertainty in a fitted peak centre would not by itself make the overlapping components resolved.

Even integrated peak areas need a response model. For isotope fraction $f_i$, let $\eta_{i,z}$ collect the probability of <ionization> into <electric charge> state $z$, transmission through the analyzer, and the detection response. In a linear instrument with overall exposure factor $C$, the component area is
$$
A_{i,z}=C f_i\eta_{i,z}.
$$
Equal $\eta_{i,z}$ at a fixed $z$ makes normalized areas equal the fractions; otherwise it does not. In the chosen example, doubling only the mass-25 response changes the normalized areas to $0.71809$, $0.18182$, and $0.10009$, although the neutral sample is unchanged. Photographic darkening introduces another possible nonlinearity: neither line darkness nor peak height is a universal measure of neutral <isotopic abundance>. Calibration of position and calibration of intensity answer different questions. Matched standards, a known <electric charge> assignment, and a response correction make isotope analysis useful; a visually impressive set of peaks alone does not supply those controls.

= Mass-to-charge ratio
{parent=Mass spectrometry}
{title2=$m/q$}
{wiki}

The ratio of an <ion>'s <mass> to its <electric charge>; in <SI> it has units $\mathrm{kg/C}$. A convenient numerical <mass spectrum> coordinate is $x=(m/\mathrm u)/z$, with $q=ze$ and positive integer $z$ here. This $x$ is <dimensionless>. Doubling $z$ at fixed <mass> halves $x$, so the <electric charge> state must be identified before assigning an <isotope>.

= Mass spectrum
{parent=Mass spectrometry}
{wiki}

= Mass spectra
{synonym}

A <mass spectrum> displays detected signal against <mass-to-charge ratio>. Peak positions identify candidate <ions>; peak areas give <isotopic abundances> only after the response and overlapping contributions have been accounted for.

\Image[neon-synthetic-mass-spectrum-weighted-mean.png]
{title=Synthetic <neon> <mass spectra> and the <weighted mean> of neutral <atomic masses>}
{description=This is a model, not a measured <mass spectrum> or a reconstruction of Aston's plate. All <ions> have $z=+1$; neutral <atomic masses> approximate <ion> <masses>. Chosen <Gaussian functions> have unit area and widths $\sigma=0.04$ and $0.70$ in the <dimensionless> coordinate $x$. Equal responses make component areas equal the displayed reference <isotopic abundances>. The inset reveals the small <neon-21> peak. The bottom diagram places the <weighted mean> between the component <atomic masses>; it is not an additional <isotope>.}

Broadening can hide a weak component without changing its area in this ideal model. An actual <mass spectrum> can also contain molecular <ions>, different <electric charge> states, background, and unequal detection responses. A missing visible peak therefore cannot by itself prove the absence of an <isotope>. Conversely, a fractional <atomic weight> is a property of a population: there is no requirement for an <ion> peak at the <weighted mean>.

= Mass resolution
{parent=Mass spectrometry}
{title2=$R=x/\Delta x$}
{wiki}

= Mass resolving power
{synonym}

<Mass resolving power> compares a peak coordinate $x$ with a stated width or separation criterion $\Delta x$. With $\Delta x$ taken as <full width at half maximum>, a <Gaussian function> of <standard deviation> $\sigma$ has
$$
\Delta x=2\sqrt{2\log2}\,\sigma,\qquad R=\frac{x}{\Delta x}.
$$
At the <neon-20> coordinate in the synthetic <mass spectrum>, $\sigma=0.04$ gives $\Delta x\approx0.0942$ and $R\approx212$, while $\sigma=0.70$ gives $\Delta x\approx1.648$ and $R\approx12.1$. The width criterion and relative peak areas both matter when judging whether a small neighboring <isotope> can be distinguished. <Mass resolution> also differs from the accuracy of a calibrated peak position.

= Distillation
{parent=Chemistry}
{wiki}

Distillation separates a <mixture> by producing <vapor> from a <liquid> and collecting the condensed <liquid>, called the distillate. The more volatile component can have a higher <mole fraction> in the <vapor>. <Simple distillation> collects this condensate directly; <fractional distillation> adds repeated contact in a <fractionating column>.<Chem2O06 Laboratory Manual by McMaster University/Distillation introduction>[Chem2O06 Laboratory Manual by McMaster University; General Principles; A Mixture of Ideal Liquids; Fractional Distillation]{ref}

Two questions must be kept separate. <Vapor-liquid equilibrium> describes which compositions can coexist. A <distillation material balance> accounts for how much of each component leaves in each product. A product can be enriched without containing all the original component, and even a feasible <distillation material balance> does not establish that an apparatus can make those products.

= Vacuum distillation
{parent=Distillation}

<Vacuum distillation> carries out <distillation> at reduced <pressure>.

= Laboratory condenser
{parent=Distillation}

A <laboratory condenser> removes <heat> from <vapor> so that it becomes <liquid>. In <distillation>, the downstream <laboratory condenser> makes collectable distillate. Complete condensation, without loss or reaction, preserves the entering <vapor>'s overall component <mole fractions>; cooling alone does not give another enrichment step. Partial condensation can instead leave two streams of different composition, requiring both <vapor-liquid equilibrium> and a <distillation material balance>.

= Simple distillation
{parent=Distillation}

In <simple distillation>, pot <vapor> passes directly to the <laboratory condenser>. An initial small condensate sample can approximate <vapor-liquid equilibrium> with the current pot <liquid>. Removing enriched distillate depletes the pot's more volatile component. A collected fraction averages the changing condensate composition, rather than preserving the first-drop <mole fraction>.<Chem2O06 Laboratory Manual by McMaster University/Distillation introduction>[Chem2O06 Laboratory Manual by McMaster University; A Mixture of Ideal Liquids, discussion of first and later fractions]{ref}

= Fractional distillation
{parent=Distillation}
{wiki}

<Fractional distillation> brings rising <vapor> into contact with descending <liquid> in a <fractionating column>. Packing supplies contact area; it does not act as a sieve for individual <molecules>. Repeated exchange can enrich the rising <vapor> in the more volatile component. Some condensed <liquid> returns downward as <reflux>, rather than all being withdrawn as distillate.<Distillation by Hong Kong University of Science and Technology>[Introduction, paragraphs on reflux and plates, trays or packing]{ref}

An <equilibrium stage> is an idealization of this contact. A real <fractionating column> has finite contact time and imperfect wetting and mixing; its length is not automatically a count of <equilibrium stages>. Product withdrawal and <reflux> affect the separation together. Thus the <repeated ideal-stage enrichment> example illustrates the composition advantage of repeated equilibration, but supplies neither a column design nor a specified <reflux> requirement.<Hyper-TVT by ETH Zurich/Distillation efficiency>[Hyper-TVT by ETH Zurich; §5.6 Efficiency, equilibrium versus actual plates and flow-dependent efficiency]{ref}

= Fractionating column
{parent=Fractional distillation}
{wiki}

A <fractionating column> provides contact between rising <vapor> and returning <liquid> during <fractional distillation>. Packing or trays provide contact area; the resulting separation depends on both <vapor-liquid equilibrium> and the actual flow and exchange between the <thermodynamic phases>.<Distillation by Hong Kong University of Science and Technology>[Introduction, paragraphs 3–4]{ref}

= Equilibrium stage
{parent=Fractional distillation}

An <equilibrium stage> models a contact whose outgoing <liquid> and <vapor> satisfy <vapor-liquid equilibrium> at the stage's <temperature> and <pressure>. It is a composition model, not a claim that each piece of packing reaches <thermodynamic equilibrium>.<Hyper-TVT by ETH Zurich/Distillation efficiency>[Hyper-TVT by ETH Zurich; §5.6 Efficiency, opening equilibrium-plate assumption]{ref}

= Reflux
{parent=Fractional distillation}
{wiki}

<Reflux> is condensed <liquid> returned toward the heated part of a <distillation> apparatus. In a <fractionating column>, descending <reflux> contacts rising <vapor>. Returning more condensate also changes the amount available for collection, so enrichment and product recovery must be considered together.<Distillation by Hong Kong University of Science and Technology>[Introduction, reflux paragraph; Intended Learning Outcomes, reflux-ratio dependence]{ref}

= Relative volatility
{parent=Distillation}
{title2=$\alpha=\dfrac{y/(1-y)}{x/(1-x)}$}
{wiki}

For a two-component <mixture>, let $x$ be component A's <mole fraction> in the <liquid> and $y$ its <mole fraction> in the coexisting <vapor>. At interior compositions, define the relative volatility of A with respect to B by the ratio of composition odds:
$$
\alpha=\frac{y/(1-y)}{x/(1-x)}.
$$
Thus $\alpha>1$ means A is enriched in the <vapor>, while $\alpha=1$ means $y=x$. Solving this definition gives
$$
\alpha x(1-y)=y(1-x),\qquad
 y=\frac{\alpha x}{1+(\alpha-1)x}.
$$
Here a constant <relative volatility> is a stipulated model of <vapor-liquid equilibrium>, not a measured property of a named <chemical solution>. Real <relative volatility> can vary with composition, <temperature>, and <pressure>. The relation specifies no absolute <temperature> scale: even fixing $\alpha=2$ does not determine a <boiling>-<temperature> curve or a <heat> requirement.

= Repeated ideal-stage enrichment
{parent=Relative volatility}
{title2=$x_{n+1}=2x_n/(1+x_n)$}

Stipulate a constant <relative volatility> $\alpha=2$. The <vapor-liquid equilibrium> model then becomes
$$
y=\frac{2x}{1+x},\qquad y-x=\frac{x(1-x)}{1+x}>0\quad(0<x<1).
$$
An initial <liquid> <mole fraction> $x_0=3/10$ gives an equilibrium <vapor> <mole fraction> $y_0=6/13\approx0.461538$. Now imagine completely condensing a small sample of that <vapor> and using this condensate as a fresh <liquid> for another equilibration. Repeating this formal operation gives
$$
x_0=\frac3{10}=0.300000
\;\longmapsto\;
x_1=\frac6{13}\approx0.461538
\;\longmapsto\;
x_2=\frac{12}{19}\approx0.631579
\;\longmapsto\;
x_3=\frac{24}{31}\approx0.774194.
$$
These are original model calculations of ideal-stage enrichment. They are not measured compositions, a <reflux> design, or successive compositions of one pot undergoing <simple distillation>. That pot loses the more volatile component and becomes depleted; each step here instead starts from the previous enriched condensate. The displayed sequence specifies compositions, not the recoverable amount at each step.

The algebra makes the enrichment especially transparent. With composition odds $r_n=x_n/(1-x_n)$, every formal step doubles the odds, so
$$
r_n=2^n\frac37,\qquad x_n=\frac{3\,2^n}{7+3\,2^n}.
$$
For every finite $n$, $x_n<1$, although $x_n\to1$ as $n\to\infty$. This explains why repeated ideal equilibration can improve composition without making finite-step perfect separation a consequence of the model.

= Distillation material balance
{parent=Distillation}
{title2=$F=D+B,\quad Fz=Dx_D+Bx_B$}

Consider a nonreacting two-component <mixture> processed into just two products, with no losses or unaccounted inventory. Let $F,D,B$ be feed, distillate, and bottoms <amount of substance> values in <moles>, and let $z,x_D,x_B$ be their component-A <mole fractions>. These are whole-stream compositions; $x_D$ is not automatically the instantaneous equilibrium <vapor> composition $y$ above the pot. Total and component balances give
$$
F=D+B,\qquad Fz=Dx_D+Bx_B.
$$
The component-B balance follows by subtracting the second equation from the first. Eliminating $B$ gives, for $x_D\ne x_B$,
$$
\frac DF=\frac{z-x_B}{x_D-x_B},\qquad
\frac BF=\frac{x_D-z}{x_D-x_B}.
$$
Nonnegative product amounts require $z$ between $x_B$ and $x_D$. For an original stipulated feed $F=1\,\mathrm{mol}$, $z=0.35$, $x_D=0.80$, and $x_B=0.10$,
$$
D=\frac5{14}\,\mathrm{mol},\qquad B=\frac9{14}\,\mathrm{mol}.
$$
Check the component inventories:
$$
Dx_D=\frac27\,\mathrm{mol},\qquad Bx_B=\frac9{140}\,\mathrm{mol},\qquad
\frac27+\frac9{140}=\frac7{20}=0.35.
$$
The distillate contains a fraction $(Dx_D)/(Fz)=40/49\approx0.816327$ of the original component A, despite its higher <mole fraction> $x_D=0.80$. This distinction between composition and recovery is useful when deciding how much enriched product to collect. These balances determine neither <temperatures>, <heat> input, nor the number of <equilibrium stages>; they do not prove that the specified products are attainable in a particular <fractionating column>.

= Paired simple and fractional distillation experiment
{parent=Distillation}

McMaster University's 1997/98 laboratory manual compares <simple distillation> and <fractional distillation> using charges of $25\,\mathrm{mL}$ each of <cyclohexane> and <p-xylene>. Part B uses a $100\,\mathrm{mL}$ round-bottom flask and a <fractionating column>. The protocol specifies a <boiling chip>, a heating mantle on a ring support, and the top of the <thermometer> bulb midway in the opening toward the <laboratory condenser>. Heating is adjusted for roughly one drop per second. Students record head <temperature> every $2\,\mathrm{mL}$ collected, up to $45\,\mathrm{mL}$, and plot both runs against collected <volume (thermodynamics)> on the same axes.<Chem2O06 Laboratory Manual by McMaster University/Experiment 3 procedures>[Chem2O06 Laboratory Manual by McMaster University; Parts A–B, Figure 3.5 position instruction, and Recording of Results]{ref}

= Azeotrope
{parent=Distillation}
{wiki}

An <azeotrope> has equal coexisting <liquid> and <vapor> compositions: $y=x$. Ordinary <fractional distillation> cannot cross this composition boundary at fixed <pressure>; more packing does not remove it. A constant head <temperature> therefore does not establish a pure <chemical substance>.<Chem2O06 Laboratory Manual by McMaster University/Distillation introduction>[Chem2O06 Laboratory Manual by McMaster University; Liquids that Form Azeotropes, constant-temperature mixtures and limits on separation]{ref} The stipulated $\alpha=2$ model has $y>x$ throughout $0<x<1$, so it has no interior <azeotrope> and cannot represent this limit.
