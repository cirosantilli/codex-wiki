= Riemannian curvature tensor

= Riemann tensor
{c}
{synonym}

The Riemann tensor measures the failure of covariant derivatives to commute.

= Commutator of covariant derivatives
{parent=Riemann curvature tensor}
{title2=$[\nabla_\alpha,\nabla_\beta]V^\mu=R^\mu{}_{\nu\alpha\beta}V^\nu$}

= Curvature commutator
{synonym}

For a <torsion-free connection>, the <commutator> of coordinate <covariant derivatives> vanishes on a <scalar field> and acts on a <vector field> through the displayed <Riemann curvature tensor>. On a <covector field> it instead gives $[\nabla_\alpha,\nabla_\beta]A_\mu=-R^\nu{}_{\mu\alpha\beta}A_\nu$. The <curvature commutator is a tensor derivation>, so each index of a general <tensor> contributes independently with the corresponding sign. Including the lower derivative index in the second <covariant derivative> is essential for this identity.

= Tensorial and directional second covariant derivative commutators
{parent=Commutator of covariant derivatives}

For a <vector field> $V$, the tensorial second derivative is $\nabla^2V(X,Y)=\nabla_X\nabla_YV-\nabla_{\nabla_XY}V$. With $R(X,Y)V=\nabla_X\nabla_YV-\nabla_Y\nabla_XV-\nabla_{[X,Y]}V$ and geometric <torsion tensor> $\mathcal T$, antisymmetrization gives
$$
\nabla^2V(X,Y)-\nabla^2V(Y,X)=R(X,Y)V-\nabla_{\mathcal T(X,Y)}V.
$$
This follows by subtracting the two definitions and using $\nabla_XY-\nabla_YX=[X,Y]+\mathcal T(X,Y)$. By contrast, the directional operator commutator subtracts no derivative-index connection and obeys $[\nabla_X,\nabla_Y]V=R(X,Y)V+\nabla_{[X,Y]}V$. On scalars the directional commutator is $[X,Y]f$, while the tensorial Hessian commutator is $-\mathcal T(X,Y)f$. Distinguishing these objects prevents an apparent contradiction when a scalar Hessian has an antisymmetric part despite commuting coordinate vector fields.

= Curvature sign convention
{parent=Riemann curvature tensor}
{title2=$R_{\rm reversed}=-R$}

Definitions of the <Riemann curvature tensor> can differ by an overall sign, independent of the <metric signature>. Reversing that definition reverses the <Ricci tensor>, <Ricci scalar> and <Einstein tensor>, as well as the <curvature> side of the <geodesic deviation> identity and the <Palatini identity>. A derivation must choose the contraction and <derivative> ordering explicitly before combining formulas from different conventions. Changing a <metric signature> alone is a separate operation.

= Double divergence of a contravariant tensor
{parent=Riemann curvature tensor}
{title2=$T^{ab}{}_{;ab}=T^{ab}{}_{;ba}$}

For a <Levi-Civita connection>, a contravariant rank-two <tensor> satisfies $T^{ab}{}_{;ab}=T^{ab}{}_{;ba}$ without itself being symmetric. The <Ricci identity> gives their difference as $R_{pb}(T^{pb}-T^{bp})$, which vanishes because the <Ricci tensor> is symmetric. Zero torsion alone for a general affine connection does not ensure this symmetry.

= Pair symmetry from the normal-coordinate curvature formula
{parent=Riemann curvature tensor}
{title2=$R_{ij,kl}=R_{kl,ij}$}

With convention $R(X,Y)=\nabla_{[X,Y]}-[\nabla_X,\nabla_Y]$, and $R_{ij,kl}=g(R(\partial_i,\partial_j)\partial_k,\partial_l)$, normal coordinates give
$$
R_{ij,kl}=\tfrac12(g_{jk,il}+g_{il,jk}-g_{jl,ik}-g_{ik,jl})
$$
at their center. Symmetry of the metric and commutation of second derivatives prove pair exchange. The opposite curvature convention changes the formula's overall sign but preserves all these algebraic symmetries.

= Bianchi identities from covariant derivative commutators
{parent=Riemann curvature tensor}

The <first Bianchi identity> follows by applying cyclic curvature commutators to the gradient of an arbitrary <scalar field>: its symmetric Hessian makes the third-derivative terms cancel, leaving $R_{abc}{}^d+R_{bca}{}^d+R_{cab}{}^d=0$. For the <second Bianchi identity>, write the vector-bundle derivative as $D_a=\partial_a+\Gamma_a$, so $[D_b,D_c]=\mathcal R_{bc}$. The operator <Jacobi identity> gives the cyclic sum of $\partial_a\mathcal R_{bc}+[\Gamma_a,\mathcal R_{bc}]$ equal to zero. Restoring the connection terms on the two curvature-form indices gives precisely $\nabla_aR_{bcd}{}^e+\nabla_bR_{cad}{}^e+\nabla_cR_{abd}{}^e=0$: the additional terms cancel because the connection is torsion-free and curvature is antisymmetric in its first pair.

= Curvature commutator is a tensor derivation
{parent=Riemann curvature tensor}
{title2=$\Delta_{ab}(Q\otimes S)=(\Delta_{ab}Q)\otimes S+Q\otimes\Delta_{ab}S$}

For a torsion-free <linear connection on a manifold>, the commutator of two <covariant derivatives> obeys the tensor-product <Leibniz rule>: mixed first-derivative terms cancel after antisymmetrizing. It vanishes on <scalar fields>, so $\Delta_{ab}(fQ)=f\Delta_{ab}Q$; its action is therefore pointwise tensorial. Its vector action defines the <Riemann curvature tensor>. Applying the commutator to a scalar contraction proves that every covariant tensor index contributes minus the corresponding curvature action, while every contravariant index contributes plus. Torsion-free does not by itself imply <metric compatibility>.

= Three-dimensional curvature from the Ricci tensor
{parent=Riemann curvature tensor}
{title2=$\operatorname{Ric}=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \operatorname{Riem}=0\quad(n=3)$}

In dimension three the <Riemann curvature tensor> is $R_{ijpq}=g_{ip}S_{jq}-g_{iq}S_{jp}-g_{jp}S_{iq}+g_{jq}S_{ip}$, where $S$ is the <Schouten tensor>. Consequently every three-dimensional <Ricci-flat Riemannian manifold> is a <flat Riemannian manifold>. The implication fails in higher dimensions.

= Kretschmann scalar
{c}
{parent=Riemann curvature tensor}
{title2=$K=R_{abcd}R^{abcd}$}

The Kretschmann scalar is the scalar <tensor contraction> of two copies of the <Riemann curvature tensor>. In <Schwarzschild spacetime>, with metric coefficient $1-2M/r$ and $G=c=1$, it is $48M^2/r^6$. Divergence of this scalar along a curve approaching a proposed regular point proves that there is no twice continuously differentiable, nondegenerate metric extension through that point: such an extension would have continuous finite curvature components and hence a finite scalar contraction. This gives a coordinate-independent test of a <curvature singularity>. Boundedness of this single scalar does not prove regularity.

= Curvature commutator on a covariant tensor
{parent=Riemann curvature tensor}

= Ricci identity
{c}
{synonym}

For a rank-two covariant tensor, the derivative commutator contributes one curvature action on each index:
$$
[\nabla_\alpha,\nabla_\beta]W_{\mu\nu}
=-R^\rho{}_{\mu\alpha\beta}W_{\rho\nu}
-R^\rho{}_{\nu\alpha\beta}W_{\mu\rho}.
$$

= Curvature commutator on a contravariant tensor
{parent=Riemann curvature tensor}

For a type-$(2,0)$ tensor, the <Ricci identity> acts once on each contravariant index:
$$
[\nabla_\alpha,\nabla_\beta]T^{\mu\nu}
=R^\mu{}_{\rho\alpha\beta}T^{\rho\nu}
+R^\nu{}_{\rho\alpha\beta}T^{\mu\rho}.
$$

= Geodesic deviation
{parent=Riemann curvature tensor}
{wiki}
{title2=$\nabla_U\nabla_UJ=R(U,J)U$}

For a family of affinely parametrized geodesics with tangent $U$ and connecting field $J$, the Jacobi equation in the convention used here is
$$
\nabla_U\nabla_UJ=R(U,J)U.
$$
It describes the relative acceleration produced by spacetime curvature.

Here $J$ describes an infinitesimal separation and the <covariant derivatives> compare it in a frame transported along the reference <geodesic>. The <Riemann curvature tensor> thus becomes something measured with neighboring trajectories. The Newtonian version follows by differentiating <gravitational acceleration> with respect to position. In the exterior of a spherical mass, let $\xi_r$ be radial separation and $\xi_\perp$ separation in either transverse direction. <Newton's law of universal gravitation> gives
$$
\ddot\xi_r=\frac{2GM}{r^3}\xi_r,\qquad
\ddot\xi_\perp=-\frac{GM}{r^3}\xi_\perp.
$$
The upper particle falls less strongly than the lower one, stretching their radial separation; side-by-side particles accelerate toward converging radial directions, compressing their transverse separation. See https://sites.science.oregonstate.edu/physics/coursewikis/GGR/book/ggr/tidal.html[Oregon State's derivation of tidal acceleration].

For a spherical <Earth> model with $r=R_\oplus=6.371\times10^6\,\mathrm m$, $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ and $GM=gR_\oplus^2$, a one-metre radial separation gives $\ddot\xi_r=2g/R_\oplus=3.07958\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$; a one-metre transverse separation gives $-1.53979\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. Initially comoving particles therefore change separation after one second by approximately $\tfrac12\ddot\xi\,t^2$: $+1.54$ micrometres radially or $-0.770$ micrometres transversely. This short-time calculation holds the reference radius fixed to leading order. Its beauty is that common falling motion drops out, leaving a measurable stretching pattern that reveals curvature.

= Tidal acceleration
{parent=Geodesic deviation}
{title2=$a_{\mathrm{rel}}^a=-R^a{}_{bcd}U^b\xi^cU^d$}

The relative acceleration of neighboring freely falling bodies is measured by <geodesic deviation>, comparing their infinitesimal separation $\xi$ in a <parallel transport> frame along a reference <timelike geodesic> with tangent $U$. With $R^a{}_{bcd}$ defined by the <covariant derivative> commutator, it has the displayed form. In the <Newtonian limit> the corresponding <tidal tensor> is the spatial gradient of the <gravitational acceleration>. A single freely falling body can remove local gravitational acceleration by its frame choice, whereas relative tidal acceleration measures <curvature>.

= Weak-field tidal curvature in a stationary metric
{parent=Tidal acceleration}
{title2=$R^i{}_{0j0}=-\tfrac12\partial_i\partial_jg_{00}$}

Use signature $(-,+,+,+)$, $x^0=ct$ and $R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu\Gamma^\rho{}_{\nu\sigma}-\partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\sigma}+\cdots$. For a weak stationary metric, the <Christoffel symbol> satisfies $\Gamma^i{}_{00}=-\tfrac12\partial_ig_{00}$ to first order. Differentiation gives the displayed <Riemann curvature tensor> component; products of connections are second order. For $g_{00}=-1+2GM/(c^2r)$ it is $GM(\delta_{ij}-3x_ix_j/r^2)/(c^2r^3)$. Its radial eigenvalue is negative and its transverse eigenvalues positive, giving radial stretching and transverse compression in the <geodesic deviation> equation with a positive curvature term on the left.

= Local tidal acceleration scaling
{parent=Geodesic deviation}
{title2=$a_{\mathrm{tidal}}=O(\mathcal R L)$}

The <geodesic deviation> equation makes the relative gravitational acceleration proportional to the separation $L$ and a typical <Riemann curvature tensor> component $\mathcal R$. Thus tidal acceleration vanishes as $L\to0$ at fixed bounded curvature. A uniform <electric field> can give different accelerations $qE/m$ at the same point, independently of separation. Relative tidal changes accumulated over a fixed long time need not vanish as a fraction of the initial separation; a local spacetime experiment must control its duration as well as its spatial size.

= Conserved deviation energy in constant curvature
{parent=Geodesic deviation}
{title2=$K=|DS|^2-\kappa|S|^2$}

In <constant sectional curvature> $\kappa$, a <deviation vector> orthogonal to a unit <timelike geodesic> obeys $D^2S=\kappa S$, where $D=\nabla_T$. Therefore $K=g(DS,DS)-\kappa g(S,S)$ is constant, since its derivative is $2g(DS,D^2S)-2\kappa g(S,DS)=0$. Its squared length $f=g(S,S)$ satisfies $\ddot f=2K+4\kappa f$. In four spacetime dimensions, $\kappa=R/12$.

= Exponential and oscillatory geodesic deviation
{parent=Conserved deviation energy in constant curvature}
{title2=$D^2S=\kappa S$}

In a <parallel-propagated orthonormal frame>, orthogonal <geodesic deviation> in <constant sectional curvature> reduces to three scalar equations $\ddot S^i=\kappa S^i$. For $\kappa>0$, $S=Ae^{\sqrt\kappa\tau}+Be^{-\sqrt\kappa\tau}$: generic initial data grow exponentially, but the pure decaying mode is a counterexample to universal growth. Initially comoving neighbors have $S=S(0)\cosh(\sqrt\kappa\tau)$. For $\kappa<0$, $S=A\cos(\sqrt{-\kappa}\tau)+B\sin(\sqrt{-\kappa}\tau)$, so the deviation is bounded and oscillatory. Simultaneous focusing requires compatible vector initial data and is not automatic for every oscillatory solution.

= Orthogonality of proper-time deviation vectors
{parent=Geodesic deviation}
{title2=$\frac{d}{d\tau}g(S,T)=0$}

For a <geodesic variation> of unit <timelike geodesics>, <proper time> normalization holds across the entire variation: $g(T,T)=-1$. <Metric compatibility> and $\nabla_TS=\nabla_ST$ then give $d g(S,T)/d\tau=\tfrac12S(g(T,T))=0$. Consequently an initially orthogonal <deviation vector> stays orthogonal. The common normalization is essential: an arbitrary family of affinely parametrized curves with different tangent norms does not imply this conclusion.

= Jacobi field
{parent=Geodesic deviation}
{wiki}

A Jacobi field is the infinitesimal connecting vector between neighboring members of a one-parameter family of geodesics. It satisfies the geodesic-deviation equation along the reference geodesic.

= Jacobi fields vanishing at their initial point
{c}
{parent=Jacobi field}
{title2=$J(t)=(d\exp_p)_{tV}(tD_tJ(0))$}

For $\gamma(t)=\exp_p(tV)$, every <Jacobi field> with $J(0)=0$ is uniquely of the form
$$
J(t)=(d\exp_p)_{tV}(tB),\qquad B=D_tJ(0)\in T_pM.
$$
Differentiate the <geodesic variation> $\exp_p(t(V+sB))$ in $s$ to obtain the formula. Uniqueness of the Jacobi equation with prescribed initial value and derivative shows that all such fields are obtained. A nontrivial field vanishing at $t=L>0$ therefore corresponds to a nonzero vector in the kernel of $(d\exp_p)_{LV}$, giving the <conjugate point> criterion.

= Jacobi curvature operator
{c}
{parent=Jacobi field}
{title2=$\mathcal R_X(v)=R(v,X)X$}

For the <Riemann curvature tensor> convention $R(X,Y)Z=\nabla_X\nabla_YZ-\nabla_Y\nabla_XZ-\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z$, define $\mathcal R_X(v)=R(v,X)X$. Curvature pair interchange makes this operator self-adjoint. If $v\perp X$, then $\langle\mathcal R_Xv,v\rangle=K(v,X)|v|^2|X|^2$. The <Jacobi field> equation is $D_t^2J+\mathcal R_{\dot\gamma}J=0$. The operator $v\mapsto R(X,v)X$ is $-\mathcal R_X$ and is likewise self-adjoint; this sign distinction matters when testing curvature inequalities.

= Jacobi fields from conjugation at a central endpoint
{c}
{parent=Jacobi field}
{title2=$J_X(t)=Xe^{tA}-e^{tA}X$}

For a <Lie group> with a <bi-invariant Riemannian metric>, vary $\gamma(t)=e^{tA}$ by conjugation: $F(s,t)=e^{sX}e^{tA}e^{-sX}$. Its <Jacobi field> is $J_X(t)=Xe^{tA}-e^{tA}X$. If $e^A$ is central, all these fields vanish at both endpoints. Their space has dimension $\dim\mathfrak g-\dim\mathfrak z(A)$, since the kernel is the <centralizer of an element of a Lie algebra>.

= Endpoint-vanishing normal Jacobi fields have dimension at most n-1
{parent=Jacobi field}
{title2=$\dim\{J\perp\dot\gamma:J(0)=J(1)=0\}\le n-1$}

In dimension $n\ge2$, along a nonconstant <geodesic>, such a <Jacobi field> is determined injectively by $D_tJ(0)$, which lies in the $(n-1)$-dimensional normal space. On the round unit sphere the geodesic from a point to its antipode, parametrized with speed $\pi$ on $[0,1]$, has fields $J(t)=\sin(\pi t)E(t)$ for all parallel normal fields $E$, attaining the bound. For a constant geodesic the endpoint-vanishing Jacobi field space is zero.

= Symmetric Hessian of a scalar field
{parent=Riemann curvature tensor}

For a torsion-free connection, $U_\mu=\partial_\mu\phi$ satisfies $\nabla_\alpha U_\beta=\nabla_\beta U_\alpha$.

= Third covariant derivatives of a scalar
{parent=Symmetric Hessian of a scalar field}

Use the sequential convention $\varphi_{;lmn}=\nabla_n\nabla_m\nabla_l\varphi$ and define $R$ by $X^a{}_{;mn}-X^a{}_{;nm}=R^a{}_{bmn}X^b$. The <symmetric Hessian of a scalar field> gives symmetry in $l,m$, but the <curvature commutator on a covariant tensor> gives $\varphi_{;l[mn]}=-R^p{}_{lmn}\varphi_{;p}/2$. Averaging the three distinct permutations yields $\varphi_{;(lm)n}=\varphi_{;(lmn)}-(R^p{}_{lmn}+R^p{}_{mln})\varphi_{;p}/3$. Thus a scalar's third covariant derivative is not generally totally symmetric.

= First Bianchi identity
{parent=Riemann curvature tensor}

For a torsion-free connection, $R^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}+R^\mu{}_{\beta\gamma\alpha}+R^\mu{}_{\gamma\alpha\beta}=0$.

= Second Bianchi identity
{parent=Riemann curvature tensor}

For the <Levi-Civita connection>, the cyclic covariant derivative of curvature vanishes: $\nabla_{[e}R^a{}_{|b|cd]}=0$. Contracting this identity gives the <contracted Bianchi identity>.

= Weyl tensor
{title2=$C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}$}
{c}
{parent=Riemann curvature tensor}
{wiki}

The Weyl tensor is the completely trace-free part of the <Riemann curvature tensor>. In four dimensions,
$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}
={}&R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}
-\frac12(
g_{\alpha\gamma}R_{\beta\delta}
+g_{\beta\delta}R_{\alpha\gamma}\\
&\qquad
-g_{\alpha\delta}R_{\beta\gamma}
-g_{\beta\gamma}R_{\alpha\delta})
+\frac R6(
g_{\alpha\gamma}g_{\beta\delta}
-g_{\alpha\delta}g_{\beta\gamma}).
\end{aligned}
$$

= Magnetic part of the Weyl tensor
{parent=Weyl tensor}
{title2=$B_{ab}=\tfrac12\epsilon_{acde}C^{de}{}_{bf}n^cn^f$}

= Magnetic Weyl tensor
{synonym}

In four spacetime dimensions, relative to a timelike <unit normal> $n$, define $B_{ab}=\tfrac12\epsilon_{acde}C^{de}{}_{bf}n^cn^f$, with the chosen spacetime <metric volume tensor>. This is the dual tidal part of the <Weyl tensor>. Normal contractions vanish by curvature and volume-tensor antisymmetry. Its sign depends on the specified orientation and dualization convention.

= Electric part of the Weyl tensor
{parent=Weyl tensor}
{title2=$E_{ab}=C_{acbd}n^cn^d$}

= Electric Weyl tensor
{synonym}

Relative to a timelike <unit normal> $n$, the electric part is $E_{ab}=C_{acbd}n^cn^d$. It describes the tidal component of the <Weyl tensor> measured by that observer. Antisymmetry in each curvature pair makes it spatial in both slots; pair symmetry and vanishing traces make it symmetric and trace-free.

= Trace-free property of the Weyl tensor
{parent=Weyl tensor}
{c}

Every contraction of the Weyl tensor vanishes. Contracting its first and third indices directly cancels the Ricci and scalar-curvature terms; antisymmetry in each pair and symmetry under exchanging the two pairs reduce all other contractions to this one or make them vanish immediately.

= Weyl tensor of a conformally flat metric
{parent=Weyl tensor}
{c}

Every <conformally flat metric> in dimension at least four has identically zero Weyl tensor. The Weyl tensor is the obstruction to local conformal flatness in dimensions at least four.

= First-pair antisymmetry of the Riemann tensor
{parent=Riemann curvature tensor}

Applying the curvature commutator to the parallel metric gives $R_{\mu\alpha\beta\gamma}=-R_{\alpha\mu\beta\gamma}$.

= Ricci tensor
{parent=Riemann curvature tensor}
{c}

The Ricci tensor is the contraction $R_{\alpha\beta}=R^\mu{}_{\alpha\mu\beta}$ and is symmetric for the Levi-Civita connection.

= Schouten tensor
{c}
{parent=Ricci tensor}
{title2=$S_{ij}=\frac1{n-2}(R_{ij}-\frac{R}{2(n-1)}g_{ij})$}

For dimension $n\geq3$, the Schouten tensor packages the <Ricci tensor> and <scalar curvature> in the trace part of the <Riemann curvature tensor>. In dimension three it is $S_{ij}=R_{ij}-Rg_{ij}/4$, and the full <Riemann curvature tensor> is reconstructed from it.

= Ricci tensor in two dimensions
{parent=Ricci tensor}

The algebraic symmetries of the <Riemann curvature tensor> leave only one independent curvature component in two dimensions and force
$$
R_{\alpha\beta}=\frac12g_{\alpha\beta}R.
$$
Consequently the <Einstein tensor> vanishes identically.

= Two-dimensional general relativity
{parent=Ricci tensor in two dimensions}

With no cosmological term, the two-dimensional <Einstein field equations> reduce identically on the geometric side to $G_{\alpha\beta}=0$. They impose $T_{\alpha\beta}=0$ and supply no local metric dynamics; the Einstein-Hilbert action is topological.

= Contracted Bianchi identity
{parent=Ricci tensor}

The Ricci tensor and scalar satisfy $\nabla^\alpha R_{\alpha\beta}=\tfrac12\nabla_\beta R$.

= Double divergence of the Riemann tensor
{parent=Contracted Bianchi identity}
{title2=$R^{ik}{}_{mn;ik}=0$}

For the <Levi-Civita connection>, the <second Bianchi identity> gives $\nabla_iR^{ik}{}_{mn}=\nabla_mR^k{}_n-\nabla_nR^k{}_m$. Applying another <covariant derivative> and the <contracted Bianchi identity> reduces its divergence to a commuting scalar Hessian plus curvature commutators on the <Ricci tensor>. The latter are the difference of symmetric tensors: a square of the <Ricci tensor> and $R^{ab}R_{anbm}$, which is symmetric in $m,n$ by curvature pair exchange and Ricci symmetry. Both differences vanish. This identity does not require vacuum field equations and is independent of the overall curvature sign convention.

= Einstein tensor
{c}
{parent=Contracted Bianchi identity}
{wiki}

The Einstein tensor is
$$
G_{\mu\nu}=R_{\mu\nu}-\frac12Rg_{\mu\nu}.
$$
The contracted Bianchi identity and metric compatibility give
$\nabla^\nu G_{\mu\nu}=0$, ensuring compatibility of the Einstein equations with covariant stress-energy conservation.

= Flat FLRW Einstein-tensor divergence
{parent=Einstein tensor}

For $ds^2=-dt^2+a(t)^2d\mathbf x^2$, the nonzero connection coefficients are
$$
\Gamma^t_{ij}=a\dot a\,\delta_{ij},
\qquad
\Gamma^i_{tj}=\Gamma^i_{jt}=H\delta^i_j.
$$
With $G_t^t=-3H^2$ and
$G_i^j=-(2\ddot a/a+H^2)\delta_i^j$, direct substitution into the mixed-tensor covariant derivative gives zero.

= de Sitter scale factor in flat slicing
{c}
{parent=Flat FLRW Einstein-tensor divergence}

The vacuum equation with positive cosmological constant gives
$H^2=\Lambda/3$. Its expanding flat-slicing solution is
$$
a(t)=a_0e^{\sqrt{\Lambda/3}\,t}.
$$

= Vacuum-dominated doubling time
{parent=de Sitter scale factor in flat slicing}
{title2=$t_2=(\log2)\sqrt{3c^2/(8\pi G\epsilon)}$}

In a spatially flat universe dominated by a positive constant <energy density> $\epsilon$, the <Friedmann equations> give $H^2=8\pi G\epsilon/(3c^2)$. On the expanding branch $a(t)=a_i e^{H(t-t_i)}$, so the <scale factor> doubles after $(\log2)/H$. This concerns a linear length scale; the enclosed physical volume grows by a factor of eight over the same interval.

= Constant sectional curvature
{title2=$R_{abcd}=K(g_{ac}g_{bd}-g_{ad}g_{bc})$}
{parent=Ricci tensor}
{wiki}

A pseudo-Riemannian manifold has constant sectional curvature $K$ when $R_{abcd}=K(g_{ac}g_{bd}-g_{ad}g_{bc})$. In dimension $n$, its Ricci tensor and scalar are $R_{ab}=(n-1)Kg_{ab}$ and $R=n(n-1)K$.

= Schur theorem in pseudo-Riemannian geometry
{parent=Constant sectional curvature}
{c}

In dimension greater than two, if sectional curvature is pointwise independent of the two-plane, the contracted Bianchi identity forces that curvature to be constant on each connected component.

The positive-definite specialization is <Schur's lemma (Riemannian geometry)>.

= Penrose wave equation
{c}
{parent=Riemann curvature tensor}

In a Ricci-flat spacetime, the Penrose wave equation is a nonlinear covariant wave equation for the Riemann tensor. Its principal part is $\nabla^e\nabla_eR_{abcd}$ and its lower-order terms are quadratic contractions of curvature.
