= Ideal gas
{wiki}

= Perfect gas
{synonym}

An ideal gas is a model of a dilute <gas> whose particle centres move according to <classical mechanics> and occupy negligible <volume (thermodynamics)> compared with their container. Treating the particles as <point masses> removes the molecular-size correction; neglecting interactions between separated particles removes the intermolecular contribution to <potential energy>. The usual <kinetic theory> model retains brief <elastic collisions> with the walls and, optionally, with other particles: these transfer <momentum> while conserving the total <kinetic energy> of the colliding bodies. There is no appreciable interaction <energy> stored between <collisions>. These assumptions lead to the <ideal gas law>, $PV=Nk_BT$. <University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/2.1 Molecular Model of an Ideal Gas>[OpenStax University Physics Volume 2, section 2.1: molecular model and ideal gas law]{ref} https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/2-2-pressure-temperature-and-rms-speed[OpenStax University Physics Volume 2, section 2.2: kinetic-model assumptions]{ref}

The model's attraction is its universality: at the same <temperature> and <number density>, different classical <ideal gases> have the same <pressure>, even when their particle <masses> differ. In <ideal gas pressure from wall collisions>, heavier particles move more slowly at a given <temperature>, and the two effects cancel. For example, one <mole> of any <ideal gas> at $300\,\mathrm K$ in $0.025\,\mathrm{m^3}$ has <pressure> approximately $99.774\,\mathrm{kPa}$, as computed under the <ideal gas law>.

The <ideal gas law> describes the translational contribution to <pressure>; it does not specify all of the <internal energy> or the <heat capacity>. A <monatomic gas> has three classical translational <degrees of freedom>, whereas a <gas> of <molecules> can also store <rotational kinetic energy> and <energy> in vibrational <normal modes>. An <equation of state> alone therefore does not determine how much <heat> a chosen <temperature> increase requires. It also does not select a process: <isothermal expansion> and <adiabatic expansion> need different additional constraints. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/2-3-heat-capacity-and-equipartition-of-energy[OpenStax University Physics Volume 2, section 2.3: internal degrees of freedom and heat capacity]{ref}

A <real gas> departs from this approximation when particle size or interactions become important, especially at high <number density> or low <temperature> near a <phase transition>. The <Van der Waals equation> adds such corrections. Independently, at sufficiently low <temperature> or high <number density>, <quantum statistics> can become essential: even particles with negligible interactions need not obey the classical <ideal gas law>. The classical statistical assumption is the <nondegenerate gas> regime, which is distinct from neglecting interactions. <University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/2.1 Molecular Model of an Ideal Gas>[OpenStax University Physics Volume 2, section 2.1: departures from ideal-gas behavior]{ref} <Statistical Physics by David Tong/3 Quantum Gases>{ref}

= Classical ideal-gas entropy limits
{parent=Ideal gas}
{title2=$S_m=S_0-R\ln(p/p_0)+C_{mp}\ln(T/T_0)$}

For constant positive <molar heat capacity>, the <ideal gas> <entropy> grows without bound on isothermal dilution to unbounded <volume>, corresponding to $p\to0$. This is a meaningful limiting configuration count, not an infinite <entropy> in a finite container. Extrapolation to $T\to0$ at fixed positive <pressure> instead predicts negative infinite <entropy>. That extrapolation fails: phase changes, <quantum statistics> and temperature-dependent <heat capacities> prevent a real equilibrium substance from retaining the same classical formula down to zero <temperature>.

= Isobaric ideal-gas internal-energy density
{parent=Ideal gas}
{title2=$U/V=p/({\gamma-1})$}

An <ideal gas> with constant <heat capacity ratio> $\gamma$ has $U=n c_{V,m}T$ up to a consistent thermal-energy reference, and $c_{V,m}=R/(\gamma-1)$. Combining this with $pV=nRT$ gives the displayed thermal internal-energy <density>. At fixed <pressure> it does not grow with <temperature>: the particle <density> falls as $1/T$. A fixed-volume space exchanging gas with the atmosphere therefore differs from a sealed fixed-particle system. Composition changes, activated internal modes or a <pressure> change require a modified comparison.

= Mean particle mass
{parent=Ideal gas}
{title2=$\bar m=\rho/n$}

The <mean particle mass> is the <mass density> divided by the total <number density> of freely moving particles. It enters $P=\rho k_BT/\bar m$. <ionization> changes the particle count without significantly changing the <mass>, so it changes $\bar m$. The <dimensionless> <mean molecular weight> is $\bar m$ divided by a stated reference <mass>, commonly the atomic <mass> unit; it must not be substituted as a <dimensionless> number into a formula requiring a <mass>.

= Diatomic gas
{parent=Ideal gas}

A gas whose constituent molecules have two atoms. In the classical ideal-gas regime with three translational and two rotational degrees of freedom active but vibration frozen, $c_v=5\mathcal R/2$, $c_p=7\mathcal R/2$ and $\gamma=7/5$. Vibrational excitation, rotational freezing or chemical changes alter this heat-capacity model.

= Specific gas constant
{parent=Ideal gas}
{title2=$\mathcal R$}

The gas constant per unit <mass> for a specified composition. An <ideal gas> obeys $p=\rho\mathcal RT$. With dimensionless <mean molecular weight> $\mu$ relative to an atomic <mass> unit, $\mathcal R=k_B/(\mu m_u)$; the common astrophysical notation $R^*/\mu$ uses a modified mass-based constant rather than the molar gas constant without a <mass> conversion.

= Two-dimensional ideal gas
{parent=Ideal gas}
{title2=$q=A/\Lambda^2$}

An <ideal gas> whose particles move in an <area> $A$ with two independent translational momentum components. In its dilute classical limit the one-particle <translational partition function> is $q=A/\Lambda^2$, where $\Lambda$ is the <thermal de Broglie wavelength>, and $Z_N=q^N/N!$. The translational <internal energy> is $Nk_{\rm B}T$, and the surface pressure conjugate to <area> is $Nk_{\rm B}T/A$. A mobile dilute adsorbed gas is an example; a fully occupied array of fixed <adsorption sites> has different state counting.

= Classical grand potential in a harmonic trap
{parent=Ideal gas}
{title2=$\Phi=-k_BT e^{\beta\mu}[1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}]^{-3}$}

For noninteracting particles in a three-dimensional isotropic <quantum harmonic oscillator>, measure the one-particle ground energy as zero. The single-particle <partition function> is $Z_1=(1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega})^{-3}$, from the product of three <geometric series>. In the dilute <Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics> regime, $\ln\Xi=e^{\beta\mu}Z_1$, so the <grand potential> is the displayed expression and $N=e^{\beta\mu}Z_1$. For $k_BT\gg\hbar\omega$,
$$
\Phi\simeq-\frac{(k_BT)^4}{(\hbar\omega)^3}e^{\beta\mu},\qquad\mu\simeq k_BT\ln\left[N\left(\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_BT}\right)^3\right].
$$
The same approximation follows from integrating the <density of states> $g(E)\simeq E^2/[2(\hbar\omega)^3]$ against $e^{-\beta E}$. Extrapolating the classical formula until the fugacity is one gives the scale of <Bose condensation in a three-dimensional harmonic trap>, but the quantitative transition temperature requires the Bose occupation law rather than Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics.

= Partial pressure
{parent=Ideal gas}
{wiki}
{title2=$p_i$}

The <partial pressure> of a component of a <gas> <mixture> is the <pressure> it would exert alone at the same <temperature> and total <volume (thermodynamics)>. For an ideal <mixture>, $p_i=n_iRT/V$ and $p=\sum_i p_i$. The <chemical potential of an ideal gas> uses $p_i$ for that component rather than the total <pressure> $p$.

= Molar gas constant
{parent=Ideal gas}
{title2=$R=N_Ak_B$}

The molar gas constant is the product of the <Avogadro constant> and the <Boltzmann constant>. It converts the particle-number form of the <ideal gas law>, $pV=Nk_BT$, into the amount-of-substance form $pV=nRT$, using $N=nN_A$ with $n$ measured in <moles>.

= Ideal gas law
{parent=Ideal gas}
{title2=$PV=Nk_BT=n_{\rm mol}RT$}
{wiki}

The <ideal gas law> is the <equation of state> relating the absolute <pressure> $P$, <volume (thermodynamics)> $V$, particle count $N$, and absolute <thermodynamic temperature> $T$ of a classical <ideal gas>:
$$
PV=Nk_BT=n_{\rm mol}RT,\qquad
N=n_{\rm mol}N_{\mathrm A},\qquad R=N_{\mathrm A}k_B.
$$
Here $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant>, $N_{\mathrm A}$ is the <Avogadro constant>, $R$ is the <molar gas constant>, and $n_{\rm mol}$ is the <amount of substance> in <moles>. The distinct symbol $n_{\rm mol}$ avoids confusing <amount of substance> with the <number density> $n=N/V$, measured in $\mathrm{m^{-3}}$: the same law can be written $P=nk_BT$. Use absolute <pressure>, relative to a <vacuum>, rather than an unconverted <gauge pressure>. <University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/2.1 Molecular Model of an Ideal Gas>[OpenStax University Physics Volume 2, section 2.1: particle-count and molar forms]{ref}

The exact <SI> definitions of the <Avogadro constant> and <Boltzmann constant> give
$$
R=(6.02214076\times10^{23})(1.380649\times10^{-23})
\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}
=8.31446261815324\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}.
$$
This numerical value is exact; the applicability of the <ideal gas> model to a <real gas> is an approximation. https://www.bipm.org/en/si-base-units/mole[BIPM, exact Avogadro constant in the mole definition]{ref} https://www.bipm.org/en/si-base-units/kelvin[BIPM, exact Boltzmann constant in the kelvin definition]{ref}

For $n_{\rm mol}=1\,\mathrm{mol}$, $T=300\,\mathrm K$, and $V=0.025\,\mathrm{m^3}$, direct substitution gives
$$
RT=2494.338785445972\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}},\qquad
P=\frac{n_{\rm mol}RT}{V}
=99773.55141783888\,\mathrm{Pa}
\approx99.774\,\mathrm{kPa}.
$$
At fixed <temperature> and particle count, doubling the <volume (thermodynamics)> to $0.050\,\mathrm{m^3}$ halves the <pressure> to approximately $49.887\,\mathrm{kPa}$: this is <Boyle's law>. At fixed <volume (thermodynamics)> and particle count, doubling the <temperature> from $300\,\mathrm K$ to $600\,\mathrm K$ doubles the <pressure> to approximately $199.547\,\mathrm{kPa}$. These ratios use <kelvins>, not readings in the <degree Celsius> scale: $300\,\mathrm K$ is $26.85\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, and $600\,\mathrm K$ is $326.85\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$.

The curved slices show <Boyle's law> at three <temperatures>; the straight slice shows proportionality to absolute <temperature> at one fixed <volume (thermodynamics)>. They are relations among equilibrium states, rather than a prescribed process history. Compression could hold the <temperature> fixed through <heat transfer>, or raise it during <adiabatic compression>. To calculate a process's <heat> or <work>, supplement the <ideal gas law> with the process constraint, the <first law of thermodynamics>, and the relevant <internal energy> or <heat capacity> information. The graph's extrapolation toward $T=0$ is mathematical; it does not assert that a <real gas> remains classical or gaseous there.

For a stellar <mixture> with fixed <mean molecular weight>, the <ideal gas law> can be written $P=\mathcal R\rho T/\mu$, where $\rho$ is <mass density> and $\mathcal R$ is the <stellar gas constant>. It assumes negligible interaction corrections and a <nondegenerate gas>; excitation or <ionization> can change the particle count and thermodynamic response.

In his 1834 memoir on the motive power of <heat>, Émile Clapeyron combined gas laws into a relation between <pressure>, <volume (thermodynamics)>, and <temperature> while analysing <heat engines> (printed page 164). The equation above uses modern <SI> notation and absolute <thermodynamic temperature>; it is not a transcription of his historical temperature convention. https://www3.nd.edu/~powers/ame.20231/clapeyron1834.pdf[Clapeyron, Mémoire sur la puissance motrice de la chaleur (1834), page 164]{ref}

= Ideal gas pressure from wall collisions
{parent=Ideal gas law}
{title2=$P=Nm\langle v_x^2\rangle/V$}

Consider a particle of <mass> $m$ in a rigid rectangular box of length $L$ along $x$ and wall <surface area> $A$, so $V=AL$ is the <volume (thermodynamics)>. Choose $+x$ toward the right wall. For an incoming normal <velocity> $v_x=v>0$, an <elastic collision> reverses the normal <momentum>:
$$
p_{x,\rm in}=mv,\qquad p_{x,\rm out}=-mv,\qquad
\Delta p_{x,\rm particle}=-2mv,\qquad
J_{x,\rm wall}=+2mv.
$$
The <impulse> delivered to the wall points outward, toward $+x$, by <Newton's third law>. The particle's opposite <momentum> change is not the wall's <impulse>. A tangential <velocity> component is unchanged by the ideal smooth-wall <collision>.

\Image[ideal-gas-wall-momentum.png]
{title=Schematic ideal gas model: an elastic bounce transfers outward momentum to a wall; this is not a real experimental setup}

Ignoring intervening particle-particle <collisions> in this counting model, the particle travels a normal distance $2L$ before hitting the same wall again. Its hit rate is $|v_x|/(2L)$; multiplying by the <impulse> per hit gives the time-averaged normal <force> and its contribution to <pressure>:
$$
\overline F_{\perp,1}=(2m|v_x|)\frac{|v_x|}{2L}
=\frac{mv_x^2}{L},\qquad
P_1=\frac{\overline F_{\perp,1}}{A}=\frac{mv_x^2}{V}.
$$
For example, stipulate $m=4\times10^{-26}\,\mathrm{kg}$, $|v_x|=500\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $L=0.10\,\mathrm m$, and $A=0.010\,\mathrm{m^2}$. Then $V=0.0010\,\mathrm{m^3}$, the <impulse> per hit is $4\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{N\,s}$, the hit rate is $2500\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, and $P_1=10^{-17}\,\mathrm{Pa}$. This is one particle's tiny time-averaged contribution to one wall's <pressure>, not an <isotropic> equilibrium <gas> by itself.

For $N$ particles of equal <mass>, add their <force> contributions and define $\langle v_x^2\rangle=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^Nv_{x,i}^2$. In the <gas>'s rest frame, <isotropy> gives $\langle v_x^2\rangle=\langle v_y^2\rangle=\langle v_z^2\rangle=\langle v^2\rangle/3$, where $v^2=v_x^2+v_y^2+v_z^2$. Hence
$$
P=\frac{Nm\langle v_x^2\rangle}{V}
=\frac{Nm\langle v^2\rangle}{3V},\qquad
\frac12m\langle v^2\rangle=\frac32k_BT
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad PV=Nk_BT.
$$
The final equality uses the classical <equipartition theorem>. The <kinetic pressure of an isotropic gas> therefore recovers the <ideal gas law> from <momentum> transfers. Doubling the normal speed would double both the <impulse> per hit and the hit rate, giving four times the <pressure> contribution; <pressure> measures a mean squared speed. Interparticle <elastic collisions> redistribute <velocities> and help establish <isotropy>; the individual round-trip times need not remain $2L/|v_x|$, but the equilibrium <momentum flux> result survives. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/2-2-pressure-temperature-and-rms-speed[OpenStax University Physics Volume 2, section 2.2: wall-collision pressure derivation]{ref}

= Monatomic gas
{parent=Ideal gas}
{title2=$\gamma=5/3$}

A monatomic gas consists of individual atoms or ions rather than molecules. In the classical dilute ideal-gas regime, its three translational degrees of freedom give $U=3Nk_BT/2$ by <equipartition theorem>. Thus $C_V=3Nk_B/2$, while $C_P-C_V=Nk_B$ follows by differentiating the ideal-gas enthalpy $H=U+PV=5Nk_BT/2$. Consequently the <adiabatic index> is $\gamma=C_P/C_V=5/3$ and the <specific internal energy> is $e=3P/(2\rho)$. The same translational result applies to a fully ionized mixture when the particle numbers are fixed. This excludes additional excitation, ionization and radiation contributions to the heat capacity.

= Mean free path
{parent=Ideal gas}
{title2=$\ell$}

The mean free path is the average distance travelled between <collisions>. For dilute targets with <number density> $n$ and effective <collision cross-section> $\sigma_c$, $\ell\sim1/(n\sigma_c)$; identical-particle conventions introduce factors such as $\sqrt2$.

= Transport mean free path
{parent=Mean free path}
{title2=$\ell_{\rm tr}=v_F\tau_{\rm tr}=\mu\hbar k_F/e$}

The length associated with relaxation of carrier momentum. In a parabolic band, <electron mobility> measures the <momentum relaxation time>, giving the displayed expression. Frequent weak small-angle scattering can make the single-particle scattering time shorter than the transport time, and <electron phase coherence> has its own lifetime.

= Mean free path through randomly oriented disc absorbers
{parent=Mean free path}
{title2=$\ell^{-1}=n_{\rm com}(1+z)^3\pi R_d^2/2$}

For an unclustered population of fully covering circular disc absorbers, the inverse <mean free path> is proper number density times mean projected area. A comoving number density must first be multiplied by $(1+z)^3$. The incidence then constrains the proper radius; covering factor and multiplicity of detectable lines can change the inference.

= Mean molecular weight
{title2=$\mu$}
{parent=Ideal gas}
{wiki}

The mean molecular weight is the average mass per freely moving gas particle in units of the atomic mass unit. A fully ionized hydrogen-helium gas with mass fractions $X,Y$ has $\mu^{-1}=2X+3Y/4$.

= Stellar gas constant
{parent=Mean molecular weight}
{title2=$\mathcal R=k_B/m_u$}

In stellar <ideal gas> equations, the <stellar gas constant> $\mathcal R=k_B/m_u$ converts a dimensionless <mean molecular weight> $\mu$ into the specific thermal relation $P=\mathcal R\rho T/\mu$. Here $m_u$ is the reference atomic mass unit, commonly approximated by the proton mass in simplified hydrogen-helium models. It has units of energy per mass per temperature. It is the molar gas constant divided by the molar mass corresponding to $m_u$, rather than the molar gas constant used with a dimensional molar molecular weight.

= Mean molecular weight per ion
{parent=Mean molecular weight}
{title2=$\mu_i^{-1}=\sum_jX_j/A_j$}

= Ion mean molecular weight
{synonym}

The <mean molecular weight> per ion counts nuclei rather than nuclei plus free electrons: $\rho/(\mu_i m_u)$ is the ion number density. For species with mass fractions $X_j$ and mass numbers $A_j$, $\mu_i^{-1}=\sum_jX_j/A_j$. It is useful when ions provide a thermal <ideal gas> pressure but <electron degeneracy pressure> supplies a separate contribution.

= Mean molecular weight per electron
{title2=$\mu_e=\rho/(m_un_e)$}
{parent=Mean molecular weight}

The mean molecular weight per electron is the mass carried per free electron in units of atomic mass $m_u$. Fully ionized hydrogen has $\mu_e=1$, while helium, carbon, and oxygen have approximately $\mu_e=2$. It converts <electron number density> to bulk <mass density> in the <equation of state of a cold electron gas>.

= Heat capacity ratio
{title2=$\gamma$}
{parent=Ideal gas}
{wiki}

= Specific-heat ratio
{synonym}

= Adiabatic exponent
{synonym}

= Adiabatic index
{synonym}

The heat capacity ratio compares <heat capacity at constant pressure> $C_p$ with <heat capacity at constant volume> $C_V$ at the same equilibrium state and fixed composition:
$$
\gamma=\frac{C_p}{C_V}
=\frac{C_{p,\mathrm m}}{C_{V,\mathrm m}}
=\frac{c_p}{c_V}.
$$
Here $C_p,C_V$ refer to the whole sample, in $\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$; <molar heat capacities> $C_{p,\mathrm m}=C_p/n$ and $C_{V,\mathrm m}=C_V/n$ are per <mole>; <specific heat capacities> $c_p=C_p/m$ and $c_V=C_V/m$ are per unit <mass>. The <amount of substance> $n$ or <mass> $m$ cancels only when both capacities use the same normalization. Thus <heat capacity ratio> is dimensionless and independent of sample size. For a fixed-composition <ideal gas> with positive finite $C_V$, <Mayer's relation> gives
$$
\gamma(T)=1+\frac{nR}{C_V(T)}=1+\frac{R}{C_{V,\mathrm m}(T)}>1,
$$
where $R$ is the <molar gas constant>. More ways to store <internal energy> make a given <temperature> increase require more <heat>, bringing <heat capacity ratio> closer to unity. https://web.mit.edu/16.unified/www/FALL/thermodynamics/notes/node18.html[MIT Unified Engineering, Specific heats: definitions and ideal-gas relations]{ref}

The <equipartition theorem> predicts $C_{V,\mathrm m}=fR/2$ when $f$ independent quadratic terms in molecular <energy> are thermally active and their number stays fixed; consequently $\gamma=1+2/f$. A classical <monatomic gas> with no appreciable electronic excitation has $f=3$, giving $C_{V,\mathrm m}=3R/2$ and $\gamma=5/3$. For an <ideal gas> of diatomic <molecules> with three translational and two rotational terms active, but its vibrational <normal mode> effectively frozen, $f=5$ gives $C_{V,\mathrm m}=5R/2$ and $\gamma=7/5$. A fully classical harmonic vibration adds both <kinetic energy> and <potential energy>, giving $f=7$ and $\gamma=9/7$. These are model regimes, not universal constants of every <gas>: rotational freezing, electronic excitation, and changing composition invalidate the simple count.<Statistical Physics by David Tong/2 Classical Gases>[§§2.2.1 and 2.4: equipartition and diatomic gases]{ref}

The mechanical significance of <heat capacity ratio> follows from the <first law of thermodynamics>. For a fixed amount of <ideal gas> in a reversible <adiabatic process>, $C_V(T)\,dT=-p\,dV$. Combining this with the <ideal gas law> gives the local relation
$$
\left(\frac{\partial\ln p}{\partial\ln V}\right)_S=-\gamma(T),\qquad
\int_{T_1}^{T_2}\frac{C_V(T)}{T}\,dT+nR\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}=0.
$$
The <partial derivative> holds at fixed <entropy> and composition even when <heat capacity ratio> varies with <temperature>. The finite-path formulas $pV^\gamma=\mathrm{constant}$ and $TV^{\gamma-1}=\mathrm{constant}$ require the additional approximation of constant <heat capacity ratio>. A rapid compression also needs internal equilibration to approximate this path; rapid motion alone does not ensure reversibility. The same local response gives <isentropic compressibility> $1/(\gamma p)$ and, for small sound disturbances in this <ideal gas> regime, <speed of sound> $a=\sqrt{\gamma p/\rho}$, where $\rho$ is <mass density>. Thus thermal storage controls mechanical stiffness, sound propagation and the measurements in <Rüchardt experiment> and <Clément-Desormes experiment>.

= Rüchardt experiment
{c}
{parent=Heat capacity ratio}

The Rüchardt experiment infers <heat capacity ratio> from the <period of an oscillation> of a piston supported by trapped <gas>. Use a vertical precision cylinder with a close-fitting movable piston, known moving <mass> $M$, face <area> $A$, trapped equilibrium <volume (thermodynamics)> $V_0$, and local atmospheric <pressure> $p_{\rm atm}$. With negligible static <friction>, <force balance> gives the absolute gas <pressure> $p_0=p_{\rm atm}+Mg/A$, where $g$ is gravitational acceleration. Displace the piston slightly and release it: compression raises <pressure>, and expansion lowers it, producing a restoring <force>. The <oscillation> is a mechanical probe of thermal response, without separately measuring two <heat> inputs. https://groups.ist.utl.pt/wwwelab/wiki/index.php?title=Determination_of_the_Adiabatic_Constant[Instituto Superior Técnico e-lab, Determination of the Adiabatic Constant: piston apparatus and period model]{ref}

Let $x$ be upward <displacement> from equilibrium, so $V=V_0+Ax$. For constant <heat capacity ratio> and a reversible <adiabatic process>, the net upward <force>, including the ambient load and weight, is
$$
F(x)=A p_0\left[\left(1+\frac{Ax}{V_0}\right)^{-\gamma}-1\right]
\simeq-\frac{\gamma p_0A^2}{V_0}x.
$$
The <linear approximation> requires $|Ax|/V_0\ll1$. Applying <Newton's second law> gives <simple harmonic motion> with
$$
\omega_0^2=\frac{\gamma p_0A^2}{MV_0},\qquad
\gamma=\frac{4\pi^2MV_0}{p_0A^2\mathcal T^2},\qquad
\mathcal T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_0}.
$$
Here $\omega_0$ is the undamped <angular frequency> and $\mathcal T$ the undamped <oscillation period>, rather than <temperature>. Record repeated cycles, measure $M,A,V_0,p_{\rm atm}$ independently, and include all connected trapped <volume (thermodynamics)>. An illustrative model with $M=0.020\,\mathrm{kg}$, $A=2.0\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$, $V_0=0.010\,\mathrm{m^3}$, $p_{\rm atm}=100000\,\mathrm{Pa}$, $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ and $\gamma=1.4$ gives $p_0=100981\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and $\mathcal T\simeq1.182\,\mathrm s$. These stipulated values are not measurements.

Upward piston <displacement> increases <volume (thermodynamics)>; the blue curve uses constant $\gamma=1.4$, its dashed tangent gives the small-<amplitude> model, and the orange curve is the <isothermal process> limit. In Shearwood and Sloan's implementation, piston-mounted magnets induce an <electromotive force> in a stationary coil through <Faraday's law of induction>; an oscilloscope records the signal. They observed faster <oscillations> at smaller trapped <volume (thermodynamics)> values and stronger <damping> at small cylinder <volume (thermodynamics)> values. The signal tracks <velocity> locally, so its <amplitude> is not directly piston <displacement>. Calibration also identified inlet dead space beyond the graduated cylinder volume. https://arxiv.org/html/2209.03323v1[Chris Shearwood and Peter A. Sloan, The Rüchardt experiment revisited, §§3–4: signal, dead volume and observations]{ref}

Repeat at smaller initial <amplitudes> to test the <linear approximation>, and compare several $V_0$ values. <Heat transfer> weakens the restoring <force> toward the <isothermal process> limit, biasing the inference toward $1$. Check leakage through resting-piston drift and before-and-after trapped <volume (thermodynamics)> readings. For viscous <damping> with envelope $e^{-t/\tau}$, the measured <angular frequency> obeys $\omega_0^2=\omega_d^2+\tau^{-2}$; this correction does not remove thermal leakage. Internal equilibration must be faster than an <oscillation period>, with <heat transfer> and leakage slow over a run. Large <amplitude> or sticking <friction> require a richer model. https://arxiv.org/html/2209.03323v1[Shearwood and Sloan, §§2.1, 2.4 and 3.2: damping, heat exchange and leakage limitations]{ref}

= Clément-Desormes experiment
{c}
{parent=Heat capacity ratio}

The Clément-Desormes experiment infers <heat capacity ratio> from a rapid <pressure> reduction followed by reheating. Use a rigid bottle, pump or bellows, a quick release valve, and a U-tube <pressure> gauge connected to the bottle and atmosphere. First pump to a small excess <pressure>, close the inlet, and wait for <thermal equilibrium> with the room at $T_0$. Measure $p_1>p_a$, where $p_a$ is local absolute atmospheric <pressure>. Briefly open the release valve until the bottle reaches $p_a$, then close it promptly. The remaining <gas> is cooler than the room. As it warms back to $T_0$ at fixed bottle <volume (thermodynamics)>, its <pressure> rises to $p_3$, with $p_a<p_3<p_1$ in the ideal procedure. The rebound reveals cooling during release. https://labs.physik.kit.edu/prakt-nebenfach-manuals/Experiments/Adiabatenexponent_Luft/[KIT Faculty of Physics, Clément-Desormes laboratory protocol: release and pressure rebound]{ref}

The bottle loses <molecules> while the valve is open: it is not a <closed system> whose geometrical <volume (thermodynamics)> expands. In the well-mixed, negligible-<heat-transfer> discharge model with constant <heat capacity ratio>, the remaining <gas> obeys $T_2/T_0=(p_a/p_1)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}$. Subsequent heating at fixed <amount of substance> and bottle <volume (thermodynamics)> gives $p_3/p_a=T_0/T_2$ by the <ideal gas law>. Eliminating the unmeasured $T_2$ yields
$$
\gamma=\frac{\ln(p_1/p_a)}{\ln(p_1/p_3)}
\simeq\frac{\Delta p_1}{\Delta p_1-\Delta p_3}
=\frac{h_1}{h_1-h_3},\qquad
\Delta p_i=p_i-p_a=\rho_{\rm liquid}gh_i.
$$
The <logarithms> use absolute <pressure> ratios. The approximation requires small <gauge pressures> $\Delta p_i\ll p_a$; $h_i$ are signed height differences between the two liquid surfaces, and the final equality uses the same liquid in both readings with <hydrostatic equilibrium>. It is not an instruction to insert gauge readings into the logarithmic formula. For synthetic states generated with $p_a=100000\,\mathrm{Pa}$, $p_1=102000\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and $\gamma=1.4$, the model gives $p_3\simeq100567.39\,\mathrm{Pa}$. The logarithmic inference recovers $1.4$; the small-<pressure> approximation gives approximately $1.3961$.

Wait for a stable initial <pressure> after pumping and for a stable final <pressure> after closing. Vary the valve-open interval and initial excess <pressure> across repeated trials: closing too early leaves residual excess <pressure>, while leaving the valve open during warming lets more <gas> escape and suppresses the rebound. Test the seal for leaks, and read both liquid levels consistently. In a variant with incomplete release, measure the actual immediate post-release absolute <pressure> $p_2$ and replace the numerator by $\ln(p_1/p_2)$. The Zurich laboratory manual explains that liquid-column <oscillations> can delay this reading until appreciable reheating has occurred; its proposed backward extrapolation assumes an exponential thermal relaxation and must be checked against the recorded readings. These controls separate a thermal signal from valve timing, instrumental lag and leakage. https://www.physik.uzh.ch/~matthias/espace-assistant/manuals/en/anleitung-cd_e.pdf[University of Zurich, PHY112/122 laboratory manual, §1.3 and Appendix 1.4(a): pressure readings and reheating correction]{ref}

= Nondegenerate gas
{parent=Ideal gas}

A gas is nondegenerate when its occupation numbers are low enough that <quantum statistics> reduce to the <Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution>. A common criterion is $n\lambda_{\rm th}^3\ll1$, where $\lambda_{\rm th}$ is the thermal de Broglie wavelength.

For an <ideal gas>, this is the classical statistical regime; nondegeneracy alone does not assert the absence of interactions.

= Internal energy of an ideal gas
{parent=Ideal gas}

For a fixed <amount of substance> and composition of classical <ideal gas>, <internal energy> depends only on <temperature>: $U=U(T,n)$, with no independent <volume (thermodynamics)> dependence. This does not require constant <heat capacity at constant volume>. Between equilibrium endpoints,
$$
dU=C_V(T)\,dT=nC_{V,\mathrm m}(T)\,dT,
\qquad U(T_2)-U(T_1)=n\int_{T_1}^{T_2}C_{V,\mathrm m}(T)\,dT.
$$
Here $C_V$ is total <heat capacity at constant volume>, $C_{V,\mathrm m}$ is <molar heat capacity>, and $U/m$ for sample <mass> $m$ is <specific internal energy>, whose change is $\int_{T_1}^{T_2}c_V(T)\,dT$. These endpoint relations apply even when the intervening process is not at fixed <volume (thermodynamics)>; they do not say that transferred <heat> always equals $\Delta U$. With constant $C_V$, the safe statement is $U(T)=U(T_*)+C_V(T-T_*)$. Writing $U=C_VT$ additionally chooses the reference <energy>; it does not prove that a physical <gas> remains classical and ideal down to zero <temperature>. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_45.html#Ch45-S1[Richard Feynman, Robert Leighton and Matthew Sands, §45-1: internal-energy derivatives and heat capacity]{ref}

A thermodynamic proof outline uses the <first law of thermodynamics> in equilibrium form, $dU=T\,dS-p\,dV$, and the <Maxwell relation> $(\partial S/\partial V)_T=(\partial p/\partial T)_V$:
$$
\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T
=T\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_V-p
=T\frac{nR}{V}-\frac{nRT}{V}=0.
$$
The last step uses the <ideal gas law> and the <molar gas constant> $R$, with fixed composition and <amount of substance>. Microscopically, separated <ideal gas> particles store no intermolecular interaction <energy>; their translational <kinetic energy> and any internal molecular <energy> are determined by <temperature>. Negligible intermolecular interactions still allow a <molecule> to have internal vibrational <potential energy>.

An original analytical model makes the distinction between an <ideal gas> and constant <heat capacity> concrete. Assume classical translation, two fully active rotational terms, and one independent <quantum harmonic oscillator> per diatomic <molecule>, with fixed <angular frequency> $\omega$. Define the vibrational <temperature> scale $\Theta=\hbar\omega/k_B$, using the reduced <Planck constant> $\hbar=h/(2\pi)$ and <Boltzmann constant> $k_B$. Summing its thermal occupations gives
$$
\frac{U(T)-U_*}{nR}=\frac52T+\frac{\Theta}{e^{\Theta/T}-1},\qquad
\frac{C_{V,\mathrm m}(T)}{R}=\frac52+\frac{x^2e^{-x}}{(1-e^{-x})^2},\qquad x=\frac{\Theta}{T}.
$$
The constant $U_*$ includes the chosen reference and <zero-point energy>, whose <temperature> derivative vanishes. When $T\ll\Theta$ the vibrational contribution to <heat capacity> is exponentially small; when $T\gg\Theta$ it tends to $R$ per <mole>. This model assumes rotations remain active throughout the interval and excludes dissociation, electronic excitation and <real gas> corrections. Its low-$T/\Theta$ edge is not a model of rotational freezing or of an actual substance at absolute zero.<Statistical Physics by David Tong/3 Quantum Gases>[§3.4: quantum vibrational spectrum, thermal occupations and zero-point energy]{ref}

For this stipulated model, heating from $T_1=0.2\Theta$ to $T_2=\Theta$ gives $\Delta U/(nR\Theta)\simeq2.57519$. Holding the frozen-vibration value $C_{V,\mathrm m}=5R/2$ throughout would give $2$, underestimating the required <internal energy> increase by about $22.3\%$ of the model's full value. The <integral> measures all thermal storage activated along the interval. At fixed <volume (thermodynamics)> with only <heat> input, this is also the supplied <heat>; at fixed <pressure>, expansion <work> must be added through <Mayer's relation>.

<Joule's free-expansion experiment> tests the independence from <volume (thermodynamics)> in a different way: an insulated rigid vessel allows <gas> into an evacuated compartment, so no external <work> is delivered and ideally no <heat> is exchanged. The <first law of thermodynamics> gives $\Delta U=0$; positive $C_V$ then predicts equal initial and final equilibrium <temperatures> for the <ideal gas>. The apparatus, thermal-capacity controls and limitations of this inference are described in <Joule's free-expansion experiment> and <temperature sensitivity of a free-expansion experiment>. This endpoint test differs from the reversible <adiabatic expansion> of a loaded piston, which delivers <work> and lowers <internal energy>.

= Mayer relation
{c}
{synonym}

Mayer's relation states that the difference between <heat capacity at constant pressure> and <heat capacity at constant volume> for a fixed-composition <ideal gas> is
$$
C_p(T)-C_V(T)=nR=Nk_B.
$$
Here both capacities are total <heat capacities> in $\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$, $n$ is <amount of substance>, $N$ is the number of particles, $R$ is the <molar gas constant> and $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant>. In other normalizations the same statement is
$$
C_{p,\mathrm m}(T)-C_{V,\mathrm m}(T)=R,\qquad
c_p(T)-c_V(T)=\frac{nR}{m}.
$$
The first is a difference of <molar heat capacities>, in $\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$; the second uses <specific heat capacities>, in $\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$. Writing $C_p-C_V=R$ with total capacities would omit the sample's <amount of substance>. The relation holds pointwise even when the capacities depend on <temperature>. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/3-5-heat-capacities-of-an-ideal-gas[Samuel J. Ling, William Moebs and Jeff Sanny, University Physics Volume 2, §3.5: ideal-gas capacity difference]{ref}

The proof outline is short: <enthalpy> is $H=U+pV=U+nRT$, and <internal energy of an ideal gas> is independent of <volume (thermodynamics)> at fixed <temperature>. Differentiating at fixed composition and <amount of substance> therefore gives $C_p=dH/dT=dU/dT+nR=C_V+nR$. It uses the <ideal gas law>, rather than assuming any particular number of molecular <degrees of freedom>. At fixed <pressure>, the additional <heat> supplies expansion <work>, $p\,dV=nR\,dT$. The two processes can have the same <temperature> endpoints and equal $\Delta U$ while requiring different <heat> inputs.

For one <mole> of the rigid-rotation diatomic <ideal gas> model, $C_V=5R/2$ and $C_p=7R/2$. Heating by $10\,\mathrm K$ with $R\simeq8.31446\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ gives $Q_V\simeq207.86\,\mathrm J$ in a rigid sealed vessel and $Q_p\simeq291.01\,\mathrm J$ under a frictionless piston at fixed <pressure>. Both gain $207.86\,\mathrm J$ of <internal energy>, while the piston delivers approximately $83.14\,\mathrm J$ of <work>. This is a calculated model budget. If the capacities vary with <temperature>, the corresponding finite statement is still
$$
Q_p-Q_V=nR(T_2-T_1),\qquad
Q_V=n\int_{T_1}^{T_2}C_{V,\mathrm m}(T)\,dT,
$$
under the fixed-<pressure> and fixed-<volume (thermodynamics)> heating assumptions in <heat capacity>. Their final <volume (thermodynamics)> values generally differ. Combining <Mayer's relation> with <heat capacity ratio> also gives $C_V=nR/(\gamma-1)$ and $C_p=\gamma nR/(\gamma-1)$ locally; constant values cannot be extrapolated across intervals in which $\gamma(T)$ changes.

For a smooth stable single <thermodynamic phase> of a general <simple compressible system>, the broader <Maxwell relation> identity is
$$
C_p-C_V=T\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_V\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_p
=\frac{TV\alpha^2}{\kappa_T},
$$
where $\alpha$ is the <coefficient of thermal expansion> and $\kappa_T$ the <isothermal compressibility>, with composition fixed throughout. Substituting the <ideal gas law> gives $nR$; a <real gas> need not do so. This distinction matters near a <phase transition> or when interactions appreciably change the <equation of state>. It explains why <Mayer's relation> is useful: thermal measurements under different constraints become linked to a mechanical response, while the difference retains a particularly simple value for an <ideal gas>.<Statistical Physics by David Tong/4 Classical Thermodynamics>[§4.4.2: Maxwell relations and the general heat-capacity difference]{ref}
