= Solution
{parent=a}

The coordinate <tangent vectors> are the partial <derivatives> of the position <vector>:
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\mathbf h_r&=(\sin\alpha\cos\beta,\sin\alpha\sin\beta,\cos\alpha),\\
\mathbf h_\alpha&=(r\cos\alpha\cos\beta,r\cos\alpha\sin\beta,-r\sin\alpha),\\
\mathbf h_\beta&=(-(1+r\sin\alpha)\sin\beta,(1+r\sin\alpha)\cos\beta,0).
\end{aligned}}
$$
Their pairwise <dot products> vanish. Their lengths, the <scale factors of orthogonal coordinates>, are $1,r,1+r\sin\alpha$. Since $R<1$, the last is positive throughout the body. Thus these <circular torus coordinates> are **orthogonal wherever $r>0$**; at $r=0$ the cross-section angle degenerates, as in ordinary <polar coordinates>.

The ordered <Jacobian determinant> is
$$
\boxed{J=\det\frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(r,\alpha,\beta)}=\mathbf h_r\cdot(\mathbf h_\alpha\times\mathbf h_\beta)=r(1+r\sin\alpha).}
$$
Its sign is positive for $r>0$, so the <volume> element is $dV=r(1+r\sin\alpha)\,dr\,d\alpha\,d\beta$. The coordinate degeneracy on the central <circle> has zero <volume> and does not obstruct <volume> <integration>.

Take the closed surface with its outward orientation. For sufficiently smooth fields defined throughout the solid, the <divergence theorem> and the identity that the <divergence> of a <curl> is zero give
$$
I=\iiint_V\nabla\cdot(\nabla\Omega+\nabla\times\mathbf U)\,dV=\boxed{\iiint_V\Delta\Omega\,dV.}
$$
Thus the <curl> term carries no net outward <flux>, and $I$ is independent of $\mathbf U$. The hole in the <solid torus> does not invalidate the <divergence theorem>; singularities of $\mathbf U$ *inside the <integration> region* would invalidate the smoothness argument and must be excluded.

Only the quartic term contributes to the <Laplacian>. The mixed cubic has zero second <derivative> in each coordinate, while the exponential–trigonometric pairs are harmonic:
$$
\Delta(z^4)=12z^2,\quad\Delta(xyz)=0,\quad\Delta(e^{-3y}\cos3x)=0,\quad\Delta(e^{2x}\sin2y)=0.
$$
Using $z=r\cos\alpha$ and the <Jacobian determinant> from part (a), the <divergence theorem> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
I&=12\int_0^{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\int_0^R r^3\cos^2\alpha(1+r\sin\alpha)\,dr\,d\alpha\,d\beta\\
&=12(2\pi)\left(\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos^2\alpha\,d\alpha\right)\frac{R^4}{4}=\boxed{6\pi^2R^4}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The term containing $\sin\alpha\cos^2\alpha$ integrates to zero. Equivalently, the <second vertical moment of a solid ring torus> with major radius one is $\int_Vz^2dV=\pi^2R^4/2$.

Remove the first spatial <derivative> by the <Dirichlet gauge transform for constant drift>:
$$
u(x,y,t)=e^{-\beta x/2}v(x,y,t),\qquad v_t=v_{xx}+v_{yy}-\frac{\beta^2}{4}v.
$$
The nonzero exponential factor preserves the zero side and top values; it also preserves $v_y=0$ on the bottom. A separated product $v=X(x)Y(y)T(t)$ has the spatial <eigenvalue problems>
$$
X''+k_x^2X=0,\quad X(0)=X(1)=0;\qquad Y''+k_y^2Y=0,\quad Y'(0)=Y(1)=0.
$$
The nontrivial solutions are $X_m=\sin(m\pi x)$ for $m\geq1$ and $Y_n=\cos((n-1/2)\pi y)$ for $n\geq1$. There is no zero spatial mode in either direction. The temporal equation gives $T=e^{-\lambda_{mn}t}$, where
$$
\lambda_{mn}=m^2\pi^2+(n-1/2)^2\pi^2+\beta^2/4.
$$
Therefore the <mixed-boundary drift-diffusion eigenmodes on a rectangle> give
$$
\boxed{u(x,y,t)=e^{-\beta x/2}\sum_{m=1}^\infty\sum_{n=1}^\infty A_{mn}\sin(m\pi x)\cos((n-1/2)\pi y)e^{-\lambda_{mn}t}.}
$$
At $t=0$ the temporal factors are one, giving the required expansion. The sine and mixed-end cosine bases are complete and orthogonal for $v$. In particular,
$$
A_{mn}=4\int_0^1\int_0^1 e^{\beta x/2}u(x,y,0)\sin(m\pi x)\cos((n-1/2)\pi y)\,dy\,dx.
$$
The factor four comes from the product of the two squared basis norms, each $1/2$.

After multiplying the initial field by $e^{\beta x/2}$, the $y$ dependence is exactly the $n=2$ cosine mode. Its <orthogonality> therefore gives $A_{mn}=0$ when $n\neq2$. For $n=2$, the remaining <Fourier coefficient> in the <Fourier sine series> is
$$
A_{m2}=2\int_0^1x(1-x)\sin(m\pi x)\,dx=\frac{4[1-(-1)^m]}{m^3\pi^3}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{A_{mn}=\begin{cases}8/(m^3\pi^3),&m\text{ odd and }n=2,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}}
$$
The coefficients are independent of $\beta$, because the initial exponential exactly cancels the drift-removing weight. Substitution into the preceding expansion gives the full subsequent solution, with decay rates $m^2\pi^2+9\pi^2/4+\beta^2/4$.

For the <Green function> of $D_x^2-1$, the homogeneous solutions satisfying the left and right endpoint conditions are $u(x)=\sinh x$ and $v(x)=\sinh(1-x)$. Their <Wronskian> is
$$
W=uv'-u'v=-\sinh1.
$$
Continuity of the kernel at $x=\xi$ and <integration> of $G_{xx}-G=\delta(x-\xi)$ require $[G_x]_{\xi-}^{\xi+}=1$. Hence the <Dirichlet modified Helmholtz Green function on an interval> is
$$
\boxed{G(x,\xi)=-\frac{\sinh(x_<)\sinh(1-x_>)}{\sinh1}.}
$$
Here $x_<=\min(x,\xi)$ and $x_>=\max(x,\xi)$. The negative sign is essential for this operator convention. Integrating the forcing against the kernel gives
$$
\boxed{y(x)=-\frac1{\sinh1}\left[\sinh(1-x)\int_0^x\sinh\xi\,f(\xi)\,d\xi+\sinh x\int_x^1\sinh(1-\xi)f(\xi)\,d\xi\right].}
$$
This satisfies both endpoint conditions. It solves the equation classically for continuous $f$, and in the corresponding weak sense for integrable forcing. A homogeneous difference satisfying both endpoint conditions must vanish, so the solution is unique.

Use the zero initial displacement and velocity given in the PDF. The characteristic roots of the homogeneous equation are $-1\pm i\mu$. Its <causal Green function of a damped oscillator> is
$$
G(t)=H(t)\frac{e^{-t}\sin(\mu t)}{\mu},
$$
where $H$ is the <Heaviside step function>. The positive-time kernel has $G(0+)=0$ and $G'(0+)=1$, so its <derivative> jump generates a unit impulse. The zero-data forced solution is consequently the causal <convolution>
$$
\boxed{x(t)=\frac1\mu\int_0^te^{-(t-s)}\sin[\mu(t-s)]g(s)\,ds,\qquad t\geq0.}
$$
Differentiating once gives $x'(0)=0$; differentiating twice supplies the endpoint term $g(t)$ and verifies the differential equation. No homogeneous transient is required with these initial data.

= Solution

The PDF bounds the *absolute value* of the forcing. Taking absolute values in the <Green function> <integral> and using $|\sin|\leq1$ yields
$$
|x(t)|\leq\frac C\mu\int_0^te^{-(t-s)}e^{-as}\,ds.
$$
For $a\neq1$, this gives
$$
\boxed{|x(t)|\leq\frac C\mu\frac{e^{-at}-e^{-t}}{1-a}\longrightarrow0.}
$$
The ratio is nonnegative for either $a<1$ or $a>1$. The missing special case is $a=1$, for which
$$
\boxed{|x(t)|\leq\frac C\mu te^{-t}\longrightarrow0.}
$$
This proves <decay of a damped oscillator under exponentially decaying forcing>. A one-sided upper bound on $g$ without an absolute value, as in the transcription, would not justify the conclusion.

The filter is a real even <triangular function>. Its <Fourier transform> is
$$
\widetilde f(\omega)=2\int_0^1(1-t)\cos(\omega t)\,dt=\boxed{\frac{2(1-\cos\omega)}{\omega^2}=\left[\frac{\sin(\omega/2)}{\omega/2}\right]^2},
$$
with $\widetilde f(0)=1$. Since the transmitted pulse is the <convolution> $b=a*f$, the <convolution theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\widetilde b(\omega)=\widetilde a(\omega)\widetilde f(\omega)=\frac{4\sin\omega(1-\cos\omega)}{\omega^3},\qquad\widetilde b(0)=2.}
$$
The normalized filter preserves the time <integral> of the pulse, while its extra inverse-frequency factors suppress high-frequency content. It is a smoothing filter rather than an exact compact-bandwidth cutoff.

The <Fourier transform of a Gaussian> gives
$$
\mathcal F[e^{-t^2}]=\sqrt\pi e^{-\omega^2/4},\qquad\mathcal F^{-1}[2e^{-\omega^2/4}]=\frac2{\sqrt\pi}e^{-t^2}\equiv q(t).
$$
The <Fourier transform of a derivative> obeys $\mathcal F[q'']=-\omega^2\widetilde q$, so multiplication of the spectrum by $1+\epsilon\omega^2$ corresponds to $q-\epsilon q''$. Since $q''=(2/\sqrt\pi)(4t^2-2)e^{-t^2}$,
$$
\boxed{s(t)=\frac2{\sqrt\pi}\left(1+2\epsilon-4\epsilon t^2\right)e^{-t^2}.}
$$
This is real and even, as required by the given spectrum. Its time <integral> is two for every $\epsilon$, consistently with $\widetilde s(0)=2$.

Let $Av_j=a_jv_j$. Commutation gives $A(Bv_j)=B(Av_j)=a_jBv_j$, so $B$ preserves each <eigenspace> of $A$. Since the <eigenvalues> of $A$ are nondegenerate, each <eigenspace> is one-dimensional and $Bv_j=b_jv_j$. The <spectral theorem> for a <Hermitian matrix> lets us choose these <vectors> as an <orthonormal eigenbasis>. With $U=(v_1,\ldots,v_n)$,
$$
\boxed{A=U\Lambda_AU^\dagger,\qquad B=U\Lambda_BU^\dagger,\qquad U^\dagger U=I.}
$$
This is <simple spectrum gives simultaneous diagonalization>. The common <eigenvectors> can be chosen up to their phases and ordering. In fact degeneracy does not prevent a common eigenbasis for commuting <Hermitian matrices>, but the one-dimensional argument already suffices here.

The convergent <matrix exponential> series respects similarity, so $e^A=Ue^{\Lambda_A}U^\dagger$ and likewise for $B$. Scalar exponentials multiply on each diagonal entry, giving
$$
\boxed{e^Ae^B=Ue^{\Lambda_A}e^{\Lambda_B}U^\dagger=Ue^{\Lambda_A+\Lambda_B}U^\dagger=e^{A+B}.}
$$
The essential hypothesis is commutation; Hermiticity supplies this convenient diagonalization proof.

Expand both sides through second order as $\epsilon\to0$, holding $X,Y$ fixed. The product is
$$
e^{\epsilon X}e^{\epsilon Y}=I+\epsilon(X+Y)+\epsilon^2\left(\frac{X^2}{2}+XY+\frac{Y^2}{2}\right)+O(\epsilon^3).
$$
Put $Z=\epsilon(X+Y)+\epsilon^2[X,Y]/2$. Its exponential is
$$
\begin{aligned}
e^Z&=I+\epsilon(X+Y)+\frac{\epsilon^2}{2}\{[X,Y]+(X+Y)^2\}+O(\epsilon^3)\\
&=I+\epsilon(X+Y)+\epsilon^2\left(\frac{X^2}{2}+XY+\frac{Y^2}{2}\right)+O(\epsilon^3).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{e^{\epsilon X}e^{\epsilon Y}=e^{\epsilon X+\epsilon Y+\epsilon^2[X,Y]/2}+O(\epsilon^3).}
$$
This is the quadratic part of the <Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula>. The remainder estimate is in any fixed finite-dimensional <matrix> <norm>, and is an asymptotic statement rather than an exact formula for arbitrary finite $\epsilon$.

The <spectral theorem> for a real <symmetric matrix> gives real <eigenvalues> and an <orthonormal eigenbasis>. Compute the roots of $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$, find a basis for each <eigenspace>, and orthonormalize within any repeated-eigenvalue <eigenspace>. <Eigenvectors> belonging to distinct <eigenvalues> are already orthogonal: if $Av=\lambda v$ and $Aw=\mu w$, symmetry gives $(\lambda-\mu)v^Tw=0$.

Putting the normalized basis <vectors> in the columns of an <orthogonal matrix> $Q$ yields
$$
\boxed{Q^TAQ=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n),\qquad A=Q\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)Q^T.}
$$
For repeated <eigenvalues>, the choice of axes inside their <eigenspace> is not unique.

The quadratic-form <matrix> has <eigenvectors>
$$
e_+=\frac{(1,1,0)}{\sqrt2},\qquad e_-=\frac{(1,-1,0)}{\sqrt2},\qquad e_3=(0,0,1),
$$
with <eigenvalues> $1,9,9$. In the <principal axes> coordinates $\xi=(x_1+x_2)/\sqrt2$, $\eta=(x_1-x_2)/\sqrt2$ and $\zeta=x_3$, the equation becomes
$$
\boxed{\frac{\xi^2}{9}+\eta^2+\zeta^2=1.}
$$
It is a <prolate spheroid>, centred at the origin, with semiaxis **three along $e_+$ and one in every perpendicular direction**. The displayed $e_-,e_3$ are one choice of short <principal axes>.

The <sphere> has equation $\xi^2+\eta^2+\zeta^2=4$. Combining it with the ellipsoid equation gives
$$
\boxed{\xi^2=\frac{27}{8},\qquad\eta^2+\zeta^2=\frac58.}
$$
The <intersection of a prolate spheroid and a concentric sphere> therefore consists of two <circles> in planes perpendicular to $e_+$. Each has radius $\boxed{\sqrt{5/8}=\sqrt{10}/4}$, and their centres are
$$
\boxed{c_\pm=\pm\sqrt{\frac{27}{8}}\,e_+=\pm\left(\frac{3\sqrt3}{4},\frac{3\sqrt3}{4},0\right).}
$$
Their plane equations in the original coordinates are $x_1+x_2=\pm3\sqrt3/2$.

Minimize or maximize $D(x)=x^Tx$ subject to $x^TAx=1$. A <Lagrange multiplier> $\Lambda$ gives
$$
2x-2\Lambda Ax=0.
$$
The constraint excludes $x=0$ and implies $Ax\neq0$, so it is a regular constraint surface. Multiplying the stationarity equation by $x^T$ gives $D=\Lambda>0$. Thus the <distance extrema on a real quadratic surface> satisfy
$$
\boxed{Ax=\frac1D x.}
$$
Every constrained extremum lies in a positive-eigenvalue <eigenspace>, with squared distance $D=1/\lambda$. If $A$ is positive definite, the global minimum is $1/\lambda_{\max}$ and the global maximum is $1/\lambda_{\min}$. For an indefinite or singular form the surface need not be bounded; <eigenvector> directions give stationary candidates, which may be saddles and need not supply both global extrema. With a repeated positive <eigenvalue>, there can be an entire family of equally distant stationary points.

Complex differentiability requires the same limit for increments along the real and imaginary directions. Real increments give $f'=u_x+iv_x$. Imaginary increments give
$$
f'=\frac{u_y+iv_y}{i}=v_y-iu_y.
$$
Equating real and imaginary parts yields the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>
$$
\boxed{u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x.}
$$
They hold throughout the open region where $f$ is <holomorphic>. Conversely, continuous first real <partial derivatives> satisfying these equations on an open set ensure holomorphy there.

Write $g=u+iv$ and assume its domain is open and connected. If the constant modulus is zero then $g=0$. Otherwise let $u^2+v^2=c^2>0$. Differentiating this identity and using the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> gives
$$
u u_x+v v_x=0,\qquad u u_y+v v_y=-u v_x+v u_x=0.
$$
These are a linear system for $u_x,v_x$ with coefficient <matrix> $\begin{pmatrix}u&v\\v&-u\end{pmatrix}$, whose <determinant> is $-c^2\neq0$. Therefore $u_x=v_x=0$, and the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> also give $u_y=v_y=0$. It follows that
$$
\boxed{g\text{ is constant on its connected domain}.}
$$
This proves the <constant-modulus holomorphic function> result directly. On a disconnected domain the same conclusion holds separately on each <connected component>, but the constants can differ.

<Partial fraction decomposition> give $h(z)=1/(z-i)+1/(z+i)$. Let $w=z-1$. Each fraction has a geometric expansion, so

\boxed{h(z)=\sum_{j=0}^\infty(-1)^j\left[\frac1{(1-i)^{j+1}}+\frac1{(1+i)^{j+1}}\right](z-1)^j.}

In normalized form, the coefficients are $p(x)=-x/(1-x^2)$ and $q(x)=n^2/(1-x^2)$. They are analytic near zero, so $\boxed{x=0\text{ is an ordinary point}}$.

At each of $x=\pm1$, $p$ has a simple pole and $q$ at most a simple pole. The products $(x-x_0)p$ and $(x-x_0)^2q$ are analytic, so both are <regular singular points>. More precisely their limiting values are $1/2$ and zero, giving <indicial equation> $\rho(\rho-1)+\rho/2=0$, with exponents $0,1/2$.

To classify infinity, set $t=1/x$ and $Y(t)=y(1/t)$. The transformed equation is
$$
t^2(t^2-1)Y''+(2t^3-t)Y'+n^2Y=0.
$$
After division, its coefficients satisfy
$$
tP(t)=\frac{2t^2-1}{t^2-1}\longrightarrow1,\qquad t^2Q(t)=\frac{n^2}{t^2-1}\longrightarrow-n^2.
$$
Both are analytic at $t=0$, while $P$ itself has a pole. Hence $\boxed{x=\infty\text{ is also a regular singular point}}$, with indicial exponents $\rho=\pm n$. These classifications remain valid at $n=0$, when the exponents at infinity coincide.

= Solution

Because zero is an <ordinary point>, substitute $y=\sum_{j\geq0}a_jx^j$. Matching powers gives
$$
(j+2)(j+1)a_{j+2}+(n^2-j^2)a_j=0,\qquad\boxed{a_{j+2}=\frac{j^2-n^2}{(j+2)(j+1)}a_j.}
$$
Even and odd coefficients evolve independently. Taking $(a_0,a_1)=(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ constructs solutions
$$
\begin{aligned}
y_{\rm e}&=1-\frac{n^2x^2}{2!}+\frac{n^2(n^2-4)x^4}{4!}-\frac{n^2(n^2-4)(n^2-16)x^6}{6!}+\cdots,\\
y_{\rm o}&=x+\frac{(1-n^2)x^3}{3!}+\frac{(1-n^2)(9-n^2)x^5}{5!}+\cdots.
\end{aligned}
$$
Their initial values and <derivatives> give $W(y_{\rm e},y_{\rm o})(0)=1$, so they are <linearly independent>. These <power series of the Chebyshev differential equation> span the local solution space. A useful check is $y_{\rm e}=\cos(n\arcsin x)$ and $y_{\rm o}=\sin(n\arcsin x)/n$, with $y_{\rm o}=\arcsin x$ at $n=0$.

For a nonterminating parity series, consecutive nonzero terms satisfy
$$
\left|\frac{a_{j+2}x^{j+2}}{a_jx^j}\right|=\left|\frac{j^2-n^2}{(j+2)(j+1)}\right||x|^2\longrightarrow|x|^2.
$$
The <ratio test> therefore gives $\boxed{R=1}$ for each nonterminating series. The nearest singular coefficient points are $x=\pm1$, both distance one from the expansion centre.

The polynomial exception is essential here. If $n$ is an integer, the series of the same parity as $|n|$ terminates and has $\boxed{R=\infty}$; the opposite-parity independent series remains nonterminating with radius one. For noninteger $n$, both have radius one. A <regular singular point> can be removable for one particular solution, so its presence does not force every individual solution to have finite convergence radius.

If $N=|n|$ is an integer, the recurrence has zero numerator at $j=N$. Choosing the parity containing $a_N$ therefore makes all later coefficients vanish. Conversely, for a nonzero polynomial of degree $N$, the coefficient of $x^N$ in the differential equation is $(n^2-N^2)a_N$, so $n^2=N^2$ is necessary.

Normalizing the terminating series by $y(1)=1$ gives the first <Chebyshev polynomials>:
$$
\boxed{T_0(x)=1,\qquad T_1(x)=x,\qquad T_2(x)=2x^2-1,\qquad T_3(x)=4x^3-3x.}
$$
Substitution verifies each differential equation and endpoint value. More generally, $T_N(x)=\cos(N\arccos x)$; its recurrence $T_{N+1}=2xT_N-T_{N-1}$ also establishes polynomiality and the normalization.

For fixed endpoint values and smooth admissible variations, the <Euler-Lagrange equation> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial f}{\partial z'}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}=0.}
$$
The first variation follows by integrating $\int(f_z\eta+f_{z'}\eta')dx$ by parts and using $\eta=0$ at the endpoints.

For the claimed first <integral>, differentiate directly:
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(f-z'f_{z'})=f_x+z'\left(f_z-\frac{d}{dx}f_{z'}\right).
$$
Along an extremal the bracket vanishes. Thus when $f_x=0$, the <Beltrami identity> is
$$
\boxed{f-z'f_{z'}=\text{constant}.}
$$
For a further regular constraint $G[z]=G_0$, introduce a constant <Lagrange multiplier> $\lambda$ and make $F-\lambda G$ stationary. Apply the <Euler-Lagrange equation> to the combined integrand, then impose the endpoints and $G=G_0$ to determine the <integration> constants and multiplier.

A curve element has <arc length> $ds=\sqrt{1+z'^2}\,dx$ and mass $dm=\mu ds$. Adding its gravitational energies gives
$$
\boxed{V[z]=\mu\int_{-a/\sqrt2}^{a/\sqrt2}\Phi(z)\sqrt{1+z'^2}\,dx.}
$$
For the fixed-length constraint, use the augmented integrand $(\mu\Phi(z)-\lambda)\sqrt{1+z'^2}$. It has no explicit $x$ dependence, so the <Beltrami identity> gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{\mu\Phi(z)-\lambda}{\sqrt{1+z'^2}}=C.}
$$
The desired lower circular arc is
$$
z(x)=h-\sqrt{a^2-x^2},\qquad h=\frac a{\sqrt2}.
$$
It joins the required endpoints, subtends angle $\pi/2$, and has length $\pi a/2$. Its slope gives $\sqrt{1+z'^2}=a/(h-z)$. Therefore a <gravitational potential producing a circular hanging chain> is
$$
\boxed{\Phi(z)=\Phi_0+\frac{\kappa}{a/\sqrt2-z},\qquad\kappa>0,\quad z<a/\sqrt2.}
$$
Choosing $\lambda=\mu\Phi_0$ makes the first <integral> constant, $C=\mu\kappa/a$. Its <gravitational acceleration> is $-\Phi'(z)=-\kappa/(h-z)^2$, which points downward. An arbitrary additive potential constant changes no force.

This construction really gives a minimum. With $w=h-z>0$, the variable part of $V$ is $\mu\kappa\int ds/w$; the $\Phi_0$ term is fixed by the length. The <calibration for semicircular hyperbolic geodesics> uses $S(x,w)=\operatorname{arsinh}(x/w)$, whose <gradient> has <norm> $1/w$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $\int ds/w\geq S(B)-S(A)$, with equality on $x^2+w^2=a^2$ traversed left to right. Thus the circular arc minimizes the energy among competitors of the same length and endpoints in the stated potential domain, rather than merely satisfying a necessary stationarity condition.

Let $R[y]=F[y]/G[y]$, where $G[y]>0$ for a nonzero admissible real function. Writing $\lambda=R[y]$, its first variation is
$$
\delta R=\frac2G\left\{[py'\delta y]_\alpha^\beta+\int_\alpha^\beta[-(py')'+qy-\lambda wy]\delta y\,dx\right\}.
$$
Choose <boundary conditions> making the boundary term vanish, for example homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, homogeneous <Neumann boundary conditions>, or a corresponding mixed pair. Arbitrary interior variations then give the <Sturm-Liouville problem>
$$
\boxed{-(py')'+qy=\lambda wy.}
$$
Multiplying this equation by $y$ and integrating by parts, with $[pyy']_\alpha^\beta=0$, yields
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\frac{\int_\alpha^\beta[p(y')^2+qy^2]dx}{\int_\alpha^\beta wy^2dx}=R[y].}
$$
Thus <eigenfunctions> are stationary directions of the quotient. The lowest <eigenvalue> is its global minimum; higher <eigenfunctions> need not be unrestricted maxima or minima. General Robin <boundary conditions> require including their boundary energy in the quotient unless the displayed endpoint products also vanish.

The <Rayleigh-Ritz method> chooses admissible trial functions depending on finitely many coefficients and minimizes this quotient over them. Expansion in a complete orthonormal basis with weight $w$ shows $R[y]\geq\lambda_0$, so the trial minimum is an upper bound on the true lowest <eigenvalue>. In a finite basis, the minimization becomes a <generalized eigenvalue problem> for the stiffness and weighted mass <matrices>. Enlarging the trial space can only lower or preserve this upper bound.

For a spherically symmetric function, the <Laplacian in spherical coordinates> reduces to
$$
\nabla^2\psi=\frac1{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{d\psi}{dr}\right).
$$
Multiplying the <Helmholtz equation> by $r^2$ gives
$$
\boxed{-\frac{d}{dr}(r^2\psi')=k^2r^2\psi,\qquad p(r)=r^2,\quad q(r)=0,\quad w(r)=r^2.}
$$
The endpoint at $r=0$ is singular in Sturm-Liouville terminology; bounded regular radial fields select the physical solution and eliminate the $1/r$ branch. At $r=a$ the field has the imposed zero Dirichlet value. For regular solutions and admissible trial functions, the endpoint term $r^2\psi\psi'$ vanishes at the origin and at the wall.

The radial <Rayleigh quotient> is
$$
k_{\rm trial}^2=\frac{\int_0^ar^2(\psi'_{\rm trial})^2dr}{\int_0^ar^2\psi_{\rm trial}^2dr}.
$$
For the given trial profile, the numerator is $4a^{-4}\int_0^ar^4dr=4a/5$. The denominator is
$$
\int_0^ar^2(1-r^2/a^2)^2dr=a^3\left(\frac13-\frac25+\frac17\right)=\frac{8a^3}{105}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{k_0^2\leq k_{\rm trial}^2=\frac{21}{2a^2}=\frac{10.5}{a^2}.}
$$
The profile is regular at the origin and vanishes at the wall, so it belongs to the admissible trial space. This is the variational estimate for the <radial Dirichlet modes of a spherical cavity>.

Set $\psi(r)=u(r)/r$ for $r>0$. Direct differentiation gives $(r^2\psi')'=ru''$, so the radial equation reduces to
$$
u''+k^2u=0.
$$
Boundedness of $\psi$ requires $u(0)=0$, and the wall condition requires $u(a)=0$. The nontrivial solutions are consequently
$$
\boxed{k_j=\frac{j\pi}{a},\qquad\psi_j(r)=C_j\frac{\sin(j\pi r/a)}r,\qquad j=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
Their origin values are the removable limits $C_jj\pi/a$. At $k=0$, the only bounded radial solution is constant and the wall forces it to vanish, so there is no zero mode. The lowest <eigenvalue> is
$$
\boxed{k_0^2=\frac{\pi^2}{a^2}\simeq\frac{9.86960}{a^2}.}
$$
It is below $10.5/a^2$, as required by the <Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle>. The trial value overestimates $k_0^2$ by approximately $\boxed{6.39\%}$.
