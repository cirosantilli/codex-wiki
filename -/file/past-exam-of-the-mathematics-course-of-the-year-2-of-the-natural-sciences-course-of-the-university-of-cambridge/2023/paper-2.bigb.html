= Solution
{parent=i}

The weight cancels the denominator in the <Sturm-Liouville operator>. Two <integrations by parts>, with real $p,q$, give the <Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator>
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle f,\mathcal Lg\rangle_w-\langle\mathcal Lf,g\rangle_w
&=\int_a^b\left[-f^*(pg')'+(pf^{*\prime})'g\right]dx\\
&=\left[p\left(f^{*\prime}g-f^*g'\right)\right]_a^b.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the boundary form must vanish for every pair in the operator domain:
$$
\boxed{\left[p(f^{*\prime}g-f^*g')\right]_a^b=0.}
$$
Separated real <Robin boundary conditions> $A_s y(s)+B_s p(s)y'(s)=0$ at $s=a,b$, with $(A_s,B_s)\ne(0,0)$, make each endpoint contribution vanish. This includes <Dirichlet boundary conditions> and <Neumann boundary conditions>. Coupled conditions can also work: periodic $y(a)=y(b)$ and $p(a)y'(a)=p(b)y'(b)$ cancel the two endpoint contributions. For the regular problem, imposing a complete pair of such self-adjoint <boundary conditions> gives a <self-adjoint differential operator>; the displayed identity is the boundary-form criterion.

First, setting $f=g=y_n$ in the <self-adjoint operator> identity shows that $\lambda_n\langle y_n,y_n\rangle_w$ is real, so every <eigenvalue> is real. For two <eigenfunctions>,
$$
\lambda_n\langle y_m,y_n\rangle_w
=\langle y_m,\mathcal Ly_n\rangle_w
=\langle\mathcal Ly_m,y_n\rangle_w
=\lambda_m\langle y_m,y_n\rangle_w.
$$
Distinct <eigenvalues> therefore imply
$$
\boxed{\langle y_m,y_n\rangle_w=\int_a^b y_m^*y_nw\,dx=0.}
$$
This is <orthogonality of Sturm-Liouville eigenfunctions>.

Use a <logarithmic change of variable in a Sturm-Liouville problem>: set $t=\log x$ and $Y(t)=y(e^t)$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
y'=\frac{Y'}x,\qquad y''=\frac{Y''-Y'}{x^2},\qquad \mathcal Ly=-Y''-Y.
$$
The endpoint conditions become $Y(0)=Y(1)=0$. Multiplying $-Y''=(\lambda+1)Y$ by $Y^*$ and integrating shows that $\lambda+1>0$ for a nonzero solution. Put $\mu=\sqrt{\lambda+1}$. The first endpoint selects $Y=A\sin(\mu t)$ and the second requires $\mu=n\pi$, $n\geq1$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\lambda_n=n^2\pi^2-1,\qquad y_n(x)=\sqrt2\sin(n\pi\log x),\quad n=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
These <eigenfunctions> are orthonormal in the specified <weighted inner product>, because $w(x)dx=dx/x=dt$ and
$$
\int_1^e\frac{y_m(x)y_n(x)}x\,dx
=2\int_0^1\sin(m\pi t)\sin(n\pi t)\,dt=\delta_{mn}.
$$

Expand the solution and the forcing in the orthonormal <Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion>. The forcing coefficients are
$$
b_n=\langle y_n,1\rangle_w
=\sqrt2\int_0^1\sin(n\pi t)\,dt
=\frac{\sqrt2[1-(-1)^n]}{n\pi}.
$$
Every $\lambda_n$ is nonzero, so the solution coefficient is $b_n/\lambda_n$. Thus
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2[1-(-1)^n]}{n\pi(n^2\pi^2-1)}\sin(n\pi\log x)
=\sum_{\substack{n\geq1\\n\text{ odd}}}\frac{4\sin(n\pi\log x)}{n\pi(n^2\pi^2-1)}.}
$$
As an independent check, solving $Y''+Y=-1$ directly with the two endpoint conditions gives
$$
y(x)=\frac{\cos(\log x-\tfrac12)}{\cos(\tfrac12)}-1.
$$
It satisfies both <Dirichlet boundary conditions> and the forced <ordinary differential equation>.

A single-valued potential must be $2\pi$-periodic in $\theta$. For a separated solution $\Phi=F(r)H(\theta)$, the <Laplace equation in polar coordinates> becomes
$$
\frac{r(rF')'}F=-\frac{H''}H=\mu.
$$
The periodic angular <eigenvalue problem> has $\mu=n^2$, $n\geq0$: its nonconstant <eigenfunctions> are $\cos n\theta,\sin n\theta$. Negative $\mu$ gives exponentials that cannot be periodic, and $\mu=0$ gives only a constant angular factor. For $n\geq1$, the radial <Cauchy-Euler equation> $r^2F''+rF'-n^2F=0$ gives $F=Ar^n+Br^{-n}$. At $n=0$ the two radial solutions are $1$ and $\log r$. <Superposition> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\Phi=A_0+B_0\log r+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[(A_nr^n+B_nr^{-n})\cos n\theta+(C_nr^n+D_nr^{-n})\sin n\theta\right].}
$$
The expansion is interpreted on an annulus where the <harmonic function> is defined; particular <boundary conditions> determine its constants and convergence region. Single-valuedness is essential: allowing a cut in the angular domain would also permit nonperiodic angular solutions.

= Solution

Treat each angular <Fourier mode> independently. Boundedness at the axis excludes the negative radial powers and the logarithm inside; decay at infinity excludes the positive powers, constant and logarithm outside. For the cosine mode write
$$
\Phi_{n,\rm in}=A_nr^n\cos n\theta,\qquad
\Phi_{n,\rm out}=B_nr^{-n}\cos n\theta.
$$
Continuity at $R$ gives $B_n=A_nR^{2n}$. The jump of the <normal derivative> is then
$$
-nB_nR^{-n-1}-nA_nR^{n-1}=-2nA_nR^{n-1}=-a_n.
$$
Thus $A_n=a_nR^{1-n}/(2n)$ and $B_n=a_nR^{n+1}/(2n)$. The sine mode has the same relation with $b_n$. Therefore the <potential of a charged cylindrical shell with angular Fourier data> is
$$
\boxed{\Phi(r,\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac R{2n}(a_n\cos n\theta+b_n\sin n\theta)
\begin{cases}(r/R)^n,&0\leq r\leq R,\\(R/r)^n,&r\geq R.\end{cases}}
$$
There is no angular mean term: the given <surface charge density> has zero average. The interior constant is zero because its boundary value must match the vanishing exterior mean. A nonzero mean charge per unit length would instead produce a logarithmic <electrostatic potential>, incompatible with the specified decay at infinity.

= Solution

Apply the <product rule> to the <vector field> $\Phi\nabla\Psi-\Psi\nabla\Phi$:
$$
\nabla\cdot(\Phi\nabla\Psi-\Psi\nabla\Phi)
=\Phi\Delta\Psi-\Psi\Delta\Phi.
$$
The gradient cross terms cancel. Integrating and applying the <divergence theorem> with outward unit normal $\mathbf n$ proves <Green's second identity>:
$$
\boxed{\int_V(\Phi\Delta\Psi-\Psi\Delta\Phi)\,dV
=\oint_S(\Phi\partial_n\Psi-\Psi\partial_n\Phi)\,dS.}
$$
Choose the <Dirichlet Green function> $G_D(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')$ with $\Delta_{\mathbf r}G_D=\delta(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')$ and $G_D=0$ on $S$. Taking $\Psi=G_D$ and using the <Dirac delta distribution>, the left side becomes $\Phi(\mathbf r')$ while the second boundary term vanishes. Hence
$$
\boxed{\Phi(\mathbf r')=\oint_S f(\mathbf r)\,\partial_{n_{\mathbf r}}G_D(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')\,dS.}
$$
The plus sign is tied to the convention $\Delta G_D=+\delta$, whose free-space <fundamental solution> is negative in three dimensions. The singularity may equivalently be handled by excising a small sphere about $\mathbf r'$ before applying the smooth identity.

Put $s=|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|$. Away from the source, the radial <Laplacian> in three dimensions gives
$$
\Delta_{\mathbf r}G_0=\frac1{s^2}\frac d{ds}\left(s^2\frac d{ds}\left[-\frac1{4\pi s}\right]\right)
=\frac1{s^2}\frac d{ds}\frac1{4\pi}=0.
$$
Thus $\boxed{\Delta G_0=0\text{ for }\mathbf r\ne\mathbf r'}$. At the source it is a <distribution>, not a smooth function; its outward gradient flux through a small sphere is $4\pi s^2/(4\pi s^2)=1$, giving $\Delta G_0=\delta$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Write $s=|\mathbf r'|$ and $\mathbf r''=a^2\mathbf r'/s^2$. If $|\mathbf r|=a$, expansion of squared <Euclidean distances> gives
$$
|\mathbf r-\mathbf r''|^2=\frac{a^2}{s^2}|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|^2.
$$
Consequently $|\mathbf r-\mathbf r''|=(a/s)|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|$, and the two terms in $H$ cancel. This <method of images> gives the <Dirichlet Green function inside a sphere>:
$$
\boxed{H(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=-\frac1{4\pi}\left[\frac1{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}-\frac{a/s}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r''|}\right].}
$$
The image point is outside the sphere, so only the physical source contributes a <Dirac delta distribution> inside. As $s\to0$, the image position tends to infinity and its coefficient also diverges, but their ratio has the finite limit
$$
\frac{a/s}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r''|}\longrightarrow\frac1a.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{H(\mathbf r,0)=-\frac1{4\pi|\mathbf r|}+\frac1{4\pi a}.}
$$
The apparent singularity of the image construction at a central source is removable; the physical source singularity at $\mathbf r=0$ remains.

Let $\overline{\mathbf r'}=(x',y',-z')$ be the reflection of the source in the equatorial plane. For a source in the upper half-ball, the <Dirichlet Green function inside a hemisphere> is
$$
\boxed{G_{\rm hemi}(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=H(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')-H(\mathbf r,\overline{\mathbf r'}).}
$$
Both terms vanish on the spherical boundary. On the flat boundary $z=0$, the two source distances are equal, and the distances to their spherical image points are also equal, so the difference vanishes there too. The reflected physical source is in the lower half-ball and both inverted image points are outside the sphere. Therefore the only source singularity in the desired region is the original one, with coefficient $+1$ in $\Delta G$. This combines reflection with the spherical <method of images>.

= Solution

Reflecting repeatedly in the two parallel planes creates an infinite <method of images>. For $\mathbf r'=(x',y',z')$, introduce
$$
\mathbf r_n^+=(x'+4n,y',z'),\qquad
\mathbf r_n^-=(-x'-2+4n,y',z'),\qquad n\in\mathbb Z.
$$
The <Dirichlet Green function between parallel planes> is
$$
\boxed{G_{\rm slab}(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=-\frac1{4\pi}\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}
\left[\frac1{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_n^+|}-\frac1{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_n^-|}\right].}
$$
Each bracket is $O(n^{-2})$, so the paired series converges absolutely away from the sources. At $x=-1$, reflection pairs the positive image indexed by $n$ with the negative image indexed by $-n$. At $x=1$ it pairs that positive image with the negative image indexed by $1-n$. The distances agree in each pairing, making both boundary values zero. This can be checked with symmetric finite partial sums first; the unmatched end terms tend to zero, so no subtraction of separately divergent series is needed. Only $\mathbf r_0^+=\mathbf r'$ lies within the slab, so $\Delta_{\mathbf r}G_{\rm slab}=\delta(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')$. This is the usual Green function decaying at large transverse separation; such a condition selects the physical solution in the unbounded region.

A function is an <analytic function> at $z_0$ if it is <holomorphic> throughout some open neighbourhood of $z_0$, equivalently if it has a convergent <power series> there. In real components $g=u+iv$, continuous first <partial derivatives> in a neighbourhood together with the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>
$$
\boxed{u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x}
$$
throughout that neighbourhood are sufficient, and for an analytic function they hold there. Satisfying these equations only at the single point is not enough to establish analyticity in a neighbourhood.

Factor the polynomial denominator as $(z-i)^2(z+i)^2$. The numerator has values $i$ and $-i$ at the two roots, so neither cancels. Therefore
$$
\boxed{z=i\text{ and }z=-i\text{ are double poles}.}
$$
The factor $z^{-1/2}$ gives a <branch point> at zero, with local behaviour $f(z)\sim z^{-1/2}$. Infinity is also a <branch point>, since in the coordinate $w=1/z$ the leading behaviour is $f(1/w)\sim w^{5/2}$. A single-valued branch requires a <branch cut> joining these points. Away from that cut and the two <poles>, the selected branch is <holomorphic>. For the integral below, use $0<\arg z<2\pi$, putting the cut along the positive real axis.

= Solution

Use a counterclockwise <keyhole contour> around the positive real <branch cut>, with small radius $\varepsilon$ and large radius $R$. On the upper bank $\arg z=0$, so the integral tends to $I$. On the lower bank $\arg z=2\pi$, $z^{1/2}=-\sqrt{x}$, giving the negative of the upper-bank integrand. That bank is traversed from $R$ to $\varepsilon$, so its contribution also tends to $I$.

The outer circle contributes $O(R^{-3/2})$, since $f=O(R^{-5/2})$ and its length is $O(R)$. The small circle contributes $O(\varepsilon^{1/2})$, since $f=O(\varepsilon^{-1/2})$. Both vanish. Hence
$$
\boxed{C=\lim_{\varepsilon\to0,\,R\to\infty}\oint_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon,R}}f(z)\,dz=2I.}
$$
The contour encloses both double <poles> $\pm i$ but excludes the branch point.

For a double <pole> at $z_0\in\{i,-i\}$, differentiate the remaining holomorphic factor:
$$
\operatorname{Res}(f,z_0)=\left.\frac d{dz}\frac{1+z+z^2}{(z+z_0)^2\sqrt z}\right|_{z=z_0}.
$$
Since $z_0^2=-1$ and $1+z_0+z_0^2=z_0$, this simplifies to
$$
\operatorname{Res}(f,z_0)=\frac{1/8-z_0/2}{\sqrt{z_0}}.
$$
On the selected branch $\sqrt i=e^{i\pi/4}$ and $\sqrt{-i}=e^{3i\pi/4}$, giving
$$
\operatorname{Res}(f,i)=\frac{\sqrt2}{16}(-3-5i),\qquad
\operatorname{Res}(f,-i)=\frac{\sqrt2}{16}(3-5i).
$$
The <residue theorem> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{C=2\pi i\left(-\frac{5i\sqrt2}{8}\right)=\frac{5\pi\sqrt2}{4},\qquad I=\frac{5\pi\sqrt2}{8}.}
$$
The equality $C=2I$ uses the limiting contour integrals established in (c).

The signal is continuous at zero with $f(0)=1$. Its envelope is $e^{-\lambda|t|}$, but its <angular frequency> falls from $2$ on the incoming side to $1$ on the outgoing side. Its one-sided slopes are $f'(0^-)=\lambda$ and $f'(0^+)=-\lambda$, producing a cusp at the peak. Both tails decay to zero.

Use $\widetilde f(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-i\omega t}f(t)\,dt$. Expanding each cosine into two exponentials and integrating its half-line gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde f(\omega)
&=\frac12\left[\frac1{\lambda-i(\omega-2)}+\frac1{\lambda-i(\omega+2)}\right]\\
&\quad+\frac12\left[\frac1{\lambda+i(\omega-1)}+\frac1{\lambda+i(\omega+1)}\right].
\end{aligned}
$$
Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{\widetilde f(\omega)=\frac{\lambda-i\omega}{(\lambda-i\omega)^2+4}+\frac{\lambda+i\omega}{(\lambda+i\omega)^2+1}.}
$$
The positive $\lambda$ guarantees convergence of the <Fourier transform> integrals.

The <Fourier transform of a derivative> gives $(-\omega^2-1)\widetilde y=-\widetilde f$. For $\lambda=1$,
$$
\boxed{\widetilde y(\omega)=\frac1{1+\omega^2}\left[\frac{1-i\omega}{(1-i\omega)^2+4}+\frac{1+i\omega}{(1+i\omega)^2+1}\right].}
$$
This selects the solution decaying at both time infinities. Indeed, the inverse transform of $1/(1+\omega^2)$ is the <one-dimensional modified Helmholtz Green function> $K(t)=e^{-|t|}/2$, and the <convolution theorem> gives $y=K*f$. The printed equation has homogeneous solutions $e^t,e^{-t}$, so these two decay conditions matter; no causal initial-value condition was specified.

For $t<0$, evaluate <Fourier inversion> by closing the contour in the lower half-plane, where $e^{i\omega t}$ decays. The contour is clockwise. The enclosed <simple poles> and <residues> of $\widetilde y$ are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
\omega&\operatorname{Res}\widetilde y\\\hline
-i&i/5\\
2-i&(-1+i)/16\\
-2-i&(1+i)/16
\end{array}
$$
Thus inversion equals $-i$ times the sum of the residues of $e^{i\omega t}\widetilde y$, giving
$$
\boxed{y(t)=e^t\left[\frac15+\frac18\cos(2t)-\frac18\sin(2t)\right],\qquad t<0.}
$$
As a check, applying $d^2/dt^2-1$ gives $-e^t\cos2t$. The convolution also gives $y(t)=e^{-t}[\tfrac18+\tfrac15\cos t+\tfrac25\sin t]$ for $t\geq0$; the two pieces have matching values $13/40$ and matching first derivatives $3/40$ at zero.

Under an <orthogonal matrix> $R$, the <Levi-Civita symbol> satisfies
$$
R_{ia}R_{jb}R_{mc}\epsilon_{abc}=(\det R)\epsilon_{ijm}.
$$
Use this twice, and use $R_{mc}R_{mf}=\delta_{cf}$ for the contracted index. It follows that
$$
R_{ia}R_{jb}R_{kc}R_{ld}\epsilon_{abe}\epsilon_{cde}
=(\det R)^2\epsilon_{ijm}\epsilon_{klm}=T_{ijkl}.
$$
Therefore $\boxed{T'_{ijkl}=R_{ia}R_{jb}R_{kc}R_{ld}T_{abcd}=T_{ijkl}}$: it is a fourth-rank <isotropic tensor>. Although a single alternating symbol is a <pseudotensor> under reflections, the two determinant factors multiply to one, so this product is an ordinary <tensor> even for improper orthogonal transformations.

= Solution

Write the inverse <tensor> as
$$
Y_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+\lambda A_{ij}+\lambda^2C_{ij}+\lambda^3D_{ij}+O(\lambda^4).
$$
Insert this into $(\delta_{ik}+\lambda X_{ik})Y_{kj}=\delta_{ij}$ and equate powers of $\lambda$. One obtains successively
$$
A_{ij}=-X_{ij},\qquad C_{ij}=-X_{ik}A_{kj}=X_{ik}X_{kj},\qquad
D_{ij}=-X_{ik}C_{kj}=-X_{ik}X_{kl}X_{lj}.
$$
Thus the <Neumann series> gives
$$
\boxed{Y_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-\lambda X_{ij}+\lambda^2X_{ik}X_{kj}-\lambda^3X_{ik}X_{kl}X_{lj}+O(\lambda^4).}
$$
Every contraction is an ordinary ordered <matrix product>. No transpose or symmetry assumption is used. Multiplying the truncated series on either side by $I+\lambda X$ leaves $I-\lambda^4X^4$, which verifies the error order. For a fixed finite matrix this expansion converges whenever $|\lambda|\,\|X\|<1$ in a submultiplicative <matrix norm>.

For the stated square side length $2a$, the volume is $4a^2h$ and the uniform <mass density> is $\rho_1=m_1/(4a^2h)$. Direct integration about its central vertical axis gives the <moment of inertia of a uniform square plate>:
$$
I_1=\rho_1h\int_{-a}^a\int_{-a}^a(x^2+y^2)\,dx\,dy
=\rho_1h\frac{8a^4}{3}
=\boxed{\frac23m_1a^2}.
$$
Thus the PDF's coefficient $1/6$ is inconsistent with its stated side length. The coefficient $1/6$ would apply to a square of side $a$.

For the disc, use <cylindrical coordinates> and $m_2=\rho_2\pi a^2h$:
$$
I_2=\rho_2h\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^a r^2r\,dr\,d\theta
=\frac{\pi\rho_2ha^4}{2}=\boxed{\frac12m_2a^2}.
$$
For the sphere, use <spherical coordinates> and $m_3=4\pi\rho_3a^3/3$:
$$
I_3=\rho_3\int_0^a r^4dr\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,d\theta\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi
=\frac{8\pi\rho_3a^5}{15}=\boxed{\frac25m_3a^2}.
$$
These agree with the <moment of inertia of a uniform disc> and the <moment of inertia of a uniform solid sphere>. The square's thickness does not enter its axial <moment of inertia> once the mass is fixed.

For a <normal subgroup> $H$, the <quotient group> consists of its <cosets> with multiplication
$$
\boxed{G/H=\{gH:g\in G\},\qquad(gH)(kH)=(gk)H.}

To check that this is well-defined, replace $g,k$ by $gh_1,kh_2$ with $h_1,h_2\in H$. Their product is

\boxed{|G|=|G/H|\,|H|,\qquad |H|\mid |G|.}
