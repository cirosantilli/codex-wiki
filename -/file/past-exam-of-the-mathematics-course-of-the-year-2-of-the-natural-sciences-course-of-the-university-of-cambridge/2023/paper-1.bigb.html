= Solution
{parent=a}

The three <coordinate basis> vectors are the partial <derivatives> of the position <vector> with respect to the three coordinates. Direct differentiation gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf h_\xi=\frac{\partial\mathbf x}{\partial\xi}=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh\xi\sin\theta\cos\phi\\\cosh\xi\sin\theta\sin\phi\\g'(\xi)\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf h_\theta=\frac{\partial\mathbf x}{\partial\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}\sinh\xi\cos\theta\cos\phi\\\sinh\xi\cos\theta\sin\phi\\-g(\xi)\sin\theta\end{pmatrix},}
$$
$$
\boxed{\mathbf h_\phi=\frac{\partial\mathbf x}{\partial\phi}=\begin{pmatrix}-\sinh\xi\sin\theta\sin\phi\\\sinh\xi\sin\theta\cos\phi\\0\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The multivariable <chain rule> then gives $d\mathbf x=\mathbf h_\xi d\xi+\mathbf h_\theta d\theta+\mathbf h_\phi d\phi$. These are coordinate <tangent vectors>, not necessarily unit <vectors>.

The azimuthal vector $\mathbf h_\phi$ is automatically orthogonal to the other two. Their remaining <dot product> is
$$
\mathbf h_\xi\cdot\mathbf h_\theta=[\sinh\xi\cosh\xi-g(\xi)g'(\xi)]\sin\theta\cos\theta.
$$
Thus <orthogonal coordinates> require $gg'=\sinh\xi\cosh\xi$, which integrates to $g^2=\sinh^2\xi+C$. The initial value gives $C=1$, and the smooth branch with $g(0)=1$ is
$$
\boxed{g(\xi)=\cosh\xi.}
$$
The corresponding coordinate-vector <norms> are $|\mathbf h_\xi|=|\mathbf h_\theta|=\sqrt{\sinh^2\xi+\sin^2\theta}$ and $|\mathbf h_\phi|=\sinh\xi\sin\theta$, as in the <hyperbolic parametrization of prolate spheroidal coordinates>. Coordinate degeneracies on the axis and focal segment are understood.

Eliminating the angular coordinates on the requested surface gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{x^2+y^2}{\sinh^21}+\frac{z^2}{\cosh^21}=1.}
$$
It is a <prolate spheroid> with long axis $z$, equatorial radius $\sinh1$, polar semiaxis $\cosh1$, and foci $(0,0,\pm1)$.

= Solution

Let $a=\sinh1$ and $c=\cosh1$. The region is the first-octant portion of the <ellipsoid> $(x^2+y^2)/a^2+z^2/c^2\leq1$. In <cylindrical coordinates>, write $\rho=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ and $0\leq\phi\leq\pi/2$. Away from the axis, the <divergence> is
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=\frac{2x+2y}{x^2+y^2}+\frac1\rho=\frac{2\cos\phi+2\sin\phi+1}{\rho}.
$$
The field is singular on the axis, so applying the classical <divergence theorem> there without qualification would be invalid. Instead remove $\rho<\varepsilon$ and apply it to the remaining region. On the added cylindrical boundary the <unit normal> is $-\mathbf e_\rho$, and
$$
\mathbf F\cdot(-\mathbf e_\rho)=-\log(\varepsilon^2)(\cos\phi+\sin\phi).
$$
Its area element is $\varepsilon\,d\phi\,dz$, so its <flux> is $O(\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|)\to0$. The axial component of the field has zero <dot product> with this normal. The <volume integral> of the <divergence> is locally integrable, because its $1/\rho$ factor cancels the cylindrical measure. Thus <removing a singular axis in a flux integral> gives the desired improper <surface integral> with no extra axial term.

The <divergence theorem> now reduces the calculation to
$$
\begin{aligned}
I&=\int_0^{\pi/2}(2\cos\phi+2\sin\phi+1)\,d\phi\int_0^c\int_0^{a\sqrt{1-z^2/c^2}}d\rho\,dz\\
&=\left(4+\frac\pi2\right)ac\int_0^1\sqrt{1-s^2}\,ds\\
&=\left(4+\frac\pi2\right)\frac{\pi ac}{4}
=\boxed{\frac{\pi(8+\pi)}8\sinh1\cosh1=\frac{\pi(8+\pi)}{16}\sinh2.}
\end{aligned}
$$
The integral over $s$ is the area of a quarter unit disk. All boundary <fluxes> are interpreted as convergent <improper integrals> at the axis.

Use <separation of variables>, $u=X(x)Y(y)$, and divide by $XY$ where nonzero. A real separation constant $\kappa$ gives
$$
\frac{X''}{X}=\frac{Y'}Y+1+\sin y=\kappa,
$$
so $X''=\kappa X$ and $Y'=(\kappa-1-\sin y)Y$. Normalizing the second factor to one at zero gives
$$
\boxed{Y_\kappa(y)=\exp[(\kappa-1)y+\cos y-1].}
$$
The spatial <ordinary differential equation> has three types of real solution:
$$
\boxed{X(x)=\begin{cases}A\cos(kx)+B\sin(kx),&\kappa=-k^2<0,\\A+Bx,&\kappa=0,\\A\cosh(kx)+B\sinh(kx),&\kappa=k^2>0.\end{cases}}
$$
Products $X Y_\kappa$ are the separated <eigenfunctions>. The negative, zero and positive separation constants give trigonometric, linear and <hyperbolic function> forms respectively. There is no discrete quantization until <boundary conditions> on $x$ are supplied. Since $\cos y-1$ is bounded, the envelope of $Y_\kappa$ decays for $\kappa<1$, grows for $\kappa>1$, and is bounded and periodic for $\kappa=1$. The scalar prefactor can also be derived by <removing a variable scalar reaction rate by an integrating factor>.

{parent=2}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The two homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions> on $x$ force negative separation constants. The initial profile is an <even function>, so only the even sine-family <eigenfunctions> are needed:
$$
X_m(x)=\cos(k_mx),\qquad k_m=\left(m+\frac12\right)\pi,\qquad m=0,1,\ldots.
$$
They obey $X_m(\pm1)=0$ and $\int_{-1}^1X_mX_n\,dx=\delta_{mn}$. Odd <eigenfunctions> have zero projection onto the even initial data. Hence the initial coefficients are
$$
\begin{aligned}
a_m&=\int_{-1}^1(1-x^2)\cos(k_mx)\,dx\\
&=2\left[-\frac{2x\cos(k_mx)}{k_m^2}+\frac{2\sin(k_mx)}{k_m^3}\right]_0^1
=\frac{4(-1)^m}{k_m^3}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The desired <eigenfunction expansion> is therefore
$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=4\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^m}{k_m^3}\cos(k_mx)\exp[-(k_m^2+1)y+\cos y-1],\qquad k_m=(m+\tfrac12)\pi.}
$$
At $y=0$ this is the <Fourier series> of $1-x^2$ on the interval; its coefficients are absolutely summable. Each term satisfies the <partial differential equation> and side conditions. For $y>0$ the additional Gaussian decay in $m$ justifies termwise <differentiation>. Since $k_m^2+1>0$ and $e^{\cos y-1}\leq1$, the whole series decays uniformly as $y\to\infty$.

Set $L=d^2/dt^2-4$ and choose $L_tG(t,t')=\delta(t-t')$, with the <Green's function> decaying at both ends. Away from the source, its homogeneous solutions are $e^{\pm2t}$. Decay at each end and continuity at $t=t'$ therefore give $G=C e^{-2|t-t'|}$. Integrating its equation across the source gives a unit jump in its first <derivative>:
$$
\partial_tG(t'^+,t')-\partial_tG(t'^-,t')=-2C-2C=1.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{A=-2,\qquad C=-\frac14,\qquad G(t,t')=-\frac14e^{-2|t-t'|}.}
$$
The solution of the <boundary value problem> is the <convolution>
$$
\boxed{x(t)=-\frac14\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-2|t-\tau|}f(\tau)\,d\tau.}
$$
There is no extra homogeneous term: no nonzero combination of $e^{2t},e^{-2t}$ decays at both infinities. The forcing must of course permit the imposed decay; integrable $f$, including part (b), is sufficient. Literally arbitrary forcing need not do so: constant nonzero forcing admits no solution vanishing at both ends.

Let $G(k)=\widetilde g(k)$. Since $g'=-xg$, <differentiation> under the <Fourier transform> integral and <integration by parts> give
$$
G'(k)=-i\,\widetilde{xg}(k)=i\,\widetilde{g'}(k)=i(ik)G(k)=-kG(k).
$$
Thus $G(k)=G(0)e^{-k^2/2}$. The <Gaussian integral> gives $G(0)=\sqrt{2\pi}$, proving the <Fourier transform of a Gaussian> formula
$$
\boxed{\widetilde g(k)=\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-k^2/2},\qquad c=\sqrt{2\pi}.}
$$
For the higher <derivatives>, start with $r_0=1$. If $g^{(m)}=r_mg$, the <product rule> gives
$$
g^{(m+1)}=(r_m'-xr_m)g,\qquad\boxed{r_{m+1}=r_m'-xr_m.}
$$
If $r_m$ has degree $m$ and leading coefficient $(-1)^m$, the recurrence gives degree $m+1$ and leading coefficient $(-1)^{m+1}$. Induction proves the assertion for every $m$. These polynomials are $r_m=(-1)^m\operatorname{He}_m$, where $\operatorname{He}_m$ is the <probabilists' Hermite polynomial>.

Assume the necessary <derivatives> decay enough for all boundary terms to vanish. One <integration by parts> gives
$$
\widetilde{f'}(k)=\int_{\mathbb R}f'(x)e^{-ikx}\,dx=ik\widetilde f(k).
$$
Repeating proves the <Fourier transform of a derivative> rule $\boxed{\widetilde{f^{(m)}}=(ik)^m\widetilde f}$. On the other hand, differentiation under the <integral> gives
$$
\frac{d^m\widetilde f}{dk^m}=\int_{\mathbb R}(-ix)^mf(x)e^{-ikx}\,dx,
$$
and therefore $\boxed{\widetilde{x^mf}=i^m\widetilde f^{(m)}}$.

Write $p_n(x)=\sum_{j=0}^na_jx^j$, with $a_n\ne0$. Applying the second rule and part (a) gives
$$
\mathcal F[p_n(x)g(x)](k)=\sqrt{2\pi}\sum_{j=0}^na_ji^jg^{(j)}(k)=q_n(k)e^{-k^2/2},
$$
where
$$
\boxed{q_n(k)=\sqrt{2\pi}\sum_{j=0}^na_ji^jr_j(k).}
$$
Only the $j=n$ term can contribute degree $n$. Its leading coefficient is $\sqrt{2\pi}a_n(-i)^n\ne0$, so $q_n$ has exactly the same degree $n$, with no possible leading-term cancellation. The coefficients of this transformed <polynomial> can be complex even when those of $p_n$ are real.

By the two <Fourier transform> rules in part (b),

Part (b) shows that the transform of $g_n$ is $q_n(k)e^{-k^2/2}$ with $q_n$ of degree $n$. Part (c) shows that it solves the same <eigenvalue> equation as $g_n$, with the same $\mu_n$. The assumed uniqueness of the polynomial solution of each degree therefore forces $q_n=c_np_n$; hence $\widetilde g_n=c_ng_n$.

The constants can also be determined. Substituting $g_n=p_ne^{-x^2/2}$ into the equation gives the <Hermite differential equation>
$$
p_n''-2xp_n'-(1+\mu_n)p_n=0.
$$
Comparison of the leading coefficient yields $\mu_n=-(2n+1)$. If the leading coefficient of $p_n$ is $a_n$, part (b) gives leading coefficient $\sqrt{2\pi}(-i)^na_n$ for $q_n$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\widetilde g_n(k)=\sqrt{2\pi}(-i)^ng_n(k),\qquad c_n=\sqrt{2\pi}(-i)^n.}
$$
Thus <Hermite functions are Fourier eigenfunctions> with the paper's unnormalized <Fourier transform>; dividing that transform by $\sqrt{2\pi}$ gives the unitary eigenvalues $(-i)^n$.

Since $M^T=-M$, $(M^2)^T=(M^T)^2=M^2$, so $M^2$ is a real <symmetric matrix>. Moreover,
$$
\boxed{x^TM^2x=-x^TM^TMx=-\|Mx\|^2\leq0,}
$$
proving that it is a <negative semidefinite matrix>.

For odd $n$, <determinant> invariance under transpose gives
$$
\det M=\det M^T=\det(-M)=(-1)^n\det M=-\det M,
$$
so $\det M=0$. Hence $M$ and $M^2$ have a zero <eigenvalue>. Finally, if $M^2w=0$, then $0=w^TM^2w=-\|Mw\|^2$, so $Mw=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\ker M^2=\ker M,}
$$
where equality of these <kernels> includes the reverse implication, which is immediate.

Because $M$ <commutes> with $M^2$,
$$
M^2v_1=\frac1{\mu_1}M(M^2u_1)=-\mu_1^2v_1.
$$
Its squared <Euclidean norm> is
$$
\|v_1\|^2=\frac1{\mu_1^2}u_1^TM^TMu_1=-\frac1{\mu_1^2}u_1^TM^2u_1=1.
$$
A scalar equals its transpose, while $M^T=-M$, so $u_1^TMu_1=-u_1^TMu_1=0$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{M^2v_1=-\mu_1^2v_1,\qquad\|v_1\|=1,\qquad u_1^Tv_1=0.}
$$
Thus each negative <eigenvalue> has at least this pair of orthonormal <eigenvectors>.

The plane spanned by $u_1,v_1$ is an <invariant subspace> for $M$, since
$$
Mu_1=\mu_1v_1,\qquad Mv_1=\frac1{\mu_1}M^2u_1=-\mu_1u_1.
$$
Its <orthogonal complement> is also invariant: for $w$ orthogonal to both, $u_1^TMw=-(Mu_1)^Tw=0$ and $v_1^TMw=-(Mv_1)^Tw=0$. The restriction of the real <symmetric matrix> $M^2$ to this nonempty complement therefore has a unit <eigenvector> $u_2$, by the <spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices>.

If its <eigenvalue> is $-\mu_2^2<0$, the argument in part (b) shows that $v_2=Mu_2/\mu_2$ is a unit <eigenvector> orthogonal to $u_2$. It also lies in the same complement; explicitly,
$$
u_1^Tv_2=-\frac{\mu_1}{\mu_2}v_1^Tu_2=0,\qquad v_1^Tv_2=\frac{\mu_1}{\mu_2}u_1^Tu_2=0.
$$
Repeating the construction decomposes every negative <eigenspace> into orthogonal pairs. The zero <eigenspace> has dimension $n-2r$ for some number $r$ of pairs, and therefore has the same parity as $n$. This is the <paired eigenspaces of the square of a real skew-symmetric matrix> property.

For $n=2m$, there can be $m$ distinct negative <eigenvalues> and no zero, or at most $m-1$ negative ones plus zero, because a nonempty zero <eigenspace> has dimension at least two. For $n=2m+1$, there are at most $m$ distinct negative ones plus the mandatory zero. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{maximum number of distinct eigenvalues of }M^2=\left\lceil\frac n2\right\rceil.}
$$
The bound is attained by skew two-by-two blocks with distinct positive parameters, adding one zero direction when $n$ is odd.

The required actions have already been identified:
$$
\boxed{Mu_1=\mu_1v_1,\qquad Mv_1=-\mu_1u_1.}
$$
In three dimensions choose a unit <vector> $w$ perpendicular to both. Its one-dimensional <orthogonal complement> to the pair is invariant under $M$. On a one-dimensional real space, a <skew-symmetric matrix> must act as zero: $Mw=\eta w$ would imply $\eta=w^TMw=0$. Thus $Mw=0$.

The columns of $W=(u_1\ v_1\ w)$ form an <orthonormal basis>, so $W$ is an <orthogonal matrix>. The displayed actions give
$$
W^TMW=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\mu_1&0\\\mu_1&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}=B,
$$
and therefore
$$
\boxed{M=WBW^T.}
$$
If $M=0$, take $\mu_1=0$ and any <orthogonal matrix> $W$. Otherwise its nonzero two-dimensional block supplies the pair used above.

For a real <symmetric matrix> $A$, find its real <eigenvalues> from $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$, then solve $(A-\lambda I)v=0$ for the corresponding <eigenspaces>. Distinct <eigenspaces> are orthogonal. Within each repeated <eigenspace>, apply the <Gram-Schmidt process> and normalize to obtain an <orthonormal basis> of <eigenvectors>.

Put these vectors in the columns of $O$. The <spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices> guarantees a full basis, so $O^TO=I$ and
$$
\boxed{O^TAO=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n),\qquad A=O\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)O^T.}
$$
This is the <orthogonal diagonalization> of $A$ and the associated <orthogonal change of coordinates>.

= Solution

For an <analytic function>, the <complex differentiability> limit is independent of the direction of approach. A real increment gives $f'(z)=u_x+iv_x$, whereas an imaginary increment gives
$$
f'(z)=\frac{u_y+iv_y}{i}=v_y-iu_y.
$$
Equality of real and imaginary parts proves the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>:
$$
\boxed{u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x.}
$$
Conversely, if $u,v$ have continuous first partial <derivatives> near a point and obey these equations, their real differentiability gives, for a complex increment $h$,
$$
f(z+h)-f(z)=(u_x+iv_x)\operatorname{Re}h+(u_y+iv_y)\operatorname{Im}h+o(|h|)=(u_x+iv_x)h+o(|h|).
$$
Dividing by $h$ proves the <complex differentiability> there. Continuous first partial <derivatives> are a sufficient regularity hypothesis for this converse; agreement along the two coordinate directions alone is only a necessary condition.

Using the complex cosine identity,
$$
\cos(x+iy)=\cos x\cosh y-i\sin x\sinh y,
$$
we find
$$
(x+iy)\cos(x+iy)=x\cos x\cosh y+y\sin x\sinh y+i[y\cos x\cosh y-x\sin x\sinh y].

$$
\boxed{f(z)=z\cos z+iC,\qquad C\in\mathbb R.}

To check the only freedom, two <analytic functions> with this <real part> differ by a function with zero real part. The <Cauchy-Riemann equations> then force both partial <derivatives> of its imaginary part to vanish, so it is constant on the connected domain. No normalization was supplied to fix $C$.

Multiply the exponential <power series> by the two prefactors:
$$
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{1-n}}{n!}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{-1-n}}{n!}.
$$
Collecting powers yields the <Laurent series>
$$
\boxed{f(z)=z+1+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac1{(m+1)!}+\frac1{(m-1)!}\right)z^{-m},\qquad0<|z|<\infty.}
$$
Its first terms are $z+1+3/(2z)+7/(6z^2)+13/(24z^3)+\cdots$. Infinitely many negative-power coefficients are nonzero, so $\boxed{z=0\text{ is an essential singularity}}$. The <residue> is the coefficient of $z^{-1}$:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Res}_{z=0}f=\frac1{2!}+\frac1{0!}=\frac32.}
$$
The phrase "power series" here refers to a <Laurent series>; no ordinary <Taylor series> about an <essential singularity> exists.

Away from zeros of the leading coefficient, the normal-form <differential equation> is
$$
y''+P(z)y'+Q(z)y=0,\qquad P(z)=\frac3{z(1+z^2)^2},\qquad Q(z)=\frac1{z^2(1+z^2)^2}.
$$
The only finite singular points are $z=0,i,-i$. At zero, $zP(z)=3/(1+z^2)^2$ and $z^2Q(z)=1/(1+z^2)^2$ are analytic, so zero is a <regular singular point>. At $z=\pm i$, the coefficient $P$ has a genuine double <pole>, so $(z\mp i)P$ is not analytic; these are <irregular singular points>. Thus
$$
\boxed{z=0\text{ regular singular};\qquad z=\pm i\text{ irregular singular}.}
$$
If infinity is included, set $t=1/z$ and $Y(t)=y(1/t)$. The transformed coefficients are $\widetilde P=2/t-3t^3/(1+t^2)^2$ and $\widetilde Q=t^2/(1+t^2)^2$. They satisfy the <regular singular point at infinity> criterion, so $\boxed{\infty\text{ is regular singular}}$ as well.

Seek a <Frobenius solution> $y=z^r\sum_{n\geq0}a_nz^n$ with $a_0\ne0$. Since $(z+z^3)^2=z^2+2z^4+z^6$, its lowest coefficient gives the <indicial equation>
$$
[r(r-1)+3r+1]a_0=(r+1)^2a_0=0.
$$
Thus the repeated <indicial root> is $\boxed{r=-1}$. Equating the coefficient of $z^{n+r}$ before setting $r=-1$ gives
$$
(n+r+1)^2a_n+2(n+r-2)(n+r-3)a_{n-2}+(n+r-4)(n+r-5)a_{n-4}=0.
$$
For $n\geq1$ this becomes the <linear recurrence relation>
$$
\boxed{a_n=-\frac{2(n-3)(n-4)a_{n-2}+(n-5)(n-6)a_{n-4}}{n^2},\qquad a_j=0\text{ for }j<0.}
$$
Every coefficient is fixed by $a_0$, since the denominator never vanishes for $n\geq1$. In particular $a_1=0$ and then every odd coefficient is zero. Therefore there is $\boxed{\text{exactly one independent pure Frobenius series}}$, not two merely because the indicial root repeats. If "power series" means an ordinary <Taylor series> with nonnegative integer powers, the only such solution is zero: its coefficient equation starts with $a_0=0$ and inductively forces all coefficients to vanish.

A second independent local solution is a <logarithmic solution from a repeated Frobenius exponent>. Indeed, <Abel's identity> gives $W=Cz^{-3}[1+O(z^2)]$. For the normalized first solution $y_1=z^{-1}[1+O(z^2)]$, <reduction of order> gives
$$
y_2=y_1\int^z\frac{\exp[-\int^sP(v)dv]}{y_1(s)^2}\,ds.
$$
After choosing the harmless multiplicative constant in the numerator, its integrand is $1/s+O(s)$, so $y_2=y_1\log z+z^{-1}\times\text{an analytic series}$. The nonzero logarithmic coefficient proves that this second solution is not another pure <Frobenius solution>.

The <Fermat principle> states that the actual path of a <light ray> makes its travel time stationary under small admissible <variations> with fixed endpoints. Equivalently, its <optical path length> is stationary:
$$
\boxed{\delta T=\delta\int\frac{ds}{v(\mathbf x)}=0,\qquad\delta\int n(\mathbf x)\,ds=0,}
$$
where $v$ is the local light speed, $n$ is the <refractive index>, and the two integrals differ by the constant vacuum light speed. In a uniform medium this reduces to stationarity of geometric path length. The principle is a stationarity statement; for the planar reflection considered next the stationary path is also the unique minimum.

Take the fixed source and receiver to be $A=(a,h_A)$ and $B=(b,h_B)$ on the same side of the mirror, with $h_A,h_B>0$. In the uniform medium, the <Fermat principle> minimizes the length through a reflection point $P=(s,0)$:
$$
L(s)=\sqrt{(s-a)^2+h_A^2}+\sqrt{(s-b)^2+h_B^2}.
$$
Its stationary equation is
$$
L'(s)=\frac{s-a}{\sqrt{(s-a)^2+h_A^2}}+\frac{s-b}{\sqrt{(s-b)^2+h_B^2}}=0.
$$
The two fractions are the signed tangential components of the ray directions. Equality of their magnitudes gives $\sin\theta_i=\sin\theta_r$, where both angles are measured from the <normal vector> to the mirror and lie in $[0,\pi/2)$. Hence the <law of reflection> is
$$
\boxed{\theta_i=\theta_r.}
$$
For uniqueness, differentiate once more:
$$
L''(s)=\frac{h_A^2}{[(s-a)^2+h_A^2]^{3/2}}+\frac{h_B^2}{[(s-b)^2+h_B^2]^{3/2}}>0.
$$
Thus $L$ is a <strictly convex function>, its <derivative> increases from $-2$ to $2$, and it has exactly one stationary point, its <global minimum>. Explicitly,
$$
\boxed{s_* =\frac{h_Ba+h_Ab}{h_A+h_B}.}
$$
Substitution gives $(s_*-a)/h_A=(b-s_*)/h_B$, verifying the reflection angles and the existence of that unique solution. Equivalently it is the point where the line from $A$ to the mirror image $(b,-h_B)$ crosses the mirror.

First expand the variational integrand:
$$
\mathcal L=f'^2+(3f-x)f'+2f^2-2x^2.
$$
Its needed partial <derivatives> are $\mathcal L_f=3f'+4f$ and $\mathcal L_{f'}=2f'+3f-x$. The <Euler-Lagrange equation> is therefore
$$
\frac d{dx}(2f'+3f-x)-(3f'+4f)=0,\qquad\boxed{f''-2f=\frac12.}
$$
The general solution is $f=A\cosh(\sqrt2x)+B\sinh(\sqrt2x)-1/4$. Subtracting the two endpoint equations gives $B=0$; adding them gives $A=1/[4\cosh\sqrt2]$. Hence
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\frac14\left[\frac{\cosh(\sqrt2x)}{\cosh\sqrt2}-1\right].}
$$
This is the unique stationary solution. It is also the unique <global minimizer>: after discarding the fixed <boundary terms>, <integration by parts> rewrites $I$ as $\int_{-1}^1[f'^2+2f^2+f-2x^2]dx$. For an endpoint-zero <variation> $\eta$, the linear part vanishes at the displayed solution and
$$
I[f+\eta]-I[f]=\int_{-1}^1[\eta'^2+2\eta^2]dx\geq0,
$$
with equality only for $\eta=0$.

The original PDF has a typographical omission: its displayed equation has $\lambda w(x)$ rather than $\lambda w(x)y(x)$ on the right. Taken literally, it is an inhomogeneous <boundary value problem>, not the stated <Sturm-Liouville problem>. The quotient and the later <eigenfunction expansion> identify the intended equation, which the following derivation produces explicitly.

Choose homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions> $y(a)=y(b)=0$ and a nonzero real admissible $y$. Then $G[y]>0$ because $w>0$. For an endpoint-zero <variation> $\eta$, <integration by parts> gives
$$
\delta F=2\int_a^b[py'\eta'+qy\eta]dx=2\int_a^b[-(py')'+qy]\eta\,dx,\qquad\delta G=2\int_a^bwy\eta\,dx.
$$
The <first variation> of the <Rayleigh quotient> is thus
$$
\delta\Lambda=\frac2{G[y]}\int_a^b[-(py')'+qy-\Lambda[y]wy]\eta\,dx.
$$
By the <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations>, stationarity is equivalent to
$$
\boxed{-(py')'+qy=\lambda wy,\qquad\lambda=\Lambda[y],\qquad y(a)=y(b)=0.}
$$
Conversely, multiply this corrected <eigenvalue> equation by $y$ and integrate. The same boundary cancellation gives $F[y]=\lambda G[y]$, so the <first variation> vanishes. This proves the requested equivalence for the intended equation. Homogeneous <Neumann boundary conditions>, or a Dirichlet condition at one end and a Neumann condition at the other, also eliminate the boundary term. General <Robin boundary conditions> would require the corresponding boundary contribution in the numerator.

Use the normalized <eigenfunctions> with the stated <weighted inner product>. <Orthogonality> gives $G[\hat y]=\sum_n|c_n|^2$. The corrected <Sturm-Liouville problem> and the vanishing boundary term give
$$
F[\hat y]=\int_a^b\overline{\hat y}\,\mathcal L\hat y\,dx=\sum_n\lambda_n|c_n|^2.
$$
For a complex trial the numerator means $\int(p|\hat y'|^2+q|\hat y|^2)dx$, agreeing with the given formula for real trials. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Lambda[\hat y]=\frac{\sum_n\lambda_n|c_n|^2}{\sum_n|c_n|^2}\geq\lambda_0.}
$$
It is a <convex combination> of the <eigenvalues>, with equality only for a trial proportional to the lowest <eigenfunction>. The <Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle> consequently estimates $\lambda_0$ by choosing a nonzero admissible trial family and minimizing this quotient over its parameters:
$$
\boxed{\lambda_0\leq\min_{\text{trial parameters}}\Lambda[\hat y].}
$$
The result is an upper bound. A more flexible trial space can lower it, while equality requires that the exact lowest <eigenfunction> belong to the trial space. One need not know the expansion coefficients to calculate the quotient directly from the trial function.

Take $\gamma>0$ and set $A=\pi/2$. The <Rayleigh quotient> of the trial for the <quantum harmonic oscillator> is
$$
R(\gamma)=\frac{\int_{-A/\gamma}^{A/\gamma}[\gamma^2\sin^2(\gamma x)+x^2\cos^2(\gamma x)]dx}{\int_{-A/\gamma}^{A/\gamma}\cos^2(\gamma x)dx}.
$$
Its denominator is $\pi/(2\gamma)$. The kinetic numerator is $\gamma^2$ times that denominator. For the remaining <integral>, put $t=\gamma x$ and use
$$
\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}t^2\cos^2t\,dt=\frac{\pi^3}{24}-\frac\pi4=\frac{\pi(\pi^2-6)}{24},
$$
obtained from $\cos^2t=(1+\cos2t)/2$ and <integration by parts>. Thus the <compact cosine trial bound for the quantum harmonic oscillator> is
$$
\boxed{R(\gamma)=\gamma^2+\frac{\pi^2-6}{12\gamma^2}.}
$$
Differentiation gives $R'=2\gamma-(\pi^2-6)/(6\gamma^3)$, so the minimizing parameter satisfies
$$
\boxed{\gamma_{\mathrm{opt}}^4=\frac{\pi^2-6}{12}.}
$$
The quotient tends to infinity at either end of $\gamma>0$, and the two positive terms are equal at this stationary point, proving it is the global minimum. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\lambda_0\leq\sqrt{\frac{\pi^2-6}{3}}\approx1.13572.}
$$
For comparison, the exact <ground state> has value $1$. The trial is admissible for the energy <quadratic form>: it is continuous, has compact support, and its <weak derivative> is square-integrable. Its first <derivative> jumps at the support endpoints, so it need not belong to the smaller twice-differentiable <operator domain>; this does not invalidate the <Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle> bound.
