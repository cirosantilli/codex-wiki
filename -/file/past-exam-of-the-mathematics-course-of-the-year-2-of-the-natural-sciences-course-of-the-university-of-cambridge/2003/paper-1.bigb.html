= Solution
{parent=1}

For $a>0$, the positive constant-$u$ surfaces in <elliptic cylindrical coordinates> satisfy
$$
\frac{x^2}{a^2\cosh^2u}+\frac{y^2}{a^2\sinh^2u}=1.
$$
They are confocal <elliptic cylinders> with focal lines $x=\pm a,y=0$. For a generic fixed $v$, eliminating $u$ gives
$$
\frac{x^2}{a^2\cos^2v}-\frac{y^2}{a^2\sin^2v}=1.
$$
Because $u\geq0$, a fixed $v$ selects the half-branch with the signs of $x$ and $y$ fixed by $\cos v$ and $\sin v$. These are <hyperbolic cylinders>. The degenerate $u=0$ surface is $y=0$, $|x|\leq a$, extended in $z$. At $v=0,\pi$ the cross-sections are the two exterior horizontal rays; at $v=\pi/2,3\pi/2$ they are vertical half-axes.

\Image[paper-1-elliptic-coordinate-net.png]
{title=Confocal elliptic and hyperbolic coordinate curves}
{description=Original cross-section with focal points marked. Ellipses are constant-u curves and the hyperbola half-branches are constant-v curves; extruding in z gives their coordinate surfaces.}

Differentiate the position <vector> to get the coordinate <tangent vectors>
$$
r_u=(a\sinh u\cos v,a\cosh u\sin v,0),\quad r_v=(-a\cosh u\sin v,a\sinh u\cos v,0),\quad r_z=(0,0,1).
$$
Their pairwise <dot products> vanish. Their squared lengths are $a^2(\sinh^2u+\sin^2v)$, $a^2(\sinh^2u+\sin^2v)$ and one. This proves <orthogonality> and gives the <scale factors of orthogonal coordinates>:
$$
\boxed{h_u=h_v=h=a\sqrt{\sinh^2u+\sin^2v},\qquad h_z=1.}
$$
The <Riemannian metric> is $ds^2=h^2(du^2+dv^2)+dz^2$. Away from the focal lines, divide the coordinate <tangent vectors> by their lengths to obtain the local orthonormal <unit vectors> $e_u,e_v,e_z$. For physical <vector field> components $A_u,A_v,A_z$, the formulas for <vector calculus in elliptic cylindrical coordinates> are
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot A=\frac{\partial_u(hA_u)+\partial_v(hA_v)+\partial_z(h^2A_z)}{h^2},}
$$
$$
\boxed{\nabla\times A=\frac{\partial_vA_z-\partial_z(hA_v)}h e_u+\frac{\partial_z(hA_u)-\partial_uA_z}h e_v+\frac{\partial_u(hA_v)-\partial_v(hA_u)}{h^2}e_z.}
$$
The <gradient> of a <scalar> <function> has components $(\phi_u/h,\phi_v/h,\phi_z)$. Apply the <divergence> formula to this <gradient>; <Laplace's equation> becomes
$$
\boxed{\frac{\phi_{uu}+\phi_{vv}}{a^2(\sinh^2u+\sin^2v)}+\phi_{zz}=0.}
$$
The formulas apply where $h>0$; the focal lines require a limit or another coordinate chart.

With <temperature> measured relative to the fixed-temperature end, the <heat equation> and its data are
$$
\boxed{u_t=\nu u_{xx}\quad(0<x<L,t>0),\qquad u(0,t)=0,\qquad k u_x(L,t)=H,\qquad u(x,0)=0.}
$$
Here $\nu>0$ is thermal <diffusivity> and $k>0$ is <thermal conductivity>. The sign of the <derivative> condition describes incoming <heat flux> at $L$: the outward conductive <heat flux> is $-k u_x=-H$.

= ii
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Choose the <stationary solution> $w''=0$ satisfying $w(0)=0$ and $kw'(L)=H$. Hence $\boxed{w(x)=Hx/k}$. Subtracting it gives the homogeneous problem
$$
\boxed{v_t=\nu v_{xx},\qquad v(0,t)=0,\qquad v_x(L,t)=0,\qquad v(x,0)=-\frac{Hx}k.}
$$
This <boundary lifting> leaves a <Dirichlet boundary condition> at one end and a <Neumann boundary condition> at the other, as in the <fixed-temperature and fixed-flux heat equation on an interval>.

= iii
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For a nonzero separated solution $v=X(x)T(t)$, <separation of variables> gives $X''+\kappa^2X=0$ and $T'=-\nu\kappa^2T$. The endpoint conditions require $X=\sin\kappa x$ and $\cos\kappa L=0$. Thus the <mixed Dirichlet-Neumann modes on an interval> have $\kappa_n=(n+1/2)\pi/L$, $n\geq0$. Their squared <L2 norms> are $L/2$, so the initial <Fourier coefficients> are
$$
b_n=-\frac{2H}{kL}\int_0^Lx\sin(\kappa_nx)dx=-\frac{2H(-1)^n}{kL\kappa_n^2}.
$$
The integration uses $\cos\kappa_nL=0$ and $\sin\kappa_nL=(-1)^n$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{v(x,t)=-\frac{8HL}{k\pi^2}\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n}{(2n+1)^2}\sin\frac{(2n+1)\pi x}{2L}\exp\left[-\frac{\nu(2n+1)^2\pi^2t}{4L^2}\right],\qquad u=\frac{Hx}k+v.}
$$
At positive time the series can be differentiated termwise and satisfies both homogeneous endpoint conditions for $v$. At time zero it recovers $-Hx/k$, so $u$ recovers the initial zero <temperature>. If $H\ne0$, the prescribed <derivative> at $(L,0)$ is incompatible with differentiating the initial zero profile; the <temperature> is still continuous there, while its spatial <derivative> has a corner layer. At long times the decaying modes leave $u\to Hx/k$.

= Solution
{parent=3}

Use the <Fourier transform> convention $\widehat f(k)=\int_{\mathbb R}f(t)e^{-ikt}dt$, with inverse factor $1/(2\pi)$. For <functions> in <L1 space>, their <convolution> is $h(t)=\int_{\mathbb R}f(s)g(t-s)ds$. <Fubini's theorem> applies because the absolute double <integral> is $\|f\|_1\|g\|_1$. Substituting $r=t-s$ therefore proves the <convolution theorem>:
$$
\widehat h(k)=\int_{\mathbb R}\int_{\mathbb R}f(s)g(r)e^{-ik(r+s)}drds=\widehat f(k)\widehat g(k).
$$
For the specified <rectangular pulse>, the support condition is $|t|<1$. Its <Fourier transform> is
$$
\boxed{\widehat f(k)=\int_{-1}^1e^{-ikt}dt=\frac{2\sin k}k,\qquad\widehat f(0)=2.}
$$
The <convolution> $f*f$ is the length of the overlap of $(-1,1)$ and $(t-1,t+1)$. Thus it is the <triangular pulse>
$$
\boxed{(f*f)(t)=\begin{cases}2-|t|,&|t|<2,\\0,&|t|\geq2,\end{cases}\qquad\widehat{f*f}(k)=\frac{4\sin^2k}{k^2}.}
$$
The transform's value at zero is four, given by its continuous extension. In this convention, the <Parseval identity> for <square-integrable functions> is
$$
\int_{\mathbb R}|f(t)|^2dt=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}|\widehat f(k)|^2dk.
$$
For the <rectangular pulse> the left side is two, giving the <sinc-squared integral>
$$
\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^2k}{k^2}dk=\pi.}
$$
For the <triangular pulse>, $\int|f*f|^2dt=2\int_0^2(2-t)^2dt=16/3$. Apply the same <Parseval identity> with transform $4\sin^2k/k^2$ to obtain the <sinc fourth-power integral>
$$
\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^4k}{k^4}dk=\frac{2\pi}3.}
$$
Both integrands have removable singularities at zero and integrable tails.

= Solution
{parent=4}

The <spectral theorem> gives a real orthonormal <eigenbasis> $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ for a real <symmetric matrix> $A$. Distinct <eigenspaces> are <orthogonal>; within a repeated <eigenspace>, choose an <orthonormal basis>, for example by <Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization>. Put these <eigenvectors> in the columns of $T$. Then $T^{-1}=T^T$ and
$$
T^{-1}AT=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n).
$$
For a change of variables $y=Sx$, choose $\boxed{S=T^T}$. Since $x=Ty$, the <quadratic form> becomes $x^TAx=\sum_i\lambda_i y_i^2$; the $\lambda_i$ are precisely the <eigenvalues> of $A$.

In the numerical case, the off-diagonal entries are half the cross-term coefficients, so
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&3\\1&1&-3\\3&-3&-3\end{pmatrix}.
$$
A direct <eigenvector> calculation gives the unit <eigenvectors> $(1,1,0)/\sqrt2$, $(1,-1,1)/\sqrt3$, $(1,-1,-2)/\sqrt6$, with <eigenvalues> two, three and minus six. They are pairwise <orthogonal>. Therefore the required <orthogonal diagonalization> is
$$
\boxed{y_1=\frac{x_1+x_2}{\sqrt2},\qquad y_2=\frac{x_1-x_2+x_3}{\sqrt3},\qquad y_3=\frac{x_1-x_2-2x_3}{\sqrt6},}
$$
and the transformed <quadratic form> is $\boxed{2y_1^2+3y_2^2-6y_3^2}$.

= Solution
{parent=5}

If $Hv=\lambda v$ for a <Hermitian matrix> and a nonzero <eigenvector>, then $v^\dagger Hv=\lambda v^\dagger v$. The <scalar> on the left equals its complex conjugate because $H^\dagger=H$, while $v^\dagger v>0$. Thus $\lambda$ is real. If also $Hw=\mu w$, then
$$
\mu v^\dagger w=v^\dagger Hw=(Hv)^\dagger w=\lambda v^\dagger w.
$$
Consequently distinct <eigenvalues> force $v^\dagger w=0$, proving <orthogonality> of their <eigenvectors>.

For a <unitary matrix>, $U^\dagger U=I$ preserves the <norm> induced by the standard <Hermitian inner product>. If $Uv=\zeta v$ with $v\ne0$, then $\|v\|^2=\|Uv\|^2=|\zeta|^2\|v\|^2$, so $\boxed{|\zeta|=1}$.

= a
{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=a}

Since $A$ and $B$ are real <symmetric matrices>, $U^\dagger=A-iB$. Expanding the <unitarity> condition gives
$$
I=U^\dagger U=(A-iB)(A+iB)=A^2+B^2+i(AB-BA).
$$
Equality of the entrywise real and imaginary parts yields
$$
\boxed{A^2+B^2=I,\qquad AB=BA.}
$$
These are the identities for the <real and imaginary parts of a symmetric unitary matrix>.

{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let $Av=\alpha v$. The commutation relation gives $A(Bv)=B(Av)=\alpha Bv$, so $B$ preserves the <eigenspace> of $A$ with <eigenvalue> $\alpha$. Because it is a <nondegenerate eigenvalue>, the <eigenspace> is one-dimensional, and $Bv=\beta v$ for some real $\beta$. This includes $Bv=0$. Thus the <eigenvectors> of $A$ form a common <eigenbasis>, and
$$
Uv=(\alpha+i\beta)v.
$$
Writing the corresponding unit-modulus <eigenvalue> of $U$ as $\zeta=e^{i\theta}$ gives
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\operatorname{Re}\zeta=\cos\theta,\qquad\beta=\operatorname{Im}\zeta=\sin\theta.}
$$
The pairing refers to the same common <eigenvector>; independent reorderings of the two real spectra would lose this information. The <simultaneous diagonalization> can also be constructed without requiring <simple eigenvalues> by diagonalizing $B$ within each <eigenspace> of $A$.

= Solution
{parent=6}

For the normalized second-order <linear differential equation> $y''+P(z)y'+Q(z)y=0$, an <ordinary point> $z_0$ has analytic $P,Q$ near $z_0$. At a <regular singular point>, which is not ordinary, $(z-z_0)P(z)$ and $(z-z_0)^2Q(z)$ are analytic. Ordinary points admit two independent convergent <power series> solutions. Regular singular points instead call for the <Frobenius method>, which permits leading powers and, in some cases, <logarithms>.

Here zero is an <ordinary point>. Substitute $y=\sum_{n\geq0}a_nz^n$ into the <Hermite differential equation> and equate coefficients:
$$
(n+2)(n+1)a_{n+2}+(\lambda-2n)a_n=0,\qquad\boxed{a_{n+2}=\frac{2n-\lambda}{(n+2)(n+1)}a_n.}
$$
The even and odd initial coefficients are independent, giving
$$
y=a_0\left[1-\frac\lambda2z^2+\frac{\lambda(\lambda-4)}{24}z^4+\cdots\right]+a_1\left[z+\frac{2-\lambda}{6}z^3+\frac{(2-\lambda)(6-\lambda)}{120}z^5+\cdots\right].
$$
These are the <power series solutions of the Hermite differential equation>, with the present parameter equal to twice the parameter in that article. The ratio of successive terms within each nonterminating parity is asymptotically $2|z|^2/n$, so both series have infinite <radius of convergence>.

For a nonzero <polynomial> of degree $m$, its highest coefficient in $-2zy'+\lambda y$ forces $\lambda=2m$. Conversely at that value the matching parity terminates. Hence
$$
\boxed{\lambda=2m,\qquad m=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
The <polynomial> solutions are <scalar multiples> of the <Hermite polynomials>. Two examples are $\boxed{\lambda=0:\ y=1;\qquad\lambda=2:\ y=z}$; direct substitution verifies both. The other independent solution generally remains nonpolynomial.

{parent=Paper 1}

= Solution
{parent=7}

Multiplication by $x^2$ puts the <eigenvalue problem> into <Sturm-Liouville form>, $-(x^2y')'=\lambda x^2y$, with weight $x^2$. If $u,v$ correspond to distinct <eigenvalues> $\lambda,\mu$, subtract their multiplied equations and integrate to get
$$
(\lambda-\mu)\int_0^\pi x^2uv\,dx=\left[x^2(uv'-vu')\right]_0^\pi=0.
$$
The upper boundary term vanishes by the <Dirichlet boundary condition>. For a bounded solution near zero, $x^2y'$ has a limit because $(x^2y')'=-\lambda x^2y$ is integrable. A nonzero limit would force a $1/x$ divergence in $y$, so that limit is zero; integration then gives $y'=O(x)$. The lower boundary term therefore also vanishes, proving the weighted <orthogonality>.

Set $z=xy$. For $x>0$, $y''+2y'/x=z''/x$, and finiteness of $y(0)$ gives $z(0)=0$. Thus $z''+\lambda z=0$ with zero values at zero and $\pi$. The <radial Dirichlet spectrum in three dimensions> gives
$$
\boxed{\lambda_n=n^2,\qquad y_n(x)=\frac{\sin nx}{x},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots,\quad y_n(0)=n.}
$$
The zero and negative <eigenvalues> yield only the zero solution under these <boundary conditions>. Also $\int_0^\pi x^2y_n^2dx=\pi/2$.

For the forced equation, the same substitution gives $z''-\beta z=x$. The <Fourier sine series> of $x$ on $(0,\pi)$ has coefficients
$$
b_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi x\sin nx\,dx=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}n.
$$
Expanding $z=\sum c_n\sin nx$ gives $-(n^2+\beta)c_n=b_n$, hence
$$
\boxed{y(x)=2\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n}{n(n^2+\beta)}\frac{\sin nx}{x}.}
$$
The value at zero is defined by a <limit>. In fact $|\sin nx/x|\leq n$, so <uniform convergence> holds even there, by comparison with $2\sum(n^2+\beta)^{-1}$. An independent check comes from solving the constant-coefficient equation directly:
$$
y(x)=-\frac1\beta+\frac{\pi\sinh(\sqrt\beta x)}{\beta x\sinh(\pi\sqrt\beta)}.
$$
It satisfies both endpoint conditions and the forcing for $x>0$, and its finite origin limit agrees with the series. For $\beta>0$ the homogeneous boundary value problem has no nonzero solution, so the solution is unique.

= Solution
{parent=8}

Substitution of $y=x^p$ into the homogeneous <Euler-Cauchy equation> gives $(p(p-1)+p-1)x^{p-2}=(p^2-1)x^{p-2}$, so $\boxed{p=1,-1}$. Use the solution $u(x)=x$ that vanishes at zero and $v(x)=1/x$ that decays at infinity. Their <Wronskian> is $uv'-u'v=-2/x$. The <half-line Green function for the first cylindrical harmonic> is therefore
$$
\boxed{G(x,s)=\begin{cases}-x/2,&0<x\leq s,\\-s^2/(2x),&0<s\leq x.\end{cases}}
$$
It is continuous at $x=s$, its first <derivative> has jump $1/2-(-1/2)=1$, and it solves the homogeneous equation elsewhere. Thus $L_xG=\delta(x-s)$ for integration with ordinary measure $ds$. The <boundary conditions> hold for each fixed $s>0$.

Integrating against the forcing gives
$$
y(x)=-\frac1{2x}\int_0^xs^2e^{-s}ds-\frac x2\int_x^\infty e^{-s}ds.
$$
Since the two <integrals> are $2-(x^2+2x+2)e^{-x}$ and $e^{-x}$, respectively,
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{(x+1)e^{-x}-1}{x}\quad(x>0),\qquad y(0)=0.}
$$
A <Taylor expansion> gives $y=-x/2+O(x^2)$ near zero, while $y\sim-1/x$ at infinity. This verifies the two endpoint limits; direct differentiation verifies $Ly=e^{-x}$ on $x>0$. The differential expression itself is singular at zero, so the endpoint condition is imposed by continuity. No nonzero combination of $x$ and $1/x$ satisfies both homogeneous endpoint conditions, proving <uniqueness>.

= Solution
{parent=9}

Vary $r$ by $r+\varepsilon\eta$ with $\eta(a)=\eta(b)=0$. The first <variation> of the <functional> is
$$
\delta I_1=\int_a^b(F_{1,r}\eta+F_{1,r'}\eta')dz=\int_a^b\left(F_{1,r}-\frac d{dz}F_{1,r'}\right)\eta\,dz.
$$
The boundary term from <integration by parts> is zero. The <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> therefore gives $\boxed{F_{1,r}-dF_{1,r'}/dz=0}$. For $I_2$, independently vary $r$ and $\theta$ with zero endpoint variations; the two <Euler-Lagrange equations> are
$$
\boxed{F_{2,r}-\frac d{dz}F_{2,r'}=0,\qquad F_{2,\theta}-\frac d{dz}F_{2,\theta'}=0.}
$$
For the optical path, <Fermat principle> makes $\int\mu\,ds$ stationary. In <cylindrical coordinates> its <integrand> is $L=\mu(r,\theta)s$, with $s=\sqrt{1+r'^2+r^2\theta'^2}$. There is no explicit $z$ dependence. The <Beltrami identity for several dependent variables> gives
$$
L-r'L_{r'}-\theta'L_{\theta'}=\mu s-\frac{\mu(r'^2+r^2\theta'^2)}s=\frac\mu s=\text{constant}.
$$
The conserved quantity is the axial component of the <optical momentum of a ray>. As $\mu>0$, squaring and inverting yields
$$
\boxed{\frac{1+r'^2+r^2\theta'^2}{\mu^2}=\text{constant}.}
$$
Now suppose $\mu=\mu(r)$ and seek a path of radius $R>0$. The angle is a <cyclic coordinate>, so $L_{\theta'}=\mu R^2\theta'/\sqrt{1+R^2\theta'^2}$ is conserved. At fixed radius this is strictly increasing in $\theta'$, forcing $\theta'=\omega$ constant. The radial <Euler-Lagrange equation> reduces to
$$
\mu'(R)(1+R^2\omega^2)+\mu(R)R\omega^2=0.
$$
Consequently the <helical rays in a radially graded refractive index> have
$$
\boxed{r(z)=R,\qquad\theta(z)=\theta_0+\omega z,\qquad\omega^2=-\frac{\mu'(R)}{R[\mu(R)+R\mu'(R)]}.}
$$
A real nonzero <helix> exists at such a radius when $0<-R\mu'(R)/\mu(R)<1$, and its <helical pitch> is $2\pi/|\omega|$. If $\mu'(R)=0$, the solution is instead a straight axial <light ray>. Thus radial dependence permits constant-radius rays for suitable profiles; an arbitrary radial profile need not support a nonzero real <helix>. This qualification is necessary, for example when the <refractive index> increases with radius.

= Solution
{parent=10}

Choose homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, $y(a)=y(b)=0$, and nonzero admissible <functions> with finite $F$ and positive $G$. Variations obey the same zero endpoint values. At a stationary <Rayleigh quotient>, set $\lambda=F[y]/G[y]$. Then
$$
0=G\,\delta\Lambda=\delta F-\lambda\delta G=2[py'\eta]_a^b+2\int_a^b\left[-(py')'+qy-\lambda wy\right]\eta\,dx.
$$
The boundary term vanishes, and the <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> gives the <Sturm-Liouville equation> $-(py')'+qy=\lambda wy$. Thus stationary <functions> are <eigenfunctions>. Conversely, multiply that equation by $y$ and integrate by parts to obtain $F=\lambda G$; its first <variation> then vanishes by the same calculation. This proves that stationary values are the corresponding <eigenvalues>. Homogeneous <Neumann boundary conditions> also make the displayed boundary terms vanish; general <Robin boundary conditions> can require a boundary contribution to the quadratic form.

For $p=1$, $q=0$, $w=x$ on $(0,\pi)$, the <polynomial> <trial function> $y=x(\pi-x)$ satisfies the endpoint conditions. Its <Rayleigh quotient> uses
$$
F=\int_0^\pi(\pi-2x)^2dx=\frac{\pi^3}3,\qquad G=\int_0^\pi x^3(\pi-x)^2dx=\frac{\pi^6}{60},
$$
so the <polynomial> estimate is $\boxed{\lambda_{\rm poly}=20/\pi^3\simeq0.64503}$. The trigonometric <trial function> $y=\sin x$ gives
$$
F=\frac\pi2,\qquad G=\int_0^\pi x\sin^2x\,dx=\frac{\pi^2}4,
$$
and hence $\boxed{\lambda_{\rm trig}=2/\pi\simeq0.63662}$. Both are **upper bounds** on the lowest <eigenvalue>, by the <Rayleigh-Ritz method>; neither trial is an exact <eigenfunction>, so the inequalities are strict.

Their closeness has a useful explanation beyond their similar single-arch shapes. Both <trial functions> are symmetric under $x\mapsto\pi-x$, forcing $\int_0^\pi xy^2dx=(\pi/2)\int_0^\pi y^2dx$. The <reflection-symmetric trials for a linearly weighted Rayleigh quotient> therefore reduce this problem to a constant multiple of the ordinary Dirichlet energy quotient. The <sine> is the best trial within this symmetric class, and the <polynomial>'s value is only a factor $10/\pi^2\simeq1.0132$ larger. The unrestricted lowest <eigenfunction> can be asymmetric because the weight $x$ is asymmetric.
