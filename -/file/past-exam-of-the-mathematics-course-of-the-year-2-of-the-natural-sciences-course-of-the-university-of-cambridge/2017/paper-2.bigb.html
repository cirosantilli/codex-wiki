= Solution
{parent=a}

The relevant <weighted inner product> is $\langle u,v\rangle_w=\int_0^1w(x)u(x)v(x)\,dx$. The normalized <eigenfunctions> of the <Sturm-Liouville problem> have the <orthonormality> property
$$
\boxed{\int_0^1w(x)y_n(x)y_m(x)\,dx=\delta_{nm}.}
$$
Here $\delta_{nm}$ is the <Kronecker delta>: the integral is one for $n=m$ and zero otherwise. The weight is $w$, rather than $p$ or the unweighted measure.

= b
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Projecting the <Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion> onto $y_m$ using the <weighted inner product> gives
$$
a_m=\langle f,y_m\rangle_w=\int_0^1w(x)f(x)y_m(x)\,dx.
$$
For the partial sum $f_N=\sum_{n=0}^Na_ny_n$, <orthonormality> therefore gives
$$
\|f_N\|_w^2=\sum_{n,m=0}^Na_na_m\langle y_n,y_m\rangle_w=\sum_{n=0}^Na_n^2.
$$
For $f$ of finite <weighted norm>, the given completeness means that $f_N\to f$ in that norm. The <triangle inequality> implies $|\|f_N\|_w-\|f\|_w|\leq\|f_N-f\|_w\to0$. Taking the limit of the finite identity proves <Parseval's identity>:
$$
\boxed{\int_0^1w(x)f(x)^2\,dx=\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n^2.}
$$
This argument justifies the limiting step without assuming that a double infinite series can be integrated term by term. As usual, the expansion is understood for <square-integrable functions> with respect to the weighted measure; the boundary and smoothness assumptions of the regular <Sturm-Liouville problem> provide the usual stronger interpretations when needed.

= Solution

Write $Y=\sum_{n\geq0}b_ny_n$ in the complete <Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion>. To determine $b_m$, multiply the differential equation by $y_m$ and integrate. The <Sturm-Liouville operator> has the symmetry identity
$$
\int_0^1\big(y_m\mathcal LY-Y\mathcal Ly_m\big)\,dx=\big[p(Yy_m'-y_mY')\big]_0^1=0,
$$
because both functions obey the homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions>. Consequently
$$
\lambda_m\int_0^1wYy_m\,dx=\alpha\int_0^1wYy_m\,dx+\int_0^1wfy_m\,dx,
$$
so that $(\lambda_m-\alpha)b_m=a_m$. The stipulated exclusion of every <eigenvalue> makes all denominators nonzero, and gives
$$
\boxed{Y(x)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{a_n}{\lambda_n-\alpha}y_n(x).}
$$
This is the <resolvent operator> applied to $f$ in the weighted space. The same projection shows <uniqueness>: a homogeneous solution would have $(\lambda_m-\alpha)b_m=0$ for every $m$, hence would vanish by completeness. For the regular problem the formula gives the solution in the operator domain when $f$ is square-integrable.

= Solution

Only two coefficients in the <Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion> are nonzero: $a_0=2$ and $a_1=2-\alpha$. Since $\alpha\ne2$, the result of part (i) simplifies to
$$
Y=\frac{2}{1-\alpha}y_0+\frac{2-\alpha}{2-\alpha}y_1=\frac{2}{1-\alpha}y_0+y_1.
$$
Using <Parseval's identity>, the prescribed <weighted norm> becomes
$$
\frac{4}{(1-\alpha)^2}+1=2.
$$
Thus $1-\alpha=\pm2$, so $\alpha=-1$ or $3$. Positivity selects
$$
\boxed{\alpha=3,\qquad Y=-y_0+y_1.}
$$
The standing nonresonance assumption still applies: this answer requires that $3$ is not one of the unspecified higher <eigenvalues>. Their actual values are not supplied. If a higher <eigenvalue> were $3$, the stated exclusion would fail and the data would not have an admissible $\alpha$ under that exclusion.

Use <separation of variables> with $\Phi=M(\mu)N(\nu)$. A nonzero product gives
$$
\frac{M''}{M}=-\frac{N''}{N}=\lambda,\qquad M''-\lambda M=0,\qquad N''+\lambda N=0.
$$
The angular factor must be a <periodic function> of <period> $2\pi$. If $\lambda<0$, its exponential solutions cannot be periodic unless they vanish. If $\lambda=0$, $N=c+d\nu$ forces $d=0$, while $M=A_0+B_0\mu$. If $\lambda>0$, periodicity requires $\sqrt\lambda=n\in\mathbb N$, giving angular factors $\cos(n\nu),\sin(n\nu)$ and radial factors $\cosh(n\mu),\sinh(n\mu)$.

Superposing these separated <harmonic functions> yields
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\Phi(\mu,\nu)={}&A_0+B_0\mu\\
&+\sum_{n=1}^\infty\Big\{[A_n\cosh(n\mu)+B_n\sinh(n\mu)]\cos(n\nu)\\
&\hspace{45mm}+[C_n\cosh(n\mu)+D_n\sinh(n\mu)]\sin(n\nu)\Big\}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
To see completeness, expand an arbitrary smooth <harmonic function> in a <Fourier series> in $\nu$ on each ellipse. Substitution into the equation forces every <Fourier coefficient> to obey the corresponding radial equation above. The series is interpreted with the convergence needed for a <harmonic function>, for example locally on compact subannuli. No boundedness condition at infinity or extension across $\mu=0$ has been imposed, so there is no reason to discard either radial branch in these <elliptic coordinates>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let $d=b-a$. In the <mixed Dirichlet-Neumann problem in an elliptic annulus>, the angular <Fourier modes> separate. The outer data excite the $\cos\nu$ mode, while the inner derivative excites $\cos2\nu$.

For the first mode, a radial factor with zero derivative at $\mu=a$ is $\cosh(\mu-a)$. Normalizing its outer value gives $F_1(\mu)=\cosh(\mu-a)/\cosh d$. For the second mode, a radial factor vanishing at $\mu=b$ is $\sinh[2(b-\mu)]$. Its derivative at $a$ must be $-1$, fixing the coefficient to $1/[2\cosh(2d)]$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Phi(\mu,\nu)=\frac{\cosh(\mu-a)}{\cosh(b-a)}\cos\nu+\frac{\sinh[2(b-\mu)]}{2\cosh[2(b-a)]}\cos2\nu.}
$$
Each term satisfies <Laplace's equation>; at $\mu=b$ the second term vanishes and the first is $\cos\nu$. At $\mu=a$, differentiating gives $0-\cos2\nu$, as required. All other modes, including the constant mode, vanish by the homogeneous mixed conditions. Alternatively, <integration by parts> applied to the difference of two solutions gives a zero integral of $|\nabla\Phi|^2$, proving <uniqueness>.

**Boundary convention.** The calculation follows the explicitly printed coordinate derivative $\partial_\mu\Phi$. In <elliptic coordinates>, the <scale factor> is $h=\sqrt{\sinh^2\mu+\sin^2\nu}$ and the outward <unit normal> at the inner boundary points toward decreasing $\mu$. A physical <normal derivative> there would be $-h^{-1}\partial_\mu\Phi$. Thus the phrase “normal derivative” in the source must be understood in its displayed coordinate sense; imposing the same numerical data on a unit-normal derivative would be a different problem.

Set $\kappa=|k|$. The differential operator in the <modified Helmholtz equation> depends only on $k^2=\kappa^2$. Let $G$ be its decaying <Green function>. Acting on the proposed integral with the operator, in the sense of <distributions>, gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
(\nabla_{\mathbf r}^2-k^2)\phi(\mathbf r)
&=\int_{\mathbb R^3}(\nabla_{\mathbf r}^2-k^2)G(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')\rho(\mathbf r')\,d^3\mathbf r'\\
&=\int_{\mathbb R^3}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')\rho(\mathbf r')\,d^3\mathbf r'\\
&=\rho(\mathbf r).
\end{aligned}
$$
The last step is the sifting property of the <Dirac delta>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\phi(\mathbf r)=\int_{\mathbb R^3}G(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')\rho(\mathbf r')\,d^3\mathbf r'.}
$$
For a regular rapidly decaying source, the $1/|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|$ singularity of the kernel found in part (ii) is locally <integrable> in three dimensions, and its convolution with the source tends to zero at infinity. This verifies both the equation and the decay condition. The sign of the kernel below corresponds to $\nabla^2-k^2$; reversing the sign of the entire operator reverses the kernel.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

By translation and rotation invariance, use a radial <Green function> $G(R)$, where $R=|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|$ is the <Euclidean distance>. Away from the source, the radial <modified Helmholtz equation> is
$$
G''+\frac2R G'-k^2G=0.
$$
Writing $u=RG$ reduces this to $u''-\kappa^2u=0$, where $\kappa=|k|$. For $\kappa>0$, decay eliminates $e^{\kappa R}/R$, leaving $G=Ae^{-\kappa R}/R$. For $\kappa=0$, the two radial branches are a constant and $1/R$; decay again leaves $A/R$.

To fix the singular normalization, integrate the defining equation over a ball of radius $\varepsilon$ about $\mathbf r'$. The <divergence theorem> gives
$$
4\pi\varepsilon^2G'(\varepsilon)-k^2\int_{R<\varepsilon}G\,d^3\mathbf r=1.
$$
The volume term is $O(\varepsilon^2)$, whereas
$$
4\pi\varepsilon^2G'(\varepsilon)=-4\pi A e^{-\kappa\varepsilon}(1+\kappa\varepsilon)\longrightarrow-4\pi A.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{A=-\frac1{4\pi},\qquad G(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=-\frac{e^{-|k||\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}}{4\pi|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}.}
$$
**Source qualification.** The printed $e^{-kR}$ is the decaying branch only when $k\geq0$, although the source declares $k$ real. For negative $k$ it grows, so it cannot be the requested decaying <Green function>. The absolute value above covers every real $k$ and reduces to the printed form for nonnegative $k$.

= Solution

The domain is the three-dimensional upper <half-space>. Reflect the source point to $\mathbf r'^*=(x',y',-z')$. The <method of images> places an opposite source at this reflected point, giving the <Dirichlet image kernel for the three-dimensional modified Helmholtz equation>
$$
\boxed{G_D(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=-\frac1{4\pi}\left[\frac{e^{-\kappa|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}-\frac{e^{-\kappa|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'^*|}}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'^*|}\right],\qquad\kappa=|k|.}
$$
On $z=0$, the two <Euclidean distances> are equal, so the terms cancel exactly. The image lies outside the domain, so its contribution satisfies the homogeneous <modified Helmholtz equation> everywhere inside; only the original source contributes a <Dirac delta>. Both terms decay at infinity, including for $k=0$, and the homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary condition> follows.

= Solution
{parent=b}

This is the <Dirichlet principle>. Put $v=w-\psi$, so $v$ vanishes on the boundary. For real, sufficiently regular functions of finite <Dirichlet energy>, <Green's first identity> gives
$$
\int_V\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla v\,dV=\int_Sv\,\partial_n\psi\,dS-\int_Vv\nabla^2\psi\,dV=0.
$$
The surface term is zero because $v=0$, and the volume term is zero because $\psi$ is a <harmonic function>. Expanding the square of $\nabla w=\nabla\psi+\nabla v$ now gives the stronger identity
$$
\boxed{\int_V|\nabla w|^2\,dV=\int_V|\nabla\psi|^2\,dV+\int_V|\nabla(w-\psi)|^2\,dV\ \geq\ \int_V|\nabla\psi|^2\,dV.}
$$
Thus the <harmonic function> minimizes <Dirichlet energy> among functions with the same boundary values. Equality requires $\nabla v=0$, hence $v$ is constant on each connected component; the zero boundary value makes that constant zero on every component having boundary. For an unbounded domain one additionally needs the boundary term at infinity to vanish; the usual bounded regular-domain setting needs no such extra condition.

= Solution

A <simple zero> of an <analytic function> has a local factorization $f(z)=(z-z_0)u(z)$, where $u$ is an <analytic function> and $u(z_0)=f'(z_0)\ne0$. Since $1/u$ is also an <analytic function> near $z_0$, its <Taylor series> gives
$$
\frac1{f(z)}=\frac1{z-z_0}\left[\frac1{f'(z_0)}+O(z-z_0)\right].
$$
The nonzero coefficient of $(z-z_0)^{-1}$ proves that this is a <simple pole>, with
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Res}_{z=z_0}\frac1{f(z)}=\frac1{f'(z_0)}.}
$$
Multiplying by the <Taylor series> $g(z)=g(z_0)+O(z-z_0)$ similarly gives
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Res}_{z=z_0}\frac{g(z)}{f(z)}=\frac{g(z_0)}{f'(z_0)}.}
$$
The assumption $g(z_0)\ne0$ ensures that the <pole> is not canceled. In either formula the <residue> is precisely the coefficient of $(z-z_0)^{-1}$ in the <Laurent series>.

Clear the apparent negative power of $z$ to obtain the <rational function>
$$
h(z)=\frac{-2iz}{z^2-2iaz-1}.
$$
Let $\Delta=\sqrt{a^2-1}>0$. Its denominator factors as $(z-z_-)(z-z_+)$, with
$$
\boxed{z_-=i(a-\Delta),\qquad z_+=i(a+\Delta).}
$$
These are distinct <simple poles>. Since $(a-\Delta)(a+\Delta)=1$ and $a+\Delta>1$, we have $0<a-\Delta<1$: only $z_-$ lies inside the <unit circle>. The apparent singularity at $z=0$ in the original expression is a <removable singularity>, with the continued value $h(0)=0$; it is not another pole.

Using the <residue> formula from part (a), with denominator derivative $2z-2ia$, gives
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Res}_{z=z_-}h=\frac{-2iz_-}{2z_--2ia}=\frac{i(a-\Delta)}{\Delta}.}
$$
There are no other finite singularities of the continued <rational function>.

First use $\sin\theta/(a-\sin\theta)=a/(a-\sin\theta)-1$. If $J=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(a-\sin\theta)^{-1}\,d\theta$, the required integral is $aJ-2\pi$.

Set $z=e^{i\theta}$. The <unit circle> is traversed counterclockwise once, $d\theta=dz/(iz)$, and $\sin\theta=(z-z^{-1})/(2i)$. Thus
$$
J=\frac1i\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{h(z)}z\,dz.
$$
The function $h(z)/z=-2i/(z^2-2iaz-1)$ is regular at zero. Its only enclosed <pole> is $z_-$ from part (b), and its <residue> is
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=z_-}\frac{h(z)}z=\frac1{z_-}\operatorname{Res}_{z=z_-}h=\frac1\Delta.
$$
By the <residue theorem>, $J=(1/i)2\pi i/\Delta=2\pi/\Delta$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\sin\theta}{a-\sin\theta}\,d\theta=2\pi\left(\frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2-1}}-1\right).}
$$
As a sign and scale check, this is positive and behaves as $\pi/a^2$ for large $a$, in agreement with expanding the integrand and integrating its first nonzero, $\sin^2\theta/a^2$, term.

Write $F(\omega)=\widetilde f(\omega)$ to distinguish the <Fourier transform> from the original function. <Integration by parts>, using the stated decay, gives
$$
\widehat{f'}(\omega)=\big[f(t)e^{-i\omega t}\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty}+i\omega\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)e^{-i\omega t}\,dt=i\omega F(\omega).
$$
Applying the same argument to $f'$ gives $\widehat{f''}=i\omega\widehat{f'}=-\omega^2F$. Differentiation with respect to the frequency gives
$$
F'(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(-it)f(t)e^{-i\omega t}\,dt=-i\widehat{tf}(\omega).
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\widehat{f'}=i\omega F,\qquad\widehat{f''}=-\omega^2F,\qquad\widehat{tf}=iF'.}
$$
The classical steps require existence of the relevant integrals and justification for differentiation under the integral, for example sufficient integrability of $f$, its derivatives and $tf$. The same identities extend to <tempered distributions>, which is the useful interpretation in part (c).

= Solution

Let $Y=\widehat y$. Transform the three terms of the order-zero <Bessel differential equation> using part (a):
$$
\widehat{ty''}=i(-\omega^2Y)'=-i(2\omega Y+\omega^2Y'),\qquad\widehat{y'}=i\omega Y,\qquad\widehat{ty}=iY'.
$$
Adding and dividing by $i$ gives
$$
\boxed{(1-\omega^2)Y'-\omega Y=0.}
$$
On $-1<\omega<1$, <separation of variables> gives $Y'/Y=\omega/(1-\omega^2)$, hence $Y=C/\sqrt{1-\omega^2}$. To obtain the usual solution regular at $t=0$, choose the compactly supported extension
$$
Y(\omega)=\frac{C\,\mathbf1_{(-1,1)}(\omega)}{\sqrt{1-\omega^2}}.
$$
This is a genuine <distributional weak solution>: the differential equation can be written $[(1-\omega^2)Y]'+\omega Y=0$, and $(1-\omega^2)Y=C\sqrt{1-\omega^2}\,\mathbf1_{(-1,1)}$ vanishes continuously at both endpoints. Thus differentiation produces no endpoint <Dirac delta> terms.

<Fourier inversion> now gives
$$
y(t)=\frac{C}{2\pi}\int_{-1}^1\frac{e^{i\omega t}}{\sqrt{1-\omega^2}}\,d\omega.
$$
This integral is smooth at the origin and solves the original equation. Its value there is $C/2$. Normalizing $y(0)=1$ fixes $C=2$, and identifies the <Bessel function of the first kind> of order zero:
$$
\boxed{J_0(t)=\frac1\pi\int_{-1}^1\frac{\cos(tu)}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\,du=\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi\cos(t\cos\theta)\,d\theta.}
$$
The last equality uses $u=\cos\theta$. Without normalization, the regular solution is an arbitrary constant times this integral; this is the <Fourier transform of the Bessel function J0> pair.

**Regularity qualification.** The source specifies the differential equation but no condition at its singular point $t=0$. The transformed equation separately has solutions $C_\pm/\sqrt{\omega^2-1}$ on the two exterior intervals. Thus it does not, by itself, determine the support or a single global multiplicative constant. The compactly supported choice above constructs the regular Bessel branch. The other independent branch, the <Bessel function of the second kind>, is singular at the origin. Also $J_0$ is not absolutely integrable on the real line, so its transform is properly taken as a <tempered distribution>, rather than relying on the stronger classical integrability assumptions of part (a).

In orthonormal <Cartesian coordinates>, a rank-$n$ <tensor> has components transforming under a change of axes $R$ as
$$
\boxed{T'_{i_1\cdots i_n}=R_{i_1j_1}\cdots R_{i_nj_n}T_{j_1\cdots j_n},\qquad R^TR=I.}
$$
Repeated indices are summed. This is the <tensor component transformation law>; for rank one it is precisely the transformation law of a <vector>.

Use the <vector triple product> to write the double <cross product> as
$$
W=u\times(\Omega\times u)=(u\cdot u)\Omega-(u\cdot\Omega)u.
$$
Both <dot products> are invariant under an orthogonal change of axes, while $u'=Ru$ and $\Omega'=R\Omega$. Therefore
$$
W'=(u\cdot u)R\Omega-(u\cdot\Omega)Ru=RW.
$$
Hence **$W$ is a vector**. This argument also avoids the sign issue for an individual <cross product> under an orientation-reversing orthogonal transformation: the double product has two such factors, whose signs cancel.

= Solution

Take the centre of the ball as the origin. An orthogonal change of variables leaves both this domain and its volume measure invariant. Consequently its Cartesian moment integrals are <isotropic tensors>. Equivalently, inversion $x\mapsto-x$ immediately makes every odd moment zero.

There is no nonzero invariant rank-one <vector>, so
$$
\boxed{\int_Vx_i\,dV=0.}
$$
An <isotropic tensor> of rank two is a scalar multiple of the <Kronecker delta>, giving $\int_Vx_ix_j\,dV=\alpha\delta_{ij}$. Contract the indices and use <spherical coordinates>:
$$
3\alpha=\int_Vr^2\,dV=4\pi\int_0^a r^4\,dr=\frac{4\pi a^5}{5}.
$$
Thus the <Cartesian moments of a three-dimensional ball> give
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac{4\pi a^5}{15},\qquad\int_Vx_ix_j\,dV=\frac{4\pi a^5}{15}\delta_{ij},\qquad\int_Vx_ix_jx_k\,dV=0.}
$$
For the last identity, one may also use the general rotation-invariant rank-three tensor $c\epsilon_{ijk}$: the moment is fully symmetric in its indices, whereas the <Levi-Civita symbol> is antisymmetric, forcing $c=0$.

The origin convention is necessary. For a ball centred at a nonzero vector $c$, its first moment would instead be $(4\pi a^3/3)c_i$, so the displayed claims implicitly refer to coordinates measured from its centre.

Use the <vector triple product> expression from part (a). Put $s=\Omega\cdot x$ and $r^2=x\cdot x$. Since $u=\Omega+\beta x$,
$$
u\cdot u=\Omega^2+2\beta s+\beta^2r^2,\qquad u\cdot\Omega=\Omega^2+\beta s.

$$
\boxed{W(x)=\beta\big[(\Omega\cdot x)\Omega-\Omega^2x\big]+\beta^2\big[r^2\Omega-(\Omega\cdot x)x\big].}

The first bracket is odd in $x$ and integrates to zero over the centred ball. With $\alpha=4\pi a^5/15$, the <Cartesian moments of a three-dimensional ball> give
$$
\int_Vr^2\,dV=3\alpha,\qquad\int_V(\Omega\cdot x)x_i\,dV=\Omega_j\int_Vx_jx_i\,dV=\alpha\Omega_i.

Therefore
$$
\boxed{\int_VW_i\,dV=2\alpha\beta^2\Omega_i=\frac{8\pi a^5\beta^2}{15}\Omega_i.}

The resulting <vector> is parallel to $\Omega$, as the rotational symmetry of the centred domain requires.

= Solution

Use displacements $x_i$ from an <equilibrium>, so the two spring extensions are $x_2-x_1$ and $x_3-x_2$. The <kinetic energy> and <elastic potential energy> are
$$
\boxed{T=\frac m2(\dot x_1^2+\mu\dot x_2^2+\dot x_3^2),\qquad V=\frac k2\big[(x_2-x_1)^2+(x_3-x_2)^2\big].}
$$
Thus $T=\dot x^TM\dot x/2$ and $V=x^TKx/2$, with the <small-oscillation mass and stiffness matrices>
$$
\boxed{M=m\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&\mu&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad K=k\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0\\-1&2&-1\\0&-1&1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
If $x_i$ instead denote absolute positions, the appropriate natural spring lengths must first be subtracted from the differences in $V$. They do not affect the equations for displacements.

<Euler-Lagrange equations> give $M\ddot x+Kx=0$, so a <normal mode> $x=ve^{i\omega t}$ obeys the <generalized eigenvalue problem for small oscillations> $Kv=\omega^2Mv$. Reflection symmetry separates antisymmetric shapes $(A,0,-A)^T$ from symmetric shapes $(A,B,A)^T$. In the antisymmetric sector the end equation is $kA=m\omega^2A$, giving $\omega^2=k/m$. In the symmetric sector the two independent equations are
$$
k(A-B)=m\omega^2A,\qquad2k(B-A)=\mu m\omega^2B.
$$
Twice the first plus the second gives $m\omega^2(2A+\mu B)=0$. If $\omega=0$, then $B=A$. Otherwise $B=-2A/\mu$, and substitution into the first equation gives $\omega^2=(k/m)(1+2/\mu)$. Thus the three independent shapes are:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
&v&\omega^2\\\hline
0&(1,1,1)^T&0\\
1&(1,0,-1)^T&k/m\\
2&(1,-2/\mu,1)^T&(k/m)(1+2/\mu)
\end{array}.
$$
For $v_0$ both springs retain their lengths, so this is uniform translation. For $v_1$ the end masses move oppositely and their forces on the middle mass cancel. For $v_2$ the end masses move together, while the middle mass moves oppositely so the <centre of mass> remains fixed. Direct substitution verifies all three eigenpairs; for positive $m,k,\mu$ the three <eigenvalues> are distinct and exhaust the three degrees of freedom. These are the <normal modes of three masses connected by two equal springs>.

A useful normalization is the <weighted inner product> induced by $M$. Since the squared mass norms are $m(\mu+2)$, $2m$ and $m(2+4/\mu)$, respectively, take
$$
\boxed{q_0=\frac{v_0}{\sqrt{m(\mu+2)}},\quad q_1=\frac{v_1}{\sqrt{2m}},\quad q_2=\frac{v_2}{\sqrt{m(2+4/\mu)}},\qquad q_i^TMq_j=\delta_{ij}.}
$$
If ordinary Euclidean unit <eigenvectors> are intended instead, divide $v_0,v_1,v_2$ by $\sqrt3,\sqrt2,\sqrt{2+4/\mu^2}$, respectively. The frequencies and physical motions are independent of this normalization choice. The zero-frequency coordinate has general motion $A+Bt$ rather than an oscillation.
