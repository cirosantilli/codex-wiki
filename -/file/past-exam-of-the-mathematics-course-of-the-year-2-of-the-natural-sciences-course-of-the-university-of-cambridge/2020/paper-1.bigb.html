= Solution

Write the position <vector> as $\mathbf r(\rho,\phi,z)=(\rho\cos\phi,\rho\sin\phi,z)$. Its coordinate tangent <vectors> are
$$
\mathbf r_\rho=(\cos\phi,\sin\phi,0),\qquad
\mathbf r_\phi=(-\rho\sin\phi,\rho\cos\phi,0),\qquad
\mathbf r_z=(0,0,1).
$$
Their pairwise <dot products> vanish. Their lengths, the <scale factors of orthogonal coordinates>, are
$$
\boxed{h_\rho=1,\qquad h_\phi=\rho,\qquad h_z=1.}
$$
Thus the <cylindrical coordinate system> is orthogonal wherever it is a nonsingular coordinate chart. At $\rho=0$, the angular tangent <vector> vanishes and the azimuth is undefined; this is the usual <coordinate singularity> on the axis, not a failure of the <Euclidean geometry>.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Dividing the coordinate tangents by their <scale factors of orthogonal coordinates> gives an <orthonormal basis>
$$
\mathbf e_\rho=(\cos\phi,\sin\phi,0),\quad
\mathbf e_\phi=(-\sin\phi,\cos\phi,0),\quad
\mathbf e_z=(0,0,1).
$$
Evaluate the Cartesian <cross products> componentwise:
$$
\mathbf e_\rho\times\mathbf e_\phi=(0,0,\cos^2\phi+\sin^2\phi)=\mathbf e_z,
$$
$$
\mathbf e_\phi\times\mathbf e_z=(\cos\phi,\sin\phi,0)=\mathbf e_\rho,
\qquad
\mathbf e_z\times\mathbf e_\rho=(-\sin\phi,\cos\phi,0)=\mathbf e_\phi.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mathbf e_\rho\times\mathbf e_\phi=\mathbf e_z,\quad
\mathbf e_\phi\times\mathbf e_z=\mathbf e_\rho,\quad
\mathbf e_z\times\mathbf e_\rho=\mathbf e_\phi.}
$$
The ordered cylindrical <basis> is right-handed: its <scalar triple product> $\mathbf e_\rho\cdot(\mathbf e_\phi\times\mathbf e_z)$ equals $+1$.

= Solution

Differentiate the position <vector>, using the angular factor from the <scale factors of orthogonal coordinates>:
$$
\boxed{d\mathbf r=\mathbf e_\rho\,d\rho+\rho\mathbf e_\phi\,d\phi+\mathbf e_z\,dz.}
$$
The <basis> is an <orthonormal basis>, so all cross terms in its squared length vanish. The Euclidean <line element> is therefore
$$
\boxed{|d\mathbf r|^2=d\rho^2+\rho^2d\phi^2+dz^2.}
$$
In particular, an angular change $d\phi$ at fixed radius traverses a physical distance $\rho\,|d\phi|$.

For a finite separation use the Cartesian distance, rather than substituting finite coordinate differences into the local <line element>. Expanding the two horizontal components gives
$$
(\rho\cos\phi-\rho'\cos\phi')^2+(\rho\sin\phi-\rho'\sin\phi')^2
=\rho^2+\rho'^2-2\rho\rho'\cos(\phi-\phi').
$$
The vertical contribution is $(z-z')^2$, independently. Thus
$$
\boxed{h=\rho^2+\rho'^2-2\rho\rho'\cos(\phi-\phi'),\qquad j=(z-z')^2,}
$$
and
$$
\boxed{R=\sqrt{\rho^2+\rho'^2-2\rho\rho'\cos(\phi-\phi')+(z-z')^2}.}
$$
Equivalently, the horizontal contribution is $(\rho-\rho')^2+4\rho\rho'\sin^2[(\phi-\phi')/2]$, which also makes its nonnegativity explicit.

The radial <unit vector> in the <spherical coordinate system> is
$$
\mathbf e_r=\frac{\mathbf r}{r}=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta).
$$
The coordinate tangent in the polar direction has length $r$, so its corresponding <unit vector> is
$$
\mathbf e_\theta=\frac1r\frac{\partial\mathbf r}{\partial\theta}
=(\cos\theta\cos\phi,\cos\theta\sin\phi,-\sin\theta).
$$
Differentiating $\mathbf e_r$ at fixed $\phi$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial\mathbf e_r}{\partial\theta}=\mathbf e_\theta,\qquad\alpha=1.}
$$
The factor is not $r$: the corresponding <scale factors of orthogonal coordinates> occur in the <derivative> of the position <vector>, whereas the question differentiates its unit direction. The formula is understood away from the <coordinate singularities> where a complete spherical <basis> is not uniquely specified.

Differentiate the <power series> term by term inside its <radius of convergence>, and reindex the second <derivative> so that every coefficient multiplies $x^m$. The <differential equation> becomes
$$
\sum_{m=0}^\infty\left[(m+2)(m+1)a_{m+2}+
\{n(n+1)-m(m+1)\}a_m\right]x^m=0.
$$
The uniqueness of power-series coefficients gives
$$
\boxed{a_{m+2}=\frac{m(m+1)-n(n+1)}{(m+1)(m+2)}a_m
=\frac{(m-n)(m+n+1)}{(m+1)(m+2)}a_m,\quad m\geq0.}
$$
The even coefficients depend only on $a_0$ and the odd coefficients only on $a_1$. These are the two local solution branches of the <Legendre differential equation> around its <ordinary point> $x=0$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For an <integer> $n\geq0$, choose the <series> whose <integer parity> agrees with $n$ and set the other initial coefficient to zero. At $m=n$, the numerator in the recurrence vanishes, so $a_{n+2}=0$ and every later coefficient in that <integer parity> vanishes. With a nonzero coefficient of $x^n$, this gives a <polynomial> of degree exactly $n$. Normalize it by $P_n(1)=1$.

For $n=0$, the even branch is constant; for $n=1$, the odd branch is proportional to $x$. For $n=2$, the recurrence gives $a_2=-3a_0$, so normalization fixes $a_0=-1/2$. Hence
$$
\boxed{P_0(x)=1,\qquad P_1(x)=x,\qquad P_2(x)=\frac12(3x^2-1).}
$$
The opposite-parity branch generally does not terminate; <polynomial> termination does not mean that both independent solutions become <polynomials>.

Substitute $U=R(r)F(\theta)$ into the axisymmetric <Laplace equation> and multiply by $r^2/(RF)$. The radial and angular terms must be separate constants:
$$
\frac1R\frac d{dr}(r^2R')=-\frac1{F\sin\theta}\frac d{d\theta}(\sin\theta F')=\lambda.
$$
Thus
$$
r^2R''+2rR'-\lambda R=0,\qquad
\frac1{\sin\theta}(\sin\theta F')'+\lambda F=0.
$$
With $x=\cos\theta$, the angular equation becomes
$$
(1-x^2)F_{xx}-2xF_x+\lambda F=0.
$$
Requiring boundedness on the entire polar interval, and using the supplied characterization of bounded <Legendre polynomial> solutions, gives $\lambda=n(n+1)$ and $F\propto P_n(\cos\theta)$ for $n=0,1,2,\ldots$. Trying $R=r^s$ gives $s(s+1)=n(n+1)$, hence $s=n$ or $s=-n-1$.

The globally angular-regular separated modes and their superposition are therefore
$$
\boxed{U_n=(A_nr^n+B_nr^{-n-1})P_n(\cos\theta),}
$$
$$
\boxed{U(r,\theta)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty(A_nr^n+B_nr^{-n-1})P_n(\cos\theta).}
$$
Coefficients and convergence are determined by the radial domain and boundary data. A domain containing the origin excludes negative radial powers for a regular solution. If the angular domain excludes a polar axis of the <spherical coordinate system> and singular angular behavior is allowed, additional Legendre-function branches are possible; the displayed family is the one regular over the complete sphere, as intended by the hint.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The far field is proportional to $rP_1(\cos\theta)$, since $P_1(\cos\theta)=\cos\theta$. After matching the growing terms, write the exterior solution as
$$
U=v_0rP_1(\cos\theta)+\sum_{n=0}^\infty B_nr^{-n-1}P_n(\cos\theta).
$$
An added constant has here been excluded by interpreting the far-field condition as $U-v_0r\cos\theta\to0$. Differentiate and impose the radial <Neumann boundary condition>:
$$
0=v_0P_1(\cos\theta)-\sum_{n=0}^\infty(n+1)B_nr_0^{-n-2}P_n(\cos\theta).
$$
The <orthogonality of Legendre polynomials> makes each coefficient vanish separately. Thus $B_n=0$ for $n\ne1$, and $2B_1/r_0^3=v_0$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{U(r,\theta)=v_0\left(r+\frac{r_0^3}{2r^2}\right)\cos\theta,\qquad r\geq r_0.}
$$
Indeed $\partial_rU=v_0(1-r_0^3/r^3)\cos\theta$, which vanishes at the boundary, while the correction decays at infinity. If the far-field condition is intended to specify only the leading growing term, an arbitrary additive constant is also allowed; its gradient has no effect on the <Neumann boundary condition>.

Direct <matrix multiplication> gives
$$
\tau_1^2=\tau_2^2=\tau_3^2=I_2.
$$
For example, the diagonal entries of $\tau_2^2$ are $(-i)i=i(-i)=1$, and its off-diagonal entries vanish. Associativity now reduces every positive <integer> power to its <integer parity>:
$$
\boxed{\tau_j^{2m}=I_2,\qquad\tau_j^{2m+1}=\tau_j.}
$$
These are the standard <Pauli matrices>, each a <Hermitian matrix> and an <involution>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Separate the defining <matrix exponential> into even and odd powers and use $\tau_j^2=I_2$:
$$
e^{i\alpha_j\tau_j}=I_2\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^m\alpha_j^{2m}}{(2m)!}
+i\tau_j\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^m\alpha_j^{2m+1}}{(2m+1)!}
=I_2\cos\alpha_j+i\tau_j\sin\alpha_j.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{A_1=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha_1&i\sin\alpha_1\\i\sin\alpha_1&\cos\alpha_1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
A_2=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha_2&\sin\alpha_2\\-\sin\alpha_2&\cos\alpha_2\end{pmatrix},}
$$
$$
\boxed{A_3=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\alpha_3}&0\\0&e^{-i\alpha_3}\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Since each $\tau_j$ is <Hermitian>, $A_j^\dagger=\cos\alpha_j I_2-i\sin\alpha_j\tau_j=A_j^{-1}$; each is a <unitary matrix>.

For $A_1$, the <determinant> is $\cos^2\alpha_1-(i\sin\alpha_1)^2=1$. For $A_2$ it is $\cos^2\alpha_2+\sin^2\alpha_2=1$, and for $A_3$ it is $e^{i\alpha_3}e^{-i\alpha_3}=1$. Multiplicativity of the <determinant> gives
$$
\boxed{\det A_1=\det A_2=\det A_3=1,\qquad\det(A_1A_2A_3)=1.}
$$
Together with <unitarity>, this places the <matrices> and their product in <SU(2)>.

M=\begin{pmatrix}ix_3&ix_1+x_2\\ix_1-x_2&-ix_3\end{pmatrix}.

Its <determinant> is

The <conjugation> therefore preserves the squared Euclidean length encoded in $M$.

Set $c=\cos(2\alpha_1)$ and $s=\sin(2\alpha_1)$. Multiplying the <matrices>, or using the <Pauli matrix multiplication law>, gives
$$
A_1^\dagger\tau_1A_1=\tau_1,\qquad
A_1^\dagger\tau_2A_1=c\tau_2+s\tau_3,\qquad
A_1^\dagger\tau_3A_1=c\tau_3-s\tau_2.
$$
Collecting the coefficients of $i\tau_j$ in $M'$ yields
$$
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}x_1'\\x_2'\\x_3'\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&c&-s\\0&s&c\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\x_3\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
This is the right-handed active <rotation> by $2\alpha_1$ about the positive $x_1$ axis. Likewise, with $c_j=\cos(2\alpha_j)$ and $s_j=\sin(2\alpha_j)$,
$$
\boxed{A_2:\ (x_1,x_2,x_3)\mapsto(c_2x_1+s_2x_3,\ x_2,\ -s_2x_1+c_2x_3),}
$$
$$
\boxed{A_3:\ (x_1,x_2,x_3)\mapsto(c_3x_1-s_3x_2,\ s_3x_1+c_3x_2,\ x_3).}
$$
Thus the other axes also have <rotation> angle $2\alpha_j$. The factor of two is essential: replacing $A_j$ by $-A_j$ leaves the <conjugation> unchanged, illustrating the two-to-one relation between <SU(2)> and three-dimensional <rotations>.

Divide by the leading coefficient for $x\ne0,1$:
$$
y''+P(x)y'+Q(x)y=0,\qquad P(x)=\frac1x,\quad Q(x)=\frac1{x(1-x)}.
$$
The <pole> of $P$ makes $x=0$ a <singular point of a differential equation>. However,
$$
xP(x)=1,\qquad x^2Q(x)=\frac{x}{1-x}
$$
are <real analytic> at zero. Therefore
$$
\boxed{x=0\text{ is a regular singular point}.}
$$
The ordinary-point power-series theorem does not apply there, but the <Frobenius method> does.

For a Frobenius exponent $r$, put $p_0=\lim_{x\to0}xP(x)=1$ and $q_0=\lim_{x\to0}x^2Q(x)=0$. The <indicial equation> is
$$
r(r-1)+p_0r+q_0=r^2=0,
$$
so
$$
\boxed{r_1=r_2=0.}
$$
A fundamental pair has the repeated-root Frobenius form
$$
y_1=\sum_{n=0}^\infty u_nx^n,\qquad
y_2=y_1\ln x+\sum_{n=0}^\infty v_nx^n,
\qquad y=C_1y_1+C_2y_2
$$
on a positive-$x$ interval. In this case a <real analytic> solution is particularly simple: $y_1=1-x$ satisfies the equation exactly. A convenient logarithmic companion, obtained below, is $y_2=x+(1-x)\ln[x/(1-x)]$ for $0<x<1$. It is independent of $y_1$ because of its logarithmic divergence at zero.

Let $A(x)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_nx^n$ denote the analytic part of the prescribed ansatz. Its logarithmic part has <derivatives>
$$
\frac d{dx}[(b+cx)\ln x]=c\ln x+\frac bx+c,\qquad
\frac{d^2}{dx^2}[(b+cx)\ln x]=\frac cx-\frac b{x^2}.
$$
After substitution, the full <differential equation> reduces to
$$
x(1-x)A''+(1-x)A'+A+2c(1-x)+(b+c)\ln x=0.
$$
The first terms are <real analytic> at zero and cannot cancel a nonzero <logarithm>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{c=-b.}
$$
The cancellation of the apparent $1/x$ terms is already built into this calculation.

With $c=-b$, equate coefficients in
$$
x(1-x)A''+(1-x)A'+A+2c(1-x)=0.
$$
The constant term is $a_1+2c=0$, and the coefficient of $x$ is $4a_2-2c=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{a_1=2b,\qquad a_2=-\frac b2.}
$$
For the coefficient of $x^{n-1}$ with $n\geq3$, the first two positive <derivative> terms contribute $n^2a_n$, while the remaining analytic terms contribute $[1-(n-1)^2]a_{n-1}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{a_n=\frac{n-2}{n}a_{n-1},\quad n\geq3.}
$$
Iteration gives $a_n=-b/[n(n-1)]$ for $n\geq2$. Using
$$
\sum_{n=2}^\infty\frac{x^n}{n(n-1)}=x+(1-x)\ln(1-x),\qquad |x|<1,
$$
the prescribed solution sums to
$$
\boxed{y=b\left[x+(1-x)\ln\frac{x}{1-x}\right].}
$$
The ansatz starts its analytic <series> at $n=1$, so it omits the freedom to add an arbitrary multiple of $1-x$. The complete two-constant real solution on $0<x<1$ is therefore
$$
\boxed{y=C(1-x)+b\left[x+(1-x)\ln\frac{x}{1-x}\right].}
$$
The narrower ansatz in (c) is a convenient choice of the logarithmic solution, not the full <general solution> by itself.

= Solution

The endpoint constraints are $y(-a)=y(a)=0$. The constants in the three costs contribute only $2aK$, $2aK'$ and $2aK''$ respectively, so they do not affect the minimizing shapes. For companies B and C, the fixed <arc length> is
$$
\mathcal L[y]=\int_{-a}^a\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx=L.
$$
The original PDF has $(y')^2$ as company A's first variable term. The local TeX transcription has lost that <derivative> dot and incorrectly repeats $y^2$; the calculation below follows the PDF.

**Company A.** The relevant <functional> is $J_A=\int(y'^2+y^2-2xy)\,dx$. The <Euler-Lagrange equation> gives
$$
2(y-x)-2y''=0,\qquad y''-y=-x.
$$
Its <general solution> is $y=x+c_1\cosh x+c_2\sinh x$. Adding and subtracting the endpoint equations gives $c_1=0$ and $c_2=-a/\sinh a$. Thus
$$
\boxed{y_A(x)=x-a\frac{\sinh x}{\sinh a}.}
$$
This <curve> is odd, negative on the left half and positive on the right half; company A has no lower-half-plane restriction. It is the unique global minimum. Indeed, for any endpoint-zero <variation> $\eta$, <integration by parts> and the <differential equation> cancel all linear terms, leaving
$$
J_A[y_A+\eta]-J_A[y_A]=\int_{-a}^a(\eta'^2+\eta^2)\,dx>0
$$
unless $\eta=0$.

**Company B.** Minimize $\int y\,dx$ under the length constraint. Introduce a constant <Lagrange multiplier> $\lambda$ and apply the <Euler-Lagrange equation> to $y+\lambda\sqrt{1+y'^2}$:
$$
1-\lambda\frac{y''}{(1+y'^2)^{3/2}}=0.
$$
The <curvature> is constant, so the required lower arc is a <circular arc>. Writing its radius as $R\geq a$ and imposing the endpoints gives
$$
\boxed{y_B(x)=\sqrt{R^2-a^2}-\sqrt{R^2-x^2}.}
$$
Since $\sqrt{1+y_B'^2}=R/\sqrt{R^2-x^2}$, its length determines the radius:
$$
\boxed{L=2R\arcsin(a/R).}
$$
Equivalently, introduce $0<\theta\leq\pi/2$ with $a=R\sin\theta$. Then $L/(2a)=\theta/\sin\theta$, which increases strictly from $1$ to $\pi/2$. The stipulated range of $L$ therefore selects one minor arc. At $L=\pi a$ it becomes the lower <semicircle>, with vertical endpoint tangents; it is still a single-valued finite-length graph.

Global minimality follows from the <convexity> of the augmented slope cost with $\lambda=R$. For a competitor $y=y_B+\eta$,
$$
R\left(\sqrt{1+y'^2}-\sqrt{1+y_B'^2}\right)
\geq\frac{Ry_B'}{\sqrt{1+y_B'^2}}\eta'=x\eta'.
$$
Consequently the augmented cost difference is at least $\int(\eta+x\eta')dx=[x\eta]_{-a}^a=0$. Competitors have the same length, so the multiplier contribution cancels and the original cost is minimized. This proves that a <circular arc maximizes area below a fixed chord>: minimizing negative signed height maximizes the area between the fence and the chord.

**Company C.** The variable cost is $J_C=\int y\,ds=\int y\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx$. The augmented integrand is $(y+\lambda)\sqrt{1+y'^2}$. Since it has no explicit $x$ dependence, the <Beltrami identity> gives
$$
\frac{y+\lambda}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}=b.
$$
The hanging branch with $b>0$ integrates to $y+\lambda=b\cosh[(x-x_0)/b]$. Equal endpoint heights <force> $x_0=0$, and the endpoint value fixes $\lambda=b\cosh(a/b)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{y_C(x)=b\left[\cosh(x/b)-\cosh(a/b)\right],\qquad b>0.}
$$
Its length is
$$
\boxed{L=\int_{-a}^a\cosh(x/b)\,dx=2b\sinh(a/b).}
$$
Writing $q=a/b$, the equation $L/(2a)=\sinh q/q$ has one positive solution for every $L>2a$: its <derivative> has numerator $q\cosh q-\sinh q>0$. The <curve> is a <catenary>, is even, and lies strictly below the chord except at the endpoints.

A direct argument proves this is a global minimum, rather than merely an extremum of an augmented <functional>. Parametrize any competitor by <arc length> $s\in[-L/2,L/2]$, from its left endpoint to its right endpoint. Then $x'^2+y'^2=1$, $\int x' ds=2a$, and <integration by parts> gives
$$
J_C=-\int sy'\,ds=2ab-\int(bx'+sy')\,ds
\geq2ab-\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}\sqrt{b^2+s^2}\,ds.
$$
The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives the last step. Equality requires $x'=b/\sqrt{b^2+s^2}$ and $y'=s/\sqrt{b^2+s^2}$, whose <integrals> are precisely the displayed <catenary> with the stated length. Thus it attains a lower bound valid for every competitor. This proves that a <catenary minimizes the integral of height along a fixed-length curve>.

When $r=p'$, write the differential expression in divergence form,
$$
\widetilde L y=(py')'+qy.
$$
Two integrations by parts give the <Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator>:
$$
\langle f,\widetilde Lg\rangle-\langle\widetilde Lf,g\rangle
=\left[p(fg'-f'g)\right]_a^b.
$$
The $q$ terms cancel because $q$ is real. Hence the exact boundary criterion, in the sense of <self-adjointness> defined in the question, is
$$
\boxed{\left[p(fg'-f'g)\right]_a^b=0\quad\text{for every admissible pair }f,g.}
$$
At regular endpoints, all separated homogeneous conditions of the following type satisfy it:
$$
\alpha_a y(a)+\beta_a p(a)y'(a)=0,\qquad
\alpha_b y(b)+\beta_b p(b)y'(b)=0,
$$
with real coefficients, not both zero at either endpoint. The allowed value-and-flux pairs at an endpoint are proportional, so their <determinant> $p(fg'-f'g)$ vanishes. This includes <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, <Neumann boundary conditions>, <Robin boundary conditions> and arbitrary mixtures of these at the two ends.

Coupled conditions can also work: periodic conditions $y(b)=y(a)$ and $p(b)y'(b)=p(a)y'(a)$ make the two endpoint expressions equal; simultaneous minus signs give the antiperiodic case. Thus requiring each endpoint term separately to vanish is sufficient, but not necessary.

More generally, for regular endpoints set $Y=(y(a),y(b))^{\mathsf T}$ and $N=(-p(a)y'(a),p(b)y'(b))^{\mathsf T}$. All maximal homogeneous self-adjoint <boundary conditions> can be written $AY+BN=0$ with real two-by-two <matrices> satisfying $\operatorname{rank}(A\ B)=2$ and $AB^{\mathsf T}=BA^{\mathsf T}$. If an endpoint is singular, the relevant limiting boundary form and admissible domain must instead be specified; the assumption $p>0$ only in the interior does not itself fix those endpoint limits.

$$

$$

A rigid <rotation> of a regular <tetrahedron> permutes its four vertices, giving an injective <group homomorphism> from its <rotation> <group> to $S_4$: fixing every vertex <forces> the <rotation> to be the identity. Its seven nontrivial <rotation> axes are four lines through a vertex and the opposite face centre, and three lines through midpoints of opposite edges.

Each vertex-face axis admits <rotations> by $\pm2\pi/3$, giving eight three-cycles on the vertices. Each opposite-edge axis admits a half-turn, giving three double <transpositions>. Along with the identity there are twelve <rotations>. All their induced <permutations> are even, so the injective image is a twelve-element <subgroup> of $A_4$, hence the whole <group>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{the tetrahedron rotation group is isomorphic to }A_4.}
$$
Its action on $\mathbb R^3$ supplies a three-dimensional <representation>. To prove irreducibility even after complexification, choose vertices
$$
(1,1,1),\quad(1,-1,-1),\quad(-1,1,-1),\quad(-1,-1,1).

The <group> contains the half-turns $D_x=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1)$ and their cyclic variants, as well as the coordinate cycle $(x,y,z)\mapsto(y,z,x)$.
