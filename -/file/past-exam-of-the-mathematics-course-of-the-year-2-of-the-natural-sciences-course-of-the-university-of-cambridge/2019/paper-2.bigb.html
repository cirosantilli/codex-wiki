A weighted <Sturm-Liouville operator> has the form
$$
\boxed{Ly=\frac1{w(x)}\left[-\frac{d}{dx}\left(p(x)\frac{dy}{dx}\right)+q(x)y\right]},
$$
where $p,q,w$ are real, $p>0$ and $w>0$ in the interval's interior, and the coefficients have the regularity required by the derivatives. The corresponding <eigenvalue equation> is $-(py')'+qy=\lambda wy$. Its natural <weighted inner product> is $\langle f,g\rangle_w=\int w f\bar g\,dx$. Suitable endpoint conditions must also annihilate $[p(f\bar g'-f'\bar g)]$ for a <self-adjoint differential operator>; the displayed differential expression alone does not specify those conditions.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Multiplication by a positive weight puts the <differential equation> into <Sturm-Liouville form> if $(xw)'=(1-x)w$. Thus $xw'=-xw$, and the constant factor can be chosen so that
$$
\boxed{w(x)=e^{-x}},\qquad -(xe^{-x}y')'=n e^{-x}y.
$$
This fixes the otherwise arbitrary normalization of the weight consistently with the requested unit norms.

Write the <polynomial> as $p_n(x)=\sum_{j=0}^n c_jx^j$. Comparing coefficients gives
$$
(j+1)^2c_{j+1}+(n-j)c_j=0,\qquad c_0=1.
$$
Consequently $c_j=(-1)^j\binom nj/j!$, identifying the solutions as <Laguerre polynomials>. In particular,
$$
\boxed{p_0=1,\qquad p_1=1-x,\qquad p_2=1-2x+\frac{x^2}{2}}.
$$
Repeated <integration by parts> gives the moments $\int_0^\infty e^{-x}x^j\,dx=j!$. Therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
\|p_0\|_w^2&=1,\\
\|p_1\|_w^2&=1-2(1!)+2!=1,\\
\|p_2\|_w^2&=1-4(1!)+5(2!)-2(3!)+\tfrac14(4!)=1.
\end{aligned}
$$
For later use, <integration by parts> in the weighted <eigenvalue equations> also gives
$$
(n-m)\langle p_n,p_m\rangle_w
=\left[xe^{-x}(p_np_m'-p_n'p_m)\right]_0^\infty=0.
$$
The endpoint expression vanishes because of the factor $x$ at zero and exponential decay at infinity. Hence distinct <Laguerre polynomials> are orthogonal, an instance of <orthogonality of Sturm-Liouville eigenfunctions>.

The optimal approximation is the <orthogonal projection> onto the <linear span> of $p_0,\ldots,p_N$. Their <orthogonality> gives
$$
\left\|f-\sum_{n=0}^N a_np_n\right\|_w^2
=\|f\|_w^2-2\sum_{n=0}^N a_n\langle f,p_n\rangle_w
+\sum_{n=0}^N a_n^2\|p_n\|_w^2.
$$
Completing each square proves both optimality and uniqueness:
$$
\boxed{a_n=\frac{\langle f,p_n\rangle_w}{\|p_n\|_w^2}}.
$$
For real $f$, these are real coefficients. If complex-valued $f$ is allowed while the coefficients remain real, replace the numerator by its real part and use the modulus-squared norm. The question's real formulation corresponds to real $f$.

Here the first three squared norms are one. Using $\int_0^\infty x^j e^{-2x}\,dx=j!/2^{j+1}$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
a_0&=\int_0^\infty e^{-2x}(1+x)\,dx=\frac12+\frac14=\frac34,\\
a_1&=\int_0^\infty e^{-2x}(1-x^2)\,dx=\frac12-\frac14=\frac14,\\
a_2&=\int_0^\infty e^{-2x}\left(1-x-\frac32x^2+\frac12x^3\right)dx
=\frac12-\frac14-\frac38+\frac3{16}=\frac1{16}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{(a_0,a_1,a_2)=\left(\frac34,\frac14,\frac1{16}\right),\qquad
f_2(x)=\frac{17}{16}-\frac38x+\frac1{32}x^2}.
$$

The coefficient formula from part (b) and the <Leibniz rule> yield <Rodrigues' formula> directly:
$$
p_n(x)=\sum_{j=0}^n\frac{(-1)^j}{j!}\binom njx^j
=\frac{e^x}{n!}\frac{d^n}{dx^n}(e^{-x}x^n).
$$
The leading coefficient is $(-1)^n/n!$, so $p_n^{(n)}=(-1)^n$. Substitute this differentiated expression into one factor in the squared <weighted norm> and apply <integration by parts> $n$ times:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\|p_n\|_w^2
&=\frac1{n!}\int_0^\infty p_n(x)\frac{d^n}{dx^n}(e^{-x}x^n)\,dx\\
&=\frac{(-1)^n}{n!}\int_0^\infty e^{-x}x^n p_n^{(n)}(x)\,dx
=\frac1{n!}\int_0^\infty e^{-x}x^n\,dx
=\boxed{1}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Every endpoint term contains a derivative of $e^{-x}x^n$ of order less than $n$: it vanishes at zero, and its exponential decay beats every <polynomial> at infinity. This proves the <normalization of the ordinary Laguerre polynomials> for all $n$, including $n=0$ without any integrations by parts.

For a separated <axisymmetric harmonic function> $\Psi=R(r)\Theta(\theta)$, multiplying the <Laplace equation> by $r^2/(R\Theta)$ gives
$$
\frac{(r^2R')'}{R}
=-\frac{(\sin\theta\,\Theta')'}{\sin\theta\,\Theta}=\lambda.
$$
The two sides depend on different variables, hence both equal a constant. Put $u=\cos\theta$ in the angular equation. Since $\sin\theta\,\Theta'=-\sin^2\theta\,d\Theta/du$, it becomes the <Legendre differential equation>
$$
\frac{d}{du}\left[(1-u^2)\frac{d\Theta}{du}\right]+\lambda\Theta=0.
$$
Regularity on both parts of the polar axis restricts $\lambda=\ell(\ell+1)$, with $\ell=0,1,\ldots$, and selects the <Legendre polynomial> $P_\ell(u)$. The other angular solution is singular at an endpoint.

The radial <Euler differential equation> is
$$
r^2R''+2rR'-\ell(\ell+1)R=0.
$$
Trying $R=r^s$ gives $s(s+1)=\ell(\ell+1)$, so $s=\ell$ or $s=-\ell-1$. <Superposition> of these separated <harmonic functions> gives
$$
\boxed{\Psi(r,\theta)=\sum_{\ell=0}^\infty
\left(A_\ell r^\ell+B_\ell r^{-\ell-1}\right)P_\ell(\cos\theta)}.
$$
The <Legendre polynomials> form the angular expansion for regular data with <rotational symmetry> about the polar axis; the radial domain and the <boundary conditions> determine which coefficients survive and where the expansion converges.

With $u=\cos\theta$, the boundary data have the finite <Legendre polynomial> expansion
$$
-2+2u+3u^2=-P_0(u)+2P_1(u)+2P_2(u).
$$
Inside the sphere, every $r^{-\ell-1}$ term is singular at the origin and must be absent. Matching at $r=a$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\Psi_{\mathrm{in}}(r,\theta)
=-1+2\frac ra\cos\theta+\frac{r^2}{a^2}(3\cos^2\theta-1)}.
$$
In the exterior, decay at infinity excludes all $r^\ell$ terms, including the constant term at $\ell=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\Psi_{\mathrm{out}}(r,\theta)
=-\frac ar+2\frac{a^2}{r^2}\cos\theta
+\frac{a^3}{r^3}(3\cos^2\theta-1)}.
$$
Both <harmonic functions> have the prescribed <Dirichlet boundary condition>, and each is finite throughout its own domain. The interior expression is regular even at the origin because $r\cos\theta=z$ and $r^2(3\cos^2\theta-1)=2z^2-x^2-y^2$. <Uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem> identifies these as the respective solutions. Their normal derivatives need not match: the two <Dirichlet problems> do not require a single <harmonic function> across the bounding sphere.

Use the sign convention in which a <fundamental solution of the Laplace equation> satisfies
$$
\Delta_{\mathbf r}G(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')
$$
in the sense of <distributions>. It is a <harmonic function> away from the source. Writing $\rho=|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|$, the radial equation is $(\rho G')'=0$, so $G=A\log\rho+B$. The <divergence theorem> on a small circle about the source fixes its total flux:
$$
1=\int_{\rho<\varepsilon}\Delta G\,d^2r
=\int_{\rho=\varepsilon}\partial_\rho G\,ds
=\frac A\varepsilon(2\pi\varepsilon)=2\pi A.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{G(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=\frac1{2\pi}\log|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|+B}.
$$
The arbitrary constant is a <harmonic function>. For the alternative convention $-\Delta G=\delta^{(2)}$, the logarithm has the opposite sign; subsequent <Green's function> formulas must use that convention consistently.

A <Dirichlet Green function> satisfies $\Delta_{\mathbf x}G_D(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)=\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)$ in the domain and $G_D=0$ when $\mathbf x$ lies on its boundary. For a source $\mathbf y\ne0$ in the <unit disc>, put its opposite image at $\mathbf y^*=\mathbf y/|\mathbf y|^2$. The <method of images> gives
$$
\boxed{G_D(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)
=\frac1{2\pi}\log\frac{|\mathbf x-\mathbf y|}{|\mathbf y|\,|\mathbf x-\mathbf y^*|}}.
$$
The image lies outside the <unit disc>, so its logarithm is a <harmonic function> inside. The original logarithm has precisely the unit source strength found in part (a). On $|\mathbf x|=1$,
$$
|\mathbf x-\mathbf y|^2
=1-2\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf y+|\mathbf y|^2
=|\mathbf y|^2|\mathbf x-\mathbf y^*|^2,
$$
so the boundary value is zero. Equivalently, in <complex coordinates>, the <Dirichlet Green function inside the unit disk> is
$$
G_D(z,\zeta)=\frac1{2\pi}\log\left|\frac{z-\zeta}{1-z\bar\zeta}\right|.
$$
This also covers a source at the origin, where $G_D(z,0)=(2\pi)^{-1}\log|z|$.

Fix the observation point $\mathbf x$ and apply <Green's second identity> in the source variable $\mathbf y$. Since $\Delta u=0$, $\Delta_{\mathbf y}G_D=\delta_{\mathbf x}$ and $G_D=0$ on the boundary, it gives
$$
u(\mathbf x)=\int_D(u\Delta G_D-G_D\Delta u)\,d^2y
=\int_{\partial D}f(\mathbf y)\partial_{n_{\mathbf y}}G_D(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\,ds_{\mathbf y}.
$$
The identity can be justified by deleting a small circle about $\mathbf x$ and letting its radius tend to zero; the logarithmic flux contributes $u(\mathbf x)$.

Set $\mathbf x=(r,\phi)$ and $\mathbf y=(\rho,\phi')$, and write $c=\cos(\phi-\phi')$. The symmetric expression for the <Green's function> is
$$
G_D=\frac1{4\pi}\log\frac{r^2+\rho^2-2r\rho c}{1+r^2\rho^2-2r\rho c}.
$$
At the boundary, the outward <normal derivative> is $\partial_\rho$ at $\rho=1$, and hence
$$
\left.\partial_\rho G_D\right|_{\rho=1}
=\frac1{4\pi}\frac{(2-2rc)-(2r^2-2rc)}{1+r^2-2rc}
=\frac1{2\pi}\frac{1-r^2}{1-2rc+r^2}.
$$
Since $ds=d\phi'$ on the <unit circle>, the <Poisson integral on the unit disk> follows, with <Poisson kernel>
$$
\boxed{P(r,\vartheta)=\frac{1-r^2}{1-2r\cos\vartheta+r^2}},\qquad
u(r,\phi)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(\phi')P(r,\phi-\phi')\,d\phi'.
$$

At the origin, the <Poisson kernel> is one, so the <mean value property for harmonic functions> gives
$$
u(0)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(\phi')\,d\phi'.
$$
For $0<r<1$, the denominator in the <Poisson kernel> satisfies $1-2r\cos\vartheta+r^2\geq(1-r)^2$. Thus
$$
0<P(r,\vartheta)\leq\frac{1-r^2}{(1-r)^2}=\frac{1+r}{1-r}.
$$
The nonnegativity of $f$ allows this upper bound to be inserted under the <Poisson integral on the unit disk>:
$$
\boxed{u(r,\phi)\leq\frac{1+r}{1-r}\,u(0)}.
$$
This is the upper bound in the <Harnack inequality on the unit disk>. The same argument with the minimum of the <Poisson kernel> gives the corresponding lower bound $(1-r)u(0)/(1+r)$.

For the simple-contour form of the <residue theorem>, let $C$ be a positively oriented, simple, closed, piecewise smooth <contour>. Assume $f$ is <holomorphic> on a neighbourhood of $C$ and its interior except at finitely many isolated singularities $z_1,\ldots,z_s$, all strictly inside $C$, with none on $C$. Then
$$
\oint_C f(z)\,dz=2\pi i\sum_{j=1}^s\operatorname{Res}_{z=z_j}f.
$$
The more general version weights each <residue> by the contour's <winding number>, with the usual requirement that the contour be homologous to zero in the domain of analyticity containing it and the nonsingular region.

For the requested application, assume $f$ is <holomorphic> on a neighbourhood of the contour and its closed interior, and $z_0$ is strictly inside it. Its <Taylor series> shows
$$
\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^2}
=\frac{f(z_0)}{(z-z_0)^2}+\frac{f'(z_0)}{z-z_0}+O(1).
$$
Only the second term contributes a <residue>. The <residue theorem> therefore proves the derivative form of the <Cauchy integral formula>:
$$
\boxed{\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^2}\,dz=f'(z_0)}.
$$

Write $a=s+ib$ with real $s,b$, and first translate the real integration variable by $s$. It remains to integrate $e^{-z^2}$ along the horizontal line $\operatorname{Im}z=b$.

$$
\int_{-R}^{R}e^{-x^2}\,dx-\int_{-R}^{R}e^{-(x+ib)^2}\,dx

Take $\lambda>0$ and $t>0$, the <positive diffusivity requirement for the forward heat kernel>. The PDF does not explicitly specify the sign of $\lambda$; the stated real <Gaussian function> formula requires this assumption. For a direct calculation, initially assume sufficiently smooth and rapidly decreasing data, so that the <Fourier transform>, its inverse and the following integrations are justified. The resulting <heat-kernel convolution> extends to wider classes of initial data.

Using the stated <Fourier transform> convention and <integration by parts>,
$$
\widetilde{u_{xx}}(k,t)=-k^2\widetilde u(k,t),\qquad
\partial_t\widetilde u=-\lambda k^2\widetilde u.
$$
The transformed <ordinary differential equation> has solution $\widetilde u(k,t)=e^{-\lambda k^2t}\widetilde u_0(k)$. <Fourier inversion> then gives
$$
u(x,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u_0(y)K_t(x-y)\,dy,\qquad
K_t(s)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda tk^2+iks}\,dk.
$$
Complete the square:
$$
-\lambda tk^2+iks
=-\lambda t\left(k-\frac{is}{2\lambda t}\right)^2-\frac{s^2}{4\lambda t}.
$$
Part (a), after scaling by $\sqrt{\lambda t}$, evaluates the remaining <Gaussian integral> even though its shift is imaginary. Therefore
$$
K_t(s)=\frac{e^{-s^2/(4\lambda t)}}{\sqrt{4\pi\lambda t}},\qquad
\boxed{u(x,t)=\frac1{\sqrt{4\pi\lambda t}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
e^{-(x-y)^2/(4\lambda t)}u(y,0)\,dy}.
$$
The <heat kernel> has integral one and is an <approximate identity> as $t\downarrow0$, which recovers the initial condition. Mere decay at spatial infinity does not by itself imply every Fourier integral is absolutely convergent; the regularity assumption above supplies a rigorous setting for the derivation.

Put $s=\lambda t>0$. In the <heat-kernel convolution>, complete the real square as
$$
\alpha^2y^2+\frac{(x-y)^2}{4s}
=\left(\alpha^2+\frac1{4s}\right)
\left(y-\frac{x}{1+4s\alpha^2}\right)^2
+\frac{\alpha^2x^2}{1+4s\alpha^2}.
$$
The <Gaussian integral> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\beta=\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1+4\lambda t\alpha^2}},\qquad
u(x,t)=\frac{\beta}{\sqrt\pi}e^{-\beta^2x^2}}.
$$
At $t=0$ the same formula gives $\beta=\alpha$. Equivalently, $\beta^{-2}=\alpha^{-2}+4\lambda t$: the <variance> of this normalized <Gaussian function> grows from $1/(2\alpha^2)$ to $1/(2\alpha^2)+2\lambda t$.

The initial profiles have unit integral and concentrate at zero as $\alpha\to\infty$. Precisely, for every smooth compactly supported <test function> $\varphi$,
$$
\int_{\mathbb R}\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt\pi}e^{-\alpha^2y^2}\varphi(y)\,dy
=\frac1{\sqrt\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-v^2}\varphi(v/\alpha)\,dv
\longrightarrow\varphi(0).
$$
Thus the limit is the <Dirac delta distribution>, rather than an ordinary function: pointwise the profiles tend to zero away from the origin and diverge at the origin. At every fixed $t>0$,
$$
\boxed{u(\cdot,0)\longrightarrow\delta_0\ \text{in distributions},\qquad
u(x,t)\longrightarrow\frac1{\sqrt{4\pi\lambda t}}e^{-x^2/(4\lambda t)}}.
$$
The limiting evolved profile is the <heat kernel> for a unit point source.

An order-$n$ Cartesian <tensor> has $3^n$ components whose values in two orthonormal Cartesian frames satisfy
$$
T'_{i_1\ldots i_n}=R_{i_1j_1}\cdots R_{i_nj_n}T_{j_1\ldots j_n},\qquad R^TR=I,
$$
with repeated indices summed as in <Einstein notation>. It is an <isotropic tensor> when its components are unchanged by every proper <rotation>, $R\in SO(3)$.

For rank two, a half-turn about any coordinate axis changes the sign of each component having exactly one index along that axis. Invariance therefore makes every off-diagonal component zero. Quarter-turns interchanging two coordinate axes then require $T_{11}=T_{22}=T_{33}$. Thus, without assuming symmetry in advance,
$$
\boxed{T_{ij}=a\delta_{ij}}.
$$
Conversely, the <Kronecker delta> is invariant because $R_{ik}R_{jk}=\delta_{ij}$, so every tensor of this form is isotropic.

For rank three the general proper-rotation invariant form is
$$
\boxed{T_{ijk}=b\epsilon_{ijk}},
$$
where $\epsilon_{ijk}$ is the <Levi-Civita symbol>. The convention matters: the <isotropic rank-three tensor and reflections> distinction says that invariance of a polar <tensor> under all $R\in O(3)$ would force $b=0$. A nonzero $\epsilon_{ijk}$ is invariant under proper <rotations>, and is a <pseudotensor> under the full orthogonal group. The shell integrals below are invariant under reflections as well, so their odd moments vanish.

For an ordinary conservative mechanical system, choose <generalized coordinates> $q_i$ measured from a static <equilibrium point>. Assume the <kinetic energy> is quadratic in velocities and there are no velocity-dependent forces. Expanding about equilibrium, the linear term in the <potential energy> vanishes. Retaining only terms of total degree two gives the <small oscillation> <Lagrangian>
$$
\boxed{L=\frac12\dot{\mathbf q}^{\,T}M\dot{\mathbf q}-\frac12\mathbf q^TK\mathbf q},\qquad
M_{ij}=\left.\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial\dot q_i\partial\dot q_j}\right|_0,\quad
K_{ij}=\left.\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial q_i\partial q_j}\right|_0.
$$
An irrelevant constant has been dropped. Coordinate dependence of $M$ contributes only terms of third or higher total degree. The matrices $M,K$ are symmetric, $M$ is positive definite for independent coordinates, and $K$ is positive semidefinite at a <stable equilibrium>. The <Euler-Lagrange equations> are
$$
\boxed{M\ddot{\mathbf q}+K\mathbf q=0}.
$$
A <normal mode> has a fixed <mode shape> $\mathbf v$ and one time factor: $\mathbf q=\mathbf v Q(t)$. Its <angular frequency> $\omega$ solves the <generalized eigenvalue problem> $K\mathbf v=\omega^2M\mathbf v$, with $\ddot Q+\omega^2Q=0$. Thus $\omega$ is the normal angular frequency, and the frequency in cycles per unit time is $\omega/(2\pi)$. A <zero mode> satisfies $K\mathbf v=0$; its time dependence is $Q=A+Bt$, describing an unopposed displacement or uniform drift rather than a restoring oscillation.

\end{array}}.

A <group> is a set $G$ equipped with a binary operation satisfying the <group axioms>: closure; <associativity>, $(gh)k=g(hk)$; an <identity element> $e$ with $eg=ge=g$; and an <inverse element> $g^{-1}$ for every $g$, with $gg^{-1}=g^{-1}g=e$.

A <subgroup> $H$ is a subset that is a <group> under the restricted operation. Equivalently, it is nonempty and contains $xy^{-1}$ whenever $x,y\in H$. A <generating set of a group> is a set whose elements and inverses can be multiplied in finite words to produce every group element.

The <group order> is its number of elements, $|G|$. The <order of a group element> $g$ is the least positive integer $r$ with $g^r=e$, or infinity if there is none. <Lagrange's theorem> states that for a finite <group> and <subgroup> $H$, $|H|$ divides $|G|$ and $|G|=[G:H]|H|$. In particular, every finite element order divides the <group order>.

A <cyclic group> is generated by one element, so every element is a power of that generator. A <group isomorphism> is a bijective <group homomorphism>: a bijection $\phi:G\to G'$ satisfying $\phi(gh)=\phi(g)\phi(h)$ for all $g,h$.

$$
These are possibilities across groups of order eight; they need not all occur in one group.

If every element has order at most two, every element equals its own inverse. Thus for any $x,y$,
$$
xy=(xy)^{-1}=y^{-1}x^{-1}=yx,
$$
so $G$ is an <abelian group>. Choose $a\ne e$, and then $b\notin\langle a\rangle$. The four elements $e,a,b,ab$ are distinct and form a <subgroup> $H$: commutativity and $a^2=b^2=e$ prove closure and inverses. Choose $c\notin H$. The elements of $H$ and $Hc$ are disjoint, since $hc\in H$ would imply $c\in H$. These two four-element sets therefore exhaust $G$.

Every element consequently has a unique expression $a^ib^jc^k$, with $i,j,k\in\{0,1\}$. The map from triples to these products is bijective and respects multiplication by addition of exponents modulo two. Hence
$$
\boxed{G\cong C_2\times C_2\times C_2},
$$
a <direct product of groups> and an <elementary abelian group>.

The <cyclic subgroup> $A=\langle a\rangle$ has order four and index two. Every index-two <subgroup> is normal: its complement is both the only nontrivial left <coset> and the only nontrivial right <coset>. Thus <conjugation> by $b$ is an <automorphism> of $A$. equivalently $ab=ba$ or $ab=ba^3$, as required. Also $b\notin A$, since otherwise $a,b$ could generate at most four elements.

In the commuting case, every word reduces uniquely to $a^jb^\varepsilon$ with $0\leq j<4$ and $\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}$. The eight expressions are distinct because $A$ and $Ab$ are disjoint. Multiplication is addition of the two exponents modulo four and two, respectively. Therefore
$$
\boxed{G\cong C_4\times C_2}.
$$
This gives the <multiplication table> independently of any other properties of $G$, proving uniqueness up to <group isomorphism>.

In the inversion case, the relation $ba=a^{-1}b$ gives the same eight unique normal forms, now with multiplication
$$
(a^ib^\varepsilon)(a^jb^\eta)
=a^{i+(-1)^\varepsilon j}b^{\varepsilon+\eta},
$$
with exponents reduced modulo four and two. Thus this case also has a uniquely determined <group> structure. A faithful geometric realization is the <dihedral group> of a square, with $a$ a quarter-turn and $b$ a reflection in a line through opposite edge midpoints. For example, on the plane,
$$
a=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
b=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
These preserve the square with vertices $(\pm1,\pm1)$ and satisfy $a^4=b^2=I$, $bab=a^{-1}$. They generate eight distinct transformations, so
$$
\boxed{G\cong D_4\quad\text{(the symmetry group of a square, of order eight)}}.
$$

The <symmetric group> $S_n$ consists of all bijections of $\{1,\ldots,n\}$, with composition as its operation; its <group order> is $n!$.

An $m$-dimensional complex <group representation> of $G$ is a <group homomorphism> $\rho:G\to GL(V)$, where $V$ is an $m$-dimensional complex <vector space>. After a choice of <basis>, each $\rho(g)$ is an invertible $m\times m$ complex <matrix>, with $\rho(gh)=\rho(g)\rho(h)$.

An <irreducible representation> has $V\ne0$ and no invariant <vector subspace> other than $\{0\}$ and $V$. Two <group representations> $\rho$ on $V$ and $\sigma$ on $W$ are <isomorphic representations> if there exists an invertible <linear map> $S:V\to W$ such that $S\rho(g)=\sigma(g)S$ for every $g$. They are inequivalent if no such map exists; in equal-dimensional <matrix> form this says no fixed change of <basis> conjugates every $\rho(g)$ to $\sigma(g)$.

For a finite <group> over $\mathbb C$, the number of inequivalent <irreducible representations> is its number of <conjugacy classes>, and the <sum of squares of irreducible representation dimensions> equals the <group order>. There are therefore five irreducibles for $S_4$, with squared dimensions summing to $24$.

First establish that part (c) found every <one-dimensional representation>. In such a representation, conjugate elements have the same scalar image. All <transpositions> are conjugate and square to the identity, so they all have one common image $s$ with $s^2=1$. <Transpositions> generate $S_4$, hence $s=1$ gives exactly the <trivial representation>, while $s=-1$ gives exactly the <sign representation>. The other three dimensions $d_1,d_2,d_3$ are integers at least two and satisfy
$$

\boxed{1,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 3}.
