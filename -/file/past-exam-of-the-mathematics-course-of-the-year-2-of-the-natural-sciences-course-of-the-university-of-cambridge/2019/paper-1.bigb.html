= Solution
{parent=a}

Use the <Levi-Civita symbol> and the <contraction of two Levi-Civita symbols> in <Cartesian coordinates>. With repeated indices summed,
$$
\begin{aligned}
[\nabla\times(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)]_i
&=\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{klm}\partial_j(A_lB_m)\\
&=(\delta_{il}\delta_{jm}-\delta_{im}\delta_{jl})\partial_j(A_lB_m)\\
&=\partial_j(A_iB_j)-\partial_j(A_jB_i)\\
&=B_j\partial_jA_i+A_i\partial_jB_j-B_i\partial_jA_j-A_j\partial_jB_i.
\end{aligned}
$$
The last line is the <product rule>. Reading its four terms in vector notation proves the <curl of a cross product> identity:
$$
\boxed{\nabla\times(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)=(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf A-\mathbf B(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)-(\mathbf A\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B+\mathbf A(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B).}
$$

= Solution
{parent=b}

For an oriented piecewise smooth surface $S$ with boundary curve $C=\partial S$, and a continuously differentiable vector field $\mathbf F$ near $S$, <Stokes theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r.}
$$
Here $\mathbf n$ is the chosen <unit normal>, $dS$ is positive surface area, and $d\mathbf r$ is the directed tangent <line element>. The positive traversal of $C$ is fixed by the right-hand rule relative to $\mathbf n$. Thus the <flux> of the <curl> through the surface equals the circulation along its oriented boundary.

Differentiate the position vector, keeping the other coordinates fixed in each <derivative>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf h_u=(a\sinh u\cos v,a\cosh u\sin v,0),\quad\mathbf h_v=(-a\cosh u\sin v,a\sinh u\cos v,0),\quad\mathbf h_z=(0,0,1).}
$$
The first two have zero <dot product>, since their two planar contributions cancel, and both are orthogonal to $\mathbf h_z$. Hence these are <orthogonal coordinates> away from coordinate degeneracies. Their scale factors, which are the lengths of these coordinate basis vectors, are
$$
\boxed{h_u=h_v=a\sqrt{\sinh^2u+\sin^2v},\qquad h_z=1.}
$$
For example $|\mathbf h_u|^2=a^2[\sinh^2u\cos^2v+\cosh^2u\sin^2v]=a^2(\sinh^2u+\sin^2v)$. The unit basis vectors are $\mathbf h_i/h_i$ when $h_i\ne0$; the given coordinate basis vectors themselves are not normalized. The <Jacobian determinant> is $a^2(\sinh^2u+\sin^2v)>0$ except on the focal lines. These are <elliptic cylindrical coordinates>.

For $u>0$, eliminating $v$ gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{x^2}{a^2\cosh^2u}+\frac{y^2}{a^2\sinh^2u}=1.}
$$
Thus constant $u$ surfaces are confocal <elliptic cylinders> parallel to the $z$ axis. The limiting $u=0$ surface is the vertical strip $y=0$, $|x|\leq a$.

For generic $v$, eliminating $u$ gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{x^2}{a^2\cos^2v}-\frac{y^2}{a^2\sin^2v}=1.}
$$
These are <hyperbolic cylinders> with the same focal lines. A specified angular value selects the half-branch with $\operatorname{sgn}x=\operatorname{sgn}(\cos v)$ and $\operatorname{sgn}y=\operatorname{sgn}(\sin v)$, because $u\geq0$. At $v=0,\pi$ the surfaces are the vertical half-planes $y=0$, $x\geq a$ or $x\leq-a$; at $v=\pi/2,3\pi/2$ they are $x=0$, $y\geq0$ or $y\leq0$.

Finally, constant $z$ gives horizontal planes. The three surface families express the <orthogonality> of <elliptic cylindrical coordinates>.

The ellipse bounding the surface is $u=1$. With the upward normal, its positively oriented boundary is traversed by increasing $v$ from zero to $2\pi$:
$$
\mathbf r(v)=(a\cosh1\cos v,a\sinh1\sin v,c),\qquad d\mathbf r=(-a\cosh1\sin v,a\sinh1\cos v,0)dv.
$$
The given components are Cartesian components, so their ordinary <dot product> with this tangent is
$$
\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=-2a\sinh1\cosh1(\sin^2v+\cos^2v)dv.
$$
Therefore <Stokes theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot d\mathbf S=-4\pi a\sinh1\cosh1=-2\pi a\sinh2.}
$$
A direct check uses $F_x=2y/a$ and $F_y=-2x/a$, hence $(\nabla\times\mathbf F)_z=-4/a$, and multiplies by the ellipse area $\pi a^2\sinh1\cosh1$. The third component cannot affect either calculation because the surface and its boundary lie at fixed $z$. Equivalently one may apply Stokes to the smooth planar field $(2y/a,-2x/a,0)$, avoiding any need to differentiate the elliptic-coordinate expression for the third component at its foci.

Write a nonzero separated solution as $T=X(x)Y(y)S(t)$. Division of the <heat equation> by this product gives
$$
\frac{S'}{\lambda S}=\frac{X''}{X}+\frac{Y''}{Y}.
$$
The two spatial factors solve independent constant-coefficient <eigenvalue> problems. Zero endpoint values in $x$ require
$$
X_n(x)=\sin(n\pi x/a),\qquad X_n''=-(n\pi/a)^2X_n,\quad n=1,2,\ldots.
$$
The insulating conditions require
$$
Y_m(y)=\cos(m\pi y/b),\qquad Y_m''=-(m\pi/b)^2Y_m,\quad m=0,1,2,\ldots.
$$
The zero cosine mode is the nonzero constant function and must be retained; there is no nonzero zero-index sine mode. The temporal equation is $S'=-\lambda\pi^2(n^2/a^2+m^2/b^2)S$. Superposing the complete sine-cosine basis yields
$$
\boxed{T(x,y,t)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\sum_{m=0}^\infty A_{nm}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}\cos\frac{m\pi y}{b}\exp\left[-\lambda\pi^2\left(\frac{n^2}{a^2}+\frac{m^2}{b^2}\right)t\right].}
$$
The constants are fixed by expansion of the initial profile. <Orthogonality> gives sine norm $a/2$; the cosine norm is $b$ at $m=0$ and $b/2$ otherwise. This is the <heat equation on a rectangle with two insulated sides>.

First decompose the initial $y$ dependence using $\sin^2(2\pi y/b)=[1-\cos(4\pi y/b)]/2$. Only the cosine indices $m=0$ and $m=4$ occur. Expand the remaining factor in a <Fourier sine series>:
$$
x(a-x)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a},\qquad b_n=\frac2a\int_0^ax(a-x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}\,dx.
$$
For $k_n=n\pi/a$, two <integrations by parts> give
$$
\int_0^ax(a-x)\sin(k_nx)dx=\frac{2[1-(-1)^n]}{k_n^3},\qquad b_n=\frac{4a^2[1-(-1)^n]}{n^3\pi^3}.
$$
Thus even $n$ vanish, while $b_n=8a^2/(n^3\pi^3)$ for odd $n$. The nonzero double-series coefficients are $A_{n0}=b_n/2$ and $A_{n4}=-b_n/2$. Their separate heat-mode decay factors give
$$
\boxed{T(x,y,t)=\frac{4a^2}{\pi^3}\sum_{\substack{n\geq1\\n\text{ odd}}}\frac{\sin(n\pi x/a)}{n^3}e^{-\lambda\pi^2n^2t/a^2}\left[1-e^{-16\lambda\pi^2t/b^2}\cos\frac{4\pi y}{b}\right].}
$$
At $t=0$ the bracket is $2\sin^2(2\pi y/b)$, which recovers the initial profile with the displayed sine coefficients. Each term satisfies the mixed <Dirichlet boundary conditions> and <Neumann boundary conditions>.

For each source point $z$, construct the <Green function> from homogeneous solutions on either side:
$$
G(x,z)=\begin{cases}A(z)y_a(x),&a\leq x<z,\\B(z)y_b(x),&z<x\leq b.\end{cases}
$$
The endpoint conditions are then automatic. The equation $L_xG=\delta(x-z)$ requires continuity of $G$ and a unit jump in its first <derivative>: a discontinuity in $G$ would introduce an unwanted delta <derivative>, while integrating across the source fixes $G_x(z^+,z)-G_x(z^-,z)=1$. Thus
$$
A y_a(z)=B y_b(z),\qquad B y_b'(z)-A y_a'(z)=1.
$$
Solving this two-by-two system, with the <Wronskian> $W=y_a y_b'-y_b y_a'\ne0$, gives
$$
\boxed{G(x,z)=\begin{cases}y_a(x)y_b(z)/W(z),&a\leq x\leq z,\\y_b(x)y_a(z)/W(z),&z\leq x\leq b.\end{cases}}
$$
This derivation also remains valid if one of the solution values at the matching point vanishes: only the <Wronskian> must be nonzero. For a regular nonsingular two-point problem it supplies the inverse kernel; at a singular endpoint, uniqueness requires separate checking, as part (d) illustrates.

Assume initially that $f,g$ are integrable, so <Fubini's theorem> justifies interchanging the integrals. Transform the <convolution> and put $u=x-z$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde h(k)&=\int_{\mathbb R}\!\int_{\mathbb R}f(z)g(x-z)e^{-ikx}\,dz\,dx\\
&=\int_{\mathbb R}f(z)e^{-ikz}\,dz\int_{\mathbb R}g(u)e^{-iku}\,du\\
&=\boxed{\widetilde f(k)\widetilde g(k)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
This is the <convolution theorem>. No factor $2\pi$ appears in this direction because the forward transform in the question is unnormalized.

The <adjoint operator> $A^\dagger$, when it exists, is defined by
$$
\boxed{\langle Ax,y\rangle=\langle x,A^\dagger y\rangle}
$$
for all vectors in the relevant domains. This convention takes the <inner product> to be conjugate-linear in its first argument. Nondegeneracy makes the adjoint unique.

For the standard complex <inner product> $\langle x,y\rangle=\overline{x}^{\,T}y$, the equality becomes $\overline{x}^{\,T}\overline{A}^{\,T}y=\overline{x}^{\,T}A^\dagger y$ for every $x,y$, so
$$
\boxed{A^\dagger=\overline A^{\,T}.}
$$
Thus the matrix adjoint is the <Hermitian conjugate>, not merely the <transpose> unless the entries are real. In a basis with positive Hermitian <Gram matrix> $G$, the same definition instead gives $A^\dagger_G=G^{-1}\overline A^{\,T}G$, making the dependence on the chosen <inner product> explicit.

= Solution

An <invertible matrix> is a square matrix for which there is a matrix $A^{-1}$ with $AA^{-1}=A^{-1}A=I$. Write its assumed <diagonalization> as $A=P\Lambda P^{-1}$, where $P$ is invertible and $\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)$. Then
$$
\det A=\det\Lambda=\prod_{j=1}^n\lambda_j.
$$
If the <determinant> is nonzero, every $\lambda_j$ is nonzero, so $\Lambda^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_j^{-1})$ exists and $A^{-1}=P\Lambda^{-1}P^{-1}$. If the <determinant> is zero, at least one <eigenvalue> is zero; its nonzero <eigenvector> is mapped to zero, which an invertible map cannot do. Hence
$$
\boxed{A\text{ is invertible}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\det A\ne0.}
$$
The equivalence holds even without diagonalizability, but the eigenbasis proves the stated case directly.

The entries of $M^TM$ are real, and the <transpose> rule gives $(M^TM)^T=M^TM$. Thus it is a real <symmetric matrix>, hence also a <Hermitian matrix>.

For any complex vector $v$,
$$
v^\dagger M^TMv=(Mv)^\dagger(Mv)=\|Mv\|^2\geq0.
$$
In particular, if $M^TMv=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$,
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\frac{\|Mv\|^2}{\|v\|^2}\geq0.}
$$
Thus $M^TM$ is <positive semidefinite>. Its zero <eigenspace> is precisely the kernel of $M$, since $\|Mv\|^2=0$ is equivalent to $Mv=0$. This is the Gram-matrix structure behind the nonnegative <eigenvalues>.

For the positive integer $k$ in the nilpotence condition, diagonalize $B=P\Lambda P^{-1}$. Then
$$
0=B^k=P\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1^k,\ldots,\lambda_n^k)P^{-1}.
$$
Every diagonal entry must vanish, so each $\lambda_j=0$. Consequently $\Lambda=0$ and $\boxed{B=0}$. This proves that <a diagonalizable nilpotent matrix is zero>.

Without diagonalizability a counterexample is
$$
\boxed{C=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\ne0,\qquad C^2=0.}
$$
Its only <eigenvalue> is zero but its kernel has dimension one, so it cannot have a basis of <eigenvectors>. It is the simplest nontrivial nilpotent <Jordan block>.

By the <spectral theorem>, a <Hermitian matrix> has real <eigenvalues> and an orthonormal eigenbasis. Choose column vectors $u_j$ with $Hu_j=\lambda_ju_j$ and $u_i^\dagger u_j=\delta_{ij}$, and form $U=(u_1\ \cdots\ u_n)$. Then $U^\dagger U=I$, so $U$ is unitary, and $HU=U\Lambda$ gives
$$
\boxed{U^\dagger HU=\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n).}
$$
The entries of $\Lambda$ are the real <eigenvalues>, repeated according to their multiplicities. Within a degenerate <eigenspace>, any <orthonormal basis> may be used. Equivalently $H=U\Lambda U^\dagger$.

A real <symmetric matrix> has a real orthonormal eigenbasis. Put those <eigenvectors> into an <orthogonal matrix> $Q$, so $Q^TAQ=\operatorname{diag}(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$. With the change of coordinates $x=Qx'$, or $x'=Q^Tx$,

\boxed{\sum_{ij}A_{ij}x_ix_j=x^TAx=x'^T(Q^TAQ)x'=\sum_i a_i(x_i')^2.}
$$
Thus the coefficients are the <eigenvalues> and the new coordinate directions are the <eigenvectors>. This <orthogonal diagonalization> preserves Euclidean lengths and angles while removing cross terms from the <quadratic form>.

For $f=u+iv$ analytic in $z=x+iy$, the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> are
$$
\boxed{u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x.}
$$
They follow by computing the same complex <derivative> along the real and imaginary coordinate directions.

{parent=7}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <Cauchy-Riemann equations> give
$$
\nabla u\cdot\nabla v=u_xv_x+u_yv_y=u_x(-u_y)+u_yu_x=0.
$$
At a regular point, each level curve has tangent perpendicular to its nonzero <gradient>. The two <gradients>, and hence the two tangents, are orthogonal. Therefore **regular level curves meet at right angles**.

The qualification is necessary: $|\nabla u|=|\nabla v|=|f'(z)|$, so at a critical point both <gradients> vanish and this argument gives no tangent direction. For example, for $f(z)=z^2$, the zero level curves of the real and imaginary parts are diagonals and coordinate axes; they meet at $45^\circ$ at zero. This is the regular-point statement of <orthogonal level curves of harmonic conjugates>.

Recognize that $z^2=(x^2-y^2)+2ixy$ and $e^{-z}=e^{-x}(\cos y-i\sin y)$. Their product has exactly the specified real part. Its imaginary part is
$$
v_0=e^{-x}[2xy\cos y-(x^2-y^2)\sin y].
$$
One can also recover it directly from the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>. Differentiating the specified real part gives
$$
u_x=e^{-x}[(2x-x^2+y^2)\cos y+2y(1-x)\sin y].
$$
Integration of $v_y=u_x$ gives $v=v_0+h(x)$; the other equation $v_x=-u_y$ then forces $h'(x)=0$. Thus the most general answer on a connected domain is
$$
\boxed{f(z)=z^2e^{-z}+iC,\qquad C\in\mathbb R.}
$$
Indeed the difference of any two <analytic functions> with the same real part has zero real part; its two <Cauchy-Riemann equations> force its imaginary part to be constant. This proves that no further analytic freedom is missing.

The denominator factors as $z^2+iz+6=(z-2i)(z+3i)$, with no cancellation against the numerator. Thus there are

The <complex exponential> never vanishes. The zeros of $\sinh z$ occur at $z=n\pi i$, $n\in\mathbb Z$, and they are simple because $\cosh(n\pi i)=(-1)^n\ne0$. The numerator is $e^{2n\pi i}=1$ there, so no cancellation occurs. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{simple poles at }z=n\pi i\ (n\in\mathbb Z);\qquad\text{no finite zeros}.}
$$
For example their residues are $1/\cosh(n\pi i)=(-1)^n$.

Infinity requires care: the <poles> themselves tend to infinity. Equivalently $f(1/w)$ has <poles> at $w=1/(n\pi i)$ accumulating at zero. Thus $\boxed{\infty\text{ is a non-isolated singularity}}$, and it is not a zero. In the usual classification of isolated singularities, it cannot be called a <removable singularity>, <pole> or isolated essential singularity. The <non-isolated singularity> distinction is necessary even though the function is meromorphic at every finite point.

For the normalized equation, $x_0$ is an <ordinary point> when both $p$ and $q$ are analytic near $x_0$. A singular point is a <regular singular point> when $(x-x_0)p(x)$ and $(x-x_0)^2q(x)$ extend analytically to $x_0$; equivalently $p$ has at most a <simple pole> and $q$ at most a double <pole> there.

After normalization, $p=-6x^2/(1-x^3)$ and $q=-6x/(1-x^3)$. Both are analytic at zero, so $\boxed{x=0\text{ is ordinary}}$. At one,

are analytic, whereas $p,q$ themselves have <poles>. Hence $\boxed{x=1\text{ is a regular singular point}}$.

Insert $y=\sum_{n\geq0}a_nx^n$. The coefficient of $x^0$ gives $a_2=0$; for $k\geq1$ the coefficient of $x^k$ gives
$$
(k+2)(k+1)a_{k+2}=(k+2)(k+1)a_{k-1}.
$$
Thus $a_n=a_{n-3}$ for $n\geq3$. The initial data give $a_0=1$, $a_1=0$, so
$$
\boxed{y(x)=1+x^3+x^6+\cdots=\frac1{1-x^3},\qquad |x|<1.}
$$
The radius is one because the three cube roots of unity are <poles> of this solution. A direct structural check observes that the differential equation is exactly $[(1-x^3)y]''=0$. Thus $(1-x^3)y=A+Bx$, and the initial data select $A=1,B=0$. This is the <product-derivative reduction of a second-order differential equation>.

Use a <Frobenius solution> $y=x^\sigma\sum_{n\geq0}a_nx^n$, with $a_0\ne0$. The lowest power $x^{\sigma-1}$ has coefficient $2\sigma(2\sigma-1)a_0$, giving the <indicial equation>
$$
\boxed{2\sigma(2\sigma-1)=0,\qquad\sigma=0,\frac12.}
$$
For $n\geq1$, matching the coefficient of $x^{n+\sigma-1}$ gives
$$
2(n+\sigma)(2n+2\sigma-1)a_n-(2n+2\sigma-1)a_{n-1}=0.
$$
For both candidate exponents the cancelled factor is nonzero in this range, so the recurrence is
$$
\boxed{a_n=\frac{a_{n-1}}{2(n+\sigma)}.}
$$
With $a_0=1$, the exponent-zero solution is
$$
\boxed{y_1(x)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{x^n}{2^nn!}=e^{x/2}.}
$$
For $\sigma=1/2$, the coefficients are $a_n=1/(2n+1)!!=2^nn!/(2n+1)!$, giving
$$
\boxed{y_2(x)=\sqrt{x}\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{x^n}{(2n+1)!!}=\sqrt{x}\left(1+\frac x3+\frac{x^2}{15}+\frac{x^3}{105}+\cdots\right).}
$$
The <ratio test> gives infinite <radius of convergence> for both series factors, since the successive ratios in magnitude are $|x|/(2n+2)$ and $|x|/(2n+3)$. The second full solution still needs a square-root branch in the complex plane; on $x>0$ it is a real solution. The distinct leading behaviors $1$ and $\sqrt{x}$ prove <linear independence>.

For an independent closed-form check on the structure, put $z=x/2$. The equation becomes $zY''+(1/2-z)Y'-(1/2)Y=0$, a <Kummer differential equation> with equal parameters. Its <exponential reduction of a confluent hypergeometric equation with equal parameters> explains the elementary first solution and the branched companion.

<Fermat principle> states that the <optical path length> $\mathcal S=\int n\,ds$, or equivalently travel time $\mathcal S/c_0$, is stationary under small variations of the ray with fixed endpoints. Stationarity need not mean a global minimum.

For a plane ray written as $y(x)$, use $\mathcal S[y]=\int F(x,y,y')dx$, with $F=n(x,y)\sqrt{1+y'^2}$. Its stationary paths obey the <Euler-Lagrange equation>
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}-\frac d{dx}\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}\right)=0.}
$$
It follows by taking the <first variation>, integrating the term in $\delta y'$ by parts, and using zero endpoint variations.

Choose the mirror as the line $y=0$, the source as $(x_A,h_A)$ and the observer as $(x_B,h_B)$, with positive heights. Within the plane of incidence, let the reflection point be $(s,0)$. In a uniform medium the optical length is a constant index times
$$
\ell(s)=\sqrt{(s-x_A)^2+h_A^2}+\sqrt{(x_B-s)^2+h_B^2}.
$$
Stationarity with respect to the point on the mirror gives
$$
0=\ell'(s)=\frac{s-x_A}{\sqrt{(s-x_A)^2+h_A^2}}-\frac{x_B-s}{\sqrt{(x_B-s)^2+h_B^2}}.
$$
These two ratios are the sines of the angles to the normal, with the stationary point between the two projected endpoints. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\sin\theta_{\rm in}=\sin\theta_{\rm out},\qquad\theta_{\rm in}=\theta_{\rm out}.}
$$
The angles lie in $[0,\pi/2)$, where sine is injective. Varying a second tangential coordinate in three dimensions also enforces that the source, normal and reflected ray lie in one plane. This derives the <law of reflection> from <Fermat principle>.

Place the interface at $y=0$, with source $(x_A,h_1)$ and observer $(x_B,-h_2)$ in the two uniform media. Let $(s,0)$ be the crossing point. The optical length is
$$
\mathcal S(s)=\mu_1\sqrt{(s-x_A)^2+h_1^2}+\mu_2\sqrt{(x_B-s)^2+h_2^2}.
$$
Differentiating with respect to $s$ and setting the result to zero gives
$$
\mu_1\frac{s-x_A}{\sqrt{(s-x_A)^2+h_1^2}}=\mu_2\frac{x_B-s}{\sqrt{(x_B-s)^2+h_2^2}}.
$$
Recognizing the ratios as the sines of the angles measured from the interface normal proves <Snell's law>:
$$
\boxed{\mu_1\sin\theta_1=\mu_2\sin\theta_2.}
$$
The stationarity condition is the continuity of the tangential optical momentum across the planar interface.

The original PDF places both endpoints at height $5/4$; the vertically stacked fraction can be misread in extracted text. The coefficient in the index is $4$, multiplying $\sqrt y$.

For the optical-length integrand $F=4\sqrt y\sqrt{1+y'^2}$, the <Beltrami identity> applies because there is no explicit $x$ dependence. It gives
$$
F-y'F_{y'}=\frac{4\sqrt y}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}=4\sqrt h,
$$
where $h>0$ is constant. Therefore $y'^2=y/h-1$. The full <Euler-Lagrange equation> is $2yy''=1+y'^2$, and combining these gives $y''=1/(2h)$. This also excludes a constant curve, which would satisfy the squared first integral but not the full variational equation. Integration yields
$$
y(x)=h+\frac{(x-x_0)^2}{4h}.
$$
Equal endpoint heights at $x=-1,1$ require $x_0=0$. The height condition then becomes $h+1/(4h)=5/4$, or $4h^2-5h+1=0$. Its two positive roots are $h=1$ and $h=1/4$. Hence the two possible stationary rays are
$$
\boxed{y(x)=1+\frac{x^2}{4}\qquad\text{or}\qquad y(x)=\frac14+x^2,\qquad-1\leq x\leq1.}
$$
Both stay in $y>0$, have the required endpoints and satisfy the variational equation. These are the <two optical rays in a square-root refractive-index profile>. Fermat stationarity supplies two rays; it does not assert that both globally minimize travel time.

Take real admissible functions with finite $F,G$, positive $G$, and a self-adjoint domain for the <Sturm-Liouville operator>. Compactly supported variations $\eta$ avoid any boundary contribution at infinity. Formal self-adjointness gives
$$
\delta F=\int_{\mathbb R}[\eta Ly+yL\eta]dx=2\int_{\mathbb R}\eta Ly\,dx,\qquad\delta G=2\int_{\mathbb R}wy\eta\,dx.
$$
For $\Lambda=F/G$, the quotient rule therefore gives
$$
\delta\Lambda=\frac2G\int_{\mathbb R}\eta(Ly-\Lambda wy)dx.
$$
Vanishing for arbitrary variations requires
$$
\boxed{Ly=\lambda wy,\qquad\lambda=\Lambda[y].}
$$
Conversely an <eigenfunction> in the self-adjoint domain makes this variation zero. Multiplying its equation by $y$ and integrating also shows directly that its stationary quotient is $\boxed{\Lambda[y_n]=\lambda_n}$.

Thus the stationary values are the admissible <eigenvalues>. Under the usual positive-weight, semibounded hypotheses the lowest is the global minimum, while higher <eigenfunctions> generally give saddle points. The assumption $y\to0$ alone is not a complete domain specification for arbitrary $\rho,\sigma$: the required integrals and boundary form must exist. The rapidly decaying oscillator trials in part (b) meet these conditions.

Normalize the Gaussian trial first. The <Gaussian integral> gives
$$
1=C^2\sqrt{\frac\pi\alpha},\qquad\boxed{C=\pm\left(\frac\alpha\pi\right)^{1/4}.}
$$
Its second and fourth moments are $\langle x^2\rangle=1/(2\alpha)$ and $\langle x^4\rangle=3/(4\alpha^2)$. Since $\psi'=-\alpha x\psi$, the kinetic term is $\int(\psi')^2=\alpha^2\langle x^2\rangle=\alpha/2$. At the specified coupling, the <energy> is therefore
$$
E(\alpha)=\frac\alpha2+\frac1{2\alpha}+\frac3{2\alpha^2}.
$$
The stationary condition is
$$
E'(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha^3-\alpha-6}{2\alpha^3}=0,\qquad\alpha^3-\alpha-6=(\alpha-2)(\alpha^2+2\alpha+3).
$$
The quadratic factor is positive for real $\alpha$, so the unique positive stationary width is $\alpha=2$. Also $E''=\alpha^{-3}+9\alpha^{-4}>0$ and $E$ diverges at both ends of $\alpha>0$, proving a global minimum within this trial family. Its value is
$$
\boxed{E_{\rm trial,min}=\frac{13}{8},\qquad\psi_*(x)=\pm(2/\pi)^{1/4}e^{-x^2}.}
$$
The associated <Sturm-Liouville problem> is
$$
\boxed{-\psi''+(x^2+2x^4)\psi=E\psi,\qquad\psi\in L^2(\mathbb R),\quad\psi\to0\text{ at }\pm\infty.}
$$
Here $\rho=1$, $\sigma=x^2+2x^4$ and $w=1$. The <Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle> makes the exact lowest <eigenvalue> the minimum over all normalized admissible states. Restricting to Gaussians can only raise this minimum, hence $\boxed{E_0\leq13/8}$. The inequality is strict because $H\psi_*/\psi_*=2-3x^2+2x^4$ is not constant: the trial is not an exact <eigenfunction>. This is the <Gaussian trial for a quadratic-plus-quartic oscillator>.

For any real coupling, the same normalized Gaussian family has <energy>
$$
E_\epsilon(\alpha)=\frac\alpha2+\frac1{2\alpha}+\frac{3\epsilon}{4\alpha^2}.
$$
If $\epsilon<0$, then as $\alpha\downarrow0$ the negative $\alpha^{-2}$ term dominates the positive $\alpha^{-1}$ and $\alpha$ terms. Each trial still has finite <energy>, is normalized and decays at infinity, but its <energy> tends to $-\infty$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\inf_{\|\psi\|_2=1}E[\psi]=-\infty;\qquad\text{there is no minimum energy.}}
$$
This variational sequence makes the instability of the negative quartic potential explicit, without mistaking any local well near the origin for a global ground-state bound.
