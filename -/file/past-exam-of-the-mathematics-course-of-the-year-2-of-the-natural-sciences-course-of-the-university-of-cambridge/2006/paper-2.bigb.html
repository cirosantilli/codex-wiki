= Solution
{parent=1}

Choose the <Green function> convention $\Delta_{\mathbf r}G(\mathbf r;\mathbf r_0)=\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0)$, with decay at infinity and no extra singularities. For sufficiently decaying source data,
$$
\boxed{\Phi(\mathbf r_0)=\int_{\mathbb R^3}G(\mathbf r;\mathbf r_0)\sigma(\mathbf r)\,d^3r,\qquad G=-\frac1{4\pi|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0|}.}
$$
Away from its source, this <fundamental solution of the Laplace equation> is harmonic. Its radial derivative is $1/(4\pi\rho^2)$, so the flux of its <gradient> through a small source-centred sphere is one. The <divergence theorem> therefore supplies exactly the unit <Dirac delta function> required by the equation. Decay at infinity excludes an added globally regular harmonic term.

For the half-space, reflect the source to $\mathbf r_0^*=(-x_0,y_0,z_0)$. The <method of images> gives the zero-boundary <Dirichlet Green function>
$$
\widetilde G=-\frac1{4\pi}\left(\frac1{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0|}-\frac1{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0^*|}\right).
$$
The two distances coincide at $x=0$, and also cancel if the source lies on the plane. The image singularity lies outside the physical domain.

The <sign conventions for a Dirichlet Green function> require care here. With $\Delta\widetilde G=+\delta$, <Green's second identity> gives $\Phi(\mathbf r_0)=\int_{\partial\Omega}f\,\partial_n\widetilde G\,dS$. At this wall the outward normal is $-\mathbf e_x$, so $\partial_n\widetilde G=-\partial_x\widetilde G$. Direct differentiation gives $\partial_x\widetilde G|_{x=0}=-x_0/(2\pi d^3)$, where $d^2=x_0^2+(y-y_0)^2+(z-z_0)^2$. Thus the printed intermediate formula with $+\partial_x\widetilde G$ needs a minus sign for its stated Green convention. The final <Poisson kernel for the upper half-space> is positive:
$$
\boxed{\Phi(\mathbf r_0)=\frac{x_0}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R^2}\frac{f(y,z)\,dy\,dz}{[x_0^2+(y-y_0)^2+(z-z_0)^2]^{3/2}}.}
$$
Its boundary integral is one for constant data, providing an independent sign check.

= Solution

For <Laplace's equation> in <polar coordinates>, put $\Phi=R(r)\Theta(\theta)$. <Separation of variables> gives $\Theta''+n^2\Theta=0$ and $r^2R''+rR'-n^2R=0$. Periodicity selects integers $n\geq0$. For $n>0$, the radial solutions are $r^n,r^{-n}$; for $n=0$, they are $1,\ln r$, while a term linear in $\theta$ is excluded by periodicity. Their superposition is
$$
\Phi=A_0+C_0\ln r+\sum_{n\geq1}[(A_nr^n+C_nr^{-n})\cos n\theta+(B_nr^n+D_nr^{-n})\sin n\theta].
$$
Inside the disk, boundedness at the origin removes the logarithm and all negative powers. The separately biased semicircular boundary values have mean $(V_1+V_2)/2$, no nonconstant cosine coefficients, and sine coefficients $2(V_1-V_2)/(\pi n)$ for odd $n$, zero for even $n$. The <disk potential with two semicircular boundary values> is therefore
$$
\Phi=\frac{V_1+V_2}{2}+\frac{2(V_1-V_2)}\pi\sum_{m\geq0}\frac{(r/a)^{2m+1}\sin((2m+1)\theta)}{2m+1}.
$$
Put $w=(r/a)e^{i\theta}$. The odd logarithmic series sums to $\sum_{m\geq0}w^{2m+1}/(2m+1)=\frac12\log[(1+w)/(1-w)]$. The quotient has positive real part for $|w|<1$, so its argument is the principal <arctangent> of $2(r/a)\sin\theta/[1-(r/a)^2]$. Taking imaginary parts gives
$$
\boxed{\Phi=\frac{V_1+V_2}{2}+\frac{V_1-V_2}\pi\arctan\frac{2ar\sin\theta}{a^2-r^2}.}
$$
The boundary limits away from the two voltage jumps are $V_1,V_2$. Along the diameter through a jump the value is the mean; incompatible adjacent boundary limits prevent a continuous extension at the junctions.

There are **<logarithmic branch points> at $z=-3i$ and infinity**. At zero, every local branch has $\log(3i)=\ln3+i(\pi/2+2\pi k)\ne0$, so division by $z^2$ gives **a <pole> of order two at zero**. = Solution
{parent=ii}

A <holomorphic function> is complex differentiable throughout an open region; equivalently it is locally represented by a convergent complex power series. For $f=u+iv$, the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> are $u_x=v_y$, $u_y=-v_x$. With continuous first partial derivatives, these equations imply holomorphicity and $f'=u_x+iv_x$.

For $e^z=e^x\cos y+i e^x\sin y$, the equations hold and $u_x+iv_x=e^x(\cos y+i\sin y)=e^z$. For $\sin z=\sin x\cosh y+i\cos x\sinh y$, they also hold, and $u_x+iv_x=\cos x\cosh y-i\sin x\sinh y=\cos z$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dz}e^z=e^z,\qquad\frac{d}{dz}\sin z=\cos z.}
$$

The <Cauchy-Riemann equations> give $v_x=2x$, $v_y=2-2y$. Integrating and matching both derivatives gives the <harmonic conjugate>
$$
\boxed{v=x^2+2y-y^2+C,\qquad f(z)=2z+iz^2+iC,\quad C\in\mathbb R.}

The real part of this polynomial is exactly the required $2x(1-y)$.

Here $v_x=-3x^2+3y^2+4y$ and $v_y=6xy+4x$. The <harmonic conjugate> and resulting polynomial are

= Solution

For a pole of order $N$ at $z_0$, the <residue> is $\operatorname{Res}_{z_0}F=\frac1{(N-1)!}\lim_{z\to z_0}\frac{d^{N-1}}{dz^{N-1}}[(z-z_0)^NF(z)]$. For the rational integral, close the real-axis contour with an upper semicircle. The poles are double at $i$ and simple at $2i$, with
$$
\operatorname{Res}_iF=\left.\frac d{dz}\frac1{(z+i)^2(z^2+4)}\right|_{z=i}=-\frac i{36},\qquad \operatorname{Res}_{2i}F=\frac1{(4i)(-3)^2}=-\frac i{36}.
$$
The arc integral vanishes as $O(R^{-5})$. By the <residue theorem> the whole real-axis integral is $\pi/9$, and evenness gives
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{(x^2+1)^2(x^2+4)}=\frac\pi{18}.}
$$
For the second calculation, reciprocal expansion of the sine gives $1/\sin z=z^{-1}+z/6+O(z^3)$. Multiplying by $\cos z=1-z^2/2+O(z^4)$ yields $\cot z=z^{-1}-z/3+O(z^3)$. Therefore
$$
\frac{\pi\cot\pi z}{z^2}=z^{-3}-\frac{\pi^2}{3}z^{-1}+O(z),\qquad\boxed{\operatorname{Res}_0=-\frac{\pi^2}{3}.}
$$
At every nonzero integer $n$, $\pi\cot\pi z$ has a simple pole with residue one, giving residue $1/n^2$ after division by $z^2$.

The <cotangent contour summation> uses the positively oriented square of half-side $R=N+1/2$. Its length is $8R$ and $|z|\geq R$ on its boundary. The supplied cotangent bound implies $|\oint Fdz|\leq8\pi C/R\to0$. Its enclosed residues sum to $-\pi^2/3+2\sum_{n=1}^Nn^{-2}$. The <residue theorem> therefore proves the <Basel problem> value
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6}.}
$$

= Solution

The one-sided <Laplace transform> is $\bar f(p)=\int_0^\infty e^{-pt}f(t)dt$. Its <Bromwich inversion formula> is $f(t)=(2\pi i)^{-1}\int_{\gamma-i\infty}^{\gamma+i\infty}e^{pt}\bar f(p)dp$, with an upward vertical contour to the right of all singularities and within the appropriate convergence half-plane.

To derive the <convolution theorem for Laplace transforms>, transform $x(t)=\int_0^tf(\tau)g(t-\tau)d\tau$. With $u=t-\tau\geq0$, the triangular integration region becomes a quadrant. <Fubini's theorem> gives $\bar x(p)=\int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty e^{-p(u+\tau)}f(\tau)g(u)du\,d\tau=\bar f(p)\bar g(p)$, under the usual absolute convergence hypotheses. Inversion gives the required convolution formula.

For the differential equation, the transformed initial conditions give $(p^2-1)\bar y=\bar g+p+1$, hence $\bar y=\bar g/(p^2-1)+1/(p-1)$. Since $\mathcal L[\sinh t]=1/(p^2-1)$ for $\operatorname{Re}p>1$, the convolution yields
$$
\boxed{y(t)=e^t+\int_0^t\sinh(t-\tau)g(\tau)d\tau.}
$$
The integral and its first derivative vanish at zero, so the homogeneous term supplies both initial values. Differentiating twice gives the forcing through the unit derivative of the hyperbolic-sine kernel at zero.

For a <Cartesian tensor> under a proper orthogonal change $R$, the rank-$n$ law is $T'_{i_1\cdots i_n}=R_{i_1j_1}\cdots R_{i_nj_n}T_{j_1\cdots j_n}$, with repeated indices summed. An <isotropic tensor> has $T'=T$ for every such rotation.

For rank two, $T=RTR^T$. A half-turn about a coordinate axis, obtained by applying a quarter-turn twice, reverses each off-diagonal component with exactly one index on that axis. Invariance forces every off-diagonal entry to vanish. A quarter-turn about the third axis exchanges $T_{11}$ and $T_{22}$; quarter-turns about the other axes equate them with $T_{33}$. Thus the <isotropic second-rank tensor> is
$$
\boxed{T_{ij}=\lambda\delta_{ij}.}
$$
Conversely $R(\lambda I)R^T=\lambda I$, proving sufficiency.

{parent=6}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The unique symmetric and antisymmetric decomposition is $S=(T+T^T)/2$, $A=(T-T^T)/2$. Both pieces are <Cartesian tensors>: transposing $T'=RTR^T$ gives $(T')^T=RT^TR^T$, so $S'=RSR^T$ and $A'=RAR^T$. Hence this is a decomposition into a <symmetric tensor> and an <antisymmetric second-rank tensor>, rather than just a decomposition of numerical arrays.

For the <axial vector of an antisymmetric second-rank tensor>, contract the two <Levi-Civita symbols>:
$$
\epsilon_{kij}a_k=\frac12\epsilon_{kij}\epsilon_{klm}A_{lm}=\frac12(\delta_{il}\delta_{jm}-\delta_{im}\delta_{jl})A_{lm}=A_{ij}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{A=\begin{pmatrix}0&a_3&-a_2\\-a_3&0&a_1\\a_2&-a_1&0\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
With this convention $Ax=x\times a$, so the displayed matrix is the negative of the <cross-product matrix> for $a\times x$.

{parent=6}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For proper rotations the <Levi-Civita symbol> is invariant, so contracting the two rank-one tensors $\partial_j$ and $v_k$ gives $c_i=\epsilon_{ijk}\partial_jv_k$ with transformation $c_i'=R_{i\ell}c_\ell$. Thus the <curl> is a vector under proper rotations. Under an orientation-reversing orthogonal change it acquires an additional determinant sign: it is an <axial vector> when $v$ is a polar vector.

Commuting smooth partial derivatives and using epsilon contraction gives the <curl of the curl identity>:
$$
[\nabla\times(\nabla\times v)]_i=\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{klm}\partial_j\partial_lv_m=(\delta_{il}\delta_{jm}-\delta_{im}\delta_{jl})\partial_j\partial_lv_m=\partial_i(\partial_jv_j)-\partial_j\partial_jv_i.
$$
Now take the curl of <Faraday's law> and use the vacuum <Ampère-Maxwell equation>: $\nabla\times\nabla\times E=-\partial_t(\nabla\times B)=-c^{-2}E_{tt}$. Since $\nabla\cdot E=0$, the identity gives $\Delta E-c^{-2}E_{tt}=0$. Taking the curl of the <Ampère-Maxwell equation> gives the same result for $B$, using $\nabla\cdot B=0$. Hence both satisfy the <electromagnetic wave equation>
$$
\boxed{-\Delta E+c^{-2}E_{tt}=0,\qquad-\Delta B+c^{-2}B_{tt}=0.}
$$

= Solution

For small oscillations with quadratic kinetic and potential energies, a <normal mode> has a fixed spatial amplitude pattern and one angular <normal frequency>. Its amplitude is a <normal coordinate>; choosing all modes diagonalizes the two quadratic forms and decouples their equations. A zero frequency describes a free symmetry motion.

Let $q_j=R[\theta_j-j\pi/2]$ be the small signed arc displacements, with cyclic indices $j=0,1,2,3$. The springs follow arcs, so their extensions are $q_{j+1}-q_j$. The <Lagrangian> of the <normal modes of a four-bead spring ring> is
$$
\boxed{L=\frac m2\sum_{j=0}^3\dot q_j^2-\frac k2\sum_{j=0}^3(q_{j+1}-q_j)^2.}
$$
The stiffness matrix divided by $k$ is the cycle <graph Laplacian>, with diagonal entries two, adjacent entries minus one, and zero opposite entries. An <orthonormal basis> of mode vectors and their frequencies is
$$
\boxed{u_0=\tfrac12(1,1,1,1),\quad\omega_0=0;\qquad u_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,0,-1,0),\quad u_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(0,1,0,-1),\quad\omega_{1,2}=\sqrt{2k/m};\qquad u_3=\tfrac12(1,-1,1,-1),\quad\omega_3=2\sqrt{k/m}.}
$$
The <normal mode coordinates> are $Q_\alpha=u_\alpha\cdot q$, with equations $\ddot Q_\alpha+\omega_\alpha^2Q_\alpha=0$. The zero mode rotates all beads together without stretching a spring and has $Q_0=A+Bt$. In each of the two degenerate middle modes, opposite beads have opposite signed arc displacements while the other pair remains stationary. Any fixed linear combination is another mode of that frequency. The last mode alternates the signed displacement of adjacent beads. The radius cancels from the frequencies because arc length supplies the same factor $R^2$ to the angular kinetic and spring energies.

= Solution
{parent=8}

A <group> has a closed <binary operation>, associativity, an identity, and an inverse for each element. For a finite group, <Lagrange's theorem> states $|G|=[G:H]|H|$ for each subgroup $H$, so $|H|$ divides $|G|$.

The <order of a group element> $g$ is the least positive $n$ with $g^n=e$, or infinity if none exists. In a finite group the powers form the <cyclic subgroup> $\langle g\rangle$ of that order; Lagrange's theorem proves $\operatorname{ord}(g)\mid|G|$. If $|G|=p$ is prime and $g\ne e$, its order must be $p$. Thus $g$ generates the whole group, proving $G\cong C_p$.

If every nonidentity element has order two, every element is its own inverse. Then $ab=(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}=ba$. This proves that a <group of exponent two is abelian>.

{parent=8}

= Solution
{parent=i}

In a <group of order six>, element orders divide six. If there were no element of order three or six, every nonidentity element would have order two, making the group abelian by the preceding argument. Choose $a\ne e$ and $b\notin\{e,a\}$. Then $\{e,a,b,ab\}$ is a subgroup of order four: its elements are distinct, it is closed because $a,b$ commute and square to $e$, and each element is its own inverse. This contradicts <Lagrange's theorem>, since four does not divide six. Hence **an element of order three or six exists**.

{parent=8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Suppose $x,y$ have order three but $x\ne y,y^2$. Their <cyclic subgroups> have trivial intersection: any common nonidentity element would generate both subgroups and force $x=y$ or $y^2$. The nine products $x^iy^j$, $0\leq i,j<3$, are distinct. Indeed $x^iy^j=x^ky^\ell$ implies $x^{i-k}=y^{\ell-j}$ in that intersection, so $i=k$ and $j=\ell$ modulo three. Nine distinct elements cannot fit in a group of order six, proving the claim without assuming commutativity.

{parent=8}

= Solution
{parent=Classification}

If an element has order six, it generates $G$, so $G\cong C_6$. Otherwise choose an element $x$ of order three. The preceding result excludes any further order-three elements outside $\{e,x,x^2\}$, and there are no elements of order six by assumption. Each of the other three elements must therefore have order two. Thus
$$
\boxed{G\cong C_6,\qquad\text{or}\qquad G=\{e,x,x^2,h_1,h_2,h_3\},\quad\operatorname{ord}(x)=3,\ \operatorname{ord}(h_i)=2.}
$$
The noncyclic case is the <symmetric group on three points>, as the classification of a <group of order six> further identifies.

= Solution
{parent=9}

A <normal subgroup> $H$ satisfies $gHg^{-1}=H$ for every $g\in G$. A <group homomorphism> preserves multiplication, $\Phi(ab)=\Phi(a)\Phi(b)$. It follows that $\Phi(e_1)=e_2$ and $\Phi(g^{-1})=\Phi(g)^{-1}$.

The <kernel of a group homomorphism> $K=\{g:\Phi(g)=e_2\}$ contains the identity and satisfies $ab^{-1}\in K$ for $a,b\in K$, so is a subgroup. For $k\in K$, $\Phi(gkg^{-1})=\Phi(g)e_2\Phi(g)^{-1}=e_2$. Thus $gKg^{-1}\subseteq K$, and applying the same inclusion to $g^{-1}$ gives equality. Hence the kernel is a <normal subgroup>.

The product $f=\prod_{i<j}(x_i-x_j)$ is a <Vandermonde determinant> up to a fixed overall sign. A transposition exchanging $x_p,x_q$ reverses their mutual factor. Every other factor involving one of these variables is paired with the corresponding factor involving the other; those paired products are unchanged. Therefore
$$
\boxed{f\longmapsto-f\quad\text{under every transposition}.}
$$
If a permutation has decompositions into $r$ and $s$ transpositions, they act as $(-1)^rf$ and $(-1)^sf$. This polynomial is nonzero, for example at pairwise distinct real inputs, so the signs must agree. Thus **all decompositions have the same parity**, and an even decomposition forces every decomposition to be even. This defines the <sign of a permutation>; its kernel is the <alternating group>, linking the parity argument to the normal-kernel result above.

= Solution

An <invariant subspace> $W$ satisfies $D(g)W\subseteq W$ for every $g\in G$; applying the inverse gives equality. The representation is <irreducible> when the only invariant subspaces are $0$ and the full space.

The inverse and <conjugate transpose> each reverse multiplication order, so their combination preserves it:
$$
D_2(gh)=[D_1(h^{-1})D_1(g^{-1})]^\dagger=D_1(g^{-1})^\dagger D_1(h^{-1})^\dagger=D_2(g)D_2(h).
$$
Its values are invertible, so this is the <conjugate contragredient representation>.

If $W$ is invariant under $D_2$, take $w\in W$ and $v\in W^\perp$. For the standard <Hermitian inner product>,
$$
\langle w,D_1(g)v\rangle=\langle D_1(g)^\dagger w,v\rangle=\langle D_2(g^{-1})w,v\rangle=0,
$$
since $D_2(g^{-1})w\in W$. Hence $D_1(g)v\in W^\perp$, proving its invariance. If $D_1$ is irreducible, $W^\perp$ is either zero or the whole space; finite-dimensional orthogonal complementation makes $W$ the whole space or zero, respectively. Thus **$D_2$ is irreducible whenever $D_1$ is irreducible**.
