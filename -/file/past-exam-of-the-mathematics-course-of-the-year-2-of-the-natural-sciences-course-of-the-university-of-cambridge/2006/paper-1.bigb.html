= Solution
{parent=1}

In <Cartesian coordinates>, $\nabla\Phi=(\Phi_x,\Phi_y,\Phi_z)$. For <orthogonal coordinates>, let $\mathbf r(q_1,q_2,q_3)$ be the position, $h_i=|\partial\mathbf r/\partial q_i|$ the <scale factors of orthogonal coordinates>, and $\mathbf e_i=h_i^{-1}\partial\mathbf r/\partial q_i$ the corresponding <unit vectors>. Since $d\mathbf r=\sum_i h_i\mathbf e_i\,dq_i$ and $d\Phi=\nabla\Phi\cdot d\mathbf r=\sum_i\Phi_{q_i}dq_i$, comparison of independent increments gives the <gradient in orthogonal coordinates>:
$$
\boxed{\nabla\Phi=\sum_{i=1}^3\frac{\mathbf e_i}{h_i}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial q_i}.}
$$
For the <hyperbolic parametrization of oblate spheroidal coordinates>, the three coordinate tangent vectors are
$$
\mathbf r_R=(\sinh R\cos\theta\cos\phi,\sinh R\cos\theta\sin\phi,\cosh R\sin\theta),
$$
$$
\mathbf r_\theta=(-\cosh R\sin\theta\cos\phi,-\cosh R\sin\theta\sin\phi,\sinh R\cos\theta),\qquad\mathbf r_\phi=(-\cosh R\cos\theta\sin\phi,\cosh R\cos\theta\cos\phi,0).
$$
Their pairwise <dot products> are zero. Their squared <Euclidean norms> are $\sinh^2R+\sin^2\theta$, $\sinh^2R+\sin^2\theta$, and $\cosh^2R\cos^2\theta$, respectively. Thus the coordinate surfaces meet orthogonally wherever this is a regular coordinate chart, and
$$
\boxed{h_R=h_\theta=\sqrt{\sinh^2R+\sin^2\theta},\qquad h_\phi=\cosh R\cos\theta.}
$$
The last expression uses the usual latitude range $|\theta|<\pi/2$; without that range a scale factor is its absolute value. Axial or ring degeneracies are excluded from a regular chart.

= Solution
{parent=2}

<Separation of variables> in the <wave equation> with fixed endpoints selects $H_n(x)=\sin(n\pi x/L)$ and angular frequencies $\omega_n=cn\pi/L$, $n\geq1$. The time factors may be chosen as $F_n(t)=\cos\omega_nt$, $G_n(t)=\sin\omega_nt$. By <Fourier sine series> orthogonality, the initial data give
$$
a_n=\frac2L\int_0^Lf(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx,\qquad b_n=\frac2{L\omega_n}\int_0^Lg(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx.
$$
Hence $u=\sum_{n\geq1}[a_n\cos\omega_nt+b_n\sin\omega_nt]\sin(n\pi x/L)$. The factor $1/\omega_n$ in $b_n$ is required because differentiating the time sine supplies $\omega_n$.

The given displacement is a symmetric triangular tent: it is zero at both endpoints and has height $k$ at $L/2$. Integrating each linear half gives $a_n=8k\sin(n\pi/2)/(n^2\pi^2)$. Release from rest gives $b_n=0$, so the <midpoint-plucked fixed-end string> is
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=\frac{8k}{\pi^2}\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^m}{(2m+1)^2}\cos\frac{(2m+1)\pi ct}{L}\sin\frac{(2m+1)\pi x}{L}.}
$$
Only odd modes occur, by symmetry about the midpoint. The initial slope corner makes this a finite-energy <weak solution>; it is a classical solution away from the propagating corner lines.

= Solution
{parent=3}

With the stated angular-frequency convention, the <inverse Fourier transform> is $f(x)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widetilde f(k)e^{ikx}dk$. Direct integration gives $\widetilde f_0(k)=\int_0^\infty e^{-(\alpha+ik)x}dx=1/(\alpha+ik)$. For $n\geq1$, <integration by parts> has zero boundary terms and gives $\widetilde f_n=n\widetilde f_{n-1}/(\alpha+ik)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\widetilde f_n(k)=\frac{n!}{(\alpha+ik)^{n+1}}.}
$$
This also follows from the <Fourier transform of a polynomial multiple>, since multiplication by $x$ corresponds to $i\partial_k$.

To prove <Parseval's identity>, substitute the inverse transform of $g$ into $\int f^*g$. Under the assumed integrability, <Fubini's theorem> permits interchange, and the inner integral is $\int f(x)^*e^{ikx}dx=\widetilde f(k)^*$. Hence
$$
\int_{\mathbb R}f(x)^*g(x)dx=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}\widetilde f(k)^*\widetilde g(k)dk.
$$
Apply this with $f=g=f_n$. The spatial <Gamma integral> is $\int_0^\infty x^{2n}e^{-2\alpha x}dx=(2n)!/(2\alpha)^{2n+1}$. The transformed squared modulus is $(n!)^2/(\alpha^2+k^2)^{n+1}$, giving the scaled <even rational Parseval integral>
$$
\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{(\alpha^2+k^2)^{n+1}}=\frac{2\pi(2n)!}{(n!)^2(2\alpha)^{2n+1}}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=i}

Decompose $\mathbb R^n=\operatorname{span}\{a\}\oplus a^\perp$. The <rank-one matrix update> $aa^T$ acts as multiplication by $\|a\|^2$ along $a$ and as zero on its <orthogonal complement>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\lambda_\parallel(A^\pm)=1\pm\|a\|^2,\quad v_\parallel\in\operatorname{span}\{a\}\setminus\{0\};\qquad\lambda_\perp=1,\quad v_\perp\in a^\perp\setminus\{0\}.}
$$
The perpendicular eigenvalue has multiplicity $n-1$ and is absent when $n=1$. Consequently $A^+$ is always an <invertible matrix>, while
$$
\boxed{A^-\text{ is invertible if and only if }\|a\|\ne1.}
$$

= ii
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Put $\beta=\|b\|>0$ and choose an oriented <orthonormal basis> with third vector $b/\beta$. The <Levi-Civita symbol> term has action $\epsilon_{ijk}b_kx_j=x\times b$, the negative of $b\times x$. Thus this <axisymmetric linear map> has block form
$$
B^\pm\sim\begin{pmatrix}1&\beta&0\\-\beta&1&0\\0&0&1\pm\beta^2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The transverse eigenvalues $1\pm i\beta$ are not real. The only real <eigenvalue> and its <eigenvectors> are therefore
$$
\boxed{\lambda(B^\pm)=1\pm\beta^2,\qquad v\in\operatorname{span}\{b\}\setminus\{0\}.}
$$
The determinants are $\det B^+=(1+\beta^2)^2>0$ and $\det B^-=(1-\beta^2)(1+\beta^2)$, proving that **$B^+$ is always invertible, and $B^-$ is invertible exactly when $\|b\|\ne1$**. The complex transverse pair is important for this determinant even though the question asks for real eigenvalues.

= Solution
{parent=5}

A matrix is <diagonalizable> if some invertible $S$ makes $S^{-1}AS$ diagonal, equivalently if it has a basis of <eigenvectors>. If $A^p=0$ and $Av=\lambda v$ for $v\ne0$, then $0=A^pv=\lambda^pv$, so every <eigenvalue> is zero. There is at least one zero eigenvalue: otherwise $A$ and hence $A^p$ would be invertible. A <diagonalizable nilpotent matrix> has an all-zero diagonal form and therefore is zero itself.

For the specified matrices, direct determinant expansion gives
$$
\boxed{\det(\lambda I-B)=\lambda^3,\qquad\det(\lambda I-C)=\lambda(\lambda-6)(\lambda+6).}
$$
The <Cayley-Hamilton theorem> gives $B^3=0$. Its entry $B_{12}=-2$ is nonzero for every real $\alpha$, so **$B$ is not diagonalizable for any $\alpha$**. The matrix $C$ has three distinct real eigenvalues and is therefore **diagonalizable over $\mathbb R$**. For an explicit check, take columns $(-1,0,1)^T$, $(2,-3,1)^T$, $(2,3,1)^T$ in $S$; then $S^{-1}CS=\operatorname{diag}(-6,0,6)$.

{parent=Paper 1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=6}

For the normalized second-order equation $y''+P(x)y'+Q(x)y=0$, an <ordinary point> has analytic $P,Q$. A <regular singular point> is a <singular point> where $(x-x_0)P$ and $(x-x_0)^2Q$ extend analytically through $x_0$. The two finite singular points here are $0$ and $1/2$. At zero, the respective limiting coefficients are $1/2,0$; at $1/2$, they are $-1,0$. Thus **both are regular singular points**, with indicial roots $0,1/2$ and $0,2$, respectively.

= i
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}

Use the <Frobenius method> and write $y=x^r\sum_{n\geq0}a_nx^n$, $a_0\ne0$, with coefficients at negative indices defined as zero. The lowest power gives the <indicial equation> $r(2r-1)=0$, so $r=0$ or $r=1/2$. Equating the coefficient of $x^{n+r-1}$ gives, for $n\geq1$,
$$
\boxed{a_n=\frac{[4(n+r-1)^2+2]a_{n-1}-12(n+r-2)a_{n-2}+8a_{n-3}}{(n+r)[2(n+r)-1]}.}
$$
For $r=0$ and $a_0=1$, the first coefficients are $1,2,2,4/3,\ldots$. For $r=1/2$ they are $1,1,1/2,1/6,\ldots$, multiplying $x^{1/2}$. The distinct nonintegrally separated indicial roots give two independent <Frobenius series>. The half-power solution is a generalized power series, rather than an ordinary Taylor series at zero.

{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The coefficients in the <Taylor series> are
$$
\boxed{e^x=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{x^n}{n!},\qquad e^{2x}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{2^nx^n}{n!}.}
$$
For either series, the <ratio test> gives absolute term ratio $|x|/(n+1)$ or $2|x|/(n+1)$, tending to zero for each fixed real or complex $x$. Both have infinite <radius of convergence>.

= iii
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Substituting $a_n=2^n/n!$ into the $r=0$ recurrence, and $a_n=1/n!$ into the $r=1/2$ recurrence, satisfies every coefficient equation. They give $e^{2x}$ and $\sqrt x\,e^x$. Thus on a positive interval not crossing a singular point,
$$
\boxed{y=A\sqrt x\,e^x+Be^{2x}.}
$$
Their <Wronskian> is $e^{3x}(2x-1)/(2\sqrt x)$, nonzero for $x>0$, $x\ne1/2$, proving independence on each ordinary interval. Both functions are analytic near $1/2$, so this is an <apparent regular singular point>: a pole in normalized coefficients need not force a singular solution. The Wronskian can vanish at that singular point without making the two functions linearly dependent on an ordinary interval.

= Solution
{parent=7}

Multiply the original equation by $p(x)=\exp(\int^xu(s)ds)>0$. Since $p'=up$, it becomes $(py')'+qy+\lambda ry=0$ with $q=pv$, $r=pw>0$. This is <Sturm-Liouville form>. For two real <eigenfunctions>, multiply the equation for $y_n$ by $y_m$, subtract the equation for $y_m$ multiplied by $y_n$, and integrate. The <Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator> gives
$$
(\lambda_m-\lambda_n)\int_a^br\,y_my_n\,dx=[p(y_my_n'-y_ny_m')]_a^b.
$$
Each separated homogeneous boundary condition makes the corresponding determinant zero, provided its pair of coefficients is not both zero. For distinct <eigenvalues>, this proves the <orthogonality of Sturm-Liouville eigenfunctions>.

For the constant drift, the <drift-removing substitution for a Dirichlet Sturm-Liouville problem> is $y=e^{-\alpha x}v$. It reduces the equation to $v''+\lambda v=0$ with $v(0)=v(\pi)=0$. Nonzero solutions occur exactly at
$$
\boxed{\lambda_n=n^2,\qquad y_n=e^{-\alpha x}\sin nx,\quad n=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
The zero and negative values give only the zero solution under both endpoint conditions. The self-adjoint expression is $(e^{2\alpha x}y')'+\alpha^2e^{2\alpha x}y+\lambda e^{2\alpha x}y=0$. Hence the weight is $r=e^{2\alpha x}$, and for the displayed normalization,
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\pi e^{2\alpha x}y_my_n\,dx=\frac\pi2\delta_{mn}.}
$$

{parent=Paper 1}

= Solution
{parent=8}

For the zero initial data, the <Green function of an undamped oscillator> is $G_0(x,s)=\sin[k(x-s)]/k$ for $0\leq s\leq x$, and zero for $s>x$. It is continuous at the source and its first derivative jumps by one. The resulting <convolution> is
$$
y(x)=2\int_0^x\sin[k(x-s)]\cos(ks)ds=\boxed{x\sin kx.}
$$
It vanishes together with its first derivative at zero and has the required resonant forcing.

For the two-endpoint problem, write $L=\pi/(6k)$, $a=\min(0,L)$, $b=\max(0,L)$ and $\kappa=|k|$. This covers both signs of the supplied nonzero $k$. Left and right homogeneous solutions are $\sin[\kappa(x-a)]$ and $\sin[\kappa(b-x)]$, with <Wronskian> $-\kappa\sin[\kappa(b-a)]$. The <Dirichlet Green function> is therefore
$$
G_D(x,s)=-\frac{\sin[\kappa(x_<-a)]\sin[\kappa(b-x_>)]}{\kappa\sin[\kappa(b-a)]},\qquad x_<=\min(x,s),\ x_>=\max(x,s).
$$
Its endpoint values are zero and its first derivative jumps by one. Here $\kappa(b-a)=\pi/6$, so the denominator does not vanish. Integrating the constant forcing gives
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{k^2}{2}\int_a^bG_D(x,s)ds=\frac12\left[1-\frac{\cos(k(x-L/2))}{\cos(\pi/12)}\right].}
$$
Equivalently $y=(1-\cos kx)/2+(\sqrt3/2-1)\sin kx$. The negative interior response is consistent with the negative kernel for this short Dirichlet interval.

= Solution
{parent=9}

Apply the <Euler-Lagrange equation> to the integrand of $F-\lambda G$, namely $py'^2-qy^2-\lambda ry^2$. With fixed zero endpoint values, it gives $(py')'+qy+\lambda ry=0$. Multiplying by $y$ and using <integration by parts> gives $F=\lambda G$, so each stationary <Rayleigh quotient> $F/G$ equals its eigenvalue.

In the half-line problem, $p=x$, $q=-x$, $r=1$, so the quotient is $R[y]=\int_0^\infty x(y'^2+y^2)dx/\int_0^\infty y^2dx$. For $y=e^{-\beta x}$, $\beta>0$, its numerator is $(\beta^2+1)/(4\beta^2)$ and denominator $1/(2\beta)$. Hence $R=(\beta+\beta^{-1})/2$, minimized at $\beta=1$:
$$
\boxed{R_{\rm exponential}=1.}
$$
For the compactly supported linear trial, $A>0$, the numerator is $A^2/2+A^4/12$ and denominator $A^3/3$. Thus $R=3/(2A)+A/4$, minimized at $A=\sqrt6$, giving
$$
\boxed{R_{\rm linear}=\frac{\sqrt6}{2}\simeq1.225.}
$$
The derivative jump at the cutoff is allowed in the quadratic-form trial space of the <Rayleigh-Ritz method>: the trial is continuous and has finite form energy.

The <exponential ground state of a linearly weighted Sturm-Liouville problem> proves which estimate is best. Vanishing endpoint terms give
$$
\int_0^\infty x(y'^2+y^2)dx=\int_0^\infty y^2dx+\int_0^\infty x(y'+y)^2dx\geq\int_0^\infty y^2dx.
$$
Equality occurs for $y=Ce^{-x}$, which also satisfies the differential equation with $\lambda=1$. Thus **the exponential trial gives the exact smallest eigenvalue**, whereas the linear trial gives a larger upper bound. Finiteness at the singular endpoint supplies the regular branch with $xyy'\to0$; all the displayed trials have the required vanishing boundary terms.

= Solution
{parent=10}

For fixed endpoint values, stationarity requires the <Euler-Lagrange equation> $f_y-d(f_{y'})/dx=0$. Differentiate $y'f_{y'}-f$ along an extremal: its derivative is $y'[d(f_{y'})/dx-f_y]-f_x$. Thus when $f_x=0$ the <Beltrami identity> makes that quantity constant.

Take $y$ as depth measured downward. <Conservation of energy> from rest gives $mv^2/2-mgy=0$, hence speed $v=\sqrt{2gy}$. With <arc length> $ds=\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx$, the <travel time> is $T=(2g)^{-1/2}\int_0^{x_B}\sqrt{(1+y'^2)/y}\,dx$. For $f=\sqrt{(1+y'^2)/y}$, the Beltrami quantity is $-1/\sqrt{y(1+y'^2)}$. Thus $y(1+y'^2)=c>0$.

Put $y=c\sin^2\theta$ and take $y'=\cot\theta$ along the first arch. Since $dy/d\theta=2c\sin\theta\cos\theta$, one obtains $dx/d\theta=2c\sin^2\theta$. Integrating from the initial cusp gives the <cycloid> solving the <brachistochrone problem>:
$$
\boxed{x=c(\theta-\tfrac12\sin2\theta),\qquad y=c\sin^2\theta.}
$$
For the equal-height target, the first complete arch has $\theta_B=\pi$, so $c=l/\pi$. Also $ds/d\theta=2c\sin\theta$ and $v=\sqrt{2gc}\sin\theta$ for $0<\theta<\pi$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{T_{\min}=\pi\sqrt{\frac{2c}{g}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi l}{g}}.}
$$
The endpoint zeros of speed cancel the zeros of $ds/d\theta$, leaving finite travel time. Global minimality follows from the <maximum-depth bound for a brachistochrone>: any competing track with maximum depth $h$ satisfies $T\geq[l/\sqrt h+\pi\sqrt h]/\sqrt{2g}\geq\sqrt{2\pi l/g}$. The displayed cycloid attains both inequalities with $h=l/\pi$.
