= Solution
{parent=1}

For the nonconstant boundary data, use <separation of variables> in <polar coordinates> and put $\phi=R(r)\cos2\theta$. <Laplace's equation> reduces to
$$
r^2R''+rR'-4R=0.
$$
This <Cauchy-Euler equation> has independent solutions $r^2$ and $r^{-2}$. Unlike a disk containing the origin, the <annulus> permits both. The zero inner boundary value requires $R=A(r^2-a^4r^{-2})$, and the outer value requires $A=Vb^2/(b^4-a^4)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\phi_0(r,\theta)=V\frac{b^2(r^4-a^4)}{r^2(b^4-a^4)}\cos2\theta,\qquad a<r<b.}
$$
Substituting $r=a$ gives zero, while $r=b$ gives $V\cos2\theta$. This is the <Dirichlet problem on an annulus for one Fourier mode>; no other angular mode is needed.

For the changed inner boundary value, use <superposition> to add a radial <harmonic function> that is $-V$ at $a$ and zero at $b$. The radial zero-mode equation is $(rR')'=0$, giving $R=C+D\log r$. Imposing the two constant boundary values gives
$$
R(r)=-V\frac{\log(b/r)}{\log(b/a)}.
$$
Therefore the second answer is
$$
\boxed{\phi(r,\theta)=V\frac{b^2(r^4-a^4)}{r^2(b^4-a^4)}\cos2\theta-V\frac{\log(b/r)}{\log(b/a)}.}
$$
The logarithmic term is independent of $\theta$, so it changes only the angular average of the <electric potential>. Both answers are unique for these <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, by uniqueness for <Laplace's equation>.

= Solution

**Green identity.** With $n$ the outward unit normal, the <product rule> gives $\nabla\cdot(u\nabla v)=u\nabla^2v+\nabla u\cdot\nabla v$. Integrating over $V$ and applying the <divergence theorem> yields <Green's first identity>:
$$
\boxed{\int_V(u\nabla^2v+\nabla u\cdot\nabla v)\,dV=\int_Su\partial_nv\,dS.}
$$

**Uniqueness for nonzero mass.** Suppose two real solutions have the same prescribed boundary data, and let $w$ be their difference. The spatial equation called the <Klein-Gordon equation> in the question is the <modified Helmholtz equation>, so $\nabla^2w=m^2w$. With $u=v=w$, <Green's first identity> becomes
$$
\int_V(|\nabla w|^2+m^2w^2)\,dV=\int_Sw\partial_nw\,dS.
$$
For identical <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, $w=0$ on $S$; for identical <Neumann boundary conditions>, $\partial_nw=0$ there. Either makes the right-hand side zero. When $m$ is real and nonzero, both integrands on the left are nonnegative, forcing $w=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{For }m\ne0,\text{ either type of boundary data determines at most one solution.}}
$$
This is the required uniqueness assertion; existence is understood for admissible smooth data on a suitable bounded region. Complex-valued solutions obey the same result by treating their real and imaginary parts separately.

**Zero mass.** For $m=0$, the argument gives $\nabla w=0$, hence $w=C$ on the connected region. <Dirichlet boundary conditions> still force $C=0$, but <Neumann boundary conditions> leave an arbitrary additive constant:
$$
\boxed{m=0:\quad\text{Dirichlet uniqueness; Neumann uniqueness only up to a constant.}}
$$
The prescribed normal data in the zero-mass case must also satisfy $\int_S\partial_n\Phi\,dS=0$, by the <divergence theorem>. For nonzero $m$, that flux can instead equal $m^2\int_V\Phi\,dV$.

**Energy minimization.** Let $\eta=w-v$ for the two functions in the last part. They share their boundary values, so $\eta=0$ on $S$. Since $v$ is a <harmonic function>, <Green's first identity> gives
$$
\int_V\nabla v\cdot\nabla\eta\,dV=\int_S\eta\partial_nv\,dS-\int_V\eta\nabla^2v\,dV=0.
$$
Expand the square in the <Dirichlet energy> of $w=v+\eta$:
$$
\int_V|\nabla w|^2\,dV=\int_V|\nabla v|^2\,dV+2\int_V\nabla v\cdot\nabla\eta\,dV+\int_V|\nabla\eta|^2\,dV.
$$
The cross term vanishes, so
$$
\boxed{\int_V|\nabla w|^2\,dV-\int_V|\nabla v|^2\,dV=\int_V|\nabla(w-v)|^2\,dV\geq0.}
$$
Equality requires $w-v$ to be constant, and the shared boundary values then make it zero. This is the <Dirichlet principle>: among functions with fixed boundary values, the <harmonic function> minimizes <Dirichlet energy>.

**Harmonic parts.** Write the <holomorphic function> as $f=u+iv$. The <Cauchy-Riemann equations> say $u_x=v_y$ and $u_y=-v_x$. Equality of <mixed partial derivatives> therefore gives
$$
u_{xx}+u_{yy}=v_{yx}-v_{xy}=0,\qquad v_{xx}+v_{yy}=-u_{yx}+u_{xy}=0.
$$
Thus both the real and imaginary parts are <harmonic functions> and satisfy two-dimensional <Laplace's equation>.

**Single-valued logarithm.** A convenient choice is
$$
f(z)=\operatorname{Log}(1+z)-\operatorname{Log}(1-z),\qquad f(0)=0,
$$
using the <principal logarithm> for each term. Its <branch cuts> in the $z$ plane are $(-\infty,-1]$ and $[1,\infty)$. The <branch points> are $-1$ and $1$: going once around the first adds $2\pi i$, while going once around the second subtracts $2\pi i$. Removing the two exterior rays makes this a consistent <branch of a multivalued function> and in particular leaves the whole open unit disk available.

**Potential inside the cylinder.** Put $z=re^{i\theta}$ with $r<1$. Multiplication by $1-\overline z$ gives
$$
\frac{1+z}{1-z}=\frac{1-r^2+2ir\sin\theta}{1-2r\cos\theta+r^2}.
$$
The denominator is positive and the real part of the numerator is positive. Thus its <argument> lies strictly between $-\pi/2$ and $\pi/2$, and
$$
\operatorname{Im}f(re^{i\theta})=\arctan\frac{2r\sin\theta}{1-r^2}.
$$
As $r\uparrow1$, this tends to $\pi/2$ on the upper open semicircle and $-\pi/2$ on the lower open semicircle. Scale the imaginary part to obtain
$$
\boxed{\Phi(r,\theta)=\frac{2V_0}{\pi}\arctan\frac{2r\sin\theta}{1-r^2},\qquad0\leq r<1.}
$$
It is a <harmonic function>, has the required boundary values, and gives $\Phi(0)=0$. The two joining points of the boundary arcs have discontinuous boundary data, so no common continuous boundary value is demanded there. Along the real diameter, including radial approaches to either joining point, the displayed solution is zero. This is the <disk potential with two semicircular boundary values>.

\Image[paper-2-opposite-semicircle-potential.png]
{title=Harmonic potential inside a disk with opposite semicircle boundary values}
{description=The upper boundary has potential +V0 and the lower boundary -V0. Contours and colour show Phi/V0; the two joining points are excluded from the prescribed boundary values.}

For a second check, the <Taylor series> of the logarithms gives $f(z)=2\sum_{j\geq0}z^{2j+1}/(2j+1)$. Taking the imaginary part yields the <Fourier series>
$$
\Phi(r,\theta)=\frac{4V_0}{\pi}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{r^{2j+1}\sin((2j+1)\theta)}{2j+1},
$$
whose boundary limit is exactly the required alternating pair of constant semicircle values.

**Poles and residues.** An isolated singularity $z_0$ is a <pole> of order $m$ if its <Laurent series> has finitely many negative powers, the lowest being a nonzero multiple of $(z-z_0)^{-m}$. The <residue> is the coefficient of $(z-z_0)^{-1}$.

If $h(z_0)=0$ and $h'(z_0)\ne0$, factor $h(z)=(z-z_0)H(z)$, where $H$ is <holomorphic> near $z_0$ and $H(z_0)=h'(z_0)$. For $g(z_0)\ne0$,
$$
\frac{g(z)}{h(z)}=\frac1{z-z_0}\frac{g(z)}{H(z)}.
$$
The last quotient is <holomorphic> and nonzero at $z_0$, proving a <simple pole> with
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Res}_{z_0}\frac gh=\frac{g(z_0)}{h'(z_0)}.}
$$

**The given function.** For noninteger $a$, specify a <branch of a multivalued function> before using $z^a$. Choose $z^a=\exp(a\log z)$ with $-\pi/2<\arg z<3\pi/2$, a cut along the negative imaginary axis. This leaves all three roots of $1+z^3$ off the cut. They are $z_+=e^{i\pi/3}$, $z_- =e^{-i\pi/3}$ and $z_0=-1$, all simple. The numerator is nonzero at these points. Since the denominator has <derivative> $3z^2$, the <residues> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{pole}&\text{residue}\\\hline
e^{i\pi/3}&\tfrac13e^{i\pi(a-2)/3}\\
e^{-i\pi/3}&\tfrac13e^{-i\pi(a-2)/3}\\
-1&\tfrac13e^{i\pi(a-2)}
\end{array}.}
$$
For noninteger $a$, zero is a <branch point>, rather than an isolated <pole>. This remains true when $-1<a<0$ and the numerator diverges there. For nonnegative integer $a$ it is a regular point. Other logarithm branches change the phases of the <residues> consistently.

**Integral and convergence.** Near zero the integrand behaves as $x^a$, requiring $a>-1$. At infinity it behaves as $x^{a-3}$, requiring $a<2$. Therefore the exact convergence range is
$$
\boxed{-1<a<2.}
$$
Use a counterclockwise sector contour with rays at angles $0$ and $2\pi/3$, inner radius $\varepsilon$ and outer radius $R$. The same <complex logarithm> branch is valid throughout this sector. Only $z_+=e^{i\pi/3}$ lies inside. The small circular arc contributes $O(\varepsilon^{a+1})$ and the large one contributes $O(R^{a-2})$, so both vanish precisely in the stated range.

The outward positive-real ray contributes $I$. On the returning ray set $z=xe^{2\pi i/3}$: the denominator is still $1+x^3$, while $z^a dz=e^{2\pi i(a+1)/3}x^a dx$, with reversed limits. The <residue theorem> gives
$$
(1-e^{2\pi i(a+1)/3})I=\frac{2\pi i}{3}e^{i\pi(a-2)/3}.
$$
Put $\alpha=\pi(a+1)/3$. The right-hand phase is $-e^{i\alpha}$ and $1-e^{2i\alpha}=-2ie^{i\alpha}\sin\alpha$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty\frac{x^a}{1+x^3}\,dx=\frac{\pi}{3\sin(\pi(a+1)/3)},\qquad-1<a<2.}
$$
The sine is positive in this range, consistent with a positive real <integral>. This is a special case of the <Mellin integral of a reciprocal binomial>.

**Laplace transform.** Use $Y(p)=\int_0^\infty e^{-pt}y(t)dt$. The initial value is zero and the <Dirac delta> contributes $e^{-p}$, so the transformed equation is $pY+Y=e^{-p}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{Y(p)=\frac{e^{-p}}{p+1},\qquad\operatorname{Re}p>-1.}
$$

**Bromwich inversion.** The <Bromwich inversion formula> is
$$
y(t)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma-i\infty}^{\gamma+i\infty}\frac{e^{p(t-1)}}{p+1}\,dp,\qquad\gamma>-1,
$$
with the vertical <Bromwich contour> traversed upwards and its symmetric improper limit understood. For $t>1$, close to the left, where the exponential decays; the enclosed <simple pole> at $p=-1$ has <residue> $e^{-(t-1)}$. For $t<1$, close to the right, which contains no <pole>. Thus
$$
\boxed{y(t)=e^{-(t-1)}\Theta(t-1),}
$$
where $\Theta$ is the <Heaviside step function>. This agrees with the <Laplace transform time-shift rule>. At $t=1$, inversion returns the average $1/2$ of the two one-sided values. The value assigned at that single point does not affect the differential equation in the distributional sense.

**Fourier transform.** State the convention explicitly:
$$
\widetilde y(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}y(t)e^{-ikt}\,dt,\qquad y(t)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widetilde y(k)e^{ikt}\,dk.
$$
The <Fourier transform> of the distributional <derivative> is $ik\widetilde y(k)$, including the jump at $t=1$. The <Dirac delta> has transform $e^{-ik}$, giving
$$
\boxed{\widetilde y(k)=\frac{e^{-ik}}{1+ik}.}
$$

**Inverse Fourier integral.** Substitute into the inverse <Fourier transform>:
$$
y(t)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ik(t-1)}}{1+ik}\,dk.
$$
The only <pole> is at $k=i$. For $t>1$, close in the upper half-plane. <Jordan's lemma> removes the large arc and the <residue theorem> gives $i\operatorname{Res}_{k=i}=i[e^{-(t-1)}/i]=e^{-(t-1)}$. For $t<1$, close in the lower half-plane, where there are no <poles>, giving zero. This reproduces
$$
\boxed{y(t)=\begin{cases}0,&t<1,\\e^{1-t},&t>1,\end{cases}\qquad y(1)=\tfrac12\text{ under symmetric inversion}.}
$$
Integrating the differential equation across the impulse checks the unit jump, $y(1^+)-y(1^-)=1$. Away from the impulse the solution obeys $y'=-y$, and it satisfies the stipulated zero past.

**Tensor transformation.** Under an <orthogonal transformation> $R$, the magnetic moment and <magnetic field intensity> have transformed components $M'=RM$, $H'=RH$ for a proper rotation. The physical relation $M=\chi H$ must retain its form, giving $RM=\chi'RH$, hence
$$
\boxed{\chi'=R\chi R^T,\qquad\chi'_{ij}=R_{ia}R_{jb}\chi_{ab}.}
$$
This is precisely the law for a <Cartesian second-rank tensor>. Both magnetic quantities are <axial vectors>; under reflections they each acquire the same determinant sign, which cancels from the transformation of the <magnetic susceptibility tensor>.

**Parallel directions.** The condition $M=\lambda H$ is the <eigenvalue> equation $\chi H=\lambda H$. For the matrix in the paper, expansion of $\det(\chi-\lambda I)$ gives
$$
\det(\chi-\lambda I)=-\lambda(\lambda-1)(\lambda-6).
$$
Solving the three resulting homogeneous systems gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\lambda&\text{direction of }H&\text{normalized eigenvector}\\\hline
0&(1,-1,2)&e_0=(1,-1,2)/\sqrt6\\
1&(-2,0,1)&e_1=(-2,0,1)/\sqrt5\\
6&(1,5,2)&e_6=(1,5,2)/\sqrt{30}
\end{array}.}
$$
The three directions are mutually <orthogonal>. For the zero <eigenvalue>, the induced magnetic moment is zero: the proportionality equation still holds with factor zero, though there is then no nonzero moment direction to assign.

**Energy extrema.** The stipulated energy is $\mathcal E=H^T\chi H$. At fixed $H^TH=H_0^2>0$, a <Lagrange multiplier> calculation gives
$$
\nabla_H[H^T\chi H-\lambda(H^TH-H_0^2)]=2\chi H-2\lambda H=0,
$$
because $\chi$ is a real <symmetric matrix>. Thus all stationary directions are the <eigenvectors>, for which $M=\lambda H$. Equivalently write $H=q_0e_0+q_1e_1+q_6e_6$; then
$$
\mathcal E=q_1^2+6q_6^2,\qquad H_0^2=q_0^2+q_1^2+q_6^2.
$$
The <Rayleigh quotient> immediately gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E_{\min}=0\text{ on }H=\pm H_0e_0,\qquad\mathcal E_{\max}=6H_0^2\text{ on }H=\pm H_0e_6.}
$$
The intermediate direction $e_1$ is stationary with $\mathcal E=H_0^2$ but is not an extremum on the sphere. At the minimum $M=0$, so the precise extremum condition is proportionality, including a vanishing proportionality factor.

**Constant-energy surface.** In the original field components,
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E=(H_1+H_2)^2+(H_3+2H_2)^2=q_1^2+6q_6^2.}
$$
For a fixed positive value $E$, $q_0$ is unrestricted and the cross-section satisfies $q_1^2/E+q_6^2/(E/6)=1$. The surface is therefore an <elliptic cylinder>, with axis along $(1,-1,2)$ and transverse semiaxes $\sqrt E$ along $e_1$ and $\sqrt{E/6}$ along $e_6$. This is an example of <level sets of a positive semidefinite quadratic form>. At $E=0$ the surface degenerates to the axis line; no negative-energy level exists.

= Solution

**General definitions.** The <Euler-Lagrange equation> gives $m_i\ddot q_i+\sum_jV_{ij}q_j=0$. Write $M=\operatorname{diag}(m_i)$ and $K=(V_{ij})$. A <normal mode> has fixed displacement pattern $v$ and common time factor, $q(t)=vQ(t)$; its <normal frequency> satisfies
$$
\boxed{Kv=\omega^2Mv,\qquad\ddot Q+\omega^2Q=0.}
$$
Here $\omega$ is angular frequency. A <normal coordinate> is the coefficient of one pattern in a complete modal expansion, so these coordinates decouple the equations. Since $M$ is positive definite, the change $z=M^{1/2}q$ turns the mode equation into the ordinary symmetric <eigenvalue problem> for $M^{-1/2}KM^{-1/2}$.

**Three-mass equations.** Let $x_1,x_2,x_3$ be horizontal displacements from the unstretched equilibrium, ordered left to right. The <kinetic energy> and <potential energy> are
$$
T=\frac m2(\dot x_1^2+2\dot x_2^2+3\dot x_3^2),\qquad V=\frac k2(x_2-x_1)^2+\frac{3k}{2}(x_3-x_2)^2.
$$
Differentiating the <potential energy> gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
m\ddot x_1&=k(x_2-x_1),\\2m\ddot x_2&=k(x_1-x_2)+3k(x_3-x_2),\\3m\ddot x_3&=3k(x_2-x_3).
\end{aligned}}
$$
Thus $M=m\operatorname{diag}(1,2,3)$ and
$$
K=k\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0\\-1&4&-3\\0&-3&3\end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Frequencies and patterns.** Put $\lambda=m\omega^2/k$. Expansion of the <generalized eigenvalue problem for small oscillations> gives
$$
\det\left[\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0\\-1&4&-3\\0&-3&3\end{pmatrix}-\lambda\operatorname{diag}(1,2,3)\right]=-6\lambda(\lambda-1)(\lambda-3).
$$
Solving at each root gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
&v&\omega\\\hline
0&(1,1,1)^T&0\\
1&(-3,0,1)^T&\sqrt{k/m}\\
3&(1,-2,1)^T&\sqrt{3k/m}
\end{array}.}
$$
The zero <normal mode> is uniform <translation>. In the lower nonzero mode, the central mass stays fixed, the two end masses move oppositely, and their unequal displacements balance both the end-mass momenta and the forces on the central mass. In the upper mode the end masses move together and the centre oppositely. Both vibrating modes leave the <centre of mass> fixed.

**Orthogonality.** Because $K$ is symmetric, two mode equations imply
$$
v_r^TKv_s=\omega_s^2v_r^TMv_s=\omega_r^2v_r^TMv_s.
$$
Distinct <eigenvalues> therefore give the <mass-matrix orthogonality of normal modes>:
$$
\boxed{v_r^TMv_s=\sum_i m_i(v_r)_i(v_s)_i=0\quad(r\ne s).}
$$
The Euclidean dot product need not vanish for unequal masses; for example $v_0^Tv_1=-2$, while $v_0^TMv_1=m(-3+3)=0$. The modal squared norms are $6m,12m,12m$.

\Image[paper-2-unequal-chain-modes.png]
{title=The three normal modes of the unequal free mass chain}
{description=Masses from left to right are m, 2m and 3m, joined by springs k and 3k. Arrow lengths show the modal amplitude ratios (1,1,1), (-3,0,1), and (1,-2,1).}

**Normal coordinates.** Expand $x=Q_0v_0+Q_1v_1+Q_3v_3$. Project using the mass-weighted <inner product> and divide by each modal norm to obtain
$$
\boxed{Q_0=\frac{x_1+2x_2+3x_3}{6},\qquad Q_1=\frac{x_3-x_1}{4},\qquad Q_3=\frac{x_1-4x_2+3x_3}{12}.}
$$
For example, the inverse equations are $x_1=Q_0-3Q_1+Q_3$, $x_2=Q_0-2Q_3$, $x_3=Q_0+Q_1+Q_3$. They reduce the energies to
$$
T=\frac m2(6\dot Q_0^2+12\dot Q_1^2+12\dot Q_3^2),\qquad V=\frac k2(12Q_1^2+36Q_3^2),
$$
and hence
$$
\boxed{\ddot Q_0=0,\qquad\ddot Q_1+(k/m)Q_1=0,\qquad\ddot Q_3+(3k/m)Q_3=0.}
$$
The first coordinate is the <centre of mass> and has free linear motion. Multiplying $Q_0,Q_1,Q_3$ by $\sqrt{6m},\sqrt{12m},\sqrt{12m}$ respectively gives mass-normalized <normal coordinates> if desired.

**Cosets and Lagrange.** A <left coset> is $gH=\{gh:h\in H\}$. The <left cosets> of a <subgroup> partition a <group>, each with $|H|$ elements. <Lagrange's theorem> states $\boxed{|G|=[G:H]|H|}$ for a finite <group>, so every <subgroup> order divides the <group order>.

**The 64-element group.** Work in the <commutative ring> $R=\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$, which has <zero divisors>. Denote the displayed lower triangular matrix by $A(a,b,c)$, where $a,b,c$ are its three entries below the diagonal. Direct <matrix multiplication> gives
$$
\boxed{A(a,b,c)A(d,e,f)=A(a+d,b+e+cd,c+f).}
$$
All arithmetic is modulo four, so the set is closed. Associativity comes from <matrix multiplication>, the <identity element> is $A(0,0,0)$, and
$$
\boxed{A(a,b,c)^{-1}=A(-a,ca-b,-c).}
$$
The formula works in either multiplication order and requires no division. Hence these matrices form a <group> of order $\boxed{|G|=4^3=64}$. This is the lower triangular version of the <Heisenberg group over a commutative ring>.

**The abelian subgroup.** If $a=c$, write $h(a,b)=A(a,b,a)$. Then
$$
h(a,b)h(d,e)=h(a+d,b+e+ad)=h(d,e)h(a,b).
$$
The identity is $h(0,0)$ and the inverse is $h(-a,a^2-b)$, proving an <abelian subgroup>. Its two independent entries give $\boxed{|H|=16}$ and $\boxed{[G:H]=4}$, in accordance with <Lagrange's theorem>. This is the <abelian plane subgroup of the Heisenberg group modulo four>.

More explicitly, $A(a,b,c)\mapsto a-c$ is a <surjective group homomorphism> to the additive group of $R$, with kernel $H$. The four <left cosets> are exactly the four sets with fixed $a-c=t$, $t=0,1,2,3$; representatives are $A(t,0,0)$. Thus $H$ is also a <normal subgroup>.

**Matrices squaring to the identity.** The product formula gives
$$
A(a,b,c)^2=A(2a,2b+ca,2c).
$$
The first and third coordinates vanish only when $a,c\in\{0,2\}$. Then $ca=0$ modulo four, so the middle coordinate vanishes exactly when $b\in\{0,2\}$. Therefore the complete set is
$$
\boxed{K=\{A(a,b,c):a,b,c\in\{0,2\}\},\qquad |K|=8.}
$$
It is a <subgroup>: multiplying two such elements keeps all three entries even, and their cross term $cd$ vanishes modulo four, so the product simply adds the three even coordinates. The identity is included and each element is its own inverse. In fact $K$ is isomorphic to the <direct product of groups> $C_2\times C_2\times C_2$. The seven nonidentity elements are <involutions>.

Finally, the last subset in the PDF is defined by $a\ne0\ne b$, with no restriction on $c$. It has $3\cdot3\cdot4=36$ elements, which cannot be a <subgroup> because $36$ does not divide $64$. It also excludes the <identity element>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\{A(a,b,c):a\ne0,\ b\ne0\}\text{ is not a subgroup}.}
$$

**Definitions.** A <group homomorphism> $\varphi:G_1\to G_2$ preserves multiplication, $\varphi(xy)=\varphi(x)\varphi(y)$. A <group isomorphism> is a bijective <group homomorphism>. The <kernel of a group homomorphism> is
$$
\boxed{K=\ker\varphi=\{x\in G_1:\varphi(x)=e_2\}.}
$$
Preserving multiplication implies $\varphi(e_1)=e_2$ and $\varphi(x^{-1})=\varphi(x)^{-1}$: the first follows by cancelling $\varphi(e_1)$ from its square, and the second follows by applying $\varphi$ to $xx^{-1}=e_1$.

**Kernel subgroup.** The identity belongs to $K$. For $x,y\in K$, $\varphi(xy)=e_2e_2=e_2$, so $xy\in K$. Also $\varphi(x^{-1})=e_2^{-1}=e_2$, so $x^{-1}\in K$. Thus $\boxed{K\leq G_1}$.

**Normality.** For $g\in G_1$ and $k\in K$,
$$
\varphi(gkg^{-1})=\varphi(g)\varphi(k)\varphi(g)^{-1}=\varphi(g)e_2\varphi(g)^{-1}=e_2.
$$
Hence every $gkg^{-1}$ is again in $K$, proving $\boxed{K\trianglelefteq G_1}$. Applying the same statement with $g^{-1}$ gives equality $gKg^{-1}=K$, and therefore $gK=Kg$.

**Group of cosets.** Define the product on the <left cosets> by
$$
\boxed{(aK)(bK)=(ab)K.}
$$
To check that this does not depend on representatives, replace $a,b$ by $ak_1,bk_2$. Then
$$
(ak_1)(bk_2)=ab(b^{-1}k_1b)k_2\in abK,
$$
because normality puts $b^{-1}k_1b$ in $K$. Thus the product is well defined. It is associative because multiplication in $G_1$ is associative; its identity is $K=e_1K$, and the inverse of $aK$ is $a^{-1}K$. Consequently the cosets form the <quotient group> $\boxed{G_1/K}$.

The map $aK\mapsto\varphi(a)$ is well defined and gives an isomorphism $G_1/K\cong\operatorname{im}\varphi$. If the original mapping is onto $G_2$, this becomes $G_1/K\cong G_2$. No surjectivity is needed for the <kernel of a group homomorphism> to be a <normal subgroup> or for the <quotient group> to exist.

**Representations.** A complex <group representation> is a <group homomorphism> $\rho:G\to\operatorname{GL}(W)$ to the invertible linear maps on a vector space. It is an <irreducible representation> when $W\ne0$ and the only invariant subspaces are $0$ and $W$.

**Group table.** The supplied matrices satisfy $A=-I$, $C=-B$, and $A^2=B^2=C^2=I$. Their <multiplication table> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|rrrr}
&I&A&B&C\\\hline
I&I&A&B&C\\
A&A&I&C&B\\
B&B&C&I&A\\
C&C&B&A&I
\end{array}.}
$$
The table proves closure and gives identity $I$ and an inverse for every element; associativity is inherited from <matrix multiplication>. The table is symmetric, so the <group> is abelian. It is the <Klein four-group> $V\cong C_2\times C_2$.

**Dimensions and conjugacy.** The <sum of squares of irreducible representation dimensions> is $|V|=4$. A two-dimensional irreducible would alone contribute four, leaving no room for the ever-present one-dimensional <trivial representation>. Hence the given two-dimensional representation is reducible. Since the <group> is abelian, every <conjugacy class> is a singleton. There are therefore four inequivalent complex <irreducible representations>, each of dimension one:
$$
\boxed{\text{classes }\{I\},\{A\},\{B\},\{C\};\qquad\text{irreducible dimensions }1,1,1,1.}
$$
Taking the <trace> of the given matrices gives their <character of a representation>:
$$
\boxed{\chi_{\rm given}(I,A,B,C)=(2,-2,0,0).}
$$

**Irreducible characters.** In a one-dimensional representation, the values on $A$ and $B$ must each be $\pm1$, since both elements square to $I$. Their values determine the value on $C=AB$. The four choices give the complete <character table>:
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|rrrr}
&I&A&B&C\\\hline
\chi_1&1&1&1&1\\
\chi_2&1&1&-1&-1\\
\chi_3&1&-1&1&-1\\
\chi_4&1&-1&-1&1
\end{array}.}
$$
Each row is a character because it respects the generating relations, and the rows have unit norm and pairwise zero <character inner product>. The multiplicities of these rows in $(2,-2,0,0)$ are respectively $0,0,1,1$, by <character orthogonality>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\rho_{\rm given}\cong\rho_3\oplus\rho_4.}
$$

**Diagonal form.** The common invariant lines are spanned by $(1,1)^T$ and $(1,-1)^T$. Use their normalized vectors as columns of
$$
U=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad U^{-1}=U^T=U.
$$
Then simultaneous <diagonalization> gives
$$
\boxed{U^TIU=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad U^TAU=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad U^TBU=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad U^TCU=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Reading the first and second diagonal entries across the four matrices recovers $\chi_3$ and $\chi_4$, respectively, verifying the decomposition explicitly.

**Fourth-order spatial formula.** For a sufficiently smooth function, pair the <Taylor expansions> at opposite shifts:
$$
\begin{aligned}
u(x+h)+u(x-h)&=2u+h^2u''+\frac{h^4}{12}u^{(4)}+\frac{h^6}{360}u^{(6)}+O(h^8),\\
u(x+2h)+u(x-2h)&=2u+4h^2u''+\frac{4h^4}{3}u^{(4)}+\frac{8h^6}{45}u^{(6)}+O(h^8).
\end{aligned}
$$
Multiply the first sum by sixteen, subtract the second, and subtract $30u(x)$. The constant and fourth-derivative terms cancel, leaving
$$
-u(x+2h)+16u(x+h)-30u(x)+16u(x-h)-u(x-2h)=12h^2u''-\frac{2h^6}{15}u^{(6)}+O(h^8).
$$
Hence the <fourth-order centered second derivative> satisfies
$$
\boxed{D_{4,h}u(x)=\frac{-u(x+2h)+16u(x+h)-30u(x)+16u(x-h)-u(x-2h)}{12h^2}=u''-\frac{h^4}{90}u^{(6)}+O(h^6).}
$$
Rearranging proves the requested $u''=D_{4,h}u+O(h^4)$, including its leading error coefficient.

**Explicit diffusion scheme.** Take $x_j=jh$, $h=1/N$, $t_n=n\tau$, and let $U_j^n$ approximate $\psi(x_j,t_n)$. Combining the <forward Euler method> in time with $D_{4,h}$ in space gives
$$
\boxed{U_j^{n+1}=U_j^n+\frac{\tau}{12h^2}\left(-U_{j+2}^n+16U_{j+1}^n-30U_j^n+16U_{j-1}^n-U_{j-2}^n\right),\qquad U_j^0=f(jh).}
$$
To check the error, insert the exact solution. The <heat equation> gives $\psi_t=\psi_{xx}$, while the time <Taylor expansion> and the spatial formula give the one-step defect
$$
\psi(x_j,t_n+\tau)-\psi(x_j,t_n)-\tau D_{4,h}\psi(x_j,t_n)=\frac{\tau^2}{2}\psi_{tt}+\frac{\tau h^4}{90}\psi_{xxxxxx}+O(\tau^3+\tau h^6).
$$
Thus its unnormalized <local truncation error> is
$$
\boxed{O(\tau^2)+O(\tau h^4),}
$$
as requested. Dividing this defect by $\tau$ gives the normalized residual $O(\tau)+O(h^4)$; these are distinct error conventions.

**Boundary implementation.** The centered stencil requires two ghost values at each end. An <even-reflection closure for Neumann diffusion> imposes
$$
\boxed{U_{-1}^n=U_1^n,\quad U_{-2}^n=U_2^n,\quad U_{N+1}^n=U_{N-1}^n,\quad U_{N+2}^n=U_{N-2}^n.}
$$
The reflected first centered difference at either endpoint is zero. Apply the same evolution formula at the endpoints after substituting these ghost values; for example,
$$
U_0^{n+1}=U_0^n+\frac{\tau}{12h^2}(-2U_2^n+32U_1^n-30U_0^n).
$$
The ghost values also close the stencil at $j=1$ and $j=N-1$. They are determined auxiliary values, not new physical data. For a smooth solution compatible with the <Neumann boundary conditions>, differentiating those conditions in time and using the <heat equation> gives $\psi_{xxx}=\psi_{xxxxx}=\cdots=0$ at the endpoints. Thus even reflection preserves the smoothness needed for fourth-order spatial accuracy. If the initial data fail the required higher compatibility conditions, a uniform smooth error expansion through $t=0$ cannot be assumed.

For completeness, the reflected spatial operator has cosine <eigenvectors> $\cos(\ell\pi j/N)$ and <eigenvalues>
$$
\lambda_\ell=-\frac4{h^2}\sin^2\frac{\ell\pi}{2N}\left(1+\frac13\sin^2\frac{\ell\pi}{2N}\right).
$$
The most negative one is $-16/(3h^2)$. Requiring $|1+\tau\lambda_\ell|\leq1$ gives the <forward Euler method> stability restriction $\boxed{\tau/h^2\leq3/8}$ in the natural trapezoid-weighted discrete norm. A fourth-order spatial stencil does not remove the explicit diffusion time-step restriction.

**Implicit alternative.** The <Crank-Nicolson diffusion scheme> using the same spatial operator and boundary closure is
$$
\boxed{\left(I-\tfrac\tau2D_{4,h}\right)U^{n+1}=\left(I+\tfrac\tau2D_{4,h}\right)U^n.}
$$
It has second-order time accuracy and fourth-order spatial accuracy for smooth compatible solutions; its one-step defect is $O(\tau^3)+O(\tau h^4)$. Each spatial <eigenvalue> $\lambda\leq0$ has amplification factor $(1+\tau\lambda/2)/(1-\tau\lambda/2)$ of magnitude at most one, so it removes the explicit restriction for this boundary closure.

The price is solving a banded linear system at every step; the same matrix factorization can be reused for fixed $h$ and $\tau$. Large time steps still reduce accuracy. Moreover, the amplification factor tends to $-1$ as $\tau|\lambda|\to\infty$, so short spatial wavelengths can alternate sign with little numerical damping. Thus unconditional linear stability does not guarantee a monotone or well-resolved transient. Boundary accuracy and initial compatibility remain necessary with either time discretization.
