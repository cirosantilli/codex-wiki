= Solution
{parent=i}

Using the <Levi-Civita symbol> and the <Einstein summation convention> in <Cartesian coordinates>, the <product rule> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla\cdot(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)
&=\partial_i(\epsilon_{ijk}A_jB_k)\\
&=\epsilon_{ijk}(\partial_iA_j)B_k+\epsilon_{ijk}A_j\partial_iB_k\\
&=B_k\epsilon_{kij}\partial_iA_j-A_j\epsilon_{jik}\partial_iB_k.
\end{aligned}
$$
The cyclic permutation in the first term preserves the sign, whereas exchanging the first two indices in the second reverses it. Recognizing the two <curls> proves
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)=\mathbf B\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf A)-\mathbf A\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B).}
$$

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <divergence theorem> states that a continuously differentiable <vector field> $\mathbf F$ on a volume $V$ with piecewise smooth closed boundary $S$ satisfies
$$
\int_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV=\int_S\mathbf F\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,dS,
$$
where $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is the outward <unit normal>. Take $\mathbf F=\nabla a\times\mathbf B$. Since the <curl of a gradient> is zero for a twice continuously differentiable <scalar field>, part (i) gives
$$
\nabla\cdot(\nabla a\times\mathbf B)=-\nabla a\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B).
$$
Integrating this identity and applying the <divergence theorem> proves
$$
\boxed{\int_V\nabla a\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)\,dV=-\int_S(\nabla a\times\mathbf B)\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,dS.}
$$

= iii
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Direct <differentiation> gives
$$
\nabla a=(y,x,2z),\qquad \nabla\times\mathbf B=(y,-1,-1),\qquad \nabla a\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)=y^2-x-2z.
$$
On the unit disk $D$, <polar coordinates> give $\int_Dy^2\,dA=\pi/4$, $\int_Dx\,dA=0$ and $\int_DdA=\pi$. Consequently the <volume integral> is
$$
\int_0^h\left(\frac\pi4-2\pi z\right)dz=\boxed{\frac{\pi h}{4}-\pi h^2}.
$$
For an independent <surface integral> calculation, the relevant <cross product> is
$$
\mathbf F=\nabla a\times\mathbf B=(x^2+2yz^2,\ 2yz-xy,\ -y^2z-xy).
$$
The bottom face contributes $\int_Dxy\,dA=0$ and the top contributes $\int_D(-hy^2-xy)\,dA=-\pi h/4$. On the cylindrical side, put $x=\cos\theta$, $y=\sin\theta$, $\hat{\mathbf n}=(x,y,0)$ and $dS=d\theta\,dz$. The <flux> density is $x^3+2xyz^2+2y^2z-xy^2$. All terms except $2y^2z$ integrate to zero because they are <odd functions> of $x$ or $y$, giving
$$
\int_{\mathrm{side}}\mathbf F\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,dS=\int_0^h2\pi z\,dz=\pi h^2.
$$
Thus minus the total outward <flux> is $-(\pi h^2-\pi h/4)$, in agreement with the <volume integral>.

For <separation of variables>, take $u(x,t)=X(x)T(t)$ in the <damped wave equation>. Dividing by $c^2XT$, with $c>0$, gives $X''/X=(T''+\alpha T')/(c^2T)=-k^2$. The separated <ordinary differential equations> are therefore
$$
\boxed{X''+k^2X=0,\qquad T''+\alpha T'+c^2k^2T=0.}
$$
The negative separation constant anticipates the positive spatial <eigenvalues> selected by the fixed endpoints.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

An <even function> satisfying the spatial equation has $X=C\cos(kx)$. The <Dirichlet boundary conditions> require $\cos(kL)=0$, so the allowed <wavenumbers> are
$$
k_n=\frac{n\pi}{2L},\qquad n=1,3,5,\ldots.
$$
There is no nonzero zero-wavenumber mode. With $T=e^{-\alpha t/2}S$, the time equation becomes $S''+\omega_n^2S=0$, where $\omega_n^2=c^2k_n^2-\alpha^2/4$. Thus <superposition> of the even <normal modes> gives
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=\sum_{\substack{n\geq1\\ n\ {
m odd}}}e^{-\alpha t/2}\cos(k_nx)\operatorname{Re}\left(A_ne^{i\omega_nt}+B_ne^{-i\omega_nt}\right).}
$$
This uses completeness of the even <Fourier series> modes. Imaginary $\omega_n$ describe <overdamping>. At <critical damping>, $\omega_n=0$, the two displayed exponentials coincide: the complete time factor must instead be $e^{-\alpha t/2}(C_n+D_nt)$. This repeated-root exception is necessary for a genuinely general solution.

= iii
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The <angular frequency> of an underdamped mode is
$$
\boxed{\omega_n=\sqrt{\frac{c^2n^2\pi^2}{4L^2}-\frac{\alpha^2}{4}}.}
$$
For ordinary <damping>, $\alpha\geq0$, mode $n$ oscillates precisely when $\alpha<cn\pi/L$. In particular, **all modes, including the fundamental, oscillate when $\alpha<c\pi/L$**. There are some oscillatory modes for every finite $\alpha$, since the allowed $n$ are unbounded; no finite damping coefficient overdamps the entire ideal string spectrum.

If $\alpha<0$, the same mode has negative <damping>: oscillatory amplitudes grow as $e^{|\alpha|t/2}$. For modes with real characteristic roots, both roots are positive, since $|\alpha|/2>\sqrt{\alpha^2/4-c^2k_n^2}$. Hence those modes also grow. Negative damping makes the equilibrium unstable.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Let $d_n$ be the even <Fourier coefficient> of the initial displacement, with the normalization $\int_{-L}^L\cos^2(k_nx)dx=L$. Writing $s_n=\sin(n\pi/2)=(-1)^{(n-1)/2}$ gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
d_n&=\frac2L\int_0^Le^{-x/l}\cos(k_nx)\,dx\\
&=\boxed{\frac{2l}{L}\frac{1+k_nl\,s_ne^{-L/l}}{1+(k_nl)^2}}\qquad(n\text{ odd}).
\end{aligned}
$$
Zero initial velocity requires the real modal time factor
$$
T_n(t)=d_ne^{-\alpha t/2}\left[\cos(\omega_nt)+\frac{\alpha}{2\omega_n}\sin(\omega_nt)\right].
$$
One consistent choice in the question's complex representation is therefore
$$
\boxed{A_n=\frac{d_n}{2}\left(1-\frac{i\alpha}{2\omega_n}\right),\qquad B_n=\frac{d_n}{2}\left(1+\frac{i\alpha}{2\omega_n}\right).}
$$
For real $\omega_n$, $B_n=\overline{A_n}$; the real-part notation otherwise makes the complex coefficients nonunique. For imaginary $\omega_n$, the same formula is interpreted using <hyperbolic functions>. Its limit at $\omega_n=0$ is $d_ne^{-\alpha t/2}(1+\alpha t/2)$.

For $l/L\to0$ at fixed mode number,
$$
\boxed{d_n\simeq\frac{2l/L}{1+(n\pi l/2L)^2},}
$$
which can be substituted into $A_n,B_n$ above. The profile specified at $t=0$ is actually nonzero at $x=\pm L$. Thus for finite $l/L$ it cannot be the initial data of a solution continuous at the initial endpoint corners with fixed endpoints. The <Fourier series> reproduces it in the open interval, with zero endpoint values; it is an <L2 space> initial-data interpretation. The assumption $l\ll L$ makes this compatibility error exponentially small, rather than exactly zero.

= Solution

The substitution $y=e^xg$ reduces the <homogeneous linear differential equation> to
$$
g''+\left(1-\frac1x\right)g'=0.
$$
A first <integration> gives $g'=Cxe^{-x}$, and a second gives $g=D-C(x+1)e^{-x}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{y=Ae^x+B(1+x).}
$$
The <Wronskian> of $1+x,e^x$ is $xe^x$, so they are <linearly independent> on either side of zero. At zero the differential equation has a <regular singular point>. The displayed solutions extend analytically through it; their linear combinations describe the global solutions when two continuous derivatives are required there. If no matching condition is imposed, the two sides have independent constants.

= ii
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Use the boundary-adapted <homogeneous solutions>
$$
u(x)=1+x,\qquad v(x)=e^x-\frac e2(1+x),\qquad W(x)=uv'-u'v=xe^x.
$$
For a source point $\xi\ne0$, the <Green function> is
$$
\boxed{G(x,\xi)=\frac1{\xi e^\xi}\begin{cases}u(x)v(\xi),&x\leq\xi,\\u(\xi)v(x),&x\geq\xi.\end{cases}}
$$
It vanishes at both endpoints and is continuous at $x=\xi$. Its first <derivative> has the jump
$$
G_x(\xi^+,\xi)-G_x(\xi^-,\xi)=\frac{u(\xi)v'(\xi)-u'(\xi)v(\xi)}{W(\xi)}=1,
$$
as required for a monic second-order <differential operator> with a <Dirac delta> source. Away from that source it is a <homogeneous solution> and extends smoothly through $x=0$.

**The interior singular point requires a qualification.** This is a <Green kernel with a vanishing Wronskian at an interior singular point>, with a $1/\xi$ singularity in its source variable. It is not an ordinary integral inverse for unrestricted forcing. For smooth forcing $f(\xi)=\xi q(\xi)$, however, the product $Gf$ extends continuously at zero and the integral is well defined. This includes part (iii). We use solutions with two continuous derivatives across zero, interpreting the equation there after multiplication by $x$. For such a solution, expansion at zero forces $f(0)=0$; arbitrary nonzero forcing at zero cannot satisfy this regularity. Merely imposing one continuous derivative would leave a piecewise homogeneous ambiguity, so the original singular coefficients alone do not specify an unrestricted boundary-value inverse.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The source factor cancels the singular denominator of the <Green function>. Splitting the <integral> at $\xi=x$ gives
$$
y(x)=v(x)\int_{-1}^x(1+\xi)e^{-\xi}d\xi+(1+x)\int_x^1\left[1-\frac e2(1+\xi)e^{-\xi}\right]d\xi.
$$
Using $\int(1+\xi)e^{-\xi}d\xi=-(\xi+2)e^{-\xi}$, these two integrals are $e-(x+2)e^{-x}$ and $5/2-x-e(x+2)e^{-x}/2$, respectively. Simplification gives
$$
\boxed{y(x)=e^{x+1}+\frac{1-e^2}{2}(1+x)-x^2.}
$$
The exponential and linear terms are homogeneous solutions, while direct <differentiation> gives $L(-x^2)=x$ for $x\ne0$. Also $y(-1)=y(1)=0$, and the expression is analytic through zero. Uniqueness in this regular class follows because any difference is a global <linear combination> $Ae^x+B(1+x)$: the left boundary forces $A=0$, and the right then forces $B=0$.

First take $f,g\in L^1(\mathbb R)$, so <Fubini's theorem> applies to the <cross-correlation> double integral. With $s=x+y$, the <Fourier transform> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat h(k)&=\int_{\mathbb R}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-ikx}\overline{f(y)}g(x+y)\,dy\,dx\\
&=\left[\int_{\mathbb R}\overline{f(y)}e^{iky}\,dy\right]\left[\int_{\mathbb R}g(s)e^{-iks}\,ds\right]\\
&=\boxed{\overline{\widehat f(k)}\,\widehat g(k)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The <complex conjugation> and the sign in $x+y$ are both essential to this convention.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Set $g=f$. The <cross-correlation> at zero is $h(0)=\int_{\mathbb R}|f(x)|^2dx$, while part (i) gives $\widehat h=|\widehat f|^2$. Evaluating the <inverse Fourier transform> at zero yields the <Parseval identity>
$$
\boxed{\int_{\mathbb R}|f(x)|^2dx=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}|\widehat f(k)|^2dk.}
$$
This derivation is immediate for <Schwartz functions>, for which all integrations and <Fourier inversion> are justified. Approximation by such functions and completeness then extend the identity to <L2 space>, defining its <Fourier transform> by the corresponding norm limit. This is the <Plancherel theorem> extension; no absolute integrability of an arbitrary square-integrable function is presumed.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The direct squared <L2 norm> is the length of the interval, namely $2$. The <Fourier transform> is
$$
\widehat f(k)=\int_{-1}^1e^{-ikx}dx=\frac{2\sin k}{k},\qquad \widehat f(0)=2.
$$
For the transformed norm, let $I=\int_0^\infty\sin^2k/k^2\,dk$. <Integration by parts>, with vanishing endpoint term $-\sin^2k/k$, gives
$$
I=2\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin k\cos k}{k}\,dk=\frac\pi2.
$$
The supplied oscillatory integral is understood as an <improper integral>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|\frac{2\sin k}{k}\right|^2dk=\frac4\pi I=2,}
$$
which agrees with the direct norm and verifies the <Parseval identity> with the specified normalization.

Taking the <conjugate transpose> of $MM^{-1}=I$ and $M^{-1}M=I$ reverses the product order and gives
$$
(M^{-1})^\dagger M^\dagger=I,\qquad M^\dagger(M^{-1})^\dagger=I.
$$
Thus $(M^{-1})^\dagger$ is the two-sided <matrix inverse> of $M^\dagger$, proving
$$
\boxed{(M^\dagger)^{-1}=(M^{-1})^\dagger.}
$$

= ii
{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Since $A$ is <skew-Hermitian>, $H=iA$ is a <Hermitian matrix>. The <spectral theorem> gives a <unitary matrix> $Q$ with $Q^\dagger HQ=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_N)$ and real <eigenvalues> $\lambda_j$. Hence $Q^\dagger AQ=\operatorname{diag}(-i\lambda_j)$ and
$$
\boxed{|\det(I+A)|^2=\prod_{j=1}^N|1-i\lambda_j|^2=\prod_{j=1}^N(1+\lambda_j^2)\geq1.}
$$
In particular the <determinant> is nonzero, so $I+A$ is invertible. Applying the same argument to $-A$ proves that $I-A$ is also invertible. Equality holds precisely when all $\lambda_j=0$, equivalently $A=0$.

= iii
{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Using part (i) and $A^\dagger=-A$ in the <Cayley transform (Lie theory)> with argument $-A$ gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
U^\dagger&=[(I+A)^{-1}]^\dagger(I-A)^\dagger\\
&=(I-A)^{-1}(I+A)\\
&=(I+A)(I-A)^{-1}=U^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The factors commute because $I-A$ and $I+A$ are polynomials in the same <matrix>, and an invertible commuting matrix also commutes with the other factor's <matrix inverse>. Both inverses exist by part (ii). Thus **$U$ is unitary**.

= iv
{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Multiplying the defining equation on the right by $I+A$ gives $(I+U)A=I-U$. Consequently the only possible answer is
$$
\boxed{A=(I+U)^{-1}(I-U)=(I-U)(I+U)^{-1}.}
$$
The factors commute because they are rational functions of $U$. Its <conjugate transpose>, using the <unitarity> $U^\dagger=U^{-1}$, is
$$
\begin{aligned}
A^\dagger&=(I+U^{-1})^{-1}(I-U^{-1})\\
&=(I+U)^{-1}U\bigl[-U^{-1}(I-U)\bigr]=-A.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus $A$ is <skew-Hermitian>. Moreover $I+A=2(I+U)^{-1}$ and $I-A=2U(I+U)^{-1}$, so substitution recovers $U$ and establishes existence as well as uniqueness. The <unitary matrix> $U=-I$ is a counterexample to invertibility of $I+U$; more generally the obstruction is an <eigenvalue> $-1$.

For a <skew-symmetric matrix>, $M^T=-M$. Invariance of the <determinant> under <transpose> gives

\boxed{\det M=\det M^T=\det(-M)=(-1)^n\det M.}

Since $M$ is real, $(M^2)^T=(M^T)^2=(-M)^2=M^2$. For every real <vector> $x$,
$$
\boxed{x^TM^2x=-(Mx)^T(Mx)=-\|Mx\|^2\leq0.}
$$
Thus $M^2$ is a real <symmetric matrix> and is <negative semidefinite>. When $n$ is odd, part (i) supplies a nonzero $x$ in the <kernel> of $M$, and then $M^2x=0$. Hence zero is an <eigenvalue> of $M^2$. In fact $\ker M^2=\ker M$, as the displayed norm identity shows.

{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Choose $e_1$ real, as permitted by the <spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices>. Matrix powers commute, so
$$
M^2(Me_1)=M(M^2e_1)=-\mu_1^2Me_1.
$$
The proposed partner $e_2=Me_1$ is nonzero because
$$
\|e_2\|^2=-e_1^TM^2e_1=\mu_1^2\|e_1\|^2>0.
$$
Also the real scalar $e_1^TMe_1$ equals its own transpose $e_1^TM^Te_1=-e_1^TMe_1$, so it vanishes. Therefore **$e_1$ and $e_2$ are orthogonal eigenvectors with the same eigenvalue**. This is the basic pairing in the <paired eigenspaces of the square of a real skew-symmetric matrix>.

= iv
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Normalize $e_1$ and put $f_1=Me_1/\mu_1$. The two vectors are an <orthonormal basis> of a plane $E$ invariant under $M$, since $Me_1=\mu_1f_1$ and $Mf_1=-\mu_1e_1$. Its <orthogonal complement> is also invariant: for $w\perp E$ and $z\in E$,
$$
\langle Mw,z\rangle=-\langle w,Mz\rangle=0.
$$
Apply the <spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices> to the restriction of $M^2$ to $E^\perp$. Any remaining negative <eigenvalue> supplies another orthonormal pair by the same construction. Induction leaves only the <kernel>. Thus **every nonzero eigenvalue of $M^2$ has even multiplicity**. The pairs can share an eigenvalue, so this does not assert distinctness of the different $-\mu_j^2$.

Collect the paired <orthonormal bases> from part (iv), followed by an orthonormal basis of $\ker M^2=\ker M$. The matrix $Q$ of these columns is an <orthogonal matrix>, and
$$
\boxed{Q^TMQ=\bigoplus_{j=1}^r\begin{pmatrix}0&-\mu_j\\\mu_j&0\end{pmatrix}\ \oplus\ 0_{n-2r},\qquad \mu_j>0.}

Indeed, each pair obeys $Me_j=\mu_jf_j$, $Mf_j=-\mu_je_j$, and all kernel vectors are annihilated. This proves the real block form of a <skew-symmetric matrix> directly, including repeated block parameters. In particular its <rank> is $2r$, always even.

For a <holomorphic function>, the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> are $u_x=v_y$ and $u_y=-v_x$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\nabla u\cdot\nabla v=u_xv_x+u_yv_y=0,\qquad |\nabla u|^2=u_x^2+u_y^2=v_x^2+v_y^2=|\nabla v|^2.}
$$
A <gradient> is normal to its <level set>, so these identities give orthogonal level curves wherever the gradients are nonzero. At a critical point where $f'=0$, both gradients vanish and a regular tangent to a <level set> need not exist; the geometric orthogonality statement is restricted to regular intersections.

= Solution

Differentiate the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> and use equality of mixed <partial derivatives>:
$$
\boxed{\Delta u=u_{xx}+u_{yy}=(v_y)_x-(v_x)_y=0.}
$$
Thus the real part is a <harmonic function>. The same calculation gives $\Delta v=0$. Every <holomorphic function> has the required smoothness.

= Solution

Inverting $u+iv=\operatorname{arcosh}z$ gives $z=\cosh(u+iv)$, hence the <elliptic coordinates>
$$
x=\cosh u\cos v,\qquad y=\sinh u\sin v.
$$
Eliminating $v$ at fixed $u>0$ gives the <ellipse>
$$
\boxed{\frac{x^2}{\cosh^2u}+\frac{y^2}{\sinh^2u}=1.}
$$
Its focal distance is $\sqrt{\cosh^2u-\sinh^2u}=1$. At fixed $v$, eliminating $u$ instead gives the <hyperbola>
$$
\boxed{\frac{x^2}{\cos^2v}-\frac{y^2}{\sin^2v}=1.}
$$
Its focal distance is also one, so both families have foci $(\pm1,0)$. A fixed $v$ traces an appropriate half of the hyperbola; complementary coordinate values complete it. Degenerate values give the focal segment and portions of the coordinate axes. Away from these degeneracies the curves are orthogonal by part (i), or by $\mathbf r_u\cdot\mathbf r_v=0$ in the displayed parametrization. A branch of the inverse is used locally; its real part with $u\geq0$ is single-valued outside the focal segment.

= Solution

On the specified <ellipse>, the semiaxes are $\sqrt2$ and $1$, so its elliptic coordinate is $u_0=\operatorname{arcosh}\sqrt2=\log(\sqrt2+1)$. Thus in the exterior,
$$
\boxed{\phi=\operatorname{Re}\log\left(z+\sqrt{z^2-1}\right)-\log(\sqrt2+1)}
$$
is a <harmonic function> with a homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary condition> on that ellipse. Choose the square root with $\sqrt{z^2-1}\sim z$ at infinity. Although a global complex logarithm requires a cut, its real part $\log|z+\sqrt{z^2-1}|$ is well defined throughout the exterior and gives the same $\phi$.

Expanding at large $|z|$ yields $z+\sqrt{z^2-1}=2z[1-1/(4z^2)+O(|z|^{-4})]$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\phi=\log\frac{2\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}{\sqrt2+1}+O\bigl((x^2+y^2)^{-1}\bigr).}
$$
In particular the potential grows logarithmically in the distance, in every direction at infinity. It is the exterior solution; no <harmonic> continuation through the focal segment in the ellipse's interior is claimed.

Use the <Wirtinger derivatives> $\partial_z=(\partial_x-i\partial_y)/2$ and $\partial_{\bar z}=(\partial_x+i\partial_y)/2$, for which $\Delta=4\partial_z\partial_{\bar z}$. Since $H$ and $G$ are <holomorphic>,

Substitute $y=vP_n$ into the <Legendre differential equation> and expand using the <product rule>. The terms proportional to $v$ cancel because $P_n$ is itself a solution. What remains is
$$
\boxed{(1-x^2)P_nv''+\left[2(1-x^2)P_n'-2xP_n\right]v'=0.}
$$
Multiplying by $P_n$ identifies an exact <derivative>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dx}\left[(1-x^2)P_n^2v'\right]=0.}
$$
The latter form is convenient for <reduction of order> on intervals where $P_n\ne0$ and $x\ne\pm1$.

= Solution

Two integrations give the general <reduction of order> formula
$$
\boxed{v(x)=C_1+C_2\int^x\frac{dt}{(1-t^2)P_n(t)^2},\qquad y(x)=P_n(x)\left[C_1+C_2\int^x\frac{dt}{(1-t^2)P_n(t)^2}\right].}
$$
The integral is local on intervals avoiding zeros of $P_n$. A pole of the integral at an interior polynomial zero need not be a singularity of $y$: the product extends by <reduction of order across a zero of the known solution>. After a standard normalization the independent second solution is the <Legendre function of the second kind> $Q_n$.

= v
{parent=8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=v}

For normalized <Legendre polynomials>, $P_n(1)=1$ and $P_n(-1)=(-1)^n$. Near the endpoints the integral factor has the asymptotics
$$
\int^x\frac{dt}{(1-t^2)P_n(t)^2}=-\frac12\log|1-x|+O(1)\quad(x\to1),
$$
and
$$
\int^x\frac{dt}{(1-t^2)P_n(t)^2}=\frac12\log|1+x|+O(1)\quad(x\to-1).
$$
Thus an independent solution, for which $C_2\ne0$, has the logarithmic endpoint singularities
$$
\boxed{y=-\frac{C_2}{2}\log|1-x|+O(1)\quad(x\to1),\qquad y=\frac{(-1)^nC_2}{2}\log|1+x|+O(1)\quad(x\to-1).}
$$
The constants are understood for a solution continued consistently across the ordinary interior points. Adding any multiple of $P_n$ cannot cancel these logarithms. This explains why boundedness at an endpoint selects the <Legendre polynomial> and excludes the independent <Legendre function of the second kind>.

{parent=8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=vi}

Write $P_n(x)\sim c_nx^n$ with $c_n\ne0$. For $n\geq1$, this branch grows. Choosing the other <reduction of order> solution by an integral from infinity gives, on $x>1$,
$$
P_n(x)\int_\infty^x\frac{dt}{(1-t^2)P_n(t)^2}\sim\frac{x^{-n-1}}{c_n(2n+1)}.
$$
Thus **for $n\geq1$, every bounded exterior solution decays as $|x|^{-n-1}$**, up to a constant factor, on each exterior interval. Equivalently the dominant-balance exponents in the <Legendre differential equation> are $n$ and $-n-1$.

There is an important exception at degree zero. Since $P_0=1$,
$$
\boxed{y=C_1+\frac{C_2}{2}\log\left|\frac{x+1}{x-1}\right|=C_1+\frac{C_2}{x}+O(|x|^{-3})\quad(n=0).}
$$
These solutions are bounded even when $C_1\ne0$; boundedness alone does not then imply decay to zero. The constants on $x>1$ and $x<-1$ are independent unless continuation across the singular endpoints is specified.

For a variation $y+\varepsilon\eta$ with $\eta(a)=\eta(b)=0$, the first variation of the <functional> is
$$
\delta I=\int_a^b(F_y\eta+F_{y'}\eta')dx=\int_a^b\left(F_y-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}\right)\eta\,dx.
$$
The boundary term vanishes by the fixed endpoints. The <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> therefore gives the <Euler-Lagrange equation>
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}-F_y=0.}
$$

When $F_y=0$, the <Euler-Lagrange equation> reduces immediately to $dF_{y'}/dx=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{F_{y'}=C.}
$$
This is conservation of the generalized momentum associated with a <cyclic coordinate>.

Along a differentiable curve, the <chain rule> gives
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(F-y'F_{y'})=F_x+y'F_y-y'\frac{dF_{y'}}{dx}=F_x
$$
by the <Euler-Lagrange equation>. Thus when $F_x=0$ the <Beltrami identity> is
$$
\boxed{F-y'F_{y'}=C.}
$$

Here $F$ has no explicit $x$ dependence, so use the <Beltrami identity>. With $p=y'$, one has $F_{y'}=yp/\sqrt{1+p^2}$ and therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{y}{\sqrt{1+(y')^2}}-\lambda y=C.}
$$
This <first integral> reduces the second-order <Euler-Lagrange equation> to a first-order relation.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Choose the tangent angle $\psi=\arctan y'$ in $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$, so $1/\sqrt{1+(y')^2}=\cos\psi$. At the maximum, the <first integral> has value $C=y_0(1-\lambda)$. Hence
$$
y\cos\psi-\lambda y=y_0(1-\lambda),\qquad
\boxed{\lambda=\frac{y_0-y\cos\psi}{y_0-y}=1+\frac{y(1-\cos\psi)}{y_0-y}.}
$$
A nonconstant regular branch below its positive maximum has $y>0$ and $\psi\ne0$, giving **$\lambda>1$**. For completeness, strictness follows from the differential equation rather than assuming it at every point: the <Euler-Lagrange equation> simplifies to
$$
yy''=1+(y')^2-\lambda[1+(y')^2]^{3/2}.
$$
At the maximum, $y_0y''=1-\lambda$ implies $\lambda\geq1$. If $\lambda=1$, the positive-height initial data $y=y_0$, $y'=0$ give the constant solution uniquely, contradicting the existence of the lower-height branch. Thus $\lambda=1$ is only the degenerate horizontal solution. The first integral with $\lambda>1$ also excludes $y=0$ on this regular branch.

= Solution

Rearrange the <first integral> from part (iv) and use the <half-angle identities>:
$$
\lambda-1=\frac{2y\sin^2(\psi/2)}{y_0-y}=\frac{2y_1\sin^2(\alpha/2)}{y_0-y_1}.
$$
Eliminating $\lambda$ gives
$$
\boxed{\sin^2\frac\psi2=\frac{y_1}{y}\frac{y_0-y}{y_0-y_1}\sin^2\frac\alpha2,\qquad y_1<y<y_0.}
$$
Squaring removes the sign of the slope; the rising and falling sides of the same profile have opposite tangent angles but obey the same height relation. Heights and angles must remain on the regular branch with $\cos\psi>0$.

= Solution

The time dependence gives $u_{tt}=-\omega^2u$, and the angular derivative vanishes for these radial <normal modes>. The <Laplacian> in <polar coordinates> therefore yields
$$
R''+\frac1rR'+\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}R=0.
$$
Multiplication by $r$ puts the <Sturm-Liouville problem> in the form
$$
\boxed{-(rR')'=\lambda rR,\qquad p(r)=r,\quad q(r)=0,\quad w(r)=r,\quad\lambda=\omega^2/c^2.}
$$
The relevant <self-adjoint operator> is $\mathcal A R=-r^{-1}(rR')'$ in the <weighted inner product> with weight $r$. A physical displacement is regular at the origin, excluding the logarithmic singular solution. In particular $R$ is bounded and $rR'\to0$ there; regular eigenfunctions also have $R'(0)=0$. This origin condition is needed in addition to the fixed outer boundary.

= ii
{parent=10}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The conditions of regularity at $r=0$ and $R(a)=0$ select the <radial vibrations of a circular membrane> as an <eigenvalue problem>. Multiply $-(rR')'=\lambda rR$ by $\overline R$ and integrate. <Integration by parts> gives
$$
\lambda\int_0^ar|R|^2dr=\int_0^ar|R'|^2dr-[r\overline RR']_0^a=\int_0^ar|R'|^2dr.
$$
The boundary term vanishes by regularity and $R(a)=0$. For a nonzero eigenfunction the denominator is positive; the numerator could vanish only for a constant function, and the outer condition would then make that function zero. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\frac{\int_0^ar|R'|^2dr}{\int_0^ar|R|^2dr}>0,\qquad \nu=\frac{c\sqrt\lambda}{2\pi}\in\mathbb R.}
$$
This also proves reality without presuming real-valued eigenfunctions. Explicitly, regular solutions are <Bessel functions of the first kind> $R=J_0(\sqrt\lambda r)$, and the positive zeros of $J_0(\sqrt\lambda a)$ give the discrete spectrum.

{parent=10}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Use the <weighted inner product>
$$
\boxed{\langle R_m,R_n\rangle_r=\int_0^ar\overline{R_m(r)}R_n(r)\,dr.}
$$
Multiply the equation for $R_n$ by $\overline R_m$, subtract the complex conjugate equation for $R_m$ times $R_n$, and integrate. Since the <eigenvalues> are real,
$$
(\lambda_n-\lambda_m)\langle R_m,R_n\rangle_r=\left[r\left(\overline{R_m'}R_n-\overline R_mR_n'\right)\right]_0^a=0.
$$
The boundary term vanishes as before. Thus **eigenfunctions with distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal**. The full disk <inner product> differs only by the angular factor $2\pi$.

{parent=10}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

The <Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle> bounds the lowest <eigenvalue> by the <Rayleigh quotient> of any nonzero admissible trial function. For $f=1-(r/a)^p$, take $p>0$, which ensures boundedness, the outer <Dirichlet boundary condition>, and finite radial Dirichlet energy. Direct <integration> gives
$$
\int_0^ar(f')^2dr=\frac p2,\qquad\int_0^arf^2dr=a^2\left[\frac12-\frac2{p+2}+\frac1{2p+2}\right]=\frac{a^2p^2}{2(p+1)(p+2)}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\lambda_1\leq\frac{(p+1)(p+2)}{a^2p}=\frac1{a^2}\left(p+3+\frac2p\right).
$$
The derivative is $1-2/p^2$, so the unique minimizing positive exponent is $p=\sqrt2$. This yields the <power-law variational bound for a circular membrane>
$$
\boxed{\lambda_1\leq\frac{3+2\sqrt2}{a^2},\qquad \nu_1\leq\frac{c(1+\sqrt2)}{2\pi a}\simeq0.384234\,\frac ca.}
$$
The optimizing function has zero derivative at the origin and is admissible for the energy principle despite its unbounded second derivative there. For comparison, the exact radial fundamental has $\sqrt{\lambda_1}a\simeq2.404826$, giving $\nu_1\simeq0.382740\,c/a$. The bound is about $0.39\%$ above this value.
