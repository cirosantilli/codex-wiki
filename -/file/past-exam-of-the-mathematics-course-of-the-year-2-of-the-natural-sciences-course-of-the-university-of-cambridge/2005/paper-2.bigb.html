= Solution
{parent=1}

For a bounded region $R$ with piecewise smooth boundary and a continuously differentiable vector field, the <divergence theorem> gives $\int_R\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV=\int_{\partial R}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,dS$, with outward unit normal $\mathbf n$. Apply it to $\mathbf F=u\nabla v-v\nabla u$. The cross terms cancel, giving the <Green's second identity>
$$
\int_{B_R}[u(\Delta-m^2)v-v(\Delta-m^2)u]dV=\int_{\partial B_R}(u\partial_nv-v\partial_nu)dS.
$$
The $m^2uv$ terms cancel identically. Sending $R\to\infty$ proves the whole-space identity **provided the boundary integral tends to zero**. The <boundary term at infinity in Green's second identity> requires adequate derivative decay as well as field decay; mere pointwise convergence $u,v\to0$ does not suffice. The screened kernels used below satisfy the required decay.

Take $m>0$. Apply the identity with $u=\phi$ and $v=G(\cdot,\mathbf r)$, treating the source through its <Dirac delta function>, or excising a small source sphere and taking its radius to zero. It gives $\phi(\mathbf r)-\int G(\mathbf r',\mathbf r)\rho(\mathbf r')dV'=0$. Symmetry of the radial <Green function> then yields the requested representation.

For separation $s=|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|>0$, the <modified Helmholtz equation> reduces to $(sG)''-m^2(sG)=0$. Decay selects $G=Ce^{-ms}/s$. Integrating the equation over a small sphere gives $4\pi s^2G'\to1$, so $C=-1/(4\pi)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{G(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=-\frac{e^{-m|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}}{4\pi|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|},\qquad\phi(\mathbf r)=\int G(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')\rho(\mathbf r')dV'.}
$$
For $\rho=e^{-\beta r}/r$ with $\beta>0$, put $h=r\phi$. Then $h''-m^2h=e^{-\beta r}$. When $\beta\ne m$, decay removes $e^{mr}$, while $h(0)=0$ removes an additional point source at the origin. Hence
$$
\boxed{\phi(r)=\frac{e^{-\beta r}-e^{-mr}}{r(\beta^2-m^2)}.}
$$
Since $G$ has a minus sign, the positive <Yukawa kernel convolution> is
$$
\boxed{\frac1{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb R^3}\frac{e^{-m|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}\frac{e^{-\beta r'}}{r'}dV'=-\frac{e^{-\beta r}-e^{-mr}}{r(\beta^2-m^2)}.}
$$
The coincident-rate limit $\beta=m$ is $e^{-mr}/(2m)$ for the convolution, and the origin limit for general rates is $1/(m+\beta)$. Both are finite; the apparent denominators in the distinct-rate formula are removable limits.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Set $\phi=R(r)\Theta(\theta)$ in <Laplace's equation>. Separation gives $\Theta''+n^2\Theta=0$ and $r^2R''+rR'-n^2R=0$. Single-valued angular periodicity requires integer $n\geq0$. For $n>0$ the radial factors are $r^n,r^{-n}$; for $n=0$ they are $1,\ln r$. A term linear in $\theta$ is excluded by periodicity. Thus the <harmonic Fourier expansions in planar concentric domains> have the general form
$$
\phi=A+B\ln r+\sum_{n\geq1}[r^n(A_n\cos n\theta+B_n\sin n\theta)+r^{-n}(C_n\cos n\theta+D_n\sin n\theta)].
$$
For $0<a<b$, the specified boundaries contain only a constant mode and a first cosine mode. The <two-boundary annular harmonic interpolation> gives
$$
\boxed{\phi(r,\theta)=U\frac{\ln(b/r)}{\ln(b/a)}+V\frac{b}{b^2-a^2}\left(r-\frac{a^2}{r}\right)\cos\theta.}
$$
At $r=a$, the cosine term vanishes and the logarithmic term is $U$; at $r=b$, the first term vanishes and the cosine amplitude is $V$. Each component is harmonic, and the <maximum principle> ensures uniqueness for these continuous <Dirichlet boundary conditions>.

= Solution

Compute the complex derivative along real increments to get $f'=u_x+iv_x$, and along imaginary increments to get $f'=v_y-iu_y$. Equality gives the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>, $\boxed{u_x=v_y,\quad u_y=-v_x}$.

For the specified real part, observe that $iz e^{-z}=e^{-x}[(x\sin y-y\cos y)+i(x\cos y+y\sin y)]$. Thus the most general <holomorphic function> with this real part on the connected plane is
$$
\boxed{f(z)=iz e^{-z}+iC,\quad C\in\mathbb R.}
$$
The difference of two such holomorphic functions has zero real part, so the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> make its imaginary part constant.

Next, $\cosh z=\cosh x\cos y+i\sinh x\sin y$. Its real and imaginary parts satisfy $u_x=\sinh x\cos y=v_y$ and $u_y=-\cosh x\sin y=-v_x$. Their partial derivatives are continuous everywhere, proving that the complex <hyperbolic cosine> is entire.

For the <Laurent series>, set $h=z-1$. Since $z^2-1=h(2+h)$,
$$
\frac{\cosh z}{z^2-1}=\frac{\cosh1+h\sinh1+O(h^2)}{2h}\left(1-\frac h2+O(h^2)\right).
$$
The <regular part at a simple pole> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{a_{-1}=\frac{\cosh1}{2},\qquad a_0=\frac{\sinh1}{2}-\frac{\cosh1}{4}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=4}

A <pole> of order $N$ at $z_0$ has $(z-z_0)^Nf(z)$ extending holomorphically to a nonzero value there. Its <residue> is the coefficient of $(z-z_0)^{-1}$ in the <Laurent series>. The <Cauchy integral theorem> gives $\oint_Cf\,dz=0$ for a closed contour in a simply connected domain of holomorphicity. The <residue theorem> replaces zero by $2\pi i$ times the enclosed residues for a positively oriented simple contour avoiding finitely many poles.

For the first integral, use the upper-half-plane semicircle, with a small clockwise upper semicircle excluding the origin. Near zero,
$$
\frac{e^{iaz}-e^{ibz}}{z^2}=\frac{i(a-b)}z+O(1).
$$
There are no poles inside the indented contour. The large arc vanishes because $a,b>0$ and the integrand there is $O(R^{-2})$; the small arc tends to $-i\pi\,i(a-b)=\pi(a-b)$. Therefore the real-axis complex principal value is $\pi(b-a)$. Taking real parts gives the ordinary convergent <cosine-difference inverse-square integral>
$$
\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos ax-\cos bx}{x^2}dx=\pi(b-a).}
$$
The real numerator cancels to second order at zero, so this final real integral itself needs no principal-value convention.

For $g(z)=e^{az}/\cosh\pi z$, the simple poles are $z_n=i(n+1/2)$, $n\in\mathbb Z$. Differentiating the denominator gives
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Res}_{z_n}g=\frac{(-1)^n e^{ia(n+1/2)}}{\pi i}.}
$$
Integrate around the rectangle with vertices $-R,R,R+i,-R+i$. It contains only $i/2$. On the top side $g(x+i)=-e^{ia}g(x)$, and reversed traversal therefore contributes $+e^{ia}$ times the bottom integral. The vertical sides vanish for $|a|<\pi$. The <residue theorem> gives $(1+e^{ia})J=2e^{ia/2}$, so the <exponential moment of the hyperbolic secant> is
$$
\boxed{J=\int_{\mathbb R}\frac{e^{ax}}{\cosh\pi x}dx=\frac1{\cos(a/2)},\qquad-\pi<a<\pi.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=5}

The one-sided <Laplace transform> is $\bar u(p)=\int_0^\infty e^{-pt}u(t)dt$ for sufficiently large $\operatorname{Re}p$. <Integration by parts>, with the exponential suppressing the upper boundary, gives
$$
\overline{u'}=p\bar u-u(0),\qquad\overline{u''}=p^2\bar u-pu(0)-u'(0),\qquad\overline{u'''}=p^3\bar u-p^2u(0)-pu'(0)-u''(0).
$$
Differentiation under the integral gives $d\bar u/dp=-\int_0^\infty te^{-pt}u(t)dt$, proving $\overline{tu}=-d\bar u/dp$. Start with $\overline{e^{-t}}=1/(p+1)$ and apply this rule repeatedly:
$$
\boxed{\bar v_0=\frac1{p+1},\quad\bar v_1=\frac1{(p+1)^2},\quad\bar v_2=\frac2{(p+1)^3},\quad\bar v_3=\frac6{(p+1)^4}.}
$$
With all three initial data zero, transforming the forced equation gives $(p+1)^3\bar y=1/(p+1)$. Thus $\bar y=(p+1)^{-4}$ and the listed inverse transform gives
$$
\boxed{y(t)=\frac{t^3e^{-t}}6,\qquad t\geq0.}
$$
The cubic factor is also the <resonant exponential particular solution> associated with the triple characteristic root $-1$.

Under an orthogonal Cartesian change $x'_i=R_{ij}x_j$, the <chain rule> gives $\partial'_i=(\partial x_j/\partial x'_i)\partial_j=R_{ij}\partial_j$. Thus the derivative operator transforms as a rank-one <Cartesian tensor>. Under a general coordinate change it has the covector transformation law when applied to a scalar; derivatives of vector components then need the usual connection terms. The calculations below use Cartesian coordinates.

= Solution

Use $w_j=\epsilon_{j\ell m}\partial_\ell u_m$ and contract the <Levi-Civita symbols>:
$$
(\mathbf w\times\mathbf u)_i=\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{j\ell m}(\partial_\ell u_m)u_k=u_\ell\partial_\ell u_i-u_m\partial_i u_m=u_\ell\partial_\ell u_i-\partial_i\frac{|\mathbf u|^2}{2}.
$$
This proves the <convective acceleration identity>, with the sign set by the displayed order $\mathbf w\times\mathbf u$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For the <curl of a cross product>, the Cartesian contraction is
$$
[\nabla\times(\mathbf w\times\mathbf u)]_i=\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{k\ell m}\partial_j(w_\ell u_m)=\partial_j(w_i u_j-w_j u_i).
$$
The <product rule> then gives
$$
\boxed{\nabla\times(\mathbf w\times\mathbf u)=(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf w+(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)\mathbf w-(\nabla\cdot\mathbf w)\mathbf u-(\mathbf w\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u.}
$$
For the fluid equation, replace the advective term by $\mathbf w\times\mathbf u+\nabla(|\mathbf u|^2/2)$ using the preceding identity. Take its curl. Both gradient terms have zero curl, while $\nabla\cdot\mathbf w=\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf u)=0$ and incompressibility gives $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$. The <vorticity equation> is consequently
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\mathbf w+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf w=(\mathbf w\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u.}
$$
The left side is the <material derivative> of <vorticity>; the right is <vortex stretching>. It follows here because the given force per mass is a gradient, in addition to the flow being inviscid and incompressible.

= Solution
{parent=7}

The <kinetic energy> is $K=\tfrac12m(3\dot x^2+3\dot y^2+4\dot z^2)$. Differentiating the given potential and applying <Newton's second law> gives
$$
\boxed{3\ddot x+\Omega^2(2x-z)=0,\quad3\ddot y+\Omega^2(2y-z)=0,\quad4\ddot z+\Omega^2(2z-x-y)=0.}
$$
These are the <normal modes of a symmetric three-mass string>, with mass matrix $M=m\operatorname{diag}(3,3,4)$ and stiffness matrix $m\Omega^2\begin{pmatrix}2&0&-1\\0&2&-1\\-1&-1&2\end{pmatrix}$. The coordinate order here is $(x,y,z)$, although the spatial order along the string is $x,z,y$.

Reflection symmetry splits the <generalized eigenvalue problem> into an antisymmetric mode $x=-y$, $z=0$, with $\omega^2=2\Omega^2/3$, and symmetric modes $x=y=q$. Writing $\mu=\omega^2/\Omega^2$, these obey $z=(2-3\mu)q$ and $(2-4\mu)z=2q$. Thus $(2-4\mu)(2-3\mu)-2=2(6\mu-1)(\mu-1)=0$. The three results are
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}\omega&x:y:z\\\hline\Omega/\sqrt6&2:2:3\\\Omega\sqrt{2/3}&1:-1:0\\\Omega&1:1:-1\end{array}.}
$$
The displacement vectors are mutually orthogonal under the mass-weighted inner product $u^TMv$, as required for these conservative <normal modes>.

A left <coset> is $gH=\{gh:h\in H\}$. The cosets partition a finite group into equally sized sets of $|H|$ elements, so <Lagrange's theorem> gives $|G|=[G:H]|H|$; in particular subgroup order divides group order.

Here $A=-I$, $C=-B$ and $B^2=I$. The <Cayley table>, with row element multiplied on the left of the column element, is

The table proves closure, $I$ is the identity and every element is its own inverse. Associativity is inherited from matrix multiplication, so this is the <Klein four-group>.

Finally, if every nonidentity element has order two, every element satisfies $h^{-1}=h$, including the identity. Then $ab=(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}=ba$. Thus a <group of exponent two is abelian>.

= Solution
{parent=9}

A <group homomorphism> preserves multiplication, $\phi(ab)=\phi(a)\phi(b)$. A <group isomorphism> is a bijective homomorphism. An isomorphism from a group to itself is a <group automorphism>.

The <automorphism group> contains the identity map. Composition is associative, and if $\phi,\psi$ are automorphisms, $(\phi\circ\psi)(ab)=\phi(\psi(a)\psi(b))=(\phi\circ\psi)(a)(\phi\circ\psi)(b)$; composition is also bijective. For $u=\phi(a)$ and $v=\phi(b)$, $\phi^{-1}(uv)=\phi^{-1}(\phi(ab))=ab=\phi^{-1}(u)\phi^{-1}(v)$, proving the inverse bijection is a homomorphism. These facts establish all group axioms.

A finite <cyclic group> is $G=\langle g\rangle=\{e,g,\ldots,g^{n-1}\}$ with $g$ of order $n$. The map $g^k\mapsto e^{2\pi ik/n}$ is well defined modulo $n$, bijective onto the <roots of unity>, and preserves multiplication. It identifies $G$ with the multiplicative group of $n$th roots of unity.

For prime $n$, <Lagrange's theorem> leaves only subgroup orders one and $n$: **there is no nontrivial proper subgroup**. The trivial subgroup $\{e\}$ is nevertheless proper when $n>1$, so that qualification is necessary in the printed claim. The characterization of <groups with no proper nontrivial subgroups> makes the distinction explicit.

For composite $n$, choose a prime divisor $p$ and take $\boxed{H_0=\{e\},\quad H_1=\langle g^p\rangle}$, of orders $1$ and $n/p$. They are distinct proper subgroups, with $H_1$ nontrivial. If the question intended two nontrivial proper subgroups, that demand is not always possible: a cyclic group of order $p^2$ has only the one of order $p$. The <subgroups and quotients of a cyclic group> are controlled by its divisors. The order-one group is a separate trivial case, with no proper subgroups.

= Solution

A <group representation> is a homomorphism $D:G\to\mathrm{GL}(V)$. It is a <reducible representation> if there is a nonzero proper <invariant subspace> of $V$ preserved by every $D(g)$, and an <irreducible representation> otherwise. With $S$ invertible,
$$
\widetilde D(gh)=SD(g)D(h)S^{-1}=(SD(g)S^{-1})(SD(h)S^{-1}),
$$
so changing basis gives another representation. Its <character of a representation> is $\chi(g)=\operatorname{tr}D(g)$, and cyclicity of the <trace> gives $\widetilde\chi(g)=\operatorname{tr}(SD(g)S^{-1})=\chi(g)$.

A <conjugacy class> consists of $hgh^{-1}$ as $h$ varies. Since $(hgh^{-1})^k=hg^kh^{-1}$, a power equals the identity exactly when $g^k$ does. Thus <conjugation preserves element order>, including infinite order.

For the square, let $r$ be rotation by $\pi/2$ and $s$ reflection in a horizontal symmetry axis. The <dihedral group> has the eight elements
$$
e,r,r^2,r^3,s,rs,r^2s,r^3s,\qquad r^4=s^2=e,\quad srs=r^{-1}.
$$
The <conjugacy classes of a finite dihedral group> specialize to
$$
\boxed{\{e\},\quad\{r^2\},\quad\{r,r^3\},\quad\{s,r^2s\},\quad\{rs,r^3s\}.}
$$
The two central rotations are singletons. Reflection conjugation reverses rotations, while conjugation by $r$ shifts a reflection exponent by two, giving the two reflection classes.

For finite groups over $\mathbb C$, <character orthogonality> and completeness make the <irreducible characters> a basis of the <class functions>. The latter space has one independent value per conjugacy class. Hence **the square group has five inequivalent irreducible complex representations**. The <sum of squares of irreducible representation dimensions> is eight, forcing four one-dimensional representations and one two-dimensional representation. The four scalar choices $r\mapsto\pm1$, $s\mapsto\pm1$ supply the former; the ordinary action on the plane supplies the latter.
