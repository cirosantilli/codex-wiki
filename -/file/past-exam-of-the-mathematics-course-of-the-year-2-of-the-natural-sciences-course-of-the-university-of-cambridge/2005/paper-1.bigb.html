= Solution
{parent=1}

For $r>0$, the <chain rule> gives $\partial r/\partial x_i=x_i/r$. Differentiating the radial factor gives $d\phi/dr=e^{ikr}(ikr-1)/(4\pi r^2)$, so in <Cartesian coordinates>
$$
\boxed{\nabla\phi=\frac{e^{ikr}(ikr-1)}{4\pi r^3}(x,y,z).}
$$
The origin is excluded because the scalar field is singular there.

The <Stokes theorem> states $\int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf F\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell$ for a sufficiently smooth vector field and oriented surface with piecewise smooth boundary. The boundary orientation follows the right-hand rule relative to $\mathbf n$.

For the <convective acceleration identity>, use the <Levi-Civita symbol> contraction $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{k\ell m}=\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jm}-\delta_{im}\delta_{j\ell}$. It gives
$$
[\mathbf F\times(\nabla\times\mathbf F)]_i=\epsilon_{ijk}F_j\epsilon_{k\ell m}\partial_\ell F_m=F_j\partial_iF_j-F_j\partial_jF_i=\frac12\partial_i(F_jF_j)-[(\mathbf F\cdot\nabla)\mathbf F]_i.
$$
Rearranging proves the required vector identity.

For <orthogonal coordinates>, define the <scale factors of orthogonal coordinates> $h_i=|\partial\mathbf r/\partial q_i|$ and tangent <unit vectors> $\mathbf e_i=h_i^{-1}\partial\mathbf r/\partial q_i$. They form an <orthonormal basis>, and $d\mathbf r=\sum_i h_i\mathbf e_i\,dq_i$. Equating $d\psi=\sum_i\partial_{q_i}\psi\,dq_i$ with $\nabla\psi\cdot d\mathbf r$ identifies each component. Thus the <gradient in orthogonal coordinates> is
$$
\boxed{\nabla\psi=\sum_{i=1}^3\frac1{h_i}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial q_i}\mathbf e_i.}
$$
This holds on a regular coordinate chart where all scale factors are positive.

= Solution

Set $T(x,t)=X(x)G(t)$ in the <heat equation>. <Separation of variables> gives $G'/(c^2G)=X''/X=-\lambda$. Zero endpoint temperatures impose <Dirichlet boundary conditions> $X(0)=X(\pi)=0$, selecting $\lambda=n^2$ for $n\geq1$. The separated factors can therefore be chosen as
$$
\boxed{F_n(x)=\sin nx,\qquad G_n(t)=e^{-c^2n^2t}.}
$$
Zero and negative separation eigenvalues give only the zero spatial solution under these boundary conditions.

The initial profile is continuous and triangular, with midpoint height $\pi/2$. Its <Fourier sine series> coefficients are
$$
a_n=\frac2\pi\left[\int_0^{\pi/2}x\sin nx\,dx+\int_{\pi/2}^\pi(\pi-x)\sin nx\,dx\right]=\boxed{\frac4{\pi n^2}\sin\frac{n\pi}2}.
$$
Consequently the <heat flow from a triangular initial temperature> is
$$
\boxed{T(x,t)=\frac4\pi\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^m}{(2m+1)^2}e^{-c^2(2m+1)^2t}\sin((2m+1)x).}
$$
For $t\geq0$, each summand has magnitude at most $4/[\pi(2m+1)^2]$. The <Weierstrass M-test> proves absolute and <uniform convergence>. The initial <Fourier sine series> equals the continuous piecewise smooth profile, and for $t>0$ the exponential factors justify termwise spatial and time differentiation on every time interval bounded away from zero.

= Solution
{parent=3}

Use the convention
$$
\widehat f(k)=\int_{\mathbb R}f(x)e^{-ikx}dx,\qquad f(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}\widehat f(k)e^{ikx}dk.
$$
These are the <Fourier transform> and <inverse Fourier transform> for suitably integrable data. Assuming the required ordinary transforms exist and the lower derivatives vanish at both ends, <integration by parts> gives $\widehat{g'}=[ge^{-ikx}]_{-\infty}^{\infty}+ik\widehat g=ik\widehat g$. Repeating this argument yields
$$
\boxed{\widehat{g^{(n)}}(k)=(ik)^n\widehat g(k).}
$$
The decay assumptions are used to discard the boundary terms at each step.

For positive diffusivity $\alpha$, transforming the <heat equation> gives $\partial_t\widehat f=-\alpha k^2\widehat f$. Thus $\widehat f(k,t)=e^{-\alpha k^2t}\widehat f_0(k)$, where $f_0(x)=f(x,0)$. The <convolution theorem> and the <Gaussian heat kernel> give
$$
\boxed{f(x,t)=\frac1{\sqrt{4\pi\alpha t}}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-(x-\xi)^2/(4\alpha t)}f_0(\xi)\,d\xi,\qquad t>0.}
$$
For $f_0(x)=e^{-x^2}$, the <Fourier transform of a Gaussian> is $\sqrt\pi e^{-k^2/4}$. Multiplying by the heat factor and inverting gives the <Gaussian initial data for the heat equation> solution
$$
\boxed{f(x,t)=\frac1{\sqrt{1+4\alpha t}}\exp\left[-\frac{x^2}{1+4\alpha t}\right].}
$$
It recovers the initial profile as $t\downarrow0$ and preserves its spatial integral $\sqrt\pi$.

= Solution

By the <spectral theorem>, the <Hermitian matrix> $A$ has a complete <orthonormal eigenbasis>. If $Av=\lambda v$, idempotence gives $\lambda^2=\lambda$, so every <eigenvalue> is zero or one. Since $\det A\ne1$, at least one is zero. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\det A=0,\qquad\operatorname{tr}A=m=\operatorname{rank}A\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}.}
$$
The <trace of an idempotent equals its rank>; here Hermiticity further identifies $A$ as an <orthogonal projection matrix>.

A complex <inner product> $\langle u,v\rangle$ is conjugate-linear in its first argument, linear in its second, conjugate symmetric and positive definite: $\langle v,u\rangle=\overline{\langle u,v\rangle}$ and $\langle u,u\rangle>0$ for nonzero $u$. An <orthonormal basis> is a spanning linearly independent family $e_i$ with $\langle e_i,e_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}$. The associated <norm> is $\|u\|=\sqrt{\langle u,u\rangle}$.

For two distinct basis elements $x,y$, their <orthogonality> and unit norms give $\|x-y\|^2=\langle x,x\rangle+\langle y,y\rangle-\langle x,y\rangle-\langle y,x\rangle=2$. Hence **their distance is $\sqrt2$**; if the same element is selected twice, it is zero.

Finally, for $B^\dagger=B$, the scalar quadratic form obeys $(x^\dagger Bx)^*=x^\dagger B^\dagger x=x^\dagger Bx$, proving **$x^\dagger Bx$ is real**.

= Solution
{parent=5}

A <unitary matrix> satisfies $U^\dagger U=I$ over the complex numbers; a real <orthogonal matrix> satisfies $Q^TQ=I$. Their inverses are their conjugate transposes and transposes, respectively.

With $d=-a$, the coordinate $e_2=(0,1,0)$ has <eigenvalue> $c$. In the $x,z$ plane, the <traceless symmetric two-by-two matrix> has eigenvalues $\pm s$, where $s=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$. If $s>0$, choose $\theta$ with $\cos2\theta=a/s$ and $\sin2\theta=b/s$. Then an <orthonormal eigenbasis> is
$$
\boxed{\lambda_+=s:\ v_+=(\cos\theta,0,\sin\theta),\quad\lambda_-=-s:\ v_-=(-\sin\theta,0,\cos\theta),\quad\lambda_2=c:\ e_2.}
$$
If $c$ equals $s$ or $-s$, the corresponding <eigenspace> is the span of the two listed vectors with that eigenvalue. If $s=0$, then $a=b=0$, the $x,z$ plane is the zero eigenspace, and $e_2$ retains eigenvalue $c$; if also $c=0$, every vector belongs to the zero eigenspace. This includes the degenerate cases that a division by $b$ would miss.

For the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>, take $b\ne0$ and remove the <orthogonal projection> of $a$ along $b$. Positivity of the squared norm gives
$$
0\leq\left\|a-b\frac{\langle b,a\rangle}{\|b\|^2}\right\|^2=\|a\|^2-\frac{|\langle b,a\rangle|^2}{\|b\|^2}.
$$
Multiplication by $\|b\|^2$ and square roots prove $|\langle a,b\rangle|\leq\|a\|\|b\|$; the case $b=0$ is immediate. Equality means linear dependence. Therefore
$$
\|a+b\|^2=\|a\|^2+\|b\|^2+2\operatorname{Re}\langle a,b\rangle\leq\|a\|^2+\|b\|^2+2\|a\|\|b\|=(\|a\|+\|b\|)^2.
$$
Taking the nonnegative square root proves the <triangle inequality>.

= Solution

For $y''+P(x)y'+Q(x)y=0$, a singular point $x_0$ is a <regular singular point> if $(x-x_0)P(x)$ and $(x-x_0)^2Q(x)$ are analytic there. The <Bessel differential equation> has $xP=1$ and $x^2Q=x^2-\nu^2$, so zero is regular singular.

Use the <Frobenius method> with $y=x^s\sum_{n\geq0}a_nx^n$, $a_0\ne0$. Matching coefficients gives $(s^2-\nu^2)a_0=0$ and $[(s+n)^2-\nu^2]a_n+a_{n-2}=0$, with missing negative-index coefficients zero. The regular root is $s=\nu$. Then $a_1=0$, all odd coefficients vanish, and the <Frobenius series for Bessel functions> has recurrence
$$
\boxed{a_{2m}=-\frac{a_{2m-2}}{4m(m+\nu)},\qquad m\geq1.}
$$
For noninteger $\nu$, the root $s=-\nu$ supplies an independent even-power series $y_2=x^{-\nu}\sum_{m\geq0}b_{2m}x^{2m}$ with $b_{2m}=-b_{2m-2}/[4m(m-\nu)]$ and $b_0\ne0$. None of these denominators vanishes. With standard normalization this is $J_{-\nu}(x)$, independent of the <Bessel function of the first kind> $J_\nu(x)$. The real powers here are understood for $x>0$.

For integer $\nu\geq0$ and the stated normalization $a_0=1/(2^\nu\nu!)$, iteration gives
$$
\boxed{a_{2m}=\frac{(-1)^m}{2^{2m+\nu}m!(m+\nu)!},\qquad J_\nu(x)=\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^m(x/2)^{2m+\nu}}{m!(m+\nu)!}.}
$$
The ratio of consecutive term magnitudes is $|x|^2/[4(m+1)(m+\nu+1)]\to0$. Thus the series converges absolutely for every finite complex $x$, defining an entire function for integer $\nu$. At integer order, $J_{-\nu}=(-1)^\nu J_\nu$; an independent solution is instead the <Bessel function of the second kind>.

= Solution
{parent=7}

Define the <weighted inner product> $\langle u,v\rangle_w=\int_a^bw(x)\overline{u(x)}v(x)dx$. For the stated operator $L=-(pD)' -q$, integration by parts gives the <Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator>
$$
\int_a^b[\overline uLv-\overline{Lu}v]dx=[p(\overline{u'}v-\overline u v')]_a^b.
$$
The endpoint terms vanish for <Dirichlet boundary conditions>. Taking $u=v$ proves each eigenvalue is real, since $w>0$ makes the squared weighted norm positive. For eigenfunctions $u_m,u_n$ of distinct eigenvalues, the same identity gives $(\lambda_n-\lambda_m)\langle u_m,u_n\rangle_w=0$, proving their orthogonality.

For the specific equation, divide by $x^2$ to obtain $-y''=\lambda x^{-2}y$, so its weight is $w=x^{-2}$, with $p=1,q=0$. Put $v=\ln x$, $\ell=\ln2$ and $Y(v)=y(e^v)$. The <logarithmic change of variable in a Sturm-Liouville problem> gives $x^2y''=Y''-Y'$. Remove the first derivative with $Y=e^{v/2}Z$; the equation becomes $Z''+(\lambda-1/4)Z=0$, with $Z(0)=Z(\ell)=0$.

The <Dirichlet spectrum with inverse-square weight> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\lambda_n=\frac14+\left(\frac{n\pi}{\ln2}\right)^2,\qquad y_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac2{\ln2}}\sqrt x\sin\frac{n\pi\ln x}{\ln2},\quad n\geq1.}
$$
Indeed $\int_1^2x^{-2}y_my_n dx=(2/\ell)\int_0^\ell\sin(m\pi v/\ell)\sin(n\pi v/\ell)dv=\delta_{mn}$. Values $\lambda\leq1/4$ give a linear or hyperbolic transformed solution and no nonzero solution vanishing at both ends.

= Solution
{parent=8}

Take $x\geq0$ and a source position $\xi>0$. Before the source, the zero initial data require $G=0$. After it, set $G=e^{-2(x-\xi)}h(x-\xi)$; the homogeneous equation becomes $h''+a^2h=0$. Continuity of $G$ and a unit jump in $G_x$ at $x=\xi$ give $h(0)=0$, $h'(0)=1$. Thus the <causal Green function of a damped oscillator> is
$$
\boxed{G(x,\xi)=H(x-\xi)e^{-2(x-\xi)}\frac{\sin[a(x-\xi)]}{a}.}
$$
Here $H$ is the <Heaviside step function>. For $a=0$, take the continuous limit $\sin(as)/a\to s$, giving $G=H(x-\xi)(x-\xi)e^{-2(x-\xi)}$. The formula is also unchanged when a nonzero $a$ changes sign. Its continuity removes a delta derivative, and its unit first-derivative jump supplies the required <Dirac delta function>.

For the forced equation use $a=2$. The zero-initial-data <Green function> representation is
$$
y(x)=\frac12\int_0^xe^{-2(x-\xi)}\sin[2(x-\xi)]\sin2\xi\,d\xi.
$$
The sine-product identity reduces this to elementary exponential-trigonometric integrals. Evaluating gives
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{\sin2x-2\cos2x+e^{-2x}(2\cos2x+\sin2x)}{20}.}
$$
For an independent check, the steady particular part is $\sin2x/20-\cos2x/10$, while the decaying homogeneous part has coefficients $1/10$ and $1/20$ that impose $y(0)=y'(0)=0$.

= Solution
{parent=9}

Introduce a <Lagrange multiplier> for the normalization and vary $J[y]=\int_0^1[p(y')^2+qy^2-\lambda wy^2]dx$. With variation $\eta$ satisfying $\eta(1)=0$, <integration by parts> gives
$$
\delta J=2[py'\eta]_0^1-2\int_0^1[(py')'-qy+\lambda wy]\eta\,dx.
$$
The upper endpoint term vanishes, and the lower term vanishes because $p(0)=0$ with the stipulated regular differentiable behavior. More generally the natural endpoint condition is $py'\to0$ there. The <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> gives $(py')'-qy+\lambda wy=0$, the stated <Sturm-Liouville problem>.

The <Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle> estimates the lowest eigenvalue by minimizing $R[y]=\int[p(y')^2+qy^2]dx/\int wy^2dx$ over an admissible finite-dimensional trial family. Restricting the family can only raise the minimum.

For the specific radial problem, $p=w=x$ and $q=0$. The trial $y=A(1-x^2)$ has $\int_0^1xy^2dx=A^2/6$. Normalization gives $\boxed{A=\sqrt6}$, with the negative sign an equivalent choice. The numerator is $\int_0^1x(2Ax)^2dx=A^2$, so
$$
\boxed{\lambda_1\leq R[y]=6.}
$$
This is the quadratic case of the <power-law variational bound for a circular membrane>. The exact first eigenvalue $5.7815$ is smaller because the polynomial trial is not the exact <Bessel function of the first kind> eigenfunction. Expanding a trial in the exact <orthonormal eigenbasis> expresses its <Rayleigh quotient> as a weighted average of eigenvalues, so no admissible trial can lie below the lowest one.

= Solution
{parent=10}

Along an extremal, differentiate $f-y'f_{y'}$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\frac d{dx}(f-y'f_{y'})=f_x+y'\left(f_y-\frac d{dx}f_{y'}\right).
$$
When $f_x=0$, the <Euler-Lagrange equation> makes the right side zero. This proves the <Beltrami identity>, $f-y'f_{y'}=k$.

Place the wall on $y=0$ with attachments at $x=\pm a$, and write the fence above it as $y(x)\geq0$. Maximize area $A=\int_{-a}^aydx$ subject to length $\int_{-a}^a\sqrt{1+y'^2}dx=2l$. The <isoperimetric constraint> gives the augmented integrand $f=y-R\sqrt{1+y'^2}$, where $R$ is a constant multiplier. Its <Euler-Lagrange equation> is $y''/(1+y'^2)^{3/2}=-1/R$, so the fence has constant negative curvature. Equivalently the <Beltrami identity> is $y-R/\sqrt{1+y'^2}=k$.

The resulting <maximum-area circular segment with fixed arc length and chord> has
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\sqrt{R^2-x^2}-\sqrt{R^2-a^2},\qquad R=\frac a{\sin\theta},\qquad\frac la=\frac\theta{\sin\theta}.}
$$
Here $0<\theta<\pi/2$ is the half-angle subtended by the arc. Its half-length is $R\theta=l$ and half-chord is $R\sin\theta=a$. The function $\theta/\sin\theta$ increases from one to $\pi/2$, so the given range $a<l<\pi a/2$ determines a unique minor arc. Its enclosed area is $A_{\max}=R^2(\theta-\sin\theta\cos\theta)$.

There is also a global maximality proof for simple fences in the chosen half-plane, without assuming competitors are graphs. Complete the candidate minor arc with the complementary major arc of the same circle, which lies below the wall. Attach this same fixed major arc to any competing fence of length $2l$. The completed closed curve always has perimeter $2\pi R$, and its area is the competitor's fenced area plus the same fixed area below the wall. The <planar isoperimetric inequality> bounds that total by $\pi R^2$, attained by the candidate circle. Hence no competitor encloses more area, and equality requires the circular arc.
