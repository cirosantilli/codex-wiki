= Solution

Let $\rho=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=uv$. The condition $u\leq v$ gives $z\leq0$. On the outer coordinate surface $v=1$, elimination of $u$ gives $z=(\rho^2-1)/2$. On $u=v$, the surface is the plane $z=0$, with $\rho=u^2\leq1$. Thus the closure of the body is
$$
\boxed{0\leq\rho\leq1,\qquad\frac{\rho^2-1}{2}\leq z\leq0.}
$$
It is a solid circular cap bounded below by an <elliptic paraboloid> of revolution and above by the unit disk in $z=0$. Its vertex is $\boxed{(0,0,-1/2)}$, its height is $\boxed{1/2}$, and its rim is the <circle> $\boxed{x^2+y^2=1,\ z=0}$. The symmetry axis is the $z$ axis. The coordinate surface $u=0$ maps to that axis rather than to an additional two-dimensional boundary. These are <parabolic coordinates>.

= Solution

Differentiate the position <vector> with respect to the three coordinates to obtain the <coordinate basis> vectors:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf h_u=\begin{pmatrix}v\cos\phi\\v\sin\phi\\u\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf h_v=\begin{pmatrix}u\cos\phi\\u\sin\phi\\-v\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf h_\phi=\begin{pmatrix}-uv\sin\phi\\uv\cos\phi\\0\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The <chain rule> gives $d\mathbf x=\mathbf h_u du+\mathbf h_v dv+\mathbf h_\phi d\phi$. The <scale factors of orthogonal coordinates> are the corresponding <norms>:
$$
\boxed{h_u=h_v=\sqrt{u^2+v^2},\qquad h_\phi=uv.}
$$
Direct <dot products> give $\mathbf h_u\cdot\mathbf h_v=uv-uv=0$, $\mathbf h_u\cdot\mathbf h_\phi=0$, and $\mathbf h_v\cdot\mathbf h_\phi=0$. Thus the <parabolic coordinates> are orthogonal wherever the coordinate vectors are nondegenerate. Their degeneracy on $uv=0$ is the usual axis degeneracy of the azimuthal coordinate.

The <Jacobian determinant> is the oriented <scalar triple product> of the three <coordinate basis> vectors:
$$
J=\det\frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,\phi)}=(\mathbf h_u\times\mathbf h_v)\cdot\mathbf h_\phi.
$$
Here $\mathbf h_u\times\mathbf h_v=(u^2+v^2)(-\sin\phi,\cos\phi,0)$, so
$$
\boxed{J=uv(u^2+v^2),\qquad dV=uv(u^2+v^2)\,du\,dv\,d\phi.}
$$
The sign is nonnegative on the stated range, and the absolute <Jacobian determinant> also equals $h_uh_vh_\phi$ as expected for <orthogonal coordinates>.

Since $\mathbf F=\nabla\Omega$, its <divergence> is the <Laplacian> of $\Omega$:
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=\nabla^2\Omega=6x+4z.
$$
The <divergence theorem> changes the outward <flux> through the whole closed boundary into $I=\int_V(6x+4z)dV$. Rotational symmetry makes the $x$ term vanish. With $z=(u^2-v^2)/2$ and the <Jacobian determinant> from part (c),
$$
\begin{aligned}
I&=2\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\int_0^1\int_0^v(u^2-v^2)uv(u^2+v^2)\,du\,dv\,d\phi\\
&=4\pi\int_0^1v\left[\frac{u^6}{6}-\frac{v^4u^2}{2}\right]_{u=0}^{u=v}dv\\
&=-\frac{4\pi}{3}\int_0^1v^7\,dv
=\boxed{-\frac\pi6}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Both the curved face and the top disk are included in this <surface integral> through the <divergence theorem>.

Remove the common nonzero factor $e^{-i\omega t}$ and expand in the small parameter $\epsilon$. To first order, the modified <Laplacian> contributes only its $y$ derivatives: its $x$ derivative term has the extra factor $\epsilon^2$. The <regular perturbation> expansion therefore gives
$$
\boxed{P=-i\omega b_0+\omega\psi_{0,y},\qquad Q=-i\omega b_1+\omega\psi_{1,y}+\psi_{0,x}-b_{0,yy},}
$$
$$
\boxed{R=-i\omega\psi_{0,yy}-\omega b_{0,y},\qquad S=-i\omega\psi_{1,yy}-\omega b_{1,y}-b_{0,x}.}
$$
Thus the two residuals are $P+\epsilon Q+O(\epsilon^2)$ and $R+\epsilon S+O(\epsilon^2)$, respectively. Subscripts after a comma denote <partial derivatives>. We retain $\omega\ne0$, as appropriate to the oscillatory wave and the elimination below.

= Solution

The first-order condition $Q=0$, combined with $b_0=-i\psi_{0,y}$, gives
$$
b_1=-i\psi_{1,y}-\frac i\omega\psi_{0,x}+\frac1\omega\psi_{0,yyy}.
$$
Differentiate this expression with respect to $y$ and insert it into $S=0$. The $\psi_1$ terms cancel, leaving
$$
\begin{aligned}
0&=-i\omega\psi_{1,yy}-\omega\left[-i\psi_{1,yy}-\frac i\omega\psi_{0,xy}+\frac1\omega\psi_{0,yyyy}\right]+i\psi_{0,xy}\\
&=2i\psi_{0,xy}-\psi_{0,yyyy}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the compatibility <partial differential equation> is
$$
\boxed{2\psi_{0,xy}+i\psi_{0,yyyy}=0.}
$$
It is exactly $\partial_y(2\psi_{0,x}+i\psi_{0,yyy})=0$. Integrating with respect to $y$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{2\psi_{0,x}+i\psi_{0,yyy}=f(x),}
$$
where the integration constant may be any function of $x$. Conversely, differentiating this last equation with respect to $y$ recovers the compatibility equation.

For <separation of variables> matching the boundary <Fourier mode>, take $\psi_0=X(x)e^{iky}$ with $X(0)=1$. Because $\partial_y^3e^{iky}=-ik^3e^{iky}$, the reduced <ordinary differential equation> is

Therefore
$$
\boxed{\psi_0(x,y)=e^{-k^3x/2}e^{iky}.}

The <decay of a Fourier mode in a third-order spatial evolution equation> requires a sign check: this satisfies the requested limit at positive infinity precisely when $\boxed{k>0}$. For $k=0$ it remains identically one, while for $k<0$ its amplitude grows exponentially. Hence for those nonpositive values there is no separated solution meeting both prescribed <boundary conditions>; the printed assumption that $k$ is merely real needs this additional restriction for decay.

The homogeneous equation is a <Cauchy-Euler equation>. With the <power-law ansatz> $y=x^r$ on $x>0$, its <indicial equation> is
$$
r(r-1)+\alpha r+\frac{(\alpha-1)^2}{4}=\left(r+\frac{\alpha-1}{2}\right)^2=0.
$$
Thus $r=(1-\alpha)/2$ is a repeated <indicial root>. The two independent solutions are $x^r$ and $x^r\log x$, giving
$$
\boxed{y_h(x)=x^{(1-\alpha)/2}(C_1+C_2\log x),\qquad x>0.}
$$
Their <Wronskian>, with $W=y_1y_2'-y_1'y_2$, is $x^{2r-1}=x^{-\alpha}\ne0$, verifying <linear independence>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Choose the <causal Green function> convention $L_xG(x,\xi)=\delta(x-\xi)$ for each source point $\xi>0$, with $G(x,\xi)=0$ when $x<\xi$. This automatically gives the two zero-origin limits. The kernel is continuous at $x=\xi$, and integrating its equation across the source requires the unit <derivative> jump $\partial_xG(\xi^+,\xi)-\partial_xG(\xi^-,\xi)=1$.

For $x>\xi$, the homogeneous basis from part (a) gives a continuous kernel proportional to $x^r\log(x/\xi)$. Its derivative at $x=\xi$ is $\xi^{r-1}$, so the normalization is $\xi^{1-r}$. Hence the <causal Green function for a repeated-root Euler operator> is
$$
\boxed{G(x,\xi)=\begin{cases}x^r\xi^{1-r}\log(x/\xi),&x\geq\xi,\\0,&0\leq x<\xi,\end{cases}\qquad r=\frac{1-\alpha}{2},\quad\xi>0.}
$$
The same formula follows from $[y_1(\xi)y_2(x)-y_2(\xi)y_1(x)]/W(\xi)$ on the right of the source, with $W(\xi)=\xi^{-\alpha}$.

The singular endpoint matters for uniqueness. If $\alpha<-1$, then $r>1$, and both $x^r$ and $x^r\log x$, together with their first <derivatives>, vanish at zero. Adding any source-dependent linear combination of these homogeneous solutions to $G$ preserves the two stated origin conditions. Thus those conditions alone do not determine a unique <Green's function> in that parameter range. The causal requirement used here selects the displayed kernel. For $\alpha\geq-1$ no nonzero homogeneous solution satisfies both zero-origin limits, so this ambiguity is absent.

Using the causal <Green's function>,
$$
y_p(x)=\int_0^xG(x,\xi)\xi\,d\xi=x^r\int_0^x\xi^{2-r}\log(x/\xi)\,d\xi.
$$
Put $\xi=xs$. Since $\alpha>-5$ is equivalent to $r<3$, the <improper integral> converges and gives
$$
y_p(x)=x^3\int_0^1s^{2-r}(-\log s)\,ds=\frac{x^3}{(3-r)^2}=\boxed{\frac{4x^3}{(\alpha+5)^2}.}
$$
The elementary identity $\int_0^1s^{p-1}(-\log s)ds=1/p^2$ follows by differentiating $\int_0^1s^{p-1}ds=1/p$ for $p>0$. Direct substitution checks $L(x^3)=(\alpha+5)^2x/4$, and the displayed solution and its first <derivative> vanish at zero.

To give all solutions allowed by the printed <boundary conditions>, retain the singular-endpoint qualification from part (b):
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{4x^3}{(\alpha+5)^2}+x^{(1-\alpha)/2}(C_1+C_2\log x),&-5<\alpha<-1,\\\dfrac{4x^3}{(\alpha+5)^2},&\alpha\geq-1.\end{cases}}
$$
Thus the causal integral gives a particular member of a two-parameter family in the first range and the unique solution in the second.

For <integrable functions> $f,g$, <Fubini's theorem> justifies exchanging the two <integrals> in the <Fourier transform> of the <convolution>. Set $s=x-t$ to obtain
$$
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde h(k)&=\int_{\mathbb R}\int_{\mathbb R}f(t)g(x-t)e^{-ikx}\,dt\,dx\\
&=\int_{\mathbb R}\int_{\mathbb R}f(t)g(s)e^{-ik(s+t)}\,ds\,dt\\
&=\left[\int_{\mathbb R}f(t)e^{-ikt}dt\right]\left[\int_{\mathbb R}g(s)e^{-iks}ds\right].
\end{aligned}
$$
This proves the <convolution theorem> with the stated normalization:
$$
\boxed{\widetilde h(k)=\widetilde f(k)\widetilde g(k).}
$$

Direct evaluation gives the first <Fourier transform>:
$$
\widetilde f(k)=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}e^{-ikx}\,dx=\boxed{\frac{2\sin(k/2)}{k}},\qquad\boxed{\widetilde f(0)=1}.
$$
For the even <triangular function>, the odd sine part integrates to zero. Two elementary <integrations by parts> or one evaluation of the cosine integral give
$$
\widetilde g(k)=2\int_0^1(1-x)\cos(kx)\,dx=\frac{2(1-\cos k)}{k^2}=\boxed{\frac{4\sin^2(k/2)}{k^2}},\qquad\boxed{\widetilde g(0)=1}.
$$
The values at zero are the respective areas, agreeing with the removable limits. Therefore $\widetilde g=\widetilde f^{\,2}$. By the <convolution theorem> and uniqueness of the <Fourier transform>,
$$
\boxed{(f*f)(x)=g(x)=\begin{cases}1-|x|,&|x|<1,\\0,&|x|\geq1.\end{cases}}
$$
There is also a direct interpretation: the <convolution> is the length of overlap of the intervals $(-1/2,1/2)$ and $(x-1/2,x+1/2)$, giving exactly that triangular profile.

The <Parseval identity> for this <Fourier transform> convention is
$$
\boxed{\int_{\mathbb R}|h(x)|^2dx=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}|\widetilde h(k)|^2dk.}

Apply it to $h=g$. Its squared <norm> is $2\int_0^1(1-x)^2dx=2/3$, so
$$
\frac23=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}\frac{16\sin^4(k/2)}{k^4}\,dk.

Under $k=2x$, the last integrand times $dk$ becomes $2\sin^4x\,dx/x^4$. Consequently

The integrand has continuous value $1$ at zero and decays integrably at infinity, so this is an ordinary convergent <improper integral>.

A square <matrix> is a <diagonalizable matrix> over the chosen field when there are an <invertible matrix> $P$ and a <diagonal matrix> $D$ such that $A=PDP^{-1}$. If $v_j$ is column $j$ of $P$ and $D_{jj}=\lambda_j$, the identity $AP=PD$ gives $Av_j=\lambda_jv_j$. Invertibility makes these $n$ columns <linearly independent> <eigenvectors>.

Conversely, put $n$ <linearly independent> <eigenvectors> in the columns of $P$ and their <eigenvalues> in $D$. Then $P$ is invertible and $AP=PD$, giving
$$
\boxed{A\text{ is diagonalizable}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ A\text{ has an eigenbasis of }n\text{ vectors}.}
$$
Repeated <eigenvalues> are allowed; the requirement is a complete <eigenbasis>, not distinctness of the eigenvalues.

= Solution

A single <eigenbasis> works for every choice of the parameters. The columns $v_1=(1,0,0)^T$, $v_2=(1,1,0)^T$, $v_3=(1,1,1)^T$ satisfy
$$
Mv_1=av_1,\qquad Mv_2=bv_2,\qquad Mv_3=cv_3.
$$
They are <linearly independent> because their column <matrix> has determinant one. Thus the <diagonalization> is
$$
\boxed{P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\0&1&1\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad P^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0\\0&1&-1\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad M=P\operatorname{diag}(a,b,c)P^{-1}.}
$$
This formula includes all coincidences among $a,b,c$, so no special repeated-<eigenvalue> cases need separate bases.

= Solution

The <diagonalization> in part (b) gives $M^k=P\operatorname{diag}(a^k,b^k,c^k)P^{-1}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{M^k\to0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ |a|<1,\ |b|<1,\ |c|<1.}
$$
Sufficiency follows from the scalar powers tending to zero; necessity also follows immediately from the diagonal entries of $M^k$. Under these conditions the <matrix geometric series> converges, and its sum is
$$
(I-M)^{-1}=P\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac1{1-a},\frac1{1-b},\frac1{1-c}\right)P^{-1}.
$$
Writing $A_0=1/(1-a)$, $B_0=1/(1-b)$ and $C_0=1/(1-c)$, all entries are explicitly
$$
\boxed{(I-M)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}A_0&B_0-A_0&C_0-B_0\\0&B_0&C_0-B_0\\0&0&C_0\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
For example, $B_0-A_0=(b-a)/[(1-a)(1-b)]$ and $C_0-B_0=(c-b)/[(1-b)(1-c)]$. These expressions remain valid when parameter values coincide.

Expand the <quadratic form> of this <Gram matrix>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(\mathbf c)&=\sum_{i,j}c_ic_j\mathbf x_i^T\mathbf x_j\\

proving the <positive definiteness of a Gram matrix> in the independent-vector case.

A <skew-Hermitian matrix> satisfies $A^\dagger=-A$, where $\dagger$ denotes the <conjugate transpose>. If $Av=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$, then
$$
\overline\lambda\,v^\dagger v=v^\dagger A^\dagger v=-v^\dagger Av=-\lambda\,v^\dagger v.
$$
Since $v^\dagger v>0$, this gives $\boxed{\overline\lambda=-\lambda,\text{ so }\lambda\in i\mathbb R}$.

A <unitary matrix> satisfies $U^\dagger U=UU^\dagger=I$. It preserves the <Hermitian inner product> and <norm>. Thus if $Uv=\mu v$,
$$
\|v\|^2=\|Uv\|^2=|\mu|^2\|v\|^2,
$$
and therefore $\boxed{|\mu|=1}$. Zero is allowed among the purely imaginary <eigenvalues> in the first statement, but not among the unitary <eigenvalues>.

Since both real <matrices> are antisymmetric, $A^\dagger=-A$ and $B^\dagger=-B$. Thus $U^\dagger=-A+iB$. Expand the two unitary identities:
$$
UU^\dagger=-A^2-B^2+i(AB-BA)=I,\qquad U^\dagger U=-A^2-B^2-i(AB-BA)=I.
$$
Subtracting and adding give $\boxed{AB=BA,\qquad A^2+B^2=-I}$. Because $A$ has simple <eigenvalues>, each of its complex <eigenspaces> is one-dimensional. Commutation makes each one invariant under $B$: if $Av=av$, then $A(Bv)=a(Bv)$. Hence $Bv=bv$ for some scalar $b$. The complete <eigenbasis> of $A$ is therefore a common <eigenbasis>, which also diagonalizes $U$.

The <skew-Hermitian decomposition of a unitary matrix> is explicit:
$$
A=\frac{U-U^\dagger}{2},\qquad B=\frac{U+U^\dagger}{2i}.
$$
If $Uv=uv$, unitarity gives $U^\dagger v=u^{-1}v=\overline u\,v$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{a=\frac{u-\overline u}{2}=i\operatorname{Im}u,\qquad b=\frac{u+\overline u}{2i}=-i\operatorname{Re}u.}
$$
Equivalently, for $u=e^{i\theta}$ the paired <eigenvalues> are $\boxed{a=i\sin\theta,\ b=-i\cos\theta}$. The hypotheses can only occur in even dimension: $U^T=-U$ and $U$ is invertible, whereas an odd-dimensional <skew-symmetric matrix> has zero <determinant>.

The <Cauchy-Riemann equations> are $\boxed{u_x=v_y,\quad u_y=-v_x}$. They give the <gradient> identity
$$
\nabla u\cdot\nabla v=u_xv_x+u_yv_y=v_yv_x-v_xv_y=0.
$$
At a regular intersection, where $f'(z)\ne0$, the two nonzero <gradients> are <normal vectors> to the two <level sets>, so their tangent directions are perpendicular. This is the <orthogonality of regular real and imaginary level curves>.

The regularity qualification is necessary: $|\nabla u|=|\nabla v|=|f'|$, so at a <critical point> both normals vanish and the curves need not have unique tangents. For example $f=z^2$ gives diagonal lines for $u=0$ and coordinate axes for $v=0$, all meeting at the origin. Thus the conclusion is $\boxed{\text{right-angle intersection wherever }f'(z)\ne0}$, rather than an unrestricted claim at critical points.

Write $g=u+iv$ and let its constant <modulus> be $C$. If $C=0$, then $g=0$. For $C>0$, differentiating $u^2+v^2=C^2$ with respect to $x$ and $y$, then applying the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>, gives
$$
u u_x+v v_x=0,\qquad u u_y+v v_y=-u v_x+v u_x=0.
$$
Thus
$$
\begin{pmatrix}u&v\\v&-u\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u_x\\v_x\end{pmatrix}=0.
$$
The coefficient <matrix> has <determinant> $-(u^2+v^2)=-C^2\ne0$, forcing $u_x=v_x=0$. The <Cauchy-Riemann equations> then also give $u_y=v_y=0$. Therefore $u,v$ are constant on a connected domain and
$$
\boxed{g\text{ is constant}.}
$$
This proves the <constant-modulus holomorphic function> result. If a domain is disconnected, the conclusion applies separately to each connected component.

The zeros of $\sin z$ are $z=n\pi$ for $n\in\mathbb Z$, all simple, and $\cos(n\pi)=(-1)^n\ne0$. Hence the finite singularities of $\cot z$ are exactly
$$
\boxed{z=n\pi,\quad n\in\mathbb Z,\quad\text{all simple poles}.}
$$
Locally $\cot z\sim1/(z-n\pi)$, so each <pole> has <residue> $1$.

= Solution

For $z\ne0$, singularities occur when $1/z=n\pi$, with $n\ne0$. Each is a <simple pole>, since the substitution has nonzero <derivative> there:
$$
\boxed{z_n=\frac1{n\pi},\qquad n\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}.}
$$
More explicitly, $1/z-n\pi\sim-(z-z_n)/z_n^2$, so $\cot(1/z)\sim-z_n^2/(z-z_n)$ and its <residue> is $-1/(n^2\pi^2)$.

These <poles> accumulate at zero. Consequently $\boxed{z=0\text{ is a non-isolated singularity}}$. The removable/pole/essential classification for <isolated singularities> cannot be applied at that accumulation point; it is not an isolated <essential singularity>.

At every $z=n\pi$, the inner function has a <simple pole>, with $\cot z=1/(z-n\pi)+h(z)$ for analytic $h$. Thus
$$
e^{\cot z}=e^{h(z)}\exp\left(\frac1{z-n\pi}\right).
$$
The first factor is analytic and nonzero, while the second has infinitely many negative powers in its <Laurent series>. Therefore, by the <exponential of a pole is an essential singularity> result,
$$
\boxed{z=n\pi,\quad n\in\mathbb Z,\quad\text{all essential singularities}.}
$$
At all other finite points the composition is analytic, so there are no additional finite singularities.

Set $w=z-1$. <Partial fraction decomposition> gives
$$
\frac{2z}{z^2+1}=\frac1{z-i}+\frac1{z+i}=\frac1{1-i+w}+\frac1{1+i+w}.
$$
Expand both terms as <geometric series>:
$$
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^n\left[(1-i)^{-n-1}+(1+i)^{-n-1}\right]w^n.
$$
The two bracketed terms are complex conjugates. Using $1\pm i=\sqrt2e^{\pm i\pi/4}$ produces the required real coefficients:
$$
\boxed{f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^n2^{(1-n)/2}\cos\left(\frac{(n+1)\pi}{4}\right)(z-1)^n.}
$$
The initial terms are $1-\tfrac12w^2+\tfrac12w^3-\tfrac14w^4+\cdots$. The nearest singularities are the <simple poles> $z=\pm i$, both at distance $\sqrt2$ from the center. Hence the <Taylor series> has
$$
\boxed{R=\sqrt2,\qquad|z-1|<\sqrt2.}
$$

A finite point $x_0$ is an <ordinary point> when the normal-form coefficients $p,q$ are analytic at $x_0$. Then arbitrary values $y(x_0),y'(x_0)$ determine a convergent <Taylor series>, and two independent choices give two <linearly independent> local analytic solutions.

A singular point is a <regular singular point> when $(x-x_0)p(x)$ and $(x-x_0)^2q(x)$ extend analytically to $x_0$. The <Frobenius method> then provides at least one solution
$$
\boxed{y=(x-x_0)^\sigma\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n(x-x_0)^n,\qquad a_0\ne0,}
$$
with a locally convergent analytic factor and an exponent fixed by the <indicial equation>. If its two <indicial roots> differ by a noninteger, there are two independent <Frobenius solutions>. For integer-separated roots a second solution may include a logarithm; a repeated root gives a <logarithmic Frobenius solution> accompanying the single pure series. Such solutions need not be finite or ordinary <Taylor series> at the singular point.

Put $t=1-x$ and $Y(t)=y(x)$. The <differential equation> becomes
$$
tY''+Y'+(c^2t+\lambda)Y=0.
$$
Substitute the <Frobenius solution> $Y=t^\sigma\sum_{n\geq0}a_nt^n$. The lowest power has coefficient $\sigma^2a_0$, so
$$
\boxed{\sigma=0\quad\text{is the repeated indicial root}.}
$$
Equating successive coefficients gives, with $a_{-1}=0$,
$$
\boxed{n^2a_n+\lambda a_{n-1}+c^2a_{n-2}=0\quad(n\geq1),\qquad a_n=-\frac{\lambda a_{n-1}+c^2a_{n-2}}{n^2}.}
$$
Thus $a_0\ne0$ is the only free coefficient: $a_1=-\lambda a_0$, $a_2=(\lambda^2-c^2)a_0/4$, and so on. Since every denominator is nonzero, there is exactly one independent pure <Frobenius solution> of this form. The repeated root supplies no second free coefficient; the other solution is logarithmic, as parts (iii) and (iv) demonstrate.

For the normal-form coefficient $p(x)=-1/(1-x)$, <Abel's identity> gives the <Wronskian>

Write $t=1-x$. The analytic solution has $y_1=a_0+O(t)$ with $a_0\ne0$, so the integrand in the <reduction of order> formula satisfies
$$
\frac1{(1-x)y_1(x)^2}=\frac1{a_0^2(1-x)}+O(1).
$$
Integrating with respect to $x$ gives $-a_0^{-2}\log(1-x)+O(1)$. Multiplication by $y_1$ therefore gives
$$
y_2(x)=-\frac1{a_0}\log(1-x)+O(1).
$$
Any solution independent of $y_1$ has a nonzero coefficient of this second solution. Hence
$$
\boxed{y(x)=K\log(1-x)+O(1),\qquad K\ne0,\quad x\to1^-.}
$$
An added multiple of $y_1$ stays bounded and cannot cancel this logarithm. On the real side $x>1$, the corresponding statement uses $\log|1-x|$.

The <Fermat principle> makes the <optical path length> stationary. For a graph $y=\xi(x)$ the variational integrand is
$$
\mathcal L(x,\xi,\xi')=n(x,\xi)\sqrt{1+\xi'^2}.
$$
The <Euler-Lagrange equation> is
$$
\frac d{dx}\left[\frac{n(x,\xi)\xi'}{\sqrt{1+\xi'^2}}\right]-n_y(x,\xi)\sqrt{1+\xi'^2}=0.
$$
Use the <chain rule> $d n(x,\xi(x))/dx=n_x+n_y\xi'$ and differentiate $\xi'/\sqrt{1+\xi'^2}$ to obtain
$$
\frac{(n_x+n_y\xi')\xi'}{\sqrt{1+\xi'^2}}+\frac{n\xi''}{(1+\xi'^2)^{3/2}}-n_y\sqrt{1+\xi'^2}=0.
$$
Multiplying through and combining the $n_y$ terms gives the required second-order <ordinary differential equation> with no derivatives in denominators:
$$
\boxed{n(x,\xi)\xi''=(1+\xi'^2)[n_y(x,\xi)-\xi'n_x(x,\xi)].}
$$
All partial <derivatives> of the <refractive index> are evaluated along the ray.

Introduce a constant <Lagrange multiplier> $\mu$ for this <isoperimetric constraint> and make $A-\mu(C-B)$ stationary. Its integrand is $(1-\mu)y-\mu y'^2$, so the <Euler-Lagrange equation> gives
$$
1-\mu+2\mu y''=0.
$$
The endpoint conditions therefore force a parabola $y=Kx(1-x)$. Its area and budget are
$$
A=\frac K6,\qquad C=\frac K6+\frac{K^2}{3}=B.
$$
Solving the quadratic and choosing positive area gives
$$
\boxed{K=\frac{\sqrt{1+48B}-1}{4},\qquad y_{\mathrm{opt}}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{1+48B}-1}{4}x(1-x),\qquad A_{\max}=\frac{\sqrt{1+48B}-1}{24}.}
$$
This requires $B>0$; no positive-area barrier is possible for a nonpositive budget. The corresponding multiplier is $\mu=1/(1+4K)>0$.

To verify a global maximum rather than just stationarity, use the <sharp area-cost bound for a barrier with fixed endpoints>. For $w=x(1-x)$, <integration by parts> gives $2A=\int_0^1y'w'dx$, and $\int_0^1w'^2dx=1/3$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> implies
$$
\int_0^1y'^2dx\geq12A^2,\qquad B\geq A+12A^2.
$$
For $A>0$ the last expression increases strictly, yielding exactly the boxed upper bound. Equality requires $y'$ proportional to $w'$, hence $y=Kw$ after imposing the endpoints. Thus the displayed optimum is unique and lies above the baseline throughout the interior.

Choose homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions> $\psi(a)=\psi(b)=0$, and take a nonzero real admissible trial $\psi$. Write the numerator as $F[\psi]$ and denominator as $G[\psi]>0$. For an endpoint-zero <variation> $\eta$, <integration by parts> gives
$$
\delta F=2\int_a^b[p\psi'\eta'+q\psi\eta]dx=2\int_a^b[-(p\psi')'+q\psi]\eta\,dx,\qquad\delta G=2\int_a^bw\psi\eta\,dx.
$$
Thus the <first variation> of the <Rayleigh quotient> is
$$
\delta\Lambda=\frac2G\int_a^b[-(p\psi')'+q\psi-\Lambda[\psi]w\psi]\eta\,dx.
$$
By the <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations>, stationarity is equivalent to
$$
\boxed{-(p\psi')'+q\psi=\lambda w\psi,\qquad\lambda=\Lambda[\psi],\qquad\psi(a)=\psi(b)=0.}
$$
Conversely, multiplying an <eigenfunction> equation by $\psi$ and integrating gives $F=\lambda G$, so its <first variation> vanishes. This establishes the equivalence for the chosen <Sturm-Liouville problem>. Homogeneous <Neumann boundary conditions>, or a mixed Dirichlet-Neumann pair, also cancel the boundary term; general <Robin boundary conditions> require adding the corresponding boundary contribution to the numerator.

Use the normalized <eigenfunctions> and their <weighted inner product> $\int_a^bw\overline{\psi_m}\psi_n dx=\delta_{mn}$. <Orthogonality> gives
$$
G[\widetilde\psi]=\sum_{n\geq0}|a_n|^2,\qquad F[\widetilde\psi]=\int_a^b\overline{\widetilde\psi}\,\mathcal L\widetilde\psi\,dx=\sum_{n\geq0}\lambda_n|a_n|^2.
$$
For complex trials, $F$ means the corresponding Hermitian <quadratic form> with $|\psi'|^2,|\psi|^2$; it agrees with the displayed functional on real trials. If $a_0\ne0$, division by $|a_0|^2$ yields
$$
\boxed{\Lambda[\widetilde\psi]=\frac{\lambda_0+\sum_{n\geq1}|b_n|^2\lambda_n}{1+\sum_{n\geq1}|b_n|^2},\qquad b_n=\frac{a_n}{a_0}.}
$$
Since $\lambda_n\geq\lambda_0$, this is at least $\lambda_0$. The <Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle> therefore gives an upper bound from any admissible trial, improved by minimizing over trial parameters or enlarging the trial space. Equality holds when the trial is entirely in the lowest <eigenspace>.

The displayed ratio using $b_n$ presupposes $a_0\ne0$. If $a_0=0$, use the undivided quotient $\sum\lambda_n|a_n|^2/\sum|a_n|^2$ instead; the same upper-bound argument still applies. No ground-state coefficient can be divided out in that case.

For the two sine modes, <orthogonality> gives
$$
\int_0^1\widetilde\psi^2dx=\frac{1+\alpha^2}{2},\qquad\int_0^1\widetilde\psi'^2dx=\frac{\pi^2}{2}(1+4\alpha^2).
$$
The diagonal potential integrals vanish, while the mixed one is
$$
\int_0^1\sin(\pi x)\sin(2\pi x)\cos(\pi x)dx=\frac14.
$$
Thus $\int_0^1\cos(\pi x)\widetilde\psi^2dx=\alpha/2$, giving the <two-sine variational bound for a Dirichlet Mathieu equation>:
$$
\boxed{R(\alpha)=\frac{\pi^2+\alpha+4\pi^2\alpha^2}{1+\alpha^2}.}
$$
Its <derivative> vanishes when $1+6\pi^2\alpha-\alpha^2=0$. Hence the two stationary parameters are $3\pi^2\pm\sqrt{9\pi^4+1}$. To identify the minimum, use the normalized sine basis: the restricted <self-adjoint operator> has real <symmetric matrix>
$$
H_{\mathrm{trial}}=\begin{pmatrix}\pi^2&1/2\\1/2&4\pi^2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its smaller <eigenvalue> is the minimum of the <Rayleigh quotient>, attained at the negative stationary parameter. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\alpha_{\mathrm{opt}}=3\pi^2-\sqrt{9\pi^4+1},\qquad\lambda_0\leq\frac{5\pi^2-\sqrt{9\pi^4+1}}2\approx9.86116.}
$$
The estimate improves on the single-sine value $\pi^2$. The <Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle> remains valid here even though $q(x)=\cos(\pi x)$ changes sign: on this finite interval the <Sturm-Liouville operator> is self-adjoint and bounded below, which is sufficient for the upper bound.
