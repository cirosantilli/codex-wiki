= Solution
{parent=a}

Seek an integrating factor $\mu$ such that the differential expression becomes $-(py')'$. Its leading coefficient requires $p=\mu(1-x^2)$, while the first-derivative coefficient requires $-p'=3x\mu$. Thus
$$
(1-x^2)\mu'-2x\mu=-3x\mu,\qquad
\frac{\mu'}\mu=-\frac{x}{1-x^2}.
$$
Choose the positive solution $\mu=\sqrt{1-x^2}$. The <Sturm-Liouville form> is therefore
$$
\boxed{-\left[(1-x^2)^{3/2}y_n'\right]'=(n^2-1)\sqrt{1-x^2}\,y_n,}
$$
with
$$
\boxed{p=(1-x^2)^{3/2},\qquad q=0,\qquad w=\sqrt{1-x^2}.}
$$
The weight is positive on the open interval. The endpoints are singular because $p$ vanishes there, but the prescribed boundedness of $y_n,y_n'$ makes the integration-by-parts boundary terms vanish.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Multiply the <Sturm-Liouville form> for $y_n$ by $y_m$ and subtract the equation for $y_m$ multiplied by $y_n$. Integration gives
$$
(n^2-m^2)\int_{-1}^1w\,y_ny_m\,dx
=\left[p(y_ny_m'-y_my_n')\right]_{-1}^1=0.
$$
The boundary expression is zero because $p\to0$ and the functions and first <derivatives> remain finite. Since $n,m$ are distinct positive integers, this proves
$$
\boxed{\int_{-1}^1y_ny_m\sqrt{1-x^2}\,dx=0.}
$$
For the <derivatives>, apply <integration by parts> once to the same <Sturm-Liouville form>:
$$
\int_{-1}^1p\,y_n'y_m'\,dx
=\left[py_n'y_m\right]_{-1}^1-\int_{-1}^1(py_n')'y_m\,dx
=(n^2-1)\int_{-1}^1w\,y_ny_m\,dx=0.
$$
Thus the new weight is
$$
\boxed{w_1(x)=(1-x^2)^{3/2}.}
$$
This is <derivative orthogonality when the Sturm-Liouville potential vanishes>. For $n=1$ the <derivative> is identically zero, which is consistent with the identity.

= Solution

Put $Y(\theta)=y(\cos\theta)$, with $0<\theta<\pi$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
y'=-\frac{Y'}{\sin\theta},\qquad
y''=\frac{Y''}{\sin^2\theta}-\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin^3\theta}Y'.
$$
Consequently the original differential expression becomes $-Y''-2\cot\theta\,Y'$. If $Y=F/\sin\theta$, direct differentiation gives
$$
Y''+2\cot\theta\,Y'=\frac{F''+F}{\sin\theta}.
$$
Taking $F=\sin n\theta$ therefore yields
$$
-Y''-2\cot\theta\,Y'=(n^2-1)Y,
\qquad
\boxed{y_n(x)=\frac{\sin(n\arccos x)}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}.}
$$
The apparent endpoint singularities are removable. Expansion at $\theta=0,\pi$ gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
y_n(1)&=n,&y_n(-1)&=(-1)^{n-1}n,\\
y_n'(1)&=\frac{n(n^2-1)}3,&y_n'(-1)&=(-1)^n\frac{n(n^2-1)}3.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus both endpoint regularity conditions hold. This is the <Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind> $U_{n-1}(x)$.

{parent=1}

= Solution
{parent=d}

The trigonometric addition formula gives
$$
\sin((n+1)\theta)+\sin((n-1)\theta)=2\cos\theta\sin(n\theta).
$$
Dividing by $\sin\theta$ and writing $x=\cos\theta$ gives the <recurrence relation>
$$
\boxed{y_{n+1}=2xy_n-y_{n-1}.}
$$
The first two members are $y_1=1$ and $y_2=2x$. If $y_n$ has degree $n-1$ and leading coefficient $2^{n-1}$, then $2xy_n$ has degree $n$ and leading coefficient $2^n$, whereas $y_{n-1}$ has lower degree and cannot cancel that term. <Mathematical induction> proves
$$
\boxed{\deg y_n=n-1,\qquad\text{leading coefficient of }y_n=2^{n-1}.}
$$
For example the sequence begins $1,2x,4x^2-1,8x^3-4x$, agreeing with the <Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind>.

= Solution

Use <separation of variables> in the form $u=X(x)T(t)$, with $T'/T=X''/X=-\lambda$. Then $T=e^{-\lambda t}$ and the spatial <Sturm-Liouville problem> is
$$
-X''=\lambda X,\qquad X'(0)=X(0),\qquad X'(1)=X(1).
$$
These real separated <Robin boundary conditions> give a <self-adjoint differential operator>, so the <eigenvalues> are real. All three sign cases must be considered.

If $\lambda=\mu^2>0$, write $X=A\cos\mu x+B\sin\mu x$. The first condition gives $B=A/\mu$, while the second reduces to
$$
-A\left(\mu+\frac1\mu\right)\sin\mu=0.
$$
Thus $\mu=n\pi$, and scaling the <eigenfunction> gives $V_n=n\pi\cos(n\pi x)+\sin(n\pi x)$.

If $\lambda=0$, then $X=A+Bx$. The first condition gives $B=A$, and the second gives $B=A+B$, so $A=B=0$; there is no zero <eigenvalue>.

If $\lambda=-\kappa^2<0$, write $X=A\cosh\kappa x+B\sinh\kappa x$. The first condition gives $B=A/\kappa$, and the second becomes
$$
A\left(\kappa-\frac1\kappa\right)\sinh\kappa=0.
$$
Hence $\kappa=1$, giving the single negative <eigenvalue> $-1$ and spatial <eigenfunction> $e^x$. Completeness of this regular <Sturm-Liouville problem> now gives
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=c_0e^{t+x}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_ne^{-n^2\pi^2t}\left[n\pi\cos(n\pi x)+\sin(n\pi x)\right].}
$$
This is <Robin diffusion with a growing mode>. The growth is compatible with the stated conditions: they prescribe the same coordinate <derivative> at both ends, not identical dissipative outward-normal conditions.

= Solution

Project the <initial condition> onto the orthogonal spatial <eigenfunctions>. Their squared norms in the ordinary <inner product> are
$$
\int_0^1e^{2x}\,dx=\frac{e^2-1}{2},\qquad
\int_0^1V_n(x)^2\,dx=\frac{n^2\pi^2+1}{2}.
$$
The latter follows from the integrals of $\cos^2(n\pi x)$ and $\sin^2(n\pi x)$ being $1/2$, with the sine-cosine cross term zero. Therefore
$$
\boxed{c_0=\frac2{e^2-1}\int_0^1f(x)e^x\,dx,\qquad
c_n=\frac2{n^2\pi^2+1}\int_0^1f(x)\left[n\pi\cos(n\pi x)+\sin(n\pi x)\right]dx.}
$$
For square-integrable data the expansion converges to the data in the <L2 norm> at $t=0$; for $t>0$ the decaying high modes are smoothed. Initial data with zero $e^x$ projection have no growing term.

Use the <Dirichlet Green function> with the sign convention
$$
\boxed{\Delta_{\mathbf r}G(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=\delta(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')\text{ in }V,\qquad G(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=0\text{ on }S.}
$$
The <normal derivative> is taken with respect to the integration variable and the outward normal of $V$. <Green's second identity> gives
$$
\int_V\left(u\Delta G-G\Delta u\right)dV
=\oint_S\left(u\partial_nG-G\partial_nu\right)dS.
$$
The <Dirac delta distribution> evaluates the first volume term at $\mathbf r'$. Substituting $\Delta u=\rho$, $u=f$ on $S$ and $G=0$ there yields
$$
u(\mathbf r')-\int_VG(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')\rho(\mathbf r)\,dV
=\oint_Sf(\mathbf r)\partial_nG(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')\,dS.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{u(\mathbf r')=\int_V\rho(\mathbf r)G(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')\,dV+\oint_Sf(\mathbf r)\partial_nG(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')\,dS.}
$$
One can justify applying the identity to the singular <Green function> by removing a small sphere around the source and then taking its radius to zero.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The three-dimensional <fundamental solution of the Laplace equation>, with $\Delta F=+\delta$, is
$$
\boxed{F(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=-\frac1{4\pi|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}.}
$$
It is <harmonic> away from the source, and its gradient has total outward <flux> one through a small source-centred sphere.

For $s=|\mathbf r'|\in(0,1)$, place the image at $\mathbf r^*=\mathbf r'/s^2$, outside the unit ball. On $|\mathbf r|=1$, expansion of squared distances gives $|\mathbf r-\mathbf r^*|=|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|/s$. The <method of images> therefore gives the <Dirichlet Green function inside a sphere>
$$
\boxed{G_0(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=-\frac1{4\pi}\left[\frac1{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}-\frac1{s|\mathbf r-\mathbf r^*|}\right].}
$$
The boundary value is zero by the distance identity, and the image term is harmonic throughout the physical ball. Thus $\Delta G_0=\delta(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')$ there. At the central source, take the finite limit of the weighted image term:
$$
\boxed{G_0(\mathbf r,0)=-\frac1{4\pi|\mathbf r|}+\frac1{4\pi}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=c}

Reflect the physical source across $z=0$, writing $\overline{\mathbf r'}=(x',y',-z')$. Its radius is still $s=|\mathbf r'|$, and its spherical image is $\overline{\mathbf r^*}$. The <Dirichlet Green function inside a hemisphere> is
$$
\boxed{G_1(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=G_0(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')-G_0(\mathbf r,\overline{\mathbf r'}).}
$$
Explicitly this four-source <method of images> is
$$
G_1=-\frac1{4\pi}\left[
\frac1{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}-\frac1{|\mathbf r-\overline{\mathbf r'}|}
-\frac1{s|\mathbf r-\mathbf r^*|}+\frac1{s|\mathbf r-\overline{\mathbf r^*}|}\right].
$$
Both $G_0$ terms vanish on the spherical cap. On the flat equatorial disc, reflection equates the two physical-source distances and also the two image-source distances, so both pairs cancel. The reflected physical source lies in the lower half-ball and the two inverted images lie outside the sphere. Consequently the only source in the upper half-ball is $\mathbf r'$, with the correct coefficient:
$$
\boxed{\Delta_{\mathbf r}G_1=\delta(\mathbf r-\mathbf r'),\qquad G_1=0\text{ on both boundary pieces}.}
$$
The <Dirichlet uniqueness for the Poisson equation> then identifies this as the required Green function.

Specify the branch before defining a signed jump. Choose $\phi<\arg z<\phi+2\pi$, and let $F_+$ be the value on the side with argument tending to $\phi$ from above, while $F_-$ is the value on the other side, with argument tending to $\phi+2\pi$ from below. The entire function $f$ has the same limiting value on both sides, so
$$
F_+(r)=f(re^{i\phi})r^{-\alpha}e^{-i\alpha\phi},\qquad
F_-(r)=e^{-2\pi i\alpha}F_+(r).
$$
Thus the <jump of a fractional power across a branch cut> is
$$
\boxed{F_+-F_-=(1-e^{-2\pi i\alpha})f(re^{i\phi})r^{-\alpha}e^{-i\alpha\phi}.}
$$
Equivalently the ratio of the limiting values is $F_-/F_+=e^{-2\pi i\alpha}$ wherever $f$ is nonzero. Reversing the definition of the jump reverses its sign. Choosing a different branch multiplies both limits by the corresponding common phase.

The factor $z^{-1/4}$ has <algebraic branch points> at zero and infinity. Near zero the leading behaviour is $z^{-1/4}$; with $w=1/z$, the behaviour near infinity is $g(1/w)\sim w^{9/4}$. These are four-sheeted branch points.

The <polynomial> denominator vanishes at $z=\pm i$, with nonzero <derivative> $2z$, and the fractional factor is finite and nonzero near either point on any chosen local branch. Hence
$$
\boxed{z=\pm i\text{ are simple poles};\qquad z=0,\infty\text{ are branch points}.}
$$
A single-valued <holomorphic> branch is obtained by choosing a <branch cut> joining zero to infinity. Points along the chosen cut mark its discontinuity, rather than additional isolated poles.

= Solution

For this real integral choose $0<\arg z<2\pi$, so the upper bank of the positive real axis has $z^{1/4}=x^{1/4}>0$. Use a counterclockwise <keyhole contour> with inner radius $\varepsilon$ and outer radius $R$, enclosing $\pm i$. The upper-bank contribution tends to $J$. On the lower bank, $z^{-1/4}=e^{-i\pi/2}x^{-1/4}=-ix^{-1/4}$, and the reversed orientation makes its contribution $+iJ$.

The large arc is $O(R^{-5/4})$ because $g=O(R^{-9/4})$, while the small arc is $O(\varepsilon^{3/4})$. Both vanish, giving
$$
\oint g(z)\,dz\longrightarrow(1+i)J.
$$
The <residues> on this branch are
$$
\operatorname{Res}(g,i)=\frac{e^{-i\pi/8}}{2i},\qquad
\operatorname{Res}(g,-i)=-\frac{e^{-3i\pi/8}}{2i}.
$$
The <residue theorem> yields
$$
(1+i)J=\pi\left(e^{-i\pi/8}-e^{-3i\pi/8}\right)
=2\pi i e^{-i\pi/4}\sin\frac\pi8.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{J=\pi\sqrt2\sin\frac\pi8=\frac{\pi}{2\sin(3\pi/8)}=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}}.}
$$
The branch cut in (a) was arbitrary; here placing it on the integration axis makes the bank contributions directly proportional to the desired positive-real integral.

Put $a=1/L\in(0,1)$. The <Fourier transform of a derivative> turns the differential operator into $k^4+5k^2+4=(k^2+1)(k^2+4)$, so
$$
\boxed{\widetilde y(k)=\frac{2a}{(k^2+a^2)(k^2+1)(k^2+4)}.}
$$
Use the <partial fraction decomposition>
$$
\widetilde y=2a\left[\frac1{(1-a^2)(4-a^2)(k^2+a^2)}
-\frac1{3(1-a^2)(k^2+1)}+\frac1{3(4-a^2)(k^2+4)}\right].
$$
The inverse-transform pair $\mathcal F^{-1}[(k^2+b^2)^{-1}]=e^{-b|x|}/(2b)$ is the <one-dimensional modified Helmholtz Green function>. Hence
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{e^{-a|x|}}{(1-a^2)(4-a^2)}-\frac{a e^{-|x|}}{3(1-a^2)}+\frac{a e^{-2|x|}}{6(4-a^2)}.}
$$
In terms of the original length scale,
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{L^4e^{-|x|/L}}{(L^2-1)(4L^2-1)}-\frac{Le^{-|x|}}{3(L^2-1)}+\frac{Le^{-2|x|}}{6(4L^2-1)}.}
$$
This is the <convolution> of $h$ with the <fourth-order Green kernel with two decay scales>, here $K(x)=e^{-|x|}/6-e^{-2|x|}/12$. It decays at both infinities. Although the individual exponential pieces have cusps, their <derivative> jumps through order three cancel, so the result has no unintended <Dirac delta distribution> at zero. The decaying solution is unique: the homogeneous solutions $e^{\pm x},e^{\pm2x}$ have no nonzero globally decaying combination.

For a Cartesian change of components $x_i'=R_{ij}x_j$, a rank-$n$ <tensor> transforms according to
$$
\boxed{T'_{i_1\cdots i_n}=R_{i_1j_1}\cdots R_{i_nj_n}T_{j_1\cdots j_n},}
$$
with repeated indices summed. An <isotropic tensor> has unchanged components under every proper <rotation matrix>.

For rank two write $T'=RTR^T$. Invariance under quarter-turns implies invariance under their squares, the coordinate half-turns
$$
R_x(\pi)=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1),\qquad R_y(\pi)=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,-1).
$$
Each off-diagonal entry changes sign under at least one of these, so all off-diagonal entries vanish. A quarter-turn about the third axis then interchanges the first two diagonal entries, making them equal; a quarter-turn about the first axis equates the other two. Therefore
$$
\boxed{T_{ij}=\lambda\delta_{ij}.}
$$
Conversely $R(\lambda I)R^T=\lambda I$ for every rotation. This proves the <isotropic second-rank tensor> classification without assuming that the original <tensor> was symmetric. Also $\lambda=T_{kk}/3$ is a <scalar>, since the <trace> is rotation-invariant.

= Solution

Let $s=A_iA_i>0$, $q=A_iT_{ij}A_j$ and $\tau=T_{ii}$. Taking the <trace> of the proposed decomposition gives
$$
\tau=3\alpha+\beta s,
$$
since $A_iB_i=0$ and $C_{ii}=0$. Contracting twice with $A$ gives $q=\alpha s+\beta s^2$. Solving these two scalar equations yields
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac12\left(\tau-\frac qs\right),\qquad
\beta=\frac{3q-s\tau}{2s^2}.}
$$
A single contraction gives $T_{ij}A_j=(\alpha+\beta s)A_i+sB_i$, and $\alpha+\beta s=q/s$. Hence
$$
\boxed{B_i=\frac{T_{ij}A_j}{s}-\frac{qA_i}{s^2}.}
$$
Finally set
$$
\boxed{C_{ij}=T_{ij}-\alpha\delta_{ij}-\beta A_iA_j-B_iA_j-B_jA_i.}
$$
This is manifestly symmetric. The formulas give $A_iB_i=q/s-q/s=0$; substituting them into $C_{ij}A_j$ gives zero, and taking the <trace> gives $C_{ii}=0$. Since $C$ is symmetric, $A_iC_{ij}=0$ also holds.

A geometric form makes the <axis decomposition of a symmetric matrix> explicit. With the transverse <orthogonal projection> $P_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-A_iA_j/s$,
$$
B_i=\frac{P_{ij}T_{jk}A_k}{s},\qquad
C_{ij}=P_{ik}T_{kl}P_{lj}-\alpha P_{ij}.
$$
Here $\alpha$ is half the transverse <trace> and $C$ is its <symmetric traceless rank-two tensor> part. Every term was forced by contractions, so the decomposition is unique.

The diagram has a connection between every pair of indices $0\leq i<j\leq n$, including the fixed beam $0$. Two parallel strings, each with <spring constant> $k/2$, together have energy $k(X_i-X_j)^2/2$. Thus the printed $E_{ij}$ is the energy of a connecting pair; calling it the energy of one string would introduce a factor-of-two discrepancy.

Take vertical coordinates positive upwards, with $X_0$ fixed. Each bar has <gravitational potential energy> $m_igX_i$ and <kinetic energy> $m_i\dot X_i^2/2$. The <Lagrangian> is
$$
\boxed{L=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^nm_i\dot X_i^2-\sum_{i=1}^nm_igX_i
-\frac k2\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq n}(X_i-X_j)^2.}
$$
Differentiating gives the <Euler-Lagrange equations>
$$
\boxed{m_i\ddot X_i+m_ig+k\sum_{\substack{0\leq j\leq n\\j\ne i}}(X_i-X_j)=0,\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.}
$$
Equivalently, with $S=\sum_{j=1}^nX_j$, the <spring> term is $k[(n+1)X_i-S-X_0]$. Gravity changes the <equilibrium> but not the <stiffness matrix>. Subtracting a static <equilibrium> and writing $\eta_i=X_i-X_i^{\rm eq}$ gives the <normal mode> system
$$
M\ddot{\boldsymbol\eta}+K\boldsymbol\eta=0,\qquad
M=\operatorname{diag}(m_1,\ldots,m_n),\qquad
\boxed{K=k\left[(n+1)I-\mathbf1\mathbf1^T\right].}
$$
This is the <spring> system underlying <normal modes of an anchored complete spring graph>.

For two bars, after subtracting the gravitational <equilibrium>,
$$
K=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad M=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&m_2\end{pmatrix}.

A <normal mode> $\boldsymbol\eta=\mathbf v e^{i\omega t}$ requires the <generalized eigenvalue problem> $(K-\omega^2M)\mathbf v=0$. With $z=\omega^2$,
$$
0=(2-z)(2-m_2z)-1=m_2z^2-2(m_2+1)z+3.
$$
Thus, for $m_2>0$,
$$
\boxed{\omega_{1,2}=\sqrt{\frac{m_2+1\mp\sqrt{m_2^2-m_2+1}}{m_2}},\qquad\omega_1<\omega_2.}
$$
Both squared <angular frequencies> are positive. A corresponding <eigenvector> is $(1,2-\omega_j^2)^T$, from the first row of the <matrix> equation.

The uniform vector has $\mathbf1\mathbf1^T\mathbf1=3\mathbf1$, whereas every vector with coordinate sum zero is annihilated by $\mathbf1\mathbf1^T$. One orthonormal choice of <normal modes> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\mathbf v_1&=\frac1{\sqrt3}(1,1,1)^T,&\omega_1&=\sqrt{k},\\
\mathbf v_2&=\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1,0)^T,&\omega_2&=2\sqrt{k},\\
\mathbf v_3&=\frac1{\sqrt6}(1,1,-2)^T,&\omega_3&=2\sqrt{k}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
If $k=1$ is retained from (b), the <angular frequencies> are $1,2,2$. The equal upper <frequencies> follow from <degenerate normal modes from permutation symmetry>: the stiffness is scalar on the two-dimensional sum-zero subspace. In the uniform mode the inter-bar strings do not change length, but all three beam connections stretch, giving the nonzero lower <frequency>. The motion is an arbitrary sine-cosine <superposition> of these modes about the gravitational <equilibrium>.

\boxed{R=\langle r\rangle=\{e,r,r^2\}\mathrel{\trianglelefteq}D_3.}

It is invariant under <conjugation> by $r$, and $srs=r^{-1}$ shows invariance under $s$ as well; since $r,s$ generate the <group>, this proves normality. The complete partition into <conjugacy classes> is
$$
\boxed{\{e\},\qquad\{r,r^2\},\qquad\{s,rs,r^2s\}.}
$$
The rotations are mutually conjugate by $s$, and the reflections by $r$; no rotation is conjugate to a reflection because <conjugation> preserves the <order of a group element>.

L_g:H\longrightarrow gH,\qquad h\longmapsto gh

is surjective by definition of a <coset> and injective by left cancellation. Its inverse is multiplication by $g^{-1}$. Therefore

Write $G=\{e,a,b,c\}$. By <Lagrange's theorem>, the <order of a group element> divides four. Since no element has order four, all three nonidentity elements have order two. Thus every element is its own inverse. For any $x,y\in G$,
$$
xy=(xy)^{-1}=y^{-1}x^{-1}=yx,
$$
so the <group> is <abelian>. Also $ab$ cannot equal $e,a$ or $b$, by cancellation and the fact that $a,b$ are distinct nonidentity elements. Hence $ab=c$. The remaining products are forced, giving
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|cccc}
&e&a&b&c\\\hline
e&e&a&b&c\\
a&a&e&c&b\\
b&b&c&e&a\\
c&c&b&a&e
\end{array}}
$$
The map $e\mapsto(0,0)$, $a\mapsto(1,0)$, $b\mapsto(0,1)$, $c\mapsto(1,1)$ preserves this table under componentwise addition modulo two. Therefore
$$
\boxed{G\cong C_2\times C_2=V,}
$$
the <Klein four-group>, also called the Vierergruppe.

A <cyclic group> is generated by one element: $G=\langle g\rangle=\{g^n:n\in\mathbb Z\}$. The hypothesis needs the qualification **no proper nontrivial <subgroup>**: every nontrivial <group> has the proper <trivial subgroup> $\{e\}$, so the literal wording admits no examples.

Under that intended hypothesis, choose $g\ne e$. The <subgroup> $\langle g\rangle$ is nontrivial and must therefore be all of $G$, proving cyclicity. If $g$ had infinite order, then $\langle g^2\rangle$ would be nontrivial and proper, since $g$ is not an even power of an infinite-order generator. Thus $g$ has finite order $N>1$ and $|G|=N$.

If $N$ were composite, choose a divisor $d$ with $1<d<N$. Then $\langle g^d\rangle$ would have order $N/d$, strictly between one and $N$, again contradicting the hypothesis. Consequently
$$
\boxed{G\cong C_p\text{ for a prime }p.}
$$
Conversely, <Lagrange's theorem> shows that a <group> of prime order has only itself and its trivial <subgroup>. This proves the characterization of <groups with no proper nontrivial subgroups>, even without assuming finiteness at the outset.
