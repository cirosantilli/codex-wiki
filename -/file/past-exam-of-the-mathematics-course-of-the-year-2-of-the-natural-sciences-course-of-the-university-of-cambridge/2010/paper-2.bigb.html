= Solution
{parent=i}

Multiplying by $x^2$ makes the first two terms a total <derivative>:
$$
x(x-1)y''+(2x-1)y'=\left[x(x-1)y'\right]'.
$$
The resulting <Sturm-Liouville form> is
$$
\boxed{-(p y')'+l(l+1)y=\lambda w y,\qquad p=x(x-1),\quad w=x^2.}
$$
Equivalently, an integrating multiplier $\mu$ must satisfy $(\mu(1-1/x))'=\mu(2/x-1/x^2)$, which reduces to $\mu'/\mu=2/x$ and gives $\mu=x^2$ up to a constant. The coefficient $p$ is positive for $1<x<R$ and vanishes linearly at the left endpoint; the weight $w$ is positive throughout. The boundedness condition removes the logarithmic local branch, giving the <no-log boundary condition at a linearly vanishing Sturm-Liouville endpoint>.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The appropriate <weighted inner product> and <Sturm-Liouville operator> are
$$
\boxed{\langle u,v\rangle_w=\int_1^R x^2\overline{u(x)}v(x)\,dx,\qquad
Lv=\frac1{x^2}\left[-(x(x-1)v')'+l(l+1)v\right].}
$$
For two functions obeying the endpoint conditions, <integration by parts> gives the <Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator>
$$
\langle u,Lv\rangle_w-\langle Lu,v\rangle_w
=\left[x(x-1)(\overline{u'}v-\overline u v')\right]_1^R=0.
$$
At $R$, both $u$ and $v$ vanish. At $1$, the factor $x(x-1)$ tends to zero while the functions and their first <derivatives> remain bounded. Thus the boundary form vanishes at both ends.

This gives the regular <self-adjoint differential operator> realization: its singular-end condition is $\lim_{x\downarrow1}x(x-1)v'=0$, together with $v(R)=0$. The bounded classical <eigenfunctions> satisfy exactly this no-log choice. More precisely the operator domain is the maximal differential-operator domain in the weighted space with these separated endpoint conditions; the preceding calculation verifies its self-adjoint boundary form, rather than merely the differential expression's formal symmetry.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Let $y\ne0$ be an <eigenfunction>, allowing complex values for the reality argument. The <self-adjoint differential operator> identity and <integration by parts> give
$$
\lambda\int_1^R x^2|y|^2dx=\int_1^R\left[x(x-1)|y'|^2+l(l+1)|y|^2\right]dx.
$$
The boundary term $[x(x-1)\overline y y']_1^R$ vanishes for the same reasons as in part ii. The denominator is real and positive; the numerator is real and strictly positive because $l\geq1$ and $y\ne0$. Hence the <Rayleigh quotient> is
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\frac{\displaystyle\int_1^R[x(x-1)|y'|^2+l(l+1)|y|^2]dx}{\displaystyle\int_1^Rx^2|y|^2dx}\in\mathbb R,\qquad\lambda>0.}
$$
The positivity uses a nondegenerate interval $R>1$.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

For real <eigenfunctions> $y_i,y_j$, multiply their <Sturm-Liouville problems> crosswise and subtract. Integration gives
$$
(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)\int_1^R x^2y_iy_j\,dx
=\left[x(x-1)(y_iy_j'-y_i'y_j)\right]_1^R.
$$
At $R$, both <eigenfunctions> vanish, while at $1$ their bounded values and bounded first <derivatives> multiply a factor tending to zero. The right side is zero. Distinct <eigenvalues> therefore give the <orthogonality of Sturm-Liouville eigenfunctions>
$$
\boxed{\int_1^R x^2y_i(x)y_j(x)\,dx=0\qquad(\lambda_i\ne\lambda_j).}
$$
For complex <eigenfunctions>, replace $y_i$ by $\overline{y_i}$, in accordance with the <weighted inner product>.

Use <separation of variables>, $\Phi=F(r)H(\theta)$, in the <Laplace equation in polar coordinates>. Dividing by $FH/r^2$ gives
$$
\frac{r(rF')'}F=-\frac{H''}H=n^2.
$$
Single-valued $2\pi$-periodic angular factors have <integer> $n\geq0$. For $n\geq1$, they are $\cos n\theta,\sin n\theta$, and the radial equation $r^2F''+rF'-n^2F=0$ has solutions $r^n,r^{-n}$. The zero angular mode gives $F=a_0+b_0\log r$. These separated products give the requested solutions; arbitrary sums may use independent coefficients for each radial-angular combination.

Boundedness at the origin removes the logarithm and all negative radial powers. The boundary function is even, so its sine <Fourier coefficients> vanish. With the convention $f(\theta)=a_0+\sum_{n\geq1}a_n\cos n\theta$, its <Fourier coefficients> are
$$
a_0=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(\theta)d\theta=\frac C2,\qquad
a_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(\theta)\cos n\theta\,d\theta=\frac{2C}{\pi n}\sin\frac{n\pi}{2}.
$$
Thus the bounded interior <harmonic extension> is
$$
\boxed{\Phi_{\rm in}(r,\theta)=\frac C2+\frac{2C}{\pi}\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^m}{2m+1}\left(\frac rR\right)^{2m+1}\cos((2m+1)\theta).}
$$
For $r<R$ the series and its derivatives converge locally uniformly, so it is <harmonic>. It is a rotated and scaled instance of the <harmonic measure of a semicircle in a disk>. For example, summing the <power series> for the complex arctangent, or differentiating the following expression, gives the equivalent real form
$$
\Phi_{\rm in}=\frac C2+\frac C\pi\arctan\left(\frac{2(r/R)\cos\theta}{1-(r/R)^2}\right),\qquad r<R.
$$
This also proves boundedness directly.

**At the two jumps, the boundary interpretation matters.** At every continuity point the radial limit equals the prescribed value. At $\theta=\pm\pi/2$, the radial limit is $C/2$, the midpoint of the two one-sided values. Consequently for $C\ne0$ there is no harmonic solution continuous on the closed disk with exactly the printed boundary values. The standard bounded <Dirichlet problem> imposes the trace away from the jumps, or almost everywhere; assigning zero separately at those two boundary points does not alter the interior solution.

For uniqueness let $w=\widetilde\Phi-\Phi$. Formally the <divergence theorem> applied to $w\nabla w$ gives
$$
0=\int_Dw\Delta w\,dA=\int_{\partial D}w\partial_nw\,ds-\int_D|\nabla w|^2dA.
$$
For continuous zero boundary data, this immediately forces $w=0$. With the actual discontinuous data, one should justify the two exceptional points rather than assume finite <Dirichlet energy> there. Let $|w|\leq M$ and choose a cutoff $\eta_\epsilon$ which is zero within $\epsilon^2$ of those points, one beyond $\epsilon$, and linear in logarithmic distance between. Its gradient satisfies $\int|\nabla\eta_\epsilon|^2=O(1/|\log\epsilon|)$. Applying the <divergence theorem> to $\eta_\epsilon^2w\nabla w$ gives
$$
\int_D\eta_\epsilon^2|\nabla w|^2dA=-2\int_D\eta_\epsilon w\nabla\eta_\epsilon\cdot\nabla w\,dA
\leq\frac12\int_D\eta_\epsilon^2|\nabla w|^2dA+2M^2\int_D|\nabla\eta_\epsilon|^2dA.
$$
The resulting energy tends to zero. Hence $\nabla w=0$ on every compact subset and $w$ is constant; its zero boundary trace on the open arcs makes it zero. This is the <logarithmic cutoff proof of harmonic uniqueness with finitely many boundary exceptions>.

For the <exterior harmonic extension of disk boundary data>, boundedness instead excludes $r^n$ and the logarithm. The same boundary coefficients give
$$
\boxed{\Phi_{\rm out}(r,\theta)=\frac C2+\frac{2C}{\pi}\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^m}{2m+1}\left(\frac Rr\right)^{2m+1}\cos((2m+1)\theta),\qquad r>R.}
$$
This is $\Phi_{\rm in}(R^2/r,\theta)$, using <inversion in a circle>. Its limit at infinity is **$C/2$**. To see why that value is forced independently of this construction, average any bounded exterior <harmonic function> over a circle. The mean satisfies $(r\overline\Phi')'=0$, hence is $A+B\log r$. Boundedness forces $B=0$, and the boundary mean fixes $A=C/2$. A constant limit at infinity must therefore be $C/2$.

Finally suppose two bounded exterior solutions have this limit. Their difference $w$ tends to zero. Under planar <inversion in a circle>, it is bounded and <harmonic> near the origin, so the isolated point there is removable. Its local expansion gives $w=O(r^{-1})$, $\partial_rw=O(r^{-2})$ in the original exterior. Apply the preceding <divergence theorem> energy argument on $R<r<L$, with logarithmic cutoffs at the two boundary jumps. After removing those cutoffs, the inner boundary term vanishes; the outer one is $\int_{r=L}w\partial_rw\,ds=O(L^{-2})$. Letting $L\to\infty$ forces $\nabla w=0$, and the zero limit gives $w=0$. Thus the bounded exterior solution is unique as well.

The <outgoing Green function for the three-dimensional Helmholtz equation> satisfies
$$
(\Delta_y+\omega^2)G_F(x,y)=-\delta^{(3)}(y-x).
$$
Its normalization follows by integrating over a small ball about $x$: $G_F\sim1/(4\pi|y-x|)$, so its outward derivative has flux $-1$. Apply <Green's second identity> in the $y$ variables:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\partial V}(\Psi\nabla_yG_F-G_F\nabla_y\Psi)\cdot n\,dS
&=\int_V(\Psi\Delta_yG_F-G_F\Delta_y\Psi)dV_y\\
&=-\int_V\Psi(y)\delta^{(3)}(y-x)dV_y.
\end{aligned}
$$
The $\omega^2$ terms cancel. Thus the surface integral equals $-\Psi(x)$ for $x\in V$, and zero for $x$ outside the closed volume. Equivalently, remove a small source ball and evaluate its flux before taking its radius to zero. A source on the boundary would require a different limiting formula and is not part of this calculation.

For the upper half-space, take a half-ball of radius $\rho$. Interpret the first far-field condition as $\Psi=O(\rho^{-1})$, and the radiation limit in its usual direction-uniform sense, namely the <Sommerfeld radiation condition>
$$
(\partial_\rho-i\omega)\Psi=o(\rho^{-1}).
$$
This is the finite-bound interpretation of the PDF's first condition. For fixed $x$ and real $\omega$,
$$
G_F=O(\rho^{-1}),\qquad(\partial_\rho-i\omega)G_F=O(\rho^{-2}).
$$
Indeed $|y-x|=\rho-\widehat y\cdot x+O(\rho^{-1})$, and its radial derivative is $1+O(\rho^{-2})$. On the large hemisphere the integrand can therefore be rewritten as
$$
\Psi(\partial_\rho-i\omega)G_F-G_F(\partial_\rho-i\omega)\Psi
=O(\rho^{-3})+o(\rho^{-2}).
$$
Its area is $2\pi\rho^2$, so its integral tends to zero. The planar integral, taken over expanding disks if needed, consequently equals $-\Psi(x)$ for $x_3>0$ and zero for $x_3<0$.

The outward <unit normal vector> on the bottom plane is $-e_3$. Denote this planar integral by
$$
I(x)=\int_{\mathbb R^2}\left[-\Psi(y',0)\partial_{y_3}G_F(x,(y',0))+G_F(x,(y',0))\partial_3\Psi(y',0)\right]d^2y'.
$$
For $x_3>0$ reflect the source point to $x^*=(x_1,x_2,-x_3)$. On the plane,
$$
G_F(x^*,y)=G_F(x,y),\qquad \partial_{y_3}G_F(x^*,y)=-\partial_{y_3}G_F(x,y).
$$
Adding $I(x)=-\Psi(x)$ and $I(x^*)=0$ cancels the boundary values of $\Psi$ and gives $2\int G_F\partial_3\Psi=-\Psi(x)$. Thus the <Neumann representation for a radiating Helmholtz half-space> is
$$
\boxed{\Psi(x)=-\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R^2}\partial_3\Psi(y',0)\frac{e^{i\omega\sqrt{|x'-y'|^2+x_3^2}}}{\sqrt{|x'-y'|^2+x_3^2}}\,d^2y',\qquad x_3>0.}
$$
The negative sign is consistent with using the upward derivative $\partial_3\Psi$ rather than the outward <normal derivative>. If that upward derivative is zero everywhere on the plane, the integral is zero. Hence **$\Psi=0$ throughout the upper half-space** in the specified radiating class; taking a difference also gives uniqueness for prescribed <Neumann boundary data>.

Put $d=\sqrt{a^2-b^2-c^2}>0$ and $h=b-ic$. The denominator of the complex <rational function> becomes
$$
P(z)=\frac h2z^2+az+\frac{b+ic}{2}.
$$
The <quadratic formula> gives its two zeros, hence the two <poles>:
$$
\boxed{z_{\rm in}=\frac{-a+d}{b-ic},\qquad z_{\rm out}=\frac{-a-d}{b-ic}.}
$$
Their product is $(b+ic)/(b-ic)$, so $|z_{\rm in}z_{\rm out}|=1$. To locate them one also needs to exclude the possibility of both lying on the <unit circle>. Here their moduli are explicit:
$$
|z_{\rm in}|=\frac{a-d}{\sqrt{b^2+c^2}}=\sqrt{\frac{a-d}{a+d}}<1,\qquad
|z_{\rm out}|=\sqrt{\frac{a+d}{a-d}}>1.
$$
Both are <simple poles>: $P'(z)=hz+a$ takes the nonzero values $d$ and $-d$ at them. In particular their <residues> are $1/d$ and $-1/d$.

For the real <integral>, use the <change of variables> $z=e^{i\theta}$. As $\theta$ runs from $-\pi$ to $\pi$, this traverses the <unit circle> once counterclockwise, and
$$
d\theta=\frac{dz}{iz},\quad\cos\theta=\frac{z+z^{-1}}2,\quad\sin\theta=\frac{z-z^{-1}}{2i}.
$$
Thus $I=(1/i)\oint_{|z|=1}f(z)dz$. Only $z_{\rm in}$ is enclosed. The <residue theorem>, or <Cauchy integral formula> after subtracting its simple-pole part, gives
$$
\boxed{I=\frac1i\frac{2\pi i}{d}=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{a^2-b^2-c^2}}.}
$$
The real denominator is positive because $a>\sqrt{b^2+c^2}$. As an independent check, write $b\cos\theta+c\sin\theta=\sqrt{b^2+c^2}\cos(\theta-\phi)$; a period shift reduces the answer to the existing <reciprocal affine sine integral>.

Differentiate the spatial <Fourier transform> under the integral, and integrate the second spatial derivative by parts twice. For sufficiently decaying temperature and first derivative, the boundary terms vanish:
$$
\partial_t\widetilde\theta(k,t)=\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-ikx}\partial_x^2\theta\,dx=(ik)^2\widetilde\theta(k,t).
$$
Hence the transformed <heat equation> is the <ordinary differential equation>
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\widetilde\theta=-k^2\widetilde\theta.}
$$

Solving the transformed <ordinary differential equation> with its <initial condition> gives $\widetilde\theta(k,t)=e^{-k^2t}\widetilde\theta(k,0)$. The <Fourier inversion theorem> therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\theta(x,t)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{ikx-k^2t}\widetilde\theta(k,0)\,dk.}
$$
For $t>0$ the Gaussian multiplier suppresses large spatial frequencies, which explains the smoothing produced by <heat conduction>.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The <convolution theorem> turns the product $e^{-k^2t}\widetilde\theta(k,0)$ into a spatial <convolution>. Its kernel is
$$
G(z,t)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-tk^2+ikz}dk,\qquad t>0.
$$
Complete the square:
$$
-tk^2+ikz=-t\left(k-\frac{iz}{2t}\right)^2-\frac{z^2}{4t}.
$$
After rescaling the variable, the supplied translated <Gaussian integral> gives
$$
\boxed{G(z,t)=\frac1{\sqrt{4\pi t}}e^{-z^2/(4t)},\qquad
\theta(x,t)=\frac1{\sqrt{4\pi t}}\int_{\mathbb R}\theta(y,0)e^{-(x-y)^2/(4t)}dy.}
$$
This is the <heat-kernel solution> for diffusivity one. Its kernel integrates to one, preserving the total temperature integral, and approaches a <Dirac delta function> as $t\downarrow0$. Its variance is $2t$; the factor is appropriate to $\theta_t=\theta_{xx}$, rather than the alternative convention with diffusivity $1/2$.

= Solution

For the <Gaussian initial data for the heat equation>, substitute the initial profile into the <heat-kernel solution>. Completing the square in $y$ gives
$$
a_0(y-x_0)^2+\frac{(x-y)^2}{4t}
=\left(a_0+\frac1{4t}\right)(y-\mu)^2+\frac{a_0(x-x_0)^2}{1+4a_0t},\qquad
\mu=\frac{4a_0tx_0+x}{1+4a_0t}.
$$
The remaining <Gaussian integral> yields
$$
\boxed{\theta_G(x,t)=\frac{A_0}{\sqrt{1+4a_0t}}\exp\left[-\frac{a_0(x-x_0)^2}{1+4a_0t}\right].}
$$
The centre stays at $x_0$ and the squared width grows linearly with time.

For the interval pulse, the convolution reduces to $B\int_a^bG(x-y,t)dy$. Using $u=(y-x)/(2\sqrt t)$ and the <error function> gives
$$
\boxed{\theta_I(x,t)=\frac B2\left[\operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{x-a}{2\sqrt t}\right)-\operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{x-b}{2\sqrt t}\right)\right],\qquad t>0.}
$$
This is the translated and scaled <heat equation with interval-indicator initial data>. At its jump points the $t\downarrow0$ limits are $B/2$; away from them the initial data are recovered pointwise.

For fixed $a<x<b$ at late time, both <error function> arguments are small. Since $\operatorname{erf}u=2u/\sqrt\pi+O(u^3)$,
$$
\boxed{\theta_I(x,t)=\frac{B(b-a)}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}+O(t^{-3/2})\quad(t\to\infty).}
$$
The estimate is uniform for $x\in[a,b]$. Equivalently, on this fixed interval the Gaussian in the convolution is $1+O((b-a)^2/t)$.

The spreading distance is more apparent on the growing spatial scale. For the Gaussian pulse the exponent is order one when $|x-x_0|=O(\sqrt t)$. For the interval pulse, set $x=c+\xi\sqrt t$ with $c=(a+b)/2$ and fixed $\xi$. Uniformly for bounded $\xi$, its convolution gives
$$
\theta_I(c+\xi\sqrt t,t)\sim\frac{B(b-a)}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}e^{-\xi^2/4}.
$$
Thus both nonzero pulses spread over a distance **$O(\sqrt t)$**, while their heights are $O(t^{-1/2})$ and their total integrals remain constant. More quantitatively, the variances of their normalized profiles are $1/(2a_0)+2t$ and $(b-a)^2/12+2t$, respectively, confirming the same width law.

In Cartesian coordinates a rank-$r$ <tensor> is an array whose components transform under a change of orthonormal axes by one <rotation matrix> factor per index:
$$
T'_{i_1\cdots i_r}=Q_{i_1j_1}\cdots Q_{i_rj_r}T_{j_1\cdots j_r}.
$$
An <isotropic tensor> has unchanged components under every proper <rotation>. The <Levi-Civita symbol> obeys
$$
Q_{ia}Q_{jb}Q_{kc}\epsilon_{abc}=(\det Q)\epsilon_{ijk}=\epsilon_{ijk}
$$
for $Q\in SO(3)$, proving <rotation invariance of the Levi-Civita symbol>. Under an orthogonal transformation of determinant $-1$ its sign reverses; including such transformations requires the <pseudotensor> distinction. Here isotropy is with respect to proper rotations.

Contract the determinant identity with $\epsilon_{ijk}$. Since $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{ijk}=3!=6$, one obtains
$$
\boxed{\det A=\frac16\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{lmn}A_{il}A_{jm}A_{kn}.}
$$
Every index on the right is contracted, so the <tensor component transformation law> leaves no free rotation factor: the <determinant> is a <scalar>. Equivalently, $A'=QAQ^T$ gives $\det A'=(\det Q)^2\det A=\det A$.

For the <matrix inverse>, define
$$
C_{ab}=\frac12\epsilon_{ajk}\epsilon_{bmn}A_{jm}A_{kn}.
$$
This is the <cofactor matrix>. To verify the inverse formula rather than just recognize it, contract with $A_{ib}$ and use the determinant identity again:
$$
A_{ib}C_{ab}=\frac12\epsilon_{ajk}(\det A)\epsilon_{ijk}=(\det A)\delta_{ia}.
$$
Here the <contraction of two Levi-Civita symbols> gives $\epsilon_{ajk}\epsilon_{ijk}=2\delta_{ai}$. Therefore $AC^T=(\det A)I$ and, when $\det A\ne0$,
$$
\boxed{(A^{-1})_{ba}=\frac{C_{ab}}{\det A}=\frac1{2\det A}\epsilon_{ajk}\epsilon_{bmn}A_{jm}A_{kn}.}
$$
These are the <Levi-Civita formulas for a three-dimensional determinant and inverse>.

Now differentiate the cubic determinant formula with respect to the individual entry $A_{ab}$. The three differentiated factors give equal contributions: exchanging two paired indices changes the sign of both epsilon symbols, leaving their product unchanged. Thus
$$
\frac{\partial\det A}{\partial A_{ab}}=\frac36\epsilon_{ajk}\epsilon_{bmn}A_{jm}A_{kn}=C_{ab}.
$$
Substituting the inverse expression proves the entrywise <derivative of the determinant>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial\det A}{\partial A_{ab}}=(\det A)(A^{-1})_{ba}=(\det A)(A^{-1})^T_{ab}.}
$$
The cofactor version remains valid for singular matrices; only the inverse version requires invertibility.

For the time-dependent <Jacobian matrix>, the stated smooth expansion implies
$$
A_{ij}(t,x)=\delta_{ij}+t\partial_i u_j(x)+O(t^2),\qquad A(0,x)=I.
$$
Applying the <chain rule> and the entrywise determinant derivative gives <Jacobi's formula> near $t=0$:
$$
\frac d{dt}\det A=\sum_{ab}C_{ab}\dot A_{ab}=(\det A)\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}\dot A).
$$
At zero, $C=I$, so $\left.\partial_t\det A\right|_0=\sum_a\partial_a u_a=\nabla\cdot u$. A <Taylor expansion> therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\det A(t,x)=1+t\nabla\cdot u(x)+O(t^2)=1+O(t^2)\quad\text{if }\nabla\cdot u=0.}
$$
The <Jacobian determinant> measures local volume change, so this establishes volume preservation to first order. An arbitrary family specified only through its first-order expansion need not preserve volume exactly at higher orders.

Translate the <generalized coordinates> so that the <equilibrium> is at zero. Its potential gradient vanishes, and its quadratic <potential energy> is $q^T\mathsf Vq/2$, where $\mathsf V$ is the symmetric <Hessian matrix>. The <Euler-Lagrange equations> for small motion are
$$
\mathsf T\ddot q+\mathsf Vq=0.
$$
A <normal mode> has fixed component ratios, $q(t)=v\cos(\omega t+\phi)$, with
$$
\mathsf Vv=\omega^2\mathsf Tv.
$$
The <angular frequency> $\omega$ is its <eigenfrequency>. A zero mode instead admits a constant or linear time factor. For modes $v_\alpha,v_\beta$, symmetry gives
$$
v_\alpha^T\mathsf Vv_\beta=\omega_\beta^2v_\alpha^T\mathsf Tv_\beta
=\omega_\alpha^2v_\alpha^T\mathsf Tv_\beta.
$$
Hence <mass-matrix orthogonality of normal modes> is
$$
\boxed{v_\alpha^T\mathsf Tv_\beta=0\quad\text{if }\omega_\alpha^2\ne\omega_\beta^2.}
$$
Within a degenerate eigenspace, a basis can be chosen orthogonal in this positive <weighted inner product>.

For three masses connected pairwise, the exact energies are
$$
T=\frac m2\sum_{i=1}^3|\dot x_i|^2,\qquad
V=\frac k2\sum_{i<j}(|x_i-x_j|-\sqrt3)^2.
$$
A fixed common <translation> $x_i\mapsto x_i+a$ preserves all differences and velocities. A fixed rigid <rotation> $x_i\mapsto Qx_i$ preserves their <Euclidean norms>. Thus both energies have these symmetries. Here the transformation is fixed in time; a moving reference frame would change the kinetic energy. Every equilateral configuration of side $\sqrt3$ has zero spring extensions, hence zero force and minimum potential, proving that it is an <equilibrium>.

For the specified displacement coordinates, write
$$
\begin{aligned}
d_{23}&=\sqrt{(\sqrt3+q_3-q_5)^2+(q_4-q_6)^2},\\
d_{13}&=\sqrt{(\sqrt3/2+q_1-q_5)^2+(3/2+q_2-q_6)^2},\\
d_{12}&=\sqrt{(-\sqrt3/2+q_1-q_3)^2+(3/2+q_2-q_4)^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The exact potential is $V=(k/2)\sum_{ij=12,13,23}(d_{ij}-\sqrt3)^2$. For an equilibrium edge vector $d$ and small perturbation $\delta d$,
$$
|d+\delta d|-|d|=\frac d{|d|}\cdot\delta d+O(|\delta d|^2).
$$
Thus only each first-order extension is needed for the quadratic potential. These extensions, with an immaterial sign choice for the horizontal edge, are
$$
s_1=q_5-q_3,\qquad
s_2=\frac12(q_1-q_5)+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}(q_2-q_6),\qquad
s_3=-\frac12(q_1-q_3)+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}(q_2-q_4).
$$
Consequently $V_2=(k/2)(s_1^2+s_2^2+s_3^2)$, with remainder $O(|q|^3)$. If $s=Bq$, then $\mathsf V=kB^TB$. Expanding the squares gives
$$
\boxed{\mathsf V=\frac k4\begin{pmatrix}
2&0&-1&\sqrt3&-1&-\sqrt3\\
0&6&\sqrt3&-3&-\sqrt3&-3\\
-1&\sqrt3&5&-\sqrt3&-4&0\\
\sqrt3&-3&-\sqrt3&3&0&0\\
-1&-\sqrt3&-4&0&5&\sqrt3\\
-\sqrt3&-3&0&0&\sqrt3&3
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The <small-oscillation mass and stiffness matrices> are $\mathsf T=mI_6$ and the displayed $\mathsf V$. Hence
$$
\boxed{L_2=\frac m2\sum_{j=1}^6\dot q_j^2-\frac12q^T\mathsf Vq,\qquad \mathsf Vv=m\omega^2v.}
$$

The two common translations give zero-mode vectors
$$
v_x=(1,0,1,0,1,0)^T,\qquad v_y=(0,1,0,1,0,1)^T.
$$
An infinitesimal counterclockwise rotation sends each equilibrium vertex $r_i$ to $Jr_i$, where $J(x,y)=(-y,x)$. Therefore a third zero-mode vector is
$$
\boxed{v_{\rm rot}=(-1,0,\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\tfrac12,-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})^T,\qquad\omega_x=\omega_y=\omega_{\rm rot}=0.}
$$
Each vector makes all three $s_j$ vanish, so lies in the <kernel of a linear map> $B$ and therefore in the kernel of $\mathsf V$. The rows of $B$ are independent: the $q_4$ and $q_6$ entries first force the coefficients of its last two rows to vanish, then the first coefficient vanishes. Thus $\operatorname{rank}B=3$, and the <rank-nullity theorem> proves that these are exactly the three zero modes.

For radial motion take
$$
v_b=(0,1,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},-\tfrac12,-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},-\tfrac12)^T,
$$
so that $x_i(t)=(1+a(t))r_i$ to first order. The force on mass $i$ is $-ka\sum_{j\ne i}(r_i-r_j)=-3ka r_i$, since $\sum_i r_i=0$. Newton's equation therefore gives $m\ddot a=-3ka$. Equivalently $\mathsf Vv_b=3kv_b$. Hence the breathing <normal mode> has
$$
\boxed{\omega_b=\sqrt{\frac{3k}{m}}.}
$$
All three masses move radially outward together, then inward together, preserving the triangular shape. The remaining two <normal modes of an equilateral spring triangle> are shear deformations with squared frequency $3k/(2m)$; together with the breathing mode and three rigid zero modes they account for all six degrees of freedom.

The <order of a finite group> $G$ is its number of elements. <Lagrange's theorem> states that for a <subgroup> $K\leq G$,
$$
|G|=[G:K]|K|,
$$
so $|K|$ divides $|G|$. In particular the <order of a group element> divides $|G|$, because it is the order of its generated <cyclic subgroup>.

For $|G|=4$, if some element has order four it generates the whole group, which is therefore the <cyclic group> $C_4$. Otherwise every nonidentity element has order two. Choose two distinct ones $a,b$ and put $c=ab$. Cancellation shows $c\notin\{e,a,b\}$, so $c$ is the remaining element. Since $c^{-1}=c$,
$$
ab=c=c^{-1}=(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}=ba.
$$
The other products follow: $ac=ca=b$, $bc=cb=a$, and $a^2=b^2=c^2=e$. Thus the second possibility is the <Klein four-group>. The two groups are not <isomorphic> because only $C_4$ has an element of order four. This proves the order-four case of the <classification of groups of order p squared>.

A <group homomorphism> $\Phi:G\to H$ obeys $\Phi(gh)=\Phi(g)\Phi(h)$. It maps identity to identity and inverses to inverses. Its <group kernel> $K=\{g:\Phi(g)=e_H\}$ contains $e_G$ and is closed under $ab^{-1}$, so the <subgroup test> makes it a subgroup. For $k\in K$ and $g\in G$,
$$
\Phi(gkg^{-1})=\Phi(g)e_H\Phi(g)^{-1}=e_H.
$$
Thus $gKg^{-1}\subseteq K$; applying the same argument to $g^{-1}$ gives equality. Hence $K$ is a <normal subgroup>.

Define $\overline\Phi:G/K\to H$ by $\overline\Phi(gK)=\Phi(g)$. If $gK=hK$, then $h^{-1}g\in K$, giving $\Phi(g)=\Phi(h)$, so this is well-defined. Coset multiplication shows it is a <group homomorphism>. Its kernel is just the identity coset, since $\Phi(g)=e_H$ means $g\in K$. It is therefore injective, and the assumed surjectivity of $\Phi$ makes it surjective as well. This proves the <first isomorphism theorem> here:
$$
\boxed{G/K\cong H.}
$$

In the <quaternion group>, the element $-1=i^2=j^2=k^2$ commutes with each of $i,j,k$, because an element commutes with its square. It then commutes with their negatives too, and with $\pm1$. The set $N=\{1,-1\}$ is a subgroup because $(-1)^2=1$, and it is central, hence a <normal subgroup>.

The four quotient <cosets> are $N,iN,jN,kN$. They square to $N$, and $(iN)(jN)=kN$. Reversed products have the same coset: for example $(ij)^{-1}=k^{-1}=-k$, while $j^{-1}i^{-1}=(-j)(-i)=ji$, so $ji=-k$. The same calculation gives $kj=-i$ and $ik=-j$. After signs are identified, these are precisely the products in the <Klein four-group>. Define
$$
\boxed{\Phi(\pm1)=e,\qquad\Phi(\pm i)=a,\qquad\Phi(\pm j)=b,\qquad\Phi(\pm k)=c.}
$$
This preserves all products, is surjective, and has kernel exactly $N$. The <first isomorphism theorem> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{Q_8/\{\pm1\}\cong V.}
$$
This is the <quaternion group modulo its centre>: the reverse-product relations also show that none of $\pm i,\pm j,\pm k$ is central, so $Z(Q_8)=N$.

Two elements are <conjugate group elements> if $h=xgx^{-1}$ for some $x\in G$. The <conjugacy class> of $g$ is $\{xgx^{-1}:x\in G\}$. Conjugacy is an <equivalence relation>: it is reflexive using $x=e$, symmetric by replacing $x$ with $x^{-1}$, and transitive because
$$
k=yhy^{-1},\quad h=xgx^{-1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad k=(yx)g(yx)^{-1}.
$$
Thus the classes partition the group: every element belongs to its own class, and any two classes that intersect are equal. This proves that every element belongs to exactly one class.

If $G$ is <abelian>, then $xgx^{-1}=g$, so every class is a singleton. In general the elements whose classes are singletons are precisely those commuting with every group element. Thus the <center of a group> is
$$
Z(G)=\{z\in G:zg=gz\text{ for every }g\in G\}.
$$
It contains $e$. If $a,b\in Z(G)$, then $ab^{-1}$ commutes with every $g$, since $b^{-1}$ does and $a$ does. The <subgroup test> therefore proves that $Z(G)$ is a subgroup. Any two of its elements commute with each other, so it is an <abelian subgroup>.

For the square centred at the origin with vertices $(\pm1,\pm1)$, put
$$
R=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad S=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The <dihedral group> $D_4$ consists of $R^j$ and $R^jS$, $j=0,1,2,3$. Its eight actions and their matrices are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{element}&\text{geometrical action}&\text{matrix}\\\hline
I&\text{identity}&\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\\
R&90^\circ\text{ counterclockwise rotation}&\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\\
R^2&180^\circ\text{ rotation}&\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\\
R^3&270^\circ\text{ counterclockwise rotation}&\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}\\
S&\text{reflection in }y=0&\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\\
RS&\text{reflection in }y=x&\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\\
R^2S&\text{reflection in }x=0&\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\\
R^3S&\text{reflection in }y=-x&\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}
\end{array}.
$$
These exhaust the symmetries: the image of one vertex has four choices and the image of an adjacent vertex has two choices, determining the entire square. For an explicit pair of nonconjugate elements take $R^2$ and $S$. Both have order two, but their <determinants> are $+1$ and $-1$. A <similarity transformation> preserves the determinant, so **$R^2$ and $S$ are not conjugate**.

To find the centre, use $SR=R^{-1}S$. A rotation $R^j$ commutes with $S$ exactly when $R^j=R^{-j}$, so $j$ is even. No reflection $R^jS$ commutes with $R$, since $(R^jS)R=R^{j-1}S$ while $R(R^jS)=R^{j+1}S$, and $R^2\ne I$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{Z(D_4)=\{I,R^2\}.}
$$

A <group representation> on a vector space $W$ is a <group homomorphism> $\rho:G\to GL(W)$: $\rho(gh)=\rho(g)\rho(h)$ and $\rho(e)=I$. An <invariant subspace> $U$ obeys $\rho(g)U\subseteq U$ for every $g$. A nonzero representation is <irreducible> if its only invariant subspaces are $0$ and $W$. It is a <faithful representation> if it is injective, equivalently if its <group kernel> is $\{e\}$. Its <character> is $\chi_\rho(g)=\operatorname{tr}\rho(g)$. For inequivalent complex <irreducible representations> of a finite group, <character orthogonality> is
$$
\boxed{\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\overline{\chi_\alpha(g)}\chi_\beta(g)=\delta_{\alpha\beta}.}
$$

Use right-to-left composition of <permutations> and assign the matrix labels consistently by
$$
I=e,\quad A=(123),\quad B=(132),\quad C=(13),\quad D=(12),\quad E=(23).
$$
The resulting <Cayley table>, with row multiplying column, is
$$
\begin{array}{c|cccccc}
 &I&A&B&C&D&E\\\hline
I&I&A&B&C&D&E\\
A&A&B&I&E&C&D\\
B&B&I&A&D&E&C\\
C&C&D&E&I&A&B\\
D&D&E&C&B&I&A\\
E&E&C&D&A&B&I
\end{array}.
$$
Conjugation relabels the entries of a cycle, preserving its length. Thus the <conjugacy classes> are
$$
\boxed{\{I\},\qquad\{A,B\},\qquad\{C,D,E\}.}
$$

Define the <permutation representation> by $P_\sigma e_j=e_{\sigma(j)}$, or $(P_\sigma)_{ij}=\delta_{i,\sigma(j)}$. Applying it to a basis vector proves $P_\sigma P_\tau=P_{\sigma\tau}$. The six assignments above produce exactly the listed matrices. They are distinct, and $P_\sigma=I$ forces $\sigma(j)=j$ for every $j$, so the representation is faithful.

Every permutation fixes $(1,1,1)$, hence fixes every vector $(c,c,c)$. Therefore $V_0$ is a one-dimensional <invariant subspace> carrying the <trivial representation>. Its <orthogonal complement> is
$$
V_0^\perp=\{(v_1,v_2,v_3):v_1+v_2+v_3=0\},
$$
since the inner product with $(c,c,c)$ is $c(v_1+v_2+v_3)$. Permuting coordinates preserves their sum, so this plane is invariant too. Every real vector has the unique decomposition
$$
v=\frac{v_1+v_2+v_3}{3}(1,1,1)+\left[v-\frac{v_1+v_2+v_3}{3}(1,1,1)\right].
$$
Thus the <permutation representation> is the <direct sum> of <group representations> on the line and the plane.

To find the <eigenvectors> of $A$, note that $A(v_1,v_2,v_3)=(v_3,v_1,v_2)$. With $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/3}$, its three <eigenvalues> and eigenspaces are
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
1&\operatorname{span}(1,1,1)\\
\zeta&\operatorname{span}(1,\zeta^2,\zeta)\\
\zeta^2&\operatorname{span}(1,\zeta,\zeta^2)
\end{array}.}
$$
The only real eigenvalue is one, whose eigenline is $V_0$, so $A$ has no nonzero real <eigenvector> in $V_0^\perp$. A proper nonzero real invariant subspace of that two-dimensional plane would be a line, giving such an eigenvector. Hence the plane representation is irreducible over $\mathbb R$.

For the later complex dimension-count theorem one should also establish irreducibility over $\mathbb C$; real irreducibility alone would not imply it in general. In the complexified plane, the only candidate $A$-invariant lines are its $\zeta$ and $\zeta^2$ eigenlines. But $C$ exchanges them:
$$
C(1,\zeta^2,\zeta)=\zeta(1,\zeta,\zeta^2),\qquad
C(1,\zeta,\zeta^2)=\zeta^2(1,\zeta^2,\zeta).
$$
Neither is invariant under the whole group, proving complex irreducibility as well. The required decomposition is therefore
$$
\boxed{P\cong\mathbf1\oplus W,\qquad \dim W=2,}
$$
where $W$ is the <standard representation of the symmetric group> for three letters. Its character is obtained by subtracting one from the number of fixed coordinates: on the three classes it is $(2,-1,0)$.

Finally, the <sign representation> gives a second one-dimensional irreducible, with value $+1$ on $I,A,B$ and $-1$ on the transpositions. These are exactly the two one-dimensional possibilities: if $r=A,s=C$, a one-dimensional representation sends them to commuting nonzero scalars $v,u$. The relations $r^3=s^2=e$ and $srs=r^{-1}$ imply $v^3=u^2=1$ and $v=v^{-1}$. Hence $v=1$ and $u=\pm1$, the trivial and sign choices. Together with the two-dimensional $W$, these give
$$
1^2+1^2+2^2=6=|\Sigma_3|.
$$
The <sum of squares of irreducible representation dimensions> leaves room for no other irreducible. Thus there are **exactly two one-dimensional and one two-dimensional complex irreducible representations**.
