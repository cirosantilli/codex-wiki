= Solution
{parent=a}

In <Cartesian coordinates>, use the <Levi-Civita symbol> and the <Einstein summation convention> to expand
$$
\begin{aligned}
[\mathbf u\times(\nabla\times\mathbf u)]_i
&=\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{klm}u_j\partial_lu_m\\
&=u_j\partial_i u_j-u_j\partial_j u_i\\
&=\frac12\partial_i(u_ju_j)-[(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u]_i.
\end{aligned}
$$
The contraction of the two symbols uses the <Kronecker delta>, and the final step is the <product rule>. Thus the <convective acceleration> identity is
$$
\boxed{(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=\frac12\nabla|\mathbf u|^2-\mathbf u\times(\nabla\times\mathbf u).}
$$
Put $\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf u$. The <curl of a gradient> vanishes, and the <curl of a cross product> identity gives
$$
\nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega)=\mathbf u\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\omega-\boldsymbol\omega\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf u+(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u-(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol\omega.
$$
Since the <divergence of a curl is zero>, taking the <curl> of the first identity yields
$$
\boxed{\nabla\times[(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u]=(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)\boldsymbol\omega+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol\omega-(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u.}
$$
Twice continuous differentiability suffices for the mixed-derivative identities used here.

Away from the axis, <cylindrical coordinates> put the field in the <line vortex> form
$$
\mathbf v=\frac{\kappa}{2\pi\rho}\mathbf e_\phi.
$$
The only potentially nonzero component of its <curl> is
$$
(\nabla\times\mathbf v)_z=\frac1\rho\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}(\rho v_\phi)=\frac1\rho\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\frac\kappa{2\pi}=0.
$$
The other components vanish because there is no axial dependence and no radial or axial velocity. Thus **the <curl> is zero for $\rho>0$**; the field is singular on the axis, so a classical <curl> there is not defined.

For a spanning surface avoiding that axis, the smoothness hypotheses of <Stokes theorem> hold and give $\oint_C\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf r=0$. If the enclosed planar region contains the axis, the singularity prevents applying that theorem directly. Excise a small disk about the origin. The resulting annulus has zero <curl>, so its outer <circulation> equals the counterclockwise circulation on the small circle:
$$
\boxed{\oint_C\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf r=\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\kappa}{2\pi\rho}\rho\,d\phi=\kappa.}
$$
This sign assumes positive, counterclockwise orientation. Clockwise orientation gives $-\kappa$; more generally the value is $\kappa$ times the <winding number> about the origin. A curve through the origin is excluded because its integral is singular. In the <distribution> interpretation the missing <vorticity> is concentrated on the axis as $\kappa\delta(x)\delta(y)\mathbf e_z$, explaining the nonzero circulation despite zero classical <curl> away from it.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Since the endpoints represent the same point of the tube, both density and diffusive flux must match. For a positive <diffusion> coefficient $\lambda$, the <periodic boundary conditions> are
$$
\boxed{u(-\pi,t)=u(\pi,t),\qquad u_x(-\pi,t)=u_x(\pi,t).}
$$
For <separation of variables>, periodic spatial modes solve $X''+n^2X=0$ and have time factors $e^{-\lambda n^2t}$. The constant mode has no decay. The <periodic diffusion on a circle> solution is therefore
$$
u(x,t)=\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty[a_n\cos(nx)+b_n\sin(nx)]e^{-\lambda n^2t},
$$
where the <Fourier coefficients> are
$$
a_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)\cos(nx)dx,\qquad b_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)\sin(nx)dx.
$$
The expression for $a_n$ includes $n=0$.

The specified profile is even, so $b_n=0$. Its mean follows from $\int_0^\pi(\pi-x)^2dx=\pi^3/3$, giving $a_0=2\pi^2/3$. For $n\geq1$, two <integrations by parts> give
$$
\int_0^\pi(\pi-x)^2\cos(nx)dx=\frac{2\pi}{n^2},\qquad a_n=\frac4{n^2}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=\frac{\pi^2}{3}+4\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{\cos(nx)}{n^2}e^{-\lambda n^2t}.}
$$
All nonconstant <normal modes> decay for $\lambda>0$, so the density becomes uniform:
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)\longrightarrow\frac{\pi^2}{3}\quad(t\to\infty).}
$$
The conserved total amount is $\int_{-\pi}^\pi u\,dx=2\pi^3/3$. The initial cusp at zero is compatible with an <L2 space> solution and is immediately smoothed by <diffusion>. At $\lambda=0$ there would be no relaxation, so positivity is needed for this long-time conclusion.

Expand the <radial modified Helmholtz equation> as $Ly=-y''-2y'/x+y$. Setting $y=z/x$ gives $Ly=(-z''+z)/x$, so
$$
y(x)=\frac{Ae^x+Be^{-x}}x.
$$
Boundedness at zero requires $A+B=0$, whereas boundedness at infinity requires $A=0$. The respective adapted <homogeneous solutions> are therefore
$$
\boxed{u(x)=\frac{\sinh x}{x}\quad\text{at zero},\qquad v(x)=\frac{e^{-x}}x\quad\text{at infinity}.}
$$
The first has limit one at zero. Their <Wronskian> is $uv'-u'v=-x^{-2}$. Since the highest-derivative coefficient of $L$ is $-1$, the <Green function> must have first-derivative jump $-1$. The <radial modified Helmholtz Green function on the half-line> is
$$
\boxed{G(x,\xi)=\frac\xi x\sinh(\min(x,\xi))e^{-\max(x,\xi)},\qquad x,\xi>0.}
$$
It is continuous at the source, and its <derivative> jump is $-1$, giving $L_xG=\delta(x-\xi)$. For fixed source it tends to $\xi e^{-\xi}$ as $x\downarrow0$ and decays at infinity. This kernel is for the original operator and ordinary measure $d\xi$; multiplying the equation by $x^2$ would change the source normalization.

For the compact source, split the <integral> at $\xi=x$ when $x<R$. The needed antiderivatives are
$$
\int\xi\sinh\xi\,d\xi=\xi\cosh\xi-\sinh\xi,\qquad\int\xi e^{-\xi}d\xi=-(\xi+1)e^{-\xi}.
$$
Thus
$$
y(x)=\frac{e^{-x}}x\int_0^x\xi\sinh\xi\,d\xi+\frac{\sinh x}{x}\int_x^R\xi e^{-\xi}d\xi\quad(x<R),
$$
while for $x>R$ the result is $e^{-x}x^{-1}\int_0^R\xi\sinh\xi\,d\xi$. Simplifying gives
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle1-(R+1)e^{-R}\frac{\sinh x}{x},&0\leq x\leq R,\\\displaystyle(R\cosh R-\sinh R)\frac{e^{-x}}x,&x\geq R.\end{cases}}
$$
The continuous value at zero is $1-(R+1)e^{-R}$, and $y'(0)=0$. Both $y$ and $y'$ match at $R$, so no extra <Dirac delta> appears there. The only <homogeneous solution> bounded at both ends is zero, establishing uniqueness.

Split the <Fourier transform> integral at zero:
$$
\boxed{\widehat g(k)=\int_0^\infty e^{-(\lambda+ik)x}dx+\int_0^\infty e^{-(\lambda-ik)x}dx=\frac{2\lambda}{\lambda^2+k^2}.}
$$
By <Fourier inversion> applied to this pair, or by the <residue theorem>, the other transform is
$$
\boxed{\widehat h(k)=\frac\pi\mu e^{-\mu|k|}.}
$$
For example, inversion with $\lambda=\mu$ gives $\int e^{ikx}(k^2+\mu^2)^{-1}dk=(\pi/\mu)e^{-\mu|x|}$; evenness supplies the desired sign convention.

Take the spatial <Fourier transform> of the <Laplace equation>. For $y>0$ it gives $\widehat\psi_{yy}-k^2\widehat\psi=0$. Removing the exponentially growing solution and matching the boundary gives
$$
\widehat\psi(k,y)=\widehat f(k)e^{-|k|y}.
$$
At $k=0$ this formula is interpreted by continuity; a single transform value does not alter the inverse integral. The previous transform pair identifies
$$
\mathcal F^{-1}[e^{-|k|y}](x)=\frac1\pi\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}=P_y(x),
$$
the <Poisson kernel for the upper half-plane>. The <convolution theorem> therefore gives the <Poisson integral>
$$
\boxed{\psi(x,y)=\frac y\pi\int_{\mathbb R}\frac{f(u)}{(x-u)^2+y^2}du.}
$$
This is the bounded harmonic solution for bounded decaying data, or the corresponding solution in an appropriate Fourier class. Such a growth condition matters if vertical decay is interpreted only pointwise: the nonzero <harmonic function> $e^{x^2-y^2}\sin(2xy)=\operatorname{Im}e^{(x+iy)^2}$ vanishes on the real axis and tends to zero for fixed $x$ as $y\to\infty$, but grows rapidly in $x$ and has no ordinary spatial <Fourier transform>. It can be excluded by requiring boundedness; the stated pointwise limits alone would not establish uniqueness.

For the interval data, direct integration gives the <Poisson extension of interval boundary data>
$$
\boxed{\psi(x,y)=\frac c\pi\left[\arctan\frac{a-x}{y}+\arctan\frac{a+x}{y}\right],\qquad y>0.}
$$
As $y\downarrow0$ it approaches $c$ for $|x|<a$, zero for $|x|>a$, and $c/2$ at $x=\pm a$. At those jump points the prescribed isolated values cannot be attained by a continuous extension; they do not affect the integral. The solution decays vertically, with $\psi\sim2ac/(\pi y)$ for fixed $x$ and large $y$.

= Solution
{parent=5}

An <eigenvalue> $\lambda$ and associated <eigenvector> $e\ne0$ satisfy $Ae=\lambda e$. The <characteristic polynomial> $\det(A-\lambda I)$ has degree equal to the positive <matrix> size. The <fundamental theorem of algebra> gives a complex root, at which $A-\lambda I$ is singular and therefore has a nontrivial kernel. Hence every nonempty square complex <matrix> has an <eigenvector>.

A nondiagonalizable example is the <Jordan block> $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$: its only <eigenvalue> is zero, and its eigenspace is one-dimensional. A <Hermitian matrix> satisfies $A^\dagger=A$ and has an <orthonormal eigenbasis> by the <spectral theorem>. One proof finds an <eigenvector>, notes that its <orthogonal complement> is invariant, and repeats. Its <eigenvalues> are real because
$$
\lambda\,e^\dagger e=e^\dagger Ae=\overline{e^\dagger Ae}=\overline\lambda\,e^\dagger e,
$$
where $e^\dagger e>0$.

With all <eigenvalues> negative, $A$ is invertible. Its same complete eigenbasis gives
$$
\boxed{A^{-1}e_i=\lambda_i^{-1}e_i,\qquad A^me_i=\lambda_i^me_i\quad(m=1,2,\ldots).}
$$
Some powered <eigenvalues> can coincide, but the original <eigenvectors> remain a complete basis. Writing $A=U\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_i)U^\dagger$, the convergent <matrix exponential> series gives
$$
\int_0^\infty e^{tA}dt=U\operatorname{diag}\left(\int_0^\infty e^{t\lambda_i}dt\right)U^\dagger=U\operatorname{diag}(-\lambda_i^{-1})U^\dagger.
$$
Thus the <matrix inverse from a decaying matrix exponential> is
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty e^{tA}dt=-A^{-1},\qquad A^{-1}=-\int_0^\infty e^{tA}dt.}
$$
**The identity printed in the original PDF is missing a minus sign.** Already for the one-by-one <matrix> $A=(-1)$ its integral is $1$, whereas its inverse is $-1$. Equivalently, integrating $d(e^{tA})/dt=Ae^{tA}$ gives $A\int_0^\infty e^{tA}dt=-I$, confirming the correction without diagonalization.

Complete the square in the <quadratic form>:
$$
Q_1=(x_1+2x_2)^2+x_2^2+6x_3^2.
$$
The real invertible <linear transformation>
$$
\boxed{x=Ly,\qquad L=\begin{pmatrix}1&-2&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1/\sqrt6\end{pmatrix}}
$$
sets $x_1+2x_2=y_1$, $x_2=y_2$, $\sqrt6x_3=y_3$, giving $Q_1=y_1^2+y_2^2+y_3^2$. The same transformation gives
$$
\boxed{Q_2=y_1^2+y_2^2-y_3^2.}
$$
Its signature is $(2,1)$. By <Sylvester's law of inertia> no real invertible change of variables can turn this second form into a sum of three positive squares.

= Solution

The <trace> of a square <matrix> is $\operatorname{tr}A=\sum_iA_{ii}$. Expanding a product and interchanging its finite sums gives
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ji}=\sum_{j,i}B_{ji}A_{ij}=\operatorname{tr}(BA).}
$$
Hence every <commutator> has trace zero. Taking the trace of $AB-BA=cI$ gives $0=cn$. Over the complex numbers, with $n\geq1$, this forces
$$
\boxed{c=0.}
$$
For that value, choose $A=B=0$, or any commuting pair. For every nonzero $c$ the trace contradiction proves nonexistence. The finite <matrix> size is essential to this trace argument.

= Solution

Differentiate the <eigenvalue equation> along the assumed smooth normalized eigenbranch:
$$
A'x+Ax'=\lambda'x+\lambda x'.
$$
Since $A$ is real symmetric, $x^TA=\lambda x^T$. Multiplying the differentiated equation by $x^T$ and using $x^Tx=1$ cancels the terms containing $x'$:
$$
x^TA'x+\lambda x^Tx'=\lambda'+\lambda x^Tx'.
$$
Thus the finite-dimensional <Hellmann–Feynman theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\left.\frac{d\lambda}{d\epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon=0}=x(0)^T\left.\frac{dA}{d\epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon=0}x(0).}
$$
Equivalently, differentiating the normalization gives $2x^Tx'=0$ and eliminates those terms from the <derivative> of $\lambda=x^TAx$. The proof needs the smooth eigenbranch assumed in the question; the smallest <eigenvalue> need not be differentiable at an arbitrary crossing without that assumption.

For a <harmonic function>, put $a=u_{xx}$ and $b=u_{xy}=u_{yx}$. Since $u_{yy}=-u_{xx}$, its <Hessian matrix> is
$$
D^2u=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&-a\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det D^2u=-a^2-b^2.
$$
A nonzero determinant is therefore negative, and the two real Hessian <eigenvalues> are $\pm\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$. At a <stationary point> the quadratic term of the <Taylor expansion> has both signs. Consequently **every such nondegenerate <stationary point> is a <saddle point>**. This is the <nondegenerate stationary points of a planar harmonic function> criterion. The same argument applies to $v$, whose <stationary points> coincide with those of $u$. A zero Hessian determinant is outside this second-order classification.

Assume the image of the domain of $f$ lies in the domain where $g$ is <holomorphic>. The complex <chain rule> gives
$$
\boxed{(g\circ f)'(z)=g'(f(z))f'(z),}
$$
so the composition is holomorphic, including at points where $f'=0$. Its real part $S(x,y)=s(u(x,y),v(x,y))$ is therefore a <harmonic function>.

A direct verification uses the <composition of harmonic conjugate pairs>. The multivariable <chain rule> gives
$$
\Delta S=s_{uu}|\nabla u|^2+2s_{uv}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+s_{vv}|\nabla v|^2+s_u\Delta u+s_v\Delta v.
$$
The <Cauchy-Riemann equations> imply $|\nabla u|^2=|\nabla v|^2$; parts (i) and (ii) remove the last three terms. Since $s$ is harmonic in its own variables,
$$
\boxed{\Delta S=|\nabla u|^2(s_{uu}+s_{vv})=0.}
$$

= Solution

In monic form the <Chebyshev differential equation> has $P(x)=-x/(1-x^2)$ and $Q(x)=p^2/(1-x^2)$. Both are analytic near zero, so it is an <ordinary point>. At $x=1$, $(x-1)P=x/(x+1)$ and $(x-1)^2Q=-p^2(x-1)/(x+1)$ are analytic; at $x=-1$, $(x+1)P=x/(x-1)$ and $(x+1)^2Q=-p^2(x+1)/(x-1)$ are analytic. Thus both endpoints are <regular singular points>.

Insert $y_q=\sum_{n\geq0}a_nx^{n+q}$ and equate coefficients. The <recurrence relation> is
$$
\boxed{a_{n+2}=\frac{(n+q)^2-p^2}{(n+q+2)(n+q+1)}a_n,\qquad q=0,1.}
$$
Explicitly the factors are $(n^2-p^2)/[(n+2)(n+1)]$ for $q=0$ and $[(n+1)^2-p^2]/[(n+3)(n+2)]$ for $q=1$. The lowest equation for $q=1$ forces $a_1=0$. For $q=0$, one may choose $a_1=0$ to select an even solution, independent of the odd $q=1$ solution. The leading coefficient $a_0$ is free in either chosen parity branch.

For a nonterminating branch the consecutive parity-term ratio tends to $x^2$. Hence the <ratio test> gives convergence for $|x|<1$, and <radius of convergence> one. Terminating branches are <polynomials> with infinite radius. For $p=m\geq0$ an integer, choose $q$ of the same parity as $m$: the recurrence stops once $n+q=m$, producing a degree-$m$ <Chebyshev polynomial>. Normalization at $1$ gives all nonzero coefficients through degree three:
$$
\boxed{T_0=1,\qquad T_1=x,\qquad T_2=2x^2-1,\qquad T_3=4x^3-3x.}
$$

Set $Y(\theta)=y(\cos\theta)$. Direct <differentiation> gives $Y''=\sin^2\theta\,y''-\cos\theta\,y'$, so the equation becomes $Y''+m^2Y=0$. For $m>0$, $Y=A\cos(m\theta)+B\sin(m\theta)$. A <polynomial> $y$ has finite <derivative> at $x=1$, and therefore $Y'(0)=-\sin0\,y'(1)=0$, forcing $B=0$. Also $Y(0)=T_m(1)=1$, so $A=1$. For $m=0$, the transformed solution is affine in $\theta$ and the same <derivative> condition removes its linear term. Thus
$$
\boxed{T_m(x)=\cos(m\arccos x),\qquad -1\leq x\leq1,\quad m=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
The endpoint values follow by continuity. The finite-derivative condition is what excludes the independent nonpolynomial branch, rather than normalization alone.

= Solution

For fixed endpoint values, the <Euler-Lagrange equation> is $f_y-df_{y'}/dx=0$. Along a stationary curve, the <chain rule> gives
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(y'f_{y'}-f)=y'\left(\frac{df_{y'}}{dx}-f_y\right)-f_x.
$$
When $f_x=0$, the <Beltrami identity> therefore takes the requested sign convention
$$
\boxed{y'f_{y'}-f=A.}
$$

By <Fermat principle>, the elapsed-time integrand for the given speed is $f=c_0^{-1}\sqrt{1-ky}\sqrt{1+(y')^2}$. Thus
$$
y'f_{y'}-f=-\frac{\sqrt{1-ky}}{c_0\sqrt{1+(y')^2}}=A.
$$
The constant is negative for a finite regular ray. Squaring gives $(1-ky)/(1+(y')^2)=c_0^2A^2$. At a maximum height $y_0$, the slope is zero, so $c_0^2A^2=1-ky_0>0$. Hence
$$
(y')^2=\frac{k(y_0-y)}{1-ky_0}.
$$
Separate variables on either side of the turning point. Integration gives the <parabolic light rays in a reciprocal-square-root speed profile>
$$
\boxed{y(x)=y_0-\frac{k(x-x_c)^2}{4(1-ky_0)}.}
$$
The quadratic opens downwards, with its greatest height $y_0$ at $x=x_c$. The positive index condition holds throughout the layer because $kh<1$. Vertical rays are a separate zero-horizontal-momentum case, outside the regular graph representation $y(x)$; they are not returning rays of the type considered below.

Equal endpoint heights at $x=\pm x_0$ force $x_c=0$. Setting $y(\pm x_0)=0$ gives
$$
\boxed{(kx_0)^2=4ky_0(1-ky_0).}
$$
For nonzero separation, a real returning ray requires $kx_0\leq1$ and has candidate turning heights
$$
\boxed{y_0=\frac{1\pm\sqrt{1-k^2x_0^2}}{2k}.}
$$
Only choices satisfying $0<y_0<h$ are rays wholly within the specified layer. Depending on its thickness there may be two, one, or no admissible choices; at zero discriminant the two algebraic candidates coincide. The entry and exit points at $y=0$ are interpreted as limiting boundary points of the open medium.

= Solution
{parent=10}

Use the real <weighted inner product> $\langle u,v\rangle_w=\int_a^bwuv\,dx$ and the energy bilinear form $B(u,v)=\int_a^b(pu'v'+quv)dx$. <Integration by parts>, the endpoint conditions, and the <Sturm-Liouville problem> give
$$
B(y_i,y_j)=\int_a^by_i[-(py_j')'+qy_j]dx=\lambda_j\langle y_i,y_j\rangle_w=\lambda_j\delta_{ij}.
$$
Thus <orthonormality> in the stated expansion gives
$$
F[y_{\rm trial}]=A^2\left(\lambda_0+\sum_{i\geq1}c_i^2\lambda_i\right),\qquad G[y_{\rm trial}]=A^2\left(1+\sum_{i\geq1}c_i^2\right),
$$
and hence the <Rayleigh quotient>
$$
\boxed{\lambda_{\rm trial}=\frac{\lambda_0+\sum_{i\geq1}c_i^2\lambda_i}{1+\sum_{i\geq1}c_i^2}.}
$$
The expansion is understood for nonzero admissible functions with finite norm and energy.

For a general endpoint-preserving variation $y+\varepsilon\eta$, explicitly
$$
\delta F=2\int_a^b[-(py')'+qy]\eta\,dx,\qquad\delta G=2\int_a^bwy\eta\,dx,
$$
so
$$
\delta\Lambda=\frac{\delta F-\Lambda\delta G}{G}=\frac2G\int_a^b[-(py')'+qy-\Lambda wy]\eta\,dx.
$$
The <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> shows that stationarity is exactly the <eigenvalue> equation with $\lambda=\Lambda$. Conversely an <eigenfunction> has vanishing first variation and quotient equal to its <eigenvalue>. Completeness gives the same weighted-average formula for any nonzero admissible expansion, even one with zero ground-state coefficient. Since every $\lambda_i\geq\lambda_0$,
$$
\boxed{\Lambda[y]\geq\lambda_0.}
$$
This is the <Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle>.

For the interval problem, the normalized <Dirichlet eigenfunctions> and their <eigenvalues>, with the paper's zero-based indexing, are
$$
y_i(x)=\sqrt2\sin[(i+1)\pi x],\qquad\lambda_i=(i+1)^2\pi^2.
$$
For $u=x(1-x)$, direct integration gives $F=\int_0^1(1-2x)^2dx=1/3$ and $G=\int_0^1x^2(1-x)^2dx=1/30$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\lambda_{\rm trial}=10>\lambda_0=\pi^2.}
$$
The <sine expansion and Rayleigh quotient of a parabolic Dirichlet trial> follow from the coefficient integral
$$
\int_0^1x(1-x)y_i(x)dx=\frac{2\sqrt2[1-(-1)^{i+1}]}{(i+1)^3\pi^3}.
$$
Consequently the requested expansion has
$$
\boxed{A=\frac{4\sqrt2}{\pi^3},\qquad c_i=\frac{1-(-1)^{i+1}}{2(i+1)^3}.}
$$
Equivalently $u=(8/\pi^3)\sum_{n\geq1,\ n\text{ odd}}\sin(n\pi x)/n^3$. In particular $c_1=0$: the first excited <eigenfunction> $\sqrt2\sin(2\pi x)$ is antisymmetric about $1/2$, whereas the trial function is symmetric. Since $10-\pi^2>0$, the final ratio is exactly
$$
\boxed{\frac{c_1^2(\lambda_1-\lambda_0)}{\lambda_{\rm trial}-\lambda_0}=0.}
$$
The first nonzero correction is $c_2=1/27$, not $c_1$. Reindexing only the nonzero terms would change the meaning of the requested ratio and is not used here.
