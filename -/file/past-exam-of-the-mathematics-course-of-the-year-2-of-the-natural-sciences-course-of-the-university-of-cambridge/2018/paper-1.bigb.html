= Solution

Apply the <product rule> in <Einstein notation>, using the <Levi-Civita symbol> to express the <cross product>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)
&=\partial_i(\epsilon_{ijk}a_jb_k)\\
&=\epsilon_{ijk}(\partial_i a_j)b_k+\epsilon_{ijk}a_j\partial_i b_k\\
&=b_k\epsilon_{kij}\partial_i a_j-a_j\epsilon_{jik}\partial_i b_k\\
&=\mathbf b\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf a)-\mathbf a\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf b).
\end{aligned}
$$
The third line uses a cyclic permutation of indices in the first term and an interchange of two indices in the second. Rearranging proves the <divergence of a cross product> identity:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf a\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf b)
=\mathbf b\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf a)-\nabla\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)}.
$$

= Solution

Take any constant <vector> $\mathbf a$. Its <curl> is zero, so part (a) and the <divergence theorem> give
$$
\mathbf a\cdot\int_V\nabla\times\mathbf F\,dV
=-\int_V\nabla\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf F)\,dV
=-\int_S(\mathbf a\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS,
$$
where $\mathbf n$ is the outward unit <normal vector>. The <scalar triple product> identity rewrites the last integrand as $\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf F\times\mathbf n)$. Since $\mathbf a$ is arbitrary, equality of these <scalar products> implies the <vector> identity
$$
\boxed{\int_V\nabla\times\mathbf F\,dV
=-\int_S\mathbf F\times\mathbf n\,dS
=\int_S\mathbf n\times\mathbf F\,dS}.
$$

First compute the <curl> directly:
$$
\nabla\times\mathbf F=(0,1,2x).
$$
Reflection symmetry in $x$ makes the last component integrate to zero. The middle component gives the <hemisphere>'s volume, so
$$
\int_V\nabla\times\mathbf F\,dV
=\left(0,\frac{2\pi R^3}{3},0\right).
$$
For an independent <surface integral>, the closed boundary consists of the curved <hemisphere> and its flat base. The right-hand side in part (b) is $\int_S\mathbf n\times\mathbf F\,dS$.

On the curved part use <spherical coordinates> with $0\leq\theta\leq\pi/2$ and $0\leq\phi<2\pi$. There,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf n&=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta),\\
\mathbf F&=(R\cos\theta,R^2\sin^2\theta,0),\qquad dS=R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi,\\
\mathbf n\times\mathbf F
&=(-R^2\cos\theta\sin^2\theta,\ R\cos^2\theta,R^2\sin^3\theta\cos\phi-R\sin\theta\cos\theta\sin\phi).
\end{aligned}
$$
The last component has zero angular integral. The first two components give
$$
\int_{S_{\mathrm{curved}}}\mathbf n\times\mathbf F\,dS
=\left(-2\pi R^4\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\sin^3\theta\,d\theta,
2\pi R^3\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta,0\right)
=\left(-\frac{\pi R^4}{2},\frac{2\pi R^3}{3},0\right).
$$
On the base, $z=0$ and the outward <normal vector> is $-\mathbf e_z$. Thus $\mathbf n\times\mathbf F=(x^2+y^2,0,0)$. In <polar coordinates> its integral is
$$
\int_{S_{\mathrm{base}}}\mathbf n\times\mathbf F\,dS
=\left(2\pi\int_0^R\rho^3\,d\rho,0,0\right)
=\left(\frac{\pi R^4}{2},0,0\right).
$$
The first components cancel, and therefore both required sides equal
$$
\boxed{\left(0,\frac{2\pi R^3}{3},0\right),\qquad \alpha=\frac{2\pi R^3}{3}}.
$$

For the <linearly damped string>, substitute $u(x,t)=X(x)T(t)$ and divide by $XT$:
$$
\frac{X''}{X}=\frac{T''+2\lambda T'}{T}=-\alpha^2.
$$
Thus <separation of variables> gives
$$
\boxed{X''+\alpha^2X=0,\qquad T''+2\lambda T'+\alpha^2T=0}.

The fixed-end <Dirichlet boundary conditions> require $X(0)=X(L)=0$. Nonzero solutions are $X_n(x)=\sin(n\pi x/L)$, with $\alpha_n=n\pi/L$, $n\geq1$. Neither $\alpha=0$ nor a negative spatial <eigenvalue> produces a nonzero function obeying both endpoint conditions.

For each sine <normal mode>, the temporal <characteristic equation> is $s^2+2\lambda s+\alpha_n^2=0$, with roots $s=-\lambda\pm\sqrt{\lambda^2-\alpha_n^2}$. Since $\lambda L/\pi=M+1/2$, the first $M$ modes are <overdamped> and all higher modes are <underdamped>. No integer $n$ gives <critical damping>.

The zero initial displacement excludes the hyperbolic cosine and cosine terms. Hence
$$
\begin{aligned}
u(x,t)=e^{-\lambda t}\bigg[&\sum_{n=1}^{M}A_n\sin(\alpha_nx)\sinh(\Omega_nt)\\
&+\sum_{n=M+1}^{\infty}A_n\sin(\alpha_nx)\sin(\omega_nt)\bigg],
\end{aligned}
$$
where
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\alpha_n&=\frac{n\pi}{L},\\
\Omega_n&=\frac\pi L\sqrt{\left(M+\frac12\right)^2-n^2}\quad(1\leq n\leq M),\\
\omega_n&=\frac\pi L\sqrt{n^2-\left(M+\frac12\right)^2}\quad(n\geq M+1).
\end{aligned}}
$$
To impose the initial <velocity>, let $f_n=\frac2L\int_0^L f(x)\sin(\alpha_nx)\,dx$ be its <Fourier sine series> coefficients. Differentiating the expansion at $t=0$ gives $A_n\Omega_n=f_n$ for the <overdamped> modes and $A_n\omega_n=f_n$ for the <underdamped> modes. These determine all coefficients. Despite the hyperbolic sine, the first modes decay: $\Omega_n<\lambda$, so both temporal exponential rates are negative.

For $x\ne\xi$, the homogeneous solutions are $e^x,e^{-x}$. The left <boundary condition> selects $\sinh x$, while boundedness at infinity selects $e^{-x}$ on the right. Thus write $G=A(\xi)\sinh x$ for $x<\xi$ and $G=B(\xi)e^{-x}$ for $x>\xi$.

A unit <Dirac delta distribution> in the second <derivative> requires continuity of $G$ and a <derivative> jump of one:
$$
A\sinh\xi=Be^{-\xi},\qquad
-Be^{-\xi}-A\cosh\xi=1.
$$
The second equation and the first give $-A e^\xi=1$, so the <Dirichlet half-line Green function for d2 minus 1> is
$$
\boxed{G(x,\xi)=-\sinh(\min\{x,\xi\})e^{-\max\{x,\xi\}}
=-\frac12\left(e^{-|x-\xi|}-e^{-(x+\xi)}\right)}.
$$
The second expression is also a <method of images> construction. Applying the <Green's function> to the forcing gives
$$
\boxed{y(x)=-e^{-x}\int_0^x\sinh\xi\,f(\xi)\,d\xi
-\sinh x\int_x^\infty e^{-\xi}f(\xi)\,d\xi}.
$$
For bounded forcing this is bounded, vanishes at zero and solves the equation; a bounded homogeneous solution vanishing at zero is zero, proving uniqueness. More general forcing is allowed when the integrals converge and the resulting function has the required growth. Arbitrary unbounded forcing need not admit a bounded solution; this distinction is relevant in part (b).

For $x>0$, differentiation of $u=y/x$ gives
$$
u'=\frac{y'}x-\frac y{x^2},\qquad
u''=\frac{y''}x-\frac{2y'}{x^2}+\frac{2y}{x^3},\qquad
u''+\frac2xu'-u=\frac{y''-y}{x}.
$$
Thus the <radial Laplacian> equation reduces to
$$
\boxed{y''-y=xg(x),\qquad f(x)=xg(x)}.
$$
Boundedness of $u$ at zero implies $y(0)=0$. Conversely, a regular reduced solution with $y(0)=0$ gives finite $u(0)=y'(0)$.

There is an important qualification to the reduction in the printed question: bounded $u$ at infinity requires $y=O(x)$, not bounded $y$. The example in part (ii) actually has $y\sim-x$, so it cannot be the bounded solution described in part (a). The differential equation and origin condition reduce as asserted, and the same <Green's function> can still be used with the correct growth condition. This is the <radial reduction and growth at infinity> distinction.

The reduced forcing is $f(\xi)=\xi\,\mathbf1_{[1,\infty)}(\xi)$. Its exponential-weighted integrals converge, even though the resulting reduced function is not bounded. For $0\leq x<1$, the <Green's function> formula gives
$$
y(x)=-\sinh x\int_1^\infty\xi e^{-\xi}\,d\xi=-\frac2e\sinh x.
$$
For $x\geq1$ it gives
$$
y(x)=-e^{-x}\int_1^x\xi\sinh\xi\,d\xi-\sinh x\int_x^\infty\xi e^{-\xi}\,d\xi.
$$
Use $\int\xi\sinh\xi\,d\xi=\xi\cosh\xi-\sinh\xi$ and $\int_x^\infty\xi e^{-\xi}\,d\xi=(x+1)e^{-x}$ to simplify this to $y(x)=-x+e^{-x-1}$. Dividing by $x$ yields
$$
\boxed{u(x)=\begin{cases}
\displaystyle-\frac2e\frac{\sinh x}{x},&0<x<1,\\[5pt]
\displaystyle-1+\frac{e^{-x}}{ex},&x\geq1,
\end{cases}\qquad u(0)=-\frac2e,\quad A=\frac1e}.
$$
Thus $a(x)=-(2/e)\sinh x/x$ with its continuous value at zero. At $x=1$, both pieces give $u=-1+e^{-2}$ and $u'=-2e^{-2}$, so neither a <Dirac delta distribution> nor its <derivative> is introduced at the forcing jump. The solution is bounded at both ends, with $u\to-1$ at infinity. Its reduced function $y=xu\sim-x$ confirms the growth qualification in part (i).

Using <Fourier inversion> with the stated convention,
$$
f(-\omega)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widetilde f(t)e^{-i\omega t}\,dt.
$$
Hence applying the transform twice gives the <duality property of the Fourier transform>:
$$
\boxed{\mathcal F[\widetilde f](\omega)=2\pi f(-\omega)}.
$$
The formula holds directly for rapidly decreasing smooth functions, and extends through the usual <L2 space> or <tempered distribution> formulations when absolute convergence is unavailable.

For a real nonzero $b$, put $s=bt$ in the <Fourier transform>. Reversing the integration limits when $b<0$ produces the absolute value:
$$
\boxed{\mathcal F[f(b\,\cdot)](\omega)=\frac1{|b|}\widetilde f\left(\frac\omega b\right)}.

This is the <scaling property of the Fourier transform>. If $b=0$ and $f(0)$ exists, the transformed function is constant in time; its transform is $2\pi f(0)\delta(\omega)$ in the sense of <tempered distributions>, rather than an ordinary convergent Fourier integral.

For $T>0$ the difference of <Heaviside step functions> is an interval indicator, and $v=1/2$ on $(-T,T)$ and zero elsewhere. Its <Fourier transform> is

\widetilde v(\omega,T)=\frac12\int_{-T}^{T}e^{-i\omega t}\,dt

Induction shows $(PAP^{-1})^k=PA^kP^{-1}$ for every nonnegative integer $k$: in a product, adjacent factors $P^{-1}P$ cancel. At $k=0$, this is $I=PIP^{-1}$. Hence each partial sum of the <matrix exponential> satisfies
$$
\sum_{k=0}^N\frac{(PAP^{-1})^k}{k!}
=P\left(\sum_{k=0}^N\frac{A^k}{k!}\right)P^{-1}.
$$
The series is absolutely convergent, since a submultiplicative <matrix norm> bounds it by $\sum_k\|A\|^k/k!=e^{\|A\|}$. Passing to the limit therefore proves that the <matrix exponential respects similarity>:
$$
\boxed{e^{PAP^{-1}}=Pe^AP^{-1}}.
$$
No <diagonalizability> assumption is required.

If $A^\dagger=-A$, then $H=-iA$ is a <Hermitian matrix>, because $H^\dagger=iA^\dagger=-iA=H$. By the <finite-dimensional spectral theorem>, there is a <unitary matrix> $Q$ with
$$
H=Q\operatorname{diag}(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_n)Q^\dagger,\qquad \theta_j\in\mathbb R.
$$
Thus $A=Q\operatorname{diag}(i\theta_j)Q^\dagger$. Since the <matrix exponential respects similarity>,
$$
\boxed{e^A=QCQ^\dagger,\qquad C=\operatorname{diag}(e^{i\theta_1},\ldots,e^{i\theta_n})}.
$$
Each diagonal entry has modulus one, so $C^\dagger C=I$. Using $Q^\dagger Q=QQ^\dagger=I$,
$$
(e^A)^\dagger e^A=QC^\dagger Q^\dagger QCQ^\dagger=I.
$$
Hence **the <exponential of a skew-Hermitian matrix> is unitary**.

The <finite-dimensional spectral theorem> gives a <spectral decomposition> of the <Hermitian matrix>:
$$
A=QDQ^\dagger,\qquad D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n),\quad\lambda_j\in\mathbb R.
$$
The <matrix exponential respects similarity>, so
$$
e^A=Q\operatorname{diag}(e^{\lambda_1},\ldots,e^{\lambda_n})Q^\dagger.
$$
The diagonal entries are real, so the <conjugate transpose> of this expression equals itself. Its <eigenvalues> are $e^{\lambda_j}>0$, proving
$$
\boxed{(e^A)^\dagger=e^A,\qquad \operatorname{spec}(e^A)=\{e^{\lambda_j}\}_j\subset(0,\infty)}.
$$
In particular, for any nonzero $v$, $v^\dagger e^Av=\sum_j e^{\lambda_j}|(Q^\dagger v)_j|^2>0$. Thus the <exponential of a Hermitian matrix> is a <Hermitian positive-definite matrix>, even when $A$ itself has negative <eigenvalues>.

A square <matrix> $A$ is a <diagonalizable matrix> over a specified field if some invertible <matrix> $P$ makes $P^{-1}AP$ diagonal. Equivalently, the underlying <vector space> has a <basis> of <eigenvectors> of $A$.

For example,
$$
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}\ \text{is diagonalizable},\qquad
\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\ \text{is not diagonalizable}}.
$$
The first is already diagonal. The second has only the <eigenvalue> one, of algebraic multiplicity two, but its <eigenspace> consists of $(x,0)^T$ and has dimension one. It cannot supply a two-vector <eigenbasis>.

The <derivative> of an <analytic function> must be independent of the direction of approach. Taking a real increment gives $f'(z)=u_x+iv_x$; taking an imaginary increment gives
$$
f'(z)=\frac{u_y+iv_y}{i}=v_y-iu_y.
$$
Equality of real and imaginary parts proves the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>:
$$
\boxed{u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x}.
$$
A real <harmonic function> is a twice continuously differentiable function whose <Laplacian> vanishes. For the given logarithmic function and $x^2+y^2>0$,
$$
u_x=\frac{2x}{x^2+y^2},\quad u_y=\frac{2y}{x^2+y^2},\quad
u_{xx}+u_{yy}=\frac4{x^2+y^2}-\frac{4(x^2+y^2)}{(x^2+y^2)^2}=0.
$$
It is therefore harmonic everywhere on the <punctured complex plane>.

On any domain admitting a <branch of the complex logarithm>, choose
$$
\boxed{v(x,y)=2\operatorname{Arg}(x+iy)+C,\qquad u+iv=2\operatorname{Log}z+iC}.
$$
Its <derivatives> are $v_x=-2y/(x^2+y^2)$ and $v_y=2x/(x^2+y^2)$, as required. For example, the principal <complex logarithm> gives such a <harmonic conjugate> on the plane cut along the negative real axis.

There is no single-valued <harmonic conjugate> on the whole punctured plane. Indeed its putative differential would satisfy
$$
\oint_{|z|=1}dv=\oint_{|z|=1}\left(-\frac{2y}{x^2+y^2}\,dx+\frac{2x}{x^2+y^2}\,dy\right)=4\pi,
$$
whereas a globally defined function has zero integral of its differential around a closed curve. This is the <log modulus has no global harmonic conjugate on the punctured plane> obstruction. Thus the conjugate requested in the question must be interpreted locally or on a cut plane.

Expand the exponential in inverse powers of $z$:
$$
g(z)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{z^{-n}}{n!}-\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{z^{2-n}}{n!}
=-z^2-z+\frac12+\sum_{k=1}^\infty\left(\frac1{k!}-\frac1{(k+2)!}\right)z^{-k}.

This is a <Laurent series>, not a <Taylor series>, and converges throughout $0<|z|<\infty$. Every displayed coefficient of a negative power is nonzero, so its principal part has infinitely many terms. Thus
$$
\boxed{z=0\ \text{is an essential singularity}}.
$$
The <residue> is the coefficient of $z^{-1}$:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Res}_{z=0}g=1-\frac1{3!}=\frac56}.
$$

Divide by $2x^2$ to put the <ordinary differential equation> into normalized form $y''+P(x)y'+Q(x)y=0$:
$$
P(x)=-\frac1{2x},\qquad Q(x)=\frac{1+x}{2x^2}.

The coefficients are singular at zero, but
$$

are both analytic there. This is precisely the <regular singular point criterion for a second-order equation>, so **$x=0$ is a <regular singular point>**.

Apply the <Frobenius method>. For $y=x^\sigma\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_nx^n$, the coefficient of the lowest power $x^\sigma$ comes only from $2x^2y''-xy'+y$; the remaining term $xy$ starts one power higher. As $a_0\ne0$, this lowest coefficient gives the <indicial equation>
$$
2\sigma(\sigma-1)-\sigma+1=2\sigma^2-3\sigma+1=(2\sigma-1)(\sigma-1)=0.
$$
Therefore the two possible <characteristic exponents at a regular singular point> are
$$
\boxed{\sigma=\frac12\quad\text{or}\quad\sigma=1}.
$$
Their difference is not an integer, so the two corresponding <Frobenius solutions> do not require a logarithmic correction.

For $n\geq1$, the coefficient of $x^{n+\sigma}$ is
$$
\big[2(n+\sigma)(n+\sigma-1)-(n+\sigma)+1\big]a_n+a_{n-1}=0.
$$
Factoring the bracket gives the <recurrence relation>
$$
\boxed{a_n=-\frac{a_{n-1}}{[2(n+\sigma)-1](n+\sigma-1)}}.
$$
For $\sigma=1/2$ this is $a_n=-a_{n-1}/[n(2n-1)]$. Iteration and $(2n)!=2^n n!(2n-1)!!$ yield
$$
\boxed{a_n=\frac{(-1)^na_0}{n!(2n-1)!!}=\frac{(-2)^na_0}{(2n)!}\quad(\sigma=1/2)}.
$$
For $\sigma=1$, the recurrence is $a_n=-a_{n-1}/[n(2n+1)]$, giving
$$
\boxed{a_n=\frac{(-1)^na_0}{n!(2n+1)!!}=\frac{(-2)^na_0}{(2n+1)!}\quad(\sigma=1)}.
$$
Here the <double factorial> products are one at $n=0$, consistent with the chosen normalization. Recognizing the cosine and sine <power series>, for positive $x$ one may also write the two <Frobenius solutions> as
$$
y_{1/2}=a_0\sqrt{x}\cos\sqrt{2x},\qquad
y_1=a_0x\frac{\sin\sqrt{2x}}{\sqrt{2x}}.
$$
The constants multiplying the two independent solutions may be chosen separately.

For fixed endpoint values and a smooth <variation> vanishing at the endpoints, the first variation is
$$
\delta T=\int_a^b(f_y\delta y+f_{y'}\delta y')\,dx
=\int_a^b\left(f_y-\frac d{dx}f_{y'}\right)\delta y\,dx.
$$
The <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> gives the <Euler-Lagrange equation>
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dx}\frac{\partial f}{\partial y'}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=0}.
$$
If $f$ is independent of $y$, then $y$ is a <cyclic coordinate>, and integration gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial f}{\partial y'}=C}.
$$
This is conservation of the corresponding <generalized momentum>.

The integrand has no explicit $y$ dependence. Thus the <Euler-Lagrange equation> gives the <conserved transverse optical momentum>
$$
p=\frac{\mu(x)y'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}.
$$
At $x=0$, $\mu(0)=\sqrt2$ and the crossing slope is one, so $p=1$. Solving for the positive slope gives $y'=1/\sqrt{\mu^2-1}$, hence
$$
\boxed{y'(x)=\begin{cases}
(1-x)^{-1/3},&-\alpha\leq x<0,\\
(1+x)^{-1/2},&0\leq x\leq\alpha.
\end{cases}}
$$
The slopes agree at zero. The same conserved momentum holds across the interface because the <refractive index> is continuous and there is no point source in the variational equation.

Here a least-time ray means the minimum between the source and the receiver point selected by this ray. For those fixed endpoints, $\partial^2[\mu\sqrt{1+y'^2}]/\partial y'^2=\mu(1+y'^2)^{-3/2}>0$ proves strict <convexity> in the slope, so the stationary ray is the unique minimum among graph paths. If the receiver could instead be freely placed anywhere on the line and its height were also minimized over, the horizontal ray would be faster; that different problem is incompatible with the prescribed crossing slope.

Substitute $T=e^{-\nu t}R(r)$ into the axisymmetric <heat equation> and cancel the exponential:
$$
-\nu R=\frac1r(r\kappa R')'.
$$
Thus the radial <ordinary differential equation> and its <Sturm-Liouville form> are
$$
\boxed{(r\kappa R')'+\nu rR=0,\qquad -(pR')'=\nu wR,\quad p(r)=r\kappa(r),\quad w(r)=r}.
$$
The weight in this <Sturm-Liouville problem> is therefore $\boxed{w=r}$. The outer <Dirichlet boundary condition> is $R(1)=0$, and the physical solution is regular at the centre. The factor $r$ in the <weighted inner product> is also the radial part of the polar area element.

{parent=10}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Multiply the separated <Sturm-Liouville problem> by real $R$ and apply <integration by parts>:
$$
\nu\int_0^1rR^2\,dr
=-\int_0^1R(r\kappa R')'\,dr
=-[r\kappa RR']_0^1+\int_0^1r\kappa R'^2\,dr.
$$
The outer endpoint term vanishes because $R(1)=0$. At zero, regularity gives vanishing radial flux. This can be obtained from the equation without assuming a bounded <derivative>: let $F=r\kappa R'$. Since $F'=-\nu rR$ and $R$ is bounded, $F$ has a finite limit at zero. If that limit were nonzero, positive bounded <thermal diffusivity> would make $R'$ have fixed sign and magnitude at least a constant times $1/r$ sufficiently near zero, contradicting bounded $R$. Hence $F(0)=0$, and in fact $F(r)=-\nu\int_0^r sR(s)\,ds=O(r^2)$. This also forces $RF\to0$ for bounded $R$.

The boundary terms therefore vanish, proving
$$
\boxed{\nu\int_0^1rR^2\,dr=\int_0^1r\kappa(r)R'(r)^2\,dr}.
$$
As usual for diffusion, $\kappa$ is nonnegative and positive for $0<r\leq1$; the later power-law case may vanish at the centre. The argument applies in that case as well.

= Solution

For a nonzero admissible radial profile define the <radial diffusion Rayleigh quotient>
$$
\mathcal R[R]=\frac{\int_0^1r\kappa R'^2\,dr}{\int_0^1rR^2\,dr}.
$$
Minimizing the numerator subject to $\int_0^1rR^2\,dr=1$ introduces a multiplier $\nu$. The <Euler-Lagrange equation> for $r\kappa R'^2-\nu rR^2$ is
$$
(r\kappa R')'+\nu rR=0.
$$
The allowed variations vanish at $r=1$, and the natural condition at the centre is zero radial flux. Thus the minimum, when attained, is the smallest decay <eigenvalue> $\nu_0$. Equivalently, expansion in weighted-orthogonal <eigenfunctions> makes $\mathcal R[R]$ a weighted average of their decay <eigenvalues>. The <Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\nu_0\leq\mathcal R[R_{\mathrm{trial}}]}.
$$
Admissible trial functions must obey the outer <boundary condition>, be nonzero and have finite <weighted norm> and <energy>; they need not solve the differential equation. If the diffusion operator has no lowest <eigenfunction>, the same variational argument bounds the infimum of its <spectrum>. Existence of a <fundamental diffusion mode> is an additional assumption for strongly vanishing diffusivity, not a consequence of the identity alone.

For the proposed trial profile, $R'=-qr^{q-1}$. Its weighted <energy> is
$$
\int_0^1r^{n+1}R'^2\,dr=q^2\int_0^1r^{n+2q-1}\,dr=\frac{q^2}{n+2q}.
$$
The squared <weighted norm> is
$$
\int_0^1r(1-r^q)^2\,dr
=\frac12-\frac2{q+2}+\frac1{2q+2}
=\frac{q^2}{2(q+1)(q+2)}.
$$
Both are finite for $q>0$ and $n\geq0$, and $R(1)=0$. Therefore the <Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle> gives
$$
\boxed{\nu_{\mathrm{trial}}(q,n)=\frac{2(q+1)(q+2)}{n+2q},\qquad
\inf\operatorname{spec}\leq\nu_{\mathrm{trial}}}.
$$
When a <fundamental diffusion mode> exists, its decay rate is the infimum and is bounded above by this expression.

The existence qualification matters for the full range of $n$ printed in the question. For $n>2$, trial functions concentrated within radius $\varepsilon$ have quotient proportional to $\varepsilon^{n-2}$, proving the <loss of a spectral gap under strongly degenerate radial diffusion>. The infimum is zero and is not attained, because zero <energy> together with $R(1)=0$ forces the zero function. Thus there is no positive lowest-mode decay rate in that case. Even at $n=2$, the mode equation is $r^2R''+3rR'+\nu R=0$: its characteristic powers are $r^{-1\pm\sqrt{1-\nu}}$, and for $\nu>0$ every nonzero solution is unbounded at the centre. Hence the asserted bounded fundamental mode cannot be assumed for all $n\geq0$. The trial quotient remains valid, and the next part's $n=0$ case has the usual fundamental mode.

For constant diffusivity, the trial <Rayleigh quotient> simplifies to
$$
\nu_{\mathrm{trial}}(q,0)=\frac{(q+1)(q+2)}q=q+3+\frac2q.
$$
Its <derivative> is $1-2/q^2$, so its only positive critical point is $q=\sqrt2$. The second <derivative> is $4/q^3>0$; also the quotient diverges as $q\downarrow0$ or $q\to\infty$. Therefore this is the global optimum within the trial family:
$$
\boxed{q_{\mathrm{opt}}=\sqrt2,\qquad \nu_0\leq\nu_{\mathrm{trial,min}}=3+2\sqrt2\approx5.82843}.
$$
The two requested profiles are
$$
\boxed{T_{\mathrm{trial}}(r,0)=1-r^{\sqrt2},\qquad
T_{\mathrm{trial}}(0,t)=e^{-(3+2\sqrt2)t}}.
$$
The initial radial graph decreases from one at the centre to zero at the rim, has horizontal tangent at the centre, and is concave downward for $0<r<1$. The central temperature decreases exponentially from one to zero, with initial time <derivative> $-(3+2\sqrt2)$. These are trial profiles, not exact solutions of the full <heat equation>. Although the radial trial function is not twice differentiable at the centre, it has finite weighted <energy> and belongs to the variational trial class used by the <Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle>.
