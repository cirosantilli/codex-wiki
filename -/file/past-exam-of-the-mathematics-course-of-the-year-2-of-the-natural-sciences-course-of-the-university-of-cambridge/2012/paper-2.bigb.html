For real positive weight $w$, the <weighted inner product> is
$$
\boxed{\langle y_1,y_2\rangle_w=\int_a^b y_1(x)^*y_2(x)w(x)\,dx.}
$$
The functions must have finite weighted norm; the conjugate is omitted for real functions. Multiplying the differential equation by $xe^{-x}$ gives
$$
(xe^{-x}y')'+ne^{-x}y=0.
$$
Thus the <Sturm-Liouville form> has $p=xe^{-x}$, $q=0$ and $w=e^{-x}$. With the positive-sign spectral convention define
$$
\boxed{\mathcal Ay=-\frac1w(py')'=-xy''+(x-1)y',\qquad\mathcal Ay=ny.}
$$
The existing <Laguerre differential operator on polynomials> uses the opposite operator $L=-\mathcal A$, with eigenvalues $-n$; keeping this sign convention explicit avoids confusing the two descriptions.

For admissible $f,g$, <integration by parts> gives
$$
\langle f,\mathcal Ag\rangle_w-\langle\mathcal Af,g\rangle_w=\left[xe^{-x}(f'^*g-f^*g')\right]_0^\infty.
$$
The boundary form must vanish for every pair in the self-adjoint domain. In the usual regular-at-zero Laguerre realization one imposes $\lim_{x\downarrow0}xe^{-x}y'=0$, excluding the logarithmic branch; at infinity one uses the weighted square-integrable domain with vanishing boundary form. A condition $y(\infty)=0$ would be inappropriate for polynomial eigenfunctions. For any pair of polynomials, the factor $x$ makes the boundary form zero at zero, while the exponential beats all polynomial growth at infinity. These functions also have finite weighted norms.

Self-adjointness yields $(n-m)\langle y_m,y_n\rangle_w=0$, giving <orthogonality of Sturm-Liouville eigenfunctions>:
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty e^{-x}y_m(x)^*y_n(x)\,dx=0\quad(m\ne n).}
$$
For the specified polynomials, direct differentiation gives $\mathcal Au=u$ and $\mathcal Av=3v$. Hence their eigenvalues in the displayed convention are **1 and 3**, respectively, or **−1 and −3** for $L=-\mathcal A$.

To find the quadratic, put $h=ax^2+bx+c$. The equation $xh''+(1-x)h'+2h=0$ reduces to $(4a+b)x+(b+2c)=0$. Thus $b=-4a$, $c=2a$. Since $h(1)=-a=1$,
$$
\boxed{h(x)=-x^2+4x-2.}
$$
These are scalar multiples of the <Laguerre polynomials> of degrees one, three and two.

For $n=0$, the first integral is $xe^{-x}y'=C$, so all solutions with the prescribed interior normalization are
$$
y(x)=1+C\int_{x_0}^x\frac{e^t}{t}dt.
$$
Two independent choices are
$$
\boxed{y_1(x)=1,\qquad y_2(x)=1+\int_{x_0}^x\frac{e^t}{t}dt.}
$$
They both equal one at $x_0$, and their Wronskian is $e^x/x\ne0$. For sufficiently large $x$, the last unit interval contributes at least $e^{x-1}/x$, proving that $y_2\to\infty$. These are the <zero-eigenvalue solutions of the Laguerre differential equation>, with the second expressible through the <exponential integral>. It also has a logarithmic singularity at zero and is not an admissible polynomial or weighted square-integrable eigenfunction. An interior value alone does not enforce the endpoint conditions of the spectral problem.

Use <separation of variables> with $u=W(x)T(t)$ and choose $W''/W=T'/T=k$. Then $T=e^{kt}$ and $W'(0)=-W(0)$, $W'(1)=-W(1)$.

If $k=0$, write $W=A+Bx$. The first condition gives $B=-A$, and the second gives $A+2B=0$. Together they force $A=B=0$, so this case contributes only the zero solution.

If $k=\alpha^2>0$, write $W=A\cosh(\alpha x)+B\sinh(\alpha x)$. The first condition gives $B=-A/\alpha$. The second becomes
$$
W'(1)+W(1)=A(\alpha-\alpha^{-1})\sinh\alpha=0.
$$
A nonzero solution therefore requires $\alpha=1$, giving $W\propto e^{-x}$ and time factor $e^t$.

If $k=-\beta^2<0$, write $W=A\cos(\beta x)+B\sin(\beta x)$. Now $B=-A/\beta$, and the remaining condition is $-A(\beta+\beta^{-1})\sin\beta=0$. Thus $\beta=n\pi$, $n\geq1$. Choosing a convenient amplitude gives $W_n=n\pi\cos(n\pi x)-\sin(n\pi x)$. The complete <Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion> is consequently
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=c_0e^{t-x}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty c_ne^{-n^2\pi^2t}W_n(x).}
$$
These are self-adjoint separated <Robin boundary conditions>: the boundary form $[f^*g'-f'^*g]_0^1$ vanishes because both functions satisfy $y'=-y$ at each end. The regular interval problem has a complete orthogonal eigenbasis; the calculation includes its one negative eigenvalue for $-D^2$, not only its positive ones.

To project the initial data, calculate the squared norms:
$$
\int_0^1e^{-2x}dx=\frac{1-e^{-2}}2,\qquad\int_0^1W_n^2dx=\frac{n^2\pi^2+1}{2}.
$$
The second identity uses the half-unit integrals of $\cos^2(n\pi x)$ and $\sin^2(n\pi x)$ and the zero integral of their product. By <orthogonal projection>,
$$
\boxed{c_0=\frac{2}{1-e^{-2}}\int_0^1e^{-x}f(x)dx,\qquad c_n=\frac{2}{n^2\pi^2+1}\int_0^1f(x)W_n(x)dx.}
$$
For square-integrable $f$, the initial expansion is understood in that norm. Classical agreement of boundary derivatives at $t=0$ requires compatible smoother data.

This is <Robin diffusion with a growing mode>, in the reflected version of its positive-coordinate-derivative example. Growth is not a contradiction: the outward derivative is $-u_x$ at the left endpoint, so these conditions are not dissipative outward-normal conditions at both ends. Indeed
$$
\frac12\frac d{dt}\int_0^1u^2dx=u(0,t)^2-u(1,t)^2-\int_0^1u_x^2dx,
$$
and the boundary can supply energy. The growing contribution is absent exactly when the displayed integral defining $c_0$ vanishes.

The $k_0^2uv$ terms cancel in $uL_Hv-vL_Hu$. For a ball $B_R$, the <product rule> and <divergence theorem> give <Green's second identity> in the form
$$
\int_{B_R}(uL_Hv-vL_Hu)\,d^3r=\int_{\partial B_R}(u\partial_nv-v\partial_nu)\,dS.
$$
Thus the whole-space integral vanishes when this boundary term tends to zero. For example, uniform bounds $u,v=O(R^{-1-\epsilon})$ and $\nabla u,\nabla v=O(R^{-1-\epsilon})$, with $\epsilon>0$, make it $O(R^{-2\epsilon})$.

**Pointwise vanishing alone, as stated in the source, is insufficient.** Smooth fields agreeing with $u=\sin(k_0r)/r$ and $v=\cos(k_0r)/r$ outside a ball both tend to zero, but their distant boundary integral is $-4\pi k_0$. One may smooth them inside the ball without changing that flux. The requested zero identity therefore needs sufficient decay or boundary/radiation conditions that actually eliminate the boundary term.

For the generalized <plane wave> eigenfunctions, their <orthogonality> is distributional:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\mathbb R^3}\psi_{k_1}^*(r)\psi_{k_2}(r)d^3r
&=\frac1{(2\pi)^3}\prod_{j=1}^3\int_{\mathbb R}e^{i(k_{2j}-k_{1j})r_j}dr_j\\
&=\prod_{j=1}^3\delta(k_{2j}-k_{1j})=\delta^{(3)}(k_1-k_2).
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $\int e^{iqx}dx=2\pi\delta(q)$ is the <Fourier representation of the Dirac delta function>. These eigenfunctions are not ordinary square-integrable functions or decaying test fields.

Expand $\Phi=\int A_k\psi_k\,d^3k$ and define $V_k=\int\psi_k^*(r')V(r')d^3r'$. Since $L_H\psi_k=(k_0^2-k^2)\psi_k$, projection of the source equation gives $(k_0^2-k^2)A_k=V_k$. Formally dividing and substituting back gives
$$
\boxed{\Phi(r)=\frac1{(2\pi)^3}\int_{\mathbb R^3}d^3k\int_{\mathbb R^3}d^3r'\,\frac{e^{ik\cdot(r-r')}}{k_0^2-k^2}V(r').}
$$
At the shell $k=k_0$, a pole prescription is required; homogeneous solutions can otherwise be added. The formula is an inverse of the <Helmholtz equation> with that convention still to be selected.

Let $H$ denote the kernel in this source formula, so $L_HH=+\delta^{(3)}$. Put $l=|r-r'|$ and align the Fourier polar axis with $r-r'$. Angular integration gives
$$
\int_{S^2}e^{ikl\cos\theta}d\Omega=2\pi\int_{-1}^1e^{ikl\mu}d\mu=4\pi\frac{\sin(kl)}{kl}.
$$
Consequently the <radial Fourier representation of the Helmholtz Green function> gives
$$
H(l)=\frac1{2\pi^2l}\int_0^\infty\frac{k\sin(kl)}{k_0^2-k^2}dk.
$$
The last Green function written in the PDF has the opposite denominator. It is obtained by setting $G=-H$, the common convention $L_HG=-\delta^{(3)}$:
$$
\boxed{G(l)=\frac1{2\pi^2l}\int_0^\infty\frac{k\sin(kl)}{k^2-k_0^2}dk,\qquad\Phi(r)=-\int G(r,r')V(r')d^3r'.}
$$
Thus the two displayed source and Green formulas are consistent only when this minus sign is retained; $G$ cannot also be the positive-delta inverse kernel.

Finally the integral is not an ordinary improper integral across $k=k_0$. A <Cauchy principal value> yields $G_{\rm PV}(l)=\cos(k_0l)/(4\pi l)$. The outgoing prescription $k^2-k_0^2-i0$ yields the <outgoing Green function for the three-dimensional Helmholtz equation>, $G_+(l)=e^{ik_0l}/(4\pi l)$; the opposite sign gives the incoming wave. The <Sokhotski–Plemelj formula> accounts for their difference. The source does not specify which prescription is intended, so the boxed radial expression must be read with one chosen consistently.

For a counterclockwise simple contour containing $z_0$, and $f$ analytic on a neighbourhood of its interior and boundary, the <Cauchy integral formula> is
$$
\boxed{\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{z-z_0}dz=f(z_0).}
$$
For the required rational integral, use <partial fraction decomposition>:
$$
\frac1{z^2-1}=\frac12\left(\frac1{z-1}-\frac1{z+1}\right).
$$
Both points $1,-1$ lie inside $|z|=2$. Applying the formula with the constant analytic function one gives $2\pi i$ for each of the two terms. They cancel, so
$$
\boxed{\oint_{|z|=2}\frac{dz}{z^2-1}=\frac12(2\pi i-2\pi i)=0.}
$$
Reversing the circle's orientation would leave this zero result unchanged.

= Solution

On a positively oriented contour encircling $z_0$ once inside an annulus of analyticity, the <Laurent series> coefficients are
$$
\boxed{a_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^{n+1}}dz,\qquad n\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
This follows by inserting the convergent Laurent expansion and integrating term by term: only the term with exponent $-1$ has nonzero contour integral. Uniform convergence on compact circles in the annulus justifies that step.

For the function at hand,
$$
\frac1{z(z-1)}=-\frac1z\frac1{1-z}=-\frac1z\sum_{j=0}^\infty z^j.
$$
The <geometric series> converges for $|z|<1$, and the pole at zero excludes the centre. Thus
$$
\boxed{\frac1{z(z-1)}=-\sum_{n=-1}^\infty z^n,\qquad 0<|z|<1.}
$$
The coefficients are $a_n=-1$ for $n\geq-1$ and zero for $n<-1$. The coefficient formula gives the same result on $|z|=\rho<1$: $a_n=(2\pi i)^{-1}\oint z^{-n-2}/(z-1)\,dz$, and expansion of $(z-1)^{-1}$ selects exactly those coefficients.

The annulus is essential. For $|z|>1$, expand instead in inverse powers:
$$
\frac1{z(z-1)}=\frac1{z^2}\frac1{1-z^{-1}}=\sum_{j=0}^\infty z^{-j-2},\qquad |z|>1.
$$
These are different Laurent expansions about the same centre on the two annuli separated by the singularity at $z=1$. The series requested in the PDF is the inner one, not a global expansion.

For integrable $f,g$, define their <convolution> by $(f*g)(x)=\int_{\mathbb R}f(y)g(x-y)dy$. Absolute integrability of the double integral permits <Fubini's theorem>. With $s=x-y$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal F[f*g](k)&=\int_{\mathbb R}\!\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-ikx}f(y)g(x-y)dy\,dx\\
&=\int_{\mathbb R}f(y)e^{-iky}dy\int_{\mathbb R}g(s)e^{-iks}ds=\widetilde f(k)\widetilde g(k).
\end{aligned}
$$
The <convolution theorem>, together with uniqueness of the <Fourier transform>, therefore identifies the inverse transform of that product with $f*g$. With the stated convention the inverse carries $1/(2\pi)$, so there is no extra factor in the convolution itself.

Now take $f=e^{-|x|}$. For $x\geq0$, split the convolution at $y=0$ and $y=x$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
h(x)&=\int_{-\infty}^0e^{2y-x}dy+\int_0^xe^{-x}dy+\int_x^\infty e^{x-2y}dy\\
&=\frac12e^{-x}+xe^{-x}+\frac12e^{-x}=(1+x)e^{-x}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The convolution of two even functions is even, so the <self-convolution of a bilateral exponential> gives
$$
\boxed{h(x)=(1+|x|)e^{-|x|}=\begin{cases}(1-x)e^x,&x<0,\\1,&x=0,\\(1+x)e^{-x},&x>0.\end{cases}}
$$
In particular the centre value is one; both pieces extend continuously there.

Splitting the Fourier integral at zero gives
$$
\widetilde f(k)=\int_0^\infty e^{-(1+ik)x}dx+\int_0^\infty e^{-(1-ik)x}dx=\frac2{1+k^2}.
$$
Hence $\widetilde h(k)=4/(1+k^2)^2$. The <Fourier inversion theorem> yields
$$
\boxed{h(x)=\frac2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ikx}}{(1+k^2)^2}dk.}
$$
The integral is absolutely convergent. At $x=0$ it gives $\int_{\mathbb R}(1+k^2)^{-2}dk=\pi/2$, providing a normalization check.

A Cartesian <tensor> of order $n$ is a multilinear geometric object whose components under an orthogonal change of axes obey the <tensor component transformation law>,
$$
T'_{i_1\cdots i_n}=Q_{i_1j_1}\cdots Q_{i_nj_n}T_{j_1\cdots j_n},\qquad Q^TQ=I.
$$
An <isotropic tensor> has the same components under every proper rotation. For the <Kronecker delta>,
$$
\delta'_{ij}=Q_{ip}Q_{jq}\delta_{pq}=Q_{ip}Q_{jp}=\delta_{ij}.
$$
Thus **$\delta_{ij}$ is isotropic**, even under improper orthogonal transformations.

To prove <multiplicativity of the determinant> from the given formula, first consider $C_{pqr}=\epsilon_{ijk}A_{ip}A_{jq}A_{kr}$. Exchanging any two of $p,q,r$ changes its sign, so it is proportional to the <Levi-Civita symbol>. Its $123$ component is the determinant, as follows by expanding the given double-epsilon expression. Therefore
$$
\epsilon_{ijk}A_{ip}A_{jq}A_{kr}=(\det A)\epsilon_{pqr}.
$$
Substitute $(AB)_{il}=A_{ip}B_{pl}$ into that same determinant formula:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(AB)&=\frac16\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{lmn}A_{ip}B_{pl}A_{jq}B_{qm}A_{kr}B_{rn}\\
&=\frac{\det A}{6}\epsilon_{pqr}\epsilon_{lmn}B_{pl}B_{qm}B_{rn}=(\det A)(\det B).
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\det(AB)=\det A\det B.}
$$
No invertibility assumption was used, so this identity includes singular matrices.

Now differentiate the double-epsilon formula for a differentiable $A(t)$. Its three differentiated terms are equal after renaming the paired dummy indices: the simultaneous swaps in both epsilon symbols cancel their signs. Thus
$$
\frac d{dt}\det A=\frac12\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{lmn}\dot A_{il}A_{jm}A_{kn}=C_{il}\dot A_{il},
$$
where $C_{il}$ is the cofactor of entry $A_{il}$. For an invertible matrix, the <adjugate matrix> identity gives $C_{il}=(\det A)(A^{-1})_{li}$. Consequently the <Jacobi determinant derivative formula> is
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}\det A=(\det A)\sum_{i,l}(A^{-1})_{li}\dot A_{il}=(\det A)\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}\dot A).}
$$
The order of the factors inside the trace follows from the reversed cofactor indices; no commutativity of $A$ and $\dot A$ is assumed. The cofactor version is also <Jacobi's formula> and remains valid when $A$ is singular, although the inverse version then cannot be used.

Use displacements $x=(x_1,x_2,x_3)^T$ of the left oxygen, carbon and right oxygen along a common positive direction. In this harmonic model the <kinetic energy> and <elastic potential energy> are
$$
T=\frac12m\dot x_1^2+\frac12M\dot x_2^2+\frac12m\dot x_3^2,\qquad V=\frac k2[(x_2-x_1)^2+(x_3-x_2)^2].
$$
Thus the <generalized eigenvalue problem for small oscillations> is $Kv=\omega^2\mathsf Mv$, where
$$
K=k\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0\\-1&2&-1\\0&-1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathsf M=\operatorname{diag}(m,M,m).
$$
The <normal modes of three masses connected by two equal springs> give the three independent solutions
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{mode}&v&\omega^2\\\hline
\text{translation}&v_0=(1,1,1)^T&0\\
\text{symmetric stretch}&v_s=(1,0,-1)^T&k/m\\
\text{antisymmetric stretch}&v_a=(1,-2m/M,1)^T&k/m+2k/M
\end{array}.}
$$
The second row follows by holding the centre fixed and moving the end masses oppositely. In the remaining internal mode the end displacements are equal, and zero displacement of the <centre of mass> gives $2mv_1+Mv_2=0$, yielding the third row; substitution verifies its frequency. Both nonzero modes have fixed centre of mass. In the symmetric stretch the two bond-length changes are equal; in the antisymmetric stretch they are opposite.

\Image[paper-2-2012-ib-molecular-modes.png]
{title=Longitudinal mode directions of the free three-mass molecular model; the zero mode translates every mass together}

The <zero mode> occurs because a uniform translation changes neither spring length, so $Kv_0=0$. It represents free <center-of-mass motion>, not an additional restoring vibration. Its general time amplitude is $A+Bt$. Only one translational zero mode exists in the restricted one-dimensional model; transverse and rotational motion are outside its specified coordinates.

For the initial impulse, all displacement amplitudes at zero time vanish. Resolve the initial velocity into these three mode shapes:
$$
(u,0,0)^T=\frac{mu}{M+2m}v_0+\frac u2v_s+\frac{Mu}{2(M+2m)}v_a.
$$
Writing $\omega_s=\sqrt{k/m}$ and $\omega_a=\sqrt{k/m+2k/M}$ gives the <impulse on an end mass of a free symmetric three-mass chain>:
$$
\boxed{x(t)=\frac{mu}{M+2m}t\,v_0+\frac{u}{2\omega_s}\sin(\omega_st)v_s+\frac{Mu}{2(M+2m)\omega_a}\sin(\omega_at)v_a.}
$$
In particular
$$
\begin{aligned}
x_1(t)&=V_{\rm cm}t+\frac{u}{2\omega_s}\sin\omega_st+\frac{Mu}{2(M+2m)\omega_a}\sin\omega_at,\\
x_2(t)&=V_{\rm cm}t-\frac{mu}{(M+2m)\omega_a}\sin\omega_at,\\
x_3(t)&=V_{\rm cm}t-\frac{u}{2\omega_s}\sin\omega_st+\frac{Mu}{2(M+2m)\omega_a}\sin\omega_at,
\end{aligned}
$$
where $V_{\rm cm}=mu/(M+2m)$. The initial derivatives are $(u,0,0)$, as required. The <carbon dioxide> model therefore drifts uniformly while undergoing both internal stretches. Its total momentum is the initial $mu$; omitting the zero mode would incorrectly hold the centre of mass fixed.

The <group order> $|G|$ is the number of elements of the finite group. A <normal subgroup> $H\leq G$ satisfies $gHg^{-1}=H$ for every $g\in G$, equivalently its left and right cosets agree.

For the <square>, let $R$ be counterclockwise rotation by $\pi/2$, and $s$ reflection in the horizontal axis. The <dihedral group> is
$$
D_4=\{I,R,R^2,R^3,s,Rs,R^2s,R^3s\},\qquad R^4=s^2=I,\quad sRs=R^{-1}.
$$
The first four elements rotate by $0,90,180,270$ degrees. The other four are <Euclidean reflections> in the horizontal axis, the line $y=x$, the vertical axis and the line $y=-x$, respectively, for a square centred at the origin with sides parallel to the axes.

The eight **nontrivial proper subgroups** are
$$
\boxed{\begin{gathered}
\{I,R^2\},\qquad\{I,R^js\}\quad(j=0,1,2,3),\\
\langle R\rangle=\{I,R,R^2,R^3\},\\
\{I,R^2,s,R^2s\},\qquad\{I,R^2,Rs,R^3s\}.
\end{gathered}}
$$
To check completeness, <Lagrange's theorem> allows proper orders one, two and four. The order-two subgroups are generated by the five involutions displayed. An order-four subgroup containing $R$ or $R^3$ is the rotation subgroup. Otherwise it must contain reflections; their product is a rotation, which must be $R^2$, so its reflections differ by that half-turn, giving the two listed Klein subgroups. The trivial subgroup $\{I\}$ is an additional proper subgroup. Thus the PDF's count of eight uses “proper” to exclude the trivial subgroup; with the usual inclusive definition there are nine proper subgroups.

A <normal subgroup of order two is central>: conjugation fixes its identity and must also fix its only other member. The half-turn $R^2$ commutes with every square symmetry, whereas the reflection subgroups are exchanged by conjugation. Hence the unique order-two normal subgroup is
$$
\boxed{\{I,R^2\}.}
$$
For the general polygon, write $D_n=\{R^j,R^js:0\leq j<n\}$ with $R^n=s^2=I$ and $sRs=R^{-1}$. A rotation $R^j$ commutes with $s$ exactly when $R^j=R^{-j}$, or $2j\equiv0\pmod n$. A reflection cannot commute with $R$ when $n\geq3$, because
$$
R(R^js)R^{-1}=R^{j+2}s\ne R^js.
$$
Therefore the <center of a group> here is $\{I\}$ for odd $n$, and $\{I,R^{n/2}\}$ for even $n$. The order-two normal subgroups correspond exactly to nonidentity central involutions, proving
$$
\boxed{\begin{cases}\text{no order-two normal subgroup},&n\text{ odd},\\\{I,R^{n/2}\}\text{ is the unique one},&n\text{ even}.
\end{cases}}
$$
This agrees with the <involutions in an even dihedral group> and uses the convention $|D_n|=2n$ throughout.

= Solution

A <group homomorphism> is a multiplication-preserving map $\phi:G_1\to G_2$, so $\phi(gh)=\phi(g)\phi(h)$. A <group isomorphism> is a bijective homomorphism; its inverse is automatically multiplication-preserving as well.

For the real <general linear group>, the identity matrix has nonzero determinant. If $A,B$ have nonzero determinants, <multiplicativity of the determinant> gives $\det(AB)=\det A\det B\ne0$. Associativity is inherited from matrix multiplication, and every such matrix has an inverse, with $\det A^{-1}=1/\det A\ne0$. Hence the given set $\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb R)$ is a group.

For the <special orthogonal group>, the identity is included and
$$
(AB)(AB)^T=A(BB^T)A^T=I,\qquad\det(AB)=1
$$
when both factors are special orthogonal. Moreover $A^{-1}=A^T$ is again orthogonal and has determinant one. Thus $\operatorname{SO}(n)$ is a subgroup. The <orientation-preserving general linear group> is also a subgroup: the identity has positive determinant, products preserve positivity, and inversion replaces the determinant by its positive reciprocal.

Now take an element $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ of the <special orthogonal group in two dimensions>. Orthogonality and determinant one imply
$$
\begin{pmatrix}a&c\\b&d\end{pmatrix}=A^T=A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Hence $d=a$, $b=-c$, and the unit length of the first column gives $a^2+c^2=1$. There is an angle $\theta$, unique modulo $2\pi$, such that $a=\cos\theta$, $c=\sin\theta$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{A=R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Define the map from the <circle group> by
$$
\boxed{\phi:U(1)\longrightarrow\operatorname{SO}(2),\qquad a+ic\longmapsto\begin{pmatrix}a&-c\\c&a\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
If $z=a+ic$ and $w=p+iq$, matrix multiplication gives $\phi(z)\phi(w)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}ap-cq&-(aq+cp)\\aq+cp&ap-cq\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\phi(zw)$. Thus it is a homomorphism. The entries determine $a,c$ uniquely, proving injectivity, and the parametrization proves surjectivity. This establishes
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{SO}(2)\cong U(1).}
$$
The use of the unit complex number, rather than an unrestricted real angle, removes the $2\pi$ ambiguity: angles add modulo $2\pi$, exactly as unit complex numbers multiply.

The <conjugacy class> of $g\in G$ is $\{hgh^{-1}:h\in G\}$. An <irreducible representation> acts on a nonzero vector space with no nonzero proper subspace invariant under every group element.

In the <quaternion group>, $1$ and $-1$ commute with all elements, so each forms its own conjugacy class. The element $i$ commutes with $\pm1,\pm i$, while conjugation by $j$ or $k$ changes it to $-i$. Thus its class is $\{i,-i\}$. The same argument for $j,k$ gives all five classes:
$$
\boxed{\{1\},\qquad\{-1\},\qquad\{i,-i\},\qquad\{j,-j\},\qquad\{k,-k\}.}
$$
Their sizes sum to eight. Equivalently each noncentral element has a four-element centralizer and hence a two-element class.

Use the following finite-group representation theorems over $\mathbb C$: the number of inequivalent irreducible representations equals the number of conjugacy classes, and their dimensions obey the <sum of squares of irreducible representation dimensions>, $\sum d_j^2=|G|$. Thus there are five irreducibles, with $\sum_{j=1}^5d_j^2=8$. Starting with five dimensions equal to one uses five of those eight units; increasing one dimension from one to two uses exactly the remaining three, while any larger increase would exceed eight. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{five complex irreducible representations, with dimensions }1,1,1,1,2.}
$$
These are the dimensions underlying the <character table of the quaternion group>. The scalar field matters: the quaternionic irreducible becomes four-dimensional over the reals. The class-count and dimension-sum statements used here have their standard complex meaning.

Let $B=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&b\\c&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ be the lower block. Then
$$
B^2=bcI_2,\qquad T(a)^2=\operatorname{diag}(1,bc,bc),\qquad T(a)^4=\operatorname{diag}(1,(bc)^2,(bc)^2).
$$
The defining relation $a^4=I$ requires $T(a)^4=I_3$, so for real constants $bc=\pm1$. Both possibilities ensure invertibility.

If $bc=-1$, the generator matrix has order four, because its square is not the identity while its fourth power is. Its four powers are distinct, giving a <faithful representation>. If $bc=1$, its square is the identity but it is not itself the identity; its lower diagonal entries are zero. The image then has order two and the <kernel of a group homomorphism> is $\{I,a^2\}$. Thus the <faithfulness criterion for a block representation of the cyclic group of order four> gives
$$
\boxed{\text{faithful exactly when }bc=-1;\qquad\text{unfaithful representation exactly when }bc=1.}
$$
Other parameter values give no representation of $Z_4$, not additional unfaithful cases; $bc=0$ also makes the assigned matrix singular. No orthogonality of these matrices was required.

For the final construction take
$$
\boxed{D(a)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad D(a^r)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&(-1)^r\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Multiplication adds exponents, so $D(a^r)D(a^s)=D(a^{r+s})$ and $D(a)^4=I_2$. The representing matrix is the identity exactly for even exponents. Therefore this is a two-dimensional representation with kernel **$\{I,a^2\}$**, as required. It factors through the quotient cyclic group of order two.
