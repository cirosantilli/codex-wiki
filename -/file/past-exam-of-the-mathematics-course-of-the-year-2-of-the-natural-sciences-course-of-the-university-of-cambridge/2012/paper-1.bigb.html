= Solution
{parent=1}

Using <Cartesian coordinates>, the <Levi-Civita symbol> and the <Einstein summation convention>, the constant-vector assumption gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
[\nabla\times(\mathbf a\times\mathbf F)]_i
&=\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{klm}a_l\partial_jF_m\\
&=(\delta_{il}\delta_{jm}-\delta_{im}\delta_{jl})a_l\partial_jF_m\\
&=a_i\partial_jF_j-a_j\partial_jF_i.
\end{aligned}
$$
Recognizing the <divergence> and <directional derivative> proves
$$
\boxed{\nabla\times(\mathbf a\times\mathbf F)=\mathbf a(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)-(\mathbf a\cdot\nabla)\mathbf F.}
$$
Here the contractions use the <Kronecker delta>.

For an oriented piecewise smooth surface $S$ with boundary $C$, <Stokes theorem> states
$$
\oint_C\mathbf Q\cdot d\mathbf l=\int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf Q)\cdot d\mathbf S,
$$
where the orientations obey the <right-hand rule> and $\mathbf Q$ is continuously differentiable near $S$. Apply it to $\mathbf Q=\mathbf F\times\mathbf a$. The <scalar triple product> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf a\cdot\oint_Cd\mathbf l\times\mathbf F
&=\oint_C(\mathbf F\times\mathbf a)\cdot d\mathbf l\\
&=\int_S\left[(\mathbf a\cdot\nabla)\mathbf F-\mathbf a(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)\right]\cdot d\mathbf S.
\end{aligned}
$$
The last integrand is $\mathbf a\cdot[(d\mathbf S\times\nabla)\times\mathbf F]$, as the component formula
$$
[(d\mathbf S\times\nabla)\times\mathbf F]_i=dS_j\partial_iF_j-dS_i\partial_jF_j
$$
shows. Since $\mathbf a$ is arbitrary, the <cross-product form of Stokes theorem> follows:
$$
\boxed{\oint_Cd\mathbf l\times\mathbf F=\int_S(d\mathbf S\times\nabla)\times\mathbf F.}
$$
The derivatives in the surface expression act on $\mathbf F$, not on the surface element.

For the specified square, the path is clockwise when viewed from positive $z$, so the compatible <unit normal> is $-\mathbf e_z$. On the first and fourth edges, $d\mathbf l$ and $\mathbf r$ are parallel, giving zero <cross product>. On the second edge, $y=1$ and $x$ runs from $0$ to $1$, so $d\mathbf l\times\mathbf r=\mathbf e_z\,dx$ contributes $\mathbf e_z$. On the third edge, $x=1$ and $y$ runs from $1$ to $0$, so the contribution is $-\mathbf e_z\int_1^0dy=\mathbf e_z$. Thus the <line integral> is $2\mathbf e_z$.

For an independent <surface integral>, $\partial_iF_j=\delta_{ij}$ and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=3$ for the <position vector> field. Its integrand is therefore $d\mathbf S-3d\mathbf S=-2d\mathbf S$. The square has unit area and downward orientation, giving
$$
\boxed{\oint_Cd\mathbf l\times\mathbf r=2\mathbf e_z=\int_S(-2d\mathbf S).}
$$

For a spherically symmetric <number density>, the radial <Laplacian> is $r^{-2}\partial_r(r^2\partial_rn)$. Applying <separation of variables> and choosing the separation constant $-k^2$ gives
$$
\frac{R''+2R'/r}{R}=\frac{T'}T-\lambda=-k^2,
$$
so the <ordinary differential equations> are
$$
\boxed{R''+\frac2rR'+k^2R=0,\qquad T'=(\lambda-k^2)T.}
$$
The radial problem is an <eigenvalue problem> for $-\Delta$ with <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, hence $k^2>0$: multiplying the radial equation by $r^2\overline R$ and integrating gives $k^2\int_0^ar^2|R|^2dr=\int_0^ar^2|R'|^2dr>0$ for a nonzero regular mode vanishing at $a$.

To solve it explicitly, set $R=f/r$, corresponding to the suggested power $p=-1$. The equation reduces to $f''+k^2f=0$, so
$$
R(r)=\frac{A\sin(kr)+B\cos(kr)}r.
$$
Finiteness at the origin forces $B=0$, and the <Dirichlet boundary condition> at $r=a$ requires $k=m\pi/a$ for an integer $m\geq1$. The absence of interior zeros excludes $m\geq2$, selecting the fundamental <normal mode>. If $n_c>0$ denotes the initial central density, the result is
$$
\boxed{n(r,t)=n_c\frac{\sin(\pi r/a)}{\pi r/a}\exp\left[\left(\lambda-\frac{\pi^2}{a^2}\right)t\right],\qquad 0\leq r\leq a,}
$$
where the sine ratio is defined as $1$ at the origin. It is strictly positive in the interior for every finite time. The arbitrary positive scale $n_c$ cannot be fixed without additional initial data.

The spatial factor is time independent, so the density and the total <neutron> population have the same exponential growth rate. Thus the <spherical reaction-diffusion growth threshold> is
$$
\boxed{\lambda>\frac{\pi^2}{a^2}.}
$$
At equality the fundamental mode is stationary; below it the mode decays. For fixed $\lambda>0$, the equivalent critical-radius condition is $a>\pi/\sqrt\lambda$. This is the competition between multiplication in the bulk and <diffusion> loss at the surface in the stated dimensionless model.

= Solution
{parent=3}

The homogeneous equation is a <Cauchy-Euler equation>. Trying $y=x^m$ gives the repeated <indicial root>
$$
m(m-1)+3m+1=(m+1)^2=0.
$$
Hence, for $x>0$,
$$
\boxed{y_h=\frac{C_1+C_2\log x}{x}.}
$$
Both independent solutions diverge at the origin, so no nonzero homogeneous combination satisfies the zero-origin conditions by continuous extension.

For a source point $\xi>0$, finiteness on $0<x<\xi$ forces the <Green function> to vanish on that side. On $x>\xi$, continuity and the unit first-derivative jump require
$$
G(\xi^+,\xi)=0,\qquad G_x(\xi^+,\xi)=1.
$$
Using the homogeneous solutions gives the <causal Green function for a repeated-root Euler operator>
$$
\boxed{G(x,\xi)=\begin{cases}0,&0\leq x<\xi,\\\displaystyle\frac{\xi^2}{x}\log\frac{x}{\xi},&x\geq\xi>0.\end{cases}}
$$
Indeed, the <Wronskian> of $x^{-1}$ and $x^{-1}\log x$ is $x^{-3}$, and direct <differentiation> gives $G_x(\xi^+,\xi)=1$. Since $G$ is continuous, its second derivative produces precisely a unit <Dirac delta>, with no derivative of a delta. For each positive source it is identically zero near the origin, so both required origin values vanish. The differential operator itself is interpreted on $x>0$; no value of its singular coefficients at zero is needed.

Integrating against the forcing yields
$$
y(x)=\int_0^\infty G(x,\xi)\xi\,d\xi=\frac1x\int_0^x\xi^3\log\frac{x}{\xi}\,d\xi=x^3\int_0^1s^3\log\frac1s\,ds.
$$
The last <integral> equals $1/16$, by <integration by parts> or differentiating $\int_0^1s^pds=1/(p+1)$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{x^3}{16}.}
$$
Substitution gives $y''+3y'/x+y/x^2=(6+9+1)x/16=x$, while $y(0)=y'(0)=0$. The divergent homogeneous solutions prove uniqueness in the regular class. No boundary condition at infinity was imposed or required.

= Solution

Complete the square in the <Fourier transform> exponent:
$$
-\lambda x^2-ikx=-\lambda\left(x+\frac{ik}{2\lambda}\right)^2-\frac{k^2}{4\lambda}.
$$
The supplied shifted <Gaussian integral> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\widehat f(k)=\sqrt{\frac\pi\lambda}\exp\left(-\frac{k^2}{4\lambda}\right).}
$$
This is the <Fourier transform of a Gaussian>, with angular-wavenumber convention $e^{-ikx}$.

Take the spatial <Fourier transform> of the <heat equation>. Two <integrations by parts>, with decaying fields and first derivatives at infinity, give $\mathcal F[\psi_{xx}]=-k^2\widetilde\psi$. Thus each mode satisfies the <ordinary differential equation>
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\widetilde\psi(k,t)=-k^2\widetilde\psi(k,t).}
$$
Inserting the transformed <initial condition> and integrating the time equation gives
$$
\boxed{\widetilde\psi(k,t)=\sqrt{\frac\pi\lambda}\exp\left[-\left(t+\frac1{4\lambda}\right)k^2\right].}
$$
Finally set $A=t+1/(4\lambda)>0$. The <inverse Fourier transform>, evaluated by the same Gaussian identity, gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\psi(x,t)&=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac\pi\lambda}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-Ak^2+ikx}dk\\
&=\frac1{2\sqrt{\lambda A}}e^{-x^2/(4A)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Equivalently the <Gaussian initial data for the heat equation> evolve as
$$
\boxed{\psi(x,t)=\frac1{\sqrt{1+4\lambda t}}\exp\left[-\frac{\lambda x^2}{1+4\lambda t}\right],\qquad t>0.}
$$
The profile broadens and its peak decreases, while its total <integral> remains $\sqrt{\pi/\lambda}$, as required by <diffusion> without loss on the whole line. Direct differentiation verifies the equation and the initial limit.

which proves <diagonalizability>.

$$
\boxed{W^{-1}BW=V^{-1}P(P^{-1}AP)P^{-1}V=D.}
$$
Thus <matrix similarity> preserves diagonalizability.

= Solution

For a positive integer $k$, cancellation of $U^\dagger U=I$ gives $A^k=UD^kU^\dagger$. The <trace> is invariant under <matrix similarity>, since $\operatorname{tr}(XY)=\operatorname{tr}(YX)$, and the <determinant> is multiplicative. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{tr}(A^k)=\operatorname{tr}(D^k)=\sum_{j=1}^n\lambda_j^k,\qquad \det(A^k)=\det(D^k)=\prod_{j=1}^n\lambda_j^k.}
$$
This includes zero eigenvalues without requiring $A$ to be invertible.

The convergent <power series> defining the <matrix exponential> gives $e^A=Ue^DU^\dagger$, with $e^D=\operatorname{diag}(e^{\lambda_1},\ldots,e^{\lambda_n})$. Hence the <matrix exponential determinant identity> is

\boxed{\det(e^A)=\prod_{j=1}^ne^{\lambda_j}=e^{\sum_j\lambda_j}=e^{\operatorname{tr}A}.}

The diagonalization proof uses the given Hermitian hypothesis; the identity itself holds for every square real or complex matrix.

For $\operatorname{Re}z>0$, the defining <Gamma integral> converges at zero and at infinity and defines a <holomorphic function>. The <Gamma function recurrence> then supplies its <meromorphic continuation> to the rest of the complex plane, as used below.

= a
{parent=ii}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=a}

For $\operatorname{Re}z>0$, <integration by parts> gives
$$
\Gamma(z+1)=\int_0^\infty t^ze^{-t}dt=\left[-t^ze^{-t}\right]_0^\infty+z\int_0^\infty t^{z-1}e^{-t}dt.
$$
The boundary term vanishes because $t^{\operatorname{Re}z}\to0$ at zero and exponential decay dominates any fixed power at infinity. Thus
$$
\boxed{\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z),\qquad \Gamma(1)=\int_0^\infty e^{-t}dt=1.}
$$
This also gives $\Gamma(n+1)=n!$ for nonnegative integers, linking the <Gamma function> to the <factorial>.

{parent=ii}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Iterate the <Gamma function recurrence> to obtain, near $z=-m$ for $m\geq0$,
$$
\Gamma(z)=\frac{\Gamma(z+m+1)}{z(z+1)\cdots(z+m)}.
$$
The numerator is holomorphic and equals $\Gamma(1)=1$ at $z=-m$. Exactly one denominator factor, $z+m$, vanishes; the product of the remaining factors there is $(-m)(-m+1)\cdots(-1)=(-1)^m m!$. Thus there is a <simple pole>, with <Gamma function residue at a nonpositive integer>
$$
\boxed{\operatorname*{Res}_{z=-m}\Gamma(z)=\lim_{z\to-m}(z+m)\Gamma(z)=\frac{(-1)^m}{m!}.}
$$
For $m=0$ the empty product is one. The same recurrence with a sufficiently large shift defines the continuation at every point away from these integers, so no further poles occur. At the poles, the recurrence is an identity of <meromorphic functions>, rather than an equality of finite values.

Insert the <power series> and equate the coefficient of $x^n$. In the <Legendre differential equation> this gives
$$
(n+2)(n+1)a_{n+2}+[\ell(\ell+1)-n(n+1)]a_n=0.

Hence the <recurrence relation> is
$$
\boxed{a_{n+2}=\frac{(n-\ell)(n+\ell+1)}{(n+1)(n+2)}a_n,\qquad n\geq0.}
$$
The free coefficients $a_0,a_1$ generate the independent even and odd series, respectively. They converge at least for $|x|<1$, up to the nearest singular points of the equation.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For a nonnegative integer $\ell$, the chain of the same parity as $\ell$ reaches $n=\ell$, where the numerator of the <recurrence relation> vanishes. The next coefficient and every later coefficient in that chain are zero, producing a polynomial of degree $\ell$. Normalizing it at $x=1$ gives the <Legendre polynomial> $P_\ell$.

The opposite-parity chain never reaches $n=\ell$. Its other possible zero, $n=-\ell-1$, is negative and cannot occur, so a nonzero initial coefficient produces a nonterminating <power series>. It is linearly independent of $P_\ell$ and can be scaled to the <Legendre function of the second kind> $Q_\ell$.

The degree statement requires **$\ell\geq0$**, as the examples in part (c) indicate. For a negative integer $\ell$, the same equation has the nonnegative degree $-\ell-1$, because $\ell(\ell+1)=(-\ell-1)(-\ell)$. A polynomial cannot literally have negative degree.

Let $W=P_\ell Q_\ell'-P_\ell'Q_\ell$. Differentiation cancels the cross terms, and the <Legendre differential equation> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
W'&=P_\ell Q_\ell''-P_\ell''Q_\ell\\
&=\frac{2x}{1-x^2}(P_\ell Q_\ell'-P_\ell'Q_\ell)=\frac{2x}{1-x^2}W.
\end{aligned}

Thus $[(1-x^2)W]'=0$, so
$$
\boxed{W(x)=\frac{A_\ell}{1-x^2},}

with $A_\ell$ independent of $x$ on each interval avoiding the singular endpoints. This is the <Abel identity>. Linear independence forces $A_\ell\ne0$, and its value depends on the chosen normalization of $Q_\ell$.

The integrand $f=n(x)\sqrt{1+(y')^2}$ has no explicit dependence on $y$, so part (i) gives

For the displayed form take $c>0$ and $1+\beta x\geq c$. Substitute $s=(1+\beta x)/c$ in the <first integral>:
$$
y-y_0=\frac c\beta\int_1^{(1+\beta x)/c}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{s^2-1}}=\frac c\beta\operatorname{arcosh}\frac{1+\beta x}{c}.

\boxed{x(y)=-\frac1\beta+\frac c\beta\cosh\left[\frac\beta c(y-y_0)\right].}

The printed expression presupposes $c>0$. The separate case $c=0$ gives $y$ constant, a vertical ray. At the turning point $dy/dx$ diverges, so $y(x)$ is not a single regular graph across it, although $x(y)$ is smooth.

x'(y)=\sinh\left[\frac\beta c(y-y_0)\right],\qquad x''(y)=\frac\beta c\cosh\left[\frac\beta c(y-y_0)\right]>0.
$$
Thus the <light ray> is symmetric about $y=y_0$, descends towards a horizontal tangent at its minimum, and then rises. It never intersects the road. This smooth upward bending in the <refractive index> gradient produces an inferior <mirage>, without reflection at the road.

Write the energy form as $Q[\psi]=\int_\alpha^\beta(p|\psi'|^2+q|\psi|^2)dx$ and the weighted squared norm as $N[\psi]=\int_\alpha^\beta w|\psi|^2dx$. For real coefficients and appropriate homogeneous boundary conditions, the <Sturm-Liouville problem> is the stationary-point equation for their <Rayleigh quotient> $Q/N$.

= Solution
{parent=a}

One suitable choice is homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, $\psi(\alpha)=\psi(\beta)=0$, with variations vanishing there. Assume real $q$ and the stated positive $p,w$. Introduce a <Lagrange multiplier> $\mu$ for $N[\psi]=1$. For real variations, <integration by parts> gives
$$
\delta(Q-\mu N)=2[p\psi'\eta]_\alpha^\beta+2\int_\alpha^\beta[-(p\psi')'+q\psi-\mu w\psi]\eta\,dx.
$$
The boundary term vanishes, so the <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> yields
$$
\boxed{-(p\psi')'+q\psi=\mu w\psi.}
$$
Multiplying this equation by $\psi$, integrating, and using the boundary conditions gives $Q[\psi]=\mu N[\psi]=\mu$. Thus the multiplier, the eigenvalue, and the constrained stationary value coincide. Conversely every normalized eigenfunction makes the first variation vanish for all tangent variations satisfying $\delta N=0$.

Homogeneous <Neumann boundary conditions>, $p\psi'=0$ at both endpoints, or mixed Dirichlet and Neumann conditions also work with this energy form. General <Robin boundary conditions> require the corresponding boundary terms in the form; they cannot simply be inserted into the displayed unmodified integral.

For a regular <self-adjoint> problem with a lowest <eigenvalue> and a complete <orthonormal eigenbasis> in the <weighted inner product>, expand an admissible trial function as $\psi=\sum_jc_j\psi_j$. Then
$$
\frac{Q[\psi]}{N[\psi]}=\frac{\sum_j\lambda_j|c_j|^2}{\sum_j|c_j|^2}\geq\lambda_0.
$$
The <Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle> therefore provides the upper bound
$$
\boxed{\lambda_0\leq\min_{\text{chosen trial family}}\frac{Q[\psi]}{N[\psi]}.}
$$
Choose a family satisfying the essential boundary conditions and finite energy, evaluate the quotient, and minimize over its parameters. Enlarging the trial family can only improve or preserve the bound; equality is attained if it includes an exact ground eigenfunction. For a finite linear trial space, this becomes a generalized matrix eigenvalue problem.

Put $C_a=\sqrt{15/(16a^5)}$, with $a>0$. Direct <integration> confirms the normalization:
$$
\int_{-a}^aC_a^2(a^2-x^2)^2dx=C_a^2\frac{16a^5}{15}=1.
$$
The <kinetic energy> and <potential energy> terms in the dimensionless <Rayleigh quotient> are
$$
K=C_a^2\int_{-a}^a4x^2dx=\frac5{2a^2},\qquad V=C_a^2\int_{-a}^ax^2(a^2-x^2)^2dx=\frac{a^2}{7}.
$$
Thus $\lambda_0\leq5/(2a^2)+a^2/7$. Differentiating with respect to $a$ gives $-5/a^3+2a/7=0$, so the unique minimizing width is $a=(35/2)^{1/4}$. The two energy contributions are equal there, and the <compact parabolic trial bound for the quantum harmonic oscillator> is
$$
\boxed{a_{\rm opt}=\left(\frac{35}{2}\right)^{1/4},\qquad\lambda_0\leq\sqrt{\frac{10}{7}}\simeq1.195229.}
$$
The trial is continuous and zero at $\pm a$, so its weak first derivative is $-2C_ax$ inside and zero outside. The jumps of that derivative do not add delta terms to the first derivative, and its squared norm is finite. The energy-form calculation is therefore valid even though the trial does not belong to the operator domain of the harmonic oscillator. Applying the second-derivative operator naively and omitting its endpoint distributions would be incorrect.

For any nonzero constant $C$, differentiating $\psi_0=Ce^{-x^2/2}$ gives $\psi_0''=(x^2-1)\psi_0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{(-d^2/dx^2+x^2)\psi_0=\psi_0,\qquad\lambda_0=1.}
