= Solution
{parent=1}

Put $\Phi(r,\theta)=R(r)Y(\theta)$ in <Laplace's equation>, multiply by $r^2/(RY)$, and separate the two variables:
$$
\frac{(r^2R')'}R=-\frac{(\sin\theta\,Y')'}{Y\sin\theta}=\lambda.
$$
With $s=\cos\theta$, the angular equation becomes $(1-s^2)Y_{ss}-2sY_s+\lambda Y=0$. Requiring a smooth <axisymmetric harmonic function> at both poles selects $\lambda=n(n+1)$ and the <Legendre polynomial> $Y=P_n(s)$, for nonnegative <integers> $n$. The radial <Cauchy-Euler equation> has solutions $r^n$ and $r^{-n-1}$. <Superposition> over the angular <eigenfunctions> therefore gives
$$
\Phi(r,\theta)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(A_nr^n+B_nr^{-n-1})P_n(\cos\theta),\qquad (1-s^2)P_n''-2sP_n'+n(n+1)P_n=0.
$$
This expansion assumes regularity on each whole angular <sphere>; without it, singular angular solutions would also be possible. The boundary data in the shell involve only the $n=0,1$ modes. Define the <spherical-shell harmonic interpolation> weights
$$
p_0=\frac{a(b-r)}{r(b-a)},\quad q_0=\frac{b(r-a)}{r(b-a)},\qquad p_1=\frac{a^2(b^3-r^3)}{r^2(b^3-a^3)},\quad q_1=\frac{b^2(r^3-a^3)}{r^2(b^3-a^3)}.
$$
Each $p_n$ equals one at the inner boundary and zero at the outer one, and $q_n$ does the reverse. Hence the steady <temperature> is
$$
\boxed{T(r,\theta)=T_0p_0(r)+T_2q_0(r)+[T_1p_1(r)+T_3q_1(r)]\cos\theta.}
$$
Each term is a <harmonic function>, and substitution verifies both prescribed boundary values. Uniqueness follows from the <maximum principle for harmonic functions>.

= Solution
{parent=2}

For $s=|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0|>0$, the radial <Laplacian> gives $\nabla^2(1/s)=s^{-2}\partial_s(s^2\partial_s(1/s))=0$. The outward <flux> of $\nabla[-1/(4\pi s)]$ through a small <sphere> centred at the source is $[1/(4\pi s^2)]4\pi s^2=1$. Applying <Green's second identity> to a smooth <test function> outside that small sphere and shrinking its radius therefore gives the distributional identity
$$
\boxed{\nabla^2\left(-\frac1{4\pi|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0|}\right)=\delta(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0).}
$$
The kernel tends to zero at infinity. Thus it is the <fundamental solution> with the sign convention $\Delta G=+\delta$. The sign will reverse when converting it into the <electrostatic potential> of a positive <point charge> through <Poisson's equation>.

= Solution

Let $\mathbf x_0^-=(x_0,y_0,-z_0)$ be the reflection of the source across the plane. The <method of images> gives the <Dirichlet Green function>
$$
\boxed{G_H(\mathbf x;\mathbf x_0)=-\frac1{4\pi}\left(\frac1{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0|}-\frac1{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0^-|}\right).}
$$
The two source distances agree on $z=0$, so the boundary value vanishes. The reflected source lies outside the upper half-space, leaving only the required <Dirac delta distribution> inside. Both terms vanish at infinity.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Put $r_0=|\mathbf x_0|$ and $\mathbf x_0^*=a^2\mathbf x_0/r_0^2$ for $0<r_0<a$. The <Dirichlet Green function inside a sphere> is
$$
\boxed{G_B(\mathbf x;\mathbf x_0)=-\frac1{4\pi}\left(\frac1{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0|}-\frac{a/r_0}{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0^*|}\right).}
$$
On $|\mathbf x|=a$, the distance identity $|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0^*|=(a/r_0)|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0|$ cancels the two terms. The inverse point lies outside the <sphere>, so it introduces no interior source. This weighted inversion is the <Kelvin transform>. For a central source, take the limit to get $G_B(\mathbf x;0)=-(4\pi)^{-1}(1/|\mathbf x|-1/a)$.

= Grounded hemisphere
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=Grounded hemisphere}

Reflect the source across the equatorial plane and subtract the two ball kernels. This gives the <Dirichlet Green function inside a hemisphere> $G_{\rm hemi}(\mathbf x;\mathbf x_0)=G_B(\mathbf x;\mathbf x_0)-G_B(\mathbf x;\mathbf x_0^-)$. It is zero on the spherical boundary because each ball kernel is zero there, and on the plane because reflection makes their values equal.

For a <point charge> $e$, <Poisson's equation> in SI units is $\Delta V=-e\delta(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0)/\varepsilon_0$, so $V=-eG_{\rm hemi}/\varepsilon_0$. Writing $\mathbf x_0^{*-}$ for the reflected inverse point gives
$$
\boxed{V(\mathbf x)=\frac{e}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac1{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0|}-\frac1{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0^-|}-\frac{a/r_0}{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0^*|}+\frac{a/r_0}{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0^{*-}|}\right].}
$$
The construction uses the real charge, its negative reflected image, a negative inversion image of magnitude $ea/r_0$, and its positive reflected image. Reflection and inversion commute. All three images lie outside the open upper half-ball, and the prescribed zero boundary values determine the <electrostatic potential> uniquely.

= Solution
{parent=3}

At a point of complex <differentiability>, the <complex derivative> is independent of the direction of approach. Taking increments along the real axis gives $f'=u_x+iv_x$; taking imaginary increments gives $f'=v_y-iu_y$. Equality of their <real parts> and <imaginary parts> proves the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> $u_x=v_y$ and $u_y=-v_x$.

For the specified <real part>, observe that $z^2e^z$ has exactly that real part. Equivalently, integrating $v_y=u_x$ and checking $v_x=-u_y$ gives
$$
v=e^x[(x^2-y^2)\sin y+2xy\cos y]+C.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{f(z)=z^2e^z+iC,\qquad C\in\mathbb R.}
$$
The freedom is only a constant imaginary part: the difference of two such <holomorphic functions> has zero real part, so its <Cauchy-Riemann equations> make it constant on each connected domain.

= Solution
{parent=i}

The square-root factor gives an algebraic <branch point> at $z=0$. The logarithm is locally analytic there but has value an odd multiple of $i\pi$, which is nonzero, so it cannot cancel that branching. The zero and pole of $(z-1)/(z+1)$ give logarithmic <branch points> at $z=1$ and $z=-1$, respectively. Thus the finite singularities are
$$
\boxed{z=0,\quad1,\quad-1\quad\text{(all branch points; no finite poles).}}
$$
Infinity is also a <branch point>. On the logarithm branch tending to zero there, $\log[(z-1)/(z+1)]=-2/z+O(z^{-3})$, so the product behaves as $-2z^{-1/2}$. Other logarithm branches add $2\pi ik\sqrt z$ and retain branching. A chosen <branch cut> joins or separates these branch points; its ordinary interior points are not additional branch points of the underlying multivalued <function>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The denominator factors as $(z+2-i)(z+2+i)$. Neither root cancels against the numerator, and both are simple, so the finite singularities are
$$
\boxed{z=-2+i,\quad z=-2-i\quad\text{(simple poles).}}
$$
This is a <rational function>, so it has no <branch points>. At infinity, putting $w=1/z$ gives $w/(1+4w+5w^2)$, analytic at zero; infinity is therefore a <removable singularity> with value zero.

= Solution
{parent=4}

The <residue theorem> states that the <contour integral> of a <meromorphic function> around a positively oriented simple closed contour equals $2\pi i$ times the sum of its enclosed <residues>, provided there are no singularities on the contour. The roots of $1+z^n$ are $z_k=e^{(2k+1)i\pi/n}$, $k=0,\ldots,n-1$. They are simple <poles>, with <residues> $1/(nz_k^{n-1})=-z_k/n$.

Use the sector contour of angle $2\pi/n$, running outward along the positive axis and inward along the rotated ray. It encloses only $z_0=e^{i\pi/n}$. The large-arc contribution is $O(R^{1-n})$, hence vanishes. With $J=\int_0^\infty dx/(1+x^n)$, the two ray contributions give
$$
(1-e^{2\pi i/n})J=-\frac{2\pi i}{n}e^{i\pi/n}.
$$
Since $1-e^{2i\alpha}=-2ie^{i\alpha}\sin\alpha$,
$$
\boxed{J=\frac\pi{n\sin(\pi/n)}.}
$$
For the second integral, use $z^{n/2}/(1+z^n)$ on a branch continuous across the sector away from zero, with a small inner arc of radius $\epsilon$. Its large arc is $O(R^{1-n/2})$ and its small arc is $O(\epsilon^{1+n/2})$, both vanishing for $n\geq3$. On the rotated ray, $z^{n/2}$ changes by $e^{i\pi}=-1$, so the two radial contributions add rather than subtract. The enclosed <residue> is $z_0^{1-n/2}/n=-ie^{i\pi/n}/n$. Consequently
$$
(1+e^{2\pi i/n})I=\frac{2\pi}{n}e^{i\pi/n},\qquad\boxed{I=\frac\pi{n\cos(\pi/n)}}.
$$
Both answers are instances of the <Mellin integral of a reciprocal binomial>. The direct contour argument also tracks the branch needed when $n$ is odd.

= Solution
{parent=i}

The unilateral <Laplace transform> is $\bar f(p)=\int_0^\infty e^{-pt}f(t)dt$ in its <region of absolute convergence of a Laplace transform>. Under the usual inversion hypotheses, the <Bromwich inversion formula> is
$$
f(t)=\frac1{2\pi i}\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{c-iR}^{c+iR}e^{pt}\bar f(p)dp.
$$
The vertical <Bromwich contour> is directed upward, with $c$ inside the transform's convergence half-plane and to the right of all its singularities. At discontinuities the standard symmetric inversion gives the mean of the one-sided values.

For a piecewise continuously <differentiable> $f$ with an integrable transformed <derivative>, <integration by parts> gives the <Laplace transform of a derivative>:
$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-pt}f'(t)dt=\left[e^{-pt}f(t)\right]_0^\infty+p\int_0^\infty e^{-pt}f(t)dt=\boxed{p\bar f(p)-f(0)}.
$$
Here the upper endpoint vanishes by the given decay condition, and right continuity at zero supplies the lower endpoint.

{parent=5}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Set $a_n=1-1/(2n)$, $b_n=1+1/(2n)$. The finite pulse has <Laplace transform> $\bar S_n(p)=n(e^{-pa_n}-e^{-pb_n})/p$. Since $f(0)=0$, transforming the equation gives
$$
\bar f(p)=\frac{n(e^{-pa_n}-e^{-pb_n})}{p(p-1)}.
$$
The <partial fraction decomposition> $1/[p(p-1)]=1/(p-1)-1/p$ has inverse $e^t-1$. By the <Laplace transform time-shift rule>,
$$
f(t)=n[(e^{t-a_n}-1)H(t-a_n)-(e^{t-b_n}-1)H(t-b_n)].
$$
This <finite-pulse response of a first-order linear equation> gives the requested outer regions:
$$
\boxed{f(t)=0\quad(t<a_n),\qquad f(t)=2n\sinh\left(\frac1{2n}\right)e^{t-1}\quad(t>b_n).}
$$
For completeness, while the pulse is active the solution is $n(e^{t-a_n}-1)$. It is continuous at both switching times. After the forcing stops it grows as $e^t$, because the homogeneous equation is $f'=f$. The apparent pole at $p=0$ in the full transform is removable; the <Bromwich contour> may be taken with $c>1$.

= Solution
{parent=6}

A two-index <Cartesian tensor> has components transforming as $T'_{ij}=R_{ik}R_{jl}T_{kl}$ under an orthogonal change of <orthonormal basis>. The tensor order here counts indices, rather than the <matrix rank>. Its <scalar, symmetric-traceless and axial tensor decomposition> follows by setting
$$
Y=\frac{T_{kk}}3,\qquad\Omega_{ij}=\frac{T_{ij}-T_{ji}}2,\qquad S_{ij}=\frac{T_{ij}+T_{ji}}2-Y\delta_{ij}.
$$
Then $T_{ij}=Y\delta_{ij}+\Omega_{ij}+S_{ij}$, with $\Omega$ antisymmetric and $S$ symmetric and traceless. There are **three independent antisymmetric components** and **five independent symmetric traceless components**: three off-diagonal entries and two independent diagonal ones in the latter case.

For the symmetric tensor relative to a nonzero <vector> $A$, put $q=A_iA_i>0$, $s=A_iT_{ij}A_j$, and $t=T_{ii}$. The <symmetric tensor decomposition relative to an axis> gives
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac12\left(t-\frac sq\right),\qquad\beta=\frac{3s/q-t}{2q},\qquad B_i=\frac{T_{ij}A_j}{q}-\frac{sA_i}{q^2}.}
$$
In particular $A_iB_i=s/q-s/q=0$. Define the <orthogonal projection> $P_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-A_iA_j/q$ onto the plane perpendicular to $A$, and set
$$
\boxed{C_{ij}=P_{ik}T_{kl}P_{lj}-\alpha P_{ij}.}
$$
This <tensor> is symmetric and kills $A$. Its <trace> is $t-s/q-2\alpha=0$. To verify the full decomposition, set $u=A/\sqrt q$ and split $T$ into its longitudinal component $(u^TTu)uu^T$, two mixed components, and $PTP$. The mixed terms are exactly $BA^T+AB^T$, and $\alpha+\beta q=s/q$ gives the longitudinal coefficient. Thus all requested constraints and the reconstruction hold.

= Solution
{parent=7}

For a conservative system near a stable equilibrium, the quadratic <Lagrangian> has the form $L=\frac12\dot q^TM\dot q-\frac12q^TKq$, with symmetric <mass matrix> $M$ and <stiffness matrix> $K$. A <normal mode> $q=v e^{i\omega t}$ satisfies the <generalized eigenvalue problem for small oscillations> $(K-\omega^2M)v=0$, so the frequency equation is $\det(K-\omega^2M)=0$.

To quadratic order, vertical <velocities> contribute only fourth-order corrections to the <kinetic energy>, leaving $T=(\dot x^2+\dot y^2+\dot z^2)/2$. A string of length $\ell$ with small horizontal difference $d$ shortens its vertical projection by $d^2/(2\ell)$. The upper segment carries all three masses, the middle segment two, and the lower segment one. Hence their increases in <gravitational potential energy> sum to
$$
V=3g\frac{x^2}{2(3a)}+2g\frac{(y-x)^2}{2(2b)}+g\frac{(z-y)^2}{2c}=\frac g2\left[\frac{x^2}{a}+\frac{(x-y)^2}{b}+\frac{(y-z)^2}{c}\right].
$$
Subtracting $V$ from $T$ gives the stated approximate <Lagrangian>. Here $M=I$ and
$$
K=g\begin{pmatrix}a^{-1}+b^{-1}&-b^{-1}&0\\-b^{-1}&b^{-1}+c^{-1}&-c^{-1}\\0&-c^{-1}&c^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.
$$
For a mode with middle displacement zero, write $v=(X,0,Z)^T$. The middle row requires $X/b+Z/c=0$, so $Z=-cX/b$. A nonzero mode then has $X,Z\ne0$, and the outer rows require $\omega^2/g=a^{-1}+b^{-1}=c^{-1}$. Therefore the necessary and sufficient condition is
$$
\boxed{\frac1a+\frac1b-\frac1c=0.}
$$
When it holds, the <mode shape> is $(1,0,-c/b)$ with $\omega=\sqrt{g/c}$. This is the force-cancellation mechanism of the <stationary middle mass in a three-mass spring chain>, applied to the equivalent quadratic stiffnesses here.

= Solution
{parent=8}

<Lagrange's theorem> states that the <group order> of a finite <group> divides the <group order>; more precisely $|G|=[G:H]|H|$.

If every element squares to the <identity element>, every element is its own <inverse element>, and $ab=(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}=ba$. Thus a <group of exponent two is abelian>. For distinct nonidentity $a,b$, the four elements $1,a,b,ab$ are distinct: for example $ab=1$ would imply $a=b$, and $ab=a$ would imply $b=1$. Products remain in this set because $a,b$ commute and square to one. Thus they form a <Klein four-group> <subgroup> of order four.

Now let $|G|=2p$ with odd <prime number> $p$. By <Lagrange's theorem>, every <element order> is one of $1,2,p,2p$. If an element has order $2p$, its square has order $p$. If no element had order $p$, all nonidentity elements would therefore have order two. There are at least two distinct such elements, producing a subgroup of order four. This contradicts $4\nmid2p$. Hence **there is an element of order $p$**.

= Solution
{parent=9}

A <group homomorphism> satisfies $\varphi(g_1g_2)=\varphi(g_1)\varphi(g_2)$; a bijective one is a <group isomorphism>. Its <kernel of a group homomorphism> is $K=\{g:\varphi(g)=1_H\}$. It is a <subgroup>, and for $k\in K$, $g\in G$,
$$
\varphi(gkg^{-1})=\varphi(g)\varphi(k)\varphi(g)^{-1}=1_H.
$$
Thus $gKg^{-1}\subseteq K$, and applying the same argument to $g^{-1}$ gives equality. Therefore $K$ is a <normal subgroup>.

Write $B_n=\begin{pmatrix}1&n\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. Direct <matrix multiplication> gives $B_nB_m=B_{n+m}$, $B_0=I$ and $B_n^{-1}=B_{-n}$. Consequently these matrices form the <integer shear group>, a <subgroup> of $GL(2,\mathbb R)$. It is an <infinite cyclic group>, generated by $B_1$, since $B_1^n=B_n$ and no nonzero integer power is the identity.

The map $n\mapsto B_n$ is a bijective <group homomorphism> from the additive <group of integers>, proving the required <group isomorphism>. **There are proper subgroups**: for example $\{B_{2n}:n\in\mathbb Z\}$ omits $B_1$. In fact every subgroup is $\{B_{mn}:n\in\mathbb Z\}$ for an integer $m\geq0$, by the classification of <subgroups of an infinite cyclic group>; $m=0$ gives the trivial subgroup and $m=1$ the whole group.

= Solution

A real two-dimensional <group representation> is a <group homomorphism> $D:G\to GL(2,\mathbb R)$, so $D(AB)=D(A)D(B)$ and $D(I)=I$.

Substitute $\tilde x=\alpha x+\beta y$ and $\tilde y=\gamma x+\delta y$ into the quadratic expression. Its coefficients of $x^2$ and $y^2$ are $\alpha^2\hat a+\gamma^2\hat b$ and $\beta^2\hat a+\delta^2\hat b$. The cross term is zero for every matrix in this particular <cyclic group>, because each has one nonzero entry per row. Thus
$$
\boxed{M(A)=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha^2&\gamma^2\\\beta^2&\delta^2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Let $J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ generate the group. For $A=\pm I$, $M=I$; for $A=\pm J$, $M=S:=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$. Since $S^2=I$, each $M$ is its own inverse and $D(J^k)=S^k$. The group law is respected: $S^{k+l}=S^kS^l$, and $S^4=I$ makes it independent of the representative modulo four. This proves that $D=M^{-1}$ is a <group representation>.

This <quarter-turn action on diagonal quadratic forms> has
$$
\boxed{\ker D=\{I,-I\},}
$$
so it is **not faithful**. Quadratic forms cannot distinguish a simultaneous sign reversal of both coordinates. The invariant coefficient directions $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ correspond to the fixed form $x^2+y^2$ and the form $x^2-y^2$ whose sign changes under a quarter-turn.
