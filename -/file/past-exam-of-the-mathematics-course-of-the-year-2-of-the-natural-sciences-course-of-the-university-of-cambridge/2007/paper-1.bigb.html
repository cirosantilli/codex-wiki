= Solution
{parent=i}

Away from the origin, $\hat{\mathbf r}=\nabla r$. Hence the <curl of a gradient> gives $\nabla\times\hat{\mathbf r}=\mathbf0$. Its <divergence> is the <Laplacian> of the radial coordinate, which in <spherical coordinates> is
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot\hat{\mathbf r}=\nabla^2r=\frac1{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}(r^2)=\frac2r,\qquad r>0.}
$$
Equivalently, differentiating $x_i/r$ in <Cartesian coordinates> gives $3/r-r^2/r^3=2/r$.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Use the right-handed orthonormal basis $(\mathbf e_r,\mathbf e_\theta,\mathbf e_\phi)$. The <cross product> with $\mathbf e_r$ rotates tangential components by a right angle; taking its <cross product> again in the opposite order projects a <gradient> onto the tangent plane. The <radial curl of angular gradient fields> then depends only on the rotated-gradient part. All coordinate calculations are made away from the polar coordinate singularities and extend wherever the original fields are smooth.

{parent=ii}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=a}

Since $\hat{\mathbf r}=\mathbf e_r$, the <gradient> formula gives
$$
\mathbf e_r\times\nabla F=-\frac{F_\phi}{r\sin\theta}\mathbf e_\theta+\frac{F_\theta}{r}\mathbf e_\phi.
$$
The <vector triple product identity> gives $(\mathbf e_r\times\nabla G)\times\mathbf e_r=\nabla G-\mathbf e_rG_r=(G_\theta/r)\mathbf e_\theta+[G_\phi/(r\sin\theta)]\mathbf e_\phi$. Adding the radial part yields
$$
\mathbf f=R\mathbf e_r+\frac1r\left(G_\theta-\frac{F_\phi}{\sin\theta}\right)\mathbf e_\theta+\frac1r\left(\frac{G_\phi}{\sin\theta}+F_\theta\right)\mathbf e_\phi.
$$

= b
{parent=ii}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The radial component of the <curl> in <spherical coordinates> is
$$
(\nabla\times\mathbf f)_r=\frac1{r\sin\theta}\left[\partial_\theta(\sin\theta f_\phi)-\partial_\phi f_\theta\right].
$$
It contains no $f_r$, so $R$ cannot contribute. Substituting the tangential components gives
$$
\partial_\theta(\sin\theta f_\phi)-\partial_\phi f_\theta=\frac1r\left[G_{\phi\theta}-G_{\theta\phi}+\partial_\theta(\sin\theta F_\theta)+\frac{F_{\phi\phi}}{\sin\theta}\right].
$$
The mixed <partial derivatives> of smooth $G$ agree, so its terms cancel. Multiplication by $r$, to take the <dot product> with the position <vector> $\mathbf r=r\mathbf e_r$, does not change this independence from $R,G$.

The remaining terms give
$$
\boxed{(\nabla\times\mathbf f)\cdot\mathbf r=\frac1r\Delta_{\theta,\phi}F,\qquad\Delta_{\theta,\phi}=\frac1{\sin\theta}\partial_\theta(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta)+\frac1{\sin^2\theta}\partial_\phi^2.}
$$
This is the <Laplace-Beltrami operator> on the unit <sphere>. Taking the <dot product> with the unit radial <vector> instead would give $r^{-2}\Delta_{\theta,\phi}F$; the extra factor $r$ here comes from the position <vector> in the question.

= Solution
{parent=i}

<Separation of variables> gives $X''=-k^2X$ and $T'=-\kappa k^2T$. The periodic matching of both the <function> and its first <derivative> selects $k=n\pi/L$, with the constant mode at $n=0$. Thus the <periodic diffusion on a circle> solution is
$$
u(x,t)=\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[a_n\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}+b_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right]e^{-\kappa(n\pi/L)^2t},
$$
where the <Fourier coefficients> of the initial <function> are
$$
a_n=\frac1L\int_{-L}^Lu_0(x)\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx,\qquad b_n=\frac1L\int_{-L}^Lu_0(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx.
$$
The formula for $a_n$ also gives $a_0$ at $n=0$. The constant mode preserves the initial spatial <mean>; every nonconstant <Fourier mode> decays. For initial data in <L2 space>, the series approaches those data in <L2 space> as $t\downarrow0$ and is smooth at positive times.

= ii
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The initial <function> is <odd>, so all $a_n$, including $a_0$, vanish. Set $k_n=n\pi/L$. For $k_n\ne1$, product-to-sum and <integration by parts> give
$$
\int_0^L\sin x\sin(k_nx)\,dx=\frac{(-1)^nk_n\sin L}{1-k_n^2},\qquad\int_0^Lx\sin(k_nx)\,dx=-\frac{L(-1)^n}{k_n}.
$$
Consequently the coefficients of the <periodic sine with an endpoint-correcting linear term> are
$$
\boxed{b_n=\frac{2(-1)^n\sin L}{n\pi[1-(n\pi/L)^2]}.}
$$
Insert them into the sine terms of the preceding solution. If $L=m\pi$ for a positive <integer> $m$, the original initial profile is simply $\sin x$, with $k_m=1$. The apparent $0/0$ in the displayed coefficient has removable <limit> $b_m=1$, while $b_n=0$ for $n\ne m$. In that case
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=e^{-\kappa t}\sin x.}
$$

= Solution

Extend $f$ evenly to $F(x)=f(|x|)$ on the whole real line, and use the <Fourier transform> convention $\widehat F(k)=\int_{\mathbb R}F(x)e^{-ikx}dx$. Its odd sine contribution vanishes, giving $\widehat F(k)=2\int_0^\infty f(x)\cos(kx)dx=\widehat f_c(k)$ for $k>0$; the transform is also <even>. The <Fourier inversion theorem> therefore gives, for $x>0$,
$$
f(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat F(k)e^{ikx}dk=\boxed{\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty\widehat f_c(k)\cos(kx)dk}.
$$
This is the inverse <Fourier cosine transform> with the factor-of-two forward convention used here.

= ii
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Take the spatial <Fourier transform> with the same convention. The <partial differential equation> becomes $i\partial_t\widehat f-k^2\widehat f=0$, so $\widehat f(k,t)=e^{-ik^2t}\widehat f_0(k)$. Inverting and substituting the transform of the initial <function> yields the <convolution>
$$
f(x,t)=\int_{\mathbb R}f_0(\xi)K_t(x-\xi)d\xi,\qquad K_t(s)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{iks-itk^2}dk.
$$
Complete the square, $iks-itk^2=is^2/(4t)-it[k-s/(2t)]^2$, and put $\ell=\sqrt t[k-s/(2t)]$. This gives
$$
\boxed{f(x,t)=\frac{c}{2\pi\sqrt t}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_0(\xi)e^{i(x-\xi)^2/(4t)}d\xi,\qquad c=\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-i\ell^2}d\ell=\sqrt\pi e^{-i\pi/4}.}
$$
The oscillatory Gaussian is a <Fresnel integral>, understood as the limit with a positive Gaussian damping factor. That prescription also justifies the transform manipulations, first for rapidly decreasing smooth data and then by <L2 space> extension. The resulting kernel is the <free-particle propagator> in units $\hbar=1$, $m=1/2$; its phase and normalization agree with the multiplier $e^{-ik^2t}$.

= Solution
{parent=4}

The <matrix trace> is $\operatorname{Tr}A=\sum_iA_{ii}$. Expanding the <matrix products> gives $\operatorname{Tr}(AB)=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ji}=\sum_{j,i}B_{ji}A_{ij}=\operatorname{Tr}(BA)$. Thus the <cyclic property of the trace> gives zero <trace> to every <commutator>. If $AB-BA=I$, taking the <trace> would give $0=n$, impossible for positive finite $n$ over the real or complex <field>.

For real $A,B$, let $p(\lambda)=\det(A+\lambda B)$, a real <polynomial> of degree at most $n$. Since $p(i)=\det C\ne0$, it is not the zero <polynomial>. It has at most finitely many real <roots of a polynomial>, so $A+\lambda B$ is an <invertible matrix> for some real $\lambda$, in fact for all but finitely many.

Finally write the invertible intertwiner as $R=A+iB$. The relation $PR=RQ$ and the reality of $P,Q$ give $PA=AQ$ and $PB=BQ$. Choose a real $\lambda$ for which $S=A+\lambda B$ is invertible. Then $PS=SQ$, so $P=SQS^{-1}$. This proves that <complex similarity of real matrices implies real similarity>.

= Solution
{parent=5}

Use the <complex inner product> $\langle a,b\rangle=\sum_j\overline{a_j}b_j=a^\dagger b$, conjugate-linear in its first argument. For $a\ne0$, put $c=\langle a,b\rangle/\langle a,a\rangle$. Positivity gives
$$
0\leq\|b-ca\|^2=\|b\|^2-\frac{|\langle a,b\rangle|^2}{\|a\|^2}.
$$
Hence the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> is $|\langle a,b\rangle|\leq\|a\|\|b\|$. If $a=0$ it is immediate; equality holds precisely when the two <vectors> are <linearly dependent>.

For a <unitary matrix>, $U^\dagger U=I$, so $\|Ua\|^2=a^\dagger U^\dagger Ua=\|a\|^2$. If $Uv=\lambda v$ with a nonzero <eigenvector>, this implies $|\lambda|\|v\|=\|v\|$. Thus every <eigenvalue> satisfies
$$
\boxed{|\lambda|=1.}
$$
For the one-parameter family, expand unitarity to first order: $(I+tA^\dagger+O(t^2))(I+tA+O(t^2))=I$, so $A^\dagger+A=0$. Thus $A$ is a <skew-Hermitian matrix>, an element of the <unitary Lie algebra>. If $Av=\mu v$, then $v^\dagger Av=\mu\|v\|^2$, whereas complex conjugation gives $(v^\dagger Av)^*=v^\dagger A^\dagger v=-v^\dagger Av$. Consequently $\overline\mu=-\mu$, proving that every <eigenvalue> of $A$ is purely imaginary, with zero permitted.

Put $s=z-1$ and seek the leading <Frobenius series> term $y\sim s^\rho$. The coefficients of $y''$ and $y'$ become $-2s(1+s)$ and $2+s$, respectively. The lowest power, $s^{\rho-1}$, therefore has coefficient $-2\rho(\rho-1)+2\rho$. The <indicial equation> is
$$
\boxed{\rho(\rho-2)=0,\qquad\rho=0,2.}
$$
The term $-y$ contributes only at the next order and does not enter this equation.

= ii
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <product rule> gives $\psi'=e^{-x^2/2}(y'-xy)$ and $\psi''=e^{-x^2/2}[y''-2xy'+(x^2-1)y]$. Substituting in the <Schrödinger equation> cancels the $x^2$ and constant terms, leaving the <Hermite differential equation>
$$
y''-2xy'+2\lambda y=0.
$$
For $y=\sum_{n\geq0}a_nx^n$, matching coefficients gives
$$
a_{n+2}=\frac{2(n-\lambda)}{(n+2)(n+1)}a_n.
$$
The <power series solutions of the Hermite differential equation> split into independent <even> and <odd> series. With initial data $(y(0),y'(0))=(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$, respectively, their first three terms are
$$
y_e=1-\lambda x^2+\frac{\lambda(\lambda-2)}6x^4+O(x^6),\qquad y_o=x+\frac{1-\lambda}{3}x^3+\frac{(1-\lambda)(3-\lambda)}{30}x^5+O(x^7).
$$
Each nonterminating parity series has infinite <radius of convergence>: the ratio of successive terms of that parity has magnitude asymptotic to $2|x|^2/n$. Their initial data prove <linear independence>. If a coefficient shown vanishes at a special parameter, the recurrence supplies the terminating series instead.

A nonzero <polynomial> solution of degree $N$ requires $\lambda=N$, by its highest-degree term, and the recurrence then terminates the matching parity. Thus all possible values are $\lambda=0,1,2,\ldots$. Up to nonzero <scalar multiples>, the degree-at-most-three solutions are the <Hermite polynomials>
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c} \lambda&y\\\hline0&1\\1&2x\\2&4x^2-2\\3&8x^3-12x\end{array}.}
$$

= Solution

Multiply the equation for $y_n$ by $y_m$ and the equation for $y_m$ by $y_n$, then subtract. The common $h$ terms cancel, and <integration by parts> gives the <Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator>:
$$
(\lambda_n-\lambda_m)\int_a^bw y_ny_m\,dx=\left[g(y_ny_m'-y_my_n')\right]_a^b.
$$
At each endpoint the two pairs $(y_n',y_n)$ and $(y_m',y_m)$ satisfy the same nontrivial homogeneous linear equation. They therefore lie on the same one-dimensional line, and their determinant vanishes. The boundary expression is zero. Since the <eigenvalues> are distinct, division gives the required <orthogonality of Sturm-Liouville eigenfunctions>:
$$
\boxed{\int_a^b w(x)y_n(x)y_m(x)\,dx=0.}
$$
For complex-valued eigenfunctions of a real self-adjoint problem one may choose real <eigenfunctions>, or use the conjugated version as a <weighted inner product>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Multiplying by $y$ and integrating with the zero endpoint values first gives $\lambda\int_1^by^2dx/x=\int_1^bx(y')^2dx>0$ for a nonzero real <eigenfunction>; hence $\lambda>0$. Using the printed <change of variables> $\xi=\lambda\ln x$, write $y(x)=Y(\xi)$. Then $(xy')'=\lambda^2Y_{\xi\xi}/x$, so $Y_{\xi\xi}+Y/\lambda=0$. The first endpoint gives $Y=A\sin(\xi/\sqrt\lambda)$, and the other gives $\sin(\sqrt\lambda\ln b)=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\lambda_n=\left(\frac{n\pi}{\ln b}\right)^2,\qquad y_n(x)=A_n\sin\left(\frac{n\pi\ln x}{\ln b}\right),\quad n=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
This is the <logarithmic change of variable in a Sturm-Liouville problem>. The measure $dx/x=d(\ln x)$ gives squared <weighted norm> $A_n^2\ln b/2$, so an orthonormal choice is $A_n=\sqrt{2/\ln b}$.

= Solution
{parent=8}

Choose the left homogeneous solution $v(x)=\sinh x$ and the right one $w(x)=\sinh(1-x)$. Their <Wronskian> is $vw'-v'w=-\sinh1$. The <Dirichlet modified Helmholtz Green function on an interval>, with operator sign $D_x^2-1$, is therefore
$$
G(x,\xi)=-\frac{\sinh(x_<)\sinh(1-x_>)}{\sinh1},\qquad x_<=\min(x,\xi),\quad x_>=\max(x,\xi).
$$
It vanishes at both endpoints, is continuous at $x=\xi$, and its first <derivative> jumps by $+1$, giving $(D_x^2-1)G=\delta(x-\xi)$. The <Green-function representation> then gives
$$
u(x)=-\frac{\sinh(1-x)}{\sinh1}\int_0^xe^\xi\sinh\xi\,d\xi-\frac{\sinh x}{\sinh1}\int_x^1e^\xi\sinh(1-\xi)\,d\xi.
$$
The two <integrals> are $(e^{2x}-1)/4-x/2$ and $e(1-x)/2-(e-e^{2x-1})/4$, respectively. Substituting them and simplifying gives
$$
\boxed{u(x)=\frac12\left[xe^x-\frac{e\sinh x}{\sinh1}\right].}
$$
Direct <differentiation> verifies $u''-u=e^x$ and both zero boundary values. The negative kernel also explains why the solution is negative inside the interval despite the positive right-hand side.

= Solution
{parent=9}

Take a variation $y_\varepsilon=y+\varepsilon\eta$ with $\eta(a)=\eta(b)=0$. Differentiating the <functional> at zero and using <integration by parts> gives
$$
\delta F=\int_b^a(f_y\eta+f_{y'}\eta')dx=\left[f_{y'}\eta\right]_b^a+\int_b^a\left(f_y-\frac d{dx}f_{y'}\right)\eta\,dx.
$$
The boundary term vanishes. Since the remaining <integral> must vanish for every smooth endpoint-zero variation, the <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> gives the <Euler-Lagrange equation> $d(f_{y'})/dx=f_y$.

For the mechanical problem put $A(r)=1-2m/r>0$. The <Lagrangian> is independent of $\theta$, a <cyclic coordinate>, so its <conjugate momentum> $\partial L/\partial\dot\theta=2r^2\dot\theta=2J$ is conserved. Therefore $J$ is constant, including the factor of two between $J$ and the canonical momentum for this normalization of $L$.

For the radial coordinate, $\partial L/\partial\dot r=2\dot r/A$ and $\partial L/\partial r=-\dot r^2A'/A^2+2r\dot\theta^2-\mu A'/A^2$. Its <Euler-Lagrange equation> is
$$
\frac{2\ddot r}{A}-\frac{A'\dot r^2}{A^2}-2r\dot\theta^2+\frac{\mu A'}{A^2}=0.
$$
For a <circular orbit> $r=R>2m$, the radial <velocity> and <acceleration> vanish. Using $A'(R)=2m/R^2$ and $\dot\theta=J/R^2$ leaves $2J^2/R^3=2m\mu/[R^2A(R)^2]$, hence
$$
\boxed{J^2=\frac{m\mu R}{(1-2m/R)^2}.}
$$
This is the <circular orbit condition for a radial kinetic Lagrangian> applied to the coefficients here.

= Solution
{parent=10}

For a nonzero real <function>, write $\Lambda=F/G$ with $G=\int_a^bw y^2dx>0$. Multiplying the <Sturm-Liouville equation> by $y$ and integrating gives
$$
\lambda G=\int_a^b\bigl[p(y')^2+qy^2\bigr]dx-\left[pyy'\right]_a^b.
$$
Thus the stated vanishing boundary product proves $\lambda=\Lambda[y]$.

For the variational statement, the <first variation> is
$$
\delta\Lambda=\frac2G\left\{\left[py'\eta\right]_a^b+\int_a^b\left[-(py')'+qy-\Lambda wy\right]\eta\,dx\right\}.
$$
With admissible <boundary conditions> that also make $[py'\eta]_a^b=0$ for every allowed variation, the <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> identifies stationary functions with the <eigenfunctions> and stationary values with their <eigenvalues>. Zero <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, zero <Neumann boundary conditions>, and matched periodic data give familiar examples. The single printed condition $[pyy']_a^b=0$ proves the quotient identity but, by itself, does not specify the admissible variations needed for stationarity; the <boundary conditions for variational Rayleigh quotients> make this distinction explicit.

For the half-line equation, multiplication by $x^{n-1}$ gives $p=w=x^{n-1}$ and $q=x^{n+1}$. Use the regular radial boundary condition at zero and square-integrable decay at infinity, under which the proposed Gaussian is admissible. With $y=e^{ax^2}$, $a<0$, $y'=2axy$, so
$$
\Lambda(a)=(4a^2+1)\frac{\int_0^\infty x^{n+1}e^{2ax^2}dx}{\int_0^\infty x^{n-1}e^{2ax^2}dx}=-n\left(a+\frac1{4a}\right).
$$
The ratio of the two <Gaussian integrals> is $n/(-4a)$, from a <change of variables> and the <Gamma function recurrence>. Differentiating gives $\Lambda'(a)=-n[1-1/(4a^2)]$, so the minimum on $a<0$ occurs at $a=-1/2$. The <Gaussian variational bound for a radial harmonic oscillator> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\lambda_0\leq n,\qquad y_{\rm trial}=e^{-x^2/2}.}
$$
Here the trial actually satisfies the <differential equation> with $\lambda=n$. Moreover, on the same regular decaying domain, <integration by parts> gives $F[y]-nG[y]=\int_0^\infty x^{n-1}(y'+xy)^2dx\geq0$. Hence the estimate is exact, with $\boxed{\lambda_0=n}$. For $n=1$, regular radial data mean the <Neumann boundary condition> $y'(0)=0$; imposing a zero <Dirichlet boundary condition> there would exclude this trial and give a different problem.
