= Solution
{parent=a}

For a continuously differentiable <vector field> $\mathbf u$ on a bounded region $V\subset\mathbb R^3$ with piecewise smooth closed boundary $S=\partial V$, the <divergence theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\int_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf u\,dV=\int_S\mathbf u\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,dS=\int_S\mathbf u\cdot d\mathbf S.}
$$
Here $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u$ is its <divergence>, $dV$ is the volume element, $dS$ is the surface area element, $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is the outward <unit normal>, and $d\mathbf S=\hat{\mathbf n}\,dS$. Thus a <volume integral> of the <divergence> equals the outward <flux> through the boundary.

= b
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Since $\mathbf a$ is constant, the <product rule> gives $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf a\psi)=\mathbf a\cdot\nabla\psi$. Applying the <divergence theorem> therefore gives
$$
\mathbf a\cdot\int_V\nabla\psi\,dV=\int_S\psi\,\mathbf a\cdot d\mathbf S=\mathbf a\cdot\int_S\psi\,d\mathbf S.
$$
This holds for every constant <vector> $\mathbf a$, so each component of the difference vanishes. Consequently the <gradient> has the boundary representation
$$
\boxed{\int_V\nabla\psi\,dV=\int_S\psi\,d\mathbf S.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=c}

The <divergence of a cross product> and $\nabla\times\mathbf a=0$ give $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf A)=-\mathbf a\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf A)$. On the boundary the <scalar triple product> gives $(\mathbf a\times\mathbf A)\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}=-\mathbf a\cdot(\hat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf A)$. Thus the <divergence theorem> gives
$$
-\mathbf a\cdot\int_V\nabla\times\mathbf A\,dV=-\mathbf a\cdot\int_S\hat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf A\,dS.
$$
Because $\mathbf a$ is arbitrary, the <curl> identity follows:
$$
\boxed{\int_V\nabla\times\mathbf A\,dV=\int_S\hat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf A\,dS.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=d}

Use <Einstein summation convention> and write $\partial_j=\nabla_j$. Since $\partial_jr_i=\delta_{ij}$, where $\delta_{ij}$ is the <Kronecker delta>, the <product rule> gives
$$
\partial_j(r_iw_j)=\delta_{ij}w_j+r_i\partial_jw_j=w_i+P_i.
$$
This proves $\boxed{P_i=\partial_j(r_iw_j)-w_i}$. Now take $w_j=\partial_j\psi$. Integrating this identity and applying the <divergence theorem> to the first term gives
$$
\int_Vr_i\nabla^2\psi\,dV=\int_Sr_i\partial_j\psi\,dS_j-\int_V\partial_i\psi\,dV.
$$
Part (b) changes the last <volume integral> of the <gradient> into a <surface integral>, yielding
$$
\boxed{\int_Vr_i\nabla^2\psi\,dV=\int_Sr_i\partial_j\psi\,dS_j-\int_S\psi\,dS_i.}
$$
Here $dS_i=\hat n_i\,dS$; the <Laplacian> is $\nabla^2\psi=\partial_j\partial_j\psi$.

Apply the <product rule> to the <tensor field>, without assuming any symmetry of $\Sigma$:
$$
\partial_j(r_i\partial_k\Sigma_{kj})=\delta_{ij}\partial_k\Sigma_{kj}+r_i\partial_j\partial_k\Sigma_{kj}=\partial_k\Sigma_{ki}+r_i\partial_j\partial_k\Sigma_{kj}.
$$
Integrating, the <divergence theorem> converts each of the two <divergences> into a boundary term:
$$
\boxed{\int_Vr_i\partial_j\partial_k\Sigma_{kj}\,dV=\int_Sr_i\partial_k\Sigma_{kj}\,dS_j-\int_S\Sigma_{ki}\,dS_k.}
$$
The repeated indices are summed according to <Einstein summation convention>, while $i$ labels the component of the resulting <vector> identity.

= Solution

Choose the symmetric <tensor field>
$$
\Sigma_{kj}=(\partial_k\psi)(\partial_j\psi)-\frac12\delta_{kj}|\nabla\psi|^2.
$$
The <product rule> and equality of mixed <partial derivatives> give
$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_k\Sigma_{kj}
&=(\nabla^2\psi)\partial_j\psi+(\partial_k\psi)\partial_k\partial_j\psi-\frac12\partial_j|\nabla\psi|^2\\
&=(\nabla^2\psi)\partial_j\psi.
\end{aligned}
$$
This is the <Euclidean scalar-gradient stress identity>. Substitute it into part (e), and use $\Sigma_{ki}\hat n_k=(\partial_n\psi)\partial_i\psi-\tfrac12|\nabla\psi|^2\hat n_i$, where $\partial_n\psi=\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\nabla\psi$. Reassembling the components gives the required pure <surface integral>:
$$
\boxed{\int_V\mathbf r\,\nabla\cdot[(\nabla^2\psi)\nabla\psi]\,dV=\int_S\left[\mathbf r(\nabla^2\psi)\partial_n\psi-(\partial_n\psi)\nabla\psi+\frac12|\nabla\psi|^2\hat{\mathbf n}\right]dS.}
$$
Sufficient smoothness of $\psi$ is understood for the displayed <derivatives> and applications of the <divergence theorem>.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Apply <separation of variables> with $\rho(x,t)=X(x)T(t)$ and temporal factor $T=e^{-\lambda t}$. The spatial <eigenvalue> equation is
$$
-DX''-\gamma X=\lambda X,\qquad X(0)=X(L)=0.
$$
The nontrivial solutions of these <Dirichlet boundary conditions> are sine <eigenfunctions>. Normalizing in the usual <inner product> on $(0,L)$ gives
$$
\boxed{\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac2L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\qquad\lambda_n=D\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2-\gamma,\qquad n=1,2,\ldots,}
$$
with $\int_0^L\psi_n\psi_m\,dx=\delta_{nm}$. The <superposition principle> and the <Fourier sine series> then give
$$
\boxed{\rho(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n\sqrt{\frac2L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\exp\left[\left(\gamma-\frac{Dn^2\pi^2}{L^2}\right)t\right].}
$$
The constants are the initial-data coefficients $a_n=\int_0^L\rho(x,0)\psi_n(x)\,dx$. Here physical diffusion means $D>0$.

= b
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

By <orthogonality> and the sampling property of the <Dirac delta distribution>,
$$
a_n=\rho_0\int_0^L\delta(x-x_0)\psi_n(x)\,dx=\boxed{\rho_0\sqrt{\frac2L}\sin\frac{n\pi x_0}{L}}.
$$
Consequently the <reaction-diffusion equation> solution is
$$
\boxed{\rho(x,t)=\frac{2\rho_0}{L}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sin\frac{n\pi x_0}{L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\exp\left[\left(\gamma-\frac{Dn^2\pi^2}{L^2}\right)t\right].}
$$
For every $t>0$ this <eigenfunction expansion> is smoothed by the Gaussian decay in $n$. As $t\downarrow0$, it recovers the specified initial condition in the sense of <distributions>.

= c
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The smallest decay-rate <eigenvalue> is $\lambda_1=D\pi^2/L^2-\gamma$, so the threshold is
$$
\boxed{\gamma_c(D,L)=\frac{D\pi^2}{L^2}.}
$$
Because $0<x_0<L$, $a_1>0$. For every fixed interior point $0<x<L$, the first sine <eigenfunction> is positive and the remaining terms have strictly faster decay relative to it. Hence
$$
\rho(x,t)\sim\frac{2\rho_0}{L}\sin\frac{\pi x_0}{L}\sin\frac{\pi x}{L}\,e^{(\gamma-\gamma_c)t}.
$$
One can justify the relative-error limit directly: after factoring out the first exponential, the absolute remainder is bounded by $(2\rho_0/L)\sum_{n\geq2}e^{-D\pi^2(n^2-1)t/L^2}$, which tends to zero. Thus $\boxed{\rho\to0\text{ if }\gamma<\gamma_c}$, and $\boxed{\rho(x,t)\to+\infty\text{ for each interior }x\text{ if }\gamma>\gamma_c}$. The boundary values stay zero. At equality the first term is stationary and the density approaches its positive sine profile.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Let $f(x)=\rho(x,0)$. The first normalized <Fourier sine series> coefficient is
$$
a_1=\sqrt{\frac2L}\int_0^Lf(x)\sin\frac{\pi x}{L}\,dx>0.
$$
Strict positivity follows because $f\geq0$, has positive total mass, and $\sin(\pi x/L)>0$ everywhere inside the interval. In particular, there is no cancellation that could remove the slowest term. This is the <positivity of the first Dirichlet reaction-diffusion coefficient>.

For finite initial mass $M=\int_0^Lf(x)\,dx$, all coefficients satisfy $|a_n|\leq\sqrt{2/L}\,M$. The <eigenfunction expansion> therefore gives the uniform bound
$$
\left|e^{\lambda_1t}\rho(x,t)-a_1\psi_1(x)\right|\leq\frac{2M}{L}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}e^{-D\pi^2(n^2-1)t/L^2}\longrightarrow0.
$$
The positive first profile thus controls the long-time behavior. We recover exactly the threshold $\boxed{\gamma_c=D\pi^2/L^2}$, with decay below it and unbounded positive growth at every interior point above it. The finite-mass condition is the usual meaning of an initial density profile here; standard positive-time smoothing gives the same conclusion in the usual broader admissible data classes.

For an exponential trial $y=e^{rx}$, the <characteristic equation> is
$$
r^2+2ar+a^2+b^2=(r+a)^2+b^2=0.
$$
Its distinct complex <roots> are $r=-a\pm ib$. Thus the general real solution of the homogeneous <second-order linear differential equation> is
$$
\boxed{y_h(x)=e^{-ax}[C_1\cos(bx)+C_2\sin(bx)].}
$$
The two displayed solutions have nonzero <Wronskian> $be^{-2ax}$ and hence <linear independence>. The same formula permits complex solutions when $C_1,C_2$ are complex.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For the zero-data <initial value problem>, choose a <causal Green function> satisfying $L_xG(x,\xi)=\delta(x-\xi)$ and $G=0$ for $x<\xi$. Continuity at $x=\xi$ prevents a derivative of the <Dirac delta distribution>, while integration across the source gives
$$
G(\xi^+,\xi)=0,\qquad\partial_xG(\xi^+,\xi)-\partial_xG(\xi^-,\xi)=1.
$$
For $x>\xi$, part (i) gives the shifted homogeneous solutions. The zero value at the join removes the cosine term, and the unit <derivative> jump fixes the sine coefficient. Therefore the <causal Green function of a damped oscillator> is
$$
\boxed{G(x,\xi)=\begin{cases}\dfrac1b e^{-a(x-\xi)}\sin[b(x-\xi)],&x\geq\xi,\\0,&x<\xi.\end{cases}}
$$
Equivalently, multiply the first expression by the <Heaviside step function> $H(x-\xi)$. The required solution is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\int_0^{\infty}G(x,\xi)g(\xi)\,d\xi=\frac1b\int_0^xe^{-a(x-\xi)}\sin[b(x-\xi)]g(\xi)\,d\xi.}
$$
Its initial value and first <derivative> vanish: the interval has zero length at $x=0$ and the kernel vanishes at its upper limit. Differentiating twice shows that the upper-limit contribution to $y''$ is precisely $g(x)$, while the remaining homogeneous kernel terms cancel in $Ly$.

= Solution

The original PDF has the absolute-value hypothesis $|g(\xi)|<Ce^{-s\xi}$, which is missing from the local OCR. From the <Green's function> formula and $|\sin|\leq1$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
|y(x)|&\leq\frac{C}{b}\int_0^xe^{-a(x-\xi)}e^{-s\xi}\,d\xi\\
&=\frac{C}{b}e^{-ax}\int_0^xe^{-(s-a)\xi}\,d\xi\\
&=\frac{C}{b(s-a)}e^{-ax}[1-e^{-(s-a)x}]\leq\frac{C}{b(s-a)}e^{-ax}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Because $s>a>0$, the upper bound tends to zero. The <squeeze theorem> proves $\boxed{y(x)\to0\text{ as }x\to\infty}$.

With the paper's <Fourier transform> convention, the <Fourier inversion theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{ikx}\widetilde f(k)\,dk.}

This holds at continuity points under the usual inversion hypotheses; at a jump with one-sided limits it gives their mean. The factor $1/(2\pi)$ and the sign change in the exponential are part of this normalization.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Insert the inverse <Fourier transforms> of $f$ and its complex conjugate, initially under enough decay to justify interchanging <integrals>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\mathbb R}|f(x)|^2\,dx
&=\frac1{(2\pi)^2}\int_{\mathbb R}\int_{\mathbb R}\widetilde f(k)\overline{\widetilde f(\ell)}\left[\int_{\mathbb R}e^{i(k-\ell)x}\,dx\right]dk\,d\ell\\
&=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}\int_{\mathbb R}\widetilde f(k)\overline{\widetilde f(\ell)}\delta(k-\ell)\,dk\,d\ell\\
&=\boxed{\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}|\widetilde f(k)|^2\,dk}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The middle step uses the stated <Dirac delta distribution> identity, and the last uses its sampling property. This is the <Parseval identity> for the chosen normalization. Approximation extends the result to <square-integrable functions>, including the function used below.

The real <odd function> has a purely imaginary odd <Fourier transform>. Setting $A=\pi/2$ and using a product-to-sum identity,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde f(k)&=-2i\int_0^A\sin x\sin(kx)\,dx\\
&=-i\left[\frac{\sin[(k-1)A]}{k-1}-\frac{\sin[(k+1)A]}{k+1}\right]\\
&=\boxed{\frac{2ik\cos(\pi k/2)}{k^2-1}},\qquad k\ne\pm1.
\end{aligned}
$$
The apparent singularities are removable. Direct evaluation or limits give
$$
\boxed{\widetilde f(1)=-\frac{i\pi}{2},\qquad\widetilde f(-1)=\frac{i\pi}{2},\qquad\widetilde f(0)=0.}
$$
In particular, the transform stays finite where its numerator and denominator both vanish.

= Solution

The squared <norm> of the original function is
$$
\int_{\mathbb R}|f(x)|^2\,dx=\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\sin^2x\,dx=\frac\pi2.
$$
Applying the <Parseval identity> to part (c), using the evenness of the squared transform, gives
$$
\frac\pi2=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}\frac{4k^2\cos^2(\pi k/2)}{(k^2-1)^2}\,dk=\frac4\pi\int_0^{\infty}\frac{k^2\cos^2(\pi k/2)}{(k^2-1)^2}\,dk.
$$
Thus the last <integral> equals $\pi^2/8$. Now set $u=\pi k/2$. Since
$$
\frac{k^2\,dk}{(k^2-1)^2}=\frac\pi2\frac{u^2\,du}{(u^2-\pi^2/4)^2},
$$
we obtain
$$
\boxed{\int_0^{\infty}\frac{u^2\cos^2u}{(u^2-\pi^2/4)^2}\,du=\frac2\pi\frac{\pi^2}{8}=\frac\pi4.}
$$
The apparent singularity at $u=\pi/2$ is removable: the continuous value of the integrand there is $1/4$. No principal-value interpretation is needed.

Let $Av=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$. Taking the <Hermitian inner product> with $v$ gives $v^\dagger Av=\lambda\|v\|^2$. For an <anti-Hermitian matrix>,
$$
\overline{v^\dagger Av}=v^\dagger A^\dagger v=-v^\dagger Av.

Consequently $\overline\lambda\|v\|^2=-\lambda\|v\|^2$. As $\|v\|^2>0$, the <eigenvalue> satisfies $\boxed{\overline\lambda=-\lambda}$, or $\boxed{\lambda=i\theta\text{ with }\theta\in\mathbb R}$. Zero is included as a purely imaginary <eigenvalue>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Taking the <conjugate transpose> term by term in the convergent <matrix exponential> gives
$$
U^\dagger=e^{A^\dagger}=e^{-A}.
$$
The <matrices> $A$ and $-A$ <commute>, so the <matrix exponential> product law gives $U^\dagger U=e^{-A}e^A=I$ and likewise $UU^\dagger=I$. Hence $\boxed{U\text{ is unitary}}$.

For $V$, use the inverse and product rules for the <conjugate transpose>:
$$
V^\dagger=[(I+A)^{-1}]^\dagger(I-A)^\dagger=(I-A)^{-1}(I+A).
$$
The factors $I\pm A$ <commute>, as do their inverses, since they are expressions in the same <matrix>. Consequently
$$
V^\dagger V=(I-A)^{-1}(I+A)(I-A)(I+A)^{-1}=I,
$$
and similarly $VV^\dagger=I$. Therefore $\boxed{V\text{ is unitary}}$. This rational construction is the skew-Hermitian form of the <Cayley transform of a Hermitian matrix>, obtained by writing $A=iH$ with $H$ a <Hermitian matrix>.

Choose a real unit <eigenvector> $e_1$ of the real <symmetric matrix> $Q$, with <eigenvalue> $\lambda_1$. Its <orthogonal complement> is an <invariant subspace>: for any $v$ with $e_1^Tv=0$,
$$
e_1^TQv=(Qe_1)^Tv=\lambda_1e_1^Tv=0.
$$
The restriction of $Q$ to this $(n-1)$-dimensional space is again real and symmetric. Applying the same argument inductively produces an <orthonormal basis> $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ of real <eigenvectors>. The base case $n=1$ is immediate. This proves the <spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices> without assuming that distinct <eigenvalues> are available.

One can also check that <eigenvectors> with different <eigenvalues> are automatically orthogonal: $\lambda_i e_i^Te_j=(Qe_i)^Te_j=e_i^TQe_j=\lambda_j e_i^Te_j$. Within a repeated <eigenspace>, the <Gram-Schmidt process> preserves the eigenvector property. Thus repeated <eigenvalues> cause no obstruction, and $\boxed{Q=ODO^T\text{ for an orthogonal }O\text{ and real diagonal }D}$.

Write $z=x+iy$ and square the <modulus> equation. It becomes
$$
x^2+(y+b)^2=P^2[x^2+(y-b)^2].
$$
For $P\ne1$, completing the square gives the <Apollonius circle>
$$
x^2+\left(y-\frac{b(P^2+1)}{P^2-1}\right)^2=\frac{4b^2P^2}{(P^2-1)^2}.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{center }\left(0,\frac{b(P^2+1)}{P^2-1}\right),\qquad\text{radius }\frac{2bP}{|P^2-1|}.}
$$
The printed statement needs the exception $\boxed{P=1\ \Longrightarrow\ y=0}$: equal distances from $ib$ and $-ib$ give their perpendicular bisector, a straight line rather than a finite-radius <circle>. For $P>1$ the center lies in the upper half-plane; this is the case needed in part (ii). The original PDF confirms the numerator $z+ib$, correcting the duplicated, corrupted OCR formula.

Subtracting their squares yields $\boxed{b=\sqrt{y_0^2-R^2}}$. Also
$$
\frac{y_0}{R}=\frac{P+P^{-1}}2=\cosh(\log P),\qquad\log P=\operatorname{arcosh}(y_0/R).
$$
The relevant <inverse hyperbolic cosine> is positive. Now $\log|(z+ib)/(z-ib)|$ is the <real part> of a local <holomorphic logarithm>, hence a <harmonic function> away from $z=\pm ib$. The point $ib$ is inside the excluded disk, because $0<y_0-b<R$, while $-ib$ is below the real axis. Thus neither singularity lies in the physical domain.

On $y=0$ the numerator and denominator have equal <modulus>, so the logarithm is zero. On the circular boundary its value is $\log P=\operatorname{arcosh}(y_0/R)$. Therefore the <harmonic potential between a grounded line and a circular boundary> is
$$
\boxed{V(x,y)=\frac{V_0}{\operatorname{arcosh}(y_0/R)}\log\left|\frac{x+iy+i\sqrt{y_0^2-R^2}}{x+iy-i\sqrt{y_0^2-R^2}}\right|.}
$$
This verifies <Laplace's equation> and both prescribed <Dirichlet boundary conditions>. It also tends to zero at infinity. A uniqueness assertion on this unbounded domain would require an additional boundedness or infinity condition; the displayed function is the natural decaying solution requested.

Iterating the <Gamma function recurrence> gives a <meromorphic continuation>

For fixed initial and final coordinates, the two <Euler-Lagrange equations> are

Use the standard regular <Sturm-Liouville problem> hypotheses: positive weight $w$, real coefficients, and real <self-adjoint> <boundary conditions>. The positive weight and boundary-domain assumptions are implicit in the requested real <orthonormal basis>; real differential coefficients alone would not guarantee them for arbitrary complex boundary conditions.

Multiply the <eigenfunction> equation by $\overline{y_n}$ and integrate. By <self-adjointness>, the number $\int_a^b\overline{y_n}\mathcal Ly_n\,dx$ is real, whereas
$$
\int_a^b\overline{y_n}\mathcal Ly_n\,dx=\lambda_n\int_a^bw|y_n|^2dx.
$$
The second <integral> is strictly positive, so $\boxed{\lambda_n\in\mathbb R}$. Because the coefficients and <boundary conditions> are real, the real and imaginary parts of any complex <eigenfunction> separately obey the same equation and conditions. At least one is nonzero, so a real <eigenfunction> can be chosen. More generally, each <eigenspace> has a real basis, which can be orthonormalized using the <weighted inner product>.

The <orthogonality of Sturm-Liouville eigenfunctions>, with normalization chosen to one, is
$$
\boxed{\int_a^bw(x)\overline{y_m(x)}y_n(x)\,dx=\delta_{mn}.}
$$
For the real choice, omit the complex conjugate.

The <Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion> of a suitable real function $f$ is
$$
f(x)=\sum_ny_n(x)\int_a^bw(\xi)y_n(\xi)f(\xi)\,d\xi=\int_a^b\left[w(\xi)\sum_ny_n(\xi)y_n(x)\right]f(\xi)\,d\xi.
$$
Comparing this identity with the sampling property of the <Dirac delta distribution> gives the completeness kernel
$$
\boxed{w(\xi)\sum_ny_n(\xi)y_n(x)=\delta(x-\xi).}
$$
This is an identity of <distributions>, rather than a pointwise-convergent series of ordinary functions.

Assuming zero is not an <eigenvalue>, define the <spectral Green function of a regular Sturm-Liouville problem> by
$$
\boxed{G(x;\xi)=\sum_n\frac{y_n(x)y_n(\xi)}{\lambda_n}.}
$$
Applying $\mathcal L_x$ gives $w(x)\sum_ny_n(x)y_n(\xi)=\delta(x-\xi)$: multiplying the preceding completeness kernel by $w(x)/w(\xi)$ leaves the <Dirac delta distribution> unchanged. Each term also obeys the same homogeneous <boundary conditions>. There is no extra weight factor in $G$ for the ordinary source measure $d\xi$.

If zero is an <eigenvalue>, the inverse <Green's function> does not exist on all forcing functions: the forcing must obey the <solvability condition at a Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue>. Omitting the zero mode instead constructs a reduced inverse, with its projection removed from the right-hand side. This exception occurs in part (c) at $\alpha=1$.

Write the endpoint condition in its linear <Robin boundary condition> form $y'(1)=y(1)/\alpha$. For a positive <eigenvalue> $\lambda=q^2$, the left <Dirichlet boundary condition> gives $y=C\sin(qx)$, and the right condition gives
$$
q\cos q=\frac1\alpha\sin q,\qquad\boxed{\tan q=\alpha q.}
$$
For each positive root $q_n$, the squared <norm> is
$$
N_n=\int_0^1\sin^2(q_nx)\,dx=\frac12-\frac{\sin(2q_n)}{4q_n}.
$$
Consequently the sine coefficients in any complete expansion are
$$
\boxed{a_n=\frac{\int_0^1f(x)\sin(q_nx)\,dx}{N_n}=\frac{2\int_0^1f(x)\sin(q_nx)\,dx}{1-\sin(2q_n)/(2q_n)}.}
$$
When $\alpha>1$, these are all modes and the <Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion> gives exactly the stated sine series, with the usual convergence interpretation. Positivity of all <eigenvalues> follows from <integration by parts> and the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>:
$$
\int_0^1y(-y'')dx=\int_0^1(y')^2dx-\frac{y(1)^2}{\alpha}\geq\left(1-\frac1\alpha\right)\int_0^1(y')^2dx>0
$$
for a nonzero admissible $y$, since $y(1)=\int_0^1y'\,dx$.

However, the original PDF states only $\alpha>0$, and with that range its sine-only completeness claim is false. The missing modes are as follows. At $\alpha=1$, $\lambda=0$ admits $y=x$, so the correct expansion is
$$
\boxed{f(x)=3x\int_0^1t f(t)\,dt+\sum_{n\geq1}a_n\sin(q_nx),\qquad\alpha=1.}
$$
For $0<\alpha<1$, a negative <eigenvalue> $\lambda=-\kappa^2$ admits $y=\sinh(\kappa x)$, with
$$
\boxed{\tanh\kappa=\alpha\kappa,\qquad\kappa>0.}
$$
There is exactly one positive root: $\tanh\kappa/\kappa$ decreases strictly from $1$ to zero. For example, its <derivative> has the sign of $\kappa\operatorname{sech}^2\kappa-\tanh\kappa<0$, because $\sinh\kappa\cosh\kappa>\kappa$. Thus the corrected expansion is
$$
\boxed{f(x)=a_-\sinh(\kappa x)+\sum_{n\geq1}a_n\sin(q_nx),\quad a_-=\frac{\int_0^1f(t)\sinh(\kappa t)\,dt}{\sinh(2\kappa)/(4\kappa)-1/2},\quad0<\alpha<1.}
$$
These are the <nonpositive modes in a Dirichlet-Robin interval expansion>. As an explicit counterexample to the printed claim, at $\alpha=1$ take $f(x)=x$: it satisfies both <boundary conditions>, and each positive root obeys
$$
\int_0^1x\sin(q_nx)\,dx=-\frac{\cos q_n}{q_n}+\frac{\sin q_n}{q_n^2}=0
$$
because $\tan q_n=q_n$. Thus all $a_n$ vanish despite $f\ne0$. Likewise $f=\sinh(\kappa x)$ is a counterexample for $\alpha<1$, by <orthogonality of Sturm-Liouville eigenfunctions> with distinct <eigenvalues>. The formulas above give the full solution without silently assuming away the omitted modes.
