Use <Einstein summation convention>, writing $\partial_j=\partial/\partial x_j$. The <contraction of two Levi-Civita symbols> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
[\nabla\times(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)]_i
&=\epsilon_{ijk}\partial_j(\epsilon_{klm}a_lb_m)\\
&=(\delta_{il}\delta_{jm}-\delta_{im}\delta_{jl})\partial_j(a_lb_m)\\
&=\partial_j(a_ib_j-a_jb_i)\\
&=a_i\partial_jb_j-b_i\partial_ja_j+b_j\partial_ja_i-a_j\partial_jb_i.
\end{aligned}
$$
The last equality is the <product rule>. Recognizing the <divergences> and <directional derivatives> proves the <curl of a cross product> identity:
$$
\boxed{\nabla\times(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)=\mathbf a(\nabla\cdot\mathbf b)-\mathbf b(\nabla\cdot\mathbf a)+(\mathbf b\cdot\nabla)\mathbf a-(\mathbf a\cdot\nabla)\mathbf b.}
$$

The <divergence theorem> states that, for a continuously differentiable <vector field> $\mathbf H$ on a bounded region $V$ with piecewise smooth boundary $S$ and outward <unit normal> $\hat{\mathbf n}$,
$$
\int_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf H\,dV=\int_S\mathbf H\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,dS.
$$
To apply it, first derive the <divergence of a cross product> using the <Levi-Civita symbol> and the <product rule>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla\cdot(\mathbf F\times\mathbf G)
&=\epsilon_{ijk}\partial_i(F_jG_k)\\
&=\epsilon_{ijk}(\partial_iF_j)G_k+\epsilon_{ijk}F_j\partial_iG_k\\
&=\mathbf G\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)-\mathbf F\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf G).
\end{aligned}
$$
In the second term the minus sign comes from interchanging the first two indices of the <Levi-Civita symbol>. The <divergence theorem> with $\mathbf H=\mathbf F\times\mathbf G$ now yields
$$
\boxed{\int_V[\mathbf G\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)-\mathbf F\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf G)]\,dV=\int_S(\mathbf F\times\mathbf G)\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,dS.}
$$

Close the <hyperboloid> segment with the two disks at $z=\pm1$, and let $V$ be its interior. The <flux> through either disk is zero because $A_z=0$. Thus the desired <surface integral> is the total outward <flux> through the closed boundary. Since
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=3x^2+3y^2,
$$
the <divergence theorem> and <cylindrical coordinates> give
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\widetilde S}\mathbf A\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,dS
&=\int_{-1}^1\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\sqrt{1+z^2}}3r^2\,r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz\\
&=\frac{3\pi}{2}\int_{-1}^1(1+z^2)^2\,dz\\
&=\frac{3\pi}{2}\left(2+\frac43+\frac25\right)
=\boxed{\frac{28\pi}{5}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The extra factor $r$ is the <volume integral> measure in <cylindrical coordinates>; omitting it would give the wrong <flux>.

For <separation of variables>, take $n=T(t)X(x)Y(y)Z(z)$ and divide the <reaction-diffusion equation> by this product wherever it is nonzero:
$$
\frac{T'}{\mu T}-\frac\gamma\mu=\frac{X''}{X}+\frac{Y''}{Y}+\frac{Z''}{Z}.
$$
Each spatial term depends on a different variable and must be constant. Write $X''=-k_x^2X$ and similarly for the other two factors. The <Dirichlet boundary conditions> give $X(0)=X(L)=0$. A zero separation constant permits only a linear function and hence the zero solution; a positive value of $X''/X$ likewise permits no nonzero function vanishing at both endpoints. The nontrivial choices are therefore
$$
\boxed{X_p(x)=\sin\frac{p\pi x}{L},\quad Y_q(y)=\sin\frac{q\pi y}{L},\quad Z_r(z)=\sin\frac{r\pi z}{L},\qquad p,q,r=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
The temporal <ordinary differential equation> is
$$
T'_{p,q,r}=\left[\gamma-\frac{\mu\pi^2}{L^2}(p^2+q^2+r^2)\right]T_{p,q,r},
$$
so, absorbing its constant multiplier into $a_{p,q,r}$,
$$
\boxed{T_{p,q,r}(t)=\exp\left\{\left[\gamma-\frac{\mu\pi^2}{L^2}(p^2+q^2+r^2)\right]t\right\}.}
$$
The <superposition principle> and completeness of the <Fourier sine series> give the stated threefold expansion with these factors. For an initial density $n_0$, <orthogonality> also determines the constants:
$$
a_{p,q,r}=\frac8{L^3}\int_0^L\int_0^L\int_0^L n_0(x,y,z)\sin\frac{p\pi x}{L}\sin\frac{q\pi y}{L}\sin\frac{r\pi z}{L}\,dx\,dy\,dz.
$$

= Solution

The fastest temporal growth occurs for the lowest spatial <eigenvalue>, with $p=q=r=1$. Its growth rate is
$$
s_{111}=\gamma-\frac{3\mu\pi^2}{L^2}.
$$
For a generic initial condition its coefficient is nonzero, so this term determines the long-time behavior. In fact, every nonzero, nonnegative initial density has $a_{111}>0$, since the product of the three first sine <eigenfunctions> is strictly positive in the interior. Consequently
$$
\boxed{L_{\mathrm{crit}}=\pi\sqrt{\frac{3\mu}{\gamma}},\qquad \text{exponential growth precisely when }L>L_{\mathrm{crit}}.}
$$
For $L<L_{\mathrm{crit}}$ every term decays. At equality the first term is stationary and all higher terms decay: equality does not produce exponential growth. This is a <reaction-diffusion equation> threshold arising from competition between production and loss through the <Dirichlet boundary condition>.

The <separation of variables> solution with <Dirichlet boundary conditions> on the cuboid is
$$
\boxed{n=\sum_{p,q,r\geq1}a_{p,q,r}\sin\frac{p\pi x}{\lambda L}\sin\frac{q\pi\sqrt\lambda\,y}{L}\sin\frac{r\pi\sqrt\lambda\,z}{L}\exp\left\{\left[\gamma-\frac{\mu\pi^2}{L^2}\left(\frac{p^2}{\lambda^2}+\lambda q^2+\lambda r^2\right)\right]t\right\}.}
$$
The constants are fixed by the initial density through <Fourier sine series>. The fastest term again has $p=q=r=1$. When $L=2L_{\mathrm{crit}}$, its growth rate becomes
$$
s_{111}=\gamma\left[1-\frac1{12}(\lambda^{-2}+2\lambda)\right].
$$
Thus the precise condition for no exponentially growing term is
$$
\boxed{\lambda^{-2}+2\lambda\geq12,\qquad\text{equivalently }2\lambda^3-12\lambda^2+1\geq0,\quad\lambda>0.}
$$
For example, $\lambda=1/4$ gives $\lambda^{-2}+2\lambda=33/2>12$, while $\lambda=6$ gives $12+1/36>12$: either a sufficiently flat or a sufficiently elongated cuboid suppresses growth despite its unchanged volume. The function $\lambda^{-2}+2\lambda$ decreases to its minimum $3$ at $\lambda=1$, then increases without bound. Hence there are two positive threshold values, with growth only between them; equality leaves one stationary term and all other terms decaying.

One integration gives $u'=C x^{-n-1}$. Therefore the two cases are

In each case the displayed functions have <linear independence> throughout the interval. Imposing both homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions> forces $A=B=0$: for $n\ne0$, $2^n\ne1$, and for $n=0$, $\log2\ne0$. This also establishes uniqueness for the inhomogeneous <boundary value problem>.

Choose the convention $L_xG(x,s)=\delta(x-s)$, where $\delta$ is the <Dirac delta distribution>, together with $G(1/2,s)=G(1,s)=0$. The <Green's function> is continuous at $x=s$ and obeys
$$
\partial_xG(s^+,s)-\partial_xG(s^-,s)=1.
$$
These conditions follow by integrating its <differential equation> across $s$; continuity prevents a derivative of the <Dirac delta distribution>. If $\ell$ and $r$ are homogeneous solutions satisfying the left and right <boundary conditions>, respectively, and $W=\ell r'-\ell'r$ is their <Wronskian>, then
$$
G(x,s)=\frac{\ell(x_<)r(x_>)}{W(s)},\qquad x_<=\min(x,s),\quad x_>=\max(x,s).
$$
For $n\ne0$ take $\ell(x)=x^{-n}-2^n$ and $r(x)=x^{-n}-1$. Their <Wronskian> is $W(s)=n(2^n-1)s^{-n-1}$, and hence
$$
\boxed{G_n(x,s)=\frac{s^{n+1}(x_<^{-n}-2^n)(x_>^{-n}-1)}{n(2^n-1)},\qquad n\ne0.}
$$
For $n=0$ take $\ell(x)=\log(2x)$ and $r(x)=\log x$, giving $W(s)=\log2/s$ and
$$
\boxed{G_0(x,s)=\frac{s}{\log2}\log(2x_<)\log x_>.}
$$
Both formulas satisfy the endpoint conditions, continuity, and the unit jump directly. The unique solution of the <boundary value problem> is
$$
\boxed{u(x)=\int_{1/2}^1G_n(x,s)f(s)\,ds.}
$$
The factor $s^{n+1}$ in the first formula matters: this <Green's function> is for the original monic <differential operator>, with ordinary measure $ds$, rather than for the operator multiplied by $x^{n+1}$.

For $n\ne0$, split the <Green's function> integral at $s=x$. Writing $\ell(x)=x^{-n}-2^n$ and $r(x)=x^{-n}-1$ gives
$$
u(x)=\frac{r(x)\int_{1/2}^{x}(1-2^ns^n)\,ds+\ell(x)\int_x^1(1-s^n)\,ds}{n(2^n-1)}.
$$
For $n\ne-1$, the two <integrals> are
$$
I_1=x-\frac12-\frac{2^n}{n+1}\left(x^{n+1}-2^{-n-1}\right),\qquad I_2=1-x-\frac{1-x^{n+1}}{n+1}.
$$
Substitution and simplification yield
$$
\boxed{u(x)=\frac1{n+1}\left[x-1+\frac{x^{-n}-1}{2(2^n-1)}\right],\qquad n\ne0,-1.}
$$
At $x=1$ both contributions vanish. At $x=1/2$ the bracket is $-1/2+(2^n-1)/[2(2^n-1)]=0$. The <differential operator> kills $x^{-n}-1$ and sends $x/(n+1)$ to $1/x$, so this also verifies the inhomogeneous equation.

The exceptional integer is $\boxed{n=-1}$, because then the <integrals> contain $s^{-1}$. In this case
$$
I_1=x-\frac12-\frac12\log(2x),\qquad I_2=1-x+\log x,
$$
and the <Green's function> integral becomes
$$
\begin{aligned}
u(x)&=2\left[(x-1)\left(x-\frac12-\frac12\log(2x)\right)+\left(x-\frac12\right)(1-x+\log x)\right]\\
&=\boxed{x\log x+(1-x)\log2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $L=d^2/dx^2$, and $u''=1/x$. Explicitly $u(1)=0$ and $u(1/2)=-(\log2)/2+(\log2)/2=0$. Thus both exceptional and nonexceptional formulas satisfy the required <boundary conditions>.

Put $D=A-iB$, so $D^\dagger=A+iB$. Because the <Hermitian matrices> $A,B$ <commute>,
$$
D^\dagger D=DD^\dagger=A^2+B^2.

For every nonzero <vector> $v$,
$$
v^\dagger(A^2+B^2)v=\|Av\|^2+\|Bv\|^2>0,
$$
since $A$ is an <invertible matrix>. Thus $D$ and $D^\dagger$ are <invertible matrices>, justifying the inverse in the definition of $U$. The <conjugate transpose> of $U=D^\dagger D^{-1}$ is $(D^\dagger)^{-1}D$. Since $D$ and $D^\dagger$ <commute>, their inverses do too, and
$$
\boxed{U^\dagger U=(D^\dagger)^{-1}DD^\dagger D^{-1}=I.}
$$
Hence $U$ is a <unitary matrix>.

Complete the square in the <Hermitian matrix> polynomial:
$$
H=\left(A+\frac\alpha2I\right)^2+\left(\beta-\frac{\alpha^2}4\right)I.
$$
For every nonzero <vector> $v$,
$$
v^\dagger Hv=\left\|\left(A+\frac\alpha2I\right)v\right\|^2+\left(\beta-\frac{\alpha^2}4\right)\|v\|^2>0.
$$
Thus $H$ is a <positive-definite matrix>, so $\boxed{0\text{ is not an eigenvalue of }H}$. In <finite-dimensional spectral theorem> terms, each <eigenvalue> is
$$
h_j=\lambda_j^2+\alpha\lambda_j+\beta=\left(\lambda_j+\frac\alpha2\right)^2+\beta-\frac{\alpha^2}4>0.
$$
If $\alpha^2\geq4\beta$, the scalar <polynomial> has real <roots of a polynomial>
$$
r_\pm=\frac{-\alpha\pm\sqrt{\alpha^2-4\beta}}2.
$$
An allowed nonzero root can be an <eigenvalue> of $A$, a <Hermitian matrix> which is also an <invertible matrix>, in which case $H$ has a zero <eigenvalue>. For example, $\alpha=2$, $\beta=1$, $A=-I$ gives $H=0$. The weaker inequality does not force failure for every $A$; it removes the general positive lower bound. There is also the harmless corner $\alpha=\beta=0$, for which $H=A^2$ stays positive definite because $A$ is invertible, even though the strict discriminant hypothesis is absent.

Termwise differentiation of the absolutely convergent <matrix exponential> series gives
$$
\frac{d}{dt}e^{tP}=e^{tP}P=Pe^{tP}.

\frac{d}{dt}\left(De^{-t(P+Q)}\right)=0

and the product is identically zero. At $t=1$ this yields

\boxed{e^Pe^Q=e^{P+Q}\quad\text{when }PQ=QP.}

For a <holomorphic function>, the derivative supplied by <complex differentiability> must have the same value along real and imaginary increments. Those two computations give
$$
f'(z)=u_x+iv_x=\frac{u_y+iv_y}{i}=v_y-iu_y.
$$
Equating real and imaginary parts proves the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>:
$$
\boxed{u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x.}
$$
Conversely, if the first partial <derivatives> are continuous near a point and obey these equations there, real differentiability gives
$$
f(z+h)-f(z)=(u_x+iv_x)\operatorname{Re}h+(u_y+iv_y)\operatorname{Im}h+o(|h|)=f'(z)h+o(|h|),
$$
which proves complex differentiability under this regularity assumption.

The <gradients> of the real and imaginary parts have <dot product>
$$
\nabla u\cdot\nabla v=u_xv_x+u_yv_y=v_yv_x-v_xv_y=0,
$$
and $|\nabla u|^2=|\nabla v|^2=|f'(z)|^2$. At every point with $f'(z)\ne0$, the two <level sets> are regular curves, their <gradients> are nonzero <normal vectors>, and their tangent directions are therefore perpendicular.

The printed phrase "at every point" needs this regular-point qualification. At a <critical point> with $f'(z)=0$, the <level sets> may cross or fail to have a unique tangent. For example, $f(z)=z^2$ gives $u=x^2-y^2$ and $v=2xy$: at the origin the zero <level sets> are respectively the diagonals and the coordinate axes. Thus the valid conclusion is $\boxed{\text{orthogonality at every regular intersection, where }f'(z)\ne0}$.

For the first function, the complex exponential has no <zeros>, while the denominator has three distinct simple <zeros>. Thus there are $\boxed{\text{simple poles at }z=0,\,2i,\,-2i;\ \text{no finite zeros}}$. To examine infinity, substitute $z=1/w$:

The factor $e^{1/w}$ has an <essential singularity> at zero, and multiplication by a nonzero finite-order factor cannot remove it. Therefore $\boxed{\infty\text{ is an isolated essential singularity}}$ of the first function.

For the second function, $\sin z$ has simple <zeros> at $z=n\pi$. At zero, its <Taylor series> gives
$$
\frac{z^2}{\sin^2z}=\left(\frac{z}{z-z^3/6+O(z^5)}\right)^2=1+\frac{z^2}{3}+O(z^4),
$$
so $\boxed{z=0\text{ is removable, with extended value }1}$. Every other $z=n\pi$ is a double <pole>, since the numerator is nonzero there. There are no finite <zeros>, including after removal at zero.

At infinity the double <poles> $n\pi$ accumulate. Equivalently, after $z=1/w$, the function $1/[w^2\sin^2(1/w)]$ has double <poles> at $w=1/(n\pi)$ tending to zero. Hence $\boxed{\infty\text{ is a non-isolated singularity}}$. The removable/pole/essential trichotomy for <isolated singularities> does not apply there; calling this an isolated <essential singularity> would be incorrect.

Take nonzero real trial functions $y$ with $y,y'$ <square-integrable> and sufficient decay for <integration by parts>. For a compactly supported <variation> $y\mapsto y+\varepsilon\eta$, the <first variations> are
$$
\delta F=2\int_{\mathbb R}(y'\eta'-V_0e^{-x^2}y\eta)\,dx=2\int_{\mathbb R}(-y''-V_0e^{-x^2}y)\eta\,dx,\qquad\delta G=2\int_{\mathbb R}y\eta\,dx.
$$
The <quotient rule> for the <functional> then yields
$$
\delta\Lambda=\frac2{G[y]}\int_{\mathbb R}\left[-y''-V_0e^{-x^2}y-\Lambda[y]y\right]\eta\,dx.
$$
By the <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations>, stationarity for all such $\eta$ is equivalent to
$$
-y''-V_0e^{-x^2}y=\Lambda[y]y.
$$
Conversely, multiplying a decaying <eigenfunction> equation by $y$ and using <integration by parts> gives $F[y]=EG[y]$, so its <Rayleigh quotient> equals $E$ and its <first variation> vanishes. Thus the stationary values of the <functional> are the energy <eigenvalues>. The word "extrema" in the question includes stationary points here: higher <eigenfunctions> can be saddle points of the unrestricted <Rayleigh quotient>.

The <Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle> gives
$$
\boxed{E_0=\inf_{y\ne0}\Lambda[y],\qquad E_0\leq\Lambda[y_T].}
$$
Choose an admissible trial family, calculate its <Rayleigh quotient>, and minimize over its parameters to obtain the best upper bound within that family. Equality holds for the exact <ground state>; a restricted family generally gives only an upper bound.
