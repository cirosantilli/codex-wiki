= Solution
{parent=a}

Multiplication by $x^2$ gives the <Sturm-Liouville form>
$$
-(x^2u')'=\lambda x^2u,
$$
so the weight is $w(x)=x^2$. Use the weighted <inner product> $\langle f,g\rangle_w=\int_0^{\pi/2}x^2\overline f g\,dx$ and the <Sturm-Liouville operator> $L=-x^{-2}(x^2d/dx)'$. <Integration by parts> gives its boundary form:
$$
\langle f,Lg\rangle_w-\langle Lf,g\rangle_w
=\left[x^2(\overline {f'}g-\overline f g')\right]_0^{\pi/2}=0.
$$
The upper endpoint term vanishes by the <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, and the lower one vanishes for the regular solutions. More precisely, the <radial Dirichlet spectrum in three dimensions> identifies $L$ under the <unitary transformation> $v=xu$ with $-v''$ on $(0,\pi/2)$ with zero endpoint values; this specifies the <self-adjoint> realization at the singular endpoint as well as proving the formal identity.

For <eigenfunctions> $u_a,u_b$ with different <eigenvalues>, the same identity gives $(\lambda_b-\lambda_a)\langle u_a,u_b\rangle_w=0$. Taking real <eigenfunctions> proves the required <orthogonality of Sturm-Liouville eigenfunctions>.

= Solution

Set $v=xu$. Direct <differentiation> gives $u''+2u'/x=v''/x$, so $-v''=\lambda v$. Finiteness of $u$ at zero requires $v(0)=0$, and the other endpoint gives $v(\pi/2)=0$. The <Dirichlet eigenvalues> are positive: <integration by parts> gives $\lambda\int|v|^2=\int|v'|^2$, and a nonzero $v$ cannot have zero right-hand side. Thus $v=C\sin(\sqrt\lambda x)$, and the second endpoint gives
$$
\boxed{\lambda_n=4n^2,\qquad u_n(x)=\frac{\sin(2nx)}x,\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
Define $u_n(0)=2n$ by its <limit>. This is the <radial Dirichlet spectrum in three dimensions> with radius $\pi/2$. Its weighted squared <norm> is $\int_0^{\pi/2}x^2u_n^2dx=\pi/4$.

= Solution

Write the equation as $(L+\beta)y=-\cos x/x$ with the positive <Sturm-Liouville operator> from part (a). A <Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion> therefore gives $a_n=-f_n/(4n^2+\beta)$, where weighted <orthogonal projection> gives
$$
f_n=\frac4\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin(2nx)\cos x\,dx
=\frac{8n}{\pi(4n^2-1)}.
$$
Here the <product-to-sum formulas> reduce the <integral> to half the sum of the integrals of $\sin((2n+1)x)$ and $\sin((2n-1)x)$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{y(x)=-\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{8n}{\pi(4n^2-1)(4n^2+\beta)}\frac{\sin(2nx)}x.}
$$
At $x=0$, use the continuous limits of the terms. The coefficients are $O(n^{-3})$, so the resulting endpoint series converges. Completeness is the ordinary <Fourier sine series> completeness after $v=xy$. For an independent check, solving $v''-\beta v=\cos x$ with zero endpoint values gives
$$
y(x)=\frac{\sinh(\sqrt\beta(\pi/2-x))/\sinh(\sqrt\beta\pi/2)-\cos x}{(1+\beta)x}.
$$
Its numerator vanishes at zero, and it has the same <orthogonal projection> coefficients. Positivity of $L+\beta$ proves <uniqueness>.

For an <axisymmetric harmonic function>, put $\Psi=R(r)\Theta(\theta)$ in the <Laplacian in spherical coordinates> and multiply by $r^2/(R\Theta)$. <Separation of variables> gives
$$
\frac{(r^2R')'}R=-\frac{(\sin\theta\,\Theta')'}{\sin\theta\,\Theta}=\ell(\ell+1).
$$
With $u=\cos\theta$, the angular equation becomes the <Legendre differential equation>. Regularity at both poles selects the <Legendre polynomials> $P_\ell(u)$ with integer $\ell\ge0$; the singular second solutions are excluded. The radial <Euler-Cauchy equation> has solutions $r^\ell$ and $r^{-\ell-1}$. <Superposition> therefore gives
$$
\Psi=\sum_{\ell=0}^\infty(A_\ell r^\ell+B_\ell r^{-\ell-1})P_\ell(\cos\theta),
$$
with coefficients chosen to ensure convergence and the required boundary regularity. The <axisymmetric harmonic extension from a sphere> gives these coefficients directly from boundary data.

= Solution

Set $u=\cos\theta$. The boundary data simplify to $u+2u^2=\frac23P_0(u)+P_1(u)+\frac43P_2(u)$. The <axisymmetric harmonic extension from a sphere> keeps only decaying radial powers outside and regular powers inside. Hence
$$
\boxed{\Psi_{\rm out}=\frac{2a}{3r}+\frac{a^2}{r^2}\cos\theta+\frac{4a^3}{3r^3}P_2(\cos\theta),\quad r\ge a,}
$$
and
$$
\boxed{\Psi_{\rm in}=\frac23+\frac ra\cos\theta+\frac{4r^2}{3a^2}P_2(\cos\theta),\quad r\le a.}
$$
Each <Legendre polynomial> coefficient agrees at $r=a$. The exterior solution tends to zero, and the interior solution is finite at $r=0$. The <maximum principle for harmonic functions> proves <uniqueness> in these respective classes.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The plane separates the exterior into two half-domains. Use an <odd Legendre extension for an equatorial Dirichlet plane> in each. On the upper hemisphere, extend $u+2u^2$ to the odd function $u+2u|u|$ on the whole sphere. On the lower hemisphere, the appropriate odd extension is $u-2u|u|$. Define, for odd $\ell$,
$$
b_\ell=2(2\ell+1)\int_0^1t^2P_\ell(t)\,dt.
$$
The <orthogonality> and parity of the <Legendre polynomials> give the solution in both half-domains as
$$
\boxed{\Psi(r,\theta)=\frac{a^2}{r^2}u+\operatorname{sgn}(u)\sum_{\ell=1,3,5,\ldots}b_\ell\left(\frac ar\right)^{\ell+1}P_\ell(u),\quad u=\cos\theta.}
$$
Set its value on $u=0$ to zero. The first coefficients are $b_1=3/2$ and $b_3=7/12$. All odd <Legendre polynomials> vanish at zero, so the plane condition holds. On the sphere the sum is $2u|u|$, and multiplication by $\operatorname{sgn}(u)$ recovers $2u^2$ on either side. Thus the original sphere data and decay at infinity both hold. A jump of the normal <derivative> at the sheet is permitted; the sheet can support induced <surface charge density>.

Since the <electric field> is <irrotational>, introduce an <electrostatic potential> with $\mathbf E=-\nabla\varphi$. <Gauss's law> becomes the <Poisson equation>
$$
\boxed{(\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2)\varphi=-\rho/\epsilon_0,\qquad\mathbf E=-\nabla\varphi.}
$$
The curl equation is automatic because the <curl of a gradient> vanishes. Independence of $z$ makes $E_z$ constant, and its required decay forces it to be zero, so a $z$-independent potential suffices.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For the rotationally invariant free-space <fundamental solution of the Laplace equation>, put $s=\sqrt{(x-x')^2+(y-y')^2}$. Away from the source, the <radial Laplacian> gives $(sG')'=0$, hence $G=A\log s^2+C$. Integrate its <Poisson equation> over a disk of radius $r$ about the source. The <divergence theorem> gives $2\pi r(2A/r)=-1/\epsilon_0$, so
$$
\boxed{G=-\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\log\big((x-x')^2+(y-y')^2\big)+C.}
$$
A fixed reference length can be inserted inside the <logarithm> and absorbed into $C$. The resulting <electric field> decays like $1/s$.

Strictly, the stated pointwise decay as $y\to\pm\infty$ alone does not justify “most general”: one can also add the <harmonic function> $\operatorname{Re}e^{(x+iy)^2}$, whose <gradient> has that decay for every fixed $x$. This is the same growth issue as a <zero-boundary harmonic function with pointwise vertical decay>. The displayed answer is the usual isotropic free-space choice; requiring the field to vanish uniformly in every direction at spatial infinity removes these extra harmonic additions. An additive constant remains undetermined.

Use the <Green-function representation> to integrate over the charged segment:
$$
\varphi(x,y)=-\frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_0^L\log((x-s)^2+y^2)\,ds+C_0.
$$
For $y\ne0$, a primitive with respect to $u$ is
$$
F(u,y)=u\log(u^2+y^2)-2u+2y\arctan(u/y),
$$
as direct <differentiation> verifies using the <derivative> of <inverse tangent>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\varphi(x,y)=-\frac{F(x,y)-F(x-L,y)}{4\pi\epsilon_0}+C_0.}
$$
This expression is even in $y$, since $y\arctan(u/y)$ is even. On $y=0$, take the continuous limit $F(u,0)=u\log u^2-2u$, with $u\log u^2=0$ at $u=0$. The finite segment has a logarithmically growing potential at large distance, but its <electric field> decays; the boundary condition constrains the field rather than the potential.

Differentiate the <Green-function representation>, valid away from the charged sheet:
$$
E_y=\frac1{2\pi\epsilon_0}\int_0^L\frac{y\,ds}{(x-s)^2+y^2}
=\frac{\arctan(x/y)-\arctan((x-L)/y)}{2\pi\epsilon_0}.

For $0<x<L$, the two <inverse tangent> limits at $y\downarrow0$ are $\pi/2$ and $-\pi/2$; approaching from below reverses both signs. Thus
$$
\boxed{E_y(x,0^+)=\frac1{2\epsilon_0},\quad E_y(x,0^-)=-\frac1{2\epsilon_0},\quad |\Delta E_y|=\frac1{\epsilon_0}.}
$$
This agrees with the normal <electric field> jump obtained by applying <Gauss's law> to a thin pillbox enclosing unit <surface charge density>.

The function $e^{-z^2}$ is <entire>, so <Cauchy's integral theorem> makes its <contour integral> around the rectangle vanish. The lower horizontal integral tends to $\sqrt\pi$ by the <Gaussian integral>. On a vertical side $z=\pm R+it$, with $t$ between $0$ and $b$, its modulus is $e^{-R^2+t^2}\le e^{-R^2+b^2}$; hence both vertical integrals tend to zero. The upper side has the opposite orientation and contributes
$$
-e^{b^2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}e^{-2ibx}\,dx.
$$
Consequently the integral in this expression is $\sqrt\pi e^{-b^2}$. Its imaginary part vanishes because $e^{-x^2}\sin(2bx)$ is <odd>, and its real part is twice the positive-half-line integral because the integrand there is <even>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}\cos(2bx)\,dx=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}e^{-b^2}.}
$$
For negative $b$ the rectangle lies below the real axis and the same cancellation holds; alternatively use the even dependence on $b$.

{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let $B=|b|>0$. For $a>0$, integrate $e^{iaz}/(z^2+B^2)$ over the upper semicircle. <Jordan's lemma> removes the arc, and the <residue theorem> at $z=iB$ gives
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{iax}}{x^2+B^2}\,dx
=2\pi i\frac{e^{-aB}}{2iB}=\frac\pi B e^{-aB}.
$$
Taking the real part and using <evenness> in both $x$ and $a$ yields
$$
\boxed{I_1(a)=\frac{\pi}{2B}e^{-B|a|}.}
$$
The case $a=0$ follows directly from an <inverse tangent> primitive. The complex exponential is essential on the contour: the cosine alone has a growing exponential component.

For $a>0$, <differentiation under the integral sign> gives $-I_1'(a)=\int_0^\infty x\sin(ax)/(x^2+B^2)\,dx$. This derivative is a conditionally convergent <improper integral>; the <Dirichlet test> makes its tails uniform on compact subintervals of $a>0$, justifying the differentiation. Setting $a=1$ gives
$$
\boxed{I_2=\frac\pi2e^{-B}.}
$$
Finally let $J(a)=\int_0^\infty\sin(ax)/[x(x^2+B^2)]\,dx$. Its differentiated integrand is absolutely integrable, so $J'(a)=I_1(a)$ and $J(0)=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{I_3=J(1)=\frac{\pi}{2B^2}(1-e^{-B}).}
$$
If $b=0$, $I_1$ and $I_3$ diverge at the origin, whereas $I_2=\int_0^\infty\sin x/x\,dx=\pi/2$ is the corresponding <Dirichlet integral>.

The <Fourier transform of a derivative> is $i\omega$ times the transform under the stated convention. Transforming the <linear ordinary differential equation> gives
$$
(-\omega^2+5i\omega+6)\widetilde x=\widetilde F,
\qquad\boxed{R(\omega)=\frac1{(i\omega+2)(i\omega+3)}.}
$$
The applied <force> is $F(t)=H(t)e^{-t}$, with $H$ the <Heaviside step function>, so $\widetilde F=(1+i\omega)^{-1}$. Partial fractions give
$$
\widetilde x=\frac{1/2}{1+i\omega}-\frac1{2+i\omega}+\frac{1/2}{3+i\omega}.
$$
Using the transform pair $H(t)e^{-ct}\leftrightarrow(c+i\omega)^{-1}$ yields
$$
\boxed{x(t)=H(t)\left(\tfrac12e^{-t}-e^{-2t}+\tfrac12e^{-3t}\right).}
$$
Its value and first <derivative> both vanish at $t=0^+$, so the joining introduces no spurious <Dirac delta> terms. Equivalently, this is the <convolution> of the <force> with the causal <impulse response> $H(t)(e^{-2t}-e^{-3t})$.

Write $q=i\omega$. A constant-coefficient <linear ordinary differential equation> has <transfer function> $1/P(q)$, where $P(q)=\sum_{n=1}^c a_nq^n$. The specified simple <poles> correspond to $q=0,-2,1$. A minimal-order choice is therefore
$$
P(q)=q(q+2)(q-1)=q^3+q^2-2q.

Thus one possible system is
$$
\boxed{x'''+x''-2x'=F,\qquad c=3,\quad(a_1,a_2,a_3)=(-2,1,1),}
$$
up to a common nonzero multiplicative constant. The root $q=1$ permits an exponentially growing homogeneous solution; the pole data therefore describe an unstable system. This does not prevent the requested algebraic reconstruction of its <transfer function>.

For a <rotation matrix> $R\in SO(3)$, the Cartesian <tensor component transformation law> is
$$
\boxed{T'_{ijkl}=R_{ia}R_{jb}R_{kc}R_{ld}T_{abcd}.}
$$
An <isotropic tensor> is unchanged under every such rotation, so its components satisfy $T_{ijkl}=R_{ia}R_{jb}R_{kc}R_{ld}T_{abcd}$ for every $R\in SO(3)$. The repeated indices are summed by the <Einstein summation convention>.

Take the <trace> first: the <antisymmetric tensor> has zero diagonal and $X$ is traceless, so $T_{ii}=3t$. Taking symmetric and antisymmetric parts then gives the <scalar, symmetric-traceless and axial tensor decomposition>:
$$
\boxed{t=\frac13T_{kk},\quad A_{ij}=\frac12(T_{ij}-T_{ji}),\quad X_{ij}=\frac12(T_{ij}+T_{ji})-\frac13T_{kk}\delta_{ij}.}
$$
The <Kronecker delta> satisfies $\delta_{ii}=3$, which verifies $X_{ii}=0$. Substitution verifies the original decomposition and also proves its <uniqueness>.

{parent=c}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

A complete pairwise <tensor contraction> of copies of $A$ is represented by closed index cycles. Each cycle is a <trace> $\operatorname{tr}(A^r)$, possibly with a sign from reversing an index pair. If a contraction is internal to one copy, it contains the already vanishing $\operatorname{tr}A$. Otherwise an odd total number of copies forces at least one cycle of odd length, since a sum of even lengths is even. That trace vanishes by the transposition argument in part (ii). Therefore **every such complete contraction is zero**. This proves <vanishing odd complete contractions of an antisymmetric matrix> without assuming that all contractions form a single trace.

Let $\mathbf q=(x,y)$ be the centre displacement and $\theta$ the small disk rotation. Put $d=l-l_0>0$. The <moment of inertia of a uniform disc> is $I=ma^2/2$, obtained from $I=\int_0^a\rho r^2(2\pi r\,dr)$ and $m=\rho\pi a^2$. Thus the <kinetic energy> is $T=m|\dot{\mathbf q}|^2/2+ma^2\dot\theta^2/4$.

For one spring, let $\mathbf n_j$ point radially towards its fixed anchor and let $\mathbf t_j$ be the perpendicular unit <vector>. The moving attachment is $\mathbf q+aR(\theta)\mathbf n_j$, while the anchor is $(a+l)\mathbf n_j$. Expanding their separation to second order gives the spring extension beyond natural length:
$$
s_j-l_0=d-\mathbf n_j\cdot\mathbf q+\frac a2\theta^2+
\frac{(\mathbf t_j\cdot\mathbf q+a\theta)^2}{2l}+O((|\mathbf q|+|\theta|)^3).
$$
Insert this in the <elastic potential energy> $V=(k/2)\sum_j(s_j-l_0)^2$. For the three symmetric directions, $\sum_j\mathbf n_j=\sum_j\mathbf t_j=0$ and $\sum_j(\mathbf n_j\cdot\mathbf q)^2=\sum_j(\mathbf t_j\cdot\mathbf q)^2=3|\mathbf q|^2/2$. Dropping the constant equilibrium energy gives
$$
V_2=\frac{3k}{4}\left(1+\frac dl\right)(x^2+y^2)+\frac{3kda(a+l)}{2l}\theta^2.
$$
Therefore the quadratic <Lagrangian> is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L_2=\frac m2(\dot x^2+\dot y^2)+\frac{ma^2}4\dot\theta^2-\frac{3k}{4}\left(1+\frac dl\right)(x^2+y^2)-\frac{3kda(a+l)}{2l}\theta^2.}
$$
The prestretch terms cannot be omitted: they supply transverse spring stiffness and the entire rotational restoring torque, as derived generally in <small oscillations of a disk attached to prestretched radial springs>.

The quadratic <Lagrangian> is diagonal in $x,y,\theta$, so the <Euler-Lagrange equations> give two pure translations and one pure rotation. Their <angular frequencies> are
$$
\boxed{\omega_x^2=\omega_y^2=\frac{3k}{2m}\left(1+\frac{l-l_0}{l}\right),\qquad
\omega_\theta^2=\frac{6k(l-l_0)(a+l)}{mal}.}
$$
A horizontal translation has $(x,y,\theta)=(A\cos\omega_xt,0,0)$; a vertical translation has $(0,A\cos\omega_yt,0)$; and the rotational mode has $(0,0,A\cos\omega_\theta t)$. Any fixed-direction translation is another choice in the same two-dimensional degenerate <normal mode> space. The rotation keeps the centre fixed.

The disk still has only **three degrees of freedom and hence three normal modes**: two centre coordinates and one in-plane rotation. Five springs alter the restoring <quadratic form> but add no disk coordinates. The fivefold <rotational symmetry> makes the translational stiffness proportional to the two-dimensional <identity matrix>, and makes translation-rotation coupling vanish. For the first <Cayley table>, <associativity> fails: $(a\ast a)\ast b=1\ast b=b$, whereas $a\ast(a\ast b)=a\ast c=d$. It therefore **does not define a group**.

For the second table, assign the residues $0,1,2,3,4$ to $1,a,c,b,d$, respectively. Every table entry agrees with <addition modulo an integer> modulo five. Thus this table **defines the cyclic group of order five**, with identity $1$ and inverses $a^{-1}=d$, $c^{-1}=b$. The identification with a known <group> verifies <associativity> as well as closure and the inverse axioms; inspecting only the identity row and inverses would not suffice.

= b
{parent=8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Use <group axioms from two-sided local inverse identities>. Choose any $a$ in the nonempty <semigroup> and put $e_R=a_L^{-1}a$, $e_L=aa_R^{-1}$. The given identities say $be_R=b$ and $e_Lb=b$ for every $b$. Therefore $e_Le_R=e_L$ by the right-identity property, and $e_Le_R=e_R$ by the left-identity property. Thus $e_L=e_R=e$ is a two-sided <identity element>.

For an arbitrary $a$, set $b=a_L^{-1}$ and $c=a_R^{-1}$. Inserting $e$ into the original identities gives $ba=e$ and $ac=e$. By <associativity>,
$$
b=be=b(ac)=(ba)c=ec=c.
$$
Thus every element has a two-sided <inverse element>. Closure and <associativity> were assumed, so **all the group axioms hold**. No cancellation or identity was used before it had been established.

Write the <dihedral group> as $D_6=\langle r,s\mid r^6=s^2=1,\ srs=r^{-1}\rangle$, where $r$ rotates through $\pi/3$. There are six rotations and six reflections, so **the order is 12**. Conjugation by $s$ reverses a rotation, and conjugation by $r$ sends $r^js$ to $r^{j+2}s$. Consequently the <conjugacy classes> and their sizes are
$$
\boxed{\{1\}\ (1),\quad\{r^3\}\ (1),\quad\{r,r^5\}\ (2),\quad\{r^2,r^4\}\ (2),\quad\{s,r^2s,r^4s\}\ (3),\quad\{rs,r^3s,r^5s\}\ (3).}
$$
The last two classes distinguish the reflection axes through opposite vertices and through opposite edge midpoints. Their sizes sum to 12.

A <group homomorphism> $f:G\to H$ preserves the operation: $f(ab)=f(a)f(b)$ for all $a,b\in G$. A <group isomorphism> is a bijective <group homomorphism>; its inverse also preserves the operation. The <kernel of a group homomorphism> is $\ker f=\{g\in G:f(g)=e_H\}$, a <normal subgroup> of $G$.

= Solution
{parent=d}

The <symmetric group> $S_3$ is generated by transpositions, each of order two. A <group homomorphism> to $\mathbb Z_3$ must map every transposition to zero, because the only target element annihilated by two is zero. Hence **the map to $\mathbb Z_3$ is necessarily trivial**.

For $S_3\to\mathbb Z_2$, the 3-cycles map to zero because their order divides three. All transpositions are <conjugate group elements>, and a map to an <abelian group> takes conjugate elements to the same value. That common value is either zero or one. Both choices work: they give **the trivial map and the sign homomorphism**, with an odd permutation sent to $1$ and an even permutation sent to $0$.

The printed wording calls $\Sigma_3$ cyclic, but its meaning in question 9 is the <symmetric group> $S_3$. Under that interpretation, take its planar <dihedral group> representation, with
$$
D(r)=\begin{pmatrix}-1/2&-\sqrt3/2\\\sqrt3/2&-1/2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
D(s)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Here $r^3=s^2=1$ and $srs=r^{-1}$. This <representation> is <irreducible> over the complex numbers: the two distinct rotation <eigenspaces> are exchanged by the reflection, so neither is invariant under the whole group. Its <character> is $2$ on the identity, $-1$ on the two 3-cycles and $0$ on the three transpositions. Squaring maps each transposition to the identity and permutes the 3-cycles. Its <Frobenius-Schur indicator> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\nu=\frac{2+3(2)+2(-1)}6=1.}
$$
If “cyclic” were intended literally, no two-dimensional complex <irreducible representation> would exist, since an <irreducible representation of a finite abelian group> is one-dimensional.

**The requested two-dimensional irreducible representation does not exist for the Vierergruppe.** The <Klein four-group> $V_4$ is an <abelian group> in which every nonidentity element has order two. Its complex <irreducible representations> are all one-dimensional. The same holds over the real numbers here: the commuting representing involutions are simultaneously diagonalizable with real eigenvalues $\pm1$, so every two-dimensional representation has an invariant line. Each genuine irreducible character satisfies $\chi(g^2)=\chi(1)=1$, and hence has <Frobenius-Schur indicator> $\nu=1$.

The printed notation $C_4$ suggests a different possible interpretation, which must not be conflated with the <Klein four-group>. For the <cyclic group of order four>, a real two-dimensional <irreducible representation> is generated by
$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its character on $1,J,-I,-J$ is $(2,0,-2,0)$, giving $\nu=(2-2+2-2)/4=0$. Over the complex numbers this representation splits into two conjugate one-dimensional characters, so it is not a two-dimensional complex irreducible representation. These alternatives explain what can be computed without silently changing the printed group or the ground field.

For the <symmetric group> representation in part (a), the identity and three transpositions contribute $4I$ to $A=\sum_gD(g^2)$. The two 3-cycles contribute $D(r)+D(r^2)=-I$. Thus
$$
\boxed{A=3I=\frac{6\cdot1}{2}I.}
$$
More generally, the <central average of representation matrices at squares> commutes with the representation, because conjugation permutes the summands. For a complex <irreducible representation>, <Schur's lemma> therefore gives $A=cI$. Taking the <trace> yields $c\dim D=|G|\nu$, proving the claimed formula under its correct hypotheses.

For the <Klein four-group> there is no two-dimensional irreducible representation, as established in part (b). Its genuine one-dimensional irreducibles have $A=4$ and $\nu=1$, so $A=(4\cdot1/1)I$.

If the intended alternative is the real quarter-turn representation of the <cyclic group of order four>, its squared matrices sum to $I-I+I-I=0$. Thus $A=0=(4\cdot0/2)I$ in that example. This equality is a direct calculation; the complex version of <Schur's lemma> cannot be applied to its reducible complexification.
