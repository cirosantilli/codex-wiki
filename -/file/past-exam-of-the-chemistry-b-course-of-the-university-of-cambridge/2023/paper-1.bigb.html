**A and C are <chiral>; B is <achiral>.** In A, a <quaternary ammonium stereocenter> occurs because <nitrogen> has four different <substituents>: phenyl, methyl, ethyl, and the sulfinylethyl chain. It has no <nitrogen> <lone pair> available for ordinary <pyramidal inversion> of an <amine>. The <sulfoxide> <sulfur> is also stereogenic: its pyramidal arrangement distinguishes <oxygen>, methyl, the <nitrogen>-containing chain, and the lone-pair direction. There are two independent <stereocenters> and no internal symmetry, giving four configurations in two pairs of <enantiomers>. This is <chirality (chemistry)> of the <cation>; a <bromide> <counterion> does not remove it.

B can adopt a trans-1,4-dimethylcyclohexane chair with both methyl groups equatorial. An <inversion centre> at the chair centre exchanges its opposite <carbon> atoms and methyl groups. Its mirror image is therefore superposable: trans <substituents> in a flat ring drawing do not by themselves prove <chirality (chemistry)>. <ring inversion> gives the less favourable diaxial conformer without changing the <trans configuration>.

In C, tetrahedral <phosphorus> has four different <substituent> directions: oxo, methoxy, ethoxy, and phenoxy. **C has a <phosphorus stereocenter>.** Delocalization in the P–O bonds does not make these four ligand environments identical. A tetrahedral drawing and its mirror cannot be superposed without exchanging two ligands.

The bridged, sterically hindered <biphenyl> framework is twisted and does not rapidly interconvert the two faces of each <methylene group> on the <NMR> timescale. Label one <hydrogen> on each CH2 $H_A$ and the other $H_B$. Molecular symmetry relates the two $H_A$ sites to each other and the two $H_B$ sites to each other.

Each methylene <hydrogen> undergoes <geminal coupling> to its partner, giving the two doublets, each accounting for two <hydrogens> overall. Their separation is $0.21$ ppm; at operating frequency $\nu_0$ in MHz it corresponds to $0.21\nu_0$ Hz. This separation is not the <geminal coupling> constant, which must be measured from the line spacing within a doublet. If the shift separation is not large compared with that coupling, an AB pattern with second-order distortion is expected rather than ideal first-order doublets. A racemic pair of twisted <conformations> still has these signals: <enantiomers> have the same spectrum in an <achiral> medium, while the two <hydrogen> environments within each <molecule> remain different.

Both permitted alternatives follow the same condensation–activation–migration sequence. <sodium> <acetate> liberates a useful concentration of <hydroxylamine> from its hydrochloride while providing a buffered medium. <nitrogen> of NH2OH attacks the <ketone> <carbonyl>; <proton transfer> gives a <carbinolamine>. <protonation> of its <carbon>-derived OH and loss of <water>, with <nitrogen> lone-pair donation, then gives the <oxime> after <deprotonation>. The two C=N geometries can be separated.

For the **B-to-D route**, <oxime O-tosylation> begins when <oxime> <oxygen> attacks <sulfur> of <tosyl chloride> and <chloride> leaves; <triethylamine> accepts the released <proton>. The resulting O-tosyl <oxime> has an excellent leaving group. The $K{-}R_a$ bond, anti to N–OTs, migrates to <nitrogen> while N–O cleaves, giving a ring-expanded <nitrilium ion>. Its local connectivity is $R_s{-}K\equiv N^+{-}R_a$, with the existing scaffold still joining the two paths. <water> attacks the nitrilium <carbon> $K$. <proton transfers> and imidic-<acid (chemistry)>/<amide> <tautomerization> produce D, with <nitrogen> inserted on the aromatic side of the old <carbonyl>.

For the **C-to-E route**, the same O-tosylation activates the other <oxime> geometry. Now the anti bond is $K{-}R_s$; its migration gives $R_a{-}K\equiv N^+{-}R_s$, and aqueous trapping produces E, with <nitrogen> inserted on the spiro-junction side. **Each <Beckmann rearrangement> expands the <ketone>'s six-membered ring to a seven-membered <lactam>.** The original <carbonyl> <carbon> remains the <amide> <carbonyl> <carbon>; <nitrogen> is the inserted atom. In this mechanism the <ketone> <oxygen> is lost during <oxime> condensation, the <oxime> <oxygen> leaves with the <sulfonate> group, and <water> supplies the new <amide> <oxygen>.

**The C–C bond anti to the activated N–O bond migrates.** This is <antiperiplanar migration in the Beckmann rearrangement>. Donation from that <sigma bond> into the developing <electron> deficiency at <nitrogen> requires the appropriate <antiperiplanar geometry>. The <lactol> is a cyclic <hemiacetal> and can open to **$\mathrm{HOCH_2CH_2{-}CH(OBn){-}CHO}$**, retaining its pre-existing alkoxy-bearing stereocentre. <magnesium> can coordinate both the <aldehyde> <oxygen> and the adjacent benzyloxy <oxygen>. Decyl attack on the less crowded face, away from the alpha-<carbon>'s hydroxyethyl chain in the chelated arrangement, forms the new C–C bond and a <magnesium> alkoxide at the former <aldehyde> <carbon>. Write the reacting terminus as $R{-}\mathrm{CH_2{-}CH{=}CH_2}$, where $R$ includes the pre-existing stereocentres and the intact $\mathrm{OCH_2C(=O)NMe_2}$ <substituent>. <ozone> undergoes 1,3-dipolar <cycloaddition> across the <terminal alkene> to give a <molozonide>. In a resonance description of <ozone> as $\mathrm{O^-{-}O^+{=}O}$, the alkene pi pair bonds to one terminal oxygen while the other terminal oxygen forms the second C–O bond and the ozone pi bond shifts onto its central oxygen. Cleavage of this unstable primary <ozonide> produces a <carbonyl> compound and a <carbonyl oxide>, which recombine to form the secondary <ozonide>. Reductive workup breaks the <peroxide> bonds and gives the <carbonyl> fragments $R{-}\mathrm{CH_2CHO}$ and <formaldehyde>.

Excess <sodium borohydride> also reduces the newly formed <aldehyde>: <hydride> attacks its <carbonyl> <carbon>, shifting the pi <electrons> onto <oxygen>, and the final acidic workup protonates the alkoxide. Thus **the retained chain becomes $R{-}\mathrm{CH_2CH_2OH}$**. The <terminal alkene> <carbon> becomes <formaldehyde> and can be reduced to <methanol>; it is no longer part of the main <product (chemistry)>. This accounts for the one-<carbon> shortening of the retained skeleton.

The original <amide> <carbonyl> is present in both H and I and is not reduced under the ordinary borohydride workup. The existing <stereocenters> and benzyl <ether> are retained. Stopping the mechanism at an <aldehyde>, or deleting the <amide> C=O when reading the smaller printed scheme, would give the wrong <product (chemistry)>. The acceptor-strength trend is **<amide> <oxygen> and <amine> <nitrogen> strongest, <ketone> <oxygen> weaker, <hydrocarbon> essentially nonaccepting**. <trimethylamine> and <isopropylamine> each provide a relatively available <nitrogen> <lone pair>; adding N–H bonds changes donor ability much more conspicuously than it changes the acceptor ranking in this comparison. Alkyl substitution and <solvation> modify the precise values, so the matching <amine> parameters should be read as the stated empirical strengths rather than a universal equality of all <amines>.

In <acetamide>, <nitrogen> lone-pair donation contributes an $\mathrm{O^-{-}C{=}N^+}$ form contributing to <resonance in chemistry>, increasing <electron density> at <oxygen> and making it a strong <hydrogen bond acceptor>. **The <amide> acceptor is <oxygen>, not <nitrogen>**: the <nitrogen> <lone pair> is delocalized into the <carbonyl>. <acetone> also accepts at <oxygen>, but lacks this <nitrogen>-to-<carbonyl> donation and has the smaller <hydrogen-bond acceptor parameter>. <isobutane> has neither a heteroatom <lone pair> nor a comparable acceptor site; its zero value is consistent with the absence of an ordinary directional <hydrogen bond acceptor>.

The clearest comparison is between the two <amines>, which have the same <molecular formula> and <molar mass>. **<isopropylamine> can donate N–H <hydrogen bonds> to neighbouring <nitrogen> <lone pairs>; <trimethylamine> has no N–H donor.** The resulting intermolecular association raises the <primary amine>'s boiling point, allowing it to remain liquid at room <temperature>, whereas <trimethylamine> is gaseous. Independent phase-change data place their normal boiling points on opposite sides of room <temperature>.<NIST Chemistry WebBook, SRD 69>[trimethylamine, CAS 75-50-3, and isopropylamine, CAS 75-31-0, phase-change data]{ref}

<acetone> has a substantial <carbonyl> dipole and London dispersion forces sufficient for a room-<temperature> liquid, even though it cannot build a strong donor–acceptor <hydrogen bond> network by itself. <isobutane> has mainly London dispersion forces and a compact shape and remains gaseous. <acetamide> combines <carbonyl> acceptors with two N–H donors, allowing an extended, directionally bonded crystal lattice; favourable packing as well as intermolecular association stabilizes its solid state.

These compounds have similar molar masses, so mass alone cannot explain the differences. The <hydrogen-bond acceptor parameter> also cannot predict the phase on its own: a pure substance needs complementary donors to exploit strong acceptor sites, and freezing additionally depends on crystal packing and <entropy>. A stronger acceptor does not automatically imply a higher melting point for arbitrary <molecules>.

**Transfer is favourable when new <water>–solute interactions compensate the cavity and reorganization costs of accommodating the solute.** The <Gibbs energy> also includes the loss of solute–<alkane> interactions; it is not simply a count of <hydrogen bonds>. <isobutane> cannot compensate those costs with strong directional interactions, explaining its positive <transfer Gibbs energy>. <acetone> and both <amines> can accept <hydrogen bonds> from <water> and have favourable electrostatic <solvation>, making their transfer moderately favourable. <isopropylamine> can additionally donate to <water>, consistent with a somewhat greater preference for <water> than <trimethylamine>.

<acetamide> combines a strong <carbonyl> acceptor with N–H donors and has the most favourable hydration in the group, accounting for its much more negative <transfer Gibbs energy>. Its pure solid lattice is not an extra sublimation penalty to insert here: the stated process starts from a dilute <alkane> solution and ends in dilute <water>, rather than dissolving the pure crystal. The nonmonotonic relationship between <hydrogen-bond acceptor parameter> and <transfer Gibbs energy> is expected because donor strength, nonpolar surface, <solvent cavity formation>, and all other <solvation> contributions also enter.

For matched <standard states>, $\Delta G^\circ_{\mathrm{tr}}=-RT\ln K_{\mathrm{tr}}$, with $K_{\mathrm{tr}}$ the <water>-to-<alkane> activity ratio. As an illustrative 298 K calculation, the <hydrocarbon> value implies $K_{\mathrm{tr}}\simeq7.0\times10^{-4}$, whereas the <amide> value implies approximately $3.6\times10^4$. These are standard-state partition preferences, not absolute aqueous solubilities. <amine> <protonation> could introduce an additional pH-dependent contribution to an experimentally measured <distribution coefficient>, but no such speciation information is supplied and it is not needed for the molecular comparison.
