= Solution

Each <thiocyanate> is a **three-metal bridge**, $\mu_3$-$\kappa N:\kappa S:\kappa S$: its <nitrogen> donates to one <metal> and its <sulfur> donates to two other <metals>. Thus the <coordination ligand> is ambidentate, but the structure uses both ends and shares the <sulfur> donor. Each <metal> receives two <nitrogen> and four <sulfur> donors, giving an approximately octahedral $\mathrm{MN_2S_4}$ <coordination sphere>. Six donor contacts per <metal> are consistent with two <coordination ligands> per <metal> because each <coordination ligand> contributes three contacts across the lattice. Sharing these contacts produces a <coordination polymer> within each layer; the interlayer <Van der Waals force> does not add coordination bonds. The index $\mu_3$ counts <metal> centres, whereas a <hapticity> label would count adjacent coordinated <coordination ligand> atoms and would not describe this connectivity correctly.<Strengthening the Magnetic Interactions in Pseudobinary First-Row Transition Metal Thiocyanates, M(NCS)2 by Euan N. Bassey and others>[Structural discussion of the layered first-row metal thiocyanates]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Charge balance fixes each <metal> as $\mathrm{M^{II}}$. In an octahedral crystal field the lower $t_{2g}$ set is $(d_{xy},d_{xz},d_{yz})$ and the upper $e_g$ set is $(d_{z^2},d_{x^2-y^2})$. The measured <spin-only magnetic moments> select the following occupations:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c}
&\text{configuration}&n_{\rm unpaired}&S&\mu_{\rm eff}/\mu_B\\\hline
\mathrm{Mn^{II}}&t_{2g}^{3}e_g^{2}&5&5/2&\sqrt{35}=5.92\\
\mathrm{Fe^{II}}&t_{2g}^{4}e_g^{2}&4&2&\sqrt{24}=4.90\\
\mathrm{Cu^{II}}&t_{2g}^{6}e_g^{3}&1&1/2&\sqrt3=1.73
\end{array}
$$
The result follows from $S=n_{\rm unpaired}/2$ and $2\sqrt{S(S+1)}=\sqrt{n_{\rm unpaired}(n_{\rm unpaired}+2)}$. **Manganese(II) and iron(II) are <high-spin complexes>**: their octahedral splitting is too small to compensate the additional pairing cost of their low-spin alternatives. Copper(II) is $d^9$, with one unpaired <electron> regardless of which member of the upper pair is singly occupied. <spin-orbit coupling> and <exchange interactions> can produce deviations from the spin-only estimate in real measurements; no such correction is required to explain the supplied values.

At fixed $+2$ <oxidation state>, increasing <effective nuclear charge> across the first transition series generally contracts the <ionic radius>. The first expectation is therefore **shorter average <metal>–<nitrogen> and <metal>–<sulfur> distances toward copper**. This is a trend in comparable coordination environments, rather than a requirement that every individual contact shorten monotonically. The occupation of <metal>–<coordination ligand> <antibonding orbitals>, donor identity and lattice distortion all affect the observed distances.

The high-spin $d^5$ and $d^6$ ions both have two <electrons> in the strongly sigma-antibonding $e_g$ set, so adding the sixth <electron> to $t_{2g}$ does not itself introduce another sigma-antibonding <electron>. Copper(II), however, has $e_g^3$ occupation and is subject to the <Jahn-Teller effect>. A <tetragonal Jahn-Teller distortion> commonly gives four shorter and two longer contacts, so an elongated copper contact can oppose the overall size trend. Here the <nitrogen> and <sulfur> donors are already chemically inequivalent; the supplied schematic alone does not determine which trans pair becomes longest. One should compare actual M–N and M–S sets separately, rather than infer a unique axis or a numerical distance from the <magnetic moments>. The experimentally determined structures illustrate why the donor geometry and individual contacts matter.<Strengthening the Magnetic Interactions in Pseudobinary First-Row Transition Metal Thiocyanates, M(NCS)2 by Euan N. Bassey and others>[Crystallographic characterization and comparison of the layered structures]{ref}

For **A, linear <nitrogen> coordination with predominantly nitrile-like bonding** is consistent with all three measurements:
$$
\mathrm{M\leftarrow N\equiv C-S^-}.
$$
The short C–N and long C–S distances correspond approximately to a <triple bond> and a <single bond>. An approximately $sp$ <nitrogen> donor has its donor <lone pair> directed along the N–C axis, explaining the linear M–N–C angle.

For **B, bent <nitrogen> coordination with increased $\mathrm{^-N=C=S}$ <chemical resonance> character** accounts for the longer C–N and shorter C–S bonds. In this limiting description both internal bonds have substantial double-bond character and <nitrogen> is approximately $sp^2$. One <nitrogen> <lone pair> can bind a <metal> with an M–N–C angle near $120^\circ$; alternatively, donation of both <nitrogen> <lone pairs> can give a trigonal, nitrogen-bridging unit:
$$
\begin{array}{c}
\mathrm{M}\\[-2pt]
\diagdown\\[-3pt]
\mathrm{N=C=S}\\[-3pt]
\diagup\\[-2pt]
\mathrm{M'}
\end{array}
$$
The nitrogen-bridging drawing is a compatible proposed bonding mode, but the listed internal distances and one angle alone do not prove the number of attached <metals>. In particular, B cannot be identified simply as terminal <sulfur> coordination: the specified angle explicitly contains an M–N bond. <resonance structures> explain relative <bond orders>; they do not imply an isolated <coordination ligand> with permanently localized integer bonds.

A distinct possibility is **terminal <sulfur> coordination**, $\mathrm{M\leftarrow S-C\equiv N}$. Unlike the <sulfur> donor in the layer, this <sulfur> need bind only one <metal>. The limiting $\mathrm{^-S-C\equiv N}$ <resonance structure> suggests **$r_{\rm CN}\approx1.15\,\mathring{\mathrm A}$ and $r_{\rm CS}\approx1.8\,\mathring{\mathrm A}$**. These are single-/triple-bond-based estimates, not exact predictions for an unspecified <metal> complex. The M–S–C linkage should be bent, consistent with <sulfur> lone-pair donation.

= Solution

Take the M–C–O direction as $z$. <carbon monoxide> binds through <carbon> because its occupied $5\sigma$ donor orbital has a substantial carbon-end lobe. End-on overlap with an empty <metal> orbital of sigma symmetry gives **CO-to-metal <sigma donation>**. At the same time, occupied <metal> $d_{xz}$ and $d_{yz}$ orbitals overlap sideways with the two corresponding empty CO $2\pi^*$ orbitals, giving **metal-to-CO <pi backbonding>**. Both channels strengthen the <metal>–<carbon> bond, while backbonding lowers the internal C–O <bond order>. The processes are synergic: <sigma donation> helps supply <metal> <electron> density for backbonding, while backbonding relieves <electron> accumulation at the <metal>.

{title=Complementary sigma donation and pi backbonding in a metal carbonyl}

The original orbital sketch shows one of the two perpendicular pi channels. It is the occupied <metal> orbital, rather than an empty <metal> orbital, that supplies <electrons> to CO <antibonding orbitals>.

The <arene> and three <carbonyl ligands> are neutral, so **<chromium> is in <oxidation state> zero**, with a $d^6$ <metal> count. The eta-six <arene> contributes six <electrons> and each CO contributes two:
$$
\boxed{6+6+3(2)=18\text{ valence electrons}.}
$$
The <methoxy group> changes the electronic properties of the <arene> without changing this formal <electron> count. The complex is a <piano-stool complex>, with the coordinated <arene> as the seat and the three <carbonyl ligands> as the legs.

{parent=B7}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The methoxy <substituent> donates <electron> density into the coordinated <arene> by <chemical resonance>. This increases the <electron> density available at <chromium> for <pi backbonding> to CO, weakening C–O and lowering its stretching frequencies. The <ester> <substituent> withdraws <electron> density from the <arene>, reducing <pi backbonding> and raising the carbonyl stretching frequencies. For both observed bands, **the methoxy-substituted compound has the lowest carbonyl frequency, the unsubstituted compound is intermediate, and the ester-substituted compound has the highest**.

In the <harmonic oscillator> approximation,
$$
\widetilde\nu=\frac{1}{2\pi c}\sqrt{\frac{k}{\mu}},
$$
so the trend reflects an increasing effective C–O <bond force constant> rather than a change in the CO <reduced mass>. For example, comparison of the higher-frequency bands gives $k_{\rm ester}/k_{\rm methoxy}\approx(1992/1983)^2=1.0091$. This is only an approximate local-bond interpretation because the observed <normal modes> couple the three CO oscillators. In the idealized local $C_{3v}$ geometry they span $A_1+E$, giving two infrared-active frequencies. A substituted <arene> can lower the exact <molecular symmetry> and split the $E$ pair, even if that splitting is unresolved experimentally.

= Solution
{parent=d}

<triphenylphosphine> is a good sigma donor and a substantially poorer pi acceptor than CO. Replacing CO by this <phosphine ligand> increases <electron> density at <chromium> and reduces competition among <carbonyl ligands> for <pi backbonding>. Backbonding into each remaining CO therefore strengthens, their internal <bond force constants> fall, and **both remaining carbonyl stretching frequencies are expected to shift downward** relative to the parent tricarbonyl.

The two remaining <carbonyl ligands> have coupled approximately symmetric and antisymmetric stretches; both are generally infrared active in this geometry. One should not treat the spectral change as simply deleting one of the original two bands. The substituted complex still has $6+6+2(2)+2=18$ valence <electrons>. No numerical frequency shift follows from <electron counting> alone.

= Solution
{parent=e}

Photoinduced CO loss gives a 16-electron fragment. In the ester-substituted complex the pendant <ester> carbonyl <oxygen> can reach <chromium> and capture that vacant site. A suitable structure is **$\mathrm{Cr}(\eta^6\!\text{-}\mathrm{C_6H_5CO_2Me})(\mathrm{CO})_2(\kappa O\!\text{-ester})$**, retaining the coordinated <arene> and adding intramolecular coordination of its <ester> <oxygen>. The added <oxygen> lone-pair donation restores an 18-electron count. The gaseous by-product is **<carbon monoxide>**.

<heptane> is a poor donor, but <intramolecular ligand trapping> avoids needing a donor solvent. Benzene has no pendant donor. Although the <methoxy group> has <oxygen> <lone pairs>, its directly attached <oxygen> does not have the same reach and favourable geometry as the projecting <ester> carbonyl <oxygen>; possession of a <lone pair> alone is insufficient. For these two substrates, <acetonitrile> can trap the unsaturated fragment through <nitrogen> to give $\mathrm{Cr}(\eta^6\!\text{-arene})(\mathrm{CO})_2(\mathrm{NCMe})$. Without a suitable trap, CO recombination can reverse the initial photochemical dissociation.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $z$ be normal to the Cp ring through its centre. The five <carbon> $p_z$ orbitals form <symmetry-adapted linear combinations> with angular patterns $m=0$, $|m|=1$, and $|m|=2$. The all-in-phase $a_1$ combination has sigma symmetry about this axis and can interact with $d_{z^2}$. The $e_1$ pair matches $(d_{xz},d_{yz})$ by pi symmetry, while the $e_2$ pair matches $(d_{xy},d_{x^2-y^2})$ by delta symmetry. Rotating within either degenerate pair changes the drawing but not the physical interaction.

For the usual eta-five geometry, **the <metal> $d$ interactions are ranked qualitatively pi > delta > sigma**. The $d_{xz},d_{yz}$ lobes overlap effectively with the occupied ring combinations. The delta interaction is weaker and involves the higher, initially unoccupied Cp combinations; the $d_{z^2}$ interaction with the ring is particularly weak because its axial and equatorial parts contribute little net overlap in this geometry. Thus the statement that a <sigma bond> is always stronger than a <pi bond> cannot be transferred indiscriminately to this delocalized ring–<metal> overlap problem. <metal> $s$ and $p_z$ orbitals also have the symmetry of the $a_1$ <coordination ligand> combination and contribute to overall bonding, so weak $d_{z^2}$ overlap does not mean the entire sigma-symmetry channel is absent.

In ionic <electron counting>, Cp is $\mathrm{C_5H_5^-}$ with six pi <electrons> in its three occupied ring combinations, hence **a six-electron donor**. The same <coordination ligand> is assigned five <electrons> in neutral counting.

= Solution
{parent=b}

In a <metallocene>, several orbitals with substantial <metal> $d$ character are <nonbonding molecular orbitals> or only weakly bonding or antibonding with respect to the strong Cp–<metal> framework. Adding or removing a small number of <electrons> from them can therefore change <oxidation state> or <spin> without destroying the principal ring–<metal> bonds. This allows stable compounds on either side of the 18-electron count: for example, the usual neutral counts for <chromocene>, <ferrocene>, <cobaltocene> and <nickelocene> are respectively **16, 18, 19 and 20**. The <18-electron rule> remains a useful reference, but occupation of a weakly <antibonding orbital> is much less disruptive than occupation of a strongly <antibonding orbital>. Redox stability also depends on geometry, <substituents> and reaction conditions, so this is a bonding explanation of the observed flexibility rather than a guarantee of equal stability for all counts.

Use neutral <electron counting> consistently: each terminal Cp supplies five <electrons>, while the eight pi <electrons> of neutral <pentalene> must be allocated across the shared fused framework rather than counted twice as two independent Cp <coordination ligands>. One set of **18-electron-consistent proposals** is:

* **Iron: syn $\mu$-$\eta^5:\eta^5$ <pentalene> with an Fe–Fe <single bond>.** The two iron–Cp fragments lie on the same face. Assigning four <pentalene> <electrons> to each iron gives $8+5+4=17$ before direct bonding; the Fe–Fe bond contributes one <electron> to each count, giving 18. Its direct bond naturally accounts for the especially short <metal>–<metal> separation. An anti arrangement cannot retain this short same-face bond.
* **Cobalt: $\mu$-$\eta^5:\eta^5$ <pentalene> without a Co–Co bond.** The count is $9+5+4=18$ at each <metal>. Syn and anti arrangements are possible at this formal level because there is no required direct bond. Removal of the bond relative to iron accounts for a larger separation.
* **Nickel: slipped $\mu$-$\eta^3:\eta^3$ coordination to the two outer allylic portions.** The fusion bond remains an uncoordinated <alkene> in this limiting bonding picture, while each outer three-carbon portion contributes three <electrons> in neutral counting: $10+5+3=18$. Equivalent ionic counting gives a nickel(II) $d^8$ centre, a six-electron Cp and a four-electron <allyl ligand>. Moving the <metal> binding sites toward the outer three-carbon portions increases the separation further. Syn and anti arrangements can again be drawn.

The original schematic shows the donor regions and the required iron–iron bond; dashed metal-to-region lines denote delocalized coordination rather than localized <carbon>–<metal> <single bonds>.

One equivalent of butyllithium removes one proton per hydrocarbon, giving the **hydropentalenyl monoanion $\mathrm{C_8H_7^-}$**. It does not give the doubly deprotonated <pentalenide>. <salt metathesis> with half an equivalent of iron(II) chloride therefore gives the sandwich complex
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{Fe}(\eta^5\!\text{-}\mathrm{C_8H_7})_2,\qquad d^6+6+6=18.}
$$
Each <coordination ligand> binds through its aromatic five-membered portion and retains an uncoordinated <double bond> in the fused ring. The orientation of this unsymmetrical fused appendage makes the two coordinated <coordination ligand> faces distinguishable. Pairing matching and opposite planar configurations gives **a racemic form, $(R_p,R_p)/(S_p,S_p)$, and a meso form, $(R_p,S_p)$**. This describes the two stereoisomeric classes, rather than assigning the two easily interconverting rotations of ordinary <ferrocene> as separate configurational isomers. The molecular structure and double-bond disorder of bis(hydropentalenyl)iron have been examined crystallographically.<Bis(η⁵-hydropentalenyl)iron by Elies Molins and others>[Acta Crystallographica C 52, 2414–2416: parallel coordinated rings and double-bond isomer disorder]{ref}

= Solution

Cleavage of the <disulfide> supplies one <methylthiolate> per cobalt. A monomer $\mathrm{CpCo(SMe)}$ would have only $7+6+2=15$ <electrons> by ionic counting. Dimerization with two bridging <thiolates> supplies a second <sulfur> interaction at each cobalt, and a direct Co–Co bond completes each count. Thus a suitable structure is **$[\mathrm{CpCo}(\mu\!\text{-}\mathrm{SMe})]_2$ with a Co–Co <single bond>**, containing an $\mathrm{Co_2S_2}$ diamond:
$$
\mathrm{Co}:\quad d^7+6\ (\mathrm{Cp})+2+2\ (\text{two S donors})+1\ (\mathrm{Co-Co})=18.
$$
Each cobalt is formally $+2$: Cp and <methylthiolate> each carry one negative charge. The stoichiometry is
$$
2\mathrm{CpCo(CO)_2}+\mathrm{MeSSMe}\longrightarrow[\mathrm{CpCo}(\mu\!\text{-}\mathrm{SMe})]_2+4\mathrm{CO}.
$$
The <molecular formula> $\mathrm{C_{12}H_{16}Co_2S_2}$ reduces to the required empirical formula. The <sulfur>–<sulfur> bond has been cleaved, both <sulfur> atoms now bridge the <metals>, and the loss of two CO molecules per cobalt is balanced. Formally cobalt(I) is oxidized to cobalt(II), while the <disulfide> is reduced to <thiolate>.

For the two-atom inorganic backbone unit, $Z_{\rm Si}+Z_{\rm O}=14+8=22$ and $Z_{\rm P}+Z_{\rm N}=15+7=22$. Their valence-electron sums also agree: $4+6=5+5=10$. Since each unit bears the same two organic groups, **$\mathrm{R_2SiO}$ and $\mathrm{R_2PN}$ are <isoelectronic species>**. This electron-count relationship does not require identical bond localization, bond angles or chemical reactivity.

= Solution
{parent=b}

In a <boroxine>, each <boron> has three <sigma bonds> and is approximately trigonal planar, with an $sp^2$ description for its sigma framework and an available perpendicular $2p$ orbital. <oxygen> can be described approximately as $sp^2$: two in-plane hybrids form B–O <sigma bonds>, one contains an in-plane <lone pair>, and a perpendicular $2p$ <lone pair> overlaps with neighbouring <boron> $2p$ orbitals. These three <oxygen> donor pairs supply a six-electron pi system around the alternating ring. The combination of good same-shell overlap and a low-strain six-membered ring favours planarity and partial B–O multiple-bond character. The bonds remain polar; a six-electron diagram alone does not establish benzene-like aromatic stabilization quantitatively.

In a <siloxane>, each <silicon> instead has four <sigma bonds>, two to <oxygen> and two to its organic groups, giving an approximately tetrahedral $sp^3$ sigma framework. <oxygen> has two <sigma bonds> and two <lone pairs>, so an approximately $sp^3$ localized description is a useful starting point, although actual Si–O–Si angles and lone-pair hybrids vary considerably. There is no comparable ring of vacant <silicon> $2p$ orbitals. A description based on strong oxygen-$2p$ to silicon-$3d$ <pi bonds> is also misleading: those orbitals have poor energy and radial matching. **Strongly polar Si–O sigma bonding, supplemented by <negative hyperconjugation> from <oxygen> <lone pairs> into appropriate Si–R <antibonding orbitals>, gives a better account**. The ring can therefore pucker rather than requiring continuous planar pi overlap. This follows the broader conclusion that strong main-group bonds and apparently expanded valence do not imply dominant valence $d$-orbital participation.<Chemical bonding in hypervalent molecules. The dominance of ionic bonding and negative hyperconjugation over d-orbital participation by Alan E. Reed and Paul von Ragué Schleyer>[JACS 112, 1434–1445: ionic contributions and negative hyperconjugation versus d-orbital models]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=c}

<boron> is sufficiently small for effective oxygen-$2p$/boron-$2p$ overlap, and three-coordinate planar <boron> benefits from the pi donation just described. A six-membered <boroxine> ring provides this overlap without severe angle strain. The larger <aluminium> centre has poorer oxygen-$2p$/aluminium-$3p$ matching and remains strongly Lewis acidic. It can lower its <electron> deficiency by accepting additional <oxygen> coordination, making a three-dimensional <aluminoxane> cage favourable. Four-coordinate <aluminium> does not require a boroxine-like planar pi system.

An appropriate **hexamer model is an alternating Al/O <hexagonal prism>**, with six <aluminium> and six <oxygen> vertices. Colour the twelve-vertex prism alternately Al and O: each <aluminium> then has three <oxygen> neighbours and an outward organic <substituent>, making it four-coordinate, while each <oxygen> bridges three <aluminium> centres. The graph has 18 Al–O edges, hence six Al centres with three Al–O bonds each. Different polyhedral connectivities and sizes can meet similar local coordination requirements, so the hexamer is a proposal rather than a claim that the empirical repeat unit fixes a unique cage.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Ring cleavage of <1,3,5-Trioxane> regenerates **<formaldehyde>**, whose strong <carbon>–<oxygen> <pi bond> stabilizes the monomer; in water much of it is further stabilized as its hydrate. <acetal hydrolysis>, particularly under acid catalysis, provides a route between the cyclic trimer and these monomeric forms.

The corresponding formal depolymerization of a <siloxane> would create **$\mathrm{R_2Si=O}$ <silanone> units**. Such ordinary small-substituent <silanones> have much less favourable pi bonding and are very reactive toward forming new Si–O–Si linkages. The connected <siloxane> is consequently preferred over a persistent aqueous solution of <silanone> monomers. This explains the intended contrast, but aqueous stability is conditional: acid or base can catalyse <siloxane> cleavage to <silanols>, and those <silanols> can condense again. Neither neutral-water inertness of every <siloxane> nor instantaneous neutral-water dissociation of every trioxane should be inferred.

First count the organic groups and then the unsatisfied <oxygen> valences. **A is the fully condensed <T8 silsesquioxane cage>, $\mathrm{Cy_8Si_8O_{12}}$**, with <silicon> at the eight vertices of a <cube> and bridging <oxygen> on its twelve edges. Each <silicon> has one <cyclohexyl group> and three <siloxane> bridges. Its eight <cyclohexyl groups> supply exactly $\mathrm{C_{48}H_{88}}$.

**B is a fully condensed <T10 silsesquioxane cage>, $\mathrm{Cy_{10}Si_{10}O_{15}}$**, for which a pentagonal-prismatic <silicon> skeleton with an <oxygen> on every edge is a standard compatible structure. Its ten groups give $\mathrm{C_{60}H_{110}}$. The formula establishes full condensation but does not, without more structural data, rule out every other possible ten-vertex cage connectivity.

**C is a corner-deficient <T7 trisilanol cage>, $\mathrm{Cy_7Si_7O_9(OH)_3}$**. Seven <cyclohexyl groups> account for $\mathrm{C_{42}H_{77}}$, leaving three hydrogen atoms as Si–OH groups. Remove one <silicon> corner of the T8 <cube> and replace the three severed connections by terminal hydroxyl groups. Nine <oxygen> atoms remain as bridges and three become terminal <silanol> <oxygen> atoms, giving precisely twelve <oxygen> atoms. There are three <silicon> sites bearing OH, three further equivalent fully condensed sites, and one opposite fully condensed site, consistent with **three <silicon> environments in a 3:3:1 site-count ratio**. The less negative resonance is naturally associated with the three silanol-bearing sites; the remaining two resonances belong to the two fully condensed classes. Peak heights alone are not quantitative integrations.

Treat the reacting <carbon> skeletons as an activated glycosyl donor and a 3-substituted <indole>. The new linkage is **an anomeric-carbon–indole-C2 <carbon>–<carbon> bond**, not a bond to <indole> <nitrogen>. A plausible purely chemical pathway is a dissociative <electrophilic aromatic substitution>:

* Activation of the anomeric diphosphate <leaving group>, for example by acid, permits cleavage of its bond to the <anomeric carbon>. The ring <oxygen> stabilizes the resulting <glycosyloxocarbenium ion>, represented by the resonance pair $\mathrm{O_{ring}-C_1^+\leftrightarrow O_{ring}^+=C_1}$. A nearby diphosphate counterion may remain in a <contact ion pair>.
* The <indole> C2–C3 <pi bond> attacks C1 through C2. This makes the new C1–C2 bond and gives an <arenium ion>, with C2 temporarily tetrahedral and still bearing its original hydrogen. Positive charge can be delocalized over the pyrrolic portion, including an indolium resonance contributor.
* A base removes that C2 hydrogen. The C2–H bond <electrons> restore the C2–C3 <pi bond> and <aromaticity>, releasing the <C-glycosylation> product while retaining N–H.

The drawn donor and product have opposite anomeric faces: the anomeric <leaving group> is on the same face as the hydroxymethyl <substituent>, while the new <carbon> <substituent> is on the opposite face. Thus the target has **net inversion at the <anomeric carbon>**. A tight <ion pair> with the departing group shielding its original face can rationalize opposite-face capture in this proposed pathway. A freely solvated planar <oxocarbenium ion> would not by itself guarantee exclusive inversion; competing faces, <hydrolysis> and other products remain possible without an experimentally specified chemical promoter. This is a plausible non-enzymatic mechanism, not a claim that the uncatalysed reaction is rapid or clean in neutral water.

The first <transition state> has a partially broken anomeric C1–O(<leaving group>) bond and developing oxocarbenium character, $[\mathrm{O_{ring}-C_1^{\delta+}\cdots O_{LG}}]^\ddagger$. Its following minimum is the <glycosyloxocarbenium ion> plus counterion. The second <transition state> has a forming C1–C2 bond, $[\mathrm{O_{ring}-C_1\cdots C_2(H)_{ind}}]^\ddagger$, and developing positive charge in the <indole>. Its following minimum is the covalently joined <arenium ion> with an intact C2–H bond. The third <transition state> has simultaneous C2–H cleavage, B–H formation and restoration of the <indole> <pi bond>, $[\mathrm{B\cdots H\cdots C_2-C_3}]^\ddagger$. It leads to the aromatic C-linked product and protonated base.
