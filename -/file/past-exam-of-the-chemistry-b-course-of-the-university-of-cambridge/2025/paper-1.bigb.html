Number the fused <heterocycle> as a 2,3-dihydrobenzofuran: <oxygen> is position 1 and the acetamido <substituent> occupies aromatic position 6. **Position 5 is the preferred site of <electrophilic substitution>.** It is para to the ring <oxygen> and ortho to the acetamido group, so both groups stabilize its <Wheland intermediate> by <resonance donation>. Position 7 also receives donation, but lies between the two <substituents> and is more crowded. Position 4 lacks the same combination of directing effects.

The aryl <oxygen> donates a <lone pair> into the aromatic system. The acetamido <nitrogen> can also donate into the ring, although its <lone pair> is shared with its own <carbonyl group>, so it is less activating than a free amino group. Comparing resonance-stabilized intermediates and steric accessibility gives the position-5 prediction; simply counting heteroatoms does not.

Substitution of one aromatic <hydrogen> by $\mathrm{C_2H_2ClO}$ gives the stated molecular formula. McGinnis and others>[Experimental section, N-(5-(2-chloroacetyl)-2,3-dihydrobenzofuran-6-yl)acetamide]{ref}

<GaCl3> activates <chloroacetyl chloride> by Lewis-acid coordination and chloride abstraction, represented by the acylium/contact-ion-pair limit $\mathrm{ClCH_2CO^+}$. The position-5 aromatic $\pi$ bond attacks the acyl <carbon>, giving a <Wheland intermediate>. Removal of the <proton> at that <carbon> restores <aromaticity>. Product–Lewis-acid coordination is reversed on work-up. The carbon-bound chlorine survives; the chlorine on the acyl <carbon> is the leaving group.

The first stage is a <Stork enamine reaction>. <Pyrrolidine> attacks the <ketone> <carbonyl>, and <proton> transfers give a <carbinolamine>. Loss of <water> gives an <iminium ion>; removal of the benzylic alpha <proton> produces the <enamine> conjugated with the <aromatic ring>. Removal of <water> and prolonged heating favour this condensation. That conjugated <enamine> is preferred over the alternative with its double bond on the other side of the original <carbonyl>.

The nucleophilic <enamine> <carbon> attacks the methylene <carbon> of bromoacetonitrile by an <SN2 reaction>, displacing bromide and making the <carbon>–<carbon> bond. It is the <carbon> beside the aromatic fusion that acquires $\mathrm{CH_2CN}$, as in D. Finally, <water> adds to the alkylated iminium <carbon>; <proton> transfers and cleavage of the C–N bond regenerate the <ketone> and release <pyrrolidine>. This explains <carbon> alkylation, its position, and restoration of the <carbonyl> rather than treating the amine as a permanent <substituent>.

Hydride removes the remaining alpha <proton> at the substituted benzylic <carbon>, releasing <hydrogen> and forming the conjugated <enolate>. Its <oxygen> attacks the <carbonyl> <carbon> of allyl chloroformate. Collapse of the <tetrahedral intermediate> expels chloride and gives the **allyl <enol carbonate>**, with the double bond between the original <carbonyl> <carbon> and the cyanomethyl-bearing <carbon>.

This is <O-acylation of an enolate>: the <carbonyl> <oxygen> is trapped as $\mathrm{OCO_2CH_2CH{=}CH_2}$, retaining the enol double bond. The competing carbon-centred resonance representation explains possible C-acylation in other circumstances, but it would give a different connectivity from E. The indicated product selects the oxygen-trapping pathway and the conjugated enolate regioisomer.

<Pyrrolidine> and 2-methylcyclohexanone first form an <enamine>, through <carbonyl> addition, <carbinolamine> formation, dehydration to an <iminium ion>, and alpha <deprotonation>. The less hindered <enamine> <carbon> is on the side opposite the methyl-bearing alpha <carbon>. It attacks the terminal beta <carbon> of <methyl vinyl ketone>, while the conjugated $\pi$ system shifts to <oxygen>. Protonation gives an alkylated iminium species; aqueous acid then hydrolyses it back to the <ketone>.

The result is a <Michael addition> product: the previously unsubstituted alpha <carbon> bears $-\mathrm{CH_2CH_2COCH_3}$. In drawing the arrows, start the new C–C bond at the <enamine> <carbon>, not at <nitrogen>. The iminium bond is restored during addition and removed by <hydrolysis>. The sequence is an enamine-mediated conjugate addition, rather than direct addition to the <methyl vinyl ketone> <carbonyl>.

A undergoes a <Robinson annulation>. For an unambiguous connectivity description, call the original ring <carbonyl> <carbon> C1 and the side-chain-bearing alpha <carbon> C6. Label the appended chain $\mathrm{C6{-}a{-}b{-}c(=O){-}d}$, where d is initially methyl. Base makes the enolate at d, which attacks C1 intramolecularly to close the six-membered ring $\mathrm{C1{-}C6{-}a{-}b{-}c{-}d}$. The <carbonyl> $\pi$ electrons move to <oxygen>, and protonation gives the beta-hydroxy <ketone>.

An <E1cb elimination> then removes the <proton> on d and the hydroxy group on C1, giving the conjugated enone **with C1=d and a <carbonyl> at c**. The original ring methyl remains on the <carbon> next to C1 on the other side. The two rings share the C1–C6 bond. The molecular formula is consistent with loss of one <water> molecule from the diketone.

The <aniline> <nitrogen> attacks the ipso <carbon> bearing <fluorine>. The aromatic $\pi$ electrons shift to give a <Meisenheimer complex>, with negative charge delocalized to the positions ortho and para to the attacked <carbon>. At either nitro <substituent>, that charge can be delocalized further onto nitro <oxygen>. Subsequent <proton> transfer and fluoride elimination restore <aromaticity> and give the diarylamine.

Both the ortho and para <nitro groups> stabilize the addition intermediate in 1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene. The para-mononitro compound lacks the additional ortho stabilization, so addition is less favourable and generally slower under the stated conditions. This comparison concerns stabilization of the addition pathway; the presence of <fluorine> does not make the reaction an ordinary aliphatic <SN2 reaction>.

The qualitative <nucleophilicity> order is **$p$-OMe $>$ $p$-Me $>$ H $>$ $p$-Cl $\gg$ $p$-NO$_2$**, matching the product-yield order at the stated stopping time. Electron donation raises the availability of the <aniline> <lone pair> for attack. A para <methoxy group> donates by resonance, while methyl is a weaker donor. Chlorine withdraws inductively and gives an <aniline> less nucleophilic than the unsubstituted compound. A para <nitro group> strongly withdraws by resonance: electron flow from <nitrogen> through the ring into the <nitro group> stabilizes a charge-separated form and greatly reduces <nucleophilicity>.

The key arrows for the nitroaniline comparison move the <nitrogen> <lone pair> into its own <aromatic ring>, shift the ring $\pi$ bonds toward the para <nitro group>, and shift a nitro N=O bond toward <oxygen>. The methoxy donor produces the opposite electronic bias. These are finite-time yields, so they support a kinetic explanation qualitatively; they are not by themselves measured <rate constants> or <equilibrium constants>.

Base deprotonates the <sulfonamide>. Its <nitrogen> attacks one <epoxide> <carbon> from the back, breaking the C–O bond at that <carbon> and producing an adjacent <alkoxide>. The <nitrogen> and <oxygen> attachments on the <cyclopentane> are therefore **trans**. The <alkoxide> then attacks the aromatic <carbon> bearing the <fluorine> ortho to the sulfonyl group. Intramolecular <nucleophilic aromatic substitution> expels fluoride and closes a seven-membered <heterocycle> with the path $\mathrm{Ar{-}S{-}N{-}C_1{-}C_2{-}O{-}Ar}$. The para <fluorine> remains.

This tandem epoxide-opening/ring-closing pathway is required by the formula of F: simple <epoxide> addition would retain both <fluorines>, whereas F contains only one. In the second stage, <pyrrolidine> replaces the remaining para <fluorine> by another <nucleophilic aromatic substitution>, producing G. The mass balance is F plus <pyrrolidine> minus HF, as required by the given formula of G.

The meso <epoxide> has <enantiotopic> ends. With achiral reagents, opening at either end gives an enantiomeric product, so **F is a racemic pair with the same trans relative configuration; G retains that configuration**.

The relevant quantity is the H–C–C–H <dihedral angle>, through the <Karplus relation>, rather than the labels cis and trans alone. Couplings tend to be larger near eclipsed or antiperiplanar arrangements and smaller nearer a right angle. The observed value averages the accessible conformations.

In A, the saturated five-membered ring is puckered. A preferred envelope-like <conformation> can place the trans <hydrogens> closer to an antiperiplanar arrangement, giving a slightly larger coupling than the cis pair. In B, the double bond restricts ring geometry toward a flatter arrangement: the cis <hydrogens> are more nearly eclipsed, whereas the trans pair has an oblique <dihedral angle>. The cis coupling is then larger. Approximate Newman projections illustrate these trends; exact angles cannot be recovered from four coupling constants without a calibrated relation and conformational populations.

Reduction makes <stereogenic centres> at C2 and C3 of the indan-1-one framework. The four products are the **cis pair $(2R,3S)$ and $(2S,3R)$**, and the **trans pair $(2R,3R)$ and $(2S,3S)$**. Each pair consists of <enantiomers>; any cis member is diastereomeric to either trans member. There is no meso product because the carbonyl-containing and aromatic sides of the ring do not make C2 and C3 symmetry-equivalent.

For checking the <Cahn-Ingold-Prelog priority rules>, at C2 the order is carbonyl-side <carbon> $>$ phenyl $>$ C3 $>$ H. At C3 it is fused aromatic <carbon> $>$ phenyl $>$ C2 $>$ H. The fused aromatic path outranks the separate phenyl path because it reaches the carbonyl-containing part of the framework.

At 400 MHz, a separation $\Delta\delta$ in ppm corresponds to $J=400\Delta\delta$ Hz. The two multiplets near 5.10 and 4.47 ppm each have a quoted separation of 0.02 ppm, giving **$J\simeq8$ Hz**. The multiplets near 4.625 and 3.865 ppm each have separation 0.01 ppm, giving **$J\simeq4$ Hz**. These are approximate values from the rounded peak labels.

The fused unsaturated/aromatic framework restricts the five-membered ring, as in the comparison in (a). Assign the larger coupling to **cis** and the smaller to **trans**. Matching equal couplings and integrals pairs the 5.10/4.47 signals with one another, and the 4.625/3.865 signals with one another. The integral ratio is
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{cis:trans}=0.7:1.0\simeq41:59}.
$$
With an achiral substrate and achiral catalytic environment, each <diastereomer> is expected as a racemic enantiomeric pair: cis $(2R,3S)/(2S,3R)$ and trans $(2R,3R)/(2S,3S)$. The reported spectrum requires both <diastereomers>; the reagent label alone cannot override that evidence. Their ratio does not establish whether the selectivity arose in the initial surface addition or after product <epimerization>.

Only the low-coupling pair remains, so the detected product is the **trans <diastereomer>**, with approximate coupling 4 Hz. The reaction is highly diastereoselective within the spectrum's detection limit. Its possible absolute configurations are $(2R,3R)$ and $(2S,3S)$.

A conventional <proton> NMR spectrum in an achiral environment cannot distinguish those <enantiomers>: they have the same shifts and couplings. Thus a chiral catalyst plus one set of resonances does **not establish an <enantiomeric excess> or an <absolute configuration>**. <Chiral chromatography>, an appropriate chiral NMR method, or a calibrated stereochemical comparison would be needed.

The products are the two mirror-image **2,3-epoxy-2,3-diphenylindan-1-ones**, shown in the bottom row of the stereoisomer diagram. The original <carbonyl> remains, and an additional <oxygen> bridges the former double bond, giving the stated formula.

In the <Weitz-Scheffer epoxidation>, hydroperoxide anion adds conjugatively to the enone. The resulting enolate closes onto the attached <peroxide> <oxygen> with O–O bond cleavage and loss of hydroxide. Attack on the two faces of the achiral planar substrate gives the two <enantiomers> in equal amounts. This is a racemic <epoxide> pair, rather than a one-to-one mixture of different diastereomeric <epoxide> connectivities.

Apply the <Felkin-Anh model> to the stereocentre alpha to the <ketone>. Its large group is the chain continuing through the phenyl-bearing <carbon>, its medium group is methyl, and its small group is <hydrogen>. In the preferred transition-state arrangement, put the large group approximately perpendicular to C=O and approach from the opposite face, passing the small <hydrogen> along a <Bürgi-Dunitz trajectory>. The remote <ether> cannot form the usual small alpha-alkoxy chelate, and the coordinating <solvent> further supports using an open-addition prediction.<Felkin-Anh and Cram Chelate by University of Windsor>[pp. 1–2: perpendicular large group and nucleophilic approach past the small group]{ref}

The predicted new stereocentre is **$S$**. In the question's zigzag projection, this corresponds to the new OH bond directed behind the page and the newly introduced methyl toward the reader, with the original stereocentres unchanged. The new-centre priority order is OH $>$ phenyl $>$ the substituted alpha-carbon chain $>$ methyl. This is a model-based major-product prediction, rather than a quantitative diastereomeric ratio.

Protonation of a <spiroacetal> <oxygen> makes the adjacent C–O bond easier to cleave. Reversible opening gives an <oxocarbenium ion>, in which the spiro <carbon> is locally planar. The tethered alcohol can attack either face; <deprotonation> closes the <acetal> and regenerates acid. Repetition interconverts the spiro epimers while retaining the other <stereogenic centres>. Methanol can participate in reversible <proton> transfer or trapping, but there is no need to invert the remote <carbon> centres.

The diagnostic feature is the large <vicinal coupling> characteristic of an axial–axial relationship in a chair. The td patterns for $H_a$ and $H_c$ contain two large couplings to neighbouring axial <hydrogens>, so both are axial. The ddd patterns for $H_b$ and $H_d$ also include a large ring <vicinal coupling>, with the smaller values reflecting the other ring <hydrogen> and the <side chain>. The assignments are
$$
\boxed{H_a,\ H_b,\ H_c,\ H_d\ \text{all axial}.}
$$
The <chemical shifts> alone do not determine this assignment. In particular, three unequal splittings of a methine are not a geminal-coupling pattern.

Use the upper-ring chair placing $H_a$ or $H_c$ axial. The aryl ester <substituent> on that <carbon> is then equatorial. Use a lower-ring chair placing $H_b$ or $H_d$ axial, with the attached <side chain> equatorial. The spiro configuration distinguishes the two structures: in **A**, the lower-ring <oxygen> bond is axial with respect to the upper ring, while the upper-ring <oxygen> bond is equatorial with respect to the lower ring. In **B**, those roles reverse.

Thus the <oxygen> placements at the shared spiro <carbon> are **axial/equatorial for A and equatorial/axial for B**. Side-chain conformations are unnecessary, but changing the lower chair without tracking its <substituent> positions would change this conclusion.

The <anomeric effect> is the preference, in suitable cyclic <acetals>, for an electronegative <substituent> at an anomeric <carbon> to occupy an axial position even when simple steric reasoning favours equatorial. An important orbital contribution is donation from an endocyclic <oxygen> <lone pair> into the <antibonding orbital> of the adjacent exocyclic C–O bond, $n_{\rm O}\to\sigma^*_{\rm C-O}$. The chair's axial arrangement supplies favourable antiperiplanar overlap. Electrostatic and <solvation> contributions can also affect the observed conformational preference.

**Each isomer has one favourable anomeric interaction.** A has the favourable axial exocyclic <oxygen> in the upper-ring chair; B has it in the lower-ring chair. The NMR-constrained conformations therefore do not provide a doubly anomeric versus non-anomeric comparison.

Both can keep important <carbon> <substituents> equatorial and each obtains one anomeric stabilization. **Appreciable amounts of both are therefore a reasonable prediction**, rather than a strong preference based solely on counting anomeric interactions. The environments of the two ring <oxygens> are not identical, so this argument does not determine an exact one-to-one equilibrium ratio; quantitative preference would require further energetic information.

For hydrogen-bond acceptance, the three strongest groups are **the terminal <carboxylate>**, **the Cys–CO–N(Pro) tertiary-amide <carbonyl>**, and **the Pro–CO–NH(Tyr) secondary-amide <carbonyl>**, in that qualitative order. Negative charge makes <carboxylate> a particularly strong acceptor. Both <amide> <oxygens> accept strongly; greater <nitrogen> alkyl donation generally makes a tertiary <amide> <oxygen> more basic than the comparable secondary-amide <oxygen>. This last ordering is a functional-group estimate, not a precise calculation of a folded <tripeptide>'s site parameters.

For donation, the order is **terminal ammonium $>$ <tyrosine> phenolic OH $>$ the peptide NH between Pro and Tyr**. The <thiol> is a weaker donor. Protonated ammonium has no available <lone pair> to accept; the <proline> <amide> <nitrogen> has no N–H bond to donate, and <amide> <nitrogen> <lone pairs> are delocalized into <carbonyl groups>. The <carboxylate> has no O–H donor in the drawn protonation state.

The <functional group interaction profile> compares donor strength $\alpha$ and acceptor strength $\beta$. In <water>, strong–strong contacts compensate for <desolvation>, giving the upper-right black region. Strong–weak contacts lose favourable <solvent> interactions without adequate replacement, giving the two grey regions. Weak–weak association gives the lower-left black region through the <hydrophobic effect>.

Ethanol's ethyl groups preferentially solvate weakly polar sites, removing this incentive for their association: the lower-left region becomes approximately neutral. Its hydroxyl group retains competition for <hydrogen bonds> elsewhere in the profile.<Functional group interaction profiles: a general treatment of solvent effects on non-covalent interactions by Mark D. There are three natural states: an unfolded single strand, a monomeric <DNA hairpin>, and an intermolecular self-duplex. A useful parsing of the sequence is $\mathrm{T\ |\ CGACT\ |\ CAAC\ |\ AGTCG}$. The two five-base arms are <reverse complements>. They can form a hairpin with a five-base-pair stem, a four-base CAAC loop, and a dangling 5′ T.

Two copies can instead pair their arms intermolecularly. The self-duplex has two five-base-pair regions, a central noncomplementary internal region, and dangling T residues; it is not a perfectly paired 15-base-pair duplex. Low temperature favours pairing. At relatively low strand concentration, the unimolecular hairpin avoids the association-entropy cost of a dimer; at sufficiently high concentration, intermolecular pairing becomes more favourable. At high temperature, the unfolded state predominates. Actual crossover temperatures require salt conditions and sequence thermodynamics.

The second strand is the complete <reverse complement> of the first. They can therefore form a **fully matched antiparallel 15-base-pair heteroduplex**, replacing the less fully paired hairpin/self-duplex states under conditions supporting hybridization:
$$
\begin{array}{rcl}
5'&\mathrm{TCGACTCAACAGTCG}&3'\\
3'&\mathrm{AGCTGAGTTGTCAGC}&5'.
\end{array}

The lower line is written antiparallel; read it from its own 5′ end to recover the added oligomer.

For a <carbonyl> <oxygen> of residue $i$ accepting from the NH of a later residue, the possibilities are:

* $i\to i+1$: a very small four-membered hydrogen-bonded loop, incompatible with a favourable repeating peptide-backbone geometry.
* $i\to i+2$: a seven-membered loop associated with a <gamma turn>; a long regular two-residue-per-turn structure is strongly constrained.
* $i\to i+3$: the ten-membered hydrogen-bonded rings of a <3-10 helix>, with about three residues per turn.
* $i\to i+4$: the thirteen-membered rings of an <alpha helix>, with about 3.6 residues per turn.
* $i\to i+5$: the sixteen-membered rings of a <pi helix>, with about 4.4 residues per turn.

\Image[paper-1-helix-projections.png]
{title=Top-view side-chain directions in 3-10, alpha and pi helices}

The <alpha helix> provides a favourable combination of allowed backbone torsions, reasonably linear <hydrogen bonds>, and side-chain packing. Its successive <side chains> advance by about $100^\circ$, rather than lining up in the three columns of the narrower <3-10 helix>. Smaller repeating rings impose more severe backbone constraints; the wider <pi helix> gives a different, generally less favourable packing arrangement. Short 3-10 and pi-helical segments can still occur in proteins, so this is a preference for a common extended secondary structure, not a prohibition of the alternatives. <Proline> and sequence-specific interactions can disrupt the idealized backbone donor pattern.
