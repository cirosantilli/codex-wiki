The <carbon> end of a <carbonyl ligand> has a filled $5\sigma$ <molecular orbital> with an outward-facing lobe. Its overlap with a vacant <metal> orbital of the same axial symmetry gives CO-to-<metal> <sigma donation>. Occupied <metal> $d_\pi$ orbitals can simultaneously overlap with the empty CO $2\pi^*$ <antibonding molecular orbitals>, giving <metal>-to-CO <pi backbonding>. Two perpendicular pi channels are possible; the schematic represents one. Orbital signs indicate phase, not <electric charge>.

\Image[paper-2-bonding.png]
{title=Complementary donation and backbonding in carbonyl and alkene coordination}

The two interactions reinforce one another: <sigma donation> transfers density toward the <metal>, and <pi backbonding> distributes it into the ligand acceptor orbitals. **Both strengthen M–C attachment, while backbonding weakens C–O.** Occupation of CO $\pi^*$ decreases the internal C–O <bond order>, generally increasing its <bond length> and lowering its stretching <spectroscopic wavenumber>. This is why a strongly backbonded <carbonyl ligand> can have a strong <metal>–<carbon> bond and a weakened internal <carbon>–<oxygen> bond at the same time.

A suitable structure is a triangular Ti$_3$ <metal cluster> with one <1,2-bis(dimethylphosphino)ethane> chelate and one terminal <chloride> at each <titanium>, and one <bridging ligand> $\mu$-Cl spanning each edge. Include a direct Ti–Ti single <metal-metal bond> along each edge. The last panel distinguishes these direct bonds from the <chloride> bridges.

The six chlorides have total formal charge $-6$, so equivalent centers are **Ti(II), $d^2$**. At each <titanium> the two <phosphorus> donors supply four <electrons>, the terminal <chloride> supplies two, and the two incident <chloride> bridges each donate a pair in ionic <electron counting>. Two incident Ti–Ti single bonds supply another two <electrons>:
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{VE(Ti)}=2_d+4_{\mathrm{P,P}}+2_{\mathrm{Cl}}+2(2)_{\mu\mathrm{-Cl}}+2_{\mathrm{Ti-Ti}}=14.}
$$
Each $d^2$ center can contribute one <electron> to each of its two edge bonds, making three bonds from six <metal> <electrons> overall. **This is a 14-<electron> early-<metal cluster>, not an 18-<electron> complex.** Forcing the <18-electron rule> here would invent excessive bond orders. <chloride> bridges donate to more than one center, so summing local counts is not the same as counting distinct <electrons> of the whole cluster.

The principal electronic variable is the net population of <metal>–<metal> <bonding molecular orbitals> relative to <antibonding molecular orbitals>. For otherwise comparable complexes, a larger <bond order> generally gives a shorter <metal-metal bond>; removing an antibonding <electron> can shorten a bond even though the total <electron> count falls. Different orbital symmetries also matter: sigma, pi and delta overlaps respond differently to distance and rotation of the <metal> fragments.

<metal> size, <oxidation state> and radial orbital extent set the underlying distance scale. Comparing a first-row <metal> with a larger heavier congener does not isolate <bond order>. Increasing positive charge often contracts the <metal> orbitals, but can also change <electron> occupancy, ligand binding and electrostatic interactions. The <ligand field> and donor/acceptor properties of the surrounding <ligands in coordination chemistry> determine which <metal> orbitals remain available for direct bonding and how much density instead participates in <pi backbonding>.

Finally, the geometry can enforce or oppose the preferred separation. <Bridging ligands> impose bond-angle and bond-length constraints; bulky substituents produce <steric hindrance>; crystal packing can perturb the observed distance. **A <bond length> is evidence for bonding only in its electronic and structural context.** In particular, two bridges can hold <metals> close without proving a high direct <bond order>, while a sterically congested formal single bond can be unusually long.

For the doubly bridged tetracarbonyl, each <iron> has one terminal <carbonyl ligand>, two <bridging carbonyl ligands> and one <cyclopentadienyl ligand>. Neutral <electron counting> gives $8+5+2+2=17$ before direct bonding, so a single Fe–Fe bond gives 18 at each <iron>. For the triply bridged tricarbonyl, each <iron> instead has $8+5+3=16$ before direct bonding; formal completion to 18 requires a double Fe–Fe bond. Equivalently, the total fragment counts before <metal>–<metal> bonding are 34 and 32 respectively.
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Cp_2Fe_2(CO)_2(\mu\! -CO)_2}:&\quad d_{\mathrm{Fe-Fe}}=2.539\ \text{Å},\quad\text{formal single bond},\\
\mathrm{Cp^{*}_2Fe_2(\mu\! -CO)_3}:&\quad d_{\mathrm{Fe-Fe}}=2.265\ \text{Å},\quad\text{formal double bond}.
\end{aligned}}

The increased direct <bond order> explains the substantial contraction. The <pentamethylcyclopentadienyl ligands> also alter steric and electronic effects, and bridge geometry changes, so this is not a controlled measurement of <bond order> alone. Nevertheless, the markedly shorter distance in the less carbonyl-saturated dimer is consistent with stronger direct Fe–Fe bonding. **The dimer has two unpaired <electrons> and a triplet ground state, $S=1$.** Its approximately threefold-symmetric Fe$_2$(CO)$_3$ core supplies a pair of degenerate frontier <molecular orbitals>. The last two <electrons> occupy separate members with parallel <spins>, favored by exchange, rather than pairing in one. The formal 18-<electron> count does not require every <molecular orbital> to be doubly occupied. It is therefore compatible with an open-shell dimer. In the ionic assignment, both tetrahedral anions would require Mn(VII), $d^0$. Such a high <oxidation state> is strongly oxidizing and is best supported by strong bonding to <oxygen>. Short Mn–O bonds combine good <orbital overlap> with <oxygen>'s resistance to further oxidation. This stabilizes <permanganate> relative to a hypothetical all-<sulfide> analogue.

<sulfide> is larger, more polarizable and much easier to oxidize than <oxide>. An attempted Mn(VII) <sulfide> would therefore combine an exceptionally oxidizing <metal> center with reducing ligands: internal <redox> can reduce <manganese> while oxidizing <sulfur>, for example toward S–S-bonded or elemental <sulfur> products. The larger <sulfur> donors also provide less favorable short multiple bonding to the small, highly charged <manganese> center. **The <oxygen> compound supports Mn(VII); the <sulfide> combination is <redox>-incompatible.** This predicts instability without requiring a specific unprovided decomposition product.

For an octahedral complex, strong axial <sigma donation> raises the <metal>–ligand antibonding $e_g$-derived orbitals. A <hydride> is a very strong sigma donor: its occupied $1s$ orbital overlaps effectively along a <metal>–ligand axis and has no filled perpendicular p orbitals to act as a conventional pi donor. It can therefore produce a large <octahedral splitting> by raising $e_g$ relative to $t_{2g}$.

<Triphenylphosphine> also gives <sigma donation>, but its useful acceptor orbitals additionally receive <metal> <pi backbonding>. This lowers the <metal> $t_{2g}$-derived levels and enlarges their separation from $e_g$. The acceptors have substantial P–C antibonding character; invoking a dominant <phosphorus> $3d$ acceptor is unnecessary. Thus **<hydride> is strong-field principally through sigma bonding, while PPh$_3$ combines <sigma donation> with pi acceptance**. The <spectrochemical series> describes the resulting splitting, not just a ranking of ligand basicity.

The <metal> is Cr(II), $d^4$. A <chloride ligand> produces a relatively weak <ligand field>, so the octahedral center is a <high-spin complex> with $t_{2g}^3e_g^1$, $S=2$. The singly occupied $e_g$ pair is orbitally degenerate: $d_{z^2}$ and $d_{x^2-y^2}$ cannot retain identical occupancies once the distortion selects one. This is a strong setting for the <Jahn-Teller effect>.

On tetragonal elongation, the two axial Cr–Cl distances increase. Repulsion is reduced most for $d_{z^2}$, so it falls relative to $d_{x^2-y^2}$; $d_{xz},d_{yz}$ also fall relative to $d_{xy}$. The single $e_g$ <electron> occupies the lower $d_{z^2}$-derived level. The electronic stabilization offsets an elastic cost until a distorted equilibrium geometry is reached. **The high-<spin> $d^4$ occupancy drives an elongated, rather than regular, octahedron.** The three lower-set <electrons> remain one per orbital in this simple diagram; it is primarily the unequal $e_g$ occupancy that produces the strong distortion.

**The all-carbonyl <hydride>, HCo(CO)$_4$, is more acidic.** Loss of $\mathrm H^+$ gives $[\mathrm{Co(CO)_4}]^-$, whose negative charge is stabilized by <pi backbonding> into four <carbonyl ligands>. Replacing one CO by <triphenyl phosphite> reduces the net <electron>-withdrawing character of the ligand set relative to four carbonyls. The substituted <hydride> is more <electron> rich and its conjugate-base anion is less stabilized against <protonation>.
$$
\mathrm{HCo(CO)_3L}\rightleftharpoons\mathrm H^++[\mathrm{Co(CO)_3L}]^-,\qquad L=\mathrm{CO}\ \text{or}\ \mathrm{P(OPh)_3}.
$$
Both <conjugate bases> have a conventional 18-<electron> count, so the difference is not a missing-<electron> argument. It is the relative stabilization of acid and base by the <ligands in coordination chemistry>. This concerns heterolytic <deprotonation>, not the homolytic Co–H <bond-dissociation energy>.

For comparable octahedral environments with the same donor set, the expected qualitative order is
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{Mn^{2+}<Fe^{2+}<Fe^{3+}<Mo^{3+}<Rh^{3+}<Pt^{4+}}.}
$$
Across comparable first-row ions, the increasing effective attraction and changing <metal>–ligand distances tend to increase <octahedral splitting>, giving Mn(II) below Fe(II). Raising the charge on the same <metal> gives Fe(III) above Fe(II), because the ligand interaction generally strengthens and <metal>–ligand distances contract. Moving from $3d$ to more radially extended $4d$ orbitals gives larger <metal>–ligand overlap, placing Mo(III) above Fe(III); Rh(III) lies further along the same $4d$ series and typically gives the larger splitting. The $5d$ ion Pt(IV) combines the heavier row with high charge and gives the largest splitting here.

**This is a matched-environment trend, not an intrinsic list of constants for bare ions.** Actual $\Delta$ also depends strongly on donor identity, geometry and distance. It would be meaningless to compare an unspecified weak-field tetrahedral compound with a strong-field octahedral compound solely through this ordering.

Start from an <octahedral molecular geometry> with ligands on $\pm x,\pm y,\pm z$, with $e_g=(d_{z^2},d_{x^2-y^2})$ above $t_{2g}=(d_{xy},d_{xz},d_{yz})$. Remove the $-z$ donor while keeping the other five positions. Loss of axial repulsion lowers $d_{z^2}$ substantially; $d_{x^2-y^2}$ still points directly at four basal donors and remains highest. Loss of an axial donor also lowers the orbitals with $z$ dependence relative to $d_{xy}$. The remaining fourfold axis keeps $d_{xz}$ and $d_{yz}$ degenerate.

The usual qualitative <square-pyramidal molecular geometry> diagram, shown in the last panel above, is therefore
$$
\boxed{d_{x^2-y^2}\ >\ d_{z^2}\ >\ d_{xy}\ >\ d_{xz}=d_{yz}.}
$$
The corresponding $C_{4v}$ labels are $b_1,a_1,b_2,e$. Energies can be re-zeroed to the new orbital barycenter after ligand removal. Symmetry fixes the degeneracy and labels, but does not universally fix the separation of $d_{z^2}$ and $d_{xy}$: a weak or distant axial donor can reverse this middle pair on approach to the square-planar limit. The displayed ordering assumes an appreciable fifth axial interaction.

Treat the two donors of <2-picolylamine> as distinct: $P$ denotes pyridyl N and $A$ denotes amino N. Each ligand must join two adjacent octahedral positions because its five-membered <chelate ring> cannot span a trans pair. The three $P$ sites can either occupy a triangular face, giving a <facial isomer>, or a meridian containing a trans pair, giving a <meridional isomer>. Each geometry has a pair of nonsuperposable mirror images. Therefore **there are four configurational <stereoisomers>: fac-$\Delta$, fac-$\Lambda$, mer-$\Delta$ and mer-$\Lambda$**. Rapid ring puckering or amine inversion does not introduce additional persistent configurations in this count.

\Image[paper-2-isomers.png]
{title=Distinct fac and mer arrangements of an unsymmetrical bidentate ligand; each has a mirror partner}

**The mer geometry is the reasonable steric prediction for the more stable form.** It spreads the bulky pyridyl rings farther apart, allowing one pyridyl–pyridyl trans relationship, whereas the fac geometry groups all three pyridyl donors on one face. This is a qualitative preference, not a proof independent of <counterions> and solvent: hydrogen bonding and packing can favor the alternative. Primary structures of different salts contain both geometries, so neither should be claimed impossible.<Spin Crossover in [Fe(2-Picolylamine)3]2+ Adjusted by Organosulfonate Anions by Xin-Hua Zhao and others>[2015, pp. 7857–7867, mer structures in compounds 1 and 2 and fac structure in compound 3]{ref}

<iron> remains Fe(II), $d^6$, in both states; the change is a <spin crossover>, rather than a change in <oxidation state>. At the higher <temperature> the <high-spin complex> has $t_{2g}^4e_g^2$: four <unpaired electrons>, $S=2$, and
$$
\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}=\sqrt{n(n+2)}\,\mu_B=\sqrt{24}\,\mu_B=4.90\,\mu_B.
$$
This matches the observed moment. At low <temperature> a <low-spin complex> with $t_{2g}^6e_g^0$ has $S=0$ and shows <diamagnetism>. **Cooling switches high-<spin> $d^6$ to low-<spin> $d^6$.** Emptying the <metal>–ligand antibonding $e_g$ orbitals strengthens and shortens the Fe–N bonds, accounting for the contraction by roughly $0.2\ \text{Å}$.

The <octahedral splitting> and <electron pairing energy> must be sufficiently close for both states to be accessible. The low-<spin> state gains electronic energy, while the high-<spin> state has greater <spin> degeneracy and usually greater vibrational <entropy> because of its softer bonds. The resulting <Gibbs free energy> balance favors high <spin> at higher <temperature>. The two endpoint observations do not by themselves specify <hysteresis> or transition cooperativity; those require a <temperature> series. Coupled structural and magnetic measurements distinguish this explanation from a mere bond-length anomaly.

The filled <alkene> $\pi$ <molecular orbital> donates into a vacant <metal> acceptor orbital directed toward the center of the C=C bond. In the reverse direction, a filled <metal> $d$ orbital can overlap with the <alkene> $\pi^*$ <antibonding molecular orbital>. These are the donation and <pi backbonding> components of the <Dewar-Chatt-Duncanson model>, illustrated in the lower part of the bonding schematic. The <alkene> is an $\eta^2$ <alkene ligand>, attached through both carbons. Increasing backbonding weakens and lengthens C=C and can push the bonding toward a metallacyclopropane description.

**Zr(IV) is $d^0$, so <alkene>-to-<metal> donation predominates.** It has suitable empty orbitals but no occupied <metal> $d$ set to provide substantial conventional backbonding. An <electron>-deficient cationic zirconocene can consequently bind and activate an <alkene> largely through its Lewis acidity; it should not be drawn as a strongly backbonded late-<metal> <alkene> merely because its formal coordination mode is $\eta^2$.

Write $R$ for the current growing chain. <Propene> first binds at the available site of $[\mathrm{Cp_2ZrR}]^+$, giving a coordinated <alkene> adjacent to the <metal>-bound chain. In the <Cossee-Arlman mechanism>, the chain migrates to the substituted <alkene> <carbon> while the terminal <carbon> forms the new Zr–C bond:
$$
[\mathrm{Cp_2Zr{-}R}]^++\mathrm{CH_2{=}CHMe}
\rightleftharpoons[\mathrm{Cp_2Zr(R)(\eta^2\! -CH_2{=}CHMe)}]^+
\longrightarrow[\mathrm{Cp_2Zr{-}CH_2{-}CH(Me){-}R}]^+.
$$
The <electron-pair arrows> for insertion take the old Zr–R bond toward the substituted <alkene> <carbon> and the <alkene> <pi bond> toward the new Zr–terminal-<carbon> bond. A four-center arrangement permits both bonds to change in one <migratory insertion>; Zr remains Zr(IV). The product again has an accessible site, so coordination and insertion can repeat.

**Predominant 1,2 insertion leaves a primary Zr–CH$_2$ chain end and generates head-to-tail <polypropylene>.** It avoids the more crowded alternative secondary Zr–CH(Me) end and unfavorable methyl contacts with the <cyclopentadienyl ligands> in the insertion geometry. This explains <regioselectivity>. An unbridged, unsubstituted zirconocene does not by itself establish a fixed <tacticity>; the schematic alignment of methyl groups in a <polymer> drawing is not evidence for an isotactic product.

A plausible explanation is a stronger, less labile Hf–C interaction, reflecting the different bonding of the heavier <metal>. Breaking and reorganizing that interaction in <migratory insertion> can raise the propagation barrier and reduce the insertion rate. At the same time, stronger retention of the <metal>-bound alkyl can suppress <beta-hydride elimination> or other <chain transfer in chain polymerization> processes even more strongly.

The chain length depends on competing rates, not on propagation speed alone. In a simple stationary competition,
$$
\overline X_n\ \sim\ \frac{k_p[\mathrm{propene}]}{k_{\mathrm{tr,eff}}},
$$
where the denominator includes the operative chain-transfer or chain-stopping rates per active chain. **The Hf catalyst can propagate more slowly yet make longer chains if transfer is suppressed by a still larger factor.** Slower insertion alone would not explain longer <polymers>. Distinguishing particular transfer pathways would require <polymer> end-group analysis and the concentration dependence of molecular mass and <polymerization> rate.

In <borazine>, each B and N is approximately trigonal planar. The alternating ring contains a sigma framework with perpendicular p orbitals; the <nitrogen> <lone pairs> can donate into the boron acceptor orbitals. Six pi <electrons> can therefore be distributed around the ring, giving partial B–N multiple-bond character rather than six ordinary isolated single bonds. Equal ring distances are compatible with this symmetric delocalization.

The <electronegativity> difference makes each B–N bond strongly polar, $\mathrm{B^{\delta+}{-}N^{\delta-}}$. The occupied pi orbitals are weighted toward N, and the empty acceptors toward B. In <benzene>, equivalent <carbon> centers give much more even pi distribution and strong resistance to addition that destroys the aromatic system. **<borazine> resembles <benzene> geometrically but has substantially more polarized, more reactive bonding.** It presents electrophilic boron sites to <nucleophiles> and <nitrogen> donors to <Lewis acids>. Polar reagents can add across B–N, and <water> can cleave the ring; <benzene> ordinarily favors substitution under suitable conditions rather than facile polar addition or <hydrolysis>. The label “inorganic <benzene>” should therefore not be interpreted as identical aromatic stabilization or reactivity. <nitrogen>-bound Lewis-acid adducts and hydrogen-halide additions demonstrate this contrast experimentally.<Aluminium and gallium trihalide adducts of 2,4,6-triorganylborazines and the synthesis of triorganylborazinium tetrabromoaluminates by B. Gemünd and others>[2008, p. 136 introduction and pp. 137–142, addition chemistry and nitrogen-bound adducts]{ref}

The methyl <nucleophile> attacks an electrophilic ring boron. That boron becomes tetrahedral, bearing its original methyl, the incoming methyl, and its two ring <nitrogen> neighbors. This interrupts its p-orbital participation, so the <metal> attaches to the remaining five-atom conjugated segment. **$A$ is the anionic $\eta^5$ heptamethylated-ring Cr(CO)$_3$ complex**, paired formally with $[\mathrm{MgBr}]^+$; solvent ligation and ion pairing at magnesium are not specified by the spectrum. The original connectivity, with the attacked boron labeled $B_1$, is shown in the first panel below.

\Image[paper-2-borazine.png]
{title=Structures inferred from nucleophilic addition, Lewis-acid binding and B–Br oxidative addition}

There are seven methyl groups. The two attached to tetrahedral $B_1$ are inequivalent because the <chromium>-bound face distinguishes their environments: one signal of relative integral one for each. The other two B–Me groups are equivalent to one another, giving a signal of integral two. The two N–Me groups adjacent to $B_1$ form another equivalent pair, while the N–Me opposite $B_1$ is unique. The predicted methyl-group multiplicities are therefore **$1,1,2,2,1$**, the same unordered set as the observed ratio. Multiplying by three gives proton integrals totaling 21. This assignment assumes no fast process interchanging the two $B_1$ methyl environments.

The six-<electron> neutral $\eta^6$ ring donor has become an anionic six-<electron> $\eta^5$ donor, so Cr(0) retains an 18-<electron> count: $6_d+6_{\mathrm{ring}}+3(2)_{\mathrm{CO}}=18$. This <borazine dearomatization> explains both the changed <hapticity> and the extra methyl without requiring attack at <chromium> or loss of a carbonyl.

**$B$ is a 1:1 <nitrogen>-bound adduct, with $\mathrm{N\rightarrow AlBr_3}$ at one ring <nitrogen>.** Its <lone pair> is diverted from N-to-B pi donation into the new coordinate bond. That <nitrogen> becomes approximately tetrahedral and the ring can pucker. The two B–N bonds adjacent to it lose pi character and lengthen; the neighboring unbound <nitrogen> atoms can donate more strongly into those borons, shortening the next pair of B–N bonds. The remaining pair forms a third class. The second structural panel marks these three two-bond sets; it represents connectivity and bond classes, not a measured geometry.

In the slow-exchange structure, two boron atoms flank the bound <nitrogen> and are equivalent to one another, while the third boron is unique. This gives **two $^{11}$B environments in a $2:1$ ratio**. At room <temperature>, rapid exchange of the <Lewis acid> among the three equivalent <nitrogen> sites averages every boron over the same environments, giving one <boron-11 nuclear magnetic resonance> signal. Cooling slows exchange relative to the resonance-frequency difference and resolves the two environments.

This is <Chemical exchange in NMR>, not restoration of equal instantaneous B–N bonds. A spectrum alone cannot distinguish intramolecular migration from reversible dissociation and reassociation; concentration dependence and crossover experiments would test that distinction. Related N-bound <borazine> adducts have been characterized by diffraction and variable-<temperature> <NMR>, supporting the structural logic while not supplying the exact <temperature> dependence of this particular hexamethyl compound.<Aluminium and gallium trihalide adducts of 2,4,6-triorganylborazines and the synthesis of triorganylborazinium tetrabromoaluminates by B. A ring-retaining bromination replaces the three hydridic B–H positions while preserving N–H. Its net stoichiometry is
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{B_3H_3N_3H_3}+3\mathrm{Br_2}\longrightarrow\mathrm{B_3Br_3N_3H_3}+3\mathrm{HBr}.}
$$
Use controlled bromination and conditions allowing elimination of hydrogen bromide from addition intermediates, rather than identifying the first <bromine> adduct as the final ring product. Boron-bound hydrogen has <hydride>-like character because boron is less electronegative than hydrogen; its replacement is consistent with electrophilic attack by <bromine>. The primary preparative work reports B-tribromoborazines obtained by bromination of <borazine> and its N-methyl analogue.<B-Tribromoborazines from the Bromination of Borazine and N-Trimethylborazine by Reed F. Riley and Carl J. Schack>[1964, pp. 1651–1653, bromination and formation of B-tribromoborazines]{ref}

**The product must retain the unsaturated B$_3$N$_3$ ring.** Merely adding three HBr molecules to <borazine> initially gives a saturated hydrogen-halide addition product, not the required formula. Addition followed by suitable elimination is distinct from simply asserting direct B–H protonolysis by HBr.

<oxidative addition> inserts Pt into one B–Br bond. All three B–Br positions of the starting ring are equivalent, so there is no distinct choice among them before the first substitution. The product is **the square-planar Pt(II) boryl bromide $\mathrm{trans\! -[Pt(PCy_3)_2(Br)(B_3Br_2N_3H_3)]}$**, with the two phosphines trans, the Pt–B bond trans to Pt–Br, and the six-membered ring intact. The third panel shows this connectivity. This is a <metal>–boron <sigma bond>, not attachment of the whole ring as an $\eta^6$ pi ligand. Formal <electron counting> gives Pt(II), $d^8$, with $8+4_{\mathrm P}+2_{\mathrm{Br}}+2_{\mathrm{boryl}}=16$ <electrons>, appropriate for square-planar coordination. The primary synthesis establishes this trans-bromo(borazinyl) structure.<Synthesis of η1-borazine complexes of palladium and platinum by Holger Braunschweig and others>[2008, pp. 3531–3534, oxidative addition products and their structural characterization]{ref}

The <electron>-rich Pt(0) reacts at the polarized B–Br bond, whose electrophilic boron and departing bromide provide a favorable bond-activation pathway. This accounts for B functionalization without needing cleavage of a ring B–N bond or selection of a <nitrogen>-bound product. It does not imply that arbitrary N–H activation is impossible under every condition.

Replacing an electronegative <bromine> substituent by a <metal> fragment changes the boron's <electron> demand. Relative to the B–Br sites, the <metal>-bound boron is less effective at accepting N-to-B pi donation, so its adjacent B–N bonds are expected to have less multiple-bond character and be longer; donation toward the remaining B–Br sites produces compensating unequal ring bonds. The original threefold equivalence is lost. The detailed lengths depend on the full electronic structure; a localized drawing with three rigid alternating double bonds would conceal the redistribution that the unequal lengths reveal.

In the <chromium> complex, Cr(0) is $d^6$ and receives substantial donation from four <carbonyl ligands>. Its occupied orbitals can give <pi backbonding> into the boron-rich acceptor orbitals of the B$_2$N$_2$ ring, while the <nitrogen>-rich occupied ring orbitals donate to the <metal>. This favors binding across the ring face, approximately $\eta^4$, with all four ring atoms participating. A nearly planar ring maintains the p-orbital alignment needed for that interaction and accounts for comparable Cr–B and Cr–N distances. A four-<electron> ring donor also gives the useful count $6_d+8_{\mathrm{CO}}+4_{\mathrm{ring}}=18$.

Ti(IV) in TiCl$_4$ is $d^0$ and strongly <electron> deficient. It is a good acceptor for the two <nitrogen> <lone pairs>, but has no filled $d$ set for comparable backbonding into the boron acceptors. **<chromium> therefore favors a face-bound pi complex, while <titanium> favors N,N sigma coordination.** Donation makes the <nitrogen> environments more nearly tetrahedral and permits the <2,4-diazadiboretidine> ring to pucker. The borons are not directly bound in the same way, explaining $d(\mathrm{Ti}\cdots\mathrm B)>d(\mathrm{Ti{-}N})$. The distinction follows from <electron> availability, rather than simply calling every short <metal>–ring contact a separate ordinary <sigma bond>.

The reactive fragment is the C6 amidine-like portion of the <purine> ring; the N9–<ribose> attachment is retained. Write it locally as $\mathrm{N1{=}C6(NH_2){-}}$, with the rest of the fused ring understood. One productive <acid catalysis> route protonates N1, making C6 more electrophilic. <water> then adds at C6 while the C6=N1 pi <electrons> move onto N1. Subsequent <proton transfers> generate a <carbinolamine> and then a protonated amino leaving group:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{N1{=}C6(NH_2)}&\xrightarrow{\ +H^+\ }\mathrm{N1H^+{=}C6(NH_2)}\\
&\xrightarrow{\ +H_2O\ }\mathrm{N1H{-}C6(OH_2^+)(NH_2)}\\
&\xrightarrow{\ -H^+\ }\mathrm{N1H{-}C6(OH)(NH_2)}\\
&\xrightarrow{\ +H^+\ }\mathrm{N1H{-}C6(OH)(NH_3^+)}\\
&\xrightarrow{\ -NH_3\ }\mathrm{N1H{-}C6({=}OH^+)}\\
&\xrightarrow{\ -H^+\ }\mathrm{N1H{-}C6({=}O)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
These are local bonding fragments: C6 retains its bond to the adjoining ring <carbon> at every step. In the addition step, an <oxygen> <lone pair> forms C6–O and the C6=N1 <pi bond> opens. During collapse, an <oxygen> <lone pair> reforms C6=O and the C6–N6 bond breaks toward the departing <nitrogen>. Solvent mediates each <proton transfer>; no direct unsupported transfer across a long distance is required.

**<water> supplies the new C6 <oxygen>, and <ammonia> is expelled after <protonation> of the leaving group.** Final <deprotonation> gives the <lactam> form of <inosine>. In acidic bulk solution the released <ammonia> is largely trapped as <ammonium>. The catalytic <proton transfers> regenerate acid around the substitution cycle; <ammonium> trapping separately affects the net proton balance. This is <hydrolytic deamination>, not cleavage of the <glycosidic bond>.

A <glutamic acid residue> supplies a carboxyl group whose <protonation> state can change with the <active site> environment. The following five schematic contributions are possible in a hypothetical <enzyme>; they are alternatives and overlapping physical effects, rather than five independently established reactions of this <enzyme>. In the diagrams $E$ is the protein tether, and $C6$ is the reacting <purine> <carbon>.

\Image[paper-2-glutamate.png]
{title=Five possible catalytic contributions of a glutamate side chain to hydrolytic deamination}

**<general acid catalysis>:** $\mathrm{E{-}CO_2H\cdots N1\to E{-}CO_2^-+H{-}N1^+}$. Donation of the carboxyl proton activates the amidine-like C6 center for <water> attack. The same acid could instead assist the amino leaving group: $\mathrm{E{-}CO_2H+{-}NH_2\to E{-}CO_2^-+{-}NH_3^+}$. These are two locations for <general acid catalysis>, rather than two different categories.

**<general base catalysis>:** $\mathrm{E{-}CO_2^-\cdots H{-}OH\to E{-}CO_2H+OH^-}$, coupled to <oxygen> attack on C6. Accepting a proton makes <water> a more effective <nucleophile>. This does not require an appreciable pool of free <hydroxide> in bulk solution.

**<covalent catalysis>:** a glutamate <oxygen> can in principle attack activated C6 to form $\mathrm{C6{-}O{-}C(O){-}E}$. After addition/elimination replaces the amino group, <water> must hydrolyze that transient <enzyme>–<substrate> bond to release product and regenerate $\mathrm{E{-}CO_2H/CO_2^-}$. Such a pathway is a chemically distinct possibility, not established by the given structure.

**<electrostatic catalysis>:** the <carboxylate> can stabilize a developing positive charge, for example in a protonated <purine> or amino leaving group; a protonated carboxyl can also donate a <hydrogen bond> to an <oxygen> developing negative charge. This contributes only if the reacting configuration is stabilized relative to the bound starting state. A <carboxylate> placed next to an <oxyanion> without compensating geometry would instead be unfavorable.

**<proximity and orientation catalysis>:** donor and acceptor <hydrogen bonds> can hold the <purine> and attacking <water> in a productive arrangement, schematically $\mathrm{E{-}CO_2H\cdots N1{-}C6\cdots OH_2}$. Binding reduces unproductive orientations and can distort the <substrate> toward a reactive geometry. This last contribution is sometimes described through <substrate> binding or strain; a binding interaction is not automatically catalytic unless it improves access to the <transition state>.

The zinc ion is coordinated by protein donors and a <water> <oxygen> placed near the reacting C6 center. **Zinc acts as a <Lewis acid> that activates and positions <water>.** Its positive charge withdraws <electron density> from <oxygen>, lowers the effective p$K_a$ of coordinated <water> and facilitates formation of the attacking <hydroxide>, potentially with assistance from a nearby base. The same <metal>–<oxygen> interaction can stabilize developing negative charge as the addition intermediate forms. Zinc stays Zn(II); this is <metal ion catalysis>, not a zinc <redox> cycle.

The structure identifies a plausible reaction geometry, but a static X-ray model does not by itself establish the <water>'s <protonation> state, proton-transfer order or the rate-limiting step. Structural work with bound transition-state analogues independently supports a zinc-containing catalytic center.<Atomic structure of adenosine deaminase complexed with a transition-state analog: understanding catalysis and immunodeficiency mutations by David K. Wilson and others>[1991, pp. 1278–1284, catalytic zinc and the bound transition-state analogue]{ref} An appropriate experimental test would compare native <enzyme>, a <metal>-depleted preparation and stoichiometrically zinc-reconstituted <enzyme>, checking retained fold and active <enzyme> concentration. Loss and recovery of activity with those controls distinguishes a catalytic <metal> requirement from nonspecific denaturation.

A chemically coherent mechanism combines zinc-activated <water> addition with a <proton relay> involving His238 and Glu217. In a simple route consistent with the displayed contacts, neutral His238 accepts a proton from zinc-bound <water> while protonated Glu217 donates to <substrate> N1. These transfers can be coupled to the bond-forming event rather than requiring accumulation of a free, doubly activated <substrate>. Zinc-bound <hydroxide> attacks C6; the C6=N1 pi <electrons> move onto N1, yielding the tetrahedral C6 intermediate bearing OH and NH$_2$ while N1 bears H.

\Image[paper-2-enzyme.png]
{title=One proton-relay mechanism consistent with water-derived oxygen and the catalytic residues}

Next, the exocyclic amino group receives a proton, through His238, Glu217 and/or intervening <water> as permitted by the changing geometry. The <oxygen> is deprotonated as it forms the C6 carbonyl, and the C6–N6 bond cleaves to release <ammonia>. These transfers can be coupled in the collapse step, avoiding expulsion of $\mathrm{NH_2^-}$. Zinc stabilizes the reacting <oxygen> and the organized intermediate; the ring regains conjugation in the <inosine> product. Product dissociation, fresh <water> binding and proton exchange with solvent reset the catalytic groups.

**The core result is zinc-assisted addition–elimination, with acid/base <proton transfers> and <water> supplying C6 <oxygen>.** The contributions are <metal ion catalysis>, <general base catalysis>, <general acid catalysis>, <electrostatic catalysis> and <proximity and orientation catalysis>. The proposed route does not require <covalent catalysis> by a protein side chain: a <substrate>–<water> C–O bond is not an <enzyme>–<substrate> covalent linkage. Asp296 can retain a structural hydrogen-bonding role without acting as an indispensable acid/base.

The data establish the importance of His238 and Glu217 but do not uniquely assign the entire <proton relay>. In particular, an alternative has Glu217 first deprotonate <water> and then deliver that proton to N1, with His238 assisting <hydroxide> stabilization and later amino-group <protonation>. The primary observation that His238 substitution need not abolish analogue hydration cautions against calling its initial-base role proven.<Site-directed mutagenesis of histidine 238 in mouse adenosine deaminase: substitution of histidine 238 does not impede hydroxylate formation by V. Sideraki and others>[1996, pp. 15019–15028, retained hydroxylate formation and proposed electrostatic contribution]{ref} pH-rate profiles, solvent isotope effects and time-resolved intermediate experiments would discriminate these refinements. The diagram is a proposed catalytic route, not a structure directly observed by the crystal experiment.
