Six inward-directed donor orbitals form <symmetry-adapted linear combinations> of $a_{1g}+e_g+t_{1u}$ symmetry. They mix respectively with <metal> $4s$, $(3d_{z^2},3d_{x^2-y^2})$ and $4p$ orbitals. Thus the sigma framework contains six occupied <bonding molecular orbitals> and six corresponding <antibonding molecular orbitals>. The <metal> $(d_{xy},d_{xz},d_{yz})$ orbitals have no sigma-compatible partner and remain nonbonding $t_{2g}$ orbitals in this model. The important metal-dominated <octahedral splitting> is
$$
\boxed{\Delta_o=\varepsilon(e_g^*)-\varepsilon(t_{2g}).}
$$
The $e_g^*$ orbitals point directly toward the donors; the $t_{2g}$ orbitals point between them. The original diagram shows the entire sigma framework, rather than treating the five <metal> d levels as the whole molecular-orbital diagram.

\Image[paper-2-orbitals.png]
{title=Sigma-only octahedral orbitals and the donation/backbonding channels of end-on dinitrogen}

For the same donor set and approximately the same geometry, **$\Delta_o(\mathrm{Co^{III}})>\Delta_o(\mathrm{Co^{II}})$**. The higher <metal> charge usually shortens the <metal>–donor distances and strengthens the interaction that raises $e_g^*$. This comparison holds other chemical variables fixed: changing the ligand at the same time could reverse a comparison between two unrelated complexes.

Neutral <pyridine> contributes no <formal charge>. If $n$ of the two <dioxolene ligands> are dianions, the combined ligand charge is $-2-n$, so the <cobalt> <oxidation state> is $2+n$. The distinct charge assignments are therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{metal}&\text{two dioxolene ligands}\\ \hline
\mathrm{Co^{II}}&L^-+L^-\\
\mathrm{Co^{III}}&L^-+L^{2-}\\
\mathrm{Co^{IV}}&L^{2-}+L^{2-}
\end{array}}
$$
Interchanging the two ligand labels in the middle row does not make another charge combination. Co(IV) belongs in this unrestricted charge-balance list even though the subsequent magnetic analysis excludes it.

A Co(III) centre has $d^6$, and charge balance requires one closed-shell <catecholate ligand> and one $S=1/2$ <semiquinonate ligand>. For low-spin Co(III), $t_{2g}^6(e_g^*)^0$, the <metal> has $S=0$, giving an <independent-spin magnetic moment> of
$$
\mu_{\rm eff}/\mu_B=2\sqrt{0+\tfrac12(\tfrac12+1)}=\sqrt3=1.732.
$$
Thus **$A^1$ contains low-spin Co(III), $S_{\rm Co}=0$**, with its observed moment residing on the <free radical> ligand. High-spin Co(III), $t_{2g}^4(e_g^*)^2$, would have $S=2$ and give $\sqrt{27}=5.20$ under the stated independent-spin approximation, so it is inconsistent with this measurement.

For Co(II), $d^7$, charge balance instead requires two $S=1/2$ <semiquinonate ligands>. High-spin Co(II) has $t_{2g}^5(e_g^*)^2$ and $S=3/2$, so
$$
\mu_{\rm eff}/\mu_B=2\sqrt{\tfrac32\tfrac52+2\left(\tfrac12\tfrac32\right)}=\sqrt{21}=4.583.
$$
Therefore **$A^2$ contains high-spin Co(II), $S_{\rm Co}=3/2$**. Low-spin Co(II), $t_{2g}^6(e_g^*)^1$, would give $\sqrt9=3.00$ when the two ligand <spins> are included. These four checks distinguish both <oxidation state> and <metal> <spin>.

The larger <octahedral splitting> at Co(III) exceeds the effective <pairing energy> and favours a <low-spin complex>; the smaller <octahedral splitting> at Co(II) favours a <high-spin complex> with occupation of both $e_g^*$ orbitals before further pairing. Interconversion also transfers an <electron> between <cobalt> and a <dioxolene ligand>, so this is <valence tautomerism>, rather than a metal-only <spin> change. Squared moments add here because the supplied model treats the centres as independent; adding the three <spins> first would be a different, exchange-coupled model.

<water> lies below <pyridine> and <cyanide ligand> in the <spectrochemical series>. At fixed <metal> and dioxolene charges, replacement by <water> decreases $\Delta_o$ and favours the high-spin limits: **Co(II) remains $S=3/2$; Co(III) can become $S=2$ if the splitting falls below the pairing threshold**. Replacement by <cyanide> increases $\Delta_o$ and favours **Co(III), $S=0$, and Co(II), $S=1/2$**. The two unchanged <semiquinonate ligands> would then give the Co(II) form a combined independent-spin moment of $3\mu_B$. The ranking predicts the direction of the change; two substituted donors alone do not guarantee a Co(III) <spin crossover>, since all six donors determine its splitting. A shift of the valence-tautomer equilibrium is also possible and is separate from this fixed-charge comparison.

Treat each <semiquinonate ligand> as a symmetric O,O <chelate>, with the <free radical> delocalized over the dioxolene framework. The ideal octahedral donor-connectivity structures are **one trans form and two mirror-related cis forms**, giving **three <stereoisomers>**. Trans refers to the two <pyridine> N donors, not to the two O atoms of a <chelate>, which must remain adjacent.

\Image[paper-2-isomers.png]
{title=The achiral trans arrangement and the two mirror-related cis donor arrangements}

In the diagram, coloured curves join the two O donors belonging to one ligand. For an unambiguous coordinate specification, the trans form has Py at $+z,-z$, with <chelate> pairs $(+x,+y)$ and $(-x,-y)$. A cis pair has Py at $+z,+x$, with <chelates> $(-z,+y)$ and $(-x,-y)$; reflecting $y\mapsto-y$ gives the other cis form. Proper rotations cannot superpose those two cis arrangements. Ring <conformations> and <pyridine> rotations are not extra donor-connectivity <isomers>.

The Co(III) centre in $A^1$ has $t_{2g}^6(e_g^*)^0$: every occupied d subset is orbitally balanced. **There is no first-order metal-centred Jahn–Teller driving force in $A^1$**. This does not require all six experimental <bond lengths> to be equal, since <pyridine> and dioxolene are different donors.

The Co(II) centre in $A^2$ has $t_{2g}^5(e_g^*)^2$. Its two $e_g^*$ orbitals each contain one <electron>, so that subset gives no first-order preference. The $t_{2g}$ hole does give a weaker Jahn–Teller instability. To compare tetragonal distortions, take a positive small splitting parameter $\delta$ and retain each subset's <orbital energy barycentre>. On axial elongation let $d_{xz},d_{yz}$ shift by $-\delta$ and $d_{xy}$ by $+2\delta$. Five <electrons> then give
$$
\Delta E_{\rm elong}=4(-\delta)+1(2\delta)=-2\delta.
$$
For equal-magnitude compression the shifts reverse, and filling the lower $d_{xy}$ orbital first gives
$$
\Delta E_{\rm compress}=2(-2\delta)+3(\delta)=-\delta.
$$
The two singly occupied $e_g^*$ orbitals contribute zero net shift in either case. Thus **the elementary tetragonal splitting model favours elongation for high-spin $d^7$**, rather than equal electronic stabilization by the two signs. In a strictly <sigma-only octahedral molecular orbital model> $t_{2g}$ is nonbonding, so this weak effect is absent; it arises when the electrostatic and non-sigma interactions neglected in that diagram are restored. <spin-orbit coupling>, unequal donors and <chelate> constraints can modify the actual distortion, so the argument does not establish which experimental Co–donor bonds must lengthen.

Four equivalents of base remove the four carboxylic-acid protons from two ligand molecules, giving two dianionic <pyridine-2,5-dicarboxylate> linkers per Pt/Mn pair. The ring <nitrogen> and neighbouring 2-carboxylate supply a five-membered N,O <chelate>. A consistent chain has **Pt(II), N$_2$O$_2$, with <square planar molecular geometry>, and Mn(II), O$_6$, with <octahedral molecular geometry>**.

At each Pt, two ligands bind through their ring N and one O of the adjacent 2-carboxylate; their remote 5-carboxylates point toward successive Mn sites. At each Mn, a remote <carboxylate> from each of two neighbouring Pt-containing units binds through both O atoms. Two coordinated <water> molecules complete Mn's six-coordinate sphere. The repeating connectivity, with each bracket denoting a single organic linker, is
$$
\cdots\mathrm{Pt}\;\underbrace{\bigl[\kappa^2(N,O)\!:\!L^{2-}\!:\!\kappa^2(O,O)\bigr]}_{\text{one linker}}\;\mathrm{Mn(H_2O)_2}\;\bigl[\kappa^2(O,O)\!:\!L^{2-}\!:\!\kappa^2(N,O)\bigr]\;\mathrm{Pt}\cdots .
$$
Each Pt has a second N,O <chelate> extending in the opposite chain direction; each Mn similarly receives two <carboxylate> <chelates>. The original donor-connectivity diagram makes those counts explicit.

\Image[paper-2-chain.png]
{title=A charge-balanced Pt–Mn chain model with N,O chelates at Pt and oxygen donors at Mn}

Pt(II) is $5d^8$ and strongly favours <square planar molecular geometry>. Its affinity for the pyridyl N donor and the <chelate effect> select the mixed N,O pocket. Mn(II) is high-spin $3d^5$, is harder, and favours the <carboxylate> and <water> O donors; six-coordinate <octahedral molecular geometry> is natural in <water>. Charge balance per repeat is $+2+2+2(-2)=0$. A suitable repeat formulation is $\mathrm{[PtMn(L)_2(H_2O)_2]_n}$. This is a justified proposed coordination chain, not a unique crystal structure established from <stoichiometry> alone; <water> content, <carboxylate> bridging modes and actual porosity require structural characterization.

Six neutral <ammonia> donors leave Cu(II), $d^9$, so

In ionic counting, two $\eta^5$ cyclopentadienyl <anions> donate $12$ <electrons>. Therefore **<ferrocene>, <cobaltocene> and <nickelocene> contain 18, 19 and 20 <valence electrons>**, respectively: Fe(II) is $d^6$, Co(II) $d^7$ and Ni(II) $d^8$. <ferrocene> fills the bonding and approximately nonbonding levels while leaving the next <metal>–ring antibonding pair empty. <cobaltocene> and <nickelocene> add one and two <electrons> to that antibonding set.

Increasing antibonding occupation lowers the net <metal>–ring <bond order>. It therefore explains the simultaneous **increase in M–C distance and decrease in dissociation enthalpy from Fe to Co to Ni**. The trend does not follow the shrinking size of the isolated <metal> <ions>; the orbital occupation is decisive here. The specified enthalpy separates the entire sandwich into a gaseous <metal> dication and two gaseous ring <anions>. It measures that complete ionic dissociation process, not ten independent localized M–C bond energies, so dividing it by ten is not a justified bond-energy determination.

Oxidizing <cobaltocene> removes its antibonding nineteenth <electron> and gives the particularly favourable 18-electron <cobaltocenium> <cation>. <iodine> can accept the <electrons>, giving **<cobaltocenium> iodide** at the simple <stoichiometry>
$$
\boxed{2\mathrm{CoCp_2}+\mathrm I_2\longrightarrow2[\mathrm{CoCp_2}]^+\mathrm I^- .}
$$
Each <cobalt> changes from Co(II), $d^7$, to Co(III), $d^6$; each <iodine> atom changes from 0 to $-1$. Excess <iodine> can change the isolated counterion to a polyiodide such as $\mathrm I_3^-$. In contrast, oxidation of 18-electron <ferrocene> would remove an <electron> from its occupied closed-shell manifold to form 17-electron ferrocenium. <iodine> is insufficient to drive that reaction under the conditions implicit here. <ferrocene> can still be oxidized by suitable stronger oxidants; an <electron> count is a useful rationale for the comparative redox behaviour, not a universal proof of a numerical electrode potential.

The anionic <nitrogen> donor replaces iodide to give
$$
\boxed{A=\mathrm{CpFe(CO)_2(\kappa N\!\text{-}C_4H_4N)},\qquad \text{byproduct}=\mathrm{KI}.}
$$
Here Cp denotes $\eta^5$ cyclopentadienyl and $\kappa N$ specifies attachment through the ring <nitrogen> of the pyrrolyl <anion>, $\mathrm{C_4H_4N^-}$. Fe(II), $d^6$, receives six <electrons> from Cp, four from the two CO ligands and two from an N-bound <pyrrolyl ligand>: $6+6+4+2=18$.

Two coupled CO stretching modes, approximately symmetric and antisymmetric combinations, account for the two strong carbonyl-region absorptions. The bent two-CO arrangement of this <piano-stool complex> permits both combinations to be infrared active. They are not evidence for two different chemical products.

Heating expels **two CO molecules** and changes the binding of the <pyrrolyl ligand> from $\eta^1$ to $\eta^5$, giving the sandwich **<azaferrocene>**,
$$
\boxed{B=\mathrm{Fe(\eta^5-C_5H_5)(\eta^5-C_4H_4N)}.}
$$
The four <electrons> lost with CO are replaced by the four additional <electrons> donated on changing the <hapticity> of the <pyrrolyl ligand>. Thus $B$ is also Fe(II), $d^6$, and 18-electron: $6+6+6=18$. Each complex has one time-averaged Cp proton environment, two pairs of protons from the <pyrrolyl ligand>, one adjacent to N and one farther from N. Hence **three proton environments, with integrals $5:2:2$**, fit both structures. For <proton nuclear magnetic resonance>, three signals means three <chemical shift> environments; the individual resonances can still show coupling.

Choose $z$ along M–N–N. The occupied dinitrogen $3\sigma_g$ orbital, with amplitude on the terminal N atoms, overlaps an empty <metal> orbital of sigma symmetry to give **N$_2$-to-metal <sigma donation>**. Filled <metal> $d_{xz}$ and $d_{yz}$ orbitals separately overlap the two empty N$_2$ $\pi_g^*$ orbitals, giving **metal-to-N$_2$ <pi backbonding>**. The lower-energy combination of each interacting pair is bonding and the higher is antibonding; each donation channel starts with two <electrons> in the occupied fragment orbital. The lower panel of the original orbital diagram shows these three channels.

\Image[paper-2-orbitals.png]
{title=Orbital-symmetry channels for sigma donation and two perpendicular pi-backbonding interactions}

As for a terminal <carbonyl ligand>, the donor and acceptor interactions reinforce one another: <sigma donation> helps make <electron density> available at the <metal>, while <pi backbonding> strengthens M–N and weakens N–N. For conventional <electron counting> an intact end-on neutral N$_2$ ligand is an L-type two-electron donor; counting the backbonded <electrons> a second time as extra donated ligand <electrons> would double count them.

Both coordinated frequencies are substantially lower than that of free N$_2$. In a local harmonic model,
$$
\widetilde\nu={1\over2\pi c}\sqrt{k\over\mu},\qquad
\boxed{{k_A\over k_{\rm free}}\simeq\left({2021\over2359}\right)^2=0.734,
\quad {k_B\over k_{\rm free}}\simeq\left({2010\over2359}\right)^2=0.726.}
$$
Thus coordination lowers the approximate N–N stretching force constant by about **27%**, with B marginally more activated than A by this measure. Donation into N$_2$ pi-antibonding orbitals provides the natural explanation: the M–N$_2$ bond has an important backbonding component and N–N loses some multiple-bond character.

The same qualitative red shift occurs for CO when metal-to-CO backbonding populates CO $\pi^*$ orbitals. N$_2$ is generally a poorer pi acceptor than CO, so low-valent electron-rich <metal> fragments are particularly helpful for stabilizing its complexes. A numerical claim that N$_2$ always shifts more or less than CO would require a matched <metal>/ligand comparison. Neither the two frequencies nor the local-force approximation establish integer N–N <bond orders>, complete N–N cleavage, or catalytic <nitrogen> conversion; they establish significant weakening of intact coordinated N$_2$.

Use ionic <electron counting>, counting each ordinary donor atom once and keeping ligand charge distinct from <denticity>. **A is 16-electron, B is 14-electron and C is 16-electron**, so none is an 18-electron complex under this convention.

A contains a modified, monoanionic PNP pincer: the exocyclic double bond and altered pyridine-ring bond pattern in the drawing matter. Its two phosphines and its central N donor provide six <electrons> altogether, while neutral N$_2$ provides two. Neutrality then gives Co(I), $d^8$, and $8+6+2=16$. Treating the modified pincer as a neutral intact <pyridine> ligand would incorrectly produce Co(0) and a 17-electron count. A four-coordinate approximately <square planar molecular geometry> in a $d^8$ complex can leave the strongly sigma-antibonding orbital empty and be stable at 16 <electrons>. The underlying modified-pincer <chemistry> has been examined using structure, spectroscopy and electronic-structure calculations.<Four-Coordinate Cobalt Pincer Complexes: Electronic Structure Studies and Ligand Modification by Homolytic and Heterolytic Pathways by Scott P. Semproni and others>[JACS 136, 9211–9224; modified PNP framework and dinitrogen derivative]{ref}

B has a neutral tertiary <amine> donor at the centre of its tripod and three anionic amido arms. Their combined charge is $-3$, so Re is Re(III), $d^4$. The four N donors give eight <electrons> and N$_2$ gives two: $4+8+2=14$. Such an <electron> count is not inherently unexpected in an amido-supported complex: strong amido <pi donation> and the imposed five-coordinate geometry alter the orbital energies, and the empty orbitals are not nine equivalent slots that must all be filled. Steric protection and <chelation> also help stabilize this coordinatively restricted structure. The 14-electron figure uses the usual two-electron ionic count for each amido donor; pi bonding should not be appended to it inconsistently.

C has an anionic acyl <carbon> donor and a neutral phosphine in the <chelate>, together with two neutral PMe$_3$ donors. Thus Co(I), $d^8$, receives $2+2+4=8$ ligand <electrons>, giving $8+8=16$. This is again a reasonable four-coordinate $d^8$ structure. The acyl C=O is part of a carbon-bound <acyl ligand>, not an additional free CO donor.

**Step 1 is <oxidative addition> of Si–H to Co(I), giving Co(III)–H and Co(III)–SiR$_3$.** It cleaves the Si–H bond and forms two <metal>–ligand bonds, increasing the formal <metal> <oxidation state> by two. The phosphine dissociation shown before this numbered step makes room for the reaction; <alkene> binding and phosphine exchange are ligand-substitution events rather than <oxidative additions>.

**Step 2 is <migratory insertion> of the coordinated <alkene> into Co–H**, equivalently intramolecular <hydride> migration to the <alkene>. One <alkene> <carbon> gains H and the other gains the Co–C bond. Co remains formally Co(III); a two-electron <alkene> ligand plus a <hydride> is replaced by one alkyl ligand, reducing the <electron> count by two before phosphine reassociation.

**Step 3 is C–Si <reductive elimination>** between the alkyl and <silyl ligands>. It releases the hydrosilylated alkane and regenerates Co(I). The <electron> count for the drawn sequence is consistent: the one-PMe$_3$ <alkene> adduct is 16-electron; <oxidative addition> gives 18; insertion gives 16; PMe$_3$ association gives 18; <reductive elimination> returns the 16-electron <catalyst>. Because insertion occurs into Co–H, this is the conventional <Chalk-Harrod mechanism>.

<oxidative addition> of an intact Si–H bond initially places **H and SiR$_3$ cis** in the <metal> coordination sphere; this is the local arrangement generated by addition of a <sigma bond>. <hydride> and <alkene> must also attain adjacent sites for intramolecular insertion, and the eventual alkyl and silyl groups must be cis for direct <reductive elimination>. Subsequent rearrangements should not be confused with the <stereochemistry> of the bond-addition event itself.

For an alkyl-substituted terminal <alkene>, insertion puts **Co on the terminal <carbon> and H on the substituted <carbon>**, producing $\mathrm{Co{-}CH_2CH_2R}$. It avoids the more crowded secondary <metal>–alkyl arrangement. Replacement of that Co–C bond by C–Si therefore gives **the linear product $\mathrm{RCH_2CH_2SiR_3}$**, an <Anti-Markovnikov addition> outcome. Hydrometallation is syn at the coordinated <alkene>, although this simple terminal substrate generally does not retain two substituents whose relative <stereochemistry> could diagnose the complete sequence.

The opposite insertion orientation gives a benzylic <metal>–<carbon> bond, $\mathrm{Co{-}CH(Ph)CH_3}$. C–Si <reductive elimination> then gives the branched product
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{PhCH(SiR_3)CH_3}.}

A plausible explanation is stabilization of the developing and final benzylic Co–C interaction by the adjacent aromatic pi system, which can outweigh the steric preference for a primary alkyl. That makes an electronically different choice from an ordinary alkyl substituent. This is a mechanistic rationale for the proposed cycle, not proof of its rate-determining step. In the absence of a chiral <catalyst> or another source of asymmetric induction, the new stereogenic <carbon> would give a <racemic mixture>.

**P$_4$ is a tetrahedron of four <phosphorus> atoms, with a P–P bond along every edge.** Each P has three P–P <sigma bonds> and one <lone pair>. The <phosphorus> centre is approximately tetrahedral, with three P–C <sigma bonds> and one short terminal P–O bond. Both $\mathrm{Me_3P{=}O}$ and $\mathrm{Me_3P^+{-}O^-}$ are useful Lewis contributors; **the bond is strongly polar and has additional multiple-bond character**. <oxygen> lone-pair donation into suitable P–C antibonding combinations provides a description of the additional stabilization without requiring a substantial localized phosphorus-3d/oxygen-2p <pi bond>. **$\mathrm{OP(OMe)_3}$ also has approximately <tetrahedral molecular geometry> at <phosphorus>**, now with three P–O–C sigma linkages and a terminal phosphoryl O. Only the latter is being compared with the terminal O of Me$_3$PO; the three P–O–C bonds are chemically distinct.

The electronegative alkoxy substituents withdraw <electron density> from P and improve its capacity to accept donation from terminal O into the surrounding antibonding framework. The elementary substituent argument therefore predicts **a stronger, stiffer terminal P–O bond in $\mathrm{OP(OMe)_3}$ than in $\mathrm{Me_3PO}$**. A description invoking polar bonding and <negative hyperconjugation> avoids assigning a literal localized d-orbital double bond solely because the formula is written P=O.

Measure the terminal P–O stretching vibration by infrared or <Raman spectroscopy> under comparable, non-hydrogen-bonding conditions. In an approximate local-mode comparison the <reduced mass> is the same, so a larger terminal stretching wavenumber indicates a larger force constant, $k\propto\widetilde\nu^2$. Mode assignment can be checked by $^{18}$O substitution at the terminal <oxygen> or by a normal-mode calculation; otherwise an alkoxy P–O–C vibration could be compared by mistake. X-ray diffraction can supply a supporting bond-length comparison, but neither <bond length> nor a single coupled vibrational frequency directly measures an isolated bond-dissociation enthalpy.

The larger value for the carbonyl bond means that its dissociation costs **$167\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ more** for the supplied comparison. A carbonyl combines a strong <sigma bond> with efficient pi overlap between spatially compatible C 2p and O 2p orbitals. This gives a substantial localized multiple-bond contribution.

The short P–O linkage in a <phosphine oxide> is also strong, but it is more polar, with a substantial $\mathrm{P^+{-}O^-}$ contribution. P 3p and O 2p orbitals have less favourable radial and energy matching than C 2p/O 2p for a simple localized <pi bond>. <oxygen> donation into P–R antibonding orbitals adds stabilization, but it is not identical to the carbonyl <pi bond>. Therefore **the formal double-bond notation does not imply equal bond strength**. A substantial P-3d contribution is not needed to explain the bonding. The stated bond-dissociation values compare complete dissociation processes, so their magnitudes should not be derived solely from one overlap integral or from the two atoms' electronegativity difference.

Join three trigonal BO$_3$ units corner to corner. The resulting **alternating six-membered B–O ring has one terminal <oxygen> on each boron**:
$$
\boxed{\left[\mathrm{{-}B(O^-){-}O{-}B(O^-){-}O{-}B(O^-){-}O{-}}\right]^{3-}_{\rm cyclic}.}
$$
The superscript records the total charge; the three explicit O$^-$ labels each carry one share of it. Atom counting gives three B, three bridging O and three terminal O. Boron is three-coordinate and approximately trigonal planar. The original structure diagram shows the ring connectivity and terminal atoms.

A planar arrangement permits the perpendicular empty boron p orbitals to accept <pi donation> from suitable <oxygen> <lone pairs>. The terminal <oxygens> can donate effectively toward one boron, giving their B–O linkages more multiple-bond character and explaining why they are shorter than bridging B–O linkages. A bridging <oxygen> shares its interactions between two boron centres. **An approximately planar ring is therefore expected** from the trigonal units and favourable orbital alignment. This reasoning does not require describing the entire <anion> as benzene-like with an assumed six-electron <aromatic ring>; the <oxygen> <lone pairs> and terminal donations must actually be counted before making an <aromaticity> claim.

Three PO$_4$ tetrahedra share corners in a ring. Each <phosphorus> has two ring-bridging <oxygens> and two terminal <oxygens>, so
$$
\boxed{\left[\mathrm{{-}P(O)(O^-){-}O{-}P(O)(O^-){-}O{-}P(O)(O^-){-}O{-}}\right]^{3-}_{\rm cyclic}}
$$
is one conventional Lewis representation of <trimetaphosphate>. Its three P and nine O atoms consist of three bridging and six terminal O atoms. Within each phosphate unit the two terminal bonds have delocalized character; drawing one P=O and one P–O$^-$ is not a claim that every free <anion> has permanently different terminal <oxygens>.

**A puckered six-membered P–O ring is expected.** The tetrahedral <phosphorus> geometry and P–O–P torsional preferences allow folding without losing a required continuous perpendicular-p orbital system. This differs from the planar trigonal-boron arrangement. Tetrahedral P alone is not a geometrical proof of nonplanarity, since its two ring bonds could lie in a plane; the argument is that puckering is energetically accessible and relieves conformational strain without sacrificing a strong planar pi-conjugation benefit. The precise chair-like or boat-like distortion depends on environment and counterions.

Label the <carbon> next to the carbonyl $C_\alpha$ and the OH-bearing <carbon> $C_\beta$. The three limiting routes are **E1, E1cb and <E2 elimination>**. They differ in whether C$_\beta$–O cleavage, C$_\alpha$–H cleavage, or both occur in the first elementary event. In the following original transition-state structures, dotted lines denote changing bonds, $\delta$ marks developing partial charge, and the bracketed structures carry $\ddagger$. Unmarked skeletal bonds remain present. The dotted line parallel to C$_\alpha$–C$_\beta$ denotes the developing <pi bond>, in addition to its existing <sigma bond>.

\Image[paper-2-elimination.png]
{title=The five transition states in E1, E1cb and concerted E2 limiting elimination pathways}

For **E1**, C$_\beta$–OH heterolysis occurs first. The <electron pair> in that bond goes to O, producing $\mathrm{CH_3COCH_2C^+(H)CH_3+OH^-}$. Its first <transition state> has a partly broken C$_\beta$–O bond, developing positive charge on C$_\beta$ and negative charge on the leaving O. Next, <water> uses a <lone pair> to remove the alpha proton; the C$_\alpha$–H <electron pair> forms the C$_\alpha$=C$_\beta$ <pi bond>. This second <transition state> has partly broken C$_\alpha$–H, partly formed water-O–H, and a partly formed C$_\alpha$–C$_\beta$ <pi bond>. Positive charge is transferred from the organic <cation> toward the incoming <water>/<hydronium> unit. <hydroxide> from the first step is a spectator in the second.

For **E1cb**, <water> first removes the alpha proton to form a resonance-stabilized <enolate> plus <hydronium>:
$$
\mathrm{CH_3C(O^-){=}CHCH(OH)CH_3\ \leftrightarrow\ CH_3COC^-(H)CH(OH)CH_3}.
$$
The first <transition state> has partly broken C$_\alpha$–H and partly formed O$_{\rm water}$–H, with developing negative charge delocalized over the alpha-carbon/carbonyl-oxygen system and positive charge on the proton-accepting <water> unit. The <enolate> then expels <hydroxide>: use the carbon-centred contributor to send its alpha-carbon <lone pair> into C$_\alpha$–C$_\beta$, while the C$_\beta$–O pair goes to <oxygen>. This second <transition state> has a partly formed <alkene> <pi bond> and partly broken C$_\beta$–O, with negative charge moving from the <enolate> framework onto the departing <hydroxide>. <hydronium> is a spectator in this second step.

For **E2**, <water> removes the alpha proton as <hydroxide> leaves in the same elementary step. Three electron-pair movements are simultaneous: <water> <lone pair> to H$_\alpha$, C$_\alpha$–H to the C$_\alpha$–C$_\beta$ <pi bond>, and C$_\beta$–O to O. Its single <transition state> therefore contains **all three changing bonds**: C$_\alpha$–H, water-O–H and C$_\beta$–O, together with the developing <pi bond>. The departing O becomes negative, the proton-accepting <water> unit becomes positive, and partial charge at <carbon> depends on the synchrony of proton loss and leaving-group departure. The reacting <conformation> should align C$_\alpha$–H and C$_\beta$–O approximately in an <antiperiplanar conformation> for efficient elimination.

These are formal limiting pathways for the displayed net process, not three claims of equal <reaction rates> in neutral <water>. In particular, unassisted loss of OH$^-$ to a secondary <carbocation> is strongly disfavoured, and <water> is a weak base for making an <enolate>. The <enolate> pathway benefits from resonance stabilization and the final product is a conjugated <enone>, but actual rate and dominant pathway depend on chemical conditions. The displayed <hydroxide> and <hydronium> also rapidly neutralize each other; retaining them separately here tracks which bond cleavage supplied each species. No biological <catalysis> is needed to describe these chemical mechanisms.
