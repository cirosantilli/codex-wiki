= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

The <method of characteristics> replaces the <first-order partial differential equation> by the <ordinary differential equations>
$$
\dot x=x,\qquad \dot y=2y+\beta x,\qquad \dot u=x+2.
$$
An <integrating factor> for the second equation gives $d(e^{-2s}y)/ds=\beta C e^{-s}$, so their general <solution of a differential equation> is
$$
\boxed{x=C e^s,\qquad y=D e^{2s}-\beta C e^s,\qquad u=C e^s+2s+E.}
$$
Here $C,D,E$ are real constants. For $C\ne0$, eliminating $s$ gives the <characteristic projections>
$$
\boxed{y=Kx^2-\beta x,\qquad K=D/C^2,}
$$
each restricted to either $x>0$ or $x<0$. These are arcs of <parabolas>, with the straight-line exception $K=0$. Along an oriented <characteristic curve>, $|x|$ increases with $s$.

For $C=0$, the <characteristic projections> lie on $x=0$: $y=D e^{2s}$ describes either vertical half-line when $D\ne0$. For $C=D=0$ the projection is the single point $(0,0)$, although $u=2s+E$ still changes. This last lifted <characteristic curve> cannot be contained in the <function graph> of a <classical solution>: at $(0,0)$ the original <partial differential equation> would require $0=2$.

Parametrize the initial curve by $(x_0(r),y_0(r))=(1,r)$, $-1\leq r\leq1$. Its tangent is $(0,1)$, whereas the projected <characteristic vector field> there is $(1,2r+\beta)$. The <determinant>
$$
\boxed{\det\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\2r+\beta&1\end{pmatrix}=1\ne0}
$$
proves that these are <noncharacteristic Cauchy data>: the initial curve is everywhere transverse to the <characteristic curves>.

There is a regularity qualification. The prescribed <function> $g(r)=\min\{r,0\}$ is <continuous> and <Lipschitz continuous>, but is not differentiable at $r=0$. Thus the data are Cauchy in this geometric sense, with data given by a <smooth function> on each of $r<0$ and $r>0$; the usual theorem giving a global <classical solution> from continuously differentiable data does not apply across $r=0$.

Use the initial conditions $x(0,r)=1$, $y(0,r)=r$, $u(0,r)=g(r)$ in the <characteristic equations for a transport equation>. The resulting parametrized <solution surface> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
x(s,r)&=e^s,\\
y(s,r)&=(r+\beta)e^{2s}-\beta e^s,\\
u(s,r)&=\min\{r,0\}+e^s-1+2s,
\end{aligned}\qquad s\in\mathbb R,\ -1\leq r\leq1.}
$$
The <Jacobian determinant> of $(s,r)\mapsto(x,y)$ is $e^{3s}>0$. In fact the inverse is explicit:
$$
s=\log x,\qquad r=q(x,y):=\frac{y+\beta x}{x^2}-\beta.
$$
Consequently the explicit <solution of a differential equation> on the region reached from the data is
$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=x-1+2\log x+\min\left\{\frac{y+\beta x}{x^2}-\beta,0\right\}.}
$$
To verify it away from $q=0$, set $D=x\partial_x+(2y+\beta x)\partial_y$. Direct <differentiation> gives $Dq=0$ and $D(x-1+2\log x)=x+2$. At $x=1$ we have $q=y$, giving precisely the prescribed <boundary condition>.

Across $q=0$ the formula remains <continuous> and <locally Lipschitz>, is a <smooth function> on each side, and solves the <partial differential equation> along every <characteristic curve>. It is also a <weak solution>, since its first <distributional derivatives> agree almost everywhere with its ordinary <derivatives>. It is not a <classical solution> throughout the region because of the inherited corner.

Every point with $x>0$ has exactly one backward <characteristic curve> meeting $x=1$, at the point $(1,q(x,y))$. It meets the specified data precisely when $-1\leq q\leq1$. Thus the whole region determined by these data, including the two bounding <characteristic curves>, is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal D_\beta=\{(x,y):x>0,\ (\beta-1)x^2-\beta x\leq y\leq(\beta+1)x^2-\beta x\}.}
$$
Its interior is the corresponding open domain for the <partial differential equation>. Uniqueness here means uniqueness in the class of <continuous functions> satisfying the <characteristic equations for a transport equation>, including the piecewise <smooth function> defining the <solution of a differential equation> above. The endpoints of the initial segment determine the two boundary values by <continuity>.

This region is maximal for determination by the given data: on $x>0$, arbitrary extensions of $g$ outside $[-1,1]$ give different values on the <characteristic curves> with $q\notin[-1,1]$. No <characteristic curve> from $x=1$ reaches $x\leq0$.

For $\beta=1$,
$$
\boxed{x>0,\qquad -x\leq y\leq2x^2-x.}
$$
The lower boundary is a straight line, the upper boundary is a <parabola>, and the characteristic carrying the corner is $y=x^2-x$. All three approach the excluded origin as $x\downarrow0$.

For $\beta=1$, $q=(y+x-x^2)/x^2$. On the two sides of the corner-carrying <characteristic curve>, the <partial derivative> is
$$
\boxed{u_y(x,y)=\begin{cases}x^{-2},&-x<y<x^2-x,\\0,&x^2-x<y<2x^2-x,\end{cases}\qquad x>0.}
$$
At every point $(x,x^2-x)$ with $x>0$, the two one-sided <partial derivatives> are $x^{-2}$ and $0$. Therefore $u_y$ does not exist on this curve; its piecewise values have a <jump discontinuity> of magnitude $x^{-2}$. There are no other interior discontinuities. The values on the outer boundaries are interpreted from within $\mathcal D_1$.

This is exactly <propagation of a derivative jump along a characteristic>. Already on $x=1$, the data have slopes $1$ and $0$ on the two sides of $y=0$. The <transport equation> transports this corner along the <characteristic curve> from $(1,0)$, which is $y=x^2-x$; it provides no smoothing. This is consistent with <noncharacteristic Cauchy data> and does not contradict the smooth-data theorem, whose differentiability hypothesis fails here.

In the convention $Av_{xx}+2Bv_{xy}+Cv_{yy}=\cdots$, the coefficients of the <principal symbol> are
$$
A=x,\qquad B=y+\frac{\beta x}{2},\qquad C=0,\qquad B^2-AC=\frac{(2y+\beta x)^2}{4}.
$$
Thus the <second-order partial differential equation> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{ll}
\text{hyperbolic},&2y+\beta x\ne0,\\
\text{parabolic},&2y+\beta x=0,\quad x\ne0.
\end{array}}
$$
There is no region of <elliptic partial differential equation> type. The terms hyperbolic, parabolic and elliptic refer to the <pointwise classification of a second-order partial differential equation>. At $(0,0)$ every second-order coefficient vanishes, so the <principal symbol> has rank zero and the equation is degenerate rather than genuinely parabolic there. Moreover the right side is $2$, so no twice continuously differentiable <classical solution> can satisfy the equation at the origin.

For $\beta=0$, the equation for the <characteristic curves> is
$$
x(dy)^2-2y\,dx\,dy=dy\,(x\,dy-2y\,dx)=0.
$$
The two families are therefore $y=\text{constant}$ and the <characteristic projections> from part (a), $y=Kx^2$, together with the vertical characteristic $x=0$. On either region $y>0$ or $y<0$, the <hyperbolic partial differential equation> admits the <characteristic coordinates>
$$
\boxed{\varphi=y,\qquad \psi=\frac{x}{\sqrt{|y|}}.}
$$
Indeed $\varphi$ is constant along the first family, while $(x\partial_x+2y\partial_y)\psi=0$. Their <Jacobian determinant> is
$$
\frac{\partial(\varphi,\psi)}{\partial(x,y)}=-\frac1{\sqrt{|y|}}\ne0.
$$
The coefficients of the two pure second <partial derivatives> in these coordinates vanish, and the coefficient of the mixed <partial derivative> is $2y/\sqrt{|y|}\ne0$. Hence they give the hyperbolic <characteristic normal form>. They also remain regular at $x=0$, provided $y\ne0$.

The alternative $\varphi=y$, $\psi=y/x^2$ is valid on patches with $xy\ne0$. On $y=0$, it is a <parabolic partial differential equation> away from the fully degenerate origin, so two independent hyperbolic <characteristic coordinates> are unavailable.

The second-order <differential operator> factors as
$$
\boxed{xv_{xx}+(2y+\beta x)v_{xy}=\bigl(x\partial_x+(2y+\beta x)\partial_y\bigr)(v_x).}
$$
Therefore setting $u=v_x$ turns the second-order <partial differential equation> into exactly the first-order <partial differential equation> in part (a). Conversely, given a sufficiently smooth <solution of a differential equation> $u$ of that equation on a coordinate rectangle, define
$$
v(x,y)=\int_{x_*}^{x}u(\xi,y)\,d\xi+F(y).
$$
The <fundamental theorem of calculus> gives $v_x=u$, so this solves the second-order equation, with an arbitrary twice continuously differentiable <function> $F$. This classical correspondence is local on smooth patches; for the cornered solution of part (b), it can instead be interpreted using <distributional derivatives>.

For the <Weierstrass M-test> proof of the <Picard-Lindelöf theorem>, the required bound is
$$
\boxed{Mh\leq k,\qquad\text{with no additional restriction on }Lh.}
$$
To see this, use <Picard iteration> starting with $y_0(x)=b$ and
$$
y_{n+1}(x)=b+\int_a^x f(s,y_n(s))\,ds.
$$
If $y_n$ takes its values in $[b-k,b+k]$, then $|y_{n+1}-b|\leq M|x-a|\leq Mh\leq k$. Thus every <Picard iterate> remains in the rectangle where the hypotheses hold. Successive applications of the <Lipschitz condition> and <integration> give the <factorial estimate for Picard iteration>
$$
|y_{n+1}(x)-y_n(x)|\leq\frac{ML^n|x-a|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}.
$$
For example, the $n=0$ estimate is $M|x-a|$, and integrating the $n$th estimate multiplied by $L$ proves the next one. The sum of the uniform bounds is
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{ML^nh^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}<\infty
$$
for every finite $Lh$, by the <exponential series>. Hence the telescoping series of <Picard iterates> converges uniformly by the <Weierstrass M-test>. The <uniform limit> stays in the closed rectangle, and the <Lipschitz condition> permits passage to the limit in the <integral equation>. The <fundamental theorem of calculus> then gives the desired <solution of a differential equation> on $[a-h,a+h]$.

For the direct <contraction mapping theorem> proof with the usual <supremum norm>, take the <complete metric space>
$$
\mathcal B=\{z\in C([a-h,a+h]):\|z-b\|_\infty\leq k\}
$$
and the <Picard iteration> map $Tz(x)=b+\int_a^x f(s,z(s))\,ds$. The estimates are
$$
\|Tz-b\|_\infty\leq Mh,\qquad
\|Tz-Tw\|_\infty\leq Lh\|z-w\|_\infty.
$$
Thus this proof requires
$$
\boxed{Mh\leq k\quad\text{and}\quad Lh<1.}
$$
The first inequality makes $T$ preserve $\mathcal B$; the strict second inequality makes it a <contraction mapping>. Its unique <fixed point> is the desired <solution of a differential equation>.

This is more restrictive than the preceding <Weierstrass M-test> argument: a single application of $T$ must shrink every difference in the <supremum norm>, whereas repeated <integration> in the other proof produces the <factorial> $(n+1)!$ and convergence even when $Lh\geq1$. If $L=0$, the additional condition is automatic. These are conditions for the two stated direct proofs; they are not necessary bounds on the actual existence interval.

With the standard constant starting <Picard iterate>, $y_0(x)=1$, the next two <Picard iterates> are
$$
y_1(x)=1+\int_1^x\left(s+\frac{s}{1}\right)\,ds=x^2

and, for $x>0$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
y_2(x)&=1+\int_1^x\left(s+\frac{s}{s^2}\right)\,ds\\
&=1+\frac{x^2-1}{2}+\log x.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{y_0(x)=1,\qquad y_1(x)=x^2,\qquad y_2(x)=\frac{x^2+1}{2}+\log x.}
$$
The restriction $x>0$ for $y_2$ is essential for the <integral> from $1$ to $x$: the preceding iterate $y_1$ vanishes at $0$, where the new integrand $s+1/s$ is undefined.

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

The rectangle avoids the singular line $y=0$ exactly when $0<k<1$. There $y>0$, and the largest $|x|$ on $[1-h,1+h]$ is $1+h$. Since $1+1/y$ decreases with $y$, the smallest feasible uniform bound is
$$
\boxed{M(h,k)=(1+h)\frac{2-k}{1-k},\qquad h>0,\ 0<k<1.}
$$
It is attained at $x=1+h$, $y=1-k$. For the <Lipschitz constant>, the <mean value theorem> gives
$$
|f(x,y)-f(x,z)|=\frac{|x|}{yz}|y-z|\leq\frac{1+h}{(1-k)^2}|y-z|.
$$
The quotients approach this upper bound as $x=1+h$ and $y,z\downarrow1-k$, so the smallest feasible constant is
$$
\boxed{L(h,k)=\frac{1+h}{(1-k)^2}.}
$$
Substitution into part (a) gives
$$
\begin{array}{ll}
\text{M-test:}&h(1+h)\leq A(k):=\dfrac{k(1-k)}{2-k},\\[4pt]
\text{contraction:}&h(1+h)\leq A(k),\quad h(1+h)<B(k):=(1-k)^2.
\end{array}
$$
The <contraction mapping> restriction is automatic for every M-test-admissible $h$ precisely when $A(k)<B(k)$. Because $0<k<1$,
$$
A(k)<B(k)\iff k<(1-k)(2-k)\iff k^2-4k+2>0.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{0<k<2-\sqrt2}
$$
is exactly the range where the two proofs guarantee all the same closed symmetric intervals, including the largest M-test interval.

For completeness, set $h_M=(\sqrt{1+4A(k)}-1)/2$ and $h_C=(\sqrt{1+4B(k)}-1)/2$. The M-test allows $0<h\leq h_M$; the <contraction mapping theorem> allows $0<h\leq h_M$ with $h<h_C$. At $k=2-\sqrt2$, these have the same supremum $h_M=h_C$, but contraction excludes the endpoint $h=h_M$. For $2-\sqrt2<k<1$, it gives the strictly shorter range $0<h<h_C<h_M$. For a particular $h$, both proofs work exactly when both displayed inequalities hold, even for these larger $k$.

Near $(1,1)$ the <solution of a differential equation> has $y>0$, so the <separable differential equation> gives
$$
\frac{y}{y+1}\,dy=x\,dx,\qquad
y-\log(1+y)=\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac12-\log2.
$$
Equivalently its trajectory lies on the <level set>
$$
\boxed{h(x,y):=y-\log(1+y)-\frac{x^2}{2}=\frac12-\log2.}
$$
On $y>0$, the <function> $G(y)=y-\log(1+y)$ is <strictly increasing> because $G'(y)=y/(1+y)>0$, and its range is $(0,\infty)$. Thus the positive branch exists exactly where
$$
\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac12-\log2>0.
$$
The <connected component> containing $x=1$ is
$$
\boxed{x>x_*:=\sqrt{2\log2-1}\approx0.621526.}
$$
The <implicit function theorem> gives a smooth <solution of a differential equation> at every such $x$. It remains finite at every finite $x>x_*$, because $G$ increases without bound. As $x\downarrow x_*$, we have $y\downarrow0$, and the original right side $x+x/y$ becomes singular. Hence this is the maximal interval $(x_*,\infty)$ through the prescribed <initial condition>.

In particular, **the solution is not defined for all $x\in(0,2)$**. It cannot pass through $x=x_*$ as a <classical solution>, since its required value there is $y=0$.

Treat both sides of $a$ at once: for $\sigma\in\{-1,1\}$ set $w(t)=v(a+\sigma t)$, $0\leq t\leq h$. Nonnegativity gives
$$
\left|\int_a^{a+\sigma t}v(s)\,ds\right|=\int_0^t w(r)\,dr,
\qquad w(t)\leq ct+A\int_0^t w(r)\,dr.
$$
Define $W(t)=ct+A\int_0^t w(r)\,dr$. By the <fundamental theorem of calculus>, $W'(t)=c+Aw(t)\leq c+AW(t)$ and $W(0)=0$. The <integrating factor> $e^{-At}$ gives
$$
\frac{d}{dt}(e^{-At}W(t))\leq ce^{-At},\qquad
W(t)\leq\frac{c}{A}(e^{At}-1).
$$
Since $w\leq W$, this proves the <affine Gronwall estimate>
$$
\boxed{v(x)\leq\frac{c}{A}\bigl(e^{A|x-a|}-1\bigr),\qquad |x-a|\leq h.}
$$

<Continuous dependence of an ODE solution on parameters> here means continuity of the map $\beta\mapsto y_\beta$ into $C([a-h,a+h])$ with its <supremum norm>: for every $\beta_0\in I$ and $\varepsilon>0$, all sufficiently close $\beta\in I$ satisfy
$$
\sup_{|x-a|\leq h}|y_\beta(x)-y_{\beta_0}(x)|<\varepsilon.
$$
Subtract the two <integral equations> and use the uniform <Lipschitz constant> $L$ on the rectangle. With $v(x)=|y_\beta(x)-y_\gamma(x)|$ we obtain
$$
v(x)\leq|\beta-\gamma|\,|x-a|+L\left|\int_a^x v(s)\,ds\right|.
$$
Part (i), with $c=|\beta-\gamma|$ and $A=L$, therefore proves, for $L>0$,
$$
\boxed{\|y_\beta-y_\gamma\|_\infty\leq\frac{e^{Lh}-1}{L}|\beta-\gamma|.}
$$
The case $\beta=\gamma$ follows from uniqueness or the same estimate with zero forcing. If $L=0$, the direct <integral> estimate gives $\|y_\beta-y_\gamma\|_\infty\leq h|\beta-\gamma|$. In either case the map is even <Lipschitz continuous>, and hence <continuous>, throughout $I$ in the stated <supremum norm>.

The <equilibrium points> satisfy $y=\beta$ and $x(x^2-1)=0$, so they are
$$
\boxed{(-1,\beta),\qquad(0,\beta),\qquad(1,\beta).}
$$
The <Jacobian matrix> and its <eigenvalue> equation are
$$
J(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\3x^2-1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \lambda^2=3x^2-1.
$$
At $x=\pm1$ the <eigenvalues> are $\pm\sqrt2$, so both points are <saddle equilibria>. Their stable and unstable tangent directions have slopes $-\sqrt2$ and $\sqrt2$, respectively.

At $x=0$ the <eigenvalues> are $\pm i$, giving a centre for the <linearized equation>. Purely imaginary <eigenvalues> alone do not classify the nonlinear system. The <first integral> established in part (ii) proves that $(0,\beta)$ is in fact a <centre equilibrium>. It is clockwise: immediately to its right, with $0<x<1$ and $y=\beta$, the <vector field> points downwards. Thus the final nonlinear classification is
$$
\boxed{(-1,\beta),(1,\beta)\text{ are saddles};\quad(0,\beta)\text{ is a clockwise centre}.}
$$

Where $y\ne\beta$, taking the quotient of the two <ordinary differential equations> gives
$$
\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{x^3-x}{y-\beta},\qquad
\frac{(y-\beta)^2}{2}=\frac{x^4}{4}-\frac{x^2}{2}+\text{constant}.
$$
Choose the <first integral>
$$
\boxed{h(x,y)=\frac{(y-\beta)^2}{2}+\frac{x^2}{2}-\frac{x^4}{4}.}
$$
The <chain rule> checks conservation even at points excluded in taking that quotient:
$$
\dot h=(x-x^3)(y-\beta)+(y-\beta)(x^3-x)=0.
$$
Thus each trajectory is contained in a <connected component> of a <level set> of $h$. At $(0,\beta)$ the <gradient> vanishes and the <Hessian matrix> is the identity, so $h$ has a strict <local minimum> there. To make the closed-trajectory argument explicit, put $z=y-\beta$ and $0<E<1/4$. The bounded component of $h=E$ is
$$
z=\pm\sqrt{2E-x^2+x^4/2},\qquad
|x|\leq r_E:=\sqrt{1-\sqrt{1-4E}}<1.
$$
The two arcs join at $(\pm r_E,0)$ to form a <simple closed curve> that is a <regular level set>. The <gradient> cannot vanish on it: the only <critical point of a dynamical system> in $|x|<1$ is the centre, whose level is $0$.

The <vector field> is tangent and nowhere zero on this <compact set>, so its speed has a positive minimum. It therefore traverses the curve in finite time and returns to its initial point, giving a <periodic orbit>. These ovals shrink to the centre as $E\downarrow0$. This is the <planar first integral criterion for a centre> and proves the required nonlinear assertion; it also gives <Lyapunov stability> without <asymptotic stability>.

The <nullclines> are
$$
\boxed{\dot x=0:\ y=\beta;\qquad \dot y=0:\ x=-1,0,1.}
$$
On $y=\beta$, away from the <equilibrium points>, the <vector field> is vertical. Its crossing direction is downwards for $x<-1$, upwards for $-1<x<0$, downwards for $0<x<1$, and upwards for $x>1$, because $\dot y=x(x^2-1)$. On each of $x=-1,0,1$, the <vector field> is horizontal: it points right when $y>\beta$ and left when $y<\beta$.

Throughout each complementary region, $\dot x$ has the sign of $y-\beta$, and $\dot y$ has the four signs just listed. These signs orient the <level sets> from part (ii). In particular, the inner <periodic orbits> are clockwise. The saddle level is $h=1/4$, whose <separatrices> satisfy
$$
\boxed{(y-\beta)^2=\frac{(x^2-1)^2}{2}.}
$$
For $-1<x<1$, the upper arc $y-\beta=(1-x^2)/\sqrt2$ goes from $(-1,\beta)$ to $(1,\beta)$; the lower arc goes back from $(1,\beta)$ to $(-1,\beta)$. These two <heteroclinic orbits> form a closed boundary around the family of <periodic orbits>. The remaining branches of the same saddle level extend outwards. Set $z=y-\beta$. Then the <autonomous planar system> becomes
$$
\dot x=z,\qquad \dot z=x^3-x,
$$
which has no $\beta$ dependence. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{changing }\beta\text{ translates the entire phase portrait vertically}.}
$$
More precisely, replacing $\beta$ by $\beta+\delta$ sends every trajectory $(x(t),y(t))$ to $(x(t),y(t)+\delta)$, with the same time parametrization. The <equilibrium points>, <nullclines>, <periodic orbits> and <separatrices> all undergo this same <translation>. Their types and orientations are independent of $\beta$, so varying $\beta$ produces no <bifurcation>.

= Solution

Applying <time reversal of an autonomous differential equation> to the system from part (i), and renaming its coordinates $(x,y)$, gives
$$
\boxed{\widetilde X(x,y)=-(y+1),\qquad \widetilde Y(x,y)=x^2-1.}
$$
The <equilibrium points> remain $(1,-1)$ and $(-1,-1)$. <Time reversal of an autonomous differential equation> preserves a <saddle equilibrium>, interchanging its stable and unstable directions; it also reverses the direction around a <centre equilibrium>. Therefore this system has precisely the required **counterclockwise centre at $(1,-1)$ and saddle at $(-1,-1)$**.

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

One suitable <autonomous planar system> is
$$
\boxed{\dot x=y^2-1,\qquad \dot y=x^2-1.}
$$
Its <equilibrium points> are exactly the four combinations $(x,y)=(\pm1,\pm1)$. Its <Jacobian matrix> is
$$
J(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}0&2y\\2x&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \lambda^2=4xy.
$$
Thus $(-1,-1)$ and $(1,1)$ have <eigenvalues> $\pm2$ and are <saddle equilibria>. At the other two points the <eigenvalues> are $\pm2i$. To establish the nonlinear <centre equilibrium> classifications, use the <first integral>
$$
H(x,y)=\frac{x^3}{3}-x-\frac{y^3}{3}+y,\qquad
\dot H=(x^2-1)(y^2-1)-(y^2-1)(x^2-1)=0.
$$
Its <Hessian matrix> is $\operatorname{diag}(2x,-2y)$, a <negative-definite matrix> at $(-1,1)$ and a <positive-definite matrix> at $(1,-1)$. Thus each is a strict <local extremum>, and nearby <regular level sets> are <closed curves> supporting <periodic orbits>, by the <planar first integral criterion for a centre>.

Immediately to the right of $(-1,1)$, the <vector field> points down because $x^2-1<0$, so that centre is clockwise. Immediately to the right of $(1,-1)$ it points up because $x^2-1>0$, so that centre is counterclockwise. This verifies both the nonlinear classifications and the prescribed orientations, with exactly four <equilibrium points>.
