= Solution
{parent=i}

Fix any $t_0\geq0$ and compare $y=R$ with $y=0$. The quotient required in a global <Lipschitz condition> in the dependent variable is
$$
\frac{|f(t_0,R)-f(t_0,0)|}{|R-0|}=|R-a(t_0)|\longrightarrow\infty
\qquad(R\longrightarrow\infty).
$$
Consequently,
$$
\boxed{f\text{ is not globally Lipschitz in }y.}
$$
It is nevertheless <locally Lipschitz> in $y$: for $|y|,|v|\leq R$,
$$
|f(t,y)-f(t,v)|=|y-v|\,|y+v-a(t)|\leq(2R+C_2)|y-v|.
$$
Thus the <Picard-Lindelöf theorem> gives local existence and uniqueness for the <initial value problem>; the remaining work establishes existence on the entire half-line for the specified small data.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Write $A(t)=\int_0^t a(s)\,ds$. Multiplying the <ordinary differential equation> by the <integrating factor> $e^{A(t)}$ and using the <product rule> gives
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(e^{A(t)}y(t)\bigr)=e^{A(t)}y(t)^2.
$$
Integration from $0$ to $t$ therefore yields the <Volterra integral equation>
$$
\boxed{y(t)=be^{-A(t)}+\int_0^t e^{-(A(t)-A(s))}y(s)^2\,ds.}
$$
Conversely, a <continuous function> satisfying this <integral equation> becomes continuously differentiable after multiplication by $e^{A(t)}$, by the <fundamental theorem of calculus>, and satisfies the original <initial value problem>.

= Solution

For $s>t$, the coefficient bound gives $A(s)-A(t)\geq C_1(s-t)$. Thus for $0\leq s\leq t$ the positive kernel satisfies
$$
K(t,s):=e^{-(A(t)-A(s))}\leq e^{-C_1(t-s)},\qquad
\int_0^t K(t,s)\,ds\leq\frac{1-e^{-C_1t}}{C_1}\leq\frac1{C_1}.
$$
The starting <Picard iterate> is continuous and has <supremum norm> $M_0=|b|$. If the $n$th <Picard iterate> is bounded and continuous, its integral defines another <continuous function>, and
$$
|y_{n+1}(t)|\leq |b|e^{-A(t)}+M_n^2\int_0^tK(t,s)\,ds
\leq |b|+\frac{M_n^2}{C_1}.
$$
Taking the <supremum norm> and applying <mathematical induction> proves boundedness of every iterate and
$$
\boxed{M_{n+1}\leq |b|+C_1^{-1}M_n^2.}
$$

= Solution

Put $B=|b|>0$ and $\gamma=4B/C_1<1$. Initially $M_0=B<2B$. If $M_n<2B$, the preceding <supremum norm> estimate gives
$$
M_{n+1}\leq B+\frac{M_n^2}{C_1}<B+\frac{4B^2}{C_1}=B(1+\gamma)<2B.
$$
Hence <mathematical induction> gives
$$
\boxed{\sup_{t\geq0}|y_n(t)|<2|b|\quad\text{for every }n\geq0.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The difference of two successive <Picard iterates> factors as
$$
y_{n+1}(t)-y_n(t)=\int_0^tK(t,s)\bigl(y_n(s)+y_{n-1}(s)\bigr)\bigl(y_n(s)-y_{n-1}(s)\bigr)\,ds.
$$
The preceding bound and the kernel estimate therefore give
$$
\boxed{\|y_{n+1}-y_n\|_\infty\leq\frac{4B}{C_1}\|y_n-y_{n-1}\|_\infty
=\gamma\|y_n-y_{n-1}\|_\infty.}
$$
Moreover $D_1:=\|y_1-y_0\|_\infty\leq B^2/C_1$. Summing the resulting <geometric series>, for $m>n$,
$$
\|y_m-y_n\|_\infty\leq D_1\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\gamma^j
\leq\frac{D_1\gamma^n}{1-\gamma}.
$$
Thus there is a <uniform limit> $y$ on $[0,\infty)$, continuous by the <uniform limit theorem>, with $\|y\|_\infty\leq2B$. The integral passes to this limit because
$$
\sup_{t\geq0}\left|\int_0^tK(t,s)\bigl(y_n(s)^2-y(s)^2\bigr)\,ds\right|
\leq\frac{4B}{C_1}\|y_n-y\|_\infty\longrightarrow0.
$$
Therefore $y$ satisfies the <Volterra integral equation>, and hence the <initial value problem>. This proves global existence by <global Picard iteration with a decaying kernel>. The same estimate gives the useful error bound
$$
\boxed{\|y-y_n\|_\infty\leq\frac{B^2\gamma^n}{C_1(1-\gamma)}.}
$$
If $b=0$, every iterate is zero and the convergence assertion is immediate.

Let $y$ be any <continuous function> solving the <integral equation> on $[0,\infty)$, and retain $B=|b|>0$. A <first-exit argument> avoids assuming in advance that its <supremum norm> is finite. If $|y|$ ever reached $2B$, continuity would give a first such time $T>0$. On $[0,T]$ we would have $|y|\leq2B$, so the kernel estimate would imply
$$
2B=|y(T)|\leq B+\frac{4B^2}{C_1}=B(1+\gamma)<2B,
$$
a contradiction. Therefore $|y(t)|<2B$ for every finite $t$.

That pointwise strict inequality alone would not give a strict bound on the <supremum norm>. Apply the <integral equation> once more, now using $|y|\leq2B$ globally:
$$
\boxed{\|y\|_\infty\leq B(1+\gamma)<2B.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Existence was proved by <Picard iteration>. If $y$ and $v$ are any two continuous solutions with the same $b\ne0$, the previous part makes both bounded by $2B$. Subtracting their <Volterra integral equations> gives
$$
\|y-v\|_\infty\leq\frac{\|y\|_\infty+\|v\|_\infty}{C_1}\|y-v\|_\infty
\leq\gamma\|y-v\|_\infty.
$$
Since $\gamma<1$, this forces $y=v$.

For $b=0$, zero is a solution. On any finite interval $[0,T]$, another continuous solution has a finite bound $K_T=\max_{[0,T]}|y|$. Since $K(t,s)\leq1$, its <integral equation> implies
$$
|y(t)|\leq K_T\int_0^t|y(s)|\,ds.
$$
The <Gronwall inequality> gives $y=0$ on every such interval. Hence
$$
\boxed{|b|<C_1/4\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{exactly one continuous solution on }[0,\infty).}
$$

An <equilibrium point> $p=(x_0,y_0)$ of an <autonomous planar system> is a point where its <vector field> vanishes:
$$
\boxed{X(x_0,y_0)=Y(x_0,y_0)=0.}
$$
For a locally well-posed <ordinary differential equation>, <Lyapunov stability> means that every prescribed neighbourhood can be protected by choosing a sufficiently small initial neighbourhood. More precisely, for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $\delta>0$ such that every trajectory starting with $\|p(0)-p\|<\delta$ exists for all $t\geq0$ and satisfies
$$
\boxed{\|p(t)-p\|<\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }t\geq0.}
$$
The number $\delta$ may depend on $\varepsilon$ but not on the particular starting point. <Asymptotic stability> additionally requires convergence to the <equilibrium point> for all sufficiently close starting points.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

At an <equilibrium point>, $x=-y^2$ and $x^2=a^2y$, so $y(y^3-a^2)=0$. With $d=|a|^{2/3}>0$, the two <equilibrium points> and their <Jacobian matrix> are
$$
\boxed{(0,0),\qquad(-d^2,d),}\qquad
J(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}2x&-a^2\\1&2y\end{pmatrix}.
$$
At $(-d^2,d)$, $\det J=-3a^2<0$. The <trace-determinant stability criterion> therefore gives an unstable <saddle equilibrium>. At the origin, the <eigenvalues> are $\lambda=\pm i|a|$: its <linearization> is a <center equilibrium>. The <linearization stability theorem> does not classify this <nonhyperbolic equilibrium> of the nonlinear system.

**Nonlinear classification.** In fact the answer depends on $|a|$:
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
0<|a|<1&\text{unstable weak focus at the origin}\\
|a|=1&\text{stable center at the origin}\\
|a|>1&\text{asymptotically stable weak focus at the origin}.
\end{array}}
$$
Here is an explicit derivation, rather than an inference from the imaginary <eigenvalues>. When $a^2=1$, set $L=1-x+y$. Near the origin $L>0$, and the <first integral>
$$
H(x,y)=\frac{1+xy}{L}+\log L
$$
has
$$
H_x=\frac{x+y^2}{L^2},\qquad H_y=\frac{y-x^2}{L^2},\qquad
\frac{dH}{dt}=0,\qquad
H=1+\frac{x^2+y^2}{2}+O\bigl((x^2+y^2)^{3/2}\bigr).
$$
Its <Hessian matrix> at the origin is the identity. Small level curves are closed ovals on which the <vector field> is nonzero, proving the <center equilibrium> classification and <Lyapunov stability>.

For $a^2\ne1$, a higher-order <Lyapunov function> calculation settles the issue. Put $k=|a|$, $U=x$, $V=ky$, $\tau=kt$, $p=k^{-1}$ and $q=k^{-2}$. Then
$$
U_\tau=-V+pU^2,\qquad V_\tau=U+qV^2.
$$
Write $R^2=U^2+V^2$ and introduce the differential operators
$$
\mathcal L=-V\partial_U+U\partial_V,\qquad
\mathcal N=pU^2\partial_U+qV^2\partial_V.
$$
Successively cancel the terms of degrees three, four and five in the derivative of $R^2+H_3+H_4+H_5$, using these <homogeneous polynomials>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
H_3&=-2pU^2V-\tfrac43pV^3+\tfrac43qU^3+2qUV^2,\\
H_4&=2p^2U^2V^2-4pq(U^3V+UV^3)+(p^2-q^2)V^4,\\
H_5&=-\tfrac{4q}{15}(p^2+8q^2)U^5
+\tfrac{16q}{3}(p^2-q^2)U^3V^2
-\tfrac{4p}{3}(p^2-q^2)U^2V^3\\
&\quad+4q(p^2-q^2)UV^4+\tfrac{4p}{15}(11q^2-2p^2)V^5.
\end{aligned}
$$
Direct differentiation verifies $\mathcal LH_3=-\mathcal N R^2$, $\mathcal LH_4=-\mathcal NH_3$ and $\mathcal LH_5=-\mathcal NH_4$. The remaining degree-six term has circular average
$$
c=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}(\mathcal NH_5)(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\,d\theta
=\frac{pq(q^2-p^2)}4=\frac{1-k^2}{4k^7}.
$$
For example, the only nonzero averages of degree-six monomials needed here are $\langle U^6\rangle=\langle V^6\rangle=5/16$ and $\langle U^4V^2\rangle=\langle U^2V^4\rangle=1/16$. Since $\mathcal L=\partial_\theta$ in <polar coordinates>, the zero-average part can be cancelled by a degree-six <homogeneous polynomial> $H_6$ satisfying
$$
\mathcal LH_6=cR^6-\mathcal NH_5.
$$
Indeed its restriction to the unit circle is obtained by integrating a zero-average trigonometric polynomial; its Fourier modes have orders $2,4,6$, so multiplication by $R^6$ gives a polynomial. Consequently the positive <Lyapunov function> $W=R^2+H_3+H_4+H_5+H_6$ satisfies
$$
\frac{dW}{d\tau}=cR^6+O(R^7).
$$
For $k>1$ this is strictly negative near the origin, giving <asymptotic stability>. For $k<1$ it is strictly positive, and $W_\tau\geq C W^3$ in a sufficiently small neighbourhood; every nonzero starting point must eventually leave that neighbourhood. Finally $\theta_\tau=1+O(R)>0$, so the nearby trajectories rotate: these are <weak foci>, with the stability stated above. Merely calling the nonlinear origin a center for every $a\ne0$ would miss this distinction.

A <characteristic curve> in $(x,y,z)$ follows the <vector field> defining the first-order <partial differential equation>. Along such a curve the <chain rule> turns the equation into
$$
\frac{dx}{ds}=xy,\qquad \frac{dy}{ds}=y^2,\qquad
\frac{dz}{ds}=-\frac13z^4.
$$
Its <characteristic projection> is the curve $(x(s),y(s))$ in the independent-variable plane.

Writing $x(0)=\xi$, $y(0)=\eta$, $z(0)=\zeta$, <separation of variables> gives
$$
\boxed{x(s)=\frac{\xi}{1-\eta s},\qquad
y(s)=\frac{\eta}{1-\eta s},\qquad
z(s)=\frac{\zeta}{(1+\zeta^3s)^{1/3}}.}
$$
These formulas hold on the maximal interval through $s=0$ where the denominators remain nonzero, using the real <cube root>. They include $\eta=0$ (a stationary <characteristic projection>) and $\zeta=0$ (the zero height). For $\zeta\ne0$, the last formula follows from $d(z^{-3})/ds=1$. The alternative zero solution must be retained when deriving this by division by $z^4$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Parametrize the data by $(\xi,\eta,\zeta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,-1)$. The determinant testing <noncharacteristic Cauchy data> is
$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\sin\theta\\
\cos\theta&\sin^2\theta\end{pmatrix}=-\sin\theta.
$$
Thus the open upper and lower semicircles are noncharacteristic, but the entire circle is not a valid noncharacteristic data curve: the determinant vanishes at $(1,0)$ and $(-1,0)$.

There is a stronger obstruction at those two points. On $y=0$, the differential equation forces $0=-z^4/3$, and hence $z=0$. This contradicts the prescribed value $z=-1$ at both endpoints. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{No classical solution satisfies the data on the entire circle.}}
$$
The obstruction is already present if the equation is imposed next to the circle and the solution and its first <partial derivatives> extend continuously to it.

Away from the endpoints, the <characteristic projections> are radial half-lines:
$$
(x,y)=r(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),\qquad r=\frac1{1-s\sin\theta}>0.
$$
For $\sin\theta>0$, $r$ increases with $s$; for $\sin\theta<0$, $r$ decreases with $s$. Each projection meets its own open semicircle exactly once and never crosses $y=0$. The endpoint projections themselves are single stationary points, although their lifted <characteristic curves> have varying height.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Use the compatible open semicircles and set $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. The <characteristic curve> through $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,-1)$ has
$$
z(s)=-(1-s)^{-1/3},\qquad s<1.
$$
The condition $s<1$ is essential: at $s=1$ there is <finite-time blowup>, so the opposite branch cannot be reached from the data. From $r=(1-s\sin\theta)^{-1}$ and $y=r\sin\theta$,
$$
s=\frac{r-1}{y}.
$$
Eliminating $s$ yields
$$
\boxed{z(x,y)=-\left(\frac{y}{1+y-\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\right)^{1/3}.}
$$
The ratio inside the <cube root> is positive precisely on the two data-determined regions
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
D_+&=\{(x,y):y>0,\ r<1+y\}
=\{(x,y):y>0,\ y>(x^2-1)/2\},\\
D_-&=\{(x,y):y<0,\ r>1+y\}
=\{(x,y):y<0,\ y<(x^2-1)/2\}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
On $D_+$ each point traces to the upper semicircle, and on $D_-$ to the lower semicircle, with $s<1$. To check the parameter ranges explicitly, for $\eta=\sin\theta>0$ they are $-\infty<s<1$, whereas for $\eta<0$ they are $1/\eta<s<1$. At the latter lower endpoint the planar projection escapes to infinity. The <inverse function theorem> and uniqueness for the characteristic <ordinary differential equations> give a unique smooth solution on each region. Equivalently, each point has the unique foot $(x/r,y/r)$ and parameter $(r-1)/y$.

A direct check is particularly short. For $s=(r-1)/y$, let $\mathcal D=xy\partial_x+y^2\partial_y$. Then $\mathcal Dr=yr$, $\mathcal Dy=y^2$, and
$$
\mathcal Ds=\frac{(yr)y-(r-1)y^2}{y^2}=1.
$$
Since $dz/ds=-z^4/3$, the displayed formula solves the <partial differential equation>, and $r=1$ gives $z=-1$ on both open semicircles. The finite boundary where $r=1+y$, away from the axis, is the <parabola> $y=(x^2-1)/2$ and corresponds to blowup. Points on $y=0$ cannot be added to a classical continuation of these regions: where they are approached from a data-determined region, the height behaves like a nonzero multiple of $|y|^{1/3}$, or has incompatible limits at the endpoints. The formula is therefore the complete classical answer for the compatible arcs, while the full-circle problem as printed has no classical solution.

Let $\mathcal Pu=u_t-\Delta u+a u_x+b u_y$ on the <unit disk> over $0<t\leq1$. Smoothness is understood to include continuity on the closed cylinder. If $u$ exceeded its nonnegative <parabolic boundary> maximum $A$, it would attain a positive global maximum $M>A$ at some spatial interior point at time $t_0>0$. There,
$$
\nabla u=0,\qquad \Delta u\leq0,\qquad u_t\geq0.
$$
At $t_0=1$ the last inequality uses the derivative from the past; at an interior time it is equality. These signs imply $\mathcal Pu+u^3\geq M^3>0$, contradicting the given <differential inequality>. Thus
$$
\boxed{u\leq A\quad\text{throughout }Q.}
$$
If $A>0$, equality at any point of $Q$ gives the same contradiction with $M=A$, so $u<A$ throughout $Q$. If $A=0$, equality is possible: $u\equiv0$ is an example. More precisely, write the inequality as $\mathcal Pu+c u\leq0$ with $c=u^2\geq0$. The <strong parabolic maximum principle> shows that an interior zero at time $t_0>0$ forces $u\equiv0$ on $B\times[0,t_0]$.

That conclusion need not extend into the future for a subsolution. For $t_0<1$, take a spatially constant function which is zero for $t\leq t_0$ and equals $-\exp(-1/(t-t_0))$ for $t>t_0$. It is smooth, nonincreasing and nonpositive, so $\mathcal Pu+u^3\leq0$, but it becomes negative after $t_0$.

With zero drift, choose the <smooth function>
$$
\boxed{u(x,y,t)=-1+\frac{t}{8}(1-x^2-y^2).}
$$
It equals $-1$ on the entire <parabolic boundary>, so $A=-1$. On the closed cylinder, $-1\leq u\leq-7/8$, while
$$
u_t-\Delta u=\frac{1-x^2-y^2}{8}+\frac t2\leq\frac58.
$$
Consequently
$$
u_t-\Delta u+u^3\leq\frac58-\left(\frac78\right)^3
=-\frac{23}{512}<0.
$$
Nevertheless the maximum at $(0,0,1)$ is
$$
\boxed{\max_{\overline Q}u=-\frac78>-1=A.}
$$
The negative cubic term permits an interior increase while the boundary stays fixed, explaining why the sign condition in the preceding part matters.

The <parabolic boundary> values are zero. An exact solution satisfies the preceding <differential inequality>, so its maximum estimate with $A=0$ gives $u\leq0$. The function $v=-u$ satisfies the same equation because both the linear differential operator and the cubic term change sign. Applying the same <maximum principle> to $v$ gives $-u\leq0$. Hence the complete solution set is
$$
\boxed{\{u\equiv0\}.}
$$
Substitution verifies existence, and the two inequalities prove uniqueness among the assumed smooth solutions for every continuous choice of the drift coefficients.

Define the squared <L2 norm> $E(t)=\int_0^1u(x,t)^2\,dx$. Multiplication of the <semilinear partial differential equation> by $u$, followed by <integration by parts>, gives
$$
\frac12E'(t)=[u u_x]_0^1-\int_0^1u_x^2\,dx
+a(t)\int_0^1u\sin^n u\,dx.
$$
At $x=1$ the <Dirichlet boundary condition> makes the boundary term zero. At $x=0$ the <Robin boundary condition> is $u_x(0,t)=\alpha u(0,t)$, so subtraction of the lower endpoint gives $-\alpha u(0,t)^2$. In particular the sign is dissipative. Thus the exact <energy identity> is
$$
\frac12E'(t)+\int_0^1u_x^2\,dx+\alpha u(0,t)^2
=a(t)\int_0^1u\sin^n u\,dx,
$$
which implies
$$
\boxed{\frac12\frac d{dt}\int_0^1|u|^2\,dx
\leq a(t)\int_0^1u\sin^n u\,dx.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For every real $r$ and integer $n\geq1$, $|\sin r|\leq\min(1,|r|)$ gives
$$
r\sin^n r\leq |r|\,|\sin r|^n\leq |r|\,|\sin r|\leq r^2.
$$
This estimate covers both even and odd $n$, without any assumption on the sign of $u$. Since $a(t)\geq0$, the <energy estimate> from part (i) becomes
$$
E'(t)\leq2a(t)E(t),\qquad E(0)=0.
$$
Equivalently, an <integrating factor> gives
$$
\frac d{dt}\left[e^{-2\int_0^t a(s)\,ds}E(t)\right]\leq0.
$$
The bracket is nonnegative and starts at zero, so it vanishes identically. This is also the zero-data case of the <Gronwall inequality>. The vanishing <L2 norm> and continuity of $u$ imply
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)\equiv0.}
$$
