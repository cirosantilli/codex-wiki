== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <autonomous planar system> has two real state variables, so its <phase space> is a subset of $\mathbb R^2$. It is autonomous because its <vector field> $(X(x,y),Y(x,y))$ has no explicit time dependence. The <nullclines> are $X(x,y)=0$ and $Y(x,y)=0$, on which the respective coordinate's instantaneous rate of change vanishes. A <critical point of a dynamical system> is an intersection where **$X=Y=0$**; the constant trajectory at that point is an <equilibrium of an autonomous differential equation>. A nullcline alone need not consist of equilibria.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Assume $X,Y$ are continuously differentiable near the equilibrium. Put $\xi=x-a$, $\eta=y-b$ and expand the <vector field>:
$$
\frac d{dt}\begin{pmatrix}\xi\\\eta\end{pmatrix}=M\begin{pmatrix}\xi\\\eta\end{pmatrix}+o\!\left(\sqrt{\xi^2+\eta^2}\right),\qquad
\boxed{M=\begin{pmatrix}X_x(a,b)&X_y(a,b)\\Y_x(a,b)&Y_y(a,b)\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
This <Jacobian matrix> defines the linearized system. For distinct real <eigenvalues>, two negative values give a stable node, two positive values an unstable node, and opposite signs a <saddle point of a dynamical system>. A complex-conjugate pair with negative real part gives a stable focus, and one with positive real part an unstable focus. Thus a hyperbolic saddle has **two real <eigenvalues> of opposite signs**, equivalently $\det M<0$.

The <linearization stability theorem> transfers these stability conclusions to the nonlinear system when no <eigenvalue> has zero real part. Distinctness alone is insufficient: a zero eigenvalue or a purely imaginary conjugate pair leaves a <nonhyperbolic equilibrium>, for which the nonlinear terms must be examined. In the purely imaginary case the linearized trajectories form a centre, but that does not establish a nonlinear centre.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

One useful form of the <Bendixson-Dulac theorem> is this: let $D\subseteq\mathbb R^2$ be open and <simply connected>, and let $X,Y,\psi$ be continuously differentiable on $D$. If
$$
\frac{\partial(\psi X)}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial(\psi Y)}{\partial y}
$$
has one strict sign throughout $D$, then **there is no nonconstant <periodic orbit> lying entirely in $D$**. More generally the divergence may have a weak sign provided it is not identically zero on any nonempty open subset. The multiplier $\psi$ is a <Dulac function>. <Green's theorem> proves the criterion: the vector field is tangent to a hypothetical closed orbit, so its scaled flux across that boundary is zero, whereas the signed area integral of its <divergence> would be nonzero.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Intersect the <nullclines> $y=x(2-x)$ and $y=kx$. Their intersection equation is $x(2-k-x)=0$, giving the origin and $(2-k,k(2-k))$. The <Jacobian matrix> is
$$
M(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}2-2x&-1\\-k&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
At the origin, $\operatorname{tr}M=3$, $\det M=2-k$ and
$$
\lambda_\pm=\frac{3\pm\sqrt{1+4k}}2.
$$
For **$k>2$**, only the origin is in the nonnegative quadrant, and it is a **saddle** because its determinant is negative. For **$0<k<2$**, the origin is an **unstable node**, with two distinct positive <eigenvalues>, and there is a second equilibrium in the open quadrant. At that second equilibrium, $\det M=k-2<0$, so it is a **saddle**. These signs give <hyperbolic equilibria>, so the nonlinear classifications follow from the <linearization stability theorem>. The boundary value $k=2$ is excluded from the two stated cases.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Draw the downward-opening <nullcline> $y=x(2-x)$, meeting the axes at $x=0,2$, and the line $y=kx$. Their positive intersection is $P=(2-k,k(2-k))$. Use the sign rules
$$
\dot x>0\iff y<x(2-x),\qquad \dot y>0\iff y>kx.
$$
Above both curves the arrows point northwest; below both they point southeast. For $0<x<2-k$, the parabola lies above the line, so arrows between them point northeast. For $2-k<x<2$, their order reverses, so arrows between them point southwest. For $x>2$, $\dot x<0$ throughout the nonnegative quadrant, with the vertical direction still determined by the line. Mark the origin as an unstable node and $P$ as a saddle, and trace sample trajectories consistent with those directions.

\Image[2017-phase-plane.png]
{title=<Nullclines> and sample trajectories for the representative value $k=1$}
{description=The displayed vector field is restricted to the nonnegative quadrant. The origin is an unstable node and the interior equilibrium is a saddle.}

The quadrant is **not positively invariant**: on $x=0,y>0$, $\dot x=-y<0$; on $y=0,x>0$, $\dot y=-kx<0$. Trajectories reaching either axis there leave the plotted region. The <phase portrait> describes the portions inside the quadrant, not a claim that all forward trajectories remain inside it.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $k>2$ and choose the positive <Dulac function>
$$
\psi(x,y)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{2(x+y)}{k-2}\right).
$$
More generally, for $\psi=e^{a(x+y)}$, direct differentiation gives
$$
\nabla\cdot(\psi(X,Y))=\psi\bigl[3+(-2+(2-k)a)x-ax^2\bigr].
$$
Taking $a=-2/(k-2)$ cancels the linear term and makes the quadratic term nonnegative:
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot(\psi(X,Y))=\psi\left(3+\frac{2x^2}{k-2}\right)>0.}
$$
The open positive quadrant is <simply connected>, so the <Bendixson-Dulac theorem> excludes every nonconstant <periodic orbit> entirely inside it. Such an orbit cannot touch $x=0,y>0$ while staying in the closed quadrant, since its differentiable coordinate $x$ would have a local minimum there but $\dot x=-y\ne0$. The same argument applies on $y=0,x>0$. It cannot pass through the origin either, by local uniqueness of this smooth <autonomous system>. Therefore **there is no nonconstant closed trajectory in the entire nonnegative quadrant**. The calculation illustrates an <exponential Dulac multiplier for a quadratic planar system>.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <contraction mapping theorem> states that a <contraction mapping> $T:S\to S$ on a nonempty <complete metric space> has a unique <fixed point>. Explicitly, if $d(Tu,Tv)\leq qd(u,v)$ for all $u,v\in S$ and one fixed $0\leq q<1$, then the iterates $u_{n+1}=Tu_n$ converge to that fixed point from every $u_0\in S$. Both self-mapping and completeness are needed; the set need not be a <vector space>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $0<\eta\leq h$, work on the closed subset
$$
C_\eta=\{y\in C([a-\eta,a+\eta]): |y(x)-b|\leq k\},\qquad d(y,z)=\|y-z\|_\infty.
$$
It is a nonempty <complete metric space> because it is closed in the <Banach space> of continuous functions with the <supremum norm>. Define the <Picard iteration> operator
$$
(Ty)(x)=b+\int_a^x f(s,y(s))\,ds.
$$
Choose $\eta$ so that **$M\eta\leq k$ and $L\eta<1$**, omitting a bound whose constant is zero. Such a positive $\eta$ exists. The first inequality makes $T$ a self-map, since $|(Ty)(x)-b|\leq M|x-a|\leq k$. The <Lipschitz condition> gives
$$
\|Ty-Tz\|_\infty\leq L\eta\|y-z\|_\infty,
$$
so the <contraction mapping theorem> yields a unique fixed point. The <fundamental theorem of calculus> makes it a differentiable solution of $y'=f(x,y)$ with $y(a)=b$; conversely every solution staying in the rectangle satisfies the integral equation and is a fixed point. This is the local <Picard-Lindelöf theorem> construction. The interval-restricted space $C_\eta$ avoids treating functions defined on a shorter interval as elements of $C_h$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Under $Mh\leq k$, the same <Picard iteration> operator maps all of $C_h$ into itself. It need not be a <contraction mapping> in one step, since $Lh$ can exceed one. Instead repeated integration gives the <factorial estimate for Picard iteration>
$$
|(T^ny)(x)-(T^nz)(x)|\leq\frac{L^n|x-a|^n}{n!}\|y-z\|_\infty,
$$
proved inductively by integrating the preceding bound between $a$ and $x$. Thus
$$
\|T^ny-T^nz\|_\infty\leq\frac{(Lh)^n}{n!}\|y-z\|_\infty.
$$
For some $n$ the coefficient is less than one, so $T^n$ is a contraction. Its unique fixed point $y_*$ is also fixed by $T$: $T^n(Ty_*)=T(T^ny_*)=Ty_*$ and uniqueness forces $Ty_*=y_*$. Conversely any fixed point of $T$ is one of $T^n$. This <iterated contraction> argument gives **a unique solution on the full interval $[a-h,a+h]$**, without the unjustified extra assumption $Lh<1$.

The uniqueness statement is for solutions in the specified rectangle, where $f$ is given and controlled. The bound $Mh\leq k$ keeps the constructed solution inside that rectangle throughout the interval.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Where $y\ne-1$, **$y'=-\sqrt{|1+y|}<0$**. By the <mean value theorem>, the solution is strictly decreasing on every interval where it avoids $-1$. At $y=-1$, the derivative is zero, so a constant segment there is possible. Thus all solutions are nonincreasing, while strict decrease need not persist through an interval spent at that equilibrium.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For example, choose $h=1/4$ and $k=1/2$ around $(a,b)=(0,0)$. On the rectangle, $1+y\geq1/2$, so $f=-\sqrt{1+y}$ is continuous, $|f|\leq\sqrt{3/2}$ and $|\partial f/\partial y|\leq1/\sqrt2$. The <mean value theorem> therefore supplies a <Lipschitz constant> $L=1/\sqrt2$. These choices even satisfy $Mh<k$ and $Lh<1$.

While $y>-1$, <separation of variables> gives $2\sqrt{1+y}=2-x$. Therefore the uniquely determined branch up to its first arrival at $-1$ is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\left(1-\frac x2\right)^2-1,\qquad 0\leq x\leq2.}
$$
It reaches $y=-1$ at $x=2$, with derivative zero. The restriction $x\leq2$ is essential: extending the same quadratic beyond two would make its derivative positive and violate the original differential equation. Beyond that time the <waiting-time continuation at a non-Lipschitz equilibrium> is nonunique.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Two solutions for $x\geq2$ are
$$
\boxed{y_1(x)=-1,\qquad y_2(x)=-1-\frac{(x-2)^2}{4}.}
$$
For the second, $y_2'=-(x-2)/2=-\sqrt{|1+y_2|}$ because $x\geq2$; both have value $-1$ and derivative zero at two. They are distinct for every $x>2$. Local <Lipschitz continuity> fails at the equilibrium, so the existence of these two continuations is compatible with the <Picard-Lindelöf theorem>.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

For each departure time $c\in[2,h]$, define
$$
y_c(x)=\begin{cases}
(1-x/2)^2-1,&0\leq x\leq2,\\
-1,&2\leq x\leq c,\\
-1-(x-c)^2/4,&c\leq x\leq h.
\end{cases}
$$
Each branch satisfies the <ordinary differential equation>, and both joins have matching value $-1$ and matching derivative zero. Thus $y_c$ is continuously differentiable and satisfies the same <initial value problem>. If $2\leq c_1<c_2\leq h$, the solutions differ on $(c_1,c_2)$, proving **infinitely many solutions on every $[0,h]$ with $h>2$**.

The uniqueness hypotheses fail when the solution reaches $-1$. Indeed
$$
\frac{|f(-1+\varepsilon)-f(-1)|}{\varepsilon}=\frac1{\sqrt\varepsilon}\longrightarrow\infty.
$$
No finite <Lipschitz constant> works on a rectangle containing that level in its interior. The local theorem still controls the earlier branch near zero; it cannot be extended through a point where its hypotheses fail. These are <waiting-time continuations at a non-Lipschitz equilibrium>.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The principal coefficients are $A=-xy$, $B=0$, $C=1$, in the convention $Au_{xx}+2Bu_{xy}+Cu_{yy}$. Their <discriminant> is **$B^2-AC=xy$**. Thus it is a <hyperbolic partial differential equation> where $xy>0$ and an <elliptic partial differential equation> where $xy<0$. Its principal part has <parabolic type> where $xy=0$; however the full equation contains $u_y/y$ and is undefined on $y=0$. On its actual domain $y\ne0$, the parabolic locus is $x=0$.

The <characteristic equation of a partial differential equation> is
$$
\boxed{A(dy)^2-2B\,dx\,dy+C(dx)^2=0,\qquad (dx)^2-xy(dy)^2=0.}
$$
In a hyperbolic region this gives two distinct real <characteristic directions>. The singular lower-order coefficient should not be mistaken for an ordinary parabolic point of the full equation on $y=0$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

In $x,y>0$, the <characteristic equation of a partial differential equation> gives $dx/\sqrt x=\pm\sqrt y\,dy$. Integration yields the <characteristic coordinates>
$$
\xi=\sqrt x+\frac{y^{3/2}}3,\qquad \eta=\sqrt x-\frac{y^{3/2}}3.
$$
Their <Jacobian determinant> is $-\sqrt y/(2\sqrt x)\ne0$, so they are valid independent coordinates. To see cancellation of the lower-order terms explicitly, first put $r=\sqrt x$, $s=y^{3/2}/3$ and $u(x,y)=U(r,s)$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
u_x=\frac{U_r}{2\sqrt x},\quad u_{xx}=\frac{U_{rr}}{4x}-\frac{U_r}{4x^{3/2}},\quad
u_y=\frac{\sqrt y}{2}U_s,\quad u_{yy}=\frac y4U_{ss}+\frac{U_s}{4\sqrt y}.
$$
Substitution cancels the first-derivative terms, leaving **$U_{ss}-U_{rr}=0$**. With $\xi=r+s$, $\eta=r-s$ and $U=V(\xi,\eta)$, the <characteristic normal form> is **$V_{\xi\eta}=0$**. Integrating once in each variable gives
$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=g\!\left(\sqrt x+\frac{y^{3/2}}3\right)+h\!\left(\sqrt x-\frac{y^{3/2}}3\right),}
$$
where $g,h$ are arbitrary twice continuously differentiable functions on the relevant coordinate intervals. This is a <curvilinear reduction to the one-dimensional wave equation>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

On $y=1$, the transformed variables satisfy $s=1/3$ and $1/2\leq r\leq1$. Differentiating the displacement data tangentially and using $u_y=1$ gives
$$
g'(r+1/3)+h'(r-1/3)=2,\qquad \tfrac12\bigl(g'(r+1/3)-h'(r-1/3)\bigr)=1.
$$
Hence $g'=2$ on $[5/6,4/3]$, $h'=0$ on $[1/6,2/3]$, and the additive constants have sum $-2/3$. The uniquely determined solution is therefore
$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=2\sqrt x+\frac23(y^{3/2}-1).}
$$
It is fixed by the data exactly where both needed characteristic arguments belong to their known intervals:
$$
\boxed{\frac56\leq\sqrt x+\frac{y^{3/2}}3\leq\frac43,\qquad\frac16\leq\sqrt x-\frac{y^{3/2}}3\leq\frac23.}
$$
Equivalently, in the $(r,s)$ plane this is the diamond $|r-3/4|+|s-1/3|\leq1/4$. In the original coordinates it is bounded by four curved <characteristic curves>. It lies wholly in $x,y>0$. The displayed formula supplies one smooth extension beyond the diamond too, but the specified finite data segment does not determine that extension: one may change $g$ or $h$ outside its known argument interval. This is the <characteristic-coordinate domain of dependence>.

=== d
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The initial curve $y^3=9x$ is **$\eta=0$**, with $\xi=2\sqrt x\in[1,2]$, so it is characteristic. The displacement data force $g(\xi)+h(0)=\xi$ and therefore $g'=1$ on that interval. Along the curve,
$$
u_y=\frac{\sqrt y}{2}\bigl(1-h'(0)\bigr),\qquad\sqrt y=3^{1/3}x^{1/6}.
$$
The derivative data in case (i) are compatible precisely when $h'(0)=-1$. They place no restriction on the rest of $h$. For example, for every real constant $C$,
$$
\boxed{u_C(x,y)=\frac23y^{3/2}+C\left(\sqrt x-\frac{y^{3/2}}3\right)^2}
$$
solves the equation and has both prescribed data on the curve. Choosing $C=0$ and $C=1$ exhibits **more than one solution** directly. The initial curve follows one characteristic family, so its data do not determine the other wave family. This is the compatible case of <compatibility of Cauchy data on a characteristic curve>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

As in case (i), the initial curve is characteristic and its displacement data force $g'=1$. The new derivative data would therefore require
$$
1=\frac{\sqrt y}{2}\bigl(1-h'(0)\bigr),\qquad h'(0)=1-\frac2{\sqrt y}.
$$
The right-hand side varies along the initial segment. A single number $h'(0)$ cannot satisfy those conditions, so **no classical solution exists**.

Characteristic <Cauchy data> impose a compatibility condition on transverse derivatives without determining the missing independent wave family. Compatible data can leave nonuniqueness, as in case (i); incompatible data have no solution, as here. The distinction follows from <compatibility of Cauchy data on a characteristic curve>, rather than a noncharacteristic existence-and-uniqueness theorem.
