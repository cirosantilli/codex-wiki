Start with the <propositional variables> as atomic symbols and close under the two formation operations
$$
p_i\in\operatorname{Form}(L_0),\qquad
\psi\in\operatorname{Form}(L_0)\Longrightarrow\neg\psi\in\operatorname{Form}(L_0),\qquad
\psi,\chi\in\operatorname{Form}(L_0)\Longrightarrow(\psi\to\chi)\in\operatorname{Form}(L_0).
$$
The <set> of <propositional formulas> is the **smallest** <set> satisfying these conditions: every <logical formula> has a finite construction from <logical variables> by <negation> and <logical implication>, and no other strings qualify. In particular, parentheses belong to the binary <logical implication> <formation rule>; they cannot be added or removed arbitrarily. Each symbol $p_i$ is one token, regardless of how its index is printed.

A <propositional variable> $p_i$ is a single symbol of the alphabet. Its only nonempty substring is that symbol itself. Every <propositional formula> is nonempty, so the only substring that is a <logical formula> is $p_i$. Therefore

= Solution
{parent=ii}

A useful consequence of the <formation rules> is that <logical formulas> form a prefix-free <set> of strings. To see this, describe a deterministic <parser> that reads one <logical formula> from its starting position. On a <logical variable> it consumes that one symbol. On a <negation> symbol it recursively reads one following <logical formula>. On an opening parenthesis it recursively reads a left <logical formula>, requires a <logical implication> symbol, recursively reads a right <logical formula>, and requires the closing parenthesis. Induction on formation proves that, if a valid <logical formula> begins at the current position, this procedure consumes exactly that <logical formula> and stops at its end.

Suppose now that both $\alpha$ and $\phi$ are <logical formulas> and $\phi=\alpha\epsilon$. The same <parser>, started at their common first symbol, must stop both at the end of $\alpha$ and at the end of $\phi$. Its deterministic stopping position cannot differ, so $\epsilon$ is empty. Hence
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\phi.}
$$
This argument applies to symbol strings; it does not use <truth values> or <logical equivalence>.

= Solution

First note that a substring which is itself a <logical formula> must coincide with a subtree of the <formula syntax tree> of the surrounding <logical formula>. Indeed, a <logical formula> can begin only at a <logical variable>, a <negation> symbol or an opening parenthesis. Every such position in a valid <logical formula> is the start of one of its parsed subtrees. If $\beta$ is the <logical formula> at that subtree and $\alpha$ is another <logical formula> starting at the same position, one is an initial segment of the other; <prefix-freeness of fully parenthesized formulas> from the preceding part forces $\alpha=\beta$.

Now consider a final <subformula>. For a <logical variable>, it can only be the whole <logical formula>. For a binary <logical implication> $(\psi\to\chi)$, every proper subtree is contained in $\psi$ or $\chi$ and finishes before the final closing parenthesis of the whole expression. It therefore cannot be a final substring of the whole. The only remaining possible outer formation is <negation>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\phi\quad\text{or}\quad\phi=\neg\psi\text{ for some formula }\psi.}
$$
The second alternative is a necessary possibility, not a claim that every negated <logical formula> has every one of its <subformulas> as a suffix.

The whole <logical formula> $\neg\psi$ is a <subformula> of itself, and any <subformula> of $\psi$ remains a contiguous substring after placing a <negation> symbol in front of $\psi$.

Conversely, a <subformula occurrence> starting at the first symbol of $\neg\psi$ is an initial <subformula>, so equals the whole <logical formula> by <prefix-freeness of fully parenthesized formulas>. An occurrence starting later lies wholly inside the following string $\psi$, and is therefore a <subformula> of $\psi$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Sub}(\neg\psi)=\{\neg\psi\}\cup\operatorname{Sub}(\psi).}
$$
Here $\operatorname{Sub}$ records distinct <logical formulas>; multiple occurrences of the same <logical formula> do not create different elements of this <set>.

A <subformula> beginning at the first opening parenthesis equals the whole <logical formula> by <prefix-freeness of fully parenthesized formulas>. Every other <subformula> is the <logical formula> of a proper syntax subtree, by the <parsing> argument in part (iii). The two children of the root are precisely $\psi$ and $\chi$, so such a subtree lies entirely inside one of those children. In particular no <logical formula> substring can begin in $\psi$ and cross the principal <logical implication> symbol into $\chi$.

Conversely, each <subformula> of either child is visibly a substring of the whole expression, as is the whole expression itself. Thus
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Sub}((\psi\to\chi))=\{(\psi\to\chi)\}\cup\operatorname{Sub}(\psi)\cup\operatorname{Sub}(\chi).}
$$
Together these cases identify the substring definition of a <subformula> with its usual syntax-tree definition.

Fix the particular occurrence of $\alpha$ that is being replaced. The <subformula> analysis above makes it a node of the <formula syntax tree> of $\phi$. Proceed by <structural induction> on the surrounding <logical formula>.

If this node is the root, the <subformula replacement> gives $\beta$, already a <logical formula>. Otherwise, if $\phi=\neg\psi$, the chosen occurrence lies inside $\psi$. The induction hypothesis gives a <logical formula> $\psi'$ after the <subformula replacement> there, and the whole result is the <logical formula> $\neg\psi'$. If $\phi=(\psi\to\chi)$, a proper chosen occurrence lies in exactly one of the two children. Replacing inside that child gives either $(\psi'\to\chi)$ or $(\psi\to\chi')$, each a <logical formula> by the <formation rule>. A <logical variable> has no proper <subformula>, so there is no additional base case.

Therefore **replacing the designated <subformula occurrence> by any <logical formula> preserves well-formedness**. The argument concerns one chosen occurrence; it does not require simultaneous <subformula replacement> of every occurrence of the same string.

Fix any <Boolean valuation> $v$. <Logical equivalence> of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ means $v(\alpha)=v(\beta)$ for this <Boolean valuation> and every other one. Follow the unique path from the replaced node to the root of the <formula syntax tree>. At a <negation> node, equal input <truth values> have equal <negations>. At a <logical implication> node, the unaffected child has the same <truth value> before and after <subformula replacement>, while the affected child has the same <truth value> by induction; the resulting <logical implications> therefore agree as well.

Induction along that finite path yields $v(\phi)=v(\phi')$. Since $v$ was arbitrary,
$$
\boxed{\phi\equiv\phi'.}
$$
This is the <replacement theorem for logical equivalence>: the semantics of each <logical connective> depends only on the <truth values> of its immediate inputs.

A collection of <logical connectives> is functionally complete, or adequate, if every <Boolean function> of finitely many <propositional variables> is represented by a <logical formula> using only those <logical connectives>. More explicitly, for every $n\geq1$ and every $F:\{0,1\}^n\to\{0,1\}$ there is a <logical formula> $\theta(p_1,\ldots,p_n)$ whose <truth value> under each <Boolean valuation> is $F(v(p_1),\ldots,v(p_n))$. <Constant functions> with Boolean values may use an otherwise irrelevant <logical variable>, for example $p\to p$ for truth and $\neg(p\to p)$ for falsity.

Translate <logical formulas> recursively, leaving <propositional variables> unchanged and translating <negation> by applying <negation> to the translation. For the other two <logical connectives> <set>
$$
T(\psi\lor\chi)=(\neg T(\psi)\to T(\chi)),\qquad
T(\psi\land\chi)=\neg(T(\psi)\to\neg T(\chi)).
$$
The first <logical implication> is false exactly when both original inputs are false; the second displayed <logical formula> is true exactly when both inputs are true. Hence each clause preserves <truth values>. <Structural induction> gives $v(T(\theta))=v(\theta)$ for every <Boolean valuation> and every <logical formula> $\theta$ in the larger language.

By the assumed <functional completeness> of <negation>, <logical conjunction> and <logical disjunction>, any finite <Boolean function> has such a <logical formula> $\theta$. Its translation $T(\theta)$ uses only <negation> and <logical implication> and represents the same <function>. **Thus $\{\neg,\to\}$ is adequate.**

An $L$-structure consists of a nonempty domain $A$, an element $c^{\mathcal A}\in A$ for each <constant symbol>, a <function> $f^{\mathcal A}:A^n\to A$ for each $n$-ary <function symbol>, and a relation $R^{\mathcal A}\subseteq A^n$ for each $n$-ary <predicate symbol>. When <equality> is part of the logic it is interpreted as actual identity on $A$.

An <assignment in a first-order structure> is a <function> $s$ from <logical variables> to $A$. It induces a <term valuation> of <first-order terms> recursively:
$$
x^{\mathcal A}[s]=s(x),\qquad c^{\mathcal A}[s]=c^{\mathcal A},\qquad
f(t_1,\ldots,t_n)^{\mathcal A}[s]=f^{\mathcal A}(t_1^{\mathcal A}[s],\ldots,t_n^{\mathcal A}[s]).
$$
For an <atomic formula>, substitute these <first-order term> values into the interpreted relation, or compare them for <equality>. Extend <satisfaction for a set structure> through the <truth tables> of the <logical connectives>, and through the <quantifier> clauses
$$
\mathcal A,s\models\forall x\theta\ \Longleftrightarrow\ (\forall a\in A)\ \mathcal A,s[x\mapsto a]\models\theta,
$$
$$
\mathcal A,s\models\exists x\theta\ \Longleftrightarrow\ (\exists a\in A)\ \mathcal A,s[x\mapsto a]\models\theta.
$$
Thus the <first-order structure> and <assignment in a first-order structure> together determine <truth values> for all <logical formulas>. A <first-order sentence> has no <free variables>, so its truth does not depend on the <assignment in a first-order structure>.

**No <finite axiomatization> exists.** Write $\Gamma$ for the <axioms> just constructed and suppose a <finite set> $\Delta$ of <first-order sentences> had exactly the same <first-order models>. Its <logical conjunction> $\delta$ is a single <first-order sentence>, and $\Gamma\models\delta$. By the <compactness theorem> there is a finite <subset> $\Gamma_0\subseteq\Gamma$ with $\Gamma_0\models\delta$: otherwise $\Gamma\cup\{\neg\delta\}$ would be finitely satisfiable and hence satisfiable, a contradiction.

Only finitely many of the integers $n$ in the torsion-free scheme occur in $\Gamma_0$. Choose a <prime number> $p$ larger than all of them. The <cyclic group> $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$ satisfies all the <group axioms> and each of these finitely many conditions from the <torsion-free abelian group axiom scheme>: <multiplication> by an <integer> $1\le n<p$ is invertible modulo $p$, so $nx=0$ implies $x=0$. It consequently satisfies $\Gamma_0$, then $\delta$, and therefore $\Delta$.

But its nonzero elements have <finite order> $p$, so it is not a <torsion-free group>, contradicting the assumed description of the <first-order models> of $\Delta$. This establishes the <nonfinite axiomatizability of torsion-free abelian groups>, ruling out every <finite set> of defining <first-order sentences>, not just finite <subsets> of the particular scheme in part (c).

For a standard classical propositional calculus, strong completeness states
$$
\boxed{\Gamma\models\phi\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Gamma\vdash\phi.}
$$
Together with <soundness>, this identifies semantic consequence with <formal derivability>. One proves the contrapositive by the <maximal consistent set in propositional logic> construction. If $\Gamma\nvdash\phi$, the <set> $\Gamma\cup\{\neg\phi\}$ is <propositionally consistent>, by the <deduction theorem for propositional logic> and classical propositional reasoning. Extend it to a <maximal consistent set in propositional logic> $M$, deciding each <logical formula> in turn. At each stage at least one selection of $\theta$ or $\neg\theta$ preserves <propositional consistency>; a contradiction in the <union> would use only finitely many assumptions and hence already occur at a stage.

Define a <Boolean valuation> by $v(p_i)=1$ exactly when $p_i\in M$. <Maximal consistency in propositional logic> gives $\neg\theta\in M$ exactly when $\theta\notin M$, and $(\theta\to\eta)\in M$ exactly when either $\theta\notin M$ or $\eta\in M$. <Structural induction> on <logical formulas> therefore proves the <maximal consistent set truth lemma> $v(\theta)=1\Longleftrightarrow\theta\in M$. Thus $v$ satisfies $\Gamma$ but falsifies $\phi$, contradicting <semantic entailment>. The countable language here permits an enumeration of <logical formulas>; the corresponding general-language argument uses a <well-ordering> or a principle of <maximality in a partially ordered set>.

<Gödel's completeness theorem> for <first-order sentences> says that, in a standard sound first-order <Hilbert calculus>,
$$
\boxed{T\models\sigma\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T\vdash\sigma.}
$$
Equivalently every <first-order consistent> <set> of <first-order sentences> has a <first-order model>: apply this existence statement to $T\cup\{\neg\sigma\}$ for the consequence formulation.

For the <first-order model> existence <formal proof> use a <Henkin construction>. Enlarge the language by fresh <constant symbols> and adjoin <Henkin witness axioms>
$$
\exists x\theta(x)\to\theta(c_\theta).
$$
Freshness of $c_\theta$ preserves <first-order consistency>. In detail, if a contradiction followed after adjoining one witness <axiom>, the <deduction theorem for first-order logic> would yield its <negation>, from which one obtains both $\exists x\theta(x)$ and $\neg\theta(c_\theta)$ over the old theory. Since the new <constant symbol> does not occur in that theory or in $\theta$, replace it by a fresh <logical variable> and generalize to derive $\forall x\neg\theta(x)$, a contradiction. Finitely many witness additions preserve <first-order consistency> successively, and <unions> preserve <first-order consistency> because <formal proofs> are finite. Iterate the enlargement so that <logical formulas> involving newly introduced <constant symbols> also receive witnesses. Include at least one <constant symbol> to guarantee <closed terms>.

Extend the resulting <first-order consistent> <witnessed first-order theory> to a <maximal consistent set of first-order sentences> $H$. In the equality-free case make the domain the <set> of <closed terms>. Interpret <constant symbols> and <function symbols> by their formal <first-order term> constructions; interpret an <atomic formula> by
$$
R(t_1,\ldots,t_n)\text{ holds in the term model}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad R(t_1,\ldots,t_n)\in H.
$$
The <truth lemma for a Henkin term model> follows by induction on <logical formulas> with <closed terms> substituted for their <free variables>. The <logical connective> steps use <maximal consistency of a first-order theory>. In the existential step, membership of $\exists x\theta$ supplies a <witness constant> and hence a <first-order term> satisfying $\theta$; conversely a satisfying <closed term> gives the <existential sentence> by an inference of the calculus. The <term model> consequently satisfies $H$ and the original theory. <Equality> is absent here, so distinct <closed terms> may remain distinct domain elements without further identifications.

In the presence of <equality>, two syntactically different <closed terms> can be asserted equal by the <Henkin theory>, so the raw <first-order term> domain from part (b) must be replaced by a <quotient set>. Define
$$
t\sim u\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (t=u)\in H.
$$
The <equality> <axioms> give reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity, so this is an <equivalence relation>. Their substitution consequences make it a <congruence relation on a first-order structure>: replacing an input by an equivalent <first-order term> preserves equivalence of <function> values and preserves membership of <atomic formulas> in $H$.

Take domain $\{[t]:t\text{ is a closed term}\}$. Define a <function symbol> by $f([t_1],\ldots,[t_n])=[f(t_1,\ldots,t_n)]$, and relations by the same membership test as before using representatives. The <congruence relation on a first-order structure> makes these definitions independent of representatives. Actual identity of <equivalence classes> now agrees with the formal <equality> <first-order sentences> in $H$:
$$
\boxed{[t]=[u]\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(t=u)\in H.}
$$
This supplies the <equality> case of the <truth lemma for a Henkin term model>; the <logical connective> and <quantifier> cases then proceed exactly as in the <Henkin construction>. Thus the <term model> built from the <quotient set> is a genuine <first-order model> with <equality> interpreted as identity.

For a countable first-order language, the <Henkin construction> introduces only countably many <constant symbols>: each round needs at most one for each <logical formula>, there are countably many finite strings in a countable alphabet, and countably many rounds suffice. There are therefore at most countably many <closed terms>. The <term model>, and also its <quotient set> by <equality>, has at most countably many elements.

It follows that **every satisfiable theory in a countable first-order language has an at most countable <first-order model>**. If the theory has no finite <first-order model>, the constructed <first-order model> is countably infinite. More generally the same counting argument gives a <first-order model> of <cardinality> at most $\max(|L|,\aleph_0)$ for any satisfiable theory in language $L$.

For the elementary-substructure version of the downward <Löwenheim-Skolem theorem>, begin with a <first-order structure> $M$ and a <set> $S$ of designated elements. Close $S$, the named <constant symbols> and one initial element under the language's <functions> and under chosen witnesses in $M$ for <existential formulas> with parameters already chosen. Iterate countably often. The resulting <set> has <cardinality> at most $\max(|L|,|S|,\aleph_0)$, and its induced substructure $N$ is elementary: induction on <logical formulas> proves the truth equivalence, with the existential case supplied by the chosen witnesses. This is the same witness mechanism as the Henkin <formal proof>, now keeping witnesses inside a given <first-order model>.

Let $T=\operatorname{Th}(\mathbb C)$ in the finite language of rings: it is the <set> of all <first-order sentences> true in the <field> of <complex numbers>. It is satisfiable, with <first-order model> $\mathbb C$, so the countable-language <Löwenheim-Skolem theorem> gives an at most countable <first-order model> $K$ of $T$.

All <field axioms> belong to $T$, hence $K$ is a <field>. For every positive <integer> $n$, the <first-order sentence> saying that there are at least $n$ distinct elements is also true in $\mathbb C$ and belongs to $T$. Thus $K$ cannot be finite, and is countably infinite.

Finally, for each <first-order sentence> $\sigma$, either $\sigma\in T$ or $\neg\sigma\in T$. Since $K$ satisfies all of $T$, it satisfies $\sigma$ exactly when $\mathbb C$ does. Therefore
$$
\boxed{|K|=\aleph_0,\qquad K\equiv\mathbb C.}
$$

First give the recursive definitions, keeping the right argument as the recursion <variable>. For <ordinals> $\alpha,\beta$ and nonzero <limit ordinals> $\lambda$,
$$
\alpha+0=\alpha,\qquad \alpha+(\beta+1)=(\alpha+\beta)+1,\qquad
\alpha+\lambda=\sup_{\xi<\lambda}(\alpha+\xi),
$$
$$
\alpha\cdot0=0,\qquad \alpha\cdot(\beta+1)=\alpha\beta+\alpha,\qquad
\alpha\lambda=\sup_{\xi<\lambda}\alpha\xi.
$$
Here $\xi+1$ is the <successor ordinal>, and the <supremum> of a <set> of <ordinals> is its <union>. These are <ordinal arithmetic>, defined by <transfinite recursion>, and differ from <cardinal sum> and <ordinal multiplication>.

**<Associativity> of <ordinal addition> is true.** Fix $\alpha,\beta$ and use <transfinite induction> on $\gamma$. The case $\gamma=0$ is immediate. In the successor step the recursive definitions and the induction hypothesis give
$$
(\alpha+\beta)+(\gamma+1)=((\alpha+\beta)+\gamma)+1
=(\alpha+(\beta+\gamma))+1=\alpha+(\beta+(\gamma+1)).
$$
For a nonzero <limit ordinal> $\lambda$, the <ordinals> $\beta+\xi$ for $\xi<\lambda$ form an increasing <cofinal subset> of the <limit ordinal> $\beta+\lambda$. The limit clause for <ordinal addition>, restricted to this <cofinal subset>, gives
$$
(\alpha+\beta)+\lambda
=\sup_{\xi<\lambda}((\alpha+\beta)+\xi)
=\sup_{\xi<\lambda}(\alpha+(\beta+\xi))
=\alpha+(\beta+\lambda).
$$
<Cofinal> restriction is valid because <ordinal addition> by a fixed <ordinal> on the left is increasing and continuous in its right argument, directly from its recursive definition. This completes the induction.

$$
\bigcap P=\{a\},\qquad \bigcup P=\{a,b\}.
$$
The first <equality> recovers $a$. Once $a$ is known, the second recovers $b$: if its <union> is the <singleton> $\{a\}$ then $b=a$, and otherwise $b$ is its unique element different from $a$. Hence <equality> of two encoded pairs forces <equality> of both coordinates, including the degenerate case $a=b$.

The <Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem> states that <injections> $f:A\to B$ and $g:B\to A$ imply the existence of a <bijection> $A\to B$. Here is the injection-chain <formal proof>, so no fixed-point theorem is needed. Define
$$
A_0=A\setminus g(B),\qquad A_{n+1}=g(f(A_n)),\qquad C=\bigcup_{n<\omega}A_n,
$$
and <set>
$$
h(a)=\begin{cases}f(a),&a\in C,\\ g^{-1}(a),&a\notin C.\end{cases}
$$
The second branch is defined because $a\notin C$ implies $a\notin A_0$, hence $a\in g(B)$, and the inverse there is unique by <injectivity> of $g$. Each branch is <injective>. A collision between branches would have $f(c)=g^{-1}(a)$ with $c\in C$ and $a\notin C$, implying $a=g(f(c))\in C$, a contradiction. Thus $h$ is <injective>.

For <surjectivity> let $b\in B$. If $g(b)\notin C$, then $h(g(b))=b$. If $g(b)\in C$, it is not in $A_0$, so it belongs to some $A_{n+1}=g(f(A_n))$. There is $a\in A_n$ with $g(b)=g(f(a))$; <injectivity> of $g$ gives $b=f(a)=h(a)$. This proves that $h$ is a <bijection> and completes the theorem without the <axiom of choice>.

For the comparison of <cardinalities> take the <power set> of $\omega$ as the reference <set>. Its <cardinality> is by definition
$$
\boxed{|\mathcal P(\omega)|=2^{\aleph_0}.}
$$
The remaining parts give explicit <injections> or <bijections> comparing each requested <set> with this one.

<Hartogs theorem> says that for every <set> $X$ there is a least <ordinal> $h(X)$ for which no <injection> $h(X)\to X$ exists. This does not require the <axiom of choice>. Form the <set> $W$ of <well-orderings> of <subsets> of $X$. It can be defined by the <axiom schema of specification> inside
$$
\mathcal P(X)\times\mathcal P(X\times X),
$$
recording a <subset> and its <well-order>. Each member of $W$ has a unique <ordinal> <order type>. **<Axiom schema of replacement> is used to collect those order types into a <set>**
$$
H=\{\operatorname{otp}(Y,R):(Y,R)\in W\}.
$$
Every member of an <ordinal> in $H$ is the <order type> of an initial segment of the corresponding <well-ordering>, so also belongs to $H$. Thus $H$ is a <transitive set> of <ordinals> and is itself an <ordinal>. An <ordinal> $\alpha$ belongs to $H$ exactly when it injects into $X$: use a <bijection> to a <well-ordered> <subset> in one direction, and transport the <ordinal> order along the <injection> in the other. If $H$ injected into $X$, this characterization would give $H\in H$, impossible for an <ordinal>. All smaller <ordinals> lie in $H$ and do inject into $X$. Therefore $H=h(X)$. Apart from the standard order-type theorem, the explicit collection of all order types is the <Axiom schema of replacement> step; no <axiom of choice> of a representative ordering for each type is needed.

<Zorn's lemma> states that a nonempty <partially ordered set> in which every <chain> has an <upper bound> has a <maximal element>. Use the <Hartogs bound on partial ordinal injections> to obtain a set-sized <partially ordered set>. Consider the partial <injections> into $X$ whose domains are <ordinals>. <Hartogs theorem> bounds every such domain strictly below $h=h(X)$. Their <graphs of a function> therefore form a <set>
$$
P=\{f\subseteq h\times X:f\text{ is an injective function and }\operatorname{dom}f\text{ is an ordinal}\},
$$
obtained by the <axiom schema of specification> from $\mathcal P(h\times X)$. Order $P$ by extension. It is nonempty because it contains the empty <function>. A <chain> has as an <upper bound> the <union> of its <graphs of a function>: extension makes this a <function>, <injectivity> is preserved, and its domain is an <union> of <ordinals> and hence an <ordinal>. Its range is contained in $X$, so <Hartogs theorem> forces its domain to be strictly below $h$; thus it belongs to $P$. The empty <chain> also has the empty <function> as an <upper bound>.

By <Zorn's lemma> choose a maximal $f:\alpha\to X$. If an element $x\in X$ lay outside its range, appending $f(\alpha)=x$ would give a larger <injection> on $\alpha+1$. Such an <injection> itself guarantees $\alpha+1<h$ by <Hartogs theorem>, so it is a legitimate member of $P$, contradicting <maximality in a partially ordered set>. Therefore $f$ is <surjective>, and
$$
\boxed{X\text{ is equinumerous with an ordinal}.}
$$
For this single extension one only selects an element of one specified nonempty <set>; no additional simultaneous choices are hidden in the argument. <Zorn's lemma> is the form of the <axiom of choice> used to obtain <maximality in a partially ordered set>.

Finally work with <cardinals>, represented by initial <ordinals>, and put $\mu=\max(\kappa,\lambda)$. It is infinite by hypothesis. Nonzeroness of each factor gives an <injection> of the larger <cardinal> into the <Cartesian product> by holding a coordinate of the other fixed. The supplied infinite-ordinal square result gives $|\mu\times\mu|=\mu$. Hence
$$
\mu\leq\kappa\lambda\leq|\mu\times\mu|=\mu.
$$
For the sum, use <disjoint union>:
$$
\mu\leq\kappa+\lambda\leq|\mu\times\{0,1\}|\leq|\mu\times\mu|=\mu.
$$
The last <injection> exists because $\mu$ is infinite and contains two distinct elements. <Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem> turns both pairs of bounds into equalities, proving
$$
\boxed{\kappa+\lambda=\kappa\lambda=\max(\kappa,\lambda).}
$$
These final operations are <cardinal sum> and <multiplication>, measuring disjoint <unions> and Cartesian products. They are not the noncommutative <ordinal arithmetic> considered in question 5.
