A <Boolean valuation> is an arbitrary <function> $v$ assigning one of the two <truth values> to each <propositional variable>. It extends uniquely to all <propositional formulas> by following the construction of the <propositional formula>:
$$
\widetilde v(p)=v(p),\quad
\widetilde v(\neg A)=\neg\widetilde v(A),\quad
\widetilde v(A\wedge B)=\widetilde v(A)\wedge\widetilde v(B),\quad
\widetilde v(A\vee B)=\widetilde v(A)\vee\widetilde v(B).
$$
For <logical implication>, $\widetilde v(A\to B)$ is false exactly when $\widetilde v(A)$ is true and $\widetilde v(B)$ is false; otherwise it is true. Here the operations on the right are <Boolean operations> on <truth values>. <Structural recursion> gives existence, and <structural induction> gives uniqueness: once the immediate subformulas have their values, the outer connective fixes the resulting value.

Two <propositional formulas> are <logically equivalent> if every <Boolean valuation> gives them the same <truth value>. A <disjunctive normal form> is a <logical disjunction> of <logical conjunctions> of <literals>, with a <literal> being a <propositional variable> or its <negation>.

Let $p_1,\ldots,p_n$ include all <propositional variables> appearing in $\phi$. For each <Boolean valuation> $s\in\{T,F\}^n$, put
$$
M_s=\bigwedge_{j=1}^n L_{s,j},\qquad
L_{s,j}=\begin{cases}p_j&s_j=T,\\\neg p_j&s_j=F.\end{cases}
$$
The <minterm> $M_s$ is true exactly for <Boolean valuations> whose restrictions to these <propositional variables> are $s$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\phi\equiv\bigvee_{s:\,\widetilde s(\phi)=T}M_s.}
$$
Indeed, an <Boolean valuation> making $\phi$ true makes its own <minterm> true, while a true <minterm> in the displayed <logical disjunction> certifies the truth of $\phi$. There are only finitely many <Boolean valuations>, so this is a finite <disjunctive normal form>. If there are no satisfying <Boolean valuations>, use $p\wedge\neg p$ for any <propositional variable> $p$; this is a <disjunctive normal form> for falsity without needing an empty <logical disjunction> convention.

Assume first that $\phi$ is false on the all-true and all-false <Boolean valuations>. Use the truth-table normal form from part (ii). Every satisfying <Boolean valuation> on the finitely many <propositional variables> must have at least one true <propositional variable> and at least one false <propositional variable>. Its <minterm> therefore has the form
$$
C_s\wedge\neg D_s,
$$
where $C_s$ conjoins its true <propositional variables> and $D_s$ disjoins its false <propositional variables>; both lists are nonempty. This is equivalent to $\neg(C_s\to D_s)$. If the satisfying <Boolean valuations> are indexed by $1,\ldots,N$, <De Morgan's laws> gives
$$
\boxed{\phi\equiv\bigvee_{i=1}^N\neg(C_i\to D_i)
\equiv\neg\bigwedge_{i=1}^N(C_i\to D_i).}
$$
If there are no satisfying <Boolean valuations>, take $N=1$ and $C_1=D_1=p$: the result is $\neg(p\to p)$, again always false.

Conversely, on the all-true <Boolean valuation> every nonempty $D_i$ is true, so every $C_i\to D_i$ is true. On the all-false <Boolean valuation> every nonempty $C_i$ is false, so every <logical implication> is again true. Negating their <logical conjunction> gives false on both <Boolean valuations>. <Logical equivalence> transfers these values to $\phi$. The usual nonempty meaning of <logical conjunction> and <logical disjunction> of <propositional variables> is used here; allowing empty $C_i$ or $D_i$ without qualification would destroy this converse.

A <logical derivation> from $\Gamma$ is a finite <sequence> of <propositional formulas>, each either a member of $\Gamma$, an instance of one of the three <axioms>, or obtained from earlier lines by one of the two displayed <inference rules>. The notation $\Gamma\vdash\alpha$ means that some such <logical derivation> ends in $\alpha$. Writing $\vdash\alpha$ means derivability with no assumptions.

<Propositional consistency> means that there is no <propositional formula> $\theta$ for which both $\Gamma\vdash\theta$ and $\Gamma\vdash\neg\theta$. Equivalently not every <propositional formula> is derivable. To check the equivalence directly for this calculus, use the identity theorem proved in (ii): from $\theta\to\theta$ and L3 obtain $\theta\to(\neg\theta\to\eta)$; two uses of <modus ponens rule> give arbitrary $\eta$. Thus a contradictory theory proves everything. Conversely a theory proving everything proves a <propositional formula> and its <negation>.

\alpha\to(\beta\to\alpha),\qquad

The instance of L2 with $\gamma=\alpha$ is

(\alpha\to(\beta\to\alpha))\to
((\alpha\to(\neg\beta\to\alpha))\to(\alpha\to\alpha)).

Apply <modus ponens rule> first with the first L1 instance and then with the second. This is an assumption-free <logical derivation>, so
$$
\boxed{\vdash\alpha\to\alpha.}
$$
In particular no semantic completeness theorem or <deduction theorem for propositional logic> has been assumed to obtain this identity theorem.

For this calculus the <deduction theorem for propositional logic> is
$$
\boxed{\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\vdash\beta
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Gamma\vdash\alpha\to\beta.}
$$
For the reverse <logical implication>, retain the <logical derivation> from $\Gamma$, add the assumption $\alpha$, and use <modus ponens rule>.

For the forward <logical implication>, induct on a <logical derivation> from $\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}$. For each line $\delta$ we construct a <logical derivation> of $\alpha\to\delta$ from $\Gamma$. If $\delta=\alpha$, use (ii). If $\delta$ is an axiom instance or belongs to $\Gamma$, it is already derivable from $\Gamma$; L1 gives $\delta\to(\alpha\to\delta)$, and <modus ponens rule> finishes that case.

If $\delta$ comes from $\eta$ and $\eta\to\delta$ by <modus ponens rule>, the <induction hypothesis> supplies
$$
\Gamma\vdash\alpha\to\eta,\qquad
\Gamma\vdash\alpha\to(\eta\to\delta).
$$
Part (iii) supplies $\Gamma\vdash\alpha\to\delta$.

If $\delta$ comes by the elimination rule E from $\eta\to\delta$ and $\neg\eta\to\delta$, the <induction hypothesis> supplies
$$
\Gamma\vdash\alpha\to(\eta\to\delta),\qquad
\Gamma\vdash\alpha\to(\neg\eta\to\delta).
$$
The L2 instance with $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(\alpha,\eta,\delta)$ combines these by two applications of <modus ponens rule>. Thus the rule E also survives the discharge construction. These are all possible lines, proving the theorem for the final line $\beta$.

Suppose both extensions were inconsistent. By the <explosion> argument in (i), the extension by $\phi$ proves $\neg\phi$, and the extension by $\neg\phi$ proves $\phi$. The <deduction theorem for propositional logic> therefore yields
$$
\Gamma\vdash\phi\to\neg\phi,\qquad
\Gamma\vdash\neg\phi\to\phi.

The identity theorem gives $\vdash\neg\phi\to\neg\phi$ and $\vdash\phi\to\phi$. Apply E to the first displayed <logical implication> and the identity <logical implication> with antecedent $\neg\phi$ to obtain $\Gamma\vdash\neg\phi$. Apply E to the identity <logical implication> with antecedent $\phi$ and the second displayed <logical implication> to obtain $\Gamma\vdash\phi$. This contradicts <propositional consistency> of $\Gamma$.

Consequently **at least one of the two one-formula extensions is <propositionally consistent>**. The <formal proof> uses the source's own calculus, including its rule E, rather than assuming an unproved completeness theorem for this presentation.

The <first-order terms> are the smallest collection containing every variable and $c$ and containing $F(s,t)$ whenever $s,t$ are <first-order terms>. The <atomic formulas> are exactly $s\approx t$ for <first-order terms> $s,t$. <Logical formulas> are then obtained from <atomic formulas> by the <logical connectives> and <quantifiers>: if $\theta,\psi$ are <logical formulas> and $x$ a variable, then $\neg\theta$, $(\theta\wedge\psi)$, $(\theta\vee\psi)$, $(\theta\to\psi)$, $\forall x\theta$ and $\exists x\theta$ are <logical formulas>. These closure rules, with no additional strings admitted, define the syntax; a presentation using fewer primitive connectives treats the others as abbreviations.

<Semantic entailment> is the condition
$$
\boxed{\Gamma\models\phi\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{for every }\mathfrak A\text{ and every assignment }v,
\bigl(\mathfrak A\models\gamma[v]\text{ for all }\gamma\in\Gamma\bigr)
\Longrightarrow\mathfrak A\models\phi[v].}
$$
The same <first-order variable assignment> is used for all premises and the conclusion. This matters when <free variables> occur; for <first-order sentences> the <truth value> is independent of the <first-order variable assignment>.

For independence, construct four <first-order structures>, each satisfying exactly the three required remaining <axioms> and failing the chosen one.

To fail <associativity>, use the carrier $\{0,1,2\}$, interpret $c$ as $0$, and define the commutative operation by its table:
$$
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
F&0&1&2\\\hline
0&0&1&2\\
1&1&0&1\\
2&2&1&0
\end{array}
$$
The first row and column give a two-sided identity, and every element is its own inverse because the diagonal entries are $0$. <Symmetry> gives <commutativity>. But
$$
F(F(1,1),2)=2\ne0=F(1,F(1,2)),
$$
so <associativity> fails.

To fail the identity axiom, take $\{0,1\}$, $c=0$, and the constant operation $F(x,y)=0$. It is associative and commutative, and every element has a two-sided partner producing $c$; but $F(1,c)=0\ne1$.

To fail the inverse axiom, use <natural numbers> under <addition> with $c=0$. <Addition> is associative and commutative and has the required identity, but $1$ has no additive inverse in this carrier.

To fail <commutativity>, use the <symmetric group> $S_3$ under <permutation> composition with $c$ its identity. <Associativity>, identity and inverses are <group> properties, while $(12)(23)\ne(23)(12)$. Each countermodel witnesses the failure of <semantic entailment> from the other three to the failed <first-order sentence>. Hence **the four source <axioms> are independent**.

<Soundness> states that <formal derivability> implies <semantic entailment>:
$$
\boxed{\Gamma\vdash_K\phi\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Gamma\models\phi.}
$$
For <first-order sentences> this means that every <first-order structure> satisfying all assumptions also satisfies every <first-order sentence> formally derived from them. In the <logical formula> version one uses the usual restrictions on generalization from assumptions with <free variables>.

Strong completeness states the converse:
$$
\boxed{\Gamma\models\phi\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Gamma\vdash_K\phi.}
$$
Equivalently, every syntactically consistent <set> of <first-order sentences> has a <first-order model>. Equality is interpreted as actual identity, and its usual logical <axioms> and substitution rules are part of the standard calculus. Both assertions hold for arbitrary <sets> of assumptions, rather than only <finite sets>.

The <compactness theorem> says that a <set> $T$ of <first-order sentences> has a <first-order model> if and only if every finite <subset> of $T$ has a <first-order model>. One direction follows by using the same <first-order model> for each <subset>.

For the nontrivial direction suppose every finite <subset> has a <first-order model>, but $T$ has none. Semantically $T\models\bot$, so completeness gives a formal <logical derivation> $T\vdash\bot$. A formal <logical derivation> is finite and uses only finitely many assumptions, say $T_0\subseteq T$. Thus $T_0\vdash\bot$. <Soundness> would make $T_0$ unsatisfiable, contradicting the hypothesis. Here $\bot$ can be any fixed contradictory <first-order sentence>, such as $\exists x\,(x\ne x)$, if it is not a primitive logical symbol. Therefore **finite satisfiability implies satisfiability**.

Take $S_1$ to consist of the three usual <first-order sentences>
$$
\forall x\ E(x,x),\qquad
\forall x\forall y\,(E(x,y)\to E(y,x)),\qquad
\forall x\forall y\forall z\,((E(x,y)\wedge E(y,z))\to E(x,z)).
$$
These assert <reflexivity>, <symmetry> and <transitivity> and therefore define exactly the <equivalence relations>.

For $S_2$, adjoin to $S_1$ the single <first-order sentence>
$$
\forall x\exists y\,[y\ne x\wedge E(x,y)\wedge
\forall z\,(E(x,z)\to(z=x\vee z=y))].
$$
Its witness $y$ gives a second member of each class, while its final universal clause excludes all others. Hence every class has exactly two members, and conversely such a relation satisfies it. This remains a <finite set> of <first-order sentences>.

For each positive <integer> $n$, write
$$
\theta_n(x)=\exists y_1\cdots\exists y_n
\left(\bigwedge_{i=1}^n E(x,y_i)\wedge
\bigwedge_{1\le i<j\le n}y_i\ne y_j\right).
$$
Then
$$
\boxed{S_3=S_1\cup\{\forall x\,\theta_n(x):n\ge1\}.}
$$
Every class in a <first-order model> has at least $n$ elements for every $n$, so cannot be finite. Conversely every infinite <equivalence class> supplies each finite collection of distinct witnesses. The displayed infinite scheme therefore has exactly the requested <first-order models>.

The <axiom of infinity> asserts that an <inductive set> exists: some <set> $I$ contains $0=\varnothing$ and contains $x^+=x\cup\{x\}$ whenever it contains $x$. Inside one such $I$, <axiom of separation> defines
$$
\omega=\{x\in I:x\text{ belongs to every inductive subset of }I\}.
$$
This <intersection> is itself inductive and is contained in every <inductive set>, by intersecting that <set> with $I$. It is therefore the <set> of <natural numbers>. If a property holds at $0$ and passes from $n$ to $n^+$, <axiom of separation> forms the <subset> of $\omega$ on which it holds. That <subset> is inductive, so must equal $\omega$. This proves <mathematical induction>, including properties with <set> parameters.

<Recursion> on $\omega$ defines the arithmetic operations by
$$
m+0=m,\qquad m+n^+=(m+n)^+,\qquad
m\cdot0=0,\qquad m\cdot n^+=m\cdot n+m.
$$
Existence and uniqueness follow from the <recursion theorem on the natural numbers>.

For <associativity> of <addition> fix $m,n$ and induct on $k$. At $k=0$ both sides equal $m+n$. If the equality holds at $k$, then
$$
m+(n+k^+)=m+(n+k)^+=(m+(n+k))^+
=((m+n)+k)^+=(m+n)+k^+.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{m+(n+k)=(m+n)+k.}
$$
Every step after the <induction hypothesis> is a defining successor equation, so <commutativity> has not been used prematurely.

Fix $m$ and induct on $n$. The initial equality $m+0=0+m$ follows from the defining <zero> clause and (ii). Assuming $m+n=n+m$, the successor clause and (iii) give
$$
m+n^+=(m+n)^+=(n+m)^+=n+m^+=n^++m.

This proves
$$
\boxed{m+n=n+m\quad(m,n\in\omega).}

The extension to arbitrary <ordinals> is false. By the <limit> clause for <ordinal addition>,
$$
1+\omega=\sup_{n<\omega}(1+n)=\omega,
$$
whereas $\omega+1$ is the <successor ordinal> of $\omega$ and is strictly greater than it. Therefore
$$
\boxed{1+\omega\ne\omega+1.}
$$
Finite <addition> is commutative; <ordinal addition> records the order of concatenated well-orders and need not be commutative.

Use the <Kuratowski ordered pair>
$$
\boxed{\langle x,y\rangle=\{\{x\},\{x,y\}\}.}
$$
The <axiom of pairing> first constructs $\{x\}$ and $\{x,y\}$, then constructs the displayed <pair> of those <sets>, proving that it is a <set>.

For any $a,b$, the <intersection> of the members of this <pair> is $\{a\}$, and their <union> is $\{a,b\}$. Thus equality of two <ordered pairs> first gives $\{a\}=\{c\}$, hence $a=c$, and then $\{a,b\}=\{a,d\}$. If $b=a$, this latter <set> is a <singleton> and forces $d=a$. If $b\ne a$, its unique member distinct from $a$ is $b$, forcing $d=b$. Hence equality of <ordered pairs> gives equality of both coordinates.

For a <set> $R$ of <ordered pairs>, both coordinates of every <pair> belong to
$$
U=\bigcup\bigcup R,
$$
which is a <set> by two applications of the <Axiom of union>. <Axiom of separation> inside $U$ gives
$$
\operatorname{dom}R=\{x\in U:\exists y\,\langle x,y\rangle\in R\},\qquad
\operatorname{ran}R=\{y\in U:\exists x\,\langle x,y\rangle\in R\}.
$$
These are <sets>, including when $R$ is empty.

The <Axiom of foundation> asserts that every nonempty <set> $A$ has an element $a$ with $a\cap A=\varnothing$. If a <descending membership sequence> $f:\omega\to V$ existed, <replacement> would give the nonempty range <set> $S=\{f(n):n\in\omega\}$. <Foundation> would give some $f(n)$ disjoint from $S$. But $f(n^+)\in f(n)$ and $f(n^+)\in S$, contradicting that disjointness. Thus <foundation> rules out every such <sequence>.

Conversely suppose there is no <descending membership sequence>, but <foundation> fails. There is then a nonempty <set> $A$ such that $a\cap A\ne\varnothing$ for every $a\in A$. The <axiom of choice> selects a <function> $h$ on $A$ with $h(a)\in a\cap A$. Choose $a_0\in A$ and use <recursion> to define
$$
f(0)=a_0,\qquad f(n^+)=h(f(n)).
$$
Every value remains in $A$, and $f(n^+)\in f(n)$ for every $n$. This is the forbidden <sequence>. Therefore, with the stated <choice> assumption, **<foundation> is equivalent to the absence of <descending membership sequences>**. The selection step explains exactly where <choice> is used; <dependent choice> would suffice.

The <Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem> says that <injections> $X\to Y$ and $Y\to X$ imply a <bijection> $X\to Y$. Thus mutual cardinal inequalities imply equal cardinalities, without a <choice> assumption.

Write $\mathfrak c=2^{\aleph_0}=|\mathbb R|$. Standard <cardinal arithmetic> gives $\mathfrak c\cdot\mathfrak c=\mathfrak c$ and $\mathfrak c^{\aleph_0}=\mathfrak c$. The correspondence $x+iy\leftrightarrow(x,y)$ is a <bijection> $\mathbb C\leftrightarrow\mathbb R^2$. Hence
$$
\boxed{|\mathbb C|=\mathfrak c.}
$$
Equivalently, the real axis supplies the lower inequality and a coding of two real coordinates supplies the upper one; Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein then gives the equality.

The <set> $D=\mathbb Q+i\mathbb Q$ is <countable> and dense in $\mathbb C$. Restriction to $D$ is <injective> on <continuous functions> $\mathbb C\to\mathbb R$: if two such <functions> agree there, their equality <set> is closed and contains a <dense set>, so is all of $\mathbb C$. Equivalently a <sequence> of points of $D$ converging to any chosen point and <continuity> determine its value uniquely.

Thus the family has <cardinality> at most
$$
|\mathbb R^D|=\mathfrak c^{\aleph_0}
=(2^{\aleph_0})^{\aleph_0}=2^{\aleph_0}=\mathfrak c.
$$
For the <lower bound>, every <real number> determines a different constant <continuous function>. Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein yields
$$
\boxed{|C(\mathbb C,\mathbb R)|=\mathfrak c.}
$$

Every strictly increasing <sequence> is in $\mathbb N^{\mathbb N}$, whose <cardinality> is $\aleph_0^{\aleph_0}=\mathfrak c$. This gives the <upper bound>.

For each binary <sequence> $\epsilon=(\epsilon_n)_{n\in\mathbb N}$, define
$$
a_n=2n+\epsilon_n.
$$
Then $a_{n+1}-a_n=2+\epsilon_{n+1}-\epsilon_n\ge1$, so the <sequence> is strictly increasing. Distinct binary <sequences> give distinct increasing <sequences>. This injects a <set> of size $2^{\aleph_0}$ into the desired family, giving the matching <lower bound>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{cardinality}=\mathfrak c.}
$$
One may also identify an increasing <sequence> with its infinite range <subset> of $\mathbb N$; the explicit binary coding establishes the <lower bound> without discarding finite <subsets> by cardinal subtraction.

The family is a <subset> of all complex <sequences>, so its <cardinality> is at most
$$
|\mathbb C^{\mathbb N}|=\mathfrak c^{\aleph_0}=\mathfrak c.
$$
For each $z\in\mathbb C$, the <sequence> $(z,0,0,\ldots)$ belongs to the family because its displayed <series> has just one nonzero <first-order term>. These <sequences> are distinct as $z$ varies, giving a <lower bound> $\mathfrak c$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{cardinality}=\mathfrak c.}
$$
The source condition is <convergence> of $\sum_j a_j^2$, without absolute-value bars. The argument uses exactly that printed condition and makes no assertion that it is equivalent to square summability of complex <sequences>. The same <upper and lower bounds> would also work for the stronger absolute-square condition, but no alteration of the source is needed.

Inclusion gives $|\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q|\le\mathfrak c$. If $\kappa=|\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q|$, the disjoint decomposition of $\mathbb R$ gives
$$
\mathfrak c=\aleph_0+\kappa.
$$
The <irrational numbers> are infinite, for instance $n+\sqrt2$ are pairwise distinct irrationals. Standard infinite cardinal <addition> gives $\aleph_0+\kappa=\max(\aleph_0,\kappa)$. Since <Cantor's theorem> gives $\mathfrak c>\aleph_0$, the equality forces $\kappa=\mathfrak c$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{|\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q|=\mathfrak c.}
$$
This is the permitted cardinal-arithmetic <formal proof>; it does not subtract infinite cardinals as though they were finite numbers.

<Zorn's lemma> states that a nonempty <partially ordered set> in which every <chain> has an <upper bound> contains a <maximal element>. Here a <chain> means a <subset> totally ordered by the inherited order; it need not have a greatest member.

View every <partial function> as its graph, a <subset> of $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R$. The family $\mathcal F$ is a <set> by <axiom of separation> from the <power set> of this product. Let $\mathcal C\subseteq\mathcal F$ be a <chain> and put $h=\bigcup\mathcal C$. If two members of the <union> assign values to the same input, the <chain> property makes one graph contain the other, so those values agree. Thus $h$ is a <partial function>.

If $x\in\operatorname{dom}h$ and $q\in\mathbb Q$, choose a <chain> member containing $x$ in its domain. Its rational-scalar closure gives $qx\in\operatorname{dom}h$. If $x,y\in\operatorname{dom}h$, choose two <chain> members containing them; the larger one contains both, and therefore contains $x+y$ with value $h(x)+h(y)$. Hence $h$ satisfies the required additive identity and domain closure. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\bigcup\mathcal C\in\mathcal F,}
$$
and it is an <upper bound> for the <chain>. For the empty <chain> its <union> is the <empty function>, which satisfies the defining <logical implications> vacuously, so this case is covered too.

A nonempty <weakly linear function> has a domain $D$ closed under rational scalars and <addition>, hence a <vector subspace> of $\mathbb R$ over $\mathbb Q$. Additivity first gives $f(0)=0$ and $f(-x)=-f(x)$, then $f(nx)=nf(x)$ for <integers> $n$. If $q=m/n$ with $n>0$, rational closure puts $qx$ in $D$ and
$$
n f(qx)=f(nqx)=f(mx)=m f(x).
$$
Thus $f(qx)=qf(x)$: it is a $\mathbb Q$-linear map. The <empty function> is not maximal because it extends to the <function> on $\{0\}$ with value <zero>.

Suppose a maximal $f$ had proper domain $D\ne\mathbb R$. Choose $t\notin D$ and any prescribed real value $a$, for instance $a=0$. Every element of $D+\mathbb Qt$ has a unique expression $d+qt$: if two expressions coincide and the coefficients differ, then $t$ would belong to $D$. Define
$$
h(d+qt)=f(d)+qa.
$$
Uniqueness makes this well-defined. The displayed <logical formula> and the $\mathbb Q$-linearity of $f$ show directly that $h$ is additive and has a domain closed under rational scalars. Hence it is a <weakly linear function> and extends $f$. It is a proper extension because $t$ is now in its domain, contradicting maximality. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{dom}f=\mathbb R\quad\text{for every maximal }f\in\mathcal F.}
$$
Part (a) and <Zorn's lemma> guarantee the existence of maximal extensions as well as this description of their domains.

Irrationality of $\sqrt2$ makes the expression $q+r\sqrt2$ unique for rational $q,r$: equality of two such expressions with different $r$ would express $\sqrt2$ as a <rational number>. Therefore the rule selecting the coefficient $r$ defines a <function> on $A$. Its domain is a <vector subspace> over $\mathbb Q$, and direct coefficient <addition> and rational scaling show it is $\mathbb Q$-linear, hence weakly linear. In particular
$$
f(1)=0,\qquad f(\sqrt2)=1.
$$
Use <Zorn's lemma> on the nonempty family of weakly <linear extensions> of this particular <function>. <Chain> <unions> remain extensions by (a); hence there is a maximal extension $h$. Any further extension in $\mathcal F$ would also contain the original <function>, so this $h$ is maximal in $\mathcal F$. Part (b) therefore gives $\operatorname{dom}h=\mathbb R$.

A <real-linear map> $\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ is <multiplication> by its value at $1$. Were $h$ real-linear, it would satisfy
$$
h(\sqrt2)=\sqrt2\,h(1)=0,
$$
contradicting the inherited value $h(\sqrt2)=1$. Thus **a <weakly linear function> on all of $\mathbb R$ need not be real-linear**. Its <rational homogeneity of an additive function> is insufficient for real homogeneity. This particular extension is also discontinuous: <rational numbers> approaching $\sqrt2$ have value <zero>, while the value at their <limit> is one.
