The alphabet of a <first-order language> separates logical symbols from nonlogical symbols. In the standard presentation here the logical symbols are the variables $x_0,x_1,\ldots$, equality $\dot=$, <negation> $\neg$, <logical implication> $\to$, the <universal quantification> $\forall$, and the punctuation symbols parentheses and comma. The nonlogical symbols are <constant symbols>, <function symbols> of each specified finite arity, and <predicate symbols> of each specified finite arity, such as $f_n^{(k)}$ and $P_n^{(k)}$.

Constants can equivalently be treated as zero-ary <function symbols>. Their arities belong to the language specification. Other <logical connectives>, and $\exists x\,\phi$ as an abbreviation for $\neg\forall x\,\neg\phi$, need not be primitive alphabet symbols. This distinction matters in counting literal symbol occurrences.

The <first-order terms> are the least collection containing every variable and constant and closed under formation of $f(t_1,\ldots,t_k)$ whenever $f$ has arity $k$ and the $t_j$ are terms. A <closed term> has no variables.

The <first-order formulas> are the least collection containing the <atomic formulas> $t_1\dot=t_2$ and $P(t_1,\ldots,t_k)$ of the appropriate arities, and closed under <negation>, <logical implication>, and <universal quantification>: from formulas $\phi,\psi$ one may form $\neg\phi$, $(\phi\to\psi)$, and $\forall x_i\phi$, with the chosen fully parenthesized grammar. Only finite expressions obtained by these rules are formulas. No additional strings belong to either collection.

Terms denote elements under an <assignment in a first-order structure>; formulas receive <truth values> in a <first-order structure>. The two syntactic categories have different formation rules even when they use some of the same punctuation.

A collection of <logical connectives> is adequate, or has <functional completeness>, if every <Boolean function> of finitely many <propositional variables> can be expressed by a formula using those connectives and variables, with the usual convention for nullary truth constants stated if those are included.

An adequate example is $\boxed{\{\neg,\to\}}$. An inadequate example is $\boxed{\{\to\}}$: formulas using <logical implication> alone preserve truth when every input is true, so cannot express <negation>. These examples concern truth functions, rather than mere syntactic formation of formulas.

The identity <logical implication> $\neg A\to\neg A$ follows by the deduction rule. Substituting $\alpha=\neg A$, $\beta=A$ into the replacement axiom and applying <modus ponens rule> therefore gives double-negation elimination:
$$
\vdash\neg\neg A\to A.
$$
Universal instantiation gives $\vdash U\to\neg\phi$. Combine it with $\vdash\neg\neg U\to U$ using <modus ponens rule> under a temporary assumption and then the deduction rule, obtaining $\vdash\neg\neg U\to\neg\phi$.

In the replacement axiom now substitute $\alpha=\neg\phi$ and $\beta=\neg U$. Its antecedent is precisely the last theorem, and its consequent is $\neg\neg\phi\to\neg U$. Thus
$$
\vdash\neg\neg\phi\to\neg\forall x_i\neg\phi.
$$
Under the fixed assumption $H$ and temporary assumption $\neg\neg\phi$, <modus ponens rule> gives $\neg U$ and then $\psi$. Discharge the temporary assumption to obtain $H\vdash\neg\neg\phi\to\psi$.

Finally apply universal generalization. The variable $x_i$ is not free in $H$, because it is bound in $U$ and is not free in $\psi$ by hypothesis. Hence the side condition is satisfied and
$$
\boxed{\{\neg\forall x_i\neg\phi\to\psi\}\vdash\forall x_i(\neg\neg\phi\to\psi).}
$$
Generalization is applied after the temporary assumption has been discharged; applying it while that assumption remained open would not be justified.

A <consistent set of formulae>, restricted here to sentences, does not derive a contradiction in the chosen classical deductive system. Equivalently there is no sentence $\theta$ for which both $\Sigma\vdash\theta$ and $\Sigma\vdash\neg\theta$ hold. By explosion, an inconsistent set derives every sentence; a consistent set does not.

This is a syntactic condition about finite derivations, rather than a definition in terms of models.

A set of sentences is <maximal consistent> if it is consistent and no proper extension by further sentences remains consistent. Equivalently it is consistent, deductively closed, and decides every sentence: for each $\theta$, exactly one of $\theta$ and $\neg\theta$ belongs to it.

For example, if $\Sigma\vdash\theta$ but $\theta\notin\Sigma$, adding $\theta$ does not create a new contradiction, contrary to maximality. Thus maximality includes deductive closure, not only having as many independent axioms as possible.

A set of sentences is <satisfiable> if some nonempty <first-order structure> in its language makes every sentence in the set true. Because the formulas are sentences, their truth is independent of the <assignment in a first-order structure>.

This is a semantic existence condition. Its equivalence with syntactic consistency is the content of the classical <Gödel's completeness theorem>, together with soundness.

Order the consistent sets of sentences extending $\Sigma$ by inclusion. This collection is nonempty. The union of a chain is consistent: any derivation of a contradiction uses finitely many assumptions, which all lie in one member of the chain. <Zorn's lemma> therefore supplies a maximal member, which is a maximal consistent extension of $\Sigma$.

For a <countable> language one can instead enumerate all sentences and successively add either a sentence or its <negation> while preserving consistency. At least one choice is consistent, since inconsistency of both extensions, together with the <deduction theorem for propositional logic> and classical reasoning, would make the previous stage inconsistent. The union of the stages is consistent by finite use of assumptions and decides every sentence, so it is maximal consistent.

The chain proof covers arbitrary set-sized languages; no restriction on the language <cardinality> was printed here. The later prohibition on choice applies to the cardinal-arithmetic question, not this logic construction.

Suppose a structure satisfies every sentence of $\Sigma$. By the <soundness theorem for first-order logic>, every sentence derivable from $\Sigma$ is true in this structure. In particular it cannot derive both a sentence and its <negation>, because they cannot both be true under the usual semantics.

In the <Henkin construction>, first extend the consistent sentence set to a complete consistent theory with witnesses in an expanded language containing fresh constants. Its <term model> has <closed terms> modulo provable equality as elements:
$$
[t]=[u]\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T\vdash t\dot=u.
$$
Functions are interpreted by applying their symbols to representative terms, and an atomic predicate holds of term classes exactly when the corresponding atomic sentence belongs to $T$. Equality axioms make these definitions independent of representatives. The <Henkin witness property> handles the existential step in the truth lemma, so the resulting structure satisfies $T$ and hence the original $\Sigma$.

An arbitrary sentence set need not already have enough <closed terms> or witnesses. Calling this a term model for $\Sigma$ means a term-model construction of a model satisfying $\Sigma$, usually through that witnessed completion; it does not assert that every arbitrary $\Sigma$ itself already supplies the construction.

= Solution

The downward <Löwenheim-Skolem theorem> says that a structure in a <countable> language has a <countable> <elementary substructure> containing any specified finite or <countable> set of its elements. More generally the size bound is $\max(|\mathcal L|,|C|,\aleph_0)$ for a required parameter set $C$. In particular any satisfiable theory in a <countable> language has a model of size at most <countable>.

To see the last assertion directly from the completeness construction, add only countably many witness constants in the <Henkin construction>. There are then countably many finite <closed terms>, and taking their quotient cannot increase <cardinality>. The resulting term model is therefore <countable>. For the elementary-substructure version, perform the witness construction inside the given structure, choosing actual witnesses and closing under the <function symbols>. The induced substructure is elementary by the <Tarski-Vaught test>. This is the same witness mechanism as the completeness construction, with the ambient structure supplying the chosen witnesses.

The upward companion states that a theory with an infinite model has models in every sufficiently large infinite cardinal, at least $|\mathcal L|+\aleph_0$. Add that many pairwise unequal constants, apply compactness, and then the size-bounded construction. The <countable> consequence is the part needed below.

Use the axioms for a strict <dense linear order without endpoints>:
$$
\forall x\,\neg(x<x),\qquad
\forall x\forall y\forall z\,((x<y\wedge y<z)\to x<z),
$$
$$
\forall x\forall y\,(x<y\vee x=y\vee y<x),\qquad
\forall x\forall y\,(x<y\to\exists z(x<z\wedge z<y)),
$$
$$
\forall x\exists y\,(y<x),\qquad\forall x\exists y\,(x<y).
$$
The first three give strict linear order; the next gives density; the final two exclude endpoints. Domains are nonempty under the adopted first-order semantics, and these axioms force them to be infinite.

Enumerate both <countable> models. Build an increasing sequence of finite order-preserving partial <bijections>. At an even stage, take the first unmatched element of the first model. Its position relative to the finitely many already matched points determines an open interval or exterior ray in the second model. Density and absence of endpoints supply an unused point there, so the map extends.

At an odd stage perform the symmetric operation starting from the second model, extending the inverse map. Every required interval has infinitely many points, so avoiding the finitely many used values is possible. The union is a total <bijection>, by the alternating enumeration requirements, and it preserves and reflects order because every finite stage does. Hence it is an <isomorphism of structures>.

This is the <back-and-forth method>, proving <countable> categoricity of the dense-order theory.

**True in ZF.** For a set $A$, the singleton map injects $A$ into its <power set>. No map $f:A\to\mathcal P(A)$ is onto: the diagonal set
$$
D=\{a\in A:a\notin f(a)\}
$$
differs from $f(a)$ at the element $a$ for every $a$. Thus there is no <bijection> between $A$ and its <power set>. The <injection> together with this failure proves $\boxed{\kappa<2^\kappa}$.

If strict comparison is formulated as <injection> in one direction but not the other, the <Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem>, which requires no choice, excludes an <injection> of the <power set> back into $A$. This is <Cantor's theorem> in a choice-free form.

**True in ZF.** A function from $M$ into the functions $B\to A$ corresponds bijectively to a function from $B\times M$ into $A$:
$$
\boxed{F\longmapsto f,\qquad f(b,m)=F(m)(b).}
$$
The inverse sends $f$ to the function $m\mapsto(b\mapsto f(b,m))$. These two explicit transformations are inverses, proving the <currying law for cardinal exponentiation>. Empty domains and codomains cause no exception: the same correspondence still applies, including the unique <empty function> when its domain is empty. No selection of representatives or choice functions is involved.

**True in ZF.** Let $A$ be a set that is not finite. For each <natural number> $n$, form
$$
E_n=\{B\subseteq A:|B|=n\}.
$$
Each $E_n$ is a subset of $\mathcal P(A)$ and is nonempty: a nonfinite set has an $n$-element subset for every finite $n$, by finite induction. The sets $E_n$ are pairwise disjoint, hence pairwise distinct. Consequently
$$
n\longmapsto E_n
$$
is an <injection> from $\mathbb N$ into $\mathcal P(\mathcal P(A))$. This is a <double-power-set countability bound without choice>.

Part (d), applied to the set $\mathcal P(A)$, says that its <power set> is not countably infinite. Combining that result with the displayed <injection> gives
$$
\boxed{\aleph_0<2^{2^\kappa}.}
$$
There is no hidden <countable> choice: the proof uses the entire definable family of $n$-element subsets, rather than choosing one subset for each $n$.

<Hartogs theorem> says that for every set $A$ there is a least <ordinal> $h(A)$ that does not inject into $A$. This theorem requires no <axiom of choice>.

To prove it, collect all <well-orderings> of subsets of $A$. They are set-sized data, coded by pairs from $\mathcal P(A)$ and $\mathcal P(A\times A)$. Each has a unique <ordinal> order type, so replacement gives a set $H$ of their order types. If $\eta\in H$ and $\xi<\eta$, restricting the corresponding <well-order> to an initial segment shows $\xi\in H$. Hence $H$ is an <ordinal>. If this <ordinal> itself injected into $A$, its transported <well-order> would put $H$ among its own elements, impossible for an <ordinal>. Every smaller <ordinal> does inject by definition. Thus $h(A)=H$ has the asserted property.

**The recursion printed in the source needs two boundary restrictions.** Its new-row successor clause allows $\beta=\alpha$, which would assign a value at $(\alpha,\alpha+1)$ outside the declared domain. Also, at $\alpha=0$, its row-start clause gives $g_1(0,0)=0$ while its column-start clause gives $g_1(0,0)=g_1(0,0)+1$. Taken literally, these clauses do not define a function.

The intended extension fills the new row first and the remaining new column second. Restrict the row successor clause to $\beta<\alpha$, and apply the column-start clause only when $\alpha>0$. With these two restrictions the domains are disjoint:
$$
\alpha\times\alpha,\qquad\{(\alpha,\beta):\beta\leq\alpha\},\qquad
\{(\beta,\alpha):\beta<\alpha\}.
$$
All successors here are <ordinal> successors. Simultaneous <transfinite induction> proves a stronger result: $g_\alpha$ is a <bijection> of $\alpha\times\alpha$ onto an <ordinal> $\gamma_\alpha$, and the functions extend one another.

At zero this is the <empty function>. Assuming it at $\alpha$, the least missing <ordinal> is $\gamma_\alpha$. Recursion down the new row gives values $\gamma_\alpha+\beta$ for $\beta\leq\alpha$; recursion down the remaining column gives $\gamma_\alpha+(\alpha+1)+\beta$ for $\beta<\alpha$. These enumerate two disjoint consecutive <ordinal> intervals above the old range, so the extension is well defined and bijective, with
$$
\boxed{\gamma_{\alpha+1}=\gamma_\alpha+(\alpha+1)+\alpha.}
$$
At a limit $\lambda$, take the union of the earlier functions. Compatibility makes it a function, and every pair of coordinates below $\lambda$ is already present at some earlier stage. Its range is the <ordinal> $\bigcup_{\alpha<\lambda}\gamma_\alpha$, and injectivity is preserved by the compatible union. This completes the construction of the <row-and-column enumeration of an ordinal square>.

For finite stages $\gamma_n=n^2$. For example the table of $g_3$, with the first coordinate indexing rows, is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}0&3&7\\1&2&8\\4&5&6\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The remaining parts refer to this domain-consistent construction. The source's literal boundary claims are preserved in the archive, rather than silently asserted as valid.
