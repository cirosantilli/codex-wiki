The assertion $\Gamma\vdash\phi$ means that there is a finite formal derivation ending in $\phi$ with its undischarged assumptions contained in $\Gamma$. In a context-labelled presentation, an axiom instance is available with empty context, a formula $\alpha$ is available from the context $\{\alpha\}$, <modus ponens rule>[modus ponens] combines derivations of $\alpha$ and $\alpha\to\beta$ to derive $\beta$, and <universal generalization rule>[generalization] derives $\forall x\alpha$ provided $x$ is not free in its open assumptions. Thinning enlarges a context without changing its conclusion.

Each line or node must specify an axiom instance, an assumption, or an application of a permitted rule to earlier lines or subderivations. The side conditions on substitution, quantification and contexts are part of correctness. In particular a derivation is a finite syntactic object, even when $\Gamma$ is infinite; it uses only finitely many assumptions before a final thinning to $\Gamma$ if necessary. Derivability $\vdash$ is thus distinct in definition from <semantic entailment> $\models$, which quantifies over structures and assignments.

A term $t$ is <free for substitution> for $x_i$ in $\alpha$ when none of its variables becomes bound at a free occurrence of $x_i$ being replaced. Equivalently, no <quantifier> surrounding such an occurrence binds a variable of $t$. Bound occurrences of $x_i$ are not replaced. This occurrence-sensitive condition prevents variable capture.

For example take $\alpha(x)=\exists y\,\neg(x=y)$ and $t=y$. On a two-element domain, $\forall x\exists y\,\neg(x=y)$ is true. Naive textual substitution produces $\exists y\,\neg(y=y)$, which is false on every nonempty domain. Thus the corresponding unrestricted A4 implication would be invalid. The problem is that the inserted free $y$ was captured by the existing <existential quantifier>. Renaming that binder first instead gives the correct instance $\exists z\,\neg(y=z)$, with $z$ fresh.

Take $\alpha=\beta=P(x)$ for a unary <predicate symbol>. The premise $\forall x(P(x)\to P(x))$ is valid, but the proposed conclusion is $P(x)\to\forall xP(x)$. On a domain $\{0,1\}$ with $P=\{0\}$ and assignment $x\mapsto0$, its antecedent is true and its consequent false.

Hence dropping the condition that $x$ is not free in $\alpha$ would turn a valid premise into an invalid conclusion. The condition permits the antecedent to remain fixed while the quantified variable ranges over the domain; an antecedent depending on that variable cannot be held true for all its values merely because it is true at the current assignment.

<Capture-safe equality replacement> gives the corrected scheme:
$$
\boxed{\vdash x_i=x_j\to(\phi\to\phi'),}
$$
where $\phi'$ replaces selected free occurrences of $x_i$ by $x_j$, without capture. One may equivalently use full capture-avoiding substitution, after renaming conflicting binders. Replacing bound occurrences as though they were free is not permitted.

Here is a syntactic proof. Write $e$ for $x_i=x_j$. First derive symmetry under $e$: A6 and A4 give $x_i=x_i$, and A7 applied to the first position in that atomic equality gives $e\to(x_i=x_i\to x_j=x_i)$. <Modus ponens rule>[Modus ponens] and the deduction theorem give $\vdash e\to x_j=x_i$. Atomic replacement in the forward direction is A7 itself; in the reverse direction apply A7 with $i,j$ exchanged and use this symmetry. Thus under $e$, an atomic formula and its designated replacement imply each other.

Induct on the formation of $\phi$, proving both directions simultaneously. For <negation>, the reverse implication for the immediate subformula gives the forward implication of its negations by contraposition, and vice versa. For $\theta\to\eta$, the two induction equivalences combine propositionally to $(\theta\to\eta)\leftrightarrow(\theta'\to\eta')$. This uses only the propositional <tautologies> allowed in this part.

For $\phi=\forall z\theta$, first take $z$ distinct from $x_i,x_j$. From assumptions $e$ and $\forall z\theta$, A4 gives $\theta$, the induction hypothesis gives $\theta'$, and <universal generalization rule>[generalization] gives $\forall z\theta'$: $z$ is free in neither open assumption. Discharge the two assumptions to obtain $e\to(\forall z\theta\to\forall z\theta')$. The reverse direction is identical. If the binder is $x_i$, no free occurrence of that variable lies beneath it and the replacement there is empty. If it is $x_j$, capture avoidance likewise forbids a changed free $x_i$ below that binder unless the binder is first renamed to a fresh variable. Bound-variable renaming is derived from A4 followed by admissible <universal generalization rule>[generalization] and A5, with the same freshness restriction. This completes the <structural induction> and proves the scheme for arbitrary formulas.

The <thinning rule>, or <weakening>, is
$$
\boxed{\frac{\Gamma\vdash\alpha\qquad\Gamma\subseteq\Delta}{\Delta\vdash\alpha}.}
$$
It expresses that extra unused assumptions do not destroy a proof. In the context-sensitive predicate-calculus presentation it also allows a theorem first proved with a fresh <universal generalization rule>[generalization] variable to be used later in a context containing that variable free. Simply replaying the same <universal generalization rule>[generalization] under the larger context could violate its side condition. For instance a proof of $\forall x(x=x)$ from the empty context may generalize on $x$; it should remain available when an unused assumption $P(x)$ is added, even though that last <universal generalization rule>[generalization] could not be replayed with $P(x)$ open.

In propositional calculus the axiom schemes and <modus ponens rule>[modus ponens] have no analogous free-variable restriction. An induction on the original proof simply reuses each axiom, assumption and modus-ponens step under the enlarged assumptions, so <weakening> is admissible and need not be postulated separately. <Context-sensitive Hilbert weakening> concerns this proof presentation: systems already tracking only the assumptions on which each line depends can build admissible <weakening> into their treatment of predicate-calculus <universal generalization rule>[generalization] as well.

With <universal generalization rule>[generalization] restricted to variables not free in open assumptions, the deduction theorem states
$$
\boxed{\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\vdash\beta\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Gamma\vdash\alpha\to\beta.}
$$
The reverse direction follows by thinning and <modus ponens rule>[modus ponens]. For the forward direction, induct on a derivation, keeping track of its actual assumption contexts. Axioms and assumptions already in $\Gamma$ yield $\alpha\to\chi$ by A1; the distinguished assumption $\alpha$ yields the permitted theorem $\alpha\to\alpha$. If a line $\chi$ is obtained by <modus ponens rule>[modus ponens] from $\eta$ and $\eta\to\chi$, the induction hypotheses and A2 derive $\alpha\to\chi$.

If a <universal generalization rule>[generalization] step depends on the discharged assumption, its variable $x$ is not free in $\alpha$ or the other open assumptions. Generalize the induction conclusion $\alpha\to\eta$ to $\forall x(\alpha\to\eta)$ and use A5 to derive $\alpha\to\forall x\eta$. If the subderivation does not depend on $\alpha$, its generalized conclusion can instead be retained as an independent derivation and A1 applied afterwards. Thinning merely enlarges the remaining context and preserves the induction claim. These cases are why assumption dependence, rather than a careless unrestricted universal rule, is essential.

In a presentation allowing <universal generalization rule>[generalization] freely from hypotheses, the corresponding theorem must instead explicitly require that no variable free in $\alpha$ is generalized in an $\alpha$-dependent line. Without that restriction $P(x)\vdash\forall xP(x)$ would incorrectly yield $\vdash P(x)\to\forall xP(x)$.

A <first-order structure> consists of a nonempty domain $A$, an interpretation $c^A\in A$ of each <constant symbol>, a total function $f^A:A^n\to A$ for each $n$-ary <function symbol>, and a relation $R^A\subseteq A^n$ for each $n$-ary <predicate symbol>. <Logical equality> is interpreted as actual identity.

An <assignment in a first-order structure> is a map $s$ from the variables into $A$. It extends recursively to terms: $x^A[s]=s(x)$, $c^A[s]=c^A$, and $f(t_1,\ldots,t_n)^A[s]=f^A(t_1^A[s],\ldots,t_n^A[s])$. Atomic truth is determined by membership of the resulting tuple in $R^A$, or equality of the two term values. <Negation> reverses the <truth value>, and an implication is false exactly when its antecedent is true and its consequent false.

For a <universal quantifier>,
$$
A,s\models\forall x\theta\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A,s[x\mapsto a]\models\theta\text{ for every }a\in A.
$$
These clauses define a valuation on every formula by <structural recursion>. Only free-variable values affect a formula's truth; consequently a sentence has the same <truth value> under every assignment. This is a semantic assignment of domain elements, not a programming assignment operation.

Fix any structure and any assignment of the three quantified variables, writing their values as $a_0,a_1,a_2$. If either of the nested antecedents is false, the corresponding implication is true. If both are true, then $f^A(a_0)=a_1$ and $f^A(a_0)=a_2$. Actual identity is transitive, so $a_1=a_2$, making the consequent true as well.

Thus the unquantified matrix is true under every assignment in every structure. The universal-quantifier semantic clause then makes its universal closure true in every structure:
$$
\boxed{\models\forall x_0\forall x_1\forall x_2\,[f(x_0)=x_1\to(f(x_0)=x_2\to x_1=x_2)].}
$$
No assumption that $f$ is <injective> is used. The argument says that one value cannot equal two distinct elements, not that distinct inputs have distinct outputs.

Choose a fresh variable $x_3$. In A7 take the equality antecedent $x_3=x_1$, the atomic formula $\phi=(x_3=x_2)$, and replace its occurrence of $x_3$ by $x_1$. This gives directly the theorem
$$

Generalize on $x_3$, with no open assumptions. A4 then instantiates that <quantifier> with the term $f(x_0)$. The substitution is free for $x_3$, since the matrix contains no <quantifiers>. One modus-ponens step yields
$$
\vdash f(x_0)=x_1\to(f(x_0)=x_2\to x_1=x_2).
$$
Finally generalize, in order, on $x_2$, $x_1$, and $x_0$. The result is
$$
\boxed{\vdash\forall x_0\forall x_1\forall x_2\,[f(x_0)=x_1\to(f(x_0)=x_2\to x_1=x_2)].}
$$
Every step is A7, <universal generalization rule>[generalization], A4, or <modus ponens rule>[modus ponens]. Neither completeness, adequacy, nor an unproved appeal to all propositional <tautologies> is needed. In particular the proof does not silently upgrade the variable-only equality axiom to an arbitrary-term axiom: quantification and instantiation perform that upgrade explicitly.

Adequacy here means <functional completeness>: every Boolean truth function of finitely many positive-arity propositional inputs can be expressed by a formula using the available connectives. It is an expressive-power claim, distinct from the completeness theorem relating derivability and <semantic entailment>.

<Negation> and implication define <conjunction> and disjunction by
$$
p\land q\equiv\neg(p\to\neg q),\qquad p\lor q\equiv\neg p\to q.
$$
Given any <truth table>, for each input row where its output is true form the <minterm> containing each input positively or negatively according to that row. The disjunction of these <minterms> is true on exactly the required rows: a <disjunctive normal form>. Replacing its conjunctions and disjunctions by the displayed definitions gives a formula using only $\neg,\to$.

The constantly true and constantly false functions are represented by $p\to p$ and $\neg(p\to p)$, using a dummy input if needed. Thus the empty or full <truth table> also causes no difficulty. If one insists on genuinely nullary formulas with no variables at all, a separate truth constant is needed; ordinary propositional adequacy allows the constant truth functions to be expressed with irrelevant input variables.

There are sixteen binary <Boolean functions>, one for each four-bit <truth table>. **Exactly eight give an adequate basis together with <negation>.**

The eight affine functions are $0,1,p,\neg p,q,\neg q,p\oplus q,\neg(p\oplus q)$. Each is affine over the field with two elements, and composition with other affine functions or <negation> remains affine. Since <conjunction> is not affine, none of these eight bases is adequate.

Each remaining <truth table> has either one true row or three true rows. In the latter case negate its output to obtain a function with one true row. Negate selected inputs to move that row to $(1,1)$: the resulting function is <conjunction>. <Conjunction> together with <negation> expresses every <disjunctive normal form>, proving adequacy of all remaining eight. They are AND, NAND, OR, NOR, the two directed implications, and the two asymmetric conjunctions $p\land\neg q$, $\neg p\land q$. The count treats the binary connective by its truth function rather than by arbitrary choices of its printed symbol.

A graph must assign exactly one output to every input, while the distinguished unary predicate must select exactly one element. Suitable sentences are
$$
\boxed{\phi=\forall x\exists y\,[P(x,y)\land\forall z(P(x,z)\to z=y)],}
$$
$$
\boxed{\psi=\exists x\,[Q(x)\land\forall z(Q(z)\to z=x)].}
$$
The existential clauses provide totality and nonemptiness of the selected output, respectively; the universal clauses provide uniqueness. Thus a structure satisfying $\phi,\psi$ uniquely determines a total unary function $f$ and a constant $c$ on its unchanged domain. Conversely any interpretations of $f,c$ determine such relations by $P(x,y)\iff f(x)=y$ and $Q(x)\iff x=c$.

For each term $t$ define an $L_2$-formula $E_t(z)$ expressing its value. Use fresh <bound variables> at every step:
$$
E_x(z):=(z=x),\qquad E_c(z):=Q(z),\qquad E_{f(t)}(z):=\exists y\,[E_t(y)\land P(y,z)].
$$
Freshness includes avoiding all relevant <free variables> of the formula being translated. Define the atomic translation by
$$
\sigma(t_1=t_2):=\exists u\exists v\,[E_{t_1}(u)\land E_{t_2}(v)\land u=v],
$$
and extend through the logical operations: $\sigma(\neg\theta)=\neg\sigma(\theta)$, $\sigma(\theta\to\eta)=\sigma(\theta)\to\sigma(\eta)$, and $\sigma(\forall x\theta)=\forall x\sigma(\theta)$. Fresh witnesses prevent variable capture and ensure the translation preserves the original free-variable <set>.

For an $L_1$-structure $A$, let $A^r$ have the graph relation of $f^A$ and <singleton> relation $\{c^A\}$. Induction on terms proves that $E_t(z)$ holds precisely when $z$ is the value of $t$. Induction on formulas then proves
$$
A,s\models\alpha\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A^r,s\models\sigma(\alpha).
$$
Conversely every $L_2$-structure satisfying $\phi,\psi$ is uniquely the relational version of an $L_1$-structure on that same domain. Hence, for arbitrary possibly infinite $\Gamma$, <semantic entailment> on the left is equivalent to <semantic entailment> from $\sigma(\Gamma)\cup\{\phi,\psi\}$ on the right, with assignments retained in the free-formula case. Applying <soundness theorem for first-order logic> and <Gödel's completeness theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\Gamma\vdash\alpha\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sigma(\Gamma)\cup\{\phi,\psi\}\vdash\sigma(\alpha).}
$$
The prohibition on completeness in the preceding direct equality question is local to that proof; here it supplies the clean semantic-to-syntactic transfer after the actual translation and model correspondence have been established.

Replace each $n$-ary <function symbol> by an $(n+1)$-ary graph predicate, with axioms asserting exactly one output for each input tuple. Replace each constant by a unary predicate asserted to hold of exactly one element. Recursively introduce witnesses for term values, then translate formulas through their logical connectives and <quantifiers> as above.

Every original structure has a unique corresponding relational structure satisfying these axioms, on exactly the same carrier, and every such relational structure reconstructs the original functions and constants. Truth under assignments and logical consequences are preserved in both directions. In this precise relative sense, **purely relational languages are adequate to represent first-order languages with functions and constants**: expressive power is retained after adjoining the graph and uniqueness axioms. Arbitrary relational interpretations that fail those axioms are not claimed to represent functions.

**Neither constants alone nor functions alone gives the same uniform adequacy on all nonempty carriers.** A one-element <set> has exactly one interpretation of each <function symbol> of any finite arity and of every <constant symbol>. In contrast it has two interpretations of a unary predicate: empty or full. Thus a fixed purely functional or constant-only language has only one structure on that <singleton>, and cannot encode both <truth values> of $\exists xP(x)$ by a same-carrier truth-preserving translation with reconstruction. This is the <singleton truth-preservation obstruction to expressive completeness>, even when <logical equality> is available.

Constants alone also cannot represent general variable-dependent relations on larger infinite carriers: a fixed formula mentions only finitely many named elements, and its remaining equality tests do not encode an arbitrary <binary relation>. Functions offer more flexibility. On carriers with at least two elements, predicates can be encoded by <characteristic functions> taking two distinguished values, and constants can be represented by constant-valued unary functions if explicit constant symbols are forbidden. Such a construction can work after adding axioms for distinct truth markers, but those axioms exclude the <singleton> case. Enlarging the carrier or adding a Boolean sort is another possible encoding; it changes the same-carrier sense established in the preceding part and must not be silently identified with it.

The <ordinal> operations are defined by <transfinite recursion> in the right argument. Addition satisfies $\alpha+0=\alpha$, $\alpha+(\beta+1)=(\alpha+\beta)+1$, and $\alpha+\lambda=\sup_{\beta<\lambda}(\alpha+\beta)$ at a nonzero limit. Multiplication satisfies $\alpha\cdot0=0$, $\alpha\cdot(\beta+1)=\alpha\cdot\beta+\alpha$, and $\alpha\cdot\lambda=\sup_{\beta<\lambda}\alpha\cdot\beta$. Exponentiation satisfies $\alpha^0=1$, $\alpha^{\beta+1}=\alpha^\beta\cdot\alpha$, and $\alpha^\lambda=\sup_{\beta<\lambda}\alpha^\beta$ at nonzero limits. These are <ordinal> operations, not commutative <cardinal arithmetic>.

For a <normal function on an ordinal>, prove domination by <transfinite induction>. At zero, $0\leq\Phi(0)$ automatically. If $\alpha\leq\Phi(\alpha)$, strict increase at successors gives $\alpha+1\leq\Phi(\alpha)+1\leq\Phi(\alpha+1)$. At a nonzero limit $\lambda$, continuity and the induction hypotheses give
$$
\Phi(\lambda)=\sup_{\alpha<\lambda}\Phi(\alpha)\geq\sup_{\alpha<\lambda}\alpha=\lambda.
$$
Therefore $\boxed{\alpha\leq\Phi(\alpha)\text{ for every ordinal }\alpha}$. No <axiom of choice> is needed for this <ordinal> induction.

For fixed $\beta$, the recursion defining $\beta+\alpha$ makes it continuous in $\alpha$ at every nonzero limit, and $(\beta+\alpha)+1>\beta+\alpha$. Thus **$\alpha\mapsto\beta+\alpha$ is normal**, including when $\beta=0$.

For $\beta>0$, take $\lambda=\beta\cdot\omega$. If $\alpha<\lambda$, choose a <natural number> $n$ with $\alpha<\beta n$; then $\alpha+\beta\leq\beta(n+1)<\lambda$. Since $\alpha+\beta\geq\alpha$, their <supremum> as $\alpha\uparrow\lambda$ is $\lambda$. But $\lambda+\beta>\lambda$, so
$$
\sup_{\alpha<\lambda}(\alpha+\beta)=\lambda<\lambda+\beta.
$$
Hence right addition by a nonzero fixed <ordinal> fails continuity and is not normal.

<Normality and argument order in ordinal arithmetic> also give the multiplication statements: **$\alpha\mapsto\beta\cdot\alpha$ is normal for $\beta>0$, while $\alpha\mapsto\alpha\cdot\beta$ is normal exactly when $\beta=1$**. At $\beta=0$ it is constant. For fixed $\beta>1$, let $\kappa=\omega^{\beta\cdot\omega}$. <Cantor normal form> shows that $\xi\beta<\kappa$ whenever $\xi<\kappa$: leading exponents below $\beta\omega$ remain below it after adding the leading exponent of $\beta$. Thus $\sup_{\xi<\kappa}\xi\beta=\kappa$, but $\kappa\beta>\kappa$, again violating continuity. Left-fixed multiplication is strictly increasing and continuous by its recursion.

For exponentiation, **$\alpha\mapsto\beta^\alpha$ is normal for $\beta\geq2$; bases zero and one are exceptions. The reverse map $\alpha\mapsto\alpha^\beta$ is normal exactly when $\beta=1$**. Exponent zero gives the constant one. For $\beta>1$, use the same $\kappa$ and put $\lambda=\omega^\kappa$. For each $\alpha<\lambda$ choose $0<\zeta<\kappa$ with $\alpha\leq\omega^\zeta$. Then $\alpha^\beta\leq\omega^{\zeta\beta}<\lambda$, while $\alpha^\beta\geq\alpha$ for positive $\alpha$. Consequently its left <supremum> is $\lambda$, but $\lambda^\beta=\omega^{\kappa\beta}>\lambda$. The bound by $\omega^\zeta$ also handles finite bases, where an unqualified leading-exponent multiplication formula would need care. All these are <ordinal> facts and use no choice.

For a <countable fixed point of a normal ordinal function>, first show that $\Phi$ maps every <countable ordinal> to a <countable ordinal>. Use <transfinite induction> below $\omega_1$. The zero case is assumed, and the successor case is the stated preservation property. If $\lambda$ is a <countable> limit, normality gives $\Phi(\lambda)=\bigcup_{\alpha<\lambda}\Phi(\alpha)$, a <countable union of countable sets>, hence <countable>. This countable-union step is where <axiom of countable choice> is used; the assumed <axiom of choice> supplies it.

Now set $\alpha_0=0$, $\alpha_{n+1}=\Phi(\alpha_n)$. The preceding result makes every term <countable>, and the domination proved earlier makes the <sequence> nondecreasing. If it ever stabilizes, it already gives the required fixed point. Otherwise $\alpha=\sup_{n<\omega}\alpha_n$ is a <countable> nonzero limit, using <axiom of countable choice> again for countability of the union. Its defining <sequence> is cofinal, so monotonicity and continuity give
$$
\boxed{\Phi(\alpha)=\sup_n\Phi(\alpha_n)=\sup_n\alpha_{n+1}=\alpha.}
$$
Apply this to $\Phi(\alpha)=\omega^\alpha$. This is normal, begins at $1$, and $\omega^{\alpha+1}=\omega^\alpha\cdot\omega$ is <countable> whenever $\omega^\alpha$ is <countable>. Thus a <countable> $\epsilon$ satisfies $\boxed{\epsilon=\omega^\epsilon}$. Starting at zero gives the familiar tower <sequence> $0,1,\omega,\omega^\omega,\ldots$, whose <supremum> is $\epsilon_0$. The finite recursion, <ordinal> identities and fixed-point continuity do not require choice; the uses of choice above concern <countable> unions of <countable ordinals>.

The <Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem> states that <injections> $f:A\to B$ and $g:B\to A$ imply the existence of a <bijection> $A\to B$. Here is a direct proof, avoiding any appeal to a fixed-point theorem. Let $A_0=A\setminus g(B)$, $A_{n+1}=g(f(A_n))$, and $C=\bigcup_{n<\omega}A_n$. Define
$$
h(a)=\begin{cases}f(a),&a\in C,\\g^{-1}(a),&a\notin C.\end{cases}
$$
The second case is defined because $A\setminus C\subseteq g(B)$. On $C$, $f$ is a <bijection> onto $f(C)$. For $b\in B$, injectivity of $g$ and the construction of $C$ give $g(b)\in C$ if and only if $b\in f(C)$: $g(b)$ cannot be in $A_0$, so membership in $C$ means membership in some $g(f(A_n))$. Hence $g^{-1}$ bijects $A\setminus C$ onto $B\setminus f(C)$. The two pieces have disjoint domains and images and together cover $A,B$, proving $h$ <bijective>. This proof itself uses no choice.

Write $\mathfrak c=|\mathbb R|=2^{\aleph_0}$. We use the standard infinite-cardinal identities $\kappa\cdot\aleph_0=\kappa$, $\kappa^n=\kappa$ for positive finite $n$ and infinite $\kappa$, and $(\kappa^\lambda)^\mu=\kappa^{\lambda\cdot\mu}$, under the permitted <axiom of choice>. The map $z\mapsto(\operatorname{Re}z,\operatorname{Im}z)$ bijects $\mathbb C$ with $\mathbb R^2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{|\mathbb C|=\mathfrak c^2=\mathfrak c.}
$$

The common amplitude $a>0$ is uniquely determined by any entry's <absolute value>. After choosing it, each entry independently has sign $+1$ or $-1$. Thus the correspondence
$$
(a,(\varepsilon_n)_{n<\omega})\longmapsto(a\varepsilon_n)_{n<\omega}
$$
is a <bijection> from $(0,\infty)\times\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb N}$ onto the <set>. The positive reals have cardinal $\mathfrak c$, and binary sign <sequences> have cardinal $2^{\aleph_0}=\mathfrak c$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathfrak c\cdot\mathfrak c=\mathfrak c.}
$$
Here “independently” refers to unconstrained deterministic choices of coordinates, not probabilistic independence; no probability distribution is being specified.

The <complex plane> has a <countable> basis of disks with positive rational radii and centres with rational real and imaginary coordinates. Each <open set> is the union of the basic disks it contains, so it is determined by a <subset> of this <countable> basis. Therefore the number of <open sets> is at most $2^{\aleph_0}=\mathfrak c$.

For the reverse inequality, the distinct disks $\{z:|z|<r\}$ for $r>0$ give $\mathfrak c$ different <open sets>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\bigl|\{U\subseteq\mathbb C:U\text{ open}\}\bigr|=\mathfrak c.}
$$
The countable-basis restriction is decisive: there are $2^{\mathfrak c}$ arbitrary <subsets> of the plane, but only continuum many open ones.

For the <cardinality of total orders on a countably infinite set>, a <total order> on $\mathbb N$ is a <subset> of $\mathbb N\times\mathbb N$, so there are at most $2^{\aleph_0}=\mathfrak c$ such orders. To obtain the lower bound, associate to every <binary sequence> $(b_n)$ an order by placing the two-element blocks $\{2n,2n+1\}$ in increasing order of $n$. Inside block $n$, use $2n<2n+1$ when $b_n=0$ and reverse them when $b_n=1$.

This is a <total order> for every <sequence>. Distinct <sequences> differ on the comparison of some adjacent pair and therefore produce distinct relations. Thus <binary sequences> inject into the order <set>, proving
$$
\boxed{\mathfrak c.}
$$
The construction counts distinct orders on the labelled <set>, even though the resulting <order types> are all isomorphic to the usual natural-number order.

<Zorn's lemma> states that a nonempty <partially ordered set> in which every chain has an <upper bound> has a <maximal element>. Order the partial <group> operations by inclusion of their graphs. The collection is a <set>, since its members are <subsets> of $X\times X\times X$. It is nonempty: choose $x_0\in X$ and put the one-element <group operation> on $\{x_0\}$.

To construct a <chain union of partial group operations>, for the given nonempty chain, let $m=\bigcup_{n\in\mathcal C}n$ and let $A$ be the union of the corresponding <group> carriers. Comparability guarantees agreement wherever two operations are both defined, so $m$ is a function. Its domain is $A\times A$: any two elements of $A$ lie together in one carrier, by taking the larger of the two chain members containing them. Their product then lies in that carrier and hence in $A$.

Any three elements similarly lie together in one chain member, where <associativity> holds; therefore <associativity> holds for $m$. The identities of nested nonempty <groups> agree: the smaller identity remains an <idempotent> under the larger <group operation>, whose only <idempotent> is its identity. Hence the whole chain has a common identity. Each element's inverse in a carrier remains its inverse in every larger carrier, so it is an inverse in the union as well. Thus $(A,m)$ satisfies all <group axioms> and
$$
\boxed{\bigcup\mathcal C\in\mathcal G.}
$$
It is an <upper bound> for the chain. The empty chain also has an <upper bound> because $\mathcal G$ is nonempty, completing the hypothesis needed for <Zorn's lemma>.

Apply <Zorn's lemma> using the chain upper bounds just proved, obtaining a maximal partial <group operation> $m$ on a nonempty carrier $A\subseteq X$. Suppose $|X\setminus A|\geq|A|$. Then choose a <subset> $B\subseteq X\setminus A$ and a <bijection> $b:A\to B$. The <set> $A\cup B$ can be identified with $A\times C_2$ by sending $(a,0)$ to $a$ and $(a,1)$ to $b(a)$.

Use <group extension by a duplicate carrier> to transport the direct-product <group operation> along this <bijection>. Its restriction to $A\times A$ is exactly the original $m$, because $(a,0)(a',0)=(m(a,a'),0)$. It therefore gives a strictly larger member of $\mathcal G$, contradicting maximality. Thus $|A|\nleq|X\setminus A|$. Cardinal comparability, allowed as a consequence of <Zorn's lemma>, leaves the strict opposite inequality:
$$
\boxed{|X\setminus A|<|A|.}
$$
The argument uses choice through <Zorn's lemma> and cardinal comparability; the extension itself is an explicit transported direct-product operation once the <bijection> with $B$ is chosen.

If $X$ is finite and nonempty, let $n=|X|\geq1$, choose a <bijection> with the <cyclic group> $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$, and transport its addition to $X$. This includes the one-element case.

If $X$ is infinite, use the maximal partial <group> $(A,m)$ from the preceding part. Its carrier must be infinite: otherwise $|X\setminus A|<|A|$ would make the complement finite too, forcing $X$ finite. Since the complement injects into $A$,
$$
|A|\leq|X|=|A|+|X\setminus A|\leq|A|+|A|=2|A|=|A|,
$$
where the last equality is the permitted infinite-cardinal doubling identity. Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein supplies a <bijection> $j:X\to A$. Define
$$
\boxed{x\star y=j^{-1}(m(j(x),j(y))).}
$$
<Associativity> follows by applying $j$ and using <associativity> of $m$; the identity is $j^{-1}(e_A)$ and the inverse of $x$ is $j^{-1}(j(x)^{-1})$. Hence $(X,\star)$ is a <group>.

This final transport need not extend the earlier partial operation on its original <subset>. That stronger claim is neither needed nor generally possible: for example a <group> of order three cannot occur as a <subgroup> of a <group> of order four. The proof establishes exactly the existence of some <group structure on every nonempty set>, using the allowed choice consequences in the infinite case.
