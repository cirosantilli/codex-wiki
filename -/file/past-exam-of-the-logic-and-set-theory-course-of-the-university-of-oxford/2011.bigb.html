A <logical derivation> of $\alpha$ from $\Gamma$ is a finite sequence $\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_n=\alpha$ in which each line belongs to $\Gamma$, is an instance of one of the three specified axiom schemes, or follows from two earlier lines $\delta_j$ and $\delta_j\to\delta_i$ by the <modus ponens rule>. **$\Gamma\vdash\alpha$ means that such a finite derivation exists.** In particular, a derivation uses only finitely many assumptions even if $\Gamma$ is infinite.

<Logical equivalence> means that the two <propositional formulas> receive the same <truth value> under every <Boolean valuation> of the <propositional variables>. Equivalently, $\alpha\leftrightarrow\beta$ is a <tautology>:
$$
\boxed{\forall v\quad v(\alpha)=v(\beta).}
$$

Assign to each <propositional formula> its finite symbol length. The set of lengths of formulas <logically equivalent> to $\alpha$ is a nonempty subset of the <natural numbers>, because it contains the length of $\alpha$. The <well-ordering principle> for the <natural numbers> gives a least such length, and hence a shortest equivalent formula.

**A shortest equivalent formula need not be unique.** For example $(p_0\to p_0)$ and $(p_0\leftrightarrow p_0)$ are distinct <tautologies> of the same minimal length. A shorter formula would contain no binary connective and hence be a variable with some number of negations, which is not a <tautology>. This counts each <propositional variable> as one symbol, as in the stated language, and proves that a <shortest equivalent formula need not be unique>.

A <conjunctive normal form> is a finite conjunction of clauses, each clause being a finite disjunction of literals. A literal is a <propositional variable> or the negation of one. Thus its shape is
$$
\boxed{\bigwedge_{i=1}^m\left(\bigvee_{j=1}^{n_i}\ell_{ij}\right),\qquad\ell_{ij}\in\{p_k,\neg p_k:k\in\omega\}.}
$$
A single clause or single literal is allowed; the necessary parentheses can be inserted according to the grammar.

First eliminate implication and biconditional using the <logical equivalences>
$$
A\to B\equiv\neg A\lor B,\qquad A\leftrightarrow B\equiv(\neg A\lor B)\land(\neg B\lor A).
$$
Push negations inwards using <De Morgan's laws> and eliminate double negations. <Structural induction> leaves only literals joined by conjunction and disjunction.

Now induct on that remaining expression. A literal is already in <conjunctive normal form>. A conjunction of two <conjunctive normal forms> is again one after associating its clauses. For a disjunction, write the two inductively obtained forms as $\bigwedge_i C_i$ and $\bigwedge_j D_j$ and use distributivity:
$$
\boxed{\left(\bigwedge_i C_i\right)\lor\left(\bigwedge_j D_j\right)\equiv\bigwedge_{i,j}(C_i\lor D_j).}
$$
Each $C_i\lor D_j$ is a clause. All operations preserve <logical equivalence>, proving the required form exists.

Use an <involution with exactly one fixed point>:
$$
\boxed{\phi=\forall x\,f(f(x))=x\ \land\ \exists z\bigl(f(z)=z\land\forall x\,(f(x)=x\to x=z)\bigr).}
$$
The first conjunct makes every orbit of the <function> $f$ have size one or two. The second says precisely one orbit has size one. A finite model therefore has $1+2m$ elements, an odd number.

Conversely, on any set of size $2m+1$, fix one element and partition the remaining $2m$ elements into pairs; let $f$ swap the two members of each pair. This defines a model of $\phi$. The case $m=0$ gives a one-element model. Thus the finite model sizes are **exactly the positive odd integers**.

This recursion defines <satisfaction for a set structure> for every <first-order formula>.

<Semantic consequence under an assignment> means that for every <first-order structure> $\mathcal A$ with the relevant <first-order variable assignment> $v$ into its domain,
$$
\boxed{\bigl(\mathcal A\models\gamma[v]\text{ for every }\gamma\in\Gamma\bigr)\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathcal A\models\phi[v].}

By induction on <first-order formulas>, their <truth values> depend only on the values of their <free variables>. A <first-order sentence> has none. If all formulas here are sentences, every displayed satisfaction statement, and therefore the consequence condition, is independent of $v$.

The <compactness theorem> states that a set of <first-order sentences> has a model if every finite subset has a model.

For each $n\geq1$, let $\chi_n$ assert that at least $n$ distinct elements exist. Consider $T=\{\phi\}\cup\{\chi_n:n\geq1\}$. A finite subset requires only some finite lower bound $N$ on the size. Choose an odd integer $m\geq N$; the given property of $\phi$ supplies a model of that size, satisfying the finite subset. By the <compactness theorem>, $T$ has a model. Its domain has at least $n$ elements for every $n$, so **$\phi$ has an infinite model**. This argument applies to every sentence with the finite-model-size property in (b), irrespective of its behavior on infinite structures.

Write $E(x,y)$ for the displayed equality predicate. A6 and A4 give $\vdash E(x,x)$, establishing a <reflexive relation>.

An instance of A7, replacing just the first occurrence of $x$ in $E(x,x)$, is
$$
E(x,y)\to(E(x,x)\to E(y,x)).
$$
Assume $E(x,y)$, apply the <modus ponens rule> twice using reflexivity, and obtain $E(y,x)$. The <deduction theorem for propositional logic> discharges the assumption. This proves symmetry.

For transitivity, A7 supplies
$$
E(y,z)\to(E(x,y)\to E(x,z)),
$$
by replacing $y$ in the atomic formula $E(x,y)$. From assumptions $E(x,y)$ and $E(y,z)$, two applications of the <modus ponens rule> give $E(x,z)$. Discharging assumptions and universally generalizing gives
$$
\boxed{\vdash\forall x\,E(x,x),\quad\vdash\forall x\forall y(E(x,y)\to E(y,x)),\quad\vdash\forall x\forall y\forall z((E(x,y)\land E(y,z))\to E(x,z)).}
$$
Thus the predicate defines an <equivalence relation> within the calculus.

**No such first-order axiom collection can force actual identity when the displayed symbol is treated as an ordinary relation.** The <duplication obstruction to axiomatizing identity> proves this even for an infinite collection of extra axioms.

Start with any nonempty <first-order structure> $\mathcal A$ in which the symbol is identity. Form $B=A\times\{0,1\}$ and the surjection $\pi:B\to A$. Interpret each relation by pulling it back along $\pi$; in particular,
$$
E^{\mathcal B}((a,i),(b,j))\iff a=b.
$$
Interpret every function on first coordinates as in $\mathcal A$ and choose second coordinate zero; likewise lift constants with second coordinate zero. Induction on <first-order terms> gives $\pi(t^{\mathcal B}(v))=t^{\mathcal A}(\pi v)$. Induction on <first-order formulas> then gives
$$
\mathcal B\models\theta[v]\iff\mathcal A\models\theta[\pi v].
$$
The quantifier steps use surjectivity of $\pi$, so every witness in $A$ lifts to $B$.

Every proposed axiom valid with actual equality is therefore true in $\mathcal B$ under every assignment, along with A1–A7. But $E^{\mathcal B}((a,0),(a,1))$ holds for two different domain elements. Thus those axioms cannot enforce identity. The usual interpretation of <logical equality> as identity is a semantic requirement.

An approximation is a function $s:n+1\to X$ with $s(0)=x_0$ and $s(k+1)=g(s(k))$ for $k<n$. By induction on $n$, there is such a function: start with $\{(0,x_0)\}$ and append the uniquely specified value $g(s(n))$.

Any two approximations agree on their common domain, by induction from their common initial value and the same transition <function> $g$. The collection $\mathcal S$ of all approximations is a set, being a definable subset of $\mathcal P(\omega\times X)$. Therefore $f=\bigcup\mathcal S$ is a <function>. Existence of approximations of every finite length gives $\operatorname{dom}(f)=\omega$. Each defining equation appears in a sufficiently long approximation, so
$$
\boxed{f:\omega\to X,\qquad f(0)=x_0,\qquad f(n+1)=g(f(n)).}
$$
This proves the <natural-number recursion theorem> without the <axiom of choice>; no arbitrary sequence of choices was made.

Suppose $h:\omega\hookrightarrow X$ is an <injection>. Shift its image by defining
$$
g(h(n))=h(n+1),\qquad g(x)=x\quad(x\notin h[\omega]).
$$
The two pieces have disjoint images, so $g$ is injective, but $h(0)$ is omitted. Thus $g$ is not surjective.

Conversely, let $g:X\to X$ be an <injection> which omits some $a\in X$. The <natural-number recursion theorem> defines $h(0)=a$, $h(n+1)=g(h(n))$. If $h(m)=h(n)$ with $m<n$, repeated injectivity cancels $m$ iterations and gives $a=g^{n-m}(a)$, putting $a$ in the image of $g$, a contradiction. Hence $h$ is injective. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\omega\preceq X\iff X\text{ admits an injective nonsurjective self-map}.}
$$
Choosing one witness $a$ from a single known nonempty set uses no <axiom of choice>. This is the <Dedekind-infinite characterization>.

The <Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem> states that <injections> $A\hookrightarrow B$ and $B\hookrightarrow A$ imply a <bijection> $A\cong B$.

Let $\mathfrak c=|\mathbb R|$ and $\mathcal D=\{S\subseteq\mathbb R:S\text{ is dense in }\mathbb R\}$. Clearly $|\mathcal D|\leq2^{\mathfrak c}$. For the reverse bound, let $C$ be the usual <Cantor set>, of <cardinality> $\mathfrak c$. It is closed with empty interior, so $D=\mathbb R\setminus C$ is dense. The map
$$
\mathcal P(C)\longrightarrow\mathcal D,\qquad A\longmapsto D\cup A
$$
is injective, because $A=(D\cup A)\cap C$. Applying the <Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{|\mathcal D|=2^{\mathfrak c}=2^{2^{\aleph_0}}.}
$$
One can see $|C|=\mathfrak c$ by coding <binary sequences> as ternary expansions using only digits zero and two, together with the usual $|\{0,1\}^{\omega}|=|\mathbb R|$. The same fixed $D$ works for all subsets. This is the <cardinality of the family of dense subsets of the reals>.

A <transitive set> $X$ is one for which every member is a subset: $\forall y\in X\,(y\subseteq X)$, equivalently $\bigcup X\subseteq X$. If $x\in y\in X$, the subset condition puts $x\in X$. Conversely, if this membership implication always holds, every element of each $y\in X$ belongs to $X$, so $y\subseteq X$. Thus
$$
\boxed{X\text{ is transitive}\iff\forall x\forall y\,(x\in y\in X\to x\in X).}
$$

The four principles are:

* <Axiom of choice>: for every family $(A_i)_{i\in I}$ of nonempty sets, there exists a <function> $c$ on $I$ with $c(i)\in A_i$ for all $i$.
* <Cardinal comparability principle>: for any sets $A,B$, there is an <injection> $A\hookrightarrow B$ or an <injection> $B\hookrightarrow A$.
* <Well-ordering theorem>: every set admits a <well-order>.
* <Zorn's lemma>: every nonempty <partially ordered set> in which every chain has an upper bound has a maximal element.

In the last assertion the upper bound belongs to the partially ordered set; it need not belong to the chain.

Assume the <well-ordering theorem>. Every set with a chosen <well-order> has an <order isomorphism> to a unique <ordinal>. Any two <ordinals> are comparable, and the smaller embeds into the larger as an initial segment. Transporting that embedding back to the original sets proves the <cardinal comparability principle>.

Conversely, assume the <cardinal comparability principle> and let $X$ be any set. The <Hartogs theorem>, provable in <ZF>, supplies an <ordinal> $h(X)$ for which no <injection> $h(X)\hookrightarrow X$ exists. Comparison forces an <injection> $X\hookrightarrow h(X)$. Pulling back the <well-order> on its image gives a <well-order> on $X$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{ZF}\vdash\mathrm{CC}\leftrightarrow\mathrm{WO}.}
$$
The assumed ordinal facts are that every well-ordered set has an ordinal order type and that any two ordinals are comparable; the other assumed theorem is precisely the stated <Hartogs theorem>.

For the standard definition of <algebraic independence>, finite tuples are required to have distinct entries. Let $\mathcal P$ be the set of all subsets of $L$ with <algebraic independence> over $K$, ordered by inclusion. It is nonempty because $\varnothing\in\mathcal P$.

If $\mathcal C$ is a chain in $\mathcal P$, put $U=\bigcup\mathcal C$. A finite tuple of distinct elements of $U$ lies in one member of $\mathcal C$: choose a member containing each element and take the largest of those finitely many members. A polynomial relation on that tuple would then be a polynomial relation in that member, contradicting its <algebraic independence>. Thus $U\in\mathcal P$ and is an upper bound. For the empty chain, $\varnothing$ is an upper bound. The <Zorn's lemma> now gives a maximal member $B$, which is a <transcendence basis> by the specified maximality criterion. To also recover the usual field-theoretic definition, if $a\in L$ were transcendental over $K(B)$, clearing denominators in a polynomial relation would show that $B\cup\{a\}$ still has <algebraic independence> over $K$, contradicting maximality. Thus $L$ is algebraic over $K(B)$. Hence **every field extension has a <transcendence basis>**.

There is a literal issue in the printed definition: it does not require the tuple entries to be distinct. With that wording, any nonempty $X$ fails the test: for $a\in X$, the nonzero polynomial $X_1-X_2$ vanishes on $(a,a)$, in every characteristic. Thus under the literal printed test the family is exactly $\{\varnothing\}$, and $\varnothing$ is its maximal member. The same chain-and-<Zorn's lemma> argument still proves existence for that literal notion, but it is not the usual <transcendence basis> notion. **The conventional result above uses distinct tuple entries.**

Let $\mathcal O$ be the set of strict total orders on $\kappa$. Each is a subset of $\kappa\times\kappa$, so the <square of an infinite cardinal> gives
$$
|\mathcal O|\leq2^{|\kappa\times\kappa|}=2^\kappa.
$$
For the lower bound, use the set $\kappa\times\{0,1\}$ and order the two-element blocks by their first coordinates, using the <well-order> of $\kappa$. For every $A\subseteq\kappa$, put $(\alpha,0)$ before $(\alpha,1)$ exactly when $\alpha\in A$, reversing them otherwise. This defines a strict total order for each $A$, and different subsets give different orders. Transport these orders to $\kappa$ along a fixed <bijection> $\kappa\times\{0,1\}\cong\kappa$. Thus $2^\kappa\leq|\mathcal O|$, and the <Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{|\mathcal O|=2^\kappa.}
$$
If using the customary <axiom of choice> development of <infinite cardinal arithmetic>, its use here is precisely to supply $\kappa\times\kappa\cong\kappa$ and $\kappa\times2\cong\kappa$. The ordering construction makes no further choices. It proves the <cardinality of the family of strict total orders>. For $\kappa$ itself an <initial ordinal>, these particular product identities can also be proved in <ZF> by well-ordering pairs according to their maximum coordinate, so full <axiom of choice> is stronger than this instance intrinsically requires.
