= Semisimple Lie algebra
{wiki}

A finite-dimensional <Lie algebra> is semisimple when it has no nonzero solvable ideals.

= Chevalley basis
{c}
{parent=Semisimple Lie algebra}

A Chevalley basis of a complex <semisimple Lie algebra> consists of suitable coroot elements and one vector $e_\alpha$ in each <root space>, chosen so all <Lie bracket> structure constants are integers. In particular $[e_\alpha,e_\beta]=N_{\alpha,\beta}e_{\alpha+\beta}$ when the sum is a <root>, with $N_{\alpha,\beta}=\pm(p+1)$ where $\beta-p\alpha$ is the last negative-direction root in the <root string>. For independent roots with no root sum the bracket is zero. The integral lattice makes reduction to other fields possible.

= Serre relations
{parent=Chevalley basis}
{c}
{title2=$(\operatorname{ad}e_i)^{1-a_{ij}}e_j=0$}

For simple-root generators $e_i,f_i,h_i$ with $[h_i,e_j]=a_{ij}e_j$, the <Serre relations> include $[e_i,f_j]=\delta_{ij}h_i$ and $(\operatorname{ad}e_i)^{1-a_{ij}}e_j=(\operatorname{ad}f_i)^{1-a_{ij}}f_j=0$ for $i\ne j$. The off-diagonal Cartan entries are nonpositive integers. These relations restrict which iterated simple-root brackets remain nonzero.

= SU(2) proof of the Serre relations
{parent=Serre relations}
{c}

For distinct simple roots, $\alpha_j-\alpha_i$ has mixed-sign simple-root coefficients and is not a root. Thus $[f_i,e_j]=0$. In the finite-dimensional adjoint representation of the simple-root SU(2) subalgebra, $e_j$ is a lowest-weight vector of weight $a_{ij}$. Standard SU(2) ladders force $a_{ij}=-n_{ij}$ with $n_{ij}\ge0$ integral and make $(\operatorname{ad}e_i)^{n_{ij}+1}e_j=0$.

= Simply laced Chevalley commutator formula
{parent=Chevalley basis}
{title2=$x_s(u)^{-1}x_r(t)^{-1}x_s(u)x_r(t)=x_{r+s}(-N_{r,s}tu)$}

For equal-length <roots> $r,s$ with a root sum, their angle is $2\pi/3$. The vectors $2r+s$ and $r+2s$ have squared length three times a root's squared length, so neither is a root. Thus $[e_r,e_{r+s}]=[e_s,e_{r+s}]=0$. Apply the <central-commutator exponential identity> to $A=t\operatorname{ad}e_r$ and $B=u\operatorname{ad}e_s$, whose commutator is $tuN_{r,s}\operatorname{ad}e_{r+s}$, to obtain the displayed identity over the complex numbers.

= Absolute Jordan decomposition
{synonym}

A complex <semisimple Lie algebra> has an intrinsic <Jordan–Chevalley decomposition> $x=x_s+x_n$ characterized by $[x_s,x_n]=0$, a diagonalizable <adjoint representation> operator $\operatorname{ad}x_s$, and a nilpotent <adjoint representation> operator $\operatorname{ad}x_n$. The <adjoint representation> is faithful because the centre is zero. Closure of <semisimple matrix Lie algebras are closed under additive Jordan decomposition> applied to this faithful representation supplies the parts and their uniqueness. For every finite-dimensional <Lie algebra representation> $\rho$, the operators $\rho(x_s)$ and $\rho(x_n)$ are the semisimple and nilpotent parts of $\rho(x)$. Thus the decomposition is independent of a matrix embedding; for a nonfaithful representation it passes to the semisimple quotient by its kernel.

= Split semisimple Lie algebra
{parent=Semisimple Lie algebra}
{title2=$\mathfrak g=\mathfrak h\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha}\mathfrak g_\alpha\quad\text{over the ground field}$}

A <semisimple Lie algebra> over a field of <characteristic zero> is split if it has a <Cartan subalgebra> whose adjoint action is simultaneously diagonalizable over that field. Its <root-space decomposition> then needs no extension of scalars. The <sl2R Lie algebra> is split, with the real diagonal <Cartan subalgebra>. The real <special unitary Lie algebra> $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ is not: its adjoint operators are skew-adjoint for the positive definite <inner product> obtained by negating its <Killing form>. Thus a split adjoint operator would have to be zero.

= Compact real form of a complex semisimple Lie algebra
{title2=$\mathfrak u\otimes_{\mathbb R}\mathbb C\cong\mathfrak g$}
{parent=Semisimple Lie algebra}

= Compact real form
{synonym}

A compact real form of a complex <semisimple Lie algebra> $\mathfrak g$ is a real <Lie subalgebra> $\mathfrak u$ whose <complexification of a Lie algebra> is $\mathfrak g$ and whose <Killing form> is negative definite. Its simply connected <Lie group> is compact. The positive internal <inner product> used in unitary gauge theory is therefore $-\kappa$. For $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb C)$ the <SU(2) Lie algebra> is a compact real form, whereas $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb R)$ is a different real form with indefinite Killing form.

= Perfect Lie algebra
{parent=Semisimple Lie algebra}

A <Lie algebra> $L$ is perfect when $[L,L]=L$. Every semisimple Lie algebra over a field of characteristic zero is perfect.

Perfectness is a property of a <Lie algebra>, defined by equality with its commutator algebra; semisimplicity is a sufficient condition in characteristic zero.

= Weyl complete reducibility theorem
{c}
{parent=Semisimple Lie algebra}
{wiki}

Every finite-dimensional representation of a complex <semisimple Lie algebra> is a direct sum of irreducible representations. Equivalently, every invariant subspace has an invariant complement.

= Haar averaging produces invariant complements
{c}
{parent=Weyl complete reducibility theorem}
{title2=$V=W\oplus W^\perp$}

For a finite-dimensional continuous complex representation $R$ of a <compact group>, normalize <Haar measure> to mass one and average any positive-definite <Hermitian inner product>:
$$
\langle v,w\rangle_K=\int_K\langle R(g)v,R(g)w\rangle_0\,d\mu(g).
$$
The resulting inner product is positive-definite and invariant. If $W$ is an <invariant subspace>, then $W^\perp$ is invariant as well, since $\langle R(g)v,w\rangle_K=\langle v,R(g^{-1})w\rangle_K$. Thus $V=W\oplus W^\perp$. Restriction to a <compact real form>, followed by <integration of a Lie-algebra representation>, proves the <Weyl complete reducibility theorem>.

= Invariant complement from an equivariant projection
{parent=Weyl complete reducibility theorem}
{title2=$V=U\oplus\ker P$}

If a submodule $U$ of a <Lie algebra representation> $V$ admits an intertwining map $P:V\to U$ with $P|_U=I_U$, then $P^2=P$ and its <kernel> is an invariant complement. For a complex <semisimple Lie algebra>, the <Hom representation> on maps $V\to U$ whose restriction is a scalar identity reduces finding $P$ to <splitting of a trivial quotient for a semisimple Lie algebra>.

= Casimir splitting of a trivial quotient
{c}
{parent=Weyl complete reducibility theorem}

= Splitting of a trivial quotient for a semisimple Lie algebra
{synonym}

In a finite-dimensional <short exact sequence> $0\to N\to E\to\mathbb C\to0$ of representations of a complex <semisimple Lie algebra>, the generalized zero <eigenspace> of the <Casimir operator> maps onto the trivial quotient. All its irreducible <composition factors> have zero <Casimir eigenvalue>, so they are trivial. The action is therefore strictly upper triangular and has solvable image; because a <semisimple Lie algebra> is a <perfect Lie algebra>, that image is zero. Every lift in this generalized zero <eigenspace> is invariant, giving a <split short exact sequence>.

= sl2 triple
{parent=Special linear Lie algebra}
{title2=$[h,e]=2e,\quad[h,f]=-2f,\quad[e,f]=h$}

An sl2 triple consists of elements of a <Lie algebra> satisfying the displayed brackets. It defines a homomorphism from $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ by sending its standard generators to $h,e,f$. A nonzero homomorphism is injective because $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ is a <simple Lie algebra>. Root vectors and their coroot give such triples in a <semisimple Lie algebra>, and their representations supply finite <weight strings>.

= Finite sl2 lowest-weight ladder
{parent=sl2 triple}
{title2=$hX_n=(2n-\lambda)X_n,\quad fX_n=n(\lambda-n+1)X_{n-1}$}

In a finite-dimensional representation of an <sl2 triple>, start with a nonzero vector $X_0$ satisfying $fX_0=0$ and $hX_0=-\lambda X_0$, and set $X_n=e^nX_0$. The <commutator> identities give the displayed weight and lowering formulas by induction. If $X_N\ne0$ and $X_{N+1}=0$, then $fX_{N+1}=(N+1)(\lambda-N)X_N$ forces $\lambda=N$, a nonnegative <integer>. The nonzero terminal vector is essential: choosing a later index after the ladder has already vanished gives no constraint. Applied to the <adjoint representation> of a <semisimple Lie algebra>, this argument proves integrality of <Cartan integers>.

= Symmetric powers of the defining sln representation
{parent=Special linear Lie algebra}
{title2=$\operatorname{Sym}^m\mathbb C^n=V(m\omega_1)$}

For $n\geq2$ and $m\geq0$, the representation $\operatorname{Sym}^m\mathbb C^n$ of $\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbb C)$ is irreducible with <highest weight> $m\omega_1$. Its monomial basis supplies weights by adding $m$ defining-representation weights, with repeated weights counted separately.

= Contraction splitting of a defining module tensor a dual symmetric square
{parent=Symmetric powers of the defining sln representation}
{title2=$V\otimes\operatorname{Sym}^2V^*=\ker C\oplus\iota(V^*)$}

For the defining $n$-dimensional <special linear Lie algebra> module, with $n\geq2$, write $T_i(p)=e_i\otimes p(y_1,\ldots,y_n)$. The equivariant contraction is $C(T_i(p))=\partial_{y_i}p$. The map $\iota(f)=\sum_i e_i\otimes y_i f$ is also equivariant, since $\sum_i e_i\otimes y_i$ is an invariant identity tensor. Direct differentiation gives $C\iota=(n+1)I$, so the displayed <direct sum> follows. Its trace-free part is an <irreducible representation> of <highest weight> $\omega_1+2\omega_{n-1}$. Its <highest-weight vector> is $e_1\otimes y_n^2$; the <Weyl dimension formula> gives dimension $n(n-1)(n+2)/2$, exactly $\dim\ker C$.

= sl3 decomposition of the symmetric-square dual tensor product
{parent=Symmetric powers of the defining sln representation}
{title2=$(S^2V)\otimes V^*\cong\Gamma_{2,1}\oplus\Gamma_{1,0}$}

For the defining three-dimensional <special linear Lie algebra> representation $V$, contraction $(uv)\otimes f\mapsto f(u)v+f(v)u$ maps $(S^2V)\otimes V^*$ onto $V$. The kernel contains the <highest-weight vector> $e_1^2\otimes e_3^*$ of <Dynkin labels> $(2,1)$. The <Weyl dimension formula> gives its irreducible module dimension $15$, the entire kernel dimension; the <Weyl complete reducibility theorem> supplies the displayed splitting.

= Weight basis of the trace-free symmetric-square dual tensor module
{parent=sl3 decomposition of the symmetric-square dual tensor product}
{title2=$\ker(S^2W\otimes W^*\to W)$}

Let $t_{ij}=(e_i\otimes e_j+e_j\otimes e_i)/2$, and let $f_i$ be the dual <basis>. The equivariant contraction sends $t_{ij}\otimes f_k$ to $\delta_{ik}e_j+\delta_{jk}e_i$. Its kernel has a <weight basis> consisting of $t_{ii}\otimes f_k$ for $i\ne k$, $t_{ij}\otimes f_k$ for three distinct indices with $i<j$, and $t_{ij}\otimes f_j-\tfrac12t_{ii}\otimes f_i$ for $i\ne j$. The three families have sizes six, three and six. This is the irreducible fifteen-dimensional <highest-weight representation> with <highest weight> $2L_1-L_3$.

= Exterior square of the sl3 representation of highest weight (2,1)
{parent=sl3 decomposition of the symmetric-square dual tensor product}
{title2=$\bigwedge^2\Gamma_{2,1}\cong\Gamma_{5,0}\oplus\Gamma_{2,3}\oplus\Gamma_{3,1}\oplus\Gamma_{1,2}\oplus\Gamma_{0,1}$}

The <formal character> identity $\chi_{\wedge^2U}(x)=\tfrac12(\chi_U(x)^2-\chi_U(x^2))$ counts unordered pairs of distinct basis vectors. Substituting $\chi_{2,1}=h_2(x)h_1(x^{-1})-h_1(x)$ and the <Weyl character formula> gives the displayed multiplicity-free decomposition. Its dimensions sum to $21+42+24+15+3=105=\binom{15}{2}$. The <sl3 interlacing character formula> provides an exact weight-enumeration check.

= Symmetric square of the sl3 representation of highest weight (2,1)
{parent=Special linear Lie algebra}
{title2=$S^2\Gamma_{2,1}$}

For the <special linear Lie algebra> $\mathfrak{sl}(3)$, with <Dynkin labels> indexing irreducible <highest-weight representations>,
$$
S^2\Gamma_{2,1}\cong\Gamma_{4,2}\oplus\Gamma_{3,1}\oplus\Gamma_{0,4}\oplus\Gamma_{1,2}\oplus\Gamma_{2,0}.
$$
The summands have dimensions $60,24,15,15,6$, summing to $120=15\cdot16/2$. The identity follows from the <formal character of a weight module> formula $\chi_{S^2W}(z)=(\chi_W(z)^2+\chi_W(z^2))/2$ and the <Weyl character formula>.

= Matrix-unit extraction lemma for special linear ideals
{parent=Special linear Lie algebra}

Over a <field> of <characteristic> different from two, any <Lie algebra ideal> in $\mathfrak{sl}_n$ containing a nonscalar <matrix> is the whole <special linear Lie algebra>. Indeed, if $A_{ji}\ne0$ for $i\ne j$, then $[E_{ij},[E_{ij},A]]=-2A_{ji}E_{ij}$ extracts a <matrix unit>. For a nonscalar diagonal <matrix>, $[E_{ij},A]=(A_{jj}-A_{ii})E_{ij}$ extracts one instead. Its bracket with $E_{ji}$ produces $H=E_{ii}-E_{jj}$, and $[H,E_{ji}]=-2E_{ji}$ extracts the reverse unit. Brackets $[E_{ij},E_{jk}]=E_{ik}$ and $[E_{ki},E_{ij}]=E_{kj}$ spread these units to every row and column; opposite pairs span all trace-zero diagonal <matrices>. Thus only ideals consisting of scalar <matrices> can be proper. This proof works even when restrictions of distinct <roots of a root system> to a <Cartan subalgebra> coincide in positive <characteristic>.

= Two conjugate SU(3) triplets with a triplet
{parent=Fundamental representations of sl3}
{title2=$\overline{\mathbf3}\otimes\overline{\mathbf3}\otimes\mathbf3=2\overline{\mathbf3}\oplus\mathbf6\oplus V_{(1,2)}$}

Split the two dual factors into antisymmetric and symmetric <tensors>. The invariant <Levi-Civita symbol> identifies the antisymmetric pair with $\mathbf3$, giving $\mathbf6\oplus\overline{\mathbf3}$ after multiplying by the remaining triplet. On the symmetric part, contraction $T^a{}_{ba}$ maps onto $\overline{\mathbf3}$; the equivariant splitting is $T^a{}_{bc}=(\delta^a_bv_c+\delta^a_cv_b)/4$. Its trace-free kernel has dimension $18-3=15$ and highest weight with <Dynkin labels> $(1,2)$. The <Weyl dimension formula> gives dimension $15$, so this is exactly the irreducible kernel. It is conjugate to the other fifteen-dimensional module with labels $(2,1)$; dimension alone does not distinguish the two.

= Tensor cube of the defining SU(3) representation
{parent=Fundamental representations of sl3}
{title2=$\mathbf3^{\otimes3}=\mathbf{10}\oplus\mathbf8\oplus\mathbf8\oplus\mathbf1$}

The decuplet is fully symmetric under quark permutations, the singlet fully antisymmetric, and the two octets constitute the two-dimensional mixed permutation multiplicity space. Applying the <Pauli constraint on three-quark flavour multiplets> gives one ground-state spin-one-half octet and one spin-three-halves decuplet.

= sl2 Lie algebra
{title2=$\mathfrak{sl}_2$}
{c}
{parent=Special linear Lie algebra}

The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ has generators $e,h,f$ with $[h,e]=2e$, $[h,f]=-2f$, and $[e,f]=h$.

This is the $n=2$ instance of the <special linear Lie algebra>. Classifying its modules is a separate representation-theory problem.

= Weyl reflection lift in an sl2 representation
{parent=sl2 Lie algebra}
{c}
{title2=$s=\exp(e)\exp(-f)\exp(e)$}

In a finite-dimensional <sl2 Lie algebra> representation, raising and lowering operators are <nilpotent operators>, so their exponentials are finite sums. In the defining two-dimensional representation, the displayed product is $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$. It sends $h$ to $-h$ by conjugation, lifting the nontrivial <Weyl group> reflection. Its square acts on the irreducible highest-weight module $L(n)=\operatorname{Sym}^n\mathbb C^2$ by $(-1)^n$. Thus a Weyl reflection can have a representative of order four on a representation.

= Explicit weight basis of the exterior square of Sym4 for sl2
{parent=sl2 Lie algebra}
{title2=$\Lambda^2(\operatorname{Sym}^4\mathbb C^2)=L(6)\oplus L(2)$}

Let $e_i=x^{4-i}y^i$ and $w_{ij}=e_i\wedge e_j$ for $i<j$. For the <sl2 Lie algebra>, the raising and lowering operators are $E=x\partial_y$, $F=y\partial_x$, and $w_{ij}$ has <weight> $8-2(i+j)$. A <weight basis> of the <irreducible representation> $L(6)$ is
$$
w_{01},\ w_{02},\ w_{03}+2w_{12},\ w_{04}+8w_{13},\ w_{14}+2w_{23},\ w_{24},\ w_{34}.
$$
A <weight basis> of the complementary $L(2)$ is
$$
w_{03}-3w_{12},\quad w_{04}-2w_{13},\quad w_{14}-3w_{23}.
$$
The first vector in each list is a <highest-weight vector>. Applying $F$ along the first list gives successive coefficients $3,2,1,12,5,2$; along the second it gives $1,2$. The final vectors are killed by $F$. At the overlapping <weights> $2,0,-2$, the two listed vectors are independent, proving the <direct sum>.

= Killing form of the SU(2) Lie algebra
{parent=SU(2) Lie algebra}
{c}
{title2=$\kappa(T^a,T^b)=-2\delta^{ab}$}

In the anti-Hermitian basis $T^a=-i\sigma^a/2$, the <SU(2) Lie algebra> has $[T^a,T^b]=\varepsilon^{abc}T^c$ and <Killing form> $\kappa(T^a,T^b)=-2\delta^{ab}$. This is negative definite; the positive internal form for compact <Yang-Mills theory> is its negative.

= Killing-normalized SU(2) quadratic Casimir
{parent=Killing form of the SU(2) Lie algebra}
{c}
{title2=$C_\kappa=\tfrac12j(j+1)I$}

With Hermitian angular-momentum generators $[J_a,J_b]=i\epsilon_{abc}J_c$, the <Killing form> is $2\delta_{ab}$ and its inverse contraction is $C_\kappa=\tfrac12\sum_aJ_a^2$. Equivalently the anti-Hermitian basis $T_a=-iJ_a$ has <Killing form> $-2\delta_{ab}$ and gives the same operator. In the complex highest-weight basis, $C_\kappa=H^2/8+(E^+E^-+E^-E^+)/4$. It acts on spin $j$ by $j(j+1)/2$, so it differs in normalization from the usual angular-momentum <quadratic Casimir operator>.

= Classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations
{parent=sl2 Lie algebra}

For every $n\geq0$ there is one irreducible $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb C)$-module $V(n)$ of dimension $n+1$, with weights $n,n-2,\ldots,-n$, each of multiplicity one. Every finite-dimensional representation is a direct sum of these modules.

= Clebsch-Gordan splitting of a defining module and its cubic symmetric power
{c}
{parent=Classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations}
{title2=$L(1)\otimes L(3)=L(4)\oplus L(2)$}

For the defining <sl2 Lie algebra> module with basis $x,y$, put $A_k=x\otimes x^{3-k}y^k$ and $B_k=y\otimes x^{3-k}y^k$. The raising operator has $eA_k=kA_{k-1}$ and $eB_k=A_k+kB_{k-1}$; the lowering operator has $fA_k=B_k+(3-k)A_{k+1}$ and $fB_k=(3-k)B_{k+1}$. The <highest-weight vectors> $A_0$ and $B_0-A_1$ generate irreducible submodules of highest weights four and two. Bases are respectively $(A_0,3A_1+B_0,A_2+B_1,A_3+3B_2,B_3)$ and $(B_0-A_1,B_1-A_2,B_2-A_3)$. Their vectors are independent in every <weight space>, proving the displayed <direct sum> without only comparing dimensions.

= Parity unimodality of an sl2 character
{parent=Classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations}
{title2=$c_j\ge c_{j+2}\quad(j\ge0)$}

For a finite-dimensional <sl2 Lie algebra> representation, write $\chi(q)=\sum_jc_jq^j$. Each irreducible summand has character $q^m+q^{m-2}+\cdots+q^{-m}$, so $c_j=c_{-j}$ and $c_j\ge c_{j+2}$ for nonnegative $j$. Thus each parity subsequence is symmetric and unimodal. If all weights have one parity, removing the unused exponents gives an ordinarily unimodal coefficient sequence. The full sequence including every integer exponent need not be unimodal: $q+q^{-1}$ has coefficients $1,0,1$.

= sl2 highest-weight lowering formula
{parent=Classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations}
{title2=$XY^{k+1}v=(k+1)(m-k)Y^kv$}

In the <sl2 Lie algebra>, if $Xv=0$ and $Hv=mv$, then the <commutator> relations give $HY^jv=(m-2j)Y^jv$ and $XY^{k+1}v=(k+1)(m-k)Y^kv$. Induct using $XY=YX+H$. This controls the <raising operator> on a string formed by the <lowering operator> and yields the <classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations>.

= Trace of a raising-lowering product in an irreducible sl2 module
{parent=sl2 highest-weight lowering formula}
{title2=$\operatorname{tr}(XY)=k(k+1)(k+2)/6$}

For a highest-weight vector $v$ of weight $k$ in an irreducible finite-dimensional <sl2 Lie algebra> module, the <basis> $v,Yv,\ldots,Y^kv$ diagonalizes $XY$, with eigenvalue $(j+1)(k-j)$ on $Y^jv$. Summing those eigenvalues gives $\operatorname{tr}(XY)=k(k+1)(k+2)/6$. The formula $XY^nv=n(k-n+1)Y^{n-1}v$ requires $v$ to be a <highest-weight vector>, not an arbitrary vector of the module.

= Invariant form on an irreducible sl2 module
{parent=Classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations}

In the irreducible <sl2 Lie algebra> module $V(m)$, use $v_j=f^jv_0$, with $0\le j\le m$. The <Lie-invariant bilinear form> $B(v_j,v_k)=(-1)^j\mathbf1_{\{j+k=m\}}$ is <nondegenerate>: its anti-diagonal is nonzero. Invariance follows from $hv_j=(m-2j)v_j$, $fv_j=v_{j+1}$ and $ev_j=j(m-j+1)v_{j-1}$. Its transpose equals $(-1)^mB$, so it is a <symmetric bilinear form> when $m$ is an <even number>, and an <alternating bilinear form> when $m$ is an <odd integer>. The <Weyl complete reducibility theorem> gives a nondegenerate invariant form on any finite-dimensional module by taking the orthogonal <direct sum> of these forms.

= Injectivity of sl2 lowering above weight zero
{c}
{parent=Classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations}

On any finite-dimensional $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-module, the lowering operator $f$ is injective from the $m$-weight space to the $(m-2)$-weight space whenever $m>0$. This follows on each irreducible summand from its standard weight string.

= Reducibility of an sl2 Verma module
{title2=$M_\lambda$ reducible iff $\lambda\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}$}
{c}
{parent=sl2 Lie algebra}

The Verma module $M_\lambda$ has basis $(f^rv_\lambda)_{r\geq0}$ and
$$
e f^rv_\lambda=r(\lambda-r+1)f^{r-1}v_\lambda.
$$
It is reducible exactly when $\lambda=m\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}$; then its unique proper nonzero submodule is generated by $f^{m+1}v_\lambda$ and is isomorphic to $M_{-m-2}$.

= Nonsplit zero-weight Verma extension for sl2
{parent=Reducibility of an sl2 Verma module}
{title2=$0\to M(-2)\to M(0)\to\mathbb C\to0$}

= Intermediate-series sl2 module

= Matrix root basis of the symplectic Lie algebra
{parent=Symplectic Lie algebra}
{title2=$\mathfrak{sp}_{2l}=H\oplus\bigoplus_{r\in C_l}\mathbb C e_r$}

Relative to $J=\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\-I&0\end{pmatrix}$, a <symplectic Lie algebra> matrix has blocks $\begin{pmatrix}P&Q\\R&-P^{\mathsf T}\end{pmatrix}$ with $Q,R$ symmetric. Its diagonal <Cartan subalgebra> has basis $E_{ii}-E_{l+i,l+i}$. Write $\varepsilon_i(h)=\lambda_i$. A <root-space decomposition> is obtained from
$$
\begin{aligned}
e_{\varepsilon_i-\varepsilon_j}&=E_{ij}-E_{l+j,l+i} &&(i\ne j),\\
e_{\varepsilon_i+\varepsilon_j}&=E_{i,l+j}+E_{j,l+i} &&(i<j),\\
e_{-\varepsilon_i-\varepsilon_j}&=E_{l+i,j}+E_{l+j,i} &&(i<j),\\
e_{2\varepsilon_i}&=E_{i,l+i},\qquad e_{-2\varepsilon_i}=E_{l+i,i}.&&
\end{aligned}
$$
The <matrix unit> rule $[h,E_{ab}]=(h_{aa}-h_{bb})E_{ab}$ proves $[h,e_r]=r(h)e_r$. These root vectors and the diagonal basis span all the permitted blocks and are independent. There are $2l^2$ roots and $l$ diagonal basis elements, giving <dimension> $l(2l+1)$.

= Rank-two subsystems of a C3 root system
{parent=C3 root system}
{title2=$A_2\text{ and }C_2$}

In the realization $\Phi(C_3)=\{\pm2e_i,\ \pm e_i\pm e_j:i<j\}$, each coordinate two-plane cuts out a <C2 root system>. Each plane $\pm x_1\pm x_2\pm x_3=0$ cuts out an <A2 root system>; there are four such planes after identifying opposite normals. Thus the irreducible full rank-two <root subsystems> comprise three of type $C_2$ and four of type $A_2$. = Weyl chamber geometry of C3
{c}
{parent=C3 root system}
{title2=$x_1>x_2>x_3>0$}

The <C3 root system> consists of $\pm2e_i$ and $\pm e_i\pm e_j$ in $\mathbb R^3$. Its <Weyl group> is the <signed symmetric group> on three coordinates, of order $48$. The reflecting planes are $x_i=0$ and $x_i=\pm x_j$. The open <Weyl chamber> $x_1>x_2>x_3>0$ has closure generated by $(1,0,0)$, $(1,1,0)$ and $(1,1,1)$. Sign choices and absolute-coordinate orderings give its $48$ translates. On the unit sphere the chamber is a spherical triangle with angles $\pi/2$, $\pi/3$, $\pi/4$, and hence area $\pi/12$; the areas of all $48$ triangles sum to $4\pi$.

= Exterior-square double cover of SO0(3,2)
{parent=Spin group}
{title2=$Sp(4,\mathbb R)/\{\pm I\}\cong SO_0(3,2)$}

Fix a volume form on a four-dimensional real <symplectic vector space>. Wedge pairing on its <exterior square> has signature $(3,3)$. The <symplectic group> fixes the bivector inverse to the symplectic form. Its orthogonal complement is five-dimensional with signature $(3,2)$ after choosing the appropriate overall sign. The induced representation has kernel $\{\pm I\}$: identity action on the entire exterior square preserves every two-plane and thus every line, forcing a scalar transformation with square one. Its derivative is injective and both group dimensions are ten, so its image is the <identity component> of $SO(3,2)$.

= Hermitian-matrix double cover of SO0(3,1)
{c}
{parent=Spin group}
{title2=$SL(2,\mathbb C)/\{\pm I\}\cong SO_0(3,1)$}

On two-by-two <Hermitian matrices>, the <determinant> of $X=tI+x\cdot\sigma$ is $t^2-|x|^2$. The action $X\mapsto AXA^\dagger$ preserves it for $A\in SL(2,\mathbb C)$. A kernel element is unitary and commutes with every Hermitian matrix, hence is $\pm I$. The derivative is injective and both real dimensions are six, giving the full proper orthochronous <Lorentz group> as the connected image.

= Symmetric-matrix double cover of SO0(2,1)
{parent=Spin group}
{title2=$SL(2,\mathbb R)/\{\pm I\}\cong SO_0(2,1)$}

Congruence $X\mapsto AXA^T$ on real symmetric two-by-two <matrices> preserves the <quadratic form> $-\det X$ of signature $(2,1)$ when $\det A=1$. Its <kernel> is $\{\pm I\}$ and its derivative is injective: $aX+Xa^T=0$ for every symmetric $X$ implies $a=0$. Both <Lie groups> have dimension three, so the connected image is the full <identity component>. Also $A^TJA=(\det A)J$ identifies $Sp(2,\mathbb R)$ with $SL(2,\mathbb R)$.

= Exterior-square double cover of SO(3,3)

Its kernel is $\{\pm I\}$: a transformation acting trivially on every bivector preserves every two-plane and hence every line, so is scalar, with scalar square one. The derivative is injective and both groups have dimension fifteen, so the connected image is the full <identity component>. This is a concrete real spin-group cover.

= Spin(4) double cover

= Rotation and boost commutators with a fixed metric signature
{parent=Lorentz algebra}
{title2=$[K_i,K_j]=-si\epsilon_{ijk}J_k,\quad[J_i,K_j]=si\epsilon_{ijk}K_k$}

For the <Lorentz algebra> convention $[M_{\mu\nu},M_{\rho\sigma}]=i(M_{\mu\sigma}\eta_{\nu\rho}+M_{\nu\rho}\eta_{\mu\sigma}-M_{\mu\rho}\eta_{\nu\sigma}-M_{\nu\sigma}\eta_{\mu\rho})$, choose $\eta=\operatorname{diag}(s,-s,-s,-s)$, $s=\pm1$, $\epsilon_{123}=1$, $J_i=\epsilon_{ijk}M_{jk}/2$, and $K_i=M_{0i}$. For Hermitian rotations $J_i$ and boosts $K_i$, the complex combinations $A_i=(J_i+iK_i)/2$ and $B_i=(J_i-iK_i)/2$ are adjoints of one another. = Finite-dimensional complex Lorentz representations

The complexified <Lorentz algebra> splits into two commuting $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ factors. Extending two <homogeneous polynomial representations of SU2> therefore gives every finite-dimensional irreducible complex <group representation> of the <Lorentz spinor double cover>. The kernel sign permits descent precisely when $j_L+j_R$ is an integer. These field <group representations> are generally nonunitary for a positive-definite inner product; infinite-dimensional unitary Lorentz <group representations> belong to a different construction.

Parity fixes $J_i$, negates $K_i$, and therefore exchanges the two chiral factors. It sends $(j_L,j_R)$ to $(j_R,j_L)$.

The quadratic contraction $M_{\mu\nu}M^{\mu\nu}$ is parity even, while $M_{\mu\nu}\widetilde M^{\mu\nu}$ is parity odd. Their linear combinations give the quadratic Casimirs of the two chiral $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ factors.

= Cartan subalgebra

{wiki}

= Maximal torus of a semisimple Lie algebra
{c}
{synonym}

For a complex semisimple <Lie algebra>, a Cartan subalgebra is a maximal abelian subalgebra consisting of semisimple elements. It is also called a maximal torus in this setting.

= Self-centralizing property of a Cartan subalgebra
{parent=Cartan subalgebra}
{title2=$C_L(H)=H$}

For a <Cartan subalgebra> $H$ of a finite-dimensional complex <semisimple Lie algebra> $L$, $H$ is abelian and $C_L(H)=H$, so it is maximal among abelian subalgebras. Starting from the nilpotent self-normalizing definition, the <generalized-weight decomposition for a nilpotent Lie algebra> and the <Engel theorem> show that the zero generalized <weight space> of its adjoint action is exactly $H$. Invariance of the <Killing form> makes $H$ orthogonal to every nonzero generalized <weight space>. The <Lie theorem> gives $B([H,H],H)=0$, and nondegeneracy then forces $[H,H]=0$. An element commuting with $H$ normalizes it, hence belongs to $H$.

= Nondegeneracy of the Killing form on a Cartan subalgebra
{parent=Cartan subalgebra}

For a complex <semisimple Lie algebra>, the <Cartan subalgebra> is orthogonal under the <Killing form> to every nonzero <root space>. A vector in the Cartan subalgebra orthogonal to that whole subalgebra is therefore orthogonal to the entire Lie algebra and must vanish. On the Cartan subalgebra the form is $\kappa(h,h')=\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi}\alpha(h)\alpha(h')$.

= Euclidean subspace of a Cartan subalgebra
{c}
{title2=$\mathfrak h_{\mathbb R}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}\{h_{\alpha_i}\}$}
{parent=Nondegeneracy of the Killing form on a Cartan subalgebra}

The real span of the simple <coroots> inside a complex <Cartan subalgebra> carries a positive-definite restriction of the <Killing form>. Equivalently it consists of the $h$ for which all roots have real values. The formula $\kappa(h,h)=\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi}\alpha(h)^2$ is positive for $h\ne0$, since the roots span the dual. The induced dual <inner product> makes the <root system> Euclidean and makes <root reflections> orthogonal.

= Killing dual of a coroot
{c}
{parent=Euclidean subspace of a Cartan subalgebra}
{title2=$\mathcal B(H_\alpha)=\sum_{\beta\in\Phi}\beta(H_\alpha)\beta$}

Let $\mathcal B(H)=B(H,\cdot)$ be the <Killing form> identification of a <Cartan subalgebra> with its dual. The <root-space decomposition> gives $B(H,K)=\sum_{\beta\in\Phi}\beta(H)\beta(K)$, and therefore the displayed root expansion. On the real <coroot> span its coefficients are real, proving $\mathcal B(\mathfrak h_{\mathbb R})\subseteq\mathbb R P$. For $\mathfrak{sl}_3$ and $H_{12}=E_{11}-E_{22}$, the formula gives $\mathcal B(H_{12})=6(L_1-L_2)$. The normalization is $B(X,Y)=\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{ad}X\operatorname{ad}Y)$, not a rescaled trace form.

= Rank of a semisimple Lie algebra
{parent=Cartan subalgebra}
{title2=$\ell$}

The rank is the dimension of a <Cartan subalgebra>, equivalently the dimension of the real span of its <root system>.

= Root-space decomposition
{parent=Cartan subalgebra}

For a Cartan subalgebra $\mathfrak t$, a semisimple <Lie algebra> decomposes as
$$
\mathfrak g=\mathfrak t\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in R}\mathfrak g_\alpha,
\qquad
\mathfrak g_\alpha=\{x:[h,x]=\alpha(h)x\text{ for every }h\in\mathfrak t\}.
$$
The nonzero functionals $\alpha$ are the roots.

= Triangular decomposition of a Lie algebra
{parent=Root-space decomposition}
{title2=$\mathfrak g=\mathfrak n^-\oplus\mathfrak h\oplus\mathfrak n^+$}

Choosing a <positive system of a root system> for a complex <semisimple Lie algebra> divides its <root spaces> into negative and positive ones. Their respective sums $\mathfrak n^-$ and $\mathfrak n^+$, together with the <Cartan subalgebra> $\mathfrak h$, give this <direct sum> of <vector spaces>. Both $\mathfrak n^\pm$ are <nilpotent Lie algebras>, and $\mathfrak h\oplus\mathfrak n^+$ is a <Borel subalgebra>. The <Poincare-Birkhoff-Witt theorem> turns this into an ordered vector-space decomposition of the <universal enveloping algebra>, useful for building <Verma modules>.

= Root-space reducedness lemma
{parent=Root-space decomposition}
{title2=$\dim\mathfrak g_\alpha=1,\quad R\cap\mathbb R\alpha=\{\alpha,-\alpha\}$}

For a complex <semisimple Lie algebra>, restrict the module $\mathfrak h\oplus\bigoplus_{j\ne0}\mathfrak g_{j\alpha}$ to the <sl2 subalgebra associated with a root>. All its weights are even. Its raising operator maps the weight-zero space $\mathfrak h$ onto the one-dimensional line of the chosen <root vector>, so there is exactly one nontrivial irreducible summand: the adjoint module of highest weight $2$. Thus the root spaces at $\pm\alpha$ are one-dimensional and $2\alpha$ is not a root. For any parallel root $c\alpha$, the integers $2c$ and $2/c$ have product $4$; excluding double and half roots leaves only $c=\pm1$.

= Root space
{title2=$\mathfrak g_\alpha$}
{parent=Root-space decomposition}

For a root $\alpha$, its root space is the simultaneous eigenspace
$$
\mathfrak g_\alpha=\{x:[h,x]=\alpha(h)x\text{ for every }h\in\mathfrak t\}
$$
of the <adjoint representation> of the <Cartan subalgebra>.

= Root bracket and the opposite-root exception
{parent=Root space}
{title2=$[\mathfrak g_\alpha,\mathfrak g_{-\alpha}]\subset\mathfrak h$}

For <root spaces> $\mathfrak g_\alpha,\mathfrak g_\beta$, the <Jacobi identity> gives $[\mathfrak g_\alpha,\mathfrak g_\beta]\subset\mathfrak g_{\alpha+\beta}$ when $\alpha+\beta\ne0$. If the bracket is nonzero, that nonzero weight must be a root. When $\beta=-\alpha$, the bracket instead lies in the <Cartan subalgebra> and can be nonzero; zero is not a root. Thus the common nonzero-bracket implication requires the opposite-root exception.

= Root vector
{parent=Root space}
{title2=$e_\alpha$}

A root vector is a nonzero element of a <root space> $\mathfrak g_\alpha$ in the <root-space decomposition> of a <semisimple Lie algebra>.

= Regular element of a semisimple Lie algebra
{parent=Root-space decomposition}

An element $x$ of a semisimple <Lie algebra> of rank $\ell$ is regular when its <centralizer> has the minimum possible dimension $\ell$.

= Regular element criterion in a Cartan subalgebra
{parent=Regular element of a semisimple Lie algebra}
{c}

For $t$ in a <Cartan subalgebra> $\mathfrak t$, the <root-space decomposition> gives
$$
\mathfrak z_{\mathfrak g}(t)=\mathfrak t\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha(t)=0}\mathfrak g_\alpha.
$$
Thus $t$ is regular exactly when $\alpha(t)\ne0$ for every root $\alpha$.

= Two-root decomposition with a nondegenerate trace form
{parent=Root-space decomposition}

Let a maximal torus $\mathfrak t$ give a decomposition $\mathfrak g=\mathfrak g_\alpha\oplus\mathfrak t\oplus\mathfrak g_\beta$. If $\mathfrak g$ has a nondegenerate trace form, then $\beta=-\alpha$, the opposite root spaces generate $\mathfrak{sl}_2$, and
$$
\mathfrak g\cong\mathfrak{sl}_2\oplus\ker\alpha,
$$
where $\ker\alpha\subset\mathfrak t$ is central and abelian.

= sl2 subalgebra associated with a root
{title2=$\mathfrak m_\alpha\cong\mathfrak{sl}_2$}
{c}
{parent=Root-space decomposition}

For every root $\alpha$ of a complex semisimple Lie algebra, there are root vectors $e_\alpha\in\mathfrak g_\alpha$ and $f_\alpha\in\mathfrak g_{-\alpha}$ and an element $h_\alpha\in\mathfrak t$ satisfying
$$
[h_\alpha,e_\alpha]=2e_\alpha,\qquad
[h_\alpha,f_\alpha]=-2f_\alpha,\qquad
[e_\alpha,f_\alpha]=h_\alpha.
$$
Their span is an $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ subalgebra.

= Nonisotropic root lemma
{parent=sl2 subalgebra associated with a root}
{title2=$\alpha(t_\alpha)\ne0$}

For a <root of a root system> of a complex <semisimple Lie algebra>, define $t_\alpha$ by $B(t_\alpha,h)=\alpha(h)$ using the <Killing form> on its <Cartan subalgebra>. Then $\alpha(t_\alpha)\ne0$. Otherwise vectors $x\in\mathfrak g_\alpha$, $y\in\mathfrak g_{-\alpha}$ with $B(x,y)=1$ would generate a <solvable Lie algebra> with $[x,y]=t_\alpha$ central. The <Lie theorem> would make $\operatorname{ad}t_\alpha$ nilpotent, while the <root-space decomposition> makes it diagonalizable, forcing $t_\alpha$ into the zero <center of a Lie algebra>.

= Root system
{parent=Root-space decomposition}
{wiki}

A root system is a finite set of nonzero vectors closed under the reflections they define and satisfying the crystallographic integrality condition when it arises from a semisimple <Lie algebra>.

= Root hyperplane
{parent=Root system}
{title2=$\ker\alpha=\alpha^\perp$}

The hyperplane perpendicular to a <root of a root system> is its root hyperplane, interpreted as the kernel of the associated linear functional on the Cartan space. The corresponding <reflection> is $s_\alpha(v)=v-2\langle v,\alpha\rangle\alpha/\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle$. For the <B2 root system>, the four distinct hyperplanes are the two coordinate axes and the two diagonals; their reflections generate the signed-permutation <Weyl group> of order eight.

= Root subsystem
{parent=Root system}
{title2=$\Psi\subseteq\Phi$}

= Sub-root system
{synonym}

A root subsystem of a <root system> $\Phi$ is a subset that is itself a root system in its real span. In particular, if a subspace $F$ is spanned by roots, $\Phi\cap F$ is a full root subsystem: <root reflections> in its roots preserve both $\Phi$ and $F$. Reflection-generated subsystems need not contain every ambient root in their span. A pair is a <simple root> basis only when every subsystem root has integral coordinates of one sign in that pair; merely choosing two independent roots is insufficient.

= Negative root
{parent=Root system}
{title2=$\Phi_-=-\Phi_+$}

After choosing <positive roots> in a <root system>, a negative root is the negative of a <positive root>. Equivalently, for a regular linear functional defining the positive roots, its value on a negative root is negative. Relative to the associated <simple roots>, the coefficients of a negative root are all nonpositive integers.

= Obtuse nonopposite roots have a root sum
{parent=Root system}
{title2=$(\alpha,\beta)<0,\ \beta\ne-\alpha\Rightarrow\alpha+\beta\in R$}

For nonproportional roots in a <reduced root system> which is a <crystallographic root system>, the negative <Cartan integers> $m=2(\alpha,\beta)/(\alpha,\alpha)$ and $n=2(\alpha,\beta)/(\beta,\beta)$ satisfy $mn=4\cos^2\theta\in\{1,2,3\}$. At least one of $m,n$ equals $-1$, so reflecting one root in the other produces their sum. The exclusion of opposite roots is essential: their inner product is negative but their sum is zero, which is not a <root>.

= Irreducible root system
{parent=Root system}

A <root system> spanning a real <inner product space> is irreducible when it cannot be partitioned into two nonempty mutually <orthogonal> <root systems>. Its <Dynkin diagram> is connected. This condition concerns the roots themselves, rather than an arbitrary ambient space larger than their span.

= Weyl orbit spans an irreducible root space
{c}
{parent=Irreducible root system}
{title2=$\operatorname{span}(W\alpha)=V$}

For an <irreducible root system> spanning $V$, the span $U$ of a nonzero root's <Weyl group> orbit is invariant, as is $U^\perp$. A root reflection preserving both subspaces must have its normal entirely in one of them: otherwise its rank-one correction mixes the subspaces. Thus a proper nonzero $U$ would partition all roots into mutually orthogonal subsets, contradicting irreducibility. Consequently some orbit root is nonorthogonal to any given root. Since <Weyl reflections> preserve lengths, the pairwise integrality restrictions for nonorthogonal roots also bound length ratios of orthogonal roots. All squared-length ratios lie in $\{1,2,1/2,3,1/3\}$; there cannot be three distinct lengths, since ratios two and three relative to a shortest root would yield the forbidden ratio $3/2$.

= Crystallographic root system
{parent=Root system}
{title2=$2(\beta,\alpha)/(\alpha,\alpha)\in\mathbb Z$}

A finite <root system> in a real <inner product space> is crystallographic when every <Cartan integer> $2(\beta,\alpha)/(\alpha,\alpha)$ is an integer. The roots of a complex <semisimple Lie algebra> satisfy this because each number is a weight of its <sl2 subalgebra associated with a root>. Reducedness is a separate axiom; the <root-space reducedness lemma> establishes it for these Lie-algebra roots.

= Root string
{parent=Root system}
{title2=$S_{\alpha,\beta}=\Phi\cap(\beta+\mathbb Z\alpha)$}

A root string is the set $S_{\alpha,\beta}=\Phi\cap(\beta+\mathbb Z\alpha)$ for roots $\alpha,\beta$ of a <root system> $\Phi$. For nonproportional roots $\alpha$ and $\beta$ of a reduced crystallographic <root system>, the roots of the form $\beta+n\alpha$ form an unbroken <root string> $\beta-p\alpha,\ldots,\beta+q\alpha$, and $p-q=\langle\beta,\alpha^\vee\rangle$. The <root-string theorem> explains the endpoint relation. If $\beta=\pm\alpha$, zero is missing and this consecutive-string statement does not apply.

= Root-string theorem
{parent=Root string}

For nonproportional roots $\gamma,\delta$ of a reduced crystallographic <root system>, $S_{\gamma,\delta}=\{\delta-p\gamma,\ldots,\delta+q\gamma\}$ is consecutive, and $p-q=\langle\delta,\gamma^\vee\rangle$. Realize the crystallographic <root system> as the roots of a complex <semisimple Lie algebra>. The sum of the one-dimensional <root spaces> in this string is a module for the <sl2 subalgebra associated with a root>. Its weights are $\langle\delta,\gamma^\vee\rangle+2n$; they have one fixed parity. Complete reducibility gives a sum of <irreducible representations> of the <sl2 Lie algebra>. Two summands of this parity would overlap at weight zero when the parity is even, or at weights $\pm1$ when it is odd, contradicting the one-dimensional <root spaces>. Thus there is one irreducible string, whose opposite endpoint weights sum to zero. This proves the relation. For distinct <simple roots>, $p=0$ and the cardinality is $1-\langle\delta,\gamma^\vee\rangle$. The nonproportional hypothesis excludes $\delta=\pm\gamma$, where zero is not a root and the consecutive-string statement fails.

= Kostant partition function
{title2=$P(\nu)$}
{c}
{parent=Root lattice}
{wiki}

The Kostant partition function $P(\nu)$ counts the ways to express a weight $\nu$ as a nonnegative integer combination of positive roots. By the <Poincare-Birkhoff-Witt theorem>, $P(\lambda-\mu)$ is the multiplicity of the weight $\mu$ in the <Verma module> $M(\lambda)$.

= Generating series of the Kostant partition function
{parent=Kostant partition function}
{title2=$\sum_\nu p(\nu)e^{-\nu}=\prod_{\alpha>0}(1-e^{-\alpha})^{-1}$}

The <Kostant partition function> counts <nonnegative integer> combinations of the positive <roots of a root system>, without ordering the summands. Multiply the formal <geometric series> $\sum_{k\geq0}e^{-k\alpha}$ for every <positive root>. The coefficient of $e^{-\nu}$ counts precisely these combinations. Every coefficient is finite because a <linear functional> strictly positive on the <positive roots> bounds every participating integer coefficient. The sign of each exponent in the cancelling product must agree with that of this series.

= Weight lattice

= Integrality of representation weights on coroots
{parent=Weight lattice}
{title2=$\mu(h_\alpha)\in\mathbb Z$}

Restrict a finite-dimensional representation of a complex <semisimple Lie algebra> to the <sl2 subalgebra associated with a root> $\alpha$. Its Cartan element is the <coroot> $h_\alpha$. The <classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations> gives only integer eigenvalues for this element, so every <weight of a representation> has integral value on every coroot. This proves containment of all such representation weights in the <weight lattice>; it does not hold for unrestricted infinite-dimensional modules.

= Group ring of a weight lattice
{parent=Weight lattice}
{title2=$\mathbb Z[P]$}

= Weight-lattice group ring
{synonym}

The <group ring> of a <weight lattice> $P$ consists of finite sums $\sum_\lambda a_\lambda e(\lambda)$ with integer coefficients, where $e(\lambda)e(\mu)=e(\lambda+\mu)$. A finite-dimensional <Lie algebra representation> has <formal character> $\sum_\lambda(\dim V_\lambda)e(\lambda)$ in this ring. The <Weyl group> acts by $e(\lambda)\mapsto e(w\lambda)$. The ambient ring need not consist of genuine <characters>; the <representation ring of a semisimple Lie algebra> of finite-dimensional modules for a complex <semisimple Lie algebra> identifies with the invariant subring $\mathbb Z[P]^W$.

= Root-binomial divisibility of reflection anti-invariants
{parent=Group ring of a weight lattice}
{title2=$s_\alpha u=-u\ \Longrightarrow\ u/(1-e(\alpha))\in\mathbb Z[P]$}

For a <root reflection> on the <weight lattice>, $s_\alpha\lambda=\lambda-m\alpha$ with $m=\lambda(H_\alpha)\in\mathbb Z$. A finite anti-invariant element of the <group ring of a weight lattice> is a sum of terms $a_\lambda[e(\lambda)-e(s_\alpha\lambda)]$; fixed weights have zero coefficient. Each pair is divisible by $1-e(\alpha)$, because $1-t^m$ is divisible by $1-t$ in $\mathbb Z[t,t^{-1}]$ for every integer $m$. Thus the quotient has finite support.

The formal expression $(\sum_{n\geq0}e(n\alpha))u$ is interpreted coefficientwise. Along each coset of $\mathbb Z\alpha$, only finitely many coefficients of $u$ are nonzero and their total is zero by reflection anti-invariance. Partial coefficient sums therefore vanish before and after that finite interval. The result has finite support, even though the individual summands $e(n\alpha)u$ do not eventually vanish.

= Real structure of the SU4 exterior square

On $\bigwedge^2\mathbb C^4$, the invariant Hermitian form and volume <tensor> define the displayed <antilinear map> with square one, commuting with <SU(4)>. = Coxeter length
{title2=$\ell(w)$}
{c}
{parent=Weyl group}

The Coxeter length $\ell(w)$ is the smallest number of simple reflections whose product is $w$. Its parity gives the sign of a Weyl-group element.

This is a particular <length function>, namely word length for the simple reflections.

= Weyl stabilizer of a dominant point
{c}
{parent=Coxeter length}

If $\lambda$ and $w\lambda$ both lie in the closed dominant chamber, then $w$ is a product of simple reflections whose walls contain $\lambda$. In particular, $w\lambda=\lambda$.

= Inversion set of a Weyl-group element
{title2=$N(w)$}
{parent=Coxeter length}

For a chosen <positive system of a root system> $\Pi$, the inversion set is
$$
N(w)=\{\beta\in\Pi:w(\beta)\in-\Pi\}.
$$
For a finite <Weyl group>, $|N(w)|=\ell(w)$.

Its cardinality is the <Coxeter length>; this root-sign definition should not be silently identified with an unspecified permutation inversion convention.

= Positive-root criterion for Coxeter length
{parent=Coxeter length}

In the geometric representation of a Coxeter system,
$$
\ell(ws_i)>\ell(w)\iff w(e_i)\text{ is a positive root},
$$
and
$$
\ell(s_iw)>\ell(w)\iff w^{-1}(e_i)\text{ is a positive root}.
$$
Replacing “positive” by “negative” reverses either length inequality.

= Dynkin diagram

{wiki}

A Dynkin diagram records the angles and relative lengths of the simple roots. A multiple-edge arrow points toward the shorter root.

= Classification of finite crystallographic Dynkin diagrams
{parent=Dynkin diagram}

The connected <Dynkin diagrams> of finite reduced <crystallographic root systems> are $A_r$, $B_r$, $C_r$, $D_r$, $E_6$, $E_7$, $E_8$, $F_4$ and $G_2$. Bonds encode products of off-diagonal <Cartan integers>; multiple-bond arrows point toward shorter roots. The simply laced branching diagrams have arm lengths $(1,1,r-3)$ for $D_r$ and $(1,2,2)$, $(1,2,3)$, $(1,2,4)$ for the three exceptional $E$ types. The $B$ and $C$ chains have an end double bond, $F_4$ a middle double bond, and $G_2$ a triple bond. This edge weighting is distinct from the vertex labels used to classify nilpotent orbits.

= Acyclicity of a finite Dynkin diagram
{parent=Dynkin diagram}

The underlying graph of a finite <Dynkin diagram> is a forest. Normalize every <simple root> to length one. Each bonded pair has inner product at most $-1/2$, and every other distinct pair has nonpositive inner product. A cycle with $m$ vertices would make the squared norm of their sum at most $m-m=0$, contradicting their <linear independence> in an <inner product space>. Multiple bonds denote root-length information, not graph-theoretic parallel-edge cycles.

= G2 root system
{title2=$G_2$}
{c}
{parent=Root system}

The $G_2$ root system has six short and six long roots. For a short simple root $\alpha_1$ and a long simple root $\alpha_2$, its positive roots are
$$
\alpha_1,\ \alpha_2,\ \alpha_1+\alpha_2,\ 2\alpha_1+\alpha_2,\ 3\alpha_1+\alpha_2,\ 3\alpha_1+2\alpha_2.
$$

These are the root data associated with <G2 (mathematics)>.

= Triple bond isolates a G2 component
{parent=G2 root system}

If two <simple roots> have angle $5\pi/6$, normalize them to unit vectors $u,v$ with $(u,v)=-\sqrt3/2$. For any other unit <simple root> $w$, set $r=(u,w)\le0$, $s=(v,w)\le0$. Its three-root <Gram matrix> has determinant $1/4-r^2-s^2-\sqrt3rs$. Any nonzero $r$ or $s$ has absolute value at least $1/2$, contradicting positive definiteness. Thus all other <simple roots> are orthogonal to $u,v$. An irreducible <root system> containing this triple bond must be the rank-two <G2 root system>.

= G2 adjoint branching to a short-root sl2 subalgebra
{c}
{parent=G2 root system}

For a <short root> $\alpha$ in the <G2 root system>, restriction of the <adjoint representation> to the <sl2 subalgebra associated with a root> gives
$$
\mathfrak g\cong V(3)^{\oplus2}\oplus V(2)\oplus V(0)^{\oplus3}.
$$
Here $V(n)$ is the irreducible <sl2 Lie algebra> representation of <highest weight> $n$ and dimension $n+1$. Every nonzero <root vector> $x\in\mathfrak g_\alpha$ has $\dim Z_{\mathfrak g}(x)=6$: the <raising operator> has a one-dimensional <kernel> in each of these six irreducible summands.

= Long-root A2 subsystem of G2
{title2=$A_2\subset G_2$}
{c}
{parent=G2 root system}

For short and long simple roots $\alpha,\beta$ of $G_2$, the long roots form an $A_2$ root system with simple roots $\delta_1=\beta$ and $\delta_2=3\alpha+\beta$. If $\omega_1,\omega_2$ are the corresponding $A_2$ <fundamental weights>, then $\alpha=\omega_2-\omega_1$ and $\alpha^\vee=\delta_2^\vee-\delta_1^\vee$.

= Restriction of the seven-dimensional G2 representation to long-root A2

The seven-dimensional fundamental $G_2$ representation has weights zero and the six short roots. Under its long-root $A_2$ subalgebra, these are the weights of the defining three-dimensional representation, its dual, and the trivial representation:

= Root-system finiteness lemma
{parent=Root system}

For roots $\alpha\ne\pm\beta$,
$$
\langle\alpha,\beta^\vee\rangle
\langle\beta,\alpha^\vee\rangle
=4\cos^2\theta\in\{0,1,2,3\},
$$
where $\theta$ is their angle. Integrality gives an integer, and nonparallelism makes it strictly smaller than four.

= Classification of rank-two root systems
{parent=Root-system finiteness lemma}

Every reduced crystallographic rank-two root system is isomorphic to $A_1\times A_1$, $A_2$, $B_2$, or $G_2$. Their simple-root angles are respectively $\pi/2$, $2\pi/3$, $3\pi/4$, and $5\pi/6$.

= Weyl group of a rank-two root system
{parent=Classification of rank-two root systems}

The two simple reflections generate a <dihedral group>. For $A_1\times A_1$, $A_2$, $B_2$, and $G_2$, the rotation has order $2,3,4,6$, so the Weyl groups have orders $4,6,8,12$ respectively.

= Dominant root-lattice highest weights have zero weight
{parent=Highest-weight representation}

If a <dominant integral weight> $\lambda$ lies in the <root lattice>, its finite-dimensional irreducible <highest-weight representation> contains zero as a weight. First $\lambda$ is a nonnegative integral combination of <simple roots>: a negative part would have negative <inner product> with $\lambda$, contradicting dominance. For any nonzero weight $\mu=\sum m_i\alpha_i$ with $m_i\geq0$, the identity $(\mu,\mu)=\sum m_i(\mu,\alpha_i)>0$ supplies an index with $m_i>0$ and $\langle\mu,\alpha_i^\vee\rangle>0$. By <injectivity of sl2 lowering above weight zero>, applying the corresponding <lowering operator> produces a nonzero vector of weight $\mu-\alpha_i$. Repetition reaches zero.

= Minuscule representation
{parent=Highest-weight representation}
{wiki}

A finite-dimensional irreducible representation of a complex <semisimple Lie algebra> is minuscule if its weights form one <Weyl group> orbit. Every <weight multiplicity> is then one, because multiplicities are invariant under the <Weyl group> and the <highest weight> has multiplicity one.

= Formal character of a minuscule representation
{parent=Minuscule representation}

The <formal character of a weight module> for a <minuscule representation> of <highest weight> $\lambda$ is $\operatorname{ch}V(\lambda)=\sum_{\mu\in W\lambda}e^\mu$. The sum is over distinct points of the <Weyl group> orbit, so $\dim V(\lambda)=|W\lambda|$.

= Minuscule weight
{parent=Minuscule representation}
{title2=$\lambda$}

A <dominant integral weight> $\lambda$ is minuscule when its irreducible <highest-weight representation> is a <minuscule representation>. Equivalently, $\langle\lambda,\alpha^\vee\rangle\in\{0,1\}$ for every <positive root> $\alpha$. This convention includes the zero weight and its <trivial Lie algebra representation>.

= Minuscule weights in the root lattice are zero
{parent=Minuscule weight}

By <dominant root-lattice highest weights have zero weight>, a <minuscule weight> in the <root lattice> has zero in its <Weyl group> orbit. Since every element of the <Weyl group> is invertible, the highest weight must itself be zero. Thus equality of the <weight lattice> and <root lattice> rules out every nontrivial <minuscule representation> without requiring a classification of <root systems>.

A ladder operator maps weight vectors to weight vectors whose weights differ by one root. Positive-root vectors raise weights and negative-root vectors lower them.

= Generalized-weight decomposition for a nilpotent Lie algebra
{parent=Generalized weight space of a Lie algebra representation}
{title2=$V=\bigoplus_\lambda V^\lambda$}

Every finite-dimensional complex <Lie algebra representation> of a <nilpotent Lie algebra> decomposes into invariant <generalized weight spaces>. The nilpotence assumption is essential: a general solvable action can have extensions between distinct weights.

Here is a proof. For $h,k\in H$, nilpotence gives $(\operatorname{ad}h)^N k=0$, hence $(\operatorname{ad}\rho(h))^N\rho(k)=0$. By the <nilpotent commutator preserves generalized eigenspaces> lemma, every <generalized eigenspace> of $\rho(h)$ is invariant under every $\rho(k)$. Split successively by the acting operators of a <basis> of $H$. Each resulting block is invariant under $H$, and each basis operator has only one <eigenvalue> on it. The <Lie theorem> triangularizes the action on each block; its diagonal entries therefore give one and the same <linear functional> $\lambda$ on every basis vector of that block. Every $\rho(h)-\lambda(h)I$ is strictly upper triangular there, proving that the block is $V^\lambda$. Distinct blocks have distinct weights, so this is a <direct sum>. On the zero generalized <weight space>, every acting operator is a <nilpotent endomorphism>, so the <Engel theorem> supplies a nonzero common annihilated vector whenever that space is nonzero.

= Zero generalized weight space of a Cartan subalgebra
{parent=Generalized-weight decomposition for a nilpotent Lie algebra}
{title2=$L^0(H)=H$}

Let $H$ be a <Cartan subalgebra> of a finite-dimensional complex <Lie algebra> $L$. In the adjoint <generalized-weight decomposition for a nilpotent Lie algebra>, its zero summand satisfies $L^0=H$. Nilpotence gives $H\subseteq L^0$. If $L^0/H$ were nonzero, all adjoint operators of $H$ on this quotient would be nilpotent, so the <Engel theorem> would supply a nonzero coset $x+H$ with $[H,x]\subseteq H$. That contradicts self-normalization. The derivation rule ensures that $L^0$ is a subalgebra, so the quotient action is well-defined.

= Weight string
{parent=Weight space}

For a finite-dimensional representation of a complex <semisimple Lie algebra>, the weights on a line $\mu+\mathbb Z\alpha$ form a contiguous string from $\mu+p\alpha$ to $\mu-q\alpha$, with $q-p=\langle\mu,\alpha^\vee\rangle$. = Weight-string enumeration algorithm
{parent=Weight string}

Start with the <highest weight> and process weights by increasing height below it. For each simple root, compute the upper endpoint from the already known higher weights, use the <weight string> endpoint relation, and append all downward steps. Repeat until the finite set stabilizes. This recovers the weights of a finite-dimensional <irreducible Lie algebra representation> without determining <weight multiplicities>.

= Dominant weight multiplicity formula for sl3
{parent=sl3 interlacing character formula}
{title2=$m_{a,b}(p,q)=\max\{0,1+\min(a,b,r,s)\}$}

For the irreducible <highest-weight representation> of the <special linear Lie algebra> $\mathfrak{sl}_3$ with <Dynkin labels> $(a,b)$, let $(p,q)$ be a <dominant integral weight>. Put $r=(2a+b-2p-q)/3$ and $s=(a+2b-p-2q)/3$. If these are not <integers>, the <weight multiplicity> is zero; otherwise it is $\max\{0,1+\min(a,b,r,s)\}$.

To derive the formula, use the <sl3 interlacing character formula> with top row $(a+b,b,0)$. The target diagonal exponents are $(s+p+q,s+q,s)$, and an interlacing middle row $(P,Q)$ has $P+Q=2s+p+2q$, with bottom entry $s+p+q$. Thus
$$
\max\{b,2s+p+2q-b,s+p+q\}\leq P\leq\min\{a+b,2s+p+2q\}.
$$
Every integral $P$ in this interval gives exactly one <Gelfand–Tsetlin basis> vector. The upper bound minus the lower bound is the minimum of six differences: $a,s,r,r+p+q,b,s+q$. Since $p,q\geq0$, this minimum reduces to $\min(a,b,r,s)$. Counting the interval proves the formula, including absent <weights of a representation>. This converts a two-dimensional <weight diagram> calculation into four elementary boundary distances.

= Alternant orthogonality on a unitary torus
{parent=Weyl character formula}
{title2=$\int_T A_\kappa\overline{A_\eta}\,dt=n!\,\delta_{\kappa\eta}$}

For strictly decreasing integer tuples $\kappa,\eta$, let $A_\kappa(z)=\det(z_i^{\kappa_j})$ on the diagonal <maximal torus> of the <unitary group>. Expanding both <determinants>, <Fourier orthogonality> makes the integral zero unless the exponent tuples have the same permutation orbit. Strict decreasing order makes that equivalent to $\kappa=\eta$; the $n!$ matching permutation terms then each contribute one. An alternating <Laurent polynomial> is a finite linear combination of these alternants. For an <irreducible character> $\chi_\lambda$, its highest monomial gives coefficient one for $A_{\lambda+\delta}$ in $\chi_\lambda A_\delta$, with $\delta=(n-1,\ldots,0)$. The <Weyl integration formula> and <Schur orthogonality relations> force the sum of squared coefficients to be one, so all other coefficients vanish. This proves the unitary <Weyl character formula> directly.

= Lowest-degree alternant proof of the Weyl dimension formula
{parent=Weyl dimension formula}

For a <root system> with $N$ positive <roots>, put $A_\nu(H)=\sum_{w\in W}\det(w)e^{(w\nu,H)}$. Its degree-$m$ Taylor coefficient is alternating separately in $\nu$ and $H$, and hence divisible in each set of variables by the product of positive-root linear forms. Thus it vanishes for $m<N$ and its degree-$N$ term is $C\prod_{\alpha>0}(\nu,\alpha)\prod_{\alpha>0}(H,\alpha)$. The <Weyl denominator formula> at $\nu=\rho$ determines the nonzero constant $C$. Taking the ratio $A_{\lambda+\rho}(tH)/A_\rho(tH)$ for regular $H$ and letting $t\to0$ gives the <Weyl dimension formula> $\dim L(\lambda)=\prod_{\alpha>0}(\lambda+\rho,\alpha)/(\rho,\alpha)$. Factors converting roots to <coroots> cancel.

For $V=L(\omega_1)$ with the <G2 root system> numbered short-first,
$$
\Lambda^2V=L(\omega_2)\oplus L(\omega_1),\qquad S^2V=L(2\omega_1)\oplus L(0).
$$
In the chain basis of the <crystal of the seven-dimensional G2 representation>, normalize $v_1=f_1v_0$, $v_2=f_2v_1$, $v_3=f_1v_2$. Then $v_0\wedge v_1$ and $v_0\wedge v_3-2v_1\wedge v_2$ are nonzero <highest-weight vectors> of weights $\omega_2$ and $\omega_1$; the identities $e_1v_1=v_0$, $e_1v_3=2v_2$, $e_2v_2=v_1$ verify both raising conditions. The <Weyl complete reducibility theorem> and <G2 dimension polynomial> exhaust the twenty-one dimensions of the <exterior square>. In the <symmetric square>, $v_0^2$ generates the twenty-seven-dimensional summand. Self-duality supplies a <Lie-invariant bilinear form>, which is symmetric because a <nondegenerate> <alternating bilinear form> cannot have odd <dimension>; its inverse gives the remaining invariant line.

= Crystal of the defining odd-orthogonal representation
{parent=Crystal basis}

The defining representation of $\mathfrak{so}_{2n+1}$ has <weights> $\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_n,0,-\varepsilon_n,\ldots,-\varepsilon_1$. Its <crystal basis> is a chain on these vertices, with lowering-edge colors $1,\ldots,n-1,n,n,n-1,\ldots,1$. The short-root string has length two through zero. With the <crystal tensor-product rule>, for $n\ge2$ the tensor square has highest vertices $1\otimes1$, $1\otimes2$ and $1\otimes\bar1$, corresponding to the traceless <symmetric square>, the <exterior square> and the <trivial Lie algebra representation>. Their <dimensions> are $(2n+1)(n+1)-1$, $n(2n+1)$ and one.

= Root-string property of a crystal
{parent=Crystal basis}

For each simple root $\alpha_i$, the $i$-colored edges of a crystal form strings. Along such a string, $\widetilde e_i$ raises the weight by $\alpha_i$ and $\widetilde f_i$ lowers it by $\alpha_i$.

= Kashiwara operator
{title2=$\widetilde e_i,\widetilde f_i$}
{c}
{parent=Crystal basis}

On a crystal, $\widetilde f_i$ follows an edge of color $i$ and $\widetilde e_i$ follows that edge backwards.

These maps are the raising and lowering operations of a <crystal basis>.

= Tensor product of crystals
{parent=Crystal basis}

The tensor product crystal uses the $i$-string data $\varepsilon_i$ and $\varphi_i$ to decide on which tensor factor a <Kashiwara operator> acts. Its connected components are highest-weight crystals and encode the decomposition of the tensor product representation.

This tensor-product operation combines one <crystal basis> with another, with factor selection governed by their string parameters.

= A2 defining tensor crystals
{parent=Tensor product of crystals}
{c}

For the defining representation of the <special linear Lie algebra> $\mathfrak{sl}_3$, the <crystal basis> is $1\xrightarrow{1}2\xrightarrow{2}3$, with weights $\varepsilon_i$ restricted to trace-zero diagonal matrices. Its dual is $\bar3\xrightarrow{2}\bar2\xrightarrow{1}\bar1$, with weights $-\varepsilon_i$. Using the <crystal tensor-product rule> with lowering on the first factor when $\varphi_i(a)>\varepsilon_i(b)$, the tensor square has highest vertices $1\otimes1$ and $1\otimes2$, of <highest weights> $2\omega_1,\omega_2$, generating components of six and three vertices. The defining tensor dual has highest vertices $1\otimes\bar3$ and $1\otimes\bar1$, generating components of eight and one vertices and weights $\omega_1+\omega_2,0$. These encode the <symmetric square> plus <exterior square>, and the trace-free endomorphisms plus scalar identity, respectively. A crystal vertex labelled by a tensor pair need not represent that same undeformed ordinary simple tensor; the actual invariant vector is $\sum_i e_i\otimes e_i^*$.

= Crystal tensor-product rule
{parent=Tensor product of crystals}

Write $\varepsilon_i(b)$ and $\varphi_i(b)$ for the numbers of raising and lowering steps of color $i$ available from a vertex of a <crystal basis>. One convention for the <tensor product of crystals> is
$$
\widetilde f_i(a\otimes b)=\begin{cases}\widetilde f_i(a)\otimes b,&\varphi_i(a)>\varepsilon_i(b),\\a\otimes\widetilde f_i(b),&\varphi_i(a)\le\varepsilon_i(b).\end{cases}
$$
The raising operator uses the first factor for $\varphi_i(a)\ge\varepsilon_i(b)$ and the second otherwise. The different strictness makes raising and lowering inverse along every edge. An edge is absent if the chosen <Kashiwara operator> vanishes. Stating the convention is essential when identifying the <highest-weight vectors> as ordered tensor vertices.

= Casimir operator
{c}
{synonym}

The quadratic Casimir element is a central element of $U(\mathfrak g)$. On a highest-weight module of highest weight $\lambda$, one standard normalization makes it act by $|\lambda+\rho|^2-|\rho|^2$.

= Quadratic Casimir operator
{parent=Casimir element}
{title2=$\sum_a t_at_a=C_2(R)I$}

For a <Lie algebra representation> with generators referred to an orthonormal invariant form, the sum $\sum_at_at_a$ commutes with every generator. Indeed, the <commutator> is proportional to a contraction of antisymmetric <structure constants of a Lie algebra> with the symmetric product $t_at_b+t_bt_a$. The <Schur lemma> makes it scalar in an irreducible complex representation; that scalar is its quadratic <Casimir eigenvalue>. With <trace> normalization $\operatorname{tr}(t_at_b)=C(R)\delta_{ab}$, taking the <trace> gives $C(R)\dim\mathfrak g=C_2(R)\dim R$.

= SU(3) Casimir in Chevalley generators
{parent=Quadratic Casimir operator}
{c}
{title2=$C=3C_2$}

For $e_3=[e_1,e_2]$, $f_3=-[f_1,f_2]$, the operator $C=h_1^2+h_2^2+h_1h_2+3(h_1+h_2)+3\sum_{i=1}^3f_ie_i$ commutes with all generators. In its commutator with $e_1$, the Cartan polynomial contributes $3h_1e_1$, while the last sum contributes $-3h_1e_1$; the two mixed terms involving $f_2e_3$ cancel. On highest weight $(p,q)$ its eigenvalue is $p^2+q^2+pq+3p+3q$. This normalization is three times the conventional <SU(3) quadratic Casimir eigenvalue>.

= SU(3) quadratic Casimir eigenvalue
{c}
{parent=Quadratic Casimir operator}
{title2=$C_2(a,b)=(a^2+ab+b^2+3a+3b)/3$}

For generators normalized by $\operatorname{tr}_{\mathbf3}(t_at_b)=\delta_{ab}/2$, the <quadratic Casimir operator> on the irreducible <SU(3)> representation of <Dynkin labels> $(a,b)$ has the displayed scalar value. In <SU(3) highest-weight coordinates> $(p,q)$ it is $p^2+q^2+p+\sqrt3q$.

To derive this, write the operator as $H_1^2+H_2^2+\sum_{i=1}^3(E_+^iE_-^i+E_-^iE_+^i)$. On a <highest-weight vector>, the raising operators vanish, and the three root commutators sum to $H_1+\sqrt3H_2$. Centrality and the <Schur lemma> extend this value to the representation. The singlet, fundamental, adjoint and decuplet values are $0,4/3,3,6$, respectively. Tensor labels and multiplet dimensions are summarized in https://pdg.lbl.gov/2024/reviews/rpp2024-rev-young-diagrams.pdf[the Particle Data Group's SU(n) multiplet guide].

= Tensor-product Casimir trace identity
{parent=Quadratic Casimir operator}
{title2=$\sum_i d(R_i)C_2(R_i)=d(R_1)d(R_2)[C_2(R_1)+C_2(R_2)]$}

For traceless generators, the <tensor product of Lie algebra representations> has generators $t_a\otimes I+I\otimes s_a$. The <trace> of their summed squares is the sum of the two factor Casimir <traces>; the cross term vanishes because $\operatorname{tr}t_a=\operatorname{tr}s_a=0$. Decomposition into <irreducible representations>, with multiplicities counted, gives the displayed identity. Without tracelessness an extra term $2\sum_a\operatorname{tr}t_a\operatorname{tr}s_a$ is present. A one-dimensional abelian representation of charge one tensored with itself has Casimir four, not the sum two; semisimple <Lie algebras> avoid this problem because every generator is a sum of <commutators>.

= Casimir eigenvalue
{c}
{parent=Casimir element}
{title2=$c_\lambda=(\lambda,\lambda+2\rho)$}

The <Casimir operator> formed from the <Killing form> acts on an <irreducible Lie algebra representation> with <highest weight> $\lambda$ by the scalar $(\lambda,\lambda+2\rho)$, where $\rho$ is the <half-sum of positive roots>. It is zero on the <trivial Lie algebra representation> and nonzero on every nontrivial finite-dimensional irreducible complex representation of a <semisimple Lie algebra>.

= Casimir eigenvalue for sl2
{title2=$\frac12m(m+2)$}
{c}
{parent=Casimir element}

For $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ with $[h,e]=2e$, $[h,f]=-2f$, and $[e,f]=h$, the Casimir
$$
\Omega=ef+fe+\frac12h^2
$$
acts on the irreducible highest-weight module $V(m)$ by the scalar $\frac12m(m+2)$.

= Principal sl2 subalgebra
{title2=$\mathfrak{sl}_2^{\mathrm{pr}}$}
{c}
{parent=Semisimple Lie algebra}

A principal sl2 subalgebra of a semisimple <Lie algebra> has semisimple generator $h_{\mathrm{pr}}=2\rho^\vee$. Restriction to it packages a representation's weights into ordinary $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ weight strings.

The infinitesimal embedding is a subalgebra of the <semisimple Lie algebra>; integrating it to a Lie-group homomorphism is a separate group-level construction.

= Construction of a principal sl2 triple
{parent=Principal sl2 subalgebra}
{title2=$e=\sum_i e_i,\quad h=2\rho^\vee,\quad f=\sum_i c_i f_i$}

Choose <simple-root> vectors with $[e_i,f_i]=h_i=\alpha_i^\vee$, and let $\rho^\vee$ be the half-sum of positive <coroots>. It satisfies $\alpha_i(\rho^\vee)=1$. Expand $h=2\rho^\vee=\sum_i c_i h_i$. For $i\ne j$, $[e_i,f_j]=0$, since the difference of distinct simple roots is not a <root of a root system>. Thus $[e,f]=h$, while $[h,e]=2e$ and $[h,f]=-2f$. These identities construct an embedded <sl2 Lie algebra> in any nonzero complex <semisimple Lie algebra>. Its raising vector is the <principal nilpotent element> with a nonzero component in each simple-root space.

= Principal nilpotent element
{parent=Principal sl2 subalgebra}
{wiki}

A principal nilpotent element of a semisimple <Lie algebra> is a nilpotent element whose <centralizer> has dimension equal to the rank. For nonzero simple-root vectors $e_i$, the sum $e=\sum_i e_i$ is principal nilpotent and forms the raising element of a <principal sl2 subalgebra>.

= Weight
{synonym}

A weight of a representation is a simultaneous eigenvalue functional for a commuting Cartan subalgebra. In general algebra representations it can be expressed as a one-dimensional character of the acting commutative algebra. A <weight vector> is an eigenvector carrying that functional; a <weight space> is the corresponding eigenspace, and <weight multiplicity> is its dimension.
