The <characteristic of a field>[characteristic] is the least positive integer $r$ with $r1_K=0$, if it exists, and zero otherwise. The <prime> subfield is the smallest subfield containing $1_K$; in <characteristic of a field>[characteristic] $p$ it is $F=\{0,1,\ldots,(p-1)1_K\}\cong\mathbb F_p$. Every <field automorphism> fixes $1_K$ and addition, so it fixes every element of this <prime> subfield.

= Solution
{parent=2009/a/a2/a}

The additive sequence $0,1,2\cdot1,\ldots$ must repeat because $K$ is finite, so the <characteristic of a field>[characteristic] is positive. If its least positive value were composite, $r=ab$ with $1<a,b<r$, then $(a1_K)(b1_K)=r1_K=0$ while neither factor would be zero. A <field> has no zero divisors, giving a contradiction. Hence $\boxed{\operatorname{char}K=p\text{ is prime}}$.

The <finite field> $K$ is a finite-dimensional <vector space> over its <prime> subfield $F$ of size $p$. A <basis> has some positive length $n$, and every element is uniquely specified by $n$ coefficients in $F$. There are $p$ choices per coefficient, so $\boxed{|K|=p^n}$ and $[K:F]=n$.

Since $K=F(\lambda)$ and the extension is finite, the element $\lambda$ is algebraic. Its <minimal polynomial> is <irreducible polynomial>[irreducible], and evaluation gives an isomorphism $F[X]/(m_\lambda)\cong F[\lambda]=F(\lambda)=K$. If its <polynomial degree>[degree] is $d$, the powers $1,\lambda,\ldots,\lambda^{d-1}$ form a <basis>. Comparing with $[K:F]=n$ gives $\boxed{\deg m_\lambda=n}$.

Define $\vartheta(x)=x^p$ on all of $K$. The binomial coefficients between the endpoints are divisible by $p$, so $(x+y)^p=x^p+y^p$; also $(xy)^p=x^py^p$ and $1^p=1$. Thus this is a <field> homomorphism. Its kernel is zero, and an <injective> self-map of the finite set $K$ is <surjective>. Hence $\boxed{\vartheta\text{ is an automorphism}}$, the <Frobenius automorphism>, with the required image of $\lambda$. It fixes $F$ by the preceding <prime>-subfield argument.

A left action is a map $(g,\omega)\mapsto g\omega$ satisfying $1\omega=\omega$ and $(gh)\omega=g(h\omega)$. The <orbit> of $\omega$ is $G\omega=\{g\omega:g\in G\}$. The action is <transitive> when there is exactly one <orbit> on the nonempty acted-on set, equivalently when any point can be moved to any other by a <group> element. An action is equivalently a homomorphism from $G$ to the permutation <group> of the set.

= Solution
{parent=2009/b/b2/ii}

Act by left multiplication on the five <cosets> of $H$. The kernel is the core $\bigcap_{g\in G}gHg^{-1}$, a <normal subgroup> contained in $H$, so the hypothesis makes it trivial. Thus $G$ embeds in $S_5$, and its image has order $60$ and index two. An index-two <subgroup> is normal, so the quotient is of order two. Every three-cycle maps trivially to that quotient, since its order divides three. Three-cycles generate $A_5$, so the image contains $A_5$; their equal order gives $\boxed{G\cong A_5}$.

For <completeness>, prove simplicity rather than merely invoking it. The nonidentity even cycle types in $S_5$ are three-cycles, products of two disjoint transpositions and five-cycles. In $A_5$, a three-cycle has <centralizer> of order three, so its <conjugacy class> has size $20$. A double transposition has <centralizer> of order four in $A_5$, giving class size $15$. A five-cycle has <centralizer> its cyclic <subgroup> of order five, giving class size $12$; the $24$ such elements therefore split into two classes of size $12$. These account for all $60$ elements with the <identity element>[identity].

A <normal subgroup> is a union of whole <conjugacy classes>, containing the <identity element>[identity], and its order must divide $60$. No nonempty proper sum of the class sizes $15,20,12,12$, plus one, is a divisor of $60$ greater than one: the possible proper nontrivial sums are $13,16,21,25,28,33,36,40,45,48$. Only the full union has order $60$. Hence $\boxed{A_5\text{ is simple}}$, and so is $G$.

Choose one regular <tetrahedron> inscribed in the <dodecahedron> and let $H$ be its stabilizer among <rotations> of the <dodecahedron>. The twelve <rotations> of that <tetrahedron> preserve the <dodecahedron>; geometrically these are its <identity element>[identity], eight <rotations> by $\pm2\pi/3$ about tetrahedral <vertex> axes and three half-turns about opposite tetrahedral <edge> axes. They act faithfully on its four <vertices> and give the twelve even permutations. Thus $\boxed{H\cong A_4,\quad|H|=12}$ without using part (ii).

To check the core condition geometrically, the fifteen half-turns of the <dodecahedron>, about opposite-<edge>-midpoint axes, are conjugate under its <rotations>. A <normal subgroup> contained in $H$ cannot contain even one of these half-turns, because normality would then force all fifteen into a <group> of order twelve. On the other hand every nontrivial <normal subgroup> of $A_4$ contains a half-turn: if it contains a three-cycle, its conjugates and their inverses generate $A_4$; otherwise its nonidentity elements are among the three double transpositions, which are conjugate in $A_4$. Apply this observation to any <normal subgroup> of the full <rotation> <group> lying inside $H$. It must be trivial, proving exactly the required condition.

Suppose there are only finitely many <primes> $p_1,\ldots,p_r\equiv3\pmod8$, and let $N$ be their product. This is odd, so $N^2+2\equiv3\pmod8$. No listed <prime> divides this number, since reduction modulo any $p_i$ gives two. Every <prime> divisor $q$ is odd and does not divide $N$, and satisfies $N^2\equiv-2\pmod q$. Thus $(-2/q)=1$, forcing $q\equiv1$ or $3\pmod8$ by (v).

A product of <prime> factors all congruent to one modulo eight could not be three modulo eight. More precisely, the product of factors congruent to three is three modulo eight exactly when their total exponent is odd. Thus at least one <prime> divisor $q\equiv3\pmod8$ occurs. It is not in the alleged <complete> list, a contradiction. Hence **there are infinitely many <primes> congruent to three modulo eight**.
