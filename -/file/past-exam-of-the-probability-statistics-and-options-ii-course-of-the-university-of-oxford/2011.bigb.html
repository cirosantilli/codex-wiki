A <splitting field> of $f$ over $F$ is an extension field in which $f$ factors into linear factors and which is generated over $F$ by those roots. The generation requirement excludes unnecessary extra elements.

For existence, induct on the degree. An irreducible factor $p$ of $f$ gives a field $F_1=F[X]/(p)$, since an <irreducible polynomial> generates a maximal ideal in the <polynomial> ring over a field. The class $\alpha$ of $X$ is a root of $p$ and hence of $f$. By the <factor theorem>, $f=(X-\alpha)g$ over $F_1$, with $\deg g=\deg f-1$. Induction gives a <splitting field> of $g$ over $F_1$. It contains all roots of $f$; taking the subfield generated by these roots over $F$ gives exactly a <splitting field> of $f$. The degree-one case is immediate. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{Every positive-degree polynomial over a field has a splitting field}.}
$$

Over $\mathbb F_2$, the cubic takes value one at both zero and one, so it has no root. A reducible cubic over a field has a linear factor and therefore a root; hence this cubic is irreducible. Adjoin a root:
$$
K=\mathbb F_2[\alpha],\qquad\alpha^3+\alpha+1=0.
$$
This extension has <basis> $1,\alpha,\alpha^2$, degree three and eight elements. Squaring preserves <polynomial> equations with coefficients in $\mathbb F_2$, so $\alpha^2$ and $\alpha^4$ are roots too. The nonzero element $\alpha$ is not one; the multiplicative group has prime order seven, so $\alpha$ has order seven. The exponents $1,2,4$ are distinct modulo seven, making these three roots distinct. Thus they are all the roots and already lie in $K$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{The splitting field is }\mathbb F_8=\mathbb F_2[X]/(X^3+X+1),\quad[K:\mathbb F_2]=3.}
$$

A <composition series> is a finite chain $1=G_0\lhd G_1\lhd\cdots\lhd G_r=G$ in which each inclusion is proper, each <subgroup> is normal in the next, and every quotient $G_i/G_{i-1}$ is a nontrivial <simple group>. These quotients are the <composition factors>.

For a finite group, use induction on its order. The trivial group has the empty series. Otherwise choose a maximal proper <normal subgroup> $N$; such a <subgroup> exists because there are finitely many subgroups and $1$ is proper. The correspondence theorem says normal subgroups of $G/N$ correspond to normal subgroups of $G$ containing $N$, so maximality makes $G/N$ simple. Inductively construct a <composition series> of the smaller group $N$ and append $G$. This proves existence.

A nonabelian example with abelian factors is
$$
\boxed{1\lhd A_3\lhd S_3,\qquad\text{factors }C_3,C_2.}
$$
Both factors are simple because their orders are prime.

The <cyclic group> of order six has two different <composition series>:
$$
\boxed{1\lhd C_2\lhd C_6,\qquad1\lhd C_3\lhd C_6.}
$$
Their factor lists are $C_2,C_3$ and $C_3,C_2$ respectively; normality follows from commutativity.

The <Jordan–Hölder theorem> states that any two <composition series> of a group have the same length and the same multiset of composition factors up to isomorphism. Equivalently, the factors of one series can be reordered to match those of the other by group isomorphisms. The actual intermediate subgroups need not be the same.

Let $C_n=\langle g\rangle$ and take $H=\langle g^p\rangle$. Since $n=mp$, the order of $g^p$ is $m$, so $H\cong C_m$. All subgroups of a <cyclic group> are normal. The quotient is generated by $gH$ and has order $n/m=p$, giving
$$
\boxed{C_n/H\cong C_p.}
$$
A group of prime order has no nontrivial proper <subgroup>. By the <subgroup> correspondence theorem, no <normal subgroup> lies strictly between $H$ and $C_n$. Thus $H$ is a maximal proper <normal subgroup>.

Given a prime factorization $n=p_1\cdots p_r$, repeatedly apply part (c) to the <cyclic group> of order $n$: it has a chain of subgroups of successive orders
$$
n,\quad n/p_1,\quad n/(p_1p_2),\quad\ldots,\quad1.
$$
Read in increasing order, this is a <composition series> with factors isomorphic to the cyclic groups of orders $p_1,\ldots,p_r$. Any second prime factorization of the same integer produces another <composition series> of the same group $C_n$.

The <Jordan–Hölder theorem> equates the two multisets of simple factors. Since $C_p\cong C_q$ for primes $p,q$ implies $p=q$ by equality of group orders, the two prime multisets agree. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{The prime factorization is unique up to permutation}.}
$$
For $n=1$ both factor lists are empty, corresponding to the trivial group.

A square residue modulo $p$ is a congruence class represented by $x^2$ for some integer $x$. For the final all-primes solvability statement, zero is included as a square. The <Legendre symbol> distinguishes the zero class from the nonzero square and nonsquare classes:
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac ap\right)=\begin{cases}0,&p\mid a,\\1,&p\nmid a\text{ and }a\equiv x^2\pmod p\text{ for some }x,\\-1,&a\text{ is not a square modulo }p.\end{cases}}
$$
When the term <quadratic residue> is reserved for nonzero classes, the zero case must be stated separately, as in this definition.

An integer square is a square modulo every prime, with the zero class allowed at primes dividing the integer. Conversely, suppose an odd integer $a$ is not an integer square. If every prime exponent in $|a|$ is even, the nonsquare condition forces $a=-b^2$, handled by part (i). Otherwise some odd prime has an odd exponent, so $a=\pm q^tb$ as in part (ii). In either case there is a positive odd $k$ with $(a/k)=-1$.

Factor $k$ into odd primes. A product of Legendre symbols equalling minus one has at least one factor equal to minus one. Thus $a$ fails to be a square modulo one of those primes, contradicting the hypothesis. Hence
$$
\boxed{a\in\mathbb Z\text{ odd is an integer square iff }x^2\equiv a\pmod p\text{ is solvable for every prime }p.}
$$
The solvability formulation makes explicit the zero-residue convention needed by the printed final assertion. The prime two causes no extra issue because every odd integer is one modulo two.

A <connected space> has no separation into two disjoint nonempty open subsets whose union is the space. A continuous surjection to the discrete space $\{0,1\}$ produces such a separation by the two point preimages. Conversely, from a separation define a function equal to zero on one open piece and one on the other; it is continuous and surjective. Thus these formulations of <connectedness> are equivalent.

An interval in $\mathbb R$ is connected: if a continuous map from it to $\{0,1\}$ took both values, the intermediate value theorem would force value $1/2$, impossible. Conversely, a subset of $\mathbb R$ that is not an interval has points $a<b$ in it and a point $c\in(a,b)$ outside it. Intersecting it with $(-\infty,c)$ and $(c,\infty)$ separates it. The empty set is connected, and singleton intervals are included.

For the union assertion, any separation of $X$ must put each nonempty connected $A_i$ entirely on one side, since intersecting the separation with $A_i$ would otherwise disconnect it. Pairwise nonempty intersections force all the $A_i$ onto the same side. Their union is $X$, contradicting a nonempty second side. The empty-union case is already connected.

The plane is the union of all lines through the origin. Each line is homeomorphic to $\mathbb R$ and connected, and every pair meets at the origin, so $\mathbb R^2$ is connected. For the punctured plane, let $R_+$ be the positive horizontal ray, and for each $r>0$ let $C_r$ be the circle of radius $r$. Each circle is a continuous image of $[0,2\pi]$ and hence connected; $C_r\cup R_+$ is connected because its two connected pieces meet at $(r,0)$. These unions all contain $R_+$ and cover the punctured plane, proving it connected as well.

Finally suppose a continuous injection $g:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R$ existed. Its image is a connected interval, since a continuous image of a <connected space> is connected. It contains at least three distinct values; choose $p$ whose value lies strictly between two others. The punctured plane $\mathbb R^2\setminus\{p\}$ is connected by translation of the preceding result. By injectivity its image is precisely $g(\mathbb R^2)\setminus\{g(p)\}$, which is disconnected by the missing interior point. This contradicts <connectedness> of a continuous image. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{There is no continuous injection }\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R.}
$$

A <sequentially compact space> is one in which every sequence has a subsequence converging to a point of the space. A <limit point> $\ell$ of $S$ means every open ball around $\ell$ meets $S\setminus\{\ell\}$.

In a compact <metric space>, a sequence with a finite set of values has a constant subsequence. If its value set is infinite and had no <limit point>, each point of the space would have a neighbourhood containing at most one point of that value set. A finite subcover would then make the value set finite, a contradiction. It therefore has a <limit point> in the space, and the permitted subsequence result gives a convergent subsequence. This proves sequential compactness.

For the remaining assertions assume the <compact space> is nonempty, as required for a fixed-point claim. The displacement function $q(x)=d(f(x),x)$ is continuous by the stated continuity of the metric and of $f$. The <extreme value theorem> gives a minimum $\delta=q(p)$. If $\delta>0$, then $p\neq f(p)$, and strict decrease of distances implies
$$
q(f(p))=d(f^2(p),f(p))<d(f(p),p)=\delta,
$$
contradicting minimality. Thus $\delta=0$ and $p$ is a <fixed point>. Two distinct fixed points would satisfy $d(p,q)=d(f(p),f(q))<d(p,q)$, also impossible. Hence the <fixed point> is unique.

On the noncompact real line, take
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\log(1+e^x).}
$$
Its derivative $e^x/(1+e^x)$ is strictly between zero and one. The <mean value theorem> therefore gives $|f(x)-f(y)|<|x-y|$ for each distinct pair. Yet $f(x)-x=\log(1+e^{-x})>0$, so it has no <fixed point>. Compactness, rather than merely pointwise strict shrinkage, supplies attainment of a zero displacement in the preceding proof.

The $j$-th <partial derivative> is the <vector> limit
$$
\partial_jf(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+he_j)-f(x)}h,
$$
when it exists. The <total derivative> is a <linear map> $Df(x):\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$ such that
$$
f(x+h)=f(x)+Df(x)h+o(\|h\|).
$$
The <Jacobian matrix> is $J_f(x)=(\partial_jf_i(x))_{i,j}$. When the <total derivative> exists, all partial derivatives exist and $J_f(x)$ is its <matrix> in the standard bases; its $j$-th column is $Df(x)e_j$. Existence of partial derivatives alone does not guarantee total differentiability.

Removing it gives a <Hamiltonian path> in $H$, labelled $u=v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n=v$.

If some $i$ has both edges $uv_{i+1}$ and $vv_i$, the path can be closed into the cycle
$$

row.match <- function(X, y) {
  stopifnot(is.matrix(X), length(y) == ncol(X))
  for (i in seq_len(nrow(X))) {
    eq <- X[i, ] == y
    if (all(!is.na(eq) & eq)) return(TRUE)
  }
  FALSE
}

The recursive worker extends a current path by one uniformly selected unused neighbour. A trapped extension returns `NULL` all the way to the outer loop, which restarts from the origin. It does not backtrack and try a different branch of the same attempt, since the stated process restarts the entire walk.

```
saw <- function(N) {
  stopifnot(length(N) == 1L, is.finite(N), N >= 0,
            N == floor(N))
  steps <- rbind(c(1L, 0L), c(-1L, 0L),
                 c(0L, 1L), c(0L, -1L))
  grow <- function(path) {
    if (nrow(path) == N + 1L) return(path)
    last <- path[nrow(path), ]
    candidates <- sweep(steps, 2L, last, "+")
    keep <- vapply(seq_len(nrow(candidates)), function(i) {
      !row.match(path, candidates[i, ])
    }, logical(1))
    candidates <- candidates[keep, , drop = FALSE]
    if (nrow(candidates) == 0L) return(NULL)
    j <- sample.int(nrow(candidates), 1L)
    grow(rbind(path, candidates[j, ]))
  }
  repeat {
    path <- grow(matrix(c(0L, 0L), nrow = 1L))
    if (!is.null(path)) return(path)
  }
}
```

The base case has exactly $N+1$ visited vertices. Every extension is a unit horizontal or vertical step to a new <vertex>, proving the output dimensions and self-avoidance by induction. A straight path has positive <probability>, so each independent attempt has a positive chance of success and repeated attempts terminate almost surely.

= Solution

**The scheme is not uniform in general.** A particular successful path has <probability> equal to the product of the reciprocals of its available-neighbour counts. Those counts depend on the path geometry.

For length four, the straight path $(0,0),(1,0),(2,0),(3,0),(4,0)$ has <probability> $1/(4\cdot3\cdot3\cdot3)=1/108$. The path $(0,0),(1,0),(1,1),(0,1),(-1,1)$ has only two available neighbours at its last choice, because the previous point and the origin block two directions. Its <probability> is $1/(4\cdot3\cdot3\cdot2)=1/72$. There is no trapping before four steps, so these are already the output probabilities. They differ, disproving uniformity on $S_4$.

At lengths where restarts occur, conditioning an attempt on success normalizes these path probabilities by a common success <probability>; it does not remove the unequal products.
