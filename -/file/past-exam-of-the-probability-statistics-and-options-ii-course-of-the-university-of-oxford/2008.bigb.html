The <characteristic of a field>[characteristic] is the smallest positive integer $p$ with $p1_F=0$. Such an integer exists because the additive <group> is finite. If it factored as $p=ab$ with $1<a,b<p$, then $(a1_F)(b1_F)=0$ with both factors nonzero, contradicting the absence of zero divisors. Hence it is <prime>. The elements generated by $1_F$ form a subfield isomorphic to $\mathbb F_p$. The finite set $F$ is a <finite-dimensional vector space> of some <dimension> $a\ge1$ over that subfield, so $\boxed{|F|=p^a}$.

A nonzero <polynomial> of <polynomial degree> $n$ over a <field> has at most $n$ distinct roots. This follows by <induction>: a root $r$ gives a factor $X-r$, and every different root must be a root of the remaining factor by cancellation. Thus $X^n-1$ has at most $n$ roots. In the finite multiplicative <group>, the elements whose order divides $n$ are exactly those satisfying $x^n=1$. The supplied cyclicity criterion therefore proves that **the multiplicative <group> is cyclic**.

For the <prime> of the specified form write $m=2^r s$ with $s$ odd. If $s>1$, then $2^{2^r}+1$ is a proper factor of $(2^{2^r})^s+1$, impossible. Thus $m$ is a <power of two>. Since $2^m\equiv-1\pmod p$, the order of two divides $2m$ but does not divide $m$. All divisors of the <power of two> $2m$ other than $2m$ itself divide $m$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ord}_p(2)=2m.}
$$
It generates the multiplicative <group> precisely when $2m=p-1=2^m$. For positive integers $m$ this holds exactly at $m=1,2$: from $m=3$ onward $2^m>2m$, and the inequality persists on increasing $m$. These give $\boxed{p=3\text{ or }5}$, equivalently the stated range among Fermat <primes>.

The $d$ distinct powers $1,a,\ldots,a^{d-1}$ all satisfy $X^d=1$. The supplied root bound says they exhaust those roots. Thus every element of order $d$ has the form $a^n$. If $g=\gcd(n,d)$, the least positive $k$ with $a^{nk}=1$ is $d/g$, so
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ord}_p(a^n)=\frac d{\gcd(n,d)}.}
$$
This proves both directions of the first assertion.

For existence of a <primitive root> one may apply the finite-<field> cyclicity criterion proved in the preceding section. There is also a direct counting proof: for each divisor $d$ of $p-1$, either no elements have order $d$, or the preceding argument shows there are exactly $\phi(d)$. <Lagrange's theorem for finite groups>[Lagrange's theorem] partitions all $p-1$ nonzero <residue> classes by such orders, while $\sum_{d\mid p-1}\phi(d)=p-1$. Every count is at most its corresponding $\phi(d)$, so equality of the totals forces equality at every divisor. In particular there are $\boxed{\phi(p-1)}$ <primitive roots>.

Put $t=g^{(p-1)/8}$. It has order eight, so $t^4=-1$ and $t^8=1$. The specified element is $k=t+t^7=t+t^{-1}$. Since $t^{-2}=-t^2$, squaring gives $\boxed{k^2=t^2+2+t^{-2}=2\pmod p}$.

Assume the regular Sturm–Liouville setting: real coefficients with $p>0$, $w>0$ and sufficient <continuity> and <differentiability> for the integrations below. Apply a multiplier to $J[y]-\lambda(\int_a^bwy^2-1)$. Its <first variation> in the direction $\eta$ is
$$
2[p y'\eta]_a^b-2\int_a^b\big((p y')'+qy+\lambda wy\big)\eta\,dx.
$$
Variations vanishing at both endpoints and the supplied fundamental lemma give
$$
\boxed{-(p y')'-qy=\lambda wy.}
$$
At a free endpoint the value of $\eta$ is arbitrary, so the natural condition is $p y'=0$ there. At a fixed endpoint $\eta=0$, so no such <derivative> condition is imposed; the prescribed endpoint value is imposed instead. This distinction also allows one endpoint of each type.

For <eigenfunctions> $u,v$ of distinct <eigenvalues> $\lambda,\mu$ under the same homogeneous self-adjoint endpoint conditions, multiply their equations by $v,u$ and subtract. Integration gives
$$
(\lambda-\mu)\int_a^b wuv\,dx=[p(uv'-u'v)]_a^b=0.
$$
The boundary expression vanishes for both natural conditions and homogeneous fixed-value conditions. Thus $\boxed{\int_a^b wuv=0}$ for different <eigenvalues>.

In the specified fixed-endpoint case the eigenproblem is $-y''-y=\lambda y$, $y(0)=y(1)=0$. There are no nonzero solutions when $\lambda+1\le0$; the elementary exponential or affine solutions and the two endpoint conditions prove this. For $\lambda+1>0$, the first condition <leaves of a graph>[leaves] a sine and the second requires frequency $n\pi$. The normalized stationary functions and <eigenvalues> are
$$
\boxed{y_n(x)=\pm\sqrt2\sin(n\pi x),\qquad\lambda_n=n^2\pi^2-1,\quad n\ge1.}
$$
Distinct sine modes are <orthogonal> by the <product-to-sum formula>, or by the preceding <Green second identity>. Although they need not satisfy the natural <derivative> conditions, their fixed zero values also annihilate the boundary expression, so <orthogonality> is expected.

The <Wirtinger inequality> $\int_0^1y'^2\ge\pi^2\int_0^1y^2$ gives $J[y]\ge\pi^2-1$. For completeness it follows by writing $y=v\sin\pi x$ for a smooth function supported away from the endpoints and integrating the cross term: $\int(y'^2-\pi^2y^2)=\int\sin^2(\pi x)v'^2\ge0$. Approximation extends it to the Dirichlet Sobolev class. Equality is attained by the first sine mode; the eigenproblem or equality condition gives only its scalar multiples. Hence the two normalized minimizers are $\boxed{y=\pm\sqrt2\sin\pi x}$ and the <minimum> is $\boxed{\pi^2-1=\lambda_1}$.

There is a wording distinction in the source: the two sign choices of the <minimum> are not <orthogonal> to each other, since their <inner product> is $-1$. <Orthogonality> applies to the stationary modes with different <eigenvalues>, not to both signs of the same minimizer.

= Solution
{parent=2008/r/2/i/b}

For a closed <Eulerian trail>, every visit to a <vertex> pairs one entering <edge> with one departing <edge>, including the initial/final <vertex>. Thus every <degree of a vertex>[degree] is even.

Conversely, in a finite <connected graph> with all degrees even, start at a <vertex> and follow unused <edges> until no extension is possible. The walk cannot stop away from its starting point: at a different endpoint an odd number of its incident <edges> would have been used, leaving at least one unused <edge> because its total <degree of a vertex>[degree] is even. Thus it closes. If any <edge> remains unused, <connectedness> gives a <vertex> of the current closed trail incident with an unused <edge>. Repeat the construction from that <vertex> in the unused-<edge> subgraph and splice the resulting closed trail into the first one. Finitely many <edges> make the procedure terminate in a closed trail using every <edge> exactly once.

Hence **a <connected> finite <graph> has an Eulerian circuit exactly when all degrees are even**. If “trail” is allowed to be open, the printed equivalence needs correction: a <connected graph> has an <Eulerian trail> when it has zero or two odd-<degree of a vertex>[degree] <vertices>. A single <edge> is an immediate counterexample to the unqualified printed statement.

= Solution
{parent=2008/s/2/a}

Assume the fixed margins are compatible, so the finite state space is nonempty. At state $X$, draw an index table $Y$ from the stipulated uniform proposal law. If $X+Y$ has any negative entry, reject and stay at $X$; otherwise accept and set the next state to $X+Y$. The margins stay fixed because the proposal has zero row and column sums.

The proposal set is invariant under $Y\mapsto-Y$. Hence proposals between two feasible distinct states are symmetric. The target density is constant, so the Metropolis–Hastings acceptance probability for a feasible proposal is one. The explicit rejection of infeasible proposals is essential: fixed margins by themselves do not guarantee nonnegative entries.

{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2008/s/2/b}

Let $L$ be the number of index tables, including the zero table. If distinct feasible states $A,B$ differ by an index table, then $P(A,B)=1/L=P(B,A)$, using the sign-reversal <bijection> for the reverse move. If they do not differ by an index table, both probabilities are zero. Since $\pi$ is uniform,
$$
\boxed{\pi(A)P(A,B)=\pi(B)P(B,A).}
$$
For equal states <detailed balance> is tautological. Thus the chain is reversible and its <uniform distribution> is stationary.

Finiteness holds because nonnegative entries with fixed finite row sums are <bounded>. = Solution

A <mixed strategy> for Player II is a probability vector $q\ge0$ with $\sum_jq_j=1$, specifying the random choice of a column. If Player I chooses row $i$, the expected payment is $(Aq)_i$. A mixture of rows cannot exceed the <maximum> of these row expectations, so the smallest payment Player II can guarantee is
$$
\boxed{v=\min_{q\ge0,\ \mathbf1^\mathsf Tq=1}\max_i(Aq)_i.}
$$
An optimal strategy is a vector attaining that <minimum>, which exists by <compactness> of the <probability simplex> and <continuity>. The <minimax theorem> identifies this guaranteed loss with Player I's maximal guaranteed gain, the value of the finite <zero-sum game>.

= ii
{parent=2008/t/2/a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2008/t/2/a/ii}

Strictly positive entries imply $v>0$. If $q$ is an optimal strategy, set $x=q/v$. Then $Ax\le\mathbf1$ and $\mathbf1^\mathsf Tx=1/v$. Conversely, a nonzero feasible $x$ with $s=\mathbf1^\mathsf Tx$ gives a strategy $q=x/s$ satisfying $\max_i(Aq)_i\le1/s$. Thus $v\le1/s$, or $s\le1/v$. The optimal objective is therefore $s_*=1/v$, and
$$
\boxed{v=1/s_*,\qquad q_*=x_*/s_*.}
$$
A nonzero feasible point exists by taking a sufficiently small positive multiple of a probability vector, and positivity of $A$ bounds the feasible objective, so the optimum is attained.

For general entries choose a constant $c$ so that $A'=A+c\mathbf1\mathbf1^\mathsf T$ is strictly positive. Every pair of strategies has its payoff increased by exactly $c$, so optimal strategies are unchanged. Solve the positive problem for $A'$, recover $v'=1/s_*'$, and then $\boxed{v=v'-c}$ for the original game.

The dual is
$$
\boxed{D:\ \min_{y\ge0}\mathbf1^\mathsf Ty\quad\text{subject to }A^\mathsf Ty\ge\mathbf1.}

For positive payoffs and an optimal solution $y_*$, <strong duality> gives $\mathbf1^\mathsf Ty_*=1/v$. Normalizing gives Player I's optimal row strategy $p_*=y_*/(\mathbf1^\mathsf Ty_*)$. Its expected payoff against every column is at least $v$. Thus the primal represents Player II's minimization of worst loss and the dual represents Player I's maximization of guaranteed gain.
