Any nonzero <vector> in $\mathbb F_2^3$ extends to a <basis>. Sending one such <basis> to another gives an element of the <general linear group>, so its <group action> on the seven nonzero <vectors> is a <transitive group action>. Counting successive independent columns gives
$$
|G|=(8-1)(8-2)(8-4)=168.
$$
By the <orbit-stabilizer theorem>, the <stabilizer> $H$ of a chosen nonzero <vector> $v$ has order $168/7=24$.

There are three two-dimensional <linear subspaces> containing $v$, corresponding to the three one-dimensional <linear subspaces> of $\mathbb F_2^3/\langle v\rangle$. The <stabilizer> $H$ acts transitively on them: extend a <basis> of either plane beginning with $v$ to a <basis> of the whole <vector space>. Thus the <stabilizer> of a flag $\langle v\rangle<P$ has order $24/3=8$.

If $Q\le G$ has order eight, each <orbit of a group action> of $Q$ on the seven nonzero <vectors> has size a power of two. Since seven is odd, at least one <orbit of a group action> has size one. Thus $Q$ fixes a nonzero <vector> $v$. Its <group action> on the three planes containing $v$ likewise has a fixed plane $P$. Therefore $Q$ is contained in, and by order equals, the <stabilizer> of that flag. The <group action> of $G$ is transitive on these flags, so all such <subgroups> are <conjugate subgroups>.

Distinct flags give distinct <stabilizers>. In a <basis> adapted to a flag, its order-eight <stabilizer> consists of
$$
\begin{pmatrix}1&a&b\\0&1&c\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad a,b,c\in\mathbb F_2.
$$
Its only common fixed nonzero <vector> is $e_1$. Its only invariant plane is $\langle e_1,e_2\rangle$: an invariant plane is the <kernel of a linear map> of a nonzero <linear functional>, and over $\mathbb F_2$ that <linear functional> must be fixed; the displayed <matrices> have only the fixed nonzero <linear functional> $e_3^*$. Hence each <subgroup> specifies its flag uniquely. There are seven choices of $v$ and three choices of $P$, giving
$$
\boxed{21\text{ subgroups of order }8.}
$$

An element $\alpha$ is an <algebraic element> over $F$ if some nonzero <polynomial> in $F[X]$ vanishes at $\alpha$. A <field extension> is an <algebraic extension> if each of its elements is an <algebraic element> over $F$. If $[K:F]=d<\infty$, the $d+1$ <vectors> $1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^d$ are <linearly dependent>, producing such a <polynomial>. Thus every <finite field extension> is an <algebraic extension>.

The converse fails for the <field of algebraic numbers> $\overline{\mathbb Q}$. It is an <algebraic extension> of $\mathbb Q$ by definition, but it contains primitive $p$th <roots of unity> of degree $p-1$ for arbitrarily large primes $p$, by the permitted irreducibility of the <cyclotomic polynomial>. The <tower law> would make every such degree at most $[\overline{\mathbb Q}:\mathbb Q]$ if the latter were finite, a contradiction.

<Wilson's theorem> states that an integer $n>1$ is prime if and only if $(n-1)!\equiv-1\pmod n$. For a prime $p$, every nonzero residue has a <multiplicative inverse>. Pair each residue with its inverse; the only self-inverse ones satisfy $(a-1)(a+1)=0$ in $\mathbb F_p$ and hence are $1,-1$ (the same residue when $p=2$). The resulting product is $-1$. Conversely, if $(n-1)!\equiv-1\pmod n$, no divisor $d$ of $n$ can satisfy $1<d<n$: it would divide both $(n-1)!$ and $n$, so could not divide $(n-1)!+1$. Hence $n$ is prime.

For a composite $n\ge6$ which is not the square of a prime, choose a proper factorization $n=ab$ with $1<a<b<n$ if possible. Both factors occur in $(n-1)!$, proving divisibility by $n$. If the initially chosen factors coincide but their common value is composite, choose a different proper divisor to obtain unequal factors; equivalently, the only composite integer all of whose proper nontrivial divisors coincide is a prime square. For $n=p^2\ge9$, the distinct factors $p$ and $2p$ lie below $p^2$ and their product is divisible by $p^2$. Thus $(n-1)!\equiv0\pmod n$ for every composite $n\ge6$.

For a prime $n$, write $(n-1)!=kn-1$. Then $\sin^2((n-1)!\pi/n)=\sin^2(\pi/n)$, so the summand is one. For a composite $n\ge6$ it is zero. The only remaining composite index is four, whose summand equals $\sin^2(3\pi/2)/\sin^2(\pi/4)=2$. Therefore the sum is the <prime-counting function> plus two whenever $x\ge4$; subtraction of two gives the claimed exact formula.

The <dominated convergence theorem> states: if measurable $f_n$ converge almost everywhere to $f$ and $|f_n|\le g$ almost everywhere for an integrable $g\ge0$, then $f$ is integrable and $\int f_n\to\int f$. In fact $\int|f_n-f|\to0$.

To derive it from the <monotone convergence theorem>, first prove <Fatou's lemma>. For nonnegative measurable $h_n$, the functions $k_N=\inf_{n\ge N}h_n$ increase to $\liminf h_n$ and satisfy $\int k_N\le\inf_{n\ge N}\int h_n$. <Monotone convergence> yields $\int\liminf h_n\le\liminf\int h_n$.

Now $|f|\le g$ almost everywhere, so $f$ is integrable. Apply <Fatou's lemma> to the nonnegative functions $2g-|f_n-f|$, which converge almost everywhere to $2g$:
$$
2\int g\le2\int g-\limsup_n\int|f_n-f|.
$$
Hence $\int|f_n-f|\to0$, and $|\int f_n-\int f|\le\int|f_n-f|$ proves the assertion. Common exceptional null sets may be removed at the beginning since the sequence is countable.

For the canonical <surjection> $p:X\to X/{\sim}$, define a subset $W$ of $X/{\sim}$ to be open exactly when $p^{-1}(W)$ is open in $X$. This is the <quotient topology>. The empty set and the whole quotient have open <inverse images>. Moreover <inverse images> commute with arbitrary unions and finite intersections, so the corresponding axioms of a <topology> follow directly from those on $X$.

The map $p$ need not be an <open map>. For example, on $X=\mathbb R$ identify every point of $[0,1]$ to one point and leave all other classes singletons. The set $U=(-1/2,1/2)$ is open, but
$$
p^{-1}(p(U))=(-1/2,1]
$$
is not open. The definition of the <quotient topology> therefore shows that $p(U)$ is not open. The obstruction is that saturating an <open set> by <equivalence classes> need not preserve openness.

Differentiability at $a$ means that there is a <linear map> $L:V\to V_1$ with
$$
\frac{\|f(a+h)-f(a)-Lh\|_1}{\|h\|}\longrightarrow0\quad(h\to0).
$$
This map is the <total derivative> $Df_a$. In the <Frobenius norm>, $|AB|\le|A||B|$, by the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> applied to <matrix> entries. Expanding with the order of all factors preserved gives
$$
(A+H)^3=A^3+A^2H+AHA+HA^2+AH^2+HAH+H^2A+H^3.
$$
The remainder after the terms linear in $H$ has norm at most $3|A||H|^2+|H|^3=o(|H|)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{Df_A(H)=A^2H+AHA+HA^2.}
$$
For $|C|<1$, the <Neumann series> $I-C+C^2-\cdots$ converges absolutely in the finite-dimensional space of <matrices>. Multiplying its partial sums by $I+C$ and passing to the limit shows that it is $(I+C)^{-1}$. Its remainder after $I-C$ has norm at most $|C|^2/(1-|C|)=o(|C|)$, proving the required expansion.

If $A$ is an <invertible matrix> and $|H|<1/|A^{-1}|$, then $A+H=A(I+A^{-1}H)$ is an <invertible matrix>. Thus the <general linear group> is an open subset of the <matrix> space. The preceding expansion also gives
$$
(A+H)^{-1}=A^{-1}-A^{-1}HA^{-1}+o(|H|).
$$
Apply the <chain rule> to $g(X)=(X^{-1})^3$, using the cubic <total derivative> at $A^{-1}$ and this <total derivative> of inversion:
$$
\boxed{Dg_A(H)=-A^{-3}HA^{-1}-A^{-2}HA^{-2}-A^{-1}HA^{-3}.}
$$
The three terms generally cannot be combined by commuting $A$ and $H$.

For $F(y,y')=y\sqrt{1+y'^2}$, the <Euler-Lagrange equation> is
$$
\sqrt{1+y'^2}-\frac d{dx}\frac{yy'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}=0,

Since $F$ has no explicit $x$ dependence, the <Beltrami identity> gives
$$
F-y'F_{y'}=\frac{y}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}=C>0.
$$
Solving this <first integral>, or substituting into the second-order equation, yields
$$
\boxed{y(x)=C\cosh\!\left(\frac{x-K}{C}\right),\qquad C>0.}
$$
The constants satisfy $y_1=C\cosh((x_1-K)/C)$ and $y_2=C\cosh((x_2-K)/C)$. There need not be a solution for arbitrary endpoint data, and when there is one these equations can have more than one pair of constants. These are the possible stationary <catenoids>, so the <axisymmetric second variation of a catenoid> still matters when deciding whether they minimize <surface area>.

The functional is unbounded above. Let $\ell$ be the positive affine interpolant of the endpoint values and choose a nonnegative, nonzero, continuously differentiable $h$ vanishing at both endpoints. The admissible functions $y_M=\ell+Mh$ satisfy
$$
J[y_M]\ge2\pi\int_{x_1}^{x_2}y_M\,dx\longrightarrow\infty.
$$
This excludes a global maximum, but **does not prove that a stationary curve is a minimum**. The concluding assertion is false without additional conditions.

An explicit counterexample uses $x_1=-2$, $x_2=2$, $y_1=y_2=\cosh2$ and the stationary function $y=\cosh x$. Its <second variation>, for a perturbation $h$ vanishing at the endpoints, is
$$
\frac{\delta^2J[h]}{2\pi}
=\int_{-2}^{2}\bigl(2\tanh x\,hh'+\operatorname{sech}^2x\,h'^2\bigr)dx
=\int_{-2}^{2}\operatorname{sech}^2x\,(h'^2-h^2)\,dx.
$$
Choose $h=1-x^2/4$. Since $\operatorname{sech}^2x\le4e^{-2|x|}$, the positive contribution is at most $\int_{\mathbb R}x^2e^{-2|x|}dx=1/2$. On $[-1,1]$, $h^2\ge9/16$, so the magnitude of the negative contribution is at least $(9/8)\tanh1>9/16>1/2$. Thus $\delta^2J[h]<0$. Small perturbations in this direction preserve positivity and the endpoint data while decreasing <surface area>. Consequently this <catenoid> is not a local minimum. The valid conclusion from part (ii) is a necessary stationary form; minimization requires a separate stability and existence analysis.

In vacuum, the source-free <Maxwell equations> are
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B,\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf B=c^{-2}\partial_t\mathbf E.
$$
Put $\psi=\omega(t-z/c)$ and denote the constant electric amplitude by $E_0$. A corresponding traveling-wave <magnetic field> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B=\frac{E_0}{c}(-\sin\psi,\cos\psi,0).}
$$
Both <divergences> vanish because both the <electric field> and the <magnetic field> depend only on $z,t$ and have zero $z$ component. Direct differentiation gives $\nabla\times\mathbf E=(\omega E_0/c)(\cos\psi,\sin\psi,0)=-\partial_t\mathbf B$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf B=(\omega E_0/c^2)(-\sin\psi,\cos\psi,0)=c^{-2}\partial_t\mathbf E$. A time-independent background <magnetic field> satisfying the homogeneous equations could be added; the formula is the wave component.

Introduce the <magnetic vector potential> and <electric potential> by $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$ and $\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi-\partial_t\mathbf A$. These identities automatically give the divergence equation for $\mathbf B$ and <Faraday's law>. For this wave choose $\phi=0$ and $\mathbf A=(c/\omega)\mathbf B$; the <derivatives> just calculated verify both potential equations.

The electromagnetic <force> is the <Lorentz force>, $Q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)$. On the smooth constraint plane $z=z_0$, the <constraint force> is normal to the plane. Both $\mathbf v$ and $\mathbf B$ are tangential, so $\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ is normal and is balanced by that <constraint force>; it does not affect the tangential motion. Thus
$$
\ddot{\mathbf r}_\parallel=\frac{QE_0}{m}(\cos\psi,\sin\psi).
$$

= Solution

Take a shortest allowed simple <graph path>. If it does not use $k$ as an intermediate <vertex>, its length is at least $u_{ij}^{(k-1)}$. If it does, simplicity makes $k$ occur only once. Splitting there gives an $i$--$k$ path and a $k$--$j$ path whose intermediate <vertices> are in $\{1,\ldots,k-1\}$. Their lengths are at least the respective two minima. In either case,
$$
\boxed{u_{ij}^{(k)}\ge\min\{u_{ij}^{(k-1)},u_{ik}^{(k-1)}+u_{kj}^{(k-1)}\}.}
$$
For identical endpoints use the usual empty path of length zero.

= Solution
{parent=2007/s/3/b}

An old shortest <graph path> is still allowed at stage $k$, so $u_{ij}^{(k)}\le u_{ij}^{(k-1)}$. Concatenating shortest $i$--$k$ and $k$--$j$ paths gives a <walk> of length $u_{ik}^{(k-1)}+u_{kj}^{(k-1)}$, but it may repeat <vertices>. Every repeated section is a closed <walk>. In the absence of negative-length <cycles in a graph>, each such closed <walk> has nonnegative length: decompose it into simple cycles. Removing these sections therefore produces an allowed simple <graph path> of no greater length. This proves the other upper bound. Together with part (a),
$$
\boxed{u_{ij}^{(k)}=\min\{u_{ij}^{(k-1)},u_{ik}^{(k-1)}+u_{kj}^{(k-1)}\}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=2007/s/3/c}

Initialize an $n\times n$ array $d$ with $d_{ii}=0$ and $d_{ij}=a_{ij}$ for $i\ne j$. For $k=1,\ldots,n$, replace every entry in place by
$$
d_{ij}\leftarrow\min\{d_{ij},d_{ik}+d_{kj}\}.
$$
This is the <Floyd-Warshall algorithm>. At the start of stage $k$, the induction hypothesis is $d_{ij}=u_{ij}^{(k-1)}$. During that stage, row $k$ and column $k$ do not change: $d_{kk}=0$ and no negative cycle can lower it. Hence the operands $d_{ik},d_{kj}$ remain the stage-start values even with in-place updates. Part (b) proves that after the stage every entry is $u_{ij}^{(k)}$. Induction gives all shortest path lengths after stage $n$. The algorithm uses $n^2$ stored lengths and $O(n^3)$ arithmetic operations, with only a fixed number of loop counters beyond the array.

For every <perfect matching> $M$, each <vertex> occurs exactly once, so the feasibility inequalities give
$$
\sum_{e\in M}c_e\ge\sum_{\{u,v\}\in M}(x_u+x_v)=\sum_{v\in V}x_v.
$$
If a <perfect matching> $M^*$ uses only tight <edges>, equality holds. It therefore attains a lower bound valid for every <perfect matching>, proving **minimum cost**. In a <bipartite graph> this is the <weak duality> and <complementary slackness> certificate for the <assignment problem>. The <Hungarian algorithm> maintains feasible row and column potentials and seeks a <perfect matching> among tight <edges>.

= Solution

Begin with $u=v=0$ and the printed matching $\{(1,1),(3,2),(5,3)\}$. Grow an alternating <tree> from unmatched row two. Its tight <edge> reaches column two, whose matched row is three. Thus the reached row set is $S=\{2,3\}$ and the reached column set is $T=\{2\}$. Among unreached columns the reduced costs are $(6,5,4,4)$ from row two and $(3,4,2,3)$ from row three, so their minimum is two.

Increase $u_2,u_3$ by two and decrease $v_2$ by two. The new tight <edge> $(3,4)$ reaches an unmatched column. Augment along $2\to2\to3\to4$, replacing $(3,2)$ by $(2,2),(3,4)$. The matching becomes $\{(1,1),(2,2),(3,4),(5,3)\}$.

Now grow from unmatched row four. <Edge> $(4,1)$ reaches matched row one, and <edge> $(1,5)$ reaches unmatched column five. Augment along $4\to1\to1\to5$, replacing $(1,1)$ by $(4,1),(1,5)$. This yields
$$
\boxed{\{(1,5),(2,2),(3,4),(4,1),(5,3)\},\qquad\text{cost }2.}
$$
No further potential update is needed. The final potentials are $u=(0,2,2,0,0)$ and $v=(0,-2,0,0,0)$. Their reduced-cost <matrix> is
$$
(c_{ij}-u_i-v_j)=
\begin{pmatrix}0&2&2&1&0\\4&0&3&2&2\\1&0&2&0&1\\0&5&1&2&1\\3&3&0&0&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Every entry is nonnegative and every chosen entry is zero. The dual sum is $\sum u_i+\sum v_j=2$, so part (a) certifies optimality.

= Solution

Before a potential update, let $S$ be the reached rows and $T$ the reached columns in an alternating forest grown from unmatched rows. Every reached column is matched, or an augmentation would already have occurred, and its matched row is reached. Each <tree> has one more reached row than reached column, so $|S|-|T|>0$.

The update amount is the smallest slack across $S\times T^c$. It is strictly positive because all zero-slack <edges> have already been explored, and the complete <assignment problem> guarantees that the set is nonempty before a <perfect matching> is found. Increasing $u_i$ for $i\in S$ and decreasing $v_j$ for $j\in T$ therefore increases the dual sum by
$$
\boxed{\Delta(|S|-|T|)>0.}
$$
All feasibility inequalities remain valid by the definition of the minimum slack.
