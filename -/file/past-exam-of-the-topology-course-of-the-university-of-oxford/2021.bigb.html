The two conditions for a <basis of a topology> both hold. First, every <real number> $x$ belongs to $[x,x+1)$, so the proposed basic <intervals> cover the <real line>. Second, if
$$
x\in[a,b)\cap[c,d),
$$
then $\max(a,c)\leq x<\min(b,d)$, and
$$
[a,b)\cap[c,d)=[\max(a,c),\min(b,d))
$$
is itself one of the proposed basic <intervals>. Thus every point in an <intersection> of two basic <sets> has a basic <neighbourhood> contained in that <intersection>.

Consequently the arbitrary <unions> of members of $\mathcal B$, including the <empty union>, form a <topology> $\mathcal T$. The <basis of a topology> conditions give closure under finite <intersections>; arbitrary <unions> are built into the definition. **This is the <lower limit topology>, also called the <Sorgenfrey line>.**

Let $U$ be <open> in the <Euclidean topology> on the <real line>, and let $x\in U$. There is an $\varepsilon>0$ with $(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)\subseteq U$. The basic <open set> $[x,x+\varepsilon)$ contains $x$ and is contained in $U$. Taking the <union> of these basic <neighbourhoods> over $x\in U$ shows that $U\in\mathcal T$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathcal T_{\mathrm{usual}}\subseteq\mathcal T.}
$$
In fact this is a strictly <finer topology>: $[0,1)$ is <open> in $\mathcal T$ but not in the <Euclidean topology>, since no usual <open ball> about $0$ is contained in it.

The usual <real line> is <connected>. In contrast,
$$
\mathbb R_{\mathcal T}=(-\infty,0)\sqcup[0,\infty)
$$
is a <separation of a topological space>. Both pieces are nonempty and disjoint; the first is <open> by part (c), and the second is the <open> <union> $\bigcup_{n\geq1}[0,n)$. **$X$ is not <compact>.** In the <subspace topology> on $X=[0,1]$, consider the <open cover>
$$
\mathcal U=\{[0,1-1/n):n\geq2\}\cup\{\{1\}\}.
$$
The <intervals> are basic <open sets> in the ambient <Sorgenfrey line>, and $\{1\}=X\cap[1,2)$ is <open> in $X$. Every $x<1$ belongs to one of those <intervals>, while the <singleton> covers $1$.

A <finite subcover> would have to include $\{1\}$. Among its finitely many other members, let $N$ be the largest index. Their <union> is $[0,1-1/N)$, leaving $1-1/N$ uncovered. Selecting no such member also fails to cover $0$. Hence no <finite subcover> exists.

**$X$ does not have <sequential compactness>.** Take the <sequence>
$$
x_n=1-\frac1n\qquad(n\geq1).

Every <subsequence> has usual <limit of a sequence> $1$. For the <sphere> model of the <real projective plane>, let
$$
S^2=\{u\in\mathbb R^3:\|u\|=1\},\qquad u\sim_S v\Longleftrightarrow v=u\text{ or }v=-u.
$$
Then $\mathbb{RP}^2=S^2/{\sim_S}$ with the <quotient topology>: each <equivalence class> is an <antipodal pair>.

For the <closed disc> model, let
$$
D^2=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb R^2:x^2+y^2\leq1\},\qquad u\sim_D v\Longleftrightarrow u=v\text{ or }(u,v\in\partial D^2\text{ and }v=-u).
$$
Give $D^2/{\sim_D}$ the <quotient topology>. **Only <antipodal pairs> on the <boundary> <circle> are identified in the <closed disc> model; interior points remain distinct.**

Let $q_S:S^2\to S^2/{\sim_S}$ and $q_D:D^2\to D^2/{\sim_D}$ be the <quotient maps>. The <continuous map>
$$
h:D^2\longrightarrow S^2,\qquad h(x,y)=\left(x,y,\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2}\right)
$$
is a <homeomorphism> onto the upper <hemisphere>; its <inverse function> is the <restriction> of the <projection map> $(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,y)$. Every <antipodal pair> on $S^2$ meets that <hemisphere>. Moreover, two points of this <hemisphere> are antipodal only if both lie on the <equator>. Thus
$$
q_S(h(u))=q_S(h(v))\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u\sim_D v.
$$
By the <universal property of the quotient topology>, $q_S\circ h$ induces a <continuous bijection>
$$
H:D^2/{\sim_D}\longrightarrow S^2/{\sim_S},\qquad H(q_D(u))=q_S(h(u)).
$$

To justify <continuity> of the <inverse function>, the source is <compact>, being a <continuous image of a compact space> $D^2$; the <Heine-Borel theorem> gives <compactness> of $D^2$. The target is <Hausdorff>, as follows. For distinct <equivalence classes> $[u]\neq[v]$, put
$$
\delta=\min(\|u-v\|,\|u+v\|)>0.
$$
In $S^2$, take the <union> $U$ of the two relative <open balls> of radius $\delta/3$ about $u,-u$, and similarly take $V$ about $v,-v$. Every distance between their respective centres is at least $\delta$, so the <triangle inequality> gives $U\cap V=\varnothing$. These <open sets> contain entire <antipodal pairs>, hence $q_S^{-1}(q_S(U))=U$ and similarly for $V$. The <quotient topology> therefore makes $q_S(U)$ and $q_S(V)$ disjoint <open> <neighbourhoods> of the two classes.

The <compact-to-Hausdorff continuous bijection theorem> now gives **the required <homeomorphism> $H$**.

The <sphere> $S^2$ is <path connected>. For $u,v\in S^2$ which are not an <antipodal pair>, a <continuous path> is
$$
\gamma(t)=\frac{(1-t)u+tv}{\|(1-t)u+tv\|},\qquad 0\leq t\leq1.
$$
The denominator never vanishes: a zero would make $u$ and $v$ antipodal. If $v=-u$, choose a <unit vector> $w$ perpendicular to $u$ and concatenate the paths from $u$ to $w$ and from $w$ to $v$ of the same form. Thus $S^2$ is <connected>. The <quotient map> $q_S$ is <continuous> and <surjective>, so the <continuous image of a connected space> theorem proves that **$\mathbb{RP}^2$ is <connected>**. In fact composing the paths above with $q_S$ also proves <path connectedness>.

**False**, with “<disjoint union>” understood as a <topological disjoint union>. Take the <rational numbers> $\mathbb Q$ with the usual <subspace topology> from the <real line>.

Every <connected subset> of $\mathbb Q$ has at most one point. Indeed, if it contained $r<s$, choose an <irrational number> $t\in(r,s)$. The two relatively <open sets>
$$
\mathbb Q\cap(-\infty,t),\qquad \mathbb Q\cap(t,\infty)
$$
partition $\mathbb Q$ and separate any <subset> containing both $r$ and $s$. Hence the <connected components> of $\mathbb Q$ are <singletons>.

If a <topological disjoint union> of nonempty <connected spaces> were <homeomorphic> to $\mathbb Q$, the <image> of each summand would be <connected>, hence a <singleton>. Every summand would therefore be a one-point <topological space>. Their <topological disjoint union> is a <discrete space>, since each summand is <open>. But $\mathbb Q$ is not a <discrete space>: for each $r\in\mathbb Q$, the distinct <rational numbers> $r+1/n$ approach $r$, so $r$ is not an <isolated point>. This is a contradiction. The <set> decomposition into <connected components> always exists; giving those pieces the <topological disjoint union> topology need not recover the original <topology>.

Write $Z=\coprod_{n\in\mathbb N}X_n$, where each $X_n$ is a labelled copy of the <topological space> $X$, with its <topological disjoint union> topology. Define the <bijection>
$$
\boxed{F:Z\longrightarrow\mathbb N\times X,\qquad F(x\text{ in }X_n)=(n,x).}
$$
For a <subset> $U\subseteq\mathbb N\times X$, let $U_n=\{x\in X:(n,x)\in U\}$. By definition of the <topological disjoint union>, $F^{-1}(U)$ is <open> in $Z$ exactly when each $U_n$ is <open> in $X$.

The same condition characterizes <open sets> in the <product topology> on $\mathbb N\times X$. If $U$ is <open>, the map $x\mapsto(n,x)$ is <continuous>, so each $U_n$ is <open>. Conversely, if each $U_n$ is <open>, then
$$
U=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb N}(\{n\}\times U_n)
$$
is <open>, since the <discrete topology> makes every $\{n\}$ <open> in $\mathbb N$. Hence $F$ and its <inverse function> are <continuous>, proving the required <homeomorphism>. This also covers the case $X=\varnothing$.

Use positive indices $n\geq1$ and write $p=(0,-1)\in S^1$. Set
$$
\boxed{A=\mathbb N\times\{p\},\qquad f(n,(u,v))=\left(\frac un,\frac{v+1}{n}\right).}
$$
For fixed $n$, this <function> is a <homeomorphism> from the <unit circle> to $C_n$: it scales its <radius> to $1/n$ and translates its centre to $(0,1/n)$. Thus it takes values in the <Hawaiian earring> $X$ and is <surjective> onto $X$. It is <continuous> on each <open> slice $\{n\}\times S^1$; since these slices form an <open cover> of $\mathbb N\times S^1$, $f$ is <continuous> on the whole <product topology>.

Distinct <circles> $C_n,C_m$ meet only at $o=(0,0)$. To see this directly, their equations are
$$
x^2+y^2=\frac{2y}{n},\qquad x^2+y^2=\frac{2y}{m}.
$$
For $m\neq n$, subtraction gives $y=0$, then $x=0$. Each slice contains exactly one <preimage> of $o$, namely $(n,p)$. Therefore the only nonsingleton <fibre> of $f$ is $A$.

Let $q:\mathbb N\times S^1\to(\mathbb N\times S^1)/A$ be the <quotient map> collapsing $A$. The <universal property of the quotient topology> supplies a unique <continuous map> $\bar f$ with $f=\bar f\circ q$. The <fibre> calculation proves <injectivity>, and <surjectivity> follows from that of $f$. Hence **$\bar f$ is the required <continuous bijection>**.

The <unit circle> is <Hausdorff> and <second countable>, since both properties pass to <topological subspaces> of the <Euclidean plane>. To exhibit its local <coordinate charts>, consider the four <open> <semicircles>
$$
U_{y,+}=S^1\cap\{y>0\},\quad U_{y,-}=S^1\cap\{y<0\},\quad U_{x,+}=S^1\cap\{x>0\},\quad U_{x,-}=S^1\cap\{x<0\}.
$$
They cover $S^1$. On $U_{y,\pm}$, the <projection map> $(x,y)\mapsto x$ is a <homeomorphism> onto the <open interval> $(-1,1)$, with <continuous> <inverse function>
$$
t\longmapsto(t,\pm\sqrt{1-t^2}).
$$
On $U_{x,\pm}$, use $(x,y)\mapsto y$, with <inverse function> $t\mapsto(\pm\sqrt{1-t^2},t)$. These <coordinate charts> show that **$S^1$ is a one-dimensional <topological manifold> with empty <manifold boundary>.**

$$
W=X\cap B_\varepsilon(o)

Suppose a <coordinate chart> of some <dimension of a manifold> $n$ existed at $o$. The case $n=0$ would make $o$ an <isolated point>, which it is not. But a <punctured ball> in $\mathbb R$ has exactly two <connected components>, and a <punctured ball> in $\mathbb R^n$ for $n\geq2$ is <path connected>. A <homeomorphism> preserves <connected components>, giving a contradiction in every <dimension of a manifold>. Thus **$X$ is not a <topological manifold>**. The argument uses arbitrarily small <coordinate charts>; the fact that the whole $X\setminus\{o\}$ has two pieces alone would not be a local obstruction.

Every complete <polygon edge> pairing gives a <compact> <connected> <surface> with empty <manifold boundary>. Here is the local reason at the vertices. Replace each <polygon> corner by a small link <interval>. The <polygon edge> pairings identify all endpoints of these link <intervals> in pairs. In a given vertex <equivalence class>, the resulting <graph> is <connected>: the endpoint identifications generating that vertex <equivalence class> also connect its corner <intervals>. Every link <graph vertex> has <degree of a vertex> two, so the <graph> is a <circle>. Its <topological cone> is a <disc>, giving the required local <surface> model. Edge and face <CW cell> interiors have the usual <disc> <neighbourhoods> as well. A finite <triangulation> compatible with the <polygon edge> pairings realizes the result as a finite <simplicial complex>, giving a <Hausdorff>, <second-countable space>.

For every such <surface>, $\chi=V-2\geq-1$. If it is <orientable>, the <classification of compact surfaces> gives $\chi=2-2g$, so $g=0$ or $1$. If it is a <nonorientable surface>, write $k\geq1$ for its <nonorientable genus>; then $\chi=2-k\geq-1$ gives $k\leq3$. The exhaustive list is therefore

* the <sphere> $S^2$;
* the <torus> $T^2$;
* the <real projective plane> $\mathbb{RP}^2$;
* the <Klein bottle> $\mathbb{RP}^2\mathbin\#\mathbb{RP}^2$;
* the <connected sum of surfaces> $\mathbb{RP}^2\mathbin\#\mathbb{RP}^2\mathbin\#\mathbb{RP}^2$, a <nonorientable surface> of <nonorientable genus> three.

To prove that every listed possibility actually occurs, the following six-letter boundary words specify complete pairings of a <hexagon>:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
\text{surface}&\text{boundary word}&V&\chi=V-2\\\hline
S^2&aa^{-1}bb^{-1}cc^{-1}&4&2\\
\mathbb{RP}^2&aabb^{-1}cc^{-1}&3&1\\
T^2&abca^{-1}b^{-1}c^{-1}&2&0\\
\text{Klein bottle}&aba^{-1}bcc^{-1}&2&0\\
\mathbb{RP}^2\#\mathbb{RP}^2\#\mathbb{RP}^2&aabbcc&1&-1
\end{array}
$$
The vertex counts follow by matching starts and ends of equally directed arrows, just as in part (b). The first and third words pair every letter with its inverse, giving <orientable> <surfaces>. Each other word contains a letter twice with the same sign, giving a <nonorientable surface>: an <orientation> on the face would induce equal, rather than opposite, boundary directions along that glued pair. With the displayed <Euler characteristics>, the <classification of compact surfaces> identifies the examples exactly as listed. **All five types occur, and no others occur.**
