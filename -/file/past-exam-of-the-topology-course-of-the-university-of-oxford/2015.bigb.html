== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

A <topological space> is <connected> if it has no <separation>: two disjoint nonempty <open sets> whose union is the whole space. Equivalently, its only <subsets> that are both <open> and <closed> are the <empty set> and the whole space.

A <subset> is <connected> when its <subspace topology> has this property. Thus the sets in a proposed <separation> of a subset need only be relatively <open>, rather than <open> in the ambient space.
$$
\boxed{A\text{ connected}\iff A\text{ has no separation in its subspace topology}.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Suppose $A\cup B=U\cup V$ were a <separation>. By <connectedness>, all of $A$ lies on one side, say $U$: otherwise $A\cap U,A\cap V$ would separate $A$. A point of $A\cap B$ then lies in $B\cap U$, so <connectedness> of $B$ forces all of $B$ into $U$ as well. This makes $V$ empty, a contradiction.

Therefore **the union of two intersecting <connected subsets> is <connected>**. The argument also proves the <connected union with a connected hub> rule for arbitrarily many connected pieces meeting one connected subset.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

If $f(A)=U\cup V$ were a <separation>, then
$$
A\cap f^{-1}(U),\qquad A\cap f^{-1}(V)
$$
would be disjoint nonempty relatively <open sets> covering $A$. They are relatively <open> by <continuity> of the restriction $f|_A:A\to f(A)$. This contradicts <connectedness> of $A$.
$$
\boxed{A\text{ connected},\ f\text{ continuous}\ \Longrightarrow\ f(A)\text{ connected}.}
$$
This is the <continuous image of a connected space> theorem; no separation property of the target is required.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the <basis of a topology> consisting of open rectangles:
$$
\boxed{\mathcal B=\{U\times V:U\in\mathcal T_X,\ V\in\mathcal T_Y\}.}
$$
It covers $X\times Y$, since $X\times Y\in\mathcal B$. Whenever a point lies in two basis rectangles, their <intersection> is another basis rectangle containing it:
$$
(U_1\times V_1)\cap(U_2\times V_2)=(U_1\cap U_2)\times(V_1\cap V_2).
$$
These are precisely the two <basis of a topology> axioms. Arbitrary <unions> of the rectangles therefore form a <topology>, the <product topology>. To check the finite-intersection axiom directly, distribute the intersection of two unions over all pairs of rectangles and use the displayed formula. The empty union gives the <empty set>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

If both factors are <Hausdorff>, two distinct product points differ in at least one coordinate. Disjoint <open neighbourhoods> in that coordinate, multiplied by the other whole factor, give disjoint <open neighbourhoods> of the product points. Thus the product is <Hausdorff>.

Conversely, assume the product is <Hausdorff>. Fix $y_0\in Y$, using nonemptiness. For $x\ne x'$, choose disjoint <open neighbourhoods> of $(x,y_0)$ and $(x',y_0)$ and refine them to basis rectangles $U\times V$ and $U'\times V'$. Since $y_0\in V\cap V'$, their disjointness forces $U\cap U'=\varnothing$. These separate $x,x'$, proving that $X$ is <Hausdorff>. Fixing a point of $X$ proves the same for $Y$.
$$
\boxed{X\times Y\text{ Hausdorff}\iff X,Y\text{ Hausdorff},\quad X,Y\ne\varnothing.}
$$
The nonempty hypothesis is essential: a product with the <empty set> is empty regardless of the other factor. This is the <Hausdorff property of a nonempty product>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Choose $x_0\in X\setminus A$ and $y_0\in Y\setminus B$, possible because the two subsets are proper. The cross
$$
H=(X\times\{y_0\})\cup(\{x_0\}\times Y)
$$
lies in the indicated complement. Its two arms are <homeomorphic> to the <connected> factors and meet at $(x_0,y_0)$, so $H$ is <connected> by part (a)(ii).

For any remaining point $(x,y)$, either $x\notin A$ or $y\notin B$. In the first case the whole <connected subset> $\{x\}\times Y$ lies in the complement and meets $H$ at $(x,y_0)$. In the second case use $X\times\{y\}$, which meets $H$ at $(x_0,y)$. These connected fibres together with $H$ cover the complement. The <connected union with a connected hub> argument therefore gives
$$
\boxed{(X\times Y)\setminus(A\times B)\text{ is connected}.}
$$
Neither proper subset needs to be <open>, <closed>, or <connected>.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

Every <topological subspace> $S\subseteq X$ again has the <cofinite topology>: its nonempty <open subsets> have finite complements in $S$, and every finite exclusion is obtained by intersecting with a cofinite open subset of $X$. As usual, the <empty set> is also open; this exception is implicit in the paper's shorthand definition.

If $S$ is infinite, any two nonempty relatively <open sets> intersect, since their complements are finite. Hence $S$ is <hyperconnected space> and therefore <connected>. If $S$ is finite, its <cofinite topology> is <discrete topology>. It is then <connected> exactly when it has at most one point. Thus
$$
\boxed{S\text{ connected}\iff |S|\leq1\text{ or }S\text{ is infinite}.}
$$
There are also **infinite disconnected subsets of the product**. Choose distinct $a,b\in X$ and put $D=\{a,b\}\times X$. This is infinite. The traces on $D$ of the ambient <open sets>
$$
(X\setminus\{b\})\times X,\qquad (X\setminus\{a\})\times X
$$
are respectively $\{a\}\times X$ and $\{b\}\times X$. They are disjoint, nonempty and cover $D$, giving a <separation>. This answers the infinite-subset question, rather than only producing two isolated product points.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

A <topological space> is <compact> if every <open cover> has a <finite subcover>:
$$
\boxed{X=\bigcup_{i\in I}U_i,\ U_i\text{ open}\ \Longrightarrow\ X=U_{i_1}\cup\cdots\cup U_{i_n}\text{ for finitely many indices}.}
$$
There is no <Hausdorff> requirement in this definition.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $(V_i)$ be an <open cover> of $Y$. By <continuity>, the <inverse images> $f^{-1}(V_i)$ are <open> in $X$; by <surjectivity>, they cover $X$. <Compactness> supplies finitely many covering $X$. Applying $f$ and using $f(X)=Y$ shows that the corresponding finitely many $V_i$ cover $Y$.

Thus **a continuous <surjective function> from a <compact space> has <compact> target**. This is the <continuous image of a compact space> theorem.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For the <canonical projection of a quotient set> $p$, define
$$
\boxed{\mathcal T_q=\{U\subseteq X/\mathcal R:p^{-1}(U)\in\mathcal T\}.}
$$
The <inverse images> of the <empty set> and whole target are $\varnothing$ and $X$. Inverse images commute with arbitrary <unions> and finite <intersections>, so the topology axioms follow from those of $\mathcal T$. This is the <quotient topology>, and it makes $p$ <continuous> by definition.

The crucial criterion is an equivalence: $U$ is open precisely when $p^{-1}(U)$ is open. This is stronger than continuity alone and is what makes the next part work.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

If $g$ is <continuous>, then so is $g\circ p$, as a <composition of continuous functions>. Conversely, suppose $g\circ p$ is continuous. For an <open subset> $W\subseteq Z$,
$$
p^{-1}(g^{-1}(W))=(g\circ p)^{-1}(W)
$$
is open in $X$. The <quotient topology> criterion therefore says $g^{-1}(W)$ is open in $X/\mathcal R$. This proves continuity of $g$.
$$
\boxed{g\text{ continuous}\iff g\circ p\text{ continuous}.}
$$
This is the <universal property of the quotient topology>.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Since $K$ misses $A$, the open set $U=X\setminus K$ contains the whole collapsed <equivalence class> $A$. Every other <equivalence class> is a <singleton>, so $U$ is saturated under the <saturation of a subset> operation:
$$
p^{-1}(p(U))=U.
$$
Its preimage is open, so the <quotient topology> gives
$$
\boxed{p(X\setminus K)\text{ is open in }X_A.}
$$
The hypothesis $K\cap A=\varnothing$ is exactly what prevents the <saturation of a subset> from adding missing points of the collapsed class.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Use $F:[0,1]\to S^1$, $F(t)=e^{2\pi it}$. This is <continuous>, identifies the two endpoints, and is otherwise injective. Its fibres are exactly the <equivalence classes> for <collapsing a subset to a point> with $A=\{0,1\}$. Hence it factors uniquely through a <bijection>
$$
\overline F:X_A\longrightarrow S^1,\qquad\overline F([t])=e^{2\pi it}.
$$
By the <universal property of the quotient topology>, $\overline F$ is <continuous>. The interval is <compact>, so its quotient $X_A$ is compact. The <unit circle> is <Hausdorff>. The <compact-to-Hausdorff continuous bijection theorem> now shows that $\overline F$ is a <homeomorphism>:
$$
\boxed{[0,1]/\{0\sim1\}\cong S^1.}
$$
Indeed the theorem makes the inverse continuous because images of closed subsets of a compact space are compact and hence closed in a Hausdorff target.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Every quotient class has a representative in $[0,1]$: the collapsed class has representatives $0,1$, and the others are the points of $(0,1)$. Thus $p|_{[0,1]}$ is a continuous surjection from a <compact interval> onto $X_A$. It follows that **$X_A$ is <compact>**, despite the noncompact original space.

Define the continuous map
$$
F(x)=\begin{cases}e^{2\pi ix},&0\leq x\leq1,\\1,&x\leq0\text{ or }x\geq1.\end{cases}
$$
The formulas agree at both junctions, and its fibres are exactly the required <equivalence classes>. It induces a continuous bijection $X_A\to S^1$, which is a <homeomorphism> by compactness and the <Hausdorff condition> on the <circle>.
$$
\boxed{\mathbb R/\bigl(({-\infty},0]\cup[1,\infty)\bigr)\cong S^1.}
$$
The quotient notation means that the displayed entire subset becomes one point.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

**Collapsing the integers gives a <Hausdorff space>.** For two distinct noninteger points, choose disjoint small open intervals around them that avoid $\mathbb Z$. Each interval is saturated under the <saturation of a subset> operation, so its image is an open neighbourhood of its class. For a noninteger $x$ and the collapsed class, choose $0<r<\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathbb Z)$. The disjoint open sets
$$
U=(x-r,x+r),\qquad V=\mathbb R\setminus[x-r,x+r]
$$
are saturated; $U$ contains $x$ and $V$ contains all integers. Their images separate the two quotient points. These exhaust all pairs of distinct classes.

**Collapsing the rationals does not give a <Hausdorff space>.** The preimage of the singleton collapsed point is $\mathbb Q$, which is not <closed> in $\mathbb R$. Therefore that quotient singleton is not closed, whereas every singleton of a <Hausdorff space> is closed.
$$
\boxed{X_{\mathbb Z}\text{ is Hausdorff},\qquad X_{\mathbb Q}\text{ is not Hausdorff}.}
$$
Equivalently, every nonempty saturated open subset of $\mathbb R$ meets the <dense set> $\mathbb Q$ and therefore contains it all. Every nonempty quotient open set contains the collapsed point, making separation from any other quotient point impossible.

==== v
{scope}

===== Solution

**No countable subset works.** For any <countable set> $A\subseteq\mathbb R$, choose $b_n\in(n,n+1/2)\setminus A$: each nonempty interval is <uncountable>. The sequence tends to infinity, so every tail
$$
K_n=\{b_k:k\geq n\}
$$
is <closed> in $\mathbb R$ and disjoint from $A$. By part (i), $p(\mathbb R\setminus K_n)$ is open in $X_A$. These sets increase and cover $X_A$, since no point belongs to every tail. But any finite selection is contained in its largest member, which still misses every quotient point $p(b_k)$ in that member's remaining tail. The $b_k$ represent distinct singleton classes.

This is an <open cover> without a <finite subcover>, proving
$$
\boxed{A\text{ countable}\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathbb R_A\text{ is not compact}.}
$$
The argument does not assume that $A$ is closed, bounded, or discrete. It is the <noncompactness after collapsing a countable real subset> obstruction.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <standard simplex> of dimension $n$ is
$$
\boxed{\Delta^n=\{(t_0,\ldots,t_n)\in\mathbb R^{n+1}:t_j\geq0,\ \sum_jt_j=1\}.}
$$
It is the <convex hull> of the coordinate vectors, and its <faces of a simplex> arise by setting selected coordinates to zero.

An <abstract simplicial complex> is a pair $(V,\Sigma)$ where $V$ is the vertex set and $\Sigma$ consists of finite nonempty subsets of $V$, contains every singleton, and contains every nonempty subset of each member. A member with $n+1$ vertices is an $n$-<simplex>.

Its <geometric realization> is obtained by gluing one <standard simplex> for each member along the faces specified by common vertex subsets. Equivalently, its points have <barycentric coordinates> $(t_v)_{v\in V}$ with finite support, nonnegative entries summing to one, and support a simplex of $K$. Use the topology induced by this gluing, equivalently the <weak topology of a simplicial realization>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

List the finite vertex set as $V=\{v_1,\ldots,v_N\}$. Map a point to its <barycentric coordinates>:
$$
\boxed{f:|K|\longrightarrow\mathbb R^N,\qquad f\left(\sum_jt_jv_j\right)=(t_1,\ldots,t_N).}
$$
On each simplex this is an <affine map> and therefore <continuous>. The definitions agree on every common face, so the <quotient topology> defining the realization makes $f$ continuous on all of $|K|$. Barycentric coordinates are unique after zero coordinates are added, so $f$ is <injective>.

Since <Euclidean space> is <Hausdorff>, distinct images have disjoint open neighbourhoods. Their inverse images separate the original points. Hence **$|K|$ is <Hausdorff>**. A continuous injection into a Hausdorff space is enough for this conclusion; no prior Hausdorff assumption on the domain is being used.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

An $n$-dimensional <topological manifold> is a <Hausdorff>, <second countable> topological space in which every point has an open neighbourhood <homeomorphic> to an open subset of $\mathbb R^n$. Equivalently, charts can be chosen onto open balls. This is the without-boundary convention.

A <topological surface> is a two-dimensional topological manifold. When connectedness is included in the convention for a surface, impose it as well; in the classification statement below the surface is explicitly connected. A <closed surface> means a compact surface without boundary, rather than merely a closed subset of another space.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

Here is an explicit <simplicial complex> on six vertices. Take every subset of one of the following ten triangular facets:
$$
\boxed{123,\ 124,\ 135,\ 146,\ 156,\ 236,\ 245,\ 256,\ 345,\ 346.}
$$
For example, $135$ denotes $\{1,3,5\}$, with all its edges and vertices included. Its <one-skeleton> has all fifteen edges of the <complete graph>. Every edge belongs to exactly two triangles. The <links of a simplex> at the six vertices are the following cycles, with the first vertex understood to repeat at the end:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{vertex}&\text{link cycle}\\\hline
1&2,3,5,6,4\\
2&1,3,6,5,4\\
3&1,2,6,4,5\\
4&1,2,5,3,6\\
5&1,3,4,2,6\\
6&1,4,3,2,5
\end{array}
$$
Thus every vertex has a disk neighbourhood, as do edge and face interior points. The realization is a compact connected <surface> without boundary. Its <Euler characteristic> is
$$
\chi=V-E+F=6-15+10=1.
$$
The <classification theorem for surfaces> identifies the unique closed connected surface with this Euler characteristic as the <real projective plane>. Consequently **this complex triangulates $\mathbb{RP}^2$**. The explicit facet and link checks make this a genuine <simplicial complex>, not a polygon whose boundary identifications have been mistaken for simplicial faces.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

A <polygon with a complete set of side-identifications> consists of a closed polygonal disk, a pairing of all its boundary sides, and a specified endpoint-matching homeomorphism for each paired pair. Form the quotient by these side maps, leaving interior points distinct. A convenient encoding labels paired sides by the same letter and gives them arrows; traversal in the arrow direction contributes $a$, and traversal against it contributes $a^{-1}$ to the boundary word. Every letter occurs exactly twice.

The orientations determine whether matched endpoints are identified in the same or reverse boundary-traversal order. This endpoint data is essential: pairing the same geometric sides with different directions can produce nonhomeomorphic surfaces. Subdividing the disk and the paired sides gives a <triangulation> of the resulting <surface>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <classification theorem for surfaces> states that every connected <closed combinatorial surface> is <homeomorphic> to exactly one of:

* the <two-sphere>;
* an <orientable surface> of <orientable genus> $g\geq1$, the <connected sum> of $g$ <tori>;
* a <nonorientable surface> of <crosscap number> $h\geq1$, the <connected sum> of $h$ <real projective planes>.

The orientable family has <Euler characteristic> $2-2g$; the nonorientable family has Euler characteristic $2-h$. Orientability together with Euler characteristic distinguishes the entries.

Boundary words giving the required <polygonal schemas> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
S^2:&\quad aa^{-1},\\
\Sigma_g:&\quad a_1b_1a_1^{-1}b_1^{-1}\cdots a_gb_ga_g^{-1}b_g^{-1},\\
N_h:&\quad a_1a_1a_2a_2\cdots a_ha_h.
\end{aligned}}
$$
A two-sided polygon here means a disk with its boundary split into two arcs; subdivision produces an ordinary polygon with more sides without changing the quotient. Alternatively the square word $aa^{-1}bb^{-1}$ represents the sphere. Each four-letter block in the orientable word adds a torus handle; each repeated-letter block in the nonorientable word adds a projective-plane summand.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

There are **exactly four homeomorphism types**: the <two-sphere>, <real projective plane>, <torus>, and <Klein bottle>.

For completeness, enumerate all pairings. Four sides have three pairings; each pair can be matched in either direction, giving twelve signed pairings before symmetries. Up to cyclic relabelling, the repeated labels are either adjacent, of form $aa^sbb^t$, or alternating, of form $aba^sb^t$, where $s,t\in\{1,-1\}$. The second adjacent pattern $abba$ rotates to the first. Endpoint identification gives the following table:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c}
\text{pattern}&(s,t)&V&\chi=V-1&\text{surface}\\\hline
\text{adjacent}&(-1,-1)&3&2&S^2\\
\text{adjacent}&(1,-1)\text{ or }(-1,1)&2&1&\mathbb{RP}^2\\
\text{adjacent}&(1,1)&1&0&\text{Klein bottle}\\
\text{alternating}&(-1,-1)&1&0&\text{torus}\\
\text{alternating}&(1,-1)\text{ or }(-1,1)&1&0&\text{Klein bottle}\\
\text{alternating}&(1,1)&2&1&\mathbb{RP}^2
\end{array}
$$
Here the quotient always has two edge classes and one face, so its <Euler characteristic> is $V-2+1=V-1$. To reproduce the vertex count, label consecutive square vertices $0,1,2,3$. For paired sides $i,j$, a same-direction pairing identifies $i$ with $j$ and $i+1$ with $j+1$; a reversed-direction pairing identifies $i$ with $j+1$ and $i+1$ with $j$, with indices modulo four. Taking the generated equivalence classes gives exactly the displayed values of $V$.

The quotient is <orientable> precisely when each label is traversed once positively and once negatively around the boundary: then the face orientation extends across every paired edge. A same-direction pair obstructs such an orientation. Thus the two $\chi=0$ possibilities are distinguished as the orientable <torus> and nonorientable <Klein bottle>; the other rows are fixed by <Euler characteristic> and the <classification theorem for surfaces>.

Explicit square representatives are
$$
\boxed{S^2:aa^{-1}bb^{-1},\quad\mathbb{RP}^2:aabb^{-1},\quad T^2:aba^{-1}b^{-1},\quad K:aba^{-1}b.}
$$
All edge interior points have disk neighbourhoods by joining two half-disks, and each identified vertex has a cyclic sequence of corner sectors, again a disk neighbourhood. There are no leftover boundary edges. The enumeration therefore describes closed surfaces, and the twelve signed pairings leave no additional cases.
