== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <closure> $\overline A$ is the <intersection> of every <closed subset> of $X$ containing $A$. Thus it is the smallest such <closed subset>. Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{x\in\overline A\iff\text{every open neighbourhood of }x\text{ meets }A.}
$$
For equivalence, if an <open neighbourhood> misses $A$, its <complement> is a <closed> superset of $A$ missing $x$. Conversely, a <closed> superset missing $x$ has an <open> <complement> that is a neighbourhood of $x$ disjoint from $A$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Suppose $f$ is <continuous> and $x\in\overline A$. Every <open neighbourhood> $V$ of $f(x)$ has an <open> <preimage> containing $x$, which meets $A$. Hence $V$ meets $f(A)$, proving $f(x)\in\overline{f(A)}$.

Conversely, suppose the indicated inclusion holds for every subset. To prove <continuity>, take any <closed set> $C\subseteq Y$ and put $A=f^{-1}(C)$. Since $f(A)\subseteq C$ and $C$ is <closed>,
$$
f(\overline A)\subseteq\overline{f(A)}\subseteq C.
$$
Thus $\overline A\subseteq f^{-1}(C)=A$, so $A$ is <closed>. Every <closed set> has <closed> <preimage>, which is the closed-set characterization of <continuity>.
$$
\boxed{f\text{ continuous}\iff f(\overline A)\subseteq\overline{f(A)}\text{ for every }A\subseteq X.}
$$
This is the <closure characterization of continuity>; it requires no <Hausdorff> or <metric> hypothesis.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

A proper <closed subset> in the <cofinite topology> is finite, so cannot contain the infinite subset $A$. Its only <closed> superset is therefore the whole space:
$$
\boxed{\overline A=X.}
$$
In particular, every infinite subset is <dense> in this <topology>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Because $f$ is <surjective>, $f^{-1}(B)$ is infinite whenever $B$ is infinite; a finite <preimage> could have only finitely many <image> points. By part (i), its <closure> is $X$. Apply part (a)(ii):
$$
Y=f(X)=f\bigl(\overline{f^{-1}(B)}\bigr)
\subseteq\overline{f(f^{-1}(B))}=\overline B.
$$
The other inclusion is automatic, so
$$
\boxed{\overline B=Y\quad\text{for every infinite }B\subseteq Y.}
$$
Both <continuity> and <surjectivity> enter the argument.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Any proper <closed subset> $F$ of $Y$ must be finite: if it were infinite, part (ii) would give $F=\overline F=Y$. Therefore every nonempty <open subset> of $Y$, being the <complement> of a proper <closed set>, is cofinite.
$$
\boxed{\mathcal T_Y\subseteq\mathcal T_{\mathrm{cofinite}}.}
$$
The empty set belongs to both topologies. Inclusion need not be equality; the target may have fewer <open> sets.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

Any two nonempty <open> sets of $Y$ are cofinite by part (iii). Their complements have <finite union>, so the two sets must intersect because $Y$ is infinite. Thus $Y$ is a <hyperconnected space>.

A <Hausdorff space> with at least two points has disjoint nonempty <open> neighbourhoods of two distinct points. This is impossible here. Therefore **$Y$ is not <Hausdorff>**.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <quotient set> $X/\mathcal R$ consists of the <equivalence classes>. Let $p(x)=[x]$. Equip that set with the <quotient topology>
$$
\boxed{U\subseteq X/\mathcal R\text{ open}\iff p^{-1}(U)\text{ open in }X.}
$$
Preimages preserve the empty set, whole set, arbitrary unions and finite intersections, so this is a <topology>. The canonical projection is <continuous> and <surjective>, and the displayed equivalence specifies the <quotient space> completely.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For a nonempty <open subset> $U$ of the quotient, $p^{-1}(U)$ is nonempty and <open> in the cofinite space. Therefore
$$
p^{-1}\bigl((X/\mathcal R)\setminus U\bigr)=X\setminus p^{-1}(U)
$$
is finite. <Surjectivity> implies that $(X/\mathcal R)\setminus U$ is finite as well: it is the <image> of that finite <preimage>. Hence every quotient-open set is cofinite or empty, proving the desired inclusion.

**Equality is not necessary.** Give $X=\mathbb Z$ its <cofinite topology> and use two equivalence classes, the even and odd integers. Neither singleton of the quotient is <open>, since its <preimage> and its <complement> are both infinite. The quotient is the two-point <indiscrete topology>, whereas the <cofinite topology> on a finite two-point set is discrete.
$$
\boxed{\mathcal T_{\mathrm{quotient}}\subsetneq\mathcal T_{\mathrm{cofinite}}\text{ can occur}.}
$$
This is the <quotient topology of a cofinite space> phenomenon, not a failure of <continuity> of the projection.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <product topology> has <basis>
$$
\boxed{\{U\times V:U\subseteq X\text{ open},\ V\subseteq Y\text{ open}\}.}
$$
<Open> subsets of the product are arbitrary unions of these rectangles. The rectangles cover the product and their finite intersections are rectangles, so they satisfy the <basis> axioms. Equivalently, this is the smallest <topology> making both coordinate projections <continuous>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

First assume **both factors are nonempty**. If the product is <compact>, its <continuous> coordinate projections are onto, so each factor is <compact> by the <continuous image of a compact space> theorem.

For the converse, let $X,Y$ be <compact> and let $(W_\lambda)$ be an <open> cover of their product. Fix $x\in X$. For every $y\in Y$, choose a <basis> rectangle $U_{x,y}\times V_{x,y}$ containing $(x,y)$ and lying in a cover member $W_{\lambda(x,y)}$. <Compactness> of $Y$ gives finitely many $V_{x,y_j}$ covering $Y$. Then
$$
U_x=\bigcap_jU_{x,y_j}
$$
is an <open neighbourhood> of $x$, and $U_x\times Y$ is covered by the corresponding finitely many $W_{\lambda(x,y_j)}$.

The sets $U_x$ cover $X$. <Compactness> gives finitely many of them covering $X$, and the finitely many cover members associated to these choices now cover $X\times Y$. Hence the product is <compact>. This is the finite-product <Tychonoff theorem> proof, with the <finite intersection> creating a uniform neighbourhood along each fibre.

As printed, the assertion omits the nonempty hypothesis. If $X=\varnothing$ and $Y=\mathbb R$, the product is <compact> while $Y$ is not. The fully correct version without any nonemptiness assumption is
$$
\boxed{X\times Y\text{ compact}\iff X=\varnothing\text{ or }Y=\varnothing\text{ or both factors are compact}.}
$$

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The interval $[0,1]$ is <compact> by the <Heine-Borel theorem>, so its square is <compact> by part (a). The half-open interval $[0,1)$ is not <compact> because it is not <closed> in the real line.

Each of the other two products has a <continuous> <surjective> coordinate projection onto $[0,1)$. If either product were <compact>, that <image> would be <compact>, a contradiction. <Compactness> is preserved by <homeomorphisms>, so **neither product is homeomorphic to the <closed> square**.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

A <boundary> reparameterization gives an explicit <homeomorphism>. Let $Q=[0,1]^2$, with centre $c=(1/2,1/2)$, and parametrize its <boundary> once by
$$
b(s)=\begin{cases}(s,1),&0\leq s\leq1,\\(1,2-s),&1\leq s\leq2,\\(3-s,0),&2\leq s\leq3,\\(0,s-3),&3\leq s\leq4.\end{cases}
$$
Here $b(0)=b(4)$. The omitted top edge of $[0,1]\times[0,1)$ is $b([0,1])$. The omitted top and right edges of $[0,1)^2$ are $b([0,2])$.

Use the increasing <boundary> parameter map
$$
\phi(s)=\begin{cases}2s,&0\leq s\leq1,\\2+\frac23(s-1),&1\leq s\leq4.\end{cases}
$$
It takes $[0,4]$ bijectively onto itself, respects the identified endpoints, and maps the first omitted arc exactly onto the second. Extend it radially:
$$
\boxed{H(c)=c,\qquad H\bigl(c+r(b(s)-c)\bigr)=c+r\bigl(b(\phi(s))-c\bigr),\quad0<r\leq1.}
$$
Every noncentral point has a unique <boundary> ray and a unique radial parameter $r=2\|q-c\|_\infty$; the only duplicate <boundary> parameters are zero and four, whose images agree. The inverse uses $\phi^{-1}$ by the same construction. Both maps are <continuous> off the centre, and at the centre their radial distances tend to zero. Thus $H$ is a <homeomorphism> of the whole <closed> square.

It carries the first omitted edge precisely onto the second omitted pair of edges, so restriction gives
$$
\boxed{H:[0,1]\times[0,1)\ \cong\ [0,1)\times[0,1).}
$$
This is the <radial extension of a square boundary homeomorphism>; it avoids any need to treat corners as topologically distinguished points.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

An <abstract simplicial complex> $K=(V,\Sigma)$ consists of a <vertex> set $V$ and a collection $\Sigma$ of finite nonempty subsets containing every singleton and every nonempty subset of each member. A member with $n+1$ vertices is an $n$-simplex.

Its <topological realization> glues a standard geometric <simplex> for each member along common faces. A concrete model is
$$
|K|=\left\{(t_v)_{v\in V}:t_v\geq0,\ \sum_vt_v=1,\ \{v:t_v>0\}\in\Sigma\right\},
$$
with finite support and the <weak topology of a simplicial realization>: a set is <closed> precisely when its <intersection> with every <simplex> is <closed>. This agrees with the <quotient topology> of the gluing construction. Specifying this <topology> matters for complexes with infinitely many vertices.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <open star in a simplicial complex> of a <vertex> $v$ is the <union> of the relative interiors of the simplices containing $v$. In barycentric coordinates it is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{st}(v)=\{p\in|K|:t_v(p)>0\}.}
$$
Let $L$ be the <subcomplex> consisting of all simplices not containing $v$. The <complement> of the star is exactly $|L|$: the positive-coordinate support of a point misses $v$ precisely when it is a <simplex> of $L$. By the allowed closed-subcomplex theorem, $|L|$ is <closed> in $|K|$. Hence the star is <open>.

This is the open-star convention required by the question. The <closed> star, which also includes the opposite faces, is a different subset and need not be <open>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <vertex> stars form an <open> cover of $|K|$: every point has a nonempty positive-coordinate support, and therefore belongs to the star of at least one <vertex>. At a <vertex> $w$ itself, only its own barycentric coordinate is positive. Thus $w$ belongs to its own star and to no other <vertex> star.

Any finite selection of stars omits every <vertex> not among their finitely many labels. Infinitely many vertices therefore prevent a finite subcover, proving
$$
\boxed{|V|=\infty\Longrightarrow |K|\text{ is not compact}.}
$$
No local-finiteness hypothesis is needed for this <vertex-star cover obstruction to compactness>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Label the endpoints around the original polygon by $v_0,v_1,v_2,v_3$ so that the successive <boundary> arcs are
$$
v_0\xrightarrow{x}v_1\xrightarrow{A}v_2\xrightarrow{x}v_3\xrightarrow{B}v_0.
$$
The two occurrences of $x$ have the same boundary-traversal direction, as prescribed by the word. We use cutting along a new diagonal followed by gluing the old $x$ sides. Cutting introduces two copies of the diagonal with opposite orientations, so rejoining them later exactly recovers the original quotient.

**First move.** Draw a diagonal $d$ from $v_0$ to $v_2$. The two pieces have <boundary> words $xAd^{-1}$ and $dxB$. Glue them along their $x$ sides, turning one piece over in the planar drawing to match the arrows. Reading the outside <boundary> of the joined disk gives
$$
Ad^{-1}d^{-1}B^{-1}.
$$
Reverse the orientation of this entire disk and change the starting point of its <boundary> word. The same side-paired disk then has word $ddA^{-1}B$. Renaming the new paired edge $d$ as $x$ yields
$$
\boxed{xAxB\sim xxA^{-1}B.}
$$

**Second move.** Instead cut along $d$ from $v_1$ to $v_3$. The pieces now have words $xdB$ and $Axd^{-1}$. Gluing along the old $x$ sides gives outside word $dBA^{-1}d$. A cyclic change of starting point gives $A^{-1}ddB$, and renaming $d$ yields
$$
\boxed{xAxB\sim A^{-1}xxB.}
$$
All side identifications inside the strings $A$ and $B$ are carried along unchanged; reversing a piece reverses the order of its string and inverts each letter. The construction therefore gives homeomorphic quotients, not merely an equality of Euler characteristics. This is the <crosscap extraction move for a polygonal word>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Apply the first extraction move to the two outer occurrences of $x$, then cyclically rotate the word to expose the two occurrences of $y^{-1}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
xyzzyx
&\sim xx\,y^{-1}z^{-1}z^{-1}y^{-1}\\
&\sim y^{-1}z^{-1}z^{-1}y^{-1}xx\\
&\sim y^{-1}y^{-1}zzxx\\
&\sim xx\,y^{-1}y^{-1}zz.
\end{aligned}
$$
In the third line the repeated letter for the move is $y^{-1}$, and the intervening string is $z^{-1}z^{-1}$, whose inverse is $zz$. Rename $y^{-1}$ as a new edge label. The final word has the standard form $aabbcc$.

Each same-direction repeated pair represents a projective-plane summand, so the <classification theorem for surfaces> identifies this word with the <connected sum> of three real projective planes:
$$
\boxed{S\cong\mathbb{RP}^2\mathbin{\#}\mathbb{RP}^2\mathbin{\#}\mathbb{RP}^2.}
$$
Thus the surface has <crosscap number> three. As a check, the original identifications make all six polygon vertices equivalent, leaving one <vertex>, three edges and one face. Its <Euler characteristic> is $1-3+1=-1=2-3$, consistent with that crosscap count.
