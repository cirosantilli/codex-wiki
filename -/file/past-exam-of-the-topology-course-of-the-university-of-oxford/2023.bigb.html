Give the nonempty <subset> $A\subseteq X$ its <subspace topology>. It is <compact> if every <open cover> admits a <finite subcover>. Equivalently, whenever $A\subseteq\bigcup_{j\in J}U_j$ with each $U_j$ <open> in $X$, there are $j_1,\ldots,j_m$ such that $A\subseteq U_{j_1}\cup\cdots\cup U_{j_m}$.

It is <connected> if it has no <separation of a topological space>: there are no disjoint nonempty <open subsets> $B,C$ of $A$ with $A=B\cup C$. Here openness is relative to the <subspace topology>; equivalently, the only <clopen sets> of $A$ are $\varnothing$ and $A$.

Applying $f$ gives $f(K)\subseteq\bigcup_{r=1}^m V_{j_r}$. Thus every <open cover> of $f(K)$ has a <finite subcover>, proving the <continuous image of a compact space> theorem. A cover by sets <open> in $f(K)$ gives the same argument after expressing them as intersections with ambient <open sets>.

Let $K\subseteq X$ be <compact>, with $X$ a <Hausdorff space>. The <empty set> is <closed>, so suppose $K\neq\varnothing$. Fix $x\in X\setminus K$. For each $y\in K$, the <Hausdorff> property supplies disjoint <open> <neighbourhoods> $U_y$ of $y$ and $V_y$ of $x$.

The $U_y$ form an <open cover> of $K$. Choose a <finite subcover> $U_{y_1},\ldots,U_{y_m}$ and put $V=\bigcap_{r=1}^m V_{y_r}$. This is an <open> <neighbourhood> of $x$. If a point of $V$ belonged to $K$, it would belong to some $U_{y_r}$ as well as $V_{y_r}$, a contradiction. Hence $V\subseteq X\setminus K$. Every point of the complement has such an <open> <neighbourhood>, so $X\setminus K$ is <open> and $K$ is <closed>.

The intended hypothesis is that $A$ is not <connected>. The original PDF has the same grammatical ambiguity as the transcription. If only $X$ were assumed not <connected>, the assertion would fail for the <discrete space> $X=\{0,1\}$ with its usual <metric> and the <closed subset> $A=\{0\}$: two disjoint sets cannot both meet that <singleton>.

Under the intended reading, choose a <separation of a topological space> $A=B\sqcup C$. Both $B$ and $C$ are nonempty and <closed> in $A$, because each is the complement of the other relatively <open> part. Since $A$ is <closed> in $X$, both are <closed> in $X$.

For a nonempty <subset> $F$ of the <metric space> $(X,d)$, the <distance to a set> satisfies
$$
|d(x,F)-d(z,F)|\leq d(x,z).
$$
Indeed, the <triangle inequality> gives $d(x,F)\leq d(x,z)+d(z,F)$, and swapping $x,z$ proves the bound. Thus the two <distance to a set> functions below are <continuous>. Set
$$
U=\{x:d(x,B)<d(x,C)\},\qquad V=\{x:d(x,C)<d(x,B)\}.
$$
These are disjoint <open sets>. For $b\in B$, we have $d(b,B)=0$ and $d(b,C)>0$, since $C$ is <closed> and $b\notin C$. Hence $B\subseteq U$. Similarly $C\subseteq V$. Therefore $A\subseteq U\cup V$ and both $U$ and $V$ meet $A$, as required. The construction is a concrete instance of the <normality of every metric space>.

Removing $1/2$ from the <closed interval> $I=[0,1]$ leaves the <separation of a topological space>
$$
I\setminus\{1/2\}=[0,1/2)\sqcup(1/2,1].
$$
Thus $1/2$ is a <cut point> of $I$.

In contrast, removing any point $p$ from the <square> $Q=[0,1]^2$ leaves a <path-connected space>. To prove this, take distinct $u,v\in Q\setminus\{p\}$. Choose an interior point $w$ outside the two <lines> through $(u,p)$ and $(v,p)$. Such a choice exists: a small <circle> in the interior meets each of those <lines> in at most two points. Since $Q$ is a <convex set>, the <line segments> $[u,w]$ and $[w,v]$ stay in $Q$; our choice of $w$ ensures that both avoid $p$. Their concatenation is a <continuous path> from $u$ to $v$ in $Q\setminus\{p\}$.

The <product topology> on $I\times I$ agrees with its usual <subspace topology> in the <Euclidean plane>. If $h:Q\to I$ were a <homeomorphism>, its <restriction> would give a <homeomorphism>
$$
Q\setminus\{h^{-1}(1/2)\}\longrightarrow I\setminus\{1/2\}.
$$
The domain is <connected>, since <path connectedness> implies <connectedness>, while the target is not <connected>. This contradicts the preservation of <connectedness> by a <homeomorphism>.

The <quotient set> $X/{\sim}$ consists of the <equivalence classes> $[x]=\{y\in X:y\sim x\}$. Its collapsing map is the <canonical projection of a quotient set>
$$
p:X\longrightarrow X/{\sim},\qquad p(x)=[x].
$$
Equip this <set> with the <quotient topology>:
$$
U\subseteq X/{\sim}\text{ is open}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p^{-1}(U)\text{ is open in }X.
$$
This is a <topology> because <preimages> preserve arbitrary <unions>, finite <intersections>, the <empty set>, and the whole <set>. The resulting <topological space> is the <quotient space>. The displayed rule immediately implies that the <preimage> of every <open set> is <open>, so $p$ is <continuous>; it is also <surjective>.

Use the <equivalence relation induced by a function>:
$$
x\sim x'\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f(x)=f(x').
$$
Equality in $Y$ supplies reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity, so this is an <equivalence relation>. Define $\bar f:X/{\sim}\to Y$ by $\bar f([x])=f(x)$. It is well-defined; it is <injective> by the definition of $\sim$ and <surjective> because $f$ is <surjective>.

For an <open set> $W\subseteq Y$,
$$
p^{-1}\bigl(\bar f^{-1}(W)\bigr)=f^{-1}(W)
$$
is <open> by <continuity> of $f$. The <quotient topology> therefore makes $\bar f^{-1}(W)$ <open>, proving that $\bar f$ is <continuous>.

The <continuous image of a compact space> theorem applied to $p$ makes $X/{\sim}$ <compact>. If $C$ is <closed> in $X/{\sim}$, it is <compact>, and $\bar f(C)$ is <compact> in the <Hausdorff space> $Y$, hence <closed>. Thus $\bar f$ is a <closed map>. Its <inverse function> is <continuous>, since its <preimage> of $C$ is precisely $\bar f(C)$. We have proved that $\bar f$ is a <homeomorphism>, including the <compact-to-Hausdorff continuous bijection theorem> used in the argument.

$$

This point has radius $r$, because $u^2+v^2=1-w^2=\sin^2(\pi r)$. The two poles have the <preimages> already described. Thus $F$ is <surjective> and its <fibers> are exactly the prescribed <equivalence classes>.

The <closed disc> is <compact> by the <Heine-Borel theorem>, and $S^2$ is <Hausdorff> with the <subspace topology> from <Euclidean space>. The preceding result gives the <homeomorphism>
$$
\boxed{D^2/{\sim}\ \cong\ S^2,\qquad [x,y]\longmapsto F(x,y)}.

An abstract <simplicial complex> consists of a <set> $V$ of <vertices of a simplicial complex> and a family $K$ of finite nonempty <subsets> of $V$, called abstract <simplexes>, such that every <singleton> $\{v\}$ belongs to $K$ and every nonempty <subset> of a member of $K$ also belongs to $K$. One may include the <empty set> as an additional face by convention. A member with $k+1$ vertices is a $k$-<simplex>; its nonempty <subsets> are its <faces of a simplex>.

The <geometric realization> has the following explicit description using a <barycentric coordinate system>:
$$
|K|=\left\{t:V\to[0,1]:\operatorname{supp}(t)\text{ is finite},\ \sum_{v\in V}t(v)=1,\ \operatorname{supp}(t)\in K\right\}.
$$
Here the <support> is $\operatorname{supp}(t)=\{v:t(v)>0\}$. Each $\rho\in K$ gives a standard geometric <simplex>
$$
|\rho|=\{t\in|K|:t(v)=0\text{ for }v\notin\rho\},
$$
with its usual <Euclidean topology>. Common <faces of a simplex> are identified using the same <barycentric coordinate system>.

Precisely, the <topology> on $|K|$ is defined by declaring $A\subseteq|K|$ <closed> if and only if $A\cap|\rho|$ is <closed> in $|\rho|$ for every $\rho\in K$. Equivalently, use the <quotient topology> on the <disjoint union> of all the standard geometric <simplexes>, identifying identical points of common <faces of a simplex>. For a finite <simplicial complex>, this agrees with the <subspace topology> in $\mathbb R^V$. For an infinite <simplicial complex>, the stated rule specifies the realization <topology> without presuming a finite-dimensional ambient space.

Fix a <vertex of a simplicial complex> $v$, and let $R$ be the <set> of vertices reachable from $v$ by an <edge path>. If a <simplex> contains a vertex of $R$, every vertex of that <simplex> lies in $R$: append its <edge> from the reachable vertex to the existing <edge path>. Thus every <simplex> has either all its vertices in $R$ or all outside $R$.

Partition the <geometric realization> as $|K|=A\sqcup B$, according to whether the <support> in the <barycentric coordinate system> lies in $R$ or outside $R$. On each geometric <simplex>, the intersection with $A$ is either the whole <simplex> or the <empty set>, and the same holds for $B$. The defining realization <topology> therefore makes both $A$ and $B$ <closed>, hence also <open> because they are complements.

The vertex $v$ belongs to $A$. If any vertex were unreachable, it would belong to the nonempty set $B$, giving a <separation of a topological space> of $|K|$. This contradicts <connectedness>. Therefore every vertex is reachable from $v$, and any two vertices can be joined by an <edge path>.

A <topological surface> is a <Hausdorff space> with a countable <basis of a topology> in which every point has an <open> <neighbourhood> <homeomorphic> to an <open subset> of the <Euclidean plane>. We use the convention that a <surface> has no boundary unless a <surface with boundary> is explicitly specified. A closed <surface> is <compact> and has no boundary. Some conventions also include <connectedness> in the definition; we impose it on the <closed combinatorial surface> below.

A <closed combinatorial surface> is a finite <connected> <simplicial complex> $K$ with the following properties: every <simplex> is a <face of a simplex> of dimension two, and the <link of a simplex> at every vertex is a <cycle graph>. Explicitly, for a vertex $v$ its link is the <simplicial subcomplex>
$$
\operatorname{lk}_K(v)=\{\rho\in K:v\notin\rho,\ \rho\cup\{v\}\in K\}.
$$
To say this is a <cycle graph> means that there are distinct vertices $w_1,\ldots,w_m$, with $m\geq3$, and exactly the link <edges> $\{w_i,w_{i+1}\}$, with indices modulo $m$. In particular, every edge of $K$ belongs to exactly two triangles. The vertex-link condition also requires that the triangles around each vertex form one cyclic fan; edge incidence alone would allow singular examples.

These conditions make $|K|$ a closed <surface>. A point inside a triangle has a disc <neighbourhood>, the two triangles at an edge give a disc <neighbourhood>, and the cyclic fan at a vertex gives a disc <neighbourhood>. Finiteness gives <compactness>, the <Hausdorff> property, and a countable <basis of a topology> from a realization in a finite-dimensional <Euclidean space>. If disconnected <closed combinatorial surfaces> are allowed, each <connected component> satisfies this definition separately.

The <classification theorem for closed combinatorial surfaces> states that the <geometric realization> of every <closed combinatorial surface> is <homeomorphic> to exactly one of the following:

* The <sphere> $S^2$.
* An <orientable> <surface> $\Sigma_g$ formed as a <connected sum of surfaces> of $g\geq1$ copies of the <torus> $T^2$.
* A <nonorientable surface> $N_k$ formed as a <connected sum of surfaces> of $k\geq1$ copies of the <real projective plane> $\mathbb{RP}^2$.

With $\Sigma_0=S^2$, the complete distinguishing data are <orientability> and the <Euler characteristic>:
$$
\chi(\Sigma_g)=2-2g,\qquad \chi(N_k)=2-k.
$$
Here $\chi(K)=V-E+F$, where $V,E,F$ count the <vertices of a simplicial complex>, <edges>, and triangles of a finite <triangulation>. Thus two such <connected> closed <surfaces> are <homeomorphic> if and only if they have the same <orientability> and <Euler characteristic>. In particular $N_1$ is the <real projective plane> and $N_2$ is the <Klein bottle>. Every listed type admits a finite <triangulation>. For disconnected finite <closed combinatorial surfaces>, apply the theorem to each of the finitely many <connected components>; the resulting unordered collection of types determines the <homeomorphism> type.

We construct both the required <triangulation> and its <polygonal schema>. Subdivide the <square> $Q=[0,1]^2$ into a $4\times4$ grid. Insert the centre of each small square and join it by <line segments> to its four corners, making four triangles per small square. Identify opposite sides of $Q$ by translations: $(0,t)\sim(1,t)$ and $(t,0)\sim(t,1)$. This is the standard <quotient space> presentation of the <torus>.

The grid and centre vertices, with these identifications, define an abstract <simplicial complex> $K_1$. Every triangle contains its own small-square centre and two consecutive grid vertices, so no triangle has identified vertices and no distinct triangles acquire the same vertex set. Only intended common sides or vertices are shared. Thus this is a genuine finite <triangulation>, whose <geometric realization> is <homeomorphic> to $T^2$. There are $32$ vertices, $96$ edges and $64$ triangles, giving <Euler characteristic> zero.

Choose the <simplex> $\tau$ represented in the interior of $Q$ by the triangle with vertices
$$
(1/4,1/4),\qquad(1/2,1/4),\qquad(3/8,3/8).
$$
Remove its <relative interior>. Before identifying the square's sides, the remainder is a <closed disc> with a triangular hole, hence an <annulus>. Cut this <annulus> along the <line segment> from $(0,0)$ to $(1/4,1/4)$, treating the two banks as distinct boundary arcs. The cut space is a <closed disc>; traversing its boundary follows the four outer edges, one bank, the three inner edges, and the other bank. It therefore has nine marked boundary sides.

A <homeomorphism> from this cut disc to a convex nine-sided <polygon> can be chosen to carry its nine marked sides to the nine polygon sides. One justification is to choose a boundary <homeomorphism> with the prescribed marked vertices and extend it over the disc by radial extension after disc parametrizations. Transport the identifications to this <convex polygon>. With labels recording traversal direction, its boundary word is
$$
\boxed{a\,b\,a^{-1}\,b^{-1}\,c\,d\,e\,f\,c^{-1}}.
$$
Identify equally labelled sides in their arrow directions: the two $a$ sides and two $b$ sides restore the torus pairings, while the two $c$ sides undo the cut. Leave $d,e,f$ unpaired. Their union is the triangular boundary of the removed <simplex>. The resulting <quotient space> is exactly
$$
X=|K_1|\setminus\operatorname{inside}(\tau).
$$
This is the <polygonal presentation of a punctured surface>. The word supplies all nine sides and every gluing; inverse exponents reverse the boundary traversal direction.

Use compatible edge identifications: choose a <bijection> between the three boundary vertices of $\tau$ and those of $\sigma$, and extend it linearly on each edge. This defines a <simplicial homeomorphism> of the two boundary <circles>. This compatibility is the usual meaning of gluing the two boundary triangles; independently chosen edge reversals need not agree at their endpoints. The classification needed here is 3(a)(iv); the printed reference to 3(a)(iii) is a source error.

Delete the two triangular <simplexes> but retain all their proper <faces of a simplex>, and identify the two resulting boundary <simplicial subcomplexes> by this map. The result is a finite abstract <simplicial complex> $L$: vertices from different sides are identified only in the boundary, and the only shared edges are boundary edges. There is no triangle consisting of all three boundary vertices after the two faces have been removed. Consequently no other <simplexes> become duplicates. Its <geometric realization> has the specified <quotient topology> and is <homeomorphic> to $Z$.

We check the <closed combinatorial surface> conditions. Away from the seam, vertex <links of a simplex> remain <cycle graphs>. At a boundary vertex of $X$ or $Y$, removing the face changes its link from a <cycle graph> to a <graph path>, with endpoints corresponding to its two boundary edges. Gluing the boundaries identifies the two paths' respective endpoints, making one <cycle graph>. Each seam edge now meets one triangle from $X$ and one from $Y$, and all other edges retain their original two incident triangles. Thus every vertex link is a <cycle graph> and every <simplex> lies in a triangle. The <simplicial complex> is <connected>, since the two <connected> punctured surfaces meet along their boundary <circle>. Hence $L$ is a <closed combinatorial surface>.

This construction is the <connected sum of surfaces> $T^2\mathbin\#\mathbb{RP}^2$. For completeness, compute its <Euler characteristic> directly. Removing a triangular face while retaining its edges and vertices subtracts one, so
$$
\chi(X)=0-1=-1,\qquad \chi(Y)=1-1=0.
$$
Gluing identifies three vertex pairs and three edge pairs, giving
$$
\chi(Z)=\chi(X)+\chi(Y)-3+3=-1.
$$
For the explicit <triangulations> above, this is also $62-189+126=-1$.

Finally $Z$ is a <nonorientable surface>. If $Z$ were <orientable>, its <restriction> would make $Y$ <orientable>. The consistent choices of <orientation of a simplex> on its triangles extend across a capping <disc>: choose the cap's <orientation of a simplex> so that the induced directions on the common boundary oppose. Capping $Y$ by $\sigma$ would therefore make the <real projective plane> <orientable>, a contradiction. The obstruction to <orientability> in the <real projective plane> can also be seen from a <Möbius band> neighbourhood of a <projective line>.

By the <classification theorem for closed combinatorial surfaces>, a <nonorientable surface> with <Euler characteristic> $-1$ has $2-k=-1$, hence $k=3$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{Z\cong\mathbb{RP}^2\mathbin\#\mathbb{RP}^2\mathbin\#\mathbb{RP}^2=N_3}.
$$
