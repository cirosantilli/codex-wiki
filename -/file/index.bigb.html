= Linguistics
{parent=Science}

= Morpheme
{parent=Linguistics}

A minimal unit contributing lexical or grammatical meaning. A <morpheme> is not generally identical to a written character, syllable or <phoneme> across all languages.

= Language comprehension
{parent=Linguistics}

Understanding linguistic input, whether spoken or written. It combines access to words and meanings with sentence-level integration, context and control.

= Auditory comprehension
{parent=Language comprehension}

Understanding language presented through <hearing>. Acoustic and <phonological analysis> add demands beyond those shared with written or nonverbal semantic tasks.

= Lexical access
{parent=Linguistics}

Access to stored information about words during comprehension or production. A failure to retrieve a name need not imply loss of the underlying concept.

= Prosody
{parent=Linguistics}

= Pragmatics
{parent=Linguistics}

Interpretation of utterances in their conversational context, including what a speaker is asking the listener to do. Making “initial search” explicit can change the interpretation of a <false-belief> question without changing the represented event.<Where to look first for children's knowledge of false beliefs by Michael Siegal and Karen Beattie>[Cognition 38, 1–12; initial-search wording manipulation]{ref}

= Grammar
{parent=Linguistics}

= Phoneme
{parent=Linguistics}

A contrastive sound unit in a <language>. A phonetic difference can be perceptible without functioning as a phonemic contrast in every <language>.

= Phonetics
{parent=Linguistics}

= Phonetic category
{parent=Phonetics}

A grouping of speech sounds defined by a relevant perceptual or articulatory organisation. Experimental discrimination of two endpoints does not, by itself, establish a complete inventory of linguistic categories.

= Phonology
{parent=Linguistics}

The organization of sound-based distinctions and patterns in a <language>. Phonological representations connect acoustic inputs, lexical information and articulatory behavior without being identical to any one of them.

= Phonological awareness
{parent=Phonology}

Explicit access to and manipulation of sound structure, including units such as syllables and <phonemes>. It is distinct from ordinary ability to understand speech.

= Phonemic awareness
{parent=Phonological awareness}

Awareness and manipulation of phoneme-sized units. Its development can support alphabetic <reading> and be altered by <learning> to read.<Does awareness of speech as a sequence of phones arise spontaneously? by José Morais and others>[Cognition 7, 323–331; illiterate and adult-literate comparisons on phone addition/deletion]{ref}

= Phonological representation
{parent=Phonology}

A representation of the sound structure relevant to a language. Recognition, temporary retention and <speech production> can use such information while imposing different additional demands.

= Phonological analysis
{parent=Phonology}

Analysis of a <speech> signal in terms of the sound structure relevant to language. Auditory discrimination, mapping sounds to words and access to meaning impose distinguishable demands.

= Semantics
{parent=Linguistics}

The study and representation of meaning in <language>. Lexical interpretation and sentence interpretation require interacting information; a contrast with a syntactic task need not isolate every semantic process.

= Semantic association
{parent=Semantics}

A relation based on meaning among concepts or expressions. Semantic-association judgments must be distinguished from perceptual similarity and response-selection demands.

= Thematic role
{parent=Semantics}

A relation specifying how an entity participates in a described event, such as an agent or recipient. Grammatical constructions can assign different roles to the same familiar words, making reversible sentences useful in comprehension tests.

= Syntax
{parent=Linguistics}

Principles and representations governing structured combinations of linguistic elements. Experiments must distinguish building or interpreting a structure from holding its elements in memory, detecting errors and selecting responses.

= Syntactic complexity
{parent=Syntax}

A description of structural demands in a sentence, such as a noncanonical dependency. Its operational definition should be explicit; longer strings, unfamiliar words and greater working-memory load do not define <syntax> by themselves.

= Agrammatism
{parent=Syntax}

An impairment in grammatical structure in production or comprehension. It is not equivalent to reduced <speech> rate, dysarthria or all forms of <aphasia>; task design must separate grammatical dependencies from lexical and memory demands.

= Sentential complement
{parent=Syntax}

An embedded clause serving as a complement, such as “that the toy is in the box” in “she says that the toy is in the box”. The full report can be true even when the embedded proposition is false, making such constructions relevant to distinguishing a representation from reality.<The influence of language on theory of mind by Courtney Melinda Hale and Helen Tager-Flusberg>[Developmental Science 6, 346–359; random allocation to complement, false-belief or relative-clause training]{ref}

= Language
{parent=Linguistics}

A system of structured representations and conventions supporting expression and interpretation. Its study distinguishes sound structure, grammatical organization and meaning without assuming that each maps one-to-one onto a single <brain> region.

= Vocabulary
{parent=Language}

Knowledge of word forms, meanings and their uses. Number of recognised words and depth or flexibility of knowledge are different dimensions.

= Incidental vocabulary learning from reading
{parent=Vocabulary}

<Learning> about unfamiliar words during engagement with text without direct <vocabulary> instruction. Small encounter-level gains can be measurable, but long-term extrapolations depend on <reading> amount, prior knowledge and test sensitivity.<Learning Words from Context by William E. Nagy and others>[Reading Research Quarterly 20, 233–253; incidental word knowledge after reading natural text and limited extrapolation]{ref}

= Vocabulary depth
{parent=Vocabulary}

Richness and precision of knowledge about a word, including relations, context and multiple meanings.

= Vocabulary breadth
{parent=Vocabulary}

The range of words known under a specified criterion. A recognition test and an expressive definition test need not identify the same boundary.

= Written language
{parent=Language}

<Language> represented through a writing system, including <reading> and written production. <Spelling>, text generation and comprehension make partly different demands.

= Written composition
{parent=Written language}

Generating and organising written text. <Vocabulary>, sentence formulation and discourse interact with <spelling>, transcription and planning demands.<Constraints in the production of written text in children with specific language impairments by Julie E. Dockrell and others>[Exceptional Children 73, 147–164; 64 children with language-impairment history, measures at eight and written text at ten]{ref}

= Transcription in writing
{parent=Written composition}

Processes producing the written form, including <spelling> and the mechanics of recording text. Dictated and independent composition differ in these demands as well as in available support.

= Spelling
{parent=Written language}

Producing conventional written word forms. Immediate phonological plausibility, knowledge of a particular <spelling> and fluent written production are distinguishable achievements.

= Orthography
{parent=Written language}

The conventional organisation of a writing system, including recurring patterns and relations to <language>.

= Orthographic depth
{parent=Orthography}

The degree of complexity or inconsistency in mappings between written and spoken forms. Its consequences depend on the units and outcomes assessed; early pronunciation accuracy, fluency and comprehension need not show identical patterns.

= Artificial orthography
{parent=Orthography}

An experimentally defined writing system with unfamiliar symbols and controlled mappings. It permits manipulation of <learning> history while requiring separate tests of generalisation to natural <reading>.<The influence of consistency, frequency, and semantics on learning to read: an artificial orthography paradigm by Joanne S. H. Taylor and others>[Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition 37, 60–76; sixteen/32 adults, novel script, frequency/consistency and pretraining]{ref}

= Orthographic consistency
{parent=Orthography}

The regularity with which a written pattern predicts a pronunciation or other linguistic outcome under a specified context. Frequency and consistency are different properties.

= Grapheme
{parent=Written language}

A unit in a writing system, distinguished from a particular font shape and from its spoken interpretation.

= Oral language
{parent=Language}

<Language> expressed or understood through speech. <Phonology>, <vocabulary>, <grammar> and discourse are distinct dimensions, and listening and speaking impose different task demands.

= Sign language
{parent=Language}

A natural <language> using structured visual-manual and other bodily signals. <Sign languages> have phonological organisation, meaning and <syntax>; they are not universal pantomime or merely a manual transcription of <speech>.

= Handshape
{parent=Sign language}

A configuration of the hand contributing to the form of a sign. Contrastive sublexical form need not be acoustic to participate in a phonological system.

= Open science
{parent=Science}

Practices making research plans, materials, analyses and outputs inspectable and reusable where ethically appropriate. <Preregistration>, transparent reporting and executable analysis support distinct aspects of openness. Protecting confidentiality can require restricted data access rather than unrestricted publication.

= Research transparency
{parent=Open science}

Making the provenance, design, measurements and analytical choices underlying a research claim sufficiently inspectable for informed evaluation. It can support <computational reproducibility> and <scientific replication>, but does not by itself establish that the measured variable represents the intended construct.

= HARKing
{parent=Open science}

= Hypothesizing after the results are known
{synonym}

Presenting a hypothesis suggested by inspected results as if it had been specified beforehand. The misrepresentation, rather than discovery itself, is the problem. Transparent <exploratory analysis>, <preregistration> and independent testing preserve the distinction between generating and evaluating a hypothesis.<Registered Reports by Center for Open Science>[Details: HARKing; Scientific Creativity and Exploration]{ref}

= Registered report
{parent=Open science}

A publication format involving review of the question and methods before outcomes are known, with in-principle publication acceptance conditional on adequate adherence and reporting. It differs from <preregistration> alone, which need not involve peer review or publication commitment.<Registered Reports by Center for Open Science>[Eligibility features and two-stage workflow]{ref}

= Preregistration
{parent=Open science}

= Pre-registration
{synonym}

A time-stamped record of a research plan made before relevant results are examined. It distinguishes planned decisions from result-informed changes; it need not prohibit <exploratory analysis>. For existing data, disclose previous access and knowledge.<Preregistration by Center for Open Science>[Definition and FAQ on existing data]{ref}

= Education
{parent=Social science}

= Science communication
{parent=Education}

Communication that supports understanding and evaluation of scientific claims. Its effects should be assessed through relevant understanding or decisions, rather than the prestige of scientific terminology alone.

= Debunking
{parent=Science communication}

Correcting a misconception with relevant evidence and an interpretable alternative. Immediate endorsement change, durable reasoning and practical decisions are distinct outcomes for evaluating <debunking>.

= Refutation text
{parent=Debunking}

A text that identifies a mistaken claim and explains an alternative account or why the claim fails. A short-term effect on a belief questionnaire does not establish a lasting change in teaching practice.

= Primary education
{parent=Education}

Early formal schooling. Psychological research in <primary education> must specify age, prior instruction, language and school context rather than assuming that one age or curriculum defines a universal developmental stage.

= Attainment grouping
{parent=Primary education}

= Ability grouping
{synonym}

Organizing pupils partly by assessed achievement. The commonly used label <ability grouping> should not imply that a placement measures a fixed innate capacity; grouping format, movement and instruction must be specified.

= Within-class grouping
{parent=Attainment grouping}

Organizing smaller groups inside a shared class for some instruction. Flexible grouping for a particular <learning> goal differs from a permanent whole-curriculum stream.

= Subject setting
{parent=Attainment grouping}

Regrouping pupils by current attainment for a particular subject. The educational effect depends on assessment, movement, teaching and the comparison arrangement rather than the name alone.

= Streaming by attainment
{parent=Attainment grouping}

Assigning pupils to a group across much of the curriculum. It differs from <subject setting> and from task-specific <within-class grouping>, and can concentrate differences in curriculum or opportunity across subjects.

= Academic achievement
{parent=Education}

Knowledge and skills acquired in an educational domain, assessed through specified tasks or records. <Mathematics> procedures, mathematical concepts, <word decoding> and <reading comprehension> are distinct outcomes; a single aggregate score can conceal different cognitive demands.

= Social stigma
{parent=Sociology}

Social devaluation associated with an attributed characteristic in a particular context. <Social stigma> concerns relationships and opportunities as well as individual attitudes; a diagnostic label does not have the same consequences in every setting.

= Structural stigma
{parent=Social stigma}

Institutional arrangements and practices that disadvantage a socially marked group. Changing personal attitudes does not alone establish that access to housing, work or appropriate care has become equitable.

= Social distance
{parent=Social stigma}

Desired or intended separation from a person or group in specified relationships. A social-distance <questionnaire> measures a stated orientation, not necessarily actual behavior or explicit denial of humanity.

= Dehumanization
{parent=Social stigma}

Denial or reduction of qualities or standing ordinarily accorded to persons, such as agency, emotional depth or equal moral consideration. <Dehumanization> is related to <prejudice> and <discrimination> but is not established by every measure of <social distance>.

= Mechanistic dehumanization
{parent=Dehumanization}

Representing a person primarily as an impersonal mechanism lacking a recognized subjective perspective or agency. A scientific biological explanation does not logically require <mechanistic dehumanization>; how an explanation is framed and used is an empirical question.

= Mental illness stigma
{parent=Social stigma}

Devaluation or exclusion associated with a perceived <mental disorder>. Public attitudes, <experienced discrimination>, <anticipated discrimination>, <self-stigma> and <structural stigma> are distinguishable and require different measures.

= Genetic essentialism
{parent=Mental illness stigma}

Treating genetic influence as a fixed, defining and categorically separating essence. Genetic contribution does not logically imply immutability, uniformity or inability to participate in ordinary social life.

= Diagnostic overshadowing
{parent=Mental illness stigma}

Allowing an existing diagnosis to substitute for adequate consideration of a separate difficulty. In mental-health settings, a psychiatric label can be inappropriately used to explain a physical complaint without examining relevant alternatives.

= Experienced discrimination
{parent=Mental illness stigma}

Reported or observed unfair treatment associated with an attributed characteristic. <Self-report>, direct observation and institutional records provide different evidence and have distinct sampling limitations.

= Anticipated discrimination
{parent=Mental illness stigma}

An expectation of unfair treatment linked to an attributed characteristic. It can influence participation or concealment before a particular rejection occurs; absence of reported prior <discrimination> does not prove that the expectation is unfounded.

= Self-stigma
{parent=Mental illness stigma}

Applying a socially devaluing <stereotype> to oneself, with consequences for self-understanding or expectations. Anticipating realistic <discrimination> is different from accepting the <stereotype> as true.

= Empirical equivalence under uniform boosts

= New riddle of induction
{parent=Problem of induction}
{wiki}

= Projectibility
{parent=New riddle of induction}

The eligibility of a predicate or generalization for extending an inference beyond examined cases. Agreement with positive instances alone does not establish projectibility: <grue> and green can fit the same past examples while supporting incompatible predictions.

= Predicate entrenchment
{parent=Projectibility}

In <Nelson Goodman>'s account, the standing a predicate acquires through a history of actual projections. It concerns the use of the predicate in inference, rather than merely the number of past objects satisfying it. Entrenchment is revisable and is not a proof of future reliability.<Fact, Fiction, and Forecast by Nelson Goodman>[Chapter IV, sections 2–4]{ref}

= Grue
{parent=New riddle of induction}
{title2=$G_t(x)$}

For a fixed cutoff $t$, let $E_t(x)$ mean examined before $t$. <Nelson Goodman>'s predicate is $G_t(x)\iff(E_t(x)\land\operatorname{green}(x))\lor(\neg E_t(x)\land\operatorname{blue}(x))$. An already examined green object remains grue after $t$ without a colour change. The conflicting projection concerns objects not examined before $t$.

= Duhem problem
{c}
{parent=Scientific confirmation}

From $T\land A\Rightarrow E$ and $\neg E$, infer only $\neg T\lor\neg A$. A failed prediction therefore does not logically isolate failure of the target $T$ from failure of <auxiliary hypotheses> $A$. Independent calibration and varied experiments can discriminate packages; the logical ambiguity does not imply that every package is equally supported.<The Aim and Structure of Physical Theory by Pierre Duhem>[part II, chapter VI, section 2]{ref}

= Auxiliary hypothesis
{parent=Scientific confirmation}

An assumption used with a target scientific hypothesis to obtain a prediction, for example an apparatus calibration or an initial condition. Independent evidence for an <auxiliary hypothesis> helps distinguish a failure of the target from a failure of the test setup.

= Bayesian confirmation
{c}
{parent=Scientific confirmation}
{title2=$P(H\mid E,K)>P(H\mid K)$}

Evidence $E$ confirms $H$ relative to background $K$ when it increases its <conditional probability>. With $0<P(H\mid K)<1$ and positive evidence <probability>, <Bayes' theorem> gives
$$
P(H\mid E,K)>P(H\mid K)\iff P(E\mid H,K)>P(E\mid\neg H,K).
$$
Indeed, writing $p=P(H\mid K)$, $a=P(E\mid H,K)$ and $b=P(E\mid\neg H,K)$ gives the <posterior probability> $pa/(pa+(1-p)b)$; comparison with $p$ reduces to $a>b$. Thus <scientific confirmation> depends on the alternatives and background, not just on a statement's logical form.

= Finite-population model of raven confirmation
{parent=Bayesian confirmation}
{title2=$L_{\mathrm{nonblack}}=(1-1/M)^{-1},\quad L_{\mathrm{raven}}=(1-1/r)^{-1}$}

Fix $r>1$ <ravens> and $M>1$ nonblack objects in a finite population. Under $H_0$, all <ravens> are black; under $H_1$, exactly one is nonblack. The total numbers $r,M$ are held fixed under both <hypotheses>, so the classification of one nonraven also changes between hypotheses. This is a deliberately specified model for <Bayesian confirmation>, not a universal rule about how bird observations supply evidence.

A uniform draw among nonblack objects returns a nonraven with <probabilities> $1$ under $H_0$ and $1-1/M$ under $H_1$. A uniform draw among <ravens> returns a black object with <probabilities> $1$ and $1-1/r$. The respective <likelihood ratios> for $H_0$ over $H_1$ are therefore $(1-1/M)^{-1}$ and $(1-1/r)^{-1}$. When $M>r$, the former is closer to one, so supplies less support under these assumptions.

With equal <prior probabilities>, <Bayes' theorem> gives <posterior probabilities> $1/(2-1/M)$ and $1/(2-1/r)$. More generally, with <prior probability> $p$ for $H_0$, replace the two expressions by $p/[p+(1-p)(1-1/M)]$ and $p/[p+(1-p)(1-1/r)]$. Previously known information or a different sampling protocol requires different <likelihoods>; selecting an object already known to be a nonraven need not supply new evidence.

= Problem of old evidence
{parent=Bayesian confirmation}

Already accepted evidence can support a newly considered theory even though conditioning again on a fact of <probability> one produces no increase in its <posterior probability>. Distinguish new learning, retrospective support and discovering a previously unrecognized theory–evidence connection.<Why I Am Not a Bayesian by Clark Glymour>[chapter 3 of Theory and Evidence]{ref}

= Raven paradox
{parent=Scientific confirmation}
{wiki}

“All ravens are black” is <logically equivalent> to “all nonblack things are nonravens”. An unrestricted rule that positive instances confirm universal generalizations would make a white shoe confirm the first statement. A <Bayesian confirmation> account instead conditions on background knowledge and the sampling procedure.<Studies in the Logic of Confirmation I by Carl Hempel>[section 5]{ref}
