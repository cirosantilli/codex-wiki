= Inverse matrix
{synonym}

For an $n$-by-$n$ <square matrix> $A$ over a <field>, its matrix inverse is an $n$-by-$n$ <matrix> $A^{-1}$ satisfying both identities
$$
AA^{-1}=A^{-1}A=I_n,
$$
where $I_n$ is the <identity matrix>. It exists exactly when $A$ is an <invertible matrix>, equivalently when its <matrix rank> is $n$ or its <determinant> is nonzero. It reverses the <linear map>: applying $A$ and then $A^{-1}$ recovers the input, while applying $A^{-1}$ and then $A$ recovers the output.https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06-linear-algebra-spring-2010/resources/lecture-3-multiplication-and-inverse-matrices/[Strang, MIT 18.06 Lecture 3: the two inverse identities]{ref}

The <matrix inverse> is unique. If $B$ and $C$ both satisfy the identities, then $B=B(AC)=(BA)C=C$. Although the definition is two-sided, for finite <square matrices> either identity alone suffices: $BA=I$ forces a <trivial kernel>, whereas $AB=I$ forces <surjectivity>; either makes $A$ an <invertible matrix> by the <rank-nullity theorem>. Rectangular <matrices> may have only one of these properties.

For a concrete example, take
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\det A=2\cdot1-1\cdot1=1,\qquad
A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Direct <matrix multiplication> gives
$$
AA^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}2-1&-2+2\\1-1&-1+2\end{pmatrix}=I_2,
\qquad
A^{-1}A=\begin{pmatrix}2-1&1-1\\-2+2&-1+2\end{pmatrix}=I_2.
$$
Consequently the <vector> $v=(1,2)^T$ follows a reversible journey:
$$
\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}
\xrightarrow{\ A\ }\begin{pmatrix}4\\3\end{pmatrix}
\xrightarrow{\ A^{-1}\ }\begin{pmatrix}4-3\\-4+6\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The columns $(2,1)^T$ and $(1,1)^T$ are the images of the <standard basis> vectors. They turn the unit square into a <parallelogram>; the <matrix inverse> restores both the square and its surrounding grid. Since $\det A=1$, this example changes shape while preserving <area>, illustrating that invertibility does not require preserving lengths or angles.

\Image[matrix-inverse-undoing-a-map.png]
{title=A <matrix inverse> restores a grid and a <vector>; a <singular matrix> merges distinct inputs}

For comparison, the <singular matrix>
$$
S=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
S\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}
=S\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}
$$
cannot have a <matrix inverse>: the output $(1,0)^T$ cannot specify which of the two inputs occurred. Their difference $(1,-1)^T$ belongs to its <kernel>. Indeed $S(x,y)^T=(x+y,0)^T$ collapses the <plane> to a <line>, its <matrix rank> is one, and its <determinant> is zero. No subsequent <function> of the output alone can reconstruct every input.

An important use of the <matrix inverse> is describing the unique solution of a <linear system>:
$$
Ax=b\iff x=A^{-1}b.
$$
For $b=(4,3)^T$, the preceding computation solves $2x_1+x_2=4$ and $x_1+x_2=3$, giving $x=(1,2)^T$. In computation, usually solve the <linear system> using <Gaussian elimination> and a factorization such as an <LU decomposition>, rather than forming every entry of the <matrix inverse>. The inverse formula specifies the answer; a factorization provides a reusable way to compute it for each right-hand side.https://www.mit.edu/~18.06/Spring17/final-sol.pdf[MIT 18.06, Spring 2017 final solutions, problem 5(b): solve with triangular factors rather than computing inverses]{ref}

Here the <LU decomposition> is especially simple:
$$
A=LU,\qquad
L=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\\tfrac12&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
U=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&\tfrac12\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Solve $Ly=b$ by <forward substitution in a triangular system>: $y_1=4$ and $y_2=3-\tfrac12\cdot4=1$. Then solve $Ux=y$ by <backward substitution in a triangular system>: $x_2=2$ and $x_1=(4-2)/2=1$. The same factors work when $b$ changes. If the whole <matrix inverse> is actually needed, its $j$th column is the solution of $Ax=e_j$, where $e_j$ is the $j$th <standard basis> vector.

Reversing several operations means undoing the last one first. For <invertible matrices> of the same size,
$$
(AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1},
$$
because $(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1})=A(BB^{-1})A^{-1}=I$, and the product in the other order is also $I$. This is why a <change-of-basis matrix> uses its <matrix inverse> to reverse the direction of coordinate conversion.

= Matrix inversion is not additive
{parent=Matrix inverse}
{title2=$(A+B)^{-1}\ne A^{-1}+B^{-1}$}

For $A=B=I$ of any positive size, $A+B=2I$ is invertible but $(A+B)^{-1}=I/2$, whereas $A^{-1}+B^{-1}=2I$. Thus even invertibility of all three matrices does not make inversion additive.

= Matrix commutation with an inverse
{parent=Matrix inverse}
{title2=$AB=BA\implies AB^{-1}=B^{-1}A$}

If two square <matrices> satisfy $AB=BA$ and $B$ is invertible, multiplication on the left and right by $B^{-1}$ gives $B^{-1}A=AB^{-1}$. Consequently a polynomial in a <matrix> commutes with the <matrix inverse> of any invertible polynomial in the same <matrix>.

= Block matrix inverse
{parent=Matrix inverse}
{title2=$M^{-1}$}

A <block matrix inverse> expresses the <matrix inverse> in terms of smaller <matrices>. Partition a <square matrix> over a <field> as
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\C&D\end{pmatrix},\qquad
A\in\mathbb F^{p\times p},\quad D\in\mathbb F^{q\times q}.
$$
If $A$ is an <invertible matrix>, define its <Schur complement> $S=D-CA^{-1}B$. Then $M$ is an <invertible matrix> exactly when $S$ is an <invertible matrix>, and
$$
\boxed{
M^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}
A^{-1}+A^{-1}BS^{-1}CA^{-1}&-A^{-1}BS^{-1}\\
-S^{-1}CA^{-1}&S^{-1}
\end{pmatrix}.
}
$$
The order of the <matrix products> matters. In particular, the lower-right block is $S^{-1}$, rather than generally $D^{-1}$: the coupling through $B$ and $C$ changes the response.<Convex Optimization by Stephen Boyd and Lieven Vandenberghe>[Appendix A.5.5, printed p. 650: inverse of a symmetric block matrix; §C.4.1, printed p. 673: elimination for general blocks]{ref}

For an original example, take
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}2&0&1\\0&3&2\\1&2&5\end{pmatrix},\quad
A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix},\quad
B=C^T=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix},\quad D=5.
$$
The <Schur complement> is $S=5-1/2-4/3=19/6$. Substitution gives the concise answer
$$
\boxed{M^{-1}=\frac1{19}\begin{pmatrix}11&2&-3\\2&9&-4\\-3&-4&6\end{pmatrix}}.
$$
Multiplying in either order gives the <identity matrix>; the lower-right entry is $6/19$, whereas $D^{-1}=1/5$. The difference records the feedback through the two eliminated variables.

\Image[block-matrix-schur-inverse-example.png]
{title=A <block matrix inverse> computed from the smaller <Schur complement>}
{description=Original exact example. Colours mark the two-by-two block $A$, the coupling blocks $B,C$, and the scalar block $D$. The same partition is retained in the <matrix inverse>; its blocks are not the entrywise inverses of the original blocks.}

Often the desired output is a solution of a <linear system>, rather than every entry of a <matrix inverse>. <Block elimination> solves with $A$ and then with $S$, recovering the eliminated variables by <back substitution>. Reusing an <LU decomposition> of $A$ avoids explicitly forming $A^{-1}$. A favourable <block diagonal matrix> or <tridiagonal matrix> block can make this useful; merely partitioning an unstructured dense <matrix> does not guarantee a faster solve.<Convex Optimization by Stephen Boyd and Lieven Vandenberghe>[§C.4.1, printed pp. 672–675: Algorithm C.4, factor-solve interpretation and dense complexity comparison]{ref}

= Block elimination
{parent=Block matrix inverse}
{title2=$Sy=g-CA^{-1}f$}

Write a <linear system> as $Ax+By=f$ and $Cx+Dy=g$, with $A$ an <invertible matrix>. Solving the first equation for $x$ and substituting in the second gives
$$
x=A^{-1}(f-By),\qquad
(D-CA^{-1}B)y=g-CA^{-1}f.
$$
Thus the <Schur complement> is the coefficient <matrix> remaining after the $x$ variables have been eliminated. When it is an <invertible matrix>, solve the reduced <linear system> for $y$ and recover $x$. In computation, expressions such as $A^{-1}B$ mean solving with each column of $B$, using the same <LU decomposition> or <Cholesky decomposition> when applicable.

For the example under <block matrix inverse> and right-hand side $(3,1,4)^T$, take $f=(3,1)^T$ and $g=4$. Since $CA^{-1}f=13/6$, the reduced equation is $(19/6)y=11/6$. <Back substitution> gives
$$
\boxed{(x_1,x_2,y)=(23,-1,11)/19}.
$$
The original equations return $3$, $1$ and $4$, respectively. The reduction retained the influence of $x_1,x_2$ on $y$ through the correction $CA^{-1}B$.

A short proof outline also explains whether $M$ is an <invertible matrix>: the exact <matrix factorization>
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\CA^{-1}&I\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\0&S\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}I&A^{-1}B\\0&I\end{pmatrix}
$$
has <invertible matrices> on the outside. Therefore $M$ is an <invertible matrix> precisely when the central <block diagonal matrix> is one. Reversing the factors gives the <block matrix inverse>, while taking <determinants> gives the <Schur complement determinant identity>.

= Choosing a different block pivot
{parent=Block matrix inverse}

An <invertible matrix> need not have an invertible chosen diagonal block. For example,
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad M^{-1}=M,
$$
has <singular matrices> as both one-by-one diagonal blocks. Neither diagonal-block formula applies in that partition. Interchanging the two rows produces the <identity matrix> and a usable <pivot element>. This is why a failed block pivot is not proof that the full <matrix> is a <singular matrix>; <pivoted LU decomposition> changes the order before eliminating.

Conversely, invertible diagonal blocks do not suffice: $M=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ has $A=D=1$ but <Schur complement> $S=0$, so it is a <singular matrix>. Both hypotheses in the <block matrix inverse> formula have work to do.

= Sherman–Morrison formula
{c}
{parent=Matrix inverse}
{wiki}
{title2=$(A+uv^T)^{-1}=A^{-1}-A^{-1}uv^TA^{-1}/(1+v^TA^{-1}u)$}

The <Sherman–Morrison formula> computes the <matrix inverse> after a <rank-one matrix update>. If $A$ is an <invertible matrix> and $\delta=1+v^TA^{-1}u\ne0$, then
$$
(A+uv^T)^{-1}
=A^{-1}-\frac{A^{-1}uv^TA^{-1}}{1+v^TA^{-1}u}.
$$
The inverse correction is itself an <outer product>, divided by the <scalar> $\delta$. The <matrix determinant lemma> shows that the nonzero-denominator condition is exactly the condition that the updated <matrix> remains an <invertible matrix>.

For $A=I$, $u=(1,2)^T$, and $v=(3,-1)^T$, we have $\delta=2$. Thus the computed inverse is
$$
\boxed{
\begin{pmatrix}4&-1\\6&-1\end{pmatrix}^{-1}
=I-\frac12\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\6&-2\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}-1/2&1/2\\-3&2\end{pmatrix}
}.
$$
Multiplication in either order gives the <identity matrix>. The result reuses the original inverse $I$ and the same correction <vectors>, rather than solving independently for all four inverse entries.

Often only the solution of an updated <linear system> is needed. If $Ax_0=b$ and $Az=u$, then
$$
(A+uv^T)x=b
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x=x_0-z\,\frac{v^Tx_0}{1+v^Tz},
$$
provided $1+v^Tz\ne0$. The two solves use the same <LU decomposition> of $A$. This expresses the computational benefit without requiring an explicitly stored <matrix inverse>. Strang's https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-065-matrix-methods-in-data-analysis-signal-processing-and-machine-learning-spring-2018/988b4c05680f40a5a4b3be7004a32500_XhSk_Lw2X_U.pdf[Lecture 14, transcript pp. 9–10] explains this reuse of the original factorization.

To see why the inverse correction works, first consider $A=I$ and put $s=v^Tu$. The <rank-one matrix> $R=uv^T$ satisfies $R^2=sR$. Expanding
$$
(I+R)\left(I-\frac{R}{1+s}\right)
=I+R-\frac{R}{1+s}-\frac{sR}{1+s}=I
$$
also works in the reverse order. The general case follows by factoring out $A$ and replacing $u$ by $A^{-1}u$. A matrix identity has reduced to cancellation of scalar coefficients.

Historically, Jack Sherman and Winifred J. Morrison studied updating an inverse when a single matrix entry changes in their https://doi.org/10.1214/aoms/1177729893[1950 paper, Adjustment of an Inverse Matrix Corresponding to a Change in One Element of a Given Matrix]{ref}. Such an entry change is the special <rank-one matrix update> $\alpha e_ie_j^T$, with <standard basis> <vectors> $e_i,e_j$. The reference and the earlier 1949 abstract on row or column changes are recorded in https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/1023004[Henderson and Searle, On Deriving the Inverse of a Sum of Matrices (1981), references 40–41]. This distinguishes the specific original setting from the more general $uv^T$ formula used here.

= Rank-one update singularity criterion
{parent=Sherman–Morrison formula}
{title2=$A+uv^T\text{ singular}\iff1+v^TA^{-1}u=0$}

For an <invertible matrix> $A$, the <rank-one matrix update> $B=A+uv^T$ is a <singular matrix> exactly when $1+v^TA^{-1}u=0$. This follows directly from the <matrix determinant lemma>. At that value, $u\ne0$ and the nonzero <vector> $w=A^{-1}u$ satisfies
$$
Bw=u+u(v^TA^{-1}u)=0.
$$
Every <vector> in the <kernel> is a multiple of $w$, since $Bx=0$ implies $x=-A^{-1}u(v^Tx)$. The <matrix rank> therefore drops by exactly one.

For $B(t)=I+tuv^T$ with $u=(1,2)^T$ and $v=(3,-1)^T$, the update becomes a <singular matrix> at $t=-1$, and $B(t)u=(1+t)u$. At $t=-0.99$, this direction is compressed by a factor $0.01$ and the inverse amplifies it by a factor 100. The actual <spectral norm> of the <matrix inverse>, which maximizes amplification over every direction, is about $700.17853409$.

\Image[sherman-morrison-near-singularity.png]
{title=An inverse grows large as the rank-one update approaches its singular denominator}
{description=Here $\delta=1+t>0$ and $B(t)=I+tuv^T$ uses the same example <vectors>. The left panel shows $\|B(t)^{-1}\|_2$ and its lower bound $1/\delta$, obtained from the direction $u$. The right panel shows the <condition number> $\kappa_2(B)=\|B\|_2\|B^{-1}\|_2$. Both are computed from <singular values>; the missing point $\delta=0$ has no <matrix inverse>.}

The exact formula can expose a large <condition number> in the updated <linear system> even when the original one had a small <condition number>. It does not guarantee <numerical stability> merely because its evaluation uses fewer arithmetic operations. The denominator condition certifies existence; the <condition number> and floating-point behavior address different questions about using the resulting answer.

= Solvability of an affine rank-one vector equation
{parent=Rank-one update singularity criterion}
{title2=$x=a+(b\cdot x)c$}

Let $a,b,c\in\mathbb R^n$, with $c\ne0$. The equation $x=a+(b\cdot x)c$ is the <linear system> $(I-cb^T)x=a$, a <rank-one matrix update> of the <identity matrix>. Every solution has $x=a+\lambda c$. Substitution reduces the entire problem to
$$
\lambda(1-b\cdot c)=b\cdot a.
$$
If $b\cdot c\ne1$, there is one solution, $x=a+c(b\cdot a)/(1-b\cdot c)$. If $b\cdot c=1$ and $b\cdot a=0$, every $\lambda\in\mathbb R$ works and the solution set is the <straight line> $a+\mathbb Rc$. If $b\cdot c=1$ and $b\cdot a\ne0$, there is no solution.

The first reduction follows directly from the original equation in any dimension. In three dimensions it can also be obtained by taking the <cross product> with $c$, giving $(x-a)\times c=0$. The singular denominator therefore represents two different possibilities, inconsistency or a family of solutions, and must not simply be discarded as an undefined formula.

= Pseudoinverse
{synonym}

The <Moore-Penrose inverse> extends the useful part of a <matrix inverse> to rectangular and singular <matrices>. A <linear system> $Ax=b$ may have no exact solution, or may have many. The <pseudoinverse> resolves both ambiguities: $x_*=A^+b$ is the <minimum-norm least-squares solution>. First minimize the <Euclidean norm> $\|Ax-b\|_2$; among all <vectors> attaining that minimum, choose the one with smallest <Euclidean norm> $\|x\|_2$. It does not pretend that inconsistent equations can all be satisfied.

For $A\in\mathbb C^{m\times n}$, its <Moore-Penrose inverse> is the unique $n\times m$ <matrix> satisfying the four <Penrose equations>. The <singular value decomposition> gives a direct construction:
$$
A=U\Sigma V^*,
\qquad
\boxed{A^+=V\Sigma^+U^*.}
$$
Here $U,V$ are <unitary matrices>, the star is the <conjugate transpose>, and $\Sigma^+$ is the $n\times m$ rectangular <diagonal matrix> obtained by replacing each nonzero <singular value> $\sigma_j$ by $1/\sigma_j$ and leaving zeros at zero. Over the <real numbers>, use <orthogonal matrices> and <transposes>. The <pseudoinverse> reverses the stretches that can be reversed; it discards output components the original <linear map> cannot produce and does not invent an input component in its <kernel>. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06sc-linear-algebra-fall-2011/resources/mit18_06scf11_ses3-8sum/[Strang, MIT 18.06SC: Left and Right Inverses; Pseudoinverse]{ref}

The two products have especially clear meanings:
$$
AA^+=P_{\operatorname{ran}A},
\qquad
A^+A=P_{(\ker A)^\perp}.
$$
They are <orthogonal projection matrices>: $AA^+$ selects the producible part of an output, while $A^+A$ selects the detectable part of an input. They are generally not <identity matrices>. For an <invertible matrix>, both are identities and $A^+=A^{-1}$.

This gives a useful order of operations for <least-squares solutions>: project the requested output onto the <column space>, then invert the restriction of $A$ from $(\ker A)^\perp$ to its <column space>. Adding a <kernel> <vector> leaves the residual unchanged but increases the squared <Euclidean norm> by the <Pythagorean theorem in an inner-product space>, because $A^+b$ is <orthogonal> to that <kernel>. The <pseudoinverse> therefore provides one canonical answer without hiding either lost information or irreconcilable data.

The name records a history with several contributors. E. H. Moore gave an earlier generalization in 1920; M. H. Pearl's 1966 paper records that work, Arne Bjerhammar's 1951 rediscovery for maximal-rank rectangular <matrices>, and Roger Penrose's independent 1955 treatment. https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/mathematical-proceedings-of-the-cambridge-philosophical-society/article/abs/on-generalized-inverses-of-matrices/9CD637F604C76ECD8D03AC029212D56E[Pearl, On generalized inverses of matrices (1966), introduction and references 6–9]{ref} Penrose's original paper proves existence and uniqueness from the four <Penrose equations> for arbitrary complex <matrices>, including rectangular ones. https://faculty.kfupm.edu.sa/math/jaafarm/lec-notes/Moore-Pinrose.pdf[Penrose, A generalized inverse for matrices (1955), Theorem 1, pp. 406–408]{ref}

= Overdetermined least-squares example
{parent=Moore-Penrose inverse}
{title2=$x_*=(2,3)^T$}

Suppose three measurements constrain two unknowns:
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
b=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\6\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The <linear system> asks for $x_1=1$, $x_2=2$, and $x_1+x_2=6$, which are incompatible. A <least-squares solution> makes a balanced compromise. The <matrix> has <full column rank>, so
$$
A^+=(A^TA)^{-1}A^T
=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&1\\-1&2&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\boxed{x_*=A^+b=(2,3)^T.}
$$
The fitted output and residual are
$$
Ax_*=(2,3,5)^T,
\qquad
r=b-Ax_*=(-1,-1,1)^T,
\qquad
A^Tr=0.
$$
Thus the two direct measurements are each overshot by one, and the measured sum is undershot by one. The residual has <Euclidean norm> $\sqrt3$ and is <orthogonal> to the <column space>, the <plane> $z=y_1+y_2$ in output coordinates $(y_1,y_2,z)$.

\Image[pseudoinverse-least-squares-projection.png]
{title=The pseudoinverse projects inconsistent data onto the attainable output plane}
{description=For $b=(1,2,6)^T$, the closest attainable output is $Ax_*=(2,3,5)^T$. The segment between them is the residual $(-1,-1,1)^T$, perpendicular to the <column space>. The drawing is an orthonormal two-dimensional slice of output space; the attainable <plane> intersects this slice in the blue <straight line>.}

For any perturbation $h\in\mathbb R^2$, the <orthogonality> of $r$ to $Ah$ gives
$$
\|b-A(x_*+h)\|_2^2=\|r\|_2^2+\|Ah\|_2^2
=3+\|Ah\|_2^2.
$$
Since the <kernel> of $A$ is trivial, every nonzero $h$ increases the residual. The <least-squares solution> is unique in this example. The formula using $A^TA$ is convenient for these small exact calculations; an actual numerical computation can use a <QR decomposition> or <singular value decomposition> to avoid the extra conditioning cost of the <least-squares normal equations>. For <full column rank>, the squared <singular values> of $A$ are the <eigenvalues> of $A^TA$, so its <condition number> in the <Euclidean norm> is $\kappa_2(A^TA)=\kappa_2(A)^2$. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-065-matrix-methods-in-data-analysis-signal-processing-and-machine-learning-spring-2018/resources/lecture-9-four-ways-to-solve-least-squares-problems/[Strang, MIT 18.065 Lecture 9: four ways to solve least-squares problems]{ref}

= Underdetermined minimum-norm example
{parent=Moore-Penrose inverse}
{title2=$x_*=(3/5,6/5)^T$}

Now take one equation for two unknowns:
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\end{pmatrix},
\qquad b=3.
$$
The <linear system> $x_1+2x_2=3$ has an entire <affine line> of exact solutions. Since $A$ has <full row rank>, its <Moore-Penrose inverse> is
$$
A^+=A^T(AA^T)^{-1}
=\frac15\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\boxed{x_*=A^+b=(3/5,6/5)^T.}
$$
The <kernel> is $\operatorname{span}\{(-2,1)^T\}$, and all exact solutions are
$$
x=x_*+t(-2,1)^T,\qquad t\in\mathbb R.
$$
Because $x_*$ is <orthogonal> to $(-2,1)^T$,
$$
\|x\|_2^2=\frac95+5t^2.
$$
The <Euclidean norm> is therefore smallest exactly at $t=0$. The zero residual alone cannot select a solution; the minimum-norm rule supplies the additional criterion.

\Image[pseudoinverse-minimum-norm-line.png]
{title=The minimum-norm solution is the closest point of the solution line to the origin}
{description=The <affine line> $x_1+2x_2=3$ is perpendicular to $x_*=(3/5,6/5)^T$. Moving along the line changes only a <kernel> component and increases $\|x\|_2^2$ by $5t^2$.}

This selection depends on the specified <inner product>. Rescaling one unknown changes what counts as a small <Euclidean norm>, so units and weighting matter in an application. A minimum-norm answer is mathematically canonical for the chosen geometry, rather than an automatic guarantee of physical plausibility.

= Small singular values in the Moore-Penrose inverse
{parent=Moore-Penrose inverse}
{title2=$\|A^+\|_2=1/\sigma_{\min,+}$}

For a nonzero <matrix> $A$, the <operator norm> induced by the <Euclidean norm> satisfies $\|A^+\|_2=1/\sigma_{\min,+}$, where $\sigma_{\min,+}$ is its smallest positive <singular value>. The <singular value decomposition> makes the danger visible: a small nonzero stretch becomes a large amplification of errors when inverted.

For instance, when $\varepsilon>0$,
$$
A_\varepsilon=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&\varepsilon\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
A_\varepsilon^+=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1/\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}.
$$
A data perturbation $(0,\eta)^T$ changes the reconstructed input by $(0,\eta/\varepsilon)^T$. At $\varepsilon=0$, however, the <pseudoinverse> is $\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$: exactly zero and merely small are different cases. Thus the <pseudoinverse> need not be <continuous> across a change of <matrix rank>. With uncertain data, <truncated singular value decomposition> or <Tikhonov regularization> can trade exact inversion of weak directions for stability; the cutoff or penalty adds a modelling choice.

= Moore--Penrose inverse of a Hilbert-space operator
{c}
{parent=Moore-Penrose inverse}

For a bounded operator $A:X\to Y$ between <Hilbert spaces>, $A^\dagger$ has domain $\mathcal R(A)\oplus\mathcal R(A)^\perp$, maps into $\mathcal N(A)^\perp$, and inverts the restriction of $A$ to $\mathcal N(A)^\perp$. It is bounded exactly when $\mathcal R(A)$ is closed.

= Penrose equations
{c}
{parent=Moore-Penrose inverse}

The Moore--Penrose inverse is characterized by
$$
AA^\dagger A=A,
\quad
A^\dagger AA^\dagger=A^\dagger,
\quad
(AA^\dagger)^*=AA^\dagger,
\quad
(A^\dagger A)^*=A^\dagger A.
$$

Here $A^\dagger=A^+$ denotes the <Moore-Penrose inverse>, while the star denotes the <conjugate transpose>. The first two identities express consistency when applying and reversing the <linear map>; the last two make the resulting <orthogonal projection matrices> <Hermitian>. They must hold together. For example, $A=(1\ \ 2)$ and $B=(1\ \ 0)^T$ satisfy $ABA=A$ and $BAB=B$, but
$$
BA=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&0\end{pmatrix}
$$
is not <Hermitian>. This $B$ is a <right inverse>, yet $Bb=(3,0)^T$ is not the <minimum-norm least-squares solution> for $b=3$. The last two <Penrose equations> impose the <orthogonality> that selects the <pseudoinverse>.

= Reverse-order law for the Moore--Penrose inverse
{c}
{parent=Moore-Penrose inverse}

The identity $(AB)^\dagger=B^\dagger A^\dagger$ does not hold for arbitrary matrices. A sufficient condition is that $A$ have <full column rank> and $B$ have <full row rank>.

= Push-through identity
{parent=Matrix inverse}

For a rectangular matrix $X$ and $\lambda>0$,
$$
X^T(XX^T+\lambda I)^{-1}=(X^TX+\lambda I)^{-1}X^T.
$$
Multiplying either side by $XX^T+\lambda I$ verifies the identity.
