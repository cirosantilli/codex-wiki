= Quantum field theory
{wiki}

Quantum field theory combines <special relativity> with <quantum theory> by treating fields as operator-valued distributions and particles as field excitations.

= Quantum field
{parent=Quantum field theory}
{title2=$\Phi(x)$}

A <quantum field> is an operator-valued <distribution> on spacetime: smearing $\Phi(x)$ against a smooth test function gives an operator, whereas pointwise products can require renormalization. For example, a free <complex scalar field> contains particle <annihilation operators> and <antiparticle> <creation operators>. Its <microcausality> condition makes field commutators vanish at spacelike separation. For <fermions>, the corresponding locality condition uses anticommutators. This combines quantum operators with the spacetime locality of a <quantum field theory>.

= Algebraic quantum field theory
{parent=Quantum field theory}
{wiki}

<Algebraic quantum field theory> assigns an observable algebra $\mathcal A(O)$ to each suitable spacetime region $O$. Inclusion of regions implies inclusion of algebras, and spacelike-separated algebras commute under <microcausality>. A state is a positive normalized functional on the observable algebra, so its restriction to a region specifies expectation values and correlations for that algebra. <Spectrum conditions> restrict translation representations, while <primitive causality> and <time-slice axioms> constrain the dynamics; these are different assumptions.

= Time-slice axiom
{parent=Algebraic quantum field theory}

The <time-slice axiom> says that an algebra generated in an arbitrarily thin suitable neighbourhood of a Cauchy surface generates the algebra of its causal development. For $O$ such a neighbourhood in its development $D(O)$, this is $\mathcal A(O)=\mathcal A(D(O))$. Precise global and local versions can differ in the regions to which they apply. For a local hyperbolic field equation, later fields can be represented using the initial field and its conjugate momentum in the corresponding <domain of dependence>.

= Primitive causality
{parent=Algebraic quantum field theory}

<Primitive causality> is a dynamical completeness condition: the state restricted to a suitable initial spacetime region determines its expectations throughout that region's domain of dependence. Algebraically one requires the later domain-of-dependence observable algebra to be contained in the initial algebra, under the identifications supplied by the dynamics. A local <time-slice axiom> gives this property for a neighbourhood of a Cauchy slice. Knowledge of the full restricted state, including correlations, is required; knowing only mean initial fields is insufficient.

= Spectrum condition
{parent=Algebraic quantum field theory}
{title2=$\operatorname{Sp}(P)\subseteq\overline V_+$}

In a translation-covariant quantum field theory on Minkowski spacetime, the joint spectrum of the energy-momentum generators is required to lie in the closed future light cone. With signature $(+,-,-,-)$ this means $p^0\ge0$ and $p^2\ge0$. The <spectrum condition> implements a positive-energy stability requirement in every inertial frame. It constrains the Fourier support and analyticity of vacuum correlation functions but does not by itself assert spacelike commutation or an initial-value property.

= Positive-energy spectrum condition
{parent=Quantum field theory}
{title2=$\operatorname{spec}P^\mu\subseteq\overline V_+$}

The joint energy-momentum spectrum lies in the closed forward <light cone>. In axiomatic <quantum field theory>, this condition permits analytic continuation of <Wightman functions> in suitable complex spacetime domains. Together with <Lorentz invariance>, <microcausality> and the positivity of the physical state space, it is one of the hypotheses behind the <CPT theorem>.

= Canonical transformation of mode operators
{parent=Quantum field theory}
{title2=$a'_i=\sum_j(U_{ij}a_j+V_{ij}a_j^\dagger)$}

A linear change of <creation operators> and <annihilation operators> is canonical when it preserves their defining brackets. For finitely many bosonic modes, the <canonical commutation relations> require $UU^\dagger-VV^\dagger=I$ and $UV^T-VU^T=0$. For fermions, the <canonical anticommutation relations> instead require $UU^\dagger+VV^\dagger=I$ and $UV^T+VU^T=0$. A <Bogoliubov transformation> uses such mixing to diagonalize a quadratic <Hamiltonian> or relate different vacuum descriptions. Infinite-mode <unitary> implementation has further conditions, as in <bosonic mode mixing implementability>.

= Bogoliubov transformation
{c}
{parent=Canonical transformation of mode operators}
{wiki}

A <Bogoliubov transformation> is a <canonical transformation of mode operators> mixing <creation operators> and <annihilation operators>. It diagonalizes quadratic <Hamiltonians> or relates different vacuum descriptions. Bosonic and fermionic canonical bracket constraints differ. A nonzero creation-mixing coefficient makes its vacuum contain particles relative to the original mode decomposition.

= Nambu spinor
{c}
{parent=Bogoliubov transformation}
{title2=$\Psi_k=(c_k,c_{-k}^\dagger)^T$}

A <Nambu spinor> groups an <annihilation operator> with an opposite-momentum <creation operator> to express pairing in a quadratic <Hamiltonian> as a matrix problem. Its components are not independent: the spinor at $-k$ is related to conjugated components at $k$. This redundancy must be accounted for when summing energies and <normal ordering> constants. Component phases are a convention and change the displayed off-diagonal matrix entries.

= Self-paired spinless fermion mode
{parent=Nambu spinor}
{title2=$k\equiv-k\pmod{2\pi}$}

In a unit-spacing periodic spinless chain, self-paired <momenta> are $k=0$ and, when available, $k=\pi$. An anomalous pair of the same <creation operator> vanishes by the <canonical anticommutation relations>. Such a mode is a single normal number level, filled or empty according to its energy sign, rather than a two-distinct-mode <Bogoliubov transformation>. This excludes the paired-angle construction at those special <momenta>.

= Bosonic Nambu normal-ordering shift
{parent=Nambu spinor}
{title2=$a_ka_k^\dagger=1+a_k^\dagger a_k$}

In a bosonic <Nambu spinor> expression, the second diagonal entry commonly contains $a_{-k}a_{-k}^\dagger$. The <canonical commutation relations> add one constant per mode when this is normal ordered. For an antiferromagnetic chain coefficient $JS$, rewriting the quadratic <Hamiltonian> as a full Nambu sum therefore changes the exterior constant from $-NJS^2$ to $-NJS(S+1)$. Omitting the shift corrupts the <ground-state energy> even when the dispersion is unchanged.

= Ising-chain Nambu spinor
{c}
{parent=Nambu spinor}
{title2=$\Psi_k=(c_k,-ic_{-k}^\dagger)^T$}

With Fourier convention $c_n=N^{-1/2}\sum_ke^{-ikn}c_k$, the <transverse-field Ising model> has $H=J\sum_k\Psi_k^\dagger[(g-\cos k)\sigma_z-\sin k\,\sigma_x]\Psi_k$. Expanding the spinor retains the constant $-JgN$ and pairs $k$ with $-k$. The physical <quasiparticle> energy is twice the positive matrix <eigenvalue> times $J$, because both <momentum> partners occur in the Nambu sum.

= Bosonic mode mixing implementability
{parent=Bogoliubov transformation}
{title2=$\sum_{i,j}|\beta_{ij}|^2<\infty$}

A bounded invertible bosonic <Bogoliubov transformation> is unitarily implementable in the usual <bosonic Fock space> precisely when its creation-mixing part is a <Hilbert-Schmidt operator>. Without this condition, the transformation still preserves the algebraic <canonical commutation relations>, but its two vacua need not be vectors in a common <Fock space>. Equal nonzero squeezing in countably many independent modes is a simple counterexample.

= Bosonic Bogoliubov diagonalization
{parent=Bogoliubov transformation}
{title2=$\omega_k=\sqrt{A_k^2-B_k^2}$}

For real even coefficients with $A_k>|B_k|$, the quadratic <Hamiltonian>
$$
H=\sum_k\left[A_ka_k^\dagger a_k+\frac{B_k}{2}(a_ka_{-k}+a_k^\dagger a_{-k}^\dagger)\right]
$$
is diagonalized by the <Bogoliubov transformation> $a_k=\cosh\theta_k\,\alpha_k-\sinh\theta_k\,\alpha_{-k}^\dagger$, with $\tanh2\theta_k=B_k/A_k$. The <canonical commutation relations> follow from $\cosh^2\theta_k-\sinh^2\theta_k=1$. The diagonal form is $\sum_k[\omega_k\alpha_k^\dagger\alpha_k+(\omega_k-A_k)/2]$, with $\omega_k=\sqrt{A_k^2-B_k^2}$. The original-mode vacuum depletion is $\sinh^2\theta_k=(A_k/\omega_k-1)/2$. At $A_k=|B_k|$, the exact zero mode requires separate infrared treatment.

= Bosonic Bogoliubov zero-point shift
{parent=Bosonic Bogoliubov diagonalization}
{title2=$\Delta E_0=\tfrac12\sum_k(E_k-A_k)$}

Canonical diagonalization of $A_kb_k^\dagger b_k$ plus an anomalous pair term gives the vacuum shift $(E_k-A_k)/2$ per momentum. Thus $H=C+\frac12\sum_k(E_k-A_k)+\sum_kE_k\alpha_k^\dagger\alpha_k$. The subtraction of $A_k/2$ accompanies the <quasiparticle> zero-point term; omitting it changes the ground-state energy.

= Canonical identities for a bosonic Bogoliubov transformation
{parent=Bogoliubov transformation}
{title2=$AA^\dagger-BB^\dagger=I,\quad AB^T=BA^T$}

For mode mixing $\psi'_i=\sum_j(A_{ij}\psi_j+B_{ij}\overline\psi_j)$, the <Klein-Gordon inner product> extracts $a'_i=\sum_j(\overline A_{ij}a_j-\overline B_{ij}a_j^\dagger)$. The <canonical commutation relation> is preserved exactly when $AA^\dagger-BB^\dagger=I$ and $AB^T=BA^T$. These follow by computing $[a'_i,a_k'{}^\dagger]$ and $[a'_i,a'_k]$; they are also the positive- and negative-frequency mode orthonormality identities. In infinitely many modes, convergence and the existence of a common <bosonic Fock space> representation require additional analysis; the expected total particle number is finite only if $B$ is a <Hilbert-Schmidt operator>.

= In-vacuum and out-vacuum
{parent=Bogoliubov transformation}

If a background is stationary in the remote past and future, positive-frequency modes in those two regions define an in-vacuum and an out-vacuum. The intervening time dependence generally makes the two mode bases differ by a <Bogoliubov transformation>.

= Out-vacuum
{parent=In-vacuum and out-vacuum}
{title2=$b_i|0_{\rm out}\rangle=0$}

The state annihilated by every annihilation operator of the positive-frequency mode basis chosen in the asymptotic future. It need not coincide with the <in-vacuum> on a time-dependent spacetime.

= In-vacuum
{parent=In-vacuum and out-vacuum}
{title2=$a_i|0_{\rm in}\rangle=0$}

The state annihilated by every annihilation operator of the positive-frequency mode basis chosen in the asymptotic past. A <Bogoliubov transformation> can make it contain particles when tested by future out-mode <number operators>.

= Particle number from Bogoliubov coefficients
{parent=In-vacuum and out-vacuum}

If
$$
u_i^{\rm out}=\sum_j(\alpha_{ij}u_j^{\rm in}+\beta_{ij}u_j^{{\rm in}*}),
$$
then the expected out-particle number in mode $i$ in the in-vacuum is
$$
\langle0_{\rm in}|N_i^{\rm out}|0_{\rm in}\rangle
=\sum_j|\beta_{ij}|^2.
$$

= Out-mode number fluctuations in the in-vacuum
{parent=Particle number from Bogoliubov coefficients}
{title2=$\langle N_s^2\rangle=2n_s^2+n_s+|c_s|^2$}

If $b_s=\sum_j(A_{sj}a_j+B_{sj}a_j^\dagger)$ and the state is the <in-vacuum>, put $n_s=\sum_j|B_{sj}|^2$ and $c_s=\sum_j A_{sj}B_{sj}$. The <canonical commutation relations> give $N_s^2=b_s^{\dagger2}b_s^2+N_s$. Acting with $b_s^2$ on the vacuum produces $c_s|0\rangle$ and a two-particle vector whose squared norm is $2n_s^2$, because its two possible vacuum contractions have equal contributions. This proves the displayed second moment and $\operatorname{Var}(N_s)=n_s(n_s+1)+|c_s|^2$. A thermal-mode formula requires $c_s=0$; a general <squeezed vacuum state> need not satisfy that restriction.

= Sudden frequency quench
{parent=Bogoliubov transformation}

For an oscillator whose positive frequency jumps from $\omega_-$ to $\omega_+$, continuity of the normalized mode and its first derivative gives the Bogoliubov coefficients
$$
\alpha=\frac{\omega_++\omega_-}{2\sqrt{\omega_+\omega_-}},
\qquad
\beta=\frac{\omega_+-\omega_-}{2\sqrt{\omega_+\omega_-}}.
$$
The initial vacuum therefore contains $|\beta|^2$ final particles in that mode.

= Massless limit
{parent=Quantum field theory}
{title2=$m\to0$}

A limit of a theory or correlator as a mass parameter tends to zero. It can be singular because of infrared effects or changing degrees of freedom, so it must be justified before neglecting a <running mass>. The <renormalization-group mass suppression criterion> controls the mass-to-energy ratio but does not by itself prove existence of this limit.

= Integrable quantum field theory
{parent=Quantum field theory}

A <quantum field theory> with enough mutually compatible conserved quantities to constrain its dynamics exactly. In massive relativistic theories in one spatial dimension, suitable higher-spin conserved charges imply elastic <factorized scattering>: the set of incoming <rapidities> is preserved and multiparticle amplitudes are assembled from two-particle <S-matrices>.

= Factorized scattering
{parent=Integrable quantum field theory}

Multiparticle <quantum scattering> is factorized when it decomposes into two-body scatterings without particle production or a new irreducible many-body amplitude. The independence of the answer from the order of exchanges imposes the <Yang-Baxter equation>. Classical <pairwise additivity of soliton shifts> is a corresponding property of integrable <soliton> collisions.

= Bound-state fusion of factorized S-matrices
{parent=Factorized scattering}
{title2=$S_{C,D}(\vartheta)=S_{A,D}(\vartheta+iu/2)S_{A,D}(\vartheta-iu/2)$}

= Bootstrap fusion
{synonym}

= Bound-state S-matrix fusion
{synonym}

For an equal-mass bound pair $C=(AA)$ at fusion <rapidity> $iu$, scatter a third particle off its two constituents with shifted <rapidities> and project onto the bound-state residue. Scalar amplitudes give the displayed product. With internal indices, bound-state coupling tensors perform the projection. The <Yang-Baxter equation> makes different scattering orders consistent; factorization eliminates independent many-body scattering contributions.

= Three-particle bound state from equal-mass fusion
{parent=Bound-state fusion of factorized S-matrices}
{title2=$m_3=m\sin(3u/2)/\sin(u/2)$}

For the <unit-modulus hyperbolic scattering block> with elementary <mass> $m$, a pair bound at <rapidity> difference $iu$ has <mass> $m_2=2m\cos(u/2)$. <Bootstrap fusion> with another elementary particle gives a further direct <pole> at $3iu/2$. It is inside the <physical rapidity strip> with positive imaginary <residue> only for $0<u<2\pi/3$. In that range the new <bound state> has rest-frame constituent <rapidities> $iu,0,-iu$, so its energy is $m(1+2\cos u)=m\sin(3u/2)/\sin(u/2)$. Charges add at the bound-state vertex. At $u=2\pi/3$ the apparent pole cancels; beyond that point it lies outside the physical strip. The formula is conditional on this particular scattering block and its direct-pole interpretation, not a universal three-body binding law.

= O(N)-invariant S-matrix
{parent=Factorized scattering}
{c}
{title2=$S=S_2I+S_3P+S_1K$}

For particles in the <vector representation> of the <orthogonal group>, the two-body <S-matrix> is a linear combination of the identity $I$, permutation $P$ and trace contraction $K$: $S=S_2I+S_3P+S_1K$. Here $K(e_i\otimes e_j)=\delta_{ij}\sum_a e_a\otimes e_a$, $P^2=I$, $PK=KP=K$ and $K^2=NK$. The <orthogonal-invariant scattering channels> diagonalize these three operators simultaneously.

= Orthogonal-invariant scattering channels
{parent=O(N)-invariant S-matrix}
{title2=$s_0=NS_1+S_2+S_3,\quad s_\pm=S_2\pm S_3$}

The two-vector <tensor product> splits into a trace singlet, symmetric traceless tensors and antisymmetric tensors. For an <O(N)-invariant S-matrix>, the corresponding <eigenvalues> are $s_0=NS_1+S_2+S_3$, $s_+=S_2+S_3$ and $s_-=S_2-S_3$. For $N\ge2$, analytic <unitarity> requires $s_a(\theta)s_a(-\theta)=1$. With <Hermitian analyticity of a two-particle S-matrix>, each channel has unit modulus on the real <rapidity> axis.

= Yang-Baxter equation
{parent=Factorized scattering}
{c}
{wiki=Yang–Baxter_equation}
{title2=$R_{12}(u)R_{13}(u+v)R_{23}(v)=R_{23}(v)R_{13}(u+v)R_{12}(u)$}

For a two-body operator $R(u)$ acting on a <tensor product> of particle species spaces, the spectral equation is $R_{12}(u)R_{13}(u+v)R_{23}(v)=R_{23}(v)R_{13}(u+v)R_{12}(u)$. It guarantees agreement between the two ways to reorder three particles in <factorized scattering>. A <Yang–Baxter operator> is a related braid-form operator; multiplying by the permutation operator converts between the braided and unbraided conventions.

= Yang-Baxter R-matrix
{c}
{parent=Yang-Baxter equation}
{title2=$R\in\operatorname{GL}(V\otimes V)$}

= R-matrix
{c}
{synonym}

An invertible linear map on $V\otimes V$ is an R-matrix when it obeys $R_{12}R_{13}R_{23}=R_{23}R_{13}R_{12}$ on $V^{\otimes3}$. Here $R_{13}$ acts on the first and third factors. The flipped operator $PR$ obeys the braid relation. This numerical-matrix notion is distinguished from a universal quasitriangular element in a Hopf algebra tensor square.

= Faddeev-Zamolodchikov algebra
{parent=Factorized scattering}
{c}
{title2=$A_i(\theta_1)A_j(\theta_2)=S_{ij}^{kl}(\theta_{12})A_l(\theta_2)A_k(\theta_1)$}

= Zamolodchikov-Faddeev algebra
{c}
{synonym}

Particle operators obey a rapidity-dependent exchange rule $A_i(\theta_1)A_j(\theta_2)=S_{ij}^{kl}(\theta_1-\theta_2)A_l(\theta_2)A_k(\theta_1)$, with repeated species indices summed. A consistent <associative algebra> must give the same coefficients when a triple product is brought to decreasing or increasing <rapidity> order by either sequence of adjacent exchanges. This is the <Faddeev-Zamolodchikov associativity constraint>. Rapidity ordering here is distinct from the creation/annihilation convention of ordinary <normal ordering>.

= Faddeev-Zamolodchikov associativity constraint
{parent=Faddeev-Zamolodchikov algebra}
{c}

Starting with three ordered <rapidities>, the adjacent-exchange sequences $121$ and $212$ must give identical coefficients for every final species word in the <Faddeev-Zamolodchikov algebra>. The exchange parameters are $\theta$, $\theta+\theta'$ and $\theta'$ because rapidity differences add. These coefficient identities express the spectral <Yang-Baxter equation>. One scalar identity is necessary but generally does not exhaust all the constraints.

= Local quantum field operator
{parent=Quantum field theory}
{title2=$\mathcal O(x)$}

A local quantum field operator is supported at a spacetime point, with operator-valued distributions understood by smearing. Products at the same point generally require <renormalization>; these give <composite operators>. This is distinct from the topos-theoretic meaning of <local operator>.

= Composite operator
{parent=Local quantum field operator}
{title2=$\mathcal O_R(x)$}

= Composite local operator
{synonym}

A composite operator is a renormalized local product of quantum fields and their derivatives. Operators with compatible quantum numbers can undergo <composite operator mixing>, and products at coincident insertion points can need <contact terms in a correlation function>.

= Contact term in a correlation function
{parent=Composite operator}

= Contact terms in a correlation function
{synonym}

A contact term is a distribution supported where insertion points coincide. Its <Fourier transform> is local in momentum, typically polynomial. Renormalizing an operator product can require additive contact counterterms even if each operator has zero <anomalous dimension>.

= Composite operator mixing
{parent=Composite operator}
{title2=$\mathcal O_B=Z\mathcal O_R$}

Renormalization can relate bare operators to a matrix of renormalized operators, $\mathcal O_{i,B}=Z_{ij}\mathcal O_{j,R}$. The corresponding <anomalous dimension> matrix acts on each insertion slot in the <Callan-Symanzik equation>.

= Soft graviton theorem
{c}
{parent=Quantum field theory}
{wiki}

The soft graviton theorem factorizes an amplitude with one low-energy graviton into the hard amplitude times a universal sum over external momenta. Gauge invariance of the soft factor requires energy-momentum conservation and universal gravitational coupling.

= Weinberg soft theorem
{c}
{parent=Soft graviton theorem}

The Weinberg soft theorem constrains interactions of a massless particle by requiring its soft-emission amplitude to be invariant under shifts of an unphysical polarization. For helicity one it gives charge conservation, for helicity two it gives universal gravitational coupling, and for higher helicity it obstructs generic long-range interactions.

= Effective field theory
{title2=EFT}
{parent=Quantum field theory}
{wiki}

An effective field theory describes phenomena below a chosen energy scale by retaining the relevant light fields and organizing local interactions by increasing suppression at that scale.

= Effective operator
{parent=Effective field theory}
{title2=$C_i\mathcal O_i/M^{\Delta_i-4}$}

An effective operator is a local interaction built from the retained fields in an <effective field theory>. Its <coefficient> records effects of heavier degrees of freedom, and allowed operators respect the relevant <symmetries>. In four dimensions an operator of <mass dimension> $\Delta_i$ has the displayed coefficient for dimensionless $C_i$ and a heavy <mass> scale $M$. For example a dimension-six <proton-decay operator> has a coefficient proportional to $M^{-2}$.

= Low-energy effective action
{parent=Effective field theory}

A low-energy effective action retains the light degrees of freedom and describes the effects of heavier ones through local interactions and a <derivative expansion>. It can be obtained by matching an <effective field theory> or by a low-energy <compactification>. A <Wilsonian effective action> implements integration of high-energy modes with a scale cutoff. The <quantum effective action> that generates <one-particle-irreducible vertices> is a different construction; the two need not coincide.

= Operator basis in effective field theory
{parent=Effective field theory}

= Operator bases in effective field theory
{synonym}

An <operator basis in effective field theory> is an independent set of allowed <local field operators> at a chosen order, modulo relevant algebraic identities, <integration by parts> and permitted <field redefinitions>. <Redundant operators> can be removed without changing on-shell amplitudes, provided their coefficient shifts are accounted for. The basis depends on the light fields and symmetries.

= Matching in effective field theory
{parent=Effective field theory}

<Matching in effective field theory> fixes <Wilson coefficients> by requiring a low-energy theory and its <ultraviolet completion> to give the same low-energy amplitudes through a specified order. Both calculations must treat shared infrared physics consistently. Contributions from retained light particles are reproduced inside the <effective field theory>; their common nonanalytic parts are not replaced by arbitrary local heavy coefficients.

= Wilson coefficient
{c}
{parent=Effective field theory}

A Wilson coefficient weights a local operator in an <effective field theory>. For an operator of <mass dimension> $d$ in four spacetime dimensions, a dimensionless coefficient is conventionally accompanied by $\Lambda^{4-d}$. The coefficient encodes short-distance dynamics and can be a matrix in flavour space.

= Ultraviolet completion
{parent=Effective field theory}

An ultraviolet completion specifies a theory valid at higher energies whose low-energy limit gives an <effective field theory>. For large-field <cosmic inflation>, it may also need to control the potential over a large field range, which is distinct from the energy scale of a process.

= Planck-suppressed corrections to inflaton potentials
{c}
{parent=Effective field theory}
{title2=$\Delta V=\sum_{p>4}c_p\phi^p/M_{\rm Pl}^{p-4}$}

In an <effective field theory>, higher-power terms in an <inflaton> <scalar potential> can be important when $\phi/M_{\rm Pl}$ is large, even if $V/M_{\rm Pl}^4$ is small. An assumed simple <monomial inflation potential> over such a field range needs a <symmetry> or <ultraviolet completion> controlling these operators and <radiative corrections>. A large field value alone does not establish Planckian <energy density> or invalidate every possible completion.

= Local operator
{disambiguate=physics}
{parent=Effective field theory}

= Local field operator
{synonym}

A local field operator is built from fields and finitely many of their derivatives at one spacetime point. Its integral can deform an action, with the operator's scaling dimension determining the engineering dimension of the associated coupling.

= Heavy-field decoupling
{parent=Effective field theory}

Heavy-field decoupling says that a field of mass $M$ affects processes with external momenta $p\ll M$ through renormalizations of existing low-energy couplings and local higher-dimensional operators suppressed by powers of $p/M$.

= Ultraviolet cutoff
{title2=$\Lambda$}
{parent=Effective field theory}
{wiki}

An ultraviolet cutoff removes modes above a chosen energy or momentum scale. An <effective field theory> encodes the influence of those omitted short-distance modes in its couplings.

= Infrared divergence
{parent=Effective field theory}
{wiki}

An infrared divergence is a divergence caused by contributions from arbitrarily low energies or long wavelengths.

= Infrared-safe observable
{parent=Infrared divergence}

An observable unchanged by adding a particle with vanishing energy or replacing one massless particle by exactly collinear particles with the same total momentum. This property permits cancellation of real and virtual <infrared divergences> and <collinear divergences> in its perturbative cross-section. Fully inclusive electron-positron hadronic rates and suitably defined jet observables are examples.

= Collinear divergence
{parent=Infrared divergence}

A singularity of a massless scattering or radiation integral when momenta of two particles become parallel. In <quantum chromodynamics>, unresolved quark-gluon splittings cause such divergences. A mass regulator or <dimensional regularization> exposes them; they cancel between suitable inclusive real and virtual contributions or are absorbed into factorized distributions where appropriate.

= Quadratic Lagrangian
{parent=Effective field theory}

A quadratic Lagrangian contains terms of second order in field fluctuations and determines their linearized propagators and dispersion relations.

= Amplitude mode

A Higgs mode is a fluctuation in the magnitude of a complex or multicomponent <order parameter>, as distinct from a phase or orientational fluctuation.

= Integrating out a field
{parent=Effective field theory}

Integrating out a field means performing its path integral while retaining the other fields as backgrounds. The result is generally a nonlocal <quantum effective action>; for a field much heavier than all external momenta, a derivative expansion turns it into local effective interactions.

= Heavy scalar exchange in effective field theory
{parent=Integrating out a field}
{title2=$\Delta\mathcal L=\frac{a^2}{8}\varphi^2(M^2+\Box)^{-1}\varphi^2$}

A heavy real scalar $S$ with quadratic mass $M$ and interaction $-aS\varphi^2/2$ can be removed at tree level using $(M^2+\Box)S=-a\varphi^2/2$. Substituting into both its source and quadratic terms gives the displayed interaction. Its <derivative expansion> starts with $a^2\varphi^4/(8M^2)-a^2\varphi^2\Box\varphi^2/(8M^4)$. For a light quartic written as $-\lambda\varphi^4/4!$, the matched quartic is $\lambda-3a^2/M^2$. The expansion is valid for momentum invariants small compared with $M^2$.

= Derivative expansion
{parent=Effective field theory}
{wiki}

A derivative expansion expresses a nonlocal effective interaction as a series of local operators with successively more derivatives. For a field of mass $M$, each additional pair of derivatives is typically suppressed by $M^{-2}$.

= Four-fermion interaction
{parent=Effective field theory}
{wiki}

A four-fermion interaction is a local operator containing four fermion fields. In four spacetime dimensions it has mass dimension six, so its coefficient has mass dimension minus two; writing it as the square of a current multiplied by a coefficient inside the square gives that coefficient mass dimension minus one.

= Naive factorization of a nonleptonic meson decay
{parent=Four-fermion interaction}

Approximate a hadronic <matrix element> of a product of two currents by a product of separate current <matrix elements>. For a decay to two <mesons>, one factor can create a <pion> from the vacuum while the other gives a <pseudoscalar-to-pseudoscalar form factor>. Nonfactorizable <quantum chromodynamics> interactions are omitted; tree-level weak vertices do not alone justify this approximation.

= Fermi interaction
{c}
{parent=Four-fermion interaction}
{wiki}

The Fermi interaction is the low-energy four-fermion approximation to the <weak charged current>. Integrating out a <W boson> gives its coefficient $G_F/\sqrt2=g^2/(8M_W^2)$ at tree level.

= Integrating out a charged vector boson
{parent=Fermi interaction}
{title2=$\mathcal L_{\rm eff}=-\frac{g^2}{8m_W^2}J^\dagger_\mu J^\mu$}

For a massive charged vector with interaction $-\kappa(W^+J+W^-J^\dagger)$, neglect derivatives at momenta small compared with its mass. Completing the square in $m_W^2W^+W^-$ leaves $-\kappa^2J^\dagger J/m_W^2$. With $\kappa=g/(2\sqrt2)$ this yields the <Fermi constant> relation $G_F/\sqrt2=g^2/(8m_W^2)$. Corrections contain extra derivatives and are suppressed by momentum squared over the heavy mass squared.

= Massless neutrino-electron contact cross-section
{title2=$\sigma_\nu=G_F^2s(c_V^2+c_Vc_A+c_A^2)/(3\pi)$}
{parent=Fermi interaction}

For a left-handed incoming neutrino, unpolarized electrons, and electron vector/axial couplings $c_V,c_A$, the <four-fermion interaction> gives $\overline{|\mathcal M|^2}=4G_F^2[(c_V+c_A)^2s^2+(c_V-c_A)^2u^2]$. Integrating massless <two-body Lorentz-invariant phase space> yields $\sigma=G_F^2s(c_V^2+c_Vc_A+c_A^2)/(3\pi)$. The electron spin average is included, while no sterile neutrino helicity is averaged in.

= Neutrino-antineutrino interchange of chiral scattering weights
{parent=Massless neutrino-electron contact cross-section}

Crossing a massless neutrino to an antineutrino reverses the antisymmetric part of its spin tensor. The $s^2$ and $u^2$ weights exchange, giving $\sigma_{\bar\nu}=G_F^2s[(c_V-c_A)^2+(c_V+c_A)^2/3]/(4\pi)$ for the same electron couplings. This is a low-energy contact statement rather than a replacement for a full weak-boson propagator.

= Charged-current shift in neutrino-electron scattering

{parent=Massless neutrino-electron contact cross-section}

Electron-neutrino scattering has a charged-current contribution absent for muon neutrinos. <Fierz rearrangement> combined with the external <fermionic sign> adds a left-electron current: $c_V\to c_V+1$, $c_A\to c_A+1$. The right-electron coupling stays unchanged; the left-electron coupling increases by two.

= Spontaneous symmetry breaking
{title2=SSB}
{parent=Quantum field theory}
{wiki}

Spontaneous symmetry breaking occurs when the action and equations have a symmetry but a chosen <ground state> is invariant only under a proper subgroup. Acting with the broken symmetry generates a degenerate <vacuum manifold>.

= Spontaneous breaking of a Z2 scalar symmetry
{parent=Spontaneous symmetry breaking}
{title2=$V(\phi)=\lambda(\phi^2-v^2)^2/4,\quad m_h^2=2\lambda v^2$}

A <real scalar field> with the displayed potential is invariant under the <Z2 symmetry> $\phi\mapsto-\phi$, but a chosen broken-phase pure vacuum has nonzero <vacuum expectation value> of one sign. Expanding about $\phi=v$ gives $V=\lambda v^2h^2+\lambda vh^3+\lambda h^4/4$. The excitation is massive, and no continuous broken generator requires a <Goldstone boson>. In a finite-volume theory with symmetry-preserving conditions, tunneling can produce a symmetric ground state. A broken pure vacuum is selected by taking the infinite-volume limit before removing an infinitesimal source, or by specifying appropriate boundary conditions. The two signs are related by the symmetry, although the individual pure vacua are not invariant under it.

= Global triplet scalar symmetry breaking
{parent=Spontaneous symmetry breaking}
{title2=$O(3)\to O(2)$}

= O(3) triplet symmetry breaking
{c}
{synonym}

For a <real scalar triplet> with $V=\frac12m^2\Phi^2+\frac\lambda4(\Phi^2)^2$, $\lambda>0$ and $m^2<0$, the vacuum sphere has radius $v^2=-m^2/\lambda$. At $\Phi_0=ve_3$, the Hessian is $\operatorname{diag}(0,0,2\lambda v^2)$. Global $O(3)\to O(2)$ breaking therefore gives two physical massless <Goldstone bosons> and one radial scalar with squared <mass> $2\lambda v^2$. The angular modes cannot be gauged away in a theory with only global rotations.

= Vacuum manifold
{parent=Spontaneous symmetry breaking}

The vacuum manifold is the space of field configurations that minimize the energy. If a group $G$ acts transitively and a chosen vacuum has stabilizer $H$, its connected component is the homogeneous space $G/H$.

= Classical vacuum
{parent=Vacuum manifold}
{title2=$V(\phi_v)=\min V$}

= Classical vacua
{synonym}

A spatially homogeneous field value minimizing the potential energy. For a <finite-energy field configuration> on an infinite domain, the potential is normally shifted so the relevant vacuum energy density is zero. Disconnected components of the <vacuum manifold> allow a <scalar-field kink> to connect distinct vacuum values.

= Scalar-field vacuum
{parent=Vacuum manifold}

= Scalar-field vacua
{synonym}

A scalar-field vacuum is a constant field at a minimum of its <potential energy>, with the minimum shifted to zero when discussing finite-energy <classical field-theory solitons>. Distinct isolated vacua allow a <scalar-field kink> to approach different values at the two ends of space.

= Stability of a cubic-quartic scalar potential
{parent=Scalar-field vacuum}
{title2=$\mu^2\le3\lambda m^2$}

For $V(\phi)=m^2\phi^2/2-\mu\phi^3/6+\lambda\phi^4/24$, positive $\lambda$ makes the classical potential bounded below. For $m^2\ge0$, completing the square gives
$$
V=\phi^2\left[\frac{\lambda}{24}\left(\phi-\frac{2\mu}{\lambda}\right)^2+\frac{m^2}{2}-\frac{\mu^2}{6\lambda}\right].
$$
Thus zero is a global minimum precisely when $\mu^2\le3\lambda m^2$. At a nontrivial equality there are degenerate minima at zero and $2\mu/\lambda$. If $m^2>0$ and the inequality fails, zero is a <false vacuum>: its Hessian is positive but another minimum has lower energy. A negative quartic coupling, or a nonzero cubic term without a quartic term, is unbounded below. These are classical or tree-level criteria.

= False vacuum
{parent=Scalar-field vacuum}
{wiki}

A local minimum of the <scalar potential> that has higher energy than a global minimum. It can be stable against infinitesimal classical fluctuations but metastable to a transition to a lower-energy vacuum, including <quantum tunnelling>. The <stability of a cubic-quartic scalar potential> gives an elementary example distinguishing local curvature from global stability.

A vacuum expectation value is the expectation of a field or operator in the vacuum state. A nonzero value that is not invariant under the full symmetry group can select a point of the <vacuum manifold> and spontaneously break the symmetry.

The scalar mass matrix is the <Hessian matrix> of the <scalar potential> at a vacuum. Its eigenvalues are the tree-level squared masses; broken symmetry directions are zero eigenvectors before a <Higgs mechanism> or explicit symmetry breaking removes the corresponding massless modes.

= Accidental massless scalar

An <accidental massless scalar> has zero quadratic mass without being required by the stated broken continuous symmetry. A singlet with potential $\kappa z^4$ around $z=0$ is an example: its <Hessian matrix> vanishes although no continuous symmetry of that single real field protects a mass term. = Goldstone's theorem
{c}
{synonym}

In a relativistic quantum field theory, each spontaneously broken generator of a continuous internal symmetry produces a massless scalar excitation. For $G\to H$, the standard setting therefore has $\dim G-\dim H$ Goldstone bosons.

= Goldstone directions in the scalar mass matrix
{c}
{parent=Goldstone theorem}
{title2=$M^2(it^a\phi_0)=0$}

For a smooth invariant <scalar potential>, differentiating $\partial_I V\,(it^a\phi)^I=0$ at a <classical vacuum> gives $M^2(it^a\phi_0)=0$. The symmetry tangent space has dimension $\dim G-\dim H_0$, where $H_0$ is the full <stabilizer subgroup>. With a positive nonsingular scalar <kinetic term>, these are massless directions of the quadratic theory. Further zero <eigenvalues> are possible unless the <Hessian matrix> is positive on the normal complement.

= Accidental massless radial modes beyond Goldstone modes
{parent=Goldstone directions in the scalar mass matrix}

= Accidental massless radial mode beyond Goldstone modes
{synonym}

The <Goldstone theorem> guarantees zero directions tangent to the vacuum orbit, not the absence of other zero eigenvalues. For $V(\varphi)=\kappa(\varphi^T\varphi-v^2)^4$, $\kappa>0$, the minima form the same sphere as in ordinary $O(n)$ breaking, but the entire <Hessian matrix> vanishes there. Thus all n scalar quadratic masses vanish, although only n-1 are symmetry-protected <Goldstone bosons>. Exact Goldstone counting requires nonzero curvature transverse to the orbit.

= Radial and angular modes of an orthogonally invariant scalar potential
{parent=Goldstone theorem}
{title2=$m_\eta^2=2\lambda v^2$}

For $V=m^2\phi^2/2+\lambda(\phi^2)^2/4$, $m^2<0$, the <vacuum manifold> is a sphere of radius $v=\sqrt{-m^2/\lambda}$. At a chosen vacuum the <Hessian matrix> is $2\lambda v^2nn^T$, where $n$ is its unit direction. The radial scalar has mass squared $2\lambda v^2$; the $n-1$ angular modes are physical <Goldstone bosons> when the symmetry is global.

= Pseudo-Goldstone boson

A pseudo-Goldstone boson is a light mode produced when a spontaneously broken continuous symmetry is also weakly broken explicitly. Its mass vanishes with the explicit symmetry-breaking parameters.

= Nonlinear sigma model
{parent=Spontaneous symmetry breaking}
{wiki}

A nonlinear sigma model has fields valued in a curved target space such as a <vacuum manifold> $G/H$. Its leading action is quadratic in spacetime derivatives but nonlinear in any unconstrained coordinates used for the target.

= O(N) nonlinear sigma model
{c}
{parent=Nonlinear sigma model}

The $O(N)$ nonlinear sigma model has an $N$-component field constrained to a sphere, commonly $\mathbf n^2=N$ or $1$. An auxiliary Lagrange-multiplier field turns the constraint into a self-consistent mass gap in the <large-N expansion>.

= One-loop stiffness of a spherical nonlinear sigma model
{parent=O(N) nonlinear sigma model}
{title2=$g_{\rm eff}^{-1}=g^{-1}-(n-2)I_D$}

For the unit-sphere <nonlinear sigma model> with action $\int d^Dx\,|\nabla\sigma|^2/(2g)$, parameterize a slowly rotating background by $n_0=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0,\ldots)$ and tangent vectors $e_1=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0,\ldots)$, $e_a$ for $a\geq2$. Write $\sigma=n_0\sqrt{1-\pi^2}+\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}e_a\pi_a$. For constant slow $\nabla\theta$, the coefficient of $|\nabla\theta|^2$ in $|\nabla\sigma|^2$ is $1-\sum_{a=2}^{n-1}\pi_a^2$. The fast <Gaussian field theory> gives $\langle\pi_a^2\rangle=gI_D$, where $I_D=\int_{\Lambda/b<|p|<\Lambda}d^Dp/[(2\pi)^Dp^2]$. The omitted linear term integrates to zero, and terms cubic in the fast variables have zero one-loop average. Thus precisely $n-2$ tangent components reduce the <phase stiffness> $1/g$. Restoring the cutoff in $D=2+\epsilon$ gives $dg/d\log b=-\epsilon g+(n-2)S_D\Lambda^\epsilon g^2/(2\pi)^D+O(g^3)$. This weak-coupling expansion requires $n\geq2$; the discrete target $S^0$ has no tangent expansion.

= Thermal eigenvalues of the spherical nonlinear sigma model
{parent=One-loop stiffness of a spherical nonlinear sigma model}
{title2=$y_T(g=0)=-\epsilon,\quad y_T(g_*)=\epsilon$}

With $A_D=S_D\Lambda^\epsilon/(2\pi)^D$, the one-loop <renormalization-group beta function> is $\beta_g=-\epsilon g+(n-2)A_Dg^2$. For $n>2$ and $\epsilon>0$, its two nonnegative <renormalization-group fixed points> are zero and $g_*=\epsilon/[(n-2)A_D]$. Differentiating the beta function gives the displayed thermal <eigenvalues>, so the nonzero point has <correlation-length critical exponent> $\nu=1/\epsilon$ to leading order. At $\epsilon=0$ the zero eigenvalue requires nonlinear analysis: $g$ is marginally relevant for $n>2$, but the $n=2$ perturbative spin-wave theory has a line of fixed points and omits vortices.

= Exponentially large correlation length in a two-dimensional spherical sigma model
{parent=Thermal eigenvalues of the spherical nonlinear sigma model}
{title2=$\xi\sim\Lambda^{-1}\exp[2\pi/((n-2)g_0)]$}

At $D=2$ and $n>2$, the weak-coupling <renormalization-group flow> obeys $dg/d\ell=(n-2)g^2/(2\pi)$. Integrating gives $1/g(\ell)=1/g_0-(n-2)\ell/(2\pi)$. The flow reaches order-one coupling at a length whose logarithm is $2\pi/[(n-2)g_0]+O(1)$ at this order. Restoring the microscopic scale $\Lambda^{-1}$ gives the displayed leading exponential <correlation length>. Higher loops affect its prefactor, and this conclusion does not apply to the compact $n=2$ model with its <Berezinskii–Kosterlitz–Thouless transition>.

= O3 nonlinear sigma model
{c}
{parent=O(N) nonlinear sigma model}
{title2=$O(3)$ nonlinear sigma model}

= CP1 nonlinear sigma model
{c}
{synonym}

In two spatial dimensions, a unit-vector field $\mathbf n:\mathbb R^2\to S^2$ has static energy $E=\int|\nabla\mathbf n|^2d^2x/2$. The sphere is also the <complex projective line>, so its <stereographic projection> gives the equivalent complex field description. A constant value at spatial infinity compactifies a regular configuration to a map $S^2\to S^2$, whose <degree of a map between oriented manifolds> is its <topological charge>.

= Stereographic energy of the O3 sigma model
{parent=O3 nonlinear sigma model}
{title2=$V=4\int\frac{|\nabla w|^2}{(1+|w|^2)^2}\,d^2x$}

In the oriented <stereographic projection> $\phi=(2\operatorname{Re}w,2\operatorname{Im}w,1-|w|^2)/(1+|w|^2)$, the sphere's metric is $4|dw|^2/(1+|w|^2)^2$. For energy $V=\int|\nabla\phi|^2$, its <topological degree> is $N=\pi^{-1}\int\operatorname{Im}(\overline{w_{x_1}}w_{x_2})/(1+|w|^2)^2$. The inequality $|w_{x_1}|^2+|w_{x_2}|^2\geq2|\operatorname{Im}(\overline{w_{x_1}}w_{x_2})|$ gives $V\geq8\pi|N|$. <Holomorphic maps> and <antiholomorphic maps> that are <rational maps> saturate the respective signs. The more common energy with a prefactor $1/2$ has bound $4\pi|N|$ instead.

= Mass-deformed spherical sigma model
{parent=O3 nonlinear sigma model}
{title2=$V(\phi)=\frac{rm^2}{4}\sin^2\phi$}

A spherical sigma model in polar fields $\phi,\alpha$ has kinetic metric $r(d\phi^2+\sin^2\phi\,d\alpha^2)/2$ in the usual one-half kinetic convention. The displayed positive potential selects the two polar vacua. For $r>0$ it supports static and rotating charged <kinks>, and a topological theta term shifts their canonical internal charge without changing the bulk equations.

= Theta term in a spherical sigma model
{parent=Mass-deformed spherical sigma model}
{title2=$\mathcal L_\theta=-\frac{\theta}{4\pi}\epsilon^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\cos\phi\,\partial_\nu\alpha$}

Locally this density is a total derivative, so it changes neither the bulk field equations nor the <energy>. Its contribution to the angular <canonical momentum> is a spatial boundary derivative. With $\epsilon^{01}=+1$, it produces the <theta-angle shift of a sigma-model kink charge>.

= Theta-angle shift of a sigma-model kink charge
{parent=Theta term in a spherical sigma model}
{title2=$Q_\theta=Q_0-\theta T/(2\pi)$}

With $\epsilon^{01}=+1$, the Noether density for the angular shift gains $\theta\partial_x\cos\phi/(4\pi)$. Its integral gives the displayed topological shift. Mechanical charge $Q_0$ determines the classical rotating-kink <energy>, while canonical symmetry charge $Q_\theta$ is conjugate to the periodic angle and is integral after quantization. Reversing the epsilon convention reverses the theta sign.

= Rotating charged kink in a spherical sigma model
{parent=Mass-deformed spherical sigma model}
{title2=$M=m\sqrt{r^2+Q_0^2}$}

= Q-kink in a spherical sigma model
{c}
{synonym}

= Dyonic kink in a spherical sigma model
{synonym}

For $\alpha=\omega t+\alpha_0$ and $|\omega|<m$, the <kink> is $\phi=2\arctan e^{\kappa(x-x_0)}$ with $\kappa=\sqrt{m^2-\omega^2}$. Its rest <energy> and mechanical internal charge are $M=rm^2/\kappa$ and $Q_0=r\omega/\kappa$. Eliminating the frequency gives the displayed <mass> relation. The orientation rotates while the <energy> profile remains stationary.

= Semiclassical charged-kink spectrum with a theta angle
{parent=Rotating charged kink in a spherical sigma model}
{title2=$M_{T,\ell}=m\sqrt{r^2+(\ell+\theta T/(2\pi))^2}$}

In units $\hbar=1$, periodic-angle quantization gives canonical charge $Q_\theta=\ell\in\mathbb Z$. The mechanical charge is $Q_0=\ell+\theta T/(2\pi)$ and the rotating-kink <mass> gives the displayed leading semiclassical spectrum. The $T=1$ and $T=-1$ families are related by reversing the <kink>. Theta periodicity relabels the integer; fluctuation corrections are not excluded by this leading calculation.

= Static kink bound in a mass-deformed spherical sigma model
{parent=Mass-deformed spherical sigma model}
{title2=$E\geq rm|T|$}

For $T=-[\cos\phi]_{-\infty}^{+\infty}/2$, complete the static <energy> into squares $(\phi_x\mp m\sin\phi)^2$ and $\sin^2\phi\,\alpha_x^2$. The boundary term is $\pm rmT$. A $T=1$ saturating <kink> is $\phi=2\arctan e^{m(x-x_0)}$, with constant $\alpha$ and <mass> $rm$. This is a model-specific <Bogomolny bound>.

= Sigma-model lump
{parent=O3 nonlinear sigma model}
{title2=$E=4\pi|N|$}

A sigma-model lump is a finite-energy <classical field-theory soliton> of the two-dimensional <O3 nonlinear sigma model>. With an orientation chosen so holomorphic maps have positive charge, the <Bogomolny bound> $E\geq4\pi|N|$ is saturated by <rational maps> of degree $N>0$ in a <stereographic projection> coordinate, and by antiholomorphic maps for negative charge. The field remains smooth at coordinate poles, which are points at the other pole of the target sphere. Scale invariance permits lumps of arbitrary size; topology alone does not prevent concentration to a singular limiting configuration.

= Based rational-map moduli of sigma-model lumps
{parent=Sigma-model lump}
{title2=$\dim_{\mathbb C}\mathcal R_k^*=2k$}

Fix the value $w(\infty)=0$ for positive-degree <sigma-model lumps>. Their holomorphic <rational maps> can be written $p/q$, where $q$ is monic of degree $k$, $p$ has degree at most $k-1$, and the polynomials have no common zero. The coefficients of $p$ and the nonleading coefficients of $q$ give $2k$ complex parameters; coprimality is an open condition. The <topological degree> is $k$, obtained by counting generic preimages with multiplicity. Allowing a common factor lowers the degree after cancellation and can describe a degeneration rather than another smooth point of this degree-$k$ parameter space.

= Unit sigma-model lump and size modulus
{parent=Sigma-model lump}
{title2=$w(z)=\rho/(z-z_0),\quad E=4\pi$}

For the <O3 nonlinear sigma model> with energy $\int|\nabla\mathbf n|^2/2$, the <rational map> $w(z)=\rho/(z-z_0)$, $\rho\ne0$, is a degree-one <sigma-model lump>. Its energy density is $4|\rho|^2/(|z-z_0|^2+|\rho|^2)^2$, whose integral is $4\pi$. The complex center $z_0$ gives its position, while $|\rho|$ gives its size and $\arg\rho$ its internal phase. Varying the size preserves energy. The limit $\rho\to0$ is singular at the center, so conserved degree under smooth deformations does not prevent concentration.

= Degree charge of an O3 sigma-model lump
{parent=Sigma-model lump}
{title2=$Q=\frac1{4\pi}\int\mathbf n\cdot(\partial_1\mathbf n\times\partial_2\mathbf n)\,d^2x$}

A unit-vector field $\mathbf n:\mathbb R^2\to S^2$ approaching a fixed value at infinity extends to $S^2\to S^2$. Its <topological charge> is the <topological degree> $Q=(4\pi)^{-1}\int\mathbf n\cdot(\partial_1\mathbf n\times\partial_2\mathbf n)\,d^2x$. This normalized target area pullback counts the number of oriented coverings.

= Bogomolny degree bound for the O3 sigma model
{c}
{parent=Degree charge of an O3 sigma-model lump}
{title2=$E\geq4\pi|Q|$}

For $E=\tfrac12\int(|\partial_1\mathbf n|^2+|\partial_2\mathbf n|^2)$, completing the square gives $E=\tfrac12\int|\partial_1\mathbf n\pm\mathbf n\times\partial_2\mathbf n|^2\pm4\pi Q$, hence $E\geq4\pi|Q|$. Saturation yields the <Bogomolny equations> of the <sigma-model lump>. With the oriented <stereographic projection> $\mathbf n=(2\operatorname{Re}w,2\operatorname{Im}w,1-|w|^2)/(1+|w|^2)$, holomorphic rational maps have positive degree and saturate the bound; antiholomorphic maps have negative degree.

= Discrete symmetry in quantum field theory

= CP

{synonym}

The combined operation of <charge conjugation> and <parity symmetry in quantum field theory>. A <weak interaction> can preserve this symmetry while violating each constituent separately.

= CP invariance of a neutral chiral vector interaction
{c}
{parent=CP symmetry}
{title2=$\mathcal L=(j_\mu-j_{5\mu})V^\mu$}

The <vector current> is odd and the <axial current> is even under <charge conjugation>. Under <parity symmetry in quantum field theory>, the first is a vector and the second a pseudovector. Thus their difference obeys $j_L^\mu(x)\mapsto-P^\mu{}_{\nu}j_L^\nu(x_P)$ under <CP>, with $P=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$. If a neutral vector has $V^\mu(x)\mapsto\xi P^\mu{}_{\nu}V^\nu(x_P)$ under <CP>, the interaction transforms by the factor $-\xi$. It is invariant for $\xi=-1$. Being a real field restricts its intrinsic phases but does not alone fix this sign. The conventional C-odd neutral gauge-vector assignment makes real diagonal <Z boson> vector and axial couplings CP invariant, despite their separate C and P violations.

= CP eigenstate
{c}
{parent=CP symmetry}
{title2=$CP|\psi\rangle=\pm|\psi\rangle$}

In a convention where the <CP> operation squares to one on the relevant bosonic state space, a <CP eigenstate> has <eigenvalue> $+1$ or $-1$. If the <Hamiltonian> commutes with <CP>, its nondegenerate eigenstates can also be <CP eigenstates>. In the neutral-<kaon> convention $CP|K^0\rangle=-|\bar K^0\rangle$, the antisymmetric flavor combination is <CP> even and the symmetric one <CP> odd. Rephasing flavor states changes the written combinations, not the invariant content.

= Z2 symmetry
{title2=$\mathbb Z_2$ symmetry}
{c}
{parent=Discrete symmetry in quantum field theory}

A Z2 symmetry is generated by an operation whose square is the identity. For a real scalar field, the sign symmetry $\phi\mapsto-\phi$ forbids every odd power of $\phi$.

= Parity symmetry in quantum field theory
{parent=Discrete symmetry in quantum field theory}

Parity symmetry means invariance under <spatial reflection>, together with the intrinsic transformation rules of the fields.

= Parity transformation of a Dirac bilinear
{parent=Parity symmetry in quantum field theory}
{title2=$\bar\psi\psi\mapsto\bar\psi\psi,\quad\bar\psi\gamma^5\psi\mapsto-\bar\psi\gamma^5\psi$}

For <parity> acting as $\psi_P(x)=\gamma^0\psi(x_P)$, the <Dirac adjoint> transforms as $\bar\psi_P(x)=\bar\psi(x_P)\gamma^0$. A bilinear with matrix $B$ therefore transforms by $B\mapsto\gamma^0B\gamma^0$, with its fields evaluated at $x_P$. Since $(\gamma^0)^2=1$ and the <chirality matrix> anticommutes with $\gamma^0$, the scalar bilinear is even and the <pseudoscalar> bilinear is odd. The same calculation shows that <parity> exchanges the <chiral projectors>.

= Parity-invariant vacuum
{parent=Parity symmetry in quantum field theory}

A parity-invariant vacuum satisfies $P|0\rangle=|0\rangle$ up to an irrelevant phase. Vacuum correlation functions then transform according to the product of the intrinsic parities of their insertions.

= Parity-odd scalar interaction
{parent=Parity symmetry in quantum field theory}

A parity-odd scalar interaction is rotationally invariant but changes sign under <spatial reflection>. It must contain a <pseudoscalar> contraction, such as a <Levi-Civita symbol> contracted with three spatial gradients.

= Charge conjugation

Charge conjugation exchanges particles with antiparticles. For a matrix-valued gauge potential it acts as $A_\mu\mapsto-A_\mu^T$.

= Charge conjugation symmetry
{parent=Charge conjugation}
{title2=$C$}

= C symmetry
{c}
{synonym}

<Charge conjugation symmetry> is invariance of the dynamics under <charge conjugation>. It pairs a process with its particle-antiparticle conjugate at equal rates. <C violation> is the failure of this invariance.

= Charge conjugation of a non-Abelian gauge field
{parent=Charge conjugation}
{title2=$\mathcal A_\mu\mapsto-\mathcal A_\mu^T$}

Write a matrix-valued <gauge field> $\mathcal A_\mu=A_\mu^aT^a$ in the <fundamental representation>. <Charge conjugation> sends it to $-\mathcal A_\mu^T$ because reordering the fermions transposes both the spinor and internal matrices. The identity $[A^T,B^T]=-[A,B]^T$ then sends the <gauge field strength> to $-\mathcal F_{\mu\nu}^T$. This distinction prevents treating all color components as individually C-odd.

= Violation of charge conjugation
{parent=Charge conjugation}

= C violation
{synonym}
{c}

A theory violates <charge conjugation> when conjugating particles to antiparticles changes a physical interaction or its transition probabilities. This is different from <CP violation>: an interaction can violate <charge conjugation> and <parity symmetry in quantum field theory> separately while preserving their combination.

= Charge-conjugation matrix
{parent=Charge conjugation}
{title2=$C^{-1}\gamma^\mu C=-(\gamma^\mu)^T$}

For a <Dirac field>, the charge-conjugation matrix implements $\psi^c=C\bar\psi^T$. In the standard <gamma matrix> representation one can take $C=i\gamma^2\gamma^0$. An overall phase is conventional. The transpose intertwining relation is different from the entrywise <complex conjugation> involved in an <antiunitary operator>.

= Intrinsic charge-conjugation phase

The phase in a field transformation $\hat C V^\mu\hat C^{-1}=\eta_C V^{\mu\dagger}$. For a real vector <relativistic quantum field>, <Hermitian conjugation> restricts this phase to $\pm1$.

= Charge conjugation of fermion bilinears

For <normal ordering> of a <fermion bilinear>, <charge conjugation> sends $\bar\psi\Gamma\psi$ to $\bar\psi(C^{-1}\Gamma C)^T\psi$. In particular the <Dirac current> is odd and the <axial current> is even under <charge conjugation>.

= Charge-conjugate particle and antiparticle spinors
{parent=Charge conjugation of a Dirac field}
{title2=$v(p,s)=C\bar u(p,s)^T$}

Choose the negative-frequency <Dirac spinors> as the charge conjugates of the positive-frequency spinors. Transposing $\bar u(\not p-m)=0$ and using the <charge-conjugation matrix> identity gives $(\not p+m)C\bar u^T=0$, so the displayed choice solves the correct antiparticle equation. In the <mode expansion of a Dirac field>, the unitary charge-conjugation operator then exchanges particle and antiparticle annihilation operators, with conjugate phases on their creation operators. A different spinor basis can insert conventional phases or a spin-index relabeling; the displayed equality specifies a compatible choice.

= Charge conjugation of the Dirac adjoint
{parent=Charge conjugation of a Dirac field}
{title2=$\overline{\psi^c}=-\psi^TC^{-1}$}

For $\psi^c=C\bar\psi^T$, use a unitary <charge-conjugation matrix> with $C^{-1}\gamma^0C=-(\gamma^0)^T$ and Hermitian $\gamma^0$ of square one. The <Dirac adjoint> then satisfies
$$
\overline{\psi^c}=\psi^T(\gamma^0)^*C^{-1}\gamma^0=-\psi^TC^{-1}.
$$
If the unitary <charge conjugation> operator sends $\psi$ to $\eta_C\psi^c$, it sends $\bar\psi$ to $-\eta_C^*\psi^TC^{-1}$. Antisymmetry $C^T=-C$ also gives $(\psi^c)^c=\psi$. The unitary operator acting on fields is not an <antiunitary operator> acting on numerical coefficients.

= Antilinear charge-conjugation intertwiner
{parent=Charge conjugation of a Dirac field}
{title2=$\psi^c=B\psi^*,\qquad\gamma^\mu B=-B\gamma^{\mu*}$}

In the complex-conjugate convention for <charge conjugation of a Dirac field>, a unitary matrix $B$ obeys the displayed <gamma matrix> identity. For $M^{\rho\sigma}=\frac14[\gamma^\rho,\gamma^\sigma]$, two minus signs cancel to give $M^{\rho\sigma}B=BM^{\rho\sigma*}$. Real <Lorentz transformation> parameters therefore give $S B=B S^*$, so $B\psi^*$ has the same <spinor representation of the Lorentz group> transformation law as $\psi$. Complex conjugating the free <Dirac equation> and multiplying by $B$ proves that the conjugate also solves it for real mass. This $B$ is the matrix multiplying $\psi^*$; the convention multiplying $\bar\psi^T$ instead uses $B(\gamma^0)^T$.

= Algebraic charge conjugation reverses chirality
{parent=Charge conjugation of a Dirac field}
{title2=$\gamma_5\psi=\eta\psi\Longrightarrow\gamma_5\psi^c=-\eta\psi^c$}

The <Dirac adjoint> of a chiral field has opposite projector placement because $\gamma_5$ anticommutes with $\gamma^0$: $\bar\psi\gamma_5=-\eta\bar\psi$. Since $C\gamma_5^TC^{-1}=\gamma_5$, the algebraic charge conjugate satisfies $\gamma_5 C\bar\psi^T=C(\bar\psi\gamma_5)^T=-\eta\psi^c$. This is conjugation of a projected spinor, distinguished from <operator conjugation of a chiral field component>, where the numerical projector is kept outside the conjugation operator.

= Operator conjugation of a chiral field component
{title2=$\hat C\psi_L\hat C^{-1}=P_L\psi^c=(\psi_R)^c$}
{parent=Charge conjugation of a Dirac field}

The unitary charge-conjugation operator acts on field operators and commutes with numerical <chiral projectors>, so $\hat C(P_L\psi)\hat C^{-1}=P_L\psi^c$. This component is left-chiral. The algebraic conjugate of an already projected spinor instead satisfies $(P_L\psi)^c=P_R\psi^c$, because its adjoint carries the opposite projector. Keeping the two operations separate avoids a false chirality conclusion.

= Transpose symmetry of a charge-conjugation matrix
{title2=$C^T=\pm C$}
{parent=Charge conjugation of a Dirac field}

If $C\gamma^{\mu T}C^{-1}=-\gamma^\mu$, transposition shows that $C^TC^{-1}$ commutes with every <gamma matrix>. Irreducibility of the complex Dirac representation and the <Schur lemma> make it scalar. Transposing $C^T=zC$ again yields $z^2=1$, so $C^T=\pm C$.

= Antisymmetry of the four-dimensional charge-conjugation matrix
{parent=Transpose symmetry of a charge-conjugation matrix}
{title2=$C^T=-C$}

In the <Weyl representation of the gamma matrices>, $C=i\gamma^2\gamma^0=\operatorname{diag}(\varepsilon,-\varepsilon)$ with $\varepsilon=i\tau_2$. The antisymmetry of $\varepsilon$ gives $C^T=-C$, and direct multiplication gives $C\gamma^{\mu T}C^{-1}=-\gamma^\mu$ and $C^\dagger C=I$. Any two such intertwining matrices differ by a scalar: their ratio commutes with all <gamma matrices>, whose products span the full four-by-four matrix algebra. Changing spinor basis sends $C$ to $SCS^T$, so transpose antisymmetry is basis-independent. Its sign is therefore fixed, not merely one of the two alternatives supplied by the general <transpose symmetry of a charge-conjugation matrix> argument.

= Unitarity of the charge-conjugation matrix
{parent=Charge conjugation of a Dirac field}
{title2=$C^\dagger C=I$}

For $\psi^C=\eta_C C\bar\psi^T$ and $\gamma^{0T}=\gamma^0=-C\gamma^0C^{-1}$, the <Dirac adjoint> transforms as $\bar\psi^C=-\eta_C^*\psi^TC^\dagger$. Anticommuting the components of the <fermion bilinear> gives $(\bar\psi\psi)^C=\bar\psi(C^\dagger C)^T\psi$. Invariance of the scalar bilinear therefore fixes $C^\dagger C=I$.

Time-reversal symmetry reverses the time coordinate and is represented antiunitarily in quantum theory.

= Time reversal of a complex scalar field
{parent=T-symmetry}
{title2=$\hat T\phi(t,\boldsymbol x)\hat T^{-1}=\eta_T\phi(-t,\boldsymbol x)$}

For a charged <spin>-zero <complex scalar field>, time reversal preserves charge and can include an intrinsic phase. In its free-field <mode expansion of a free field>, <antiunitarity> conjugates the plane-wave coefficients. Relabel spatial momentum to its negative and compare the annihilation and creation terms: $\hat Ta(\boldsymbol p)\hat T^{-1}=\eta_Ta(-\boldsymbol p)$ and $\hat Tb^\dagger(\boldsymbol p)\hat T^{-1}=\eta_Tb^\dagger(-\boldsymbol p)$. Particle annihilation remains annihilation; unlike <charge conjugation>, this operation does not exchange particle and <antiparticle> operators.

= Intrinsic time-reversal phase
{parent=Time reversal of a complex scalar field}
{title2=$|\eta_T|=1,\quad\eta_T\mapsto e^{-2i\chi}\eta_T$}

In the scalar transformation $\hat Ta(p)\hat T^{-1}=\eta_Ta(p_P)$, preservation of canonical commutators or the involution condition gives $|\eta_T|=1$. <Antiunitarity> conjugates the phase on a second application, so the condition does not restrict it to plus or minus one. Redefine $a'=e^{i\chi}a$; then $\hat Ta'\hat T^{-1}=e^{-2i\chi}\eta_Ta'(p_P)$. Choosing $\chi=\arg(\eta_T)/2$ makes the intrinsic phase one. Oppositely rephasing the <antiparticle> annihilator implements the same change as a phase convention for the whole scalar field.

= Time-reversal reciprocity of the scattering matrix
{parent=T-symmetry}
{title2=$\langle f|S|i\rangle=\langle\hat Ti|S|\hat Tf\rangle$}

If a Hermitian interaction-picture Hamiltonian satisfies $\hat TV(t)\hat T^{-1}=V(-t)$ for an <antiunitary operator>, its <Dyson series> conjugates $(-i)^n$ to $i^n$. The substitution $t_j\mapsto-t_j$ converts ordered integration to anti-ordered integration, giving $\hat TS\hat T^{-1}=S^\dagger$. <Antiunitarity> then gives the displayed reciprocity: the time-reversed process has the reversed outgoing state as its incoming state and vice versa. It does not merely reverse the momenta while leaving their incoming/outgoing roles fixed.

= Relative weak phase condition for time reversal
{parent=T-symmetry}
{title2=$\operatorname{Im}(g_Ag_V^*)=0$}

If a <Fermi interaction> contains vector and axial operators with the same <time-reversal symmetry> component signs, <antiunitarity> conjugates their coupling constants without exchanging the two operators. After removing a common field phase, invariance requires $g_A/g_V$ real when $g_V\ne0$. Equivalently $\operatorname{Im}(g_Ag_V^*)=0$. Fixed intrinsic phases can additionally constrain the common phase; the relative condition is independent of that choice. Adding a Hermitian-conjugate interaction guarantees a <Hermitian operator> Hamiltonian, but does not guarantee <time-reversal symmetry>.

= Time reversal of an electromagnetic gauge field
{parent=T-symmetry}
{title2=$A_0(x)\mapsto A_0(x_T),\quad A_i(x)\mapsto-A_i(x_T)$}

The vector <Dirac current> has an even temporal component and odd spatial components under <time-reversal symmetry>. Invariance of its real minimal coupling therefore gives the displayed transformation of the <gauge field>, up to a <gauge transformation>. Differentiation at $x_T=(-x^0,\boldsymbol x)$ yields $F_{\mu\nu}(x)\mapsto-t_\mu t_\nu F_{\mu\nu}(x_T)$, with $t=(1,-1,-1,-1)$. Thus the <electric field> is even and the <magnetic field> is odd.

= Time reversal of a Dirac field
{parent=T-symmetry}
{title2=$\hat T\psi(x)\hat T^{-1}=B\psi(-x^0,\boldsymbol x)$}

The spinor matrix $B$ obeys $B^{-1}\gamma^{\mu*}B=t_\mu\gamma^\mu$, with $t_0=1$ and $t_i=-1$. Conjugating the explicit $i$ and three spatial matrices also gives $B^{-1}\gamma^{5*}B=\gamma^5$. With spin phases $f_s=(-1)^{1/2-s}$ and identities $f_su^{-s*}(p_T)=\gamma^5Cu^s(p)$, relabeling spin and momentum in the <mode expansion of a Dirac field> gives $B=-\gamma^5C$, because $f_{-s}=-f_s$. That last relation is phase-convention dependent; the <quantum time-reversal operator> itself is not just a spinor matrix.

= Time reversal of a real neutral chiral vector interaction
{parent=Time reversal of a Dirac field}
{title2=$B\gamma^{\mu*}B^{-1}=t_\mu\gamma^\mu,\quad B\gamma^{5*}B^{-1}=\gamma^5$}

Let $B=\gamma^5C$ with the unitary <charge-conjugation matrix> C, and $t_0=1,t_i=-1$. Hermitian-adjoint gamma identities give $C\gamma^{\mu*}C^{-1}=-\gamma^0\gamma^\mu\gamma^0$, and multiplication by $\gamma^5$ gives the displayed identities. An <antiunitary operator> conjugates the explicit i in $\gamma^5=i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3$, which is essential to the second identity. Both vector and axial currents therefore have the same time-reversal component signs; their contraction with a similarly transforming neutral vector is invariant for real couplings.

= Spin phase in the Dirac time-reversal matrix
{parent=Time reversal of a Dirac field}

The matrix in <time reversal of a Dirac field> depends on the convention for the spin-flip phases and on the sign of the spinor identity used when relabeling the <mode expansion of a Dirac field>. If $\epsilon_s=(-1)^{1/2-s}$ and $\epsilon_su^{-s*}(p_T)=-\gamma^5Cu^s(p)$, the relabelled coefficient is $\epsilon_{-s}u^{-s*}(p_T)=\gamma^5Cu^s(p)$, giving $B=\gamma^5C$. A common phase changes $B$ but leaves its <gamma matrix> conjugation property and the induced <fermion bilinear> transformation unchanged. The <antiunitary operator> acts on both numerical coefficients and creation or annihilation operators.

= CP violation
{c}
{parent=Discrete symmetry in quantum field theory}
{wiki}

CP violation is the failure of a theory to be invariant under the combination of charge conjugation and parity. Under CPT invariance it is equivalent to time-reversal violation.

= Direct CP asymmetry
{parent=CP violation}
{title2=$A_{\rm CP}=(\Gamma-\bar\Gamma)/(\Gamma+\bar\Gamma)$}

A <direct CP asymmetry> compares <decay widths> for a process and its <CP symmetry> conjugate. Let two <decay amplitudes> contribute with real nonnegative magnitudes $a_1,a_2$, <absorptive phases> $\delta_i$ and <weak phases> $\phi_i$. Then $\mathcal A=a_1e^{i(\delta_1+\phi_1)}+a_2e^{i(\delta_2+\phi_2)}$ and $\overline{\mathcal A}=a_1e^{i(\delta_1-\phi_1)}+a_2e^{i(\delta_2-\phi_2)}$. Expanding the absolute squares gives
$$
|\mathcal A|^2-|\overline{\mathcal A}|^2=-4a_1a_2\sin(\delta_1-\delta_2)\sin(\phi_1-\phi_2).
$$
Thus two nonzero contributions and both kinds of relative phase are required in this example. Equal total <decay widths> under <CPT symmetry> constrain the sum over channels and do not forbid individual channel asymmetries.

The fermion electric dipole moment operator couples a fermion pseudotensor bilinear to the <electromagnetic field tensor>. Its nonrelativistic interaction is proportional to $\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf E$, so it is odd under parity and time reversal and signals CP violation.

= Time-reversal parity of a fermion electric dipole operator
{parent=Fermion electric dipole moment operator}

For real $a$, the Hermitian effective interaction $ia\bar\psi\sigma^{\mu\nu}\gamma^5\psi F_{\mu\nu}$ is odd under <time-reversal symmetry>. The spinor tensor and <gauge field strength> both acquire $-t_\mu t_\nu$, while the <chirality matrix> is even. Their signs cancel, leaving the minus sign from the explicit $i$ under <antiunitarity>. By the <CPT theorem> this is also <CP violation>. It is not a renormalizable tree-level <Standard Model> term, but a radiatively generated low-energy dipole is allowed because of the <CP-violating phase> in the <CKM matrix>.

= CPT theorem
{c}
{parent=Discrete symmetry in quantum field theory}
{wiki}

The CPT theorem states that every local Lorentz-invariant quantum field theory with a Hermitian Hamiltonian is invariant under the combined action of charge conjugation, parity and time reversal. An operator that is odd under CP must therefore also be odd under time reversal if CPT is preserved.

= CPT constraints on particle properties
{c}
{parent=CPT theorem}

In a <CPT>-invariant vacuum, a <subatomic particle> and its <antiparticle> have equal masses and total <decay widths>, equal spin magnitudes and opposite conserved additive charges. <CPT> exchanges initial and final states in a scattering experiment; it relates a process to the reversed process with antiparticles and reversed spins. It does not force equality of every pair of conjugate partial <decay widths> when <CP violation> is present.

= Local Lagrangian argument for CPT invariance
{parent=CPT theorem}

Under <CPT>, a Hermitian local tensor monomial picks up a minus sign for each Lorentz vector index, including derivative indices. A <Lorentz scalar> contracts all such indices, so the total sign is positive; charged terms are exchanged with their <Hermitian conjugates>. Therefore a local Hermitian <Lagrangian density> obeys $\Theta\mathcal L(x)\Theta^{-1}=\mathcal L(-x)$, leaving its <action> invariant. This field-level argument illustrates the <CPT theorem> for ordinary scalar, spinor and vector theories; the axiomatic theorem also uses positive energy and <microcausality>.

= CPT transformation of Dirac bilinears
{c}
{parent=CPT theorem}
{title2=$\Theta\overline\psi\Gamma\psi\Theta^{-1}$}

For a Hermitian <fermion bilinear>, an <antiunitary operator> implementing <CPT> conjugates numerical coefficients and exchanges particle and antiparticle fields at $-x$. Reordering the <fermions> then gives $\Theta:\overline\psi\Gamma\psi(x):\Theta^{-1}=:\overline\psi\gamma_5\Gamma\gamma_5\psi(-x):$ when $\Gamma^\dagger=\gamma^0\Gamma\gamma^0$. Thus scalar, pseudoscalar and tensor bilinears are even, while vector and axial-vector bilinears are odd. Each derivative contributes another minus sign. The graded reordering is essential; treating <Dirac fields> as commuting quantities gives the wrong transformation.

= Global symmetry in field theory
{parent=Classical field theory}

A global symmetry has transformation parameters constant across spacetime and preserves the action. It can relate physically distinct states. Continuous global symmetries give <Noether currents> and conserved charges when boundary fluxes vanish; discrete symmetries do not receive currents from the same infinitesimal theorem. A <gauge redundancy> instead uses local parameters to relate descriptions of the same physical state, except that nontrivial boundary transformations may carry physical charges. The <global phase symmetry of a complex scalar field> is an important internal example.

= Scattering charge selection rule from global gauge symmetry
{parent=Global symmetry in field theory}
{title2=$[Q,S]=0\ \Longrightarrow\ (Q_f-Q_i)\langle f|S|i\rangle=0$}

For a conserved charge $Q$ and invariant vacuum, its global symmetry commutes with the <S-matrix>. Matrix elements between charge eigenstates give the displayed selection rule. Constant electromagnetic phases cancel at every QED vertex, and nonzero physical amplitudes conserve total charge. Local <gauge invariance> strengthens this global rule to the <Ward-Takahashi identity>.

= Massive two-form field
{parent=Classical field theory}
{title2=$\partial^mF_{mnp}=\mu^2A_{np}$}

= Massive antisymmetric tensor field
{synonym}

An antisymmetric tensor $A_{mn}$ with $F=dA$ obeys $\partial^mF_{mnp}=\mu^2A_{np}$ for $\mu\ne0$. Taking a divergence yields $\partial^mA_{mn}=0$, and the remaining equation is the <Klein-Gordon equation> for each component. The physical <particle polarizations> form the <exterior square> of the massive <little group> vector representation, of dimension $(D-1)(D-2)/2$. Unlike a <two-form gauge field>, it has physical longitudinal polarizations.

= Light-cone decomposition of a massive two-form
{parent=Massive two-form field}
{title2=$A_{-i},\ A_{ij}$}

If $\partial_-$ is invertible, the divergence constraint of a <massive two-form field> makes $A_{-i}$ and $A_{ij}$ independent. It gives $A_{+-}=-\partial_-^{-1}\partial_iA_{-i}$ and $A_{+i}=\partial_-^{-1}(-\partial_+A_{-i}+\partial_jA_{ji})$. All independent components obey the <Klein-Gordon equation>. Their count is $(D-2)+\binom{D-2}{2}=\binom{D-1}{2}$.

= Hamiltonian density

A <Hamiltonian density> is a local integrand for the field-theory <Hamiltonian>. For canonical fields with invertible velocity-momentum relation it is the <Legendre transform in mechanics> of the <Lagrangian density>, summing over all conjugate field components. <Gauge theories> require constraints and multiplier terms as well.

= Canonical Hamiltonian density of a real scalar field
{parent=Hamiltonian density}
{title2=$\mathcal H=\tfrac12\pi^2+\tfrac12|\nabla\phi|^2+V(\phi)$}

For a <real scalar field> with <Lagrangian density> $\mathcal L=\dot\phi^2/2-|\nabla\phi|^2/2-V(\phi)$, the <canonical momentum> is $\pi=\dot\phi$. Its <Hamiltonian density> is the sum of momentum energy, gradient energy and potential energy. Hamilton's equations recover the <Euler-Lagrange equation>.

= Gauge theory
{parent=Classical field theory}
{wiki}

A <classical field theory> is a gauge theory when its description has a local redundancy represented by <gauge transformations>. A <gauge field> defines the <covariant derivative> that compares matter fields at different points, and its <gauge field strength> measures the curvature of that comparison. <Electromagnetism> is the familiar Abelian example; <Yang-Mills theory> generalizes it to a non-Abelian internal group. Both classical equations and their <quantum field theory> can have this structure. Physically equivalent gauge descriptions must give the same observables, even though the <gauge potential> changes.

= Noether gauging principle
{c}
{synonym}

Localizing a parameter of a global <symmetry> produces its <Noether current> times a derivative of that parameter. Introduce a <gauge field> with an inhomogeneous transformation and couple it to the <Noether current> to cancel this variation. Recompute the remaining variation and add the required interaction and transformation terms. Closure of the local <symmetry> algebra constrains the completion. For <supersymmetry>, its <supercurrent> couples to the <gravitino>, and the translation in the <supersymmetry> algebra requires a <vierbein>.

= Internal symmetry of a classical field theory
{parent=Classical field theory}

An internal symmetry changes field values while leaving spacetime coordinates fixed, preserving the <action>. A constant phase rotation of a <complex scalar field> is an example. This distinguishes internal charges from the spacetime translation charges in the <canonical stress-energy tensor>. Localizing the parameter generally requires gauge fields; a global internal symmetry is not automatically a gauge symmetry.

= Variational symmetry of a Lagrangian density
{parent=Classical field theory}

A differentiable infinitesimal transformation is a variational symmetry when its change of the <Lagrangian density> is a divergence, $\delta\mathcal L=\varepsilon\partial_\mu K^\mu$. Under suitable <boundary conditions> the <action> is invariant, so exact pointwise invariance is unnecessary. The off-shell variation identity gives the <Noether current for a first-derivative scalar field>. This definition is for a constant transformation parameter; a spacetime-dependent parameter is a different, gauge-symmetry problem.

= Scalar field
{parent=Classical field theory}
{wiki}

A scalar field assigns one scalar to each point of <spacetime>. Under a change of coordinates, its value at a physical point is unchanged.

= Minimally coupled scalar field
{parent=Scalar field}
{title2=$L=-\tfrac12g^{ab}\nabla_a\phi\nabla_b\phi-V(\phi)$}

A real <scalar field> is minimally coupled to a <metric tensor> when its kinetic term uses the metric contraction of first derivatives and its <scalar potential> depends only on the field, without an explicit <scalar curvature> coupling. For <metric signature> $(-,+,+,+)$ the displayed <Lagrangian> and volume density $\sqrt{-g}$ give $\Box\phi-V'(\phi)=0$. Its <stress-energy tensor> is $T_{ab}=\nabla_a\phi\nabla_b\phi-g_{ab}[\tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2+V]$. The <scalar stress-energy divergence identity> explains its conservation on the field equation.

For a <minimally coupled scalar field> with a <Levi-Civita connection>, <metric compatibility> and the <symmetric Hessian of a scalar field> give the displayed off-shell identity. It is a special case of the <diffeomorphism Noether identity for a scalar field>, and gives <stress-energy conservation> when the scalar equation is satisfied. A <scalar field> with no spacetime coordinates reduces its <path integral> to an ordinary integral. It preserves <Wick contractions> and <Feynman-diagram symmetry factors>, making it a useful model of <perturbative quantum field theory>.

= Zero-dimensional quartic perturbation series
{parent=Zero-dimensional scalar field theory}
{title2=$I(\lambda)/I(0)=1-\lambda/(8m^4)+35\lambda^2/(384m^8)+\cdots$}

For $m^2>0$, the <zero-dimensional scalar field theory> with <Euclidean action> $m^2x^2/2+\lambda x^4/4!$ has a <Gaussian integral> normalization $I(0)=\sqrt{2\pi/m^2}$. Its <Feynman rules> assign $1/m^2$ to each <propagator>, $-\lambda$ to each quartic vertex, and the reciprocal <Feynman-diagram symmetry factor> to each <vacuum diagram>. The integral itself includes disconnected <vacuum diagrams>; its logarithm selects connected ones. At second order the three possible numbers of edges joining the two vertices are zero, two and four, with <Feynman-diagram symmetry factors> $128,16,48$ respectively. Their weights add to $35\lambda^2/(384m^8)$.

The <Wick contractions> give the general coefficient $I(0)(-1)^v(4v-1)!!/[v!(4!)^v m^{4v}]$. Successive absolute coefficients have ratio $(4v+3)(4v+1)/[24(v+1)m^4]$, which tends to infinity. Thus this is an <asymptotic expansion> as $\lambda\downarrow0$, rather than a convergent <power series>. For every fixed truncation the <Taylor theorem> remainder estimate for $e^{-u}$, $u\geq0$, can be integrated against the <Gaussian distribution> to justify the corresponding remainder bound.

A scalar potential is the derivative-free energy density $V(\phi)$ of a <scalar field>. Its derivative supplies the force term in the field equation, and its local minima describe candidate vacuum field values.

= Flat direction of a scalar potential
{parent=Scalar potential}

= Flat directions of a scalar potential
{synonym}

In vacuum analysis, an exact <flat direction of a scalar potential> is a nonconstant continuous family of degenerate stationary vacua. The <scalar potential> stays constant along this family and its <gradient> vanishes there. An ordinary level set does not suffice: $V(x,y)=x^2+y^2$ is constant on every circle, but its vacuum at the origin has no flat direction. A vanishing <eigenvalue> of the quadratic <Hessian matrix> also does not suffice: $V(x)=x^4$ has zero quadratic mass at zero but no family of nearby vacua. Exact flat directions can be lifted by corrections absent from the classical <scalar potential>.

A scalar-field shift symmetry acts by $\phi(x)\mapsto\phi(x)+c$ for constant $c$. Its <Noether charge> is the spatial integral of the canonical momentum, up to the normalization of $c$, and interactions invariant under the symmetry contain derivatives of the scalar field.

Lagrangian field theory specifies an <action> by integrating a Lagrangian density $\mathcal L(\phi,\partial\phi,x)$ over spacetime. Requiring its <first variation> to vanish for compactly supported field variations yields the field <Euler-Lagrange equations>. The variational <Lagrangian> is the integrand, whereas this field-theoretic formalism specifies the continuum fields and their dynamics.

A <Lagrangian density> is integrated over spacetime to give the <action> $S=\int d^dx\,\mathcal L$ in <Lagrangian field theory>.

A kinetic term contains derivatives of a field and determines its free propagation. Its quadratic differential operator becomes the inverse <quantum field theory propagator>.

= Gauge-covariant kinetic term

A gauge-covariant kinetic term replaces ordinary derivatives by covariant derivatives. Expanding it generates interactions between the matter field and one or more gauge fields, including contact vertices with two gauge bosons.

A mass term is a quadratic term without derivatives whose coefficient sets the mass scale of a free field.

A field interaction term is nonlinear in the fields and produces the interaction vertices of perturbation theory.

= Euler-Lagrange field equation
{parent=Lagrangian density}

For a field $\Phi$,
$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\Phi}
-\partial_\mu\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\Phi)}=0.
$$

A field configuration or identity is on shell when the <Euler-Lagrange field equations>[equations of motion] hold.

= Noether current
{title2=$j^\mu$}
{parent=Classical field theory}
{c}

A continuous variational symmetry gives a current satisfying $\partial_\mu j^\mu=0$ <on shell>. Integrating $j^0$ over space gives a conserved charge when boundary flux vanishes.

= Conformal current
{parent=Noether current}
{title2=$j^\mu=\Theta^{\mu\nu}\omega_\nu$}

For a symmetric conserved <stress-energy tensor> $\Theta$ and a <conformal Killing vector field> $\omega$ in dimension $d$, the displayed <conformal current> has divergence $(\partial\cdot\omega)\Theta^\mu{}_{\mu}/d$. This follows by contracting the <conformal Killing equation> with $\Theta$. A traceless tensor therefore gives a conserved current for every conformal generator.

= Virial current
{parent=Noether current}
{title2=$T^\mu{}_{\mu}=\partial_\mu V^\mu$}

A <virial current> $V^\mu$ represents the <trace> of a conserved <stress-energy tensor> as a divergence. Then the dilation <Noether current> $D^\mu=x_\nu T^{\mu\nu}-V^\mu$ is conserved, since its divergence is $T^\mu{}_{\mu}-\partial_\mu V^\mu$. This establishes <scale invariance>; a traceless <stress-energy tensor improvement> is the extra condition needed for <conformal currents>.

= Noether current for a spacetime symmetry
{c}
{parent=Noether current}
{title2=$j^\mu=\sum_a\Pi_a^\mu Q_a+\mathcal L\xi^\mu-K^\mu$}

For $x'^\mu=x^\mu+\varepsilon\xi^\mu$ and $\phi'_a(x')=\phi_a(x)+\varepsilon\Delta_a$, the fixed-coordinate field variation is $Q_a=\Delta_a-\xi^\nu\partial_\nu\phi_a$. Suppose action invariance is expressed as $\delta_0\mathcal L/\varepsilon+\partial_\mu(\mathcal L\xi^\mu)=\partial_\mu K^\mu$. Writing $\Pi_a^\mu=\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)$ and $E_a=\mathcal L_{\phi_a}-\partial_\mu\Pi_a^\mu$, the product rule gives $\partial_\mu j^\mu=-\sum_a E_aQ_a$. Thus $j^\mu$ is a <conserved current> <on shell>. A vanishing spatial boundary flux is additionally required for its <Noether charge> to be conserved. This formula includes internal transformations, translations and <Lorentz transformations>.

= Noether current for a first-derivative scalar field
{c}
{parent=Noether current}
{title2=$j^\mu=\mathcal L_{\partial_\mu\phi}X-K^\mu$}

For $\delta\phi=\varepsilon X$ and a <variational symmetry of a Lagrangian density>, write $\Pi^\mu=\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)$ and $\mathcal E=\mathcal L_\phi-\partial_\mu\Pi^\mu$. The product rule gives $\delta\mathcal L/\varepsilon=\mathcal E X+\partial_\mu(\Pi^\mu X)$. Therefore $j^\mu=\Pi^\mu X-K^\mu$ obeys $\partial_\mu j^\mu=-\mathcal E X$, so it is a <conserved current> <on shell>. Spatial integration gives a conserved <Noether charge> only when the boundary flux vanishes. The formula extends to several components by summation.

= Conserved current
{parent=Noether current}

= Current conservation
{synonym}

A conserved current is a vector field $j^\mu$ satisfying the continuity equation $\partial_\mu j^\mu=0$. Its spatial integral is constant in time when no current crosses the boundary.

= Conserved charge
{parent=Conserved current}
{title2=$Q=\int j^0\,dx$}

An integral of the time component of a <conserved current> is a <conserved charge> when the spatial boundary flux vanishes. Charges need not come from an immediately apparent elementary symmetry; a field theory can have an infinite higher-derivative current hierarchy. A <Noether charge> is obtained from the symmetry current supplied by the <Noether theorem>.

= Conserved-current two-point function
{parent=Conserved current}

In a massless four-dimensional theory, current conservation gives a transverse tensor structure, $\int d^4x\,e^{-ikx}\langle J_\mu(x)J_\nu(0)\rangle=(k_\mu k_\nu-\delta_{\mu\nu}k^2)G(k^2)$. With current <mass dimension> three, the scalar coefficient has dimension zero.

= Noether charge
{title2=$Q$}
{c}
{parent=Noether current}

A Noether charge is the spatial integral $Q=\int j^0d^3x$ of a <Noether current>. It is time-independent when the current has vanishing flux through the spatial boundary, and in a Hamiltonian formulation it generates the associated continuous symmetry.

= Dilatation current
{title2=$D^\mu$}
{parent=Noether current}

The dilatation current associated with scale transformations is $D^\mu=x_\nu T^{\mu\nu}$ up to improvement terms. Its divergence is the trace of the stress-energy tensor, so classical scale invariance requires a traceless improved tensor on shell.

= Canonical stress-energy tensor
{title2=$T^{\mu\nu}_{\mathrm{can}}$}
{parent=Noether current}

= Canonical energy-momentum tensor
{synonym}

Spacetime translations give the canonical stress-energy tensor
$$
T^\mu{}_{\nu}=\sum_a
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\Phi_a)}
\partial_\nu\Phi_a-\delta^\mu_\nu\mathcal L.
$$

= Scalar-field momentum flux
{parent=Canonical stress-energy tensor}
{title2=$T^{01}=-\phi_t\phi_x,\quad T^{11}=\tfrac12\phi_t^2+\tfrac12\phi_x^2-V$}

For a real <scalar field> in one spatial dimension with metric signature $(+,-)$ and potential $V$, the <stress-energy conservation> law is $\partial_tT^{01}+\partial_xT^{11}=0$. The force on the field to the left of a fixed cut is $-T^{11}$ at the cut when the far-left boundary flux vanishes. <Finite-energy field configurations> also permit this conclusion by spatial cutoffs, without assuming pointwise decay of all derivatives.

= Four-momentum of a free real scalar field
{parent=Canonical stress-energy tensor}
{title2=$P^\mu=\int T^{0\mu}\,d^3x$}

For the free <real scalar field> with signature $(+,-,-,-)$,
$$
E=\frac12\int(\dot\phi^2+|\nabla\phi|^2+m^2\phi^2)d^3x,
\qquad \mathbf P=-\int\dot\phi\,\nabla\phi\,d^3x.
$$
The <canonical stress-energy tensor> is $T^{\mu\nu}=\partial^\mu\phi\partial^\nu\phi-\eta^{\mu\nu}\mathcal L$. Its divergence vanishes by the <Klein-Gordon equation>, giving constant integrated charges when integrals and boundary flux are well behaved. The minus sign in contravariant spatial <momentum> is from $\partial^i=-\partial_i$; lower-index spatial charges have the opposite sign. A <plane wave> $\cos(Et-\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x)$ has <momentum> density pointing along $\mathbf k$.

= Normal-ordered free scalar four-momentum
{parent=Four-momentum of a free real scalar field}
{title2=$P^\mu=\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}p^\mu a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger a_{\mathbf p}$}

For a canonically quantized <real scalar field>, the quadratic Hamiltonian contains $\frac12\sum_{\mathbf p}E_{\mathbf p}$ in a regulated box. <Normal ordering> subtracts this <zero-point energy>, leaving
$$
P^\mu=\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}p^\mu a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger a_{\mathbf p}.
$$
The vacuum momentum is zero with an inversion-symmetric regulator. The <canonical commutation relation> then gives $[P^\mu,a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger]=p^\mu a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger$, identifying the momentum and energy added by one particle. The un-subtracted energy differs by a constant and is not literally the same operator.

= Translation of a scalar creation operator
{parent=Normal-ordered free scalar four-momentum}
{title2=$e^{-i\mathbf P\cdot\mathbf y}a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger e^{i\mathbf P\cdot\mathbf y}=e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf y}a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger$}

The <canonical commutation relation> gives $[P^\mu,a_p^\dagger]=p^\mu a_p^\dagger$ for a free <real scalar field>. Differentiate the exponential conjugation: its derivative is a scalar multiple of the same operator, yielding the displayed phase. The <annihilation operator> receives the opposite phase, so at equal time $e^{-i\mathbf P\cdot\mathbf y}\phi(\mathbf x)e^{i\mathbf P\cdot\mathbf y}=\phi(\mathbf x+\mathbf y)$. The <spatial translation operator> translates a state's position wavefunction in the opposite argument direction.

= Canonical stress-energy tensor of the Maxwell field
{parent=Canonical stress-energy tensor}
{title2=$T^{\mu\nu}=-F^{\mu\rho}\partial^\nu A_\rho+\frac14\eta^{\mu\nu}F_{\rho\sigma}F^{\rho\sigma}$}

For signature $(+,-,-,-)$, translation invariance of the <Maxwell Lagrangian> gives this tensor. It is generally neither symmetric nor gauge invariant. The <antisymmetric superpotential improvement> $-\partial_\rho(F^{\rho\mu}A^\nu)$ produces, on the source-free equations of motion, the symmetric <electromagnetic stress-energy tensor> $\Theta^{\mu\nu}=-F^{\mu\rho}F^\nu{}_{\rho}+\eta^{\mu\nu}F^2/4$, which is traceless in four spacetime dimensions.

= Gauge-invariant Maxwell stress-energy tensor
{parent=Canonical stress-energy tensor of the Maxwell field}
{title2=$\Theta^{\mu\nu}$}

In signature $+---$, the <Maxwell Lagrangian> yields
$$
\Theta^\mu{}_{\nu}=-F^{\mu\rho}F_{\nu\rho}+\tfrac14\delta^\mu{}_{\nu}F_{\rho\sigma}F^{\rho\sigma}.
$$
It is symmetric after raising its second index, <gauge invariant>, and conserved on the source-free <Maxwell equations>. Its energy density is $\frac12(\mathbf E^2+\mathbf B^2)$ and its energy flux is the <Poynting vector>. Its difference from the <canonical stress-energy tensor> is an antisymmetric superpotential divergence plus an equation-of-motion term, so integrated charges coincide when the constraints and vanishing boundary terms are imposed.

= Antisymmetric superpotential improvement
{parent=Canonical stress-energy tensor}
{title2=$T^{\mu\nu}\mapsto T^{\mu\nu}+\partial_\rho B^{\rho\mu\nu}$}

If $B^{\rho\mu\nu}=-B^{\mu\rho\nu}$, commuting partial derivatives gives $\partial_\mu\partial_\rho B^{\rho\mu\nu}=0$. Such an improvement preserves conservation of a <stress-energy tensor> and changes integrated charges only by a spatial boundary term. The <Belinfante-Rosenfeld stress-energy tensor> is obtained through this type of improvement.

= Belinfante-Rosenfeld stress-energy tensor

{parent=Canonical stress-energy tensor}

The Belinfante-Rosenfeld construction adds the divergence of a spin-current superpotential to the <canonical stress-energy tensor>, producing a symmetric conserved tensor with the same integrated four-momentum under suitable boundary conditions.

= Lorentz current
{title2=$J^{\mu\rho\sigma}$}
{c}
{parent=Canonical stress-energy tensor}

For a symmetric conserved stress-energy tensor, the Lorentz current is $J^{\mu\rho\sigma}=x^\rho T^{\mu\sigma}-x^\sigma T^{\mu\rho}$. Its spatial integrals generate rotations and boosts.

= Dirac current
{c}
{title2=$j^\mu=\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\psi$}
{parent=Noether current}
{wiki}

The global phase symmetry of a <Dirac field> gives the conserved Dirac current $j^\mu=\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\psi$.

= Causal Dirac spinor current
{parent=Dirac current}
{title2=$V^0=\epsilon^\dagger\epsilon,\quad V^2\le0$}

For a commuting spinor in mostly-plus signature, orient its <Dirac current> to the future and write $V^i=\epsilon^\dagger\alpha^i\epsilon$, with Hermitian $\alpha^i=-\gamma^0\gamma^i$. For every spatial <unit vector> $u$, $(u\cdot\alpha)^2=1$ implies $|u\cdot V|\le\epsilon^\dagger\epsilon=V^0$. Choosing $u$ along the spatial current proves $V^2\le0$. Thus spinor bilinears supply causal <vectors> suitable for energy-condition estimates. A Grassmann-valued spinor does not have this ordinary positivity interpretation.

= Null current of a commuting Majorana spinor
{parent=Causal Dirac spinor current}
{title2=$K^\mu K_\mu=0$}

In four dimensions a single commuting <Majorana spinor> is specified by a two-component complex spinor $\xi$. Its future <Dirac current> is proportional to $(\xi^\dagger\xi,\xi^\dagger\boldsymbol\sigma\xi)$, and the <Pauli matrix multiplication law> implies $|\xi^\dagger\boldsymbol\sigma\xi|^2=(\xi^\dagger\xi)^2$. Thus it is null. A Dirac spinor or a sum of independent Majorana currents can be timelike. This distinction matters when applying a timelike-current classification to minimal real supersymmetry.

= Gordon identity
{c}
{parent=Dirac current}
{title2=$2m\bar u\prime\gamma^a u$}

= Gordon decomposition
{c}
{synonym}

The <Gordon identity> rewrites a vector-current matrix element between equal-mass on-shell <Dirac spinors> using a momentum term and an antisymmetric spin term. In the mostly-minus convention $\{\Gamma^a,\Gamma^b\}=2\eta^{ab}$, $(\not p-m)u=0$, and $\Gamma^{ab}=[\Gamma^a,\Gamma^b]/2$, it reads $2m\bar u'\Gamma^au=\bar u'[(p'+p)^a-\Gamma^{ab}(p'_b-p_b)]u$. Derive it by adding the two external <Dirac equation> identities. The equivalent formula in the <mostly-plus Dirac convention> is $\bar u'[\gamma^{ab}(p'_b-p_b)+2m\gamma^a+p^a+p'^a]u=0$. The identity before division by $m$ remains valid for massless equal-mass states.

= Dirac electromagnetic current
{c}
{parent=Dirac current}

The Dirac electromagnetic current is the electric charge times the <Dirac current>. Its coupling $-ej^\mu A_\mu$ to the <electromagnetic four-potential> is the matter interaction of <quantum electrodynamics>.

= Derivative coupling to a conserved current
{parent=Dirac current}

An interaction $-\lambda j^\mu\partial_\mu\phi$ with an exactly <conserved current> is redundant: integration by parts gives $\lambda\phi\,\partial_\mu j^\mu$ up to a boundary term. For the <Dirac current>, the local phase redefinition $\psi=e^{-i\lambda\phi}\chi$ removes the coupling, so its on-shell scattering amplitudes vanish.

= Fock space
{c}
{parent=Quantized field}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mathcal F_\pm(H)$}

For a one-particle <Hilbert space> $H$, the bosonic or fermionic <Fock space> is $\mathcal F_\pm(H)=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}H^{\otimes_\pm n}$, using symmetric or antisymmetric tensor powers. Its zeroth summand is the one-dimensional vacuum sector. <Creation operators> and <annihilation operators> change particle number. The <bosonic Fock space> and <fermionic Fock space> implement the corresponding exchange statistics.

= One-particle state
{parent=Fock space}
{title2=$a^\dagger(f)|0\rangle$}

A <one-particle state> belongs to the one-excitation sector of a <Fock space>. In a momentum basis with $[a_{\mathbf p},a_{\mathbf q}^\dagger]=(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p-\mathbf q)$, the wavepacket $\int d^3p\,f(\mathbf p)a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger|0\rangle/(2\pi)^3$ has norm squared $\int d^3p\,|f(\mathbf p)|^2/(2\pi)^3$. Sharp-momentum states have distributional rather than unit normalization; factors of $\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}$ convert to <relativistic normalization of a one-particle state>.

= Occupation number
{parent=Fock space}
{title2=$n_k$}

The eigenvalue of a mode number operator counts excitations in that mode. <Bosonic occupation numbers> range over all nonnegative integers; <fermionic occupation numbers> are zero or one.

= Fermionic occupation number
{parent=Occupation number}
{title2=$n^F_k\in\{0,1\}$}

For $\{f,f^\dagger\}_+=1$ and $f^2=0$, the <occupation number> operator $f^\dagger f$ has eigenvalues zero and one. This gives the single-mode exclusion principle.

= Bosonic occupation number
{parent=Occupation number}
{title2=$n^B_k\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}$}

For $[a,a^\dagger]=1$, the <occupation number> operator $a^\dagger a$ has eigenvalues $0,1,2,\ldots$ on the bosonic oscillator <Fock space>.

= Operator-valued distribution
{parent=Quantized field}
{title2=$\phi(f)=\int\phi(x)f(x)\,d^dx$}

An operator-valued distribution assigns a <linear operator> on a common dense domain to each <smooth> <test function> of <compact support> $f$, continuously and linearly in the appropriate weak sense. A <quantized field> is generally of this type: $\phi(x)$ is formal point notation, while the smeared $\phi(f)$ has operator meaning. Equal-time <canonical commutation relations> and <momentum> oscillators are interpreted after smearing, which avoids treating a <Dirac delta distribution> or a coincident field as a finite ordinary operator. Products at coincident points can need further regularization even when individual smeared fields are defined.

= Mode expansion of a free field
{parent=Quantized field}

A free field is expanded in positive- and negative-frequency plane waves multiplied by annihilation and creation operators.

= Coincident-point vacuum field variance
{parent=Mode expansion of a free field}
{title2=$\langle X(\mathbf x)^2\rangle=\int d\log k\,\Delta_X(k)$}

For a free field in the annihilation-operator vacuum, the coincident two-point function is its mode-power integral. Unsmeared vacuum products generally have an ultraviolet divergence. A finite band or spatial smearing defines a regulated variance without altering the meaning of a mode-by-mode <dimensionless power spectrum>.

= Positive-frequency part of a quantum field
{parent=Mode expansion of a free field}
{title2=$A^{(+)}$}

For a free field, its positive-frequency part consists of the annihilation terms multiplying $e^{-i\omega t}$ with $\omega>0$; its negative-frequency part consists of the creation terms. The sign refers to positive <energy>, even though the exponential has a minus sign. Imposing the <Lorenz gauge> only through $(\partial\cdot A)^{(+)}|\mathrm{phys}\rangle=0$ is the subsidiary condition in <Gupta-Bleuler quantization>, not an operator identity for the whole divergence.

= Wronskian normalization
{parent=Mode expansion of a free field}

The <canonical commutation relation> fixes the antisymmetric product of a mode function $f$ and its canonical-momentum mode $g$. In the convention $[a,a^\dagger]=1$, one commonly has $fg^*-f^*g=i$.

= Fock state

A Fock state has specified occupation numbers. Applying creation operators to the vacuum constructs multiparticle momentum eigenstates.

= Relativistic normalization of a one-particle state
{parent=Fock state}
{title2=$\langle\mathbf p|\mathbf q\rangle=2E_{\mathbf p}(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p-\mathbf q)$}

If creation operators obey $[a_{\mathbf p},a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}]=(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p-\mathbf q)$, set $|\mathbf p\rangle=\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle$. Their <inner product> then has the displayed value. The associated completeness measure is $d^3p/((2\pi)^3 2E_{\mathbf p})$, the invariant positive-energy <mass shell> measure. Specifying this normalization prevents hidden external energy factors in a <scattering amplitude>.

= Fock vacuum

The oscillator vacuum is annihilated by all <annihilation operators>. Acting with <creation operators> constructs <Fock states>. In single-string quantization this is the unexcited string, not the empty spacetime state.

= Intrinsic parity
{title2=$\gamma_p$}
{parent=Quantized field}
{wiki}

Intrinsic parity is the phase by which a field transforms under spatial inversion after its spacetime argument is reflected. For a Hermitian scalar field it is $+1$ or $-1$.

= Opposite intrinsic parities of a Dirac particle and antiparticle
{parent=Intrinsic parity}

In the <Dirac representation of the gamma matrices>, the <parity symmetry in quantum field theory> action $\psi_P(x)=\gamma^0\psi(x_P)$ and the <charge conjugation> action $\psi_C=i\gamma^2\psi^*$ obey $(\psi_P)_C(x)=-\gamma^0\psi_C(x_P)$, since $\gamma^0$ is real and anticommutes with $\gamma^2$. At rest, positive-frequency <Dirac spinors> have nonzero upper components and negative-frequency <Dirac spinors> have nonzero lower components; they are respectively $+1$ and $-1$ eigenvectors of $\gamma^0$. With the common conventional real intrinsic-parity phase, particle and antiparticle therefore have opposite <intrinsic parity>. The overall sign is conventional, whereas their relative sign is fixed.

= Parity action on canonical massive spin states
{parent=Intrinsic parity}
{title2=$P|p,s,\sigma\rangle=\eta_P|p^0,-\mathbf p,s,\sigma\rangle$}

Choose canonical spin states by applying the rotationless <Lorentz boost> $L(p)$ to rest-frame spin states. Spatial inversion $\Pi=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$ commutes with rest-frame rotations, so <Schur lemma> makes its action on one irreducible rest spin multiplet a phase $\eta_P$. Since $\Pi L(p)\Pi=L(\Pi p)$, the formula follows. Canonical spin labels are unchanged; <helicity> reverses. With $P^2=I$, the <intrinsic parity> is $\eta_P=\pm1$.

= Gaussian functional integral
{parent=Quantized field}

A Gaussian functional integral has an exponent quadratic in a bosonic field. Completing the square expresses its source dependence through the inverse quadratic operator, the propagator.

= Euclidean scalar source functional and Laplacian sign
{c}
{parent=Gaussian functional integral}
{title2=$Z[J]=\mathcal N\exp[-\tfrac12JGJ],\quad\Delta G=\delta$}

For $S[\phi]=\tfrac12\int(\partial\phi)^2$ and a source appearing as $-\int J\phi$ in the exponent, the positive quadratic operator is $A=-\Delta$. Completing the square gives $Z[J]=\mathcal N\exp[\tfrac12JA^{-1}J]$. If one writes it with a negative quadratic source exponent, its kernel must be $G=-A^{-1}=\Delta^{-1}$. In the plane $G(z,w)=(2\pi)^{-1}\log(|z-w|/L)$, up to harmonic additions and infrared conventions. The physical covariance is its negative. Zero modes are regulated or separated; oscillatory sources in a string path integral produce <momentum conservation> from the constant mode.

= Gaussian covariance differentiation identity
{c}
{parent=Gaussian functional integral}
{title2=$\partial_t\mathbb E_{C_t}W=\mathbb E_{C_t}[\partial_tW+\tfrac12\dot C_{ab}\partial_a\partial_bW]$}

For a positive differentiable covariance $C_t$ and a sufficiently regular, integrable $W$, differentiating the normalized <Gaussian integral> and integrating by parts twice gives the displayed identity. An unnormalized integral has the additional field-independent term $\operatorname{Tr}(C_t^{-1}\dot C_t)/2$ times the integral. A finite-mode regulator makes the analogous <Gaussian functional integral> identity precise before taking a continuum limit.

= Gaussian evaluation of a free scalar generating functional
{parent=Gaussian functional integral}
{c}

Completing the square in the free scalar <path integral> gives a quadratic exponential in the source. If $\Delta_F$ includes the factor $i$ in the momentum-space <Feynman propagator>, the normalized Minkowski functional is $\exp[-\int J\Delta_FJ/2]$. Two source derivatives with their $1/i^2$ factor recover $\Delta_F$, while higher derivatives give <Wick contractions>.

= Functional determinant
{parent=Gaussian functional integral}
{wiki}

A Gaussian path integral over a bosonic field with quadratic operator $A$ produces a factor proportional to $(\det A)^{-1/2}$. When $A$ depends on background fields, this functional determinant contributes to their quantum effective action.

= Functional Jacobian
{parent=Functional determinant}

For a change of field variables $\phi\mapsto F[\phi]$, the functional Jacobian is the <functional determinant> of the linearized change. At a finite regulator it is an ordinary <Jacobian determinant> on the finitely many field coordinates; the continuum expression denotes the regulated limit. It changes the <functional integral> measure and can depend on the background field. The <Faddeev-Popov determinant> is the example obtained by replacing the integration coordinate along a <gauge orbit> by a chosen <gauge fixing> condition.

= Grassmann Gaussian integral
{c}
{parent=Quantized field}

= Fermionic Gaussian integral
{synonym}

A Grassmann Gaussian integral satisfies $\int D\bar\psi D\psi\,e^{-\bar\psi A\psi}=\det A$. Adding Grassmann sources and completing the square produces an exponent containing $A^{-1}$.

= Real Grassmann Gaussian integral
{parent=Grassmann Gaussian integral}
{title2=$\int d^{2m}\omega\,e^{-\omega^TA\omega/2}\propto\operatorname{Pf}(A)$}

For an <antisymmetric matrix> $A$, a <Berezin integral> extracts the top coefficient of the exponential. Expansion into pairings gives the <Pfaffian>, with a constant sign depending on the order of the differentials. This is a polynomial in the entries of $A$, not the positive square root of its <determinant>: in dimension two it is proportional to $A_{12}$ and changes sign with that entry. The identity $\operatorname{Pf}(A)^2=\det A$ determines its square but does not discard its orientation.

= Real-complex compatibility of Grassmann Gaussian integrals
{parent=Real Grassmann Gaussian integral}
{title2=$\operatorname{Pf}(\operatorname{diag}(M,M))=\det M$}

For an even-dimensional <antisymmetric matrix> $M$, put $\theta=(u+iv)/\sqrt2$ and $\bar\theta=(u-iv)/\sqrt2$, with independent <Grassmann variables> $u,v$. Anticommutation cancels the mixed terms, giving $\bar\theta^TM\theta=(u^TMu+v^TMv)/2$. The constant <Grassmann change-of-variables formula> changes only the normalization. The two real <Pfaffians> multiply to $\operatorname{Pf}(M)^2=\det M$, agreeing with the complex <Grassmann Gaussian integral>. In odd dimension $\det M=0$ and both top-degree integrals vanish.

= Ordered Grassmann Gaussian contractions
{parent=Grassmann Gaussian integral}
{title2=$\langle\theta_i\bar\theta_j\theta_k\bar\theta_l\rangle=C_{ij}C_{kl}-C_{il}C_{kj},\quad C=B^{-1}$}

Use the <Berezin integral> orientation $\int\prod_i d\bar\theta_i d\theta_i\,\theta_1\bar\theta_1\cdots\theta_n\bar\theta_n=1$. For an invertible <matrix> $B$, the normalized weight $e^{-\bar\theta B\theta}$ has $\langle\theta_i\bar\theta_j\rangle=(B^{-1})_{ij}$. Differentiate its <determinant> with respect to $B_{ji}$ to obtain this formula; two derivatives give the displayed four-point formula. The second pairing carries a <fermionic sign>. These are ordered contractions, so exchanging two odd factors changes their sign.

= Covariance of Grassmann bilinears
{parent=Ordered Grassmann Gaussian contractions}
{title2=$\langle(\bar\theta M\theta)(\bar\theta N\theta)\rangle_c=-\operatorname{tr}(MCNC)$}

For $C=B^{-1}$, <ordered Grassmann Gaussian contractions> give $\langle\bar\theta M\theta\rangle=-\operatorname{tr}(MC)$ and $\langle(\bar\theta M\theta)(\bar\theta N\theta)\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(MC)\operatorname{tr}(NC)-\operatorname{tr}(MCNC)$. The disconnected contribution has two <fermion loops>; the connected contribution has one, giving its minus sign. This covariance is an algebraic normalized <Berezin integral>, rather than a positivity statement about a probability measure.

= Bosonic trace vertices from a finite fermionic determinant
{parent=Grassmann Gaussian integral}
{title2=$V_{\rm eff}(x)=-\operatorname{Tr}\log[I-gB^{-1}\sum_kx_kC^{(k)}]$}

Integrating finite <Grassmann variables> out of a quadratic action gives $\det[B-gC(x)]$. If $B$ is invertible, divide by $\det B$ and expand its logarithm to obtain $V_{\rm eff}=\sum_{m\geq1}g^m\operatorname{Tr}[B^{-1}C(x)]^m/m$. Since the bosonic variables commute, an $m$-leg effective <Feynman rule> uses the fully symmetrized trace tensor, with weight $-g^m(m-1)!\operatorname{Sym}\operatorname{Tr}[B^{-1}C^{(k_1)}\cdots B^{-1}C^{(k_m)}]$. The minus sign comes from $e^{-V_{\rm eff}}$. This organizes closed <fermion loops> into bosonic vertices. It is a formal perturbative identity; a singular $B$ prevents the normalized expansion, even though the unfactored determinant polynomial still exists.

= Gaussian generating functional for a Dirac field
{c}
{parent=Grassmann Gaussian integral}
{title2=$Z[J,\bar J]=Z[0,0]e^{-i\bar J K_F^{-1}J}$}

For a free <Dirac field> with action $\int\bar\psi K\psi$ and independent odd sources coupled as $\bar\psi J+\bar J\psi$, translation invariance of the <Berezin integral> and completion of the square give $Z[J,\bar J]=Z[0,0]\exp(-i\bar J K_F^{-1}J)$. Here $K_F^{-1}$ has <Feynman i-epsilon prescription> boundary conditions, and each bilinear includes its spacetime integrations. A left <Grassmann derivative> with respect to $\bar J$, followed by a right <Grassmann derivative> with respect to $J$, has normalized value $-iK_F^{-1}$ at zero sources. Multiplying by $-1$ removes the two insertion factors $i^2$ and yields the <Dirac propagator> $S_F=iK_F^{-1}$. Keeping source order fixed prevents a spurious <fermionic sign>.

= Propagator
{parent=Quantized field}
{wiki}

= Quantum field theory propagator
{synonym}

A propagator is a two-point kernel describing propagation and, in a free theory, a <Green function> for the quadratic kinetic operator. Its boundary prescription determines its type: the time-ordered <Feynman propagator> uses an $i\epsilon$ prescription, while retarded and advanced propagators impose causal boundary conditions.

= Feynman propagator
{parent=Propagator}
{wiki}

The Feynman propagator is the time-ordered Green function selected by the $i\epsilon$ prescription.

= Scalar Feynman propagator pole prescription
{parent=Feynman propagator}
{title2=$\widetilde\Delta_F(p)=i/(p^2-m^2+i0)$}

With $\Delta_F=\langle0|T\{\phi(x)\phi(y)\}|0\rangle$, for a mode of positive <energy> $E>0$ the <Feynman i-epsilon prescription> puts poles $+E-i0$ and $-E+i0$ in opposite half-planes. The <energy> <contour integral> closes clockwise below for positive time separation and counterclockwise above for negative separation. The <residue theorem> gives $e^{-iE|t|}/(2E)$ in either case. Thus the four-dimensional Fourier integral has numerator $i$ and denominator $p^2-m^2+i0$. The integral is understood as a <distribution> limit, not an ordinary absolutely convergent integral; changing the definition to $i\Delta_F$ would change the displayed normalization.

= Derivative jump of a free scalar time-ordered two-point function
{parent=Scalar Feynman propagator pole prescription}
{title2=$g'_E(0^+)-g'_E(0^-)=-i$}

At fixed spatial <momentum> with mode <energy> $E>0$, $g_E(t)=e^{-iE|t|}/(2E)$ is continuous, with derivatives $-i/2$ and $+i/2$ at zero from opposite sides. Hence its second <distributional derivative> has a $-i\delta(t)$ term, while away from zero $g''_E+E^2g_E=0$. Therefore $(\partial_t^2+E^2)g_E=-i\delta(t)$ and, after the spatial <Fourier transform>, $(\Box+m^2)\Delta_F=-i\delta^4$. This checks the propagator numerator, contour signs and equal-time <canonical commutation relations> independently of residue calculation.

= Feynman i-epsilon prescription
{title2=$i\epsilon$ prescription}
{c}
{parent=Feynman propagator}

= i-epsilon prescription
{synonym}
{c}

The Feynman $i\epsilon$ prescription displaces poles or tilts an integration contour by an infinitesimal imaginary part. It selects vacuum boundary conditions and damps oscillatory integrals at infinite time.

= Wightman function
{parent=Propagator}
{wiki}

A Wightman function is an unordered vacuum correlation function. For a free scalar mode $f_k(t)$, the positive-frequency two-point function is $G^>(t_1,t_2;k)=f_k(t_1)f_k^*(t_2)$.

= Dirac propagator
{parent=Propagator}

The free Dirac propagator is $S_F(p)=i(\not p+m)/(p^2-m^2+i\epsilon)$ and inverts the Dirac kinetic operator with Feynman boundary conditions.

= Mostly-plus Dirac propagator with a real derivative kernel
{parent=Dirac propagator}
{title2=$\langle T\psi\bar\psi\rangle(p)=i(-i\gamma\cdot p+m)/(-p^2-m^2+i0)$}

For action $-\int\bar\psi(\gamma\cdot\partial+m)\psi$, choose <gamma matrices> with $\{\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu\}=2\eta^{\mu\nu}$ and signature $(-,+,\ldots,+)$. The Fourier kernel of $D=\gamma\cdot\partial+m$ is $i\gamma\cdot p+m$. Its algebraic inverse is $(-i\gamma\cdot p+m)/(p^2+m^2)$. The <Grassmann Gaussian integral> for $e^{-i\bar\psi D\psi}$ has covariance $-iD_F^{-1}$, giving the displayed <Dirac propagator>. Its denominator has positive-energy poles below and negative-energy poles above the real energy axis by the <Feynman i-epsilon prescription>. Both the metric and the factor of $i$ in the derivative kernel must be specified when comparing propagator formulas.

= Photon propagator
{parent=Propagator}
{wiki}

In a covariant gauge, the photon propagator is the inverse of the gauge-fixed Maxwell kinetic operator.

= Transverse and longitudinal momentum projectors
{parent=Photon propagator}
{title2=$P_L{}^\mu{}_\nu=k^\mu k_\nu/k^2,\quad P_T=I-P_L$}

At non-null <four-momentum>, these complementary <projectors> satisfy $P_L^2=P_L$, $P_T^2=P_T$ and $P_LP_T=0$. The <gauge-fixed Maxwell kinetic operator> is $-k^2(P_T+\xi^{-1}P_L)$; its inverse gives propagator $-i(P_T+\xi P_L)/k^2$. Transversality here removes one four-dimensional longitudinal direction, leaving rank three; it does not by itself project onto the two physical photon polarizations. The pole requires the <Feynman i-epsilon prescription>.

= Hadronic photon spectral density
{parent=Photon propagator}
{title2=$\rho_h(s)$}

Define the inclusive photon-field tensor for hadronic final states by $W_A^{\mu\nu}=2\pi\rho_h(s)(-g^{\mu\nu}+q^\mu q^\nu/s)$ for timelike $q$ with $s=q^2$ and $q^0>0$. Here each final state has relativistic normalization and the sum includes the four-momentum delta function. This <hadronic photon spectral density> has mass dimension $-2$. At leading electromagnetic order, $\rho_h=e^2\rho_J/s$, with $\rho_J$ the dimensionless <hadronic electromagnetic-current spectral density>. Stating the tensor convention prevents mismatched powers of $s$ or $e$ in an inclusive cross-section.

= Coulomb-gauge photon propagator
{parent=Photon propagator}
{c}
{title2=$D_{00}=i/|\mathbf q|^2$}

For $q=(q^0,\mathbf q)$ with $\mathbf q\ne0$, the <Coulomb gauge> propagator has
$$
D_{00}(q)=\frac{i}{|\mathbf q|^2},\qquad D_{0i}=D_{i0}=0,\qquad D_{ij}(q)=\frac{i}{q^2+i0}\left(\delta_{ij}-\frac{q_iq_j}{|\mathbf q|^2}\right).
$$
The temporal component is instantaneous; the spatial part projects onto transverse photon polarizations. Although these components are not individually Lorentz covariant, their contraction with conserved external currents equals the covariant answer. Zero-spatial-momentum limits should be taken after combining the current contractions.

= Coulomb-gauge propagator between conserved currents
{parent=Coulomb-gauge photon propagator}
{c}
{title2=$J^\mu D_{\mu\nu}^{\rm C}K^\nu=-iJ\cdot K/(q^2+i0)$}

For $q\cdot J=q\cdot K=0$, the instantaneous term in the <Coulomb-gauge photon propagator> combines with its spatial longitudinal subtraction:
$$
J^\mu D_{\mu\nu}^{\rm C}(q)K^\nu=-\frac{i}{q^2+i0}J\cdot K.
$$
The identity follows from $\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf J=q^0J^0$ and $q^2=(q^0)^2-|\mathbf q|^2$. It is equality after contraction with conserved currents, not equality of the uncontracted propagator tensors. It is especially useful in showing that tree scattering amplitudes computed in <Coulomb gauge> are Lorentz invariant.

= Radiation-gauge photon propagator
{parent=Photon propagator}
{title2=$\widetilde D^F_{ij}(k)=i(\delta_{ij}-k_ik_j/|\mathbf k|^2)/(k^2+i0)$}

Time-ordering the <canonical transverse photon field> gives a propagator with numerator equal to the spatial transverse projector and denominator $k^2+i0$. Its two energy poles encode forward propagation of the positive-frequency term and backward propagation of the negative-frequency term. This is the reduced free-field propagator; it is distinct from the covariant four-potential propagator.

= Quantum field theory in curved spacetime
{parent=Quantum field theory}
{wiki}

Quantum field theory in curved spacetime quantizes matter fields on a prescribed classical spacetime geometry.

= Particle creation by a nonstationary spacetime
{parent=Quantum field theory in curved spacetime}
{title2=$\langle N_i^{\rm out}\rangle_{\rm in}=\sum_j|\beta_{ij}|^2$}

= Gravitational particle creation
{synonym}

Positive-frequency modes in stationary asymptotic past and future regions can differ by a <Bogoliubov transformation> mixing positive and negative frequency solutions. The conserved <Klein-Gordon inner product> gives its canonical normalization. The out annihilation operator then contains in creation operators, yielding the displayed number expectation in the initial vacuum. A nonzero mixing coefficient changes the particle content relative to final observers without violating deterministic field evolution.

= Unruh effect
{c}
{parent=Quantum field theory in curved spacetime}
{title2=$T_U=\hbar a/(2\pi c k_B)$}
{wiki}

An observer at constant proper acceleration in the Minkowski vacuum detects a thermal response at this temperature. The associated <Rindler coordinates> separate the observer's accessible wedge from a horizon. This is an observer-dependent particle/detector response, not an assertion that inertial observers measure a thermal bath.

= Hadamard condition
{parent=Quantum field theory in curved spacetime}
{c}

The <Hadamard condition> requires the two-point function of a physical free-field state to have the universal local short-distance singularity prescribed by the spacetime geometry and field equation. It permits state-dependent smooth contributions and enables local renormalization of the <stress-energy tensor>. It does not select a unique <Fock vacuum>.

= Klein-Gordon field
{parent=Quantum field theory in curved spacetime}
{c}
{title2=$\phi$}

A free <Klein-Gordon field> is a scalar obeying $(\Box-m^2-\xi R)\phi=0$ in the mostly-plus <metric signature>. <Global hyperbolicity> provides a well-defined initial-value problem. Quantization additionally needs a state or positive-norm mode splitting; the equation alone does not select particles.

= Symplectic form on scalar-field solutions
{parent=Klein-Gordon field}
{title2=$\Omega(\phi_1,\phi_2)$}

For real <Klein-Gordon field> solutions with suitable support or boundary conditions, $\Omega(\phi_1,\phi_2)=\int_\Sigma(\phi_1n^a\nabla_a\phi_2-\phi_2n^a\nabla_a\phi_1)d\Sigma$ is a conserved <symplectic form>. The field equation makes its current divergence-free. Its complexification gives $(u,v)_{KG}=i\Omega(u^*,v)$, the <Klein-Gordon inner product>. A compatible <complex structure on the Klein-Gordon solution space> is additional data needed for a particle representation.

= Covariant wave operator
{parent=Klein-Gordon field}
{title2=$\Box=\nabla_a\nabla^a$}

On a scalar, the <covariant wave operator> is $\Box\phi=|g|^{-1/2}\partial_a(|g|^{1/2}g^{ab}\partial_b\phi)$. For the <metric signature> $(-+++)$ it has the flat-space form $-\partial_t^2+\nabla^2$. This divergence expression is convenient for <separation of variables> and for integrating the <Klein-Gordon equation> by parts.

= Complex structure on the Klein-Gordon solution space
{parent=Klein-Gordon field}
{title2=$J^2=-I$}

On the real <Klein-Gordon field> solution space with conserved <symplectic form> $\Omega$, a compatible real-linear map $J$ obeys $J^2=-I$, preserves $\Omega$, and makes $\mu(f,g)=\Omega(f,Jg)$ positive definite. Its $+i$ eigenspace in the complexified space has positive <Klein-Gordon inner product> with the convention $(u,v)_{KG}=i\Omega(\bar u,v)$. It defines the one-particle <Hilbert space> and a free-field <Fock vacuum>.

= Conformally coupled scalar field
{parent=Quantum field theory in curved spacetime}
{wiki}

A conformally coupled scalar field includes the curvature coupling that makes the massless scalar action invariant under local conformal rescaling in the appropriate dimension. In four-dimensional de Sitter spacetime its mode equation is equivalent to that of a flat-space massless scalar after conformal rescaling.

= Klein-Gordon inner product
{c}
{parent=Quantum field theory in curved spacetime}

For complex solutions of the <Klein-Gordon equation>,
$$
(\phi_1,\phi_2)_{KG}
=i\int_\Sigma d\Sigma^a
\left(\phi_1^*\nabla_a\phi_2-\phi_2\nabla_a\phi_1^*\right).
$$
The associated current is conserved, so the inner product is independent of the Cauchy hypersurface when boundary flux vanishes.

= Invariant-normalized scalar mode extraction
{parent=Klein-Gordon inner product}
{title2=$a(\mathbf p)=i\int d^3x\,f_p^*\overleftrightarrow{\partial_t}\phi$}

For a free <real scalar field> expanded with measure $d^3p/[(2\pi)^3 2E_p]$ and $f_p=e^{-ip\cdot x}$, the displayed <Klein-Gordon inner product> extracts its <annihilation operator>. Indeed $(f_p,f_q)=(2\pi)^3 2E_p\delta^3(\mathbf p-\mathbf q)$ and $(f_p,f_q^*)=0$; spatial <Fourier inversion> removes the measure. Taking the <Hermitian adjoint> gives $a^\dagger(\mathbf p)=-i\int d^3x\,f_p\overleftrightarrow{\partial_t}\phi$. The equal-time <canonical commutation relation> then gives $[a(\mathbf p),a^\dagger(\mathbf q)]=(2\pi)^3 2E_p\delta^3(\mathbf p-\mathbf q)$.

= Positive-frequency solution
{parent=Quantum field theory in curved spacetime}

= Positive frequency
{synonym}

Relative to a timelike Killing field $K$, a positive-frequency solution obeys $i\mathcal L_Ku=\omega u$ with $\omega>0$. Such solutions define the one-particle space and annihilation operators of a stationary vacuum.

= Klein-Gordon plane-wave normalization
{c}
{parent=Positive-frequency solution}
{title2=$\psi_{\mathbf p}=e^{-ip^0t+i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}/[(2\pi)^{3/2}\sqrt{2p^0}]$}

For $p^0=\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2}>0$ in <Minkowski spacetime>, these modes obey $i\partial_t\psi_{\mathbf p}=p^0\psi_{\mathbf p}$ and have <Klein-Gordon inner product> $(\psi_{\mathbf p},\psi_{\mathbf q})=\delta^3(\mathbf p-\mathbf q)$. Their conjugates have the opposite norm and are orthogonal to the positive-frequency modes. Plane waves are a generalized, delta-normalized basis; normalizable wave packets have positive norm $\int |a(\mathbf p)|^2d^3p$. Completeness follows by applying the spatial <Fourier transform> to the <Klein-Gordon equation>, which gives the two branches $e^{\mp ip^0t}$ for each momentum.

= Vacuum state in a stationary spacetime
{parent=Positive-frequency solution}

In a strictly stationary spacetime, the timelike Killing flow gives a preferred positive-frequency splitting. The corresponding vacuum is annihilated by every annihilation operator associated with a positive-frequency mode.

= Positive stationary Hamiltonian selects a particle splitting
{parent=Vacuum state in a stationary spacetime}
{title2=$i\mathcal L_Ku_\omega=\omega u_\omega,\quad\omega>0$}

For a stable free <Klein-Gordon field> with a complete timelike Killing flow and a positive self-adjoint one-particle energy generator, its positive spectral subspace selects <positive-frequency solutions> of positive <Klein-Gordon inner product>. Their annihilation operators define the ground-state <Fock vacuum>. Degenerate modes can be mixed by a unitary matrix without changing this splitting or vacuum. Merely being stationary near infinity is insufficient if the Killing field becomes spacelike in an <ergoregion of a stationary spacetime>; zero modes, instability or additional boundary conditions also require separate treatment. The choice of a ground state does not prevent other states, such as thermal states, from using the same particle operators.

= Bosonic Fock space
{parent=Vacuum state in a stationary spacetime}

This is the symmetric-particle construction of <Fock space>.

For one-particle Hilbert space $\mathcal H_1$, the bosonic Fock space is
$$
\mathcal F_s(\mathcal H_1)=\bigoplus_{n=0}^\infty\operatorname{Sym}^n\mathcal H_1.
$$
Creation operators add one symmetrized particle and annihilation operators remove one.

= Vacuum ambiguity in a nonstationary spacetime
{parent=Quantum field theory in curved spacetime}

Without a preferred timelike Killing flow there is no canonical positive-frequency splitting. Different choices are related by a <Bogoliubov transformation> and generally define different particle notions and vacuum states.

= Keldysh formalism
{parent=Quantum field theory}
{wiki}

= In-in formalism
{synonym}

The in-in formalism computes expectation values at a finite time by evolving a state forward and backward along a closed time contour. At first order in the interaction Hamiltonian,
$$
\langle Q(\tau)\rangle
=\langle Q_I(\tau)\rangle
-i\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}d\tau'
\langle[Q_I(\tau),H_I(\tau')]\rangle.
$$
An $i\epsilon$ tilt of the early-time contour projects onto the interacting vacuum.

= In-in bispectrum conjugation rule
{parent=Keldysh formalism}
{title2=$\langle\zeta(0)\zeta^{\prime}(\tau)\rangle\propto u(0)u^{\prime *}(\tau)$}

With $\zeta_k(\tau)=u_k(\tau)a_k+u_k^*(\tau)a^\dagger_{-k}$, the operator order external field then vertex gives $\langle\zeta_k(0)\zeta_p'(\tau)\rangle=(2\pi)^3\delta(k+p)u_k(0)u_p'{}^*(\tau)$. For positive-frequency $u\propto e^{-ik\tau}$, these vertex contractions contain $e^{+iK\tau}$ and converge on $\tau_i=-\infty(1-i0)$. Conjugating the contraction representation also conjugates the outside in-in prefactor. Mixing the two changes signs and defeats the vacuum prescription.

= Vacuum prescription for inflationary in-in integrals
{parent=Keldysh formalism}
{title2=$\tau_i=-\infty(1-i\varepsilon),\quad\varepsilon>0$}

The Gaussian <Bunch-Davies vacuum> is selected by tilting the early endpoint to $-\infty(1-i\varepsilon)$ for integrands $e^{iK\tau}$ with $K>0$. They then decay at the early boundary. The complex-conjugate integrand requires the opposite tilt. Ordered contractions and their contour must be conjugated together.

= Late-time scalar gradient vertex integral
{parent=Vacuum prescription for inflationary in-in integrals}
{title2=$\operatorname{Im}I=c_s[-K+s_2/K+s_3/K^2]$}

For $K=k_1+k_2+k_3$, $s_2=k_1k_2+k_1k_3+k_2k_3$ and $s_3=k_1k_2k_3$, the convergent <in-in formalism> integral is $I=\int_{-\infty(1-i0)}^{0^-}\tau^{-2}\prod_i(1-ic_sk_i\tau)e^{ic_sK\tau}d\tau$. Its real endpoint divergence drops out of the expectation value, but the finite imaginary endpoint term $-c_sK$ must be kept. The remaining terms contribute $c_ss_2/K+c_ss_3/K^2$. Reversing the exponential requires conjugating both the contour and the outside prefactor.

= Schwinger-Keldysh propagator
{c}
{parent=Keldysh formalism}

The closed time contour of the <in-in formalism> gives four bulk propagators: time-ordered $G_{RR}$, anti-time-ordered $G_{LL}$, and the two Wightman functions $G_{LR}$ and $G_{RL}$. Vertices on the two contour branches carry opposite factors of $i$.

= Cosmological bulk-to-boundary propagator
{parent=Schwinger-Keldysh propagator}

For a free mode function $f(k,\eta)$ and a correlator evaluated at $\eta_0$, the two cosmological bulk-to-boundary propagators are $K_R=f(k,\eta)f^*(k,\eta_0)$ and $K_L=f^*(k,\eta)f(k,\eta_0)$, up to the chosen contour-label convention.

= Cosmological bulk-to-bulk propagator
{parent=Schwinger-Keldysh propagator}

For a free mode $f(k,\eta)$, the right-right bulk propagator is time ordered,
$$
G_{RR}(\eta_1,\eta_2;k)=
\theta(\eta_1-\eta_2)f(\eta_1)f^*(\eta_2)
+\theta(\eta_2-\eta_1)f(\eta_2)f^*(\eta_1),
$$
whereas the mixed propagators are Wightman functions.

= Schwinger-Keldysh conjugation relation
{c}
{parent=Schwinger-Keldysh propagator}

For real couplings and real external data, reflection of every contour label complex conjugates an in-in diagram: $B_{LL}=B_{RR}^*$ and $B_{RL}=B_{LR}^*$.

= Interaction Hamiltonian
{title2=$H_I$}
{parent=Keldysh formalism}

The interaction Hamiltonian generates interaction-picture time evolution. To leading order for many cubic field interactions it is the negative of the cubic interaction Lagrangian, although derivative interactions require the canonical Legendre transform to justify this relation.

= Cubic Legendre transform for scalar derivative interactions
{parent=Interaction Hamiltonian}
{title2=$\mathcal H_3(\pi,\phi)=-\mathcal L_3(\phi,\dot\phi=\pi)$}

For $\mathcal L=\dot\phi^2/2-\mathcal V_2+\mathcal L_3(\phi,\dot\phi)$, where $\mathcal L_3$ is cubic in perturbation amplitude, the <canonical momentum> has $\pi=\dot\phi+O(\phi^2)$. Put $\dot\phi=\pi+\Delta$ with $\Delta$ second order. Then $\pi\dot\phi-\dot\phi^2/2=\pi^2/2-\Delta^2/2$, so through cubic order the <Legendre transform in mechanics> gives $\mathcal H_3=-\mathcal L_3$ evaluated with the free momentum. At fourth order there are extra terms, so derivative interactions do not obey this simple rule at all orders.

= Bogomolny equations
{c}

= Bogomolny equation
{c}
{synonym}

Bogomolny equations are first-order field equations obtained by setting every nonnegative square in a <Bogomolny bound> to zero. Their solutions automatically satisfy the corresponding second-order Euler-Lagrange equations.

= Bogomolny bound
{c}
{parent=Bogomolny equations}

A Bogomolny bound rewrites a field-theory energy as a sum of nonnegative squares plus a topological term. Configurations satisfying the resulting first-order <Bogomolny equations> saturate the bound.

= Square completion for a one-dimensional kink
{parent=Bogomolny bound}
{title2=$E\geq|W(\phi_+)-W(\phi_-)|$}

For the static energy $E=\int[\phi'^2+W'(\phi)^2]dx/2$, <completing the square> gives
$$
E=\frac12\int(\phi'\mp W')^2dx\pm[W(\phi_+)-W(\phi_-)].
$$
The <Bogomolny bound> is the absolute boundary difference. Equality requires the <Bogomolny equation> $\phi'=\pm W'(\phi)$ with the sign selected by that difference. Differentiating it gives the <Euler-Lagrange field equation> $\phi''=W'W''$. A stationary solution of that second-order equation need not saturate the bound in a general field theory.

Finite-temperature quantum field theory represents thermal expectation values by evolution through an imaginary-time interval of length $\beta=1/T$.

= Retarded Green function

For a bosonic operator $A$, the retarded Green function is $G_R(t)=-i\theta(t)\langle[A(t),A(0)]\rangle$. Causality makes its Fourier transform analytic in the upper half frequency plane.

The Lehmann representation expands a thermal correlation function in energy eigenstates. Its poles occur at transition frequencies, and its residues are thermal occupation differences times operator matrix elements.

= Spectral function

The spectral function records the weighted energy differences connected by an operator. With the convention $G_R=-i\theta\langle[A,A]\rangle$, it is proportional to $-\operatorname{Im}G_R$.

= Fluctuation-dissipation theorem
{c}

{wiki}

The fluctuation-dissipation theorem relates an equilibrium correlation function to the dissipative part of the corresponding linear response. If $\rho=-2\operatorname{Im}G_R$, one common convention is $S(\omega)=\rho(\omega)/(1-e^{-\omega/T})$, which becomes $S(\omega)\simeq T\rho(\omega)/\omega$ in the classical limit $|\omega|\ll T$.

= Spectral representation of a thermal correlation function

A Matsubara correlator is obtained from the retarded spectral density by a Cauchy transform evaluated at an imaginary frequency.

= Drude response

A Drude response has a single relaxation pole, $G_R(\omega)=\sigma_0/(1-i\omega\tau_0)$. Causality places the pole in the lower half plane when $\tau_0>0$.

= Imaginary-time-ordered correlation function
{parent=Finite-temperature quantum field theory}

An imaginary-time-ordered correlation function orders operators along the Euclidean thermal circle. Bosonic correlators are periodic with period $\beta$.

= Kubo--Martin--Schwinger condition

= Matsubara frequency

Bosonic Matsubara frequencies are $\omega_n=2\pi nT$. They are the Fourier modes on the periodic imaginary-time circle.

= Bosonic Matsubara sum

{parent=Matsubara frequency}

= Matsubara sum

{synonym}

For $E>0$, contour summation over bosonic Matsubara frequencies gives

T\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\frac1{\omega_n^2+E^2}
=\frac1{2E}\coth\frac{E}{2T}.

It separates into the zero-point term $1/(2E)$ and a thermal Bose--Einstein occupation term.

At an upper critical dimension, a nominal power-law critical scaling acquires a multiplicative logarithmic correction because the coupling is marginal.

= Path integral
{parent=Quantum field theory}
{wiki}

A path integral represents a quantum amplitude or correlation function as an integral over field histories weighted by the exponential of the action.

= Time-sliced configuration-space path integral
{parent=Path integral}

For a <Hamiltonian operator> with <kinetic term> $\hat p^2/2$, insert position-basis resolutions between <Trotter product formula> factors. The measure is the limit of $(2\pi i\Delta)^{-M/2}\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}dq_j$, and the exponent contains $\sum_j[(q_j-q_{j-1})^2/(2\Delta)-\Delta V(q_{j-1})]$. Its continuum notation is $\int\mathcal Dq\,e^{iS[q]}$, with fixed endpoint values. A damping prescription defines the oscillatory limit.

= Gaussian path integral
{c}
{parent=Path integral}
{title2=$\int\mathcal D\eta\,e^{i\langle\eta,A\eta\rangle}\propto(\det A)^{-1}$}

A quadratic <path integral> becomes a product of <Gaussian integrals> after diagonalizing its fluctuation operator. One real coordinate per mode gives an inverse square root of the <functional determinant>; one complex coordinate, or two real coordinates, gives its inverse. A regulator and normalization are essential: <determinant> ratios are often meaningful before an absolute <determinant> is defined.

= Classical-path factorization of a quadratic path integral
{parent=Gaussian path integral}
{title2=$K(q_f,q_i;T)=N(T)e^{iS[q_c]}$}

For a quadratic <potential energy>, shifting a path to its classical solution leaves an exactly quadratic fluctuation <action> with homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions>. The first variation vanishes by the <Euler-Lagrange equation>. The <Gaussian path integral> prefactor is an inverse square root of a regulated <functional determinant> and does not depend on the endpoints. At conjugate times the ordinary formula must instead be read by distributional continuation.

= Dirichlet oscillator determinant ratio
{c}
{parent=Gaussian path integral}
{title2=$\det(-\partial_t^2-\omega^2)/\det(-\partial_t^2)=\sin(\omega T)/(\omega T)$}

On $[0,T]$ with vanishing endpoint fluctuations, the sine-mode <eigenvalues> are $(\pi n/T)^2-\omega^2$. Divide by the free <eigenvalues> and use the <sine infinite product>. The ratio determines the <harmonic oscillator transition kernel> once the free <configuration-space path integral> is normalized. Zeros correspond to caustics where the fluctuation operator has a zero mode.

= Hubbard–Stratonovich transformation
{parent=Path integral}
{c}
{title2=$\int dM\,e^{-M^2/(2U)+Mj}\propto e^{Uj^2/2}$}
{wiki}

= Hubbard–Stratonovich decoupling
{c}
{synonym}

A Gaussian auxiliary-field identity replacing a quadratic expression in a composite field by a linear coupling to a new field. Completing the square in $M^2/(2U)-Mj$ yields an exponential $e^{Uj^2/2}$ after integrating $M$, with normalized Gaussian prefactors. It converts suitable quartic field interactions into bilinear couplings and permits fermionic fields to be integrated out.

= Spin-vector Hubbard–Stratonovich decoupling
{parent=Hubbard–Stratonovich transformation}
{title2=$\mathbf j^2=-6\bar\psi_\uparrow\bar\psi_\downarrow\psi_\downarrow\psi_\uparrow$}

For two Grassmann spin components, $\mathbf j=\bar\psi\boldsymbol\sigma\psi$ satisfies $\mathbf j^2=-6\bar\psi_\uparrow\bar\psi_\downarrow\psi_\downarrow\psi_\uparrow$. A real vector auxiliary field with cost $\mathbf M^2/(2U)$ and coupling $-\mathbf M\cdot\mathbf j$ therefore reproduces the repulsive $3U$ contact action. All three spin components are essential to that normalization.

= Coherent-state time slicing
{parent=Path integral}

Insert the <coherent-state resolution of identity> between short-time evolution operators. For a normally ordered <Hamiltonian>, the kernel uses the next bra label and current ket label: $\langle\psi_{j+1}|e^{-\epsilon H}|\psi_j\rangle=\exp(\bar\psi_{j+1}\psi_j-\epsilon H(\bar\psi_{j+1},\psi_j)+O(\epsilon^2))$. Together with the Gaussian measure this yields the first-order derivative term $\bar\psi\partial_\tau\psi$. The adjacent-label prescription retains operator ordering information that a formal continuum expression alone can hide.

= Coherent-state thermal boundary conditions
{parent=Coherent-state time slicing}
{title2=$\psi(\beta)=\zeta\psi(0)$}

The thermal trace closes bosonic coherent-state paths periodically and fermionic coherent-state paths antiperiodically: $\psi(\beta)=\zeta\psi(0)$ and $\bar\psi(\beta)=\zeta\bar\psi(0)$, with $\zeta=1$ for bosons and $-1$ for fermions. The minus sign follows from the <fermionic coherent-state trace>, not from choosing a different Hamiltonian. These boundary conditions determine the allowed <Matsubara frequencies>.

= Fermionic one-level thermal determinant
{parent=Coherent-state thermal boundary conditions}
{title2=$\det_{\rm AP}(\partial_\tau+\xi)=1+e^{-\beta\xi}$}

An antiperiodic free <fermion> field with inverse propagator $\partial_\tau+\xi$ has a thermally normalized determinant $1+e^{-\beta\xi}$. The factor counts empty and occupied states of one level. Its logarithmic Matsubara sum is meaningful only with a regularization fixing the determinant normalization; an unqualified infinite product is not convergent.

= Scalar field path integral
{parent=Path integral}
{title2=$Z[J]=\int\mathcal D\phi\,e^{i(S[\phi]+\int J\phi)}$}

= Scalar field functional integral
{synonym}

The <scalar field path integral> generalizes the <configuration-space path integral> by replacing finitely many coordinates with field values. Its regulated measure integrates one field variable at every spacetime lattice point. Fixed boundary fields give transition kernels; vacuum boundary conditions and sources give the <normalized vacuum generating functional>.

= Path-integral perturbation by source derivatives
{parent=Scalar field path integral}
{title2=$Z[J]\propto e^{iS_{\rm int}[(1/i)\delta/\delta J]}Z_0[J]$}

An interaction depending on the field can be represented by substituting $(1/i)\delta/\delta J$ into its action and acting on the free <generating functional>. Expanding that exponential produces <Feynman diagrams>, contractions and vertex weights. Normalize by the resulting value at $J=0$ to cancel vacuum diagrams.

= Vacuum projection by imaginary time
{parent=Scalar field path integral}
{title2=$e^{-TH}|n\rangle=e^{-TE_n}|n\rangle$}

Long imaginary-time evolution multiplies an <energy eigenstate> by $e^{-TE_n}$. After normalization it projects onto the lowest energy component with nonzero initial overlap, or onto the lowest-energy subspace if the vacuum is degenerate. This provides vacuum boundary conditions for a <path integral> and is related to the <Feynman i-epsilon prescription>.

= Configuration-space path integral
{parent=Path integral}
{title2=$K(q_f,q_i)=\int_{q_i}^{q_f}\mathcal Dq\,e^{iS[q]}$}

A <configuration-space path integral> integrates histories of the coordinates with weight $e^{iS}$. Fixed endpoint conditions specify a transition amplitude. For a quadratic kinetic energy it follows by Gaussian momentum integration from the <phase-space path integral>. Time-slicing fixes the normalization, and a regulator gives meaning to the otherwise formal <functional measure>.

= Classical path
{parent=Configuration-space path integral}
{title2=$q_c(t)$}

A <classical path> joining prescribed endpoints is a stationary point of the <action>, satisfying the <Euler–Lagrange equation> and the endpoint <boundary conditions>. Expanding the <path integral> about each such path separates its classical phase from its quadratic fluctuation determinant. There may be multiple classical paths or none within a chosen real saddle class; the <semiclassical propagator> must respect that saddle structure rather than assuming a unique trajectory for every potential.

= Euclidean configuration-space transition kernel
{c}
{parent=Configuration-space path integral}
{title2=$\langle q_f|e^{-H\tau/\hbar}|q_i\rangle$}

For $H=p^2/(2m)+V(q)$, time slicing gives a <Euclidean path integral> over paths with the specified endpoints and action $S_E=\int[m\dot q^2/2+V(q)]dt$. Each step contributes normalization $(m/(2\pi\hbar\delta))^{1/2}$. This normalization fixes the units and endpoint factors of the kernel, which differ from amplitudes between normalized localized wavepackets.

= Phase-space path integral
{parent=Path integral}
{title2=$K=\int\mathcal Dq\,\mathcal Dp\,e^{i\int(p\dot q-H)dt}$}

A <phase-space path integral> represents a quantum transition kernel by integrating both coordinates and conjugate momenta with action $\int(p\dot q-H)dt$. It follows from inserting position and momentum completeness relations between short-time evolution operators. For a quadratic momentum dependence the momentum variables can be integrated by a <Gaussian integral>.

= Gaussian momentum integration in a phase-space path integral
{c}
{parent=Phase-space path integral}
{title2=$\int dp\,e^{-\Delta t p^2/(2m)+ip\Delta q}=\sqrt{2\pi m/\Delta t}\,e^{-m\Delta q^2/(2\Delta t)}$}

Integrating each momentum in a quadratic <Hamiltonian> yields the configuration-space kinetic action and its time-slice normalization. A raw $dp$ measure gives $\sqrt{2\pi m/\Delta t}$; the normalized Fourier measure $dp/(2\pi)$ gives $\sqrt{m/(2\pi\Delta t)}$. Dropping the time dependence changes the <quantum-mechanical propagator>.

= Time-sliced equivalence of phase-space and configuration-space path integrals
{parent=Gaussian momentum integration in a phase-space path integral}

For $H=p^2/(2m)+V(q)$, time slices of length $\delta$ use $N$ <momentum> measures $dp_j/(2\pi\hbar)$ and $N-1$ intermediate coordinate measures. Each <momentum> <Gaussian integral> gives $[m/(2\pi i\hbar\delta)]^{1/2}\exp[im(q_j-q_{j-1})^2/(2\hbar\delta)]$. The resulting configuration integral has normalization $[m/(2\pi i\hbar\delta)]^{N/2}$. A common endpoint convention for $V$ and the <Trotter product formula> identify both limits with the same operator transition kernel; a damping or imaginary-time prescription defines the oscillatory integrals.

= Time slicing of a phase-space path integral
{parent=Phase-space path integral}
{title2=$\dot q_i=(q_{i+1}-q_i)/\Delta t_i$}

The first-order term means $\sum_i p_i(q_{i+1}-q_i)$ in a finite-dimensional integral. Specify the endpoints and an ordering prescription, then take the maximum time step to zero. Typical Euclidean paths are not classically differentiable, so the discrete prescription is essential.

= Euclidean path integral
{c}
{parent=Path integral}
{title2=$Z=\int\mathcal D\phi\,e^{-S_E[\phi]}$}

A <path integral> with weight $e^{-S_E}$ uses the <Euclidean action> obtained by <Wick rotation>. Bosonic convergence requires appropriate positivity and boundary conditions.

= Instanton
{parent=Euclidean path integral}
{wiki}

An <instanton> is a localized finite-action classical solution in Euclidean spacetime, used in a semiclassical description of <quantum tunnelling> and other nonperturbative effects. In one-dimensional quantum mechanics it can connect distinct degenerate potential minima over infinite Euclidean time. A <quartic double-well instanton> is an elementary example; a <Yang-Mills instanton> is a gauge-field example with different geometric structure.

= Euclidean bounce
{parent=Instanton}

A <Euclidean bounce> is a finite-action Euclidean saddle associated with decay of a <false vacuum>. It returns to the same metastable vacuum at the two ends of Euclidean time, instead of joining two degenerate stable vacua. Its characteristic negative fluctuation direction produces the imaginary part associated with a decay rate; the leading semiclassical suppression is $e^{-S_B/\hbar}$. Translation zero modes are treated as collective coordinates. It should not be confused with the stable double-well tunnelling instanton whose repeated crossings give a real level splitting.

= Anti-instanton
{parent=Instanton}

The orientation-reversed Euclidean trajectory of an <instanton>. In a symmetric double well it goes from the right vacuum to the left vacuum and has the same positive action as the forward crossing. Its topological orientation changes sign, not its Euclidean action.

= Dilute instanton gas
{parent=Instanton}
{title2=$\kappa=Ke^{-S_I/\hbar}\ll\omega$}

When individual <instantons> are much narrower than their typical separation, multi-crossing paths factor at leading semiclassical order. Integrating $n$ ordered centers over time $\tau$ contributes $\tau^n/n!$. In a double well, crossings alternate direction and even/odd counts yield hyperbolic cosine/sine amplitudes. This approximation requires large action relative to $\hbar$, matched local well structure and control of corrections from interactions and local anharmonicity.

= Instanton fluctuation prefactor
{parent=Instanton}
{title2=$K=\sqrt{S_I/(2\pi\hbar)}\,[\det L_0/\det' L_I]^{1/2}$}

For a one-dimensional <quantum tunnelling> <instanton>, the prefactor combines nonzero Gaussian fluctuation <eigenvalues> with the translation zero-mode Jacobian. Here $L_I=-\partial_t^2+V''(q_I)/m$, $L_0=-\partial_t^2+\omega^2$, and the prime excludes the zero mode. Common endpoint regularization and reference normalization define the <determinant> ratio. The resulting $K$ has frequency units, while $Ke^{-S_I/\hbar}$ is the dilute crossing rate. This <determinant>/collective-coordinate structure is also discussed in https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.10128[Dunne, Sulejmanpasic and Ünsal's treatment]; unequal neighbouring well frequencies require care and need not share the symmetric-double-well result.

= Instanton translation zero mode
{parent=Instanton fluctuation prefactor}
{title2=$L_I\dot q_I=0$}

Differentiating the Euclidean classical equation for an <instanton> gives a zero eigenfunction proportional to its time derivative. Its norm satisfies $\mu\int\dot q_I^2d\tau=S_I$, so replacing the Gaussian zero-mode integral by the center coordinate gives $\sqrt{S_I/(2\pi\hbar)}d\tau_0$. The nonzero modes belong in the primed <functional determinant>. This removes the false divergence without deleting the center's integration volume.

= Quartic double-well instanton
{parent=Instanton}
{title2=$q_I(t)=a\tanh[\omega(t-t_0)/2],\quad S_I=2m\omega a^2/3$}

For $V(q)=m\omega^2(q^2-a^2)^2/(8a^2)$, the zero Euclidean-energy condition is $m\dot q^2/2=V(q)$. Integrating gives the displayed <instanton> from $-a$ to $a$. Its action is $\int_{-a}^a\sqrt{2mV(q)}dq=2m\omega a^2/3$. The arbitrary center $t_0$ supplies a translation zero mode, treated as a collective coordinate in the <instanton fluctuation prefactor>.

= Quartic double-well fluctuation determinant
{parent=Quartic double-well instanton}
{title2=$\det' L_I/\det L_0=1/(12\omega^2)$}

The <quartic double-well instanton> has $L_I=-\partial_\tau^2+\omega^2[1-3\operatorname{sech}^2(\omega\tau/2)/2]$. For a positive regulator $\varepsilon$ and $r=2\sqrt{1+\varepsilon/\omega^2}$, the common infinite-line determinant ratio is $(r-1)(r-2)/[(r+1)(r+2)]$. Its zero-mode factor is $\varepsilon/(12\omega^2)$ to leading order, giving the displayed primed ratio. Therefore the <instanton fluctuation prefactor> is $K=\omega\sqrt{6S_I/(\pi\hbar)}$ and the <double-well tunneling splitting> is $2\hbar K e^{-S_I/\hbar}$.

= Operator-path-integral equivalence
{parent=Path integral}

With a time-slice regulator, inserting field-coordinate resolutions of identity in evolution kernels produces a bosonic phase-space <path integral>; integrating the <canonical momentum> gives the configuration-space <action>. For <fermions>, resolutions in <fermionic coherent states> instead produce a <Berezin integral> and the first-order kinetic term. Vacuum endpoint projection supplies the <Feynman i-epsilon prescription>. Both constructions reproduce the same free oscillator correlators and their <Wick theorem> expansions, and thus agree order by order in a regulated <perturbation series>.

= Euclidean worldline path integral
{c}
{parent=Path integral}

A thermal <path integral> represents the <canonical partition function> by paths periodic in Euclidean time of circumference $\beta$. For $H=p^2/2+V(x)$, its formal action is $S_E[x]=\int_0^\beta(\dot x^2/2+V(x))\,d\tau$. The <periodic boundary condition> results from taking the trace of the evolution kernel. The continuum measure requires a regularization and normalization; finite-dimensional measures make both choices explicit.

= Fourier-cutoff oscillator functional determinant
{c}
{parent=Euclidean worldline path integral}
{title2=$Z_N=A_N\omega^{-1}\prod_{r=1}^N(\omega^2+\nu_r^2)^{-1}$}

Use an orthonormal real <Fourier series> basis on a circle of length $\beta$, with $\nu_r=2\pi r/\beta$. Retain the constant coefficient and the sine/cosine pair for each $1\le r\le N$. The oscillator action becomes $\tfrac12\omega^2q_0^2+\tfrac12\sum_r(\omega^2+\nu_r^2)(q_r^2+s_r^2)$. A product of real <Gaussian integrals> gives the displayed <functional determinant>, with $A_N$ independent of $\omega$. Ratios for two frequencies converge regardless of $A_N$. The absolute limit requires its normalization: $A_N=\beta^{-1}\prod_r\nu_r^2$ gives the <thermal partition function of a quantum harmonic oscillator> in the limit. Multiplying this choice by an oscillating positive sequence preserves all frequency ratios but destroys the absolute limit.
