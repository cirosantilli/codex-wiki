= Solution

A convenient sufficient condition is that $f\in C^2(\mathbb R^d)$ has a bounded <gradient>. The <Itô formula> for standard $d$-dimensional <Brownian motion> gives
$$
f(B_t)-\frac12\int_0^t\Delta f(B_s)\,ds
=f(0)+\sum_{j=1}^d\int_0^t\partial_jf(B_s)\,dB_s^j.
$$
If $|\nabla f|\leq C$, the <Itô isometry> gives
$$
\mathbb E\left|\sum_j\int_0^t\partial_jf(B_s)\,dB_s^j\right|^2
=\mathbb E\int_0^t|\nabla f(B_s)|^2\,ds\leq C^2t.
$$
Thus the right side is a <square-integrable> <martingale>, rather than merely a <local martingale>. In particular,
$$
\boxed{f\in C^2(\mathbb R^d),\quad \sup_x|\nabla f(x)|<\infty
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad M^f\text{ is a martingale}.}
$$
There are many nonconstant examples, such as $f(x)=\sin x_1$ or $f(x)=x_1$. More generally it is enough that $\mathbb E\int_0^t|\nabla f(B_s)|^2ds<\infty$ for each finite $t$. Smooth <test functions> with <compact support> satisfy the bounded-gradient condition and will provide the domain in the next part.

Take
$$
\boxed{\mathcal D(\Delta/2)=C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^d).}
$$
Part (i) proves that <Brownian motion> solves this <martingale problem>. For the converse, let $X$ be any continuous <adapted process> satisfying this <martingale problem> and $X_0=0$.

Set $\tau_n=\inf\{t\geq0:|X_t|\geq n\}$. Choose a <smooth cutoff function> $\chi_n$ equal to one on a neighbourhood of the closed ball of radius $n$. Apply the <martingale problem> to $\chi_n(x)x_i$ and $\chi_n(x)x_ix_j$, and stop at $\tau_n$. Inside that ball, their <Laplace operators> are respectively $0$ and $2\delta_{ij}$. The <optional sampling theorem> therefore shows that
$$
X^i_{t\wedge\tau_n},\qquad
X^i_{t\wedge\tau_n}X^j_{t\wedge\tau_n}-\delta_{ij}(t\wedge\tau_n)
$$
are <martingales>. Continuity makes $\tau_n\uparrow\infty$ <almost surely>. Hence every $X^i$ is a <continuous local martingale>. The <Itô product rule> and the fact that a <continuous finite-variation local martingale is constant> identify its <quadratic covariations>:
$$
[X^i,X^j]_t=\delta_{ij}t.
$$
Here $\delta_{ij}$ is the <Kronecker delta>.

The <Lévy characterization of multidimensional Brownian motion> now applies. To see explicitly why it identifies the entire law, for $\xi\in\mathbb R^d$ the <Itô formula> makes
$$
E_t(\xi)=\exp\!\left(i\xi\cdot X_t+\frac12|\xi|^2t\right)
$$
a complex <local martingale>. Its absolute value on $[0,T]$ is at most $e^{|\xi|^2T/2}$, so the <bounded local martingale criterion> makes it a true <martingale> there. For $0\leq s\leq t$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\!\left[e^{i\xi\cdot(X_t-X_s)}\mid\mathcal F_s\right]
=e^{-|\xi|^2(t-s)/2}.}
$$
This conditional <characteristic function> identifies $X_t-X_s$ as having a centered <multivariate normal distribution> with <covariance matrix> $(t-s)I$, <independent> of $\mathcal F_s$. Together with continuity and $X_0=0$, these are exactly the defining properties of standard $d$-dimensional <Brownian motion>. Thus the <martingale problem for Brownian motion> has a unique law.

**The existence assertion is false under the hypotheses printed in the source.** No measurability of $\theta$ is specified. Even requiring $\theta$ to be a <Borel measurable function> does not suffice: a coefficient can be positive at every point while approaching zero too rapidly near the starting point. This is the obstruction described by <pointwise positivity does not ensure weak existence>.

Here is a counterexample in dimension one, with $\Theta=1$:
$$
\theta(0)=1,\qquad \theta(x)=\min(x^2,1)\quad(x\ne0).
$$
Suppose that a solution to the <martingale problem> with domain $C_c^\infty(\mathbb R)$ existed. The cutoff argument from part (a)(ii), now with $\mathcal L=\theta\Delta$, makes $X$ a <continuous local martingale> with
$$
[X]_t=2\int_0^t\theta(X_s)\,ds\leq2t.
$$
The criterion for a <continuous local martingale with bounded quadratic variation> makes it a <square-integrable> <martingale>, and the <Itô isometry> gives $\mathbb EX_t^2=\mathbb E[X]_t$.

Apply the <occupation-times formula> with the indicator of the singleton $\{0\}$. Since that singleton has zero <Lebesgue measure>,
$$
0=\int_0^t\mathbf1_{\{X_s=0\}}\,d[X]_s
=2\int_0^t\mathbf1_{\{X_s=0\}}\,ds.
$$
Thus $X$ spends zero ordinary time at zero. Away from zero, $\theta(x)\leq x^2$, so
$$
0\leq\mathbb EX_t^2
=2\mathbb E\int_0^t\theta(X_s)\,ds
\leq2\int_0^t\mathbb EX_s^2\,ds.
$$
The <Gronwall inequality> forces $\mathbb EX_t^2=0$ for every $t$. Continuity then forces $X\equiv0$ <almost surely>, contradicting either the zero occupation time just proved or $[X]_t=2t$ for that constant path. Consequently
$$
\boxed{0<\theta\leq\Theta\text{, even Borel measurable, does not ensure existence}.}
$$

For completeness, the intended construction works if $\theta$ is a <Borel measurable function> and bounded away from zero on every compact set. In particular, it works for continuous strictly positive $\theta$. Let $B$ be standard $d$-dimensional <Brownian motion>, and define a clock and its inverse by
$$
A_u=\int_0^u\frac{dr}{2\theta(B_r)},\qquad
\tau_t=\inf\{u\geq0:A_u>t\},\qquad X_t=B_{\tau_t}.
$$
The Brownian path on each finite interval has compact range, so the added hypothesis makes $A_u$ finite. The clock is continuous and strictly increasing, and $A_u\geq u/(2\Theta)$, so $A_u\to\infty$. Its inverse is continuous and satisfies $\tau_t\leq2\Theta t$. Each $\tau_t$ is a <stopping time> for the Brownian <filtration>.

For $f\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^d)$, the <optional sampling theorem> applied at these bounded random times shows that
$$
f(B_{\tau_t})-\frac12\int_0^{\tau_t}\Delta f(B_r)\,dr
$$
is a true <martingale> in the <filtration> $\mathcal G_t=\mathcal F^B_{\tau_t}$. The inverse-clock substitution $d\tau_t=2\theta(X_t)dt$, valid almost everywhere, rewrites it as
$$
f(X_t)-\int_0^t\theta(X_s)\Delta f(X_s)\,ds.
$$
This proves the <Brownian time change for a variable diffusion speed>:
$$
\boxed{X_t=B_{A^{-1}(t)}\text{ solves the corrected martingale problem}.}
$$
The factor $2$ in the clock is necessary because standard <Brownian motion> has <infinitesimal generator> $\Delta/2$.

A sufficient condition is that $x\mapsto\sqrt{2\theta(x)}$ is <globally Lipschitz>. A more familiar, stronger condition implying this is that $\theta$ is <globally Lipschitz> and $\theta\geq\varepsilon>0$. Both conditions repair the existence issue in part (i).

To connect this to the suggested <stochastic differential equation>, apply the coordinate and coordinate-product cutoff tests to any solution of the <martingale problem>. As before they give
$$
[X^i,X^j]_t=2\delta_{ij}\int_0^t\theta(X_s)\,ds.
$$
Define, using <stochastic integrals> against the coordinates of $X$,
$$
W_t^i=\int_0^t\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\theta(X_s)}}\,dX_s^i.
$$
These integrals exist even without a global positive lower bound: their <quadratic variations> are exactly $t$. Indeed,
$$
[W^i,W^j]_t
=\int_0^t\frac{1}{2\theta(X_s)}\,d[X^i,X^j]_s
=\delta_{ij}t.
$$
The <Lévy characterization of multidimensional Brownian motion> identifies $W$ as standard $d$-dimensional <Brownian motion>. By <associativity of stochastic integration>,
$$
\boxed{dX_t=\sqrt{2\theta(X_t)}\,dW_t,\qquad X_0=0.}
$$
The diffusion matrix $\sqrt{2\theta(x)}I$ is <globally Lipschitz> under the stated condition, and boundedness of $\theta$ gives bounded growth. The <global existence theorem for stochastic differential equations with Lipschitz coefficients> gives a unique <strong solution of a stochastic differential equation> and <uniqueness in law>. Every solution to the <martingale problem> has just been represented as a <weak solution of a stochastic differential equation> of this form, so they all have the same law. Conversely, the <Itô formula> applied to any $f\in C_c^\infty$ gives a true <martingale>, since its stochastic integrand is bounded. Thus the <martingale problem> is well posed under this sufficient condition.

Write $N_t=M_t-M_0$ and $V_t=\langle M\rangle_t=[N]_t$. The <Dambis-Dubins-Schwarz theorem> supplies a standard <Brownian motion> $W$ such that
$$
N_t=W_{V_t}.
$$
On the event $V_\infty=\infty$, continuity and monotonicity imply that the range of $t\mapsto V_t$ is all of $[0,\infty)$.

For completeness, <Brownian motion> hits every positive integer <almost surely>. The <Brownian reflection principle> gives, for $a>0$,
$$
\mathbb P\!\left(\sup_{0\leq u\leq R}W_u\geq a\right)
=2\mathbb P(W_R\geq a)\longrightarrow1\qquad(R\to\infty).
$$
Intersect over integer $a$. The times of these increasingly large values must escape every compact interval because each Brownian path is continuous there. Therefore $\limsup_{u\to\infty}W_u=\infty$. The unbounded clock transfers these values to arbitrarily late times for $N$, and adding the finite initial value $M_0$ preserves unboundedness. Hence
$$
\boxed{\limsup_{t\to\infty}|M_t|=\infty\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$
This is an instance of <infinite quadratic variation forces unbounded oscillations>; applying the same argument to $-W$ also gives negative oscillations.

Write the diffusion and <drift coefficients> as
$$
\sigma(x)=\frac1{1+x^2},\qquad b(x)=-\frac1{2(1+x^2)^2}.
$$
Both are continuously differentiable, with
$$
\sigma'(x)=-\frac{2x}{(1+x^2)^2},\qquad
b'(x)=\frac{2x}{(1+x^2)^3}.
$$
These derivatives are bounded. For example $2|x|\leq1+x^2$ bounds both absolute values by one. The <mean value theorem> therefore makes both coefficients <globally Lipschitz>. They are also bounded, so satisfy the <linear growth condition for an SDE>.

The <global existence theorem for stochastic differential equations with Lipschitz coefficients> consequently gives
$$
\boxed{\text{a global strong solution, unique up to indistinguishability}.}
$$
In particular, there is no finite explosion time. The solution has continuous paths, so all subsequent level-hitting and stopping arguments apply.

|f(x)^2-f(y)^2|\leq2KC|x-y|,
$$
both coefficients $f$ and $-f^2/2$ are bounded and <globally Lipschitz>. The <global existence theorem for stochastic differential equations with Lipschitz coefficients> gives a unique global <strong solution of a stochastic differential equation>.

The same <Itô formula> cancellation as before gives
$$
d(e^{X_t})=e^{X_t}f(X_t)\,dB_t,\qquad
[e^X]_t=\int_0^te^{2X_s}f(X_s)^2\,ds.
$$
For $S=T_{-a}\wedge T_b$, the stopped exponential is bounded between $e^{-a}$ and $e^b$. Continuity and strict positivity of $f$ give
$$
c_{a,b}:=\min_{x\in[-a,b]}e^{2x}f(x)^2>0.
$$
The <Itô isometry> therefore yields
$$
1+c_{a,b}\mathbb E[t\wedge S]
\leq\mathbb E e^{2X_{t\wedge S}}\leq e^{2b}.
$$
The <monotone convergence theorem> proves $\mathbb ES<\infty$. The bounded <optional stopping> argument is now identical to part (b)(iii), and gives
$$
\mathbb P(T_b<T_{-a})=\frac{1-e^{-a}}{e^b-e^{-a}},\qquad
\mathbb P(T_b<\infty)=e^{-b}.
$$
Passing from level hitting to strict exceedances as in part (b)(iv),
$$
\boxed{X^\star\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1).}
$$
No global positive lower bound on $f$ was needed: compact-interval positivity suffices for each exit argument. The law is independent of the particular $f$ because the <scale function> is always $s(x)=e^x$: its <infinitesimal generator> satisfies
$$
\mathcal Ls=\frac{f^2}{2}(s''-s')=0.
$$
This is the <exponential scale function for a drift tied to diffusivity>. Changing $f$ changes how quickly the path is traversed, but leaves these boundary probabilities and the maximum law unchanged.
