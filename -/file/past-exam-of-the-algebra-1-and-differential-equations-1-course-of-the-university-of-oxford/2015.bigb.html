= Solution

The identity $T^2=T$ gives the decomposition
$$
v=(v-Tv)+Tv,\qquad T(v-Tv)=0.
$$
The first summand lies in the <kernel of a linear map> $\ker T$, and the second in the <image of a linear map> $\operatorname{im}T$. If $w$ belongs to their intersection, write $w=Tu$; then $Tw=T^2u=Tu=w$, while $Tw=0$. Thus $w=0$, proving the <direct sum>
$$
\boxed{V=\ker T\oplus\operatorname{im}T.}
$$
On these two <invariant subspaces>, $T$ is respectively zero and the <identity map>. Joining a <basis> of each produces a <basis> of <eigenvectors>, with <matrix>
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{diag}(0,\ldots,0,1,\ldots,1).}
$$
Hence every <linear projection> is <diagonalizable>, over the original <field>, including in <characteristic> two.

= Solution

Put $n=\dim V>0$ and $r=\operatorname{rank}T$. The <direct sum> in part (i) gives
$$
\boxed{\chi_T(x)=x^{n-r}(x-1)^r.}
$$
The <eigenvalues> are the members of $\{0,1\}$ actually present: zero occurs exactly when $r<n$, and one exactly when $r>0$. Since $T$ is <diagonalizable>, its <minimal polynomial> contains each present <eigenvalue> once:
$$
\boxed{m_T(x)=\begin{cases}
x,&T=0,\\
x-1,&T=I,\\
x(x-1),&T\ne0,I.
\end{cases}}
$$
In particular, a nonzero <vector space> does not force both <eigenvalues> to occur. The zero and <identity maps> are important special cases.

The same <invariant subspaces> work for $S=\alpha I+\beta T$: it acts by $\alpha$ on $\ker T$ and by $\alpha+\beta$ on $\operatorname{im}T$. Its <minimal polynomial> is therefore the product of the distinct <linear polynomials> belonging to the nonzero summands. Explicitly,
$$
\boxed{m_S(x)=\begin{cases}
x-\alpha,&\beta=0\text{ or }T=0,\\
x-(\alpha+\beta),&\beta\ne0,\ T=I,\\
(x-\alpha)(x-\alpha-\beta),&\beta\ne0,\ T\ne0,I.
\end{cases}}
$$
When $\beta\ne0$, the two candidate <eigenvalues> are distinct in any <field>. This is the <minimal polynomial of an affine function of a projection>.

Expand the square using $E^2=E$ and $F^2=F$:
$$
(E+F)^2-(E+F)=EF+FE.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{E+F\text{ is a linear projection}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad EF=-FE.}

This <sum of two linear projections> criterion holds in every <characteristic>; it uses an equality of <linear maps>, not cancellation of factors in the noncommutative <associative algebra> of <linear endomorphisms>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Suppose first that $E+F$ is a <linear projection>. Part (i) gives $EF=-FE$. Multiplying on the left by $E$ gives $EF=-EFE$, and multiplying on the right by $E$ gives $EFE=-FE=EF$. Combining them yields
$$
EF=-EF,\qquad 2EF=0.
$$
In a <field> of <characteristic> different from two, the nonzero <scalar> $2$ is invertible. Hence $EF=0$, and $FE=-EF=0$ too. Conversely these two zero products make the expansion in part (i) equal zero. Therefore
$$
\boxed{E+F\text{ is a linear projection}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad EF=FE=0.}
$$
The argument cancels a nonzero <scalar>, never a possibly singular <linear map>.

= Solution

**The proposed equivalence is false.** On a nonzero <vector space> over any <field> of <characteristic> different from two, choose
$$
E=I,\qquad F=I,\qquad G=0.
$$
These <linear projections> commute and satisfy $EFG=0$. Nevertheless their sum is $2I$, and
$$
\boxed{(2I)^2-2I=2I\ne0.}
$$
Thus the proposed conditions are not sufficient. For commuting <linear projections> the actual obstruction is the sum of the pairwise products:
$$
(E+F+G)^2-(E+F+G)=2(EF+EG+FG).
$$
The single condition on the triple product does not remove this obstruction.

A <linear endomorphism> is <diagonalizable> over its <field> precisely when its <minimal polynomial> is a product of **distinct <linear polynomials> over that same <field>**. Splitting alone is insufficient: repeated factors permit nontrivial <Jordan blocks>.

For the displayed <matrix>, a <determinant> expansion gives
$$
\chi_A(x)=x^3+3x^2+6x-10=(x-1)(x^2+4x+10).
$$
These formulas can be reduced in each requested <characteristic>. The four cases below distinguish failure to split from failure to have distinct factors.

Over the <complex numbers>, the three <eigenvalues> are
$$
1,\qquad -2+i\sqrt6,\qquad -2-i\sqrt6.
$$
They are distinct. Each is a root of the <minimal polynomial>, so here the <minimal polynomial> equals the <characteristic polynomial> and has distinct <linear polynomials>. Consequently **$A$ is <diagonalizable> over $\mathbb C$**.

The <primary decomposition theorem for linear maps> states that if
$$
m_T(x)=\prod_{j=1}^r p_j(x)^{e_j}
$$
with distinct monic <irreducible polynomials> $p_j$, then
$$
\boxed{V=\bigoplus_{j=1}^r\ker\bigl(p_j(T)^{e_j}\bigr).}
$$
Each summand is a $T$-<invariant subspace>. More generally the same <direct sum> conclusion holds for any factorization of an annihilating <polynomial> into pairwise <coprime polynomials>, using the corresponding kernels. The underlying mechanism is the <Bézout's identity for polynomials>, which separates the mutually coprime factors.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Since $T$ is <diagonalizable>, its <minimal polynomial> is $\prod_{j=1}^r(x-\lambda_j)$ with distinct <eigenvalues> $\lambda_j$ in the <field>. Apply the <primary decomposition theorem for linear maps>:
$$
\boxed{V=\bigoplus_{j=1}^r\ker(T-\lambda_jI)=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\mathbb F}V_\lambda.}
$$
For a <scalar> that is not an <eigenvalue>, the associated <eigenspace> is zero, so the sum indexed by the whole <field> has only finitely many nonzero summands. The <eigenspaces> need not be one-dimensional.

For $v\in V_\lambda$, use commutation to obtain

T(Sv)=S(Tv)=S(\lambda v)=\lambda Sv.

Thus $Sv\in V_\lambda$, proving that **every $T$-<eigenspace> is an $S$-<invariant subspace>**. This does not assert that an arbitrary <eigenvector> of $T$ is already an <eigenvector> of $S$.

= Solution

On each nonzero <eigenspace> $V_\lambda$, the restricted <linear map> $S_\lambda=S|_{V_\lambda}$ is <diagonalizable>. Indeed $m_S(S_\lambda)=0$, so the <minimal polynomial> of $S_\lambda$ divides $m_S$. A divisor of a product of distinct <linear polynomials> has the same property, proving the <diagonalizable restriction lemma>.

Choose a <basis> of $S_\lambda$-<eigenvectors> in each $V_\lambda$. Every chosen vector is simultaneously a $T$-<eigenvector>, with <eigenvalue> $\lambda$. The <direct sum> in part (ii) shows that joining these bases gives a <basis> of $V$. Therefore **$S$ and $T$ have a common <eigenbasis>**, which is exactly <simultaneous diagonalization>.

Suppose a single <basis> gives the <diagonal matrices> $D_S,D_T$. Entrywise multiplication gives $D_SD_T=D_TD_S$. If $P$ is the <change-of-basis matrix>, then

ST=P D_SD_TP^{-1}=P D_TD_SP^{-1}=TS.

Thus **<simultaneous diagonalization> implies commutation**.

= Solution
{parent=c}

**Yes, provided “commute” means pairwise commute.** Necessity follows because three <diagonal matrices> commute pairwise.

For sufficiency, first choose the common <eigenbasis> for the first two <linear maps>, say $S,T$, obtained in part (b). Their joint <eigenspaces>
$$
W_{\lambda,\mu}=\ker(S-\lambda I)\cap\ker(T-\mu I)
$$
give a <direct sum> decomposition of $V$. The third map $U$ preserves each $W_{\lambda,\mu}$, since $SUv=USv=\lambda Uv$ and $TUv=UTv=\mu Uv$. By the <diagonalizable restriction lemma>, $U|_{W_{\lambda,\mu}}$ has a <basis> of <eigenvectors> there. Every such vector already has fixed <eigenvalues> for $S$ and $T$.

Joining these bases therefore gives **a common <eigenbasis> for all three maps**. The same induction proves <simultaneous diagonalization> for any finite pairwise commuting family of <diagonalizable> <linear endomorphisms>.

The <dual basis> is the collection of <linear functionals> characterized by
$$
\boxed{e'_i(e_j)=\delta_{ij}.}
$$
Explicitly, $e'_i(\sum_j\alpha_je_j)=\alpha_i$. Every <linear functional> $\ell\in V'$ satisfies $\ell=\sum_i\ell(e_i)e'_i$, proving that the displayed functionals span the <dual space>. Evaluating a zero <linear combination> at each $e_j$ makes all its coefficients zero, so they are also <linearly independent>. Thus they form a <basis> of $V'$.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Define the <bidual evaluation map> by evaluation:
$$
\boxed{J:V\longrightarrow V'',\qquad J(v)(\ell)=\ell(v).}
$$
For fixed $v$, this is a <linear functional> of $\ell$, so it belongs to the <double dual>. It is also linear as a function of $v$. If $v\ne0$, some coordinate of $v$ in a <basis> is nonzero, and the corresponding <dual basis> functional detects it. Hence $J(v)\ne0$, proving <injectivity>.

The spaces $V,V',V''$ all have the same finite <dimension>. Therefore an <injective> <linear map> $J:V\to V''$ is <surjective>, proving the required <linear isomorphism>.

This <linear isomorphism> is natural because evaluation requires no <basis>. More explicitly, for any <linear map> $A:V\to W$, its <dual map> satisfies
$$
\bigl(A''J_V(v)\bigr)(\ell)=J_V(v)(\ell\circ A)=\ell(Av)=J_W(Av)(\ell),
$$
so $A''J_V=J_WA$.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Writing $u=\sum_i u_ie_i$ and $v=\sum_i v_ie_i$, evaluation against the <dual basis> gives
$$
\boxed{\phi(u,v)=\sum_i u_i\overline{v_i}.}
$$
**This has codomain $\mathbb C$, not the printed $\mathbb R$.** For a nonzero <vector space>, take $u=ie_1$ and $v=e_1$ to obtain $\phi(u,v)=i$. Only the diagonal values $\phi(v,v)$ must be real. The displayed formula defines the intended <complex inner product>.

It is linear in the first argument and conjugate-linear in the second:
$$
\phi(au+bw,v)=a\phi(u,v)+b\phi(w,v),\qquad
\phi(u,av+bw)=\overline a\phi(u,v)+\overline b\phi(u,w).
$$
It has <conjugate symmetry>, since $\phi(v,u)=\overline{\phi(u,v)}$. Finally,
$$
\phi(v,v)=\sum_i|v_i|^2\geq0,
$$
with equality exactly when every coordinate vanishes, equivalently $v=0$. This proves <positive definiteness>. Since $\phi(e_i,e_j)=\delta_{ij}$, the original <basis> is <orthonormal>. This is the <inner product determined by a basis>.

Use the convention of part (a): the <inner product> is linear in its first argument. If $Tv=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$, the <adjoint operator> identity and $T^*=-T$ give
$$
\lambda\langle v,v\rangle=\langle Tv,v\rangle
=\langle v,T^*v\rangle=-\langle v,Tv\rangle
=-\overline\lambda\langle v,v\rangle.
$$
<Positive definiteness> makes $\langle v,v\rangle>0$, so
$$
\boxed{\lambda=-\overline\lambda,\qquad\operatorname{Re}\lambda=0.}
$$
Thus every <eigenvalue> of a <skew-adjoint operator> is purely imaginary, including zero.

Take $v\in U^\perp$ and $u\in U$. Because $U$ is an <invariant subspace>, $Tu\in U$. The <adjoint operator> relation yields
$$
\langle Tv,u\rangle=\langle v,T^*u\rangle=-\langle v,Tu\rangle=0.
$$
This holds for every $u\in U$, so $Tv\in U^\perp$. Therefore **the <orthogonal complement> is also an <invariant subspace>**.

Proceed by induction on the <dimension>. For positive <dimension>, the <fundamental theorem of algebra> gives a root of the <characteristic polynomial>, hence a nonzero <eigenvector> $v$. Normalize it to have <norm> one. Its span is an <invariant subspace>, and part (ii) makes its <orthogonal complement> invariant too:
$$
V=\operatorname{span}\{v\}\oplus\operatorname{span}\{v\}^{\perp}.
$$
The restriction of $T$ to the complement remains <skew-adjoint>, since the same <inner product> identity holds there. The induction hypothesis supplies an <orthonormal eigenbasis> for that smaller space. Adjoining $v$ gives an <orthonormal eigenbasis> for $V$.

The zero-dimensional base case is immediate. Thus **$T$ is <diagonalizable>, indeed unitarily diagonalizable**. This proves the finite-dimensional case of the <spectral theorem> for a <skew-adjoint operator> without assuming that distinct <eigenvalues> have multiplicity one.

= Solution

Regard the real <skew-symmetric matrix> as acting on $\mathbb C^n$. Its <Hermitian adjoint> is $A^*=A^T=-A$. Parts (i) and (iii) show that its <eigenvalues> are purely imaginary and its complex <minimal polynomial> has no repeated roots.

Since $A$ has real entries, nonreal <eigenvalues> occur in conjugate pairs. A nonzero pair has the form $i\alpha,-i\alpha$, with $\alpha>0$, and contributes the real <quadratic polynomial>
$$
(x-i\alpha)(x+i\alpha)=x^2+\alpha^2.
$$
Zero, if present, contributes one factor $x$. Hence the real <minimal polynomial> is
$$
\boxed{m_A(x)=x^{\varepsilon}\prod_{j=1}^r(x^2+\alpha_j^2),\qquad
\varepsilon\in\{0,1\},\quad\alpha_j>0\text{ distinct}.}
$$
The product annihilates $A$ after <complexification of a real vector space>, so it also annihilates the original real <linear map>; no factor can be removed because each displayed complex root is an <eigenvalue>. This proves both the asserted form and its minimality. The case $A=0$ has $\varepsilon=1$ and an empty product.

An <autonomous differential equation> has no explicit dependence on the independent time variable: its <vector field> depends only on the current state. The two families of <nullclines> are
$$
\boxed{X(x,y)=0\quad\text{and}\quad Y(x,y)=0.}
$$
On the first family the horizontal velocity is zero; on the second the vertical velocity is zero. A <critical point of a dynamical system> is a state where both components vanish. It gives a constant solution, also called an <equilibrium>.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Translate the <equilibrium> to the origin by $z=(x-a,y-b)^T$. If the <vector field> is continuously differentiable, its first-order <Taylor expansion> is
$$
\dot z=Mz+r(z),\qquad\frac{\|r(z)\|}{\|z\|}\longrightarrow0.
$$
The <Jacobian matrix> $M$ gives the <linearization> $\dot z=Mz$, whose general solution is $z(t)=e^{tM}z(0)$ using the <matrix exponential>. Its <eigenvalues> determine the exponential factors, while its <eigenvectors> and, when necessary, <generalized eigenvectors> determine their directions.

For distinct <eigenvalues> $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ and corresponding <eigenvectors> $v_1,v_2$,
$$
\boxed{z(t)=c_1e^{\lambda_1t}v_1+c_2e^{\lambda_2t}v_2.}
$$
For a real <matrix> with nonreal conjugate <eigenvalues>, choose conjugate coefficients to obtain real solutions. Equivalently, if $\lambda=a+ib$ and $v=p+iq$, a real pair of independent solutions is
$$
e^{at}(p\cos bt-q\sin bt),\qquad e^{at}(p\sin bt+q\cos bt).
$$
For a repeated <eigenvalue> $\lambda$, there are two cases:

* If two independent <eigenvectors> exist, $M=\lambda I$ and $\boxed{z(t)=e^{\lambda t}(c_1v_1+c_2v_2)}$.
* Otherwise choose a <Jordan chain> $(M-\lambda I)v=0$, $(M-\lambda I)w=v$. Then $\boxed{z(t)=e^{\lambda t}\bigl[c_1v+c_2(w+tv)\bigr]}$.

The factor $t$ in the second case is essential. These are exact solutions of the <linearization>; the remainder describes the error for the nonlinear <differential equation>. When no <eigenvalue> has zero real part, the <hyperbolic equilibrium> theory justifies the corresponding local qualitative classification. Zero real parts require additional nonlinear analysis.

Factor each component of the <vector field>. The horizontal-velocity <nullclines> and vertical-velocity <nullclines> are, respectively,
$$
\boxed{\dot x=0:\ x=0,\ y=1,\ x+y=1;\qquad
\dot y=0:\ y=0,\ xy=2.}

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Intersect the two <nullcline> families. The axes yield $(0,0)$ and $(1,0)$, and $y=1$ with $xy=2$ yields $(2,1)$. The possible remaining intersection would have $x+y=1$ and $xy=2$, requiring $x^2-x+2=0$, whose <discriminant> is $-7$. Hence there are no further real <equilibria>.

The <Jacobian matrix> is
$$
J(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}
(1-y)(1-2x-y)&x(x+2y-2)\\
-y^2&2-2xy
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Evaluate it at each <equilibrium>:

* At $(0,0)$ it is $\operatorname{diag}(1,2)$. Both <eigenvalues> are positive, so the point is an **<unstable node>**.
* At $(1,0)$ it is $\begin{pmatrix}-1&-1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$. The <eigenvalues> $-1,2$ have opposite signs, so this is a **<saddle point>**. Its stable direction is $(1,0)$ and its unstable direction can be taken as $(-1,3)$.
* At $(2,1)$ it is $\begin{pmatrix}0&4\\-1&-2\end{pmatrix}$. Its <characteristic polynomial> is $\lambda^2+2\lambda+4$, so $\lambda=-1\pm i\sqrt3$. This is an **<stable focus>**, or spiral sink.

All three are <hyperbolic equilibria>, so their classifications persist for the nonlinear system. Immediately to the right of $(2,1)$ on $y=1$, the velocity is vertically downward, showing that the attracting spiral turns **clockwise**.

= Solution
{parent=i}

For a second-order <linear partial differential equation> written $A u_{xx}+2B u_{xy}+C u_{yy}+\cdots=0$, the discriminant is $B^2-AC$. Here
$$
A=-x^2,\qquad B=0,\qquad C=1,\qquad
\boxed{B^2-AC=x^2.}
$$
It is positive exactly when $x\ne0$, proving that the equation is a <hyperbolic partial differential equation> on each half-plane. At $x=0$ its principal part degenerates and it is not hyperbolic.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <characteristic curves> satisfy
$$
-x^2(dy)^2+(dx)^2=0,\qquad\frac{dy}{dx}=\pm\frac1x.
$$
Integrating suggests the <characteristic coordinates>
$$
\boxed{\xi=y+\ln|x|,\qquad\eta=y-\ln|x|.}
$$
Their <Jacobian determinant> is $2/x$, so this is an invertible local <change of variables> whenever $x\ne0$. Write $u(x,y)=U(\xi,\eta)$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
u_x&=\frac{U_\xi-U_\eta}{x},\\
x^2u_{xx}&=U_{\xi\xi}-2U_{\xi\eta}+U_{\eta\eta}-U_\xi+U_\eta,\\
u_{yy}&=U_{\xi\xi}+2U_{\xi\eta}+U_{\eta\eta}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Substitution cancels both unmixed second derivatives and both first derivatives:
$$
-x^2u_{xx}+u_{yy}-xu_x=4U_{\xi\eta}.
$$
The <canonical form of a second-order partial differential equation> is therefore
$$
\boxed{U_{\xi\eta}=1.}
$$
The cancellation of the first derivatives is the key feature of these <logarithmic characteristic coordinates>.

Integrate the <canonical form of a second-order partial differential equation> first in $\eta$ and then in $\xi$. The first integration gives $U_\xi=\eta+g'(\xi)$, and the second gives
$$
U(\xi,\eta)=\xi\eta+g(\xi)+h(\eta).
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=y^2-\ln^2|x|+g\bigl(y+\ln|x|\bigr)+h\bigl(y-\ln|x|\bigr).}
$$
The two arbitrary twice continuously differentiable <functions> describe the two <characteristic> families. Conversely, substituting this expression into $U_{\xi\eta}=1$ verifies every such solution. The formula applies on $x>0$ or $x<0$; independent choices can be made on the two half-planes. Smooth extension across the degenerate line $x=0$ requires extra compatibility and is not supplied by the logarithmic formula alone.

Use the classical regularity setting $u\in C^2(D)\cap C(\overline D)$, with $D$ bounded. Continuity on the <compact set> $\overline D$ guarantees existence of a minimum. The inequality $\Delta u=f\leq0$ means that $u$ is <superharmonic function>.

A strict inequality $\Delta u<0$ would immediately exclude an interior minimum, because its <Hessian matrix> there would be positive semidefinite and have nonnegative <trace>. To handle equality, introduce the <quadratic perturbation proof of the minimum principle>:
$$
u_\varepsilon(x,y)=u(x,y)-\varepsilon(x^2+y^2),\qquad
\Delta u_\varepsilon=f-4\varepsilon<0.
$$
Its minimum must lie on $\partial D$. Choose $R$ so that $x^2+y^2\leq R^2$ on $\overline D$. For any $p\in D$,
$$
u_\varepsilon(p)\geq\min_{\partial D}u_\varepsilon
\geq\min_{\partial D}u-\varepsilon R^2.
$$
Letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ gives $u(p)\geq\min_{\partial D}u$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\min_{\overline D}u=\min_{\partial D}u.}
$$
This asserts that a minimum is attained on the boundary, not that it cannot also be attained inside; a constant <function> attains it everywhere.

For a <harmonic function>, $\Delta u=0$ and $\Delta(-u)=0$. Apply the preceding minimum argument to $u$ and to $-u$:
$$
\boxed{\min_{\overline D}u=\min_{\partial D}u,\qquad
\max_{\overline D}u=\max_{\partial D}u.}
$$
These are the weak minimum and <maximum principle for harmonic functions>.

The difference $w=v-u$ is a <harmonic function>, because the <Laplacian> is a <linear differential operator>:
$$
\Delta w=\Delta v-\Delta u=0.
$$
The boundary inequality says $w\geq0$ on $\partial D$. The minimum principle from part (ii) gives
$$
\boxed{v(p)-u(p)=w(p)\geq\min_{\partial D}w\geq0\quad(p\in D).}
$$
This is the <comparison principle for harmonic functions>. Applying it in both directions also shows why fixed <Dirichlet boundary conditions> determine at most one <harmonic function>.

Work with the <L1 norm> on $\mathbb R^2$. The scalar Lipschitz hypothesis implies
$$
\begin{aligned}
\|\mathbf f(x,\mathbf y)-\mathbf f(x,\mathbf z)\|_1
&=|y_2-z_2|+|F(x,\mathbf y)-F(x,\mathbf z)|\\
&\leq(1+L)\|\mathbf y-\mathbf z\|_1.
\end{aligned}
$$
We may take $L\geq0$. The <compact set> $S$ and continuity give a finite bound $M=\max_S\|\mathbf f\|_1$. Choose
$$
\boxed{0<\eta\leq\min\left\{h,\frac{k}{M+1},\frac1{2(1+L)}\right\}.}
$$
Let $I=[a-\eta,a+\eta]$. The space $C_\eta$ of continuous maps $I\to B_k(\mathbf b)$ is complete in the <supremum norm>: it is a closed subset of the <Banach space> $C(I,\mathbb R^2)$. Define the <Picard operator on a closed ball> by
$$
(\mathcal P\mathbf g)(x)=\mathbf b+\int_a^x\mathbf f(t,\mathbf g(t))\,dt.
$$
For any $\mathbf g\in C_\eta$, the <integral triangle inequality> gives
$$
\|\mathcal P\mathbf g(x)-\mathbf b\|_1\leq |x-a|M\leq\eta M<k.
$$
The integrand is continuous, so the result is continuous and remains in the prescribed <closed ball>. Thus $\mathcal P$ is a self-map of the complete space. Moreover,
$$
\|\mathcal P\mathbf g-\mathcal P\mathbf h\|_\infty
\leq\eta(1+L)\|\mathbf g-\mathbf h\|_\infty
\leq\frac12\|\mathbf g-\mathbf h\|_\infty.
$$
It is a <contraction mapping>. The <contraction mapping theorem> gives a unique <fixed point> $\mathbf y$. By the <fundamental theorem of calculus>, the fixed-point identity makes $\mathbf y$ continuously differentiable and gives $\mathbf y'=\mathbf f(x,\mathbf y)$ and $\mathbf y(a)=\mathbf b$. Its first component is twice continuously differentiable, because its derivative is the second component.

Conversely, integrating any solution that remains in the ball makes it a <fixed point>, so it equals $\mathbf y$. A solution starting at $\mathbf b$ cannot first reach the boundary of this ball during $I$: before such an exit the same estimate gives distance at most $\eta M<k$, contradicting distance $k$ at the first exit. This proves **existence and uniqueness on $|x-a|\leq\eta$**, not merely formal convergence of an iteration. It is the local <Picard-Lindelöf theorem> construction.

In position-velocity variables $(u,v)$, the scalar right-hand side is
$$
F(x,u,v)=\frac{v^2}{u}+2xv.
$$
Its only relevant singularity is $u=0$. The initial state $(x,u,v)=(0,1,1)$ is separated from it. For an explicit application of part (a), take $h=1$ and $k=1/2$ in the <L1 norm>. On the resulting <closed ball>, $1/2\leq u,v\leq3/2$, and $|x|\leq1$.

The <partial derivatives> obey
$$
|F_u|=\frac{v^2}{u^2}\leq9,\qquad
|F_v|=\left|\frac{2v}{u}+2x\right|\leq8.
$$
The ball is <convex>, so integrating these <partial derivatives> along a segment between two states gives the uniform <Lipschitz condition>
$$
|F(x,u,v)-F(x,\widetilde u,\widetilde v)|
\leq9\bigl(|u-\widetilde u|+|v-\widetilde v|\bigr).
$$
Continuity holds on the whole slab, and
$$
|F|\leq\frac{(3/2)^2}{1/2}+2\cdot1\cdot\frac32=\frac{15}2,
\qquad\|(v,F)\|_1\leq9.
$$
Thus $L=9$, $M\leq9$, and the choice $\boxed{\eta=1/20}$ satisfies the self-map and contraction bounds in part (a). This proves **a unique solution on $[-1/20,1/20]$**, with $y$ bounded away from zero there.

There is also a useful exact representation. Where $y>0$, set $q=y'/y$, the <logarithmic derivative>. Differentiation cancels the quadratic term:
$$
q'=\frac{y''}{y}-\frac{(y')^2}{y^2}=2xq,\qquad q(0)=1.
$$
Solving this <first-order linear differential equation> gives $q=e^{x^2}$, and a second integration yields
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\exp\left(\int_0^x e^{t^2}\,dt\right).}
$$
This <function> is positive and smooth for every real $x$, satisfies both initial data, and verifies the original <ordinary differential equation> by direct differentiation. It therefore extends the unique local solution to the entire real line. The <logarithmic-derivative reduction of a second-order equation> explains why this apparently nonlinear example is explicitly integrable by <integration>.
