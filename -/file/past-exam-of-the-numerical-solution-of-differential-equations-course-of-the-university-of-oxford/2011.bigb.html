= Solution
{parent=a}

Use the normalized <local truncation error>
$$
T_n=\frac{y(x_{n+1})-y(x_n)}h-\theta y'(x_{n+1})-(1-\theta)y'(x_n).
$$
The <Taylor theorem> expansions about $x_n$ are
$$
\frac{y(x_n+h)-y(x_n)}h=y'(x_n)+\frac h2y''(x_n)+\frac{h^2}6y'''(\xi_n),\qquad
y'(x_n+h)=y'(x_n)+hy''(x_n)+\frac{h^2}2y'''(\eta_n).
$$
Substitution gives exactly
$$
\boxed{T_n=\frac h2(1-2\theta)y''(x_n)+\frac{h^2}6[y'''(\xi_n)-3\theta y'''(\eta_n)]}.
$$
Thus the <theta method> has **order two exactly for $\theta=1/2$**, and otherwise order one, as a statement about the method on a sufficiently smooth class of problems. The leading term vanishes at one half, and a function with nonzero third <derivative> shows that the next term does not vanish universally. As usual, a uniform order estimate assumes bounded relevant <derivatives>; mere existence of $y'''$ on the open interval gives the stated pointwise remainders but not, by itself, a uniform bound. Special exact solutions can of course have zero truncation error even for a first-order method.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Put $e_n=y(x_n)-y_n$. Subtracting the numerical update from the exact update with its <local truncation error> gives
$$
e_{n+1}=e_n+h\theta[f(x_{n+1},y(x_{n+1}))-f(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})]+h(1-\theta)[f(x_n,y(x_n))-f(x_n,y_n)]+hT_n.
$$
The <Lipschitz condition> in the solution variable and the <triangle inequality> imply
$$
\boxed{(1-L\theta h)|e_{n+1}|\leq[1+L(1-\theta)h]|e_n|+h|T_n|.}
$$
The assumed positive denominator permits division. With $K=[1+L(1-\theta)h]/(1-L\theta h)$ and $e_0=0$, repeated substitution of this <Lipschitz one-step error bound> yields
$$
|e_n|\leq\frac h{1-L\theta h}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}K^{n-1-j}|T_j|
\leq\boxed{\frac h{1-L\theta h}\frac{K^n-1}{K-1}\max_{0\leq j<n}|T_j|.}
$$
Here $K>1$ because $L,h>0$, so the displayed <geometric series> quotient is well defined. For implicit steps, the same condition makes the scalar update map a <contraction mapping>, ensuring a unique numerical next value.

= Solution

On the <Dahlquist test equation>, set $z=h\lambda$. The <theta method> has <stability function>
$$
R(z)=\frac{1+(1-\theta)z}{1-\theta z},\qquad y_n=R(z)^n.
$$
With the decaying-solution convention, its region of <absolute stability> is $|R(z)|<1$. Writing $z=x+iy$, squaring numerator and denominator gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal S_\theta=\{z:2x+(1-2\theta)|z|^2<0\}.}
$$
For $0\leq\theta<1/2$ this is the open disk with centre $-1/(1-2\theta)$ and radius $1/(1-2\theta)$. At $\theta=1/2$ it is the open left half-plane. For $\theta>1/2$ it is the exterior of the closed disk with centre $1/(2\theta-1)$ and radius $1/(2\theta-1)$. The pole $z=1/\theta$, when present, lies outside the stability region.

Every $z$ with $\operatorname{Re}z<0$ satisfies the inequality exactly for $\theta\geq1/2$. If $\theta<1/2$, sufficiently large negative real $z$ fails it. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{The method is A-stable exactly when }1/2\leq\theta\leq1.}
$$
If <absolute stability> is instead defined by bounded amplification, the boundary $|R|=1$ is included; this convention does not change the <A-stability> conclusion for the open left half-plane.

= Solution
{parent=a}

In the normalization used by the supplied coefficient expansion, the <local truncation error> of the <linear multistep method> is
$$
\boxed{T_n=\frac{\sum_{j=0}^k\alpha_jy(x_{n+j})-h\sum_{j=0}^k\beta_jy'(x_{n+j})}{h\sum_{j=0}^k\beta_j}.}
$$
The nonzero sum in the denominator is an explicit hypothesis. <Consistency of a numerical method> requires $T_n\to0$ uniformly on a fixed finite time interval for every sufficiently smooth exact solution as $h\to0$. Equivalently, the two <polynomial> identities are
$$
\boxed{\rho(1)=0,\qquad\rho'(1)=\sigma(1),\quad
\rho(\zeta)=\sum_j\alpha_j\zeta^j,\quad\sigma(\zeta)=\sum_j\beta_j\zeta^j.}
$$
These follow by inserting constant and affine functions, or by setting the first two Taylor coefficients $C_0,C_1$ to zero.

= Solution
{parent=b}

<Zero-stability> means that perturbations of the starting values of the zero-step recurrence remain bounded by a fixed multiple of their initial size, uniformly as the number of steps tends to infinity on a fixed time interval. In particular solutions of $\sum_{j=0}^k\alpha_je_{n+j}=0$ obey $\max_{n\leq N}|e_n|\leq C\max_{j<k}|e_j|$ with $C$ independent of $N$.

The equivalent <root condition for a multistep method> is
$$
\boxed{\text{Every root of }\rho(\zeta)\text{ has }|\zeta|\leq1,\text{ and roots with }|\zeta|=1\text{ are simple}.}
$$
A root outside the unit circle gives exponential growth of a <recurrence relation> mode; a repeated root on it gives polynomial growth. Repeated roots strictly inside it are harmless because their polynomial factor is dominated by decay.

{parent=2}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The first characteristic <polynomial> factors as
$$
\rho(\zeta)=\zeta^3+\alpha\zeta^2-\alpha\zeta-1
=(\zeta-1)[\zeta^2+(\alpha+1)\zeta+1].
$$
The two quadratic roots have product one. Both can lie in the closed unit disk only if both have modulus one, requiring $|\alpha+1|\leq2$. At $\alpha=-3$ the root one is triple and $\beta=0$; at $\alpha=1$ the root minus one is double. Both fail the <root condition for a multistep method>. In the intervening open interval the two quadratic roots are distinct conjugate unit-modulus roots, different from one. Thus
$$
\boxed{-3<\alpha<1\quad\text{is exactly the zero-stability range}.}
$$
Using $2\beta=\alpha+3$, the supplied expansion gives
$$
C_0=C_1=C_2=0,\qquad C_3=\frac{9-\alpha}{12},\qquad
C_4=\frac{9-\alpha}{8},\qquad C_5=\frac{231-23\alpha}{240}.
$$
Throughout the <zero-stable> range, $C_3\ne0$, so **every such method has order two**. There is a unique higher-order choice:
$$
\boxed{\alpha=9,\qquad\beta=6.}
$$
Here $C_3=C_4=0$ and $C_5=1/10\ne0$, giving exactly order four, with normalized leading truncation error $h^4y^{(5)}/120$. But the extra roots are $-5\pm2\sqrt6$, one of modulus greater than one. By the <Dahlquist equivalence theorem> this consistent but non-<zero-stable> method is **not convergent** for arbitrary consistent starting values.

= Solution

Let $\delta_{xx}U_{ij}=(U_{i+1,j}-2U_{ij}+U_{i-1,j})/(\Delta x)^2$, and define $\delta_{yy}$ analogously. The <Crank-Nicolson diffusion scheme> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{U_{ij}^{m+1}-U_{ij}^m}{\Delta t}
=\frac12(\delta_{xx}+\delta_{yy})(U_{ij}^{m+1}+U_{ij}^m),\qquad1\leq i,j\leq N-1.}
$$
Use $U_{ij}^0=u_0(x_i,y_j)$ and zero values on every boundary node for every time level. This is the <theta method> with $\theta=1/2$ applied to the spatial <finite difference method> for the <heat equation>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Write $s=\mu_x+\mu_y$. The update can be rearranged as
$$
\begin{aligned}
(1+s)U_{ij}^{m+1}-\frac{\mu_x}2(U_{i+1,j}^{m+1}+U_{i-1,j}^{m+1})-\frac{\mu_y}2(U_{i,j+1}^{m+1}+U_{i,j-1}^{m+1})
={}&(1-s)U_{ij}^m+\frac{\mu_x}2(U_{i+1,j}^m+U_{i-1,j}^m)+\frac{\mu_y}2(U_{i,j+1}^m+U_{i,j-1}^m).
\end{aligned}
$$
When $s\leq1$, the right side is a <convex combination> of old values: its coefficients are nonnegative and sum to one. Assume all old values lie in $[U_{\min},U_{\max}]$. A new maximum $v>U_{\max}$ is interior because boundary values are zero and $U_{\max}\geq0$. At its node the left side is at least $(1+s)v-sv=v$, while the right side is at most $U_{\max}$, a contradiction. A new minimum $v<U_{\min}$ similarly makes the left side at most $v$ and the right side at least $U_{\min}$, again a contradiction.

The initial values satisfy the bounds by construction; <mathematical induction> proves
$$
\boxed{U_{\min}\leq U_{ij}^m\leq U_{\max}\quad\text{at every node and time level}.}
$$
The new-time matrix is a <strictly diagonally dominant matrix> with positive diagonal and nonpositive off-diagonal entries, so the linear update is uniquely solvable. This <Crank-Nicolson maximum principle under a step restriction> concerns preservation of value bounds, which is a stronger requirement than scalar <A-stability>.

= Solution

For nonnegative initial data, $U_{\min}=0$. The proved <Crank-Nicolson maximum principle under a step restriction> immediately gives $\boxed{U_{ij}^m\geq0}$ for all indices. For nonpositive initial data, $U_{\max}=0$ and the same bound gives $\boxed{U_{ij}^m\leq0}$. Equivalently, apply positivity to $-U$, since the <finite difference method> is linear and has homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions>. Both conclusions use $\mu_x+\mu_y\leq1$.

= Solution
{parent=a}

For the second-order <boundary value problem>, introduce an unknown initial slope $s$ and solve the <initial value problem> $u'=v$, $v'=g(x,u,v)$ with $u(a)=\alpha$, $v(a)=s$. Its endpoint mismatch is $F(s)=u(b;s)-\beta$. The <shooting method> seeks a root $F(s)=0$; in general the dependence of the flow on $s$ is nonlinear. A sign-changing bracket for a continuous $F$ allows the <bisection method>, provided the intervening initial value solutions exist up to $b$.

For <Newton's method>, solve the <parameter sensitivity equation> alongside the trajectory. With $z=\partial_su$ and $w=\partial_sv$,
$$
z'=w,\qquad w'=g_u z+g_{u'}w,\qquad z(a)=0,\quad w(a)=1.
$$
Then $F'(s)=z(b)$ and the update is $\boxed{s_{\mathrm{new}}=s-F(s)/z(b)}$ whenever $z(b)\ne0$.

For the third-order problem, shooting from $a$ leaves both $u'(a)$ and $u''(a)$ unknown, requiring two shooting parameters to satisfy two right-endpoint conditions. Shooting backwards from $b$ already knows $u(b)=\beta$ and $u'(b)=\gamma$; only $u''(b)$ is unknown, with the single residual $u(a)-\alpha$. **Backward shooting reduces the nonlinear solve from two scalar unknowns to one.** This parameter-count advantage does not by itself guarantee numerical stability of backward integration.

{parent=4}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <four-point midpoint third derivative> uses the four values at $x_{j-1},x_j,x_{j+1},x_{j+2}$, centred at $x_{j+1/2}$. The <Taylor theorem> about that midpoint gives
$$
\frac{u(x_{j+2})-3u(x_{j+1})+3u(x_j)-u(x_{j-1})}{h^3}
=u'''(x_{j+1/2})+\frac{h^2}{8}u^{(5)}(x_{j+1/2})+O(h^4).
$$
Thus, for $n\geq2$, take the $n-1$ interior equations
$$
\boxed{U_{j+2}-3U_{j+1}+3U_j-U_{j-1}=h^3f(x_{j+1/2}),\qquad j=1,\ldots,n-1.}
$$
Impose the two value conditions exactly, $U_0=\alpha$, $U_{n+1}=\beta$, and approximate the remaining condition with the second-order backward <finite difference>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{3U_{n+1}-4U_n+U_{n-1}}{2h}=\gamma.}
$$
This gives $n$ equations for $U_1,\ldots,U_n$. The endpoint <derivative> formula equals $u'(b)-h^2u'''(b)/3+O(h^3)$, so its residual tends to zero along with those of the four-point equations. Every stencil lies within $[a,b]$; no unprescribed ghost value is introduced. The construction is therefore a consistent <finite difference method> for all three boundary conditions.

= Solution

The five-point equations for the <Poisson equation> are
$$
\boxed{(L_hU)_{jk}:=\frac{4U_{jk}-U_{j+1,k}-U_{j-1,k}-U_{j,k+1}-U_{j,k-1}}{h^2}=f(x_j,y_k),\quad1\leq j,k\leq n,}
$$
with prescribed boundary values. If $L_h\phi\leq0$, an interior maximal value is at most the average of its four neighbours. Since no neighbour exceeds the maximum, equality forces all four to equal it. Repeating along adjacent nodes propagates that value to the boundary. Thus the <discrete maximum principle> gives $\max\phi\leq\max_{\partial\Omega_h}\phi$, in particular the usual positive-maximum bound with zero included.

For an $O(h^2)$ error estimate assume the exact solution has bounded continuous fourth partial <derivatives> up to the boundary. The <local truncation error> $\tau=L_hu-f$ then satisfies $\|\tau\|_\infty\leq Ch^2$ by the <Taylor theorem>. Put $e=u|_{\Omega_h}-U$, so $L_he=\tau$ and $e=0$ on boundary nodes. The <discrete Poisson barrier on a square>
$$
B(x,y)=\frac{x(1-x)+y(1-y)}4
$$
satisfies $L_hB=1$ exactly, $B\geq0$ on the grid boundary, and $B\leq1/8$. Set $\varepsilon=\|\tau\|_\infty$. Then $L_h(\pm e-\varepsilon B)\leq0$, with nonpositive boundary values. Applying the <discrete maximum principle> twice gives
$$
\boxed{|e_{jk}|\leq\varepsilon B(x_j,y_k)\leq\frac18\|\tau\|_\infty=O(h^2).}
$$
For nonzero smooth <Dirichlet boundary data> $g$, impose $U=g$ at the boundary nodes. The error still vanishes there, so **the same argument and order apply**, under the same fourth-derivative regularity of the exact solution. Smooth boundary values alone do not remove every possible corner-regularity issue.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Replace $L_h$ by $\boxed{\widetilde L_h=L_h+I}$. At a positive interior maximum $v>0$, the neighbour average is no larger than $v$, so $(\widetilde L_h\phi)_{jk}\geq v>0$. Thus $\widetilde L_h\phi\leq0$ cannot have such an interior maximum. The positive-maximum form of the <discrete maximum principle> therefore remains valid:
$$
\max\phi\leq\max(0,\max_{\partial\Omega_h}\phi).
$$
With $e=u|_{\Omega_h}-U$ the error equation is $\widetilde L_he=\tau$, where $\tau=O(h^2)$ under the preceding regularity assumptions. Since $\widetilde L_hB=1+B\geq1$ for the same <discrete Poisson barrier on a square>, the comparison functions $\pm e-\|\tau\|_\infty B$ prove
$$
\boxed{|e_{jk}|\leq\tfrac18\|\tau\|_\infty=O(h^2).}
$$
There is **no loss of uniqueness**: apply the positive-maximum argument to both signs of a homogeneous discrete solution. The continuous <weak formulation> also has the <coercive bilinear form> $\int_\Omega(|\nabla v|^2+v^2)$, so the added positive reaction term strengthens its positive energy.

= Solution
{parent=6}

Let $x_*=A^{-1}b$, $e_k=x_k-x_*$ and $G=M^{-1}N$. Subtract the exact fixed-point equation to obtain $e_k=G^ke_0$. Hence convergence for every initial vector is equivalent to $G^k\to0$. If this holds, it holds after complexification; applying it to a complex <eigenvector> gives $\lambda^kv\to0$, so $|\lambda|<1$. Conversely, the complex <Jordan normal form> expresses each power as a finite sum of terms $\binom{k}{r}\lambda^{k-r}$ from its blocks. Every term tends to zero when all <eigenvalues> have modulus below one. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{Convergence for every starting vector}\iff\rho(G)<1.}
$$
The <convergence criterion for a stationary iteration> needs complex triangularization or <Jordan normal form>. The paper's assertion that every real matrix is similar to a real upper-triangular matrix is false when nonreal <eigenvalues> occur; complex triangularization supplies the intended argument. In the <Gauss-Seidel method>, with $A=D+L+U$ split into diagonal, strictly lower and strictly upper parts, $\boxed{M=D+L,\ N=-U}$.

For $n\geq4$, eliminating the ghost values gives the symmetric pentadiagonal <matrix>
$$
\boxed{A=\begin{pmatrix}
7&-4&1&0&\cdots\\
-4&6&-4&1&\cdots\\
1&-4&6&-4&\ddots\\
0&1&-4&6&\ddots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots
\end{pmatrix},\quad A_{nn}=7,\ A_{jj}=6\ (2\leq j\leq n-1),\quad A_{j,j+1}=-4,\quad A_{j,j+2}=1.}
$$
All other off-diagonal entries vanish. The first two equations are
$$
7U_1-4U_2+U_3=h^4f_1,\qquad
-4U_1+6U_2-4U_3+U_4=h^4f_2,
$$
and the last two are
$$
U_{n-3}-4U_{n-2}+6U_{n-1}-4U_n=h^4f_{n-1},\qquad
U_{n-2}-4U_{n-1}+7U_n=h^4f_n.
$$
For $n=3$ the matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}7&-4&1\\-4&6&-4\\1&-4&7\end{pmatrix}$, for $n=2$ it is $\begin{pmatrix}7&-4\\-4&7\end{pmatrix}$, and for $n=1$ it is $(8)$ because both ghost contributions meet the same unknown. The diagonal entries are positive in every case, so the lower triangular <matrix> $D+L$ is invertible and the <Gauss-Seidel iteration> is well defined.

An <eigenvalue> $\lambda$ of $G=-(D+L)^{-1}U$ makes $H(\lambda)=\lambda(D+L)+U$ singular. Let $\ell_i$ and $r_i$ be the sums of absolute lower and upper entries in row $i$. The row's diagonal-dominance margin is
$$
|\lambda|d_i-|\lambda|\ell_i-r_i.
$$
Interior full rows have $d_i=6$, $\ell_i=r_i=5$, giving margin $|\lambda|-5$. Boundary rows have larger margins; for instance the first gives $7|\lambda|-5$, and the second gives at least $2|\lambda|-5$. Therefore $|\lambda|>5$ would make $H(\lambda)$ a <strictly diagonally dominant matrix>, hence nonsingular, a contradiction.

At $|\lambda|=5$ it is weakly diagonally dominant with strict boundary rows. Its nonzero nearest-neighbour entries make it an <irreducibly diagonally dominant matrix>. The nonsingularity theorem for that class again contradicts singularity. For the small matrices above all rows are already strict at this value. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{Every iteration eigenvalue has }|\lambda|<5;\text{ equality is impossible}.}
$$
There is also a stronger conclusion. For $T=\operatorname{tridiag}(-1,2,-1)$,
$$
A=T^2+2e_1e_1^T+2e_ne_n^T,
$$
including the $n=1$ overlap. Since $T$ is positive definite, $A$ is a <symmetric positive-definite matrix>. The <Gauss-Seidel convergence from diagonal dominance and positive definiteness> theorem consequently gives the stronger bound $\rho(G)<1$.
