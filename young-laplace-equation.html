Young–Laplace equation (Δp=γκ)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Fluid mechanics Surface tension
OurBigBook.com Words: 583 Articles: 4
The Young–Laplace equation relates the pressure jump across a stationary interface to its curvature and surface tension. Choose a unit normal vector n pointing from the inside region to the outside region, and define κ=∇s​⋅n to be positive for a convex sphere. In mechanical equilibrium, with constant surface tension and no additional normal viscous stresses,
Δp=pin​−pout​=γκ=γ(k1​+k2​).
(10)
Here k1​,k2​ are the signed principal curvatures in this convention. Their sum is twice the corresponding signed mean curvature; stating the convention prevents a factor-of-two ambiguity. The Young–Laplace equation is a local normal stress balance for one interface. [ref]
A spherical liquid droplet of radius R in a gas has one interface, with k1​=k2​=1/R. An ideal thin soap bubble has two liquid–gas interfaces. Adding their pressure jumps gives
Δpdroplet​=R2γ​,Δpsoap bubble​≃R4γ​.
(11)
For the soap bubble, Δp now means the inner gas pressure minus the outer gas pressure. The second formula assumes equal surface tensions on both faces and film thickness negligible relative to R, so their radii can be identified. Retaining distinct radii gives 2γ/Rinner​+2γ/Router​. A gas bubble inside bulk liquid has only one interface and therefore uses 2γ/R. [ref]
With the assumed constant toy surface tension γ=0.072Nm−1, a droplet with R=1mm has excess pressure 144Pa; at R=0.5mm it has 288Pa. A thin soap bubble assigned the same γ and R=1mm has excess pressure 288Pa. These are differences from the surrounding pressure, rather than absolute pressures. Halving the radius doubles the capillary pressure; doubling the interface count also doubles it at fixed radius and surface tension.
The spherical case has a short work argument. A virtual increase in radius gives dV=4πR2dR and dA=8πRdR. At mechanical equilibrium, the net pressure work balances the increase in surface energy:
pressure workΔp4πR2dR​​=surface energy increaseγ8πRdR​​⟹Δp=R2γ​.
(12)
For a thin soap bubble, both interfaces gain approximately the same surface area, doubling the right-hand side and giving 4γ/R. This is a virtual work balance between neighboring spherical configurations, rather than an assumption that a fixed-volume droplet actually expands. The general Young–Laplace equation replaces the spherical area-to-volume ratio by local curvature, making geometry directly measurable through pressure.
