Second-order logic (SO)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Foundations of mathematics Mathematical logic
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 7
Second-order logic extends first-order logic by allowing existential quantification and universal quantification over second-order relation variables, as well as over individual objects. In the relational presentation used here, an individual variable x ranges over a domain D, while a second-order relation variable R of arity k denotes a subset of Dk. Unary second-order relation variables denote subsets of D; binary ones denote binary relations. Presentations with function variables can instead describe their graphs by second-order relation variables, with first-order formulas requiring a unique output for every input.
For a graph with vertices D and fixed graph adjacency E, the first-order sentence ∀x∃yE(x,y) says that every vertex has a neighbour. It selects individual vertices while E retains its given interpretation. By contrast,
∃P(∃xP(x) ∧ ∀x∀y((P(x)∧P(y))→¬E(x,y)))
(1)
selects an entire subset P and says that it is a nonempty independent set. Here P is a unary second-order relation variable, rather than a symbol in the input first-order signature. This particular property also has a simple first-order sentence on simple graphs; the example illustrates the change in what can be selected, rather than proving a separation in expressive power.
The usefulness of second-order logic is that one can describe a whole proposed solution, such as a graph coloring, and then impose local constraints on it. Syntax alone does not specify which proposed relations are available: full semantics for second-order logic permits all external subsets of Dk, whereas Henkin semantics supplies designated ranges. The second-order encoding of three-colorability makes this distinction concrete.
