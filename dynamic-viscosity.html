Dynamic viscosity (μ)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Fluid mechanics
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 10
The dynamic viscosity μ is the coefficient relating shear stress to shear rate in a Newtonian fluid. For a simple shear flow u=(u(y),0,0),
τxy​=μdydu​=μγ˙​,[μ]=Pas=kgm−1s−1.
(1)
Here τxy​ is the x component of viscous traction on a plane with normal vector +ey​. The shear rate γ˙​=du/dy has units s−1. Thus dynamic viscosity measures the stress required to sustain a given rate of deformation. It is the shear coefficient; the separate coefficient for volume change is volume viscosity. [ref]
For a worked Couette flow example, place a Newtonian fluid of constant dynamic viscosity μ=0.10Pas between parallel plates separated by h=0.0010m. Hold the lower plate fixed and move the upper plate at U=0.020ms−1. Assume steady flow, no streamwise pressure gradient, and the no-slip boundary condition. Then
u(y)=hUy​,γ˙​=hU​=20s−1,τxy​=hμU​=2.0Pa.
(2)
The fluid pulls backward on the moving plate with force magnitude F=τxy​A, where A is its wetted area. For A=0.010m2 this is 0.020N. Measuring that force, the gap and the plate velocity determines μ=Fh/(AU): a direct way to measure dynamic viscosity. The linear profile and opposing wall tractions are the standard plane Couette flow solution. [ref]
The velocity gradient matters, rather than the velocity itself. Adding the same constant velocity to both plates and the fluid leaves du/dy, and hence shear stress, unchanged. In uniform flow, every layer translates together and du/dy=0. More generally, even a nonzero velocity gradient need not generate viscous stress: solid-body rotation has zero strain-rate tensor, as the viscous stress tensor example shows. Thin gaps amplify shear stress for a fixed plate-speed difference, making dynamic viscosity useful for predicting the force needed to move lubricated surfaces.

Lateral viscosity (νh​)

Words: 39

An effective horizontal transport coefficient for momentum, often representing unresolved eddies in ocean models. A simple constant-coefficient closure adds νh​∇h2​u to the Cauchy momentum equation and νh​∇h2​ξ to the vorticity equation. Its balance with the beta effect sets the width of a Munk boundary layer.

Kinematic viscosity (ν=μ/ρ)

Words: 292 Articles: 3
Dynamic viscosity OurBigBook.com
The kinematic viscosity is dynamic viscosity divided by mass density:
ν=ρμ​,[ν]=m2s−1.
(3)
It measures the rate at which viscous transport smooths differences in velocity. For constant μ and ρ in incompressible flow, dividing the Navier-Stokes equation by mass density makes the viscous acceleration ν∇2u, where ∇2 is the Laplacian. [ref]
For example, stipulate two Newtonian fluids with the same dynamic viscosity 0.10Pas but different mass densities. In the same steady Couette flow as above, both have shear stress 2.0Pa, but
ρ1​ρ2​​=1000kgm−3,=500kgm−3,​ν1​ν2​​=1.0×10−4m2s−1,=2.0×10−4m2s−1.​
(4)
The same viscous stress transports momentum through less mass in the second fluid, so its velocity responds faster to a given nonuniform viscous force.
To see the diffusion equation directly, consider an unsteady parallel velocity field u=(u(y,t),0,0) with constant material coefficients and no streamwise pressure gradient or body force. Its convective acceleration vanishes, and the Navier-Stokes equation reduces to
∂t​u=ν∂y2​u.
(5)
Balancing u/t against νu/h2 gives the viscous diffusion time tν​∼h2/ν. For the 1.0mm gap, the two fluids have scales 0.010s and 0.0050s. This is a characteristic scale, not an exact settling time. For example, a perturbation of the Couette flow profile satisfying zero perturbation velocity at both plates is
δu(y,t)=Vsin(hπy​)exp(−h2π2νt​).
(6)
Substituting verifies the diffusion equation; its amplitude falls by a factor e in h2/(π2ν). Kinematic viscosity is therefore useful for predicting how quickly a moving wall influences the interior, while dynamic viscosity gives the shear stress at a prescribed shear rate.

Momentum diffusion (∂t​u=ν∂z2​u)

Viscous transfer of tangential momentum smooths gradients of velocity. For unidirectional incompressible flow without a pressure gradient, the Navier-Stokes equation reduces to the displayed diffusion equation, whose diffusion coefficient is the kinematic viscosity ν=η/ρ.

Viscous stress tensor (σvisc​=2μD)

Words: 470 Articles: 1
Dynamic viscosity OurBigBook.com
The viscous stress tensor is the part of the Cauchy stress tensor associated with deformation rates. For an isotropic Newtonian fluid in incompressible flow,
σ=−pI+σvisc​,σvisc​=2μD,D=21​[∇u+(∇u)T],trD=∇⋅u=0.
(7)
Here p is pressure, I is the identity matrix, μ is dynamic viscosity, and D is the strain-rate tensor. The convention is (∇u)ij​=∂j​ui​; T denotes transpose and tr denotes trace. Across a plane with unit normal vector n, the viscous traction is σvisc​n. The tensor makes this rule work for any plane orientation. [ref]
In the worked Couette flow, u=(Uy/h,0,0), so
D=2hU​​010​100​000​​,σvisc​=​020​200​000​​Pa.
(8)
In particular, Dxy​=10s−1 while the shear rate is 20s−1; the factor 2 in 2μD recovers τxy​=2Pa. On the upper face of a fluid slice, n=ey​ gives viscous traction +2ex​Pa; on the lower face it is −2ex​Pa. Constant stress can therefore transmit a force between the plates even though its divergence, and the net viscous force on each slice, vanish.
Compare planar extension u=(ax,−ay,0) with solid-body rotation u=(−Ωy,Ωx,0):
Dextension​=​a00​0−a0​000​​,∇urotation​=​0Ω0​−Ω00​000​​,Drotation​=0.
(9)
Extension changes lengths and produces viscous normal stresses ±2μa. Rotation preserves distances and gives zero viscous stress, despite its nonzero velocity gradient and vorticity 2Ωez​. For a=Ω=1s−1 and μ=0.10Pas, extension gives σvisc,xx​=0.20Pa and σvisc,yy​=−0.20Pa; rotation gives zero. A rotating fluid may still have a pressure gradient supplying its centripetal acceleration.
For an isotropic Newtonian fluid in three-dimensional compressible flow, separate shape change from volume change:
σvisc​=2μ(D−3∇⋅u​I)+ζ(∇⋅u)I.
(10)
The first term is deviatoric stress, with zero trace; ζ is volume viscosity, also called bulk viscosity. For uniform expansion u=bx, D=bI and ∇⋅u=3b, so the shear contribution vanishes and the viscous stress is 3ζbI. Thus dynamic viscosity and volume viscosity describe distinct responses, and incompressible flow removes only the volume-change term. [ref]

Viscous force (Fvisc​=∫∂V​τndS)

Words: 114
Viscous stress tensor OurBigBook.com
The viscous force on a fluid region is the resultant of the traction supplied by the viscous stress tensor τ. With n the outward unit normal of the region, its surface contribution is the displayed surface integral. For an incompressible Newtonian fluid, τ=η[∇u+(∇u)T], where η is dynamic viscosity and u is velocity. A force scale is ηUL for a body of size L moving at speed U, while the inertial force scale is ρU2L2; their ratio is the Reynolds number. Total hydrodynamic drag on a body includes both viscous traction and the pressure traction induced by the flow. For example, the full Stokes drag law gives 6πηRU for a no-slip sphere, rather than just the viscous-traction part alone.

Reynolds number (Re=ρUL/μ=UL/ν)

Words: 249
Dynamic viscosity OurBigBook.com
The Reynolds number Re=UL/ν compares inertial and viscous effects. Here U is a characteristic velocity, L a characteristic length, μ the dynamic viscosity, ρ the mass density, and ν=μ/ρ the kinematic viscosity. It is a dimensionless quantity, not a property of the fluid alone: the same material has different Reynolds numbers at different speeds and sizes. Specify the chosen scales when reporting it.
The comparison comes from the Navier-Stokes equation. For velocity variations on scale L, convective acceleration is of order U2/L, while the viscous term is of order νU/L2. Their ratio is UL/ν. Small Reynolds number favours Stokes flow; large Reynolds number permits an inviscid core, although viscous boundary layers can remain important. Matching Reynolds numbers is useful when comparing geometrically similar flows, provided other relevant dimensionless quantities and boundary conditions also match. [ref]
In the constriction example under incompressible flow, use the mean axial velocity and pipe diameter, L=D=2R. Taking ρ=1000kgm−3 and a stipulated dynamic viscosity μ=0.001Pas gives
Re1​=0.0011000(0.20)(0.020)​=4000,Re2​=0.0011000(0.80)(0.010)​=8000.
(11)
Halving the radius at fixed volumetric flow rate doubles this diameter-based Reynolds number, because mean velocity increases fourfold. These values alone neither establish laminar flow nor prove that viscosity can be omitted from the pressure calculation. Increasing μ to 0.10Pas with the same imposed flow gives 40 and 80, but strengthens viscous effects; small Reynolds number does not justify Bernoulli's principle.
The name recalls Osborne Reynolds's 1883 experimental investigation of water motion and resistance in pipes. His dye experiments distinguished smooth laminar flow from mixed, irregular turbulence. [ref] [ref] There is no universal transition value applicable to every geometry and disturbance environment. [ref]
