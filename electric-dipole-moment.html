Electric dipole moment

 Home Physics Branches of physics Electromagnetism Electrostatics
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 3
The electric dipole moment measures the first spatial moment of signed electric charge. For localized point charges qi​ at positions ri​, or an integrable charge density ρ(r) with a finite first moment, define
p=∑i​qi​ri​,p=∫rρ(r)d3r,Q=∑i​qi​=∫ρ(r)d3r.
(193)
[ref].
The electric dipole moment is a vector with SI units Cm: coulombs times length in metres. Opposite electric charges can cancel in Q while contributing in the same direction to p. For a neutral pair with positive electric charge q>0 at r+​ and negative electric charge −q at r−​,
d=r+​−r−​,p=qd.
(194)
Thus the electric dipole moment points from negative to positive electric charge, not along the attraction between the two point charges. [ref].
Changing the coordinate origin changes the position vectors, not the physical electric charge distribution. If the new origin is at a relative to the old origin, ri′​=ri​−a, so direct substitution gives
p′=∑i​qi​(ri​−a)=p−Qa.
(195)
Consequently charge neutrality makes the electric dipole moment independent of the origin. For a system with nonzero total electric charge, always specify the origin: a single point charge at the old origin has p=0 but p′=−Qa. This is coordinate bookkeeping, not a newly created electric dipole. The full electric potential stays the same at the same physical point; the terms of its electric multipole expansion and the observation coordinates change together.
As a stipulated numerical example, put +2nC at (1,0,0)mm and −2nC at (−1,0,0)mm. Their total electric charge is zero and p=(4×10−12,0,0)Cm. Translating the origin by (3,−2,0)mm leaves this electric dipole moment unchanged. Adding +1nC at the old origin instead gives Q=1nC and p′=(1,2,0)×10−12Cm. Distinguishing these two cases prevents the mistaken claim that every electric dipole moment is independent of the origin.
The usefulness of the electric dipole moment is compression: many internal electric charges determine one vector governing the leading distant electric potential of a neutral localized object when p=0. [ref]. A polar molecule need not literally contain two point charges; its atomic nuclei and electron density collectively give an electric dipole moment. For example, the chemical polarity of water reflects its bent molecular geometry, whereas the symmetric linear molecular geometry of carbon dioxide makes its permanent electric dipole moment vanish. [ref]. A collection of such electric dipole moments per unit volume gives electric polarization. [ref].
