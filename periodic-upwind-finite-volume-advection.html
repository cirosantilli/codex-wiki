Periodic upwind finite volume advection (Ujn+1​=(1−ν)Ujn​+νUj−1n​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Numerical analysis Finite volume method
OurBigBook.com Words: 379
Consider the advection equation ut​+aux​=0 with constant speed a>0, no sources, and periodic boundary conditions on [0,1]. Use N equal cells of width h=1/N, time step k>0, and indices modulo N. The exact solution is the periodic translation u(x,t)=u0​(x−at). At a face, the Riemann problem transports the left state to the right, so the two-state upwind numerical flux is F(L,R)=aL, with F(U,U)=aU.[ref]
The finite volume method with this numerical flux and an explicit Euler method step gives
Fj+1/2n​=aUjn​,ν=hak​,Ujn+1​=(1−ν)Ujn​+νUj−1n​.
(6)
Here Ujn​ represents a cell average; the recurrence also occurs in the upwind finite difference scheme. Impose the specific CFL condition 0<ν≤1. Each updated cell average is then a convex combination of two old ones. This proves preservation of their initial minimum and maximum, including nonnegativity for nonnegative data, and contraction of the maximum norm of the difference of two solutions on this periodic grid. These statements concern this constant-speed scalar update with this time step.
For an original synthetic example, take N=8, a=1, h=1/8, k=1/16, hence Courant number ν=1/2. Choose a function constant on each successive cell, with the following values, so these are exact initial cell averages. One step gives
jUj0​Uj1​​001/2​100​221​322​413/2​501/2​600​711/2​​M0=M1=81​⋅6=43​.
(7)
For example, cell zero receives from cell seven across the periodic face: U01​=(0+1)/2=1/2. All eight face transfers cancel in the total by shared face flux cancellation. More generally this periodic recurrence conserves the total algebraically for any ν, even though the CFL condition is needed for the convex combination argument. With the same initial data and ν=3/2, one step gives U21​=−1 and U41​=5/2, despite preserving the total.
After four steps, t=1/4, the exact translated profile has moved two cells to the right. Repeated averaging instead gives the explicit binomial coefficient formula
Uj4​=161​(Uj0​+4Uj−10​+6Uj−20​+4Uj−30​+Uj−40​),
(8)
with all indices modulo eight. The diagram compares these computed cell averages with the exact translated cell averages. Their integrals agree, but the numerical jumps spread across cells. The finite volume method is useful for jump discontinuities because it retains the integral balance even at a jump; conservation alone does not guarantee a sharp profile. At ν=1 this particular update is an exact one-cell shift, whereas for 0<ν<1 it mixes neighboring states. No accuracy conclusion at a jump follows just from the conserved total.
