Noninvertible map

Words: 73
Discrete dynamical system OurBigBook.com
A map is noninvertible when it is not one-to-one and onto on the phase space under consideration. Forward iteration can be well-defined while backward states are absent or nonunique. A zero Jacobian multiplier can provide strong contraction at a fixed point. For the Lozi family, setting b=0 sends the whole plane into a line after one iteration, illustrating why a discrete saddle may have a zero stable multiplier without a unique local backward evolution.

Aperiodic orbit

Words: 55
Discrete dynamical system OurBigBook.com
An aperiodic orbit does not repeat with a finite period. An orbit that is not eventually periodic never enters a finite cycle after any number of transients. Aperiodicity alone is insufficient for chaos: an irrational circle rotation is aperiodic but has no exponential separation of nearby states. Chaotic interval maps can have both uncountably many aperiodic orbits and dense periodic points.

Lozi map (F(x,y)=(1+y−a∣x∣,bx))

Words: 152 Articles: 1
Discrete dynamical system OurBigBook.com
A piecewise affine planar map whose derivative changes across x=0. Away from that line its Jacobian is (−asgnxb​10​), with determinant −b. For b=0 it is invertible; at b=0 an iteration collapses the image onto a line. The switching line requires piecewise analysis rather than applying a smooth-map theorem across it without checking its hypotheses.

Lozi fixed points and the alternating-sign two-cycle (x1​=(a+1−b)/(a2+(1−b)2),x2​=(1−b−a)/(a2+(1−b)2))

Words: 97
Lozi map OurBigBook.com
The positive fixed point exists when b<1+a and equals (1,b)/(1+a−b); the negative one exists when b>1−a and equals (1,b)/(1−a−b). Equality on either boundary does not give a branch, because its fixed-point equation becomes 0=1. For a genuine two-cycle and a2=(1−b)2, subtracting its scalar equations rules out equal signs of the two first coordinates. Solving with x1​>0>x2​ gives the displayed coordinates and second coordinates y1​=bx2​, y2​=bx1​. Their signs require exactly a>∣1−b∣. The cycle is unique up to interchanging its two points. Degenerate boundary parameter lines can support additional period-two families and are outside this statement.

Area-preserving map (∣detDF∣=1)

Words: 133 Articles: 1
Discrete dynamical system OurBigBook.com
A planar diffeomorphism is area-preserving if the phase-space area of every measurable set is unchanged. The change-of-variables formula proves this property when ∣detDF∣=1 everywhere, and conversely gives that determinant condition for a smooth map preserving all sufficiently small areas. Such a map cannot have an asymptotically attracting fixed point with a positive-area local basin: iterates of a small invariant neighborhood would have to enter arbitrarily small disks while retaining its phase-space area. For a map with constant positive determinant b, invariance of a bounded region of positive phase-space area instead forces b=1.

Nonlinear shear map ((u,v)↦(u+f(v),v))

Words: 47
Area-preserving map OurBigBook.com
This triangular planar map has diagonal Jacobian matrix entries one and determinant one, for differentiable f. Its inverse subtracts f(v), so it is an area-preserving map. The analogous vertical map (u,v)↦(u,v+g(u)) has the same property. Their composition preserves area even when the shifts are nonlinear, as in the logarithmic area-preserving seasonal predator-prey map.

Orbit equations for a triangular cubic map (S[1+(S−a−2)3]=b(a+2))

Words: 88
Discrete dynamical system OurBigBook.com
For F(x,y)=((a+1)x−x3+y,(b−1)y−(b−1)x3+y3), the origin multipliers are a+1, b−1. A nonzero fixed point has S=x2>0, y=x(S−a), and S[1−(S−a)3]=a(2−b). Since the map is odd, a symmetric two-cycle has F(x,y)=−(x,y); it satisfies y=x(S−a−2) and S[1+(S−a−2)3]=b(a+2). Eliminating y from the two map equations gives these identities directly. They locate the side of each pitchfork or flip without relying on a potentially misleading cubic sign in the original coordinates. At (a,b)=(−1,0) the second identity becomes 3S2−3S3+S4=b, giving the quartic amplitude scaling of a generalized flip bifurcation.

Hénon map

Words: 237 Articles: 2
Discrete dynamical system OurBigBook.com
The Hénon map is a two-parameter quadratic planar map, conventionally (X,Y)↦(1−aX2+Y,bH​X). The alternative family (x,y)↦(y,μ−bx−y2) has constant Jacobian determinant b. When μ=0, the change X=y/μ, Y=−bx/μ gives the conventional parameters a=μ, bH​=−b. Fixed points in the alternative normalization satisfy x=y and y2+(1+b)y−μ=0. Its invertibility requires b=0; at b=1 it preserves area.

Conservative Hénon stability boundary (detDF=1(b=1))

Words: 101
Hénon map OurBigBook.com
At b=1 the Hénon map has unit Jacobian determinant. The upper fixed point is linearly elliptic for −1<μ<3, and its newborn two-cycle is elliptic for 3<μ<4. These are neutral multiplier statements, not attraction. The endpoints have double multipliers +1 and −1. Constant-area scaling also excludes a simple invariant closed curve enclosing positive area when b>0 and b=1: invariance of its interior would require both area(F(D))=barea(D) and F(D)=D. Thus the b=1 elliptic stability boundary is a conservative degeneracy, not a generic dissipative Neimark-Sacker bifurcation. Arbitrary non-area-preserving perturbations can change this behavior even though generic fold and flip curves persist.

Sine fixed-point iteration (xn+1​=sinxn​)

Words: 57
Iterated function OurBigBook.com
The sine fixed-point iteration converges to zero from every real starting value. After one step, ∣xn​∣≤1, and ∣xn+1​∣=sin∣xn​∣. Since 0<sint<t for 0<t≤1, the absolute values form a bounded monotone sequence. Its limit a satisfies a=sina by continuity, forcing a=0. This proves convergence even though the sine is not a contraction mapping on any interval containing zero in its interior.

Discontinuous trapping of decreasing iterates (fn(x)→1/(j+1) for x∈(1/(j+1),1/j])

Words: 71
Iterated function OurBigBook.com
A map on (0,1) can satisfy 0<f(x)<x and still have no orbit tending to zero. Partition the domain into Ij​=(1/(j+1),1/j]∩(0,1) and define f(x)=(x+1/(j+1))/2 on Ij​. Every interval is invariant under the iteration of a map, and fn(x)=1/(j+1)+2−n(x−1/(j+1)) has a positive limit. The discontinuities at interval boundaries prevent that limit from being a fixed point. In contrast, continuity of a self-map satisfying f(x)<x permits passage to a positive orbit limit and would force an impossible fixed point.

Entropy of a hyperbolic toral automorphism (htop​(A)=log∣λu​∣(A∈GL(2,Z)))

Words: 81
Topological entropy OurBigBook.com
For a two-dimensional hyperbolic toral automorphism, the stable and unstable eigenvalues obey ∣λs​∣<1<∣λu​∣. In a sufficiently small Bowen ball, lifting to the stable and unstable coordinates gives widths proportional to ϵ and ϵ∣λu​∣−(n−1). Its normalized Haar measure is therefore proportional to ϵ2∣λu​∣−(n−1), uniformly in the centre. Maximal separated sets cover by radius-ϵ Bowen balls and pack disjoint radius-ϵ/2 Bowen balls, so their exponential growth rate is log∣λu​∣. Smallness of ϵ must exclude nonzero lattice jumps between successive lifted iterates; this is essential to the ball-volume argument.

Coefficient Banach space for normalized even maps (p(x)=1−x2h(x2),∥h∥=∑n​∣hn​∣)

The normalized even maps p(x)=1−x2h(x2) form an affine copy of that space and are analytic where ∣x2−1∣<R. Words: 81

Logistic map (Fr​(x)=rx(1−x),0≤r≤4)

Words: 817 Articles: 2
Interval map OurBigBook.com
The logistic map repeatedly applies a quadratic function:
xn+1​=Fr​(xn​)=rxn​(1−xn​),x0​∈[0,1],0≤r≤4.
(6)
The closed interval [0,1] is a positively invariant set: 0≤Fr​(x)≤r/4≤1. An orbit is the entire resulting sequence x0​,x1​,x2​,…, not just one application of the formula. For example, with r=2 and x0​=0.2, the first states are 0.2,0.32,0.4352,0.49160192,…, approaching the fixed point 1/2.
As a simple model of population dynamics, xn​ is a scaled abundance in generation n. When abundance is small, xn+1​/xn​=r(1−xn​) is close to r; increasing abundance reduces this per-capita multiplier. The falling side of the quadratic function represents sufficiently severe crowding reducing the next generation. The scale x=1 is not generally the carrying capacity: the positive fixed point is 1−1/r. The logistic map is a discrete-generation model; the continuous-time logistic growth equation has a different evolution rule and does not produce its period-doubling cascade.
The practical question is how abundance behaves after the initial adjustment: does it settle, alternate, or continue varying? The same formula permits all these possibilities. The following figure shows three orbits, their cobweb plots, and a bifurcation diagram. In a cobweb plot, moving vertically to y=Fr​(x) calculates the next state, and moving horizontally to y=x makes that value the input for the next step.
The fixed points solve Fr​(x)=x:
x=0,x∗​=1−r1​(r>0).
(7)
The second fixed point is in [0,1] for r≥1 and coincides with zero at r=1. The derivative is Fr′​(x)=r(1−2x), so the fixed-point multipliers are r at zero and 2−r at x∗​. The fixed point stability for an iteration test gives an attracting zero for 0≤r<1, and a locally asymptotically stable positive fixed point for 1<r<3. A multiplier of modulus one needs a nonlinear argument; the strict derivative test alone settles neither r=1 nor r=3.
This derivative test has a direct interpretation. A small error en​=xn​−x∗​ obeys
en+1​=(2−r)en​−ren2​.
(8)
At r=2.8, the leading multiplier is −0.8: the error changes sign, explaining the alternating approach, while its magnitude eventually shrinks by approximately a factor 0.8 per step. At r=3.2, the multiplier is −1.2, and the positive fixed point repels nearby states. However, a different outcome has asymptotic stability: the logistic map two-cycle attracts nearby orbits.
For 3<r<1+6​, the attracting period-two orbit has the two values
x±​=2rr+1±(r−3)(r+1)​​.
(9)
At r=3.2 they are approximately 0.513045 and 0.799455, with Fr​(x−​)=x+​ and Fr​(x+​)=x−​. Their periodic-orbit multiplier is 4+2r−r2=0.16, so a small displacement shrinks over two steps even though the fixed point is unstable. The brief algebraic justification is to factor Fr2​(x)−x, remove the fixed point factors, and multiply the two derivatives using the chain rule.
At r=3, the positive fixed-point multiplier passes through −1 and a period-doubling bifurcation creates the period-two orbit. At r=1+6​≈3.44949, its periodic-orbit multiplier passes through −1 and an attracting period-four periodic orbit emerges. At r=3.5, the period-four values are approximately 0.382820,0.826941,0.500884,0.874997 in that order. Further period-doubling bifurcations accumulate near r≈3.56995. Beyond that value there are also parameter windows with attracting periodic orbits, including a period-three window near r=3.83. Neither a crowded numerical plot nor a parameter beyond the accumulation value proves sensitive dependence on initial conditions on the attracting set.
The endpoint r=4 supplies an analytically transparent example. The homeomorphism h(u)=sin2(πu/2) satisfies
F4​∘h=h∘T,
(10)
where T is the full tent map. Thus these maps have topological conjugacy; stretching and folding in the tent map explain sensitive dependence on initial conditions and Devaney chaos for F4​ on [0,1]. This does not make every individual orbit nonperiodic: x0​=3/4 stays fixed, and x0​=1/2 follows 1/2,1,0,0,…. Sensitivity means that these exceptional orbits also have arbitrarily close initial states that eventually depart substantially.
Robert M. May's "Simple mathematical models with very complicated dynamics" (1976), Nature 261, pp. 459–467, reviewed how simple deterministic recurrence relations can pass from stable abundances through period-doubling cascades to irregular behaviour. May presented the logistic map as an especially accessible example and discussed the relevance of such models to population dynamics. The paper explicitly describes itself as a review of results found by several researchers, bringing their findings together for a broad scientific audience.[ref]

For a continuous function from an interval to itself with a three-cycle a<b<c, reflection if needed gives F(a)=b,F(b)=c,F(c)=a. Set I=[a,b],J=[b,c]. The intermediate value theorem gives F(I)⊇J and F(J)⊇I∪J. Successive closed-interval pullbacks realize every cyclic covering word by a point fixed under the corresponding iterate. The word IJn−1 has a unique visit to the interior of I per cycle, forcing least period n; the only boundary ambiguity is the original three-cycle, which realizes n=3 and cannot realize any other such word. A covering of J by itself also gives a fixed point. Thus every positive least period occurs.
