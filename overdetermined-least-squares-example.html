Overdetermined least-squares example (x∗​=(2,3)T)

Suppose three measurements constrain two unknowns:
A=​101​011​​,b=​126​​.
(144)
The linear system asks for x1​=1, x2​=2, and x1​+x2​=6, which are incompatible. A least-squares solution makes a balanced compromise. The matrix has full column rank, so
A+=(ATA)−1AT=31​(2−1​−12​11​),x∗​=A+b=(2,3)T.​
(145)
The fitted output and residual are
Ax∗​=(2,3,5)T,r=b−Ax∗​=(−1,−1,1)T,ATr=0.
(146)
Thus the two direct measurements are each overshot by one, and the measured sum is undershot by one. The residual has Euclidean norm 3​ and is orthogonal to the column space, the plane z=y1​+y2​ in output coordinates (y1​,y2​,z).
For any perturbation h∈R2, the orthogonality of r to Ah gives
∥b−A(x∗​+h)∥22​=∥r∥22​+∥Ah∥22​=3+∥Ah∥22​.
(147)
Since the kernel of A is trivial, every nonzero h increases the residual. The least-squares solution is unique in this example. The formula using ATA is convenient for these small exact calculations; an actual numerical computation can use a QR decomposition or singular value decomposition to avoid the extra conditioning cost of the least-squares normal equations. For full column rank, the squared singular values of A are the eigenvalues of ATA, so its condition number in the Euclidean norm is κ2​(ATA)=κ2​(A)2. [ref]
