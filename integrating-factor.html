Integrating factor (μ(t)=exp(∫t0​t​a(s)ds))

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Differential equation Ordinary differential equation Linear ordinary differential equation
OurBigBook.com Words: 923 Articles: 5
An integrating factor is a nonzero multiplier that makes a differential equation directly integrable. For a first-order linear differential equation, it gathers the unknown function and its derivative into one product rule expression. Suppose a and b are continuous functions on an interval containing t0​, and consider the initial value problem
y′+a(t)y=b(t),y(t0​)=y0​.
(33)
Define A(t)=∫t0​t​a(s)ds and μ(t)=eA(t). The exact solution of a differential equation throughout that interval is
y(t)=e−A(t)[y0​+∫t0​t​eA(s)b(s)ds].
(34)
The definite integral fixes μ(t0​)=1, so the initial condition appears without an extra constant to determine. If the equation originally has a coefficient multiplying y′, first divide by that coefficient on an interval where it is nonzero; a zero of that coefficient needs separate attention. See OpenStax's first-order linear equations chapter for this normalization and the integrating factor procedure.
For a concrete balance, solve y′+2y=3 with y(0)=0. Here μ=e2t, so multiplying the entire ordinary differential equation gives (e2ty)′=3e2t. Accumulating from zero to t gives
e2ty(t)=∫0t​3e2sds=23​(e2t−1),y(t)=23​(1−e−2t).
(35)
The computed values are y(0.5)≈0.94818, y(1)≈1.29700 and y(2)≈1.47253. Its derivative is 3e−2t, which verifies y′+2y=3 exactly. Interpreting 3 as a steady input and 2y as proportional removal explains the shape: initially all input increases y, then removal catches up. The constant particular solution yp​=3/2 balances input and removal, and the homogeneous solution yh​=−23​e−2t is the transient response that corrects it to the initial condition. Because this autonomous differential equation has a time-independent right-hand side, 3/2 is also an equilibrium point.
The first panel adds the particular solution and homogeneous solution; the second shows the same balance in rates, 3=y′+2y. This links the algebra to a physical accounting rule. Newton's law of cooling has this form with a=k and b=kTa​, while an electrical circuit can have a varying input. In both cases the integrating factor systematically handles the input rather than requiring a guess for a particular solution.
Variable coefficients work just as directly. For y′+2ty=t, y(0)=0, the integrating factor is et2, and
(et2y)′=tet2,et2y(t)=21​(et2−1),y(t)=21​(1−e−t2).
(36)
Thus y(0.5)≈0.11060, y(1)≈0.31606 and y(2)≈0.49084. The derivative y′=te−t2 verifies y′+2ty=t, including at zero, where both coefficient and input vanish. No division by t is necessary. The general solution is y=1/2+Ce−t2: 1/2 is a constant particular solution, and Ce−t2 solves the associated homogeneous linear differential equation. Although this special forcing term admits a constant trajectory, this is a nonautonomous differential equation: it depends explicitly on time. Merely setting its right-hand side to zero at one instant would not establish a constant solution of a differential equation; at t=0 every state has zero slope.
The direction field shows the selected trajectory leaving its initially horizontal tangent line. For t≥0, the difference between any two solutions of a differential equation is multiplied by e−t2, rather than e−2t in the constant-rate example. This dependence on elapsed history is clearer if the exact formula is rewritten as
y(t)=y0​e−∫t0​t​a(u)du+∫t0​t​e−∫st​a(u)dub(s)ds.
(37)
For t≥t0​, each contribution from the input at s is weighted by the accumulated coefficient between s and t. The homogeneous solution remembers the initial condition; the particular solution given by the second term accumulates the forcing term with zero initial value. This decomposition differs from choosing a constant particular solution, but gives the same total. Initial-data memory tends to zero when ∫t0​t​a→+∞; a negative accumulated coefficient instead amplifies it. The reciprocal relation between integrating factor and homogeneous solution is developed in MIT's notes on solutions to linear first-order equations.
Why does the trick work? The fundamental theorem of calculus and chain rule give μ′=aμ. Consequently the product rule gives
(μy)′=μy′+μ′y=μ(y′+ay)=μb.
(38)
Integrating this identity and dividing by the positive exponential function μ produces the stated formula. Any nonzero constant multiple of μ works equally well; normalization is a convenience. This is the beauty of the method: a sum involving an unknown function becomes one exact derivative, leaving only an integral of known functions. See Oregon State's product-rule derivation. The related integrating factor for a differential one-form extends the idea of making an expression exact beyond this linear setting.
