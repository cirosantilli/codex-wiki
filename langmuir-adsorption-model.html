Langmuir adsorption model (θ=bP/(1+bP))

 Home Chemistry Physical chemistry Adsorption Adsorption isotherm
OurBigBook.com Words: 852 Articles: 7
Consider an ideal gas in chemical equilibrium with homogeneous, identical adsorption sites at fixed temperature. Each intact molecule occupies one site; only one monolayer is allowed. Occupation of distinct sites is independent: neighboring bound molecules do not change binding or release rates. The simplest Langmuir adsorption model has one adsorbing chemical species, no splitting of molecules, and no competitors. Irving Langmuir's 1918 paper treats simple adsorption alongside more complicated cases; its scope exceeds this particular model.[ref]
The derivation is a balance between arrivals finding vacancies and departures from occupied adsorption sites. Let ka​P be the arrival-and-binding rate per vacant site and kd​ the departure rate per occupied site. Thus ka​ has units of inverse pressure per unit time, whereas kd​ has units of inverse time. The mean surface coverage obeys
dtdθ​=ka​P(1−θ)−kd​θ.
(13)
At chemical equilibrium, setting the two terms equal gives
ka​P(1−θ)=kd​θ,b=kd​ka​​,θ=1+bPbP​​.
(14)
The product bP is dimensionless. Here P is the adsorbing gas's partial pressure, expressed in modern bar, with 1bar=105Pa, and b is expressed in bar−1. The pascal is the SI unit of pressure. This dimensional b is not itself a dimensionless thermodynamic equilibrium constant. A larger b gives greater surface coverage at the same P and temperature, but does not specify how quickly chemical equilibrium is reached: multiplying both rate coefficients by the same factor leaves b unchanged.
Choose b=0.5, 2, and 8bar−1 as illustrative parameters at one fixed temperature, without assigning a particular material. At P=0.5bar, substitution gives, respectively,
θ=1.250.25​=51​,θ=21​,θ=54​.
(15)
Solving θ=1/2 gives P1/2​=1/b: the three half-occupation pressures are 2, 0.5, and 0.125bar. For b=2bar−1, reaching θ=0.9 instead requires bP=9, or P=4.5bar. If the complete-monolayer capacity is stipulated to be qm​=10mmolg−1, then q=qm​θ predicts q=5mmolg−1 at P=0.5bar. This last number is an original hypothetical example, not a measured uptake.
The shape follows directly from the finite number of adsorption sites. For bP≪1, θ≈bP: nearly every arriving molecule finds a vacancy. For bP≫1, 1−θ=1/(1+bP)≈1/(bP): vacancies become scarce. More formally,
dPdθ​=(1+bP)2b​>0,dP2d2θ​=−(1+bP)32b2​<0.
(16)
Thus surface coverage rises with diminishing slope and approaches one only as P→∞. This saturation is a site-capacity limit, not a claim that the gas has reached its condensation pressure. A second layer, different site binding strengths, interactions between bound molecules, or a changing surface invalidate parts of the setup. A good fit alone does not establish identical adsorption sites: a restricted pressure range can hide such differences. The Langmuir adsorption model is useful because it makes the capacity and the pressure scale of filling separate, testable quantities.
