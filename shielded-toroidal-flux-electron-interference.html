Shielded toroidal-flux electron interference

 ... Branches of physics Quantum theory Topological quantum matter Anyon Charge-flux composite Aharonov-Bohm effect
OurBigBook.com Words: 253
In 1986, Akira Tonomura and collaborators coated a ferromagnet shaped as a torus with superconducting niobium and copper. A cooled stage held it in a 150 kV field-emission electron microscope. An electron biprism overlapped object and reference waves; film holograms were reconstructed optically. Room-temperature leakage screening selected samples; varying copper thickness tested electron penetration.[ref]
Below the superconducting transition, through-hole and outside electron waves differed in wave phase by zero or π modulo 2π. Warming through it changed the interference reversibly. Leakage and coherent-penetration bounds do not prove zero magnetic field.[ref]
Why these two phases? The shield's superconducting flux quantum is h/(2e), whereas the probing electron has electric charge −e. Substituting Φ=Nh/(2e) gives
eiδ=e−iNπ=(−1)N.
(6)
An odd N therefore moves a carrier fringe by half its spacing; an even N leaves it unchanged modulo a full spacing. The phase quantization provides an internal check on the superconducting enclosure. It does not mean that zero wave phase proves zero trapped magnetic flux.[ref]
The readout is electron holography in the off-axis holography geometry: a known tilted reference converts an unknown relative wave phase into a spatial carrier displacement. Reconstructing a magnetic wave phase assumes stable reference phase, adequate coherence and a calibrated carrier; electrostatic charging and material traversal can also shift wave phase. Optical wave phase amplification rescales the displayed fringe displacement. The experiment's shielding and comparison tests support the magnetic inference; an interferogram by itself measures the total relative wave phase, rather than separately certifying every contribution. The ideal field-free model describes the limiting geometry, while finite leakage and penetration remain questions of experimental bounds.
