From Heisenberg arrays to Born-Jordan commutation

In 1925, Werner Heisenberg proposed replacing classical orbital quantities with arrays of transition amplitudes and frequencies. His paper's opening demanded relations between quantities observable in principle; section 1 built products by summing over an intermediate state and noted their dependence on order. The formulas above express that construction in modern linear operator notation.[ref]
Pumping and a heated bath prepared Mercury vapor around a hot platinum wire, mesh and collector in the original Franck-Hertz experiment. A galvanometer read collector electric current while accelerating voltage varied at fixed retarding voltage. Retarding scans distinguished outgoing electron energies.[ref] The losses constrain excitation of chemical atoms; they do not determine every optical coupling. The Franck-Hertz experiment explains the original mistaken ionization interpretation.
Such excitation and spectral line evidence motivated a mechanics of discrete energy levels and transitions. Replacing one representation by another was a theoretical construction; the Franck-Hertz experiment does not measure a matrix product or establish a unique representation of an observable.
Later in 1925, Max Born and Pascual Jordan developed the array calculus systematically. Their section 3 states pq−qp=hI/(2πi), equivalently the modern canonical commutation relation
[X,P]=iℏI.

This relates position and momentum through an intrinsically noncommuting product.[ref]
For the quantum harmonic oscillator, the canonical commutation relation and creation and annihilation operators give H=ℏω(a†a+I/2), recovering both the spacing and zero-point energy algebraically.
