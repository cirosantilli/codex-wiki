Nuclear S-factor variation across a Gamow window (δE≃σG2​dlogS/dE)

OurBigBook.com Words: 272
The Gaussian approximation to a nonresonant thermonuclear rate already gives the saddle and curvature. In its notation EG​=4EB​, so the same result reads
E0​=[4EG​(kB​T)2​]1/3,σG2​=32E0​kB​T​,Δ1/e​=22​σG​=316E0​kB​T​​.
(16)
The full width Δ1/e​ joins the two points at e−1 of the peak of the approximating Gaussian function; it is neither its standard deviation σG​ nor its full width at half maximum 22log2​σG​. These are local Gaussian approximation widths, not exact equal-height endpoints of the asymmetric kernel.
Why keep the astrophysical S factor inside the integral? If S(E) is positive and smooth, stationarity of logS(E)−Φ(E) gives a leading peak displacement δE≃σG2​(dlogS/dE)E0​​. The slow-variation test is therefore ∣dlogS/dE∣σG​≪1, together with small curvature corrections. More generally any energy-dependent prefactor shifts the saddle; a temperature-dependent prefactor also changes the local temperature exponent of a thermonuclear reaction. A sharp nuclear feature can invalidate this expansion altogether.
Choose T=15MK and EG​=493keV for an illustrative proton pair. Then kB​T=1.293keV, E0​=5.905keV and Δ1/e​=6.380keV. The relative Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution has energy-density maximum kB​T/2=0.646keV, far below the reaction kernel's maximum. Now choose the deliberately artificial enhancement
S0​S(E)​=1+5exp[−2(0.7keV)2(E−10keV)2​].
(17)
Numerical integration raises the rate by about 54.2% at the same temperature, even though the enhancement is away from E0​. This is a sensitivity experiment, not a claimed proton–proton nuclear level or a physical nuclear line-shape model. It illustrates why extrapolating S(E0​) without checking nearby structure can fail.
