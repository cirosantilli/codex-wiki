Computer science studies how information can be represented and transformed by algorithms, and what those transformations can achieve. Its central questions connect mathematics to practical design: what answer should a procedure produce, why is it correct, and how much work does it require as its input grows? Computability theory asks which tasks can be solved at all; computational complexity theory asks what resources a solution needs.
The beauty is that choosing a representation can change what is feasible. Finding a name among a million entries sounds like a million checks. Store the entries in a sorted array, and binary search needs at most twenty middle-entry comparisons. The order makes each unsuccessful comparison eliminate a whole region of possibilities. A data structure supplies useful organization; an algorithm exploits it; time complexity explains the resulting savings.
Algorithm correctness covers every permitted input, including absent names and empty collections. Time complexity predicts scaling; statistical program profiling investigates a particular implementation.
