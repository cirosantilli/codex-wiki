Low-temperature heat capacity measurement

 ... Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Heat Heat capacity
OurBigBook.com Words: 616 Articles: 1
Low-temperature calorimetry measures a small sample's heat capacity by resolving a small temperature change after a known electrical energy input. The experimental challenge is that the support, heater and thermometer also warm up. Their calorimeter addenda must be removed before interpreting the sample's phonons or electronic heat capacity. The Debye T-cubed test turns the corrected response into a test of the low-temperature vibrational spectrum.
Manuel Brando's 2009 experiment used a sapphire platform suspended by four alloyed platinum wires from a copper ring fixed to a cryostat stage. Grease attached the sample; a chip heater and ruthenium-oxide resistance thermometer served platform PLS-2. A controller stabilized the bath; a lock-in amplifier supplied heating pulses and read the calibrated sensor. The steady rise calibrated thermal conductance as G=P/ΔT. A separate 59.1mg, 99.99% pure gold sample, compared with reference heat capacities, established the calorimeter addenda. Small pulses limited property variation; imperfect sample contact required a second relaxation time. The platform contribution rose below 1.5K, making simple extrapolation unsafe.[ref]
For an original idealization, assume sample and platform share one temperature T, the bath remains at Tb​, and both the total heat capacity and the weak-link thermal conductance G are approximately constant across one small pulse. Ignore other heat transfer and any phase transition. The first law of thermodynamics becomes
(Cs​+Ca​)dtdT​=P(t)−G(T−Tb​),τ=GCs​+Ca​​.
(42)
Here Cs​ is sample heat capacity, Ca​ is calorimeter addenda, P is deposited electrical power from Joule heating, and τ is a time constant. This is the same balance as calorimeter heat-loss correction, used here with a deliberately weak cryogenic link and a separately subtracted platform. For a constant pulse P0​ beginning at bath temperature, then switched off at th​,
T(t)−Tb​=⎩⎨⎧​GP0​​(1−e−t/τ),GP0​​(1−e−th​/τ)e−(t−th​)/τ,​0≤t≤th​,t>th​.​
(43)
The cooling exponential decay determines τ, while the heating plateau determines G=P0​/(T∞​−Tb​). Together with calorimeter addenda, they give Cs​=Gτ−Ca​. Cooling alone determines a ratio, not an absolute heat capacity. With finite th​, the observed end rise is smaller than the plateau; fit the full response or apply the displayed correction rather than identifying the two.
For the stipulated figure parameters, G=5.00×10−8WK−1, τ=20.0s, and Ca​=2.00×10−7JK−1 give Cs​=8.00×10−7JK−1. The asymptotic rise is 0.0400K, only 2% of bath temperature. At 100s, the rise is 0.0397305K. Of 2.00×10−7J input, only 3.97305×10−8J remains stored: the other 1.60270×10−7J has flowed to the bath. Treating this long pulse as an isolated Q/C experiment would overestimate total heat capacity by a factor of 5.034.
Suppose independent standard uncertainties are u(G)=0.05×10−8WK−1, u(τ)=0.2s and u(Ca​)=0.10×10−7JK−1. First-order propagation of uncertainty gives
u(Cs​)2=τ2u(G)2+G2u(τ)2+u(Ca​)2,Cs​=(8.00±0.17)×10−7JK−1.
(44)
The uncertainty is one standard deviation under those assumptions, about 2.2% of sample heat capacity. Shared thermometer calibration errors can correlate fitted G and τ; then their covariance contributes too. Smaller samples amplify the relative influence of calorimeter addenda. Proposed checks include varying pulse amplitude, repeating with a reference sample, checking sensor self-heating, and comparing single- and two-time-constant fits. A drifting bath, slow internal equilibration or a transition invalidates this simple reduction even if a cooling curve looks smooth.
