Torsion submodule (T(M))

 ... Area of mathematics Algebra Commutative algebra Module theory Module (mathematics) Torsion module
OurBigBook.com Words: 304 Articles: 1
For a module M over an integral domain R, its torsion submodule is
T(M)={m∈M:rm=0 for some 0=r∈R}.
(21)
This is a submodule. If rm=0 and sn=0 with r,s=0, then rs(m+n)=0 and rs=0; also r(am)=a(rm)=0 for every a∈R. The integral domain hypothesis supplies both the nonzero product and commutativity. These are the conventions of [ref].
A mixed example is the direct sum M=Z2⊕Z/6Z. If a nonzero integer kills (a,b,c), then a=b=0. Conversely, 6 kills every (0,0,c). Thus
T(M)={(0,0,c):c∈Z/6Z},M/T(M)≅Z2.
(22)
The torsion submodule isolates the finite-order part while the quotient module keeps the independent integer directions. In general M/T(M) is a torsion-free module: if r(m+T(M))=0 with r=0, some s=0 kills rm, so sr=0 kills m.
The hypothesis matters. In the ring R=Z/6Z viewed as a module over itself, the elements 2 and 3 are killed by nonzero scalars 3 and 2, but their sum 5 is a unit and is killed by no nonzero scalar. Thus using arbitrary nonzero scalars in a ring with zero divisors need not give a submodule. A common broader convention uses regular elements of a ring as annihilators; the integral domain convention here avoids that ambiguity.
