Altitude (γ)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Astrophysics Horizontal coordinate system
OurBigBook.com Words: 705 Articles: 1
Altitude γ, also called elevation angle, is the signed angle from the astronomical horizon to a viewing direction, measured in the vertical plane containing that direction and the zenith. Its range is −90∘≤γ≤90∘: positive altitude is above the horizontal plane, zero altitude lies on the astronomical horizon, and 90∘ points to the zenith. Altitude measures direction, rather than a Euclidean distance or a height in metres. In the local horizontal coordinate system, sinγ=n⋅eU​; the dot product makes the sign unambiguous even for directions below the astronomical horizon.
A concrete motivating observation is the Exploratorium Teacher Institute's Height Sight activity. Its inclinometer uses a sighting tube fixed to a card and an angular scale, with a string and small weight hanging from the scale's pivot. A partner reads where the freely hanging string meets the scale. Sighting a level target should give a readout near zero; sighting a taller target gives an angular height. The activity asks the observer to move closer and farther: the closer position gives the larger angle. The interpretation is that the readout measures the tube's tilt relative to the gravity reference, while converting that angle to a physical height also requires distance and eye height. The activity also extends the inclinometer to sky observations. [ref].
The following calibration and sky sequence is a proposed extension, with no claimed observing dataset. First establish two reference marks at the same height with a water level, rather than assuming that a sloping floor or distant ridge is horizontal. Keep the inclinometer in a vertical plane, sight between the marks, wait for the weight to settle, and read the zero several times. Next sight a mark whose independently measured rise q above the tube and horizontal separation d are known; a right triangle predicts γref​=arctan(q/d). A synthetic q=3m, d=5m gives γref​=30.964∘. Checking both zero and this nonzero angle can reveal a wrongly attached tube or a scale read from the wrong end. Use level separation, not distance along inclined ground.
If the scale's indicated altitude is m=γapp​+b, estimate its additive zero b from the level sight, then obtain apparent altitude γapp​=m−b. For synthetic readings b=1.0∘ and m=32.0∘, the corrected altitude is 31.0∘, close to the 30.964∘ calibration prediction. Declaring illustrative readout bounds of 0.5∘ for each reading gives a conservative combined bound of 1.0∘ on m−b; these bounds are assumptions, not measured performance. Repeatability tests the variation between readings, whereas a persistent zero offset remains a systematic measurement error. Include uncertainty in the reference rise and separation before judging agreement.
At a fixed site, sight a recognizable star, record the observation time, corrected altitude and north-through-east azimuth, and repeat over a short interval. Record the terrain boundary at the same azimuth separately from the gravity-defined zero. The fixed-direction model in horizontal coordinate system predicts changing angular readouts as the Earth rotates; this is a prospective test, rather than a fabricated measurement curve. Keep the tube aligned, the string free of contact and shielded from wind, the eye at the same reference position, and repeat the level check afterward. Timestamp each sight so genuine sky motion is not mistaken for poor repeatability. An optical telescope with an angular readout can replace the sighting tube, but its mounting still needs an independently established level and angular zero.
The corrected instrument reads apparent altitude, including atmospheric refraction. Under ordinary conditions atmospheric refraction raises a low direction, so if R is the upward angular correction, γapp​=γgeom​+R. Subtracting the instrument zero does not subtract R. Record site height, temperature and pressure if applying a refraction model: these are explicit observer inputs in the US Naval Observatory's NOVAS software. A coarse inclinometer may be unable to resolve the correction, and a local weather reading alone does not determine every atmospheric layer. [ref].
