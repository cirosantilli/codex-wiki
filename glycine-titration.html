Glycine titration

 ... Chemical substance Organic acid Carboxylic acid Amino acid Alpha-amino acid Glycine protonation
OurBigBook.com Words: 596 Articles: 1
Adding sodium hydroxide to acidified glycine successively removes its two titratable protons. Two broad buffer solution regions appear around the two pKa values, separated by a steep pH rise near the isoelectric point. This is useful evidence for two separated acid-base chemistry steps even though the changing chemical species are invisible. In the approximation that added hydroxide only changes glycine protonation, b=1−zˉ is the added base amount of substance per initial amount of substance of glycine. Then b≈0.5, 1 and 1.5 correspond to pKa1​, pI and pKa2​; endpoints are not exactly pure chemical species. [ref]
For the solid-curve model, start with nG​=0.00200mol of glycine in V0​=0.0200L and exactly nG​ of fully dissociated hydrochloric acid; this fixes the chloride inventory, but does not force every glycine molecule to remain G+. Let the sodium hydroxide concentration be CB​=0.100molL−1 and b=nNaOH​/nG​. Assume volume is additive and no other ions are present; stipulate Kw​=10−14 for self-ionization of water at a temperature of 25∘C. Then
V=V0​+CB​bnG​​,CT​=VnG​​,[Na+]=bCT​,[Cl−]=CT​.
(194)
Balancing all positive and negative electric charges requires
c∘(h−hKw​​)+CT​(b+zˉ−1)=0.
(195)
Solving this equation gives the solid curve. It remains finite at b=0 and beyond b=2, where ignoring free hydronium and hydroxide would fail. At the first pKa, the full model gives b≈0.436, rather than 0.5: appreciable free hydronium makes that half-equivalent shortcut inaccurate. At the second pKa, b≈1.502. Known acid inventory, dilution, and thermodynamic activity assumptions are therefore part of any quantitative interpretation, not optional corrections inferred from the curve's shape.
