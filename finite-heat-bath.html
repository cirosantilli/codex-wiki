Finite heat bath (ΔTb​=Qb​/Cb​)

 ... Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Thermal equilibrium Heat bath
OurBigBook.com Words: 777 Articles: 1
A finite heat bath has a finite whole-body heat capacity Cb​ and therefore changes temperature when it absorbs heat Qb​. For a rigid closed system with no work, fixed composition and no phase transition, the first law of thermodynamics gives
Qb​=ΔUb​=∫Tb,0​Tb,f​​Cb​(T)dT.
(7)
If the heat capacity at constant volume Cb​ is constant throughout this temperature interval, the integral becomes Qb​=Cb​(Tb,f​−Tb,0​). This statement concerns an internal energy difference; Cb​Tb​ need not be the body's absolute internal energy.
For an original example, put a rigid body 1 and a finite bath 2 into thermal contact inside an isolated system. Stipulate constant whole-body heat capacities C1​=100JK−1 and C2​=900JK−1, with initial temperatures T1,0​=350K and T2,0​=290K. Each body remains internally uniform; the connection stores negligible energy; no external work, matter exchange or phase transition occurs. These are constructed model values. The argument in thermal equilibration of two finite bodies applies directly: equal final temperatures and conserved total internal energy give
C1​(Tf​−T1,0​)+C2​(Tf​−T2,0​)=0,Tf​=C1​+C2​C1​T1,0​+C2​T2,0​​=296K.
(8)
Thus the heat Q transferred from body 1 to bath 2 is
Q=C1​(350−296)K=C2​(296−290)K=5400J.
(9)
Body 1 loses 5400J of internal energy and bath 2 gains exactly that amount. Body 1 cools by 54K, while bath 2 warms by 6K. Equal heat transfers produce unequal temperature changes because the heat capacities differ.
The heat bath approximation can now be assessed against a chosen allowed temperature change δT>0. Constant Cb​ requires ∣Qb​∣/Cb​≤δT, rather than merely calling the bath large. For this example, an allowed change of 0.1K would fail because the computed change is 6K. The exact bath shift and its ideal-reservoir limit are
Tf​−T2,0​=C1​+C2​C1​​(T1,0​−T2,0​),limC2​→∞​Tf​=T2,0​=290K.
(10)
Hold C1​ and both initial temperatures fixed in this limit. The transferred heat then approaches C1​(T1,0​−T2,0​)=6000J. The finite bath receives less heat because its warming reduces the available temperature difference. For heat capacities that vary, use the two integrals in the energy balance instead of this weighted average.
Conservation of energy also permits imagining the reverse transfer, so it cannot explain the direction of equilibration by itself. Entropy from heat capacity gives the additional test, using reversible comparison paths between equilibrium endpoints:
ΔS1​=C1​ln350296​≃−16.75737JK−1,ΔS2​=C2​ln290296​≃18.43068JK−1,
(11)
ΔStot​=C1​lnT1,0​Tf​​+C2​lnT2,0​Tf​​≃1.673308JK−1>0.
(12)
All ratios inside the natural logarithms use absolute temperatures. The actual exchange across unequal temperatures is irreversible, even though reversible paths calculate the endpoint entropy differences.
There is a short general proof of positivity. Put wi​=Ci​/(C1​+C2​) for positive constant heat capacities and positive initial temperatures. The strict concavity of the natural logarithm gives
C1​+C2​ΔStot​​=ln(w1​T1,0​+w2​T2,0​)−w1​lnT1,0​−w2​lnT2,0​≥0,
(13)
with equality only for equal initial temperatures. In this proof all temperatures inside the logarithms mean their numerical values in one common absolute unit. This is Jensen's inequality applied to the same weighted average that conservation of energy selected. The direction of heat transfer and the stability of its endpoint also follow from entropy maximization under thermal contact.
