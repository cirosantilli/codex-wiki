A relativistic theory must accommodate the energy-momentum relation E2=p2+m2, causal interactions and the possibility of particle creation. Promoting the squared relation to a wave equation gives the Klein-Gordon field equation
(∂t2​−∇2+m2)ϕ=0.
(8)
A plane wave ϕ=Ne−iEt+ip⋅x has
E=±Ep​,Ep​=p2+m2​.​
(9)
The positive and negative branches both solve the local differential equation. Its being second order in time means that specifying only a wavefunction, as in the nonrelativistic Schrödinger equation, is not the complete initial datum.
For a complex scalar, the conserved Klein-Gordon inner product comes from the current
jμ=i(ϕ∗∂μϕ−ϕ∂μϕ∗),∂μ​jμ=0.
(10)
For the plane wave, j0=2E∣N∣2, which is negative on the negative-frequency branch. Thus this conserved density cannot be a positive single-particle probability density for arbitrary Klein-Gordon solutions. Merely replacing it by ∣ϕ∣2 does not supply the missing conserved relativistic probability law. The indefinite Klein-Gordon charge density is an obstacle to that naive one-particle interpretation, not evidence that a consistent scalar field theory has negative probabilities.
The Dirac equation is first order,
i∂t​ψ=HD​ψ,HD​=α⋅p+βm.
(11)
The gamma-matrix anticommutation relations imply HD2​=(p2+m2)I, so its spectrum again contains ±Ep​. But its conserved Dirac current has density j0=ψ†ψ≥0. The Dirac equation solves the density-sign difficulty while leaving both energy branches. Treating every negative-energy solution as an ordinary accessible one-electron state would leave the energy unbounded below and permit radiative descent without a stable ground state.
The historical Dirac sea filled the negative-energy electron states and interpreted holes as positive-energy antiparticles. It motivates the Positron but is not the general modern explanation: bosons do not obey an exclusion principle and cannot be stabilized by filling a sea. Quantum field theory instead promotes the fields to operators and interprets negative-frequency modes using antiparticle creation operators.
For a complex scalar field, schematically,
ϕ​(x)=∫(2π)32Ep​​d3p​(ap​e−ipx+bp†​eipx).
(12)
Bosonic commutation relations give a normal-ordered Hamiltonian and conserved charge of the form
:H:=∫(2π)3d3p​Ep​(ap†​ap​+bp†​bp​),Q∝Na​−Nb​.
(13)
Both particle species have positive excitation energies. The sign-changing scalar current now represents charge, rather than particle probability; opposite charges are physically allowed. The ordinary positive Fock space norm gives nonnegative probabilities. A real scalar field is its own antiparticle and has no corresponding complex-field U(1) charge.
For the Dirac field, the analogous expansion contains electron annihilation operators and positron creation operators. Fermionic anticommutation relations turn the negative-frequency contribution into a positive antiparticle excitation energy after the vacuum constant is removed. The Pauli exclusion principle and relativistic locality are thereby incorporated in a field description, rather than a postulated infinite population of physical negative-energy electrons. The normal-ordering step removes an additive vacuum constant; it does not by itself settle the separate gravitational vacuum-energy problem.
Interactions can change particle number while conserving total energy, momentum and charge. Trying to localize a particle on its Compton wavelength scale requires momenta of order its rest mass, where accessible interactions can involve pair creation. A fixed-particle wavefunction is consequently not a general description of such processes. Creation and annihilation operators act between different particle-number sectors, and field interactions describe scattering and production within one framework.
Finally, relativistic causality concerns observables: spacelike separated local bosonic observables commute, while fermionic fields satisfy the appropriate anticommutation conditions. This microcausality prevents a controllable influence through spacelike separated local operations. Free relativistic one-particle theories remain useful, and low-energy fixed-number approximations can be excellent. What fails is the naive insistence that unrestricted relativistic interactions, positive probabilities, stability and arbitrary particle production all fit an unchanged one-particle Schrödinger picture.[ref]
