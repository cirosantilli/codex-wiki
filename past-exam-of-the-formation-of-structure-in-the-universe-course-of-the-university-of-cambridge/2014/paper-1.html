For a monatomic ideal gas, write the thermal energy density as u=3nkB​T/2. At low optical depth, radiative loss uses an astrophysical cooling function, often written C=ne​nH​Λ(T,Z), while H contains heating. At fixed density the net radiative gas cooling time is approximately u/(C−H) when the denominator is positive; a cooling flow closer to constant pressure must include enthalpy and compressional work. Comparing this time with the free-fall time of a uniform sphere determines whether pressure can be lost during gravitational contraction.
Hot ionized gas cools by bremsstrahlung and recombination; collisional excitation followed by photon escape supplies atomic and metal-line cooling at lower temperatures. molecular line cooling through rotational or vibrational lines and dust thermal emission become important in cold dense gas. Abundances, ionization state, density-dependent collisional de-excitation, and photon trapping all change the cooling function. Optically thick gas requires radiative transfer, not a simple local density-squared loss law.
Heating comes from photoionization, grain photoelectric heating driven by ultraviolet light, cosmic rays, shocks, turbulent dissipation and compression. photoionization heating deposits the photon energy above the ionization threshold as electron kinetic energy. A thermal equilibrium satisfies H=C; its stability depends on how the net loss changes under the relevant pressure or density constraint, not merely on equality. Different stable balances permit a multiphase interstellar medium. Spectral-line ratios constrain temperature and ionization, but interpreting them requires density diagnostics, interstellar extinction corrections and a radiation-field model.

A cold self-gravitating core initially contracts on a dynamical scale tff​∼(Gρ)−1/2 if cooling prevents pressure from halting collapse. Increasing optical depth eventually traps radiation and forms a pressure-supported first hydrostatic core. Dissociation of molecular hydrogen absorbs compressional energy, softens the equation of state and triggers a second protostellar collapse to a compact protostar. Rotation produces a disk; angular-momentum transport permits continuing accretion, while outflows remove some mass and angular momentum.
An accreting object's luminosity includes a contribution of order Lacc​∼GMM˙/R, with a coefficient set by where accretion energy is radiated or retained. After accretion subsides, gravitational contraction supplies internal luminosity over the Kelvin-Helmholtz cooling time tKH​∼GM2/(RLint​). Hydrostatic contraction heats the interior even while energy is radiated, as follows from the virial theorem and the negative heat capacity of a virialized gravitational system. accretion luminosity must not be substituted for Lint​ in the contraction time without an energy-budget justification.
Low-mass, nearly fully convective pre-main-sequence stars contract down a Hayashi track at roughly constant effective temperature and decreasing radius and luminosity. A developing radiative core can lead onto a Henyey track toward higher effective temperature; the route depends on mass and opacity. The deuterium-burning energy-generation rate can temporarily moderate contraction, but sustained core hydrogen burning marks arrival on the main sequence. Massive objects may begin hydrogen burning while still accreting, and substellar objects never establish long-lived hydrogen burning. An infrared spectral class reflects envelope and disk emission as well as inclination and interstellar extinction, so it is not a unique stellar age or interior evolutionary track.

Stellar radiation, stellar winds and supernovae return energy, momentum, mass and metals to surrounding gas. Heating, ionization, turbulent support and gas removal can reduce subsequent star formation; swept-up shells can also compress gas locally. Whether thermal energy drives an outflow depends on its radiative gas cooling time relative to expansion: radiative losses can remove energy while leaving substantial momentum transfer. Thus deposited energy alone does not specify the dynamical effect.
The galactic gas regulator has mass budget M˙g​=M˙in​−(1−R+η)Ψ, where R is the returned fraction and η=M˙out​/Ψ is the mass-loading factor. In a quasi-steady state, Ψ≃M˙in​/(1−R+η). This connects feedback to the available gas supply. A wind's kinetic power M˙out​vw2​/2 and momentum flux M˙out​vw​ must fit the source budget and gravitational binding cost. Gas that fails to escape may form a galactic fountain and later return; preferential metal loss changes chemical evolution.
Absorption against a galaxy's stellar continuum provides a concrete outflow test: compare interstellar sodium absorption with the systemic velocity and stellar absorption spectrum. A primary starburst survey found blueshifted interstellar absorption and an inclination dependence consistent with minor-axis outflows. Its saturated lines also illustrate why velocity detection is more direct than a unique column density or mass flux.[ref] Deprojection, covering fraction, ionization, radius and the unseen hot phase limit total outflow-rate estimates.
An accreting supermassive black hole adds radiation, winds and astrophysical jets. Coupling these to the interstellar or circumgalactic medium can suppress cooling or expel gas, especially in deep potentials where stellar driving alone may be inadequate. Jet cavities, line-emitting gas and multiphase outflows test different channels; coexisting activity and low star formation alone do not prove a causal feedback history.

Small primordial fluctuations seed later structure. For adiabatic cosmological perturbations on scales outside the horizon, the uniform-density curvature perturbation is conserved when the non-adiabatic pressure perturbation and the decaying mode can be neglected. A density contrast outside the horizon is gauge-dependent, so its apparent power-law growth should not be confused with directly measured local collapse.
For subhorizon, pressureless matter, the linearized continuity equation, Euler equation and Poisson equation combine into
δ¨+2Hδ˙−4πGρˉ​m​δ=0​.
(16)
Hubble expansion damps peculiar motion; self-gravity amplifies overdensities. During radiation domination, radiation pressure drives acoustic oscillations and cold dark matter growth after horizon entry is only logarithmic. During matter domination the growing and decaying solutions are δ∝a and δ∝a−3/2. Dark-energy domination later suppresses growth relative to the matter-era law.
For a pressure-supported fluid the Fourier-mode equation also contains +cs2​k2δ/a2, giving a Jeans length separating acoustic support from gravitational growth. Before recombination, baryons are tightly coupled to photons and oscillate rather than freely following dark matter. Silk damping from photon diffusion damps small-scale photon-baryon fluctuations; after decoupling, baryons can fall into dark-matter potential wells. free streaming smooths species-dependent scales and distinguishes cold from warm or hot dark matter.
Schematically, the linear matter power spectrum can be written Pδ​(k,a)=Akns​T2(k)D2(a) under the usual primordial-curvature normalization. The cosmological transfer function records horizon entry, matter-radiation equality, acoustic physics and damping; the linear growth factor records later amplification. density contrasts approaching unity require nonlinear collapse and halo formation beyond this approximation. cosmic microwave background anisotropies and galaxy clustering test the same initial perturbations through different transfer histories; galaxy bias, redshift-space distortions and observational selection enter the galaxy comparison.
