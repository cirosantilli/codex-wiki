gas heats when compression, shocks or absorbed radiation increase its internal energy. Important sources include accretion shocks, stellar winds, supernovae, photoionization, photoelectric emission from interstellar dust, cosmic rays and energy deposited by active galactic nuclei. photoionization heating uses the photoelectron energy above the ionization threshold; compression contributes mechanical work rather than photon absorption.
For specific internal energy u, an idealized thermal balance is
ρDtDu​=−P∇⋅v+H−C,C=ne​nH​Λ(T,Z).
(1)
Here H,C are powers per volume and Z describes abundances. The electron-hydrogen normalization of the cooling function must be specified: some conventions instead factor out nH2​. For a monatomic gas with total particle density n,
tcool​=2ne​nH​Λ3nkB​T​​
(2)
when heating is negligible. Compare this with the dynamical time: rapid cooling permits loss of pressure support, whereas long cooling times allow a hot atmosphere to persist.
collisional excitation followed by spontaneous emission cools partially ionized gas; radiative recombination also produces continuum cooling. metal lines enhance cooling over substantial intermediate-temperature ranges, while thermal bremsstrahlung gives a roughly Λ∝T1/2 high-temperature tail. Atomic hydrogen cooling becomes inefficient below roughly 104K, where molecular line cooling and interstellar dust can be important. inverse Compton scattering cools hot electrons against a colder radiation field. A tabulated cooling curve assumes a composition and ionization model; photoionization or nonequilibrium ionization can change it.[ref]
X-ray line ratios and continuum shape constrain temperature and ionization, while the emission measure constrains ∫ne​nH​dV. Turning that into a density and radiative gas cooling time also requires a geometry; unresolved clumping, foreground absorption and multiphase gas are important controls.

galactic feedback couples star formation and black-hole accretion back to the gas supply. Stellar radiation, winds, supernovae and cosmic rays inject energy and momentum; active-galactic-nucleus feedback adds radiation, jets and winds powered by accretion. Heating, turbulence or gas ejection can suppress later star formation, while compression can sometimes promote it. Negative feedback permits self-regulation rather than unrestricted gas consumption.
Define the mass-loading factor η=M˙out​/M˙∗​. If the launch speed scales with the escape speed and the injected energy per stellar mass is fixed, conservation of energy suggests η∝vesc−2​; fixed injected momentum instead suggests η∝vesc−1​. Coupling efficiencies, radiative losses and recycling change these ideal scalings. Thermal energy injected into dense gas can radiate away before driving an outflow, so an energy budget alone does not demonstrate effective feedback.
Radio-filled X-ray cavities provide a mechanical diagnostic. For a cavity containing a relativistic fluid with γ=4/3, its enthalpy is γPV/(γ−1)≃4PV; dividing by an estimated age gives a mechanical-power estimate. Chandra X-ray Observatory observations of the Perseus cluster revealed fronts and ripples associated with radio bubbles, interpreted as weak shocks and pressure waves that can transport jet energy into the cooling atmosphere.[ref]
The test requires ambient density and temperature estimates, radio/X-ray spatial comparison, projected cavity volumes and an age model. Brightness edges must be distinguished from absorption and cool rims; wave energy is not automatically thermalized locally. These controls determine whether inferred mechanical heating can actually offset radiative cooling.

cold dark matter is nonrelativistic and effectively collisionless on the scales of interest. Its small velocity dispersion limits free streaming, allowing small-scale gravitational wells to survive. baryons coupled to photons undergo acoustic motion and damping before recombination; dark matter does not share that photon pressure, and provides potential wells into which baryons can later fall.
For a subhorizon pressureless perturbation, once a Newtonian treatment is applicable,
δ¨+2Hδ˙−4πGρˉ​m​δ=0.
(6)
During radiation domination the matter contribution to the background gravity is small; neglecting it gives δ≃C1​+C2​logt. This slow subhorizon growth is the Mészáros effect. During matter domination,
δ=C+​a+C−​a−3/2​
(7)
in an Einstein-de Sitter universe. Once δ approaches unity, linear theory fails and collapse, virialization and merging become important.
Modes entering the horizon during radiation domination therefore grow less before equality than longer modes entering later. This scale dependence is encoded in a transfer function, with a schematic linear matter power spectrum P(k,a)=Akns​T2(k)D2(a). cold dark matter supports hierarchical assembly; a sufficiently large warm- or hot-dark-matter free-streaming length suppresses small-scale structure. Superhorizon density contrasts change under a gauge transformation in cosmological perturbation theory, so the subhorizon growth laws should not be applied indiscriminately outside the horizon. The original cold-dark-matter formation model explicitly connects weak free streaming to the spectrum that seeds galaxies and clusters.[ref]
