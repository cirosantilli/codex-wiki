galactic feedback couples star formation and black-hole accretion back to the gas supply. Stellar radiation, winds, supernovae and cosmic rays inject energy and momentum; active-galactic-nucleus feedback adds radiation, jets and winds powered by accretion. Heating, turbulence or gas ejection can suppress later star formation, while compression can sometimes promote it. Negative feedback permits self-regulation rather than unrestricted gas consumption.
Define the mass-loading factor η=M˙out​/M˙∗​. If the launch speed scales with the escape speed and the injected energy per stellar mass is fixed, conservation of energy suggests η∝vesc−2​; fixed injected momentum instead suggests η∝vesc−1​. Coupling efficiencies, radiative losses and recycling change these ideal scalings. Thermal energy injected into dense gas can radiate away before driving an outflow, so an energy budget alone does not demonstrate effective feedback.
Radio-filled X-ray cavities provide a mechanical diagnostic. For a cavity containing a relativistic fluid with γ=4/3, its enthalpy is γPV/(γ−1)≃4PV; dividing by an estimated age gives a mechanical-power estimate. Chandra X-ray Observatory observations of the Perseus cluster revealed fronts and ripples associated with radio bubbles, interpreted as weak shocks and pressure waves that can transport jet energy into the cooling atmosphere.[ref]
The test requires ambient density and temperature estimates, radio/X-ray spatial comparison, projected cavity volumes and an age model. Brightness edges must be distinguished from absorption and cool rims; wave energy is not automatically thermalized locally. These controls determine whether inferred mechanical heating can actually offset radiative cooling.
