heating and cooling balance in the interstellar medium depends on density, ionization, radiation and shielding. Write net cooling per volume as C=n2Λ(T)−nΓ. Ultraviolet grain photoelectric heating is important in diffuse neutral gas; photoionization heats ionized regions, cosmic rays penetrate shielded gas, and shock waves from stellar winds and supernovae supply hot gas. Cooling includes collisionally excited atomic lines, fine-structure metal lines at lower temperatures, molecular lines in shielded clouds, and continuum radiation and dust emission where appropriate. The heating efficiency and cooling abundances matter as much as a nominal temperature.
A multiphase interstellar medium can contain a cold neutral medium near 102 K, a warm neutral medium and warm ionized medium near 104 K, a hot ionized medium near 106 K, and still colder molecular clouds. Cold and warm atomic equilibrium branches can coexist at similar thermal pressure over a finite pressure interval; this is a calculated heating/cooling balance, not a requirement that all galactic gas have the same pressure.[ref]
For net cooling per unit mass L=C/ρ, an isobaric perturbation at equilibrium obeys cp​∂t​δT=−(∂L/∂T)p​δT. Thus a negative constant-pressure cooling derivative is thermally unstable: a cool perturbation cools further and condenses. Sound crossing must be fast enough to maintain pressure balance; thermal conduction suppresses sufficiently short wavelengths. Supernova-driven motions, magnetic pressure, finite cooling times and interfaces make the actual medium dynamic, so equilibrium branches describe useful phases rather than a static collection of perfectly separated components.

Collective self-gravity, orbital resonances and shear can sustain or repeatedly amplify non-axisymmetric patterns; a visible spiral need not be a single permanent rigid material structure.

Galactic star formation is limited by how much gas becomes cold, shielded and self-gravitating, and by how long it remains in that state. A useful local estimate is
ρ˙​∗​≃ϵff​tff​ρsf​​,tff​=32Gρsf​3π​​.
(3)
The star-formation efficiency per free-fall time ϵff​ absorbs the fact that turbulence, magnetic support and stellar feedback prevent all eligible gas from collapsing in one free-fall time of a uniform sphere. Rotation and disk stability influence the supply of dense clouds, while gravitational collapse and gravitational fragmentation operate inside them.
For galaxy-averaged measurements, the Kennicutt–Schmidt law has ΣSFR​∝ΣgN​, with the classic mixed spiral/starburst sample giving N≃1.4. This empirical relation was obtained using H-alpha and far-infrared star-formation tracers together with atomic-hydrogen and CO gas measurements.[ref] Dust attenuation, contamination by older stellar heating, the stellar initial mass function and the CO-to-molecular-gas conversion must be controlled. A disk-averaged total-gas exponent is not automatically a small-scale cloud law.
Young stars then photoionize gas, drive winds and eventually inject supernova energy and momentum. These processes can disrupt clouds, sustain random motions or expel gas; radiative cooling and fresh inflow rebuild the cold supply. Large-scale star formation is a regulated conversion of gas, rather than unimpeded free fall of the entire disk. A long gas-depletion time Σg​/ΣSFR​ is compatible with short galactic dynamical times in the dense fraction.

A dense cloud core first collapses when self-gravity overwhelms its effective support. While radiation escapes readily, compression can remain approximately isothermal and the Jeans mass decreases as density rises, encouraging gravitational fragmentation. Angular momentum usually leads to a disk rather than unrestricted spherical infall.
As the central optical depth increases, cooling becomes slower than compression and pressure produces a first hydrostatic core. molecular hydrogen dissociation near 2000 K absorbs compressional energy and lowers the effective adiabatic response, permitting a second collapse of a protostar. After dissociation the gas stiffens and a much more compact stellar core forms. An accretion shock and surrounding disk then convert infall energy into radiation. A small central core forms before most of the envelope arrives, as demonstrated in radiation-hydrodynamic collapse calculations.[ref]
The isothermal inside-out collapse of a singular isothermal sphere model provides one idealized supply law, M˙∼aT3​/G, but rotation, magnetic fields and an unsteady reservoir modify real accretion. Its luminosity scale is Lacc​∼GMM˙/r∗​. Dust can absorb the emerging short-wavelength radiation and re-emit it in the infrared, so an embedded source need not have a directly visible stellar photosphere.
As envelope accretion declines, gravitational contraction becomes important on the Kelvin-Helmholtz cooling time tKH​∼GM2/(r∗​L). deuterium-burning energy-generation rate can temporarily influence the radius; sustained core hydrogen burning eventually establishes the main sequence if the final mass is sufficient. A low-mass convective object approaches along a Hayashi-like track, while more massive objects need not share that track. accretion, contraction and nuclear burning are distinct energy sources, and a protostar is not generally a fixed-mass object.

Growing and decaying modes. Neglect pressure and insert the Einstein-de Sitter background. A trial Δ∝tp gives

In a static homogeneous Jeans model, Δ¨=(4πGρˉ​−k2cs2​)Δ, so an unstable mode grows exponentially, e4πGρˉ​−k2cs2​​t. Expansion introduces the 2HΔ˙ damping term and a falling background density, yielding power-law growth instead. If the wavevector in the supplied pressure term is comoving, that term would need R−2; the pressureless result does not depend on this convention.
Amplitude problem. Using the stated recombination epoch, matter-era growth from then until today is only about 1501. A baryonic density perturbation of order 10−5 would reach merely 1.5×10−2, far below the order-unity regime needed for nonlinear collapse. photon coupling also prevents baryons from freely collapsing before recombination. The observed microwave temperature contrast is not an exact direct measurement of baryon density contrast at every scale: acoustic transfer and gravitational redshift matter. The estimate expresses the insufficient growth of tiny photon-coupled baryonic seeds, not an exact conversion between the two observables.

Physical consequence. cold dark matter is not held to the radiation by Thomson scattering, and can build gravitational wells before baryon decoupling, with appreciable matter-era growth after equality. Its growing perturbations then attract baryons once pressure and radiation drag become unimportant. The relative offset stays constant or decays while the common mode grows, so baryons catch up. Nonlinear structure need not grow solely from the tiny baryonic perturbation inferred at decoupling. cold dark matter growth during radiation domination is slower than the matter-era law, and the stated formula is neither a radiation-era nor a nonlinear-collapse solution.
