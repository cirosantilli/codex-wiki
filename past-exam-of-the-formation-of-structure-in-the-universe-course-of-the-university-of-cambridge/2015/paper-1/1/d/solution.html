In a matter-dominated Einstein-de Sitter universe, the growing linear density contrast is proportional to the scale factor. When ∣δ∣∼1, linear evolution no longer describes collapse. An overdense region initially expands more slowly than the background, reaches cosmological turnaround, and then contracts under self-gravity.
The spherical top-hat collapse model supplies a useful idealization: pressureless shells obey r¨=−GM/r2. At formal collapse, the linearly extrapolated growing mode is
δc​=53​(23π​)2/3≃1.686.​
(4)
This is a linear extrapolation, not the true finite nonlinear density at that instant. In the ideal energy-conserving spherical model, virial equilibrium gives a virial radius about half the turnaround radius; its mean density at the collapse time is 18π2≃178 times the cosmic matter density.
Real perturbations are nonspherical: tides and anisotropic collapse produce sheets, filaments and haloes. Collisionless dark matter develops shell crossing and phase mixing rather than radiatively losing its orbital energy. Baryonic gas can shock, cool and collect within the resulting potential wells. Hierarchical merging then assembles larger systems where small-scale fluctuations have sufficient initial power. Press-Schechter formalism connects a collapse threshold with the statistics of the smoothed initial field, but halo abundances and internal structure need more than the single spherical model. These ideas apply after perturbations become nonlinear; one must not extend pressureless subhorizon equations through all radiation-era or horizon-scale conditions.[ref]
