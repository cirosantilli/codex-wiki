A spiral density wave is an organized density and gravitational-potential perturbation through which stars and gas can move; it is not generally a permanently labelled set of stars. Differential rotation winds a material arm rapidly. In a wave description, a perturbation has phase mϕ+∫kdr−ωt, with pattern speed Ωp​=ω/m distinct from the local stellar angular speed.
For a thin fluid disk the local tightly wound dispersion relation is
(ω−mΩ)2=κ2+cs2​k2−2πGΣ∣k∣.
(1)
Rotation and pressure resist self-gravity. A collisionless stellar disk requires its stellar response function instead of simply replacing cs​ by a dispersion; the familiar local axisymmetric stability parameter is Q⋆​=σR​κ/(3.36GΣ). This is distinct from the gas Qg​=cs​κ/(πGΣ).
Waves exchange angular momentum at Lindblad resonances and near corotation radius. Swing amplification can strengthen a leading disturbance as shear turns it into a trailing one. Bars or companions can force a coherent spiral; recurrent self-excited disturbances also occur. Thus a single indefinitely steady wave is one model, not a description established for every spiral galaxy. Gas compression and shocks along arms can organize star formation, while gravitational torques redistribute angular momentum. Arm brightness, recent star formation and stellar mass density need not trace precisely the same pattern.[ref]

Supermassive black holes are compact galactic nuclei with masses typically of millions to billions of solar masses. Their gravitational influence is measured from the motion of surrounding matter; accretion luminosity is evidence of feeding, not a necessary property of every black hole. Keplerian stellar orbits near the Galactic Centre and molecular-maser rotation curves in other nuclei provide particularly direct compact-mass constraints. A rough black-hole sphere of influence is rinfl​∼GM∙​/σ⋆2​; resolving it helps dynamical mass estimates.
Accretion converts binding energy into radiation,
L=ηM˙in​c2,LE​=κes​4πGM∙​c​.​
(2)
The latter is the Eddington luminosity for isotropic radiation opposed by electron-scattering opacity, not an absolute luminosity ceiling for every geometry. The retained black-hole mass grows at (1−η)M˙in​. Sustained feeding can produce an active galactic nucleus, whereas gas-poor nuclei may be faint. Loss of gas angular momentum is a central feeding problem.
Black-hole mass correlates with classical-bulge stellar velocity dispersion through the M-sigma relation. Accretion-powered radiation, winds and jets can heat or remove gas, linking nuclear growth to galaxy evolution. Such correlations do not establish one universal feedback history, and disks and pseudobulges do not follow the same tight relations as classical bulges. Seed formation, accretion and mergers all contribute; seed masses and feeding histories remain model dependent.[ref]

Galactic star formation requires gas to collect into cold dense regions that can become self-gravitating. Disk-scale gravity, turbulent motions, shear, pressure and magnetic fields affect this collection; formation then proceeds within molecular clouds. A useful local parametrization is
ρ˙​⋆​=ϵff​tff​ρ​,tff​=32Gρ3π​​,
(3)
where the star-formation efficiency per free-fall time summarizes inefficient conversion rather than requiring every cloud to collapse at once.
Across galactic disks the empirical Kennicutt–Schmidt law relates star formation rate surface density to gas surface density, ΣSFR​∝ΣgN​. A slope near 1.4 is a familiar integrated total-gas fit, while molecular-gas relations in ordinary disks can be closer to linear; the slope and normalization depend on tracer, averaging scale and environment. The gas-depletion time tdep​=Mg​/Ψ is the corresponding fuel-consumption scale, not a prediction that inflow and recycling cease.
Massive stars supply ionizing radiation, winds and supernova energy and momentum. This stellar feedback can disperse clouds, sustain turbulence and drive outflows, regulating rather than simply extinguishing all future star formation. Fresh accretion and recycled gas replenish the reservoir. UV and recombination-line emission trace young stars, while infrared emission traces absorbed and re-radiated light; extinction, stellar populations and the assumed initial mass function affect inferred rates. A galactic rate reflects supply, cloud formation and feedback together, rather than only the free-fall time of its densest gas.[ref]

In a matter-dominated Einstein-de Sitter universe, the growing linear density contrast is proportional to the scale factor. When ∣δ∣∼1, linear evolution no longer describes collapse. An overdense region initially expands more slowly than the background, reaches cosmological turnaround, and then contracts under self-gravity.
The spherical top-hat collapse model supplies a useful idealization: pressureless shells obey r¨=−GM/r2. At formal collapse, the linearly extrapolated growing mode is
δc​=53​(23π​)2/3≃1.686.​
(4)
This is a linear extrapolation, not the true finite nonlinear density at that instant. In the ideal energy-conserving spherical model, virial equilibrium gives a virial radius about half the turnaround radius; its mean density at the collapse time is 18π2≃178 times the cosmic matter density.
Real perturbations are nonspherical: tides and anisotropic collapse produce sheets, filaments and haloes. Collisionless dark matter develops shell crossing and phase mixing rather than radiatively losing its orbital energy. Baryonic gas can shock, cool and collect within the resulting potential wells. Hierarchical merging then assembles larger systems where small-scale fluctuations have sufficient initial power. Press-Schechter formalism connects a collapse threshold with the statistics of the smoothed initial field, but halo abundances and internal structure need more than the single spherical model. These ideas apply after perturbations become nonlinear; one must not extend pressureless subhorizon equations through all radiation-era or horizon-scale conditions.[ref]

With constant isothermal sound speed aT​, the equation of state is p=aT2​ρ. Spherical hydrostatic equilibrium and mass continuity require
aT2​drdρ​=−ρr2GM(r)​,drdM​=4πr2ρ.
(5)
Try ρ=A/r2. With no central point mass, M(r)=4πAr, and hydrostatic balance becomes 2aT2​A=4πGA2. The positive-density branch is therefore
A=2πGaT2​​,ρ=2πGr2aT2​​,M(r)=G2aT2​r​.​
(6)
The constant external pressure fixes the truncation radius rs​ through p(rs​)=p0​, giving
rs​=2πGp0​​aT2​​,Ms​=G2aT2​rs​​.​
(7)
This is the pressure-truncated singular isothermal gas sphere, one hydrostatic solution, not a claim that every isothermal cloud has a singular centre. Its central density diverges, but its enclosed mass tends to zero as r→0.

For inside-out collapse of a singular isothermal sphere, the expansion front is at rf​=aT​t. The original mass swept by this front is
Mswept​=G2aT2​rf​​=G2aT3​t​.
(8)
Thus the only time-independent accretion scale is aT3​/G. The front-sweeping estimate has coefficient two, but gas takes time to fall from the front to the object; it is not an exact central accretion coefficient. Write
M˙⋆​=m0​GaT3​​∝(μmH​kB​T​)3/2∝T3/2,​
(9)
for fixed mean particle mass μmH​. Determining the dimensionless coefficient m0​ requires the collapse solution; it is not fixed by front propagation alone and is not needed for the requested temperature scaling.[ref]
Well inside the front, where the point mass dominates and infall is approximately pressureless and locally steady, ∣vr​∣≃2GM⋆​/r​. Conserving the radial mass flux gives the free-fall density profile around a growing point mass:
ρ(r,t)=4πr2∣vr​∣M˙⋆​​≃4π2GM⋆​(t)​M˙⋆​​r−3/2.​
(10)
The inner slope is −3/2, versus −2 in the undisturbed outer cloud. For constant M˙⋆​ and negligible initial object mass, M⋆​∝t and the inner normalization varies as t−1/2. These are inner asymptotes, not an assertion of an abrupt exact power-law join at the front.

The dust-cooled equilibrium is thermally stable under the stated constant irradiation, with fixed dust properties and dust-to-gas ratio. At a fixed position, absorbed power per grain is independent of its temperature, while emission is 4πa2σSB​T4. A positive temperature perturbation increases cooling, causing a restoring energy loss; a negative perturbation reduces cooling, allowing reheating.
For heat capacity C>0 of a grain together with its thermally coupled gas,
CδT˙=−16πa2σSB​T03​δT,
(13)
so the perturbation decays. The thermal stability of externally heated blackbody dust relies on remaining below sublimation with the grain population intact; destruction, variable illumination or finite dust-gas equilibration can change the response. An isobaric parcel with a fixed grain-to-gas ratio has the same restoring sign when heating and cooling are compared per particle.

First specify the perturbation: take fixed gas density, fixed ionic abundance and the same temperature-independent heating, so thermal stability requires dC/dT>0 at an equilibrium. Let θ=ΔE/kB​, ne​ be the collider density and nA​=n1​+n2​ the atomic density. A two-level optically thin balance is
n1​ne​q12​=n2​(A21​+ne​q21​),C=n2​A21​ΔE.
(14)
The critical density of an atomic transition is approximately A21​/q21​. At low density, each rare collisional excitation is followed by an escaping photon, so
C≃nA​ne​q12​ΔE∝T−1/2e−θ/T,dlnTdlnC​=−21​+Tθ​.
(15)
The isochoric thermal stability of a collisionally excited line therefore gives
T<2θ: stable;T>2θ: unstable.​
(16)
At T=2θ the linear restoring coefficient vanishes; the cooling maximum is marginal to first order and is not robust two-sided stability.
At high density, collisional de-excitation competes successfully with radiative decay, and collisions establish local thermodynamic equilibrium level populations. Detailed balance gives n2​/n1​=ge−θ/T, where g=g2​/g1​. The cooling is then
C=nA​A21​ΔEfu​(T),fu​=1+ge−θ/Tge−θ/T​,dlnTdlnfu​​=Tθ​(1−fu​)>0.
(17)
Thus the high-density fixed-density branch is stable for finite T, becoming weakly restoring as the upper fraction saturates at high temperature. It is incorrect to retain the low-density factor T−1/2 in the radiative cooling law once populations are thermalized.[ref]
The isobaric thermal stability of a two-level coolant is a distinct question. If the intended heating is a fixed rate per particle, then n∝T−1 and stability tests the cooling per particle. The low-density boundary becomes T<2θ/3; the high-density cooling per particle remains proportional to fu​, so remains stable. If instead heating is constant per volume, the isobaric low-density boundary is T<2θ/5, and the high-density stability condition is θ(1−fu​)/T>1. The source gives no pressure constraint or explicit heating normalization: the boxed 2θ result is the fixed-density answer, with these conditional alternatives stated to avoid asserting a universal boundary.

For constant inflow Φ and efficiency ϵ>0, with no recycling or outflow, mass conservation gives the constant-inflow gas regulator solution:
M˙g​=Φ−ϵMg​,Mg​(t)=ϵΦ​+(M0​−ϵΦ​)e−ϵt.​
(18)
The equilibrium Mg,eq​=Φ/ϵ is stable: a gas-mass perturbation obeys δM˙g​=−ϵδMg​. At late times the star formation rate approaches Ψ=Φ, while the gas reservoir approaches a constant.
Integrating M˙⋆​=ϵMg​ with M⋆​(0)=0 gives the stellar mass in a constant-inflow gas regulator:
M⋆​(t)=Φt+(M0​−ϵΦ​)(1−e−ϵt).​
(19)
It also follows immediately from Mg​+M⋆​=M0​+Φt. The gas mass equilibrates; the stellar mass generally keeps growing. With Φ=0, these reduce to exponential gas depletion and M⋆​=M0​(1−e−ϵt).
A constant inflow is implicit in the requested steady asymptote, not automatic for an arbitrary prescribed rate. For time-dependent Φ(t) the exact integrating-factor result is
Mg​(t)=M0​e−ϵt+∫0t​e−ϵ(t−s)Φ(s)ds;
(20)
a constant late-time gas mass then requires a corresponding asymptotically steady supply. Both printed mark groups remain in this single unlettered continuation of part(a).

Let D be the separation of galaxy centres and x a small displacement from the satellite centre along the line of centres. Expanding the central point-mass field gives the radial differential acceleration
δgtide​≃D32GM​x.
(21)
Inside a uniform-density satellite, the restoring self-gravity is
gself​(x)=x2G(4πρx3/3)​=34πGρ​x.
(22)
Comparing the two at the same displacement yields the differential-gravity disruption limit of a uniform satellite:
Dmin​≃(2πρ3M​)1/3∝(ρM​)1/3.​
(23)
The satellite radius cancels because both accelerations are linear in x. This is the instantaneous tidal-gravity estimate requested by the given criterion, not a full rotating Roche calculation. Including centrifugal terms for a circular corotating orbit replaces the radial coefficient two by three, giving Dmin​≃[9M/(4πρ)]1/3 while preserving the required scaling. An eccentric orbit requires checking the stronger tide near periapsis, and extended density profiles require a more detailed restoring-force calculation.

Put H=2/(3t) and let fB​=ρB​/ρˉ​, fD​=1−fB​ be constant background matter fractions. Define the total matter perturbation S=fB​ΔB​+fD​ΔD​ and the relative mode D=ΔD​−ΔB​. Adding with the background weights and subtracting the two given equations respectively gives
S¨+3t4​S˙−3t22​S=0,D¨+3t4​D˙=0.
(24)
The total and relative density modes of two pressureless matter fluids are consequently
S=At2/3+Bt−1,D=C+Et−1/3,ΔB​=S−fD​D,ΔD​=S+fB​D.​
(25)
These are the general consequences of the supplied equations; a single ratio additionally needs initial-mode assumptions.
For the usual dark-matter-dominated growing-mode approximation, take fB​≪1, neglect the decaying modes, and set the relative-velocity mode E=0. Then ΔD​∝t2/3∝R. With ΔB​(tr​)=0 at recombination, C=ΔD​(tr​), so the baryon catch-up approximation after recombination is
ΔB​(t)=ΔD​(t)[1−R(t)R(tr​)​]=ΔD​(t)[1−1+z0​1+z​].​
(26)
At redshifts z,z0​≫1, replacing 1+z by z gives the printed ΔB​≃ΔD​(1−z/z0​). The printed relation is a high-redshift approximation with a specified relative mode, not an exact identity for arbitrary solutions of the two-fluid equations.
For example, retaining a finite baryon fraction with a growing total mode and constant relative mode gives, with x=R/R(tr​),
ΔD​ΔB​​=fD​x+fB​fD​(x−1)​.
(27)
Alternatively, if initially both ΔB​=0 and Δ˙B​=0 while the dark-matter growing mode is already present, then in the dark-matter-dominated limit the relative transient is not zero and
ΔD​ΔB​​=1−3x−1+2x−3/2.
(28)
These different solutions show why the initial-velocity convention matters. The quoted catch-up law is the dark-matter-dominated branch with the relative decaying mode neglected, together with the high-redshift approximation stated above. The general solution retains the transient and a finite baryon fraction, so the additional assumptions needed for that special case remain explicit.
The physical conclusion is robust: after decoupling, baryonic gas can fall into pre-existing dark-matter potential wells and its fractional density perturbation catches up. The absolute constant offset need not vanish; it becomes small relative to the growing common mode. The pressureless approximation applies on scales above the baryonic Jeans scale and while perturbations remain linear; subsequent collapse requires nonlinear treatment.[ref]
