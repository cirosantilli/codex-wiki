For the standard definition of algebraic independence, finite tuples are required to have distinct entries. Let P be the set of all subsets of L with algebraic independence over K, ordered by inclusion. It is nonempty because ∅∈P.
If C is a chain in P, put U=⋃C. A finite tuple of distinct elements of U lies in one member of C: choose a member containing each element and take the largest of those finitely many members. A polynomial relation on that tuple would then be a polynomial relation in that member, contradicting its algebraic independence. Thus U∈P and is an upper bound. For the empty chain, ∅ is an upper bound. The Zorn's lemma now gives a maximal member B, which is a transcendence basis by the specified maximality criterion. To also recover the usual field-theoretic definition, if a∈L were transcendental over K(B), clearing denominators in a polynomial relation would show that B∪{a} still has algebraic independence over K, contradicting maximality. Thus L is algebraic over K(B). Hence every field extension has a transcendence basis.
There is a literal issue in the printed definition: it does not require the tuple entries to be distinct. With that wording, any nonempty X fails the test: for a∈X, the nonzero polynomial X1​−X2​ vanishes on (a,a), in every characteristic. Thus under the literal printed test the family is exactly {∅}, and ∅ is its maximal member. The same chain-and-Zorn's lemma argument still proves existence for that literal notion, but it is not the usual transcendence basis notion. The conventional result above uses distinct tuple entries.
