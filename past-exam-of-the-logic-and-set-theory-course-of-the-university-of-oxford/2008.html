Start with the propositional variables as atomic symbols and close under the two formation operations
pi​∈Form(L0​),ψ∈Form(L0​)⟹¬ψ∈Form(L0​),ψ,χ∈Form(L0​)⟹(ψ→χ)∈Form(L0​).
(1)
The set of propositional formulas is the smallest set satisfying these conditions: every logical formula has a finite construction from logical variables by negation and logical implication, and no other strings qualify. In particular, parentheses belong to the binary logical implication formation rule; they cannot be added or removed arbitrarily. Each symbol pi​ is one token, regardless of how its index is printed.

Use structural induction on the formation of a propositional formula. A propositional variable has no parentheses. Applying negation to a logical formula adds no parentheses, so preserves equality of the two counts. If ψ and χ each have equally many opening and closing parentheses, the formation of (ψ→χ) adds one of each to their combined counts. Thus every propositional formula satisfies
# opening parentheses=# closing parentheses.​
(2)
This is a necessary condition for being a logical formula; it is not sufficient, since the logical connectives and the placement of symbols must also follow the formation rules.

The unique readability theorem says that every propositional formula has exactly one of the following forms: a unique propositional variable; ¬ψ for a unique logical formula ψ; or (ψ→χ) for a unique ordered pair of logical formulas (ψ,χ). ii OurBigBook.com
A useful consequence of the formation rules is that logical formulas form a prefix-free set of strings. To see this, describe a deterministic parser that reads one logical formula from its starting position. On a logical variable it consumes that one symbol. On a negation symbol it recursively reads one following logical formula. On an opening parenthesis it recursively reads a left logical formula, requires a logical implication symbol, recursively reads a right logical formula, and requires the closing parenthesis. Induction on formation proves that, if a valid logical formula begins at the current position, this procedure consumes exactly that logical formula and stops at its end.
Suppose now that both α and ϕ are logical formulas and ϕ=αϵ. The same parser, started at their common first symbol, must stop both at the end of α and at the end of ϕ. Its deterministic stopping position cannot differ, so ϵ is empty. Hence
α=ϕ.​
(4)
This argument applies to symbol strings; it does not use truth values or logical equivalence.

First note that a substring which is itself a logical formula must coincide with a subtree of the formula syntax tree of the surrounding logical formula. Indeed, a logical formula can begin only at a logical variable, a negation symbol or an opening parenthesis. Every such position in a valid logical formula is the start of one of its parsed subtrees. If β is the logical formula at that subtree and α is another logical formula starting at the same position, one is an initial segment of the other; prefix-freeness of fully parenthesized formulas from the preceding part forces α=β.
Now consider a final subformula. For a logical variable, it can only be the whole logical formula. For a binary logical implication (ψ→χ), every proper subtree is contained in ψ or χ and finishes before the final closing parenthesis of the whole expression. It therefore cannot be a final substring of the whole. The only remaining possible outer formation is negation. Thus
α=ϕorϕ=¬ψ for some formula ψ.​
(5)
The second alternative is a necessary possibility, not a claim that every negated logical formula has every one of its subformulas as a suffix.

The whole logical formula ¬ψ is a subformula of itself, and any subformula of ψ remains a contiguous substring after placing a negation symbol in front of ψ.
Conversely, a subformula occurrence starting at the first symbol of ¬ψ is an initial subformula, so equals the whole logical formula by prefix-freeness of fully parenthesized formulas. An occurrence starting later lies wholly inside the following string ψ, and is therefore a subformula of ψ. Consequently
Sub(¬ψ)={¬ψ}∪Sub(ψ).​
(6)
Here Sub records distinct logical formulas; multiple occurrences of the same logical formula do not create different elements of this set.

A subformula beginning at the first opening parenthesis equals the whole logical formula by prefix-freeness of fully parenthesized formulas. Every other subformula is the logical formula of a proper syntax subtree, by the parsing argument in part (iii). The two children of the root are precisely ψ and χ, so such a subtree lies entirely inside one of those children. In particular no logical formula substring can begin in ψ and cross the principal logical implication symbol into χ.
Conversely, each subformula of either child is visibly a substring of the whole expression, as is the whole expression itself. Thus
Sub((ψ→χ))={(ψ→χ)}∪Sub(ψ)∪Sub(χ).​
(7)
Together these cases identify the substring definition of a subformula with its usual syntax-tree definition.

Fix the particular occurrence of α that is being replaced. The subformula analysis above makes it a node of the formula syntax tree of ϕ. Proceed by structural induction on the surrounding logical formula.
If this node is the root, the subformula replacement gives β, already a logical formula. Otherwise, if ϕ=¬ψ, the chosen occurrence lies inside ψ. The induction hypothesis gives a logical formula ψ′ after the subformula replacement there, and the whole result is the logical formula ¬ψ′. If ϕ=(ψ→χ), a proper chosen occurrence lies in exactly one of the two children. Replacing inside that child gives either (ψ′→χ) or (ψ→χ′), each a logical formula by the formation rule. A logical variable has no proper subformula, so there is no additional base case.
Therefore replacing the designated subformula occurrence by any logical formula preserves well-formedness. The argument concerns one chosen occurrence; it does not require simultaneous subformula replacement of every occurrence of the same string.

Fix any Boolean valuation v. Logical equivalence of α and β means v(α)=v(β) for this Boolean valuation and every other one. Follow the unique path from the replaced node to the root of the formula syntax tree. At a negation node, equal input truth values have equal negations. At a logical implication node, the unaffected child has the same truth value before and after subformula replacement, while the affected child has the same truth value by induction; the resulting logical implications therefore agree as well.
Induction along that finite path yields v(ϕ)=v(ϕ′). Since v was arbitrary,
ϕ≡ϕ′.​
(8)
This is the replacement theorem for logical equivalence: the semantics of each logical connective depends only on the truth values of its immediate inputs.

A collection of logical connectives is functionally complete, or adequate, if every Boolean function of finitely many propositional variables is represented by a logical formula using only those logical connectives. More explicitly, for every n≥1 and every F:{0,1}n→{0,1} there is a logical formula θ(p1​,…,pn​) whose truth value under each Boolean valuation is F(v(p1​),…,v(pn​)). Constant functions with Boolean values may use an otherwise irrelevant logical variable, for example p→p for truth and ¬(p→p) for falsity.

Translate logical formulas recursively, leaving propositional variables unchanged and translating negation by applying negation to the translation. For the other two logical connectives set
T(ψ∨χ)=(¬T(ψ)→T(χ)),T(ψ∧χ)=¬(T(ψ)→¬T(χ)).
(9)
The first logical implication is false exactly when both original inputs are false; the second displayed logical formula is true exactly when both inputs are true. Hence each clause preserves truth values. Structural induction gives v(T(θ))=v(θ) for every Boolean valuation and every logical formula θ in the larger language.
By the assumed functional completeness of negation, logical conjunction and logical disjunction, any finite Boolean function has such a logical formula θ. Its translation T(θ) uses only negation and logical implication and represents the same function. Thus {¬,→} is adequate.

Yes, for soundness and completeness of theoremhood. An axiom-only complete propositional calculus is obtained as follows. Declare every propositional tautology, and only such a tautology, to be an axiom; supply no inference rules. A formal proof then consists of a single axiom. Every proved logical formula is valid, giving soundness, and every valid logical formula is already an axiom, giving completeness.
This is not merely an unspecified collection: whether a finite propositional formula is a tautology is decidable by its finite truth table, so the axiom set can be recognized effectively. It is a deliberately uninformative Hilbert proof system, since all the work has been put into axiom recognition. If one instead asked for strong completeness of derivations from arbitrary additional assumptions, a system whose only formal proof lines are axioms or assumptions would not suffice; the affirmative construction here concerns ⊢ϕ versus ⊨ϕ.

An L-structure consists of a nonempty domain A, an element cA∈A for each constant symbol, a function fA:An→A for each n-ary function symbol, and a relation RA⊆An for each n-ary predicate symbol. When equality is part of the logic it is interpreted as actual identity on A.
An assignment in a first-order structure is a function s from logical variables to A. It induces a term valuation of first-order terms recursively:
xA[s]=s(x),cA[s]=cA,f(t1​,…,tn​)A[s]=fA(t1A​[s],…,tnA​[s]).
(12)
For an atomic formula, substitute these first-order term values into the interpreted relation, or compare them for equality. Extend satisfaction for a set structure through the truth tables of the logical connectives, and through the quantifier clauses
A,s⊨∀xθ ⟺ (∀a∈A) A,s[x↦a]⊨θ,
(13)
A,s⊨∃xθ ⟺ (∃a∈A) A,s[x↦a]⊨θ.
(14)
Thus the first-order structure and assignment in a first-order structure together determine truth values for all logical formulas. A first-order sentence has no free variables, so its truth does not depend on the assignment in a first-order structure.

No finite axiomatization exists. Write Γ for the axioms just constructed and suppose a finite set Δ of first-order sentences had exactly the same first-order models. Its logical conjunction δ is a single first-order sentence, and Γ⊨δ. By the compactness theorem there is a finite subset Γ0​⊆Γ with Γ0​⊨δ: otherwise Γ∪{¬δ} would be finitely satisfiable and hence satisfiable, a contradiction.
Only finitely many of the integers n in the torsion-free scheme occur in Γ0​. Choose a prime number p larger than all of them. The cyclic group Z/pZ satisfies all the group axioms and each of these finitely many conditions from the torsion-free abelian group axiom scheme: multiplication by an integer 1≤n<p is invertible modulo p, so nx=0 implies x=0. It consequently satisfies Γ0​, then δ, and therefore Δ.
But its nonzero elements have finite order p, so it is not a torsion-free group, contradicting the assumed description of the first-order models of Δ. This establishes the nonfinite axiomatizability of torsion-free abelian groups, ruling out every finite set of defining first-order sentences, not just finite subsets of the particular scheme in part (c).

a OurBigBook.com
For a standard classical propositional calculus, strong completeness states
Γ⊨ϕ⟹Γ⊢ϕ.​
(19)
Together with soundness, this identifies semantic consequence with formal derivability. One proves the contrapositive by the maximal consistent set in propositional logic construction. If Γ⊬ϕ, the set Γ∪{¬ϕ} is propositionally consistent, by the deduction theorem for propositional logic and classical propositional reasoning. Extend it to a maximal consistent set in propositional logic M, deciding each logical formula in turn. At each stage at least one selection of θ or ¬θ preserves propositional consistency; a contradiction in the union would use only finitely many assumptions and hence already occur at a stage.
Define a Boolean valuation by v(pi​)=1 exactly when pi​∈M. Maximal consistency in propositional logic gives ¬θ∈M exactly when θ∈/M, and (θ→η)∈M exactly when either θ∈/M or η∈M. Structural induction on logical formulas therefore proves the maximal consistent set truth lemma v(θ)=1⟺θ∈M. Thus v satisfies Γ but falsifies ϕ, contradicting semantic entailment. The countable language here permits an enumeration of logical formulas; the corresponding general-language argument uses a well-ordering or a principle of maximality in a partially ordered set.

b OurBigBook.com
Gödel's completeness theorem for first-order sentences says that, in a standard sound first-order Hilbert calculus,
T⊨σ⟹T⊢σ.​
(20)
Equivalently every first-order consistent set of first-order sentences has a first-order model: apply this existence statement to T∪{¬σ} for the consequence formulation.
For the first-order model existence formal proof use a Henkin construction. Enlarge the language by fresh constant symbols and adjoin Henkin witness axioms
∃xθ(x)→θ(cθ​).
(21)
Freshness of cθ​ preserves first-order consistency. In detail, if a contradiction followed after adjoining one witness axiom, the deduction theorem for first-order logic would yield its negation, from which one obtains both ∃xθ(x) and ¬θ(cθ​) over the old theory. Since the new constant symbol does not occur in that theory or in θ, replace it by a fresh logical variable and generalize to derive ∀x¬θ(x), a contradiction. Finitely many witness additions preserve first-order consistency successively, and unions preserve first-order consistency because formal proofs are finite. Iterate the enlargement so that logical formulas involving newly introduced constant symbols also receive witnesses. Include at least one constant symbol to guarantee closed terms.
Extend the resulting first-order consistent witnessed first-order theory to a maximal consistent set of first-order sentences H. In the equality-free case make the domain the set of closed terms. Interpret constant symbols and function symbols by their formal first-order term constructions; interpret an atomic formula by
R(t1​,…,tn​) holds in the term model⟺R(t1​,…,tn​)∈H.
(22)
The truth lemma for a Henkin term model follows by induction on logical formulas with closed terms substituted for their free variables. The logical connective steps use maximal consistency of a first-order theory. In the existential step, membership of ∃xθ supplies a witness constant and hence a first-order term satisfying θ; conversely a satisfying closed term gives the existential sentence by an inference of the calculus. The term model consequently satisfies H and the original theory. Equality is absent here, so distinct closed terms may remain distinct domain elements without further identifications.

In the presence of equality, two syntactically different closed terms can be asserted equal by the Henkin theory, so the raw first-order term domain from part (b) must be replaced by a quotient set. Define
t∼u⟺(t=u)∈H.
(23)
The equality axioms give reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity, so this is an equivalence relation. Their substitution consequences make it a congruence relation on a first-order structure: replacing an input by an equivalent first-order term preserves equivalence of function values and preserves membership of atomic formulas in H.
Take domain {[t]:t is a closed term}. Define a function symbol by f([t1​],…,[tn​])=[f(t1​,…,tn​)], and relations by the same membership test as before using representatives. The congruence relation on a first-order structure makes these definitions independent of representatives. Actual identity of equivalence classes now agrees with the formal equality first-order sentences in H:
[t]=[u]⟺(t=u)∈H.​
(24)
This supplies the equality case of the truth lemma for a Henkin term model; the logical connective and quantifier cases then proceed exactly as in the Henkin construction. Thus the term model built from the quotient set is a genuine first-order model with equality interpreted as identity.

For a countable first-order language, the Henkin construction introduces only countably many constant symbols: each round needs at most one for each logical formula, there are countably many finite strings in a countable alphabet, and countably many rounds suffice. There are therefore at most countably many closed terms. The term model, and also its quotient set by equality, has at most countably many elements.
It follows that every satisfiable theory in a countable first-order language has an at most countable first-order model. If the theory has no finite first-order model, the constructed first-order model is countably infinite. More generally the same counting argument gives a first-order model of cardinality at most max(∣L∣,ℵ0​) for any satisfiable theory in language L.
For the elementary-substructure version of the downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem, begin with a first-order structure M and a set S of designated elements. Close S, the named constant symbols and one initial element under the language's functions and under chosen witnesses in M for existential formulas with parameters already chosen. Iterate countably often. The resulting set has cardinality at most max(∣L∣,∣S∣,ℵ0​), and its induced substructure N is elementary: induction on logical formulas proves the truth equivalence, with the existential case supplied by the chosen witnesses. This is the same witness mechanism as the Henkin formal proof, now keeping witnesses inside a given first-order model.

Let T=Th(C) in the finite language of rings: it is the set of all first-order sentences true in the field of complex numbers. It is satisfiable, with first-order model C, so the countable-language Löwenheim-Skolem theorem gives an at most countable first-order model K of T.
All field axioms belong to T, hence K is a field. For every positive integer n, the first-order sentence saying that there are at least n distinct elements is also true in C and belongs to T. Thus K cannot be finite, and is countably infinite.
Finally, for each first-order sentence σ, either σ∈T or ¬σ∈T. Since K satisfies all of T, it satisfies σ exactly when C does. Therefore
∣K∣=ℵ0​,K≡C.​
(25)
Elementary equivalence concerns first-order first-order sentences and does not imply an isomorphism; the two fields have different cardinalities. The stronger downward form may even choose K as a countable elementary subfield of C.

First give the recursive definitions, keeping the right argument as the recursion variable. For ordinals α,β and nonzero limit ordinals λ,
α+0=α,α+(β+1)=(α+β)+1,α+λ=supξ<λ​(α+ξ),
(26)
α⋅0=0,α⋅(β+1)=αβ+α,αλ=supξ<λ​αξ.
(27)
Here ξ+1 is the successor ordinal, and the supremum of a set of ordinals is its union. These are ordinal arithmetic, defined by transfinite recursion, and differ from cardinal sum and ordinal multiplication.
Associativity of ordinal addition is true. Fix α,β and use transfinite induction on γ. The case γ=0 is immediate. In the successor step the recursive definitions and the induction hypothesis give
(α+β)+(γ+1)=((α+β)+γ)+1=(α+(β+γ))+1=α+(β+(γ+1)).
(28)
For a nonzero limit ordinal λ, the ordinals β+ξ for ξ<λ form an increasing cofinal subset of the limit ordinal β+λ. The limit clause for ordinal addition, restricted to this cofinal subset, gives
(α+β)+λ=supξ<λ​((α+β)+ξ)=supξ<λ​(α+(β+ξ))=α+(β+λ).
(29)
Cofinal restriction is valid because ordinal addition by a fixed ordinal on the left is increasing and continuous in its right argument, directly from its recursive definition. This completes the induction.

Hartogs theorem says that for every set X there is a least ordinal h(X) for which no injection h(X)→X exists. This does not require the axiom of choice. Form the set W of well-orderings of subsets of X. It can be defined by the axiom schema of specification inside
P(X)×P(X×X),
(53)
recording a subset and its well-order. Each member of W has a unique ordinal order type. Axiom schema of replacement is used to collect those order types into a set
H={otp(Y,R):(Y,R)∈W}.
(54)
Every member of an ordinal in H is the order type of an initial segment of the corresponding well-ordering, so also belongs to H. Thus H is a transitive set of ordinals and is itself an ordinal. An ordinal α belongs to H exactly when it injects into X: use a bijection to a well-ordered subset in one direction, and transport the ordinal order along the injection in the other. If H injected into X, this characterization would give H∈H, impossible for an ordinal. All smaller ordinals lie in H and do inject into X. Therefore H=h(X). Apart from the standard order-type theorem, the explicit collection of all order types is the Axiom schema of replacement step; no axiom of choice of a representative ordering for each type is needed.
Zorn's lemma states that a nonempty partially ordered set in which every chain has an upper bound has a maximal element. Use the Hartogs bound on partial ordinal injections to obtain a set-sized partially ordered set. Consider the partial injections into X whose domains are ordinals. Hartogs theorem bounds every such domain strictly below h=h(X). Their graphs of a function therefore form a set
P={f⊆h×X:f is an injective function and domf is an ordinal},
(55)
obtained by the axiom schema of specification from P(h×X). Order P by extension. It is nonempty because it contains the empty function. A chain has as an upper bound the union of its graphs of a function: extension makes this a function, injectivity is preserved, and its domain is an union of ordinals and hence an ordinal. Its range is contained in X, so Hartogs theorem forces its domain to be strictly below h; thus it belongs to P. The empty chain also has the empty function as an upper bound.
By Zorn's lemma choose a maximal f:α→X. If an element x∈X lay outside its range, appending f(α)=x would give a larger injection on α+1. Such an injection itself guarantees α+1<h by Hartogs theorem, so it is a legitimate member of P, contradicting maximality in a partially ordered set. Therefore f is surjective, and
X is equinumerous with an ordinal.​
(56)
For this single extension one only selects an element of one specified nonempty set; no additional simultaneous choices are hidden in the argument. Zorn's lemma is the form of the axiom of choice used to obtain maximality in a partially ordered set.
Finally work with cardinals, represented by initial ordinals, and put μ=max(κ,λ). It is infinite by hypothesis. Nonzeroness of each factor gives an injection of the larger cardinal into the Cartesian product by holding a coordinate of the other fixed. The supplied infinite-ordinal square result gives ∣μ×μ∣=μ. Hence
μ≤κλ≤∣μ×μ∣=μ.
(57)
For the sum, use disjoint union:
μ≤κ+λ≤∣μ×{0,1}∣≤∣μ×μ∣=μ.
(58)
The last injection exists because μ is infinite and contains two distinct elements. Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem turns both pairs of bounds into equalities, proving
κ+λ=κλ=max(κ,λ).​
(59)
These final operations are cardinal sum and multiplication, measuring disjoint unions and Cartesian products. They are not the noncommutative ordinal arithmetic considered in question 5.
