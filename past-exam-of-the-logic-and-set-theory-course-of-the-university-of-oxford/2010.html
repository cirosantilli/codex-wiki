The alphabet of a first-order language separates logical symbols from nonlogical symbols. In the standard presentation here the logical symbols are the variables x0​,x1​,…, equality =˙, negation ¬, logical implication →, the universal quantification ∀, and the punctuation symbols parentheses and comma. The nonlogical symbols are constant symbols, function symbols of each specified finite arity, and predicate symbols of each specified finite arity, such as fn(k)​ and Pn(k)​.
Constants can equivalently be treated as zero-ary function symbols. Their arities belong to the language specification. Other logical connectives, and ∃xϕ as an abbreviation for ¬∀x¬ϕ, need not be primitive alphabet symbols. This distinction matters in counting literal symbol occurrences.

The first-order terms are the least collection containing every variable and constant and closed under formation of f(t1​,…,tk​) whenever f has arity k and the tj​ are terms. A closed term has no variables.
The first-order formulas are the least collection containing the atomic formulas t1​=˙t2​ and P(t1​,…,tk​) of the appropriate arities, and closed under negation, logical implication, and universal quantification: from formulas ϕ,ψ one may form ¬ϕ, (ϕ→ψ), and ∀xi​ϕ, with the chosen fully parenthesized grammar. Only finite expressions obtained by these rules are formulas. No additional strings belong to either collection.
Terms denote elements under an assignment in a first-order structure; formulas receive truth values in a first-order structure. The two syntactic categories have different formation rules even when they use some of the same punctuation.

True. In the formation rules, commas occur only between arguments in a function application or an atomic predicate application. Each such argument list includes an opening parenthesis. Subsequent construction of larger terms or formulas retains those symbols.
Equivalently prove by simultaneous structural induction on terms and formulas that an expression containing no opening parenthesis contains no comma. A collection of logical connectives is adequate, or has functional completeness, if every Boolean function of finitely many propositional variables can be expressed by a formula using those connectives and variables, with the usual convention for nullary truth constants stated if those are included.
An adequate example is {¬,→}​. An inadequate example is {→}​: formulas using logical implication alone preserve truth when every input is true, so cannot express negation. These examples concern truth functions, rather than mere syntactic formation of formulas.

i OurBigBook.com
A consistent set of formulae, restricted here to sentences, does not derive a contradiction in the chosen classical deductive system. Equivalently there is no sentence θ for which both Σ⊢θ and Σ⊢¬θ hold. By explosion, an inconsistent set derives every sentence; a consistent set does not.
This is a syntactic condition about finite derivations, rather than a definition in terms of models.

ii OurBigBook.com
A set of sentences is maximal consistent if it is consistent and no proper extension by further sentences remains consistent. Equivalently it is consistent, deductively closed, and decides every sentence: for each θ, exactly one of θ and ¬θ belongs to it.
For example, if Σ⊢θ but θ∈/Σ, adding θ does not create a new contradiction, contrary to maximality. Thus maximality includes deductive closure, not only having as many independent axioms as possible.

A set of sentences is satisfiable if some nonempty first-order structure in its language makes every sentence in the set true. Because the formulas are sentences, their truth is independent of the assignment in a first-order structure.
This is a semantic existence condition. Its equivalence with syntactic consistency is the content of the classical Gödel's completeness theorem, together with soundness.

i OurBigBook.com
Order the consistent sets of sentences extending Σ by inclusion. This collection is nonempty. The union of a chain is consistent: any derivation of a contradiction uses finitely many assumptions, which all lie in one member of the chain. Zorn's lemma therefore supplies a maximal member, which is a maximal consistent extension of Σ.
For a countable language one can instead enumerate all sentences and successively add either a sentence or its negation while preserving consistency. At least one choice is consistent, since inconsistency of both extensions, together with the deduction theorem for propositional logic and classical reasoning, would make the previous stage inconsistent. The union of the stages is consistent by finite use of assumptions and decides every sentence, so it is maximal consistent.
The chain proof covers arbitrary set-sized languages; no restriction on the language cardinality was printed here. The later prohibition on choice applies to the cardinal-arithmetic question, not this logic construction.

Suppose a structure satisfies every sentence of Σ. By the soundness theorem for first-order logic, every sentence derivable from Σ is true in this structure. In particular it cannot derive both a sentence and its negation, because they cannot both be true under the usual semantics.
Therefore Σ satisfiable ⟹ Σ consistent​. This direction uses soundness alone, without constructing a model from a proof-theoretic assumption.

i OurBigBook.com
In the Henkin construction, first extend the consistent sentence set to a complete consistent theory with witnesses in an expanded language containing fresh constants. Its term model has closed terms modulo provable equality as elements:
[t]=[u]⟺T⊢t=˙u.
(13)
Functions are interpreted by applying their symbols to representative terms, and an atomic predicate holds of term classes exactly when the corresponding atomic sentence belongs to T. Equality axioms make these definitions independent of representatives. The Henkin witness property handles the existential step in the truth lemma, so the resulting structure satisfies T and hence the original Σ.
An arbitrary sentence set need not already have enough closed terms or witnesses. Calling this a term model for Σ means a term-model construction of a model satisfying Σ, usually through that witnessed completion; it does not assert that every arbitrary Σ itself already supplies the construction.

Solution

ii OurBigBook.com
The downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem says that a structure in a countable language has a countable elementary substructure containing any specified finite or countable set of its elements. More generally the size bound is max(∣L∣,∣C∣,ℵ0​) for a required parameter set C. In particular any satisfiable theory in a countable language has a model of size at most countable.
To see the last assertion directly from the completeness construction, add only countably many witness constants in the Henkin construction. There are then countably many finite closed terms, and taking their quotient cannot increase cardinality. The resulting term model is therefore countable. For the elementary-substructure version, perform the witness construction inside the given structure, choosing actual witnesses and closing under the function symbols. The induced substructure is elementary by the Tarski-Vaught test. This is the same witness mechanism as the completeness construction, with the ambient structure supplying the chosen witnesses.
The upward companion states that a theory with an infinite model has models in every sufficiently large infinite cardinal, at least ∣L∣+ℵ0​. Add that many pairwise unequal constants, apply compactness, and then the size-bounded construction. The countable consequence is the part needed below.

Enumerate both countable models. Build an increasing sequence of finite order-preserving partial bijections. At an even stage, take the first unmatched element of the first model. Its position relative to the finitely many already matched points determines an open interval or exterior ray in the second model. Density and absence of endpoints supply an unused point there, so the map extends.
At an odd stage perform the symmetric operation starting from the second model, extending the inverse map. Every required interval has infinitely many points, so avoiding the finitely many used values is possible. The union is a total bijection, by the alternating enumeration requirements, and it preserves and reflects order because every finite stage does. Hence it is an isomorphism of structures.
This is the back-and-forth method, proving countable categoricity of the dense-order theory.

Hartogs theorem says that for every set A there is a least ordinal h(A) that does not inject into A. This theorem requires no axiom of choice.
To prove it, collect all well-orderings of subsets of A. They are set-sized data, coded by pairs from P(A) and P(A×A). Each has a unique ordinal order type, so replacement gives a set H of their order types. If η∈H and ξ<η, restricting the corresponding well-order to an initial segment shows ξ∈H. Hence H is an ordinal. If this ordinal itself injected into A, its transported well-order would put H among its own elements, impossible for an ordinal. Every smaller ordinal does inject by definition. Thus h(A)=H has the asserted property.
The recursion printed in the source needs two boundary restrictions. Its new-row successor clause allows β=α, which would assign a value at (α,α+1) outside the declared domain. Also, at α=0, its row-start clause gives g1​(0,0)=0 while its column-start clause gives g1​(0,0)=g1​(0,0)+1. Taken literally, these clauses do not define a function.
The intended extension fills the new row first and the remaining new column second. Restrict the row successor clause to β<α, and apply the column-start clause only when α>0. With these two restrictions the domains are disjoint:
α×α,{(α,β):β≤α},{(β,α):β<α}.
(34)
All successors here are ordinal successors. Simultaneous transfinite induction proves a stronger result: gα​ is a bijection of α×α onto an ordinal γα​, and the functions extend one another.
At zero this is the empty function. Assuming it at α, the least missing ordinal is γα​. Recursion down the new row gives values γα​+β for β≤α; recursion down the remaining column gives γα​+(α+1)+β for β<α. These enumerate two disjoint consecutive ordinal intervals above the old range, so the extension is well defined and bijective, with
γα+1​=γα​+(α+1)+α.​
(35)
At a limit λ, take the union of the earlier functions. Compatibility makes it a function, and every pair of coordinates below λ is already present at some earlier stage. Its range is the ordinal ⋃α<λ​γα​, and injectivity is preserved by the compatible union. This completes the construction of the row-and-column enumeration of an ordinal square.
For finite stages γn​=n2. For example the table of g3​, with the first coordinate indexing rows, is
