A logical derivation of α from Γ is a finite sequence δ1​,…,δn​=α in which each line belongs to Γ, is an instance of one of the three specified axiom schemes, or follows from two earlier lines δj​ and δj​→δi​ by the modus ponens rule. Γ⊢α means that such a finite derivation exists. In particular, a derivation uses only finitely many assumptions even if Γ is infinite.

∀vv(α)=v(β).​
(3)

Solution

ii OurBigBook.com
Assign to each propositional formula its finite symbol length. The set of lengths of formulas logically equivalent to α is a nonempty subset of the natural numbers, because it contains the length of α. The well-ordering principle for the natural numbers gives a least such length, and hence a shortest equivalent formula.
A shortest equivalent formula need not be unique. For example (p0​→p0​) and (p0​↔p0​) are distinct tautologies of the same minimal length. A shorter formula would contain no binary connective and hence be a variable with some number of negations, which is not a tautology. This counts each propositional variable as one symbol, as in the stated language, and proves that a shortest equivalent formula need not be unique.

i=1⋀m​(j=1⋁ni​​ℓij​),ℓij​∈{pk​,¬pk​:k∈ω}.​

First eliminate implication and biconditional using the logical equivalences
A→B≡¬A∨B,A↔B≡(¬A∨B)∧(¬B∨A).
(5)
Push negations inwards using De Morgan's laws and eliminate double negations. Structural induction leaves only literals joined by conjunction and disjunction.
Now induct on that remaining expression. A literal is already in conjunctive normal form. A conjunction of two conjunctive normal forms is again one after associating its clauses. For a disjunction, write the two inductively obtained forms as ⋀i​Ci​ and ⋀j​Dj​ and use distributivity:
(i⋀​Ci​)∨(j⋀​Dj​)≡i,j⋀​(Ci​∨Dj​).​
(6)
Each Ci​∨Dj​ is a clause. All operations preserve logical equivalence, proving the required form exists.

Semantic consequence under an assignment means that for every first-order structure A with the relevant first-order variable assignment v into its domain,
(A⊨γ[v] for every γ∈Γ) ⟹ A⊨ϕ[v].​
(10)

By induction on first-order formulas, their truth values depend only on the values of their free variables. A first-order sentence has none. If all formulas here are sentences, every displayed satisfaction statement, and therefore the consequence condition, is independent of v.

The compactness theorem states that a set of first-order sentences has a model if every finite subset has a model.
For each n≥1, let χn​ assert that at least n distinct elements exist. Consider T={ϕ}∪{χn​:n≥1}. A finite subset requires only some finite lower bound N on the size. Choose an odd integer m≥N; the given property of ϕ supplies a model of that size, satisfying the finite subset. By the compactness theorem, T has a model. Its domain has at least n elements for every n, so ϕ has an infinite model. This argument applies to every sentence with the finite-model-size property in (b), irrespective of its behavior on infinite structures.

A logical derivation is a finite proof in the specified calculus, with conclusion Γ⊢ϕ. Assumptions from Γ and instances of A1–A7 are available; the rules are the modus ponens rule, universal generalization rule, and weakening.
In judgement form the universal generalization rule takes Δ⊢θ to Δ⊢∀xi​θ only if xi​ is not free in any undischarged assumption in Δ. Weakening permits Δ⊢θ to become Δ∪Σ⊢θ. All instances of A4 and A5 must meet their stated substitution and free variable conditions. Derivability means existence of such a finite proof with assumptions contained in Γ. Equivalently one tracks the finite assumption set used by each proof line.

The first-order term t is free for substitution for xi​ in ϕ if, at every free occurrence of xi​ to be replaced, no enclosing quantifier binds a variable occurring in t. Equivalently, the substitution introduces no variable capture. Only free occurrences of xi​ are replaced.

Write E(x,y) for the displayed equality predicate. A6 and A4 give ⊢E(x,x), establishing a reflexive relation.
An instance of A7, replacing just the first occurrence of x in E(x,x), is
E(x,y)→(E(x,x)→E(y,x)).
(12)
Assume E(x,y), apply the modus ponens rule twice using reflexivity, and obtain E(y,x). The deduction theorem for propositional logic discharges the assumption. This proves symmetry.
For transitivity, A7 supplies
E(y,z)→(E(x,y)→E(x,z)),
(13)
by replacing y in the atomic formula E(x,y). From assumptions E(x,y) and E(y,z), two applications of the modus ponens rule give E(x,z). Discharging assumptions and universally generalizing gives
⊢∀xE(x,x),⊢∀x∀y(E(x,y)→E(y,x)),⊢∀x∀y∀z((E(x,y)∧E(y,z))→E(x,z)).​
(14)
Thus the predicate defines an equivalence relation within the calculus.

No such first-order axiom collection can force actual identity when the displayed symbol is treated as an ordinary relation. The duplication obstruction to axiomatizing identity proves this even for an infinite collection of extra axioms.
Start with any nonempty first-order structure A in which the symbol is identity. Form B=A×{0,1} and the surjection π:B→A. Interpret each relation by pulling it back along π; in particular,
EB((a,i),(b,j))⟺a=b.
(15)
Interpret every function on first coordinates as in A and choose second coordinate zero; likewise lift constants with second coordinate zero. Induction on first-order terms gives π(tB(v))=tA(πv). Induction on first-order formulas then gives
B⊨θ[v]⟺A⊨θ[πv].
(16)
The quantifier steps use surjectivity of π, so every witness in A lifts to B.
Every proposed axiom valid with actual equality is therefore true in B under every assignment, along with A1–A7. But EB((a,0),(a,1)) holds for two different domain elements. Thus those axioms cannot enforce identity. The usual interpretation of logical equality as identity is a semantic requirement.

An approximation is a function s:n+1→X with s(0)=x0​ and s(k+1)=g(s(k)) for k<n. By induction on n, there is such a function: start with {(0,x0​)} and append the uniquely specified value g(s(n)).
Any two approximations agree on their common domain, by induction from their common initial value and the same transition function g. The collection S of all approximations is a set, being a definable subset of P(ω×X). Therefore f=⋃S is a function. Existence of approximations of every finite length gives dom(f)=ω. Each defining equation appears in a sufficiently long approximation, so
f:ω→X,f(0)=x0​,f(n+1)=g(f(n)).​
(17)
This proves the natural-number recursion theorem without the axiom of choice; no arbitrary sequence of choices was made.

The Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem states that injections A↪B and B↪A imply a bijection A≅B.
Let c=∣R∣ and D={S⊆R:S is dense in R}. Clearly ∣D∣≤2c. For the reverse bound, let C be the usual Cantor set, of cardinality c. It is closed with empty interior, so D=R∖C is dense. The map
P(C)⟶D,A⟼D∪A
(20)

∣D∣=2c=22ℵ0​.​

The four principles are:
  • Axiom of choice: for every family (Ai​)i∈I​ of nonempty sets, there exists a function c on I with c(i)∈Ai​ for all i.
  • Cardinal comparability principle: for any sets A,B, there is an injection A↪B or an injection B↪A.
  • Well-ordering theorem: every set admits a well-order.
  • Zorn's lemma: every nonempty partially ordered set in which every chain has an upper bound has a maximal element.
In the last assertion the upper bound belongs to the partially ordered set; it need not belong to the chain.

Assume the well-ordering theorem. Every set with a chosen well-order has an order isomorphism to a unique ordinal. Any two ordinals are comparable, and the smaller embeds into the larger as an initial segment. Transporting that embedding back to the original sets proves the cardinal comparability principle.
Conversely, assume the cardinal comparability principle and let X be any set. The Hartogs theorem, provable in ZF, supplies an ordinal h(X) for which no injection h(X)↪X exists. Comparison forces an injection X↪h(X). Pulling back the well-order on its image gives a well-order on X. Therefore
ZF⊢CC↔WO.​
(26)
The assumed ordinal facts are that every well-ordered set has an ordinal order type and that any two ordinals are comparable; the other assumed theorem is precisely the stated Hartogs theorem.

c OurBigBook.com
For the standard definition of algebraic independence, finite tuples are required to have distinct entries. Let P be the set of all subsets of L with algebraic independence over K, ordered by inclusion. It is nonempty because ∅∈P.
If C is a chain in P, put U=⋃C. A finite tuple of distinct elements of U lies in one member of C: choose a member containing each element and take the largest of those finitely many members. A polynomial relation on that tuple would then be a polynomial relation in that member, contradicting its algebraic independence. Thus U∈P and is an upper bound. For the empty chain, ∅ is an upper bound. The Zorn's lemma now gives a maximal member B, which is a transcendence basis by the specified maximality criterion. To also recover the usual field-theoretic definition, if a∈L were transcendental over K(B), clearing denominators in a polynomial relation would show that B∪{a} still has algebraic independence over K, contradicting maximality. Thus L is algebraic over K(B). Hence every field extension has a transcendence basis.
There is a literal issue in the printed definition: it does not require the tuple entries to be distinct. With that wording, any nonempty X fails the test: for a∈X, the nonzero polynomial X1​−X2​ vanishes on (a,a), in every characteristic. Thus under the literal printed test the family is exactly {∅}, and ∅ is its maximal member. The same chain-and-Zorn's lemma argument still proves existence for that literal notion, but it is not the usual transcendence basis notion. The conventional result above uses distinct tuple entries.

Let O be the set of strict total orders on κ. Each is a subset of κ×κ, so the square of an infinite cardinal gives
∣O∣≤2∣κ×κ∣=2κ.
(27)
For the lower bound, use the set κ×{0,1} and order the two-element blocks by their first coordinates, using the well-order of κ. For every A⊆κ, put (α,0) before (α,1) exactly when α∈A, reversing them otherwise. This defines a strict total order for each A, and different subsets give different orders. Transport these orders to κ along a fixed bijection κ×{0,1}≅κ. Thus 2κ≤∣O∣, and the Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem gives
∣O∣=2κ.​
(28)
If using the customary axiom of choice development of infinite cardinal arithmetic, its use here is precisely to supply κ×κ≅κ and κ×2≅κ. The ordering construction makes no further choices. It proves the cardinality of the family of strict total orders. For κ itself an initial ordinal, these particular product identities can also be proved in ZF by well-ordering pairs according to their maximum coordinate, so full axiom of choice is stronger than this instance intrinsically requires.
