The assertion Γ⊢ϕ means that there is a finite formal derivation ending in ϕ with its undischarged assumptions contained in Γ. In a context-labelled presentation, an axiom instance is available with empty context, a formula α is available from the context {α}, modus ponens combines derivations of α and α→β to derive β, and generalization derives ∀xα provided x is not free in its open assumptions. Thinning enlarges a context without changing its conclusion.
Each line or node must specify an axiom instance, an assumption, or an application of a permitted rule to earlier lines or subderivations. The side conditions on substitution, quantification and contexts are part of correctness. In particular a derivation is a finite syntactic object, even when Γ is infinite; it uses only finitely many assumptions before a final thinning to Γ if necessary. Derivability ⊢ is thus distinct in definition from semantic entailment ⊨, which quantifies over structures and assignments.

A term t is free for substitution for xi​ in α when none of its variables becomes bound at a free occurrence of xi​ being replaced. Equivalently, no quantifier surrounding such an occurrence binds a variable of t. Bound occurrences of xi​ are not replaced. This occurrence-sensitive condition prevents variable capture.
For example take α(x)=∃y¬(x=y) and t=y. On a two-element domain, ∀x∃y¬(x=y) is true. Naive textual substitution produces ∃y¬(y=y), which is false on every nonempty domain. Thus the corresponding unrestricted A4 implication would be invalid. The problem is that the inserted free y was captured by the existing existential quantifier. Renaming that binder first instead gives the correct instance ∃z¬(y=z), with z fresh.

Take α=β=P(x) for a unary predicate symbol. The premise ∀x(P(x)→P(x)) is valid, but the proposed conclusion is P(x)→∀xP(x). On a domain {0,1} with P={0} and assignment x↦0, its antecedent is true and its consequent false.
Hence dropping the condition that x is not free in α would turn a valid premise into an invalid conclusion. The condition permits the antecedent to remain fixed while the quantified variable ranges over the domain; an antecedent depending on that variable cannot be held true for all its values merely because it is true at the current assignment.

Capture-safe equality replacement gives the corrected scheme:
⊢xi​=xj​→(ϕ→ϕ′),​
(1)
where ϕ′ replaces selected free occurrences of xi​ by xj​, without capture. One may equivalently use full capture-avoiding substitution, after renaming conflicting binders. Replacing bound occurrences as though they were free is not permitted.
Here is a syntactic proof. Write e for xi​=xj​. First derive symmetry under e: A6 and A4 give xi​=xi​, and A7 applied to the first position in that atomic equality gives e→(xi​=xi​→xj​=xi​). Modus ponens and the deduction theorem give ⊢e→xj​=xi​. Atomic replacement in the forward direction is A7 itself; in the reverse direction apply A7 with i,j exchanged and use this symmetry. Thus under e, an atomic formula and its designated replacement imply each other.
Induct on the formation of ϕ, proving both directions simultaneously. For negation, the reverse implication for the immediate subformula gives the forward implication of its negations by contraposition, and vice versa. For θ→η, the two induction equivalences combine propositionally to (θ→η)↔(θ′→η′). This uses only the propositional tautologies allowed in this part.
For ϕ=∀zθ, first take z distinct from xi​,xj​. From assumptions e and ∀zθ, A4 gives θ, the induction hypothesis gives θ′, and generalization gives ∀zθ′: z is free in neither open assumption. Discharge the two assumptions to obtain e→(∀zθ→∀zθ′). The reverse direction is identical. If the binder is xi​, no free occurrence of that variable lies beneath it and the replacement there is empty. If it is xj​, capture avoidance likewise forbids a changed free xi​ below that binder unless the binder is first renamed to a fresh variable. Bound-variable renaming is derived from A4 followed by admissible generalization and A5, with the same freshness restriction. This completes the structural induction and proves the scheme for arbitrary formulas.

The thinning rule, or weakening, is
Δ⊢αΓ⊢αΓ⊆Δ​.​
(2)
It expresses that extra unused assumptions do not destroy a proof. In the context-sensitive predicate-calculus presentation it also allows a theorem first proved with a fresh generalization variable to be used later in a context containing that variable free. Simply replaying the same generalization under the larger context could violate its side condition. For instance a proof of ∀x(x=x) from the empty context may generalize on x; it should remain available when an unused assumption P(x) is added, even though that last generalization could not be replayed with P(x) open.
In propositional calculus the axiom schemes and modus ponens have no analogous free-variable restriction. An induction on the original proof simply reuses each axiom, assumption and modus-ponens step under the enlarged assumptions, so weakening is admissible and need not be postulated separately. Context-sensitive Hilbert weakening concerns this proof presentation: systems already tracking only the assumptions on which each line depends can build admissible weakening into their treatment of predicate-calculus generalization as well.

With generalization restricted to variables not free in open assumptions, the deduction theorem states
Γ∪{α}⊢β⟺Γ⊢α→β.​
(3)
The reverse direction follows by thinning and modus ponens. For the forward direction, induct on a derivation, keeping track of its actual assumption contexts. Axioms and assumptions already in Γ yield α→χ by A1; the distinguished assumption α yields the permitted theorem α→α. If a line χ is obtained by modus ponens from η and η→χ, the induction hypotheses and A2 derive α→χ.
If a generalization step depends on the discharged assumption, its variable x is not free in α or the other open assumptions. Generalize the induction conclusion α→η to ∀x(α→η) and use A5 to derive α→∀xη. If the subderivation does not depend on α, its generalized conclusion can instead be retained as an independent derivation and A1 applied afterwards. Thinning merely enlarges the remaining context and preserves the induction claim. These cases are why assumption dependence, rather than a careless unrestricted universal rule, is essential.
In a presentation allowing generalization freely from hypotheses, the corresponding theorem must instead explicitly require that no variable free in α is generalized in an α-dependent line. Without that restriction P(x)⊢∀xP(x) would incorrectly yield ⊢P(x)→∀xP(x).

A first-order structure consists of a nonempty domain A, an interpretation cA∈A of each constant symbol, a total function fA:An→A for each n-ary function symbol, and a relation RA⊆An for each n-ary predicate symbol. Logical equality is interpreted as actual identity.
An assignment in a first-order structure is a map s from the variables into A. It extends recursively to terms: xA[s]=s(x), cA[s]=cA, and f(t1​,…,tn​)A[s]=fA(t1A​[s],…,tnA​[s]). Atomic truth is determined by membership of the resulting tuple in RA, or equality of the two term values. Negation reverses the truth value, and an implication is false exactly when its antecedent is true and its consequent false.
For a universal quantifier,
A,s⊨∀xθ⟺A,s[x↦a]⊨θ for every a∈A.
(4)
These clauses define a valuation on every formula by structural recursion. Only free-variable values affect a formula's truth; consequently a sentence has the same truth value under every assignment. This is a semantic assignment of domain elements, not a programming assignment operation.

Fix any structure and any assignment of the three quantified variables, writing their values as a0​,a1​,a2​. If either of the nested antecedents is false, the corresponding implication is true. If both are true, then fA(a0​)=a1​ and fA(a0​)=a2​. Actual identity is transitive, so a1​=a2​, making the consequent true as well.
Thus the unquantified matrix is true under every assignment in every structure. The universal-quantifier semantic clause then makes its universal closure true in every structure:
⊨∀x0​∀x1​∀x2​[f(x0​)=x1​→(f(x0​)=x2​→x1​=x2​)].​
(5)
No assumption that f is injective is used. The argument says that one value cannot equal two distinct elements, not that distinct inputs have distinct outputs.

Choose a fresh variable x3​. In A7 take the equality antecedent x3​=x1​, the atomic formula ϕ=(x3​=x2​), and replace its occurrence of x3​ by x1​. This gives directly the theorem
⊢x3​=x1​→(x3​=x2​→x1​=x2​).
(6)

⊢f(x0​)=x1​→(f(x0​)=x2​→x1​=x2​).
(7)

⊢∀x0​∀x1​∀x2​[f(x0​)=x1​→(f(x0​)=x2​→x1​=x2​)].​

Adequacy here means functional completeness: every Boolean truth function of finitely many positive-arity propositional inputs can be expressed by a formula using the available connectives. It is an expressive-power claim, distinct from the completeness theorem relating derivability and semantic entailment.
Negation and implication define conjunction and disjunction by
p∧q≡¬(p→¬q),p∨q≡¬p→q.
(9)
Given any truth table, for each input row where its output is true form the minterm containing each input positively or negatively according to that row. The disjunction of these minterms is true on exactly the required rows: a disjunctive normal form. Replacing its conjunctions and disjunctions by the displayed definitions gives a formula using only ¬,→.
The constantly true and constantly false functions are represented by p→p and ¬(p→p), using a dummy input if needed. Thus the empty or full truth table also causes no difficulty. If one insists on genuinely nullary formulas with no variables at all, a separate truth constant is needed; ordinary propositional adequacy allows the constant truth functions to be expressed with irrelevant input variables.

There are sixteen binary Boolean functions, one for each four-bit truth table. Exactly eight give an adequate basis together with negation.
The eight affine functions are 0,1,p,¬p,q,¬q,p⊕q,¬(p⊕q). Each is affine over the field with two elements, and composition with other affine functions or negation remains affine. Since conjunction is not affine, none of these eight bases is adequate.
Each remaining truth table has either one true row or three true rows. In the latter case negate its output to obtain a function with one true row. Negate selected inputs to move that row to (1,1): the resulting function is conjunction. Conjunction together with negation expresses every disjunctive normal form, proving adequacy of all remaining eight. They are AND, NAND, OR, NOR, the two directed implications, and the two asymmetric conjunctions p∧¬q, ¬p∧q. The count treats the binary connective by its truth function rather than by arbitrary choices of its printed symbol.

A graph must assign exactly one output to every input, while the distinguished unary predicate must select exactly one element. Suitable sentences are
ϕ=∀x∃y[P(x,y)∧∀z(P(x,z)→z=y)],​
(10)
ψ=∃x[Q(x)∧∀z(Q(z)→z=x)].​
(11)
The existential clauses provide totality and nonemptiness of the selected output, respectively; the universal clauses provide uniqueness. Thus a structure satisfying ϕ,ψ uniquely determines a total unary function f and a constant c on its unchanged domain. Conversely any interpretations of f,c determine such relations by P(x,y)⟺f(x)=y and Q(x)⟺x=c.

Replace each n-ary function symbol by an (n+1)-ary graph predicate, with axioms asserting exactly one output for each input tuple. Replace each constant by a unary predicate asserted to hold of exactly one element. Recursively introduce witnesses for term values, then translate formulas through their logical connectives and quantifiers as above.
Every original structure has a unique corresponding relational structure satisfying these axioms, on exactly the same carrier, and every such relational structure reconstructs the original functions and constants. Truth under assignments and logical consequences are preserved in both directions. In this precise relative sense, purely relational languages are adequate to represent first-order languages with functions and constants: expressive power is retained after adjoining the graph and uniqueness axioms. Arbitrary relational interpretations that fail those axioms are not claimed to represent functions.

ii OurBigBook.com
Neither constants alone nor functions alone gives the same uniform adequacy on all nonempty carriers. A one-element set has exactly one interpretation of each function symbol of any finite arity and of every constant symbol. In contrast it has two interpretations of a unary predicate: empty or full. Thus a fixed purely functional or constant-only language has only one structure on that singleton, and cannot encode both truth values of ∃xP(x) by a same-carrier truth-preserving translation with reconstruction. This is the singleton truth-preservation obstruction to expressive completeness, even when logical equality is available.
Constants alone also cannot represent general variable-dependent relations on larger infinite carriers: a fixed formula mentions only finitely many named elements, and its remaining equality tests do not encode an arbitrary binary relation. Functions offer more flexibility. On carriers with at least two elements, predicates can be encoded by characteristic functions taking two distinguished values, and constants can be represented by constant-valued unary functions if explicit constant symbols are forbidden. Such a construction can work after adding axioms for distinct truth markers, but those axioms exclude the singleton case. Enlarging the carrier or adding a Boolean sort is another possible encoding; it changes the same-carrier sense established in the preceding part and must not be silently identified with it.

The ordinal operations are defined by transfinite recursion in the right argument. Addition satisfies α+0=α, α+(β+1)=(α+β)+1, and α+λ=supβ<λ​(α+β) at a nonzero limit. Multiplication satisfies α⋅0=0, α⋅(β+1)=α⋅β+α, and α⋅λ=supβ<λ​α⋅β. Exponentiation satisfies α0=1, αβ+1=αβ⋅α, and αλ=supβ<λ​αβ at nonzero limits. These are ordinal operations, not commutative cardinal arithmetic.
For a normal function on an ordinal, prove domination by transfinite induction. At zero, 0≤Φ(0) automatically. If α≤Φ(α), strict increase at successors gives α+1≤Φ(α)+1≤Φ(α+1). At a nonzero limit λ, continuity and the induction hypotheses give
Φ(λ)=supα<λ​Φ(α)≥supα<λ​α=λ.
(16)
Therefore α≤Φ(α) for every ordinal α​. No axiom of choice is needed for this ordinal induction.

For fixed β, the recursion defining β+α makes it continuous in α at every nonzero limit, and (β+α)+1>β+α. Thus α↦β+α is normal, including when β=0.
For β>0, take λ=β⋅ω. If α<λ, choose a natural number n with α<βn; then α+β≤β(n+1)<λ. Since α+β≥α, their supremum as α↑λ is λ. But λ+β>λ, so
supα<λ​(α+β)=λ<λ+β.
(17)
Hence right addition by a nonzero fixed ordinal fails continuity and is not normal.
Normality and argument order in ordinal arithmetic also give the multiplication statements: α↦β⋅α is normal for β>0, while α↦α⋅β is normal exactly when β=1. At β=0 it is constant. For fixed β>1, let κ=ωβ⋅ω. Cantor normal form shows that ξβ<κ whenever ξ<κ: leading exponents below βω remain below it after adding the leading exponent of β. Thus supξ<κ​ξβ=κ, but κβ>κ, again violating continuity. Left-fixed multiplication is strictly increasing and continuous by its recursion.
For exponentiation, α↦βα is normal for β≥2; bases zero and one are exceptions. The reverse map α↦αβ is normal exactly when β=1. Exponent zero gives the constant one. For β>1, use the same κ and put λ=ωκ. For each α<λ choose 0<ζ<κ with α≤ωζ. Then αβ≤ωζβ<λ, while αβ≥α for positive α. Consequently its left supremum is λ, but λβ=ωκβ>λ. The bound by ωζ also handles finite bases, where an unqualified leading-exponent multiplication formula would need care. All these are ordinal facts and use no choice.

For the cardinality of total orders on a countably infinite set, a total order on N is a subset of N×N, so there are at most 2ℵ0​=c such orders. To obtain the lower bound, associate to every binary sequence (bn​) an order by placing the two-element blocks {2n,2n+1} in increasing order of n. Inside block n, use 2n<2n+1 when bn​=0 and reverse them when bn​=1.
This is a total order for every sequence. Distinct sequences differ on the comparison of some adjacent pair and therefore produce distinct relations. Thus binary sequences inject into the order set, proving
c.​
(27)
The construction counts distinct orders on the labelled set, even though the resulting order types are all isomorphic to the usual natural-number order.

Zorn's lemma states that a nonempty partially ordered set in which every chain has an upper bound has a maximal element. Order the partial group operations by inclusion of their graphs. The collection is a set, since its members are subsets of X×X×X. It is nonempty: choose x0​∈X and put the one-element group operation on {x0​}.
To construct a chain union of partial group operations, for the given nonempty chain, let m=⋃n∈C​n and let A be the union of the corresponding group carriers. Comparability guarantees agreement wherever two operations are both defined, so m is a function. Its domain is A×A: any two elements of A lie together in one carrier, by taking the larger of the two chain members containing them. Their product then lies in that carrier and hence in A.
Any three elements similarly lie together in one chain member, where associativity holds; therefore associativity holds for m. The identities of nested nonempty groups agree: the smaller identity remains an idempotent under the larger group operation, whose only idempotent is its identity. Hence the whole chain has a common identity. Each element's inverse in a carrier remains its inverse in every larger carrier, so it is an inverse in the union as well. Thus (A,m) satisfies all group axioms and
⋃C∈G.​
(28)
It is an upper bound for the chain. The empty chain also has an upper bound because G is nonempty, completing the hypothesis needed for Zorn's lemma.

Use group extension by a duplicate carrier to transport the direct-product group operation along this bijection. Its restriction to A×A is exactly the original m, because (a,0)(a′,0)=(m(a,a′),0). It therefore gives a strictly larger member of G, contradicting maximality. Thus ∣A∣≰∣X∖A∣. Cardinal comparability, allowed as a consequence of Zorn's lemma, leaves the strict opposite inequality:
∣X∖A∣<∣A∣.​

If X is finite and nonempty, let n=∣X∣≥1, choose a bijection with the cyclic group Z/nZ, and transport its addition to X. This includes the one-element case.
If X is infinite, use the maximal partial group (A,m) from the preceding part. Its carrier must be infinite: otherwise ∣X∖A∣<∣A∣ would make the complement finite too, forcing X finite. Since the complement injects into A,
∣A∣≤∣X∣=∣A∣+∣X∖A∣≤∣A∣+∣A∣=2∣A∣=∣A∣,
(30)
where the last equality is the permitted infinite-cardinal doubling identity. Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein supplies a bijection j:X→A. Define
x⋆y=j−1(m(j(x),j(y))).​
(31)
Associativity follows by applying j and using associativity of m; the identity is j−1(eA​) and the inverse of x is j−1(j(x)−1). Hence (X,⋆) is a group.
This final transport need not extend the earlier partial operation on its original subset. That stronger claim is neither needed nor generally possible: for example a group of order three cannot occur as a subgroup of a group of order four. The proof establishes exactly the existence of some group structure on every nonempty set, using the allowed choice consequences in the infinite case.
