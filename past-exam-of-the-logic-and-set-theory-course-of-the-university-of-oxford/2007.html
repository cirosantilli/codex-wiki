A Boolean valuation is an arbitrary function v assigning one of the two truth values to each propositional variable. It extends uniquely to all propositional formulas by following the construction of the propositional formula:
v(p)=v(p),v(¬A)=¬v(A),v(A∧B)=v(A)∧v(B),v(A∨B)=v(A)∨v(B).
(1)
For logical implication, v(A→B) is false exactly when v(A) is true and v(B) is false; otherwise it is true. Here the operations on the right are Boolean operations on truth values. Structural recursion gives existence, and structural induction gives uniqueness: once the immediate subformulas have their values, the outer connective fixes the resulting value.

Two propositional formulas are logically equivalent if every Boolean valuation gives them the same truth value. A disjunctive normal form is a logical disjunction of logical conjunctions of literals, with a literal being a propositional variable or its negation.
Let p1​,…,pn​ include all propositional variables appearing in ϕ. For each Boolean valuation s∈{T,F}n, put
Ms​=⋀j=1n​Ls,j​,Ls,j​={pj​¬pj​​sj​=T,sj​=F.​
(2)
The minterm Ms​ is true exactly for Boolean valuations whose restrictions to these propositional variables are s. Consequently
ϕ≡s:s(ϕ)=T⋁​Ms​.​
(3)
Indeed, an Boolean valuation making ϕ true makes its own minterm true, while a true minterm in the displayed logical disjunction certifies the truth of ϕ. There are only finitely many Boolean valuations, so this is a finite disjunctive normal form. If there are no satisfying Boolean valuations, use p∧¬p for any propositional variable p; this is a disjunctive normal form for falsity without needing an empty logical disjunction convention.

Assume first that ϕ is false on the all-true and all-false Boolean valuations. Use the truth-table normal form from part (ii). Every satisfying Boolean valuation on the finitely many propositional variables must have at least one true propositional variable and at least one false propositional variable. Its minterm therefore has the form
Cs​∧¬Ds​,
(4)
where Cs​ conjoins its true propositional variables and Ds​ disjoins its false propositional variables; both lists are nonempty. This is equivalent to ¬(Cs​→Ds​). If the satisfying Boolean valuations are indexed by 1,…,N, De Morgan's laws gives
ϕ≡i=1⋁N​¬(Ci​→Di​)≡¬i=1⋀N​(Ci​→Di​).​
(5)
If there are no satisfying Boolean valuations, take N=1 and C1​=D1​=p: the result is ¬(p→p), again always false.
Conversely, on the all-true Boolean valuation every nonempty Di​ is true, so every Ci​→Di​ is true. On the all-false Boolean valuation every nonempty Ci​ is false, so every logical implication is again true. Negating their logical conjunction gives false on both Boolean valuations. Logical equivalence transfers these values to ϕ. The usual nonempty meaning of logical conjunction and logical disjunction of propositional variables is used here; allowing empty Ci​ or Di​ without qualification would destroy this converse.

A logical derivation from Γ is a finite sequence of propositional formulas, each either a member of Γ, an instance of one of the three axioms, or obtained from earlier lines by one of the two displayed inference rules. The notation Γ⊢α means that some such logical derivation ends in α. Writing ⊢α means derivability with no assumptions.
Propositional consistency means that there is no propositional formula θ for which both Γ⊢θ and Γ⊢¬θ. Equivalently not every propositional formula is derivable. To check the equivalence directly for this calculus, use the identity theorem proved in (ii): from θ→θ and L3 obtain θ→(¬θ→η); two uses of modus ponens rule give arbitrary η. Thus a contradictory theory proves everything. Conversely a theory proving everything proves a propositional formula and its negation.

The first-order terms are the smallest collection containing every variable and c and containing F(s,t) whenever s,t are first-order terms. The atomic formulas are exactly s≈t for first-order terms s,t. Logical formulas are then obtained from atomic formulas by the logical connectives and quantifiers: if θ,ψ are logical formulas and x a variable, then ¬θ, (θ∧ψ), (θ∨ψ), (θ→ψ), ∀xθ and ∃xθ are logical formulas. These closure rules, with no additional strings admitted, define the syntax; a presentation using fewer primitive connectives treats the others as abbreviations.
Semantic entailment is the condition
Γ⊨ϕ⟺for every A and every assignment v,(A⊨γ[v] for all γ∈Γ)⟹A⊨ϕ[v].​
(16)
The same first-order variable assignment is used for all premises and the conclusion. This matters when free variables occur; for first-order sentences the truth value is independent of the first-order variable assignment.

For independence, construct four first-order structures, each satisfying exactly the three required remaining axioms and failing the chosen one.
To fail associativity, use the carrier {0,1,2}, interpret c as 0, and define the commutative operation by its table:
F012​0012​1101​2210​​
(17)
The first row and column give a two-sided identity, and every element is its own inverse because the diagonal entries are 0. Symmetry gives commutativity. But
F(F(1,1),2)=2=0=F(1,F(1,2)),
(18)
so associativity fails.
To fail the identity axiom, take {0,1}, c=0, and the constant operation F(x,y)=0. It is associative and commutative, and every element has a two-sided partner producing c; but F(1,c)=0=1.
To fail the inverse axiom, use natural numbers under addition with c=0. Addition is associative and commutative and has the required identity, but 1 has no additive inverse in this carrier.
To fail commutativity, use the symmetric group S3​ under permutation composition with c its identity. Associativity, identity and inverses are group properties, while (12)(23)=(23)(12). Each countermodel witnesses the failure of semantic entailment from the other three to the failed first-order sentence. Hence the four source axioms are independent.

Soundness states that formal derivability implies semantic entailment:
Γ⊢K​ϕ⟹Γ⊨ϕ.​
(19)
For first-order sentences this means that every first-order structure satisfying all assumptions also satisfies every first-order sentence formally derived from them. In the logical formula version one uses the usual restrictions on generalization from assumptions with free variables.
Strong completeness states the converse:
Γ⊨ϕ⟹Γ⊢K​ϕ.​
(20)
Equivalently, every syntactically consistent set of first-order sentences has a first-order model. Equality is interpreted as actual identity, and its usual logical axioms and substitution rules are part of the standard calculus. Both assertions hold for arbitrary sets of assumptions, rather than only finite sets.

The compactness theorem says that a set T of first-order sentences has a first-order model if and only if every finite subset of T has a first-order model. One direction follows by using the same first-order model for each subset.
For the nontrivial direction suppose every finite subset has a first-order model, but T has none. Semantically T⊨⊥, so completeness gives a formal logical derivation T⊢⊥. A formal logical derivation is finite and uses only finitely many assumptions, say T0​⊆T. Thus T0​⊢⊥. Soundness would make T0​ unsatisfiable, contradicting the hypothesis. Here ⊥ can be any fixed contradictory first-order sentence, such as ∃x(x=x), if it is not a primitive logical symbol. Therefore finite satisfiability implies satisfiability.

The axiom of infinity asserts that an inductive set exists: some set I contains 0=∅ and contains x+=x∪{x} whenever it contains x. Inside one such I, axiom of separation defines
ω={x∈I:x belongs to every inductive subset of I}.
(27)
This intersection is itself inductive and is contained in every inductive set, by intersecting that set with I. It is therefore the set of natural numbers. If a property holds at 0 and passes from n to n+, axiom of separation forms the subset of ω on which it holds. That subset is inductive, so must equal ω. This proves mathematical induction, including properties with set parameters.
Recursion on ω defines the arithmetic operations by
m+0=m,m+n+=(m+n)+,m⋅0=0,m⋅n+=m⋅n+m.
(28)
Existence and uniqueness follow from the recursion theorem on the natural numbers.
For associativity of addition fix m,n and induct on k. At k=0 both sides equal m+n. If the equality holds at k, then
m+(n+k+)=m+(n+k)+=(m+(n+k))+=((m+n)+k)+=(m+n)+k+.
(29)
Thus
m+(n+k)=(m+n)+k.​
(30)
Every step after the induction hypothesis is a defining successor equation, so commutativity has not been used prematurely.

Use the Kuratowski ordered pair
⟨x,y⟩={{x},{x,y}}.​
(43)
The axiom of pairing first constructs {x} and {x,y}, then constructs the displayed pair of those sets, proving that it is a set.
For any a,b, the intersection of the members of this pair is {a}, and their union is {a,b}. Thus equality of two ordered pairs first gives {a}={c}, hence a=c, and then {a,b}={a,d}. If b=a, this latter set is a singleton and forces d=a. If b=a, its unique member distinct from a is b, forcing d=b. Hence equality of ordered pairs gives equality of both coordinates.
For a set R of ordered pairs, both coordinates of every pair belong to
U=⋃⋃R,
(44)

domR={x∈U:∃y⟨x,y⟩∈R},ranR={y∈U:∃x⟨x,y⟩∈R}.

The Axiom of foundation asserts that every nonempty set A has an element a with a∩A=∅. If a descending membership sequence f:ω→V existed, replacement would give the nonempty range set S={f(n):n∈ω}. Foundation would give some f(n) disjoint from S. But f(n+)∈f(n) and f(n+)∈S, contradicting that disjointness. Thus foundation rules out every such sequence.
Conversely suppose there is no descending membership sequence, but foundation fails. There is then a nonempty set A such that a∩A=∅ for every a∈A. The axiom of choice selects a function h on A with h(a)∈a∩A. Choose a0​∈A and use recursion to define

(46)

Zorn's lemma states that a nonempty partially ordered set in which every chain has an upper bound contains a maximal element. Here a chain means a subset totally ordered by the inherited order; it need not have a greatest member.
View every partial function as its graph, a subset of R×R. The family F is a set by axiom of separation from the power set of this product. Let C⊆F be a chain and put h=⋃C. If two members of the union assign values to the same input, the chain property makes one graph contain the other, so those values agree. Thus h is a partial function.
If x∈domh and q∈Q, choose a chain member containing x in its domain. Its rational-scalar closure gives qx∈domh. If x,y∈domh, choose two chain members containing them; the larger one contains both, and therefore contains x+y with value h(x)+h(y). Hence h satisfies the required additive identity and domain closure. Consequently
⋃C∈F,​

A nonempty weakly linear function has a domain D closed under rational scalars and addition, hence a vector subspace of R over Q. Additivity first gives f(0)=0 and f(−x)=−f(x), then f(nx)=nf(x) for integers n. If q=m/n with n>0, rational closure puts qx in D and
nf(qx)=f(nqx)=f(mx)=mf(x).
(59)
Thus f(qx)=qf(x): it is a Q-linear map. The empty function is not maximal because it extends to the function on {0} with value zero.
Suppose a maximal f had proper domain D=R. Choose t∈/D and any prescribed real value a, for instance a=0. Every element of D+Qt has a unique expression d+qt: if two expressions coincide and the coefficients differ, then t would belong to D. Define
h(d+qt)=f(d)+qa.
(60)
Uniqueness makes this well-defined. The displayed logical formula and the Q-linearity of f show directly that h is additive and has a domain closed under rational scalars. Hence it is a weakly linear function and extends f. It is a proper extension because t is now in its domain, contradicting maximality. Therefore
domf=Rfor every maximal f∈F.​

Its domain is a vector subspace over Q, and direct coefficient addition and rational scaling show it is Q-linear, hence weakly linear. In particular
f(1)=0,f(2​)=1.
(62)
Use Zorn's lemma on the nonempty family of weakly linear extensions of this particular function. Chain unions remain extensions by (a); hence there is a maximal extension h. Any further extension in F would also contain the original function, so this h is maximal in F. Part (b) therefore gives domh=R.
A real-linear map R→R is multiplication by its value at 1. Were h real-linear, it would satisfy
h(2​)=2​h(1)=0,
(63)
contradicting the inherited value h(2​)=1. Thus a weakly linear function on all of R need not be real-linear. Its rational homogeneity of an additive function is insufficient for real homogeneity.
