Beta distribution (Beta(α,β))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Probability distribution
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 6
The beta distribution is an absolutely continuous probability distribution for a random variable X between zero and one. It can describe uncertainty about a probability, such as an unknown coin's chance of heads. For any real α,β>0, its probability density function is
fX​(x)=B(α,β)xα−1(1−x)β−1​,0<x<1,
(12)
and zero outside [0,1]. The normalizer is the Euler beta function, related to the gamma function by the beta-gamma identity:
B(α,β)=∫01​xα−1(1−x)β−1dx=Γ(α+β)Γ(α)Γ(β)​.
(13)
Thus the probability density function integrates to one. [ref].
The endpoint behavior explains why the restrictions are exactly α,β>0: near zero the probability density function behaves like a constant times xα−1, and near one like a constant times (1−x)β−1. These improper integrals converge for positive parameters, even when the probability density function diverges at zero for α<1 or at one for β<1. There is no probability mass at either endpoint: P(X=0)=P(X=1)=0. A probability density function's height is not a probability; an interval's probability is the area under it.
Some useful examples of the beta distribution are:
  • Beta(1,1) is the uniform distribution on (0,1), with probability density function fX​(x)=1.
  • Beta(2,2) has probability density function fX​(x)=6x(1−x): values near one half are favored, and it is a symmetric probability distribution about one half.
  • Beta(2,5) has probability density function fX​(x)=30x(1−x)4. Its mean is 2/7 and its variance is 10/(49⋅8)=5/196≈0.0255102; most of its area lies toward the lower end.
  • Beta(1/2,1/2) is the arcsine distribution, with probability density function fX​(x)=1/(πx(1−x)​). It favors values near both endpoints despite having mean 1/2; its variance is 1/8.
The arcsine distribution curve is plotted only for 0.001≤x≤0.999; its finite plotted ends do not represent finite endpoint values of the probability density function. Using the inverse sine, the shaded central interval has exact probability P(0.1<X<0.9)=1−4arcsin(0.1​)/π≈0.5903, calculated analytically rather than by treating the cropped curve as normalized.
To separate location from spread, write κ=α+β and m=α/κ. The mean and variance of the beta distribution are
E[X]=m=κα​,Var(X)=κ2(κ+1)αβ​=κ+1m(1−m)​.
(14)
The mean specifies the balance between the parameters; their sum κ controls concentration about that mean. Multiplying both parameters by the same positive number preserves the mean and changes the variance. Larger κ puts more area near m and makes the probability density function taller there; the total area stays one. Small κ allows much greater spread, sometimes favoring the endpoints rather than the mean itself.
For example, keeping mean m=0.3 and taking κ=5,20,80 gives the beta distributions Beta(1.5,3.5), Beta(6,14), and Beta(24,56). Their variances are 0.035, 0.01, and 0.21/81≈0.00259259, respectively. Parameters need not be integers.
The formulas follow from two short moment calculations. Multiplying the probability density function by x or x2 increases the first parameter of the Euler beta function:
E[X]=B(α,β)B(α+1,β)​=κα​,E[X2]=B(α,β)B(α+2,β)​=κ(κ+1)α(α+1)​.
(15)
Apply the Gamma function recurrence to the beta-gamma identity, then subtract (E[X])2 from E[X2] to obtain the variance.
The beta distribution is especially convenient as a prior distribution for the unknown success probability P of Bernoulli trials. The Beta-binomial conjugacy says that h successes and t failures update a prior distribution Beta(α,β) to a posterior distribution Beta(α+h,β+t). For example, suppose a coin has prior distribution Beta(2,2) and we observe eight heads and two tails. Its posterior distribution is then Beta(10,4), with posterior mean 5/7. [ref].
