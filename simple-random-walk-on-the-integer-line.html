Simple random walk on the integer line (Sn​=2Bn​−n)

 ... Probability and statistics Probability theory Markov process Markov chain Random walk Random walk on a graph
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 10
A simple random walk on the integer line starts at S0​=0 and takes independent and identically distributed random variables ξj​ as unit increments, with
P(ξj​=+1)=p,P(ξj​=−1)=1−p,Sn​=∑j=1n​ξj​,0<p<1.
(24)
The simple symmetric random walk has p=1/2; a biased random walk has p=1/2. At p=0 or p=1 every step is deterministic, giving Sn​=−n or Sn​=n respectively.
Count the rightward steps using indicator functions:
Bn​=∑j=1n​1{ξj​=+1}​,Bn​∼Binomial(n,p),Sn​=Bn​−(n−Bn​)=2Bn​−n.
(25)
Thus the endpoint is a shifted and rescaled random variable with a binomial distribution. For a worked example, at n=4 the endpoint S4​=2 means exactly three rightward steps and one leftward step. There are (34​)=4 possible orders, so its probability is 4p3(1−p), equal to 1/4 in the fair case. More generally the binomial coefficient counts the orders, giving the exact probability mass function
P(Sn​=s)=⎩⎨⎧​((n+s)/2n​)p(n+s)/2(1−p)(n−s)/2,0,​s∈{−n,−n+2,…,n},otherwise.​
(26)
In particular, the endpoint must have the same integer parity as n: Sn​≡n(mod2). For 0<p<1 the support of a probability distribution is exactly {−n,−n+2,…,n}. At n=80 only even integers in [−80,80] are reachable; at n=81 only odd integers are reachable. This restriction is easy to lose if one draws a smooth curve instead of discrete probability masses. The fair-walk counting argument is also presented in Grinstead and Snell, Introduction to Probability, section 12.1.
The endpoint identity and the binomial distribution give the expected value, variance, and standard deviation
ESn​=n(2p−1),Var(Sn​)=4np(1−p),σn​=2np(1−p)​.
(27)
A short direct check gives Eξj​=2p−1 and Eξj2​=1, hence Var(ξj​)=1−(2p−1)2=4p(1−p). Then apply linearity of expectation and the variance of a sum of independent random variables.
The absolute width and the width relative to the number of steps answer different questions. In the fair case the standard deviation grows from 20​≈4.472 to 80​≈8.944, while σn​/n shrinks from 0.2236 to 0.1118. At n=80,p=0.65, the standard deviation is 72.8​≈8.532, or 0.9539 times the fair width, and σn​/n≈0.1067. Centering and dividing by each walk's own standard deviation gives, for n>0,
Zn​=2np(1−p)​Sn​−n(2p−1)​,EZn​=0,Var(Zn​)=1.
(28)
This rescaling equalizes the variances, not the entire probability distributions. The spacing between reachable endpoints is two, so its rescaled spacing is 2/σn​: approximately 0.4472 for the fair 20-step walk, 0.2236 for the fair 80-step walk, and 0.2344 for the biased 80-step walk.
The positions themselves are dependent. For 1≤m≤n, Sn​=Sm​+(Sn​−Sm​), where the second term is independent of Sm​. Their covariance is therefore
Cov(Sm​,Sn​)=Var(Sm​)=4mp(1−p)>0.
(29)
For example, in the fair case S1​=1 allows S2​=0 or 2, whereas S1​=−1 allows S2​=−2 or 0. Nevertheless the position process is a Markov chain: given its current position, the next fresh increment has no dependence on its past. Its transition probabilities are
P(x,y)=⎩⎨⎧​p,1−p,0,​y=x+1,y=x−1,otherwise.​
(30)
These transition probabilities are the same at every time and every integer position: shifting both x and y by the same integer leaves P(x,y) unchanged. Thus the Markov property and independent increments coexist with dependent positions.
