Gravity-capillary wave (ω2=gk+(γ/ρ)k3)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Fluid mechanics Surface tension Capillary wave
OurBigBook.com Words: 522 Articles: 1
A gravity-capillary wave has restoring contributions from both gravity and surface tension. Consider an initially resting, horizontally unbounded liquid layer of constant depth h, with uniform mass density ρ, incompressible flow, inviscid flow and irrotational flow. The upper gas exerts constant pressure and has negligible inertia. Assume a clean interface with constant surface tension γ: no surfactant stresses, imposed current or wind. The wave amplitude a must satisfy ka≪1 and a≪h, so linearized boundary conditions apply. Deep water means kh≫1, rather than a particular depth in metres. [ref]
Let z=0 be the undisturbed free surface, with liquid below it. Potential flow gives a velocity potential ϕ with u=∇ϕ, and incompressible flow gives the Laplace equation. The linearized kinematic boundary condition for a free-surface graph and the Unsteady Bernoulli equation, including gravity and the Young–Laplace equation, give
ϕxx​+ϕzz​=0(z<0),ηt​=ϕz​,ϕt​+gη−ργ​ηxx​=0(z=0).
(16)
Here η(x,t) is the interface displacement; the signed small-slope pressure jump is pliquid​−pgas​=−γηxx​. A wave crest has ηxx​<0, so its capillary pressure is positive. Choose a right-travelling Fourier mode of positive wavenumber k and angular frequency ω:
η=acos(kx−ωt),ϕ=kaω​ekzsin(kx−ωt).
(17)
The factor ekz decays into the deep liquid, satisfies the Laplace equation, and makes ϕz​=ηt​ at z=0. Substituting in the dynamic boundary condition leaves
−kaω2​+ga+ργ​k2a=0,ω2=gk+ργ​k3​.
(18)
This derivation displays the two restoring contributions with the same sign. Dimensional analysis also checks the result: γ/ρ has units m3s−2, so both gk and (γ/ρ)k3 have units s−2. [ref]
At finite depth, a flat impermeable bottom at z=−h requires ϕz​=0. Replacing ekz by the normalized vertical factor cosh(k(z+h))/cosh(kh) makes the surface ratio ϕz​/ϕ=ktanh(kh). Eliminating the mode amplitudes then gives
ω2=(gk+ργ​k3)tanh(kh).
(19)
Thus the same model supplies its own depth check: use the deep-water formula only when tanh(kh) is sufficiently close to one for the required accuracy. For example, kh=3 reduces ω by about 0.248% relative to deep water, while kh=1 reduces it by about 12.7%. These percentages are calculations from this factor. [ref]
In deep water the phase velocity magnitude follows a wave crest, while the group velocity magnitude follows the wave envelope of a narrow wave packet. Differentiating the positive-frequency dispersion relation gives
cp​=kω​=kg​+ργk​​,cg​=dkdω​=2gk+γk3/ρ​g+3γk2/ρ​,cp​cg​​=2(1+(kℓc​)2)1+3(kℓc​)2​.
(20)
For kℓc​≪1, the surface gravity wave limit has cg​≃cp​/2. For kℓc​≫1, the deep-water capillary-wave dispersion relation has cg​≃3cp​/2: a wave crest slips backward relative to the faster wave envelope, even while both advance. Neither speed is the oscillating fluid element's own velocity. [ref]
There is a particularly neat coincidence at the crossover. Differentiate cp2​=g/k+γk/ρ: its unique minimum is at kc​=ρg/γ​=1/ℓc​, exactly where the restoring terms are equal. Therefore
cp,min​=(ρ4gγ​)1/4,cg​(kc​)=cp​(kc​).
(21)
For the stipulated parameters in capillary length, the minimum is 0.231ms−1. At λ=3mm, direct substitution gives f=ω/(2π)≃131Hz, cp​≃0.394ms−1 and cg​≃0.580ms−1. This short wavelength is capillary dominated, but its group velocity is still slightly below the pure-capillary ratio 3cp​/2 because gravity has not vanished.
