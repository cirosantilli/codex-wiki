Free fall

 Home Physics Branches of physics Classical mechanics Newton's law of universal gravitation
OurBigBook.com Words: 863 Articles: 5
Free fall is motion under gravity alone: there is no supporting normal force, engine thrust or appreciable drag force. In the Newtonian mechanics model, a test particle of mass m obeys mr¨=mg(r), hence r¨=g(r). The mass cancels. Bodies released at the same position with the same velocity follow the same path in this model, even if their gravitational weights differ. [ref]
A dropped stone is one example; a coasting satellite is another. Free fall does not require motion straight down or decreasing distance to the attracting body. An ideal circular orbit has constant radius while its velocity continually turns under centripetal acceleration. The circular orbit as continuous free fall connects the familiar drop with orbital motion.
Near a surface, treating the gravitational acceleration as constant gives a useful first prediction. Over larger heights, the inverse-square law changes the acceleration during the fall. Radial free fall from rest uses conservation of mechanical energy to account for that change exactly, within the specified Newtonian gravitational field. The contrast matters: the simplest formula is an approximation to a physical model, rather than a law with the same numerical g everywhere.
History also makes the idealization tangible. Galileo's Two New Sciences (1638), Third Day, Theorem II, derives distance proportional to the square of time for uniformly accelerated motion from rest; its first corollary gives successive distances in the ratios 1,3,5,7,… over equal time intervals. This is a kinematic statement, predating the gravitational inverse-square law. [ref]
On Apollo 15's final lunar surface excursion, David Scott released a hammer and a feather together. The near-vacuum removed the large difference in drag force familiar on Earth. NASA's report describes their arrival together within the accuracy of the release; this demonstration illustrates the free fall prediction without establishing infinite experimental precision. [ref]
