Initially small ripples grow into pronounced intensity peaks and deplete the uniform background. Dispersion and self-phase modulation can then support localized bright pulses. A fundamental temporal bright soliton obeys γPs​T02​=∣β2​∣, so its width is comparable to 1/Ωc​ when its peak power is comparable to the original power. The dominant ripple spacing is 2π/∣Ω∣max​, not simply 1/Ωc​. Propagation distance z is the evolution variable; T measures time within the envelope. An ideal conservative fibre need not settle permanently into a unique pulse train: nonlinear compression, interactions and recurrence depend on the seed and boundary conditions.

A degenerate optical parametric amplifier sends a strong pump at 2ω through a phase-matched second-order nonlinear medium, creating pairs in a selected mode near ω. With the pump treated classically and undepleted, the effective interaction is quadratic in the mode's creation and annihilation operators. Vacuum input evolves into an optical squeezed vacuum; the pump phase fixes the quiet optical quadrature. A cavity enhances the interaction below oscillation threshold. balanced homodyne detection compares this optical quadrature's noise with the blocked-signal vacuum reference; phase locking, detector balance, electronic-noise measurement and loss calibration distinguish squeezing from an instrumental noise reduction. A primary cavity experiment observed below-vacuum noise by degenerate down conversion and balanced homodyne readout.[ref]

An optical soliton maintains a localized profile because nonlinear refraction compensates linear spreading. A temporal optical soliton balances group velocity dispersion against self-phase modulation; a spatial optical soliton balances diffraction against self-focusing. The relevant coordinate and sign of nonlinearity must be specified.
For anomalous group velocity dispersion and positive γ, a fundamental temporal bright soliton is
A(z,T)=Ps​​sech(T/T0​)eiγPs​z/2,γPs​T02​=∣β2​∣.​
(13)
The identity (sechu)′′=sechu−2sech3u verifies both the width relation and propagation phase. Its dispersion length LD​=T02​/∣β2​∣ equals its nonlinear length LNL​=1/(γPs​). Bright pulses decay to zero background. dark solitons are intensity depressions on a nonzero background with a phase change across the dip; black dips reach zero intensity, while grey dips do not. For positive γ the corresponding temporal defocusing regime has normal group velocity dispersion.
A higher-order temporal soliton has soliton order Ns​=γPs​T02​/∣β2​∣​ equal to an integer greater than one for the special initial Ns​sech profile in normalized units. It breathes through compression and splitting/recombination, rather than keeping one fixed shape. Separated fundamental solitons can interact and acquire collision shifts. An arbitrary pulse with noninteger Ns​ is not automatically a pure higher-order soliton; dispersive radiation can carry part of its energy.
In ideal one-dimensional cubic propagation, dispersion and nonlinearity support stable fundamental bright pulses. Loss, higher-order dispersion, delayed nonlinear response and finite bandwidth change real-fibre propagation. A one-dimensional spatial bright solution also does not guarantee stability against two-dimensional collapse. Profile preservation, finite-energy background conditions and robustness should be checked rather than inferred from any solitary-looking peak.

A detector too slow to resolve a femtosecond field can measure its integrated nonlinear response instead. Split the pulse, delay one replica, overlap both in a thin phase-matched crystal, and select the sum-frequency signal. For negligible depletion and sufficiently broad phase matching, the cross-generated field is proportional to A(t)A(t−τ); integrated signal gives the second-harmonic intensity autocorrelation
C(τ)∝∫∣A(t)A(t−τ)∣2dt=∫I(t)I(t−τ)dt.
(14)
A noncollinear geometry can reject each replica's individual second harmonic. The optical response supplies the fast gate while the detector integrates slowly. Crystal thickness, group-velocity mismatch, spectral acceptance, beam overlap and delay calibration limit resolution.
For a Gaussian pulse specified by its intensity profile, the autocorrelation's full width at half maximum is 2​ times the intensity full width at half maximum. For a sech-squared intensity profile the ratio is about 1.543. These are model-dependent conversion factors. An intensity autocorrelation is symmetric in delay and loses phase information; different pulses can share it. An interferometric autocorrelation retains rapid carrier fringes but still does not generally reconstruct an arbitrary complex field uniquely.
frequency-resolved optical gating also resolves the gated signal's spectrum, giving a two-dimensional delay-frequency record. For second-harmonic frequency-resolved optical gating,
IFROG​(ω,τ)∝​∫A(t)A(t−τ)e−iωtdt​2,
(15)
where ω is measured relative to the signal's carrier. An iterative retrieval seeks an envelope whose predicted record matches the measurement. Alternatively, polarization-gating frequency-resolved optical gating uses the optical Kerr effect to gate the probe by a delayed replica's intensity, with signal envelope proportional to A(t)∣A(t−τ)∣2. The original FROG work introduced nonlinear optical gating plus spectral resolution to characterize arbitrary femtosecond pulses.[ref]
Absolute wave phase and absolute time origin remain undetermined by these intensity records. The SHG record also cannot distinguish A(t) from A∗(−t), so its time-direction ambiguity remains. Retrieval agreement, an independently measured spectrum, adequate signal dynamic range and gate-response calibration are practical consistency checks. A pulse train that changes between shots can yield an averaged record that no single pulse faithfully represents. Thus nonlinear gating solves the detector-speed problem but does not remove inverse problem ambiguities or experimental limitations.

For X=(a+a†)/2 and Y=(a−a†)/(2i), [X,Y]=i/2, so ΔXΔY≥1/4. Vacuum and coherent states have variance 1/4 in either optical quadrature. quadrature squeezing means that some rotated optical quadrature has variance below this reference, with compensating noise elsewhere. A reduced second moment about zero is not the correct test when the field's mean is displaced.
For S(ζ)=exp[(ζ∗a2−ζa†2)/2] and ζ=reiθ, the quiet principal axis of the optical squeezed vacuum is at angle θ/2 under the squeeze-operator convention used above, and
VarXθ/2​=41​e−2r,VarXθ/2+π/2​=41​e2r.​
(16)
The pure Gaussian quantum state saturates the uncertainty product. Displacement moves its centre without changing its covariance ellipse; squeezed thermal states instead have a larger uncertainty product. The optical squeezed vacuum has zero mean field, even photon-number parity and mean occupation sinh2r; it need not have sub-Poissonian photon statistics. number squeezing and quadrature squeezing are different conditions.
Degenerate parametric amplification generates single-mode squeezing; a two-mode squeezed vacuum instead has correlated occupations and joint quadrature squeezing. Either individual mode of an ideal two-mode state is thermal after the other is ignored, not individually squeezed. balanced homodyne detection measures the noise versus local oscillator phase. Reference phase drift admixes the anti-squeezed optical quadrature, while electronic noise and detector imbalance need separate checks.
For an optical loss channel with efficiency η and vacuum entering the unused port, optical squeezing degradation by loss gives
Vout​=ηVin​+(1−η)/4.​
(17)
The linear-loss limit follows directly by writing the output field as η​a+1−η​v with independent vacuum mode v. Loss therefore places a floor under measured squeezing. Squeezed input can improve phase-sensitive measurements when aligned with the measured optical quadrature; it does not reduce every noise source or both conjugate uncertainties at once.
