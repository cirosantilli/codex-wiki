A finite beam has an intensity gradient across its wavefront. In an instantaneous optical Kerr model, n(r)=n0​+n2​I(r). A bright centre therefore sees a larger index for n2​>0, accumulating more phase than the wings and producing self-focusing. Negative n2​ gives self-defocusing. Propagation combines diffraction with the nonlinear phase. Choose ∣A∣2=I and a scalar paraxial envelope, giving
i∂z​A+2k1​∇⊥2​A+γ∣A∣2A=0,γ=k0​n2​.

The diffraction length is of order kw2, while the nonlinear length is 1/(∣γ∣I0​). Their competition depends on total beam power as well as spot size. In two transverse dimensions, balancing them gives a critical power scale Pcr​∼λ02​/(n0​n2​) for positive n2​; the numerical constant requires a specified transverse profile and critical solution. In the ideal unsaturated cubic model, sufficiently strong focusing can collapse rather than form a stable beam. Real saturation, absorption, ionization or nonparaxial propagation can stop that ideal singularity; the appropriate mechanism depends on the material and pulse regime.
A broad focusing beam can also break into filaments. Perturb the uniform solution A0​eiγI0​z by real amplitude and phase components with transverse wave number q. Linearization gives the transverse modulational instability rate
g2=2kq2​(2γI0​−2kq2​).​
(11)
For positive γ, modes with 0<q2<4kγI0​ grow. diffraction suppresses very fine perturbations. A finite beam's boundaries and available propagation length limit which filaments develop. The defocusing sign has no growing band in this simple instantaneous local model.
A spatial optical soliton instead has a profile whose nonlinear focusing balances diffraction. A one-transverse-dimensional cubic focusing model supports a stationary bright solution; the existence of that solution does not establish robust two-dimensional cubic self-trapping. A beam can also act on another finite beam through cross-phase modulation, producing an induced lens or steering it towards a high-index region. Unequal polarization response can rotate or reshape the profile. Temporal self-phase modulation is a related consequence of a time-dependent intensity, but should be distinguished from these transverse effects.
For an experimental check, vary power at fixed input waist and compare near- and far-field profiles with a low-power diffraction reference. Repeat with a second beam blocked to separate cross-phase modulation from self-action. Vary pulse duration or repetition rate to test thermal lensing against a prompt electronic response, and monitor transmission to distinguish nonlinear refraction from preferential absorption. A smaller output spot alone does not establish a soliton or a pure Kerr response.
