Frequency-resolved optical gating characterizes an ultrashort pulse using a nonlinear gate and a measured two-dimensional spectrogram. In second-harmonic frequency-resolved optical gating, split the pulse into two replicas, vary their relative delay, overlap them noncollinearly in a thin second-harmonic crystal, and spectrally resolve the cross-generated beam. Its propagation direction separates it from the individual beams' self-generated harmonics. For complex pulse envelope E(t), the ideal trace is
IFROG​(Ω,τ)∝​∫E(t)E(t−τ)e−iΩtdt​2,​
(12)
where Ω denotes the output frequency offset in the envelope convention. An iterative phase retrieval algorithm seeks a pulse consistent with both this measured Fourier magnitude and the nonlinear product relation. The delay axis provides timing structure, while the resolved spectrum adds information missing from a plain intensity autocorrelation.[ref]
The measured trace does not fix absolute optical phase or absolute time origin. SHG FROG also has a conjugate time-reversal ambiguity: E(t) and E∗(−t) produce the same trace. Extra information, such as a known dispersion change and a second measurement, can resolve that direction-of-time ambiguity. Finite phase-matching bandwidth, group-velocity mismatch, spatial chirp, spectrometer calibration, delay error, noise and instability over the scan must be controlled. A thin gate helps avoid temporal blurring, but trades against signal. FROG therefore reconstructs a pulse under an explicit instrument model rather than reading optical phase directly from a single intensity image.
