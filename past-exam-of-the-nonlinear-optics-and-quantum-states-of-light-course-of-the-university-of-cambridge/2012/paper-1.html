Second-harmonic generation doubles optical frequency, hence halves the vacuum wavelength. It requires a nonzero effective second-order nonlinear optical susceptibility for the selected fields, overlap in the nonlinear medium, and enough intensity and interaction length for a useful signal. Bulk electric-dipole second-order response vanishes in an inversion-symmetric medium. Low absorption at both frequencies and phase matching prevent generated amplitudes from being lost or canceling during propagation. Pulses additionally need suitable temporal overlap and bandwidth acceptance.
For the stated lossless amplitude equations, choose real α, input A0​=∣A0​∣eiϕ and no second-harmonic seed. Perfect phase matching admits
Aω​(z)=A0​sech(λz),A2ω​(z)=i∣A0​∣e2iϕtanh(λz),λ=α∣A0​∣.​
(1)
The second equation follows by substituting the first into Aω′​=iαA2ω​Aω∗​. Its derivative then equals iαA02​sech2(λz), verifying the other coupled equation. Equivalently, λ2=α2∣A0​∣2; changing the sign of λ does not change the pump profile. The intensity convention in the question therefore gives
I2ω​(z)=I0​tanh2(αI0​​z),Iω​(z)=I0​sech2(αI0​​z).​
(2)
Their sum is I0​, as follows directly from the supplied equal-coupling equations. This uses their normalization; physically normalized photon-flux equations can place frequency factors in the two coupling coefficients. A nonzero input harmonic would require a different boundary-value solution.
For an undepleted pump at nonzero mismatch, direct integration gives
A2ω​(z)=iαA02​zeiΔkz/2sinc(Δkz/2),I2ω​(z)=α2I02​z2sinc2(Δkz/2).
(3)
Here sincx=sinx/x. The perfectly matched small-signal curve grows quadratically and its depleted continuation saturates. For ΔkL=π, destructive phase accumulation reduces output; in the undepleted limit its endpoint is 4/π2 of the matched small-signal output. For ∣Δk∣L≫π, successive regions alternately generate and cancel harmonic amplitude, giving rapid low-amplitude oscillations with spatial period 2π/∣Δk∣. For substantial depletion, the complete curve also depends on αI0​​L; specifying mismatch alone does not determine a unique numerical sketch.
For the separated crystals, use the undepleted approximation and perfect matching inside each crystal. Up to a common output phase, the harmonic generated in the first crystal contributes iαA02​Leik(2ω)d, while the second crystal contributes iαA02​Le2ik(ω)d. The different phases come from propagation of an already generated harmonic versus propagation of two pump factors through the gap. Ideal antireflection coatings remove the multiple-reflection complication. Thus
I2ωout​(d)=4α2I02​L2cos2[2Δkg​d​],Δkg​=c2ω​[n(ω)−n(2ω)].​
(4)
This is two-crystal second-harmonic interference. Maxima require Δkg​d=2πm. With nonzero dispersion, their spacing is 2π/∣Δkg​∣=λ0​/[2∣n(ω)−n(2ω)∣], where λ0​ is the pump vacuum wavelength. If the gap has no index difference between these frequencies, every separation is constructive within this ideal propagation model.
Finite depletion makes the amplitude entering the second crystal smaller and permits coherent backconversion, so the simple cosine-squared envelope no longer applies and the total converted intensity cannot exceed I0​. The gap phase remains periodic. In the ideal identical lossless case the constructive setting is equivalent to a matched crystal of length 2L, giving I0​tanh2(2αI0​​L). The opposite relative phase can make the second crystal reverse the first conversion, restoring the pump and leaving zero harmonic. Thus depletion does not necessarily fill an ideal destructive minimum; unequal crystals, loss or other phase errors can spoil that cancellation.

A coherent pump has a Poisson distribution of photon numbers. Second-harmonic conversion couples a pump number state ∣n⟩ to a state with two fewer pump photons with a matrix element proportional to n(n−1)​. Different number components therefore evolve at different rates, and the pump becomes correlated with the harmonic mode. Its reduced state is generally neither pure nor exactly Poisson coherent. An undepleted classical-pump approximation suppresses precisely this backaction; it cannot prove that the exact finite-depletion quantum state stays coherent. Vacuum input or zero interaction are trivial exceptions.
Let A=a†2, B=a2 and N=a†a. The oscillator algebra gives [A,B]=−4N−2, [N,A]=2A and [N,B]=−2B. Substituting into the definitions proves closure:
[K1​,K2​]=−4iK3​,[K2​,K3​]=iK1​,[K3​,K1​]=iK2​.​
(5)
The factors depend on the stated normalization: K3​ has an extra factor one-half relative to N+1/2. The Robertson uncertainty relation consequently gives
(ΔK1​)2(ΔK2​)2≥41​∣⟨[K1​,K2​]⟩∣2=4⟨K3​⟩2.
(6)
For a coherent state with n=∣α∣2, normal ordering gives
⟨AB⟩=n2,⟨BA⟩=n2+4n+2,⟨A2⟩=α∗4,⟨B2⟩=α4.
(7)
Thus ⟨K1​⟩=Reα2 and ⟨K2​⟩=−Imα2. In either variance, the fourth-power and n2 terms cancel against the square of the mean, leaving
(ΔK1​)2=(ΔK2​)2=n+21​,⟨K3​⟩=21​(n+21​).​
(8)
The variance product is exactly 4⟨K3​⟩2, so the coherent state saturates the stated bound.
Amplitude-squared squeezing in either of these axes means
(ΔKj​)2<2∣⟨K3​⟩∣=⟨N⟩+21​(j=1 or 2).​
(9)
The other variance must then rise sufficiently to respect the product inequality. A rotated field-squared quadrature can reveal squeezing in another direction; the criterion concerns the declared quadrature and reference, not merely a reduction in mean optical intensity.

For stationary photodetection, the normalized correlation in second-order optical coherence is g(2)(τ)=⟨E−(t)E−(t+τ)E+(t+τ)E+(t)⟩/⟨E−E+⟩2. Normal ordering expresses the absorption of two photons. In a single mode,
g(2)(0)=⟨N⟩2⟨N(N−1)⟩​=1+⟨N⟩2VarN−⟨N⟩​.
(10)
A coherent state has g(2)(0)=1. A single-mode thermal field has g(2)(0)=2 and bunches, while a number state with n>0 gives 1−1/n; a single-photon state has zero ideal coincidence probability at zero delay. Antibunching, a zero-delay suppression relative to nearby delays, is distinct from simply reporting an intensity variance. Multimode averaging and finite detector response reduce observed contrast.
The Hanbury Brown and Twiss effect is measured by splitting the light onto two detectors and histogramming coincidences versus delay. Background counts, detector timing, dead time and afterpulsing need calibration. Two detectors avoid confusing the single detector's inability to register arbitrarily close counts with real antibunching. First-order interference and second-order correlations contain different information: weak first-order coherence does not imply an absence of intensity bunching.[ref]

For a second-order response, Pi(2)​(ω3​)=ϵ0​∑jk​χijk(2)​(ω3​;ω1​,ω2​)Ej​(ω1​)Ek​(ω2​), with the appropriate frequency conservation and negative-frequency convention for difference-frequency processes. Interchanging the two input field-frequency pairs gives intrinsic permutation symmetry of nonlinear susceptibility:
χijk(2)​(ω3​;ω1​,ω2​)=χikj(2)​(ω3​;ω2​,ω1​).
(11)
For second-harmonic generation the input frequencies agree, so the last two Cartesian indices are symmetric and can be contracted into six input-pair labels. This reduces the unrestricted 27 Cartesian entries to at most 18 before crystal symmetries are imposed.
Crystal point-group transformations must leave the material tensor invariant and can force entries to vanish or become related. Under spatial inversion both electric field and polarization reverse, whereas the field product is even. A centrosymmetric bulk therefore has zero electric-dipole χ(2). Surfaces and interfaces can break inversion symmetry, and higher-multipole processes are outside that simple prohibition.
Kleinman symmetry is a stronger approximate permutation symmetry used sufficiently far from resonances when dispersion can be neglected over the participating frequencies. It is not justified merely because two input frequencies are equal; resonant or appreciably dispersive media need the full frequency-dependent relations. These distinctions determine which polarization and propagation geometries have nonzero effective coupling, in addition to whether they satisfy phase matching.[ref]

Frequency-resolved optical gating characterizes an ultrashort pulse using a nonlinear gate and a measured two-dimensional spectrogram. In second-harmonic frequency-resolved optical gating, split the pulse into two replicas, vary their relative delay, overlap them noncollinearly in a thin second-harmonic crystal, and spectrally resolve the cross-generated beam. Its propagation direction separates it from the individual beams' self-generated harmonics. For complex pulse envelope E(t), the ideal trace is
IFROG​(Ω,τ)∝​∫E(t)E(t−τ)e−iΩtdt​2,​
(12)
where Ω denotes the output frequency offset in the envelope convention. An iterative phase retrieval algorithm seeks a pulse consistent with both this measured Fourier magnitude and the nonlinear product relation. The delay axis provides timing structure, while the resolved spectrum adds information missing from a plain intensity autocorrelation.[ref]
The measured trace does not fix absolute optical phase or absolute time origin. SHG FROG also has a conjugate time-reversal ambiguity: E(t) and E∗(−t) produce the same trace. Extra information, such as a known dispersion change and a second measurement, can resolve that direction-of-time ambiguity. Finite phase-matching bandwidth, group-velocity mismatch, spatial chirp, spectrometer calibration, delay error, noise and instability over the scan must be controlled. A thin gate helps avoid temporal blurring, but trades against signal. FROG therefore reconstructs a pulse under an explicit instrument model rather than reading optical phase directly from a single intensity image.
