Rocket equation

 Home Physics Branches of physics Classical mechanics Momentum
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 24
The ideal rocket equation gives the change in signed velocity produced by ejecting rocket propellant. Assume one-dimensional Newtonian mechanics in an inertial frame, no external force, and a fixed positive rocket exhaust speed u directed rearward relative to the instantaneous rocket. The rocket's instantaneous mass M includes its remaining rocket propellant; burning it gives dM<0. For initial and final masses Minitial​≥Mfinal​>0,
Mdv=−udM,Δv=vfinal​−vinitial​=ulnMfinal​Minitial​​.
(4)
Here ln is the natural logarithm, and its argument is the rocket mass ratio. This is the constant-rocket exhaust speed, zero-pressure-correction version of the ideal rocket equation. [ref]
For a toy rocket with u=2km/s, Minitial​=1000kg and rocket dry mass Mfinal​=250kg after all rocket propellant is spent, the rocket mass ratio is 4 and
Δv=2ln4km/s≃2.7725887km/s.
(5)
Burning from 1000 to 500kg gains 2ln2km/s≃1.3863km/s. Burning from 500 to 250kg gains exactly the same velocity increment, using only 250kg of rocket propellant instead of 500kg. Both burns halve the remaining mass. The second burn accelerates a lighter rocket; equal fractional mass losses give equal velocity gains.
The natural logarithm makes the rocket equation both useful and revealing: multiplying successive rocket mass ratios adds their velocity gains. Conversely, the required rocket mass ratio is the exponential function Minitial​/Mfinal​=eΔv/u. Each extra uln2 of ideal velocity gain doubles the required initial mass for fixed final mass. This explains why a larger rocket exhaust speed is valuable and why carrying ever more rocket propellant has diminishing returns. Changing the burn duration alone does not change this ideal endpoint result.
For a short proof outline, follow the rocket together with a tiny parcel about to be expelled. Before ejection, their total momentum is Mv. After ejection, the remaining rocket has mass M+dM and velocity v+dv, while the parcel has positive mass −dM and, to first order, inertial velocity v−u. Momentum conservation gives
Mv=(M+dM)(v+dv)+(−dM)(v−u),0=Mdv+udM+dMdv.
(6)
Discarding the second-order product dMdv gives Mdv=−udM; taking the integral gives Δv=−u∫Minitial​Mfinal​​dM/M. Evaluating the integral yields the natural logarithm above. This is a variable-mass system calculation: the rocket alone is not a closed system, and its momentum changes as the expelled material carries away momentum. The fixed collection consisting of the rocket and all its expelled material has conserved total momentum when external forces vanish.
Fixed rocket exhaust speed means fixed relative speed at release, not fixed inertial velocity. As the rocket's velocity v changes, newly emitted material has changing inertial velocity v−u; previously released parcels coast at their own release velocities in this ideal model. If v>u, the exhaust even moves forward in the inertial frame, although it is emitted rearward relative to the rocket. No surrounding medium is needed for this exchange of momentum.
Real flight adds gravity, drag force and possibly a pressure correction to rocket thrust. A varying rocket thrust due solely to a changing mass loss rate still gives the same ideal rocket equation if u is constant; it changes the acceleration and burn duration. A changing rocket exhaust speed instead requires Δv=−∫udM/M, and external forces require their own contribution to the velocity change. The existing constant mass-loss propulsion with quadratic drag and constant-burn rocket with ground friction illustrate such additional forces; the forward-exhaust rocket equation reverses the ejection direction and therefore the sign of the recoil.
