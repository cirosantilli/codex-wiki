Spherical wave (e±ikr/r)

Words: 40
Wave OurBigBook.com
For time dependence e−iωt, the three-dimensional radial solutions eikr/r and e−ikr/r describe outgoing and incoming spherical waves away from the origin. Their inverse-radius amplitudes make their flux through spheres independent of radius. They form the asymptotic radial ingredients of partial-wave scattering.

Waveguide

Words: 56
Wave OurBigBook.com
A waveguide confines a wave transversely while allowing propagation along a longitudinal direction. Material properties and boundary conditions select transverse normal modes, whose longitudinal dispersion relations may have cutoffs even when the bulk material is nondispersive. Examples include an optical fiber, a hollow electromagnetic waveguide, and a membrane waveguide. Guiding reduces geometric spreading along the chosen route but does not eliminate absorption, scattering or leakage.

Polarization (waves, E(t)=(Ax​cosωt,Ay​cos(ωt+δ)))

Words: 2k Articles: 14
Wave OurBigBook.com
Polarization of waves describes the direction and time-dependent geometry of a transverse wave's vector during its oscillation at a fixed position. In three spatial dimensions, a transverse wave can have two independent directions of oscillation, both perpendicular to its direction of propagation. For polarization of an electromagnetic wave, the relevant vector is the transverse electric field; for a mechanical transverse wave, it can be the transverse displacement. A purely longitudinal wave has no choice between these transverse directions, and the orientation of a static electric field is not polarization of waves.
Choose transverse axes x,y in a Cartesian coordinate system. A monochromatic wave with a fixed phase difference has the real electric field
E(t)=(Ax​cosωt, Ay​cos(ωt+δ)),Ax​,Ay​≥0,ω>0.
(1)
Here Ax​,Ay​ are wave amplitudes, ω is the angular frequency, and δ is the phase difference of the y oscillation relative to the x oscillation. The vector's tip traces its path during the wave period T=2π/ω. Both plotted coordinates are real electric field components; neither axis represents the imaginary part of a complex amplitude.
For nonzero wave amplitudes, δ=0 or π modulo 2π gives linear polarization: the vector moves back and forth along a fixed line. Equal nonzero wave amplitudes and δ=±π/2 modulo 2π give circular polarization: the tip traces a circle of constant radius. Unequal nonzero wave amplitudes with δ=±π/2 give a noncircular ellipse whose axes coincide with x,y. More generally, nonzero wave amplitudes and sinδ=0 give elliptical polarization, with circular polarization as a special case. If exactly one wave amplitude vanishes, the nonzero wave has linear polarization for every δ; if both vanish, there is no nonzero wave to assign a polarization of waves. [ref].
For example, take ω=2πs−1, so the wave period is T=1s. The choices (Ax​,Ay​,δ)=(2,1,0), (1,1,π/2) and (2,1,π/2), with electric field wave amplitudes in V/m, produce respectively the line segment Ey​=Ex​/2, the circle Ex2​+Ey2​=1 and the ellipse Ex2​/4+Ey2​=1, when coordinates are measured in V/m. At t=T/4, their tips are respectively (0,0), (0,−1) and (0,−1)V/m. The last two share a point without having the same path.
Resolving the electric field into two transverse components makes polarization of waves useful: the same frequency and wave amplitudes can describe different paths when their phase difference changes. An ideal linear polarizer selects the component along its pass axis, letting its orientation probe the direction of linear polarization through Malus's law. A fixed pair of wave amplitudes and a fixed phase difference describe one stable path; unpolarized light requires a statistical description rather than one fixed ellipse.

Degree of polarization (P=(μ1​−μ2​)/(μ1​+μ2​))

J(z)=e−γz(cosh(δz)isinh(δz)​−isinh(δz)cosh(δz)​)=e−Kz,K=(γ−iδ​iδγ​).

Elliptical polarization (Ex2​/Ax2​+Ey2​/Ay2​−2Ex​Ey​cosδ/(Ax​Ay​)=sin2δ)

Words: 457 Articles: 2
Polarization (waves) OurBigBook.com
For an elliptically polarized wave, the tip of its transverse vector traces an ellipse at a fixed point during each wave period. For polarization of an electromagnetic wave, this is the electric field vector. With the real components in polarization of waves, assume Ax​,Ay​>0 and sinδ=0. Eliminating time gives
Ax2​Ex2​​+Ay2​Ey2​​−Ax​Ay​2Ex​Ey​​cosδ=sin2δ.
(3)
A short derivation uses ϕ=ωt and the cosine addition formula to obtain Ey​/Ay​=(Ex​/Ax​)cosδ−sinϕsinδ; squaring and using the sine and cosine identity sin2ϕ=1−cos2ϕ gives the displayed equation. Outside phase quadrature, the mixed term gives a tilted ellipse; Ax​,Ay​ are component wave amplitudes and need not be its semi-major and semi-minor axes.
In phase quadrature, δ=±π/2, the mixed term vanishes. For (Ax​,Ay​)=(2,1)V/m, the ellipse is Ex2​/4+Ey2​=1, its semi-major and semi-minor axes have lengths 2 and 1V/m, and its electric field magnitude varies between 1 and 2V/m. At ϕ=π/4 with δ=π/2, the electric field is (2​,−1/2​)V/m and has magnitude 5/2​V/m. Equal wave amplitudes instead give circular polarization, a special case of elliptical polarization with constant magnitude. As δ approaches 0 or π, the ellipse collapses to a line segment and gives linear polarization; this limit is degenerate rather than a nondegenerate ellipse.
Changing δ from π/2 to −π/2 reverses traversal of the same ellipse. In the displayed x,y plot, δ=π/2 moves from (Ax​,0) toward negative y as time increases. A label such as right- or left-handed circular polarization additionally requires a stated viewing direction and naming convention: viewing along propagation and viewing against propagation reverse the apparent rotation. [ref].
The ellipse records both the relative wave amplitudes and the phase difference of two transverse oscillations. This separates a change of polarization of waves from a change of overall wave amplitude: multiplying both components by the same positive factor enlarges the ellipse while preserving its shape and orientation.
A rotating ideal linear polarizer can probe this geometry. For the 2:1 ellipse in phase quadrature, orthogonal projection onto a pass axis at angle θ gives Eout​(t)=2cosθcosωt−sinθsinωt, in V/m. Averaging its square over a wave period gives (4cos2θ+sin2θ)/2, while the incoming squared electric field magnitude averages to 5/2. Thus the transmitted fraction of light intensity is (4cos2θ+sin2θ)/5: it is 4/5 at 0∘, 1/2 at 45∘ and 1/5 at 90∘. There is no single incident line of linear polarization to insert into Malus's law; projecting the electric field and averaging its square is the appropriate generalization. This measurement cannot distinguish δ=π/2 from δ=−π/2, because reversing the traversal leaves all these squared averages unchanged.

Polarization ellipse

The polarization ellipse is the curve traced by the transverse electric field of a monochromatic wave at a fixed point. Malus's law (Iout​=Iin​cos2θ)

Words: 401 Articles: 1
Polarization (waves) OurBigBook.com
For incident light with linear polarization, an ideal linear polarizer transmits
Iout​=Iin​cos2θ,
(4)
where I is light intensity and θ is the angle between the incident electric field's line of oscillation and the polarizer's pass axis. The input and output are compared in the same medium. The classical explanation is an orthogonal projection: for a unit vector u along the pass axis,
Eout​=(Ein​⋅u)u.
(5)
The dot product reduces the transmitted wave amplitude by cosθ. For a plane electromagnetic wave in the same medium, light intensity is proportional to squared electric field wave amplitude, giving Malus's law. For example, θ=60∘ transmits one quarter of the incident light intensity, although the transmitted wave amplitude is one half. [ref].
Start with light already in linear polarization along the horizontal 0∘ axis and let its light intensity be I0​. Two ideal absorbing linear polarizers with pass axes 0∘ and 90∘ give I0​cos20∘cos290∘=0. Insert a 45∘ polarizer between them: the first leaves I0​ unchanged, the middle transmits I0​/2 with linear polarization along 45∘, and the last transmits I0​/4 with linear polarization along 90∘. Each angle in Malus's law is relative to the preceding output, so the final step uses 90∘−45∘=45∘:
Iout​=I0​cos20∘cos245∘cos2(90∘−45∘)=4I0​​.
(6)
For these ideal absorbing polarizers, the rejected electric field component transfers energy to the filter. In the three-filter arrangement the middle absorbs the fraction I0​/2, and the last absorbs I0​/4, leaving I0​/4 transmitted. Conservation of energy holds at each step. Inserting the middle polarizer changes the linear polarization arriving at the last one; it does not create light. The effect illustrates why successive orthogonal projections cannot generally be replaced by a single comparison of the first and last axes.
The initially horizontal assumption matters. For unpolarized light of light intensity I0​, the first ideal linear polarizer transmits I0​/2: averaging cos2θ over uniformly distributed incident orientations gives 1/2. The same 0∘,45∘,90∘ sequence then transmits I0​/8. This extra first-stage factor does not apply to the already horizontally polarized input in the figure.

Wave absorption

Words: 60
Wave OurBigBook.com
Wave absorption transfers energy from a propagating wave to other degrees of freedom of a medium. For a plane wave that is a monochromatic wave, with amplitude proportional to ei(k′+ik′′)z and k′′>0, its wave intensity decreases as I(z)=I(0)e−2k′′z. The intensity absorption coefficient is therefore 2k′′ when this loss is due to absorption. A decreasing transmitted wave intensity can also result from wave reflection or wave scattering, which redistribute energy rather than necessarily converting it into heat.

Wave reflection

Words: 51
Wave OurBigBook.com
Wave reflection returns part of an incident wave into the medium from which it arrived, as a consequence of an interface or spatial variation of material properties. An amplitude reflection coefficient compares the reflected and incident waves; its squared modulus gives their wave intensity ratio when both use the same medium and normalization. Reflected energy has not necessarily undergone wave absorption.

Anderson localization
Words: 1k Articles: 8
Wave propagation in a random medium OurBigBook.com
Anderson localization is the confinement of electron wave functions, or other wave modes, by interference between repeated wave scattering paths in static disorder. A localized eigenstate occupies a limited region even in a much larger sample. Disorder can therefore suppress diffusion without absorbing the wave. In 1958, Philip Warren Anderson studied random site energies and intersite coupling, finding a regime without transport. His paper develops a theoretical model; it does not report an Anderson laboratory measurement.[ref]
The distinction from ordinary multiple scattering is the retention of wave phases: alternative paths contribute probability amplitudes before their squared modulus is taken. Adding path probabilities alone loses the cross terms responsible for interference. This makes Anderson localization useful as a warning about transport models: repeated deflections need not produce unlimited diffusion. It also connects electron transport with wave propagation in a random medium, although the detailed equations and experimental diagnostics differ between electrons, light and acoustic waves.
Philip Warren Anderson's 1977 lecture distinguishes localized magnetic moments from disorder-localized states. It credits Nevill Francis Mott with recognizing the significance of the mobility edge. A Mott insulator instead requires electron interactions and can exist without disorder.[ref]
John Hasbrouck Van Vleck developed quantum mechanics explanations of magnetic susceptibility, including its temperature dependence; this is a distinct contribution from Anderson localization.[ref]

Doppler effect (Δf=fo​−fs​)

Words: 2k Articles: 12
Wave OurBigBook.com
The Doppler effect is a change between emitted and received frequency caused by motion of the source or receiver. An approaching siren can send more wave crests per second to a microphone than the source emits per second; a receding siren sends fewer. The source need not change its own oscillation. The Doppler shift is Δf=fo​−fs​, where fs​>0 is emitted frequency and fo​>0 is received frequency, both measured in hertz.
For acoustic waves, motion must be specified relative to the material carrying the wave. The speed of sound is measured in that material's rest reference frame. Moving the source changes the spacing of successive wavefronts; moving the receiver changes how quickly it crosses those wavefronts. These two mechanisms give different exact factors in the classical Doppler effect for sound, even for the same closing relative velocity.
This makes a microphone a motion probe: a stable tone supplies a repeating clock, and changes in arrival spacing reveal the source's motion toward or away from the receiver. An acoustic Doppler measurement can test the prediction directly. A passing source also illustrates why geometry matters: its speed can stay constant while the received frequency changes continuously, as in Doppler frequency along a trajectory.
The following sound model assumes linear acoustics in uniform stationary material with isotropy, constant speed of sound and negligible wave dispersion. Source and receiver speeds are below the speed of sound, and there is a single direct propagation path. Air motion requires transforming source and receiver velocities into the air's rest reference frame; a nonuniform flow requires a more general propagation model. At the sonic threshold the simple periodic-arrival picture fails; moving-point acoustic wavefronts and the Mach cone describe the relevant geometry, while finite shock waves require further physics. Light in vacuum instead requires the relativistic Doppler effect: the acoustic formula cannot be converted into its exact optical counterpart just by replacing the speed of sound with the speed of light.

These inversions need a specified speed of sound. An offset path changes the projection, as in Doppler frequency along a trajectory; neither inversion measures the full speed near closest passage. The protocol's controls cannot remove arbitrary acceleration, background measurement noise or unresolved reflected paths. No new measurements are claimed here.
