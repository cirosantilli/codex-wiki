Molecular structure from rotational constants

 Home Chemistry Molecule Molecular rotation Rotational spectroscopy
OurBigBook.com Words: 178
Recovering molecular geometry from rotational constants is an inverse problem. In the synthetic assignment, B=5×109Hz gives I=h/(8π2B)≃1.6784×10−45kgm2. For a diatomic molecule with known masses, rotational constant explains how to infer an effective bond length. For a longer linear molecule, one I cannot determine every bond length: several different arrangements can have the same moment of inertia.
For a fixed three-atom linear model with positions 0,a,a+b and positive masses m1​,m2​,m3​, subtracting the centre of mass gives
I=m1​+m2​+m3​m1​m2​a2+m2​m3​b2+m1​m3​(a+b)2​.
(14)
This is one equation for two unknown bond lengths. Even fixing the order of the chemical atoms leaves a family of solutions. Additional isotope substitutions change known masses and supply independent equations, provided the effective molecular geometry can be treated as shared. For nonlinear molecules, three principal moments of inertia give frequency-valued constants A=h/(8π2Ia​), B=h/(8π2Ib​), and C=h/(8π2Ic​); a single evenly spaced comb is generally insufficient.
Vibrational averaging, unresolved hyperfine structure, internal motion, and centrifugal distortion limit structural inference. A ground-vibrational fitted rotational constant is not automatically the equilibrium constant. Ordinary field-free rotational spectroscopy also gives identical spectra for opposite enantiomers under the usual parity-symmetric molecular Hamiltonian; assigning composition and conformation does not by itself determine handedness. Molecular identification from a rich fingerprint can therefore succeed even when a unique full molecular geometry cannot be reconstructed.
