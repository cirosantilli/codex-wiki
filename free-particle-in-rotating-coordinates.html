Free particle in rotating coordinates (rrot​=(utcosΩt,−utsinΩt))

OurBigBook.com Words: 236
Consider a ball moving horizontally with no net horizontal force in the laboratory. Treat its horizontal motion as that of a free particle: rlab​=(ut,0) with constant u>0. Axes rotating counterclockwise at constant angular velocity Ω=Ωez​ about the launch point assign it the position
rrot​=(cosΩt−sinΩt​sinΩtcosΩt​)(ut0​)=(utcosΩt−utsinΩt​).
(70)
This is an exact rotation matrix transformation, with the axes coinciding at t=0. Taking u=0.25 ms−1, Ω=0.8 rads−1, and 0≤t≤3 s gives the two views below. At t=3 s, the laboratory position is (0.75,0) m, but the rotating position is approximately (−0.5530,−0.5066) m. Both assign the same distance 0.75 m from the origin.
The entire rotating acceleration must include the centrifugal acceleration as well as the Coriolis acceleration. To see the balance explicitly, write c=cosΩt and s=sinΩt. Direct differentiation gives
vrot​=u(c−Ωts,−s−Ωtc),r¨rot​=u(−2Ωs−Ω2tc,−2Ωc+Ω2ts).
(71)
The two apparent contributions are
acor​acf​r¨rot​​=u(−2Ωs−2Ω2tc,−2Ωc+2Ω2ts),=uΩ2t(c,−s),=acor​+acf​.​
(72)
At launch r=0, so only the Coriolis acceleration contributes to the initial bending. Later the centrifugal acceleration also contributes; the full curved trajectory cannot be explained by keeping only the Coriolis force. In particular, ∣vrot​∣2=u2(1+Ω2t2) grows even though the Coriolis force does no work: the rotating-frame kinetic energy increase is supplied by work from the centrifugal force. The laboratory kinetic energy remains mu2/2.
