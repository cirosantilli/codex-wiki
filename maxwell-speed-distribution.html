Maxwell speed distribution (g(v))

The Maxwell speed distribution is the probability density function for the magnitude v=∣v∣. Unlike a signed velocity component, speed cannot be negative:

For 0≤a<b, the probability of a speed in [a,b] is ∫ab​g(v)dv, and ∫0∞​g(v)dv=1. The density has units of inverse speed, so its height alone is not a probability. [ref]
All velocities with magnitudes from v to v+dv occupy a thin spherical shell of volume 4πv2dv in velocity space. The isotropy of the Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution makes its density constant over each shell, giving g(v)=4πv2f(vex​) for any fixed unit direction ex​. A larger radius offers more directions and more volume. The increasing shell factor initially wins over the decreasing Boltzmann factor, so g(0)=0 and the most probable speed is positive. This is the geometric reason that the peaks of the vector and speed densities disagree.

Consequently the mean translational kinetic energy is m⟨v2⟩/2=3kB​T/2, as required by the equipartition theorem. [ref] The mean speed weights fast particles more than the density peak does, and the root mean square weights them more strongly still.
Changing temperature or particle mass only changes s∝T/m​. At fixed mass, four times the temperature doubles every characteristic speed; at fixed temperature, four times the mass halves them. A stretched density becomes lower so that its area stays one. For two species at the same temperature, equal mean translational kinetic energy therefore means different typical speeds.
