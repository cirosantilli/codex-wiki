Linear congruence (ax≡b(modn))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Number theory Modular arithmetic Modular congruence
OurBigBook.com Words: 256
A linear congruence asks for the integers x satisfying ax≡b(modn), where a,b are integers and n>1. Let d=gcd(a,n) be their greatest common divisor. There are solutions exactly when d divides b. When solutions exist, there are exactly d solution classes modulo n, obtained from one solution x0​ by
x≡x0​+jdn​(modn),j=0,…,d−1.
(5)
The divisibility condition follows immediately from ax−b=kn: every common divisor of a,n must divide b. Conversely, Bézout's identity constructs a solution when d∣b. Differences between any two solutions obey n∣a(x−x0​), equivalently n/d∣x−x0​, since a/d and n/d are coprime. This explains both the spacing and the number of solution classes.
For a complete example, solve 6x≡9(mod15). Here d=3 divides nine. Dividing the corresponding divisibility statement by three changes the integer modulus too:
6x≡9(mod15)⟺2x≡3(mod5).
(6)
Because 2⋅3≡1(mod5), multiplying by the modular inverse three gives x≡9≡4(mod5). Lifting this class back to modulus fifteen yields x≡4,9,14(mod15). Direct substitution gives 24, 54 and 84, each with remainder nine after division with remainder by fifteen. By contrast, 6x≡10(mod15) has no solutions, because three does not divide ten.
This is why ordinary-looking division cannot be performed blindly in modular arithmetic. Reducing the equation and its integer modulus together keeps all solutions; retaining modulus fifteen after dividing by three would lose valid classes. When d=1, the modular inverse of a exists and gives a unique class x≡a−1b(modn). The Euclidean algorithm computes the Bézout coefficients needed for this construction. [ref]
