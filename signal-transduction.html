Signal transduction

 Home Biology
OurBigBook.com Words: 9k Articles: 120
Signal transduction converts a detected signal into a change inside a biological cell. For chemical signals, distinguish reception by a receptor, intracellular signal transduction, and the response: altered gene expression, enzyme activity or ion channel opening, for example. A cell-surface receptor can relay a ligand's presence across a cell membrane without the ligand entering the cytoplasm. Thus a hydrophilic signal need not cross the membrane to influence the interior. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 9.2: intracellular signal propagation.
For a computed example of possible signal amplification, suppose one activated receptor activates five relay proteins, and each relay activates twenty target proteins during a chosen interval. If the targets are distinct and available, 1×5×20=100 targets are activated. Those assumed counts illustrate multiplication, not a measured pathway or a universal gain. Actual signal transduction can branch, saturate or inhibit activity. Changes in gene expression and changes in existing enzyme activity are different responses. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 9.3: cellular responses.
Signal amplification, signaling feedback and signaling adaptation are possible properties of signal transduction, rather than requirements of every cascade. Intracellular relays can use proteins or second messengers; different biological cells can interpret the same ligand differently because they contain different receptors and relay machinery. The useful and striking idea is that recognition and response are separable: an extracellular chemical message can control many internal tasks without itself performing those tasks. See General Principles of Cell Communication.
An original experimental route to this separation appears in T. W. Rall and Earl W. Sutherland's 1958 work on cAMP. They described a heat-stable factor formed from ATP in separated biological cell preparations; it affected enzyme activity in other preparations where the initiating chemical signals had no direct effect. This helped distinguish an initial signal from a molecule carrying its effect to another target. [ref]

Second messenger

Words: 1k Articles: 7
Signal transduction OurBigBook.com
A second messenger is a small intracellular molecule or ion that relays a received signal to target proteins. Its production, release, removal or binding changes its availability after signal reception. For a cell-surface receptor, distinguish the extracellular ligand, often called the first messenger, from the intracellular second messenger. An activated relay protein is not thereby a second messenger: the term identifies small chemical mediators, rather than every downstream component of signal transduction. [ref]
Two examples clarify what changes. A biological cell can make cAMP from ATP; alternatively, release of stored calcium ions can raise the free calcium concentration in its cytoplasm. The latter need not increase the total calcium content of the biological cell. Availability to target proteins, not merely the total amount present, carries the message. Removal and sequestration help terminate such signals. [ref] [ref]
The experimental motivation is unusually tangible: can a hormone's effect be transferred to machinery that barely responds to the hormone itself? In Rall, Sutherland and Berthet's 1957 experiment, washed cat-liver particles were incubated with ATP and magnesium ions, with or without epinephrine and glucagon, two hormones. After five minutes at 30∘C, the mixtures were heated in boiling water for three minutes, chilled, and separated to remove insoluble material. Small samples of the clarified extracts were transferred to a separate soluble dog-liver preparation, again supplied with ATP and magnesium ions. The readout was the change in glycogen phosphorylase activity over five minutes, not the amount of newly synthesized protein. [ref]
The hormone-conditioned extract caused a larger increase in glycogen phosphorylase activity. Water, extract made without hormones, and hormones added directly to the soluble preparation produced smaller effects. These comparisons distinguish transferred activity from baseline activity, preparation effects and simple hormone carryover. [ref]
The inference is a transferable, heat-stable intermediary between the hormone-sensitive particles and the soluble machinery. This does not identify the intermediary merely from heat resistance, establish its direct target, or show that every hormone uses the same mechanism. Rall and Sutherland's 1958 paper reports the factor's identification as cAMP in accompanying chemical work, and follows its production from ATP. It also reports an important limit: washed heart and skeletal-muscle particles produced detectable cAMP with epinephrine under their test conditions, whereas brain particles produced similar amounts with and without it. Tissue and preparation matter. [ref]
The useful and beautiful separation is between recognition and execution: an extracellular ligand can control intracellular enzymes through a chemically different intermediary. Making many second messenger molecules can support signal amplification, while different target proteins give the same mediator different consequences. A second messenger identifies a mode of relaying information; its presence alone does not specify the size or direction of the response. [ref]

Inositol trisphosphate (IP3​)

Words: 847 Articles: 5
Second messenger OurBigBook.com
In signal transduction, inositol trisphosphate usually means inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate: a soluble intracellular second messenger. An extracellular ligand, its cell-surface receptor, IP3 and the released calcium ions are distinct components. IP3 binds an IP3 receptor on the endoplasmic reticulum, promoting stored Ca2+ release into the cytosol.[ref]
The useful separation is spatial: an extracellular message can control an internal store without entering it. The existing intracellular-injection experiment under metabotropic receptor tests bypassing reception; the Pancreatic IP3 store fractionation experiment below tests which internal compartment supplies the released calcium. Neither question is answered merely by observing a downstream membrane electric current.

Inositol trisphosphate receptor (IP3​R)

Words: 89
Inositol trisphosphate OurBigBook.com
An IP3 receptor is an intracellular ligand-gated ion channel, rather than the extracellular signal's cell-surface receptor. Its opening is regulated by both IP3 and cytosolic calcium: IP3 binding is not a command that fixes one constant release rate. Mammalian IP3 receptors have three subtypes, with possible mixed assemblies and splice variants; these need not have identical regulation or cellular roles. The 2010 authored account distinguishes established channel behavior from then-unresolved structural explanations.[ref]

IP3-sensitive calcium store

Words: 586 Articles: 2
Inositol trisphosphate OurBigBook.com
An IP3-sensitive calcium store contains releasable calcium behind a membrane bearing functional IP3 receptors. The endoplasmic reticulum is an important example. Pumping calcium into it or exporting calcium across the cell membrane lowers free cytosolic calcium; intracellular redistribution and loss from the whole biological cell are different balances.[ref]
Finite availability matters: a release pathway cannot deliver more calcium than its accessible store contains unless replenishment occurs. A falling cytosolic calcium response can reflect reduced release, increased removal, or both. Store content, release rate and free cytosolic concentration therefore answer different questions. Binding to proteins can also change the free fraction without changing the total number of calcium ions.
Pancreatic IP3 store fractionation experiment
Words: 155
IP3-sensitive calcium store OurBigBook.com
A 1984 primary experiment localized IP3-evoked release using pancreatic acinar cells isolated from rat pancreas with collagenase. A homogenized preparation was separated by differential centrifugation, then by a sucrose step density gradient or precipitation with magnesium chloride. The readout was IP3-induced calcium release per mass of protein across fractions. Release tracked two endoplasmic reticulum markers, RNA content and an enzyme activity, across several separation methods. Marker activities for mitochondria and the cell membrane supplied contrasting comparisons: release correlated negatively with them.[ref]
Agreement across distinct fractionations supports an endoplasmic reticulum-associated source in this preparation. “Nonmitochondrial” alone only excludes a candidate; positive compartment markers add localization evidence. Marker comparisons control the localization inference, rather than replacing a no-IP3 release comparison. Fractionation may mix or damage compartments, and correlation does not itself identify the IP3 receptor protein. This calcium release readout also does not directly measure secretion or establish identical responses in every biological cell.
Finite calcium-store release model
Words: 327
IP3-sensitive calcium store OurBigBook.com
For an original mathematical model, imagine a sustained, imposed release pathway, with no store refill during the plotted interval. Let S be releasable store amount of substance, X excess freely dissolved cytosolic calcium amount of substance, and Q the cumulative amount removed from X into a separate sink. Choose S(0)=1amol, X(0)=Q(0)=0, cytosolic volume Vc​=1pL, release rate constant k=0.4s−1 and clearance rate constant h=0.8s−1. These are stipulated values, not physiological measurements or a model of IP3 receptor gating.
Assume fixed volume, uniform mixing, no binding, no reverse transfer, no outside entry and first-order loss from each donating compartment. Then
dtdS​=−kS,dtdX​=kS−hX,dtdQ​=hX,S+X+Q=S(0).
(1)
The last equality is conservation of mass for the tracked calcium across the store, mobile excess and sink together; it does not assert conservation inside a biological cell if the sink represents export. A constant background cb​=0.1μM is held separate from this excess bookkeeping. Since 1amol/1pL=1μM, the response is c=cb​+X/Vc​.
Solving these ordinary differential equations gives
S=S(0)e−kt,X=h−kkS(0)​(e−kt−e−ht),Q=S(0)−S−X.
(2)
Release kS declines from the start, yet X initially rises because delivery exceeds clearance. Its peak occurs when kS=hX, at t∗​=log(h/k)/(h−k)=log(2)/0.4≈1.73s. There X=0.25amol and c=0.35μM. Ultimately S and X tend to zero, Q tends to 1amol and c returns to cb​. The delayed peak is a consequence of material balance, even though this model has no delayed channel activation.
This distinction guides interpretation: a large released amount need not mean a large simultaneous free cytosolic concentration. Clearance and volume intervene. Real biological cells add binding, store refilling, IP3 turnover and IP3 receptor regulation; the two plotted curves isolate one finite-store effect rather than explaining every calcium response.

Signal amplification

Words: 403
Signal transduction OurBigBook.com
Signal amplification is an increase in a specified downstream response relative to an initiating signal. In a molecular-count description, one activated component can generate or activate many downstream molecules. Repeated catalysis by an enzyme supplies a mechanism: the enzyme remains available for another chemical reaction. Successive stages can multiply the response. For a biological example, Cooper describes the epinephrine-to-glycogen phosphorylase pathway, where production of many cAMP molecules and repeated downstream protein phosphorylation provide opportunities for signal amplification. This is an example, not a claim that every signal transduction pathway has the same stages or gain. [ref]
Here is a counting toy extending the example under signal transduction. Suppose N0​ activated receptors each activate a=5 distinct relay proteins during a first interval. During a subsequent interval, each relay activates b=20 distinct target proteins. Assume enough inactive relays and targets, no shared targets, and no loss of activity during the count. Then
N1​=aN0​,N2​=bN1​=abN0​.
(3)
For N0​=1, this gives N1​=5 and N2​=100: the target-to-receptor count ratio is 100. These are assumed integer counts, not measured rates or a universal physiological amplification factor. The intervals are sequential; the equations do not specify a time course.
Availability limits the multiplication. As a second toy assumption, let there be only T=250 initially inactive target proteins shared by all branches, and suppose the branches can activate distinct targets until this pool is exhausted. Then
N2​=min(abN0​,T)=min(100N0​,250).
(4)
Two activated receptors give 200 active targets, whereas three give 250, not 300. At three activated receptors, the total count ratio is 250/3≃83.3; adding a fourth activates no additional targets. Thus total output per input and the increment caused by an extra input are different notions of gain. This finite-pool rule is an illustrative bound, not an enzyme kinetics law.
Signal amplification makes a small initiating signal capable of a larger response, but it does not supply the material or energy for downstream chemical reactions. In the second messenger transfer experiment, both stages still required added ATP and magnesium ions. Counts, changes in concentration, and changes in enzyme activity are different readouts; an amplification claim should name its input, output and observation interval. [ref]

Adrenaline

Words: 581 Articles: 1
Hormone OurBigBook.com
Adrenaline, also called epinephrine, is an endogenous catecholamine messenger: the body makes it and can release it into blood as a hormone. The adrenal medulla, the inner region of an adrenal gland, secretes adrenaline in response to sympathetic nervous system stimulation. A circulating hormone broadcasts a signal, but each receiving biological cell interprets it through its own receptors and intracellular machinery. Adrenaline activates both alpha-adrenergic receptors and beta-adrenergic receptors; it does not command one identical response everywhere.[ref][ref]
Fuel mobilization is a useful example. A beta-adrenergic receptor can increase adenylyl cyclase activity through a stimulatory G protein, increasing the intracellular second messenger cAMP. Protein kinase A responds to cAMP; a phosphorylation relay through phosphorylase kinase activates glycogen phosphorylase, favoring glycogenolysis. Here adrenaline is the extracellular message, cAMP is the intracellular message, and glycogen is the reserve being mobilized. These are three different roles.[ref][ref][ref]
The two reserves provide different material. Glycogenolysis draws on glycogen; lipolysis breaks down stored triglycerides, releasing fatty acids and glycerol. A skeletal muscle biological cell's own reserve and fuel released by adipose tissue are different supplies. Increasing a signal cannot mobilize an empty reserve: receptor activation changes the use of available material rather than creating fuel. The adrenergic adipocyte assay below gives direct human evidence for the lipolysis branch.[ref]
Receptor context can even oppose this branch. In the human adipocyte preparation below, beta-adrenergic receptors promoted cAMP accumulation, whereas alpha-2 adrenergic receptors opposed it. Alpha-1 adrenergic receptors were associated with a different phospholipid readout. The balance of these responses matters more than the name of the messenger alone.[ref]
This separation is useful when explaining signal transduction: ask which receptor is present, which relay it controls, which reserve or target is available, and which response was actually measured. Receptor binding occupancy, second messenger production and final fuel release are distinct observations. A stronger cAMP signal need not imply an equally large change in every downstream process; substrate availability and the rest of the receiving biological cell still matter.
The extract-transfer experiment under second messenger supplies the earlier experimental separation between hormone reception and an intracellular intermediary. Its existing account includes the preparations and controls. The complementary adrenergic adipocyte assay asks a different question: can the same adrenaline stimulus engage opposing receptors within a human preparation?

Glucagon

Words: 371 Articles: 1
Hormone OurBigBook.com
Glucagon is a peptide hormone made by alpha cells in the pancreatic islets. Its mature polypeptide has 29 amino acid residues. Alpha cells store glucagon in secretory granules and release it by exocytosis. Glucagon is a message about fuel availability; glucose is the sugar whose supply it helps regulate. A falling blood glucose concentration favors glucagon release, whereas a rise usually suppresses it. Amino acids can also stimulate release, so a meal containing protein need not produce the same hormone pattern as a meal dominated by carbohydrates. [ref]
The principal blood glucose-raising target is the liver. In a hepatocyte, the glucagon receptor is a G protein-coupled receptor: activation of a stimulatory G protein drives adenylyl cyclase, increasing the second messenger cAMP and activating protein kinase A. Downstream phosphorylation, including the phosphorylase kinase relay to glycogen phosphorylase, favors glycogenolysis. Glucagon also supports gluconeogenesis. These are different supplies: glycogenolysis draws on a finite glycogen reserve, whereas gluconeogenesis makes glucose from other substrates and depends on their availability. Longer fasting depletes the glycogen reserve; a stronger hormone signal cannot release material that is no longer there. [ref] [ref]
Do not copy the liver pathway into every target of insulin. Skeletal muscle uses its own glycogen reserve locally; its glucose uptake and the liver's glucose output are different flows. Nor is direct glucagon-stimulated lipolysis in human adipose tissue as firmly established as its action in the liver. Glucagon action depends on the tissue, other hormones, and available substrates. [ref]
There is a useful surprise inside the pancreatic islets: local glucagon can support insulin release from neighboring beta cells. The islet perifusion experiment distinguishes that interaction from glucagon's action on the liver. Thus the two hormones can oppose each other's effects on blood glucose while cooperating locally. Delta cells supply another local brake through somatostatin. [ref] [ref]

Signaling feedback

Words: 3k Articles: 32
Signal transduction OurBigBook.com
Signaling feedback returns the effect of a downstream event to an earlier stage of signal transduction, closing a loop analogous to feedback control. Trace the causal arrows: if an earlier relay activates a later relay and the later relay changes the earlier relay's activity, the return path closes the loop. Negative feedback opposes the initiating change; positive feedback reinforces it. Signaling feedback is a possible property of signal transduction, not a requirement of every pathway.
For a schematic example, let relay A activate relay B. If B inhibits A, increased A eventually produces an opposing influence: negative feedback. If B activates A, increased A produces a reinforcing influence: positive feedback. These signs describe effects on activity, rather than the direction in which molecules travel. A closed loop containing two inhibitions can have positive feedback, because inhibiting an inhibitor reinforces the original change. The sign belongs to the whole loop.
Negative feedback can restrain excessive activation and help preserve a usable range of responses. Positive feedback can support a decisive transition: Alberts and colleagues describe an enzyme whose product increases its own activity. Such reinforcement can contribute to switchlike behavior, but a positive feedback arrow alone does not prove bistability. A different structure, an incoherent feedforward loop, sends opposing influences forward from the same input; inhibition there is not a downstream return. [ref] [ref]
A worked mathematical model shows why negative feedback does not automatically produce complete signaling adaptation. Let s be a fixed normalized input, r a normalized response, g≥0 a dimensionless feedback gain, and τ>0 a time constant. Suppose response removal contributes −r and a return proportional to the response contributes −gr. The ordinary differential equation is
τdtdr​=s−r−gr=s−(1+g)r.
(5)
Setting the derivative to zero gives the equilibrium point r∗​=s/(1+g). A displacement δr=r−r∗​ obeys τd(δr)/dt=−(1+g)δr, so its decaying exponential function establishes asymptotic stability. For a unit input switched on at t=0, initial condition r(0)=0, g=2 and τ=1 second,
r(t)=31−e−3t/τ​,r(1s)≃0.316737,limt→∞​r(t)=31​.
(6)
The negative feedback reduces the eventual response from 1 to 1/3, but this response rises monotonically and never returns to its zero baseline. Attenuation and complete signaling adaptation are different properties. This deliberately simple mathematical model specifies no molecular pathway; integral action provides a contrasting way to remove a lasting offset in a stable feedback control system. [ref]

Negative feedback

Words: 801 Articles: 14
Signaling feedback OurBigBook.com
Negative feedback closes a causal loop whose net effect opposes an initiating change. In signal transduction, activation followed by a downstream inhibition of the earlier activating stage is one example of negative feedback. Loop sign alone does not establish asymptotic stability or complete signaling adaptation; see the worked signaling feedback example.
A useful biological example is chemotaxis feedback regulation: an Escherichia coli biological cell adjusts its response to a sustained attractant instead of remaining at its immediate response. Increased CheA activity promotes CheB phosphorylation; CheB reverses receptor methylation, opposing their activation. After attractant addition, reduced activity instead favors receptor methylation by CheR, restoring activity. This is a return from receptor activity to the machinery regulating that activity. The response need not return exactly at every dose. [ref]
A motivating measurement is the chemotaxis FRET assay. CheY and CheZ carrying yellow and cyan fluorescent tags were observed in immobilized Escherichia coli biological cells on a coverslip in a flow chamber. Adding and removing attractant changed the yellow-to-cyan emission ratio, reporting the association of phosphorylated CheY with CheZ. This measures a molecular readout upstream of motor behavior. Comparisons with biological cells lacking CheR, CheB, or both tested the dependence on receptor modification; these perturbations also change starting activity and sensitivity, so they are not pure changes of one feedback gain. [ref]
The later chemotaxis feedback regulation experiment used only the Tar receptor and nonmetabolizable methylaspartate, supplied and removed by flow. Long recordings separated slow recovery from incomplete signaling adaptation; correction for bleaching addressed drift in the optical readout. Activity recovered incompletely after sufficiently large additions, with detectable imprecision already at 10μM in that preparation. Restricting the receptor type and preventing consumption of the stimulus help interpret the perturbation, but limit transfer to other receptors and conditions. The readout infers CheA activity through CheY-CheZ association, rather than directly measuring every molecular step. [ref]
Negative feedback can also overshoot or sustain oscillations if its return arrives too late. The worked signaling feedback example gives the contrasting instantaneous case; the delayed negative feedback model below isolates the effect of timing. Loop sign describes causation, while asymptotic stability describes what happens to displacements over time.

Positive feedback

Words: 2k Articles: 16
Signaling feedback OurBigBook.com
Positive feedback closes a causal loop whose net effect reinforces an initiating change. A loop with two inhibitory links can therefore have positive feedback. In signal transduction, a product that increases the activity of the enzyme making it illustrates reinforcement; the consequences depend on the full mathematical model, not just the loop sign. See signaling feedback.
A biological example is cell-cycle activation feedback. Cyclin B binds Cdc2, whose activity promotes activation of Cdc25; Cdc25 removes inhibitory phosphorylation from Cdc2, permitting further activation. Cdc2 also inhibits Wee1, an enzyme that inhibits Cdc2 through phosphorylation. That second return contains two inhibitions and therefore also has positive feedback. These are regulatory paths, not a claim that every connection is a single direct molecular encounter. [ref]
Reinforcement can preserve a decision after its initiating stimulus weakens: returning from the high state may require a lower input than entering it. The cell-cycle hysteresis experiment below tests this history dependence at matched cyclin B concentrations. A steep response alone is insufficient evidence of bistability, and finite observation times can make slow relaxation look like memory. A proposed switch must specify its input, readout and observation interval.

Saturating positive feedback

Saturating positive feedback limits the reinforcing rate as activity increases. Here is an explicitly synthetic ordinary differential equation for normalized nonnegative input u, response x, and return strength β:
dtdx​=f(x;u,β)=u+1+x2βx2​−x,u≥0,β≥0.
(11)
The return is an increasing, nonproportional function of x, while removal is proportional to x. Time and activities are dimensionless variables. At x=0 the vector field points into the nonnegative domain, and dx/dt≤u+β−x prevents unbounded growth there. This makes the toy useful for asking about a bounded switch; it supplies no molecular mechanism for the chosen rate law.
At an equilibrium point, f=0. For a simple equilibrium point, the sign of the derivative
fx​=(1+x2)22βx​−1
(12)
determines local asymptotic stability. If β=1, the maximum reinforcing slope is 33​/8<1, so f decreases strictly: every u≥0 has one asymptotically stable equilibrium point despite positive feedback.
Three equilibrium points become possible only if the reinforcing slope exceeds removal's slope somewhere. Its maximum is 33​β/8, at x=1/3​. Consequently this toy requires
β>33​8​≃1.53960
(13)
for two distinct folds. This is a necessary threshold for a bistability interval in this toy, not a universal biochemical number. At a fold, both f=0 and fx​=0, giving
(1+x2)2=2βx,u=x−1+x2βx2​.
(14)
For β=1.8, the folds are at (x,u)≃(0.350003,0.153563) and (0.887240,0.094405). Between these input values there are two asymptotically stable equilibrium points separated by an unstable one. At u=0.12, the three activities are approximately 0.171331, 0.621230 and 1.127439; the low and high ones are stable.
A sufficiently slow increasing sweep follows the low branch to uon​≃0.153563 and then jumps upward. A decreasing sweep follows the high branch to uoff​≃0.094405 and then jumps downward: hysteresis. Signaling adaptation

Words: 703
Signal transduction OurBigBook.com
Signaling adaptation is a decline of a response toward its earlier level while the initiating signal remains present. Specify which response is measured and its baseline: complete signaling adaptation means eventual return to that baseline; partial signaling adaptation leaves a lasting displacement. The continuing signal distinguishes signaling adaptation from simply switching off the stimulus. Signaling adaptation describes behavior, while signaling feedback describes a causal arrangement, so neither implies the other.
By reducing a persistent response to background ligand concentration, signaling adaptation can make a biological cell sensitive to changes rather than only to absolute levels. Resetting the response helps leave room for another change over a useful range of ligand concentrations. This is valuable in chemotaxis: swimming bacteria encounter changing chemical conditions, and their signal transduction influences whether a run continues or a tumble occurs. Signaling adaptation helps separate a change encountered during motion from a lasting background. This usefulness does not imply exact return or equal sensitivity at every possible concentration. [ref] [ref]
Here is a solvable incoherent feedforward loop toy that exhibits complete signaling adaptation without signaling feedback. The input s(t) jumps from 0 to 1 at t=0 and remains 1, a Heaviside step function. Let x be a fast activating branch, a a slower inhibitory branch, and r=x−a a readout. The activities s,x,a,r are dimensionless variables; physical time is in seconds. Choose time constants τx​=1 second and τa​=5 seconds, with initial conditions x(0)=a(0)=0:
τx​dtdx​=s−x,τa​dtda​=s−a,r=x−a.
(16)
Both branches read s directly. Neither branch depends on r, and a does not change x: the subtraction only defines the readout. Thus the slow opposing influence is feedforward, with no closed feedback control loop.
For t≥0, solving the two ordinary differential equations gives
x(t)=1−e−t/τx​,a(t)=1−e−t/τa​,r(t)=e−t/τa​−e−t/τx​.
(17)
Writing t for the numerical time in seconds, these are x=1−e−t, a=1−e−t/5 and r=e−t/5−e−t. Initially r=0, then x rises faster than a, producing a pulse. At long times x and a both approach 1, so r returns to 0 despite sustained s=1. For this upward step e−t/5≥e−t, hence r(t)≥0. The striking feature of this signaling adaptation toy is that both branches remain active while the readout becomes quiet: the continuing background and the response to its arrival are separable.

tpeak​=45​ln5≃2.0118 seconds,rpeak​=54​5−1/4≃0.53499.
(19)
Before this time the derivative is positive; afterwards it is negative. Together with r(0)=0 and limt→∞​r(t)=0, this proves that it is the unique pulse maximum. As another numerical check, at t=10 seconds x≃0.999955, a≃0.864665 and r≃0.135290: the slow branch is still catching up.
The subtraction r=x−a is a mathematical readout, not a claim that an actual chemical concentration equals this difference for arbitrary inputs. Nonnegativity was established only for the specified upward step; other input histories can give a negative signed readout. Complete signaling adaptation here also relies on equal eventual branch gains: changing the readout to r=x−0.9a leaves r∗​=0.1 for s=1. This toy illustrates cancellation and different time constants; it does not establish molecular implementation or robustness to arbitrary parameter changes. Ma and colleagues' 2009 study separately analyzed biochemical mathematical models with negative feedback and incoherent feedforward loops, identifying additional conditions for signaling adaptation in those models. [ref]
A historical connection to feedback control comes from Yi, Huang, Simon and Doyle's 2000 analysis of a bacterial chemotaxis model. They identified integral action in its regulation of receptor activity: accumulated deviations alter a slower adjustment, which in turn changes the measured activity. At a stationary state, an adjustment that integrates a nonzero error cannot remain constant, explaining how zero error can follow. Their conclusion depended on stated biochemical assumptions and stable behavior; it was not a claim that every biological cell uses that mechanism. A transient pulse alone, as the incoherent feedforward loop toy shows, cannot identify the underlying signaling feedback architecture. [ref]

Incoherent feedforward loop

Words: 80
Signal transduction OurBigBook.com
An incoherent feedforward loop carries influences from a shared input to a shared output along routes with opposite net signs. In the signaling adaptation toy, s drives fast x and slow a, which contribute with opposite signs to r=x−a. Despite the conventional word "loop", those routes do not return the output to an earlier stage: this arrangement is distinct from negative feedback. Opposing routes alone do not guarantee complete signaling adaptation. [ref]
