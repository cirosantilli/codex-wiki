Dielectric

 Home Physics Branches of physics Electromagnetism Permittivity
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 16
A dielectric is a material in which an electric field produces electric polarization while transport of electric charge is sufficiently small for the intended use. An insulating solid in a capacitor is an example; real dielectrics can still have dielectric loss and nonzero electrical conductivity.
The important distinction is between rearranging bound electric charge and transporting mobile charge carriers through the specimen. Bound electric charges can move a little within a chemical atom or molecule, or an existing electric dipole can turn. Their averaged electric dipole moments produce electric polarization even if there is negligible steady electric current between the electrodes. A changing electric polarization does give a bound current; the absence of steady conduction does not mean that the material is electrically unresponsive.[ref]
For uniform electric polarization P=Px in a rectangular slab, polarization density gives zero bound volume charge density in the interior and opposite bound surface charge densities −P,+P on the left and right faces. The electric charge has been redistributed, not created. More generally, the divergence theorem makes the cancellation explicit:

Here n is the outward normal vector on the material boundary. This accounts for both bound volume charge density and bound surface charge density; omitting either can falsely suggest that an initially neutral dielectric acquired net electric charge.
An electrical insulator between capacitor plates supports a useful combination: appreciable electric polarization, little leakage electric current, and tolerance of the intended electric field. This lets a small parallel-plate capacitor store separated electric charge and recoverable capacitor energy. The comparison in dielectric capacitor response explains why specifying fixed electric charge or fixed electric potential difference changes the answer. High relative permittivity alone does not establish good insulation or a large usable energy density; dielectric loss and dielectric breakdown matter as well.
