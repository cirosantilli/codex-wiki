Conductometric saponification assay

 ... Chemical reaction Chemical kinetics Reaction rate Rate law Order of reaction Second-order reaction
OurBigBook.com Words: 502
How can an invisible change in concentration produce a readable signal? The saponification of ethyl acetate by hydroxide in water gives ethanol and an acetate ion:
CH3​COOC2​H5​+OH−⟶CH3​COO−+C2​H5​OH.
(47)
The rate law used for this assay is r=k2​[ethyl acetate][OH−]. Each reactant has coefficient one, so r equals either disappearance rate. If the two initial concentrations are equal, they remain equal under this stoichiometry, giving −dc/dt=k2​c2 and kobs​=k2​, without a factor of two.
Manual for Advanced Physical Chemistry Laboratory-II by Nasima Arshad (Unit 7, Experiment 2, printed pp. 66–67) specifies a conductivity cell holding about 60mL in constant-temperature baths, with 0.040molL−1 ethyl acetate and sodium hydroxide stocks equilibrated separately. Mixing 30mL of each initiates the run. The readout is electrical resistance: every minute for ten minutes, every two minutes for ten more, then every five minutes for twenty-five. The cell is rinsed with distilled water. A matched sodium hydroxide–water dilution estimates the initial resistance; a potassium chloride standard determines the cell constant. The manual asks for comparison with the extrapolated initial signal.[ref]
The post-mixing initial concentration is c0​=0.040(30/60)=0.020molL−1 for each reactant, assuming additive volumes. This matches our illustrative starting concentration, but the prescribed kobs​=0.50 is not a measured value from the manual. As hydroxide is replaced by the less mobile acetate ion, electrical conductance decreases. The preceding experiment describes two platinum electrodes and an alternating-voltage Wheatstone bridge to limit polarization at the electrodes. For measured electrical resistance R(t), electrical conductance is G(t)=1/R(t); electrical conductivity is κ(t)=Kcell​G(t). Keep the probe geometry fixed and use one signal convention throughout.[ref]
For the equal-starting-concentration model, assume an affine calibration between electrical conductance and remaining hydroxide concentration, with endpoints G0​ and G∞​ at the same temperature. This gives
c0​c(t)​=G0​−G∞​G(t)−G∞​​,G(t)−G∞​G0​−G(t)​=c(t)c0​−c(t)​=k2​c0​t.
(48)
The last plot has slope k2​c0​, with units s−1, whereas plotting 1/c against time has slope k2​, with units Lmol−1s−1. These are different slopes. This endpoint transformation also shows why raw resistance must not be substituted for conductance.
Useful additional controls are repeat runs, fixed immersion depth, an independently checked final signal, and comparisons of several starting concentrations. Mixing delay obscures early conversion; temperature drift changes both the rate constant and the signal; contamination adds mobile ions. The affine calibration can fail when ionic mobilities change with composition. Near completion, G−G∞​ becomes small, so endpoint error is strongly amplified. Those are methodological limits and proposed checks, not new experimental observations. A conductivity trace supports a calibrated rate law over its tested range; it does not independently reveal the full reaction mechanism.
For unequal starting concentrations a=[A]0​ and b=[B]0​ in a one-to-one chemical reaction, let x be the consumed concentration. Then [A]=a−x, [B]=b−x, and
dtdx​=k2​(a−x)(b−x),b−a1​ln(b(a−x)a(b−x)​)=k2​t(a=b).
(49)
The natural logarithm acts on a dimensionless quantity; differentiating the left side with respect to x gives 1/((a−x)(b−x)), verifying the relation. Mixing 20mL of the stated ethyl acetate stock with 40mL of the sodium hydroxide stock would give a=0.01333… and b=0.02666…molL−1. The difference [B]−[A]=b−a stays constant, rather than their ratio. Neither the equal-concentration reciprocal formula nor a pseudo-first-order reaction approximation is justified solely by that twofold excess.
