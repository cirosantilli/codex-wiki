Cosmological redshift (1+z=a(t0​)/a(te​))

 Home Physics Branches of physics Cosmology Scale factor (cosmology)
OurBigBook.com Words: 738 Articles: 12
The cosmological redshift is the stretching of a freely propagating photon's wavelength between emission at cosmic time te​ and reception at t0​ in an FLRW metric. For a comoving observer at each endpoint,
1+z=λe​λ0​​=a(te​)a(t0​)​,νe​ν0​​=1+z1​.
(47)
Here λe​,λ0​ are emitted and received wavelengths, and νe​,ν0​ are the corresponding frequencies. A positive cosmological redshift means that the scale factor grew while the photon travelled. The name describes a shift toward longer wavelengths; it applies to light beyond the visible range as well.
For a computed example, let the scale factor double from a(te​)=0.5 to a(t0​)=1. An emitted wavelength of 500nm becomes 1000nm, giving z=1000/500−1=1. The received frequency is half the emitted frequency. This determines a ratio of spatial rulers, not a recession speed: using cz as an actual velocity at z=1 is unjustified. The wavelength panel in the illustration under Hubble's law shows this doubling.
A short derivation follows successive wave crests along a radial null geodesic in FLRW spacetime. Their source and receiver have fixed comoving coordinates, so each crest traverses the same comoving distance:
χ=∫te​t0​​a(t)cdt​.
(48)
Shift both endpoint times to the next crest while keeping χ fixed. To first order in the very short periods δte​,δt0​, this gives δt0​/a(t0​)=δte​/a(te​). Locally, wavelength equals the speed of light times the crest period, yielding the displayed cosmological redshift relation. These periods are much shorter than the timescale on which the scale factor changes.
The relation isolates the smooth expansion contribution. An observed redshift can also contain a Doppler effect from source or observer peculiar velocity and a gravitational redshift. See combined kinematic and cosmological redshift for the multiplicative combination of endpoint motion and expansion. Treating every observed redshift as pure cosmological redshift can therefore misassign a nearby source's distance.
The useful insight is that a received wavelength ratio records how much the scale factor changed during the photon's journey. Turning that ratio into a distance requires an expansion history, through the Hubble parameter, and a specified convention such as luminosity distance or proper distance in cosmology; the ratio alone supplies neither.
