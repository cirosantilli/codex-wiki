Initial value problem (u(t0​)=u0​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Differential equation Ordinary differential equation
OurBigBook.com Words: 587 Articles: 2
An initial value problem combines an ordinary differential equation with an initial condition, for example y′=f(t,y) and y(t0​)=y0​. The task is to find a solution of a differential equation on an interval containing t0​ which has that starting value. For a continuous linear differential equation u′=A(t)u, every prescribed finite vector u0​ determines a unique solution on the coefficient interval. A boundary value problem can instead prescribe values at distinct endpoints.
For the cooling initial value problem y′=−k(y−Ta​), y(t0​)=y0​, where k>0 and Ta​ are constant, the unique solution is
y(t)=Ta​+(y0​−Ta​)e−k(t−t0​).
(24)
Take Ta​=20 degrees Celsius, y0​=80 degrees Celsius, t0​=0, and k=0.1 per minute. Then y(10)=20+60e−1≈42.07 degrees Celsius. Reaching 30 degrees Celsius requires 60e−0.1t=10, so t=10log6≈17.92 minutes. The time constant 1/k=10 minutes reduces the excess temperature by a factor e−1; it does not reduce the absolute temperature by that factor.
Uniqueness needs assumptions. If f is a continuous function on an open neighborhood of (t0​,y0​) and satisfies a local Lipschitz condition in y, uniformly in t, the Picard-Lindelöf theorem gives a unique local solution. Concretely, a finite L bounds ∣f(t,u)−f(t,v)∣≤L∣u−v∣ in a sufficiently small rectangle. Cooling satisfies this with L=k. Local existence does not promise existence for all future time: y′=y2, y(0)=1, has y(t)=1/(1−t) on (−∞,1) and finite-time blowup at t=1.
Continuity alone is insufficient for uniqueness. Consider the dimensionless initial value problem involving a square root and an absolute value:
y′=∣y∣​,y(0)=0.
(25)
Besides y(t)≡0, each delay a≥0 gives a solution for t≥0:
ya​(t)={0,(t−a)2/4,​0≤t≤a,t>a.​
(26)
At t=4, delays a=0,1,2 give y=4,9/4,1, despite identical starting data. These are valid solutions of a differential equation: for a>0, both one-sided derivatives at the join are zero; for a=0, the right derivative at the starting time is zero. After the join, ya′​=(t−a)/2=∣ya​∣​ because t>a. Squaring alone would also suggest a parabola before the join, but its negative derivative there would violate the equation. Since y′≥0, a forward solution starting from zero stays nonnegative.
The right-hand side is a continuous function but fails the Lipschitz condition at zero: for h>0, ∣∣h∣​−0∣/∣h∣=1/h​ is unbounded. Thus the Peano existence theorem still supplies local existence, while the Picard-Lindelöf theorem does not supply uniqueness. Related waiting-time examples and a sufficient uniqueness condition appear in MIT 18.034, Lecture 2: Fundamental Principles.
A brief proof outline explains the mechanism: rewrite the initial value problem as the integral equation y(t)=y0​+∫t0​t​f(s,y(s))ds. On a short enough interval, the Lipschitz condition makes the right-hand side a contraction mapping on a closed set of continuous functions near y0​. The contraction mapping theorem gives a unique fixed point; the fundamental theorem of calculus turns it back into a solution.
