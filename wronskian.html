Wronskian (W(f,g)=fg′−f′g)

 ... Area of mathematics Analysis Differential equation Ordinary differential equation Linear ordinary differential equation Second-order linear differential equation
OurBigBook.com Words: 724 Articles: 2
For two differentiable functions f,g on an interval I, their Wronskian is the function
W(f,g)(x)=det(f(x)f′(x)​g(x)g′(x)​)=f(x)g′(x)−f′(x)g(x).
(21)
The order matters: exchanging f and g reverses the sign. For n sufficiently differentiable functions, the Wronskian is the determinant det(fj(i−1)​(x))i,j=1n​.[ref]
The Wronskian measures whether the vectors (f(x),f′(x)) and (g(x),g′(x)) supply independent value-and-derivative data at that point. For real-valued functions, its absolute value is the area of the parallelogram they span. If a constant linear combination af+bg is zero throughout I, then af′+bg′=0 too. A nonzero Wronskian at even one point makes this pair of linear equations invertible and forces a=b=0. Thus a nonzero Wronskian somewhere always proves linear independence of the functions on I.
For an explicit example, the exponential functions
y1​(x)=e−x,y2​(x)=e2x
(22)
solve the same homogeneous linear differential equation y′′−y′−2y=0 on the whole real line: substitution gives 1+1−2=0 and 4−2−2=0, respectively. Their Wronskian is
W(y1​,y2​)=e−x(2e2x)−(−e−x)e2x=3ex>0.
(23)
For the initial value problem y(0)=2, y′(0)=−1, the linear combination y=c1​y1​+c2​y2​ requires c1​+c2​=2 and −c1​+2c2​=−1. Hence c1​=5/3, c2​=1/3, and y=35​e−x+31​e2x. The nonzero Wronskian makes the two initial conditions independently adjustable.
The stronger converse requires a common equation. Suppose y1​,y2​ solve y′′+p(x)y′+q(x)y=0 with continuous functions p,q on one interval I. Then the functions are linearly independent if and only if their Wronskian is nonzero at one point, equivalently at every point. If W(x0​)=0, choose constants a,b, not both zero, making z=ay1​+by2​ satisfy z(x0​)=z′(x0​)=0. The superposition principle makes z a solution of a differential equation for the same equation, and the uniqueness theorem for ordinary differential equations forces z to be identically zero. This proves linear dependence.[ref]
For arbitrary differentiable functions, an identically zero Wronskian need not imply linear dependence. Consider, on I=(−2,2),
f(x)=x4,g(x)=x∣x∣3={−x4,x4,​x<0,x≥0.​
(24)
Both functions have three continuous derivatives. On each side of zero, g is a constant multiple of f, so W(f,g)=0; at zero their values and first derivatives are all zero, so the Wronskian is zero there too. Nevertheless af+bg=0 evaluated at x=−1 gives a−b=0, while evaluation at x=1 gives a+b=0. Hence a=b=0: these functions are linearly independent on the whole interval. There is no single constant multiplier valid on both sides of the common zero.
These functions cannot both solve any equation y′′+py′+qy=0 with continuous functions p,q throughout (−2,2): even f alone would contradict uniqueness because f(0)=f′(0)=0 but f is not identically zero. They do solve the singular equation x2y′′−3xy′=0, whose leading coefficient vanishes at zero. Dividing by x2 gives p=−3/x only away from zero; Abel's identity cannot be applied across that point. The example pinpoints why checking the equation and its interval is essential before using a zero Wronskian to infer linear dependence.
