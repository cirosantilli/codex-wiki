Cauchy's functional equation (f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Functional equation
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 7
Cauchy's functional equation asks for functions f:R→R satisfying
f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)(x,y∈R).
(8)
The identity must hold for every pair of real numbers, and a solution is an additive function. It says that combining two inputs before applying f gives the same result as combining their outputs.[ref]
For example, f(x)=3x satisfies the identity because 3(x+y)=3x+3y. At x=2/3 and y=5/6 the two sides give
f(32​+65​)=f(23​)=29​,f(32​)+f(65​)=2+25​=29​.
(9)
For positive widths, a rectangle of height three makes this visible: cutting its width into x and y partitions its area into 3x and 3y. The same identity also holds for negative inputs, where 3x is a signed output rather than an ordinary area.
Every function f(x)=cx with a fixed real number c solves Cauchy's functional equation. The surprising question is whether these are all the solutions. Rational homogeneity of an additive function forces f(q)=qf(1) for every rational number q, but reaching an irrational number requires an additional argument. Continuity at just one point or being a bounded function on an interval of positive length supplies that argument; without such a condition, discontinuous additive functions exist.[ref]
This makes Cauchy's functional equation a useful test of the boundary between algebra and analysis: a rule about addition fixes all rational multiples, while a tiny amount of continuity fixes the entire real line. For example, the continuous exponential functional equation reduces to an additive function after taking a logarithm, so this result explains why its nonzero solutions are exponential functions.
