Geometrical optics

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics
OurBigBook.com Words: 9k Articles: 83
Geometrical optics describes propagation by rays and Snell's law, neglecting diffraction on scales much larger than the wavelength.
A light ray is a path in an approximation, not a physical thread of zero width. In a transparent, homogeneous, isotropic medium it is straight and perpendicular to a wavefront; boundaries redirect it by the law of reflection or Snell's law. The approximation is useful when the wavelength is small compared with relevant apertures, surface curvature scales, and distances over which the refractive index changes. For an illustrative wavelength of 500nm and a 10mm aperture, their ratio is 5×10−5. That small ratio supports ray tracing for image location, but does not eliminate diffraction when calculating the fine structure of a focused spot. At a wave caustic, geometrical optics can fail despite large optical elements. [ref].
The mechanism of refraction is a change in phase velocity. For transparent isotropic media at the chosen frequency, the refractive index is
n=vphase​c​,n1​sinθ1​=n2​sinθ2​,
(1)
where c is the vacuum speed of light and the angles are measured from the interface normal vector. At a stationary planar interface the frequency and tangential wavevector match across the boundary. Since the wavenumber is k=nω/c, that matching gives Snell's law. The transmitted wavelength changes while the frequency stays fixed. Here n uses phase velocity, which need not equal group velocity. [ref]. [ref].
Choose model air with n1​=1 and model glass with n2​=1.5, without claiming these are exact values for every sample or wavelength. At θ1​=45∘, Snell's law gives
θ2​=arcsin(1.5sin45∘​)≈28.1255∘.
(2)
The transmitted light ray bends toward the normal vector; its phase velocity is c/1.5, and its wavelength is two thirds of the air value. By reversibility of an optical ray, incidence from glass at 28.1255∘ returns the outgoing air angle 45∘.
Incidence from glass at 45∘ is a different experiment. It would require sinθ2​=1.5sin45∘≈1.06066, so there is no real transmitted angle. The critical angle for total internal reflection is
θc​=arcsin(1/1.5)≈41.8103∘.
(3)
Above this angle, an ideal lossless glass-air boundary produces total internal reflection. The physical optics description still permits an evanescent wave in the air, although no propagating transmitted light ray crosses the boundary. [ref]. [ref].
The usefulness of geometrical optics is that complicated instruments can be assembled from these local rules. Applying Snell's law at successive curved surfaces changes a spreading bundle into a converging one. The Fermat principle unifies the construction through stationary optical path length; stationarity does not require a global minimum. [ref]. Fang's MIT notes explicitly connect this principle to specular reflection, refraction, and thin lens imaging, providing a route from individual paths to complete instruments. [ref].

Deviation angle (δ)

The angle through which an optical element changes a light ray's propagation direction, with the chosen branch and convention specified. For the one-internal-reflection branch through a spherical drop, entry refraction, internal specular reflection, and exit refraction give δ=π+2α−4β, with sinα=nsinβ. Spherical ray invariant (p=n(r)rsinψ)

Words: 95
Geometrical optics OurBigBook.com
For a light ray in a spherically stratified refractive index n(r), p=n(r)rsinψ is constant, where ψ is the angle to the radius. Approximate the medium by concentric homogeneous shells. Straight propagation within each shell conserves rsinψ, the line's distance from the centre; Snell's law at each shell boundary conserves nrsinψ. Passing to the smooth limit proves the result. In an exterior region with n=1, p is the distance of the asymptotic straight light ray from the centre. Consequently a surface-tangent light ray at radius R and refractive index ns​ has vacuum asymptote at altitude (ns​−1)R, provided the light ray reaches the exterior without trapping.

Optical rays in a reciprocal-height refractive index (μ(y)=1/y,(x−c)2+y2=R2)

Words: 70
Geometrical optics OurBigBook.com
For the refractive index μ=1/y on the upper half-plane, Fermat principle and the Beltrami identity give 1/[y1+(dy/dx)2​]=1/R. The nonvertical rays are arcs of circles centered on y=0; vertical lines are the remaining rays. These are also geodesics of the Poincaré half-plane model. The line y=0 is approached only after infinite optical path length because the refractive index diverges there.

Mirage

Words: 366 Articles: 2
Geometrical optics OurBigBook.com
A mirage is an apparent displaced image caused by refraction through a spatially varying refractive index. Above a hot surface, low-index air near the ground can bend a light ray back upwards before it reaches the surface. An observer tracing the arriving ray backwards along its local tangent can then infer an apparent image below the turning region. This explains the appearance of a distant reflective surface without requiring reflection at the road itself.

Wave caustic

Words: 50
Geometrical optics OurBigBook.com
A wave caustic is a locus where the projection of a family of rays or wavefronts becomes singular. Several ray paths may coalesce there, and the geometrical approximation can predict divergent intensity. Finite-wavelength diffraction replaces that ideal divergence by a resolved wave pattern. The construction applies to acoustic as well as optical waves.

Etendue (G=n2AΩ)

Words: 1k Articles: 8
Geometrical optics OurBigBook.com
Etendue measures how much position-and-direction space a bundle of light rays occupies. A small spot accepting a wide range of directions can have the same etendue as a large spot accepting a narrow range. This makes etendue useful for deciding whether an optical instrument can deliver an extended source's entire admitted radiant flux to a small receiver. For forward crossings of a surface, the geometrical optics definition is
G=∫B​n2cosθdAdΩ,0≤θ<2π​,
(5)
where B is the occupied set of surface positions and directions, n is the local refractive index, dA is actual surface area, dΩ is solid angle, and θ is the angle to the forward unit normal. The cosine supplies the projected area. Opposite crossing directions must be counted separately with positive projected area, rather than cancelling them in a signed integral. [ref]
The shorthand G≃n2AΩ assumes constant refractive index, a narrow nearly normal bundle, and the same admitted directions across the whole area A. At larger angles, use the cosine-weighted integral; a position-dependent angular acceptance cannot generally be replaced by the product of two independent extents.

Conservation of etendue (Gout​=Gin​)

Words: 207 Articles: 1
Etendue OurBigBook.com
Lossless reversible propagation preserves the etendue of the same bundle of light rays. Here the model uses stationary isotropic media, specular reflection and refraction, with every selected light ray reaching the output and with no optical gain or scattering of light. An aperture that blocks some incident light rays changes which bundle is counted; the full incident and transmitted etendue need not be equal. [ref]
The underlying phase space has four coordinates. In a planar section with constant refractive index, let (x,y) locate a light ray and set
px​=nsinθcosϕ,py​=nsinθsinϕ.
(6)
The Jacobian determinant from spherical coordinates gives
dpx​dpy​=n2sinθcosθdθdϕ=n2cosθdΩ,dG=dxdydpx​dpy​.
(7)
Thus etendue is a four-dimensional volume, preserved by the optical counterpart of Liouville theorem in Hamiltonian mechanics. Individual position and direction extents may change, and correlations between them matter. The Lagrange optical invariant is a related paraxial approximation invariant for pairs of light rays, rather than the full four-dimensional measure.

(11)

(13)

(14)

An ideal optical point source does not fill that four-dimensional set. Diffraction, optical coherence, or coupling a Gaussian beam into an optical fiber require a physical optics calculation; the cone bound is not a universal guarantee for a single wave mode. Likewise, optical gain or fluorescence changes the propagation assumptions.

Etendue bench measurement

Words: 237
Etendue OurBigBook.com
A proposed optical bench test uses a steady illuminated diffuse patch, a relay made from two optical lens elements, interchangeable centred circular stops, and a calibrated optical power meter large enough to collect the whole output bundle. Start with small cone angles so an ordinary relay approximates the area-angle tradeoff. Measure the patch's image diameter and the accepted angular extent at both conjugate planes; the transverse magnification gives the spatial scale, but does not by itself measure etendue.
Map the local radiance with a baffled photodiode on translation and rotation stages, using two small aligned holes to select a narrow known solid angle. Keep the sampled position fixed while scanning direction, calibrate the sampling footprint and angular response, and repeat at several positions. Numerically integrate n2cosθdAdΩ over the occupied samples, and integrate (L/n2)dG to predict the collected radiant flux. This checks the filled-cone assumption instead of inferring it from two outer dimensions.
Compare the relay without a stop and with a stop of 0.6 times the illuminated diameter in the same plane. The uniform-bundle prediction is a radiant flux ratio of 0.36, with unchanged local radiance in the surviving samples. Cover the input for a dark reading, monitor source drift, repeat the open-stop reading, check detector linearity with calibrated optical attenuators, and enlarge the collecting detector or scan it to check for missed light rays. Lens losses, angular response, illumination gradients, alignment errors and diffraction can spoil the ideal prediction; their effects must be measured separately. This is a procedure to try, with predicted readouts, not purported experimental data.

Words: 3k Articles: 21

An optical lens is a transparent element, or assembly of elements, that redirects a bundle of light rays to form images or change how the bundle spreads. In an ordinary glass optical lens, refraction at the surfaces provides the redirection: their changing normal vectors make Snell's law bend different light rays by different amounts. A converging optical lens brings incoming light rays parallel to its optical axis toward an optical focus; a diverging optical lens makes them spread as if from an optical focus on the incoming side. [ref].
For a usual glass thin lens in air, a form thicker at the centre than at the edges normally converges, with positive focal length; one thinner at the centre normally diverges. Shape alone is insufficient: the refractive index relative to the surroundings also matters, as the lensmaker's equation shows. An aperture selects which light rays pass; an optical lens also changes their directions. The distinction explains how an optical camera can gather light from one object point over many incoming directions and deliver it to one image location.
The beautiful simplification is that an entire bundle can be characterized by a focal length, and two constructed light rays can locate an image. The thin lens examples below turn that geometry into a prediction of image position, orientation and transverse magnification. A real optical lens retains optical aberrations and diffraction, so the construction is a useful leading model rather than an exact solution of Maxwell's equations.[ref]

Coma makes an off-axis optical point source appear asymmetric, often with a compact head and a flaring tail like a comet. Different parts of the optical pupil send its light rays to different transverse image positions. Moving the receiving plane can favour one colour without bringing all colours into focus. [ref]; [ref].

Φ(λ)=f(λ)1​=[n(λ)−1](Ra​1​−Rb​1​),nblue​>nred​ ⟹ Φblue​>Φred​>0,fblue​<fred​.
(33)
Here λ is wavelength, n is refractive index, Φ is optical power, f is focal length, and Ra​,Rb​ are the signed surface radius of curvature values in the lensmaker's equation. The blue light rays therefore bend more strongly and meet sooner. This ordering is a statement about ordinary visible optical dispersion, not a universal rule for every material and every spectral range. [ref].
For a worked example, take a single positive thin lens with reference optical power Φd​=5D and Abbe number Vd​=60, hence reference focal length fd​=0.2m. Its F–C optical power difference is ΔΦ=ΦF​−ΦC​=Φd​/Vd​=5/60D≈0.08333D, by the achromatic doublet power balance relation below. This difference alone does not locate the reference optical power within the F–C interval. To draw a transparent example, introduce a synthetic colour coordinate q, a dimensionless quantity, with red q=−1/2, reference q=0, and blue q=+1/2, and choose the toy optical dispersion model
Φ(q)=Φd​(1+Vd​q​),f(q)=Φ(q)1​.
(34)
The diagram's red and blue endpoints represent the C and F endpoints of this toy model. Its reference point is placed symmetrically in optical power by construction; q is not a measured wavelength scale, and the model is not measured optical glass data. The endpoint focal lengths are
fred​=5(1−1/120)1​m=11924​m≈0.201681m,fblue​=5(1+1/120)1​m=12124​m≈0.198347m.
(35)
Thus the axial chromatic aberration in this example separates the endpoint optical foci by about 3.334mm. A single receiving plane cannot coincide with both optical foci.
The useful design idea is to cancel changes of optical power while retaining a positive total optical power. An achromatic lens does this with materials having different Abbe numbers. It greatly reduces chromatic aberration, but correcting two chosen wavelengths still leaves the possibility of a secondary spectrum.

Lateral chromatic aberration, also called transverse chromatic aberration, is colour-dependent image scale: an object away from the optical axis can have different image heights at different wavelengths. Achromatic lens

Words: 884 Articles: 3
Chromatic aberration OurBigBook.com
An achromatic lens combines optical lens elements with different optical dispersion so that two specified wavelengths have the same focal length in the paraxial approximation. This brings their parallel incoming light rays to a common optical focus, helping an optical camera form a sharper image under mixed-colour illumination. A common implementation is an achromatic doublet: a positive crown glass element paired with a negative flint glass element. [ref].
The achromatic doublet does not require either material to have zero optical dispersion. Instead, the positive element gains positive optical power toward blue, while the negative element becomes more negative. Suitable Abbe numbers and signed optical powers let these changes cancel even though their reference optical powers do not cancel. For example, the achromatic doublet power balance calculation below combines +10D with −5D to obtain +5D, or focal length 0.2m, with zero primary F–C optical power difference. The smaller Abbe number of the negative element makes its weaker reference optical power sufficient to oppose the positive element's colour change.
An achromatic lens is corrected at two chosen wavelengths, not necessarily at every wavelength between them. The remaining focal length variation is the secondary spectrum. Correcting axial chromatic aberration also does not automatically remove lateral chromatic aberration throughout an imaging system, spherical aberration, or diffraction. Actual designs therefore need ray tracing with the intended wavelengths, element thicknesses, separations and apertures. [ref]; [ref].
Achromatic doublet

An achromatic doublet is an achromatic lens with two optical lens elements. In the contact thin lens model, their optical powers add and their primary colour changes cancel according to achromatic doublet power balance. Achromatic doublet power balance
Words: 575
Achromatic lens OurBigBook.com
Achromatic doublet power balance chooses two signed optical powers to meet two goals: obtain the required focal length and cancel the primary F–C colour difference. Model two thin lens elements in contact in air, using the paraxial approximation. Let i=c,f label the crown glass and flint glass elements, and let Φi,d​ denote each element's optical power at the reference d line. The Abbe number for each material is
Vi​=ni,F​−ni,C​ni,d​−1​.
(36)
The subscripts label standard spectral lines: blue F near 486.1nm, red C near 656.3nm, and reference d near 587.6nm. These are three specified wavelengths, not colour bands. A larger Abbe number means less relative optical dispersion between F and C. [ref].
For a fixed thin lens shape in air, write the lensmaker's equation as Φi​(λ)=Ki​[ni​(λ)−1], with signed shape factor Ki​=1/Ri,a​−1/Ri,b​. Subtracting its C value from its F value gives the primary optical power difference
ΔΦi​=Φi,F​−Φi,C​=Ki​(ni,F​−ni,C​)=Vi​Φi,d​​.
(37)
The negative element has negative ΔΦi​: its blue optical power is more negative, not less negative. The contact rule for optical power therefore gives
Φd​=Φc,d​+Φf,d​=fd​1​,ΔΦtotal​=Vc​Φc,d​​+Vf​Φf,d​​=0.
(38)
This zero is equality of the total F and C optical powers, hence equality of their focal lengths when the total optical power is nonzero. The relation follows directly from the lensmaker's equation and the Abbe number; it is exact within the fixed-shape contact thin lens model. [ref]. Tatum uses a yellow D-line reference; here the Abbe number convention uses the d line consistently.
For a toy design, choose Vc​=60, Vf​=30, Φc,d​=10D and Φf,d​=−5D. Then
Φd​=(10−5)D=5D,fd​=5m−11​=0.2m,ΔΦtotal​=(6010​−305​)D=0.
(39)
The positive element contributes +1/6D to the F–C difference and the negative element contributes −1/6D. This retains the same reference focal length as the single 5D, Vd​=60 thin lens in chromatic aberration, while cancelling its primary colour split. The values are illustrative design parameters, not a catalogue optical glass pair.
Use the same synthetic colour coordinate q=−1/2,0,+1/2 and toy optical dispersion as in chromatic aberration. The two element optical powers and their sum become
Φc​(q)=10(1+60q​)D,Φf​(q)=−5(1+30q​)D,Φtotal​(q)=(5+q[6010​−305​])D=5D.
(40)
At the red endpoint, the element optical powers are 9.916667D and −4.916667D; at the blue endpoint, they are 10.083333D and −5.083333D. Both sums are 5D. In this first-order toy model alone, the sum is constant for every q and the focal length remains 0.2m throughout. Real material optical dispersion is not captured by these two straight slopes, so the flat line is not a claim that a real achromatic doublet focuses all colours perfectly.
For specified nonzero reference optical power Φd​ and distinct Abbe numbers, solving the two balance equations gives
Φc,d​=Vc​−Vf​Φd​Vc​​,Φf,d​=−Vc​−Vf​Φd​Vf​​.
(41)
Thus Φd​>0 and Vc​>Vf​>0 require positive crown glass optical power and negative flint glass optical power. If the two finite Abbe numbers were equal, cancellation would force zero total optical power. If they are close, the required element optical powers become large compared with the total: cancelling primary colour can demand much stronger individual elements.
The contact assumption matters. For two thin lens elements separated by distance s in air, the equivalent optical power is Φc​+Φf​−sΦc​Φf​, evaluated at each wavelength; its colour change need not satisfy the contact balance. Finite thickness, departures from the paraxial approximation, and the secondary spectrum also limit the simple prediction.[ref]
Secondary spectrum

The secondary spectrum is residual chromatic aberration after an achromatic lens brings two selected wavelengths to the same optical focus. For example, equal red and blue focal lengths can coexist with a different green focal length. The Abbe number fixes one F–C relative difference, not the complete shape of optical dispersion between or beyond those wavelengths. The perfectly flat sum in the achromatic doublet power balance diagram deliberately omits this residual effect. [ref].
