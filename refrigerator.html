Refrigerator

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics
OurBigBook.com Words: 395 Articles: 2
A refrigerator removes heat from the region we want to keep cold and releases heat to warmer surroundings. Here we consider a work-driven cyclic device: its useful output is the cold-side extraction Qc​, and its input is net work W. A food compartment is a familiar example; the same cooling objective applies when removing heat from a room. The coefficient of performance measures extraction per unit work, rather than the fraction of input energy converted into output. [ref]
Choose the complete machine as the thermodynamic system. Let Qc​>0 be heat entering from the colder side, Qh​>0 be heat delivered to the hotter side, and W>0 be net work supplied, all over the same complete cycle. Assume these are its only energy transfers, no matter crosses the outer boundary, and there is no net change in stored energy. Returning to the initial state gives ΔU=0, so the first law of thermodynamics reads
0=Qc​−Qh​+W,Qh​=Qc​+W.
(20)
The heat leaving the warmer side includes both the extracted heat and the supplied work. For instance, stipulated transfers Qc​=3kJ and W=1kJ require Qh​=4kJ; the cooling coefficient of performance is 3. This is an illustrative budget, not measured performance. At steady operation the same balance holds for average transfer rates, Q˙​h​=Q˙​c​+Pin​, where Pin​ is input power.
This budget explains a useful counterexample: operating a refrigerator with its door open inside an otherwise isolated room cannot cool the room as a whole. If both heat transfers occur within the room and the electrical input comes from outside, the room gains Qh​−Qc​=W each cycle. Locally cold air does not imply net cooling. The Second law of thermodynamics permits cold-to-hot transfer because work is supplied; it forbids a cyclic device whose sole effect is that transfer. See the Clausius statement of the second law.
