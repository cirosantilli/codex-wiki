Singular value decomposition (A=UΣV∗)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 7
The singular value decomposition separates any matrix into input directions, stretching factors, and output directions. Every m×n complex matrix admits
A=UΣV∗,U∈Cm×m,Σ∈Rm×n,V∈Cn×n.
(17)
Here U,V are unitary matrices, V∗=V† is the conjugate transpose, and Σ is a rectangular diagonal matrix. Its p=min(m,n) diagonal entries are the singular values, ordered σ1​≥⋯≥σp​≥0. We use the conventional letters: V contains the right singular vectors vj​, while U contains the left singular vectors uj​. Writing A=VΣU† instead merely swaps these two matrix names; it requires swapping their roles consistently. For real matrices, choose orthogonal matrices and replace the conjugate transpose with the transpose.[ref]
Reading from right to left, V∗ expresses the input in an orthonormal basis, Σ stretches or annihilates those coordinates, and U assembles the output in another orthonormal basis. Unlike matrix similarity, the input and output bases can differ, and even have different sizes. A 3×2 matrix has full factors of sizes 3×3, 3×2, and 2×2. If its matrix rank is 2, one can retain only the active columns, giving sizes 3×2, 2×2, and 2×2 for Ur​,Σr​,Vr∗​; the full U additionally describes an unused output direction.
For a fully computed nonsymmetric example, take
A=(3−4/5​43/5​),U=I,Σ=(50​01​),V=51​(34​−43​).
(18)
Multiplication gives UΣVT=A. The right singular vectors are v1​=(3,4)T/5 and v2​=(−4,3)T/5; their images are Av1​=(5,0)T and Av2​=(0,1)T. Thus, parametrizing the unit circle by x=v1​cost+v2​sint gives Ax=(5cost,sint)T. Its image is the ellipse y12​/25+y22​=1. The two singular values are its semiaxis lengths, and the left singular vectors point along its axes. The singular value decomposition finds precisely the perpendicular input directions whose images stay perpendicular.
For compression, write the matrix as a sum of outer products and retain the largest terms:
A=∑j=1r​σj​uj​vj∗​,Ak​=∑j=1k​σj​uj​vj∗​,∥A−Ak​∥F2​=∑j=k+1r​σj2​,
(19)
where r is the matrix rank. Among all matrices of matrix rank at most k, Ak​ minimizes the Frobenius norm error; this is the Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem. The operator norm error is σk+1​ for k<r. These statements concern specified norms, rather than guaranteeing that discarded details are visually or scientifically unimportant.[ref]
Consider an 8×10 brightness matrix B=pqT+abT/2, with
p=(1,2,3,4,4,3,2,1)T,q=(1,2,3,4,5,5,4,3,2,1)T,
(20)
and ai​=(−1)i−1, bj​=(−1)j−1. The broad bright patch pqT and alternating checkerboard abT/2 have orthogonal row and column directions: pTa=qTb=0. Their singular values are 60⋅110​=6600​ and 8⋅10​/2=20​. This low-rank approximation keeps the first outer product, giving B1​=pqT, with Frobenius norm error 20​≈4.472 and relative error 20/6620​≈5.50%. For example, B11​=1.5 becomes 1, and B12​=1.5 becomes 2: every entry changes by 0.5 in magnitude. The discarded pattern is small but entirely lost.
For this illustrative image compression, storing one normalized left singular vector, one normalized right singular vector, and one singular value requires 8+10+1=19 scalars instead of 80. This counts scalars at equal precision, before storage overhead. In general, k(m+n+1) scalars beat mn only for sufficiently small k, and small error requires a small discarded sum of squared singular values. The usefulness comes from measuring the tradeoff before throwing information away.[ref]
