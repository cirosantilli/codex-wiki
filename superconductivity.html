OurBigBook.com Words: 9k Articles: 85
Superconductivity is a phase of matter that supports steady electric current with zero bulk electrical resistivity and exhibits the Meissner effect: equilibrium screening of a sufficiently weak, static magnetic field from the interior. It occurs below a material-dependent superconducting transition temperature, provided the electric current and applied magnetic field are also sufficiently small. [ref] Bulk expulsion means that a sample much thicker than its London penetration depth has an almost field-free interior; the magnetic field need not vanish at its surface. Conventional BCS theory explains superconductivity through correlated electrons forming Cooper pairs. [ref]
The two observations answer different questions. A resistance-versus-temperature experiment for superconductivity asks whether a steady electric current requires a measurable electric potential difference. A Field-cooled Meissner experiment asks whether a magnetic field already present in the material is expelled when it enters superconductivity. An ideal perfect conductor with zero electrical resistivity can preserve that pre-existing magnetic field; zero electrical resistance alone therefore does not establish the Meissner effect.
The usefulness follows directly from Joule heating, P=I2R: maintaining a steady electric current through a segment with zero electrical resistance produces no resistive heating in that segment. This makes persistent electric currents attractive for producing steady magnetic fields. The beauty is that one phase has both a transport property and an equilibrium magnetic response, and the second cannot be inferred just by making an ordinary electrical resistance very small. The London equations encode this extra magnetic response. [ref]

Volovik effect (γ(B)∝B​)

Words: 82
D-wave superconductivity OurBigBook.com
Vortex superflow Doppler-shifts low-energy quasiparticles in a nodal superconductor. For a circular cell of radius R∝B−1/2 and flow magnitude proportional to 1/r, its spatially averaged magnitude is proportional to 1/R. A linearly vanishing nodal density of states then acquires a zero-energy contribution proportional to B​, producing a low-temperature linear heat-capacity coefficient with that scaling. This is a clean semiclassical mixed-state estimate; the vector flow average can be zero, and disorder, screening, core states and other field effects change its range of validity.

Persistent current (superconductivity, I)

Words: 221
Superconductivity OurBigBook.com
A superconducting persistent current is an electric current circulating in a closed path with superconductivity after the external driving electromotive force has ceased. Its associated magnetic field makes the circulation observable without inserting a measuring instrument into the path. This article concerns macroscopic circulation in superconductivity. A finite experiment establishes persistence over its observation window with a stated measurement error, rather than directly observing an infinite lifetime.
The ideal circuit connection is flux conservation in a superconducting loop. In a fixed loop with self-inductance L, a single circulating electric current I, and externally imposed linked magnetic flux Φext​, the lumped circuit approximation gives
LdtdI​+RI=−dtdΦext​​.
(1)
When the electrical resistance R vanishes, integrating gives LI+Φext​=constant. Removing the external linked magnetic flux therefore changes the circulating electric current; holding it fixed afterwards preserves that electric current. This is a circuit approximation with a specified self-inductance, not a derivation of the microscopic state of superconductivity. For thin paths where the carrier contribution matters, fluxoid and flux quantization in a superconductor supplies the more careful description.
For constant L>0, constant effective electrical resistance R≥0, and no changing external linked magnetic flux, the RL circuit instead predicts
I(t)=I0​e−Rt/L,τ=RL​(R>0).
(2)
This exponential decay follows by separating dI/I=−(R/L)dt. A long relaxation time tests extremely small electrical resistance without trying to resolve an equally tiny steady electric potential difference. The stored magnetic energy is U=LI2/2, so the same model gives dU/dt=−RI2, exactly the Joule heating loss. The finite energy stored in the loop supplies the magnetic readout; the circulating electric current is not a continuously powered source of useful work.

Persistent current in a superconducting loop experiment

Words: 691 Articles: 1
Superconductivity OurBigBook.com
The persistent current in a superconducting loop experiment prepares a closed cold path, changes the externally linked magnetic flux, and follows the resulting superconducting persistent current through its magnetic field. The essential measurement starts after the inducing magnetic field has stopped changing.
Heike Kamerlingh Onnes's 1914 Communication 140c describes a lead coil closed by fused lead connections. Cooling in a magnetic field and removing the inducing magnet prepared the circulation. A magnetic compass sensed it without contacts; a separate copper coil, cooled in liquid air, supplied a measured balancing magnetic field. Reversing orientation and compensation reduced offsets. [ref]
The liquid-helium cryostat maintained low temperature, but refilling temporarily warmed the apparatus and changed the inferred electric current. A later run used a smaller initial electric current. Apparatus deformation and needle-zero drift limited comparison; cutting the closure still left a smaller magnetic signal, warning against assigning every signal to circulation around the whole loop. The report consequently gave an upper bound under a residual Ohm's law hypothesis. [ref]
For interpreting such a measurement, write a locally calibrated magnetic signal as
S(t)=k(t)I(t)+b(t).
(3)
Here k is the coupling and gain, while b contains the background magnetic field and readout offset. Only after establishing stable k and correcting b does a ratio of signals determine a ratio of electric currents. A gradual change in geometry, a drifting background magnetic field, or a change of temperature can imitate or conceal a decay. A pickup coil connected only to a voltage meter obeys Faraday's law of induction and detects a changing linked magnetic flux; its zero steady output cannot demonstrate that the underlying static magnetic field has disappeared. A magnetic compass can respond to that static magnetic field.
Opening the path, reversing the preparation, and repeating with smaller initial electric current are useful controls for separating whole-loop circulation, magnetic background and a superconducting critical current. Keep the temperature and external magnetic field stable during the interval used to infer electrical resistance. A sudden reduction near a superconducting critical current is not evidence for the constant-R exponential decay model at smaller electric current. Persistence also does not replace the Field-cooled Meissner experiment: transport around a hole and magnetic expulsion from the material test different properties.

Resistance upper bound from persistent-current decay (R≤−(L/T)ln(1−ϵ))

Words: 327
Persistent current in a superconducting loop experiment OurBigBook.com
Suppose a calibrated measurement bounds the fractional loss of a nonzero superconducting persistent current by ϵ, with 0<ϵ<1, over a duration T>0. Take I0​>0 by choosing the circulation direction. Under the constant-L, constant-R, undriven RL circuit model,
I0​I(T)​≥1−ϵ⟹0≤R≤−TL​ln(1−ϵ)≃TLϵ​.
(4)
The inequality follows because the logarithm is increasing. The final approximation requires ϵ≪1 and slightly understates the exact upper bound. If L is uncertain, an independently established upper bound on L gives the conservative electrical resistance bound. The uncertainty in the initial reading, the final reading, background subtraction and drift must all be included in ϵ; a display resolution alone does not provide it.
For an original illustrative example, let L=1μH and suppose the corrected electric current ratio after T=3h=10800s is at least 0.999. Then
R≤9.264×10−14Ω,τ≥1.07946×107s≃124.94days.
(5)
These are conditional bounds for stipulated parameters, not values attributed to the 1914 apparatus. With the same L but R=10−11Ω, the model instead gives τ=105s and I(24h)/I0​=e−0.864≃0.42147: a readily visible loss on the chosen scale.
A resolved decay can be checked using its logarithm:
lnI0​I(t)​=−LR​t,R=−Ldtdln∣I∣​.
(6)
A straight line supports this constant-parameter model; curvature or jumps demand a different account. If an effective electrical resistance varies with time while L remains fixed and the loop remains undriven, only its time average is constrained:
I(T)=I0​exp[−L1​∫0T​R(t)dt],T1​∫0T​R(t)dt≤−TL​ln(1−ϵ).
(7)
This does not bound every instantaneous value of R(t). Changing self-inductance or external linked magnetic flux adds terms to the circuit equation, so an uncorrected magnetic signal cannot simply be substituted for I(t). Nonobservation of decay leaves arbitrarily small positive constant electrical resistance compatible with a finite observation: it is not a logical proof that R=0.

Type-II superconductor

Superconductivity OurBigBook.com
A type-II superconductor can admit magnetic flux through quantized vortices while retaining superconductivity between their cores. Its superconducting mixed state lies between the lower critical magnetic field and upper critical magnetic field. These are equilibrium bulk distinctions; sample shape, flux pinning and preparation affect a measured magnetic response.
The motivating thin-film experiments changed an apparently minor preparation detail. Assume a triangular array of straight, singly quantized Abrikosov vortices with nearest-neighbor distance a. Here B>0 is the magnitude of the spatially averaged internal magnetic field, and Φ0​=h/(2e) is the superconducting flux quantum; e>0 is the elementary charge. Two lattice vectors of length a meeting at 60∘ span area Ac​=3​a2/2. One flux quantum per cell gives

Resistance-versus-temperature experiment for superconductivity

Words: 561 Articles: 1
Superconductivity OurBigBook.com
The motivating observation is an abrupt disappearance of detectable electrical resistance on cooling, rather than a smooth improvement in conduction. The diagram pairs the measuring arrangement with an idealized transition. Its curves are deliberately synthetic; neither their scales nor their abrupt step are a reconstruction of measured mercury data.
In a report communicated in November 1911, Heike Kamerlingh Onnes described a mercury thread in seven connected glass U-tubes immersed in a stirred liquid-helium bath. Expansion spaces kept freezing mercury from breaking the glass or interrupting the thread. Separate leads supplied electric current and sensed electric potential difference. He compensated a troublesome thermoelectric effect and reversed the main electric current to extract the resistive signal. He reported a rapid fall between 4.21 and 4.19K on his stated historical temperature scale, with electrical resistance disappearing at the lower value. The apparatus and control of spurious electric potential differences are part of the discovery, not incidental details. [ref]
A modern version places a contacted sample and a calibrated thermometer close together in a cooling environment. Use four-terminal sensing, record the small applied electric current I, the sensed electric potential difference V, and the sample temperature T, then plot R(T)=V/I. Repeat on warming as well as cooling, and allow readings to stabilize near thermal equilibrium. Disagreement between warming and cooling is a reason to check sample–thermometer lag and reproducibility before quoting a superconducting transition temperature. Mikhailov and Kordosky's undergraduate experiment uses this strategy with liquid-nitrogen cooling. [ref]
Choose the measuring electric current small enough that Joule heating does not appreciably change the sample temperature, and repeat with a smaller electric current to check that the transition persists. Cancel stable offsets using the reversal described in four-terminal sensing. A reading indistinguishable from zero supplies an experimental upper bound, not a proof of exact zero electrical resistance. If δV bounds the unresolved resistive electric potential difference, then
∣R∣≲∣I∣δV​.
(10)
For a stipulated sensitivity δV=20nV and I=1mA, this is 20μΩ. The bound applies between the sensing contacts; it is not the electrical resistance of the complete wiring. A conducting path through part of an inhomogeneous sample can also make V negligible, so the Field-cooled Meissner experiment adds independent evidence about the material's magnetic response.

Four-terminal sensing (R=[V(+I)−V(−I)]/(2I))

Words: 158
Resistance-versus-temperature experiment for superconductivity OurBigBook.com
Four-terminal sensing uses one pair of contacts to carry electric current and a separate pair to sense electric potential difference. The sensing instrument draws negligible electric current, so the voltage drops along its own leads are negligible, and the current-contact drops outside the sensing interval are excluded. A measurement using just two shared leads includes their electrical resistance and contact drops, which can overwhelm the small sample signal in a resistance-versus-temperature experiment for superconductivity. Four contacts do not automatically remove offsets from the thermoelectric effect. [ref]
For a stable offset V0​, let I>0 denote the magnitude of the reversed electric current. The measured electric potential differences are V(+I)=IR+V0​ and V(−I)=−IR+V0​, so subtraction gives the displayed electrical resistance. For example, with stipulated I=1mA, R=0.2mΩ and V0​=3μV, the two readings are 3.2 and 2.8μV; their difference correctly recovers R. If the offset changes between readings, its change leaves a residual measurement error; the sample and measuring circuit must remain sufficiently stable during reversal.

Field-cooled Meissner experiment

Words: 632
Superconductivity OurBigBook.com
The Field-cooled Meissner experiment starts above the superconducting transition temperature with a weak, steady applied magnetic field already penetrating a solid sample. Cooling while keeping the applied magnetic field fixed tests whether the interior magnetic flux changes. This is the decisive contrast with cooling in zero magnetic field and switching the magnetic field on afterwards. The diagram shows the two histories for the same final applied magnetic field.
Meissner and Ochsenfeld reported magnetic expulsion in 1933, showing that the transition involves more than vanishing electrical resistance. A modern motivating version appears in MIT's lead-sample experiment: surround the sample with a solenoid supplying a steady magnetic field and a separate pickup coil connected to a recording instrument. Apply the magnetic field while the lead is still normal, then cool through its transition without changing the solenoid electric current. Expulsion produces a transient pickup-coil electric potential difference; warming through the transition produces the opposite sign as magnetic flux returns. MIT also checks the signal direction by switching off the applied magnetic field while the sample is normal. [ref] [ref]
Faraday's law of induction explains the readout. For a stationary N-turn pickup coil, with the same linked magnetic flux Φ per turn and a fixed orientation,
Vpick​(t)=−NdtdΦ​,∫t1​t2​​Vpick​(t)dt=−N[Φ(t2​)−Φ(t1​)].
(11)

For stipulated N=100 and ΔΦ=−2×10−8Wb per turn, the signed pulse area is +2×10−6Vs. The apparatus geometry determines how a change in interior magnetic field contributes to that linked magnetic flux. Keeping the applied magnetic field fixed, repeating on warming, and checking a normal-state control make the expulsion interpretation testable; a stationary pickup coil has no sustained signal after the magnetic flux has stopped changing.
Why does the preparation history matter? In the stationary, ideal perfect conductor model, the interior electric field is zero. The differential form of Faraday's law of induction then gives
∂t∂B​=−∇×E=0.
(12)
If the interior magnetic field was B0​ when perfect conduction began, it remains B0​; if it was zero, it stays zero when an external magnetic field is subsequently applied. Both a perfect conductor prepared in zero magnetic field and a sample exhibiting the Meissner effect can therefore exclude a newly applied weak magnetic field. Only the field-cooled comparison tests expulsion of an already present magnetic field. This argument concerns a stationary sample and the ideal zero-electric field model, not a moving conductor.
The equilibrium London equations supply the additional constraint. In a homogeneous region without penetrating flux tubes, they imply ∇2B=B/λL2​. For a half-space z≥0 with a magnetic field parallel to its flat boundary, fixed surface value Bs​, and a bounded solution deep inside, this gives

Unlike the frozen interior magnetic field of a perfect conductor, this equilibrium profile does not retain an arbitrary initial B0​. The extra constitutive relation, and not zero electrical resistance by itself, selects the screened state. Expulsion occurs during the transition, when transient electric fields can be present; the steady-state zero-electric field argument does not describe the process of establishing superconductivity. The 1985 spectroscopy experiment used niobium-based tunnel Josephson junctions on silicon, thermally anchored with final bias low-pass filters to a dilution refrigerator. Calibrated germanium and cobalt thermometers measured temperature. A filtered coaxial cable injected microwaves; repeated switches to nonzero electric potential difference measured escape with irradiation on and off. Bias scans at fixed microwave frequency resolved separate enhancements matching calculated unequal energy level spacings. Raising temperature merged the structure toward classical response. Junction parameters were calibrated in the classical regime; reducing the superconducting critical current with a magnetic field checked thermal readout. Parameter uncertainties limited absolute resonance positions.[ref]
The companion escape experiment mounted a tunnel Josephson junction on an attenuating coaxial cable, cold-anchored its bias filters, ramped the electric current, and counted switches to nonzero electric potential difference. Rapid current shutoff limited heating. Classical escape and microwave resonance calibrated the superconducting critical current, capacitance and environmental loading. Low-temperature escape agreed with the quantum prediction using these calibrated parameters. A magnetic field reduced the superconducting critical current to keep the same apparatus thermally activated at lower temperature, testing excess noise. Frequency-dependent loading contributed uncertainty; the predicted damping correction was smaller than combined errors, so the experiment did not resolve that correction.[ref]
Together these tests connect spectral structure and escape of the same kind of collective coordinate. They do not constitute a demonstration of coherent gate operations on a qubit, or prove that every macroscopic coordinate retains quantum behavior under arbitrary environmental coupling. The attraction is more concrete: a fabricated electrical circuit admits both an independently measurable classical model and quantitative quantum tests of its phase dynamics.

Single-electron superconducting tunneling

Words: 605 Articles: 1
Superconductivity OurBigBook.com
Single-electron superconducting tunneling transfers individual electrons across a barrier into or out of quasiparticle states of a material with superconductivity. In a normal-metal–insulator–superconductor junction, abbreviated NIS, only one electrode has superconductivity. Such a junction can reveal a superconducting energy gap without supporting a DC Josephson effect between two superconducting reservoirs.
For an elementary interpretation, assume an opaque barrier, approximately constant normal-electrode density of states, and an energy-independent tunneling matrix element over the range probed. Write the superconducting density of states divided by its normal-state value as ns​(E), with E measured from its chemical potential. A signed voltage convention chosen for positive normal-electrode injection gives
Iqp​(V)=eRN​1​∫−∞∞​ns​(E)[f(E−eV)−f(E)]dE,
(39)
where f is the Fermi-Dirac distribution at a common temperature and RN​ is the normal-state junction electrical resistance. This current convention is local to the NIS measurement. The expression counts occupied initial and empty final states; it is a quasiparticle transport model, not a current-phase relation of a superconducting weak link.
An original analytic illustration uses the ideal fully gapped BCS theory spectrum at zero temperature, with positive gap edge Δ:
ns​(E)={0,∣E∣/E2−Δ2​,​∣E∣<Δ,∣E∣>Δ.​
(40)
For positive V, the occupation difference restricts the integral to 0<E<eV. Since dE2−Δ2​/dE=E/E2−Δ2​, integration and odd extension give
Iqp​(V)=⎩⎨⎧​0,eRN​sgn(V)​(eV)2−Δ2​,​∣eV∣≤Δ,∣eV∣>Δ.​
(41)
The threshold is Δ/e for this NIS model. In the corresponding zero-temperature single-electron process between two fully gapped superconductors, creating excitations on both sides instead requires ∣eV∣≥Δ1​+Δ2​. That quasiparticle threshold does not forbid coherent pair electric current below it. At finite temperature, thermally populated quasiparticles soften the simple threshold argument.
At zero temperature in the NIS approximation, RN​dIqp​/dV=ns​(eV) away from the singular gap edge. Thus a voltage sweep probes the excitation density of states. The ideal divergence is rounded in real measurements, and at nonzero temperature the derivative samples a thermally broadened range of energies. For ∣eV∣>Δ, the model has VIqp​>0: electrical work creates excitations and is dissipated on relaxation. This distinguishes its steady transport from the conservative Josephson coupling energy.

Giaever gap experiment

Words: 312
Single-electron superconducting tunneling OurBigBook.com
Ivar Giaever's gap experiment used a metal–insulator–metal sandwich. His lecture describes evaporating aluminium onto glass, briefly exposing it to air to form an oxide barrier, then evaporating lead cross-strips. Cooling in liquid helium made the lead superconducting while the aluminium stayed normal in the measurements compared. A voltmeter and ammeter supplied the junction electric potential difference and electric current readouts. Thick oxides initially gave unmeasurably small electric currents; the thinner barrier made the measurement practical. Temperature comparisons and a subsequent controlled magnetic field test changed the current-voltage characteristic with the superconducting state. [ref]
The 1960 letter compares measurements at 4.2 and 1.6K. An applied parallel magnetic field of 2.7kOe kept the lead normal; removing the magnetic field strongly suppressed the small-voltage electric current. Figure 2 divides the superconducting dI/dV by the normal-state slope. The letter calls its gap estimates preliminary and notes a temperature-dependent broadened peak. It also flags a limitation of interpreting tunneling directly through the density of states; the ideal NIS calculation above states additional assumptions explicitly. [ref]
The central inference is that the barrier lets electric current interrogate available quasiparticle states. Suppression at small electric potential difference and the rise near the gap edge support a gapped spectrum; tracking the superconducting state helps distinguish that feature from an arbitrary nonlinear barrier. A leakage path through a metallic short, imperfect barrier, or an energy-dependent tunneling matrix element limits a literal density of states interpretation. The preparation and state controls therefore matter as much as seeing a nonlinear current-voltage characteristic.

High-temperature superconductivity

Superconductivity OurBigBook.com
High-temperature superconductivity is a historical name for superconductivity above earlier metallic benchmarks. The original Bednorz-Müller experiment operated at tens of kelvins, requiring deep cooling; cuprate superconductors opened a new materials family.[ref]
A higher superconducting transition temperature enlarges the available operating range, but useful transport also needs a sufficient superconducting critical current at the operating temperature and magnetic field. In a ceramic, connections between grains can limit the whole specimen even when individual grains support superconductivity. This connects the materials problem to superconducting weak links and the Josephson effect. The parallel-path model of a superconducting ceramic below isolates why connectivity matters.

Bednorz-Müller experiment

Words: 160
High-temperature superconductivity OurBigBook.com
In 1986, Johannes Georg Bednorz and Karl Alexander Müller precipitated mixed oxalates from aqueous barium, lanthanum and copper nitrates using oxalic acid. Heating at 900∘C for five hours preceded pressing at 4kbar and reheating for sintering. X-ray diffraction resolved three phases. Rectangular pieces carried gold electrodes and indium wires; four-terminal sensing in a continuous-flow cryostat measured electrical resistance versus temperature. Annealing atmosphere and measuring electric current density were varied; larger electric current densities suppressed the drop. An onset near 35K differed from the much lower temperature where the drop approached three orders of magnitude.[ref]
This was evidence for possible granular superconductivity, with phase identification unfinished. The paper deferred Meissner effect and heat capacity tests; its proof note corrected bulk lanthanum/barium ratios. A resistive onset did not establish a uniform bulk superconducting transition temperature.[ref]
Their 1987 authored lecture describes later magnetometer checks on lead, followed by magnetic susceptibility measurements: samples with a resistive drop developed diamagnetism at somewhat lower temperatures. Combining magnetic, transport and X-ray diffraction results identified barium-substituted La2​CuO4​ as the superconducting phase. These later results strengthened the case beyond the initial mixed-phase transport evidence.[ref]

Transport and magnetic tests of superconducting ceramics

High-temperature superconductivity OurBigBook.com
Four-terminal sensing tests a conducting connection between the voltage contacts. A magnetometer instead tests the sample's magnetic response. These measurements provide complementary constraints: the first is sensitive to a continuous path, while the second also depends on screening geometry and preparation history.
For transport, define Vab​ between the inner contacts and use the reversed-electric current estimator from four-terminal sensing. Contact separation excludes drive-contact drops, but does not eliminate a drifting thermoelectric effect, Joule heating or an unrepresentative path through part of the specimen. Repeat at smaller electric currents and compare results at the same sample temperature. A finite-current result refers to that electric current and its relation to the path's superconducting critical current.
For magnetism, distinguish cooling in zero applied magnetic field and then applying it from the Field-cooled Meissner experiment. Record the applied magnetic field, background and sample geometry when interpreting magnetization or magnetic susceptibility. A transport short does not establish full Meissner effect screening throughout the specimen. Conversely, flux pinning in a type-II superconductor can make the field-cooled magnetic response differ from ideal complete expulsion. Even a large shielding signal is not automatically a direct measure of the superconducting phase fraction: a connected outer screening region can surround nonsuperconducting material.

The last case follows directly from the ideal short's constraint V=0, provided it can carry the imposed electric current below its superconducting critical current.
