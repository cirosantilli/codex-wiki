Elementary reaction

 Home Chemistry Chemical reaction Chemical kinetics Reaction mechanism
OurBigBook.com Words: 303 Articles: 1
An elementary reaction is one microscopic step in a reaction mechanism, with no intervening reaction intermediate needed within that step. Its molecularity counts the participating reacting entities. In an ideal dilute, uniformly mixed model, the law of mass action gives the directional reaction rate from the step's reactant concentrations: A⟶P has r=k[A], while A+B⟶P has r=k[A][B]. Applying those powers to a net chemical equation requires separate evidence that it is elementary.[ref]
For a reversible step A+B⇌I, apply that rule separately: rf​=kf​[A][B] and rr​=kr​[I], giving net production of I as rf​−rr​. Their difference can vanish at chemical equilibrium while both directional reaction rates remain nonzero. For 2A⟶P, the elementary normalized reaction rate is r=k[A]2, whereas A's disappearance rate is 2r; see second-order reaction for the complete factor-of-two example.
Calling a step slow does not remove the need for its actual reactant concentrations. If a reaction intermediate enters its rate law, determine that concentration from the other rate equations or a justified approximation. In the consecutive first-order reaction mechanism, an intermediate maximum is only one instant at which its production and consumption match. Replacing its entire evolution by that one balance would lose the initial buildup and delayed reaction product formation. A successful overall first-order reaction fit likewise does not establish an elementary event: both worked reaction mechanisms above provide ways for microscopic detail to remain hidden in one reactant trace.
