Power series (∑n=0∞​an​(z−c)n)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Sequence and series
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 33
A power series combines a sequence of coefficients with monomials in the displacement from a fixed centre:
∑n=0∞​an​(z−c)n.
(48)
The variable z, centre c, and coefficients can be real numbers or complex numbers. Its degree-N partial sum PN​(z)=∑n=0N​an​(z−c)n is a polynomial, so a convergent power series can turn a complicated function into increasingly accurate finite calculations. Convergence, and the error after stopping, are essential parts of that claim.
The geometric series supplies the simplest computed example:
1−z1​=1+z+z2+⋯,∣z∣<1.
(49)
At z=1/2, stopping after degree ten gives P10​(1/2)=1.9990234375, differing from the exact value 2 by 1/1024=0.0009765625. At z=0.99, the same degree gives only about 10.4662 instead of 100. The same formula has very different practical accuracy at different points; the geometric power-series remainder quantifies it.
The structure is useful beyond numerical evaluation. Within its radius of convergence, termwise differentiation of a power series turns a question about a derivative into a question about coefficients. A Taylor series chooses an​=f(n)(c)/n! when it represents the function locally. A formal power series instead uses the coefficients algebraically, without asserting numerical convergence; the existing generating function examples use that distinction to encode counting problems.
