Coercive bilinear form (B(v,v)≥α∥v∥2,α>0)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra Bilinear form
OurBigBook.com Words: 654 Articles: 1
A real bilinear form B on a normed vector space X is coercive when there is one constant α>0 such that
B(v,v)≥α∥v∥2(v∈X).
(21)
The lower bound uniformly controls every direction in the chosen norm. Unlike the upper bound defining a bounded bilinear form, it forces positive diagonal values at every nonzero vector. No symmetry is assumed. For complex Hilbert spaces, the usual analogue uses a sesquilinear form and ReB(v,v)≥α∥v∥2; it is not the same assertion for a complex bilinear form.
A concrete bounded and coercive example on R2, with the Euclidean norm, is
A=(2−1​13​),B(u,v)=uTAv.
(22)
Its quadratic form is
q(x,y)=B((x,y),(x,y))=2x2+3y2≥2(x2+y2).
(23)
The best lower constant is α=2, attained at (1,0). Nevertheless, B(e1​,e2​)=1 and B(e2​,e1​)=−1, so coercivity does not imply a symmetric bilinear form. The symmetric part of a matrix S=(A+AT)/2=diag(2,3) determines q, while the skew-symmetric matrix part changes mixed pairings without changing q. This is the symmetric and alternating parts of a bilinear form decomposition.[ref]
For the upper constant, direct multiplication gives ATA=(5−1​−110​). Its eigenvalues are (15±29​)/2, so the optimal upper constant, the spectral norm, is C=(15+29​)/2​. The two constants answer different questions: how large a mixed pairing can be, and how small a self-pairing can be relative to squared norm.
The plotted quadratic forms show the mechanism: x2−y2 descends in one direction, x2 leaves one direction uncontrolled, and 2x2+3y2 controls both. These are self-pairings; their level sets cannot reveal the skew-symmetric matrix part. Calling q a quadratic energy here refers to this diagnostic quantity, not to a claim that a nonsymmetric equation comes from minimizing an energy functional.
On a real finite-dimensional vector space with any norm, strict positivity B(v,v)>0 for every nonzero v does imply coercivity. Indeed the quadratic form is continuous, and its positive minimum on the compact unit sphere supplies α. If B is also a symmetric bilinear form, this is positive definiteness. For general B, it is the symmetric and alternating parts of a bilinear form decomposition that isolates the positive part. Switching between equivalent norms preserves coercivity, but usually changes its constant.
The infinite-dimensional vector space distinction is explicit on the real l2 sequence space:
B(u,v)=∑n=1∞​nun​vn​​.
(24)
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives boundedness with C=1, and B(v,v)>0 for every nonzero vector. But B(en​,en​)=1/n with ∥en​∥2​=1, so no positive α works. On the span of e1​,…,eN​, the best lower constant is 1/N: each finite restriction is coercive, but the control disappears as N grows. Strict positivity is therefore weaker than a uniform lower bound.
The practical payoff is a stability estimate for a weak formulation. Assume H is a real Hilbert space, B:H×H→R is both a bounded bilinear form and a coercive bilinear form, and the load ℓ∈H′ is a bounded linear functional. The Lax-Milgram theorem gives a unique u∈H satisfying B(u,v)=ℓ(v) for all v∈H, including nonsymmetric B.[ref] The dual norm ∥ℓ∥H′​=supv=0​∣ℓ(v)∣/∥v∥ measures load size. Testing with v=u gives the short energy estimate
α∥u∥2≤B(u,u)=ℓ(u)≤∥ℓ∥H′​∥u∥,∥u∥≤α∥ℓ∥H′​​.
(25)
For u=0 the bound holds directly; otherwise divide by ∥u∥. Subtracting the equations for two loads and using the same argument gives ∥u1​−u2​∥≤∥ℓ1​−ℓ2​∥H′​/α. Thus a positive lower constant limits the response to load errors. The upper bound and completeness of the Hilbert space enter the existence theorem; the estimate itself uses the lower bound and the load's dual norm.
For the displayed matrix A, take ℓ(v)=fTv. Then B(u,v)=ℓ(v) for every v means ATu=f, not Au=f, with this ordering of the bilinear form. Since detA=7,
u=71​(3−1​12​)f,∥u∥2​≤21​∥f∥2​.
(26)
For f=t(1,0)T, the exact response is u=t(3,−1)T/7 and ∥u∥2​=10​∣t∣/7<∣t∣/2. For f=t(0,1)T, it is u=t(1,2)T/7, with norm 5​∣t∣/7. The actual maximal amplification is the operator norm ∥(AT)−1∥2​=2/(15−29​)​<1/2: the coercivity estimate is valid but need not be sharp for a nonsymmetric bilinear form.
