An algebra homomorphism is a K-linear map preserving multiplication, and preserving the unit under the unital convention. An algebra isomorphism is a bijective algebra homomorphism; its inverse then preserves the same operations.

A composition series is a chain 0=V0​⊊V1​⊊⋯⊊Vr​=V of submodules with every factor Vi​/Vi−1​ simple; its length is r. Suppose 0⊂S⊂V is one of length two, and put T=V/S.
For any nonzero proper submodule U, either U∩S=S or U∩S=0, by simplicity of S. In the first case, simplicity of V/S forces U=S. In the second, the quotient map embeds U nontrivially into T, so U≅T and its image is all of T. Hence V=S⊕U, and V/U≅S. Thus every nonzero proper submodule is simple and has simple quotient; every composition series has length two.
The Jordan–Hölder theorem states that any two composition series of a finite-length module have equal length and the same multiset of simple factors, up to isomorphism and order. The preceding argument proves this for length two: either U=S, giving factors S,T, or U=S, giving factors T,S.
The map A→S, a↦ax, is a surjective module homomorphism with kernel I=AnnA​(x). The first isomorphism theorem for modules gives A/I≅S as left modules; I need only be a left ideal. Every simple module is cyclic, since any nonzero x generates a nonzero submodule. As a quotient of the finite-dimensional regular module A, it is finite-dimensional. Extend I to a composition series of A; its simple quotient A/I is one of the factors. Jordan-Hölder shows that its isomorphism type belongs to the fixed finite list of factors of any one composition series of A. Hence there are finitely many simple-module isomorphism types.

Its kernel on W is a submodule of Sk​, so is zero or Sk​. If zero, W≅∏j<k​Sj​. But its projection onto Sk​ is onto, making Sk​ a composition factor of that product, contrary to Jordan-Hölder and the pairwise distinct types. Thus the kernel is all of Sk​. Surjectivity on the first factors together with this kernel proves W is the entire product. The first isomorphism theorem now gives

A simple module is a nonzero module with no proper nonzero submodule. For a finite-dimensional simple complex module S, any endomorphism φ has a complex eigenvalue λ. The nonzero kernel of φ−λI is an A-submodule, hence all of S. Thus φ=λI, proving Schur's lemma.
More generally a nonzero map between two simple modules is an isomorphism, because its kernel is zero and its image is the whole target. The endomorphism algebra of S1​⊕S2​ is the block algebra with entries HomA​(Sj​,Si​). For nonisomorphic summands its off-diagonal entries vanish and its diagonal entries are scalars, giving C×C. For isomorphic summands, choose one fixed isomorphism to identify them; every block is scalar and block composition is matrix multiplication, giving M2​(C).
A finite-dimensional algebra is a semisimple algebra when its left regular module is a direct sum of simple modules. The complex Artin–Wedderburn theorem gives A≅∏i​Mdi​​(C), with the multiset of positive integers di​ uniquely determined. Its dimension is ∑i​di2​. For dimension eight only sizes one and two can occur; the number of size-two blocks is zero, one, or two. Thus there are exactly three isomorphism types:
C8,M2​(C)×C4,M2​(C)×M2​(C).​
(3)
They are distinct by the uniqueness clause, or by their numbers of one-dimensional simple modules. If G is nonabelian, CG is noncommutative because its group elements form a basis, so the first type is excluded. The augmentation ∑ag​g↦∑ag​ provides a one-dimensional simple module, excluding the third type. Consequently
CG≅C4×M2​(C).​
(4)
There is one simple module for each matrix block, of dimension the block size, so exactly four are one-dimensional. Every nonabelian group of order eight therefore has this same complex group algebra.

Complete reducibility and character orthogonality imply that the squared multiplicities sum to three. The trivial constituent has multiplicity one, so the remaining two units come from two distinct nontrivial constituents, each with multiplicity one. Their degrees add to 4−1=3, so they are two and one. Thus
χΩ​=1+χ+η,χ(1)=2,η(1)=1.​

The two-dimensional constituent is the complexification of the geometric action on the plane. Every subgroup of Zn has at most n generators, by induction: its projection onto the first coordinate is either zero, reducing to Zn−1, or dZ for some d>0. A Sylow subgroup for the prime p is a maximal p-subgroup; in a finite group of order pam with p∤m it has order pa. Sylow theorems assert existence, containment of every p-subgroup in a Sylow p-subgroup, conjugacy of all Sylow p-subgroups, and the counting conditions np​∣m, np​≡1(modp).
Since N is a normal subgroup, NP is a subgroup and
[N:N∩P]=[NP:P]∣[G:P].

The final index is prime to p. Since N∩P is a p-subgroup, its index in N being prime to p means it contains the entire p-part of ∣N∣. Hence N∩P is Sylow in N.

Define G(0)=G and G(i+1)=[G(i),G(i)]. A group is a soluble group if this derived series reaches the identity after finitely many steps. Its derived length is the least d with G(d)=1.
Let V={1,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)}≤A4​. This Klein four-group is a normal subgroup and Abelian; A4​/V has order three, so A4′​⊆V. The group A4​ is nonabelian, so its derived subgroup is nontrivial. Conjugation by a three-cycle permutes the three nonidentity elements of V transitively. Since the derived subgroup is a normal subgroup, containing any one forces it to contain all three. Thus A4′​=V, and V′=1. Therefore A4​ has derived length two.
If H≤G, every commutator in H is one in G, and induction gives H(i)⊆G(i) for all i. Thus a subgroup of a solvable group is solvable, with no larger derived length.
The sign map gives S4′​⊆A4​. The commutator [(12),(23)] is a three-cycle. Normality of S4′​ supplies every conjugate three-cycle, and three-cycles generate A4​. Hence S4′​=A4​, so the complete derived series is
S4​⊃A4​⊃V⊃1.​
(13)
Every inclusion is strict, proving solvability and derived length three.

For the preliminary index assertion, the coset action gives a homomorphism G→Sn​. Its kernel is a normal subgroup and cannot be all of G, because the action is transitive on n>1 points. Simplicity makes the kernel trivial. The sign homomorphism of this faithful action must be trivial: otherwise simplicity would identify G with C2​, impossible for a transitive action of degree at least three. Thus G embeds in An​, and Lagrange gives
∣G∣∣n!/2.​
(14)
A group of order 112=16⋅7 has a Sylow two-subgroup of index seven. If the group were simple, the preceding result would give 112∣7!/2=2520. This fails because 112 is divisible by 16 while 2520 has only three factors of two. Thus there is no simple group of order 112.
For order 56, Sylow gives n7​∈{1,8}. If it is one, the order-seven Sylow is a normal subgroup. Otherwise its eight Sylows of order seven intersect only at the identity, giving 8⋅6=48 nonidentity elements of order seven. Only eight elements remain including the identity. Every Sylow two-subgroup has order eight and consists entirely of these remaining elements. Hence all Sylow two-subgroups coincide and the order-eight subgroup is a normal subgroup. Either case gives a nontrivial proper normal subgroup, so no group of order 56 is simple.

Distinct subgroups of either prime order intersect only at the identity, and elements of the two orders are disjoint.
