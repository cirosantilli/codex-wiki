The center of an associative algebra is the subalgebra
Z(A)={z∈A:za=az for every a∈A}.
(1)
For a pair (z1​,z2​), commuting with every (a1​,a2​) means z1​a1​=a1​z1​ and z2​a2​=a2​z2​. Varying the two coordinates independently proves both inclusions, hence
Z(A1​×A2​)=Z(A1​)×Z(A2​).​

The Artin–Wedderburn theorem says that a finite-dimensional semisimple algebra over C is a finite product ∏j​Mdj​​(C): the division algebras in the general theorem reduce to C because the base field is algebraically closed. Each factor has center of an associative algebra CIdj​​. Consequently a central element corresponds to (λj​Idj​​)j​. If it is nilpotent, then λjn​=0 for every j, whence every λj​=0. Therefore z=0​. The centrality assumption matters: a full matrix algebra has nonzero nilpotent elements.

The Jordan–Hölder theorem applies to a module of finite length: if
0=M0​⊊M1​⊊⋯⊊Mr​=M
(5)
is a composition series of a module, its successive quotients are simple modules. Any two such composition series of a module have the same length r, and their composition factors are pairwise isomorphic after a permutation, with multiplicities preserved. Every finite-dimensional module over A has finite length, since strict inclusions increase vector-space dimension. A finite-dimensional algebra over a field does not by itself make every possible module finite-dimensional, so the finite-length hypothesis should be retained in the statement.

For a∈A, m∈M,
(f⋅x)(am)=f(am)x=af(m)x=a(f⋅x)(m).

By the Artin–Wedderburn theorem, a finite-dimensional complex simple algebra is Md​(C), with a unique simple module up to isomorphism, namely the column module S=Cd. Its left regular module is S⊕d. The Schur lemma gives EndA​(S)=C, so
dimC​VM​=d=dimC​M=dimC​A​.​
(8)
The right action can be seen explicitly. For a row vector r, the map fr​(m)=mr is A-linear, and all such maps are obtained this way, since the row space and VM​ both have dimension d. Moreover fr​⋅x=frx​. Any nonzero row generates the whole row space under right multiplication by matrices: if rj​=0, suitable matrix units send it to each coordinate row. Thus VM​ is a simple right A-module.

The Jacobson radical is J=Ce12​. Indeed J2=0, so J is contained in the Jacobson radical, and A/J≅C×C is a semisimple algebra, giving the reverse inclusion. The radical of a module of the left regular module is therefore
rad(A​A)=Ce12​.​

Every finite-dimensional C[X]-module is a finitely generated torsion module over the principal ideal domain C[X]. The structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain gives
M≅⨁j=1r​C[X]/(pj​),p1​∣p2​∣⋯∣pr​,
(17)
where the pj​ are uniquely determined monic nonconstant polynomials. Equivalently, factoring over C and using the Chinese remainder theorem gives
M≅λ∈C⨁​ j⨁​C[X]/((X−λ)mλj​).​
(18)
There are finitely many summands, and the multisets of positive integers mλj​ are uniquely determined. Each displayed primary cyclic summand is an indecomposable module.

Apply the decomposition to the module on which X acts as α. On C[X]/((X−λ)m) put t=X−λ and use the ordered basis
tm−1, tm−2,…, t, 1.

A two-dimensional indecomposable module over C[X] is exactly A/((X−λ)2) for some λ∈C. Write its operator as T=λI+N with N2=0 and N=0. On restriction of scalars,
Y=T2=λ2I+2λN.
(27)
For λ=0 this is the zero operator on a two-dimensional space, which splits as two one-dimensional submodules. For λ=0, its nilpotent part is nonzero of square zero, so it is one Jordan block and is indecomposable. Therefore the full list is
A/((X−λ)2),λ∈C∖{0}.​
(28)
Different λ give nonisomorphic A-modules; the restricted B-modules for λ and −λ are isomorphic.

a OurBigBook.com
If ∣G∣=pam with p∤m, a Sylow subgroup for p has order pa. Let P,Q be two such subgroups. The action of P by left translation on G/Q has orbits of powers of p, while ∣G/Q∣=m is prime to p. There is a fixed coset gQ, giving g−1Pg≤Q; equality follows from their orders. Thus all Sylow p-subgroups are conjugate.
Let P now act by conjugation on their set. If it fixes Q, then P≤NG​(Q), so PQ is a subgroup of p-power order and must equal Q; hence P=Q. The unique fixed point is P itself and all other orbits have size divisible by p. Consequently np​≡1(modp)​.
For the strengthened conclusion, the stabilizer of Q=P is H=P∩NG​(Q). The product HQ is a subgroup of p-power order, so H≤Q. If distinct Sylow subgroups meet trivially, then H≤P∩Q=1. Every other orbit therefore has the full size ∣P∣, giving
np​≡1(mod∣P∣).​
(29)
Conjugacy also gives np​=[G:NG​(P)], a divisor of [G:P].

The quaternion group of order eight has a unique involution, which generates its center of a group. A nontrivial intersection S∩S′ is a nontrivial finite 2-group, so by Cauchy's theorem for finite groups it contains an involution. This must be both the unique involution t of S and the unique involution of S′. Thus t∈Z(S′).
It follows that S′≤CG​(t)=NG​(S). But S is a normal Sylow subgroup of its normalizer, so it is that normalizer's unique Sylow subgroup. Hence S′=S​. Distinct Sylow subgroups therefore intersect trivially. From the preceding result,
n2​≡1(mod8),n2​∣168/8=21.
(30)
Of 1,3,7,21, only 1 is congruent to 1 modulo 8. Therefore n2​=1, so S is normal in G.

The class equation implies that a nontrivial finite p-group has nontrivial center of a group. Here a center of order 9 would make G/Z(G) cyclic; a group with cyclic quotient by its center of a group is abelian, contradicting the hypothesis. Thus
∣Z(G)∣=3,G/Z(G)≅C3​×C3​.​
(31)
The quotient of order nine must be noncyclic for the same reason. For x∈/Z(G), ⟨x,Z(G)⟩ has order at least nine and lies in the proper centralizer CG​(x). Lagrange's theorem therefore forces ∣CG​(x)∣=9​. Also 1=G′≤Z(G), so G′=Z(G) and all commutators are central of order dividing three.
Classification and splitting. First suppose a has order nine. Then N=⟨a⟩ is normal of index three and Z(G)=⟨a3⟩. For b∈/N, conjugation on N is nontrivial, since otherwise a,b would generate an abelian group. Its order divides three, so, after replacing b by its inverse if needed, bab−1=a4. Write b3=a3k. Central commutators and 3∣(23​) give (aib)3=a3ib3; choose i=−k modulo three to obtain an element c=aib of order three outside N. This proves
G≅C9​⋊C3​,cac−1=a4.​
(32)
There is a unique resulting isomorphism type, since the two nontrivial actions of order three are interchanged by reversing the complement's generator.
If there is no element of order nine, every nonidentity element has order three. Choose a,b whose images form a basis of G/Z(G) and put c=[a,b]. These elements generate G, and c generates Z(G). The subgroup N=⟨a,c⟩≅C32​ has index three and is normal. The line ⟨b⟩ meets N trivially and complements it. Conjugation by b fixes c and sends a to ac or ac−1; choosing the central generator removes this sign. Thus there is one isomorphism type,
G≅C32​⋊C3​≅UT3​(F3​),​
(33)
with the nontrivial unipotent action on F32​. These presentations are realized by the indicated semidirect products and have order 27. Their exponents are nine and three respectively, so they are not isomorphic. This proves both existence and completeness of the two cases.
Their ordinary character tables are the same, up to relabelling. In either group each of the 24 noncentral elements has conjugacy class of size three, giving eight noncentral classes and three central singleton classes. Each noncentral class is xZ(G), since conjugates of x differ from x by central commutators and its class has size three. There are nine linear characters, arising from G/G′≅C32​, and eleven irreducible characters altogether. The two remaining degrees satisfy d12​+d22​=27−9=18, so both are three.
Put Z(G)=⟨z⟩ and ω=e2πi/3. Label the noncentral classes by nonzero (i,j)∈F32​. For (r,s)∈F32​ the nine linear characters have values
ψrs​(1)=ψrs​(z)=ψrs​(z2)=1,ψrs​(i,j)=ωri+sj.
(34)
At a noncentral element, column orthogonality gives a squared column norm of nine. The nine linear characters already contribute nine, so both degree-three characters vanish there. By the Schur lemma, a central element has value 3ωk in an irreducible representation of degree three. The central character cannot be trivial, since that would factor the representation through the abelian quotient. Orthogonality of the identity and z columns then forces the two nontrivial central characters to occur once each. The final two rows, on 1,z,z2 and the eight other columns, are
(3,3ω,3ω2,0,…,0),(3,3ω2,3ω,0,…,0).
(35)
This description gives the same entire character table in both cases. The differing element orders are additional power-map data, which an ordinary character table need not determine.

The identity column gives the group order as the sum of squared irreducible character degrees:
∣G∣=1+4⋅72+82+3⋅92=504.​
(36)
By column orthogonality, the squared norm of a column is the corresponding centralizer order. In source order these orders and the resulting conjugacy class sizes are
∣CG​(g)∣=(504,8,9,9,9,9,7,7,7),​
(37)
∣gG∣=(1,63,56,56,56,56,72,72,72).
(38)
For example the fourth column has squared norm 1+1+(∣α∣2+∣β∣2+∣γ∣2)+1=9, while the seventh has squared norm 1+1+(∣ξ∣2+∣η∣2+∣ϕ∣2)=7.
An element's order divides its centralizer order. Cauchy's theorem for finite groups provides an involution, and only C2​ has an even centralizer order. Hence that entire class consists of involutions. The last three classes consist of elements of order seven. In C3​,C4​,C5​,C6​ the possibilities are three and nine. The three real numbers α,β,γ are roots of t3−3t+1, which has no rational root and is irreducible over Q. A real character value at an element of order three belongs to Q(e2πi/3)∩R=Q. Therefore the irrational real entries in C4​,C5​,C6​ rule out order three there. Cubing any of these order-nine elements produces an element of order three, which must belong to the sole remaining class C3​. Thus
ord(C1​),…,ord(C9​)=(1,2,3,9,9,9,7,7,7).​
(39)
The three deductions below give nine Sylow subgroups for 2 with pairwise trivial intersections. Their conjugation action is transitive. Fix one, S. If T=S, put H=S∩NG​(T). The product HT is a 2-subgroup, so maximality of T gives H≤T. Trivial intersection then gives H=1. Thus the orbit of T under S has size eight, exhausting the other eight points. The point stabilizer NG​(S) is therefore transitive on the remaining points: the action is doubly transitive.
For a doubly transitive action, its permutation character has the form 1+θ with θ irreducible. Here θ(1)=8, and the unique degree-eight row is χ6​. Therefore the numbers of fixed points are the entries of
π=χ1​+χ6​=(9,1,0,0,0,0,2,2,2).​
(40)
Every nonidentity element consequently fixes at most two points. This also shows that the action is faithful.

Over Fq​, the first nonzero column of an invertible 2×2 matrix has q2−1 choices and the second has q2−q choices. The surjective determinant homomorphism onto Fq×​ has kernel SL2​(q). Thus
∣SL2​(8)∣=8−1(82−1)(82−8)​=504.​
(43)
It acts on the nine one-dimensional subspaces of F82​, namely the projective line F8​∪{∞}, by fractional linear transformations. Any element acting trivially fixes 0,∞,1, which forces it to be scalar. A scalar matrix in SL2​(8) has a2=1; in characteristic two this implies a=1. Thus the action is faithful.
The action is transitive: any nonzero vector can be completed to a determinant-one basis, so any line can be reached. The stabilizer of ∞=[1:0] is
{(a0​ba−1​):a∈F8×​, b∈F8​}≅(C2​)3⋊C7​.​
(44)
On finite points this is x↦a2x+ab. Squaring permutes the seven nonzero field elements, so these are exactly the affine maps x↦ux+v. In particular translations act transitively on all eight finite points. Thus the full action is doubly transitive. The point stabilizer has order 8⋅7=56, and all point stabilizers are conjugate.
