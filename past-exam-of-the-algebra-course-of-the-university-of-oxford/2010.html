The structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain applies because every Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain. In its cyclic decomposition form, every finitely generated module has a decomposition
M≅Rr⊕R/(d1​)⊕⋯⊕R/(dt​),d1​∣d2​∣⋯∣dt​,​
(1)
where the di​ are nonzero nonunits. The nonnegative integer r and the invariant factors of a finitely generated module di​ are unique, with each di​ determined up to multiplication by a unit. The zero module corresponds to no summands. Equivalently, factor the di​ into powers of prime elements and use the Chinese remainder theorem to obtain a direct sum of cyclic prime-power torsion modules and a free summand.

A simple module is a nonzero module with no submodules other than zero and itself. Equivalently, every nonzero element generates the entire module: Am=M whenever m=0.

ii OurBigBook.com
A composition series of a module is a finite chain
0=M0​⊊M1​⊊⋯⊊Mr​=M
(9)
of submodules whose factors Mj​/Mj−1​ are simple modules. A finite-dimensional module has such a series by induction on its dimension, choosing a maximal proper submodule at each step.
The Jordan–Hölder theorem for modules states that any two composition series of a module have the same length and the same simple factors up to isomorphism and reordering. Thus the multiset of composition factors is an invariant of the module, even when the series itself is not unique.

iii OurBigBook.com
The general Schur's lemma says that a homomorphism f:M→N between simple modules over any unital ring is either zero or an isomorphism. Indeed, kerf is a submodule of M and imf is a submodule of N. If f=0, simplicity gives kerf=0 and imf=N. In particular, EndR​(M) is a division ring.
For a complex algebra and a finite-dimensional simple module M, the stronger conclusion is
EndR​(M)=CidM​.​
(10)
For f∈EndR​(M), the fundamental theorem of algebra gives an eigenvalue λ∈C. The endomorphism f−λI has nonzero kernel, so the general Schur's lemma forces it to be zero. Consequently a homomorphism between two such simple modules is zero if they are nonisomorphic, and their homomorphism space is one-dimensional if they are isomorphic.
The finite-dimensional qualification is essential for this scalar conclusion. For example, the field C(t) is a complex algebra, simple as a module over itself, and its endomorphisms include multiplication by t, which is not a complex scalar.

Let M be a nonzero simple module for A=k[X]/(X3) and let N be the action of X. Since X is central, NM is a submodule, and N3=0. If NM=M, iterating would give N3M=M, impossible. Thus NM=0.
The action therefore factors through A/(X)≅k. A simple module over a field is one-dimensional: the span of any nonzero vector is already a nonzero submodule. Conversely, k with X acting as zero is simple. Hence there is exactly one simple module up to isomorphism, namely
A/(X)≅k,X⋅v=0.​
(11)
This argument covers all simple modules, without assuming their finite dimensionality in advance.

A semisimple module is a direct sum of simple modules. A finite-dimensional semisimple algebra is one whose left regular module is semisimple; equivalently, all its finite-dimensional modules are semisimple, or every submodule of a finite-dimensional module has a complementary submodule.
The Maschke theorem states that, for a finite group G and a field k with ∣G∣=0 in k, every finite-dimensional kG-module is semisimple, so kG is a semisimple algebra. The assumption is equivalent to saying that the characteristic of a field does not divide ∣G∣. The averaging construction makes a projection onto a submodule equivariant by replacing P with ∣G∣−1∑g∈G​gPg−1.

For the Abelian case of Cauchy theorem for groups, use induction on ∣G∣. Choose a=e. If p divides m=∣a∣, then am/p has order p. Otherwise ⟨a⟩ is a nontrivial normal subgroup of order prime to p, and G/⟨a⟩ has smaller order still divisible by p. Induction gives a coset of order p; any lift b has order divisible by p, because the order of its image divides its own order. A suitable power of b then has order p. This uses no classification of finite Abelian groups.
For a finite p-group, the class equation is
∣G∣=∣Z(G)∣+∑[g]={z}​[G:CG​(g)],
(22)
where the sum is over noncentral conjugacy classes. Each index is a positive power of p, hence divisible by p. Therefore p divides ∣Z(G)∣, and the center of a group is nontrivial.
If ∣G∣=pn with n>1 and Z(G)=G, this is already a proper nontrivial normal subgroup. If Z(G)=G, the group is Abelian; the established case of Cauchy theorem for groups gives a subgroup of order p, which is normal and proper. Thus
No group of order pn (n>1) is simple.​
(23)

If ∣G∣=pam with p∤m, a Sylow p-subgroup has order pa. The Sylow theorems say that such subgroups exist; every p-subgroup is contained in one; they are all conjugate; and their number np​ divides m and satisfies np​≡1(modp).
Suppose ∣G∣=p2q with distinct primes and G were simple. A Sylow subgroup of order q has order q, and nq​>1 divides p2, so nq​=p or p2. If nq​=p, conjugation gives a nontrivial homomorphism G→Sp​. Its kernel is normal and, by simplicity, trivial. Hence p2q would divide p! by Lagrange's theorem, impossible because p! has only one factor of p.
If nq​=p2, distinct Sylow subgroups of order q meet only in the identity and together contain p2(q−1) nonidentity elements. Only p2 elements of G remain, including the identity. Every Sylow p-subgroup lies entirely in that remaining set and itself has p2 elements. They must therefore all be the same subgroup, making it normal, again a contradiction. Thus no group of order p2q is simple. If p=q, the preceding p-group argument applies.
For distinct p,q, a finite Abelian group is the direct product of groups of its primary subgroups. A group of order p2 is either cyclic or has every nonidentity element of order p, in which case choosing two independent elements gives Cp​×Cp​. Consequently the possibilities are
Cp2q​,Cp​×Cpq​(p=q).​
(24)
If the primes coincide, the Fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups gives instead Cp3​, Cp2​×Cp​, and Cp3​. This classification is used only for the requested list, not for the proof above.

First establish the two general claims about polycyclic groups. Intersect a subnormal cyclic-factor series (Gi​) with H≤G. Then H∩Gi​⊴H∩Gi+1​, and the inclusion induces an injective homomorphism
H∩Gi​H∩Gi+1​​⟶Gi​Gi+1​​.
(25)
A subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic, so this is a cyclic-factor subnormal series for H, after omitting repetitions. This proves subgroups of polycyclic groups are polycyclic.
To prove the generator bound, choose gi​∈Gi​ whose coset generates Gi​/Gi−1​ whenever that factor is nontrivial. Inductively Gi​=⟨Gi−1​,gi​⟩, so G=⟨g1​,…,gn​⟩. This is the generator bound from a cyclic-factor series: at most n generators suffice.
For the first example, take G=C2m​ with its coordinate flag
0<C2​<C22​<⋯<C2m​.
(26)
It has length exactly m and each factor is C2​, so all factors are cyclic.

In a finite group, choose a maximal chain of normal subgroups from 1 to G. Finiteness ensures such a chain exists, and maximality leaves no normal subgroup of G strictly between successive terms. This is a chief series.
The infinite cyclic group has no chief series. In any finite chain starting at zero, the first nonzero subgroup is nZ. But 2nZ is a nonzero proper subgroup of it and is normal in Z. Thus zero is not maximal among the normal subgroups in that first term, contradicting the definition.
For any chief series, Gn−1​ is a maximal proper normal subgroup of G. The subgroup correspondence theorem identifies normal subgroups of G/Gn−1​ with normal subgroups of G containing Gn−1​. Consequently the top chief factor is simple.
Other chief factors need only have no proper nonzero subgroup invariant under the action of the whole group. In A4​,
1<V4​<A4​​
(29)
is a chief series, where V4​={1,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)}. Conjugation in A4​ permutes the three involutions transitively, so none of its three order-two subgroups is normal in A4​. Thus V4​ is a minimal nontrivial normal subgroup; the top quotient is C3​. But the bottom chief factor V4​/1≅C2​×C2​ is not simple, since it has proper order-two normal subgroups as an abstract group.

χ↓H​=∑i​di​ψi​,di​∈Z≥0​.
