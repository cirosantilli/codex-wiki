Use two collinear quantum point contacts in a clean two-dimensional electron gas. Surface split gates deplete the electron gas beneath them, leaving two short constrictions facing one another across an open region. The first injects a directional beam from a biased reservoir; the second collects carriers into a downstream reservoir, with electric current flowing in the same spatial direction through both constrictions. Additional surrounding contacts can absorb carriers that miss the collector. A direct collector signal at zero magnetic field, sensitive to alignment and to obstruction of the intervening path, demonstrates ballistic electron transport across the separation.
An illustrative GaAs device uses gate gaps of a few hundred nanometres and electrically tunable constriction widths of tens to a few hundred nanometres, with injector–collector separation of a few micrometres. These are typical design scales, not dimensions supplied for a particular chip in the question. The electrical width can be much smaller than the lithographic gap because depletion extends laterally. A one-mode opening has width comparable to roughly half a Fermi wavelength; it is not simply any opening below one micrometre. Low-temperature operation suppresses inelastic scattering and resolves transverse modes. The direct trajectory length should be comfortably below the relevant transport mean free path.

Here “focusing” refers primarily to collimation and efficient injector–collector coupling, not a claim that every emitted trajectory converges to one exact geometric point.
In scanning gate microscopy, a conducting tip biased sufficiently negatively creates a local repulsive potential and, when strong enough, a small depleted region in the electron gas beneath it. Raster the tip and record the collector current or differential conductance. Placing the perturbation on a strongly contributing path deflects or backscatters electrons and changes collection, producing spatial contrast. A primary scanning-probe experiment records conductance changes as a negatively charged tip depletes and backscatters the two-dimensional gas.[ref]
The resulting image is a map of transport sensitivity to the tip, not a literal nonperturbing photograph of an electron trajectory. Broad electrostatic gating of either constriction should be distinguished from local beam disruption. Weak smooth disorder can create branches even in a high-mobility sample. Where electron phase coherence is preserved, interference between alternative paths can add fringes, often with a scale comparable to half a Fermi wavelength for a round-trip path-length change. Ballistic motion and phase-coherent interference are related but distinct requirements.

Mobility measures momentum relaxation. Predominantly small-angle elastic scattering can occur more frequently than that transport time, and the phase-coherence time is different again. Thus the calculation supports a several-micrometre transport scale, without proving that every electron experiences literally no perturbation over that distance.
As temperature rises, the directed ballistic collector signal decreases. Increased electron-phonon scattering and other inelastic processes reduce survival of the directed beam; thermal averaging broadens the mode and angular selection, and interference contrast also diminishes. A diffuse background current can remain, so disappearance of beam contrast need not mean all current through the device vanishes.
