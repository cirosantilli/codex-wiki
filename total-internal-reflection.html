Total internal reflection

OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 4
Total internal reflection occurs when a wave travels from higher to lower refractive index above the critical angle for total internal reflection, leaving no propagating transmitted plane electromagnetic wave normal to the interface.
At an infinite planar interface, total internal reflection returns all incident normal power in the reflected plane electromagnetic wave. Assume homogeneous dielectrics with isotropy, linearity, no dielectric loss, magnetic permeability μ0​, and positive real refractive indices n1​>n2​. Above the critical angle for total internal reflection, both TE polarization and TM polarization have unit optical reflectance despite the external evanescent wave.[ref]
This makes total internal reflection useful for guiding light in an optical fiber: suitable internal incidence angles keep energy inside successive boundaries. Its more surprising feature is that perfect optical reflectance coexists with an external electric field. The evanescent field in total internal reflection can interact with a nearby object, as the frustrated total internal reflection experiment demonstrates.
For an original idealized example, choose n1​=1.5, n2​=1 at vacuum wavelength λ0​=633nm. At angle of incidence 40∘, Snell law for electromagnetic waves gives an angle of refraction arcsin(1.5sin40∘)≈74.6186∘, so a transmitted light ray still emerges. At θc​≈41.8103∘, its direction becomes tangent to the boundary. At 50∘, the proposed sine of a real angle of refraction would be 1.5sin50∘≈1.14907>1: the transmitted wavevector must instead have an imaginary normal component. These are predictions for the stipulated media, not measured properties of a particular block.
A real semicircular-block experiment isolates this threshold. The University of Maryland laboratory instructions place a laser, motor-driven rotation stage and photodetector together; an aperture limits the detector voltage. The semicircular block is centered on the rotation axis, with the photodetector aligned along its flat face. The instructions identify a strong grazing-beam signal and a weaker signal just before the critical angle for total internal reflection. The readout is detector voltage versus stage angle. Retracing the incident laser beam identifies normal incidence for alignment.[ref]
The semicircular shape supplies a geometric control: a light ray passing radially through the curved face enters at normal incidence, avoiding additional entrance refraction. The weaker signal near the flat face locates a limiting angle of refraction; a vanishing remote-detector signal alone would not establish that the external electric field vanishes. Beam width and alignment limit angular resolution; no measured threshold or decay depth is supplied.[ref]
