Pascal's triangle (Tn,k​=(kn​))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Combinatorics Binomial coefficient
OurBigBook.com Words: 394
Row n of Pascal's triangle consists of the binomial coefficients (0n​),…,(nn​), beginning with row 0. Boundary entries are one, and Pascal's identity says each interior entry is the sum of the two entries above it. The hockey-stick identity evaluates sums along its diagonals.
The highlighted entry in the binomial coefficient illustration is (25​)=10. Split two-person teams according to whether they contain the fifth person: six choose both members from the other four, and four contain the fifth person together with one of those four. Thus 10=6+4. All entries can be generated by the same local rule, so a large table of counts needs no repeated enumeration of its subsets.
Reading row seven. Label the entries in each row from k=0 on the left through k=n on the right. Rows zero through seven are shown below. The highlighted entry T7,3​=35 comes from T6,2​=15 and T6,3​=20. Starting with row zero (1), the edges remain one and Pascal's rule determines every interior entry, so mathematical induction uniquely determines the whole array.
The triangular and rectangular layouts describe the same entries. For a north-east lattice path ending at (a,b), set n=a+b and k=b, so P(a,b)=Tn,k​. The predecessor (a−1,b) has indices (n−1,k), while (a,b−1) has indices (n−1,k−1). Thus the grid's west and south arrivals are exactly the triangle's two parents. This change of indices explains why the north-east lattice path recurrence and Pascal's rule agree.
There is also a global check on the local construction: row n sums to 2n. Every subset of an n-element set appears exactly once among the classes of sizes 0,…,n, and each element has two inclusion choices by the rule of product. In row seven, 1+7+21+35+35+21+7+1=128=27. Replacing every subset by its complement reverses the row, explaining Tn,k​=Tn,n−k​. The binomial theorem packages the whole row as the coefficients of (1+x)n.
Blaise Pascal's Traité du triangle arithmétique, published in 1665, presents an arithmetic array and connects it to combinations, games of chance, and powers of binomial expressions. This makes the connection between a counting table and several applications visible in the original work.
