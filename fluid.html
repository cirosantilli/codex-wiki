Fluid

 Home Physics Branches of physics Fluid mechanics
OurBigBook.com Words: 185
A fluid is a material that flows under sustained shear stress and cannot maintain a static shear stress in its fluid state. Liquids and gases are the usual examples. A Newtonian fluid has deviatoric stress proportional to its rate-of-strain tensor, whereas more general fluid constitutive equations can be nonlinear or have memory. In a porous medium, the fluid occupies connected voids within a load-bearing solid framework. The important distinction is the response to a continuing tangential force: an ordinary elastic solid can settle into a fixed deformation, while an ordinary fluid continues to deform. A fluid can nevertheless be at rest when no unbalanced shear stress drives it. [ref]
For a worked example, place a Newtonian fluid between parallel plates separated by h=1mm, keep the lower plate fixed and move the upper one at U=0.10ms−1. With no-slip boundary conditions, steady flow, no imposed pressure gradient and negligible edge effects, Couette flow has u(y)=Uy/h. For dynamic viscosity μ=0.001Pas, the shear stress magnitude is
τ=μdydu​=μhU​=0.10Pa.
(10)
The velocity gradient describes neighbouring layers sliding at different speeds. Doubling U doubles the required shear stress; maintaining the same velocity keeps producing deformation, rather than fixing a final shape. This gives viscosity a concrete meaning: resistance to a rate of motion, not a prohibition on motion.
