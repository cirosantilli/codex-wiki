Ramsey number for triangles (R(3,3)=6)

 ... Area of mathematics Combinatorics Ramsey theory Ramsey's theorem Finite Ramsey theorem Ramsey number
OurBigBook.com Words: 486 Articles: 2
Every red-blue edge coloring of the complete graph K6​ contains a monochromatic triangle in a graph, and some coloring of K5​ contains none. Together the five-cycle Ramsey coloring and six-vertex monochromatic triangle argument give
5<R(3,3)≤6,R(3,3)=6.
(7)
The threshold is exact: one carefully chosen coloring establishes the lower bound, while the upper bound must handle every coloring.
A familiar interpretation uses six people and colors each pair red if they know one another and blue if they do not. Assuming the relation is symmetric and every pair falls into exactly one case, there are either three mutual acquaintances or three mutual strangers. The conclusion concerns all three pairs within the group, not merely two relationships through a common person. There is no prediction about which color wins or which three people form the group.
The small size makes exhaustive verification practical. The complete graph K5​ has (25​)=10 edges, giving 210=1024 red-blue colorings of labeled vertices; exactly 12 have no monochromatic triangle in a graph. They are the choices of a red cycle graph of length five, whose blue complement graph is another five-cycle. The complete graph K6​ has (26​)=15 edges, giving 215=32768 colorings. Checking all (36​)=20 triples in every coloring finds none without a monochromatic triangle in a graph; in fact the minimum number is two. These counts can be reproduced by assigning one bit to each unordered pair and testing whether the three bits of each triple agree. The monochromatic-triangle counting formula gives a separate explanation of the stronger minimum-two conclusion.
