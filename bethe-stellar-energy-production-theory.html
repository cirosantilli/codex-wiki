Bethe stellar energy-production theory

OurBigBook.com Words: 382
Why can a star shine for so long, and why do hotter stellar cores favor different nuclear reactions? A useful theory must supply both an adequate energy reservoir and the right stellar energy-generation rate. Nuclear binding energy supplies the reservoir; the thermonuclear reaction rate, stellar composition and stellar structure determine how quickly it is spent. The proton–proton chain and CNO cycle are two routes for hydrogen burning with very different temperature sensitivities.
In his 1967 lecture, Hans Albrecht Bethe contrasted the short Kelvin-Helmholtz cooling time with long ages inferred from terrestrial and stellar evidence.[ref]
For an original solar-scale estimate, choose mass M=1.99×1030kg, radius R=6.96×108m and luminosity L=3.83×1026W. The characteristic Kelvin-Helmholtz cooling time GM2/(RL) is about 31Myr; the precise gravitational reservoir has a structure-dependent coefficient. If a fraction f=0.10 of the mass can burn its initial hydrogen mass fraction X=0.70, with a chosen rest-energy conversion fraction η=0.007, the fuel estimate is
tfuel​≃LηfXMc2​≃7.3Gyr.
(11)
This assumes fixed luminosity and neglects neutrino losses. It demonstrates the reservoir advantage, rather than predicting the Sun's age or its full stellar evolution. Having enough fuel still does not identify a reaction: the fuel must release energy at a rate compatible with hydrostatic equilibrium and radiative transfer.
Hans Albrecht Bethe and Charles Louis Critchfield calculated deuterium formation from two protons in 1938, combining quantum tunnelling with a weak conversion of a proton into a neutron. Their calculated nuclear reaction cross-section was a theoretical input to hydrogen burning.[ref] In 1939, Hans Albrecht Bethe analysed the catalytic carbon–nitrogen route and compared required stellar core temperatures with stellar structure estimates in Table VIII. His reported solar agreement used historical abundance and structure assumptions; it should not be read as a modern demonstration that the CNO cycle dominates the Sun.[ref]
Hans Albrecht Bethe's 1967 lecture credits Carl Friedrich von Weizsäcker's independent 1938 carbon–nitrogen cycle discovery, distinguishing it from calculation of rates and temperature dependence.[ref] The 1967 Nobel Prize in Physics recognized Hans Albrecht Bethe's contributions to nuclear reaction theory, especially stellar energy production.[ref]
Laboratory nuclear reaction cross-sections were inputs, not measurements of an artificial star. The 1939 paper converted thick-target yields to nuclear reaction cross-sections using a range–energy relation and tabulated capture data.[ref] A concrete apparatus example is the Cockcroft-Walton lithium disintegration experiment, with an accelerated proton beam, lithium target, zinc sulfide screen and beam-blocking control. Its nuclear reaction differs from both stellar burning routes. The 1967 lecture stresses extrapolation below laboratory access and tests for nearby nuclear levels.[ref]
