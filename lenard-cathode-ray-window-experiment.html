Lenard cathode-ray window experiment

OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 1
The Lenard cathode-ray window experiment lets cathode rays leave a gas-discharge tube through a thin aluminium foil while preserving the tube's vacuum. This separates production from propagation.
Philipp Lenard's lecture, delivered on 28 May 1906, describes sealing a small hole in a metal plate with aluminium foil. Luminous phosphor grains first rested on the window, then glowed above it in air. His improved tube separated production and observation chambers; coated paper served as a fluorescent screen. An aperture revealed spreading beyond a narrow straight beam. Evacuating the observation chamber reduced spreading and attenuation without interrupting production. Interposed layers and stepped foil stacks gave shadow comparisons; approximate scaling with mass had exceptions.[ref]
The experimental advantage is unusually clean: reducing the gas content in the observation chamber tests whether gas is needed to carry the cathode rays, while retaining the conditions needed to generate them. The window is a boundary that transmits some electrons while confining the source environment. A spot outside establishes a transmitted effect; the aperture and changed observation environment provide additional ways to distinguish propagation from the source's behavior. This logic is useful whenever a complicated source would otherwise be changed by the very intervention intended to test the emitted beam.
Several inferences require care. A darker fluorescent screen can mean fewer arriving electrons, less deposited energy per electron, or redistribution by electron scattering beyond the observed area. A visible endpoint depends on the screen's sensitivity and background. It is not an intrinsic electron range independent of initial kinetic energy, gas density, and observation geometry. For a modern comparison, measure a reference at fixed source conditions, use background subtraction, and compare the whole luminous patch as well as a small central region. A wider collection area tests redistribution; changing source-to-screen separation also changes collection geometry. These are proposed controls for interpreting a readout, not additional historical observations attributed to Lenard.
Nor is fluorescence a unique signature of cathode rays: X-rays can also excite it. Lenard notes that a later tube could transmit both, and that slow cathode rays failed to pass its window.[ref] In modern terms, cathode rays are electrons carrying negative electric charge, whereas X-rays are photons with zero electric charge. Magnetic bending of the incident electrons, interpreted through the Lorentz force, supplies a discrimination that brightness alone cannot. Passage through foil alone determines neither the sign of electric charge nor its magnitude, and it does not measure charge-to-mass ratio. The separate Thomson charge-to-mass experiment explains that inference. Even a measured charge-to-mass ratio needs an independent electric charge measurement to determine mass.
