The two conditions for a basis of a topology both hold. First, every real number x belongs to [x,x+1), so the proposed basic intervals cover the real line. Second, if
x∈[a,b)∩[c,d),
(1)
then max(a,c)≤x<min(b,d), and
[a,b)∩[c,d)=[max(a,c),min(b,d))
(2)
is itself one of the proposed basic intervals. Thus every point in an intersection of two basic sets has a basic neighbourhood contained in that intersection.
Consequently the arbitrary unions of members of B, including the empty union, form a topology T. The basis of a topology conditions give closure under finite intersections; arbitrary unions are built into the definition. This is the lower limit topology, also called the Sorgenfrey line.

Let U be open in the Euclidean topology on the real line, and let x∈U. There is an ε>0 with (x−ε,x+ε)⊆U. The basic open set [x,x+ε) contains x and is contained in U. Taking the union of these basic neighbourhoods over x∈U shows that U∈T. Thus
Tusual​⊆T.​
(5)
In fact this is a strictly finer topology: [0,1) is open in T but not in the Euclidean topology, since no usual open ball about 0 is contained in it.

The usual real line is connected. In contrast,
RT​=(−∞,0)⊔[0,∞)
(6)
is a separation of a topological space. Both pieces are nonempty and disjoint; the first is open by part (c), and the second is the open union ⋃n≥1​[0,n). Thus the Sorgenfrey line is not connected. Since a homeomorphism preserves connectedness, the two topological spaces are not homeomorphic. Merely showing that their topologies differ would not by itself exclude some other homeomorphism between them.

X is not compact. In the subspace topology on X=[0,1], consider the open cover
U={[0,1−1/n):n≥2}∪{{1}}.
(7)
The intervals are basic open sets in the ambient Sorgenfrey line, and {1}=X∩[1,2) is open in X. Every x<1 belongs to one of those intervals, while the singleton covers 1.
A finite subcover would have to include {1}. Among its finitely many other members, let N be the largest index. Their union is [0,1−1/N), leaving 1−1/N uncovered. Selecting no such member also fails to cover 0. Hence no finite subcover exists.

X does not have sequential compactness. Take the sequence
xn​=1−n1​(n≥1).
(8)
Every subsequence has usual limit of a sequence 1. If a subsequence converged in the subspace topology of the Sorgenfrey line, it would also converge in the usual subspace topology, since part (c) makes the identity function from the former to the latter continuous. Solution

ii OurBigBook.com
Let qS​:S2→S2/∼S​ and qD​:D2→D2/∼D​ be the quotient maps. The continuous map
h:D2⟶S2,h(x,y)=(x,y,1−x2−y2​)
(14)
is a homeomorphism onto the upper hemisphere; its inverse function is the restriction of the projection map (x,y,z)↦(x,y). Every antipodal pair on S2 meets that hemisphere. Moreover, two points of this hemisphere are antipodal only if both lie on the equator. Thus
qS​(h(u))=qS​(h(v))⟺u∼D​v.
(15)
By the universal property of the quotient topology, qS​∘h induces a continuous bijection
H:D2/∼D​⟶S2/∼S​,H(qD​(u))=qS​(h(u)).
(16)
To justify continuity of the inverse function, the source is compact, being a continuous image of a compact space D2; the Heine-Borel theorem gives compactness of D2. The target is Hausdorff, as follows. For distinct equivalence classes [u]=[v], put
δ=min(∥u−v∥,∥u+v∥)>0.
(17)
In S2, take the union U of the two relative open balls of radius δ/3 about u,−u, and similarly take V about v,−v. Every distance between their respective centres is at least δ, so the triangle inequality gives U∩V=∅. These open sets contain entire antipodal pairs, hence qS−1​(qS​(U))=U and similarly for V. The quotient topology therefore makes qS​(U) and qS​(V) disjoint open neighbourhoods of the two classes.
The compact-to-Hausdorff continuous bijection theorem now gives the required homeomorphism H.

The sphere S2 is path-connected. For u,v∈S2 which are not an antipodal pair, a continuous path is
γ(t)=∥(1−t)u+tv∥(1−t)u+tv​,0≤t≤1.
(18)
The denominator never vanishes: a zero would make u and v antipodal. If v=−u, choose a unit vector w perpendicular to u and concatenate the paths from u to w and from w to v of the same form. Thus S2 is connected. The quotient map qS​ is continuous and surjective, so the continuous image of a connected space theorem proves that RP2 is connected. In fact composing the paths above with qS​ also proves path connectedness.

ii OurBigBook.com
False, with “disjoint union” understood as a topological disjoint union. Take the rational numbers Q with the usual subspace topology from the real line.
Every connected subset of Q has at most one point. Indeed, if it contained r<s, choose an irrational number t∈(r,s). The two relatively open sets
Q∩(−∞,t),Q∩(t,∞)
(19)
partition Q and separate any subset containing both r and s. Hence the connected components of Q are singletons.
If a topological disjoint union of nonempty connected spaces were homeomorphic to Q, the image of each summand would be connected, hence a singleton. Every summand would therefore be a one-point topological space. Their topological disjoint union is a discrete space, since each summand is open. But Q is not a discrete space: for each r∈Q, the distinct rational numbers r+1/n approach r, so r is not an isolated point. This is a contradiction. The set decomposition into connected components always exists; giving those pieces the topological disjoint union topology need not recover the original topology.

Write Z=∐n∈N​Xn​, where each Xn​ is a labelled copy of the topological space X, with its topological disjoint union topology. Define the bijection
F:Z⟶N×X,F(x in Xn​)=(n,x).​
(20)
For a subset U⊆N×X, let Un​={x∈X:(n,x)∈U}. By definition of the topological disjoint union, F−1(U) is open in Z exactly when each Un​ is open in X.
The same condition characterizes open sets in the product topology on N×X. If U is open, the map x↦(n,x) is continuous, so each Un​ is open. Conversely, if each Un​ is open, then
U=⋃n∈N​({n}×Un​)
(21)
is open, since the discrete topology makes every {n} open in N. Hence F and its inverse function are continuous, proving the required homeomorphism. This also covers the case X=∅.

Use positive indices n≥1 and write p=(0,−1)∈S1. Set
A=N×{p},f(n,(u,v))=(nu​,nv+1​).​
(22)
For fixed n, this function is a homeomorphism from the unit circle to Cn​: it scales its radius to 1/n and translates its centre to (0,1/n). Thus it takes values in the Hawaiian earring X and is surjective onto X. It is continuous on each open slice {n}×S1; since these slices form an open cover of N×S1, f is continuous on the whole product topology.
Distinct circles Cn​,Cm​ meet only at o=(0,0). To see this directly, their equations are
x2+y2=n2y​,x2+y2=m2y​.
(23)
For m=n, subtraction gives y=0, then x=0. Each slice contains exactly one preimage of o, namely (n,p). Therefore the only nonsingleton fibre of f is A.
Let q:N×S1→(N×S1)/A be the quotient map collapsing A. The universal property of the quotient topology supplies a unique continuous map fˉ​ with f=fˉ​∘q. The fibre calculation proves injectivity, and surjectivity follows from that of f. Hence fˉ​ is the required continuous bijection.

Let W=(N×S1)/A, and choose in each circle the point opposite o:
zn​=(0,2/n)∈Cn​,B={zn​:n≥1}⊆X.
(24)
In the subspace topology from the Euclidean plane, the sequence zn​ converges to o, which does not belong to B. Thus B is not closed in X.
On the other hand, let D=fˉ​−1(B)⊆W. With the quotient map q from part (i),
q−1(D)=N×{(0,1)}.
(25)
This is closed in N×S1: its complement is the open set N×(S1∖{(0,1)}). The definition of the quotient topology, applied to complements, says that D is closed in W. A homeomorphism would carry this closed set to the closed set B, a contradiction. Therefore fˉ​ is not a homeomorphism.
The obstruction occurs at the common point. The open set W∖D contains the collapsed point, but its image X∖B contains no neighbourhood of o, since zn​→o. The wedge sum topology allows a separate small neighbourhood on each circle; a neighbourhood of o in the Hawaiian earring must contain all sufficiently small whole circles.

The unit circle is Hausdorff and second countable, since both properties pass to topological subspaces of the Euclidean plane. To exhibit its local coordinate charts, consider the four open semicircles
Uy,+​=S1∩{y>0},Uy,−​=S1∩{y<0},Ux,+​=S1∩{x>0},Ux,−​=S1∩{x<0}.
(26)
They cover S1. On Uy,±​, the projection map (x,y)↦x is a homeomorphism onto the open interval (−1,1), with continuous inverse function
t⟼(t,±1−t2​).
(27)
On Ux,±​, use (x,y)↦y, with inverse function t↦(±1−t2​,t). These coordinate charts show that S1 is a one-dimensional topological manifold with empty manifold boundary.

ii OurBigBook.com
Let o=(0,0) be the common point of the two circles. For sufficiently small ε>0, the open neighbourhood
W=X∩Bε​(o)
(28)
has the following local structure: W∖{o} consists of four disjoint open arcs, an upper and a lower branch from each circle. These are its four connected components, each open and closed relative to W∖{o}.
Suppose a coordinate chart of some dimension of a manifold n existed at o. The case n=0 would make o an isolated point, which it is not. For n≥1, shrink the chart inside W so that its image is an open ball centred at the image of o. Its domain of a function V is an open neighbourhood of o, so it meets all four arms of W. Intersecting those arms with V∖{o} gives four disjoint nonempty clopen sets partitioning V∖{o}; hence there are at least four connected components.
But a punctured ball in R has exactly two connected components, and a punctured ball in Rn for n≥2 is path-connected. A homeomorphism preserves connected components, giving a contradiction in every dimension of a manifold. Thus X is not a topological manifold. The argument uses arbitrarily small coordinate charts; the fact that the whole X∖{o} has two pieces alone would not be a local obstruction.

Label the hexagon polygon vertices v0​,…,v5​ in the direction in which its boundary word is abca−1b−1c−1, with the first side running from v0​ to v1​. Matching the endpoints according to the arrows gives
a:b:c:​v0​∼v4​,v1​∼v3​,v1​∼v5​,v2​∼v4​,v2​∼v0​,v3​∼v5​.​
(29)
Thus the two vertex equivalence classes are {v0​,v2​,v4​} and {v1​,v3​,v5​}.
The polygonal schema gives a compact connected surface with empty manifold boundary. Interior points have disc neighbourhoods; a paired polygon edge glues two half-disc neighbourhoods into a disc. At each of the two vertex equivalence classes, its three corner sectors join cyclically, so the vertex link in a polygonal schema is a circle, again giving a disc neighbourhood. Pairing every label once positively and once negatively makes these gluings consistent with an orientation, so the resulting surface is orientable.
The resulting CW complex has CW cells comprising two vertices, three edges and one face. Its Euler characteristic is
χ=V−E+F=2−3+1=0.
(30)
By the classification of compact surfaces, a connected closed orientable surface with χ=0 has genus g satisfying 2−2g=0, hence g=1. The quotient space is a torus.

ii OurBigBook.com
Every complete polygon edge pairing gives a compact connected surface with empty manifold boundary. Here is the local reason at the vertices. Replace each polygon corner by a small link interval. The polygon edge pairings identify all endpoints of these link intervals in pairs. In a given vertex equivalence class, the resulting graph is connected: the endpoint identifications generating that vertex equivalence class also connect its corner intervals. Every link graph vertex has degree of a vertex two, so the graph is a circle. Its topological cone is a disc, giving the required local surface model. Edge and face CW cell interiors have the usual disc neighbourhoods as well. A finite triangulation compatible with the polygon edge pairings realizes the result as a finite simplicial complex, giving a Hausdorff, second-countable space.
For every such surface, χ=V−2≥−1. If it is orientable, the classification of compact surfaces gives χ=2−2g, so g=0 or 1. If it is a nonorientable surface, write k≥1 for its nonorientable genus; then χ=2−k≥−1 gives k≤3. The exhaustive list is therefore
  • the sphere S2;
  • the torus T2;
  • the real projective plane RP2;
  • the Klein bottle RP2#RP2;
  • the connected sum of surfaces RP2#RP2#RP2, a nonorientable surface of nonorientable genus three.
To prove that every listed possibility actually occurs, the following six-letter boundary words specify complete pairings of a hexagon:
surfaceS2RP2T2Klein bottleRP2#RP2#RP2​boundary wordaa−1bb−1cc−1aabb−1cc−1abca−1b−1c−1aba−1bcc−1aabbcc​V43221​χ=V−22100−1​​
(38)
The vertex counts follow by matching starts and ends of equally directed arrows, just as in part (b). The first and third words pair every letter with its inverse, giving orientable surfaces. Each other word contains a letter twice with the same sign, giving a nonorientable surface: an orientation on the face would induce equal, rather than opposite, boundary directions along that glued pair. With the displayed Euler characteristics, the classification of compact surfaces identifies the examples exactly as listed. All five types occur, and no others occur.
