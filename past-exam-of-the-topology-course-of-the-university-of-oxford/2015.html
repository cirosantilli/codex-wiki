A topological space is connected if it has no separation: two disjoint nonempty open sets whose union is the whole space. Equivalently, its only subsets that are both open and closed are the empty set and the whole space.
A subset is connected when its subspace topology has this property. Thus the sets in a proposed separation of a subset need only be relatively open, rather than open in the ambient space.
A connected⟺A has no separation in its subspace topology.​
(1)

Solution
Words: 78
ii OurBigBook.com
Suppose A∪B=U∪V were a separation. By connectedness, all of A lies on one side, say U: otherwise A∩U,A∩V would separate A. A point of A∩B then lies in B∩U, so connectedness of B forces all of B into U as well. This makes V empty, a contradiction.
Therefore the union of two intersecting connected subsets is connected. The argument also proves the connected union with a connected hub rule for arbitrarily many connected pieces meeting one connected subset.

If f(A)=U∪V were a separation, then
A∩f−1(U),A∩f−1(V)
(2)
would be disjoint nonempty relatively open sets covering A. They are relatively open by continuity of the restriction f∣A​:A→f(A). This contradicts connectedness of A.
A connected, f continuous ⟹ f(A) connected.​

This is the continuous image of a connected space theorem; no separation property of the target is required.

Use the basis of a topology consisting of open rectangles:
B={U×V:U∈TX​, V∈TY​}.​
(4)
It covers X×Y, since X×Y∈B. Whenever a point lies in two basis rectangles, their intersection is another basis rectangle containing it:
(U1​×V1​)∩(U2​×V2​)=(U1​∩U2​)×(V1​∩V2​).
(5)
These are precisely the two basis of a topology axioms. Arbitrary unions of the rectangles therefore form a topology, the product topology. To check the finite-intersection axiom directly, distribute the intersection of two unions over all pairs of rectangles and use the displayed formula. The empty union gives the empty set.

Solution

ii OurBigBook.com
If both factors are Hausdorff, two distinct product points differ in at least one coordinate. Disjoint open neighbourhoods in that coordinate, multiplied by the other whole factor, give disjoint open neighbourhoods of the product points. Thus the product is Hausdorff.
Conversely, assume the product is Hausdorff. Fix y0​∈Y, using nonemptiness. For x=x′, choose disjoint open neighbourhoods of (x,y0​) and (x′,y0​) and refine them to basis rectangles U×V and U′×V′. Since y0​∈V∩V′, their disjointness forces U∩U′=∅. These separate x,x′, proving that X is Hausdorff. Fixing a point of X proves the same for Y.
X×Y Hausdorff⟺X,Y Hausdorff,X,Y=∅.​
(6)
The nonempty hypothesis is essential: a product with the empty set is empty regardless of the other factor. This is the Hausdorff property of a nonempty product.

Choose x0​∈X∖A and y0​∈Y∖B, possible because the two subsets are proper. The cross
H=(X×{y0​})∪({x0​}×Y)
(7)

For any remaining point (x,y), either x∈/A or y∈/B. In the first case the whole connected subset {x}×Y lies in the complement and meets H at (x,y0​). In the second case use X×{y}, which meets H at (x0​,y). These connected fibres together with H cover the complement. The connected union with a connected hub argument therefore gives
(X×Y)∖(A×B) is connected.​
(8)
Neither proper subset needs to be open, closed, or connected.

Every topological subspace S⊆X again has the cofinite topology: its nonempty open subsets have finite complements in S, and every finite exclusion is obtained by intersecting with a cofinite open subset of X. As usual, the empty set is also open; this exception is implicit in the paper's shorthand definition.
If S is infinite, any two nonempty relatively open sets intersect, since their complements are finite. Hence S is hyperconnected space and therefore connected. If S is finite, its cofinite topology is discrete topology. It is then connected exactly when it has at most one point. Thus
S connected⟺∣S∣≤1 or S is infinite.​
(9)
There are also infinite disconnected subsets of the product. Choose distinct a,b∈X and put D={a,b}×X. This is infinite. The traces on D of the ambient open sets
(X∖{b})×X,(X∖{a})×X
(10)
are respectively {a}×X and {b}×X. They are disjoint, nonempty and cover D, giving a separation. This answers the infinite-subset question, rather than only producing two isolated product points.

Solution

Let (Vi​) be an open cover of Y. By continuity, the inverse images f−1(Vi​) are open in X; by surjectivity, they cover X. Compactness supplies finitely many covering X. Applying f and using f(X)=Y shows that the corresponding finitely many Vi​ cover Y.
Thus a continuous surjective function from a compact space has compact target. This is the continuous image of a compact space theorem.

For the canonical projection of a quotient set p, define
Tq​={U⊆X/R:p−1(U)∈T}.​
(12)
The inverse images of the empty set and whole target are ∅ and X. Inverse images commute with arbitrary unions and finite intersections, so the topology axioms follow from those of T. This is the quotient topology, and it makes p continuous by definition.
The crucial criterion is an equivalence: U is open precisely when p−1(U) is open. This is stronger than continuity alone and is what makes the next part work.

If g is continuous, then so is g∘p, as a composition of continuous functions. Conversely, suppose g∘p is continuous. For an open subset W⊆Z,
p−1(g−1(W))=(g∘p)−1(W)

is open in X. The quotient topology criterion therefore says g−1(W) is open in X/R. This proves continuity of g.
g continuous⟺g∘p continuous.​
(14)
This is the universal property of the quotient topology.

Use F:[0,1]→S1, F(t)=e2πit. This is continuous, identifies the two endpoints, and is otherwise injective. Its fibres are exactly the equivalence classes for collapsing a subset to a point with A={0,1}. Hence it factors uniquely through a bijection
F:XA​⟶S1,F([t])=e2πit.
(17)
By the universal property of the quotient topology, F is continuous. The interval is compact, so its quotient XA​ is compact. The unit circle is Hausdorff. The compact-to-Hausdorff continuous bijection theorem now shows that F is a homeomorphism:
[0,1]/{0∼1}≅S1.​
(18)
Indeed the theorem makes the inverse continuous because images of closed subsets of a compact space are compact and hence closed in a Hausdorff target.

Solution

iv OurBigBook.com
Collapsing the integers gives a Hausdorff space. For two distinct noninteger points, choose disjoint small open intervals around them that avoid Z. Each interval is saturated under the saturation of a subset operation, so its image is an open neighbourhood of its class. For a noninteger x and the collapsed class, choose 0<r<dist(x,Z). The disjoint open sets
U=(x−r,x+r),V=R∖[x−r,x+r]
(21)
are saturated; U contains x and V contains all integers. Their images separate the two quotient points. These exhaust all pairs of distinct classes.
Collapsing the rationals does not give a Hausdorff space. The preimage of the singleton collapsed point is Q, which is not closed in R. Therefore that quotient singleton is not closed, whereas every singleton of a Hausdorff space is closed.
XZ​ is Hausdorff,XQ​ is not Hausdorff.​
(22)
Equivalently, every nonempty saturated open subset of R meets the dense set Q and therefore contains it all. Every nonempty quotient open set contains the collapsed point, making separation from any other quotient point impossible.

No countable subset works. For any countable set A⊆R, choose bn​∈(n,n+1/2)∖A: each nonempty interval is uncountable. The sequence tends to infinity, so every tail
Kn​={bk​:k≥n}
(23)
is closed in R and disjoint from A. By part (i), p(R∖Kn​) is open in XA​. These sets increase and cover XA​, since no point belongs to every tail. But any finite selection is contained in its largest member, which still misses every quotient point p(bk​) in that member's remaining tail. The bk​ represent distinct singleton classes.
This is an open cover without a finite subcover, proving
A countable ⟹ RA​ is not compact.​
(24)
The argument does not assume that A is closed, bounded, or discrete. It is the noncompactness after collapsing a countable real subset obstruction.

i OurBigBook.com
The standard simplex of dimension n is
Δn={(t0​,…,tn​)∈Rn+1:tj​≥0, j∑​tj​=1}.​
(25)
It is the convex hull of the coordinate vectors, and its faces of a simplex arise by setting selected coordinates to zero.
An abstract simplicial complex is a pair (V,Σ) where V is the vertex set and Σ consists of finite nonempty subsets of V, contains every singleton, and contains every nonempty subset of each member. A member with n+1 vertices is an n-simplex.
Its geometric realization is obtained by gluing one standard simplex for each member along the faces specified by common vertex subsets. Equivalently, its points have barycentric coordinates (tv​)v∈V​ with finite support, nonnegative entries summing to one, and support a simplex of K. Use the topology induced by this gluing, equivalently the weak topology of a simplicial realization.

Solution

ii OurBigBook.com
List the finite vertex set as V={v1​,…,vN​}. Map a point to its barycentric coordinates:
f:∣K∣⟶RN,f(j∑​tj​vj​)=(t1​,…,tN​).​
(26)
On each simplex this is an affine map and therefore continuous. The definitions agree on every common face, so the quotient topology defining the realization makes f continuous on all of ∣K∣. Barycentric coordinates are unique after zero coordinates are added, so f is injective.
Since Euclidean space is Hausdorff, distinct images have disjoint open neighbourhoods. Their inverse images separate the original points. Hence ∣K∣ is Hausdorff. A continuous injection into a Hausdorff space is enough for this conclusion; no prior Hausdorff assumption on the domain is being used.

An n-dimensional topological manifold is a Hausdorff, second countable topological space in which every point has an open neighbourhood homeomorphic to an open subset of Rn. Equivalently, charts can be chosen onto open balls. This is the without-boundary convention.
A topological surface is a two-dimensional topological manifold. When connectedness is included in the convention for a surface, impose it as well; in the classification statement below the surface is explicitly connected. A closed surface means a compact surface without boundary, rather than merely a closed subset of another space.

Here is an explicit simplicial complex on six vertices. Take every subset of one of the following ten triangular facets:
123, 124, 135, 146, 156, 236, 245, 256, 345, 346.​
(27)
For example, 135 denotes {1,3,5}, with all its edges and vertices included. Its one-skeleton has all fifteen edges of the complete graph. Every edge belongs to exactly two triangles. The links of a simplex at the six vertices are the following cycles, with the first vertex understood to repeat at the end:
vertex123456​link cycle2,3,5,6,41,3,6,5,41,2,6,4,51,2,5,3,61,3,4,2,61,4,3,2,5​​
(28)
Thus every vertex has a disk neighbourhood, as do edge and face interior points. The realization is a compact connected surface without boundary. Its Euler characteristic is
χ=V−E+F=6−15+10=1.
(29)
The classification theorem for surfaces identifies the unique closed connected surface with this Euler characteristic as the real projective plane. Consequently this complex triangulates RP2. The explicit facet and link checks make this a genuine simplicial complex, not a polygon whose boundary identifications have been mistaken for simplicial faces.

i OurBigBook.com
A polygon with a complete set of side-identifications consists of a closed polygonal disk, a pairing of all its boundary sides, and a specified endpoint-matching homeomorphism for each paired pair. Form the quotient by these side maps, leaving interior points distinct. A convenient encoding labels paired sides by the same letter and gives them arrows; traversal in the arrow direction contributes a, and traversal against it contributes a−1 to the boundary word. Every letter occurs exactly twice.
The orientations determine whether matched endpoints are identified in the same or reverse boundary-traversal order. This endpoint data is essential: pairing the same geometric sides with different directions can produce nonhomeomorphic surfaces. Subdividing the disk and the paired sides gives a triangulation of the resulting surface.

ii OurBigBook.com
The classification theorem for surfaces states that every connected closed combinatorial surface is homeomorphic to exactly one of:
  • the two-sphere;
  • an orientable surface of orientable genus g≥1, the connected sum of g tori;
  • a nonorientable surface of crosscap number h≥1, the connected sum of h real projective planes.
The orientable family has Euler characteristic 2−2g; the nonorientable family has Euler characteristic 2−h. Orientability together with Euler characteristic distinguishes the entries.
Boundary words giving the required polygonal schemas are
S2:Σg​:Nh​:​aa−1,a1​b1​a1−1​b1−1​⋯ag​bg​ag−1​bg−1​,a1​a1​a2​a2​⋯ah​ah​.​​
(30)
A two-sided polygon here means a disk with its boundary split into two arcs; subdivision produces an ordinary polygon with more sides without changing the quotient. Alternatively the square word aa−1bb−1 represents the sphere. Each four-letter block in the orientable word adds a torus handle; each repeated-letter block in the nonorientable word adds a projective-plane summand.

There are exactly four homeomorphism types: the two-sphere, real projective plane, torus, and Klein bottle.
For completeness, enumerate all pairings. Four sides have three pairings; each pair can be matched in either direction, giving twelve signed pairings before symmetries. Up to cyclic relabelling, the repeated labels are either adjacent, of form aasbbt, or alternating, of form abasbt, where s,t∈{1,−1}. The second adjacent pattern abba rotates to the first. Endpoint identification gives the following table:
patternadjacentadjacentadjacentalternatingalternatingalternating​(s,t)(−1,−1)(1,−1) or (−1,1)(1,1)(−1,−1)(1,−1) or (−1,1)(1,1)​V321112​χ=V−1210001​surfaceS2RP2Klein bottletorusKlein bottleRP2​​
(31)
Here the quotient always has two edge classes and one face, so its Euler characteristic is V−2+1=V−1. To reproduce the vertex count, label consecutive square vertices 0,1,2,3. For paired sides i,j, a same-direction pairing identifies i with j and i+1 with j+1; a reversed-direction pairing identifies i with j+1 and i+1 with j, with indices modulo four. Taking the generated equivalence classes gives exactly the displayed values of V.
The quotient is orientable precisely when each label is traversed once positively and once negatively around the boundary: then the face orientation extends across every paired edge. A same-direction pair obstructs such an orientation. Thus the two χ=0 possibilities are distinguished as the orientable torus and nonorientable Klein bottle; the other rows are fixed by Euler characteristic and the classification theorem for surfaces.
Explicit square representatives are
S2:aa−1bb−1,RP2:aabb−1,T2:aba−1b−1,K:aba−1b.​
(32)
All edge interior points have disk neighbourhoods by joining two half-disks, and each identified vertex has a cyclic sequence of corner sectors, again a disk neighbourhood. There are no leftover boundary edges. The enumeration therefore describes closed surfaces, and the twelve signed pairings leave no additional cases.
