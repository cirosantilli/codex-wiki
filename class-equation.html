Class equation (∣G∣=∣Z(G)∣+∑i​[G:CG​(xi​)])

 ... Area of mathematics Algebra Group theory Group action Conjugation action Conjugacy class
OurBigBook.com Words: 261 Articles: 1
For a finite group G, choose one representative xi​ from each conjugacy class with more than one element. The class equation is
∣G∣=∣Z(G)∣+∑i​[G:CG​(xi​)]=∣Z(G)∣+∑i​∣CG​(xi​)∣∣G∣​,
(43)
where Z(G) is the center of a group and CG​(xi​) is the centralizer of xi​. Each central element contributes its own one-element conjugacy class. Every remaining conjugacy class contributes its size, given by the orbit-stabilizer theorem. The sum counts each element exactly once. See McKernan, Group actions and automorphisms, lemma 23.6.
For conjugacy classes of the symmetric group on three letters, take x1​=(12) and x2​=(123). Their centralizers have sizes two and three, and the center of a group has size one, giving
6=1+26​+36​=1+3+2.
(44)
For an abelian group, every centralizer is the whole group, every conjugacy class has size one, and the sum over noncentral representatives is empty.
The power of this counting identity is that the sizes of conjugacy classes constrain group structure. For a nontrivial finite p-group with group order pn, every noncentral conjugacy class has size a positive power of p, by Lagrange's theorem. The class equation therefore gives ∣Z(G)∣≡0(modp). Since the identity element lies in the center of a group, the center of a group must have at least p elements. This obtains an element commuting with the entire group without constructing one. See McKernan, lemma 23.7.
Historically, these ideas belong to the nineteenth-century development of permutation groups and normal subgroups. Milne's historical opening records Galois's introduction of normal subgroups in 1832 and Jordan's systematic treatment of permutation groups in 1870. This is context for the conjugation action and the class equation, rather than a claim that either author first stated the modern formula.[ref]
