An enzyme is a biological catalyst: it accelerates a chemical reaction and is regenerated after a successful catalytic cycle. Most enzymes are proteins, but catalytic RNA molecules, called ribozymes, also exist. Many reactions needed by a biological cell would proceed too slowly under its ordinary conditions without enzymes. See Cooper, The Cell: the central role of enzymes as biological catalysts.
The biological beauty is selective speed. A biological cell contains many possible reactants; its enzymes help determine which transformations happen on a useful timescale. In metabolism, one step's reaction product can become another step's substrate, so selective catalysts support an organized flow of material.
The ribosome is a striking biological example: the machinery that builds proteins has RNA at its catalytic centre. The complete ribosome contains both RNA and proteins, but its bond-forming active site is built from RNA. Structural evidence established this distinction in Nissen et al., The structural basis of ribosome activity in peptide bond synthesis, Science 289, 920–930 (2000).
Selection occurs at an active site, the region where substrate binding and catalytic chemistry take place. Shape and chemical interactions favour some substrates over others; binding can also reshape the enzyme, an induced fit. Recognition alone is insufficient: holding a substrate extremely tightly need not make it react quickly. Catalysis requires a favourable route through the transition state, the high-barrier configuration between reactants and reaction products. Positioning reactive groups and stabilizing this configuration relative to the bound substrate can lower that barrier. Specificity is often strong, but need not mean exactly one acceptable substrate. See Cooper, The Cell: mechanisms of enzymatic catalysis.

This separates chemical kinetics from chemical equilibrium. An enzyme changes how rapidly the system approaches chemical equilibrium; it does not change the equilibrium constant for the same overall chemical reaction under the same conditions. Faster catalysis therefore supplies no extra driving energy. In a biological cell, continuing supply of reactants and removal of reaction products can nevertheless sustain a net flow: the surroundings keep that system away from chemical equilibrium. See IUBMB, Symbolism and terminology in enzyme kinetics: basic definitions.
Some enzymes require a cofactor, an additional nonprotein component such as a metal ion or a small organic molecule. Organic helpers are often called coenzymes; they can assist transfers that the protein alone cannot readily perform. The cofactor may remain bound or exchange with the surroundings, depending on the system. A coenzyme can be chemically changed in one reaction and regenerated by another: regeneration of the enzyme does not mean every helper is unchanged in that individual reaction. Some enzymes need no additional cofactor. See Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: small molecules associated with proteins and Alberts et al., activated carriers and their regeneration.
How much faster does adding substrate make a reaction? Enzyme kinetics gives a quantitative answer when a model's assumptions fit. For an illustrative reaction obeying Michaelis-Menten kinetics, use the Michaelis-Menten equation with substrate concentration s, limiting reaction rate Vmax​ and Michaelis constant KM​. At s/KM​=0.1, substitution gives v/Vmax​=0.1/1.1=1/11≈0.0909. At s/KM​=1, it gives 1/2; at s/KM​=10, it gives 10/11≈0.9091. Thus a hundredfold increase in substrate between the first and last examples yields only a tenfold increase in reaction rate.
For a chosen Vmax​=110μMmin−1, those fractions mean reaction rates of 10, 55 and 100μMmin−1. Doubling substrate from 10KM​ to 20KM​ raises the normalized rate only from 10/11 to 20/21≈0.9524, a relative increase of about 4.76%. In this simplest model, the catalytic machinery is already nearly fully occupied. Doubling the amount of active enzyme instead doubles Vmax​ and the predicted reaction rates at fixed free substrate concentration, provided the other conditions are unchanged. This distinction helps decide whether additional substrate or additional catalytic capacity could improve throughput.
The interpretation has limits. The simplest Michaelis-Menten kinetics assumes a single substrate, fixed active enzyme amount, a suitable quasi-steady-state approximation, and initial measurements with negligible reverse reaction from accumulated reaction product. Substantial depletion, interacting binding sites, or inhibition by excess substrate can invalidate this model. Within it, KM​ is the substrate concentration giving half the limiting reaction rate; it is not generally the binding dissociation constant Kd​. See IUBMB, enzyme kinetics: steady-state approximation and substrate dissociation and IUBMB, Michaelis-Menten and non-Michaelis-Menten kinetics. Measuring activity also requires specified temperature, pH and necessary cofactor availability: a weak signal can reflect unsuitable conditions rather than little enzyme. See Brooks et al., Assay Guidance Manual: basics of enzymatic assays for HTS.

Ribozyme

Words: 1k
Enzyme OurBigBook.com
A ribozyme is an RNA molecule whose folded structure provides catalysis of a chemical reaction. A protein enzyme builds its active site from an amino acid chain; a ribozyme builds a working active site from RNA, sometimes with metal ions and associated proteins. Base pairing can help recognize a substrate, but recognition alone does not establish catalysis: the RNA must promote chemical conversion. A passive messenger RNA template being read by a ribosome is not thereby a ribozyme. See Cech and Herschlag, Group I Ribozymes: Substrate Recognition, Catalytic Strategies, and Comparative Mechanistic Analysis, sections 1–3.
The surprise is that an information-bearing polymer can also perform chemistry. Examples include the self-splicing group I intron of Tetrahymena thermophila and the catalytic RNA component of bacterial RNase P, discussed below. The ribosome supplies an everyday example: its peptide bond-forming active site is made of ribosomal RNA, although the complete machine also contains proteins. Nissen and colleagues examined structures of a large ribosome subunit with substrate analogues and found RNA contacting those analogues at the catalytic centre. This supports an RNA active site, not a claim that isolated ribosomal RNA can carry out all of biological translation. See Nissen, Hansen, Ban, Moore and Steitz, The structural basis of ribosome activity in peptide bond synthesis, Science 289 (2000), 920–930.
For the Tetrahymena group I intron, RNA splicing removes the intron and joins its flanking exons. The first chemical step uses the free 3′ hydroxyl group of an external guanosine to attack the phosphodiester bond at the 5′ splice junction. This releases the upstream exon with a free 3′ hydroxyl group and attaches the guanosine to the intron. That newly freed hydroxyl group then attacks the phosphodiester bond at the other splice junction, joining the exons and releasing the linear intron. Each step exchanges one backbone connection for another; it is not simply separation of base pairs. See Cech, Zaug and Grabowski, 1981: the phosphoester-transfer mechanism.
The motivating experiment began with an unexpected control. As Cech recounts, Zaug purified a ribosomal RNA precursor from Tetrahymena using a detergent and phenol extraction, then compared test tubes with and without added cell nucleus extract. Incubation in salts and nucleotides released the same amount of small intron RNA in both cases, detected by gel electrophoresis. Repetition made a missing ingredient or accidental omission less plausible. Activity also survived treatments intended to destroy proteins. Nevertheless, absence of added extract did not by itself exclude a tightly attached, resistant protein, or prove that the small RNA had been newly excised rather than released from an already processed complex. See Thomas R. Cech, 1989 Nobel lecture, pp. 655–659: the unexpected control and alternative explanations.
An independent chemical readout addressed the latter problem. Cech mixed precursor RNA with phosphorus-32-labelled guanosine bearing three phosphate groups (GTP), and used gel electrophoresis followed by exposure to X-ray film to locate the label. Label appeared in the excised intron with magnesium present; it was absent when magnesium was omitted or labelled ATP replaced the labelled guanosine reagent. This experiment is described in Cech, 1989 Nobel lecture, pp. 657–658: labelled-guanosine controls. The 1981 study established attachment through the guanosine 3′ hydroxyl group and showed that guanosine without a triphosphate also supported excision: breaking down the added nucleotide to supply energy was unnecessary. See Cech, Zaug and Grabowski, In vitro splicing of the ribosomal RNA precursor of Tetrahymena, Cell 27 (1981), 487–496: primary abstract. Separately, sequencing and terminal analysis found an extra 5′ guanosine absent from the corresponding DNA sequence. See Zaug and Cech, The intervening sequence excised from the ribosomal RNA precursor of Tetrahymena contains a 5-terminal guanosine residue not encoded by the DNA, Nucleic Acids Research 10 (1982), 2823–2838. Together, incorporation and sequence evidence support a new covalent bond, beyond a change in how RNA associates.
Kruger, Grabowski, Zaug, Sands, Gottschling and Cech's 1982 test removed dependence on a precursor purified from Tetrahymena. They cloned the intron and adjacent exons into a DNA template, made precursor RNA with purified bacterial RNA polymerase under conditions suppressing RNA splicing, and removed proteins by detergent–phenol extraction. Denaturing gel electrophoresis separated precursor species. Purified precursor was then incubated with the guanosine reagent, magnesium and salt; matched samples without this incubation supplied a precursor baseline. Their standard assay used 10mM magnesium chloride, 0.1mM of that guanosine reagent, 100mM ammonium sulfate and a buffer solution at pH 7.5, for 30 minutes at temperature 37∘C, with specified variations in individual experiments. Gel electrophoresis and radioactive-film detection showed excised intron RNA; sequencing verified its 5′ junction. See Kruger et al., Self-Splicing RNA, Cell 31 (1982), 147–157, figures 3–4 and Experimental Procedures.
Excision alone could leave disconnected exons. To test joining, the authors paired labelled RNA with DNA spanning the joined-exon sequence, removed exposed RNA with enzymes, and separated the protected pieces by denaturing gel electrophoresis. Spliced samples yielded protected RNA long enough to span the junction. See Kruger et al., 1982, figure 6: independent evidence for exon ligation. The experimental logic matters: an intron-sized band identifies a candidate product, while sequence and junction assays test its chemical identity.
These controls support sufficiency of the prepared RNA under the assay conditions. They do not show that every RNA self-splices, or that proteins are dispensable inside a biological cell. Many group I introns use proteins to help attain an active fold. See Cech and Herschlag, Group I Ribozymes, section 6: protein facilitation. Nor does a failed assay necessarily identify an inactive nucleotide sequence: unsuitable ions, pH, or temperature can prevent a working active site from forming.
The distinction between self-processing and repeated use became especially clear with bacterial RNase P. In 1983 Guerrier-Takada, Gardiner, Marsh, Pace and Altman separated the RNA and protein components of RNase P from two bacteria. Purified RNA cleaved precursor transfer RNA under conditions containing 60mM magnesium ions, or 10mM magnesium ions plus 1mM spermidine. The protein components alone had no catalytic activity. At 5–10mM magnesium without spermidine, both components were needed. Thus the comparison demonstrated an RNA catalyst while also explaining why protein assistance could matter under other conditions. RNase P produces the mature 5′ end of transfer RNA by removing its leader; it acts on a separate substrate and can be used again. See Guerrier-Takada et al., The RNA moiety of ribonuclease P is the catalytic subunit of the enzyme, Cell 35 (1983), 849–857: primary abstract.
Self-splicing demonstrates RNA chemistry, but one precursor completing one rearrangement is not yet evidence for catalytic turnover: repeated conversion of separate substrates by a regenerated catalyst. The self-splicing precursor changes its own connectivity and incorporates external guanosine. In 1986 Zaug and Cech showed that a shortened Tetrahymena intron RNA could cleave and rejoin separate short RNA substrates in a sequence-dependent reaction. This demonstrated an RNA enzyme acting beyond its own self-processing. See Zaug and Cech, The intervening sequence RNA of Tetrahymena is an enzyme, Science 231 (1986), 470–475: primary abstract.

Active site

Words: 1k Articles: 3
Enzyme OurBigBook.com
An active site is the region of an enzyme where a substrate binds and undergoes a chemical reaction. In a protein enzyme, it includes the amino acid residues that contact the substrate and those that directly assist catalysis. These roles can overlap: an amino acid side chain can both orient a substrate and participate in chemistry. The active site occupies only part of the folded protein; the remaining amino acid residues help establish its protein structure, stability and movement. An active site need not be a consecutive stretch of protein primary structure: protein folding can bring distant amino acid residues together. A ribozyme instead supplies an RNA active site. See Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell, Protein Function: binding sites and catalytic sites.
Binding and catalysis answer different questions. A protein binding site recognizes shape and chemistry through such interactions as hydrogen bonds, electrostatics and the hydrophobic effect. At an active site, appropriate positioning also helps a substrate reach the transition state of a chemical reaction. Some amino acid side chains transfer a proton; others participate transiently in a covalent bond. The enzyme-substrate complex is the associated enzyme and substrate, before conversion to a reaction product. Merely accumulating this complex does not establish rapid catalysis: stabilizing the bound substrate without sufficiently stabilizing the transition state can leave a large barrier. The important complementarity is chemical as well as geometric. See Geoffrey M. Cooper, The Cell, The Central Role of Enzymes as Biological Catalysts: substrate binding and transition-state stabilization.
The cartoon makes a useful experimental distinction. Changing a contacting amino acid residue might weaken substrate binding, change the reaction rate, or do both. Changing a distant amino acid residue can also disrupt the active site by changing protein folding. Therefore a loss of activity after a mutation alone does not locate a catalytic group: compare binding, protein structure and enzyme kinetics as well. This is an interpretation guide, not a report of a particular mutation experiment.
A concrete example is hexokinase: it transfers the terminal phosphate group of ATP to the 6-position hydroxyl group of a sugar such as glucose. Specificity need not mean only one acceptable substrate: the hexokinase reaction class includes several possible acceptor sugars. Which are used, and at what reaction rates, depends on the particular enzyme and conditions. See Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics, ENZYME entry EC 2.7.1.1: hexokinase reaction and acceptor range.
Bennett and Steitz compared yeast hexokinase A crystallized with glucose against hexokinase B crystallized without glucose. X-rays scattered by the crystals supplied diffraction measurements from which the authors reconstructed protein structure; comparing coordinates located movement, rather than measuring a reaction rate. The glucose-containing cleft was narrower: one lobe was rotated by about 12∘ relative to the other, with some protein backbone displacements reaching 8A˚. This makes the active site a moving arrangement of interacting groups, rather than a permanent hole. See William S. Bennett Jr. and Thomas A. Steitz, Glucose-induced conformational change in yeast hexokinase, PNAS 75 (1978), 4848–4852.
The structural comparison has two explicit limitations: different forms of hexokinase and different crystal environments were compared, and static coordinates do not record the order of binding and movement. Closure is consistent with induced fit, but those structures alone cannot exclude conformational selection. The solution experiment under induced fit addresses whether compaction also occurs outside a crystal.

Induced fit

Words: 668

In induced fit, a substrate binds to an enzyme and the resulting enzyme-substrate complex subsequently changes its protein structure. This adjustment can position interacting groups at the active site for catalysis. The mechanistic claim concerns order: binding precedes the relevant change. In conformational selection, the unbound enzyme already visits an alternative protein structure, which the substrate preferentially binds. Both processes can occur in one system. Let E and E∗ denote two structures of the same enzyme, and S a substrate. The limiting routes are
induced fit:conformational selection:​E+S⇌ES⇌E∗S,E⇌E∗,E∗+S⇌E∗S.​
(1)
The star labels a structure, not a different protein primary structure, and does not by itself mean catalytic activity. Each reversible arrow abbreviates a step, not the whole catalytic cycle. See Gordon G. Hammes, Yu-Chu Chang and Terrence G. Oas, Conformational selection or induced fit: a flux description of reaction mechanism, PNAS 106 (2009), 13737–13741.
A rigid lock-and-key cartoon usefully suggests selective substrate recognition, but it suppresses movement of the protein and chemical changes at the active site. Replacing it with two static drawings, “open” and “closed”, still does not establish induced fit: both routes above can end at E∗S. Also, if complete closure prevents a substrate entering, the pre-existing structure selected in conformational selection might be partly open and undergo further induced fit after binding. The usefulness of the distinction is that it turns an attractive shape story into questions about enzyme kinetics. Relative traffic through the routes depends on populations, rate constants and free substrate concentration, rather than affinity alone. See Hammes, Chang and Oas, 2009: coupled pathways, their concentrations and the limitations of endpoint structures.
For this analytical illustration, hold the free substrate concentration s constant and assume independent waiting times with exponential distributions of constant rates. The law of mass action gives the binding rate a=kon​s; the competing change has rate b. The probability density function for binding to happen first at time t is ae−(a+b)t, because neither event has happened earlier. Its integral is
P(binding first)=∫0∞​ae−(a+b)tdt=a+ba​.
(2)
With the stated invented rate constants, s=0.1,1,10μM gives 1/11,1/2,10/11, respectively. Raising free substrate concentration makes binding more likely to win this initial competition. This probability is deliberately narrower than a full-route fraction: returns to E, later changes and the binding properties of E∗ would all matter for the latter. The illustration explains why timing and concentration must accompany structural evidence, without assigning a binding mechanism to a real enzyme.
A motivating experiment tested the crystal interpretation in solution. McDonald, Steitz and Engelman prepared solutions of monomeric yeast hexokinase B and compared the unbound preparation with sugar-bound preparations. Their apparatus measured small-angle scattering of X-rays; the spatial extent inferred from that readout was the radius of gyration, rather than a catalytic reaction rate. Binding glucose reduced the radius of gyration by 0.95±0.24A˚; binding the phosphorylated glucose product reduced it by 1.25±0.28A˚, as reported by the authors. The glucose change agreed with the reduction calculated from the crystal coordinates. See Robert C. McDonald, Thomas A. Steitz and Donald M. Engelman, Yeast hexokinase in solution exhibits a large conformational change upon binding glucose or glucose 6-phosphate, Biochemistry 18 (1979), 338–342: primary abstract.
The comparison without added substrate supplies a baseline within the same form of hexokinase; the second bound preparation tests another binding condition. Together with the crystal comparison, solution compaction supports closure beyond crystal packing. It still gives an average spatial readout, not a movie of individual binding events, and product-associated compaction does not demonstrate ongoing catalysis. Neither experiment alone proves that binding precedes movement. To distinguish induced fit from conformational selection, structural comparisons must be combined with measurements sensitive to timing and with a model of the relevant populations and enzyme kinetics.

ATP synthase is a molecular motor that couples downhill proton movement across a cell membrane to formation of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate. In oxidative phosphorylation, this is the machinery that makes chemiosmosis productive: an electrochemical gradient becomes reusable chemical driving capacity.[ref]
Its usefulness is a conversion between currencies. A membrane-spanning proton-motive force can power a local machine, while newly formed ATP can participate in energy coupling elsewhere. The enzyme supplies a route between these forms of free energy; it cannot make an energetically uphill total cycle favourable merely by accelerating its chemistry.

ATP synthase rotary mechanism

Words: 357
ATP synthase OurBigBook.com
The membrane motor F0​ contains a rotating ring of c subunits beside stationary subunit a. Two offset water-accessible half-channels connect opposite membrane sides to the ring. A proton enters one half-channel and protonates a glutamic acid residue on a c subunit; neutralization permits that site to enter the hydrophobic lipid bilayer. Rotation carries it around to the other half-channel, where it releases the proton. A conserved arginine in subunit a helps separate the routes and promotes release. Downhill proton transfer biases rotation; this is not a freely open transmembrane pore.[ref]
The central shaft transmits torque to F1​; a peripheral stalk prevents the catalytic head from simply co-rotating. Its three β catalytic sites, within an α3​β3​ head, cycle through different binding shapes. In the simplified binding-change picture, a loose site captures ADP and inorganic phosphate, a tight site favours bound ATP, and opening releases ATP. Rotation changes binding and release as well as chemistry.[ref]
One complete, tightly coupled turn cycles each catalytic site once, producing three ATP molecules. If the ring has n proton-binding subunits, the ideal motor budget is n transferred protons per turn, or n/3 per ATP. Ring size varies between organisms; the worked example chooses n=10, as in the studied bacterial systems, rather than assigning ten to every ATP synthase.[ref]
For this example the ring's repeat angle is 360∘/10=36∘, whereas the three-site catalytic cycle repeats after 120∘. Their ratio is 10/3, not an integer. Counting complete turns avoids inventing a fixed integer proton count for every catalytic event. The schematic does not require both parts to advance simultaneously as rigid gears; elasticity allows temporary twisting while their cycles proceed.[ref]

Define side P as the higher proton electrochemical potential reservoir and side N as the synthesis-side reservoir. For transfer P→N at common temperature T, define the positive downhill proton-motive force magnitude Δpdrive​ by
Δμ​P→N​=RTlnaP​aN​​+F(ψN​−ψP​)=−FΔpdrive​.
(3)
Here a is thermodynamic activity, ψ is electric potential, R is the molar gas constant and F is the Faraday constant. Thus both a higher proton thermodynamic activity and a more positive electric potential on side P contribute to the driving magnitude. This definition fixes the sign; conventions for a signed proton-motive force differ. The calculation under chemiosmosis separates these two contributions numerically.
Assume fixed reservoirs, tight mechanical energy coupling, one transported proton per c subunit per revolution, and three ATP produced per revolution. Neglect leaks, slipping and membrane transport of the chemical reagents. Let gATP​>0 be the local molar Gibbs free energy cost of ATP formation at the stipulated composition, pH and magnesium-binding convention. It is not necessarily the standard state value. Adding changes in the state function gives, per mole of complete turns,
ΔGturn​=3gATP​−nFΔpdrive​.
(4)
For this model net synthesis is thermodynamically favoured when ΔGturn​<0. Equality is a reversible balance, not a promise of a useful reaction rate. A negative value establishes an available driving margin; it does not specify how fast the enzyme runs.

nFΔpdrive​=173.673kJmol−1,3gATP​=150.0kJmol−1,
(5)
and ΔGturn​=−23.673kJmol−1. Per ATP, the ideal input is 57.891kJmol−1, leaving a driving margin of 7.891kJmol−1. The balance point is
Δp∗​=nF3gATP​​≈0.15546V.
(6)

The machine is reversible: sufficient ATP hydrolysis can drive opposite rotation and pump protons uphill. Actual direction also depends on inhibition and available substrates; a weak proton-motive force need not make every intact enzyme immediately run backward. Respiratory uncoupling supplies a different failure mode: a parallel proton leak dissipates the gradient without productive shaft rotation.[ref]

Trypsin

Words: 951 Articles: 3
Enzyme OurBigBook.com
Trypsin is a serine protease: an enzyme that performs proteolysis by catalysing peptide bond hydrolysis. It preferentially cuts on the C-terminus side of lysine and arginine amino acid residues, leaving the selected amino acid residue in the preceding fragment.[ref]
The bond being broken is the backbone amide linkage, rather than the lysine or arginine amino acid side chain. A formal connectivity equation for hydrolysis is
R1−C(=O)−NH−R2+H2​O⟶R1−COOH+H2​N−R2.
(7)
Here R1 and R2 stand for the connected portions of the original peptide. Actual terminal protonation and electric charge depend on pH; the equation displays connectivity. Water supplies the elements needed to form the two new ends, while the enzyme is regenerated.
Selective cutting turns an unwieldy protein into shorter peptides, making peptide mapping useful for comparing proteins. In cell culture, trypsin also helps detach adherent biological cells: the HeLa culture viability experiment provides a concrete example of its use and subsequent stopping of proteolysis. The same enzyme can therefore serve both chemical analysis and preparation of a biological cell suspension.

Trypsin cleavage specificity

Words: 413 Articles: 1
Trypsin OurBigBook.com
Write a peptide's protein primary structure from its N-terminus toward its C-terminus. The usual simplified trypsin cleavage specificity rule places a cut immediately after K (lysine) or R (arginine), unless the next amino acid residue is P (proline). The following amino acid residue is essential: a terminal K or R has no following peptide bond to cut. The inspected institutional documentation describes proline as usually blocking cleavage, and explicitly lists sequence contexts in which that blocking is negligible. It also warns that experimental results can differ from sequence-based predictions. Thus “never before proline” is too strong.[ref]
For an original worked example, stipulate complete cleavage at all sites allowed by that simplified rule in the twelve-amino acid residue sequence
AGKARPKAGRAK⟶AGK∣ARPK∣AGR∣AK.
(8)
A denotes alanine, G denotes glycine, and K, R and P have the meanings above. Cuts follow positions 3, 7 and 10. Position 5 is R, but its following P suppresses that cut in the stipulated rule; position 12 is terminal K. Notice that the R in ARPK is retained inside the fragment, despite being a usually favoured trypsin target.
Near neutral pH, the lysine and arginine amino acid side chains undergo predominant protonation and carry positive electric charge. This helps distinguish those amino acid residues from the uncharged letter symbols used to write the sequence; those symbols specify identities, not whole-fragment electric charges.[ref]
Fragment accounting checks the prediction: lengths 3+4+3+2=12 conserve the twelve amino acid residues. The original linear chain has eleven peptide bonds; the four fragments retain 2+3+2+1=8, so three were hydrolysed. Three water molecules are consumed per original chain in this formal stoichiometry. More generally, cutting m distinct bonds of one linear peptide makes m+1 fragments: each cut splits one connected piece into two. This argument assumes an unbranched chain without additional links joining the pieces.
A missed trypsin cleavage at position 7 would instead give AGK∣ARPKAGR∣AK, merging two neighbouring fragments without changing their order. Even a correct cleavage preference does not guarantee complete proteolysis; predicted fragment boundaries describe a model, while actual boundaries require evidence from the digest.

Trypsin colorimetric assay

Words: 388
Trypsin OurBigBook.com
A trypsin colorimetric assay follows enzyme activity through absorbance at 405nm. Trypsin cleaves an amide bond in the synthetic arginine derivative BAPNA, releasing p-Nitroaniline.[ref]
The inspected Sigma procedure prepares fresh trypsin in cold 1mM hydrochloric acid and fully dissolves 1mg/ml BAPNA. Its buffer solution stock contains 200mM triethanolamine and 20mM calcium chloride, at pH 7.8. Three 3.00ml cuvettes compare trypsin, trypsin plus the enzyme inhibitor aprotinin, and a blank replacing trypsin with its acid solvent. Sodium chloride solution replaces aprotinin in the uninhibited control. A thermostatted instrument for spectrophotometry holds 25∘C and a 1cm light path. After equilibration, adding BAPNA starts approximately five minutes of absorbance recording. The uninhibited sample checks activity; the blank checks background; the inhibited sample tests suppression.[ref]
Let sU​, sI​ and sB​ denote the measured absorbance slopes per minute over the same linear time interval. Blank subtraction gives sU​−sB​ and sI​−sB​. If p-Nitroaniline accounts for the changing absorbance, the Beer-Lambert law gives
A405​=εlc,dtdc​=εlsU​−sB​​.
(9)
Here c is product molar concentration and l is the geometric light path through the chemical solution. Sigma uses ε=9.96mM−1cm−1.[ref]
Choose original illustrative slopes sU​=0.1036, sI​=0.0538 and sB​=0.0040min−1. The corrected uninhibited slope is 0.0996min−1, giving dc/dt=0.0100mMmin−1=10.0μMmin−1 at l=1cm. In 3.00ml, the product amount of substance increases at 0.0300μmolmin−1. The corrected inhibited slope is 0.0498min−1, half as large. The relative suppression is therefore
100(1−sU​−sB​sI​−sB​​)=100sU​−sB​sU​−sI​​=50%.
(10)
The background cancels in the numerator, but remains essential in the denominator. Using uncorrected slopes would instead give about 48.07%. Differentiating A405​=εlc with fixed ε and l explains the rate conversion; dimensional analysis checks it independently, since min−1/(mM−1cm−1cm)=mMmin−1.
The inference is a reaction rate on BAPNA under the stated conditions, rather than a direct count of trypsin molecules or proof of every cut in a protein. Cloudiness, other absorbing solutes, or different product protonation can compromise the Beer-Lambert law interpretation. A matched blank and a linear interval are therefore part of the measurement's meaning. A coloured enzyme inhibitor may require its own matched blank; a changed substrate or pH requires a fresh calibration. The constructed constant slopes above describe only that interval, not indefinite product accumulation.

Enzyme inhibitor

Words: 1k Articles: 8
Enzyme OurBigBook.com
An enzyme inhibitor lowers an enzyme's reaction rate. In reversible enzyme inhibition, reducing free inhibitor concentration can restore activity; slow release can make this difficult to distinguish experimentally from irreversible inactivation. Competitive inhibition and mixed inhibition describe different changes in enzyme kinetics; pure noncompetitive inhibition is a special case of mixed inhibition, not a name for every inhibitor that binds away from an active site.[ref]
The useful question is how adding substrate changes the inhibition. The model below connects binding to a measurable reaction rate, predicts when more substrate can overcome inhibition, and supplies examples for comparison with an enzyme inhibition initial-rate assay. Its inhibition constants are explicitly defined; neither a binding cartoon nor one reduced reaction rate by itself determines a mechanism.

Reversible enzyme inhibition

Words: 729 Articles: 5
Enzyme inhibitor OurBigBook.com
Construct a minimal model with one substrate and no appreciable accumulated reaction product. Only the enzyme-substrate complex ES produces product, with catalytic rate constant kcat​; EI and ESI are inactive. Write s=[S] and I=[I] for free molar concentrations, and ET​ for total enzyme molar concentration. Assume a suitable quasi-steady-state approximation, rapid inhibitor binding and release relative to turnover, and negligible depletion of free inhibitor. In this construction the population relations are
[ES]=[E]KM​s​,[EI]=[E]Ki​I​,[ESI]=[ES]Ki′​I​,α=1+Ki​I​,α′=1+Ki′​I​.
(11)
Here KM​ is the uninhibited Michaelis constant, and Ki​,Ki′​ are the inhibition constants for the two indicated binding branches. The symbol I denotes a free concentration, not an amount added to the tube. Define Vmax​=kcat​ET​. Conservation of enzyme gives an original short derivation:
ET​v​=[E]+[EI]+[ES]+[ESI]=[E](α+α′KM​s​),=kcat​[ES]=αKM​+α′sVmax​s​=(α/α′)KM​+s(Vmax​/α′)s​.​
(12)
Thus fitting a Michaelis-Menten equation at a fixed I would return the apparent parameters
Vmax,app​=α′Vmax​​,KM,app​=α′α​KM​.
(13)
“Apparent” means conditional on that inhibitor concentration. The uninhibited Michaelis constant has not been redefined. Dimensional analysis checks the denominator: both αKM​ and α′s have units of molar concentration; the resulting v has units of reaction rate.
The shared numerical choices make the mechanisms comparable. At I=0 and s=20μM, all three models give v=50μMmin−1. At I=10μM, the respective reaction rates are 100/3, 25, and 30μMmin−1; at I=30μM they are 20, 12.5, and 50/3μMmin−1. These are independently chosen teaching models, not measured inhibition constants for a named enzyme.

Inhibition constant (Ki​, Ki′​)

Words: 93
Reversible enzyme inhibition OurBigBook.com
An inhibition constant sets an inhibitor concentration scale in an enzyme kinetics model. In the rapid-binding construction under reversible enzyme inhibition, Ki​=[E]I/[EI] and Ki′​=[ES]I/[ESI] are dissociation constants. Smaller values favour the corresponding bound population at fixed free inhibitor concentration. More generally, fitted inhibition constants need not be true binding dissociation constants; a mechanism must justify that interpretation.[ref]
For example, choosing I=Ki​ makes [EI]=[E] in this model, but does not imply that half of all enzyme is EI: ES and ESI also contribute to the total population.

Competitive inhibition

In the model of reversible enzyme inhibition, competitive inhibition makes substrate binding and inhibitor binding mutually exclusive: EI is allowed but ESI is absent. Set α′=1, equivalently take Ki′​→∞. Then
v=αKM​+sVmax​s​,Vmax,app​=Vmax​,KM,app​=αKM​.
(14)
For the chosen I=30μM, α=4: the half-limiting substrate concentration rises from 20 to 80μM, while the limiting reaction rate remains 100μMmin−1. At s=200μM the inhibited reaction rate is 500/7≈71.43μMmin−1, compared with the uninhibited 1000/11≈90.91μMmin−1. “Overcome by substrate” means that their ratio tends to one as s→∞, not that any finite addition removes the difference.

Mixed inhibition

Words: 194 Articles: 1
Reversible enzyme inhibition OurBigBook.com
In the branching model, mixed inhibition retains inhibitor binding to both free enzyme and the enzyme-substrate complex. Unequal Ki​ and Ki′​ shift both apparent parameters; their equality gives pure noncompetitive inhibition. For the chosen unequal case, I=30μM gives α=4 and α′=2, hence Vmax,app​=50μMmin−1 and KM,app​=40μM. Because Ki​<Ki′​, binding favours free enzyme, and the apparent Michaelis constant increases. Reversing that inequality decreases it.
The limiting behaviour provides a useful diagnostic within this model:
lims→0​sv​=αKM​Vmax​​,lims→∞​v=α′Vmax​​.
(15)
Low substrate concentration isolates α through the initial slope; high substrate concentration isolates α′ through the plateau. At I=30μM, the chosen competitive inhibition and unequal mixed inhibition examples have the same low-s slope, 1.25min−1, but different plateaus, 100 and 50μMmin−1. Measuring only low substrate concentrations would miss that distinction.

The protocol used in the trypsin colorimetric assay requires 40–60% inhibition and rejects hazy or yellow BAPNA solution.[ref]
