Tail-sum formula (E[Y]=∫0∞​P(Y>t)dt)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Expected value
OurBigBook.com Words: 805 Articles: 2
The tail-sum formula expresses an expected value as the area under a survival function. For every nonnegative random variable Y,
E[Y]=∫0∞​P(Y>t)dt.​
(5)
This Lebesgue integral and the expected value may both be +∞; a probability density function is unnecessary. For a random variable N taking values in the nonnegative integers, the corresponding sum is
E[N]=k=1∑∞​P(N≥k)=k=0∑∞​P(N>k).​
(6)
The events {N>k} and {N≥k+1} coincide because N takes integer values. Thus the strict sum starts at zero, while the inclusive sum starts at one.[ref]
For an original example, let the probability mass function of N be
P(N=0)=51​,P(N=1)=103​,P(N=3)=21​.
(7)
Its expected value computed directly is 0(1/5)+1(3/10)+3(1/2)=9/5=1.8. The strict tail probabilities at thresholds 0,1,2,3 are 4/5,1/2,1/2,0: their sum is again 1.8. There is no probability mass at two, yet the event {N>2} still has probability 1/2. The survival function has heights 4/5,1/2,1/2 on the successive intervals [0,1),[1,2),[2,3); each interval has length one, so its integral is the same sum.
The indexing matters numerically. Starting the inclusive sum at zero adds P(N≥0)=1, giving 2.8 for this example. Starting the strict sum at one omits P(N>0)=4/5, giving 1. For a general nonnegative random variable, replacing > with ≥ inside the integral is harmless: the difference is P(Y=t), nonzero at only a countable set of thresholds. For each positive integer m, at most m thresholds can have probability mass at least 1/m; their countable union contains all thresholds with positive probability mass and has Lebesgue measure zero. This reasoning concerns an integral, not equality of the two survival function conventions at every point.
For a continuous original example, take the uniform distribution on the interval [0,4]. Its survival function is S(t)=1−t/4 for 0≤t≤4 and zero for t>4. The area is a triangle of base four and height one:
E[Y]=∫04​(1−4t​)dt=2.
(8)
Directly integrating against the probability density function gives the same expected value, ∫04​y(1/4)dy=2. The integral of the probability density function alone is one; it is the survival function whose area is the expected value.
The proof counts layers of each outcome. For a realized value y≥0, the indicator function 1{y>t}​ equals one over an interval of length y, so
Y=∫0∞​1{Y>t}​dt,E[Y]=∫0∞​E[1{Y>t}​]dt=∫0∞​P(Y>t)dt.
(9)
Tonelli theorem permits the interchange because the indicator function is nonnegative; no finite expected value is assumed. Similarly, an outcome N=n contributes one indicator random variable for each of the n events {N≥k} that occur:
N=∑k=1∞​1{N≥k}​,E[N]=∑k=1∞​E[1{N≥k}​]=∑k=1∞​P(N≥k).
(10)
Again Tonelli theorem applies to the nonnegative sum, or equivalently the monotone convergence theorem applies to its partial sums. This exchanges counting layers within each outcome with averaging across outcomes. The existing integer-valued hypothesis in the tail-sum formula explains what changes when the values are not integers; the tail integral formula for moments weights the layers to obtain higher moments.
For an integrable random variable X that can have either sign, apply the nonnegative formula separately to its positive part X+​=max(X,0) and negative part X−​=max(−X,0):
E[X]=∫0∞​P(X>t)dt−∫0∞​P(X<−t)dt.
(11)
Integrability makes both integrals finite. More generally the difference defines an extended expected value when at least one is finite; if both are infinite, subtraction does not define an expected value.[ref]
Tail probabilities are often easier to measure or bound than a whole probability density function. For a device's survival time, the survival function records the probability of still operating at each threshold; its integral gives the mean operating time. A finite observation window naturally gives the expected value of a capped nonnegative random variable. For a mathematical bound, P(Y>t)≤e−t/2 for all t≥0 implies E[Y]≤∫0∞​e−t/2dt=2. The tail-sum formula turns control of a whole family of events into control of an average.
