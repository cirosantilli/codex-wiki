Signed spatial filter model of a simple cell (D=∑i,j​Kij​Cij​,R=max(0,D))

 Home Biology Neuroscience Primary visual cortex Simple cell
OurBigBook.com Words: 388
A signed spatial filter model of a simple cell assigns a weight Kij​ to each sampled location and takes its dot product with the visual contrast image Cij​. Positive weights favor bright features; negative weights favor dark features. The signed drive
D=∑i,j​Kij​Cij​,R=max(0,D)
(50)
is a linear function of the image, while the rectified linear unit output R is nonlinear. D and R below are arbitrary model units. To obtain a predicted firing rate one would need a calibrated gain, background response and possibly a different output function. The model does not generate a spike train; a linear-nonlinear-Poisson cascade model adds a stochastic event-generation assumption.
For the first diagram, every one of five rows has weights
(Ki1​,Ki2​,Ki3​,Ki4​,Ki5​)=(−1/20,−1/20,1/5,−1/20,−1/20).
(51)
A bright central column, with Ci3​=1 and zero elsewhere, gives D=5/5=1. A bright middle row gives D=−1/20−1/20+1/5−1/20−1/20=0; a uniformly bright patch also gives D=0, since every row sums to zero. Move the bright column one site right: D=5(−1/20)=−1/4, so R=0. Make that shifted column dark instead: D=1/4 and R=1/4. Thus an OFF-like weight can increase output for darkness. A negative weight is a descriptive sensitivity, not evidence for a particular inhibitory synapse.
For a smooth example use the real, even Gabor filter
K(x,y)=exp(−2σx2​x2​−2σy2​y2​)cos(qx),Cθ,φ​(x,y)=cos(q(xsinθ−ycosθ)+φ).
(52)
The Gaussian function localizes the weights; the cosine supplies alternating polarity. Choose σx​=1/2, σy​=1 and q=π in arbitrary spatial units. The stripes' spatial frequency is q/(2π)=1/2 cycles per unit. Their axis makes angle θ with horizontal, while brightness changes across the perpendicular axis. φ is their spatial phase. Normalize the full-plane integral of KCθ,φ​ by its value at θ=π/2, φ=0. The Gaussian integral and the product identity for cosines give
D(θ,φ)=A(θ)cosφ,R(θ,φ)=max(0,D(θ,φ)),
(53)
where
A(θ)=1+exp(−2σx2​q2)exp[−2q2​(σx2​(sinθ−1)2+σy2​cos2θ)]+exp[−2q2​(σx2​(sinθ+1)2+σy2​cos2θ)]​.
(54)
At φ=0, horizontal stripes give R≈0.004159, 60∘ stripes give R≈0.286741, and vertical stripes give R=1. At the same vertical orientation, φ=π/2 gives D=R=0, while reversing the polarity with φ=π gives D=−1 and R=0. The preferred axis persists while the response changes dramatically with position. Shifting the vertical stripes by a quarter of their wavelength changes phase by π/2 up to sign, without rotating them.
These are two different toy filters, not discretizations of the same fitted receptive field. The discrete filter exactly rejects a uniform contrast patch; this Gabor filter need not reject uniform contrast because its weight integral need not vanish. Neither toy includes stimulus history, recurrent interactions, saturation or a measured response baseline. Their value is a transparent prediction: orientation selectivity can emerge from spatial summation, and it does not by itself imply tolerance to phase.
