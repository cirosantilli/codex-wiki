Group action (G×X⟶X)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Group theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 11k Articles: 119
A left group action of a group G on a set X is a function (g,x)↦g⋅x satisfying
e⋅x=x,(gh)⋅x=g⋅(h⋅x)(g,h∈G, x∈X),
(1)
where e is the identity element of G. The second rule means that h moves the point first and g moves it next. These rules let group multiplication describe consistent rearrangements of a set; see Ackerman's algebra notes, definition 0.4.1.1.
For each g, the function x↦g⋅x is a bijection: its inverse is x↦g−1⋅x, using the inverse element of g. Equivalently, a group action is a group homomorphism ρ:G→Sym(X) into the symmetric group of all permutations of X, with ρ(g)(x)=g⋅x and ρ(gh)=ρ(g)∘ρ(h). See MIT's group-action notes, definition 23.1.
For a concrete group action, put the vertices of a polygon of a square at A=(1,1), B=(−1,1), C=(−1,−1) and D=(1,−1). Let r(x,y)=(−y,x) be a counterclockwise quarter-turn rotation and let s(x,y)=(y,x) be a reflection. Their eight permutations of the vertices of a polygon form the dihedral group
D4​={e,r,r2,r3,s,rs,r2s,r3s},r4=s2=e,srs=r−1.
(2)
Here D4​ means the symmetry group of a four-sided polygon, with group order eight; the alternative convention calls this same group D8​. Products apply the rightmost operation first. For example, s⋅B=D and (rs)⋅B=r⋅D=A. The same group also has separate group actions on the sides of a polygon, the diagonals of a polygon, and the singleton set containing the centroid of the square. Specifying X matters: changing what is moved changes the orbits of a group action and the stabilizer subgroups.
A group action need not be a faithful group action. For example, the cyclic group C4​=Z/4Z acts on X={0,1} by k⋅x=x+k(mod2), using modular arithmetic. Only the parity of k matters: 0,2 fix both points and 1,3 exchange them. The associated group homomorphism C4​→Sym(X) has group kernel {0,2}, of group order two. Either point has a group orbit of size two and a stabilizer subgroup of group order two, so the orbit-stabilizer theorem gives 4=2⋅2.
The useful questions are which positions can be exchanged, and which transformations survive after imposing a constraint such as keeping one marked point fixed. An orbit of a group action answers the first question; a stabilizer subgroup answers the second. This turns a list of transformations into a count of distinct configurations, and explains why several transformations can produce the same configuration.
