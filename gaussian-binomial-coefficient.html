Gaussian binomial coefficient ((kn​)t​=∏j=1k​(1−tn−k+j)/(1−tj))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Combinatorics Binomial coefficient
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 8
A Gaussian binomial coefficient refines an ordinary binomial coefficient into a polynomial that remembers a size statistic. For nonnegative integers n and 0≤k≤n, write
Gn,k​(t)=(kn​)t​=∏j=1k​1−tj1−tn−k+j​.
(4)
The product represents a polynomial; it is not an instruction to divide by zero when t=1 or a denominator vanishes. The Gaussian binomial coefficient recurrence gives a way to compute it with only addition and multiplication. The empty product for k=0 is one, and Gn,n​(t)=1 as well. Setting t=1 after obtaining the polynomial recovers (kn​).
For example,
G4,2​(t)=1+t+2t2+t3+t4.​
(5)
At t=1 this gives six objects, but its coefficients reveal how those six objects are distributed across sizes 0,1,2,3,4: there are two of size two and one of each other size. The lattice-path interpretation of Gaussian binomial coefficients makes that information visible. Take unit steps north or east from (0,0) to (2,2). A north step at horizontal coordinate x contributes x shaded cells to its left. The six step words have total shaded areas 0,1,2,2,3,4.
This is useful because an aggregate count can hide the feature one actually wants. A generating function retains the distribution of a statistic, so coefficient extraction answers questions such as “how many allowed diagrams contain exactly two cells?” The partition interpretation of Gaussian binomial coefficients makes every coefficient a nonnegative integer without relying on cancellation in the product formula.
An unexpected second use comes from linear algebra. If q is a prime power, the value Gn,k​(q) counts the k-dimensional vector subspaces of Fqn​. Thus the same expression that counts six paths at t=1 counts 35 two-dimensional subspaces of F24​ at t=2. The Gaussian binomial coefficient counts finite-field subspaces article explains why selecting a vector subspace differs from selecting a subset of coordinates. [ref]
This relationship illustrates a q-analogue: the parameter can encode a statistic in one interpretation and the size of a finite field in another. Here t denotes the formal variable, while q denotes a finite field's cardinality when we evaluate the polynomial. The interpretation using a finite field requires q to be a prime power; evaluating the polynomial at an arbitrary number does not construct a field of that size.
