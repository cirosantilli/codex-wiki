Flat-plate boundary-layer traverse experiment

 Home Physics Branches of physics Continuum mechanics Boundary layer Blasius boundary layer
OurBigBook.com Words: 452
The Louisiana Tech LAB 8 - Velocity Profile above a Flat Plate describes a 14-inch plate in a wind tunnel. A Pitot-static tube of 0.10-inch outside diameter traverses normally about 12 inches downstream; its screw scale has 0.001-inch increments. A water-column manometer reads the difference between stagnation pressure and static pressure. The procedure establishes a wall position, zeros the tunnel indicator, sets fan speed, and records at least ten positions inside the boundary layer, together with room temperature and barometric pressure. The instrument list includes a flow-stream thermometer implemented with a temperature-sensitive resistor. The manual requests comparison of outer velocity with the tunnel indicator and of the recovered velocity profile with laminar or turbulent predictions; it provides no observed dataset. These are apparatus specifications and instructions, not a reported confirmation of laminar flow.
Under incompressible flow conditions, an ideal aligned Pitot tube gives q(y)=p0​(y)−p(y)=ρu(y)2/2. Convert the manometer head h to q=(ρwater​−ρ)gh, then infer u=2q/ρ​. Here g is gravitational acceleration; mass density ρ can be estimated from temperature and absolute pressure using the ideal gas law. A normalized velocity profile rises from the unresolved near-wall region to a plateau, whose value defines U; downstream profiles should broaden and collapse when plotted against η if the Blasius boundary layer assumptions hold. Integrating a recovered velocity profile gives displacement thickness and momentum thickness.
Additional controls for interpreting this experiment follow from the measurement model: check differential-pressure calibration with equal port pressures and known liquid heads; leak-check tubing; align the Pitot tube with the incoming velocity; convert wall contact to the inlet-centre coordinate rather than treating outside contact as y=0; repeat traverses and outer velocity readings to detect drift. Keep the plate smooth and aligned, and check outer static pressure at multiple downstream stations before assuming zero pressure gradient. Repeating at another station or outer velocity tests the predicted boundary-layer scaling more directly than fitting one curve. These controls are proposed checks, not actions documented as completed in the manual.
Finite probe size, wall interference, flow fluctuations, and weak near-wall dynamic pressure limit inference. Even the manual's probe-based Reynolds number criterion, Red​=ud/ν>1000 for its ideal Bernoulli equation conversion, must be checked with local u, not just U. In the illustrative model at x=0.3048m, δ99​≃3.32mm; the quoted outside diameter is 2.54mm, a substantial fraction of that thickness. A real inlet's sensing footprint and correction cannot be deduced from outside diameter alone. For an ideal footprint that averages dynamic pressure, the inferred velocity is ⟨u2⟩​ rather than ⟨u⟩. Differentiating such sparse readings to obtain wall shear stress magnifies measurement error; the momentum thickness route is less sensitive to this differentiation but still needs the unresolved near-wall contribution. The no-slip boundary condition is a model constraint here, not a direct reading from a probe centred on the wall.
