Write n=n1​ and m=n2​. The three stoichiometric vectors are (1,0), (0,1) and (−1,−1). The law of mass action gives the respective reaction propensity functions
a1​(n,m)=2,a2​(n,m)=2n​,a3​(n,m)=nm.
(1)
In particular, producing the second chemical species does not consume the first chemical species. Since the two reactants in the third chemical reaction are different chemical species, the number of possible pairs is nm.
For each Markov chain state, the chemical master equation adds incoming probability flux and subtracts outgoing probability flux:
∂t∂p(n,m,t)​=​2p(n−1,m,t)+2n​p(n,m−1,t)+(n+1)(m+1)p(n+1,m+1,t)−(2+2n​+nm)p(n,m,t).​​
(2)
Here p(n,m,t)=0 if either index is negative, and the initial condition is p(n,m,0)=1{n=m=0}​. These conventions also give the correct chemical master equation on the coordinate axes: for example, the third chemical reaction has zero outgoing reaction propensity function when either chemical species is absent.

Conditioning on the observed time. The native PDF defines τ2​ as the first production time and then conditions on that production occurring at τ2​. If the intended information is its numerical value τ2​=t, the answer depends on t. We first compute this conditional probability using a conditional probability density; conditioning on an exact value of a continuous random variable means a density ratio, since P(τ2​=t)=0.
Before τ2​, no molecules of the second chemical species are present, so the joint-removal chemical reaction cannot occur. Molecules of the first chemical species arrive as a rate-2 Poisson process, and each independently starts a clock with a rate-1/2 exponential distribution for producing the first molecule of the second chemical species. Define the unnormalized probability mass function
qn​(t)=P(A1​(t)=n, τ2​>t),F(z,t)=∑n=0∞​qn​(t)zn.
(6)
The chemical master equation before the first production, with production treated as killing, is
qn′​=2qn−1​−(2+2n​)qn​,qn​(0)=1{n=0}​.
(7)
Multiplying by zn and summing gives a partial differential equation for the probability generating function:
Ft​=2(z−1)F−2z​Fz​,F(z,0)=1.
(8)
Its solution, verifiable by direct differentiation, is
F(z,t)=exp[−2t+m(t)z],m(t)=4(1−e−t/2).
(9)
Thus qn​(t)=e−2tm(t)n/n!, the survival function is S(t)=F(1,t)=e−2t+m(t), and the conditional distribution of A1​(t) given survival to t is a Poisson distribution of mean m(t).
Production in (t,t+dt) from Markov chain state n has joint probability (n/2)qn​(t)dt+o(dt). Therefore the probability density function of τ2​ is
fτ2​​(t)=21​∑n≥1​nqn​(t)=21​m(t)S(t),t>0.
(10)
The first production leaves A1​ unchanged. Taking the density ratio gives a size-biased distribution of the surviving count:
P(A1​(τ2​)=n∣τ2​=t)=m(t)S(t)nqn​(t)​=e−m(t)(n−1)!m(t)n−1​,n≥1.
(11)
In particular, under this reading the requested conditional probability is
P(A1​(τ2​)=2∣τ2​=t)=4(1−e−t/2)exp[−4(1−e−t/2)],t>0.​
(12)
The shift by one matters: an observation made exactly when production occurs preferentially selects larger counts, since their production reaction propensity function is larger.
Without observing the numerical time. If the conditioning merely repeats the definition of τ2​, the requested probability is instead the unconditional count at that stopping time. Before production, A1​ increases by one at rate 2, while production occurs at rate n/2 in Markov chain state n. The competing exponential clocks imply
P(A1​(τ2​)=2)=2+1/22​2+11​=154​​.
(13)
The first factor requires a second arrival before production from count one; the second requires production before a third arrival. The first arrival itself is certain. Production eventually occurs with probability one, as is also clear from S(t)→0. As a consistency check, averaging the time-conditioned answer against fτ2​​ gives
∫0∞​m(t)e−m(t)2m(t)​e−2t+m(t)dt=8∫0∞​e−2t(1−e−t/2)2dt=154​.
(14)
These are two distinct, explicitly specified interpretations of the printed conditioning.

A product-Poisson stationary distribution is suggested by balancing the three reaction fluxes around the directed cycle of reaction complexes. If their mean counts are a,b, this complex balance condition is
2=2a​=ab,
(15)
which gives a=4 and b=1/2. This suggests the joint probability mass function
π(n,m)=e−9/2n!4n​m!(1/2)m​,n,m≥0.
(16)
It is normalized because it is the product of two Poisson distributions. We verify it directly in the chemical master equation, rather than assuming a product-Poisson stationary distribution theorem. The three incoming terms satisfy
2π(n−1,m)2n​π(n,m−1)(n+1)(m+1)π(n+1,m+1)​=2n​π(n,m),=nmπ(n,m),=2π(n,m).​
(17)
With negative-index values set to zero, these identities also hold on the axes. Their sum equals (2+n/2+nm)π(n,m), precisely the outgoing probability flux. The stationary average total exit rate is 2+Eπ​n/2+Eπ​(nm)=6<∞, so these stationary balance equations do define an invariant distribution of a continuous-time Markov chain for the nonexplosive model verified below. This is complex balance; it does not assert pairwise detailed balance for the irreversible chemical reactions.
To identify this invariant distribution with the limiting probability distribution in the question, first check that the model is a nonexplosive continuous-time Markov chain. On any finite time interval, A1​ is bounded by its initial count plus the finite number of arrivals in a rate-2 Poisson process. Conditional on that bound, the rate of producing A2​ is bounded, so only finitely many such productions occur. The number of joint removals cannot exceed the available molecules. Thus only finitely many chemical reactions occur in finite time.
The continuous-time Markov chain is also an irreducible Markov chain. From a Markov chain state with both counts positive, repeated joint removals reduce both. On the A2​=0 axis, producing one A2​ and then removing a pair reduces A1​ by one. On the A1​=0 axis, adding one A1​ and then removing a pair reduces A2​ by one. Each prescribed finite sequence has positive probability, so every Markov chain state reaches (0,0). Conversely, arrivals followed by productions reach any (n,m) with n>0; to reach (0,m), reach (1,m+1) and remove one pair. The continuous-time Markov chain is therefore positive recurrent, and the continuous-time Markov chain convergence theorem gives ϕ=π from the empty initial condition.
The two Poisson factors happen to have cancelling numerical coefficients at (2,2):
ϕ(2,2)=e−9/22!42​2!(1/2)2​=e−9/2.​
(18)

The native PDF repeats dWy​ in its list of independent noise terms, whereas its third stochastic differential equation uses dWz​. We use the intended three independent coordinate Brownian motions Wx​,Wy​,Wz​.
Put X=(X,Y,Z), r=∣X∣ and S=r2. Before the origin is reached, Itô's formula gives
dS​=2X⋅dX+(dX)2+(dY)2+(dZ)2=(6−2α)dt+22​X⋅dW.​
(19)
The 6 is the sum of three quadratic variation terms, each 2dt. The drift contribution is −2αdt because the numerator is ∣X∣2. The radial stochastic integral can be written using a scalar Brownian motion B:
dS=2(3−α)dt+22S​dB,d(S/2)=(3−α)dt+2S/2​dB.
(20)
Thus S/2 is a squared Bessel process of dimension δ=3−α until it reaches zero. If this equation is taken globally with the usual nonnegative squared Bessel process continuation, taking expected values gives
ES(t)=r02​+2(3−α)t,t→∞lim​tES(t)​=6−2α.​
(21)
The stochastic integral has zero expected value: stopping first at large levels bounds the integrands, yields a finite first moment for S, and then makes E∫0t​S(u)du finite, so the unrestricted integral is a square-integrable martingale.
Boundary qualification. The punctured domain in the question does not itself specify that continuation. The boundary classification of a Bessel process says that the origin is inaccessible from r0​>0 when δ≥2, equivalently α≤1. In this parameter range the original stochastic differential equation is global in the stated domain and the boxed result holds as written.
For 1<α<3, we have 0<δ<2: the origin is reached in finite time with probability one. One can also see accessibility directly from the radial generator of an SDE diffusion
Lr​f=f′′+r2−α​f′.
(22)
For r<b, the harmonic function 1−(r/b)α−1 is the probability of reaching zero before b. Finite-time access follows by using the nonnegative function
vb​(r)=2(3−α)b3−αrα−1−r2​,Lr​vb​=−1,vb​(0)=vb​(b)=0,
(23)
in the stopped Itô formula; it bounds the expected value of the time to reach zero or b. Letting b→∞ gives almost sure finite hitting of zero. The coefficients of the original vector stochastic differential equation are undefined there, so its stated punctured-domain version has no prescribed long-time evolution in this range.
The natural continuing radial squared Bessel process gives 6−2α, but stopping and holding the particle at the origin would give a different answer. Indeed, with T0​ the first hit of zero, Itô's formula for the stopped process gives
ES(t∧T0​)=r02​+2(3−α)E(t∧T0​).
(24)
Since T0​<∞ almost surely, dominated convergence applied to (t∧T0​)/t≤1 gives
limt→∞​tES(t∧T0​)​=0.
(25)
Therefore 6−2α is the usual continued-radial answer; for 1<α<3 a boundary convention is necessary. It is not a uniquely defined limit for the punctured-domain model alone.

At α=0 the transverse coordinates are independent Brownian motions with diffusion coefficient D=1. The cylinder is infinitely long, so the axial coordinate cannot affect its first contact time. By rotational invariance of Brownian motion, the mean first-passage time u depends only on the transverse radius ρ=x2+y2​.
The Kolmogorov backward equation for a mean first-passage time is Lu=−1. Here L=∂x2​+∂y2​, so the radial boundary value problem is
u′′(ρ)+ρ1​u′(ρ)=−1(0<ρ<R),u(R)=0.
(26)
The absorbing boundary condition for a diffusion is imposed on the circular cross-section. At ρ=0, the solution must extend smoothly over the axis. Multiplying by ρ and integrating gives
(ρu′)′=−ρ,u′=−2ρ​+ρC​,u=−4ρ2​+Clogρ+C0​.
(27)
Smoothness at the axis excludes the logarithm term, and u(R)=0 fixes C0​=R2/4. Consequently
τ(r0​)=4R2−r02​​.​
(28)
This also verifies the finiteness assumption behind the Kolmogorov backward equation. Let T be the first contact time and apply the stopped Itô formula to the bounded nonnegative function u just obtained. Its bounded derivatives on the disk make the stopped stochastic integral a martingale, giving
E(t∧T)=u(r0​)−Eu(ρ(t∧T))≤u(r0​).
(29)
By monotone convergence, T has finite expected value; continuity and dominated convergence then make the final term vanish as t→∞. Hence u(r0​)=ET. In particular, the mean first-passage time is R2/4 on the axis and tends to zero at the adsorbing wall.

For a radial function f(r) in three dimensions, the Laplacian is f′′+2f′/r, while the imposed radial drift contributes −αf′/r. Thus the radial generator of an SDE diffusion is
Lr​f=f′′+r2−α​f′.
(30)
The hitting probability for the inner sphere is harmonic for this diffusion generator in the shell, with value one on the inner sphere and zero on the outer sphere. At α=1, the boundary value problem is therefore
p′′(r)+r1​p′(r)=0,p(R1​)=1,p(R2​)=0.
(31)
Integrating (rp′)′=0 gives p(r)=Alogr+B. The two boundary conditions determine
p(r0​)=log(R2​/R1​)log(R2​/r0​)​.​
(32)
Both arguments of the logarithms are dimensionless. The result decreases from one to zero across the shell and lies strictly between zero and one for R1​<r0​<R2​.
For a direct probability interpretation, stop at the first contact T with either sphere. The stopped process p(r(t∧T)) is a bounded martingale by Itô's formula. The shell has positive inner radius, so its drift is bounded and its diffusion is nondegenerate; exit occurs almost surely. Optional stopping and dominated convergence therefore give
p(r0​)=Ep(r(T))=P(r(T)=R1​).
(33)
The logarithm appears because the inward drift cancels one of the two geometric f′/r terms in the three-dimensional radial Laplacian, leaving the same radial diffusion generator as a planar Brownian motion with diffusion coefficient one.

We use the equilibrium generalized Langevin equation and its generalized fluctuation-dissipation relation, with the factor 5 in the noise autocorrelation fixing the equilibrium velocity variance. Let g be the velocity relaxation function and let a tilde denote a one-sided Laplace transform:
g′(t)=−∫0t​κ(u)g(t−u)du,g(0)=1,g​(s)=s+κ(s)1​.
(34)
The equilibrium velocity autocorrelation function is χ(t)=5g(t), so
χ​(s)=s+κ(s)5​,χ(0)=5.​
(35)
For completeness, this relation follows without assuming independence of the noise and velocity in a stationary infinite history. The two-sided noise power spectral density is SR​(ω)=10Reκ(iω). The stable linear equation has velocity transfer function H(iω)=[iω+κ(iω)]−1, so
SV​(ω)=∣H(iω)∣2SR​(ω)=10ReH(iω).
(36)
This is precisely the power spectral density of the even extension of 5g(t). Stable relaxation removes initial transients with a finite second moment, giving the long-time autocorrelation function defined in the question. All the parameter choices used below have stable relaxation.
Integrating the velocity autocorrelation function twice gives the mean squared displacement from a fixed initial position:
E[(X(t)−X(0))2]=2∫0t​(t−u)χ(u)du.
(37)
A decaying initial transient does not change its asymptotic slope. Therefore the Green-Kubo formula for diffusion gives
D=∫0∞​χ(u)du=χ​(0)=κ(0)5​.
(38)
For the given friction memory kernel, direct integration yields
κ(s)=s+c3​c1​​+(s+c3​)2c1​c2​​,κ(0)=c3​c1​​+c32​c1​c2​​.
(39)
At c1​=c2​=c3​=1, the integrated friction is 2, hence
D=25​.​
(40)
The relaxation denominator here is s3+2s2+2s+2. Its roots have negative real parts by the cubic Routh-Hurwitz criterion, since all coefficients are positive and 2⋅2>2. This justifies the decay and integrability used above. The diffusion coefficient depends on the area under the friction memory kernel, not just its value at zero.
