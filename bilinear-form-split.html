Bilinear form (B:V×V→F)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra
OurBigBook.com Words: 5k Articles: 42
A bilinear form takes two vectors in a vector space V over a field F and returns a scalar in F. Its defining property is separate linearity: holding either input fixed gives a linear functional of the other. For scalars a,b and vectors u,v,w,
B(au+bv,w)B(u,av+bw)​=aB(u,w)+bB(v,w),=aB(u,v)+bB(u,w).​
(1)
This is a scalar-valued bilinear map; the chosen field is part of the definition.[ref]
Separate linearity does not mean that B is a linear map on the vector space V×V with componentwise vector addition and scalar multiplication. Over the real numbers, B(2v,2w)=4B(v,w), whereas a linear map on the pair would give 2B(v,w). The simplest example is B(x,y)=xy on the one-dimensional vector space R: fixing y=3 gives x↦3x, but doubling both x and y quadruples their product.
A bilinear form measures how two inputs interact. The dot product measures alignment; a symmetric bilinear form can describe a quadratic form; an alternating bilinear form can measure signed area. A nondegenerate bilinear form turns each vector into a unique linear functional and so connects geometry with the dual space. The same short definition therefore supports lengths, bilinear orthogonal complements, and symplectic vector spaces, depending on the additional properties imposed.
Over the complex numbers, a bilinear form is linear in both inputs without conjugating either scalar. A Hermitian form instead is a sesquilinear form and conjugates when its inputs are exchanged. For complex matrices, the adjoint A∗ used with Hermitian forms and matrix C-star algebras is the conjugate transpose, whereas a bilinear form uses the ordinary transpose AT. For example, B(z,w)=zw has B(i,i)=−1, while the Hermitian form H(z,w)=zw has H(i,i)=1.[ref]
