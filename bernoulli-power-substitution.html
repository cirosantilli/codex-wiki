Bernoulli power substitution (z=y1−n,n=0,1)

OurBigBook.com Words: 350
For arbitrary real n=0,1, work first on an interval where y>0. Define exponentiation by yr=exp(rlogy), using the real logarithm. The change of variable is differentiable and invertible there, and the chain rule gives
z′=(1−n)y−ny′,y=z1/(1−n),z>0.
(97)
Multiplying the Bernoulli differential equation by (1−n)y−n yields the first-order linear differential equation
z′+(1−n)P(x)z=(1−n)Q(x).
(98)
This is an exact transformation of nonzero solutions of a differential equation on the chosen branch; it is valid far from any reference state and does not discard higher powers as a local linearization would.
Given the initial condition y(x0​)=y0​>0, the integrating factor and transformed solution of a differential equation are
μ(x)=exp((1−n)∫x0​x​P(s)ds),z(x)=μ(x)y01−n​+(1−n)∫x0​x​μ(s)Q(s)ds​.
(99)
The product rule verifies (μz)′=(1−n)μQ. Recover y=z1/(1−n) only on the connected component of {x:z(x)>0} containing x0​, inside the original coefficient interval. The first-order linear differential equation can have a longer existence interval than this inverse transformation allows.[ref]
Negative values need a separately justified real branch. For integer n, the required powers are real and differentiable for y<0 too, but the inverse must retain the sign selected by the initial condition. For n=3, z=y−2>0 represents either y=+1/z​ or y=−1/z​; both square roots are taken of positive z. For n=2, z=1/y retains the sign directly. For a general noninteger exponent, use the positive branch unless a specific differentiable real negative branch has been established. Never evaluate a real fractional power of a negative value merely because the linear formula has become negative.
Division by yn excludes y=0. When n>0 and the original real power is defined at zero, y≡0 is an additional solution of a differential equation and must be checked in the original equation. When n<0, the equation as written is undefined at zero. On a positive branch the right-hand side is locally Lipschitz in y, so the Picard-Lindelöf theorem gives local uniqueness. At zero, the same hypothesis needs a fresh check: it holds for the polynomial n=2 example, whereas for 0<n<1 a nonzero power coefficient prevents local Lipschitz continuity there. If z↓0, the inverse sends y→∞ for n>1, but sends y↓0 for 0<n<1. The latter endpoint can lead to finite-time extinction in a sublinear Bernoulli equation, where the original equation determines how to join to zero.
