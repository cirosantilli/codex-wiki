Inductor

An inductor is an electrical circuit component designed to provide self-inductance, often a coil such as a solenoid. An ideal linear inductor has constant L>0 and zero electrical resistance. Its stored magnetic energy, with zero chosen at zero electric current, is
U=21​LI2.
(26)
For self-inductance 0.5H and electric current 3A, this gives U=2.25J. Unlike Joule heating, ideal storage is recoverable: the inductor can return energy to an electrical circuit when its electric current decreases. [ref].
The short derivation uses the terminal convention in self-inductance. The power absorbed by an ideal inductor is
PL​=vL​I=LIdtdI​=dtd​(21​LI2).
(27)
Starting from zero electric current, the integral of this power gives
U=∫0t​PL​(t′)dt′=∫0I​Lidi=21​LI2.
(28)
Positive PL​ increases stored magnetic energy; negative PL​ returns energy. At constant electric current 3A in the example, PL​=0 and induced electromotive force is zero, but U=2.25J still resides in the magnetic field. The battery in the steady RL circuit supplies Joule heating in the electrical resistance, rather than further increasing the inductor's magnetic energy.
During the closed-loop release in RL circuit, stored magnetic energy plus accumulated Joule heating stays 2.25J. A real coil also has electrical resistance, so its Joule heating must be included separately from ideal inductor storage. The constant-L formulas assume a fixed linear magnetic response.
