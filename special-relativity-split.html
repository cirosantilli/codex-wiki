Special relativity

 Home Physics Branches of physics
OurBigBook.com Words: 16k Articles: 217
Special relativity describes physics in Minkowski spacetime, where inertial frames assign different space and time coordinates to the same spacetime events. Its two postulates are Einstein's principle of relativity and the same vacuum speed of light c in every inertial frame, independent of the source's motion. Together they replace the Galilean transformation with the Lorentz transformation, coupling space and time rather than providing a universal clock. Einstein's 1905 paper developed this account through measuring rods, clocks, and light signals. [ref].
A spacetime event is an occurrence at a definite place and time: a pulse reaching a detector, for example. An inertial frame supplies a Cartesian coordinate system and clocks at rest throughout it. Einstein synchronisation sets a distant clock's reflection reading to the midpoint between a light signal's departure and return readings at the sending clock. The resulting coordinate time records the local synchronized clock at each spacetime event; it differs from the time when a distant observer receives its image. This distinction makes time dilation, length contraction, and the relativity of simultaneity precise measurements. [ref].
The beauty of special relativity is that these different measurements fit one geometry: all inertial frames agree on the Minkowski interval. It lets us translate an experiment between a laboratory and a moving apparatus while preserving the light-signal relations between spacetime events.
