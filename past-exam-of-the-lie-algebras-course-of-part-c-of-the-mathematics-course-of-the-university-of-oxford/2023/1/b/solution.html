Choose a basis x,y of the two-dimensional abelian Lie algebra a, and lifts with the same names. Every alternating bilinear form is determined by c=α(x,y), and every such form is a Lie algebra two-cocycle because every Lie bracket in a is zero. Thus every central extension of a Lie algebra has a basis x,y,z with
[x,y]=cz,[x,z]=[y,z]=0.
(6)
If c=0, all Lie brackets vanish, so this is the direct sum of Lie algebras a⊕kz≅k3. If c=0, replace the central basis vector by z′=cz. Then [x,y]=z′, which is the Heisenberg Lie algebra h3​(k). This change of basis shows that every nonzero c gives the same Lie algebra isomorphism class.
These two Lie algebras cannot be isomorphic: their derived algebras have dimensions zero and one, respectively, and a Lie algebra isomorphism preserves the derived algebra. Moreover, the Heisenberg Lie algebra cannot be a direct sum of Lie algebras kz⊕a, since that direct sum of Lie algebras would have zero Lie bracket. Therefore
a≅k3orh3​(k).​
(7)
Here “up to isomorphism” means the Lie algebra isomorphism class of the middle Lie algebra, allowing a change of the identifications at the ends of the short exact sequence. If instead equivalence of central extensions of a Lie algebra must induce the identity on both kz and a, the parameter c cannot change: the formula in part (a) has t([x,y])=0. That stricter notion has one class for each c∈k, rather than two. This distinction explains the convention required by the question.
