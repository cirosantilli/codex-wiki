Stirling formula (n!∼2πn​(n/e)n)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Asymptotic analysis Asymptotic equivalence
OurBigBook.com Words: 794 Articles: 5
Stirling's formula turns the long product defining a factorial into a short expression. For positive integers n, put
Sn​=2πn​(en​)n.
(22)
Then n!∼Sn​ as n→∞. This is asymptotic equivalence: Sn​/n!→1, so the relative error tends to zero. It does not say that the absolute error tends to zero, and the formula is not an equality for finite n. The Gamma function extends this approximation beyond integer inputs. [ref]
For n=10,
10!=3628800,S10​≃3598695.619,10!S10​−10!​≃−0.00829596.
(23)
The leading estimate is about 0.830% low. Multiplying by the first correction factor gives
S10​(1+1201​)≃3628684.749,
(24)
which is about 0.003176% low. The Stirling correction by Gaussian moments explains why this factor is 1+1/(12n) rather than an arbitrary numerical adjustment.
The usefulness is in ratios as well as in enormous numbers. Applying Stirling's formula to the three factorials in a central binomial coefficient gives
(n2n​)∼πn​4n​.
(25)
This counts the ways to choose exactly half of 2n objects. Equivalently, for 2n independent fair coin tosses, the binomial distribution assigns probability (n2n​)/4n∼1/πn​ to exactly n heads. An exponentially large count becomes a simple probability scale after cancellation.
For numerical calculations, taking the natural logarithm yields
log(n!)=nlogn−n+21​log(2πn)+rn​,
(26)
where rn​→0. Computing a logarithm of a large factorial avoids representing the factorial itself. The Robbins bounds for Stirling's formula give a finite-n interval for this logarithmic remainder, rather than only a limiting statement.
The factor 2πn​ is beautiful because a Gaussian integral lies behind a discrete product. The Gaussian peak behind Stirling's formula displays this connection through the Gamma integral and Laplace's method. A rigorous proof must also control the tails of the integral; the local expansion explains the geometry but is not by itself a complete proof.
James Stirling published Methodus differentialis in 1730, a treatise on summation and interpolation of infinite series. This setting connects the formula with a practical calculation problem: taking logarithms turns a long product into a sum, whose growth can be approximated. [ref]
