Benford law from logarithmic uniformity (P(D=i)=log10​(1+1/i))

 ... Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Probability distribution Discrete probability distribution Benford law
OurBigBook.com Words: 350
For a positive random variable X, write its base-ten logarithm as an integer part plus a fractional part:
K=⌊log10​X⌋,Z={log10​X}=log10​X−K∈[0,1),X=10K10Z.
(5)
The floor function handles values below one as well: for X=0.0042, K=−3 and the decimal significand is 10Z=4.2. Since 10Z∈[1,10), the first nonzero decimal digit is D=⌊10Z⌋. The monotonicity of the logarithm gives the exact event equivalence
D=d⟺d≤10Z<d+1⟺log10​d≤Z<log10​(d+1).
(6)
If Z has the uniform distribution on [0,1), the probability of this interval is its length. Consequently P(D=d)=log10​(d+1)−log10​d, proving the Benford law. This argument assumes uniformity of the fractional part of the logarithm, rather than uniformity of X itself. See the logarithmic-coordinate explanation in Peter Sheridan Dodds, Benford's Law, essential story.
An exact constructed example is the log-uniform distribution on [1,10), with probability density function fX​(x)=1/(xln10) there and zero elsewhere. Its total mass is ∫110​dx/(xln10)=1. The change of variables formula with x=10z gives fZ​(z)=fX​(10z)(ln10)10z=1 for 0≤z<1. Equivalently, integrating directly gives
P(D=d)=∫dd+1​xln10dx​=ln10ln(d+1)−lnd​=log10​(dd+1​).
(7)
For example, the probability of first digit four is log10​(5/4)≈0.096910013. By contrast, an ordinary uniform distribution for X on [1,10) has probability density function 1/9 there, so P(D=d)=∫dd+1​dx/9=1/9 for every digit. This is a concrete counterexample to any claim that the Benford law holds for every positive random variable.
More generally, a log-uniform distribution on [A,B) with 0<A<B has uniform Y=log10​X on [log10​A,log10​B). If its logarithmic span log10​(B/A)=m is a positive integer, reduction to the fractional part wraps an interval of length m around the unit interval exactly m times, making Z uniform even when A is not a power of ten. Arbitrary partial-decade truncations generally do not have this property: for a log-uniform distribution on [1,2), the first digit is always one.
Under the uniform fractional-logarithm assumption, multiplication by any constant c>0 replaces Z by Z′={Z+log10​c}. This is a rotation modulo one, which preserves the uniform distribution and therefore preserves the Benford law; see scale invariance of decimal significands. For the constructed [1,10) example, multiplying by 0.001 gives a log-uniform distribution on [0.001,0.01) with the same digit probabilities. The claim concerns the decimal significand law: there is no proper probability density function proportional to 1/x over all of (0,∞), since its integral diverges. The existing scale-invariant prior describes that unnormalizable measure, while a finite log-uniform distribution is normalizable.
