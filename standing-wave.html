Standing wave

 Home Physics Mathematical physics
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 7
A standing wave results from the superposition principle applied to equal-frequency, equal-amplitude waves travelling in opposite directions. Its spatial nodes of a standing wave remain fixed while its displacement oscillates in time.
For a pure standing wave, the two travelling waves also have equal amplitudes: their interference leaves a fixed mode shape, rather than a profile moving along the string. Choose the position origin and time origin so that their transverse displacements are
y+​(x,t)=asin(kx−ωt),y−​(x,t)=asin(kx+ωt).
(98)
Here a>0 is each constituent's amplitude, k=2π/λ is the wavenumber, λ is the wavelength, and ω=2πf is the angular frequency corresponding to frequency f. Their wave speed is c=ω/k. The superposition principle and the sine addition formula give
y=y+​+y−​=2asin(kx)cos(ωt).
(99)
The spatial factor stays put; the temporal factor changes its size and sign. Other constant wave phases shift the position or time origin. Unequal constituent amplitudes generally leave moving displacement at the would-be nodes of a standing wave, rather than this exact factorization.[ref]
For an original ideal-string example, set L=1m, c=20ms−1, and choose the third normal mode. Then k=3πm−1, ω=60πs−1, and f=30Hz by fixed-end standing wave frequencies. With each constituent amplitude a=5mm,
y(x,t)=0.010msin(1m3πx​)cos(1s60πt​).
(100)
Its nodes of a standing wave are at x=0,1/3,2/3,1m and its antinodes at x=1/6,1/2,5/6m. These are constructed model parameters, not experimental readings.
At t=1/120s, every point has zero displacement, but most have nonzero transverse velocity: the string is crossing its straight configuration. The black dots alone mark permanent nodes of a standing wave. At an antinode the greatest transverse speed is 2aω=0.6πms−1, much smaller than the chosen 20ms−1 wave speed. One is motion of string material; the other is propagation of the constituent profiles.
The beauty of a standing wave is that motion reveals geometry: fixed nodes of a standing wave turn a moving disturbance into a spatial ruler. Their separation measures wavelength, and boundary conditions turn the continuous choice of wavenumber into a discrete set of normal modes. This is useful both for interpreting string vibrations and for understanding how a bounded wave equation selects its eigenfrequencies.
