Metric space ((X,d))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Topological analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 6k Articles: 117
A metric space is a set X together with a metric d measuring how far apart its elements are. The elements can be positions, functions, or other objects: what matters is that the metric obeys the same basic rules. This lets one discuss convergence in a metric space, continuous maps, and Cauchy sequences without first assigning Cartesian coordinates. The useful abstraction is that many arguments require only comparisons of distances, rather than the particular objects being compared.
Even the same set can support several informative metrics. On the plane R2 with Cartesian coordinates, write x=(x1​,x2​) and y=(y1​,y2​). Three standard choices are Manhattan distance, Euclidean distance, and Chebyshev distance:
d1​(x,y)d2​(x,y)d∞​(x,y)​=∣x1​−y1​∣+∣x2​−y2​∣,=(x1​−y1​)2+(x2​−y2​)2​,=max{∣x1​−y1​∣,∣x2​−y2​∣}.​
(5)
They come from the L1 norm, Euclidean norm, and maximum norm, respectively, through d(x,y)=∥x−y∥. For O=(0,0) and P=(3,4), Manhattan distance is 3+4=7, Euclidean distance is 9+16​=5, and Chebyshev distance is max{3,4}=4. All three answers are correct for their respective metrics.
The choice encodes the question being asked. Manhattan distance models travel along a rectangular street grid. Euclidean distance measures unrestricted travel along a line segment. Chebyshev distance measures the time needed when two Cartesian coordinates can change simultaneously, each at speed at most one. Thus choosing a metric is part of modelling, rather than a consequence of knowing the endpoints. These examples have the same metric topology, but different metric path lengths and different collections of metric geodesics.
A historical reference for the general study of metric spaces is Maurice Fréchet's 1906 paper Sur quelques points du calcul fonctionnel, published in volume 22 of the Rendiconti del Circolo Matematico di Palermo. [ref]
