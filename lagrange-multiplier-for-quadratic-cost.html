Lagrange multiplier for quadratic cost (minx+y=c​(x2+2y2)=2c2/3)

Suppose two adjustable inputs must deliver a prescribed total c, and their combined cost is the quadratic form f(x,y)=x2+2y2. These are chosen, dimensionless quantities; the coefficient 2 makes concentrating an input in y more expensive. Initially allow all real numbers x,y, with the equality optimization constraint g(x,y)=x+y=c. The optimization Lagrangian with our chosen sign is
L=x2+2y2−λ(x+y−c).
(84)
The stationary point equations give 2x=λ, 4y=λ, and x+y=c. Consequently
x∗​=32c​,y∗​=3c​,λ∗​=34c​,V(c)=32c2​.​
(85)
For c=3, the unique global minimum is at (2,1), with cost 6 and Lagrange multiplier 4. Equal allocation would cost 6.75; assigning everything to x would cost 9. At the optimum, the two marginal costs agree: ∂x​f=∂y​f=4. Moving a little input from one component to the other therefore has zero first-order effect on cost. When c>0, this solution also meets the extra nonnegativity optimization constraints x,y≥0, so it remains optimal with those added.
Here the Lagrange multiplier equations locate a candidate, and a short independent check certifies it. On the entire feasible set, substitute y=c−x and use completing the square:
x2+2(c−x)2=3(x−32c​)2+32c2​.
(86)
The squared term is nonnegative and vanishes only at x=2c/3. Thus the candidate is the unique global minimum; there is no global maximum on this unbounded feasible set, a line. This separates finding a stationary point from proving optimality. The geometric beauty is that equal marginal costs, parallel gradients, tangency of level sets, and completing the square identify the same allocation.
