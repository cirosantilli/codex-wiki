Harmonic series (∑n=1∞​n1​)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Sequence and series Series (mathematics)
OurBigBook.com Words: 441 Articles: 1
The harmonic series is the positive series
1+21​+31​+41​+⋯=∑n=1∞​n1​.
(33)
Its partial sums are the harmonic numbers HN​. Although the individual terms tend to zero, HN​ grows without bound, so the harmonic series is not a convergent series. This is a basic warning against using the size of one term as a bound on all the terms still to come.
The growth is slow enough to be visually deceptive. The partial sums at N=10,100,1000,10000 are approximately 2.92896825,5.18737752,7.48547086,9.78760604. Adding ten times as many terms increases the accumulated total by roughly log10≈2.30258509, rather than multiplying the total by ten. The telescoping series ∑1/[n(n+1)] has similarly vanishing terms but approaches the finite sum one.
A short block argument proves the divergence without trusting a finite plot. For each integer k≥0, the block from 2k+1 through 2k+1 contains 2k terms, each at least 1/2k+1. Therefore
∑n=2k+12k+1​n1​≥21​,H2m​≥1+2m​.
(34)
Every doubling supplies at least another half, and arbitrarily many doublings are possible. A collection of tiny terms can still have a substantial combined contribution. See Jiří Lebl, Basic Analysis I, Example 2.5.11 for this grouping proof.
Integral comparison sharpens the growth estimate. Here log denotes the natural logarithm. Since 1/x is a strictly decreasing function for positive x, comparison of rectangles with the integral gives
log(N+1)≤HN​≤1+logN.
(35)
Thus the divergence is logarithmic. The finer difference HN​−logN tends to the Euler--Mascheroni constant, approximately 0.57721566; the harmonic number expansion supplies higher-order corrections. This turns the counterexample into a usable quantitative estimate of accumulated reciprocal contributions.
Euler's De progressionibus harmonicis observationes, written in 1734 and published in 1740, studies these sums beyond the yes-or-no question of convergence. Sections 2–3 compare long blocks to show continued growth, and later sections compare the sums with logarithms and calculate the constant offset. The [ref] identifies the dates and original publication; [ref] makes the calculations accessible. This is not a claim that Euler first discovered divergence: the paper's interest includes measuring how slowly a divergent sum grows.
