Laplacian (Δ)

 ... Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Calculus Multivariable calculus Vector calculus
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 12
The Laplacian takes the divergence of a scalar field's gradient. For a real-valued function f on an open region of Euclidean space, with continuous partial derivatives through order two, its expression in a Cartesian coordinate system is
Δf=∇⋅∇f=∑i=1n​∂xi2​∂2f​=tr(D2f).
(38)
Thus the Laplacian, also called the Laplace operator, adds the coordinate second derivatives; equivalently it is the matrix trace of the Hessian matrix. Its output is another scalar field.[ref]
Three original examples distinguish shape from this sum. The bowl f(x,y)=x2+y2 has Δf=2+2=4. The saddle g(x,y)=x2−y2 instead has Δg=2−2=0: opposite second derivatives cancel. It is a harmonic function, although its graph of a function is not a plane. For the Gaussian function h(x,y)=e−r2, with r2=x2+y2, differentiation gives
Δh=4(r2−1)e−r2.
(39)
Its Laplacian is negative inside the circle of radius one, zero on that circle, and positive outside it. The Gaussian function itself is positive throughout: its value and its Laplacian describe different properties.
The sum does not favor a particular orientation. Under a change of Cartesian coordinate system represented by an orthogonal matrix Q, the Hessian matrix becomes QT(D2f)Q. The cyclic property of the trace and QQT=I preserve its matrix trace. This makes the Laplacian useful when the behavior depends on spatial differences equally in every direction, rather than on the arbitrary choice of axes.
For a vector field, the Euclidean Laplacian acts separately on components expressed in a fixed Cartesian coordinate system. Components in a position-dependent basis require differentiating that basis too; applying a scalar formula separately to polar components omits those terms.[ref] The scalar formula in Laplacian in polar coordinates is valid away from r=0; smoothness at the origin must be handled in coordinates that remain regular there.
