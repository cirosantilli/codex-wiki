Elastic rebound. Tectonic motion loads a fault whose shallow portion is locked. Rock on either side deforms and stores elastic strain energy while slip is inhibited. When failure permits rapid slip, part of the stored energy becomes seismic radiation and part is dissipated in friction and damage. The adjacent rock partly relaxes, while a permanent displacement remains across the fault. Geodetic strain accumulation and coseismic offsets support this loading–release picture.[ref]
The model is useful for brittle crustal faulting but is not a prediction of precise recurrence, rupture size or frictional evolution. Aseismic creep can release deformation without a large sudden event. Viscoelastic flow, afterslip, fluid-pressure changes and interacting fault segments complicate a simple locked-fault cycle. Deep-focus earthquakes and non-shear source components require additional physics; not every earthquake source is adequately described by a shallow brittle double couple. Rebound also need not release all the pre-existing stress.
A thin negative-gradient velocity zone. In horizontal layering, use the seismic ray parameter p=sini/v, the conserved horizontal slowness. A ray turns when pv=1. Downward passage through decreasing speed makes pv smaller, so a surface ray cannot turn in the negative-gradient interior. Rays either turn above the zone or penetrate it and turn below where speed has recovered sufficiently. This produces missing turning depths and a seismic shadow range from a low-velocity zone in the ideal ray model.

The figures use an explicit illustrative profile, not measured Earth parameters: speed rises from 1 to 2 over depths 0 to 1, falls to 1.8 by depth 1.2, then rises with its original gradient. The asthenosphere beneath oceanic lithosphere is an important setting for a low-velocity zone. A major P-wave speed reduction also occurs at the boundary between the mantle and the outer core of the Earth; S waves do not propagate through the liquid outer core of the Earth.[ref]
