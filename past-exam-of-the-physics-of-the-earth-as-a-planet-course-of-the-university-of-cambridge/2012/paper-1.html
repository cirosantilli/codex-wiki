Write Ts​ for the fixed surface temperature and θ=T−Ts​. The heat capacity per unit volume is ρ0​cp​=k/κ. Per unit horizontal area the layer stores sensible heat (k/κ)dθ. Its increase equals the input less the output:
κkd​dtdT​=F−E(t).​
(1)
The same equation holds for θ because Ts​ is constant. It assumes the supplied constant-property, fixed-thickness layer, without internal heat production, latent heat, or mechanical contributions to its heat budget. The infinite horizontal area is handled by taking the budget per unit area.

Vigorous convection keeps the interior nearly uniform, so the upper thermal boundary layer carries a temperature drop of order θ. A boundary layer becomes unstable when its local Rayleigh number reaches an order-fixed critical value Rc​:
Raδ​=ηκρ0​gαθδ3​∼Rc​.
(2)
This is marginal-stability thermal boundary-layer scaling using the Local Rayleigh-number closure: boundary-layer buoyancy overcomes viscous and thermal-diffusive stabilization. Equivalently, a buoyancy-driven velocity scale u∼ρ0​gαθδ2/η makes uδ/κ order Rc​. Thus
δ∼(ρ0​gαθRc​ηκ​)1/3,E(t)∼δkθ​=Cθ4/3,C=k(Rc​ηκρ0​gα​)1/3.​
(3)
There is no d in this closure, as required for a thin boundary layer controlled by its own stability. The exponent is fixed by scaling; its absolute coefficient is not. Here Rc​ represents the geometry and boundary conditions, together with any order-one boundary-layer coefficient. The question supplies no numerical value for it. The assumption requires δ≪d, positive θ, and properties sufficiently constant over the temperature contrast.

For flux-driven convective layer cooling, use E=Cθ4/3 with the same unspecified coefficient as in (b). The steady heat balance gives
T∗​=Ts​+θ∗​,θ∗​=(F/C)3/4=(kF​)3/4(ρ0​gαRc​ηκ​)1/4.​
(4)
The corresponding boundary-layer thickness is
δ∗​=Fkθ∗​​=(ρ0​gαFkRc​ηκ​)1/4.
(5)
Set θ=θ∗​+ϵ and expand the heat loss to first order. Since E′(θ∗​)=(4/3)Cθ∗1/3​=4F/(3θ∗​),
κkd​ϵ˙=−3θ∗​4F​ϵ,ϵ(t)=ϵ(0)e−t/τ,τ=4κF3kdθ∗​​=4κ3dδ∗​​.​
(6)
This is the near-equilibrium relaxation time. The full equation is nonlinear, so an arbitrary large initial temperature departure need not decay as one exponential. Positive heat loss derivative makes the steady state stable. In particular, τ scales with dδ∗​, rather than d2, because convection shortens the effective heat-transport distance to the boundary layer.

A persistent barrier to whole-mantle circulation adds an internal thermal bottleneck and permits the deep mantle to retain a larger temperature excess. In a single well-mixed layer there is one interior temperature and one surface heat-loss law. The coupled thermal histories of layered mantle convection arise because separately mixed layers can have different temperatures, exchanging heat through an interfacial boundary layer as well as losing heat at the surface. Their thermal histories then have coupled relaxation modes rather than the single mode in (c).
For example, let θl​,θu​ be lower- and upper-layer temperature excesses, Hl​,Hu​ their heat capacities per area, and
J=Ci​(θl​−θu​)4/3,E=Cs​θu4/3​.
(7)
With no internal heating the budgets are Hl​θ˙l​=F−J and Hu​θ˙u​=J−E. At steady state,
θu​=(F/Cs​)3/4,θl​=θu​+(F/Ci​)3/4.
(8)
Thus a fixed basal flux still leaves the surface eventually with E=F, but the lower layer is hotter than it would be in the single-temperature model. For a given deep temperature instead, the extra interfacial drop reduces outward heat transfer. This favours delayed loss of deep primordial heat and distinct upper- and lower-mantle cooling histories. A numerical change in cooling time cannot be obtained from layer count alone: interface transport, layer thicknesses, temperature-dependent viscosity, internal heating, and whether material can cross the interface matter. Merely replacing d by d/2 in (c) misses the added interface and is not a model of two-layer cooling.

Assume straight rays in a homogeneous medium. At horizontal distance x−x0​ the source distance is R=(x−x0​)2+z02​​. With vA​>vB​,
Δt=R(vB​1​−vA​1​),(Δt)2=M(x−x0​)2+B,
(9)
where M=(1/vB​−1/vA​)2 and B=(Δt0​)2=Mz02​. Clock offsets cancel in a difference measured at one station. The difference curve is symmetric about the epicentre and reaches its minimum directly above the source.
The original plot uses the given numerical observations. Its broad minimum is consistent with
x0​≃140±15km,Δt0​≃15.0±0.5s.​
(10)
These are conservative graph-reading estimates, allowing for one-second rounding; the three equal smallest entries do not imply a flat physical minimum. A quadratic fit to squared differences refines the vertex to x0​=140.17km and Δt0​=14.896s. These extra digits describe the fit, not the precision of an individual observation. Formal statistical errors require a model for the rounding and other measurement errors.

For a general assumed speed ratio r=vA​/vB​,
tA​=t0​+r−1Δt​.
(14)
When r=2, each station estimates t0​ by tA​−Δt. Averaging gives
t0​=15.0s.​
(15)
The individual estimates have sample standard deviation 2.74s and range 11 to 19s. If station clock errors are independent with zero mean, the standard error of their mean is 2.74/13​=0.76s. Thus one may quote 15.0±0.8s as a conditional one-standard-error mean, with about ±3s scatter for an individual station. The problem does not bound a common clock bias: adding the same offset to every clock shifts this inferred origin time by that offset without changing any time difference. Therefore no unconditional absolute-time accuracy follows from the supplied data.
The speed-ratio check is an origin-time and speed-ratio regression of tA​ on Δt. It gives
tA​≃20.00s+(0.732±0.237)Δt,
(16)
where the uncertainty is a residual-based standard error of slope. The factor-two hypothesis predicts slope 1, about 1.13 standard errors from the fitted value: the ratio 2 is satisfactory at the accuracy allowed by these clocks, but is not precisely verified. Formally r=1+1/slope≃2.37, with a local propagated standard error about 0.44. This inference assumes clock errors have no systematic dependence on station position or time difference. With completely unrestricted station offsets, a regression cannot identify r at all.
Let K=(1/vB​−1/vA​)=M​. The difference curve determines K, x0​, and z0​, regardless of the particular value assigned to r. Changing the ratio to 2.25 while refitting the individual speeds gives
vA​=Kr−1​≃10.01kms−1,vB​=rKr−1​≃4.45kms−1.
(17)
Hence
Δx0​=0,Δz0​=0,t0​(2.25)=tA​−0.8Δt​=18.738s,Δt0​=+3.738s.​
(18)
The conditional standard error of this new mean is 0.72s. Its predicted slope 0.8 is also compatible with the regression. Holding one speed fixed while changing r would alter the depth, but that adds a constraint not present in this question: here both speeds are inferred from the same difference data and chosen ratio.

In the three-dimensional Earth, a network identifies P- and S-wave arrivals and fits source coordinates (x0​,y0​,z0​) and origin time t0​ using a travel-time model. Time differences constrain path lengths without requiring a common absolute clock zero; calibrated absolute times then fix the origin time. Real inversions trace refracted rays through a layered or heterogeneous velocity model and use many stations, azimuths, and phases, rather than literal circles at fixed wave speed. Residuals and the sensitivity matrix quantify uncertainty and expose poor station geometry or a poor velocity model.
Depth and origin time are often the weakest pair, especially for a shallow event observed by distant surface stations. In the homogeneous example,
ti​=t0​+vri2​+z02​​​,δti​=δt0​+vri2​+z02​​z0​​δz0​.
(19)
If the depth sensitivities are small or nearly alike across the network, a deeper source's longer travel time can be offset by an earlier origin time. In contrast, horizontal displacement often gives opposite-sign residuals on opposite sides of an event and is better constrained by good azimuthal coverage. Nearby stations and depth-sensitive reflected phases can break the earthquake depth–origin-time trade-off; the ranking is not universal for every network or event depth.

Hawaii: short-wavelength volcanic loading and long-wavelength mantle support must be distinguished. A dense volcanic edifice replacing seawater adds mass and gives a positive free-air gravity anomaly. Its load bends the lithosphere down, producing a surrounding moat and an outer flexural arch. The associated displaced density interfaces contribute negative and positive gravity signals at neighbouring distances. Observed gravity near the Hawaiian ridge includes strong positive anomalies over the ridge, negative flanks, and a broader positive belt. At much longer wavelengths, the Hawaiian swell also correlates with a positive gravity anomaly, with a smaller gravity-to-topography ratio that requires compensation; the precise source of support is not determined by that correlation alone.[ref]
The mechanical distinction can be expressed with a simple elastic-plate model. For load height h, downward deflection w, plate rigidity D, loading density contrast Δρl​ relative to water, and restoring density contrast Δρb​ at an interface,
D∇4w+Δρb​gw=Δρl​gh.
(20)
For a Fourier component of horizontal wavenumber q, this gives wq​=Δρl​ghq​/(Dq4+Δρb​g). The load's direct gravity contribution competes with the negative contribution of a depressed interface at reference depth t. In a linear thin-interface approximation,
Z(q)≡hq​δgq​​=2πGΔρl​[1−1+Dq4/(Δρb​g)e−qt​].
(21)
At short wavelengths rigidity suppresses compensation, leaving relatively high positive gravity admittance. At long wavelengths the plate can deflect and compensate more completely, reducing admittance. Fitting the wavelength dependence constrains effective elastic thickness through D=EY​Te3​/[12(1−ν2)], subject to density structure, load distribution and subsurface loading assumptions. This explains why local Airy compensation alone misses the spatially extended flexural response.
The broad swell needs a deeper explanation than the narrow volcanic pile: hot, buoyant mantle and mantle flow can support regional topography, while deep density anomalies and surface elevation jointly determine its gravity. A free-air anomaly keeps the attracting mass of the topography; a Bouguer correction removes an assumed direct topographic attraction and can reveal its compensating mass deficit. Gravity plus topography constrain support as a function of wavelength; neither alone uniquely fixes the mantle density distribution.
For the OR alternative, triple junctions are points where three plate boundaries meet. Each boundary can be a spreading ridge, subduction trench, or transform fault. The three plate velocities satisfy
vAB​+vBC​+vCA​=0.
(22)
This velocity triangle is necessary but not enough: a single junction velocity uJ​ must also keep the meeting point on all three moving boundaries. For symmetric spreading between A and B, the ridge constraint is [uJ​−(vA​+vB​)/2]⋅n=0. For a transform, vA​⋅n=vB​⋅n, and the junction must have that same normal component. For a trench, its normal migration must match the adopted subduction/rollback model; a trench fixed relative to the overriding plate shares that plate's normal velocity.
In velocity space each boundary supplies a line of possible junction velocities. A common intersection means the junction can migrate while preserving its local configuration; incompatible constraints force boundary reorganization. This is kinematic stability, not the claim that the junction is stationary. A symmetric ridge–ridge–ridge example has three arms meeting at 120∘ and a common junction velocity; unequal spreading rates do not require those equal angles. Transform and trench junctions depend on the allowed relative motions and boundary geometry. Stable and unstable evolution were formulated through plate-velocity geometry in the original triple-junction analysis.[ref]

For the ray sketches in (i) and (ii), take a horizontally layered isotropic medium, a surface source and receiver, depth z positive downwards, and wave speed v(z). Snell's law conserves p=sini/v, where i is measured from the vertical. A returning diving ray turns where pv(zt​)=1, with
X(p)=2∫0zt​​1−p2v(z)2​pv(z)​dz,T(p)=2∫0zt​​v(z)1−p2v(z)2​dz​,dXdT​=p.
(23)
For a uniform positive gradient v=vs​+βz, rays are circular arcs and
X=β2​vt2​−vs2​​,T=β2​arcosh(vt​/vs​)=β2​arsinh(2vs​βX​).
(24)
Thus ordinary travel time increases with distance but has a decreasing slope as deeper rays sample faster material. The following two leaves discuss the labelled changes to this reference structure.
For the OR alternative, the supplied fault-plane diagrams, assume the usual convention that shaded quadrants are compressional first motions on a lower-hemisphere projection. Each pair of nodal planes represents a fault plane and an auxiliary plane; first-motion polarity alone cannot choose which slipped. Geological mapping, aftershock locations or rupture observations can resolve that ambiguity.[ref]
Diagram (i): normal faulting. The central unshaded band indicates the extensional dip-slip pattern. It is compatible with crustal extension, such as a continental rift or a spreading environment, with a hanging wall moving down relative to the footwall on the actual fault. Its two curved nodal traces are the possible fault and auxiliary planes.
Diagram (ii): strike-slip faulting. The two straight nodal diameters divide four alternating quadrants, as expected for two vertical candidate planes. It is compatible with a transform plate boundary or continental shear zone. One candidate is right lateral and the conjugate is left lateral; polarity alone does not select the actual plane. Without a north arrow, geographical strikes cannot be assigned.
Diagram (iii): oblique-slip faulting with a reverse component. The off-centre nodal intersection and curved traces distinguish it from pure vertical strike slip, while shading through the centre indicates the reverse component under the stated convention. It is compatible with transpression, such as oblique convergence at a plate margin or a compressional strike-slip bend. Both strike-slip and dip-slip components are required; it is not merely a rotated pure strike-slip pattern. Exact strike, dip, rake, and slip-plane selection cannot be recovered numerically from this uncalibrated printed sketch. Reversing the shading convention would reverse the normal/reverse interpretation, so the convention is essential.

A thin high-positive-gradient zone bends rays back rapidly, bunches their return distances, and can produce a triplicated travel-time curve. Rays turning above it retain the ordinary branch. Rays entering and turning within it have a stronger curvature and can return at smaller distances than slightly shallower rays; deeper-turning rays below it eventually regain increasing range. The mapping from turning depth to distance can therefore fold twice. In the overlapping distance interval, three rays with different turning depths have distinct travel times.
The travel-time curve has two fold points, with branches joined at caustics. Its first-arrival curve is the lower envelope, changing branch where travel times cross; later branches remain physically distinct arrivals. The apparent surface slowness on each branch is dT/dX=p, with the deeper, faster-sampling ray generally supplying a smaller slope. A finite high-gradient zone does not require a velocity jump or a separate reflected ray. Whether the branches actually triplicate, and the ranges of the caustics, depend on gradient contrast and thickness, which are not numerically supplied.
The original upper-row example uses a specified illustrative piecewise-linear speed profile to show the folds. The lower row is the low-velocity example in (ii). All distances and times in this illustration come from those chosen profiles, not from extra exam data.

In a zone with dv/dz<0, a descending ray bends towards the vertical instead of turning back towards the surface. A ray cannot have its ordinary smooth diving-ray turning point within that decreasing-speed interval. There are rays turning above the zone and rays that penetrate it and turn deeper, after speed has increased sufficiently. The latter spend extra time traversing slower material, so their branch is delayed relative to a simple extrapolation of the original uniform-gradient curve.
Turning depths within the low-velocity zone are missing. In a piecewise-gradient example this can leave a range interval without a returning diving ray: a geometrical shadow zone between the shallow and deeper branches. The lower row of the original plot shows that situation, with the interrupted shallow branch and the delayed deeper branch. The boundaries are profile-dependent; reflected, refracted or diffracted energy can still arrive, and smoothing an idealized velocity maximum can change the branch limits. Thus the robust signature is the interruption/reorganization of the ordinary turning-ray family, not a universal claim of zero recorded signal. The supplied description alone does not specify unique numerical travel times or shadow-zone widths.
