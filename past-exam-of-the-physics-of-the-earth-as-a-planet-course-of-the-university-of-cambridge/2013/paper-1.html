In the oceanic plate cooling model, T is temperature, z height above the plate base, a plate thickness, and t age since formation at the spreading axis. With half-spreading rate v, a parcel at distance x has t=x/v. The surface and basal temperatures are T0​ and T1​ respectively. The positive integer n labels a sine diffusion mode. For constant thermal diffusivity κ,
τ0​=π2κa2​.​
(1)
Substituting one sine mode into Tt​=κTzz​ fixes this normalization; a2/κ itself is the diffusion timescale before the factor π2.
Source qualification. The printed transient coefficients have the wrong sign for the intended initially hot plate. Indeed, in the interior,
∑n≥1​nπ2(−1)n​sinanπz​=−az​.
(2)
Thus the literal zero-age profile is T0​+ΔT(1−2z/a), where ΔT=T1​−T0​, rather than T1​. It would even approach T0​−ΔT from inside the upper boundary. The intended hot-ridge cooling solution reverses the transient sign, using 2(−1)n+1/(nπ). Both versions have the same boundary temperatures and old-age limit. Part (b) gives both surface-flux expressions; the physical ridge-elevation and ridge-push calculations explicitly use the hot initial plate. This sign is present in the original PDF as well as the held transcription.

At the ridge axis, T(z,0)=T1​ for 0<z<a in the intended cooling model. Upwelling and magmatic heat supply continually bring hot material into the newly formed plate. The abrupt meeting of this hot interior with the cold upper boundary is an idealized corner discontinuity, resolved diffusively for t>0. The printed series fails this initial condition, as shown in the parent Solution.

Far from the ridge, T(z,∞)=T0​+ΔT(1−z/a). Transients have decayed and the heat equation reduces to Tzz​=0. A finite basal heat supply sustains this gradient. Thermal thickness, seafloor depth and surface heat flux therefore approach finite limits rather than continuing the laws of the half-space cooling model indefinitely.

Take positive heat flux upwards, along increasing z, and denote thermal conductivity by k. Fourier's law gives qs​=−kTz​∣z=a​. Since cos(nπ)=(−1)n, differentiation of the literal printed expression gives
qs,printed​(t)=akΔT​[1−2n=1∑∞​e−n2t/τ0​].​
(3)
Its negative young-age flux is another sign of the unphysical initial profile. For the explicitly corrected hot initial plate, the surface heat flux of a cooling plate is instead
qs​(t)=akΔT​[1+2n=1∑∞​e−n2t/τ0​].​
(4)
The old-age limit is kΔT/a. For t≪τ0​, replacing the Gaussian sum by its leading integral gives qs​∼kΔT/πκt​, recovering the half-space cooling model. Neither k nor κ is numerically specified, so the age-dependent flux remains symbolic as requested.

Use the intended hot-ridge state and a linear law of thermal expansion about mantle density ρm​ at T1​. Let s be depth below the old seafloor. Its excess density is
δρ(s)=ρm​αΔT(1−s/a),0<s<a,
(5)
and its excess column mass is ∫0a​δρds=ρm​αΔTa/2. Isostasy requires equal pressure at a compensation depth below both columns. Lowering the old seafloor by h replaces rock by water, reducing column mass by (ρm​−ρw​)h. Therefore the isostatic ridge elevation in a plate model is
h=2(ρm​−ρw​)ρm​αΔTa​=3.90×103m=3.9km.​
(6)
Here αΔT=0.052; the result is a first-order estimate and neglects sediments, crustal-composition differences and flexural support. If one instead insists on the literal printed zero-age profile, its column is denser than the old plate and the formal relative elevation reverses sign, −3.9km to this order. That profile cannot describe the elevated hot ridge posited by the question.

Ridge push is a horizontal pressure force integrated with depth, equivalently a difference in gravitational potential energy per horizontal area. It is not the weight of a slab multiplied by the surface slope at one chosen point.
For an explicit compensated-column derivation, let y measure depth below the ridge seafloor; the old seafloor is at y=h. Set b=ρm​αΔT. The hot column has greater pressure. Its pressure excess over the old column is
Δp(y)=⎩⎨⎧​g(ρm​−ρw​)y,2agb​(a−s)2,0,​0<y<h,y=h+s,0<s<a,y>h+a.​
(7)
The second line follows by integrating δρ(s)=b(1−s/a) upwards from the compensation level. The two expressions agree at y=h because (ρm​−ρw​)h=ba/2. Integrating this positive pressure excess gives the ridge-push force in a plate model, directed away from the ridge:
F=∫Δpdy=6ρm​gαΔTa2​+2g(ρm​−ρw​)h2​.
(8)
Consistently retaining only first order in thermal expansion gives
F≃2.60×1012Nm−1.​
(9)
If the topographic term is retained within the same linear-density column model, it adds 1.52×1011Nm−1, giving F≃2.75×1012Nm−1. This is a useful small correction, not a claim that all other second-order material effects have been included. Both values use the intended hot initial plate, not the inconsistent printed zero-age profile.

The depth-averaged added horizontal compressive stress is σˉ=F/a. Resolving its traction on a plane dipping at angle δ gives the fault shear stress from a plate-integrated force,
τˉ=σˉsinδcosδ,∣τˉ∣=2aF​≃1.30×107Pa=13MPa(δ=45∘).​
(10)
Keeping the previous topographic correction gives about 13.8MPa. This is the mean additional shear from ridge push, assuming the force is transmitted through thickness a and resolving the nonisotropic stress increment. An isotropic lithostatic pressure adds normal traction but no shear. The force integral alone does not specify the local stress at each point on the fault or establish failure.

a OurBigBook.com
Oceanic magnetic anomalies. Newly formed oceanic crust contains magnetic minerals. As it cools through their magnetic blocking temperatures, it acquires thermoremanent magnetization recording the ambient field polarity. Continuing seafloor spreading carries that record away from the ridge. Geomagnetic reversals therefore generate approximately paired marine magnetic stripes parallel to the ridge, with older crust farther from its axis. A magnetometer measures the superposed field above this magnetized material; subtracting a reference field reveals magnetic anomalies. The anomaly sign and shape depend on magnetization direction, latitude, source depth and geometry, so a positive measured anomaly is not universally identical to normal rock polarity.[ref]
Matching a sequence of stripes to an independently dated reversal chronology gives plate ages and spreading histories. A stripe of width w formed over interval Δt implies half-spreading rate v=w/Δt under steady symmetric spreading; full separation speed is 2v. Unequal widths on opposite flanks can reflect asymmetric spreading or later deformation. Alteration, remagnetization and overlapping magnetic sources complicate inversion. The sequence correlation is stronger evidence than an isolated stripe or a field-strength peak.
Rayleigh-Bénard convection. Consider a horizontal fluid layer of thickness d, hotter below by ΔT>0. The conductive state is motionless with a linear temperature gradient. A rising warm parcel is buoyant, but viscosity resists its motion and thermal diffusion erases its temperature anomaly. In the Boussinesq approximation, density variations are retained in buoyancy while the flow is otherwise incompressible. The competing effects are measured by the Rayleigh number and Prandtl number,
Ra=νκgαΔTd3​,Pr=κν​.
(11)
For stress-free impermeable, fixed-temperature boundaries, linear stationary disturbances with horizontal wavenumber q/d give
Ra(q)=q2(q2+π2)3​.
(12)
Minimizing gives qc​=π/2​ and the critical Rayleigh number for an isothermal layer, Rac​=27π4/4≃657.5. Two rigid no-slip, fixed-temperature boundaries instead give approximately 1708; the critical value is not independent of boundary conditions.[ref]
Above onset, buoyancy can sustain overturning rolls and heat transfer exceeds pure conduction, with Nusselt number Nu>1. Nonlinear advection eventually limits the disturbance; larger driving can produce time-dependent or turbulent states. The ideal Boussinesq problem does not require hexagonal cells: planform selection depends on boundaries and non-Boussinesq effects. Convection driven by surface tension at a free surface is a distinct mechanism. An adverse temperature gradient supplies driving, but overcoming diffusion and viscosity requires a finite threshold.

Earthquake location. The hypocentre is rupture initiation; its surface projection is the epicentre. Record calibrated arrival times at stations of known coordinates, identify P wave and S wave phases, and predict their travel times through a stated velocity model. Earthquake location by arrival times fits
ti​=t0​+Ti​(x),
(13)
for the three source coordinates and origin time t0​. Linearization supplies residual equations δti​=δt0​+∇Ti​⋅δx. A grid search or iterative weighted least squares inversion can use pick uncertainties, while station geometry controls whether those parameters are well constrained. Four independent arrival constraints are the minimum for four unknowns, not a guarantee of a reliable solution.
In a homogeneous medium, an arrival-time difference between an S wave and a P wave cancels origin time and estimates source-to-station distance,
r=1/vS​−1/vP​tS​−tP​​.
(14)
Several distances intersect to locate the event; real layered or three-dimensional Earth structure requires curved-ray travel times. Nearby stations and diverse takeoff angles help resolve depth. Clock errors, phase misidentification, poor azimuthal coverage and incorrect velocities introduce uncertainty or systematic bias. Closely spaced earthquakes can be relocated precisely relative to each other because common-path errors partly cancel, even when their absolute locations remain uncertain. Report a location uncertainty and model assumptions, not merely a best-fit point.
H. F. Reid's earthquake-cycle model. A fault can remain locked while long-term tectonic motion slowly deforms the surrounding rocks. Elastic strain energy and shear stress accumulate. When resistance is overcome, rapid fault slip releases part of that stored energy as seismic waves, frictional heat and other irreversible work. The adjoining rocks partly recover their elastic distortion while retaining a permanent displacement discontinuity across the fault: this is elastic rebound. Surface markers initially crossing the fault become bent during loading and offset after slip.[ref]
A simple sketch is a slow rise of stress followed by a rapid stress drop, then renewed loading; the plate displacement continues throughout. Geodetic deformation, observed fault offsets and earthquake stress changes can test the picture. Actual cycles need not repeat with identical slips or times: creep, afterslip, stress transfer and heterogeneous friction complicate the idealization. The release need not remove all pre-existing stress. Elastic rebound explains storage and release; it is not a deterministic earthquake clock.

The phase velocity in Figure 1 is measured along the Earth's surface. Spherical geometry makes the local horizontal phase speed at radius r equal to cr/RE​. In the ray approximation, squared radial shear wavenumber is
qr2​=ω2[β(r)21​−c2r2RE2​​].
(19)
The spherical surface-wave evanescence threshold immediately below the discontinuity is therefore
c<5.086371−4106371​≃5.43kms−1.​
(20)
Equality is the grazing limit. Using 5.08kms−1 directly would omit the requested spherical correction. Immediately above the discontinuity the corresponding boundary is 4.87(6371/5961)≃5.20kms−1. Hence the interval 5.20<c<5.43kms−1 specifically describes propagation just above the jump and evanescence just below it. Slower modes can already be evanescent above 410km.
Intersecting the plotted branches with 5.43kms−1 gives the following approximate upper periods. Below each upper period, that branch is evanescent immediately beneath the discontinuity:
  • fundamental mode: throughout the displayed 10–100s range;
  • first and second overtones: approximately 67 and 38s;
  • successive higher curves: approximately 27, 21, 18, 15, 13 and 11s.
For these overtones the displayed ranges thus start near 10s and end at their respective crossings. Any still higher curve already above the threshold at the lower plotted period has no evanescent interval within the displayed range. Readings of the closely spaced higher curves are only accurate to about a second, and the plot does not establish their continuation to unshown periods. Nor does the local inequality prove that a mode remains evanescent at every greater depth in an unspecified Earth model.

Use the flat two-layer model, with thickness h=30km, crustal shear speed β1​=3.5kms−1 and mantle speed β2​=4.5kms−1. For SH-wave travel times in a crustal waveguide, unfolding each reflected path gives all reflection orders at once:
tD​=3.5x​,tm​=3.5x2+(60m)2​​,m=1,2,…,​
(21)
with x in kilometres and times in seconds. The direct wave begins at the origin; reflected orders have intercepts 60m/3.5=17.14ms and approach the direct-wave slope at large distance.
The critical angle, measured from the vertical, is ic​=arcsin(3.5/4.5)≃51.1∘. Associated critical refraction and its multiples give
tH,m​=4.5x​+2mhβ1−2​−β2−2​​=4.5x​+10.77m,x≥xc,m​=2mhtanic​≃74.25mkm.​
(22)
Each head-wave line is tangent to its reflection curve at the critical distance. In particular the primary head wave exists before it becomes the first arrival; it overtakes the direct wave only around 170km.
The slope of a reflected travel-time curve is p=dtm​/dx=sini/β1​. For x<xc,m​, p<1/β2​, so the mantle supports outgoing shear radiation and successive reverberations leak energy. For x>xc,m​, 1/β2​<p<1/β1​: the mantle field is evanescent and ideal interface reflection is total. These leaking and trapped SH-wave reverberations explain the annotations in the original travel-time drawing below. A reflected ray is not itself a separate Love mode: constructive interference of the trapped contributions, including reflection phase, selects the normal modes.
For a horizontally propagating mode with β1​<c<β2​, define vertical wavenumbers
q=ωβ1−2​−c−2​,s=ωc−2−β2−2​​.
(23)
The free upper surface and welded interface impose the Love-wave dispersion condition
tan(qh)=μ1​qμ2​s​,qh=nπ+arctanμ1​qμ2​s​,n=0,1,2,…,
(24)
where μj​=ρj​βj2​ are shear moduli. Each phase-velocity branch falls towards 3.5kms−1 with increasing frequency. Higher branches join 4.5kms−1 at their low-frequency cutoffs. At cutoff s→0, so
fc,n​=2hβ1−2​−β2−2​​n​≃0.0928nHz.​
(25)
Thus the first three overtones have maximum periods about 10.77, 5.39 and 3.59s. The fundamental mode has n=0 and no positive-frequency cutoff in this flat layer-over-half-space model: it approaches c=β2​ as f→0, with ever-deeper penetration. The phase-velocity sketch uses an explicitly illustrative equal-density choice, because the question gives speeds but no density or rigidity ratio. That missing ratio changes detailed branch curvature, not the cutoff values or the requested branch topology.
For the actual finite spherical Earth, the very-long-period continuation must instead be described by discrete toroidal normal modes, not a flat infinite waveguide at arbitrary wavelength. The flat-model answer therefore does not assert a continuum of arbitrarily low-frequency global nonrigid oscillations. A global lowest oscillation frequency cannot be computed from the supplied crust-and-half-space data alone.[ref]
