Glycine titration experiment

 ... Organic acid Carboxylic acid Amino acid Alpha-amino acid Glycine protonation Glycine titration
OurBigBook.com Words: 325
The Amrita Virtual Lab procedure uses 0.1molL−1 glycine, hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide, two burettes, a pH meter, beaker and stirrer. A pipette transfers 20mL of glycine into a 100mL beaker. Calibrate the meter with standard buffer solutions at pH 4, 7 and 10, then record the starting pH. Add hydrochloric acid in 0.3mL increments, recording pH after each addition until 1.6. Rinse the electrode in distilled water, take a separate fresh 20mL portion, and add sodium hydroxide in 0.3mL increments until pH 12.5. Its observed readout is paired added volume and pH, plotted as an acid-base titration curve; it does not measure each glycine form directly. [ref]
These are two titrations from the starting glycine chemical solution, rather than the single acidified-sample protocol assumed by the solid curve in glycine titration. Retain each portion's initial volume and the signed added acid/base amount of substance; do not concatenate raw burette readings and assume the result begins at b=0. For this procedure's 0.00200mol of glycine, each 0.3mL addition supplies 0.000030mol of acid or base, a 0.015 increment relative to the glycine inventory. This calculated resolution is not a reported measurement.
Useful additional controls are a fresh repeat portion, independently checked acid/base concentrations, a water blank, and a calibration-check buffer solution after the run. Stir without striking the glass electrode, keep its sensing region immersed and wait for stable readings; record temperature and background salts. Reduce addition size near a steep pH change. These are suggested controls for testing the model, beyond the published steps. A water blank can reveal contamination but must not be subtracted directly from pH, which is a logarithm.
The standards at pH 4, 7 and 10 do not bracket either endpoint of this protocol. Extreme readings therefore require independently checked instrument performance, and alkaline samples are sensitive to atmospheric carbon dioxide uptake. Electrode drift, incomplete mixing, volume error, dilution and changing ionic strength can shift apparent pKa values. A titration constrains the acid/base model; it cannot establish chirality or separate every microscopic protonation form. The two original diagrams show calculated predictions to compare with measured points, not reconstructed experimental data.
