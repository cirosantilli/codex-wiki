Matrix similarity (B=S−1AS)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 12
Two square matrices A,B over the same field are similar if B=S−1AS for an invertible matrix S. They can describe one linear operator using two different ordered bases, with the same basis used for input and output in each description. Fix the convention x=Sy: x is the old coordinate vector, y the new coordinate vector, and the columns of S are the new basis vectors in old coordinates. This is the convention of the change-of-basis matrix.[ref]
For a concrete linear operator on the physical plane, let T double horizontal components and multiply vertical components by five. In the old standard basis E=(e1​,e2​), its matrix of a linear map is A=diag(2,5). Choose the new ordered basis F=(b1​,b2​) with b1​=e1​, b2​=e1​+e2​. Then
S=(10​11​),S−1=(10​−11​),B=S−1AS=(20​−35​).
(78)
The second column of B says T(b2​)=−3b1​+5b2​=2e1​+5e2​. Its off-diagonal entry records how the chosen basis vectors describe the output; the physical linear operator still stretches along the old horizontal and vertical directions.
Take the physical vector p=2e1​+e2​=b1​+b2​. Its two coordinate vectors, and the two descriptions of T(p), are
x=(21​),y=(11​),Ax=(45​),By=(−15​).
(79)
Reconstruction gives T(p)=4e1​+5e2​=−b1​+5b2​. The output coordinate vectors differ, but the endpoint in the physical plane is the same.
This is why matrix similarity is useful: a convenient basis can simplify calculations without changing the linear operator. A diagonalizable matrix can be replaced by a diagonal matrix, making repeated application a calculation with powers of its eigenvalues. In the example, A makes the two independent stretch factors visible, while B expresses the same action in directions chosen for another purpose. Matrix similarity invariants separate properties of the linear operator from features of its address system.
