First fix distinct q1​,…,qk​ and let Q=q1​+⋯+qk​. A negative-degree divisor has no sections: if 0=f∈L(D), then (f)+D is an effective divisor of degree degD, since a principal divisor has degree zero, so degD cannot be negative. As degK=0 and deg(K−Q)=−k<0, the Riemann-Roch theorem therefore gives
ℓ(Q)=degQ+1−g+ℓ(K−Q)=k.​
(43)
Now choose distinct p1​,…,pk​ outside the support of Q, and put P=p1​+⋯+pk​. A function with the required zeros and poles must be a nonzero element of L(Q−P). Conversely, if 0=f∈L(Q−P), then (f)+Q−P is an effective divisor of degree zero, hence is zero. It follows that (f)=P−Q, giving precisely the required simple zeros and simple poles, with no others. Thus the existence question is exactly whether L(Q−P)=0.
Take a basis f1​,…,fk​ of the Riemann-Roch space L(Q). Evaluation at the pi​ defines the linear map
EP​:L(Q)⟶Ck,f⟼(f(p1​),…,f(pk​)),
(44)
whose kernel is L(Q−P). The evaluation criterion for a prescribed principal divisor becomes
L(Q−P)=0⟺Δ(p1​,…,pk​):=det(fj​(pi​))1≤i,j≤k​=0.
(45)
The crucial point is that this determinant does not vanish identically; a count of unknowns and equations alone would not prove this.
To verify it, begin with V0​=L(Q), of dimension k. Whenever Vj−1​=0, choose a nonzero f∈Vj−1​. Its zeros are finite. Choose pj​ outside those zeros, the support of Q and the previously chosen points. Evaluation at pj​ is then a nonzero linear functional on Vj−1​, so
Vj​={h∈Vj−1​:h(pj​)=0},dimVj​=dimVj−1​−1.
(46)
After k steps, Vk​=0. Thus there exists a tuple of distinct points with Δ=0.
The entries of this determinant vary holomorphically away from the qi​, and are rational functions on the nonsingular projective curve. Its vanishing is therefore a proper closed exceptional locus on this configuration space; its complement is open and dense in the Zariski topology. For a generic choice of the pi​, EP​ is invertible and
ℓ(Q−P)=0,so no function with the prescribed zeros and poles exists.​
(47)
This holds for every fixed tuple of distinct qi​, which proves the assertion for generic choices of both sets of points. At k=1, it is especially transparent: L(q1​)=C, so no nonzero member can vanish at a distinct p1​.
