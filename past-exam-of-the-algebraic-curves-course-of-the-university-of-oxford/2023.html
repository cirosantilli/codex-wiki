Write C=V(P), where P∈C[x,y,z] is a squarefree polynomial and a homogeneous polynomial of degree d. A projective point p∈C is a singular point precisely when
Px​(p)=Py​(p)=Pz​(p)=0.
(1)
If p=[p0​:p1​:p2​] is a nonsingular point of an algebraic curve, its projective tangent line is
Tp​C={[x:y:z]:Px​(p)x+Py​(p)y+Pz​(p)z=0}.​
(2)
The coefficients are not all zero, and the Euler theorem for homogeneous functions gives p0​Px​(p)+p1​Py​(p)+p2​Pz​(p)=dP(p)=0, so the line passes through p. Changing the representative of the projective point only rescales the equation.
The strong form of Bézout's theorem says that two projective plane curves C,D of degrees d,e, with no common irreducible component, have finitely many intersections and
p∈C∩D∑​Ip​(C,D)=de.​
(3)
Each intersection multiplicity is a positive integer. At p∈C∩D,
Ip​(C,D)=1⟺p is nonsingular on both curves and Tp​C=Tp​D.​
(4)
This is the criterion for a transverse intersection of plane curves. In particular, an intersection at a singular point of either curve contributes at least two.

If d=1, C is a line and has no singular points. Suppose d>1 and take P to be an irreducible polynomial. First, Px​ is not the zero polynomial: otherwise P∈C[y,z], and a homogeneous polynomial in two variables of degree greater than one factors into linear factors over C, contrary to irreducibility.
Let D=V(Px​) be the polar curve in this coordinate direction. Since degPx​=d−1<d, the irreducible polynomial P cannot divide Px​. Thus C,D have no common irreducible component, and Bézout's theorem applies. One may count repeated factors of Px​ with their multiplicities; alternatively, passing to its reduced equation only decreases degD.
Every singular point p of C satisfies Px​(p)=0 and has Ip​(C,D)≥2 by part (a). Consequently, if s is the number of distinct singular points,
2s≤∑p∈C∩D​Ip​(C,D)≤d(d−1),s≤2d(d−1)​.​
(5)
This argument also proves that the singular locus is finite.

There is a nonsingular point of an algebraic curve on C, since part (b) makes its singular locus finite whereas a projective curve has infinitely many complex points. Apply a projective transformation taking one such point to p=[1:0:0], and use P for the transformed homogeneous polynomial. As in part (b), Px​ is nonzero and D=V(Px​) has no common irreducible component with C.
The Euler theorem for homogeneous functions and its differentiated identities give
Px​(p)=dP(p)=0,Pxx​(p)=(d−1)Px​(p)=0,
(6)
and
Pxy​(p)=(d−1)Py​(p),Pxz​(p)=(d−1)Pz​(p).
(7)
Since p is nonsingular on C, at least one of Py​(p),Pz​(p) is nonzero. Because d>1, the polar curve D is also nonsingular at p, and these identities show that Tp​D=Tp​C. Hence Ip​(C,D)≥2.
This additional intersection is separate from all the singular points of C. Thus Bézout's theorem gives
2s+2≤∑q∈C∩D​Iq​(C,D)≤d(d−1),s≤2d(d−1)​−1.​
(8)
The improvement comes from making the polar curve tangent at a chosen nonsingular point, thereby reserving at least two of the available intersections.

Let di​=degCi​. Under the reduced plane curve convention, the distinct irreducible components have ∑i​di​=d. Away from intersections with other components, a point of Ci​ is nonsingular on the union exactly when it is nonsingular on Ci​. For example, a local equation of the union is the equation of Ci​ multiplied by a nonvanishing factor. At an intersection of two components, differentiating their product makes every first partial derivative vanish. Therefore
Sing(C)=⋃i​Sing(Ci​) ∪ ⋃i<j​(Ci​∩Cj​).
(9)
Part (b) bounds the number of singular points of Ci​ by di​(di​−1)/2. For each distinct pair of irreducible components, Bézout's theorem bounds the number of distinct intersections by di​dj​. The cardinality of a union is at most the sum of the cardinalities, even if some points have been counted several times. Hence
#Sing(C)​≤i∑​2di​(di​−1)​+i<j∑​di​dj​=21​​(i∑​di​)2−i∑​di​​=2d(d−1)​​.​
(10)
The reduced convention matters. If one instead allows the equation L2=0 as a nonreduced curve and uses its equation to test singularity, every point on its supporting line has vanishing first partial derivatives. A finite bound would then be false.

Take a union of d distinct lines in general position for projective lines, meaning that no three pass through one point. Each pair contributes one distinct singular point, and every other point lies on just one nonsingular line. Thus the union has degree of a projective curve d and exactly
(2d​)=2d(d−1)​​
(11)
distinct singular points. For d=1, this gives a line with zero singular points, as required.
For a concrete construction, choose distinct t1​,…,td​∈C and set
Li​:y=ti​x+ti2​z,C:∏i=1d​(y−ti​x−ti2​z)=0.
(12)
For i=j, the intersection is [−(ti​+tj​):−ti​tj​:1]. Substitution into the equation of Lk​ gives −(tk​−ti​)(tk​−tj​), which is nonzero when k is distinct from i,j. Thus there are no triple intersections, including at infinity.

A nonsingular point of an algebraic curve p is an inflection point of an algebraic plane curve, or flex, if its projective tangent line has contact of order at least three:
Ip​(C,Tp​C)≥3.
(13)
Here the projective tangent line must not be a component of C. Ordinary tangency has contact order two.
Let C=V(F), where F is a homogeneous polynomial of degree d≥2. Its Hessian curve is defined by
HF​=det​Fxx​Fyx​Fzx​​Fxy​Fyy​Fzy​​Fxz​Fyz​Fzz​​​=0.
(14)
Each entry has degree d−2, so a nonzero HF​ has degree 3(d−2). By the Hessian criterion for a flex, the flexes are exactly the points of C∩V(HF​). The stated no-common-component assumption and Bézout's theorem therefore give
#{distinct flexes of C}≤3d(d−2).​
(15)
This counts distinct points; higher contact can use more than one unit of intersection multiplicity at a single point.
For completeness, the Hessian criterion for a flex follows by putting p=[0:0:1] and Tp​C={y=0}, with Fy​(p)=0. The Euler theorem for homogeneous functions gives Fxz​(p)=Fzz​(p)=0 and Fyz​(p)=(d−1)Fy​(p), whence
HF​(p)=−(d−1)2Fy​(p)2Fxx​(p).
(16)
The restriction F(x,0,1) already has vanishing constant and linear terms. Its quadratic term vanishes exactly when the intersection multiplicity with the tangent is at least three, proving the criterion.
For d≥3 the bound is the actual maximum number of flexes of a smooth plane curve: a general nonsingular curve has only contact of order three, and the corresponding intersections with its Hessian curve are simple. One way to see genericity is to vary a point and a line through it, a three-dimensional family. Contact of order at least four imposes four independent linear conditions on the coefficients of a degree-d equation for d≥3. Thus the locus of equations with such contact is a proper exceptional locus. Locally, if the curve is y=h(x), its Hessian curve restricted to C is a nonvanishing factor times h′′(x); contact exactly three gives a simple zero.
A nonsingular plane conic has no flexes, agreeing with the formula at d=2. A line is excluded from this formulation: its tangent is itself and its Hessian matrix is zero, so the no-common-component hypothesis fails. The formula is therefore intended for d≥2, rather than giving the meaningless negative value at d=1.

For F=x3+y3+3xz2, the first partial derivatives are
Fx​=3(x2+z2),Fy​=3y2,Fz​=6xz.
(17)
Their simultaneous vanishing would force y=0, xz=0 and x2+z2=0, hence x=y=z=0, which is not a projective point. This also verifies that the given plane cubic is nonsingular.
The Hessian matrix and its determinant are
HessF=6​x0z​0y0​z0x​​,HF​=216y(x2−z2).
(18)
Thus the Hessian curve is the union of y=0, x=z and x=−z. On y=0, the equation of C reduces to x(x2+3z2)=0, giving
[0:0:1],[i3​:0:1],[−i3​:0:1].​
(19)
On x=z one must have z=0, so put z=x=1 and obtain y3=−4. On x=−z similarly put z=1, x=−1, obtaining y3=4. Choose r∈C with r3=4 and let ω=exp(2πi/3). The remaining points are
[1:−rωj:1],[−1:rωj:1],j=0,1,2.​
(20)
All nine listed projective points are distinct. They exhaust the intersection with the Hessian curve, so the Hessian criterion for a flex proves that these, and only these, are the flexes in this example. There are none at infinity: z=0 and HF​=0 would force x=y=0 on C.

First make the invertible linear change of homogeneous coordinates
U=−y,V=3​z,W=x.
(21)
The original equation becomes
V2W=U3−W3=(U−W)(U−ωW)(U−ω2W),ω=exp(2πi/3).
(22)
This takes the flex [0:0:1] to the point at infinity [0:1:0] of a Weierstrass model.
Put h=ω−1 and choose s∈C with s2=h3. Both are nonzero. Define
X=hU−W​,Y=sV​,Z=W.
(23)
Since U=hX+Z, the three factors on the right are hX, h(X−Z) and h(X−λZ), where
λ=ω−1ω2−1​=1+ω=21+i3​​.​
(24)
The resulting equation is precisely
Y2Z−X(X−Z)(X−λZ)=0,​
(25)
the Legendre form of an elliptic curve. In particular, λ=0,1.
Combining the two changes gives an explicit projective transformation from the original coordinates:
​XYZ​​=​−1/h01​−1/h00​03​/s0​​​xyz​​.
(26)
Its determinant is −3​/(hs)=0, so this is a valid transformation of the whole projective plane, including the points where an affine coordinate would be undefined.

Let (r0​,r1​,r2​) be any ordering of the three distinct numbers 0,1,λ. Set h=r1​−r0​ and choose s=0 with s2=h3. The projective transformation
​XYZ​​=​1/h00​01/s0​−r0​/h01​​​xyz​​
(27)
has the required matrix form. As x=hX+r0​Z and y=sY, substitution gives
Y2Z=X(X−Z)(X−λZ),λ=r1​−r0​r2​−r0​​.​
(28)
The choice s2=h3 is exactly what cancels the coefficient introduced by the change in x.
The six orderings give the Legendre parameter orbit:
(r0​,r1​,r2​)(0,1,λ)(1,0,λ)(0,λ,1)(λ,0,1)(1,λ,0)(λ,1,0)​λλ1−λ1/λ(λ−1)/λ1/(1−λ)λ/(λ−1)​​
(29)
Every entry is different from 0,1 when λ=0,1.
There are no further possibilities for a matrix of the stated form. Its determinant is acd, so a,c,d must be nonzero. On z=1 it induces the affine change u↦(a/d)u+b/d and scales v=y/z by c/d. It preserves the line y=0, whose three intersections with the plane cubic have u-coordinates 0,1,λ. Their images must have coordinates 0,1,λ. Choosing the two roots sent to 0,1 determines the affine change and leaves exactly the six orderings above.
For general λ the six values are distinct. They coincide in some special cases: λ∈{−1,2,1/2} gives three values, and λ2−λ+1=0 gives two. In particular, for the value obtained in part (c), the two possible values are (1±i3​)/2. This does not contradict the paper's qualification that there are six for general λ.

A divisor on an algebraic curve is a finite formal sum
D=∑p∈C​np​p,np​∈Z.
(30)
Its degree of a divisor is degD=∑p​np​. It is an effective divisor, written D≥0, when every np​≥0.
A meromorphic differential on a Riemann surface is a one-form represented in every local holomorphic coordinate t by a(t)dt, where a is a meromorphic function. Under a coordinate change t=t(u), its coefficient becomes a(t(u))t′(u), so these expressions agree on overlapping charts. Since t′(u) is a nonvanishing holomorphic function for a valid coordinate change, the order of a zero or pole of the differential is independent of coordinate. Equivalently, for a fixed nonconstant meromorphic function h, any such differential can be written fdh with f meromorphic.
A canonical divisor is the divisor on an algebraic curve
K=(η)=∑p∈C​ordp​(η)p
(31)
of a nonzero meromorphic differential on a Riemann surface η. If η′=fη, then (η′)=(η)+(f), so all canonical divisors are related by linear equivalence of divisors. Their degree is
degK=2g−2.​
(32)
The Riemann-Roch theorem states that, for every divisor on an algebraic curve D and any canonical divisor K on the nonsingular projective curve C of genus g,
ℓ(D)−ℓ(K−D)=degD+1−g,​ℓ(D)=dimC​L(D).
(33)
The term ℓ(K−D) measures the failure of the naive count of conditions on meromorphic functions.

Choose a nonzero meromorphic differential on a Riemann surface η and write K=(η). Multiplication defines a linear map
L(K)⟶HD(C),f⟼fη.
(34)
It takes values in the holomorphic differential forms because (fη)=(f)+K≥0. It is injective since η=0. It is surjective because, for any holomorphic differential form ω, the ratio ω/η is a meromorphic function and has (ω/η)+K=(ω)≥0. Thus this map identifies HD(C) with the canonical Riemann-Roch space.
Furthermore, L(0)=C: a meromorphic function with no poles is holomorphic on the compact Riemann surface C, and hence constant by the maximum modulus principle. Applying the Riemann-Roch theorem with D=0 gives
1−ℓ(K)=1−g.
(35)
Therefore
dimC​HD(C)=ℓ(K)=g.​
(36)

The generator η=fdh is a nonzero holomorphic differential form. Its canonical divisor (η) is an effective divisor, since there are no poles. In genus one, the degree of a divisor formula in part (a) gives
deg(η)=2g−2=0.
(37)
An effective divisor of degree zero has every coefficient zero. Thus
(η)=0,fdh has no zeros anywhere on C.​
(38)
This proves nonvanishing of holomorphic differentials in genus one. It concerns the differential itself: a zero or pole of the particular coefficient f may be cancelled by the local behavior of dh.

An explicit answer, initially written where yz=0, is the meromorphic differential on a Riemann surface
η=y/zd(x/z)​=yzzdx−xdz​.​
(39)
The ratios x/z and y/z are functions on C, so the expression does not depend on the representative of its homogeneous coordinates. It is a scalar multiple of an invariant differential on an elliptic curve.
On the complex torus C/Λ, the differential dw descends from C because translation by any element of the period lattice leaves dw unchanged. It is holomorphic and nowhere zero. The given biholomorphism satisfies, wherever the displayed quotient is initially defined,
Φ∗η=℘′(w)d℘(w)​=dw.
(40)
Consequently, η=(Φ−1)∗(dw) extends across every apparent denominator zero and across [0:1:0] as a nonzero holomorphic differential form on all of C.
One can also check the apparent exceptional points directly. On z=1, put P(X)=4∏i​(X−ei​), so Y2=P(X). At Y=0 the roots are distinct, and differentiating gives
YdX​=P′(X)2dY​,
(41)
which is regular and nonvanishing there. At [0:1:0], use u=x/y, v=z/y. The equation is v=4∏i​(u−ei​v), so u is a local parameter on a smooth algebraic curve and v=4u3+O(u5). Hence
η=vd(u/v)=du−vu​dv=(−2+O(u2))du,
(42)
also regular and nonvanishing.
Only distinctness of the ei​ is needed. The paper additionally writes ei​=0, which is not true for every period lattice: for Λ=Z+iZ, the symmetry ℘(iw)=−℘(w) makes the half-period value at w=(1+i)/2 zero, since iw≡w(modΛ). This unnecessary restriction has no effect on the answer.

First fix distinct q1​,…,qk​ and let Q=q1​+⋯+qk​. A negative-degree divisor has no sections: if 0=f∈L(D), then (f)+D is an effective divisor of degree degD, since a principal divisor has degree zero, so degD cannot be negative. As degK=0 and deg(K−Q)=−k<0, the Riemann-Roch theorem therefore gives
ℓ(Q)=degQ+1−g+ℓ(K−Q)=k.​
(43)
Now choose distinct p1​,…,pk​ outside the support of Q, and put P=p1​+⋯+pk​. A function with the required zeros and poles must be a nonzero element of L(Q−P). Conversely, if 0=f∈L(Q−P), then (f)+Q−P is an effective divisor of degree zero, hence is zero. It follows that (f)=P−Q, giving precisely the required simple zeros and simple poles, with no others. Thus the existence question is exactly whether L(Q−P)=0.
Take a basis f1​,…,fk​ of the Riemann-Roch space L(Q). Evaluation at the pi​ defines the linear map
EP​:L(Q)⟶Ck,f⟼(f(p1​),…,f(pk​)),
(44)
whose kernel is L(Q−P). The evaluation criterion for a prescribed principal divisor becomes
L(Q−P)=0⟺Δ(p1​,…,pk​):=det(fj​(pi​))1≤i,j≤k​=0.
(45)
The crucial point is that this determinant does not vanish identically; a count of unknowns and equations alone would not prove this.
To verify it, begin with V0​=L(Q), of dimension k. Whenever Vj−1​=0, choose a nonzero f∈Vj−1​. Its zeros are finite. Choose pj​ outside those zeros, the support of Q and the previously chosen points. Evaluation at pj​ is then a nonzero linear functional on Vj−1​, so
Vj​={h∈Vj−1​:h(pj​)=0},dimVj​=dimVj−1​−1.
(46)
After k steps, Vk​=0. Thus there exists a tuple of distinct points with Δ=0.
The entries of this determinant vary holomorphically away from the qi​, and are rational functions on the nonsingular projective curve. Its vanishing is therefore a proper closed exceptional locus on this configuration space; its complement is open and dense in the Zariski topology. For a generic choice of the pi​, EP​ is invertible and
ℓ(Q−P)=0,so no function with the prescribed zeros and poles exists.​
(47)
This holds for every fixed tuple of distinct qi​, which proves the assertion for generic choices of both sets of points. At k=1, it is especially transparent: L(q1​)=C, so no nonzero member can vanish at a distinct p1​.
