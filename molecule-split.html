Molecule

 Home Chemistry
OurBigBook.com Words: 12k Articles: 166
A molecule, using the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) convention, is an electrically neutral entity containing two or more chemical atoms. Its constituents form a bound unit through chemical bonds; merely drawing a boundary around neighboring particles does not make a molecule. The neutrality requirement concerns total electric charge, not the absence of internal charge separation: water is a polar molecule with zero total electric charge.[ref]
One water molecule, H2​O, contains two hydrogen and one oxygen chemical atom. One molecular oxygen molecule, O2​, contains two oxygen chemical atoms; a molecule need not contain different chemical elements. One carbon dioxide molecule, CO2​, contains one carbon and two oxygen chemical atoms. The molecular formula records these inventories, while molecular geometry describes their arrangement. Water and carbon dioxide are chemical compounds; molecular oxygen is a form of one chemical element.
Ordinary crystalline sodium chloride, NaCl, contains an extended arrangement of sodium cations Na+ and chlorine anions Cl−. Its formula unit specifies one of each for compositional accounting, without selecting a unique isolated pair. Bulk neutrality therefore does not imply a material consists of neutral molecules. This statement concerns the ionic crystal; the physical state and conditions matter when deciding what particles a chemical formula represents.[ref]
A polyatomic ion is a charged bound group of chemical atoms, such as hydronium, H3​O+. A molecular ion formed by removing an electron from intact water, H2​O∙+, retains the same atom inventory but has net electric charge +e. Neither satisfies the neutral-molecule definition. Distinguishing these entities makes molecular count and amount of substance and mass spectrometry interpretable: say whether the count is of chemical atoms, neutral molecules, ions, or formula units.
The useful surprise is that a molecule can be treated as one particle for motion and counting while retaining internal structure. Its molar mass connects a particle inventory to bulk mass; molecular rotation and molecular vibration reveal internal organization. The continuum approximation uses bulk fields in place of individual molecular positions and motions.
