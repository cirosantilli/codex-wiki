Young's modulus (E)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Continuum mechanics Elasticity (physics) Linear elasticity
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 6
Young's modulus is the ratio of axial stress to axial strain in uniaxial linear elasticity. For an isotropic plate, the bending rigidity is Et3/[12(1−ν2)], with thickness t and Poisson's ratio ν.
A tensile modulus experiment asks how much a specimen stretches under an axial force, while allowing its sides to contract freely. For initial cross-sectional area A0​ and an extensometer spanning initial gauge length Lg​, define engineering stress σ=F/A0​ and engineering strain ε=ΔLg​/Lg​. In the proportional range,
E=dεdσ​=A0​Lg​​d(ΔLg​)dF​.
(13)
The ratio σ/ε agrees with this derivative when the line passes through the unloaded origin. A fitted offset should not be mistaken for a material property. Young's modulus has units of pressure: 1 Pa=1 Nm−2. By contrast, the spring constant of a uniform tensile specimen of length L is ks​=EA0​/L, with units Nm−1. Two rods made of the same material can therefore have very different spring constants while sharing Young's modulus.
The normalization removes a useful geometrical ambiguity: doubling L doubles the extension at a given force, whereas doubling A0​ halves it. For a round specimen A0​=πd2/4; for a rectangular one A0​=wt, using initial diameter d, width w and thickness t. Diameter error matters twice at first order: δA0​/A0​≃2δd/d. Specify material direction, temperature and loading rate when comparing Young's modulus. Lateral restraint changes the stress state and can prevent this experiment from measuring the uniaxial modulus.
The name commemorates Thomas Young, whose formulation of a measure of elasticity is recorded by the Royal Society. This attribution alone does not establish priority for every modern definition or experimental method.[ref]
