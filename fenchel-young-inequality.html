Fenchel–Young inequality (f(x)+f∗(p)≥p⋅x)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Mathematical optimization Convex optimization Convex conjugate
OurBigBook.com Words: 585 Articles: 1
The Fenchel–Young inequality says that every input and every slope obey
f(x)+f∗(p)≥p⋅x,x,p∈Rn,
(56)
where f is any proper extended-real function and f∗ is its convex conjugate. No convexity assumption on f is needed for this inequality: the defining supremum is at least the particular value p⋅x−f(x). If either term on the left is +∞, the inequality still holds, but cannot be an equality because the dot product on the right is finite.
For a proper convex function, equality is equivalent to the subgradient condition
f(x)+f∗(p)=p⋅x⟺p∈∂f(x).
(57)
To see this, with f(x) finite, equality says that x attains the supremum defining f∗(p), so p⋅z−f(z)≤p⋅x−f(x) for every z. Rearranging gives the subgradient inequality f(z)≥f(x)+p⋅(z−x). Geometrically, the affine minorant ℓp​(z)=p⋅z−f∗(p) touches f at x.
If the proper convex function is also closed, meaning its epigraph is a closed set, or equivalently it is lower semicontinuous, then
p∈∂f(x)⟺x∈∂f∗(p).
(58)
The forward implication holds without closedness; the reverse one requires the additional hypothesis in general. Applying the equality criterion to f∗ relates x∈∂f∗(p) to f∗∗(x)+f∗(p)=p⋅x, and the Fenchel-Moreau theorem supplies f∗∗=f when f is closed. See Bertsekas, Convex Optimization Theory: A Summary, §5.4.1, Proposition 5.4.3 for precisely this distinction.
A concrete failure of the reverse implication occurs for the indicator functional of a constraint set f of the open interval (−1,1). Its convex conjugate is f∗(p)=∣p∣: the endpoint gives the supremum, even though it is excluded from the interval. At (x,p)=(1,1), x∈∂f∗(p)={1}, but f(1)=+∞, so equality fails and ∂f(1) is empty. Closing the interval changes the endpoint value and restores equality there.
For the convex quadratic function f(x)=x2/2, the Fenchel–Young inequality is x2/2+p2/2≥px, and equality holds exactly when x=p. For the absolute value f(x)=∣x∣, finite equality requires p=sgn(x) at x=0, where sgn is the sign function; at x=0 every p∈[−1,1] gives equality. These are exactly the subgradients of the absolute value, including its entire interval of slopes at the corner.
