Centrifugal distortion (EJ​/h=BJ(J+1)−D[J(J+1)]2+⋯)

Real chemical bonds can stretch as molecular rotation increases. The resulting larger moment of inertia lowers the energy eigenvalues relative to a rigid rotor. Within one vibrational quantum state, a leading effective correction is
hEJ​​=Bv​J(J+1)−Dv​[J(J+1)]2+⋯,νJ→J+1​=2Bv​(J+1)−4Dv​(J+1)3+⋯.
(8)
Both Bv​ and Dv​ are in frequency units here; v labels the vibrational quantum state. For positive Dv​, the adjacent-line spacing becomes
νJ+1→J+2​−νJ→J+1​=2Bv​−4Dv​(3J2+9J+7),
(9)
so the departures grow with J. Molecular vibration also changes the effective rotational constant: even the lowest vibrational quantum state does not have motionless atomic nuclei.[ref]
The quartic correction is a limited expansion, not a model to extrapolate to arbitrary J. It is small relative to the rigid contribution only while (Dv​/Bv​)J(J+1)≪1; eventually higher terms, changed internal motion, or chemical bond breaking matter. In the synthetic plot, the largest upper-state label is J=5, giving a stipulated correction ratio of 0.03. No universal maximum valid J follows without a particular molecule and precision target.
The quantum numbers also have limits of meaning. Here J describes spin-free rotation. In a molecule with electronic spin, rotation alone is commonly labelled N, while J includes electronic angular momentum; including nuclear spin introduces additional total-angular momentum labels and resolved splittings. An applied electric field can mix different field-free J states through the Stark effect. For identical atomic nuclei, the Pauli exclusion principle can restrict combinations of rotational and nuclear-spin states. The simple 2J+1 degeneracy and every-integer-J ladder therefore describe the stated idealization, not all observed sublevels.[ref]
