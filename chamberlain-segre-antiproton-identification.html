Chamberlain-Segrè antiproton identification

OurBigBook.com Words: 212
In 1955, Owen Chamberlain, Emilio Segrè, Clyde Wiegand, and Thomas Ypsilantis used Bevatron protons on copper, selecting negative secondaries. Magnetic fields selected p=1.19GeV/c; scintillation counters S1 and S2 measured time of flight over 40ft. Cherenkov detector C1 flagged β>0.79; C2 accepted 0.75<β<0.78 after material losses. S3 checked traversal. The cuts rejected negative pions.[ref]
Chamberlain's 1959 account describes C2's quartz radiator, mirrors, and photomultiplier readout. The trigger combined delayed scintillation counter signals with C2; photographed oscilloscope traces exposed fast particles and accidental coincidences through C1 pulses or extra pulses. Reversing the magnetic fields provided a positive-proton control and revealed an alignment error. Changing selected momentum at fixed velocity acceptance tested for a proton-mass peak.[ref]
Terminal-event studies remained unfinished; the 1955 identification did not rely on annihilation.[ref]
The logic is useful far beyond this experiment. A magnetic field separates electric charge signs and magnetic rigidity, but particles of different rest masses can share both. A time of flight signal adds velocity, while a Cherenkov detector independently checks whether that velocity is plausible. An accidental coincidence can imitate the delay of a slow particle without being one particle traversing the apparatus. Agreement between independent measurements is therefore part of the inference, rather than a redundant decoration.
