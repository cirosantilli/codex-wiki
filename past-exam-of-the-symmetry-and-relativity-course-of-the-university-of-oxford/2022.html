Write the Lorentz transformation as x′μ=Λμν​xν. Acting on a scalar field, the chain rule gives the four-gradient
∂μ′​=∂x′μ∂xν​∂ν​=(Λ−1)νμ​∂ν​.
(1)
For the specified Lorentz boost, the inverse is obtained by changing β to −β, so
∂0′​=γ(∂0​+β∂1​),∂1′​=γ(∂1​+β∂0​),∂2′​=∂2​,∂3′​=∂3​.
(2)
These are precisely the covector component rules for the lowered coordinates. Raising the index with the Minkowski metric ημν=diag(−1,1,1,1) reverses the time component, yielding
∂μ​=(c−1∂t​,∂x​,∂y​,∂z​),∂μ=(−c−1∂t​,∂x​,∂y​,∂z​).​
(3)
Indeed ∂′0=γ(∂0−β∂1) and ∂′1=γ(∂1−β∂0), the contravariant four-vector rules. Thus the index on a partial derivative with respect to an upper coordinate is lower.

Take the divergence of the Ampère-Maxwell equation and use that the divergence of a curl is zero:
0=∇⋅(∇×B)=μ0​∇⋅J+μ0​ϵ0​∂t​(∇⋅E)=μ0​(∇⋅J+∂t​ρ).
(6)
The last step uses Gauss's law. Thus the charge continuity equation is exactly
Jμ=(cρ,J),∂μ​Jμ=∂t​ρ+∇⋅J=0.​
(7)
For a direct justification that this four-current is a four-vector, take one locally comoving population of electric charges, with rest charge density ρ0​ and four-velocity Uμ=γ(c,v). Invariance of electric charge and length contraction give ρ=γρ0​ and J=ρv, hence Jμ=ρ0​Uμ. Summing populations preserves the four-vector rule. In particular, cρ′=γ(cρ−βJx​) and Jx′​=γ(Jx​−βcρ). The Lorentz invariance of its four-divergence then explains why charge conservation holds in every inertial frame. A single zero-divergence condition alone would not determine a field's transformation law; the physical charge density and current density provide the additional content.

The Lorenz gauge condition is ∇⋅A=−c−2∂t​ϕ. Substitute the electric potential and magnetic vector potential into Gauss's law:
−∇2ϕ−∂t​(∇⋅A)=ρ/ϵ0​,□ϕ=−ρ/ϵ0​.
(8)
Next the Ampère-Maxwell equation, the curl of the curl identity, and the Lorenz gauge condition give
∇(∇⋅A)−∇2A□A​=μ0​J−c−2∇∂t​ϕ−c−2∂t2​A,=−μ0​J.​
(9)
The gradient terms cancel. Since μ0​ϵ0​c2=1, the time component obeys □(ϕ/c)=−μ0​cρ, so together
∂ν∂ν​Aa=−μ0​Ja,Aμ=(ϕ/c,A).​
(10)
The four-potential can be chosen to transform as a four-vector. To make the argument rigorous, choose the retarded potential with no freely added homogeneous solution. If □Gret​(x)=δ(4)(x), this choice is
Aμ(x)=−μ0​∫Gret​(x−y)Jμ(y)d4y.
(11)
The retarded Green function is a Lorentz scalar under proper orthochronous Lorentz transformations, and d4y is invariant. The four-vector rule for Jμ therefore proves A′μ(x′)=Λμν​Aν(x). More generally, transform any homogeneous initial data by this same rule. Both the wave equation and the Lorenz gauge condition are then preserved.
Qualification. The wave equation by itself cannot prove the transformation rule for every independently chosen gauge potential: the d'Alembert operator has a nontrivial kernel of a linear map. For a concrete counterexample, zero sources and zero fields admit Aμ=0 in one inertial frame and A′μ=(0,C,0,0) in another. Both satisfy the wave equation and the Lorenz gauge condition, but the latter is not the Lorentz transformation of zero. It is a residual Lorenz gauge symmetry, generated by χ=Cx′. Thus the usual assertion is a covariant choice of four-potential, with a consistent gauge transformation and homogeneous data, rather than a uniqueness result from the displayed equation.

Let s=kμxμ​. The Lorenz gauge condition on the plane electromagnetic wave gives
kμ​ϵμ=0.
(12)
The vacuum wave equation gives kμ​kμ=0 for a nonzero amplitude; equivalently k0=ω/c and ∣k∣=ω/c. Use the gauge transformation A′μ=Aμ+∂μχ with

(13)
Because □χ=0, this is a residual Lorenz gauge symmetry and kμ​ϵ′μ=0. For a propagating plane electromagnetic wave, k0=0, so take λ=−ϵ0/k0. Then

Use c=1 and define the total four-momentum Pμ=(E,P)=∑i​(Ei​,pi​). For an inertial frame moving with velocity V relative to the laboratory, the Lorentz boost gives
P∥′​=γV​(P∥​−VE),P⊥′​=P⊥​.
(15)
Choose V parallel to P and require zero total three-momentum. Then
VCM/lab​=EP​=∑i​Ei​∑i​pi​​.​
(16)
This requires E>∣P∣, so that the total four-momentum is timelike. Its invariant mass is E2−∣P∣2​, the total relativistic energy in the center-of-momentum frame. If all particles are exactly parallel photons, the total four-momentum is null and no finite-velocity center-of-momentum frame exists.
Sign qualification. The minus sign printed in the source describes the laboratory's velocity in the center-of-momentum frame, or the parameter of an active Lorentz boost with a plus-sign spatial rule. It is not the velocity of the center-of-momentum frame relative to the laboratory under question 1's passive convention. For example, a single particle with (E,Px​)=(5,3) has its rest frame moving at Vx​=3/5. The printed Vx​=−3/5 would give Px′​=(5/4)(3+3)=15/2, which is not zero. This explicitly resolves the sign rather than assigning a rest frame moving away from the particle.

In the center-of-momentum frame, four-momentum conservation gives two opposite photon momenta, each of energy Mπ​/2. Let z=cosθ∗​, with the axis along the Lorentz boost from that frame to the laboratory. Reversing the axis specified in the source changes z to −z and has no effect on the following product. The Lorentz transformation of four-momentum gives
E1​=2γMπ​​(1+βz),E2​=2γMπ​​(1−βz).
(19)
Writing s=sin(A/2), b now implies
s2=γ2(1−β2z2)1​,∣z∣=βγsγ2s2−1​​.
(20)
The isotropy assumption is dN=a∣dz∣ on −1≤z≤1. Each interior opening angle has two inverse branches, z and −z, both of which must enter the change of variables for probability densities. The positive branch has derivative
​dAdz​​=2βγsin2(A/2)γ2sin2(A/2)−1​cos(A/2)​.
(21)
Consequently the diphoton opening-angle distribution is
dAdN​=γa​βsin2(A/2)γ2sin2(A/2)−1​cos(A/2)​,f(γ)=γ−1.​
(22)
Its support is 2arcsin(1/γ)<A<π for 0<β<1, and its integral is 2a, the integral of the original angular probability density before normalization. Thus the normalized probability density is (2a)−1dN/dA. Omitting one inverse branch would incorrectly halve the total count.

From c, sin(A/2)≥1/γ, with equality when z=0: the photons are emitted perpendicular to the Lorentz boost axis in the center-of-momentum frame. Therefore
Amin​=2arcsin(γ−1),Apeak​=Amin​.​
(23)
At the minimum, the map z↦A is stationary: writing A=Amin​+δ, its leading behavior is δ=βz2/γ+O(z4). A nonzero interval of uniformly distributed z is therefore compressed near Amin​, producing an integrable inverse-square-root singularity in the opening-angle probability density. No differentiation of that probability density is needed. For finite γ>1, the probability density vanishes at A=π. For γ=1 the center-of-momentum frame is the laboratory and every event has A=π: the probability distribution becomes a Dirac delta distribution, rather than the singular β=0 substitution in c. At large γ, Amin​∼2/γ in radians.

−c2dτ2=−c2dt2+dx⋅dx=−c2dt2(1−∣u∣2/c2).
(24)

We use Uα for the source's uα to distinguish four-velocity from velocity u. Since proper time is invariant and dxα is a four-vector, Uα is a four-vector, with Uα​Uα=−c2. The rest-frame result Uα=(c,0) follows immediately.

Let v1​=(c/2,0,0) and v2​=(c/(22​),c/(22​),0) in A. Each has Lorentz factor g=2/3​. The relative Lorentz factor of S2​ in the rest frame of S1​ follows from their four-velocity tensor contraction:
Γ=−c2U1​⋅U2​​=g2(1−c2v1​⋅v2​​)=68−2​​.
(31)
Let b=1−Γ−2​ be the relative speed divided by c. In the rest frame of S1​, take θ between the photon's propagation direction and S2​'s velocity. The observed angular frequency is the invariant ω=−k⋅U. Applying it to the emitter, whose rest angular frequency is ω0​, gives ω0​=Γω1​(1−bcosθ). Since ω1​=ω0​,
cosθ=b1−Γ−1​=Γ+1Γ−1​​=14−2​2−2​​​,θ≃77.54∘.​
(32)
This is a relativistic Doppler effect calculation using both emitters' and receivers' rest clocks; the time delay of reception introduces no extra factor for constant velocities.
For the direction in A, write the wave four-vector as kμ=(ωA​/c)(1,n), where n is a unit vector. Equality of the emitted and received angular frequencies gives k⋅(U1​−U2​)=0. Since the two laboratory Lorentz factors are equal, their time components cancel:
n⋅(v1​−v2​)=0,ny​=(2​−1)nx​.
(33)
Thus the complete answer in three spatial dimensions is
n=cosψ(cos(π/8),sin(π/8),0)+sinψ(0,0,1),0≤ψ<2π.​
(34)
If propagation is additionally assumed to lie in the stars' motion plane, this reduces to either direction along the internal angle bisector, 22.5∘ or 202.5∘ from +x. Reflection exchanging the stars' laboratory velocities explains the angle bisector by symmetry. The source does not specify coplanar propagation or the stars' positions, so it does not fix the sign or exclude an out-of-plane ray. For example n=(0,0,1) gives ω1​=ω2​=gωA​ and satisfies the same no-shift condition. Initial positions can be chosen to make such a ray connect emission and reception. All these possibilities give the same rest-frame θ above.

For constant laboratory force F and initial rest, integration gives three-momentum p=Ft. The energy–momentum relation gives E=m2c4+c2∣F∣2t2​, and v=c2p/E, so
v(t)=1+∣F∣2t2/(m2c2)​Ft/m​.​

Both the muon and electron have the same electric charge magnitude. At equal kinetic energy K per particle, their Lorentz factors are γ=1+K/(mc2), so the heavier muon has the smaller Lorentz factor. At equal total relativistic energy the same ordering follows from γ=E/(mc2), provided that energy is large enough to create either particle.
If the equal-acceleration comparison of c is retained, Pcircular​=Cq​a2γ4≤Cq​a2γ6=Plinear​. Thus the four-way instantaneous comparison selects
a muon in a circular accelerator, at fixed energy and coordinate acceleration.​
(43)
For positive K, its smaller Lorentz factor reduces the power relative to an electron, and transverse acceleration reduces the power relative to longitudinal acceleration of the same magnitude. This conclusion follows from the constraint actually stated in c.
For the customary engineering comparison at equal applied force, the result changes. From c,
Plinear​=6πϵ0​m2c3q2F2​,Pcircular​=γ2Plinear​.
(44)
Under that comparison the choice is
a muon in a linear accelerator, at fixed energy and applied force.​
(45)
Its longitudinal radiative power is smaller than an electron's by (me​/mμ​)2, while avoiding bending removes the extra γ2 factor. At a fixed bend radius R, the circular loss is Cq​γ4v4/R2, giving the familiar m−4 suppression at fixed ultrarelativistic energy and R.
An energy budget alone does not specify a unique optimization. The force, acceleration, bend radius, acceleration time, and length are otherwise unrestricted. For example, in a linear accelerator a constant small force F gives radiative power proportional to F2, and reaching a specified final three-momentum takes time proportional to F−1; the total radiated energy therefore tends to zero as F→0. One cannot infer a unique accelerator choice without an additional constraint. The standard practical preference is the second boxed answer, with its stated comparison. Muon decay introduces a separate lifetime constraint if slow acceleration is contemplated, but it was not part of the specified radiative-loss calculation.
