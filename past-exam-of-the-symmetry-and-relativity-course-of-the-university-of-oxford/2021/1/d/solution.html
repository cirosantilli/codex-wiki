Each incoming particle must acquire about 80.378489GeV of kinetic energy, travelling about 16.08km. Equality is the ideal kinematic threshold; a nonzero production rate needs energy above it. This estimate neglects acceleration losses and treats the W bosons with the supplied nominal rest mass.
A linear particle accelerator avoids repeatedly bending high-energy electrons and Positrons. This greatly reduces the bending synchrotron radiation loss, which at fixed bending radius grows approximately as the fourth power of the relativistic energy for a given particle species. Its useful collision energy can therefore be increased without the severe circular electron collider radiation penalty. The price is long structures or high accelerating gradients: each bunch normally passes once, requiring substantial beam power, fresh bunches, and very tight focusing for high luminosity. A circular particle accelerator reuses its accelerating structures and circulates bunches through many collisions, but needs a large ring and substantial radiofrequency power to replace synchrotron radiation losses. Heavier particles such as protons radiate far less at a given energy and radius, although a proton's constituent structure makes the hard-collision energy less sharply specified than in an electron-positron annihilation experiment.
