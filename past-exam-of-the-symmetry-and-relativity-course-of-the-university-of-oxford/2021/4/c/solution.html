The commutator measures the leading difference between performing two infinitesimal rotations in opposite orders. For example, with X=−iαJ1​ and Y=−iβJ2​,
eXeYe−Xe−Y=I+[X,Y]+O(α2β,αβ2)=I−iαβJ3​+O(α2β,αβ2).

The failure to commute is itself an infinitesimal rotation about the perpendicular axis; the Lie algebra closes on the same three generators of rotations.
To pass from this local statement to the full group property, put Ak​=−iJk​. These are real antisymmetric matrices, with [Ai​,Aj​]=ϵijk​Ak​. The Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula shows locally that the logarithm of a product of their exponentials remains a linear combination of the Ak​, because all nested commutators do. Globally, each exponential has RTR=I and detR=etrA=1. If R1​,R2​ have these properties, then
(R1​R2​)T(R1​R2​)=R2T​R2​=I,det(R1​R2​)=1.
(47)
Every real three-dimensional orthogonal matrix of determinant +1 has an eigenvector with eigenvalue +1: its nonreal eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs, and the product of all three is +1. On the perpendicular plane it acts as a two-dimensional rotation. It therefore has axis-angle form and can be written e−iθn^kJk​. Thus the product is again a rotation. The identity is θ=0, the inverse reverses θ, and associativity comes from matrix multiplication. This proves that all these rotations form the special orthogonal group SO(3). The commutator supplies local closure, while the orthogonality and axis-angle argument supplies the necessary global step.
