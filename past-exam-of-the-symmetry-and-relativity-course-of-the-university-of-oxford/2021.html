Let e>0 be the magnitude of the electron's electric charge, m=me​, F=eE>0, and ℓ=mc2/F. The Lorentz force is dpx​/dt=qE, so the electron and Positron acquire opposite momenta:
pe,x​=−Ft,pp,x​=Ft,Ee​=Ep​=m2c4+F2c2t2​.
(1)
Using the relativistic energy-momentum relation, ux​=pc2/E, and integrating from rest gives the world lines
xe​(t)=−ℓ[1+(ct/ℓ)2​−1],xp​(t)=d+ℓ[1+(ct/ℓ)2​−1](t≥0).​
(2)
For t<0, the world lines are vertical at x=0,d on a spacetime diagram. They join smoothly to the accelerated branches with zero initial velocity. The asymptotes are lightlike, while every finite-time tangent is timelike.

Write Γ=(1−v2/c2)−1/2 for the Lorentz factor of the inertial frame S′, distinguishing it from the particles' changing Lorentz factors. Apply the Lorentz transformation
x′=Γ(x−vt),t′=Γ(t−vx/c2)
(3)
to each world line in a. Both particles initially have velocity −v in S′. The switching events have coordinates
(te′​,xe′​)=(0,0),(tp′​,xp′​)=(−Γvd/c2,Γd).
(4)
Thus the Positron starts accelerating earlier in S′. Its velocity first becomes less negative, vanishes when its laboratory velocity reaches v, and then becomes positive. The electron continues at −v until t′=0, then accelerates toward −c. The relativistic velocity-addition formula makes this precise:
ue′​=1−vue​/c2ue​−v​,up′​=1−vup​/c2up​−v​.
(5)
The electric field parallel to the Lorentz boost has unchanged magnitude, but its switching surface is t′+vx′/c2=0, rather than t′=0 everywhere.
In the laboratory, the total three-momentum is zero for all t: the two Lorentz forces cancel at equal laboratory times. In S′, each active force is still qE, since this is one-dimensional motion with a parallel electric field. Define tp′​=−Γvd/c2. For v>0 the total three-momentum, evaluated at equal t′, is
Px′​(t′)=⎩⎨⎧​−2Γmv,−2Γmv+F(t′−tp′​),−2Γmv+ΓFvd/c2,​t′<tp′​,tp′​≤t′<0,t′≥0.​​
(6)
This follows by integrating dpp′​/dt′=F from its switching event and dpe′​/dt′=−F from its later switching event. During the interval between them only the Positron feels the prescribed electric field, so momentum conservation for the particle pair fails. Once both forces act, the total three-momentum is constant again.
The equal-time momentum of an externally driven particle pair illustrates a distinction. The sum of particle four-momenta at a fixed pair of events is always a four-vector. However, a fresh sum at equal time in each inertial frame generally uses different events. Here transforming an equal-t laboratory sum would give −2Γvm2c4+F2c2t2​/c2, which varies with t; this differs from the equal-t′ answer above. For example, with c=m=F=d=1 and v=0.6, the late-time equal-t′ sum is −0.75, whereas transforming the equal-t=0 sum gives −1.5. This is a concrete failure of treating the pair's equal-time sums as components of a single frame-independent four-vector.
A closed system's total four-momentum, including the electromagnetic field and the apparatus supplying it, is conserved and independent of the choice of spacelike integration surface under the usual isolated-system boundary conditions. The particles alone exchange energy and momentum with an external system, so neither that independence nor universal particle-only momentum conservation is guaranteed.

With the reversed electric field, reflection about x=d/2 exchanges the electron and Positron. They therefore meet there with equal and opposite momenta. Each travels d/2, and the work-energy theorem gives its kinetic energy:
K=eE2d​=e(5.0×106Vm−1)(0.100m),Ke​=Kp​=0.500MeV.​
(7)
Their combined kinetic energy is 1.000MeV, while each total relativistic energy is E=0.511+0.500=1.011MeV. The laboratory is the center-of-momentum frame of the colliding pair.
Local four-momentum conservation in the electron-positron annihilation excludes one real photon: the incoming total four-momentum is timelike, with zero spatial component and positive energy, while a single photon's four-momentum is null and has ∣p∣=E/c>0. Two photons can balance their momenta by travelling in opposite directions, so
Nmin​=2,Eγ​=1.011MeV,λ=Eγ​hc​≃1.226×10−12m.​
(8)
The equal photon energies follow from opposite equal momenta, not merely from sharing the incoming energy arbitrarily. Here hc≃1.23984MeVpm. This is the free local annihilation idealization implicit in the question; significant momentum transfer to the external apparatus would change the kinematic system being considered.

The laboratory remains the center-of-momentum frame. At the relativistic collision mass threshold, the two final W bosons can have arbitrarily small opposite momenta. The available relativistic energy is 2me​c2+eEd, so
2me​c2+eEd≥2mW​c2,dmin​=eE2(mW​−me​)c2​=5.0×106eVm−1160.756978×109eV​≃3.21514×104m=32.15km.​
(9)
Each incoming particle must acquire about 80.378489GeV of kinetic energy, travelling about 16.08km. Equality is the ideal kinematic threshold; a nonzero production rate needs energy above it. This estimate neglects acceleration losses and treats the W bosons with the supplied nominal rest mass.
A linear particle accelerator avoids repeatedly bending high-energy electrons and Positrons. This greatly reduces the bending synchrotron radiation loss, which at fixed bending radius grows approximately as the fourth power of the relativistic energy for a given particle species. Its useful collision energy can therefore be increased without the severe circular electron collider radiation penalty. The price is long structures or high accelerating gradients: each bunch normally passes once, requiring substantial beam power, fresh bunches, and very tight focusing for high luminosity. A circular particle accelerator reuses its accelerating structures and circulates bunches through many collisions, but needs a large ring and substantial radiofrequency power to replace synchrotron radiation losses. Heavier particles such as protons radiate far less at a given energy and radius, although a proton's constituent structure makes the hard-collision energy less sharply specified than in an electron-positron annihilation experiment.

For an ultrarelativistic particle, relativistic beaming concentrates the synchrotron radiation within an angle of order 1/γ around its instantaneous velocity. A fixed viewing direction in the orbital plane receives an intense signal only when the tangent sweeps through that direction. One electron, or one sufficiently short bunch, therefore produces one pulse per revolution, separated by T≃10.48μs. Several bunches produce the corresponding additional pulses per revolution.
The time during which the velocity points within that cone is of order r/(γc), but this is the emission interval. If ta​ is the arrival time at a distant detector,
ta​=t+cD−n⋅x(t)​,dtdta​​=1−n⋅β.
(18)
Near alignment, 1−βcosθ≃(γ−2+θ2)/2=O(γ−2). Thus synchrotron pulse compression reduces the observed duration by a further factor of order γ2:
Δta,single​∼γ3cr​≃8.3×10−18s.​
(19)
A more explicit geometric estimate chooses the cone edges θ=±1/γ. With ω=u/r, x=rsinθ along the line of sight, the difference in arrival-time compression for a moving source is
Δta​=c2r​[βγ1​−sin(γ1​)]≃3cγ34r​≃1.10×10−17s.
(20)
The order-one coefficient depends on how pulse width is defined; the robust scale is r/(cγ3). The usual central-cone estimate r/(2cγ3) gives 4.1×10−18s. These estimates agree on the attosecond scale and must not be confused with r/(γc)≃2.84×10−10s.[ref]
Bunch qualification. The source says only that the bunches are short, without their longitudinal length Lb​. Different electrons pass the same emitting tangent at times separated by up to about Lb​/u, with essentially the same path length from that tangent to the detector. The bunch-length broadening of synchrotron radiation means that the measured bunch envelope is the single-electron pulse convolved with the bunch's passage-time profile; its scale is generally at least of order max{Lb​/u,r/(cγ3)}, with additional detector bandwidth effects. For example, a 1mm bunch, very short compared with this ring, gives an approximately 3.3ps envelope, much longer than the single-electron duration. No unique numerical bunch-envelope width follows from the supplied data. The pulse argument also presumes that the accepted +x direction is tangent to the orbit; a direction far outside the orbital plane receives no comparable intense forward pulse.

A symmetry is a transformation of a physical system that preserves its laws: it maps admissible solutions and consistently transformed data to admissible solutions. The central symmetry of special relativity is Lorentz invariance, expressing equivalence of inertial frames and preservation of the Minkowski interval and the universal speed of light. With spacetime translations included, the spacetime symmetry group is the Poincaré group.
A practical sufficient check is to express an equation as an equality between Lorentz tensors of the same type, with every repeated upper/lower pair correctly contracted and the same free indices on both sides. Every ingredient, including sources and background fields, must have its stated Lorentz transformation rule. For example, pμpμ​=−m2c2 is a Lorentz scalar equality, and dpμ/dτ=qFμν​Uν equates four-vectors. Such manifest Lorentz covariance makes the form of the equation the same in every inertial frame.
This is a sufficient test when the transformation rules are genuine physical ones, not a guarantee from writing index symbols. A fixed preferred four-vector could make an equation formally covariant if it too were transformed, while its chosen physical background breaks Lorentz invariance. Conversely, an equation written in three-dimensional components can be fully consistent with special relativity, as the Lorentz force is, even when the covariance is not manifest.

To pass from this local statement to the full group property, put Ak​=−iJk​. These are real antisymmetric matrices, with [Ai​,Aj​]=ϵijk​Ak​. The Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula shows locally that the logarithm of a product of their exponentials remains a linear combination of the Ak​, because all nested commutators do. Globally, each exponential has RTR=I and detR=etrA=1. If R1​,R2​ have these properties, then
(R1​R2​)T(R1​R2​)=R2T​R2​=I,det(R1​R2​)=1.
(47)
Every real three-dimensional orthogonal matrix of determinant +1 has an eigenvector with eigenvalue +1: its nonreal eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs, and the product of all three is +1. On the perpendicular plane it acts as a two-dimensional rotation. It therefore has axis-angle form and can be written e−iθn^kJk​. Thus the product is again a rotation. The identity is θ=0, the inverse reverses θ, and associativity comes from matrix multiplication. This proves that all these rotations form the special orthogonal group SO(3). The commutator supplies local closure, while the orthogonality and axis-angle argument supplies the necessary global step.

Let Ki​ generate Lorentz boosts with the same exponential convention as the rotation generators of rotations Ji​. The Lie algebra of the Lorentz group is
[Ji​,Jj​]=iϵijk​Jk​,[Ji​,Kj​]=iϵijk​Kk​,[Ki​,Kj​]=−iϵijk​Jk​.​
(48)
Consequently non-collinear Lorentz boosts do not close among themselves: the product contains a rotation, the Wigner rotation. For an explicit counterexample, in coordinates (ct,x,y) write ca​=cosha, sa​=sinha and similarly for b. Then
By​(b)Bx​(a)=​cb​ca​−sa​−sb​ca​​−cb​sa​ca​sb​sa​​−sb​0cb​​​.
(49)
A pure rotationless Lorentz boost has a symmetric matrix, whereas this product has spatial entries (xy)=0 and (yx)=sa​sb​=0 for a,b=0. It is therefore a concrete product of two pure Lorentz boosts that is not a pure Lorentz boost. Collinear Lorentz boosts are the special case that do form a one-parameter group, with additive rapidities.
Allowing all spatial directions and closure generates both the Ki​ and, through their commutators, all Ji​. The smallest connected group containing all these Lorentz boosts is the proper orthochronous Lorentz group
SO+(1,3)={Λ:ΛTgΛ=g, detΛ=+1, Λ00​≥1}.​
(50)
It contains arbitrary compositions of spatial rotations and Lorentz boosts continuously connected to the identity. It has six continuous parameters. Here “smallest” refers to the subgroup generated by all continuous pure Lorentz boosts, not to an arbitrary collection already including discrete reversals.
The full Lorentz group O(1,3) has four connected components, distinguished by the sign of the determinant and the direction of time. Representatives are the identity, parity P=diag(1,−1,−1,−1), time reversal T=diag(−1,1,1,1), and PT=−I. Thus
O(1,3)=SO+(1,3) ∪ PSO+(1,3) ∪ TSO+(1,3) ∪ PTSO+(1,3).
(51)
Multiplication by these discrete transformations relates the components algebraically; no continuous path within the Lorentz group joins different components. Translations are not required for closure here; adding them gives the larger Poincaré group.
