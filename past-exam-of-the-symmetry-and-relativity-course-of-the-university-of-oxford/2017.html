a OurBigBook.com
For a massive particle on a timelike world line, the four-velocity is Uμ=dXμ/dτ=γ(c,v), with U2=c2. Its four-momentum is Pμ=mUμ=(E/c,p) and its four-force is Fμ=dPμ/dτ. Here τ is proper time. A massless trajectory does not admit this massive-particle proper-time parametrization.
The asserted classification of arbitrary orthogonal vectors is false. With the chosen Minkowski metric, D=(0,1,0,0) and G=(0,0,1,0) are nonzero, orthogonal and both spacelike vectors. Also D=(1,1,0,0) and G=2D are orthogonal null vectors. The valid statement is that a nonzero vector orthogonal to a timelike vector is spacelike. To prove it, choose the rest frame of the timelike vector, D=(d,0,0,0); then D⋅G=dG0=0 forces G0=0, so G2=−∣G∣2<0.
The relativistic energy-momentum relation gives
P⋅P=c2E2​−∣p∣2=m2c2.
(1)
Therefore P2=0 ⟹ m=0​. A photon is an example: E=c∣p∣. A collection of photons need not itself have zero invariant mass.

Along a massive particle's world line, proper time satisfies dτ=dt1−v2/c2​. A pure force preserves its rest mass. Differentiating P=mU and U2=c2 gives
F⋅U=dτdm​U2+mdτdU​⋅U=c2dτdm​,
(2)
so F⋅U=0​ for a pure force. This is a restriction on rest-mass transfer, not a statement that its work in every frame vanishes.
Write a=dv/dt. Since γ˙​=γ3v⋅a/c2, the four-acceleration is
Aμ=(cγ4v⋅a​,γ2a+c2γ4v⋅a​v).
(3)
If v⋅a=0, this reduces to A=(0,γ2a), whence
AμAμ​=−γ4a2,α2≡−A2=γ4a2.​
(4)
Here α is the proper acceleration. The positive expression printed in the source is A2 in the opposite metric signature, or the squared acceleration magnitude in ours. For example, at rest with nonzero acceleration, A=(0,a) has A2=−a2 in our convention. Thus one must not combine the printed positive sign with a mostly-minus norm.

There is no nonzero, literally constant four-force that is a pure force throughout this motion. Initially U=(c,0,0,0), so purity forces F0=0. If the full four-vector were constant, U(τ)=U(0)+Fτ/M0​; its square would be c2+F2τ2/M02​, which cannot remain c2 for a nonzero initially spatial F. This is a genuine ambiguity in the source wording.
The usual consistent interpretation is constant laboratory relativistic force f=dpx​/dt, equivalently constant one-dimensional proper acceleration f/M0​. Take f>0 and let s=ft/(M0​c). Integrating momentum conservation with the applied impulse gives px​=ft, so
Uμ(t)=c(1+s2​,s,0,0),β(t)=1+s2​s​,γ(t)=1+s2​.​
(5)
The corresponding four-force has components Fμ=γf(β,1,0,0), which are not constant, although its invariant magnitude is F2=−f2. Its temporal component is indeed F0=(γ/c)dE/dt. The speed rises from zero and approaches c asymptotically; γ starts at one, initially rises quadratically and becomes linear in t at late times.
If instead only the spatial four-force component Fx=fx​ is held constant, the distinct consistent answer is most simply parametrized by u=fx​τ/(M0​c):
Uμ=c(1+u2​,u,0,0),M0​cfx​t​=21​[u1+u2​+arsinhu],F0=1+u2​fx​u​.​
(6)
This follows by integrating dUx/dτ=fx​/M0​ and dt/dτ=U0/c. The temporal component must still vary. These two interpretations must not be conflated with a constant four-vector.

The equal and opposite incoming momenta make the laboratory the centre-of-mass frame. The initial total four-momentum is timelike. One outgoing photon would have null four-momentum, so an isolated two-body annihilation cannot produce exactly one photon. Two photons are kinematically possible and must have opposite momenta and equal energies:
Nγ,min​=2,Eγ​=2Ktot​+2me​c2​=500.511MeV.​
(7)
The quoted 0.511MeV is the rest energy me​c2, not a mass in SI units. The energy balance includes both rest energies as well as the 1GeV total kinetic energy.
This minimum concerns the complete final state. A detector with incomplete acceptance could register only one of the two photons, or none; conservation laws do not impose a minimum detected count without an ideal-detection assumption. Additional outgoing photons are also possible.

For a vacuum electromagnetic wave, the four-wavevector is Kμ=(ω/c,k) with ∣k∣=ω/c and K2=0. Its phase can be written
φ=Kμ​Xμ+φ0​=ωt−k⋅x+φ0​.
(9)

For a hypothetical free emission e→e+γ, four-momentum conservation gives P=P′+Q. The initial and final electrons have P2=P′2=me2​c2, and Q2=0. Squaring gives P′⋅Q=0. In the final electron's rest frame, however, P′⋅Q=me​Eγ​>0 for a nonzero future-directed photon. Thus
an isolated on-shell electron cannot emit a real photon in vacuum.​
(10)
An external field, another body or a medium can exchange momentum and change this conclusion; those are different systems.

For the electric-dominated crossed-field orbit, choose a boost V=c2B/E along x. Since ∣E∣>c∣B∣, it is subluminal. The Lorentz transformation of electromagnetic fields gives Bz′​=0 and Ey′​=E/Γ, where Γ=(1−V2/c2)−1/2. The initially stationary electron has U′(0)=(Γc,−ΓV,0,0), not zero velocity in this frame.
Let q=−e, a=qEy′​/me​ and s=aτ/c. Neglect radiation reaction, as in the prescribed external-field model. Integrating the relativistic Lorentz force in this purely electric frame gives the exact trajectory
t′=aΓc​sinhs,x′=−ΓVτ,y′=aΓc2​(coshs−1),z′=0.​
(16)
Indeed U′0=Γccoshs, U′x=−ΓV, and U′y=Γcsinhs satisfy both the force equation and U′2=c2. The constant transverse momentum must be retained.
The inverse boost gives a useful exact laboratory parametrization as well:
t=Γ2[ac​sinhs−c2V2​τ],x=Γ2V[ac​sinhs−τ],y=y′.​
(17)
For E,B>0, the lab electron starts accelerating toward negative y and bends toward positive x, the direction of E×B. It never acquires a cyclotron orbit in this electric-dominated case. At late times, vx​→V and vy​→sgn(qE′)c/Γ, so its speed approaches c along an oblique asymptote. Reversing field signs reverses the appropriate directions. The zero-B limit is the ordinary electric-field hyperbolic motion.

x′=−ΩV​sin(Ωt′),y′=ΩV​[1−cos(Ωt′)],z′=0.​

Three relevant observable effects are the relativistic Doppler effect in spectral-line shifts, including the transverse time-dilation contribution; relativistic aberration, observable as a change of apparent light direction with observer motion; and relativistic beaming of moving emitters, associated with strongly asymmetric jet brightness. Apparent superluminal motion supplies a related light-travel-time test: vapp​/c=βsinϑ/(1−βcosϑ) can exceed one without a material speed exceeding c. These interpretations require an emission and source-motion model; a cosmological or gravitational redshift by itself is not a direct test of special-relativistic kinematics.
For Compton scattering from an initially stationary electron, let incoming and outgoing photon energies be ε and ε′ and their mutual angle be ψ. Four-momentum conservation gives P+Q=P′+Q′. Squaring P′=P+Q−Q′ and using both electron mass shells and both photon null norms gives
2P⋅(Q−Q′)=2Q⋅Q′,me​(ε−ε′)=c2εε′​(1−cosψ).
(22)
Consequently the outgoing angular frequency and shift are
ω′=1+me​c2ℏω​(1−cosψ)ω​,Δω=−ω1+me​c2ℏω​(1−cosψ)me​c2ℏω​(1−cosψ)​.​
(23)
Equivalently, the Compton wavelength shift is λ′−λ=h(1−cosψ)/(me​c). The electron recoil is essential; treating its energy as unchanged would suppress the effect.

The asserted necessity of equal masses is false. Equal unsigned outgoing angles imply equal longitudinal momenta when the transverse momenta cancel; they do not imply equal final speeds or energies for unequal masses.
An explicit counterexample uses c=1, masses M1​=m and M2​=2m, initial projectile energy E1​=2m, and a stationary target. The initial total energy is 4m and momentum is (3​m,0,0). Choose final four-momenta
P1′​=m(813​,23​​,857​​,0),P2′​=m(819​,23​​,−857​​,0).​
(27)
They sum to the incoming four-momentum. Their squared norms are respectively m2 and 4m2, so the collision is elastic. Both trajectories make the same acute angle with positive x:
tanθ1​=tanθ2​=419​​,M1​=M2​.​
(28)
Thus no proof of the printed claim is possible.
The general centre-of-mass frame construction shows why. Let p∗ be the outgoing momentum magnitude there, θ∗ the projectile scattering angle, and E1∗​,E2∗​ the two energies. After a boost βCM​ along x, the equal-angle condition for two forward nonzero trajectories is
p∗cosθ∗+βCM​E1∗​=−p∗cosθ∗+βCM​E2∗​.
(29)
With E1​ the initial projectile energy, its solution is
cosθ∗=2M2​(E1​+M2​)M22​−M12​​(c=1),​
(30)
provided the right side lies between minus and plus one and both final longitudinal momenta are positive. Equal masses are one allowed case, giving θ∗=π/2; they are not necessary. For the counterexample the right side is 3/16. Even equal masses do not force equal angles for every scattering direction: the symmetric outgoing configuration is an additional choice.

Take the stated static vector potential with zero scalar potential. In the laboratory,
E=0,B=∇×A=(A0​ku​sin(ku​z),0,0).
(31)
Let S′ move along z at the electron's reference velocity v. The inverse transformation is z=γ(z′+vt′). The Lorentz transformation of electromagnetic fields gives
Bx′​=γA0​ku​sin[γku​(z′+vt′)],Ey′​=γvA0​ku​sin[γku​(z′+vt′)],​

with all other components zero. The sign follows from v×B=vBx​ey​.

(cω′​)2−(kz′​)2=−ku2​<0.​

Fμν=Ccosψ​00ω/c0​0000​−ω/c00−k​00k0​​.​

∂t​ρ+∇⋅j=0,∂μ​Jμ=∂μJμ​=0.​

For two identical wires, their lab interaction is attractive, with magnitude μ0​I2/(2πd) per length. The total four-current is spacelike, J2=−jx2​<0, so no inertial frame makes it a pure charge density. Also B2−E2/c2=B2>0 forbids a frame in which the exterior field is purely electric. For an arbitrary boost along the wires, the electrical part is repulsive and the magnetic part attractive:
L′FE′​​=+c2Γ2u2​2πdμ0​I2​,L′FB′​​=−Γ22πdμ0​I2​.
(45)
The latter always dominates, and their sum remains −μ0​I2/(2πd).
Even a more general boost cannot make the entire-wire force electrical alone. At the second wire choose y=d,z=0, so B=Bez​ and j=jex​ with jB>0. The physical Lorentz force density is f=(0,0,−jB,0). If the boosted magnetic force vanished, j′×B′=0, then E′⋅B′=0 would also give j′⋅E′=0. Thus f′0=0, which from the boost of f requires Vy​=0. But for such a boost f′y=−jB<0, whereas ρ′Ey′​=ΓV2​Vx2​jB/c2≥0. The electrical term alone cannot equal the actual force. Therefore there is no such frame for both complete wires, although the force on electrons considered separately can be electrical in their rest frame.

Let ε=Eph​ and γ=W/(M0​c2). For a head-on photon and particle collision, boost to the particle's initial rest frame. The incident photon energy is ε∗​=γ(1+β)ε. On the inverse-Compton branch ε<γβM0​c2, the maximal outgoing lab energy corresponds to rest-frame backscattering into the particle's forward direction. The exact Compton scattering recoil formula gives ε∗′​=ε∗​/[1+2ε∗​/(M0​c2)]. Boosting the backscattered photon back to the lab gives
εmax′​=1+2γ(1+β)ε/(M0​c2)γ2(1+β)2ε​.​
(46)
For γ≫1 this becomes
εmax′​≃1+4γε/(M0​c2)4γ2ε​.​
(47)
Only in the additional Thomson scattering limit 4γε≪M0​c2 may the denominator be neglected, yielding the printed estimate 4[W/(M0​c2)]2Eph​. Ultrarelativistic motion alone does not justify it. For example γ=1000 and ε=M0​c2 would give 4×106M0​c2 from that estimate, exceeding the available total energy; the recoil-corrected maximum is about 999.75M0​c2.
For an arbitrarily hard incident photon, maximizing over direction needs a further qualification: if ε≥γβM0​c2, the largest outgoing photon energy is the unchanged ε, rather than this amplified backscattering branch. The exact photon energy gain is Δεmax​=εmax′​−ε. Calling the outgoing energy the gain is accurate only when amplification is large. These distinctions prevent the inverse Compton scattering estimate from violating conservation of energy outside its low-rest-frame-photon-energy regime.
