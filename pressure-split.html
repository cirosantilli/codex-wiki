Pressure (P=F⊥​/A)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics
OurBigBook.com Words: 7k Articles: 65
Pressure measures the compressive normal force per unit surface area. For a uniformly loaded surface of surface area A, the magnitude of the perpendicular force is F⊥​=PA; locally, P=dF⊥​/dA. Its SI unit is the pascal, 1Pa=1Nm−2. A force parallel to the surface contributes shear stress, rather than pressure. In a gas, the steady macroscopic force comes from many microscopic transfers of momentum; ideal gas pressure from wall collisions makes this connection explicit. [ref]
For a computed example, a uniform pressure of 100kPa on a piston of surface area 10cm2=10−3m2 produces a perpendicular force of 100N. If the opposite face is exposed to 80kPa, the net force due to the two pressures is (100−80)×103×10−3=20N, directed toward the lower-pressure side. The pressure on either face and their difference answer different questions; gauge pressure records a difference from a specified reference, often atmospheric pressure.
A scalar pressure describes a gas in local thermodynamic equilibrium with isotropic random particle velocities: every wall orientation has the same normal force per unit surface area. Without this isotropy, normal stresses can depend on direction and a pressure tensor is needed. Thus random motion alone is insufficient; a preferred direction matters.
In thermodynamics, pressure and volume are thermodynamic conjugate variables. For a simple compressible system, reversible expansion delivers work δW=PdV, and at fixed entropy S and particle number N,
P=−(∂V∂U​)S,N​,
(1)
where U is internal energy. A large pressure therefore means both a large wall force per unit surface area and a large amount of expansion work per small increase in volume.
