Expectation value (⟨A⟩ψ​=⟨ψ∣A∣ψ⟩)

 ... Physics Branches of physics Quantum mechanics Quantum system Quantum state Observable
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 3
The expectation value of a quantum observable is the expected value of its measurement outcome random variable in a specified quantum state. It predicts the mean of outcomes from many identical quantum state preparations. It need not equal any one possible outcome: for a spin one-half particle, equal probabilities of +ℏ/2 and −ℏ/2 give mean zero even though zero is never an outcome of that spin component. Here ℏ is the reduced Planck constant.
Let A=A† be a Hermitian operator on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, and let ∣ψ⟩ be a normalized pure state, ⟨ψ∣ψ⟩=1. The spectral theorem writes
A=∑r​ar​Pr​,Pr​Ps​=δrs​Pr​,∑r​Pr​=I.
(70)
The ar​ are distinct real eigenvalues, Pr​ are orthogonal projections onto their eigenspaces, and I is the identity operator. For an ideal projective measurement of A, the Born rule and the expectation value give
pr​=⟨ψ∣Pr​∣ψ⟩=∥Pr​∣ψ⟩∥2,∑r​pr​=1,⟨A⟩ψ​=∑r​ar​pr​=⟨ψ∣A∣ψ⟩.
(71)
Thus the expectation value is a probability-weighted average of eigenvalues, including all vectors in a degenerate eigenspace in its Pr​. In an orthonormal eigenbasis, ∣ψ⟩=∑j​cj​∣j⟩, this is ∑j​aj​∣cj​∣2, where repeated aj​ are allowed. The weighted average lies between the smallest and largest eigenvalues; it can lie in a gap between them. The statistical meaning and operator expression are connected in this way in the authored treatment of average energies.[ref]
If a nonzero vector ∣v⟩ has not been normalized, use ⟨v∣A∣v⟩/⟨v∣v⟩; the denominator is essential. A global phase cancels between the bra and ket, so ∣ψ⟩ and eiγ∣ψ⟩ have the same expectation value and outcome probabilities.
The outcome variance measures the spread about this mean, whereas the quantum uncertainty is its standard deviation:
Varψ​(A)=∑r​pr​(ar​−⟨A⟩ψ​)2=⟨A2⟩ψ​−⟨A⟩ψ2​=(ΔA)2.
(72)
For a pure state, zero variance means that ∣ψ⟩ lies entirely in one eigenspace. That eigenspace need not be one-dimensional. Knowing only a mean generally does not determine the entire outcome probability distribution.
For a mixed state, the same formulas use its density matrix ρ, a positive operator with trace one:
pr​=Tr(ρPr​),⟨A⟩ρ​=Tr(ρA),Varρ​(A)=Tr(ρA2)−Tr(ρA)2.
(73)
A pure state is the special case ρ=∣ψ⟩⟨ψ∣. For a quantum state ensemble ρ=∑k​wk​ρk​, the expectation value is ∑k​wk​⟨A⟩ρk​​. This linearity is useful when preparations vary between trials; different quantum state ensembles can have the same density matrix.
These formulas require no operator-domain qualifications in finite dimension. For an unbounded self-adjoint operator, the spectral theorem instead gives a spectral probability measure; a finite mean requires an integrable absolute first moment, and finite variance requires a finite second moment. Writing ⟨ψ∣A∣ψ⟩ as an operator inner product additionally requires ∣ψ⟩ in the operator domain of A. The quadratic form of a positive quantum Hamiltonian explains the useful wider form-domain version.
