Latent heat of fusion (L)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 2
An ice bath can absorb heat while its temperature barely changes: the heat converts ice into water. The latent heat of fusion quantifies this melting budget. For a pure substance at its equilibrium melting temperature Tm​(p) and specified pressure p, define it here per unit mass:
L(p)=hl​(Tm​(p),p)−hs​(Tm​(p),p),h=H/m.
(11)
Here hl​ and hs​ are the specific enthalpies of the coexisting bulk liquid and solid, and H is enthalpy. For ordinary melting, L>0, in Jkg−1; an amount m has a total fusion enthalpy mL in joules. A molar fusion enthalpy instead has units Jmol−1 and equals ML, where M=m/n is mass divided by amount of substance in kgmol−1. Always check the normalization before inserting a tabulated value into an energy balance.[ref] [ref]
For a closed system converting completely from solid to liquid at fixed pressure, with only pressure-volume work and negligible changes in bulk kinetic energy and potential energy, the first law of thermodynamics gives Qp​=ΔH=mL. Both endpoints are at Tm​(p): warming initially colder ice or subsequently warming the resulting water requires additional sensible heat. Since H=U+pV, the corresponding specific internal energy jump is Δu=L−pΔv, where v=V/m; L includes the work associated with the change in volume. Heat is transferred energy, whereas enthalpy is a state function. [ref]
Let x=ml​/m be the liquid phase fraction by mass, rather than by volume. Neglecting interfacial contributions to enthalpy, equilibrium phase coexistence at fixed pressure gives
h=(1−x)hs​+xhl​=hs​+xL,Qp​=mL(x2​−x1​),T=Tm​(p).
(12)
In the model above, reaching Tm​ takes 2.10kJ of sensible heat; the plateau then absorbs 33.40kJ, ending at a cumulative 35.50kJ. At 18.80kJ, half the mass is liquid and the temperature is still Tm​. The final warming takes another 4.18kJ. The rounded L and cl​ are inspired by the introductory ice-melting calorimetry values in Case Western's demonstration; constant properties and exact equilibrium throughout are stipulated model assumptions. [ref]
Within one thermodynamic phase, specific heat capacity at constant pressure is cp​=(∂h/∂T)p​, in Jkg−1K−1; a sample's heat capacity at constant pressure is Cp​=mcp​, in JK−1. The plateau is a finite enthalpy change at zero temperature change through varying x, so neither a finite single-phase cp​ nor Q=mcp​ΔT represents it. The heating curve needs separate sensible heat intervals and a fusion contribution. [ref]
The plateau assumes a pure substance, a stable bulk solid-liquid phase coexistence pair, fixed pressure, and sufficiently slow, uniform heating to remain near thermodynamic equilibrium. The total mass and composition must remain fixed, with negligible evaporation and no other phase transition. A fast heater can produce temperature gradients, so a local thermometer need not read the equilibrium Tm​. Neither Tm​ nor L is universal across pressures or compositions: common-pressure melting-point slope describes the pressure dependence of Tm​, while mixtures can melt over a temperature range as their coexisting compositions change. Their liquidus must be consulted instead of assuming a pure-substance plateau. [ref]
This makes ice useful as a temperature buffer: under these conditions it can absorb ms​L before the remaining solid mass ms​ disappears, after which the water warms. If a stipulated constant net input power P goes entirely into melting, that buffer lasts ms​L/P. Reversing the equilibrium conversion releases the same fusion enthalpy during freezing. The beautiful thermodynamic connection is L=Tm​(sl​−ss​), with s the specific entropy and Tm​ absolute temperature: equal Gibbs free energies at phase coexistence allow different enthalpies and entropies. The nearly stationary thermometer hides a finite, measurable change of state. [ref]
