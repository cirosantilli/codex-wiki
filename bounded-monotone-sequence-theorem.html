Bounded monotone sequence theorem

 ... Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Sequence and series Sequence Monotone sequence
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 5
A monotone sequence of real numbers has a finite limit of a sequence exactly when it is a bounded sequence. In the increasing case, only an upper bound needs to be supplied, because the first term already gives a lower bound; in the decreasing case, only a lower bound needs to be supplied. The limit of a sequence is the exact edge of the set of terms:
an​≤an+1​≤M⟹an​⟶L=sup{an​:n≥1},
(59)
m≤bn+1​≤bn​⟹bn​⟶L=inf{bn​:n≥1}.
(60)
The upper bound M or lower bound m need not itself be the limit of a sequence. A supremum or infimum identifies the sharp bound. [ref]
For example, an​=1−1/n is increasing and bounded above by one, and bn​=1+1/n is decreasing and bounded below by one. Both have limit of a sequence one, although neither ever equals one. Their absolute errors are exactly 1/n. They also bracket any sequence xn​ satisfying an​≤xn​≤bn​: the squeeze theorem gives xn​→1, even if (xn​) itself is not a monotone sequence.
The theorem is useful when a recurrence relation gives successive improvements and a bound, before the answer has been identified. It proves existence of a limit of a sequence; further algebra, often using continuity, identifies the answer. A finite plot illustrates the formulas but does not establish the infinite claim. Error bounds for bounded monotone sequences require additional quantitative information.
A real increasing sequence bounded above converges to its supremum; a decreasing sequence bounded below converges to its infimum. For the increasing case, the definition of supremum provides a term above L−ε, and every later term remains between that term and L. Negation reduces the decreasing case to the increasing case. This elementary result is distinct from the measure-theoretic monotone convergence theorem for integrals.
The existence of the real supremum in this short proof uses the least-upper-bound property, equivalently completeness of the real numbers. The approximation property of a supremum supplies aN​>L−ε, and monotonicity keeps every later term within the same tolerance:
n≥N⟹0≤L−an​≤L−aN​<ε.
(61)
The corresponding decreasing statement follows from the approximation property of an infimum. The converse uses the fact that every convergent sequence is a bounded sequence. [ref]
