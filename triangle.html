Triangle (△ABC)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Geometry and topology Euclidean geometry
OurBigBook.com Words: 784 Articles: 8
A triangle is the convex hull of three noncollinear points: the three vertices must not lie on one line. Its boundary consists of the three joining line segments; depending on context, "triangle" can mean this boundary or the filled region it encloses. Here a triangle's area refers to the filled region. The noncollinearity assumption excludes a collapsed shape with zero area, for which ordinary interior angles are not defined.
For example, the positions (0,0), (4,0) and (0,3) form a right triangle with a right angle at the origin. Taking the side of length 4 as its base gives perpendicular height 3, so its area is 4⋅3/2=6. The height is measured at a right angle to the chosen base; it is not generally the length of another side. In this example it happens to be a side because the triangle is a right triangle.
By contrast, side lengths 3,4,6 define a valid triangle: each side is shorter than the sum of the other two, as required by the strict triangle inequality. But 32+42=25=36=62, so this is not a right triangle and the Pythagorean theorem cannot calculate its longest side from the other two. The law of cosines instead gives the opposite interior angle C through
cosC=2⋅3⋅432+42−62​=−2411​,C≈117.28∘.
(73)
This example separates the definition of a triangle from the additional assumption of a right angle.
Every triangle in the Euclidean plane has interior angles summing to π radians, a result proved in Euclid's Elements I.32.[ref] It lets two measured angles determine the third, and underlies the angle-angle criterion for similar triangles. The qualification "Euclidean plane" matters: the earlier spherical triangle demonstrates a different sum.
