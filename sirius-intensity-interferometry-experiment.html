Sirius intensity interferometry experiment

 ... Physics Branches of physics Quantum theory Quantum optics Hanbury Brown and Twiss effect Stellar intensity interferometry
OurBigBook.com Words: 142
In their 1956 Sirius report, Robert Hanbury Brown and Richard Q. Twiss used two 1.56-m searchlight reflectors feeding photomultipliers. The photocurrent fluctuations passed through 5–45-MHz amplifiers; an adjustable delay aligned arrival times. A linear mixer multiplied the outputs; an integrating motor accumulated their product. A second motor monitored its root-mean-square fluctuation to control gain changes. Separate lamps illuminated capped photomultipliers before and after observations to test electronic coupling and drift; blank-sky runs tested correlated sky background. They fitted a uniformly bright disk and an unknown zero-baseline scale to relative correlations at different interferometric baselines. Broadband response and possible baseline-dependent systematic errors limited the diameter inference.[ref]
This astronomical test extends the preceding laboratory Hanbury Brown and Twiss effect to two independently collected samples of a star. The historical motor reading was an instrument-specific correlation statistic. It should not be identified numerically with the normalized g(2) below without the corresponding measurement calibration.
