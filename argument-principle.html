Argument principle (N−P=wind(f∘γ,0))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Complex analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 12
The argument principle turns a boundary journey into an interior count: follow the values of a meromorphic function around a contour, and count their net turns around zero. Every enclosed zero of a function contributes its multiplicity of a zero; every enclosed pole subtracts its order. There is no need to find each zero of a function separately.
Let γ be a piecewise smooth simple closed curve with positive orientation of a contour. Suppose f is a meromorphic function on an open neighborhood of the curve and its interior, with no zeros of a function or poles on γ. Then
N−P=2πi1​∮γ​f(z)f′(z)​dz=wind(f∘γ,0)​.
(167)
Here N is the sum of the zero multiplicities inside γ, and P is the sum of the orders of its enclosed poles. The image contour f∘γ may cross itself or revisit a point. Its signed winding number, rather than the number of visually bounded regions, is the quantity that counts.[ref]
For the polynomial f(z)=z3 and the unit circle γ(t)=eit, 0≤t≤2π, the image is e3it. It goes around zero three times although its drawn outline is just one circle. The interior contains one distinct zero of a function, at zero, with multiplicity of a zero three: the count is three. Reversing the orientation of a contour reverses the winding number and the sign of the contour integral.
An argument must be followed continuously along the journey. Subtracting the principal arguments at the endpoints loses whole turns: a closed image contour has the same endpoint value even when its winding number is nonzero. Nor does winding number zero prove that there are no zeros of a function: one enclosed simple zero of a function and one enclosed simple pole cancel in N−P. Known poles must be added back to recover N.
The mechanism is short. Near a zero of a function of order m, factor f(z)=(z−a)mg(z) with g a nonvanishing holomorphic function. Its logarithmic derivative is f′/f=m/(z−a)+g′/g, so its residue is m. A pole of order m gives residue −m. The residue theorem adds those contributions. Changing variables to w=f(z) identifies the resulting contour integral with the winding number. This is a local algebraic count joined to a global geometric count.[ref]
The usefulness goes beyond explicit polynomials. Rouché's theorem keeps a count unchanged when a boundary perturbation cannot push the image contour through zero. The Nyquist stability criterion applies the same counting idea to poles governing a system under feedback control.[ref]
