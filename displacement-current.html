Displacement current (JD​=ϵ0​∂t​E)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Electromagnetism Maxwell equations Ampère's circuital law
OurBigBook.com Words: 207
In vacuum, displacement current is the term JD​=ϵ0​∂t​E in the Ampère-Maxwell equation. It has the units of current density, but need not describe electric charge crossing the region. In Maxwell equations in matter, the corresponding term is ∂t​D, with D the electric displacement field.
Consider a slowly charging parallel-plate capacitor with vacuum gap, plate area A, and charge Q(t) on its positive plate. Neglect fringing and assume the charging timescale is long compared with the light-travel time across the apparatus. The nearly uniform gap electric field has magnitude E=Q/(ϵ0​A), so the displacement current through the gap is
ID​=ϵ0​AdtdE​=dtdQ​=I.
(213)
For A=0.010m2 and charging electric current I=1.0mA, this gives dE/dt≃1.13×1010Vm−1s−1 and JD​=0.10A/m2. No conducting electric current crosses the gap. A spanning surface cutting the wire and another passing between the plates nevertheless give the same magnetic circulation when the full Ampère-Maxwell equation is used. [ref].
The local consistency check is short. Without displacement current, taking the divergence of ∇×B=μ0​J would force ∇⋅J=0, allowing no local accumulation of electric charge. With displacement current, the divergence of a curl is zero and Gauss's law give
0=μ0​(∇⋅J+ϵ0​∂t​∇⋅E)=μ0​(∇⋅J+∂t​ρ).
(214)
This is precisely the charge continuity equation. Electric charge conservation fixes the required divergence of the added term; it does not by itself exclude other divergence-free additions. The specific Maxwell equations also yield the electromagnetic wave equation, linking this consistency correction to propagation at the speed of light.
