Finite propagation speed

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Partial differential equation Wave equation
OurBigBook.com Words: 472 Articles: 5
For the unforced, constant-c one-dimensional wave equation, finite propagation speed says that changing Cauchy data outside [x∗​−ct∗​,x∗​+ct∗​] cannot change the solution at (x∗​,t∗​), where c>0 and t∗​≥0. This interval is the initial-data domain of dependence. Its endpoints are found by following the two characteristic lines for the one-dimensional wave equation backwards from the observation. D'Alembert's formula makes the dependence sharper: initial displacement is sampled at the endpoints, while initial velocity is integrated over the entire interval.[ref]
For the synthetic choice c=1, (x∗​,t∗​)=(0.5,1.5) has initial-data domain of dependence [−1,2]. A change confined near x=3 is irrelevant to that observation, however large it is. If both Cauchy data vanish outside [−a,a], their compact support can influence only [−a−ct,a+ct] at t≥0. Thus an observer at x>a sees exactly zero displacement for 0≤t<(x−a)/c. There is no nonzero precursor beyond this causal cone for the constant-speed wave equation in the ideal model. This is an exact statement about the partial differential equation, stronger than an experimental failure to detect a small signal.
Support widening for the wave equation is a bound on possible influence, not a claim that every point inside the expanding interval moves. A bump function released with zero initial velocity splits into two separated travelling waves; the middle can become empty. An initial kick can instead leave an interior tail, as in the D'Alembert formula with initial velocity. Consequently finite propagation speed does not imply the Strong Huygens principle. A finite string needs boundary conditions, and the whole-line formula applies locally only until its backward causal cone for the constant-speed wave equation encounters an endpoint.
