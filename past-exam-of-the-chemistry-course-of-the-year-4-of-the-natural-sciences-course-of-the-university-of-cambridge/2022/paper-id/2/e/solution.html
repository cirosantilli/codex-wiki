Lower winter temperatures favour formation and persistence of polar stratospheric clouds, providing particle surfaces on which reservoir chlorine can be activated, for example
HCl+ClONO2​⟶Cl2​+HNO3​,HCl+HOCl⟶Cl2​+H2​O.
(14)
These are heterogeneous surface processes; cooling changes particle abundance, adsorption, phase and reaction uptake, so a simple gas-phase Arrhenius argument is insufficient. Removal of nitric acid in sedimenting particles can also delay conversion of active chlorine back into chlorine nitrate. The relevant condensation thresholds depend on pressure and water/nitric-acid abundances, rather than a single universal temperature.
Once sunlight is present, Cl2​+hν→2Cl provides active chlorine. In the chlorine monoxide dimer cycle,
2(Cl+O3​ClO+ClO+MClOOCl+hνClOO+M​⟶ClO+O2​),⇌ClOOCl+M,⟶Cl+ClOO,⟶Cl+O2​+M.​
(15)
The net reaction is 2O3​→3O2​, with chlorine regenerated. Low temperature favours formation of the ClOOCl dimer and suppresses its thermal dissociation, making photodissociation rather than thermal return of two ClO radicals more competitive. Laboratory flow experiments formed the dimer at 220–240 K and distinguished its infrared and ultraviolet signatures, supporting the chemical identity needed for this mechanism.[ref]
Colder polar conditions can prepare and sustain greater springtime ozone loss, but darkness alone does not drive the photolytic cycle. In polar night reactive precursors can accumulate; sunlight is required for rapid dimer-cycle ozone destruction. This differs from the middle-stratospheric gas-phase cooling response derived above.[ref]
