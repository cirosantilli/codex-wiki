For a liquid cloud at fixed water content and geometrical thickness, more cloud condensation nuclei can produce more, smaller droplets. The increased total extinction area raises optical depth and reflected sunlight: the Twomey effect gives negative shortwave radiative forcing. It is an indirect aerosol effect mediated by cloud microphysics, rather than direct aerosol scattering.[ref]
Smaller droplets can also delay collision-coalescence and drizzle, potentially increasing liquid-water retention, cloud lifetime or coverage; that adjustment would usually strengthen cooling for low reflective clouds. It is not universal: evaporation and mixing with dry air can reduce water content or cloud cover, and dynamical changes can oppose the simple lifetime argument. Absorbing particles can heat an atmospheric layer, sometimes evaporating cloud and producing a positive semi-direct forcing, but their vertical position can instead stabilize a cloud layer and reverse the response. Aerosols can additionally alter ice nucleation and mixed-phase precipitation, with no single forcing sign independent of cloud type. The net response combines shortwave reflection and longwave thermal effects, and depends on cloud height, optical depth and adjustments. Thus the fixed-water Twomey sign is robust within its assumptions; adjustment and ice-cloud signs require context.[ref]
