Activation as a cloud condensation nucleus depends primarily on dry particle size, soluble composition and hygroscopicity, together with wetting and surface-tension effects. In Köhler theory, droplet curvature raises the equilibrium vapor pressure, while dissolved solute lowers water activity. Each particle therefore has a critical supersaturation; larger or more hygroscopic particles generally activate at a lower value. A size distribution cannot be replaced by an undifferentiated total aerosol count.[ref]
Actual droplet number also depends on the supersaturation attained in the cloud updraught. Adding particles increases competition for condensing water vapor and can lower the peak supersaturation, leaving some particles unactivated. Updraught speed, entrainment and the aerosol size distribution therefore make the number of activated droplets a nonlinear function of the available nuclei. Suitable nuclei need sufficiently low activation thresholds, and additional aerosol does not guarantee one additional droplet per particle.
