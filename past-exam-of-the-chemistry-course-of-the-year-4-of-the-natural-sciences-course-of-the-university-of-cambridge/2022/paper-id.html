i OurBigBook.com
Prefer a replacement that releases little reactive chlorine or bromine into the stratosphere: ideally omit those elements, or give the parent gas a short troposphere removal time compared with transport to the stratosphere. A carbon–hydrogen bond can permit rapid attack by the hydroxyl radical; other readily oxidized functionality can also shorten the atmospheric residence time. The degradation products must themselves avoid transporting long-lived reactive halogen to altitude. Merely retaining fewer chlorine atoms is less effective than preventing their delivery altogether.
Both chlorine and bromine participate in catalytic ozone destruction. Bromine is much more effective per delivered atom, by a factor of roughly 60 in the usual lower-stratospheric comparison; the precise effectiveness depends on chemical partitioning and location. Fluorine is largely trapped in very stable hydrogen fluoride and does not sustain an analogous ozone-destroying cycle. Thus short-lived gases without chlorine or bromine have the smallest direct ozone-depletion potential. Fluorination alone does not ensure a small climate impact.[ref]

A small global warming potential requires both a small greenhouse gas radiative efficiency per unit emitted mass and a short atmospheric lifetime. Weak absorption in the terrestrial atmospheric window is desirable, particularly where absorption by existing gases is weak; strong carbon–fluorine vibrational bands can make even an ozone-safe substitute an efficient greenhouse gas. Rapid removal by lower-atmospheric chemistry limits the integrated burden, provided it does not generate abundant, long-lived radiatively active products. The atmospheric residence time and the absorption spectrum must therefore be assessed separately.

In a vertical-beam, effective-wavelength Beer-Lambert law approximation, the optical depth above altitude z is τ(z)=σO2​​NO2​​(z)+σO3​​NO3​​(z). Using centimetres consistently gives
τ20​=1.50+12.60=14.10,τ40​=0.084+0.340=0.424.
(1)

J(20 km)J(40 km)​=eτ20​−τ40​=e13.676=8.70×105.​
(2)

There is no single first-order atmospheric lifetime for an added carbon dioxide pulse. Air–sea gas exchange and adjustment of the surface mixed layer act on years-to-decades scales; redistribution into the ocean interior takes centuries to millennia. These transfers partition carbon between atmosphere and ocean rather than making the entire added carbon inventory disappear. Dissolution also changes the ocean's carbonate speciation: conversion among dissolved carbon dioxide, bicarbonate and carbonate buffers the uptake, but provides a finite capacity at a given alkalinity. A substantial perturbation therefore remains airborne after the faster exchanges have adjusted.
Further removal involves dissolution of carbonate sediments and carbonate compensation over several thousand years, followed by the much slower silicate-weathering and burial pathway, typically tens to hundreds of thousands of years. The exact fractions and times depend on pulse size and model conditions. A thousand years exceeds the fast exchange times but not the complete ocean–solid-Earth adjustment time, so a residual atmospheric perturbation is expected. An intercomparison found a substantial long tail after ocean equilibration, with further carbonate neutralization on roughly 3–7 kyr scales.[ref]

For a gas whose lifetime is much less than the selected horizon, the numerator of its global warming potential has nearly saturated: ∫0H​ai​mi​(t)dt≃ai​mi​(0)τi​. The carbon-dioxide denominator continues to accumulate because of its long-lived response. Consequently a shorter horizon usually gives a larger GWP for this short-lived substitute; a longer horizon gives a smaller GWP. If the horizon becomes comparable to, or shorter than, the substitute's lifetime, the numerator also depends strongly on the horizon and cannot be replaced by its infinite-time limit.
The horizon is a value choice, and integrated forcing is not the same as temperature at a chosen future date. Equal-GWP pulses can therefore have different near-term warming and long-term temperature paths. The result also depends on greenhouse gas radiative efficiency, background concentrations, lifetime estimates and which indirect chemical effects or feedbacks are included. A pulse-based metric does not by itself describe a continuing emissions trajectory, nor does it measure ozone depletion or local air-pollution impacts. Comparisons should state the horizon, the emission scenario and the chosen climate outcome.[ref]

Take a fixed amount of dry air, with no phase change, moving quasistatically so that its pressure matches the surrounding atmosphere. From the first law of thermodynamics and H=U+pV,
dH=dU+pdV+Vdp=dq+Vdp.
(5)
For adiabatic motion dq=0. Treating the parcel as an ideal gas of fixed composition makes its enthalpy depend only on temperature, so for n moles dH=nCp​dT even though the pressure changes. Hence nCp​dT=Vdp. The environment is in hydrostatic equilibrium, dp/dz=−ρg, and the parcel is taken to have the ambient density at the displacement point. Using ρ=nM/V gives
nCp​dzdT​=Vdzdp​=−nMg,dzdT​=−Cp​Mg​=−cp​g​.​
(6)
Here Cp​ is molar and cp​=Cp​/M is mass-specific heat capacity. Positive z points upward; the commonly quoted positive dry adiabatic lapse rate is Γd​=−dT/dz. Approximate constancy of g and heat capacity gives a constant gradient. Condensation, external heating, entrainment or composition changes invalidate this dry-parcel relation.

Much stratospheric heating is supplied by absorption of solar ultraviolet radiation by oxygen and ozone, whereas carbon dioxide is an important emitter of thermal infrared radiation. In the usual stratospheric balance, increasing carbon dioxide enhances net infrared cooling at the original temperature without supplying a matching increase in the solar heating. Temperature must fall until infrared emission and absorption again balance the heating.
A useful optically thin schematic is Qsolar​=cCO2​​K[B(T)−J], where B(T) represents the relevant thermal source function and J the incident infrared radiation field. With positive fixed solar heating and an approximately fixed radiation field, increasing cCO2​​ lowers the required B(T)−J, hence lowers T. This explains why the surface greenhouse argument cannot simply be applied locally to the stratosphere. Spectral blocking of upwelling radiation contributes too; the real response depends on altitude, ozone heating and radiative transfer, rather than emissivity alone.[ref]

Lower winter temperatures favour formation and persistence of polar stratospheric clouds, providing particle surfaces on which reservoir chlorine can be activated, for example
HCl+ClONO2​⟶Cl2​+HNO3​,HCl+HOCl⟶Cl2​+H2​O.
(14)
These are heterogeneous surface processes; cooling changes particle abundance, adsorption, phase and reaction uptake, so a simple gas-phase Arrhenius argument is insufficient. Removal of nitric acid in sedimenting particles can also delay conversion of active chlorine back into chlorine nitrate. The relevant condensation thresholds depend on pressure and water/nitric-acid abundances, rather than a single universal temperature.
Once sunlight is present, Cl2​+hν→2Cl provides active chlorine. In the chlorine monoxide dimer cycle,
2(Cl+O3​ClO+ClO+MClOOCl+hνClOO+M​⟶ClO+O2​),⇌ClOOCl+M,⟶Cl+ClOO,⟶Cl+O2​+M.​
(15)
The net reaction is 2O3​→3O2​, with chlorine regenerated. Low temperature favours formation of the ClOOCl dimer and suppresses its thermal dissociation, making photodissociation rather than thermal return of two ClO radicals more competitive. Laboratory flow experiments formed the dimer at 220–240 K and distinguished its infrared and ultraviolet signatures, supporting the chemical identity needed for this mechanism.[ref]
Colder polar conditions can prepare and sustain greater springtime ozone loss, but darkness alone does not drive the photolytic cycle. In polar night reactive precursors can accumulate; sunlight is required for rapid dimer-cycle ozone destruction. This differs from the middle-stratospheric gas-phase cooling response derived above.[ref]

Photodissociation of nitrogen dioxide forms an oxygen atom, followed by third-body recombination:
NO2​+hν→NO+O,O+O2​+M→O3​+M.
(16)
If NO is converted back to nitrogen dioxide only by NO+O3​→NO2​+O2​, this is a null cycle with no net ozone production. Oxidation of a volatile organic compound by OH generates organic peroxy radicals, and often hydroperoxyl radicals, which instead oxidize NO without consuming ozone:
RO2​+NO→RO+NO2​,HO2​+NO→OH+NO2​.
(17)
The newly formed nitrogen dioxide can then photolyse, giving net ozone production. These chain reactions require sunlight, radical production and both precursor families.
In an NOx-limited ozone-production regime, NO is too scarce for efficient peroxy-radical conversion; competing peroxide-forming radical termination becomes important. Reducing nitrogen-oxide emissions then reduces ozone production. In a VOC-limited ozone-production regime, plentiful nitrogen oxides remove OH through OH+NO2​+M→HNO3​+M, so organic oxidation and radical supply are limiting. Reducing volatile-organic emissions is effective there, while a modest nitrogen-oxide reduction can initially increase ozone by reducing radical termination and NO titration. Sufficiently large nitrogen-oxide reductions can move the system into the limited regime and eventually lower ozone. The appropriate control therefore depends on the chemical regime and the regional transport history, not merely the largest local emission. The ozone isopleth diagram below uses an explicitly schematic response function to show derivative signs, not a fitted concentration or emissions-control prediction.[ref]

For a liquid cloud at fixed water content and geometrical thickness, more cloud condensation nuclei can produce more, smaller droplets. The increased total extinction area raises optical depth and reflected sunlight: the Twomey effect gives negative shortwave radiative forcing. It is an indirect aerosol effect mediated by cloud microphysics, rather than direct aerosol scattering.[ref]
Smaller droplets can also delay collision-coalescence and drizzle, potentially increasing liquid-water retention, cloud lifetime or coverage; that adjustment would usually strengthen cooling for low reflective clouds. It is not universal: evaporation and mixing with dry air can reduce water content or cloud cover, and dynamical changes can oppose the simple lifetime argument. Absorbing particles can heat an atmospheric layer, sometimes evaporating cloud and producing a positive semi-direct forcing, but their vertical position can instead stabilize a cloud layer and reverse the response. Aerosols can additionally alter ice nucleation and mixed-phase precipitation, with no single forcing sign independent of cloud type. The net response combines shortwave reflection and longwave thermal effects, and depends on cloud height, optical depth and adjustments. Thus the fixed-water Twomey sign is robust within its assumptions; adjustment and ice-cloud signs require context.[ref]

Activation as a cloud condensation nucleus depends primarily on dry particle size, soluble composition and hygroscopicity, together with wetting and surface-tension effects. In Köhler theory, droplet curvature raises the equilibrium vapor pressure, while dissolved solute lowers water activity. Each particle therefore has a critical supersaturation; larger or more hygroscopic particles generally activate at a lower value. A size distribution cannot be replaced by an undifferentiated total aerosol count.[ref]
Actual droplet number also depends on the supersaturation attained in the cloud updraught. Adding particles increases competition for condensing water vapor and can lower the peak supersaturation, leaving some particles unactivated. Updraught speed, entrainment and the aerosol size distribution therefore make the number of activated droplets a nonlinear function of the available nuclei. Suitable nuclei need sufficiently low activation thresholds, and additional aerosol does not guarantee one additional droplet per particle.
