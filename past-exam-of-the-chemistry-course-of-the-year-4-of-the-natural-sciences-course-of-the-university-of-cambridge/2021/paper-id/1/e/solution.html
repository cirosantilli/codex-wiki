For fluorine, the key is chemical sequestration, not an inability of free F to react. Fluorine atoms readily form very stable hydrogen fluoride through reaction with hydrogen-containing gases. Breaking hydrogen fluoride or regenerating a sustained free-fluorine radical population under ordinary stratospheric conditions is difficult, so an efficient analogous catalytic family is not maintained.
For iodine, the traditional approximation is limited delivery to the stratosphere. Many iodine source gases are short lived in the troposphere, and their reactive products are efficiently taken up or removed before slow large-scale ascent. This historically motivated omitting iodine from the major stratospheric chlorine/bromine budget. It does not establish that iodine atoms are intrinsically ineffective catalysts, or that stratospheric iodine is exactly zero.
Indeed, the blanket omission needs qualification even for this exam's date. Aircraft observations published in 2020 detected both IO and particulate iodine in stratospheric air and inferred continuing multiphase recycling and ozone loss. Gas/particle partitioning and measurement sensitivity must be considered together; a small gas-phase concentration alone can miss an active reservoir. Thus the traditional supply argument is the intended contrast with fluorine's stable-HF sink, but zero stratospheric iodine chemistry is not supported by those observations.[ref]
