Information entropy (H(X))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Information theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 5k Articles: 44
For a finite discrete random variable with probability mass function p, its information entropy is the expected value of its information content:
H(X)=−∑x:p(x)>0​p(x)log2​p(x)=E[−log2​p(X)].
(1)
Base-two logarithms give bits; choosing a different base rescales the units. The convention 0log2​0=0 is justified by a limit, so outcomes of zero probability contribute nothing. The definition also applies to countable distributions, but the sum may be infinite.
A fair bit has H=1. A certain outcome has H=0. A Bernoulli random variable that is one with probability 0.1 has H=h2​(0.1)≈0.469 bits, because most of its outcomes are predictable. A rare outcome still has large information content: the outcome of probability 0.1 contributes −log2​(0.1)≈3.322 bits of surprise when it occurs. Entropy is the average surprise, rather than the surprise of each outcome.
For m possible outcomes, 0≤H(X)≤log2​m, with the upper bound attained exactly by the uniform distribution on a finite set. The Gibbs inequality proves this by comparing p with the uniform distribution: D(p∥u)=log2​m−H(X)≥0. Thus increasing predictability reduces the required average description length. The four-symbol prefix code in information theory attains H=7/4 bits exactly; for general probabilities, integer codeword lengths prevent such a perfect match for every individual symbol. Shannon's source coding theorem explains how coding sufficiently long independent blocks approaches the entropy per symbol.
The logarithm is especially natural because independent outcomes have additive surprise: −log2​(pq)=−log2​p−log2​q. Taking expected values gives additive information entropy for independent random variables; for dependent ones, the chain rule for information entropy replaces the second term by conditional entropy. This is why the same quantity can govern both a single observation and a long message.
