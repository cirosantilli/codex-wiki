Foundations of mathematics asks what our objects mean, what we assume about them, and why an argument establishes its conclusion. Set theory gives a common language for collections and functions; mathematical logic makes the structure of statements and deductions precise. A mathematical definition fixes a meaning, an axiom supplies an assumption, and a mathematical proof explains what follows. Separating these roles makes results reusable: once the group axioms are enough for a conclusion, that conclusion applies to every group, regardless of how its members are represented.
The assumptions can change the answer to a familiar calculation. Over the real numbers, x2=x factors as x(x−1)=0, so x is zero or one. The same reasoning holds in any integral domain, because a product can be zero only when a factor is zero. In the ring of integers modulo six, however, 32≡3 and 42≡4(mod6): there are four solutions, represented by 0,1,3,4. The extra solutions are idempotents, and the failed step involves zero divisors. Writing the domain is part of explaining the answer.
This precision also reveals surprising possibilities. A two-dimensional grid of natural numbers is a countable set, while all infinite binary sequences form an uncountable set. The Cantor pairing function supplies a complete enumeration of the first; Cantor's diagonal argument defeats every proposed enumeration of the second. Both arguments are short, yet distinguish two sizes of infinity. The subject's appeal is this ability to turn a vague question into one whose assumptions, construction, and limits can all be inspected.

Foundations of mathematics OurBigBook.com
A specification of the meaning of a mathematical term or notation. A structural definition specifies conditions, such as the group axioms; a definition by a universal property characterizes an object by its arrows. Such a characterization does not by itself prove that the object exists. For example, existence of an exponential object is an additional property of a category.

Shuffle of formal languages (L1​⊕L2​)

Words: 46
Formal language theory OurBigBook.com
The shuffle of two formal languages consists of words whose positions can be partitioned into two subsequences, each preserving its order, with the two resulting words belonging to the respective languages. For regular languages, a product-state nondeterministic finite automaton advances exactly one coordinate for each letter. Common letters allow either choice; the powerset construction proves closure.

Padded convolution of words (u⊗v)

Words: 48
Formal language theory OurBigBook.com
The padded convolution of words reads two finite words over an alphabet in parallel, extending the shorter with a fresh padding symbol # until the longer ends. Its length is max(∣u∣,∣v∣). Padding may only occur as a suffix, and a (#,#) column is excluded. This encodes synchronous two-tape relations as formal languages over a finite paired alphabet.

Recursive language

Concatenation (uv)

Words: 43
String OurBigBook.com
Concatenation joins two strings end to end, retaining the order of their symbols. For example, joining ab and ba gives abba. It has the associative property: joining (uv)w or u(vw) gives the same word over an alphabet. The empty word is its identity element. All finite words over an alphabet with this binary operation form a free monoid.

To convert a context-free grammar to Chomsky normal form, first add a fresh start symbol. Finite-state machine

Words: 2k Articles: 29
Formal language theory OurBigBook.com
A finite-state machine processes inputs using a finite set of automaton states and input-dependent automaton transitions. An automaton state records everything the machine remembers; an automaton transition says how that memory changes on the next symbol. A finite-state machine may also emit outputs. Here the emphasis is on recognizing a formal language: start in a designated automaton state, read a word from an alphabet, and accept exactly when the final automaton state is designated accepting.
Finite memory does not impose a maximum input length. A finite-state machine can read arbitrarily long words, revisiting its automaton states indefinitely. The restriction is that the part already read must be compressed into one of finitely many summaries. For divisibility by three, a word of binary digits can describe an enormous integer, yet its congruence class modulo three is all that the machine needs to retain. Two already-read words giving the same automaton state behave identically under every subsequent continuation in a DFA.
This makes finite-state machines useful whenever past details can be forgotten safely: checking whether a sequence of commands follows an allowed order, or recognizing a pattern with a regular expression. The beautiful design question is which distinctions about the past matter to the future. DFA minimization makes that question precise by merging indistinguishable states of a deterministic finite automaton. Both DFAs and NFAs recognize exactly the regular languages.

Mathematical proof

Words: 1k Articles: 13
Foundations of mathematics OurBigBook.com
A mathematical proof is a finite deductive argument from stated assumptions to a conclusion. It explains why the conclusion follows for every instance covered by its hypotheses. An informal mathematical proof can omit routine detail while making its assumptions and deductions clear; a formal proof expresses them in a specified formal language with specified inference rules. The distinction between assumptions, propositions, and deductions is introduced in Leighton and Rubinfeld, What is a proof?, sections 1–3.
Checking examples can suggest a pattern, but need not establish a universally quantified claim. For example, the polynomial n2+n+41 is a prime number for every integer 0≤n≤39, as direct trial division verifies. At n=40 it equals 1681=412, a composite number. Those first forty successes do not prove the claim for all natural numbers; one counterexample disproves it. The point is not to discard computation: it can find a conjecture, detect a mistake, or verify a finite exhaustive case analysis. The question is whether the cases checked cover the statement being claimed.
For a positive example, the sum of two even integers is even: writing them as 2a and 2b with a,b∈Z gives 2a+2b=2(a+b), and a+b is an integer. This direct argument handles every allowed pair at once. A useful mathematical proof therefore does more than certify an answer: it exposes the mechanism and the exact assumptions that make the answer transferable.

Mathematical induction (P(n0​),P(n)⇒P(n+1))

Words: 1k Articles: 8
Mathematical proof OurBigBook.com
A mathematical induction proof establishes a property P(n) for every integer n≥n0​ by proving its induction base case and its inductive step. If a counterexample existed, the well-ordering principle would give a least one; its predecessor and the inductive implication exclude it. The starting case excludes a counterexample at n0​.
The useful idea is to cover infinitely many cases with two finite pieces of reasoning. The induction base case starts the chain, and the inductive step shows that success at an arbitrary stage propagates to the next. It is especially natural when a construction adds one item at a time: another row of a diagram, another input processed by an algorithm, or another term of a sequence. The loop invariant method is the same propagation idea applied to successive stages of a computation.
For the triangular number Tn​=1+2+⋯+n, the proposed formula is Tn​=n(n+1)/2. At n=1, both sides are one. Assuming the formula for a fixed positive integer n, add the next row:
Tn+1​=Tn​+(n+1)=2n(n+1)​+(n+1)=2(n+1)(n+2)​.
(40)
This is the desired formula at n+1, proving the inductive step. For example, T5​=15 and T6​=15+6=21; these numerical cases illustrate the general step rather than replace it.
The second picture explains where the formula comes from: two copies of the triangular arrangement tile an n by n+1 rectangle. Thus 2Tn​=n(n+1). The mathematical induction argument certifies how the formula continues, while the rearrangement makes its factors visible. Neither picture is restricted to the five rows drawn: both constructions are specified for every positive integer n.

Strong induction (P(n0​),[∀k∈{n0​,…,n}, P(k)]⇒P(n+1))

Words: 764 Articles: 3

Strong induction establishes a proposition P(n) for all integers n≥n0​. Prove P(n0​); then, for each n≥n0​, assume every P(k) with n0​≤k≤n and prove P(n+1). The induction hypothesis includes all earlier cases, so the inductive step can use whichever smaller indices it needs. It cannot use P(n+1) itself.[ref]
This is useful when a construction breaks into smaller pieces of varying sizes. A prime factorization splits a composite number into smaller integer divisors, which need not be one less than the original integer. A recursive procedure can likewise call itself on several smaller inputs. The available cases should match those dependencies. Strong induction lets the mathematical proof follow the construction rather than forcing every step through the immediately preceding size.
Strong induction with four starting cases
Words: 318
Strong induction OurBigBook.com
Consider a chosen packing problem: batches contain either four or seven identical items, with any nonnegative number of batches allowed. Which integer totals can be formed? The claim is that every total of at least eighteen is attainable:
∀n≥18,∃a,b∈N:n=4a+7b.
(41)
Here a,b count batches, so they must be nonnegative integers; the convention N={0,1,2,…} permits omitting either size. Four direct starting cases are
18=4+7+7,19=4+4+4+7,20=4+4+4+4+4,21=7+7+7.
(42)
If n≥21 and every total from 18 through n is attainable, then n−3≥18 is one of the cases available in the induction hypothesis. Add a batch of four to its representation to obtain n+1. Thus the inductive step applies to every new total from 22 onward. Formally, one can start the strong induction at 18 and handle the next three totals directly before using the uniform step. The finite checks and the propagation together establish all totals, not merely those drawn below.
Checking only 18 and repeatedly adding four would reach only totals congruent to 18 in modular arithmetic modulo four. The four induction base cases supply all four residue classes. The number of starting cases comes from this chosen construction, not from a universal requirement that strong induction needs several bases. A different construction can need different starting information.
The threshold is sharp for these batch sizes. If 17=4a+7b, reducing modulo four gives 3b≡1(mod4), so b≡3(mod4). A nonnegative b would therefore be at least three, giving 7b≥21>17, a contradiction. A finite failed search alone would need an explicit bound on a,b; this short modular arithmetic argument supplies a general obstruction.
Strong induction for factorization existence

Every integer n≥2 has a prime factorization. The induction base case 2 is prime. For a larger integer, either it is prime or it is ab, with 2≤a,b<n. The induction hypothesis supplies prime factorizations of both smaller integer divisors; multiplying them gives one for n. This proves existence, while uniqueness requires the additional argument under the fundamental theorem of arithmetic.[ref]
For the chosen number 84, one possible recursive decomposition is
84=6⋅14=(2⋅3)(2⋅7)=22⋅3⋅7.
(43)
The step at 84 needs results for 6 and 14, rather than a result for 83. Each composite number in this factorization tree splits into strictly smaller positive integers, so recursive descent terminates at prime numbers. The tree describes one construction; another choice of integer divisors can give a differently shaped factorization tree.
The same pattern explains the usefulness of strong induction for algorithm correctness. When a recursive procedure returns answers for smaller inputs, the induction hypothesis can justify those answers; the remaining task is to justify how the current call combines them. Strictly decreasing a nonnegative integer size measure establishes termination. Correctness also requires each call's conditions and its combination rule; termination alone does not make an algorithm correct.
