An irreducible spin representation is a nonzero Hilbert space with no proper nonzero closed invariant subspace under all three angular momentum operators. Equivalently, the associated unitary representation of SU(2) cannot be decomposed into two nonzero orthogonal invariant pieces. The irreducible possibilities are labelled by a spin j∈{0,21​,1,23​,…} and have dimension 2j+1.
Choose an orthonormal basis ∣j,ms​⟩, with ms​=−j,−j+1,…,j. The angular momentum commutation relations imply [S2,Si​]=0, [S3​,S±​]=±ℏS±​, [S+​,S−​]=2ℏS3​, and S+†​=S−​. The complete action is
S2∣j,ms​⟩S3​∣j,ms​⟩S±​∣j,ms​⟩​=ℏ2j(j+1)∣j,ms​⟩,=ℏms​∣j,ms​⟩,=ℏ(j∓ms​)(j±ms​+1)​∣j,ms​±1⟩.​​
(1)
In particular, the spin raising operator annihilates ∣j,j⟩ and the spin lowering operator annihilates ∣j,−j⟩. The coefficient follows by taking the squared norm and using S∓​S±​=S2−S32​∓ℏS3​. Thus the ladder operators connect every member of the basis, while terminating at its two endpoints.

On the tensor product of spins j1​ and j2​, the combined angular momentum operator is J=S(1)⊗I+I⊗S(2). The Clebsch-Gordan decomposition is
Hj1​​⊗Hj2​​≅J=∣j1​−j2​∣⨁j1​+j2​​HJ​,​
(2)
where J increases by one and each irreducible spin representation occurs once. The dimensions agree: ∑J​(2J+1)=(2j1​+1)(2j2​+1).
The uncoupled orthonormal basis diagonalizes S3(1)​,S3(2)​; the coupled orthonormal basis diagonalizes J2,J3​. The Clebsch-Gordan coefficients are their change-of-basis entries, with convention
∣J,M⟩=∑m1​,m2​​Cj1​,j2​​(J,M;m1​,m2​)∣j1​,m1​⟩⊗∣j2​,m2​⟩.
(3)
They vanish unless m1​+m2​=M. Since both bases are orthonormal, this change-of-basis matrix obeys unitarity. Overall phases of the coupled basis vectors are fixed by a convention and do not change the energy eigenvalues.

Write ∣↑⟩,∣↓⟩ for the two S3​ eigenstates, of eigenvalues ±ℏ/2. The spin-spin interaction can be evaluated using
S(1)⋅S(2)=S3(1)​S3(2)​+21​(S+(1)​S−(2)​+S−(1)​S+(2)​).
(4)
For ∣β∣≫∣α∣, take H0​=β(S3(1)​−S3(2)​)/ℏ and V=αS(1)⋅S(2)/ℏ2. The unperturbed energy eigenvalues are β,−β,0,0 on ∣↑↓⟩,∣↓↑⟩,∣↑↑⟩,∣↓↓⟩, respectively. For an isolated normalized energy eigenstate ∣n⟩, first-order nondegenerate perturbation theory gives ΔEn(1)​=⟨n∣V∣n⟩. The two antiparallel spins have expectation −α/4, so
E↑↓​=β−4α​+O(α2/∣β∣),E↓↑​=−β−4α​+O(α2/∣β∣).​
(5)
For the zero-energy degenerate eigenspace, degenerate perturbation theory requires diagonalizing the restriction of V. On the ordered basis (∣↑↑⟩,∣↓↓⟩) it is (α/4)I: the ladder operators cannot take one parallel state to the other. Indeed neither parallel state couples to anything else under H, so the remaining two levels are exactly α/4, retaining their twofold quantum degeneracy.
For ∣α∣≫∣β∣, take H0​=αS(1)⋅S(2)/ℏ2 and V=βD, where D=(S3(1)​−S3(2)​)/ℏ. The spin-one-half triplet state basis and spin-one-half singlet state are
∣t+​⟩=∣↑↑⟩,∣t0​⟩=2​∣↑↓⟩+∣↓↑⟩​,∣t−​⟩=∣↓↓⟩,∣s⟩=2​∣↑↓⟩−∣↓↑⟩​.
(6)
The identity S(1)⋅S(2)=(J2−3ℏ2/2)/2 gives unperturbed energy eigenvalues Et(0)​=α/4 and Es(0)​=−3α/4. Direct action gives
D∣t±​⟩=0,D∣t0​⟩=∣s⟩,D∣s⟩=∣t0​⟩.
(7)
Consequently the restriction of V to the entire triplet degenerate eigenspace is zero, so no first-order splitting is missed. The singlet expectation is also zero. In second-order nondegenerate perturbation theory, ΔEn(2)​=∑r=n​∣⟨r∣V∣n⟩∣2/(En(0)​−Er(0)​). Within the triplet, the corresponding second-order effective matrix is β2∣t0​⟩⟨t0​∣/α, already diagonal in this basis. Thus, through second order,
Et±​​=4α​,Et0​​=4α​+αβ2​,Es​=−43α​−αβ2​.​
(8)
The last two expressions have higher corrections beginning at O(β4/∣α∣3), as the exact calculation in part (d) also verifies. For positive α, coupling to the lower singlet raises the triplet's M=0 level and lowers the singlet; the signs are precisely the energy denominators above.

Let X exchange the two equal-spin factors, so X†=X=X−1. For the unperturbed Hamiltonian operator, addition of angular momentum gives
EJ(0)​=2α​[J(J+1)−2j(j+1)],J=0,1,…,2j.
(13)
For nonzero α, different J have distinct energy eigenvalues, while each eigenspace has the 2J+1 values of M. The stated symmetry of the Clebsch-Gordan coefficients gives the exchange parity of two coupled identical spins:
X∣J,M⟩=(−1)2j−J∣J,M⟩.
(14)
However, D=(S3(1)​−S3(2)​)/ℏ changes sign: XDX=−D. For any two basis vectors in the same J eigenspace, insertion of X2=I therefore gives
⟨J,M∣D∣J,M′⟩=(−1)2(2j−J)⟨J,M∣XDX∣J,M′⟩=−⟨J,M∣D∣J,M′⟩=0.
(15)
Thus the entire projected perturbation PJ​βDPJ​ is zero, including its off-diagonal matrix elements. Degenerate perturbation theory now proves that every first-order-in-β energy correction vanishes. This is first-order cancellation for an exchange-odd perturbation; checking only the individual diagonal expectations would not by itself settle a degenerate problem.

For E>0, put k=2mE​ and q=k/ℏ>0. Outside the support of the potential energy, a generalized eigenfunction of the Hamiltonian operator has the plane wave form
ψ(x)={Aeiqx+Be−iqx,Ceiqx+De−iqx,​x<−R0​,x>R0​.​
(16)
Here R0​ denotes the support radius, to distinguish it from the quantum reflection probability R. The momenta are ±k and the velocities are ±k/m. Thus A,D describe incoming scattering channels from the left and right, and B,C describe outgoing scattering channels towards the left and right. These energy eigenfunctions are not square-integrable functions; normalizable incoming wave packets are formed by superposition over energy, and their passage through the interaction region gives the physical quantum scattering experiment.
For incidence from the left, normalize A=1,D=0 and write B=rL​,C=tL​. The quantum reflection amplitude is rL​ and the transmission amplitude is tL​. The ratios of outgoing to incident probability currents define
R=∣rL​∣2,T=∣tL​∣2.​
(17)
No extra velocity ratio occurs because both exterior regions have the same potential energy and mass. The scattering matrix, with a fixed choice of channel order, is
(BC​)=S(q)(AD​),S(q)=(rL​tL​​tR​rR​​).​
(18)
Its first and second columns are the responses to unit incidence from the left and right, respectively. For a reflection-symmetric potential energy, these conventions give rL​=rR​=r and tL​=tR​=t.

For a real potential energy and a stationary state, the probability current is
j(x)=2miℏ​(ψ∗ψ′−ψψ′∗)=mℏ​Im(ψ∗ψ′).
(19)
The time-independent Schrödinger equation gives ψ′′=2m(V−E)ψ/ℏ2, so j′=ℏIm(ψ∗ψ′′)/m=0. In an exterior plane wave superposition, interference terms contribute no current and
jleft​=mk​(∣A∣2−∣B∣2),jright​=mk​(∣C∣2−∣D∣2).
(20)
Consequently ∣A∣2+∣D∣2=∣B∣2+∣C∣2 for every choice of incoming amplitudes. If u=(A,D)T, this says u†(I−S†S)u=0 for every complex vector u. Since I−S†S is Hermitian, vanishing of its quadratic form implies that the matrix itself vanishes. Therefore
S†S=I,R+T=1.​
(21)
The second identity follows by choosing u=(1,0)T. Since S is a square matrix, the first also implies SS†=I, proving unitarity. A real delta potential preserves this proof: continuity of ψ and the real derivative jump make the current continuous across the interaction point.

Continue the transmission amplitude to complex wavenumber q=iκ with κ>0, equivalently complex momentum k=iℏκ. Then E=−ℏ2κ2/(2m)<0. In the left exterior region the A term grows at negative infinity and the B term decays; in the right exterior region the D term grows and the C term decays. A bound state thus requires A=D=0, with nonzero B,C. The transfer matrix relation gives M22​(iκ)=0. Equivalently, a zero of the incoming coefficient is a bound-state pole of t(q), whose numerator is nonzero at q=iκ.
The denominator found in part (c) gives
0=(iκ+iμ)2+μ2e−4aκ=−(κ+μ)2+μ2e−4aκ.
(29)

−κ[1+tanh(κa)]=2μ,−κ[1+coth(κa)]=2μ.

κeven​=−μ(1+e−2aκeven​),κodd​=−μ(1−e−2aκodd​),Eeven,odd​=−2mℏ2κeven,odd2​​.​

For g≥0, the Hamiltonian operator is nonnegative: its quadratic form is ℏ2∫∣ψ′∣2dx/(2m)+g(∣ψ(a)∣2+∣ψ(−a)∣2)≥0. Thus it has no negative-energy bound states. A nonnegative-energy exterior wavefunction cannot be square integrable unless it vanishes identically, so there are no other bound states either.
For g<0, set ν=−mg/ℏ2>0. The even equation is the zero of f(κ)=ν(1+e−2aκ)−κ. Since f(0)=2ν>0, f(κ)→−∞ and f′(κ)=−2aνe−2aκ−1<0, the intermediate value theorem and strict monotonicity give exactly one positive equation root.
The odd equation is the zero of h(κ)=ν(1−e−2aκ)−κ. Here h(0)=0, h′(0)=2aν−1, h′′(κ)=−4a2νe−2aκ<0, and h(κ)→−∞. If 2aν≤1, strict concavity makes h(κ)<0 for every κ>0. If 2aν>1, h initially increases, has exactly one maximum, and then decreases through zero exactly once. This proves the bound-state count of an attractive symmetric double-delta potential:
N(g)=⎩⎨⎧​0,1,2,​g≥0,−2maℏ2​≤g<0,g<−2maℏ2​.​​
(32)
At the threshold g=−ℏ2/(2ma), the odd zero-energy solution is proportional to x inside (−a,a) and to asgnx outside. It is bounded but not a square-integrable function, so it is a zero-energy scattering resonance rather than a second bound state. Each negative-energy bound state is either even or odd by nondegeneracy of one-dimensional bound states; the two branches above exhaust the count.

The WKB approximation fails at a simple classical turning point xt​, because p(xt​)=0 makes its amplitude singular and violates the WKB validity condition. Locally replace V(x)−E by V′(xt​)(x−xt​). For a forbidden region on the right, V′(xt​)>0 and the rescaled coordinate z=[2mV′(xt​)/ℏ2]1/3(x−xt​) changes the time-independent Schrödinger equation into the Airy ordinary differential equation ψzz​−zψ=0. Reversing the coordinate handles a forbidden region on the left.
The two independent Airy functions have asymptotic forms, for z,w>0,
Ai(z)Ai(−w)​∼2π​z−1/4​e−2z3/2/3,∼π​w−1/4​sin(2w3/2/3+π/4),​Bi(z)Bi(−w)​∼π​z−1/4​e2z3/2/3,∼π​w−1/4​cos(2w3/2/3+π/4).​
(36)
Match these to the WKB approximation in an overlap region where the linearization of the potential energy and the large-argument asymptotic expansions both apply. If Kt​=ℏ−1∣∫xt​x​χdx∣ on the forbidden side and st​=ℏ−1∣∫xt​x​pdx∣ on the allowed side, the Airy turning-point connection formula is
χ​B​e−Kt​ ⟷ p​2B​sin(st​+π/4),χ​A​eKt​ ⟷ p​A​cos(st​+π/4).​
(37)
Thus a decaying exponential acquires a factor two in its allowed-side amplitude, whereas a growing exponential does not. The phase convention uses positive integrals measured away from the classical turning point, so it applies at both ends of the well without an extra orientation sign. These are leading semiclassical matching rules for simple, separated classical turning points.

For V(−x)=V(x), the parity operator Pψ(x)=ψ(−x) commutes with the Hamiltonian operator. Also P2=I, so its eigenvalues are ±1 and the energy eigenspaces admit a basis of definite parity. More strongly, nondegeneracy of one-dimensional bound states says that each bound state energy here is nondegenerate: the Wronskian of two solutions at the same energy is constant by the Schrödinger equation, and decay at infinity makes it zero, forcing linear dependence. K0​=ℏ1​∫0x1​​χ(x)dx,ϕ(E)=2K0​=ℏ1​∫−x1​(E)x1​(E)​2m[V(x)−E]​dx.

This is an implicit condition, with all action integrals evaluated at the corrected energy. To convert it into an explicit leading energy correction, let En(0)​ solve θ(En(0)​)=(n+21​)π and define the full classical oscillation period in one well by T(E)=2m∫x1​x2​​dx/p(x). Differentiation of the action integral has no endpoint terms because p(x1​)=p(x2​)=0, and gives
θ′(E)=ℏm​∫x1​x2​​p(x)dx​=2ℏT(E)​>0.
(49)
Consequently, to leading exponentially small order within the WKB approximation,
En,σ​≃En(0)​−σT(En(0)​)ℏ​e−ϕ(En(0)​),En,−​−En,+​≃T(En(0)​)2ℏ​e−ϕ(En(0)​).​
(50)
The even member lies below its odd partner. For an approximately harmonic oscillator well, T≃2π/ω, giving the familiar double-well tunneling splitting ΔE≃(ℏω/π)e−ϕ. The explicit energy formula retains only its leading exponential correction; varying ϕ and θ′ with the shifted energy also contributes at higher orders.
The ground state has an even, everywhere same-sign wavefunction with a peak in each well and a small nonzero value beneath the central potential barrier. Its derivative vanishes at zero. The first excited state is odd, has opposite-sign lobes in the two wells, and has exactly one interior node of a wavefunction, at zero. Both wavefunctions decay outside the wells. In terms of localized normalized wavefunctions φL​,φR​, with overlap s=⟨φL​∣φR​⟩, their qualitative shapes are the lowest parity states of a symmetric double well:
ψground​≃2(1+s)​φL​+φR​​,ψfirst​≃2(1−s)​φR​−φL​​.
(51)
The overall sign of the odd wavefunction is arbitrary. The sketch uses localized Gaussian profiles to show the parity and the central node of a wavefunction, rather than claiming exact energy eigenfunctions for an unspecified potential energy.
