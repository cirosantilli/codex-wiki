Residue theorem (∮Γ​f(z)dz=2πi∑j​Res(f,aj​))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 11
Let Γ be a piecewise smooth contour forming a simple closed curve, positively oriented: travel counterclockwise, with the interior on the left. Suppose f is holomorphic on an open set containing Γ and its interior, except at finitely many isolated singularities a1​,…,am​ strictly inside. Then
∮Γ​f(z)dz=2πi∑j=1m​Res(f,aj​).
(135)
No isolated singularity may lie on Γ: the ordinary contour integral must be defined there. Reversing the direction of the contour reverses the sign of the answer. The theorem applies to essential singularities as well as poles. [ref]
Return to f(z)=1/(z2+1). A counterclockwise circle ∣z∣=2 encloses both simple poles, so
∮∣z∣=2​z2+1dz​=2πi(−2i​+2i​)=0.
(136)
Yet f is nonzero everywhere it is defined. A zero contour integral does not imply a zero function, or even absence of poles: the enclosed residues can cancel. By contrast, the counterclockwise circle ∣z−i∣=1/2 encloses only i, giving
∮∣z−i∣=1/2​z2+1dz​=2πi(−2i​)=π.
(137)
The picture compares these two selections. Changing the shape of a contour while keeping the same enclosed poles leaves the contour integral unchanged, provided the contour deformation stays where f is holomorphic.
This is the practical appeal of the residue theorem: local Laurent coefficients determine a global contour integral. For a rational function, a few simple pole computations can replace a long parametrized calculation. It also makes real improper integrals accessible by treating the real line as part of a closed contour in the complex plane.
To compute I=∫−∞∞​dx/(1+x2), take R>1 and travel along the real line from −R to R, then return along the upper semicircle AR​, parametrized by z=Reit for 0≤t≤π. This contour is positively oriented and encloses the simple pole i only; −i is outside. Hence
∫−RR​1+x2dx​+∫AR​​1+z2dz​=π.
(138)
The added arc must be controlled before taking a limit. On AR​, the reverse triangle inequality gives ∣z2+1∣≥R2−1, and the arc length is πR. The estimation lemma therefore yields
​∫AR​​1+z2dz​​≤R2−1πR​⟶0.
(139)
The denominator grows quadratically while the arc length grows only linearly. This quantitative comparison, rather than a sketch alone, explains why closing the contour does not alter the limiting real improper integral. It even supplies an error bound: the difference between π and the finite real integral is at most πR/(R2−1).
For a numerical check, R=10 gives the finite real integral 2arctan(10)≈2.94226, leaving about 0.19934 to reach π. The arc bound is 10π/99≈0.31733, comfortably larger than that error. The graph shows both quantities approaching zero. The exact tail here can be checked with the antiderivative arctanx; the residue theorem method remains useful for improper integrals where a convenient real antiderivative is unavailable.
Finally, both one-sided improper integrals converge absolutely: for ∣x∣≥1, 0<1/(1+x2)≤1/x2, whose tails are integrable. Thus the symmetric limit computes the ordinary improper integral, not merely a Cauchy principal value, and
∫−∞∞​1+x2dx​=π.
(140)
Closing below instead would enclose the simple pole −i. With the same left-to-right traversal of the real line, that contour is clockwise, so its contour integral is −2πiRes(f,−i)=π again. This provides a useful sign check: the opposite residue is accompanied by the opposite direction around the enclosed region.
The same strategy extends to many other rational functions: choose a contour, identify its enclosed isolated singularities, compute their residues, and bound the added pieces. The choice of contour and the bound are as much part of the calculation as the residues. [ref]
