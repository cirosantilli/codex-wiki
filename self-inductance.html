Self-inductance (Λ=LI,Eind​=−LI˙)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Electromagnetism Inductance
OurBigBook.com Words: 456 Articles: 1
Self-inductance is inductance associated with a coil's own electric current, rather than magnetic flux supplied by another coil. In the linear model, fixed geometry and magnetic response give constant L in Λ=LI. With the turn orientations defined in magnetic flux linkage, Faraday's law of induction gives the self-induced electromotive force
Eind​=−dtdΛ​=−LdtdI​.
(154)
The final equality requires constant L; if the geometry changes, use −dΛ/dt. The minus sign expresses Lenz's law: the induced electromotive force opposes the change in electric current. It does not always oppose the electric current itself. When positive electric current decreases, the induced electromotive force acts in its direction. [ref].
For terminal sign bookkeeping, label the ideal inductor's terminals a,b, choose positive electric current entering a and leaving b, and define vL​ as the terminal electric potential difference drop from a to b in the lumped electrical circuit model. This passive convention gives
vL​=+LdtdI​=−Eind​.
(155)
Thus a coil with self-inductance 0.5H and increasing electric current dI/dt=12A/s has terminal drop vL​=6V and induced electromotive force Eind​=−6V. These signs describe the same process with different reference conventions. The RL circuit uses the terminal-drop convention throughout.
A finite terminal electric potential difference cannot make the electric current of an ideal inductor with constant L>0 jump. Integrating its differential equation gives
I(t2​)−I(t1​)=L1​∫t1​t2​​vL​(t)dt.
(156)
If vL​ stays bounded, the integral tends to zero as the interval shrinks, ensuring continuity of electric current. A step in terminal electric potential difference changes dI/dt immediately, while electric current remains continuous. At constant electric current, the self-induced electromotive force is zero, although the inductor's magnetic energy can remain nonzero.
