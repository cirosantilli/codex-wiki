Hamilton cycle

A Hamilton cycle, also called a Hamiltonian cycle, is a cycle in a graph containing every vertex of the graph. Here the convention is a finite simple graph whose edges are undirected, with n=∣V∣≥3. A traversal is a sequence
v0​,v1​,…,vn−1​,vn​=v0​,
(36)
where v0​,…,vn−1​ are all the distinct vertices and {vi​,vi+1​}∈E for 0≤i<n. Thus "once" means once before the closing return: the start appears again at the end, and there are exactly n traversed edges. The graph may contain additional unused edges.[ref]
A Hamiltonian path visits all vertices once without requiring a return. Removing an edge from a Hamilton cycle gives a Hamiltonian path; a particular Hamiltonian path closes only if its endpoints are adjacent. An Euler circuit instead traverses every edge exactly once and can revisit vertices midway. The two requirements are independent, as these original labelled examples show.
For the complete graph K4​, use V={A,B,C,D} and E={AB,AC,AD,BC,BD,CD}, where AB abbreviates the unordered pair {A,B}. The route A,B,C,D,A is a Hamilton cycle: it visits every vertex and uses four of the six edges, omitting AC and BD. It is not an Euler circuit, and the graph admits no Euler circuit at all: every vertex degree is three, contradicting the Euler circuit criterion. The crossing of the drawn diagonals is not another vertex.
Conversely, take two triangles in a graph sharing only X, with V={X,A,B,C,D} and E={XA,AB,BX,XC,CD,DX}. The route X,A,B,X,C,D,X is an Euler circuit, using all six edges once; the vertex degrees are 4,2,2,2,2. It cannot be a Hamilton cycle, since X occurs midway as well as at the start and end. More strongly, no Hamilton cycle exists: X is a cut vertex, and deleting it leaves the two separate edges AB and CD. Nevertheless, A,B,X,C,D is a Hamiltonian path, whose endpoints are not adjacent.
