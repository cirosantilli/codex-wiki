Capacitance (C)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Electromagnetism
OurBigBook.com Words: 885 Articles: 12
The capacitance of a linear two-terminal capacitor is the constant relating its separated electric charge to its terminal electric potential difference:
Q=CV,C=VQ​(V=0).
(15)
Here Q is the electric charge on one electrical conductor, the other carries −Q, and V is their electric potential difference with the same ordering. Reversing the terminal ordering reverses both Q and V and leaves capacitance unchanged. An ideal passive capacitor has C≥0; an ordinary pair of separated electrical conductors with positive permittivity has C>0. At V=0, use Q=CV rather than the undefined ratio 0/0.
For example, electric charge magnitude Q=0.8854nC at terminal electric potential difference V=10V gives capacitance C=88.54pF. At 20V, the same linear capacitor has Q=1.7708nC and the same capacitance. The plots in capacitor energy show that capacitance is the slope of the electric charge versus terminal electric potential difference graph.
For fixed geometry and linear material response, capacitance depends on dimensions and permittivity, rather than the source's terminal electric potential difference. This is why capacitance is a useful property of a component: once it is known, Q=CV predicts the separated electric charge for each operating terminal electric potential difference. The SI unit is the farad, 1F=1C/V. [ref].
For combinations of capacitors, the same terminal definition gives an equivalent capacitance. Two identical capacitors of capacitance C connected in parallel share the same terminal electric potential difference, and their electric charges add, giving Ceq​=2C. Connected in series with an initially neutral isolated junction, they carry equal plate electric charge magnitudes and their terminal electric potential differences add, giving Ceq​=C/2. [ref].
