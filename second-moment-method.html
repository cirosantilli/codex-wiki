Second moment method

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Probability inequality
OurBigBook.com Words: 463 Articles: 1
The second moment method uses a random variable's expected value and second moment to establish that a counted or measured quantity is positive with substantial probability. It is useful when a positive average alone could be caused by extremely large values on very rare outcomes. Controlling the variance rules out too much of that concentration in rare outcomes.
For a nonnegative random variable N with m=EN>0 and finite second moment, the second-moment bound for positivity gives
P(N>0)≥EN2(EN)2​=m2+Var(N)m2​.​
(10)
Equivalently, P(N=0)≤Var(N)/(m2+Var(N)). The method also includes the simpler Chebyshev's inequality bound P(N=0)≤Var(N)/m2. Thus a sequence of such random variables with Var(N)/(EN)2→0 has N>0 with probability tending to one. These statements require no independence assumption. The variance argument and its use for random counts are developed in Zhao's lecture notes. [ref].
For an original toy count, let N count successes in ten independent Bernoulli trials with success probability p=1/10. The binomial distribution gives
EN=1,Var(N)=9/10,EN2=19/10.
(11)
Consequently P(N>0)≥10/19≈0.526316. The exact probability is 1−(9/10)10≈0.651322. The first moment method and union bound only give the upper bound P(N>0)≤min{1,EN}=1 here. First and second moments therefore answer complementary existence questions.
For a sum of indicator random variables, the variance of a sum is
Var(∑i​Ii​)=∑i​Var(Ii​)+2∑i<j​Cov(Ii​,Ij​).
(12)
Independence makes the covariances vanish, but the formula itself also covers dependent counts. For the same ten success probabilities, let every trial share a single Bernoulli random variable B: then N=10B, with mean one, variance nine, and P(N>0)=1/10. The second-moment lower bound is exactly 1/10. Ignoring the positive covariances would produce the false independent-count conclusion. Zhao's discussion of sums of indicators explains why overlapping counted structures require these pairwise terms. [ref].
