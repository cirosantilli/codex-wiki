Linear algebra

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra
OurBigBook.com Words: 73k Articles: 1k
Linear algebra studies vector spaces and linear maps: operations that preserve vector addition and scalar multiplication. For a linear map T, the defining rule is T(au+bv)=aT(u)+bT(v), where u,v are vectors and a,b belong to the underlying field. The vectors might be arrows, lists of measurements, or polynomials. What matters is that their linear combinations make sense and that the same rule governs their transformation.
A concrete linear map is T(x,y)=(x+y,x−y). Its two outputs record a sum and a difference. Since T(1,0)=(1,1) and T(0,1)=(1,−1), we immediately obtain T(2,1)=2(1,1)+(1,−1)=(3,1). Knowing the outputs of the basis vectors determines the output of every vector; a matrix stores exactly that information in its columns.
The attraction of linear algebra is that one small collection of ideas answers several different questions. A system of linear equations asks which inputs produce a prescribed output. The image of a linear map describes the attainable outputs, while the kernel describes the invisible input changes. A basis gives every vector an address, and change of basis lets us choose addresses that make a linear map easier to understand. This shared language turns a collection of separate calculations into a picture of what is possible and how much freedom remains. Gilbert Strang's MIT course emphasizes these connections and their usefulness across other disciplines.[ref]
