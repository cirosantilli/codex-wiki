Aperture synthesis

 Home Physics Branches of physics Astrophysics Observational astronomy Radio astronomy
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 25
Aperture synthesis reconstructs the sky's brightness from complex visibilities measured at different interferometric baselines. Each measurement constrains a Fourier transform component of the brightness, rather than supplying a little photograph. Combining many such components can distinguish sources that each individual antenna leaves unresolved. The remarkable economy is that changing separations and the rotation of Earth can provide many measurements with only a few physical antennas.
Martin Ryle's Cambridge group made aperture synthesis a practical method of astronomical imaging at the Mullard Radio Astronomy Observatory. Cambridge's institutional history connects that work with the One-Mile Telescope and its larger successors.[ref]
In his 1974 lecture Radio Telescopes of Large Resolving Power, Martin Ryle recalled the problem of distinguishing weak extragalactic sources and resolving their structure. He explained sequential wavefront sampling using one simultaneously observed element as a wave phase reference, and the saving obtained by letting Earth rotate the apparent baseline.[ref]
The 1974 Nobel Prize in Physics distinguished Martin Ryle's observations and inventions, especially aperture synthesis, from Antony Hewish's role in discovering pulsars. The latter is a separate astronomical discovery, with its own observational history.[ref]
