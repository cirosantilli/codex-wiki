Refraction

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 7
Refraction changes the direction of a transmitted wave when it crosses between media with different phase velocities. For light at a planar interface, Snell's law relates the angles measured from the normal vector through the refractive indices. At normal incidence the direction remains unchanged even though the wavelength and phase velocity can change. The worked example under geometrical optics distinguishes transmission from total internal reflection.
For light, refraction explains why a submerged object looks nearer the surface and why an optical lens can form an image. A wavefront crossing a boundary must retain a consistent wave phase along that boundary: its normal spacing changes with phase velocity, and oblique light rays change direction. Normal incidence separates these two effects especially clearly.
Take stationary, transparent, homogeneous media with isotropy, linearity of their optical response, and real positive refractive indices, at one chosen frequency f. With c the vacuum speed of light and λ0​=c/f the vacuum wavelength, medium j has
nj​=vphase,j​c​,vphase,j​=fλj​,λj​=nj​λ0​​,kj​=λj​2π​=cnj​ω​,ω=2πf.

The frequency is unchanged at a stationary boundary; the phase velocity, wavelength and wavenumber can change. Here nj​ is a phase refractive index, not the group refractive index. If there is optical dispersion, evaluate nj​ at this same frequency on both sides.[ref]
For an original idealized air–water example, stipulate na​=1 and nw​=4/3, with vacuum wavelength 600nm. Then f=c/(600nm)≈4.99654×1014Hz, the water phase velocity is 3c/4, and its wavelength is 450nm. At an air angle of incidence 30∘, Snell's law gives a water angle of refraction arcsin(3/8)≈22.0243∘. These stipulated values illustrate the relations; actual refractive indices depend on material conditions and frequency. By reversibility of an optical ray, the same path emerging from water has outgoing air angle 30∘.
