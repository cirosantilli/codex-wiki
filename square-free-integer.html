Square-free integer

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Number theory Integer
OurBigBook.com Words: 437
A positive integer n is square-free when no square number greater than one is a divisor of n. It suffices to exclude squares of prime numbers; equivalently, every prime valuation is zero or one:
n is square-free⟺p2∤n for every prime p⟺vp​(n)∈{0,1} for every prime p.
(116)
Thus the prime factorization of a square-free integer is a product of distinct prime factors, with no repetitions. One is included: its prime factorization is an empty product and all its prime valuations are zero. These equivalent tests are stated in Grinberg's notes. [ref]
For signed integers, extend the definition by requiring ∣n∣ to be a square-free integer, where ∣n∣ denotes absolute value. Hence −30 and both 1,−1 qualify. Zero does not: every prime number has its square as a divisor of zero. The Möbius function and radical of an integer below use their existing positive integer domains, so apply them to ∣n∣ when handling a negative integer.
For example, 30=2⋅3⋅5 is a square-free integer, whereas 12=22⋅3 is not, because 4∣12 and v2​(12)=2. Being a composite number causes no problem: 6,10,15 are all square-free integers. A square-free integer need not be coprime to another integer: 6 and 10 are both square-free integers but have greatest common divisor two. In fact the only signed integers coprime to every integer are 1,−1, since gcd(n,n)=∣n∣.
For positive integers a,b, the useful product criterion is
ab is square-free⟺a,b are square-free and gcd(a,b)=1.
(117)
The proof outline is just addition of prime valuations: vp​(ab)=vp​(a)+vp​(b) can stay at most one only if neither input repeats a prime factor and no prime factor occurs in both. Thus 6⋅5=30 is a square-free integer, but 6⋅10=60 is not. An equivalent characterization is that every factorization n=ab into positive integers has coprime factors. If p2∣n, the factorization n=p(n/p) supplies two factors sharing p; conversely, a shared prime factor forces its square to divide n. For 30, the unordered factor pairs (1,30),(2,15),(3,10),(5,6) all have greatest common divisor one; for 12, the pair (2,6) fails.
Two existing arithmetic functions turn the definition into practical tests:
rad(n)=n⟺n is square-free,μ(n)2={10​n is square-free,otherwise,​n>0.
(118)
The radical of an integer keeps one copy of each prime factor, so it changes precisely the integers with a repeated prime factor. Its definition is given in Evertse's exercise sheet. [ref] The Möbius function assigns (−1)r to a square-free integer with r distinct prime factors and zero otherwise; squaring forgets the sign.
