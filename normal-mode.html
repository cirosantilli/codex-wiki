Normal mode

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Partial differential equation Wave equation
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 7
A normal mode, also called an eigenmode, is a pattern of free oscillation in a linear system whose relative displacements stay fixed while a single temporal factor changes their size and sign. For an undamped stable vibrational mode,
u(x,t)=ϕ(x)q(t),q(t)=Acos(ωt+φ).
(37)
Here ϕ is the mode shape, A sets the amplitude, ω is the eigenfrequency expressed as an angular frequency, and φ is the oscillation phase. Every snapshot is a scalar multiple of the same mode shape; neighbouring regions may move in opposite directions. Normal modes of a double pendulum show that normal modes also describe coupled discrete systems, beyond strings. Their shapes are eigenvectors, rather than spatial eigenfunctions. [ref]
For the ideal wave equation on a string, take a uniform string of length L with constant tension τ and linear mass density μ. Assume small transverse slope, negligible bending stiffness, no damping, and fixed endpoints. Its wave speed is c=τ/μ​, and the nonzero normal modes are
un​(x,t)=Asin(Lnπx​)cos(ωn​t+φ),ωn​=Lnπc​,fn​=2πωn​​=2Lnc​,n=1,2,3,….
(38)
The angular frequency ωn​ is in radians per second; the frequency fn​ is in hertz. The wavelength is 2L/n. The mode shape has n loops separated by nodes of a standing wave, including both fixed endpoints. [ref]
As a worked example, choose L=1m, τ=1N, and μ=1kgm−1. Then c=1ms−1 and f1​=0.5Hz, f2​=1Hz, f3​=1.5Hz. Their oscillation periods are T1​=2s, T2​=1s, and T3​=2/3s. These are deliberately simple model parameters, rather than measurements of a musical instrument. The plot divides displacement by A so that the three mode shapes can be compared, and uses each mode's own oscillation period for its snapshots.
For φ=0, at t=Tn​/4 every point has zero displacement, but most points have nonzero velocity. Only zeros of the mode shape are permanent nodes of a standing wave. At t=Tn​/2 the original mode shape has reversed sign, and at t=Tn​ it returns. The synchronized conversion between kinetic energy and potential energy is shown in normal-mode energy exchange.
The short derivation uses separation of variables: substituting u=ϕq into the wave equation gives ϕ′′+k2ϕ=0 and q′′+c2k2q=0. The fixed-end boundary conditions remove the cosine spatial term and require sin(kL)=0, hence k=nπ/L and ωn​=ck. Different geometries and boundary conditions give different eigenfunctions; a sine mode shape is a property of this uniform fixed string, not a universal form of an eigenmode.
The usefulness is that a complicated vibration becomes a collection of harmonic oscillators. For sufficiently regular fixed-end initial data, the Fourier sine series gives
u(x,t)=∑n=1∞​sin(Lnπx​)[an​cos(ωn​t)+bn​sin(ωn​t)].
(39)
The initial displacement and velocity determine an​ and bn​. This is the superposition principle in a Fourier sine basis, and modal energy distribution measures how strongly each normal mode is excited. The striking simplification is that each component keeps its identity while their sum can continually change shape. [ref]
These independent normal modes belong to the linear model. At larger slopes, changing tension introduces nonlinear effects and can couple the modes. A linearly damped string still admits a Fourier sine basis, but its temporal factors decay and need not oscillate; its energy dissipation identity for a linearly damped string replaces conservation of energy. More general damping need not leave undamped mode shapes independent.
