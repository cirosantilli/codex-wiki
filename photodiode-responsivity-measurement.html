Photodiode responsivity measurement

 ... Physics Branches of physics Optics Optical instrument Photodetector Photodiode
OurBigBook.com Words: 407
Thorlabs' silicon FDS100 experiment sends a collimated, fibre-coupled laser beam through a beam splitter to the photodiode and a reference optical power meter. Varying reverse bias and electrical load tests linearity. The measured readout approaches saturation when its electric potential difference consumes the bias.[ref] A separate low-signal setup uses a 637nm laser, polarizers, an optical chopper, optical attenuators and a lock-in amplifier inside a light-tight enclosure. These are actual apparatus choices, not the parameters of the constructed example above.[ref]
For an independent dark current check, Thorlabs enclosed a photodiode in nested metal boxes to exclude light and suppress interference, used a Keithley 6487 ammeter supplying reverse bias, and monitored temperature. Only one specimen per type was tested; the nearby temperature sensor did not directly contact the semiconductor. Such a dark control measures the baseline but cannot certify spectral responsivity.[ref]
To infer spectral responsivity at a selected wavelength, measure the radiant flux actually reaching the test photodiode, rather than just the reference arm. A reference-arm measurement calibration must include the beam splitter ratio and path losses. With signed readouts in one fixed orientation, subtract a beam-blocked baseline and use

for direct electric current readout or a calibrated linear transimpedance amplifier, respectively. Repeating at several incident powers tests whether this ratio is constant. Blocking only the source estimates ambient light plus electronic baseline; covering the detector excludes ambient light too. Their difference distinguishes optical background from dark current and readout offset. For a broad optical spectrum, the ratio is a spectrum-weighted response, not generally R at one wavelength.
For independent Poisson distribution collection, the root mean square shot noise in a one-sided rectangular frequency band of signal bandwidth B>0 is
ishot​=2q(Iph​+Id​)B​,
(43)
Here Id​ is reverse dark current; thermal electrical noise and amplifier noise also contribute.[ref]
Subtracting a mean dark current removes its offset, not its fluctuations: subtracting a constant leaves the variance unchanged. Consequently Iph​≫Id​ makes dark current shot noise small relative to signal shot noise; it does not by itself establish high signal-to-noise ratio. With an independently measured baseline, the variance of that estimate must also be propagated. Increasing the measurement signal bandwidth raises the admitted noise. Calibration of wavelength, reference radiant flux, readout gain and collection geometry, together with drift and linearity, determines what measurement uncertainty can be claimed.
