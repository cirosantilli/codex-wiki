Evolution

 Home Biology
OurBigBook.com Words: 4k Articles: 168
Evolution is descent with inherited modification. Within a population, changes in allele frequencies provide a precise genetic description; over longer histories, branching descent connects populations through common descent. An individual growing larger is biological development, whereas an inherited variant becoming more frequent across generations is evolution. See UC Museum of Paleontology: the definition of evolution.
Mutation supplies new alleles, genetic recombination reshuffles inherited material, natural selection favors variants with greater reproductive contribution in a specified environment, and genetic drift changes frequencies through chance. Gene flow moves variants between populations. Consequently, an increasing allele frequency alone does not identify natural selection. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: population genetics.
Evidence includes ordered fossils, shared inherited structures and similarities in DNA; phylogenetics uses such observations to infer relationships. A phylogenetic tree is an inferred historical account, with uncertainty about particular branches. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: evidence of evolution. Evidence also comes from experiments: Blount, Borland and Lenski reported an Escherichia coli lineage able to use citrate with oxygen present by 31,500 generations, and tested historical dependence by replaying evolution from stored ancestors. See Blount, Borland and Lenski, 2008: original experimental report. Such observations constrain explanations; no single frequency equation captures every evolutionary process.
