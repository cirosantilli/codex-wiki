Write R=OK​. We prove unique factorization of ideals in a number field, rather than assuming this conclusion as a property of a Dedekind domain. Three preliminary facts about R will be useful. Its additive group is a finite-rank free abelian group, so every ideal is finitely generated and the ascending chain condition holds. It is an integrally closed domain, by integral dependence is transitive. Finally, every nonzero prime ideal is a maximal ideal. For this last fact, choose 0=a∈P. Multiplication by a is an injective endomorphism of the finite-rank free abelian group R with nonzero determinant, so R/(a) is finite. Consequently R/P is a finite integral domain and hence a field.
First, every nonzero proper ideal contains a product of nonzero prime ideals. Otherwise choose a maximal counterexample J under inclusion, using the ascending chain condition. It cannot be a prime ideal. Choose x,y∈/J with xy∈J. The strictly larger ideals J+(x) and J+(y) each contain a product of nonzero prime ideals, allowing the empty product R when necessary. Multiplying those products gives a product contained in
(J+(x))(J+(y))⊆J,
(28)
a contradiction.
Next fix a nonzero prime ideal P, choose 0=a∈P, and choose a product of nonzero prime ideals
P1​⋯Pr​⊆(a)
(29)
with r minimal. Because (a)⊆P, primality and maximality imply Pj​=P for some j; reorder so that P1​=P. Minimality provides b∈P2​⋯Pr​ with b∈/(a), taking the remaining product to be R if r=1. Thus
x=b/a∈/R,xP⊆R.
(30)
Define the fractional ideal
P−1={z∈K:zP⊆R}.
(31)
It contains R and x. The ideal PP−1 lies between P and R, so it is P or R. If it were P, then xP⊆P. Applying the determinant trick to a finite generating set of an ideal for P gives a monic polynomial over R satisfied by x. Since R is an integrally closed domain, this would put x in R, a contradiction. Therefore
PP−1=R​.
(32)
This proves that each nonzero prime ideal is an invertible fractional ideal, independently of the desired factorization theorem.
Now take a nonzero proper ideal A and a maximal ideal P containing it. The ideal B=P−1A satisfies
A⊆B⊆R,A=PB.
(33)
The first inclusion is strict: if A=B, the same x∈P−1∖R would stabilize A, and the determinant trick would again force x∈R. Repeating gives a strictly ascending chain until it reaches R; the ascending chain condition ensures this happens after finitely many steps. The identities A=PB then express A as a product of nonzero prime ideals.
For uniqueness, suppose P1​⋯Pr​=Q1​⋯Qs​. Since the right-hand side is contained in P1​, some Qj​ is contained in P1​. Both are nonzero maximal ideals, so Qj​=P1​. Multiplication by P1−1​ cancels this factor. Repeat. A nonempty product of proper prime ideals cannot equal R, so both products finish together. This proves uniqueness up to reordering, including repeated factors.
