For a number field K of degree of a field extension n, an integral basis is a basis ω1​,…,ωn​ over Q such that
OK​=Zω1​⊕⋯⊕Zωn​.
(1)
Thus every algebraic integer in K has a unique expression with integer coefficients in this basis. Merely requiring its entries to be algebraic integers would not suffice: for example, 1,22​ is a rational basis of Q(2​) but misses the algebraic integer 2​ in its integer span.
Here is a proof of the existence of an integral basis. Start with any rational basis v1​,…,vn​. Each vj​ has a nonzero integer multiple αj​ that is an algebraic integer. Indeed, if
vm+cm−1​vm−1+⋯+c0​=0,ci​∈Q,
(2)
choose a positive integer q clearing all the denominators. Then qv satisfies the monic polynomial
Xm+qcm−1​Xm−1+q2cm−2​Xm−2+⋯+qmc0​∈Z[X].
(3)
The αj​ are still a rational basis. Put M=∑j​Zαj​ and form the matrix
T=(TrK/Q​(αi​αj​))i,j​,D=detT.
(4)
Its entries are integers, because products and sums of algebraic integers are algebraic integers and a rational algebraic integer is an integer. The nondegeneracy of the trace form of a number field gives D=0: if x=0, then Tr(xx−1)=n=0.
For x=∑j​xj​αj​∈OK​, each Tr(xαi​) is an integer, so
T(x1​,…,xn​)T∈Zn.
(5)
The adjugate matrix formula for T−1 gives Dxj​∈Z. Consequently
M⊆OK​⊆D−1M.
(6)
The submodule theorem for free modules over a principal ideal domain says that every subgroup of the finite-rank free abelian group D−1M is again a free abelian group of rank at most n. Since OK​ contains M, its rank is exactly n. A basis of this free abelian group is the required integral basis.

Put α=m1​​, β=m2​​ and F=Q(α). The hypotheses make L/Q a biquadratic extension. To check the degree of a field extension is four, if β=x+yα with rational x,y, squaring gives xy=0. The case y=0 would make m2​ a rational square; the case x=0 would make m2​/m1​ a rational square. Neither can occur for the specified distinct coprime square-free integers. Hence [L:F]=2.
The nonidentity field automorphism of L/F fixes α and sends β to −β. Thus, for γ=(r+sα)+(t+uα)β, the field norm and field trace are
NL/F​(γ)=(r+sα)2−m2​(t+uα)2,TrL/F​(γ)=2(r+sα)​.
(13)

γ2−Tγ+N=0

Write R=OK​. We prove unique factorization of ideals in a number field, rather than assuming this conclusion as a property of a Dedekind domain. Three preliminary facts about R will be useful. Its additive group is a finite-rank free abelian group, so every ideal is finitely generated and the ascending chain condition holds. It is an integrally closed domain, by integral dependence is transitive. Finally, every nonzero prime ideal is a maximal ideal. For this last fact, choose 0=a∈P. Multiplication by a is an injective endomorphism of the finite-rank free abelian group R with nonzero determinant, so R/(a) is finite. Consequently R/P is a finite integral domain and hence a field.
First, every nonzero proper ideal contains a product of nonzero prime ideals. Otherwise choose a maximal counterexample J under inclusion, using the ascending chain condition. It cannot be a prime ideal. Choose x,y∈/J with xy∈J. The strictly larger ideals J+(x) and J+(y) each contain a product of nonzero prime ideals, allowing the empty product R when necessary. Multiplying those products gives a product contained in
(J+(x))(J+(y))⊆J,
(28)
a contradiction.
Next fix a nonzero prime ideal P, choose 0=a∈P, and choose a product of nonzero prime ideals
P1​⋯Pr​⊆(a)
(29)
with r minimal. Because (a)⊆P, primality and maximality imply Pj​=P for some j; reorder so that P1​=P. Minimality provides b∈P2​⋯Pr​ with b∈/(a), taking the remaining product to be R if r=1. Thus
x=b/a∈/R,xP⊆R.
(30)
Define the fractional ideal
P−1={z∈K:zP⊆R}.
(31)
It contains R and x. The ideal PP−1 lies between P and R, so it is P or R. If it were P, then xP⊆P. Applying the determinant trick to a finite generating set of an ideal for P gives a monic polynomial over R satisfied by x. Since R is an integrally closed domain, this would put x in R, a contradiction. Therefore
PP−1=R​.
(32)
This proves that each nonzero prime ideal is an invertible fractional ideal, independently of the desired factorization theorem.
Now take a nonzero proper ideal A and a maximal ideal P containing it. The ideal B=P−1A satisfies
A⊆B⊆R,A=PB.
(33)
The first inclusion is strict: if A=B, the same x∈P−1∖R would stabilize A, and the determinant trick would again force x∈R. Repeating gives a strictly ascending chain until it reaches R; the ascending chain condition ensures this happens after finitely many steps. The identities A=PB then express A as a product of nonzero prime ideals.
For uniqueness, suppose P1​⋯Pr​=Q1​⋯Qs​. Since the right-hand side is contained in P1​, some Qj​ is contained in P1​. Both are nonzero maximal ideals, so Qj​=P1​. Multiplication by P1−1​ cancels this factor. Repeat. A nonempty product of proper prime ideals cannot equal R, so both products finish together. This proves uniqueness up to reordering, including repeated factors.

By the correspondence theorem for ideals, an ideal of R/Pn has the form J/Pn with Pn⊆J⊆R. If J=R, apply unique factorization of ideals in a number field to J. Every prime ideal Q dividing J contains J and hence Pn. Primality gives P⊆Q, and maximality gives Q=P. Thus J=Pi for some i≥1.
The inclusion Pn⊆Pi forces i≤n: if i>n, cancellation using the invertible fractional ideal Pn would give R⊆Pi−n, which is impossible. The zero ideal corresponds to i=n, while the unit ideal corresponds to i=0. Consequently the nonzero proper ideals are exactly
Pi/Pn,1≤i<n​.
(34)
When n=1, there are none, in agreement with R/P being a field.

The printed domain assertion is false. For example, take K=Q and I=(4). Then OK​/I=Z/4Z has the nonzero zero divisor 2+(4), so it is not an integral domain and cannot be a principal ideal domain. The valid conclusion is that OK​/I is a principal ideal ring. This error is present in the original PDF, not just its transcription.
To prove the corrected conclusion, factor
I=∏j=1s​Pjnj​​
(42)
into powers of distinct nonzero prime ideals. These powers are pairwise comaximal ideals. Indeed, a maximal ideal containing two such powers would contain both underlying prime ideals, forcing them to be equal. The Chinese remainder theorem gives
R/I≃∏j=1s​R/Pjnj​​.
(43)
Every factor is a principal ideal ring by the preceding part. Every ideal H in a finite direct product of rings is a product H1​×⋯×Hs​ of ideals in the factors: multiplication by the coordinate idempotent elements separates the coordinates of every element of H. Write Hj​=(hj​). Then
H1​×⋯×Hs​=((h1​,…,hs​)),
(44)
so R/I is a principal ideal ring. This proves the principal ideal property of quotients of a Dedekind domain in the present setting. In fact, this finite quotient ring is an integral domain precisely when I is a prime ideal, in which case it is a field.
For the final deduction, choose any 0=a∈I and apply the result to the nonzero proper ideal (a). The ideal I/(a) of R/(a) is generated by one residue class b+(a), which can be represented by b∈I. For each x∈I, some r∈R satisfies x−rb∈(a). Hence
I=(a,b)​.
(45)
This proves two generators for an ideal in a Dedekind domain; moreover, its first generator can be any prescribed nonzero element of I.

(4π​)n(n!nn​)2.

To compute the Ideal class group of Q of square root thirty-five, put θ=35​. The ring of integers of a quadratic field formula gives R=Z[θ], and the field discriminant is 4⋅35=140. Both field embeddings are real, so the Minkowski bound for ideal classes is
222!​140​=35​<6.
(52)
Every ideal class therefore has an integral ideal representative of ideal norm at most five. By prime ideal factorization and multiplicativity of the ideal norm, the ideal class group is generated by prime ideals of ideal norm at most five.
Apply the Dedekind factorization theorem to X2−35. At two and five it has a repeated linear factor, giving
(2)=P22​,P2​=(2,θ−1),N(P2​)=2;(5)=P52​,P5​=(5,θ),N(P5​)=5.
(53)
At three, X2−35≡X2+1 has no root, so (3) itself is a prime ideal of ideal norm nine, outside the bound. These are all the relevant prime ideals: a prime ideal of ideal norm four would have to lie above two with residue degree two, but the factorization above two has only the single factor of degree one. Consequently [P2​] and [P5​] generate, and each has order of a group element dividing two.
The algebraic integer 5+θ has field norm 25−35=−10. It belongs to both P2​ and P5​. Hence its principal ideal is contained in P2​∩P5​=P2​P5​. Both have ideal norm ten, so
(5+θ)=P2​P5​.
(54)
It follows that [P5​]=[P2​]−1=[P2​], and the ideal class group is generated by one element of order of a group element at most two.
Finally, P2​ is not a principal ideal. If P2​=(a+bθ) with a,b∈Z, the equality of ideal norm and absolute field norm would imply
a2−35b2=±2.
(55)
Modulo five this requires a2≡2 or 3, whereas squares modulo five are 0,1,4. Thus [P2​]=1, proving
Cl(Q(35​))≃Z/2Z​.
(56)
This computes the ordinary ideal class group, with no positivity restriction on generators.

π4​333!​243​=π83​​<5.

For p≡1(mod20), put θ=−p​. Since −p≡3(mod4), the ring of integers of a quadratic field formula gives R=Z[θ]. Modulo five,
X2+p≡X2+1=(X−2)(X+2).
(64)
The two roots are distinct, so the Dedekind factorization theorem gives
(5)=PP,P=(5,θ−2),P=(5,θ+2),N(P)=N(P)=5.
(65)
In particular P=P.
We prove that [P] has order of a group element greater than n. Suppose instead that Pk is a principal ideal for some 1≤k≤n. Since Pk⊆R, its generator is in R: write Pk=(a+bθ) with a,b∈Z. Taking ideal norms gives the positive field norm
5k=a2+pb2.
(66)
But 5k≤5n<p, so b=0. Thus a2=5k, forcing k to be even and a=±5k/2. This would give
Pk=(a)=(5)k/2=Pk/2Pk/2,
(67)
contrary to unique factorization of ideals in a number field and P=P. Consequently no such k exists. The finiteness of the ideal class group ensures that [P] has finite order of a group element, so
ord([P])>n​.
(68)
For every positive integer n, the allowed infinitude of prime numbers in the progression 1(mod20) supplies one larger than 5n. Applying the construction proves unbounded element orders in imaginary quadratic ideal class groups. In particular, this establishes elements of arbitrarily large order of a group element, rather than merely unbounded class numbers.
