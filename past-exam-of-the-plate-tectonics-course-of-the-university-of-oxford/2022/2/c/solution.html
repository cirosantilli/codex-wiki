The positive centers in the supplied maps are examples of lunar mascons, especially associated with large impact basins. An empty excavation by itself removes mass and would not explain a positive central anomaly. Basin formation can thin the low-density lunar crust and raise denser lunar mantle beneath the floor; impact melting, collapse and later cooling can also create dense material and lock in a mass excess. Where a basin was later flooded, dense mare basalt adds another contribution. Mare infill is therefore one possible contributor, not a necessary explanation of every mascon.[ref]
The difference between the maps is the modeled topographic contribution. On a common reference surface, write schematically
ΔgFA​=gtopo​+ginternal​,ΔgB​=ΔgFA​−gtopo​.
(2)
The free-air gravity anomaly retains relief-related attraction. Rims, excavated floors and central peaks therefore contribute fine structure. The Bouguer gravity anomaly removes the attraction predicted from the measured topography at an assumed crustal density, making internal density and interface structure more apparent. A low basin floor has a negative topographic contribution; subtracting it can enhance an underlying positive central anomaly. Removing a positive rim contribution can weaken a rim high. The supplied lower map is correspondingly smoother at short wavelengths and shows prominent residual basin centers and surrounding rings. This is a change of gravity reduction, not a physical change of the craters.[ref]
For scale, a Bouguer slab approximation gives Δg≃2πGΔρd. A 5 km layer with geophysical density contrast 300kgm−3 produces about 63 mGal above an ideal infinite slab. Actual basin geometry, burial, porosity and mantle uplift require a finite three-dimensional model. Smaller craters can have central lows, damaged porous interiors or more complex rings; the maps do not imply that every crater has a positive anomaly or that positive anomalies measure basalt thickness uniquely.
