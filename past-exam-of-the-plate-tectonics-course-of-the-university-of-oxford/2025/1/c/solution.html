I would prioritize an accessible exposure of ancient, fine-grained lacustrine deposits associated with a river delta, preferably with nearby hydrothermal deposits or groundwater alteration that offer a second environmental window. A good target maximizes three separate probabilities: past habitability, preservation of a biosignature, and practical access to the preserved material. A spectacular former water channel alone need not score well on the second or third.
First choice: a stratigraphically resolved ancient lake basin. Look for a closed or formerly overflowing basin fed by an inlet valley, a river delta with coherent layering, and adjacent fine mudstone deposited below strong wave or channel disturbance. Delta foresets and bottomsets, channel relationships and laterally continuous lacustrine deposits provide a testable paleoenvironment, rather than relying on an isolated fan shape. An inlet plus an outlet and consistent shoreline elevations can constrain lake level and water throughput. Select Noachian or early Hesperian units where available, while avoiding the assumption that all younger environments were uninhabitable.
Quiet lacustrine deposits can concentrate material from a wider drainage area; clay minerals provide large surfaces for adsorption of organic matter. Rapid burial and limited exposure to oxidizing fluids favor preservation. Carbonate or silica cements can trap chemical or microscopic evidence, although later diagenesis may erase or redistribute it. A near-neutral, not excessively saline paleoenvironment is attractive for known life, but should be inferred from mineral assemblages and sedimentary context rather than assumed from the presence of water.
This strategy has direct primary-source precedents. An ancient lake mudstone investigated by a rover preserved evidence for near-neutral pH, low salinity and variable oxidation states, making a plausible habitat for chemolithotrophy. This established past habitability, not past life.[ref] Independently, rover images of another basin established a delta-lake succession followed by high-energy floods. That distinction helps choose fine background deposits rather than assuming the coarsest flood layers are the best biosignature archive.[ref]
Second choice: ancient water-rock reaction systems. Target exhumed hydrothermal deposits associated with old volcanic terrain, basin-floor alteration, or impact-generated hydrothermal circulation. Especially useful settings contain silica, clay minerals, carbonates and reduced iron or sulfur phases in a coherent geological association. Chemical gradients between reacted fluids and surrounding water can supply free energy for chemolithotrophy; rapid mineral precipitation may entomb biosignatures. Habitable cooler mixing zones are more promising than assuming the hottest vent was habitable. Mineralogical mapping of thick, ancient basin deposits on Mars has motivated a deep-water hydrothermal alteration interpretation, providing a physically grounded alternative to shallow lakes. The interpretation remains a hypothesis to test in situ, not a detection of life.[ref]
Additional targets: protected groundwater archives. Old fractures, mineral veins and groundwater-fed sedimentary deposits exposed by recent erosion can sample former subsurface habitats protected from surface ultraviolet radiation. Evaporites can preserve inclusions and record water chemistry, but strongly acidic or hypersaline terminal brines may be poor habitats, and a salt deposit alone does not distinguish a long-lived ecosystem from brief evaporation. Choose a traverse that can test the relation between these deposits and the lake or hydrothermal alteration succession.
Preservation and sampling can overturn the apparent ranking. Favor ancient rocks that were buried for most of their history and only recently exhumed, with little thermal alteration, pervasive oxidation or recrystallization. Drill beneath weathered rinds and sample fresh bedrock; loose modern regolith mixes unrelated sources. Cosmic ionizing radiation penetrates deeper than ultraviolet radiation, so shallow abrasion is not complete protection. Laboratory work reported by National Aeronautics and Space Administration shows that survival depths depend on molecule, mineral matrix and accumulated dose; meter-scale burial or a short exposure history can be important, rather than one universal safe drilling depth.[ref]
The landing ellipse needs safe terrain and a feasible traverse to several related exposures. Orbital spectroscopy, imaging and surface topography should establish the initial target; field observations must then test stratigraphy, sedimentary textures and alteration history. Combine organic chemistry, isotope ratios, mineral associations and microscopic structures in their geological context, using contamination controls and samples from neighboring noncandidate material. Organic molecules, carbon isotope fractionation or a cell-like shape alone can have abiotic explanations. A convergent suite of contextual observations is a stronger case for ancient life than any single signal.
