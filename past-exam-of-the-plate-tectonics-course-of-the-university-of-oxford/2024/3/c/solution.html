Cooling makes the oceanic lithosphere denser and contracts it; isostatic adjustment therefore lowers the seafloor. The cooling layer includes substantial mantle, not just the approximately fixed-thickness igneous oceanic crust.
New plate near a spreading ridge is hot and relatively buoyant. As it moves away, conductive cooling penetrates more deeply, and material formerly in the hot asthenosphere becomes part of the colder thermal boundary layer. To first order,
δρ(z,t)=ρm​α[Tm​−T(z,t)],
(3)
where α is the volumetric thermal expansion coefficient, ρm​ a reference mantle density, Tm​ the hot reference temperature, and t plate age. A positive depth-integrated density anomaly makes the column negatively buoyant relative to the ridge column. Isostasy is restored by downward displacement and mantle redistribution; cold rock also occupies less volume.
At a fixed sufficiently deep datum, deepening the seafloor by Δd removes an equivalent thickness of rock from the column and replaces it with less dense seawater. In the usual constant-property approximation this gives
(ρm​−ρw​)Δd≃∫0zc​​δρ(z,t)dz,
(4)
where ρw​ is seawater density and zc​ lies below the relevant thermal anomaly. This explains why contraction alone and water-loaded seafloor subsidence are not identical numerical quantities.
For a half-space cooling model,
T(z,t)=Ts​+(Tm​−Ts​)erf(2κt​z​),
(5)
so integration yields
Δd(t)=ρm​−ρw​2ρm​α(Tm​−Ts​)​πκt​​.​
(6)
Here κ is thermal diffusivity and z is measured downward from the surface of the thermal model. The leading oceanic thermal subsidence is proportional to t​. A finite oceanic plate cooling model limits this growth at old ages. Sediment loading, dynamic topography, anomalous crust thickness and hydrothermal heat transport can produce departures from the simple relation.[ref]
