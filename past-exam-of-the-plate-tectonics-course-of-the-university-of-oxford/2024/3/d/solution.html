OurBigBook.com Words: 250
Choose a common horizontal compensation depth beneath the deepest cooling-induced density anomaly, within mantle that can relax pressure differences over the timescale considered. In a normal oceanic plate model, this means below the old plate's thermal lithosphere, not at the base of the approximately 7 km thick oceanic crust.
The reason is that cooling changes density through both crust and lithospheric mantle. A datum at the Mohorovičić discontinuity would omit most of the changing column mass. A different datum for each age would also compare unlike columns. Once the common datum is sufficiently deep, extending it farther into laterally uniform mantle adds the same mass per unit area to each column and does not change the predicted relative subsidence.
There is no unique number fixed by the question. For an illustrative oceanic plate cooling model with a thermal thickness about 100–125 km, a common datum beneath that layer, of order 150–200 km below sea level, is a defensible choice provided the model has no substantial lateral density anomaly there. These are model choices, not measured universal boundaries. In a half-space cooling model, use the oldest age tmax​ to choose a practical cutoff: zc​≳4κtmax​​ makes the remaining temperature anomaly less than about 0.5% of its surface contrast. For κ=10−6m2s−1 and tmax​=100 million years, this is about 225 km. A half-space model has no mathematically sharp finite thermal base.
Finally, equal column mass is the local, approximately constant-gravitational acceleration representation of equal pressure at the datum. A weak asthenosphere permits pressure relaxation through slow solid-state material creep; it need not be liquid. Short-wavelength loads, lithospheric flexure or dynamic topography can violate local column-by-column isostasy, requiring a more complete mechanical model.
