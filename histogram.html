Histogram

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Descriptive statistics
OurBigBook.com Words: 630
A histogram groups numerical observations into adjacent intervals called bins and draws one rectangle per bin. Its attraction is that a long list of values becomes a visible pattern of concentration, gaps and skewness. Specify the bin edges, the treatment of values on those edges, and what the vertical axis means: count, proportion, or density. Those choices are part of the graphic. [ref]
Let n>0 observations be covered by disjoint bins Bj​ with positive widths Δj​ and counts nj​, so ∑j​nj​=n. For example, use left-closed, right-open bins, closing the final right edge too. The three common height choices have different meanings:
  • Count height nj​ gives the number in bin j; the sum of heights is n.
  • Proportion height nj​/n gives the fraction in bin j; the sum of heights is 1.
  • Density height nj​/(nΔj​) makes the area, rather than the height, equal to the fraction in bin j.
For the density convention, a histogram is a piecewise constant probability density function estimate:
f​(x)=nΔj​nj​​(x∈Bj​),f​(x)=0(x∈/⋃j​Bj​).
(5)
Its normalization follows by adding rectangular areas:
∫Bj​​f​(x)dx=nnj​​,∫R​f​(x)dx=∑j​nnj​​=1.
(6)
If the observations have units, density has reciprocal units; a density height is not a probability and can exceed 1. With equal bin widths, count heights and density heights differ only by a common scale factor. With unequal widths, count-height areas nj​Δj​ give wider bins extra visual weight unrelated to their counts. Dividing by width removes that distortion. R's histogram documentation explicitly describes area-one normalization for unequal bins. [ref]
Reuse the twelve invented values under box plot, with bins [0,2),[2,4),[4,8),[8,22]. The counts are (1,3,6,2) and the widths are (2,2,4,14). Hence the density heights and areas are exactly
(241​,81​,81​,841​),(121​,41​,21​,61​).
(7)
The second and third bars have equal density heights even though the third contains twice as many observations: it is also twice as wide. The last bar contains only two observations; its count-height area would be 2⋅14=28, larger than the six-observation bar's 6⋅4=24. Its correct density area is only 1/6.
Bin choice trades detail against stability. Broad bins merge distinctions: the toy bin [8,22] hides the empty stretch between 8 and 20. Narrow bins expose gaps but can make each individual observation dominate a bar. With one-unit bins starting at 0, the bins [2,3),[4,5),[6,7) each contain two values and appear equally prominent; that apparent pattern depends on grouping the repeated integers together. Moving the bin origin also changes membership without changing the observations. For density estimation, this is a form of the bias-variance tradeoff: averaging over broad bins loses local detail, while narrow bins give more variable density estimates across repeated statistical samples. [ref] A useful practice is to compare a few plausible widths and offsets, keeping common edges when comparing groups. Automatic bin selectors are starting points, rather than guarantees of a unique correct picture. [ref]
A histogram retains only bin membership. Its density estimate spreads each bin's fraction uniformly across that bin, so our last bar assigns density throughout [8,22] even though the only toy values there are 8 and 20. It does not recover within-bin positions or observation order, and a visually convincing shape does not establish a population probability distribution. A box plot provides a complementary compact summary of quartiles; neither graphic replaces the original observations.
