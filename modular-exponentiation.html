Modular exponentiation (abmodm)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Number theory Exponentiation by squaring
OurBigBook.com Words: 596 Articles: 1
Modular exponentiation computes the remainder anmodm for an integer base a, a nonnegative integer exponent n, and, here, an integer modulus m≥2. It applies exponentiation by squaring while reducing after each multiplication, including each squaring. The result is the unique representative in {0,…,m−1}. There is no requirement that the base be coprime to the modulus when the exponent is nonnegative.[ref]
Take a=2, n=13=(1101)2​, and m=17. Initialize r=2; the remaining digits give
111101101​:22mod17=4,4⋅2mod17=8,:82mod17=64mod17=13,:132mod17=169mod17=16,16⋅2mod17=15.​
(25)
Hence 213mod17=15. The final operation multiplies 16 by 2, since the preceding square has already been reduced. The computation never needs to construct 213=8192.
In general reduce the base first, x=amodm. For n>0, initialize r=x and process the remaining binary digits with r←r2modm, followed, for a digit one, by r←rxmodm. The loop invariant is
0≤r<m,r≡ap(modm),
(26)
where p is the processed prefix value. The congruence is preserved because modular arithmetic respects multiplication: if u≡v(modm), then u2≡v2 and ux≡va(modm). Thus changing representatives never changes the final congruence class.
Both factors in every product are less than m, so the unreduced product is less than m2, and the stored result is again less than m. The same bounds hold for a gigantic exponent, such as n=21000: there are 1000 squarings, each on bounded-size residues. For base two, the corresponding ordinary power would have 21000+1 binary digits. The benefit is avoiding the huge ordinary power altogether, rather than constructing it and taking one final remainder. Initial reduction is essential when the supplied base is much larger than the modulus.
For n=0, return 1 under the same empty-product convention as exponentiation by squaring. For n=−k<0, require gcd(a,m)=1, find the modular multiplicative inverse a−1modm, and compute (a−1)kmodm; the extended Euclidean algorithm can find that inverse. Its cost is separate from the positive-exponent binary powering multiplication count. For example, 2−1≡9(mod17), while 2−1mod4 is undefined because 2 and 4 are not coprime.
A reversible computation gives the coherent quantum modular exponentiation used in quantum order finding and discrete-logarithm Fourier sampling.
