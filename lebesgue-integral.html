Lebesgue integral (∫X​fdμ)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Measure theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 534 Articles: 3
For a nonnegative measurable function f:X→[0,∞] on a measure space, define
∫X​fdμ=sup{∫X​sdμ:s is a nonnegative simple function, 0≤s≤f}.
(34)
The supremum may be infinite. A simple function assigns finitely many heights to measurable sets, so its integral is height times measure, summed over those pieces. This definition groups points by their values rather than requiring a partition into short intervals. See MIT lecture 11, Definition 108.
A real-valued function is Lebesgue integrable when ∫X​∣f∣dμ<∞. Its integral is ∫X​f+​dμ−∫X​f−​dμ, where f+​=max(f,0) is its positive part and f−​=max(−f,0). This finiteness requirement avoids subtracting two infinities. For functions taking values in the complex numbers, integrate the real part and imaginary part separately.
Consider the Dirichlet function f=1Q∩[0,1]​ on [0,1]. It is a simple function, and
∫[0,1]​fdλ=λ(Q∩[0,1])=0.
(35)
Its Riemann integral does not exist. For every partition of an interval 0=x0​<⋯<xn​=1, each nondegenerate [xi−1​,xi​] contains both rational numbers and irrational numbers, by their respective density of the rational numbers and density of the irrational numbers. Thus its infimum there is 0 and its supremum is 1. The lower Darboux sum is ∑i​0(xi​−xi−1​)=0, whereas the upper Darboux sum is ∑i​1(xi​−xi−1​)=1. Refinement never closes this gap. A finite scatterplot cannot represent this behavior: the assertion concerns every subinterval and every partition of an interval.
Another view builds the Lebesgue integral from horizontal layers. For any nonnegative measurable function, the layer cake representation states
∫X​fdμ=∫0∞​μ({x∈X:f(x)>t})dt.
(36)
Both sides may be infinite. The right side integrates the measure of a superlevel set against Lebesgue measure in the height variable t. See Gautam Iyer's measure theory notes, Theorem 9.5.
For f(x)=x2 on [0,1] with Lebesgue measure, the superlevel set at 0≤t<1 is (t​,1], of Lebesgue measure 1−t​; for t≥1 it is empty. Consequently
∫01​x2dλ(x)=∫01​(1−t​)dt=[t−32​t3/2]01​=31​.
(37)
At height t=1/4, the layer extends from x=1/2 to x=1, a width of 1/2. The picture shows why the same area can be accumulated vertically or horizontally. In a probability space, the identical formula expresses the expected value of a nonnegative random variable through its exceedance probabilities.
A proof outline avoids extra assumptions on the measure space. For a nonnegative simple function s=∑j​aj​1Aj​​ with disjoint measurable sets Aj​, the superlevel set has measure ∑aj​>t​μ(Aj​). Integrating this finite sum gives ∑j​aj​μ(Aj​)=∫sdμ. Next use approximation by nonnegative simple functions sn​↑f. For every t, the superlevel sets {sn​>t} increase to {f>t}. Continuity from below of a measure and the monotone convergence theorem on the height variable give the formula for f.
