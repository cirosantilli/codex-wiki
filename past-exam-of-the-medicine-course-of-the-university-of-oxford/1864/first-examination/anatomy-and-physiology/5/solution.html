Starch granules can often be distinguished by their shape, size distribution, hilum and lamellae; dextrin and inulin are distinguished chiefly by their molecular constitution, solubility and chemical reactions. An isolated particle of uncertain origin cannot always be identified uniquely by one feature.
In a water-mounted preparation of unheated starch, potato granules are usually relatively large, oval or pear-shaped, often with an eccentric hilum near the narrower end and conspicuous curved growth markings. Wheat gives a characteristic mixture of larger flattened lenticular granules and small rounded granules; the hilum and rings are less conspicuous. Rice starch consists of very small angular or polyhedral granules, often still associated into compound groups. These are distributions rather than a guarantee that every potato granule is larger than every wheat granule.[ref]
Maize commonly gives polygonal or rounded granules with a central point or fissured hilum; oats characteristically include aggregates of small angular granules. Arrowroot commonly has oval or elongated granules, sometimes with a transverse fissure towards one end. Preparation, variety and processing change the apparent population. Rotation between crossed polarizers shows the organized granule structure through a characteristic extinction cross in many intact granules. Swelling and loss of birefringence on heating with sufficient water help demonstrate starch gelatinization, although the precise temperature depends on the starch source.
Cold water normally leaves native starch granules as a suspension rather than a clear molecular solution. On heating, granules swell and a paste develops. With iodine in iodide solution, ordinary starch rich in sufficiently long amylose helices produces an intense blue or blue-black complex. Its intensity depends on chain structure and conditions; the response can weaken on heating and return on cooling if the chains remain suitable. Continued acid or enzymatic hydrolysis shortens chains and eventually destroys this characteristic blue response.
Dextrin denotes a range of shorter or modified glucose polymers derived from starch, rather than one pure substance. Many preparations dissolve or disperse in water more readily and no longer show intact native granules. Their iodine reactions vary with chain length: some give violet or reddish-brown colours, while extensively shortened material gives no characteristic colour. It is therefore too absolute to say that every dextrin is chemically identical or that no dextrin can react with iodine. The modern international specifications themselves distinguish starch modifications and their variable iodine colours.[ref][ref]
Inulin is principally a fructose-based fructan, with a different linkage pattern from glucose-based starch. It occurs, for example, in chicory and related storage organs. It does not give starch's blue iodine complex. It dissolves more readily in hot water, and may separate as deposits on cooling or precipitation, but such deposits are not the characteristic native starch granules. Hydrolysis yields mainly fructose, whereas complete starch or dextrin hydrolysis yields glucose. A copper-reduction test after hydrolysis detects reducing products in both cases and is therefore insufficient by itself to distinguish them. Their different hydrolysis products, interpreted with iodine reaction and microscopy, give a more specific comparison.[ref]
A sound comparison uses separately labelled reference preparations, equal iodine conditions and separate unheated and heated mounts. Inspecting an unknown mixture only after boiling destroys much of the morphological evidence. The combined observations distinguish source-related morphology from the effects of processing.
