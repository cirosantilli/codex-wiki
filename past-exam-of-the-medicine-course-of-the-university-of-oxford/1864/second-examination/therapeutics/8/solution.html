The four agents have different proposed purposes. Their indications must be tied to a mechanism and a diagnosis, and their historical use must not be converted into a general modern recommendation. In particular, a historical effect on a discharge, pulse or bowel movement is not proof of cure of the disease causing it.
Elaterium was a drastic hydragogue purgative, the dried deposit from the juice of squirting cucumber fruit. The intended use was to remove fluid through the intestine in severe dropsy, particularly some cardiac cases. The same action explains the danger: violent diarrhoea, abdominal irritation, dehydration and collapse. Frailty, existing fluid depletion, inflammatory disease of the intestine, suspected bowel obstruction and pregnancy make such treatment particularly objectionable. Variable potency further undermines a fixed-dose rule.[ref] In current care, identify the cause of oedema; cardiac fluid overload may require monitored diuretic treatment, not deliberate drastic purging. Elaterium is not a substitute for a modern diuretic.
Digitalis preparations, historically obtained from foxglove leaves, were used to slow and strengthen cardiac action and promote diuresis in selected forms of cardiac dropsy. An appropriate indication therefore requires assessment of the heart and circulation, not the mere presence of swelling. Modern digoxin is a standardised cardiac glycoside, used in selected heart failure and atrial fibrillation; it is not equivalent to an arbitrary amount of crude leaf. Important contraindications include certain atrioventricular blocks, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation and particular accessory-pathway arrhythmias. Renal impairment, hypokalaemia and interacting medicines increase the risk of toxicity and demand attention to dosing and monitoring. A very slow pulse, new cardiac arrhythmia, nausea or visual disturbance during treatment calls for assessment, not additional doses to obtain more diuresis.[ref]
Aloes means the dried aloe leaf juice used as a stimulant laxative, not the relatively different leaf gel. Historically it was used for sluggish bowel action and sometimes as an emmenagogue, on theories of pelvic stimulation. The latter is not a present treatment for an unexplained absent period. The recognised current herbal indication is short-term occasional constipation, with limits on duration and age. Contraindications include bowel obstruction, inflammatory bowel disease, unexplained abdominal pain, severe dehydration, pregnancy and breastfeeding. Repeated purgation can disturb water and electrolyte balance; constipation caused by a serious obstruction should not be treated by progressively stronger laxatives.[ref]
Copaiba is an oleoresin, historically given for gonorrhoea and persistent urethral discharge, and sometimes other mucosal complaints. Period selection tended to favour a less acutely inflamed stage; irritation of the stomach and urinary tract, nausea, diarrhoea or a rash argued against continuing it. The contemporary monograph also warns against its use in febrile states. Existing marked urinary irritation is poor ground for an irritant empirical remedy.[ref] Modern gonorrhoea requires appropriate microbiological diagnosis, effective antibiotic treatment and partner management. Current CDC guidance uses ceftriaxone-based treatment; copaiba is not an alternative to eradication of the infection. Apparent reduction of discharge cannot establish microbiological cure.[ref]
Thus the period shorthand is fluid removal by elaterium, selected cardiac treatment by digitalis preparations, bowel stimulation by aloes, and a disputed treatment of mucosal discharge by copaiba. Present interpretation preserves the useful distinction between actions while rejecting the assumption that all four historical indications remain valid.
