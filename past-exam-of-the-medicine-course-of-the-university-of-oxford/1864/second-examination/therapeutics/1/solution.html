 ... Medicine course of the University of Oxford Past exam of the medicine course of the University of Oxford 1864 Second examination Therapeutics 1
OurBigBook.com Words: 341
Support the acute illness, relieve painful arthritis, and repeatedly assess the heart; modern treatment also eradicates the antecedent streptococcal infection and prevents recurrence. Acute rheumatic fever is an inflammatory consequence of preceding Group A streptococcal infection, rather than infection of each painful anatomical joint by the organism. The distinction matters because an antibiotic and an anti-inflammatory drug have different purposes.[ref]
In an 1864 answer, the defensible foundation is rest during the febrile illness, attentive nursing, adequate drinks and food, comfortable support of painful anatomical joints, and relief of pain. The physician would follow the temperature, pulse, breathing, ability to drink and urine output. Examination with a stethoscope should be repeated for a new heart murmur, a pericardial friction rub or signs of heart failure; improvement of the arthritis does not prove that the rheumatic carditis has ended. Historical alkaline treatment with potassium bicarbonate or other alkalis was intended to counter a supposed acid state. Opium was used for severe pain and sleeplessness, with risks of constipation and respiratory depression. Neither that acid theory nor abolition of pain establishes prevention of damage to the heart valves. Contemporary discussion already disputed whether the apparent benefit of alkalis exceeded that of rest and nourishment, and recognised the difficulty of comparing unlike cases.[ref]
Current assessment includes an electrocardiogram and echocardiography, since valvulitis may be present without an obvious heart murmur. A suitable penicillin regimen eradicates residual Group A streptococcal infection; subsequent secondary antibiotic prophylaxis reduces further attacks. An anti-inflammatory drug such as naproxen or ibuprofen treats the established inflammatory symptoms, with choice and monitoring appropriate to age and illness. These measures should not be described as reversing an already scarred heart valve. Heart failure from severe rheumatic carditis needs specialist management; severe Sydenham's chorea may require specific treatment as well as protection, rest and support. Activity is adjusted to the clinical and cardiac findings rather than prescribed as indefinite immobility. Bloodletting, deliberately induced dehydration and historical counterirritation provide no substitute for these measures.[ref]
The treatment outline therefore has two time scales: control the present inflammatory illness and its complications, then prevent another immune-mediated attack. Modern salicylate treatment should not be projected backwards as the settled Oxford practice of December 1864.
