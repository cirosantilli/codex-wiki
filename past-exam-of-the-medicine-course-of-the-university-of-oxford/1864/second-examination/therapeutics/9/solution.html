There is no single reliably poisonous dose for each opium preparation. Identify the preparation and exposure, but diagnose and treat poisoning from its effects, especially depressed breathing. Toxicity depends on the actual alkaloid content, route, age, opioid tolerance, coexisting illness and other depressants. A quantity tolerated by one person may produce dangerous respiratory depression in another. In particular, neither a historical ordinary dose nor a published fatal quantity defines a boundary below which every person is safe.[ref]
For the historical numerical part, separate formulation strength from a poisonous threshold. The relevant British formulations are those associated with the British Pharmacopoeia by the General Medical Council (1864), not a later edition or the bracketed American recipes in The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865). In the following comparisons, a grain is a mass unit and a minim a volume unit; a fluid ounce is 480 minims. These comparisons describe period preparations and are not administration instructions.
  • Opium tincture and the 1864 Vinum Opii are each described as containing the soluble constituents of one grain of dry opium in approximately 14½ minims. Tinctura Camphorae cum Opio contains one grain in half a fluid ounce, or 240 minims. Confusing these liquids therefore creates a large error in exposure.
  • Extractum Opii is an aqueous extract with potency dependent on extraction and starting material. The contemporary comparison gives about half a grain of dry extract as equivalent to one grain of raw opium. Extractum Opii Liquidum contains about one grain of extract in 22 minims. This is an approximate raw-drug equivalence, not an assay of morphine.
  • Pilula Opii contains one part opium in five parts pill mass; Pilula Plumbi cum Opio one in eight. The latter also carries the independent hazard of a lead compound.
  • Pulvis Ipecacuanhae cum Opio, Pulvis Kino cum Opio and Pulvis Cretae Aromaticus cum Opio contain one part opium in ten, twenty and forty parts respectively. Other ingredients do not abolish opioid toxicity.
These strengths make clear how to compare exposures once their quantities are known. For example, if a preparation contains a mass fraction f of dry opium and a mass m of it was taken, its nominal raw-drug content is

Two entries require explicit textual caution. The monograph for Trochisci Opii specifies 72 grains of Extractum Opii divided into 720 lozenges, hence one tenth of a grain of extract per lozenge; its summary table instead says one tenth of a grain of opium. Those are not interchangeable descriptions. Similarly, the Enema Opii recipe specifies 30 minims of opium tincture with two fluid ounces of starch decoction, whereas the summary table describes one grain of opium in two fluid ounces. At the stated tincture strength the recipe contributes about two grains to the mixture, so the discrepancy should not be silently turned into a precise rectal poisonous dose.[ref]
The external preparations also belong to the officinal list: Emplastrum Opii, Linimentum Opii and Unguentum Gallae cum Opio. The plaster contains one part powdered opium to nine parts resin plaster; the liniment mixes equal volumes of opium tincture and soap liniment. The galls ointment is described as approximately one part opium in 14½ parts of ointment. These are not oral medicines. Exposure over damaged skin, contact with mucosa or accidental swallowing cannot be evaluated by assigning a universal number of spoonfuls or applications.[ref]
If preparations derived from the purified alkaloid are included, distinguish them from raw opium: Liquor Morphiae Hydrochloratis contains four grains of morphine hydrochloride per fluid ounce; Suppositoria Morphiae contain a quarter grain each; Trochisci Morphiae and Trochisci Morphiae et Ipecacuanhae contain one thirty-sixth of a grain of the salt per lozenge. A raw-drug fraction is not the same thing as a mass of purified morphine hydrochloride.[ref]
Deep historical narcotism means profound opioid poisoning, with depressed consciousness and dangerously slow or ineffective breathing, often with small pupils. Current priorities are emergency help, an open airway, assisted ventilation and oxygen therapy as required, and prompt naloxone. If there is cardiac arrest, start appropriate cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Repeat naloxone or use an infusion when clinically required, and continue observation because the opioid may outlast the reversal. A transient awakening is not proof of sustained recovery.[ref][ref]
Older attempts to empty the stomach or to rouse an unconscious person must not displace respiratory support. Do not give oral drinks or an emetic to an unconscious person, and do not perform unprotected gastric instrumentation: aspiration can add a second cause of respiratory failure. The concise therapeutic answer is therefore support ventilation and reverse the opioid effect, rather than waiting for a calculated poisonous dose to be exceeded.
