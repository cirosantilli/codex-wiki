Seek and control the infective source, drain accessible collections, support failing organs, and recognise that established pyaemia had a very poor outlook before effective antibiotics. The historical term commonly described severe systemic illness, often after a wound or operation, with recurrent rigors and secondary abscesses. Modern explanations include bacteraemia, an infected thrombus producing septic embolism, and metastatic infection. It is misleading to reduce the condition to an inert quantity of pus freely floating through the blood.
In the 1864 setting, examine the original wound and any painful swollen region for retained infected material, an undrained abscess, dead tissue or obstructed drainage. An accessible collection calls for surgical drainage; a superficially healed wound should not conceal an undrained deeper focus. Remove obvious sources of continuing contamination, provide careful dressings, cleanliness, ventilation, nursing and tolerable nourishment, and relieve pain. Period practice used quinine and alcoholic stimulants as supportive remedies. A surgical report covering July 1863 to October 1864 records these measures in the Royal Infirmary; its publication in 1865 documents the period rather than supplying an Oxford model answer.[ref] Such measures did not reliably sterilise the blood or inaccessible secondary abscesses. Neither repeated purging nor bloodletting has a sound role in an already depleted, seriously infected person. Later organised surgical antisepsis should not be presented as an established system available at this examination.
Current care treats the presentation as possible sepsis with disseminated infection. Assess airway, breathing, circulation, mental state and organ function urgently. Obtain appropriate blood cultures and source samples without delaying necessary antibiotic treatment; investigate the original wound and likely secondary sites. Ultrasonography or other imaging can locate a collection for drainage. Persistent bacteraemia, embolic manifestations or a new heart murmur should prompt assessment for infective endocarditis, often including echocardiography. Source control may require drainage, removal of infected material or debridement. Initial antibiotics are chosen for the likely source and resistance risks, then narrowed using microbiological evidence.
Provide oxygen therapy when needed and carefully reassessed intravenous fluids; persistent circulatory failure requires critical-care treatment rather than indiscriminate fluid loading. Monitor urine output, renal function, blood pressure and response to treatment. The modern prognosis depends on source, organ injury and speed and adequacy of treatment, so the historical description alone does not support a numerical prediction.[ref]
