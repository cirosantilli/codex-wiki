The principal period groups were digitalis preparations, squill, broom, juniper oil, buchu, nitrous ether spirit, and several soluble potassium salts. They were grouped by observed promotion of urine flow, rather than by one common mechanism. The following are historical adult quantities per administration, expressed in the contemporary units; they are not present-day prescriptions.
  • Digitalis preparations: powdered leaves, ½–2 grains; Infusum Digitalis, 2–4 fluid drachms; Tinctura Digitalis, 5–40 minims; digitalin, 1/40–1/10 grain in the period table. Historical digitalin was not simply a modern pure digoxin product.
  • Squill: powder, 1–2 grains; Pilula Scillae Composita, 5–10 grains; Tinctura Scillae, 10–20 minims; Syrupus Scillae, ½–1½ fluid drachms.
  • Broom: Decoctum Scoparii, 1–3 fluid ounces; Succus Scoparii, 1–4 fluid drachms.
  • Buchu: powdered leaves, 20–40 grains; Infusum Bucco, 1–2 fluid ounces; Tinctura Bucco, 1–2 fluid drachms.
  • Juniper oil: Spiritus Juniperi, 10–30 minims in the 1864 commentary. This unusually concentrated spirit contains one volume of juniper oil in ten; its dose must not be borrowed from a later, weaker formula.
  • Nitrous ether spirit, Spiritus Aetheris Nitrosi: 30 minims to 2 fluid drachms.
  • Potassium acetate: 10–20 grains; potassium citrate: 20–60 grains; potassium nitrate: 5–20 grains; potassium tartrate: 10–60 grains; potassium hydrogen tartrate: 20–60 grains.
  • Ammonium acetate solution, Liquor Ammoniae Acetatis: 10–40 minims of the concentrated British preparation, diluted for administration.

Other recognised period agents included dandelion and copaiba. The dandelion decoction was given in quantities of 1–2 fluid ounces, its soft extract in quantities of 10–30 grains, and its preserved juice in quantities of ½–2 fluid drachms. Copaiba was given in quantities of 15–30 minims, and copaiba oil in quantities of 5–20 minims. Their historical inclusion does not establish that they are suitable substitutes for current diuretic therapy. [ref]
The distinction between preparations matters more than a single “dose of the plant”: an infusion, tincture, juice and powder have different concentrations. Likewise, a salt's small historical diuretic dose is not its larger purgative dose. A modern explanation also separates a direct effect on renal tubules from improved cardiac output in appropriate heart failure: digitalis preparations cannot be understood simply as agents that make every kidney excrete more water. Modern digoxin is used for selected cardiac indications, rather than as a general treatment for low urine output. [ref]
