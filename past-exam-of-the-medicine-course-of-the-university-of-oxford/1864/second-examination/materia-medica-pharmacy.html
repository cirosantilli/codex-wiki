Original question.
What officinal preparations of the British Pharmacopœia contain Opium, and in what proportions ?

Solution

Words: 388
1 OurBigBook.com
The 1864 preparations contain either crude opium or its aqueous Extractum Opii; those quantities must be distinguished from a quantity of isolated morphine. The following are the officinal opium preparations. Ratios for solids mean the mass fraction in the compounded preparation; liquid strengths specify mass per volume or the actual ingredients used.
  • Emplastrum Opii: powdered opium in resin plaster: 1 in 10.
  • Enema Opii: opium tincture 30 minims with starch decoction 2 fluid ounces. Thus the tincture is 1 volume to 32 volumes of vehicle, before mixing.
  • Extractum Opii: aqueous solvent extraction of 1 avoirdupois pound of sliced opium with 6 Imperial pints of distilled water, in three successive portions, followed by evaporation. The prescribed starting quantities do not impose a fixed yield or a universal crude-drug-to-extract potency ratio.
  • Extractum Opii Liquidum: Extractum Opii 1 avoirdupois ounce, distilled water 17 fluid ounces, rectified spirit 3 fluid ounces: 1 ounce of extract to the pint of liquid ingredients.
  • Linimentum Opii: equal volumes of opium tincture and soap liniment: half the tincture's nominal opium strength.
  • Pilula Opii: powdered opium with hard soap: 1 in 5 of the nominal pill ingredients.
  • Pilula Plumbi cum Opio: powdered opium with lead acetate and confection of roses: 1 in 8.
  • Pulvis Cretae Aromaticus cum Opio: powdered opium with aromatic chalk powder: 1 in 40.
  • Pulvis Ipecacuanhae cum Opio: powdered opium with ipecacuanha and potassium sulfate: 1 in 10.
  • Pulvis Kino cum Opio: powdered opium with kino and cinnamon: 1 in 20.
[ref]
  • Tinctura Camphorae cum Opio: opium 40 grains to 1 Imperial pint of proof spirit, with benzoic acid, camphor and anise oil: 2 grains of opium per fluid ounce.
  • Opium tincture, Tinctura Opii: powdered opium 1½ avoirdupois ounces to 1 Imperial pint of proof spirit. Nominally this is 32.8125 grains per fluid ounce, or approximately 1 grain per 14.63 minims.
  • Trochisci Opii: 72 grains of Extractum Opii in 720 lozenges: one tenth of a grain of extract per lozenge.
  • Unguentum Gallae cum Opio: powdered opium 32 grains added to 1 avoirdupois ounce of gall ointment. The actual nominal total is 437.5+32=469.5 grains, giving 32/469.5 by mass, approximately 1 in 14.67; “32 grains to the ounce of ointment taken” is not 32 grains per final ounce.
  • Vinum Opii: powdered opium 1½ avoirdupois ounces, sherry 1 Imperial pint: the tincture's nominal opium proportion, in a different solvent. This formula uses crude opium, not Extractum Opii.
[ref]
The arithmetic uses the new avoirdupois ounce of 437.5 grains, not the old apothecaries' ounce of 480 grains. These are historical formulation proportions, not modern assays of morphine delivered after solvent extraction. Confectio Opii was omitted in 1864, and this edition's Vinum Opii contains no spices. The separate morphine hydrochloride preparations—Liquor Morphiae Hydrochloratis, Suppositoria Morphiae, Trochisci Morphiae, and Trochisci Morphiae et Ipecacuanhae—contain an isolated alkaloid salt rather than crude opium. [ref]

Original question.
Mention the chief Diuretics used in England, and give the doses of their several preparations.

Solution

Words: 345
2 OurBigBook.com
The principal period groups were digitalis preparations, squill, broom, juniper oil, buchu, nitrous ether spirit, and several soluble potassium salts. They were grouped by observed promotion of urine flow, rather than by one common mechanism. The following are historical adult quantities per administration, expressed in the contemporary units; they are not present-day prescriptions.
  • Digitalis preparations: powdered leaves, ½–2 grains; Infusum Digitalis, 2–4 fluid drachms; Tinctura Digitalis, 5–40 minims; digitalin, 1/40–1/10 grain in the period table. Historical digitalin was not simply a modern pure digoxin product.
  • Squill: powder, 1–2 grains; Pilula Scillae Composita, 5–10 grains; Tinctura Scillae, 10–20 minims; Syrupus Scillae, ½–1½ fluid drachms.
  • Broom: Decoctum Scoparii, 1–3 fluid ounces; Succus Scoparii, 1–4 fluid drachms.
  • Buchu: powdered leaves, 20–40 grains; Infusum Bucco, 1–2 fluid ounces; Tinctura Bucco, 1–2 fluid drachms.
  • Juniper oil: Spiritus Juniperi, 10–30 minims in the 1864 commentary. This unusually concentrated spirit contains one volume of juniper oil in ten; its dose must not be borrowed from a later, weaker formula.
  • Nitrous ether spirit, Spiritus Aetheris Nitrosi: 30 minims to 2 fluid drachms.
  • Potassium acetate: 10–20 grains; potassium citrate: 20–60 grains; potassium nitrate: 5–20 grains; potassium tartrate: 10–60 grains; potassium hydrogen tartrate: 20–60 grains.
  • Ammonium acetate solution, Liquor Ammoniae Acetatis: 10–40 minims of the concentrated British preparation, diluted for administration.
[ref] [ref]
Other recognised period agents included dandelion and copaiba. The dandelion decoction was given in quantities of 1–2 fluid ounces, its soft extract in quantities of 10–30 grains, and its preserved juice in quantities of ½–2 fluid drachms. Copaiba was given in quantities of 15–30 minims, and copaiba oil in quantities of 5–20 minims. Their historical inclusion does not establish that they are suitable substitutes for current diuretic therapy. [ref]
The distinction between preparations matters more than a single “dose of the plant”: an infusion, tincture, juice and powder have different concentrations. Likewise, a salt's small historical diuretic dose is not its larger purgative dose. A modern explanation also separates a direct effect on renal tubules from improved cardiac output in appropriate heart failure: digitalis preparations cannot be understood simply as agents that make every kidney excrete more water. Modern digoxin is used for selected cardiac indications, rather than as a general treatment for low urine output. [ref]

Original question.
Enumerate the “mixtures” for which directions are given in the British Pharmacopœia and state their composition.

Solution

Words: 267
3 OurBigBook.com
The seven named mixtures of the 1864 British Pharmacopoeia are Mistura Ammoniaci, Mistura Amygdalae, Mistura Creasoti, Mistura Cretae, Mistura Ferri Composita, Mistura Guaiaci and Mistura Scammonii. Their compositions are as follows; avoirdupois ounces are used for solids and fluid ounces for liquids.
  • Mistura Ammoniaci: ammoniacum ¼ avoirdupois ounce, distilled water 8 fluid ounces, rubbed together and strained. Its own gum helps disperse its resin; ammoniacum is not Ammonia. [ref]
  • Mistura Amygdalae: compound almond powder 2½ avoirdupois ounces, distilled water 1 Imperial pint. The compound powder is made from sweet almonds 8 parts, sugar 4 parts and gum arabic 1 part by mass. Trituration produces an emulsion. [ref]
  • Mistura Creasoti: creosote 16 minims, glacial acetic acid 16 minims, Spiritus Juniperi ½ fluid drachm, syrup 1 fluid ounce, distilled water 15 fluid ounces. Here the spirit has the particularly strong 1864 juniper oil content. [ref]
  • Mistura Cretae: prepared chalk and powdered gum arabic, ¼ avoirdupois ounce each; syrup ½ fluid ounce; cinnamon water 7½ fluid ounces. The chalk is suspended, not dissolved. [ref]
  • Mistura Ferri Composita: powdered myrrh 60 grains, potassium carbonate 25 grains, rose water 8 fluid ounces, ferrous sulfate 30 grains, Spiritus Myristicae 1 fluid drachm, sugar 60 grains. Triturate the myrrh with the spirit and potassium carbonate, then add the rose water with sugar, followed by ferrous sulfate; keep the resulting preparation stoppered. Formation of ferrous carbonate and its subsequent oxidation explain why the order and storage matter. [ref]
  • Mistura Guaiaci: powdered guaiacum resin ½ avoirdupois ounce, sugar ½ avoirdupois ounce, powdered gum arabic ¼ avoirdupois ounce, cinnamon water 1 Imperial pint. The gum assists dispersion of the poorly water-soluble resin. [ref]
  • Mistura Scammonii: scammony resin 4 grains with unskimmed milk 2 fluid ounces, triturated into a uniform dispersion. [ref]
The category is an official preparation name, not every possible bedside combination of medicines. Camphor water and acacia mucilage are not additional entries under Mistura: the 1864 names are Aqua Camphorae and Mucilago Acaciae. [ref]

Original question.
What is the composition of the Pulvis Jalapæ compositus, of the Pilula Scillæ composita, of the Liquor Morphiæ hydrochloratis, and of the Liquor Strychniæ of the British Pharmacopœia ?

Solution

Words: 275
4 OurBigBook.com
The compositions must be specified ingredient by ingredient; the two alkaloidal solutions contain 4 grains per nominal fluid ounce, but one contains a morphine salt and the other strychnine.
Pulvis Jalapae Compositus contains powdered jalap 5 avoirdupois ounces, potassium hydrogen tartrate 9 avoirdupois ounces, and powdered ginger 1 avoirdupois ounce. Thus the mass proportions are 5:9:1, and jalap supplies one third of the powder's mass. This is a compound purgative, not pure jalap resin. [ref]
Pilula Scillae Composita contains powdered squill 1¼ avoirdupois ounces, powdered ginger, ammoniacum and hard soap 1 avoirdupois ounce each, with treacle 2 avoirdupois ounces, or sufficient to make a pill mass. Using the specified 2 avoirdupois ounces of treacle, the whole is 6¼ avoirdupois ounces and contains one fifth squill by mass. The allowance of sufficient treacle makes consistency a compounding requirement, not a claim that every moist pill batch has an analytically exact 20% squill content. [ref]
Liquor Morphiae Hydrochloratis contains morphine hydrochloride 4 grains, dilute hydrochloric acid 8 minims, rectified spirit 2 fluid drachms and distilled water 6 fluid drachms. Its conventional strength is ½ grain of morphine hydrochloride per fluid drachm, not ½ grain of free morphine. The small quantity of hydrochloric acid maintains an acid solution. [ref]
Liquor Strychniae contains strychnine 4 grains, dilute hydrochloric acid 6 minims, rectified spirit 2 fluid drachms and distilled water 6 fluid drachms. Dissolve the strychnine in the acid mixed with part of the water, using the prescribed assistance of heat, then add the spirit and remaining water. Its conventional strength is ½ grain of strychnine per fluid drachm. Acid promotes solubility by forming a soluble salt of the alkaloid. [ref]
For the last two solutions, the stated historical strengths use the nominal 8 fluid drachms of spirit and water. The extra few minims of dilute acid must still appear in the actual recipe; treating all ingredient volumes as perfectly additive would not give an exact modern analytical concentration.

Original question.
Name the medicines which impart colouring matter to the excretions.

Solution

Words: 348
5 OurBigBook.com
The chief historical examples are rhubarb, senna, santonin and logwood for urine; iron preparations, bismuth preparations and charcoal for darkening the faeces. There are two different processes: a substance absorbed and subsequently excreted can colour a secretion, while material remaining in the intestinal lumen can colour the stools without demonstrating systemic drug absorption.
  • Rhubarb colours urine yellow to yellow-brown; the colour becomes redder in an alkaline solution. The contemporary account also reports coloration of sweat and breast milk. This is a historical observation about passage of colouring matter, not a claim that every constituent passes unchanged into every secretion. [ref]
  • Senna can give urine a yellow-brown or red-brown colour. The effect is still recognised in current medicinal information. [ref]
  • Santonin, used historically as an anthelmintic, can make urine conspicuously yellow. This must be distinguished from the drug's separate effect on colour vision: a yellow-looking visual field is not itself excretion of a yellow pigment. [ref]
  • Logwood can give urine a pink or reddish colour, particularly when it is an alkaline solution; the historical description notes that addition of Ammonia brings out the colour in a previously acid specimen. [ref]
  • Iron preparations can darken faeces; bismuth preparations also cause dark or black stools. In these examples the result need not be a pre-existing vegetable dye simply passing unchanged through the body. Current information confirms black stools with oral iron and with the modern bismuth medicine bismuth subsalicylate; the latter is corroboration of the colour effect, not an identification of that modern formulation with the 1864 bismuth articles. [ref] [ref]
  • Charcoal supplies a black material directly to the bowel contents. Its appearance in the stools does not require its absorption and subsequent renal excretion.
Colour alone does not identify either the drug or a disease. A medicinal pigment can resemble blood or a bile pigment; pH, timing after administration and appropriate chemical or microscopic examination distinguish possibilities. Conversely, a history of taking a colouring medicine does not prove that an observed specimen contains no blood. These examples describe pharmacological and historical observations, not results from an unseen specimen.

Original question.
What are the preparations of Iron in the British Pharmacopœia ? For what purpose is Ferri Peroxidum Hydratum used ? How is it ordered to be made ?

Solution

Words: 370
6 OurBigBook.com
The 1864 iron list includes metallic, ferrous, ferric and compound preparations; its wet Ferri Peroxidum Hydratum was used historically as an antidote to arsenic poisoning. It must be distinguished from dried Ferri Peroxidum and from later editions' reassignment of similar Latin names.
The officinal articles and preparations are:
  • metallic forms: iron wire, Ferrum, and Ferrum Redactum;
  • simple salts and oxides: Ferri Arsenias, Ferri Carbonas Saccharata, Ferri Iodidum, Ferri Oxidum Magneticum, Ferri Peroxidum, Ferri Peroxidum Hydratum, Ferri Phosphas, Ferri Sulphas, Ferri Sulphas Exsiccata and Ferri Sulphas Granulata;
  • complex salts: Ferri et Ammoniae Citras, Ferri et Quiniae Citras and Ferrum Tartaratum;
  • solutions: Liquor Ferri Perchloridi, Liquor Ferri Pernitratis and the preparative Liquor Ferri Persulphatis;
  • compounded forms: Mistura Ferri Composita, Pilula Ferri Carbonatis, Pilula Ferri Iodidi, Syrupus Ferri Iodidi, Syrupus Ferri Phosphatis, Tinctura Ferri Perchloridi and Vinum Ferri.
[ref] [ref]
Prepare the hydrated peroxide by inducing precipitation from the ferric solution with sodium hydroxide, collecting and washing the wet precipitate, and preserving it without drying in a covered vessel. The starting solution is Liquor Ferri Persulphatis, whose “persulphate” here means ferric sulfate, not a modern peroxydisulfate salt. Add the appropriately diluted ferric solution to the alkaline liquor with stirring, allow the precipitate to settle, collect it on a calico filter, and wash with distilled water until the washings no longer give a precipitate with barium chloride. This tests removal of soluble sulfate. The resulting fresh, hydrated material is kept wet; drying gives the separate dried preparation. [ref]
In modern chemical notation the precipitation can be idealised as
Fe3++3OH−⟶Fe(OH)3​(s).
(1)
The fresh solid is a hydrated ferric oxide material with variable water content; the equation is not a claim that every pharmaceutical batch is one pure, crystalline iron hydroxide. Its historical use depended on binding dissolved arsenic into less available material in the digestive tract, especially the freshly precipitated, wet state. It was not a universal antidote to every metal or every stage of absorbed arsenic poisoning. Contemporary discussion explicitly identified the new hydrated peroxide as an arsenic antidote. [ref] [ref]
Modern treatment of significant arsenic poisoning concerns systemic toxicity and may involve specialist-directed chelation therapy; a nineteenth-century ferric paste is not the modern treatment standard. [ref]

Original question.
What classes of medicines are fitted for preparation by means of decoction and of infusion respectively ? How are liquid extracts made ?

Solution

Words: 366
7 OurBigBook.com
Decoction suits material whose useful constituents are water-soluble and withstand boiling; infusion avoids sustained boiling and can better preserve delicate or volatile constituents. The choice depends on both the plant's structure and the chemistry of the constituent being extracted.
Hard bark, wood and many kinds of root need comminution and more prolonged contact with hot water to penetrate their tissues; a decoction can assist extraction when the desired material is stable. Boiling is unsuitable when it drives off a needed volatile oil, decomposes the active constituent, or extracts an unwanted excess of starch or other material. A hard root is therefore not automatically an instruction to boil. Period examples suitable for decoction include oak bark, logwood and pareira root; appropriate infusions include aromatic cloves and chamomile. Cold infusion of calumba avoids dissolving its starch, illustrating that suitability depends on the desired selectivity of solvent extraction, not merely on whether a drug is a leaf or a root. [ref]
For an infusion, the appropriately divided drug is left in a measured solvent for a prescribed time, commonly in a covered vessel. Hot water assists extraction, while a cover reduces loss of volatile matter. Cold infusion can be chosen when heat is undesirable. The result must be strained or filtered; time, temperature, particle size and solvent quantity affect its strength. Neither method guarantees that every constituent is extracted in the same proportion.
A liquid extract is made by extracting the specified drug with a suitable solvent, separating the liquid from the exhausted material, and bringing the extract to its prescribed concentration and volume. Maceration can precede percolation; expression or washing recovers retained liquid. A percolator passes fresh solvent through a packed bed, rather than indefinitely soaking the drug in an increasingly saturated liquid. Where concentration is necessary, controlled evaporation limits damage; an alcoholic fraction can be reserved or recombined as the monograph directs. Different liquid extracts can use aqueous, alcoholic or ethereal processes. For example, the liquid extract of male fern is an ethereal extraction of a resinous and oily fraction, whereas the liquid extract of opium is compounded from a previously made aqueous extract. These are chemically different operations. There is no warrant for assigning all 1864 extracts one modern universal drug-to-extract ratio. [ref]

Original question.
What is the temperature at which Linseed, Mustard, Charcoal, and Yeast Poultices are to be made ?

Solution

Words: 251
8 OurBigBook.com
For the contemporary formulary, use boiling water for the linseed, charcoal and mustard poultices, and water at 100°F for the yeast poultice. These are preparation temperatures, not temperatures at which boiling material is placed on skin.
For Cataplasma Lini, hot water hydrates the ground linseed into a cohesive moist mass; the contemporary account also notes the addition of olive oil when oil has been removed from the meal. For Cataplasma Carbonis, the hot bread-and-linseed basis is made with boiling water, then the charcoal is incorporated and part used at the surface. At ordinary atmospheric pressure the boiling-water preparation temperature is approximately 212∘F=100∘C​. [ref]
For Cataplasma Fermenti, the stated 100∘F≈37.8∘C​ permits the yeast to ferment the flour mixture. Boiling would injure its fermenting activity. The flour, yeast and warm water are mixed and kept warm while the mass rises. This is a historical compounding process, not evidence that a fermented dressing treats every wound. [ref]
The mustard entry requires a historical qualification. The contemporary British recipe reproduced by the text directs powdered mustard and linseed to be mixed, then stirred into boiling water; the same text observes that excessive heat impairs development of the pungent volatile oil. Thus “boiling” is the contemporary formula's instruction, while lukewarm preparation better preserves development of mustard's pungent volatile oil. These are different claims. The later practical preference for lukewarm water should not silently replace the 1864 recipe, nor should that recipe be mistaken for optimal modern biochemical practice. [ref]

Original question.
Explain the principle of Volumetric Analysis.

Solution

Words: 223
9 OurBigBook.com
Volumetric analysis determines an amount of substance from the measured volume of a reagent of known strength that reacts with it in a known proportion. A measured aliquot is placed in a suitable vessel, the standard solution is delivered from a burette, and the volume required to reach the chosen titration endpoint is read. A pipette measures the aliquot; a volumetric flask prepares or dilutes the standard solution.
For a reaction aA+bB⟶products, if the concentration of the titrant B is cB​ and the corrected volume delivered is VB​, then
nA​=ba​cB​VB​​.
(2)
With volume in litres and concentration in moles per litre, the answer is an amount in moles. The corresponding mass is mA​=MA​nA​, using the appropriate molar mass. For an unknown solution of aliquot volume VA​,
cA​=bVA​acB​VB​​​.
(3)
These are modern symbols for the same proportional reasoning that a nineteenth-century analyst could express with equivalent weights and a specified standard solution.
[ref]
A suitable reaction is sufficiently rapid, complete and selective, with a recognisable endpoint. In an acid-base titration, an indicator can change colour near neutralization; other reactions use precipitation, redox or a colour intrinsic to a reagent. The observed titration endpoint must represent the required equivalence point closely enough. The reagent's nominal preparation alone is insufficient if its concentration changes in storage; standardisation of a solution, a reagent blank where needed, and correct reading of the meniscus control systematic error. A titration measures the intended analyte only insofar as interfering substances have been excluded or accounted for.

Original question.
Give the equivalents of the weights and measures employed in the British Pharmacopœia, in French weights and measures.

Solution

Words: 184
10 OurBigBook.com
The 1864 solid units are the avoirdupois pound, avoirdupois ounce and grain; the liquid units are the Imperial measures. The grain is unchanged, while the old apothecaries' pound and ounce are not the units of the new formulary. [ref]
The relations for solids are
1lb=16oz=7000gr,1oz=437.5gr.
(4)
Their approximate French metric equivalents are:
  • one grain: 0.0648g​, about 64.8mg;
  • one avoirdupois ounce: 28.35g​;
  • one avoirdupois pound: 453.59g​.
For liquids,
1gal=8pt,1pt=20floz,1floz=8fldr,1fldr=60min.
(5)
The approximate metric volumes are:
  • one minim: 0.0592mL​;
  • one fluid drachm: 3.552mL​;
  • one fluid ounce: 28.41mL​;
  • one Imperial pint: 568.26mL​;
  • one Imperial gallon: 4.5461L​.
A millilitre is a cubic centimetre, and a litre is a cubic decimetre. These displayed equivalents are rounded; small differences from a period table's last digits reflect rounding and historical calibration conventions. A fluid ounce is a volume and an avoirdupois ounce is a mass. They cannot be interchanged for an arbitrary liquid without its density. Likewise, a drop is not a defined minim: its volume depends on the liquid and the dropper.
[ref]
