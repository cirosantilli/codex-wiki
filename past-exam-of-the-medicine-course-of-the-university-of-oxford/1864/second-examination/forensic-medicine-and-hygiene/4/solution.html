Oxalic acid poisoning may produce corrosive gastrointestinal injury, early circulatory collapse and later kidney injury; no single post-mortem appearance proves the diagnosis. The severity of local injury and systemic poisoning need not be identical in every case.
After ingestion, burning pain in the mouth, throat or upper abdomen may be followed by vomiting, sometimes containing blood, abdominal tenderness and diarrhoea. Weakness, a small rapid pulse, cold clammy skin and collapse may develop. A dilute preparation may produce less obvious surface injury than concentrated oxalic acid, while still giving systemic poisoning. Current chemical safety information recognizes both corrosive effects and disturbance of calcium balance.[ref]
The absorbed oxalate can bind calcium and form calcium oxalate. A fall in ionized calcium can contribute to neuromuscular disturbance and cardiac instability, but hypocalcaemia must not be asserted to occur in every case or inferred from a symptom alone. If the person survives the early illness, acute kidney injury can follow. In modern pathological investigations, calcium oxalate crystals in renal tubules and associated tubular damage provide a mechanism for this later complication.[ref][ref]
At autopsy, examine the mouth, oesophagus and stomach for corrosive changes. Depending on concentration and survival, the mucous membrane may be pale or discoloured, inflamed, eroded, haemorrhagic or softened; blood-stained contents and severe gastric damage may be present. Perforation is possible, but is not a necessary finding. The pre-examination report Case of Criminal Poisoning with Oxalic Acid, in Which Perforation of the Stomach Took Place by Henry Duncan Littlejohn documents a particular severe example with marked softening and perforation. It is evidence that these changes can occur, rather than a description that every fatal case must reproduce.[ref]
Examine the kidneys as well as the gastrointestinal tract; histological tubular changes and crystals may matter especially after delayed death. Nevertheless, calcium oxalate deposition is not unique to direct ingestion of oxalic acid, and gross corrosive damage is not unique to this poison. Preserve material for controlled toxicological analysis, and interpret the chemical findings alongside the history and pathological distribution. The older classification of oxalic acid as a “narcotic” does not adequately explain the corrosive, electrolyte and renal mechanisms now recognized.
