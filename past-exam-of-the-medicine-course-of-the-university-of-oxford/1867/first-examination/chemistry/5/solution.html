Atomic theory explains fixed composition by fixed numbers of chemical atoms, and multiple proportions by different small whole-number combinations of the same chemical atoms. The two laws are empirical regularities which the theory explains; they are not themselves direct observations of individual chemical atoms.
For the law of definite proportions, suppose a pure chemical compound has composition Ax​By​. If the atomic masses are mA​ and mB​, every formula unit contains masses xmA​ and ymB​. Enlarging the sample multiplies both by the same number, so
mass of Amass of B​=xmA​ymB​​.​
(11)
The mass ratio is therefore independent of sample size or preparation route, provided the same pure stoichiometric chemical compound is obtained. Ordinary water supplies an example: its hydrogen-to-oxygen mass ratio is approximately 1:8.
For the law of multiple proportions, compare Ax​By​ and Au​Bv​. At a fixed mass of A, the masses of B are in the ratio
v/uy/x​=vxyu​.​
(12)
This is a ratio of integers, and is often a ratio of small integers for simple chemical compounds. With carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide, about 12 mass units of carbon combine with about 16 and 32 of oxygen: the oxygen masses are in the ratio 1:2. The chemical formulas explain why adding a second oxygen chemical atom doubles the oxygen contribution without changing the carbon contribution.
The laws alone do not uniquely determine the correct chemical formulas or the atomic weights; independent information is needed to distinguish, for example, one formula unit from a multiple of it. Modern qualifications include variations in isotopic composition and non-stoichiometric compounds. These refine the simple nineteenth-century formulation without removing its usefulness for pure compounds of fixed composition.
