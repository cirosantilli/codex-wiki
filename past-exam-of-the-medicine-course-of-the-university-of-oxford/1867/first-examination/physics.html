Original question.
If two parallel forces act upon a rigid body in opposite directions, state how to determine the magnitude and position of their single resultant.
Discuss the case in which the two forces become equal in magnitude.
Describe the arrangement and action of the muscles by which the motion of the eye-ball is produced.

Solution

Words: 317
1 OurBigBook.com
Let an upward force P act along x=0 and a downward parallel force Q along x=d>0. An equivalent resultant force must preserve both total force and torque. Its signed vertical component is R=P−Q, and taking torques about the first line gives RxR​=−Qd. Thus
R=P−Q,xR​=−P−QQd​(P=Q).​
(1)
Its magnitude is ∣P−Q∣ and its direction agrees with the larger force. Its line lies outside the two original lines on the side of that larger force; for example, P>Q gives xR​<0. The products of the original forces and their perpendicular distances from this line are equal.
If P=Q=0 and d=0, the total force vanishes but the torque is −Pd. No single force is equivalent to this system: it is a force couple of magnitude Pd. Its torque is independent of the reference point because translating that point changes the torque by the cross product of the shift and the zero resultant force. Equal opposite forces on the same line instead cancel completely.
The eye is rotated by six extraocular muscles. The rectus muscles approach it from behind; the oblique muscles have oblique lines of pull, with the superior oblique muscle tendon redirected by an orbital pulley. Attachments on the sclera give lever arms about the rotation centre. In the straight-ahead position their actions are as follows; vertical and torsional contributions vary with gaze direction.[ref]
Extraocular muscleActionsInnervation
Medial rectus muscleTurns the eye inwardOculomotor nerve, III
Lateral rectus muscleTurns the eye outwardAbducens nerve, VI
Superior rectus muscleElevates; also turns inward and rotates the upper pole inwardOculomotor nerve, III
Inferior rectus muscleDepresses; also turns inward and rotates the upper pole outwardOculomotor nerve, III
Superior oblique muscleRotates the upper pole inward; also depresses and turns outwardTrochlear nerve, IV
Inferior oblique muscleRotates the upper pole outward; also elevates and turns outwardOculomotor nerve, III
Coordinated contraction selects the desired rotation and balances unwanted components. This illustrates torque control; the full ocular geometry is not simply the parallel-force pair of the first calculation.

Original question.
When a force produces motion in a body, how is the work done expressed in terms of the force and the circumstances of the motion ? Explain generally the appropriateness of this expression.
When a man walks along a horizontal road, in what way does he perform work ?

Solution

Words: 157
2 OurBigBook.com
For constant force F and displacement s, the work is
W=F⋅s=Fscosθ.​
(2)
For a variable force, W=∫C​F⋅dr. The work-energy theorem explains this choice: Newton's second law for fixed mass gives F⋅dr=ma⋅vdt=d(mv2/2). The component along the motion changes kinetic energy; a perpendicular force can change direction without doing work. Exerting a force on a stationary object likewise does no work on it.
Walking on level ground still requires muscular work. The centre of mass rises and falls during each step; the limbs gain and lose kinetic energy; and muscles perform positive work that replaces energy dissipated in impacts and tissue motion. Air resistance can add another cost. Returning to the same height makes the net change of gravitational potential energy zero, not every intermediate work contribution.
At ideal no-slip contact with an immovable road, the contact point is instantaneously stationary, so the road's force does no work through that point. The muscles convert internal chemical energy into motion. Zero net gain of height is compatible with positive muscular work and energy expenditure.

Original question.
If a body falls in vacuo, express the velocity, and the distance of the body from the starting point, at any instant in terms of the time which has elapsed since motion commenced.
Account for the comparatively small velocity of a drop of rain when it reaches the earth, and state what is the effective force upon the drop at that time.

Solution

Words: 113
3 OurBigBook.com
Take downward displacement as positive and gravitational acceleration g as constant. In a vacuum, mdv/dt=mg, so with initial downward velocity u,
v=u+gt,s=ut+21​gt2.​
(3)
For release from rest these become v=gt and s=gt2/2.
A raindrop encounters an increasing upward drag force as its speed grows. At terminal velocity, gravitational weight is balanced by drag force and the small buoyancy contribution:
Fnet​=mg−Fdrag​−Fbuoyancy​=0,a=0.​
(4)
The drop remains in motion: zero acceleration does not imply zero velocity. In a quadratic drag force model, Fdrag​=ρair​CD​Av2/2, giving vt​=2(m−ρair​V)g/(ρair​CD​A)​. That formula assumes a fixed effective area and drag coefficient; sufficiently small drops or deforming large drops need an appropriate model. The question's “effective force” at settled terminal velocity is the approximately zero resultant force, not its nonzero gravitational weight.

Original question.
Describe some experiment illustrating the property, possessed by a fluid, of transmitting pressure applied to it equally in all directions.
Should the pulse occur simultaneously in all parts of the body ? What is the result of experiment on this point ?

Solution

Words: 213
4 OurBigBook.com
Pascal's law concerns a pressure increment imposed on a confined fluid at rest. A water-filled vessel with small openings in differently oriented walls and a close-fitting piston illustrates it: pressing the piston produces jets in different directions, showing that pressure acts normally to each local surface. Compare ports at the same height to remove differences of hydrostatic pressure. Pressure gauges at equally high ports show the same imposed increment after the disturbance settles.
For an ideal hydraulic press with piston areas A1​,A2​,
Δp=A1​F1​​=A2​F2​​,F2​=F1​A2​/A1​.​
(5)
Conservation of fluid volume gives A1​s1​=A2​s2​, hence F1​s1​=F2​s2​: the larger output force accompanies a smaller displacement and does not create energy.
The arterial pulse is not simultaneous throughout the body. Pascal's law is an equilibrium statement, not instantaneous transmission. An arterial pulse travels through coupled blood and elastic arterial walls. Synchronized recordings at two sites show different arrival times of corresponding waveform features. For effective propagation distance L, pulse wave velocity is cp​=L/Δt. It differs from the transport velocity of individual blood cells.[ref]
A bench demonstration uses an elastic liquid-filled tube, two pressure sensors at known positions, and a short pressure pulse at one end. The delayed second recording tests finite pressure wave propagation independently of the eventual static pressure balance.

Original question.
Enunciate Boyle's law connecting the volume and tension of a gas.
Explain the process of respiration, as far as it is due to the motion of the Diaphragm.
Assuming the lungs to contain 1600 cubic centimetres of air at the termination of an expiration, and 400 cubic centimetres to be introduced by each inspiration, if, after an expiration a vertical tube be placed in the mouth, with its lower end dipping in mercury, and, the nose being stopped, the act of inspiration be performed, to what height will the mercury rise in the tube ? The height of the mercury in the barometer being 760 m.m., and the air in the tube being neglected.

Solution

Words: 141
5 OurBigBook.com
Boyle's law states that a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature obeys
pV=constant,​
(6)
where p is absolute pressure, not gauge pressure. It is an ideal-gas approximation, not an assertion that every rapid compression is isothermal.
The respiratory diaphragm contracts and lowers its dome, increasing thoracic volume. Mechanical coupling through the pleural space lets the lungs expand; alveolar pressure falls below atmospheric pressure, drawing air in. Relaxation of the respiratory diaphragm and elastic recoil drive quiet expiration. These respiratory mechanics describe ventilation, distinct from chemical cellular respiration.[ref]
For the stipulated sealed, isothermal gas, initial and final volumes are 1600 and 2000cm3. Expressing absolute pressure as a mercury-column height,
pf​=76020001600​=608mmHg.
(7)
The mercury column is supported by the atmospheric-to-internal pressure difference:
h=760−608=152mm=15.2cm.​
(8)
The specified volume change belongs to the historical idealized model. Real tube-air volume, temperature changes and limits on respiratory-muscle pressure generation would affect a physical apparatus.

Original question.
How are differences of quality in musical sounds of the same pitch accounted for ?
Describe briefly the apparatus in the human ear which, it seems probable, is employed in producing the sensation of quality in musical sounds.

Solution

Words: 137
6 OurBigBook.com
Equal pitch need not mean equal frequency spectrum. For an ideal periodic sound wave of fundamental frequency f, a Fourier series has the form
p(t)=∑n≥1​An​cos(2πnft+ϕn​).
(9)
Changing relative amplitudes An​ changes the waveform and generally its timbre without changing its fundamental period. Attack, decay and the time evolution of the frequency spectrum also matter for real musical notes.
The middle ear transfers vibration into the cochlea. The basilar membrane supports a frequency-selective travelling wave: higher-frequency responses occur nearer the base and lower-frequency responses nearer the apex. Hair cells in the organ of Corti transduce mechanical stimulation into signals transmitted through the auditory nerve. Different spectral and temporal patterns therefore produce different sensory-activity patterns.[ref]
This supplies a physical basis for timbre discrimination. It does not make the cochlea a set of independent strings, nor does excitation position alone explain every perceptual distinction: the cochlear amplifier and temporal neural coding also contribute.

Original question.
Describe the way in which an image of an object is. formed by a convex lens, and state the relation between the distances of the object and image from the lens.
In what way is the eye enabled to produce, upon the retina, distinct images of objects at different distances from it ?

Solution

Words: 177
7 OurBigBook.com
In the paraxial thin lens approximation, let a converging lens have focal length f>0, real-object distance u>0, and signed image distance v, positive beyond the lens. Then
u1​+v1​=f1​,m=−uv​.​
(10)
A ray parallel to the axis emerges through the far focus; a ray through the optical centre is approximately undeviated. Their intersection gives a real, inverted image for u>f: reduced for u>2f, equal-sized at u=2f, and enlarged for f<u<2f. At u=f the emerging rays are parallel. For u<f their backward extensions meet on the object side: v<0 and the image is virtual and upright.
The retina remains at an approximately fixed distance from the refracting apparatus. Ocular accommodation changes the optical power. A nearer object increases 1/u, so a fixed retinal image distance requires larger 1/f.
For near vision, the ciliary muscle contracts and reduces tension in the zonular fibres. The elastic crystalline lens becomes more rounded, increasing its optical power. For distant vision the ciliary muscle relaxes, zonular tension flattens the crystalline lens, and less power is needed. The eye's several refracting surfaces make the single thin lens a qualitative model rather than its full optical prescription.

Original question.
Describe the common thermometer, and the way in which it is graduated.
To what change is a thermometer liable, which produces errors in the indications of the instrument ?

Solution

Words: 182
8 OurBigBook.com
A liquid-in-glass thermometer has a bulb connected to a narrow graduated capillary. Thermal expansion of its liquid relative to the glass raises or lowers the column. A simple linear model is
Δℓ≃AV0​(βliquid​−βglass​)​ΔT,
(11)
with capillary area A and volumetric expansion coefficients β.
For the historical Celsius graduation, establish an ice point in an equilibrium mixture of pure ice and water, and a steam point in saturated steam at standard atmospheric pressure, here a barometer height of 760mm. Mark 0∘C and 100∘C, then divide the interval into one hundred equal parts. This is a historical two-fixed-point instrument calibration, not the modern definition of the kelvin or of Celsius temperature. Nonuniform capillary area and nonlinear expansion limit exactness.
Bulb contraction raises the column and makes the reading too high; retained expansion makes it too low. Glass volume changes with age and thermal history; heating can cause temporary zero-point depression followed by slow recovery. Rechecking the ice point detects a shifted zero, although it does not diagnose every scale error.[ref]

Original question.
Define the specific heat of a body.
If 100 grammes of iron be boiled for some time in water, when the height of the barometer is 760 m.m., and be then quickly transferred to a vessel containing 150 grammes of water at 10∘C, what will be the common temperature of the iron and water ? It being assumed that no heat is lost, that a gramme of hot water adheres to the iron when it is transferred to the cold water, and that the specific heat of iron is 0.114.

Solution

Words: 121
9 OurBigBook.com
Specific heat capacity is heat capacity per unit mass. For a specified thermodynamic process without a phase transition,
c=m1​dTdQ​.​
(12)
The constraint matters: constant-pressure and constant-volume heat capacities need not coincide. Here the supplied iron value and water value are treated as constants.
At the stipulated barometer reading, iron left in boiling water reaches 100∘C. Taking water's specific heat capacity as one in the given units, the iron has heat capacity 100(0.114)=11.4. The adhering gramme of hot water adds another one. Thus the hot material has total heat capacity 12.4, compared with 150 for the initially cool water.
The no-loss calorimetry energy balance is
100(0.114)(100−T)+1(100−T)=150(T−10),
(13)
so
T=162.412.4(100)+150(10)​=2033425​∘C≃16.8719∘C.​
(14)
The adhering water enters on the hot side, not as extra water initially at 10∘C. The vessel's heat capacity is neglected, as none is supplied.

Original question.
Explain how a person may be knocked down in a thunderstorm without being actually struck by lightning.

Solution

Words: 126
10 OurBigBook.com
A direct lightning strike is unnecessary. A nearby strike can produce a ground current, putting the feet at different electric potentials and driving current through the body by a step voltage. A side flash from a struck object or conduction along a contacted conductor also provides an indirect current path.[ref]
Rapid air heating by a nearby lightning discharge also produces an expanding pressure disturbance. Its blast can impart a mechanical impulse, causing a fall or blunt injury without current passing through the person.[ref]
The absence of a direct strike therefore does not distinguish electrical incapacity from mechanical displacement. Observations would be needed to identify the mechanism in a particular event.

Original question.
If round an electromagnet, a quantity of insulated wire be coiled, in the same way as the coil of the magnet itself, and the ends of this wire be connected with the terminals of a multiplying galvanometer, describe what takes place, when a current is sent round the coil of the magnet, and when this current is stopped.

Solution

Words: 140
11 OurBigBook.com
The coils form a mutual induction apparatus. Changing primary electric current changes secondary magnetic flux linkage Λ2​. Faraday's law of induction gives
E2​=−dtdΛ2​​.​
(15)
On closing the primary circuit, increasing electric current produces a transient secondary electric current and galvanometer deflection. By Lenz's law its direction opposes the increasing magnetic flux. Once primary electric current and core magnetization are steady, the derivative vanishes: the galvanometer returns to zero despite the continuing magnetic field.
On opening the circuit, the decreasing magnetic flux induces a secondary electric current tending to sustain that flux. The galvanometer deflects oppositely, then returns to zero. Making and breaking produce opposite transient deflections; steady primary current produces no sustained induced current.
For fixed linear coils, Λ2​=MI1​ and E2​=−MdI1​/dt, with mutual inductance M. Opposite equal changes of primary electric current give opposite equal areas under the induced-electromotive force curves in that model. Peak sizes can differ with switching times. Real iron-core magnetic hysteresis and remanent magnetization can also change the flux excursion, so equality of the actual peaks is not required.
