Original question.
What are the most commonly used preparations of Iron, and in what form does the metal exist in them ?

Solution

Words: 551
1 OurBigBook.com
The main distinction is between metallic iron, iron(II) preparations, iron(III) preparations, and mixed iron oxides. The old prefixes “proto-” and “per-” ordinarily distinguish the lower and higher oxidation states here. “Peroxide of iron” means the historical iron(III) oxide preparation, not a modern peroxide containing an oxygen–oxygen bond.
The principal medicinal preparations can be grouped by the chemical form supplied:
  • Iron(II) sulfate: Ferri sulphas, its granulated form, and its dried form supply iron(II). The ordinary and granulated salts are represented as FeSO4​⋅7H2​O; the 1867 dried preparation is represented as FeSO4​⋅H2​O. Drying principally changes the water of crystallization, rather than converting iron(II) into iron(III).
  • Iron(II) carbonate: Ferri carbonas saccharata, the carbonate pill, and the compound iron mixture contain freshly formed iron(II) carbonate, FeCO3​. Exposure to air introduces higher iron oxides; sucrose helps preserve the lower oxidation state. Thus the saccharated material is a protected preparation rather than a pure, permanently stable salt.
  • Iron(II) iodide: Ferri iodidum, its syrup, and its pill supply mainly FeI2​. The historical solid includes water of crystallization and some iron oxide; the syrup again employs sucrose to retard deterioration.
  • Iron(II) phosphate: Ferri phosphas and the corresponding syrup supply the lower iron phosphate, conventionally Fe3​(PO4​)2​. The official precipitated material is partly oxidized, so describing the actual powder as chemically pure iron(II) phosphate would be too strong.
  • Iron(III) chloride: the solution and tincture of “perchloride of iron” contain the higher chloride, FeCl3​, in an acidic liquid. This historical “perchloride” is unrelated to a perchlorate.
  • Iron(III) nitrate: solution of “pernitrate of iron” contains the higher nitrate, Fe(NO3​)3​, again in an acidic liquid. The solution of “persulphate” used to manufacture several other preparations supplies iron(III) sulfate, Fe2​(SO4​)3​; it is useful to distinguish this manufacturing reagent from the widely administered iron(II) sulfate.
  • Iron(III) with organic acids: citrate of iron and ammonium, and tartarated iron, contain the higher iron in citrate or tartrate combinations. Citrate of iron and quinine is prepared from a higher iron compound, but should not be assumed to contain exclusively iron(III): the 1867 monograph reports blue reactions with both potassium ferrocyanide and potassium ferricyanide, consistent with an iron(II) component as well. The tartarated preparation also contains potassium; citrate of iron and quinine is made with Ammonia. They should be described as soluble pharmaceutical combinations, without pretending that every historical batch had a single simple molecular formula. The acetate tincture similarly contains the higher iron combined with acetic acid.
  • Iron oxides: Ferri peroxidum hydratum (dried hydrated “peroxide”) and Ferri peroxidum humidum (the moist material) contain iron(III) as iron(III) oxide or hydrated iron(III) oxide. The magnetic oxide is a mixed iron(II)–iron(III) oxide preparation; Fe3​O4​ expresses the ideal mixed oxide, rather than a claim that the historical hydrated powder was pure crystalline magnetite.
  • Metallic iron: Ferrum redactum, used also in lozenges, contains finely divided iron in oxidation state zero together with magnetic oxide. The official preparation is therefore not all elemental iron. Iron wine made by contacting wire with wine obtains a dissolved tartrate-containing iron preparation from reaction with the wine's acids; the wire placed in the vessel should not be confused with the chemical form in the liquid ultimately dispensed.
These identifications follow the historical monographs, rather than the names of present commercial supplements.[ref] A green iron(II) sulfate solution gives the characteristic blue reaction with potassium ferricyanide, whereas the higher iron solutions give blue with potassium ferrocyanide. Those diagnostic reactions explain why the official tests distinguish the two oxidation states; the historical colour test alone does not establish every detail of a complex preparation's composition.

Original question.
How is Sulphate of Quinia prepared, and what are the tests of its purity given in the British Pharmacopœia ?

Solution

Words: 633
2 OurBigBook.com
Prepare quinine sulfate by acidic extraction of cinchona bark, alkaline precipitation of the quinine base, conversion into the sulfate, and purification by crystallization. “Quinia” is the historical name for quinine.
The 1867 process starts with one pound of yellow cinchona bark in coarse powder, three fluid ounces of hydrochloric acid, four pints of solution of sodium hydroxide, and sufficient distilled water and diluted sulfuric acid. The hydrochloric acid is diluted with ten pints of distilled water. The bark is first moistened with diluted sulfuric acid, macerated for twenty-four hours with occasional stirring, and then exhausted in a percolator with the diluted hydrochloric acid. The historical endpoint is disappearance of the bitter character of the emerging liquid. This describes the period's test, not an instruction to taste an unidentified medicinal material.[ref]
The soluble alkaloid salts enter the percolate. Adding solution of sodium hydroxide liberates the much less water-soluble quinine base. After settling, the liquid is decanted and the precipitate collected on a filter and washed with cold distilled water until the washings cease to be coloured. The precipitate is then treated warm with distilled water and diluted sulfuric acid, added gradually to obtain a neutral liquid with nearly all the precipitate dissolved. The hot solution is filtered through paper, the filter washed with distilled water, and the filtrate concentrated until a surface film appears. It is then set aside for crystallization; the crystals are dried on filter paper without applied heat. This is a separation of a naturally occurring alkaloid, not a synthesis of the quinine skeleton.
Writing B for the quinine base, the central changes are:
BH++OH−⟶B↓+H2​O,2B+H2​SO4​⟶(BH)2​SO4​.
(1)
The successive changes in solubility explain the apparatus: a percolator washes soluble salts out of the powder; a filter retains the liberated base; a heated porcelain vessel dissolves the crude base as its sulfate; cooling then recovers the less soluble crystals. Other cinchona alkaloids can accompany the product, which is why appearance and bitterness are insufficient evidence of purity.
The 1867 “Characters and Tests” combine identity and purity checks. They must be read as a group:
  • Appearance and solubility: filiform, silky, white crystals, sparingly soluble in water, with the characteristic bluish appearance of the solution. A little dilute acid increases solubility. The visible blue phenomenon is associated with quinine fluorescence.
  • Sulfate identity: barium chloride gives a white precipitate insoluble in nitric acid. The precipitate is barium sulfate.
  • Quinine identity: treating the solution first with chlorine water and then with Ammonia gives an emerald-green colour, the thalleioquin test. A positive colour reaction establishes a characteristic chemical response, rather than proving that no other alkaloid is present.
  • Strong-acid colour test: dissolution in pure sulfuric acid gives only a faint yellowish tint, with no further colour change on gentle warming. Marked colouring suggests foreign organic matter, such as sucrose or other readily altered material.
  • Acid dissolution and liberation of the base: ten grains with ten minims of diluted sulfuric acid and half a fluid ounce of water form a complete solution; Ammonia then gives a white precipitate. Material insoluble under the acid conditions would fail the dissolution requirement.
  • Ether separation: the precipitated base must pass into diethyl ether on shaking the whole with half a fluid ounce of diethyl ether, without crystalline matter remaining at the boundary between the two liquid layers. Less ether-soluble cinchona alkaloids, particularly cinchonine, can leave material at this boundary. This is a period screening test, not a modern quantitative determination of every related impurity.
  • Water of crystallization: twenty-five grains lose 3.6 grains on drying at 212∘F, equivalent to 100∘C. This checks excessive moisture or a materially different degree of hydration.
The historical quantity and endpoint requirements belong to the 1867 monograph.[ref] Its formula represents the then-described heptahydrate; the current “Quinine sulfate” instead specifies a dihydrate and modern tests for related cinchona alkaloids. The modern specification explains that distinction; the exam's historical tests remain those listed above.[ref]

Original question.
What parts of the following plants are directed by the British Pharmacopœia to be used; viz., Sambucus, Scilla, Rosa Gallica, Pterocarpus, Physostigma venenosum, Theobroma Cacoa, Croton Tiglium, Taraxacum.

Solution

Words: 493
3 OurBigBook.com
The required sequence is fresh flowers; sliced and dried bulb; unexpanded petals; heartwood; seeds; expressed seed fat; expressed seed oil; and fresh and dried root. The plant organ, its state, and the actual official material must all be specified.
  • Sambucus nigra: fresh flowers. The official article is Sambuci flores, elder flowers, gathered from the native plant. The flower stalks are separated when making elder-flower water; the preparation also allows an equivalent quantity of flowers preserved fresh with common salt. Neither elder berries nor elder bark answers the botanical part asked here.[ref]
  • Squill: the sliced and dried bulb. The historical botanical source is Urginea scilla, also identified there as Scilla maritima. The medicinal slices consist of the fleshy bulb scales; this is an underground shoot structure, not a root. The official article is Scilla.[ref]
  • Rosa gallica: the fresh and dried unexpanded petals. These are Rosæ gallicæ petala, the red-rose material. “Unexpanded” refers to collection before the flower has opened fully. The petals must be distinguished from the rose hip used for a different official preparation.[ref]
  • Pterocarpus santalinus: the sliced or rasped heartwood. This is Pterocarpi lignum, red sandalwood. The answer is the wood, rather than bark or the exudate known as kino. Botanical relationship between two medicinal materials does not make their official definitions interchangeable.[ref]
  • Physostigma venenosum: the seed. Physostigmatis faba, the Calabar bean, is botanically a seed, not the entire pod. Its two large cotyledons lie inside a hard seed coat. The official alcoholic extract is made from the powdered seed.[ref]
  • Theobroma cacao: the concrete oil obtained by expression and heat from the ground seeds. The official article is Oleum theobromæ, cocoa butter. Naming the seed gives its source organ, but omits the important fact that the official pharmaceutical material is the separated fat. It is distinct from coconut oil.[ref]
  • Croton tiglium: the oil expressed from the seeds. The official material is Oleum crotonis, croton oil, rather than the whole plant or an unspecified leaf preparation. The seeds are its source; the expressed oil is the article used in the official formulations.[ref]
  • Taraxacum officinale: the fresh and dried root. The official article is Taraxaci radix, dandelion root, called Taraxacum dens leonis in the 1867 standard, gathered between September and February from British meadows and pastures. The collection season and fresh/dried distinction belong to the historical standard; the answer is not the leaves or flowers.[ref]
The names in the paper sometimes use the shorter terminology of the first British Pharmacopoeia, whereas the 1867 edition explicitly renamed many entries to identify the medicinal organ. Its alteration list, for example, maps Sambucus to Sambuci flores, Pterocarpus to Pterocarpi lignum, and Taraxacum to Taraxaci radix.[ref] That is a nomenclature change, not permission to choose any part of the named plant.

Original question.
What is the active agent in the various means of producing blisters on the skin, and by what physiological processes do they act beneficially ?

Solution

Words: 484
4 OurBigBook.com
Ordinary cantharides blisters owe their activity to cantharidin; other blistering methods act through different chemical irritants or through physical injury. There is no single substance common to every way of raising a blister.
In the standard historical preparations, the dried blister beetle supplies cantharidin. A plaster, blistering paper, blistering liquid, or cantharides vinegar is a different vehicle for delivering the same vesicant to the skin. Extracting the beetle into a liquid does not make the solvent the specific active principle. The 1867 British Pharmacopoeia includes Emplastrum cantharidis, Charta epispastica, Liquor epispasticus and Acetum cantharidis.[ref]
Other historical means need separate explanations. Moist mustard develops an irritant volatile product, principally allyl isothiocyanate from black mustard; its usual intended effect is reddening, with prolonged exposure capable of more destructive injury. This differs from the fixed mustard seed oil: the irritant volatile product develops after moistening the powdered seeds.[ref] Strong Ammonia acts as an alkaline irritant. Croton oil can produce an inflammatory and pustular eruption, which is not the same lesion as a clean cantharides blister. A sufficiently hot object or hot liquid raises a blister through thermal damage, without a medicinal active molecule. These differences matter because erythema, a fluid-filled blister, a pustule, and tissue destruction are not interchangeable endpoints.
For cantharidin, the modern tissue explanation involves loss of adhesion between epidermal cells, including disruption of desmosomes, with separation within the epidermis and accumulation of fluid. The blister is therefore a local lesion of cell adhesion accompanied by inflammation, rather than a pocket containing disease drawn from a remote organ. The FDA review describes this acantholysis and distinguishes it from the incompletely understood mechanism of clinical benefit in a particular modern indication.[ref]
A nineteenth-century answer would usually describe two supposed routes to benefit:
  • Counterirritation. A new superficial irritation was believed to relieve a deeper painful or inflamed part by altering nervous activity and the distribution of blood. A physiologically intelligible component is that input from sensory nerves can change pain processing and reflex output. That does not establish the stronger historical claim that creating an artificial inflammation cures an unrelated internal inflammation.
  • Serous drainage or “depletion”. Fluid escaping from the raised blister was thought to remove fluid from the circulation and reduce local fullness. Fluid does indeed leave damaged blood vessels and collect in the blister, but the observation alone does not demonstrate beneficial selective removal of a disease-producing substance. Maintaining a draining sore was a historical practice, not a consequence required by current physiology.
Thus the demonstrable processes are local injury, inflammation, fluid exudation, and sensory stimulation; the broad historical claims of remote benefit require separate evidence. Current approved topical cantharidin use for molluscum contagiosum does not validate nineteenth-century blistering for diseases of internal organs. The modern label explicitly states that the mechanism of action in molluscum contagiosum is unknown and documents local skin reactions.[ref]

Original question.
‘A remedy must be absorbed before it can exert any remote action on the Animal Economy.’
Is this true ? Illustrate the statement.

Solution

Words: 502
5 OurBigBook.com
The assertion is too absolute: a remedy can cause a distant response through a reflex or another intervening physiological change without first being absorbed into the blood. It is substantially right for the direct action of a circulating drug on a distant target, but does not describe every remote effect of treatment.
Three mechanisms should be distinguished:
  • Direct action after drug absorption. An orally administered drug that acts directly in the brain must gain access to that tissue. Absorption from the gastrointestinal tract, transport in the blood, and passage to the target explain such a systemic action. Changing the route changes the first step: a subcutaneous medicine must leave its local depot, whereas an intravenous medicine is introduced directly into the circulation. The literal word “absorbed” is consequently too restrictive even before considering reflexes.
  • Local action followed by a neural response. Stimulation of sensory nerves at one surface can cause a response elsewhere through the nervous system. Gastric stimulation can evoke vomiting, including coordinated movement of respiratory and abdominal muscles; sensory input from the gastrointestinal tract reaches brainstem circuits through vagal afferents. No requirement follows that the original gastric irritant must itself circulate to every contracting muscle. A swallowed emetic can have more than one mechanism, however: an observed instance of vomiting alone does not prove that absorption played no part.[ref]
  • Local action followed by a change in the body's contents. As a present-day illustration, an osmotic laxative can retain water in the intestinal lumen and promote evacuation with limited systemic absorption. Any secondary change in fluid balance or symptoms is then a consequence of the local intestinal action, not proof that the laxative has directly acted on a remote organ. The polyethylene glycol 3350 information from MedlinePlus describes retention of water in stool; the regulatory monograph records minimal absorption.[ref][ref]
The distinction is between transport of the medicinal substance and transmission of its consequences. A reflex transmits activity in neurons; a locally acting intestinal agent changes a physical condition; a circulating drug delivers molecules to its distant target. The same remedy may combine these routes, and an apparently rapid effect does not by itself settle which route caused it.
A useful experimental design would therefore compare remote responses with measurements of the agent in blood, and would test the proposed neural pathway separately. In an appropriate physiological preparation, recording the response while interrupting a relevant afferent pathway can test whether that pathway is necessary; preserving the circulation while interrupting nerve conduction distinguishes neural transmission from vascular transport. Conversely, a remote effect after vascular transfer supports a circulating agent, provided contamination and other transferred mediators are controlled. Failure to detect a drug is only as strong as the analytical detection limit.
Absorption is one route to remote action, not a universal prerequisite for every distant physiological consequence. The historical language “animal economy” means the functioning organism; it should not conceal the distinction between a direct pharmacological effect and a secondary physiological response.

A valid examination of the missing materials would first record each label separately and describe the actual material: whole or powdered form; size and shape; external and cut surfaces; texture and fracture; and any diagnostic plant organ or animal structure. For a botanical material, microscopy could distinguish bark, wood, root, leaf and seed tissues, with suitable reference material and the historical pharmacopoeia definition used for comparison. A putative chemical preparation would instead require appropriate solubility and reagent tests. No colour, smell, section, chemical reaction, botanical origin or medicinal constituent is asserted for an unseen sample. Historical descriptions involving tasting are documentary evidence, not a method to apply to unknown potentially poisonous materials.
