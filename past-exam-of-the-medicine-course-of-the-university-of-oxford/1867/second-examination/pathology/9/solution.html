In infants, pneumonia often has a patchy airway-centred pattern and less typical localizing symptoms, while collapse and systemic illness complicate its recognition. The classical comparison is with an adult's acute lobar pneumonia, but age does not impose an absolute anatomical division.
The adult reference pattern is relatively continuous consolidation of a lobe. In the traditional pathological sequence, congestion is followed by red hepatization, with red blood cells, neutrophils and fibrin in the alveoli; later breakdown of the red blood cells contributes to grey hepatization, followed by clearance or organization. These stages are a descriptive model, can overlap, and are not a mandatory timetable for every adult infection.[ref]
By comparison, an infant may have bronchopneumonia: separate foci centred on small bronchi and bronchioles, extending into adjacent alveoli, sometimes in both lungs. Airway secretions and inflammatory material coexist with patches of atelectasis; lesions of different ages can be present together. Coalescent foci can eventually resemble a larger area of consolidation. The distinction is anatomical rather than proof of one specific infecting organism.[ref]
Question 8 supplies an essential historical qualification: some lesions labelled infantile “lobular pneumonia” were actually collapse. Distinguishing atelectasis from inflammatory exudate prevents that diagnostic inflation. The two conditions can nevertheless coexist.[ref]
Clinically, an infant cannot report pleuritic chest pain and usually swallows rather than expectorates sputum. The adult combination of rigors, chest pain and conspicuous rusty sputum is therefore a poor required checklist. More useful infant signs include rapid breathing, lower chest indrawing, nasal flaring, grunting, poor feeding, reduced activity and sometimes cyanosis. Fever can be present, but the youngest infants may instead show temperature instability and nonspecific illness. Physical signs can be scattered when the lesions are scattered; absence of one large area of dullness does not exclude pneumonia.[ref][ref]
In current practice, the age group also changes the differential. Early neonatal pneumonia can be part of generalized sepsis acquired around birth; later infant infections include both viral and bacterial causes. Assessment integrates the illness history, oxygen saturation, examination and selected laboratory or imaging evidence rather than assigning a diagnosis from age alone. A positive airway culture can reflect colonization, so the organism must be interpreted with the clinical and inflammatory evidence.[ref]
The decisive comparison is therefore patchy and mixed pathology, greater diagnostic difficulty, and frequent systemic presentation in infancy, with lobar pneumonia possible in children and bronchopneumonia possible in adults.
