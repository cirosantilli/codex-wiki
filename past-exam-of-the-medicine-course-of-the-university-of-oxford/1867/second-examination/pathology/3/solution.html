Historical “amyloid disease” denotes the waxy or lardaceous visceral change, especially in the spleen, liver and kidneys; modern amyloidosis denotes deposition of misfolded protein fibrils. The old descriptive appearance and the modern chemical explanation must be kept distinct.
In nineteenth-century usage the affected organs could be enlarged, firm and unusually translucent or wax-like on section. The name amyloid means starch-like. The historical chemical argument relied on colour reactions with iodine and sulfuric acid, suggesting affinity with starch or cellulose. Such a reaction was a clue to recognition, not a modern identification of the deposited substance. Rudolf Virchow's 1856 account explicitly discusses the waxy change of the spleen, liver and kidneys, and its occurrence with longstanding destructive disease.[ref]
The three principal visceral examples can be described more precisely:
  • In the spleen, deposits in the splenic white pulp may produce translucent granules, the so-called sago spleen. More diffuse involvement gives a widespread lardaceous appearance.
  • In the liver, deposits along the hepatic sinusoids can compress adjacent hepatocytes; extensive deposition may produce a large firm liver.[ref]
  • In the kidney, deposits in glomeruli, blood vessels and sometimes the interstitium interfere with function. Involvement of the glomeruli explains the albuminuria of Question 1; it may progress to nephrotic syndrome and kidney failure.
The distribution is not confined to those three organs. Amyloidosis can also affect the gastrointestinal tract, heart, blood vessels and other sites; the pattern depends on the precursor protein. Modern examples include AA amyloidosis associated with sustained inflammation, AL amyloidosis involving immunoglobulin light chains, and transthyretin amyloidosis. This classification is a present-day explanation, not a list of molecular diagnoses asserted for unnamed 1867 patients.[ref]
Modern confirmation uses histology with Congo red and examination with a polarizing microscope, followed by identification of the deposited protein, for example with mass spectrometry. The characteristic birefringence supports identification of amyloid; typing distinguishes biologically different diseases. These are later laboratory methods, not apparatus available to the candidate in 1867.[ref]
Thus an adequate historical list starts with spleen, liver and kidneys, while the modern explanation is extracellular protein deposition rather than conversion of an organ into starch.
