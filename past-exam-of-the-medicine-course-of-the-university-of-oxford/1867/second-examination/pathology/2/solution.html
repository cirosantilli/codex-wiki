There is no single constant anatomical lesion of epilepsy. A sound autopsy answer separates an underlying brain lesion, changes associated with longstanding epilepsy, and the consequences of the final seizure or death.
The potentially causal group includes healed traumatic scars, old cerebral infarctions or intracranial haemorrhages, developmental abnormalities of the cerebral cortex, brain tumours, and inflammatory or infectious destruction of brain tissue. Such abnormalities can leave an enduring network capable of generating recurrent seizures. Their importance depends on location and the lifetime history; an incidental abnormality is not automatically the cause. A modern acute symptomatic seizure during a transient metabolic disturbance should also not automatically be called epilepsy.[ref]
In longstanding cases one may find atrophy or scarring of particular brain regions. Hippocampal sclerosis is an important modern example: loss of neurons with gliosis in the hippocampus, associated especially with temporal-lobe epilepsy. Association does not settle whether a particular lesion preceded the first seizure, resulted from repeated seizures, or shares another cause with them. Conversely, genetically determined disturbances of neuronal excitability can produce recurrent seizures without a grossly visible destructive lesion.[ref]
For the historical question, vascular redness or congestion and alterations around the medulla oblongata deserve mention as contemporary observations rather than established universal causes. A January 1864 report describes microscopic examination of fourteen longstanding cases, including dilated blood vessels and altered nervous material in that region. Its proposed localization of epilepsy to the medulla oblongata is a historical theory; modern epilepsy is not defined by that site or by those vascular appearances.[ref]
The terminal group includes cerebral oedema, hypoxic-ischaemic brain injury, and pulmonary oedema or aspiration in some deaths. Congestion is nonspecific and may be terminal; injuries can result from a fall during a seizure. None of these alone establishes that epilepsy was the original cause. A modern autopsy study of sudden deaths in people with epilepsy found both structural abnormalities and nonspecific changes; exclusion of another anatomical or toxicological cause remains central to classifying sudden unexpected death in epilepsy.[ref]
The appropriate investigation is therefore inspection followed by systematic histology, especially of the cerebral cortex and hippocampus on both sides, interpreted alongside the recorded seizure history and examination of other organs. A normal-looking brain does not exclude epilepsy, and a congested one does not prove it.
