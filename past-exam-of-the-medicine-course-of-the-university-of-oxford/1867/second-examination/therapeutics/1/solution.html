An 1867 answer would distinguish general depletion to relieve a supposed dangerous circulatory overload from local depletion intended to relieve a circumscribed inflammatory condition. General bloodletting meant opening a vein by venesection. Local bloodletting meant medicinal leeches or wet cupping; ordinary dry cupping did not remove blood. In wet cupping, superficial incisions and a partially evacuated cup withdrew blood from the skin. The apparatus and the quantity removed mattered: a supposedly local procedure could still produce substantial systemic blood loss.
In the period's vocabulary, a robust person with an early “sthenic” attack, a strong full pulse, severe oppression of breathing and an apparently engorged lung might be considered for general bloodletting. The intention was to lessen vascular distension and the work imposed on the heart, or to arrest an advancing inflammation. Local bloodletting over a painful region was intended to moderate local inflammation without the full depletion of venesection. These were therapeutic theories, not proof that blood beneath the skin drained an inflamed organ selectively. A contemporary account of pneumonia explicitly preferred early bloodletting in severe “sthenic” cases and distinguished them from debilitated “asthenic” cases.[ref]
Even within that framework, the decision needed the stage of illness, previous blood loss, age, general strength and response to any previous depletion. A weak rapid pulse, pallor, faintness or exhausted convalescence argued against further bloodletting. “There is fever” or “the blood looks inflammatory” was not an adequate indication. A hot flushed face alone could not establish excess circulating volume. The period evidence also needs scrutiny: selecting the most severe cases for bloodletting, then comparing their recovery with mild unbled cases, confounds treatment with initial severity. The reported recoveries establish what was done; they do not isolate its causal effect.[ref]
Modern correction: remove blood only for a defined indication, not to treat ordinary fever, pneumonia or a presumed surplus of “inflammatory” blood. In haemochromatosis, controlled venesection removes iron contained in red blood cells; repeated replacement of those red blood cells draws down the body's iron stores. The amount and frequency depend on haemoglobin and ferritin monitoring.[ref] In polycythaemia vera, venesection reduces excessive red blood cell mass and the associated risk of thrombosis.[ref] These measurable targets are different from the historical idea of “plethora”. In an exhausted person, indiscriminate bloodletting can worsen anaemia and circulatory failure; modern care identifies and treats the cause of the respiratory distress or inflammation instead.
