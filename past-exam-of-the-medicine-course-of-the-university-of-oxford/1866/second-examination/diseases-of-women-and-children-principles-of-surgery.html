Original question.
From what various conditions may suppression of Menstruation arise; and how are they to be dealt with respectively ?

Solution

Words: 428
1 OurBigBook.com
Absent menstruation is a sign with several causes; treatment must distinguish physiological absence, failure of menstrual cycling and obstruction of outflow. The word “suppression” does not establish that retained blood is poisoning the body or that producing a bleed will cure the underlying problem.
First distinguish primary amenorrhoea, where menstruation has never begun, from secondary amenorrhoea, where established cycles have stopped. Ask about the previous pattern, possibility of pregnancy, breastfeeding, age, illness, nutrition, exercise, medicines and associated symptoms. Pregnancy, lactation and menopause can explain physiological absence. Pregnancy must be considered before any proposed menstrual stimulant, with assessment for complications when pain or abnormal bleeding accompanies the missed period.[ref]
A second group has impaired hormonal signalling or ovarian function. Severe illness, inadequate energy intake, major exertion and stress can suppress the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis. Other possibilities include thyroid disease, hyperprolactinaemia, primary ovarian insufficiency and chronic anovulation. These modern categories separate processes that an 1866 clinician might group under debility, constitutional disease or disordered ovarian activity. Anaemia and wasting deserve investigation and treatment when present, but are not universal explanations for every missed period.
A third group has an anatomical explanation. Cyclical pain with absent visible menstruation can indicate retained menstrual material behind an imperforate hymen or other outflow obstruction. Congenital absence or abnormal development of reproductive organs is different again. Acquired scarring of the uterine cavity may interrupt bleeding after instrumentation or disease. The response to obstruction is diagnosis-specific correction by an appropriate specialist; neither a “tonic” nor a drug intended to provoke uterine contraction repairs an absent or blocked passage.[ref]
In period terms, treatment of a genuine associated illness, improved nourishment and protection from exhausting conditions could be sensible. Warm baths and drugs called emmenagogues were also employed, but their use should not be mistaken for proof of efficacy, or attempted before excluding pregnancy. Menstrual absence should not automatically be interpreted as the cause of mental or systemic illness.
Modern correction: investigate the relevant physiological pathway and treat its cause. Selected investigations include a pregnancy test, prolactin, thyroid-stimulating hormone, ovarian and pituitary hormone measurements and pelvic ultrasonography. For functional hypothalamic suppression, restore energy availability and address contributing illness or disordered eating; simply imposing withdrawal bleeds can conceal persisting energy deficiency. Hormone replacement may be appropriate for ovarian insufficiency, while thyroid, prolactin-related and anatomical disorders need their own pathways. Management of chronic anovulation includes reproductive goals and protection of the endometrium, rather than just the appearance of regular bleeding.[ref][ref]

Original question.
What are the chief causes of Acute Mania in adult women? Describe the affection and its treatment.

Solution

Words: 475
2 OurBigBook.com
Historical “acute mania” in women must be separated into a manic syndrome, psychosis associated with childbirth and disturbed behaviour caused by physical disease or drugs. Reproductive events can be clinically relevant without making female anatomy a sufficient explanation for mental illness.
A typical manic episode involves a marked change from the person's usual state: elevated or irritable mood, increased activity, little felt need for sleep, rapid or pressured speech, distractibility, impulsive decisions and exaggerated confidence. Severe illness can include delusions, hallucinations, disorganization and dangerous loss of judgement. Physical exhaustion or failure to eat can follow sustained activity. These are possible features of the syndrome, not assertions about an unspecified woman.[ref] Fluctuating attention, disorientation, fever or a changing level of consciousness instead prompts particular concern for delirium and its cause.
In an 1866 account, chief associations would include the puerperal period, prolonged nursing with exhaustion, previous attacks, family predisposition, severe emotional disturbance and general illness. Historical “puerperal insanity” also included depressive and psychotic presentations, not just what is now called bipolar disorder. A contemporary journal summary distinguished pregnancy, puerperal and lactational groups and noted hereditary associations, recurrence and suicidal behaviour. Its observed associations do not establish menstrual suppression or nursing itself as a single sufficient cause.[ref]
The practical historical aims were protection from injury, restoration of sleep and nutrition, a calm environment, attentive nursing and treatment of physical illness. Someone unable to care safely for herself or an infant required continuous help. Opiates, baths and other sedating measures were tried; quietness after sedation did not prove cure. Depleting a person already exhausted by childbirth or blood loss could worsen the illness. Neither punishment nor treatment of an assumed uterine defect addresses a properly established mental disorder.
Modern correction: assess risk and distinguish primary psychiatric illness from a medical emergency. Consider bipolar disorder, medication- or substance-related illness, thyroid disease, neurological disease and infection. After childbirth, distinguish postpartum psychosis from delirium due to sepsis, metabolic disturbance or another obstetric complication. Protect the woman and baby while arranging prompt specialist assessment. NICE recommends immediate assessment, within four hours of referral, for sudden symptoms suggesting postpartum psychosis.[ref]
For established mania, current treatment may include an appropriate antipsychotic, specialist use of a mood stabilizer, and review of medicines such as an antidepressant that may be contributing. Pregnancy, breastfeeding, previous treatment response, physical disease and monitoring requirements affect the choice. In severe or treatment-resistant illness, additional specialist interventions may be needed. Treat delirium by addressing its physical cause alongside safe supportive care; it is not interchangeable with a manic episode.[ref][ref] The organizing principle is a diagnosis and a safe care plan, not a presumption that all acute behavioural change in a woman is “uterine mania”.

Original question.
Under what circumstances is Ovariotomy to be recommended? Describe the operation.

Solution

Words: 489
3 OurBigBook.com
In 1866, ovariotomy was justified for selected serious ovarian tumours when removal offered a better prospect than continued enlargement, repeated temporary drainage or progressive disability. The diagnosis, probable resectability, operative danger and the woman's informed preference all mattered.
Before recommending it, establish as far as contemporary examination allowed that the mass was ovarian rather than pregnancy, ascites, a uterine tumour or another abdominal lesion. Progressive enlargement, pain, pressure on the urinary bladder or intestine, difficulty breathing and loss of function strengthened the case for intervention. Repeated refilling after tapping showed why evacuation of a cyst's contents was often temporary palliation. A small tolerable mass, severe intercurrent disease, extreme exhaustion, extensive fixation or suspected widely disseminated disease altered the balance. It would be equally mistaken to operate on every abdominal swelling or to wait until avoidable debility made an otherwise feasible operation desperate.
The period operation can be described by its purposes and stages. Following preparation and anaesthesia, the surgeon opened the abdomen through a midline incision and identified the tumour. In a large cystic mass, a trocar and cannula could evacuate fluid under control so the mass could be delivered through a smaller opening. Adhesions had to be assessed and separated only as safely practicable, with particular care around the intestine, urinary bladder and vascular attachments. The tumour's pedicle was secured against haemorrhage, then the mass was detached. The operator inspected for bleeding, protected the abdominal contents and closed the wound; postoperative observation looked for haemorrhage, peritonitis, collapse and other complications. This is a historical operative description, not a present surgical instruction.[ref]
Management of the pedicle was a central technical problem. Clamps, ligatures and cautery embodied different approaches to securing blood vessels and dealing with the stump. One period method retained a clamped pedicle externally at the wound; other methods differed. The apparatus mattered because inadequate control risked fatal haemorrhage, while devitalized tissue, contamination and its relation to the wound created other dangers. A 1866 discussion specifically compared modes of dealing with the pedicle; there was no single timeless “ovariotomy technique”.[ref]
Modern correction: management of an ovarian mass begins with risk assessment, not automatic removal of an ovary. History, examination and ultrasonography, with selected laboratory investigations, guide observation, benign gynaecological treatment or referral to gynaecological oncology. Many simple cysts can be observed; symptomatic or suspicious masses may need surgery. Ovarian cystectomy may preserve ovarian tissue where appropriate, while suspected ovarian cancer requires specialist planning and attention to staging and avoiding unnecessary rupture or spillage. A modern oncological operation is not equivalent to the historical practice of decompressing a large cyst through a trocar.[ref] Assessment of operative fitness, fertility wishes, the other ovary, alternatives and consent remains essential. Modern asepsis, haemostasis, anaesthetic care and postoperative support also prevent the historical risk estimates from being transferred directly to current practice.

Original question.
What leads you to suspect retroversion of the Uterus? How is it to be treated?

Solution

Words: 378
4 OurBigBook.com
Suspect uterine retroversion from examination and the clinical setting, while distinguishing an incidental position from symptomatic disease or an incarcerated pregnant uterus. Pelvic pressure, backache, painful menstruation, pain with intercourse, difficulty voiding or rectal pressure can prompt assessment, but none proves that the uterine position is the cause.
In retroversion the uterus tilts backwards as a whole; uterine retroflexion instead describes bending between its body and cervix. The two can coexist. On an appropriate pelvic examination, the uterine body may be felt posteriorly while the cervix points more anteriorly. Mobility, tenderness, a pelvic mass and the possibility of pregnancy affect interpretation. Symptoms may come from associated endometriosis, adhesions or another pelvic disorder rather than the tilt itself.[ref]
The especially important historical presentation is early or mid-pregnancy with increasing pelvic pressure and urinary retention. An enlarged backward-lying uterus can remain trapped beneath the pelvic brim, distorting the cervix and bladder outlet. Dribbling urine can represent overflow from retention, not adequate emptying. A distended urinary bladder and a posterior pelvic mass require careful assessment rather than treatment with a diuretic. A pre-1866 account of retroversion after conception emphasized retention as a principal presenting problem.[ref]
Historical treatment aimed first to empty an overdistended urinary bladder with a catheter, then to restore position where appropriate and prevent recurrence, sometimes with positional measures or a vaginal pessary. A fixed, painful or possibly pregnant mass made blind forceful manipulation particularly hazardous. Describing the aim of a period manoeuvre does not make repeated unassessed attempts at repositioning acceptable.
Modern correction: an asymptomatic retroverted uterus commonly needs no treatment. Symptoms call for investigation of their actual cause; retroversion alone does not establish infertility or explain every miscarriage. For selected symptomatic mobile cases, conservative support or a fitted vaginal pessary may be considered, while associated disease is managed on its merits.[ref] Suspected incarceration in pregnancy, especially with urinary retention, needs urgent obstetric assessment. Ultrasonography can clarify the anatomy; magnetic resonance imaging may help when the relation of the uterus, urinary bladder and cervix remains uncertain. Drainage of the urinary bladder and any attempt at reduction are specialist decisions guided by gestation, anatomy and complications. Published evidence is largely case reports and series, so a single successful method is not a universal rule.[ref]

Original question.
Enumerate the causes and modes of treatment (1) of Infantile Paralysis, (2) of Infantile Convulsions.

Solution

Words: 490
5 OurBigBook.com
Discuss paralysis and convulsions separately: both historical labels describe manifestations with several causes. Their occurrence in infancy does not establish one diagnosis, and neither permits invention of examination findings absent from the paper.
For infantile paralysis, distinguish an acute loss of movement after an illness from impaired movement present from birth, delayed motor development and progressive weakness. Note distribution, muscle tone, tendon reflexes, sensation, pain, muscle atrophy, and bulbar or respiratory involvement. The classic acute flaccid, often asymmetric syndrome later identified with poliomyelitis must be distinguished from cerebral injury, cerebral palsy, peripheral nerve injury, spinal disease and painful failure to move a limb. A child who avoids movement because it hurts has pseudoparalysis, not necessarily loss of motor innervation.
An 1866 explanation might attribute some cases to teething, chill or constitutional weakness, and use rubbing, warmth, supports or electrical stimulation. Such attributions need qualification: a preceding event does not prove the mechanism, and electrical contraction does not demonstrate restoration of damaged motor neurons. The clinically useful period aims were preservation of comfort, nutrition and limb position and prevention of deformity while following recovery. No historical stimulant can be assumed to regenerate destroyed neural tissue.
Modern correction: acute weakness requires urgent localization and evaluation of breathing and swallowing. Poliomyelitis results from poliovirus infection and can permanently damage motor function; there is no specific curative treatment for paralytic disease. Supportive care and appropriate rehabilitation address respiratory risk, function and complications; vaccination is preventive rather than a reversal of established paralysis.[ref] Cerebral palsy describes a different developmental motor disorder associated with disturbances of the developing brain; assessment, investigation when appropriate, physiotherapy, occupational support and treatment of associated problems are individualized. Not every case is attributable to a single event during delivery, and loss of previously attained abilities requires reconsideration of a static diagnosis.[ref]
For infantile convulsions, consider fever, meningitis, encephalitis, hypoglycaemia, hypocalcaemia and other metabolic disorders, head injury, structural cerebral disease, toxic exposure and epilepsy. Clarify the age, duration, whether movements are focal, recovery between attacks and the child's condition before and afterwards. Neonatal seizures and convulsions in an older febrile infant should not be collapsed into one reassuring category. “Teething” is not a satisfactory explanation for a serious seizure, and a warm bath or gum-lancing must not replace investigation.
During an attack, protect the airway and prevent injury; do not put objects or oral medicines into the mouth. Assess breathing and circulation, time the event, check blood glucose promptly and identify reversible causes. A convulsive seizure lasting five minutes or more is a medical emergency requiring resuscitation and a current status epilepticus pathway, beginning with an appropriate benzodiazepine and escalating if needed. Hypoglycaemia, infection and electrolyte disturbances need direct treatment as well as suppression of movements.[ref][ref] Following the event, investigate the cause and arrange appropriate follow-up; one provoked convulsion does not by itself establish chronic epilepsy.

Original question.
What are the symptoms of Infantile Syphilis ?

Solution

Words: 405
6 OurBigBook.com
Early congenital syphilis can affect the skin, mucosa, bones, viscera and nervous system, yet an infected infant may look well at birth. The symptoms are a variable constellation, not a compulsory sequence seen in every child.
The classical infantile picture includes persistent nasal discharge or “snuffles”, difficulty feeding, poor growth and a rash that can involve the palms and soles. The eruption may be maculopapular or blistering, with desquamation; colour descriptions such as “coppery” are neither universal nor diagnostic alone. Fissures around the mouth and other mucocutaneous lesions may occur. Look also for hepatosplenomegaly, jaundice, anaemia, enlarged lymph nodes and evidence of systemic illness.[ref]
Bone disease is particularly important when interpreting an apparently motionless limb. Osteochondritis or periostitis can make movement painful, producing pseudoparalysis. This is distinct from the loss of motor function discussed under infantile paralysis. The nervous system can also be involved, including meningitis or seizures. Severe disease can be associated with prematurity, hydrops, stillbirth or neonatal death, although a living infant with mild signs may present much later.[ref]
The period term “hereditary syphilis” should not be read as a modern account of Mendelian inheritance. Modern science identifies infection by Treponema pallidum, transmitted to the fetus during pregnancy, rather than an inherited constitutional taint. Absence of obvious maternal symptoms does not exclude transmission. Equally, a rash or nasal discharge alone is not enough to prove syphilis or justify an allegation about a parent's behaviour.[ref]
Later disease is useful for completing the history, but is not the usual neonatal picture. It can include notched permanent incisors or abnormal molars, a saddle-shaped nose, anterior bowing of the shins, interstitial keratitis and sensorineural hearing loss. These permanent dental signs cannot be demanded of a young infant before the relevant teeth have developed.[ref]
Modern correction: examine and test in the context of maternal infection and treatment, even when the baby has no symptoms. Infant and maternal quantitative nontreponemal results, examination, treatment history and selected direct or additional tests guide assessment. Passive maternal antibodies complicate interpretation of infant serology; a positive test does not have the same meaning in every setting. Appropriate penicillin treatment and follow-up are determined by the clinical scenario, not by waiting for a characteristic rash. Historical mercurial treatment is not its modern substitute.[ref]

Original question.
What are the points to observe in the diagnosis of strangulated Hernia ?

Solution

Words: 444
7 OurBigBook.com
Suspected strangulation means threatened or impaired blood supply to herniated tissue; it is more serious than irreducibility alone and can occur without complete intestinal obstruction. The diagnostic assessment must combine the local swelling, abdominal symptoms, general condition and time course.
Ask about a previous hernia, whether it was reducible, a new painful swelling, abrupt failure of reduction, the onset of pain and vomiting, bowel function and prior attempts at manipulation. Examine the relevant sites, including both groins and the femoral regions, rather than attributing unexplained vomiting and abdominal pain solely to a gastric illness. A previously familiar painless lump can become dangerous after years of intermittent reduction. A small femoral hernia may be less conspicuous than its systemic consequences suggest.
Locally, new tenderness, tension, irreducibility and loss of a usual cough impulse raise concern. Erythematous or discoloured overlying skin is more alarming, but can be absent early. Abdominal distension, persistent vomiting and failure to pass stool or flatus support intestinal obstruction. Increasing pain, tachycardia, fever, dehydration, peritonitis or shock suggest advancing compromise. Do not wait for every late sign: prolonged delay can lead to intestinal ischaemia, necrosis and sepsis.[ref]
Several negative observations are unsafe reassurances. Passage of stool may merely empty bowel below the obstruction. If only part of the bowel wall is trapped, as in a Richter hernia, complete obstruction may never occur. A very ill person may describe less pain late in the course. Disappearance of the external swelling after a forceful manipulation does not prove safe restoration of viable bowel: persistence of pain or obstruction demands reassessment, including possible reduction en masse.[ref]
An 1866 surgeon relied heavily on these bedside features and on the distinction from an inflamed lymph node, hydrocele, another groin mass or non-strangulated hernia. Contemporary reports describe sequences of attempted reduction, baths, enemata and chloroform before operation. They document period practice; they must not be transferred into a rule that strangulation begins only when repeated reduction has failed.[ref]
Modern correction: suspected strangulation needs immediate surgical assessment and timely operative treatment. Selected blood tests and imaging, commonly contrast-enhanced computed tomography when useful and feasible, support assessment but should not delay action in a convincing emergency. Normal early laboratory results do not exclude local ischaemia. Current guidance distinguishes an acutely irreducible hernia without evidence of bowel compromise, where supervised reduction may be considered, from suspected strangulation, where emergency surgery is indicated. Ongoing symptoms after apparent reduction also require observation and reassessment.[ref] The diagnostic goal is not merely to name the lump, but to recognize endangered tissue before irreversible injury.

Original question.
State fully the symptoms and usual history of Schirrus of the Breast. What reasons incline you to advise or dissuade operation? What treatment other than removal has been recommended ?

Solution

Words: 587
8 OurBigBook.com
The expected historical picture is a hard, infiltrating breast tumour, often initially painless, followed variably by local tethering, lymphatic spread, ulceration and distant disease. “Scirrhus” describes a fibrous pattern of carcinoma, not a complete modern subtype. A soft or painless lump cannot thereby be excluded from breast cancer, and a hard lump alone is not proof of it.
The person may notice a small firm lump or an altered contour before experiencing substantial discomfort. An irregular hard mass can become less mobile as it involves surrounding tissues. Skin dimpling, tethering or puckering, nipple retraction, altered nipple discharge and enlargement of axillary lymph nodes are important associated findings. Later local disease may fix the breast to deeper structures, ulcerate, bleed or produce malodorous discharge. Pain can be lancinating or persistent, but waiting for pain is unsafe because early breast cancer often causes none.[ref]
The course varies over months or years; not every sign appears in sequence. Local growth can coexist with initially good general health. Subsequent metastasis may affect bones, lungs, liver or other organs and cause symptoms distant from the breast. Historical observers could recognize recurrence after apparently complete removal without possessing modern staging or receptor classification. A temporary reduction in pain, apparent softening or improved appetite did not establish elimination of malignant tissue.[ref]
In an 1866 decision, reasons favouring operation included a localized lesion that could apparently be removed, reasonable general strength, no evidence of distant dissemination and willingness to accept the considerable contemporary risks. The prospect of effective local removal argued against waiting for ulceration and exhaustion. Extensive fixation, very widespread nodal or skin involvement, distant disease, severe intercurrent illness or a risk disproportionate to expected benefit argued against an attempted curative removal. Axillary findings needed assessment rather than a simplistic rule that any enlarged node either guaranteed or prohibited success. Palliation and the woman's preferences remained relevant even when cure was implausible.
Treatment other than removal had included local protection or compression, dressings for ulceration and bleeding, analgesia, dietary or tonic measures and destructive chemical applications. A 1865 clinical text records plasters, iron, cod-liver oil and treatment directed at ulcer discharge and haemorrhage. Those are observations of symptomatic management, not proof of tumour eradication.[ref] Contemporary discussion also proposed caustics or injections of chemicals such as zinc chloride, sometimes after cutting or removing tissue. These methods could damage tissue and relieve or alter a surface lesion without demonstrating durable control of the whole disease. They should not be represented as effective alternatives to evidence-based treatment.[ref]
Modern correction: establish tissue diagnosis and stage, then plan local and systemic treatment together. Clinical examination and appropriate breast imaging lead to a core needle biopsy or another indicated sampling method. Histology, extent of disease and relevant biomarkers guide management; “scirrhous” appearance cannot substitute for these. Axillary assessment forms part of pretreatment evaluation.[ref] Depending on the cancer and circumstances, treatment may include breast-conserving surgery with appropriate radiotherapy, mastectomy, systemic treatment before or after surgery, endocrine therapy or targeted therapy. Some locally advanced disease needs systemic treatment before a surgical decision, so the historical judgement “fixed, therefore never operable” is not a universal modern rule.[ref] For advanced disease, treatment also addresses pain, wound problems and other symptoms. Palliative benefit is a valid aim, but must be distinguished from a claim of cure.
