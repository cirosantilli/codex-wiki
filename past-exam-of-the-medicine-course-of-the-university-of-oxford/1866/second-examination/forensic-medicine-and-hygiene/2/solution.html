Retain the original material, examine separate portions for arsenic and digitalin, and require corroboration beyond a single colour or deposit. One is an inorganic element; the other is a historical pharmaceutical preparation of organic cardiac poisons. A procedure suited to one can destroy or lose the other.
Record the receipt, container, quantity and condition of the stomach contents. Preserve a sealed portion unchanged. Mix the remainder sufficiently to obtain representative aliquots, retaining solid particles as well as liquid. Record the portions consumed by each test and retain the resulting preparations. Test the reagents separately and include an unpoisoned comparison material where available. An analyst must distinguish poison originally present from contamination introduced by apparatus, acids or metals.
For arsenic detection, a useful period screen is deposition on clean copper warmed in an acidified portion of the material. A dark coating is only presumptive: other substances, including antimony, can interfere. Wash and dry the coated copper, then heat it in a small glass tube, examining the cooler portion for a sublimate of arsenic trioxide and its characteristic crystals under a microscope. A separately prepared clear extract allows corroborating reactions, such as the yellow arsenic trisulfide precipitate and the appropriate silver reaction. An 1844 primary account already stressed both reagent purity and the danger of confusing arsenic with other metals.[ref]
A second classical approach converts the suspected arsenic into volatile arsine in a hydrogen-generating apparatus and decomposes it in a heated glass tube, producing a metallic mirror. This separates a volatile product from much of the original organic material. A mirror alone is insufficient, since antimony can give a similar deposit; establish the deposit's identity by further chemistry. The initial blank must include the very zinc and acid used in the examination. The apparatus produces extremely poisonous gas: this is an explanation of historical laboratory evidence, not a procedure for an uncontained demonstration. The original method of James Marsh explains the value of separating the poison in gaseous form; later confirmation must still distinguish arsenic from interfering material.[ref]
For digitalin, work on a fresh aliquot without mineralizing it. Use an alcoholic extraction, filter away coarse material, concentrate gently, and retain rather than discard fractions until their behaviour has been checked against a reference preparation. “Digitalin” did not denote one uniform pure compound: a contemporary 1864 report distinguished preparations with markedly different solubilities. Accordingly, a negative result after one solvent partition is inconclusive; it would be particularly misleading to assume that the substance must follow the acid–base extraction behaviour of a simple alkaloid.[ref]
Colour reactions could support the examination of a cleaned extract. Contemporary work described a brown or reddish colour with sulfuric acid, changed to violet by bromine vapour; the hydrochloric-acid green colour was already acknowledged to lack specificity. Compare a reference and a blank under the same conditions. Colour in a dirty stomach extract is not an identification, and tasting an unknown extract is not an acceptable analytical test.[ref]
A particularly relevant development available before this examination was the physiological test investigated by Charles Hilton Fagge and Thomas Stevenson in 1865. An extracted fraction was compared with known digitalin using the response of a frog's heart. The informative effect was irregular beating followed by arrest with the cardiac ventricle pale and firmly contracted, rather than mere illness or death. Crucially, their experiments also showed that ordinary human stomach contents could injure frogs, and other cardiac poisons could resemble digitalin. A solvent blank, a comparable unpoisoned extract and a reference poison therefore matter. These are historical experiments, not a proposal to repeat animal testing for this solution.[ref][ref]
The defensible conclusion combines extraction behaviour, chemical reactions, physiological specificity and the clinical circumstances, acknowledging uncertainty if these disagree. A modern laboratory would instead seek specific validated instrumental identification, for example chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry for an identified cardiac glycoside, with reference material and controls. That later capability must not be credited to an 1866 examiner.[ref]
