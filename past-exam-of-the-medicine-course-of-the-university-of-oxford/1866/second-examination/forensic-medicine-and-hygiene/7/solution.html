 ... Medicine course of the University of Oxford Past exam of the medicine course of the University of Oxford 1866 Second examination Forensic medicine and hygiene 7
OurBigBook.com Words: 757
Take representative samples of a measured volume, identify the impurity by an appropriate reaction, and express the blank-corrected amount per volume of ward air. Excess carbon dioxide, moisture, organic contamination, Ammonia and suspended material are the principal groups to investigate; combustion or defective drains may introduce additional gases. Smell alone measures none of them reliably.
Record occupancy, the position and height of sampling, open doors or windows, heating and lighting, temperature and sampling time. Compare indoor and outdoor samples collected under comparable conditions. Avoid breathing into the collecting vessel. A sample near an open window may miss conditions at the beds, and a sample during cleaning may contain unusually disturbed dust. Explain the sampling conditions with the result.
For carbon dioxide, “carbonic acid” in period terminology, collect a known volume in a calibrated vessel and introduce a measured portion of standardized barium hydroxide solution. Shake to promote absorption. Carbon dioxide consumes the alkaline reagent and forms barium carbonate:
CO2​+Ba(OH)2​⟶BaCO3​↓+H2​O.
(1)
Determine the residual alkali in the clear liquid, excluding the carbonate precipitate from an acid determination that would redissolve it. Compare with an equal unexposed reagent portion. An 1864 report describes 7–10 litre globes and checks against gravimetric absorption, so this is an available period method rather than an instrument projected backwards.[ref]
If c is the hydrogen ion equivalent concentration of the titrating acid, and vb​ and vs​ are the respective blank and exposed-solution titration volumes, then, for the entire absorption charge,
nCO2​​=2c(vb​−vs​)​,xCO2​​=PVair​nCO2​​RT​.​
(2)
The factor two arises because one carbon dioxide molecule consumes two alkali equivalents. If only an aliquot is titrated, multiply by the ratio of total solution volume to aliquot volume. Here P, T and Vair​ describe the same measured gas sample, with consistent wet- or dry-gas conventions; R is the molar gas constant. Report 106xCO2​​ as parts per million by volume. Other absorbed acidic gases are potential interferences. The calculation is a stoichiometric and ideal gas law interpretation, not a direct measure of infectiousness.
For water vapour, compare dry- and wet-bulb thermometers to estimate relative humidity, or pass a known volume through a suitable preweighed drying absorber and determine its mass gain. The latter gives collected water mass divided by sampled air volume, with a blank and appropriate control of other absorbable vapours. Humidity is relevant to ward conditions, but is not by itself evidence that the air contains a particular disease agent.
For Ammonia, moist red litmus becoming blue is a screening reaction, not a selective quantitative analysis. Absorb a measured volume of air into a known amount of dilute acid; the reduction in residual acidity measures absorbed volatile bases. Other alkaline vapours interfere, so do not call the result pure Ammonia without further discrimination. A modern comparison is the validated acid-treated sorbent collection and ion chromatography of the institutional air-sampling method; it too requires calibrated flow, blanks and checks for sample loss.[ref]
For suspended dust, collect material on a preweighed filter, condition and reweigh it, and divide the blank-corrected mass increase by sampled volume. Microscopy can describe fibres, cells and other particles, but cannot turn an unspecified speck into an identified pathogen. A modern gravimetric sampling method illustrates the importance of controlled filter handling and air-volume measurement.[ref]
Period investigators also assessed oxidizable organic contamination through the consumption of an oxidizing reagent such as potassium permanganate. Such a result is an operational measure of chemical consumption, dependent on the procedure; it does not reveal a unique substance or count living organisms. An 1865 review already warned that inorganic reducing substances also decolourized the reagent, making supposed quantitative organic estimates unreliable.[ref] Similarly, a lead-salt darkening reaction may support the presence of hydrogen sulfide where drains are implicated, but this gas need not be present in every ward. Investigate carbon monoxide if combustion is suspected: it is odourless and cannot be excluded because the room smells fresh.[ref]
A useful report therefore separates measured carbon dioxide, humidity, chemical contamination and particles, states detection limits and interferences, and avoids declaring ward air safe from a single result. Infectious aerosols require a separate assessment; settling plates or a few microscopic fields do not quantitatively characterize everything that patients inhale. Current infection-control guidance likewise warns about the limitations of nonspecific environmental sampling.[ref]
