In a period account of bread adulteration, give first place to alum, then distinguish undeclared flour or starch substitutions, water-retaining additions, and alleged mineral or metallic adulterants. Their actual occurrence must be demonstrated; a long list in a textbook is not proof that every loaf contains them.
Alum was added to alter the appearance and baking behaviour of inferior flour and to help produce an apparently acceptable white loaf. It is the especially well-supported English example. Arthur Hill Hassall's 1855 investigations reported alum in two series of bread samples, while finding neither potato nor other foreign flour in those series. The same work found none of the suspected mineral fillers in its examined series of forty-four flour samples. These are observations about the samples examined, not a warrant to declare all English bread adulterated.[ref]
The organic substitutions to consider include potato or potato starch, and cheaper flours from rice, maize, rye or beans. Their economic significance is the substitution of a different material for flour sold as wheat, rather than the proposition that every such food is intrinsically poisonous. A declared mixed-grain loaf is a different case from concealed substitution. Defective or damaged flour can also be disguised through additions that improve apparent texture or colour. Moisture matters because a loaf retaining excessive water can deliver less food material for the same purchased mass.
Historical reports also discussed chalk, gypsum or plaster of Paris, clay, magnesia and other mineral fillers, together with copper(II) sulfate, or “blue vitriol”, as a baking addition. These should be presented as reported or suspected adulterants of bread or its flour, with prevalence qualified. An 1851 contemporary review gives the broad period list; the later direct analyses show why allegations require testing.[ref]
The comparison can be made experimentally. Examine flour particles with a microscope for foreign starch granules and plant tissues, comparing reference materials; baking disrupts many structures, so unbaked flour is often more informative than a cooked crumb. Determine moisture by drying a weighed portion, and examine the ash for unusual inorganic residue. Neither excess ash alone nor a metal detected in trace quantity identifies the material added or its intention. Sampling, blanks and the natural composition of flour must be considered.
In particular, testing for alum requires distinguishing introduced material from naturally present mineral constituents and from contamination of the reagents. A pre-examination 1865 review explicitly criticized analytical errors in separating aluminium compounds from bread ash and disputed the claim that alum was invariably present. Thus the answer should name the principal adulterants while keeping the strength of the evidence visible. It should not repeat historical claims of harm as modern toxicological conclusions without an appropriate exposure assessment.[ref]
