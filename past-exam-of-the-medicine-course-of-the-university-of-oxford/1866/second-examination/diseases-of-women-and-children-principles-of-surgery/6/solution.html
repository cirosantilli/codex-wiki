Early congenital syphilis can affect the skin, mucosa, bones, viscera and nervous system, yet an infected infant may look well at birth. The symptoms are a variable constellation, not a compulsory sequence seen in every child.
The classical infantile picture includes persistent nasal discharge or “snuffles”, difficulty feeding, poor growth and a rash that can involve the palms and soles. The eruption may be maculopapular or blistering, with desquamation; colour descriptions such as “coppery” are neither universal nor diagnostic alone. Fissures around the mouth and other mucocutaneous lesions may occur. Look also for hepatosplenomegaly, jaundice, anaemia, enlarged lymph nodes and evidence of systemic illness.[ref]
Bone disease is particularly important when interpreting an apparently motionless limb. Osteochondritis or periostitis can make movement painful, producing pseudoparalysis. This is distinct from the loss of motor function discussed under infantile paralysis. The nervous system can also be involved, including meningitis or seizures. Severe disease can be associated with prematurity, hydrops, stillbirth or neonatal death, although a living infant with mild signs may present much later.[ref]
The period term “hereditary syphilis” should not be read as a modern account of Mendelian inheritance. Modern science identifies infection by Treponema pallidum, transmitted to the fetus during pregnancy, rather than an inherited constitutional taint. Absence of obvious maternal symptoms does not exclude transmission. Equally, a rash or nasal discharge alone is not enough to prove syphilis or justify an allegation about a parent's behaviour.[ref]
Later disease is useful for completing the history, but is not the usual neonatal picture. It can include notched permanent incisors or abnormal molars, a saddle-shaped nose, anterior bowing of the shins, interstitial keratitis and sensorineural hearing loss. These permanent dental signs cannot be demanded of a young infant before the relevant teeth have developed.[ref]
Modern correction: examine and test in the context of maternal infection and treatment, even when the baby has no symptoms. Infant and maternal quantitative nontreponemal results, examination, treatment history and selected direct or additional tests guide assessment. Passive maternal antibodies complicate interpretation of infant serology; a positive test does not have the same meaning in every setting. Appropriate penicillin treatment and follow-up are determined by the clinical scenario, not by waiting for a characteristic rash. Historical mercurial treatment is not its modern substitute.[ref]
