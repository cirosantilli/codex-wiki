A carbuncle is a deep infection involving connected adjacent hair follicles. Local damage, bacterial carriage and impaired host defence can favour its development; a drainable focus needs drainage. It is more extensive than a single superficial follicular pustule.
Relevant predisposing circumstances include diabetes, friction or injury at the skin, close contact or shared contaminated articles, and impaired immunity. Ask about recurrent skin infections, medication and systemic health. A lesion on the nape in someone with diabetes is a familiar pattern, but the source supplies neither its site nor a test result, so neither may be asserted as an observation. Evaluate for fever, spreading cellulitis, severe pain and sepsis, and consider whether a lesion labelled a “carbuncle” is actually another disorder.[ref]
In historical language, “debility”, age, poor nourishment and a disordered constitutional state were often invoked. Some of these descriptions might correspond to a recognizable predisposition; others were vague explanations after the event. Period local treatments included poulticing, incisions to relieve tension and evacuate dead or suppurating tissue, and attempts at destruction with caustics. A November 1866 letter on boils and carbuncles is evidence that both their origin and treatment were actively disputed. Its proposed “parasiticide” treatment must not be read as proof of a fungal cause or as a modern instruction to apply corrosive chemicals.[ref]
Modern correction: the usual process is bacterial, commonly involving Staphylococcus aureus, and a fungal theory inferred from apparent response to a caustic is inadequate. Assess for a drainable collection; incision and drainage is the core treatment of a carbuncle. Microbiological culture of pus can guide therapy, especially in difficult or recurrent cases. Systemic antibiotics are added according to illness severity, surrounding infection, host factors and the suspected or demonstrated organism. Severe illness requires escalation, not delay while waiting for the lesion to “ripen”.[ref]
Address diabetes or other identified predispositions, dress draining lesions and reduce transmission through hand hygiene and avoiding shared personal articles. Repeated lesions call for reassessment of diagnosis and sources of reinfection. A destructive historical incision or caustic is not equivalent to controlled drainage with appropriate analgesia, infection control and follow-up.
