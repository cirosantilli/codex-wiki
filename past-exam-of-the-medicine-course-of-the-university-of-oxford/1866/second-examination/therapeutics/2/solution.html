Treat scarlet fever as an infectious illness, support fluid and food intake, and distinguish local suppuration, invasive infection and delayed immune complications. The severity of the throat disease and the general condition matter more than the brilliance of the rash.
In 1866, reasonable supportive care meant separation from susceptible contacts, attentive nursing, a comfortable ventilated room, rest and food and fluids that could be swallowed. Severe throat pain, difficulty swallowing, increasing neck swelling, breathing difficulty, marked prostration or altered awareness required repeated examination. Convalescence also needed observation of the urine and swelling. Historical attempts to drive out a rash by excessive heat, aggressive purging or stimulants did not establish a way to remove the cause. Renal complications after apparently successful recovery were already recognized: a contemporary digest discusses delayed albuminuria after the apparent convalescence from scarlet fever.[ref]
Modern correction: scarlet fever is associated with toxin-producing group A streptococcal infection, and appropriate testing and antibiotic treatment replace supposed rash-specific remedies. A compatible illness may be confirmed by a rapid antigen test, molecular test or throat microbiological culture. Penicillin or amoxicillin is a usual treatment for confirmed disease; allergy and local resistance affect alternatives. Supportive fluid intake and appropriate relief of pain remain useful. Infection-control measures and the applicable exclusion interval after treatment reduce transmission.[ref]
The complications require different responses. Otitis media, cervical lymphadenitis and a peritonsillar abscess or retropharyngeal abscess result from spread of infection. New focal pain or swelling calls for examination; an abscess may require drainage as well as antibiotics. Airway compromise, profound illness or signs of sepsis require urgent hospital care, rather than simply changing a gargle. These complications are distinct from acute rheumatic fever and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, which arise through the response to the preceding infection.[ref]
For the renal complication, look for dark or scanty urine, haematuria, oedema and hypertension, including after the throat and rash have improved. Assess kidney function and fluid balance. Treatment addresses hypertension and fluid excess; severe renal complications require specialist management. Antibiotics eradicate persisting group A streptococcal infection and reduce its spread, but are not proved to prevent every case of post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. When acute rheumatic fever occurs, assess for carditis, treat the inflammatory manifestations and any heart failure, eradicate residual streptococcal infection and arrange appropriate secondary antibiotic prophylaxis. Prevention must not be reduced to waiting for swollen anatomical joints or obvious heart disease.[ref][ref] Thus “counteracting the complications” means identifying the actual process and its consequences, not applying one remedy to every late symptom.
