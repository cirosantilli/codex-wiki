Disappearance of renal oedema is not proof that the kidney disease has resolved. Reassess recovery, make the return to work gradual and address actual exposures and recurrent illness. “Renal dropsy” is a clinical description, not one modern histological diagnosis. It could accompany an acute nephritic illness or persist as part of more chronic disease; the question does not establish which.
A period answer would warn against returning immediately from convalescence to prolonged wet work, chilling, exhaustion and poorly supported living conditions. Dry clothing, suitable warmth, adequate food and rest, avoidance of excess ethanol, and opportunities to change out of wet clothes were practical recommendations. The intended rationale was to reduce strains thought to precipitate another renal attack. This advice should be labelled historical: cold exposure is not a complete modern explanation for glomerulonephritis, and an artisan's occupation does not establish any particular toxic exposure.
The important transferable precaution is continued observation. Re-examine for swelling, breathlessness, changes in urine output, persistent albuminuria and the return of constitutional illness. A nineteenth-century examination could include testing urine for protein and examining its sediment, rather than declaring cure from the appearance of the face or legs. Contemporary reporting of delayed albuminuria after apparent recovery from an eruptive illness illustrates the reason for follow-up, without proving that every later abnormality is a relapse of the same disease.[ref]
Modern correction: the prevention plan follows the actual renal diagnosis and objective recovery. Assess blood pressure, serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate and urinary albumin or protein as appropriate, and compare serial results. Some people recover completely from an acute illness; others need surveillance and treatment for persistent chronic kidney disease. Ongoing hypertension, substantial albuminuria or declining renal function requires attention rather than merely warmer clothing. Medicines and supplements should be reviewed for nephrotoxicity, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs require particular caution in susceptible people.[ref]
For work, establish the actual demands: heavy exertion, inability to drink or take breaks, heat, wet conditions and contact with potentially nephrotoxic materials. Adjust duties to the degree of recovery, with occupational assessment where needed. Do not assume a toxin or impose permanent unemployment merely from the word “artisan”. Fluid or salt restrictions and drugs should be individualized to the renal condition; indiscriminate restriction or repeated purging can worsen depletion. The aim is sustainable recovery with follow-up, not a claim that one garment or tonic guarantees against relapse.
