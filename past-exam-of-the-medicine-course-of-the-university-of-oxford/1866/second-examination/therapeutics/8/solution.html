The sound historical indication was selected dropsical states associated with disordered cardiac action; the modern uses of particular cardiac glycosides are narrower and explicitly rhythm-dependent. A digitalis preparation was not simply a drug for every slow urinary flow or rapid pulse.
Before 1866, William Withering's account had already demonstrated both the possible relief of dropsy and the danger of careless use. He recorded successes and failures and warned about nausea, vomiting, visual disturbance and excessive slowing of the pulse. These observations support careful assessment of effect and toxicity, not a method of administering foxglove until poisoning occurs.[ref] The historical assessment would follow breathing, swelling, pulse, urine and general condition. Increased urine and reduced oedema could represent relief while the original cardiac disorder persisted.
Calling the drug a cardiac “sedative” from the slowing of the pulse could obscure a different effect on contraction. Likewise, apparent diuresis did not establish it as a universal remedy for renal dropsy. Period applications extended into febrile or haemorrhagic illnesses, but such claims should not be promoted into modern indications merely because the pulse changed. The problem is to distinguish treatment of a useful physiological target from suppression of a sign.
Modern correction: digoxin increases myocardial contractility and slows conduction through the atrioventricular node. In selected heart failure it can improve symptoms and reduce hospitalization. A randomized, placebo-controlled trial in people with chronic heart failure and normal sinus rhythm found fewer hospitalizations but no reduction in overall mortality.[ref] It can also control resting ventricular rate in selected adults with chronic atrial fibrillation. It is not a general treatment for a fast pulse, and the presence of an accessory conduction pathway can make its use hazardous. Ventricular fibrillation is a contraindication.[ref]
The margin between useful and toxic exposure is small. Renal function, age, body composition, interactions and electrolyte disturbances influence risk. Monitor the clinical response, electrocardiogram, relevant laboratory results and drug concentrations when indicated. Nausea, vomiting, visual symptoms, marked bradycardia or new cardiac arrhythmias should raise concern for toxicity, not be treated as confirmation that the drug is beginning to work.[ref] Historical leaf or tincture preparations must therefore not be substituted for a modern standardized preparation, or converted into modern doses from a shared name alone.
