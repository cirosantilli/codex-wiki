The main differences concern cellular construction and the usual modes of obtaining food, movement and development; the main interdependence concerns transfers of matter and energy through ecosystems. The nineteenth-century animal–vegetable division is useful as a comparison of familiar forms, but it is not a complete modern classification of life.
A typical plant cell has a cell wall, a large central vacuole and, in photosynthetic tissue, chloroplasts. An animal cell lacks a cellulose wall and chloroplasts and has a different extracellular and tissue organization. Both possess a cell membrane, cytoplasm, a cell nucleus and, in their usual aerobic cells, mitochondria. Roots and other non-green parts of a plant need not contain functional chloroplasts; an erythrocyte's loss of its cell nucleus is likewise not a definition of all animal cells.[ref]
Most green plants acquire carbon through photosynthesis, taking carbon dioxide from their surroundings and mineral nutrients and water chiefly through roots. Familiar animals obtain preformed organic material by feeding and digesting it or absorbing material derived from a host. Animals generally have conspicuous coordinated movement, and many have nervous systems and muscles; higher plants regulate growth and movement without such organs. These are useful generalizations with exceptions. Some plants depend on other organisms for carbon; some animals are permanently attached; and neither lack of locomotion nor a green appearance alone settles every microscopic classification. Fungi and bacteria should not be forced into one of these two kingdoms in a modern account.
One major interdependence is nutritional. Photosynthesis converts light input into chemical energy stored in organic material. Plant material feeds herbivores and, through them, carnivores; it also enters detrital food webs. An animal that eats an animal can therefore remain indirectly dependent on primary production by plants, algae or other photosynthetic organisms. Chemical elements pass between organisms, but energy is progressively dissipated as heat; it is not recycled in the same closed manner as an element.[ref]
A second relation is gas exchange. In its simplest overall form, oxygenic photosynthesis may be represented by
6CO2​+6H2​O+light energy⟶C6​H12​O6​+6O2​.
(1)
Aerobic utilization of the resulting glucose has the complementary overall balance
C6​H12​O6​+6O2​⟶6CO2​+6H2​O+released energy.
(2)
These balanced equations show complementary material transfers without making the underlying pathways literal reversals of one another. Plants also perform cellular respiration, by day as well as by night. They can therefore release carbon dioxide while consuming oxygen; a sufficiently illuminated leaf may simultaneously show net uptake of carbon dioxide because photosynthesis exceeds respiration. Stating that plants only purify air and animals only spoil it would erase this essential distinction between gross processes and net exchange.
Other relations close material cycles. Animals return organic matter through excretion and death; microbial decomposition and mineral transformations return nutrients in forms available for plant uptake. Most atmospheric nitrogen is not directly available to ordinary plants merely because animals have breathed or excreted near them: nitrogen fixation, ammonification and often nitrification involve particular microorganisms. Plants and animals also interact through pollination, seed dispersal, grazing, parasitism and shelter. Such relations may benefit both partners, one partner at the other's expense, or different partners under different conditions; interdependence is not universal mutual benevolence.
A closed illuminated aquarium containing plants and animals gives an instructive experiment, provided gas exchange with air, illumination, nutrient supply and organismal amounts are specified. Dissolved oxygen and carbon dioxide should be followed over light and dark periods. Survival alone is an ambiguous readout: microorganisms, stored food and air exchange may support it temporarily. A more controlled comparison includes illuminated and dark plant vessels and animal-only vessels. The expected alternation of net gas exchange makes the physiological distinction visible while exposing why no simple rule guarantees indefinite balance in any arbitrarily stocked closed jar.
