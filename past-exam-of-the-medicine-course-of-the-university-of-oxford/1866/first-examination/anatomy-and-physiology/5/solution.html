Chyle is intestinal lymph enriched with absorbed dietary lipids; its usual route ends in the venous circulation at the left jugulosubclavian junction. The milky appearance after a fat-containing meal is chiefly due to suspended lipid-rich particles, now called chylomicrons.
Formation begins with lipid digestion in the small intestine. Pancreatic lipase helps hydrolyse dietary triglycerides; bile salts help the digestion products reach the intestinal absorptive surface in mixed micelles. Enterocytes take up the products, reconstruct much of the long-chain triglyceride, and package it with other lipids and apolipoproteins into chylomicrons. These leave the basolateral side of the enterocytes and enter the central lacteals of the intestinal villi. Thus the particles carried in chyle are not simply intact globules of a meal passing through an open hole from the intestinal lumen.[ref]
From the lacteals, chyle enters lymphatic channels in the intestinal wall and then collecting vessels in the mesentery. These vessels pass through mesenteric lymph nodes, with afferent vessels entering and efferent vessels leaving the nodes. Successive collectors join the intestinal lymphatic trunk or trunks. They usually communicate with the cisterna chyli, an expanded origin of the thoracic duct in the upper lumbar region, near the bodies of L1–L2 and to the right of the aorta. The cisterna chyli also receives lymph from lumbar trunks; the stream beyond it therefore contains material from more than the intestine. A conspicuous sac is not present in every individual.
The thoracic duct passes through the aortic hiatus of the respiratory diaphragm and ascends in the posterior mediastinum, typically between the aorta and azygos vein, behind the oesophagus. It crosses towards the left in the middle or upper thorax, continues to the root of the neck and arches to its terminal venous opening. The usual endpoint is the left venous angle, where the left internal jugular vein joins the left subclavian vein; terminal branches and the precise opening show anatomical variation. It is not normally the right venous angle or a direct entrance into the portal vein.[ref]
The flow is promoted by intrinsic contraction of collecting lymphatic vessels, movements of surrounding muscles and respiratory changes of pressure. Lymphatic valves limit backward movement. Lymph nodes are interposed biological structures, not pumps that propel every particle independently.[ref]
This route applies particularly to the long-chain dietary lipids carried by chylomicrons. Most absorbed glucose and amino acids, and much shorter-chain fatty acid material, follow portal vascular routes instead. Confusing these pathways would obscure the reason for asking separately about chyle. In a classical anatomical demonstration, the mesenteric channels of an appropriately fed animal appear white, and an injected or dissected preparation can trace their continuity towards the thoracic duct. The timing after a fatty meal matters: an empty-looking channel in a fasted preparation does not prove absence of a lymphatic vessel. The molecular account of chylomicrons is a modern explanation of the older gross observation.
