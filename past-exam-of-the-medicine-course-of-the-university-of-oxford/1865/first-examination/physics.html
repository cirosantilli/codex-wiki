Original question.
What are the Volume, the Mass, and the Weight of a body ? Has the same body the same weight under all circumstances ?

Solution

Words: 199
1 OurBigBook.com
The volume V is the three-dimensional space occupied by the body. Its mass m measures its inertia: in Newtonian mechanics the resultant force is ma. Its gravitational weight is the force exerted on it by gravity,
W=mg.​
(1)
For a uniform body, m=ρV, where ρ is its mass density; more generally m=∫ρdV. Volume can change with temperature or pressure while the body's mass remains fixed in the classical closed-system model. Equal volumes of different materials need not have equal masses.
The same mass need not have the same gravitational weight. The local gravitational acceleration changes between planets and with position on Earth. Outside a spherical Earth, g(r)=GM⊕​/r2, so increasing altitude reduces it. Earth's rotation and shape also affect terrestrial weighing: a scale at rest on the rotating Earth responds to the effective acceleration including the centrifugal contribution, rather than simply to a spherical gravitational field.
Distinguish gravitational weight from apparent weight. A supporting scale measures a contact force. In a vertically accelerating cabin, upward acceleration b gives N=m(g+b) while contact is maintained. During common free fall, N=0 although mg remains nonzero. Immersion in a fluid can similarly reduce the support needed through buoyancy, without removing the gravitational attraction. These distinctions explain why a change in a balance reading is not automatically a change of mass.

Original question.
What is the law of universal gravitation ? How is it proved to be true ?

Solution

Words: 249
2 OurBigBook.com
For point masses m1​,m2​ separated by distance r, Newton's law of universal gravitation gives an attractive central force of magnitude
F=Gr2m1​m2​​.​
(2)
The vector force on body 2 is −Gm1​m2​(r2​−r1​)/∣r2​−r1​∣3. For extended bodies one adds their elemental interactions. The shell theorem explains why a spherically symmetric body acts externally like its total mass placed at its centre.
The law has experimental and astronomical support, rather than a deductive proof from definitions. Kepler's laws provide one test. For a circular orbit of radius r and period T, the required centripetal acceleration is 4π2r/T2. With T2∝r3, it varies as r−2. The full elliptical-orbit analysis, with the law of areas identifying a central interaction, gives the same inverse-square dependence and predicts the orbit's form.
The Earth–Moon comparison connects celestial motion to terrestrial falling. Using illustrative mean values R⊕​≃6.37×106m, rM​≃3.84×108m and g0​≃9.81ms−2, the inverse-square prediction is
g0​(rM​R⊕​​)2≃2.70×10−3ms−2.
(3)
The circular estimate 4π2rM​/TM2​, with TM​≃27.3 days, is about 2.72×10−3ms−2. Their agreement is the relevant check; the numbers are approximate mean-orbit values, not exact observations of a circular Moon.
The Cavendish experiment adds a laboratory test. Small suspended masses twist a fine fibre when larger source masses are moved nearby. Comparing the torque with the suspension's calibrated restoring response measures the weak attraction between laboratory bodies. Reversing source positions helps separate the gravitational signal from a persistent instrumental offset. Planetary perturbations and additional laboratory geometries provide further tests of the common law. Its excellent accuracy in ordinary weak-field conditions does not make it an exact theory in every regime.

Original question.
Shew that the weight of a body floating in a fluid is equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces, and that the centres of gravity of the body and of the displaced fluid are in the same vertical line.

Solution

Words: 209
3 OurBigBook.com
Take a body floating at rest in a homogeneous fluid, with uniform gravity, no tether or other support, and negligible air buoyancy. Imagine replacing the submerged part by the same volume of the surrounding fluid. That imaginary fluid parcel would be at rest: the surrounding hydrostatic pressure would balance its gravitational weight. The actual body experiences the same pressure traction over the submerged boundary, so the resulting buoyant force is upward with magnitude
B=ρf​gVdisplaced​.
(4)
The body's vertical force balance therefore gives Archimedes' principle in the requested floating case:
mg=ρf​gVdisplaced​.​
(5)
The argument determines displaced submerged volume, not necessarily the total volume of a partly emerged body.
The pressure resultant acts through the centre of buoyancy, the centre of mass of the imaginary displaced fluid. Let G be the body's centre of gravity and Bc​ the centre of buoyancy. The two remaining external forces are equal and opposite vertical forces. If their lines of action were distinct, they would form a nonzero couple and the body would turn. Rotational equilibrium consequently requires
G and Bc​ lie on the same vertical line.​
(6)
This is a condition for equilibrium, not sufficient evidence of its stability. Tilting changes the displaced shape and hence the position of the centre of buoyancy; the resulting torque determines whether the body returns towards its original orientation. A centre of gravity above the centre of buoyancy does not by itself settle that floating-body stability question.

Original question.
What is the principle of a barometer ? What corrections have to be applied to the reading of a cistern barometer ?

Solution

Words: 386
4 OurBigBook.com
A mercury barometer balances atmospheric pressure against the hydrostatic head between the open cistern surface and the mercury surface in a closed tube. Writing pt​ for the pressure above the column, the ideal relation is
patm​=pt​+ρHg​(T)glocal​h.​
(7)
The relevant h is the vertical difference between the two mercury levels. It is not simply the length of filled glass, and the top space need not be an absolutely perfect vacuum.
A cistern barometer needs the following corrections or controlled adjustments:
  • Cistern level and scale zero. When mercury rises in a fixed tube, the reservoir level falls. A fixed-zero scale must allow for both motions. For uniform tube area At​ and effective cistern free area Ac​, volume conservation gives Ac​Δzc​=−At​Δzt​, so Δh=(1+At​/Ac​)Δzt​. In a Fortin barometer, an adjustable cistern brings its surface to a fixed index before the column is read.
  • Temperature. Both mercury density and scale length change. To express a corrected physical height h as an equivalent height at reference density ρ0​ and standard gravity gs​, use hs​=ρ(T)glocal​h/(ρ0​gs​). If a scale marked at the reference temperature expands with coefficient αs​, while mercury has volume-expansion coefficient β, a reading R at temperature difference ΔT has the first-order thermal factor 1+(αs​−β)ΔT. For an ordinary metal scale warmer than its reference, the mercury-density effect dominates and the thermal correction reduces the reading.
  • Gravity. The same physical mercury height represents a different pressure where g differs. Reduction to a conventional mercury pressure unit includes glocal​/gs​; this does not assert that local gravity has physically changed.
  • Capillarity, scale and sighting. Correct the capillary depression and calibrated scale/index errors, read the proper part of the meniscus, avoid parallax, and set the instrument vertical. Mercury contamination and changes in the meniscus can compromise a previously determined capillary correction.
  • Top-space pressure. Trapped gas and mercury vapour add pt​. A significant trapped-air error cannot be cured by pretending the top space is empty.
The observational precautions are also described in the Guide to Meteorological Instruments and Methods of Observation by the World Meteorological Organization.[ref] The quantitative corrections are separated in Mercury Barometers and Manometers by W. G. Brombacher and others, particularly the cistern, temperature and gravity discussions. Reduction of a weather-station pressure to sea level is a separate atmospheric comparison, not an intrinsic correction to the instrument.[ref]

Original question.
By what means are the highest and the lowest known temperatures obtained, and how are such temperatures measured thermometrically ?

Solution

Words: 299
5 OurBigBook.com
For the historical laboratory context, intense heating could be obtained from an electric arc between carbon electrodes and from an oxyhydrogen flame. The first converts electrical work into a very hot emitting region; the second releases chemical energy rapidly in combustion. An intense light or an ability to melt a particular solid establishes a useful physical effect, but does not by itself provide a calibrated numerical temperature.
The cold end could be reached by rapidly evaporating a volatile material and reducing the surrounding pressure. In Faraday's carbon-dioxide and ether cooling experiment, a bath of solid carbon dioxide and diethyl ether was placed under an air-pump receiver. Removing vapour promoted further cooling. Faraday's 1845 report gives an alcohol-thermometer reading near −106∘F in the atmospheric-pressure bath and −166∘F, approximately −110∘C, under reduced pressure. He explicitly noted thermometer lag, thickening alcohol and possible errors of several Fahrenheit degrees. These are dated reported estimates, not a claim to a modern cold-temperature record.[ref]
Measurement must be separated from temperature production. A liquid-in-glass thermometer works only while its liquid, glass and graduation remain usable. A gas thermometer infers temperature from reproducible gas pressure or volume changes, with calibration and departures from ideal-gas behaviour accounted for. At high temperatures, suitable expansion or calorimetric pyrometers can be compared against known points, but material changes and extrapolation introduce error. For example, transferring a hot body of known thermal properties into a calorimeter estimates its starting temperature only after allowing for its heat capacity and heat lost in transfer. The very hottest arc regions are not safely assigned the reading of an ordinary mercury thermometer.
Faraday's published lectures demonstrate the carbon-electrode electric light and electrical heating of platinum. They provide contemporary apparatus context; the later reprint's date is distinguished in its bibliography entry.[ref]

Original question.
What is the boiling point of a fluid ? What causes will raise or lower the boiling point ?

Solution

Words: 227
6 OurBigBook.com
At a stated external pressure, the boiling point of a pure liquid is the temperature at which its saturation vapor pressure equals that pressure. A vapour bubble inside the liquid must also withstand the hydrostatic head and any capillary pressure; the simple free-surface boiling-point rule neglects those small local corrections.
Increasing external pressure raises the usual boiling point, while lowering it lowers the boiling point. The Clapeyron equation explains the direction:
dTdpsat​​=TΔvL​>0
(8)
for ordinary vaporization with positive latent heat L and a larger vapour specific volume. Consequently water can boil below its usual open-vessel temperature at altitude or under partial vacuum, and above it in a sufficiently pressurized vessel.
Composition also matters. A nonvolatile dissolved solute generally lowers the solvent's vapour pressure and produces boiling-point elevation at fixed external pressure. A volatile second component can instead raise or lower the boiling temperature according to the mixture's phase equilibrium; an azeotrope is a useful warning against applying the nonvolatile-solute rule to every mixture.
Finally, distinguish the equilibrium boiling point from the observed onset of bubbles. A smooth clean vessel or a shortage of nucleation sites can permit superheating; agitation or suitable existing sites can make boiling begin nearer equilibrium. Heating power mainly controls the rate of sustained boiling under otherwise fixed conditions, rather than setting an arbitrary new equilibrium boiling point. A thermometer must be placed to measure the intended liquid or saturated vapour region, rather than a locally overheated wall.

Original question.
What reasons are there for supposing heat to be a form of motion ?

Solution

Words: 239
7 OurBigBook.com
The strong evidence is the reproducible conversion between mechanical work and thermal changes. In Joule's paddle-wheel experiment, descending masses drive vanes in a liquid, and a measured input of mechanical energy produces a measured rise in temperature. Allowing for the vessel and thermal losses gives a quantitative mechanical equivalent. Sustained frictional heating, including Rumford's cannon-boring experiment, also challenged a simple account in which rubbing merely released a fixed stored supply of a material heat fluid.[ref]
Electrical and compressional experiments reinforce the connection. A resistor warms under Joule heating, with electrical input ∫VIdt. A gas compressed with little heat transfer can rise in temperature, showing that mechanical work can increase its internal energy. Conversely an expanding gas can perform work while cooling. Such experiments connect several kinds of change through a conserved energy balance.
The kinetic theory of gases supplies a microscopic model. Translational molecular motion produces momentum transfer to walls; for a dilute monatomic ideal gas,
p=31​VN​m⟨v2⟩,U=23​NkB​T.
(9)
Thus increasing molecular kinetic energy is reflected in temperature and pressure changes. These formulas describe a specified model, not a proof that every substance's internal energy consists only of translational motion. Rotations, vibrations and intermolecular potential energy also matter.
In modern terminology, heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. The body has internal energy, and microscopic motion accounts for part of it. Calling heat “a form of motion” captures the historical rejection of a conserved material caloric, but should not obscure that distinction between stored energy and an energy-transfer process.

Original question.
Explain the formation of images by a plane mirror, and by a convex lens.

Solution

Words: 276
8 OurBigBook.com
A plane mirror sends each incident ray into its specularly reflected direction, with equal incidence and reflection angles measured from the surface normal. For a point object in front of the mirror, the backward extensions of the reflected rays meet at the reflected point behind the plane. The image is therefore virtual, upright, equal in size and as far behind the mirror as the object is in front. The actual rays do not travel behind the mirror. In coordinates with mirror plane x=0, the object point (x,y,z) is mapped to (−x,y,z).
A converging convex lens refracts a paraxial pencil according to the thin-lens equation
u1​+v1​=f1​,m=−uv​,​
(10)
where u>0 is the distance to a real object, f>0 is the focal length, and v>0 denotes an image on the opposite side. Two constructed rays locate the image: a ray parallel to the optical axis emerges towards the far optical focus, and a ray through the ideal thin lens's optical centre proceeds undeviated. Their intersection is a real image; if they diverge, their backward extensions locate a virtual one.
For u>2f the real inverted image is reduced; at u=2f it is equal in size; for f<u<2f it is enlarged. At u=f the emergent pencil is parallel and the image is at infinity in the ideal model. For 0<u<f, the image is virtual, upright and enlarged on the object's side. A distant object gives an image near the far focal plane. The lensmaker's equation explains why shape alone does not guarantee convergence: the surrounding medium and refractive index also matter. Spherical aberration, chromatic aberration and diffraction limit the ideal point-image construction. The statement that the centre ray is undeviated is the thin-lens approximation, rather than a general rule for a thick compound optical instrument.

Original question.
How do you account for the colours of a rainbow, of a soap-bubble, of mother-of-pearl ?

Solution

Words: 281
9 OurBigBook.com
For a primary rainbow, sunlight is refracted into a water drop, internally reflected once, and refracted out. The refractive index varies with wavelength, producing optical dispersion. If i is the incidence angle and r the refracted angle, a spherical-drop ray has deviation
D=π+2i−4r,sini=nsinr.
(11)
A stationary deviation concentrates neighbouring rays into a bright angular region. For visible light in water, the primary bow lies around 42∘ from the antisolar direction, with red outside violet. Each observer receives the suitable colour from drops on the corresponding cone; the bow is not a fixed coloured object attached to one particular group of drops. Further internal reflections produce secondary bows with different angles and colour order.
A soap film uses thin-film interference. Rays reflected from its two surfaces acquire an optical path difference 2ntcosr. With air on both sides of a nonabsorbing film, one of the surface reflections has a phase reversal. A constructive reflected condition is consequently
2ntcosr=(j+21​)λ.​
(12)
Different thicknesses, angles and wavelengths therefore give different reflected colours. A sufficiently thin symmetric film becomes dark because its leading reflected contributions cancel. The colour is chiefly an interference effect, rather than a pigment carried by the soap.
Mother-of-pearl, or nacre, has a structured arrangement of mineral tablets and organic layers. Repeated refractive-index contrasts produce wavelength-selective reflection; surface grooves can also act as diffraction gratings. Thus both multilayer interference and surface diffraction can contribute. A representative repeating-layer condition is 2(n1​d1​cosr1​+n2​d2​cosr2​)=jλ. Modern structural-colour measurements and modelling distinguish these two contributions instead of treating every nacre appearance as a single grating effect.[ref] That microscopic account uses later structural evidence, not techniques available to an 1865 candidate.

Original question.
Describe some form of Polariscope.

Solution

Words: 202
10 OurBigBook.com
A simple polariscope contains a fixed polarizer and a rotatable optical analyzer, with a space for a specimen between them. A historically suitable implementation uses two Nicol prisms. Each selects one linearly polarized component from the incident light through the crystal's optical action. A lamp and a collimating arrangement illuminate the first prism, and the observer examines the transmitted field through the second.
With no specimen and ideal elements, Malus's law predicts
I=I0​cos2θ,​
(13)
where I0​ is the intensity after the polarizer and θ is the relative transmission-axis angle. Parallel settings are brightest; crossed settings give extinction. Rotating the analyser supplies an immediate check on the first element's polarization.
Insert a transparent birefringent plate between crossed elements with its principal directions oblique to their axes. The two components can acquire different phases, so they need not recombine into the original linear polarization; light can then pass the crossed analyser. With white light, wavelength-dependent retardation gives colours. Rotation of the plate, and a check without it, distinguish this response from simple sample opacity or a poor extinction adjustment. An optically active sugar solution can instead rotate the polarization plane, as in Biot's saccharimeter. The instrument therefore reveals changes of polarization through an analysed intensity, rather than making polarization directly visible to the eye.

Original question.
Two insulated spherical conductors (charged with the same electricity) are suspended near one another. What influence will they exercise upon one another ?

Solution

Words: 325
11 OurBigBook.com
At a large separation, the two like net charges give the familiar leading repulsion kQ1​Q2​/R2, where k=(4πϵ0​)−1 and R is centre separation. Each conductor also changes the other's surface charge distribution through electrostatic induction. The facing and remote surface charge densities need not remain equal, and an external insulating suspension prevents net charge transfer without preventing redistribution within either sphere.
Like net charges do not guarantee repulsion for arbitrary conducting spheres at every separation. Sufficiently unequal charges can give an attractive resultant after polarization. This does not contradict Coulomb's law: the law still applies to the distributed charges, while the point-charge approximation has lost the relevant induced contribution.
A useful controlled example takes a small conducting sphere of radius a and fixed positive charge Q2​ in the nearly uniform field of a much more strongly charged distant sphere. For a≪R, the small sphere has electric polarizability α=4πϵ0​a3 and induced dipole moment p=αE. Its leading interaction energy is
U(R)≃RkQ1​Q2​​−21​α(R2kQ1​​)2.
(14)
Differentiation gives the outward radial force
FR​≃R2kQ1​Q2​​−R52kQ12​a3​.​
(15)
The second term is attractive. With η=Q2​/Q1​>0, this leading model gives FR​<0 when η<2(a/R)3. One can choose a sufficiently small positive η while retaining a/R≪1, so the example does not require a neutral or earthed sphere. It is an asymptotic model, not an exact finite-sphere threshold.
An exact analysis confirms that close-range attraction is possible for like-charged conducting spheres and identifies a special contact-charge ratio for which the force remains repulsive.[ref] This later analysis supplies a precise qualification to the elementary expectation; the source question gives neither a charge ratio nor a separation regime that would justify asserting universal repulsion.

Original question.
How can the intensity of two Voltaic currents be compared ?

Solution

Words: 235
12 OurBigBook.com
Compare the currents using the same calibrated detector and unchanged geometry. With a tangent galvanometer, a circular coil gives Bc​=μ0​NI/(2a) at its centre, perpendicular to the horizontal terrestrial field BH​. A small compass needle aligns with their resultant, so
tanθ=BH​Bc​​,I2​I1​​=tanθ2​tanθ1​​.​
(16)
Use the same coil and location, set its plane in the magnetic meridian, and reverse each current to help identify zero offsets or unwanted local fields. The needle must be sufficiently small relative to the coil for the nearly uniform central field approximation. A finite deflection is not generally proportional to current; replacing the tangent ratio by the angle ratio requires a small-angle approximation or a different calibrated instrument.
Electrolysis offers another comparison. With identical suitable voltameters, the same reaction and constant currents acting for equal times, the deposited mass or collected reaction product is proportional to charge Q=It, according to Faraday's laws of electrolysis. Thus I1​/I2​=m1​/m2​ when the same product is collected with equal current efficiency. For unequal observation times, compare m1​/t1​ with m2​/t2​. Gas collection additionally requires matching pressure, temperature and vapour corrections. Current-dependent side reactions can invalidate a naive product ratio.
An essential circuit qualification is that adding the detector can change the current. Either compare the currents in the circuits as actually measured or account for detector resistance and source loading. Comparing two voltaic sources' currents through one specified circuit is not the same as establishing a ratio of their electromotive forces.

Original question.
What hypotheses have been invented to explain the phænomena of magnetism ?

Solution

Words: 306
13 OurBigBook.com
Several historical models represented the same broad magnetic observations in different ways:
  • Magnetic fluids and poles. Opposite magnetic states were represented by two imponderable fluids, with attraction between unlike poles and repulsion between like poles. A magnetized body was described through separation of those fluids, often within elementary constituents. This gave useful mathematical pole descriptions, but the fluids were hypothetical entities, not liquids isolated in an experiment.
  • André-Marie Ampère's molecular currents. Tiny circulating electric currents were proposed as the source of the elemental magnetic action. A current loop has a magnetic dipole moment, so an array of such loops can reproduce a magnet's external field. This connects magnetism to the observed magnetic action of an electric current.
  • Wilhelm Eduard Weber's pre-existing molecular magnets. The elemental magnets were assumed already present in unmagnetized material, with their directions cancelling in aggregate. An applied field turned them towards common alignment, giving a net magnetization and a limiting magnetic saturation. This differs from supposing that the elementary magnetic separation is first created by the applied field.
James Clerk Maxwell's later treatment explicitly contrasts the induction model associated with Siméon Denis Poisson with Weber's permanently magnetic molecules and discusses the predicted saturation limit.[ref] Its 1873 date is retained; the chapter is used to explain the earlier hypotheses, not attributed to an 1865 publication.
Experiments constrain these models. Cutting a magnet produces smaller magnets with both poles, rather than simply separating free north and south charges. A current loop and an ordinary magnet also have closely related external dipole fields. Such facts explain the utility of molecular dipoles and currents, but do not uniquely identify the microscopic constituents. Modern magnetic domains, electron spin and quantum magnetic ordering refine the account; classical loops and freely turning isolated molecular magnets are incomplete microscopic pictures of a real ferromagnet. An effective pole calculation must likewise be distinguished from claiming an observed isolated magnetic monopole.
