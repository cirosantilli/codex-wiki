Hydatids, in the broader nineteenth-century zoological usage, include several bladder-like larval forms of tapeworms; their adult and larval appearances belong to connected life cycles. The main forms to distinguish are the cysticercus, coenurus and echinococcal hydatid cyst. A fluid-filled cyst is not by itself proof of a living parasite.
A cysticercus is a fluid bladder with a single invaginated protoscolex, the future attachment region of a tapeworm. The historical Cysticercus cellulosae is the larval stage of Taenia solium, not a separate adult species. Eggs from a human intestinal tapeworm carrier can be ingested by pigs, and the released embryos penetrate the gut and reach tissues where cysticerci develop. A human eating infected pork containing cysticerci can acquire an intestinal adult. Humans can also acquire tissue cysticercosis by ingesting eggs. Egg ingestion causing tissue larvae and larval ingestion causing an intestinal adult are different routes. The same distinction prevents the false conclusion that a bladder worm inside a person's brain came there as an adult from the intestine.[ref]
A coenurus bears numerous protoscolices on the wall of a common bladder. The familiar cerebral form in sheep, historically Coenurus cerebralis, belongs chiefly to the life cycle of Taenia multiceps. Canids carry the adult and shed eggs; sheep and other suitable herbivores develop the tissue larva. A canid eating affected tissue can then acquire adults. Taenia serialis produces another coenurus form, commonly associated with rabbits and with a different distribution of tissue lesions. Humans are accidental intermediate hosts rather than normal hosts of intestinal adults of these coenurus-producing species. The multiple heads on one larval bladder distinguish a coenurus from the ordinary one-headed cysticercus.[ref]
In cystic echinococcosis, the larva of the Echinococcus granulosus complex forms a cyst with an outer laminated parasite layer and an inner living germinal layer of a hydatid cyst. The host adds its own surrounding tissue reaction, which is not the same structure as the parasite wall. The germinal layer of a hydatid cyst can produce brood capsules, protoscolices and daughter cysts; free brood material may form hydatid sand. A cyst without visible heads, historically called an acephalocyst, need not represent a fundamentally unrelated animal. It can be an immature or sterile form, so absence of heads in one preparation is not evidence for spontaneous generation.
The mature echinococcal tapeworm is small and inhabits the intestine of a suitable definitive host, classically a dog or another canid. Eggs shed in faeces reach herbivorous intermediate hosts; the released six-hooked embryos cross the intestinal wall and develop in organs, especially liver and lungs. The definitive host completes the cycle by eating infected tissue. Humans acquire the larval infection accidentally by ingesting eggs and are not the usual carriers of the adult. The route is therefore not ordinary consumption of hydatid-bearing meat followed by a human adult echinococcus. Modern classification also distinguishes organisms such as Echinococcus multilocularis, whose branching alveolar larval growth differs from a single typical cyst. Those finer distinctions should not be projected without qualification onto every historical hydatid name.[ref]
The natural-history discovery was that dramatic differences of size, habitat and body form can be stages of one organism. Feeding and transmission experiments, together with the continuity of hooks and attachment structures during development, supplied stronger evidence than resemblance between two isolated cysts. Thomas Spencer Cobbold's 1864 treatment already organizes larval forms alongside their cestode relationships.[ref] The broader word hydatid thus needs a stage and a host attached to it. Its descriptive use for some non-parasitic cysts is not a licence to assign every cyst to one of these life cycles.
