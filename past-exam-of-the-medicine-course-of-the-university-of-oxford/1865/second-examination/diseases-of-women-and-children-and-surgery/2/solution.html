Separate evidence produced by the mother from evidence identifying the fetus. The classical presumptive, probable and positive grouping expresses increasing diagnostic confidence; it is not a numerical probability scale.
Presumptive signs include missed menstruation, nausea or pregnancy-associated vomiting, breast enlargement and tenderness, changes in the areola, urinary frequency and the mother's sensation of fetal movement, traditionally called quickening. Each can have another explanation. Amenorrhoea may result from illness or endocrine disturbance; abdominal enlargement can arise from a pelvic mass, ascites or other causes. A report of movement is not equivalent to movement detected independently by the examiner.
Probable physical signs include a progressively enlarged uterus with the appropriate change in contour, softening of the cervix and uterine tissues, increased vascularity and colour change of the genital mucosa, and intermittent uterine contractions. Ballottement is the rebound of a movable object within fluid when it is displaced: in the right clinical setting it supports a gravid uterus, but the physical phenomenon alone does not identify a living fetus. A vascular sound heard over the uterus follows the maternal circulation and is not the fetal heartbeat.
Positive evidence available to the 1865 examiner was a fetal heartbeat heard with a stethoscope, unmistakable fetal movements appreciated during examination, and identification of fetal parts. Repeated examination as pregnancy advances strengthens their interpretation. The examiner should compare the perceived heartbeat with the maternal pulse rather than accept any abdominal sound as fetal. These findings generally become useful later than the earliest symptoms, so their absence early on does not exclude pregnancy. The mother’s quickening remains a symptom; independently established fetal movement is a different kind of evidence.
Modern additions. Human chorionic gonadotropin testing can detect pregnancy-related tissue before the classical physical signs become conclusive. A positive test does not establish location or continuing viability: it can occur in ectopic pregnancy and must be interpreted in context. Ultrasound can establish location and show an embryo or fetus with cardiac activity. Very early uncertain findings may require repeat assessment rather than a premature conclusion. These technologies are later developments, not signs that an 1865 candidate could have obtained. [ref] [ref]
