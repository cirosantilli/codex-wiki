Classify the bleeding by anatomical source and by its relation to pregnancy, childbirth and menstruation; treatment must address that source. The amount of visible genital haemorrhage does not measure all the loss: a ruptured ectopic pregnancy or concealed placental abruption can cause major internal blood loss. Initial assessment of severe bleeding concerns the circulation, anaemia and the need for resuscitation while the cause is established. Bleeding from the urethra or rectum must first be distinguished from genital bleeding.
Local sources outside the uterine cavity. The vulva and vagina may bleed from lacerations after childbirth or other trauma, an inflamed lesion, a foreign body, a vascular lesion or cancer. Examination localizes the loss; treatment is local compression and repair of a significant laceration, removal of the foreign body, cause-directed treatment of infection, or specialist management of the lesion. A cervical polyp, cervical ectropion, cervicitis or cervical cancer can cause bleeding from the cervix, often noticed between periods or after intercourse. A suitable polyp can be removed and examined; inflammation requires its cause to be established, while a suspicious lesion requires diagnostic assessment rather than indiscriminate cauterization. After menopause, thin, fragile vulvovaginal tissues can also bleed; this does not remove the need to investigate a suspicious lesion. These are different conditions, not one disease called “uterine haemorrhage”. [ref]
Bleeding from the endometrium. Heavy or irregular menstruation may accompany ovulatory dysfunction, uterine fibroids, endometrial polyps, adenomyosis, a coagulopathy, medication effects or endometrial cancer. Modern assessment includes the menstrual and drug history, a full blood count, and selected ultrasound, hysteroscopy or tissue sampling according to the suspected cause. Where appropriate, menstrual bleeding can be reduced with tranexamic acid or hormonal treatment; an intracavitary polyp or uterine fibroid may instead need targeted removal. Persistent postmenopausal bleeding must not simply be treated as an ordinary period. [ref]
Early pregnancy. A miscarriage may bleed while pregnancy tissue separates or remains in the uterus. In a stable, confirmed case, expectant, medical or surgical management may be appropriate; heavy continuing loss or infection changes the urgency. Ectopic pregnancy requires a separate plan: selected stable cases may be monitored or treated medically, but rupture with haemorrhagic collapse requires urgent operative control, not treatment of the vagina as though it were the principal source. A hydatidiform mole is another pregnancy-related source of abnormal bleeding; current management includes removal by suction evacuation and subsequent human chorionic gonadotropin surveillance for persistent disease. [ref] [ref] [ref]
Later pregnancy and labour. Placenta praevia commonly produces relatively painless bleeding, whereas placental abruption more often causes pain and uterine tenderness; these are useful patterns, not infallible rules. Management depends on the mother's circulation, the fetus, the placental lesion and the stage of pregnancy, with urgent delivery when required. A blind digital cervical examination is inappropriate before a possible placenta praevia has been excluded. Ruptured vasa praevia causes fetal rather than maternal bleeding and demands a different interpretation. A small blood-stained mucous “show” during cervical change is not equivalent to these pathological losses. [ref] [ref]
After delivery. The principal categories are failure of uterine contraction, retained placental tissue, trauma and disordered coagulation. For uterine atony, the aim is to restore contraction with uterine massage and an appropriate uterotonic. Retained tissue, a genital laceration or uterine inversion requires correction of that particular cause; a coagulopathy requires haemostatic support. With placenta accreta spectrum, attempted forcible separation can provoke severe haemorrhage. Current care combines resuscitation with cause-directed measures, and can require tranexamic acid, balloon tamponade, embolisation or surgery when initial measures fail. Later postpartum bleeding also raises retained tissue and endometritis. [ref]
The 1865 distinction. An answer of the period could distinguish menstrual loss, local lesions, pregnancy-related haemorrhage and post-delivery failure of contraction through the history and physical examination. Compression, local plugging, removal of an accessible lesion and obstetric intervention were among the available approaches; their use was debated. The 1864 discussion of placenta praevia documents such disagreement. Modern imaging, blood banking, the present drug regimens and current operative pathways must not be attributed to that practice. Nor should historical astringents or depletion be substituted for identifying the source. [ref]
