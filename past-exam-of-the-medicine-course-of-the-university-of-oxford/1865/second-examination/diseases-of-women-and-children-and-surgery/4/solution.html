Muguet denotes the adherent white fungal coating of oral thrush, now oral candidiasis; it is not simply any white tongue or any mouth ulcer. Patches occur on the tongue and oral mucosa and may merge into broader plaques. The underlying surface can be red, sore and liable to bleed when a plaque is disturbed. An infant may feed poorly because the mouth hurts. Milk residue lacks the same persistent inflamed adherent lesion, while an aphthous ulcer is a focal ulcer rather than a confluent fungal plaque. [ref]
Cause and nature. The modern explanation is opportunistic overgrowth of Candida, commonly Candida albicans, on a susceptible mucosal surface. An immature or impaired immune system, illness and altered local conditions favour it. Antibiotics, corticosteroids, diabetes and other causes of reduced host defence are relevant modern risk factors. These were not all understood in 1865, and exposure to the organism alone does not explain every case. Local oral candidiasis also does not, by itself, establish invasive fungal disease. [ref]
Evidence available in the nineteenth century. Fungal forms could already be observed with a microscope. A contemporary 1849 review reports Charles West's suggestion that the organisms could be transferred by shared cups and spoons, while the reviewer preferred an explanation involving decomposition and acidity. The review is evidence of the disagreement, not authority for spontaneous generation of a fungus. It also illustrates why historical “aphthae”, ulceration and Muguet should not all be treated as identical modern diagnoses. [ref]
Diagnosis and treatment. Clinical inspection is often sufficient; when uncertain or atypical, material from the lesion can be examined microscopically for fungal forms, with further laboratory testing when needed. Repeated or unusually severe disease calls for attention to underlying illness and host defence, rather than simply repeating local cleansing. [ref]
The historically defensible measures include maintaining adequate feeding, gentle mouth care and clean feeding utensils. Harsh scraping and treating every white patch as the same disease are inappropriate. Present-day treatment adds an age-appropriate antifungal drug, such as a suitable topical nystatin preparation; more extensive or resistant disease may require systemic treatment such as fluconazole. Drug choice and formulation depend on the patient's age and clinical state. Those agents are later additions and must not be placed in an 1865 prescription. Hygiene and correction of predisposing factors accompany, rather than replace, effective antifungal treatment. [ref]
