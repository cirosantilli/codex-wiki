A defensible period design answer for hospital bed space is roughly 1,600 cubic feet, or 45 cubic metres, per bed in a properly ventilated ordinary ward. This is a volume allowance, not a ventilation rate or a universal legal minimum. In the 1863 third edition of Notes on Hospitals by Florence Nightingale, Florence Nightingale gives a twenty-patient ward measuring 80 by 25 by 16 feet, or alternatively 80 by 26 by 15 feet. These examples give 1,600 or 1,560 cubic feet per bed, respectively.[ref]
For the first arrangement,
NV​=2080×25×16​=1600 ft3≃45.3 m3 per bed.​
(1)
This is a cited design example, not a measurement of an unseen ward. Floor area, separation between beds, access for nursing, air distribution and the condition of the patients also matter. Simply adding height cannot compensate for crowding patients together or leaving stagnant zones around the beds. A modern hospital would apply its current design and infection-control requirements rather than treat the 1863 figure as sufficient for every clinical purpose.
To determine hospital ward ventilation, first measure the ward's internal volume V. Measure the area A of each effective inlet or outlet and the mean normal velocity uˉ across it, using an anemometer and several positions rather than one favourable point. The volume flow is Q=Auˉ, or the sum of the flows through independent openings of the same side of the balance. Do not add both the incoming and outgoing flow and call their sum new air. Reverse flow, leakage, changes of wind and temperature, and recirculation must be considered. Smoke may reveal direction and stagnant regions, but its visible movement alone is not a quantitative flow measurement. Modern institutional instrument guidance also explains why velocity must be sampled across a section; the simple area–velocity relation itself does not depend on a modern electronic instrument.[ref]
With Q expressed per hour, the mean air changes per hour are
a=VQ​,qbed​=NQ​.​
(2)
The quantities have different meanings: V/N is space per bed, Q/N is fresh-air supply per bed per hour, and a is the nominal exchange rate. A system may move much recirculated air while supplying relatively little new outdoor air.
A second method is to follow the fall of a tracer gas after its source stops. Under a well-mixed, constant-flow model with constant outdoor concentration Co​ and no continuing internal generation,
dtd(C−Co​)​=−VQ​(C−Co​),a=t2​−t1​ln[(C1​−Co​)/(C2​−Co​)]​.​
(3)
Here time is in hours if a is to be reported per hour. The formula follows by integrating the mass balance. Compare measurements at several positions and state the conditions. Poor mixing, continuing generation by occupants and changing wind invalidate a simple one-exponential interpretation. Current institutional guidance describes this decay method; the modern monitoring protocol is a later comparison, while the elementary balance explains what “rate of change” measures.[ref]
The ordinary ward impurities to examine are excess carbon dioxide and water vapour from occupants, volatile material including Ammonia, organic and inorganic dust, and potentially infectious aerosols. Carbon monoxide or irritant combustion gases may enter where heating or lighting is defective; drain-associated gases are a contingent additional hazard, not something necessarily present in every ward. Record occupancy, combustion sources, windows, sampling position, temperature and time, and take an outdoor comparison sample.
For carbon dioxide, collect a known volume of air in a calibrated vessel and expose it to a measured amount of standardized barium hydroxide. Absorption produces barium carbonate. Compare the remaining alkali with a reagent blank, excluding carbonate precipitate from an acid titration that would dissolve it. The consumed alkali gives the absorbed carbon dioxide; express that amount per original volume of air. A report from 1864 describes the method in 7–10 litre globes with a gravimetric comparison, establishing a procedure available before the examination.[ref]
Estimate humidity with wet- and dry-bulb thermometers, or gravimetrically by collecting moisture in a weighed drying absorber from a measured volume of air. For volatile bases, pass a measured sample through dilute acid and determine the uptake, interpreting possible Ammonia alongside interfering bases. Mere change of litmus is a screen, not a selective low-concentration quantitative assay. A modern example uses acid-treated collection medium and ion chromatography, with calibrated sampling and blanks; that later analytical specification must not be credited to 1865.[ref]
Collect suspended material on a suitable filter or surface and examine it by microscopy; a controlled gravimetric collection gives particle mass per volume. Such measurements do not by themselves identify pathogens or count every inhalable organism. Period assessments also used potassium permanganate decolourization to investigate oxidizable contamination. The 1865 chemical press already warned that other reducing substances could decolourize it, so a supposed “organic matter” result was nonspecific.[ref]
Finally, report chemical cleanliness, particle loading and ventilation performance separately. They are related aspects of ward conditions, but one cannot derive freedom from infection from a low carbon dioxide result or a weak smell. Modern infection-control guidance similarly emphasizes that microbial air samples depend on collection method, location, timing and an interpretable sampling plan.[ref]
