“Carbonic acid” in this question means gaseous carbon dioxide, not liquid acid poisoning. Dangerous accumulation causes hypercapnia, impaired consciousness and potentially death; prevention requires controlling its production and providing effective fresh-air replacement. At high concentrations, displacement of oxygen can contribute, but lack of oxygen is not the only mechanism.
Early symptoms can include headache, dizziness, discomfort, restlessness, sweating, rapid or laboured breathing and an increased pulse. Greater exposure can cause confusion, loss of coordination, profound drowsiness, collapse, convulsions, coma and death. In a heavily contaminated space, collapse may occur before a person can recognize the danger or escape. Carbon dioxide is colourless and odourless, so absence of an offensive smell provides no reassurance.[ref]
The physiological point matters. Increased inspired carbon dioxide impairs elimination of the body's own carbon dioxide and alters the acid–base state of the blood. A hazardous atmosphere can therefore remain dangerous even when enough oxygen is present to sustain a flame. The modern institutional discussion of carbon dioxide fire suppression distinguishes the gas's direct physiological effects from mere dilution of oxygen.[ref]
The characteristic settings are poorly ventilated spaces in which carbon dioxide is continuously generated or accumulates: brewery or wine-fermentation vessels, cellars, pits, wells, vaults and places containing decomposing organic material. Fermentation and cellular respiration generate the gas; combustion is another source. In a badly ventilated occupied room, human breathing and unflued fires or lamps add to the burden. A dangerous combustion atmosphere may also contain carbon monoxide, which is a separate poison and requires a separate assessment. Carbon dioxide is denser than air, so low or poorly swept spaces deserve attention; currents and mixing mean that one cannot assume a sharp, motionless layer with safe air above it.[ref]
For prevention, remove or enclose the source where practicable and maintain building ventilation that actually sweeps the occupied and low-lying spaces. Exhaust contaminated air to a safe place and bring replacement air from a clean source. Opening a small window elsewhere in a building does not establish an adequate supply at the worker's position. Fermentation vessels, pits and similar spaces should be arranged for inspection and cleaning without entry where possible.
The nineteenth-century emphasis on fresh air and caution before entering such spaces remains sound. Current practice adds atmosphere testing for carbon dioxide, oxygen deficiency and other relevant gases, together with controlled entry and rescue arrangements. A burning candle is an inadequate safety test: flame behaviour neither measures carbon dioxide nor rules out a different toxic gas. A rescuer who enters an unknown atmosphere without appropriate protection can become a second casualty. Trained rescue with suitable supplied breathing air and retrieval arrangements must be distinguished from simply holding one's breath and climbing in.[ref]
For ordinary rooms and wards, prevention also means limiting overcrowding, maintaining reliable air inlets and outlets, and removing products of combustion through proper flues. These principles concern continuing air exchange: a large room merely delays contamination if its air is never renewed. They explain why the following ward question asks separately about cubic space and the rate of change of air.
