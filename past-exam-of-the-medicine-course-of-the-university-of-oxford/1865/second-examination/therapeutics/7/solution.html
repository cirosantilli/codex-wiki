External water treatment can clean, transfer heat, provide moisture and apply mechanical pressure; the useful method depends on the object, not on a general claim that water expels disease. Historical classifications distinguished general and local bathing, cold and warm applications, affusion, showers, compresses, packing and vapour baths.
Cold applications included sponging, pouring water over the body, immersion, a local wet compress and a cold shower or douche. A renewed wet cloth removes heat by contact and evaporation; a wrapping allowed to warm and then covered is a different intervention. In 1865 these methods were used for febrile heat, local pain or swelling, and sometimes to obtain a supposed stimulating reaction in chronic debility. A cold douche also adds mechanical impact and an abrupt circulatory stimulus. Historical wet-sheet packing enclosed the body in a wet sheet with outer coverings and was either renewed to sustain cooling or left to warm. The contemporary description distinguishes these uses; the claims about stimulation and cure are historical claims.[ref]
Tepid and warm immersion, a warm local bath or sitz bath, and warm moist fomentations were used to soothe painful or spasmodic conditions, support cleansing and soften crusts or secretions. Local applications avoid heating the whole body. The apparatus might be a bath, basin, irrigating vessel or repeatedly renewed cloth; a warm moist dressing must not become a source of sustained scalding. Historically, hot immersion and vapour baths were also used to induce sweating in rheumatic complaints and supposed retention of harmful material. Heat-induced sweating is real, but removal of a disease-specific poison by that route is a separate claim requiring evidence. Showering and douching similarly cannot be treated as dose-equivalent to quiet immersion.[ref]
Current examples show why the distinction matters. Heatstroke requires rapid cooling and emergency care: cold-water immersion or other active cooling is used to reduce dangerous body temperature, not to provoke a later “reaction”. In contrast, routine tepid sponging is not recommended for fever in children under five. Febrile thermoregulation can provoke shivering and distress with external cooling, so the treatment of ordinary fever should not be inferred from the management of hyperthermia.[ref][ref]
For a thermal burn, current first aid uses cool running water for 20 minutes, with the rest of the person kept warm; ice or an ice-water application is not the appropriate substitute. Cleansing or irrigation has a further purpose independent of temperature: physically removing contamination, with the fluid and technique chosen for the tissue and injury. A steam application to the face is not a harmless version of warm bathing: spilled hot water and steam cause scalds. The historical use of inhalation therefore does not validate leaning over boiling water as a general respiratory treatment. These examples specify an actual therapeutic objective without endorsing the broad claims of nineteenth-century hydropathy.[ref][ref]
