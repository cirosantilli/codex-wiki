At an equilibrium point, x(1−x−y)=0 and 2−2x−y=0. The first equation gives x=0 or y=1−x. Substituting in the second gives, respectively,
(x,y)=(0,2)or(1,0).​
(1)
These are all the equilibrium points. The Jacobian matrix of the vector field is
J(x,y)=(1−2x−y−2​−x−1​).
(2)
At (0,2),
J(0,2)=(−1−2​0−1​).
(3)
The eigenvalue −1 has algebraic multiplicity two and eigenspace span{(0,1)T}. For the linearized coordinates u=x, v=y−2,
u(t)=Ae−t,v(t)=(B−2At)e−t.
(4)
Both components tend to zero. The repeated eigenvalue with one independent eigenvector makes this a stable improper degenerate node, a sink equilibrium. The term te−t is the signature of the nontrivial Jordan block. Since it is a hyperbolic equilibrium point, the linearization stability theorem gives local asymptotic stability of the nonlinear system. Approaching trajectories are tangent to the vertical eigendirection.
At (1,0),
J(1,0)=(−1−2​−1−1​),λ+​=−1+2​,v+​=(1−2​​),λ−​=−1−2​,v−​=(12​​).
(5)
One eigenvalue is positive and one negative, so this point is a saddle equilibrium. Its stable manifold is tangent to y=2​(x−1), and its unstable manifold is tangent to y=−2​(x−1).
For the phase portrait, the nullclines are
x˙=0:x=0 or y=1−x,y˙​=0:y=2−2x.
(6)
Above the second line the vertical flow is downward, and below it upward. For x>0, horizontal flow is to the right below y=1−x and to the left above it; for x<0 the directions reverse. The line x=0 is invariant, with y˙​=2−y, so it flows toward (0,2). Together with the saddle's two invariant directions, these signs determine the sketch.

The Bendixson-Dulac theorem says that a continuously differentiable planar vector field has no nonconstant periodic orbit in a simply connected region if a continuously differentiable Dulac function makes its rescaled divergence have one sign and not vanish identically on any open subset of that region. Isolated zeros of the divergence are allowed in this form.
Take the Dulac function to be one. For the exact vector field (P,Q),
∂x∂P​+∂y∂Q​=3x2+7y2+12x2y2.
(7)
This is positive except at the origin. The plane is simply connected, so there are no nonconstant periodic solutions. Equivalently, a hypothetical closed trajectory would have zero outward flux of its tangent vector field, while Green's theorem would make that flux the strictly positive area integral of the divergence.
The origin is the only equilibrium point: Q=0 forces y=0, and then P=x3 forces x=0. Its Jacobian matrix is zero, so the linearized equations are x˙=y˙​=0. They give no stability classification.
Keeping cubic terms gives the homogeneous approximation
x˙=x(x2+y2),y˙​=2y3.
(8)
In a sector where xy=0, use y as the independent variable and put w=x−2. Dividing the two equations gives
dydx​=2y3x(x2+y2)​,dydw​+yw​=−y31​.
(9)
The integrating factor y gives (yw)′=−y−2, hence yw=1/y+C0​. Rearranging and renaming the arbitrary constant yields the implicit trajectories
y1​−x2y​=C,equivalentlyx2−y2=Cx2y.​
(10)
This is the first integral of the cubic approximation, not an exact first integral of the higher-degree original system. The invariant axes, excluded by the divisions, must be added: on y=0, x˙=x3; on x=0, y˙​=2y3. The diagonal trajectories y=±x correspond to C=0. For fixed finite C, non-axis trajectories approaching the origin satisfy y2/x2=1−Cy→1, so their limiting tangent lines are the diagonals. This is the cubic planar flow with diagonal approach directions.
Every nonzero trajectory moves outward locally. For V=x2+y2, the exact system gives
V˙=2x4+2x2y2+4y4+2x4y2+6x2y4≥V2>0.
(11)
This implies departure from any fixed small circle for every nonzero starting point inside it: while a trajectory stayed inside forever, dtd​(1/V)≤−1 would eventually force 1/V negative. Thus the origin is unstable and repelling for both the cubic approximation and the full system. The argument establishes nonlinear instability despite the zero linearization.

Work first on 0<x<1. Divide by x−1 to put the equation in leading-coefficient-one form. Its first-derivative coefficient is −x/(x−1), so the integrating factor for the self-adjoint differential operator is
p(x)=exp(∫x−1−x​dx)=1−xe−x​.
(12)
The resulting coefficients are
p(x)=1−xe−x​,q(x)=−(1−x)2e−x​,F(x)=−(1−x)2e−x​.​
(13)
Indeed p′(x)=xe−x/(1−x)2, and multiplying the original equation by −e−x/(1−x)2 gives (py′)′+qy=F. These transformed coefficients are singular at the right endpoint; the boundary value there is understood as the endpoint limit.
Substituting y=x and y=ex in the homogeneous original equation gives zero in both cases. Their Wronskian is
W(x,ex)=xex−ex=(x−1)ex,
(14)
which is nonzero for 0<x<1. Thus they are linearly independent on the solution interval.
To construct a Dirichlet Green function, choose homogeneous solutions adapted to the two endpoints:
u(x)=x,v(x)=ex−ex,u(0)=0,v(1)=0.
(15)
Their Wronskian is again (x−1)ex, and pW=−1. Therefore the Green function for L=(pd/dx)′+q with zero endpoint values is
G(x,s)=−u(min(x,s))v(max(x,s))={−x(es−es),−s(ex−ex),​x≤s,s≤x.​​
(16)
For fixed s∈(0,1) it is continuous in x, vanishes at both endpoints, and satisfies the unit-flux jump condition
p(s)[Gx​(s+,s)−Gx​(s−,s)]=1.
(17)
Consequently Lx​G(x,s)=δ(x−s) in the sense of distributions. This is a singular-endpoint Green function with a double-zero right solution.
The endpoint data are nonhomogeneous, so use the homogeneous lifting 2x and write
y(x)=2x+∫01​G(x,s)F(s)ds.
(18)
Although F(s) is singular at one, the integral exists for each interior x: the right solution satisfies v(s)∼e(1−s)2/2, cancelling the (1−s)−2 forcing when s>x.
Splitting the integral at s=x gives
y(x)=2x+v(x)I1​(x)+xI2​(x),
(19)
where elementary antiderivatives yield
I1​(x)=∫0x​(1−s)2se−s​ds=1−xe−x​−1,
(20)
I2​(x)=∫x1​(1−s)2(es−es)e−s​ds=1−xe1−x−1​−1.
(21)
For the second equality, an antiderivative is (1−e1−s)/(1−s), whose endpoint limit at one is −1. Simplifying the Green representation gives
y(x)=1+(1+e)x−ex.​
(22)
It has the prescribed endpoint values. Direct substitution gives (x−1)y′′−xy′+y=1, and at the degenerate endpoint it also obeys −y′(1)+y(1)=1. The only homogeneous solution satisfying both zero endpoint conditions is zero, so this solution is unique in the corresponding classical class.

Let X=(x,y) be the integration variable and P=(ξ,η) the interior source point. Green's second identity, obtained from Green's theorem by applying the divergence theorem to u∇G−G∇u, is
∫D​(uΔG−GΔu)dA=∫∂D​(u∂n∂G​−G∂n∂u​)ds.
(23)
The outward normal is used throughout. To obtain the prescribed representation with its positive volume term, choose
ΔX​G(X,P)=δ(X−P),G=0 on ∂D1​,∂n​G=0 on ∂D2​.​
(24)
Indeed, substituting Δu=f makes the left side u(P)−∫D​Gf. On the Dirichlet portion, u=g and G=0 leave only g∂n​G. On the Neumann portion, both normal derivatives vanish. Rearranging gives exactly the desired boundary-plus-volume representation. The distributional identity can equivalently be justified by excluding a small circle around P and using the logarithmic singularity there.
For the quarter-plane, the fundamental solution of the Laplace equation with this sign convention is Φ(X,P)=(2π)−1log∣X−P∣, since ΔΦ=δP​. The method of images uses an equal-sign reflection across the Neumann boundary x=0 and an opposite-sign reflection across the Dirichlet boundary y=0. Hence
G(X,P)=2π1​[log∣X−(ξ,η)∣+log∣X−(−ξ,η)∣−log∣X−(ξ,−η)∣−log∣X−(−ξ,−η)∣].
(25)
Equivalently,
G(x,y,ξ,η)=4π1​log((x−ξ)2+(y+η)2)((x+ξ)2+(y+η)2)((x−ξ)2+(y−η)2)((x+ξ)2+(y−η)2)​.​
(26)
Only (ξ,η) lies inside the domain, so the interior Laplacian has exactly the required unit point source. At y=0 numerator and denominator agree, giving G=0. The formula is even in x, giving ∂x​G=0 at x=0, and therefore zero outward normal derivative there.
On the Dirichlet boundary y=0, the outward normal is −∂y​, and differentiation gives the useful check
∂n∂G​(x,0,ξ,η)=πη​[(x−ξ)2+η21​+(x+ξ)2+η21​]>0.
(27)
This is the mixed Dirichlet-Neumann Green function in a quadrant. The image solution tends to zero at spatial infinity; on the unbounded domain the representation uses data and solutions for which the boundary term at infinity in Green's second identity vanishes.

Write the equation as ϵx3−(x−1)2=0. For ϵ→0+, two roots of a polynomial approach the double root one. Their splitting follows a fractional-power dominant balance, rather than an ordinary expansion in integer powers.
Put δ=ϵ​ and x=1+aδ+bδ2+⋯. Substitution gives
0=(1−a2)δ2+(3a−2ab)δ3+O(δ4).
(28)
Thus a=±1 and b=3/2. The two bounded roots have the Puiseux series expansions
x±​=1±ϵ​+23​ϵ+O(ϵ3/2).​
(29)
The first two nonzero terms are 1±ϵ​; the displayed correction also resolves their common order-ϵ shift.
The third root escapes to infinity because the leading polynomial degree drops when ϵ=0. Balance ϵx3 against x2, giving x=O(ϵ−1). Set X=ϵx; the equation becomes
X3−X2+2ϵX−ϵ2=0.
(30)
For X=1+Aϵ+Bϵ2+⋯, the first two coefficient equations are A+2=0 and B+2A2+2A−1=0. Hence
x∞​=ϵ−1−2−3ϵ+O(ϵ2).​
(31)
Its first two nonzero terms are ϵ−1−2. These three distinct branches account for all roots of the cubic for sufficiently small positive ϵ. The Vieta formulas provide a consistency check: their sum is ϵ−1, with the constant and order-ϵ terms cancelling.
If ϵ approaches zero through negative values, the bounded pair is complex: x±​=1±i∣ϵ∣​+23​ϵ+O(∣ϵ∣3/2). The large-root expansion is unchanged as a real asymptotic formula. The positive-parameter regime is used for the boundary-layer problem below.

Y′′=Y−(1−ϵ​X)2+ϵ(2+Y2).
(38)
Since the rescaled forcing contains ϵ​, an inner expansion naturally uses powers of ϵ​. Its leading term satisfies
Y0′′​=Y0​−1,Y0​(0)=0.

The asymptotic matching condition with the leading outer solution is
Y0​(X)⟶y0​(1)=1(X→∞).
