Extend the given functions continuously to zero by w1​(0)=w2​(0)=0. This is forced by their stated differentiability. Their derivatives at zero are both zero, since the corresponding difference quotients tend to zero.
For x<0, w2​=w2′​=0, and for x>0, w1​=w1′​=0. At zero all four quantities vanish. Thus the Wronskian is
W(w1​,w2​)(x)=w1​w2′​−w1′​w2​=0for all x.​
(1)
Nevertheless, if the constant linear combination Aw1​+Bw2​ vanishes identically, evaluation at x=−1 gives −A=0, and evaluation at x=1 gives B=0. Therefore the two functions are linearly independent.
This illustrates why a vanishing Wronskian does not imply linear dependence for arbitrary differentiable functions. Here both functions are continuously differentiable, but neither has a second derivative at zero. The common regular differential equation hypothesis in the next part is absent.

Use a Green function satisfying Lx​G(x,ξ)=δ(x−ξ) with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions G(0,ξ)=G(π,ξ)=0, for 0<ξ<π. Since Gxx​=0 away from x=1 and x=ξ, it is continuous and piecewise affine, with possible derivative jumps at those two locations.
For ξ=1, the transmission conditions for a delta coefficient and the unit source condition are
[G]1​[G]ξ​​=0,=0,​[Gx​]1​[Gx​]ξ​​=−G(1,ξ),=1.​
(10)
If ξ=1, there is a single junction, and the correct combined condition is
[G]1​=0,[Gx​]1​+G(1,1)=1.​
(11)
For example, a suitable piecewise form when 0<ξ<1 is
G(x,ξ)=⎩⎨⎧​Ax,Bx+C,D(π−x),​0≤x≤ξ,ξ≤x≤1,1≤x≤π,​
(12)
where the coefficients depend on ξ. The two continuity conditions, B−A=1 and −D−B=−G(1,ξ) determine them. When 1<ξ<π, use the same affine form on the intervals cut at 1,ξ, with B−A=−G(1,ξ) and −D−B=1 instead. This gives all requested boundary and jump conditions without needing the coefficients.
An explicit compact kernel also makes the construction transparent. The left and right homogeneous solutions at α=1 are
u(x)={x,1,​x≤1,x≥1,​v(x)={1+(π−2)x,π−x,​x≤1,x≥1.​
(13)
They obey the interaction jump and have Wronskian uv′−u′v=−1 on either side. Therefore
G(x,ξ)=−u(min(x,ξ))v(max(x,ξ)).​
(14)
Differentiating its two branches at the source gives a unit derivative jump whenever ξ=1. At ξ=1, G(1,1)=−(π−1) and [Gx​]1​=π, so the combined condition is also satisfied. This is the Dirichlet Green function for a point interaction specialized to the present parameters.
The Green kernel itself has zero endpoint values. The prescribed nonzero endpoint values enter through the homogeneous lift:
y(x)=c1​v(x)+c2​u(x)+∫0π​G(x,ξ)f(ξ)dξ.​
(15)
Indeed, v(0)=u(π)=1 and u(0)=v(π)=0. The kernel accounts for the forcing; the lift accounts for the boundary data. Uniqueness follows from part (i), since 1=π/(π−1).

The Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator gives
∫αβ​(uMv−Muv)dx=[a(uv′−u′v)]αβ​.
(18)
In the intended class of functions whose values and first derivatives stay finite at the endpoints, the endpoint factor a makes this entire boundary term vanish. Thus no separately prescribed endpoint values are needed to eliminate the boundary form. The same integration by parts argument then proves that the eigenvalues are real.
The admissible endpoint behaviour is part of this conclusion: it is the vanishing endpoint coefficient and the Sturm-Liouville boundary form, together with endpoint regularity, that replaces explicit boundary conditions. If singular functions are allowed, a vanishing coefficient alone need not suffice: a(x)=x(1−x) and v(x)=logx have a(x)v′(x)→1 as x↓0. A self-adjoint differential operator therefore still needs a specified domain of admissible functions.

The useful change of independent variable in a second-order ODE is t=logx, followed by y(x)=xv(t). The chain rule gives
y′=v+vt​,y′′=x−1(vt​+vtt​),Ly=xvtt​.
(19)
Thus the eigenvalue equation becomes vtt​=λv on 0<t<1, and the endpoint values become v(0)=v(1)=0. At the specified eigenvalues,
v(t)=Acos(kπt)+Bsin(kπt).
(20)
The first endpoint forces A=0; the second holds for every positive integer k. Choosing a convenient normalization yields
yk​(x)=xsin(kπlogx),k=1,2,….​
(21)
Each nonzero constant multiple is the same eigenfunction up to normalization. This logarithmic conjugacy of a repeated-root Euler operator turns the Cauchy-Euler equation into the familiar Fourier sine series problem on a unit interval.
