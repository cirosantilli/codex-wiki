On an interval with x>0, use the power-law ansatz y=xr. Substitution gives r(r−1)+2r+1=r2+r+1=0, whose roots are r=−1/2±i3​/2. Taking real and imaginary parts of xr gives
y(x)=x−1/2[C1​cos(23​​logx)+C2​sin(23​​logx)].​
(1)
These two real solutions are linearly independent: their Wronskian is (3​/2)x−2=0. They therefore span the solution space of the second-order linear ordinary differential equation on that interval.
For x<0, replace x−1/2 by ∣x∣−1/2 and logx by log∣x∣, with independent real constants on the negative interval. This follows equally by the change of variable t=log∣x∣, which turns the Cauchy-Euler equation into d2y/dt2+dy/dt+y=0. The coefficient of y′′ vanishes at zero, so the general formula is stated separately on either side. Every nonzero mode has an unbounded oscillatory envelope as x→0; a continuous extension through zero requires the solution to vanish identically on both sides.

Use the usual inner product ⟨u,v⟩=∫01​uvdx. Moving four derivatives by integration by parts leaves L∗=D4+1 and gives the fourth-order boundary identity
⟨u,Lv⟩−⟨Lu,v⟩=[uv′′′−u′v′′+u′′v′−u′′′v]01​.
(2)
Thus the differential expression is formally self-adjoint. If both functions satisfy the clamped boundary conditions, every displayed endpoint product vanishes, proving symmetry on the specified domain.
To check full self-adjointness, also identify the adjoint domain. Allow v to run through the clamped domain. At either endpoint, v′′ and v′′′ can be chosen independently, for instance using a polynomial near that endpoint multiplied by a smooth cutoff function. The remaining boundary form is [uv′′′−u′v′′]01​. It vanishes for every such v precisely when u=u′=0 at both endpoints. These are exactly the original boundary conditions. On the regular finite interval, the adjoint domain consequently equals {u∈H4(0,1):u(0)=u′(0)=u(1)=u′(1)=0}, rather than a larger domain. Here H4 is the Sobolev space of functions whose weak derivatives through order four are square-integrable.
The operator together with these boundary conditions is fully self-adjoint. As a further consistency check, integration by parts gives ⟨y,Ly⟩=∫01​(∣y′′∣2+∣y∣2)dx>0 for every nonzero admissible y. The reusable calculation is the Lagrange identity for a fourth-order differential operator.

Start with a fundamental solution of the second derivative operator:
g​(x,ξ)=(x−ξ)H(x−ξ),∂x​g​=H(x−ξ),∂x2​g​=δ(x−ξ).
(3)
Here H is the Heaviside step function and the last equality holds in the sense of distributions. The factor x−ξ removes a jump in the function itself, while its first derivative has a unit jump. Adding any homogeneous solution preserves the Dirac delta source.
Choose y1​(x)=x−2 and y2​(x)=x−3. They are independent homogeneous solutions, with B1​y1​=0, B2​y2​=0, B1​y2​=−1, and B2​y1​=1. Since 1<ξ<2, the fundamental solution has B1​g​=1 and B2​g​=2−ξ. Imposing the two boundary conditions on the proposed combination therefore gives −A2​+1=0 and A1​+2−ξ=0. Consequently
A1​(ξ)=ξ−2,A2​(ξ)=1,g(x,ξ)=(ξ−2)(x−2)+(x−3)+(x−ξ)H(x−ξ).​
(4)
Equivalently,
g(x,ξ)={(ξ−1)x+1−2ξ,ξx+1−3ξ,​1≤x≤ξ,ξ≤x≤2.​
(5)
At x=ξ both expressions equal ξ2−3ξ+1 and the right slope minus the left slope is ξ−(ξ−1)=1. At the endpoints, g(1,ξ)=−ξ and gx​(2,ξ)=ξ, while gx​(1,ξ)=ξ−1 and g(2,ξ)=1−ξ. These explicitly verify both coupled boundary conditions. The homogeneous equation has only affine solutions; its two boundary conditions give 2a+b=3a+b=0, hence a=b=0, proving uniqueness of the Green function.
The reciprocity defect is g(x,ξ)−g(ξ,x)=2(x−ξ). It records how the coupled boundary conditions affect self-adjointness, even though the differential expression D2 is formally self-adjoint. The construction is an instance of the boundary-matrix construction of an ordinary differential Green function.

r(r−1)+(−21​)r+0=0,r1​=0,r2​=23​.​
(6)
At x=1, both normalized coefficients are analytic functions, so x=1 is an ordinary point. These are the standard normalized-coefficient and indicial tests.[ref]
For infinity, set t=1/x and Y(t)=y(1/t). The chain rule gives y′=−t2Y′ and y′′=t4Y′′+2t3Y′, so the transformed normalized equation is
Y′′+(2t5​−2t31​)Y′+2t4λ​Y=0.

(9)

First classify nonzero polynomial solutions of the differential equation itself. If y has degree d and leading coefficient ad​=0, the coefficient of xd+1 is (d+λ)ad​. Hence λ=−d is necessary.
An analytic solution at zero belongs to the exponent-zero Frobenius branch. Its odd-power coefficients vanish, because a1​=0 and the recurrence relation preserves parity. For the even-power coefficients,
a2j+2​=−(2j+2)(4j+1)2j+λ​a2j​.
(10)
All denominators are nonzero for j≥0. With λ=−2m, the coefficients through a2m​ are nonzero when a0​=0, the next coefficient vanishes, and every subsequent coefficient vanishes. This proves sufficiency as well as the restriction to even polynomial degree:
λ=−2m,m=0,1,2,…,degy=2m=−λ.​
(11)
For example, setting a0​=1 gives 1 when λ=0, 1+x2 when λ=−2, and 1+2x2+x4/5 when λ=−4. A polynomial cannot instead begin with a positive integer power: its lowest power must have an indicial exponent, and the only integer indicial exponent is zero. This also proves that no odd-degree family was missed. The finite-series mechanism is a terminating Frobenius series.
If the additional endpoint conditions from part (b) are imposed on this part too, there is no nonzero polynomial solution: every nonzero polynomial just classified has y(0)=a0​=0. The displayed family and degree answer therefore concern the differential equation without those extra endpoint conditions.

For a positive eigenvalue write μ=λ​>0. The general solution of the limiting constant-coefficient differential equation is y=Acos(μx)+Bsin(μx). The endpoint at zero gives A=0. A nonzero eigenfunction then requires B=0, and the endpoint at π gives sin(μπ)=0. Consequently
λk​=k2,yk​(x)=Ck​sin(kx),k=1,2,…,Ck​=0.​
(16)
For real eigenfunctions the constants may be any nonzero real amplitudes. Requiring unit L2 norm would set ∣Ck​∣=2/π​, but that normalization is not required by the question. The lowest eigenvalue is λ1​=1 and its eigenspace is spanned by sinx. For completeness, zero yields only an affine solution annihilated by both endpoints, and negative eigenvalues yield hyperbolic functions whose two zero endpoint values also force the zero solution.

Choose the real leading eigenfunction y1​=Csinx, keeping its amplitude C=0 fixed. At first order in the regular perturbation expansion, the equation becomes
y11′′​+y11​=−λ11​y1​−y12​,y11​(0)=y11​(π)=0.
(17)
The operator L=D2+1 with these homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions is self-adjoint, as allowed in the question, and its null space is spanned by y1​. The Fredholm solvability condition requires the forcing to be orthogonal to that null space:
0=∫0π​y1​(−λ11​y1​−y12​)dx.
(18)
The same condition follows directly by integrating y1​Ly11​ by parts: the endpoint term [y1​y11′​−y1′​y11​]0π​ vanishes, and Ly1​=0. Since a nonzero real y1​ has a positive squared integral,
λ11​=−∫0π​y1​(x)2dx∫0π​y1​(x)3dx​.​
(19)
This is the requested expression and is an instance of the leading nonlinear eigenvalue shift in a Sturm-Liouville problem. In the amplitude convention above, ∫0π​sin2xdx=π/2 and ∫0π​sin3xdx=4/3, so λ11​=−8C/(3π). For the particular choice C=1, it is −8/(3π).
The amplitude dependence of a nonlinear eigenvalue correction follows here from the quadratic term: rescaling the eigenfunction changes the eigenvalue shift. There is no single numerical correction independent of the chosen leading amplitude. Once the solvability condition is met, y11​ is determined up to addition of a multiple of y1​; a normalization such as ∫0π​y1​y11​dx=0 can fix that remaining freedom.
