Take l>0 and use the inner product ⟨u,v⟩=∫0l​uvdx on the Hilbert space L2(0,l). The differential operator has domain
D(L)={u∈H2(0,l):u(0)=u(l)=0},
(1)
where H2 is a Sobolev space. Twice applying integration by parts gives the Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator
⟨u,Lv⟩−⟨Lu,v⟩=[uv′−u′v]0l​.
(2)
The Dirichlet boundary conditions make this expression zero for u,v∈D(L), and the formal adjoint is L∗=d2/dx2+1. To obtain the domain of the adjoint operator, initially impose the boundary conditions only on u. The remaining expression is −[u′v]0l​. Since u′(0) and u′(l) vary independently, it vanishes for all such u exactly when v(0)=v(l)=0. The adjoint domain is therefore the original domain: this is full self-adjointness, including the boundary conditions.
Consequently the eigenvalues are real, eigenfunctions for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal, and regular Sturm-Liouville theory supplies a complete orthonormal eigenbasis. Here these objects can be exhibited explicitly:
ϕn​(x)=l2​​sinlnπx​,λn​=1−(lnπ​)2,n=1,2,….​
(3)
Indeed Lϕn​=λn​ϕn​, and the Fourier sine basis is complete in L2(0,l). There are no additional eigenvalues: for a nonzero eigenfunction u, integration by parts gives
λ=1−∫0l​∣u∣2dx∫0l​∣u′∣2dx​<1,
(4)
so u′′+(1−λ)u=0 and the endpoint values force 1−λ​l=nπ. All these eigenvalues are simple. The eigenvectors are precisely the nonzero multiples of these eigenfunctions, regarded as vectors in the Hilbert space.
