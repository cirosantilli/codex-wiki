Take l>0 and use the inner product ⟨u,v⟩=∫0l​uvdx on the Hilbert space L2(0,l). The differential operator has domain
D(L)={u∈H2(0,l):u(0)=u(l)=0},
(1)
where H2 is a Sobolev space. Twice applying integration by parts gives the Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator
⟨u,Lv⟩−⟨Lu,v⟩=[uv′−u′v]0l​.
(2)
The Dirichlet boundary conditions make this expression zero for u,v∈D(L), and the formal adjoint is L∗=d2/dx2+1. To obtain the domain of the adjoint operator, initially impose the boundary conditions only on u. The remaining expression is −[u′v]0l​. Since u′(0) and u′(l) vary independently, it vanishes for all such u exactly when v(0)=v(l)=0. The adjoint domain is therefore the original domain: this is full self-adjointness, including the boundary conditions.
Consequently the eigenvalues are real, eigenfunctions for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal, and regular Sturm-Liouville theory supplies a complete orthonormal eigenbasis. Here these objects can be exhibited explicitly:
ϕn​(x)=l2​​sinlnπx​,λn​=1−(lnπ​)2,n=1,2,….​
(3)

(4)
so u′′+(1−λ)u=0 and the endpoint values force 1−λ​l=nπ. All these eigenvalues are simple. The eigenvectors are precisely the nonzero multiples of these eigenfunctions, regarded as vectors in the Hilbert space.

f(x)=∑n=1∞​fn​sinlnπx​,fn​=l2​∫0l​f(t)sinlnπt​dt,y(x)=∑n=1∞​cn​sinlnπx​.

(17)
When l=mπ, the exceptional index is n=m, for which the eigenfunction is sinx. If fm​=0, its coefficient equation is 0=fm​, an inconsistency: there is no solution. If fm​=0, the coefficient equation is 0=0, and cm​ is arbitrary. The full family is
y(x)=Csinx+n≥1n=m​∑​1−(n/m)2fn​​sinmnx​,C∈R.​
(18)
This is the Fredholm alternative directly in the coefficient equations: nonzero eigenvalues fix their coefficients; a zero eigenvalue requires zero forcing in that direction and leaves the corresponding coefficient free. An inconsistent equation at a zero eigenvalue cannot be repaired by assigning an infinite coefficient.
For convergence, Lipschitz continuity implies absolute continuity of f, with bounded derivative almost everywhere. One integration by parts gives fn​=O(n−1); since the denominators grow quadratically, cn​=O(n−3) apart from the single free coefficient. The solution series and its first derivative therefore converge uniformly. The second derivative converges in L2, and the resulting Sobolev space solution satisfies y′′=f−y. As f−y is a continuous function, this is a classical C2 solution, with both boundary conditions satisfied. The forcing's Fourier sine series is interpreted on the open interval or in L2; its endpoint values need not equal the endpoint values of f.

Putting both data at zero makes this an initial value problem. The uniqueness theorem for ordinary differential equations applies, and one can also prove uniqueness directly: the difference of two solutions is a homogeneous solution Acosx+Bsinx whose value and first derivative at zero are both zero, forcing A=B=0. In fact, for every prescribed continuous function f,
y(x)=∫0x​sin(x−t)f(t)dt​
(21)
satisfies both initial data and y′′+y=f. Consequently non-uniqueness is impossible for every l>0, including l=mπ. In this setting the kernel is trivial. The boundary conditions at a single endpoint do not give the same self-adjoint operator domain as the two separated Dirichlet boundary conditions, so the earlier exceptional lengths do not apply.

The power-law ansatz y=xr gives
ϵr(r−1)+r−1=(r−1)(ϵr+1)=0.

Since ϵ>0, the equation roots 1 and −1/ϵ are distinct. The Cauchy-Euler equation therefore has general solution
y(x)=C1​x−1/ϵ+C2​x.​

For C1​=C2​=1, the first derivative and second derivative are
y′=1−ϵ1​x−1/ϵ−1,y′′=ϵ21+ϵ​x−1/ϵ−2>0.
(46)
The strictly convex function diverges to +∞ as x→0+, passes through (1,2), and has a unique lowest point at
xmin​=ϵ−ϵ/(1+ϵ),ymin​=(1+ϵ)xmin​.​
(47)
As x→∞, y−x→0, so the line y=x is an asymptote. When 0<ϵ<1, the lowest point is to the right of one; at ϵ=1 it is at one, and for ϵ>1 it is to the left of one.
The ratio of the two terms is
xx−1/ϵ​=x−(1+1/ϵ).
(48)
Thus x−1/ϵ is larger for 0<x<1, the terms are equal at x=1, and x is larger for x>1. In the stronger asymptotic sense as ϵ→0+, x−1/ϵ dominates at each fixed x<1, and x dominates at each fixed x>1. Their crossover has ∣logx∣=O(ϵ), equivalently x=1+O(ϵ), as described by the power-law crossover in a singular perturbation. In that region x=1+ϵX with bounded X gives y∼1+e−X.
Substitute the proposed regular perturbation y=y0​+ϵy1​+⋯ into the differential equation. The leading and subsequent equations are
xy0′​−y0​=0,xyj′​−yj​=−x2yj−1′′​(j≥1).
(49)
Consequently y0​=Cx, and in fact each successive coefficient has the form yj​=Cj​x, because the preceding second derivative is zero. The leading approximation is therefore

(50)

Those assumed in the question require a nonzero x−1/ϵ coefficient, which a power series in ϵ cannot recover: at fixed x>1 it is exponentially small, and at fixed x<1 it grows without bound as ϵ→0+. Thus the regular perturbation describes the specified solution only in the region to the right of the crossover, away from x=1; it is not a uniform approximation on (0,∞).
