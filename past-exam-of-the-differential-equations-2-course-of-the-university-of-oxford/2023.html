y1​(x)=e−xcosx,y2​(x)=e−xsinx​.

Set a=π/2. To construct the Green's function from a Dirac delta function, require Lx​g(x,ξ)=δ(x−ξ) and zero endpoint values. Away from x=ξ, the homogeneous solutions satisfying the left and right boundary conditions are respectively
u(x)=e−xsinx,v(x)=e−xcosx.
(5)
Write g=A(ξ)u(x) for x<ξ and g=B(ξ)v(x) for x>ξ. A discontinuity in g would generate a derivative of the Dirac delta function, which is absent from the desired equation. Thus g is continuous. Integrating that equation through ξ then gives the jump condition
gx​(ξ+,ξ)−gx​(ξ−,ξ)=1.
(6)
The resulting two equations are Au(ξ)=Bv(ξ) and Bv′(ξ)−Au′(ξ)=1. Since the Wronskian uv′−u′v=−e−2ξ, they give
g(x,ξ)=−eξ−x{sinxcosξ,sinξcosx,​0≤x≤ξ,ξ≤x≤π/2.​​
(7)
Both endpoint values vanish. At x=ξ, subtracting the two first derivatives gives sin2ξ+cos2ξ=1, verifying the sign and normalization of the Dirac delta function.

At a=π, the nonzero homogeneous solution e−xsinx satisfies both zero boundary conditions. Thus the boundary value problem has a nontrivial kernel and cannot have an inverse for arbitrary forcing.
More directly, the adjoint operator has the zero-endpoint homogeneous solution w(x)=exsinx. If an ordinary Green's function existed, the integration by parts identity would imply
0=∫0π​w(x)Lx​g(x,ξ)dx=∫0π​w(x)δ(x−ξ)dx=w(ξ).
(10)
But w(ξ)=0 for 0<ξ<π. This contradiction rules out the Green's function for the unmodified operator. It is the Fredholm alternative obstruction.

Use t=x+1. The Legendre differential equation becomes
t(2−t)ytt​+2(1−t)yt​+Ny=0.
(17)
Substituting a Frobenius series y=tr∑k≥0​ak​tk, a0​=0, gives the indicial equation 2r2=0: the indicial root zero is repeated. For r=0, the coefficient of tk yields
2(k+1)2ak+1​+[n(n+1)−k(k+1)]ak​=0,
(18)
or
ak+1​=2(k+1)2(k−n)(k+n+1)​ak​​.
(19)
For 0≤k<n the multiplying factor is nonzero. At k=n it vanishes, so an+1​ and all later coefficients are zero. Thus one solution is a polynomial of exact polynomial degree n, including a nonzero constant when n=0. Denote a chosen normalization by Pn​.
The linear recurrence relation determines every analytic coefficient from a0​, so there cannot be a second independent analytic solution at t=0. To see the required logarithm explicitly, reduction of order gives the second solution locally as
Pn​(x)∫x(1−s2)Pn​(s)2ds​.
(20)
Near t=0, the integrand is 1/(2a02​t) plus an analytic function. After rescaling the second solution, its form is therefore
y2​(x)=Pn​(x)log(x+1)+k=0∑∞​bk​(x+1)k​.
(21)
The analytic remainder converges locally; its constant term may be set to zero by adding a multiple of Pn​. The nonzero logarithmic coefficient makes y2​ linearly independent of Pn​. This Frobenius expansion of the Legendre equation at an endpoint gives a Logarithmic Frobenius solution; the same endpoint calculation at x=1 explains why the independent Legendre function of the second kind is excluded when boundedness at both ends is required.

Write the operator in divergence form:
Ly=((1−x2)y′)′.
(22)
The Legendre differential equation therefore gives
LPn​=−n(n+1)Pn​,n=0,1,2,…​.

These are the eigenvalues of L. With the common positive-sign convention for a Sturm-Liouville problem, use −L instead:
−(pPn′​)′=λn​wPn​,p=1−x2,q=0,w=1,λn​=n(n+1)​.
(24)
This is a singular Sturm-Liouville problem, because p vanishes at the endpoints. Bounded eigenfunctions select the nonlogarithmic branches and satisfy pPn′​→0 there. The Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator gives
[m(m+1)−n(n+1)]∫−11​Pm​Pn​dx=[(1−x2)(Pm​Pn′​−Pn​Pm′​)]−11​=0.

The weight is one. No choice of normalization of the Legendre polynomials is needed for this conclusion.

First take the usual endpoint-regular interpretation f∈C([−1,1]). Integrating the divergence form shows that F=(1−x2)y′ has finite endpoint limits. A nonzero limit would make y′ proportional to 1/(1∓x) near that endpoint, forcing a logarithmically unbounded y. Thus boundedness requires F(−1)=F(1)=0, and consequently
∫−11​f(x)dx=0​.
(27)
This is orthogonality to the constant eigenfunction P0​, hence the Fredholm alternative condition. It is also sufficient under the stated endpoint regularity: put
F(x)=∫−1x​f(t)dt,y(x)=C+∫0x​1−s2F(s)​ds.
(28)
If M=max∣f∣, the zero-mean condition gives ∣F(s)∣≤Mmin(1+s,1−s). Hence the last integrand is bounded, so y extends continuously to both endpoints. Any two bounded solutions differ by a constant: their difference solves ((1−x2)z′)′=0, whose other solution is 21​log((1+x)/(1−x)).
For the requested eigenfunction expansion, set
Nk​=∫−11​Pk​(t)2dt,fk​=Nk​∫−11​f(t)Pk​(t)dt​.
(29)
The Legendre polynomials span all polynomials, so their completeness follows from the Weierstrass approximation theorem. Projecting Ly=f onto each eigenfunction gives −k(k+1)yk​=fk​ for k≥1. The zero mode is free, while f0​=0. Thus the full family is
y(x)=C−k=1∑∞​k(k+1)Pk​(x)​∫−11​Pk​(t)2dt∫−11​f(t)Pk​(t)dt​,C∈R​.
(30)
The formula is unchanged by rescaling any Pk​. For the usual normalization Pk​(1)=1, the facts ∣Pk​(x)∣≤1 on [−1,1] and Nk​=2/(2k+1), together with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, bound each summand by a constant times k−3/2. The series therefore converges absolutely and uniformly. Its identification with the preceding integral solution avoids unjustified termwise second differentiation.
There is a source ambiguity: continuity only on (−1,1) does not supply the endpoint control used above. Even for integrable forcing, zero mean alone is insufficient. For example, let
H(x)=log1−x2e​,F(x)=H(x)1​,f(x)=F′(x)=(1−x2)H(x)2−2x​.
(31)
This f is continuous in the interior, absolutely integrable, and has zero mean because F(±1)=0. Yet every candidate with zero endpoint values of (1−x2)y′ has
y′(x)=(1−x2)H(x)1​,
(32)
whose integral diverges logarithmically in H at each endpoint. Thus no bounded solution exists. For a merely interior-continuous f, the exact criterion is that some constant B makes
∫0x​1−s2B+∫0s​f(t)dt​ds
(33)
bounded as x approaches both endpoints. The Bounded solvability for the Legendre operator criterion above covers the intended regular forcing and makes this endpoint limitation explicit.

When n=0, the forcing has nonzero integral ∫−11​1dx=2, violating the Fredholm alternative condition. There is no bounded solution. Directly, integration gives (1−x2)y′=x+B; cancellation of its logarithmic divergence at x=−1 requires B=1, while cancellation at x=1 requires B=−1.
For n≥1, the constant 1/[n(n+1)] is a particular solution. Subtract it from any solution. The remainder satisfies the Legendre differential equation, whose general solution is a combination of Pn​ and the logarithmically unbounded Legendre function of the second kind. Boundedness removes the latter coefficient, giving
⎩⎨⎧​no bounded solution,y(x)=n(n+1)1​+CPn​(x),C∈R,​n=0,n≥1.​​
(34)
The constant forcing is orthogonal to Pn​ for n≥1, consistently with Orthogonality of Legendre polynomials and the Fredholm alternative.

Take ϵ→0+. There are two real branches, on different scales. For a root tending to zero, the Taylor series gives
−6x3​+O(x5)=ϵ(1−x2).
(37)
The dominant balance is x3∼−6ϵ, so a fractional-power root expansion yields
x−​∼−(6ϵ)1/3​.
(38)
More precisely x−​=−(6ϵ)1/3+O(ϵ). An ordinary power series in integer powers of ϵ would miss this scale.
There is also a root escaping to positive infinity. Boundedness of the sine term makes the leading dominant balance x−ϵx2=0. The nonzero branch is
x+​∼ϵ−1​.
(39)
Indeed the equation rearranges to ϵx+2​−x+​=ϵ−sinx+​, which yields x+​=ϵ−1+O(1) on this branch. The bounded oscillatory correction need not have a constant limiting value.
To check that these are all the real solutions, solve for the parameter:
ϵ=E(x):=x2−1x−sinx​.
(40)
Positive values occur only on (−1,0) and (1,∞); x=±1 are not roots. A useful exact derivative is
E′(x)=−(x2−1)22{xcos(x/2)−sin(x/2)}2​≤0.
(41)
It vanishes only at isolated points, so E is strictly decreasing on each of these intervals. Its endpoint limits are +∞ and zero on each interval. This monotone parametrization of sine perturbation roots gives exactly one root in each interval for every ϵ>0, proving that both asymptotic branches, and no others, have been included. If negative small parameters are allowed, the transformation (x,ϵ)↦(−x,−ϵ) reflects these two branches and gives all real solutions for that sign as well.
