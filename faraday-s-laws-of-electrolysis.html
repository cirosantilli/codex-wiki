Faraday's laws of electrolysis (m=MQ/(zF))

 Home Chemistry Electrochemistry Electrolysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 320
Faraday's laws of electrolysis state that the mass of material formed or consumed by a specified electrode reaction is proportional to the transported electric charge, and that equal electric charge produces masses proportional to M/z for different reactions. Here M is the product's mass per mole, and z is the positive number of electrons transferred per product entity. For complete use of the electric charge in that reaction,
ne−​=FQ​,nproduct​=zFQ​,m=zFMQ​.
(10)
The first relation uses the Faraday constant; the second uses half-reaction stoichiometry; the third converts amount of substance to mass. For equal transported electric charge in two ideal cells,
m2​m1​​=M2​/z2​M1​/z1​​.
(11)
The number z belongs to the balanced half-reaction, rather than to the metal's name alone: copper deposited from Cu2+ needs z=2. These modern forms of both laws are given in KIT's practical.[ref]
If other same-direction electrode reactions share the measured electric current, define an integrated current efficiency η=Qproduct​/Q. Provided the product is neither subsequently consumed nor lost, the retained mass is
m=ηzFMQ​,η=∫I(t)dt∫Iproduct​(t)dt​.
(12)
Here 0≤η≤1 follows from the specified same-direction partition of electric current. It is not a universal bound on every signed partial-current convention: IUPAC notes that simultaneous oxidation and reduction at one electrode can produce a current-efficiency ratio above one.[ref] When efficiency varies during a run, use the charge-weighted ratio above, rather than an unweighted average over time. If deposition removes material from a multicomponent chemical solution, specify which chemical species is being counted and balance its half-reaction before choosing z.
Michael Faraday's accessible 1834 paper, Experimental Researches in Electricity, Seventh Series, §783 states proportionality between chemical action and the quantity of electricity passed. In §§789–791 he describes connecting a molten tin-chloride cell in series with a water-decomposition charge indicator, weighing the receiving electrode before and after, and comparing the deposited metal with the indicator's gas readout. The common series electric current makes these two readouts comparable. This was a quantitative test of chemical equivalents, rather than merely an observation that a deposit appeared. It is historical evidence, not a suggested classroom procedure; the modern electron-counting derivation above uses concepts unavailable to Faraday.[ref]
