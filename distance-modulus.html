Distance modulus (μ)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Astrophysics Astronomical magnitude
OurBigBook.com Words: 337 Articles: 1
The distance modulus compares received brightness with intrinsic brightness through μ=m−M, the difference between apparent magnitude and absolute magnitude in the same photometric passband and magnitude zero point. With no interstellar extinction, fixed emitted spectrum and luminosity, isotropy of emission, and the local inverse-square law, it determines Euclidean distance:
μ=m−M=5log10​(10pcd​),d=10pc10μ/5.
(20)
The ratio inside the logarithm is dimensionless. To see the factor 5, use the inverse-square law in the magnitude difference formula:
F10​F(d)​=(d10pc​)2,m−M=−2.5log10​[(d10pc​)2].
(21)
For example, m=5 and M=0 give μ=5, hence d=10pc×10=100pc. A tenfold increase in distance adds five magnitudes; an increase of one magnitude multiplies distance by 101/5≃1.584893. The distance modulus is a logarithmic scale for distance, not a distance in length units. [ref].
At 10 parsecs, μ=0 and m=M. Within 10 parsecs, the distance modulus is negative; for example, μ=−5 gives the positive distance d=1pc. A negative distance modulus does not imply negative distance. At cosmological distances, the inference needs luminosity distance and a K correction; the simple local formula must not silently absorb these effects.
Interstellar extinction increases apparent magnitude. The observed distance modulus then includes both geometric dimming and attenuation; extinction bias in photometric distance explains how to separate them.
