Thévenin's theorem (V=Voc​−IRth​)

 Home Electrical engineering
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 7
Thévenin's theorem replaces the terminal behavior of a linear resistive circuit by an ideal voltage source in series with a Thévenin resistance, whenever its terminal electric potential difference is a well-defined affine function of the delivered electric current. Fix terminals a,b, write V=ϕ(a)−ϕ(b), and take I positive out of the network at a into the electrical load, returning through b. Then
V=Voc​−IRth​.
(32)
The open-circuit voltage Voc​ is the intercept at I=0; −Rth​ is the slope. Thus two parameters predict what happens when many different electrical loads are connected. The Wheatstone bridge detector loading calculation uses precisely this replacement to find the detector reading even when the bridge's two voltage dividers interact through its detector.
For example, a 12V ideal voltage source with 2kΩ above and 4kΩ below a voltage divider junction supplies Voc​=8V and Rth​=34​kΩ at that junction relative to the lower terminal. Attaching a 4kΩ electrical load reduces its voltage to 6V and draws 1.5mA. The equivalent explains why an unloaded divider ratio alone cannot predict a loaded output.
The equivalence concerns the chosen terminals, including the power VI delivered there. It does not identify internal branch electric currents or internal Joule heating. At open circuit the original divider still draws 2mA from its 12V supply, while the equivalent's series branch carries no electric current. [ref]
