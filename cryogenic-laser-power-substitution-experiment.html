Cryogenic laser-power substitution experiment

 ... Physics Branches of physics Optics Radiometry Electrical substitution radiometer Cryogenic radiometer
OurBigBook.com Words: 243
In NIST's 2001 comparison, the Laser-Optimized Cryogenic Radiometer held its cavity near 5.4K and controlled the heat sink separately. Opening the beam reduced heater power at fixed cavity temperature; an AC bridge read the temperature. Tests used stabilized, spatially filtered lasers at 488, 514 and 633nm, with horizontal input and a movable photodiode transfer detector in the cavity's optical plane. The paper contrasts this with the High Accuracy Cryogenic Radiometer's optical heating followed by two electrical heating cycles and interpolation.[ref]
The two laboratories compared calibrations of the same transfer detectors, and checked their spatial response. Alignment differences and transported dust limited that comparison; its agreement did not prove universal equivalence. Its correction tables retain window transmission, cavity radiometric absorptance, alignment, electrical readout and optical-electrical heating non-equivalence. The heat sink was controlled, but §3 does not specify its numerical setpoint: the 5.0K reference in the preceding example is stipulated.[ref]
For a reproducible substitution measurement, record the beam's wavelength and entrance boundary, receiver and reference temperatures, heater terminal voltage and electric current, dark readings before and after illumination, and the settling rule. Check beam collection at the aperture, window transmission, radiometric absorptance, deposited lead heating, and sensitivity to beam position. Repeatability alone misses a stable correction error. Photometric calibration can subsequently use this radiant flux scale, but photometry still needs the appropriate spectral visual weighting; the cold receiver does not itself measure luminous flux.
