Born rule (pj​=∣⟨j∣ψ⟩∣2)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Quantum mechanics Wave function Probability amplitude
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 11
The Born rule connects a quantum state to measurement probabilities. Let {∣j⟩} be a complete orthonormal basis, finite or countably infinite, for a Hilbert space, and let
∣ψ⟩=∑j​aj​∣j⟩,aj​=⟨j∣ψ⟩,⟨ψ∣ψ⟩=1.
(18)
An ideal measurement distinguishing these basis vectors returns label j with probability
pj​=∣aj​∣2=∣⟨j∣ψ⟩∣2.
(19)
This assignment is a physical measurement rule. Its compatibility with total probability one follows from orthonormality and normalization:
∑j​pj​=∑j​∣aj​∣2=∑j,k​aj∗​ak​⟨j∣k⟩=⟨ψ∣ψ⟩=1.
(20)
The probabilities are the squared moduli, not the amplitudes themselves or the squares aj2​. [ref].
If the basis is an eigenbasis of an observable, the reported numerical value is its eigenvalue. Several vectors can share one eigenvalue; the probability of that value is then the sum over the corresponding basis labels. More generally, a projective measurement with orthogonal projections Pr​ assigns
pr​=⟨ψ∣Pr​∣ψ⟩=∥Pr​∣ψ⟩∥2,pr​=Tr(ρPr​)
(21)
for a pure state and a general density matrix ρ, respectively. This also covers mixed states and degenerate eigenspaces. The Born rule determines outcome statistics; the ideal conditional state change is specified separately by the Lüders rule. See projective measurement for that formulation and its assumptions. [ref].
For a worked example, take the normalized qubit quantum state
∣ψϕ​⟩=23​​∣0⟩+2eiϕ​∣1⟩.
(22)
The squared moduli add to 3/4+1/4=1. A computational-basis measurement, equivalently a measurement of the Z Pauli matrix, has
p0​=​23​​​2=43​,p1​=​2eiϕ​​2=41​.
(23)
These probabilities do not depend on the relative quantum phase ϕ. Now choose the Hadamard basis
∣±⟩=2​∣0⟩±∣1⟩​,
(24)
whose vectors are eigenstates of the X Pauli matrix, also used by the Pauli X gate. The new probability amplitudes and probabilities are
b±​=⟨±∣ψϕ​⟩=22​3​±eiϕ​,p±​=∣b±​∣2=21​±43​​cosϕ.
(25)
Both probabilities are nonnegative and their sum is one. The relative quantum phase becomes visible because the two probability amplitudes add before their squared modulus is taken:
  • At ϕ=0, p+​=(2+3​)/4≃0.9330127 and p−​≃0.0669873.
  • At ϕ=π/2, p+​=p−​=1/2.
  • At ϕ=π, p+​≃0.0669873 and p−​≃0.9330127.
A Hadamard gate followed by a computational-basis measurement realizes these X statistics, since H∣±⟩=∣0⟩,∣1⟩, respectively. Compare the two bases using fresh, identically prepared copies: a preceding measurement may change the state. The quantum superposition section shows why replacing the preparation by the mixed state diag(3/4,1/4) keeps the Z statistics but gives p+​=p−​=1/2 for every ϕ.
The usefulness of the Born rule is this conversion from linear algebra to experimental predictions: calculate overlaps in the basis actually measured, then take squared moduli. Its appealing geometry is that changing basis redistributes probability while preserving total squared norm. The same rule relates an energy eigenstate expansion to an energy measurement and a position wavefunction to a probability density.
Born's 1926 paper on quantum scattering interpreted the outgoing-wave coefficients in terms of the probabilities of collision outcomes; a corrective footnote specified proportionality to the square of the amplitude. This is a historical scattering formulation, rather than the general density matrix formula above. [ref]; [ref].
