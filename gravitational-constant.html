Gravitational constant (G)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Classical mechanics Newton's law of universal gravitation Newtonian gravitational field
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 3
The gravitational constant G sets the strength of Newton's law of universal gravitation. Two point masses M and m a center-to-center distance r apart attract with force magnitude
F=r2GMm​.
(21)
For fixed masses and separation, doubling G would double the force. For bodies with spherical symmetry, the same exterior formula follows from the spherical shell theorem; for arbitrary extended bodies, add the attractions of their mass density elements instead. The gravitational constant connects a laboratory attraction to the Newtonian gravitational field controlling a Kepler orbit.
The gravitational constant G and local gravitational acceleration g are different quantities. Applying Newton's second law to a test particle gives g=F/m=GM/r2 for one spherical source. The source's mass and the observer's position change g; G remains the same parameter of the law. A local falling-body experiment therefore measures a field, not G alone. On rotating Earth, an instrument fixed to the surface also responds to centrifugal acceleration, while surrounding mass density and altitude change the gravitational contribution.
Dimensional analysis explains the units. Denote the base physical dimensions of length, mass and time by L,M,T. Since force has dimension MLT−2,
[G]=[M][m][F][r]2​=L3M−1T−2,G has SI unit m3kg−1s−2.
(22)
By contrast, [g]=LT−2 and the standard gravitational parameter μ=GM has dimension L3T−2. A small numerical value of a dimensional constant depends on the units chosen: it is the resulting force for specified physical masses and separation that conveys the laboratory scale.
NIST's current complete listing gives
G=6.67430(15)×10−11m3kg−1s−2
(23)
for the 2022 CODATA adjustment, the latest adjustment listed when checked on 14 October 2026. Parentheses indicate a standard uncertainty u(G)=0.00015×10−11m3kg−1s−2, approximately 22 parts per million relative to G. The adjustment year is 2022; NIST's May 2024 data update is a different date.[ref] [ref]
For an original scale calculation, stipulate two 1kg point masses separated by 1m and insert this recommended G: F=6.67430×10−11N. The corresponding acceleration of either mass is 6.67430×10−11ms−2 at that instant. Reducing the separation to 0.10m multiplies both by 100. These are ideal model predictions, not observations; their tiny scale explains why stray forces matter.
Being a constant of a physical law does not make the numerical value exact. The SI fixes the numerical values of seven defining constants, including the speed of light and Planck constant; G is determined experimentally. Exact unit definitions still require physical realizations with measurement uncertainty: calibrating a length or a mass does not become error-free.[ref]
