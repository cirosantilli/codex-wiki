Let each drive region have length ℓ and the drive-free gap have length L, measured edge to edge. A particle of constant speed v>0 experiences τ(v)=ℓ/v and T(v)=L/v. For a normalized detected-beam speed probability density function f(v), sum the transition probabilities, not the probability amplitudes of different particles:
P(δ)=∫0∞​f(v)Pg→e​(δ;ℓ/v,L/v)dv,f(v)=u42v3​e−(v/u)2,v>0.
(64)
The displayed f is an ideal effusion flux distribution, with speed scale u, uniform transmission and speed-independent detection efficiency. It is not a claim about any particular clock's selected beam. The substitution x=(v/u)2 gives f(v)dv=xe−xdx, proving normalization. Actual apertures, state selection and readout can change the effective weights.
For an original example choose ℓ=0.10m, L=1.00m, u=v∗​=200ms−1 and Ω=2π×500rads−1. The reference particle has τ∗​=0.500ms, T∗​=5.00ms and resonant π/2 pulses. The instantaneous-pulse width would be 100Hz. The exact finite-pulse width for that single speed is about 88.7Hz; the chosen distribution also reduces the on-resonance average to approximately 0.738 because not every particle receives a π/2 pulse.
Different velocities smear side fringes because δL/v varies across the beam. Nevertheless every term is even in δ, so this symmetric ideal-model average has no odd line asymmetry that could displace the central lock point. Reduced contrast or altered width alone is not evidence of a shifted line center. A speed-dependent transition shift or unequal drive quantum phases changes that conclusion; atomic clock frequency shifts explains why these distinctions matter experimentally.
