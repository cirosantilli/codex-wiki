Vector space (V)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra
OurBigBook.com Words: 17k Articles: 300
A vector space over a field F is a set V equipped with vector addition and scalar multiplication satisfying the vector space axioms. Its vectors can represent directed displacements, lists of measurements, polynomials, or entire functions. The common structure is that adding two elements and multiplying an element by a scalar remain meaningful operations inside the same space.
For example, R2 uses coordinatewise addition and scaling. With
b1​=(2,1),b2​=(1,2),v=(5,4),
(1)
we have v=2b1​+b2​. Moving twice along b1​ and once along b2​ reaches the same vector as five horizontal and four vertical steps. The two descriptions refer to different bases of the same space. A basis makes such descriptions unique; a spanning set makes them possible.
The space R[x]≤2​ of real polynomials of degree at most two has basis 1,x,x2: the polynomial 3−2x+x2 is the linear combination with coordinates (3,−2,1). The zero vector here is the zero polynomial, and addition combines coefficients. A derivative is a linear map on this space; its matrix records how it acts on the three basis vectors. The same coordinate machinery therefore handles both geometric motion and operations on functions.
The choice of field matters. The complex numbers form a one-dimensional vector space over C with basis 1, but a two-dimensional vector space over R with basis 1,i. The question "how many independent directions?" has an answer only after the allowed scalars are specified. Likewise, polynomials of degree exactly two do not form a vector space: subtraction can remove the leading term, and the zero polynomial is absent.
The usefulness of the abstraction is that a linear map is determined by its values on a basis. Once those images are known, linear combinations give its action on every vector. The rank-nullity theorem relates the number of attainable outputs to the number of invisible input directions, while change of basis lets us choose coordinates suited to the problem. Eigenvectors, for example, choose directions on which repeated application of a linear operator becomes scalar multiplication.
A historical source is Hermann Grassmann's Die lineale Ausdehnungslehre (1844). Its full title advertises applications to other branches of mathematics, statics, mechanics, magnetism and crystallography; the original edition is digitized by ETH-Bibliothek. This gives an early setting for treating geometric quantities algebraically across different applications, rather than attributing the modern axiom list to that title alone.[ref]
