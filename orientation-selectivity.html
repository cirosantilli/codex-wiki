Orientation selectivity

 Home Biology Neuroscience Primary visual cortex
OurBigBook.com Words: 532 Articles: 2
Orientation selectivity is a systematic dependence of a visual neuron's response on the axis of a bar, edge or stripe pattern presented in its receptive field, under specified stimulus conditions. Here the angle θ measures the stripe axis from horizontal: 0∘ is horizontal and 90∘ vertical. An axis has no arrow, so θ and θ+180∘ describe the same orientation. A preference for one direction of movement across that axis is an additional property; the 1962 recordings included both equal and unequal responses to opposite movements. [ref]
A particularly clear experiment using extracellular single-unit recording appears in the 1959 paper, Figures 2–3. A bright 1∘×8∘ slit was centered on the same receptive field and rotated through successive angles; each presentation lasted one second. The vertical slit strongly increased the extracellularly recorded spike train, whereas the horizontal slit was ineffective. Small-spot tests had identified ON and OFF receptive-field subregions, with a central ON region and opposing flanks: rotation illuminated less of the center and more of the flanks. Geometry and location therefore mattered together. These dimensions belong to that recorded unit, not to every neuron. [ref]
An orientation tuning curve summarizes a controlled angle sweep. Keep location, size and visual contrast fixed; for a stripe pattern also specify spatial frequency and phase. The same signed spatial filter model of a simple cell can produce strong orientation selectivity and strong dependence on phase, as the following original toy illustrates. Its smooth curves are calculated predictions, not digitized or invented biological measurements.
