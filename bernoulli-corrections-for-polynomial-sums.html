For a concrete comparison, take f(x)=x2 on [0,6]. The integral is I=∫06​x2dx=72, whereas the unit-width left and right Riemann sums are L=55 and R=91. The exact integer power sum formula gives
L=I−262​+66​=55,R=I+262​+66​=91.
(9)
The endpoint choice explains the opposite signs of the 18 correction. The remaining correction is one in both cases.
The same method is useful far beyond squares of numbers. Substituting B0​, B1​, B2​ and B4​ into the integer power sum formula gives the exact fourth-power result
∑k=1N​k4=5N5​+2N4​+3N3​−30N​.
(10)
At N=10, this is
integral20000​​+endpoint5000​​+B2​ term31000​​​−B4​ term31​​​=25333.
(11)
Thus a finite polynomial calculation replaces ten additions, or a million additions if N is a million. Although the individual corrections can be rational numbers, the result is the exact integer sum.
There is also a useful numerical integration interpretation. The average of the left and right Riemann sums is the composite trapezoidal rule. For x2, the endpoint corrections cancel but its value is 73, one above the true integral. Subtracting the B2​ correction makes this particular polynomial example exact. For f(x)=x4, the two interior corrections above can be written B2​(f′(N)−f′(0))/2! and B4​(f′′′(N)−f′′′(0))/4!. These are exact identities for these polynomials; extending such corrections to general functions requires a separate error analysis.
