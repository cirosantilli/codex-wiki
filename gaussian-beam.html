Gaussian beam (I(r,z)=2Pe−2r2/w(z)2/(πw(z)2))

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics Physical optics
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 6
A fundamental Gaussian beam is an ideal beam of light whose transverse wave amplitude has a Gaussian function profile. Here consider a circularly symmetric beam in vacuum, with propagation along z, two transverse coordinates x,y, and radius r=x2+y2​ from the optical axis. With the beam waist at z=0, total power P>0, and vacuum wavelength λ, its time-averaged light intensity, meaning irradiance in Wm−2, is
I(r,z)=πw(z)22P​exp(−w(z)22r2​),w(z)=w0​1+(zR​z​)2​,zR​=λπw02​​.
(12)
Here w0​ is the minimum beam radius, and zR​ is the Rayleigh range. Far from the beam waist, w(z)∼θ∣z∣ with beam divergence half-angle θ=λ/(πw0​). These are the fundamental circular Gaussian beam laws within the scalar paraxial approximation, appropriate when w0​≫λ and the beam divergence is small. The underlying envelope solves the parabolic wave equation exactly; this does not make it an exact vector solution of Maxwell equations. [ref].
The radius convention matters. Writing U for the complex wave amplitude envelope, the same w(z) is the wave amplitude 1/e radius and the light intensity 1/e2 radius:
∣U(0,z)∣∣U(r,z)∣​=e−r2/w(z)2,I(0,z)I(r,z)​=e−2r2/w(z)2.
(13)
At r=w(z) these ratios are e−1≈0.367879 and e−2≈0.135335, respectively. The light intensity 1/e radius is instead w(z)/2​. None of these radii is a sharp edge: the ideal Gaussian function has nonzero tails at every finite radius. Kogelnik and Li explicitly use the wave amplitude 1/e radius in their fundamental-mode treatment. [ref].
For a numerical example, take vacuum wavelength λ=632.8nm and beam waist radius w0​=0.500mm. Substitution gives Rayleigh range zR​≈1.24114754m and far-field beam divergence half-angle θ≈0.402852992mrad. At z=zR​, the beam radius is w=2​w0​≈0.707107mm and the on-axis irradiance is half its waist value. At the same wavelength, halving the beam waist radius to 0.250mm quarters the Rayleigh range to 0.310286885m and doubles the beam divergence half-angle to 0.805705984mrad. At fixed power, that smaller beam waist also quadruples the peak irradiance.

The normalization expresses conserved power during free propagation. In transverse polar coordinates, an annulus has area 2πrdr, so
∫R2​Idxdy=2π∫0∞​I(r,z)rdr=P,PP(r<a)​=1−e−2a2/w(z)2.
(14)
A proof outline is the substitution s=2r2/w(z)2 in this integral. Spreading reduces peak irradiance as w−2 while increasing transverse area as w2, leaving power unchanged. In particular, the disk of radius w(z) contains 1−e−2≈0.8646647168, or 86.4665%, of the total power; the local light intensity at its rim is only 13.5335% of the on-axis light intensity. These are different quantities.

For an ideal absorbing circular aperture of radius a centered on the optical axis, the same fraction gives the power passing the aperture at that plane. An aperture of radius w clips about 13.5335% of the incident power; radius 2w clips only e−8≈0.000335463, or 0.0335463%. After clipping, extra diffraction means the transmitted field no longer obeys the unmodified ideal Gaussian beam law. Every finite aperture cuts some ideal tail. Kogelnik and Li distinguish their large-aperture beam analysis from their finite-aperture diffraction analysis. [ref].
The useful tradeoff is that a small bright spot cannot remain narrow indefinitely. An optical lens can create a smaller beam waist, but diffraction then makes the beam spread faster. The particularly simple relation w0​θ=λ/π ties localization directly to beam divergence. A proof outline for the propagation law starts with U(x,y,0)=U0​e−(x2+y2)/w02​. The parabolic wave equation separates into two transverse factors, and a Gaussian integral in each coordinate gives
U(x,y,z)=q(z)U0​​exp(−w02​q(z)x2+y2​),q(z)=1+izR​z​,k=λ2π​.
(15)
Here q is dimensionless and k is the wavenumber; the carrier convention is ei(kz−ωt), with angular frequency ω. Taking the squared complex modulus gives the stated w(z) and its w−2 light intensity scaling. Each transverse factor contributes q−1/2, explaining the q−1 prefactor here and the different prefactor in the preserved Gaussian beam with one transverse coordinate.
Historically, Gaussian beam theory developed alongside early laser resonator theory and lens-guided transmission. In their 1966 review, Kogelnik and Li credit Boyd and Gordon, and Goubau and Schwering, with early beam treatments; they summarize earlier work rather than claim the whole construction as a new discovery. Their paper brings beam propagation, focusing and finite-aperture diffraction into a common practical account. [ref].
