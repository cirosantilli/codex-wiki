Newton's identities (mem​=∑r=1m​(−1)r−1em−r​pr​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Polynomial Elementary symmetric polynomial
OurBigBook.com Words: 736 Articles: 4
Newton's identities let two different summaries of the same polynomial roots talk to each other. Elementary symmetric polynomials record products of distinct root positions; power sums record sums of individual powers, pr​=∑i=1n​xir​. Either can be easier to obtain in a particular problem. The identities turn one into the other, often avoiding any need to find the individual polynomial roots.
Write e0​=1 and em​=0 for m>n. For every positive integer m, the identity is
mem​=r=1∑m​(−1)r−1em−r​pr​​.
(41)
It is a polynomial identity over the integers, so it remains true on substitution in any commutative ring. To solve it for em​ by division, however, m must have a multiplicative inverse. Over a field of characteristic zero, the first n power sums recover every elementary symmetric polynomial. The same recovery works when the positive characteristic of a field is greater than n; small characteristic needs care.[ref]
The first three relations are e1​=p1​, 2e2​=e1​p1​−p2​, and 3e3​=e2​p1​−e1​p2​+p3​. When division by two and three is permitted, this becomes
e1​=p1​,e2​=2p12​−p2​​,e3​=6p13​−3p1​p2​+2p3​​.
(42)
For the three roots 1,2,3, their power sums are p1​=6, p2​=14, and p3​=36. The formulas give e2​=(36−14)/2=11 and e3​=(216−252+72)/6=6, recovering the polynomial z3−6z2+11z−6 from aggregate data.
The beauty is that the summaries do not depend on the order of the polynomial roots. In symmetric polynomials, one can work with quantities that survive every relabeling; in linear algebra, traces of matrix powers give the same kind of aggregate information about eigenvalues. The identities convert those summaries into coefficients of a characteristic polynomial.
A short derivation packages the identities into a formal power series. Let E(t)=∏i​(1+xi​t)=∑m≥0​em​tm. Since its constant coefficient is one, it has an inverse as a formal power series, and its formal derivative satisfies
E(t)E′(t)​=∑i​1+xi​txi​​=∑r≥1​(−1)r−1pr​tr−1.
(43)
Multiply by E(t) and compare the coefficient of tm−1. This gives Newton's identities without a convergence assumption or division by m. The division issue arises only when using them to recover em​. The logarithmic-derivative approach is also developed in Newton's Identities (January 2019 notes) by Milan Mossé (§4, pp. 6–8).
The alternate name Newton-Girard identities recalls Albert Girard's earlier work. A modern historical account credits formulas through sixth powers to Albert Girard in 1629 and discusses their later appearance in Isaac Newton's Arithmetica universalis.[ref] This citation refers to that account; the original historical editions have not been inspected here.
