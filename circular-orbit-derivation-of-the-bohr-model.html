Circular-orbit derivation of the Bohr model (rn​=n2ℏ2/(μκ),En​=−μκ2/(2ℏ2n2))

 Home Physics Mathematical physics Hydrogen atom Bohr model
OurBigBook.com Words: 275
Consider a single electron of mass me​ and a point atomic nucleus of mass M and positive electric charge Ze, with e>0 the elementary charge. Use nonrelativistic classical mechanics, omit external fields and interactions with other electrons, and write
μ=me​+Mme​M​,κ=4πϵ0​Ze2​,ℏ=2πh​.
(14)
Here μ is the Coulomb two-body reduced mass, ϵ0​ the vacuum permittivity, and h the Planck constant. The separation r and relative speed v describe the two-particle relative coordinate; neither body is silently pinned in place. The electron's own radius about the centre of mass is Mr/(M+me​).
For the assumed circular orbit, Coulomb's law provides the required centripetal acceleration, and the imposed orbital angular momentum condition is
rμv2​=r2κ​,L=μvr=nℏ,n=1,2,3,….
(15)
Substitute v=nℏ/(μr) into the first equation and cancel r−2 to obtain
rn​=μκn2ℏ2​,vn​=nℏκ​.
(16)
The kinetic energy is K=μv2/2=κ/(2r) and the Coulomb potential energy is U=−κ/r, with zero energy at infinite separation. Hence
En​=K+U=−2rn​κ​=−2(4πϵ0​)2ℏ2n2μZ2e4​.
(17)
This derives the energy sequence from the stated circular model; the modern Schrödinger equation derivation is the distinct series-termination quantization of the Coulomb radial equation. The radius above scales as n2/Z, whereas the binding energy scales as μZ2/n2. The Bohr radius article supplies the corresponding Coulomb potential length scale. The nonrelativistic assumption requires vn​≪c, with c the speed of light.
For an original numerical example choose Z=1, M=1.67262192369×10−27kg, me​=9.1093837015×10−31kg, e=1.602176634×10−19C, h=6.62607015×10−34Js, ϵ0​=8.8541878128×10−12Fm−1, and c=299792458ms−1. Treat these as stipulated constants for the calculation. They give μ/me​=0.99945568, r1​=0.0529465nm, E1​=−13.598287eV and v1​/c≃0.00730, where eV denotes an electronvolt. At n=2, r2​=4r1​ and E2​=E1​/4; the ground-state ionization energy is −E1​.
Taking M→∞ gives μ→me​. A finite nuclear mass reduces every binding energy by μ/me​ and increases each separation by me​/μ. This follows from relative motion, not a fitted correction to individual spectral lines. Replacing the atomic nucleus by a heavier isotope changes the same factor, providing another check of the model.
