Linear differential equation (L[y]=g(t))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Differential equation Ordinary differential equation
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 18
A scalar linear differential equation of order n≥1 has the form
L[y](t)=an​(t)y(n)(t)+an−1​(t)y(n−1)(t)+⋯+a1​(t)y′(t)+a0​(t)y(t)=g(t).
(56)
Here the coefficients aj​ and the forcing term g are prescribed functions of the independent variable t. A classical solution of a differential equation is an n-times differentiable function satisfying the identity at every point of its interval. The unknown function y and its derivatives enter to the first power, without products between them or coefficients depending on y. Equivalently, the differential operator L is a linear map: for constant scalars α,β,
L[αu+βv]=αL[u]+βL[v].
(57)
The forcing term may be zero, giving a homogeneous linear differential equation, or nonzero, giving an inhomogeneous linear differential equation. Linearity of L does not mean that every function graph of a solution of a differential equation is a straight line.
To use the usual order-n initial value problem theory on an interval I, assume that an​(t)=0 throughout I and that all aj​ and g are continuous functions there. Dividing by an​ normalizes the highest derivative; specifying y(t0​),y′(t0​),…,y(n−1)(t0​) at t0​∈I then selects one solution of a differential equation throughout I. A zero of an​ needs separate analysis: one cannot divide through it and retain these conclusions. The first-order normalization appears in OpenStax's definition and standard form of linear equations; the corresponding system theorem is in MIT's existence and uniqueness notes for linear systems.
The coefficients need not be linear functions of t. For example, t2y′+y=0 is a linear differential equation on t>0, although t2 is quadratic. Its solution of a differential equation with initial condition y(1)=1 is y(t)=e1/t−1: its derivative is y′=−y/t2, so substitution gives t2y′+y=0, and y(2)=e−1/2≈0.60653. The zero of the highest coefficient at t=0 explains the domain restriction. In contrast, y′+y2=0, yy′+y=0 and y′+siny=0 are nonlinear ordinary differential equations because of their dependence on the unknown function. Variable coefficients and nonlinear ordinary differential equations are different issues; see MIT's definitions and examples of first-order linear equations.
The practical benefit of linearity is that it separates the effect of the initial condition from the effect of the forcing term. Once one particular solution yp​ satisfies L[yp​]=g, every solution of a differential equation is
y=yp​+h,h∈kerL.
(58)
Here kerL is the kernel of a linear map, consisting of solutions of a differential equation to the associated homogeneous linear differential equation. Indeed, L[y−yp​]=g−g=0, and conversely L[yp​+h]=g+0=g. Thus the family for a fixed forcing term is an affine space obtained by translating kerL. If g is not identically zero, the zero function is absent, so this family is not a vector space under the usual operations on functions. This short argument explains why a general solution is a particular solution plus a homogeneous solution.
