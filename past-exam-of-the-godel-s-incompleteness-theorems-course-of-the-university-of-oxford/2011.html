a OurBigBook.com
Write BA=P(┌A┐), with the corner notation understood as the numeral for the Gödel number. An arithmetical provability predicate numerates the theorems of S in the standard natural numbers and satisfies the Hilbert-Bernays-Löb derivability conditions:
​S⊢A⟹S⊢BA,an external rule;S⊢B(A→C)→(BA→BC);S⊢BA→B(BA).​
(1)
The last two conditions are internal theorems, not rules authorizing reflection from BA to A.
Here P(x)=∃yProv(x,y) numerates the theorem set by the given assumption. If S⊢A, its proof supplies a genuine natural-number witness, making BA a true Sigma-1 formula. Sigma-1 completeness gives S⊢BA, proving the first condition. The second condition is supplied explicitly. Since BA is itself a Sigma-1 formula, the given formalized Sigma-1 completeness yields S⊢BA→B(BA), the third condition. Thus the specified existential proof predicate is an arithmetical provability predicate.

b OurBigBook.com
Write BA=∃yProv(┌A┐,y) and fix a refutable first-order sentence ⊥, so S⊢¬⊥ and ConS​=¬B⊥. First prove the internal equivalence
S⊢G↔ConS​.​
(2)
Because ⊥→G is provable, the derivability conditions give B⊥→BG. The fixed-point equivalence G↔¬BG therefore yields G→¬B⊥.
For the reverse implication, necessitating G→¬BG and applying distribution gives BG→B(¬BG). positive introspection gives BG→B(BG). Finally the provable implication BG→(¬BG→⊥), necessitated and distributed, gives
B(BG)→(B(¬BG)→B⊥).
(3)
Consequently S⊢BG→B⊥, so ¬B⊥→¬BG→G. The same argument works with any chosen refutable first-order sentence in place of ⊥; their formal consistency statements are provably equivalent.
By the classical deduction theorem for propositional logic applied to adjoining a first-order sentence, S+{G} is consistent exactly when S⊬¬G. The displayed equivalence makes S+{G} and S+{ConS​} deductively equivalent. Therefore
S⊬¬G⟺S+{ConS​} is consistent.​
(4)
No soundness assumption on that extension is being substituted for consistency.

c OurBigBook.com
If S is inconsistent, it proves ¬R by explosion. Conversely, suppose S is consistent and, towards a contradiction, has a proof of ¬R with standard code n. Consistency excludes any proof of R. In particular, for each k≤n, the closed bounded statement ¬Prov(┌R┐,k) is true and is provable in S by Sigma-1 completeness. The true bounded statement Prov(┌¬R┐,n) is also provable.
Using the given finite-numeral exhaustion below n, these finitely many nonproof facts give
S⊢∀y≤n¬Prov(┌R┐,y).
(5)
For arbitrary y, the given comparison splits into y≤n or n≤y. In the first case the antecedent Prov(┌R┐,y) is false. In the second case the known negative proof at code n supplies the required witness z≤y. Hence S proves
∀y(Prov(┌R┐,y)→∃z≤yProv(┌¬R┐,z)).
(6)
The Rosser sentence equivalence now gives S⊢R, contradicting consistency. Thus
S⊬¬R⟺S is consistent.​
(7)
Only a fixed finite list of closed bounded facts was used; no assumption that the first-order theory is omega-consistent is needed.

The preceding Gödel sentence argument gives S⊢G→ConS​. The permitted arithmetized positive half of the Rosser sentence argument is
S⊢ConS​→¬BR.
(8)
Pure first-order logic gives ¬BR→R: if there is no proof of R, each antecedent in its defining universal implication is false. Therefore
S⊢G→ConS​→¬BR→R,S⊢G→R.​
(9)
This uses the supplied internal Rosser implication, rather than replacing an external nonprovability fact by an unproved internal first-order sentence.

Let T=S+{ConS​} and assume T is consistent. The allowed arithmetizations of the previous equivalences give
S⊢¬B(¬R)↔ConS​,S⊢¬B(¬G)↔Con(T).
(10)
Suppose S⊢R→G. Its contrapositive is ¬G→¬R, and the Hilbert-Bernays-Löb derivability conditions then give
S⊢B(¬G)→B(¬R),S⊢¬B(¬R)→¬B(¬G).
(11)
Combining with the two internal equivalences yields S⊢ConS​→Con(T). Since T contains ConS​, it would prove Con(T), contrary to the given Gödel second incompleteness theorem for this consistent extension. Hence
S⊬R→Gif S+{ConS​} is consistent.​
(12)
Together with the preceding implication, this is the Strict Gödel-to-Rosser implication under the stated consistency hypothesis.

a OurBigBook.com
Löb's theorem states that, for a first-order theory with the stated provability and diagonalization properties,
S⊢(BA→A)⟹S⊢A,​
(13)
where BA=PS​(┌A┐). It does not require an extra consistency assumption.
By the diagonal lemma, choose D with S⊢D↔(BD→A). necessitation rule and distribution applied to D→(BD→A) give
S⊢BD→B(BD→A),S⊢BD→(B(BD)→BA).
(14)
Using positive introspection BD→B(BD) gives the unconditional theorem S⊢BD→BA. If S⊢BA→A, it follows that S⊢BD→A, and the reverse fixed-point implication gives S⊢D. Necessitating gives S⊢BD, hence S⊢BA and finally S⊢A.
The same derivation also gives the Internal form of Löb's theorem, needed for modal soundness. Put H=(BA→A). From the unconditional BD→BA, propositional reasoning and the fixed point give S⊢H→D. necessitation rule and distribution imply S⊢BH→BD, and hence
S⊢B(BA→A)→BA.​
(15)
This internal statement was derived without necessitating a temporary assumption.

The Gödel second incompleteness theorem says that a consistent, effectively axiomatized first-order theory with enough arithmetic and its standard derivability and diagonalization properties cannot prove its formal consistency statement ConS​=¬B⊥.
If a consistent S proved ConS​, it would prove B⊥→⊥ by classical propositional logic. Apply Löb's theorem to A=⊥. This would give S⊢⊥, contradicting consistency. Therefore
S consistent⟹S⊬ConS​.​
(16)
For an inconsistent first-order theory the conclusion need not hold, since it proves every first-order sentence.

ii OurBigBook.com
Assume S⊢¬X and S⊢BX→X. Combining these theorems gives S⊢¬BX. Because X is refutable, ¬BX is a legitimate formal consistency statement for S: the standard contradiction first-order sentence and X are provably interderivable, and the derivability conditions transfer those implications to their provability statements.
Thus if S were consistent, the Gödel second incompleteness theorem would contradict S⊢¬BX. Consequently the assumed reflection theorem forces S to be inconsistent; explosion then gives S⊢X. This establishes the instance of Löb's theorem
S⊢BX→X⟹S⊢X​
(17)
for every X refutable in S, using only the second incompleteness theorem.

Assume S⊢BS​X→X and put T=S+{¬X}. Then T⊢¬BS​X. The given extension-provability implication, with Y=⊥, gives
S⊢BT​⊥→BS​(¬X→⊥).
(18)
The provable classical implication (¬X→⊥)→X, together with distribution, gives S⊢BS​(¬X→⊥)→BS​X. Hence
T⊢BT​⊥→BS​X,T⊢¬BT​⊥=Con(T).
(19)
If T were consistent, this would violate its Gödel second incompleteness theorem. Thus T is inconsistent. The deduction theorem for propositional logic and double-negation elimination give S⊢¬¬X, hence S⊢X. Therefore the second incompleteness theorem for S+{¬X} yields Löb's theorem for arbitrary X.

Use just the assumed local reflection instance with Y=¬X:
S+{X}⊢BS​(¬X)→¬X.
(20)
By the deduction theorem for propositional logic, S⊢X→(BS​(¬X)→¬X). Classical propositional logic makes this imply the unconditional reflection statement S⊢BS​(¬X)→¬X: if X is false the consequent is already true, and if X is true the displayed implication supplies it. Löb's theorem applied to ¬X now gives
S⊢¬X.​
(21)
Thus full local reflection in a one-sentence extension is inconsistent; in this argument even the single instance concerning ¬X suffices.

Put T=S+{X} and choose its Gödel sentence Z, so T⊢Z↔¬BT​Z. The assumed reflection scheme gives the particular theorem
T⊢BS​(X→Z)→(X→Z).
(22)
The given extension-provability implication is S⊢BT​Z→BS​(X→Z). The fixed-point equivalence gives T⊢¬Z→BT​Z, so these implications compose to
T⊢¬Z→(X→Z).
(23)
The supplied propositional tautology ((¬Z→(X→Z))→(X→Z)) gives T⊢X→Z, and T⊢X then yields T⊢Z. The first of the derivability conditions for T gives T⊢BT​Z, while its Gödel equivalence gives T⊢¬BT​Z. Hence T is inconsistent. By the deduction theorem for propositional logic,
S⊢¬X.​
(24)
This proof uses diagonalization and proof transfer for the extension, and invokes neither Löb's theorem nor the second incompleteness theorem.

a OurBigBook.com
The Gödel-Löb logic GL uses propositional variables, ⊥, implication, and a unary modal operator □. Other Boolean connectives are abbreviations; for example ¬A means A→⊥ and ⊤ means ⊥→⊥.
Its axioms are all classical propositional tautologies and the two schemes
K:L:​□(A→B)→(□A→□B),□(□A→A)→□A.​​
(25)
Its rules are the modus ponens rule and the necessitation rule, GL⊢A⇒GL⊢□A. Uniform propositional substitution is allowed, or equivalently built into the axiom schemes. necessitation rule applies to theorems; a temporary assumption cannot simply be boxed. In provability logic, □A is read as the formal assertion that A is provable.

b OurBigBook.com
Induct on a derivation in Gödel-Löb logic. Propositional axiom instances translate to theorems of Peano arithmetic. The image of ⊥ is a refutable sentence, so it behaves as falsity in those translated propositional arguments.
The translation of axiom K is the distribution clause of the Hilbert-Bernays-Löb derivability conditions. The translation of axiom L is
PA⊢B(BA→A)→BA,
(26)
the Internal form of Löb's theorem, whose derivation was given above and applies to the stipulated arithmetical provability predicate for Peano arithmetic. Thus every axiom has a provable translation.
The modus ponens rule is preserved because the interpretation commutes with implication. The necessitation rule is preserved by the first derivability condition: if PA⊢Ii​(A), then
PA⊢Pr(┌Ii​(A)┐)=Ii​(□A).
(27)
Here the equality denotes the definition of the translated formula, not an arithmetic equality of truth values. A propositional substitution merely changes the arithmetic sentences assigned to the variables. The induction proves the Arithmetical soundness of GL:
GL⊢A⟹PA⊢Ii​(A).​
(28)

Take Y(p)=p→q and put ⊤=(⊥→⊥), H=Y(⊤) and F0​=Y(□H)=□H→q. The first supplied substitution identity reads
GL⊢□H↔□Y(□H)=□F0​.
(29)
Use the supplied replacement of equivalents in the context D(p)=p→q. It gives
GL⊢F0​↔(□F0​→q).
(30)
But H=(⊤→q) is equivalent to q. Replacement under a box gives □H↔□q, so F0​ is equivalent to
F=(□q→q).​
(31)
Replacing equivalents once more transfers the fixed-point equation to F, proving GL⊢F↔(□F→q).
A direct check of this GL fixed point for a provability implication is useful: the Löb axiom gives □F→□q, and necessitating the tautology q→F gives □q→□F by K. Thus □F↔□q, and replacing the antecedent in □F→q recovers F.

Use the preceding fixed point with q=⊥. If the chosen arithmetic interpretation sends ⊥ to a PA-refutable sentence Z, it gives the arithmetic sentence
C=(BZ→Z),PA⊢C↔(BC→Z).
(32)
This follows from Arithmetical soundness of GL. Since Peano arithmetic proves ¬Z, the two implications are equivalent to negated provability:
PA⊢C↔¬BZ,PA⊢C↔¬BC.
(33)
Thus C expresses the formal consistency statement for Peano arithmetic and is also a negative provability fixed point, like its Gödel sentence G.
Apply the supplied modal uniqueness formula to the context with a modalized occurrence X(p)=(□p→⊥), with two fresh variables interpreted as G and C. A modalized occurrence means that each indicated variable occurrence lies inside a box. Peano arithmetic proves both arithmetic fixed-point equations. The first derivability condition boxes those equations, so the translated uniqueness formula gives
PA⊢B(G↔C).
(34)
One must not infer G↔C by reflection. Instead, distribution and the provability of the two propositional projections give PA⊢BG↔BC. Negating and using the two fixed-point equations now yields
PA⊢G↔C↔¬BZ=Con(PA).​
(35)
This proves the Equivalence of a Gödel sentence and a consistency statement using the required modal soundness, fixed point and uniqueness results.
