Independent and identically distributed random variables (Xi​∼iidν)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Random variable Independent random variables
OurBigBook.com Words: 596 Articles: 4
An independent and identically distributed sequence combines two conditions: its members are independent random variables, and every member has the same probability distribution. For a finite sequence X1​,…,Xn​ with common law ν, this means that for all measurable sets B1​,…,Bn​,
P(X1​∈B1​,…,Xn​∈Bn​)=i=1∏n​ν(Bi​).​
(22)
For an infinite sequence, require this condition for every finite collection of distinct indices. Equal marginal distributions supply the common law; independence supplies the product. Neither condition supplies the other. Grinstead and Snell call the combination an independent trials process.[ref]
Choose a Bernoulli distribution with success probability 1/2. Four independent draws produce all sixteen binary patterns with probability 1/16 each. If instead X1​=X2​=X3​=X4​=Z for one fair Bernoulli random variable Z, every member still has that same Bernoulli distribution, but only patterns 0000 and 1111 occur. Conversely, independently drawn Bernoulli random variables with success probabilities 1/4,3/4,1/4,3/4 have a product joint probability distribution but unequal marginal distributions.
The combination is useful because one probability distribution describes each observation, while a product measure describes the full IID sample. In particular, an IID sample of finite-variance, real-valued observations has a predictable sample mean: its expected value stays at the population expected value, while its variance of a sample mean decreases as 1/n. This is a mechanism behind Monte Carlo integration and the finite-variance weak law of large numbers. Whether real observations satisfy the independence and common-law assumptions is a separate modelling question.
