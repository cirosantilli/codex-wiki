Ice-melting calorimetry

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Latent heat of fusion
OurBigBook.com Words: 755 Articles: 1
Ice-melting calorimetry measures the fusion enthalpy through conservation of energy, rather than inferring it from the small temperature change of the melting ice itself. In a mixing experiment, warm water and the container cool while added ice melts and the resulting water warms. Their combined energy balance separates fusion from sensible heat.
Weigh the container and stirrer, then weigh the added warm water to obtain mw​. Let the container and water reach a common initial temperature Ti​. Prepare ice in a clean ice-water mixture near Tm​, rather than assuming freezer ice is already at Tm​; drain and blot its surface immediately before transfer. Add it without splashing, stir gently, and record temperature before addition and until after the last ice disappears. Reweigh to find the added mass mi​. Wisconsin's laboratory guide specifies drying each piece, weighing before and after, and recording the temperature history; it places the initial and final temperatures on opposite sides of the jacket temperature to reduce net environmental exchange. [ref]
For fully melted, initially dry ice at Tm​, a common final temperature Tf​>Tm​, constant water specific heat capacity at constant pressure cw​, and apparatus heat capacity Ccal​ initially at Ti​,
(mw​cw​+Ccal​)(Ti​−Tf​)+Qenv​=mi​L+mi​cw​(Tf​−Tm​).
(12)
Here Qenv​>0 denotes net heat entering the apparatus from its surroundings. The left side supplies the fusion enthalpy and the sensible heat on the right; the water produced by melting must be warmed too. The balance extends Case Western's mixing demonstration by including the apparatus and environmental terms. If the specific heat capacities vary appreciably, replace their products with the corresponding temperature integrals. [ref]
Determine Ccal​ independently, for example by a warm/cold water mixing balance with no phase transition, including the wetted stirrer and thermometer in the apparatus. Check the thermometer calibration, keep the lid in place between transfers, and monitor the surroundings. Before/after drift and a comparable run without ice help bound Qenv​; such corrections assume comparable heat transfer conditions and are not automatically exact. Choosing a jacket temperature between Ti​ and Tf​ reduces exchange but does not prove Qenv​=0. Ensure all the ice has melted and the contents are well mixed before using the measured Tf​: with residual ice, the weighed addition is not the mass that has undergone fusion. Repeat the experiment and report measurement errors in mass, temperature, Ccal​ and the leak correction.
An independent way to see the plateau is David Roundy's Oregon State experiment: heat an ice-water mixture slowly with a waterproof resistor in an insulated cup, stir, and record temperature, heater electric current I(t), electric potential difference V(t) and elapsed time. Plot temperature against Eel​=∫I(t)V(t)dt, recording changes in I or V. The initial solid mass must exclude accompanying water. For a boundary enclosing the heater as well as the sample, this input is electrical work, so ΔH=Eel​+Qenv​ under the constant-pressure assumptions above. Across an ideal isothermal plateau the apparatus has no sensible heat change, giving ms​L=Eel​+Qenv​; departures from uniform temperature require accounting for the heater and container response.
