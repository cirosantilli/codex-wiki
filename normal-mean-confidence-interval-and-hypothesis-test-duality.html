Normal mean confidence interval and hypothesis test duality (z=(xˉ−μ0​)/(σ/n​),p=2[1−Φ(∣z∣)])

For the same normal distribution model, a two-sided test compares the null hypothesis H0​:μ=μ0​ with the alternative hypothesis H1​:μ=μ0​. Under the null hypothesis, the standardized test statistic has standard normal distribution. Write Φ for the standard normal distribution function. With xˉ=1.2, σ=2, n=16 and μ0​=0, the observed test statistic is z=1.2/0.5=2.4. Its p-value is
p=Pμ0​​(∣Z∣≥2.4)=2[1−Φ(2.4)]=0.01639507185….
(34)
Thus the p-value is a tail probability computed under the null hypothesis, including departures in both directions. It is not P(H0​∣x), the posterior probability of the null hypothesis, nor the probability that the observations arose "by chance". The normal distribution test statistic and its calibration appear in NIST's teaching reference for testing a process mean; the tail interpretation is stated in Penn State's hypothesis-testing notes.
Choose the significance level α=0.05 before observing the sample. The rejection region is ∣Z∣>z0.975​, whose probability under H0​ is exactly 0.05. Here 2.4>1.9599639845…, so reject H0​. This agrees with zero lying outside [0.22,2.18]. More generally, a proposed μ0​ belongs to the unrounded 95% confidence interval precisely when its two-sided test does not reject under this strict cutoff. The equality boundary has probability zero in this continuous statistical model.
The significance level and statistical power describe different repeated-experiment probabilities. At a specified alternative μ, put δ=(μ−μ0​)/(σ/n​) and q=z1−α/2​. Then
Pμ0​​(reject H0​)β(μ)=Pμ​(do not reject H0​)Pμ​(reject H0​)​=α,=Φ(q−δ)−Φ(−q−δ),=1−β(μ).​
(35)
The first is the Type I error probability; the second is the Type II error probability at that alternative. For a planning alternative μ=1, keeping μ0​=0, σ=2 and n=16 gives δ=2, β(1)≈0.483995 and statistical power 0.516005. At n=64, δ=4 and statistical power rises to 0.979327. These are model calculations for specified alternatives, not posterior probabilities inferred from a nonsignificant result. They explain why failure to reject cannot establish equality and why choosing sample size before an experiment is useful.
