Ionic approximation of bonds (OSi​=vi​−Niassigned​)

 Home Chemistry Chemical reaction Redox Oxidation Oxidation state
OurBigBook.com Words: 255
For the routine examples here, assign all bonding electrons between different chemical elements to the more electronegative partner; divide bonding electrons between identical chemical elements equally. Retain each lone pair on its original chemical atom. Subtract the assigned valence electron count Niassigned​ from the neutral chemical atom's valence electron count vi​. The IUPAC convention uses Allen electronegativity, based on free-chemical atom valence electron energies. Close electronegativity comparisons and some reversibly bound chemical compounds require care beyond this simple rule.[ref]
The examples explain exceptions to memorized rules:
  • Water, H−O−H: oxygen receives both O–H bond pairs plus its two lone pairs, giving eight assigned valence electrons and 6−8=−2. Each hydrogen receives none, giving 1−0=+1.
  • Hydrogen peroxide, H−O−O−H: each oxygen receives its O–H bond pair, one electron from O–O, and four electrons in lone pairs. Thus each has 6−7=−1, while each hydrogen remains +1. The O–O connection is why the usual −2 oxygen shortcut fails.
  • Molecular oxygen, O2​: equal splitting of the O–O bonding electrons leaves each oxygen assigned six valence electrons, so each has oxidation state zero.
  • Sodium hydride, NaH: hydrogen is more electronegative than sodium. The formula unit is assigned sodium +1 and hydrogen −1, consistent with the hydrogen anion. The usual +1 hydrogen shortcut fails here.
These drawings record allocations, not molecular geometry or a physical process of breaking the chemical bonds. In particular, the O–O line in the molecular oxygen panel only marks the connection; it does not assert a complete Lewis structure or spin state.
