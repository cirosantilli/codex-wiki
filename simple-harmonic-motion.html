Simple harmonic motion (x¨+ω2x=0)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Classical mechanics Particle dynamics One-dimensional conservative motion
OurBigBook.com Words: 536 Articles: 3
Simple harmonic motion is motion in which the acceleration is proportional to the displacement from equilibrium and points back towards it:
x¨=−ω2x,ω>0.
(158)
Here x is measured from equilibrium, and ω is the constant angular frequency. For a point mass attached to an ideal spring, Hooke's law gives the restoring force F=−kx, with stiffness k>0, and Newton's second law yields ω=k/m​. The minus sign matters: for x>0 the force is negative, and for x<0 it is positive. A restoring force need not oppose velocity; on the return towards equilibrium it increases speed.
The general solution is
x(t)=Acos(ωt+φ),v(t)=−Aωsin(ωt+φ),P=ω2π​.
(159)
The amplitude A≥0 and oscillation phase φ encode the initial conditions. We use P for the oscillation period to distinguish it from kinetic energy T. For A>0, the oscillation period is independent of amplitude, the property of isochronous harmonic motion. At A=0 the body remains at equilibrium. [ref]
Choose m=0.5kg, k=2N/m, and release the mass from rest at displacement A=0.3m. Then ω=2s−1, P=πs, and φ=0. Writing time t in seconds,
x(t)=0.3cos(2t)m,v(t)=−0.6sin(2t)m/s.
(160)
At t=0 the velocity vanishes, but the acceleration is −1.2m/s2: a turning point is not a state of rest that persists. At t=π/4s, the body crosses equilibrium with velocity −0.6m/s and zero acceleration. At t=π/2s it reaches the opposite turning point, x=−0.3m.
Taking potential energy zero at equilibrium, the same solution gives
V(t)=21​kx(t)2=0.09cos2(2t)J,T(t)=21​mv(t)2=0.09sin2(2t)J,E=T+V=0.09J.
(161)
At each turning point all mechanical energy is potential energy; at each equilibrium crossing it is kinetic energy. Both repeat twice during one complete position cycle because squaring removes the sign. At t=π/8s, for example, each contributes 0.045J.
The physical position trace covers a line segment, −0.3≤x≤0.3 in metres. In the phase plane, the paired position and velocity instead trace the ellipse
(0.3m)2x2​+(0.6m/s)2v2​=1.
(162)
This is precisely mv2/2+kx2/2=E, rewritten: the single conserved mechanical energy fixes the amplitude and the whole ellipse. It does not fix the starting oscillation phase. At x=0 both v=−0.6m/s and v=+0.6m/s lie on it; position alone cannot distinguish them. Replacing v by momentum p=mv gives the equivalent phase-space trajectory. The arrows show increasing time, clockwise in (x,v) coordinates.
This closed ellipse assumes a time-independent spring and no damping or applied drive. Adding a resisting force −bv, b>0, gives mx¨+bx˙+kx=0 and dE/dt=−bv2≤0. An underdamped damped harmonic oscillator spirals inward in the phase plane, crossing successively smaller energy contours. The ideal harmonic oscillator is also useful beyond springs: near a smooth potential energy minimum with V′′(0)>0, its Taylor expansion begins with V(0)+V′′(0)x2/2. For sufficiently small displacements, this gives the same restoring force to leading order, explaining why small oscillations share one solvable pattern.
