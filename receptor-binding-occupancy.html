Receptor binding occupancy (θ)

 Home Biology Protein Receptor (biochemistry)
OurBigBook.com Words: 316
Receptor binding occupancy is the fraction of binding sites occupied by a ligand. Consider reversible, noncooperative binding at chemical equilibrium, with homogeneous independent sites: every site has the same binding properties, and binding at one site does not change binding at another. Let L denote free ligand, R an unoccupied receptor site and RL the bound complex. In this dilute concentration model,
L+R⇌RL,Kd​=[RL][L][R]​,RT​=[R]+[RL],θ=RT​[RL]​=Kd​+[L][L]​.
(14)
Square brackets denote concentrations; RT​ is the total site concentration. Here the dissociation constant Kd​ has concentration units. Use free ligand in excess so binding negligibly depletes it; only under that approximation may the supplied total ligand concentration substitute for [L]. See Analysis of Receptor–Ligand Interactions: equilibrium binding and saturation.
For an illustrative dissociation constant Kd​=10nM, where 1nM=10−9molL−1, free ligand concentrations 1, 10 and 100nM give θ=1/11≈0.091, θ=1/2=0.5 and θ=10/11≈0.909, respectively. At [L]=Kd​, exactly half the sites are occupied in this model. Raising [L] tenfold from 10 to 100nM raises occupancy from 50% to about 91%, rather than tenfold: the finite pool of sites causes saturation.
A short derivation uses the law of mass action: at chemical equilibrium, association balances dissociation, giving [RL]=[L][R]/Kd​. Substitute this into θ=[RL]/([R]+[RL]) and cancel [R] to obtain the displayed formula. The limiting values follow directly: zero free ligand gives zero occupancy, and increasing [L] far above Kd​ drives occupancy toward one.
Receptor binding occupancy is not automatically the fraction of maximal response. Signal amplification, differing effects of bound ligands, and downstream saturation can change the relationship. Consequently, [L]=Kd​ specifies half occupancy here; it does not universally specify either half-maximal or maximal cellular response. These numbers describe a toy binding model, not measured responses of every receptor. See Higham and Colquhoun, 2024: the affinity–efficacy problem.
