Acid-base chemistry

 Home Chemistry
OurBigBook.com Words: 8k Articles: 69
Acid-base chemistry studies how chemical acids and chemical bases exchange protons or electron pairs. Here the focus is transfer of protons in water: a chemical acid donates a proton, and a chemical base accepts it. The resulting chemical equilibrium determines pH, while a buffer solution can resist changes in pH. This connects microscopic chemical reactions with a compact, measurable description of a chemical solution.
The important distinction is between how much chemical acid is present and how readily it transfers a proton. A weak acid and a strong acid at the same total concentration need not have the same pH. Conversely, two buffer solutions can have the same pH while containing very different total quantities of their chemical acids and chemical bases.

Isoelectric point (pI)

Words: 144
Acid-base chemistry OurBigBook.com
The isoelectric point is the pH at which a specified molecule's mean net electric charge is zero. Different protonation states can still carry nonzero electric charges; their contributions cancel in the average. For the two-step glycine speciation model, this gives pI=(pKa1​+pKa2​)/2. Additional ionizable amino acid side chains require their own chemical equilibrium calculations; averaging every pKa is not a general rule. [ref]
Below its isoelectric point, free glycine has positive mean electric charge and an electric field drives it toward the negative electrode; above its isoelectric point, the mean electric charge is negative and the direction reverses. At its isoelectric point the charge-driven contribution vanishes. Diffusion and bulk movement of the chemical solution can still move the sample. Gel electrophoresis applies this charge dependence to separation, although protein size, shape and interactions also affect migration. The pKa values of free glycine do not determine a protein's isoelectric point: its free N-terminus, C-terminus and ionizable amino acid side chains supply a different inventory. [ref]

Weak acid

Words: 4k Articles: 31
Acid (chemistry) OurBigBook.com
A weak acid establishes a chemical equilibrium between its proton-donating form and its conjugate base in water. For a neutral monoprotic acid HA, the aqueous chemical equation is
HA(aq)+H2​O(l)⇌H3​O+(aq)+A−(aq).
(1)
Water accepts a proton to become hydronium; A− is the conjugate base. The double arrow denotes reversible exchange at chemical equilibrium, not equal populations. The acid dissociation constant and composition determine the hydronium concentration; substituting total chemical acid concentration into the pH formula is generally incorrect.
“Weak” describes the tendency to transfer a proton in a specified solvent, rather than how much chemical acid was dissolved. A weak acid can be concentrated, and a strong acid can be dilute. Even the same weak acid can have a large weak acid dissociation fraction after sufficient dilution, while its acid dissociation constant stays fixed at the same temperature and standard state conventions.[ref]

Weak acid dissociation fraction (α=[A−]/CT​)

Words: 169
Weak acid OurBigBook.com
For a monoprotic acid with analytical concentration CT​=[HA]+[A−], define the dimensionless weak acid dissociation fraction
α=CT​[A−]​,1−α=CT​[HA]​.
(2)
This counts acid units in the conjugate base form. Identifying it with [H3​O+]/CT​ additionally requires negligible self-ionization of water and no other sources of electric charge. In the ideal dilute chemical solution model, with h=[H3​O+]/c∘, the acid dissociation constant gives α=Ka​/(Ka​+h). Thus lowering hydronium thermodynamic activity favors the conjugate base.
The diagram uses stipulated dimensionless acid dissociation constant Ka​=10−5 and self-ionization of water ion product Kw​=10−14 at fixed temperature; these are model inputs, not measurements of a named substance. It solves the weak acid mass and charge balances. At CT​=0.010molL−1, the calculated weak acid dissociation fraction is about 3.11% and pH about 3.51; at CT​=10−4molL−1, they are about 27.0% and 4.57. More acid units are dissociated as a fraction of the total, yet fewer hydronium ions occur per volume.
At extreme dilution, self-ionization of water makes h approach Kw​​, so pH approaches 7 for the chosen model. The limiting weak acid dissociation fraction is Ka​/(Ka​+Kw​​)≈0.9901, rather than exactly one. The calculation assumes pure added water, a neutral monoprotic acid, and no competing chemical reactions; actual contamination becomes increasingly important as CT​ falls.

Weak acid mass and charge balances

Words: 273 Articles: 1
Weak acid OurBigBook.com
Consider only neutral HA added to pure water, at fixed temperature, with ideal dilute thermodynamic activities, water thermodynamic activity approximated by one, and no added salts or other chemical species. Normalize every concentration by c∘=1molL−1:
c=CT​/c∘,h=[H3​O+]/c∘,x=[A−]/c∘,y=[HA]/c∘,o=[OH−]/c∘.
(3)
Conservation of acid units and zero net electric charge, respectively, require
x+y=c,h=x+o.
(4)
The second equation balances positive hydronium against negative conjugate base and hydroxide. It would need all additional ions if salts or a chemical base were added. Together with acid dissociation constant Ka​=hx/y and the self-ionization of water relation Kw​=ho, these give
x=Ka​+hKa​c​,y=Ka​+hhc​,o=hKw​​,h−hKw​​−Ka​+hKa​c​=0.
(5)
This is exact within the stated ideal model, including depletion and self-ionization of water. Multiplication by h(Ka​+h) yields the cubic equation
h3+Ka​h2−(Kw​+Ka​c)h−Ka​Kw​=0.
(6)
For positive Ka​,Kw​,c, the unmultiplied left-hand side increases strictly from negative to positive values as h increases from zero to infinity; its derivative is 1+Kw​/h2+Ka​c/(Ka​+h)2>0. There is therefore exactly one positive root of a polynomial. A bisection method can find it without choosing an unphysical root of a polynomial.
If Kw​/h2≪1, neglect hydroxide, so h≈x and the quadratic equation retaining acid depletion gives
c−xx2​=Ka​,x=2Ka2​+4Ka​c​−Ka​​=Ka2​+4Ka​c​+Ka​2Ka​c​.
(7)
The last form avoids subtracting nearly equal numbers. If also x/c≪1, neglect depletion to obtain
h≈x≈Ka​c​,[H3​O+]≈Ka​c∘CT​​.
(8)
Check both conditions after computing: x/c measures the fraction lost from the initial undissociated weak acid inventory, while Kw​/h2 measures the omitted hydroxide contribution to electric charge balance. A 5% depletion criterion is a chosen tolerance, not a universal boundary. Keeping depletion does not cure the neglect of self-ionization of water. These weak acid mass and charge balances also explain why the square root estimate cannot be extended to arbitrarily dilute chemical solutions.

Weak acid dissociation measurement

Words: 4k Articles: 26
Weak acid OurBigBook.com
A pH measurement becomes evidence for a weak acid chemical equilibrium when paired with a known analytical concentration and a calibrated glass electrode. For the pure-weak acid model, measured pH estimates h=10−pH only in the ideal thermodynamic activity approximation. Then o=Kw​/h, x=h−o, y=c−x, and Ka​=hx/y. Require 0<x<c; values outside that range signal inconsistent measurements or an inadequate model. Repeating this reconstruction across a dilution series tests whether one acid dissociation constant describes the data, rather than merely fitting one point.
The Analytical Sciences Digital Library's Potentiometric Titration of an Unknown Weak Monoprotic Acid provides a complementary acid-base titration experiment. Its apparatus includes a glass electrode and meter, a stirring magnet, a volumetric flask, pipettes, and a burette containing standardized sodium hydroxide. Prepare an aqueous acid stock of known analytical concentration, transfer a measured portion, and stir gently with the glass electrode clear of the magnet. Record added volume and stable pH after each addition; use a preliminary run to locate the steep region and smaller additions there on a fresh portion. The readout is the pH versus added volume curve, not a visual count of dissociated molecules.
Calibrate with fresh standard buffer solutions bracketing the pH range being interpreted. Use their assigned values at the actual temperature; a pair near pH 4 and 7 can cover the midpoint of many weak acid examples, but does not cover an entire alkaline endpoint region. Rinse between standards and samples to limit carryover, keep the sensing bulb and reference electrode junction immersed, and wait for a stable reading. These instrument practices are documented in HI5221 & HI5222 Instruction Manual: pH and mV and ISE and Temperature Bench Meters by Hanna Instruments (pp. 45–47, 49–50 and 73–74: calibration, measurement, rinsing and electrode conditioning).
Locate the acid-base titration equivalence region from the steepest change of pH with added volume. For one-to-one neutralization, standardized sodium hydroxide concentration times equivalence volume estimates the initial amount of substance of acid. Half that volume gives approximately equal [HA] and [A−] only when subsequent chemical equilibrium redistribution is small. The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation then gives pH≈pKa​. Unequal activity coefficients or appreciable hydronium or hydroxide inventories can shift this estimate. The full chemical equilibrium curve supplies a stronger consistency check than its midpoint alone.
Useful controls for this model test are a fresh independently prepared acid portion, a water blank, a check standard after measurement to detect instrument drift, and fixed recorded temperature. A blank diagnoses contamination but should not be subtracted directly from pH, which is a logarithm. Keep exposure to atmospheric carbon dioxide comparable, especially at high dilution, and record any background salt that changes ionic strength. These are experimental-design controls suggested by the model's assumptions. The observed curve and its uncertainty determine whether the model is adequate; the diagrams here contain no experimental readouts. An aqueous acid dissociation constant cannot be inferred unchanged from a sample requiring a different solvent mixture, and a nearly neutral dilute sample makes the difference h−Kw​/h particularly sensitive to pH error.

Acid-base titration
Words: 3k Articles: 16
Weak acid dissociation measurement OurBigBook.com
An acid-base titration adds measured quantities of a standardized chemical acid or chemical base to determine an initially unknown acid or base inventory through neutralization. Monitoring pH also constrains chemical equilibrium parameters such as the acid dissociation constant.
The added reagent is the titrant; a pipette selects an aliquot of the sample and a burette measures the added volume. Known stoichiometry converts this volume into amount of substance. This separates two questions that a single pH reading cannot answer: how much chemical acid is present, and how readily it donates a proton. A large inventory of weak acid can consume more titrant than a small inventory of strong acid despite having a higher initial pH.
Equivalence point (Veq​)
Words: 122
Acid-base titration OurBigBook.com
The equivalence point is the composition at which added titrant and the initial sample inventory match the specified chemical reaction's stoichiometry. It is a theoretical inventory condition; the titration endpoint is an observed stopping criterion. For a monoprotic acid consuming one hydroxide per acid unit, initial acid molar concentration cA​, sample volume VA​, and base titrant molar concentration cB​ give
nA​=cA​VA​=cB​Veq​,Veq​=cB​cA​VA​​.
(9)
The proof is amount of substance conservation with one-to-one neutralization; a different stoichiometry requires its coefficients. All volumes must use compatible units. Since 1mL=10−3L, 0.100molL−1×10.00mL=0.001000mol, not 1mol.
An equivalence point does not prescribe pH 7. A weak acid titrated with a strong base leaves its conjugate base, which reacts with water; the corresponding chemical equilibrium can make the chemical solution alkaline. The neutral strong acid/strong base case additionally depends on temperature, through self-ionization of water.[ref]
Titration endpoint
Words: 223 Articles: 2
Acid-base titration OurBigBook.com
A titration endpoint is the experimentally assigned point indicated by a specified signal: for example, a persistent color of a pH indicator, or a feature extracted from a potentiometric titration. An equivalence point is defined by stoichiometry; an endpoint estimates its location. State the signal rule and its repeatability. A fleeting local color where a drop enters an unmixed sample need not satisfy the rule for the mixed sample.
Define the endpoint offset ΔV=Vend​−Veq​. For one-to-one neutralization, using the endpoint without correction gives inferred acid inventory nA​=cB​Vend​ and
nA​nA​−nA​​=Veq​ΔV​.
(10)
Thus an illustrative 0.05mL overshoot at Veq​=10.00mL overestimates the inventory by 0.5%. Repeatedly reaching the same color can reduce scatter while retaining this systematic offset. This example stipulates an offset; it is not a measured drop size.

Strong acid titration model
Words: 372
Acid-base titration OurBigBook.com
Consider an original ideal model: VA​=10.00mL of hydrochloric acid at cA​=0.100molL−1, titrated with sodium hydroxide at cB​=0.100molL−1. Treat these numbers as exact model inputs. Assume complete dissociation of an ionic compound, complete strong acid ionization, additive volumes, uniform mixing, and activity coefficients equal to one. Use stipulated Kw​=10−14, approximately appropriate near 25∘C. These are calculated pH values, not measurements of these materials.
The chemical equation for neutralization is
H3​O++OH−⟶2H2​O.
(11)
Initial acid inventory is nA​=1.000mmol, so the equivalence point is v=10.00mL. Added water does not change this inventory. But the final volume VA​+v, including the titrant, determines all concentrations.
Keep self-ionization of water even at the equivalence point. With c∘=1molL−1, define the dimensionless acid excess d, hydronium level h, and hydroxide level o by
d=c∘(VA​+v)cA​VA​−cB​v​,h=c∘[H3​O+]​,o=c∘[OH−]​.
(12)
The sodium cation and chloride anion inventories fix the electric charge balance h−o=d, while chemical equilibrium gives ho=Kw​. Eliminating o yields a quadratic equation h2−dh−Kw​=0 with exactly one positive root. For stable calculation on either side, write
s=d2+4Kw​​,h={(d+s)/2,2Kw​/(s−d),​d≥0,d<0,​o=hKw​​,pH=−log10​h.
(13)
The second expression follows by multiplying by s−d; it avoids subtracting two nearly equal large quantities on the alkaline side. A logarithm acts on the dimensionless h, as required by the pH definition.
At v=5.00mL, the excess is 0.500mmol in 15.00mL, giving d=1/30 and pH 1.477121…. At v=15.00mL, excess hydroxide is 0.500mmol in 25.00mL, giving d=−1/50 and pH 12.301030…. Neglecting dilution would give the wrong values even far from the equivalence point.
Exactly at v=10.00mL, d=0 and h=o=Kw​​=10−7, giving pH 7 in this model. At 9.999mL and 10.001mL, the same smooth expression gives pH 5.300835… and 8.699121…. The familiar excess-only formulas would predict zero hydronium or zero hydroxide at the equivalence point; retaining self-ionization of water removes that failure. A steep curve is still continuous.
The sensitivity is especially revealing. Differentiating h−Kw​/h=d gives
ddd(pH)​=−ln(10)d2+4Kw​​1​,dvdd​​v=VA​​=−2c∘VA​cA​​
(14)
when cA​=cB​. At this model's equivalence point, the derivative is about 1.086×104 pH units per millilitre. This is a local derivative, not a linear prediction over a whole drop. Real endpoint accuracy also depends on dosing, mixing, measurement uncertainty, temperature, extra reacting chemical species, and nonideal thermodynamic activities.
Acid-base titration measurement
Words: 303
Acid-base titration OurBigBook.com
The institutional experiment The Potentiometric Titration of an Acid Mixture, from Massachusetts Institute of Technology's 5.310 Laboratory Chemistry, determines hydrochloric acid and phosphoric acid inventories. Dried potassium hydrogen phthalate standardizes sodium hydroxide. A volumetric pipette selects an acid aliquot, diluted with water in a beaker. A burette feeds a stirred sample beside a glass electrode; the apparatus isolates the sample from stirrer warming. The burette is conditioned with titrant and its tip freed of bubbles. Stable pH and added volume are recorded, with smaller additions near the transition. Controls include replicate standardizations, a consistent pale-pink phenolphthalein endpoint, and a standard buffer solution check between sample runs. The readout supports inventory estimates through known stoichiometry; it does not directly count molecules.[ref]
The same protocol requires drainage time for burette readings and warns that an undelivered hanging drop spoils the association between volume and pH. Its color-persistence wording differs between the main paragraph and footnote, so a reproducible titration endpoint needs an explicitly chosen criterion. Its acid mixture is not the single-strong acid ideal model above.[ref]
A distinct control appears in Process Measurement Procedures: Sulfuric Acid Emissions, §1.4.4.2: the phenolphthalein standardization is repeated with water replacing the acid, matching the sample aliquot and indicator quantity. The volume needed for the same pink endpoint is subtracted from the sample reading.[ref]
The independent bookkeeping for such a matched blank is nA​=cB​(Vsample​−Vblank​), assuming one-to-one neutralization and equal background consumption. A blank cannot correct an unmatched chemical reaction or guarantee exact coincidence of endpoint and equivalence point. Subtracting pH readings would not give this inventory correction, because pH is a logarithm. Repeatability constrains scatter; an independently known check sample also tests systematic bias. Carbon dioxide uptake, incomplete mixing, electrode drift, and uncertain titrant concentration limit the inference.
Titrant standardization
Words: 1k Articles: 6
Acid-base titration OurBigBook.com
Titrant standardization determines the actual titrant concentration by reaction with a known inventory, often a primary standard. It is distinct from calibrating the delivered volume or a glass electrode's response. For a known monoprotic acid inventory ns​ and a suitable blank-corrected endpoint,
cB​=Vend​−Vblank​ns​​.
(15)
An original illustrative standard of 0.980mmol requiring 9.85mL, with matched blank 0.05mL, gives cB​=0.100molL−1. The measured quantities here are stipulated examples. Independent solution preparation and volumetric calibration tests the pipette, burette, and volumetric flask; titrant standardization tests the reacting inventory per delivered volume.
The useful chain is known mass, known composition, known amount of substance, and then a measured titre through a specified stoichiometry. If each standard unit consumes r units of titrant, the general inventory equation is cB​V=rns​, where V is the corrected titre. The one-to-one formula above has r=1. A titration endpoint supplies an observable stopping rule; whether it represents the equivalence point accurately remains a separate question. Standardization endpoint and blank bias makes the direction of the resulting error explicit.
Primary standard
Words: 841 Articles: 2
Titrant standardization OurBigBook.com
A primary standard supplies a reliably known reacting inventory from which a titrant concentration can be determined.
The defining advantage is known high purity: the weighed portion can be assigned a known amount of substance. A titrant whose concentration is established against that inventory is a secondary standard solution in IUPAC terminology.[ref]
An accurate balance determines total mass, including any unwanted water or other material. To infer reacting inventory, one also needs known composition, stability during storage and weighing, complete transfer and dissolution, and a sufficiently complete chemical reaction of known stoichiometry. A finely resolved balance cannot establish these chemical facts. Potassium hydrogen phthalate is useful because a suitably prepared portion provides a known acid inventory for sodium hydroxide titrant standardization; the KHP standardization protocol below shows how that claim is put into practice.
Preparation belongs to the particular material and method. For example, the MIT handout specifies drying potassium hydrogen phthalate at 110∘C for 1.5 hours or overnight, cooling in a desiccator, and weighing by difference; its footnote identifies removal of superficial moisture as the purpose. This is a condition of that potassium hydrogen phthalate procedure, not a universal instruction to heat every primary standard in the same way.[ref]
Primary standard inventory (ns​=mp/M)
Words: 341
Primary standard OurBigBook.com
Let m be the transferred sample mass, p the known mass fraction of the intended chemical compound, and M its molar mass. In a model with complete dissolution and chemically inactive impurities,
ns​=Mmp​,cB​=MVrmp​.
(16)
The purity fraction is dimensionless: 99.95% would mean p=0.9995, not 99.95. These equations assume that the impurity contributes no titrant demand; an impurity that also reacts needs its own inventory equation. The value of p must refer to the same preparation state as m, for example the specified dry material. A mass fraction assigned before unaccounted uptake of water cannot simply be used after it.
For an original worked example, stipulate pure potassium hydrogen phthalate, abbreviated KHP, so p=1, a selected mass m=0.51055g, and rounded molar mass M=204.22gmol−1. The NIST Chemistry WebBook lists formula C8​H5​KO4​ and molecular weight 204.2212, consistent with the rounded molar mass used here.[ref] The selected mass, purity assumption and volumes in this example are chosen inputs, not measured results or a claim about a real batch's purity.
The acid-bearing anion of potassium hydrogen phthalate loses one proton to hydroxide. The net chemical equation is
HC8​H4​O4−​+OH−⟶C8​H4​O42−​+H2​O.
(17)
Thus sodium hydroxide has one-to-one stoichiometry with potassium hydrogen phthalate in this neutralization, giving
ns​=204.22gmol−10.51055g​=0.002500mol.
(18)
Choose a delivered endpoint titre of 24.85mL and matched titration blank of 0.05mL. Then

Acid dissociation constant (Ka​)

Words: 260 Articles: 1
Acid (chemistry) OurBigBook.com
The acid dissociation constant quantifies the chemical equilibrium for loss of one proton from a chemical acid. In water, write the actual proton transfer as HA+H2​O⇌H3​O++A−. Its full dimensionless equilibrium constant is
Krxn​=aHA​aH2​O​aH3​O+​aA−​​.
(21)
The usual aqueous acid dissociation constant uses the solvent convention with water thermodynamic activity approximated by one:
Ka​=aHA​aH+​aA−​​,pKa​=−log10​Ka​.
(22)
Here hydrated hydrogen ions are understood; H+ is shorthand, not an isolated bare proton in the chemical solution. All thermodynamic activities and Ka​ are dimensionless quantities, using consistent standard states. If water thermodynamic activity differs appreciably from one, retain it in the full equilibrium constant instead of silently mixing conventions.
With activity coefficients γi​ and reference concentration c∘=1molL−1, put ai​=γi​[i]/c∘ for each solute. Then
Ka​=γHA​γH+​γA−​​c∘[HA][H+][A−]​≈c∘[HA][H+][A−]​.
(23)
The last expression is the dilute ideal concentration approximation, not the definition in a nonideal chemical solution. Omitting c∘ instead produces a quotient with units of concentration; its numerical value depends on the chosen units. Do not take a logarithm of that dimensional quotient. Conditional concentration quotients can change with ionic strength even at fixed temperature, whereas the thermodynamic acid dissociation constant retains the activity coefficient corrections. The acid dissociation constant entry distinguishes these conventions.
At the same temperature, solvent and standard states, a larger acid dissociation constant, equivalently a smaller pKa, indicates a stronger tendency to donate a proton. It does not specify an analytical concentration, a pH, or a reaction rate. Composition and the weak acid mass and charge balances are needed to predict pH; chemical kinetics addresses how quickly the chemical equilibrium is approached.

(27)
The thermodynamic activity is a dimensionless quantity, so the logarithm has a dimensionless argument. The definition uses thermodynamic activity, rather than total chemical acid concentration. aH+​≈c∘[H+]​,c∘=1molL−1,pH≈−log10​(1molL−1[H+]​).

Buffer solution

Words: 2k Articles: 8
Acid-base chemistry OurBigBook.com
A buffer solution contains appreciable quantities of a weak acid and its conjugate base, and resists changes in pH caused by small quantities of added chemical acid or chemical base. The conjugate base accepts added protons; the weak acid supplies protons when a chemical base consumes them. Consuming most of either partner undermines the buffering action. See Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, buffers.
For a separate toy calculation, stipulate pKa 5 for a weak acid HA; this is an input, not measured material data. Initially 1L of buffer solution contains 0.10mol of HA and 0.10mol of its conjugate base A−. In the ideal concentration approximation, the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation gives initial pH 5. Add 0.010mol of hydroxide with negligible volume change. The dominant neutralization has one-to-one stoichiometry:
HA+OH−⟶A−+H2​O.
(31)
Each mole of consumed hydroxide removes one mole of weak acid and forms one mole of conjugate base and water. Thus the inventories after neutralization are
nHA​≈(0.10−0.01)mol=0.09mol,nA−​≈(0.10+0.01)mol=0.11mol.
(32)
The shared volume cancels from the concentration ratio. The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation therefore predicts
pH≈5+log10​(0.090.11​)=5+log10​(911​)=5.0871501757….
(33)
This small increase illustrates how conversion between abundant partners absorbs added chemical base. The calculation neglects the tiny free hydronium and hydroxide inventories, changes during re-establishment of chemical equilibrium, self-ionization of water, and activity coefficient corrections. The displayed inventories approximate the final chemical species distribution; they are not an exact solution of all chemical equilibrium conditions.
A buffer solution has a finite buffer capacity: its weak acid inventory supplies protection against added chemical base, and its conjugate base inventory supplies protection against added chemical acid. Here an addition approaching 0.10mol of hydroxide uses up the available HA in the dominant stoichiometry calculation. Close to that limit, the neglected chemical equilibrium contributions matter; once HA is exhausted, inserting a zero denominator into the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation cannot predict the actual pH. Excess hydroxide and the remaining chemical species require a fresh chemical equilibrium calculation. This is why a buffer solution stabilizes conditions for chemical reactions over a finite range of additions. See Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, buffer capacity and added base.

pH=pKa​+log10​(aHA​aA−​​).
(34)

pH≈pKa​+log10​([HA][A−]​).
(35)
The concentrations are at chemical equilibrium; using initial values requires negligible equilibration changes. Below, also neglect self-ionization of water and competing chemical reactions.

pH≈4.76+log10​2=5.061029995…≈5.06103.

Henderson-Hasselbalch equation (pH≈pKa​+log10​([A−]/[HA]))

Words: 399
Buffer solution OurBigBook.com
The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation estimates buffer solution pH using a weak acid's acid dissociation constant and the conjugate base to weak acid concentration ratio.
Its activity form is an exact rearrangement of the chosen chemical equilibrium relation. Write the acid dissociation step as HA⇌H++A−, with hydrated hydrogen ions understood in water and the solvent convention incorporated into the acid dissociation constant. For consistent standard states,
Ka​=aHA​aH+​aA−​​,pKa​=−log10​Ka​,pH=pKa​+log10​(aHA​aA−​​).
(37)
Here each ai​ is a dimensionless thermodynamic activity at chemical equilibrium. Exactness refers to this specified acid dissociation constant relation and the chosen single-ion thermodynamic activity convention, not to identifying the ratio with quantities initially mixed. If water is instead written explicitly as a reactant, its thermodynamic activity must be treated consistently. See Hippler and Metcalfe, Using activities to correct the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation (2020), equations 3–4.
For a toy weak acid with stipulated pKa 5, use the ideal dilute concentration approximation and put r=[A−]/[HA]. The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation becomes pH≈5+log10​r:
r=0.1 ⇒ pH≈4,r=1 ⇒ pH≈5,r=10 ⇒ pH≈6.
(38)
More conjugate base relative to weak acid means higher pH. Each tenfold increase in the ratio contributes exactly one to the calculated pH. The stipulated pKa is not a tabulated value for an identified substance.
The distinction between thermodynamic activity and concentration is explicit when ai​=γi​[i]/c∘, where γi​ is an activity coefficient and c∘ is the reference concentration:
pH=pKa​+log10​([HA][A−]​)+log10​(γHA​γA−​​).
(39)
Neglecting the final term gives the usual approximate Henderson-Hasselbalch equation. It is justified when the ratio of activity coefficients is sufficiently close to one, as in a suitable dilute ideal approximation. Ionic strength, including contributions from other dissolved ions, affects activity coefficients; a nominally dilute weak acid alone does not establish that all corrections are small. The ratio must use chemical equilibrium concentrations. Replacing these by prepared amounts or post-neutralization inventories is a further approximation, suitable only when subsequent redistribution is small. See Hippler and Metcalfe, concentration, activity, and analytical-concentration approximations.
The short derivation is to solve the acid dissociation constant relation for aH+​, take a negative base-ten logarithm, and use the definitions of pH and pKa. Its useful beauty is that a chemical equilibrium becomes a simple rule about a ratio: select a weak acid with pKa near the desired pH, then adjust the partner ratio. Scaling both partner concentrations equally leaves the ideal pH prediction unchanged, while changing buffer capacity. In practical buffer solutions, temperature, ionic strength, and the available inventories still matter.

β=dpHdb​=V1​dpHdnB​​=−V1​dpHdnA​​.

The readout pairs each added amount of substance with the measured pH. With negligible added volume, neighboring readings give the finite difference estimate

assigned to the interval's midpoint. High buffer capacity appears as a small change of pH for a measured reagent dose. Choose increments small enough to resolve changing buffer capacity, but large enough that the change of pH exceeds measurement noise. Repeat with a fresh portion and different increment sizes; remeasure a standard afterward to check drift. A water blank can reveal contamination, but its pH must not be subtracted from the sample pH. These controls are proposed tests of the measurement and model assumptions.

Limitations include incomplete mixing, slow glass electrode response, uncertain reagent concentration, and changes in temperature or ionic strength. Dissolved atmospheric carbon dioxide introduces an additional chemical acid system; comparable exposure and freshly prepared samples help distinguish it from the intended monoprotic acid response. Additional reacting chemical species and nonideal activity coefficients invalidate the simple formula. Especially when pH increments are tiny, measurement noise can dominate the inferred derivative; reporting the dose, pH interval, volume convention, and repeatability is part of reporting a meaningful buffer capacity.
