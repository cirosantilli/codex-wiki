Divide-and-conquer product ledger

 Home Computer science Algorithm Divide-and-conquer algorithm
OurBigBook.com Words: 453
Splitting two integers in positional notation gives x=aq+b and y=cq+d, where q=Bm shifts a block by m numeral digits in fixed radix B≥2. The distributive property gives
xy=acq2+(ad+bc)q+bd.
(32)
Computing ac,ad,bc,bd separately needs four block multiplications. Merely halving the operands therefore gives an ideal recurrence relation F(n)=4F(n/2)+O(n), still with quadratic time complexity. The Karatsuba three-product identity instead computes ac, bd and (a+b)(c+d), recovering the middle coefficient by two subtractions. The MIT notes present precisely this four-product versus three-product distinction.[ref]
For an original small example, multiply 47 by 63. Set B=q=10, a=4, b=7, c=6, d=3. Four separate block products would give ac=24, ad=12, bc=42, bd=21. The three-product calculation is
uh47⋅63​=ac=24,=w−u−v=99−24−21=54,=100u+10h+v=2400+540+21=2961.​v=bd=21,w=(a+b)(c+d)=11⋅9=99,
(33)
The crucial reuse is that u and v supply the outer coefficients and also remove the unwanted terms from w. There is no need to compute ad and bc separately. Expanding w=ac+ad+bc+bd proves the identity. Induction extends it to Karatsuba multiplication once each smaller block product is correct and Karatsuba recursion termination supplies decreasing sizes and complete base cases.
The operation model matters. Let n count numeral digits in fixed radix B, and count single-digit multiplication, addition, subtraction and copying as constant-cost operations. The nonrecursive work on size-n operands is O(n): it includes forming sums, positional shifts and Karatsuba carry handling. Treating an arbitrarily large integer multiplication as one operation would hide the very saving being studied. The coefficients 24,54,21 above must be shifted and added; concatenating them does not give 2961.
In the ideal equal-half model, Karatsuba multiplication has K(n)=3K(n/2)+O(n). At depth j its additional work is at most a constant times n(3/2)j, and its leaf count is 3k=nlog2​3 for n=2k. A geometric series gives K(n)=O(nlog2​3), where log2​3≈1.585. Four recursive products would instead give 4k=n2 leaves. Saving one block product repeatedly changes the exponent, even though the combination requires extra addition and subtraction.
For the literal sum-based version, each operand sum can occupy one extra numeral digit. Its worst-case upper recurrence relation is K(n)≤3K(⌈n/2⌉+1)+Cn for a nondecreasing cost envelope, above a fixed base case threshold of at least three digits. Karatsuba digit-operation cost proves that this extra digit preserves the exponent; Karatsuba operand splitting handles odd lengths and leading zero padding. For signed integers, apply the nonnegative procedure to their absolute values and restore the sign. The small ledger establishes an exact identity, while Karatsuba practical crossover explains why it alone cannot predict a speedup for two-digit operands.
