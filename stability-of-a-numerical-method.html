Stability of a numerical method

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Numerical analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 163
For a numerical evolution, numerical stability means that perturbations remain controlled over a fixed finite interval by a bound independent of sufficiently small discretization parameters. With perturbations δn​ to starting data and added step defects dn​, a typical estimate is
maxnh≤T​∥δn​∥≤CT​(∥δ0​∥+∑nh<T​∥dn​∥),
(107)
where CT​ is independent of h. For a linear homogeneous recurrence, a uniform bound on powers of its update matrix supplies this control. von Neumann stability analysis and the energy method provide complementary tools.
Finite-interval numerical stability differs from absolute stability at a fixed step size. Applying the Euler method to y′=−2y multiplies a perturbation by 1−2h each step. At h=0.2 this is 0.6, so perturbations shrink; at h=1.2 it is −1.4, so they alternate and grow. Nevertheless the Euler method converges as h→0 for this problem: failure at a coarse step does not contradict the convergence theorem. Absolute stability asks whether a selected step preserves boundedness of the Dahlquist test equation over arbitrarily many steps. MIT's analysis uses strict decay, ∣1+hλ∣<1, rather than the boundary-inclusive boundedness convention.[ref]
