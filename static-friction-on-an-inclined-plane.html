Static friction on an inclined plane (tanθc​=μs​)

 ... Physics Branches of physics Classical mechanics Force Static friction Coefficient of static friction
OurBigBook.com Words: 695 Articles: 1
An inclined plane tilted slowly from horizontal turns a coefficient of static friction into a critical angle. Assume a nonrotating block on a stationary, rigid, dry surface, with gravitational weight and the contact forces as its only forces. Take 0≤θ<π/2 to be the angle between the uphill surface direction and horizontal. Let et​ be a unit vector pointing uphill and en​ a perpendicular unit vector pointing outward from the surface. Resolving the gravitational weight gives
W=−mgsinθet​−mgcosθen​.
(6)
The angle between the downward vertical and the inward normal −en​ is also θ. Thus the inward component uses cosine and the downhill component uses sine. These are components of one force, not two additional forces.
Before downhill contact slip, zero acceleration and Newton's second law require
fs​=mgsinθ,N=mgcosθ.
(7)
The required static friction points uphill. Substituting into the bound and dividing by the positive mgcosθ gives
mgsinθ≤μs​mgcosθ⟺tanθ≤μs​.
(8)
The tangent function increases on this range of angles, so the last admissible resting angle is
θc​=arctanμs​,tanθc​=μs​.
(9)
The inverse tangent takes a dimensionless quantity to an angle. At equality the ideal model still permits a limiting resting state; increasing θ further excludes it. At a smaller angle, fs​/N=tanθ is the demand ratio, not the coefficient of static friction.[ref]
For the stipulated m=2kg, g=9.8m/s2 and μs​=0.40, the critical angle is approximately 21.8014∘. At θ=15∘, N≃18.932N and fs​≃5.073N, comfortably below the bound μs​N≃7.573N. At θ=25∘, maintaining rest would require fs​≃8.283N, but the bound is only 7.105N: rest is impossible. At θc​, N≃18.198N and fs​≃7.279N. Notice that the limiting tangential force here is smaller than the horizontal 7.84N limit because tilting reduces the normal force.
Cancellation of mass makes this relation useful: the ideal critical angle tests a contact coefficient without needing a calibrated force gauge. Doubling the block's mass doubles both demanded static friction and its bound, leaving θc​ unchanged if μs​ is unchanged. This is a testable model prediction. It need not survive a change in surface condition, a direction-dependent coefficient, appreciable deformation, or vibration.
The derivation also assumes continued contact and that sliding occurs before tipping or rolling without slipping. The resultant contact force must act within the supporting footprint so that the block can satisfy torque balance; a tall block may tip first. No extra tension, imposed tangential pull, or lift is included. In a slowly raised apparatus, quasistatic process means that motion of the board contributes negligible inertial forces while locating the threshold. Once downhill contact slip begins, a constant-velocity sliding angle would concern kinetic friction, not the onset of static friction.
