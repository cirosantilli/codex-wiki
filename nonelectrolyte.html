Nonelectrolyte

 Home Chemistry Electrochemistry Electrolyte
OurBigBook.com Words: 134
A nonelectrolyte supplies neutral molecules rather than ions on dissolving in the specified solvent, as illustrated by sucrose in water.
For example, dissolving an idealized 1mmol sucrose inventory gives 1mmol dissolved sucrose molecules, rather than separate cations and anions. Dissolving the same amount of substance of a fully dissociated one-to-one strong electrolyte instead gives 1mmol of each ion. Both can disappear visibly into the solvent; the resulting chemical species distinguish them.[ref]
A nonelectrolyte is not necessarily insoluble: sucrose provides a soluble counterexample. Nor does the label assert exactly zero measured electrical conductivity. Self-ionization of water, impurities or residual electrolyte from a probe can supply background ions. Comparing with a solvent blank and rinsing between samples, as in the strong electrolyte experiment, tests whether the dissolved substance itself produces appreciable additional mobile ions. This separates visible dissolution from ion production without treating lamp darkness as an absolute absence of electric charge.
