Huber location estimator (θk,s​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Statistical inference Robust statistics
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 5
The Huber location estimator solves ∑i​ψk​(xi​−θ)=0, where ψk​ clips its argument to [−k,k].
More generally, fix a known scale s>0 and a dimensionless threshold k>0 before fitting. For observations x1​,…,xn​, a Huber location estimator is a minimizer of the summed Huber loss, equivalently a root of its estimating equation:
Qk,s​(θ)=∑i=1n​ρk​(sxi​−θ​),θk,s​∈argminθ∈R​Qk,s​(θ),∑i=1n​ψk​(sxi​−θk,s​​)=0.
(20)
Here ρk​ is the Huber loss and ψk​ the Huber score. The physical clipping distance is ks, in the observations' units; the initial formula uses s=1. A large observation keeps contributing a bounded signed score rather than being discarded. The original clipped location criterion appears in Peter J. Huber's 1964 paper.[ref]
For the original data (0,1,2,3,4,40), take s=1 and k=2. At θ=2.5, the standardized differences and their Huber scores are
(xi​−2.5)i=16​=(−2.5,−1.5,−0.5,0.5,1.5,37.5),(ψ2​(xi​−2.5))i=16​=(−2,−1.5,−0.5,0.5,1.5,2).
(21)
Their sum is zero, so the Huber location estimator is 2.5. Four scores remain in the central linear region, making the root unique. By comparison, the sample mean is 50/6≃8.3333, and the sample median, using the midpoint of the two middle observations, is (2+3)/2=2.5. Agreement with the sample median is a feature of these data, rather than an identity between the two estimators.
The geometry explains both existence and possible nonuniqueness. The Huber loss is a convex function, so Qk,s​ is a convex function of θ and its derivative is −s−1∑i​ψk​((xi​−θ)/s). For n≥1, Qk,s​(θ) tends to infinity as ∣θ∣ grows, hence attains a minimum; its zero derivative characterizes every minimizer. Between clipping transitions, the summed score has slope −m/s, where m is the number of observations strictly within ks of θ. A root with m>0 is unique. If m=0, a flat interval is possible: for data (0,10) with s=1,k=2, every θ∈[2,8] is a minimizer. A complete single-valued estimator then needs a selection rule, such as the interval's midpoint.
A bounded Huber score limits one observation's contribution to the estimating equation. The influence function of a Huber location estimator additionally divides by the population's central mass, so the size of its bound depends on the reference probability distribution. Clipping does not by itself make the bias of an estimator zero or prove a universal breakdown point; the separate local influence versus replacement breakdown argument specifies what fixed scale permits. Estimating s from the same data changes the procedure and requires separate analysis.
