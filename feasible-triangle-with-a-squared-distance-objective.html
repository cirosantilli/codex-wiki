Feasible triangle with a squared-distance objective (F={(x,y):x≥0, y≥0, x+y≤3})

OurBigBook.com Words: 273
Take the decision domain D=R2 and minimize the objective function
f(x,y)=(x−2)2+(y−2)2subject to x≥0,y≥0,x+y≤3.
(25)
Each inequality permits a closed half-space; their intersection is the filled triangle with vertices (0,0), (3,0) and (0,3), including its edges. The feasible region is a compact set and a convex set. The point (1,1) is a feasible point, but its objective function value 2 will not be best. Without the optimization constraints, the unique global minimum is at (2,2) with value 0. That point is infeasible because 2+2>3.
The objective function is the squared Euclidean distance from (2,2). Its positive level sets are circles centered there. Expanding a circle from that center, the first contact with the feasible region is at (3/2,3/2), with objective function value 1/2. Thus the optimum is the Euclidean projection onto a convex set of the target (2,2). Squaring a nonnegative Euclidean distance preserves which point is nearest.[ref]
Here is a direct certificate, without relying on the picture. Put s=x+y and d=x−y. The objective function becomes
f(x,y)=2(s−4)2+d2​≥21​(0≤s≤3).
(26)
Equality requires s=3 and d=0, so the unique optimum is (3/2,3/2). At this point x+y≤3 is an active constraint, while x≥0 and y≥0 are strict. An active constraint identifies a tight restriction at a specified point; it alone does not establish optimality. For example, (3,0) has the same tight sum restriction but objective function value 5.
For a check using the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions, write the inequalities as −x≤0, −y≤0, g=x+y−3≤0. Their Lagrange multipliers at the optimum are (0,0,1). The gradient equation is
∇f(3/2,3/2)+1∇g=(−1,−1)+(1,1)=(0,0).
(27)
Feasibility, nonnegative Lagrange multipliers and complementary slackness also hold. With this strictly convex function and affine optimization constraints, the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions certify the unique global minimum.[ref]
