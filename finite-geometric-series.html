Finite geometric series

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Sequence and series Geometric series
OurBigBook.com Words: 333 Articles: 3
A finite geometric series is a sum of consecutive terms of a geometric progression. With first term a and N terms, including N=0 for the empty sum,
SN​=j=0∑N−1​arj={a(1−rN)/(1−r),Na,​r=1,r=1.​​
(94)
Unlike an infinite geometric series, this identity does not need ∣r∣<1. For example, 3+6+12+24+48=3(25−1)=93. The number of terms is five; the largest exponent is four.
The cancellation behind the formula is especially useful to remember: multiplying SN​ by the common ratio shifts all terms one place. In SN​−rSN​, every interior term cancels, leaving a−arN. Division by 1−r gives the identity when r=1; at r=1 the sum is directly Na. The same argument works for complex numbers.
For integers m≤n and r=1, the shifted version is
∑j=mn​rj=rm1−r1−rn−m+1​.
(95)
There are n−m+1 terms. All powers must be defined; if a negative exponent is present, r must be nonzero. At r=1 this sum is simply n−m+1. The finite trigonometric sum uses this identity with a complex exponential as the common ratio.
Finite sums of positive integers in a geometric progression already appear in Euclid's Elements, Book IX, Proposition 35. The argument there compares differences of proportional numbers; Joyce's guide rewrites its conclusion as the modern finite-sum formula. This historical result concerns finite sums, without asserting an infinite limit. [ref]
