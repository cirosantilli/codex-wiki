Kepler's second law (dA/dt=h/2)

 Home Physics Mathematical physics Kepler's laws of planetary motion
OurBigBook.com Words: 308
The relative displacement of a Kepler orbit sweeps equal areas about the attracting focus of a conic in equal times. A short interval near periapsis covers a wide angle at small separation; the same interval near apoapsis covers a narrow angle at large separation. The swept areas are equal even though the travelled distances are different. In particular, the sectors have curved ellipse boundaries: triangles formed by joining their endpoints only approximate the swept areas for short intervals.
Let h=∣r×r˙∣ be the relative specific angular momentum. The areal velocity is
dtdA​=21​∣r×r˙∣=2h​.
(52)
A central force exerts zero torque about its centre, so conservation of angular momentum makes this rate constant. This explanation also applies to other central forces; the ellipse shape specifically comes from Newtonian gravity.
In the a=1, e=0.6, μ=1 example, specific angular momentum is h=μa(1−e2)​=0.64​=0.8, giving areal velocity 0.4. At periapsis and apoapsis, the velocity is perpendicular to the relative displacement, so h=rv. The endpoint speeds are consequently
vp​=0.40.8​=2,va​=1.60.8​=0.5.
(53)
The fourfold speed difference is consistent with the fourfold separation difference. Away from these endpoints, h/r gives only the transverse component of velocity; the full speed follows from the vis-viva equation.
To actually place equal-time markers, use Kepler's equation. Write mean anomaly as M=nt, with t=0 at periapsis and mean motion n=μ/a3​=1. The different symbol M avoids confusing this angle with the mass M. Solve for eccentric anomaly E in radians, then compute the relative position:
M=E−esinE,x=a(cosE−e),y=bsinE.
(54)
This timing procedure is explained in NASA educational archive, How Orbital Motion is Calculated.
For the first interval 0≤t≤π/6, the final eccentric anomaly is E≈1.041495. For the equal interval π≤t≤7π/6 starting at apoapsis, it ends at E≈3.471065. Both swept areas are ΔA=(h/2)Δt=0.4π/6=π/15≈0.209440, one twelfth of the entire ellipse's area 0.8π.
