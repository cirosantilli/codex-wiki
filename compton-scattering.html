Compton scattering (λ′−λ=λC​(1−cosθ))

 Home Physics Mathematical physics Compton wavelength
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 16
Compton scattering redistributes photon energy and momentum in a collision with a charged subatomic particle, here an electron. For a free electron initially at rest in the chosen inertial frame, an incident photon of energy E=hc/λ leaves with energy E′=hc/λ′ after deflection through θ. The angle θ∈[0,π] is between the incident and outgoing photon directions; it is not the electron recoil angle. Four-momentum conservation gives
E′=1+me​c2E​(1−cosθ)E​,λ′−λ=me​ch​(1−cosθ),Te​=E−E′,
(3)
where me​ is the electron rest mass and Te​ its final kinetic energy. The photon energy loss pays for the electron recoil: neither energy nor momentum disappears.
For an original worked example, stipulate E=70keV, the rounded electron rest energy me​c2=511keV, and the rounded Compton wavelength λC​=2.426pm; keV means 103 electronvolts. Treat these rounded inputs as the example's constants. They give the following calculated values, with energies rounded to three decimal places:

Since 0≤1−cosθ≤2, every outgoing photon has 0<E′≤E and Te​≥0. At zero deflection there is no recoil; backscattering transfers the most energy. At fixed θ, the absolute wavelength increase is independent of incident energy, while the fractional energy loss grows with E/(me​c2). This makes Compton scattering useful for inferring energy transfers from directions in photon detectors.
The free-electron-at-rest assumption matters. An electron in a bound state has binding energy and an initial momentum distribution. Neglecting these is useful when the transferred energy is large compared with the relevant binding energy and the initial momentum is small compared with the resolved momentum transfer. Otherwise the outgoing photon energies can broaden or depart from the stationary-target formula; an initially moving electron can even give energy to the photon.
A material may also scatter X-rays through a coherent elastic scattering channel with an effectively unshifted component. When an intact chemical atom of mass M takes the recoil, the corresponding Compton wavelength h/(Mc) is much smaller than the electron value. The single free-electron curve does not predict that component's weight. More generally, energy versus angle is a kinematic constraint; angular wave intensity requires a differential scattering cross-section, together with target and detector information.
