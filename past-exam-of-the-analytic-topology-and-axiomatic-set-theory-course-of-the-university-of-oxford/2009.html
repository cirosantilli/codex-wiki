i OurBigBook.com
Let H be the set of separately continuous real-valued functions on the plane. Take as a subbasis all sets h−1(U), where h∈H and U is open in the usual real line. Unions of finite intersections of these sets form a topology, and every h is continuous for it by construction. Conversely every topology making every h continuous must contain the subbasis, hence this entire generated topology. Thus the required coarsest topology exists and is unique: it is the initial topology of the family H.
The family is a set, being a subset of RR2, so this construction has no proper-class issue. The coordinate projections belong to H; consequently this topology contains the ordinary Euclidean topology. This last observation will also make convergence in the new topology imply ordinary coordinatewise convergence.

Fix a horizontal line L=R×{c}. The restriction of every member of H to L is continuous in its usual topology. Therefore every subbasic open set intersects L in a usual open set, so the induced topology is no finer than the usual one. Conversely the first coordinate projection is continuous for the initial topology, and its inverse images induce every usual open set on L. The two topologies on L are equal. The second projection gives the identical argument for a vertical line.
Write F=YX. For every nonzero rational r, the intersection of Y with {r}×R is dense in that vertical line, since the nonzero rational numbers are dense in the real line. Hence {r}×R⊆F. For each real c, it follows that F∩(R×{c}) contains (Q∖{0})×{c} and is closed in that line. This dense subset forces the whole horizontal line into F. Thus F=X: Y is a countable dense subset. The two successive uses of line density are necessary; the initial topology is finer than the Euclidean topology, so Euclidean density alone would not suffice.

For a fixed nonzero second coordinate c, the rational expression defining h(x,c) is continuous on the half-line where xc>0. On the opposite half-line it is constant. At x=0 the ratio tends to 1, agreeing with the value on the other side. If c=0 the entire restriction is constant. By symmetry the restrictions with the first coordinate fixed are continuous too. Hence h is separately continuous, and so is ht​(x,y)=h(x−t,y−t) for every real t.
On the diagonal D, ht​(t,t)=1, while ht​(s,s)=0 for s=t. Therefore
D∩ht−1​((1/2,3/2))={(t,t)}.
(1)
Each diagonal singleton is open in the induced topology; arbitrary unions show that D has the discrete topology.
If X were metrizable, its countable dense subset from the preceding part would make it second countable. Indeed balls with rational radii about dense points contain a countable basis after the usual smaller-ball argument. Every subspace of a second-countable space is second countable. A discrete second-countable space is countable, since each singleton must itself occur as a basis member. But D is uncountable. This contradiction proves X is not metrizable.

Suppose for contradiction that pn​=(un​,vn​)∈Y converges to the origin in the initial topology. The coordinate projections then give un​→0 and vn​→0 in the usual real line. Rectangular bumps detecting nonconvergence, using anisotropic supports, avoids any unsupported regularity assertion about a radial maximum. Set
ϕ(t)=max(1−∣t∣,0),rn​=∣un​∣/4,sn​=∣vn​∣/4,
(2)
bn​(x,y)=ϕ(rn​x−un​​)ϕ(sn​y−vn​​),B(x,y)=supn​bn​(x,y).
(3)
The radii are positive since neither coordinate of pn​ vanishes. Each rectangular support misses both coordinate axes, so B vanishes on those axes. For a fixed y=c=0, all but finitely many vertical support intervals miss c, because their centres and radii tend to zero. Urysohn's lemma states that if X is normal and A,B are disjoint closed subsets, there is a continuous function u:X→[0,1] with u∣A​=0 and u∣B​=1. The values on the rest of X remain in that interval. Interchanging A,B or composing with an affine function gives any two prescribed real endpoint values. This is functional separation of closed sets, stronger than merely choosing disjoint open neighbourhoods.

A C-embedded subset Y⊆X is one when every continuous real-valued function on Y extends continuously to X. It is a Cb-embedded subset when this is required for every bounded continuous real-valued function, with a bounded extension. These definitions concern the subspace topology on Y.
Here is the bounded Tietze extension construction from Urysohn's lemma. Let g:Y→R be continuous with ∣g∣≤M. The case M=0 has the zero extension. For M>0, the sets A={g≤−M/3} and B={g≥M/3} are disjoint and closed in X, since Y is closed. Urysohn's lemma gives a continuous u0​:X→[−M/3,M/3] taking the endpoint values on A,B. On Y,
∣g−u0​∣≤2M/3.
(4)
Indeed on A or B this follows from the prescribed endpoint; on their complement both ∣g∣ and ∣u0​∣ are at most M/3.
Repeat on the residual. With Mn​=M(2/3)n, choose continuous un​ satisfying ∥un​∥∞​≤Mn​/3 and reducing the residual on Y to at most Mn+1​. The series F=∑n≥0​un​ converges uniformly by the geometric majorant. Its sum is continuous, has ∣F∣≤∑Mn​/3=M, and restricts to g, since the residual tends uniformly to zero. Thus every bounded continuous function extends, with its bound preserved, proving Cb-embeddedness without assuming the extension theorem being proved.

A filter base on a set X is a nonempty family F of nonempty subsets such that for every F,G∈F there is H∈F with H⊆F∩G. Repeated application gives a member contained in any specified finite intersection. In particular every finite intersection is nonempty, so the family has the finite intersection property. Its upward closure {A⊆X:∃F∈F,F⊆A} is the corresponding proper filter.

For a connected intersection of a closed filter base, the filter-base property implies the finite intersection property. Compactness, in its closed-set formulation, therefore gives K=⋂F=∅. It is closed in X, hence compact.
Suppose additionally that each base member is connected but K is disconnected. Write K=A∪B with A,B disjoint nonempty closed subsets of K. They are compact, hence closed in the Hausdorff space X. Normality of a compact Hausdorff space gives disjoint open sets U,V containing A,B, respectively. The compact set W=X∖(U∪V) misses K. For every w∈W some Fw​∈F misses w, so the open sets X∖Fw​ cover W. A finite subcover gives F1​∩⋯∩Fm​⊆U∪V. Choose F0​∈F inside this finite intersection. If W is empty, choose any base member instead.
Since F0​ contains K, it meets both U and V; but it lies in their disjoint union. These two relatively open nonempty pieces disconnect F0​, a contradiction. Thus the intersection is nonempty and connected. Compactness is used to pass from an infinite exclusion condition to a single base member.

A Hausdorff compactification of X is an embedding h:X→Y into a compact Hausdorff space with dense image. The complement Y∖h[X] is the remainder; a one-point or two-point compactification has a remainder of that cardinality.
For one point, take the unit circle and
h1​(x)=(cos(2arctanx),sin(2arctanx)).
(7)
The angle runs over (−π,π), so this is a homeomorphism onto the circle with (−1,0) removed. That image is dense, and the circle is compact Hausdorff. For two points, take Y=[−π/2,π/2] and h2​(x)=arctanx. This is a homeomorphism onto its dense open interval, leaving exactly the two endpoints. Therefore both required compactifications exist; in the first the positive and negative ends meet, whereas in the second they remain distinct.

A set T is transitive if every element of an element of T is itself in T, equivalently each element of T is a subset of T. Proceed by transfinite induction. The empty set is transitive. If T is transitive and u∈v∈P(T), then v⊆T gives u∈T, and transitivity of T gives u⊆T. Thus u∈P(T), proving the successor stage transitive. A union of transitive sets is transitive: if u∈v∈⋃i​Ti​, choose i with v∈Ti​ and infer u∈Ti​. This proves the limit step and hence every Vα​ is transitive.

Take a nonzero limit ordinal λ and x∈Vλ​. Choose β<λ with x∈Vβ​. Transitivity gives x⊆Vβ​. Hence every actual subset of x belongs to P(Vβ​)=Vβ+1​, and the actual set P(x) belongs to Vβ+2​. Here the absoluteness fact used is: every bounded formula is absolute between transitive sets or classes with the actual membership relation. To prove replacement failure at V omega plus omega, put M=Vω+ω​. The set ω belongs to M, and for every n∈ω the ordinal ω+n also belongs to M. The relation saying that y is obtained by taking n successive ordinal successors of ω is definable, with parameter ω, and is single valued in M.

König's theorem for cardinal numbers states that, for a set-indexed family with κi​<λi​ at every index,
i∈I∑​κi​<i∈I∏​λi​.​
(14)
Here the sum is the cardinality of a disjoint union and the product is the cardinality of a Cartesian product. Work in ZFC. Choose disjoint sets Ai​ of sizes κi​ and sets Bi​ of sizes λi​. For any function f:⨆i​Ai​→∏i​Bi​, its ith coordinate values on Ai​ form a subset of Bi​ of cardinality at most κi​. Choose bi​∈Bi​ outside that subset, using choice. Then b=(bi​)i​ cannot equal f(a) for any a: if a∈Aj​, its jth coordinate was specifically excluded. Thus there is no surjection from the disjoint union onto the product.
Cardinal comparability under choice now implies the displayed strict inequality. Empty index set gives 0<1 directly; empty individual Ai​ cause no difficulty, since the corresponding coordinate excludes an empty image. This is the cardinal diagonal theorem, rather than the finitely branching tree result also bearing König's name.

Let U1​⊆U2​ be transitive classes, both interpreted with actual membership. A formula ϕ(xˉ) is upward absolute from U1​ to U2​ if for every tuple of parameters aˉ from U1​,
(U1​,∈)⊨ϕ(aˉ)⟹(U2​,∈)⊨ϕ(aˉ).​
(23)
For proper classes this means the usual relativization of the fixed formula's quantifiers. Full absoluteness requires the implication in both directions. The qualification on parameters matters: the smaller class must contain every object named in the comparison.

A sufficient syntactic condition is that ϕ be a sigma-one formula in set theory, written Σ1​: an existential block ∃z1​⋯∃zm​θ with θ bounded, meaning that each of its quantifiers is of the form ∀t∈a or ∃t∈a. A bounded formula is also called Δ0​ and is absolute in both directions between transitive classes.
Prove the latter fact by structural induction. Atomic membership and equality have their actual meanings in both classes, and Boolean connectives preserve equivalence of truth. For a bounded quantifier with bound a∈U1​, transitivity ensures that every element of a belongs to U1​; thus its range is exactly the same in both classes, and induction applies to its matrix. This proves the bounded case. If the smaller class satisfies a Σ1​ formula, take its existential witnesses in U1​. They remain available in U2​, and bounded absoluteness preserves the matrix. Hence Σ1​ formulas are upward absolute. An arbitrary unbounded universal quantifier has no such witness-preservation argument.

A bounded expression for the successor relation is
y∈x ∧ (∀z∈y z∈x) ∧ (∀z∈x (z∈y∨z=y)).​
(26)
The first two clauses put y and all its elements into x, while the last forbids any additional member of x. Extensionality identifies this exactly with x=y∪{y}. Since the formula is Δ0​, it is absolute in both directions between the transitive classes and therefore upward absolute. This bounded description also justifies the successor computations used earlier in the rank-stage Replacement counterexample.

To prove that power-set equality is not upward absolute, use the standard constructible universe theorem: L is a transitive inner model of ZF containing every ordinal, and set rank is absolute between L and V. Since V=L, some rank stage contains a nonconstructible set. Let α be the least ordinal with Vα​⊈L. It is not zero; it cannot be a limit, since a union of earlier stages contained in L is still contained in L. Thus α=β+1 and Vβ​⊆L.
We need not merely this inclusion, but that y=Vβ​ itself belongs to L. Inside L, ZF constructs its own rank stage (Vβ​)L, which is a set of L. By transitivity and rank absoluteness,
(Vβ​)L=Vβ​∩L=Vβ​.
(27)
Consequently y∈L. Let x=(P(y))L, the power set computed in L. Then x∈L and L⊨x=P(y).
By minimality there is z∈Vβ+1​∖L. Externally z⊆Vβ​=y, but z∈/x, because every member of the set x∈L is in the transitive class L. Hence V⊨x=P(y). Taking U1​=L and U2​=V proves power-set equality is not upward absolute, even between transitive models of ZF.
There is no contradiction with absoluteness of z⊆y: that bounded relation agrees whenever both parameters are present in the smaller class. Power-set equality quantifies over all subsets, including a new z absent from the smaller class. The step proving Vβ​∈L is essential; inclusion in a class alone would not imply membership in it.
