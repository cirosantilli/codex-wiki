A T0 space distinguishes any two distinct points by an open set containing one and not the other. A Tychonoff space, also called a completely regular Hausdorff space, is a T1​ space in which a point outside a closed set can be separated from it by a continuous function: after rescaling, f(x)=0 and f∣C​=1 with values in [0,1].
The product topology has the subbasis of a topology
{πλ−1​(U):λ∈Λ, U open in Xλ​}.​
(1)
A map into the product is continuous exactly when each coordinate map is continuous. One direction follows by composition with the continuous product projections; the other follows by taking inverse images of the displayed subbasic sets.
Define the diagonal evaluation map Δ(x)=(fλ​(x))λ∈Λ​. Its coordinates are continuous, so the map is continuous by continuity into a product space. Point separation makes it injective. For a basic open set B=fλ−1​(U),
Δ(B)=Δ(X)∩πλ−1​(U).
(2)
Every open subset of X is a union of such B, so its image is open in Δ(X). The inverse on the image is continuous, and Δ is a topological embedding​. If X is T0​, any two distinct points are distinguished by an open set and then by one basic neighborhood within it. Equality of all coordinates would prevent that distinction. Thus the basis hypothesis alone implies point separation in a T0​ space. The unsubscripted f in the printed separation hypothesis is understood as the chosen coordinate fλ​.
For the Sierpiński space S={0,1} with opens ∅,{1},S, each open U⊆X gives a continuous characteristic map χU​:X→S with χU−1​({1})=U. These inverse images form the whole topology, hence a basis. In a T0​ space they separate points, proving the embedding into Sτ.
For a Tychonoff space, use all continuous maps X→[0,1]. Given x∈U with U open, complete regularity supplies f(x)=0 and f=1 on X∖U. Then f−1([0,1/2)) is an open neighborhood of x contained in U. These inverse images form a basis. The diagonal theorem gives an embedding into a product of closed unit intervals.
A three-point space works even without any separation axiom. Take
Y={0,1,2},τY​={∅,{2},Y}.​
(3)
For each open U of an arbitrary space, let gU​=2 on U and 0 elsewhere. It is continuous. For each point a, let ha​=1 at a and 0 elsewhere. It is also continuous, because its image lies in the subspace {0,1} of Y, which has the indiscrete topology; equivalently ha−1​({2})=∅. The gU​ supply every open set as an inverse image, and the ha​ distinguish every pair of points. Using the diagonal evaluation map for their combined family gives a universal three-point space for product embeddings:
X↪Yτ⊔X.​
(4)
Finally embed the given completely regular Hausdorff space into a cube K=[0,1]I. The Tychonoff theorem makes K compact; it is Hausdorff and hence normal. The image of the compact subset C is compact, so closed in K. The Tietze extension theorem says that a continuous bounded real-valued function on a closed subset of a normal space extends continuously to the whole space. The given function on C is bounded by compactness, so apply this theorem to its transported function on the image of C in K. Restrict the extension to the image of X and pull it back along the embedding. This yields F:X→R continuous, F∣C​=f​. This proves that compact subsets of Tychonoff spaces are C-embedded, using normality of the compact cube.

For y∈Y, every neighborhood of y in the subspace is of the form V∩Y with V an ambient open neighborhood. Since A⊆Y, this meets A exactly when V meets A. Thus
AY=Y∩AX.​
(5)
A Hausdorff compactification is a topological embedding h:X→K with dense image in a compact Hausdorff space K. Define the domination preorder by
(h,K)≤(h′,K′) ⟺ ∃q:K′→K continuous with q∘h′=h.​
(6)
It is reflexive by the identity map and transitive by composition. Any comparison map is onto: its image is compact and hence closed in K, and contains the dense set h(X). Two compactifications are equivalent if a homeomorphism u:K→K′ satisfies u∘h=h′.
Such a homeomorphism and its inverse give domination both ways. Conversely, if comparison maps exist in both directions, each composite agrees with the identity on the dense embedded copy of X. Two continuous maps to a Hausdorff space agreeing on a dense set agree everywhere. The comparison maps are therefore inverse homeomorphisms. Thus equivalence is exactly mutual domination.
The Stone-Čech extension property says that every continuous map from X into any compact Hausdorff space extends continuously to the compactification. The extension is unique by density. For two compactifications with this property, extend each embedding through the other compactification. This gives comparison maps both ways, hence equivalence by the preceding argument.
For the perfect-map characterization by Stone-Čech remainders, the universal property first gives a unique continuous F:βX→βY extending the composite Xf​Y↪βY. The required remainder condition is equivalent to
F−1(Y)=X.​
(7)
One inclusion is already forced by the extension property; the other forbids any point of the remainder mapping into Y.
Suppose first that f is a perfect map. Fix y∈Y and let K=f−1(y), a compact subset of X and hence a closed subset of βX. If p∈F−1(y)∖K, normality of βX supplies an open U with K⊆U and p∈/U. Because f is closed,
V=Y∖f(X∖U)
(8)
is an open neighborhood of y and f−1(V)⊆U∩X. Choose an open V′ of βY with V′∩Y=V. The open set F−1(V′) contains p, and its intersection with the dense subset X lies in U. Every point of that open set is consequently in U, contradicting the choice of p. Therefore F−1(y)=K⊆X for every y∈Y, proving the remainder condition. This argument also handles an empty fiber.
Conversely suppose the remainder condition holds. Every fiber f−1(y)=F−1(y) is a closed subset of βX, hence compact, and lies in X. To show that f is closed, let A be closed in X and set D=AβX. The initial closure identity gives D∩X=A. Compactness makes F(D) closed in βY, and the remainder condition gives
f(A)=F(D)∩Y.
(9)
Indeed any preimage in D of a point of Y must belong to X and hence to A. Thus f(A) is closed in Y. Together with compact fibers this proves
f perfect ⟺ F(βX∖X)⊆βY∖Y.​
(10)

The open sets U and X∖Cλ​ cover X. A finite subcover exists by compactness, and taking complements yields
λ∈Λ0​⋂​Cλ​⊆U​
(11)
for a finite index set. This is the finite intersection characterization of compactness in a neighborhood form; the finite index set may be empty when U=X.
The Šura-Bura lemma states that in a compact Hausdorff space the connected component C(x) of a point equals its quasi-component, the intersection of all clopen neighborhoods of that point. It also follows that each component has a neighborhood basis of clopen sets. Here is a proof of both assertions.
Let Q be the intersection of the clopen neighborhoods of x. Every connected set containing x lies in each such neighborhood, so C(x)⊆Q. The set Q is closed and compact. If Q were disconnected, write it as the disjoint union of nonempty relatively closed sets A,B, choosing x∈A. Both are compact and closed in X. Normality gives disjoint open sets OA​,OB​ containing them. By the finite-intersection result, a finite intersection V of clopen neighborhoods of x satisfies Q⊆V⊆OA​∪OB​. Then V∩OA​ is clopen in V, hence clopen in X, and contains x while excluding B. This contradicts the definition of Q. Thus Q is connected and equals C(x). Given any open neighborhood of the component, the same finite-intersection argument supplies a clopen neighborhood inside it. This proves the neighborhood-basis form as well.
For an explicit noncompact counterexample to the Šura-Bura lemma, put p=(0,0), q=(1,0), and εm​=1/(m+2) for m≥1. Let Am​ be the polygonal arc with successive vertices
(εm​,0),(εm​,m),(1−εm​,m),(1−εm​,0),
(12)
and give Z={p,q}∪⋃m≥1​Am​ its subspace topology from the plane. The arcs are pairwise disjoint: a higher arc has its vertical sides outside the horizontal span of any lower arc. Each Am​ is compact and relatively open in Z, so is clopen. For relative openness, all sufficiently high arcs have vertical sides close to x=0,1 and horizontal sides above a fixed bounded neighborhood of Am​; the remaining finitely many compact disjoint arcs have positive distance from it.
A connected subset containing p cannot meet any of these clopen arcs, so it lies in {p,q} and is just {p}. But any clopen neighborhood of p contains the left endpoints of all sufficiently high arcs. Connectedness of each arc forces the entire arc into that neighborhood, and closedness then forces the limit q of their right endpoints into it. Conversely the clopen set Z∖Am​ excludes every point of Am​. Therefore
CZ​(p)={p},QZ​(p)={p,q}.​
(13)
The compact set containing the endpoints has lost the connection supplied by arcs escaping to arbitrarily large height; compactness of the ambient space is essential to the lemma.
Now let D=⋂n​Cn​ for the decreasing compact connected sets in the compact Hausdorff setting. Every finite intersection is one of the nonempty Cn​, so the finite intersection property gives D=∅. It is closed and hence compact. If it were disconnected, its two nonempty compact pieces would have disjoint open neighborhoods U,V in X. Since D⊆U∪V, the initial finite-intersection result and the decreasing property give CN​⊆U∪V for some N. This set meets both neighborhoods because it contains D, contradicting its connectedness. Thus
D is nonempty, compact, and connected.​
(14)
The argument does not need connectedness of the whole ambient space.
For the requested failure without ambient compactness, add the central ray L={(1/2,t):t≥1} to the previous example and put X=Z∪L. For n≥1 define
Cn​={p,q}∪m≥n⋃​Am​∪{(1/2,t):t≥n}.​
(15)
Each graph formed by the displayed arcs and ray is connected: each arc meets the ray at its top. Adding p,q, which are limit points of the graph, preserves connectedness because the closure of a connected set is connected. Its relative closure in X adds exactly these two points, so Cn​ is closed in X. To check that no omitted point is a further limit, a bounded neighborhood away from the lines x=0,1 meets only finitely many arcs; the deleted initial arcs are disjoint compact sets, and the deleted ray segment lies strictly below the remaining ray. All other planar limit points on x=0,1 are outside X.
The sets are decreasing and nonempty, and X=C1​ is connected and Hausdorff. Every individual arc and every point of finite height on the ray disappears from sufficiently late sets. Consequently
n≥1⋂​Cn​={p,q},​
(16)
a nonempty compact disconnected set. Reindexing by n+1 gives the source's indexing by ω. The graph is unbounded, so the ambient subspace is not compact. This is an explicit decreasing closed connected sets with disconnected compact intersection example.

For a set A, its definable power set consists of the subsets of A definable by a first-order formula over (A,∈) using finitely many parameters from A. The quantifiers range over A; this is generally much smaller than its full power set.
The constructible universe is defined by transfinite recursion:
L0​=∅,Lα+1​=Def(Lα​),Lλ​=α<λ⋃​Lα​(λ a limit ordinal),L=α∈On⋃​Lα​.​
(17)
The last union denotes a definable proper class, not a set-sized union over a set of all ordinals. At a successor stage, only definitions over the previously constructed stage are permitted.

Use transfinite induction. The assertion is immediate at stage zero, and at a limit stage it follows by taking the increasing union of the earlier stages.
Assume Lα​∩On=α. Any ordinal γ∈Lα+1​ is a subset of Lα​, since members of the definable power set are such subsets. Every element of γ is an ordinal, so
γ⊆Lα​∩On=α,
(18)
which implies γ≤α. This gives the upper bound on the ordinals appearing at the successor stage.
For the reverse inclusion, the smaller ordinals are already in Lα​ and hence in Lα+1​. The remaining ordinal α itself is the definable subset Lα​∩On. The fact used here is absoluteness of ordinalhood between a transitive set and the universe: ordinalhood has a bounded definition, and bounded formulas are absolute for transitive sets. Thus the formula asserting that a member is an ordinal, interpreted over Lα​, defines exactly α. Therefore α∈Def(Lα​)=Lα+1​.
This proves
Lα​∩On=αfor every ordinal α.​
(19)
In particular each ordinal α is a member of Lα+1​, so On⊆L​.

The axiom of infinity fails. First prove Ln​=Vn​ for every finite ordinal n. The base stage is empty in both hierarchies. If Ln​=Vn​, that stage is a finite set. Every one of its subsets is definable over it with finitely many parameters, by a disjunction of equalities to the selected elements; the empty subset is defined by a false formula. Hence
Def(Ln​)=P(Vn​)=Vn+1​.
(20)
Induction and the limit-stage definitions give
Lω​=Vω​,​
(21)
the set of all hereditarily finite sets. Every member of this set is finite externally.
If this transitive structure satisfied the axiom of infinity, it would contain an inductive set I: zero belongs to I, and whenever x∈I, its successor x∪{x} belongs to I. These operations are the actual empty set and actual set successor, since they are absolute for this transitive structure and all relevant finite-rank sets lie in it. External induction would then place every finite ordinal in I, contradicting the finiteness of I.
Every individual finite ordinal does belong to Lω​, but the set ω of all finite ordinals does not. The distinction between containing each finite ordinal and containing one inductive set is precisely the issue.

The cardinal sum is the cardinality of a disjoint union, and the cardinal product is the cardinality of the set of choice functions:
α<β∑​κα​=∣{(α,ξ):α<β, ξ<κα​}∣,α<β∏​κα​=∣{f:domf=β, f(α)<κα​}∣.​
(22)
Here the cardinals are represented by initial ordinals. Tagging by α makes the summands disjoint even when their underlying sets overlap. For an empty index set the sum is zero and the product is one, because there is exactly one empty function. If any factor in a nonempty product is zero, that product is empty.

This is König theorem for cardinal numbers. Let D=⨆α<β​κα​ and P=∏α<β​λα​. For any proposed map g:D→P, consider the values of the αth coordinates on the αth summand:
Eα​={g(α,ξ)(α):ξ<κα​}.
(23)
This set has cardinality at most κα​<λα​. Choose yα​∈λα​∖Eα​, using the axiom of choice, and assemble y=(yα​)∈P. For any (α,ξ)∈D, its image disagrees with y at coordinate α. Thus y is outside the image of g, and no such g is surjective.
Every λα​ is nonzero. The product is nonempty by choice; if D is empty, strict inequality is immediate. Otherwise cardinal comparability principle in ZFC says that ∣P∣≤∣D∣ would yield a surjection from D onto the nonempty P, which the diagonal argument excludes. Therefore
α<β∑​κα​<α<β∏​λα​.​
(24)
The diagonal coordinate is tied to the summand containing the argument; this is why a sum on the left pairs naturally with a product on the right.

The membership induction theorem is the following schema, for every formula φ(x) with any fixed set parameters:
[∀x((∀y∈xφ(y))→φ(x))] ⟹ ∀xφ(x).​
(33)
Its hypothesis says the property is progressive under membership. In ZF it follows from the Axiom of foundation: if a counterexample x exists, take the nonempty set of counterexamples in the transitive closure TC({x}), which contains x. A membership-minimal counterexample supplied by the Axiom of foundation has no counterexample among its elements, contradicting progressivity. The transitive closure ensures that all elements relevant to this argument remain in the set being considered.

Compose the two assumed isomorphisms to obtain a membership-preserving and membership-reflecting bijection
f=π2​∘π1−1​:M1​→M2​.
(34)
Transitivity of both targets implies, for every a∈M1​,
f(a)={f(b):b∈a}.
(35)
For one inclusion use preservation of membership. For the other, if c∈f(a), transitivity puts c in M2​; surjectivity gives c=f(b) for b∈M1​, and reflection gives b∈a.
Apply the membership induction theorem to the assertion that every a∈M1​ is fixed. If all members b of a are fixed, the displayed equation gives f(a)=a. Thus f is the identity on M1​. Its range is M2​, so M1​=M2​, and the definition of f gives
π1​=π2​,M1​=M2​.​
(36)
This proves uniqueness of the Mostowski collapse theorem. Existence of the two isomorphisms already supplies the membership structure needed in this argument.

Read epsilon as actual set membership restricted to X, the interpretation in which ambient ZF makes the relation well-founded. The structure's axiom of extensionality assumption means
x,x′∈X,x∩X=x′∩X⟹x=x′.
(37)
By well-founded recursion on membership, define
π(x)={π(y):y∈x∩X}(x∈X).​
(38)
The recursion theorem applies because the Axiom of foundation makes the restricted relation well-founded and each predecessor collection is a set. The Axiom schema of replacement gives the set M=π[X].
To prove injectivity, use membership induction on x for the property that x∈X and π(x)=π(x′) with x′∈X imply x=x′. Suppose this property holds for every y∈x∩X. Equality of the two images means that each y∈x∩X has some y′∈x′∩X with π(y)=π(y′), so the induction hypothesis gives y=y′. Conversely every y′∈x′∩X has the same image as some y∈x∩X, and the same induction hypothesis again makes them equal. Hence x∩X=x′∩X, and the structure's axiom of extensionality gives x=x′.
The map is onto M by definition. The recursion immediately preserves membership, and its injectivity makes membership reflection valid as well. Moreover if z∈π(x) then z=π(y) for a predecessor y∈X, so z∈M. Therefore M is transitive and π is the required membership isomorphism. This proves the existence direction of the Mostowski collapse theorem.
An arbitrary binary relation must also be well-founded. Axiom of extensionality alone is insufficient in that interpretation: the one-element relation with a self-loop is extensional, but any collapse onto a transitive set would produce a set containing itself, contrary to Axiom of foundation. The actual-membership interpretation above supplies precisely the hypothesis otherwise missing; no analogous claim is made for a general ill-founded relation.

The cumulative hierarchy is defined by transfinite recursion:
V0​=∅,Vα+1​=P(Vα​),Vλ​=α<λ⋃​Vα​(λ a limit ordinal).​
(39)
This recursion on ordinals is available without assuming the Axiom of foundation for all sets. The stages are increasing and transitive, by transfinite induction using their definitions.
Work internally in the specified model of ZF without the Axiom of foundation, and assume the membership induction theorem. Apply it to
φ(x)≡∃α∈On (x∈Vα​).
(40)
Suppose every member y of x lies in some stage. Assign to each such y the least ordinal αy​ for which y∈Vαy​​. The Axiom schema of replacement gives a set of these ordinals, and let β be their supremum, taking β=0 for the empty collection. Monotonicity gives x⊆Vβ​, and therefore x∈Vβ+1​.
This proves progressivity of φ, so membership induction gives it for every set of the model. Every stage already consists of sets in that model. Consequently
V∗=α∈On⋃​Vα​.​
(41)
The equality is internal to the given model; its ordinals and power sets are computed there.

Assume a nonempty set a has no membership-minimal element, so for every x∈a there is y∈x∩a. Consider the formula φ(x)≡x∈/a.
It is progressive: if every element of x is outside a but x∈a, the assumed absence of a minimal element supplies y∈x∩a, contradicting that hypothesis. The membership induction theorem therefore implies x∈/a for every set x. This contradicts a=∅.
Thus every nonempty set a contains an element x with x∩a=∅, exactly the Axiom of foundation:
a=∅ ⟹ ∃x∈a (x∩a=∅).​
(42)
Only the induction schema and elementary set reasoning were needed; no prior use of Axiom of foundation occurs in the proof.
