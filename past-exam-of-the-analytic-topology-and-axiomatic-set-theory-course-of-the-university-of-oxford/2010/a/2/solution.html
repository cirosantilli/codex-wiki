A Hausdorff compactification is a topological embedding h:X→K with dense image in a compact Hausdorff space K. Define the domination preorder by
(h,K)≤(h′,K′) ⟺ ∃q:K′→K continuous with q∘h′=h.​
(6)
It is reflexive by the identity map and transitive by composition. Any comparison map is onto: its image is compact and hence closed in K, and contains the dense set h(X). Two compactifications are equivalent if a homeomorphism u:K→K′ satisfies u∘h=h′.
Such a homeomorphism and its inverse give domination both ways. Conversely, if comparison maps exist in both directions, each composite agrees with the identity on the dense embedded copy of X. Two continuous maps to a Hausdorff space agreeing on a dense set agree everywhere. The comparison maps are therefore inverse homeomorphisms. Thus equivalence is exactly mutual domination.
The Stone-Čech extension property says that every continuous map from X into any compact Hausdorff space extends continuously to the compactification. The extension is unique by density. For two compactifications with this property, extend each embedding through the other compactification. This gives comparison maps both ways, hence equivalence by the preceding argument.
For the perfect-map characterization by Stone-Čech remainders, the universal property first gives a unique continuous F:βX→βY extending the composite Xf​Y↪βY. The required remainder condition is equivalent to
F−1(Y)=X.​
(7)
One inclusion is already forced by the extension property; the other forbids any point of the remainder mapping into Y.
Suppose first that f is a perfect map. Fix y∈Y and let K=f−1(y), a compact subset of X and hence a closed subset of βX. If p∈F−1(y)∖K, normality of βX supplies an open U with K⊆U and p∈/U. Because f is closed,
V=Y∖f(X∖U)
(8)
is an open neighborhood of y and f−1(V)⊆U∩X. Choose an open V′ of βY with V′∩Y=V. The open set F−1(V′) contains p, and its intersection with the dense subset X lies in U. Every point of that open set is consequently in U, contradicting the choice of p. Therefore F−1(y)=K⊆X for every y∈Y, proving the remainder condition. This argument also handles an empty fiber.
Conversely suppose the remainder condition holds. Every fiber f−1(y)=F−1(y) is a closed subset of βX, hence compact, and lies in X. To show that f is closed, let A be closed in X and set D=AβX. The initial closure identity gives D∩X=A. Compactness makes F(D) closed in βY, and the remainder condition gives
f(A)=F(D)∩Y.
(9)
Indeed any preimage in D of a point of Y must belong to X and hence to A. Thus f(A) is closed in Y. Together with compact fibers this proves
f perfect ⟺ F(βX∖X)⊆βY∖Y.​
