The dual space is the vector space of all linear functionals on V:
V′=HomF​(V,F).​
(1)
Addition and scalar multiplication are defined pointwise. The annihilator of a vector subspace is
U0={f∈V′:f(u)=0 for every u∈U}.​
(2)
It is a vector subspace of V′: linear combinations of functionals vanishing on U also vanish on U.

Choose a basis u1​,…,um​ of U. Because v∈/U, the list u1​,…,um​,v is linearly independent. Extend it to a basis of V. A linear functional is specified uniquely by its values on a basis, so define f to be zero on the uj​ and on every added basis vector other than v, and set f(v)=1.
Then f vanishes on every linear combination of the uj​, so f∈U0, while
f(v)=1=0.​
(3)
This proves that the annihilator of a vector subspace separates every vector outside the subspace from that subspace; no inner product or preferred coordinates are needed.

The adjoint operator T∗ is characterized by
⟨Tv,w⟩=⟨v,T∗w⟩for all v,w∈V.​
(7)
This identity works with either convention for which argument of the complex inner product is linear, provided the convention is used consistently.
For uniqueness, subtract the two given identities to get ⟨v,(S−T∗)w⟩=0 for every v,w. Fix w and choose v=(S−T∗)w. Then positive definiteness gives
∥(S−T∗)w∥2=0,
(8)
so (S−T∗)w=0. As w was arbitrary, S=T∗​. The decisive fact is that a vector orthogonal to every vector, including itself, must be zero.

First suppose E=E∗. For u∈U=imE, choose v with Ev=u. For w∈W=kerE,
⟨u,w⟩=⟨Ev,w⟩=⟨v,Ew⟩=0.
(9)
Thus U⊆W⊥. The direct sum of vector spaces gives dimU=dimV−dimW, while the orthogonal complement has dimW⊥=dimV−dimW. Equal finite dimensions imply U=W⊥.
Conversely, suppose U=W⊥. Decompose arbitrary vectors as v=u+w and v′=u′+w′, with u,u′∈U and w,w′∈W. Since E is the indicated projection, Ev=u and Ev′=u′. Orthogonality makes the cross terms vanish, so
⟨Ev,v′⟩=⟨u,u′⟩=⟨v,Ev′⟩.
(10)
Therefore E satisfies the defining identity of its own adjoint operator. By uniqueness from part (a), E=E∗. Hence
E=E∗⟺U=W⊥.​
(11)
This is the characterization of an orthogonal projection among projections along a complementary subspace.

A Euclidean domain is an integral domain R admitting a Euclidean function δ:R∖{0}→Z≥0​ such that division with a smaller remainder is possible: for every a∈R and nonzero b∈R, there are q,r∈R with
a=qb+r,r=0 or δ(r)<δ(b).​
(12)
Thus the Euclidean function measures progress in repeated division. Definitions which additionally normalize it to be nondecreasing under multiplication give the same class of Euclidean domains; the division property is the one needed below.

For the zero ideal, choose d=0. Otherwise choose a nonzero d∈I for which the Euclidean function δ(d) is minimal; the well-ordering principle guarantees such a choice. For any a∈I, perform Euclidean division:
a=qd+r,r=0 or δ(r)<δ(d).
(13)
Because I is an ideal, r=a−qd∈I. A nonzero remainder would contradict the minimal choice of d, so r=0 and a∈Rd. Thus I⊆Rd. Conversely, d∈I implies Rd⊆I, proving
I=Rd.​
(14)
Every ideal is therefore a principal ideal; in particular, a Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain.

For a piecewise continuously differentiable contour γ:[a,b]→U, the contour integral is defined by
∫γ​f(z)dz=∫ab​f(γ(t))γ′(t)dt.​
(27)
If the contour has corners, integrate on its finitely many differentiable pieces and sum the results. The integral on the right is taken componentwise as an ordinary real-parameter integral of a complex-valued function. The factor γ′(t) encodes both the speed and orientation of the contour.

An isolated singularity at a means that f is holomorphic in some punctured disk 0<∣z−a∣<r, while a itself is an excluded point or a point where the given function is not holomorphic. There are no other singularities arbitrarily close to a. By Laurent's theorem, the function has a unique Laurent series on that punctured disk:
f(z)=∑j=−∞∞​cj​(z−a)j.
(34)
The negative-power terms form its principal part of a Laurent series.

A removable singularity is an isolated singularity for which the function on the punctured disk extends to a holomorphic function on the full disk, by assigning or correcting its value at a. In Laurent series terms,
cj​=0 for every j<0.​
(35)
The remaining power series defines the extension with value c0​ at a. Conversely, a holomorphic extension has a Taylor series there, so uniqueness of the Laurent series forces all negative coefficients to vanish.

The complex sine has simple zeros exactly at z=kπ, k∈Z, because sin(kπ)=0 and cos(kπ)=(−1)k=0. Hence the denominator has a zero of order three at each of these points and nowhere else.
At 0, the numerator has order two, so the quotient has a simple pole. Indeed its leading term is −π4/z. At π, the numerator has order one, so there is a pole of order two. At −π, the numerator has order three, cancelling the denominator's order-three zero, so the singularity is removable. Its extension value is
limz→−π​sin3zz2(z−π)(z+π)3​=cos3(−π)(−π)2(−2π)​=2π3.
(45)
At every remaining kπ, the numerator is nonzero, giving a pole of order three. Thus the complete finite-plane classification is
z0π−πkπ,k∈Z∖{−1,0,1}​classificationpole of order 1pole of order 2removable, extension value 2π3pole of order 3​​​
(46)
Away from this discrete set the denominator is nonzero, so the quotient is holomorphic.
