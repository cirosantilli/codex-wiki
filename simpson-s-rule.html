Simpson's rule (∫aa+2h​f≈3h​[f(a)+4f(a+h)+f(a+2h)])

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Numerical analysis Numerical integration Quadrature rule
OurBigBook.com Words: 898 Articles: 4
Simpson's rule approximates a definite integral by integrating the quadratic polynomial obtained by polynomial interpolation at two endpoints and their midpoint. For an interval [a,b] with a<b, write m=(a+b)/2 and h=(b−a)/2. The three quadrature nodes are a,m,b, and the quadrature rule is
S(f)=3h​[f(a)+4f(m)+f(b)].
(8)
Thus its quadrature weights are h/3,4h/3,h/3. Their sum is 2h=b−a, as required for exactness on constant functions. This is the three-node Newton-Cotes closed quadrature; its degree of precision of a quadrature rule is three, even though the interpolating polynomial has polynomial degree at most two.[ref]
For an original worked example, take the cubic polynomial f(x)=x3−2x+4 on the interval [−1,2]. Here m=1/2 and h=3/2, so the function evaluations give
f(−1)=5,f(1/2)=825​=3.125,f(2)=8.
(9)
Simpson's rule gives
S(f)=21​(5+4⋅825​+8)=451​=12.75.
(10)
An antiderivative supplies an independent exact definite integral:
∫−12​(x3−2x+4)dx=[4x4​−x2+4x]−12​=451​.
(11)
The quadratic polynomial through the same quadrature nodes is p2​(x)=23​x2−21​x+3. It differs from f between the quadrature nodes, but its definite integral agrees exactly. The picture shows why: the signed polynomial interpolation error has two lobes with opposite integrals.
Three function evaluations therefore capture more information about an integral than about the whole function. When an antiderivative is unavailable, composite Simpson's rule repeats this inexpensive local construction over a partition of an interval. For a sufficiently smooth function, halving the node spacing reduces its truncation error by approximately a factor of sixteen.
