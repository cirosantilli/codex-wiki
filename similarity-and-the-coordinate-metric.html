Similarity and the coordinate metric (G=STS,BTG=GB)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra Matrix similarity Similarity transformation
OurBigBook.com Words: 153
For real matrices, orthogonal similarity is the special case B=Q−1AQ=QTAQ with Q an orthogonal matrix. Then a symmetric matrix remains symmetric, because BT=QTATQ=B. The expression QAQT describes the reverse conversion and has the same property. An orthonormal basis retains the ordinary dot product formula for its coordinate vectors.
An arbitrary change of basis need not preserve entrywise symmetry. In the opening example, A is a symmetric matrix but B=(20​−35​) is not. The geometric inner product has not disappeared: in the new coordinates it is represented by the Gram matrix
G=STS=(11​12​),(Sy)T(Sz)=yTGz.
(87)
For p with new coordinate vector y=(1,1)T, the squared Euclidean norm is yTGy=5, agreeing with xTx=22+12. The naive coordinate sum yTy=2 would use the wrong inner product.
If AT=A, direct multiplication gives
BTG=GB=STAS.
(88)
This is self-adjointness expressed in the new inner product, rather than entrywise symmetry relative to the identity matrix. For the example, both products equal (22​27​). Thus arbitrary matrix similarity preserves the linear operator, while its geometric interpretation requires transforming the Gram matrix as well. Matrix congruence supplies the corresponding rule for a bilinear form and should be distinguished from matrix similarity.
