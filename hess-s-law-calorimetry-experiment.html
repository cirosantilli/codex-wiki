Hess's law calorimetry experiment

 Home Chemistry Thermochemistry Hess's law
OurBigBook.com Words: 513
A practical test compares three transformations involving sodium hydroxide: dissolution of the solid, neutralization of its chemical solution by hydrochloric acid, and reaction of the solid directly with hydrochloric acid. In the ideal compatible-state notation, the hydrogen ion and hydroxide give
D:N:D+N:​NaOH(s)⟶Na+(aq)+OH−(aq),H+(aq)+OH−(aq)⟶H2​O(l),NaOH(s)+H+(aq)⟶Na+(aq)+H2​O(l).​
(25)
The hydroxide cancels, whereas the newly formed water remains. Sodium and chloride spectator ions must still be included in the full material inventory when matching actual chemical solutions.
The teaching protocol Calorimetry: Heat of Neutralization Reaction & Hess's Law, Procedure A–C, uses nested insulated cups, a cardboard lid and a thermometer. It mixes 75mL each of 2.0molL−1 hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide chemical solutions; separately it adds 6.00g solid sodium hydroxide to diluted hydrochloric acid, or to 150mL water. It records initial temperatures, stirs, reads temperature every 30s, repeats runs, and extrapolates cooling trends to mixing time. Rapid weighing limits moisture uptake. The Introduction neglects vessel heat capacity and room losses; these are approximations, not a measured calibration. The readout tests whether the inferred enthalpies add, rather than directly measuring a state function.[ref]
Matching the chemical equations alone is insufficient. The direct-acid recipe uses 0.160mol hydrochloric acid, whereas the solution-mixing recipe uses 0.150mol. The dissolution run also does not automatically reproduce the water inventory of the mixed-solution run. Exact closure requires matching initial and final chemical solution compositions and adding any necessary mixing or dilution enthalpies. Agreement after approximate corrections is a limited test, rather than proof of exact cancellation for those recipes.
To improve the calorimetry, use calorimeter calibration for the participating hardware, and estimate calorimeter heat-loss correction under comparable conditions. With an approximately constant total receiving heat capacity Ctot​=mcp​+Ccal​, common initial temperature, constant external pressure, only expansion work, negligible escaping material and negligible bulk kinetic energy and potential energy changes, the balance is
qrxn​+Ctot​ΔT+Qloss,out​=0,ΔHrxn​=qrxn​.
(26)
Here m is the chemical solution mass, cp​ its specific heat capacity at constant pressure, Ccal​ the hardware heat capacity, and Qloss,out​ the signed outward heat loss. Different initial temperatures require separate warming terms. Calorimeter calibration and matching chemical solution compositions address different errors.
For an original constructed model, choose Ctot​=120JK−1 and ΔT=3K. Neglect outward heat loss and other corrections. The receiving chemical solution and hardware gain qreceiver​=360J, so qrxn​=−360J. If the completed extent of reaction is ξ=0.020mol for a unit-coefficient chemical equation, the inferred reaction enthalpy per mole of that reaction is
ξΔHrxn​​=0.020mol−360J​=−18kJmol−1.
(27)
These chosen numbers illustrate the signs and normalization; they are not measured sodium hydroxide data and are independent of the abstract A,B,C example.
A loss of heat to the room makes the uncorrected warming underestimate the magnitude of a heat-releasing reaction. Incomplete dissolution, incomplete reaction, uncertain amount of substance, or a slow thermometer response can also spoil closure. A useful experimental residual is r=ΔHdirect​−(ΔHD​+ΔHN​) after all changes have been placed on the same amount and state basis. Compare r with the measurement uncertainty, accounting for shared calibration errors; averaging repeated runs reduces scatter without removing a common bias.
