Cantor set (C)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Geometry and topology Fractal geometry Self-similarity
OurBigBook.com Words: 393 Articles: 2
The Cantor set is obtained from [0,1] by repeatedly deleting the open middle third of every remaining interval. Its first stages retain [0,1/3]∪[2/3,1], then four intervals of length 1/9, then eight of length 1/27. After level n, there are 2n retained intervals of length 3−n. The Cantor set is the intersection of all these stages, rather than the set of endpoints visible in any finite drawing.
Equivalently,
C={∑j=1∞​3jaj​​:aj​∈{0,2}}.
(77)
Thus its points admit a base-three expansion using only zero and two. The word “admit” handles numbers with two expansions: 1/3=0.13​=0.02222…3​ is retained as an endpoint. Every infinite binary choice determines a point of C, and differing choices give differing points, so C is uncountable.
Its total retained interval length is (2/3)n→0, so C has zero Lebesgue measure. It is nevertheless nonempty, compact, and uncountable, and has Hausdorff dimension log2/log3: the covers and mass bounds are worked out in Hausdorff dimension of the Cantor set. This separates counting, ordinary length, and fine-scale geometric size in one concrete example.
The self-similarity is exact:
C=31​C ∪ (32​+31​C).
(78)
The Cantor distribution puts half the probability into each copy at every split. It has a continuous cumulative distribution function, the Cantor function, while all its mass lies on a Lebesgue null set. This makes the construction useful in measure theory as well as fractal geometry: it provides a concrete model in which several plausible meanings of “small” disagree.
