Ionotropic receptor

 Home Biology Protein Receptor (biochemistry)
OurBigBook.com Words: 528
An ionotropic receptor is a receptor in which binding of a ligand directly gates an ion channel within the same protein complex. The binding site detects the chemical signal; the pore supplies a path across the cell membrane. At a chemical synapse, a neurotransmitter therefore changes synaptic conductance without an obligatory intervening G protein or second messenger. [ref]
A familiar example is the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor of skeletal muscle: acetylcholine binding opens a pore passing cations, including sodium and potassium. The muscarinic acetylcholine receptor instead uses intracellular signal transduction; the same neurotransmitter can engage different mechanisms. Direct gating does not mean that an ionotropic receptor pumps ions: electrochemical gradients drive passage through the open ion channel. Nor does “ionotropic” specify excitation. The reversal potential, initial membrane potential and other conductances determine whether the effect favors an action potential, causes hyperpolarization, or supplies shunting inhibition. [ref]
A concrete electrical approximation separates opening from drive. Let N identical ion channels each have open-channel electrical conductance γ and opening probability p(t). At fixed membrane potential V, with common reversal potential Erev​, the expected value of inward-positive electric current is
Iin​(t)=Nγp(t)(Erev​−V).
(36)
For stipulated values N=100, γ=20pS, p=0.2, V=−60mV and Erev​=0, the mean open count is 20, the population electrical conductance is 0.4nS and Iin​=24pA. One open ion channel carries 1.2pA. These are illustrative values, not measurements. At V=Erev​, the net electric current vanishes despite open ion channels; neither binding nor opening alone determines the readout. The approximation omits changes in ion concentrations and voltage-dependent open-channel electrical conductance.
A motivating experiment made the elementary electrical events visible. As Bert Sakmann recounts, chronically denervated frog muscle fibers had acetylcholine-sensitive receptors spread beyond the original nerve contact. Mild enzyme treatment exposed the cell membrane; a polished glass pipette sealed over a small patch to reduce background noise. Two intracellular electrodes held the local membrane potential through voltage clamp, while the patch pipette recorded tiny electric currents. Applying suberyldicholine, a ligand that activates the acetylcholine receptor and prolongs openings, revealed abrupt, approximately rectangular current events. Their amplitudes agreed reasonably with estimates from whole-membrane current fluctuations. This established discrete opening and closing events; it did not make every ligand response instantaneous. Background noise and finite recording bandwidth concealed sufficiently small or brief events.[ref]
Recording an intact membrane patch alone does not exclude intracellular relays. Tank, Huganir, Greengard and Webb supplied a complementary isolation test in 1983: they purified nicotinic acetylcholine receptors from an electric ray's electric organ, incorporated them into artificial lipid membranes, and fused small vesicles into larger vesicles by freezing and thawing. Sealed glass-pipette recordings from isolated membrane patches resolved functional ion channels with properties resembling those in muscle. Removing the intact cytoplasm and its signaling machinery supports the sufficiency of the receptor complex for channel function; an artificial lipid environment still cannot reproduce every native regulatory interaction.[ref]
Direct gating supports millisecond communication at many chemical synapses. Response duration also depends on neurotransmitter clearance, binding and channel-state transitions. Continued ligand exposure can prolong activity. Classify an ionotropic receptor by its direct connection between recognition and pore gating, rather than a universal duration cutoff. [ref]
