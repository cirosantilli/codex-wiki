Delay stability of proportional integrator control (kτ<π/2)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Control theory Closed-loop control Feedback delay
OurBigBook.com Words: 462
For the constant-coefficient model x′(t)=−kx(t−τ) with k>0 and τ≥0, the zero equilibrium point is asymptotically stable if and only if kτ<π/2. For positive τ, perturbations are measured by the supremum norm of the initial history of delayed feedback; in the stable regime, every continuous history produces a solution tending to zero. At equality, nondecaying sinusoids prevent asymptotic stability. For kτ>π/2, growing oscillatory solutions exist. These statements concern this ideal linear time-invariant system, rather than an arbitrary controlled plant. The corresponding linear equation and its threshold are developed in Cain and Reynolds, section 6.4, pages 174–177.
Substitute the exponential function x(t)=est, allowing a complex number s. The characteristic equation is
s+ke−sτ=0.
(32)
For positive feedback delay this is not a characteristic polynomial: it has infinitely many roots. A root's real part determines growth or decay of its exponential mode, and its imaginary part determines the angular frequency. To find the first boundary, set s=iω with ω>0 and use Euler's formula:
kcos(ωτ)=0,ω−ksin(ωτ)=0.
(33)
The smallest positive crossing is ω=k and ωτ=π/2. Thus at τc​=π/(2k) the complex conjugate roots are s=±ik. In fact, with the matching initial history of delayed feedback,
x(t)=Acos(kt)+Bsin(kt)
(34)
solves the delay differential equation exactly: a quarter-cycle feedback delay turns the past displacement into precisely the current derivative. Its cycle lasts 2π/k=4τc​. The amplitude is arbitrary; this linear model does not select a finite-amplitude oscillation.
The roots cross toward growth as feedback delay increases. Differentiating the characteristic equation at fixed k gives
dτds​=−1+sτs2​,Re(dτds​)s=ik,τ=τc​​=1+(π/2)2k2​>0.
(35)
This calculation identifies the direction of the first destabilization. The full threshold also requires ruling out earlier unstable roots: if s=α+iβ with α≥0 and β>0, the characteristic equation implies cos(βτ)≤0 and 0<β≤k, hence kτ≥βτ≥π/2. A real nonnegative root is impossible because s+ke−sτ>0 there. This supplies the geometric reason the threshold is a quarter-cycle rather than an arbitrary fitting constant.
The tradeoff is concrete. Without feedback delay, increasing k shortens the exponential decay time constant 1/k. With known τ>0, the same proportional control must satisfy k<π/(2τ). For τ=0.2s, the threshold is k≈7.854s−1: k=5s−1 is stable and k=8s−1 is unstable. A design close to the boundary leaves little room for additional feedback delay; reducing k or reducing measurement latency increases that room. The zero-delay time constant is only a comparison scale, not the decay time of the delayed response. Frank discusses the gain–delay compromise in Control Theory Tutorial, section 13.4 and figure 13.3.
