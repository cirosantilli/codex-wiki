Protease protection assay

OurBigBook.com Words: 320
A protease protection assay asks whether a barrier prevents an added protease from reaching a protein. A membrane-disruption comparison tests whether protection depends on that barrier.
The membrane-disruption test was performed by David D. Sabatini and Günter Blobel in 1970. Rat-liver microsomes purified by sucrose centrifugation incorporated radioactive leucine during cell-free translation. Samples received cold trypsin and chymotrypsin, with or without membrane-solubilizing detergents. Electron microscopy checked vesicle preservation; size-exclusion chromatography separated protected fragment sizes. Large membrane-associated fragments lost protection after disruption, whereas small ribosome-protected fragments persisted. An exposed ribosome preparation remained digestible when mixed with microsomes, checking that membranes did not simply inhibit the proteases.[ref]
To read the qualitative diagram, first compare untreated aliquots: they establish that detectable cargo exists before digestion. Next compare external protease with intact versus disrupted membranes. Persistence only while the barrier remains intact supports enclosure, provided the exposed-cargo control confirms active protease in both chemical conditions. The detergent-only aliquot checks that disruption itself does not destroy recovery or detection. Adding membranes after synthesis tests a different alternative: capture of a completed product rather than entry during biological translation.
This is an argument from controlled accessibility. Binding predicts membrane association but generally leaves an exposed cargo digestible. Enclosure predicts protection that depends on membrane integrity. However, protein aggregation, intrinsic resistance to proteolysis, inactive protease or undetected small fragments can obscure this distinction. For a transmembrane protein, expect protection of the enclosed portion rather than necessarily the full chain. Combine fragment size, compartment controls and membrane topology; a protected signal alone does not uniquely specify the whole protein's location.
