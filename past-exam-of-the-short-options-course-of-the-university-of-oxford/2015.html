The binomial theorem isolates the only terms that can survive reduction modulo the prime number p:
(a+b)p=ap+bp+∑r=1p−1​(rp​)ap−rbr.
(1)
For 1≤r≤p−1, neither r! nor (p−r)! is divisible by p, whereas p! is. Consequently p∣(rp​), and every intermediate term vanishes in modular arithmetic. Thus
(a+b)p≡ap+bp(modp).​
(2)
This works for arbitrary integers a,b, including those divisible by p. It is the additive identity behind the Frobenius endomorphism in characteristic p.

For an odd prime number p, Euler's criterion states that
a(p−1)/2≡(pa​)(modp),​
(3)
where the Legendre symbol is zero if p∣a, one if a is a nonzero quadratic residue, and minus one otherwise. Including the zero case makes this formulation valid for every integer a.
Apply Euler's criterion to a, b, and ab. Multiplication in modular arithmetic gives
(pab​)≡(ab)(p−1)/2≡a(p−1)/2b(p−1)/2≡(pa​)(pb​)(modp).
(4)
If p divides either factor, both sides are zero. Otherwise both sides belong to {−1,1}; these two values are distinct modulo the odd prime number p. The congruence is therefore an equality of integers, proving multiplicativity of the Legendre symbol:
(pab​)=(pa​)(pb​).​
(5)

In Pollard's p-1 algorithm, a large exponent containing many small factors makes aM−1 divisible by a prime factor whose predecessor divides M, by Fermat's little theorem. A greatest common divisor with the number being factored can then separate that prime factor from the others.
Choose base a=2 and build factorial exponents using modular exponentiation. The successive exponents 2!, 3!, and 4! give
22≡4,26≡64,224≡644≡35(mod1003).
(7)
The first two greatest common divisors are gcd(3,1003)=gcd(63,1003)=1. At the next step the Euclidean algorithm gives
gcd(224−1,1003)=gcd(34,1003)=17,1003=29⋅34+17.
(8)
Hence
17 is a factor of 1003,1003=17⋅59.​
(9)
Here the element order of 2 modulo 17 is 8, which divides 24, even though 17−1=16 does not divide 24. The algorithm only needs the element order to divide the chosen exponent; divisibility by the full predecessor is a sufficient condition, not a necessary one.

Use the cross-ratio convention compatible with the permutation identities in this paper. Represent a projective point Pi​∈CP1 by a nonzero vector pi​∈C2, and write [pi​,pj​]=det(pi​,pj​). Define
(P0​P1​:P2​P3​)=[p0​,p3​][p1​,p2​][p0​,p2​][p1​,p3​]​.​
(26)
The denominator is nonzero because the four projective points are distinct. Rescaling any one representative multiplies numerator and denominator by the same nonzero scalar, so the definition is independent of the chosen homogeneous coordinates. In finite affine coordinates it reads
(z0​z1​:z2​z3​)=(z0​−z3​)(z1​−z2​)(z0​−z2​)(z1​−z3​)​,
(27)
with the point at infinity of the complex projective line handled by the homogeneous formula.
A projective transformation is represented by an invertible matrix A∈GL2​(C). Since [Api​,Apj​]=det(A)[pi​,pj​], the two factors of det(A) in the numerator cancel the two in the denominator. Therefore every projective transformation preserves this cross-ratio. This also shows that the formula descends from matrices to the projective linear group, whose representatives differ by a nonzero common scalar.

The needed general-position theorem is sharp triple transitivity of the projective linear group on the projective line: for any two ordered triples of distinct projective points, exactly one projective transformation takes the first triple to the second.
In particular, let TP​ take (P0​,P1​,P2​) to (∞,0,1). In these coordinates,
(∞0:1z)=z,
(28)
so cross-ratio invariance gives TP​(P3​)=λ, where λ=(P0​P1​:P2​P3​). Distinctness implies λ=0,1 and λ is finite. The entire ordered quadruple is thus normalized to
(∞,0,1,λ).​
(29)
For a second quadruple (Q0​,Q1​,Q2​,Q3​) with the same cross-ratio, its normalizing map TQ​ yields the same quadruple. Consequently
TQ−1​∘TP​(Pi​)=Qi​(0≤i≤3).​
(30)
This proves the equivalence. Uniqueness on the first three points also shows that the projectivity carrying one ordered quadruple to the other is unique. The cross-ratio normalization of four projective points therefore gives a complete invariant for this ordered classification.

The cross-ratio permutation orbit is generated by the two operations λ↦1/λ and λ↦1−λ from part (d). The full list is
λ,λ1​,1−λ,1−λ1​,λλ−1​,λ−1λ​.​

They form the Klein four-group inside the symmetric group on four symbols, leaving at most 24/4=6 different values; the displayed operations attain all six rational expressions.
There are fewer than six values exactly when a nonidentity operation in this six-element action fixes λ: if two operations give the same value, composing one with the inverse of the other gives such a fixed point. For real λ=0,1, the fixed-point equations are
λ=1/λλ=1−λλ=λ/(λ−1)​⟹λ=−1,⟹λ=21​,⟹λ=2.​

λ∈{−1,21​,2} are exactly the real exceptional values.​
(39)

A map g is differentiable at a when a linear map Dga​:Rn→Rk approximates its increment to first order:
g(a+h)=g(a)+Dga​(h)+o(∥h∥).​
(42)
This Fréchet derivative is unique, and its matrix in the standard bases is the Jacobian matrix. Differentiability on a domain means differentiability at each point there.
A standard sufficient condition for a zero locus to be a submanifold is the regular level set theorem: if g is C1 on an open domain and Dga​ is surjective at every a∈g−1(0), then
M=g−1(0) is an embedded C1 submanifold of dimension n−k.​
(43)
For smooth g, the submanifold is smooth. The full-rank condition is the relevant constraint qualification.
Under this regularity hypothesis, its tangent space is
Ta​M=kerDga​.​
(44)
Indeed, if a differentiable curve γ lies in M and passes through a, the chain rule gives Dga​(γ′(0))=0. Thus all tangent velocities lie in the kernel of a linear map. The rank-nullity theorem makes this kernel (n−k)-dimensional, the same dimension as the tangent space, proving equality. In coordinates, solve the homogeneous linear equations given by the Jacobian matrix.
The regularity qualification matters: g(x)=x2 has zero set {0}, a zero-dimensional submanifold, but Dg0​=0. Its kernel is all of R, whereas its tangent space is zero. This is the phenomenon that singular defining maps can overestimate tangent spaces.

A tree is a nonempty connected graph with no graph cycle. A leaf of a tree is a vertex of vertex degree one. As usual in this finite graph theory problem, trees are finite; the assertion would fail for a doubly infinite graph path.
Take a longest path v0​,v1​,…,vm​ in a tree with at least two vertices. Connectedness gives m≥1. If v0​ had a neighbour outside this graph path, the path could be extended, contradicting maximal length. If it had a neighbour vj​ with j≥2, that edge together with the segment v0​,v1​,…,vj​ would make a graph cycle. Thus its only neighbour is v1​, and it is a leaf of a tree. The same argument at the other end makes vm​ a second, distinct leaf of a tree. Hence
Every finite tree with at least two vertices has at least two leaves.​
(77)

A matching in a graph is a set of edges no two of which share a vertex. A cover here means a vertex cover: a set C of vertices such that every edge has at least one endpoint in C. It is not an edge cover.
Kőnig's theorem for bipartite matching states that, in any finite bipartite graph, the maximum size of a matching in a graph equals the minimum size of a vertex cover:
ν(G)=τ(G).​
(80)
Here ν is the matching number and τ is the vertex cover number.
