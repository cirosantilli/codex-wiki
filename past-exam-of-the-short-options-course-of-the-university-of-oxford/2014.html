A simple group is a nontrivial group whose only normal subgroups are itself and the trivial subgroup.
A composition series is a chain G=G0​▹G1​▹⋯▹Gr​=1 in which each Gi+1​ is a normal subgroup of Gi​ and each quotient group Gi​/Gi+1​ is simple and nontrivial. Its length is r, and these quotients are its composition factors. The individual terms need not all be normal in G.
A solvable group is a group whose derived series reaches the trivial group: starting with G(0)=G, define G(i+1)=[G(i),G(i)]. For finite groups this is equivalent to having a composition series whose factors are all cyclic groups of prime order.

Let 1◃N◃G be the given length-two composition series. Thus N and G/N are simple groups. Let M be any proper nontrivial normal subgroup of G, such as the penultimate term of another composition series.
If M=N, its two factors are already the given ones. Suppose M=N. Since M∩N is normal in the simple group N, it is either 1 or N. The latter would mean N⊆M, but then M/N would be a proper nontrivial normal subgroup of G/N, impossible. Thus M∩N=1.
Also M cannot be contained in N, since it would then be a nontrivial normal subgroup of N distinct from N. The product MN is a normal subgroup of G, strictly containing N. Simplicity of G/N therefore forces MN=G. The second isomorphism theorem for groups gives
M≅G/N,G/M≅N.​
(10)
In particular M is simple. Every other composition series has such an M as its last proper term, and no nontrivial refinement inside a simple M is possible. Hence every composition series has length two.
The general Jordan–Hölder theorem states that any two composition series of a finite group have the same length and the same composition factors up to group isomorphism and permutation. The argument above proves both assertions in this length-two case: another series either uses N itself or has factors isomorphic to the original pair in the opposite order. No general uniqueness theorem was used to prove this special case.

Factor the group order as 588=22⋅3⋅72. The Sylow theorems say that the number of Sylow subgroups for the prime seven divides 12 and is congruent to one modulo seven. Among 1,2,3,4,6,12, only one qualifies. Thus there is a unique normal subgroup N of order 49.
Every group of prime-square order is Abelian. Indeed its center of a group is nontrivial, and a cyclic quotient by the center of a group forces the whole group to be abelian. Consequently N is either C49​ or C7​×C7​, and in either case it has two composition factors, both C7​.
It remains to consider Q=H/N, of order twelve. Its number of Sylow subgroups for the prime three is either one or four:
  • If it is one, the normal order-three subgroup has factor C3​, and the remaining quotient has order four. An order-four group is abelian and has two factors C2​.
  • If it is four, these order-three subgroups have disjoint nonidentity elements and account for eight elements. Any Sylow subgroup for the prime two, of order four, must consist of the four remaining elements, since its nonidentity elements have power-of-two orders. It is therefore unique and normal. Its two factors are C2​, and the quotient has order three, giving C3​.
Lift the composition series of Q to H using the subgroup correspondence theorem, and join it to a composition series inside N. This gives
C2​, C2​, C3​, C7​, C7​​
(11)
as the multiset of composition factors. The Jordan–Hölder theorem makes this independent of the chosen series. In particular every group of order 588 is a solvable group, with composition length five; that is a statement about composition length, not necessarily its derived length.

The required general linear position condition is that every n+1 of the n+2 representing vectors are linearly independent. Such an ordered configuration is a projective frame.
Choose representatives p0​,…,pn​ of the first n+1 projective points; they form a basis. Write a representative of the last point as pn+1​=∑i​ai​pi​. Every ai​ is nonzero: otherwise the last point together with the other n basis points would fail the independence condition. Rescale the basis representatives to p​i​=ai​pi​, so that pn+1​=∑i​p​i​.
Do the same for the target configuration, obtaining representatives q​i​ with qn+1​=∑i​q​i​. The unique linear map sending the basis p​i​ to q​i​ is invertible and also sends pn+1​ to qn+1​. Its induced projective transformation sends every point to its prescribed partner. Thus any two ordered projective frames are projectively equivalent.

A projective quadric is the zero locus of a nonzero homogeneous quadratic polynomial q(v)=vTBv, with B symmetric over a field of characteristic different from two. A projective point on it is singular when all first partial derivatives vanish. Since ∇q(v)=2Bv, the quadric is nonsingular exactly when B is invertible.
Over the complex numbers, a nonzero quadratic form in three variables can be diagonalized by an invertible change of basis. Its rank classifies a projective conic:
  • Rank three gives a nonsingular conic.
  • Rank two gives two distinct projective lines, since the reduced form X2+Y2 factors as (X+iY)(X−iY). Their intersection is the singular point.
  • Rank one gives a double projective line, defined by a squared linear form. The gradient vanishes along that line.
The zero polynomial is excluded from the definition of a conic. Thus these last two cases are precisely the singular conics.

A Hamilton cycle is a cycle visiting every vertex of a finite graph exactly once before returning to its starting point. A Hamilton path is a path visiting every vertex exactly once, with no requirement to return or to have its endpoints adjacent. Both conditions concern visiting vertices, rather than traversing every edge.

Let v1​…vk​ be a longest path in the finite simple graph. Every neighbor of either endpoint lies on this path, since an outside neighbor would extend it. Define two subsets of {1,…,k−1}:
I={i:v1​vi+1​∈E},J={i:vi​vk​∈E}.
(41)
Their sizes are d(v1​) and d(vk​). The degree hypothesis gives ∣I∣+∣J∣≥n≥k, but there are only k−1 possible indices. Hence some i belongs to both. The edges at that index close a cycle through every vertex of the path:
v1​,v2​,…,vi​,vk​,vk−1​,…,vi+1​,v1​.
(42)
The graph is connected: any component has at least n/2+1 vertices by the minimum degree, so two components would exceed n vertices. If k<n, connectedness gives an edge from this cycle to an outside vertex. Starting at that vertex and traversing the cycle without its closing edge gives a path with k+1 vertices, a contradiction.
Thus k=n and the cycle is a Hamilton cycle, proving Dirac's theorem. The overlap-of-indices argument is why the degree threshold is half the number of vertices.

Take the complete bipartite graph K3,4​​. Its seven vertices have degrees four on one side and three on the other. Deleting any two vertices leaves at least one vertex on each side, so the remaining complete bipartite graph is connected.
Every cycle in a bipartite graph alternates between its two parts, using equal numbers of vertices from each. A Hamilton cycle here would have to use three vertices from one part and four from the other, which is impossible. Thus three-vertex connectivity and minimum degree three do not suffice for Hamiltonicity in this seven-vertex example.

The Ramsey number R(k,ℓ) is the least positive integer N such that every red-blue coloring of the edges of KN​ has a red Kk​ or a blue Kℓ​. Equivalently, every N-vertex graph has a clique of size k or an independent set of size ℓ.
For N=k, either there is a blue edge or every edge is red, producing the red Kk​. Conversely an entirely red Kk−1​ has neither a red Kk​ nor a blue edge. Therefore
R(k,2)=k.​
(43)

R(k,ℓ)≤R(k−1,ℓ)+R(k,ℓ−1).​

Fix w∈W. It has five incident edges, so three of them have the same color by the pigeonhole principle. Suppose they are red, with other endpoints a,b,c. Any existing edge among a,b,c must be blue, or it would complete a red triangle with w.
If all three edges among a,b,c existed, they would therefore form a blue triangle. Thus their triple must contain the unique missing edge uv. In particular, both u and v belong to the three red neighbors, so wu and wv are both red. The identical argument works if the chosen three edges are blue.
Therefore wu and wv have the same color for every w∈W. The missing edge is crucial: it is the only way the monochromatic neighbor triangle can fail to be present.

Take spacetime coordinates X=(ct,x,y,z). Differentiating the initial segment gives the four-velocity
U=dsdX​=c(coshωs,sinhωs,0,0).​
(48)
The identity cosh2r−sinh2r=1 gives g(U,U)=c2, and dt/ds=coshωs>0. The proper time element therefore satisfies dτ2=g(dX,dX)/c2=ds2, with the future orientation selecting dτ=ds. Thus s is proper time along this path.

Differentiate each segment and express the four-velocity using a continuous rapidity:
U(s)=c(coshθ(s),sinhθ(s),0,0),θ(s)=⎩⎨⎧​ωs,ω(2T−s),ω(s−4T),​0≤s<T,T≤s<3T,3T≤s≤4T.​
(51)
At T both rapidities are ωT; at 3T both are −ωT. Hence the four-velocity is continuous at both junctions. This expression also gives g(U,U)=c2 everywhere, so the parameter remains proper time on the later segments.
Away from the junctions,
A(s)=cθ′(s)(sinhθ(s),coshθ(s),0,0),
(52)
with θ′ successively +ω,−ω,+ω. The rapidity is continuous but its slope reverses. Thus the four-acceleration has a finite jump to its negative at each junction, while its magnitude stays cω on either side. There is no velocity jump; the idealized path is continuously differentiable but not twice differentiable at the switching events.

At s=0 and s=4T, the mission is at the planet's position x=c/ω. The final coordinate time from the last segment is
treturn​=ω4​sinh(ωT).
(53)
The planet is at rest in these coordinates, so its proper time elapsed equals this coordinate-time difference. The astronauts' proper time is the parameter interval, 4T. Therefore
Δτplanet​=ω4​sinh(ωT),Δτship​=4T.​
(54)
For proper acceleration cω=10 ms−2 and c=3×108 ms−1, ω−1=3×107 seconds, approximately one year. Thus ωT=16 and e16≈30002=9×106. Neglecting e−16 in the hyperbolic sine,
Δτplanet​≈18×106 years,Δτship​=64 years.​
(55)
The clocks compare two different timelike paths between the same departure and reunion events.
