Let ∣G∣=p2. The allowed center of a group theorem gives ∣Z(G)∣>1. By Lagrange's theorem its order is p or p2. In the first case G/Z(G) has order p and is a cyclic group. A cyclic quotient by the center forces G to be Abelian: if G/Z(G)=⟨gZ(G)⟩, write arbitrary elements as gaz,gbw with z,w∈Z(G), and commute them directly. Hence in every case
∣G∣=p2 ⟹ G is abelian.​

For two examples at prime-cube order, take the dihedral group of a square, ⟨r,s∣r4=s2=1, srs=r−1⟩, and the quaternion group Q8​={±1,±i,±j,±k}. Both have order 8=23. They are nonabelian because sr=rs and ij=k=−k=ji, respectively. They are distinct examples even up to group isomorphism: the dihedral group has five elements of order two and the quaternion group has only one. The request asks for two examples; it does not require the prime to be arbitrary.
