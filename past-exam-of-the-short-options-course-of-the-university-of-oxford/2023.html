A group presentation ⟨X∣R⟩ denotes the quotient F(X)/⟨⟨R⟩⟩ of the free group on the generator symbols by the normal closure of the relator words. The symbols generate the quotient, and each relator becomes the identity. The phrase also asserts that no additional relations are imposed except consequences of these relators and the group axioms. Equivalently, an assignment of the generators to a group extends to a group homomorphism from the presented group exactly when all relators evaluate to the identity; this is the universal property of a group presentation.

A projective conic in P2(C) is the zero locus of a nonzero homogeneous quadratic polynomial, up to nonzero scaling of that polynomial. Under an invertible linear change of homogeneous coordinates, its symmetric matrix has rank 3, 2, or 1. Over C, the corresponding forms are a nonsingular conic such as XZ−Y2=0, two distinct lines such as XY=0, or a double line X2=0. In the last case the repeated factor records multiplicity, even though the underlying point set is one line.
A projective line is the projectivization of a two-dimensional linear subspace of C3, equivalently the zero locus of one nonzero linear form. For two such subspaces U,W, the dimension formula for a sum of subspaces gives dim(U∩W)≥2+2−3=1. A nonzero vector in the intersection therefore supplies a common projective point. Distinct lines have exactly one such point.

For Dijkstra's algorithm, initialize d(x)=0 and d(v)=∞ for other vertices, with no predecessor initially. Repeatedly select an unfinalized vertex u of least tentative distance, finalize it, and relax each edge uv: if d(u)+ℓ(uv)<d(v), replace d(v) by that value and set the predecessor of v to u. Continue until all vertices are finalized. The predecessor edges form the shortest-path tree.
To justify finalization, consider a supposedly shorter route to the next selected vertex. Its first unfinalized vertex is preceded by a finalized one, whose relaxation would already have offered a tentative distance no greater than the length of that route's prefix. Nonnegative remaining lengths would contradict the selected vertex's minimal tentative label. Thus final labels are the true shortest distances. Strictly positive lengths also make predecessors have smaller distances, ruling out predecessor cycles in a graph.

Take a minimum spanning tree T, for example one constructed by Kruskal's algorithm. Fix v and let e be a longest edge of the tree's unique xv path. Deleting e partitions T into two components separating x and v. Any alternative xv path crosses this graph cut. If all of its edges were shorter than e, a crossing edge f would let us replace e by f, producing a spanning tree of smaller total length. This contradicts minimality of T.
Therefore every competing path has an edge at least as long as e, and the tree path solves the bottleneck shortest path problem. The same minimum spanning tree works simultaneously for every v; this is the minimax path property of a minimum spanning tree.
