Saponification

 Home Chemistry Chemical reaction Hydrolysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 639 Articles: 2
Saponification is hydrolysis of an ester under basic conditions. The conductometric saponification assay follows ethyl acetate consumption by hydroxide, producing ethanol and an acetate ion; its equal-starting-concentration case provides a practical second-order reaction example.
Saponification is ester hydrolysis promoted by a chemical base, with net consumption of hydroxide to form a carboxylate and an alcohol. For a carboxylic acid-derived ester, the net chemical equation is
RCOOR′+OH−⟶RCOO−+R′OH.
(31)
The hydroxide is a reactant, rather than merely a regenerated catalyst.[ref]
For ethyl acetate, the balanced chemical equation becomes
CH3​COOC2​H5​+OH−⟶CH3​COO−+C2​H5​OH.
(32)
Both sides contain four carbon chemical atoms, nine hydrogen chemical atoms, three oxygen chemical atoms, and net electric charge −1. A sodium ion supplied by sodium hydroxide is a spectator ion; the net ionic equation omits it without losing electric charge conservation.
In the usual reaction mechanism, hydroxide adds to the carbonyl group, making a reaction intermediate. Departure of the alcohol's conjugate base gives a carboxylic acid; transfer of a proton then gives the carboxylate and the alcohol. Subsequent addition of a chemical acid gives the carboxylic acid by protonation:
RCOO−+H3​O+⟶RCOOH+H2​O.
(33)
This separate acidification changes the reaction product's protonation state.[ref]
The conductometric saponification assay makes hydroxide consumption observable. A university procedure equilibrates separate ethyl acetate and sodium hydroxide stocks in constant-temperature baths, then mixes them in a rinsed conductivity cell. Two platinum electrodes and an alternating-voltage Wheatstone bridge supply an electrical resistance readout. A matched sodium hydroxide–water dilution checks the initial signal; potassium chloride calibrates the cell constant. Replacing hydroxide with less mobile acetate ions lowers electrical conductance, supporting an inference about changing concentration. The manual supplies a procedure rather than measured outcomes. Temperature drift or additional ions can change the signal; it cannot alone identify the full reaction mechanism.[ref]
