Acoustic time-of-flight speed measurement (c=d/Δt)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Partial differential equation Wave equation Wave speed
OurBigBook.com Words: 360 Articles: 3
The experiment in A time of flight method to measure the speed of sound using a stereo sound card by Carlos C. Carvalho and others makes wave speed visible as a delay. Two powered microphones feed separate channels of one stereo line input. A clap beyond one end of their common axis reaches the nearer microphone first. The displayed signals show two separated onsets when enlarged; count their sample separation N to obtain Δt=N/fs​ and infer speed of sound c=d/Δt from the measured microphone spacing d. The reported sampling rate is 44100s−1. Repeating at different spacings gives a distance-versus-delay plot whose slope estimates speed of sound; dependence on distance helps identify acoustic travel rather than a fixed electronic delay. These are the paper's apparatus and observed readout, not measurements made for this article.[ref]
A controlled implementation also needs timing calibration for acoustic speed measurements. Put the microphones side by side to estimate the channel-delay offset τ0​, swap channels to check its sign, and check the acquisition clock against an independent timing reference. Measure spacings between consistent points on the microphones, keep the source outside their span and on their axis, and fit Δt=d/c+τ0​ by linear regression rather than forcing a zero intercept. Repeat pulses and compare the same onset criterion before reflections arrive. These are proposed calibration and control checks, not additional procedures claimed for the paper.
One sample corresponds to 1/fs​≃22.7μs for the reported sampling rate, so a delay of only a few samples has large relative measurement error. Background measurement noise, different microphone responses and threshold choice can move the apparent onset; reflections can supply a later competing pulse. Record temperature, keep air motion small, and compare several spacings. The result estimates speed of sound under those conditions; it cannot prove an exactly zero precursor below the instrument's sensitivity or establish finite propagation speed for every real medium.
