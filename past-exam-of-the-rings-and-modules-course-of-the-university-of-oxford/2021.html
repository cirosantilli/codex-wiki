We prove the stated Eisenstein criterion directly. Assume n≥1. Suppose f=gh in the polynomial ring R[X]. Since the principal ideal domain R is an integral domain, polynomial degree is additive under polynomial multiplication, and the product of the leading coefficients of g,h is the leading coefficient 1 of f. These two coefficients are therefore units. Multiplying g and h by inverse units, without changing their product, makes both factors monic polynomials.
If either factor is a constant polynomial, that factor is now 1, so the original factor was a unit. It remains to rule out r=degg>0 and s=degh>0.
The prime element p generates a maximal ideal in the principal ideal domain R. Indeed, if (p)⊆(d), then p=de, and the irreducible element property of p forces either (d)=R or (d)=(p). Consequently k=R/(p) is a field. Apply reduction modulo an ideal to every coefficient. The monic polynomial factors retain their polynomial degrees, and the hypotheses give
g​h=f​=Xnin k[X].
(1)
The polynomial ring over a field is a unique factorization domain, and X is an irreducible polynomial. Thus its only monic polynomial divisors in Xn are powers of X. Hence g​=Xr and h=Xs. Both have zero constant term, so p∣g(0) and p∣h(0). This gives p2∣g(0)h(0)=a0​, a contradiction.
The monic polynomial f has positive polynomial degree, so it is a nonzero nonunit. Every factorization therefore has a unit factor, proving
f is irreducible in R[X]​.
(2)

Regard R[X,Y] as the polynomial ring (R[X])[Y]. The coefficient ring R[X] is a Euclidean domain, hence a principal ideal domain. The element X is a prime element: the quotient ring R[X]/(X)≅R is an integral domain.
The monic polynomial Y2+X has its other coefficients divisible by X, while X2∤X. Part (a)(i), with X playing the role of p and Y the indeterminate, proves that Y2+X is an irreducible polynomial.
Now use the automorphism of a polynomial ring
τ:R[X,Y]⟶R[X,Y],τ(X)=X,τ(Y)=Y+1.
(3)
Its inverse fixes X and sends Y to Y−1. A ring isomorphism preserves units and factorizations, hence preserves irreducible elements. Since
τ(Y2+X)=(Y+1)2+X=Y2+2Y+1+X,
(4)
we obtain
Y2+X and Y2+2Y+1+X are irreducible in R[X,Y]​.
(5)
Applying the Eisenstein criterion directly to the second expression with the prime element X would fail, since X∤2; the change of indeterminate supplies the required argument.

All principal ideals in this part are taken inside the subring T, rather than inside R[X]. First, T is a subring: sums and additive inverses retain zero coefficient of X, and the coefficient of X in a product uv is u(0)[X]v+v(0)[X]u=0. It contains 1.
The principal ideal I=(X3)T​=X3T is precisely
I={a3​X3+∑j≥5​aj​Xj:only finitely many aj​=0}.
(6)
Indeed, multiplying an element of T by X3 gives a possible term of polynomial degree 3 and terms of polynomial degrees at least 5, with no X4 term. Conversely, dividing any displayed polynomial by X3 gives a polynomial with no X term, hence an element of T.
Given p∈T, let a,b,c be its coefficients of 1,X2,X4, respectively. Subtracting these terms leaves an element of I, so
p+I=a+bX2+cX4+I​.
(7)
For uniqueness, if two such representatives differ by an element of I, their difference has possible monomials only 1,X2,X4, whereas every element of I has zero coefficients at all three. Comparing coefficients gives a=a′, b=b′, c=c′.
In particular, X4∈/I. Treating I as the principal ideal of R[X] instead would incorrectly discard this surviving term.

Set I=(X3)T​ and J=(Y3)R[Y]​. Part (b)(i) gives a unique representative a+bX2+cX4 for each element of the quotient ring T/I. By polynomial division, each element of the quotient ring R[Y]/J likewise has a unique representative a+bY+cY2. Thus the proposed map Φ is well-defined and a bijection.
It preserves addition and 1. To verify multiplication, all terms of polynomial degree at least 5 lie in I. Hence
​(a+bX2+cX4)(a′+b′X2+c′X4)+I=aa′+(ab′+ba′)X2+(ac′+bb′+ca′)X4+I.​
(8)
In the other quotient ring, Y3=0, giving exactly the same coefficient formula:
(a+bY+cY2)(a′+b′Y+c′Y2)+J=aa′+(ab′+ba′)Y+(ac′+bb′+ca′)Y2+J.
(9)
Therefore
Φ:T/(X3)T​ ≅ ​R[Y]/(Y3),a+bX2+cX4+I⟼a+bY+cY2+J​
(10)
is a ring isomorphism. Both sides are examples of a truncated polynomial ring; the generator on the left is the class of X2, whose square is nonzero and whose cube vanishes.

No such property exists. The ring isomorphism in part (b)(ii) identifies quotient rings by elements having different irreducible element behaviour.
The element X3 is an irreducible element of T. It is nonzero and a nonunit. Every nonzero nonunit of T has polynomial degree at least 2: the only possible polynomials of smaller polynomial degree are constant polynomials, and every nonzero constant polynomial is a unit. If X3=uv with u,v∈T both nonunits, additivity of polynomial degree in the integral domain R[X] would give
3=degu+degv≥2+2=4,
(11)
which is impossible. Thus one factor must be a unit.
In R[Y], however, Y3=Y⋅Y2 is a product of two nonunits, so Y3 is not an irreducible element. If a ring isomorphism invariant P characterized irreducible elements by the indicated quotient ring, the irreducible element X3∈T would imply that T/(X3)T​ has P. Its isomorphic quotient ring R[Y]/(Y3) would then have P, incorrectly making Y3∈R[Y] an irreducible element.
Consequently,
irreducibility cannot be determined from the isomorphism type of R/(x)​.
(12)

The required property is
P:being an integral domain​.
(13)
For a nonzero element x of an integral domain R, this follows from
x is a prime element​⟺(x) is a prime ideal⟺R/(x) is an integral domain.​
(14)
Indeed, ab=0 in the quotient ring exactly when x∣ab. The prime element condition says this forces x∣a or x∣b, exactly the absence of zero divisors in the quotient ring. The nonunit condition makes the quotient ring nonzero. Being an integral domain is preserved by every ring isomorphism.
With the usual definition of a prime element, x=0 is understood. The prime ideal formulation works for every ideal of any commutative ring, including the zero ideal; it avoids this convention about elements.

Choose a finite generating set of a module n1​,…,nt​ for the finitely generated module N. For any m∈M, the surjectivity of the module homomorphism ϕ gives n∈N with ϕ(n)=m. Write n=∑j=1t​rj​nj​. Since ϕ is a module homomorphism,
m=ϕ(∑j=1t​rj​nj​)=∑j=1t​rj​ϕ(nj​).
(15)
Thus
M=Rϕ(n1​)+⋯+Rϕ(nt​)​
(16)
is a finitely generated module. The argument includes N=0, in which case surjectivity forces M=0 and the empty generating set of a module suffices.

Let n1​,…,nt​ generate the submodule N, and let m1​+N,…,ms​+N generate the quotient module M/N. Choose each mi​∈M as a representative of its class. For any m∈M, there are r1​,…,rs​∈R such that
m+N=∑i=1s​ri​(mi​+N).

m=∑i=1s​ri​mi​+∑j=1t​uj​nj​.

M=Rm1​+⋯+Rms​+Rn1​+⋯+Rnt​​
(19)

Let e1​,…,en​ denote the standard basis of a module of the free module Rn. Since the module homomorphism ϕ:Rm→M is surjective, choose vj​∈Rm such that ϕ(vj​)=ψ(ej​) for each j. Define
q:Rn⟶Rm,q(j=1∑n​rj​ej​)=j=1∑n​rj​vj​​.
(20)
Uniqueness of coordinates in a basis of a module makes this well-defined, and the formula preserves addition and scalar multiplication, so q is a module homomorphism. For every x=∑j​rj​ej​,
ϕ(q(x))=∑j​rj​ϕ(vj​)=∑j​rj​ψ(ej​)=ψ(x).
(21)
Thus ϕ∘q=ψ​. This is the lifting property of a free module; only the surjectivity of ϕ is needed for this part.

Put K=kerϕ, H=kerψ, and Q=Imq. The kernel of a module homomorphism H is a submodule, so its image L=q(H) is a submodule. For x∈H, ϕ(q(x))=ψ(x)=0, showing L⊆K. Thus the quotient module K/L exists. Also L⊆Q.
Consider the restriction to K of the natural module homomorphism Rm→Rm/Q:
α:K⟶Rm/Q,y⟼y+Q.
(22)
Its kernel of a module homomorphism is K∩Q. We have L⊆K∩Q. Conversely, if y∈K∩Q, choose x∈Rn with y=q(x). Then
ψ(x)=ϕ(q(x))=ϕ(y)=0,
(23)
so x∈H and y∈L. Hence K∩Q=L​.
The map α is surjective. Given v+Q∈Rm/Q, use the surjectivity of ψ to choose x∈Rn with ψ(x)=ϕ(v). Set y=v−q(x). Then
ϕ(y)=ϕ(v)−ψ(x)=0,y+Q=v+Q.
(24)
Thus y∈K is a preimage of the arbitrary class v+Q.
By the first isomorphism theorem for modules, α induces the module isomorphism
α:kerϕ/L ≅ ​Rm/Imq,y+L⟼y+Imq​.
(25)
Explicitly, replacing y by y+ℓ with ℓ∈L leaves its class modulo Q unchanged, proving well-definedness. Its formula preserves addition and scalar multiplication. If its value is zero, y∈K∩Q=L, so y+L=0, proving injectivity; surjectivity was proved above. These checks give all the requested properties of the induced map.

Assume H=kerψ is a finitely generated module. The restriction q:H→L is a surjective module homomorphism, so part (a)(i) makes L a finitely generated module.
The free module Rm has its finite standard basis of a module. Its quotient module Rm/Imq is therefore a finitely generated module by part (a)(i). Part (c) transfers this property to the isomorphic quotient module kerϕ/L. Finally apply part (a)(ii) to the submodule L⊆kerϕ. It follows that
kerψ finitely generated ⟹ kerϕ finitely generated​.
(26)
More concretely, take the images under q of finitely many generators of H, and adjoin lifts in kerϕ of finitely many generators of kerϕ/L. This finite list generates kerϕ. The argument shows why finite presentation of a module is independent of the chosen finite generating set of a module.

The exact orders printed in the question are inconsistent. The source PDF says that the orders of group elements a,b,c are 4,12,24, respectively, and also imposes 3a+2b=0. Multiplying this relation by 6 and using 12b=0 gives 18a=0. Since 4a=0 also holds, subtracting 4(4a)=0 gives 2a=0, contradicting the stated order of a group element 4. Thus
no group satisfies all the hypotheses literally as printed​.
(27)
The natural abelian group presentation interpretation is to impose the relations 4a=12b=24c=0 and 3a+2b=0, with no further relations, without requiring the orders of the images of the generators to stay 4,12,24. Under this interpretation the calculation is as follows. Since 3a=−a follows from 4a=0, the mixed relation gives a=2b. Consequently 8b=0, and together with 12b=0 this gives 4b=0. Conversely, a=2b, 4b=0, and 24c=0 imply every original relation. Eliminating a leaves two independent cyclic groups:
Gpres​≅Z4​⊕Z24​,n=2,d1​=4,d2​=24​.
(28)
To verify that these relations force no further identifications, map a,b,c to (2,0),(1,0),(0,1) in Z4​⊕Z24​. All defining relations hold, and the inverse group homomorphism sends the two standard generators to b,c. These group homomorphisms are mutually inverse. The divisibility condition 4∣24 gives the requested invariant factors of a finitely generated module over Z. In this presented group, a,b,c actually have orders 2,4,24.
Equivalently, the relation matrix
​4003​01202​00240​​
(29)
has nonzero Smith normal form entries 1,4,24, with the trivial Z1​ factor omitted. The displayed direct sum of modules is the answer to the presentation interpretation; the inconsistency of the literal exact-order formulation remains.

Every multiple of dh is a multiple of both d and h, so dhZ⊆dZ∩hZ.
For the reverse inclusion, let z=du=hv lie in this intersection of ideals. Since d,h are coprime integers, Bézout's identity supplies r,s∈Z with rd+sh=1. Multiplying by u gives
u=rdu+shu=rhv+shu=h(rv+su).
(30)
Thus h∣u, and dh∣du=z. Consequently
dZ∩hZ=dhZ​.
(31)
Here Bézout's identity follows from the allowed principal ideal domain property of Z: the ideal dZ+hZ is generated by their greatest common divisor, which is 1.

kerθ=dZ∩hZ=dhZ.

Let N=∣G∣ be a square-free integer. If N=1, then G is the trivial cyclic group. Otherwise, finiteness means that a direct sum of modules decomposition into cyclic groups has only finitely many nontrivial factors, each of finite group order. Omit trivial factors and write
G≅Zn1​​⊕⋯⊕Znr​​,nj​>1,N=∏j=1r​nj​.
(36)
The integers ni​,nj​ are coprime integers whenever i=j. If a prime number p divided both, then p2∣N, contradicting the square-free integer hypothesis.
Moreover, n1​⋯nj−1​ and nj​ are coprime integers: any common prime divisor would divide one of the earlier factors as well as nj​. Thus part (b)(ii) can be applied repeatedly to combine factors, giving
G≅Zn1​⋯nr​​=ZN​​.
(37)
In particular G is a cyclic group. Under the displayed identifications, the element whose coordinate in every factor is the class of 1 is a generator of a group.

Write q=∣F∣. The unit group U(F)=F∖{0} is a finite abelian group of group order q−1, since multiplication in a field satisfies commutativity. The structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups, applied to this finite group, gives invariant factors of a finitely generated module
U(F)≅Zd1​​⊕⋯⊕Zdr​​,1<d1​∣d2​∣⋯∣dr​,
(38)
after omitting trivial factors. If there are no factors, the unit group is trivial and hence a cyclic group.
Otherwise put D=dr​. Every element u∈U(F) satisfies uD=1, because the order of a group element in each coordinate divides D. Hence all q−1 distinct nonzero elements of F are roots of a polynomial XD−1. The root bound for a polynomial over a field gives
q−1≤D.
(39)
For completeness, that bound follows by induction from the factor theorem: if a nonzero polynomial f vanishes at a, write f=(X−a)g; every other root of a polynomial is a root of g, because a field has no zero divisors. Each new distinct root therefore uses one unit of polynomial degree.
On the other hand, the group order in the decomposition is
q−1=d1​⋯dr​≥dr​=D.
(40)
Equality is forced, so d1​⋯dr−1​=1. As every retained factor is greater than 1, there can be only one. Consequently
U(F)≅Zq−1​ is cyclic​.
(41)
The same proof establishes the more general result finite multiplicative subgroup of a field is cyclic. The square-free integer result in part (c)(i) alone would not suffice, since q−1 need not be a square-free integer; the root bound for a polynomial rules out multiple invariant factors of a finitely generated module even then.
