Multiplication in a direct product of rings is coordinatewise. Thus (r1​,…,rn​) has a two-sided inverse exactly when each rj​ has a two-sided inverse in Rj​, and then
(r1​,…,rn​)−1=(r1−1​,…,rn−1​).
(1)
Consequently its unit group is the direct product of groups
U(R1​×⋯×Rn​)=U(R1​)×⋯×U(Rn​)​.
(2)
The identification preserves multiplication because both sides use coordinatewise multiplication. This is the unit group of a direct product of rings formula.

Write T=∏j=1n​Rj​, and let ρj​:T→Rj​ be the coordinate ring homomorphism. Set Ij​=ρj​(I). Each Ij​ is an ideal: it is an additive subgroup, and multiplying a lift in I on either side by a tuple with prescribed jth coordinate proves closure under left and right multiplication by Rj​.
Certainly I⊆∏j​Ij​. Conversely, let a=(a1​,…,an​)∈∏j​Ij​. For each j, choose b(j)∈I with jth coordinate aj​, and let ej​ have 1Rj​​ in coordinate j and zero elsewhere. Since I is an ideal, ej​b(j)∈I, and
a=∑j=1n​ej​b(j)∈I.
(3)
Therefore
I=I1​×⋯×In​​.
(4)
The finite sum is essential to this proof of ideals of a finite product of rings.

The element −1R​ lies in the unit group and satisfies (−1R​)2=1R​. Its order of a group element divides two and, by Lagrange's theorem, divides the odd number ∣U(R)∣. Its order is therefore one, so −1R​=1R​ and 2⋅1R​=0.
It follows that
F2​⟶R,a⟼a⋅1R​​
(5)
is a well-defined unital ring homomorphism. For a nonzero ring, this says its characteristic of a ring is two. If the zero ring is permitted, the same map still exists. This is the odd unit group forces characteristic two argument, with that harmless exception to the characteristic wording.

Since the irreducible element x is not a unit, the principal ideal (x) is proper. Suppose (x)⊆J⊆R. As R is a principal ideal domain, write J=(d). Then x=dr for some r∈R. The definition of an irreducible element says that d or r is a unit.
If d is a unit, then J=R. If r is a unit, then d and x are associate elements, so J=(x). Thus (x) is a maximal ideal. By the permitted criterion that a quotient ring by a maximal ideal is a field,
R/(x) is a field​.
(6)

Every multiple of x1​⋯xr​ is a multiple of each xj​, giving one inclusion. For the reverse inclusion, a principal ideal domain is a unique factorization domain, so each irreducible element xj​ is a prime element.
Let a∈⋂j=1r​(xj​). The case a=0 is immediate. Otherwise, write a=x1​b. Since x2​∣a and the prime element x2​ cannot divide the non-associate irreducible element x1​, it divides b. Write a=x1​x2​b2​, and repeat. At stage j, the prime element xj​ divides none of x1​,…,xj−1​, so it divides the remaining factor. Hence x1​⋯xr​∣a, proving
(x1​⋯xr​)=(x1​)∩⋯∩(xr​)​.
(7)

The polynomial ring F2​[X] is a euclidean domain under polynomial degree, hence a principal ideal domain. By part (b)(i), each
Kj​=F2​[X]/(fj​)
(8)
is a field. By polynomial division, every residue class has exactly one representative of polynomial degree less than nj​. Thus 1,X,…,Xnj​−1 form a basis over F2​, and ∣Kj​∣=2nj​.
For j=k, the ideals (fj​) and (fk​) are distinct maximal ideals, so they are comaximal ideals: their sum is an ideal properly containing (fj​) and must be the whole ring. The Chinese remainder theorem for ideals and part (b)(ii) therefore give the ring isomorphism
F2​[X]/(f1​⋯fr​) ≅ j=1∏r​Kj​,g​⟼(g+(fj​))j=1r​​.
(9)
In particular, this is a product of finite fields of the required sizes. The map preserves multiplication as well as addition and is surjective by the Chinese remainder theorem for ideals.

Choose a generator u of the cyclic group U(R), so up=1. Part (a)(iii) gives a unital ring homomorphism F2​→R. Its image lies in the centre of R, so even if R is not a commutative ring, evaluating a polynomial at u defines a ring homomorphism
ϕ:F2​[X]⟶R,∑j​aj​Xj⟼∑j​aj​uj.
(10)
Because ϕ(Xp−1)=0, it factors through the quotient ring to give
ϕ​:F2​[X]/(Xp−1)⟶R,X⟼u​.
(11)
Its image is the subring S=F2​[u]. Every unit of S is a unit of R, because an inverse in S also lies in R. Conversely, every member of U(R) is uj for some j, and both uj and its inverse up−j lie in S. Hence
U(S)=U(R)​.
(12)
The inverse argument matters: merely belonging to a subring would not by itself make an ambient unit invertible there.

Using the assumed square-free polynomial property, factor
Xp−1=f1​⋯fr​
(13)
into distinct monic polynomials that are irreducible polynomials, with nj​=degfj​. Part (b)(iii) identifies T=F2​[X]/(Xp−1) with ∏j​Kj​, where ∣Kj​∣=2nj​.
By the first isomorphism theorem for rings, the image S from part (c)(i) is T/J, where J is the kernel of a ring homomorphism. By part (a)(ii), J=∏j​Jj​ for ideals Jj​⊆Kj​. A field has only the zero ideal and the whole field as ideals, so each Kj​/Jj​ is either Kj​ or the zero ring. Consequently, for a subset D⊆{1,…,r},
S≅∏j∈D​Kj​.
(14)
This is the quotient of a finite product of fields principle. The subset is nonempty because ∣U(S)∣=p>2, whereas the zero ring has only one unit. By part (a)(i),
p=∣U(S)∣=j∈D∏​(2nj​−1)​.
(15)
Each factor is a positive integer. Since p is a prime number, exactly one factor is p and all remaining factors are one. Thus
p=2n−1 for some n≥2​,
(16)
so p is a Mersenne prime. If n=ab with a,b>1, then 2a−1 would be a proper divisor of 2ab−1; thus n is itself a prime number, although only the displayed form is needed.
The assumed square-free polynomial property can also be checked directly: in characteristic of a ring two, the formal derivative of Xp−1 is Xp−1 because p is odd. These two polynomials have greatest common divisor one, since X does not divide Xp−1, so no repeated irreducible polynomial factor occurs.

A subset E⊆M is a basis of a module if every m∈M has a unique expression
m=e∈E∑​re​e,re​∈R,re​=0 for all but finitely many e​.
(17)
Uniqueness concerns the entire family of coefficients, including the zero coefficients. Equivalently, E generates M and is linearly independent in a module: every finite relation ∑e∈H​re​e=0, with H⊆E, has all re​=0.
A module is a free module if it has a basis of a module. An infinite basis of a module still uses only finite sums in each expression; arbitrary infinite sums are not part of the module operations.

Solution

ii OurBigBook.com
Yes. An F-module over a field is a vector space. Start with a finite generating list. If it is linearly dependent, one vector has a nonzero coefficient in a linear relation; dividing by that coefficient expresses the vector using the others, so it can be removed without changing the span. Repeating terminates with a linearly independent generating list, which is a basis and therefore a basis of a module.
Thus every finitely generated module over a field is a free module. For the zero module, the empty list is the required basis of a module.

In the direct sum of modules M=N⊕P, every m∈M has a unique expression m=n+p, with n∈N and p∈P. Define
π:M→M,π(n+p)=p​.
(18)
The uniqueness makes this well-defined. For m=n+p, m′=n′+p′ and r∈R,
π(m+m′)=p+p′=π(m)+π(m′),π(rm)=rp=rπ(m),
(19)
so π is a module homomorphism. Finally, π(n+p)=0 exactly when p=0, equivalently m∈N. Therefore kerπ=N​. Its image is P, and π2=π, so it is the projection onto that direct summand.

Put N=⟨E′⟩ and P=⟨E′′⟩, the submodules generated by the two parts of the basis of a module. Every finite expression in the basis of a module splits into its E′ terms and its E′′ terms. Thus M=N+P.
If m∈N∩P, there are finite expressions
m=∑e∈H′​ae​e=∑e∈H′′​be​e,H′⊆E′,H′′⊆E′′.
(20)
Subtracting gives a finite relation among distinct members of E, because E′∩E′′=∅. Their linear independence in a module forces every coefficient to vanish, so m=0. Hence
M=⟨E′⟩⊕⟨E′′⟩​.
(21)
This proves the basis partition gives a module direct sum lemma, also when one part is empty.

Choose a countable generating family (nk​) for N. For each k, the expression of nk​ in the basis of a module E has a finite set Hk​ of nonzero coefficients, its coordinate support in a free module. Let
E′=⋃k​Hk​.
(22)
By the countable union of countable sets property, E′ is a countable set. Every nk​ belongs to ⟨E′⟩, so all finite R-linear combinations of the nk​ do too. Therefore
N⊆⟨E′⟩for a countable E′⊆E​.
(23)
Here and below, countable includes finite. The same construction applies to a finite or empty generating family. This is the countable basis support lemma.

The Gaussian integers form an integral domain, since they are a subring of the complex numbers. Use the Gaussian integer norm
N(a+bi)=a2+b2=∣a+bi∣2,
(42)
which is a positive integer for nonzero a+bi and satisfies N(αβ)=N(α)N(β).
For α,β∈Z[i] with β=0, write α/β=x+iy. Choose integers m,n with ∣x−m∣≤1/2 and ∣y−n∣≤1/2, put q=m+in, and set r=α−βq. Then
α=βq+r,N(r)=N(β)((x−m)2+(y−n)2)≤21​N(β)<N(β).
(43)
Thus division with remainder has either r=0 or a strictly smaller Gaussian integer norm. This proves that N is a euclidean function and
Z[i] is a Euclidean domain​.
(44)

Over a principal ideal domain D, an m-by-n matrix is in Smith normal form if all entries off the main diagonal vanish and its nonzero diagonal entries d1​,…,dr​ occur first and satisfy
d1​∣d2​∣⋯∣dr​​.
(45)
All remaining diagonal entries are zero. The zero matrix is allowed, with r=0. For an arbitrary matrix M, the Smith normal form theorem gives invertible matrices P∈GLm​(D) and Q∈GLn​(D) with
PMQ=diag(d1​,…,dr​,0,…,0),
(46)
where the right side is interpreted as a rectangular diagonal matrix when necessary. The dj​ are determined up to multiplication by units. Positivity can be required over Z, but is not a condition over the Gaussian integers.

Regard A as a matrix over the rational numbers. Part (a)(ii), with n=2, gives its characteristic polynomial X2+1. Therefore, writing i for the imaginary unit,
det(iI−A)=i2+1=0.
