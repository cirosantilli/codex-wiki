For a ring R, its ring characteristic is the least positive integer n with n1R​=0, or zero if no positive such integer exists. Equivalently it is the least positive integer satisfying nr=0 for every r∈R, because nr=(n1R​)r. This additive formulation also defines the characteristic of a rng when an identity is not assumed.
Let R be an integral domain. Since 1R​=0, its characteristic is not one. If its positive characteristic were a composite integer n=ab with 1<a,b<n, minimality would give a1R​=0 and b1R​=0, but
(a1R​)(b1R​)=n1R​=0.
(1)
This contradicts the absence of zero divisors. Therefore
charR=0or a prime number.​
(2)
This is the characteristic of an integral domain theorem. If an integral domain is defined without requiring an identity, the same conclusion follows from the additive definition: for a hypothetical composite characteristic n=ab, choose r with ar=0, which exists by minimality of n. Then (ar)(bs)=n(rs)=0 for every s, forcing bs=0 for every s and contradicting minimality again.

ii OurBigBook.com
No such ring exists. Characteristic two gives r+r=0 for every r. Choose a nonzero element a, which exists in a five-element set. Translation r↦r+a is an involution without fixed points: applying it twice gives r, while r+a=r would force a=0 by additive cancellation. Its disjoint pairs would partition all five elements, which is impossible because five is odd.
This argument applies even to a nonunital ring. With the unital convention, it is also the case n=2 of finite ring characteristic divides cardinality.

iii OurBigBook.com
No such ring exists: every finite integral domain is a field. Here is the proof. For a=0, the map La​:R→R defined by La​(x)=ax is injective, since ax=ay gives a(x−y)=0 and therefore x=y. An injective self-map of a finite set is surjective, so some x satisfies ax=1. Since an integral domain is commutative, xa=1 as well. Thus every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse, making R a field.
The conclusion still holds if an identity was not initially required. Surjectivity first gives an element e with ae=a. Then a(ex−x)=0 implies ex=x for every x, and commutativity makes e a two-sided identity. Applying the preceding argument with e supplies all inverses.

A suitable field with four elements is
R=F2​[t]/(t2+t+1).​
(3)
Write α for the class of t. Monic polynomial division over a ring gives a unique representative a+bα, with a,b∈F2​: existence follows by division, and uniqueness follows because a nonzero multiple of the degree-two modulus cannot have degree less than two. The four elements are therefore 0,1,α,α+1.
The defining relation gives α2=α+1, and hence α(α+1)=1. Thus the nonzero elements have inverses
1−1=1,α−1=α+1,(α+1)−1=α.
(4)
The quotient ring is commutative and every nonzero element is a unit, so it is a field. This verifies the field property directly, without quoting a theorem about irreducible-polynomial quotients.

v OurBigBook.com
No field has six elements. A finite field has positive characteristic: the sequence 0,1,2⋅1,… must repeat. By part (a), this characteristic is a prime number p. Translation by 1 partitions the field into cycles of length p, since returning to a starting point after k steps is equivalent to k1=0. Thus p divides six and p=2 or p=3.
The multiples of 1 form a copy of the prime field Fp​. For completeness, the inverse of a nonzero residue a lies among these multiples: the integer Bezout identity ab+cp=1 gives (a1)(b1)=1. The entire field is a vector space over this subfield. A maximal linearly independent set is finite and spans, so it is a basis of some positive size d. Unique basis coordinates give exactly pd elements. This proves finite field cardinality is a prime power here. But six is neither a power of two nor a power of three, a contradiction.

vi OurBigBook.com
Under the unital ring convention used elsewhere in this wiki, no such pair exists. In a two-element ring the elements are 0 and 1. Additive cancellation forces 1+1=0, and the identity axiom forces 1⋅1=1. The distributive property forces products involving zero to vanish. Every operation is therefore determined, giving the prime field F2​ as the unique possibility up to ring isomorphism.
If the word ring allows a nonunital ring, there are instead two non-isomorphic examples: F2​ and the zero-multiplication rng on the additive group Z/2Z. In the second example the unique nonzero element u satisfies u+u=0 and u2=0; every product is zero, so associativity and both distributive laws hold. An isomorphism would have to send 1 to u because it preserves zero and is bijective, but then it would send 12=1 to both u2=0 and u, which is impossible. These exhaust the rngs with two elements, since u2 can only be 0 or u. Specifying the identity convention is essential for this part.

A Euclidean domain is an integral domain R with a Euclidean function ν:R∖{0}→Z≥0​ such that, for a,b∈R with b=0, there exist q,r∈R satisfying
a=qb+r,r=0 or ν(r)<ν(b).
(5)
The polynomial ring Q[x] has this property with ν(f)=degf. To perform polynomial division, subtract an appropriate multiple of the divisor to cancel the leading term of the current remainder. Its polynomial degree decreases strictly at each step, so the process terminates below the divisor's degree. The required division of leading coefficients is possible in the field Q. Thus a polynomial ring over a field is Euclidean, with the same argument applying to every field.
Now let I be a nonzero ideal of a Euclidean domain, and choose b∈I∖{0} with the least possible value of ν(b). For every a∈I, divide a=qb+r. The remainder belongs to I, since r=a−qb. If it were nonzero, its smaller Euclidean value would contradict the choice of b. Thus r=0 and every a∈I lies in (b). Conversely (b)⊆I because I is an ideal. Hence I=(b). The zero ideal is already (0), proving every Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain.

The three answers, in the given order, are no, yes, yes.
For Z[x], consider the ideal I=(2,x). Its members have even constant coefficient, so 1∈/I and the ideal is proper. If I=(g), then g divides the constant 2, forcing g to be constant by additivity of polynomial degree in this integral domain. Since g also divides x, it must divide the coefficient 1, giving g=±1. That would make I the whole ring, a contradiction. Thus Z[x] is not a principal ideal domain, and part (a) shows it cannot be a Euclidean domain. This is the nonprincipal ideal generated by a prime and an indeterminate obstruction.
Evaluation at i gives the ring isomorphism
Z[x]/(x2+1)≅Z[i].​
(6)
Indeed, division by the monic polynomial x2+1 leaves a unique remainder a+bx, and its value a+bi is zero only when both integer coefficients vanish. Thus the quotient maps bijectively, with operations preserved, onto the Gaussian integers. These form a Euclidean domain for the Gaussian integer norm N(a+bi)=a2+b2, a standard Euclidean example allowed in this part. Geometrically, rounding the real and imaginary coordinates gives a quotient error with squared modulus at most 1/2<1.
Finally,
Z[x,y]/(2,x)≅F2​[y],​
(7)
via f(x,y)↦f(0,y)mod2. Every polynomial over F2​ occurs. The kernel consists exactly of polynomials whose x-independent part has even coefficients: write f(x,y)=xg(x,y)+h(y) to see that this is precisely (2,x). Hence the induced quotient map is a bijective ring homomorphism. Its target is a Euclidean domain with Euclidean function deg: a polynomial ring over a field is Euclidean by the division algorithm in part (a).

Since ω2=−1−ω, products of integer linear combinations of 1,ω remain of the same form. Thus the Eisenstein integers E=Z[ω] form a subring of C, and hence an integral domain. Their Eisenstein norm is
N(a+bω)=(a+bω)(a+bω)=a2−ab+b2=(a−2b​)2+43​b2.
(8)
It is a positive integer on nonzero elements and is multiplicative, because the complex modulus is multiplicative.
For α,β∈E with β=0, write α/β=u+vω with real u,v. Choose integers m,n with ∣u−m∣,∣v−n∣≤1/2, and let q=m+nω. With s=u−m and t=v−n,
​βα​−q​2=s2−st+t2≤s2+∣st∣+t2≤43​<1.
(9)
Consequently r=α−qβ belongs to E and satisfies N(r)≤43​N(β)<N(β) when r=0. This proves E is a Euclidean domain with Euclidean function N, the Euclidean norm on the Eisenstein integers.
For the representation of the prime p, first note that 3=N(1−ω). Now suppose p>3 and p∣n2+3. The factorization
n2+3=(n+1+2ω)(n−1−2ω)
(10)
shows that the Eisenstein integer p divides a product. It divides neither factor: an element a+bω lies in pE exactly when both integer coefficients are divisible by p, and the second coefficients here are 2 and −2. Hence p is not a prime element of E.
To justify the necessary factorization step, in any principal ideal domain an irreducible element π is prime. If π∤a, write (π,a)=(d). Since d∣π, irreducibility makes d a unit or an associate of π. The latter would give π∣a, so d is a unit and uπ+va=1 for some u,v. If π∣ab, multiplication of this identity by b gives π∣b. This proves irreducible elements of a principal ideal domain are prime without assuming unique factorization.
By part (a), E is a principal ideal domain. The integer p has norm p2>1, so it is a nonzero nonunit. Since it is not prime, the result just proved implies that it is reducible: p=uv with u,v both nonunits. A nonzero element of E is a unit exactly when its norm is one, since N(z)=1 gives inverse z and multiplicativity proves the converse. Thus
1<N(u),N(v),N(u)N(v)=N(p)=p2.
(11)
The only possible positive integer factors exceeding one are N(u)=N(v)=p. Writing u=a+bω gives
a2−ab+b2=p.​
(12)
This is the prime representation by the Eisenstein norm argument.

Write d=dimV. A cyclic vector for S is a vector v whose successive iterates span V; equivalently, when d>0,
v,Sv,…,Sd−1v
(13)
are a basis. The equivalence follows because the first linear dependence in the iterates makes their preceding span invariant under S, so no later iterate can enlarge it.
In this basis there are unique coefficients c0​,…,cd−1​ with Sdv=−∑j<d​cj​Sjv. Put f(t)=td+∑j<d​cj​tj. Then f(S)v=0, and commutation with powers of S gives f(S)Sjv=Sjf(S)v=0. Therefore f(S) kills an entire basis and is the zero map. No nonzero polynomial of degree less than d annihilates v, because that would be a linear dependence of the displayed basis. Thus mS​=f.
The matrix of S in the cyclic basis is the companion matrix C(f) with ones on its subdiagonal and last column (−c0​,…,−cd−1​)T. Expanding det(tI−C(f)) along its last column gives
c0​+c1​t+⋯+cd−2​td−2+(t+cd−1​)td−1=f(t):
(14)
the cofactors are 1,t,…,td−1. This proves the characteristic polynomial of a companion matrix formula here. Hence
mS​(t)=cS​(t)=f(t).​
(15)
This proves that a cyclic operator has equal minimal and characteristic polynomials. If V={0}, both polynomials are one by convention, so the equality remains valid.

The rational canonical form of T is a block diagonal matrix
C(f1​)⊕⋯⊕C(fr​),f1​∣f2​∣⋯∣fr​,
(16)
similar to a matrix of T, where the fj​∈F[t] are monic nonconstant polynomials and the sum of their degrees is dimV. They are the invariant factors of a linear operator. The block C(fj​) is the companion matrix for fj​, representing multiplication by t on F[t]/(fj​). The invariant factors are uniquely determined by T.
Every companion block has a cyclic vector and therefore has both minimal polynomial and characteristic polynomial equal to fj​, by part (a). The determinant of a block diagonal matrix is the product of its block determinants, giving
cT​(t)=j=1∏r​fj​(t).​
(17)
A polynomial h annihilates the whole matrix exactly when it annihilates every block. On block j, dividing h by fj​ leaves a remainder of smaller degree; if that remainder annihilated the block without being zero, it would contradict minimality of fj​. Thus the annihilation condition is exactly fj​∣h for every j. The monic polynomial of least degree with this property is their least common multiple. The divisibility chain makes this fr​, so
mT​(t)=fr​(t).​
(18)
These are the minimal and characteristic polynomials from invariant factors formulae. On the zero vector space the product is empty and both polynomials are defined to be one.
