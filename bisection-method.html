Bisection method (mk​=(ak​+bk​)/2)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Numerical analysis Bracketing method
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 4
The bisection method finds a zero of a function by repeatedly halving a root bracket. Let f be a real-valued continuous function on the closed interval [a,b], with a<b. First check for endpoint zeros of a function; otherwise require strictly opposite signs at the endpoints. Evaluate the midpoint and retain the half with strictly opposite endpoint signs, stopping immediately if the midpoint is a zero of a function. The intermediate value theorem ensures that every retained root bracket still contains a zero of a function. This is the construction in MIT's root-finding notes.[ref]
For a worked square root calculation, use the polynomial f(x)=x2−2 on [1,2]. Write [ak​,bk​] for the root bracket after k halvings, with [a0​,b0​]=[1,2]. The candidate evaluated during update k is the old midpoint mk−1​:
update k1234​candidate mk−1​1.51.251.3751.4375​f(mk−1​)0.25−0.4375−0.1093750.06640625​retained [ak​,bk​][1,1.5][1.25,1.5][1.375,1.5][1.375,1.4375]​​
(10)
The initial midpoint is m0​=1.5. After four halvings the new midpoint is m4​=1.40625, and the bisection error bound is ∣m4​−2​∣≤0.03125. Its actual error is approximately 0.00796356237310. The last tested candidate m3​=1.4375 and the reported midpoint m4​ are different points.
The useful feature of the bisection method is that the retained width halves on every update, independently of the shape of the function graph. The bisection error bound therefore supplies an accuracy guarantee before the zero of a function is known. No derivative is needed, and a valid initial root bracket gives convergence without a close initial guess. This makes the bisection method a dependable fallback for the faster Newton root-finding iteration.
