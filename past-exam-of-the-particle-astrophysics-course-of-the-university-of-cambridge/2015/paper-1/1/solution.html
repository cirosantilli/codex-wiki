Cosmological variables and conventions. The scale factor R(t) describes the relative separation of comoving observers; dots denote derivatives with respect to cosmic time t. The Hubble parameter is H(t)=R˙/R, usually time dependent; “Hubble constant” conventionally means its present value. Here ρ is mass-equivalent density, so ϵ=ρc2 is energy density, while P is isotropic pressure. G is the gravitational constant and c the speed of light. The constant K specifies cosmological spatial curvature: with R a length, K=kc2 and k=+1,0,−1 describe closed, flat and open spatial geometries. Alternative normalizations of R move a constant factor into K. In the printed equations Λ has units s−2: it is c2 times a cosmological constant defined in inverse square metres. This time-unit convention for the cosmological constant prevents a missing factor of c2.

It expresses local energy conservation, including pressure work. It does not assert that U is constant: radiation loses internal energy as it does work; negative pressure makes U grow during expansion. A generic expanding spacetime has no global time-translation symmetry from which a conserved total energy follows.

The acoustic ruler and accelerated expansion. Before baryon drag becomes ineffective, pressure in the photon-baryon fluid drives acoustic waves around initial linear cosmological density perturbations. Their comoving sound horizon at the baryon drag epoch is

After decoupling, this preferred separation survives in galaxy clustering as baryon acoustic oscillations: a peak in the two-point correlation function and oscillations in the matter power spectrum. Its value is calibrated by early-universe physics, commonly constrained by the cosmic microwave background. The SDSS luminous-red-galaxy measurement established a peak near 100h−1 Mpc; its use as a distance indicator depends on the ruler calibration rather than the length of an individual galaxy.[ref]

so sufficiently precise measurements of the shape of H(z) can test q<0. In general relativity, accelerated expansion requires a sufficiently negative total ρ+3P/c2; a positive cosmological constant supplies it.
A single acoustic angular scale does not independently determine acceleration, cosmological spatial curvature, the absolute sound horizon and the dark-energy equation of state. One combines redshift coverage with early-universe calibration and independent matter-density or distance information, comparing accelerating and nonaccelerating models. The galaxy selection function and redshift completeness must be modeled with random catalogues, survey covariance estimated with mock catalogues, and redshift-space distortions, galaxy bias and nonlinear broadening controlled. These tests distinguish a cosmological feature from a survey artifact. The evidence is the measured expansion history within an explicit cosmological framework, rather than the existence of an acoustic peak alone.
