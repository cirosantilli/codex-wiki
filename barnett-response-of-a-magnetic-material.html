Barnett response of a magnetic material

The two-state calculation is not a general formula for a ferromagnet. Exchange interactions, magnetic domains, magnetic domain-wall pinning, magnetic anisotropy, and hysteresis can determine which response is accessible. Both spin angular momentum and orbital angular momentum can contribute; a single constant gyromagnetic ratio may be only a restricted effective description. Rotation sets an internal bias, while the material and its preparation determine magnetization. The Barkhausen effect illustrates why a smooth bias can produce jumps instead of the toy smooth curve.
For a limited, reversible, isotropic linear model, suppose the same intrinsic volume magnetic susceptibility χv​ responds to the applied bias and to BΩ​/μ0​. For a uniformly magnetized ellipsoid along a principal axis, include its demagnetizing factor Nd​:
Hint​=Happ​−Nd​Mz​,Mz​=χv​(Happ​−Nd​Mz​+μ0​BΩ​​).
(73)
Solving this linear equation yields
Mz​=1+Nd​χv​χv​​(Happ​+μ0​γΩ​).
(74)
Here Hint​ and Happ​ are actual magnetic field intensities; the added rotational bias is an equivalent drive in the constitutive response. In SI, dimensionless χv​ multiplies an intensity in A/m, so Mz​=χv​Ω/γ would omit μ0​ and have the wrong units. A susceptibility defined instead as ∂M/∂B has different units. State the convention before comparing formulas.
At zero applied magnetic field intensity, a stipulated χv​=3 gives prefactor 3/2 in a sphere, with Nd​=1/3, and a prefactor approaching 3 in a long axial ellipsoid. This is a geometry check of the assumed linear model. A real cylinder does not have an exactly uniform demagnetizing field, and an irreversible ferromagnet cannot in general be represented by this single linear equation. Inferring an intrinsic gyromagnetic ratio requires an independently calibrated material response and a geometry-dependent conversion of the measured stray magnetic field.
