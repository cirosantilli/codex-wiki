Linear step-growth molecule count (N=N0​−J)

 Home Chemistry Polymer Polymerization Step-growth polymerization
OurBigBook.com Words: 457 Articles: 1
Use an ideal feed in which every starting molecule has exactly two suitable functional groups: either one A and one B, or two A groups mixed with two B groups. Only an A–B pairing can form a joining chemical bond. Assume no loss of feed-derived material, no splitting of chains, no competing consumption of the counted functional groups, no branching, and no cyclization in step-growth polymerization. Linear here describes connectivity; a chain can bend in space. Small byproducts of polycondensation do not enter the chain-bearing molecule count.
Let N0​ count all initial feed molecules, J the number of intermolecular joins, and N all remaining chain-bearing molecules, including unreacted monomers. Each join turns two molecules into one, so N=N0​−J exactly. If chain i contains ji​ feed-derived monomeric units, conservation gives ∑i=1N​ji​=N0​. Consequently the number-average degree of polymerization is
Xn​=N∑i=1N​ji​​=N0​−JN0​​.
(74)
This is a count of both feed species' monomeric units, rather than a count of interior constitutional repeating units. For AA+BB chemistry an interior repeat often contains two feed-derived units, as explained in monomer. Equal functional group reactivities are not needed for this bookkeeping at a specified J; predicting a time course or a chain-length probability distribution requires additional assumptions.
For the figure's eight AB feed molecules, three joins can give lengths (2,3,1,1,1), so N=5 and Xn​=8/5. Joining the first two chains gives (5,1,1,1): the incorporated total stays eight, N becomes four, and Xn​=2. A graph makes the bookkeeping visible: each starting monomer molecule is a vertex of a graph, and each intermolecular joining chemical bond connects two previously distinct connected components of a graph. The connected pieces are the counted molecules.
