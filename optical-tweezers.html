Optical tweezers

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 13
Optical tweezers hold and move a small particle using the force exerted by a tightly focused beam of light, usually from a laser. They can position transparent beads in a liquid and manipulate suitable biological cells. Attaching a molecule to a trapped bead turns the bead into a handle: a measured bead displacement, together with a calibrated spring constant, reveals the force exerted on the attachment. This makes microscopic manipulation and measurement possible without a mechanical tool touching the bead. Living biological cells were manipulated with infrared beams by Ashkin, Dziedzic and Yamane in 1987; Svoboda and Block used trapped beads to measure forces from individual molecules in 1994. [ref] [ref].
Why can light pull a bead towards a focus? Light carries momentum. A transparent bead with a higher refractive index than the surrounding liquid bends transmitted light rays by refraction, changing their momentum; conservation of momentum requires an opposite transfer to the bead. In the geometrical optics picture, unequal illumination of a displaced bead produces a net restoring force towards the brighter region. For a particle much smaller than the wavelength, a physical optics description replaces that ray picture. The detailed force depends on particle size, refractive index, beam shape and wavelength; the qualitative attraction does not supply a universal formula for every particle.
The optical gradient force draws a suitable transparent bead towards high illumination, while the optical scattering force generally pushes along beam propagation. A stable three-dimensional optical trap needs a balance with a restoring force in each direction: transverse confinement alone does not ensure axial confinement. Strong focusing can make the backward axial gradient contribution large enough to balance the forward scattering contribution, with the equilibrium displaced from the focus. Higher refractive index alone is insufficient; absorbing particles, different index contrasts and different beam geometries can behave differently. [ref].
Historically, Ashkin's 1970 experiments accelerated and trapped particles using radiation pressure. The 1986 paper by Ashkin, Dziedzic, Bjorkholm and Chu demonstrated the single-beam gradient optical trap for transparent particles. These are distinct steps: the earlier work established optical manipulation, and the later work supplied the tightly focused single-beam arrangement. [ref] [ref].
Near a stable equilibrium, a harmonic optical trap behaves like a spring; thermal calibration of an optical trap uses its Brownian motion to determine the effective spring constant. The later passage through a translating optical trap sections instead consider a moving localized potential energy, which need not remain harmonic throughout a passage.
