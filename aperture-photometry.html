Aperture photometry (r=(S−nb)/Δt)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Astrophysics Light curve
OurBigBook.com Words: 248
Aperture photometry estimates brightness by summing calibrated detector pixels in an extraction region and subtracting the estimated background in that region. For n equally weighted pixels, let S be their total accumulated signal in electrons, b the estimated background electrons per pixel over the same exposure, and Δt the exposure duration. Then r=(S−nb)/Δt is a background-subtracted electron rate. Kepler applied aperture photometry to calibrated pixels with a fitted sky background. [ref].
This measurement trades source collection against background: enlarging the extraction region can retain more starlight but includes more sky brightness and neighbouring sources. Photon shot noise, read noise, and uncertainty in background subtraction contribute to its uncertainty. Dividing r by a consistently measured reference rate gives a dimensionless light curve; it does not supply an absolute observed radiative flux. If contaminating light remains, the reference includes it and the inferred exoplanet transit depth suffers transit dilution.
In the first toy figure, the source's reference rate is 100,000 electrons per second, the summed sky contribution is 4,000 electrons per second, and a known multiplicative response is g(t)=1+0.0003t with t in hours from mid-transit. The artificial exoplanet transit depth is 0.010, its total duration four hours, and its flat-bottom duration 2.8 hours; the straight ingress and egress are a toy approximation, not an exact exoplanet transit photometry model. Each synthetic sample averages a 0.5-hour exposure and adds seeded normal distribution noise with relative standard deviation 0.0006. Subtracting the known sky rate and dividing by the known response and reference rate recovers the dip. Real observations require estimating those corrections and carrying their uncertainty.
