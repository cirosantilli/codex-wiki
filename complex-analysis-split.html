Complex analysis

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 33k Articles: 525
Complex analysis studies functions of a complex number, especially holomorphic functions. Its distinctive feature is that a single complex derivative controls variation in every direction of the complex plane. The map f(z)=z2 gives a first picture: straight coordinate lines become curved, yet small perpendicular arrows remain perpendicular wherever f′(z)=0.
The bottom panels show displacements rather than absolute positions. Multiplication by f′(1+i)=2+2i predicts the image of each small arrow; the exact image differs by h2. The origin is exceptional because f′(0)=0. This distinction between local conformal maps and a critical point is central: complex differentiability imposes strong geometric structure, without making every holomorphic function globally one-to-one.
The remarkable next step is that holomorphic functions are determined inside a circle by their values on its boundary. Precisely, let R>0, let D={z:∣z−a∣<R}, and suppose f is a holomorphic function on an open set containing the closed disc D. Orient its boundary counterclockwise. The Cauchy integral formula states that, for every w∈D,
f(w)=2πi1​∮∣z−a∣=R​z−wf(z)​dz.
(1)
The contour integral combines boundary values with a kernel depending on the requested interior point. It is a reconstruction formula, not merely a trick for evaluating an integral. The hypothesis about the closed disc ensures that all boundary values and the contour integral are well defined. [ref]
At the centre, substituting z=a+Reiθ cancels the denominator against dz=iReiθdθ:
f(a)=2π1​∫02π​f(a+Reiθ)dθ.
(2)
Thus the centre value is the angular average of the boundary values. For the polynomial f(z)=z2, a=1+i and R=1/2, expand the boundary data as
f(a+Reiθ)=2i+(1+i)eiθ+41​e2iθ.
(3)
Both oscillating terms have zero average, leaving f(a)=2i. Away from the centre the weights change: on the unit circle,
f(w)=2π1​∫02π​1−we−iθf(eiθ)​dθ,∣w∣<1.
(4)
With f(z)=z2 and w=1/2, expanding the denominator as a geometric series leaves only its second-power term after integration, giving f(1/2)=1/4. This also explains how the Cauchy integral formula recovers an entire Taylor series from boundary data.
When a holomorphic function has isolated singularities, a different boundary calculation detects their residues. Suppose Γ is a counterclockwise simple closed contour and f is holomorphic on a neighbourhood of it and its interior except at finitely many interior isolated singularities aj​. The residue theorem gives
∮Γ​f(z)dz=2πi∑j​Res(f,aj​).
(5)
A residue is the coefficient of (z−aj​)−1 in the Laurent series. A short proof idea is to deform the contour to small circles around the isolated singularities using the Cauchy integral theorem. Integrating the Laurent series on each circle kills every power except −1. [ref]
Here is how a complex pole determines a real improper integral. Set g(z)=1/(1+z2) and use the contour consisting of the segment from −R to R, followed by the upper semicircle AR​, with R>1. Only the simple pole i lies inside, and
Res(g,i)=limz→i​(z−i)(z+i)z−i​=2i1​,∫−RR​1+x2dx​+∫AR​​g(z)dz=π.
(6)
The arc must actually disappear. On AR​, the reverse triangle inequality gives ∣1+z2∣≥R2−1, and its length is πR, so
​∫AR​​g(z)dz​≤R2−1πR​⟶0.
(7)
The real integrand is bounded by x−2 for ∣x∣≥1, so both tails converge separately. Taking the limit therefore proves the ordinary improper integral
∫−∞∞​1+x2dx​=π.
(8)
For example, at R=10 the real segment contributes 2arctan(10)≈2.942255, and the arc contributes 0.199337, below the bound 10π/99≈0.317333. The usefulness of the residue theorem is that the same strategy works for many rational functions whose real antiderivatives are cumbersome: find the enclosed poles, compute their residues, and control the connecting arc. [ref]
Historically, Cauchy's 1825 memoir on integrals with imaginary limits develops complex contour integration and its use in transforming and evaluating definite integrals. The opening sections define complex path integrals, discuss changing the path, and examine what happens near singular points. These are recognizable ancestors of the modern link between holomorphic functions, contours and residues. [ref]
