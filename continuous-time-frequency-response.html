Continuous-time frequency response (G(iω))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Control theory Transfer function
OurBigBook.com Words: 293 Articles: 1
The continuous-time frequency response is the frequency response of a continuous-time linear time-invariant system. From zero initial state, a BIBO stable system driven by a sustained sinusoid u(t)=Acos(ωt) approaches the steady response
yss​(t)=A∣G(iω)∣cos(ωt+argG(iω)).
(56)
Any decaying initial or switching transient is separate. The modulus ∣G(iω)∣ is the amplitude gain; the complex argument is the shift in oscillation phase. Thus evaluating a transfer function at s=iω measures both how much of an oscillation passes through and how far it lags.
For the first-order low-pass filter, with K>0, direct complex number arithmetic gives
G(iω)=1+iωτK​,∣G(iω)∣=1+(ωτ)2​K​,argG(iω)=−arctan(ωτ).
(57)
The phase uses the inverse tangent branch between −π/2 and π/2. Low angular frequencies have gain close to K and little lag. High angular frequencies have gain approximately K/(ωτ) and lag approaching π/2. This is why the model is a first-order low-pass filter: it suppresses fast variation compared with slow variation. Rowell relates frequency response gain and phase to poles and then introduces Bode plots in MIT 2.004, lecture 32, sections 1 and 2.
At the corner angular frequency,
ωc​=τ1​,∣G(iωc​)∣=2​K​,argG(iωc​)=−4π​.
(58)
For K=2 and τ=3s, ωc​=1/3rads−1, the gain is 2​≈1.4142, and the lag is 45 degrees. The angular frequency ω is measured in radians per second; ordinary frequency f is measured in hertz (cycles per second), with ω=2πf. Consequently the same corner is fc​=1/(2πτ)≈0.0531Hz, not 1/3Hz.
