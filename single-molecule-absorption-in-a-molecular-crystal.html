Single-molecule absorption in a molecular crystal

 Home Chemistry Molecule Single-molecule experiment
OurBigBook.com Words: 221
In 1989, William E. Moerner and Lothar Kador reported individual pentacene absorbers in para-terphenyl in Optical detection and spectroscopy of single molecules in a solid by W. E. Moerner and L. Kador (pp. 2535–2538). Different local environments spread photon absorption frequencies; tuning into the sparse wings selected few resonant molecules despite many molecules in the illuminated volume.
Their tunable dye laser focused through a 0.85 numerical aperture lens onto a 100–200μm crystal immersed in superfluid helium at 1.6K. Frequency modulation accompanied either a 5kHz electric field between coated sapphire disks, detected at 10kHz, or a 2MHz quartz shear transducer. An avalanche photodiode measured transmitted-light photon absorption, not fluorescence. Double modulation suppressed residual laser amplitude background.[ref]
Repeated scans gave the predicted modulated line shape. Blank crystals and off-resonance scans gave background; changing modulation frequency expanded and contracted the feature as predicted. Agreement between both perturbation methods supported the individual-absorber inference. The authors acknowledged out-of-focus molecules and the unlikely alternative of multiple absorbers with exactly coincident frequencies.[ref]
This is evidence for a selected absorber in a prepared, known chemical substance and host. It does not establish arbitrary unknown molecule identity from one spectral feature, provide an atom-resolved photograph, or count every molecule in the crystal. A measurement sensitive to only a narrow excitation range cannot detect a molecule whose transition lies outside that range. This distinction separates the total particle inventory from the number responding to a particular probe.
