Front velocity (vf​)

The front velocity is the propagation speed of the earliest boundary of a newly introduced disturbance's support. It asks when an initially unaffected observer can first respond, whereas group velocity asks how a narrow wave envelope translates. A detector threshold defines a practical arrival time, but can miss arbitrarily small earlier responses; a packet peak is another distinct timing convention. In a causal dielectric model, a high-angular frequency contour argument gives exactly zero field for t<x/c, even when phase velocity exceeds c. This is a statement about that model's relativistic causality, not a universal identification of group velocity with front velocity.[ref]
For the stipulated Klein-Gordon equation with compactly supported real Cauchy data, front velocity is bounded by c, and c is the model's maximal speed of influence. A short energy method outline uses

Integration outside an expanding interval shows that zero initial energy there remains zero. Thus initial support in [−a,a] cannot influence x>a before t=(x−a)/c. The positive energy density includes the restoring term ω02​u2/2: this adds oscillation behind the front without extending its domain of dependence. A particular disturbance may fail to excite the leading boundary, so the maximal speed is not a promise of a detectable response for every source.
A Gaussian function is nonzero arbitrarily far away, so the Gaussian envelope in a Klein-Gordon medium has no sharp initial support boundary. Its small distant tails are already in the initial data. Likewise, keeping only a narrow interval of the frequency spectrum cannot reproduce an exact abrupt onset. Cutting off a Gaussian tail changes its Fourier transform and the accompanying initial velocity; it is a different initial-value problem. The exponential initial-velocity tail for the Klein-Gordon equation is another concrete example of a pre-existing tail outside the main disturbance.
The water-tank measurement in phase velocity records free surface elevation with calibrated resistance probes and uses a ruler and stopwatch for an independent check. The inspected protocol calls the front or back of a group a timing feature. That visible edge establishes an envelope transit estimate, not the first nonzero disturbance in the sense of front velocity.[ref] A proposed additional control would repeat the transit fit at several detection thresholds and train durations, using matched probe calibration and the same sampling rate. Dependence on the threshold diagnoses wave envelope reshaping or limited sensitivity; records should be compared before reflections return. Such a test could expose limitations of a group velocity estimate, but would not establish an exact causal front or validate the stipulated Klein-Gordon equation for water waves.
