Take nonrelativistic quantum mechanics, a short-range interaction, a fixed heavy target, and a common projectile mass m. An incoming plane wave in atomic state a has unit coefficient. In an open scattering channel i, the target finishes in a normalized atomic state χi​, and the projectile has positive velocity magnitude vi​=ℏki​/m. At large distance,

Here Ω denotes direction, dΩ is a solid angle element, and εi​ is an internal atomic energy. Different χi​ are orthogonal. The scattering amplitude fia​ has units of length. A closed scattering channel has no propagating outgoing probability current at this total energy.
The incoming probability current density is jin​=va​. The outgoing radial probability current density in channel i is jr,i​∼vi​∣fia​∣2/r2. Multiplying by the area r2dΩ and dividing by jin​ gives the differential scattering cross-section:
dΩdσi​​=va​vi​​∣fia​(Ω)∣2.

The r−2 dilution cancels the detector's area growth. The velocity ratio remains: a squared wave coefficient alone is not generally a flux fraction. A differential scattering cross-section has units of area per solid angle, rather than being a normalized directional probability density. The denominator specifies the event selection; it need not equal one square unit of area. Absolute rates require incident flux, target number and detector efficiency in addition to these relative weights.
