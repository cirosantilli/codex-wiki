The extension. Urysohn's lemma states that disjoint closed subsets A,B of a normal topological space can be separated by a continuous function u:X→[0,1] with u∣A​=0 and u∣B​=1.
For the bounded Tietze extension theorem, first suppose g:C→[−M,M] is continuous. The subsets A={g≤−M/3} and B={g≥M/3} are closed in X, because C is closed. Urysohn's lemma supplies a continuous function h:X→[−M/3,M/3] with h=−M/3 on A and h=M/3 on B. Directly checking the two outer ranges and the middle range of g gives
∥h∥∞​≤M/3,∥g−h∣C​∥∞​≤2M/3.
(1)
Empty level sets cause no difficulty: take the appropriate constant, or zero if both are empty. This is the Urysohn two-thirds approximation.
Apply this successively to the residual on C, beginning with f and M0​=1. We get hn​ with ∥hn​∥∞​≤31​(32​)n and residual after the first n terms at most (2/3)n. The Weierstrass M-test gives uniform convergence of F=∑n≥0​hn​. By the uniform limit theorem, F is continuous; the residual bound proves F∣C​=f, and the sum of the norm bounds is one. Thus
F:X→[−1,1] is continuous and F∣C​=f.​
(2)
Closed sets as exact zero sets. If X is a perfectly normal space, write a closed C as ⋂n≥0​Un​ with each Un​ open. Apply Urysohn's lemma to C and X∖Un​ to obtain fn​:X→[0,1] vanishing on C and equal to one on X∖Un​. The uniformly convergent series
f=n≥0∑​2−n−1fn​satisfiesf−1(0)=C.​
(3)
Indeed all summands vanish on C; outside C, at least one summand is strictly positive. The uniform limit theorem proves continuity. This realizes the closed G-delta set as a zero set construction.
Conversely, suppose each closed set is the zero set of a continuous function. For disjoint closed C,D, select f,g:X→[0,1] with zero sets C,D. Their sum never vanishes, and
h=f+gf​:X→[0,1],h−1(0)=C,h−1(1)=D.​
(4)
The quotient is continuous. This also proves normality by inverse images of disjoint endpoint neighborhoods. Each zero set is a G-delta set, since f−1(0)=⋂n≥1​f−1([0,1/n)); thus this property characterizes perfectly normal spaces among T1 spaces.
Subspaces. Let Y⊆X and let A,B be disjoint closed subsets of Y. Put C=AX and D=BX. Since C∩Y=A and D∩Y=B, the intersection C∩D misses Y. Choose continuous functions f,g on X whose zero sets are C,D. On the open neighborhood W=X∖(C∩D) of Y, the same quotient f/(f+g) is continuous and is zero on A, one on B. Its inverse images of [0,1/3) and (2/3,1], restricted to Y, are disjoint open neighborhoods. Therefore every subspace is a normal topological space: perfect normality implies hereditary normality.
A strict counterexample to the converse. Let D be an uncountable discrete space, and let X=D∪{∞} be its one-point compactification. Its neighborhoods of ∞ have finite complement in D, because compact subsets of a discrete space are finite. The space is compact Hausdorff, hence a T1 space and normal. A subspace missing ∞ is discrete. A subspace containing ∞ is closed in X, since its complement is a subset of the open discrete part; it is therefore compact Hausdorff and normal. Thus all subspaces are normal.
But {∞} is not a G-delta set. A countable intersection of its open neighborhoods omits at most countably many elements of D, and cannot omit all of uncountable D. Hence X is not a perfectly normal space. This is the uncountable discrete one-point compactification counterexample.

Compactifications. A Hausdorff compactification of X is a compact Hausdorff space K together with a topological embedding j:X→K having dense image. We identify X with that image.
For noncompact locally compact Hausdorff X, adjoin ∞ and give X+=X∪{∞} its one-point compactification topology: retain the open sets of X, and take {∞}∪(X∖C), with C compact, as a neighborhood base at ∞. Compact subsets are closed, making this a topology with the original subspace topology on X.
An open cover contains a neighborhood of ∞, leaving a compact subset of X to cover by finitely many more members. Hence X+ is compact. Points of X are separated using its Hausdorff property; to separate x∈X from ∞, use local compactness to choose an open U∋x with compact closure and take X+∖U at ∞. Finally every neighborhood of ∞ meets X, because X itself is not compact. Thus X is dense and X+ is a Hausdorff compactification with one-point remainder.
Comparison maps. The Stone-Čech compactification of a Tychonoff space has the universal property that every continuous map from X to a compact Hausdorff space extends continuously to βX. Applying it to any Hausdorff compactification K gives a continuous surjection π:βX→K fixing X. Surjectivity follows because its compact image is closed and contains the dense set X.
We need the fibers of compactification comparison maps property
π−1(X)=X.​
(5)
Suppose π(z)=x∈X with z=x. By regularity of compact Hausdorff βX, choose an open neighborhood U of x with z∈/U. The embedding X⊆K supplies open V⊆K containing x and satisfying V∩X⊆U∩X. The open set π−1(V) contains z. Density of X implies z∈π−1(V)∩X​=V∩X⊆U, a contradiction. Thus the displayed identity holds.
Equivalence of (a), (b), (c). Clearly (b) implies (c). If (c) holds in K, inverse images under π and π−1(X)=X show that X is a G-delta set in βX, giving (a). Conversely assume (a) and write X=⋂n​Un​ in βX. The sets Fn​=βX∖Un​ are compact and miss X. Their images π(Fn​) are closed in K and miss X by the fiber identity. Since π is onto, they exhaust K∖X. Hence
X=n⋂​(K∖π(Fn​)).​
(6)
This proves (b), for every K. This G-delta compactification invariance defines a Čech-complete space.
The Baire property. Suppose X=⋂n​Gn​ in a compact Hausdorff K, with X dense and all Gn​ open. Each Gn​ is dense. For dense open subsets Un​ of X, choose open On​⊆K with Un​=X∩On​. Every nonempty open subset of K meets X in a nonempty relatively open set and hence meets Un​, so each On​ is dense in K.
The assumed Baire category theorem in K makes ⋂n​(Gn​∩On​) dense in K. This set lies in X and equals ⋂n​Un​, so it is also dense in X. Therefore Čech-complete spaces are Baire spaces.
Enumerate Q={qn​:n∈ω}. Each Q∖{qn​} is open and dense in Q, while their intersection is empty. Thus Q is not a Baire space, and consequently
Q is not a Gδ​ in any Hausdorff compactification of Q.​
(7)
If Q were a G-delta set in R, it would also be one in R+: the subspace R is open in its one-point compactification, and adjoining that open condition to a countable intersection preserves the G-delta set property. Since Q is dense in R and hence in R+, this would be a Hausdorff compactification of Q contradicting the preceding conclusion. Therefore Q is not Gδ​ in R​.

A zero-dimensional space has a base of clopen sets. A totally disconnected space has no connected subset with more than one point, equivalently all its connected components are singletons. We use these definitions in the stated Hausdorff setting.
If a Hausdorff space has a clopen base, any two different points can be separated by a clopen set. A connected subset containing both would be split into two nonempty disjoint relatively open sets. Thus zero-dimensional implies totally disconnected here.
The compact converse, including the component lemma. For a point x of a compact Hausdorff K, put
Qx​=⋂{N:x∈N, N clopen in K}.
(8)
This is its quasicomponent, a closed compact set. We prove the Šura-Bura component lemma that Qx​ equals the connected component of x.
Suppose Qx​=A∪B were a separation into nonempty disjoint relatively closed sets, with x∈A. Both are compact, hence closed in K. Normality of compact Hausdorff spaces gives disjoint open neighborhoods U⊇A, V⊇B. The compact set H=K∖(U∪V) misses Qx​. For each y∈H, choose a clopen neighborhood Ny​ of x missing y; these exist by the definition of Qx​. The open complements cover H, so finitely many suffice. Their intersection N is clopen, contains Qx​, and lies in U∪V. If H is empty take N=K.
Now N∩U is clopen in K: it is open, and its complement is (K∖N)∪(N∩V), also open. It contains x and misses B, contradicting that B⊆Qx​ lies in every clopen neighborhood of x. Hence Qx​ is connected. Conversely every connected subset containing x lies in every clopen neighborhood of x, so the connected component is contained in Qx​. Equality follows.
If K is totally disconnected, then Qx​={x}. Given an open neighborhood O of x, for each y∈K∖O choose a clopen neighborhood of x missing y. Compactness again gives a finite intersection N with x∈N⊆O. These N form a clopen base. Therefore
K compact Hausdorff:K zero-dimensional⟺K totally disconnected.​
(9)
The Stone-Čech characterization. The stated clopen separation property is strong zero-dimensionality in this normal Hausdorff setting. A normal T1 space is a Tychonoff space, so its Stone-Čech compactification exists. We use its extension property for maps into compact Hausdorff spaces and the density of X.
First assume βX is a zero-dimensional space, and let C,D be disjoint closed subsets of X. Urysohn's lemma gives f:X→[0,1] equal to zero on C and one on D. Its continuous extension to βX puts CβX and DβX in disjoint zero and one level sets. Using a clopen base, cover the first compact set by finitely many clopen sets missing the second; their union H is clopen and separates them. Thus U=H∩X and V=X∖U are disjoint clopen sets containing C,D.
Conversely, assume the stated clopen separation property on X. Let A,B be disjoint closed subsets of βX. Normality supplies a continuous function f:βX→[0,1] with f∣A​=0, f∣B​=1. The sets
C=X∩f−1([0,1/3]),D=X∩f−1([2/3,1])
(10)
are disjoint and closed in X. Select clopen U⊆X containing C and missing D; the given disjoint pair of clopen neighborhoods supplies such a U. Its characteristic function, one on U and zero outside, extends from X to a continuous map βX→{0,1}. Let H be its one level set, a clopen set.
Every a∈A is in CβX: any neighborhood of a, intersected with f−1([0,1/3)), is an open neighborhood meeting the dense set X. Likewise B⊆DβX. Since C⊆H and D⊆βX∖H, it follows that A⊆H and B∩H=∅.
Apply this to A={z} and B=βX∖O, for any point z and open neighborhood O. It yields a clopen H with z∈H⊆O, proving that βX has a clopen base. Hence the strong zero-dimensionality and Stone-Čech compactification criterion is
X has the stated clopen separation property⟺βX is zero-dimensional.​
