Node order in Newton interpolation

 ... Analysis Numerical analysis Interpolation Polynomial interpolation Divided difference Newton polynomial
OurBigBook.com Words: 185
Reordering the interpolation nodes changes the successive subsets used for the divided differences and the factors of the Newton polynomial. The final interpolation polynomial remains unchanged in exact arithmetic. For f(x)=x3−2x+1, reversing (−1,0,2,3) gives (3,2,0,−1) and values (22,5,1,2). The successive divided difference columns are now

For example, the first second divided difference is (2−17)/(0−3)=5. The top row becomes (22,17,5,1), giving
q0​(x)q1​(x)q2​(x)q3​(x)​=22,=22+17(x−3)=17x−29,=22+17(x−3)+5(x−3)(x−2)=5x2−8x+1,=22+17(x−3)+5(x−3)(x−2)+(x−3)(x−2)x=x3−2x+1=p3​(x).​
(22)
The earlier partial interpolants differ because they fit different subsets: p2​ fits −1,0,2, whereas q2​ fits 3,2,0. The highest divided difference is invariant because both complete top rows finish with the same set of four interpolation nodes; this is symmetry of divided differences.
This distinction is useful when checking a divided difference table: permuting all the interpolation nodes must preserve its final divided difference, even though the intermediate entries change. It also explains why data can be appended in their arrival order. In floating-point arithmetic, the subtractions and divisions may accumulate different rounding errors in different orders; exact invariance describes the mathematical polynomial, rather than guaranteeing bit-for-bit equality of numerical results.
