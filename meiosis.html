Meiosis

 Home Biology Genetics
OurBigBook.com Words: 394 Articles: 4
Meiosis reduces two sets of chromosomes to one. In the ordinary diploid life cycle, this makes it possible for fertilization of two haploid gametes to restore the diploid state rather than double the number of sets in every generation. Its two divisions perform different tasks: meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes, whereas meiosis II separates sister chromatids. One round of DNA replication precedes both divisions; there is no second round between them. See [ref].
Two homologous chromosomes carry corresponding genetic loci, potentially with different alleles; two sister chromatids originate by DNA replication of one chromosome. After chromosomal crossover between nonsister chromatids, the sister chromatids need not carry identical alleles everywhere. Separating the two relationships explains why a haploid product of meiosis I still contains replicated chromosomes.
The two divisions explain Mendelian segregation; variable orientation of different homologous chromosome pairs connects it to independent assortment. Genetic recombination adds another source of allele combinations. These mechanisms reshuffle inherited material without requiring a new mutation.
The symmetric diagram is useful for counting, but human oogenesis produces one functional ovum rather than four. The polar bodies receive discarded chromosomes and little cytoplasm. See Human Reproductive Anatomy and Gametogenesis (§43.3, Oogenesis and Figure 43.13: unequal divisions and polar bodies).
