The displayed percentages use row denominators. The relevant comparison is therefore the conditional probability of each response within each recorded gender group, rather than the gender composition within a response category.
For the male group, 364/1042≈34.93% strongly agree and 286/1042≈27.45% agree. For the female group, the corresponding percentages are 225/1093≈20.59% and 243/1093≈22.23%. Combining the two agreement categories gives

The female group has more neutral responses, 24.25% versus 17.47%, and a higher combined disagreement percentage, 32.94% versus 20.15%. Thus the observed distributions differ in more than a single cell.
The Pearson chi-squared test assesses the null hypothesis of independence in the complete 2×5 contingency table. Under that null, the expected frequency in a cell is its row total times its column total divided by 2,135. Recalculation gives
X2=∑i,j​Eij​(Oij​−Eij​)2​≈90.56953,ν=(2−1)(5−1)=4.
(6)
The reported p-value, approximately 9.97×10−19, gives very strong evidence against independence under the test's sampling assumptions. The minimum expected frequency is about 106.88, so sparse expected cells are not a problem here. The test concerns the full five-category distribution, not just the combined agreement comparison.
Cramér's V summarizes the magnitude without imposing an ordering on the gender labels:
V=2135min(2−1,5−1)90.56953​​≈0.206.
(7)
The small p-value does not imply a nearly deterministic relationship. For example, the column-dependent Goodman and Kruskal's lambda is only about 0.026: choosing the modal response separately within the two groups reduces prediction errors from 1,546 to 1,506, a relative reduction of 40/1546≈2.59%.
The positive Goodman and Kruskal's gamma must be interpreted using the table's chosen ordering. The agreement categories run toward less willingness, and gender has no intrinsic ordinal direction. A sign attached to an arbitrary group coding cannot replace the substantive comparison of conditional probabilities.
The NaN displayed for the likelihood-ratio calculation is not a valid p-value and is not evidence of no association. The Pearson chi-squared statistic can be independently recomputed from the positive cell counts. None of these calculations identifies a causal effect; the table has not controlled for income or other possible confounders.
