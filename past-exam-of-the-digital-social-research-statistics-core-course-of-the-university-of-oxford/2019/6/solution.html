The fitted age slope is very small and negative, about −0.00319 efficacy points per year, with no conventional evidence against a zero linear slope: t≈−0.951, p≈0.342. The model explains only about 0.0367% of the observed efficacy variation. These results concern a linear association, not proof of no possible age relationship or a causal effect of ageing.
The descriptive statistics report 2466 observed ages and efficacy scores, with no missing values for either variable. Age ranges from 14 to 97 years, with sample mean 52.689 years and sample standard deviation 19.064 years. Efficacy ranges from 0 to 16, with sample mean 9.348 and sample standard deviation 3.176 scale points. The standard deviations use different units and should not be compared as though their numerical ratio measured relative substantive variability. The efficacy mean retains the conflict with Question 1's group summaries; that conflict is not resolved by this separate regression output.
A simple linear regression models E(Y∣x)=β0​+β1​x, where Y is efficacy and x is age in years. Ordinary least squares chooses the coefficients to minimize the residual sum of squares. From the printed coefficients the fitted line is
y​=9.516515−0.003192x.​
(6)
Holding to this model, ten additional years correspond to −0.03192 fitted scale points. For example, the fitted mean is about 9.453 at age 20 and 9.261 at age 80, a difference of about −0.192 points. These are fitted means, not exact predictions of individual scores. The line does not imply that every person's efficacy falls at that rate, nor that cross-sectional age differences identify within-person changes over time.
The intercept is the predicted mean at age zero, about 9.517. Since zero is outside the observed 14–97 age range, its direct interpretation requires extrapolation. The printed standard error of 0.188043 and t≈50.608 yield the extremely small displayed p-value for a zero intercept. That test does not test whether age is a useful predictor. Recentring age at its observed sample mean would put the intercept near 9.516515−0.003192(52.689)≈9.34833, the fitted mean at a substantively relevant reference age. This agrees with the rounded overall efficacy mean.
The age standard error is 0.003356 points per year. The coefficient divided by this standard error gives −0.003192/0.003356≈−0.9511, agreeing with the printed t value. Estimating an intercept and one regression slope from 2466 observations leaves 2466−2=2464 residual statistical degrees of freedom. Under a correctly specified normal linear model, the Student t-distribution provides the stated two-sided p-value. At 5%, or 1%, it does not reject H0​:β1​=0. The p-value is not a 34.2% probability that age has no effect, and nonrejection does not establish a practically negligible effect size.[ref]
A 95% confidence interval calculated from the printed estimate and standard error is approximately
β1​∈[−0.009773, 0.003389] scale points per year,​
(7)
or about [−0.09773,0.03389] points per decade. It includes zero and gives the scale of slopes compatible with these summaries and assumptions. Assessing a practically negligible effect would require an explicit substantive tolerance and an appropriate equivalence test, not simply the fact that p>0.05. A nonlinear association could also coexist with a near-zero overall linear slope.
The coefficient of determination is R2=0.000367, meaning that the fitted line explains about 100R2=0.0367% of the sample's centred efficacy sum of squares compared with an intercept-only fit. This is not 36.7%, a fraction of people whose efficacy is predicted correctly, or the proportion of efficacy caused by age. The adjusted coefficient of determination, approximately −0.00003873, is slightly negative because its penalty for estimating the extra coefficient exceeds the tiny improvement in fit. It need not be constrained to be nonnegative and does not mean a negative amount of explained variation.
The overall F≈0.9045 on 1 and 2464 statistical degrees of freedom, with p≈0.3417, tests the same zero-slope hypothesis as the age t test because there is only one predictor. The simple-regression output identities give F=tslope2​ and F=(2464)R2/(1−R2); the printed numbers agree allowing their displayed precision. The signed Pearson correlation coefficient is consequently about −R2​≈−0.0192. These are related summaries of the same weak linear association, not independent corroborating tests.
The residual standard error of 3.177 points estimates the typical unexplained error scale under the fitted model. It is close to the efficacy sample standard deviation, as expected when the line explains almost none of the variation. It is not a standard error of the slope or a guarantee that each individual's prediction error lies within 3.177 points. The residual summary spans roughly −9.46 to 6.75, with sample median about 0.573 and central quartiles about −1.437 and 2.579. An ordinary least-squares fit with an intercept has zero mean residual, not necessarily zero median residual. These few quantiles cannot establish normality, constant conditional spread, absence of outliers or linearity. The apparent last-decimal discrepancies among rounded error scales and other summary fields do not force an additional contradiction: their rounding intervals admit compatible regression summaries.
The conventional uncertainty calculations assume independent, mean-zero errors conditional on age, an appropriate linear mean, and homoscedasticity; normal errors give the usual exact finite-sample t and F calibration. Large samples can relax some distributional requirements but do not remove confounding, selection bias or dependence. A residual-versus-fitted plot could reveal curvature or changing spread, and a normal probability plot could assess error shape; neither plot can be reconstructed from the printed residual quantiles. A prediction interval for an individual includes residual variation and is wider than a confidence interval for the mean response. The supplied output supports a very weak, imprecisely signed linear association with little predictive gain from age alone, while leaving nonlinear, causal and design-based questions unresolved.
