Employment is observed for all 2053 listed cases. Social media use is observed for 1424 cases, with 629 missing. The age percentages describe the five visible rows, whose counts total 1730; they cannot be treated as a complete age distribution for the stated 2053 cases.
A frequency table describes category counts and their relative frequencies. The full-sample percentage uses every listed case as denominator; a valid percentage excludes missing data for that variable. A cumulative percentage adds the valid percentages up to an ordered category. The denominator matters as much as the number: a statement about respondents with recorded use need not describe every respondent.
Employment is a binary categorical variable: 1216 cases are employed and 837 are not, or about 59.23% and 40.77%. Neither employment category is missing in this output. Employment labels have no intrinsic numerical spacing; codes zero and one identify the two categories under a chosen ordering convention. Cumulative percentages for a nominal categorical variable depend on the arbitrary display order and add little to this two-category description.
Age groups are an ordinal categorical variable because their intervals have a natural ordering, although the grouped representation loses exact ages. Among the five visible age rows, ages 25–44 form the largest group, with 562/1730≈32.49%, and ages 14–24 the smallest, with 182/1730≈10.52%. The other visible groups account for 20.98%, 16.42% and 19.60% on that denominator. About 43.01% of the visible five-row count is below 45. These percentages should not be silently applied to the stated total 2053: for example, the same 562 cases are only 27.37% of 2053.
The age variable key advertises six categories, whereas only five appear. Their counts sum to 1730, leaving a difference of 323 against the printed total; the table nevertheless prints zero missing values. This is an internal source discrepancy, not permission to label those 323 cases as a particular unseen age group or as recorded missing data. The oldest row is labelled 65+ here but 65–74 in Figure 2. Even the age percentage total has a small display discrepancy: the five rounded percentages sum to 100.01%, while the printed valid total and last cumulative percentage say 99.99%. The much larger count inconsistency remains after allowing ordinary rounding.
Social media use is also an ordinal categorical variable. Its six ordered categories express increasing frequency, not equally spaced measurements: averaging category labels or claiming a one-unit change has a constant time meaning would require an additional scoring convention. Among the 1424 observed responses, the mode is never, at 459/1424≈32.23%, followed by daily, at 28.37%. Weekly use accounts for 18.19%, use more than once a day for 11.38%, less-than-monthly use for 6.39%, and monthly use for 3.44%. The first three categories together comprise about 42.06% of valid responses. Consequently the middle ordered response lies in the weekly category: cumulative frequency first passes one half there.
Combining the two daily categories gives 566/1424≈39.75% of valid responses using social media at least daily. The same recorded responses are 566/2053≈27.57% of all listed cases. The second percentage is the fraction recorded as daily users, not an estimate that classifies all missing responses as nonusers. Missing social media responses make up 629/2053≈30.64%. The directly calculated valid fraction is 69.36%; summing the individually rounded full-sample percentages produces the printed 69.37%, a minor rounding difference. With no account of the missing-data mechanism, the observed valid distribution should not automatically be generalized to those with missing use. Employment being fully observed does not make social media missingness ignorable. The substantive distributional summary must retain both the valid-response denominator and the missing-data qualification.
