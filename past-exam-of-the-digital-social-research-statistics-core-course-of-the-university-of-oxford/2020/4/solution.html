Within the 1424 complete records displayed here, employed respondents report more frequent use on average in the ordinal sense of positive pairwise association. The overall independence test is significant, but its sample association magnitude is modest and the result is observational. Some printed ordinal summaries are internally inconsistent.
Compare row percentages because employment groups have different sizes. Never-use is reported by 219/599=36.56% of the non-employed group and 240/825=29.09% of the employed group. Daily use is 24.21% versus 31.39%; more-than-daily use is 9.02% versus 13.09%. Combining the last two categories gives 33.22% versus 44.48%, an employed-minus-non-employed difference of about 11.26 percentage points. That threshold comparison is a descriptive summary of the six-category table, not a replacement for the original omnibus test or a newly declared prespecified endpoint. It does not assign equal spacing to the six frequency categories.
Here the row counts, column counts and grand total are additive. Under the null hypothesis of employment–use independence, a 2×6 contingency table has (2−1)(6−1)=5 statistical degrees of freedom. The Pearson chi-squared statistic recomputed from the cells is 21.132241, agreeing with the printed value, and its p-value is about 0.0007648. The likelihood-ratio test statistic G2≈21.25379 gives p≈0.000725. Both reject independence at the conventional 5% and 1% significance levels under the sampling assumptions. The minimum expected frequency, about 20.61, gives no sparse-cell warning for this table. The tests compare whole category distributions; they do not by themselves identify one category as the source of the association or establish its causal direction.
Cramér's V is 21.132241/1424​≈0.12182. The generalized phi coefficient coincides with it for this two-row table, while the contingency coefficient is about 0.121. These are modest magnitudes; a small p-value does not make the relationship large or useful for individual prediction. The employment–use association could change after conditioning on age or other covariates, and the sample excludes 629 cases without recorded use. Relative to the employment margins in Figure 1, the omitted complete-case counts are 238 non-employed and 391 employed, or about 28.43% and 32.15% of those groups. Different missing fractions alone do not identify the missing-data mechanism, but they rule out treating complete records as automatically representative of everyone.
The count-based Goodman and Kruskal's gamma is

With the employment categories ordered non-employed then employed and use ordered from never to more than daily, this positive value means concordant pairs outnumber discordant pairs among pairs untied on both variables. It is not a 16.9% increase in a person's use and is not a causal effect. Reversing one ordering reverses its sign. Excluding ties can make gamma appreciably larger than tie-adjusted measures.
For use as response and employment as predictor, the cells give Somers' D about 0.12976, Kendall's tau-b about 0.10362 and Stuart's tau-c about 0.12650. The printed Somers value 0.141 and tau-b 0.108 should not be presented as verified calculations: the table prints 77297 second-variable-only ties, whereas its cells give 115073. The other four pair classes agree with the cells; the printed five classes sum to 975400, not (21424​)=1013176. The source does not establish an alternative tie convention that repairs those ordinary identities. The direction remains a weak positive association under either description, but the exact measures require coherent pair counts.
Modal prediction provides another perspective. Without employment information, predicting the globally most frequent use category, never, makes 1424−459=965 errors. Predicting a separate modal use category in each employment group makes 1424−(219+259)=946 errors. Thus Goodman and Kruskal's lambda for use given employment is (965−946)/965≈0.01969: about a 1.97% reduction in modal-classification errors. For employment given use the corresponding reduction is 3/599≈0.00501. These identify the targets explicitly; the source's row-dependent/column-dependent labels should not be used to reverse the target interpretation. The two small numbers show why an independence test can be significant while coarse modal prediction improves very little. The supported overall finding is a modest positive association among observed respondents, requiring conditional analysis and a missing-data qualification before broader claims.
