Every displayed age stratum has a higher proportion of employed respondents reporting use at least daily, and each has positive ordinal association. The visible counts do not support the printed claim of a conventional significant omnibus result in any one stratum: several test fields are contradictory, and sparse cells further limit the chi-squared approximation. Age-specific significance labels do not establish interaction or causation.
Conditioning on age means comparing the employment groups within each displayed age category. Each within-row percentage estimates a use distribution for that age–employment combination. This helps assess a marginal association that could reflect different age compositions, but broad age categories do not remove all residual confounding. The relevant conditional-independence null hypothesis is that employment and use are independent within every age stratum. It differs both from marginal independence and from a null hypothesis that the strength of association is identical across ages.
For the 14–24 stratum, N=169. The non-employed group reports use at least daily in (27+15)/69=60.87% of cases, versus (48+24)/100=72.00% of employed cases: an 11.13-percentage-point difference. Daily use alone is 39.13% versus 48.00%. This is not a uniform upward shift at every threshold: never-use is also higher among employed respondents, 8.00% versus 4.35%, whereas weekly use is lower, 11.00% versus 24.64%. The count-based Cramér's V is about 0.199 and Goodman and Kruskal's gamma about 0.100. The cells give X2=6.721811 on 5 statistical degrees of freedom, with p≈0.24217, agreeing qualitatively with the likelihood-ratio result G2=6.670009, p≈0.246. The printed Pearson X2=9.721811 and p=0.0491651 disagree with the cells and with one another: that printed statistic at 5 statistical degrees of freedom would give about 0.08351, not 0.04917. Four of twelve expected frequencies are below five, with minimum about 2.45, so an exact conditional test or a Monte Carlo test is preferable for calibrated inference rather than trusting a marginal printed significance label.
For ages 25–44, N=519. At-least-daily use is 93/202=46.04% in the non-employed group and 181/317=57.10% in the employed group, a difference of 11.06 percentage points. Never-use is 17.82% versus 15.46%, daily use 35.15% versus 39.75%, and more-than-daily use 10.89% versus 17.35%. The count-based Cramér's V is about 0.142 and Goodman and Kruskal's gamma about 0.174. The visible cells give X2=10.475487, p≈0.06283, and G2=10.451334, p≈0.06341, each with 5 statistical degrees of freedom. These differ materially from the printed Pearson 12.47549, p=0.04283048, and printed likelihood-ratio 12.451, p=0.043. Even the printed Pearson statistic at its stated statistical degrees of freedom gives p≈0.02882, not the stated p-value. The minimum expected frequency is about 5.06, so the principal difficulty here is corrupted numerical output rather than a listed sparse-cell warning. The recomputed 5% test fails to reject independence, although its p-value is not evidence that the positive descriptive contrast is exactly zero.
For ages 45–54, N=304. At-least-daily use is 33/119=27.73% versus 68/185=36.76%, a difference of 9.03 percentage points. Never-use is 37.82% versus 31.89%, and daily use 19.33% versus 27.03%. The count-based Cramér's V is about 0.105 and Goodman and Kruskal's gamma about 0.133. The cells give X2=3.350752, p≈0.64608, consistent with the likelihood-ratio G2=3.382693, p≈0.641. The printed Pearson 9.350752 and p=0.046081 are incompatible with the cells and with one another: using that statistic at 5 statistical degrees of freedom gives p≈0.09587. The smallest expected frequency is about 6.26. Thus this is another positive descriptive contrast without a count-supported significant omnibus result at 5%.
For ages 55–64, N=203. At-least-daily use is 16/86=18.60% versus 31/117=26.50%, a difference of 7.89 percentage points. Never-use is 54.65% versus 48.72%, and daily use 11.63% versus 17.95%. Cramér's V is about 0.125 and Goodman and Kruskal's gamma about 0.133. The printed and count-based Pearson result agrees: X2=3.190901, 5 statistical degrees of freedom, p≈0.67058. The likelihood-ratio p-value is about 0.668. Two of twelve expected frequencies are below five, with minimum about 3.39; the nominal result does not indicate rejection, but a simulation-based check would be prudent if formal calibration mattered. Neither a large p-value nor the smaller sample establishes an absence of association.
For the oldest stratum, labelled 65+ in Figure 8, N=155. At-least-daily use is 10/82=12.20% versus 13/73=17.81%, a difference of 5.61 percentage points. Never-use is 70.73% versus 56.16%. Cramér's V is about 0.217 and Goodman and Kruskal's gamma about 0.269, the largest of the five displayed strata. Yet X2=7.269606 on 5 statistical degrees of freedom gives p≈0.20135, while the likelihood-ratio p-value is about 0.117. There are four expected frequencies below five, with minimum about 0.471. Sampling uncertainty and a sparse distribution therefore matter greatly; the larger descriptive ordinal magnitude is not evidence for a significant age interaction. The zero observed entries do not establish zero population probabilities. An exact conditional test or a Monte Carlo test can retain the six categories without relying on a doubtful asymptotic approximation. Collapsing categories should have a substantive justification; searching for a grouping that produces significance changes the inferential problem.[ref]
The following original analytical plot summarizes a common threshold rather than reproducing any source table. Its overall reference is deliberately separated: the five age strata total 1350, not the 1424 in Figure 3, so their percentages cannot be pooled and called the reported overall result.
Across the visible strata, frequent use declines strongly with age in both employment groups, while the at-least-daily employment difference remains positive. This is compatible with age being important to the use distribution and with a positive employment association persisting after age stratification. It is not a demonstrated Simpson's paradox: none of these displayed threshold differences reverses direction. The evidence also does not establish how much age confounds the overall result, because the strata do not reproduce its sample and the oldest age labels disagree across figures. Among young respondents the positive ordinal association coexists with a reversed never-use contrast, so the threshold summary should not be described as stochastic domination of every conditional distribution.
Ordinal measures summarize a different aspect from the omnibus tests. Counting pairs from each visible table gives Goodman and Kruskal's gamma approximately 0.100,0.174,0.133,0.133,0.269 in age order. For use as response, the corresponding Somers' D values are about 0.072,0.132,0.102,0.090,0.152; Kendall's tau-b values are 0.059,0.105,0.081,0.076,0.144; and Stuart's tau-c values are 0.070,0.125,0.097,0.088,0.152. They are all positive and modest, but not interchangeable. Gamma discards pairs tied on either variable; Somers' D includes response ties separated by employment; Kendall's tau-b adjusts for ties in both variables; Stuart's tau-c adjusts for table dimensions. The printed second-variable tie counts violate the unordered-pair partition for ordinal data in all five strata, so the printed Somers and tau-b values should not be accepted as independently verified numbers.[ref]
Goodman and Kruskal's lambda for predicting the use category from employment is zero in all five visible strata: each employment row has the same modal use category as its stratum overall. This does not contradict their nonzero gamma or V, because the rest of each category distribution can change without its mode changing. Reversing the prediction target gives about 0.087,0.025,0,0.023,0.123 for predicting employment from use. In the original display these are the second lambda values; identify the target directly rather than relying on ambiguous row/column-dependent labels. The contingency coefficient, another unsigned transformation of the Pearson statistic, adds no new direction and cannot repair a corrupted test statistic.
Even if all printed p-values were trusted, a significant result in one age group and a nonsignificant result in another would not prove that their effects differ. A statistical interaction needs a direct comparison of effects on a specified scale, not a comparison of significance labels. Five separate tests also create a multiple-testing issue if the aim is to claim at least one association; choosing categories or a threshold after inspection adds further selection. A joint conditional independence test for stratified contingency tables or an appropriate categorical-response model can target a clear combined hypothesis, with exact or simulation-based calibration where needed. Such a model would require coherent input tables before it could resolve the source discrepancies. The sound conclusion is a consistent positive descriptive tendency within the five shown strata, with no count-supported nominal 5% omnibus rejection in an individual stratum, and insufficient coherent output for a claimed causal or precisely quantified age-adjusted effect.
