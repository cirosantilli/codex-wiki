Taken at face value, the printed two-sided p-value rejects equality of average political efficacy at the 5% level, with the non-employed group scoring higher. The effect magnitude and strength of evidence cannot be reported as one coherent result because the estimates, interval, test statistic and p-value contradict one another.
Let μ0​ and μ1​ be the expected political efficacy scores in the two employment groups in the target population. The null hypothesis is H0​:μ0​−μ1​=0 and the two-sided alternative hypothesis is H1​:μ0​−μ1​=0. A Welch's t-test compares the difference of sample means with its standard error, estimating the two group variances separately:
t=s02​/n0​+s12​/n1​​yˉ​0​−yˉ​1​​.
(1)
The reference Student t-distribution has approximate, generally noninteger statistical degrees of freedom; it does not require equality of the two variances. Observations should be independent, with finite group variances; normal distributions give the usual small-sample rationale, while large samples permit an approximate normal sampling distribution under suitable conditions. A large sample cannot correct a biased sampling procedure or dependence between observations.[ref]
A p-value is a probability calculated under the null hypothesis and the sampling assumptions: for this two-sided test it measures the probability of an absolute test statistic at least as large as the observed one. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, the probability that chance caused the observed difference, or a measure of the effect size. If the significance level α=0.05 was chosen in advance, the printed p=0.049521 falls just below it. This would reject H0​ at 5%, but not at 1%. The significance level describes the long-run probability of a type I error, rejecting a true null hypothesis; a type II error instead fails to reject a false null hypothesis. Statistical power is the rejection probability under a specified alternative hypothesis. None of these errors for this particular realised study can be read as a posterior probability from p.
Both the printed sample means and the positive confidence interval point toward greater political efficacy in group 0, the non-employed group. Their magnitudes disagree. Subtracting the printed sample means gives 8.656650−6.322812=2.333838 scale units. Yet the printed 95% confidence interval has midpoint (0.2822355+0.6743412)/2=0.47828835, not 2.333838. A two-sided Welch interval is centred on the difference of sample means. This violates the consistency identities for a two-sided t-test; the interval and estimates cannot describe the same reported comparison. If the interval alone were trusted, it would exclude zero and indicate an increase between about 0.28 and 0.67 units, but that is explicitly a conditional interpretation. A 95% confidence interval refers to repeated-sample coverage of a fixed statistical parameter, not a 95% posterior probability for that realised interval.[ref]
There are additional arithmetic warnings. The printed t=6.1616 at 2022.4 statistical degrees of freedom gives a two-sided p-value about 8.67×10−10, not 0.049521. Thus taking the test statistic as authoritative would imply much stronger evidence, not merely a marginal 5% result. The employment counts sum to 2311, but their percentages are 905/2311≈39.16% and 1406/2311≈60.84%, not 39.27% and 60.73%. If those counts also apply to the two political efficacy sample means, their weighted arithmetic mean would be about 7.237, not the reported overall 8.348; separate complete-case group sizes are not supplied, so that last cross-output comparison is conditional on a common sample. No uniquely corrected difference, standard error or interval follows from these contradictory fields.
The political efficacy summary describes a bounded observed scale from 0 to 16, with median 9 and middle quartiles 7 and 12. The interquartile range is 5; the overall sample mean 8.348 being below the median does not, by itself, establish a particular distributional shape. These descriptive statistics cannot supply the missing coherent group variances or a standardized effect size. Even a corrected small p-value would establish an association under a statistical model, rather than a causal effect of employment. Age, other background covariates, selection bias and the quality of the political efficacy measurement remain relevant. A defensible report gives the conditional 5% decision and direction, identifies the contradictions, and withholds a single numerical effect-and-uncertainty claim until the output is checked.

If its own field were trusted, residual standard error 1.793 estimates the unexplained response standard deviation, in device-count units. It is neither the standard error of the slope nor a guarantee that every prediction is accurate to 1.793 devices. The residual five-number summary has extremes near −5.206 and 5.086 and median near −0.060, with central quartiles near −1.372 and 1.252. The roughly central median does not establish normally distributed errors, homoscedasticity, independence or a linear conditional expectation. A residual-versus-fitted plot could check curvature and spread; a normal probability plot could assess an error-shape approximation. Neither diagnostic can be recovered from five residual quantiles alone. Individual prediction intervals include response variation as well as coefficient uncertainty and are wider than confidence intervals for the conditional expectation.
For conventional finite-sample t and F calibration, a normal linear model assumes independent mean-zero normal errors of common variance with an appropriate predictor specification. Mean-zero errors conditional on skill and adequate sampling remain essential even when large-sample approximations relax normality. A bounded count response can have nonconstant conditional variance, so residual diagnostics and a scientifically suitable response model matter; count data do not become normally distributed just because the sample is large. Poisson regression is one possible alternative to investigate, but its own conditional mean–variance assumptions and the bounded device range require assessment, not automatic substitution. Complete-case selection can also bias the fitted association if it depends on the response in a relevant way. The only safe summary is conditional: the printed slope is positive, but the numerical effect, its standard error, t/F evidence and explanatory fit require a corrected, coherent output before a single definitive regression claim is made.
