Henkin semantics

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Foundations of mathematics Mathematical logic Second-order logic
OurBigBook.com Words: 360
In Henkin semantics, a domain D is accompanied by designated ranges Hk​⊆P(Dk) for second-order relation variables of each arity k. A formula ∃Rφ(R) searches only Hk​, and ∀Rφ(R) checks only Hk​. These ranges can be proper subcollections of the external power set. Keeping D and its input relations fixed therefore need not fix the truth of a second-order logic sentence.
As a toy illustration of restricted quantification, take D={a,b} and unary range H1​={∅,D}. The sentence
∀a∃P∀x(P(x)↔x=a)
(5)
is true under full semantics for second-order logic, but false with this selected range, because neither singleton is available. Likewise, on the complete graph K3​, the unary range {∅,D} admits no witness for the second-order encoding of three-colorability, even though a usual proper vertex coloring exists. This toy range illustrates the truth definition only: it fails second-order comprehension for the formula x=a, and is not a model of a system requiring that comprehension instance.
Admissible Henkin semantics for a specified deductive system must satisfy its axioms, including its chosen second-order comprehension requirements or analogous closure conditions for predicate abstraction. Closure is relative to the permitted formulas and parameters, rather than a blanket property of every selected family. In particular, on a finite domain, allowing logical equality, individual parameters, all finite logical disjunctions, and comprehension for those formulas forces every finite-arity relation to be available: enumerate its tuples and disjoin the formulas naming them. Thus a proper restricted finite range cannot satisfy that entire scheme.
Treating each Hk​ as an additional sort, with an application relation between its members and k individuals, translates this relational presentation into first-order logic with several sorts. Include axioms saying that predicates with the same members are equal, together with whatever second-order comprehension the system requires. This makes Henkin semantics useful for bringing first-order logic methods to reasoning about predicates; it does not license conclusions about all external relations.
Leon Henkin's Completeness in the Theory of Types, Journal of Symbolic Logic 15 (1950), pp. 81–91 develops general models and proves completeness for his typed deductive system. Its opening footnote 5 explicitly discusses closure under operations associated with formula formation.
