Discrete probability distribution

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Probability distribution
OurBigBook.com Words: 10k Articles: 96
A discrete probability distribution is a probability distribution concentrated on a finite or countable set of possible values S: a random variable X with this law satisfies P(X∈S)=1. Its probability mass function assigns a probability to each value:
pX​(x)=P(X=x),pX​(x)≥0,∑x∈S​pX​(x)=1.
(1)
Thus the probability of an event is obtained by adding masses, rather than integrating a probability density function. The possible values need not be equally spaced, or even integers. When the relevant moments are finite, its expected value and variance are
μ=EX=∑x∈S​xpX​(x),Var(X)=∑x∈S​(x−μ)2pX​(x)=E[X2]−μ2.
(2)
For a constructed example, assign values 0,2,5 masses 0.5,0.3,0.2. They sum to one; P(X≥2)=0.3+0.2=0.5, EX=0(0.5)+2(0.3)+5(0.2)=1.6, and E[X2]=4(0.3)+25(0.2)=6.2. Hence the variance is 6.2−1.62=3.64. A mean can lie between possible values: no outcome here equals 1.6.
The useful organizing question is what an experiment records. One yes/no indicator random variable gives a Bernoulli distribution; a success count in a fixed number of independent attempts gives a binomial distribution; the attempt number of the first success gives a geometric distribution. A Poisson distribution describes an event count under different assumptions. Naming the quantity first helps prevent selecting a familiar formula for the wrong experiment.
