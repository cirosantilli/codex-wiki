Feasible point (x∈F)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Mathematical optimization
OurBigBook.com Words: 216 Articles: 1
A feasible point satisfies every optimization constraint of an optimization problem. For the budget constraint example, the feasible set is exactly
F={(x,10−x):0≤x≤10}.
(12)
It is the line segment from (0,10) to (10,0), a compact set and a convex set. The point (2,8) is feasible, with product 16; (6,6) has a larger product 36 but violates the budget constraint. Feasibility answers whether a decision is allowed; optimality asks whether any allowed decision is better. The extreme value theorem guarantees that this continuous objective function attains its maximum on this nonempty compact set.
Eliminating y gives a one-variable objective function:
q(x)=x(10−x)=25−(x−5)2,0≤x≤10.
(13)
By completing the square, q(x)≤25, with equality exactly at x=5. Alternatively its derivative is q′(x)=10−2x, which vanishes at 5; the endpoint values are both zero. Completing the square proves global optimality directly, whereas the first-order optimality condition only identifies a candidate. It also quantifies the cost of imbalance: moving to (5+δ,5−δ) with ∣δ∣≤5 loses exactly δ2 units of product. The arithmetic-geometric mean inequality gives another short certificate: xy​≤(x+y)/2=5, with equality exactly when x=y.
For a transportation problem with capacity inequalities, feasibility requires nonnegative shipments, row totals no greater than factory capacities and column totals equal to shop demands. Feasibility alone does not prove minimal cost: a matching transportation dual certificate with capacity inequalities provides an optimality proof. The feasible set can be empty, and a finite infimum need not be attained even when the set is nonempty.
