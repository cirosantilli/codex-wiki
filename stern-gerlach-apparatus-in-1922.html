Stern-Gerlach apparatus in 1922

 ... Physics Branches of physics Quantum mechanics Angular momentum operator Spin Stern-Gerlach experiment
OurBigBook.com Words: 285
In 1922, Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach used an electrically heated oven to emit neutral silver chemical atoms. Two platinum apertures, a pinhole and a slit 0.03–0.04mm wide, supplied collimation. The beam passed along a 3.5cm knife-edge pole in a nonuniform magnetic field. At its end, a glass plate collected the condensed silver. Continuous pumping maintained vacuum; the thin deposit required development even after eight hours.[ref]
The field-off image established the undeflected slit width. A comparison put field-on and field-off deposits on the same plate. Microscopy revealed two deflected bands, no detectable central component, and broadening attributed to the velocity distribution. Alignment and distance from the knife-edge affected the pattern; precise magnetic field gradient measurements were still pending.[ref]
The apertures act as a collimator: they constrain the initial transverse positions and velocities, so a magnetic displacement can be distinguished from an initially broad beam. Vacuum reduces collisions that would destroy this preparation. Deposition integrates arrivals over the exposure; a darkened band is not a time-resolved record of individual chemical atoms. The field-off comparison tests whether the magnet changes the spatial distribution, while alignment checks address a geometric source of apparent asymmetry. Finite sensitivity qualifies the absence of a central deposit: it is an experimental upper limit, not a proof that exactly zero atoms landed there.
