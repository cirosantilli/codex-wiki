Coma (optics)

 ... Physics Branches of physics Optics Geometrical optics Optical aberration Seidel aberration
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 2
Coma makes an off-axis optical point source appear asymmetric, often with a compact head and a flaring tail like a comet. Different parts of the optical pupil send its light rays to different transverse image positions. A sharp central star image therefore does not guarantee sharp images across an optical telescope's field of view. In an idealized, aligned system with rotational symmetry, coma vanishes on the optical axis; misalignment can introduce coma even at the nominal field centre.[ref]
Coma differs from spherical aberration, whose dependence on optical pupil radius can blur an on-axis point without selecting a transverse direction. Astigmatism gives different optical foci in two perpendicular sections of an off-axis bundle; a through-focus sequence can change its elongated image's orientation. Diffraction persists when these optical aberrations vanish. A real asymmetric point spread function may mix several effects, so its appearance alone is not a unique diagnosis of coma.
The classical primary description belongs to the nineteenth-century Seidel aberration classification of five primary monochromatic image errors.[ref] It remains useful in modern design: OpticStudio's Seidel Coefficients by ANSYS, Inc. documentation lists coma as W131​ and tabulates each surface's contribution as well as the system sum (Discussion). Cancellation of contributions can reduce coma without making every surface individually free of it. The same documentation limits this calculation to specified systems with axially symmetric surfaces; higher-order terms and broken rotational symmetry require more general ray tracing.
