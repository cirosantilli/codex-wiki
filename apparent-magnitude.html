Apparent magnitude (m)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Astrophysics Astronomical magnitude
OurBigBook.com Words: 285 Articles: 2
Apparent magnitude m is a calibrated description of the observed radiative flux received from an astronomical source. It answers how bright the source appears in a specified photometric passband, rather than how much luminosity the source emits. Define Fref​>0 as the measurement assigned zero apparent magnitude by the chosen magnitude zero point. Then
m=−2.5log10​(Fref​F​).
(18)
Here F is the positive passband measurement, with the same units and response convention as Fref​. For an energy-weighted detector this measures received radiative flux through its response; for a photon-counting detector use the corresponding calibrated count rate for both quantities. Actual photometric passbands include the instrument's response across wavelength, so naming the photometric system is part of reporting an apparent magnitude. [ref]. [ref].
As toy examples in one photometric passband, F=Fref​ gives m=0, F=100Fref​ gives m=−5, and F=0.01Fref​ gives m=+5. A negative apparent magnitude therefore means more received radiative flux than the reference; it is physically allowed. Zero apparent magnitude does not mean zero radiative flux. The ordinary logarithm formula requires F>0.
A small apparent magnitude alone cannot distinguish a nearby faint source from a distant source with large luminosity: this is the flux-distance degeneracy. Stellar parallax supplies independent distance information, while a calibrated standard candle supplies intrinsic brightness. Interstellar extinction also changes the received radiative flux and hence the measured apparent magnitude.
