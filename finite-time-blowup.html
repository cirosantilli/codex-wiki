Finite-time blowup (limt↑T​∥y(t)∥=∞,T<∞)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Differential equation Ordinary differential equation
OurBigBook.com Words: 272
A solution of a differential equation has finite-time blowup in norm when ∥y(t)∥→∞ as t approaches a finite endpoint T of its maximal interval of existence. This is stronger than merely approaching an excluded boundary of an open state domain while staying bounded. The finite-dimensional continuation criterion distinguishes these obstructions; for a locally Lipschitz continuous ordinary differential equation on I×Rd, a finite maximal endpoint inside I necessarily entails finite-time blowup.
A simple example with a smooth right-hand side is
y′=1+y2,y(0)=0,y(t)=tant,−2π​<t<2π​.
(53)
The derivative identity (tant)′=1+tan2t verifies the equation and the initial condition. Also ∣u2−v2∣=∣u−v∣∣u+v∣≤2R∣u−v∣ for ∣u∣,∣v∣≤R, giving local Lipschitz continuity and therefore uniqueness. Yet the positive blowup time is T=π/2≈1.57079633. Computed values show the accelerating growth:
y(1)≈1.55740772,y(1.4)≈5.79788372,y(1.5)≈14.10141995.
(54)
The right panel under the Picard-Lindelöf theorem deliberately clips its vertical axis at 10: the trajectory has already left the picture at t=arctan10≈1.47112767, before reaching T. A plot ceiling is a display limit, not a finite value at blowup.
The tangent function also quantifies the last part of the trajectory. With δ=T−t>0,
y(T−δ)=sinδcosδ​,δy(T−δ)⟶1(δ↓0).
(55)
Thus the height is approximately the reciprocal of the remaining time. At remaining times δ=0.1,0.01,0.001, the heights are approximately 9.96664442,99.99666664,999.99966667. These follow from the sine and cosine limits at zero and explain the steep rise without relying on the appearance of a clipped function graph.
The distinction is useful when interpreting a solution of a differential equation: uniqueness certifies a trajectory while it exists, while growth estimates decide how far it can be followed. For y′=1+y2, the Lipschitz constant 2R on a bounded state interval grows with R, and the right-hand side grows quadratically. There is no global Lipschitz constant or linear-growth bound of the kind used by the finite-dimensional continuation criterion. Smoothness at every finite state is entirely compatible with finite-time blowup.
