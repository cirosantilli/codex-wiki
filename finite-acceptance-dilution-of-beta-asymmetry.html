A detector collects a range of solid angles, not a single direction. If its nonnegative angular response is w(Ω), define
G=∫w(Ω)dΩ,k=G1​∫w(Ω)cosθdΩ.
(9)
Integrating the polarized beta-decay angular distribution gives a count proportional to G(1+ak). For a uniform forward cone with half-angle θ0​, put c0​=cosθ0​. Direct integration gives
k=∫c0​1​du∫c0​1​udu​=21+c0​​.
(10)
The opposite cone has mean cosine −k. A hemisphere gives k=1/2; a narrow cone tends to k=1. Hence a spin-reversal cross-ratio for beta decay measures ak, and an acceptance correction is needed to obtain a.
With unequal angular responses, let the two laboratory mean cosines be k+​ and k−​, including their signs. Then the actual expectation is
R=(1+ak−​)(1−ak+​)(1+ak+​)(1−ak−​)​,
(11)
which reduces to the simple square only when k−​=−k+​. The cancellation of stable multiplicative detection efficiencies survives, but their angular shapes still matter. An energy-integrated response likewise averages A(E)Pβ(E)cosθ with the accepted spectrum; a mean speed cannot in general be substituted independently of acceptance.
Additive backgrounds, unequal polarization magnitudes or detection efficiencies that change on reversal do not have the assumed factorization. For example, a reversal-dependent efficiency cross-ratio multiplies R by η++​η−−​/(η−+​η+−​). Background subtraction also creates statistical complications: positive expected signal counts are assumed in this derivation, whereas finite samples with Poisson distributions can contain zero counts. Taking square roots of negative background-subtracted counts is not a valid estimator. These limits explain why reversal and independent preparation checks belong together.
