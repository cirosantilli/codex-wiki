Electrolytic cell

 Home Chemistry Electrochemistry Electrolysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 316
An electrolytic cell uses an external source of electrical work to drive electrode chemical reactions. In the ordinary two-terminal driven mode, its positive anode supports oxidation and its negative cathode supports reduction. The source removes electrons from the anode and supplies electrons to the cathode; through the electrolyte, net conventional current goes from anode toward cathode. Terminal signs refer to the driven cell's terminals relative to each other. The source supplies electrical power to the cell. See 17.7 Electrolysis (§17.7: electrolysis and electroplating).
For example, transferring copper from a soluble copper anode onto a conducting object at the cathode has the ideal half-reactions
positive anode:negative cathode:​Cu(s)⟶Cu2+(aq)+2e−,Cu2+(aq)+2e−⟶Cu(s).​
(13)
Their sum transfers copper between surfaces. For identical bulk states, this transfer need not have a positive bulk Gibbs free energy change; finite electric current still needs applied drive to overcome losses. Thus "consumes electrical work" does not mean every electrolytic cell stores all that work as chemical Gibbs free energy. Electrolysis can instead redistribute material. The charge-to-amount relation remains ξ=Q/(2F) when only these half-reactions carry the electric current; competing water reactions would change the deposited amount. The electroplating principle and charge accounting are described in 17.7 Electrolysis (§17.7: electroplating and quantitative aspects).
The University of Washington's teaching demonstration makes this material transfer visible with a copper electrode and a stainless-steel electrode in a glass cell containing 0.5molL−1 CuSO4​. The external source connects its positive lead to copper and negative lead to steel; the reported observation is a copper coating on the steel. Reversing the source polarity removes the deposited copper again: the coated surface now supports oxidation and acts as the anode. This polarity reversal changes the drive, unlike swapping the measurement leads in the Daniell cell voltage and load experiment. Coating appearance alone does not measure charge efficiency or exclude competing chemical reactions. See University of Washington, Electroplating Copper on Stainless Steel: apparatus and reversible coating demonstration.
The copper cathode in this driven example is negative, while the copper cathode of a discharging Daniell cell is positive: both support exactly the same reduction half-reaction. Likewise, an anode need not lose material if its conducting surface is absent from its net half-reaction. Neither terminal sign nor a universal mass-change rule defines an anode or cathode.
