Peltier coefficient (Π)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Electromagnetism Thermoelectric effect Peltier effect
OurBigBook.com Words: 439 Articles: 1
At uniform temperature, define the material Peltier coefficient by transported heat current along positive conventional current:
Q˙​tr​=ΠI.
(17)
Its units are W/A=J/C=V. In one dimension, q=Πj−κdT/dx, with electric current density j and thermal conductivity κ. Here heat excludes the electrochemical potential contribution to total energy flux.[ref]
Now orient positive conventional current I from material A into material B, at a common junction temperature T. Define Q˙​abs​>0 as heat taken from the junction's thermal surroundings. The outgoing transported heat minus the incoming transported heat gives
ΠA→Babs​=ΠB​−ΠA​,Q˙​abs​=(ΠB​−ΠA​)I.
(18)
The local released heat is Q˙​rel​=−Q˙​abs​. Authors defining a junction coefficient by released heat therefore use the opposite sign. Exchanging A and B reverses the coefficient; reversing I at fixed A and B reverses the junction's thermal response. An individual material coefficient and a junction difference are different quantities.
For an original stipulated example, choose SA​=−100μV/K and SB​=+150μV/K at T=300K. The Kelvin thermoelectric relation gives ΠA​=−0.030V, ΠB​=+0.045V. A positive 2A flowing A to B absorbs (0.075V)(2A)=0.150W. A separate 0.010Ω junction electrical resistance would dissipate 0.040W for either direction. If all that dissipation heats the junction, its net absorbed power is 0.110W forwards and −0.190W backwards. These numbers illustrate a chosen thermal accounting boundary, not measured material data or a universal allocation of contact heating.
