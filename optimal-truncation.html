Optimal truncation

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Asymptotic expansion
OurBigBook.com Words: 280
Optimal truncation chooses how many terms to retain for a useful finite approximation. In a divergent asymptotic expansion, increasing the truncation order eventually makes the terms larger rather than smaller. Stopping near a smallest term often gives the best direct approximation, but the actual remainder must be checked. A small last term alone is not a universal error bound.[ref]
For a divergent factorial series can approximate a convergent integral, Tk​=k!/zk decreases while k+1<z and increases while k+1>z. Thus the useful cutoff is near k=z, rather than at arbitrarily large order. At z=10, T9​=T10​=0.00036288 are the two smallest terms. Retaining N=10 terms gives
S10​(10)=0.91545632,∣F(10)−S10​(10)∣≤0.00036288.
(11)
The actual error is about 0.0001770. Retaining twenty terms instead gives S20​(10)≈0.9076137084, with error about 0.008020: more work produces a worse answer.
For this example the bound N!/zN is explicit, so a requested tolerance can be tested before using a truncation. For a different integral, a nearby saddle point or simple pole can change the relevant remainder scale. This is why the smallest-term rule is a useful guide, rather than a replacement for remainder analysis.
The cutoff for factorial terms n!z−n has n=∣z∣+O(1). Better analysis can expose contributions smaller than every retained inverse power, linking optimal truncation to the Stokes phenomenon. Such contributions can be important even when many asymptotic coefficients agree. Directly summing ever more terms does not automatically recover them.
