Residue (Res)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 409 Articles: 1
The residue of a function f at an isolated singularity a is its Laurent coefficient of degree −1. More explicitly, if f is holomorphic for 0<∣z−a∣<ρ, write its Laurent series as
f(z)=∑n=−∞∞​cn​(z−a)n,Res(f,a)=c−1​.
(169)
This one Laurent coefficient determines the contour integral around a sufficiently small counterclockwise circle about a. A complicated local Laurent series can therefore leave a remarkably simple answer after one complete trip around the point.
For a simple pole, multiplying by z−a removes the pole, leaving a holomorphic extension whose value at a is the residue:
Res(f,a)=limz→a​(z−a)f(z).
(170)
There is a useful shortcut for a quotient f=g/h. If g and h are holomorphic near a, h has a simple zero there, and g(a)=0, then f has a simple pole and
Res(g/h,a)=h′(a)g(a)​.
(171)
The first-order Taylor series of h explains the denominator: dividing h(z) by z−a leaves its derivative at a in the limit. This computes a residue without finding the full Laurent series, which is especially useful when the numerator or denominator is complicated. [ref]
For example, the rational function f(z)=1/(z2+1) has simple poles at i and −i. Factoring the denominator makes both residues immediately visible:
f(z)=(z−i)(z+i)1​,Res(f,i)=2i1​=−2i​,Res(f,−i)=−2i1​=2i​.
(172)
The signs matter: these residues cancel when both poles are enclosed, but either one contributes when enclosed alone. The residue theorem below turns this distinction into different contour integrals of the same function.
A residue is not the same thing as a pole, nor does it measure the order of a pole. The function 1/z2 has a double pole at zero but residue zero. Conversely,
e1/z=1+z1​+2!z21​+3!z31​+⋯
(173)
has an essential singularity at zero and residue one. Infinitely many negative powers cause no difficulty in reading off that particular Laurent coefficient. At a removable singularity the residue is zero. [ref]
Why does only this Laurent coefficient survive? Parametrize a counterclockwise circle by z=a+reit, 0≤t≤2π, with 0<r<ρ. For every integer n,
∮∣z−a∣=r​(z−a)ndz=irn+1∫02π​ei(n+1)tdt={2πi,0,​n=−1,n=−1.​
(174)
When n=−1, the complex exponential completes a whole number of turns and its contributions cancel; when n=−1, it is constant. Uniform convergence of the Laurent series on this circle justifies termwise integration, giving ∮f(z)dz=2πiRes(f,a). Thus the residue isolates exactly the local information that a closed contour integral detects. [ref]
This selection rule explains why a very large value near an isolated singularity need not produce a large contour integral. The double pole 1/z2 grows faster near zero than 1/z, yet its contributions around a circle cancel completely. What matters is the arrangement of complex values along the path, including the differential dz, rather than their moduli alone. A residue records that surviving contribution, not the size of the function near the point.
