Random variable (X:Ω→R)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 36
A real-valued random variable is a measurable function X from a probability space (Ω,F,P) to the real numbers with their Borel sigma-algebra. More general targets give random elements. For every Borel set A, measurability ensures that {ω:X(ω)∈A} is an event, so the question "does the observed value lie in A?" has a defined probability.
The function X is fixed; its input outcome is uncertain. This separates what we measure from how the uncertainty is modeled. A probability distribution is the resulting pushforward measure, PX​(A)=P(X∈A). A cumulative distribution function, probability mass function, or probability density function describes that measure in an appropriate setting. The random variable and its probability distribution are different objects: the former also retains its relationship to other measurements on the same sample space.
For two independent uniform dice, let U,V record their results and define S=U+V and M=max(U,V). The 36 ordered pairs are equally likely, but the resulting probability mass functions differ:
P(S=s)=366−∣s−7∣​(2≤s≤12),P(M=m)=362m−1​(1≤m≤6).
(1)
For the sum, count the diagonal of the outcome grid having total s. For the maximum, subtract the (m−1)2 pairs with both results below m from the m2 pairs with both results at most m. These examples show how a deterministic function transforms a simple input probability distribution into a nonuniform output one.
The corresponding expected values are ES=7 and EM=161/36. The first follows immediately from linearity of expectation; the second follows from ∑m=16​m(2m−1)/36. Neither number must be an attainable result. An expected value describes an average under the probability measure, not a prediction that the next observation will equal it.
The distinction between a random variable and its probability distribution matters for dependence. U and V have the same discrete uniform distribution and are independent random variables. U and a second copy of U also have that same marginal probability distribution, but they always agree. Their joint probability distribution is concentrated on the diagonal; the marginals alone cannot say which relationship holds. This is why a model for several measurements must specify their joint law or an appropriate independence assumption.
Indicator random variables turn events into numbers: 1A​ equals one on A and zero elsewhere, and E1A​=P(A). This connects event counting with expected value and variance, and lets tools for random variables answer questions about probabilities. More complicated functions of observations can describe totals, waiting times, or estimation errors using the same framework.

Random element (X:Ω→E)

Words: 70
Random variable OurBigBook.com
A random element is a measurable function from a probability space (Ω,F,P) to a measurable space (E,E). Its law is the pushforward measure PX​(A)=P(X−1(A)) for A∈E. Real-valued random variables and vector-valued random variables are examples; a stochastic sample path can also be a random element when its path-space sigma-algebra is specified. For a Polish space E, the usual choice is its Borel sigma-algebra. A large deviation principle concerns the laws of a family of such random elements.

Statistical dependence

Words: 46
Random variable OurBigBook.com
Two random variables are statistically dependent when their joint probability distribution differs from the product of their marginal probability distributions. Equivalently, some events determined by the two variables violate the product rule that defines independence. Nonzero covariance implies statistical dependence when the moments exist, but zero covariance does not in general imply independence.

Random sum (Z=∑j=1N​Yj​)

Random variable OurBigBook.com
A random sum has a random number N of summands, usually with N a nonnegative integer-valued random variable. The empty sum is zero. The dependence between N and the summands must be stated; independent summands do not by themselves imply independence from N.

The law of total expectation gives the composition of the count's probability generating function with the summand's moment-generating function. This identity is valid as an extended nonnegative expectation; finiteness near zero requires additional exponential-integrability assumptions.

Discrete random variable

Words: 96
Random variable OurBigBook.com
A random variable is discrete when some finite or countable set D⊆R has P(X∈D)=1. Its probability mass function is pX​(x)=P(X=x); these nonnegative masses sum to one. An event determined by the value has probability P(X∈A)=∑x∈A∩D​pX​(x). Values outside D may still occur on a zero-probability event without changing this definition.
Countable support need not consist of integers: a random variable constantly equal to 3/2 is discrete. Integer-indexed tail-sum formulas therefore require a separate integer-valued hypothesis. For the dice sum in random variable, the expected value is ∑s=212​spS​(s)=7 and the variance is ∑s=212​(s−7)2pS​(s)=35/6; the same answer follows by adding the variances of its independent summands.

Joint probability mass function (pX,Y​(x,y)=P(X=x,Y=y))

Words: 63
Random vector OurBigBook.com
For discrete random variables X,Y, their joint probability mass function assigns a probability to each possible ordered pair. Its entries are nonnegative and sum to one. Marginalization gives the probability mass functions pX​(x)=∑y​pX,Y​(x,y) and pY​(y)=∑x​pX,Y​(x,y). In a table with rows indexed by x and columns by y, these are the row and column totals. The joint probability distribution records how the coordinates occur together, while the marginal distributions describe each separately.

Independent random variables (P(X1​,…,Xn​)​=PX1​​⊗⋯⊗PXn​​)

Words: 2k Articles: 19
Random variable OurBigBook.com
Random variables X1​,…,Xn​ are independent when, for every choice of Borel sets A1​,…,An​,
P(⋂i=1n​{Xi​∈Ai​})=∏i=1n​P(Xi​∈Ai​).
(3)
The requirement covers all these events, not only one selected combination of values. Equivalently, the generated sigma-algebras are independent sigma-algebras, or the joint probability distribution is the product measure of the marginal distributions. For an infinite family, the same condition must hold for every finite subfamily. For random elements, replace Borel sets by measurable sets in their specified target spaces.
For discrete random variables, checking every entry of the joint probability mass function is enough:
pX1​,…,Xn​​(x1​,…,xn​)=∏i=1n​pXi​​(xi​).
(4)
Summing this identity over the desired sets proves the event formulation. For general real-valued random variables, checking point probabilities alone can say nothing: a pair with no point masses has P(X=x,Y=y)=P(X=x)P(Y=y)=0 even when Y=X. The all-set definition avoids that trap. See Gautam Iyer, Conditional Probability and Independence, definitions 12, 16 and 18 and proposition 19, with the continuous case treated in his [ref].
For a concrete comparison, let X,Y be independent fair bits: each has a Bernoulli distribution with parameter 1/2. Their joint probability mass function, with rows X=0,1 and columns Y=0,1, is
Jind​=(1/41/4​1/41/4​).
(5)
All four entries equal the product of the corresponding row and column totals, (1/2)(1/2). For example, P(Y=1∣X=0)=1/2: learning one bit leaves the other one's probability distribution unchanged.
Now keep a fair X but set Y=X. The same marginal distributions give the same product table, yet the actual joint probability mass function is
Jsame​=(1/20​01/2​).
(6)
Here P(X=0,Y=1)=0 differs from (1/2)(1/2)=1/4, and P(Y=1∣X=1)=1. Thus matching marginal distributions does not establish independence. Grinstead and Snell formulate the discrete product criterion in Introduction to Probability, section 4.1, definition 4.4, printed page 143.
Independence is stronger than zero covariance. For square-integrable independent random variables, E[XY]=E[X]E[Y], so they are uncorrelated random variables. The converse fails: take X with a discrete uniform distribution on {−1,0,1} and Y=X2. Direct calculation gives
E[X]=0,E[Y]=2/3,E[XY]=E[X3]=0,Cov(X,Y)=0.
(7)
Nevertheless, Y is a nonconstant function determined by X. The event X=0 is exactly the event Y=0, so P(X=0,Y=0)=1/3, whereas P(X=0)P(Y=0)=1/9. The positive and negative contributions to the covariance cancel without erasing the statistical dependence.
The usefulness of independence is that a large joint probability distribution can be assembled from small pieces. For n fair independent bits, each of the 2n patterns has probability 2−n. Products also simplify expected values, and vanishing cross covariances simplify the variance of a sum. Separate measurable functions of independent random variables remain independent: each output event is already an input event, so the defining product rule still applies. These are consequences of a model's independence assumption; whether the assumption describes actual observations is a question for statistical inference.

Pairwise independent random variables (Xi​⊥⊥Xj​(i=j))

Words: 304 Articles: 1
Independent random variables OurBigBook.com
Random variables are pairwise independent when every two distinct members are independent random variables. Each pair's joint probability distribution must factor for all its events; examining only a pair's covariance is insufficient. Full independence implies pairwise independence by taking the other sets in the definition to be the whole target spaces. The reverse implication fails once there are three or more variables.
Pairwise independence is enough to add variances: for a finite family of square-integrable pairwise independent random variables,
Var(∑i=1n​Xi​)=∑i=1n​Var(Xi​),
(8)
because all cross covariances vanish in the variance of a sum. Thus pairwise independence can support calculations about the size of fluctuations while failing to determine the full joint probability distribution. The following example makes that distinction visible.

Pairwise independence does not imply joint independence (Z=X⊕Y)

Words: 202
Pairwise independent random variables OurBigBook.com
Let X,Y be independent fair bits, and let Z=X⊕Y be their exclusive or: Z=1 precisely when X=Y. The four possible triples are 000,011,101,110, each with probability 1/4. Each coordinate is a fair bit. Writing out all eight patterns gives
(x,y,z)pX,Y,Z​(x,y,z)pX​(x)pY​(y)pZ​(z)​0001/41/8​00101/8​01001/8​0111/41/8​10001/8​1011/41/8​1101/41/8​11101/8​​
(9)
Every pair has all four cells 00,01,10,11 with probability 1/4:
JX,Y​=JX,Z​=JY,Z​=(1/41/4​1/41/4​).
(10)
For (X,Z), the four allowed triples project to 00,01,11,10; for (Y,Z) they project to 00,11,01,10. Each projection lists each pair exactly once, proving pairwise independence. But P(X=0,Y=0,Z=0)=1/4 differs from the product 1/8, so the three are not independent random variables. Indeed, any two determine the third. A single coordinate reveals nothing about another, while two coordinates together reveal it completely.
The exclusive or example is the complementary-bit version of the two-toss exercise in Grinstead and Snell, Introduction to Probability, section 4.1, exercise 7, printed page 151. It is a compact reason to check the full product rule whenever a calculation uses three-way or higher events.

Conditional independence (X⊥⊥Y∣Z)

Words: 1k Articles: 14
Independent random variables OurBigBook.com
Random variables X and Y are conditionally independent given Z when, for every pair of Borel sets A,B, the following identity holds almost surely:
P(X∈A,Y∈B∣Z)=P(X∈A∣Z)P(Y∈B∣Z).
(11)
Here conditional probabilities are conditional expectations of the corresponding indicator random variables given the sigma-algebra generated by Z. This defines the condition even when P(Z=z)=0. In terms of regular conditional distributions, the conditional joint law is the product of the conditional marginal distributions for almost every conditioning value.
For discrete random variables, at every z with P(Z=z)>0, the condition becomes
pX,Y∣Z=z​(x,y)=pX∣Z=z​(x)pY∣Z=z​(y).
(12)
Where P(X=x,Z=z)>0, this is equivalent to P(Y=y∣X=x,Z=z)=P(Y=y∣Z=z). Once Z is known, observing X provides no further information about Y. See Cosma Shalizi, Information Theory I, section Conditional independence and conditional mutual information for this predictive interpretation.
For a worked example, suppose a latent variable T chooses between two environments with P(T=0)=P(T=1)=1/2. Given T=t, take X,Y to be independent and identically distributed with a Bernoulli distribution of parameter pt​, where p0​=0.1 and p1​=0.9. Think of two separate trials run under the same quiet or active environment. Their conditional joint probability mass functions, with rows X=0,1 and columns Y=0,1, are
J0​=(0.810.09​0.090.01​),J1​=(0.010.09​0.090.81​).
(13)
Each is exactly the product of its own conditional marginal distributions: (0.9,0.1) for T=0, and (0.1,0.9) for T=1. Thus X,Y are conditionally independent given T.
If the environment is unobserved, the law of total probability averages the two tables:
J=21​J0​+21​J1​=(0.410.09​0.090.41​).
(14)
Both unconditional marginal distributions are fair, but 0.41=0.5×0.5. The law of total expectation gives
E[X]=E[Y]=0.5,E[XY]=21​(0.12+0.92)=0.41,Cov(X,Y)=0.41−0.25=0.16.
(15)
Observing X=1 makes the active environment more plausible, so it raises P(Y=1∣X=1) to 0.41/0.5=0.82. Knowing the actual environment removes this source of statistical dependence. The covariance induced by a shared latent variable lemma explains the calculation without listing all the outcomes.
This is why conditional independence is useful in statistical modelling: a model can multiply simple conditional laws and then average over a shared latent variable. The resulting observations can remain statistically dependent. Conditional independence graphs organize such information relations; their interpretation as causes requires additional assumptions from causal inference. Conditioning describes the information available to an observer. It does not, by itself, describe what would happen if an experimenter changed a variable.

Conditioning can destroy independence (X⊥⊥Y⟹X⊥⊥Y∣Z)

Words: 101
Conditional independence OurBigBook.com
Return to pairwise independence does not imply joint independence, where X,Y are independent fair bits and Z=X⊕Y is their exclusive or. Given Z=0, only 000 and 110 remain, each with conditional probability 1/2. Given Z=1, only 011 and 101 remain, again each with conditional probability 1/2. Consequently,
JX,Y∣Z=0​=(1/20​01/2​),JX,Y∣Z=1​=(01/2​1/20​).
(16)
The conditional marginal distributions are still fair, so their product has four entries 1/4 in either case. The actual tables differ: Y=X given Z=0, and Y=1−X given Z=1. Thus unconditional independence need not survive conditioning. Observing whether two bits agree restricts their possible combinations; no physical interaction between the original trials is required to produce that information constraint.

Covariance induced by a shared latent variable (Cov(A,B)=Var(g(T)))

Words: 149
Conditional independence OurBigBook.com
Let A,B be indicator random variables with conditional independence given a latent variable T, and suppose E[A∣T]=E[B∣T]=g(T). Then
Cov(A,B)=Var(g(T))≥0.
(17)
For a short proof, conditional independence gives E[AB∣T]=g(T)2. The law of total expectation gives E[AB]=E[g(T)2] and E[A]=E[B]=E[g(T)]. Subtract their product to obtain the claimed variance. Since 0≤g(T)≤1, all the required expected values exist.
In the two-environment example of conditional independence, g(T) is 0.1 or 0.9 with equal probabilities. Hence E[g(T)]=0.5 and Var(g(T))=((0.1−0.5)2+(0.9−0.5)2)/2=0.16. The two trials have zero conditional covariance in either environment, yet their unconditional covariance is positive because their success probabilities rise and fall together.
More generally, square-integrable conditionally independent random variables satisfy
Cov(A,B)=Cov(E[A∣T],E[B∣T])
(18)
by the law of total covariance. Equal conditional means reduce this to the displayed variance; unequal conditional means need not give a nonnegative covariance. The equal-mean result explains correlated component evolutionary states in a coeval binary population without correlation between initial masses: variation in a shared environment can supply the entire covariance.
