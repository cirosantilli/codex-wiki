Proceed by induction on n=dimV. The zero-dimensional case is immediate. Over C, the fundamental theorem of algebra supplies a root λ of the characteristic polynomial. Since det(T−λI)=0, there is a nonzero vector v1​ with Tv1​=λv1​. The line W=Cv1​ is invariant, so T induces a linear map T on V/W. By induction, choose an upper triangular basis for that quotient and lift its vectors to v2​,…,vn​. Then Tvj​∈span(v1​,…,vj​) for every j. Thus v1​,…,vn​ is the required basis and T is triangularizable​.
The same conclusion holds over F when the characteristic polynomial splits over F. Indeed an eigenvalue then lies in F, and the block matrix arising from the invariant line gives χT​(X)=(X−λ)χT​(X). The quotient characteristic polynomial therefore also splits, so the induction works over F without requiring algebraic closure.

Write N=T−I. Cayley-Hamilton theorem gives N3=0. Its minimal polynomial is therefore Xk for exactly one k∈{1,2,3}, so the possibilities for that of T are
X−1,(X−1)2,(X−1)3.​
(1)
Let d=dimkerN, which is the dimension of the only eigenspace. If d=3, then N=0 and T=I.
If d=2, rank-nullity theorem gives dimimN=1. The restriction of the nilpotent map N to this invariant line is zero, so N2=0. Choose v3​ with v2​=Nv3​=0, and choose v1​∈kerN independent of v2​. Then v3​∈/kerN, so these three vectors form a basis; its matrix for T is the printed J1​(1)⊕J2​(1).
If d=1, then imN has dimension two, so N2 cannot vanish: otherwise imN⊆kerN. Choose v3​ with N2v3​=0 and set v2​=Nv3​, v1​=N2v3​. Applying N2 and then N to a putative linear relation proves these three vectors independent. The matrix is the printed J3​(1).
These cases exhaust the possible eigenspace dimensions. They are pairwise dissimilar because similarity preserves the eigenspace dimension, equivalently the three different minimal polynomials. Hence every matrix is similar to exactly one of the three displayed forms.

Write n=dimV, which is finite by hypothesis. This supplies the meaning of the n used later in the variant; the proof is otherwise unchanged.
The functional ℓ(u)=⟨u,v⟩ is linear and nonzero because ℓ(v)=⟨v,v⟩>0. It therefore has rank one, and rank-nullity theorem gives
v⊥=kerℓ,dimv⊥=n−1.​
(2)
No Gram–Schmidt argument is needed. Every vector decomposes uniquely as u=w+av, where w⊥v and a=⟨u,v⟩/⟨v,v⟩. Substitution gives Sv​(w+av)=w−av. Thus the eigenspace for −1 is Rv, and that for +1 is v⊥. The latter is an actual eigenvalue when n≥2; when n=1 its eigenspace is zero and only −1 occurs. In the plane the map is reflection in the line perpendicular to v.

For equal-length crystallographic root configurations in a plane, taking u=v in the closure condition gives Sv​(v)=−v, so every vector occurs with its negative. For fixed v∈Φ, a vector u∈Φ has ⟨u,v⟩∈{−1,−1/2,0,1/2,1}, because both vectors are unit vectors. Thus its angle relative to v belongs to the finite list 0,±π/3,±π/2,±2π/3,π.
If all vectors are collinear, the set is exactly {v,−v}, giving two opposite points of the unit circle.
If an orthogonal vector w occurs, closure supplies ±v,±w. A unit vector making an angle of π/3 or 2π/3 with v would have inner product ±3​/2 with w, contradicting integrality of twice that inner product. Thus these four points are the whole set: the vertices of a square.
In the remaining case a noncollinear pair makes an angle of π/3 or 2π/3. Changing a sign, take their angles as 0,π/3. Reflecting the second in the line perpendicular to the first supplies the angle 2π/3, and negation supplies the other three hexagon vertices. No perpendicular vector can occur, by the preceding inner-product obstruction. The initial finite list now leaves only these six points. Conversely the opposite pair, square and regular hexagon each satisfy both reflection closure and the integrality condition. Hence
∣Φ∣∈{2,4,6}.​
(3)

For the polynomial ring, use the Euclidean function δ(f)=degf on nonzero polynomials. If a,b∈F[X] and b=0, subtract the multiple of b that cancels the leading term of a. The leading coefficient of b is invertible in the field, and every subtraction strictly lowers the degree of the remainder. This process terminates with a=qb+r and either r=0 or degr<degb. Thus F[X] is a Euclidean domain​.
For the Gauss lemma for polynomials, call an integer polynomial primitive when its coefficient gcd is one. If two primitive polynomials had a nonprimitive product, a prime p would divide every product coefficient. Reducing modulo p would give a zero product of two nonzero polynomials in the integral domain Fp​[X], a contradiction. Therefore the product of primitive integer polynomials is primitive.
To transfer a rational factorization, write each nonzero rational factor as a rational scalar times a primitive integer polynomial by clearing denominators and removing the coefficient gcd. Thus f=qAB with primitive A,B∈Z[X] and q∈Q. Their product is primitive by the lemma. If q=m/n in lowest terms, integrality of every coefficient of f forces n to divide every coefficient of AB, so n=1. Hence q∈Z; absorbing q into either factor produces the required integer factorization with the same positive factor degrees. In particular irreducibility of a primitive polynomial over Z and over Q is equivalent.
Finally, let a/b in lowest terms, b>0, be a rational root of a monic degree-d integer polynomial. Multiplying the equation by bd shows b∣ad. Coprimality forces b=1. Thus every rational root of a monic integer polynomial is an integer​.

If a holomorphic bijection existed, its inverse would be an entire function taking values in the unit disc. Liouville theorem would make that inverse constant, contradicting bijectivity. Thus no holomorphic bijection exists.
A continuous bijection does exist, even a homeomorphism:
h(z)=1−∣z∣z​,h−1(w)=1+∣w∣w​.​
(6)
Both maps preserve the argument and change the radius by the mutually inverse strictly increasing functions r↦r/(1−r) and s↦s/(1+s). The displayed formulas also prove continuity at zero and everywhere else.

An isolated singularity at a means that the function is holomorphic throughout some punctured disc 0<∣z−a∣<r. Its residue is the Laurent coefficient c−1​, equivalently (2πi)−1∫∣z−a∣=ρ​f(z)dz. The singularity is removable if all negative Laurent coefficients vanish, a pole of order k if only finitely many are nonzero and the lowest power is −k, and essential if infinitely many negative coefficients are nonzero.
For the final contour integral, expand etzet/z. On the circle these exponential series converge absolutely and uniformly for t in every bounded set, so their product can be integrated term by term. In
∑j,k≥0​j!k!tj+k​zj−k−2,
(7)
only j−k−2=−1 contributes. Set k=m, j=m+1. Thus
bm​=m!(m+1)!2πi​,m≥0.​
(8)
The resulting power series converges for every complex t by the ratio test. The coefficients are independent of the positive radius R.

The identity theorem for holomorphic functions says that a holomorphic function on a connected open set which has distinct zeros accumulating at a point of the set is identically zero. Equivalently, two holomorphic functions agreeing on such a set of points agree everywhere in that connected open set.
To prove it, expand at an accumulation point a. If the first nonzero Taylor coefficient had index m, the function would factor as (z−a)mh(z) with h(a)=0. Continuity would make h nonzero near a, so no other zeros could approach a, a contradiction. Thus all coefficients vanish and the function vanishes on a neighborhood of a. Let E be the set of points having such a zero neighborhood. It is nonempty and open. If a point in the domain is a limit point of E, it is either already in E or is an accumulation point of distinct zeros, and the same Taylor argument puts it in E. Thus E is relatively closed as well. Connectedness forces E to be the whole domain. Apply this result to the difference of two functions for the equivalent formulation.

Differentiate the Wronskian:
w′=uv′′−u′′v=u(−qv)−(−qu)v=0.
(12)
It is therefore constant. Linear dependence plainly makes it zero. Conversely, if it is zero, the two initial-data vectors (u(0),u′(0)) and (v(0),v′(0)) are linearly dependent. Some constants A,B, not both zero, make Au+Bv and its derivative zero at zero. Uniqueness for a linear second-order equation with continuous coefficients then makes Au+Bv identically zero. Thus w=0⟺u,v are linearly dependent​.
For the prescribed integral put K(x,s)=[u(x)v(s)−u(s)v(x)]/w. Then K(x,x)=0, Kx​(x,x)=−1, and Kxx​(x,s)=−q(x)K(x,s). Differentiation under the integral, using continuity of the coefficients and forcing, gives
y′(x)=∫0x​Kx​(x,s)f(s)ds,y′′(x)=−f(x)−q(x)y(x).
(13)
This proves the required inhomogeneous equation, with the sign of the forcing as printed.
For the explicit forcing take u=coshx, v=sinhx, so w=1 and K(x,s)=−sinh(x−s). Since 2/(es+e−s)=sechs, the integral is
−∫0x​sinh(x−s)sechsds=−xsinhx+coshxlogcoshx.

Indeed expand sinh(x−s)=sinhxcoshs−coshxsinhs and integrate 1 and tanhs. Adding the entire homogeneous solution gives

A critical point is an equilibrium: f(a,b)=g(a,b)=0​. Put X=x−a, Y=y−b. The Jacobian matrix J gives the first-order linearized equation (X˙,Y˙)T=J(X,Y)T. The following eigenvalue tests classify that linearized system. For a continuously differentiable nonlinear system, the cases with nonzero real parts persist as the usual local node, saddle or spiral classifications. The purely imaginary case needs extra nonlinear information, as explained in (iv).

Suppose a nonconstant periodic orbit exists for the continuously differentiable planar vector field. Uniqueness implies that its image over a minimal period is a simple closed curve, enclosing a region Ω of positive area. Along the orbit, the differential form fdy−gdx vanishes because dx=fdt and dy=gdt. Green's theorem, with positive orientation, therefore gives
0=∮∂Ω​(fdy−gdx)=∬Ω​(fx​+gy​)dxdy<0,
(23)
a contradiction. This is the Bendixson-Dulac criterion in the whole plane; the fact that the enclosed region also lies in the domain is essential.
For the second-order equation set y=x˙. The equivalent system is x˙=y, y˙​=−(1+x2)y−cosx. Its divergence is −(1+x2)<0​ throughout the plane. Hence there are no nonconstant periodic solutions.
The nonconstant qualification is necessary: the equilibrium solutions x(t)≡π/2+kπ, k∈Z, do exist and are periodic in the literal sense of satisfying every positive period. The source's usual dynamical interpretation of “periodic solution” here is a nonconstant periodic orbit.
