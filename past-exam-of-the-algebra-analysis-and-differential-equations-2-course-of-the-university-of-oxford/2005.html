An integral domain is a nonzero commutative ring with identity and no zero divisors. A principal ideal domain is an integral domain in which every ideal is generated by one element.
The leading coefficient of a product of nonzero real polynomials is nonzero, so R[x] is an integral domain. The zero ideal is principal. In any nonzero ideal J, choose a nonzero polynomial g of least degree. Divide an arbitrary f∈J by g: f=qg+r with r=0 or degr<degg. Since r∈J, minimality forces r=0. Thus J=(g) and R[x] is a principal ideal domain.
In Z[x] consider J=(2,x). If J=(h), then h divides the constant polynomial 2, so h is constant, and divisibility of x forces h=±1. That would give J=Z[x]. But the constant coefficient of every element 2p+xq is even, so 1∈/J. This contradiction proves Z[x] is not a principal ideal domain.

For a bounded entire function, the preceding result with n=0 gives Liouville's theorem. If a nonconstant polynomial p had no complex root, 1/p would be entire. The leading term gives ∣p(z)∣→∞ at infinity, so 1/p is bounded outside a large disc and is also bounded on the remaining compact disc. Liouville's theorem would make 1/p, and hence p, constant. This proves the fundamental theorem of algebra.
Finally, away from the zeros of cosz, the holomorphic quotient h=f/cosz has modulus at most C. At each zero it therefore has a removable singularity. These isolated points have no finite accumulation, so removing all of them gives a bounded entire function. Liouville's theorem gives a constant c with ∣c∣≤C, and
f(z)=ccosz.​
(13)
Conversely every such multiple satisfies the assumed bound.

For a nonzero principal part the discriminant Δ=b2−ac classifies the equation: it is hyperbolic for Δ>0, parabolic for Δ=0, and elliptic for Δ<0. These are the three definitions requested by the inline roman labels.
In the hyperbolic case the characteristic equation a(dy)2−2bdxdy+c(dx)2=0 gives two distinct real tangent directions. Locally, with suitably smooth coefficients, solve the corresponding characteristic ordinary differential equations to obtain two independent families of curves. Label their level sets ϕ=constant and ψ=constant. Their gradients satisfy
aϕx2​+2bϕx​ϕy​+cϕy2​=0,aψx2​+2bψx​ψy​+cψy2​=0,
(30)
and the coordinate Jacobian is nonzero. The chain rule therefore eliminates uϕϕ​ and uψψ​. The remaining mixed coefficient 2[aϕx​ψx​+b(ϕx​ψy​+ϕy​ψx​)+cϕy​ψy​] is nonzero: otherwise the invertible coordinate change would send the nondegenerate principal quadratic form to zero. Divide by this coefficient and absorb all first-order terms into g, giving uϕψ​=g(ϕ,ψ,u,uϕ​,uψ​). This canonical reduction is local; a global chart is not automatic.
For the specific equation a=y,b=0,c=−1, so it is hyperbolic exactly on y>0, elliptic for y<0, and degenerate parabolic on y=0. For ϕ=3x−2y3/2 and ψ=3x+2y3/2, the Jacobian is 18y​=0. Direct differentiation gives
yuxx​−uyy​=36yuϕψ​+2y​3​(uϕ​−uψ​).
(31)
Since ψ−ϕ=4y3/2, the canonical form is
uϕψ​=6(ψ−ϕ)uψ​−uϕ​​.​
(32)
The characteristic families are 3x−2y3/2=c and 3x+2y3/2=c, with slopes dx/dy=±y​. They are distinct for y>0 and meet the degeneracy line with coincident vertical tangent directions.
