An element a of an integral domain is an irreducible element when it is nonzero and not a unit, and any factorization a=bc has at least one unit factor. The exclusions of zero and units are part of the definition.

An element a is a prime element when it is nonzero and not a unit, and a∣bc implies a∣b or a∣c. If a=bc and a is a prime element, assume for example b=ad. Cancellation in a=adc gives dc=1, so c is a unit. This proves every prime element is an irreducible element.
In a Euclidean domain, each nonzero ideal has a generator of least Euclidean value: division of another member by that generator leaves a remainder in the ideal of smaller value, so the remainder must vanish. Let a be an irreducible element and suppose a∣bc but a∤b. Write (a,b)=(d). Since d∣a, irreducibility says d is a unit or is associate to a. The second case would give a∣b, so d is a unit. Hence 1=ra+sb for some r,s. Multiplying by c shows a∣c. Thus irreducibles in a Euclidean domain are prime.
For R=Z[i7​], use the multiplicative integer field norm N(m+ni7​)=m2+7n2. Units have field norm one and are exactly ±1. If 2=uv were a product of nonunits, their norms would be integers greater than one multiplying to four, hence both would have field norm two. But m2+7n2=2 has no integer solution: n=0 makes the left side at least seven, and n=0 would give m2=2. Therefore 2 is an irreducible element.
On the other hand,
(1+i7​)(1−i7​)=8
(7)
is divisible by 2, while neither factor is, because divisibility by 2 requires both integer coefficients to be even. Thus 2 is not a prime element in this ring. It is also not a prime element in Z[i]: 2∣(1+i)2=2i, but 2∤1+i. In fact 2=(1+i)(1−i) is already a nontrivial factorization there.

At a zero a, Taylor's theorem gives f(z)=∑n≥0​cn​(z−a)n near a. If some coefficient is nonzero, let m be the least such index. Then f(z)=(z−a)mq(z) with q(a)=0, and continuity makes q nonzero on a smaller disc. Thus a is an isolated zero. A limit point of zeros is also a zero by continuity and cannot be isolated, so all Taylor coefficients there vanish and f vanishes on a nonempty open disc.
Let A be the set of points having a neighborhood on which f vanishes. It is nonempty and open. A point in its relative closure either already belongs to A or is a limit point of distinct zeros; the preceding argument puts it in A in either case. Thus A is also relatively closed. Connectedness of D gives A=D, proving f is identically zero.
For conjugate reflection, the disc is invariant under conjugation, and
wh(z+w)−h(z)​=wˉg(zˉ+wˉ)−g(zˉ)​​⟶g′(zˉ)​.
(18)
Hence h is holomorphic, with derivative g′(zˉ)​. If g is real on the real diameter, then for real x∈(−1,1), h(x)=g(x)​=g(x). The holomorphic difference g−h therefore has zeros accumulating at zero, and the identity theorem just proved gives g=h on the entire disc.

Assume the vector field and multiplier are continuously differentiable, as required for Green's theorem and uniqueness of trajectories. A nonconstant periodic trajectory of a planar autonomous system is a simple closed orbit: uniqueness prevents crossings, and one traversal can be taken over its least period. Let Ω be its bounded interior. Along the orbit dx=fdt, dy=gdt, so
∮∂Ω​ϕfdy−ϕgdx=∫ϕ(fg−gf)dt=0.
(19)
Green's theorem identifies this with ∬Ω​[∂x​(ϕf)+∂y​(ϕg)]dxdy, which is strictly positive by hypothesis, a contradiction. Thus there is no nonconstant periodic orbit. This standard periodic-orbit criterion does not by itself exclude stationary solutions.
For the first concrete system choose ϕ=−1. Then
∂x​(ϕf)+∂y​(ϕg)=e−x>0.
(20)
There are also no equilibria: f=0 requires y=1, but then g=−e−x−x2<0. Consequently it has no periodic solution, including constant ones.
For the second system the origin is outside the domain of the printed vector field. For r>0,
r˙=rxx˙+yy˙​​=1−r,θ˙=r2xy˙​−yx˙​=1.
(21)
Thus for an admissible initial point with ρ=a2+b2​>0,
r(t)=1+(ρ−1)e−t,θ(t)=α+t,​
(22)
where a=ρcosα, b=ρsinα. These formulas are valid while r>0, in particular for every t≥0. Trajectories rotate counterclockwise, spiral outward when 0<r<1, and inward when r>1, approaching the unit circle. On r=1 they give x(t)=cos(α+t), y(t)=sin(α+t), a periodic orbit with least period 2π. It is an attracting limit cycle. There is no contradiction with the preceding criterion: this vector field is singular at the origin and has the exhibited closed orbit.
