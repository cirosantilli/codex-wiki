The minimal polynomial of T is the monic nonzero polynomial of smallest degree annihilating T. Such a polynomial exists, since sufficiently many powers of T are linearly dependent in the finite-dimensional space End(V). If two monic annihilators had the same least degree, their difference would be an annihilator of smaller degree and hence zero. Thus it is unique. Division with remainder also shows that every annihilating polynomial is a multiple of it.
The primary decomposition theorem states that if m=∏j=1r​fjej​​ with distinct monic irreducible polynomials, then
V=⨁j=1r​Vj​,Vj​=kerfj​(T)ej​.
(4)
These subspaces are T-invariant, and the restriction to Vj​ has minimal polynomial fjej​​.
Let r=gcd(q,m), chosen monic. The Bézout's identity for polynomials gives r=aq+bm. If q(T)v=0, then r(T)v=a(T)q(T)v+b(T)m(T)v=0. Conversely, q=rh, so r(T)v=0 implies q(T)v=0. Therefore
kerq(T)=kergcd(q,m)(T).​
(5)

If a basis consists of eigenvectors, let λ1​,…,λr​∈K be the distinct eigenvalues. A polynomial annihilates the diagonal action exactly when it vanishes at each λj​. Consequently the minimal polynomial is ∏j​(X−λj​), a product of distinct linear factors. It therefore has no repeated irreducible factor, proving (i) implies (ii).

Suppose m has no repeated irreducible factor. For every polynomial p and integer n≥1, its monic greatest common divisors satisfy gcd(pn,m)=gcd(p,m), since raising p to a power changes no membership of an irreducible factor of the square-free m. This is the square-free minimal polynomial criterion for polynomial kernel stabilization. The kernel identity proved above gives
ker(p(T)n)=kergcd(pn,m)(T)=kergcd(p,m)(T)=kerp(T).
(6)
Thus (ii) implies (iii) over every field.
Over C, the distinct irreducible factors of m are linear. The primary decomposition theorem therefore reads V=⨁j​ker(T−λj​I), a direct sum of eigenspaces. Taking a basis in each summand gives an eigenbasis, proving (ii) implies (i) over the complex numbers.
Over R, take T=(01​−10​). Its minimal polynomial is X2+1, which is an irreducible polynomial over R and has no repeated factor. But it has no real eigenvalues, so no real eigenbasis. This is the required counterexample. Over a general field, the phrase “no repeated factors” here refers to irreducible factors in K[X], rather than a prior assumption that those factors split.

An ideal is an additive subgroup I⊆R with rI⊆I for all r∈R. A ring homomorphism preserves addition and multiplication; for the rings with identity here use the standard unital convention θ(1R​)=1S​. If a,b∈kerθ, then θ(a−b)=0, and if r∈R then θ(ra)=θ(r)θ(a)=0. Thus the kernel is an ideal.
A maximal ideal is a proper ideal I=R such that an ideal J satisfying I⊆J⊆R is either I or R.

ii OurBigBook.com
Use the standard definition that a prime ideal is proper, in addition to the product condition. The quotient operations are
(r+I)+(s+I)=(r+s)+I,(r+I)(s+I)=rs+I.
(8)
Its zero and identity are I and 1+I, which differ because I is proper. If (r+I)(s+I)=I, then rs∈I, so primality gives r∈I or s∈I. Thus one factor is zero. The commutative quotient ring has no nonzero zero divisors and is therefore an integral domain.
The printed definition omits properness. If that omission were read literally, I=R would satisfy its product condition, but R/I would be the zero ring, which is not an integral domain under the standard convention 1=0. Properness is the necessary qualification, retained explicitly in this proof.

The inverse image I=θ−1(J) is an ideal, by preservation of differences and multiplication. It is proper since θ(1)=1∈/J. If ab∈I, then θ(a)θ(b)∈J; primality of J gives θ(a)∈J or θ(b)∈J, hence a∈I or b∈I. Therefore the inverse image is prime.
For maximality, take the inclusion Z↪Q and J=(0). This is a maximal ideal of the field Q, but its inverse image is (0) in Z, strictly contained in the proper ideal 2Z, so it is not maximal. Preservation of identity matters for proper inverse images: without it the zero homomorphism could pull a proper ideal back to the whole ring.

Use the boundary form of Cauchy's integral theorem: the integral of a holomorphic function around the oriented boundary of a compact region on whose neighborhood it is holomorphic is zero, including annular regions with the inner boundary oppositely oriented. Apply it to f(z)/(z−a) between the circles of radii r and ρ, both around a. It gives
2πi1​∫∣z−a∣=r​z−af(z)​dz=2π1​∫02π​f(a+ρeit)dt⟶f(a)
(9)
as ρ↓0, by continuity. This proves the Cauchy integral formula.
For a bounded entire function with ∣f∣≤M, apply that formula at variable points inside a fixed circle and differentiate its kernel under the integral. At its center a, this gives
f′(a)=2πi1​∫∣z−a∣=R​(z−a)2f(z)​dz,∣f′(a)∣≤RM​.
(10)
The circle can have any radius because the function is entire. Letting R→∞ gives f′(a)=0 at every a, so f is constant. This proves Liouville's theorem.
Finally, if a nonconstant entire function omitted all points within distance ϵ of some w, then g=1/(f−w) would be entire and bounded by 1/ϵ. Liouville's theorem would make g, and hence f, constant. Therefore every such neighborhood contains a value of f, proving the dense image of a nonconstant entire function property.

A Möbius transformation is M(z)=(az+b)/(cz+d) with ad−bc=0, extended to the Riemann sphere at its pole and at infinity. Wherever its finite coordinate is defined,
M′(z)=(cz+d)2ad−bc​=0.
(14)
At a pole, the ordinary complex derivative is undefined, rather than zero; using the target coordinate 1/M gives a nonzero derivative there as well. Thus the source's nowhere-zero assertion is interpreted in local sphere coordinates, or on the finite domain with the pole excluded.
Composition corresponds to multiplication of the invertible matrices (ac​bd​), with scalar multiples giving the same map. The identity matrix gives the identity, and the inverse matrix gives an inverse Möbius map. This proves the group property on the Riemann sphere.
The two boundary circles meet at ±i. Put
w=M(z)=z+iz−i​,z=i1−w1+w​.​
(15)
For w=u+iv, direct substitution gives
∣z+1∣2−2=∣1−w∣24(u−v)​,∣z−1∣2−2=∣1−w∣24(u+v)​.
(16)
This gives a lens-to-half-plane map through the intersection points. Both lens inequalities hold exactly when u<−∣v∣, the open sector with continuous argument between 3π/4 and 5π/4. This proves bijectivity, since the inverse is defined throughout that sector and the excluded endpoint −i is not in the lens.
Rotate the sector through −3π/4 and square it, obtaining a bijection onto the upper half-plane. Therefore a conformal map is
F(z)=i(z+iz−i​)2.​
(17)
Squaring is injective on this sector because it contains no pair of opposite points. Its derivative and the Möbius derivative are nonzero in the interior, so the bijection is conformal.

A critical point of a dynamical system is a point where both components of the vector field vanish. For a continuously differentiable vector field, strictly negative real parts of both eigenvalues of its Jacobian matrix imply asymptotic stability of the equilibrium. If an isolated critical point is a center of a planar dynamical system and the eigenvalues are distinct, they must be a nonzero purely imaginary conjugate pair. Purely imaginary eigenvalues alone do not in general prove nonlinear stability; the nonlinear ordinary differential equation system must still be examined.
Here the critical points are (0,0) and (±1,0), with Jacobian (01−3x2​10​). At zero its eigenvalues are 1,−1, giving an unstable saddle equilibrium. The unstable direction is (1,1) and the stable direction is (1,−1). Thus trajectories leaving the origin are tangent to y=x, and those returning are tangent to y=−x, with both opposite rays included.
An exact first integral is
H(x,y)=2y2​−2x2​+4x4​,dtdH​=(−x+x3)y+y(x−x3)=0.
(18)
The two homoclinic loops belong to H=0:
y2=x2(1−2x2​),∣x∣≤2​.
(19)
They confirm the incoming and outgoing tangent directions. For example the right loop is x=2​sech(t−t0​), y=−2​sech(t−t0​)tanh(t−t0​); changing both signs gives the left loop.
At (1,0) put x=1+X, y=Y. The exact equations are X′=Y, Y′=−2X−3X2−X3, so the linearization of a dynamical system gives X′′+2X=0. Its nonconstant solutions have
T=2​2π​=2​π,2X2+Y2=constant.​
(20)
The same linear classification holds at (−1,0). For the nonlinear ordinary differential equation system, H has strict local minima −1/4 at both points. Levels −1/4<H<0 are two families of smooth closed ovals, proving that both are stable centers, rather than asymptotically stable equilibria. These give actual periodic orbits of the nonlinear ordinary differential equation system, with small-amplitude periods tending to 2​π.

The method of characteristics uses
dsdx​=a(x,y,u),dsdy​=b(x,y,u),dsdu​=c(x,y,u).
(21)
For a graph parametrized by (x,y,u(x,y)), a normal vector is N=(−ux​,−uy​,1). The PDE says N⋅(a,b,c)=0, so the characteristic vector is tangent to the solution surface. Flowing a suitable initial curve along these directions constructs a surface; where its projection to the (x,y) plane has nonzero Jacobian determinant, the inverse function theorem makes it a graph. Local uniqueness of this general flow needs the usual regularity assumptions beyond mere continuity. The particular characteristic system below is smooth and can be solved explicitly.
Label the initial points by η≥0. Since dx/ds=1, take x=s. The other two equations integrate to
u=1+sinη+2x2​,y=η+x(1+sinη)+6x3​.
(22)
Eliminating η=y−xu+x3/3 gives the implicit solution
u=1+2x2​+sin(y−xu+3x3​).​
(23)
For each fixed ∣x∣<1, the derivative of the displayed y with respect to η is 1+xcosη≥1−∣x∣>0. It increases from its value at η=0 to infinity, so it is a bijection from [0,∞) onto [yL​(x),∞), where
yL​(x)=x+6x3​.​
(24)
Thus there is exactly one characteristic label, and hence one solution value, throughout the requested region, including the initial-label boundary as a trace.
The characteristic fold for sinusoidal data in a forced Burgers equation is seen by differentiating with respect to y:
uy​=1+xcosηcosη​.
(25)
For a fixed integer k≥0, let y∗​=(2k+1)π+7/6. As x→1− at this fixed y∗​, the label tends to (2k+1)π, giving uy​→−∞. To justify the label limit, the limiting map at x=1 is strictly increasing despite isolated zero derivatives, so its inverse has a unique continuous limit. Similarly, at any fixed y∗​=2kπ−7/6 with k≥1, the limit as x→−1+ has η→2kπ and uy​→+∞. These fixed values lie within the region near the respective boundary, proving the requested gradient blow-up.

∫0R​e−ptf′(t)dt=e−pRf(R)−f(0)+p∫0R​e−ptf(t)dt.

h(p)=∫0∞​f(s)∫s∞​e−ptdtds=pf​(p)​​.
