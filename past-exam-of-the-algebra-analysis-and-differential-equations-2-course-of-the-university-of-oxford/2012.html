The minimal polynomial mT​(t) is the unique monic polynomial of least positive polynomial degree satisfying mT​(T)=0. Such an annihilating polynomial exists without using the Cayley-Hamilton theorem: the vector space End(V) has dimension of a vector space equal to n2, so I,T,…,Tn2 are linearly dependent. Normalize a nonzero annihilating polynomial to be monic and choose one of least degree. Two distinct monic choices of that degree would have an annihilating difference of lower degree, contradicting minimality. A nonzero constant cannot annihilate T because V=0.
Choose a basis B and let A=[T]B​. The characteristic polynomial is
χT​(t)=det(tI−A).​
(1)
It is independent of the chosen basis. Indeed a change of basis replaces A by a similar matrix P−1AP, and
det(tI−P−1AP)=det(P−1(tI−A)P)=det(tI−A)
(2)
by multiplicativity of the determinant. Thus this monic degree-n polynomial is an invariant of the linear transformation itself.

Since v is a cyclic vector and V=0, v=0. Finite dimension of a vector space forces a first dependence in the sequence v,Tv,T2v,…. Let k≥1 be the smallest index with Tkv in the linear span of its predecessors. Then

An orthonormal basis (q1​,…,qn​) is a basis with ⟨qi​,qj​⟩=δij​, where δij​ is the Kronecker delta. Each vector has norm one and distinct vectors are orthogonal.
The Gram-Schmidt process starts from a basis (e1​,…,en​) and defines
u1​=e1​,q1​=∥u1​∥u1​​,uk​=ek​−∑j=1k−1​⟨ek​,qj​⟩qj​,qk​=∥uk​∥uk​​.
(9)
The coefficients are in this order because the inner product is linear in its first argument. For i<k, taking the inner product with qi​ gives ⟨uk​,qi​⟩=0. The residual uk​ cannot be zero: otherwise ek​ would belong to the linear span of e1​,…,ek−1​, contradicting the initial basis. Normalization makes ∥qk​∥=1, while induction preserves
span(q1​,…,qk​)=span(e1​,…,ek​).
(10)
Thus the process produces an orthonormal basis of all of V.

A unit in a ring is an element u with a multiplicative inverse in R, so uv=1 for some v∈R.
An irreducible element is a nonzero nonunit a such that every factorization a=bc has at least one of b,c a unit in a ring.
A prime element is a nonzero nonunit p such that p∣bc implies p∣b or p∣c. Equivalently its principal ideal (p) is a nonzero prime ideal. These are definitions in the given commutative ring; the equivalence between irreducibility and primality requires additional hypotheses on the ring.

A Euclidean domain is an integral domain R with a function d:R∖{0}→N0​ such that, for a∈R and nonzero b∈R, there exist q,r∈R with a=qb+r and either r=0 or d(r)<d(b). This is the Euclidean-ring convention used here.
If an ideal I is zero, it is generated by zero. Otherwise choose nonzero a∈I with d(a) minimal. For any b∈I, Euclidean division gives b=qa+r, with r∈I. A nonzero remainder would have d(r)<d(a), contradicting the choice. Hence r=0 and b∈aR. The reverse inclusion follows from a∈I, proving
I=aR.​
(20)
Now suppose a is an irreducible element. Its principal ideal is proper because a is not a unit. If aR⊆J⊆R, write J=bR by the result just proved. Then a=bc for some c∈R. Irreducibility gives either b a unit, so J=R, or c a unit, so bR=aR. There is no intermediate proper ideal, and therefore aR is a maximal ideal.
For a field F, the polynomial ring F[X] is an integral domain and the polynomial division algorithm gives A=QB+R with R=0 or degR<degB. Thus the polynomial degree is a Euclidean function, proving F[X] is a Euclidean domain.

The polynomial Xn−2 is irreducible over Q by Eisenstein's criterion with prime two: all coefficients except the leading one are divisible by two, while the constant coefficient is not divisible by four. Together with Gauss's lemma, this establishes irreducibility in Q[X]. Part (b) shows that its principal ideal is a maximal ideal, so
Kn​=Q[X]/(Xn−2) is a field.​
(21)
Let α denote the residue class of X. The polynomial division algorithm gives every residue class a representative of degree less than n, so 1,α,…,αn−1 span Kn​ over Q. If a linear combination of them vanished, its degree-less-than-n representative would be a multiple of the degree-n polynomial Xn−2, and hence would be zero. They are therefore a basis, giving
dimQ​Kn​=n.​
(22)
The quadratic polynomial X2+1 has no rational root, so it is irreducible over Q. The same maximal-ideal argument proves L=Q[X]/(X2+1) is a field.
These two quadratic fields are not isomorphic. In L, the class of X squares to −1. But K2​ embeds in R by sending its class of X to 2​: evaluation at 2​ has kernel (X2−2), by irreducibility and polynomial division. No real number squares to −1, so no element of K2​ does. A field isomorphism preserves one, minus one and squaring, so it cannot carry L to K2​.

The Cauchy integral formula says that if f is holomorphic on the simply connected open set U, and γ is a positively oriented simple closed curve in U, then, for every z in its interior,
f(z)=2πi1​∫γ​ζ−zf(ζ)​dζ.​
(23)
Here the contour and its enclosed region lie in U; the contour can be taken piecewise continuously differentiable. More generally, the same formula needs only holomorphic function on an open neighbourhood of that closed region. The orientation is counterclockwise, so the winding number about an interior point is one.

A critical point of a dynamical system or equilibrium is a point (a,b) with f(a,b)=g(a,b)=0, so the constant function (x,y)=(a,b) is a solution. Put u=x−a, v=y−b. For a continuously differentiable vector field, its linearization is
dtd​(uv​)=M(uv​),M=D(f,g)(a,b),
(45)
with remainder o(u2+v2​) in the original nonlinear system. For distinct eigenvalues, M is diagonalizable over C, and the linear solutions are combinations of eλj​twj​, where wj​ are corresponding eigenvectors. Real parts determine growth or decay; nonzero imaginary parts give rotation. For hyperbolic equilibria, meaning no eigenvalue has zero real part, the Hartman-Grobman theorem transfers the local stability and qualitative orbit pattern to the nonlinear system. A purely imaginary pair is not hyperbolic: it determines a linear centre, while higher-order terms decide whether the nonlinear system actually has a centre.

The Bendixson-Dulac theorem states that if (P,Q) is a continuously differentiable planar vector field on a simply connected open region D, and a continuously differentiable Dulac function B makes
∂x∂(BP)​+∂y∂(BQ)​
(56)
have one weak sign throughout D, without vanishing identically on any nonempty open subset, then there is no nonconstant periodic orbit contained in D. To see why, a hypothetical simple periodic orbit bounds a region in D. The divergence theorem gives the integral of this expression as the outward flux of B(P,Q) across the orbit. The flux is zero because the vector field is tangent to its orbit, whereas the one-sign area integral is nonzero, a contradiction. The usual stricter version assumes a strictly positive or strictly negative expression everywhere.

For a quasilinear partial differential equation, the characteristic equations for a quasilinear equation are the ordinary differential equations
dsdx​=P(x,y,z),dsdy​=Q(x,y,z),dsdz​=R(x,y,z).​
(74)
A solution surface is locally a differentiable graph z=u(x,y) whose tangent plane contains the vector (P,Q,R) at every point. Its normal is (−ux​,−uy​,1), so the tangency condition is precisely Pux​+Quy​=R. Thus every characteristic lying on that graph follows the vector field without leaving the graph.
Parameterize the given initial curve by γ(t)=(x0​(t),y0​(t),z0​(t)). For each t, solve the characteristic equations for a quasilinear equation with initial value γ(t) at s=0. The resulting map (s,t)↦(x(s,t),y(s,t),z(s,t)) sweeps out a candidate solution surface. If the coefficients and initial data are continuously differentiable, the Picard-Lindelöf theorem supplies this local family. To make it a graph we also need the projection (s,t)↦(x,y) to be locally invertible. At s=0 its Jacobian determinant is
J(0,t)=P(γ(t))y0′​(t)−Q(γ(t))x0′​(t).

The same characteristic initial-value uniqueness gives local uniqueness of this solution wherever the projection remains invertible and the relevant characteristic meets the prescribed data.
