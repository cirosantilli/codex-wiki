If c(λ)=det(λI−T)=0, then λI−T has nonzero kernel of a linear map. A nonzero vector v in that kernel of a linear map satisfies Tv=λv, proving that λ is an eigenvalue. Extend v to a basis. Its first column is (λ,0,…,0)T, so the matrix has block form
[T]=(λ0​∗B​),c(X)=(X−λ)det(XI−B).
(1)
Here B represents the induced operator on V/⟨v⟩.
If the algebraic multiplicity is r, repeat this argument in that quotient: its characteristic polynomial still has λ with multiplicity r−1. Induction and lifting a quotient basis gives a basis whose first r columns have the prescribed upper-triangular form. With U the span of those r vectors, the full matrix is
(λI+N0​∗C​),Nr=0,det(C−λI)=0.
(2)
Thus U is invariant and is contained in Wλ​. Conversely, if (T−λI)sv=0, project to V/U. The induced map C−λI is invertible, so its sth power cannot kill a nonzero class. Hence v∈U. Consequently Wλ​=U, dimWλ​=r​; this proves simultaneously that it is a subspace and T-invariant. It also proves (T−λI)r=0 on this space, without using Cayley-Hamilton theorem in advance.
To prove independence of the different spaces, take a relation ∑λ​vλ​=0 with vλ​∈Wλ​. Fix λ and apply Pλ​(T)=∏μ=λ​(T−μI)rμ​. It kills all terms except vλ​. On Wλ​, each factor T−μI=(λ−μ)I+N is invertible: its inverse is a finite geometric series in the nilpotent N. Hence Pλ​(T)vλ​=0 implies vλ​=0. The sum is direct, and its dimension is ∑rλ​=dimV, so
V=λ⨁​Wλ​.​
(3)
Finally c(T)=∏λ​(T−λI)rλ​ kills each summand, hence every vector. This proves c(T)=0​ without circularly assuming the theorem during the decomposition.
