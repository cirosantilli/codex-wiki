If c(λ)=det(λI−T)=0, then λI−T has nonzero kernel of a linear map. A nonzero vector v in that kernel of a linear map satisfies Tv=λv, proving that λ is an eigenvalue. Extend v to a basis. Its first column is (λ,0,…,0)T, so the matrix has block form
[T]=(λ0​∗B​),c(X)=(X−λ)det(XI−B).
(1)
Here B represents the induced operator on V/⟨v⟩.
If the algebraic multiplicity is r, repeat this argument in that quotient: its characteristic polynomial still has λ with multiplicity r−1. Induction and lifting a quotient basis gives a basis whose first r columns have the prescribed upper-triangular form. With U the span of those r vectors, the full matrix is
(λI+N0​∗C​),Nr=0,det(C−λI)=0.
(2)
Thus U is invariant and is contained in Wλ​. Conversely, if (T−λI)sv=0, project to V/U. The induced map C−λI is invertible, so its sth power cannot kill a nonzero class. Hence v∈U. Consequently Wλ​=U, dimWλ​=r​; this proves simultaneously that it is a subspace and T-invariant. It also proves (T−λI)r=0 on this space, without using Cayley-Hamilton theorem in advance.
To prove independence of the different spaces, take a relation ∑λ​vλ​=0 with vλ​∈Wλ​. Fix λ and apply Pλ​(T)=∏μ=λ​(T−μI)rμ​. It kills all terms except vλ​. On Wλ​, each factor T−μI=(λ−μ)I+N is invertible: its inverse is a finite geometric series in the nilpotent N. Hence Pλ​(T)vλ​=0 implies vλ​=0. The sum is direct, and its dimension is ∑rλ​=dimV, so
V=λ⨁​Wλ​.​
(3)
Finally c(T)=∏λ​(T−λI)rλ​ kills each summand, hence every vector. This proves c(T)=0​ without circularly assuming the theorem during the decomposition.

The orthogonal complement is W⊥={v∈V:⟨v,w⟩=0 for every w∈W}. Put Pv=∑i​⟨v,ei​⟩ei​, so T=I−P. Orthonormality gives P2=P, and therefore T2=T. Also ⟨Tv,ej​⟩=0 for every j, so Tv∈W⊥. Every v=Pv+Tv is thus a sum from W and W⊥, and their intersection is zero, since a vector orthogonal to itself has squared norm zero. This proves V=W⊕W⊥. The decomposition is unique, so its two projection maps depend only on W, not on the chosen orthonormal basis.
Finite dimensionality of W cannot simply be dropped. Take the real Hilbert space V=ℓ2(N;R) and W=c00​, the finitely supported sequences. This is an infinite-dimensional proper subspace. A vector orthogonal to every coordinate unit vector is zero, so W⊥={0}. Yet, for example, (2−j)j≥1​ belongs to V∖W. Hence W⊕W⊥=W=V. The failure occurs even though V itself is complete; the proper dense subspace W is not closed.

Use ordinary matrix addition and multiplication on R=K[A]. Sums and products of polynomials in A remain such polynomials, and they commute because powers of the same matrix commute. The identity is A0=I. Evaluation ϕ(∑ci​Xi)=∑ci​Ai is a surjective unital algebra homomorphism over K. It is unique among maps fixing the scalar matrices and sending X to A.
The scalar condition is needed for the word “unique”: as an unrestricted ring-homomorphism assertion over an arbitrary field, prescribing only the image of X does not suffice. For example, over C with a real scalar matrix A, conjugating every coefficient gives another unital ring homomorphism with the same image of X. The intended polynomial-evaluation map is the K-algebra map just defined.
The kernel of a ring homomorphism is an ideal of the principal ideal domain K[X]. It is nonzero because finitely many powers of A must become linearly dependent in the finite-dimensional matrix space. Its unique monic generator is precisely the minimal polynomial m: division by m shows that a polynomial killing A has zero remainder, by minimality. Hence kerϕ=(m) and R≅K[X]/(m).
If gcd(f,m)=1, Bézout's identity gives uf+vm=1; evaluation gives u(A)f(A)=I. Conversely an inverse in R has form g(A), so fg−1∈(m), which forces gcd(f,m)=1. Thus invertibility in this ring is exactly coprimality with the minimal polynomial.

The displayed matrix is block diagonal with a 1×1 block 1 and a 2×2 block B having characteristic polynomial X2−4X−5=(X−5)(X+1). The latter block is not scalar, so its minimal polynomial has degree two and is exactly that polynomial. Taking the least common multiple of the block minimal polynomials gives m=(X−1)(X−5)(X+1) over R.
Ideals of K[X]/(m) correspond to ideals of K[X] containing (m), namely the principal ideals generated by the monic divisors of m. Every one of the three distinct linear factors may be included or omitted, so
#{ideals of R}=23=8.​
(6)
This includes both the zero ideal and the whole ring. Equivalently the Chinese remainder theorem identifies this ring with R3.

For t>0, integrate eitz/(1+z2) over the upper semicircle of radius R. The arc tends to zero, since ∣eitz∣≤1 there and the denominator is of order R2. The sole pole is i, of residue e−t/(2i), so the real part gives I(t)=πe−t. The integrand defining I is even in t, and I(0)=π by the arctangent integral. Hence
I(t)=πe−∣t∣(t∈R).​
(11)
For J use eitz/[z(1+z2)] with a small clockwise semicircular indentation above zero. Its residue at zero is one, so the small arc tends to −iπ. The pole i has residue −e−t/2, and the large arc again vanishes. Thus
PV∫−∞∞​x(1+x2)eitx​dx−iπ=−iπe−t.
(12)
The imaginary part is the ordinary convergent integral J(t): its apparent singularity at zero is removable, with limit t, and its tails are absolutely integrable. Therefore J(t)=π(1−e−t) for t>0. Oddness in t and the value zero at t=0 give
J(t)=⎩⎨⎧​π(1−e−t),0,−π(1−et),​t>0,t=0,t<0.​​
(13)
Differentiation is valid at every real t, including zero. Indeed the mean value theorem bounds the absolute difference quotient of sin(tx)/[x(1+x2)] with respect to t by 1/(1+x2), an integrable majorant independent of t. Dominated convergence therefore gives J′(t)=I(t) for all t∈R​. In particular both one-sided derivatives at zero equal π.

Continuity on the compact rectangle gives finite constants M=maxR​∣F∣ and L=maxR​∣Fy​∣. The real mean value theorem in the second coordinate gives ∣F(x,y)−F(x,z)∣≤L∣y−z∣ within the rectangle. Define the Picard iterates on the entire interval by
y0​(x)=b,yn+1​(x)=b+∫ax​F(t,yn​(t))dt.
(21)
If the previous iterate stays in the vertical range of R, then ∣yn+1​(x)−b∣≤M∣x−a∣≤Mh≤k. Induction therefore makes all these iterates well defined on ∣x−a∣≤h.
Repeated use of the Lipschitz bound gives the factorial estimate
∣yn+1​(x)−yn​(x)∣≤(n+1)!MLn∣x−a∣n+1​(n≥0).
(22)
The case n=0 is the direct integral bound; integrating the estimate for n−1 proves the next one, on either side of a. The series of uniform bounds converges, so the iterates converge uniformly to a continuous function y taking values within the closed vertical range. The Lipschitz estimate permits passage through the integral to give y(x)=b+∫ax​F(t,y(t))dt. The fundamental theorem of calculus now gives y′=F(x,y) and y(a)=b. Thus the solution exists on the full stated interval; no extra restriction Lh<1 was used.
For the specified equation take k=1/4, so 3/4≤y≤5/4. On ∣x∣≤h,

The condition Mh≤k holds at h3=9/64, namely h=32/3/4​. The rectangle avoids y=0, so both continuity hypotheses hold there.
Direct separation gives 4y3y′=3x2, hence (y4)′=(x3)′ and the initial value yields y4=1+x3. The branch through y(0)=1 is

For the particular equation, write Dx​=x∂x​ and Dy​=y∂y​. Its left side is (Dx2​−Dy2​)u. Choose ϕ=xy and ψ=x/y; these are smooth invertible coordinates in the positive quadrant, with inverse x=ϕψ​, y=ϕ/ψ​. In them Dx​=ϕ∂ϕ​+ψ∂ψ​ and Dy​=ϕ∂ϕ​−ψ∂ψ​. Subtraction of their squares leaves 4ϕψuϕψ​, so the canonical equation is uϕψ​=0. Integrating it gives u=F(ϕ)+G(ψ).
Parameterize the data curve by (x,y)=(s,s2), s≥1. Its coordinate values are (ϕ,ψ)=(s3,s−1), so
F(s3)+G(s−1)=s6+s−1,s2F′(s3)+s−2G′(s−1)=2s5+s−2.

(27)
Its boundary consists of xy=1 up to (1,1) and the ray y=x from that point onward. It is unbounded upward. The original data curve lies inside this region. The sketch displays both characteristic boundaries and that data arc.
To establish the exact extent of uniqueness, outside this region either ϕ<1 or ψ>1. A smooth function supported in that unprescribed coordinate range can be added to F or G without changing either datum on the arc. Hence the displayed global formula is one extension, but data alone do not uniquely determine values outside the shaded region. Boundary values are fixed by continuity.
