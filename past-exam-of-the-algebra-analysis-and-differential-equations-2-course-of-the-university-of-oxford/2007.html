A dual basis has the same number of elements as the original basis, so dimV=dimV∗=dimV∗∗. The rank-nullity theorem now makes θ an linear isomorphism.

The adjoint on an inner product space α∗:V→V is characterized by
⟨αv,w⟩=⟨v,α∗w⟩(v,w∈V).
(6)
Here is a finite-dimensional construction that supplies every required basis fact. If v1​,…,vn​ is any basis, its coordinate linear functionals form a basis of V∗: a linear functional is uniquely specified by its values on these n vectors. Hence dimV∗=n. The linear map
J:V→V∗,J(w)(v)=⟨v,w⟩
(7)
is injective, because J(w)=0 implies ⟨w,w⟩=0, and positive definiteness implies w=0. Equal dimensions make J an linear isomorphism. If α′ is the dual map, then
α∗=J−1α′J.
(8)
This establishes existence and linearity. If two possible vectors u,w represent the same linear functional through the inner product, then J(u−w)=0, so u=w. Applied to every argument, this proves uniqueness of the adjoint on an inner product space.
For linear maps S,T, two applications of the defining inner product identity give
⟨STv,w⟩=⟨Tv,S∗w⟩=⟨v,T∗S∗w⟩.
(9)
Uniqueness therefore gives (ST)∗=T∗S∗. Likewise (S+T)∗=S∗+T∗, (cT)∗=cT∗ for real c, and I∗=I. An induction gives (αn)∗=(α∗)n, and applying additivity to a polynomial with real coefficients gives
f(α)∗=f(α∗).​
(10)
Symmetry of the real inner product also gives (α∗)∗=α. If m is the minimal polynomial of α, the displayed identity gives m(α∗)=0, so the minimal polynomial of α∗ divides m. Applying the same argument to α∗ gives reverse divisibility. Both polynomials are monic, hence their minimal polynomials are equal.
The distinct eigenvalues are exactly the roots of the minimal polynomial in the chosen field. An eigenvector v with eigenvalue λ satisfies m(α)v=m(λ)v, so m(λ)=0. Conversely, if m(X)=(X−λ)q(X) and α−λI were a linear isomorphism, then m(α)=0 would imply q(α)=0, contradicting minimal degree. Thus α−λI is not injective, giving an eigenvector. It follows that α and α∗ have the same distinct eigenvalues. This includes the complex eigenvalues after complexification of a real vector space; over the real field it states equality of their real eigenvalues.

A maximal ideal is a proper ideal I of R such that an ideal J with I⊆J⊆R is either I or R. Suppose first that I is a maximal ideal. If a∈/I, then I+(a)=R, so 1=i+ra for some i∈I and r∈R. In the quotient ring, (r+I)(a+I)=1+I. Every nonzero element of R/I therefore has a multiplicative inverse, so R/I is a field. Conversely, if R/I is a field and J properly contains I, choose a∈J∖I. The element a+I has a multiplicative inverse r+I, whence 1−ra∈I⊂J, giving 1∈J and J=R. Thus
I is maximal⟺R/I is a field.​
(11)
The squares in F7​ are 0,1,2,4, which do not include −1=6. Hence X2+1 has no root in F7​. A polynomial of degree two or three over a field is an irreducible polynomial exactly when it has no root: any proper polynomial factorization of such a degree has a factor of degree one. The polynomial ring over a field is a Euclidean domain, so the ideal generated by an irreducible polynomial is a maximal ideal. It follows that
F=F7​[X]/(X2+1)
(12)
is a field. Every coset has a unique representative a+bX, by polynomial division, so ∣F∣=72=49.
Let j be the coset of X in this field. Since j2=−1, the map

is a ring homomorphism. It is surjective by the representative description. Since 1,j are linearly independent over F7​, its kernel of a ring homomorphism is
kerϕ=7Z[i]=(7).​
(14)
There is also a field with 343 elements. The cubes in F7​ are 0,1,−1, so X3−2 has no root and is an irreducible polynomial. Therefore
F7​[X]/(X3−2)​
(15)
is a field whose unique representatives have degree below three. There are 73=343 such representatives.

Use the following boundary version of Cauchy's integral theorem: if a function is holomorphic on a neighborhood of a compact region bounded by finitely many piecewise smooth simple closed curves, its integral around the positively oriented boundary is zero. On an annulus this means that its integrals around the two circles, both taken counterclockwise, are equal.
For 0<ρ<r, the function f(z)/(z−w) is holomorphic on a neighborhood of ρ≤∣z−w∣≤r. The theorem therefore gives
2πi1​∫∣z−w∣=r​z−wf(z)​dz=2π1​∫02π​f(w+ρeit)dt.
(16)
As ρ↓0, continuity of f makes the integrand converge uniformly to f(w). The right side tends to f(w), proving Cauchy integral formula without assuming that formula in an intermediate step.
Let I denote the requested boundary integral. Parametrize the unit circle by z=eit. Since dz=ieitdt,
I=2π1​∫02π​1−ae−itf(eit)​​dt.
(17)
Taking complex conjugates and using eit=e−it gives
I=2π1​∫02π​1−aeitf(eit)​dt=2πi1​∫∣z∣=1​z(1−az)f(z)​dz.
(18)
This is the conjugated Cauchy integral on a circle identity. The function h(z)=f(z)/(1−az) is holomorphic on a neighborhood of the closed unit disc, because ∣a∣<1. Applying Cauchy integral formula at zero gives I=h(0)=f(0). Thus
I=f(0)​.​
(19)
The complex conjugates in these formulas are essential; the boundary integrand contains f(z)​.

First consider the usual local reduction with sufficiently smooth coefficients. The hyperbolic partial differential equation has two distinct characteristic directions because S2−RT>0. Choose independent local characteristic coordinates ξ,η whose gradients satisfy
Rξx2​+2Sξx​ξy​+Tξy2​=0,Rηx2​+2Sηx​ηy​+Tηy2​=0.

(40)

2Buξη​+Aξ​uξ​+Aη​uη​,Aξ​=Rξxx​+2Sξxy​+Tξyy​,
(41)
with the analogous expression for Aη​. The original first derivatives are zx​=uξ​ξx​+uη​ηx​ and zy​=uξ​ξy​+uη​ηy​. Substitution and division by 2B yield the requested mixed-derivative canonical form. The construction is local, in a neighborhood where the two characteristic foliations give a diffeomorphism.
There is a regularity qualification in the printed hypotheses: merely continuous coefficients need not give twice continuously differentiable characteristic coordinates. Such coordinates are needed for the second-derivative chain rule above. For example, take R=0, S=1/2 and T=∣x∣ near the origin. The characteristic equation factors as ξy​(ξx​+∣x∣ξy​)=0. One family is x=constant and the other has first integrals η=h(y−x∣x∣/2). For an independent coordinate h′=0, but ηxx​ has opposite one-sided limits at x=0. Hence no twice continuously differentiable characteristic coordinate system exists there. This illustrates that continuous hyperbolic coefficients need not admit smooth characteristic coordinates. The formal general argument requires additional smoothness, for example twice continuously differentiable coefficients. The concrete coefficients below are smooth, so this issue does not affect their solution.

A sufficient condition for existence of the Laplace transform is that y be piecewise continuous on each bounded interval and have exponential order: ∣y(t)∣≤Ceat for sufficiently large t. The defining integral then converges absolutely for real p>a, and more generally defines a holomorphic function for complex p with Rep>a. The boundary p=a may or may not be included, depending on y.
Write Y(p)=L[y](p). To solve the differential equation by Laplace transform, initially seek a sufficiently regular solution with y(0)=1 and finite y′(0)=b. The Laplace transform of a derivative gives
L[y′]=pY−1,L[y′′]=p2Y−p−b.
(46)
Using L[ty]=−Y′ from part (i), the transformed differential equation becomes
−(2pY+p2Y′−1)−Y−pY′−(pY−1)−Y=0,
(47)
or
p(p+1)Y′+(3p+2)Y=2.
(48)
After division by p(p+1), an integrating factor is p2(p+1), since (3p+2)/(p(p+1))=2/p+1/(p+1). Consequently
dpd​[p2(p+1)Y]=2p,Y=p+11​+p2(p+1)C​.
(49)
For a bounded y, ∣Y(p)∣≤∥y∥∞​/p for p>0. Letting p↓0 forces C=0, an instance of how boundedness selects a Laplace transform at a singular endpoint. By the exponential formula in part (iii), this yields
y(t)=e−t.​
(50)
Direct substitution checks the differential equation, boundedness and the required limit.
To complete the uniqueness argument, no unproved endpoint regularity is needed. The two functions e−t and t−1 solve the homogeneous linear differential equation on (0,∞), and their Wronskian is te−t=0. They form a fundamental system of solutions on that interval, so every classical solution is Ae−t+B(t−1). Boundedness forces B=0 and the limit at zero forces A=1. Thus the bounded solution with the specified limit is unique. The limit alone would not suffice: e−t+C(t−1+e−t) has the same limit for every C, but is unbounded when C=0.
