Separating pupils by attainment is not, by itself, a reliable solution to individual differences. Benefits depend on whether the arrangement changes the instruction pupils receive, while costs can arise from restricted opportunities, inaccurate placement and persistent labels. Evidence distinguishes whole-curriculum separation from subject-specific and flexible grouping; it does not justify treating all forms as one intervention. Current achievement should also not be mistaken for a fixed measure of learning potential.
Streaming by attainment assigns a pupil to a group across much of the curriculum. Subject setting regroups pupils for a particular subject, allowing different placements in, for example, reading and mathematics. Within-class grouping creates smaller instructional groups inside a shared classroom and can be revised by task. These arrangements differ in exposure to peers, teachers, curriculum and opportunities to move. A finding about flexible mathematics groups therefore cannot automatically defend an enduring all-subject stream.
The favorable hypothesis is instructional fit. A narrower range of prior knowledge may let a teacher select examples, pace and support more accurately, while advanced pupils avoid unnecessary repetition. But the group label must actually change the lesson in a useful way. Separating pupils and then delivering the same unsuitable instruction supplies little reason to expect benefit. Nor does an appropriate lesson logically require fixed separation: formative assessment and flexible task allocation can address differences within a mixed classroom.
Robert E. Slavin's best-evidence synthesis distinguished assignment to self-contained ability classes, which lacked a general achievement advantage, from some subject regrouping and within-class mathematics arrangements with more favorable results. Its conditions emphasized reassessment and adapting the level and pace of teaching. The historical importance is the distinction between organization and instruction, not a timeless verdict that any arrangement called “grouping” works. A synthesis also inherits variation in the quality of its constituent comparisons.[ref]
Saiying Steenbergen-Hu and colleagues' later second-order meta-analysis likewise separated forms of grouping. Across thirteen meta-analyses, between-class grouping showed little overall achievement benefit, while within-class grouping, cross-grade subject grouping and specialized arrangements had more positive average results. Acceleration was analyzed separately and should not be collapsed into ordinary attainment sorting. The evidence spans school ages and implementations, so its averages are not estimates of one particular primary-school policy. Reviews that share primary studies are also not independent replications to be counted as separate votes.[ref]
These conclusions do not establish that tracking must harm low-attaining pupils in every setting. Esther Duflo and colleagues conducted a randomized tracking experiment in 121 Kenyan primary schools. In that context, tracking benefited lower-achieving pupils through better instructional targeting, despite the potential advantages of exposure to higher-achieving peers. The experiment provides a causal counterexample to a universal claim that mixed-attainment classes are always best. Its teacher incentives, curriculum and wider school conditions also limit transfer to a different education system.[ref]
The Kenyan result helps distinguish two effects often conflated in discussion. Peer effects concern how classmates' skills or behavior influence learning; instructional targeting concerns what a teacher teaches to whom. They can act in opposite directions. A lower-attaining group might lose some peer opportunities yet gain a lesson that it can use. A different system could concentrate less experienced teachers or narrow tasks in the same group, producing a different outcome. The causal question is therefore the effect of the whole implemented arrangement, not a social label in isolation.
Placement creates further challenges. An assessment contains measurement error and reflects prior opportunity, language and familiarity with the task. Selecting pupils at one time point can therefore turn temporary performance differences into apparently stable categories. Even frequent movement on paper is insufficient if movement rarely occurs in practice. Researchers need placement histories and the material actually taught, not only the school's description of its policy. Comparing final scores of high and low groups is especially uninformative because initial attainment helped define those groups.
Teacher expectations and children's interpretations of their placement are plausible additional pathways. A label can affect the challenge offered or the opportunities to demonstrate improvement; a child may interpret it as a statement of fixed worth. These are hypotheses requiring direct measurement rather than automatic explanations for every group difference. Self-concept, participation and belonging matter even when an average test score is unchanged. Conversely, a pupil may value appropriately demanding peers and tasks, so increased challenge should not be equated with social harm merely because it involves grouping.
A useful evaluation would randomize schools or classes to well-specified arrangements, measure baseline attainment before allocation and monitor curriculum, staffing and movement. It should retain an intention-to-treat comparison rather than excluding pupils whose placements changed. Multilevel analysis or appropriate uncertainty estimates are required when pupils share teachers or classrooms. Prespecified subgroup analyses can ask whether effects differ by starting attainment, while distributional outcomes show whether an average gain masks widening gaps. Longer follow-up is important because early curricular restriction may have consequences beyond an immediate assessment.
The relevant comparison should be equally credible. A well-resourced setting program compared with an under-supported mixed class does not identify separation alone as the active ingredient. Equally, a mixed class receiving no help to differentiate instruction is not a decisive test of responsive mixed teaching. Comparing arrangements under explicit resource and implementation conditions makes the evidence more useful for practice. It also permits testing whether flexibility and good assessment capture the benefit without committing pupils to permanent categories.
For primary education, where performance profiles and opportunities can change quickly, the most defensible principle is responsiveness. Grouping may serve a specific instructional purpose when it is revisable and tied to what the pupil needs to learn next. Its effects should be monitored rather than justified by a presumed innate hierarchy. Maintaining ambitious curriculum access and checking actual movement are safeguards whose value should be assessed alongside attainment, not assumed to follow from a policy name.
The research supports evaluating instructional fit, opportunity and flexibility rather than endorsing streaming or setting as default answers to individual differences. Broad between-class sorting has weak average support, while particular subject and within-class arrangements can help under specified conditions. A successful contextual experiment does not universalize the policy, and an overall null average does not make the content and quality of teaching irrelevant.
