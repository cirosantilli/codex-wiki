Assumptions of the bounded monotone sequence theorem

Dropping either condition destroys the implication. The increasing sequence an​=n has a lower bound one but no upper bound, and no finite limit of a sequence. For any real threshold T, the Archimedean property gives an index N>T, and monotonicity gives an​>T for all n≥N. Its divergence to +∞ in the extended real number line is different from convergence to a real number.
Conversely, bn​=(−1)n is a bounded sequence, with −1≤bn​≤1, but is not a monotone sequence and has no limit of a sequence. Its subsequence at even integers is constantly one, and its subsequence at odd integers is constantly minus one. These two subsequential limits cannot both equal a single limit of a sequence. [ref]
The ambient real numbers also matter. An original example using the floor function is
qn​=2n⌊2n2​⌋​,n≥1.
(63)
Every qn​ is a rational number, qn​≤qn+1​ because ⌊2x⌋≥2⌊x⌋, and
0<2​−qn​<2−n.
(64)
The strict lower inequality uses the irrationality of the square root of two. Hence this bounded sequence converges to 2​ in the real numbers, but has no limit of a sequence in the rational numbers. Convergence to a rational candidate would also be convergence in the real numbers, contradicting uniqueness of the limit of a sequence. The formulas give q1​=1, q2​=5/4, q3​=11/8, q4​=11/8: this counterexample even has a plateau. This illustrates why completeness of the real numbers matters: the rational numbers lack the least-upper-bound property needed to contain the required supremum. [ref]
