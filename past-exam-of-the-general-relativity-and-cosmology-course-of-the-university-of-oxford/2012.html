dτd​(2Br˙)+c2t˙2∇A−r˙,2∇B=0.​

For the connection coefficients, take x0=ct, so g00​=−A and gij​=Bδij​. Substituting the diagonal inverse metric into the Christoffel symbol formula gives all nonzero component types, including lower-index symmetry:
Γ00i​=Γ0i0​Γi00​Γijk​​=2AAi​​=Ar3μxi​​,=2BAi​​=Br3μxi​,=2Bδki​Bj​+δji​Bk​−δjk​Bi​​=−Br3μ​(δki​xj​+δji​xk​−δjk​xi).​​

All other component types vanish. If the time coordinate is t rather than ct, replace Γi00​ by Γitt​=c2Γi00​; the displayed time–space mixed coefficients are unchanged. Spatial indices in these formulas are raised with the background Euclidean metric.
For the circular orbit let u=μ/R and Ω=dϕ/dt=ω/t˙. Both A and B are constant along it. The radial spatial equation gives (1+u)ω2=(GM/R3)t˙2, so, treating the displayed metric coefficients algebraically, Ω2=GM/[R3(1+u)]. The weak-field approximation securely determines the leading Keplerian orbit result

One must distinguish the proper-time angular frequency in the supplied path from the coordinate-time angular frequency. The formal factor 1+u in the frequency equation comes from treating the truncated metric as exact; a complete relative post-Newtonian correction to the orbital period would also require the omitted higher-order temporal metric terms.

Use the curvature sign convention
Rρσμν​=∂μ​Γρνσ​−∂ν​Γρμσ​+Γρμλ​Γλνσ​−Γρνλ​Γλμσ​,

Their ratio is μ/r≪1. The metric is stationary, hence the temporal derivative term vanishes. Consequently
Ri0j0​≃∂j​Γi00​=−21​∂i​∂j​g00​.
(6)

Ri0j0​=c2r3GM​(δij​−3r2xixj​)+O(μ2/r4).​
(7)
Printed sign inconsistency. The source's positive half-Hessian and its final negative-prefactor expression are the opposites of this result. The convention used here and in the next part gives the physical radial stretching and transverse compression.

Geodesic deviation compares neighboring members of a family of freely falling trajectories. Subtracting their geodesic equations to first order in the connecting vector gives a covariant relative-acceleration equation governed by the Riemann curvature tensor. For a slowly moving satellite, u0=ct˙≃c dominates the spatial components. Choose simultaneous spatial separation in a locally transported frame, take the weak-field approximation, and neglect terms quadratic in the small separation and higher velocity corrections. The leading spatial equation becomes
dτ2d2Δi​+c2Ri0j0​Δj≃0.​
(8)
Replacing the transported covariant derivatives by ordinary derivatives is a leading local or Newtonian approximation; a rotating orbital frame would introduce additional inertial terms. In the convention established above, the instantaneous tidal acceleration stretches radial separations and compresses transverse ones.
The growth expected for nearby different-radius circular orbits is especially transparent from Kepler's third law. If the radii are R and R+δR, their coordinate angular frequencies differ by δΩ≃−(3/2)ΩδR/R. Initially aligned orbits therefore acquire an along-track separation RδΩt≃−(3/2)ΩδRt. Over the interval in which the nearby-orbit approximation remains valid, this produces a growing separation without needing to solve the deviation equation.
This growth is not universal or indefinitely unbounded. Equal-radius co-orbiting geodesics with a fixed phase offset have constant separation, and slightly inclined equal-radius circular orbits have bounded, periodically varying separation. Even different-radius circular orbits have bounded exact positions; their relative angle eventually wraps around. The secular drift statement assumes distinct radii and describes the local differential-orbit regime.

For radial null geodesics in a de Sitter static patch, the conserved time quantity and the stated normalization at the origin give αt˙=1. The null condition then gives
0=−c2αt˙2+α−1r˙2,r˙2=c2α2t˙2=c2.

For the static-emitter redshift in a de Sitter patch, a stationary clock at radius rE​ satisfies dτE​=αE​​dtE​. The geometry is stationary, so successive inward pulses emitted at the same radius have the same coordinate travel delay. Their coordinate arrival interval is therefore ΔtO​=ΔtE​. At the central receiver αO​=1, giving
ΔτO​=1−rE2​/rS2​​ΔτE​​.​
(16)
For rE​=rS​/2, this becomes
ΔτO​=3​2​ΔτE​,1+z=νO​νE​​=3​2​,z=3​2​−1.​
(17)
The pulses are redshifted at the centre. At fixed proper emission period, taking a stationary emitter toward the horizon gives ΔτO​→∞, νO​→0 and z→∞. The inward coordinate travel delay also diverges. A stationary worldline at the horizon itself is not timelike, and the proper acceleration required to hold an emitter static diverges in this limit; the result concerns a sequence of idealized emitters with rE​<rS​, not a stationary physical lighthouse exactly on the horizon.

Normalize a(t0​)=1, taking a dimensionless and r as a length, so the source's curvature constant k has units of inverse length squared. With densities in the mass-equivalent convention, the first Friedmann equation is
H2=38πG​i∑​ρi​−a2kc2​+3Λc2​.​
(18)
The sum can include dust, radiation and other material components; vacuum energy must be represented either in that sum or by Λ, without double counting. For physical energy densities ui​, replace ρi​ by ui​/c2. The companion acceleration equation is a¨/a=−(4πG/3)∑i​(ρi​+3pi​/c2)+Λc2/3, with energy conservation ρ˙​i​+3H(ρi​+pi​/c2)=0 for separately conserved components.
For an empty universe with Λ=0, H2=−kc2/a2. On the expanding branch with H0​>0,
k=−c2H02​​<0.​
(19)
Equivalently, in the convention where the normalized curvature label is k=−1 and the scale factor has length units, a0​=c/H0​. A rescaling of comoving coordinates changes the numerical magnitude of k but not its negative sign. The degenerate empty case H0​=0 has k=0 and a constant scale factor; it does not have a finite age 1/H0​.

For the expanding empty model, a˙=H0​ in the preceding normalization. If tB​ denotes the origin of the expanding slicing,
a(t)=H0​(t−tB​),q0​=−a˙2aa¨​=0,t0​−tB​=H0−1​.​
(20)
The constant-time spatial slices are negatively curved hyperbolic spaces, with curvature radius Rcurv​=a/−k​=c(t−tB​) and three-dimensional scalar curvature 6k/a2. In the usual simply connected description they are copies of hyperbolic three-space. This is the Milne model: its four-dimensional spacetime is actually flat. Setting T=(t−tB​)coshχ and R=c(t−tB​)sinhχ converts the metric to the interior of a future light cone in Minkowski spacetime. Its zero-scale-factor boundary is a slicing boundary, not a curvature singularity.
Add separately conserved pressureless matter, with present mass-equivalent density ρM0​. Define the cosmological density parameters
ΩM​=3H02​8πGρM0​​,ΩK​=−H02​kc2​,ΩM​+ΩK​=1.
(21)
Then
H2=H02​(ΩM​a−3+ΩK​a−2),q(a)=2(ΩM​+ΩK​a)ΩM​​,q0​=2ΩM​​.​
(22)
Curvature and dust have equal magnitudes at aK​=ΩM​/ΩK​, giving
1+zK​=ΩM​ΩK​​,zK​=ΩM​ΩK​​−1.​
(23)
For the almost-empty open case 0<ΩM​<1/2, curvature domination begins at positive redshift. Equality is today for ΩM​=1/2 and in the future for 1/2<ΩM​<1. With ΩM​=0, curvature dominates at all times and there is no finite matter–curvature equality; with ΩK​=0, curvature never dominates.

At fixed radial comoving distance and fixed emission scale factor, open spatial geometry has transverse radius sinh(κDC​)/κ>DC​. Hence its angular diameter distance exceeds the flat value, and a standard object of fixed proper size has
δθ=ℓ/DA​smaller in the open geometry.​
(28)
The same comparison can be expressed at fixed proper radial distance on the emission slice. A comparison at fixed redshift must additionally account for the different expansion histories in DC​(z); curvature and expansion effects must not be silently conflated.
Known-size cosmological standard rulers with measured redshifts provide DA​(z) through their angular sizes. The function depends on the spatial-curvature parameter directly and on matter, radiation or dark energy through the expansion history. For a comoving ruler such as a calibrated baryon acoustic oscillation scale, the transverse angular measurement determines (1+z)DA​. Angular acoustic scales in the cosmic microwave background similarly probe the ratio of a calibrated early-time length to a cosmological distance. A range of redshifts constrains parameter-dependent shapes and the overall distance scale, but one angular measurement cannot uniquely separate all density parameters. Ruler calibration, source evolution and lensing must be modeled, and independent expansion or distance information helps break the degeneracies.

For thermal photons, the available thermal length is ℓT​=ℏc/(kB​T). The number of occupied modes per volume scales as ℓT−3​, while their characteristic energy is of order kB​T=ℏc/ℓT​. Thus nγ​∝ℓT−3​ and uγ​=ργ​c2∝ℏcℓT−4​. More explicitly, the two photon polarizations and the Bose-Einstein distribution give

Assume an ideal nondegenerate hydrogen gas in thermal equilibrium and chemical equilibrium, with ground-state atoms and charge neutrality. Let np​=ne​=XnB​ and nH​=(1−X)nB​. The ground-state Saha equation, with the usual electron/proton spin factors cancelling the atomic ground-state degeneracy, is
nH​ne​np​​=(2πℏ2me​kB​T​)3/2e−B/(kB​T).
(33)
Invert it and use nB​=nγ​/s=(0.24/s)(kB​T/(ℏc))3. This gives
X21−X​=s0.24(2π)3/2​(me​c2kB​T​)3/2eB/(kB​T),
(34)
so
X21−X​≃s3.8​(me​c2kB​T​)3/2expkB​TB​.​
(35)
Here me​ is the electron mass denoted Me​ in the source, and 0.24(2π)3/2=3.78. At X=1/2, the left side is two. At kB​T of order B, the right side is extremely small because 1/s∼10−9 and B/(me​c2)≪1. A large exponential is needed to compensate, requiring B/(kB​T)≫1 rather than a temperature of order the binding energy.
Physically, there are enormously many photons per baryon, so even a small high-energy tail of the thermal photon distribution can maintain ionization. Recombination is delayed until that tail is sufficiently depleted. This explains the low recombination temperature without solving the equilibrium equation; actual cosmological recombination also eventually requires rate equations once equilibrium fails.

Big Bang nucleosynthesis is a kinetic process in an expanding universe. A common particle temperature does not imply chemical equilibrium for every nuclear reaction: reaction rates must also exceed the expansion rate and connect the required species efficiently. Around 109K, nuclear abundances retain the history of earlier freeze-out and cannot be inferred from equilibrium Boltzmann factors alone.
In particular, weak reactions cease to maintain neutron-proton chemical equilibrium. Subsequent free-neutron decay after freeze-out changes the surviving neutron inventory. The large photon-to-baryon ratio delays deuterium survival through photodissociation; once the deuterium bottleneck opens, reactions rapidly bind many surviving neutrons into helium-4. Coulomb barriers, decreasing density and further freeze-out prevent unrestricted equilibration of all nuclei. The final helium abundance therefore requires a reaction network and expansion history, not just the temperature and nuclear binding energies.
For illustration, if essentially all available neutrons end in helium-4 and the neutron-to-proton ratio at that stage is r, simple particle counting gives YHe​≃2r/(1+r). The crucial input r is itself set by out-of-equilibrium history. Kinetic thermalization of the plasma can remain effective while these composition-changing processes are out of equilibrium.
