Use metric signature (−,+,+,+), x0=ct, and retain terms linear in Φ/c2 or Ψ/c2. The inverse metric is g00=−(1−2Φ/c2) and gij=(1+2Ψ/c2)δij. In the Christoffel symbol formula
Γαβγ​=21​gαδ(∂β​gδγ​+∂γ​gδβ​−∂δ​gβγ​),
(1)
the differentiated metric perturbation is already first order, so the prefactor can be replaced by the flat inverse metric. For example Γ0ij​=21​∂0​gij​=−δij​∂0​Ψ/c2 and Γk00​=−21​∂k​g00​=∂k​Φ/c2. Evaluating the remaining components gives

(2)
The lower-index symmetry follows from the torsion-free connection. Products of these first-order Christoffel symbols are beyond the retained weak-field approximation.

A pressureless relativistic fluid has stress-energy tensor Tμν=ρuμuν, with four-velocity normalized by uμuμ​=−c2. In its rest frame, ui=0, so T00​=−ρc2, Tij​=0​. Here ρ is rest-frame mass density, or energy density divided by c2.
For the linearized Ricci tensor and scalar, discard the quadratic connection terms. The contracted connection is Γμμν​=∂ν​(Φ−3Ψ)/c2. Differentiation then gives
R00​R0i​Rij​R​=c2∇2Φ+3∂02​Ψ​,=c22∂0​∂i​Ψ​,=c2∂i​∂j​(Ψ−Φ)+δij​(∇2Ψ−∂02​Ψ)​,=c24∇2Ψ−2∇2Φ−6∂02​Ψ​.​​
(3)
The Ricci scalar uses the zeroth-order inverse metric, R=−R00​+δijRij​, because multiplying first-order curvature by another metric perturbation would again be second order. These are the linearized curvature of a two-potential weak-field metric in the paper's Riemann curvature tensor sign convention.

The Einstein tensor is Gμν​=Rμν​−21​δνμ​R. Thus G00​=−R00​−R/2, where the time raising introduces the minus sign. Substitution cancels the Φ and time-derivative terms in that component and gives
G00​Gij​​=−c22∇2Ψ​=−c28πGρ​,=c22∂02​Ψδji​+∇2(Φ−Ψ)δji​+∂i∂j​(Ψ−Φ)​=0.​​

The right sides follow from the Einstein field equations Gμν​=(8πG/c4)Tμν​ with zero cosmological constant. The full equations also contain the momentum constraint G0i​=−2∂0​∂i​Ψ/c2=0 for dust at rest to this order. Specifying arbitrary time-dependent potentials without that constraint would not give a solution of all the Einstein field equations.

For a massive particle parametrized by proper time, the geodesic equation is
dτ2d2xμ​+Γμαβ​dτdxα​dτdxβ​=0,gαβ​dτdxα​dτdxβ​=−c2.
(5)
The weak-field approximation alone does not imply Newtonian motion. Also take a nonrelativistic limit, ∣v∣/c≪1, in the usual quasistatic regime so the potentials' time variation does not compensate for the small velocity factors. Reparametrizing by coordinate time and retaining the leading velocity-independent spatial term gives
dt2d2xi​=−c2Γi00​=−∂i​Φ.​
(6)
In empty space the first field equation gives ∇2Ψ=0. For static potentials, the spatial equations imply ∇2(Φ−Ψ)=0 and then ∂i​∂j​(Φ−Ψ)=0. The difference is at most affine; isolated-system boundary conditions fixing both potentials to zero at infinity remove it. Thus Φ=Ψ and
∇2Φ=0in vacuum,​
(7)
the empty-space Newton-Poisson equation. With the same quasistatic assumptions and dust present it becomes ∇2Φ=4πGρ. Keeping rapid time dependence instead leaves ∇2Φ=−3∂02​Ψ in vacuum, so the static qualification matters.

Although ∇β​f=∂β​f for a scalar field, its first derivative is a covector field. Therefore
∇α​∇β​f=∂α​∂β​f−Γγαβ​∂γ​f.
(8)
Commuting partial derivatives and using the torsion-free connection gives [∇α​,∇β​]f=0​. For a vector field, differentiate the (1,1) tensor ∇β​Vμ with both its indices included:
∇α​∇β​Vμ=∂α​(∇β​Vμ)+Γμαϵ​∇β​Vϵ−Γϵαβ​∇ϵ​Vμ.
(9)
Subtract the expression with α,β exchanged. Second derivatives of V cancel; the terms multiplying first derivatives cancel pairwise, and the final lower-index connection terms cancel by symmetry. The remaining coefficient of Vν is
[∇α​,∇β​]Vμ=(∂α​Γμβν​−∂β​Γμαν​+Γμαϵ​Γϵβν​−Γμβϵ​Γϵαν​)Vν=Rμναβ​Vν.​
(10)
This is the curvature commutator. On a covariant index the sign reverses, giving the curvature commutator on a covariant tensor used in the next part.

For a torsion-free connection, the connection terms in the antisymmetrized covariant derivative cancel: Fμν​=∂μ​Aν​−∂ν​Aμ​. Thus the electromagnetic field tensor is the exterior derivative of the four-potential. It is a tensor, is invariant under Aμ​↦Aμ​+∂μ​χ, and obeys the homogeneous Maxwell equations ∇[α​Fβγ]​=0. The covariant divergence equation is the generally covariant form of the local source equation; use its minus sign as specified in the paper.
Expand that equation before imposing the Lorenz gauge condition:
∇μFμν​=∇μ∇μ​Aν​−∇μ∇ν​Aμ​.
(11)
The curvature commutator on a covariant tensor gives
[∇ρ​,∇ν​]Aμ​=−Rαμρν​Aα​,∇μ∇ν​Aμ​=∇ν​(∇μAμ​)+Rαν​Aα​.
(12)
Here the contraction gμρRαμρν​=−Rαν​ accounts for the second sign reversal. Hence in Lorenz gauge
∇μ∇μ​Aν​−Rαν​Aα​=−μ0​Jν​.​
(13)
This curved-spacetime electromagnetic potential wave equation contains a Ricci tensor term even in a locally inertial coordinate system: a connection can vanish at one point while its derivatives and curvature remain nonzero.

For a minimally coupled massless scalar field, the covariant wave operator is
□ϕ=−g​1​∂μ​(−g​gμν∂ν​ϕ)=a21​[−∂02​ϕ−2H∂0​ϕ+∇2ϕ].
(16)
The Fourier mode ϕ=u(η)eik⋅r therefore satisfies
uηη​−η2​uη​+ω2u=0,ω=c∣k∣.
(17)
Substituting u=(A+Bη)e−iωη leaves 2(iωA−B)e−iωη/η. Thus
B=iωA=ic∣k∣A,ϕ=A(1+ic∣k∣η)e−ic∣k∣η+ik⋅r.​

Use mass density units for the ρ appearing in the Friedmann equation; an actual energy density is ρc2. Conservation, ρ˙​+3H(ρ+P/c2)=0, gives ρM​=ρM,0​a−3,ρR​=ρR,0​a−4​ for pressureless matter and radiation in cosmology, respectively, with a0​=1. The first scaling counts dilution of particle number, while the second includes the cosmological redshift of each photon's energy.
Define the present cosmological density parameters by ΩX,0​=8πGρX,0​/(3H02​). Then
H02​H2​=ΩM,0​a−3+ΩR,0​a−4+Ωk,0​a−2,Ωk,0​=−H02​kc2​=1−ΩM,0​−ΩR,0​.
(19)
For k=0, conformal time gives da/dη=a2H, so the expanding branch obeys (da/dη)2=H02​(ΩM,0​a+ΩR,0​). Set η=0 at the Big Bang. Integrating gives
ΩM,0​a+ΩR,0​​=ΩR,0​​+21​ΩM,0​H0​η,
(20)
and hence the flat matter-radiation conformal solution
a(η)=ΩR,0​​H0​η+41​ΩM,0​(H0​η)2.​
(21)
The limiting pure-radiation and pure-matter cases follow continuously.

For dust alone, write a˙2=c2(A/a−k), where
A=3c28πGρM,0​​=c2ΩM,0​H02​​.​

Set θ=k​cη and a=(A/k)sin2(θ/2). Differentiation gives a˙=ck​cot(θ/2) once dt=adη is used, verifying the Friedmann equation. Integrating the time relation from the Big Bang yields the closed matter-dominated Friedmann solution

At maximum expansion, amax​=A/k. With a0​=1, the present Friedmann equation gives kc2=H02​(ΩM,0​−1), so positive curvature requires ΩM,0​>1. Taking H0​>0 for the presently expanding branch gives
amax​=ΩM,0​−1ΩM,0​​,tlife​=H0​(ΩM,0​−1)3/2πΩM,0​​,q0​=2ΩM,0​​.​

The lifetime of a closed dust universe follows by setting θ=2π. The deceleration parameter follows from the Friedmann acceleration equation, a¨/a=−4πGρM​/3. Thus this model always decelerates while expanding. Its total lifetime includes the elapsed age; it is not the remaining lifetime.
The supernova discovery of accelerating expansion does not support this dust-only recollapse model as a description of the observed expansion history. Already the 1998 and 1999 Type Ia supernova analyses favoured accelerating expansion, requiring ingredients beyond this zero-cosmological constant, positive-curvature dust model within the usual FLRW metric interpretation.[ref][ref]

Throughout this question use natural units with c=1. For zero potential and nonzero kinetic energy, the homogeneous canonical scalar field has P=ρ=φ˙​2/2, so w=1​. Its equation of motion becomes d(a3φ˙​)/dt=0, giving φ˙​∝a−3 and therefore
ρ∝a−6.​

Write M=MPl​ for the reduced Planck mass. For a nontrivial power law with λ=0, H=p/t, φ˙​=BM/t, and V=V0​(t/t0​)−λB. Matching its time dependence to the kinetic and Friedmann equation terms requires λB=2. The scalar field equation of motion and the Friedmann equation then reduce to
B(3p−1)M2=λV0​t02​,3M2p2=2B2M2​+V0​t02​.
(28)
Solving gives the exponential-potential scalar-dominated power-law solution
p=λ22​,B=λ2​,t02​=λ4V0​2M2(6−λ2)​.​
(29)
Choose t>0 and the positive square root for t0​. The existence conditions for exponential-potential scaling matter: if V0​>0, a finite real t0​ requires 0<λ2<6, rather than an inclusive bound on signed λ. At λ2=6, nonzero V0​ is incompatible with this logarithmic ansatz. If V0​<0, the corresponding scaling solution instead requires λ2>6. The sign of λ changes the field's rolling direction, not p. For λ=0 the potential is constant and this finite-p ansatz does not apply; with positive V0​, a constant field gives de Sitter spacetime expansion.

The scaling solution has ρ=3M2p2/t2 and ρ+P=φ˙​2=4M2/(λ2t2). Therefore
w=−1+3λ2​,q0​=p1​−1=2λ2​−1.​
(30)
More generally any real canonical scalar field with positive total energy density obeys ρ+P=φ˙​2≥0, so it cannot have w<−1. A negative potential does not reverse this conclusion.
The proper particle horizon is a(t)∫0t​dt′/a(t′). For a∝tp the lower-limit integral diverges exactly when p≥1. Thus, assuming the same power law extends to the initial boundary, there is no finite particle horizon for 0<λ2≤2​. For the strict inequality, p>1, the proper cosmological event horizon and present age are
REH,0​=p−1tnow​​=(2−λ2)H0​2​,tnow​=H0​p​=λ2H0​2​.​
(31)
At λ2=2, a∝t has an infinite particle-horizon integral but also an infinite future event-horizon integral, so no finite event horizon exists. The age remains H0−1​. The λ=0 constant-potential case is separate: its flat de Sitter patch has event-horizon radius H0−1​ and no finite-age power-law Big Bang.
