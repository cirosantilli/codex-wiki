Spatial geometry. An embedding of the three-sphere is
w=Acosχ,z=Asinχcosθ,x=Asinχsinθcosϕ,y=Asinχsinθsinϕ.
(24)
Taking differentials and collecting terms gives the induced metric tensor
dℓ2=A2dχ2+A2sin2χ(dθ2+sin2θdϕ2).
(25)
Put rˉ=Asinχ. Then drˉ=Acosχdχ, so
dℓ2=1−rˉ2/A2drˉ2​+rˉ2dΩ2​,dΩ2=dθ2+sin2θdϕ2.

The areal radius rˉ is not a global radial coordinate on the entire three-sphere: its maximum occurs at the equator and two hemispheres share each smaller value. Use separate charts there; the coordinate singularity at rˉ=A is not a physical singularity.

Homogeneity, isotropy and redshift. The FRW metric has constant-time spatial slices of constant curvature k/a2(t), with k=+1,0,−1 selecting the three geometries above. Their isometries act transitively on points and isotropically about every point, implementing the cosmological principle. Multiplication by one common time-dependent scale factor preserves these properties. With the source's k=±1 normalization, r is dimensionless and a has units of length; using a dimensionless scale factor instead rescales the spatial curvature parameter. The origin of the spherical coordinates is arbitrary; it does not identify a physical centre. Cosmic time is the proper time of the comoving observers, and the absence of time-space cross terms makes their worldlines orthogonal to the homogeneous slices.
For a radial null geodesic between comoving endpoints,
∫tE​t0​​a(t)cdt​=∫0R​1−kr2​dr​.
(29)

1+z=λE​λ0​​=ν0​νE​​=aE​a0​​​.
(30)
Both observers' clocks are cosmic proper-time clocks. Peculiar velocities and local gravitational-potential shifts have been left out of this homogeneous calculation.
Angular size and brightness. For a galaxy small compared with its distance, the angular diameter distance is DA​=aE​R and its angular diameter is
Δθ≃aE​Rd​=a0​R(1+z)d​​.
(31)
Here the emitted proper diameter is measured transverse to the line of sight; radial proper distance is a different integral when k=0. Ignoring absorption, lensing caustics and intrinsic evolution, photon-number conservation spreads an isotropic-equivalent luminosity across the sphere 4πa02​R2. Each photon loses energy by 1+z, and its arrival interval is stretched by the same factor. The received bolometric flux is
F=4πa02​R2(1+z)2L​,DL​=(1+z)a0​R=(1+z)2DA​.
(32)
The disc's proper projected area is Ad​=πd2/4, and its observed solid angle is Ωd​≃Ad​/DA2​. Therefore its surface brightness is
Iobs​=Ωd​F​=4πAd​L​(1+z)−4=π2d2L​(1+z)−4​.
(33)
This is cosmological surface-brightness dimming. The normalization uses L as isotropic-equivalent luminosity along the viewing direction; another disc emission law changes that normalization but not the redshift power. This is a bolometric result. In a specified observed frequency band the emitted spectrum is sampled at a shifted frequency and requires a spectral correction.
The distinction in telescope aperture and resolved surface brightness matters here. A larger telescope collects more photons and improves angular resolution, but cannot restore the incoming radiance lost by cosmological dimming. For aperture diameter D and a diffraction resolution element of solid angle Ωres​∼(λ/D)2, the collected power from uniform resolved brightness scales as
Iobs​Atel​Ωres​∝Iobs​λ2,
(34)
independent of D. This is consistent with conservation of etendue. It explains why increasing aperture while keeping every gain in resolution does not increase counts per native resolution element from a smooth extended source. It does not make larger telescopes useless: at a fixed angular bin the counts grow as D2, so a photon-noise-limited or uniform-sky-noise-limited signal-to-noise ratio grows roughly as Dtexp​​. They also resolve smaller structures and improve point-source sensitivity. Aperture, exposure and angular binning help measure a dim galaxy, without changing its intrinsic (1+z)−4 brightness penalty.
