Metric (d)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Topological analysis Metric space
OurBigBook.com Words: 502 Articles: 9
A metric on a set X is a function d:X×X→[0,∞) satisfying, for every x,y,z∈X:
  • d(x,y)≥0: distances are nonnegative and finite.
  • d(x,y)=0 if and only if x=y: distinct elements have positive distance.
  • d(x,y)=d(y,x): exchanging the endpoints leaves the distance unchanged.
  • d(x,z)≤d(x,y)+d(y,z): the triangle inequality.
A metric equips its underlying set with a metric space structure; the distance function and the space carrying it are distinct concepts. The second requirement distinguishes a metric from a pseudometric, which may identify distinct elements at zero distance. The symmetry requirement also explains why directed travel times, such as uphill versus downhill times, need not define a metric.
A tempting alternative on the real numbers is d(x,y)=(x−y)2. It is nonnegative, symmetric, and zero exactly on equal endpoints, but fails the triangle inequality: d(0,2)=4>1+1=d(0,1)+d(1,2). Checking only the first three requirements would miss the failure.
