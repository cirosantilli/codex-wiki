ATP synthase rotary mechanism

 Home Biology Enzyme ATP synthase
OurBigBook.com Words: 357
The membrane motor F0​ contains a rotating ring of c subunits beside stationary subunit a. Two offset water-accessible half-channels connect opposite membrane sides to the ring. A proton enters one half-channel and protonates a glutamic acid residue on a c subunit; neutralization permits that site to enter the hydrophobic lipid bilayer. Rotation carries it around to the other half-channel, where it releases the proton. A conserved arginine in subunit a helps separate the routes and promotes release. Downhill proton transfer biases rotation; this is not a freely open transmembrane pore.[ref]
The central shaft transmits torque to F1​; a peripheral stalk prevents the catalytic head from simply co-rotating. Its three β catalytic sites, within an α3​β3​ head, cycle through different binding shapes. In the simplified binding-change picture, a loose site captures ADP and inorganic phosphate, a tight site favours bound ATP, and opening releases ATP. Rotation changes binding and release as well as chemistry.[ref]
One complete, tightly coupled turn cycles each catalytic site once, producing three ATP molecules. If the ring has n proton-binding subunits, the ideal motor budget is n transferred protons per turn, or n/3 per ATP. Ring size varies between organisms; the worked example chooses n=10, as in the studied bacterial systems, rather than assigning ten to every ATP synthase.[ref]
For this example the ring's repeat angle is 360∘/10=36∘, whereas the three-site catalytic cycle repeats after 120∘. Their ratio is 10/3, not an integer. Counting complete turns avoids inventing a fixed integer proton count for every catalytic event. The schematic does not require both parts to advance simultaneously as rigid gears; elasticity allows temporary twisting while their cycles proceed.[ref]
