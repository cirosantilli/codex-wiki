Consecutive first-order reaction mechanism (Ak1​​Ik2​​P)

 Home Chemistry Chemical reaction Chemical kinetics Reaction mechanism
OurBigBook.com Words: 398
A consecutive first-order reaction mechanism passes material through a reaction intermediate. Consider a stipulated chemical reaction network Ak1​​Ik2​​P, with constant positive rate constants, fixed temperature and volume, uniform mixing, and no reverse or competing chemical reactions. All three labels carry one conserved unit of material. Set [A](0)=A0​>0 and [I](0)=[P](0)=0. The law of mass action gives
dtd[A]​=−k1​[A],dtd[I]​=k1​[A]−k2​[I],dtd[P]​=k2​[I].
(14)
Adding the three rate equations proves [A]+[I]+[P]=A0​. Each step has a first-order reaction rate law; this does not make all three concentrations simple exponential decays.
For k1​=k2​, the exact solution is
[A]=A0​e−k1​t,[I]=A0​k2​−k1​k1​​(e−k1​t−e−k2​t),[P]=A0​−[A]−[I].
(15)
To derive the middle expression, substitute the first into the first-order linear differential equation for I and multiply by the integrating factor ek2​t. Then d(ek2​t[I])/dt=k1​A0​e(k2​−k1​)t, which can be integrated directly with the stated initial condition. The material balance supplies P without another integration.
The apparent division by zero at equal rate constants is removable. For k1​=k2​=k, the same integrating factor gives
[I]=A0​kte−kt,[P]=A0​[1−(1+kt)e−kt].
(16)
This is also the limit of the unequal-rate constant expression. Initially [P]/A0​=(kt)2/2+O((kt)3), so reaction product formation begins with zero slope despite immediate reactant loss. By contrast, a direct A⟶P first-order reaction with the same k has [P]/A0​=1−e−kt and nonzero initial slope. Their A traces agree exactly; their P traces do not.
For the chosen equal-rate constant example, I peaks at t=1/k=5.00s, because d[I]/dt=A0​ke−kt(1−kt). At that time [A]=[I]=A0​/e≈0.367879mmolL−1 and [P]≈0.264241mmolL−1; the direct model would already have [P]≈0.632121mmolL−1. The peak means formation and consumption of I are momentarily equal, not that the whole system has reached chemical equilibrium.
More generally the peak is at t∗​=ln(k2​/k1​)/(k2​−k1​), which is positive for either ordering of the two positive rate constants and tends to 1/k as they coincide. When k2​≫k1​, a brief initial adjustment can be followed by [I]≈(k1​/k2​)[A] and approximately equal formation and consumption reaction rates. The exact solution states when this small-intermediate approximation can be reasonable. It is inappropriate for the illustrated equal-rate constant case: at the peak, over one third of the conserved material is I. Such timing matters if the goal is to collect a useful reaction intermediate before it becomes the final reaction product.
