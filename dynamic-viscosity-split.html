Dynamic viscosity (μ)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Fluid mechanics
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 10
The dynamic viscosity μ is the coefficient relating shear stress to shear rate in a Newtonian fluid. For a simple shear flow u=(u(y),0,0),
τxy​=μdydu​=μγ˙​,[μ]=Pas=kgm−1s−1.
(1)
Here τxy​ is the x component of viscous traction on a plane with normal vector +ey​. The shear rate γ˙​=du/dy has units s−1. Thus dynamic viscosity measures the stress required to sustain a given rate of deformation. It is the shear coefficient; the separate coefficient for volume change is volume viscosity. [ref]
For a worked Couette flow example, place a Newtonian fluid of constant dynamic viscosity μ=0.10Pas between parallel plates separated by h=0.0010m. Hold the lower plate fixed and move the upper plate at U=0.020ms−1. Assume steady flow, no streamwise pressure gradient, and the no-slip boundary condition. Then
u(y)=hUy​,γ˙​=hU​=20s−1,τxy​=hμU​=2.0Pa.
(2)
The fluid pulls backward on the moving plate with force magnitude F=τxy​A, where A is its wetted area. For A=0.010m2 this is 0.020N. Measuring that force, the gap and the plate velocity determines μ=Fh/(AU): a direct way to measure dynamic viscosity. The linear profile and opposing wall tractions are the standard plane Couette flow solution. [ref]
The velocity gradient matters, rather than the velocity itself. Adding the same constant velocity to both plates and the fluid leaves du/dy, and hence shear stress, unchanged. In uniform flow, every layer translates together and du/dy=0. More generally, even a nonzero velocity gradient need not generate viscous stress: solid-body rotation has zero strain-rate tensor, as the viscous stress tensor example shows. Thin gaps amplify shear stress for a fixed plate-speed difference, making dynamic viscosity useful for predicting the force needed to move lubricated surfaces.
