Quantum superposition (∣ψ⟩=∑j​aj​∣j⟩,∑j​∣aj​∣2=1)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Quantum mechanics Quantum system Quantum state

A quantum superposition is a linear combination of quantum state vectors in a complex Hilbert space. In a specified orthonormal basis, a normalized pure state has the displayed expansion, where aj​ are probability amplitudes. A nonzero linear combination can always be normalized; an arbitrary sum of normalized vectors need not already have norm one. Having more than one nonzero component depends on the chosen basis: the Hadamard basis vector ∣+⟩ is a quantum superposition of the two computational-basis states, but is a single vector of the Hadamard basis. [ref].
The distinction from classical ignorance is quantitative. Consider the normalized qubit quantum state
∣ψϕ​⟩=23​​∣0⟩+2eiϕ​∣1⟩.
(14)
Its density operator, written in the computational basis, is
ρϕ​=∣ψϕ​⟩⟨ψϕ​∣=(3/4(3​/4)eiϕ​(3​/4)e−iϕ1/4​).
(15)
Preparing ∣0⟩ with probability 3/4 and ∣1⟩ with probability 1/4, while discarding the preparation label, instead gives the mixed state
ρmix​=43​∣0⟩⟨0∣+41​∣1⟩⟨1∣=(3/40​01/4​).
(16)
The diagonal entries agree, so both preparations give the same computational-basis measurement probabilities. The off-diagonal entries of ρϕ​ retain quantum coherence and the relative quantum phase. In the Hadamard basis, the Born rule gives
⟨±∣ρmix​∣±⟩=21​,⟨±∣ρϕ​∣±⟩=21​±43​​cosϕ.
(17)
The density matrix representation retains this distinction between a pure state and a mixed state. [ref].
For example, at ϕ=0, the quantum superposition yields the plus outcome with probability about 0.9330, whereas the mixed state yields it with probability 0.5. At ϕ=π/2 these particular probabilities agree, although the density matrices still differ: equality for one measurement does not establish equality of quantum states. The probability amplitude figure shows the comparison throughout a full cycle of relative quantum phase.
This is why a quantum superposition is useful: quantum gates act linearly on its probability amplitudes, and unitary operators preserve the full pure state information, including relative quantum phases. A Hadamard gate can turn otherwise hidden quantum coherence into different outcome probabilities. The beautiful feature is that the same basis weights can support different predictions when amplitudes recombine. The expansion specifies a preparation and its measurement predictions; it does not assign every unmeasured outcome a simultaneous classical value. See projective measurement for the separate question of the conditional state after a measurement.
