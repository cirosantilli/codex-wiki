Electrical engineering

 Home
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 22
Electrical engineering designs systems that generate, transmit, measure and control electrical energy and information. An electrical circuit makes this concrete: resistors, capacitors and inductors turn electric potential differences into predictable electric currents and time-dependent responses. Signal processing then asks which information those responses preserve, suppress or reveal.
The connection is useful in both directions. A series RLC circuit selects a band of frequencies through its physical dynamics; a moving average selects frequencies by arithmetic on recorded samples. Both can be studied through a frequency response. The models differ, but the question is shared: what happens to each component of the input, and what does that do to the output we care about?

Null measurement

Words: 54
Electrical measurement OurBigBook.com
A null measurement adjusts a known comparison until a detector indicates zero difference. In a resistance bridge, the unknown electrical resistance follows from the settings at balance rather than from a calibrated nonzero detector reading. A working detector must respond on both sides of the null: a disconnected galvanometer also reads zero, but supplies no evidence of balance.

Resistance bridge

Words: 1k Articles: 6
Electrical measurement OurBigBook.com
A resistance bridge compares electrical resistances by making two paths through an electrical circuit produce the same intermediate electric potential. The Wheatstone bridge turns this into a null measurement: adjust one arm until a detector joining the two intermediate points reads zero, then infer the unknown from a ratio. This is useful because the balance relation does not require an accurately calibrated detector gain or supply electric potential difference, provided the supply is nonzero and the resistors obey Ohm's law.

Wheatstone bridge

Words: 992 Articles: 5
Resistance bridge OurBigBook.com
The Wheatstone bridge has four resistor arms and two diagonals. Here the supply holds node A at constant electric potential difference Vs​>0 relative to node D, our electrical reference potential. The left path is A→R1​→B→R2​→D; the right path is A→R3​→C→Rx​→D. The unknown is Rx​, and R3​ is adjustable. A voltmeter or galvanometer connects B to C. We define its positive reading as VB​−VC​; exchanging its leads reverses the sign. All four electrical resistances are positive, the supply is ideal, and the calculation describes steady electric currents at fixed temperature.
Charles Wheatstone described the instrument in his 1843 Bakerian lecture while explicitly crediting Samuel Hunter Christie. The account from King's College London's collection identifies that attribution; the familiar name should not be read as an assertion that Wheatstone alone originated the bridge.[ref]
Wheatstone bridge balance (Rx​=R3​R2​/R1​)
Words: 141
Wheatstone bridge OurBigBook.com
First disconnect the detector. Each side is a voltage divider. Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's voltage law give the open-circuit intermediate electric potentials and their difference:
VB​=Vs​R1​+R2​R2​​,VC​=Vs​R3​+Rx​Rx​​,Voc​=VB​−VC​=Vs​(R1​+R2​R2​​−R3​+Rx​Rx​​).
(1)
Setting this difference to zero and cross-multiplying gives
R2​R3​=R1​Rx​,R2​R1​​=Rx​R3​​,Rx​=R3​R1​R2​​.
(2)
The balance compares ratios, not necessarily equal arm values or equal branch electric currents. At balance the detector carries no electric current, so Kirchhoff's current law again makes the electric current constant along each two-arm path.[ref]
For a worked null measurement, suppose R1​=1.000kΩ and R2​=2.000kΩ, and the detector null occurs at adjustable R3​=1.500kΩ. Then
Rx​=(1.500kΩ)1.000kΩ2.000kΩ​=3.000kΩ.
(3)
For Vs​=3.000V, both intermediate electric potentials are 2.000V. The left branch carries 1.000mA and the right branch 0.6667mA: the zero detector reading does not mean that the unknown carries no electric current. These are stipulated ideal values illustrating the calculation, not experimental observations.
Wheatstone bridge detector loading (Vd​=Voc​Rd​/(Rth​+Rd​))
Words: 288
Wheatstone bridge OurBigBook.com
A real voltmeter has finite input electrical resistance Rd​; a galvanometer also has a finite winding electrical resistance. Away from balance, its electric current changes both intermediate electric potentials. This is voltmeter loading when the detector is a voltmeter. Model the detector as a passive resistor, with no offset or injected electric current. Thévenin's theorem supplies the full response without pretending the connected bridge remains two independent voltage dividers.
With the detector removed, the equivalent source is Voc​. Replace the ideal supply by a wire to calculate the source electrical resistance seen between B,C: nodes A,D merge, making R1​,R2​ parallel and R3​,Rx​ parallel. Consequently
Rth​=R1​+R2​R1​R2​​+R3​+Rx​R3​Rx​​,Id​=Rth​+Rd​Voc​​,Vd​=Id​Rd​=Voc​Rth​+Rd​Rd​​.
(4)
Since the attenuation factor is strictly positive for Rd​>0, the null remains R2​R3​=R1​Rx​. A smaller detector electrical resistance reduces the measured electric potential difference off balance but does not shift the ideal null. Its electric current sensitivity instead depends on 1/(Rth​+Rd​); detector gain and resolution also matter in deciding how closely a null can be located.
In the plotted example, Rx​=1.100kΩ gives Voc​=−119.048mV and Rth​=1.02381kΩ. A detector with Rd​=1.000kΩ instead reads Vd​=−58.824mV. Using that loaded reading in the unloaded voltage divider formula would underestimate the deviation of Rx​ from 1kΩ. Balancing the bridge avoids this particular systematic measurement error.
The local derivative makes the sensitivity explicit. For R1​=R2​=R3​=R and Rx​=R+δR, the first-order Taylor expansion is
Vd​=−4RVs​​R+Rd​Rd​​δR+O((δR)2).
(5)
At the plotted null, the open-circuit slope is −1.25mV/Ω, compared with −0.625mV/Ω for Rd​=1kΩ. Increasing the supply improves this ideal sensitivity but also increases Joule heating. A finite source electrical resistance requires recomputing Rth​; the displayed parallel-pair expression assumes an ideal supply.
Slide-wire resistance bridge experiment
Words: 335
Wheatstone bridge OurBigBook.com
PHYWE's documented experiment uses a one-metre slide wire, a connection box, an unknown resistor, a known comparison resistor, a supply, and a digital multimeter in steady-current mA mode as the null detector. Its two yellow detector leads join the fixed-resistor junction and the slider. Move the slider until the indicated electric current vanishes; read the two wire lengths on the ruler and infer the unknown electrical resistance from their ratio. The guide advises changing the comparison resistor if no null is reachable or the slider lies near an end.[ref]
The inference requires uniform electrical resistivity and wire cross-sectional area, so the two wire electrical resistances have the ratio of their lengths, by R=ρel​L/A. Retaining PHYWE's labeling, its equation (6) is Rx​=RC​ℓ1​/ℓ2​, where RC​ is the comparison electrical resistance. This is a different arm placement from our schematic. The guide's example table reports larger discrepancies near wire ends.[ref]
Useful additional controls distinguish a true null measurement from an insensitive or disconnected detector. Check the detector zero with the supply off, then move the adjustable arm slightly to either side of the candidate balance and verify opposite detector signs. Reconnect the unknown with short, secure leads and repeat the balance. Any lead or contact electrical resistance included in that arm contributes to the inferred value, even though a detector lead carries no electric current at exact balance. For very small unknown electrical resistances, four-terminal sensing addresses this separate connection problem; a detector null alone does not remove it.
Check for Joule heating by repeating at lower supply electric potential difference and with shorter powered intervals, holding ambient temperature fixed. At balance the unknown still dissipates power
Px​=(R3​+Rx​Vs​​)2Rx​.
(6)
Halving Vs​ quarters this power while halving ideal detector sensitivity. A balance that moves with excitation or elapsed time suggests changing electrical resistance, including self-heating; it is not explained by passive detector loading alone. These are proposed controls, not claimed outcomes of PHYWE's experiment. Standard-resistor accuracy, wire uniformity, contact reproducibility and finite detector resolution still limit the electrical measurement. Large electrical resistances also make unwanted leakage paths consequential.

Voltage divider (Vout​=Vs​Rb​/(Ra​+Rb​))

Words: 41
Electrical engineering OurBigBook.com
A voltage divider takes an electric potential difference across two series resistors and measures the share across one. With no electric current drawn from the junction, Ohm's law gives Vout​=Vs​Rb​/(Ra​+Rb​) for the lower resistor Rb​. Drawing electric current from that junction changes the ratio through voltmeter loading; the Wheatstone bridge compares two such junctions.

Thévenin's theorem (V=Voc​−IRth​)

Words: 77
Electrical engineering OurBigBook.com
For a linear resistive electrical circuit with a well-defined two-terminal response, Thévenin's theorem replaces its terminal behavior by an ideal source of electric potential difference Voc​ in series with electrical resistance Rth​. Here I is the electric current delivered to the external load. With independent sources only, calculate Rth​ by setting ideal voltage sources to zero (wires) and ideal current sources to zero (open connections). With dependent sources, retain them and determine the terminal ratio using a test source. The Wheatstone bridge detector loading calculation applies the independent-source version.

Signal processing

Words: 1k Articles: 10
Electrical engineering OurBigBook.com
Signal processing transforms or analyzes signals to extract information, change a representation, or improve a measurement. Examples include smoothing a sensor record, selecting a band of frequencies, and decomposing a recording through Fourier analysis. A signal is a mathematical representation of a physical record; deciding which variation is meaningful and which is noise is part of the problem.
A linear filter offers a simple entry point. Its output is assembled from weighted copies of its input. A moving average can reduce rapid variation, but it also changes timing and can erase useful detail. The worked example below makes that tradeoff explicit rather than treating a smoother plot as automatic improvement.
Two complementary views make the subject appealing. An impulse response describes the effect of a localized input; a frequency response describes the effect of a sustained oscillation. For a linear time-invariant system, both describe the same operation, connected by convolution and Fourier analysis. [ref].

Signal (x(t),x[n])

Words: 176 Articles: 2
Signal processing OurBigBook.com
A signal is a function or sequence representing a quantity that carries information. An electric potential difference measured over time can be written x(t); regularly recorded values give a discrete-time signal x[n]. The meaning includes the measured quantity, units, time reference and sampling interval. The same list of numbers sampled once per second or once per millisecond describes different physical frequencies.

Discrete-time signal (x[n],n∈Z)

Words: 71
Signal OurBigBook.com
A discrete-time signal assigns a value to each of a discrete collection of times, often indexed by the integers. Regular sampling at rate fs​ gives x[n]=x(n/fs​), so a sinusoid with normalized frequency f cycles per sample has physical frequency ffs​. Discreteness of time does not itself require the amplitudes to be quantized; rounding the values is an additional operation. The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem gives conditions for recovering a band-limited continuous signal from its samples.

Noise (signal processing)

Words: 49
Signal OurBigBook.com
Noise is unwanted variation in a signal. Whether a component is unwanted depends on the measurement task: suppressing fast variation helps only when that variation is expendable. White noise provides one mathematical model; it does not describe every source of disturbance. A linear filter may reduce some noise while also altering the desired signal.

Linear filter

Words: 531 Articles: 4
Signal processing OurBigBook.com
A linear filter obeys the superposition principle: filtering αx+βz gives αT[x]+βT[z]. Many familiar examples are also linear time-invariant systems, represented by an impulse response and convolution. Linearity alone does not imply time invariance: multiplying x[n] by the index n is linear but changes its behavior when the same input is shifted.
The moving average is a finite impulse response example implemented by arithmetic; a band-pass filter can instead be built from a series RLC circuit. Their frequency responses explain which oscillations pass through. Choosing a filter means choosing how much amplitude, timing and detail may change, not simply removing every visible fluctuation.

Causal filter

Words: 49
Linear filter OurBigBook.com
A causal filter uses only present and past input values to produce its current output. The trailing moving average is causal; a centered average that uses future samples is not. Future samples can be available when processing a finished recording, but they are unavailable to a live measurement at the current time.

Finite impulse response (y[n]=∑j=0M−1​h[j]x[n−j])

Words: 349 Articles: 1
Linear filter OurBigBook.com
A linear time-invariant system has finite impulse response when its impulse response is nonzero at only finitely many indices. For a causal filter supported at indices 0,…,M−1,
y[n]=∑j=0M−1​h[j]x[n−j].
(7)
This is a finite discrete convolution. The coefficients h[j] specify the complete operation; they need not be positive or equal. Such a filter has a simple bounded-input estimate, ∣y[n]∣≤∥x∥∞​∑j​∣h[j]∣, so bounded inputs remain bounded. The moving average chooses h[j]=1/M.
Moving average (y[n]=M−1∑j=0M−1​x[n−j])
Words: 289
Finite impulse response OurBigBook.com
A moving average replaces each value by an average over a moving window of neighboring values. The simple trailing version with positive integer window length M is the causal filter
y[n]=M1​∑j=0M−1​x[n−j].
(8)
It is a finite impulse response linear time-invariant system. A constant input remains unchanged because the weights sum to one. Weighted and centered averages are other choices; the definition of the window determines both the weights and whether future values are needed.
For an exact five-sample example, let
x[n]=sin(2π(0.025)n)+0.45sin(2π(0.2)n),
(9)
defined at every integer index. The first component completes one cycle in forty samples and the second in five. Averaging five consecutive samples cancels the second component exactly. The first becomes
y[n]=Asin(2π(0.025)(n−2)),A=5sin(π(0.025))sin(5π(0.025))​≈0.975498.
(10)
The remaining component is slightly smaller and delayed by two samples; the filter does not recover the original component unchanged.
The example is in steady operation: its early output values use the specified negative-index samples. Starting a device at n=0 with an unknown past requires an initialization rule. Treating earlier samples as zero, shortening the initial window, and postponing output until a full window is available produce different initial values.
For zero-mean uncorrelated random variables ε[n] with common variance σ2, the averaged disturbance has
Var(M1​∑j=0M−1​ε[n−j])=Mσ2​.
(11)
The standard deviation decreases by M​, but successive filtered errors share samples and are generally correlated. This variance calculation also assumes the wanted component is not damaged by averaging. A rapidly changing feature can be suppressed along with the disturbance.
The frequency response explains the cancellation and limits of this method. A moving average preserves low frequencies comparatively well, yet its side lobes make it a poor substitute for a sharply selective frequency cutoff. Smith develops this time-domain versus frequency-domain distinction in The Scientist and Engineer's Guide to Digital Signal Processing, chapter 15: moving-average filters.

Band-pass filter

Words: 42
Linear filter OurBigBook.com
A band-pass filter preferentially transmits signals within a range of frequencies and attenuates sufficiently lower and higher frequencies. Taking the output voltage across the resistor in a series RLC circuit gives one example: the resonant series RLC response selects a band around 1/LC​. [ref].

Linear time-invariant system

Words: 397 Articles: 1
Signal processing OurBigBook.com
A linear time-invariant system combines two properties. Linearity means that the output from a linear combination of inputs is the same linear combination of their outputs. Time invariance means that shifting an input by an integer delay shifts its output by exactly that delay. The operation has no preference for a particular time origin.
For discrete time, write the unit impulse as δ[n]=1 at n=0 and zero otherwise, a Kronecker delta. Its response h[n]=T[δ][n] is the impulse response. A finite input can be decomposed into shifted impulses, giving
x[n]=∑k​x[k]δ[n−k]⟹y[n]=∑k​x[k]h[n−k]=(x∗h)[n].
(12)
The superposition principle supplies the sum and time invariance supplies the shifted copies of h. Infinite sums need convergence assumptions; for a bounded input and an impulse response h with absolute summability, the discrete convolution converges absolutely. For a physical system with stored initial energy, this construction describes the response with zero initial state; a separate free response accounts for nonzero initial conditions.
The gain is reuse. Measure or calculate one impulse response, and the convolution predicts the response to every allowed signal without solving a new dynamical problem each time. Alternatively, the frequency response predicts how each oscillatory component changes. [ref].

Frequency response (H(ω)=∑j​h[j]e−iωj)

Words: 217
Linear time-invariant system OurBigBook.com
The frequency response gives the change in complex amplitude of a sustained oscillation under a linear time-invariant system. If the impulse response is finite or has absolute summability, substituting x[n]=eiωn into its discrete convolution yields
y[n]=∑j​h[j]eiω(n−j)=H(ω)eiωn,H(ω)=∑j​h[j]e−iωj.
(13)
Thus ∣H(ω)∣ is the amplitude gain and its complex argument is the phase change. Real sinusoids follow by taking real or imaginary parts. Here ω is radians per sample; ω=2πf for f cycles per sample. A physical sampling rate converts that normalized frequency into cycles per second.
For a trailing moving average,
H(ω)=M1​∑j=0M−1​e−iωj=e−iω(M−1)/2Msin(ω/2)sin(Mω/2)​,
(14)
with removable singularities evaluated from the finite sum. In particular H(0)=1. For M=5 and ω=2π/5, the five roots of unity sum to zero, so the oscillation is canceled. At small positive frequencies in the first pass region, the real sine ratio is positive and the phase factor gives a delay of (M−1)/2 samples.
Increasing M moves the first zero to ω=2π/M, narrowing the main region of large gain. It does not create an ideal low-frequency cutoff: later side lobes transmit some higher-frequency components, and suppressing a component is only helpful when that component is unwanted. The frequency plot is the same operation as the delayed-sample average; the five-vector diagram explains one exact zero geometrically.
