Interval bisection

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Numerical analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 589
Interval bisection solves a target equation f(x)=t by repeatedly halving a closed interval known to contain a solution. It is the bisection method applied to g(x)=f(x)−t, so the target becomes a zero of a function. This reduction from an equation to root finding is described in Demanet's notes.[ref] Here the emphasis is on a continuous strictly increasing function: the target identifies one input, making interval bisection a way to evaluate its inverse function without an explicit inverse formula.
Let f:[a0​,b0​]→R be a continuous function, with a0​<b0​ and f(a0​)≤t≤f(b0​). The intermediate value theorem guarantees existence; a strictly increasing function also guarantees uniqueness. Check endpoint equality first. Otherwise, for the current root bracket [ak​,bk​], evaluate its midpoint mk​=(ak​+bk​)/2. If f(mk​)<t, retain [mk​,bk​]; if f(mk​)>t, retain [ak​,mk​]; if f(mk​)=t, return that exact solution. These comparisons preserve the target bracket. General bisection method needs continuity and opposite signs of g at the endpoints, without requiring monotonicity or a unique zero of a function; see limitations of bisection.
An original dimensionless polynomial example is
f(x)=x3+x,t=10,[a0​,b0​]=[0,3].
(37)
Here f(0)=0<10<30=f(3) and the derivative f′(x)=3x2+1>0, so there is exactly one solution. In fact x∗​=2; knowing this lets us check the computed absolute error independently of the guarantee. The first four halvings give the following exact rational numbers:
update k1234​tested mk−1​3/29/415/833/16​f(mk−1​)−10−41/8233/64−785/5123425/4096​retained [ak​,bk​][3/2,3][3/2,9/4][15/8,9/4][15/8,33/16]​​
(38)
The beauty of interval bisection is that the enclosure itself supplies the certificate: one comparison removes half the possible inputs, regardless of the steepness of the function graph. In exact arithmetic, after k completed halvings without exact termination, the width and the bisection error bound for the new midpoint are
Wk​=bk​−ak​=2k3​,∣mk​−x∗​∣≤2Wk​​=2k+13​.
(39)
The reported midpoint after update four is m4​=63/32, rather than the last tested point m3​=33/16. Its certified absolute error is at most 3/32, and its actual absolute error is 1/32. For a requested position tolerance 10−3, the bisection stopping criterion requires eleven halvings: ten give 3/2048>10−3, while eleven give
[a11​,b11​]=[4095/2048,2049/1024],m11​=8193/4096,∣m11​−2∣≤3/4096<10−3.
(40)
The actual absolute error is 1/4096. The new midpoint need not be evaluated to obtain this certificate.
The signed output residual is r=f(m)−t; the position approximation error is m−x∗​. They answer different questions: how close is the output to the target, and how close is the input to the solution? Driscoll and Braun explain why a small residual needs information about the derivative to imply small position error.[ref] For this particular polynomial, direct factorization gives
f(m)−10=(m−2)(m2+2m+5).
(41)
Thus at m4​=63/32 the residual is −13121/32768≈−0.400421, despite position absolute error 1/32=0.03125. If a differentiable function satisfies 0<ℓ≤f′(x)≤L throughout the bracket, the mean value theorem gives the rigorous conversion
ℓ∣m−x∗​∣≤∣f(m)−t∣≤L∣m−x∗​∣.
(42)
Our example permits ℓ=1 and L=28 on [0,3]. Strict increase alone supplies no positive lower derivative bound: f(x)=x3 is strictly increasing but f′(0)=0. A small residual alone is therefore not a universal position certificate; even the target equation 10−6(x−2)=0 has residual 10−6 at x=3, a full unit from its solution.
The guarantees require continuity and correct evaluations, not just narrowing endpoints. For the strictly increasing function h(x)=x when x<0 and h(x)=x+1 when x≥0, the target 1/2 lies between h(−1) and h(1) but is never attained: a jump discontinuity defeats the existence argument. With floating-point arithmetic or an approximate evaluation, an incorrect comparison can discard the true solution. A certified interval enclosure of f(m) wholly below or above t certifies the corresponding decision; an enclosure containing t is inconclusive, rather than proof of equality. If rounding makes the midpoint equal an endpoint, report the precision limit; see bisection stopping criterion for the rounded position bound. Both figures are exact mathematical examples, not measured data.
