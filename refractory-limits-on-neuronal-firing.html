Refractory limits on neuronal firing (f≤1/tabs​)

 Home Biology Neuroscience Neuron Action potential Neuronal refractory period
OurBigBook.com Words: 306
If every interspike interval is at least tabs​>0, a sustained spike train has mean firing rate at most 1/tabs​. Across a window of duration T, the event count satisfies N(T)≤1+T/tabs​; dividing by T and taking the long-time limit gives the bound. The extra one accounts for a possible first event. The bound permits much slower firing and says nothing about whether stronger drive can maintain the limiting rate in a real neuron.
For the chosen phenomenological paired-pulse recovery, the hold alone gives 500Hz. Add the further assumption that every successful event resets precisely the same recovery curve, with no accumulation from earlier events. Then 0.35nA onset-timed pulses can sustain at most 1000/4.942488≈202.327Hz in that model. This repeatable reset is an extra assumption: a two-pulse result alone cannot establish it. A real neuron's sustained rate also depends on neural adaptation, input delivery and evolving ion-channel inactivation. The existing leaky integrate-and-fire model similarly adds integration time to its imposed hold.
A homogeneous Poisson process of rate λ has interspike intervals with an exponential distribution, so
Pr(S<tabs​)=1−e−λtabs​>0.
(22)
With the purely illustrative choices λ=100Hz and tabs​=0.002s, this is 1−e−0.2≈0.181269. Thus about 18.13% of intervals predicted by that model violate this chosen hold. Matching a mean spike train count does not make a stimulus-only Poisson process respect recent firing.
A minimal alternative is a renewal process with independent and identically distributed intervals S=tabs​+X, where X has an exponential distribution of rate λ. Its hazard function is zero during the hold and λ afterwards. Its mean interval is tabs​+1/λ and long-run firing rate is
f=1+λtabs​λ​.
(23)
The same 100s−1 rate after a 2ms hold gives 83.333Hz, not 100Hz. This separate stochastic example has no gradual relative recovery; it illustrates why an available-state rate differs from an observed mean firing rate. Smooth dependence on time since the last event can extend the hazard function, while longer histories require more than this simple renewal process. Time-rescaled spike-train diagnostics can test the resulting history-dependent predictions against recorded spike trains.
