Write the optical system's spectral response as A(ω)eiϕ(ω). Expanding its spectral phase around the pulse's central angular frequency gives
ϕ(ω)=ϕ(ω0​)+ϕ(1)(ω0​)(ω−ω0​)+21​ϕ(2)(ω0​)(ω−ω0​)2+⋯.
(1)
The group delay is ϕ(1)=dϕ/dω, with units of time. The linear spectral phase term delays the pulse envelope by this amount. The group delay dispersion is ϕ(2)=d2ϕ/dω2, with units of time squared. It makes the group delay frequency dependent: tg​(ω)≃ϕ(1)+ϕ(2)(ω−ω0​). Positive group delay dispersion delays higher frequencies more and gives positive chirp. A nonzero quadratic spectral phase broadens an initially transform-limited pulse; applied to an already chirped pulse, a suitable opposite sign can compress it.
In chirped pulse amplification, an ultrashort seed pulse first passes through a dispersive stretcher, often a grating-based system. This correlates frequency with arrival time and increases pulse duration while approximately preserving pulse energy, thereby lowering peak intensity. An optical amplifier can then increase pulse energy without exposing the gain medium to the original short pulse's enormous peak intensity, reducing optical damage and unwanted nonlinear phase accumulation. A final dispersive compressor, typically using gratings, cancels the accumulated spectral phase to recover a short, high-energy pulse. The essential components are therefore a short-pulse source, stretcher, amplifier and matched compressor; spectral gain bandwidth and higher-order dispersion limit the achievable compression.

K(x,x′)=L0​+(Ax2+Dx′2−2xx′)/(2B)exp{ik[L0​+(Ax2+Dx′2−2xx′)/(2B)]}​.​

The printed minus sign is inconsistent with forward propagation under these conventions. A concrete counterexample is free space: A=D=1, B=L>0, C=0. Its true geometrical path is

When B=0, propagation is an imaging limit rather than an ordinary integral with division by B.

Four complementary classical features of a large-amplitude coherent state are:
  • It is an eigenstate of the annihilation operator, a∣z⟩=z∣z⟩. Normally ordered field amplitudes therefore behave like a classical complex amplitude; normalized photon subtraction leaves the same state.
  • Free harmonic evolution preserves its form, ∣z⟩→∣ze−iωt⟩. The mean electric field follows a classical sinusoidal wave with an amplitude and phase set by z.
  • Its photon number has Poisson distribution, with Δn/nˉ=1/nˉ​ and g(2)(0)=1. Relative intensity fluctuations become small at large amplitude.
  • Its optical quadratures X=(a+a†)/2 and P=(a−a†)/(2i) have minimum, balanced uncertainties, ΔX=ΔP=1/2 and ΔXΔP=1/4. The fixed vacuum-scale fluctuation becomes small relative to the large phase-space displacement.
These properties identify a particular classical oscillation, rather than a state of definite photon number. Absolute quantum fluctuations remain even when their relative size is small.

A universal quantum gate set can approximate any unitary operation on any finite register, to arbitrary requested accuracy, using a finite sequence of its gates on the register. Unobservable overall global phase is ignored. Approximation matters: a fixed finite alphabet has only countably many finite circuits, whereas there are uncountably many exact unitary matrices.
The Hadamard gate H and T gate generate a dense set of single-qubit operations, but cannot themselves entangle different qubits. Adding the controlled-NOT gate gives a universal multi-qubit set. More generally, an available two-qubit gate must be genuinely entangling: it must take at least one product input to an entangled output. A product of local unitaries, or a SWAP gate composed with local unitaries, cannot provide this resource. Together with arbitrary single-qubit control, an entangling two-qubit gate on a connected interaction network supplies universal register control.

For d-dimensional unitaries, tr(U†U)=d. The relative propagator W=U†V is unitary, and its eigenvalues lie on the unit circle. The triangle inequality gives ∣trW∣≤d, with equality precisely when all eigenvalues have the same phase, or V=eiγU. Therefore the specified trace-overlap gate fidelity lies between zero and one, is insensitive to global phase, and equals one exactly when the implemented gate has the same physical action as the target. It compares propagators independently of any particular input state; it is not the identically defined quantity as average state or average gate fidelity.

In heteronuclear liquid-state NMR quantum computing, different gyromagnetic ratios give well-separated resonance frequencies. A resonant transverse radio-frequency pulse selects one nucleus; its phase sets the rotation axis in the transverse plane and its duration and amplitude set the rotation angle. Detuning, free precession and rotating-frame phase changes supply z rotations. For homonuclear spins, differing chemical shifts can provide frequency selectivity, but selective pulses must be narrow enough not to rotate the other spin. Exactly magnetically equivalent spins cannot be independently addressed by a spatially uniform radio-frequency field merely because they are labelled as different qubits.
In the weak-coupling regime, the secular NMR scalar coupling is
HJ​=2πℏJI1z​I2z​=2πℏJ​Z1​Z2​,
(48)
with J in hertz. Free coupling evolution for 1/(2∣J∣) gives an entangling propagator exp[−isgn(J)πZ1​Z2​/4]. Suitable single-qubit z rotations convert it to a Controlled-Z gate, and Hadamard conjugation on the target converts controlled-Z to a controlled-NOT gate. Refocusing pulses remove unwanted Zeeman evolution and unwanted couplings. This construction applies to resolved, weakly coupled homonuclear spins as well as heteronuclear spins; strongly coupled or equivalent spins need a different control analysis.
These controls supply a universal gate set on a finite addressable connected register, but conventional liquid-state molecular NMR is not an architecture that scales straightforwardly to arbitrary register size. A finite chemical-shift bandwidth causes spectral crowding as more spins and coupling-split transitions are added; increasingly selective pulses become slow relative to coherence times, and complicated coupling networks require extensive refocusing. The exponentially small pseudo-pure-state signal further limits the complete ensemble scheme. Formal unitary universality therefore does not establish scalable physical addressability, gate speed or initialization.

One implementation uses type-II phase matching for spontaneous parametric down-conversion in a nonlinear birefringent crystal pumped by a coherent laser. A pump photon produces two lower-frequency photons of orthogonal polarizations, subject to energy and momentum phase matching.
