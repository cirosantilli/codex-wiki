Each source has density matrix
ρs​=p∣Φ+⟩⟨Φ+∣+(1−p)4I4​​.
(55)
Assume the two sources and the detector's random choices are independent. In the p2 branch both resources are ideal. If either resource is I4​/4, the remote pair remains I4​/4 after any specified Bell-basis measurement outcome. An ideal detector and the correction of part (b) therefore yield
ρideal detector​=p2∣Ψ−⟩⟨Ψ−∣+(1−p2)4I4​​.
(56)
When the detector instead reports an independent uniformly random label, the corresponding four Pauli gates average the conditional pair to I4​/4. Thus
ρ14​=v∣Ψ−⟩⟨Ψ−∣+(1−v)4I4​​,v=p2q.​
(57)
Here q is the probability of the reliable-detector branch, not its total chance of being correct: that chance is q+(1−q)/4.
For the usual two-setting CHSH inequality, the spin singlet state has correlation −a⋅b and the white-noise part has zero correlation, so E(a,b)=−va⋅b. With Alice's axes z,x and Bob's axes (z+x)/2​,(z−x)/2​, the absolute CHSH inequality combination is 22​v. These settings are optimal for an isotropic correlation tensor, giving
Smax​=22​p2q>2⟺p2q>2​1​.​
(58)
This is the threshold for the standard CHSH inequality with optimized projective polarization measurements. The unqualified phrase “the Bell inequality” needs this choice of test; entanglement alone is a weaker requirement, and no claim about every possible Bell inequality is needed for this calculation.
