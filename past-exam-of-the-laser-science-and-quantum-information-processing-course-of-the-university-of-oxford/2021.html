A chirp is variation of the instantaneous frequency through a signal. Here the group delay relative to the pulse centre at the optical lens is
tin​(ω)=dωdψ​=2η(ω−ω0​).
(10)
Consequently η>0 orders lower frequencies before higher frequencies; η<0 reverses that ordering. A representative strongly chirped optical pulse is
E(t)=A(t)cos(ω0​t+4ηt2​+ϕ0​),ωinst​(t)=ω0​+2ηt​,
(11)
with a smooth localized envelope A(t). The carrier oscillations become closer together towards later times for positive η and farther apart for negative η.
The relation between the spectral phase and a locally assigned instantaneous frequency is a stationary phase method, not an exact reconstruction from spectral phase alone: the spectral wave amplitude also matters. For example, if the spectral envelope is e−(ω−ω0​)2/(2σ2), a Fourier transform of a Gaussian gives the exact real-field phase ω0​t+ηt2/(4∣a∣2) up to a constant, where a=1/(2σ2)−iη. The slope retains the sign of η and approaches 1/(2η) for ∣η∣σ2≫1.

Place the optical lens at z=0. For a sufficiently broad incident Gaussian beam, the beam waist positions approach the focal lengths frequency by frequency:
zω​≃f0​+β(ω−ω0​).
(12)
Add the group delay at the optical lens to propagation through vacuum:
tω​=2η(ω−ω0​)+czω​​=cf0​​+(2η+cβ​)(ω−ω0​).​
(13)
Here an arbitrary common time origin has been omitted. Differentiating both equations with respect to angular frequency yields
cvf​​=β+2ηcβ​=(1+β2ηc​)−1.​
(14)
For β>0, define u=2ηc/β. The velocity graph in the preceding figure has horizontal asymptote zero and a vertical asymptote at u=−1. For η>0, 0<vf​<c: the higher-frequency components both enter later and travel farther. At η=0, vf​=c. For −β/(2c)<η<0, vf​>c: the farther-focusing components leave earlier, partly compensating their extra travel. At η=−β/(2c) every component focuses at the same time, so the trajectory is not a finite-velocity function of time. For η<−β/(2c) the farther positions focus first, giving vf​<0; it tends to zero from below as η→−∞.
A flying focus is a moving pattern assembled from different portions of an optical pulse. Each portion propagates causally at c in this model. The superluminal or backward motion of the focus therefore does not describe a material object or information overtaking light. The calculation assumes negligible additional frequency-dependent group delay from the optical lens; any such delay must be included in ψ.

A quasi-Fermi level is the chemical potential parameter of an approximately thermalized carrier population within one energy band. Rapid intraband scattering can establish a Fermi-Dirac distribution in each energy band before slower interband recombination equilibrates their populations. Thus separate EFc​ and EFv​ describe a pumped population inversion even though the entire semiconductor is far from thermal equilibrium.
At a common positive temperature, the Fermi-Dirac distribution is strictly decreasing in E−EF​. Consequently
fc​(E2​)>fv​(E1​)​⟺E2​−EFc​<E1​−EFv​⟺ℏω<EFc​−EFv​.​
(18)
The joint density of states is nonzero only above the band gap, so
Eg​<ℏω<EFc​−EFv​.​
(19)
In particular the quasi-Fermi-level splitting must exceed Eg​. The upper boundary is transparency, where stimulated emission balances absorption; the lower boundary has zero joint density of states in this ideal three-dimensional parabolic model. The strict occupation comparison uses T>0; at exactly zero temperature occupations become step functions, and their supports should be checked explicitly, as in the next part.

A double heterostructure laser sandwiches a narrow-band gap active layer between wider-band gap cladding layers. In a type-I band alignment, the conduction band edge is lower and the valence band edge higher in the active layer. Electrons therefore see an energy well, and electron holes also see a well because their excitation energy increases when the valence band edge decreases. Both carrier populations remain concentrated where stimulated emission occurs.
The active layer also normally has the higher refractive index, forming an optical waveguide. The optical normal mode overlaps the injected carriers strongly, and the wider-band gap cladding has less interband absorption at the emitted photon energy. Carrier confinement reduces leakage, while optical confinement reduces the required pump current and hence heating.
Writing Γ for the fraction of the optical normal mode overlapping the active layer, the threshold condition is Γg=αi​+αm​, with internal and mirror losses αi​,αm​. A larger overlap and a higher carrier number density at a given current both lower the threshold current. The figure shows composition-induced offsets; a working junction also has band bending under bias, which is omitted to make the confinement mechanism visible.

A universal quantum gate set can approximate any finite-register unitary operator to arbitrary accuracy, disregarding a common global phase. A finite example is the Hadamard gate, T gate and CNOT gate. In the ordered one-qubit basis (∣g⟩,∣e⟩),
H=2​1​(11​1−1​),T=(10​0eiπ/4​).
(41)
In the ordered two-qubit basis (∣gg⟩,∣ge⟩,∣eg⟩,∣ee⟩), with the first qubit the control,
CX​=​1000​0100​0001​0010​​.​
(42)
The one-qubit gates generate a dense set of one-qubit unitary operators, and CNOT gates supply entangling operations. Approximate universality is the appropriate qualification for a finite discrete set: its finite circuits cannot exactly equal every member of a continuous uncountable unitary group.

Without interaction, the two-qubit Hamiltonian operator is Ha​⊗I+I⊗Hb​, so its evolution factors as Ua​⊗Ub​. It maps each product state to another product state, and cannot implement an entangling two-qubit gate such as a CNOT gate.
In an ion trap, the Coulomb interaction couples the ions' displacements into collective vibrational normal modes. The qubit states are internal atomic states; lasers coupling internal excitation to creation or annihilation of a shared vibrational quantum allow that motion to carry conditional information between ions. Thus the physical mediator is a collective motional normal mode of the Coulomb-coupled chain, rather than a direct resonant exchange between isolated internal qubit transitions. Ground-state cooling and resolved motional sidebands support the ideal pulse model used here.

An AND gate maps four possible pairs to one bit and loses information, so it is not reversible. A reversible embedding retains both inputs and uses a target bit:
(a,b,c)⟼(a,b,c⊕ab).​
(48)
This is the Toffoli gate; initializing c=0 computes ab while preserving enough information to invert the operation.
Classical reversible gates on one or two bits alone cannot synthesize every such operation. Every permutation of two bits is an invertible affine map over the finite field F2​: there are 4⋅∣GL(2,2)∣=4⋅6=24 of them, exactly the 4! permutations. Their extensions to a larger register and their compositions remain affine. The nonlinear term ab in the Toffoli gate therefore cannot be generated. Three-bit reversible gates remove this obstruction, with ancillary bits initialized appropriately.
There is no conflict with quantum universality: one- and two-qubit gates may pass through quantum superpositions and use relative phases at intermediate steps. They are not restricted to permutations of computational basis states at every step. For example the Hadamard gate, T gate and CNOT gate can decompose a Toffoli gate exactly. Thus the classical affine obstruction does not apply to their intermediate unitary operators.

Quantize a single electromagnetic normal mode of angular frequency ωc​ and represent one isolated atomic transition as a two-level system with raising operator σ+​=∣e⟩⟨g∣. The bare Hamiltonian operator is
H0​=ℏωc​a†a+2ℏωa​​σz​.
(62)
The first main approximation is the electric-dipole approximation: the field varies negligibly across an atom, kaatom​≪1, and the leading coupling is Hd​=−d⋅E(r0​). For states of definite opposite parity, the relevant dipole part is off-diagonal. After choosing operator phases, this gives Hd​=ℏg(a+a†)(σ+​+σ−​), with g fixed by the transition dipole and the one-photon field wave amplitude.
In the interaction picture, aσ+​ and a†σ−​ oscillate at the difference ωa​−ωc​, whereas aσ−​ and a†σ+​ oscillate at the sum ωa​+ωc​. The second main approximation is the rotating-wave approximation: near resonance and for gn+1​≪ωa​+ωc​, the latter terms average away on the interaction time scale. The resulting Jaynes-Cummings model has
HI​=ℏg(aσ+​+a†σ−​).​
(63)
The two-level system and single-normal mode truncations are additional assumptions: other atomic transitions and optical normal modes must be sufficiently far off resonance or weakly coupled. Dissipation is neglected over the interaction time. The retained terms exchange one atomic excitation for one photon, conserving a†a+σ+​σ−​.

The practical requirements are strong coherent coupling of both atoms to the same optical normal mode with stable relative coupling phases, preparation of ∣ee;0⟩, and interaction timing on the scale g−1 before spontaneous emission or cavity loss destroys quantum coherence. The field must then be measured or extracted after halting the coherent exchange, with enough photon number resolution to distinguish one from two. Otherwise an inefficient detector can mistake a two-photon event for the desired herald. Even ideally the one-photon success probability is at most 1/3, so trials must be repeated.
“No photons detected” implies ∣ee⟩ only for the ideal zero-number projection specified above. With loss or finite detector efficiency, no click also includes missed one- and two-photon events and generally leaves a conditional mixed state. Detecting a leaking photon during the interaction is also a different measurement protocol from the end-time projective measurement analyzed here.
