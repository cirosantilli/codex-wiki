In Q-switching, hold the optical resonator's quality factor low while pumping builds population inversion, then rapidly reduce the loss. The stored excitation produces a large optical gain, the intracavity light intensity grows, and stimulated emission depletes the population inversion until the optical gain falls below the laser threshold. The resulting optical pulse is a transient discharge of stored excitation.
Its duration depends on the high-quality factor photon lifetime, initial population inversion, saturation dynamics and switching time. For example, a simple laser rate equation model is
S˙=(g(N)−τc−1​)S,N˙=Rp​−N/τu​−g(N)S,
(1)
where S is intracavity photon number, N is the available inversion in compatible units, Rp​ is the pump rate and τc​ is the photon lifetime. During the short discharge, pumping and upper-state decay can often be neglected. The build-up and depletion rates determine the width; there is no universal equality between the width and τc​. The initially stored energy and the output coupling also matter. Typical Q-switching produces nanosecond-scale optical pulses.

In mode locking, fixed relative phases between many longitudinal normal modes produce constructive interference during a short part of each optical resonator round trip. The round-trip time sets the repetition period. The available coherent gain bandwidth, the number of locked normal modes, the spectral phase, and residual group velocity dispersion determine the optical pulse duration.
For M consecutive equally weighted normal modes of angular frequency spacing Δω, their wave amplitude sum is proportional to
sin(Δωt/2)sin(MΔωt/2)​.
(2)
Its central peak has width of order 1/(MΔω), while its repetition period is 2π/Δω. A transform-limited pulse therefore becomes shorter as more coherent gain bandwidth is used. This mechanism can give picosecond or femtosecond optical pulses: it does not require depletion of the whole stored population inversion during each peak, as Q-switching does. Mode locking generally accesses a much shorter time-bandwidth product scale than the gain-discharge time of Q-switching.

In second-harmonic generation, the quadratic nonlinear polarization contains products of pairs of comb components. Their angular frequencies are
ωp​+ωq​=2ωce​+(p+q)Δω,
(10)
including 2ωp​ when p=q. Original comb components have angular frequencies ωr​=ωce​+rΔω, hence
(ωp​+ωq​)−ωr​=ωce​+(p+q−r)Δω.
(11)

ωbeat,min​=k∈Zmin​∣ωce​+kΔω∣,​
(12)
which is ∣ωce​∣ for the stipulated small offset. For 0≤ωce​<Δω it is min(ωce​,Δω−ωce​), provided the corresponding teeth are in the overlap.

This calls for very short broadband optical pulses, or coherent external spectral broadening. Zero-point fields are the irreducible electromagnetic field fluctuations in the vacuum state. Each normal mode has ground energy ℏω/2. Although ⟨E⟩=⟨B⟩=0 in the vacuum state, d gives ⟨E2⟩=⟨B2⟩=1. These are quantum fluctuations, not a classical random coherent field with a known instantaneous value.
Two consequences are the Lamb shift, where coupling to the quantized electromagnetic field changes atomic energy levels, and spontaneous emission, where an excited chemical atom can emit a photon into an initially empty normal mode. In the latter process the creation operator has a nonzero matrix element ⟨1∣a†∣0⟩=1; stimulated-plus-spontaneous factors have the form n+1, which does not vanish at n=0. Descriptions splitting spontaneous emission into vacuum fluctuations and radiation reaction depend on operator-ordering conventions; the measurable total rate does not. Zero-point fields do not mean that the vacuum state contains a nonzero mean photon number.

The five DiVincenzo criteria are: a scalable collection of well-characterized qubits; preparation in a known fiducial quantum state; relevant coherence times long compared with quantum gate times; a universal quantum gate set; and qubit-specific quantum measurement. The criterion is useful control of the whole computation, including errors, rather than merely observing two resolved levels.[ref]
For trapped ions, the implementation is:
  • Scalable characterized qubits. Two long-lived hyperfine structure levels or an optical ground/metastable pair in each ion encode ∣0⟩,∣1⟩. An ion trap confines individually addressed ions. Extending a chain adds qubits, but increasing motional complexity, addressing errors and heating limit straightforward scaling; segmented traps and transport can help.
  • Initialization. Optical pumping prepares the internal quantum state. Doppler cooling and, where required, resolved sideband cooling prepare the shared motion used for quantum gates.
  • Coherence. Long-lived internal levels give coherence much longer than controlled pulse times. Magnetic noise, laser noise, scattering and motional heating cause quantum decoherence; suitable clock-state encodings and control reduce these errors.
  • Universal control. Resonant optical, microwave or Raman transitions give arbitrary one-qubit rotations. Shared motional normal modes, coupled through the Coulomb force, mediate entangling two-qubit gates. Together these supply a universal quantum gate set.
  • Readout. State-selective cycling fluorescence, often with electron shelving, produces a bright/dark signal resolved for individual ions.
For trapped neutral atoms, the corresponding implementation is:
  • Scalable characterized qubits. Long-lived ground-state hyperfine structure levels encode qubits in an optical lattice or an array of optical tweezers. Large arrays are feasible, but random loading, vacancies, atom loss and individual addressing must be controlled.
  • Initialization. Cooling plus optical pumping prepares the internal quantum state. Filling or rearranging traps prepares a usable register; motional cooling is needed when the chosen quantum gate depends on motion.
  • Coherence. Weak environmental coupling of neutral ground-state chemical atoms allows long coherence. Trap-light scattering, differential light shifts and magnetic noise limit it; switching traps or choosing suitable encodings can reduce these effects.
  • Universal control. Microwave or Raman transitions implement one-qubit rotations. Ground-state chemical atoms do not have the ions' long-range Coulomb force, so that motional-bus construction cannot simply be copied. Controlled collisions or Rydberg blockade can nevertheless supply entangling two-qubit gates.
  • Readout. State-dependent fluorescence or state-selective removal followed by imaging identifies a chosen qubit. Optical resolution, cross-talk and atom loss constrain repeated measurements.
Thus neither platform is forbidden from meeting a criterion in principle. Weak neutral-ground-state interactions make entangling control harder, but claiming that trapped neutral atoms cannot implement a universal quantum gate set would be false. Gate-model quantum algorithms using Rydberg blockade had already been demonstrated in a programmable neutral-atom processor by 2022.[ref]

Encode 0 by phase zero and 1 by phase π. Alice and Bob choose their bits independently and keep their choices private. Bob announces the trial numbers with a D0 click over an authenticated public channel. For each retained trial, c guarantees that their bits agree. In the ideal symmetric setup, the two equal-phase cases 00 and 11 give the same click probability, so announcing success does not itself reveal which shared bit was used.
The matching-phase cases occur with probability 1/2, and a matching case succeeds with probability 1/16. Therefore
P(retained trial)=321​,E[Kraw​]=32N​.​
(65)
For independent ideal trials, the raw-key length has a binomial distribution with standard deviation 31N​/32. These are bits before sacrificing test positions or performing privacy amplification.
A missing click does not certify unequal phases: the same phases also fail to click with probability 15/16. With uniform choices,
P(ϕA​=ϕB​∣no click)=3115​,P(ϕA​=ϕB​∣no click)=3116​.
(66)
Thus a no-click trial supplies neither a known equal bit nor a known complemented bit. This is why it cannot be retained by the direct error-free sifting rule. The source's “cannot use” should be understood in this sense: the events are not statistically independent, so an absolute claim that they contain no information would be false.
To obtain a secret final key, reveal and discard a random subset of retained bits to test the observed error rate, then apply reconciliation and privacy amplification as required by a security analysis of the actual implementation. Resolving which time path a photon took removes the accepted histories' quantum coherence and can turn destructive interference into false D0 clicks, providing a simple disturbance test. Equal-phase click correlations alone are not a proof of security against every attack, particularly in the presence of losses and device imperfections. The supplied ideal model fixes the sifted rate N/32; it does not fix a positive secure final length without an adversary model and observed parameters.
