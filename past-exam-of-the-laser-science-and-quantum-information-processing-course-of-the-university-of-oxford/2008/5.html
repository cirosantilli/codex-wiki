For a normalized pure state, expand ∣ψ⟩=∑m​cm​∣m⟩ in an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of the measured Hermitian operator. The Born rule assigns outcome m the probability pm​=∣cm​∣2. Under the ideal projective measurement update, a nondegenerate outcome leaves ∣m⟩, up to a global phase. The sum of the outcome probabilities is one, and the expectation value is ∑m​m∣cm​∣2; a single trial reports one eigenvalue, not that average.
If M∣m⟩=m∣m⟩, then
m⟨m∣m⟩=⟨m∣M∣m⟩=⟨m∣M∣m⟩​=m⟨m∣m⟩,
(1)
since M=M†. A nonzero eigenvector has positive squared norm, so m is real. For two eigenvectors,
⟨n∣M∣m⟩=m⟨n∣m⟩=⟨Mn∣m⟩=n⟨n∣m⟩.
(2)
Thus (m−n)⟨n∣m⟩=0: distinct eigenvalues have orthogonal eigenvectors.

The overlap ⟨0∣+⟩=1/2​ is nonzero, whereas perfect discrimination of pure states requires orthogonality. Consequently no single-copy quantum measurement can identify both preparations with certainty. After a nondegenerate projective measurement, the loss of preparation information after a nondegenerate projective measurement prevents further measurements from recovering the missing label: conditional on the first outcome, the post-measurement state is the same for every compatible initial hypothesis. Repeating the same basis gives that outcome again; changing basis probes the collapsed state. Fresh, independently prepared copies would be additional resources.
In the computational basis, input ∣0⟩ always gives zero and remains ∣0⟩. Input ∣+⟩ gives zero or one with probability 1/2, leaving the corresponding basis state. Combining these likelihoods with equal prior probabilities, the Bayesian update after a projective measurement gives
outcome01​P(outcome)3/41/4​P(input 0∣outcome)2/30​P(input +∣outcome)1/31​​.
(3)
For example, Bayes' theorem gives P(0 input∣0 outcome)=(1/2)/(3/4)=2/3. Outcome one certifies input ∣+⟩; outcome zero is ambiguous. Both inputs that give outcome zero leave exactly the same post-measurement state ∣0⟩.
In the Hadamard basis, input ∣+⟩ always gives +, while input ∣0⟩ gives + or − equally. Therefore
outcome+−​P(outcome)3/41/4​P(input 0∣outcome)1/31​P(input +∣outcome)2/30​​.
(4)
Outcome minus certifies input ∣0⟩; outcome plus is ambiguous. Guessing the more likely input after the ambiguous outcome gives success probability 3/4 for either of these two fixed-basis measurements.

Put q=cos2(π/8)=(2+2​)/4 and 1−q=sin2(π/8). In the computational basis, input ∣a⟩ has outcome probabilities (q,1−q), while ∣b⟩ has (1−q,q). The marginal outcomes are equally likely; the Bayesian posterior probabilities are
outcome01​P(outcome)1/21/2​P(a∣outcome)q1−q​P(b∣outcome)1−qq​​.
(5)
Guess a after zero and b after one. Each guess is correct with probability q≃0.853553 and wrong with probability 1−q≃0.146447. Neither outcome is certain evidence for one input.
For a real state ∣ψθ​⟩=cosθ∣0⟩+sinθ∣1⟩, the Born rule in the Hadamard basis gives P(±∣θ)=(1±sin2θ)/2. Both angles π/8 and 3π/8 have sin2θ=1/2​. Thus both preparations give P(+)=q and P(−)=1−q. By Bayes' theorem, either outcome leaves the two input hypotheses equally likely. The biased outcome frequencies carry no information about which state was prepared; the best success probability with this measurement is 1/2.

The Bloch vectors of ∣a⟩ and ∣b⟩ are
ra​=(1/2​,0,1/2​),rb​=(1/2​,0,−1/2​).
(6)
They share the same x component and differ along z. The equal-prior discrimination of qubit states therefore selects the Z axis: it maximizes the separation of their projected outcome probabilities. This also explains why the X measurement in part 3 is uninformative.
For ∣0⟩ and ∣+⟩, the Bloch vectors are (0,0,1) and (1,0,0). Their difference points along n=(−1,0,1)/2​. Hence Carl W. Helstrom's Helstrom measurement for the zero and plus states measures
Mopt​=2​Z−X​,P±​=2I±Mopt​​.​
(7)
Guess ∣0⟩ after P+​ and ∣+⟩ after P−​. An explicit orthonormal basis for these projectors is
∣h+​⟩=cos(π/8)∣0⟩−sin(π/8)∣1⟩,∣h−​⟩=sin(π/8)∣0⟩+cos(π/8)∣1⟩.
(8)
The positive measurement axis is tilted by π/4 from +z towards −x on the Bloch sphere; the corresponding state-vector rotation angle is −π/8.
