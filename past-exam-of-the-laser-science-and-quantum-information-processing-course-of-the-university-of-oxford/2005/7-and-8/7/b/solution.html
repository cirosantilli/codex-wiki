In entanglement swapping, start with independent Bell pairs ∣ϕ+⟩12​∣ϕ+⟩34​. Send qubits 2 and 3 to a central Bell state analyzer, retaining 1 and 4 at separate endpoints. Reordering the tensor product factors to group 23 and 14 gives the identity
∣ϕ+⟩12​∣ϕ+⟩34​=21​(∣ϕ+⟩23​∣ϕ+⟩14​+∣ϕ−⟩23​∣ϕ−⟩14​+∣ψ+⟩23​∣ψ+⟩14​+∣ψ−⟩23​∣ψ−⟩14​).
(3)
Consequently each ideal Bell-basis measurement outcome has probability 1/4 and projects 14 onto the corresponding Bell state, even though those two photons have never interacted. Communicating the two-bit result permits an endpoint Pauli gate to convert every outcome into the same chosen Bell pair. Without that record, the endpoints have ρ14​=I4​/4, a product state, rather than a known entangled state.
The optical Bell state analyzer overlaps photons 2 and 3 at a balanced beam splitter, with matched spatial modes, spectra and arrival times, and resolves the output photon polarizations using polarizing beam splitters and single-photon detectors. Two-photon interference maps Bell states to different output patterns. The antisymmetric ∣ψ−⟩ gives one photon in each output with opposite polarizations. The symmetric ∣ψ+⟩ gives two opposite-polarization photons in the same output; the two polarization detectors there can resolve this pattern. The ∣ϕ±⟩ states give same-polarization pairs and remain mutually ambiguous in this basic setup. The detected pattern therefore heralds a particular endpoint Bell state. With ideal detection this arrangement succeeds unambiguously on two of four equally likely inputs, with probability 1/2; the successful events can be selected. A complete analyzer would accept all four outcomes. The endpoint quantum entanglement is conditional on the measurement record, so it enables no instantaneous signalling.[ref]
