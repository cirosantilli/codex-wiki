Treat ∣a⟩=∣0⟩ and ∣b⟩=∣1⟩ as an isolated ground-state hyperfine qubit. Their transition angular frequency is ω0​=2π×320.7MHz. The radiofrequency magnetic field couples them through an allowed magnetic dipole transition. After choosing its phase reference, take the two-level laboratory Hamiltonian operator to be
H(t)=−2ℏω0​​Z+ℏΩcos(ωt)X,
(1)
where X,Z are Pauli operators. Here Ω is defined by the on-resonance rotating-frame off-diagonal matrix element ℏΩ/2.
Use a quantum rotating frame and the rotating-wave approximation, valid for Ω,∣δ∣≪ω0​, with Rabi detuning δ=ω0​−ω. Ignoring off-resonant coupling to other levels gives
HR​=2ℏ​(ΩX−δZ).
(2)
At resonance δ=0, the unitary time evolution is
UR​(t)=cos2Ωt​I−isin2Ωt​X,∣ψR​(t)⟩=cos2Ωt​∣a⟩−isin2Ωt​∣b⟩.
(3)
Thus the Born rule gives coherent Rabi oscillations,
Pa​(t)=cos22Ωt​,Pb​(t)=sin22Ωt​.​
(4)
The stated amplitudes are in the rotating frame; the laboratory carrier phases are tracked by the drive reference.
These logical states are orthogonal, individually resolvable hyperfine levels in the same electronic ground state. They avoid fast optical decay, can be coherently coupled by radiofrequency control, and have a state-selective optical readout through ∣c⟩. The excited level ∣c⟩ is useful for fluorescence detection rather than for the storage superposition in this model. Long lifetimes alone do not guarantee a useful qubit: control isolation and adequate quantum coherence are also required.

A resonant Rabi pi pulse satisfies ΩTπ​=π. The supplied Tπ​=0.256s therefore gives
Ω=0.256sπ​≃12.27rads−1,2πΩ​=1.953125Hz.​
(5)
The ideal rotating-frame operation is −iX, a Pauli X gate up to global phase.
Both logical states belong to the electronic ground level. A fast electric dipole transition between them is forbidden, and spontaneous radiative relaxation at a hyperfine radiofrequency is weak. The optical readout field is off during the coherent pulse. Neglecting relaxation is therefore consistent with a population lifetime much longer than 0.256s. Magnetic noise, collisions and technical dephasing are additional limitations; the ideal coherent calculation assumes their effects are small over this interval.
At detuning δ, define Ωeff​=Ω2+δ2​. The same two-level Hamiltonian operator gives
Pb​(t)=Ω2+δ2Ω2​sin2(2tΩ2+δ2​​).
(6)
For an accurate fixed-duration π rotation, the detuning must be small compared with the Rabi angular frequency. Writing δν=δ/(2π),
∣δν∣≪2πΩ​=1.95Hz,​
(7)
a fractional mismatch much smaller than 6.1×10−9 relative to the 320.7MHz carrier. This is an accuracy scale, not an assertion that a particular nonzero detuning gives a perfect gate.
The small-detuning error of a Rabi pi pulse quantifies a chosen tolerance. For an initial ∣a⟩ and the resonant pulse duration,
1−Pb​(Tπ​)=(Ωδ​)2+O((δ/Ω)4).
(8)
A population-transfer error at most ϵ consequently requires approximately ∣δν∣≲1.95ϵ​Hz; for example, about 0.20Hz for one percent error. Full gate calibration also includes pulse area and drive phase, beyond this population-transfer criterion.

In the ideal state-selective fluorescence detection model, driving the a↔c optical transition produces a bright record only for a, while b is dark. A sufficiently discriminating pulse therefore measures the basis {∣a⟩,∣b⟩}. The optical cycle is assumed to return population to the appropriate logical state without leakage. Ignoring the recorded outcome removes the off-diagonal quantum coherence between a and b.
Take n equally spaced measurements at tj​=jTπ​/n, including the final time. Between checks the resonant drive rotates through angle ΩTπ​/n=π/n. The probability of changing basis state in one interval is
p=sin22nπ​.
(9)
After each ideal projective measurement, the populations therefore follow the symmetric two-state Markov chain
(Pa′​Pb′​​)=(1−pp​p1−p​)(Pa​Pb​​).
(10)
The population difference is multiplied by 1−2p=cos(π/n) at each step. Starting in a, the nonselective two-level Zeno dynamics gives
Pb​(Tπ​)=21−cosn(π/n)​=4nπ2​+O(n−2)⟶0.​
(11)
For n=64, the exact final population is Pb​≃0.03712, while the requested large-n estimate is π2/256≃0.03855, about four percent. Thus the transition that an uninterrupted pi pulse would complete is strongly inhibited.
If “leaving” instead means departing from a at least once in the complete measurement record, use the repeated projective-measurement survival probability. The probability that every check returns a is
Palla​=cos2n2nπ​,1−Palla​≃0.03782(n=64).
(12)
It has the same leading estimate π2/(4n), but is not exactly the final b population: a trajectory can leave a and subsequently return. Keeping these events separate also distinguishes postselected records from an experiment that measures only final populations.
The mechanism is the quadratic short-time quantum survival probability: each short interval has transition probability of order (Tπ​/n)2, so n checks leave only an order-1/n total transition probability. The quantum Zeno effect here requires effective, sufficiently fast measurements; a finite-duration optical probe is a physical interaction whose imperfections must be included when comparing with data.
The beryllium-ion quantum Zeno experiment of 1990 used the same ground-state transition and a finite-duration optical probing sequence, followed by a calibrated ensemble fluorescence readout. Its measured final populations motivate the nonselective model; the instantaneous projections here idealize those physical pulses.
