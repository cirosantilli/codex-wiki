Twin paradox clock experiments

 ... Physics Branches of physics Special relativity Lorentz factor Time dilation Twin paradox
OurBigBook.com Words: 341 Articles: 1
The 1972 paper Around-the-World Atomic Clocks: Observed Relativistic Time Gains by J. C. Hafele and others reports four cesium-beam atomic clocks flown around the world in October 1971, eastward and westward. Their readings were compared with the U.S. Naval Observatory reference time scale before and after travel. An electronic time-interval counter resolved differences to the nearest nanosecond; intercomparisons among the flying clocks continued during travel. The final ensemble results were −59±10ns eastward and +273±7ns westward; the quoted spreads are standard deviations. These are corrected clock offsets, not raw readings or aircraft travel times. Four clocks allowed identification of individual rate changes through patterns in their mutual differences. The authors also report magnetic shielding and laboratory orientation checks. Their analysis could not detect a disturbance common to every flying clock, and the small ensemble limits statistical conclusions. This is a concrete reunited-clock experiment with apparatus, readout and drift controls.[ref]
The companion paper Around-the-World Atomic Clocks: Predicted Relativistic Time Gains by J. C. Hafele and others computes −40±23ns eastward and +275±21ns westward from the recorded flight paths. These are predictions, with flight-data uncertainty estimates. Both time dilation from motion and gravitational time dilation matter: aircraft clocks at altitude experience a different gravitational potential, while the rotating Earth means a ground clock is not the inertial home clock of our simple model. Eastward and westward motion change the aircraft's velocity relative to an approximately nonrotating inertial frame, not merely its speed relative to the ground. The measured agreement therefore tests combined special relativity and general relativity; the westward gain does not contradict the maximum-proper time result for fixed endpoints in Minkowski spacetime.[ref]
A complementary microscopic test is Measurements of relativistic time dilatation for positive and negative muons in a circular orbit by J. Bailey and others. The authors measured muon decay lifetimes in a storage ring at Lorentz factor γ=29.33: 64.419±0.058μs for positive muons and 64.368±0.029μs for negative muons. The reported positive-muon lifetime agrees with the independently measured lifetime at rest and special relativity to a fractional error of 2×10−3 at 95% confidence. This is a decay-lifetime readout under continuous centripetal acceleration, rather than a reunion of portable clocks. It supports the clock hypothesis for this physical process and regime; the inspected abstract does not establish universal immunity of every clock mechanism to acceleration. [ref]
