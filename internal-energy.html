Internal energy (U)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 6
Internal energy U is the energy stored in the microscopic degrees of freedom of a thermodynamic system: random molecular kinetic energy, internal molecular excitations, and interaction potential energy. Its value requires a specified system and an energy reference. In thermodynamic equilibrium, internal energy is a state function; it records the present state, without remembering whether a previous change came from stirring, compression or heat transfer. This makes internal energy the common accounting quantity connecting mechanical experiments to thermal ones.[ref]
Heat and work describe energy crossing the system boundary during a process. They are not separately stored contents of the system. For a closed system whose bulk kinetic energy and external potential energy have unchanged endpoint values, the first law of thermodynamics gives
ΔU=Qin​+Won​=Qin​−Wout​.
(1)
Here Qin​ is positive for incoming heat, and Won​=−Wout​ is positive for net work done on the system. Two routes between the same equilibrium states have the same ΔU, although their heat and work can differ. The infinitesimal notation dU=δQin​+δWon​ distinguishes a state function change from path-dependent transfers.[ref]
For a calculated example, take one mole of a classical monatomic gas in the ideal gas regime, at fixed volume, from 300 to 310K. With stipulated molar gas constant R=8.314Jmol−1K−1, its internal energy change is 3nRΔT/2=124.71J. A purely thermal route supplies Qin​=124.71J with Won​=0. An ideal insulated stirring route supplies Won​=124.71J with Qin​=0, measured between equilibrium endpoints after the motion dies away. The same stored increment has two different transfer histories; neither history defines a separate kind of stored energy.
The familiar internal energy of an ideal gas is a useful special case. Neglect intermolecular interactions, hold the amount of substance n and composition fixed, and suppose that f independent quadratic terms per particle obey the classical equipartition theorem. Choosing the constant reference contribution as zero gives
U=2f​nRT,CV​=2f​nR.
(2)
Here T is absolute temperature, R is the molar gas constant, and CV​ is the whole sample's heat capacity at constant volume. A monatomic gas has f=3 translational terms. A rigid diatomic molecule has f=5 when its two rotational terms are active and vibration is frozen; activating one harmonic oscillator vibration adds two terms, one kinetic energy and one potential energy, giving f=7. The counting is of quadratic contributions, so a vibrational coordinate is not just one contribution.[ref]
This constant-f formula applies only over a regime with those same active terms, classical translational statistics, nonrelativistic motion and unchanged composition. Quantum freezing, quantum degeneracy, chemical reactions or appreciable intermolecular interactions invalidate that simple counting. More generally, at fixed amount and composition an ideal gas can still have U=U(T) with varying heat capacity at constant volume, so use ΔU=∫T1​T2​​CV​(T)dT. A nonzero constant reference adds U∗​ to fnRT/2 without altering ΔU.

Thermodynamic internal pressure (πT​=(∂U/∂V)T​=T(∂p/∂T)V​−p)

Words: 84
Internal energy OurBigBook.com
For a simple compressible system at fixed composition and particle number, the internal pressure is the partial derivative of internal energy with respect to volume at fixed temperature. From dU=TdS−pdV and the Maxwell relation (∂S/∂V)T​=(∂p/∂T)V​, it has the measurable expression in the title. It is distinct from the mechanical pressure p.
For an ideal gas, p=nRT/V gives πT​=0, so its internal energy depends only on temperature at fixed amount of gas. For the Dieterici equation p=nRTexp[−an/(VRT)]/(V−nb), direct differentiation gives
πT​=V(V−nb)an2​exp(−VRTan​).
(3)
Thus an equation of state determines this volume sensitivity even without determining the full caloric function U(T,V).

Internal energy and system boundaries

Words: 177
Internal energy OurBigBook.com
For an ordinary stationary laboratory sample, a useful convention is
Etotal​=U+Kbulk​+Φext​,ΔU+ΔKbulk​+ΔΦext​=Qin​+Won​.
(4)
The bulk kinetic energy Kbulk​ includes organized whole-body motion; for pure translation it is Mvcm2​/2, with total mass M and center of mass speed vcm​. The external potential energy Φext​ includes elevation in an imposed gravitational field, approximately Mgz in a uniform field. Accelerating a sealed bottle or lifting it without otherwise changing its state changes these mechanical terms, rather than its internal energy. Molecular kinetic energy is measured relative to the chosen bulk motion.[ref]
State the convention rather than applying this separation blindly. If an experiment separately tracks a rotating apparatus or an organized flow, its mechanical kinetic energy must not also be counted in U. In Joule's paddle-wheel experiment, waiting for the apparatus and fluid to come to rest removes such an endpoint ambiguity. Including the vessel and paddle changes whose internal energy is measured; including the falling weights additionally puts their changing gravitational potential energy inside the enlarged system's total energy balance.

Internal energy at constant temperature

Words: 237
Internal energy OurBigBook.com
Constant temperature does not generally mean constant internal energy. At fixed amount and composition, within one smooth thermodynamic phase of a simple compressible system, an equilibrium identity is
dU=CV​dT+[T(∂T∂p​)V​−p]dV.
(5)
The bracket vanishes for the ideal gas law pV=nRT. It need not vanish for a real gas: changing the volume changes the average interaction potential energy, even when the random-motion contribution stays at the same temperature. The identity follows by writing dU=TdS−pdV and using a Maxwell relation; it does not require the actual process to be reversible. [ref]
For a Van der Waals equation model with volume per mole v=V/n, p=RT/(v−b)−a/v2 gives (∂U/∂V)T,n​=a/v2. Thus an isothermal expansion from v1​ to v2​>v1​, at fixed amount of substance n, increases internal energy by
ΔU=na(v1​1​−v2​1​)>0.
(6)
Separating attractive particles makes their interaction potential energy less negative. For stipulated n=1mol, a=0.10Pam6mol−2, b=10−5m3mol−1, T=300K, v1​=0.010 and v2​=0.020m3mol−1, the calculated increase for this one-mole sample is 5J, despite ΔT=0. This dilute stable-branch model example is not a measurement of a named gas. [ref]
A phase transition gives another reason. During equilibrium vaporization of a pure substance at fixed pressure, the temperature can stay at its phase coexistence value while the phase fraction changes. Molecular separation changes interaction potential energy.[ref] If the closed system does only pressure-volume work, its latent heat input obeys
Qin​=ΔH,ΔU=ΔH−pΔV.
(7)
Here H=U+pV is enthalpy. A vaporization latent heat therefore is not automatically the internal energy increment: some input supplies expansion work. A fixed temperature reading alone cannot tell how much internal energy changed.

Joule's paddle-wheel experiment

Words: 547 Articles: 1
Internal energy OurBigBook.com
Joule's paper On the Mechanical Equivalent of Heat (1850) describes a copper vessel holding water and a brass paddle with stationary vanes. Descending lead weights drove its shaft through strings and pulleys; the vanes opposed simple co-rotation. A boxwood shaft segment reduced thermal conduction. He measured fall distances and initial/final temperatures, repeating twenty descents per run and disconnecting the drive for rewinding. Calibrated thermometers read the water and room.[ref]
His matched resting trials retained the water amount, duration, surroundings and reading procedure, omitting paddle motion to estimate environmental heat exchange. A screen reduced thermal radiation from his body. His calculation included the vessel and paddle heat capacities, external pulley/string losses, descent kinetic energy and string elasticity; it acknowledged residual vibration and sound.[ref]
The measured observable is a corrected temperature rise, interpreted through the participating heat capacity; it is not a direct view of molecular motion. The experiment tests whether measured mechanical input accounts quantitatively for thermal storage. Frictional warming by itself would establish neither the conversion factor nor the absence of heat exchange. A modern authored demonstration at UCSB instead measures crank turns and a cylinder's temperature with an electrical thermometer: the apparatus differs, but again both mechanical input and thermal response must be measured. [ref]

Paddle-wheel calorimetry energy balance

Words: 284
Joule's paddle-wheel experiment OurBigBook.com
Choose the water, vessel and immersed paddle as the closed system, with fixed outer volume and negligible endpoint changes in bulk mechanical energy. Between common equilibrium temperatures T1​ and T2​,
ΔUassembly​=∫T1​T2​​Ceff​(T)dT=Wshaft,on​+Qroom,in​.
(8)
The effective heat capacity Ceff​ includes every component that participates in the common temperature change. For nearly constant Ceff​, the readout is Ceff​ΔT. Heat transferred between water and paddle stays inside this boundary; it must not be added again as external input. The drive supplies shaft work even though the vessel has no expansion work.
For a simplified modern model, let M be the total descending mass, let each of N descents cover height h, and let Wloss,out​ be energy diverted by the external drive. With negligible remaining drive kinetic energy and elastic storage,
Wshaft,on​=NMgh−Wloss,out​,NMgh=Ceff​ΔT+Wloss,out​+Qroom,out​.
(9)
The signed Qroom,out​=−Qroom,in​ may be negative if the room warms the assembly. This model omits the small endpoint and elastic corrections explicitly considered in the historical analysis. It does not equate all of the weights' lost gravitational potential energy with the water's gained internal energy.
As a proposed modern protocol, measure the falling masses and distances, calibrate the thermometer and Ceff​ independently, record room temperature and resting drift before and after each run, and wait for fluid motion to decay before the final equilibrium reading. Repeat with different fall budgets and run durations. A stable ratio of corrected input to corrected thermal storage supports the first law of thermodynamics over the tested range. Resting controls only estimate leakage if their thermal conditions represent the driven run; sensor lag, nonuniform temperature, unmeasured drive losses, evaporation or changing heat capacity can spoil the inference. Those are limits of the measurement, rather than evidence that heat was stored as a separate substance.

Specific internal energy (e)

Words: 30
Internal energy OurBigBook.com
Specific internal energy is internal energy per unit mass. For a perfect gas with constant specific-heat ratio, e=p/[(γ−1)ρ]. Its material balance is ρDe/Dt=−p∇⋅u+Q, where Q is heat supplied per unit volume per unit time.
