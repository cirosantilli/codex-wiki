Borsuk-Ulam theorem

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Geometry and topology Algebraic topology
OurBigBook.com Words: 826 Articles: 3
The Borsuk-Ulam theorem says that recording n continuous real-valued measurements on an n-dimensional unit sphere must give identical records somewhere on opposite sides. The measurements can vary wildly; continuity alone forces a coincidence at an antipodal pair.
Precisely, for every integer n≥1 and every continuous map f:Sn→Rn, there is an x∈Sn such that
f(x)=f(−x),Sn={x∈Rn+1:∑j=1n+1​xj2​=1}.
(3)
Here x and −x form an antipodal pair on the unit sphere; the antipodal map reverses every position coordinate. The target is the Euclidean space Rn. Both points have the same entire output, so every one of the n measurements agrees at this common pair. See Hatcher, Algebraic Topology, Corollary 2B.7, p. 176.
Circle example. Parametrize the unit circle by x(θ)=(cosθ,sinθ), with angles taken modulo 2π, and record the first coordinate using the cosine function:
F:S1→R,F(x(θ))=cosθ,F(x(π/2))=F(x(3π/2))=0.
(4)
The top and bottom points of the unit circle form the required antipodal pair. Their first coordinates agree, while their second coordinates are 1 and −1.
Sphere example. On the unit sphere in three-dimensional Euclidean space, take the orthogonal projection onto the equatorial plane and identify that plane with R2:
f:S2→R2,f(x,y,z)=(x,y),f(0,0,1)=f(0,0,−1)=(0,0).
(5)
These poles are the only coincident antipodal pair for this orthogonal projection: equality of (x,y) and (−x,−y) forces x=y=0, and the unit sphere equation then gives z=±1. The Borsuk-Ulam theorem guarantees an antipodal pair for every continuous map into R2, even when no convenient projection formula is available.
Equal output need not mean equal position coordinates. Keeping all three coordinates gives a continuous map i:S2→R3 defined by i(x,y,z)=(x,y,z), the restriction of the identity map of R3. Its outputs at an antipodal pair are always distinct, since u=−u would force u=0, which is outside the unit sphere. Thus replacing the target R2 by R3 destroys this guarantee.
Why the circle case follows from the intermediate value theorem. For an arbitrary continuous map f:S1→R, write F(θ)=f(x(θ)) using the unit circle parametrization above. The continuous function
g(θ)=F(θ)−F(θ+π)
(6)
satisfies g(θ+π)=−g(θ) because F has period 2π. If g(0)=0, the first antipodal pair already works. Otherwise g(0) and g(π) have opposite signs, and the intermediate value theorem supplies a zero between them. At that zero, the two antipodal values of f agree. In the cosine function example, g(θ)=2cosθ. This short argument handles only n=1. See Hatcher, Algebraic Topology, discussion of Theorem 1.10, p. 32.
An ideal weather interpretation. Model the surface of Earth as a sphere. At one fixed instant, suppose temperature T and pressure P are globally defined continuous scalar measurements on that sphere. Apply the Borsuk-Ulam theorem to f(u)=(T(u),P(u)) to obtain a common antipodal pair satisfying
T(u)=T(−u),P(u)=P(−u).
(7)
The two equalities hold at the same pair; applying the circle argument separately to two measurements would not ensure that. This is a conclusion about the ideal continuous model, with both measurements taken at the same instant, rather than an assertion that an exactly matching pair has been empirically measured. Matoušek gives this motivating interpretation and explicitly cautions that physical temperature need not be continuous.[ref]
Equivalent formulations. A continuous map h:Sn→Rn is odd when h(−x)=−h(x), extending the symmetry of an odd function. The Borsuk-Ulam theorem equivalently says every such map has a zero. For a general f, the difference h(x)=f(x)−f(−x) has this symmetry; conversely, equal antipodal outputs of an odd map must be zero. Another equivalent formulation is that no odd map between spheres Sn→Sn−1 exists. Normalizing a nonvanishing odd map by its Euclidean norm would produce such an odd map between spheres. These formulations are useful starting points for algebraic topology; the general proof is beyond the circle argument here.[ref]
For further study, Jiří Matoušek's Using the Borsuk-Ulam Theorem (2003) develops the theorem and its applications to combinatorics and Euclidean geometry. An early publication is Karol Borsuk's 1933 paper Drei Sätze über die n-dimensionale euklidische Sphäre.
