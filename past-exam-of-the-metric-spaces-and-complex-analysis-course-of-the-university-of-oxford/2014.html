The closure A is the smallest closed subset of M containing A, equivalently the intersection of all such closed subsets. In a metric space it is characterized by
x∈A⟺B(x,r)∩A=∅ for every r>0.​
(2)
If some ball misses A, its centre lies outside a closed superset of A; conversely the complement of any closed superset is an open neighbourhood disjoint from A. This verifies the equivalence of the two descriptions.

Nonnegativity and symmetry follow from the corresponding metric axioms in the two factors. The maximum is zero exactly when both coordinate distances are zero, hence exactly when the ordered pairs are equal.
For the triangle inequality, put p=(m1​,n1​), q=(m2​,n2​) and r=(m3​,n3​). Then
δ(p,r)​≤max{dM​(m1​,m2​)+dM​(m2​,m3​), dN​(n1​,n2​)+dN​(n2​,n3​)}≤max{dM​(m1​,m2​),dN​(n1​,n2​)}+max{dM​(m2​,m3​),dN​(n2​,n3​)}=δ(p,q)+δ(q,r).​
(9)
Therefore the maximum formula defines a metric, the maximum product metric.

Each coordinate distance is at most the maximum, so convergence of the pairs implies convergence of both coordinates. Conversely, for any ϵ>0, coordinate convergence gives indices NM​,NN​ after which the respective distances are below ϵ. Beyond their maximum, both are below ϵ, and so is the maximum product metric.
(xn​,yn​)→(x,y)⟺xn​→x and yn​→y.​
(10)
The same distance comparison will also handle Cauchy sequences.

Let (xn​,yn​) be a Cauchy sequence in the product. Since each coordinate distance is bounded by δ, both (xn​) and (yn​) are Cauchy in their respective factors. By completeness they have limits x∈M, y∈N. Part (ii) then gives convergence of the pairs to (x,y).
Thus the product of two complete metric spaces is complete under the maximum product metric. This checks every Cauchy sequence, rather than assuming beforehand that it has a coordinate limit.

At z=x+iy, compute the complex derivative first along real increments and then along imaginary increments. Holomorphy gives
f′(z)=ux​+ivx​=iuy​+ivy​​=vy​−iuy​.
(16)
Equating real and imaginary parts proves the Cauchy-Riemann equations:

(17)
The partial derivatives exist because these directional difference quotients are limits of the holomorphic derivative. This argument proves the necessary equations; it does not claim that their pointwise existence alone is sufficient for holomorphy.

Morera's theorem says that a continuous complex-valued function f on an open set U⊆C is holomorphic if
∫∂T​f(z)dz=0​
(27)
for every triangle T whose closed interior is contained in U. Triangle boundaries are traversed with positive orientation. The continuity hypothesis matters: the zero-integral condition is combined with continuity, not used in isolation.

For the usual piecewise smooth complex path γ, let ℓ(γ) denote its finite length. The estimation lemma gives
​∫γ​fdz−∫γ​fn​dz​≤ℓ(γ)supz∈γ​∣f(z)−fn​(z)∣.
(28)
By uniform convergence, the supremum tends to zero, so
∫γ​fdz=n→∞lim​∫γ​fn​dz.​
(29)
The uniform limit is continuous on the path, so its integral exists. This is uniform convergence and contour integration; the path length is fixed while the functions vary.

Let the common domain be an open set U. The uniform limit theorem makes the limit f continuous. For every triangle whose closed interior lies in U, Cauchy's integral theorem gives ∫∂T​fn​=0. Write the Laurent series about a as ∑k∈Z​ck​(z−a)k. The isolated singularity is:
  • removable singularity if every negative-index coefficient is zero;
  • a pole of order m≥1 if c−m​=0 and ck​=0 for k<−m;
  • an essential singularity if infinitely many negative-index coefficients are nonzero.
These cases exhaust the possible principal parts. The residue is
res(f;a)=c−1​=2πi1​∫∣z−a∣=ρ​f(z)dz,0<ρ<r.​
(35)
For the first example, sinz=z+O(z3) gives
sin2z1​=z21​+31​+O(z2).
(36)
Thus zero is a double pole, with residue zero. For the other example,
1−cosz=2z2​+O(z4),1−ez=−z+O(z2),
(37)
so the ratio is −z/2+O(z2). Its singularity is removable, with extended value zero and residue zero. The numerator's zero has higher order than the denominator's, so there is no pole.

When all three points are finite, use
g(z)=(z−z3​)(z2​−z1​)(z−z1​)(z2​−z3​)​.​
(49)
The distinctness assumptions ensure that it is a nonconstant Möbius transformation, and direct substitution gives the three required values. If one of the specified points is infinity, the limiting formulas are

Let G be the subgroup preserving the three-point set. Its action gives a group homomorphism Φ:G→S3​. If Φ(h) is the identity permutation, then h fixes all three points, so part (i) forces h to be the identity Möbius map. Thus the kernel is trivial and Φ is injective.
For any permutation σ, part (i) gives a normalization gσ​ sending the ordered triple (zσ(1)​,zσ(2)​,zσ(3)​) to (0,1,∞). If g normalizes the original triple, then hσ​=gσ−1​∘g sends zj​ to zσ(j)​. Hence every permutation is attained, so Φ is surjective.
G≅S3​.​
(51)
This is the setwise stabilizer; its six maps need not fix each point individually.

Choose a Möbius map T with T(z1​)=0 and T(z2​)=∞, for example by normalizing the ordered triple (z1​,z3​,z2​). Conjugation h↦T∘h∘T−1 takes the requested subgroup to the subgroup fixing zero and infinity individually.
Such a transformation must be z↦cz with c∈C∗: the zero condition removes the constant numerator term and the infinity condition removes the nonconstant denominator term. Conversely every such dilation is Möbius and fixes the two points. Composition multiplies its nonzero parameter.
{h:h(z1​)=z1​, h(z2​)=z2​}≅(C∗,×).​
