A metric space (X,d) is a bounded set if there is a finite constant M≥0 such that
d(x,y)≤Mfor every x,y∈X​.
(1)
For nonempty X, this is equivalent to containment in an open ball of finite radius: the triangle inequality converts a radius bound into a diameter bound, and conversely a diameter bound gives a ball of any slightly larger radius about a chosen point.
It is a complete metric space if every Cauchy sequence in X has a limit of a sequence belonging to X. Explicitly, the hypothesis on (xn​) is
∀ϵ>0 ∃N ∀m,n≥N: d(xm​,xn​)<ϵ,
(2)
and completeness requires an x∈X with d(xn​,x)→0. Thus
every Cauchy sequence converges in X​.
(3)
Boundedness controls distances; completeness ensures that these limiting points remain in the space.

A subset F⊆X is a closed set in X when its complement is an open set in the metric topology. Equivalently,
x∈X∖F ⟹ ∃r>0: B(x,r)∩F=∅​.
(4)
The open ball is taken inside the ambient metric space X; closedness always depends on that ambient space.

First suppose F is a closed set. If x∈/F, the definition in part (ii) gives an open ball B(x,r) disjoint from F. Thus every y∈F has d(x,y)≥r, and the distance to a set satisfies dist(x,F)≥r>0. Its contrapositive is the required implication.

Write h(A,B)=supa∈A​dist(a,B), so that ρ(A,B)=max{h(A,B),h(B,A)} is the Hausdorff distance. Since X is a bounded set, there is a finite M with d(x,y)≤M. Nonemptiness of A,B therefore gives 0≤h(A,B)≤M, so ρ is finite. Its symmetry and nonnegativity are immediate, as is ρ(A,A)=0.
If ρ(A,B)=0, then every a∈A has zero distance to a set B. Since B is a closed set, part (a)(iii) gives A⊆B. Interchanging the sets gives B⊆A. Thus ρ(A,B)=0 implies A=B. Closedness is essential here: without it a set and its closure have Hausdorff distance zero.
For the triangle inequality, fix a∈A and ϵ>0. The approximation property of an infimum supplies b∈B with d(a,b)<dist(a,B)+ϵ, and then c∈C with d(b,c)<dist(b,C)+ϵ. Consequently,
dist(a,C)≤d(a,c)<dist(a,B)+h(B,C)+2ϵ.
(6)
Let ϵ↓0 and take the supremum over a to obtain h(A,C)≤h(A,B)+h(B,C). Applying this inequality in the reverse direction as well yields
ρ(A,C)≤ρ(A,B)+ρ(B,C)​.
(7)
All metric axioms follow. Boundedness was used to ensure finiteness, and closedness to distinguish different sets; neither was needed for the triangle inequality.

The intersection G is a closed set because arbitrary intersections of closed sets are closed. Its nonemptiness needs a construction: bounded closed sets need not be compact sets in this setting.
Fix n, xn​∈Fn​, and η>0. Recursively choose xk+1​∈Fk+1​, for k≥n, using the approximation property of an infimum so that
d(xk​,xk+1​)<2−k+η2n−k−1.
(8)
This is possible because xk​∈Fk​ and dist(xk​,Fk+1​)≤ρ(Fk​,Fk+1​)≤2−k. The two bounds form convergent geometric series, so the triangle inequality shows (xk​)k≥n​ is a Cauchy sequence. By completeness it converges to some x∈X.
For each fixed j, all sufficiently late xk​ lie in Fj​, by nesting. Closedness of Fj​ gives x∈Fj​, and hence x∈G. In particular, starting with any x1​∈F1​ proves G=∅. Moreover,
d(xn​,x)≤∑k=n∞​(2−k+η2n−k−1)=21−n+η.
(9)
For each starting point xn​, let η↓0 in this bound on its distance to a set G. This does not assert that a nearest point exists. It proves dist(xn​,G)≤21−n. Since G⊆Fn​, the other directed Hausdorff distance is zero, and
G∈F(X),ρ(Fn​,G)≤21−n⟶0​.
(10)
This is the Summably nested Hausdorff intersection theorem; the summable movement replaces any need for compactness.

δn​=supp,q≥n​ρ(Fp​,Fq​),An​=⋃m≥n​Fm​​.

ρ(Fn​,An​)≤δn​.

ρ(An​,Am​)≤δn​.

A metric space X is a compact space if every open cover X=⋃α∈I​Uα​, with all Uα​ open in X, has a finite subcover: finitely many indices α1​,…,αr​ cover X.
It is a sequentially compact space if every sequence (xn​) in X has a convergent subsequence (xnk​​) whose limit of a sequence belongs to X. In formulas,
∀(xn​)⊆X ∃x∈X, n1​<n2​<⋯: d(xnk​​,x)→0​.
(17)
A finite subcover is the defining test for compactness; a convergent subsequence is the defining test for sequential compactness.

Let (xn​) be any sequence in the compact metric space X. There must be an x∈X such that every open ball about x contains xn​ for infinitely many indices n. Otherwise, for each x∈X choose a ball B(x,rx​) containing only finitely many terms, counted by indices. These balls are an open cover. A finite subcover would imply that only finitely many indices occur anywhere in X, a contradiction.
Now choose successively nk​>nk−1​ with xnk​​∈B(x,1/k); infinitely many available indices make this possible. Then d(xnk​​,x)<1/k→0, so (xnk​​) is a convergent subsequence. Thus
compact metric space ⟹ sequentially compact space​.
(18)
The proof counts indices rather than distinct points, so it also handles a value repeated infinitely often.

The source prints m=0 in the zero case. Since the coordinates are indexed by positive integers, that overlaps its m=n case and does not define a sequence. We use the intended coordinate vectors, with em(n)​=1 for m=n and em(n)​=0 for m=n.
Every e(n) belongs to X, because its entries are bounded by one and its coordinate sequence eventually vanishes. If n=k, their supremum norm distance is
d(e(n),e(k))=1​.
(19)
Indeed, the difference is 1 at coordinate n, −1 at coordinate k, and zero elsewhere. No subsequence is a Cauchy sequence, since arbitrarily late distinct terms remain distance one apart. Every convergent sequence is a Cauchy sequence, so no convergent subsequence exists. Therefore X is not sequentially compact​.

By the permitted sequential compactness of the product metric space X×X, the sequence of pairs (ϕ(k)x,ϕ(k)y) has a convergent subsequence, and hence a Cauchy subsequence. Choose two of its indices r<s sufficiently late that
d(ϕ(r)x,ϕ(s)x)+d(ϕ(r)y,ϕ(s)y)<ϵ.
(23)
The distance-increasing assumption iterates: d(ϕ(r)u,ϕ(r)v)≥d(u,v). With n=s−r≥1, use u=x,v=ϕ(n)x, and then u=y,v=ϕ(n)y, to obtain
d(x,ϕ(n)x)+d(y,ϕ(n)y)≤d(ϕ(r)x,ϕ(s)x)+d(ϕ(r)y,ϕ(s)y)<ϵ.
(24)
Both summands are nonnegative, so
d(x,ϕ(n)x)<ϵ,d(y,ϕ(n)y)<ϵ​.
(25)
The use of paired iterates ensures that the same positive return time works for both points.

At every z=x+iy∈U, the Cauchy-Riemann equations are
ux​=vy​,uy​=−vx​​.
(29)
To derive them from complex differentiability, approach z along real increments h. The complex derivative gives
f′(z)=limh→0​hf(z+h)−f(z)​=ux​+ivx​.
(30)
Approaching along imaginary increments ih instead gives
f′(z)=limh→0​ihf(z+ih)−f(z)​=vy​−iuy​.
(31)
These limits exist because f is holomorphic. Equating their real and imaginary parts proves the equations, together with f′=ux​+ivx​=vy​−iuy​.

A sufficient condition is that U is a connected space. Suppose, towards a contradiction, that ∣f∣ is the same constant R throughout U. If R=0, then f=0, contradicting f(a)=f(b).
If R>0, differentiate u2+v2=R2 in x and y, and use the Cauchy-Riemann equations from part (a). They give

(32)
The coefficient matrix for (ux​,vx​) has determinant −(u2+v2)=−R2=0. Hence ux​=vx​=0, so f′=0 everywhere. A holomorphic function with zero complex derivative is locally constant: within a small disc, differentiate along a straight segment. The identity theorem states: if V⊆C is a nonempty connected open set and p,q are holomorphic functions on V, and their equality set has a limit point in V, then
p=qthroughout V​.
(36)
The interior location of the limit point and connectedness are essential hypotheses.
For the proof put h=p−q. At any z0​∈V, its convergent Taylor series either has every coefficient zero, making h zero on a neighbourhood, or has a first nonzero coefficient, of degree m≥0. In the latter case,
h(z)=(z−z0​)mk(z),k(z0​)=0,
(37)
where k is holomorphic near z0​. By continuity, k is nonzero on a smaller disc. Thus any zero at z0​ is isolated unless h vanishes on a neighbourhood. An accumulation of distinct zeros at the assumed interior limit point therefore forces the neighbourhood alternative.
Let E be the set of points of V with a neighbourhood on which h=0. We have just proved E=∅, and E is an open set. It is also a closed set relative to V: a point of its closure outside E would be approached by distinct points of E, hence by zeros, and the same Taylor series argument would place it in E. Since V is connected, E=V. Thus h=0 on V, proving the theorem.

A function has an isolated singularity at a if, for some r>0, it is defined and holomorphic on the punctured disc 0<∣z−a∣<r, while a holomorphic extension through a has not been assumed. In particular, its domain omits a, or its assigned value there fails to give such an extension. The key local condition is
f holomorphic on B(a,r)∖{a}​.
(40)
The convention includes removable singularities, even when a suitable extension would eliminate the missing point.

We prove the Casorati-Weierstrass theorem by contradiction. Fix an allowed ρ. If the stated image were not a dense set in C, there would be a disc B(w,ϵ) disjoint from it. Hence
∣f(z)−w∣≥ϵ(0<∣z−a∣<ρ).
(44)
The holomorphic function h(z)=1/(f(z)−w) is bounded by 1/ϵ on that punctured disc. By the permitted Riemann removable singularity theorem, it extends to a holomorphic function H on the full disc.
If H(a)=0, then w+1/H is holomorphic near a and agrees with f away from a. This makes a a removable singularity of f.
If H(a)=0, then H is not identically zero, since h is nowhere zero away from a. Its Taylor series therefore has a finite first nonzero coefficient: otherwise the identity theorem would make it identically zero. Write H(z)=(z−a)mK(z) with m≥1 and K(a)=0. Then
f(z)=w+K(z)(z−a)−m​
(45)
has a pole of order m at a, since multiplying by (z−a)m gives the nonzero limit 1/K(a).
Both cases contradict an essential singularity. Thus
f(B(a,ρ)∖{a})​=Cfor every allowed ρ>0​.

A conformal map at a point z0​ is a map that preserves oriented angles between regular differentiable curves through z0​. In the differential formulation, it is real differentiable at z0​ and its derivative is an orientation-preserving similarity: a rotation followed by multiplication of lengths by a positive constant. The map is conformal on U when it has this property at every point:
Df(z0​)=λRα​,λ>0​.
(66)
Here Rα​ is a planar rotation. Conformality is a local property and does not itself require global injectivity. We use the oriented convention standard in complex analysis; a reflection reverses oriented angles.

Suppose F:D→C were a holomorphic bijection. By the allowed nonvanishing derivative result, F′(z)=0 throughout D. The holomorphic inverse function theorem gives a holomorphic inverse locally at every image point. Since F is globally bijective, these local inverses agree with its global inverse H:C→D.

there is no holomorphic bijection D→C​.
