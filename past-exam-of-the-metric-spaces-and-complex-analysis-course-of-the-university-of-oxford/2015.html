The Heine-Borel theorem says that a subset K of finite-dimensional Euclidean space is compact exactly when it is both closed and bounded:
K⊆Rn compact⟺K closed and bounded.​
(1)
Here compactness refers to the subspace topology induced by the usual Euclidean metric, and closedness and boundedness are measured in the ambient Euclidean space. This characterization does not hold for arbitrary metric spaces.

b OurBigBook.com
The empty set is bounded. Otherwise choose p in the compact metric space X. The open balls B(p,n), n=1,2,…, form an open cover. A finite subcover exists by compactness; because the balls are nested, the largest one already covers X. Hence X⊆B(p,N) for some N.
For x,y∈X, the triangle inequality gives d(x,y)≤d(x,p)+d(p,y)<2N. Thus
diamX≤2N<∞,so X is bounded.​
(2)
The proof uses only compactness and the metric, not a coordinate system.

By the Heine-Borel theorem, a noncompact subset X⊆R is either unbounded or not closed. If X is unbounded, the restriction f(x)=x is a continuous function with unbounded range.
Otherwise X is bounded and not closed. Choose a∈X∖X, and define
f(x)=∣x−a∣1​,x∈X.​
(3)
Its denominator is nonzero on X, so it is continuous. Since a is in the closure, for every n there is xn​∈X with 0<∣xn​−a∣<1/n. Hence f(xn​)>n, proving that this continuous function is unbounded. These two cases establish unbounded continuous witnesses to noncompactness of a real subspace.

A metric space is connected when it has no separation of a topological space: it cannot be written as the union of two disjoint nonempty open subsets. Equivalently, the only subsets that are both open and closed are the empty set and the whole space.
X connected⟺no two disjoint nonempty open sets cover X.​
(7)
The open sets here are those of the metric topology.

A metric space is path-connected if every pair x,y can be joined by a path: a continuous map γ:[0,1]→X with γ(0)=x and γ(1)=y.
∀x,y∈X ∃γ:[0,1]→X continuous joining x to y.​
(8)
The path must remain in X, including at its endpoints.

b OurBigBook.com
Suppose a path-connected space had a separation X=U∪V. Choose u∈U, v∈V, and a path γ from u to v. The inverse images γ−1(U) and γ−1(V) are disjoint open sets in the subspace topology of [0,1], cover it, and are nonempty because they contain its opposite endpoints. This contradicts the connectedness of an interval.
Therefore every path-connected space is connected. Notice that it is the subspace topology on [0,1] that is used in the inverse-image argument.

Each vertical line segment over a rational coordinate meets the horizontal line at height one. A point on such a segment can move vertically to the top, then horizontally along that line; points already at height one use only the horizontal motion. Concatenating these motions joins any two points.
Thus the rational comb with a deleted attachment point construction, before deletion, is path-connected and hence connected. The infinitely many vertical pieces cause no difficulty because each individual path uses only finitely many segments.

Write C′=C∖{(1,1)} and V={1}×[0,1). Let L and R be the portions of C′ with first coordinate respectively less than and greater than one. Each is path-connected: move along a rational tooth to its corresponding open half of the top line, then horizontally there. Use the elementary fact that adjoining any collection of limit points to a connected set preserves connectedness. Indeed, in a proposed separation, the connected subset lies on one side; relative openness would prevent any point on the other side from being a limit point of it. Therefore L∪V and R∪V are connected. They intersect in V, so their union C′ is connected.
There is, however, no path from (1,0) to (0,1). If γ(t)=(x(t),y(t)) were one, let t0​ be the first time at which y(t0​)=1. Such a first time exists by continuity and compactness of [0,1], and t0​>0. The continuous image of a connected space is connected, and a connected subset of Q is a singleton: an irrational number between any two distinct rational values would separate it. C′ is connected but not path-connected.​

One standard form of Cauchy's residue theorem is the following. Let Γ be a positively oriented contour that is a simple closed curve, and let f be a holomorphic function on an open set containing Γ and its interior except for finitely many interior isolated singularities z1​,…,zm​. There are no singularities on Γ. Then
∫Γ​f(z)dz=2πij=1∑m​Res(f,zj​).​
(35)
The residue Res(f,zj​) is the coefficient of (z−zj​)−1 in the Laurent series at zj​. Each interior isolated singularity contributes once because the winding number of this positively oriented contour about each such point is one. Reversing the orientation of a contour reverses the sign. This form of Cauchy's residue theorem applies to the rectangle below.

The three contacts in the strip. Work with the external-contact arrangement drawn in the original diagram: both disks lie between the two parallel lines and touch opposite boundaries. A rigid motion puts those boundaries at x=0 and x=d, with d>0, and makes P=(0,0). Write R=(d,h), and let the positive radii be r1​,r2​. Because a tangent to a circle is perpendicular to the corresponding radius, the centres are
O1​=(r1​,0),O2​=(d−r2​,h).
(59)
At the external tangency, the two radial segments form the segment O1​O2​, with ∣O1​Q∣=r1​ and ∣QO2​∣=r2​. Hence the internal division formula gives
Q=r1​+r2​r2​O1​+r1​O2​​=(r1​+r2​r1​d​,r1​+r2​r1​h​)=r1​+r2​r1​​R.
(60)
Thus
Q=P+r1​+r2​r1​​(R−P),P,Q,R are collinear.​
(61)
The coefficient is strictly between zero and one, so Q lies on the actual line segment PR. This proves collinearity for tangent circles between parallel lines. We will also use the fact that PQ and QR are chords of the respective circles; the entire line segment PR therefore lies in the union of their closed disks.
The four contacts. Label the successive contact points T12​,T23​,T34​,T41​, with Tjk​ belonging to Cj​ and Ck​. The original diagram has four positive-radius circles with disjoint interiors, successive external tangencies, and distinct contact points. Set T=T41​ and apply the reciprocal Möbius transformation
w=z−T1​.
(62)
By part (b), the two circles C1​,C4​ through T become straight lines ℓ1​,ℓ4​. These straight lines cannot meet at a finite point, since their inverse images would give a second intersection of C1​,C4​. They are therefore distinct parallel lines. The other two circles avoid T, so their images D2​,D3​ are ordinary circles missing zero.
The three remaining contacts retain their tangencies. Indeed, away from T the map and its inverse are holomorphic functions with nonzero derivatives, hence are local conformal maps. In particular they preserve a shared tangent line. Equivalently, bijectivity preserves the unique intersection of two generalized circles, which characterizes their tangency here.
The finite closed disks bounded by D2​,D3​ lie between ℓ1​,ℓ4​, have external tangency to each other, and touch opposite boundaries at the images of T12​ and T34​. To track the sides as well as the boundaries, a closed disk avoiding T maps to the bounded closed disk of its image circle, while each disk with T on its boundary maps to a closed half-plane. The two latter half-planes have disjoint interiors, so their complement is the strip containing zero. Disjointness of the original interiors puts D2​,D3​ inside that strip. Neither image closed disk contains zero, since the reciprocal Möbius transformation sends only infinity to zero.
The first part now makes the three finite transformed contact points collinear, on a straight line ℓ. It is essential that ℓ misses zero. If it passed through zero, its segment between ℓ1​ and ℓ4​ would contain zero, since zero lies strictly within the strip. But the first part shows that this whole line segment is covered by the two image closed disks, neither of which contains zero. This contradiction proves 0∈/ℓ.
The inverse image of a straight line missing zero is a genuine circle through T, by the same coefficient transformation in part (b), or by applying the involution u↦1/u in reverse and translating back. This circle contains T12​,T23​,T34​, while T is the inverse image of the point at infinity on ℓ. Therefore
T12​, T23​, T34​, T41​ lie on one circle.​
(63)
This is the four contact points of a circle chain result; the four contacts form a cyclic quadrilateral.
The pictured arrangement matters. Arbitrary mixtures of internal tangency and external tangency can instead give four distinct collinear contacts: take four circles whose diameter endpoints on the real axis are respectively [0,3], [0,1], [1,2], and [2,3]. Their successive contacts are 0,1,2,3, so no ordinary circle contains all four. The proof above establishes the requested genuine-circle conclusion for the external-contact arrangement actually drawn, rather than silently treating every generalized circle as an ordinary circle.
