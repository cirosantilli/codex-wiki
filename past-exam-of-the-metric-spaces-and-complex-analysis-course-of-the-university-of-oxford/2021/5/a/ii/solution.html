The Liouville theorem states that every bounded entire function is constant.
By the Laurent theorem, f has a unique Laurent series f(z)=∑n=−∞∞​cn​zn on R<∣z∣<S. Split it into
g(z)=n=0∑∞​cn​zn,h(z)=m=1∑∞​c−m​z−m.​

Here g is holomorphic on DS​ and h is holomorphic on AR,∞​. To check their extended regions of convergence explicitly, fix r∈(R,S) and put Mr​=max∣z∣=r​∣f(z)∣. The Laurent coefficient formula gives
∣cn​∣≤Mr​r−n(n≥0),∣c−m​∣≤Mr​rm(m≥1).
(70)
The first bound ensures convergence uniformly on every closed disk ∣z∣≤r′<r; letting r range up to S covers DS​. The second gives convergence uniformly on ∣z∣≥r′′>r; letting r range down to R covers AR,∞​. Locally uniform convergence of these power series, in z and 1/z respectively, proves the claimed holomorphy. On the original annulus their sum is f.
The second estimate also proves h(z)→0 as ∣z∣→∞: for any fixed r∈(R,S) and ∣z∣>r,
∣h(z)∣≤Mr​∑m=1∞​(r/∣z∣)m=∣z∣−rMr​r​⟶0.
(71)
For uniqueness under this condition, suppose f=g1​+h1​=g2​+h2​. Define F=g1​−g2​ on DS​ and F=h2​−h1​ on AR,∞​. The two definitions agree on the overlap, and their domains cover C, so they define an entire function. Moreover, F(z)→0 at infinity. It is bounded outside a large disk by this limit, and inside by continuity and the extreme value theorem. The Liouville theorem makes F constant, and its limit makes that constant zero. Hence g1​=g2​ and h1​=h2​. Without the limiting condition, adding a constant to g and subtracting it from h would preserve the decomposition.
