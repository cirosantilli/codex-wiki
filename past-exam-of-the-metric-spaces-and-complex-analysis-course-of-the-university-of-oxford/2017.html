A sequence (xn​) in a metric space (X,d) is a Cauchy sequence if
∀ε>0 ∃N ∀m,n≥N:d(xm​,xn​)<ε.
(1)
The index N may depend on ε, but must work for every pair of sufficiently late terms. No candidate limit is part of this definition.

A complete metric space is one in which every Cauchy sequence converges to a point of that space. For an incomplete example, take (0,1) with the usual Euclidean metric and xn​=1/(n+1). It is Cauchy because it converges to zero in R. It cannot converge inside (0,1): any such convergence would also hold in R, contradicting uniqueness of the limit, since zero is excluded. Thus the ambient metric and the underlying set both matter to completeness.

A topological space is connected if it cannot be expressed as a union of two disjoint nonempty sets open in that space. Suppose f(X) had such a separation U∪V. Here U,V are open in the subspace topology of f(X). Continuity makes f−1(U) and f−1(V) open in X. They are disjoint, nonempty because U,V contain image points, and cover X. This contradicts connectedness. Hence a continuous image of a connected space is connected.

A space is path-connected if for every pair x0​,x1​ it contains a continuous path γ:[0,1]→X with γ(0)=x0​ and γ(1)=x1​. For any y0​,y1​∈f(X) choose preimages x0​,x1​. A path between those preimages maps to f∘γ, a continuous path from y0​ to y1​ staying in f(X). Thus a continuous image of a path-connected space is path-connected. Surjectivity onto the ambient codomain Y is not needed; the conclusion concerns f(X).

The identity theorem says that two holomorphic functions on a connected domain which agree on a set with an accumulation point inside that domain agree everywhere there.
No such entire function exists. On the subsequence 1/(2m)→0 indexed by even numbers, the proposed function equals sinz. The identity theorem would force f(z)=sinz throughout C. At the points indexed by odd numbers it is instead required to equal −sinz, a contradiction since sin(1/n)>0 for positive n. The accumulation point zero lies inside the assumed domain of holomorphy, which is essential to this argument.

Morera's theorem states that a continuous function on a domain is holomorphic if its contour integral around every triangle whose filled-in interior lies in the domain is zero.
The uniform limit f is a continuous function, by the uniform limit theorem. For any such triangle T, the estimation lemma gives
​∫∂T​(f−fn​)(z)dz​≤length(∂T)supz∈∂T​∣f(z)−fn​(z)∣⟶0.
(20)
Each integral of fn​ vanishes by the Cauchy integral theorem. Hence the integral of f vanishes too, and Morera's theorem proves f is holomorphic on D. This is the basic locally uniform convergence of holomorphic functions argument; the given uniform convergence on all of D is more than needed.

For a holomorphic map, conformality means preservation of oriented angles locally; it holds at every point of G exactly when f′(z)=0 there. A Möbius transformation is M(z)=(az+b)/(cz+d) with ad−bc=0, extended to the Riemann sphere at its pole and at infinity.
For three distinct finite points define
Tz​(z)=(z−z3​)(z2​−z1​)(z−z1​)(z2​−z3​)​,
(33)
which sends z1​,z2​,z3​ to 0,1,∞. If one point is infinite, take the corresponding limit: for z3​=∞ use (z−z1​)/(z2​−z1​); for z1​=∞ use (z2​−z3​)/(z−z3​); for z2​=∞ use (z−z1​)/(z−z3​). Construct Tw​ similarly. Then M=Tw−1​∘Tz​ has the required three images.
For uniqueness, normalize any two proposed maps by these same transformations. Composing one normalized map with the inverse of the other fixes 0,1,∞. A Möbius map fixing infinity has c=0, one fixing zero then has b=0, and fixing one forces a=d up to the irrelevant common nonzero scalar. It is the identity. This proves sharp three-transitivity of Möbius transformations.

On the first quadrant choose the holomorphic logarithm with 0<argw<π/2. It maps w=ρeiθ to logρ+iθ, so its image is precisely the strip 0<Imζ<π/2. Its inverse there is the exponential function; both derivatives are nonzero. Composing with part (i) gives
F(z)=Log((1+i)1−z1+z​).​
(39)
This is a bijective conformal map from the lens onto the strip, with inverse
z=eζ+(1+i)eζ−(1+i)​.
(40)
The logarithm's branch is fixed by its argument range on the quadrant. Using a different branch would shift the strip vertically by an integer multiple of 2π. This is the lens-to-strip logarithmic conformal map.
