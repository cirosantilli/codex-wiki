Put αj​=1−βj​, so the interior angle at the jth polygon vertex is παj​. A conformal map straightens that corner by a power: locally,
f(z)−f(xj​)∼Aj​(z−xj​)αj​,f′(z)∼αj​Aj​(z−xj​)−βj​.
(1)
This follows by composing with the power 1/αj​ that maps the corner to a half-plane and using Schwarz reflection along its straight edges. The reflected map has a nonzero first derivative in the straightened coordinates.
For an upper-half-plane branch of the complex logarithm, define
P(z)=∏j=1n​exp(−βj​Log(z−xj​)).
(2)
Along each open interval of the real axis, P has constant argument. Crossing xj​ from left to right decreases arg(z−xj​) from π to zero, so the argument of P increases by βj​π. This is exactly the exterior turning angle of the polygon boundary. Thus f′/P has one constant boundary argument on all the intervals. The corner asymptotics remove its apparent singularity at each prevertex.
For a bounded polygon, the sum of exterior angles gives ∑j​βj​=2. Since every vertex has a finite prevertex, infinity maps to an ordinary point of an edge. In the local coordinate 1/z, Schwarz reflection gives f(z)=f(∞)+d/z+O(z−2) with d=0. Hence f′/P has a finite nonzero limit at infinity. After multiplication by one unit complex number, its boundary values are real. Reflecting across the real axis extends it to an entire function, including each removed prevertex, and it is bounded at infinity. Liouville's theorem makes it constant. Therefore the Schwarz-Christoffel formula is
f′(z)=cj=1∏n​(z−xj​)−βj​,c=0.​
(3)
Integration introduces one further additive constant.
The Möbius transformations preserving the upper half-plane have three real parameters:
z⟼cz+daz+b​,a,b,c,d∈R,ad−bc>0,
(4)
with common nonzero scaling irrelevant. They can send any cyclically ordered triple of boundary points to another such triple. Consequently three prevertices may be fixed independently; the other n−3 are constrained by the polygon's shape, through its side-length ratios. The constants of integration and multiplication account for translation, rotation and scale of the image, rather than further prevertex freedom.
If the last prevertex is infinity, use 1/z as its local coordinate. Its factor is absorbed into the overall normalization, leaving
f′(z)=cj=1∏n−1​(z−xj​)−βj​.​
(5)
For a finite image vertex at infinity's preimage, this has asymptotic order zβn​−2, because f(z)−f(∞) is of order z−(1−βn​). Thus the omitted factor is not evaluated by substituting infinity into the finite product; its angle is encoded by the behavior at infinity.

The two approaches to the slit base are distinct boundary points, each with interior angle π/2, whereas the slit tip has interior angle 2π. Their exponents in the Schwarz-Christoffel formula are therefore −1/2, 1 and −1/2. Write a=x2​∈(−1,1) and take
S(z)=z2−1​
(6)
on the upper half-plane, with the branch normalized by S(z)∼z at infinity. The derivative has the form
f′(z)=cS(z)z−a​.
(7)
On −1<x<1, the upper boundary value is S+​(x)=i1−x2​. Both endpoints of this interval map to the slit base, so
0=f(1)−f(−1)=c∫−11​i1−x2​x−a​dx=iπca.
(8)
The odd term integrates to zero, and c=0; hence x2​=0​. Now S′(z)=z/S(z), so f=cS+b. The endpoint values force b=0, and the tip value, using S(0)=i as a boundary limit, forces c=1. Thus
f(z)=z2−1​,f(z)∼z at infinity.​
(9)
The branch is important: on x>1 its boundary value is positive real, on x<−1 negative real, and on (−1,1) positive imaginary. The intervals (−1,0) and (0,1) traverse the two sides of the slit, meeting at its tip.
The map is injective in the upper half-plane: equal images imply z12​=z22​, and the alternative z1​=−z2​ cannot put both points in that half-plane. Its derivative has no interior zero. The image has positive imaginary part, by continuity of the branch and the fact that an interior real image would require z2−1 to be nonnegative real, which is impossible for Imz>0. Conversely, a point ζ in the upper half-plane outside the slit gives a unique upper-half-plane root of z2=ζ2+1. A point ζ=iy with 0<y≤1 gives only real roots or zero and is excluded. These observations verify that the image is exactly the required slit domain, rather than just matching its boundary.

The imaginary part of the complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow is the stream function. Its constant values on the wall and the free boundary make the potential domain the horizontal strip
0<Imw<1.​
(10)
The lower edge is the rigid wall, with B at w=0. The upstream end has Rew→−∞, and the downstream end has Rew→+∞. The upper edge is the free streamline. Thus AB and BC are the negative and positive portions of the real w axis, respectively.
Write q=w′=u−iv for the complex velocity. Downstream q→1. Upstream the fluid moves towards the corner along a wall of inclination π/4, so its physical velocity has argument −3π/4 and its complex velocity has argument 3π/4. Along each straight wall the velocity direction is fixed. At the corner the fluid angle is π/4, giving local behavior w∼K(z−B)4 and hence q→0: B is a stagnation point. On the free streamline, the speed is one, so ∣q∣=1.
The corresponding simple-jet hodograph plane is therefore the circular sector
0<argq<43π​,0<∣q∣<1.​
(11)
The radius at argument 3π/4 represents AB, the positive real radius represents BC, and the unit-circle arc represents the free boundary. The upstream and downstream ends are e3πi/4 and one, respectively; the corner is the sector's origin.

Starting from the hodograph plane, take the complex power
s=q4/3,0<argq<3π/4.
(12)
It takes the velocity sector bijectively to the upper half of the unit disk. The boundary points representing the upstream end, the corner and the downstream end become −1, zero and one. Next use the Möbius transformation
T=s−1s+1​.
(13)
The diameter −1<s<1 maps to the negative real axis, while for s=eiθ on the upper semicircle,
T=−icot(θ/2),
(14)
which is on the negative imaginary axis. The interior maps to the third quadrant. Squaring therefore gives a conformal bijection from that quadrant to the upper half-plane:
η=T2=(q4/3−1q4/3+1​)2.
(15)
The upstream end, corner and downstream end map to zero, one and infinity, respectively. The two walls map to the positive real axis, and the free boundary maps to the negative real axis.
Independently, the exponential function η=eπw takes the potential strip to the same upper half-plane with the same boundary correspondence. The strip height fixes the coefficient π, and the normalization w(B)=0 fixes the remaining positive multiplicative constant in η to one. Consequently
eπw=((w′)4/3−1(w′)4/3+1​)2.​
(16)

q4/3=p+1p−1​=tanh(4πw​),q=[tanh(4πw​)]3/4,

with the power taking arguments in (0,π) to (0,3π/4). It gives q→e3πi/4 upstream, q→1 downstream, and q→0 at B, verifying both the branch choice and the velocity normalization.

Let ζ=ζ(z,t) be the inverse conformal map. With Q real and nonzero, the function Qlog∣ζ∣ is harmonic away from the source, vanishes on the moving boundary, and has the required logarithmic singularity. Indeed F(ζ,t)=a1​(t)ζ+O(ζ2) with a1​=0, so log∣ζ∣=log∣z∣−log∣a1​∣+o(1) near the origin. Subtracting this candidate from the physical velocity potential leaves a bounded harmonic function near the source. Its singularity is removable, and the difference has zero boundary values. The maximum principle for harmonic functions makes it identically zero. Therefore
ϕ(z,t)=Qlog∣ζ(z,t)∣=Re(QLogζ(z,t)).​
(18)
Only the real part is needed, so the multivalued imaginary part of the logarithm causes no ambiguity in ϕ.
Differentiate F(ζ(z,t),t)=z while holding the physical point z fixed:
ζt​=−Fζ​Ft​​,ϕt​=−QReζFζ​Ft​​.
(19)
Also the local analytic complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow has derivative
wz​=ζFζ​Q​,∣∇ϕ∣2=∣wz​∣2=∣ζFζ​∣2Q2​.
(20)
The distinction between differentiating at fixed z and fixed ζ is essential because the boundary map depends on time. Substituting into the boundary evolution law and multiplying by ∣ζFζ​∣2/Q gives
Re(Ft​ζFζ​​)=Q.
(21)
Taking the conjugate inside a real part leaves it unchanged, so the desired conformal map equation for planar Darcy growth is
Re(ζFζ​Ft​​)=Q,∣ζ∣=1.​
(22)
As a normalization check, parameterize the boundary by ζ=eiθ. Its outward normal vector times arclength is ζFζ​dθ, so its area growth rate is ∫02π​Re(Ft​ζFζ​​)dθ=2πQ, exactly the specified source flux. For an expanding circular map F=R(t)ζ, the equation reduces to RR˙=Q.

Orient the contour and call its left-hand side the plus side. At a smooth interior point t, take a local arclength parameter γ(s) with γ(0)=t. The Cauchy principal value means the symmetric deletion limit
PV∫Γ​ζ−tf(ζ)​dζ=limε↓0​∫Γ∖γ((−ε,ε))​ζ−tf(ζ)​dζ.
(23)
The two local pieces are deleted with the same arclength cutoff. For a sufficiently smooth regular contour, an equal-radius deletion about t gives the same limit. The point is assumed to be away from endpoints and self-intersections.
To derive the Sokhotski–Plemelj formula, split the contour into a short smooth arc through t and its complement. The complementary integral has the same limit from both sides. On the short arc subtract f(z) from the numerator. Because f is holomorphic nearby, (f(ζ)−f(z))/(ζ−z) extends continuously as ζ approaches z; this part also has the same limit from either side. The remaining local term is f(z)∫dζ/(ζ−z). Straightening the oriented tangent shows its extra contribution is +iπf(t) from the left and −iπf(t) from the right: on a real segment this is the direct limit of ∫−δδ​du/(u∓iε). Dividing by 2πi yields
w±​(t)=±21​f(t)+2πi1​PV∫Γ​ζ−tf(ζ)​dζ.​
(24)
In particular the jump is w+​−w−​=f, while the sum is the principal-value integral divided by πi. Smooth densities on a real interval satisfy the same formulas at every interior point, by the identical subtraction step with a Hölder-continuous remainder.
Now put h=w/w. On the interval, the sign reversal of the auxiliary boundary values gives
h+​−h−​=w+​w+​​−w−​w−​​=w+​w+​+w−​​=w+​G​.
(25)
Thus the required Cauchy transform density is
F(ξ)=w+​(ξ)G(ξ)​.​
(26)
Whenever this density is integrable at the endpoints, taking h=C[F] constructs a function with the required jump. Multiplication back gives w+​+w−​=G. The jump of the constructed w is
f(x)=w+​(x)−w−​(x)=w+​(x)(h+​(x)+h−​(x))=πiw+​(x)​PV∫01​w+​(ξ)(ξ−x)G(ξ)​dξ.​
(27)
To conclude that this jump solves the final singular equation, one must additionally justify w=C[f]. This reconstruction requires endpoint and infinity conditions; the sign reversal of w alone does not ensure it. Here is a complete valid normalization. Let
S(z)=z(z−1)​,S(z)∼z at infinity,w(z)=S(z)1​.
(28)
The branch is holomorphic off [0,1], with S+​(x)=ix(1−x)​ and S−​=−S+​. If G extends smoothly to [0,1], the density F=G/w+​=ix(1−x)​G vanishes like a square root at each endpoint. Its Cauchy transform h is bounded at the endpoints and is O(z−1) at infinity. Therefore w=h/S is at most inverse-square-root singular at each endpoint and is O(z−2) at infinity. Its jump f is integrable.
For Cauchy reconstruction from a jump across a finite interval, apply the Cauchy integral formula to w on a large slit contour, and shrink its banks to [0,1]. The large circle contributes zero in the limit; each small endpoint circle contributes O(ε​) and also vanishes. The two banks give the jump f, proving w=C[f]. The sum part of the Sokhotski–Plemelj formula now gives
πi1​PV∫01​ξ−xf(ξ)​dξ=G(x),0<x<1.​
(29)
This proves the intended finite Hilbert transform inversion with admissible endpoint behavior. Adding a multiple of 1/x(1−x)​ to f gives a homogeneous solution; equivalently one can add a constant multiple of 1/S to w, whose boundary sum is zero.
The omitted normalization is a real limitation of the printed claim. To see it explicitly, choose instead w=S and G=1. This auxiliary function satisfies all the stated holomorphicity, nonvanishing and sign-reversal requirements. But
∫01​ξ(1−ξ)​(ξ−z)dξ​=−S(z)π​,h=C[1/S+​]=2S(z)1​,w=Sh=21​.
(30)
The displayed integral identity follows from ξ=(1+cosθ)/2 and its continuation from real z>1. Hence the proposed jump formula gives f=0, which does not solve the equation with G=1. The failure is the nonzero constant at infinity: w−C[f]=1/2. Thus the final implication is valid with suitable endpoint and decay conditions, but is false for an unrestricted auxiliary function as printed. The valid reciprocal-square-root normalization above supplies an actual solution for G smooth up to the two endpoints.

In the Laplace integral method for a differential equation, differentiate the integral three times with respect to z, and replace zezζ by ∂ζ​ezζ. Integration by parts gives
w′′′+zw=[g(ζ)ezζ]Γ​+∫Γ​(ζ3g(ζ)−g′(ζ))ezζdζ.
(31)
Thus the weight equation for this construction and its boundary condition are
g′=ζ3g,[g(ζ)ezζ]Γ​=0,
(32)
which give
g(ζ)=Aeζ4/4,A=0.​
(33)
These are the conditions obtained by eliminating the transformed interior term and the endpoint term separately. As usual for this method, differentiation and integration by parts need convergence assumptions; the contours constructed next make every such step valid locally uniformly for all complex z. A literal necessity claim for arbitrary weights and contours would additionally require uniqueness of the chosen representation by a Laplace integral; a null weight in a contour Laplace representation can otherwise be added without changing the solution.
Let
uj​=ei(π/4+jπ/2),j=0,1,2,3.
(34)
The quartic decay sectors for a contour integral contain the four rays ζ=ruj​. Along each ray, the quartic term is ζ4/4=−r4/4. For j=1,2,3, choose Γj​ to travel from infinity on ray u0​ to zero, and then from zero to infinity on ray uj​. Set A=1 and define
wj​(z)=∫Γj​​eζ4/4+zζdζ,j=1,2,3.​
(35)
On any compact set of z values, the integrand and each of its z derivatives are bounded by a constant times rme−r4/4+Mr for some M. These bounds are integrable at infinity, so differentiation under the integral sign is justified and the functions are entire. The endpoint term vanishes at both infinite ends. There is no extra term at zero because the two pieces join with the same integrand value and opposite endpoint orientations. Therefore all three functions solve the differential equation.
To prove independence, evaluate their first three initial data. For m=0,1,2,
wj(m)​(0)=Cm​(ujm+1​−u0m+1​),Cm​=∫0∞​rme−r4/4dr=4(m−3)/4Γ(4m+1​)>0.
(36)
If ∑j=13​cj​wj​=0, these initial data give ∑j=13​cj​(ujℓ​−u0ℓ​)=0 for ℓ=1,2,3. Define c0​=−∑j=13​cj​. Then
∑j=03​cj​ujℓ​=0,ℓ=0,1,2,3.
(37)
The coefficient matrix is a Vandermonde matrix at four distinct fourth roots of −1, and hence is invertible. All cj​ vanish. There is also a concrete reason for the earlier qualification about necessity. Deform the incoming ray of Γ1​ to angle π/3 and its outgoing ray to angle 2π/3, within their respective quartic decay sectors. The quartic integral is unchanged and remains nonzero. On this deformed contour, the extra entire weight eζ2 contributes zero for every z: throughout the closing wedge, Re(ζ2)≤−r2/2, so the large closing arc tends to zero and the Cauchy integral theorem gives a zero integral. Thus g=eζ4/4+eζ2 represents the same entire solution and has vanishing endpoint terms, although it does not satisfy g′=ζ3g. The canonical weight equation is the standard sufficient construction, rather than an unconditional uniqueness assertion about contour representations.
Thus the three contours give linearly independent nonzero solutions, and they form a fundamental system of solutions for this third-order linear equation. This proves independence directly instead of inferring it merely from the presence of three different contours.

Let η=Imk. On x>0, the modulus of the Fourier factor is e−ηx, so the given decay bound makes Y+​ converge absolutely when γ+η>0. On x<0, write x=−r; the bound becomes e−(γ−η)r, so Y−​ converges absolutely when η<γ. Uniform domination on compact subsets permits differentiation with respect to k, proving
Y+​ holomorphic for Imk>−γ,Y−​ holomorphic for Imk<γ.​
(42)
Thus the half-planes guaranteed directly by the given common decay bound have α=−γ and β=γ, and the equation holds on their overlap
−γ<Imk<γ.​
(43)
This is a statement about the domains guaranteed by that bound, not necessarily their largest possible domains. The exact solution below decays at different rates on the two half-lines and gives the sharper convergence half-planes Imk>−Ima and Imk<Imb, with maximal common strip −Ima<Imk<Imb.

The constant-coefficient equations and decay imply that only the modes eiax on the positive half-line and e−ibx on the negative half-line can occur; the opposite modes grow. Continuity and the nonzero derivative jump then imply nonzero amplitudes, so any common decay exponent satisfies 0<γ≤Ima<Imb. In particular −b lies below the plus holomorphic half-plane, and a lies outside the minus half-plane. This locates the poles needed for the transform calculation, without yet computing the amplitudes.
Rearrange the equation from part (i) as
k+bk+a​Y+​−(a+b)(k+b)1​=−(k−ak−b​Y−​+(a+b)(k−a)1​).
(44)
The left side is holomorphic in the plus half-plane, and the right side in the minus half-plane. Their equality on the overlap glues them into one entire function. The assumed decay of Y±​, together with the rational factors tending to one and the subtracted fractions tending to zero, makes this entire function tend to zero at infinity. It is therefore identically zero by Liouville's theorem. This is the additive splitting step of the Wiener-Hopf method. Solving the two zero identities gives
Y+​(k)=(a+b)(k+a)1​,Y−​(k)=−(a+b)(k−b)1​.​
(45)
Their apparent cancellation formulas extend holomorphically wherever a cancelled factor vanishes; the only actual poles are at −a and b.
With the Fourier convention in use, inversion has factor e−ikx and normalization 1/(2π). For x>0, close the contour in the lower half-plane clockwise, collecting the pole at −a. For x<0, close in the upper half-plane counterclockwise, collecting the pole at b. The residue theorem gives
y(x)=⎩⎨⎧​i(a+b)eiax​,i(a+b)e−ibx​,​x>0,x<0.​​
(46)
Equivalently, each displayed transform follows immediately by integrating the corresponding decaying exponential on its half-line, so Fourier uniqueness gives the same inversion without a contour estimate. Both values at zero are 1/(i(a+b)), and the derivative jump is (a+b)/(a+b)=1, directly checking the interface conditions.
The moduli are a nonzero constant times e−Imax for x>0 and e−Imb∣x∣ for x<0. Hence the largest common exponential decay rate is
γmax​=Ima.​
(47)
Strictly, the stated big-O bound is also true for every smaller positive γ; it does not specify a unique exponent unless the sharp rate is intended. The two transform convergence half-planes are exactly the sharper domains stated in part (ii), and the poles on their boundaries prevent extending either half-line integral domain any farther.
