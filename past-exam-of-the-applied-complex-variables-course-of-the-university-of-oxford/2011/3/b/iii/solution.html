The constant-coefficient equations and decay imply that only the modes eiax on the positive half-line and e−ibx on the negative half-line can occur; the opposite modes grow. Continuity and the nonzero derivative jump then imply nonzero amplitudes, so any common decay exponent satisfies 0<γ≤Ima<Imb. In particular −b lies below the plus holomorphic half-plane, and a lies outside the minus half-plane. This locates the poles needed for the transform calculation, without yet computing the amplitudes.
Rearrange the equation from part (i) as
k+bk+a​Y+​−(a+b)(k+b)1​=−(k−ak−b​Y−​+(a+b)(k−a)1​).
(44)
The left side is holomorphic in the plus half-plane, and the right side in the minus half-plane. Their equality on the overlap glues them into one entire function. The assumed decay of Y±​, together with the rational factors tending to one and the subtracted fractions tending to zero, makes this entire function tend to zero at infinity. It is therefore identically zero by Liouville's theorem. This is the additive splitting step of the Wiener-Hopf method. Solving the two zero identities gives
Y+​(k)=(a+b)(k+a)1​,Y−​(k)=−(a+b)(k−b)1​.​
(45)
Their apparent cancellation formulas extend holomorphically wherever a cancelled factor vanishes; the only actual poles are at −a and b.
With the Fourier convention in use, inversion has factor e−ikx and normalization 1/(2π). For x>0, close the contour in the lower half-plane clockwise, collecting the pole at −a. For x<0, close in the upper half-plane counterclockwise, collecting the pole at b. The residue theorem gives
y(x)=⎩⎨⎧​i(a+b)eiax​,i(a+b)e−ibx​,​x>0,x<0.​​
(46)
Equivalently, each displayed transform follows immediately by integrating the corresponding decaying exponential on its half-line, so Fourier uniqueness gives the same inversion without a contour estimate. Both values at zero are 1/(i(a+b)), and the derivative jump is (a+b)/(a+b)=1, directly checking the interface conditions.
The moduli are a nonzero constant times e−Imax for x>0 and e−Imb∣x∣ for x<0. Hence the largest common exponential decay rate is
γmax​=Ima.​
(47)
Strictly, the stated big-O bound is also true for every smaller positive γ; it does not specify a unique exponent unless the sharp rate is intended. The two transform convergence half-planes are exactly the sharper domains stated in part (ii), and the poles on their boundaries prevent extending either half-line integral domain any farther.
