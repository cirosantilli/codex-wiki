Orient the contour and call its left-hand side the plus side. At a smooth interior point t, take a local arclength parameter γ(s) with γ(0)=t. The Cauchy principal value means the symmetric deletion limit
PV∫Γ​ζ−tf(ζ)​dζ=limε↓0​∫Γ∖γ((−ε,ε))​ζ−tf(ζ)​dζ.

The two local pieces are deleted with the same arclength cutoff. For a sufficiently smooth regular contour, an equal-radius deletion about t gives the same limit. The point is assumed to be away from endpoints and self-intersections.
To derive the Sokhotski–Plemelj formula, split the contour into a short smooth arc through t and its complement. The complementary integral has the same limit from both sides. On the short arc subtract f(z) from the numerator. Because f is holomorphic nearby, (f(ζ)−f(z))/(ζ−z) extends continuously as ζ approaches z; this part also has the same limit from either side. The remaining local term is f(z)∫dζ/(ζ−z). Straightening the oriented tangent shows its extra contribution is +iπf(t) from the left and −iπf(t) from the right: on a real segment this is the direct limit of ∫−δδ​du/(u∓iε). Dividing by 2πi yields
w±​(t)=±21​f(t)+2πi1​PV∫Γ​ζ−tf(ζ)​dζ.​
(24)
In particular the jump is w+​−w−​=f, while the sum is the principal-value integral divided by πi. Smooth densities on a real interval satisfy the same formulas at every interior point, by the identical subtraction step with a Hölder-continuous remainder.
Now put h=w/w. On the interval, the sign reversal of the auxiliary boundary values gives
h+​−h−​=w+​w+​​−w−​w−​​=w+​w+​+w−​​=w+​G​.

Thus the required Cauchy transform density is
F(ξ)=w+​(ξ)G(ξ)​.​
(26)
Whenever this density is integrable at the endpoints, taking h=C[F] constructs a function with the required jump. Multiplication back gives w+​+w−​=G. The jump of the constructed w is

To conclude that this jump solves the final singular equation, one must additionally justify w=C[f]. This reconstruction requires endpoint and infinity conditions; the sign reversal of w alone does not ensure it. Here is a complete valid normalization. Let
S(z)=z(z−1)​,S(z)∼z at infinity,w(z)=S(z)1​.
(28)
The branch is holomorphic off [0,1], with S+​(x)=ix(1−x)​ and S−​=−S+​. If G extends smoothly to [0,1], the density F=G/w+​=ix(1−x)​G vanishes like a square root at each endpoint. Its Cauchy transform h is bounded at the endpoints and is O(z−1) at infinity. Therefore w=h/S is at most inverse-square-root singular at each endpoint and is O(z−2) at infinity. Its jump f is integrable.
For Cauchy reconstruction from a jump across a finite interval, apply the Cauchy integral formula to w on a large slit contour, and shrink its banks to [0,1]. The large circle contributes zero in the limit; each small endpoint circle contributes O(ε​) and also vanishes. The two banks give the jump f, proving w=C[f]. The sum part of the Sokhotski–Plemelj formula now gives
πi1​PV∫01​ξ−xf(ξ)​dξ=G(x),0<x<1.​
(29)
This proves the intended finite Hilbert transform inversion with admissible endpoint behavior. Adding a multiple of 1/x(1−x)​ to f gives a homogeneous solution; equivalently one can add a constant multiple of 1/S to w, whose boundary sum is zero.
The omitted normalization is a real limitation of the printed claim. To see it explicitly, choose instead w=S and G=1. This auxiliary function satisfies all the stated holomorphicity, nonvanishing and sign-reversal requirements. But
∫01​ξ(1−ξ)​(ξ−z)dξ​=−S(z)π​,h=C[1/S+​]=2S(z)1​,w=Sh=21​.
(30)
The displayed integral identity follows from ξ=(1+cosθ)/2 and its continuation from real z>1. Hence the proposed jump formula gives f=0, which does not solve the equation with G=1. The failure is the nonzero constant at infinity: w−C[f]=1/2. Thus the final implication is valid with suitable endpoint and decay conditions, but is false for an unrestricted auxiliary function as printed. The valid reciprocal-square-root normalization above supplies an actual solution for G smooth up to the two endpoints.
