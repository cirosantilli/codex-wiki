Monosaccharide

 Home Chemistry Chemical substance Carbohydrate Sugar
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 6
A monosaccharide is a single-unit carbohydrate that cannot be split into smaller sugars by hydrolysis. Ribose, deoxyribose, and glucose are examples. The unit is a whole molecule containing many chemical atoms; a disaccharide instead contains two linked monosaccharide units.[ref]
Classification combines carbon count with open-chain connectivity. An aldose has an aldehyde group; a ketose has a ketone group. Thus glucose is an aldohexose, while fructose is a six-carbon ketose. These names describe the open-chain reference forms even when cyclic forms predominate in water.[ref]
Check the distinction by counting the groups in the drawings. Open-chain glucose contains one CHO, four CHOH and one CH2​OH group. Open-chain fructose contains two CH2​OH, three CHOH and one C=O group. The respective hydrogen totals are 1+4(2)+3=12 and 2(3)+3(2)=12; each structure also has six carbon and six oxygen chemical atoms. Their shared molecular formula C6​H12​O6​ therefore cannot identify the carbonyl group position or the stereochemistry.
The beauty is that a familiar sugar name can embrace an interconverting family of forms. Monosaccharide ring-chain interconversion connects those drawings to mutarotation, a change visible with polarimetry. Spatial arrangement also matters to biology: an enzyme's active site encounters a particular arrangement of hydroxy groups, rather than merely an inventory of chemical atoms. The existing enzyme and chirality articles explain that recognition principle.
