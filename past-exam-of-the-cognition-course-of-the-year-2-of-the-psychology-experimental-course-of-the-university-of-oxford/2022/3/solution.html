Broca's area contributes to sentence processing, but the evidence does not justify identifying it with a single necessary-and-sufficient syntax processor. “Syntax” includes representing hierarchical relations, combining words, maintaining unresolved dependencies and using structure in interpretation or production. A region can support one of these operations, or the memory and control demands accompanying it, without implementing all of them. The anatomical label itself usually refers to parts of the left inferior frontal gyrus, especially regions corresponding approximately to areas 44 and 45; it is not a functionally homogeneous unit.
The historical association is important but logically limited. Paul Broca's 1861 observations linked severe impairment of articulate speech with left frontal injury. Loss of fluent output is not a selective test of syntactic knowledge, and a large lesion cannot localize every component of its resulting syndrome to the surface region that is most visible. In 2007, Nina F. Dronkers and colleagues used high-resolution magnetic resonance imaging to re-examine the preserved brains from the historic cases and found damage extending substantially beyond the traditionally identified superficial region. This undermines a simple inference from a broad historical syndrome to a small isolated syntax center; it does not invalidate the importance of left frontal language mechanisms.[ref]
There is positive experimental evidence for frontal sensitivity to syntax. David Embick and colleagues contrasted visually presented sentences containing grammatical word-order errors with sentences containing spelling errors, asking participants to identify the number of errors. The grammatical condition elicited greater activity in Broca's area, with a larger grammatical–spelling difference there than in comparison language regions. Matching lexical material and requiring error detection in both conditions make this more informative than comparing difficult sentences with an unrelated baseline. It supports a contribution to syntactic processing beyond merely seeing words.[ref]
Nevertheless, grammatical violations require detection, reanalysis, conflict resolution and response selection. Matching overt task format does not necessarily equate these internal demands. A greater functional magnetic resonance imaging response therefore does not establish that the region stores grammar rules, is syntax-exclusive or is necessary for ordinary successful comprehension. Those stronger claims would require converging tests separating syntactic computation from accompanying processes. This is the problem of reverse inference: activation associated with a task component does not uniquely identify the process generating it.
Corianne Rogalsky and colleagues directly tested one alternative mechanism. Participants comprehended subject-relative and more demanding object-relative sentences while performing concurrent speech articulation, a manual control task, or no secondary task. Articulation selectively interfered behaviorally with the harder sentence condition. In imaging, concurrent articulation eliminated the complexity effect in pars opercularis, whereas the manual task did not; pars triangularis showed a different secondary-task profile. These results support an articulatory rehearsal contribution to maintaining information during demanding comprehension and show that the frontal anatomical region contains distinguishable operations.[ref]
This is evidence against treating every complexity-related frontal response as syntax-specific, not evidence that syntax is entirely reducible to rehearsal. Articulation may alter strategies and general task demands, and absence of a difference under dual-task conditions can have several causes. Conversely, a syntactic account must explain why an independently motivated memory manipulation changes the response. A satisfactory theory should predict when syntactic dependencies need rehearsal or control and when successful structure building proceeds without those extra demands.
Individual functional anatomy supplies another constraint. Evelina Fedorenko and colleagues identified language-selective and domain-general subregions side by side within the broad anatomical Broca's area. They then tested responses across linguistic and nonlinguistic tasks, including harder and easier memory and control conditions. The distinct profiles were detectable within individuals and did not simply align with the traditional 44/45 boundary. Group averaging across anatomically variable people can therefore merge adjacent populations and produce an apparent multifunctional center. Importantly, language selectivity does not equal syntax selectivity: a region responding to sentences may support lexical–semantic combinations as well as structure.[ref]
Lesion evidence challenges strict necessity claims. In their 2018 study, Corianne Rogalsky and colleagues tested patients using sentence–picture matching and plausibility judgments, including canonical and noncanonical constructions. Analyses implicated superior temporal and inferior-parietal regions in impaired comprehension; patients with frontal damage did not uniformly show the predicted agrammatic comprehension pattern. A traditional diagnostic label and damage to Broca's area cannot therefore be substituted for an independently measured syntactic deficit.[ref]
Yet preserved performance after a chronic lesion does not prove that the region never contributes in an intact brain: spared tissue, compensation, task sensitivity and unequal lesion coverage matter. Likewise, comprehension findings should not automatically settle the neural implementation of syntactic production, where planning and sequencing impose different demands. The strongest interpretation combines these limitations rather than choosing imaging or lesions as universally decisive.
Broca's area is a heterogeneous participant in a distributed language network. The evidence supports frontal contributions to syntactic and sentence-level processing, with some responses attributable to rehearsal and control and others belonging to language-selective tissue. It weighs against both an exclusive syntax organ and a claim of no language-specific frontal contribution. Progress requires explicit computational hypotheses, individual localization, matched comparison conditions and convergent perturbation or lesion evidence, while distinguishing representations from the processes that operate on them.
