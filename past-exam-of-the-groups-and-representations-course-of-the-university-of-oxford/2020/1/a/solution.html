A group representation on a vector space V over a field K is a group homomorphism R:G→GL(V). In particular, R(gh)=R(g)R(h), R(e)=I and R(g−1)=R(g)−1. Choosing a basis identifies it with a matrix representation.
Two group representations R on V and S on W are equivalent representations when an invertible linear map T:V→W satisfies TR(g)=S(g)T for every g. Thus S(g)=TR(g)T−1; they differ only by a choice of basis.
A nonzero group representation is a reducible representation if there is a vector subspace U with 0=U=V and R(g)U⊆U for every g. It is an irreducible representation if it has no such invariant subspace. Reducibility alone does not assert the existence of an invariant complementary vector subspace; that stronger conclusion says that the representation is a semisimple representation.
The Schur lemma states that an intertwiner T between irreducible representations is either zero or an isomorphism. Consequently an intertwiner between inequivalent irreducible representations is zero. For a finite-dimensional irreducible representation over the complex numbers, every commuting endomorphism is scalar:
TR(g)=R(g)T(∀g)⟹T=λI.​
(1)
For the first assertion, the kernel and image of T are invariant subspaces, so a nonzero T is injective and surjective. For the scalar conclusion, choose an eigenvalue λ of T. The intertwiner T−λI has a nonzero kernel, so it must vanish. The finite-dimensional complex hypothesis matters: the scalar conclusion need not hold over the real numbers, as a real planar rotation through π/2 is an irreducible representation of the cyclic group of order four, while the rotation itself is a commuting nonscalar endomorphism.
