A subgroup H of a group G is a nonempty subset closed under the inherited multiplication and inverses; equivalently, xy−1∈H whenever x,y∈H. A normal subgroup satisfies gHg−1=H for every g∈G.
A group representation on a vector space V is a group homomorphism R:G→GL(V), so R(gh)=R(g)R(h) and R(e)=IV​. An irreducible representation has V=0 and no invariant subspaces other than 0 and V: there is no proper nonzero W with R(g)W⊆W for every g∈G. We use finite-dimensional complex representations, with continuity for Lie groups, throughout.

Branching of a representation describes the irreducible representation content of a restriction of a representation:
R​H​≅⨁λ​mλ​Sλ​.
(1)
This direct decomposition applies when the restriction of a representation is a semisimple representation, as for finite groups or continuous finite-dimensional representations of compact groups. For an arbitrary group and subgroup, complete reducibility need not hold. A vector subspace preserved by every R(h) for h∈H need not be preserved by the additional R(g) for g∈/H, so an irreducible representation can become reducible.
For a concrete example, take the defining representation of G=SU(2) on C2 and H={diag(z,z−1):∣z∣=1}, a subgroup isomorphic to the circle group. The defining representation is irreducible: SU(2) acts transitively on unit vectors, so no complex line is preserved by all its elements. Under H, the two coordinate lines are invariant subspaces, and
2​U(1)​=1+1​⊕1−1​.​
(2)
Here the two one-dimensional representations act by z and z−1.

Let ζ=e2πi/n. The group homomorphism Zn​→U(1) given by [k]↦ζk is injective, identifying the finite cyclic group with the subgroup of nth roots of unity in the circle group.
The continuous irreducible representations of the circle group are the one-dimensional representations RQ​(eiθ)=eiQθ with Q∈Z. One way to see this is to make the representation unitary by averaging, simultaneously diagonalize its commuting unitary matrices, and impose RQ​(ei(θ+2π))=RQ​(eiθ). Its restriction of a representation is
RQ​(ζk)=e2πiQk/n=e2πiqk/n,q=Q(modn).​
(3)
Thus each irreducible representation remains one-dimensional, and representations whose integer labels differ by a multiple of n become equivalent. Every irreducible representation of the finite cyclic group occurs this way.

Choose a normalization in which the circle group has period 2π and the U(1) charges Q​i​ of the complex scalar fields are integers. By restriction of a representation,
q​i​=Q​i​(modn).​
(4)
Write vi​=⟨ϕi​⟩. A vacuum expectation value vi​=0 is preserved by eiθ precisely when eiQ​i​θ=1. Therefore the specified finite cyclic group survives spontaneous symmetry breaking exactly when every condensing complex scalar field obeys
Q​i​≡0(modn).
(5)
Only U(1) charges of fields with nonzero vacuum expectation values enter this test; in the paper all N fields condense.
Suppose at least one condensing U(1) charge is nonzero and put d=gcd(∣Q​i​∣:vi​=0). Since each such U(1) charge is a multiple of d, every dth root of unity preserves the vacuum expectation values. Conversely, the Bezout identity expresses d as an integer combination of these U(1) charges, so a common stabilizer obeys eidθ=1. The discrete remnant of a circle symmetry is consequently
Hunbroken​=Zd​,nmax​=d.​
(6)
A nontrivial retained finite cyclic group exists exactly when d>1, and its possible orders are the divisors n>1 of d. For example, condensing U(1) charges 6 and 10 leave Z2​. If d=1, the stabilizer is trivial. If all condensing U(1) charges vanish, the entire circle group is unbroken and there is no largest finite n; this case does not break the symmetry assumed in the question.

Normalize the circle group as in solution and count all Weyl fermions as left-handed; a right-handed Weyl fermion is replaced by its left-handed charge conjugate. The restriction of a representation gives
qa​=Qa​(modn).​
(7)
The triangle coefficient of the cubic U(1) gauge anomaly is proportional to ∑a​Qa3​. In a theory with this single gauge group, cancellation of its local pure gauge anomaly requires
a=1∑M​Qa3​=0.​
(8)
If absence of the mixed gauge-gravitational anomaly is also required, as when coupling to a general gravitational background, one additionally needs ∑a​Qa​=0. Additional gauge group factors would require their mixed gauge anomalies to cancel as well.
Choose integer representatives and write Qa​=qa​+nka​. Expanding the cubic gives
0=∑a​Qa3​=∑a​qa3​+n∑a​(3qa2​ka​+3nqa​ka2​+n2ka3​).
(9)

a∑​qa3​≡0(modn),a∑​qa​≡0(modn)(if mixed gravitational cancellation is imposed).​
(10)
These statements concern the same complete fermion spectrum used in the parent theory. They are consequences of an anomaly-free integer lift, rather than a full classification of intrinsic discrete gauge anomalies or a sufficient test for such a lift. They also do not imply an ordinary equality for the chosen residues: for n=3, the anomaly-free U(1) charges (1,−1) have residues (1,2), whose cubes sum to 9, rather than zero. Integrating out heavy fermions can require extra contributions to the effective discrete gauge anomaly accounting.

Schur's lemma states that an intertwiner T:V→W between irreducible representations is either zero or an isomorphism. In particular, inequivalent irreducible representations have no nonzero intertwiners. For a finite-dimensional irreducible representation over the complex numbers, every commuting endomorphism is scalar:
TR(g)=R(g)T for all g⟹T=λI.​
(23)
Indeed, the kernel and image of an intertwiner are invariant subspaces, proving the first statement. For the second, choose an eigenvalue λ of T. The intertwiner T−λI has nonzero kernel, so it must vanish. The scalar conclusion uses an algebraically closed coefficient field: over the real numbers, planar rotation through π/2 gives an irreducible real representation of Z4​ and commutes with itself without being a real scalar matrix.

Its neutrality permits a Majorana mass term without violating the remaining Standard Model gauge group.
