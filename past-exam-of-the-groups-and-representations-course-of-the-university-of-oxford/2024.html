A group representation on a vector space V is a group homomorphism R:G→GL(V): R(gh)=R(g)R(h) and R(e)=I. A reducible representation has a nonzero proper invariant subspace W⊂V, meaning R(g)W⊆W for every g. Over the complex numbers, a finite-dimensional representation of a finite group is a direct sum of irreducible representations, by Maschke's theorem.
The character of a representation is χR​(g)=trR(g). The cyclic property of the trace makes it a class function:
χR​(hgh−1)=tr(R(h)R(g)R(h)−1)=χR​(g).
(1)
Thus a character is specified by one number per conjugacy class, much less information than all the representation matrices. Moreover,
χR⊕S​=χR​+χS​,χR⊗S​=χR​χS​,mr​=∣G∣1​g∈G∑​χr​(g)​χR​(g).​
(2)
Here character orthogonality says that mr​ is the multiplicity of the irreducible representation r. Consequently characters determine finite-dimensional complex representations up to equivalence of group representations and compute tensor product of group representations decompositions without constructing a new basis explicitly.

The scalar fields commute, so a degree-d expression in one doublet belongs to a symmetric power, rather than an arbitrary tensor product. Put x=ϕ1​, y=ϕ2​. The generators of a group r=τ1​τ2​ and s=τ3​ act by
r:(x,y)↦(αx,α−1y),s:(x,y)↦(y,x).
(12)
A monomial xayb is fixed by r precisely when a−b is divisible by three. Imposing s then pairs the exponents. Through degree four the resulting holomorphic polynomial invariants are
degree01234​basis1nonexyx3+y3x2y2​​
(13)
In particular, two explicit invariant terms are ϕ1​ϕ2​ and ϕ13​+ϕ23​. Their invariance follows directly from α3=1 and the interchange of the two components. A quartic example is (ϕ1​ϕ2​)2.
For a physical real scalar potential, complex conjugate fields are allowed as well. The Hermitian inner product ϕ†ϕ and its square are also invariant, while a holomorphic term can enter with its complex conjugate, for example b(ϕ13​+ϕ23​)+bˉ(ϕˉ​13​+ϕˉ​23​). There is still no invariant linear term, because neither the doublet nor its conjugate representation contains a trivial representation. Thus the possible nonconstant total degrees are
S3​:2,3,4.​
(14)
The constant term is unrestricted.
For the fundamental representation of the special unitary group SU(2), its central element −I sends ϕ↦−ϕ and ϕˉ​↦−ϕˉ​. Every odd-degree polynomial changes sign, excluding degrees one and three. Further, SU(2) acts transitively on the unit sphere in C2: a unit vector (u,v)T is the first column of
(uv​−vˉuˉ​)∈SU(2).
(15)

V=V0​+m2ϕ†ϕ+λ(ϕ†ϕ)2,V0​,m2,λ∈R.​
(16)

If the notation V(ϕ) is instead read as restricting to holomorphic polynomials alone, the distinction matters: a single commuting SU(2) doublet has no nonconstant holomorphic polynomial invariant. The candidate contraction ϵab​ϕa​ϕb​=ϕ1​ϕ2​−ϕ2​ϕ1​ vanishes identically. The quadratic and quartic SU(2) terms above require complex conjugate fields. This also explains why counting a trivial representation in 2⊗2 alone would give an incorrect holomorphic answer.

Use the mathematical Lie algebra convention U(t)=I+tX+O(t2). Differentiating U†U=I and detU=1 at t=0 gives X†=−X and trX=0. Conversely, for such an X, the matrix exponential is unitary and has determinant detetX=ettrX=1. Thus
su(4)={X∈M4​(C):X†=−X, trX=0},[X,Y]=XY−YX.​
(17)
The four diagonal entries are purely imaginary, and the six entries above the diagonal are arbitrary complex numbers determining the entries below it. This gives 4+2⋅6=16 real parameters before imposing the one real trace constraint, so dimR​su(4)=15.
A Cartan subalgebra of this compact Lie algebra is a maximal commuting Lie subalgebra; choose
h={idiag(h1​,h2​,h3​,h4​):hi​∈R, ∑i​hi​=0}.
(18)
A simple basis is iH1​,iH2​,iH3​, where
H1​=diag(1,−1,0,0),H2​=diag(0,1,−1,0),H3​=diag(0,0,1,−1).
(19)
Any matrix commuting with every diagonal element is itself diagonal, which proves maximality. Commuting skew-Hermitian matrices can also be simultaneously diagonalized, so no larger commuting Lie subalgebra is possible. Consequently
dimsu(4)=15,ranksu(4)=dimh=3.​
(20)
The Hermitian quantum generator convention instead uses the Hermitian Ha​ and U=exp(iθaHa​). The two conventions describe the same Lie algebra.

A meson has quark model flavour content qi​qˉ​j, so four quark flavours give sixteen flavour states in
4⊗4ˉ=15⊕1.​
(31)
This counts a flavour set for a specified spin and orbital assignment; it does not count every excited meson. Three quark flavours instead give the familiar nine states 3⊗3ˉ=8⊕1.
Taking the fourth quark to be the charm quark, the first nine combinations qα​qˉ​β, with α,β∈{u,d,s}, are the light sector. The extra seven are qα​cˉ in 3, cqˉ​α in 3ˉ, and ccˉ in 1 under light-flavour SU(3). For the lowest pseudoscalar mesons, the open-charm states are the three D meson/Ds meson states and their antiparticles, and the hidden-charm state is the eta c meson. The two neutral trivial representation states can mix when the flavour symmetry is broken.
The intended limitation is the accuracy of flavour symmetry: the charm quark is much heavier than the three light quarks, so a quark mass term is far from SU(4) invariant. Exact flavour SU(4) would relate light and charmed states with the same spin, contrary to their large observed mass splittings. However, saying that a four-flavour quark model is never considered would be too strong: flavour SU(4) is used as classification bookkeeping, with much stronger symmetry breaking than light-flavour SU(3).[ref] This is a flavour special unitary group, distinct from the three-colour gauge group of Quantum chromodynamics.

Merely containing one Standard Model family does not guarantee cancellation of the cubic SU(6) gauge anomaly. In the convention A(6)=1, one has A(6ˉ)=−1 and A(15)=2, so the displayed sum has A(R)=1. With these types of representations, the minimal anomaly-free completion is instead

Beyond one family this adds a vectorlike gauge spectrum 5⊕5ˉ and two trivial representations.
