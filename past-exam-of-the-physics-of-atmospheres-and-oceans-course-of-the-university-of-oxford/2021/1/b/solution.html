A convenient parameter is the water vapor mole fraction itself. The mass conservation calculation yields
P(x)=Pd​+1+axϵΔpx​,E(x)=xP(x).
(7)
Both functions increase for 0≤x≤1. At the endpoint,
P(1)=E(1)=Pd​+Δp.
(8)
Therefore the physically admissible hydrogen–water vapor mixture requires
E(Ts​)<Pd​+Δp.​
(9)
Beyond this bound, the formal positive quadratic equation root requires x>1, equivalently a negative hydrogen partial pressure. It is the physical constraints, rather than loss of real roots, that end the solution branch. For a concrete example, put Pd​=Δp=1 in consistent pressure units, ϵ=9, and E=3. The positive root of P2+23P−51=0 is about 2.037, giving x≃1.473, which is impossible.
The limiting temperature is obtained from E(T∗​)=Pd​+Δp:
T∗​1​=Tt​1​−Lv​Rv​​lnEt​Pd​+Δp​.
(10)
This finite limit exists above Tt​ if Et​<Pd​+Δp<Et​exp[Lv​/(Rv​Tt​)]. The upper qualification matters mathematically: the stipulated constant-latent heat formula has a finite limiting saturation pressure as T→∞, so for an arbitrarily large unspecified Pd​+Δp the printed assertion of a finite breakdown temperature is not unconditional. For a concrete formal counterexample, choose Pd​+Δp=1015Pa: with the usual triple-point constants the model has E(∞)≃2.5×1011Pa, so it never reaches the proposed inventory limit. This is a counterexample inside the stipulated equations, not a physically credible high-pressure ideal gas ocean. Actual liquid water also ceases to exist above its critical point, before such an extrapolation would make sense.
Physically, the imposed finite-pressure-thickness layer can hold no more than Δp/g of water vapor per unit area. As x→1, it expels all its hydrogen into the dry upper layer. A still larger surface saturation pressure cannot coexist with this geometry. The layer must deepen, the temperature profile must change, or the assumed phase equilibrium must fail. If Pd​<Δp, the additional restriction P>Δp can also exclude the low-temperature end of the branch.
