Interpret the stated detector width d=20μm as one square spatial sample. At the point directly below the spacecraft, the small-angle approximation gives pixel angular width d/f≃Δx/h. Thus
f=Δxdh​=500(20×10−6)(1.70087×107)​m≃0.680m.​
(41)
There is a definition error in the source: the usual focal ratio, or f-number, divides focal length by the entrance-aperture diameter D, whereas the printed parenthesis divides by detector width d. Reporting both avoids silently changing the question:
(df​)printed​≃3.40×104,(Df​)conventional​≃0.500.680​=1.36.​
(42)
The actual optical design is therefore approximately f/1.36, a fast optical telescope, not an optically slow f/34000 instrument. Such a focal ratio is demanding but possible in principle: optical aberrations, obscuration, field coverage, stray light and alignment need careful design. The single-sample angular width is 2.94×10−5rad. Spatial sampling alone does not guarantee true resolving power; diffraction, pointing and signal-to-noise ratio also matter. At large viewing angles, the same angular pixel covers a larger, distorted surface footprint. If the detector is literally one element, scanning is additionally needed to form a map.
