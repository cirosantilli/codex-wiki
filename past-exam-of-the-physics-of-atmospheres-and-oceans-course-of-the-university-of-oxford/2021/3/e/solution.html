A rising parcel cools, lowering the saturation pressure. Once its water vapor supply exceeds that value, atmospheric supersaturation develops. Homogeneous nucleation of pure water is strongly inhibited at the small atmospheric supersaturations typical of clouds; existing aerosol particles provide cloud condensation nuclei. Their dry sizes and composition, together with the parcel's ascent rate, determine how many cloud droplets activate.
Köhler theory combines the Kelvin equation, which raises equilibrium vapor pressure over a curved surface, with Raoult's law, which lowers it for a chemical solution. In a dilute diameter convention, with dry diameter Dd​ and wet diameter D,

The maximum of the Köhler curve is the critical supersaturation. Larger or more hygroscopic particles generally have lower thresholds. Cloud-droplet activation requires overcoming that maximum; total aerosol number is not the activated number.

The value smax​ must be calculated, not guessed from the aerosol distribution alone. Prescribe parcel upward velocity w, initial pressure, temperature and humidity, then integrate expansion cooling, latent heat release, conservation of total water, and diffusional cloud droplet growth. where as​,bs​>0, G includes water vapor diffusion and thermal conduction, and ql​ is liquid mass fraction. Rising motion produces atmospheric supersaturation; growing particles consume it. Their competition selects the peak and which particles cross their activation barriers. Assume a well-mixed closed parcel, liquid water, soluble spherical particles, specified mixing state, and negligible collision-coalescence and fallout during activation. Slow kinetic growth, weakly soluble particles or entrainment require a more detailed treatment than the threshold integral.
