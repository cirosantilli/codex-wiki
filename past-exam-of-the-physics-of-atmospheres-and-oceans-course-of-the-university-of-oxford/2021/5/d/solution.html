For a nonzero zonal wavenumber k, stationarity requires
0=kω​=U−k2+l2β​,Ks2​=Uβ​.
(53)
A real propagating solution therefore requires a westerly U>0. For 0<∣k∣<Ks​ there are two meridional wavenumbers:
l±​=±Uβ​−k2​.​
(54)
The source's assertion of two stationary solutions needs these qualifications: at ∣k∣=Ks​ they coincide, and at ∣k∣>Ks​ there is no real meridionally propagating solution. For example k=2β/U​ gives l2=−3β/U. Modes with k=0 are a separate zonally invariant degeneracy and do not have a defined zonal phase velocity.
Adding U to the zonal group velocity and imposing stationarity gives
cg​=Ks2​2Uk​(k,l),dxdy​=cgx​cgy​​=kl​.​
(55)
Thus a localized midlatitude disturbance radiates stationary Rossby wave activity downstream, along one poleward and one equatorward branch. For k>0 in the Northern Hemisphere, l+​ gives the northeastward branch and l−​ the southeastward branch. With strictly constant U,β, these wave rays are straight. Changing latitude cannot bend them within that constant-coefficient model.
To discuss a planet, extend it locally to slowly varying β(ϕ)=2Ωcosϕ/ap​ and possibly U(ϕ). The conserved zonal wavenumber in a locally zonally uniform medium then has
l2(ϕ)=U(ϕ)β(ϕ)​−k2.
(56)
For constant U and northward propagation in the Northern Hemisphere, decreasing β reduces l until l=0: the wave ray becomes zonal at a turning latitude and can refract equatorward. The equatorward branch initially encounters larger allowed Ks​ and becomes more meridional. This local midlatitude description eventually needs replacement near the equator or wherever the background varies rapidly.
Orographic forcing by a mountain range or persistent spatially localized heating can supply such a disturbance. Its steady forcing selects the ground-stationary part of the response. The corresponding local total wavelength is
Λs​=Ks​2π​=2πβU​​.​
(57)
For fixed U it increases toward the pole as β decreases. Locally the zonal integer scale has upper bound nmax​≃ap​cosϕKs​=2Ωap​cos3ϕ/U​, so fewer planetary-scale zonal waves fit around higher-latitude circles. This is not an unconditional trend in actual weather: changing U, its curvature, stratification and the forcing spectrum can change the dominant Rossby wave wavelengths.
