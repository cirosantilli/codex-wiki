The etendue is the area–solid-angle acceptance of a ray bundle, more generally G=nmopt2​∫cosθdAdΩ. In an ideal lossless optical system it is conserved. Increasing pupil area increases accepted power only together with the appropriate field and detector cone; passive optics cannot arbitrarily increase radiance. For a small nadir pixel here, AΩ denotes entrance-pupil area times the solid angle of its surface footprint.
Choose 1.00–1.03μm within the surface-sensitive window. A representative band-mean total radiance read from the original blue curve is Lˉλ​≃4μWcm−2sr−1μm−1, uncertain at roughly the tens-of-per-cent level. Its band integral is
L≡∫Lλ​dλ≃4(0.03)×10−2=1.2×10−3Wm−2sr−1.

The provided SNR formula depends on LΔν only through this band integral. If ν is wavenumber, the selected band spans about 291cm−1 and Lν​dν=Lλ​∣dλ∣; numerical radiance densities in the two coordinates must not be mixed. The Rayleigh criterion independently requires only D≳1.22λ/(200/h)≃1.6mm at 1.015μm, so the noise requirement dominates diffraction. The 200μm detector size corresponds to focal length about 0.259m for this pixel field. This assumes one detector footprint per stated feature size; stricter spatial sampling would need smaller footprints and a revised photon budget.
The estimate assumes a clear, non-scattering atmosphere as in the graph, a night-side or otherwise isolated thermal signal, negligible extra stray-light and read-noise penalties beyond the supplied detectivity, and transmission 0.7 for the instrument alone. Atmospheric attenuation is already included in the plotted incoming radiance. The selected band has little atmospheric-only signal, but actual hot-spot detection concerns contrast relative to the background: SNR 50 on total radiance is not a guarantee for an arbitrarily small temperature anomaly. An illustrative radiance range 3–5 in the plotted units would give diameters about 0.20–0.15m.
