Optical response at fixed cloud structure. Put b=1−g and write the two-stream cloud albedo as A=bτ/(2+bτ). At fixed scattering asymmetry parameter,
dτdA​=(2+bτ)22b​,A(1−A)=(2+bτ)22bτ​,dτdA​=τA(1−A)​​.
(1)
At zero optical depth the last quotient is understood by its finite limiting value. Optical depth counts extinction along a ray. For an approximately homogeneous, monodisperse cloud,
τ=∫0h​Nσext​dz=NQext​πr2h≃2πNr2h.
(2)
This uses spherical droplets, independent extinction, representative constant N,r, and the geometric optics limit in which the droplet radius greatly exceeds the relevant visible wavelength and Qext​≃2. Diffraction supplies part of the extinction cross-section; it is not legitimate to obtain the factor two from geometric interception alone. A size distribution requires its second moment rather than an arbitrary mean radius.
With liquid-water density ρw​ and cloud liquid-water content L, the volume constraint gives r3=3L/(4πρw​N). Consequently
τ=2πh(4πρw​3L​)2/3N1/3,dlnNdlnτ​=31​+32​dlnNdlnL​+dlnNdlnh​.
(3)
This proves
dNdτ​=3Nτ​(1+2dlnNdlnL​+3dlnNdlnh​)​.
(4)
Keeping L,h,ρw​,g fixed isolates the Twomey effect:
dNdA​=3NA(1−A)​​.
(5)
It is a partial response, not a claim that cloud water and cloud geometry stay unchanged under every real aerosol perturbation.
Absolute and fractional susceptibility. Choose N∗​ so that A=1/2 there, and put n=N/N∗​. Then
A=1+n1/3n1/3​,N∗​dNdA​=3(1+n1/3)2n−2/3​.
(6)
The cloud albedo susceptibility per added droplet decreases monotonically. Its small-n asymptote is proportional to n−2/3, while its large-n asymptote is proportional to n−4/3. Thus a fixed absolute droplet-number addition has much more leverage in a clean cloud than in an already droplet-rich cloud. The formal divergence as N→0 is not a physical infinite response: fixed water content would require indefinitely large drops, eventually invalidating the optical and cloud assumptions.
For a fixed fractional number addition the response is instead dA/dlnN=A(1−A)/3, maximal at A=1/2 and small at either very low or nearly saturated albedo. This distinction is essential when comparing interventions specified by an absolute particle addition with those specified by a percentage increase.
Brightening and net radiation. Marine cloud brightening would increase the population of suitable cloud condensation nuclei, for example by producing sea-salt aerosol from seawater and allowing transport into susceptible marine boundary-layer clouds. Activation depends on particle size, hygroscopicity and the cloud's attained supersaturation; adding particles does not guarantee a one-for-one increase of droplets. At fixed cloud water, more activated droplets mean smaller radii, larger total extinction area, larger shortwave optical depth and more reflected sunlight.
Promising physical conditions combine relatively clean, persistent low clouds, appreciable incident sunlight and a dark underlying ocean. The extensive marine stratocumulus regions over the eastern subtropical oceans are examples of this combination. An already polluted, optically saturated cloud has little remaining shortwave leverage. Bright clouds over snow or ice also add relatively little contrast with the surface. Low clouds have temperatures closer to the surface than high clouds, so their extra longwave greenhouse effect is generally less able to offset the shortwave cooling. Sunlit seasons and latitudes matter; no shortwave benefit occurs at night.
Schematically, the fixed-cloud shortwave perturbation is δFSW​∼−Sfc​δA, where S is the local incident solar flux and fc​ the affected cloud fraction. Changes in cloud fraction, precipitation, entrainment, liquid water and longwave emission must also be evaluated before equating the positive derivative above with a guaranteed net cooling. The derivative identifies susceptibility, not a complete climate-response calculation.
