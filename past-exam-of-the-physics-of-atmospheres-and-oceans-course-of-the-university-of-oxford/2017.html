Use u as a spectroscopic wavenumber, in the same units throughout. Write the mass absorption coefficient of the absorbing species as
κν​=Sϕ(ν−ν0​),∫−∞∞​ϕ(δν)dδν=1,S=∫κν​dν.​
(1)
Here S is the spectral line strength, the integrated mass absorption coefficient; it is not the line-centre coefficient. If κν​ has units m2kg−1 and ν is in cm−1, then S has units m2kg−1cm−1. For absorber mass density ρa​, the volume coefficient in Beer-Lambert law is ρa​κν​.
Lifetime broadening. Finite lifetimes of the upper and lower states destroy optical coherence, even for an isolated molecule. Exponential coherence decay gives a Lorentzian line shape. For population decay rates Γu​,Γl​ and no extra dephasing, its wavenumber half-width is (Γu​+Γl​)/(4πc), with c expressed in the length unit reciprocal to ν. This sets a natural linewidth and can matter at extremely low collision rates; it is generally much smaller than atmospheric collisional or thermal widths.
Thermal Doppler broadening. A Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution of molecular line-of-sight velocities gives
ϕD​(δν)=π​ΔD​e−(δν/ΔD​)2​,ΔD​=cν0​​ma​2kB​T​​.
(2)
This Gaussian distribution has a 1/e half-width ΔD​, determined by absorber molecular mass ma​ and temperature. It is especially relevant in the rarefied upper atmosphere, where collisions are infrequent.
Pressure broadening. Collisions perturb transition frequencies and phases. In the impact regime, exponential phase decorrelation gives
ϕL​(δν)=π1​δν2+ΔL2​ΔL​​.
(3)
Here ΔL​ is the half-width at half maximum, proportional to the coherence-destroying collision rate. It depends on perturber density, molecular collision properties, and temperature; it is often parameterized by ΔL​∝p(T∗​/T)nT​ for a specified broadener. It dominates much of the dense lower atmosphere. The Lorentzian line shape has algebraic δν−2 wings, whereas the Doppler profile has rapidly decreasing Gaussian wings. Where both mechanisms matter, their convolution is a Voigt profile. A pure pressure-broadened profile should not be extrapolated to arbitrarily low pressure while discarding the finite Doppler core.

Let m be the absorber mass path along a ray. If the spectral line profile is independent of position, Beer-Lambert law gives Tν​(m)=e−mκν​. A finite-band mean and the line's equivalent width are respectively
TB​(m)=ΔνB​1​∫B​e−mSϕ(ν)dν,E(m)=∫−∞∞​[1−e−mSϕ(ν)],dν.​
(4)
When the band contains all significant absorption, ∫B​Tν​dν≃ΔνB​−E. An unqualified integral of transmission over the entire infinite line domain diverges because transmission tends to one far from line centre; E is the finite and physically useful line deficit.
This is nonexponential band transmission: each monochromatic transmission is exponential, but their average generally is not. Differentiating with respect to mass path gives
T′=−⟨κe−mκ⟩B​<0,T′′=⟨κ2e−mκ⟩B​>0.
(5)
More decisively, define the absorption coefficient averaged over the transmitted spectrum by
aeff​(m)=−dmdlogT​=⟨e−mκ⟩⟨κe−mκ⟩​.
(6)
Then aeff′​(m)=−Varm​(κ)≤0​. The core saturates first and the surviving radiation increasingly samples weakly absorbing wings. The effective attenuation per additional mass therefore decreases. Jensen's inequality also gives T≥e−m⟨κ⟩, with equality for a spectrally constant coefficient. Thus the band transmission decays more slowly than the exponential with its initial mean attenuation.
The corresponding curve of growth has E(m)≃mS for an optically weak line, and sublinear growth after saturation. For the Gaussian profile, with τ0​=mS/(π​ΔD​)≫1, absorption extends to ∣δν∣≃ΔD​logτ0​​, so E∼2ΔD​logτ0​​. For a strongly saturated pure Lorentz profile the wings instead give E∼2mSΔL​​. These asymptotes apply while the line remains isolated and its important wings remain within the range where the assumed profile and nearly constant Planck function are appropriate. The integrated absorption is not an exponential either: E′>0 and E′′≤0.

Take a weak line in the radiative sense: the integrated slant optical depth is small wherever absorption materially contributes, not merely a small greenhouse-gas mass mixing ratio. Normalize every local spectral line profile so that ∫κν​(p)dν=S, with the line strength independent of pressure and, as stipulated, temperature. Keep a finite Doppler or natural core, or a Voigt profile, so that a pressure-only zero-width core at the top does not invalidate a uniform optical-depth expansion. The line must also be narrow enough to use Bν0​​ across it.
To first order,
E(p,q)≃gμd​q​∫0p​∫κν​(p′)dνdp′=gμd​qSp​.
(14)
Thus the detailed normalized line shape drops out, even if its width varies with pressure. Substituting in the deficit in c gives
σSB​Ts4​≃F∗​+gμd​πqSps​​[Bν0​​(Ts​)−∫01​Bν0​​(Ts​rκ)dr].​
(15)
The neglected term is second order when the relevant squared optical depths are integrable and uniformly small. A very narrow saturated line need not be weak simply because q≪1. More generally the displayed first-order integrated limit follows from the finite integrated opacity even if an idealized line centre makes a pointwise expansion nonuniform; a finite physical core is the straightforward sufficient assumption.
For an explicitly perturbative temperature estimate, evaluate the bracket at Tbare​:
Ts​−Tbare​≃4gμd​σSB​Tbare3​πqSps​​[Bν0​​(Tbare​)−∫01​Bν0​​(Tbare​rκ)dr]>0.​
(16)
At fixed ps​,g,q,S and radiative conditions, increasing κ=Rb​/cp,b​ makes every level 0<r<1 cooler relative to the surface, enlarging the bracket and the warming. Increasing cp,b​ at fixed Rb​ has the opposite effect. The limit κ→0 is isothermal and gives zero line-induced deficit. A change of background molecular mass alone need not change this leading result: for ideal gases with the same active degrees of freedom, Rb​ and cp,b​ scale together and their ratio is unchanged. Comparing only the height-coordinate lapse rate g/cp,b​ would miss that the absorber column is naturally a pressure-coordinate quantity.

Below cloud base, an initially unsaturated closed parcel expands as ambient pressure falls. In the absence of heat exchange its temperature falls approximately at the dry adiabatic lapse rate g/cp,d​. Its vapour mixing ratio is conserved, but its saturation mixing ratio decreases strongly as it cools. Hence relative humidity rises until the lifting condensation level is reached. The density normally decreases through expansion; the environmental profile determines the parcel's buoyancy and thus whether ascent can continue.
Near saturation, slight atmospheric supersaturation permits suitable cloud condensation nuclei to activate. Köhler theory describes the competition between curvature, which raises equilibrium vapour pressure, and dissolved solute, which lowers it. Cooling generates supersaturation, while condensation onto the new droplets consumes it. Their balance normally keeps in-cloud relative humidity close to, but not identically at, saturation. The activated droplet number depends on the aerosol population and the maximum supersaturation produced by the ascent.
Above cloud base, condensation lowers the vapour content and raises the liquid-water content. The latent heat released partly offsets expansion cooling, so the moist adiabat has a smaller lapse-rate magnitude than the dry one. In a dilute approximation,
Γm​≃cp,d​g​1+Lv2​qs​/(cp,d​Rv​T2)1+Lv​qs​/(Rd​T)​.
(17)
A closed adiabatic parcel conserves total water qt​=qv​+ql​ and approximately its moist entropy; retaining condensate makes this a reversible-cloud idealization, rather than a precipitating pseudoadiabat. In a shallow cloud, the saturation-mixing-ratio decrease and slow density variation yield nearly constant condensation per height, so liquid-water content is approximately linear above base. Once activation is complete, assuming nearly fixed droplet number per volume gives increasing droplet size as condensate accumulates. Entrainment, continued activation, collision-coalescence and atmospheric precipitation can all violate that simplified description; they are excluded from the stated retrieval model.

Put the rigid lid at z=0, the interface at z=−h(x,y,t) and ρ1​=ρ0​−Δρ. Assume hydrostatic vertical balance and a resting deep layer. Its pressure is p2​=C−ρ0​gz, with horizontally uniform C. Pressure continuity at the interface then fixes the upper-layer pressure:
p1​=C+ρ0​gh−ρ1​g(z+h)=C−ρ1​gz+Δρgh.
(27)
Hence its horizontal pressure gradient at fixed z is ∇h​p1​=Δρg∇h​h, independent of depth. Taking the horizontal momentum equation and a vertically uniform upper-layer velocity gives
DtDu​+fk×u+g′∇h​h=0,g′=gρ1​Δρ​≃gρ0​Δρ​.​
(28)
The exact density denominator for upper-layer acceleration is ρ1​; the familiar ρ0​ denominator is the small-density-contrast Boussinesq approximation. The restoring pressure is weaker than for a free surface because only the density difference contributes.
The horizontal shallow-layer equations use the vertical component of planetary rotation: f=2Ωsinφ. Thus the source's vector notation is interpreted as the projected vector fk after the traditional approximation. Taking the full magnitude 2Ω as a midlatitude Coriolis parameter would discard the latitude dependence. Together with layer-volume conservation, the model has ∂t​h+∇h​⋅(hu)=0.

Both descriptions are depth-averaged hydrostatic shallow-layer models. In a one-layer free-surface shallow water equations, a homogeneous layer overlies a rigid bottom and its upper surface moves. Surface elevation changes give a restoring acceleration based on full gravity g, with external-wave speed gH0​​. Barotropic coastal storm surges, long surface gravity waves or a basin seiche are examples.
In the rigid-lid reduced gravity model, the movable boundary is a density interface beneath the active upper layer, while the much deeper lower layer remains at rest. The internal interface mode instead has speed g′h0​​ and represents, for example, thermocline displacements and an upper-ocean baroclinic eddy. The mathematical shallow-water form is retained with g replaced by g′ and with the appropriate interface-depth convention. A freely moving two-layer surface would support both external and internal modes; the rigid lid filters out the fast external mode, and the resting abyss removes its independent dynamics.

Let K=(k2+l2)1/2 and c=g′h0​​. The three branches are
ω=0,ω±​=±f2+c2K2​.​
(32)

For a unit mass of ideal gas, the first law of thermodynamics in enthalpy form is δQ=cp​dT−vdp. A reversible adiabatic displacement has δQ=0, and the ideal gas law gives v=RT/p. Thus
TdT​=cp​R​pdp​,T(p)=T0​(p/p0​)γ,γ=R/cp​.​
(35)
The atmospheric profile approaches this dry adiabat when convection efficiently mixes potential temperature and there is no condensation or other strong diabatic source. The first law by itself determines a parcel path, not every possible environmental temperature profile; the convective mixing assumption is needed. Hydrostatic balance gives the corresponding height lapse rate dT/dz=−g/cp​.
For rigid linear molecules with translation and rotation active but vibration frozen, the molecular heat capacities are CV​=(5/2)Ru​ and CP​=(7/2)Ru​, hence R/cp​=2/7. Earth is dominated by the diatomic gases nitrogen and oxygen. Mars is dominated by carbon dioxide, which is triatomic but linear and has the same two rotational degrees of freedom. In the rigid-molecule approximation it therefore has the same ratio. The value is not exact for carbon dioxide when its bending vibrations contribute appreciably to heat capacity, and variable heat capacities give a nonconstant dry-adiabatic exponent. Here γ denotes R/cp​, not the distinct adiabatic index cp​/cv​.

Assume a plane-parallel atmosphere in local thermodynamic equilibrium, negligible infrared scattering, a black surface, and surface temperature equal to the adjacent air temperature. For a specified ray, let T be its transmittance from a level to space, increasing from T0​ at the surface to one at the top. The formal radiative transfer solution is
L=B0​T0​+∫T0​1​BdT.
(36)
Using T0​+∫T0​1​dT=1 yields
L=B0​+∫T0​1​(B−B0​)dT.​
(37)
This expresses absorption and re-emission together: opacity does not simply remove surface radiation without adding atmospheric radiation. An absorber close to a thermally coupled surface has nearly the same Planck function as that surface, so B−B0​ is small. Its emission nearly compensates the surface radiation it absorbs, giving weak radiance sensitivity to near-surface concentration. In an exactly isothermal atmosphere at surface temperature, L=B0​ for every optical depth, a concrete example of complete concentration ambiguity in this thermal measurement. Finite surface emissivity, reflection, scattering or a surface-air temperature discontinuity would require additional terms.

Weather-forecast errors arise from uncertainty in the initial atmospheric state, including measurement noise, incomplete spatial coverage and imperfect data assimilation; from model error, including unresolved clouds, convection and turbulence, approximate physical parameterizations and imperfect boundary forcing; and from discretization, time stepping and roundoff. Sensitive dependence on initial conditions amplifies suitable small errors through nonlinear dynamics, limiting deterministic predictability even with a good model. It is an amplification mechanism rather than a separate observational noise source. Ensemble forecasts sample some initial-state and model uncertainty, but their spread need not represent every systematic error.
