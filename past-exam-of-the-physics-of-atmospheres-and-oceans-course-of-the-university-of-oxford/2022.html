For the variable-latent-heat saturation curve of a pure saturated water atmosphere, the total pressure equals the saturation pressure. The exact Clausius-Clapeyron relation along the phase coexistence curve is
dTdp​=T(vv​−vc​)L(T)​,
(1)
where vv​,vc​ are the specific volumes of the vapor and condensed thermodynamic phase. Neglecting vc​ and using the ideal gas law, vv​=Rwv​T/p, gives
dTdlnp​=Rwv​T2L(T)​,lnp∗​p​=∫T∗​T​Rwv​θ2L(θ)​dθ.
(2)
The triple point provides the integration constant. Below it, the prescribed latent heat is constant, so
lnp∗​p​=Rwv​Ls​​(T∗​1​−T1​),T≤T∗​.​
(3)
Above it, write K=L∗​/[Rwv​(Tc​−T∗​)]. Integrating K(Tc​/T2−1/T) gives
lnp∗​p​=K[Tc​(T∗​1​−T1​)−lnT∗​T​],T∗​≤T<Tc​.​
(4)
These are the requested implicit temperature profiles T(p). Both branches meet at (T∗​,p∗​), and each is monotone before the limiting liquid-gas critical point. The lower branch describes equilibrium with ice, rather than a stable liquid surface. A stable liquid ocean surface belongs to the upper branch; the colder upper atmosphere may use the lower branch. Hydrostatic balance is needed to convert this relation into a height profile, but not to obtain T(p).

No. Near the liquid-gas critical point, the mass densities of liquid and vapor approach each other, so the neglected condensed-phase specific volume becomes comparable with the vapor specific volume. The vapor also ceases to obey the dilute ideal gas law. Seq​(r)=eS​(∞)eSsol​(r)​=(1−r3B​)exp(rA​).

Let 1<S<Sc​ be the ambient saturation ratio. There are two equilibrium radii: r−​<rc​<r+​. Condensation occurs if S>Seq​(r); evaporation occurs if S<Seq​(r). Therefore droplets with r<r−​ grow towards r−​, while those with r−​<r<rc​ shrink towards r−​. The smaller equilibrium is stable.
Above rc​, droplets with rc​<r<r+​ still undergo evaporation and ultimately approach r−​. Droplets with r>r+​ grow, and the larger equilibrium r+​ is unstable. Merely exceeding rc​ is therefore not sufficient for continued growth at subcritical ambient atmospheric supersaturation. For example, if (S−1)/(Sc​−1)=0.6, the two intersections have approximately r−​/rc​=0.676 and r+​/rc​=2.349; a droplet of radius 1.5rc​ undergoes evaporation. If instead S>Sc​, there is no equilibrium barrier in this approximation and droplets activate into growing cloud droplets.
Köhler theory explains activation of cloud condensation nuclei, rather than rapid production of atmospheric precipitation. Diffusion-controlled droplet growth is limited by diffusion of water vapor and removal of released latent heat; approximately d(r2)/dt is proportional to the ambient atmospheric supersaturation. It slows in radius and tends to narrow a droplet size distribution. The available water vapor, changing temperature and shared depletion of atmospheric supersaturation also invalidate unlimited growth at prescribed S. Formation of rain-sized drops normally requires collision-coalescence, while colder clouds can additionally use cloud ice nucleation versus ice growth.

A Michelson interferometer uses a beam splitter to divide collimated input light between a fixed mirror and a moving mirror. Returning beams recombine at the beam splitter; one output reaches a photodetector. A compensator plate can equalize the glass traversed in the two arms. Moving one mirror by x changes the optical path difference by 2x.
The usual nominal Fourier transform spectroscopy specification is δν≃1/dmax​, where dmax​ is the maximum optical path difference measured from equal arms. It gives
dmax​≃0.5cm−11​=2cm,xmax​≃1cm.​
(30)
This is a nominal spectral resolution element convention.[ref] A symmetric rectangular scan from −dmax​ to dmax​ instead has a sinc instrumental line shape with first-zero offset 1/(2dmax​). Quoting that first-zero criterion would give dmax​=1 cm; interferogram apodization and other linewidth criteria change the numerical factor. An exact mathematical minimum requires such a criterion.
In the measurement chain, the Michelson interferometer is the spectral analyzer or modulator, between the collection optics and the photodetector. It encodes the incoming radiation spectrum into an interferogram; detector electronics digitize that signal and a Fourier transform reconstructs the radiation spectrum. It does not itself turn radiant power into an electrical signal.

The constants Ra​,Rb​,Rd​ are effective reservoir capacities: stored carbon dioxide mass anomaly per unit effective concentration anomaly, in GtCO2​ per ppmv. Only Ca​ is a literal atmospheric mole fraction; Cb​,Cd​ are scaled reservoir variables chosen to share its equilibrium value. The exchange coefficients r,s are conductances, in GtCO2​ per ppmv per year: r(Ca​−Cb​) is transfer from the atmosphere to the fast reservoir and s(Cb​−Cd​) transfer from that reservoir to the deep ocean. Use a shallow, homogeneous active upper layer of mean thickness h0​ and mass density ρ1​, over a much deeper, effectively stationary layer with mass density ρ2​>ρ1​. The Boussinesq approximation keeps the small density contrast only in buoyancy, giving reduced gravity g′=g(ρ2​−ρ1​)/ρ0​. The hydrostatic approximation and shallow-water approximation make horizontal velocity nearly depth independent and turn the interface slope into the horizontal force −g′∇h.
Linearize about rest and uniform h0​, with ∣h−h0​∣≪h0​, neglecting products of perturbations and convective acceleration. The f-plane approximation sets the Coriolis parameter to constant f0​. Neglect wind forcing, viscosity, entrainment and bottom drag. The two horizontal momentum equations then balance local acceleration, the Coriolis force and the pressure gradient. The signs −f0​v and +f0​u follow from eastward and northward coordinates.
The thickness equation is the linearized conservation of mass law: horizontal divergence decreases h, while horizontal convergence increases it. Here h is active-layer thickness, not total ocean depth, and g′ rather than g gives the restoring force because the motion principally displaces the thermocline.

Coastal Kelvin-wave radiation of long Rossby waves requires a common phase convention to match the two waves. The supplied Rossby wave formula conventionally multiplies ei(kR​x+ℓR​y−ωR​t), while the coastal expression uses ei(ωK​t−ℓK​y). Since physical fields are real, matching may use the complex-conjugate representative; in either convention the boundary forcing fixes the absolute frequency and alongshore wavenumber. Thus ∣ωR​∣=∣ωK​∣ and ∣ℓR​∣=∣ℓK​∣.
For kR2​+ℓR2​≪Ld−2​, the shallow-water Rossby-wave dispersion relation reduces to
ωR​≃−βLd2​kR​,∣ωK​∣≃β∣kR​∣Ld2​=f02​β∣kR​∣g′h0​​.​
(60)
Choosing kR​>0 recovers the printed magnitude relation. Both the zonal phase velocity and zonal group velocity are approximately −βLd2​, so the long Rossby waves carry the coastal signal westwards into the basin.
The coastal condition ∣ℓK​∣Ld​≪1 alone does not guarantee a long zonal wavelength. Since ∣ωK​∣=c∣ℓK​∣, matching requires ∣kR​∣=c∣ℓK​∣/(βLd2​) and hence the stronger consistency condition
∣ℓK​∣≪cβLd​​,equivalently∣ωK​∣≪βLd​.

Thus the source's proposed excitation is possible at sufficiently low frequency; it is not an automatic consequence of its meridional long-wave assumption.
At 10∘ N or S the beta plane parameter is
β=RE​2Ωcos10∘​=2.25×10−11m−1s−1,βLd2​≃0.225ms−1.
