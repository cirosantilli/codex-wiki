a OurBigBook.com
For an ideal gas of fixed molecular composition, translational energy is supplemented by rotational, vibrational and eventually electronic excitations as their level spacings become thermally accessible. Activating more internal degrees of freedom generally increases the heat capacity. In a harmonic vibrational mode the contribution rises from nearly zero when kB​T≪ℏω to its classical limit when kB​T≫ℏω; summing modes with different frequencies gives a smooth increase over an intermediate temperature range.
A monatomic gas has no molecular rotations or vibrations, so its mass-specific cp​ is nearly constant until electronic excitation or ionization becomes important: B is then small. A simple diatomic gas can also have a small slope when its rotations are already active but its vibrational modes remain frozen. Large flexible polyatomic molecules have many internal modes, often with relatively low excitation energies, and can give a larger slope over their activation range.
The linear law is a local approximation, not an asymptotic result. A finite set of modes approaches finite classical heat-capacity contributions, whereas A+BT grows without bound. At still higher temperatures dissociation and ionization change the composition and gas constant, invalidating the fixed-molecule ideal-gas model as well. The constants have units [A]=Jkg−1K−1 and [B]=Jkg−1K−2.

I+​ and I−​ are the positive upward and downward hemispherically integrated infrared fluxes, in Wm−2, rather than directional specific intensities. The source is the blackbody hemispheric flux σT4. Absorption removes a fraction proportional to the flux along a ray, and thermal emission replaces it according to Kirchhoff's law. Local thermodynamic equilibrium sets that emission source to the local Planck function.
A plane-parallel, non-scattering, gray atmosphere with a two-stream/angular closure reduces the angle- and frequency-dependent transfer equation to these two flux equations. The angular path factors are absorbed into the effective optical depth. Since the supplied coordinate increases upwards, the upward flux loses I+​dτ while gaining σT4dτ in moving upward; the downward ray traverses the coordinate in the opposite direction, reversing the differential signs in its equation. The equations therefore encode emission as well as absorption and are consistent with an isothermal source approaching thermal radiation in an optically thick medium. The source's use of “radiance” for these integrated quantities should not introduce an extra factor of π.

S=e/esl​ is the ambient saturation ratio over a flat liquid-water surface; S−1 is the liquid atmospheric supersaturation. Curvature increases the surface equilibrium vapour pressure, producing the Kelvin penalty A/r, while dissolved solute lowers it and gives the opposing Raoult contribution B/r3. Thus the dilute Köhler equilibrium curve is Seq​−1=A/r−B/r3, and the numerator is the atmospheric supersaturation available to drive growth beyond surface equilibrium.
Fdl​ describes the resistance of vapour diffusion through the surrounding air. Fkl​ describes the thermal-conduction resistance to removing latent heat released by condensation: a warmed droplet surface has a higher equilibrium vapour pressure and hence a weaker mass-transfer drive. Common continuum approximations are Fdl​≃Rv​T/(Dv​esl​) and Fkl​≃Lv​[kair​T]−1[Lv​/(Rv​T)−1], showing the roles of diffusivity, conductivity, saturation pressure and latent heat. Both resistances have the supplied units smkg−1 and add for this coupled transport problem.
For a spherical droplet of constant density, m=4πρr3/3. Multiplying the radius equation by 4πρr gives
dtdm​=Fkl​+Fdl​4πr​[(S−1)−rA​+r3B​].
(28)
At sufficiently large radius, curvature and solute corrections are negligible, so
dtdm​≃4πrFkl​+Fdl​S−1​.​
(29)
This limit also requires sustained positive atmospheric supersaturation. It need not describe the earliest submicrometre stage or an unactivated haze particle.

In the quasisteady diffusion approximation, the vapour-density disturbance outside a small stationary particle satisfies Laplace's equation, with a specified equilibrium value on its surface and a uniform far-field value. This has the same mathematical boundary-value problem as the electrostatic potential outside a conductor held at fixed potential. The integrated normal gradient is proportional to the conductor's geometrical capacitance.
Here C is a length: a sphere of radius r has C=r. The corresponding electrical capacitance in SI units would be 4πϵ0​C, not C farads. Replacing the spherical diffusive conductance 4πr by 4πC accounts for the crystal's non-spherical geometry. Coupling the analogous heat-conduction problem to vapour diffusion supplies the ice-specific thermal and diffusional resistances.
The approximation assumes continuum diffusion, equilibration of the surface vapour concentration, an approximately isothermal particle, a slowly changing shape, weak perturbations and a far field sufficiently distant relative to the crystal size. It neglects ventilation by falling motion, interactions with neighbouring particles and facet-dependent attachment kinetics. Shape affects diffusive growth through capacitance, not merely through projected collecting area as in a collision calculation.

In the northern hemisphere the coast is on the wave's right when looking in its direction of propagation. In the ocean-x<0 convention above it travels northward, with land east of the flow; for the opposite ocean side it travels southward. This follows from combining gηx​=f0​v with a sea-level bulge that decreases offshore, rather than choosing propagation independently of trapping.
For a positive northward bulge, v=(c/H)η is northward and strongest at its crest, and there is no cross-shore velocity in the ideal mode. Ahead of the crest the current decreases northwards, so vy​<0: horizontal flow convergence increases sea level, ηt​=−Hvy​>0. Behind the crest vy​>0: divergence lowers sea level. At the crest itself the alongshore derivative and instantaneous horizontal flow convergence vanish. These signs reverse appropriately for a trough or the opposite coast, while the trapping rule remains the same.

For an eastward tidal signal entering the English Channel from the Atlantic, the northern coast of France lies on the right of propagation, with land to the south. It is therefore the trapping coast of the corresponding northern-hemisphere Kelvin mode. The south coast of England is across the ocean from that boundary and receives a smaller amplitude through the offshore exponential decay. For a uniform channel of width W, this ideal mode would have AEngland​/AFrance​=e−W/LR​.
Thus the mode assumption predicts the greater tidal range on the French side. It does not mean all local tides are a single Kelvin wave: channel depth and shape, friction, reflections, resonances and interference also influence the actual tidal range. The explanation concerns the stated eastward incoming tidal-mode idealization, not a universal comparison at every location or tidal constituent.

Linearize about a uniform horizontal flow U and a hydrostatic, vertically stratified basic state. The basic-flow material derivative is D0​=∂t​+U∂x​. The first two equations are the horizontal and vertical perturbation momentum balances: pressure gradients accelerate the disturbance, while the vertical equation retains the buoyancy force −gρ′/ρ0​ after the basic hydrostatic balance is subtracted.
The Boussinesq approximation neglects fractional density variations in inertia and enforces incompressibility, ux​+wz​=0, but retains them in buoyancy. Advecting the stable background density gradient gives D0​ρ′+wdρˉ​/dz=0. With N2=−(g/ρ0​)dρˉ​/dz>0, this is the fourth supplied equation. A displaced parcel then has a restoring buoyancy tendency.
Small amplitude allows products of perturbations to be discarded; two-dimensional motion removes y dependence and transverse flow. Rotation, viscosity, diffusion, diabatic heating and mean shear are neglected. ρ0​ is the reference inertial density, whereas the small background density gradient still sets N. In a compressible real atmosphere the appropriate stability measure involves potential temperature; this Boussinesq system is a local idealization that filters acoustic waves rather than a full compressible atmospheric model.

A mountain width is not an exactly specified sinusoidal wavelength. Taking its characteristic horizontal inverse length as ∣k∣∼1/a with a=10km gives ∣k∣∼10−4m−1. Since N/U=10−3m−1, the stationary vertical wavenumber is
m=−(N/U)2−k2​≃−9.95×10−4m−1,λz​=∣m∣2π​≃6.3km.​
(49)
This is close to the long-horizontal-scale/hydrostatic estimate 2πU/N=6.28km. If instead the quoted width is identified with a sinusoidal wavelength λx​=10km, then ∣k∣=2π/λx​ and λz​≃8.1km. Thus several kilometres, roughly 6–8km under these two common width conventions, is the justified estimate; the terrain profile would fix its actual forcing spectrum.
For the inverse-width choice, both k and m are negative for upward energy radiation. The stationary phase lines kx+mz=constant tilt upstream with increasing height, while energy propagates upward and slightly downstream. Numerically cg​​≃(0.10,0.995)ms−1, with ray inclination about 84.3∘. Intrinsic phase propagation points upstream and downwards; the ground pattern itself remains fixed. The original sketch below uses this explicit convention.
A ground observer may see stationary lenticular or wave clouds where ascent cools sufficiently moist air, with continuing air flow through a cloud whose position remains almost fixed. Descending regions can clear or warm. An aircraft crossing the pattern encounters alternating updraughts and downdraughts; gliders can exploit the lift. Strong amplitudes, shear or rotors may cause turbulence, but a linear gravity wave can also be smooth, and clear-air waves need not produce any visible cloud. Mountain height, humidity and nonlinear stability are not supplied, so neither a vertical-wind amplitude nor a definite atmospheric precipitation forecast follows.

Assuming the mean wind remains 10ms−1, the weakly stratified upper layer has
mupper2​=(1010−3​)2−k2=10−8m−2−k2.
(50)
For the inverse-width estimate ∣k∣∼10−4m−1, it is near the turning threshold m=0, with negligible upward group-velocity component. Components of larger ∣k∣ are evanescent aloft and are substantially reflected; only horizontal wavelengths exceeding 2πU/Nupper​≃62.8km can propagate vertically there. For the alternative 10km sinusoidal wavelength, mupper2​<0 and the evanescent decay length is about 1.6km.
The sharp inversion and change of stability can therefore cap the upward-radiating disturbance. Reflection between the ground and cap may trap energy in the lower layer and allow downstream lee-wave trains or resonant guided modes, with permitted wavelengths depending on the interface and lower-layer structure. Reflection, leakage and possible resonance are distinct: a specified 5km height does not by itself fix a unique mode amplitude or wavelength.
Wave clouds may become confined below the cap or organized into persistent lee bands if there is enough moisture. Inversion-limited vertical mixing can also retain low cloud, haze or pollutants; sufficiently strong trapped motions can promote rotors or turbulence. These are possible changes, not inevitable weather outcomes: humidity, wave amplitude, detailed stability and wind shear are unspecified. The new structure suppresses upward energy transmission for the dominant short horizontal scales even though the lower atmosphere can still support waves.
