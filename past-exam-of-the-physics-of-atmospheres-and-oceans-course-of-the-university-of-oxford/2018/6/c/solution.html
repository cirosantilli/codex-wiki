A mountain width is not an exactly specified sinusoidal wavelength. Taking its characteristic horizontal inverse length as ∣k∣∼1/a with a=10km gives ∣k∣∼10−4m−1. Since N/U=10−3m−1, the stationary vertical wavenumber is
m=−(N/U)2−k2​≃−9.95×10−4m−1,λz​=∣m∣2π​≃6.3km.​
(49)
This is close to the long-horizontal-scale/hydrostatic estimate 2πU/N=6.28km. If instead the quoted width is identified with a sinusoidal wavelength λx​=10km, then ∣k∣=2π/λx​ and λz​≃8.1km. Thus several kilometres, roughly 6–8km under these two common width conventions, is the justified estimate; the terrain profile would fix its actual forcing spectrum.
For the inverse-width choice, both k and m are negative for upward energy radiation. The stationary phase lines kx+mz=constant tilt upstream with increasing height, while energy propagates upward and slightly downstream. Numerically cg​​≃(0.10,0.995)ms−1, with ray inclination about 84.3∘. Intrinsic phase propagation points upstream and downwards; the ground pattern itself remains fixed. The original sketch below uses this explicit convention.
A ground observer may see stationary lenticular or wave clouds where ascent cools sufficiently moist air, with continuing air flow through a cloud whose position remains almost fixed. Descending regions can clear or warm. An aircraft crossing the pattern encounters alternating updraughts and downdraughts; gliders can exploit the lift. Strong amplitudes, shear or rotors may cause turbulence, but a linear gravity wave can also be smooth, and clear-air waves need not produce any visible cloud. Mountain height, humidity and nonlinear stability are not supplied, so neither a vertical-wind amplitude nor a definite atmospheric precipitation forecast follows.
