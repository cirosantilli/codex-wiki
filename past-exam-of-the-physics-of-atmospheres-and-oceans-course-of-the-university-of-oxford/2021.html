Notation. The source uses ps​ for both the hypothetical dry surface pressure and the actual surface pressure after evaporation. Distinguish them as Pd​ and P, respectively, and interpret the specified pressure thickness Δp as fixed. This conserves the original hydrogen inventory and permits P to vary. Reading both occurrences as one fixed number would be inconsistent with adding water vapor to a closed atmospheric column mass budget.
Let E(T) denote the saturation pressure of water, let x be the mole fraction of water vapor in the mixed layer, and let Md​,Mv​ be the molar masses of hydrogen and water. Surface phase equilibrium and the ideal gas law give
x=PE(Ts​)​,pv​(p)=xp.
(1)
Because the layer has constant temperature, its relative humidity is
RH(p)=E(Ts​)pv​(p)​=Pp​​,P−Δp≤p≤P.
(2)
Thus every level strictly above the surface is subsaturated: hydrostatic balance makes p<P, whereas the saturation pressure stays constant. The result does not require a dilute water vapor approximation.
The Clausius-Clapeyron relation, neglecting the specific volume of liquid water and treating the water vapor as an ideal gas, gives
dTdlnE​=Rv​T2Lv​​,E(T)=Et​exp[Rv​Lv​​(Tt​1​−T1​)],Rv​=Mv​R​.
(3)
Here Tt​,Et​ are the triple point values, Lv​ is the specific latent heat, and R is the molar gas constant. All temperatures in these expressions are absolute. As throughout the question, this is a constant-latent heat, ideal gas model, not an extrapolation valid through the real critical point of water.
For a well-mixed ideal gas mixture, its mean molar mass and water vapor mass fraction are
M=Md​(1+ax),wv​=1+axϵx​,ϵ=Md​Mv​​≃9,a=ϵ−1≃8.
(4)
By hydrostatic balance, the mixed layer has total mass per unit area Δp/g, of which wv​Δp/g is water vapor. The remaining atmosphere contains no water vapor. Consequently conservation of hydrogen mass gives
gP​=gPd​​+gΔp​wv​,P=Pd​+ΔpP+aE(Ts​)ϵE(Ts​)​.
(5)
Equivalently the requested quadratic equation is
(P−Pd​)(P+aE)=ϵΔpE.​
(6)
For each Ts​, calculate E, take the positive root of this quadratic equation, and retain it only if 0≤x<1 and P>Δp. The last condition leaves a nonempty dry layer. In particular the dry constituent's surface partial pressure is not generally Pd​: Pd​ measures its column weight, and the hydrogen and water vapor have different molar masses.

A convenient parameter is the water vapor mole fraction itself. The mass conservation calculation yields
P(x)=Pd​+1+axϵΔpx​,E(x)=xP(x).
(7)
Both functions increase for 0≤x≤1. At the endpoint,
P(1)=E(1)=Pd​+Δp.
(8)
Therefore the physically admissible hydrogen–water vapor mixture requires
E(Ts​)<Pd​+Δp.​
(9)
Beyond this bound, the formal positive quadratic equation root requires x>1, equivalently a negative hydrogen partial pressure. It is the physical constraints, rather than loss of real roots, that end the solution branch. For a concrete example, put Pd​=Δp=1 in consistent pressure units, ϵ=9, and E=3. The positive root of P2+23P−51=0 is about 2.037, giving x≃1.473, which is impossible.
The limiting temperature is obtained from E(T∗​)=Pd​+Δp:
T∗​1​=Tt​1​−Lv​Rv​​lnEt​Pd​+Δp​.
(10)
This finite limit exists above Tt​ if Et​<Pd​+Δp<Et​exp[Lv​/(Rv​Tt​)]. The upper qualification matters mathematically: the stipulated constant-latent heat formula has a finite limiting saturation pressure as T→∞, so for an arbitrarily large unspecified Pd​+Δp the printed assertion of a finite breakdown temperature is not unconditional. For a concrete formal counterexample, choose Pd​+Δp=1015Pa: with the usual triple-point constants the model has E(∞)≃2.5×1011Pa, so it never reaches the proposed inventory limit. This is a counterexample inside the stipulated equations, not a physically credible high-pressure ideal gas ocean. Actual liquid water also ceases to exist above its critical point, before such an extrapolation would make sense.
Physically, the imposed finite-pressure-thickness layer can hold no more than Δp/g of water vapor per unit area. As x→1, it expels all its hydrogen into the dry upper layer. A still larger surface saturation pressure cannot coexist with this geometry. The layer must deepen, the temperature profile must change, or the assumed phase equilibrium must fail. If Pd​<Δp, the additional restriction P>Δp can also exclude the low-temperature end of the branch.

At the interface the pressure is continuous. Immediately below it the temperature is Ts​ and mean molar mass is Md​(1+ax); immediately above it the dry hydrogen has temperature T1​. The ideal gas law therefore gives
ρ−​=RTs​p1​Md​(1+ax)​,ρ+​=RT1​p1​Md​​.
(11)
An infinitesimal interchange is unstable to convection when the lower fluid is less dense than the upper fluid. Hence
T1​Ts​​>1+aP(Ts​)E(Ts​)​.​
(12)
Equality gives neutral interfacial buoyancy; reversing the inequality gives a stable density jump. This is an interfacial criterion, not a claim that the whole dry upper atmosphere is stable. Its vertical temperature profile has not been specified. Also, RH(p1​)=p1​/P<1, so infinitesimal displacement at the interface initially involves no condensation or release of latent heat.
There are two competing effects. Increasing Ts​ directly reduces the lower density, favouring convection. Increasing the mole fraction of heavy water vapor increases its mean molar mass, opposing convection. With x≪1, the criterion is approximately Ts​>T1​. With x→1, it tends to Ts​>ϵT1​≃9T1​: substantial surface heating need not overcome the compositional stabilization in hydrogen.
For a specified Pd​,Δp,T1​, solve Ts​/T1​−1−ax(Ts​)=0 on the admissible branch to locate the transitions. Its derivative is 1/T1​−ax′(Ts​), so there is no universal monotone trend: rapid growth of the saturation pressure can suppress an instability that was present at lower Ts​. Without numerical parameter values, neither the number of crossings nor a unique interval of unstable temperatures can be asserted.

Write κ=Rd​/cp​, where Rd​ is the specific gas constant and cp​ the specific heat capacity at constant pressure. Conservation of potential temperature on a dry adiabatic process gives
T(p)=Tg​(ps​p​)κ,Tc​=qTg​,q=(ps​pc​​)κ.
(13)
Let Q=(1−αc​)S be the absorbed stellar irradiance, and let T0​=(Q/σ)1/4, with σ the Stefan-Boltzmann constant. The transparent gas contributes no thermal radiation. For a cloud with emissivity e, Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation gives thermal absorptivity e; assume thermal transmissivity 1−e and negligible thermal reflection, as in the usual one-layer grey body model. The outgoing radiant flux is the transmitted surface emission plus upward cloud emission:
Q=(1−e)σTg4​+eσTc4​.
(14)
Thus
Tg​=T0​[1−e+eq4]−1/4,Tc​=qTg​.​
(15)
No factor of four is needed: S is the incident irradiance of the specified column, rather than a stellar-beam irradiance that still needs a spherical average.
For fixed αc​,pc​,ps​, increasing e monotonically warms the ground when 0<q<1. At e=0, Tg​=T0​; at e=1, Tc​=T0​ and Tg​=T0​/q. A cloud at the surface, q=1, produces no such warming. Comparing with a genuinely cloud-free column also requires including its different albedo: relative to (S/σ)1/4, the ratio is [(1−αc​)/(1−e+eq4)]1/4. Reflection can outweigh the greenhouse effect.
The governing balance here is the whole-column radiation budget. Imposing an additional purely radiative surface balance would incorrectly remove the convective heat transfer that the prescribed dry adiabatic process may require.

At fixed ground temperature, warming the cloud increases its downward radiant flux eσTc4​, giving a positive contribution to the ground's radiative heating. That is the immediate radiative effect. A self-consistent final answer must also remove the convective heat transfer used to pin the original cloud to the dry adiabatic process.
For pure radiative equilibrium of both bodies, with no net nonradiative energy exchange, their two balances are
σTg4​=Q+eσTc4​,eσTg4​=2eσTc4​.
(19)
For e>0 this gives
Tg​=T0​(1−e/2)−1/4,Tc​=T0​(2−e)−1/4.​
(20)
The e→0+ limit gives the selected limiting cloud temperature; at exactly e=0, only Tg​=T0​ is fixed radiatively. The maximum greenhouse effect at fixed Q occurs at e=1:
Tg​=21/4T0​≃1.189T0​.​
(21)
The pinned dry-adiabatic process case instead has maximum factor 1/q=(ps​/pc​)κ. It exceeds 21/4 for a high, cold cloud with q<2−1/4, equals it at the level found in part b, and is smaller for a low, warm cloud.
In particular, for q4<1/2 the final purely radiative ground is cooler than the pinned solution, even though a warmer cloud increases downward radiation at fixed Tg​. To see why these statements are compatible, let H be the net upward nonradiative energy transfer from the ground to the cloud in the pinned state. Its conservation of energy equation requires
H=eσ(2Tc4​−Tg4​),Q+eσTc4​−σTg4​=H.
(22)
For the cold cloud, H<0: maintaining it on the assumed dry adiabatic process requires removing its net radiative heating and returning energy elsewhere. Setting H=0 changes that energy redistribution. It is therefore incorrect to infer the final ground temperature by changing only the downward radiant flux. Indeed the final cloud is warmer than the pinned one for q4<1/2 and 0<e<1, while the final ground is cooler. At e=1 the cloud temperature is T0​ in both whole-column equilibria.
Fixed cloud properties are an artificial constraint. Warming a saturated cloud increases the saturation pressure, tending to evaporate its cloud droplets; the released water vapor, changing latent heat budget and altered buoyancy can change its altitude and emissivity. Cooling favours condensation and potentially atmospheric precipitation. A warm perturbation that survives evaporation can rise by convection, but neither a unique vertical displacement nor survival of the cloud follows without moisture supply and an environmental temperature profile. Finally, pure radiative equilibrium of the two bodies does not require the intervening transparent gas to remain on the original dry adiabatic process; imposing that extra constraint is generally inconsistent except at the matching level in part b.

Before condensation, assume a closed parcel with fixed atmospheric mixing ratio, constant specific heat capacities, and negligible irreversible heating. The first law of thermodynamics for a dry adiabatic process is
cp​dT−ρ1​dp=0.
(23)
The parcel remains in approximate pressure equilibrium with a hydrostatic environment. With the standard approximation that parcel and environmental densities are close, dp/dz≃−ρg, hence
dzdT​=−cp​g​=−Γd​,T(z)=Ts​−Γd​z.​
(24)
This is the dry adiabatic lapse rate, about 9.8Kkm−1 for air. More precisely the environmental hydrostatic balance gives dT/dz=−gρenv​/(cp​ρparcel​); the familiar constant dry adiabatic lapse rate uses their ratio close to one.

A rising parcel cools, lowering the saturation pressure. Once its water vapor supply exceeds that value, atmospheric supersaturation develops. Homogeneous nucleation of pure water is strongly inhibited at the small atmospheric supersaturations typical of clouds; existing aerosol particles provide cloud condensation nuclei. Their dry sizes and composition, together with the parcel's ascent rate, determine how many cloud droplets activate.
Köhler theory combines the Kelvin equation, which raises equilibrium vapor pressure over a curved surface, with Raoult's law, which lowers it for a chemical solution. In a dilute diameter convention, with dry diameter Dd​ and wet diameter D,
seq​(D)≃DAK​​−D3κh​Dd3​​,AK​=RTρw​4σw​Mw​​,sc​(Dd​,κh​)=(27κh​Dd3​4AK3​​)1/2.
(32)
Here s=ev​/E(T)−1 is atmospheric supersaturation, σw​ is surface tension, and κh​ measures aerosol hygroscopicity, not the dry-adiabatic process exponent. The maximum of the Köhler curve is the critical supersaturation. Larger or more hygroscopic particles generally have lower thresholds. Cloud-droplet activation requires overcoming that maximum; total aerosol number is not the activated number.
Given an observed aerosol number size distribution nj​(Dd​)=dNj​/dlnDd​ for composition classes j, first assign each class a hygroscopicity and compute its critical supersaturation. If the parcel reaches maximum atmospheric supersaturation smax​, the equilibrium-threshold estimate is
Dd,min,j​=(27κh,j​smax2​4AK3​​)1/3,Nact​=j∑​∫Dd,min,j​∞​nj​(Dd​)dlnDd​.​
(33)
For each fitted lognormal distribution, this is its upper-tail integral; it can be evaluated analytically with an error function or numerically from measured bins.
The value smax​ must be calculated, not guessed from the aerosol distribution alone. Prescribe parcel upward velocity w, initial pressure, temperature and humidity, then integrate expansion cooling, latent heat release, conservation of total water, and diffusional cloud droplet growth. Schematically,
dtds​=as​w−bs​dtdql​​,dtdri2​​=2G[s−seq,i​(ri​)],
(34)
where as​,bs​>0, G includes water vapor diffusion and thermal conduction, and ql​ is liquid mass fraction. Rising motion produces atmospheric supersaturation; growing particles consume it. Their competition selects the peak and which particles cross their activation barriers. Assume a well-mixed closed parcel, liquid water, soluble spherical particles, specified mixing state, and negligible collision-coalescence and fallout during activation. Slow kinetic growth, weakly soluble particles or entrainment require a more detailed treatment than the threshold integral.

The optical telescope supplies collecting area and spatial selection, the spectral selector supplies a passband, and the photodetector converts received radiant flux into a measured signal. For an extended source, a useful schematic photon count is
Ns​≃ητAΩ∫band​hcLλ​λ​dλ,
(35)
where AΩ is the etendue, τ the integration time, η the net transmission and quantum efficiency, and Lλ​ the spectral radiance. An illustrative independent-noise model gives
SNR≃Ns​+Nb​+Nd​+σread2​​Ns​​.
(36)
Here Nb​ and Nd​ denote background and dark counts, and σread​ is readout standard deviation. This makes the roles of the three components explicit.
A larger optical telescope collects more photons at fixed angular pixel size and improves the diffraction-limited resolution. Its limits include optical aberrations, alignment, launch size, stray light and pointing. Conservation of etendue constrains simultaneous collecting area and angular acceptance. Increasing spatial resolution by narrowing a pixel's solid angle reduces its photons.
A narrower spectral passband or higher spectral resolving power separates diagnostic features and rejects unwanted background, but usually reduces photons per channel and hence signal-to-noise ratio. Selector transmission, leakage, slit width, detector format and the required time cadence limit the trade. A wider band helps only insofar as it adds useful signal rather than mostly background.
The photodetector's quantum efficiency, dark current, read noise, dynamic range and response time determine conversion losses and added noise. Cooling can suppress thermal background and dark current, but cannot remove photon shot noise. Longer integration improves a stationary signal's signal-to-noise ratio until motion, saturation, drift or required cadence becomes limiting.

A geostationary orbit provides continuous viewing of approximately the same terrestrial region, favouring fast cloud evolution, storm tracking and the diurnal cycle. The large range makes fine spatial resolution demanding, high latitudes have poor viewing geometry, and a single platform has no global coverage. A polar orbit gives much smaller range and broad latitude coverage as Earth rotates beneath it, favouring detailed global mapping; any one location is sampled intermittently. A Sun-synchronous orbit can hold approximately fixed local solar time for comparing observations, but that sampling can miss much of the diurnal evolution. The science therefore sets a trade between spatial coverage, spatial resolution and temporal sampling.
The Moon's very slow, tidally locked rotation makes a lunar stationary orbit fundamentally problematic. Let its orbital angular velocity around Earth be n, approximately also its spin rate. For lunar mass m, terrestrial mass M, and separation a, the two-body estimates give
n2≃a3GM​,rsync​=(n2Gm​)1/3≃a(Mm​)1/3,rH​≃a(3Mm​)1/3.
(37)
Thus rsync​≃31/3rH​ lies outside the lunar Hill sphere. Earth's tidal perturbations prevent the desired simple stable, lunar-centred stationary orbit. This is stronger than merely saying that the required orbit is high; specially maintained three-body orbits are a different construction.

A calibrated multispectral filter radiometer is a natural choice for rapid thermal mapping: a few selected bands provide higher photon throughput and faster imaging than spreading the light over many spectrometer channels. Several bands can help separate temperature, dust emissivity and atmospheric effects. A spectrometer would be justified if identifying composition or resolving spectral structure were a central additional objective; one broadband radiance alone cannot generally determine both temperature and unknown emissivity.
Thermal infrared radiation is preferable to reflected visible light for measuring temperature. The Rayleigh criterion sets
1.22Dλ​≲h500​,λ≲12.0μm.​
(43)
A band near 10μm is therefore a reasonable compromise between useful planetary thermal emission and the required diffraction-limited resolution. At that wavelength, the nominal surface Rayleigh criterion separation is about 415m; at 15μm it is about 623m and fails the target. The exact bands must avoid or model strong atmospheric absorption and account for the dust spectrum. The 500m sampling pitch is still not a guarantee of faithful reconstruction of arbitrary 500m features: detector sampling and the point spread function must be considered together.

Take a Northern-Hemisphere cyclonic vortex, with positive relative vorticity. For a fixed-depth incompressible layer, conservation of absolute vorticity gives
DtD​(ξ+f)=0,DtDξ​=−βv.
(44)
The anticlockwise flow on the east side moves parcels northward, increasing their planetary vorticity and creating negative relative vorticity anomalies. On the west side it moves parcels southward and creates positive relative vorticity anomalies. Initially,
∂t​ξ≃−βv,
(45)
so the positive anomaly grows on the western flank while the eastern flank loses positive vorticity. The cyclonic pattern consequently shifts westward. The induced anomalies themselves drive meridional flow and further relative vorticity anomalies, transmitting a Rossby wave pattern through the surrounding fluid. The restoring process is this exchange between planetary vorticity and relative vorticity, rather than a parcel executing a permanently westward translation.
The sketch shows the initial tendency, with the generated signs separated from the initial positive core. An isolated disturbance contains many wavevectors and disperses; westward phase velocity does not imply that all its wave energy travels westward. A Southern-Hemisphere cyclonic vortex reverses the signs of the initial and generated relative vorticities, while the same beta plane mechanism remains.

For a nonzero zonal wavenumber k, stationarity requires
0=kω​=U−k2+l2β​,Ks2​=Uβ​.
(53)
A real propagating solution therefore requires a westerly U>0. For 0<∣k∣<Ks​ there are two meridional wavenumbers:
l±​=±Uβ​−k2​.​
(54)
The source's assertion of two stationary solutions needs these qualifications: at ∣k∣=Ks​ they coincide, and at ∣k∣>Ks​ there is no real meridionally propagating solution. For example k=2β/U​ gives l2=−3β/U. Modes with k=0 are a separate zonally invariant degeneracy and do not have a defined zonal phase velocity.
Adding U to the zonal group velocity and imposing stationarity gives
cg​=Ks2​2Uk​(k,l),dxdy​=cgx​cgy​​=kl​.​
(55)
Thus a localized midlatitude disturbance radiates stationary Rossby wave activity downstream, along one poleward and one equatorward branch. For k>0 in the Northern Hemisphere, l+​ gives the northeastward branch and l−​ the southeastward branch. With strictly constant U,β, these wave rays are straight. Changing latitude cannot bend them within that constant-coefficient model.
To discuss a planet, extend it locally to slowly varying β(ϕ)=2Ωcosϕ/ap​ and possibly U(ϕ). The conserved zonal wavenumber in a locally zonally uniform medium then has
l2(ϕ)=U(ϕ)β(ϕ)​−k2.
(56)
For constant U and northward propagation in the Northern Hemisphere, decreasing β reduces l until l=0: the wave ray becomes zonal at a turning latitude and can refract equatorward. The equatorward branch initially encounters larger allowed Ks​ and becomes more meridional. This local midlatitude description eventually needs replacement near the equator or wherever the background varies rapidly.
Orographic forcing by a mountain range or persistent spatially localized heating can supply such a disturbance. Its steady forcing selects the ground-stationary part of the response. The corresponding local total wavelength is
Λs​=Ks​2π​=2πβU​​.​
(57)
For fixed U it increases toward the pole as β decreases. Locally the zonal integer scale has upper bound nmax​≃ap​cosϕKs​=2Ωap​cos3ϕ/U​, so fewer planetary-scale zonal waves fit around higher-latitude circles. This is not an unconditional trend in actual weather: changing U, its curvature, stratification and the forcing spectrum can change the dominant Rossby wave wavelengths.
