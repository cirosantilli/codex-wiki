Let RA​,RB​ be the specific specific gas constants and cpA​,cpB​ the specific heat capacities of the noncondensible gas and vapour. Write es​(T) for the saturation vapor pressure, r for vapour mass per unit dry-gas mass, and ϵ=RA​/RB​. For a dilute ideal gas mixture,
r=p−es​ϵes​​≃ϵpes​​,dTdlnes​​=RB​T2L​.
(1)
The second equation is the Clausius-Clapeyron equation, neglecting condensed-phase volume and taking constant latent heat L. The first law of thermodynamics for saturated ascent, with condensate removed and negligible condensate heat capacity, is to leading order
cpA​dT−RA​Tdlnp=−Ldr.
(2)
Since dr=r[LdT/(RB​T2)−dlnp], the resulting moist adiabat has logarithmic slope
dlnpdlnT​=cpA​RA​​1+L2r/(cpA​RB​T2)1+Lr/(RA​T)​.
(3)
Consequently a sufficient dry adiabat criterion is
pes​​≪1,cpA​rcpB​​≪1,RA​TLr​≪1,cpA​RB​T2L2r​≪1.​
(4)
The first two conditions justify neglecting the vapour's effects on the gas law and sensible heat capacity; the last two suppress latent heating. With x=RA​/cpA​ and β=L/(RB​T), the latent conditions are βes​/p≪1 and xβ2es​/p≪1. Usually xβ≫1, so the squared-latent term gives the stronger condition. At sufficiently low surface temperature, the exponential smallness of es​(Ts​) makes these conditions hold and
T(p)≃Ts​(p/ps​)x.​
(5)
These are local conditions along the column. They can be checked at the surface when xβs​>1+2x: on this dry profile the strongest latent parameter is proportional to exp[(1+2x)ζ−βs​(exζ−1)] and decreases upwards. Without that restriction a surface-only test need not bound every altitude. If suspended condensate is retained, its own heat capacity and loading must also be negligible; saturation alone does not specify those quantities.

Pressure broadening is proportional to collision frequency and therefore becomes small in the upper atmosphere. Doppler broadening remains finite at finite temperature, with a characteristic width of order ν0​kB​T/(mc2)​ up to the stated width convention. Where that exceeds the collisional width, the line core is Doppler dominated. A Voigt profile combines the two mechanisms; natural broadening can contribute at still lower pressures. Thus a Lorentz pressure-broadened profile cannot hold all the way to p=0.
At line centre, replacing the fictitious 1/p opacity by a finite Doppler-core coefficient removes the divergent column optical depth. The emitting pressure is then finite rather than arbitrarily close to zero. If that level still lies in the isothermal stratosphere and the core is opaque, the central brightness temperature stays approximately Tstrat​, so the flux floor changes little. If it lies below the stratosphere, or the Doppler-core column becomes thin, warmer layers or the surface contribute and the core brightens. The core-to-wing transition changes shape and width, but Lorentz wings formed mainly at higher pressures remain approximately as in part (c). The size of the correction needs the missing spectroscopic widths, but no unique numerical correction is requested.

For a non-scattering atmosphere above a blackbody surface, the difference between the two curves is the transmitted surface term Bλ​(Ts​)Tλ,s​. The atmospheric-only curve is an integral of local thermal emission weighted by transmission to space. Molecular absorption blocks some wavelengths and permits narrow windows at others. In a transparent window, adding the hot surface substantially increases the emerging radiance; in an opaque interval, the two curves nearly coincide because the surface contribution is extinguished.
The original graph shows the clearest surface-dominated window around 0.99–1.04μm, with the blue curve near 5μWcm−2sr−1μm−1 at its maximum and the atmospheric-only contribution small. Weaker possibilities are around 0.85–0.92μm and near 0.8μm, with less available signal. The 1.17–1.20μm peak contains a measurable surface contribution but a larger atmospheric background. The near-equality around 1.27–1.29μm makes that peak a poor surface thermometer despite its appreciable total radiance. These are approximate readings, not precisely tabulated band edges.
At Ts​≃766K, the Wien displacement law puts the thermal wavelength peak near 3.8μm. A 1μm window is still in the Wien approximation regime, but its radiance is exponentially larger than for an Earth-like 300K or colder Martian surface. For example, at 1μm the Planck function ratio Bλ​(766)/Bλ​(300) is of order 1012. A modest hot-spot temperature increase likewise gives a relatively large fractional radiance change. Inverting this signal for surface temperature requires an atmospheric transmission and emission model, not just identifying a peak.

A broad initial cloud-droplet size distribution can be seeded by giant or unusually soluble cloud condensation nuclei, producing a minority of larger droplets before gravitational collection becomes efficient. Variable supersaturation histories, entrainment and mixing can broaden the distribution relative to a perfectly homogeneous monodisperse model. Turbulent relative motion and preferential concentration can increase encounters, and rare successful collisions can create unusually rapid stochastic collectors. These mechanisms can work together; a single deterministic condensational law applied equally to identical droplets cannot create the initial differential fall speeds by itself. For warm rain, the subsequent amplification is liquid collision-coalescence, distinct from ice-phase precipitation mechanisms.

In the horizontal rotating-fluid equation of motion, scale advective acceleration as U2/L and Coriolis acceleration as ∣f∣U, where f=2Ωsinϕ. Their ratio is the Rossby number,
Ro=∣f∣LU​.​
(33)
A small Rossby number means that rotation strongly constrains the flow; with slow evolution, weak friction and a suitable pressure field it permits geostrophic balance. Local time dependence introduces the separate condition 1/(∣f∣tevol​)≪1. A synoptic midlatitude cyclone with U∼10ms−1, L∼1000km and ∣f∣∼10−4s−1 has Ro∼0.1. A tornado with U∼50ms−1 and L∼1km has Ro∼500; its rotational motion does not mean that the Earth's rotation dominates its local dynamics.
The vertical relative vorticity is ζ=∂x​v−∂y​u∼U/L, while planetary vorticity is f. Thus ∣ζ/f∣ is of the order of the Rossby number, although it is not an identity for arbitrary velocity fields: an irrotational strain can have nonzero U/L but zero ζ. Absolute vorticity is f+ζ.
On large, slowly evolving midlatitude scales, the horizontal pressure gradient force is mainly balanced by the Coriolis force, which is perpendicular to velocity. Consequently the wind is nearly parallel to atmospheric isobars, rather than accelerating directly into a low or out of a high. Curvature adds a gradient wind correction, and surface friction permits the cross-isobar component considered in part (d).

Its cross-isobar component is −α∇h​p/[ρ(f2+α2)], always towards lower pressure, in either hemisphere. Thus the surface wind spirals into a cyclone and out of an anticyclone. In the northern hemisphere, the nearly tangential circulation is counterclockwise around the low and clockwise around the high; those rotations reverse in the southern hemisphere. The original force diagrams below show pressure, drag and Coriolis force closing to zero at the east side of each system.
Surface convergence into a low requires upward motion out of the friction layer by the continuity equation; divergence from a high requires subsidence. In unsaturated adiabatic motion the rising parcel expands and cools, while descending air compresses and warms. The relevant warming or cooling relative to its surroundings depends on the environmental stratification, and latent heating can modify the ascending-air result.
For otherwise unsupported weather systems this is quasi-geostrophic Ekman spin-down: drag dissipates kinetic energy, convergence tends to fill a low and divergence tends to erode a high. In a simple column with upward velocity at its base and negligible vertical velocity aloft, cyclonic vorticity decreases because ∂z​w<0; the opposite sign weakens anticyclonic vorticity. Cooling beneath a fixed upper-level pressure also tends to raise the surface pressure through the hydrostatic column, while warming tends to lower it. Real baroclinic development, upper-level mass transport and condensation can sustain or deepen a cyclone despite surface friction, so temperature changes alone do not prove a universal decay rate.

The underlying principle is material conservation of potential vorticity for adiabatic, inviscid, rotating, stably stratified motion. In the quasi-geostrophic approximation, the leading horizontal velocity is nondivergent and geostrophic:
u′=−ψy′​,v′=ψx′​,ψ′=ρ0​f0​p′​.
(42)
Here f0​=2Ωsinϕ0​ is the reference Coriolis parameter, β=(df/dy)0​=2Ωcosϕ0​/a on a spherical planet, and f=f0​+βy. The positive buoyancy frequency squared is N2; in a Boussinesq fluid it is −(g/ρ0​)dρˉ​/dz. To quasi-geostrophic order the potential vorticity is the background f0​+βy plus the stated perturbation q′.
Linearizing its material derivative about uniform zonal velocity U gives
(∂t​+U∂x​)q′+v′β=0,
(43)
which is the supplied equation. The uniform basic flow contributes no relative-vorticity gradient or vertical shear term.
The approximation needs small Rossby number, time scales long compared with ∣f0​∣−1, vertical hydrostatic balance, stable stratification, small departures from the reference state and meridional extent small enough for a local beta plane. Buoyancy and rotation must balance at the relevant aspect ratio. Friction, diabatic heating and strong nonlinear advection are neglected here. It applies to balanced midlatitude ocean and atmospheric motions; an atmospheric version also requires a consistent reference-density or pressure-coordinate treatment. It fails in a sufficiently near-equatorial region where f0​ vanishes.

The quasi-geostrophic potential vorticity perturbation combines relative vorticity, ∇h2​ψ′, with the stretching contribution f02​∂z​(N−2ψz′​). The latter measures how buoyancy surfaces and rotating fluid columns are displaced in a stratified fluid. Even a flow with no vertical dependence can have nonzero relative vorticity, while its explicit stretching term vanishes.
For a vertically uniform plane wave with horizontal wavenumbers (k,l), q′=−(k2+l2)ψ′. Substitution gives
ω−Uk=−k2+l2βk​,cpx​−U=−k2+l2β​<0(β>0).​
(44)
Thus phase propagates westward relative to the current. Physically, northward parcel displacement carries lower background planetary vorticity into a region with larger f, requiring negative relative vorticity to conserve total potential vorticity; southward displacement requires positive relative vorticity. The velocity induced by that vorticity pattern shifts the displacement wave westward. This is a statement about phase motion, not a claim that every Rossby-wave packet has westward group velocity.
