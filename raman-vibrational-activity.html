Raman vibrational activity ((∂α/∂Q)0​=0)

The simplest off-resonant explanation is a vibrating polarizability. Let Q(t)=Q0​cos(2πνv​t) be a small normal mode coordinate of molecular vibration, and use one scalar component of polarizability, α(Q)≃α0​+α0′​Q. For incident electric field E(t)=E0​cos(2πν0​t), the induced electric dipole moment is
p(t)​=α(Q(t))E(t)=α0​E0​cos(2πν0​t)+2α0′​Q0​E0​​[cos(2π(ν0​−νv​)t)+cos(2π(ν0​+νv​)t)].​
(40)
The product-to-sum formula produces the two displaced frequencies. A nonzero derivative α0′​ gives vibrational Raman scattering in this linear model. Real polarizability is generally a tensor, and the selected components depend on incident and observed wave polarization. This condition differs from vibrational infrared activity, which requires a changing molecular electric dipole moment without an applied optical electric field. A vibration can therefore appear in one measurement and be absent in the other.
This classical modulation argument explains line locations but supplies equal sideband wave amplitudes; it does not explain their thermal asymmetry or the nonzero Stokes Raman scattering from a vibrational ground state. Those require the quantum harmonic oscillator population and transition strengths. The far-off-resonance drawing uses dashed virtual intermediate levels: they represent terms in a scattering probability amplitude, not a long-lived occupied electronic energy eigenstate. Near an electronic resonance, a more detailed treatment of the intermediate response is needed.
