The passage belongs to the English reception of Euclid's number theory. Henry Billingsley's The Elements of Geometrie by Henry Billingsley, published in 1570, brought the Elements into English with extensive explanatory material. The original title page advertises translation and annotations; the extract's worked examples show what explanatory prose could add to a compact definition. Henry Billingsley's numbering calls this Definition 23 of Book VII; the familiar modern numbering is VII, Definition 22. The difference is editorial, not a difference in the perfect number being defined.[ref][ref]
Here a number's parts are its positive proper divisors, rather than arbitrary pieces of a sum or all smaller integers. In modern notation the definition is
d∣n1≤d<n​∑​d=n,equivalently σ(n)=2n,​
(1)
where σ is the sum-of-divisors function. Thus 12 is not a perfect number: its positive proper divisors total 1+2+3+4+6=16. It is essential to exclude n itself: including it would double the required sum. The pairing of a divisor d with the fraction 1/(n/d) of the whole explains the extract's language of halves, thirds and so on. Its examples are complete checks because they identify every positive proper divisor, not merely some convenient decomposition.
The larger mathematical context is the construction in Book IX, Proposition 36. If q=2p−1 is a prime number, then N=2p−1q is a perfect number. To reconstruct the argument, prime factorization gives all its divisors as 2j and q2j, with 0≤j≤p−1. A finite geometric series therefore gives
σ(N)=(1+q)∑j=0p−1​2j=2p(2p−1)=2N.
(2)
The choices p=2,3,5,7 produce 6,28,496,8128. The primality assumption matters: choosing p=4 produces 120, whose sum-of-divisors function is 360, not 240. The construction gives a reason for the examples and connects perfect numbers with Mersenne primes.[ref]
The extract itself supplies a definition and examples, not a classification or a mathematical proof that such numbers are rare. In particular, the converse that every even perfect number has this form belongs to the later Euclid-Euler theorem; it should not be read back into this passage. The historically significant feature is the transmission of deductive arithmetic through vernacular explanation: a book called geometry also teaches the divisibility theory needed for a general construction.

The figure illustrates Pierre de Fermat's use of adequality to construct a tangent to a parabola. Reading the local exam image is decisive: D is the vertex of a parabola, DC lies on its axis of symmetry, B lies on the parabola, and the proposed tangent BE meets the extended axis at E. The horizontal through the auxiliary point O meets the axis at I; O lies on BE, outside the parabola. In particular, O must not be treated as a second point of the parabola. The target is the subtangent CE. The diagram and this construction occur in the tangent discussion associated with Varia opera mathematica by Pierre de Fermat. The 1679 book was posthumous: its date does not date the invention of the method.[ref][ref]
Write d=CD>0, a=CE>0, and e=CI, with I between C and D. These are lengths, and the increment e is distinct from the labelled point E. Then DI=d−e and EI=a−e. The parabola's characteristic relation makes the square of an ordinate proportional to its distance from the vertex of a parabola. At height I, the actual ordinate of the parabola is shorter than OI. Hence
d−ed​>OI2BC2​=(a−e)2a2​,
(3)
where the equality follows from similar triangles BCE and OIE.
The method of adequality replaces the comparison by a relation used to extract the condition of contact:
d(a−e)2∼a2(d−e).
(4)
Expanding and cancelling da2 gives −2dae+de2∼−a2e. Divide by the nonzero increment e, then suppress the remaining term containing e. The result is a2=2da, so, since a>0,
CE=2CD.​
(5)
Thus DE=DC: the intersection with the axis lies as far above the vertex of a parabola as C lies below it. This is a concrete construction, not just a rule for computing a numerical slope.
A modern check clarifies why the calculation succeeds without pretending that Pierre de Fermat used a modern limit. Put D=(0,0), let y increase down the axis, and write the parabola as x2=ky, k>0. For B=(b,d), with b2=kd, the proposed tangent through E=(0,−d) is y=2bx/k−d. Substitution gives
x2−ky=(x−b)2.
(6)
It meets the parabola with a double root at B, and for every other point its y coordinate is less than that of the parabola. This also verifies the strict comparison used for O.
The significance is a systematic algebraic condition for geometric contact. Subtracting neighbouring expressions, dividing by an increment, and discarding its remaining terms anticipates differentiation. But the historical question is precisely what licenses the last step. The symbol ∼ above marks adequality, not ordinary equality at a finite displacement. Treating the construction as an already stated epsilon-delta definition of a limit would conceal the methodological innovation and its unresolved explanatory burden.

Alexandre-Théophile Vandermonde presents a programme in elimination theory: remove unknowns from simultaneous polynomial equations in a way that works uniformly as the number of equations and their polynomial degrees vary. The immediate achievement is narrower than that programme: a general construction for linear equations. The academy year 1772 used in the exam is not the printing date of the memoir, which appeared in 1776. Its compact indices distinguished the equation containing a coefficient from the unknown it multiplied; they were not powers. That notational separation is crucial to interpreting the work.[ref][ref]
In modern language take a system of linear equations Ax=b with n unknowns. Its determinant is an alternating expression in the coefficients:
D=detA=∑π∈Sn​​sgn(π)∏i=1n​ai,π(i)​.
(7)
Each product selects one coefficient from each equation and each unknown's column. The sign of a permutation determines whether it is added or subtracted. This makes the number n a genuine parameter of a general rule, rather than merely supplying separately memorised formulas for two or three unknowns. The notation here is modern, not a transcription of Alexandre-Théophile Vandermonde's symbols.
For two unknowns, multiply ax+by=u by d and cx+dy=v by b, then subtract. Similarly eliminate x. This gives
(ad−bc)x=du−bv,(ad−bc)y=av−cu.
(8)
The same principle in arbitrary size is Cramer's rule. If Aj​(b) is obtained by replacing column j of A by b, then
xj​=detAdetAj​(b)​when detA=0.​
(9)
An original verification uses the multilinearity of the determinant: replace b by the column combination ∑k​xk​Ak​. Every resulting determinant vanishes through repeated columns except the k=j term, which is xj​detA. This explains how an elimination expression isolates one unknown.
The condition D=0 is substantive. If D=0, multiplying by an expression which vanishes cannot justify division. For instance, x+y=1 and 2x+2y=3 are inconsistent, whereas replacing 3 by 2 gives infinitely many solutions. Thus an elimination formula must distinguish exceptional coefficients and preserve the connection to the original system of linear equations. Nor does the displayed rule solve the much broader problem of arbitrary nonlinear equations.
The passage's importance is the move from particular elimination calculations towards a theory of alternating expressions. It would be misleading to turn this into the claim that the familiar Vandermonde determinant, formed from powers of variables, is what this extract displays. A positional upper index and an exponent have different meanings; the history of that eponym requires separate evidence. Likewise, modern matrix algebra is a useful explanatory language here, not the institutional or conceptual setting of an eighteenth-century memoir.[ref]

Joseph Fourier's Théorie analytique de la chaleur by Joseph Fourier (1822) makes trigonometric series instruments for solving problems of heat conduction. A prescribed temperature distribution need not be given by one power series: it may describe different heating on different parts of a body. The extract occurs in Chapter III, §233, where the earlier assumption of an odd-power expansion is relaxed. The innovation concerns both representation and what can count as a function in an analytical calculation.[ref]
Use a separate integration variable t to unpack the printed notation. The proposed Fourier sine series has coefficients
bn​=π2​∫0π​f(t)sin(nt)dt,f(x)∼n=1∑∞​bn​sin(nx).​
(10)
The integral signs in the extract denote definite integrals over [0,π], not antiderivatives; the author explicitly supplies these bounds in the surrounding sentence. Their absent printed bounds are a notational convention, not permission to choose arbitrary constants of integration.
The key calculation is the orthogonality relation
∫0π​sin(nt)sin(mt)dt={π/20​n=m,n=m.​
(11)
The product-to-sum formulas prove this by integrating cosines of integer multiples of t. If an expansion can be integrated term by term, multiplication by sin(mt) followed by integration selects exactly one coefficient. This derives the only possible coefficients, but does not by itself prove the expansion converges to the proposed function. That distinction is central to assessing the extract's claim.
For a revealing example take f(x)=1 on 0<x<π. Direct integration gives bn​=2(1−(−1)n)/(πn), and therefore
1=π4​(sinx+3sin3x​+5sin5x​+⋯),0<x<π.
(12)
Every term is zero at 0 and π, so the series cannot take the value 1 at those endpoints. Its natural interpretation uses the odd extension followed by periodic extension; the endpoints become jump discontinuities. More generally, for a piecewise continuously differentiable function, the Fourier series converges at an interior jump to the average of the two one-sided limits, irrespective of an individually assigned value there. Changing one value also leaves the integrals unchanged. Consequently the unqualified assertion of representation for every arbitrary function cannot be a modern pointwise theorem. Dirichlet's theorem on Fourier series supplies sufficient conditions and the correct distinction between continuity points and jumps; its 1829 source explicitly addresses the proof problem.[ref]
The physical payoff is explicit. For a rod scaled to 0<x<π with zero end temperature, separation of variables in the heat equation produces
u(x,τ)=∑n=1∞​bn​e−κn2τsin(nx),κ>0.
(13)
Each mode solves uτ​=κuxx​ and the end boundary conditions; the Fourier sine coefficients encode the initial temperature. This rod is an explanatory example, distinct from the stationary rectangular-solid problem opening Joseph Fourier's Chapter III. The factor e−κn2τ damps high modes rapidly for positive time. The representation of arbitrary data makes the physical solution possible; the proof of that representation remains a separate task.

Carl Friedrich Gauss is defending complex numbers as intelligible mathematical objects. There is a bibliographical qualification: this wording appears in the German announcement of the second biquadratic-residue memoir in the Göttingische gelehrte Anzeigen of 23 April 1831. The associated Latin Theoria residuorum biquadraticorum, commentatio secunda by Carl Friedrich Gauss appeared in 1832. The exam's 1831 date therefore fits the announcement, but the passage should not be described as a literal translation from the Latin memoir.[ref][ref]
The proposed directional vocabulary is mathematically specific. Represent z=a+bi by the point (a,b) in the complex plane. The addition of complex numbers adds components. Multiplication of complex numbers is
(a+bi)(c+di)=(ac−bd)+(ad+bc)i.
(14)
Multiplication by −1 reverses direction through a half-turn, while multiplication by i sends (a,b) to (−b,a), a quarter-turn. Consequently i2=−1 says that two quarter-turns make a half-turn. More generally the moduli multiply and the complex arguments add. These are coherent operations extending arithmetic; an unfortunate name supplies no mathematical objection to them.
The memoir's setting is number theory, specifically the question of whether a integer congruence x4≡a(modp) is soluble, and the reciprocal relations between such questions. Passing to the Gaussian integers Z[i] provides arithmetic that is closed under addition and multiplication. For example,
5=(2+i)(2−i),N(a+bi)=a2+b2,N(zw)=N(z)N(w).​
(15)
The Gaussian integer norm follows directly by multiplying by the complex conjugate. Here it gives N(2+i)=5 and N(5)=25, consistent with the factorization. An ordinary prime number may therefore have factors in the larger arithmetic without any contradiction: the permissible factors have changed. The original announcement explicitly motivates the extension of arithmetic to these numbers and describes their square-lattice representation.[ref]
It is helpful to separate two domains. Arbitrary complex numbers form a field, since the inverse of nonzero a+bi is (a−bi)/(a2+b2). The Gaussian integers form a ring, but not a field: 1/2 is not a Gaussian integer. Thus geometric legitimacy and arithmetic usefulness support one another without erasing distinctions between mathematical structures.
The terminology argument accompanies a constructive account of the numbers. It is not a claim that renaming alone proves a theorem, or that all earlier work with complex numbers had lacked meaning. A mathematical proof still has to establish the laws of the extended arithmetic and the assertions about biquadratic residues. Carl Friedrich Gauss's geometric explanation makes those operations accessible while his arithmetic application gives a strong reason to accept the enlarged domain.

Heinrich Weber's 1882 paper concerns prime numbers represented by quadratic forms. Its definition abstracts a finite group from the nature of its elements: what matters is a closed binary operation, associativity, and left and right cancellation laws. Here degree h means the number of elements, now called the group order; it is not the number of letters acted on by a permutation group. This matters when relating the passage to earlier work on substitutions. The definition fits the use of groups of classes of quadratic forms, where the elements are already mathematical objects more elaborate than individual permutations.[ref][ref]
The apparent omission of an identity element and inverse elements is not fatal. Every nonempty finite cancellative semigroup is a group. Here is a proof identifying exactly where the assumption of a finite set enters. For fixed a∈G, the maps La​(x)=ax and Ra​(x)=xa are injective by cancellation. An injective function from a finite set to itself is surjective, so choose e with ae=a. For every x,
a(ex)=(ae)x=ax,
(16)
and left cancellation gives ex=x. To show e is also a right identity element, use
(xe)x=x(ex)=xx.
(17)
Right cancellation gives xe=x. Thus the same e works on both sides for every x; it has not merely been chosen as an element fixing a.
Surjectivity now gives b,c with ab=e and ca=e. By associativity,
c=ce=c(ab)=(ca)b=eb=b.
(18)
So b is a two-sided inverse element of a. Since a was arbitrary, the usual group axioms follow. The nonempty hypothesis is tacit in a system of h elements intended to have positive h: the empty system would satisfy the displayed universal conditions vacuously but would have no identity element.
The infinite case exposes the force of the qualification. The positive integers under addition are closed, associative and cancellative, but have no additive identity element. Even the nonnegative integers have an identity element without additive inverse elements for positive elements. The failure is precisely that injective functions of an infinite set to itself need not be surjective. Heinrich Weber's conditions are therefore not an unrestricted replacement for the modern group axioms.
Historically the abstraction is important because it allows arguments to be applied across different kinds of elements. An assumption of commutativity is absent: class-group applications do not make commutativity part of the general definition. For example, the symmetric group S3​ meets the conditions but is noncommutative. The definition also does not require a prior presentation as permutations, although Cayley's theorem provides such a representation. The passage documents the separation of an algebraic structure from any one representation of it, with finiteness carrying logical work that later axiom systems usually assign to explicit identity element and inverse element axioms.

Augustin-Louis Cauchy's importance lies in the interaction of foundations, methods and applications. He made the justification of limits, series and integrals a programme for teaching and research; developed complex analysis into a source of powerful general methods; and contributed to permutation groups, geometry and continuum mechanics. His work is best assessed through particular achievements and their limitations, rather than by treating every theorem now bearing his name as a modern statement he already possessed. His engineering training and subsequent mathematical career also make a rigid separation of pure and applied work historically unhelpful.[ref]
The Cours d'analyse by Augustin-Louis Cauchy (1821) makes convergence a condition requiring argument. A series is studied through its partial sums; a limit describes a variable approaching a fixed value; an infinitesimal is a variable tending to zero, rather than simply a mysterious fixed quantity smaller than every positive number. The introduction explicitly challenges unrestricted transfers of rules from finite expressions to infinite ones. This is not an abandonment of calculation: it seeks to identify when calculations are valid.[ref]
For example, the Cauchy criterion for series asks whether tails can all be made small:
∀ε>0 ∃N ∀m>n≥N:​∑k=n+1m​ak​​<ε.
(19)
This is a modern formulation of the convergence requirement, with the completeness of the real numbers understood. Merely requiring an​→0 is weaker. In the harmonic series, grouping the terms from 2j+1 to 2j+1 gives a block of at least 1/2, so its tails cannot satisfy the criterion. In contrast, the root test compares a tail with a geometric series: if limsup∣an​∣1/n<q<1, then eventually ∣an​∣≤qn, which proves absolute convergence. Such arguments explain the usefulness of convergence tests instead of reducing Augustin-Louis Cauchy's contribution to a vocabulary of definitions.
His Résumé des leçons sur le calcul infinitésimal by Augustin-Louis Cauchy (1823) extends the programme to differentiation and integration. For a continuous function on a compact interval, an integral can be approached through sums of values times subinterval lengths. A modern justification uses uniform continuity: for a partition of mesh at most δ, two choices of sample points give sums differing by at most (b−a)ωf​(δ), where the modulus of continuity ωf​(δ) tends to zero. Refinement then establishes the common limit. This reconstruction makes clear why control of error, rather than a geometrical picture of area alone, is decisive. It does not attribute the later full Riemann integral theory for discontinuous functions to the 1823 text.[ref]
The achievement has limits. The 1821 Sum theorem of Cauchy asserts continuity of sums of convergent series of continuous functions in terms that, under a pointwise reading, are insufficient. The original proof treats the tail as negligible without establishing the uniform control needed near the point concerned. A modern diagnostic is fn​(x)=xn on [0,1]: every fn​ is continuous, but its pointwise limit is zero for x<1 and one at 1. Thus a proof must distinguish pointwise convergence from uniform convergence. Augustin-Louis Cauchy's use of variable and infinitesimal language deserves historical interpretation, but it does not license silently importing modern hypotheses into his printed theorem.[ref]
In complex analysis, the Mémoire sur les intégrales définies, prises entre des limites imaginaires by Augustin-Louis Cauchy (1825) exemplifies a new productive setting for integration. A contour integral depends on a path in the complex plane; deforming that path and accounting for isolated singularities can evaluate otherwise difficult definite integrals. In modern form, Cauchy's integral theorem applies to a holomorphic function on a suitable domain and Cauchy integral formula states, for a circle enclosing z and lying with its interior in that domain,
f(z)=2πi1​∫γ​ζ−zf(ζ)​dζ.​
(20)
The importance is structural: local values and derivatives are determined by data on a surrounding path. Historical proofs and hypotheses should be distinguished from later general forms of the theorem.[ref]
A short modern consequence measures the power of this method. For an entire function bounded by M, the derivative version of Cauchy integral formula on a circle of radius R gives ∣f′(z)∣≤M/R. Since R is arbitrary, f′=0, proving Liouville's theorem. If a nonconstant polynomial P had no root, then 1/P would be an entire function, bounded outside a large disc by the leading term and inside the disc by compactness. Liouville's theorem would force it to be constant, a contradiction. This proves the fundamental theorem of algebra. The chain is an assessment of the reach of the methods, not a claim that Augustin-Louis Cauchy alone originated every named result.
Augustin-Louis Cauchy's algebra concerns concrete permutations as well as analytical formulas. His 1845 investigations developed the theory of substitutions; Cauchy theorem for groups says that a prime number dividing a finite group order occurs as an element order. One can appreciate its generality from a modern argument: rotate the coordinates of the tuples (g1​,…,gp​) satisfying g1​⋯gp​=e. Rotation preserves this condition, since g2​⋯gp​g1​=g1−1​(g1​⋯gp​)g1​=e. There are ∣G∣p−1 such tuples; nonfixed rotation orbits have p elements, and fixed tuples correspond to gp=e. When p∣∣G∣, the number of fixed tuples is divisible by p, so besides e there is an element of element order p. This is explanatory mathematics, not Augustin-Louis Cauchy's original proof. The dating itself needs care: Peter Michael Neumann's archival argument places the major burst of work in 1845 despite an 1844 date traditionally attached to a volume.[ref]
Finally, Augustin-Louis Cauchy's work on elasticity shows the strength of making a local mathematical object out of physical interactions. Instead of describing only a force perpendicular to a surface, one studies the traction on a plane through a point, with its direction dependent on the plane's unit normal. In modern notation the relation is t(n)=σn, where the Cauchy stress tensor contains the necessary directional information. Together with balance of linear momentum, this yields equations for the internal motion of a body. The modern tensor notation is later explanatory language; the conceptual contribution is the reduction of the forces on arbitrary internal planes to a finite collection of local components.[ref]
Augustin-Louis Cauchy's lasting contribution was a powerful research programme, not a finished foundation. His insistence on justified passage to a limit reshaped analysis, while complex integration, substitutions and local stresses opened methods whose value extended beyond their first applications. The inadequacy of a pointwise convergence hypothesis, the need to state domains in complex analysis, and the later construction of the real numbers show that nineteenth-century rigour remained a collective and revisable achievement. These qualifications strengthen an assessment of his influence by identifying what later mathematicians could extend and repair.

Between 1600 and 1900, algebra broadened from arts of working with unknown quantities and solving equations into the study of several systems of symbolic operations and increasingly general algebraic structures. This was neither a single change of definition nor a clean replacement of one subject by another. Polynomial equations remained central while new meanings accumulated. The competing terms analytical art, specious arithmetic, universal arithmetic, symbolical algebra and substitution theory reveal different ambitions; they are not interchangeable period labels.
Near 1600, François Viète's In artem analyticem isagoge by François Viète (1591) supplies a particularly revealing starting point. His analytical art uses letters for known quantities as well as unknown ones. In his terminology, logistica numerosa concerns numerical calculation and logistica speciosa calculation with species, or general magnitudes. Vowels represent unknowns and consonants known magnitudes. This allows a single relation to represent many particular problems, but it is still tied to geometrical homogeneity: a square and a length do not simply become quantities of the same kind because they are written with letters.[ref][ref]
For example, a relation we write as A2+BA=C has quantities of compatible dimensions if A and B are lengths and C is an area. Letters do more than abbreviate a numerical question: they express relations between whole families of magnitudes. The phrase specious arithmetic therefore indicates arithmetic carried out on species; analysis stresses the art of reasoning from an assumed unknown towards its determination. Neither term should be reduced to the modern subject real analysis.
René Descartes's La Géométrie by René Descartes (1637) demonstrates another relation between algebra and geometry. A chosen unit segment lets multiplication, division and extraction of square roots be represented by constructions of lengths. The product of two measured lengths can itself be represented by a length relative to that unit. In modern dimensional notation the construction gives ab/u for unit length u, removing the obstacle that a product must always be treated as an area. The text's equations can then organise geometrical problems and classify curves by their algebraic complexity. Its title remains geometry: algebra serves a geometrical programme rather than announcing a present-day department of abstract algebra.[ref]
A further term, universal arithmetic, stresses the extension of calculation from particular numbers to general quantities. Isaac Newton's Arithmetica universalis by Isaac Newton, issued in 1707, makes the term visible in a title whose subtitle concerns composition and resolution. A title is evidence for the vocabulary, not a sufficient definition of all contemporary usage.[ref] In Leonhard Euler's Elements of Algebra by Leonhard Euler, published in German in 1770, general numerical calculation and the solution of equations remain a substantial conception of the subject. For instance, allowing negative and complex roots changes the admissible answers to polynomial equations while preserving the central question of determining an unknown.[ref]
The eighteenth-century elimination theory exemplified by Alexandre-Théophile Vandermonde's memoir broadens the scale of the task. Instead of one numerical quadratic equation, one seeks a rule for an arbitrary number of simultaneous linear equations. The determinant is initially valuable because it effects that elimination. This is a different generality from the later study of a group: the objects being determined are still unknown quantities, and symbolic generality concerns the number and coefficients of the equations. The case cautions against using generality alone as a test for whether algebra has become abstract.[ref]
In nineteenth-century British discussion, George Peacock's A Treatise on Algebra by George Peacock (1830) distinguishes arithmetical algebra from symbolical algebra. If the symbols mean positive numbers, a−(a+b) has no positive-number result. Yet algebraists manipulate this expression without restriction. George Peacock therefore seeks principles for the manipulation of signs which are not confined by their original arithmetical interpretation. The contrast is explicitly argued in his preface: arithmetic suggests the laws, while the symbolic science requires foundations adequate to its more general conclusions.[ref]
The familiar extension (−1)(−1)=1 illustrates the stakes. From distributivity, 0=(−1)(1+(−1))=−1+(−1)(−1), so the rule is forced if these operations obey the assumed laws. This explains how a law can survive a change in interpretation. It does not establish that every familiar law must survive every extension: once the allowable systems themselves change, an argument must state which laws it assumes. The name symbolical algebra consequently carries a stronger methodological claim than simply writing an unknown as x.
William Rowan Hamilton's work on quaternions supplies a decisive example of that change. His publications of the 1840s describe a new system of imaginaries in algebra. With quaternion units,
i2=j2=k2=−1,ij=k,ji=−k.​
(21)
Thus multiplication need not satisfy the commutativity even when it remains associative and supports division by nonzero elements. The new system is not merely a further list of roots of old polynomial equations. It has its own operations, interpretation and laws. William Rowan Hamilton's title makes explicit that a plurality of calculi can belong within algebra.[ref]
George Boole's An Investigation of the Laws of Thought by George Boole (1854) offers a different interpretation of symbols. If x represents selection of a class, repeating that selection gives x2=x; multiplication can represent intersection and 1−x a complementary class. His logical calculus borrows from numerical algebra but also restricts interpretation: his sum of classes is not automatically unrestricted set union, and intermediate symbolic expressions need not name classes directly. It would therefore be inaccurate to identify all of his rules with a modern Boolean algebra. The example nevertheless shows why algebraic reasoning could extend beyond magnitudes.[ref]
The shift can also take a concrete linear form. Arthur Cayley's A Memoir on the Theory of Matrices by Arthur Cayley (1858) treats matrices with operations of their own. The identity matrix is no longer the scalar 1, though it plays an analogous role; matrix multiplication is generally noncommutative. For example,
A=(00​10​),B=(01​00​)
(22)
produce AB=diag(1,0) and BA=diag(0,1). This is a checkable reason why universal arithmetic can no longer mean that every rule of ordinary numbers applies unchanged.[ref]
Meanwhile, substitution theory studies permutations and their compositions, originally in close relation to polynomial equations. Évariste Galois's work connects the solvability by radicals of an equation with its associated group. Augustin-Louis Cauchy's work on substitutions and Heinrich Weber's 1882 definition illustrate successive forms of generalisation: from particular permutations towards arbitrary elements satisfying operational conditions. This is not a claim that one man supplied the complete modern hierarchy of groups, rings and fields at once. It identifies the change in the question: one can now investigate what follows from the laws of an operation, independently of whether the elements are numbers, substitutions or classes.[ref]
By 1900, algebra could concern the laws and structures of several calculi. Earlier meanings persisted in teaching and in the theory of equations. The strongest account of the change therefore combines continuity with enlargement: letters for general quantities, symbolic operations with explicit laws, nonnumerical interpretations, and structural questions about those laws. Analytical art foregrounded a method of discovery, universal arithmetic foregrounded general calculation, symbolical algebra foregrounded formal operations, and substitution theory foregrounded a particular class of transformations. Their differing uses help explain the expansion more precisely than a single date for the birth of abstraction.

Standards of mathematical proof changed between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries because both the objects studied and the operations performed on them changed. A finite geometrical construction, an infinite expansion, and an abstract group raise different questions about justification. There was no universal seventeenth-century indifference to rigour followed by its sudden nineteenth-century invention. The more defensible thesis is that successful methods repeatedly exposed unstated assumptions; later work made some of those assumptions into explicit definitions, hypotheses and existence arguments.
Pierre de Fermat's adequality illustrates the distinction between an effective method and its justification. For F(a)=a(L−a), comparing neighbouring expressions gives
F(a+e)−F(a)=e(L−2a)−e2.
(23)
Dividing by e and suppressing the remaining term in e produces a=L/2. There is an independent finite verification:
F(a)=4L2​−(a−2L​)2≤4L2​.​
(24)
Thus the result can be secure even when the general procedure raises a question: why is the increment first nonzero for division and then discarded? In the parabola construction of question 2, the same issue appears in replacing a strict geometrical comparison by adequality and extracting the subtangent. A historical explanation must describe this operation on its own terms rather than silently equate it with a modern derivative.[ref]
Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz supplied different languages for the new calculus. Isaac Newton's Introduction to the Quadrature of Curves by Isaac Newton, first published with the 1704 Opticks, presents quantities generated by motion and considers their rates of change. Its mature language of first and last ratios seeks exact relations as increments vanish; it should not be confused with simply asserting that a small error is harmless. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz's 1684 Nova methodus pro maximis et minimis by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz presents a calculus of differentials and operational rules. Their common problem was to explain why procedures using vanishing increments establish exact results, while their chosen objects and modes of explanation differed.[ref][ref]
George Berkeley's The Analyst by George Berkeley (1734) exposes the logical pressure point. For the cube,
h(x+h)3−x3​=3x2+3xh+h2(h=0).
(25)
The finite identity does not become a legal division at h=0. The modern repair is to keep h=0 throughout the identity and prove that its right-hand side tends to 3x2. That limit is different from a quotient evaluated at zero. George Berkeley's criticism therefore concerns the grounds of inference, not whether the established rules often produce correct answers. His theological polemic also reminds us that standards of mathematical proof could be contested outside specialist calculation.[ref]
The infinite introduced another burden. Joseph Fourier's 1822 use of trigonometric series to represent arbitrary functions greatly extended what calculations in mathematical physics could express. But deriving Fourier coefficients by orthogonality only shows what those coefficients must be if the proposed operations are legitimate. It does not prove convergence, equality with the initial data, or interchange of an integral and an infinite sum. The example of a constant expanded in a Fourier sine series makes a hidden qualification visible: the terms all vanish at the endpoints, even when the assigned data do not. The correct theorem must specify the domain and mode of representation.[ref]
Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet's 1829 memoir supplies a particularly precise example of changing proof obligations. It criticises a convergence argument of Augustin-Louis Cauchy's which used complex substitution into an arbitrary function and an insufficient comparison between signed series. Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet supplies the instructive pair
an​=n​(−1)n​,bn​=n​(−1)n​+n1​.
(26)
The first series converges by the alternating series test; the second diverges because their difference is the harmonic series. Nevertheless bn​/an​=1+(−1)n/n​→1. Thus asymptotic equality of terms does not transfer convergence between conditionally convergent signed series. A comparison principle valid for positive terms needs additional justification when cancellation is involved. The example refutes a mode of inference rather than merely correcting a numerical answer.[ref]
Augustin-Louis Cauchy's own foundations programme is part of this change. The 1821 Cours d'analyse by Augustin-Louis Cauchy makes limits, continuity and series convergence central to elementary reasoning. Yet its Sum theorem of Cauchy also shows why announcing rigour does not make every proof adequate. In modern terminology, continuous functions can have a discontinuous pointwise limit. An explicit series version is
∑n=1∞​(xn−xn+1)={x0​0≤x<1,x=1.​
(27)
Each term is a polynomial and the partial sum is x−xN+1. The convergence does not hold in uniform convergence: with xN​=1−1/N, the error xNN+1​ tends to e−1 rather than zero. The modern uniform limit theorem repairs the inference by requiring one bound on the error to work throughout the domain. This example diagnoses the missing control under a pointwise reading; it does not settle every historical interpretation of Augustin-Louis Cauchy's infinitesimal language.[ref]
The issue is the order of dependence in quantifiers. Pointwise convergence permits the threshold N to depend on both the error tolerance and the point. Uniform convergence requires a threshold depending only on the tolerance. If fN​ is continuous near x0​ and the convergence is uniform, choose N so that ∣f−fN​∣<ε/3 nearby, then use the continuity of this fixed fN​ to make ∣fN​(x)−fN​(x0​)∣<ε/3. The triangle inequality proves ∣f(x)−f(x0​)∣<ε. Writing out this proof reveals what a vague statement that the tail is small leaves unproved.
Another nineteenth-century development concerns the foundations of the real numbers themselves. Richard Dedekind's Continuity and Irrational Numbers by Richard Dedekind (1872) asks for an arithmetical account of the continuity often borrowed from a drawn line. A Dedekind cut separates the rational numbers into lower and upper classes. For instance,
A={q∈Q:q<0 or q2<2},B=Q∖A
(28)
defines a gap in the rational numbers corresponding to 2​. The cut is determined without assuming that a rational number fills it. The extended number system then supports existence theorems that a geometrical picture alone had suggested.[ref]
This matters for apparently elementary proofs. Suppose f is continuous on [a,b] with f(a)<0<f(b). Repeated bisection constructs nested intervals whose endpoint values have opposite signs and whose lengths tend to zero. To conclude that there is a common limiting point, one needs the completeness of the real numbers. Continuity then forces its value to be zero. Over the rational numbers, f(q)=q2−2 changes sign between 1 and 2 but has no rational zero. Thus even a proof that avoids pictures must explain the existence of its limiting object. Richard Dedekind's account makes that requirement an explicit part of the mathematical setting, rather than an intuition about a continuous line.[ref]
There is a parallel change in algebra. Heinrich Weber's 1882 definition in question 6 describes arbitrary elements through laws of composition. To assess such a definition one proves implications between axioms. The passage's finite set hypothesis implies that cancellative multiplication has an identity element and inverse elements; without that hypothesis, the positive integers under addition supply a counterexample. This is a new kind of proof obligation: one must establish that a proposed abstract definition has the intended consequences, rather than rely on their familiarity in particular numerical or permutation examples.[ref]
The principal change was towards explicit control of operations, hypotheses and existence. Effective symbolic algorithms persisted, and geometric reasoning remained valuable. What became increasingly demanding was the passage from success in examples to a general claim: an infinitesimal step needed justification, an infinite expansion needed a convergence theorem, a limiting point needed a complete number system, and an abstract definition needed its consequences proved. The examples also prevent a simple progress story: nineteenth-century mathematicians disagreed about admissible reasoning, and a programme devoted to rigour could itself become the object of a successful critique.
