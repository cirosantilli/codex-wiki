Alexandre-Théophile Vandermonde presents a programme in elimination theory: remove unknowns from simultaneous polynomial equations in a way that works uniformly as the number of equations and their polynomial degrees vary. The immediate achievement is narrower than that programme: a general construction for linear equations. The academy year 1772 used in the exam is not the printing date of the memoir, which appeared in 1776. Its compact indices distinguished the equation containing a coefficient from the unknown it multiplied; they were not powers. That notational separation is crucial to interpreting the work.[ref][ref]

Each product selects one coefficient from each equation and each unknown's column. The sign of a permutation determines whether it is added or subtracted. This makes the number n a genuine parameter of a general rule, rather than merely supplying separately memorised formulas for two or three unknowns. The notation here is modern, not a transcription of Alexandre-Théophile Vandermonde's symbols.
For two unknowns, multiply ax+by=u by d and cx+dy=v by b, then subtract. Similarly eliminate x. This gives

An original verification uses the multilinearity of the determinant: replace b by the column combination ∑k​xk​Ak​. Every resulting determinant vanishes through repeated columns except the k=j term, which is xj​detA. This explains how an elimination expression isolates one unknown.
The condition D=0 is substantive. If D=0, multiplying by an expression which vanishes cannot justify division. For instance, x+y=1 and 2x+2y=3 are inconsistent, whereas replacing 3 by 2 gives infinitely many solutions. Thus an elimination formula must distinguish exceptional coefficients and preserve the connection to the original system of linear equations. Nor does the displayed rule solve the much broader problem of arbitrary nonlinear equations.
The passage's importance is the move from particular elimination calculations towards a theory of alternating expressions. It would be misleading to turn this into the claim that the familiar Vandermonde determinant, formed from powers of variables, is what this extract displays. A positional upper index and an exponent have different meanings; the history of that eponym requires separate evidence. Likewise, modern matrix algebra is a useful explanatory language here, not the institutional or conceptual setting of an eighteenth-century memoir.[ref]
