Joseph Fourier's Théorie analytique de la chaleur by Joseph Fourier (1822) makes trigonometric series instruments for solving problems of heat conduction. A prescribed temperature distribution need not be given by one power series: it may describe different heating on different parts of a body. The extract occurs in Chapter III, §233, where the earlier assumption of an odd-power expansion is relaxed. The innovation concerns both representation and what can count as a function in an analytical calculation.[ref]
Use a separate integration variable t to unpack the printed notation. The proposed Fourier sine series has coefficients
bn​=π2​∫0π​f(t)sin(nt)dt,f(x)∼n=1∑∞​bn​sin(nx).​
(10)
The integral signs in the extract denote definite integrals over [0,π], not antiderivatives; the author explicitly supplies these bounds in the surrounding sentence. Their absent printed bounds are a notational convention, not permission to choose arbitrary constants of integration.
The key calculation is the orthogonality relation
∫0π​sin(nt)sin(mt)dt={π/20​n=m,n=m.​
(11)
The product-to-sum formulas prove this by integrating cosines of integer multiples of t. If an expansion can be integrated term by term, multiplication by sin(mt) followed by integration selects exactly one coefficient. This derives the only possible coefficients, but does not by itself prove the expansion converges to the proposed function. That distinction is central to assessing the extract's claim.
For a revealing example take f(x)=1 on 0<x<π. Direct integration gives bn​=2(1−(−1)n)/(πn), and therefore
1=π4​(sinx+3sin3x​+5sin5x​+⋯),0<x<π.
(12)
Every term is zero at 0 and π, so the series cannot take the value 1 at those endpoints. Its natural interpretation uses the odd extension followed by periodic extension; the endpoints become jump discontinuities. More generally, for a piecewise continuously differentiable function, the Fourier series converges at an interior jump to the average of the two one-sided limits, irrespective of an individually assigned value there. Changing one value also leaves the integrals unchanged. Consequently the unqualified assertion of representation for every arbitrary function cannot be a modern pointwise theorem. Dirichlet's theorem on Fourier series supplies sufficient conditions and the correct distinction between continuity points and jumps; its 1829 source explicitly addresses the proof problem.[ref]
The physical payoff is explicit. For a rod scaled to 0<x<π with zero end temperature, separation of variables in the heat equation produces
u(x,τ)=∑n=1∞​bn​e−κn2τsin(nx),κ>0.
(13)
Each mode solves uτ​=κuxx​ and the end boundary conditions; the Fourier sine coefficients encode the initial temperature. This rod is an explanatory example, distinct from the stationary rectangular-solid problem opening Joseph Fourier's Chapter III. The factor e−κn2τ damps high modes rapidly for positive time. The representation of arbitrary data makes the physical solution possible; the proof of that representation remains a separate task.
