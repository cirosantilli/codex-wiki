Factor base

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Number theory Integer factorization
OurBigBook.com Words: 410 Articles: 2
A factor base is a finite set of small prime numbers over which selected auxiliary integers are completely factored. Some methods also record a sign coordinate for −1, which is not a prime number. A positive integer supported entirely on the chosen factor base gives an exponent vector; reducing that vector modulo two records which prime exponents are odd integers. The product is a square of a number precisely when every prime exponent is an even integer.
For the original toy N=77, use factor base {2,3,5} and the two relations
132≡15=3⋅5(mod77),402≡60=22⋅3⋅5(mod77).
(1)
The residues have exponent vectors (0,1,1) and (2,1,1) in the order (2,3,5). Adding the two vectors modulo two gives (0,0,0), so multiplying the residues gives 15⋅60=900=302. Consequently
(13⋅40)2≡302(mod77),X=13⋅40mod77=58,Y=30,
(2)
and gcd(X−Y,77)=7 and gcd(X+Y,77)=11. Neither individual residue was a square; combining the relations made all exponents even.

Finding such a selection is a linear dependence problem over the finite field F2​: the indicator vector of the chosen relations belongs to the kernel of a linear map given by the parity matrix. This is why a problem about prime factors recruits linear algebra. The final congruence of squares may still be trivial, so another selection can be needed. [ref].
There is a tradeoff: a small factor base gives a smaller parity matrix, but fewer auxiliary integers factor completely over it. A larger factor base accepts more relations but makes the linear algebra larger. Negative auxiliary values need an additional sign coordinate, and a selected product needs even sign parity as well as even prime exponents. Pomerance's account explains this balance and the historical development of combining smooth relations; the toy example omits algorithm-specific sieving and running-time claims. [ref].
