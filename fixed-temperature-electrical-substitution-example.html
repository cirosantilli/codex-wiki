Fixed-temperature electrical substitution example (Pin​=(2−1.005)/0.995mW=1mW)

Stipulate a model receiver held at T=5.4K with a reference at Tb​=5.0K. Choose G=5mWK−1, so outward thermal conduction is G(T−Tb​)=2mW. Ignore other heat transfer, take Ppar​=0 in both states, and stipulate perfect optical/electrical thermal equivalence. These values specify an original example; they are not measurements or claimed performance of a NIST instrument.
With the beam blocked, supply Ph,d​=2mW. With light admitted, the required heater power is Ph,l​=1.005mW. For stipulated radiometric absorptance a=0.995 at the chosen single wavelength,
Pabs​=2−1.005=0.995mW,Pin​=0.9950.995mW​=1.000mW.
(32)
The illuminated receiver still gets 1.005+0.995=2mW of heating. The remaining 0.005mW of incident radiant flux escapes without heating it. Calling the measured heater reduction “incident power” would underestimate the stipulated incident radiant flux by 0.5%.
Two variations expose different pitfalls. First, if admitting the beam also adds 0.010mW of stray heating while the same 0.995mW is absorbed from the intended beam, the heater falls to 0.995mW. The apparent reduction is now 1.005mW; subtracting ΔPpar​=0.010mW recovers the beam's absorbed power. Second, keep the original heater readings but stipulate a light-state receiver offset δT=+1mK at fixed reference temperature. The link carries an extra GδT=0.005mW; the inferred absorbed power becomes 1.000mW, and incident radiant flux becomes 1.000/0.995≃1.005025mW. This second variation deliberately violates exact fixed-temperature substitution.
The heater can supply only nonnegative power in this model. Consequently an absorbed beam above the available dark heater power cannot be compensated at the chosen setpoint merely by reducing the heater. The operating point, link, or admitted radiant flux must change; zero heater demand is a range boundary, not evidence of greater radiant flux sensitivity.
