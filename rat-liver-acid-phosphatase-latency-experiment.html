Rat-liver acid-phosphatase latency experiment

 Home Biology Biological cell Organelle Lysosome Lysosomal enzyme latency
OurBigBook.com Words: 211
In the 1951 study by Jacques Berthet and Christian de Duve, liver from fasted rats was immediately chilled in an ice-cold isotonic chemical solution. The tissue was generally pressed through wire mesh; the weighed pulp was homogenized in cold 0.25molL−1 sucrose using a glass Potter-Elvehjem homogenizer. Their acid phosphatase test concerned activity toward added glycerophosphate at pH 5: the readout was substrate hydrolysis, rather than an image of a particle.[ref]
Christian de Duve's retrospective describes successive centrifugation steps, separating centrifugation pellets and supernatants, and comparing enzyme distributions across the recovered fractions. Membrane disruption exposed previously inaccessible acid phosphatase activity, providing the crucial comparison with the membrane-preserving preparation. Joint fractionation patterns and release behavior of several acid hydrolases supported a shared population of digestive particles. By 1955, this evidence supported the lysosome concept.[ref]
The inference was biochemical: a barrier restricted access to active enzymes. Co-sedimentation could confuse distinct particles; a crude mitochondrial fraction did not establish residence inside mitochondria. Lysosomal enzyme latency neither images an organelle nor determines its shape. Electron microscopy of enriched fractions supplied separate morphological evidence.[ref]
