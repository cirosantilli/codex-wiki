Pascal's identity is
(kn​)=(kn−1​)+(k−1n−1​).
(11)
For an integer n≥1 and any integer k, Pascal's rule holds with the convention (kn​)=0 outside 0≤k≤n. The boundary values are (0n​)=(nn​)=1, including (00​)=1. Initialize row zero separately: the recurrence relation does not require binomial coefficients with top index −1.[ref]
Proof by a marked element. Count the k-element subsets of {1,…,n}, marking the element n as special. Those omitting n are exactly the k-element subsets of {1,…,n−1}. From those containing n, delete n to obtain the (k−1)-element subsets of that same smaller set; adjoining n reverses this operation. The rule of sum adds these two disjoint counts. The proof includes k=0 and k=n because the impossible class contributes zero.
For an original worked instance, choose three labels from {1,2,3,4,5,6,7} and mark 7. Omitting it gives (36​)=20 selections. Including it leaves two labels to choose from the other six, giving (26​)=15. Therefore (37​)=20+15=35. Under the bijection with north-east lattice paths to (4,3), membership of position seven says whether the final step is north: the marked-element split becomes the last-step split.
This makes Pascal's rule useful beyond an algebraic identity. A counting problem may have an obvious yes-or-no decision even when its factorial formula is obscure; splitting at that decision exposes a recurrence relation. When the smaller counts are shared between many cases, dynamic programming reuses them. The beauty of Pascal's triangle is that one local addition rule simultaneously organizes subsets, binary strings, north-east lattice paths, and the binomial theorem.
