Ellipsoid level sets of a positive-definite quadratic form (q(x)=k>0,rj​=k/λj​​)

 ... Algebra Linear algebra Bilinear form Symmetric bilinear form Positive-definite bilinear form Positive definiteness
OurBigBook.com Words: 189
For a real positive-definite matrix A in finite dimension, write A=QΛQT by the spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices. In the coordinates in an orthonormal basis w=QTx, the positive level set q(x)=k becomes
∑j=1n​λj​wj2​=k,∑j=1n​k/λj​wj2​​=1.
(13)
This is an ellipsoid, with semi-major and semi-minor axes in two dimensions forming an ellipse; each semi-axis has length rj​=k/λj​​ and direction given by the corresponding eigenvector. In the opening example, the shorter semi-axis is k​/2 along e+​, while the longer is k/2​ along e−​. A stiffer direction, meaning a larger eigenvalue, reaches the same energy at a smaller displacement. The ratio of longest to shortest semi-axis is λmax​/λmin​​, the square root of the spectral condition number of a positive-definite matrix.[ref]
Strict positivity matters because λmin​>0 gives
λmin​∥x∥2≤q(x)≤λmax​∥x∥2.
(14)
Hence a fixed positive level set cannot extend arbitrarily far. The Cholesky decomposition A=LLT gives another view: q(x)=∥LTx∥2, so the ellipsoid is the inverse image of a sphere under an invertible linear map. A singular positive semidefinite matrix has a nonzero kernel; adding a kernel vector preserves its quadratic form, so any nonempty positive level set is unbounded. These statements concern finite dimension; the positive definite symmetric operator entry explains why strict positivity alone need not give a uniform lower bound on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space.
