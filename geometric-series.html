Geometric series (∑n=0∞​arn)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Sequence and series
OurBigBook.com Words: 799 Articles: 5
A geometric series adds the terms of a geometric progression:
a+ar+ar2+⋯=∑n=0∞​arn.
(120)
Its ordinary infinite sum is the limit of a sequence of partial sums SN​=∑j=0N−1​arj, rather than a new operation that adds infinitely many terms at once. For a,r that are real numbers or complex numbers, if ∣r∣<1, the series is absolutely convergent and
n=0∑∞​arn=1−ra​.​
(121)
When a=0, this condition on the modulus of r is also necessary: for ∣r∣≥1 the terms fail to tend to zero. The all-zero series a=0 converges for every r. The finite geometric series identity and rN→0 give the result with almost no extra algebra. [ref]
With a=1 and r=1/2, the partial sums approach 2 from below. Changing the common ratio to −1/2 makes the partial sums approach 2/3 from alternating sides: their increments still shrink. At r=−1, those increments never shrink, and the partial sums remain 0,1,0,1,…. Substituting r=−1 into 1/(1−r) does not supply the ordinary sum of that divergent series.
The formula is useful because many apparently different problems share a fixed multiplier. A repeating decimal such as 0.27 is
10027​+100227​+⋯=1−1/10027/100​=113​.
(122)
A geometric distribution counting failures before the first success normalizes by the same identity: for 0<q<1, the probabilities (1−q)qn on n=0,1,… add to one. The comparison test for series uses a bound by a decaying geometric progression to establish absolute convergence without an exact formula for the series being studied. The iterated contraction gives a summable orbit result uses precisely this idea for repeated applications of a map.
The remainder of a geometric series gives more than an eventual limit: it states exactly how much an approximation omits. A common ratio close to one still gives a convergent series, but can require many terms for a useful approximation.
