Sanger's insulin sequencing experiment

 Home Biology Protein Protein structure Protein primary structure
OurBigBook.com Words: 513
The motivating question was experimental: how could one recover the order in a protein when breaking all its peptide bonds destroys that order? Frederick Sanger and colleagues used complementary tests on portions of insulin. The following is an explanation of their evidence, not a laboratory recipe.
First, mark ends before destroying the chains. Sanger used the colored reagent fluorodinitrobenzene, abbreviated FDNB, to label free amino groups. After acid hydrolysis, the attached label remained on the end-residue products. Separating these derivatives by their different movement through solvents and comparing them with reference derivatives made the labels readable. The N-terminus products identified glycine and phenylalanine. Sanger emphasized that finding a stable label and a way to separate its products were both essential. See Sanger, Citation Classic (1985), discussing The free amino groups of insulin (1945). FDNB also labels suitable free amino groups on amino acid side chains, so a labeled product is not automatically an N-terminus product. See Sanger, The Chemistry of Insulin, Nobel lecture (1958), pp. 546–547.
Next, separate chains and preserve local order. Oxidation with performic acid broke the protein disulfide bonds, allowing A and B chains to be isolated. Partial hydrolysis and enzyme treatments produced fragments; these were separated and their composition and local sequences characterized. Overlaps between different fragment sets linked smaller sequences into longer ones. Many more fragments than minimally necessary were checked for consistency. See Sanger's authored Nobel lecture, pp. 547–552: chain separation, fragment analysis and sequence reconstruction.
The toy fragments make the logic visible. Suppose one cut pattern yields MAG and AMG, and another yields MA, GA and MG. The first set alone permits MAGAMG or AMGMAG. The fragment GA in the second set crosses the MAG–AMG boundary in MAGAMG; it does not occur in AMGMAG. Among concatenations of the first set, therefore, the second set selects MAGAMG. This example assumes intact, correctly identified fragments from one homogeneous chain and known fragment membership in each cut pattern. Repeated stretches, missing fragments or sample mixtures can leave ambiguities; the picture illustrates inference from overlaps, not a universal guarantee of reconstruction.
Finally, determine the protein disulfide bonds separately. Oxidation used to separate whole chains erases their original connections. Sanger and colleagues instead studied fragments still joined by protein disulfide bonds, then oxidized the isolated joined fragments to identify their component pieces and locate the connected chain regions. An important trap was rearrangement of disulfide bonds during treatment: products can acquire connections absent from the starting protein. Their work therefore had to establish conditions that prevented rearrangement before interpreting a linked fragment as evidence. See Ryle and Sanger, A Disulphide Interchange Reaction, Nature 171, 1025–1026 (1953) and Sanger's Nobel lecture, pp. 553–554: preserving and identifying disulfide connections. The result was an experimentally constrained chemical structure rather than a composition list or a photograph of a fold.
