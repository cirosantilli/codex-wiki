Definite integral (∫ab​f(x)dx)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Calculus Integral
OurBigBook.com Words: 542 Articles: 2
A definite integral accumulates an integrand between specified endpoints, retaining the sign of each contribution. For a real-valued function on a finite real interval, a finite definite integral is a scalar; an indefinite integral describes a family of antiderivatives. The lower and upper limits of integration a,b specify where accumulation starts and ends. The integration variable is a placeholder: ∫ab​f(x)dx=∫ab​f(u)du.
This is useful whenever a varying rate must be converted into a total change. For motion along a straight line with continuous velocity v(t) and position s(t) satisfying s′(t)=v(t), the fundamental theorem of calculus gives displacement, while accumulating speed gives the distance traveled from velocity:
s(b)−s(a)=∫ab​v(t)dt,D=∫ab​∣v(t)∣dt,a<b.
(10)
Negative velocity reduces displacement while adding to the distance traveled from velocity. The physical dimension of a definite integral is the product of the integrand's physical dimension and the integration variable's physical dimension: here (m/s)s=m. An area on a rate graph need not be a spatial area.[ref]
As an explicitly synthetic motion model, let v(t)=(2ms−2)t−2ms−1 for 0≤t≤3s, with s(0)=0. Using numerical values in seconds and metres, an antiderivative gives s(t)=t2−2t. The velocity changes sign at t=1: the model first moves 1 m in the negative direction, then 4 m in the positive direction. Its displacement is −1+4=3 m, and its distance traveled from velocity is 1+4=5 m. The initial position fixes the constant of integration, but does not change either accumulated quantity.
Orientation matters independently of whether the integrand is positive or negative. For a function integrable on the interval containing the endpoints, use the conventions
∫aa​f(x)dx=0,∫ba​f(x)dx=−∫ab​f(x)dx,∫ac​f(x)dx=∫ab​f(x)dx+∫bc​f(x)dx.
(11)
For the preceding velocity model, accumulation from 3 seconds back to 0 seconds gives −3 m. Reversing the bounds of the speed integral gives −5 m, which is an oriented integral, not a negative distance traveled from velocity; physical elapsed-time totals use the earlier endpoint first. The splitting rule holds even when b is outside the interval between a and c, provided integrability holds on the interval containing all three endpoints.[ref]
Existence needs hypotheses. For a bounded function f on [a,b] with a<b, a finite Riemann integral exists when every tagged Riemann sum approaches the same value as the largest cell width tends to zero:
∫ab​f(x)dx=limmaxi​(xi​−xi−1​)→0​∑i=1n​f(ξi​)(xi​−xi−1​),a=x0​<⋯<xn​=b,ξi​∈[xi−1​,xi​].
(12)
The limit must be independent of both the partition of an interval and the tags. Merely making the number of cells large can leave a wide cell unresolved. On a finite closed interval, every continuous function, and every bounded function that is continuous except at finitely many points, is Riemann integrable.[ref] Boundedness alone is insufficient: the Dirichlet function on [0,1] is 1 on the rational numbers and 0 on the irrational numbers. Tags can all be chosen rational or all irrational, giving Riemann sums of 1 or 0 at arbitrarily small cell widths.[ref]
For a finite signed Lebesgue integral, the condition is instead that f be a measurable function for Lebesgue measure and ∫[a,b]​∣f∣dx<∞. A measurable function for Lebesgue measure that is also a bounded function on a finite interval satisfies this condition since ∣f∣≤M gives ∫∣f∣≤M(b−a). The Dirichlet function therefore has Lebesgue integral zero: the rational numbers form a null set, so it equals zero Lebesgue almost everywhere. The Riemann integral and Lebesgue integral agree for the continuous functions used in the diagrams.[ref] An unbounded integrand or infinite endpoint requires further analysis, for example the limits defining an improper integral; the notation alone does not establish convergence.
