Use the Gibbs-Duhem equation for a homogeneous, extensive, single-component thermodynamic phase:
SdT−Vdp+Ndμ=0.
(1)
At fixed temperature, this becomes dp=ndμ, with number density n=N/V. Separately, at fixed particle number,
(∂p∂n​)T,N​=−V2N​(∂p∂V​)T,N​=nκT​.
(2)
The chain rule along the same isothermal process in the equation of state now gives
(∂μ∂n​)T​=(∂p∂n​)T​(∂μ∂p​)T​=nκT​n.
(3)
Consequently
κT​=n21​(∂μ∂n​)T​.​
(4)
This is isothermal compressibility from chemical-potential response. Since chemical potential has units of energy per particle, the right-hand side has units of volume per energy, or Pa−1, as required. The derivation assumes a smooth bulk equation of state; changing the fractions of coexisting thermodynamic phases at fixed pressure needs separate treatment.

Measure single-particle energy from the lowest state and write εk​=ckα, with c>0. In a homogeneous system, the number of wavevector states in a thin spherical shell is proportional to kd−1dk. Changing variables from k to ε gives the density of states per unit spatial volume,
D(ε)=(2π)dαSd−1​​c−d/αεd/α−1,
(5)
where Sd−1​ is the area of the unit sphere in d dimensions; an additional internal state degeneracy would multiply this expression by a constant.
The Bose-Einstein distribution has μ≤0. At saturation, μ→0−, the maximum excited-state number density is
nexmax​(T)=∫0∞​eβε−1D(ε)​dε,β=kB​T1​.
(6)
Only the lower endpoint can cause divergence. There the integrand is proportional to
βεεd/α−1​∝εd/α−2.
(7)
The improper integral converges precisely when its power exceeds −1. Thus
d>αis the condition for Tc​>0.​
(8)
When d=α, the divergence is logarithmic; when d<α, it is a power divergence. In either case the excited states can accommodate any fixed finite density at every positive temperature, so there is no bulk Bose-Einstein condensation at nonzero temperature.
For completeness, putting s=d/α>1 and using the Gamma function and Riemann zeta function gives
nexmax​(T)=(2π)dαSd−1​​c−sΓ(s)ζ(s)(kB​T)s.
(9)
At fixed density n, the right-hand side reaches n at Tc​; below it the excess particles occupy the lowest state, with condensate fraction 1−(T/Tc​)s. This is Bose condensation with power-law dispersion in the homogeneous thermodynamic limit. A finite vessel or a trapping potential changes the low-energy state count and must not be silently substituted for that setting.

Use the classical dilute-gas convention
nkB​Tp​=1+B2​(T)n+O(n2),
(10)
so the second virial coefficient has units of volume. The Mayer f-function formula for an isotropic pair potential is
B2​(T)=2π∫0∞​[1−e−βϕ(r)]r2dr.
(11)
Only separations smaller than a contribute. Define x=βε>0 and substitute t=r3/a3, so r2dr=(a3/3)dt:
B2​(T)=32πa3​∫01​[1−e−x(1−t)]dt=32πa3​[1−x1−e−x​],x=kB​Tε​.​
(12)
This is the second virial coefficient of a bounded cubic repulsion. For every finite positive T, 1−e−x<x, so B2​(T)>0. There is no finite positive Boyle temperature. At high temperature, expanding the exponential gives
B2​(T)=32πa3​(2x​−6x2​+O(x3))∼3kB​Tπa3ε​⟶0+.
(13)
Thus the intended limiting statement is
TB​=+∞in the sense that B2​(T)→0 only as T→∞.​
(14)
At the other extreme the classical expression tends to 2πa3/3, the hard-exclusion value for separation a. This is a check on the integral, not a claim that a real molecular gas stays classical at arbitrarily low temperature. A molar virial expansion would instead use B2,m​=NA​B2​.

Canonical probabilities. A canonical ensemble describes a system of fixed particle number and fixed external parameters such as volume, weakly coupled to a much larger thermal reservoir. They exchange energy and reach thermal equilibrium, while the reservoir's temperature stays effectively constant. The interaction energy is negligible in the state counting. The combined isolated system is described by equal probabilities for its accessible microstates.
If its total energy is Etot​, the probability of a particular system microstate i is proportional to the reservoir multiplicity at Etot​−Ei​. Writing that multiplicity as eSR​/kB​, expand its entropy:
SR​(Etot​−Ei​)=SR​(Etot​)−(∂ER​∂SR​​)Ei​+⋯=SR​(Etot​)−TEi​​+⋯.
(15)
The large-reservoir limit suppresses the higher terms. Normalization therefore gives
Pi​=Ze−βEi​​,Z=j∑​e−βEj​,β=kB​T1​.​
(16)
The sum counts individual microstates; when grouping them by energy, their state degeneracies must be included. This Z is the canonical partition function.
Classical orientational heat capacity. Hold the magnetic field fixed. Orientations of the magnetic dipole moment have the solid angle measure dΩ=sinθdθdφ. With x=βmB, the angular partition function is
Zang​=∫excosθdΩ=2π∫−11​exudu=4πxsinhx​.
(17)
Choosing a normalized angular measure would remove 4π, with no effect on the energy derivatives. Differentiating at fixed B,
U=−∂β∂lnZang​​=−mB(cothx−x1​)=−mBcothx+kB​T.
(18)
The angular average ⟨cosθ⟩=cothx−1/x is the Langevin function. Since dx/dT=−x/T,
CB​=(∂T∂U​)B​=kB​[1−(sinhxx​)2].​
(19)
This is the heat capacity of a classical magnetic dipole, in JK−1 for one magnetic moment. The word specific here refers to that single moment; N independent magnetic moments have N times this heat capacity. At B=0 the energy is independent of orientation and CB​=0, obtained by the continuous x→0 limit. At fixed nonzero B, the classical low-temperature limit instead gives CB​→kB​.
Equipartition and its proof. The equipartition theorem states that an independent quadratic term aq2/2 in a classical Hamiltonian, with a>0, contributes kB​T/2 to its mean energy. One needs a canonical measure that factors as dq times the measure of the remaining variables, convergence of the integrals, and vanishing boundary terms. The coordinate must not be restricted by a thermally important hard boundary or a coupled constraint.
For H=aq2/2+H0​, integration by parts gives
0=∫−∞∞​∂q∂​(qe−βH)dq=∫−∞∞​(1−βq∂q∂H​)e−βHdq.
(20)
Integrate over all other variables and divide by Z. It follows that
⟨q∂q∂H​⟩=kB​T,⟨2aq2​⟩=21​kB​T.​
(21)
The same reasoning applies to quadratic momenta among the canonical variables. It is the number of quadratic energy contributions that is counted, not an unrestricted count of coordinate labels.
Two soft orientational directions. When x≫1, the moment is concentrated near alignment. Introduce local Cartesian coordinates in the tangent plane q1​=θcosφ and q2​=θsinφ. To leading order,
E=−mB+2mB​(q12​+q22​)+O(mBθ4),dΩ≃dq1​dq2​.
(22)
The two quadratic terms each contribute kB​T/2, so
U=−mB+kB​T+o(T),CB​⟶kB​.​
(23)
This agrees with the exact angular calculation. Treating θ alone as one Cartesian quadratic variable would miss the sinθ measure and the second tangent direction. There are no extra rotational kinetic terms in the stipulated Hamiltonian; adding such terms would be a different model.
Quantum spin one. The spin projections are ms​=−1,0,1, with Sz​=ℏms​. Put Δ=ℏ∣γ∣B>0, where γ is the gyromagnetic ratio. The three energies are −Δ,0,+Δ, irrespective of the sign of γ. Relative to the ground state they are 0,Δ,2Δ. Thus, with q=e−βΔ,
Z=eβΔ(1+q+q2),U=−Δ+Δ1+q+q2q+2q2​.
(24)
Differentiation, or the canonical energy fluctuation identity CB​=kB​β2Var(E), gives
CB​=kB​(βΔ)2(1+q+q2)2q(1+4q+q2)​.
(25)
In the requested limit,
CB​∼kB​(kB​TΔ​)2e−Δ/(kB​T),Δ=ℏ∣γ∣B.​
(26)
This spin-1 paramagnet has a nondegenerate ground state and a finite spectral gap. Excitations are exponentially rare, so its heat capacity tends to zero. Classical orientational equipartition assumed a continuum of arbitrarily small excitation energies; that assumption fails when kB​T≪Δ. This is a change in the accessible state spectrum, not a violation of the equipartition theorem within its classical domain.

Availability and total entropy. The reservoir changes by dUR​=−dU and dVR​=−dV. Its particle number and the system's particle number are fixed. The reservoir's first law of thermodynamics therefore gives
dSR​=TR​dUR​+pR​dVR​​=−TR​dU+pR​dV​.
(27)
Add the system's entropy change. Because TR​,pR​ are constant,
dStot​=dS−TR​dU+pR​dV​=−TR​1​d(U+pR​V−TR​S).
(28)
Thus define the availability
A=U+pR​V−TR​S,ΔStot​=−TR​ΔA​.​
(29)
An additive constant in A is immaterial; its value above the equilibrium reference is the exergy. The Second law of thermodynamics gives ΔStot​≥0, hence ΔA≤0. A stable equilibrium maximizes total entropy over the allowed variations and therefore minimizes A. The environmental TR​,pR​ enter this definition even when the system has not yet reached their values. Because both thermal and mechanical exchanges are allowed, equilibrium here includes mechanical balance; equality of temperatures alone does not ensure the minimum.
Probability and curvature. For a fixed value of a macroscopic coordinate x, let S(x) count the compatible system microstates. The product of the system and reservoir multiplicities is
W(x)∝exp[kB​S(x)​−kB​TR​U(x)+pR​V(x)​]=exp[−kB​TR​A(x)​].
(30)
All factors independent of x enter a normalization constant, giving
P(x)=ℵe−A(x)/(kB​TR​),ℵ−1=∫e−A(x)/(kB​TR​)dx.​
(31)
Here A(x) is the constrained or effective availability for this coordinate, including the multiplicity of any eliminated variables. A nonuniform coordinate measure must likewise be retained or incorporated in that effective availability.
At a stable interior minimum, A′(x0​)=0 and A′′(x0​)>0. Its Taylor expansion gives
A(x)=A(x0​)+21​A′′(x0​)(x−x0​)2+⋯.
(32)
Keeping the quadratic term yields a normal distribution,
P(x)≃2πkB​TR​A′′(x0​)​​exp[−2kB​TR​A′′(x0​)(x−x0​)2​],⟨(x−x0​)2⟩≃A′′(x0​)kB​TR​​.​
(33)
This is availability curvature and equilibrium fluctuations. The quoted fluctuation formula holds at quadratic order in the Gaussian fluctuation approximation; it is not an exact identity for an arbitrary nonlinear A(x). Higher terms can shift the mean away from its most probable value x0​.
Bubble equilibrium. Keep the number of air molecules fixed and include the surface energy in the system's total internal energy. For constant surface tension Γ, this contribution is ΓΩ=ΓαV2/3, with no surface entropy under the stated approximation. Its differential is
dU=TdS−pair​dV+ΓdΩ.
(34)
Hence
dA=(T−TR​)dS+[pR​−pair​+32​ΓαV−1/3]dV.
(35)
Independent thermal and volume variations give T=TR​ and
pair​−pR​=32​ΓαV0−1/3​,V0​=[3(pair​−pR​)2Γα​]3.​
(36)
The air pressure is evaluated at V0​; since the bubble is closed it generally depends on volume, so this equation is implicit until an air equation of state is supplied. For a sphere, α=(36π)1/3, Ω=4πR2, and the pressure jump becomes 2Γ/R, the Young–Laplace equation for a single gas–liquid interface. There is no second surface as in a thin soap film.
Isothermal volume fluctuations. With the air maintained at TR​, eliminate its entropy in favor of its Helmholtz free energy:
AT​(V)=Fair​(TR​,V,N)+pR​V+ΓαV2/3.
(37)
The identity (∂Fair​/∂V)T,N​=−pair​ gives
AT′′​(V0​)=−(∂V∂pair​​)TR​,N,V0​​−92​ΓαV0−4/3​=V0​1​[κT−1​−92​ΓαV0−1/3​].
(38)
Using the curvature result,
⟨ΔV2⟩≃κT−1​−92​ΓαV0−1/3​kB​TR​V0​​.​
(39)
These are the volume fluctuations of a closed gas bubble. Stability requires the denominator to be positive. The negative surface contribution reflects the concavity of V2/3; gas compression supplies the positive restoring curvature. For ideal air, κT−1​=pair​, and mechanical equilibrium reduces the denominator to
D=pR​+94​ΓαV0−1/3​>0.​
(40)
The units are consistent: D is pressure, so kB​TR​V0​/D has units m6, the units of a volume variance. Holding pair​ externally fixed while varying V would remove the gas-compression term and change this stability problem; that is not the fixed-particle-number situation here.
Numerical size of the fluctuations. The volume conversion is important:
V0​=10−3mm3=10−12m3,V01/3​=10−4m,TR​=298.15K.
(41)
Use the specified α=4.84, Γ=0.072Nm−1 and pR​=105Pa. Then
pair​−pR​=32​(0.072)(4.84)(104)Pa=2323.2Pa,
(42)
D=105+94​(0.072)(4.84)(104)=101548.8Pa.
(43)
With the Boltzmann constant kB​=1.380649×10−23JK−1,
kB​TR​=4.1164×10−21J,⟨ΔV2⟩≃4.05×10−38m6.
(44)
Thus the fractional root mean square fluctuation and fractional variance are respectively
V0​⟨ΔV2⟩​​≃2.01×10−7,V02​⟨ΔV2⟩​≃4.05×10−14.​
(45)
The absolute root mean square fluctuation is 2.01×10−19m3. The very small fractional value supports the local Gaussian fluctuation approximation. This estimate neglects exchange of dissolved air, variations of surface tension, shape fluctuations and temperature fluctuations, consistently with the prescribed fixed-number, isothermal bubble model.

Occupation and the two-dimensional Fermi energy. For an independent single-particle state, the Fermi-Dirac distribution is
n(ε)=eβ(ε−μ)+11​,β=kB​T1​.​
(46)
The chemical potential enforces the chosen mean particle number through N=∑j​n(εj​), including spin states in the sum. It is common to all states in particle-exchange equilibrium and must generally change with temperature or field when the total mean number is held fixed. At zero temperature, the distribution becomes a Heaviside step function at the Fermi energy.
Let the sample area be S and let gs​ be its internal spin spin degeneracy. In the continuum limit, filling the disk k<kF​ gives
N=gs​(2π)2S​πkF2​,kF2​=gs​S4πN​.
(47)
Therefore
μ(T=0)=εF​=2mℏ2kF2​​=gs​m2πℏ2​SN​.​
(48)
For spin-half fermions with both spin states available, gs​=2 and εF​=πℏ2N/(mS); for a spin-polarized gas gs​=1. The question does not specify the area or spin degeneracy, so these conventions must be stated rather than extracting an energy from N alone. Although the original text calls k momentum, the supplied ℏ2k2/(2m) dispersion makes it a wave vector; physical momentum is p=ℏk. The result agrees with the constant two-dimensional free-electron density of states. Discrete level spacings in a finite sample would require a specified boundary spectrum.
One level occupied on average. Write δ=μB​B and z=eβ(ε−μ)>0. The two mean spin occupations are
N+​=1+zeβδ1​,N−​=1+ze−βδ1​.
(49)
Imposing N+​+N−​=1 gives 2+z(eβδ+e−βδ)=1+z(eβδ+e−βδ)+z2, hence z2=1. Thus, for every positive temperature,
μ=ε,N+​−N−​=−tanh(2βμB​B​).​
(50)
The total magnetic moment is M=−μB​(N+​−N−​). Consequently
χ=(∂B∂M​)B=0​=2kB​TμB2​​.​
(51)
This is the grand-canonical spin susceptibility of a half-filled single level. Empty, singly occupied and doubly occupied orbital states are allowed in the grand canonical ensemble. At zero field, the singly occupied configurations together have probability 1/2, explaining the factor of two relative to one permanent spin-half moment. If exactly one electron were required at all times, a canonical two-state partition function would instead give M=μB​tanh(βμB​B) and χ=μB2​/(kB​T). That different answer does not implement the stated average-number constraint.
Density-of-states formula for the susceptibility. Here g is the zero-field single-spin density of states. Regard its argument E as the full spin-shifted energy. The shifted functions are g±​(E)=g(E∓μB​B), so
N±​(B)=∫−∞∞​g(E∓μB​B)n(E;μ(B),T)dE.
(52)
The total number is even in B; thus μ(B)=μ(0)+O(B2) at fixed total number on a smooth branch. More directly, terms from μ′(0) cancel in the spin difference because the two densities coincide at zero field. To first order,
N+​−N−​=−2μB​B∫−∞∞​g′(E)n(E;μ(0),T)dE+O(B3).
(53)
It follows that
χ=2μB2​∫−∞∞​g′(E)n(E)B=0​dE.​
(54)
For a physical energy band, take g to vanish below its lower edge. Provided the endpoint term vanishes, integration by parts also gives
2μB2​χ​=∫−∞∞​g(E)[−n′(E)]dE.​
(55)
This form displays the positive response and the narrow thermal window around μ. A sharp band edge is included by the corresponding distributional derivative in the first expression. The susceptibility defined here is the derivative of total magnetic moment with respect to B, with units JT−2; it is not automatically a dimensionless SI volume susceptibility. A density-of-states convention per unit volume likewise gives a response per unit volume. These are the field conventions for magnetic susceptibility.
Chemical potential at fixed number. Define the integrated single-spin state count
G(E)=∫−∞E​g(u)du,KT​(E)=−n′(E).
(56)
The probability kernel KT​ has unit integral, mean μ, and variance a(kB​T)2. Using integration by parts,
2N​=∫g(E)n(E)dE=∫G(E)KT​(E)dE.
(57)
Expand G about μ. The linear term integrates to zero, while G′′=g′ gives
2N​=G(μ)+2a​(kB​T)2g′(μ)+O(T4).
(58)
This is the number form of the Sommerfeld expansion. Since N/2=G(εF​) at zero temperature, set μ=εF​+δμ with δμ=O(T2):
0=g(εF​)δμ+2a​(kB​T)2g′(εF​)+O(T4).
(59)
Therefore
μ=εF​−2a​g(εF​)g′(εF​)​(kB​T)2+O(T4).​
(60)
The coefficient can be obtained directly. With y=(E−μ)/(kB​T),
a=∫−∞∞​(1+ey)2y2ey​dy=4∑ℓ=1∞​ℓ2(−1)ℓ+1​=3π2​​.
(61)
For example, the sum follows by expanding e−y/(1+e−y)2 on y>0, using the evenness of the kernel and integrating each exponential. Thus a/2=π2/6. A density of states increasing with energy gives a downward quadratic chemical-potential shift. For the constant two-dimensional density of states that shift vanishes to this order; a finite lower band edge still allows exponentially small corrections.
Low-temperature susceptibility at fixed number. Apply the same kernel expansion to g itself:
2μB2​χ​=g(μ)+2a​(kB​T)2g′′(μ)+O(T4).
(62)
The shift of μ must be retained in the first term. Writing gF​=g(εF​) and similarly for its derivatives,
g(μ)=gF​+gF′​δμ+O(T4)=gF​−2a​gF​gF′2​​(kB​T)2+O(T4).
(63)
Consequently
2μB2​χ​=gF​−2a​(gF​gF′2​​−gF′′​)(kB​T)2+O(T4),a=3π2​.​
(64)
This is the fixed-number low-temperature Pauli susceptibility. The gF′′​ contribution is thermal broadening at fixed chemical potential; the −gF′2​/gF​ contribution enforces fixed total number. Both are needed. If the density of states were instead defined with both spin directions included, the prefactor 2 would be absorbed into that definition. Only the spin response has been calculated; no orbital magnetic response is included.
The expansion assumes gF​>0, sufficient smoothness near the Fermi energy, and a temperature small compared with the energy scale over which g changes or encounters a band edge. It does not apply unmodified at a singular density of states. For the ideal two-dimensional parabolic band, gF′​=gF′′​=0, so the leading Pauli response is temperature independent, consistently with the earlier state count.
