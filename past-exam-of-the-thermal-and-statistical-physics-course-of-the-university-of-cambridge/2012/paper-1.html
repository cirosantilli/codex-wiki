Use thermal de Broglie wavelength λT​=h/2πmkB​T​ and spacing a=n−1/3. Degeneracy onset from thermal wavelength occurs at
Tλ​=2πmkB​h2n2/3​.​
(1)
The numerical factor depends on the convention for a typical momentum, but the scale ℏ2n2/3/(mkB​) does not. For fermions the Fermi temperature TF​=ℏ2(3π2n)2/3/(2mkB​) gives a comparable scale for spin degeneracy two.
Representative estimates, with the assumed densities stated explicitly, are:
SystemAssumed number densityAssumed particle massTλ​
Conduction electrons1029m−3me​1.2×105K
Dilute heavy atomic cloud1019m−387u1.6×10−7K
Neutrons at nuclear-scale density1044m−3mn​6.5×1011K
Here u is the atomic mass constant. Thus the characteristic orders are 105K for electrons, sub-microkelvin for a dilute heavy atomic gas, and 1012K for neutrons. There is no density-independent quantum-degeneracy temperature for atomic gases: for example, taking n=2.5×1025m−3 and mass 4u instead gives about 0.065K. These are illustrative input estimates, not values supplied by the paper. Denser neutron-star cores require relativistic and interaction corrections; the estimate identifies the degeneracy scale rather than a precise stellar temperature.

For either phase, the entropy differential at fixed composition is
dS=TCP​​dT−(∂T∂V​)P​dP=TCP​​dT−VαT​dP.
(6)
The pressure derivative uses the Maxwell relation from dG=−SdT+VdP. Differentiating the equality of entropies along coexistence gives the general relation
TΔCP​​dT=Δ(VαT​)dP.
(7)
For a differentiable coexistence curve with pressure varying, equality of Gibbs free energies also implies −ΔSdT+ΔVdP=0. Since ΔS=0, the two phases then share V. Thus, with ΔCP​=0,
dPdT​=ΔCP​TVΔαT​​.​
(8)
This is the Ehrenfest entropy-continuity relation. Entropy equality alone would give TΔ(VαT​)/ΔCP​ without the shared-volume condition; a constant-pressure boundary or vanishing discontinuities needs separate limiting analysis.

Landau theory expands a thermodynamic potential in an order parameter, constrained by symmetry. For a scalar with sign-reversal symmetry, a useful free-energy density is
f=f0​+2a(T−Tc​)​η2+4b​η4−hη,a,b>0.
(9)
Here h is the conjugate field. At h=0, minimizing rather than merely finding stationary points gives
ηeq​=0(T>Tc​),ηeq​=±ba(Tc​−T)​​(T<Tc​).​
(10)
The symmetry-related minima represent spontaneous symmetry breaking, as for a simple uniaxial ferromagnet with magnetization as order parameter. An infinitesimal field selects one sign. Above Tc​, the susceptibility is 1/[a(T−Tc​)]; below it the stable-branch susceptibility is 1/[2a(Tc​−T)]. Minimization lowers f by −a2(T−Tc​)2/(4b) below Tc​, producing a heat-capacity density jump Tc​a2/(2b) with continuous entropy.
A negative quartic coefficient requires a stabilizing +cη6/6 with c>0. It can produce a first-order transition before the quadratic coefficient changes sign: coexistence occurs at a(T−Tc​)=3b2/(16c) and η2=−3b/(4c). Odd terms are allowed when the assumed symmetry is absent. Landau theory predicts mean-field exponents; spatial fluctuations can change critical behaviour, especially close to a critical point in low dimensions. A gradient term extends the model to nonuniform order parameters but does not by itself supply dynamical equations.

Brownian motion arises from many microscopic collisions. A one-dimensional Langevin description is
mv˙=−ζv−κx+ξ(t),⟨ξ(t)⟩=0,⟨ξ(t)ξ(t′)⟩=2ζkB​Tδ(t−t′).
(11)
The fluctuation-dissipation relation ties random forcing to drag, ensuring thermal equilibrium. For a free particle set κ=0. With initially thermal velocities, its mean-square displacement is
⟨[x(t)−x(0)]2⟩=2D[t−τv​(1−e−t/τv​)],D=ζkB​T​,τv​=ζm​.​
(12)
It is ballistic, (kB​T/m)t2, at short times and diffusive, 2Dt, for t≫τv​. In three dimensions the corresponding long-time result is 6Dt. At diffusive times, the position propagator from a specified origin is Gaussian with variance 2Dt per coordinate. A small sphere in a viscous liquid supplies a physical example; in the slow unbounded-fluid limit its drag is 6πηf​a.
For a harmonic trap, overdamped relaxation has time τκ​=ζ/κ, equilibrium variance kB​T/κ and stationary correlation
⟨x(t)x(0)⟩=κkB​T​e−∣t∣/τκ​.
(13)
Consequently stationary trapped Brownian displacement obeys
⟨[x(t)−x(0)]2⟩=κ2kB​T​(1−e−t/τκ​).​
(14)
Confinement makes this displacement saturate, unlike free diffusion. A trajectory initialized exactly at the centre instead has variance (kB​T/κ)(1−e−2t/τκ​); confusing that conditional variance with the stationary displacement gives an erroneous factor of two. The equilibrium position distribution is proportional to exp[−κx2/(2kB​T)]. More general finite confinement has a geometry-dependent equilibrium distribution and bounded displacement, not necessarily a Gaussian one.

Bose-Einstein condensation is macroscopic occupation of a single lowest-energy one-particle state. For a homogeneous three-dimensional ideal gas of conserved massive bosons, set that energy to zero. The excited-state population is
Nex​=λT3​V​g3/2​(z),z=eμ/(kB​T)≤1,λT​=mkB​T2πℏ2​​.
(15)
Here g3/2​(z)=∑r≥1​zr/r3/2. As μ→0−, the excited states can hold only Vζ(3/2)/λT3​ particles. Additional particles occupy the ground state. Thus
Tc​=mkB​2πℏ2​[ζ(3/2)n​]2/3,NN0​​=1−(Tc​T​)3/2(T<Tc​).​
(16)
The isolated ground state must be counted separately from the continuum density of states; otherwise one misses the condensate. A dilute atomic gas is a physical realization of the quantum-degeneracy regime, but trapping geometry and interactions modify the homogeneous ideal-gas formulas.[ref]
In the homogeneous ideal gas, one and two dimensions do not have finite-temperature condensation in the thermodynamic limit because the low-energy population integral diverges. Finite size or a trap changes that conclusion's assumptions. Condensation and superfluidity are distinct: the ideal quadratic gas has zero Landau critical velocity, while interactions can support a sound mode and superfluid flow. Neither zero viscosity nor phase-rigid flow follows from the occupation formula alone.

Let β=1/(kB​T). Each independent fermion orbital is either empty or occupied once, so its grand partition factor is 1+e−β(ϵj​−μ). Multiplying factors and taking the logarithm gives
Ξ=∏j​[1+e−β(ϵj​−μ)],Φ=−kB​TlnΞ=−kB​T∫0∞​g(ϵ)ln[1+e−β(ϵ−μ)]dϵ.
(17)
Since 1−nF​=[1+e−β(ϵ−μ)]−1, this is the requested form. Spin or other orbital degeneracy is already included in g.
For the Fermi-window convolution of the grand potential, define
A(ϵ)=∫0ϵ​g(t)dt,Φ0​(ϵ)=−∫0ϵ​A(t)dt,F(ϵ)=kB​Tln[1−nF​(ϵ,μ)].
(18)
Direct differentiation gives F′=+nF​. The supplied PDF hint has the opposite sign; using that sign would contradict its grand-potential formula. Integrating twice with A(0)=Φ0​(0)=0 and vanishing upper boundary terms,
Φ=∫0∞​A′Fdϵ=−∫0∞​AnF​dϵ=∫0∞​Φ0′​nF​dϵ=∫0∞​Φ0​(ϵ)[−∂ϵ​nF​]dϵ.​
(19)
At zero temperature −∂ϵ​nF​→δ(ϵ−μ), confirming Φ0​(μ)=Φ(0,μ).
Use the source's implicit unit normalization of the constant density of states; restoring a constant baseline g0​ multiplies the following potentials by g0​. For the sinusoidal density of states, direct integration gives
Φ0​(ϵ)=−2ϵ2​+4π2αΔ2​[cos(Δ2πϵ​)−1].
(20)
Physical nonnegative density of states requires ∣α∣≤1. Write t=kB​T and y=ϵ−μ. The Fermi window
w(y)=4t1​sech2(2ty​)
(21)
is even, with full-line moments ∫wdy=1, ∫ywdy=0 and ∫y2wdy=π2t2/3. The last integral follows, for example, by expanding ex/(1+ex)2 on x>0 into its alternating exponential series. When t≪μ, extending the lower integration limit to −∞ introduces only exponentially small band-edge corrections. The supplied Fourier transform gives
∫−∞∞​w(y)cos[Δ2π(μ+y)​]dy=sinhXX​cos(Δ2πμ​),X=Δ2π2t​.
(22)
The odd sine term vanishes. Substitution proves
Φ(T,μ)=Φ0​(μ)−6π2t2​+4π2αΔ2​(sinhXX​−1)cos(Δ2πμ​).​
(23)
The thermal damping of a sinusoidal density of states is therefore X/sinhX in the full oscillatory potential. The requested ratio instead concerns the thermal change Φ(T,μ)−Φ0​(μ). Its oscillatory thermal-change amplitude ratio is
R(X)=X26∣α∣​(1−sinhXX​).​
(24)
For positive X it decreases smoothly from the limit ∣α∣ to zero:
R=∣α∣[1−607X2​+O(X4)](X≪1),R∼X26∣α∣​(X≫1).
(25)
At exactly T=0 both thermal changes vanish; the quoted value is their limiting ratio. The amplitude is the envelope in chemical potential. At a particular fixed μ, the signed contribution ratio has αcos(2πμ/Δ) in place of ∣α∣; at an oscillation node the actual oscillatory contribution is zero. The large-X behaviour still requires kB​T≪μ, so the useful range is 1≪X≪2π2μ/Δ.

For two localized energy levels 0,δ with degeneracies d0​,d1​, let x=δ/(kB​T). Canonical equilibrium gives
z=d0​+d1​e−x,p1​=zd1​e−x​,u=δp1​,s=kB​(lnz+xp1​),c=kB​x2p1​(1−p1​).
(26)
Here p1​ is the total upper-level occupation probability and u,s,c are per unit. For nondegenerate levels the entropy rises from zero to kB​ln2, while the heat capacity vanishes at both low and high temperatures and has a Schottky maximum near kB​T=0.417δ. A spin one-half in a field, or a localized defect with two configurations, supplies an example. Higher levels must be thermally inaccessible; unequal degeneracies change the populations and peak. Distinguishable localized units have total partition function zN, unlike a gas of indistinguishable particles sharing just two orbitals.
For the magnetic atom, choose level labels with Em​=−mgμB​B, m=−J,…,J. Put x=TB​/T=gμB​B/(kB​T). Its partition function and exact entropy of a finite magnetic multiplet are
ZJ​(x)=m=−J∑J​emx=sinh(x/2)sinh[(2J+1)x/2]​,kB​sJ​​=lnZJ​−xdxdlnZJ​​.​
(27)
The ratio form has removable limit ZJ​(0)=2J+1. Since sJ​ is a function of x only, it depends on field and temperature solely through B/T. For fixed J at small x, symmetry removes the odd powers and the supplied sum of m2 gives
ZJ​=(2J+1)[1+6J(J+1)​x2+O(x4)],sJ​=kB​[ln(2J+1)−6J(J+1)​T2TB2​​+O(x4)].​
(28)
For large J, the expansion requires J∣x∣≪1, not merely ∣x∣≪1.
For demagnetization with a linear-heat-capacity load, integrate its specified CL​/T:
SL​(T)=SL​(0)+θMkB​​T.
(29)
Isothermal magnetization at Ti​ removes spin entropy; the bath receives the corresponding heat. During the following reversible, thermally isolated demagnetization, it is the combined entropy of spins and load that stays constant. At the minimum-field endpoint Bf​=0, the ideal independent spins reach smax​=kB​ln(2J+1). Therefore
NsJ​(Ti​,Bi​)+SL​(Ti​)=NkB​ln(2J+1)+SL​(Tmin​),
(30)
N[kB​ln(2J+1)−sJ​(Ti​,Bi​)]=θMkB​​(Ti​−Tmin​).​
(31)
The bracket is the initial magnetic entropy deficit. The PDF's intermediate equality uses the initial entropy itself instead; with its preceding definition of s, that equality omits the maximum-entropy term. The corrected balance recovers its final temperature formula:
Tmin​=Ti​−6MkB2​Ti2​NθJ(J+1)g2μB2​Bi2​​.​
(32)
Without the small-field expansion, the exact independent-spin prediction is Ti​−(Nθ/M)[ln(2J+1)−sJ​(Ti​,Bi​)/kB​]. As a check, zero initial field gives zero entropy deficit and no cooling. The incorrectly printed intermediate balance would fail this check.
The result requires the load law throughout the temperature interval, hence Ti​≪θ; a small initial Zeeman splitting, quantitatively JTB,i​/Ti​≪1; positive final temperature; negligible additional heat capacities and heat leaks; thermal equilibration between subsystems; and a reversible sweep slow enough to maintain equilibrium. A negative extrapolated temperature is a breakdown of this approximation, not a cooling prediction. Spins alone would keep B/T fixed, but the attached load changes that adiabat by transferring entropy to them as it cools.
Interactions invalidate the product of independent one-site partition functions. The entropy must be computed from the many-body energy spectrum or a justified interacting approximation. Correlations and possible magnetic ordering introduce an interaction scale and can remove the independent-spin entropy available for cooling near zero field. Internal fields and ordering commonly limit practical demagnetization. The detailed correction and any residual entropy depend on the interaction Hamiltonian, dimensionality and frustration; neither a universal ordering temperature nor a numerical final-temperature correction is specified here.
