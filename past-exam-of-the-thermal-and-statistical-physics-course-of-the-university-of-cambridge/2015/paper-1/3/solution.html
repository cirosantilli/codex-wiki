Ensemble and applications. A grand canonical ensemble describes a system exchanging both energy and particles with a large reservoir. Its fixed control parameters are T,V,μ, while microstates with different N have probabilities
Pr​=Ξ−1e−β(Er​−μNr​),Ξ=∑r​e−β(Er​−μNr​).
(20)
Weak coupling allows the system's energy levels to remain meaningful; equilibrium fixes the reservoir temperature and chemical potential. The grand potential is Φ=−kB​TlnΞ=U−TS−μN, with N now the mean population. It generates entropy, pressure and mean particle number by differentiation. This ensemble is convenient for gases in contact with particle reservoirs, independently occupied quantum levels and surface adsorption; its particle-number fluctuations are part of the description, not an error in counting.
Classical single-level limit. For the single-level grand partition function in the dilute limit, put xk​=e−β(ϵk​−μ). Empty and singly occupied levels contribute 1 and xk​. In the dilute classical limit xk​≪1, higher occupancies contribute only at order xk2​ or beyond:
Ξk​=1+xk​+O(xk2​)≃1+exp[−(ϵk​−μ)/(kB​T)]​.
(21)
This truncation does not impose the Pauli principle on a classical gas. A fermion state has exactly 1+xk​, a boson state has (1−xk​)−1, and Maxwell-Boltzmann occupancy weights give exk​; they all agree to this order. In particular, lnΞk​=xk​+O(xk2​), with the classical Maxwell-Boltzmann result exactly xk​.
grand potential and chemical potential. With z=eβμ, summing the single-level logarithms and using the stated density of states gives
lnΞ≃z∫0∞​g(ϵ)e−βϵdϵ.

∫0∞​ϵ1/2e−βϵdϵ=2(kB​T)3/2∫0∞​x2e−x2dx=2π​​(kB​T)3/2.
(23)
Combining this with the density-of-states prefactor gives lnΞ=zV/λ3. Thus
Φ=−kB​Tλ3V​eμ/(kB​T)​.
(24)
Differentiating at fixed T,V,
N=−(∂μ∂Φ​)T,V​=λ3V​eμ/(kB​T),μ=kB​TlnVNλ3​​.
(25)
Similarly p=−(∂V​Φ)T,μ​=kB​Tz/λ3, so pV=NkB​T. This is the grand-canonical chemical potential of a classical ideal gas. The condition Nλ3/V≪1 states the classical regime. The supplied density of states counts one internal state; extra internal degeneracies would have to be included consistently rather than inserted at only one stage.
One adsorption site. Take the free-vapour energy reference as zero and the binding energy as −ϵ. A site is either empty or singly occupied; the latter has vibrational partition function zs​(T). Its adsorption-site grand partition function and mean occupation are
Ξsite​=1+zs​eβ(μs​+ϵ),θ=1+zs​eβ(μs​+ϵ)zs​eβ(μs​+ϵ)​.
(26)
The chemical potential of an adsorbed species on equivalent sites follows by inverting the occupation ratio
μs​=−ϵ+kB​Tln(1−θ)zs​θ​​.
(27)
The factor 1−θ is the vacant-site probability. The binding reference and the energies included in zs​ must be chosen consistently, so that vibrational zero-point energy is not counted twice.
Isotherm. The vibrational factor in a Langmuir equilibrium constant is fixed by adsorption equilibrium, μs​=μvap​. Neglecting vapour internal degrees of freedom and writing its number density as p/(kB​T) gives eβμvap​=pλ3/(kB​T). Therefore
bs​(T)=kB​Tzs​(T)λ3​eϵ/(kB​T),θ(p,T)=1+bs​(T)pbs​(T)p​​.
(28)
This is the Langmuir isotherm: initially linear, increasing monotonically and concave down, approaching unit coverage. Half coverage occurs at p1/2​=1/bs​(T). The coefficient has inverse-pressure units. It assumes equivalent independent sites, at most one molecule per site and no lateral interaction or multilayer adsorption. The molecular mass appropriate to the vapour determines λ.
A real uptake measurement must separate adsorbed molecules from free gas in the apparatus. Langmuir's original mica experiment compared a mica-containing bulb with a similar blank and inferred uptake from calibrated gas volumes and pressures. His stated blank contribution and bath-temperature drift limited the inferred adsorption; a saturating curve alone does not establish ideal equivalent sites.[ref]
