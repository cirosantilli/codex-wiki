In the classical equilibrium circuit, the capacitor stores CV2/2. Equipartition assigns this quadratic term mean energy kB​T/2, so
Vrms​=CkB​T​​≃6.4μV(T≃300K).​
(1)
The mean voltage is zero and its variance is kB​T/C. Resistance determines the equilibration time and noise spectrum, not this full-band equilibrium variance. A finite-bandwidth or appreciably loading readout can measure less than the ideal result.

Let Δ=ℏΩ and choose the nondegenerate energy levels as 0 and Δ. For N independent identical units, Z=(1+e−βΔ)N; set N=1 for a single two-level system. Differentiating its logarithm gives
U=1+eΔ/(kB​T)NΔ​,CV​=NkB​(1+ex)2x2ex​,x=kB​TΔ​.​
(2)
At low temperature, U∼NΔe−x and CV​∼NkB​x2e−x: the upper level is frozen out. At high temperature, the two levels approach equal populations, giving U→NΔ/2 while CV​∼NkB​x2/4→0. The energy therefore rises monotonically to a plateau, while the heat capacity has a Schottky peak:
kB​Tpeak​≃0.417Δ,CV,peak​≃0.439NkB​.​
(3)
Unequal degeneracies would change the populations and peak; the usual equal-degeneracy model is stated here. Shifting both energy levels changes the energy baseline but not heat capacity.

Use a three-dimensional ideal gas at the same number density n for both isotopes. An unpolarized helium-3 gas has spin degeneracy two, hence
kB​TF​=2m3​ℏ2​(3π2n)2/3.
(4)
Helium-4 has a single spin state, and the ideal Bose-gas saturation condition gives
kB​TB​=m4​2πℏ2​[ζ(3/2)n​]2/3.
(5)
Eliminating n, using m3​/m4​≃3/4 and ζ(3/2)=2.612, yields
TF​TB​​=(3π2)2/3ζ(3/2)2/34π​m4​m3​​≃0.519,TB​≃2.6K.​
(6)
The fermionic spin degeneracy must be included. This is an ideal-gas estimate; strong interactions in liquid helium prevent identifying it with an exact experimental transition temperature.

Equipartition gives mean energy kB​T/2 for each independent quadratic term in a classical canonical Hamiltonian. For example, p2/(2m) and kx2/2 each contribute this amount in a harmonic oscillator. It is a thermal average, not an instantaneous equality; constraints, nonquadratic terms or quantum level spacings large compared with kB​T require a different calculation.
The fluctuation-dissipation theorem connects equilibrium fluctuations with the dissipative part of linear response. If an applied force couples as −f(t)x, use response to e−iωt and define the two-sided angular-frequency spectrum Sxx​(ω)=∫eiωt⟨δx(t)δx(0)⟩dt. The classical relation is
Sxx​(ω)=ω2kB​T​Imχ(ω).​
(7)
For a viscously damped particle, mv˙=−ζv+ξ(t), the corresponding white-noise force satisfies ⟨ξ(t)ξ(t′)⟩=2ζkB​Tδ(t−t′). Noise replenishes energy lost by friction; their strengths cannot be varied independently at fixed equilibrium temperature.
Electrically, the one-sided voltage-noise spectrum per hertz of an equilibrium resistor is 4kB​TR. A capacitor in parallel filters it to
SV(1)​(f)=1+(2πfRC)24kB​TR​,∫0∞​SV(1)​(f)df=CkB​T​.
(8)
The same equilibrium voltage variance follows from equipartition. Increasing R makes the spectrum taller and narrower without changing its integrated area. This thermodynamic connection between dissipative conductance and thermal electrical fluctuations is the content of the classical noise calculation.[ref]

A black body absorbs incident electromagnetic radiation and, in equilibrium, emits a spectrum determined by temperature alone. A cavity with a small aperture approximates this ideal. Photon number is not conserved in thermal equilibrium, so its chemical potential is zero. Combining the Bose occupation with the two-polarization electromagnetic mode density gives the spectral energy density
uω​=π2c3ℏω3​eℏω/(kB​T)−11​.
(9)
Changing variable to x=ℏω/(kB​T) shows directly why the total energy density is proportional to T4:
u(T)=π2ℏ3c3(kB​T)4​∫0∞​ex−1x3dx​=15ℏ3c3π2kB4​​T4.
(10)
Three powers of T arise from the number of thermally populated modes and one from their characteristic energy. Isotropic radiation incident on a surface has outward hemispherical flux cu/4, giving
j⋆=σSB​T4,σSB​=60ℏ3c2π2kB4​​.​
(11)
The factor 1/4 is the hemispherical angular average; multiplying energy density by c alone would overcount. The spectrum's frequency scale grows with T. Classical equipartition over all modes would instead produce the Rayleigh-Jeans ultraviolet divergence; the Planck occupation suppresses high-frequency modes. Real materials can have wavelength-dependent emissivity, and a body's net radiative exchange also includes absorbed environmental radiation.

An equation of state relates equilibrium pressure, temperature and density. The ideal-gas form p=nkB​T neglects intermolecular forces and excluded volume. A low-density real-gas expansion is
nkB​Tp​=1+B2​(T)n+B3​(T)n2+⋯,
(12)
where the virial coefficients encode interaction effects and generally depend on temperature.
A simple phenomenological example is the molar Van der Waals equation
p=v−bRT​−v2a​.​
(13)
Here v is molar volume, b represents excluded volume and the attractive interaction lowers pressure through a. Its dilute expansion gives pv/(RT)=1+[b−a/(RT)]/v+⋯. It recovers the ideal-gas limit but is not a quantitatively universal equation for all real gases.
Below its critical temperature, the raw isotherm contains a region with (∂p/∂v)T​>0, corresponding to negative isothermal compressibility and mechanical instability. Equilibrium phase separation replaces that segment by a coexistence pressure satisfying equal chemical potentials, equivalently the Maxwell equal-area construction. The critical point follows from pv​=pvv​=0:
vc​=3b,Tc​=27Rb8a​,pc​=27b2a​.​
(14)
The distinction between an unstable homogeneous branch and a stable two-phase state is essential when interpreting a real-gas equation of state.

Write z=eβ(μ−ϵ). For one nondegenerate fermionic quantum state, the allowed occupations 0 and 1 give ΞF​=1+z. For a bosonic state, summing all nonnegative occupations gives ΞB​=(1−z)−1, requiring z<1 or μ<ϵ. Differentiating the logarithm,
nˉ=z∂z∂lnΞ​,nˉF​=eβ(ϵ−μ)+11​,nˉB​=eβ(ϵ−μ)−11​.​
(15)
The interacting point defect has two distinct spin states, not one fermionic state. Its four microstates have grand-canonical weights 1,z,z,qz2, with q=e−βU. The first two single-electron weights are separate up/down spin possibilities; the double state has one electron of each spin. Thus
Ξ=1+2z+qz2,nˉ=1+2z+qz22z+2qz2​.​
(16)
For U=0, Ξ=(1+z)2 and nˉ=2z/(1+z). The sketch is a single smooth step from 0 to 2, centred on μ=ϵ, where nˉ=1. As T→0 its width tends to zero.
For positive U with βU≫1, the lowest grand energies are successively 0, ϵ−μ and 2ϵ+U−2μ. Away from the two crossings,
nˉ≃⎩⎨⎧​0,1,2,​μ<ϵ,ϵ<μ<ϵ+U,μ>ϵ+U.​​
(17)
Finite temperature smooths each step over an energy range of order kB​T. Near the lower step double occupation is exponentially suppressed, so nˉ≃2z/(1+2z). Near the upper step put y=eβ(μ−ϵ−U) and neglect the empty state, giving nˉ≃1+y/(2+y). Spin degeneracy shifts the half-step positions to ϵ−kB​Tln2 and ϵ+U+kB​Tln2.
For a grand-canonical probability ps​∝e−β(Es​−μns​), differentiation gives ∂μ​ps​=β(ns​−nˉ)ps​. Multiplying by ns​ and summing proves
σ2=kB​T(∂μ∂nˉ​)T​.​
(18)
For this defect, direct state counting gives
⟨n2⟩=Ξ2z+4qz2​,σ2=(1+2z+qz2)22z(1+2qz+qz2)​.
(19)
At μ=ϵ, z=1 and therefore
σ2=(3+e−βU)22+6e−βU​⟶92​.​
(20)
The same limit gives nˉ=2/3: empty, up and down states have equal weights, while the double state is suppressed. Replacing this degeneracy by a two-outcome equal-weight model would incorrectly give variance 1/4 at this chemical potential.
Across the full chemical-potential range the variance has two well-separated narrow peaks and tends to zero on the 0, 1 and 2 plateaux. The limiting shapes are 2z/(1+2z)2 near the lower step and 2y/(2+y)2 near the upper one. Each peak has height 1/4, at the shifted half-step position; the variance at the unshifted crossings is instead 2/9.

For H=0 and a<0, the free energy is a symmetric double well. The central stationary point is a maximum; the two stable minima are
M0​=±−ba​​,Fmin​=−4ba2​.​

These are the magnetisations of the two symmetry-broken phases, selected by an infinitesimal field or a boundary condition. An exactly symmetric finite-system ensemble can have ⟨M⟩=0 by averaging both wells; that does not make the central maximum a stable homogeneous phase.

4 OurBigBook.com
At zero applied field, time-reversal symmetry interchanges M and −M without changing the free energy, so the analytic expansion contains only even powers. The term −μ0​HM breaks this symmetry at fixed nonzero H. Stationarity and local stability require
μ0​H=a(T)M+bM3,a(T)+3bM2>0.​
(24)
For T>Tc​ at small field, the cubic term is higher order and M≃μ0​H/a(T). Thus the susceptibility as defined in the question is
χ=(∂H∂M​)T​H→0​a(T)μ0​​.​
(25)
The three requested free-energy sketches and their minima are given separately above.
For the plotted isotherms, divide the equation of state by nonzero M and rearrange:
M2=bμ0​​MH​−ba(T)​.​
(26)
This is an Arrott plot: its slope determines b, and the isotherm through the origin identifies the mean-field Curie temperature.[ref] Under the paper's stated uniform-field Landau model, use H as supplied on its axis.
The ordinate unit is (104Am−1)2=108A2m−2. Reading the original graph gives slopes about 0.076–0.080 ordinate units per unit H/M, so the physical slope is approximately 7.8×106A2m−2. Consequently
b≃1.6×10−13JmA−4.​
(27)
Approximate horizontal intercepts and the corresponding a=μ0​(H/M)M2=0​ are:
  • 4.2K: intercept −62, giving a≃−7.8×10−5Hm−1.
  • 13K: intercept −40, giving a≃−5.1×10−5Hm−1.
  • 19K: intercept −15, giving a≃−1.9×10−5Hm−1.
  • 25K: intercept +17, giving a≃+2.2×10−5Hm−1.
The sign change brackets the transition between 19 and 25 K. A linear fit of a(T)=α(T−Tc​) across the four read-offs gives approximately α=4.8×10−6Hm−1K−1 and
Tc​≃22K.​
(28)
Interpolating only the two nearest isotherms also gives about 22 K. These are graph estimates, not exact recovered measurements.
Below Tc​ at zero field, the stable branches have M02​=−a/b. Substitution gives the free-energy density relative to the omitted smooth background:
Fmin​=−4ba(T)2​=−4bα2(T−Tc​)2​.
(29)
The equilibrium excess heat-capacity density is cH​=−T∂T2​Fmin​=Tα2/(2b) below Tc​ and zero above it. Therefore
ΔcH​=2bTc​α2​>0.​
(30)
The all-isotherm linear fit gives about 1.5×103Jm−3K−1. Estimating the slope only between the 19 K and 25 K curves instead gives α≃6.9×10−6Hm−1K−1 and a jump near 3×103Jm−3K−1. The lines are not exactly parallel and the intercepts are not perfectly linear in T, so a jump of order a few 103Jm−3K−1 is supported; finer precision is not. This is per volume because F is a density; no sample volume is given to convert it to a total heat capacity.
If b<0, the quartic truncation is unbounded below. Add a positive sixth-order term cM6/6 with c>0. At zero field the coexistence conditions give
Mjump2​=−4c3b​,atransition​=16c3b2​>0.
(31)
Thus the zero-field transition is generically first order, with a discontinuous spontaneous magnetisation and possible metastability. It occurs before the quadratic coefficient reaches zero. The case b=0 is the tricritical boundary of this mean-field model; no value of c is invented.
