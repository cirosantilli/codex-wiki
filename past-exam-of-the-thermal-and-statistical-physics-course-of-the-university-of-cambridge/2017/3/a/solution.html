Component chemical potentials. Define the per-spin quantum concentration nQ​=λT−3​=(mkB​T/(2πℏ2))3/2. For a classical component with one-particle potential energy Uσ​(r), the local Helmholtz free energy density is kB​Tnσ​[ln(nσ​/nQ​)−1]+nσ​Uσ​. Its derivative with respect to number density gives the full local chemical potential, including the magnetic energy. With the up state defined as the lower level,
μ↑​=kB​TlnnQ​n↑​​−μB​B,μ↓​=kB​TlnnQ​n↓​​+μB​B.​
(23)
The Bohr magneton μB​ is a magnetic moment; μB​B is an energy. The factor two for the two spin states is not already included in nQ​.
Total equilibrium chemical potential. If spin conversion is allowed, a particle can be added in either spin state at the same equilibrium free-energy cost. Thus
μ=μ↑​=μ↓​,​
(24)
not their sum or zero. The total particle number, or a reservoir, fixes this common value; the supplied information does not specify a numerical chemical potential. A useful local expression is μ=kB​Tln[n/(2nQ​coshx)], with x=μB​B/(kB​T), whose density dependence is found below.
Why it is uniform and common. Moving a small number dN of one species from r1​ to r2​ changes the free energy by [μσ​(r2​)−μσ​(r1​)]dN. If this difference were nonzero, a transfer could lower the free energy. Equilibrium under diffusion therefore requires each full chemical potential to be spatially constant. A spin flip transferring dN from down to up similarly changes free energy by (μ↑​−μ↓​)dN, proving equality when spin relaxation is permitted. This is chemical equilibrium of a classical spin gas. Mere thermal contact at fixed separate spin populations would not enforce the latter equality; full spin equilibrium is the necessary assumption. A spatially varying magnetic field is balanced by a spatially varying density, not by a varying full chemical potential.
Magnetization. Invert the component equations at the common μ:
n↑​(r)=nQ​eβμex(r),n↓​(r)=nQ​eβμe−x(r).
(25)
The two levels have moments +μB​ and −μB​ along the field. The magnetization, magnetic moment per unit volume, is therefore
M(r)=μB​(n↑​−n↓​)=2μB​nQ​eβμsinhx(r).​
(26)
Its SI units are Am−1. This expression neglects interactions and magnetic feedback of the gas on the specified external field.
High-temperature response. For ∣x∣≪1, sinhx≃x and coshx≃1, yielding
M(r)≃kB​T2μB2​nQ​eβμ​B(r)≃kB​Tn(r)μB2​​B(r).​
(27)
At fixed number density, this is the Curie law: the susceptibility with respect to B is nμB2​/(kB​T). The conventional dimensionless susceptibility with respect to H is approximately χv​=μ0​nμB2​/(kB​T) when B≃μ0​H. At fixed μ, however, nQ​eβμ changes with temperature, so M itself need not scale simply as 1/T. The moment per particle has the Curie behaviour; the density constraint must be stated.
Total density. Adding the two populations gives
n(r)=2nQ​eβμcoshx(r),n(r)M(r)​=μB​tanhx(r).​
(28)
For a finite container and fixed N, normalization gives eβμ=N/[2nQ​∫coshx(r)d3r]. In a weak field the leading density is uniform, with changes only of order x2. Particles preferentially occupy regions where the magnitude of the field lowers one of the spin energies, even though the common chemical potential remains uniform.
