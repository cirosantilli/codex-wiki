First solve the equation of state for the volume:
V(T,p,N)=pNkB​T​−NA(T).
(1)
The Gibbs free energy has differential dG=−SdT+Vdp+μdN. At fixed temperature and particle number, integrate dG=Vdp between the two pressures:
ΔG=∫p0​p1​​[pNkB​T​−NA(T)]dp.
(2)
Consequently
ΔG=NkB​Tlnp0​p1​​−NA(T)(p1​−p0​).​
(3)
Use S=−(∂G/∂T)p,N​, keeping both endpoint pressures fixed while differentiating the difference:
ΔS=−NkB​lnp0​p1​​+NA′(T)(p1​−p0​).​
(4)
Equivalently, the Maxwell relation (∂S/∂p)T,N​=−(∂V/∂T)p,N​ gives −NkB​/p+NA′(T), whose pressure integral is the same result. These are the Gibbs free-energy change for a pressure-linear gas correction identities. The physical path requires p0​,p1​>0 and positive volume throughout. The correction A(T) has units of volume per particle, so each term in ΔG is an energy and each term in ΔS has units of energy per kelvin.

For a phonon mode of angular frequency ω, the equilibrium occupation is
nω​=eℏω/(kB​T)−11​.
(5)
Well above the Debye temperature, the relevant vibrational energies are small compared with kB​T. Expanding the exponential gives
nω​≃ℏωkB​T​,Nph​=∑q,s​nq,s​≃ℏkB​T​∑q,s​ωq,s​1​.
(6)
The spectrum and its cutoff are fixed in the model, so the remaining sum is temperature independent. Thus
Nph​∝T,ρ∝T(T≫ΘD​).​
(7)
For example, the three-dimensional Debye model with D(ω)=9Nω2/ωD3​ gives Nph​≃(9/2)NT/ΘD​. This is the high-temperature phonon number in the Debye model. The number of phonon quanta can exceed 3N; 3N counts modes, not their total occupation. Under the stipulated proportionality to phonon number, only the phonon contribution to resistivity has been calculated. A temperature-independent residual resistivity would add an intercept in a real sample.

Particles within each species are indistinguishable, while the two species are physically distinguishable and their numbers are fixed. Independence makes the canonical partition function factor by species:
Z=NA​!Z1NA​​​NB​!Z1NB​​​=NA​!NB​!Z1N​​,N=NA​+NB​.​
(8)
Since Z1​∝T3/2 at fixed volume, U=−∂β​lnZ=3NkB​T/2. The canonical entropy is therefore
S=kB​[NlnZ1​−ln(NA​!)−ln(NB​!)+23​N].​
(9)
For large species populations, Stirling's approximation gives the equivalent Sackur-Tetrode equation form
S≃kB​i=A,B∑​Ni​[lnNi​Z1​​+25​].​
(10)
For structureless particles with the same translational partition function, Z1​=V/λ3, where λ is their common thermal de Broglie wavelength. More generally the supplied common constant factor in Z1​ should be retained in the entropy.
Writing xi​=Ni​/N separates the total-number contribution from the composition term:
S≃NkB​[lnNZ1​​+25​]−NkB​(xA​lnxA​+xB​lnxB​).
(11)
The last term is the entropy of mixing relative to separated ideal components at the same temperature and pressure. This is the entropy of a two-species classical ideal gas. Artificially labeling otherwise identical chemical atoms would not justify dividing by separate factorials or adding a mixing entropy. The pure-species limit is obtained by xlnx→0.

Fermion occupation and ordinary metallic heat capacity. The Fermi-Dirac distribution for a single-particle state is
⟨n(E)⟩=e(E−μ)/(kB​T)+11​.​
(12)
At low temperature it changes appreciably only in an energy window of width of order kB​T about μ≃EF​. If the full density of states D(E) is smooth and nonzero there, only about D(EF​)kB​T states change population. Each excited particle or hole carries energy of order kB​T relative to the Fermi surface, so U(T)−U(0) is of order D(EF​)(kB​T)2. Differentiation therefore gives Cel​∝T.
More precisely, the fixed-number Sommerfeld expansion gives
U(T)−U(0)=6π2​D(EF​)(kB​T)2+O(T4),Cel​=3π2​D(EF​)kB2​T+O(T3).​
(13)
Here D includes all counted internal species. This argument requires kB​T≪EF​ and a density of states varying slowly across the thermal window; a band edge or vanishing density of states can change the power law.
Two-dimensional linear dispersion at zero chemical potential. Let g count identical positive-energy branches, keeping the state-counting convention explicit. The number of wavevector states in a ring of radius k is gA(2πkdk)/(2π)2. With E=ℏvk, this gives
D+​(E)=2πℏ2v2gAE​,E≥0.
(14)
At μ=0, the thermal energy of this branch is
U+​(T)=∫0∞​D+​(E)eE/(kB​T)+1EdE​=2πℏ2v2gA(kB​T)3​∫0∞​ex+1x2​dx.
(15)
The dimensionless integral is 3ζ(3)/2. Differentiating gives
C+​(T)=4π9gζ(3)​ℏ2v2AkB3​T2​∼ℏ2v2AkB3​T2​.​
(16)
This is the heat capacity for a two-dimensional linear fermion dispersion. A physical neutral particle–hole-symmetric Dirac cone also has electron holes in its filled negative-energy band. Their excitation energy gives an equal contribution, doubling C+​ for the same cone count. The T2 scaling is unchanged. For a positive-energy-only gas, holding μ=0 allows its mean particle number to vary; a neutral two-band surface instead maintains zero net excess charge as electron–hole pairs are thermally excited. These conventions should not be conflated when specifying an exact coefficient.
Nonzero chemical potential. The ordinary metallic regime is
kB​T≪μorT≪μ/kB​.​
(17)
The thermal window then lies far from the cone tip. Since D(E)∝E, its fractional change across that window is only of order kB​T/μ. Treating it as locally constant restores the Sommerfeld result Cel​∝D(μ)T∝μT. When kB​T is comparable to or larger than μ, the window probes the vanishing density of states near the tip and crosses toward the neutral T2 behavior, provided the linear dispersion remains valid.
Bulk phonons and the surface-to-bulk comparison. For the topological insulator model, assume bulk electronic excitations are frozen out while the conducting surface remains active. In three dimensions, acoustic phonons have a density of modes proportional to ω2. Normalize the Debye spectrum to 3N modes:
Dph​(ω)=ωD3​9Nω2​,ℏωD​=kB​ΘD​.
(18)
The thermal part of the energy is
Uph​=ωD3​9Nℏ​∫0ωD​​eℏω/(kB​T)−1ω3​dω.
(19)
At T≪ΘD​, extending the upper integration limit to infinity introduces an exponentially small error. With x=ℏω/(kB​T) and ∫0∞​x3/(ex−1)dx=π4/15,
Uph​≃53π4​NkB​ΘD3​T4​,Cph​≃512π4​NkB​(ΘD​T​)3.​
(20)
Temperature-independent zero-point energy makes no contribution to this heat capacity. The requested ratio consequently scales as
Cph​Cel​​∼ℏ2v2NTAkB2​ΘD3​​.​
(21)
For a fixed shape and composition, A∝L2 and N∝L3, so the ratio grows as 1/(LT). Smaller samples and lower temperatures favor the surface contribution. It dominates if the dimensionless right-hand side, including its state-counting prefactor, exceeds unity. This is the surface-electron and bulk-phonon heat-capacity crossover.
The continuum electronic law also requires a thermal energy larger than the surface finite-size level spacing, of order ℏv/L. The phonon continuum has its own finite-size cutoff. Therefore dominance obtained by formally taking T→0 cannot always be inferred from the two continuum power laws alone.
There is a numerical-normalization qualification in the paper. Combining the explicit one-positive-branch result above with the conventional 3N-mode Debye normalization gives ratio prefactor 15gζ(3)/(16π5); including electron holes doubles it. For g=1, this is approximately 0.00368 before that doubling, rather than the supplied 5/(12π2)≃0.0422. The problem specifies neither a cone/spin multiplicity that equates these nor another mode normalization. Its supplied prefactor is therefore used as the stipulated approximation for the requested numerical estimate, rather than claimed as an exact consequence of the single-branch count.
Numerical estimate. A cubic sample of volume 1mm3 has side L=10−3m. Counting the conducting surface on all six faces gives
A=6L2=6×10−6m2.
(22)
For one atom per cubic cell of side a=1.5×10−9m,
N≃a3L3​=2.96×1017.
(23)
Use kB​=1.380649×10−23JK−1 and ℏ=1.05457×10−34Js. Inserting the stated v=5×105ms−1, ΘD​=500K and T=1K into the paper's supplied prefactor gives
Cph​Cel​​≃7.33×10−6.​
(24)
Thus bulk phonons dominate at this temperature in this estimate. If only one face contributes, the ratio is 1.22×10−6; if there are ν chemical atoms per unit cell, divide the corresponding estimate by ν. These geometrical and crystallographic factors are not supplied explicitly.
With the same prefactor and geometry, extrapolating 1/T would suggest equality near 7.3μK. However, the surface finite-size scale is already of order ℏv/(kB​L)≃3.8mK for this specimen. That extrapolated crossover lies outside the continuum electronic regime, so it is not a reliable prediction of the sample's ultimate lowest-temperature heat capacity.

Classical translational statistics. Write the number density as n=N/V and the thermal de Broglie wavelength as λ=2πℏ2/(mkB​T)​. Quantum exchange is negligible when wave packets rarely overlap:
nλ3≪1orn−1/3≫λ.​
(25)
The equivalent state-counting statement is that the mean occupation of each thermally relevant one-particle state is much smaller than one. The equations in this question count one internal species; with g equally populated spin states the relevant density per species is n/g.
Let z=eβμ be the dimensionless quantum gas fugacity, with the lowest one-particle energy chosen as zero. Both quantum distributions can be written
nη​(E)=z−1eβE−η1​=1−ηze−βEze−βE​,η={+1−1​bosons,fermions.​
(26)
If z≪1, then nη​(E)≃ze−βE≪1 for all E≥0, recovering Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics. Conversely, low occupation even at the spectrum's bottom requires z/(1−z)≪1 for bosons or z/(1+z)≪1 for fermions; either condition implies
z=eβμ≪1.​
(27)
A single high-energy state can be sparsely occupied even in a degenerate gas, so the condition must hold across the relevant spectrum, not just for one arbitrarily chosen energy. Also, classical translational statistics does not imply that all internal excitation energies or other quantum effects can be ignored.
Grand partition function, density and pressure. Noninteracting particles have independent occupations of each one-particle state. Put xk​=ze−βεk​. Summing the allowed occupations gives
Ξk​={∑r=0∞​xkr​=(1−xk​)−11+xk​​bosons,fermions,​Ξ=∏k​(1−ηxk​)−η.
(28)
Therefore
lnΞ=−η∑k​ln(1−ηze−βεk​).
(29)
The grand potential is ΩG​=−kB​TlnΞ=−pV for this homogeneous gas. Replacing the sum by V∫d3k/(2π)3 and using εk​=ℏ2k2/(2m) gives
βp=−η∫(2π)3d3k​ln[1−ηze−βℏ2k2/(2m)].​
(30)
The mean number is N=z∂z​lnΞ, giving
VN​=∫(2π)3d3k​z−1eβℏ2k2/(2m)−η1​.​
(31)
For bosons, the grand sum requires z<1 when the ground energy is zero. A macroscopic condensate at the limiting z→1 must be separated from the continuum integral in the condensed phase. The following small-z calculation is in the normal, nondegenerate regime, so that complication does not enter.
Density expansion and inversion. Expand the occupation as a geometric series:
nη​(E)=ze−βE+ηz2e−2βE+z3e−3βE+⋯.
(32)
The Gaussian wavevector integral for its ℓth term is
∫(2π)3d3k​e−ℓβℏ2k2/(2m)=(2πℓβℏ2m​)3/2=λ3ℓ3/21​.
(33)
It follows that
nλ3=z+23/2ηz2​+O(z3).​
(34)
For the inverse series, put y=nλ3 and try z=y+cy2+O(y3). Substitution gives 0=(c+η/23/2)y2+O(y3), hence
z=nλ3−23/2η​(nλ3)2+O((nλ3)3).​
(35)
The leading equality nλ3≃z directly connects the density and occupancy criteria for classical behavior.
Pressure and its first quantum correction. Expanding the logarithm in the pressure expression gives one extra factor 1/ℓ compared with the density series:
βpλ3=z+25/2ηz2​+O(z3).
(36)
Insert the inverse series. To second order in y=nλ3,
βpλ3=y−23/2ηy2​+25/2ηy2​+O(y3)=y−25/2ηy2​+O(y3).
(37)
Thus
pV=NkB​T[1−25/2η​VNλ3​+O((Nλ3/V)2)].​
(38)
The first quantum correction at fixed density and temperature is
Δp=−25/2η​kB​Tn2λ3.​
(39)
It is negative for bosons and positive for fermions, and vanishes in the classical density limit.
Virial coefficient. Define the number-density virial expansion by
nkB​Tp​=1+B2​(T)n+B3​(T)n2+⋯=∑j=1∞​Bj​(T)nj−1,B1​=1.
(40)
The coefficient Bj​ has units volumej−1 in this convention. Comparing terms gives
B2ex​(T)=−25/2η​λ3=−25/2η​(mkB​T2πℏ2​)3/2.​
(41)
This is the exchange contribution to the second virial coefficient. It arises without a pair-interaction potential; the underlying gas is noninteracting. A molar-density convention would multiply this B2​ by the Avogadro constant. For g equally populated internal species, exchange occurs within each species and the coefficient in terms of total density is smaller by a factor g.
Why the two signs differ. Bosonic exchange symmetry enhances the probability of nearby identical bosons, whereas fermionic exchange symmetry suppresses nearby identical fermions in the same internal state. In the dilute regime, the normalized equal-time pair detection correlation is
g(2)(r)=1+ηe−2πr2/λ2.
(42)
The exchange part follows by squaring the Gaussian one-particle coherence factor e−πr2/λ2. At zero separation this gives g(2)(0)=2 for bosons and 0 for one-component fermions. Integrating the exchange pair weight provides a direct coefficient check:
B2ex​=−21​∫[g(2)(r)−1]d3r=−2η​(2λ2​)3/2=−25/2ηλ3​.
(43)
Bunching therefore acts like an effective statistical attraction in the dilute equation of state, lowering the pressure needed at fixed density. Exclusion acts like an effective statistical repulsion, raising it. Neither description asserts an actual interparticle force. Different internal fermion species do not exclude each other from a common spatial location in the same way, explaining the importance of the species convention.

Order parameter and the free-energy landscape. In Landau theory, take a scalar magnetization component at fixed volume and composition. It is an order parameter: its nonzero spontaneous value distinguishes an ordered phase with ferromagnetism from the symmetric high-temperature phase. One first constructs a constrained order-parameter free energy at specified magnetization by summing over microscopic states compatible with it. An external magnetic field is an independent control, so it can also appear in that constrained function. For a total magnetic moment the conjugate term is −BM; if M is a magnetization density, the total-energy term is −VBM, or −BM in free-energy density. Minimizing over M then yields the equilibrium thermodynamic potential as a function of the imposed field.
Thus treating both M and B as arguments of a Landau function does not treat them as independent natural variables of the already-minimized equilibrium free energy. The first is the fluctuating or constrained order parameter, and the second its conjugate field.
At zero field, reversal symmetry requires F(M)=F(−M), excluding odd powers. Near a continuous transition, assume the homogeneous constrained free energy is analytic for small M, that the quadratic coefficient crosses zero linearly, and that the quartic coefficient is positive. Then
F(T,M)=F0​(T)+b(T−Tc​)M2+dM4,b>0,d>0.
(44)
The temperature Tc​ is where the zero-field quadratic coefficient vanishes and spontaneous ordering begins in this approximation. Higher powers are negligible sufficiently close to it; spatial fluctuations are neglected in this mean-field treatment.
Stationarity gives 2M[b(T−Tc​)+2dM2]=0. Above Tc​, the only minimum is at zero. Below it, zero becomes a maximum and two minima appear:
Meq​=0(T>Tc​),Meq​=±2db(Tc​−T)​​(T<Tc​).​
(45)
The two signs describe spontaneous symmetry breaking: they are the average magnetizations in the symmetry-broken pure phases. A strictly finite system at exactly zero field samples the two wells symmetrically and has ensemble average ⟨M⟩=0. A spontaneous pure-phase value is defined by selecting a phase, for example taking the thermodynamic limit before removing a small field. This qualification distinguishes a minimum's location from the average over a symmetric two-well distribution.
Minimum free energy and the heat-capacity jump. At a nonzero minimum, M2=−b(T−Tc​)/(2d). Substituting this value gives
b(T−Tc​)M2+dM4=−2db2(T−Tc​)2​+4db2(T−Tc​)2​.
(46)
Hence
Fmin​(T)=F0​(T)−4db2(T−Tc​)2​(T<Tc​),​
(47)
while Fmin​=F0​ above the transition. Take the temperature derivatives only after minimizing, so the equilibrium order parameter is allowed to change with temperature. With C0​=−TF0′′​(T),
CV​={C0​+Tb2/(2d),C0​,​T<Tc​,T>Tc​.​
(48)
Therefore
ΔCV​≡CV​(Tc−​)−CV​(Tc+​)=2dTc​b2​.​
(49)
This is the heat-capacity jump in quartic Landau theory. The entropy is continuous at Tc​, so there is no latent heat. If F denotes free-energy density rather than total free energy, these expressions denote heat capacity per unit volume with the same normalization.
Stabilizing a tricritical expansion. At a tricritical point, the quartic coefficient is tuned to zero at the same point where the quadratic coefficient changes sign. Reversal symmetry still excludes odd powers. The next available stabilizing term is therefore
F=F0​+b(T−Tc​)M2+d′M6,d′>0.​
(50)
A negative or vanishing leading sextic coefficient would not stabilize this truncation for large ∣M∣; a higher positive term would then be needed and the present tricritical form would be insufficient. The ordinary quartic theory has a continuous transition for d>0, while a stabilized theory with d<0 can have a first-order transition. Their meeting at zero field is what makes the simultaneous tuning tricritical.
The tricritical minimum. At zero field and T<Tc​, stationarity gives
∂M∂F​=2M[b(T−Tc​)+3d′M4]=0,Meq4​=3d′b(Tc​−T)​.​
(51)
The nonzero solutions are minima: their curvature is 2b(T−Tc​)+30d′M4=8b(Tc​−T)>0. Put t=Tc​−T>0. Since d′M6=(bt/3)M2 at stationarity,
Fmin​−F0​=−btM2+3bt​M2=−32​bt3d′bt​​.
(52)
Thus
Fmin​(T)=F0​(T)−27d′​2b3/2(Tc​−T)3/2​.​
(53)
The order parameter now vanishes as (Tc​−T)1/4 rather than the quartic theory's square root.
Heat-capacity singularity. Define K=2b3/2/27d′​>0, so the ordered-side singular free energy is Fsing​=−K(Tc​−T)3/2. Its derivatives are
dTdFsing​​=23​K(Tc​−T)1/2,dT2d2Fsing​​=−43​K(Tc​−T)−1/2.
(54)
Consequently
CVsing​=−TdT2d2Fsing​​=23d′​Tb3/2​(Tc​−T)−1/2,α=21​.​
(55)
This is the tricritical heat-capacity amplitude in a sextic Landau expansion. It is positive on the ordered side and diverges as T→Tc−​, rather than remaining a finite jump. This value of the heat-capacity critical exponent is the mean-field result along the path keeping the quartic term zero; a detuning to d>0 ultimately restores ordinary quartic behavior. Fluctuation corrections to the mean-field theory are not included in the stipulated polynomial minimization.
