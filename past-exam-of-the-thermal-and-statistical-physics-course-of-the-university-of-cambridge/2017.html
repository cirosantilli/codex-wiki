Put ξ=ϵ−ϵF​ and Δ=∣Δ∣>0. The BCS quasiparticle spectrum has E=ξ2+Δ2​, so on either inverse branch
ξ±​=±E2−Δ2​,​dEdξ±​​​=E2−Δ2​E​.
(1)
Conservation of states on one branch gives Dbranch​(E)dE=g(ϵF​)∣dξ∣. Consequently the single-branch expression requested in the source is
Dbranch​(E)=E2−Δ2​g(ϵF​)E​,E>Δ.​
(2)
There are no such states for 0<E<Δ in this ideal fully gapped model. Near the edge the density of states diverges as (E−Δ)−1/2, an integrable singularity; at large E/Δ it approaches its normal-state value.
Counting qualification. The energy map in the source is two-to-one. If g(ϵ) literally counts all normal-state levels both above and below ϵF​, the unweighted positive-energy quasiparticle density from a folded spectrum must sum both preimages, and is instead
Dqp​(E)=±∑​g(ϵF​+ξ±​)​dEdξ±​​​=E2−Δ2​2g(ϵF​)E​.​
(3)
Already at Δ=0, folding ξ into ∣ξ∣ doubles the positive-axis density. Thus the printed formula is not the unweighted count of all states under the literal mapping without an additional normalization convention.
There is also a standard physical quantity with exactly the printed normalization: the BCS electronic density of states, measured for example by single-electron superconducting tunneling. At positive E, the electron spectral weights of the two roots are the BCS coherence factors u±2​=(1+ξ±​/E)/2. They sum to one, so the weighted sum is g(ϵF​)E/E2−Δ2​. This supplies a valid electronic-DOS interpretation, while keeping it distinct from counting every positive-energy excitation. Spin degeneracy must be included consistently in g and the transformed density; it does not remove the branch-counting issue.

Single-occupancy cells. For N indistinguishable particles, specifying which N of the M distinct cells are occupied completely specifies a configuration. Thus
Windist​=(NM​)=N!(M−N)!M!​.​
(5)
For distinguishable particles, each chosen occupied set admits N! assignments of particle labels:
Wdist​=(NM​)N!=(M−N)!M!​.​
(6)
Equivalently, successively place labeled particles in M,M−1,…,M−N+1 available cells. The single-occupancy constraint is essential: this is not unrestricted stars and bars. Both counts give one empty configuration at N=0; at N=M they give one unlabeled occupation pattern and M! labeled assignments.

In the grand canonical ensemble, the system exchanges both energy and particles with a reservoir at fixed temperature T and chemical potential μ. The probability of a particular many-body microstate i, with energy Ei​ and particle number Ni​, is its Boltzmann weight e−β(Ei​−μNi​) divided by the grand canonical partition function Ξ, where β=1/(kB​T). The denominator normalizes ∑i​pi​=1; a degeneracy is accounted for by counting distinct microstates, rather than changing an individual state's probability.
For noninteracting particles, specify a microstate by the occupation numbers nr​ of one-particle levels of energy ϵr​. Then E−μN=∑r​(ϵr​−μ)nr​, so the Gibbs probability factorizes between levels. Put xr​=e−β(ϵr​−μ).
For fermions, the Pauli exclusion principle permits nr​=0,1 in each complete one-particle state, giving Ξr​=1+xr​. Its mean occupancy is
⟨nr​⟩=1+xr​0+xr​​=eβ(ϵr​−μ)+11​,​
(7)
the Fermi-Dirac distribution.
For bosons, nr​=0,1,2,…, so the geometric sum is Ξr​=1/(1−xr​) when xr​<1. Differentiating that sum gives
⟨nr​⟩=∑n=0∞​xrn​∑n=0∞​nxrn​​=1−xr​xr​​=eβ(ϵr​−μ)−11​,​
(8)
the Bose-Einstein distribution. Finite-system convergence requires μ<ϵmin​; condensation is the limiting approach to the lowest energy with macroscopic ground-state occupation. Both quantum distributions reduce to e−β(ϵr​−μ) when occupation is small. The quantities derived here are mean occupancies of states, rather than normalized probabilities over the set of one-particle energies.

Structure through pair correlations. For a homogeneous isotropic monatomic liquid of number density n=N/V, the radial distribution function g(r) is defined by
dNneighbours​=4πnr2g(r)dr.​
(9)
It compares the conditional density around a tagged particle with the bulk density. A repulsive core makes close neighbours unlikely; a first peak identifies a preferred coordination shell, and damped peaks at larger radii describe short-range order. Far away, g(r)→1 in the bulk limit. Unlike a crystal, an ordinary liquid has no indefinitely repeated positional order. An ideal uncorrelated gas has g(r)=1 away from the tagged particle. The shell factor 4πr2 must not be confused with g(r) itself.
The same pair structure enters the static structure factor measured through scattering. In the bulk isotropic convention,
S(k)=1+4πn∫0∞​[g(r)−1]r2krsinkr​dr.
(10)
Thus real-space coordination and reciprocal-space scattering peaks describe the same correlations.
Energy from the structure. If interactions are sums of a central pair potential v(r), the equipartition theorem gives the classical kinetic energy 3NkB​T/2. Count the interaction of a tagged particle with its neighbours and divide by two to avoid counting each pair twice. The pair-correlation expression for internal energy is
U=23​NkB​T+2πNn∫0∞​r2v(r)g(r)dr.​
(11)
This makes structure thermodynamically useful: short-distance repulsion and the attractive region contribute with different weights. Additional internal molecular modes or many-body interactions require extra terms. The formula is a classical bulk pair-potential result, not a universal energy formula for every liquid.
Pressure and the dilute expansion. The mechanical virial theorem relates the pair forces to pressure. The virial pressure from pair correlations is
p=nkB​T−32πn2​∫0∞​r3v′(r)g(r)dr.
(12)
At low density, g(r)≃e−βv(r), giving the virial expansion
nkB​Tp​=1+B2​(T)n+B3​(T)n2+⋯,B2​(T)=−2π∫0∞​[e−βv(r)−1]r2dr.​
(13)
The second virial coefficient has units of volume per particle; higher coefficients encode additional cluster correlations. A hard-sphere exclusion diameter d gives B2​=2πd3/3>0. Attractions contribute negative terms and can make B2​ negative. Although the pair-correlation formulas can describe a dense liquid when its actual g(r) is known, truncating the virial series after a few terms is normally a dilute-fluid approximation. It should not be silently extrapolated through condensation or into a dense liquid.

Excited-state saturation. Consider first a uniform three-dimensional noninteracting gas of identical spinless bosons in a large box. Set the one-particle ground energy to zero. The Bose-Einstein distribution is nˉ(ϵ)=[z−1eβϵ−1]−1, with quantum gas fugacity z=eβμ≤1. For dispersion ϵ=p2/(2m), the excited density of states is
g(ϵ)=4π2V​(ℏ22m​)3/2ϵ​.
(14)
Using the thermal de Broglie wavelength λT​=2πℏ2/(mkB​T)​, integration gives
Nex​=λT3​V​Li3/2​(z),N0​=1−zz​.
(15)
The polylogarithm is Lis​(z)=∑ℓ≥1​zℓ/ℓs. Its finite value at z=1 implies a maximum excited population Vζ(3/2)/λT3​, with ζ(3/2)≃2.612. Once this is less than N, the excess particles occupy the ground state macroscopically. This is Bose-Einstein condensation, rather than occupation of a state by merely one or a few atoms.
Transition and condensate fraction. Setting the saturation number equal to N gives the homogeneous ideal Bose gas condensation temperature
Tc​=mkB​2πℏ2​[ζ(3/2)n​]2/3.​
(16)
Below this temperature, μ approaches zero from below in the thermodynamic limit, and particle conservation gives
NN0​​=1−(Tc​T​)3/2(0≤T≤Tc​).​
(17)
Above Tc​ there is no macroscopic condensate and μ<0 adjusts to keep N fixed. At finite N, the chemical potential remains slightly below the ground energy and the transition is rounded.
Energy and the role of geometry. The excited-state internal energy is
U=23​λT3​VkB​T​Li5/2​(z).
(18)
The zero-energy condensate contributes no term to this energy in the ideal model. Below Tc​,
U=23​NkB​Tζ(3/2)ζ(5/2)​(Tc​T​)3/2,CV​=415​NkB​ζ(3/2)ζ(5/2)​(Tc​T​)3/2.
(19)
Condensation depends on low-energy state counting: for a uniform quadratic-dispersion gas, a finite-temperature bulk condensate requires spatial dimension greater than two. A trap changes the density of states and therefore the temperature law; a three-dimensional harmonic trap gives a cubic, rather than 3/2, condensate-fraction exponent. Finally, an ideal-gas condensate should not automatically be called a superfluid: its free quadratic spectrum has zero critical speed by the Landau criterion. Interactions matter for the robust collective-flow properties of real dilute condensates.

Quanta of collective motion. An elementary excitation is a low-energy departure from the many-body ground state that can often be treated as a quasiparticle. Near an ordered equilibrium, small collective motions form normal modes. Quantization turns a harmonic mode of frequency ωk​ into bosonic excitations with energy ℏωk​. Their number is not an independently conserved thermodynamic variable in equilibrium, so their chemical potential is zero and
nˉk​=eℏωk​/(kB​T)−11​,U−U0​=k,ν∑​ℏωk,ν​nˉk,ν​.​
(20)
Here ν labels branches, and U0​ contains the ground-state contribution, including phonon zero-point energy where appropriate. The approximation is useful while interactions and damping of the excitations are weak enough that the modes remain identifiable.
Phonons. A phonon is a quantum of an elastic lattice-vibration mode. Long-wavelength acoustic phonon branches have ω≃cν​k, with one longitudinal and two transverse polarizations in a three-dimensional monatomic solid. A multi-atom primitive cell can also support optical phonon branches, generally with nonzero frequency at k=0. Linear acoustic dispersion gives D(ϵ)∝ϵ2, so the energy integral has U−U0​∝T4 and the heat capacity obeys the Debye T3 law. In the Debye model,
CV​≃512π4​NkB​(ΘD​T​)3(T≪ΘD​).​
(21)
At temperatures high compared with all phonon energies but below structural changes, the classical harmonic limit gives approximately CV​=3NkB​. Optical modes are frozen out at temperatures well below their gaps.
Spin waves. A spin wave is a spatially varying collective precession of ordered spins; its quantum is a magnon. In a simple isotropic ferromagnet, a long-wavelength gapless branch has energy ϵk​≃Ds​k2, where the spin stiffness Ds​ has units Jm2. State counting gives D(ϵ)∝ϵ1/2. For one isotropic branch in three dimensions,
VU−U0​​=4π2Ds3/2​Γ(5/2)ζ(5/2)​(kB​T)5/2,CVmagnon​∝T3/2.​
(22)
This exponent is specific to a gapless quadratic ferromagnetic branch. Long-wavelength antiferromagnetic spin waves can be linear and instead give a T3 heat-capacity law in three dimensions. Magnetic anisotropy or an applied field can open a gap, causing activated behaviour at still lower temperatures. Near a magnetic ordering transition, a dilute noninteracting spin-wave description is no longer adequate.
These examples illustrate the low-temperature energy of gapless bosonic excitations: for ϵ∝ks in d dimensions, D(ϵ)∝ϵd/s−1, hence U−U0​∝T1+d/s and CV​∝Td/s. The exponent records both the spatial dimension and the dispersion, making calorimetry a probe of elementary excitations.

Component chemical potentials. Define the per-spin quantum concentration nQ​=λT−3​=(mkB​T/(2πℏ2))3/2. For a classical component with one-particle potential energy Uσ​(r), the local Helmholtz free energy density is kB​Tnσ​[ln(nσ​/nQ​)−1]+nσ​Uσ​. Its derivative with respect to number density gives the full local chemical potential, including the magnetic energy. With the up state defined as the lower level,
μ↑​=kB​TlnnQ​n↑​​−μB​B,μ↓​=kB​TlnnQ​n↓​​+μB​B.​
(23)
The Bohr magneton μB​ is a magnetic moment; μB​B is an energy. The factor two for the two spin states is not already included in nQ​.
Total equilibrium chemical potential. If spin conversion is allowed, a particle can be added in either spin state at the same equilibrium free-energy cost. Thus
μ=μ↑​=μ↓​,​
(24)
not their sum or zero. The total particle number, or a reservoir, fixes this common value; the supplied information does not specify a numerical chemical potential. A useful local expression is μ=kB​Tln[n/(2nQ​coshx)], with x=μB​B/(kB​T), whose density dependence is found below.
Why it is uniform and common. Moving a small number dN of one species from r1​ to r2​ changes the free energy by [μσ​(r2​)−μσ​(r1​)]dN. If this difference were nonzero, a transfer could lower the free energy. Equilibrium under diffusion therefore requires each full chemical potential to be spatially constant. A spin flip transferring dN from down to up similarly changes free energy by (μ↑​−μ↓​)dN, proving equality when spin relaxation is permitted. This is chemical equilibrium of a classical spin gas. Mere thermal contact at fixed separate spin populations would not enforce the latter equality; full spin equilibrium is the necessary assumption. A spatially varying magnetic field is balanced by a spatially varying density, not by a varying full chemical potential.
Magnetization. Invert the component equations at the common μ:
n↑​(r)=nQ​eβμex(r),n↓​(r)=nQ​eβμe−x(r).
(25)
The two levels have moments +μB​ and −μB​ along the field. The magnetization, magnetic moment per unit volume, is therefore
M(r)=μB​(n↑​−n↓​)=2μB​nQ​eβμsinhx(r).​
(26)
Its SI units are Am−1. This expression neglects interactions and magnetic feedback of the gas on the specified external field.
High-temperature response. For ∣x∣≪1, sinhx≃x and coshx≃1, yielding
M(r)≃kB​T2μB2​nQ​eβμ​B(r)≃kB​Tn(r)μB2​​B(r).​
(27)
At fixed number density, this is the Curie law: the susceptibility with respect to B is nμB2​/(kB​T). The conventional dimensionless susceptibility with respect to H is approximately χv​=μ0​nμB2​/(kB​T) when B≃μ0​H. At fixed μ, however, nQ​eβμ changes with temperature, so M itself need not scale simply as 1/T. The moment per particle has the Curie behaviour; the density constraint must be stated.
Total density. Adding the two populations gives
n(r)=2nQ​eβμcoshx(r),n(r)M(r)​=μB​tanhx(r).​
(28)
For a finite container and fixed N, normalization gives eβμ=N/[2nQ​∫coshx(r)d3r]. In a weak field the leading density is uniform, with changes only of order x2. Particles preferentially occupy regions where the magnitude of the field lowers one of the spin energies, even though the common chemical potential remains uniform.

Gibbs free energy and its minimum. For fixed atom number N, there are N+n sites and
W(N,n)=(nN+n​)
(29)
configurations with n indistinguishable vacancies. Their configurational entropy is kB​lnW. Relative to the vacancy-free Gibbs free energy G0​, creating n vacancies costs energy nϵ and pressure-volume work npv. Put A=ϵ+pv>0. Then
G(n)=G0​+nA−kB​TlnN!n!(N+n)!​.
(30)
At leading thermodynamic order, Stirling's approximation gives
G(n)≃G0​+nA−kB​T[(N+n)ln(N+n)−NlnN−nlnn].
(31)
The minimum obtained from the stationary point below is
Gmin​≃G0​+NkB​Tln(1−e−A/(kB​T)).​
(32)
If the perfect crystal's internal energy and other entropy are chosen as zero, its pressure term is G0​=pNv. More generally the information given fixes the vacancy contribution rather than the absolute bulk free energy. The pressure-volume term must be retained because the number of sites, and hence the volume, changes.
Equilibrium vacancy population. Differentiating the extensive free energy at fixed N,T,p gives
∂n∂G​=A+kB​TlnN+nn​,∂n2∂2G​=n(N+n)kB​TN​>0.
(33)
The stationary point is consequently the unique interior minimum. Write q=e−A/(kB​T); its condition is n/(N+n)=q, giving the requested thermodynamic result for equilibrium vacancies with variable lattice size,
n∗​≃1−qNq​=e(ϵ+pv)/(kB​T)−1N​.​
(34)
Substitution back into G cancels the terms containing n∗​ and yields the boxed minimum. In the dilute-defect limit A≫kB​T, n∗​≃Ne−A/(kB​T). The Bose-like denominator is a consequence of the changing number of lattice sites and combinatorial entropy; it does not assert that vacancies here are an ideal gas of bosonic particles.
There is a finite-size correction to a variable-site vacancy model if “average” is interpreted exactly. The microscopic count written above gives the relative isothermal-isobaric sum
Y=∑n=0∞​(nN+n​)qn=(1−q)−N−1,
(35)
so
⟨n⟩exact​=q∂q​lnY=1−q(N+1)q​.​
(36)
Its relative equilibrium free energy is −kB​TlnY=(N+1)kB​Tln(1−q). Thus the source's N numerator is correct to leading extensive order, but is not the exact finite-N mean for that stated configuration count. A free-energy minimum is likewise not generally identical to the mean of a finite distribution. These distinctions vanish relatively as N→∞.
Thermal volume. Each atom or vacancy occupies volume v, so at the same thermodynamic accuracy
⟨V(T)⟩≃v(N+n∗​)=1−e−(ϵ+pv)/(kB​T)Nv​.​
(37)
For the exact counting model, replace n∗​ by (N+1)q/(1−q) in v(N+⟨n⟩). The vacancy-free limit is Nv as T→0. This model isolates expansion from vacancies, taking v and ϵ constant; it omits ordinary phonon thermal expansion. Its growth at high temperature should not be extrapolated to arbitrarily large vacancy fractions in a stable crystal, and A>0 is essential for a normalizable variable-site ensemble.

One particle. Use the classical phase-space normalization of one quantum state per volume h3, with no unspecified internal degeneracy. For a>0 and β=1/(kB​T), the canonical partition function factorizes into momentum and position integrals:
Z1​=h31​∫e−βp2/(2m)d3p∫e−βard3r.
(38)
The Gaussian momentum integral is (2πmkB​T)3/2. In spherical coordinates, put t=βar:
∫e−βard3r=4π(akB​T​)3∫0∞​t2e−tdt=8π(akB​T​)3.
(39)
Therefore the classical ideal gas in a spherical linear trap has
Z1​(T,a)=λT3​8π​(akB​T​)3=h3a38π(2πm)3/2(kB​T)9/2​.​
(40)
Here a is a force, in Jm−1, so kB​T/a is a length and Z1​ is dimensionless. A nonconfining a≤0 would not give this finite spatial integral. As required by the classical phase-space approximation, the temperature must exceed the trap's discrete level scale.
Indistinguishable dilute particles. Independent labeled particles would give Z1N​. In the classical dilute regime, division by N! removes the overcounting of permutations of identical particles:
ZN​=N!Z1N​​.​
(41)
This is the Maxwell-Boltzmann limit, not an exact quantum canonical partition function at high occupation. Exchange effects cannot be captured merely by continuing this formula beyond its dilute regime.
Free energy, energy and entropy. The Helmholtz free energy is
F=−kB​TlnZN​=−NkB​TlnZ1​+kB​TlnN!.
(42)
With leading Stirling's approximation, lnN!≃NlnN−N, this becomes
F≃−NkB​T[lnNZ1​​+1].​
(43)
At fixed trap strength, ∂T​lnZ1​=9/(2T). Differentiating F gives the requested entropy,
S=−(∂T∂F​)N,a​≃NkB​[lnNZ1​​+211​].​
(44)
The 11/2 is 1+9/2: the first term comes from indistinguishability through Stirling's formula, and the second from the temperature dependence of Z1​. Without the large-N approximation, S=kB​[NlnZ1​−lnN!+(9/2)N]. Independently,
U=−∂β​lnZN​=29​NkB​T,Ca​=29​NkB​.
(45)
The kinetic contribution is 3NkB​T/2 and the linear-trap potential contribution is 3NkB​T, also obtained from generalized equipartition.
Entropy curves and reversible cooling. For a fixed N and a reference pair (T∗​,a∗​), the dependence can be written without logarithms of dimensional quantities:
NkB​S​=NkB​S∗​​+29​lnT∗​T​−3lna∗​a​.
(46)
At each fixed a, the entropy increases logarithmically and is concave downward in T; decreasing a shifts the curve upward. Its formal classical divergence as T→0 marks failure of classical statistics, not a physical negative-infinite entropy.
For a reversible adiabatic change, S is constant at fixed N, so
0=29​dlnT−3dlna,Tf​=Ti​(ai​af​​)2/3.​
(47)
Hence reducing a lowers the temperature: this is isentropic decompression of a spherical linear trap. The gas does work on the control that weakens the trap. Equivalently dU=⟨∂H/∂a⟩da=N⟨r⟩da, with ⟨r⟩=3kB​T/a; inserting U=9NkB​T/2 gives the same relation. Reversibility requires an equilibrium sequence without heat input or entropy production. Because Z1​ stays constant on this adiabat, N/Z1​ also stays constant: this mechanism cools the cloud but does not by itself increase quantum degeneracy.
Chemical potential and quantum threshold. From the extensive free energy,
μ=(∂N∂F​)T,a​≃kB​TlnZ1​N​.​
(48)
Thus the quantum gas fugacity is z=eμ/(kB​T)≃N/Z1​. Classical statistics requires z≪1; quantum effects become important as the chemical potential comes within order kB​T of the lowest one-particle energy. With the semiclassical zero at the potential minimum, the quantum degeneracy threshold in a spherical linear trap is consequently
Nquantum​∼Z1​(T,a)=λT3​8π​(akB​T​)3.​
(49)
There are no numerical values of m,T,a in the paper, so this parameter-dependent estimate is the requested atom number. It also follows from the peak density: n(0)=N/[8π(kB​T/a)3], and degeneracy starts when n(0)λT3​ is order one. The criterion is a crossover estimate, not an assertion that the classical formula remains valid at μ=0. For spinless bosons the continuum excited-state saturation is more specifically Nc​=ζ(9/2)Z1​, while for fermions the onset is occupation constrained by exclusion. At temperatures comparable with the quantum trap spacing, the actual ground energy must be included consistently rather than identified exactly with the classical minimum.
