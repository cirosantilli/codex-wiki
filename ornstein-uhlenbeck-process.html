Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Stochastic process Gaussian process
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 19
An Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process combines mean reversion with continuing random fluctuations. In the scalar model, its stochastic differential equation is
dXt​=−θ(Xt​−μ)dt+σdWt​,θ>0,σ>0,μ∈R,
(27)
where W is a standard Brownian motion. The drift points towards μ: it is negative above μ and positive below it. The coefficient θ sets the speed of mean reversion, while σ sets the strength of the random forcing. We consider a fixed or normal initial value independent of future Brownian increments, giving a Gaussian process and a Markov process.
For a stipulated example, use θ=1, μ=2, σ=2​ and the fixed initial value X0​=6, in arbitrary consistent state and time units. Then the expected value relaxes as 2+4e−t and the variance grows as 1−e−2t. At time one they are about 3.4715 and 0.8647; the limiting variance is one. The sample paths keep fluctuating even after the expected value is close to two. The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck transition distribution describes this evolving uncertainty; Ornstein-Uhlenbeck stationary initialization instead starts with that limiting normal distribution already in place.
A physical motivation is the velocity of a particle undergoing Brownian motion: linear drag removes a persistent initial velocity, while random forcing continually renews it. The original calculation gives a normal distribution for the velocity after subtracting its exponentially decaying initial contribution, with decay rate equal to the friction coefficient divided by the particle's mass.[ref] A position coordinate in an overdamped harmonic optical trap gives another interpretation of the same scalar equation, with θ=k/ζ for spring constant k and drag coefficient ζ. Its physical assumptions and connection to measurements are developed in thermal calibration of an optical trap.
