Positive-definite bilinear form (B(v,v)>0(v=0))

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra Bilinear form Symmetric bilinear form
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 5
A real symmetric bilinear form is positive-definite when B(v,v)>0 for every nonzero vector. It defines an inner product and the associated norm B(v,v)​. Symmetry is part of this definition: positive diagonal values by themselves do not imply symmetry.[ref]
The identity matrix A=diag(1,1) gives B(v,w)=v1​w1​+v2​w2​ and B(v,v)=v12​+v22​. For v=(3,4) this is 25, giving norm 5. A weighted example, A=diag(4,1), gives norm 4x2+y2​: the first coordinate costs twice as much in length. Such inner products let geometry assign different weights to different directions.
Every positive-definite bilinear form is nondegenerate: a vector in its radical of a bilinear form has B(v,v)=0, so positivity forces v=0. The converse fails, as diag(1,−1) shows. Even positivity of the associated quadratic form does not force a general bilinear form to be symmetric: A=(1−1​11​) has vTAv=x2+y2 but B(e1​,e2​)=1 and B(e2​,e1​)=−1.
The complex Killing form is not positive-definite on the entire complex Cartan subalgebra; its restriction to the Euclidean subspace of a Cartan subalgebra is a real positive-definite form.
