Amplitude modulation sidebands (ωc​±Ω)

 Home Electrical engineering Amplitude modulation
OurBigBook.com Words: 459 Articles: 1
Apply the product-to-sum formula to the voltage model:
s(t)=Acos(ωc​t)+2Am​cos((ωc​+Ω)t)+2Am​cos((ωc​−Ω)t).
(5)
The carrier wave component has peak voltage amplitude A; each AM sideband has peak voltage amplitude Am/2. These are temporal frequency spectrum components, unlike the spatial sidebands used in pattern stability. The separation Ω>0 makes the three positive angular frequencies distinct, and Ω<ωc​ keeps the lower one positive. The span between sidebands is 2Ω, or 2fm​ in hertz when fm​=Ω/(2π); this is the single-tone span, not a universal signal bandwidth for arbitrary messages.
A one-sided cosine-amplitude display assigns A to the carrier line. A two-sided complex-exponential expansion instead assigns A/2 at each of ±ωc​, and Am/4 at each of the four sideband locations. The signal is identical; confusing the conventions creates a factor-of-two error. An ideal infinite-duration sinusoid gives a spectral line. A finite recording and finite analyser resolution broaden the displayed peak.
For a voltage applied across an ideal resistor of electrical resistance R>0, a component of peak voltage B has root mean square voltage B/2​ and mean power B2/(2R) by Ohm's law. Thus
Pc​=2RA2​,P+​=P−​=8RA2m2​=4m2​Pc​,Ptot​=Pc​(1+2m2​).
(6)
The proof of additivity is to average s(t)2/R over a long time: cross-products of distinct frequencies have zero mean. Consequently the whole signal's root mean square voltage is A(1+m2/2)/2​, not simply its maximum wave envelope divided by 2​. The carrier-component A/2​ remains unchanged as m varies.
With A=2V, R=50Ω and m=0.5, the carrier supplies 40mW and each 0.5V peak sideband supplies 2.5mW, giving 45mW total. At m=1.4 each sideband is 1.4V peak and supplies 19.6mW, giving 79.2mW total. Neither case changes the carrier-component power.
The fraction of mean power in the message-bearing sidebands is m2/(2+m2). It is 1/9 at m=0.5 and 1/3 at m=1. Conventional AM spends the remaining power on a carrier that enables an inexpensive envelope detector. Increasing m beyond 1 increases this fraction in the linear model but breaks ordinary envelope recovery. Removing the carrier gives suppressed-carrier amplitude modulation and changes the receiver requirement.
