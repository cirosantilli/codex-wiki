Random walk (Sn​=S0​+∑j=1n​ξj​)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Markov process Markov chain
OurBigBook.com Words: 4k Articles: 70
A random walk is a stochastic process formed by accumulating random steps. In its additive form, the increments ξj​ are independent and identically distributed random variables and the position after n steps is
Sn​=S0​+∑j=1n​ξj​.
(1)
The steps can have more choices than −1 and +1: they may be integers, real numbers, or vectors. A random walk on a graph instead describes steps between vertices along edges; its available moves can depend on the current vertex.
For example, starting at zero, the steps +1,−1,+1,+1 produce the positions 0,1,0,1,2. This is one sample path; other step sequences can have the same endpoint. A simple random walk on the integer line makes this distinction particularly tangible: repeated Bernoulli trials determine both a whole sample path and an endpoint with a binomial distribution. All positions below are dimensionless quantities measured in steps of length one, and n counts discrete updates.
The model is useful because it separates an average tendency from accumulated fluctuations. A fair simple symmetric random walk has zero expected value at every time, yet its standard deviation grows: cancellation across many possible sample paths does not keep each walker near zero. The two figures below compare sample paths with exact endpoint probabilities. For the basic additive construction and its independent increments, see MIT, Stochastic Processes I, section 2.

Upward skip-free random walk (Xn​∈Z,Xn​≤1)

Words: 165 Articles: 1
Random walk OurBigBook.com
An upward skip-free random walk has integer-valued increments bounded above by one. Arbitrarily large downward jumps are permitted, but a first crossing of a nonnegative integer level k from below must land exactly at k. This removes the overshoot from the stopped likelihood ratio under exponential tilting and permits the exact geometric maximum of an upward skip-free random walk. For a real threshold k, the first crossing is the crossing of ⌈k⌉, so the overshoot-free formula must use that integer level.

Geometric maximum of an upward skip-free random walk (P(supn≥0​Sn​=j)=(1−e−λ0​)e−λ0​j)

Words: 95
Upward skip-free random walk OurBigBook.com
Let the increments define an upward skip-free random walk, and suppose ϕ(λ)=EeλX1​ is finite for all λ≥0. If λ0​>0 satisfies ϕ(λ0​)=1 and ϕ′(λ0​)>0, the exponential tilting at λ0​ gives integrable increments of positive mean. The strong law of large numbers makes every nonnegative integer level attainable under this tilted law. Upward jumps are at most one, so the value at a first level hit is exactly that level. The stopped likelihood ratio under exponential tilting therefore gives hitting probability e−λ0​k for integer k≥0. Subtracting consecutive tail probabilities yields the displayed geometric distribution on j=0,1,…. For real thresholds use ⌈k⌉ in place of k.

Gaussian random walk spends zero density of time in bounded sets (n−1∑j≤n​1B​(Sj​)→0)

Words: 72
Random walk OurBigBook.com
For independent N(0,1) increments, a random walk has Sj​∼N(0,j). If B is a bounded measurable set, its occupation time probability obeys P(Sj​∈B)≤∣B∣/2πj​. Consequently E∑j​1B​(Sj​)/j<∞, and Tonelli's theorem makes this series finite almost surely. Kronecker lemma then gives the displayed zero occupation density. This also shows why its empirical occupation probabilities cannot converge to a probability measure simultaneously on all bounded intervals and the whole real line: continuity from below would contradict the preserved total mass one.

Mean-square displacement of an isotropic planar random walk (E∥Sn​∥2=n)

Words: 54
Random walk OurBigBook.com
Independent planar unit steps with uniformly distributed direction have zero vector expected value. The squared Euclidean norm of their sum has expectation n, because each diagonal term is one and all cross terms vanish by independence. More generally, zero-mean independent steps with common second moment s2 give mean-square displacement ns2; nonzero drift adds a quadratic contribution.

Exponential martingale of a random walk (eθSn​−nκ(θ))

Random walk OurBigBook.com
For a random walk with independent and identically distributed random variables as increments and finite moment-generating function at θ, put κ(θ)=logEeθX1​. Then eθSn​−nκ(θ) is a martingale by conditioning on the next independent increment. In particular, if r>0 and ErX1​=1, then rSn​ is a martingale. For a random walk with independent and identically distributed random variables as increments, let Mn​=eλSn​/ϕ(λ)n define its finite-time exponential tilting. Under the tilted law, Ln​=Mn−1​ is a martingale: the conditional mean of ϕ(λ)e−λXn+1​ is one. Exponential first-passage transform for an upward skip-free random walk (E[M(τ)−Tb​]=e−τb)

Words: 119
Exponential martingale of a random walk OurBigBook.com
Let Sn​ have integrable, mean-zero independent and identically distributed random variables as increments, taking integer values at most one, with P(X1​=1)>0. For a positive integer b, put Tb​=inf{n:Sn​=b} and M(τ)=EeτX1​, τ>0. Then Tb​ is finite almost surely. Indeed, b−Sn∧Tb​​ is a nonnegative martingale; finite convergence on {Tb​=∞} would force its integer increments eventually to vanish, contrary to the Borel-Cantelli lemmas applied to {Xn​=1}.
The exponential martingale of a random walk eτSn​/M(τ)n stopped at Tb​ is bounded by eτb because Sn∧Tb​​≤b and M(τ)≥1. The dominated convergence theorem therefore preserves its mean one at the limit and proves the displayed first-passage time transform. This argument accommodates arbitrarily large negative jumps; the restriction concerns upward jumps. For a simple symmetric random walk, M(τ)=coshτ, giving Ee−αTb​=(eα−e2α−1​)b.

Non-backtracking random walk

Words: 62
Random walk on a graph OurBigBook.com
A non-backtracking random walk remembers its preceding vertex and excludes that neighbor from the next choice. At vertices of degree at least two, it is a Markov chain on directed edges: (a,b) moves to (b,c) with probability 1/(degb−1) for each neighbor c=a. Dead ends require an additional convention. An initial vertex without a preceding edge uses the ordinary first-step neighbor distribution.

Edge deletion preserves recurrence

Words: 62
Recurrent graph OurBigBook.com
Deleting an edge of a locally finite recurrent graph leaves recurrent Markov chain connected components. On an infinite component choose a root away from the deleted edge's endpoints, so its vertex degree does not change. The Rayleigh monotonicity principle increases resistance to wired outer boundaries, and effective resistance to infinity therefore remains infinite. Finite components are recurrent Markov chain too. This is a recurrence-type comparison; at an endpoint, changes in the vertex degree factor prevent a blanket monotonicity claim about first-return probabilities.

Kirchhoff cycle law
Words: 75
Current flow OurBigBook.com
The Kirchhoff cycle law says the directed sum of voltage drops around every graph cycle is zero. For unit edge resistance this is the sum of signed currents around the graph cycle. With unit edge resistance, the current is a voltage gradient: define the voltage difference by summing drops along a graph path. The difference of the sums along two graph paths is a sum around closed walks; decomposing these into graph cycles makes it zero. Together with the Kirchhoff node law, the resulting graph Laplacian equation determines current uniquely on a finite connected network.

Commute time identity OurBigBook.com
Let simple random walk travel from a=z to its first visit to z, then stop on its subsequent first hit of a in a finite connected unweighted loopless graph. Every directed edge u→v has expected traversal count
ES(u,v)=Reff​(a,z).
(8)

Simple random walk

Words: 826 Articles: 12
Random walk on a graph OurBigBook.com
A simple random walk on a locally finite graph chooses each vertex in the current vertex's graph neighbourhood with equal probability, assuming there is at least one available neighbour. On the integer line there are two choices, one unit left or right, so each has probability 1/2. This is the one-dimensional simple symmetric random walk. Terminology varies: a simple random walk on the integer line is also often allowed unequal step probabilities, with “symmetric” specifying the fair case.
For a simple symmetric random walk starting at S0​=0, each increment has the Rademacher distribution, with expected value zero and variance one. Thus linearity of expectation and the variance of a sum of independent random variables give
ESn​=0,Var(Sn​)=n,σn​=n​.
(14)
For example, after 80 steps the expected value is zero, the variance is 80, and the standard deviation is 80​≈8.944. Doubling the typical distance scale takes four times as many steps. This square-root growth explains why a random walk can wander far even when there is no preferred direction.
The colored curves are illustrative sample paths, not averages over all possible walks. The dashed curves show the expected values; the shaded bands show the expected value plus or minus one standard deviation at each time. These bands are a scale of spread, not confidence intervals, and give no guarantee that an entire sample path stays inside them.
Zero expected value does not make this a stationary process: its one-time probability distribution changes with n, as the increasing variance already proves. It does have stationary increments and independent increments, because blocks of fresh steps have the same probability distribution at any starting time and disjoint blocks use independent random variables. This separation of position from increment is one reason the random walk is such a useful first example of a stochastic process.

Multinomial collision proof of three-dimensional walk transience (P(S2n​=0)=4n(n2n​)​∑i+j+k=n​(3ni!j!k!n!​)2=O(n−3/2))

Simple symmetric random walk OurBigBook.com
For the nearest-neighbor symmetric walk on Z3, a return at time 2n uses i,j,k positive and the same numbers of negative steps along each axis, with i+j+k=n. Counting arrangements gives the displayed identity. The sum of squares of the three-category multinomial distribution probabilities is at most their maximum. This maximum occurs at balanced counts and is O(n−1) by Stirling's formula; the central binomial factor is O(n−1/2). The total return probability is therefore summable. The expected number of visits is finite, so the probability of returning after time zero is strictly below one, proving that the origin is a transient state.

Origin capture by a sufficiently sleepy planar random walk
Words: 87
Simple symmetric random walk OurBigBook.com
Suppose two independent planar simple symmetric random walks are compared while one stays at its jth position throughout a deterministic time block. If each block has an unconditional probability bounded below by a positive constant that the other walk visits the origin, simultaneous origin visits occur infinitely often almost surely. Markov-chain recurrence gives infinitely many origin blocks for the sleepy walk. The block bound yields positive probability of infinitely many coincidences; eventual coupling of planar symmetric random walks then makes this future event have probability zero or one. Simple random walk on the integer line (Sn​=2Bn​−n)

Words: 989 Articles: 8
Random walk on a graph OurBigBook.com
A simple random walk on the integer line starts at S0​=0 and takes independent and identically distributed random variables ξj​ as unit increments, with
P(ξj​=+1)=p,P(ξj​=−1)=1−p,Sn​=∑j=1n​ξj​,0<p<1.
(18)
The simple symmetric random walk has p=1/2; a biased random walk has p=1/2. At p=0 or p=1 every step is deterministic, giving Sn​=−n or Sn​=n respectively.
Count the rightward steps using indicator functions:
Bn​=∑j=1n​1{ξj​=+1}​,Bn​∼Binomial(n,p),Sn​=Bn​−(n−Bn​)=2Bn​−n.
(19)
Thus the endpoint is a shifted and rescaled random variable with a binomial distribution. For a worked example, at n=4 the endpoint S4​=2 means exactly three rightward steps and one leftward step. There are (34​)=4 possible orders, so its probability is 4p3(1−p), equal to 1/4 in the fair case. More generally the binomial coefficient counts the orders, giving the exact probability mass function
P(Sn​=s)=⎩⎨⎧​((n+s)/2n​)p(n+s)/2(1−p)(n−s)/2,0,​s∈{−n,−n+2,…,n},otherwise.​
(20)
In particular, the endpoint must have the same integer parity as n: Sn​≡n(mod2). For 0<p<1 the support of a probability distribution is exactly {−n,−n+2,…,n}. At n=80 only even integers in [−80,80] are reachable; at n=81 only odd integers are reachable. This restriction is easy to lose if one draws a smooth curve instead of discrete probability masses. The fair-walk counting argument is also presented in Grinstead and Snell, Introduction to Probability, section 12.1.
The endpoint identity and the binomial distribution give the expected value, variance, and standard deviation
ESn​=n(2p−1),Var(Sn​)=4np(1−p),σn​=2np(1−p)​.
(21)
A short direct check gives Eξj​=2p−1 and Eξj2​=1, hence Var(ξj​)=1−(2p−1)2=4p(1−p). Then apply linearity of expectation and the variance of a sum of independent random variables.
The absolute width and the width relative to the number of steps answer different questions. In the fair case the standard deviation grows from 20​≈4.472 to 80​≈8.944, while σn​/n shrinks from 0.2236 to 0.1118. At n=80,p=0.65, the standard deviation is 72.8​≈8.532, or 0.9539 times the fair width, and σn​/n≈0.1067. Centering and dividing by each walk's own standard deviation gives, for n>0,
Zn​=2np(1−p)​Sn​−n(2p−1)​,EZn​=0,Var(Zn​)=1.
(22)
This rescaling equalizes the variances, not the entire probability distributions. The spacing between reachable endpoints is two, so its rescaled spacing is 2/σn​: approximately 0.4472 for the fair 20-step walk, 0.2236 for the fair 80-step walk, and 0.2344 for the biased 80-step walk.
The positions themselves are dependent. For 1≤m≤n, Sn​=Sm​+(Sn​−Sm​), where the second term is independent of Sm​. Their covariance is therefore
Cov(Sm​,Sn​)=Var(Sm​)=4mp(1−p)>0.
(23)
For example, in the fair case S1​=1 allows S2​=0 or 2, whereas S1​=−1 allows S2​=−2 or 0. Nevertheless the position process is a Markov chain: given its current position, the next fresh increment has no dependence on its past. Its transition probabilities are
P(x,y)=⎩⎨⎧​p,1−p,0,​y=x+1,y=x−1,otherwise.​
(24)
These transition probabilities are the same at every time and every integer position: shifting both x and y by the same integer leaves P(x,y) unchanged. Thus the Markov property and independent increments coexist with dependent positions.

A biased random walk on the integer line takes unit steps right and left with unequal probabilities p and 1−p, using independent and identically distributed random variables as increments. For S0​=0, the expected value is n(2p−1): positive for p>1/2 and negative for p<1/2. Changing p to 1−p reverses the preferred direction. (27)

Maximum of a downward-biased random walk (P(M=j)=(1−p/q)(p/q)j,j≥0)

For a nearest-neighbour random walk with upward probability 0<p<q=1−p, start at S0​=0 and put M=supn≥0​Sn​. To prove this, the exponential martingale of a biased simple random walk Zn​=(q/p)Sn​ has expectation one. Stop at the first hit Hk​ of k.
