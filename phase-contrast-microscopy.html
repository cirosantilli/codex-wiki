Phase-contrast microscopy

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics Optical instrument Optical microscope
OurBigBook.com Words: 911 Articles: 7
Phase-contrast microscopy converts specimen-dependent phase differences into visible light intensity differences. It can reveal structures in living, unstained biological cells, allowing their changes to be followed without first making an absorbing preparation.[ref] The key question is how an optical microscope can read a phase when an ordinary detector records light intensity.
Consider a transparent specimen with complex amplitude transmission t(x,y)=eiϕ(x,y). In ideal in-focus coherent imaging with all relevant spatial frequencies transmitted, its image has I=∣t∣2=1: varying ϕ alone produces no brightness detail. This is a particular model, not a claim that all transparent specimens are invisible. Diffraction, defocus, absorption and a finite optical pupil can already change a real image.
A phase-contrast microscope images an illuminating annulus onto a phase plate ring near the objective's rear focal plane. The aligned ring attenuates and shifts direct light relative to most diffracted light; their interference changes image brightness.[ref]
The method is beautiful because the bright direct beam becomes its own reference: a small specimen-dependent complex amplitude can interfere with it and change the image at first order. Merely magnifying an invisible phase pattern would leave ∣t∣2 unchanged.
