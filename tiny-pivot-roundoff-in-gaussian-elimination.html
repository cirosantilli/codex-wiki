Tiny-pivot roundoff in Gaussian elimination (fl3​,ε=10−6)

 ... Area of mathematics Analysis Numerical analysis Numerical linear algebra Gaussian elimination Columnwise partial pivoting
OurBigBook.com Words: 479
A nonzero pivot element can still be a poor numerical choice. Partial pivoting bounds the absolute values of the elimination multipliers by one in exact arithmetic, because each numerator is no larger than the selected denominator. This usually improves numerical stability, but does not prevent every possible growth of intermediate entries or guarantee accurate answers for every linear system.[ref]
To see the mechanism without depending on hardware, define a toy floating-point arithmetic model. The operator fl3​(z) rounds an exact real number to the nearest value with three significant decimal digits; halfway cases choose the even final digit, and fl3​(0)=0. Allow an unbounded exponent range. Round the inputs and the result of every addition, subtraction, multiplication and division separately; a product followed by a subtraction rounds twice. This is a prescribed arithmetic model, not a measurement of a physical computer. All inputs in the following original example are represented exactly:
ε=10−6,A=(ε1​11​),b=(12​).

u22​=fl3​(1−fl3​(m⋅1))=fl3​(−999999)=−106,
(53)
c2​=fl3​(2−fl3​(m⋅1))=fl3​(−999998)=−106.
(54)
Two distinct exact values now have the same rounded representation. Back substitution therefore returns
x2​=fl3​(c2​/u22​)=1,x1​=fl3​(εfl3​(1−fl3​(1⋅x2​))​)=0.

The small difference carrying information about x1​ was lost during elimination; the final subtraction only exposes that loss. Dividing by the tiny pivot element amplifies sensitivity to the lost information.
With partial pivoting, interchange the two rows, including their right-hand sides, to start from [1ε​11​21​]. Now m=fl3​(ε/1)=ε, and the rounded updates are
u22​=fl3​(1−fl3​(ε⋅1))=fl3​(0.999999)=1,
(56)
c2​=fl3​(1−fl3​(ε⋅2))=fl3​(0.999998)=1.
(57)
Back substitution gives x2​=fl3​(1/1)=1 and x1​=fl3​(fl3​(2−fl3​(1⋅1))/1)=1. Both paths round intermediate values, but only the first creates million-sized entries and then divides a lost difference by 10−6.
