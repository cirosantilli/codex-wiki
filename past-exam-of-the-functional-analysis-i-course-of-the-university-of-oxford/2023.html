For a normed space X, let X∗ be its continuous dual space and X∗∗=(X∗)∗ its bidual space. Its canonical embedding into the bidual is
JX​:X→X∗∗,(JX​x)(ℓ)=ℓ(x).
(26)
The definition of a reflexive space in this setting is
JX​(X)=X∗∗.​
(27)
The Hahn-Banach theorem makes JX​ a linear map that is an isometry. For any normed space Z, its continuous dual space is a Banach space: a Cauchy sequence (ℓn​) in the operator norm has scalar limits ℓ(z)=limn​ℓn​(z), and the uniform Cauchy bound proves both boundedness of ℓ and ∥ℓn​−ℓ∥→0. Apply this with Z=X∗ to see that X∗∗ is complete. If JX​ is onto, the isometry transfers this completeness to X. Therefore
X reflexive ⟹ X complete.​

Writing B(X) for the Banach space of bounded linear operators on X, the spectrum is
σ(T)={λ∈C:λI−T has no bounded two-sided inverse in B(X)}.​
(43)
Here I is the identity operator. Since X is a Banach space, the bounded inverse theorem makes this equivalent to failure of bijectivity of λI−T.
The spectral radius is
r(T)=sup{∣λ∣:λ∈σ(T)}.​
(44)
For a nonzero complex Banach space, the spectrum of a bounded linear operator is nonempty and compact, so the supremum is a maximum. If the zero space is allowed, its empty spectrum is assigned spectral radius zero.

For a continuous function g on [0,1], the L-infinity norm of its restriction to (0,1) equals its supremum norm on [0,1]. Indeed, values arbitrarily close to a maximum occur on an interval of positive Lebesgue measure, even if the maximum lies at an endpoint. The point evaluation functional Ex0​​(g)=g(x0​) is complex-linear and satisfies ∣Ex0​​(g)∣≤∥g∥∞​. The constant function one attains equality, so ∥Ex0​​∥=1. The complex Hahn-Banach theorem extends this bounded linear functional from that linear subspace to X, preserving its operator norm. Therefore
