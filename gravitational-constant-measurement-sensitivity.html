Gravitational constant measurement sensitivity (Ginferred​=Fr2/(Mm))

A laboratory determination of the gravitational constant must connect a sensor's reading to a gravitational signal from independently characterized masses. For an ideal two-point mass force measurement, invert the model:
Ginferred​=MmFr2​,GδG​≃FδF​+2rδr​−MδM​−mδm​.
(26)
These are signed small changes obtained by taking the logarithmic derivative, not an instruction to add standard uncertainties with signs. For independent small input uncertainties, linear propagation of uncertainty instead gives
(Gu(G)​)2≃(Fu(F)​)2+4(ru(r)​)2+(Mu(M)​)2+(mu(m)​)2.
(27)
Correlated inputs require their covariances too. In particular, common geometric calibration errors must not be treated as independent simply because several distances were recorded.
For a concrete model check, hold the actual force and masses fixed but overestimate a true r=0.100m by 1μm. The exact inferred ratio is
Gtrue​Ginferred​​=(1+10−5)2=1.0000200001.
(28)
The resulting bias is 20.0001 parts per million, already comparable with the relative standard uncertainty of the recommended gravitational constant. A sensor can resolve very small changes while its inferred answer remains limited by source geometry. The example concerns one idealized separation; it is not an uncertainty estimate for any actual experiment.
Making the source mass larger or bringing it closer strengthens the signal, but also makes detailed mass density and geometry important. Nearby apparatus supplies its own Newtonian gravitational field; electromagnetic shielding does not remove that attraction. Sensor offsets, changing temperature, vibration and source motion can imitate or alter a signal. The Cavendish experiment illustrates calibration of a torsion constant; a different sensor requires its own calibrated response and a full source model.
Repeating readings can reduce independent measurement noise, but a fixed scale error survives averaging. A useful experiment deliberately changes the source configuration, checks whether the signal follows the predicted change, and characterizes what moves with the source. A systematic error that changes with that configuration also survives background subtraction. Comparing methods with different sensors and different dominant errors is therefore valuable.
