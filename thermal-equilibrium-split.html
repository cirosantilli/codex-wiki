Thermal equilibrium

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 11
Two internally equilibrated systems are in thermal equilibrium if bringing them into contact that permits only heat exchange causes no net heat transfer. In ordinary nonrelativistic thermodynamics without appreciable gravitational temperature shifts, their thermodynamic temperatures are equal. Merely preventing heat transfer with insulation does not establish thermal equilibrium: two insulated bodies can have different temperatures. [ref]
A thermometer illustrates the definition. Its own temperature settles to the object's temperature after thermal contact, provided its effect on the object is negligible. The existing zeroth law of thermodynamics makes this comparison transitive: two objects separately in thermal equilibrium with the same thermometer are in thermal equilibrium with each other. This lets one calibrated scale compare different materials.
Equal temperatures do not imply equal internal energies or equal heat capacities. Nor does thermal equilibrium by itself imply mechanical equilibrium or chemical equilibrium; these require additional allowed exchanges. There is also a distinction between no net exchange in thermal equilibrium and a constant temperature maintained by equal incoming and outgoing heat. The latter can be a steady state with ongoing entropy production. Microscopic exchanges can continue at thermal equilibrium, but their opposing contributions balance on average.
Historically, OpenStax credits the name “zeroth law” to Ralph Fowler in the 1930s: the already numbered laws needed a logically earlier statement about thermal equilibrium. [ref]
