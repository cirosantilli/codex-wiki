Phospholipid

 Home Chemistry Chemical substance Lipid
OurBigBook.com Words: 615 Articles: 3
Phospholipids are lipids containing a phosphate group as part of their structure linked by covalent bonds. The familiar membrane-forming phospholipids are amphiphilic: a hydrophilic head region with chemical polarity coexists with a hydrophobic, largely nonpolar hydrocarbon region. These descriptions refer to different parts of the same molecule, not to two separate substances. See IUPAC Gold Book: phospholipids and IUPAC Gold Book: amphiphilic.
A typical two-chain glycerophospholipid provides a useful example: a glycerol scaffold carries two fatty acid-derived acyl groups through ester linkages, plus a phosphate-containing head region. Phosphatidylcholine is one example. This scaffold describes an important subclass, rather than all phospholipids: sphingomyelin has a different backbone, and even glycerophospholipids need not all have two acyl groups. See IUPAC–IUB, Nomenclature of Lipids (1976), Lip-1.5 and Lip-2.1–2.6.
Polar does not mean negatively charged overall. At near-neutral pH, phosphatidylcholine is zwitterionic: its phosphate region carries negative electric charge and its choline region positive electric charge, with zero net electric charge for the molecule. Counting those opposite charges gives (−1)+(+1)=0; it does not remove their local interactions with water. See Lodish et al., Molecular Cell Biology: membrane lipids.
In a lipid bilayer, two membrane leaflets orient their hydrophilic heads toward the water on either side and their hydrophobic chains toward the shared interior. The hydrophobic effect helps explain this organization through the free energy of the water and lipids together. It is not a special repulsive chemical bond between chains and water. Which organization is favored also depends on molecular geometry, temperature, concentration, and the surrounding chemical solution; a phospholipid label alone does not guarantee a lipid bilayer. See Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: the lipid bilayer.
This organization supplies a flexible barrier for compartments. A fluid lipid bilayer allows lipids to undergo diffusion within each membrane leaflet; barrier function therefore coexists with motion of molecules, as in the fluid mosaic model. It is not completely impermeable: small nonpolar molecules such as molecular oxygen and carbon dioxide cross much more readily than ions. In a cell membrane, ion channels and other membrane transport proteins provide selective routes; the existing membrane transport article explains their driving forces. See Alberts et al.: principles of membrane transport.
In 1925, E. Gorter and F. Grendel extracted lipids from red blood cells and compared the surface area of a one-molecule-thick film on water with the estimated total surface area of the red blood cells. Their table gave ratios near two, supporting a two-layer covering; they also proposed heads facing the aqueous surroundings on both sides. The inference depends on recovering the relevant lipids, estimating the red blood cell surface area correctly, and comparing compatible packing in the film and the cell membrane. It was indirect evidence, not an image of the lipid bilayer. The paper itself notes possible losses during evaporation. See Gorter and Grendel, On Bimolecular Layers of Lipoids on the Chromocytes of the Blood, Journal of Experimental Medicine 41 (1925), 439–443.
