If (a(n)) is Cauchy in the maximum norm on Cm, every coordinate is Cauchy in C and has a limit aj​. Given ε>0, for sufficiently large n,k every coordinate difference is at most ε. Letting k→∞ gives maxj​∣aj(n)​−aj​∣≤ε. Thus Cm with its maximum norm is complete.
Let F(a)=∑aj​zj​. The triangle inequality gives
∥F(a)∥≤(∑j=1m​∥zj​∥)∥a∥∞​.
(1)
Take δ=∑∥zj​∥. This estimate also makes a↦∥F(a)∥ continuous on the maximum-norm unit sphere, which is compact by coordinatewise Bolzano-Weierstrass. Linear independence makes this function strictly positive there, so its minimum γ is positive. Homogeneity then gives
γ∥a∥∞​≤∥F(a)∥≤δ∥a∥∞​.​
(2)
A Cauchy sequence in X consequently has Cauchy coordinate vectors in Cm. Their coordinate limit maps by the upper estimate to a limit in X. Therefore every finite-dimensional normed complex space is a Banach space.

The dual space X′ consists of bounded linear maps g:X→C, with operator norm ∥g∥=sup∥x∥≤1​∣g(x)∣. It is Banach even if X is not: an operator-norm Cauchy sequence has pointwise limits, these define a bounded linear functional, and the uniform Cauchy estimate on the unit ball gives operator-norm convergence.
Restriction R(g)=g∣Z​ is linear and contractive. If Z is dense, for every x∈X choose zn​∈Z with zn​→x. The bound ∣g(zn​)∣≤∥Rg∥∥zn​∥ passes to the limit, proving ∣g(x)∣≤∥Rg∥∥x∥. Hence ∥g∥=∥Rg∥ and restriction is injective. Conversely, for h∈Z′ define g(x)=limh(zn​). The estimate ∣h(zn​)−h(zk​)∣≤∥h∥∥zn​−zk​∥ gives existence and independence of the approximating sequence. It also gives linearity and ∥g∥≤∥h∥. Thus restriction is an onto linear isometry of dual spaces.
For the sequence subspace in this part, the norm is the unweighted ℓ1 norm; only its membership condition uses the weight j. It contains all finitely supported sequences, hence is dense in ℓ1. Applying restriction to X=ℓ1 and using its known dual yields
Z′≅ℓ∞ isometrically,hb​(α)=j=1∑∞​bj​αj​,∥hb​∥=∥b∥∞​.​

The complex Hahn-Banach theorem says that every bounded complex-linear functional on a linear subspace of a normed complex space extends to the whole space with the same norm.
For the separation assertion choose x0​∈/M and d=dist(x0​,M)>0. On M+Cx0​ define g0​(m+λx0​)=λd. The inequality ∥m+λx0​∥≥∣λ∣d makes its norm at most one; approximating the distance d shows the norm is exactly one. Hahn-Banach gives an extension g of norm one annihilating M.
Each geometric sequence satisfies ∥xk​∥1​=(1−∣βk​∣)−1≤(1−δ)−1. Let M be their closed linear span. If M=ℓ1, the separation just proved gives a nonzero bounded functional annihilating all xk​. Represent it by b∈ℓ∞ and form
F(z)=∑j=1∞​bj​zj−1,∣z∣<1.
(4)
The series converges locally uniformly and defines a holomorphic function. It vanishes at every βk​. These distinct points lie in the compact disk ∣z∣≤δ<1, so have an accumulation point inside the domain. The identity theorem gives F=0, hence every Taylor coefficient bj​=0, contradicting the functional's nonzero norm. Therefore
span{xk​:k≥1}​=ℓ1.​
(5)

The spectrum of a bounded operator is σ(T)={λ:λI−T has no bounded two-sided inverse}. Its point spectrum is the set of eigenvalues, where λI−T is not injective.
Here T is linear and takes continuous functions to continuously differentiable functions. Also ∥Tx∥∞​≤2∥x∥∞​; the constant function one attains this bound at t=1, so ∥T∥=2. Its range is contained in C1([0,1]), a proper subset of C([0,1]), so zero is in its spectrum. It is not an eigenvalue: Tx=0 gives x(0)=0 at zero and x=0 upon differentiation.
For a nonzero eigenvalue, λx=Tx implies x′=x/λ and (λ−1)x(0)=0. A nonzero solution is therefore possible exactly at λ=1, with eigenfunctions x(t)=cet. Hence pσ(T)={1}.
For the proposed resolvent put
F(t)=et/λ∫0t​y(s)e−s/λds,F′=y+F/λ.
(6)
The proposed x is y/λ+y(0)et/λ/[λ(λ−1)]+F/λ2. Consequently x(0)=y(0)/(λ−1) and
∫0t​x(s)ds=λF(t)​+λ−1y(0)​(et/λ−1).
(7)
Adding x(0) and subtracting from λx(t) gives exactly y(t). Thus λI−T is onto for λ=0,1. It is also injective by the eigenvalue calculation. The displayed formula defines a bounded map in the supremum norm, since its exponential kernels are bounded on the compact interval. It is therefore the bounded inverse, and
σ(T)={0,1},pσ(T)={1}.​

Use the convention that the inner product is linear in its first argument. If v=0, nonnegativity of ∥u−⟨u,v⟩v/∥v∥2∥2 gives
∣⟨u,v⟩∣≤∥u∥∥v∥.​
(9)
The case v=0 is immediate. This Cauchy-Schwarz inequality shows that fv​ is bounded linear with norm at most ∥v∥. For v=0, evaluation at v/∥v∥ gives equality; for v=0 both norms vanish. The Banach dual is the space of all bounded linear functionals with the operator norm.
The Riesz representation theorem asserts that every f∈V′ is uniquely f(u)=⟨u,v⟩ for some v∈V, with ∥f∥=∥v∥. If f=0, take v=0. Otherwise put W=kerf, a closed subspace, and use the projection theorem to obtain w∈W⊥ with f(w)=1. Since u−f(u)w∈W, ⟨u,w⟩=f(u)∥w∥2. Thus v=w/∥w∥2 represents f. If two vectors represent it, their difference has inner product zero with every vector, in particular itself, giving uniqueness. The norm statement was already proved.
The space Pn​ is finite-dimensional and hence complete; evaluation at zero is bounded there by equivalence of finite-dimensional norms. Riesz therefore supplies exactly one qn​∈Pn​. In any orthonormal polynomial basis,
qn​(t)=j=0∑n​pj​(0)​pj​(t),∥qn​∥2=j=0∑n​∣pj​(0)∣2.​
(10)
For the real Legendre functions the bars may be omitted.
The printed even-degree normalization has an error. Orthogonality forces a degree-two polynomial to be a scalar multiple of 3t2−1, whose squared integral on [−1,1] is 8/5. Hence any orthonormal choice has ∣p2​(0)∣2=5/8, whereas the printed formula gives 3/8. The factor under its square root must be 4m+1 instead of 2m+1. Signs of the individual polynomials can be chosen so that the values at zero are positive; these signs do not affect qn​ or its norm.
Put M=⌊n/2⌋ and cm​=(m2m​)/4m. With the consistent orthonormal normalization the norm is
∥qn​∥2=21​m=0∑M​(4m+1)cm2​=2(2M+1)2cM2​​,∥qn​∥=2​(2M+1)cM​​.​
(11)
The finite sum telescopes because cm​=(2m−1)cm−1​/(2m), so (2m+1)2cm2​−(2m−1)2cm−12​=(4m+1)cm2​. If one substitutes the erroneous printed values formally instead, one obtains 21​∑m=0M​(2m+1)cm2​; that is not the norm of the actual orthonormal Legendre evaluation representer.
For a polynomial r of degree d, the defining identity gives ⟨r,qn​⟩=r(0) for every n≥d, so the requested limit is eventually constant. It does not extend to all of L2. First, evaluation at a point is not well-defined on equivalence classes. More strongly, the functional norms ∥qn​∥ are unbounded. Indeed the largest binomial coefficient in row 2m is at least the average, so cm​≥1/(2m+1), and the displayed sum dominates a divergent constant multiple of the harmonic series. The Uniform boundedness principle then provides an L2 function for which supn​∣⟨r,qn​⟩∣=∞. Thus no choice of point representative can restore the assertion for all L2 functions.

the partial sum is snf​=a0​/2+∑j=1n​(aj​cosjx+bj​sinjx). It is the orthogonal projection onto the first trigonometric modes. Completeness of the trigonometric orthonormal system therefore gives ∥snf​−f∥2​→0.

Write λ=a+ib with b=0. Since aI−T is self-adjoint, ⟨(aI−T)v,v⟩ is real. The cross term in the squared norm therefore vanishes:
∥(λI−T)v∥2=∥(aI−T)v∥2+b2∥v∥2=∥(λI−T)v∥2.

This proves the equality and lower bound, and makes both S=λI−T and S∗=λI−T injective.

Define f♯(s)=f(−s)​. Interchanging the integrals and substituting variables gives
⟨Tf​g1​,g2​⟩=⟨g1​,Tf♯​g2​⟩.
(24)
Moreover Cauchy-Schwarz in the inner integral gives
∣⟨Tf​g1​,g2​⟩∣​≤∫R​∣f(s)∣​∫R​g1​(t−s)g2​(t)​dt​ds≤∥f∥1​∥g1​∥2​∥g2​∥2​.​
(25)
Taking the supremum over unit g2​ proves ∥Tf​g1​∥2​≤∥f∥1​∥g1​∥2​, so Tf​ is bounded with norm at most ∥f∥1​, and the adjoint identity is justified.
It remains to ensure necessity is a condition on the kernel itself. If Th​=0 for some h∈L1, convolve h with a smooth compactly supported approximate identity ηε​∈L1∩L2. Since Th​ηε​=0, all these convolutions vanish. But h∗ηε​→h in L1, by continuity of translations in L1, so h=0 almost everywhere. Thus the map from kernels to convolution operators is injective. Applying this to h=f−f♯ yields the exact criterion
Tf​=Tf∗​⟺f(s)=f(−s)​ for almost every s.​
(26)
For real-valued kernels this reduces to evenness; real-valuedness by itself is insufficient.
