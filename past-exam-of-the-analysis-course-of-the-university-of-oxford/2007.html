A norm on a complex vector space is a map ∥⋅∥:X→[0,∞) that vanishes only at zero, satisfies ∥λx∥=∣λ∣∥x∥, and obeys the triangle inequality. The space is Banach when every Cauchy sequence in the resulting metric converges in X.
If X is Banach and ∑j​∥xj​∥<∞, the estimate ∥∑j=mn​xj​∥≤∑j=mn​∥xj​∥ shows that its partial sums are Cauchy, hence converge. Conversely let every such series converge and take a Cauchy sequence (yn​). Choose a subsequence with ∥ynk+1​​−ynk​​∥<2−k. The series consisting of yn1​​ followed by these successive differences is absolutely summable in norm, so converges by hypothesis; its partial sums are exactly this subsequence. A Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges to the same limit, proving the absolute-series criterion for completeness.
On the absolutely summable sequence space ℓ1, coordinatewise addition and scalar multiplication preserve membership, and summing the scalar triangle inequality verifies its norm axioms. To prove completeness using the criterion, let x(k)=(xj(k)​)∈ℓ1 with ∑k​∥x(k)∥1​<∞. For each j the sum xj​=∑k​xj(k)​ converges absolutely, and the permitted interchange of nonnegative double sums gives
∑j​∣xj​∣≤∑j​∑k​∣xj(k)​∣=∑k​∥x(k)∥1​<∞.
(1)
Thus x∈ℓ1. Moreover
​x−∑k=1N​x(k)​1​≤∑k>N​∥x(k)∥1​→0.
(2)
Every absolutely norm-summable series therefore converges in ℓ1, proving ℓ1 is a Banach space.

The continuous dual space X′ consists of all bounded complex-linear functionals f:X→C, with pointwise vector operations and norm ∥f∥=sup∥x∥≤1​∣f(x)∣. This is finite by boundedness. The scalar norm identity and triangle inequality follow under the supremum; if the norm is zero, scaling every nonzero vector into the unit ball shows that f vanishes. Hence X′ is a normed complex vector space.
For the dual of the convergent sequence space, take β∈ℓ1. The displayed formula is a bounded linear functional, since the series is absolutely convergent and
∣ϕ(β)(α)∣≤(∣β1​∣+∑j≥1​∣βj+1​∣)∥α∥∞​.
(3)
It is linear in β, so ∥ϕ(β)∥≤∥β∥1​. For equality fix N, choose αj​ for j≤N of modulus at most one so that αj​βj+1​=∣βj+1​∣, and make all later terms equal a constant c of modulus at most one with cβ1​=∣β1​∣. The choices for a zero coefficient can be arbitrary. This sequence lies in the unit ball of c and converges to c. The reverse triangle inequality gives
∣ϕ(β)(α)∣≥∣β1​∣+∑j=1N​∣βj+1​∣−∑j>N​∣βj+1​∣.
(4)
Letting N→∞ proves ∥ϕ(β)∥=∥β∥1​, including injectivity.
For surjectivity let F∈c′. By the permitted description of c0′​, there is γ∈ℓ1 with F(z)=∑j​γj​zj​ on c0​. Every α∈c decomposes as α−α∞​1∈c0​ plus α∞​1. Consequently
F(α)=∑j​γj​αj​+α∞​(F(1)−∑j​γj​).
(5)
Take βj+1​=γj​ and β1​=F(1)−∑j​γj​. This is an ℓ1 sequence and F=ϕ(β), proving the stated isometric linear isomorphism onto c′. No complex conjugation is inserted, since these are complex-linear dual functionals.

The complex Hahn-Banach theorem extends every bounded complex-linear functional on a subspace to the whole normed space with exactly the same norm. Let d=dist(x0​,M)>0 and define on M+Cx0​
g0​(y+λx0​)=λd.
(6)
The representation is unique. The distance inequality ∥y+λx0​∥≥∣λ∣d gives ∥g0​∥≤1. Taking y=−m with ∥x0​−m∥↓d proves the reverse norm bound. Extend by the Hahn-Banach theorem to g∈X′; then g∣M​=0, ∥g∥=1, and g(x0​)=d=0. The labels (a)-(c) specify these simultaneous properties of this one functional.
Let Y=spanF​. Every functional vanishing on F vanishes on Y by linearity and continuity, so Y⊆(F∘)∘​. If x∈/Y, the separation just proved gives a bounded functional zero on Y and nonzero at x. It belongs to F∘ and excludes x from its preannihilator. Therefore
(F∘)∘​=spanF​.​
(7)
For the dual-side counterexample take X=ℓ1, X′=ℓ∞, and G=c0​. It is norm closed, since a uniform limit of sequences tending to zero still tends to zero. Every coordinate unit vector belongs to G, so the only x∈ℓ1 annihilated by every element of G has all coordinates zero. Thus G∘​={0} and
(G∘​)∘=ℓ∞=c0​=G.​
(8)
The dual-side double annihilator corresponds to weak-star closure, which need not equal norm closure.

The spectrum of a bounded operator consists of λ for which T−λI has no bounded two-sided inverse. The point spectrum consists of eigenvalues, so ker(T−λI)=0. The approximate point spectrum consists of λ admitting unit vectors xn​ with ∥(T−λI)xn​∥→0; equivalently T−λI is not bounded below.
If T−λI is invertible, its inverse bounds it below and its Banach-space adjoint is invertible. Thus neither alternative on the right of the claimed union occurs. Conversely suppose λ belongs to neither term and write S=T−λI. There is c>0 with ∥Sx∥≥c∥x∥, so S is injective and has closed range: convergence of Sxn​ makes xn​ Cauchy, and completeness supplies a preimage of its limit. Also kerS′=0. The allowed annihilator identity makes the range dense, hence equal to X. Its inverse has norm at most 1/c. This proves the spectrum decomposition through the Banach-space adjoint:
σ(T)=apσ(T)∪pσ(T′).​
(9)
For the right shift on ℓ1, ∥T∥=1, so the allowed norm bound puts its spectrum in the closed unit disc. Under the linear pairing with ℓ∞, its Banach-space adjoint is the left shift T′(b1​,b2​,…)=(b2​,b3​,…). For each ∣λ∣≤1 the bounded nonzero sequence (1,λ,λ2,…) is an eigenvector of T′ with eigenvalue λ. The union identity gives the reverse inclusion. Hence σ(T)={λ:∣λ∣≤1}. This Banach-space adjoint uses the complex-linear dual, so its eigenvalue is λ itself.

Additivity applied to u+(−u)=0 gives P(−u,v)=−P(u,v). Multiplication by i preserves the norm, so
B(iu,v)=−B(u,iv),B(iu,iv)=B(u,v).

For a fixed second argument the norm formula makes P continuous in its first argument. Rational homogeneity therefore extends to real homogeneity by rational approximation; additivity and P(iu,v)=iP(u,v) then give full complex linearity in that argument.
The real pairing B is symmetric, and B(v,iu)=B(iu,v)=−B(u,iv). Hence P(v,u)=P(u,v)​, giving conjugate linearity in the second argument. Finally B(u,u)=Q(u) and B(u,iu)=0, because ∥(1+i)u∥=∥(1−i)u∥. Therefore
P(u,u)=∥u∥2,​
(16)
which is positive for u=0. This proves P is a complex inner product inducing exactly the original norm. The assumed Banach completeness makes the resulting inner product space Hilbert. This is the Jordan-von Neumann theorem.

∫0δ​s∣f(t+s)+f(t−s)−L+​−L−​∣​ds<∞,
(23)
then snf​(t)→(L+​+L−​)/2. A step function on a finite interval is constant on each side of any given point for sufficiently small distance, so its displayed numerator is zero there. Dini test for Fourier series gives exactly the asserted midpoint of the two one-sided values, including at jumps.
Let Pn​=sn​ on L2. It is the orthogonal projection onto the span of the displayed trigonometric functions through frequency n, hence ∥Pn​∥≤1. For a step-function approximation ψ,
∥Pn​f−f∥2​≤∥Pn​(f−ψ)∥2​+∥Pn​ψ−ψ∥2​+∥ψ−f∥2​≤2∥f−ψ∥2​+∥Pn​ψ−ψ∥2​.

Anti-self-adjoint means T∗=−T. Unitary means T∗T=TT∗=I, so T−1=T∗. For a unitary operator ∥Tu∥2=⟨u,T∗Tu⟩=∥u∥2, and the space is nonzero. Thus ∥T∥=1.
The series for the exponential converges absolutely in the Banach algebra B(V), since ∑n​∥Tn/n!∥≤e∥T∥. The adjoint is norm continuous and conjugate linear, and its action on these real scalar coefficients gives (eT)∗=eT∗. The absolutely convergent Cauchy product gives
eTe−T=e−TeT=I,
(27)
because its degree-n coefficient for n>0 is Tn∑j=0n​(−1)j/[j!(n−j)!]=0. If T∗=−T, these identities prove eT is unitary.
If T is invertible, zero is outside the spectrum of T−1. For μ=0 the factorization
T−1−μI=−μT−1(T−μ−1I)
(28)
shows that the left side is invertible exactly when the final factor is. Hence
σ(T−1)={λ−1:λ∈σ(T)}.​
(29)
For unitary T, both T and T−1=T∗ have norm one. The norm bound on each spectrum gives ∣λ∣≤1 and ∣λ−1∣≤1, forcing σ(T) to lie on the unit circle.
The converse fails: on C2 with its usual inner product take
T=(10​11​).​
(30)
Its spectrum is {1}, but T∗T=(11​12​)=I, so it is not unitary.
