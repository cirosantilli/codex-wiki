For α,β∈ℓ1,2​ and real scalars c,d, the scalar triangle inequality gives
∑j≥1​j2∣cαj​+dβj​∣≤∣c∣∑j​j2∣αj​∣+∣d∣∑j​j2∣βj​∣<∞.
(1)
The zero sequence belongs to the space, so it is a real vector space. The weighted sum is nonnegative and homogeneous, and the same inequality with c=d=1 proves its triangle inequality. Since every weight j2 is strictly positive, zero weighted sum forces every coordinate to vanish. Therefore ∥⋅∥1,2​ is a norm.

Define U:ℓ1,2​→ℓ1​ by (Uα)j​=j2αj​. This is a linear bijection, with inverse (U−1γ)j​=j−2γj​, and
∥Uα∥1​=∥α∥1,2​.​
(2)
Thus U is an isometric isomorphism of normed spaces onto the absolutely summable sequence space, which is a Banach space. Explicitly, a Cauchy sequence α(n) maps to a Cauchy sequence Uα(n)→γ∈ℓ1​; setting α=U−1γ gives convergence in the weighted norm. Hence ℓ1,2​ is a Banach space, an instance of weighted summable sequence space isometry.

Let D be the set of finitely supported sequences with rational coordinates. It is countable: it is the union over N of copies of QN. Given α∈ℓ1,2​ and ε>0, choose N with ∑j>N​j2∣αj​∣<ε/2. Choose rational numbers qj​ for 1≤j≤N so that ∑j=1N​j2∣qj​−αj​∣<ε/2, and put qj​=0 thereafter. Then q∈D and ∥q−α∥1,2​<ε. Thus D is countable and dense, so the space is separable.

Equip the continuous dual space X′ with the operator norm
∥f∥=sup∥x∥≤1​∣f(x)∣.
(5)
Continuity of a linear functional makes this finite; homogeneity, definiteness and the triangle inequality follow directly from the supremum formula.
For completeness of the dual space, let (fn​) be Cauchy in this norm. For each x∈X, ∣fn​(x)−fm​(x)∣≤∥fn​−fm​∥∥x∥, so (fn​(x)) is Cauchy in R. Define f(x)=limn​fn​(x). Passing to the limit in the linearity identities makes f linear. Since a Cauchy sequence is bounded, supn​∥fn​∥≤C and ∣f(x)∣≤C∥x∥, so f∈X′.
Given ε>0, choose N with ∥fn​−fm​∥≤ε for n,m≥N. Let m→∞ pointwise; then ∣fn​(x)−f(x)∣≤ε∥x∥ and therefore ∥fn​−f∥≤ε. Thus X′ is a Banach space. This proof in fact works even if X itself is not complete.

If a bilinear map is continuous, continuity at (0,0) gives δ>0 such that ∥F(x,y)∥≤1 whenever ∥x∥,∥y∥<δ. For nonzero x,y, apply this to δx/(2∥x∥) and δy/(2∥y∥). Bilinearity gives
∥F(x,y)∥≤δ24​∥x∥∥y∥.​
(6)
If either input is zero, the inequality follows from bilinearity directly.
Conversely, suppose the product bound holds with C≥0. At a fixed point (x,y),
F(x+h,y+k)−F(x,y)=F(h,y)+F(x,k)+F(h,k).
(7)
Its norm is at most C(∥h∥∥y∥+∥x∥∥k∥+∥h∥∥k∥), which tends to zero as (h,k)→(0,0). Thus the product bound is equivalent to joint continuity. This is boundedness criterion for a bilinear map.

The spectrum of a bounded operator is σ(T)={λ∈C:T−λI has no bounded inverse}. The point spectrum consists of those λ for which ker(T−λI)=0, namely the eigenvalues. The spectral radius is r(T)=supλ∈σ(T)​∣λ∣.
If ∥T∥<1, the Neumann series S=∑n=0∞​(−T)n converges in the Banach space of bounded linear operators, since its term norms are bounded by a convergent geometric series. Multiplying the finite sums on either side by I+T gives I−(−T)N+1, which tends in operator norm to I. Therefore
(I+T)−1=n=0∑∞​(−T)n.​
(11)

For the multiplication operator, ∥Tf∥∞​≤∥g∥∞​∥f∥∞​=∥f∥∞​. Taking f=1 gives equality, so ∥T∥=1.
An eigenfunction obeys (g(t)−λ)f(t)=0 at every t. If λ∈[0,1), its level set is the single point t=λ, and a continuous function supported at a single point is zero. If λ∈/[0,1], that level set is empty. For λ=1, every continuous function vanishing on [0,1] is an eigenfunction, and nonzero examples exist, such as f(t)=max{t−1,0}. Thus σp​(T)={1}.
If λ∈/[0,1], multiplication by (g−λ)−1 is a bounded inverse of T−λI. If λ∈[0,1], choose t0​ with g(t0​)=λ. Every function in the range of T−λI vanishes at t0​, so the range omits the constant function one. Hence it is not onto. Therefore
∥T∥=1,σp​(T)={1},σ(T)=[0,1].​
(14)

The unilateral shift operator R is not onto, since its outputs have first coordinate zero. The left shift operator L is not injective, since Le1​=0. Both have operator norm one, and LR=I. Consequently
L(R+A)=LR+LA=I+LA.
(15)
If ∥A∥<1, then ∥LA∥<1, so the Neumann series makes I+LA invertible. If R+A were invertible too, the identity above would give
L=(I+LA)(R+A)−1,
(16)
a product of invertible operators, contradicting the nontrivial kernel of L. Thus
∥A∥<1⟹R+A is not invertible.​
(17)
This is norm stability of noninvertibility for the unilateral right shift.

The Baire category theorem states that a nonempty complete metric space cannot be a countable union of closed nowhere dense sets. The Uniform boundedness principle says that if V is a Banach space, W a normed vector space, and T⊆B(V,W) is pointwise bounded, meaning supT∈T​∥Tx∥<∞ for every x∈V, then supT∈T​∥T∥<∞.
To prove it, let En​={x:supT∈T​∥Tx∥≤n}. Each set is closed by continuity of the individual operators, and their union is V. The Baire category theorem gives a ball B(x0​,r) inside some EN​. For ∥h∥<r, both x0​+h and x0​ are in EN​, so ∥Th∥≤∥T(x0​+h)∥+∥Tx0​∥≤2N for every T. Scaling gives ∥T∥≤2N/r uniformly. The zero-space and empty-family cases are immediate.
For the first family on a Hilbert space, fix x. The functionals y↦⟨y,Tx⟩ have operator norm ∥Tx∥ by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and equality on the direction of Tx. Their values are pointwise bounded by the hypothesis, after taking complex conjugates if necessary. Apply the Uniform boundedness principle to obtain supT∈E​∥Tx∥<∞. Apply it a second time to the operator family itself to conclude supT∈E​∥T∥<∞.
For the diagonal hypothesis, the answer depends on the scalar field. Over C, let qT​(x)=⟨Tx,x⟩, using the convention that the inner product is linear in its first variable. The complex polarization identity gives
4⟨Tx,y⟩=qT​(x+y)−qT​(x−y)+iqT​(x+iy)−iqT​(x−iy).
(18)
The assumed bounds at these four fixed vectors make every matrix coefficient uniformly bounded, so the preceding argument applies. Thus the complex-space answer is yes, even without assuming self-adjointness.
Over R, take X=R2 and
J=(01​−10​),F={nJ:n≥1}.
(19)
Every diagonal value ⟨nJx,x⟩ is zero, whereas ∥nJ∥=n. Thus the real-space answer is no in general. Real diagonal values do not detect the skew-symmetric part of an operator. This is diagonal boundedness of operator families over real and complex Hilbert spaces.

A bounded linear operator on a complex Hilbert space is self-adjoint if T=T∗, equivalently ⟨Tx,y⟩=⟨x,Ty⟩ for every x,y.
If Tx=0, then ⟨x,Ty⟩=⟨Tx,y⟩=0 for all y, so x∈(RanT)⊥. Conversely, membership in that orthogonal complement gives ⟨Tx,y⟩=0 for every y, and choosing y=Tx yields Tx=0. Therefore
kerT=(RanT)⊥.​
(20)

For a positive operator, ⟨Tx,x⟩≥0. We show directly that every nonreal or negative real λ lies in the resolvent set.
If Imλ=0, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and reality of ⟨Tx,x⟩ give
∥(T−λI)x∥∥x∥≥∣⟨(T−λI)x,x⟩∣≥∣Imλ∣∥x∥2.
(22)
Thus T−λI is bounded below, hence injective with closed range: a convergent sequence of outputs has Cauchy preimages, whose limit maps to that output. Its adjoint is T−λˉI, which has trivial kernel by the same estimate. The identity Ran(A)⊥=kerA∗, proved by the same inner-product calculation as in (i), makes its range dense. Being closed and dense, it is all of X, and the lower bound makes the inverse bounded.
If λ<0 is real, positivity gives ⟨(T−λI)x,x⟩≥(−λ)∥x∥2, so the same argument applies with lower bound −λ. Here the operator is self-adjoint, so its range is dense because its own kernel is zero. Hence
σ(T)⊆[0,∞).​
(23)

For finite subsets F⊂Z, orthonormality gives
​∑n∈F​αn​en​​2=∑n∈F​∣αn​∣2.
(33)
For SN​=∑∣n∣≤N​αn​en​, the squared norm of SN​−SM​ is the corresponding finite tail sum. Since ∑n∈Z​∣αn​∣2<∞, these tails tend to zero. Thus (SN​) is a Cauchy sequence in the complete Hilbert space and converges.
The same estimate for arbitrary finite tails proves that the sum is unconditional, so the bilateral orthogonal series converges in X independently of its ordering. This is the Riesz-Fischer theorem for an orthonormal system.

Use the inner product convention ⟨f,h⟩=∫−ππ​fhˉ. For h=1[−π/2,π/2]​, integration gives
⟨e0​,h⟩=π/2​,⟨ek​,h⟩=2/π​ksin(kπ/2)​(k=0).

For the convolution identity, fix any real t and define ht​(s)=g(t−s)​. Periodicity makes ht​∈L2(−π,π) with ∥ht​∥2​=∥g∥2​. A change of variable and integration over one full period give
