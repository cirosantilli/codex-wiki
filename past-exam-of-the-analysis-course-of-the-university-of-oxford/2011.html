A normed vector space over R is a real vector space X with a function ∥⋅∥:X→[0,∞) satisfying
∥x∥=0⟺x=0,∥ax∥=∣a∣∥x∥,∥x+y∥≤∥x∥+∥y∥.
(1)
Its distance is d(x,y)=∥x−y∥. It is a Banach space if this metric is complete: every Cauchy sequence converges to an element of X.

The series ∑n≥1​xn​ converges in X when its partial sums sN​=∑n=1N​xn​ converge in norm to an element of X. It is absolutely convergent when ∑n​∥xn​∥<∞.
If X is complete, then
∥sM​−sN​∥≤∑n=N+1M​∥xn​∥⟶0
(2)
for an absolutely convergent series. Its partial sums are Cauchy and therefore converge.
Conversely suppose every absolutely convergent series converges. Given a Cauchy sequence (zn​), choose a subsequence (znk​​) with ∥znk+1​​−znk​​∥≤2−k. The series
zn1​​+∑k=1∞​(znk+1​​−znk​​)
(3)
is absolutely convergent. Its partial sums are precisely the subsequence, which therefore converges to some z∈X. A Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges to that same limit: estimate ∥zn​−z∥ by ∥zn​−znk​​∥+∥znk​​−z∥. Hence
X is Banach⟺every absolutely convergent series in X converges.​
(4)

Show that c is a closed linear subspace of ℓ∞. Suppose a(j)∈c and ∥a(j)−a∥∞​→0. Given ε>0, choose j with uniform error below ε/3. Since a(j) converges, choose N such that ∣am(j)​−an(j)​∣<ε/3 for m,n≥N. Then
∣am​−an​∣≤∣am​−am(j)​∣+∣am(j)​−an(j)​∣+∣an(j)​−an​∣<ε.

The continuous dual space X′ is the vector space of bounded scalar-valued linear functionals, with operator norm
∥f∥=∥x∥≤1sup​∣f(x)∣.​
(10)
This makes it a normed space, and in fact a Banach space whether or not X is complete.
The norm-preserving Hahn-Banach theorem states that a bounded linear functional f0​ on any linear subspace M⊆X extends to a bounded linear functional f on all of X with f∣M​=f0​ and ∥f∥=∥f0​∥. The subspace need not be closed. This holds over both the real and complex scalar fields, using the complex version when necessary.

For the centered ball, the Hahn-Banach theorem supplies a norm-one real functional with f(y)=∥y∥>r. Hence f(z)≤r on the ball, and any t∈(r,∥y∥) separates it strictly from y.

Let Z=span​{xn​:n≥1}. If Z=X, part (a) provides a nonzero bounded functional annihilating Z; normalize it to have norm one. Density of the sequence in the dual unit ball gives an index n with ∥fn​−f∥<1/4. Since f(xn​)=0 and ∥xn​∥≤1,
∣fn​(xn​)∣≤∥fn​−f∥<41​.
(16)
But the hypothesis and reverse triangle inequality give
∣fn​(xn​)∣≥21​∥fn​∥>21​(1−41​)=83​,
(17)
a contradiction. Therefore the span is dense.
If X′ is separable, its unit ball has a countable dense set. For each nonzero fn​, the definition of its norm permits choosing xn​ in the unit ball with ∣fn​(xn​)∣≥∥fn​∥/2; choose zero for a zero functional. The preceding result gives a dense countable span over Q, or over Q+iQ in the complex case. Thus X is separable.
The converse fails: ℓ1 is separable, since finite rational-coordinate sequences are dense, but (ℓ1)′≅ℓ∞ is not separable. The dual identification sends a bounded sequence a to x↦∑n​an​xn​, isometrically; conversely a bounded functional is determined by its values on the coordinate vectors and hence has this form by truncation. The uncountable family of zero-one sequences in ℓ∞ has pairwise distance one, which no countable dense set can approximate within a radius smaller than 1/2.

For a bounded linear operator on a complex Banach space, its spectrum is
σ(T)={λ∈C:λI−T has no bounded two-sided inverse}.​
(18)
The complement is the resolvent set. For a real space, one may use its complexification when speaking of the complex spectrum; the algebraic arguments below apply over the underlying scalar field as well.

If T has a bounded inverse, it is onto and ∥x∥≤∥T−1∥∥Tx∥, giving the claimed lower bound. Conversely a positive lower bound makes T injective. Together with surjectivity it gives an everywhere-defined linear inverse with norm at most 1/ε. Hence it is bounded, proving the equivalence.
If R,S are individually invertible, their product has inverse S−1R−1. Conversely assume RS=SR and put A=RS, with A invertible. Both factors commute with A and therefore with A−1. Direct multiplication gives
R(SA−1)=I=(SA−1)R,qquadS(RA−1)=I=(RA−1)S.
(21)
These are bounded two-sided inverses, so an invertible commuting product has both factors invertible. The commutation assumption is used when verifying the second side of each inverse.

a OurBigBook.com
The graph G(T)={(x,Tx):x∈X} is closed if it is a closed subset of X×Y. Equivalently, xn​→x and Txn​→y imply Tx=y.
The closed graph theorem states that an everywhere-defined linear map between Banach spaces is bounded if and only if its graph is closed. Completeness of both spaces is part of the theorem. A bounded map has a closed graph by continuity.
Conversely, if the graph is closed, it is a Banach space in the product norm ∥(x,y)∥=∥x∥+∥y∥. The coordinate projection P:G(T)→X, P(x,Tx)=x, is a bounded linear bijection. The open mapping theorem makes P−1 bounded. The other coordinate projection Q:G(T)→Y is bounded, and T=QP−1 is therefore bounded. This proves the theorem from the permitted result.

The orthogonal complement is
Y⊥={w∈X:⟨w,y⟩=0 for every y∈Y}.​
(34)
It is a linear subspace and is closed, since it is an intersection of kernels of the continuous linear functionals w↦⟨w,y⟩. Continuity follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
The minimizing point and orthogonality proved below write every x as x=y0​+(x−y0​)∈Y+Y⊥. If w∈Y∩Y⊥, then ∥w∥2=⟨w,w⟩=0, so the intersection is zero. Consequently
X=Y⊕Y⊥.​
(35)
In particular the minimizing point is unique: an alternative orthogonal decomposition would have a difference in this zero intersection.

Use the convention that the inner product is linear in its first argument. The precise polarization identity is
⟨x,y⟩=41​(∥x+y∥2−∥x−y∥2+i∥x+iy∥2−i∥x−iy∥2).​
(42)
If (1) holds, apply this formula before and after U to get preservation of all inner products, proving (2). If (2) holds, the identity ⟨Ux,Uy⟩=⟨x,U∗Uy⟩ gives U∗U=I. For z=Ux, surjectivity then gives UU∗z=UU∗Ux=Ux=z, proving (3).
From (3), ∥Ux∥2=⟨x,U∗Ux⟩=∥x∥2 and likewise ∥U∗x∥=∥x∥, proving (4). Finally, if (4) holds, apply the same polarization identity to U∗ to obtain UU∗=I. This makes U onto; the other norm identity supplies the remaining requirement of (1). Thus
(1)⟺(2)⟺(3)⟺(4).​
(43)
These four statements are conditions in a single equivalence proof, rather than four independent source questions.
