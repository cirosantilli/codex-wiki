Argmin consistency under uniform convergence in probability

Words: 135 Articles: 1
M-estimator OurBigBook.com
On a compact parameter set, a continuous deterministic objective with a unique minimum has a positive separation gap outside each neighbourhood of its minimizer. Uniform convergence in probability of the random objectives bounds the deterministic objective difference at any attained random minimizer by twice the supremum discrepancy. This proves statistical consistency of each measurable minimizing selection.

Global argmin may escape a compact convergence set (a(θ)=θ2/(1+θ4))

Words: 86
Argmin consistency under uniform convergence in probability OurBigBook.com
Uniform convergence in probability of empirical criteria on a compact set proves consistency for minimizers in that set; it does not confine a minimizer over a larger domain. A bounded identifiable regression function can approach its true value again at infinity. For median regression, a(θ)=θ2/(1+θ4) has unique zero at zero, but a small positive fitted sample median c has a second solution θ2=(1+1−4c2​)/(2c). This solution diverges as c↓0, although uniform convergence on any fixed compact interval holds. Constraining the estimator, or proving suitable global separation and localization, repairs the argument.

Influence function (IF(x;T,F))

Words: 2k Articles: 14
Robust statistics OurBigBook.com
An influence function asks how much a population answer moves when an infinitesimal fraction of its probability distribution is relocated to a specified point. It distinguishes the unbounded response of the mean from the bounded response of the median and the Huber location estimator. The normal-location influence curves and finite contamination bias of normal location below make that difference quantitative.
Let T be a real-valued statistical functional, F a reference probability measure, and δx​ the Dirac measure concentrated at x. Along the epsilon-contamination neighborhood path Fε,x​=(1−ε)F+εδx​, define the right derivative
IF(x;T,F)=limε↓0​εT(Fε,x​)−T(F)​,T(Fε,x​)=T(F)+εIF(x;T,F)+o(ε).
(6)
The name influence curve refers to the same object. The derivative is in the signed direction δx​−F, so the calculation removes baseline mass as well as adding a Dirac measure. The definition is a limit for each fixed x; it does not automatically give a remainder uniform over arbitrarily distant x.[ref]
The statistical functional must be defined on these contaminated probability measures, and the right derivative must exist and be finite. For a mean, a finite first absolute moment suffices. For a median, require a unique baseline median and a probability density function continuous and strictly positive there. For a smooth M-estimator, require an isolated population root, justified differentiation of its expected value, and a nonzero derivative with respect to the fitted parameter. These are specific conditions for the formulas below. Existence of the pointwise influence function alone proves neither an asymptotic linear representation nor pathwise differentiability of a statistical functional; each needs control of its own remainder and admissible statistical paths.

Influence function of the mean (IF(x;μ,F)=x−μ)

Words: 141
Influence function OurBigBook.com
For the statistical functional T(F)=μ=EF​X, assuming EF​∣X∣<∞, the contaminated mean is exactly
T(Fε,x​)=(1−ε)μ+εx.
(7)
Thus the influence function of the mean is x−μ, and its gross-error sensitivity is infinite on an unbounded sample space. The linear approximation is exact here: any fixed positive contamination fraction allows an arbitrarily large change by moving x far enough. No finite second moment is needed for this derivative, although a finite variance is needed for the usual central limit theorem for the sample mean.
For comparison, the influence function of the sample median is
IF(x;Med,F)=2f(m)sgn(x−m)​,x=m,
(8)
when the median m is unique and the probability density function f is positive and continuous at m. The sign function records which side of m receives the contaminating mass. The influence function of a quantile supplies the short derivation. With the generalized-inverse quantile convention, contamination exactly at x=m leaves the median at m, giving derivative zero there. If f(m) is very small, this bounded influence function can still be large; if f(m)=0, the displayed formula is unavailable.

Influence function of a Huber location estimator (IF(x;T,F)=sψk​((x−θ)/s)/A)

Words: 273
Influence function OurBigBook.com
Fix a scale s>0 and cutoff k>0. Define the population Huber location estimator by the root
EF​ψk​(sX−θ​)=0,θ=T(F),A=EF​ψk′​(sX−θ​)>0.
(9)
Here ψk​ is the Huber score. It has Lipschitz continuity, so dominated convergence justifies differentiation when F puts no mass at θ±ks. Then A=PF​(∣X−θ∣<ks), and the nonzero derivative identifies the local population root. The implicit function theorem applies away from the contaminating score's kinks; its Lipschitz continuity also gives the first-order expansion at those kinks. Differentiating its contaminated equation yields
0=−sA​IF(x;T,F)+ψk​(sx−θ​),IF(x;T,F)=Asψk​((x−θ)/s)​.
(10)
The normalization matters: the Huber score alone is not the influence function. The gross-error sensitivity is sk/A, while the rejection point of an influence function is infinite because the clipped tails stay nonzero. The original clipped estimating criterion and its derivative normalization also appear in the population analysis of M-estimators.[ref]
Scale sets what counts as a large residual. For a normal distribution N(μ,σ2) with fixed s=σ, symmetry gives θ=μ and A=2Φ(k)−1, where Φ is the standard normal distribution function. If the scale is instead a statistical functional S(F), differentiation of the joint equation adds the scale contribution:
IF(x;T,F)=Asψk​(rx​)−BIF(x;S,F)​,rx​=sx−θ​,B=EF​[rX​ψk′​(rX​)].
(11)
Under a symmetric baseline and an odd Huber score, B=0, so scale estimation drops out at first order. This cancellation needs regularity of S and does not protect finite contamination changes: an inflated standard deviation enlarges the physical cutoff ks. A robust choice, such as a suitable Scale M-estimator, needs its own influence function and breakdown point checked. Fixing a numeric cutoff in raw units also sacrifices scale equivariance when units change.[ref]

Consider the population statistical model Fε,x​=(1−ε)N(0,1)+εδx​. The following are deterministic statistical functional calculations, not observed contamination experiments. Compare the actual location bias with the linear approximation εIF(x;T,F). The mean bias is exactly εx. For positive x exceeding the shifted median and 0≤ε<1/2, the generalized-inverse median bias is exactly
bMed​(ε,x)=Φ−1(2(1−ε)1​).
(13)
This formula applies to both x=1 and x=8 throughout the plotted interval 0≤ε≤0.1. If the contaminating atom lies inside the quantile jump containing probability 1/2, the generalized-inverse median instead equals that atom.
For the Huber location estimator with s=1 and k=1.345, solve its actual population equation, rather than substituting the influence function:
(1−ε)gk​(t)+εψk​(x−t)=0,gk​(t)=Eψk​(Z−t),Z∼N(0,1).
(14)
Writing a=t−k and b=t+k, integration of the normal distribution gives
gk​(t)=ϕ(a)−ϕ(b)−t[Φ(b)−Φ(a)]+k[1−Φ(b)−Φ(a)],
(15)
where ϕ is the probability density function of N(0,1). Its derivative is −[Φ(t+k)−Φ(t−k)]<0, so the mixture score is strictly decreasing for ε<1. For ε<1/2, its limits at negative and positive infinity have opposite signs even with arbitrary distant point contamination. The displayed examples use the unique root, evaluated by the bisection method to numerical precision.
At x=8 and ε=0.01, the exact mean, median, and Huber location estimator biases are 0.080000, 0.012660, and 0.016541; the influence function predictions are 0.080000, 0.012533, and 0.016375. At ε=0.05, they become 0.400000, 0.066012, and 0.086241, versus predictions 0.400000, 0.062666, and 0.081875. The bounded alternatives control the effect of the distant atom, but their actual response curves bend away from the tangent.
The nearby atom x=1 gives a useful comparison: at ε=0.05, the Huber location estimator bias is 0.060237, slightly below its linear approximation 0.060874, whereas the median bias is still 0.066012. The direction and size of approximation error depend on the contaminating location. Even accurate local influence calculations must specify F, the scale, the cutoff, and the actual contamination fraction.

Local influence versus replacement breakdown

Words: 244
Influence function OurBigBook.com
The influence function describes an infinitesimal change at a specified reference probability distribution. The replacement breakdown point asks how many observations in a particular finite sample can be replaced before an estimator becomes unbounded. The finite-sample maximum bias records the intervening changes. These are different questions: a bounded influence function at one baseline is not a general bound over all epsilon-contamination neighborhoods, nor a proof of a positive replacement breakdown point.[ref]
The mean illustrates agreement between local and global fragility: its gross-error sensitivity is infinite and one replacement can send the sample mean arbitrarily far. The median has finite gross-error sensitivity under the stated positive-density assumptions, while its sample median withstands fewer than about half the observations being replaced under ordinary tie conventions. Its population contamination bias above still diverges as ε↑1/2 when the atom remains beyond the shifted median. Here x must also move outward; taking that limit with a fixed finite atom would invalidate the displayed quantile formula.
For scalar location with a fixed positive scale, the Huber score has a direct global argument: if strictly more than half of the sample stays fixed, then beyond every retained observation by more than ks, their saturated scores outweigh all possible replaced scores. Every root therefore stays in a bounded interval. This is a separate finite-replacement argument; estimating scale changes its premises. A smooth bounded influence function is valuable local protection, but global robustness also depends on the full estimating rule, scale choice, and root selection.

Rejection point of an influence function (ρ∗(T,F))

Words: 67
Influence function OurBigBook.com
For observations expressed in centered, standardized coordinates, the rejection point is
ρ∗(T,F)=inf{r≥0:IF(x;T,F)=0 whenever ∣x∣>r},
(16)
with value ∞ if the set is empty. A finite rejection point means sufficiently distant contamination has zero first-order effect. Boundedness of the influence function does not imply a finite rejection point: the influence function of a quantile has nonzero constant tails. Nor does tending to zero at infinity guarantee a finite rejection point unless those tails become identically zero.

Influence function of a quantile (IF(z;qp​,F))

Influence function OurBigBook.com

Huber location estimator (θk,s​)

Words: 1k Articles: 5
Robust statistics OurBigBook.com
The Huber location estimator solves ∑i​ψk​(xi​−θ)=0, where ψk​ clips its argument to [−k,k].
More generally, fix a known scale s>0 and a dimensionless threshold k>0 before fitting. For observations x1​,…,xn​, a Huber location estimator is a minimizer of the summed Huber loss, equivalently a root of its estimating equation:
Qk,s​(θ)=∑i=1n​ρk​(sxi​−θ​),θk,s​∈argminθ∈R​Qk,s​(θ),∑i=1n​ψk​(sxi​−θk,s​​)=0.
(20)
Here ρk​ is the Huber loss and ψk​ the Huber score. The physical clipping distance is ks, in the observations' units; the initial formula uses s=1. A large observation keeps contributing a bounded signed score rather than being discarded. The original clipped location criterion appears in Peter J. Huber's 1964 paper.[ref]
For the original data (0,1,2,3,4,40), take s=1 and k=2. At θ=2.5, the standardized differences and their Huber scores are
(xi​−2.5)i=16​=(−2.5,−1.5,−0.5,0.5,1.5,37.5),(ψ2​(xi​−2.5))i=16​=(−2,−1.5,−0.5,0.5,1.5,2).
(21)
Their sum is zero, so the Huber location estimator is 2.5. Four scores remain in the central linear region, making the root unique. By comparison, the sample mean is 50/6≃8.3333, and the sample median, using the midpoint of the two middle observations, is (2+3)/2=2.5. Agreement with the sample median is a feature of these data, rather than an identity between the two estimators.
The geometry explains both existence and possible nonuniqueness. The Huber loss is a convex function, so Qk,s​ is a convex function of θ and its derivative is −s−1∑i​ψk​((xi​−θ)/s). For n≥1, Qk,s​(θ) tends to infinity as ∣θ∣ grows, hence attains a minimum; its zero derivative characterizes every minimizer. Between clipping transitions, the summed score has slope −m/s, where m is the number of observations strictly within ks of θ. A root with m>0 is unique. If m=0, a flat interval is possible: for data (0,10) with s=1,k=2, every θ∈[2,8] is a minimizer. A complete single-valued estimator then needs a selection rule, such as the interval's midpoint.
A bounded Huber score limits one observation's contribution to the estimating equation. The influence function of a Huber location estimator additionally divides by the population's central mass, so the size of its bound depends on the reference probability distribution. Clipping does not by itself make the bias of an estimator zero or prove a universal breakdown point; the separate local influence versus replacement breakdown argument specifies what fixed scale permits. Estimating s from the same data changes the procedure and requires separate analysis.

Huber location response to one outlier (T(M))

Words: 213
Huber location estimator OurBigBook.com
Hold (0,1,2,3,4) fixed and move only the sixth observation M. For M≥5, the sample mean is (10+M)/6, the sample median is 2.5, and the Huber location estimator with fixed s=1,k=2 stays at 2.5. Once M−2.5≥2, its Huber score is already 2; moving it farther changes the Huber loss but leaves the root unchanged.
For a closer view of the transition, the exact Huber location estimator over 0≤M≤40 is
T(M)={(8+M)/5,2.5,​0≤M≤4.5,4.5≤M≤40.​
(22)
To verify the first branch, when 0≤M≤2 the score of 4 is saturated and the other five scores sum with it to 8+M−5θ. When 2≤M≤4.5, the score of 0 is saturated instead, giving the same expression. At M=4.5, the moving observation saturates too, and the equation becomes 10−4θ=0. The piecewise formula is continuous at the transition.
The rising sample mean comes from an unbounded linear score, the flat distant-outlier response from a bounded Huber score. This is a finite-data response curve with a specified scale and threshold, rather than a population influence function or a general claim about arbitrary numbers of outliers.

Huber score (ψk​(u))

Words: 172 Articles: 1
Huber location estimator OurBigBook.com
The Huber score clips a residual at a fixed threshold:
ψk​(u)=max(−k,min(u,k)).
(23)
For k>0, the Huber score is the derivative of the Huber loss:
ψk​(u)=ρk′​(u)=⎩⎨⎧​−k,u,k,​u<−k,−k≤u≤k,u>k.​
(24)
It is a continuous odd function and a monotone function, with Lipschitz continuity and ∣ψk​(u)∣≤k. In a Huber location estimator, its argument is the standardized difference u=(x−θ)/s. Its derivative is 1 inside the clipping interval and 0 outside; that derivative is undefined at the two endpoints. The score itself remains continuous there. This estimating score need not be the score function of a separately specified statistical model, nor is it already a normalized influence function.

Optimal bounded influence function for normal location (hK​(x)=2Φ(K)−1max(−K,min(x,K))​)

Words: 84
Huber score OurBigBook.com
For standard normal distribution location estimation, an influence function h obeys E[Zh(Z)]=1. Under ∣h∣≤C, minimize Eh2 by projecting ax onto [−C,C], choosing a to meet that identity. For C>π/2​ this gives the displayed Huber score normalized by 2Φ(K)−1, with C=K/(2Φ(K)−1). Pointwise minimality of h2−2axh proves global optimality after integration. At C=π/2​ the only feasible normalized function is Csgn(x) almost everywhere, the influence function of the sample median. The endpoint is a limit of rescaled clipped scores, not the identically zero score obtained by substituting K=0 literally.

Huber loss (ρk​(u))

Words: 298 Articles: 1
Huber location estimator OurBigBook.com
The Huber loss is quadratic near zero and linear beyond a fixed cutoff; its derivative is the clipped Huber score.
For threshold k>0, the Huber loss is the loss function
ρk​(u)={21​u2,k∣u∣−21​k2,​∣u∣≤k,∣u∣>k.​
(25)
The subtraction k2/2 makes the values meet at ∣u∣=k; the slopes also meet, so the Huber loss is continuously differentiable with Huber score as its derivative. It is a convex function, since that derivative is a monotone function. These quadratic and linear pieces define the original clipped location criterion.[ref]
For k=2, ρ2​(1)=1/2, ρ2​(2)=2, and ρ2​(5)=8, whereas the squared criterion u2/2 at u=5 is 12.5. Increasing a standardized difference from 5 to 6 raises the Huber loss by 2, but raises the squared criterion by 5.5. The compromise preserves quadratic sensitivity to small differences while limiting the slope for large ones. This makes the Huber loss useful when ordinary observations should retain their detailed distances yet isolated outliers should exert limited pull.
For a fixed finite dataset and scale, taking k large enough that every difference from the sample mean lies within ks recovers that sample mean. Toward the other extreme, for each fixed u, ρk​(u)/k→∣u∣ as k↓0, connecting the criterion to median minimizes expected absolute loss. The normalization is essential: putting k=0 into the unscaled Huber loss gives zero everywhere, not a usable median criterion.

Huber gradient regularizer

Words: 53
Huber loss OurBigBook.com
A Huber loss applied to a discrete gradient combines quadratic penalization of small slopes with linear penalization of large slopes. This interpolates between squared-gradient smoothing and total variation denoising, allowing smooth regions while retaining an edge-preserving large-gradient regime. The threshold should be chosen consistently with the signal's units and discretization. It can reduce staircasing in total variation denoising without guaranteeing its elimination.
