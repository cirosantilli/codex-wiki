Amplitude modulation

 Home Electrical engineering
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 9
Amplitude modulation encodes a message by varying the multiplying wave amplitude of a carrier wave. Conventional AM adds a positive constant before multiplication, allowing a simple envelope detector to recover the message within its limits. Multiplication translates the message's frequency spectrum into a band around the carrier, adapting a slow message to a faster channel. This is an application of the modulation property of the Fourier transform.[ref]
For an original single-message-frequency voltage model, choose
s(t)=A[1+mcos(Ωt)]cos(ωc​t),A>0,0<Ω<ωc​,m≥0.
(2)
Here t is time, A is the peak voltage amplitude of the unmodulated carrier wave, ωc​ is its angular frequency, Ω is the message angular frequency, and m is the dimensionless AM modulation index. The message shape is cos(Ωt); m measures how deeply it changes the multiplying factor. Define the signed factor q(t)=1+mcos(Ωt). The nonnegative wave envelope of the representation z(t)=Aq(t)eiωc​t is
E(t)=∣z(t)∣=A∣q(t)∣.
(3)
The instantaneous voltage s(t) crosses zero every carrier cycle, even when E(t) is large. Its value at one time, its wave envelope, and its carrier-component amplitude are three different quantities. The narrow-envelope intuition additionally needs Ω≪ωc​; the algebraic model only assumes Ω<ωc​.
The beauty is that two views reveal different parts of the same operation. In time, the message appears to ride on the oscillations. In the frequency spectrum, amplitude modulation sidebands show it as a pair of translated components. An envelope detector reads their combined shape without needing the carrier's wave phase; coherent demodulation instead uses a synchronized reference and preserves the signed factor.
