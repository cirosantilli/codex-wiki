Polymerase chain reaction

 Home Biology Genetics Molecular genetics
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 19
The polymerase chain reaction amplifies a selected DNA region using repeated copying steps. Amplified material can be examined by DNA sequencing; appropriate template controls help distinguish a new rearrangement from material already present in the starting preparation.
The essential trick is to choose two PCR primers on opposite DNA strands with their extendible 3′ ends facing into the same interval. A DNA polymerase extends each PCR primer 5′→3′; the resulting DNA can itself become a template. The amplicon is therefore selected by two opposed PCR primers, rather than by the DNA polymerase recognizing a named gene. This makes PCR amplification useful for inspecting one interval within a much larger genome. Here PCR means polymerase chain reaction; the statistical abbreviation PCR means principal component regression.[ref]
For a deliberately tiny nucleotide sequence example, display the upper strand as 5′-ACGT-GGAATT-CAGC-3′. The left PCR primer is 5′-ACGT-3′ and binds the lower strand. The right PCR primer, written in its own 5′-to-3′ direction, is 5′-GCTG-3′: the reverse complement of CAGC. It binds the upper strand and extends leftward in the drawing. With flanks outside this interval, the bounded amplicon contains 4+6+4=14 base pairs, including both PCR primer sequences. These four-nucleotide PCR primers illustrate direction and counting only; they are not a proposed experimental design.
A useful way to understand the polymerase chain reaction is to separate recognition from construction. Base pairing positions a PCR primer; a DNA polymerase then joins new nucleotides onto that existing strand. The PCR primer becomes part of the copied DNA, rather than merely marking a place for an independent strand to start. A PCR thermal cycle repeatedly exposes templates, positions starting ends and permits construction. This makes DNA copying possible outside biological cells, with the chosen PCR primers specifying which interval is repeatedly accessible.
