Point process

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 16
A point process records random locations by a random measure that counts them. Examples include arrival times of a queue, positions of stars, and the finite eigenvalue configuration of a random matrix. The count in an observation window is a random variable; its expected value is supplied by the intensity measure of a point process, while its fluctuations and relationships with other windows require more information.
For a precise spatial convention, take E=Rd with its Borel sigma-algebra and a probability space (Ω,F,P). A locally finite point process Π is a measurable function from Ω to the space Nlf​(E) of locally finite point measures, equipped with the sigma-algebra generated by evaluations ν↦ν(A) for Borel sets A. Each realization has the form
Πω​=∑j∈J(ω)​δXj​(ω)​,Πω​(A)=∑j∈J(ω)​1{Xj​(ω)∈A}​,
(9)
where J(ω) is finite or countable and repeated locations are allowed; δx​ is a Dirac measure. Here a point measure is a counting measure with possible multiplicities, rather than the counting measure of a set of distinct locations.[ref]
Local finiteness means Πω​(B)<∞ for every bounded Borel set B, on a single almost sure event. In Rd it suffices to check the countable collection of balls of integer radius centered at the origin, and this is equivalent to finiteness on every compact set. On a general measurable space there is no notion of boundedness without additional structure: state a metric or an explicit family of observation windows. Another convention on locally compact spaces requires finiteness on compact sets; this need not coincide with finiteness on all bounded sets outside Rd. Local finiteness does not require finitely many points in all of E.[ref]
