Write the plate area as S and separation as d, so the original capacitance is C=ε0​S/d. Neglect edge fields. The greatest capacitance occurs when each material occupies half the plate area and spans the full separation. These two regions are capacitors in parallel, sharing the same electric potential difference:
Cmax​=2C​+2ϵC​=21+ϵ​C.​
(1)
The least capacitance occurs when a dielectric layer of thickness d/2 covers the whole area, leaving a vacuum layer of the same thickness. The two layers are capacitors in series: the normal electric displacement field is continuous and the voltage drops add. Their individual capacitances are 2ϵC and 2C, giving
Cmin​=(2C1​+2ϵC1​)−1=1+ϵ2ϵ​C.​
(2)
The arithmetic-harmonic bounds for dielectric capacitance show that these are genuine extrema over other arrangements of the same two volumes, within the ideal parallel-plate model. A uniform trial electric field in the fixed-voltage energy principle bounds C above by the arithmetic mean of the two permittivities; a uniform trial electric displacement field in the fixed-charge principle bounds it below by their harmonic mean. The two layer arrangements attain those bounds. Both reduce to C when ϵ=1.

Cold air is denser than room air at approximately the same pressure. The ideal gas law gives a fractional mass density contrast of order Δρ/ρ∼ΔT/T. The initial outflow is therefore a gravity current driven by reduced gravity
g′=gρΔρ​∼gTΔT​.
(13)

t∼g′H​H​=g′H​​∼1–2 s,of order seconds.​

Images and uniqueness. The method of images replaces a conducting boundary by fictitious charges outside the physical field region. Choose them so that the resulting electric potential satisfies the required conductor potentials. The Uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem then guarantees the same physical exterior field. For example, a point charge q at height h above a grounded plane has an image −q at height −h: the plane is an equipotential at zero, and the charge is attracted toward it by q2/(16πε0​h2). The image is a computational device; the physical conductor carries a continuous induced surface charge density.
Potential of a line. Surround an infinite line charge λ by a coaxial Gaussian cylinder of radius r and length L. Gauss's law gives Er​(2πrL)=λL/ε0​, hence Er​=λ/(2πε0​r). Integrating Er​=−dV/dr gives
V(r)−V(r0​)=−2πε0​λ​lnr0​r​.​
(15)
Writing an additive constant instead is equivalent. The reference length makes the logarithm dimensionless; an isolated infinite line cannot be assigned a finite zero potential at infinity.
Locating the two lines. Put the grounded plane at x=0 and the physical cylinder at (x,y)=(d,0), with axis along z and radius a<d. Set c=d2−a2​, and place line charges +λ and −λ at (c,0) and (−c,0). If R+​ and R−​ are their distances from an observation point, the cylinder-plane image charges give
V(x,y)=2πε0​λ​lnR+​R−​​,R±2​=(x∓c)2+y2.
(16)
On x=0, R−​=R+​, so V=0. To check the cylinder, define q=(d+c)/a. The level set R−​=qR+​ is a circle: completing the square gives
(x−cq2−1q2+1​)2+y2=(q2−12cq​)2.
(17)
Since d2−c2=a2, its centre is d and radius is a. The positive line lies inside the cylinder; the negative line lies behind the plane. There is consequently no fictitious charge in the exterior physical region. A Gaussian contour around the cylinder encloses +λ only, so the required physical charge per unit length is also correct. These boundary conditions and the far-field behaviour determine the physical solution by Uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem.
Cylinder voltage. The constant distance ratio on the cylinder immediately gives
Vc​=2πε0​λ​lnad+d2−a2​​=2πε0​λ​arcoshad​.​
(18)
Its capacitance per unit length is C′=2πε0​/arcosh(d/a). In the thin-cable limit, arcosh(d/a)≃ln(2d/a).
Cable attraction. Because the cable is idealized as infinite, the meaningful answer is force per unit length. For a=0.020 m, centre height d≃10 m and Vc​=1.1×104 V,
λ=arcosh(500)2πε0​Vc​​≃8.86×10−8 Cm−1.
(19)
The electrostatic attraction per unit length of a cylinder above a plane is
f=4πε0​d2−a2​λ2​≃7.05×10−6 Nm−1,downward.​
(20)
For example, this follows without any ambiguity about moving image positions from Fd​=21​Vc2​dC′/dd at fixed voltage, including the work of the voltage source. Its sign is negative because capacitance decreases with height. A cable length L experiences approximately fL downward when end effects are negligible. This is the electrostatic contribution only.

Inductance and interface conditions. For a linear coil, self-inductance is the ratio L=Λ/I of its own magnetic flux linkage Λ=NΦ to current. Its induced electromotive force is −LdI/dt for fixed L. Across an interface with unit normal n from medium 1 to 2,
n⋅(B2​−B1​)=0,n×(H2​−H1​)=Kf​.​
(21)
These electromagnetic boundary conditions follow from Gauss's law for magnetism and Ampère's law. Here Kf​ is free surface current. At the iron–air faces there is no free surface current, so normal magnetic flux density and tangential magnetic field intensity are continuous. In each isotropic linear medium, B=μ0​μr​H.
Complete core. A slender toroid has approximately uniform H along mean path length ℓ. Ampère's law gives Hℓ=NI, so B=μμ0​NI/ℓ. The flux per turn is Φ=BA, yielding the inductance of a toroidal winding,
L0​=INΦ​=ℓμμ0​N2A​.​
(22)
This uses constant relative magnetic permeability μ and neglects leakage, fringing and saturation.
Why the gap lowers inductance. The gap replaces an easy magnetic path in iron with air. Maintaining the same flux now requires a much larger magnetic field intensity in part of the path and therefore more ampere-turns. Equivalently, magnetic reluctance increases, so the flux linkage for a fixed current and hence self-inductance decrease. A narrow gap can matter greatly because its permeability is smaller by the factor μ.
Oblique-gap calculation. In the original diagram t is the perpendicular separation of the cut faces. Let s point along the core, and let n,q be normal and tangential to the gap faces. With the face at angle θ to the core direction, s=sinθn+cosθq. Write the core flux density as Bs. The boundary conditions give
Bg,n​=Bsinθ,Hg,q​=μμ0​Bcosθ​,
(23)
so
Hg​=μ0​B​(sinθn+μcosθ​q).
(24)
The gap area normal to its nearly normal field is A/sinθ, not A. Its leading reluctance is consequently tsinθ/(μ0​A). Adding the core reluctance gives the inductance of a toroid with an oblique narrow air gap,
NI≃μ0​B​(μℓ​+tsinθ),L≃ℓ/μ+tsinθμ0​N2A​.
(25)
Thus at θ=45∘,
L≃ℓ+μt/2​μμ0​N2A​.​
(26)
The approximation retains the possibly order-one ratio μt/ℓ while neglecting t/ℓ, fringing and the small tangential gap contribution. More precisely, a local uniform-field path along the core, with missing iron length t/sinθ subtracted, gives denominator ℓ/μ+tsinθ(1−1/μ). Its correction is beyond the stipulated large-μ, narrow-gap accuracy; simply neglecting μt would not be justified.
Field in the slot. The component expression above gives
Hg​=μ0​B​sin2θ+μ2cos2θ​​.
(27)
For the 45∘ gap and μ≫1,
Hg​≃2​μ0​B​≃t+2​ℓ/μNI​.​
(28)
Its units are Am−1. The field is almost perpendicular to the cut faces, rather than following the iron direction straight across the cut. Its small angle away from the face normal satisfies tanα=1/μ at 45∘; the sense reverses if the current reverses.

Field law and centre value. The Biot-Savart law for a steady thin wire is
dB=4πμ0​I​∣r−r′∣3dℓ′×(r−r′)​.
(29)
Here dℓ′ follows the conventional electric current and the displacement points from source to observation point. For a circular loop of radius a, every element at the centre has distance a and contributes in the same axial direction. Choose +z by the right-hand rule. Then
B(0)=4πa2μ0​I​(2πa)z=2aμ0​I​z.​
(30)
On-axis integration. At axial distance z, every wire element has distance a2+z2​. Azimuthal symmetry cancels transverse components. Parameterizing r′=(acosφ,asinφ,0) gives axial cross-product element a2dφ, so the on-axis magnetic field of a circular current loop is
B(0,z)=B0​(z)z,B0​(z)=2(a2+z2)3/2μ0​Ia2​.​
(31)
It points the same way on the two halves of the axis; its strength is even in z.
Directions near the axial point. At a point on the positive axis the field is axial, but nearby lines spread outward because B0​ decreases as z increases. On the negative-axis side they converge toward the axis while still progressing in the positive axial direction. There is no azimuthal field. The near-axis sketch shows this local spreading; the complete magnetic field lines return around the outside of the loop and form closed curves.
Radial component from zero divergence. Smooth axial symmetry gives Bz​(ρ,z)=B0​(z)+O(ρ2) and Bρ​=O(ρ). By Gauss's law for magnetism,
ρ1​∂ρ​(ρBρ​)+∂z​Bz​=0.
(32)
Integrate radially from zero, excluding a singular 1/ρ term, to obtain the near-axis radial magnetic field, Bρ​=−ρB0′​(z)/2+O(ρ3). Differentiating the axial field therefore gives
Bρ​≃4(a2+z2)5/23μ0​Ia2zρ​.​
(33)
For z>0 it is outward; for z<0 it is inward. This supplies the direction reasoning used in the sketch.
Force on the second loop. Put the second loop at z=d and radius ρ=a. For d≫a, the radial field due to the first loop is Bρ​≃3μ0​Ia3/(4d4). The element dℓ=adφφ​ experiences Lorentz force
dF=Iadφ(Bz​ρ​−Bρ​z).
(34)
The radial contributions cancel as vectors around the loop. The axial contributions add, giving the force between distant coaxial current loops,
Fz​=−2πaIBρ​≃−2d43πμ0​I2a4​.​
(35)
The sign follows φ​×ρ​=−z. Both the small radial coordinate relative to d and the dipole approximation are justified by a/d≪1.
Force direction. The second loop is pulled back toward the first: the force is attractive for the same current sense. Reversing one current reverses the force. The much larger axial field chiefly produces radial element forces; retaining the smaller radial field is essential for the net axial attraction.

Field equations. In a homogeneous source-free nonmagnetic medium, D=ϵε0​E and B=μ0​H. Maxwell's equations become
∇⋅E=0,∇⋅H=0,∇×E=−μ0​∂t​H,∇×H=ϵε0​∂t​E.​
(36)
The first equation follows from ∇⋅D=0 for spatially constant nonzero ϵ. When the effective relative permittivity is negative below, its charge response is already included in D; it must not also be counted as an extra free source.
Amplitude relations and bulk dispersion. Work with complex phasors proportional to eikx−Kz−iωt. For H=(0,Hy​,0), the Ampere-Maxwell law gives
(KHy​,0,ikHy​)=−iωϵε0​(Ex​,0,Ez​),
(37)
so the amplitudes must obey
Ex​=ωϵε0​iK​Hy​,Ez​=−ωϵε0​k​Hy​.​
(38)
They are not arbitrary independent constants: the dispersion relation alone would not make arbitrary amplitudes satisfy the equations. These relations give ikEx​−KEz​=0, so the electric divergence vanishes; the magnetic divergence vanishes because there is no y dependence. The only nonzero component of Faraday's law of induction is
−KEx​−ikEz​=iωμ0​Hy​.
(39)
Substitution then gives
k2−K2=ω2ϵε0​μ0​=c2ω2ϵ​.​
(40)
This proves that all four field equations hold for the specified transverse magnetic polarization when both the amplitude relations and the dispersion relation are imposed.
Interface matching. Let region 1 be vacuum above the interface, with ϵ1​=1, K=β1​>0; region 2 has ϵ2​=ϵ, K=β2​<0. These signs make the evanescent fields decay as z→±∞. In the absence of free surface current, tangential Hy​ is continuous, giving a common interface amplitude. Tangential Ex​ is also continuous, hence
ωε0​iβ1​Hy​​=ωϵε0​iβ2​Hy​​,ϵβ1​=β2​.​
(41)
Normal Dz​ is continuous as well: both sides have Dz​=−kHy​/ω. This does not require Ez​ itself to be continuous; the material can have polarization surface charge. The normal magnetic field is zero on both sides.
Existence and surface dispersion. The opposite signs of β1​ and β2​ require ϵ<0. The two bulk equations are
k2−β12​=c2ω2​,k2−β22​=c2ω2ϵ​.
(42)
Use β22​=ϵ2β12​ to eliminate the decay constants. For the nontrivial interface branch,
k2=c2ω2​ϵ+1ϵ​,β12​=−ϵ+1ω2/c2​.​
(43)
A positive β12​ together with real propagating k gives ϵ<−1. A negative permittivity between −1 and zero is insufficient. This lossless planar surface-plasmon dispersion describes a surface plasmon polariton; ϵ=1 would instead be identical media and supplies no decaying bound mode.
Frequency range and long wavelengths. With ϵ=1−ωp2​/ω2, the existence condition becomes
0<ω<2​ωp​​.​
(44)
The upper value is a limiting frequency approached as k→∞, not an attained maximum at finite k. Substitution into the dispersion relation gives
k2=c2ω2​ωp2​−2ω2ωp2​−ω2​.
(45)
For ω≪ωp​, choosing propagation in the positive x direction,
k=cω​[1+2ωp2​ω2​+O(ωp4​ω4​)]≃cω​.​
(46)
It approaches the vacuum light line from larger k. The vacuum decay constant is then β1​≃ω2/(cωp​), so confinement becomes weak at low frequency. This calculation assumes the stated real, lossless effective permittivity; dissipation would require complex propagation and decay constants.

Changing basis and acceleration. Attach orthonormal unit vectors ei​(t) to the rotating frame, with a common origin fixed in the inertial frame. An infinitesimal rotation dθ=ωdt changes a vector fixed in those axes by dA=dθ×A: its tip moves tangentially to the circle about the rotation axis. More generally, write A=∑i​Ai​ei​. Differentiating the components and the rotating basis separately proves the rotating-frame derivative formula,
dtdA​​0​=dtdA​​S​+ω×A.​
(47)
Apply the formula first to position: r˙0​=v+ω×r, where v=(dr/dt)S​. Apply it again to both terms. For constant ω,
r¨0​=a+2ω×v+ω×(ω×r).​
(48)
One cross term comes from differentiating v in the inertial frame and another from differentiating r in ω×r. The displayed source formula implicitly requires constant angular velocity and an unaccelerated common origin. For changing rotation rate there is also ω˙×r; an accelerating origin supplies its own translational acceleration. Moving the rotation terms to the force side gives the apparent Coriolis force and centrifugal force with negative signs.
Southward motion on Earth. Use local east, north and up axes, with latitude λ positive northward. Then Ω=Ω(0,cosλ,sinλ) and v=(0,−v,0). The Coriolis acceleration in local Earth coordinates gives
Fcor​=−2mΩ×v=−2mΩvsinλeE​.​
(49)
It is westward in the northern hemisphere, eastward in the southern hemisphere and zero for exactly southward motion at the equator. There is no vertical Coriolis component for this particular velocity.
Correct steering. On the desired meridional track, drag has no eastward component because it is antiparallel to the velocity. If the thrust makes signed angle ϕ east of south, its eastward component is Tsinϕ. The cross-track force balance is therefore
Tsinϕ−2mΩvsinλ=0,
(50)
so
ϕ=sin−1(T2mΩvsinλ​).​
(51)
The argument must have magnitude at most one for this simple thrust-only correction to be feasible. The signed angle changes sign at the equator: correction is east of south in the north and west of south in the south. Thus the phrase “east of south” cannot describe a positive correction throughout the entire flight.
Wrong-sign correction and the miss distance. A literally constant velocity vector cannot develop a cross-track displacement. The physically useful interpretation of the last task is constant speed, with an erroneous lateral steering correction. In the usual leading-track model, thrust and drag balance along the actual motion, and the applied transverse correction has the correct magnitude but the wrong sign. It therefore reinforces rather than cancels the Coriolis force. Starting at the equator, use the unperturbed southward track λ≃−vt/R, and let xE​ be the eastward departure from that track. To first order in the departure,
x¨E​=−4Ωvsinλ=4ΩvsinRvt​,xE​(0)=x˙E​(0)=0.
(52)
There is a factor two relative to uncompensated Coriolis deflection because the wrong correction adds an equal lateral force. Integration gives the wrong-sign Coriolis steering error,
x˙E​=4ΩR(1−cosRvt​),xE​=4ΩR[t−vR​sinRvt​].
(53)
For southern angular distance Λ=16π/180=0.27925, R=6.4×106 m and v=800/3.6=222.2 ms−1, the nominal flight time after the equator is
tf​=vRΛ​≃8.04×103 s=2.23 h.
(54)
Taking Ω≃2π/(86400 s) gives
xE​(tf​)≃v4ΩR2​(Λ−sinΛ)≃1.94×105 m,about 200 km east.​
(55)
The small-latitude approximation sin(vt/R)≃vt/R gives x¨E​≃4Ωv2t/R and xE​≃2Ωv2t3/(3R), or 1.95×105 m, confirming the scale and direction.
This is an estimate under an explicit steering model, not an exact trajectory forced by the wording. For example, retaining spherical transport of the local axes while still linearizing in the cross-track motion gives, with s=−λ, u˙−(v/R)utans=4Ωvsins. Consequently ucoss=2ΩRsin2s and the longitude departure is Δℓ=2ΩR(tanΛ−Λ)/v. The distance along the destination parallel is then RcosΛΔℓ≃1.93×105 m. Corrections from the changed track and a more detailed control law are not fixed by the problem. If “keeps the velocity constant” instead means the full vector, the pilot would need an additional compensating force and there would be no miss; if thrust is kept at a specified geographical bearing despite drift, its along-track component and lateral drag must also be modelled.

Radial energy and effective potential. For a central force with potential energy U(r), angular momentum conservation fixes J=mr2ϕ˙​ and confines motion to a plane. The kinetic energy separates into radial and angular parts, giving

The first-order orbit equation. Write A=GMm. Since f=−dU/dr=−A/r2−Aγ/r3, choose zero potential at infinity to get

r0​≃[GM(2πP​)2]1/3≃5.80×1010 m,
(65)
using P=88(86400) s, M=2.0×1030 kg and G=6.6743×10−11 m3kg−1s−2. A century contains about 36525/88=415.1 orbits. Convert the observed advance to radians per orbit before using it:
Δϖ=415.143​180(3600)π​≃5.02×10−7 rad.
(66)
Hence
γ≃π(5.80×1010)(5.02×10−7)​≃9.3×103 m≃9.3 km.​
(67)
The positive sign strengthens the attraction and gives prograde precession. The fractional perturbation γ/r0​≃1.6×10−7 validates the small-deviation expansion. This is the parameter fitting the proposed phenomenological force law under the specified small-eccentricity approximation, not a derivation of that law as the physical explanation of Mercury's anomaly.

Normal-mode idea. Near a stable equilibrium, expand the potential energy to quadratic order and the kinetic energy as a quadratic form in generalized velocities. The Euler-Lagrange equations then take the linear form Mx¨+Kx=0. A normal mode has fixed displacement ratios, x=qcos(ωt+φ), with Kq=ω2Mq. Real systems admit this description for sufficiently small motion, before higher-order nonlinearities or strong damping matter. Superposition permits a general motion to be assembled from the modes. A free symmetry direction may instead be a zero mode, with constant or linearly changing coordinate.
Lagrangian and bead equations. Take each xi​ as an arc-length displacement positive in the same sense around the fixed circular wire. Spring length changes are x2​−x1​, x3​−x2​ and x1​−x3​. Equal equilibrium spring tensions give no net tangential force; their linear energy terms sum to zero. Dropping an irrelevant constant, the Lagrangian is
L=2m​(x˙12​+x˙22​+x˙32​)−2k​[(x2​−x1​)2+(x3​−x2​)2+(x1​−x3​)2].​
(68)
For example, differentiating the potential with respect to x1​ gives k(2x1​−x2​−x3​). The three Euler-Lagrange equations therefore are
mx¨1​mx¨2​mx¨3​​=−k(2x1​−x2​−x3​),=−k(2x2​−x3​−x1​),=−k(2x3​−x1​−x2​).​​
(69)
The wire supplies normal constraint forces but does no tangential work. These are the equal-mass case of the normal modes of three beads connected by springs on a circular hoop.
Frequencies. The stiffness matrix divided by k is
K/k=​2−1−1​−12−1​−1−12​​=3I−11T,1=(1,1,1)T.
(70)
It annihilates 1. On the two-dimensional subspace q1​+q2​+q3​=0, its action is multiplication by three. Thus the angular frequencies of the normal modes are
ω0​=0,ω1​=ω2​=m3k​​.​
(71)
The repeated frequency is a degeneracy of normal modes, while the zero frequency expresses free rigid rotation of all beads along the wire.
General solution. Two independent vectors spanning the vibrating subspace are qA​=(1,−1,0)T and qB​=(1,0,−1)T. Each amplitude satisfies Q¨​+ω2Q=0, with ω=3k/m​. The uniform coordinate satisfies Q¨​0​=0. Hence
x(t)=qA​Acos(ωt+ϕ)+qB​Bcos(ωt+ψ)+1(Ct+D).​
(72)
The six freely specifiable real constants A,ϕ,B,ψ,C,D give the six initial data; ω is fixed by k,m and is not an additional independent integration constant. The linear zero-mode term must be included to allow nonzero total tangential momentum.
Shapes and uniqueness. In the first term beads 1 and 2 have opposite equal displacements and bead 3 remains at its equilibrium position. In the second term beads 1 and 3 do this while bead 2 stays fixed. The last term moves all three identically, leaving spring extensions unchanged: D is a rigid phase shift and C a uniform drift around the wire. The sketches show positive modal amplitudes; all arrows reverse half a vibration period later.
The two nonzero-frequency eigenvectors are not unique, because any independent linear combinations in their degenerate two-dimensional eigenspace form an equally valid basis. In particular the displayed pair is independent but not orthogonal; one could use (1,−1,0) and (1,1,−2) instead. The zero eigenspace is one-dimensional, so its vector is fixed up to scale and sign.
Specified release. Zero initial velocities permit ϕ=ψ=0 and require C=0. At t=0, the three displacement conditions become A+B+D=a, −A+D=0, −B+D=0. Therefore one convenient set of constants is
A=B=D=3a​,C=0,ϕ=ψ=0,ω=3k/m​.​
(73)
Equivalently,
x1​(t)=3a​[1+2cos(ωt)],x2​(t)=x3​(t)=3a​[1−cos(ωt)].​
(74)
Their sum remains a, as required by zero initial total tangential momentum. Alternative amplitude signs with compensating phase shifts represent the same motion.

Constitutive law and Poisson ratio. Use positive stress for tension. In an isotropic linearly elastic medium, Hooke's law gives
e1​=Eτ1​−σ(τ2​+τ3​)​.​
(75)
The analogous equations follow by cyclic permutation. Under uniaxial tension in direction 1, e1​=τ1​/E and e2​=e3​=−στ1​/E. Thus Poisson's ratio σ=−e2​/e1​ measures transverse contraction relative to longitudinal extension when the transverse stresses are zero. It is dimensionless; it is not the ratio of transverse stress to longitudinal stress.
Radial and hoop strains. Let a material point originally at radius r move to r+R(r), with small axisymmetric displacement. Two neighbouring radii separated by dr move apart by dr+dR, so the radial fractional extension is dR/dr. A circle's circumference changes from 2πr to 2π(r+R), so its fractional extension is R/r. Consequently
er​=R′(r),eϕ​=rR(r)​.​
(76)
The absence of longitudinal stress is a plane stress condition, not a claim of zero longitudinal strain.
Equilibrium of an annular wedge. Consider a slice of unit axial length, radial thickness dr and small angular width dϕ. The difference of the outward and inward radial face forces is (rτr​)′drdϕ to leading order. The two tangential face tensions have inward radial resultant τϕ​drdϕ: each contributes τϕ​drsin(dϕ/2). With no body force, their balance gives the radial equilibrium of a thick elastic cylinder,
τϕ​=drd​(rτr​)=τr​+rτr′​.​
(77)
This hoop contribution is why the radial stress need not be spatially constant in a thick wall.
Displacement equation. With τz​=0, the two Hooke's law equations read
R′=Eτr​−στϕ​​,rR​=Eτϕ​−στr​​.
(78)
Invert them:
τr​=1−σ2E​(R′+σrR​),τϕ​=1−σ2E​(rR​+σR′).
(79)
Substitute in τϕ​=(rτr​)′. After cancelling the common factor, the right-hand side is rR′′+(1+σ)R′, so
rR​+σR′=rR′′+(1+σ)R′,r2R′′+rR′−R=0.​
(80)
The cancellation explains why this displacement equation does not depend on the value of Poisson's ratio.
Power laws and wall pressure. Try R∝rn. The indicial equation is n(n−1)+n−1=n2−1=0, so
R=c1​r+rc2​​.
(81)
The resulting Lamé stress distribution in a pressurized cylindrical wall can be written
τr​=P−r2Q​,τϕ​=P+r2Q​,P=1−σEc1​​,Q=1+σEc2​​.
(82)
The pressure boundary tractions are τr​(a)=−p1​ and τr​(b)=−p0​. Solving them yields
P=b2−a2p1​a2−p0​b2​,Q=b2−a2(p1​−p0​)a2b2​.
(83)
Hence the positive compressive radial pressure in the wall is
p(r)=−τr​(r)=b2−a2p0​b2−p1​a2+(p1​−p0​)a2b2/r2​,a≤r≤b.​
(84)
It equals p1​ at a and p0​ at b. This directional radial pressure is not an isotropic fluid pressure: radial and tangential stresses differ. The associated displacement is R=[(1−σ)Pr+(1+σ)Q/r]/E. Also ez​=−σ(τr​+τϕ​)/E=−2σP/E, generally nonzero even though τz​=0.
Maximum absolute tangential stress. For p1​>p0​, Q>0 and τϕ′​=−2Q/r3<0. Therefore the maximum of its absolute value must be at one of the two surfaces. Keeping the specified external pressure gives the fully general answer
a≤r≤bmax​∣τϕ​(r)∣=b2−a21​max{​p1​(a2+b2)−2p0​b2​,​2p1​a2−p0​(a2+b2)​}.​
(85)
For the usual high internal pressure with negligible external pressure, the largest magnitude is the tensile stress on the inner wall:
τϕ,max​≃τϕ​(a)=b2−a2p1​(a2+b2)​(p0​≃0).​
(86)
For finite p0​, the inner-wall expression is [p1​(a2+b2)−2p0​b2]/(b2−a2), but its absolute value is not always the larger endpoint value. For example, nearly equal internal and external pressures can leave the outer surface more compressive. With p1​=p0​=p, both stresses correctly reduce to −p everywhere. These results use a homogeneous linear elastic wall without axial loading; a closed-ended pipe would require a different longitudinal-stress condition.
