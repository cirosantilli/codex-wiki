Maxwell's displacement current is the electric-field contribution needed to make Ampère's law consistent with charge conservation. In macroscopic form,
∇×H=Jf​+∂t∂D​.​
(15)
The displacement current density Jd​=∂t​D has units Am−2. Since D=ϵ0​E+P, it contains the vacuum field term and, in matter, the polarization-current contribution. It need not represent free charge moving through a material.
Taking the divergence gives 0=∇⋅Jf​+∂t​(∇⋅D). Gauss's law, ∇⋅D=ρf​, then yields the continuity equation ∂t​ρf​+∇⋅Jf​=0. Without the displacement term, Ampère's original law would incorrectly require every current distribution to be divergence-free even while charge accumulates.
A charging parallel-plate capacitor makes this concrete. Ignoring fringing, D=Q/A, so the displacement current across its dielectric gap is Id​=A∂t​D=dQ/dt, equal to the conduction electric current in its wire. The integral law
∮H⋅dℓ=∫S​Jf​⋅dS+dtd​∫S​D⋅dS
(16)
therefore gives the same answer whether the spanning surface cuts the wire or passes through the gap. Together with Faraday's law, this field term also permits self-consistent electromagnetic waves in vacuum, with speed c=1/μ0​ϵ0​​.
