Constitutive law and Poisson ratio. Use positive stress for tension. In an isotropic linearly elastic medium, Hooke's law gives
e1​=Eτ1​−σ(τ2​+τ3​)​.​
(75)
The analogous equations follow by cyclic permutation. Under uniaxial tension in direction 1, e1​=τ1​/E and e2​=e3​=−στ1​/E. Thus Poisson's ratio σ=−e2​/e1​ measures transverse contraction relative to longitudinal extension when the transverse stresses are zero. It is dimensionless; it is not the ratio of transverse stress to longitudinal stress.
Radial and hoop strains. Let a material point originally at radius r move to r+R(r), with small axisymmetric displacement. Two neighbouring radii separated by dr move apart by dr+dR, so the radial fractional extension is dR/dr. A circle's circumference changes from 2πr to 2π(r+R), so its fractional extension is R/r. Consequently
er​=R′(r),eϕ​=rR(r)​.​
(76)
The absence of longitudinal stress is a plane stress condition, not a claim of zero longitudinal strain.
Equilibrium of an annular wedge. Consider a slice of unit axial length, radial thickness dr and small angular width dϕ. The difference of the outward and inward radial face forces is (rτr​)′drdϕ to leading order. The two tangential face tensions have inward radial resultant τϕ​drdϕ: each contributes τϕ​drsin(dϕ/2). With no body force, their balance gives the radial equilibrium of a thick elastic cylinder,
τϕ​=drd​(rτr​)=τr​+rτr′​.​
(77)
This hoop contribution is why the radial stress need not be spatially constant in a thick wall.
Displacement equation. With τz​=0, the two Hooke's law equations read
R′=Eτr​−στϕ​​,rR​=Eτϕ​−στr​​.
(78)
Invert them:
τr​=1−σ2E​(R′+σrR​),τϕ​=1−σ2E​(rR​+σR′).
(79)
Substitute in τϕ​=(rτr​)′. After cancelling the common factor, the right-hand side is rR′′+(1+σ)R′, so
rR​+σR′=rR′′+(1+σ)R′,r2R′′+rR′−R=0.​
(80)
The cancellation explains why this displacement equation does not depend on the value of Poisson's ratio.
Power laws and wall pressure. Try R∝rn. The indicial equation is n(n−1)+n−1=n2−1=0, so
R=c1​r+rc2​​.
(81)
The resulting Lamé stress distribution in a pressurized cylindrical wall can be written
τr​=P−r2Q​,τϕ​=P+r2Q​,P=1−σEc1​​,Q=1+σEc2​​.
(82)
The pressure boundary tractions are τr​(a)=−p1​ and τr​(b)=−p0​. Solving them yields
P=b2−a2p1​a2−p0​b2​,Q=b2−a2(p1​−p0​)a2b2​.
(83)
Hence the positive compressive radial pressure in the wall is
p(r)=−τr​(r)=b2−a2p0​b2−p1​a2+(p1​−p0​)a2b2/r2​,a≤r≤b.​
(84)
It equals p1​ at a and p0​ at b. This directional radial pressure is not an isotropic fluid pressure: radial and tangential stresses differ. The associated displacement is R=[(1−σ)Pr+(1+σ)Q/r]/E. Also ez​=−σ(τr​+τϕ​)/E=−2σP/E, generally nonzero even though τz​=0.
Maximum absolute tangential stress. For p1​>p0​, Q>0 and τϕ′​=−2Q/r3<0. Therefore the maximum of its absolute value must be at one of the two surfaces. Keeping the specified external pressure gives the fully general answer
a≤r≤bmax​∣τϕ​(r)∣=b2−a21​max{​p1​(a2+b2)−2p0​b2​,​2p1​a2−p0​(a2+b2)​}.​
(85)
For the usual high internal pressure with negligible external pressure, the largest magnitude is the tensile stress on the inner wall:
τϕ,max​≃τϕ​(a)=b2−a2p1​(a2+b2)​(p0​≃0).​
(86)
For finite p0​, the inner-wall expression is [p1​(a2+b2)−2p0​b2]/(b2−a2), but its absolute value is not always the larger endpoint value. For example, nearly equal internal and external pressures can leave the outer surface more compressive. With p1​=p0​=p, both stresses correctly reduce to −p everywhere. These results use a homogeneous linear elastic wall without axial loading; a closed-ended pipe would require a different longitudinal-stress condition.
