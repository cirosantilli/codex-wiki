This is the one-mole, or equivalently molar volume, form of the Van der Waals equation, an approximate equation of state for a real gas:
p=V−bRT​−V2a​.
(1)
Here p is the gas pressure, T its absolute temperature, R the molar gas constant, and V the volume occupied by one mole. If V denotes molar volume explicitly, its units are m3mol−1.
The positive parameter b represents excluded volume arising from the finite size and short-range repulsion of the molecules. The volume available for translational motion is reduced from V to V−b. This is an effective excluded volume, not simply the sum of the molecules' geometric volumes. Its molar units are m3mol−1.
The positive parameter a measures intermolecular attraction in a mean-field approximation. Attractions reduce the force exerted on the walls, lowering the measured pressure by a/V2 relative to the repulsion-only expression. Thus p+a/V2 is the pressure corrected for this attraction. The squared inverse-volume dependence reflects a contribution proportional to the square of the molecular number density. The molar units of a are Pam6mol−2.
For an arbitrary amount n measured in moles, with total container volume Vtot​, the corresponding formula is
(p+Vtot2​an2​)(Vtot​−nb)=nRT.​
(2)
At low density, when b≪V and a/V2≪RT/V, it reduces to the ideal gas law. The model captures qualitative attraction, repulsion and liquid-gas behavior, but does not supply an exact equation of state for every material.

The third law of thermodynamics, in its perfect-crystal form, states that the entropy of a pure, perfectly ordered crystal with a unique ground state tends to zero as T→0. Its more general entropy-difference formulation says that the entropy difference between equilibrium states related by a finite change of a regular external control parameter tends to zero at absolute zero:
limT→0​[S(T,X2​)−S(T,X1​)]=0.
(3)
The common limiting entropy is independent of X. Ground-state degeneracy or frozen disorder can produce residual entropy, so zero entropy is not an unconditional assertion about every sample.
In the usual regular thermodynamic setting, absolute zero cannot be attained from positive temperature by a finite sequence of cooling operations with finite resources. An entropy-temperature plot shows why a finite reversible adiabatic step cannot reach the common zero-temperature endpoint from a state with positive entropy above that endpoint: an isentropic process follows a horizontal line, while all the regular entropy curves meet only at T=0.
The figure illustrates this with two possible settings having S−S0​=a1​T and S−S0​=a2​T, where 0<a1​<a2​. First change the setting isothermally from the upper to the lower curve, rejecting heat; then change it reversibly and adiabatically back to the upper curve. Entropy conservation on the latter step gives
a1​Tn​=a2​Tn+1​,Tn+1​=a2​a1​​Tn​,Tn​=T0​(a2​a1​​)n>0
(4)
for every finite n. The staircase approaches the origin but reaches it only after infinitely many such stages. Irreversibility generates entropy and cannot improve on the reversible endpoint.
This is a thermodynamic illustration of unattainability of absolute zero, not a claim that every cooling apparatus has this particular linear entropy law. Its conclusion presumes regular equilibrium states and bounded control parameters; dynamical statements about finite cooling time additionally require physical assumptions about the available interactions and cooling rates.

The Magnus effect is the transverse force produced when a spinning body moves relative to a fluid. Rotation changes the flow and circulation around the body; the resulting asymmetric pressure and wake deflect the fluid, with the opposite transverse force on the body. Examples include the curved flight of spinning balls and the lift of rotating-cylinder sails.
A useful quantitative model is steady two-dimensional incompressible flow past a cylinder of radius a, with fluid velocity U=Ux^ far away. Let circulation Γ be positive anticlockwise as viewed from +z. The potential-flow velocity at the cylinder surface is
vθ​(a,θ)=−2Usinθ+2πaΓ​.
(26)
The Bernoulli equation outside the boundary layer gives
p(θ)=p∞​+2ρ​(U2−vθ2​).
(27)
Integrating the pressure force around the cylinder, per unit span, yields
Fy′​=−a∫02π​p(θ)sinθdθ=−ρUΓ.
(28)
Thus the Kutta–Joukowski theorem gives
F′=ρU×(Γz^),∣F′∣=ρU∣Γ∣.​
(29)
The sign here refers to the fluid velocity relative to the cylinder. For a body moving through otherwise stationary fluid, that relative fluid velocity points opposite to the body's translational velocity.
Real viscosity and the no-slip boundary condition transmit the surface rotation to the fluid and help set the circulation and separation pattern. Pure inviscid flow with only an impermeability condition does not determine Γ from the cylinder's angular speed. Therefore the circulation must not be assigned a universal spin-rate formula without additional assumptions. The cylinder result illustrates the lift mechanism; quantitative forces on a finite spinning ball also depend on its geometry, Reynolds number, rotation rate, surface roughness and wake.

Electromagnetic waves drive mobile charges in a conducting medium. Their current changes both the phase and attenuation of the wave. A description in terms of a real, frequency-independent refractive index is therefore generally insufficient: the material response and the propagation constant are complex and frequency dependent.
Use phasors proportional to ei(kz−ωt), with k=k′+ik′′ and k′′≥0 for a passive medium. In a homogeneous, isotropic, linear material, let ϵb​ represent the permittivity associated with bound-charge electric polarization, μ the magnetic permeability and σ(ω) the mobile-charge conductivity. The macroscopic Maxwell equations give
∇×E=iωB,∇×H=(σ−iωϵb​)E.
(30)
For a transverse plane wave, taking another curl yields
k2=μϵb​ω2+iμσ(ω)ω=μω2ϵeff​(ω),ϵeff​=ϵb​+ωiσ(ω)​.​
(31)
This division of the response avoids counting mobile electrons both in ϵb​ and in σ. Define the complex refractive index n=ck/ω=n+iκ. For a nonmagnetic medium, n2=ϵeff​/ϵ0​. Its real part determines the phase variation, while the positive optical extinction coefficient κ gives amplitude attenuation e−ωκz/c and intensity attenuation e−2ωκz/c. The amplitude penetration length is
δ=k′′1​=ωκc​.
(32)
A simple microscopic explanation comes from the Drude model. Let electrons of charge −e, mass m and number density ne​ move against an effectively stationary positive background. Collisions relax their mean velocity at rate ν:
m(v˙+νv)=−eE,J=−ne​ev.
(33)
Consequently
σ(ω)=m(ν−iω)ne​e2​=ν−iωϵ0​ωp2​​,ωp2​=mϵ0​ne​e2​.​
(34)
With a vacuum-like bound-charge background, this gives
ϵ0​ϵeff​​=1−ω(ω+iν)ωp2​​.
(35)
Its imaginary part is positive for positive ω,ν, consistent with dissipation under the chosen phasor convention. Real materials can require an effective electron mass, a nontrivial bound-charge response, or additional absorption associated with the electronic band structure.
A plasma is a conducting ionized medium with mobile electrons and ions, approximately neutral on large scales because of plasma screening. For the electron-frequency transverse waves considered here, the heavy ions can often be treated as stationary. In a dilute weakly collisional plasma, ν≪ω, so
n2=1−ω2ωp2​​,ω2=ωp2​+c2k2.​
(36)
For ω>ωp​, k is real in this collisionless model and the wave propagates. Its phase velocity is vph​=c/n>c, while its group velocity is vg​=cn<c; their product is c2. A phase crest exceeding c does not imply superluminal information transport. For ω<ωp​, the refractive-index square is negative and
k=icωp2​−ω2​​,δ=ωp2​−ω2​c​.
(37)
The transmitted field is evanescent rather than a traveling bulk wave. A sufficiently thick collisionless plasma reflects such radiation without Ohmic absorption. Collisions make the response complex and permit absorption. This accounts for a plasma cutoff and, qualitatively, the reflection of some radio frequencies by ionized atmospheric layers. The simple model describes transverse waves; plasmas also support longitudinal collective modes.
In a metal, positive ions usually form a crystal lattice. Mobile conduction electrons occupy states described by the electronic band structure and normally form a degenerate Fermi gas. The conduction-electron density is usually much higher than in a dilute gas plasma. Thus its plasma frequency is commonly in the optical or ultraviolet range, rather than the lower radio-frequency values possible in dilute plasmas. Electron scattering from the lattice, defects and other excitations gives a finite relaxation rate. The distinction is not that every plasma is collisionless and every metal collisional: the controlling ratio for either material is ω/ν, together with its carrier density and bound-charge response.
At sufficiently low frequency, ω≪ν, the metal's conductivity is approximately real, σ0​=ne​e2/(mν). In a good conductor the conduction current dominates the displacement current, σ0​≫ωϵb​. The wave equation then gives
k2≃iμσ0​ω,k≃(1+i)2μσ0​ω​​=δ1+i​,δ=μσ0​ω2​​.​
(38)
For example, a tangential electric field varies as
E(z,t)=E0​e−z/δcos(δz​−ωt).
(39)
The current J=σ0​E is therefore concentrated within a few skin depths of the surface: this is the skin effect. As frequency increases within this regime, δ∝ω−1/2. In a wire whose radius greatly exceeds δ, only a thin outer region carries appreciable alternating current, increasing the effective resistance. In a conducting shield, the same penetration suppresses the field transmitted through a sufficiently thick wall.
The Poynting theorem identifies the loss as Joule heating. The cycle-averaged heating density for a complex conductivity is
⟨J⋅E⟩=21​Reσ∣E∣2.
(40)
The wave impedance is Z=μω/k. For incidence from vacuum at normal incidence, the electric reflection amplitude is r=(Z−Z0​)/(Z+Z0​), where Z0​=μ0​/ϵ0​​. A good conductor has small ∣Z∣ and therefore strong reflection, while the part of the incident power entering it is dissipated over the skin depth.
At high enough frequency that ω≫ν, a metal's free-electron response also approaches the collisionless plasma form. The low-frequency formula δ∝ω−1/2 must not simply be extrapolated into that regime. Real optical behavior additionally depends on the electronic band structure and transitions between bands. Both plasmas and metals are therefore described by the same electromagnetic response framework, but their relevant densities, scattering rates and frequency regimes differ.

The relation describes the slope of the phase coexistence curve of a pure substance in the pressure-temperature plane:
dTdp​=TΔVL​.​
(41)
Here p(T) is the common equilibrium pressure of the two phases, T is their common absolute temperature in kelvins, and the derivative is taken along their coexistence curve. Choose a direction of transformation from phase 1 to phase 2. Then L is the reversible latent heat for that transformation and ΔV=V2​−V1​ is the corresponding volume change, using the same amount of substance for both quantities.
In the molar convention used below, L=hm,2​−hm,1​ has units Jmol−1 and ΔV=vm,2​−vm,1​ has units m3mol−1, so the right-hand side has units PaK−1. For vaporization, both L and ΔV are positive. The displayed general relation is also called the Clapeyron equation; the ideal-vapor approximation used in (c) gives its usual Clausius form.

At coexistence, the two phases of a pure substance have equal chemical potential, or equivalently equal molar Gibbs free energy:
μ1​(T,p)=μ2​(T,p).
(42)
For either phase, dμ=−sm​dT+vm​dp. Move an infinitesimal distance along the coexistence curve, where the equality must remain true. Subtracting the two differentials gives
0=−(sm,2​−sm,1​)dT+(vm,2​−vm,1​)dp,dTdp​=Δvm​Δsm​​.

dTdp​=Δvm​Δsm​​=TΔvm​L​.​
(44)
The derivation assumes thermodynamic equilibrium between two bulk phases of a single pure substance, with the same temperature and pressure, and only ordinary pressure-volume work. Surface-curvature shifts, additional external-field work and composition changes are omitted. It applies to a first-order phase boundary where the latent heat and volume discontinuity are defined. No ideal-gas assumption and no constant-L assumption are needed for this differential relation. At the critical endpoint both discontinuities vanish, so their ratio requires a limiting treatment rather than direct substitution of two zeros.

The expression is the canonical partition function for a system in thermal equilibrium with a large heat bath at fixed temperature T. The system exchanges energy with the bath, while its particle number and external mechanical parameters, such as volume, are fixed. The bath is large enough that its temperature is not appreciably changed by this exchange.
The system has allowed energy levels ϵj​. The index j labels distinct energy values, and the integer degeneracy factor gj​ counts distinct quantum states with that same energy. kB​ is the Boltzmann constant, and exp[−ϵj​/(kB​T)] is a state's Boltzmann factor. The dimensionless sum Z normalizes the probabilities:
P(one state at ϵj​)=Ze−ϵj​/(kB​T)​,P(level j)=Zgj​e−ϵj​/(kB​T)​.​
(53)
Thus the degeneracy multiplies the probability of occupying the level, not the probability assigned to each individual state within it.
The canonical model is not restricted to a classical ideal gas. It applies to a quantum rotor, for example, provided the sum counts all of that system's allowed states with their proper degeneracies. For a many-particle system, ϵj​ would be the energy of a many-particle state, with any quantum-statistical restrictions already built into the state count.

The internal-energy identity from (b), followed by differentiation with respect to temperature at fixed moment of inertia, yields

(65)
For one mole of independent rotors, the rotational energy is RT and the rotational molar heat capacity is R≃8.31Jmol−1K−1.
This agrees with the equipartition theorem. The given spectrum describes a linear rotor with two independent rotational degrees of freedom, each contributing kB​T/2 to the high-temperature mean energy. Rotation about the molecular axis is not an additional degree of freedom of this ideal linear-rotor model, so the result is kB​ rather than 3kB​/2 per rotor. These are rotational contributions alone; translational and vibrational heat capacities have not been included. At low temperature the discrete excitation gap freezes rotational excitation out, so the rotational heat capacity tends to zero instead of remaining at its classical value.
