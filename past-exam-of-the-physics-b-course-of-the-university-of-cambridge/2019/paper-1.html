The original PDF places this complete question before B6 without a visible question number. It is included here under a descriptive heading.
Why images work. In the method of images, place fictitious image charges outside the region where the physical electric field is required. Choose them so that the combined electric potential satisfies the conductor boundary conditions. The Uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem then proves that this construction gives the physical exterior field. The images do not describe the actual surface-charge locations.
Two examples are a charge q above a grounded conducting plane, represented by −q at the mirror point, and the image charge for a grounded conducting sphere used below. In each case the image lies inside the excluded conductor and makes its surface an equipotential of the required value.
Potential of the two charges. By the law of cosines, the distances from the observation point to charges at xj​ are sj​=(r2+xj2​−2rxj​cosθ)1/2. The superposition principle and Coulomb's law therefore give
ϕ(r,θ)=4πε0​q​[r2+x22​−2rx2​cosθ​1​−r2+x12​−2rx1​cosθ​α​].​
(14)
The grounded sphere. Set r=a and require s1​=αs2​ at every polar angle. Matching the coefficients of cosθ and the constant terms gives
x1​=α2x2​,a2+x12​=α2(a2+x22​).
(15)
The useful noncoincident solution is
x1​=x2​a2​,α=x2​a​,q′=−x2​aq​.​
(16)
Indeed s1​=(a/x2​)s2​ on the sphere, so the two potential terms cancel there. A Gaussian surface enclosing the sphere but not the external charge measures the total image charge in the exterior representation. Thus the physical induced net charge is
Qsphere​=−x2​aq​.​
(17)
An isolated neutral sphere. The grounded image would give nonzero net charge. Add a central image q0​=+aq/x2​ to cancel it. A central image changes the surface potential by a constant, preserving the equipotential condition, whereas an arbitrary off-centre image would generally spoil it. The image charges for a neutral conducting sphere give
q0​=x2​aq​,ϕsphere​=4πε0​aq0​​=4πε0​x2​q​.​
(18)
The potential is measured relative to zero at infinity for each finite x2​.
Fixed applied field and dipole moment. For positive q on the positive x axis, the applied central field is E0​=−qx/(4πε0​x22​). The central image contributes no electric dipole moment, so
p=q′x1​x=−x22​qa3​x=4πε0​a3E0​.​
(19)
Its units are coulomb metres. Keeping E0​ fixed makes q/x22​ constant, so this induced moment is independent of the distance.
The distant-charge limit. As x2​→∞ with q/x22​ fixed, the applied field becomes uniform over any fixed neighbourhood of the sphere. The neutral pair of opposite images approaches a point dipole of the finite moment above. The external potential contains a growing spatial constant q/(4πε0​x2​), so the infinity-referenced conductor potential does not have a finite limit. Subtracting this irrelevant constant gives the usual induced charge on a conducting sphere in a uniform electric field; the exterior potential becomes
ϕ=−E0​⋅r+r3a3E0​⋅r​,r≥a.​
(20)
This has zero potential on the sphere, but a uniform applied field cannot simultaneously have its potential vanish at spatial infinity.

Induction and its sign. The Maxwell-Faraday equation is ∇×E=−∂t​B. For a fixed loop, Stokes theorem gives
E=∮E⋅dℓ=−dtd​∫B⋅dS=−dtdΦ​.​
(21)

The inductive current. Let the loop normal make angle θ with the applied field, with positive current defined by that normal. The external magnetic flux is Φext​=AB0​sinωtcosθ. With negligible electrical resistance, the circuit equation is LI˙=−Φ˙ext​, so total linked flux LI+Φext​ is constant. For zero initial trapped flux,
I=−LAB0​​cosθsinωt.​
(22)
The printed result needs this initial-flux assumption; in general an additional constant current is allowed. The units are amperes because a weber divided by a henry is an ampere.
Current and forces at one instant. Take B>0 along +x and 0<θ<π/2, so I<0 relative to the chosen normal. The loop magnetic dipole moment μ=IAn therefore has a component opposite the applied field. Each wire element experiences Lorentz force dF=Idℓ×B. Opposite sides have equal and opposite forces, giving zero net force in the uniform field but a nonzero couple.
The horizontal top and bottom wire segments also experience equal and opposite vertical forces. They cancel without adding torque about the vertical rotation axis; the displayed force pair supplies that torque.
Average torque. Since τ=μ×B, its generalized component for increasing θ is τθ​=−IABsinθ. Substitution gives
τθ​=LA2B02​​sinθcosθsin2ωt,
(23)
and, using ⟨sin2ωt⟩=1/2,
⟨τθ​⟩=2LA2B02​​sinθcosθ=4LA2B02​​sin2θ.​
(24)
The orientation torque on a flux-conserving loop drives its normal away from the field for the stated initial angle. Its magnitude has units joules, or newton metres.
Free motion with large inertia. Write J for the mechanical moment of inertia. If its orientation changes little during each field cycle, the slow equation is Jθ¨=⟨τθ​⟩, with effective potential
Uav​=4LA2B02​​cos2θ.
(25)
The stable orientation is θ=π/2, where the loop plane contains the applied field. Released from rest between 0 and π/2, the loop accelerates toward that orientation, overshoots and librates around it. In the leading averaged, undamped model its turning angles are θi​ and π−θi​. It does not settle without a dissipative mechanism. For small librations the squared angular frequency is A2B02​/(2LJ).
Negligible inductance and finite resistance. Now the current is proportional to the induced electromotive force, not to the flux. Including slow rotation,
I=−R1​dtd​(ABcosθ)=−RA​B˙cosθ+RA​Bsinθθ˙.
(26)
The rotational damping of a resistive loop in an oscillating field follows from
τθ​=RA2​BB˙sinθcosθ−RA2​B2sin2θθ˙.
(27)
At fixed orientation, the first term alternates at 2ω and has zero mean. The loop can therefore rock slightly, but there is no leading mean aligning torque like that in the inductive case. Slow rotation gives
⟨τθ​⟩=−2RA2B02​​sin2θθ˙,​
(28)
which opposes motion and produces Joule heating. Initial-phase transients can initiate motion, and the instantaneous torque is not zero. This description retains the large-inertia, slow-motion approximation; it does not assert an exact stationary solution at every starting angle.

C=dε0​πa2​,I=RV​=−CV˙.

Two interface effects. For n2​<n1​, Snell's law gives sinθt​=(n1​/n2​)sinθi​. If sinθi​>n2​/n1​, no propagating transmitted ray exists: there is total internal reflection, with an evanescent wave on the far side. An optical fibre uses this to guide light.

The reflected p-polarized component, whose electric field lies in the incidence plane, vanishes; the reflected s-polarized component generally remains. Reflection polarizers and polarized glare reduction exploit this selectivity.

Modes, stability and symmetry. A normal mode of the linearized system has a fixed displacement pattern multiplied by one temporal oscillation. For n independent generalized coordinates, a conservative small-disturbance system has
Mq¨​+Kq=0,Ka=ω2Ma.
(61)
When the mass matrix M is positive definite and the stiffness K is a symmetric matrix, the generalized eigenvalue problem for small oscillations has n independent mode directions, counting degeneracy. Positive ω2 gives stable oscillation, negative ω2 an exponentially growing and decaying pair, and zero ω2 a neutral direction whose nonlinear stability needs further analysis. An arbitrary linear motion is a superposition of these modes.
If a symmetry preserves both kinetic and potential energies, it maps modes to modes of the same frequency. Mode patterns can be classified by that symmetry, for example reflection-even and reflection-odd patterns. Degenerate eigenspaces admit different bases, so a mode shape need not be uniquely selected inside such a subspace.
Two pendulums free to swing in any direction. Let xj​ be the small horizontal displacement along the line joining the supports and yj​ the perpendicular horizontal displacement. Put ω02​=g/L and κ=k/m. To quadratic order,
T=2m​∑j=12​(x˙j2​+y˙​j2​),V=2mω02​​∑j=12​(xj2​+yj2​)+2k​(x2​−x1​)2.
(62)
The spring is unstretched at equilibrium. Its first-order length change is only x2​−x1​; a transverse difference changes its length at second order and produces no quadratic transverse restoring energy. Consequently the four modes are
​(x1​,x2​)∝(1,1),y1​=y2​=0:(x1​,x2​)∝(1,−1),y1​=y2​=0:(y1​,y2​)∝(1,1) or (1,−1),x1​=x2​=0:​​ω=ω0​,ω=ω02​+2κ​,ω=ω0​.​​
(63)
This is the transverse degeneracy of unstretched spring-coupled pendulums: ω0​ has a three-dimensional eigenspace. Independent transverse motions form an equally valid basis for two of those directions. A pre-tensioned spring would change the transverse result.
The open three-pendulum chain. Restrict motion to the vertical plane containing the supports, with horizontal coordinates x1​,x2​,x3​. There are springs between neighbours and no wall springs. The equations are
x¨+​ω02​1+κ​1−10​−12−1​0−11​​​x=0.
(64)
The spring matrix has eigenvalues 0,1,3, with respective eigenvectors (1,1,1), (1,0,−1) and (1,−2,1). The normal modes of three freely ended coupled pendulums are therefore
(1,1,1):(1,0,−1):(1,−2,1):​ω2=ω02​,ω2=ω02​+κ,ω2=ω02​+3κ.​​
(65)
The lowest mode stretches neither spring. In the middle mode the outer masses oppose each other and the central mass is stationary. The highest mode has the central mass moving oppositely with twice the outer amplitude. Reflection about the middle support makes the first and last patterns even and the middle pattern odd.
An even ring. For a large ring, use tangential displacement xj​ and periodic indexing xj+N​=xj​. The locally straight nearest-neighbour equations are
x¨j​=−ω02​xj​−κ(2xj​−xj−1​−xj+1​).
(66)
Cyclic translation symmetry selects Fourier modes xj​∝ei(qj−ωt), with q=2πs/N. Hence
ωs2​=ω02​+4κsin2Nπs​,s=0,…,N−1.
(67)
For even N, the lowest pattern has equal tangential displacements, while the highest alternates signs around the ring. Thus
ωmin​=Lg​​,ωmax​≃Lg​+m4k​​(N large).​
(68)
These are the even and odd pendulum-ring modes. For exact finite regular-polygon geometry with straight chord springs, the first-order spring extension includes a factor cos(π/N), so its squared contribution is multiplied by cos2(π/N). That correction tends to one in the requested large-N limit.
An odd ring. Perfect alternation cannot close after an odd number of steps. The two highest wave indices are instead s=(N−1)/2 and (N+1)/2. They give a degenerate pair, represented by real sine and cosine standing patterns, with
ωmax2​≃ω02​+4κcos22Nπ​.​
(69)
The maximum is slightly below the even-ring limiting value, and the sign pattern must contain a gradual phase mismatch rather than exact alternation. The uniform lowest mode is unchanged, and there are still N independent tangential mode directions.

C12 OurBigBook.com
Flow visualization and the idealization. A streamline is everywhere tangent to the instantaneous velocity field at one fixed time. A streakline is the locus, at one observation time, of particles that previously passed through a specified injection point. A marked particle's own trajectory is a pathline. These coincide geometrically in steady flow, but need not do so in an unsteady draining flow.
The intended ideal liquid is approximately incompressible and inviscid: viscosity and associated shear stresses are negligible on the scales and times being considered, the Mach number is small, and the density is effectively constant. This does not require zero vorticity; the initially rotating state is already a counterexample. It also does not justify ignoring viscosity in an arbitrarily thin boundary layer or at a real drain.
Spiralling flow and angular momentum. In an axisymmetric inviscid flow, pressure and gravity exert no torque about the vertical axis. A parcel therefore conserves axial specific angular momentum,
DtD​(ruϕ​)=0,r2ω=r02​ω0​.
(70)
The late, shallow remnant in the intended draining picture is supplied by parcels initially near the outer rim, so r0​≃R. The slow-drain angular-momentum amplification then gives
ω(r)≃ω0​r2R2​,uϕ​(r)≃rω0​R2​.​
(71)
The assumption about the parcels' origin is necessary: torque conservation by itself does not assign r0​=R to every parcel at every time.
A continuous dye feed near the outer free surface produces inward spirals that wind increasingly rapidly near the drain. The figure uses a late quasisteady model, ur​=−D/r and uϕ​=C/r with positive constants D,C, giving ϕ=ϕ0​−(C/D)ln(r/r0​). Its streaklines and streamlines approximately coincide over an observation interval short compared with the overall draining time. Strictly, individually marked particles at the initial surface trace pathlines, not a continuously injected streakline.
The free-surface depression. Let z=H(r) be the surface. Slow radial draining makes radial inertial corrections small compared with the azimuthal centripetal acceleration. The radial Euler equation and vertical hydrostatic equilibrium give
ρ1​∂r∂p​=ruϕ2​​,p(r,z)=patm​+ρg[H(r)−z].
(72)
Thus gH′(r)=uϕ2​/r=C2/r3, with C=ω0​R2. Integrating from r to the rim gives
h=H(R)−H(r)=2gC2​(r21​−R21​).
(73)
For r≪R this becomes
h≃2gr2ω02​R4​.​
(74)
The surface rises outward because the inward pressure gradient force must sustain the circular motion. The dimensions are metres, and the 1/r2 profile is not valid inside the finite drain or vortex core.
The terrestrial estimate. Use the vertical component of Earth's rotation as the ideal initial angular speed, ω0​≃ΩE​sinλ. At Cambridge, taking λ≃52.2∘ and ΩE​=7.292×10−5 s−1, this gives ω0​≃5.76×10−5 s−1. Hence, for R=1 m, r=0.01 m and g=9.81 ms−2,
h≃1.7×10−6 m=1.7 μm.​
(75)
Taking the whole terrestrial angular speed rather than its local vertical projection would give about 2.7 μm. The Cambridge estimate uses the projection appropriate to the vertical vortex. This very small head assumes terrestrial rotation supplies the only initial swirl; imposed residual rotation would change it quadratically.
Curl of the azimuthal velocity. For axisymmetric uϕ​=rω(r) with no vertical dependence, the rotational part of the vorticity is vertical:
ζ=∇×u=r1​drd(ruϕ​)​z=(2ω+rdrdω​)z.​
(76)
The ideal quasisteady radial sink has no curl outside its core. The source's symbol Ω denotes this vertical vorticity component, not the fluid's angular velocity. In general its magnitude is the absolute value of the bracket.
The two limiting states. For initial uniform rotation, ω=ω0​, whereas the late approximation has ω=C/r2. Therefore
Ωinitial​=2ω0​,Ωlate​≃0(r outside the drain core).​
(77)
The late flow can have large angular velocity and nonzero circulation while being locally irrotational. The drain core is excluded from the domain of the 1/r velocity field.
What is actually conserved. Inviscid does not mean that each parcel's vorticity magnitude is fixed in three dimensions. For an incompressible barotropic liquid with conservative body forces, the vorticity equation is
DtDζ​=(ζ⋅∇)u.
(78)
Vertical compression of a material column changes its vorticity through vortex stretching, even while its volume is conserved by horizontal expansion. In the columnar shallow-layer limit, vorticity dilution in a shallow draining layer gives
DtD​(Hζ​)=0.​
(79)
Thus a remnant much shallower than its original column can have much smaller vertical vorticity. At finite depth there is generally a residual value; the exactly irrotational late profile is a leading limiting approximation, not an exact parcelwise conversion of nonzero vorticity to zero in finite time.
Kelvin's circulation theorem conserves circulation around a material loop, not around a fixed spatial circle. A loop whose original radius is near R has circulation about 2πω0​R2, consistent with the late uϕ​=C/r field. Fluid also leaves through the drain, so the remaining tank contents are not a fixed material system. If the calculation is expressed explicitly in the Earth-fixed rotating frame, the corresponding shallow-water potential vorticity is (ζ+f)/H, with f=2ΩE​sinλ; the conserved quantity involves absolute vorticity.
