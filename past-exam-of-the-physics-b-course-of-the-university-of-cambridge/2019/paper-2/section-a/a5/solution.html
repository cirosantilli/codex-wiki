Past exam of the physics B course of the University of Cambridge / 2019 / Paper 2 / Section A / A5 / Solution

For an ideal electrical transformer, let n=Np​/Ns​. The reflected secondary load is Rin​=n2RL​. Matching a 4Ω load to an 8Ω source therefore requires
n=48​​=2​.​
(15)
With the generator replaced by its internal resistance, its resistance referred to the secondary is
Rout​=(Np​Ns​​)2(8Ω)=4Ω.​
(16)
This is the source output resistance relevant to matching and maximum power transfer.
To find the windings, use the inductance of a toroidal winding. The mean magnetic path is ℓ=2π(0.10)m, the core area is A=30×10−4=0.003m2 and μ=500μ0​. Thus
Lp​=ℓμ0​μr​Np2​A​,Np​=μ0​(500)(0.003)(0.75)2π(0.10)​​=500.
(17)
The ideal secondary turn count is Ns​=500/2​=353.55, giving the practical nominal design
Np​=500,Ns​≃354.​
(18)
With integer turns the irrational ratio cannot be exact: 500:354 reflects the 4Ω load as 7.98Ω, an approximately 0.25% resistance mismatch. These turn counts use the stated 0.75H self-inductance; no operating frequency was specified from which to set a separate minimum inductance.
The 200Ω condition must refer to the unloaded magnetizing reactance, not to the matched loaded input resistance. With Lp​=0.75H,
∣Xp​∣=2πfLp​≥200Ω⟹f≥42.4Hz.
(19)
The secondary self-inductance is Ls​=Lp​/n2=0.375H, so its corresponding unloaded coil impedance satisfies
Zs,coil​=iωLs​,∣Zs,coil​∣≥100Ω.​
(20)
If output impedance means this transformed coil reactance, 100Ω is its minimum magnitude. It is distinct from the 4Ω transformed source resistance. This is the magnetizing reactance and reflected resistance in a transformer distinction. A finite magnetizing inductance adds a parallel branch, Zin​=(8Ω)∥(iωLp​), so exact purely resistive matching is an ideal large-reactance approximation; interpreting the loaded input itself as both 8Ω and at least 200Ω would be impossible.
