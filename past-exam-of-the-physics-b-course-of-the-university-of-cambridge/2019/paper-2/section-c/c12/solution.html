Configuration count. Choose the end-to-end direction as positive and let NR​,NL​ count rightward and leftward links. Then
NR​+NL​=N,a(NR​−NL​)=L,NR​=2N+L/a​,NL​=2N−L/a​.
(82)
A configuration is determined by choosing the positions of its leftward links, so
Ω(L)=NR​!NL​!N!​.​
(83)
The counts must be nonnegative integers; in particular, allowed signed extensions occur in steps of 2a. If distance means ∣L∣ with neither direction selected, there are two corresponding sets for L>0, adding a factor of two. That constant does not affect the tension. There is no excluded-volume penalty in this one-dimensional idealized count.
Entropy from the multiplicity. Put x=L/(Na), so ∣x∣<1. Applying the leading Stirling's approximation to all three large factorials gives
S=kB​lnΩ≃kB​[NlnN−NR​lnNR​−NL​lnNL​]
(84)
S(x)≃NkB​ln2−2NkB​​[(1+x)ln(1+x)+(1−x)ln(1−x)].​
(85)
Terms subleading in N, including logarithmic factorial prefactors, have been omitted consistently. Expanding near zero extension gives
S(L)≃NkB​ln2−2Na2kB​L2​+O(NkB​x4).
(86)
Stretching reduces the number of available configurations and hence the entropy.
Tension and the range of Hooke's law. The model assigns no extension-dependent internal energy. At fixed temperature, the externally applied tension is F=(∂F/∂L)T​=−T(∂S/∂L)T​. Differentiating the full leading entropy gives
F=2akB​T​ln1−x1+x​=akB​T​artanhx.​
(87)
It is the external force needed to oppose the chain's restoring entropic force. Only when ∣L∣≪Na can one put artanhx≃x, yielding
F≃Na2kB​T​L,∣L∣≪Na.​
(88)
The paper's displayed linear law therefore requires this small-extension approximation, which is not explicitly stated in its premise L<L0​. It is not the exact force law up to full extension. The entropic spring constant grows with temperature because stronger thermal disorder makes a given reduction in configurational entropy cost more free energy. This is the one-dimensional entropic chain force-extension relation.
A sparse number of backward links. Write q=NL​≪N, so NR​=N−q and L=a(N−2q). Exactly,
Ω=q!N(N−1)⋯(N−q+1)​=q!Nq​∏j=0q−1​(1−Nj​).
(89)
For fixed q and large N, each numerator factor approaches N, giving
Ω≃NL​!NNL​​.​
(90)
More precisely, the logarithm of the product is −q(q−1)/(2N)+O(q3/N2). A small relative error in the multiplicity requires q2/N≪1; merely q/N≪1 need not make the omitted product close to one. The weaker sparse-link condition does suffice for the leading entropy density and leading high-extension tension. These are sparse backward links near full chain extension.
Tension in that limit. In the overlap regime 1≪q≪N, use Stirling also for q!:
S≃kB​[qlnN−qlnq+q],dLdq​=−2a1​.
(91)
Thus
F≃2akB​T​lnNL​N​.​
(92)
The full leading entropy would give (kB​T/(2a))ln[(N−NL​)/NL​], which has the same dominant logarithm when NL​/N→0.
Approaching full extension. The continuum force law diverges logarithmically as NL​→0, or L→Na. One must not literally apply Stirling to a zero factorial: at the discrete endpoint there is exactly one directed configuration, Ω=1, and its entropy is zero. A continuous fixed-extension derivative is not defined at that endpoint.
An independent force-ensemble calculation makes the limiting physical statement precise. For applied tension F, a link has partition function z=2cosh(βFa), giving the exact mean extension
⟨L⟩=Natanh(βFa).​
(93)
It approaches Na only as F→∞ at positive temperature, and inversion reproduces the logarithmic force law for mean extension. In a real chain, bond extension or failure eventually replaces the ideal inextensible-link model.
