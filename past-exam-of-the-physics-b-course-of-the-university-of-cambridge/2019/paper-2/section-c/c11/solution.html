Two equivalent second-law statements. The Clausius statement of the second law forbids a cyclic device whose sole net effect is transferring heat from a colder reservoir to a hotter one. A refrigerator can make that transfer when supplied with work, so the sole-effect qualification matters. The Kelvin-Planck statement of the second law forbids a cyclic device whose sole net effect is drawing heat from a single reservoir and converting all of it into work. A heat engine can produce work when it also rejects heat to a colder reservoir.
The two statements are equivalent by combining a supposed violating device with an ordinary reversible engine or refrigerator: one can cancel one reservoir exchange and obtain a violation of the other statement. Microscopic fluctuations. The second law is a macroscopic statistical law. A finite fluctuation can transfer a small heat amount Q from cold to hot, with reservoir entropy change Q/Th​−Q/Tc​<0. Such an individual event does not imply that a cyclic engine can extract systematic useful work from equilibrium. Entropy-decreasing trajectories become exceedingly improbable when the entropy involved is large compared with kB​. Under suitable microscopic reversibility conditions, fluctuation relations quantify the relative probabilities of positive and negative entropy production and give nonnegative mean total entropy production. Thus a deterministic no-fluctuation reading has a limit at small scales or short times; the statistical second law remains applicable.

This is the indoor-temperature limit of a Carnot heat pump. It assumes the ideal pump retains its stated input-power capability over the operating range. Real heat exchangers require finite temperature differences and incur irreversibility, so the reversible result is an ideal upper performance bound.
Conventional heater. At this limiting outdoor temperature, the required delivered heating power is simply the house's heat loss:
Q˙​gas​=λΔTmax​=3.83kW.​
(81)
This is heat delivered to the house. The fuel-input power would be 3.83/ηkW for heater efficiency η. At the earlier outdoor temperature of 273K, the required delivered heat would instead be 0.1(293−273)=2.00kW; the final question's reference temperature must be kept clear.
If 0.5kW is read literally as a maximum delivered heat rate rather than a work-input rating, the alternative answers are Q˙​h,max​=0.5kW, Tc,min​=293−0.5/0.1=288K, and a conventional heater delivering 0.5kW at that limit. Such a pump could not balance the stated house loss at 273K. Both readings follow consistently once the ambiguous power rating is defined; the Carnot calculation above uses the work-input reading.
