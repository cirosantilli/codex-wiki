Three phase coexistence curves meet at the triple point, where ice, liquid water and water vapour have equal chemical potential. In the ordinary-pressure region, the low-temperature phase is ice, the high-pressure moderate-temperature phase is liquid, and the low-pressure high-temperature phase is vapour. The triple point is approximately
Tt​=273.16K,pt​=611.7Pa.​
(6)

The liquid–vapour curve terminates at the liquid-gas critical point, approximately 647.1K and 22.06MPa, beyond which there is no liquid–vapour phase boundary.

dTdp​=ΔvΔs​.

A dielectric responds to an electric field mainly by displacing or orienting bound charges, rather than by sustaining a steady conduction current. The polarization density P is electric dipole moment per unit volume. It creates bound charge
ρb​=−∇⋅P,σb​=P⋅n.
(24)
Separating these charges from free charge motivates
D=ϵ0​E+P,∇⋅D=ρf​.
(25)
For an isotropic linear dielectric, P=ϵ0​χe​E, so
D=ϵE,ϵ=ϵ0​(1+χe​)=ϵ0​ϵr​.​
(26)
The electric susceptibility χe​ describes material polarization and is not the magnetic susceptibility. More generally it is a tensor and can depend on frequency, field strength and temperature.
Electronic polarization displaces an electron cloud relative to its nucleus; ionic polarization displaces positive and negative ions; orientational polarization partially aligns permanent molecular dipoles against thermal disorder. For independent permanent moments of magnitude p, weak-field orientational response gives χe​≃np2/(3ϵ0​kB​T) when local-field corrections and interactions are negligible. This explains a common inverse-temperature contribution without asserting that every dielectric follows that law.
At a static interface, n⋅(D2​−D1​)=σf​ and the tangential electric field is continuous. Bound surface charge is already included through P. For a homogeneous filled capacitor, C=ϵA/d, and a reversible linear dielectric stores energy density u=E⋅D/2. At fixed voltage, insertion raises the stored field energy but the voltage source also supplies energy; force arguments must include that source. At fixed charge the capacitor energy instead decreases on insertion.
At frequencies too high for a polarization mechanism to follow the field, its contribution weakens. A complex permittivity represents dissipative dielectric loss as well as phase lag; the sign of its imaginary part depends on the time convention. Strong fields can cause nonlinear response or dielectric breakdown, beyond the reversible linear model.

Magnetization and field response. A magnetic material contains microscopic magnetic moments associated with electron spin and orbital motion. The magnetization M is magnetic dipole moment per unit volume. In SI the macroscopic fields satisfy
B=μ0​(H+M).
(33)
Here H separates free-current excitation from the material response. A uniform isotropic linear material has M=χm​H, giving
B=μH,μr​=1+χm​.​
(34)
This constant scalar magnetic permeability model is useful only within its linear range. Crystals can have a susceptibility tensor; strongly magnetic materials depend on field and magnetic history. For a finite specimen the internal H also includes its demagnetizing field. In a uniformly magnetized ellipsoid, Hint​=Happlied​−Nd​M along a principal axis, so sample shape affects the apparent susceptibility.
Diamagnetic and paramagnetic response. Diamagnetism is a weak negative susceptibility caused by induced orbital response opposing the applied field. It occurs in all matter but can be masked by larger positive contributions. Closed-shell materials such as bismuth or many organic compounds show it clearly. Ordinary diamagnetism is often only weakly temperature dependent; superconducting flux exclusion is a separate collective phenomenon and is not described by an arbitrarily small negative susceptibility.
Paramagnetism arises when available magnetic moments partially align with a field. Thermal disorder prevents complete alignment at weak fields. For independent localized moments of magnitude m, the classical weak-field result is
M≃3kB​Tnm2B​,χm​≃3kB​Tμ0​nm2​,
(35)
where the second expression uses B≃μ0​H in a weakly magnetic sample. Thus localized moments obey a Curie law in this regime. Quantum angular momentum changes the coefficient to the appropriate J(J+1) expression and gives saturation at strong field. Conduction electrons instead exhibit Pauli paramagnetism: only states near the Fermi surface can change spin populations, so a degenerate gas has a nearly temperature-independent response. Its orbital diamagnetism must also be included for a full metallic susceptibility.
Collective magnetic order. Exchange interactions can favor parallel alignment, producing ferromagnetism below the Curie temperature. Iron, cobalt and nickel are familiar examples. A material may contain regions of spontaneous magnetization, or magnetic domains, while having little net external moment because the domains point in different directions. Applied fields move domain walls and rotate moments; at large fields the magnetization approaches saturation. Pinning and energy barriers make this process hysteretic. Remanence is the magnetization left after removing the driving field, and coercivity measures the reverse field needed to bring the chosen response back to zero.
The area of a closed B–H loop is the energy dissipated per unit volume per quasistatic cycle, ∮HdB, with positive dissipated magnitude. Consequently the magnetic permeability is neither one universal constant nor enough information to describe a ferromagnet: differential magnetic permeability dB/dH, saturation and hysteresis all matter. Above the ordering temperature the spontaneous moment disappears, and a Curie–Weiss law is often an appropriate approximate paramagnetic description away from the critical region.
In antiferromagnetism, ordered opposing sublattices have equal moments and cancel macroscopically in the ideal compensated case. The order disappears above the Néel temperature. In ferrimagnetism, opposing sublattices have unequal moments and leave a net magnetization. Ferrites combine ferrimagnetic response with relatively high electrical resistivity, making them valuable when eddy-current losses would be substantial in a metallic core.
Electromagnets and engineering choice. A coil supplies free-current magnetomotive force NI. For a simple uniform magnetic path, Ampère's law gives Hℓ≃NI and flux Φ≃μANI/ℓ. A high-magnetic permeability soft magnetic core concentrates flux and reduces the current required for a given field. With an air gap g,
NI≃μ0​μr​Bℓc​​+μ0​Bg​.
(36)
Even a short gap can dominate the magnetic reluctance. The approximate attractive force across one uniform gap is F≃B2A/(2μ0​), obtained from the field energy, with fringing neglected.
Soft magnetic materials have small coercivity and narrow hysteresis loops; they suit relays, electrical transformer cores and controllable electromagnets. Hard magnetic materials retain their magnetization and suit permanent magnets. Laminating metallic cores breaks large eddy-current loops; ferrites are useful at higher frequencies because of their resistivity. Increasing current cannot increase B indefinitely through the linear formula: core saturation, coil heating and leakage limit performance. A good design selects material for the field amplitude, switching frequency and acceptable loss, rather than simply choosing the largest quoted magnetic permeability.

A spatially uniform external gravitational acceleration moves a body's centre of mass without stretching it. Tidal forces arise from the variation of that acceleration across the body. Let a distant mass M lie at distance D along unit vector n, and let ∣r∣≪D denote position relative to the body's centre. Subtract the centre's acceleration. To first order,
atidal​=D3GM​[3(n⋅r)n−r].​
(49)
The tidal tensor is Tij​=GM(3ni​nj​−δij​)/D3. It stretches along the line to the source with eigenvalue 2GM/D3 and compresses in the two perpendicular directions with eigenvalues −GM/D3. Its trace vanishes outside the source, consistently with Laplace's equation for the gravitational potential.
After removal of the constant and uniform-acceleration terms, the tidal potential is
Φtidal​=−2D3GM​[3(n⋅r)2−r2].
(50)
An ideal equilibrium ocean on a sphere of radius R follows an equipotential of the body's gravity plus this perturbation. For surface gravity g, the leading displacement is
δh(ψ)=−gΦtidal​(R,ψ)​=2gD3GMR2​(3cos2ψ−1),
(51)
up to a constant fixing total water volume. The quadrupolar form produces bulges on both the near and far sides; the far-side bulge follows from differential acceleration, without postulating a repulsive gravitational force.
The Moon and Sun both raise terrestrial tides, with relative strength set mainly by M/D3, rather than by the total gravitational pull M/D2. Their contributions reinforce near new and full Moon to produce spring tides, and partly cancel near quadrature to produce neap tides. Earth's rotation relative to the tidal pattern gives a strong semidiurnal component in the simplest geometry. Real tide heights and timing depend on ocean depth, coastlines, basin resonances, Coriolis acceleration, friction and the declination of the forcing bodies. Tidal dissipation produces phase lags and torques, exchanging rotational and orbital angular momentum and gradually altering the spin and orbit. The equilibrium bulge is therefore a useful starting model, not an exact local tide forecast.

Pressure and its physical terms. Solving the molar Van der Waals equation for pressure gives

For one mole, V is the sample volume with the molar amount already fixed; b has units of that volume and a of pressure times volume squared.

Coexistence isotherm. Below the Van der Waals critical point, the formal homogeneous isotherm has a local minimum and maximum. Its middle branch has ∂P/∂V>0, hence negative isothermal compressibility, and is mechanically unstable. Portions of the negative-slope branches can be metastable rather than globally stable. The equilibrium isotherm replaces the loop between liquid volume Vl​ and vapour volume Vg​ by a constant saturation pressure Ps​. The two volume are fixed by

The spinodals are the turning points of the formal isotherm; they are distinct from the coexistence endpoints.

Two equivalent second-law statements. The Clausius statement of the second law forbids a cyclic device whose sole net effect is transferring heat from a colder reservoir to a hotter one. A refrigerator can make that transfer when supplied with work, so the sole-effect qualification matters. The Kelvin-Planck statement of the second law forbids a cyclic device whose sole net effect is drawing heat from a single reservoir and converting all of it into work. A heat engine can produce work when it also rejects heat to a colder reservoir.
The two statements are equivalent by combining a supposed violating device with an ordinary reversible engine or refrigerator: one can cancel one reservoir exchange and obtain a violation of the other statement. The following original schematic shows the forbidden sole effects, with arrows indicating energy flow.
Microscopic fluctuations. The second law is a macroscopic statistical law. A finite fluctuation can transfer a small heat amount Q from cold to hot, with reservoir entropy change Q/Th​−Q/Tc​<0. Such an individual event does not imply that a cyclic engine can extract systematic useful work from equilibrium. Entropy-decreasing trajectories become exceedingly improbable when the entropy involved is large compared with kB​. Under suitable microscopic reversibility conditions, fluctuation relations quantify the relative probabilities of positive and negative entropy production and give nonnegative mean total entropy production. Thus a deterministic no-fluctuation reading has a limit at small scales or short times; the statistical second law remains applicable.

Temperature as an equilibrium relation. Classical thermodynamics begins with the zeroth law of thermodynamics: thermal equilibrium is transitive. A thermometer can therefore assign a common empirical temperature to systems in mutual thermal equilibrium. Different thermometer properties initially give different monotone empirical scales; a resistance reading by itself does not define the absolute scale.
The Second law of thermodynamics provides a substance-independent thermodynamic scale. A reversible engine operating between reservoirs has a heat ratio depending only on their temperatures. One chooses absolute temperature so that Qh​/Qc​=Th​/Tc​, with Qh​,Qc​ the positive heat magnitudes. Its reversible efficiency is 1−Tc​/Th​. Equivalently, reversible heat defines entropy through dS=δQrev​/T. For an equilibrium simple compressible system,
dU=TdS−pdV+μdN,T1​=(∂U∂S​)V,N​.​
(94)
Classical thermodynamics uses this relation without needing a microscopic account of entropy. A dilute-gas thermometer realizes an empirical scale approaching the thermodynamic scale after corrections for nonideal behavior.
Statistical meaning of the same derivative. In the microcanonical description, an equilibrium energy shell containing Ω(U,V,N) accessible states has S=kB​lnΩ. Its statistical temperature is consequently
T1​=kB​(∂U∂lnΩ​)V,N​.​
(95)
To see why this is an equilibrium temperature, let two weakly coupled systems exchange a small energy while their total energy stays fixed. The combined multiplicity is ΩA​(UA​)ΩB​(Utot​−UA​). Maximizing its logarithm gives
∂UA​∂lnΩA​​=∂UB​∂lnΩB​​,
(96)
which is equality of the two inverse temperatures. Stability requires the total entropy maximum, not only stationarity. The overwhelmingly dominant macrostate in a large system therefore realizes the thermodynamic equilibrium condition.
A large reservoir gives a small subsystem the canonical probabilities pi​∝e−βEi​, with β=1/(kB​T). In that ensemble, F=−kB​TlnZ and S=−kB​∑i​pi​lnpi​ agree with macroscopic thermodynamic relations in their usual equilibrium regime. The equipartition identity for a classical dilute monatomic gas, ⟨p2/(2m)⟩=3kB​T/2, is a useful operational connection between microscopic motion and temperature, but is not a universal definition: quantum modes need not obey equipartition, and a system away from equilibrium need not have one temperature at all.
For finite systems one must specify the entropy convention and energy resolution; surface and fluctuation corrections can distinguish definitions that agree in the ordinary thermodynamic limit. Bounded-energy population-inverted systems can also have a negative entropy derivative and hence a negative statistical temperature. That possibility lies outside an ordinary positive-temperature ideal-gas thermometer; it does not mean temperature is merely an arbitrary average kinetic energy.
Obtaining the energy–temperature conversion. The Boltzmann constant connects entropy measured in JK−1 with the dimensionless logarithm of a state count. Historically it could be determined from the ideal gas law pV=NkB​T: measure the molar gas constant R and independently determine the Avogadro constant NA​, then obtain kB​=R/NA​. The independent particle-number determination matters; using R alone would not fix energy per particle.
An electrical method uses Johnson-Nyquist noise. A resistor of resistance Re​ at known temperature has, in the classical range hf≪kB​T, a one-sided open-circuit voltage-noise spectrum SV​(f)=4kB​TRe​. Over a calibrated flat bandwidth Δf,
kB​=4TRe​Δf⟨v2⟩​.​
(97)
A practical measurement uses an equilibrium resistor, a calibrated low-noise amplifier and a known transfer function, subtracts or independently characterizes amplifier noise, and replaces a nominal bandwidth by the actual effective noise bandwidth. Varying temperature and fitting the mean-square voltage is more informative than treating one amplified voltage sample as the thermal variance. Other primary methods relate temperature to sound speed in a dilute gas or to calibrated particle diffusion and mobility.
There is a historically relevant unit-definition qualification: by the date of this exam, the revised SI had already taken effect on 20 May 2019. The kelvin is now defined by fixing
kB​=1.380649×10−23JK−1exactly.​
(98)
The same experiments now realize or compare thermodynamic temperatures using this fixed constant, whereas historically they measured the conversion constant on an independently defined temperature scale.
