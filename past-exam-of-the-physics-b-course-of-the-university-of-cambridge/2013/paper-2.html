Find the potential from E=−∇ϕ and ∇⋅(ϵ∇ϕ)=−ρf​. One must specify boundary data: a potential on each Dirichlet boundary, a normal displacement on each Neumann boundary, or an appropriate mixture. Conductors are equipotentials; at dielectric interfaces ϕ is continuous and the normal displacement jumps by the specified free surface charge. With no singular dipole sheet, tangential electric field is continuous.
To establish uniqueness of Poisson equation, subtract two solutions with the same charge and boundary data, writing their difference as u. Integration by parts gives
∫V​ϵ∣∇u∣2dV=∫∂V​uϵ∂n​udA,

Magnetization M is magnetic dipole moment per unit volume. In SI units,
B=μ0​(H+M),M=χH,μ=μ0​(1+χ)
(15)
in a linear isotropic response. The internal field matters: for a uniformly magnetized ellipsoid it is Hint​=Happlied​−Nd​M. Sample shape therefore affects an apparent magnetic susceptibility.
Diamagnetism opposes the applied field, giving small negative magnetic susceptibility from the induced orbital response. It is often only weakly temperature dependent. Paramagnetism aligns pre-existing moments and gives positive magnetic susceptibility; independent localized moments obey approximately χ=C/T in weak fields before saturating. Pauli paramagnetism of conduction electrons is a different, weakly temperature-dependent mechanism.
Ferromagnetism involves collective exchange-driven alignment and spontaneous magnetization below the Curie temperature. A specimen can nevertheless have nearly zero total moment because magnetic domains point in different directions. Field-driven domain-wall motion and rotation change the net magnetization. Antiferromagnets have compensating ordered sublattices below a Néel temperature; ferrimagnets have oppositely ordered but unequal sublattice moments and retain a net moment. Ferrites are useful examples of electrically resistive ferrimagnetic core materials.
Ferromagnetic response is nonlinear and history dependent. A hysteresis loop displays remanent magnetization, magnetic coercivity and saturation. A single constant magnetic permeability cannot describe the entire loop: differential magnetic permeability describes its local slope. The quasistatic energy loss per unit volume and cycle is the loop area ∮HdB. Soft magnetic materials have low magnetic coercivity and usually high useful magnetic permeability; hard magnetic materials retain magnetization and suit permanent magnets.
An electromagnet normally uses a soft, high-permeability core to guide flux generated by a coil. For a uniform-section core with length ℓc​ and air gap g, neglecting leakage and fringing,
NI≃Hc​ℓc​+μ0​Bg​,B≃g+ℓc​/μr​μ0​NI​.​
(16)
An air gap can dominate the magnetic reluctance even when the core magnetic permeability is large. Core saturation limits the improvement with current, while winding resistance causes heating. For alternating operation, laminations suppress eddy-current loops in metallic cores; high-resistivity ferrites also reduce those losses. Low hysteresis loss is important in repeatedly cycled cores, whereas high magnetic coercivity is valuable in a permanent magnet. Useful design therefore depends on the full magnetic response, electrical losses and operating frequency, rather than the largest quoted magnetic permeability alone.

Viscosity transfers momentum between adjacent fluid layers. A Newtonian fluid has shear stress proportional to strain rate: for simple shear, τxy​=η∂ux​/∂y​. Thus parallel plates of area A, separation d and relative speed U require force magnitude ηAU/d in a steady linear profile.
For an incompressible isotropic Newtonian fluid,
σij​=−pδij​+η(∂i​uj​+∂j​ui​),ρDtDu​=−∇p+η∇2u+ρg
(18)
for constant viscosity. With strain-rate tensor eij​, the viscous dissipation rate per unit volume is 2ηeij​eij​≥0. Kinematic viscosity ν=η/ρ is the momentum diffusivity: a disturbance spreads over distance of order νt​.
The Reynolds number ρUL/η compares inertial and viscous forces. Small Reynolds number supports a creeping-flow approximation, while large values permit thin viscous boundary layers and possible turbulence. It does not mean molecular viscosity has disappeared. Gas viscosity results from molecular momentum transport; a liquid's microscopic transport depends strongly on intermolecular interactions. Non-Newtonian materials require a more general constitutive law.

For torque-free rotation, angular momentum is fixed in inertial space, but angular velocity need not point along it. In principal body axes,
I1​ω˙1​+(I3​−I2​)ω2​ω3​=0
(23)
and cyclic permutations. A symmetric top with transverse moment A and axial moment C has constant ω3​, while ω1​+iω2​ rotates in body coordinates at signed frequency (C−A)ω3​/A. The symmetry axis precesses around the fixed angular momentum in space at rate L/A. These body-frame and space-frame rates describe different motions and need not coincide.

Its normal at the angular-velocity point is parallel to L, and L⋅ω=2T places that point in a fixed plane perpendicular to L. A thermodynamic potential packages the equilibrium state in variables suited to the imposed constraints. For a simple fluid, U(S,V,N) has differential dU=TdS−pdV+μdN. Legendre transforms give H=U+pV, F=U−TS and G=U+pV−TS, with natural variables of a thermodynamic potential (S,p,N), (T,V,N) and (T,p,N) respectively. The graph below covers a full annual cycle. At z/δ=π/2, the amplitude is 20e−π/2=4.16 K and the minimum lags January by a quarter-year. At z/δ=π, the amplitude is only 0.864 K and the minimum lags by half a year: the ground is slightly warmer than its annual mean in January.

The supplied model therefore supports a finite seasonal thermal optimum, while not fixing a practical burial depth in metres.

Phase equilibrium minimizes Gibbs free energy at specified temperature, pressure and composition. Coexisting phases have equal temperature, pressure and chemical potential. For a pure substance, a first-order transition has continuous equilibrium Gibbs free energy but a jump in its first derivatives, such as entropy or volume; the latent heat is TΔS. Continuous transitions lack that latent heat and can instead have singular or discontinuous higher responses.
The van der Waals molar equation
p=v−bRT​−v2a​
(40)
combines an excluded-volume correction with attractive interactions. Its inflection conditions give
vc​=3b,Tc​=27Rb8a​,pc​=27b2a​.​
(41)
Below Tc​, the oscillatory isotherm contains a mechanically unstable segment with (∂p/∂v)T​>0. Equilibrium instead replaces the loop by a constant-pressure coexistence line. Equality of chemical potentials gives the Maxwell construction,
∫vℓ​vg​​[p(v,T)−psat​]dv=0.
(42)
On isothermal compression the gas reaches saturation, then converts progressively into liquid at fixed pressure. The mean molar volume obeys v=(1−x)vℓ​+xvg​, where x is the gas fraction. Metastable superheated or supercooled states can persist when nucleation is delayed; they are not the global equilibrium selected by this construction.
A pressure–temperature phase diagram contains coexistence curves separating solid, liquid and gas. Three phases meet at a triple point; the liquid–gas coexistence curve ends at a thermodynamic critical point, above which liquid and gas can be connected without crossing a first-order phase boundary. A generic solid–liquid line continues beyond the liquid–gas critical temperature. For one component the Gibbs phase rule is F=3−P, so a three-phase equilibrium fixes both temperature and pressure.
Along a two-phase coexistence curve, dμ=−sdT+vdp for each phase gives
dTdp​=ΔvΔs​=TΔvL​.​
(43)
This is the Clapeyron equation, valid with consistent molar or specific quantities. For vaporization, neglecting the liquid volume and treating the vapour as ideal gives Δv≃RT/p, hence dlnp/dT=L/(RT2). With approximately constant molar latent heat,
lnp1​p2​​=−RL​(T2​1​−T1​1​).​
(44)
This Clausius–Clapeyron approximation explains the rapid rise of vapour pressure with temperature. It fails close to the thermodynamic critical point, where phase volumes converge, and is not a universal expression for melting. The sign of a melting-line slope depends on its volume change; water's denser liquid gives a familiar negative slope.
