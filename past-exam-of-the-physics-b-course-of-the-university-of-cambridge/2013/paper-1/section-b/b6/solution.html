The image charge construction replaces conductor-induced charge by fictitious sources outside the physical domain. Match the conductor potentials and the far-field condition; uniqueness then fixes the potential in the physical region. For a grounded plane, reflect a charge and reverse its sign, giving attraction q2/(16πϵ0​h2). For a grounded sphere of radius a, a charge at distance R>a has image −qa/R at radial distance a2/R. These are examples with prescribed zero potential, not arbitrary isolated conductors.[ref]
Use horizontal x and upward y in the upper right-angle wedge. The sheets meet electrically, so take their common potential as zero and assume no externally imposed field. For a real charge at (0,y), the image charges in a right-angle conducting wedge are −q at (y,0) and (−y,0), and +q at (0,−y). The first two contributions have cancelling horizontal components. The electrostatic softening above a conducting wedge reduces the restoring force; the small-charge vertical equilibrium remains stable.
For the lateral stability of a spring-supported charge in a wedge, the off-axis images of (x,y) are −q at (y,x) and (−y,−x) and +q at (−x,−y). Expansion near the bisector gives
Fx​=Dx​x+O(x3),Dx​=ye3​Ke​q2​(2​+41​)>0.
(10)
So the electrostatic force is laterally destabilizing: moving toward either sheet strengthens its attraction. In the simplified model where the spring supplies only a vertical force, the overall equilibrium is a saddle and is unstable.
A three-dimensional spring suspension also has geometrical transverse stiffness. If its charged-equilibrium tension is T and length is ℓ, a small sideways displacement gives spring force −(T/ℓ)x, with T=mg+β/ye2​. The full stability conditions in this suspension model are
k>ye3​2β​,ℓT​>Dx​.​
(11)
Motion parallel to the intersection line has no electrostatic stiffness in the ideal infinite-wedge geometry and is stabilized by the positive spring tension. Thus lateral electrostatic destabilization alone does not prove instability of a real hanging spring. At fixed finite support geometry, a sufficiently small charge preserves stability because Dx​=O(q2) while T/ℓ→mg/ℓ>0. The given vertical-displacement approximation does not specify the attachment length or by itself establish the transverse inequality for an arbitrary charge. The general criterion above states what can be concluded; the saddle verdict applies to the distinct idealization with zero lateral support stiffness.
