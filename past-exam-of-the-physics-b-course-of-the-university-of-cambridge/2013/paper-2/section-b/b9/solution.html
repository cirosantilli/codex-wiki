For torque-free rotation, angular momentum is fixed in inertial space, but angular velocity need not point along it. In principal body axes,
I1​ω˙1​+(I3​−I2​)ω2​ω3​=0
(23)
and cyclic permutations. A symmetric top with transverse moment A and axial moment C has constant ω3​, while ω1​+iω2​ rotates in body coordinates at signed frequency (C−A)ω3​/A. The symmetry axis precesses around the fixed angular momentum in space at rate L/A. These body-frame and space-frame rates describe different motions and need not coincide.
Poinsot construction expresses the same torque-free motion geometrically. The energy ellipsoid ∑Ii​ωi2​=2T is fixed in the body. Its normal at the angular-velocity point is parallel to L, and L⋅ω=2T places that point in a fixed plane perpendicular to L. The ellipsoid rolls on this invariable plane without slipping: the instantaneous velocity of its contact point is proportional to ω×ω=0. The contact curve is the polhode on the body ellipsoid and the herpolhode on the plane.[ref]

The extreme-moment axes are stable; the intermediate one is unstable. The major-axis relaxation theorem adds weak internal dissipation while retaining negligible external torque. At fixed L,
T=∑i​2Ii​Li2​​≥2I3​L2​.​

Energy loss favours rotation about the axis of largest principal moment. Here “major axis” means maximum inertia, not necessarily the body's longest geometric direction. A perfectly rigid torque-free body does not dissipatively settle; its energy is conserved. Distinguishing stability from relaxation also explains why minimum-inertia rotation can be stable in the ideal rigid model.

It exhibits slow and fast precession branches when real solutions exist. A generic release excites nutation as well: inclination oscillates instead of staying fixed. Axis constraints and bearing forces must be included when specifying which angular momentum and pivot are being used.
