The image charge construction replaces conductor-induced charge by fictitious sources outside the physical domain. Match the conductor potentials and the far-field condition; uniqueness then fixes the potential in the physical region. For a grounded plane, reflect a charge and reverse its sign, giving attraction q2/(16πϵ0​h2). For a grounded sphere of radius a, a charge at distance R>a has image −qa/R at radial distance a2/R. These are examples with prescribed zero potential, not arbitrary isolated conductors.[ref]
Use horizontal x and upward y in the upper right-angle wedge. The sheets meet electrically, so take their common potential as zero and assume no externally imposed field. For a real charge at (0,y), the image charges in a right-angle conducting wedge are −q at (y,0) and (−y,0), and +q at (0,−y). The first two contributions have cancelling horizontal components. With Ke​=1/(4πϵ0​),
Fy​=−2​y2Ke​q2​+4y2Ke​q2​=−y2β​,β=16πϵ0​q2(22​−1)​.​
(8)
Thus the force magnitude at the original height is β/y02​, directed down toward the intersection. The sphere is treated as a point charge, with size much smaller than its distances from the sheets.
Since gravity is already balanced in the uncharged equilibrium, the charged vertical equilibrium satisfies k(ye​−y0​)+β/ye2​=0. For β/(ky03​)≪1, ye​≃y0​−β/(ky02​). Differentiating the force gives
ωy2​=m1​(k−ye3​2β​),ω0​ωy​−ω0​​≃−ky03​β​,ω0​=k/m​.​
(9)
The electrostatic softening above a conducting wedge reduces the restoring force; the small-charge vertical equilibrium remains stable.
For the lateral stability of a spring-supported charge in a wedge, the off-axis images of (x,y) are −q at (y,x) and (−y,−x) and +q at (−x,−y). Expansion near the bisector gives
Fx​=Dx​x+O(x3),Dx​=ye3​Ke​q2​(2​+41​)>0.
(10)
So the electrostatic force is laterally destabilizing: moving toward either sheet strengthens its attraction. In the simplified model where the spring supplies only a vertical force, the overall equilibrium is a saddle and is unstable.
A three-dimensional spring suspension also has geometrical transverse stiffness. If its charged-equilibrium tension is T and length is ℓ, a small sideways displacement gives spring force −(T/ℓ)x, with T=mg+β/ye2​. The full stability conditions in this suspension model are
k>ye3​2β​,ℓT​>Dx​.​
(11)
Motion parallel to the intersection line has no electrostatic stiffness in the ideal infinite-wedge geometry and is stabilized by the positive spring tension. Thus lateral electrostatic destabilization alone does not prove instability of a real hanging spring. At fixed finite support geometry, a sufficiently small charge preserves stability because Dx​=O(q2) while T/ℓ→mg/ℓ>0. The given vertical-displacement approximation does not specify the attachment length or by itself establish the transverse inequality for an arbitrary charge. The general criterion above states what can be concluded; the saddle verdict applies to the distinct idealization with zero lateral support stiffness.

Opposite surface currents give magnetic field B=μ0​I/a between the strips by Ampère's law; the magnetic flux per unit length is Bd, hence L=Bd/I=μ0​d/a. Therefore

twhole​=1−rV2​e2iδ(1−rV2​)eiδ​,Twhole​=[1+4α2(α2−1)2​sin2δ]−1.​

For a moving loop, Faraday's law of induction is E=∮(E+u×B)⋅dl=−dΦB​/dt, where u is the local wire velocity, ΦB​=∫B⋅dS, and the oriented boundary and surface follow the right-hand rule. For a fixed loop the motional term vanishes. Lenz's law is the minus sign: the induced response opposes the change of linked flux, not necessarily the applied field itself.
Let s increase downhill and choose positive current I to produce an uphill magnetic force. The normal component of the imposed field is Bcosθ, so the changing external flux is Blcosθs. With initially zero current and no resistance, conservation of flux linkage gives LI=Blcosθs. The rod feels gravity vertically downward, a rail reaction normal to the slope, and magnetic force of magnitude IlB horizontally uphill. Its downhill component is −IlBcosθ; its normal component presses the rod into the rails, giving N=mgcosθ+IlBsinθ.
The inductive oscillation of a sliding rod on inclined rails follows from
ms¨=mgsinθ−LB2l2cos2θ​s,κ=Ll2cos2θ​.​
(17)
The magnetic force is not itself parallel to the rails. Defining ωB​=Blcosθ/mL​ and se​=mgsinθ/(mωB2​), release from rest gives
s(t)=se​(1−cosωB​t),sfirst stop​=2se​=B2l2cos2θ2mgLsinθ​.​
(18)
The first stop occurs at t=π/ωB​; the rod then returns and oscillates between 0 and 2se​. At se​ the force is zero but the speed is maximal. Mechanical potential energy is reversibly exchanged with kinetic energy and LI2/2; a perfectly conducting circuit provides no damping.
For the resistive long-time drift of an inductive sliding rod, with loop resistance R>0, use
LI˙+RI=Blcosθs˙,ms¨=mgsinθ−BlcosθI.
(19)
Joule heating damps the transient oscillation. On sufficiently long rails the asymptotic motion is steady sliding, not permanent rest:
I∞​=Blcosθmgsinθ​,v∞​=B2l2cos2θRmgsinθ​.​
(20)
The zero-resistance model has persistent oscillations; its late-time limit cannot be interchanged with taking R→0. These results assume Bcosθ=0, smooth contacts, negligible rod self-inductance and constant geometry.

Converting the density gives n=1018m−3, so ωp​=5.64×1010s−1 and fp​=8.98GHz. At 10GHz,
ϵr​≃0.19384,np​≃0.4403.​

In a convention treating it explicitly as a surface charge, the normal vacuum-displacement jump equals that charge instead.
For the downward transmitted wave, transversality gives kx​Ex​+kz​Ez​=0. The measured field therefore has kx​/(−kz​)=3, so tanθt​=3 and sinθt​=3/10​. Tangential phase matching gives

Hence Ei​≃1.2464Vm−1 and Er​≃−0.1458Vm−1. Since these are peak phasor amplitudes,

This is incident power per area perpendicular to its propagation. Per area of the interface the incoming normal flux is ⟨Si​⟩cosθi​≃1.87×10−3Wm−2. The reflected sign is a phase reversal, not negative reflected energy.
At 1GHz<fp​, the relative permittivity is negative and there is no propagating transverse bulk wave. The field decays evanescently into the plasma and the ideal collisionless half-space reflects all incoming normal power. Collisions would allow absorption; a complex refractive index here does not by itself imply dissipative heating.

A fictitious force is a term required when writing Newton's second law in an accelerating frame; it comes from the coordinate choice rather than a new interaction. For axes rotating at constant Ω about a fixed inertial origin,
(dtdA​)I​=(dtdA​)R​+Ω×A.
(26)
Apply this identity twice to position to get aI​=aR​+2Ω×vR​+Ω×(Ω×r). Moving the last two terms to the right of maI​=F gives the stated Coriolis and centrifugal forces. An accelerating origin or changing rotation rate would add further terms.
A slowly rotating fluid planet follows an equipotential of gravitational plus centrifugal potential. The centrifugal potential is −Ω2r2cos2λ/2, most negative at the equator. An outward equatorial shift raises gravitational potential and restores the balance, producing an oblate figure. Self-gravity also changes with the shape; no flattening coefficient follows from rotation alone without a density model.
Using local north and radial-up unit vectors, the centrifugal acceleration on a stationary mass is
acf​=Ω2R(cos2λeU​−sinλcosλeN​).
(27)
It points away from the rotation axis: upward and equatorward, at angle λ to radial up in the northern hemisphere. Its vertical fraction of gravitational force is
mgFcf,U​​=gΩ2R​cos2λ≃3.45×10−3cos2λ,​
(28)
using a 24h day for this approximate number. The resultant effective weight tilts equatorward relative to radial down. A plumb-defined local vertical already follows effective gravity; the component calculation uses the spherical radial vertical. A supported stationary mass of course has zero net acceleration.
For the Coriolis deflection of vertical launch and drop, retain only first order in Ω, use effective constant g, and neglect horizontal velocities in the Coriolis term as instructed. With east positive, vertical velocity gives x¨=−2Ωcosλz˙ and no northward acceleration. For launch, z=ut−gt2/2 and the flight time is 2u/g. Integrating with zero initial horizontal velocity gives
x˙=−2Ωcosλz,xX​=−3g24Ωu3cosλ​,yX​=0.​
(29)
The landing is westward. The westward velocity accumulated on ascent is only recovered on descent, so the two displacement contributions do not cancel.
For release from rest at h0​=u2/(2g), z=h0​−gt2/2, tfall​=u/g, and x¨=2Ωgtcosλ. Thus
xX′​=3g2Ωu3cosλ​=−41​xX​,yX′​=0.​
(30)
The drop lands eastward with one quarter the launch-return displacement magnitude. North–south corrections from horizontal Coriolis coupling are beyond the retained approximation.

A normal mode is a pattern in which all coordinates share one frequency and fixed relative amplitudes. For a conservative linear system, it solves a generalized eigenvalue equation with stiffness matrix K and mass matrix M. For this free three-mass spring chain,
K=k​1−10​−12−1​0−11​​,M=mdiag(1,α,1).
(36)
Solving Ku=ω2Mu, for α>0, gives
ω20k/m(k/m)(1+2/α)​u(1,1,1)(1,0,−1)(1,−2/α,1)​​.​
(37)
Their overall normalization is arbitrary; distinct modes are orthogonal in the mass inner product. The zero mode is rigid translation because neither end is fixed. In the antisymmetric mode the middle mass stays still, so changing its mass cannot alter the frequency. The symmetric internal mode keeps the centre of mass fixed through its middle displacement −2/α. Its frequency rises without bound as α→0 and approaches k/m​ as α→∞, becoming degenerate with the antisymmetric frequency in that limit.
For equal masses put ω1​=k/m​ and ω2​=3k/m​. Positions are equilibrium positions plus displacements
q(t)=(A0​+B0​t)u0​+(A1​cosω1​t+B1​sinω1​t)u1​+(A2​cosω2​t+B2​sinω2​t)u2​.
(38)
The translational mode needs the linear-in-time term; a cosine at zero frequency alone would miss arbitrary centre-of-mass velocity.
Taking the springs initially at equilibrium, the impulse gives q˙​(0)=(v,0,0)=(v/3)u0​+(v/2)u1​+(v/6)u2​. Their squared Euclidean norms are 3,2,6, so their initial kinetic energies are mv2/6,mv2/4,mv2/12. Relative to total mv2/2,

(39)
Subsequent motion conserves each mode's total energy, exchanging its kinetic and spring contributions. The zero-mode share remains centre-of-mass kinetic energy.

Locally, zero curl permits v=∇Φ; for a global single-valued potential the circulation around every noncontractible loop must also vanish. incompressibility gives ∇⋅v=∇2Φ=0. The exterior of a cylinder is multiply connected, so local irrotationality alone does not exclude a circulating solution.
For inviscid constant-density flow under conservative gravity, Euler's equation is ∂t​v+(v⋅∇)v=−∇(p/ρ+ϕg​). The identity (v⋅∇)v=∇(v2/2)−v×(∇×v) gives, for irrotational flow,
∂t​Φ+ρp​+2v2​+ϕg​=C(t).
(40)
For steady flow, absorb the potential's time-only gauge term and obtain p+ρv2/2+ρϕg​=constant throughout the connected fluid region. Without irrotationality the steady constant need only be common along each streamline.
Use U for the uniform upstream speed and take the flow along +x. The boundary conditions are vr​(a,θ)=0 and v→Uex​ at infinity. A perfect inviscid model imposes no penetration, not no slip. For the intended no-circulation solution, require a globally single-valued potential, so the coefficient of θ is zero. The logarithmic term would be a net source and violates no penetration, so its coefficient is also zero. Growing terms with n≥2 violate the uniform far field. For each remaining n≥2, the surface normal condition then forces its decaying coefficient to vanish. The n=1 terms satisfy C1​=U and D1​=Ua2:
Φ=U(r+a2/r)cosθ,vr​=U(1−a2/r2)cosθ,vθ​=−U(1+a2/r2)sinθ.​
(41)
An irrelevant additive potential constant is understood. At the same elevation, Bernoulli balance with the upstream stream gives
p(a,θ)=p∞​+21​ρU2(1−4sin2θ).​
(42)
The force per unit height is
F′=−a∫02π​p(a,θ)(cosθ,sinθ)dθ=0.​
(43)
pressure symmetry gives zero drag and zero lift in this zero-circulation idealization. D'Alembert's paradox is the failure of this inviscid model to represent real separated viscous wakes and their drag, not a claim that a real pole feels no force.
The zero-circulation assumption matters: Φ→Φ+Γθ/(2π) leaves the stated velocity boundary conditions intact but adds Γ/(2πa) to the surface tangential speed. It produces Fy′​=−ρUΓ for positive counterclockwise circulation. Thus the boundary conditions alone do not justify excluding the angular term; an initially noncirculating inviscid start, or an explicitly single-valued potential, supplies the missing condition.[ref]
