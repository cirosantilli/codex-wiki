A Carnot cycle consists of two reversible isothermal branches joined by two reversible adiabatic branches. Let the hot isothermal expansion run from volume Va​ to Vb​, and the cold isothermal compression from Vc​ to Vd​. For n moles of an ideal gas, their absorbed and rejected heat magnitudes are
Qh​=nRT1​ln(Vb​/Va​),Qc​=nRT2​ln(Vc​/Vd​).
(47)
On a reversible ideal-gas adiabat, TVγ−1 is constant. Applying this to the two joining branches gives Vb​/Va​=Vc​/Vd​. Therefore
Qh​Qc​​=T1​T2​​,ηC​=Qh​Qh​−Qc​​=1−T1​T2​​.​
(48)
The result is independent of the gas's heat-capacity ratio. This derivation presumes the usual constant-heat-capacity ideal-gas model; more generally the equal entropy changes on the two isotherms give the same heat ratio without that restriction.
To establish the upper bound, suppose an engine were more efficient between these same two reservoirs. Use its output work to drive a reversed Carnot cycle, scaled so that the work magnitudes match. For fixed work W, the hypothetical engine would take W/η from the hot bath, whereas the reversed Carnot cycle would return W/ηC​>W/η to it. The combined device would transfer heat from the cold bath to the hot bath without any net external work. That contradicts the Clausius statement of the second law. Hence no such two-reservoir engine can exceed 1−T2​/T1​.
For the triangular cycle, call the three successive vertices A,B,C, and measure energies in units of p0​V0​. For one mole of a monatomic ideal gas, U=3pV/2, and positive work done by the gas is W=∫pdV. The net values on the three complete branches are
branchA→BB→CC→A​ΔU/(p0​V0​)3/20−3/2​W/(p0​V0​)03/2−1​Qnet​/(p0​V0​)3/23/2−5/2​​
(49)
Thus the net work is Wcycle​=p0​V0​/2, equal to the triangle's enclosed area.
There is a small but important distinction between the net heat on a branch and its total positive heat input to a cycle. On the sloping branch put x=V/V0​, so p/p0​=3−x, with 1≤x≤2. Then
p0​V0​U​=23​x(3−x),p0​V0​δQ​=(215​−4x)dx.
(50)
There is a heat-flow reversal along a thermodynamic path at x=15/8. The first part absorbs heat; the final part rejects it. Integrating the two signed contributions separately gives
p0​V0​QBC,+​​=∫115/8​(215​−4x)dx=3249​,p0​V0​QBC,−​​=∫15/82​(215​−4x)dx=−321​.
(51)
The true total absorbed and rejected heats and thermal efficiency are therefore
Qin​=3297​p0​V0​,Qout​=3281​p0​V0​,η=Qin​Wcycle​​=9716​≃0.16495.​
(52)
For clarity, the positive heat input on A→B is 3p0​V0​/2, on B→C it is 49p0​V0​/32, and on C→A it is zero. Using just the positive net heats of the complete branches would give Qin​=3p0​V0​ and η=1/6; that shortcut misses the short heat-rejection interval on B→C.
Finally, put T0​=p0​V0​/R. The minimum temperature is T0​ at A. Along B→C,
T/T0​=x(3−x),
(53)
which has its maximum 9/4 at x=3/2, not at either endpoint. Thus the corresponding two-reservoir Carnot efficiency is
ηC,extremes​=1−Tmax​Tmin​​=1−94​=95​≃0.556.​
(54)
The triangular cycle receives heat at a range of temperatures below Tmax​ and rejects it at temperatures above Tmin​. It therefore makes less work per unit input heat than a Carnot cycle using only those extrema. The geometric path alone does not prove irreversibility: it could in principle exchange heat reversibly with a continuum of matched-temperature reservoirs, while still having this lower efficiency. Restricting it to only the two extreme-temperature reservoirs would introduce additional irreversible heat transfers.
