Elasticity describes recoverable deformation. For small displacements u(x), the infinitesimal strain tensor is the symmetric part of the displacement gradient,
ϵij​=21​(∂i​uj​+∂j​ui​).​
(42)
Its diagonal entries describe extension, while its off-diagonal entries describe shear. The engineering shear strain is γxy​=2ϵxy​; the antisymmetric part of the displacement gradient is a small rigid rotation, not elastic strain.
The stress tensor specifies the force per unit area on each oriented plane: t(n)=σn. Positive normal stress is tensile in the convention used here; tangential traction gives shear stress. In the absence of internal couples, angular-momentum balance makes the stress tensor symmetric. Force balance gives ∇⋅σ+f=0 in static equilibrium, where f is body force per unit volume.
Linear elasticity relates stress and strain. A homogeneous isotropic solid needs two independent elastic constants:
σij​=λϵkk​δij​+2Gϵij​.​
(43)
Here G is the shear modulus and λ is the other Lamé parameter. For uniaxial loading, Young's modulus is E=σxx​/ϵxx​, with lateral contraction described by Poisson's ratio ν=−ϵyy​/ϵxx​. The bulk modulus is K=−p/(ΔV/V) for a small hydrostatic compression. The relations between these constants are
E=2G(1+ν)=3K(1−2ν),K=λ+32G​.​
(44)
Stable isotropic elastic strain energy density requires G>0 and K>0, implying −1<ν<1/2. The incompressible limit is approached as ν→1/2. Anisotropic crystals generally need more constants; the isotropic relations should not be imposed on arbitrary directions in such a crystal.
The elastic energy density is w=σ:ϵ/2. In the isotropic linear model this becomes λ(trϵ)2/2+Gϵ:ϵ. The quadratic form explains why reversible loading stores energy and unloading can return it, unlike plastic deformation or a dissipative hysteresis cycle.
For a slender beam described by the Euler-Bernoulli beam equation, plane sections approximately remain plane. A fibre a distance y from the neutral axis has axial strain −yκ, where κ is curvature, and stress −Eyκ. Integrating its moment about the neutral axis gives, with consistent signs,
M=EIA​κ,IA​=∫A​y2dA.​
(45)
IA​ is a second moment of area, with units of length to the fourth power; it is not a mass moment of inertia. For a rectangular section of width b and thickness h, IA​=bh3/12, explaining the strong dependence of bending stiffness on thickness. For small transverse deflection w(x), curvature is approximately w′′, and the Euler-Bernoulli beam equation is EIA​w′′′′=q(x) with a suitable load-sign convention. A cantilever of length ℓ under end load F has tip deflection Fℓ3/(3EIA​) and largest bending stress of magnitude Fℓc/IA​, where c is the furthest distance from the neutral axis.
In torsion of a solid circular shaft, a cross-section rotated by angle θ(x) has shear strain rθ′(x). Therefore
τ(r)=Grθ′,T=GJθ′,J=∫A​r2dA=2πa4​.​
(46)
Here J is the polar second moment of area. A uniform shaft of length ℓ under constant torque twists by Tℓ/(GJ), and its largest shear stress is Ta/J. Noncircular sections can warp, so replacing their torsional stiffness by G times their polar area moment is not generally valid. Both bending and torsion formulas presume small, elastic deformations; exceeding the elastic limit can produce permanent deformation or fracture.
