An electric dipole consists of equal and opposite electric charges separated by a displacement d. Its electric dipole moment is p=qd, directed from negative to positive electric charge. At distances large compared with the separation, its leading electric potential and electric field are
ϕ(r)=4πϵ0​r2p⋅r​,E=4πϵ0​r33(p⋅r)r−p​.​
(14)
There is no monopole term because the net electric charge is zero. The axial electric field points along p with magnitude 2p/(4πϵ0​r3), while the equatorial electric field points oppositely with half that magnitude.
A uniform applied electric field exerts zero net force on an ideal fixed electric dipole, but it exerts torque τ=p×E. Its orientation-dependent potential energy is U=−p⋅E, so alignment with the electric field is stable. A nonuniform applied electric field can also exert a net force, F=∇(p⋅E) for a fixed permanent electric dipole moment.
A permanent electric dipole moment exists without an applied electric field. An induced electric dipole moment instead develops in response to the local electric field; in an isotropic linear model p=αE. Creating that induced moment requires accounting for the work of polarization, so its mechanical potential energy in a specified applied electric field is −αE2/2, rather than −αE2. In matter, the dipole moment per unit volume is the polarization density P.
