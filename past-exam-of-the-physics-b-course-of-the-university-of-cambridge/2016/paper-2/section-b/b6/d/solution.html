A permanent magnet retains a substantial magnetization without an applied coil electric current. In a ferromagnetic material this reflects microscopic magnetic moments, magnetic domains and their resistance to rearrangement. The history-dependent relation between applied field and magnetization gives magnetic hysteresis, remanence and coercivity. A soft core used in an electromagnet or electrical transformer is chosen for easy magnetization and small hysteretic loss; a permanent-magnet material must resist demagnetization.[ref]
The macroscopic fields obey
B=μ0​(H+M),∇×H=Jf​,∇⋅B=0​
(21)
in magnetostatics. The magnetization can be represented by equivalent bound currents,
Jb​=∇×M,Kb​=M×n.
(22)
Thus both permanent magnetic matter and free coil electric currents generate a magnetic field. Its magnetic field lines form continuous loops; apparent north and south poles are not isolated magnetic charges.
In a long ideal solenoid, H≃nI. A linear core would give B≃μ0​μr​nI, until saturation or other nonlinear effects invalidate a constant magnetic permeability. In a finite magnetic circuit, air gaps and demagnetizing fields affect the field strongly: because the gap has low magnetic permeability, it often needs most of the available magnetomotive force. A permanent magnet can have a nonzero internal H even when Jf​=0, because its boundaries create a demagnetizing field. Hence setting H=0 merely because there is no free coil electric current is generally incorrect.
