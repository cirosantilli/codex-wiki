A horizontal pressure gradient force accelerates air from higher toward lower pressure. In a rotating frame the Coriolis force deflects the flow. Away from strong friction, at large spatial scales and small Rossby number, a slowly changing flow can reach geostrophic balance:
fz×ug​=−ρ1​∇h​p,ug​=ρf1​z×∇h​p​,
(28)
where f=2Ωsinφ is the Coriolis parameter. The geostrophic wind runs along atmospheric isobars, with speed ∣∇h​p∣/(ρ∣f∣). Closely spaced atmospheric isobars therefore indicate stronger geostrophic wind when density and latitude are comparable. In the northern hemisphere lower pressure lies to the left of this wind, producing counterclockwise circulation around a low; the sense reverses in the southern hemisphere.
A curved flow also has centripetal acceleration. For cyclonic circular flow in the northern hemisphere, the gradient wind relation is
rv2​+fv=ρ1​drdp​.
(29)
Near the ground, friction reduces wind speed and weakens its Coriolis contribution, so air crosses atmospheric isobars toward lower pressure. The simple geostrophic formula fails near the equator, where f becomes small, and where rapid changes or tight curvature make neglected acceleration important. Vertically, large-scale motion is often approximated by hydrostatic balance, dp/dz=−ρg.
Bernoulli's principle can relate speed changes to pressure changes along an appropriate inviscid streamline, but its simple dynamic-pressure relation is not a replacement for the rotating force balance governing a broad atmospheric pressure map.
