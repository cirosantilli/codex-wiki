Coupled oscillators have more than one collective pattern of motion. For two equal masses m, each attached to a fixed support by a spring of stiffness k and linked to each other by stiffness κ, small displacements obey
mx¨1​+bx˙1​+(k+κ)x1​−κx2​=F1​cosΩt,
(30)
mx¨2​+bx˙2​+(k+κ)x2​−κx1​=F2​cosΩt.
(31)
Here equal linear damping b is included to make finite steady-state responses at resonance. The normal mode coordinates q+​=(x1​+x2​)/2​ and q−​=(x1​−x2​)/2​ diagonalize these equations. Their natural angular frequencies are
ω+2​=k/m,ω−2​=(k+2κ)/m.​
(32)
The symmetric normal mode leaves the coupling spring unextended; the antisymmetric normal mode stretches it and therefore has the higher frequency. With F±​=(F1​±F2​)/2​, their complex steady-state responses are
q​±​=m(ω±2​−Ω2)+ibΩF±​​.​
(33)
Thus the drive's spatial pattern matters as well as its frequency. Equal in-phase forces drive only the symmetric normal mode; opposite forces drive only the antisymmetric one; driving a single mass generally excites both. Finite damping broadens the resonance peaks and produces a phase lag. In the ideal undamped system, a resonant drive with nonzero projection onto a normal mode produces secular growth rather than a bounded resonant steady state.
The displacement of a particular mass adds contributions from both normal modes. They can cancel at an antiresonance. For F2​=0 and no damping, x1​ vanishes at Ω2=(k+κ)/m, while the partner still moves. This differs from the two nearby resonances, where a mode responds strongly; a small measured response need not mean that no motion or stored energy exists elsewhere.
