An electrostatic field calculation begins by specifying the charge information, material response and boundary conditions. In vacuum, prescribed volume and surface electric charge densities can be superposed directly using Coulomb's law. For a volume distribution,
ϕ(r)=4πϵ0​1​∫∣r−r′∣ρ(r′)​d3r′,E=−∇ϕ.
(23)
Differentiating this electric potential gives the electric field. This is useful for known source distributions, but in the presence of conductors the induced surface electric charge is usually an unknown rather than an independently specified input.
Gauss's law is especially effective when symmetry fixes the direction and magnitude of the electric field on a suitable closed surface. A spherical charge distribution gives a radial field, an infinitely long uniform line gives a cylindrical field, and a uniform infinite sheet gives a planar field. In an arbitrary geometry, the flux law remains true but does not alone determine the pointwise electric field. A multipole expansion is another useful approximation: far from a localized charge distribution, successive monopole, dipole and higher terms separate the effects of total charge and its spatial structure.
For a boundary-value formulation, use the electrostatic equations
∇×E=0,∇⋅E=ρ/ϵ0​.
(24)
They imply Poisson equation ∇2ϕ=−ρ/ϵ0​, or Laplace equation where ρ=0. A conductor in electrostatic equilibrium is an equipotential with zero internal electric field. Its exterior normal electric field fixes the surface electric charge through En​=σ/ϵ0​. One must specify whether each conductor is grounded, maintained at a prescribed voltage, or isolated with prescribed total electric charge.
Uniqueness of Poisson equation justifies methods that construct a convenient candidate solution. With fixed boundary electric potential and suitable behaviour at infinity, a solution satisfying the field equation is the solution. With normal-derivative boundary conditions, the source and flux must be compatible, and the electric potential is determined only up to an additive constant.
For example, a point charge q a distance h above a grounded infinite plane has the same upper-half-space electric potential as q and a mirrored image charge −q below it. Their potentials cancel on the plane. The image is a mathematical source in the excluded region, not a second physical charge. Separation of variables similarly constructs solutions suited to rectangular, cylindrical or spherical boundaries; matching coefficients enforces the prescribed boundary data.
In dielectrics, the distinction between total and free charge is essential. In a linear isotropic material,
D=ϵ(r)E,∇⋅[ϵ(r)∇ϕ]=−ρf​.​
(25)
Only where permittivity is constant may this be reduced to ∇2ϕ=−ρf​/ϵ. At an ordinary interface, ϕ and the tangential electric field are continuous, while the jump in normal electric displacement field is the free surface charge. Applying Gauss's law to total charge with ϵ0​, or its displacement-field form to free charge, gives consistent descriptions; mixing their sources and permittivities does not.
A useful interface example is the image charge at a dielectric interface. If q lies in medium 1, the source-side potential is represented by
ϕ1​=4πϵ1​1​(r1​q​+r2​q′​),q′=qϵ1​+ϵ2​ϵ1​−ϵ2​​​,
(26)
where r1​ is distance to the real charge and r2​ to its mirrored position. The other-side potential can be written ϕ2​=qt​/(4πϵ2​r1​), where qt​=2ϵ2​q/(ϵ1​+ϵ2​). Continuity of ϕ and of normal D fixes both coefficients. The equal-permittivity limit gives no image; the conductor limit ϵ2​→∞ gives q′→−q.
For a sphere of radius a and relative permittivity ϵr​ in a uniform external electric field E0​, matching the spherical solutions gives a uniform internal electric field and an external induced electric dipole moment:
Ein​=ϵr​+23​E0​,p=4πϵ0​a3ϵr​+2ϵr​−1​E0​.​
(27)
This illustrates how a material boundary modifies an otherwise simple applied electric field.
For complex boundaries or spatially varying materials, finite difference methods or the finite element method solve the same boundary value problem numerically. A sound calculation checks its boundary conditions, charge or flux balance and convergence with mesh refinement. The governing equations, source classification and uniqueness argument remain the organizing principles even when an analytic formula is unavailable.
