An electric dipole consists of equal and opposite electric charges separated by a displacement d. Its electric dipole moment is p=qd, directed from negative to positive electric charge. At distances large compared with the separation, its leading electric potential and electric field are
ϕ(r)=4πϵ0​r2p⋅r​,E=4πϵ0​r33(p⋅r)r−p​.​
(14)
There is no monopole term because the net electric charge is zero. The axial electric field points along p with magnitude 2p/(4πϵ0​r3), while the equatorial electric field points oppositely with half that magnitude.
A uniform applied electric field exerts zero net force on an ideal fixed electric dipole, but it exerts torque τ=p×E. Its orientation-dependent potential energy is U=−p⋅E, so alignment with the electric field is stable. A nonuniform applied electric field can also exert a net force, F=∇(p⋅E) for a fixed permanent electric dipole moment.
A permanent electric dipole moment exists without an applied electric field. An induced electric dipole moment instead develops in response to the local electric field; in an isotropic linear model p=αE. Creating that induced moment requires accounting for the work of polarization, so its mechanical potential energy in a specified applied electric field is −αE2/2, rather than −αE2. In matter, the dipole moment per unit volume is the polarization density P.

Λ1​=L1​I1​+MI2​,Λ2​=MI1​+L2​I2​.

The stored magnetic energy is
U=21​L1​I12​+MI1​I2​+21​L2​I22​.
(16)
Its nonnegativity for arbitrary electric currents requires M2≤L1​L2​. Strong coupling makes ∣M∣ approach L1​L2​​; flux leakage makes it smaller.
An ideal electrical transformer has two windings linked by the same time-varying core magnetic flux. The induced winding voltages are proportional to their turn counts. For appropriately chosen terminal polarities,
V1​V2​​=N1​N2​​,V1​I1​=V2​I2​,I1​I2​​=N2​N1​​.​
(17)

Free charge is charge assigned to externally supplied or mobile carriers in a macroscopic description. Bound charge describes the net charge associated with a material's polarization density P. Its bulk and surface densities are
ρb​=−∇⋅P,σb​=P⋅n,​
(18)
where the normal points out of the polarized material. Uniform bulk polarization density has no bulk bound charge, but it can still have surface bound charge.
The electric field is sourced by the total electric charge. Introducing the electric displacement field separates the free source in the macroscopic field equation:
∇⋅E=ϵ0​ρf​+ρb​​,D=ϵ0​E+P,∇⋅D=ρf​.​
(19)
This does not mean that bound charges are physically absent from D: they are represented through P. In a linear isotropic dielectric, P=ϵ0​χe​E and D=ϵE, with ϵ=ϵ0​(1+χe​).
Across a stationary ordinary dielectric interface, electrostatics gives continuity of tangential electric field, while a free sheet charge gives
n×(E2​−E1​)=0,n⋅(D2​−D1​)=σf​.​
(20)
The normal electric field need not be continuous. For a fully filled parallel-plate capacitor at fixed free plate charge, polarization reduces the internal electric field by ϵr​ and increases capacitance by the same factor. If a battery instead holds the voltage fixed, extra free plate charge flows from the battery; the constraint must be stated before comparing stored energies.[ref]

A permanent magnet retains a substantial magnetization without an applied coil electric current. In a ferromagnetic material this reflects microscopic magnetic moments, magnetic domains and their resistance to rearrangement. The history-dependent relation between applied field and magnetization gives magnetic hysteresis, remanence and coercivity. A soft core used in an electromagnet or electrical transformer is chosen for easy magnetization and small hysteretic loss; a permanent-magnet material must resist demagnetization.[ref]
The macroscopic fields obey
B=μ0​(H+M),∇×H=Jf​,∇⋅B=0​
(21)
in magnetostatics. The magnetization can be represented by equivalent bound currents,
Jb​=∇×M,Kb​=M×n.
(22)
Thus both permanent magnetic matter and free coil electric currents generate a magnetic field. Its magnetic field lines form continuous loops; apparent north and south poles are not isolated magnetic charges.
In a long ideal solenoid, H≃nI. A linear core would give B≃μ0​μr​nI, until saturation or other nonlinear effects invalidate a constant magnetic permeability. In a finite magnetic circuit, air gaps and demagnetizing fields affect the field strongly: because the gap has low magnetic permeability, it often needs most of the available magnetomotive force. A permanent magnet can have a nonzero internal H even when Jf​=0, because its boundaries create a demagnetizing field. Hence setting H=0 merely because there is no free coil electric current is generally incorrect.

An electrostatic field calculation begins by specifying the charge information, material response and boundary conditions. In vacuum, prescribed volume and surface electric charge densities can be superposed directly using Coulomb's law. For a volume distribution,
ϕ(r)=4πϵ0​1​∫∣r−r′∣ρ(r′)​d3r′,E=−∇ϕ.
(23)
Differentiating this electric potential gives the electric field. This is useful for known source distributions, but in the presence of conductors the induced surface electric charge is usually an unknown rather than an independently specified input.
Gauss's law is especially effective when symmetry fixes the direction and magnitude of the electric field on a suitable closed surface. A spherical charge distribution gives a radial field, an infinitely long uniform line gives a cylindrical field, and a uniform infinite sheet gives a planar field. In an arbitrary geometry, the flux law remains true but does not alone determine the pointwise electric field. A multipole expansion is another useful approximation: far from a localized charge distribution, successive monopole, dipole and higher terms separate the effects of total charge and its spatial structure.
For a boundary-value formulation, use the electrostatic equations
∇×E=0,∇⋅E=ρ/ϵ0​.
(24)
They imply Poisson equation ∇2ϕ=−ρ/ϵ0​, or Laplace equation where ρ=0. A conductor in electrostatic equilibrium is an equipotential with zero internal electric field. Its exterior normal electric field fixes the surface electric charge through En​=σ/ϵ0​. One must specify whether each conductor is grounded, maintained at a prescribed voltage, or isolated with prescribed total electric charge.
Uniqueness of Poisson equation justifies methods that construct a convenient candidate solution. With fixed boundary electric potential and suitable behaviour at infinity, a solution satisfying the field equation is the solution. With normal-derivative boundary conditions, the source and flux must be compatible, and the electric potential is determined only up to an additive constant.
For example, a point charge q a distance h above a grounded infinite plane has the same upper-half-space electric potential as q and a mirrored image charge −q below it. Their potentials cancel on the plane. The image is a mathematical source in the excluded region, not a second physical charge. Separation of variables similarly constructs solutions suited to rectangular, cylindrical or spherical boundaries; matching coefficients enforces the prescribed boundary data.
In dielectrics, the distinction between total and free charge is essential. In a linear isotropic material,
D=ϵ(r)E,∇⋅[ϵ(r)∇ϕ]=−ρf​.​
(25)
Only where permittivity is constant may this be reduced to ∇2ϕ=−ρf​/ϵ. At an ordinary interface, ϕ and the tangential electric field are continuous, while the jump in normal electric displacement field is the free surface charge. Applying Gauss's law to total charge with ϵ0​, or its displacement-field form to free charge, gives consistent descriptions; mixing their sources and permittivities does not.
A useful interface example is the image charge at a dielectric interface. If q lies in medium 1, the source-side potential is represented by
ϕ1​=4πϵ1​1​(r1​q​+r2​q′​),q′=qϵ1​+ϵ2​ϵ1​−ϵ2​​​,
(26)
where r1​ is distance to the real charge and r2​ to its mirrored position. The other-side potential can be written ϕ2​=qt​/(4πϵ2​r1​), where qt​=2ϵ2​q/(ϵ1​+ϵ2​). Continuity of ϕ and of normal D fixes both coefficients. The equal-permittivity limit gives no image; the conductor limit ϵ2​→∞ gives q′→−q.
For a sphere of radius a and relative permittivity ϵr​ in a uniform external electric field E0​, matching the spherical solutions gives a uniform internal electric field and an external induced electric dipole moment:
Ein​=ϵr​+23​E0​,p=4πϵ0​a3ϵr​+2ϵr​−1​E0​.​
(27)
This illustrates how a material boundary modifies an otherwise simple applied electric field.
For complex boundaries or spatially varying materials, finite difference methods or the finite element method solve the same boundary value problem numerically. A sound calculation checks its boundary conditions, charge or flux balance and convergence with mesh refinement. The governing equations, source classification and uniqueness argument remain the organizing principles even when an analytic formula is unavailable.

A horizontal pressure gradient force accelerates air from higher toward lower pressure. In a rotating frame the Coriolis force deflects the flow. Away from strong friction, at large spatial scales and small Rossby number, a slowly changing flow can reach geostrophic balance:
fz×ug​=−ρ1​∇h​p,ug​=ρf1​z×∇h​p​,
(28)
where f=2Ωsinφ is the Coriolis parameter. The geostrophic wind runs along atmospheric isobars, with speed ∣∇h​p∣/(ρ∣f∣). Closely spaced atmospheric isobars therefore indicate stronger geostrophic wind when density and latitude are comparable. In the northern hemisphere lower pressure lies to the left of this wind, producing counterclockwise circulation around a low; the sense reverses in the southern hemisphere.
A curved flow also has centripetal acceleration. For cyclonic circular flow in the northern hemisphere, the gradient wind relation is
rv2​+fv=ρ1​drdp​.
(29)
Near the ground, friction reduces wind speed and weakens its Coriolis contribution, so air crosses atmospheric isobars toward lower pressure. The simple geostrophic formula fails near the equator, where f becomes small, and where rapid changes or tight curvature make neglected acceleration important. Vertically, large-scale motion is often approximated by hydrostatic balance, dp/dz=−ρg.
Bernoulli's principle can relate speed changes to pressure changes along an appropriate inviscid streamline, but its simple dynamic-pressure relation is not a replacement for the rotating force balance governing a broad atmospheric pressure map.

Coupled oscillators have more than one collective pattern of motion. For two equal masses m, each attached to a fixed support by a spring of stiffness k and linked to each other by stiffness κ, small displacements obey
mx¨1​+bx˙1​+(k+κ)x1​−κx2​=F1​cosΩt,
(30)
mx¨2​+bx˙2​+(k+κ)x2​−κx1​=F2​cosΩt.
(31)
Here equal linear damping b is included to make finite steady-state responses at resonance. The normal mode coordinates q+​=(x1​+x2​)/2​ and q−​=(x1​−x2​)/2​ diagonalize these equations. Their natural angular frequencies are
ω+2​=k/m,ω−2​=(k+2κ)/m.​
(32)
The symmetric normal mode leaves the coupling spring unextended; the antisymmetric normal mode stretches it and therefore has the higher frequency. With F±​=(F1​±F2​)/2​, their complex steady-state responses are
q​±​=m(ω±2​−Ω2)+ibΩF±​​.​
(33)
Thus the drive's spatial pattern matters as well as its frequency. Equal in-phase forces drive only the symmetric normal mode; opposite forces drive only the antisymmetric one; driving a single mass generally excites both. Finite damping broadens the resonance peaks and produces a phase lag. In the ideal undamped system, a resonant drive with nonzero projection onto a normal mode produces secular growth rather than a bounded resonant steady state.
The displacement of a particular mass adds contributions from both normal modes. They can cancel at an antiresonance. For F2​=0 and no damping, x1​ vanishes at Ω2=(k+κ)/m, while the partner still moves. This differs from the two nearby resonances, where a mode responds strongly; a small measured response need not mean that no motion or stored energy exists elsewhere.

A Newtonian fluid exerts viscous shear stress proportional to the rate of strain. For an incompressible fluid of constant dynamic viscosity η,
σ=−pI+η[∇u+(∇u)T].
(34)
The local momentum balance is the Navier-Stokes equation:
ρ(∂t∂u​+u⋅∇u)=−∇p+η∇2u+ρg.​
(35)
The left side is density times material acceleration. A steady velocity field can still have convective acceleration when a moving parcel changes speed or direction; setting ∂u/∂t=0 does not eliminate it.
The Reynolds number Re=ρUL/η compares inertia with viscous effects. At low Reynolds number, the fluid's own inertia can often be neglected, giving Stokes flow. This does not say that every immersed body has zero acceleration: its mass and the net pressure, viscous and body forces still enter its own equation of motion.
For a small rigid sphere moving through a slowly varying viscous background, Stokes drag is −6πηa(v−u). In still fluid, a simple settling model includes weight, buoyancy and drag:
mv˙=(m−ρf​Vs​)g−6πηav.
(36)
Ignoring fluid added mass and unsteady history forces, this gives relaxation time m/(6πηa) and terminal velocity
vt​=9η2a2(ρs​−ρf​)g​.​
(37)
At the terminal velocity the net force and acceleration vanish, while viscous drag and gravity remain individually nonzero. The dissipated power 6πηavt2​ is supplied by the loss of gravitational potential energy relative to buoyant support. The creeping-flow and quasisteady-drag assumptions must be checked before applying this expression at large speed or immediately after a rapid start.

R=Mm1​r1​+m2​r2​​,r=r1​−r2​,M=m1​+m2​.

Elasticity describes recoverable deformation. For small displacements u(x), the infinitesimal strain tensor is the symmetric part of the displacement gradient,
ϵij​=21​(∂i​uj​+∂j​ui​).​
(42)
Its diagonal entries describe extension, while its off-diagonal entries describe shear. The engineering shear strain is γxy​=2ϵxy​; the antisymmetric part of the displacement gradient is a small rigid rotation, not elastic strain.
The stress tensor specifies the force per unit area on each oriented plane: t(n)=σn. Positive normal stress is tensile in the convention used here; tangential traction gives shear stress. In the absence of internal couples, angular-momentum balance makes the stress tensor symmetric. Force balance gives ∇⋅σ+f=0 in static equilibrium, where f is body force per unit volume.
Linear elasticity relates stress and strain. A homogeneous isotropic solid needs two independent elastic constants:
σij​=λϵkk​δij​+2Gϵij​.​
(43)
Here G is the shear modulus and λ is the other Lamé parameter. For uniaxial loading, Young's modulus is E=σxx​/ϵxx​, with lateral contraction described by Poisson's ratio ν=−ϵyy​/ϵxx​. The bulk modulus is K=−p/(ΔV/V) for a small hydrostatic compression. The relations between these constants are
E=2G(1+ν)=3K(1−2ν),K=λ+32G​.​
(44)
Stable isotropic elastic strain energy density requires G>0 and K>0, implying −1<ν<1/2. The incompressible limit is approached as ν→1/2. Anisotropic crystals generally need more constants; the isotropic relations should not be imposed on arbitrary directions in such a crystal.
The elastic energy density is w=σ:ϵ/2. In the isotropic linear model this becomes λ(trϵ)2/2+Gϵ:ϵ. The quadratic form explains why reversible loading stores energy and unloading can return it, unlike plastic deformation or a dissipative hysteresis cycle.
For a slender beam described by the Euler-Bernoulli beam equation, plane sections approximately remain plane. A fibre a distance y from the neutral axis has axial strain −yκ, where κ is curvature, and stress −Eyκ. Integrating its moment about the neutral axis gives, with consistent signs,
M=EIA​κ,IA​=∫A​y2dA.​
(45)
IA​ is a second moment of area, with units of length to the fourth power; it is not a mass moment of inertia. For a rectangular section of width b and thickness h, IA​=bh3/12, explaining the strong dependence of bending stiffness on thickness. For small transverse deflection w(x), curvature is approximately w′′, and the Euler-Bernoulli beam equation is EIA​w′′′′=q(x) with a suitable load-sign convention. A cantilever of length ℓ under end load F has tip deflection Fℓ3/(3EIA​) and largest bending stress of magnitude Fℓc/IA​, where c is the furthest distance from the neutral axis.
In torsion of a solid circular shaft, a cross-section rotated by angle θ(x) has shear strain rθ′(x). Therefore
τ(r)=Grθ′,T=GJθ′,J=∫A​r2dA=2πa4​.​
(46)
Here J is the polar second moment of area. A uniform shaft of length ℓ under constant torque twists by Tℓ/(GJ), and its largest shear stress is Ta/J. Noncircular sections can warp, so replacing their torsional stiffness by G times their polar area moment is not generally valid. Both bending and torsion formulas presume small, elastic deformations; exceeding the elastic limit can produce permanent deformation or fracture.

A Carnot cycle consists of two reversible isothermal branches joined by two reversible adiabatic branches. Let the hot isothermal expansion run from volume Va​ to Vb​, and the cold isothermal compression from Vc​ to Vd​. For n moles of an ideal gas, their absorbed and rejected heat magnitudes are
Qh​=nRT1​ln(Vb​/Va​),Qc​=nRT2​ln(Vc​/Vd​).
(47)
On a reversible ideal-gas adiabat, TVγ−1 is constant. Applying this to the two joining branches gives Vb​/Va​=Vc​/Vd​. Therefore
Qh​Qc​​=T1​T2​​,ηC​=Qh​Qh​−Qc​​=1−T1​T2​​.​
(48)
The result is independent of the gas's heat-capacity ratio. This derivation presumes the usual constant-heat-capacity ideal-gas model; more generally the equal entropy changes on the two isotherms give the same heat ratio without that restriction.
To establish the upper bound, suppose an engine were more efficient between these same two reservoirs. Use its output work to drive a reversed Carnot cycle, scaled so that the work magnitudes match. For fixed work W, the hypothetical engine would take W/η from the hot bath, whereas the reversed Carnot cycle would return W/ηC​>W/η to it. The combined device would transfer heat from the cold bath to the hot bath without any net external work. That contradicts the Clausius statement of the second law. Hence no such two-reservoir engine can exceed 1−T2​/T1​.
For the triangular cycle, call the three successive vertices A,B,C, and measure energies in units of p0​V0​. For one mole of a monatomic ideal gas, U=3pV/2, and positive work done by the gas is W=∫pdV. The net values on the three complete branches are
branchA→BB→CC→A​ΔU/(p0​V0​)3/20−3/2​W/(p0​V0​)03/2−1​Qnet​/(p0​V0​)3/23/2−5/2​​
(49)
Thus the net work is Wcycle​=p0​V0​/2, equal to the triangle's enclosed area.
There is a small but important distinction between the net heat on a branch and its total positive heat input to a cycle. On the sloping branch put x=V/V0​, so p/p0​=3−x, with 1≤x≤2. Then

(53)
which has its maximum 9/4 at x=3/2, not at either endpoint. Thus the corresponding two-reservoir Carnot efficiency is

It therefore makes less work per unit input heat than a Carnot cycle using only those extrema. The geometric path alone does not prove irreversibility: it could in principle exchange heat reversibly with a continuum of matched-temperature reservoirs, while still having this lower efficiency. Restricting it to only the two extreme-temperature reservoirs would introduce additional irreversible heat transfers.

For the two unlabelled follow-up requests, distinguish re-equilibrating effusion from collisionless effusion. The calculations below show that escaping atoms carry mean kinetic energy 2kB​T, whereas a bulk atom has mean kinetic energy 3kB​T/2. If the aperture is small and interatomic collisions maintain near-equilibrium conditions, faster atoms escape preferentially, and the remaining gas relaxes toward a lower-temperature Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution. No heat supplied by the walls is available to replace the lost energy.
For a monatomic gas at fixed container volume, conservation of energy gives a quantitative check on this effusive cooling picture:
d(23​NkB​T)=2kB​TdN,TdT​=31​NdN​.​
(62)
Since dN<0, the temperature falls. Under these idealizations T∝N1/3; the gas is not left with a permanently truncated velocity tail when collisions rapidly restore equilibrium.
If interatomic collisions are absent, the individual surviving atoms do not rethermalize to a new common temperature. Their speed distribution becomes selectively depleted, generally favouring slower survivors, and their angular and spatial distribution can also develop structure determined by which trajectories reach the hole. A single lower temperature does not describe the full nonequilibrium distribution. With a direction-randomizing but energy-preserving wall model, an illustrative survival law is f(v,t)∝f(v,0)e−κvt; specular walls can give more detailed directional selection and even persistent nonescaping trajectories. An equation of state relates thermodynamic variables within a material model; a phase diagram records which equilibrium phase or phases are stable under specified controls. For a pure substance at fixed temperature and pressure, stability means minimizing Gibbs free energy. Two phases coexist when their molar chemical potentials agree. The equation of state and the free-energy stability condition must therefore be used together: an algebraic relation between p,V,T alone need not identify the stable branch.
A pressure-temperature phase diagram contains single-phase regions separated by phase coexistence curves. For a one-component system the Gibbs phase rule gives F=3−P: a single phase has two independent controls, two-phase coexistence lies on a curve, and three phases coexist at a triple point. The liquid-gas coexistence curve ends at a liquid-gas critical point; beyond it a continuous path connects liquidlike and gaslike states without crossing a first-order coexistence boundary. A fixed p,T point on a coexistence curve does not specify the proportions of the two phases: an additional extensive constraint, such as total volume, fixes the phase fraction.
At a first-order phase transition, first derivatives of Gibbs free energy, such as molar entropy or volume, are discontinuous. The conversion has latent heat L=TΔs. At a continuous transition the first derivatives match, while response functions associated with higher derivatives can be singular or discontinuous. At an ordinary liquid-gas critical endpoint, the two coexisting densities approach one another and the latent heat tends to zero; the material does not retain a sharp liquid-gas boundary above it.
The coexistence slope follows directly from equality of the two molar chemical potentials. Since dμ=−sdT+vdp, differentiating μα​=μβ​ along the boundary gives
−ΔsdT+Δvdp=0,dTdp​=ΔvΔs​=TΔvL​.​
(69)
This is the exact Clapeyron equation, also commonly called the Clausius-Clapeyron relation. For vaporization, both L and Δv are positive, so the saturation pressure rises with temperature. For ordinary ice melting into liquid water, L>0 but the molar volume decreases, giving the familiar negative melting-curve slope. The sign follows from the volume change, not from a different law.
For a dilute ideal vapour with vg​≫vl​, Δv≃RT/p. The ideal-vapor Clausius-Clapeyron approximation becomes
dTdlnpsat​​≃RT2L​.
(70)
If molar latent heat is approximately constant over the interval,
lnpsat​(T0​)psat​(T)​=−RL​(T1​−T0​1​).​
(71)
These approximations deteriorate close to the critical point, where liquid and gas volumes become comparable, or over a range in which latent heat changes appreciably.[ref]
The ideal gas law pV=nRT is an equation of state for a dilute gas, but it has no liquid branch and cannot predict condensation. A simple model including repulsion and attraction is the Van der Waals molar equation,
p=v−bRT​−v2a​,
(72)
where b>0 represents excluded molar volume and a>0 represents attraction. At its critical point, ∂p/∂v=∂2p/∂v2=0, giving
vc​=3b,pc​=27b2a​,Tc​=27Rb8a​.​
(73)
Below Tc​, the homogeneous isotherm has an interval with ∂p/∂v>0. That would have negative isothermal compressibility and is mechanically unstable. Equilibrium liquid-gas coexistence replaces the loop by a constant-pressure segment. Equality of chemical potentials gives the Maxwell construction:
p(vl​,T)=p(vg​,T)=p∗​,∫vl​vg​​[p(v,T)−p∗​],dv=0.​
(74)
The equal-area condition enforces the common tangent to the appropriate free energy; it is a stability construction rather than permission to follow the unstable loop physically. The average molar volume between vl​ and vg​ varies by changing the phase fraction.
The model captures a liquid-gas critical point and qualitative coexistence, but its simple constants are not a complete quantitative description of real fluids and it has no solid phase. A full phase diagram needs additional solid free energies and can contain several crystal phases. Conversely, an experimentally determined coexistence curve and latent heat constrain equations of state through the Clapeyron equation. This is the useful connection: local thermodynamic response, global free-energy stability and the geometry of phase boundaries describe the same material at different levels.
