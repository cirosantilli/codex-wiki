Bx​(0,z)=2πwμ0​I​∫−w/2w/2​x′2+z2zdx′​=πwμ0​I​tan−12zw​,z>0.
(1)
The field reverses below the strip. The sketch is an original field integration; the conductor thickness is idealized as negligible.

At perpendicular distance z≪w from the middle of a face, but outside the conductor, the strip approximates an infinite current sheet. The tangential field jump is μ0​K and symmetry gives equal opposite fields on the two sides. Hence B≃μ0​I/(2w) on either side. The centreline integral gives the same limit as tan−1(w/(2z))→π/2. The method of images replaces induced charges or currents on a boundary by fictitious sources outside the physical region, chosen to satisfy that boundary's conditions. For example, a point charge q a height d above a grounded conducting plane has an image −q at the reflected point. The sum has zero potential on the plane, and the electrostatic uniqueness theorem identifies it with the physical solution in the upper half-space. Images do not describe a second physical charge below the metal.
For the superconducting plane, use the different boundary condition Bz​=0 at its surface: magnetic flux cannot enter the ideal bulk. Choose the real ring moment m=mz, where m=IπR2, at z=h. Its image has moment −mz at z=−h. This superconducting plane image dipole makes the normal fields cancel at z=0, while their tangential fields add. The image is a computational continuation; the physical field inside the plane is zero. The original sketch shows the dipole-scale field, appropriate to h≫R; the fine field close to the ring wire is outside that approximation.
In the vacuum region the magnetic scalar potential is
ϕm​(ρ,z)=4πm​[(ρ2+(z−h)2)3/2z−h​−(ρ2+(z+h)2)3/2z+h​],B=−μ0​∇ϕm​.
(7)
At the surface z=0, it reduces to −mh/[2π(ρ2+h2)3/2]. Its tangential derivative therefore gives
B(a,0)=−2π(a2+h2)5/23μ0​mha​ρ​=−2(a2+h2)5/23μ0​IR2ha​ρ​.​
(8)
Both Bz​ and Bϕ​ vanish there; the sign is inward radially for the chosen positive moment. The expression is the leading dipole result, not an exact finite-ring formula at arbitrary R/h.
For the force, calculate the external image field on the axis near the real dipole:
Bim,z​(z)=−2π(z+h)3μ0​m​.
(9)
Differentiate this field with the image location held fixed, then evaluate at z=h. The dipole levitation force above a superconducting plane is
Fz​=m∂z∂Bim,z​​​z=h​=32πh43μ0​m2​=32h43πμ0​I2R4​​,
(10)
directed upward. Differentiating a substituted separation 2h while moving both dipoles in the force derivative would incorrectly double the result.
To levitate ring mass M, balance this force against Mg:
Ilev​=3πμ0​R432Mgh4​​.​
(11)
A light, thin wire and the smallest practical gap reduce current. At fixed gap and wire cross-section s, M=2πRsρw​ gives Ilev​∝h2sρw​​/R3/2, favouring a larger ring until the dipole-limit constraint on R/h matters. There is no unique least current without geometric and material constraints. As a geometrically consistent order-of-magnitude example, take R=1mm, h=10mm, wire radius 50μm and an assumed metallic-scale density ρw​=6.0×103kgm−3. Then M=2.96×10−7kg and Ilev​≃2.8×102A. The large value reflects the weak h−4 force in the stipulated far-field geometry. Uniformly shrinking this geometrical example reduces current as size to the power 3/2, but increases required electric current density; arbitrarily thin wire is therefore not a free optimization.
This balance estimate is not a verified superconducting design. The actual wire must carry that current without quenching, its self-field counts toward the limit, the plane must remain in its assumed flux-excluding state, and leads or supports add load. Transport critical-current measurements specify temperature, applied field and the voltage criterion rather than a material-independent limiting current.[ref]

Faraday's law for the moving loop is E=−dΦB​/dt, equivalently E=∮(E+v×B)⋅dℓ for its moving circuit. Lenz's law fixes its orientation: the induced current opposes the change in linked magnetic flux. The minus sign represents this opposition, not opposition to the existing field in every phase of motion.
Unroll the small loop's motion locally into translation to the right at speed v≃bω, where b is the radius marked in the source figure. Take the imposed field into the page. On entry the linked into-page flux increases, so the current is counterclockwise. The leading vertical side carries current upward and feels a leftward magnetic force iBa. The in-field parts of the horizontal sides feel equal opposite vertical forces; the trailing side is outside the field. On exit the current is clockwise. The trailing vertical side is now the one in the field and again carries upward current, producing leftward drag; the horizontal forces reverse and still cancel. When the entire loop is within the uniform region, its flux is constant, so i=0 and every side force is zero under the stated negligible-self-inductance approximation.
For a loop crossing a magnetic field boundary, overlap changes at rate av, hence
∣E∣=Bav,∣i∣=RBav​,Fx​=−RB2a2v​.
(12)
For approximately constant speed this gives two negative rectangular force pulses of duration a/v, separated by a short zero-force interval while the loop is fully inside. Their time integral is
Jx​=−2RB2a2v​va​=−R2B2a3​.​
(13)
The quoted impulse is its magnitude; this impulse of magnetic braking opposes the motion. In fact its independence of speed remains exact within this locally translational sharp-boundary model even if speed changes: each crossing contributes −(B2a2/R)∫vdt=−(B2a2/R)∫dx over distance a.
Write the system's moment of inertia as I. Since dKrot​/dt=τω=Fx​bω=Fx​v, the local energy balance during overlap is
−dtdKrot​​=RB2a2v2​=i2R.
(14)
Thus rotational energy lost is exactly resistive heat in this model. At nearly constant v, each pass gives
ΔKloss​=∣Jx​∣v=R2B2a3v​=2i2Rva​.​
(15)
For a finite speed reduction the exact relation is the integral of i(t)2R, rather than initial speed times impulse; the equality of work and heating still holds. Fringing fields, loop curvature and finite self-inductance smooth the ideal pulses. A practical user control is to vary the electromagnet current, hence B: braking scales as B2.

E=21​mr˙2+Veff​(r),Veff​(r)=V(r)+2mr2L2​.

Stationary points are circular-orbit candidates; a strict local minimum gives radial stability. irrotational flow has ∇×v=0, so locally v=∇Φ; the simply connected fluid domain here permits a globally single-valued velocity potential. Incompressibility gives ∇⋅v=0, hence ∇2Φ=0. In a multiply connected domain, zero local vorticity alone would not rule out circulation and a multivalued potential.
Take k>0 so the disturbance decays at depth. Differentiating the given candidate potential yields
vx​=h0​ωcos(ωt)cos(kx)ekz,vy​=0,vz​=h0​ωcos(ωt)sin(kx)ekz.
(42)
The x and z second derivatives of Φ cancel in the Laplacian. At z=0, vz​=h0​ωcos(ωt)sin(kx)=∂t​Z, so the linearized kinematic free-surface condition holds. This uses kh0​≪1; advection of the displaced surface is higher order.
Since cos2(kx)+sin2(kx)=1, the local squared speed is h02​ω2cos2(ωt)e2kz. The depth integral is therefore
T=2ρh02​ω2​cos2(ωt)∫−∞0​e2kzdz=4kρh02​ω2​cos2(ωt).​
(43)
It is independent of horizontal position; no further spatial average is needed for this kinetic energy. Replacing the upper boundary Z by zero changes it only beyond quadratic order in amplitude.
Relative to the flat surface, the gravitational energy at each horizontal position changes by ρg∫0Z​zdz=ρgZ2/2. Averaging over one wavelength gives
⟨U⟩x​=4ρgh02​​sin2(ωt).​
(44)
The energy of a deep-water standing gravity wave, averaged over wavelength, is T+⟨U⟩x​ and must be constant for a free inviscid standing wave, which requires equal coefficients of the complementary squared sine and cosine:
ω2=gk.​
(45)
As an independent check, the linearized dynamic boundary condition ∂t​Φ+gZ=0 at z=0 gives exactly g−ω2/k=0. Infinite depth, negligible surface tension and no dissipative forcing are part of this gravity-wave model.
