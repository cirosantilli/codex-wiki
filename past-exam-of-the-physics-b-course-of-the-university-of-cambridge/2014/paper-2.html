The Biot-Savart law gives the magnetic field of a steady thin-wire electric current in vacuum:
B(r)=4πμ0​I​∫∣r−r′∣3dℓ′×(r−r′)​.
(8)
The cross product fixes the circulation direction by the right-hand rule; fields from different electric current elements superpose. Integration gives B=μ0​I/(2πr) outside a straight infinite wire and B=μ0​I/(2a) at the centre of a circular loop of radius a. A distributed electric current replaces Idℓ′ by JdV′. This is a magnetostatic law: arbitrary time-dependent electric currents require retarded electromagnetic fields, not instantaneous substitution.

In magnetostatics, Ampère's circuital law in vacuum is
∮C​B⋅dℓ=μ0​Ienc​,∇×B=μ0​J.
(9)
It is especially useful when symmetry makes the tangential field constant on an Amperian loop. It gives B=μ0​I/(2πr) for an infinite straight wire, B≃μ0​nI inside a long solenoid, and B≃μNI/(2πr) in an ideal toroid. The long-solenoid and toroidal expressions neglect fringing.
For time-dependent fields, displacement current is essential:
∮C​B⋅dℓ=μ0​Ienc​+μ0​ϵ0​dtdΦE​​.
(10)
In matter, the corresponding macroscopic form is ∮H⋅dℓ=Ifree​+d∫D⋅dA/dt. The enclosed electric current alone is sufficient only in the magnetostatic limit.

c OurBigBook.com
Because ∇⋅B=0, a magnetic vector potential can be introduced with B=∇×A. It is not unique: A+∇χ gives the same field. In magnetostatics, the Coulomb gauge ∇⋅A=0 gives ∇2A=−μ0​J in vacuum. For localized electric currents,
A(r)=4πμ0​​∫∣r−r′∣J(r′)​dV′.
(11)
A magnetic scalar potential is more restricted. In a simply connected current-free region of constant permeability, write B=−∇ψ; then ∇2ψ=0. Alternatively define the scalar potential for H, differing by a constant permeability factor. A single-valued scalar potential generally cannot cover a domain encircling a net electric current, even where the local curl vanishes. The vector potential does not require the field itself to be curl-free.

Guided electromagnetic waves propagate along a structure whose boundaries select transverse field patterns. At a perfect conductor the tangential electric field vanishes. In a hollow rectangular guide, TE modes have Ez​=0 and TM modes have Hz​=0. Their longitudinal wavenumber obeys
kz2​=v2ω2​−k⊥2​,k⊥2​=(amπ​)2+(bnπ​)2.
(12)
Below the waveguide cutoff frequency ωc​=vk⊥​, that mode is evanescent rather than freely propagating. TM modes require both indices positive; TE modes may have one zero but not both. For a>b, the lowest cutoff is the TE10​ mode, with fc​=v/(2a). A hollow single-conductor guide has no TEM mode, whereas a two-conductor transmission line can support one without this geometrical cutoff.[ref]
For a propagating TE or TM mode, vp​=v/1−ωc2​/ω2​ and vg​=v1−ωc2​/ω2​. The phase velocity can exceed v without superluminal information transfer. Frequency-dependent group delay can broaden pulses; excitation of several modes adds intermodal dispersion.

B7 OurBigBook.com
Electromagnetic induction produces an electromotive force when magnetic flux linkage changes. For an oriented moving loop,
E=∮(E+v×B)⋅dℓ=−dtdΦB​​.
(13)
For a stationary loop this reduces to Faraday's law of induction, ∇×E=−∂t​B. Motion in a static field can instead supply the motional term. Lenz's law gives the minus sign: the induced electric current opposes the flux change, with the required electrical energy supplied by mechanical work or the field source.
For linear fixed-geometry circuits, flux linkages satisfy Λ1​=L1​I1​+MI2​ and Λ2​=MI1​+L2​I2​. Self-inductance gives E1​=−L1​I˙1​; mutual inductance gives the additional term −MI˙2​. Reciprocity gives the same mutual coefficient for both circuits, with its sign determined by winding orientation. The stored field energy is
WB​=21​L1​I12​+21​L2​I22​+MI1​I2​,∣M∣≤L1​L2​​.
(14)
The bound follows from nonnegative energy for arbitrary electric currents. In a linear medium the equivalent field expression is WB​=21​∫B⋅HdV.
An ideal electrical transformer links both windings with one changing core flux, giving V1​/V2​=N1​/N2​ for corresponding voltage magnitudes. A loaded secondary drives an opposing magnetomotive force and increases primary electric current; ideally I1​/I2​=N2​/N1​ and input and output powers agree. A electrical transformer exchanges voltage for electric current, not for extra power. Real devices have winding resistance, leakage flux, eddy-current and hysteresis losses, and finite magnetizing electric current. A steady DC input gives no continuing secondary voltage after the switching transient and may cause excessive primary electric current or core saturation.

An isotropic linear elastic solid has two independent elastic constants. For infinitesimal strain,
σij​=2Gϵij​+λϵkk​δij​,K=λ+32​G.
(15)
The shear modulus G governs shape change and the bulk modulus K governs volume change. Young's modulus E and Poisson's ratio ν are equivalent parameters:
G=2(1+ν)E​,K=3(1−2ν)E​.
(16)
Positive strain energy requires G,K>0, hence −1<ν<1/2; ν→1/2 is the incompressible limit. Small-amplitude bulk waves have speeds cP​=(K+4G/3)/ρ​ and cS​=G/ρ​. Isotropy removes directional dependence, not the distinction between compression and shear. These laws apply within the elastic small-strain regime, not after yielding or fracture.

Iij​=∫ρ(r)(r2δij​−ri​rj​)dV,L=Iω,Trot​=21​ω⋅Iω.

The inertia tensor is real and symmetric, so orthogonal principal axes diagonalize it. Angular momentum is parallel to angular velocity for rotation about a principal axis, but need not be parallel in a general direction. Its eigenvalues are the principal moments of inertia.
If the origin is translated from the centre of mass by a, the parallel-axis theorem becomes Iij​=IijCM​+M(a2δij​−ai​aj​). In body axes, Euler's equation is τ=(dL/dt)body​+ω×L. Thus the full tensor is needed whenever the rotation is not confined to one fixed principal axis.

For two bodies interacting through U(r1​−r2​), define
R=Mm1​r1​+m2​r2​​,r=r1​−r2​,M=m1​+m2​,μ=Mm1​m2​​.

d OurBigBook.com
Dynamic viscosity measures resistance to a velocity gradient. In Newtonian simple shear, τxy​=η∂ux​/∂y; positive viscosity dissipates mechanical energy into heat. Kinematic viscosity is ν=η/ρ. For an incompressible fluid of constant viscosity,
ρ(∂t​u+u⋅∇u)=−∇p+η∇2u+f.
(20)
Scaling velocity by U and length by L gives the Reynolds number Re=ρUL/η=UL/ν, the ratio of inertial to viscous terms. At small Reynolds number, creeping-flow approximations neglect inertia; at large Reynolds number, inertia dominates much of the bulk but viscosity can remain crucial in boundary layers. A large Reynolds number permits instability and turbulence; it does not by itself specify a universal transition threshold. Geometry and disturbances also matter.

B9 OurBigBook.com
A central potential conserves angular momentum and confines a nonradial orbit to a plane. With reduced mass μ, the radial motion obeys
E=21​μr˙2+Ueff​(r),Ueff​=U(r)+2μr2L2​.
(21)
Turning points satisfy E=Ueff​. A radially bounded allowed interval gives bound motion, whereas an interval reaching infinity permits escape. The energy sign alone classifies these only with suitable assumptions about U(∞) and barriers. Circular orbits satisfy Ueff′​=0 and are radially stable when Ueff′′​>0. Bound orbits in a general central potential need not close: successive periapses can precess.
For attractive inverse-square gravity, U=−κ/r and
r=1+ecosϕp​,p=μκL2​,e2=1+μκ22EL2​.
(22)
Ellipses have E<0, e<1, and the attracting centre at a focus; parabolic and hyperbolic escape have e=1 and e>1. Conservation of angular momentum gives equal swept areas in equal times. The inverse-square law additionally yields T2=4π2a3/[G(m1​+m2​)]. These are Kepler's laws; a harmonic central potential is another example with closed bound orbits, while generic central laws produce precession.
In a gravitational assist, a spacecraft's asymptotic speed relative to a planet is approximately unchanged but its direction turns. Transforming to the frame in which the planet has velocity V gives
Δε=V⋅(uout​−uin​).
(23)
The spacecraft can gain or lose orbital energy; the planet supplies the compensating energy and momentum, with a very small velocity change because of its large mass.
Repulsive Coulomb scattering instead has U=κC​/r. For incident relative kinetic energy E and impact parameter b, the hyperbolic orbit gives
b=2EκC​​cot2θ​,dΩdσ​=(4EκC​​)2csc42θ​.​
(24)
This Rutherford scattering formula follows by equating the incident annulus 2πbdb to the scattered solid-angle ring. It assumes classical elastic scattering by a point Coulomb centre; screening, finite nuclear size or quantum effects can change the applicable regime.

C10 OurBigBook.com
A heat engine operates cyclically, absorbing heat Qh​ from a hotter source, rejecting heat Qc​ to a colder sink and delivering net work W=Qh​−Qc​. Its efficiency is η=W/Qh​. The working substance returns to its original state; the reservoirs and surroundings need not.

a

Words: 70 Articles: 1
C10 OurBigBook.com
Solution
Words: 70
a OurBigBook.com
Suppose an engine were more efficient than a reversible Carnot engine between the same reservoirs. Couple it to a reversed Carnot engine, scaling the cycles to have equal work. With no net external work, their combined effect would be to transfer heat from the colder reservoir to the hotter one, contradicting the Second law of thermodynamics. No such engine can exceed ηC​=1−Tc​/Th​. Equivalently, the cyclic entropy inequality gives −Qh​/Th​+Qc​/Tc​≥0, hence the same bound.

b OurBigBook.com
The reversible working substance changes entropy by Qh​/Th​ on its hot isotherm and −Qc​/Tc​ on its cold isotherm, with zero change on the two reversible adiabats. Closure requires Qh​/Th​=Qc​/Tc​. The substance has zero entropy change over a cycle, while the reservoirs have changes −Qh​/Th​ and Qc​/Tc​. Thus ΔSuniverse​=0 for the complete Carnot cycle.

In the canonical ensemble, Z(β)=∑i​e−βEi​ normalizes the probabilities pi​=e−βEi​/Z. For temperature-independent levels,
E=−Z1​dβdZ​=−dβdlnZ​,C=dTdE​=−kB​T21​dβdE​.​
(39)
The derivative holds external parameters, including magnetic field, fixed.
For one independent symmetric three-level system put ϵ=μB and x=βϵ. Then Z=1+2coshx and
E=−1+2coshx2ϵsinhx​.​
(40)
At nonzero fixed field, the ground state dominates as T→0, so E→−μ∣B∣. At high temperature all three levels become nearly equally populated and their mean tends to zero from below. At fixed positive temperature the energy is even in B, is quadratic near zero field, and approaches −μ∣B∣ at large ∣B∣ as the lowest level becomes occupied. The heat capacity vanishes both when excitations are frozen out and when populations are almost equal, with a positive Schottky anomaly between those limits.
At ∣x∣≪1, expanding the hyperbolic functions gives
E≃−3kB​T2μ2B2​,C≃3kB​T22μ2B2​.​
(41)
Interpreting the stated heat capacity on the same one-atom basis as the preceding calculation, its tail coefficient gives
μ=B1​23akB​​​≃2.89×10−5eVT−1=4.64×10−24JT−1≃0.500μB​.​
(42)
Here kB​=8.61733×10−5eVK−1 is used consistently with a in electronvolt kelvin. For a sample of N independent atoms, the total coefficient would be N times the one-atom coefficient; it must first be divided by N.
Finally, two derivatives of Z supply the second energy moment:
E2=ZZ′′​,dβdE​=−(ZZ′′​−Z2Z′2​)=−Var(E).
(43)
Combining this with the heat-capacity derivative yields the canonical energy fluctuation relation
C=kB​T2Var(E)​,ΔE=kB​T2C​.​
(44)
It also explains why this canonical heat capacity is nonnegative.[ref]

C13 OurBigBook.com
Molecules. In a dilute classical gas, collisions establish a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution. The normalized speed distribution and its first two moments are
f(v)=4π(2πkB​Tm​)3/2v2e−mv2/(2kB​T),v=πm8kB​T​​,v2=m3kB​T​.
(45)
Momentum transfer to a wall gives p=nmv2/3=nkB​T. Translational internal energy is U=3NkB​T/2; rotational and vibrational degrees of freedom add energy when thermally populated, without changing the dilute ideal-gas pressure law. Classical equipartition gives kB​T/2 per quadratic contribution, but quantum level spacing can freeze out modes.
Only the molecules moving towards a surface contribute to its one-sided number flux:
J=41​nv=2πmkB​T​p​.
(46)
For an effusive molecular energy flux, faster molecules are preferentially sampled, giving mean translational energy 2kB​T per crossing molecule and FE​=2kB​TJ. This differs from multiplying J by the bulk mean 3kB​T/2. Collisions and finite molecular interactions produce transport and departures from the ideal-gas equation of state.
Photons. All photons travel at c in vacuum, so their thermal distribution is over frequency, not over a range of speeds. The two polarizations and Bose-Einstein occupation with zero chemical potential give
uν​dν=c38πhν3​ehν/(kB​T)−1dν​,u≡∫0∞​uν​dν=aR​T4,aR​=15h3c38π5kB4​​.
(47)
Here the spectral quantity is uν​ and the total energy density is u. Momentum flux gives p=u/3, while isotropic angular averaging gives the one-sided energy flux FE​=cu/4=σSB​T4 through a small aperture of a black-body cavity. The photon number flux is similarly cnγ​/4.
Both pressures arise from momentum transfer, but their equations of state differ: a classical molecular gas has pV=NkB​T with fixed molecule number, whereas a photon gas has pV=U/3, U=aR​VT4 and an equilibrium photon number that changes with temperature. Photon creation and absorption by the walls are essential to the zero-chemical-potential equilibrium description.
