A1 OurBigBook.com
For rolling without slipping through height h, conservation of energy gives
mgh=21​mv2+21​I(v/R)2,v2=1+I/(mR2)2gh​.​
(1)
A uniform solid sphere has I/(mR2)=2/5, a solid cylinder 1/2, and a pipe (1+rin2​/R2)/2>1/2. Thus the ball is fastest, then the solid cylinder, then the pipe. A smaller moment of inertia leaves more energy in translation. This assumes release from rest through the same height, adequate static friction and negligible rolling resistance; a thin pipe has v2=gh.

For isotropic planar motion, the planar effusion flux gives an escape rate through a gap of width w is Γ=w(N/A)⟨v⟩/π, where A is tray area. At fixed equilibrium kinetic energy, or fixed shape of the scaled speed distribution, v∝m−1/2. Hence halving the mass increases the rate by 2​. This planar effusion result assumes the same spatial density and an unrestricted small opening. Strictly, average energy alone fixes ⟨v2⟩, not ⟨v⟩; an arbitrary change of distribution shape would need more information.

B6 OurBigBook.com
Specifying potential on the complete boundary gives a Dirichlet boundary condition and a unique harmonic solution. Specifying its normal derivative gives a Neumann boundary condition, with the zero-net-flux compatibility condition and an undetermined additive constant. Mixed conditions on complementary portions also give uniqueness when a potential reference is fixed. In an exterior domain, conditions at infinity are needed. These statements follow by applying Green's first identity to the difference of two solutions. A metal in electrostatic equilibrium is an equipotential: Φ=V0​, tangential electric field zero, and normal field En​=σ/ϵ0​ just outside a vacuum interface.
For the conducting-wedge potential expansion, put u=Φ−V0​ in the empty sector 0<θ<α. For u=rνΨ(θ), Laplace's equation gives Ψ′′+ν2Ψ=0. Vanishing values on both faces require Ψ∝sin(nπθ/α), hence
Φ=V0​+n=1∑∞​(an​rnπ/α+bn​r−nπ/α)sinαnπθ​.​
(6)
Bounded potential at the corner sets bn​=0. The coefficients still require outer boundary data; the metal potential alone cannot select them. For a generic nonzero a1​, put p=π/α. Differentiation gives
Er​≃−pa1​rp−1sinpθ,Eθ​≃−pa1​rp−1cospθ.​
(7)
If a1​=0, the first nonzero higher harmonic replaces this leading term. For α=π/2, u≃2a1​xy, so E=−2a1​(y,x) and field lines satisfy x2−y2=constant, meeting the metal faces normally and tending to zero field at the corner. For α=3π/2, the electrostatic corner-field exponent is −1/3. A real tip's rounding cuts off this divergence; breakdown or field emission can limit the attainable field.

Applying Gauss's law for magnetism to a pillbox and Ampère's law to an interface-crossing rectangle gives
n^⋅(B2​−B1​)=0,n^×(H2​−H1​)=Kf​.​
(8)
Here n^ points from material 1 to 2 and Kf​ is free surface current. With no such current, Bn​ and Ht​ are continuous. For linear isotropic media, Bt2​/μ2​=Bt1​/μ1​ and μ2​Hn2​=μ1​Hn1​. The magnetic fields need not have equal magnitudes on either side.
With negligible leakage, Gauss's law for magnetism makes each cross-section of a magnetic flux tube carry the same flux F. High permeability guides it through the core; a short gap permits neglect of fringing. Flux conservation across these particular geometric sections is an approximation, not a consequence of zero divergence without controlling side leakage.
Let L be the constant-radius arc marked in the figure, and μr​=μ/μ0​. For each straight taper, the radius changes linearly from R to r, giving the reluctance of a conical magnetic pole
∫taper​μπa(x)2dx​=μπRrs​.
(9)
Summing magnetic reluctances to find the gap field of a tapered electromagnet, and using Bg​=F/(πr2) gives
NI=F(μπR2L​+μπRr2s​+μ0​πr2h​),Hg​=h+μr−1​(Lr2/R2+2sr/R)NI​,Bg​=μ0​Hg​.​
(10)
If L is instead defined as the total magnetic-material length, replace the displayed uniform-arc length by L−2s. The comparison untapered core has uniform radius R and material length L+2s, so
Hg0​Hg​​=h+(Lr2/R2+2sr/R)/μr​h+(L+2s)/μr​​.​
(11)
The original PDF specifies s/L≪r/R; the TeX transcription reverses this inequality. In the printed limit the taper reluctances are small relative to that of the broad arc. The gain is approximately [h+L/μr​]/[h+Lr2/(μr​R2)], approaching (R/r)2 if the gap reluctance is also negligible. If the gap dominates, both fields instead approach NI/h and the gain approaches one. magnetic saturation near the small poles, gap fringing, leakage and winding heating limit practical enhancement.

∂x​Ey​=−μ0​∂t​Hz​,J=(0,−∂x​Hz​−ϵ0​∂t​Ey​,0).​
(13)

Z is the terminating load impedance, Z0​ the line's characteristic impedance, and k the propagation wave number; l is its length. In the lossless convention printed, an open circuit gives Zin​=−iZ0​cotkl, a short circuit gives iZ0​tankl, and a matched load Z=Z0​ gives Zin​=Z0​ for every length. The divergent or zero limits at isolated electrical lengths are understood continuously.
Put θ=kl, cθ​=cosθ, sθ​=sinθ. The voltage/current matrix of one section is
Mj​=(cθ​isθ​/Zj​​iZj​sθ​cθ​​).
(17)
For a pair beginning with Z1​, write M1​M2​=(AC​BD​). Its entries are A=cθ2​−(Z1​/Z2​)sθ2​, D=cθ2​−(Z2​/Z1​)sθ2​, B=i(Z1​+Z2​)sθ​cθ​, and C=i(1/Z1​+1/Z2​)sθ​cθ​. The alternating-impedance transmission line has unchanged input X, its Bloch impedance, obeying X=(AX+B)/(CX+D). Hence, away from the removable dividing zeros,
iX2(Z1​+Z2​)+X(Z12​−Z22​)tanθ−iZ1​Z2​(Z1​+Z2​)=0,
(18)
X=2i(Z1​−Z2​)tanθ ±4Z1​Z2​−(Z1​−Z2​)2tan2θ​​.​
(19)
For real positive Z1​,Z2​, select the branch carrying energy into the line or decaying into it. A positive real part selects the pass-band root; infinitesimal passive loss resolves the stop-band branch. At θ=mπ the cell matrix is the identity, so the exact fixed-point condition alone does not determine X; at odd quarter-wave lengths the appropriate limiting impedance can be zero or infinite.
In a transmission-line stop band, a lossless semi-infinite ladder totally reflects because its physical input is reactive. Equivalently its cell has

A fictitious force accounts for acceleration of the chosen reference frame rather than an additional interaction. For axes rotating at constant Ω, differentiating a vector uses (d/dt)lab​=(d/dt)rot​+Ω×. Twice applying this gives
alab​=a+2Ω×v+Ω×(Ω×r),
(22)
so moving the last two terms to the force side produces the Coriolis and centrifugal forces. Here v is measured in rotating axes.
The rotating saddle trap has potential force (−mω2x,+mω2y). Therefore
x¨=(Ω2−ω2)x+2Ωy˙​,y¨​=(Ω2+ω2)y−2Ωx˙.
(23)
The trial solution gives
(ϵ2+Ω2)2−ω4−4Ω2ϵ2=0,ϵ±2​=Ω2±ω2.​
(24)
Both modes are bounded for arbitrary sufficiently small initial displacements and velocities precisely when ∣Ω∣>ω. At equality there is a zero-frequency secular mode; the exactly stationary solution does not establish stability.
For rapidly rotating saddle confinement with Ω≫ω>0, real mode ellipses can be written
(x,y)+​=A(cosϵ+​t,−ϵ+​Ω​sinϵ+​t),(x,y)−​=B(cosϵ−​t,−Ωϵ−​​sinϵ−​t).
(25)
They are nearly circular clockwise motions at rates ϵ±​≃Ω±ω2/(2Ω). Rotating these axes back to the laboratory cancels the large angular rate: the leading laboratory motion is a slow approximately harmonic ellipse with frequency ω2/(2Ω), plus small rapid micromotion. It results from the changing saddle force, not a static potential minimum. At next order the ellipse precesses prograde at ω4/(8Ω3). The rotating-potential model and this guiding-centre distinction are analysed in the primary work cited here.[ref]

At distance y from the neutral axis, bending strain has magnitude y/R and the corresponding stress is Ey/R. Its moment is therefore
B=∫A​yREy​dA=REI​,I=∫A​y2dA.​
(30)
The second moment of area is about the neutral bending axis and has units of length to the fourth power. This assumes a slender linear-elastic beam, small strains and plane sections remaining plane, not necessarily small deflections.
For horizontal compression F>0, moment balance across a short arc gives dB/ds=−Fdy/ds=−Fsinθ. Since B=EIθ′, the exact-angle Euler elastica equation and its first integral are
EIθ′′+Fsinθ=0,2EI​θ′2−Fcosθ=C.​
(31)
The first integral follows by multiplying by θ′ and integrating; no small-angle replacement has been made.
For tension set F=−T. The straight far-field ends give C=T, so on the increasing-angle tensile elastica loop branch
θ′=2EIT​​sin2θ​,lntan4θ​=sEIT​​.
(32)

θ(s)=4arctaneαs,α=T/(EI)​.​

In irrotational flow, the velocity has a velocity potential locally, and globally in a simply connected flow domain. incompressibility then gives ∇⋅v=∇2Φ=0. For a sphere moving at speed U along z through fluid at rest far away, the potential of a translating sphere is a decaying axisymmetric dipole. Its solution and its impermeability condition give
Φ=r2βcosθ​,∂r​Φ∣a​=Ucosθ,β=−21​Ua3.​
(35)
These are laboratory fluid velocities expressed relative to a moving origin. A velocity field measured in the sphere-rest frame instead requires adding −Urcosθ to the potential.
The vector identity printed in the PDF has its signs reversed. The product rule gives the consistent identity v2=∇⋅(Φv)−Φ∇⋅v. With outward normal of the exterior fluid equal to −r^ at the sphere, the divergence theorem yields
∫r>a​v2dV=∫r=a​Φ(−vr​)dS=2U2a​∫cos2θdS=32πa3U2​.
(36)
The integral at infinity vanishes. Hence
Kf​=3πρ0​a3U2​=21​ma​U2,ma​=32πρ0​a3​,meff​=34πa3​(ρ+ρ0​/2).​
(37)
This added mass of a sphere is half the displaced fluid mass. For a spring of unchanged stiffness, buoyancy only shifts the static equilibrium. Its oscillation frequency with fluid added mass is
ωs′​=ωs​ρ+ρ0​/2ρ​​.​
(38)
For a small-angle simple pendulum, buoyancy also reduces restoring torque from mgℓθ to (m−ρ0​V)gℓθ, while the inertia is (m+ma​)ℓ2. Thus
ωp′​=ωp​ρ+ρ0​/2ρ−ρ0​​​.​
(39)
The downward equilibrium is stable only for ρ>ρ0​. The ideal results assume unbounded fluid, negligible viscosity, small excursions, and no important free-surface or container corrections.
