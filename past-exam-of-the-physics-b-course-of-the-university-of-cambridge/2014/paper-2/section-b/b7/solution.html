Electromagnetic induction produces an electromotive force when magnetic flux linkage changes. For an oriented moving loop,
E=∮(E+v×B)⋅dℓ=−dtdΦB​​.
(13)
For a stationary loop this reduces to Faraday's law of induction, ∇×E=−∂t​B. Motion in a static field can instead supply the motional term. Lenz's law gives the minus sign: the induced electric current opposes the flux change, with the required electrical energy supplied by mechanical work or the field source.
For linear fixed-geometry circuits, flux linkages satisfy Λ1​=L1​I1​+MI2​ and Λ2​=MI1​+L2​I2​. Self-inductance gives E1​=−L1​I˙1​; mutual inductance gives the additional term −MI˙2​. Reciprocity gives the same mutual coefficient for both circuits, with its sign determined by winding orientation. The stored field energy is
WB​=21​L1​I12​+21​L2​I22​+MI1​I2​,∣M∣≤L1​L2​​.
(14)
The bound follows from nonnegative energy for arbitrary electric currents. In a linear medium the equivalent field expression is WB​=21​∫B⋅HdV.
An ideal electrical transformer links both windings with one changing core flux, giving V1​/V2​=N1​/N2​ for corresponding voltage magnitudes. A loaded secondary drives an opposing magnetomotive force and increases primary electric current; ideally I1​/I2​=N2​/N1​ and input and output powers agree. A electrical transformer exchanges voltage for electric current, not for extra power. Real devices have winding resistance, leakage flux, eddy-current and hysteresis losses, and finite magnetizing electric current. A steady DC input gives no continuing secondary voltage after the switching transient and may cause excessive primary electric current or core saturation.
