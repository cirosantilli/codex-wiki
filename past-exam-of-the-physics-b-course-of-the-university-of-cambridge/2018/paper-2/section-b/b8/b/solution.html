 ... Past exam of the physics B course of the University of Cambridge 2018 Paper 2 Section B B8 b
OurBigBook.com Words: 207
Let a coordinate frame rotate with angular velocity Ω relative to an inertial frame. For any vector u,
(dtdu​)inertial​=(dtdu​)rotating​+Ω×u.
(35)
Applying this twice to position, with coincident nonaccelerating origins, gives
ainertial​=arotating​+2Ω×vrotating​+Ω×(Ω×r)+Ω˙×r.
(36)
Newton's second law written using rotating coordinates therefore contains the Coriolis force
FC​=−2mΩ×vrotating​.​
(37)
The other inertial corrections are the centrifugal force and, for changing rotation rate, the Euler force. An accelerating origin introduces an additional translational fictitious force.
The Coriolis force depends on velocity and is perpendicular to it, so FC​⋅vrotating​=0: the Coriolis force does no work in the rotating frame. It bends trajectories without directly changing their rotating-frame speed. It is absent for motion parallel to the rotation axis.
For locally horizontal motion on Earth at latitude λ, take x east, y north and z up. Keeping the vertical component Ωsinλ gives
v˙x​=fvy​,v˙y​=−fvx​,f=2Ωsinλ.
(38)
Horizontal motion is deflected right in the northern hemisphere and left in the southern hemisphere. In the ideal absence of other horizontal forces these equations give inertial circles with angular frequency ∣f∣. The same velocity-dependent correction explains the precession of a Foucault pendulum, whose precession-rate magnitude is Ω∣sinλ∣. Terrestrial vertical motions also involve Earth's horizontal rotation component, so the reduced horizontal equations are not the full force law.
