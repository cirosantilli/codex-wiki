From molecular dipoles to macroscopic fields. A dielectric contains charges which remain bound to its atoms or molecules. An applied electric field displaces positive and negative charge centres and can orient permanent electric dipoles. The electric polarization P is electric dipole moment per unit volume, in Cm−2. Its associated bound charge is
ρb​=−∇⋅P,σb​=P⋅n
(25)
at the boundary of the polarized material, where n points outward. A uniform electric polarization has no volume bound charge, but generally has surface bound charge. The electric field remains the force per unit test charge; it includes the fields of both free and bound charges.
Introducing the electric displacement field D=ϵ0​E+P packages the free-charge source separately:
∇⋅E=ϵ0​ρf​+ρb​​,∇⋅D=ρf​,∇×E=0.
(26)
For a homogeneous linear isotropic dielectric,
P=ϵ0​χe​E,D=ϵE,ϵ=ϵ0​(1+χe​)=ϵ0​ϵr​.
(27)
The electric susceptibility χe​ is dimensionless; the permittivity ϵ has units Fm−1. This is a constitutive approximation. An anisotropic dielectric requires a permittivity tensor, while a nonlinear dielectric need not have electric polarization proportional to electric field. The field in these equations is the macroscopic field, not necessarily the local field at an individual molecule.
Interfaces. Let n point from medium 1 into medium 2. Integrating Gauss's law over a thin pillbox, and the electrostatic curl equation around a thin loop, gives the electrostatic interface boundary conditions
n⋅(D2​−D1​)=σf​,n×(E2​−E1​)=0.​
(28)
Thus normal electric displacement is continuous when there is no free surface charge, whereas tangential electric field is continuous even if the permittivity changes. Normal electric field generally jumps because the interface carries bound charge. The electric potential is continuous for an ordinary interface without an ideal dipole sheet. These conditions, together with Laplace's equation in each homogeneous charge-free region, determine the field.
Energy and electrical sources. Reversibly assembling free charge requires incremental work δW=∫E⋅δDdV. For a linear dielectric whose response can be reversibly integrated from zero field, the electrostatic energy is
W=21​∫E⋅DdV.​
(29)
This includes the material polarization response, rather than simply using ϵ0​E2/2 everywhere. In a capacitor, W=Q2/(2C)=CV2/2. Inserting a dielectric increases capacitance. At fixed charge the stored energy decreases. At fixed voltage the stored field energy increases, but the voltage source supplies VdQ; the appropriate mechanical energy including that source changes by −V2dC/2. Both cases predict attraction of dielectric into the capacitor. A force calculation must state which electrical quantity is fixed. A lossy or hysteretic dielectric also dissipates energy, so the reversible quadratic expression does not describe its full history.
Shape and depolarization. Suppose the dielectric is surrounded by vacuum and the distant imposed electric field is E0​. Bound surface charge produces a depolarization field. For a uniformly polarized ellipsoid, along a principal axis,
Ein​=E0​−ϵ0​Nd​P​,P=ϵ0​(ϵr​−1)Ein​,Ein​=1+Nd​(ϵr​−1)E0​​.​
(30)
Here Nd​ is the depolarization factor of a dielectric ellipsoid. This self-consistent field explains why the same material responds differently in different shapes.
An infinite slab with electric field normal to its faces has Nd​=1, giving Ein​=E0​/ϵr​; its opposite surface bound charges reduce the internal field. For electric field parallel to the faces, Nd​=0, giving Ein​=E0​. End effects are neglected.
A very long circular rod in an axial electric field has negligible depolarization away from its ends, so Ein​≃E0​. In a transverse electric field the infinite-cylinder solution has Nd​=1/2, giving Ein​=2E0​/(ϵr​+1). To see the latter directly, take the applied field along x. The interior and exterior electric potentials can be written ϕin​=−Brcosθ and ϕout​=−E0​rcosθ+Acosθ/r. Continuity of potential and normal electric displacement at r=a gives B=2E0​/(ϵr​+1).
For a sphere of radius a, Nd​=1/3 and
Ein​=ϵr​+23​E0​,p=4πϵ0​a3ϵr​+2ϵr​−1​E0​.​
(31)
For example, write ϕin​=−Brcosθ and ϕout​=−E0​rcosθ+Ccosθ/r2. Continuity of potential gives B=E0​−C/a3, and continuity of normal electric displacement gives ϵB=ϵ0​(E0​+2C/a3). Solving yields B=3E0​/(ϵr​+2) and C=a3E0​(ϵr​−1)/(ϵr​+2). The 1/r2 potential is that of the electric dipole moment above. For ϵr​=1 all these induced effects vanish; as ϵr​ becomes very large the normal-field sphere and slab approach the corresponding conductor field exclusion. The ideal infinitely long rod in an exactly axial imposed field has a different, singular end-free limit.
