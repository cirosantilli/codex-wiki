Integral conservation. Electric charge cannot be created by ordinary transport. For a fixed volume V, outward current reduces the enclosed charge:
dtd​∫V​ρe​dV=−∮∂V​J⋅ndA.
(16)
Here ρe​ has units Cm−3 and J has units Am−2. Applying the divergence theorem to an arbitrary volume gives the local continuity equation
∂t∂ρe​​+∇⋅J=0.​
(17)
It is local charge conservation: a net outflow must be accompanied by depletion. In a steady state, ∇⋅J=0. Integrating over a small circuit junction then gives Kirchhoff's current law. During a transient the junction can accumulate charge and the incoming and outgoing conduction currents need not yet match.
Consistency with Maxwell's equations. In vacuum, take the divergence of the Ampère-Maxwell equation:
∇×B=μ0​J+μ0​ϵ0​∂t​E,0=μ0​∇⋅J+μ0​ϵ0​∂t​(∇⋅E).
(18)
Gauss's law, ∇⋅E=ρe​/ϵ0​, reproduces the continuity equation. The displacement current term makes these equations consistent for time-dependent charge distributions, such as a charging capacitor. It is not a stream of free charges across the insulating gap. In matter, ∇⋅D=ρf​ and ∇×H=Jf​+∂t​D give the analogous conservation law for free charge; total charge also includes bound charge.
