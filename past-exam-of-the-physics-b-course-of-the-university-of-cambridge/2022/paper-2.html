B6 OurBigBook.com
Newton's second law takes its familiar form ma=F in an inertial frame. Axes fixed to a turning apparatus or to the Earth form a rotating reference frame. Extra inertial forces account for the acceleration of these coordinates. Their values depend on the chosen reference frame; physical interactions such as gravity, tension and contact forces must still be included separately.
Let the rotating origin have inertial acceleration A, and let its axes have angular velocity Ω(t). For any vector q, the rotating-frame derivative formula is
(dtdq​)I​=(dtdq​)R​+Ω×q.
(17)
Each rotating basis vector changes by Ω×ei​, which proves this rule by expanding q in that basis. Apply it twice to the position relative to the moving origin. With v′ and a′ measured in the rotating axes,
aI​=A+a′+2Ω×v′+Ω˙×r+Ω×(Ω×r).
(18)
Consequently the equation in the rotating reference frame is
ma′=F−mA−2mΩ×v′−mΩ˙×r−mΩ×(Ω×r).
(19)
The additional terms are the translational inertial force, Coriolis force, Euler force and centrifugal force, respectively. The last two simplify when the angular velocity is constant: the Euler force vanishes, while the other rotation terms remain.
The centrifugal force points away from the rotation axis. If r⊥​ is the perpendicular displacement from that axis, then
Fcf​=mΩ2r⊥​.​
(20)
For uniform rotation it has potential energy
Φcf​=−21​m∣Ω×r∣2,Fcf​=−∇Φcf​.
(21)
A mass at rest relative to a turntable can therefore be described as having an inward tension or friction balancing its outward centrifugal force. In an inertial frame, the inward physical force instead produces the required centripetal acceleration. These are two descriptions of the same motion; one must not insert the rotating-frame outward term into the inertial equation as an additional physical interaction.
A rotating liquid gives a useful quantitative example. For a liquid in hydrostatic equilibrium relative to a vessel rotating uniformly about the vertical axis,
∂s∂p​=ρΩ2s,∂z∂p​=−ρg.
(22)
Along the free surface the pressure is constant, so Ω2sds−gdz=0 and
z(s)=z(0)+2gΩ2s2​.
(23)
The surface is a elliptic paraboloid. This is the balance of gravity and the centrifugal force in the rotating description. On the Earth, the same outward acceleration reduces effective gravity, most strongly at the equator, and contributes to the planet's equatorial bulge.
The Coriolis force depends on relative motion:
Fcor​=−2mΩ×v′.​
(24)
It is perpendicular to both Ω and v′, vanishes for motion along the rotation axis, and reverses when the relative velocity reverses. The dot product gives
Fcor​⋅v′=0.
(25)
Thus the Coriolis force does no work in the rotating reference frame: it changes the direction of motion rather than directly changing the rotating-frame kinetic energy. For example, on a horizontal turntable rotating anticlockwise as viewed from above, an outward-moving slider has a clockwise Coriolis acceleration, whereas an inward-moving slider has an anticlockwise one. The deflection can be observed by comparing a laboratory camera with a camera rotating with the table.
For slow horizontal motion on the Earth at latitude λ, keep the vertical component ΩE​sinλ of its angular velocity. In east-north coordinates (u,v) the horizontal Coriolis acceleration is
(aE​,aN​)=(fv,−fu),f=2ΩE​sinλ.
(26)
It deflects motion to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern hemisphere. In large-scale atmospheric and oceanic motion it can balance a horizontal pressure gradient, giving geostrophic balance. With z^ upward, the steady horizontal equation without friction is
fz^×v=−ρ1​∇h​p.
(27)
The resulting velocity runs along lines of equal pressure. This approximation requires rotation to dominate local inertial changes; it is inappropriate close to the equator where f tends to zero.
The Foucault pendulum gives another terrestrial example. In the small-angle approximation, its horizontal coordinates satisfy
x¨+ω02​x=fy˙​,y¨​+ω02​y=−fx˙,ω02​=ℓg​.
(28)
To first order in the small ratio ΩE​/ω0​, the oscillation plane precesses relative to the ground at angular rate −ΩE​sinλ: clockwise in the northern hemisphere, zero at the equator, and one revolution per sidereal day at a pole. The Coriolis force explains this changing orientation without requiring energy input. Together these examples show why inertial forces are useful whenever the apparatus or environment supplies naturally rotating coordinates.

The method of images solves certain electrostatics problems by replacing a conductor by fictitious electric charges outside the region where the solution is required. The replacement must satisfy the actual boundary values of the electric potential as well as the prescribed sources. The uniqueness of Poisson equation then establishes that the constructed electric potential equals the physical one in that region. The image locations are mathematical devices; they are not real charges inside the metal.
For a point charge q at (0,0,a) above an infinite grounded conducting plane z=0, put an image charge −q at (0,0,−a). In a cylindrical coordinate system with coordinates (s,z) the potential in the physical region is
ϕ(s,z)=4πϵ0​q​[s2+(z−a)2​1​−s2+(z+a)2​1​],z>0.
(29)
This vanishes at z=0, has the required singularity at the real charge, and tends to zero at infinity. With electric field E=−∇ϕ, the induced surface charge density follows from the normal field immediately above the conductor:
σ(s)=ϵ0​Ez​(s,0+)=−2π(s2+a2)3/2qa​,∫0∞​σ(s)2πsds=−q.
(30)
The force on q is the field of the image evaluated at the real charge:
F=−16πϵ0​a2q2​z^.​
(31)
The electrostatic energy relative to infinite separation is U=21​qϕimage​=−q2/(16πϵ0​a). The factor 1/2 accounts for the fact that the induced charge changes as the real charge is assembled; treating the image as an independently fixed physical charge would give the wrong energy.
A second standard geometry is a grounded conducting sphere of radius b with a charge q a distance d>b from its centre. Its exterior field is generated by an image q′=−qb/d at distance b2/d from the centre on the line towards the real charge. On the sphere the distances to the real and image charges are in the ratio d/b, making their potentials cancel. For an isolated neutral sphere, an additional central image +qb/d makes the total induced charge zero and leaves the surface an equipotential. The construction is powerful for suitable symmetric boundaries, but arbitrary boundaries do not in general admit a finite image system.

An electrical insulator has no freely conducting bulk charge in the ideal static description, but an applied electric field can displace bound positive and negative charge relative to one another. The resulting electric dipole moments per unit volume define the polarization density P. Distortion of electronic charge clouds, relative displacement of ions, and partial alignment of permanent molecular electric dipoles can all contribute. Thermal agitation opposes the last mechanism.
For a weak field in a linear isotropic dielectric, the constitutive law is
P=ϵ0​χe​E,D=ϵ0​E+P=ϵE,ϵ=ϵ0​(1+χe​)=ϵ0​ϵr​.
(32)
Here χe​ is the electric susceptibility, ϵ is the permittivity, and D is the electric displacement field. In an anisotropic material, χe​ is a tensor and P need not be parallel to E. These response laws describe the macroscopic field, not necessarily the microscopic field acting on one molecule.
Spatial variation and surface termination of the polarization density give bound charge and bound current through the bound charge densities
ρb​=−∇⋅P,σb​=P⋅n^,
(33)
where n^ is outward from the material. Thus uniform polarization density has no bulk bound charge, but generally has opposite bound charges on opposing surfaces. Gauss's law can be rewritten as
∇⋅D=ρfree​.
(34)
With no free surface charge, the normal component of D is continuous across an interface; the tangential electric field is continuous in electrostatics.
For example, fill a parallel-plate capacitor of plate area A and gap d with a uniform dielectric, neglecting fringing. At fixed free plate charge Q, D=Q/A, so
E=ϵAQ​,V=Ed,C=dϵA​=ϵr​C0​.​
(35)
The induced bound surface charges oppose the original field, reducing the electric field and potential difference at fixed Q. At fixed applied potential difference, the field remains V/d and the battery instead supplies more free charge. The electric displacement field separates these free-charge and bound-charge roles.

An incident electromagnetic wave generally produces reflected and transmitted waves because the two media have different electromagnetic response. Consider a flat interface between homogeneous, linear, lossless media with permittivities ϵj​ and magnetic permeabilities μj​. Their wave speeds and wave impedances are
vj​=μj​ϵj​​1​,Zj​=ϵj​μj​​​.
(42)
For each plane electromagnetic wave, H=k^×E/Zj​. With no free surface charge or current, the interface conditions for electromagnetic fields require continuous tangential E,H and normal D,B. Equality of the phase at every point on the boundary gives equal angular frequencies and tangential wavevectors. Hence the reflection angle equals the incidence angle and Snell's law is
n1​sinθi​=n2​sinθt​,
(43)
where nj​=c/vj​ and each angle is measured from the normal.
At normal incidence use the same tangential axis for all electric amplitudes. The two independent boundary equations are
Ei​+Er​=Et​,Z1​Ei​−Er​​=Z2​Et​​.
(44)
Solving gives the amplitude Fresnel equations
r=Ei​Er​​=Z2​+Z1​Z2​−Z1​​,t=Ei​Et​​=Z1​+Z2​2Z2​​.​
(45)
For nonmagnetic media, these become r=(n1​−n2​)/(n1​+n2​) and t=2n1​/(n1​+n2​). Reflection into a medium of larger refractive index therefore reverses the electric amplitude's sign at normal incidence.
The normal Poynting vector determines the reflected and transmitted fractions of incident power. At normal incidence,
R=∣r∣2,T=Z2​Z1​​∣t∣2,R+T=1.​
(46)
It is the power fractions, rather than the squared electric amplitudes alone, that add to unity.
At oblique incidence separate transverse electric polarization (s, electric field perpendicular to the incidence plane) from transverse magnetic polarization (p, electric field in that plane). For nonmagnetic media, writing ci​=cosθi​ and ct​=cosθt​, the Fresnel equations are
rs​=n1​ci​+n2​ct​n1​ci​−n2​ct​​,ts​=n1​ci​+n2​ct​2n1​ci​​,
(47)
rp​=n2​ci​+n1​ct​n2​ci​−n1​ct​​,tp​=n2​ci​+n1​ct​2n1​ci​​.
(48)
Here choose a common s direction and, for each ray, the p direction as s^×k^. This makes the reflected p basis reverse its tangential direction at normal incidence, explaining why the displayed rp​ then has the opposite sign to rs​. Physical reflectance is independent of this sign convention. For either polarization with propagating transmission,
R=∣r∣2,T=n1​cosθi​n2​cosθt​​∣t∣2.
(49)
For p polarization, zero reflection occurs at the Brewster angle tanθB​=n2​/n1​. If n1​>n2​, incidence above the critical angle for total internal reflection sinθc​=n2​/n1​ produces an evanescent field in total internal reflection and R=1 for an isolated lossless interface. A lossy second medium requires complex response coefficients and an absorption term in the energy balance.

Use an ideal gas to illustrate the Carnot cycle, with hot and cold heat baths at Th​=T2​ and Tc​=T1​. The four processes are all reversible thermodynamic processes. Lettered states below belong only to this illustration; the engine later in the question uses numbered states.
Starting at A, perform the following four stages:
  • A→B: isothermal expansion at Th​. The gas absorbs heat Qh​ from the hot heat bath and does work. Its internal energy is unchanged, so Qh​=WAB​=nRTh​ln(VB​/VA​).
  • B→C: reversible ideal-gas adiabat during expansion. Insulation prevents heat transfer; work done by the gas reduces its internal energy and lowers its temperature from Th​ to Tc​.
  • C→D: isothermal compression at Tc​. Work done on the gas is rejected as heat Qc​ to the cold heat bath, with Qc​=nRTc​ln(VC​/VD​)>0.
  • D→A: insulated adiabatic compression, performed reversibly, raises the temperature back to Th​ and closes the cycle.
The clockwise enclosed area in the pressure-volume diagram is the positive net work per cycle, W=∮pdV=Qh​−Qc​.

Because the working substance returns to its original thermodynamic state, its net entropy change is zero. There is no entropy change on either reversible ideal-gas adiabat; on each isothermal process the entropy transfer is the exchanged heat divided by the bath's absolute temperature. Hence
Th​Qh​​−Tc​Qc​​=0,Qh​Qc​​=Th​Tc​​.

The complete cycle must be a reversible thermodynamic process: no friction, viscous dissipation, unrestrained expansion, or other source of entropy. Each moving boundary must be arbitrarily close to mechanical equilibrium. Heat transfer to or from a bath must occur across an infinitesimal temperature difference, and the insulated stages must be genuinely adiabatic processes. There must also be no direct heat leak between the heat baths.
For an engine exchanging heat only with these two heat baths, the Second law of thermodynamics gives
Sgen​=Tc​Qc​​−Th​Qh​​≥0,
(52)
and thus
η=1−Th​Tc​​−Qh​Tc​Sgen​​≤ηCarnot​.
(53)
Practical engines generate entropy through finite temperature differences, friction and other dissipative effects. They therefore fall below the Carnot cycle limit. Approaching reversible heat exchange generally requires vanishingly slow operation for a finite heat-transfer apparatus, limiting useful power.

A cubic reflecting cavity supports standing waves with positive integer mode indices and wavevector components ki​=πni​/L. A cell in mode space has volume (π/L)3. In the continuum approximation, a spherical shell of radius k and thickness dk has one-eighth of its volume in the positive octant. There are two independent transverse photon polarizations for a generic wavevector. Hence
dN=2×81​×4πk2dk(πL​)3=π2L3k2​dk.
(64)
The photon energy is ϵ=ℏω=ℏck, so dk=dϵ/(ℏc). Therefore
dN=π2ℏ3c3L3ϵ2​dϵ.​
(65)
This is the leading bulk density of states, valid when the cavity is large compared with the relevant wavelengths and many modes lie in the counting interval. A finite cavity has a discrete mode spectrum, with boundary corrections beyond this approximation. Modes with vanishing indices require separate boundary counting but do not change the leading L3 term. Here ℏ is the reduced Planck constant, as printed in the original PDF; replacing it by h would introduce an erroneous factor of (2π)3.

Each independent cavity mode is a quantum harmonic oscillator of angular frequency ω. Its occupation numbers n=0,1,… represent the numbers of photons in that mode. At thermal equilibrium, photons can be absorbed and emitted by the walls, so their number is not conserved and the photon chemical potential is zero. The Boltzmann distribution of the oscillator excitations then gives
⟨n⟩=eℏω/(kB​T)−11​,Uˉ=ℏω⟨n⟩.
(66)
The expression supplied in the paper is the thermal excitation energy. The oscillator's temperature-independent zero-point energy is excluded from the emitted thermal radiation.
Putting ϵ=ℏω and dϵ=ℏdω into the mode count from (a) gives the number of modes per unit cavity volume:
L3dN​=π2c3ω2​dω.
(67)
Multiplying by the mean thermal energy in each mode yields Planck's law in the angular-frequency convention:
u(T,ω)dω=π2c3ω2dω​eℏω/(kB​T)−1ℏω​.​
(68)
The two photon polarizations are already included in the density of states; no further factor of two is needed. u(T,ω)dω has units Jm−3, whereas u(T,ω) has units Jsm−3.

The equilibrium photon gas is isotropic, so the energy density per unit solid angle in a narrow angular-frequency interval is u(T,ω)dω/(4π). For rays making angle θ with an outward surface normal, a surface element dA is crossed in time dt by rays initially in a volume cdtdAcosθ. Only the outward hemisphere contributes. Thus
e(T,ω)=4πcu(T,ω)​∫02π​dϕ∫0π/2​cosθsinθdθ=4cu(T,ω)​​.
(69)
The hemispherical restriction supplies a factor 1/2 and the directional projection supplies another 1/2. A small aperture in a cavity therefore emits this spectral power per unit area. A blackbody at the same temperature emits the same amount: otherwise radiation exchange between it and the aperture would violate thermal equilibrium.

Let the spacecraft radius be a, and assume that heat redistribution keeps its whole surface at a common temperature T. Neglect background radiation, and let R≫r,a. The Sun's luminosity, from the Stefan–Boltzmann law, is
LS​=4πr2σTS4​.
(74)
At distance R, the incident irradiance is FS​=LS​/(4πR2)=σTS4​(r/R)2. The spacecraft intercepts radiation over its projected disc, area πa2, but emits from its whole spherical area 4πa2. The radiative equilibrium condition is therefore
πa2σTS4​R2r2​=4πa2σT4.
(75)
Cancelling the common factors gives
T=TS​2Rr​​ ∝ TS​Rr​​.​
(76)
The radius of the spacecraft cancels. The factor 1/2​ follows from the ratio of absorbing projected area to emitting surface area. Uniform surface temperature is the intended additional idealization behind a single equilibrium surface temperature. A poorly conducting spacecraft would have position-dependent temperatures; for example, a locally reradiating patch facing the Sun would instead have Tsubsolar​=TS​r/R​, and a different maximum-temperature constraint.

Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation equates spectral absorptivity and spectral emissivity at the same wavelength and direction. A surface that is a poor absorber in the thermal infrared is also a poor emitter there; a coating cannot be assigned its solar absorption independently of its thermal emission spectrum.
(i) All-wavelength black paint. It absorbs the sunlight and also radiates maximally at the spacecraft's own temperature. With the isothermal-sphere assumptions of (d), the limiting black-surface distance is
rRmin,black​​=21​(Tmax​TS​​)2=21​(6006000​)2=50.​
(77)
The spacecraft can operate at R≥50r in this model. A surface already assumed to be a blackbody is not improved by another black coating; relative to option (ii), however, this paint permits closer operation.
(ii) Black at short wavelengths and grey in the infrared. By Wien's displacement law, changing the black-body radiation temperature from 6000K to 600K shifts the wavelength peak from about 0.5μm to about 5μm. Most solar power is therefore in the highly absorbing short-wavelength band, whereas most spacecraft emission lies beyond 2μm, where the spectral emissivity is reduced. The coating retains most of the heating but suppresses much of the cooling. Under the intended two-band grey body approximation, option (ii) makes the spacecraft hotter at the same distance and requires a larger minimum distance than black paint.
To express the comparison without inventing a grey absorptivity, let αˉS​ be radiometric absorptance averaged over the solar spectrum and ϵˉT​ be emissivity averaged over the spacecraft's own thermal spectrum. The radiative equilibrium equation becomes
αˉS​σTS4​R2r2​=4ϵˉT​σT4,T=TS​2Rr​​(ϵˉT​αˉS​​)1/4.
(78)
At the failure threshold,
rRmin​​=50ϵˉ600​αˉS​​​.​
(79)
For an ideal two-band coating take a constant radiometric absorptance g<1 beyond λc​=2μm, and unity below it. If f(T) is the fraction of the power emitted by a blackbody below λc​, Planck's law gives
f(T)=π415​∫hc/(λc​kB​T)∞​ex−1x3​dx,αˉS​=g+(1−g)f(TS​),ϵˉ600​=g+(1−g)f(600K).
(80)
Since f(TS​)>f(600K), this model has αˉS​>ϵˉ600​ and hence Rmin​>50r. The paper supplies neither a numerical value of g nor a detailed spectrum, so no unique numerical distance for the grey paint follows. If its long-wavelength absorption is not approximately grey, the spectrum must be integrated explicitly rather than inferred from a peak alone. A coating designed to improve close approach would instead reflect much of the solar radiation while retaining strong thermal infrared emission.
