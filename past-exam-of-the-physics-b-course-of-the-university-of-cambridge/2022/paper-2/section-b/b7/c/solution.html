Because the magnetic field obeys ∇⋅B=0, it can be represented locally as the curl of a magnetic vector potential:
B=∇×A.​
(36)
This automatically enforces the absence of magnetic monopoles. It is also a global representation throughout ordinary all-space problems without monopole singularities. It is not unique: the gauge transformation
A⟼A+∇χ
(37)
leaves B unchanged because the curl of a gradient vanishes. In a time-dependent problem,
E=−∇ϕ−∂t∂A​,ϕ⟼ϕ−∂t∂χ​
(38)
makes the same transformation leave both physical fields unchanged and automatically satisfies Faraday's law.
In vacuum magnetostatics, choose the Coulomb gauge ∇⋅A=0. Substitution into Ampère's law and the identity ∇×(∇×A)=∇(∇⋅A)−∇2A gives
∇2A=−μ0​J,A(r)=4πμ0​​∫∣r−r′∣J(r′)​d3r′
(39)
for a localized steady electric current density and a potential vanishing at infinity. Taking the curl recovers the Biot-Savart law. The magnetic vector potential converts a constrained vector field problem into componentwise Poisson equations once a gauge is chosen.
For a uniform magnetic field, A=21​B×r is a convenient choice. For a localized magnetic dipole moment m, the leading far-field potential is
A(r)=4πμ0​​r3m×r​.
(40)
Another useful relation is Stokes theorem, which relates a closed-path integral to the magnetic flux through a spanning surface:
∮∂S​A⋅dℓ=∫S​B⋅dS.
(41)
The units of A are Tm, equivalently Wbm−1.
