Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation equates spectral absorptivity and spectral emissivity at the same wavelength and direction. A surface that is a poor absorber in the thermal infrared is also a poor emitter there; a coating cannot be assigned its solar absorption independently of its thermal emission spectrum.
(i) All-wavelength black paint. It absorbs the sunlight and also radiates maximally at the spacecraft's own temperature. With the isothermal-sphere assumptions of (d), the limiting black-surface distance is
rRmin,black​​=21​(Tmax​TS​​)2=21​(6006000​)2=50.​
(77)
The spacecraft can operate at R≥50r in this model. A surface already assumed to be a blackbody is not improved by another black coating; relative to option (ii), however, this paint permits closer operation.
(ii) Black at short wavelengths and grey in the infrared. By Wien's displacement law, changing the black-body radiation temperature from 6000K to 600K shifts the wavelength peak from about 0.5μm to about 5μm. Most solar power is therefore in the highly absorbing short-wavelength band, whereas most spacecraft emission lies beyond 2μm, where the spectral emissivity is reduced. The coating retains most of the heating but suppresses much of the cooling. Under the intended two-band grey body approximation, option (ii) makes the spacecraft hotter at the same distance and requires a larger minimum distance than black paint.

If its long-wavelength absorption is not approximately grey, the spectrum must be integrated explicitly rather than inferred from a peak alone. A coating designed to improve close approach would instead reflect much of the solar radiation while retaining strong thermal infrared emission.
