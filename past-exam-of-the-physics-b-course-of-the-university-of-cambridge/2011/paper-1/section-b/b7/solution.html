The microscopic magnetic response becomes a macroscopic field problem through the magnetization M, the magnetic dipole moment per unit volume. Define
B=μ0​(H+M),∇⋅B=0,∇×H=Jfree​
(18)
in magnetostatics. Bound currents are represented by Jb​=∇×M and surface current Kb​=M×n^. Normal B is continuous across an interface, while n^×(H2​−H1​)=Kfree​. It is generally incorrect to impose continuity of every component of both fields. A linear isotropic material has M=χH and μr​=1+χ, but a ferromagnet usually requires a nonlinear, history-dependent constitutive relation.
Internal field differs from externally applied field. For uniform magnetization along a principal axis of an ellipsoid, the demagnetizing field is −Nd​M and Hint​=Happlied​−Nd​M. A linear specimen therefore has apparent susceptibility χ/(1+Nd​χ) relative to the applied field. Shape is especially important for large susceptibility. A long closed magnetic circuit reduces stray fields; a bar magnet still has demagnetizing fields despite the absence of magnetic monopoles. Equivalent magnetic-pole descriptions are bookkeeping for magnetization divergence, not free magnetic charge.[ref]
In paramagnetism, permanent localized moments are preferentially aligned by the field, against thermal disorder. For independent spin-one-half moments with projections ±μ, the two Zeeman energies give
Z1​=2cosh(kB​TμB​),M=nμtanh(kB​TμB​),χ≃kB​Tμ0​nμ2​>0
(19)
in the dilute weak-field limit with B≃μ0​H. This is a Curie law; the response saturates at nμ when alignment dominates. More generally, spin J gives χ=μ0​n(gμB​)2J(J+1)/(3kB​T). Interactions can produce a Curie–Weiss response C/(T−Θ) over a suitable range. Conduction-electron Pauli paramagnetism need not show the localized-moment 1/T law.
In diamagnetism, the field induces a magnetic moment opposing it, so the linear susceptibility is negative. The microscopic cause is the change in orbital electronic motion, and a closed-shell insulating material has no permanent atomic moment to orient. This weak response is often only mildly temperature dependent away from other transitions. It also exists as a background in materials with a stronger positive response. Perfect flux expulsion in a superconductor is a distinct collective phenomenon and should not be identified with ordinary weak atomic diamagnetism.
In ferromagnetism, exchange interactions stabilize spontaneous magnetic order below the Curie temperature. A specimen can have zero net moment while its magnetic domains remain locally magnetized in different directions. Domain-wall motion and moment rotation produce a large response; anisotropy and pinning lead to remanence, coercivity and hysteresis. Increasing field eventually approaches saturation. Heating above the ordering transition removes spontaneous order, although an applied field still induces a response. Soft magnetic cores favor easy reversal and small cyclic loss; hard permanent-magnet materials favor retained magnetization and resistance to reversal. These properties concern collective order and barriers, not merely independent moments all following a weak field.[ref]
A practical distinction requires a controlled field sweep and a separate magnetic readout. A toroidal core with drive winding N1​, path length l and current I has approximately H=N1​I/l. A pickup winding measures E2​=−N2​AdB/dt; integrating after calibration gives B, and M=B/μ0​−H. Reversing the sweep reveals a loop whose positive loss area per volume is ∮HdB. An air-core/empty-holder reference, known geometry and turn count, offset correction, temperature control and slower sweeps test background, heating and rate-dependent eddy-current losses.
