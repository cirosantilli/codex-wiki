OurBigBook.com Words: 170
A tidal force measures the difference of gravitational acceleration across an extended object. A freely falling reference frame removes a spatially uniform acceleration, but not its gradient. For a distant point mass M at distance R and direction n^ from the reference centre, a small internal displacement x gives
δg=R3GM​[3n^(n^⋅x)−x],∣x∣≪R.
(23)
The tidal tensor has eigenvalues 2GM/R3 radially and −GM/R3 in each transverse direction: radial stretching and transverse squeezing. Its trace vanishes in the source-free region, consistent with the gravitational Gauss law. An associated relative potential is Φtide​=−GM[3(n^⋅x)2−x2]/(2R3).
The scale is GMl/R3 across size l, not the centre's absolute gravity GM/R2. This distinction explains how a more distant massive object can exert a large force but a modest tide. Opposite-side stretching supports the elementary two-bulge picture; real ocean tides also involve rotation, basin geometry and fluid dynamics, so the static gradient alone does not predict coastal heights or timing. The same gradient stresses satellites and can disrupt a self-gravitating body when it competes with the body's own gravitational restoring acceleration.
