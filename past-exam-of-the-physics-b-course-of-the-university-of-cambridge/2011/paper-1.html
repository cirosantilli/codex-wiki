Gauss's law connects electric flux through a closed surface to its enclosed charge:
∮∂V​E⋅dA=ϵ0​Qenc​​,∇⋅E=ϵ0​ρ​.
(11)

The microscopic magnetic response becomes a macroscopic field problem through the magnetization M, the magnetic dipole moment per unit volume. Define
B=μ0​(H+M),∇⋅B=0,∇×H=Jfree​
(18)
in magnetostatics. Bound currents are represented by Jb​=∇×M and surface current Kb​=M×n^. Normal B is continuous across an interface, while n^×(H2​−H1​)=Kfree​. It is generally incorrect to impose continuity of every component of both fields. A linear isotropic material has M=χH and μr​=1+χ, but a ferromagnet usually requires a nonlinear, history-dependent constitutive relation.
Internal field differs from externally applied field. For uniform magnetization along a principal axis of an ellipsoid, the demagnetizing field is −Nd​M and Hint​=Happlied​−Nd​M. A linear specimen therefore has apparent susceptibility χ/(1+Nd​χ) relative to the applied field. Shape is especially important for large susceptibility. A long closed magnetic circuit reduces stray fields; a bar magnet still has demagnetizing fields despite the absence of magnetic monopoles. Equivalent magnetic-pole descriptions are bookkeeping for magnetization divergence, not free magnetic charge.[ref]
In paramagnetism, permanent localized moments are preferentially aligned by the field, against thermal disorder. For independent spin-one-half moments with projections ±μ, the two Zeeman energies give
Z1​=2cosh(kB​TμB​),M=nμtanh(kB​TμB​),χ≃kB​Tμ0​nμ2​>0
(19)
in the dilute weak-field limit with B≃μ0​H. This is a Curie law; the response saturates at nμ when alignment dominates. More generally, spin J gives χ=μ0​n(gμB​)2J(J+1)/(3kB​T). Interactions can produce a Curie–Weiss response C/(T−Θ) over a suitable range. Conduction-electron Pauli paramagnetism need not show the localized-moment 1/T law.
In diamagnetism, the field induces a magnetic moment opposing it, so the linear susceptibility is negative. The microscopic cause is the change in orbital electronic motion, and a closed-shell insulating material has no permanent atomic moment to orient. This weak response is often only mildly temperature dependent away from other transitions. It also exists as a background in materials with a stronger positive response. Perfect flux expulsion in a superconductor is a distinct collective phenomenon and should not be identified with ordinary weak atomic diamagnetism.
In ferromagnetism, exchange interactions stabilize spontaneous magnetic order below the Curie temperature. A specimen can have zero net moment while its magnetic domains remain locally magnetized in different directions. Domain-wall motion and moment rotation produce a large response; anisotropy and pinning lead to remanence, coercivity and hysteresis. Increasing field eventually approaches saturation. Heating above the ordering transition removes spontaneous order, although an applied field still induces a response. Soft magnetic cores favor easy reversal and small cyclic loss; hard permanent-magnet materials favor retained magnetization and resistance to reversal. These properties concern collective order and barriers, not merely independent moments all following a weak field.[ref]
A practical distinction requires a controlled field sweep and a separate magnetic readout. A toroidal core with drive winding N1​, path length l and current I has approximately H=N1​I/l. A pickup winding measures E2​=−N2​AdB/dt; integrating after calibration gives B, and M=B/μ0​−H. Reversing the sweep reveals a loop whose positive loss area per volume is ∮HdB. An air-core/empty-holder reference, known geometry and turn count, offset correction, temperature control and slower sweeps test background, heating and rate-dependent eddy-current losses. A single force or susceptibility reading cannot establish spontaneous order or hysteresis. The original curves below are illustrative responses, not measured material data.

A galilean transformation relates inertial frames in uniform relative translation. For V the velocity of the primed origin,
r′=r−Vt,t′=t,v′=v−V,a′=a.
(20)
Absolute time and invariant acceleration make Newton's second law retain its form for the usual nonrelativistic interactions. For a particle of mass m, p′=p−mV and K′=K−V⋅p+mV2/2. Momentum and kinetic energy are frame dependent, although an isolated system's conservation laws hold in either inertial frame. Relative velocities and collision predictions are consistent between frames. A rotating or accelerating frame instead requires inertial-force terms. Maxwell equations with a fixed light speed are not Galilean invariant; Lorentz transformations replace these relations when relativistic effects matter.

A tidal force measures the difference of gravitational acceleration across an extended object. A freely falling reference frame removes a spatially uniform acceleration, but not its gradient. For a distant point mass M at distance R and direction n^ from the reference centre, a small internal displacement x gives
δg=R3GM​[3n^(n^⋅x)−x],∣x∣≪R.
(23)
The tidal tensor has eigenvalues 2GM/R3 radially and −GM/R3 in each transverse direction: radial stretching and transverse squeezing. Its trace vanishes in the source-free region, consistent with the gravitational Gauss law. An associated relative potential is Φtide​=−GM[3(n^⋅x)2−x2]/(2R3).
The scale is GMl/R3 across size l, not the centre's absolute gravity GM/R2. This distinction explains how a more distant massive object can exert a large force but a modest tide. Opposite-side stretching supports the elementary two-bulge picture; real ocean tides also involve rotation, basin geometry and fluid dynamics, so the static gradient alone does not predict coastal heights or timing. The same gradient stresses satellites and can disrupt a self-gravitating body when it competes with the body's own gravitational restoring acceleration.

A transverse torque changes its direction according to dL/dt=τ; it does not initially push the axle in the direction of the applied force.

Ω≃I3​ω3​Mgl​.​

A fluid is described on a continuum scale by density, pressure and velocity fields. An Eulerian field is measured at fixed position; a material element changes at rate D/Dt=∂t​+v⋅∇. mass conservation requires
∂t​ρ+∇⋅(ρv)=0.
(25)
For incompressible flow this becomes ∇⋅v=0. A streamline is instantaneously tangent to velocity, whereas a pathline follows a particle through time; they coincide in a steady flow but need not in an unsteady one.
For an ideal inviscid fluid, pressure and body forces give the Euler equation. In the constant-density gravitational case,
ρDtDv​=−∇p+ρg.
(26)
A stationary impermeable boundary requires zero normal velocity, but inviscid theory does not impose no slip. Taking its steady projection along a streamline gives p/ρ+v2/2+gz=constant. If the flow is also irrotational in the connected region, that constant is shared between streamlines. Inviscid does not mean irrotational: rotating ideal-fluid flows are possible. A velocity potential exists locally for an irrotational field, and in incompressible flow it obeys Laplace's equation. The Bernoulli assumptions must be checked before converting a pressure difference into a speed.[ref]
Real fluids transmit shear stress. An incompressible Newtonian fluid with constant dynamic viscosity η obeys
ρDtDv​=−∇p+η∇2v+ρg.
(27)
The constitutive shear stress is proportional to strain rate, and stationary solid boundaries usually impose no slip. viscosity diffuses vorticity and converts organized mechanical energy into heat; its local dissipation 2ηeij​eij​ is nonnegative. It cannot generally be included merely by changing the constant in a lossless Bernoulli equation.
In a long straight circular tube, steady fully developed laminar flow has
vz​(r)=4ηLΔp​(a2−r2),Q=8ηLπa4Δp​.
(28)
This Hagen-Poiseuille equation follows by balancing the axial pressure gradient with viscous shear and setting vz​(a)=0. The parabolic profile, the fourth-power radius sensitivity and the pumping power ΔpQ are experimentally distinct consequences. Entrance regions, rough walls, non-Newtonian response or a changing temperature require additional modelling.
The Reynolds number Re=ρUL/η compares inertia with viscosity. Small values favor creeping-flow balances; high values permit thin boundary layers, separation and turbulence, although transition depends on geometry and perturbations, not one universal critical number. At high Reynolds number an outer flow can be approximately inviscid while near-wall viscosity still determines drag. This resolves why a zero-drag ideal potential-flow model cannot predict the resistance of an ordinary moving body. In turbulent flow, fluctuating Reynolds stresses redistribute momentum and increase the required pressure drop; averaging the equations introduces closure requirements.
A controlled tube experiment measures pressure difference and collected volume per time while changing forcing. Use tracers or a velocity probe to test profile shape, allow sufficient entrance length, and monitor radius and temperature independently. Repeat with reversed flow and at several rates to test offsets and linearity. Dye mixing alone does not measure viscosity or establish a fully developed velocity profile. Although each process can be reversible, the isobaric heat transfer spans a range of temperatures; making the whole cycle reversible requires a succession of matched-temperature reservoirs, rather than just the hottest and coldest reservoirs.

A microstate specifies the microscopic state: classically the positions and momenta of all particles, or quantum mechanically an allowed many-body state. A macrostate specifies a few coarse variables such as energy, volume and particle number. Many microstates can give the same macrostate. A statistical ensemble is a probability assignment over hypothetical copies prepared with the same macroscopic constraints; it does not require a sample to be literally divided into many physical copies. An observable's ensemble mean is ⟨A⟩=∑i​pi​Ai​, with an integral replacing the sum in classical phase space.
For an isolated system at fixed U,V,N, the microcanonical ensemble assigns equal probability to accessible microstates in a narrow energy shell. If their number is Ω(U,V,N), the Boltzmann entropy is S=kB​lnΩ. Thermal contact between two initially isolated systems allows energy to be shared while their sum stays fixed. The most probable division maximizes S1​(U1​)+S2​(U−U1​), so equilibrium requires equal ∂S/∂U. This gives the statistical temperature definition
T1​=(∂U∂S​)V,N​,β=kB​T1​=(∂U∂lnΩ​)V,N​.​
(42)
For positive temperature, energy tends to flow from lower entropy slope to higher entropy slope until those slopes agree. The narrow energy-shell width represents macroscopic resolution; it must not exclude the physically accessible states.
For a small subsystem exchanging energy with a much larger bath, the bath has energy Utot​−Ei​ when subsystem microstate i has energy Ei​. Expanding its entropy gives Sb​(Utot​−Ei​)=Sb​(Utot​)−Ei​/T+⋯. Counting compatible bath states therefore yields the Boltzmann distribution and canonical ensemble:
pi​=Ze−βEi​​,Z=∑i​e−βEi​,U=−∂β∂lnZ​,F=−kB​TlnZ.
(43)
The exponential weights individual microstates. An energy level of degeneracy gj​ has total probability gj​e−βEj​/Z; a more degenerate excited level can outweigh one ground-state level even though each of its microstates is less probable. The Gibbs entropy −kB​∑i​pi​lnpi​ gives S=kB​lnZ+U/T. The equilibrium energy variance is ⟨(ΔE)2⟩=kB​T2cV​, linking measurable fluctuations to a macroscopic response.
If particle exchange is also allowed, a reservoir fixes T and chemical potential μ. The grand canonical ensemble weights a state by e−β(Ei​−μNi​)/Ξ, with Ξ=∑N,i​e−β(Ei​−μN). Mean particle number is kB​T∂lnΞ/∂μ, while volume remains a constraint in this version. Thus the ensemble follows the physical exchange conditions: fixed energy, thermal contact, or thermal and particle contact.
In ordinary large short-range systems, relative fluctuations often decrease as N−1/2 and the ensembles give the same bulk thermodynamics away from singular limits. Finite systems, critical fluctuations and long-range interactions require care. Interpreting a time average as an ensemble average additionally requires appropriate sampling or mixing dynamics; it is not guaranteed solely by naming an ensemble. thermal equilibrium probability assignments do not describe every driven or relaxing state.
