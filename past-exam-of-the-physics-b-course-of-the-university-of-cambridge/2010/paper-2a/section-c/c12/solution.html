Let Jp​=∫r2dA=πa4/2 be the polar second moment of area. A twist gradient ∂x​θ gives shear strain r∂x​θ and shear stress Gr∂x​θ. Integrating its moment over a cross section yields
C=GJp​lϕ​=2lπa4G​ϕ.​
(30)
The difference in torque on an element dx is GJp​∂x2​θdx, while its angular inertia is ρJp​dx. Hence the torsional wave equation in a circular shaft is
∂t2​θ=ρG​∂x2​θ,cT​=G/ρ​.​
(31)
For uniform rotation at Ω, transmitted power is P=CΩ, so the given values give C=100Nm. The elastic strain energy and rotational kinetic energy are
ϕ=GJp​Cl​=9.95×10−3rad​,U=2GJp​C2l​=0.497J​,K=21​ρJp​lΩ2=181J​.
(32)
The torsional transit time is l/cT​=6.32×10−4s. If the requested maximum rates mean stored-energy-over-transit-time estimates, they are
lcT​U​≃786W,lcT​K​≃286kW.​
(33)
These estimates require a physical qualification. They are not separate upper bounds on transmitted mechanical power. The exact local energy flux of a torsional wave is the stress-work rate
P=−GJp​(∂x​θ)(∂t​θ).
(34)
For the steadily rotating twisted shaft its magnitude is CΩ=30kW, even though this exceeds cT​U/l. Writing local energy densities us​=GJp​θx2​/2 and uk​=ρJp​θt2​/2 gives ∣P∣=2cT​us​uk​​≤cT​(us​+uk​). A single travelling torsional wave has equal strain and kinetic energies; the steady shaft can instead be represented by oppositely travelling components whose powers largely cancel. Its stored strain pattern is not a fuel consumed once per transit. This distinguishes an energy-content estimate from the actual stress-mediated transport.
