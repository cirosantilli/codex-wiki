Stokes theorem converts circulation around an oriented boundary into curl flux through a spanning surface. For a sufficiently smooth vector field on an oriented piecewise smooth surface S,
∮∂S​A⋅dl=∫S​(∇×A)⋅ndS.​
(54)
The boundary orientation follows the right-hand rule relative to n. The field must be smooth on the surface, not merely on its boundary; a singularity crossing the surface cannot be ignored. For a fixed loop, applying the theorem to Faraday's differential law gives the integral flux law of induction.
The planar form is Green theorem. For a region D with positively oriented boundary and sufficiently smooth P,Q,
∮∂D​(Pdx+Qdy)=∬D​(Qx​−Py​)dxdy.​
(55)
The outer boundary is counterclockwise and any holes have clockwise boundaries. For instance P=−y/2,Q=x/2 makes the area of D equal to half the line integral of xdy−ydx; the unit circle gives area π.
A closely related use of “Green's theorems” in field theory is Green's identities, obtained by applying the divergence theorem to ϕ∇ψ:
∫V​(ϕ∇2ψ+∇ϕ⋅∇ψ)dV=∮∂V​ϕ∂n​ψdS,
(56)
and, after subtracting the expression with ϕ,ψ exchanged,
∫V​(ϕ∇2ψ−ψ∇2ϕ)dV=∮∂V​(ϕ∂n​ψ−ψ∂n​ϕ)dS.​
(57)
These relate interior sources to boundary data in electrostatics and other boundary-value problems. For example, a harmonic potential difference vanishing on a Dirichlet boundary condition has zero integral of its squared gradient by the first identity, establishing uniqueness. Both the planar circulation theorem and the field identities are included here to make the convention clear.
