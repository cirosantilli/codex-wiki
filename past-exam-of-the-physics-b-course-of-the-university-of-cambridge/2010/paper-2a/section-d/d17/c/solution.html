Convolution describes the response of a linear time-invariant system to an arbitrary input. If h is its impulse response and f its input,
g(t)=(h∗f)(t)=∫−∞∞​h(t−τ)f(τ)dτ.​
(60)
This is a weighted sum of responses to shifted impulses. For integrable functions the operation is commutative and associative. A causal system has h(t)=0 for t<0, restricting the integral to past inputs. With the Fourier convention F(ω)=∫f(t)e−iωtdt, the convolution theorem gives G=HF.
For example, an RC low-pass frequency response has h(t)=τ0−1​e−t/τ0​Θ(t) and H(ω)=(1+iωτ0​)−1, where Θ is the Heaviside step function. Fast input variations are smoothed. The same mathematics describes optical blurring by a point spread function and many other linear response measurements.
Deconvolution tries to recover f from measured g and known h. In noise-free data one formally sets F=G/H where H=0. Zeros of H lose corresponding information; small values amplify measurement noise. If the measured spectrum is G+N, the direct estimate has error N/H, which can be very large at frequencies strongly suppressed by the instrument. An inverse is therefore not automatically unique or stable.
One regularized spectral deconvolution reconstruction is
Fλ​(ω)=∣H(ω)∣2+λH(ω)∗G(ω)​,λ>0.​
(61)
This minimizes ∣HF−G∣2+λ∣F∣2 at each frequency and limits noise amplification, at the cost of bias and lost resolution. Choosing the regularization requires assumptions about noise and the desired signal; deconvolution cannot restore components that the data do not determine without such additional information.
