A Fourier series separates a periodic physical signal into harmonics. For example, a periodic voltage applied to a linear circuit can be treated one frequency at a time; the harmonic responses then superpose to give the output waveform.
Write w=ΔT for the pulse width and δ=w/T for its duty cycle, with 0<w<T. The waveform is a train of positive rectangular pulses centred on t=jT, each with area one. It is even about t=0, so every sine coefficient vanishes. Cosine orthogonality gives
a0​=T2​∫−T/2T/2​f(t)dt=T2​,
(41)
and, for n≥1,
an​=Tw2​∫−w/2w/2​cosT2πnt​dt=πnw2​sinTπnw​.
(42)
This is the Fourier series of a periodic rectangular pulse:
an​=T2​sinc(πnδ)(n≥0),sincz=zsinz​,sinc0=1.​
(43)
The actual constant term in the series is a0​/2=1/T, not a0​. As usual for a jump discontinuity, the series converges to the average of the one-sided values at each pulse edge, namely 1/(2w), irrespective of the value assigned at that isolated endpoint. Elsewhere it converges to the rectangular waveform.
Counting the constant as the first term, the first three terms are
T1​,T2​sinc(πδ)cosT2πt​,T2​sinc(2πδ)cosT4πt​,
(44)
so their sum is
S2​(t)=T1​+T2​[sinc(πδ)cosT2πt​+sinc(2πδ)cosT4πt​].​
(45)
If “first three” instead means the first three nonconstant harmonics, add a3​cos(6πt/T); the original plot shows that alternative partial sum as well. The three-term approximation rounds the narrow peaks and ripples between them; many harmonics are needed to represent a sharp pulse.
The original sketches use the illustrative duty cycle δ=0.15 and cover −T≤t≤T. The coefficient panel also displays the first spectral zero.
For nδ≪1, expand the sinc function:
an​=T2​[1−6π2n2δ2​+O(n4δ4)]≃T2​.​
(46)
Thus the low-index coefficients, including a0​, have nearly equal values, although their constant-series contribution still has the factor 1/2. The first sinc zero occurs at nδ=1, or n=T/w. If this is not an integer, the closest integer coefficient is near zero rather than exactly zero: writing nδ=1+ε gives an​≃−(2/T)ε. This pulse-width scale of Fourier coefficients means that a narrower pulse requires significant harmonics extending to proportionally larger indices, the reciprocal time-width/frequency-width relation.
