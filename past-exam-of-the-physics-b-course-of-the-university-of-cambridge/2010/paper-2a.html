Suppose two distinct reversible adiabatic processes intersect at an equilibrium state C. Choose an isothermal process that meets the two branches at distinct states A and B, forming a nonzero-area three-sided thermodynamic cycle. There is no heat exchange on AC or BC, so the only net heat comes from the one heat reservoir on AB. Since ΔU=0 around a cycle, Wcycle​=QAB​.
Choose the direction in which this nonzero cycle delivers positive work. It would then convert heat from a single reservoir entirely into work, violating the Kelvin-Planck statement of the second law. Distinct reversible adiabats cannot intersect. Equivalently, their constant entropy labels cannot differ at one equilibrium state. The argument concerns an ordinary equilibrium family of reversible adiabats, not arbitrary irreversible insulated paths. The usual local uniqueness of an equilibrium adiabat excludes coincident segments described as distinct curves.

The trihydrogen cation is a nonlinear three-nucleus molecule. Removing three translations and three rigid rotations leaves 3(3)−6=3 vibrational degrees of freedom. One normal mode is the symmetric in-plane breathing motion: every nucleus moves radially toward or away from the centre. The remaining two normal modes form a degenerate in-plane distortion pair, stretching one direction while compressing another.
For an explicit sketch, let rj​ be the equilibrium vertices relative to their centre. Displacements proportional to
uj(1)​=rj​,uj(2)​=(10​0−1​)rj​,uj(3)​=(01​10​)rj​

The fundamental mode of an ideal coaxial cable is a transverse electromagnetic mode: the electric field is radial between the conductors, the magnetic field is azimuthal, and energy flows along the cable through the dielectric. There is no cutoff for this mode; higher modes, conductor losses and dielectric dispersion can matter outside the ideal transmission-line regime.
Ignoring edge fields in the parallel-strip geometry, Ex​=V/a and Hy​=I/b. Per unit length the charge is ϵbV/a and the linked magnetic flux is μaI/b. Thus the parallel-plate transmission line has
C=ϵab​,L=μba​,LC=μϵ.​
(15)
For a segment dz, charge conservation, equivalently the appropriate integral of the Ampère-Maxwell equation, gives I(z)−I(z+dz)=Cdz∂t​V. Applying Faraday's law around a longitudinal rectangle between the plates gives V(z)−V(z+dz)=Ldz∂t​I. These are the lossless telegrapher's equations,
∂z​I=−C∂t​V,∂z​V=−L∂t​I.
(16)

∂z2​V=LC∂t2​V,∂z2​I=LC∂t2​I.​

Electrons locally adjust their motion as the field arrives; one electron need not travel the cable's length to transmit a signal. The Poynting vector in the dielectric integrates to power VI.

A Fourier series separates a periodic physical signal into harmonics. For example, a periodic voltage applied to a linear circuit can be treated one frequency at a time; the harmonic responses then superpose to give the output waveform.
Write w=ΔT for the pulse width and δ=w/T for its duty cycle, with 0<w<T. The waveform is a train of positive rectangular pulses centred on t=jT, each with area one. It is even about t=0, so every sine coefficient vanishes. Cosine orthogonality gives
a0​=T2​∫−T/2T/2​f(t)dt=T2​,
(41)
and, for n≥1,
an​=Tw2​∫−w/2w/2​cosT2πnt​dt=πnw2​sinTπnw​.
(42)
This is the Fourier series of a periodic rectangular pulse:
an​=T2​sinc(πnδ)(n≥0),sincz=zsinz​,sinc0=1.​
(43)
The actual constant term in the series is a0​/2=1/T, not a0​. As usual for a jump discontinuity, the series converges to the average of the one-sided values at each pulse edge, namely 1/(2w), irrespective of the value assigned at that isolated endpoint. Elsewhere it converges to the rectangular waveform.
Counting the constant as the first term, the first three terms are
T1​,T2​sinc(πδ)cosT2πt​,T2​sinc(2πδ)cosT4πt​,

The original sketches use the illustrative duty cycle δ=0.15 and cover −T≤t≤T. The coefficient panel also displays the first spectral zero.

This pulse-width scale of Fourier coefficients means that a narrower pulse requires significant harmonics extending to proportionally larger indices, the reciprocal time-width/frequency-width relation.

Cancellation at one spatial position for an interval of time does not imply cancellation of the spatial derivative there. At u=±1/2,±3/2, the classical slope is not defined; the adjacent pieces specify its one-sided limits.
At x=ca the two windows meet without an open overlap. Their displacements combine into
y1​(u)={−21​sin(πu),0,​−2≤u≤2,∣u∣>2,​​
(52)
with slope
cayx​=⎩⎨⎧​−2π​cos(πu),2π​cos(πu),0,​−2<u<0,0<u<2,∣u∣>2 or u=0.​​
(53)
At u=±2 the classical slope is undefined. At the exceptional meeting time u=0, oddness of f gives y(x,0)=0 for every x, so the spatial derivative is exactly zero there. Its one-sided time limits at x=ca are −π/(2ca) and +π/(2ca); these do not determine its value at the meeting point.
The original graphs distinguish continuous displacements, open-ended one-sided slope traces, and the zero slope at the isolated meeting point. The sharp temporal window is not differentiable at its endpoints, so the overall solution is piecewise classical and globally weak. In particular the literal boundary slope at (x,t)=(0,±a) is not a classical derivative: the even spatial profile has a cusp there. The zero-slope boundary prescription is satisfied at regular times and in its almost-everywhere trace sense; it cannot be read as a globally smooth classical condition for this nonsmooth f.

Stokes theorem converts circulation around an oriented boundary into curl flux through a spanning surface. For a sufficiently smooth vector field on an oriented piecewise smooth surface S,
∮∂S​A⋅dl=∫S​(∇×A)⋅ndS.​
(54)
The boundary orientation follows the right-hand rule relative to n. The field must be smooth on the surface, not merely on its boundary; a singularity crossing the surface cannot be ignored. For a fixed loop, applying the theorem to Faraday's differential law gives the integral flux law of induction.
The planar form is Green theorem. For a region D with positively oriented boundary and sufficiently smooth P,Q,
∮∂D​(Pdx+Qdy)=∬D​(Qx​−Py​)dxdy.​
(55)
The outer boundary is counterclockwise and any holes have clockwise boundaries. For instance P=−y/2,Q=x/2 makes the area of D equal to half the line integral of xdy−ydx; the unit circle gives area π.
A closely related use of “Green's theorems” in field theory is Green's identities, obtained by applying the divergence theorem to ϕ∇ψ:
∫V​(ϕ∇2ψ+∇ϕ⋅∇ψ)dV=∮∂V​ϕ∂n​ψdS,
(56)
and, after subtracting the expression with ϕ,ψ exchanged,
∫V​(ϕ∇2ψ−ψ∇2ϕ)dV=∮∂V​(ϕ∂n​ψ−ψ∂n​ϕ)dS.​
(57)
These relate interior sources to boundary data in electrostatics and other boundary-value problems. For example, a harmonic potential difference vanishing on a Dirichlet boundary condition has zero integral of its squared gradient by the first identity, establishing uniqueness. Both the planar circulation theorem and the field identities are included here to make the convention clear.

Separation of variables replaces a compatible partial differential equation by ordinary differential eigenvalue equations. For example, with a thermally diffusive rod, ut​=Duxx​, try u=X(x)T(t). Wherever division is valid,
DTT′​=XX′′​=−λ.
(58)
The two sides depend on independent variables, so each must be constant. Homogeneous end temperatures u(0,t)=u(L,t)=0 give Xn​=sin(nπx/L) and λn​=(nπ/L)2. The general initial-temperature profile is represented by superposing these separated modes:
u(x,t)=n=1∑∞​bn​sinLnπx​e−D(nπ/L)2t,bn​=L2​∫0L​u(x,0)sinLnπx​dx.​
(59)
Each spatial mode decays at its own rate. For a fixed-end wave equation the same spatial eigenfunctions instead have oscillatory time factors An​cos(cnπt/L)+Bn​sin(cnπt/L), with coefficients fixed by initial displacement and velocity. Similar constructions separate Laplace's equation in suitable coordinate systems.
A single product is usually only one mode, not the most general solution. Linear superposition and completeness of the spatial eigenfunctions are what extend the method to general allowed data. Nonhomogeneous boundary values can first be removed by a known stationary or particular solution. Separation is effective when the geometry, coefficients and boundary conditions admit a common separated eigenproblem; an arbitrary nonlinear equation or inseparable geometry need not do so. Self-adjoint Sturm-Liouville problems explain the weighted orthogonality and completeness in many physical applications.

Convolution describes the response of a linear time-invariant system to an arbitrary input. If h is its impulse response and f its input,
g(t)=(h∗f)(t)=∫−∞∞​h(t−τ)f(τ)dτ.​
(60)
This is a weighted sum of responses to shifted impulses. For integrable functions the operation is commutative and associative. A causal system has h(t)=0 for t<0, restricting the integral to past inputs. With the Fourier convention F(ω)=∫f(t)e−iωtdt, the convolution theorem gives G=HF.
For example, an RC low-pass frequency response has h(t)=τ0−1​e−t/τ0​Θ(t) and H(ω)=(1+iωτ0​)−1, where Θ is the Heaviside step function. Fast input variations are smoothed. The same mathematics describes optical blurring by a point spread function and many other linear response measurements.
Deconvolution tries to recover f from measured g and known h. In noise-free data one formally sets F=G/H where H=0. Zeros of H lose corresponding information; small values amplify measurement noise. If the measured spectrum is G+N, the direct estimate has error N/H, which can be very large at frequencies strongly suppressed by the instrument. An inverse is therefore not automatically unique or stable.
One regularized spectral deconvolution reconstruction is
Fλ​(ω)=∣H(ω)∣2+λH(ω)∗G(ω)​,λ>0.​
(61)
This minimizes ∣HF−G∣2+λ∣F∣2 at each frequency and limits noise amplification, at the cost of bias and lost resolution. Choosing the regularization requires assumptions about noise and the desired signal; deconvolution cannot restore components that the data do not determine without such additional information.

Orthogonality means zero inner product, not merely that two objects look different. For complex vectors, ⟨u,v⟩=∑i​ui∗​vi​; for functions a common choice is ⟨u,v⟩=∫w(x)u(x)∗v(x)dx with positive weight w. An orthonormal set has ⟨um​,un​⟩=δmn​.
For an orthogonal set, the component of f along un​ has coefficient
cn​=⟨un​,un​⟩⟨un​,f⟩​.​
(62)
A complete orthonormal set gives f=∑n​cn​un​ in the appropriate norm and Parseval's identity ∥f∥2=∑n​∣cn​∣2. Without completeness there can be a nonzero residual orthogonal to the entire chosen set. Orthogonal nonzero vectors are linearly independent, but independence alone does not imply orthogonality.
Fourier coefficients are projections because
∫0T​e−2πimt/Te2πint/Tdt=Tδmn​.
(63)
Likewise, for a self-adjoint operator, Aun​=λn​un​ gives (λn​−λm​)⟨um​,un​⟩=0, so distinct eigenvalues have orthogonal eigenvectors. Degeneracy does not itself force a chosen pair to be orthogonal, but an orthonormal basis can be selected within each eigenspace. The differential version uses the correct Sturm-Liouville weight and boundary conditions.
For mechanical normal modes with unequal masses the relevant inner product is ⟨u,v⟩M​=u†Mv. This makes the modes in D14 orthogonal even when an ordinary unweighted dot product does not. Expanding in such coordinates diagonalizes the kinetic and potential quadratic forms, explaining why modal motions evolve independently and their energies add.
