The vorticity of a fluid velocity field is ω=∇×v. A vortex line is an integral curve tangent to that vector, not generally a particle path or streamline. Since ∇⋅ω=0, vorticity flux through successive cross-sections of a vortex tube is equal; a regular line cannot simply end inside the fluid. By Stokes's theorem, the tube strength is the circulation
Γ=∫S​ω⋅dA=∮∂S​v⋅dl.
(19)
For an inviscid barotropic fluid with conservative body force, Kelvin's circulation theorem conserves circulation around a material circuit. Under those assumptions vortex tubes move with the fluid. In three-dimensional incompressible ideal flow,
DtDω​=(ω⋅∇)v;
(20)
stretching a material tube raises its vorticity as its cross-section shrinks. In two-dimensional incompressible flow there is no such stretching, so scalar vorticity is materially conserved. Constant viscosity adds a diffusion term ν∇2ω; baroclinicity and boundaries can also generate vorticity.
An ideal line vortex has vϕ​=Γ/(2πr) and zero ordinary vorticity for r>0, with its circulation concentrated in the singular core. Circular trajectories therefore do not imply nonzero local vorticity everywhere. A finite physical core removes the velocity singularity.
