In paramagnetism, a material develops magnetization along a weak applied field, with positive susceptibility; in diamagnetism, it develops an induced moment opposing it, with negative susceptibility. In a simple localized-moment paramagnet, Zeeman alignment competes with thermal disorder. For independent two-state moments ±μ,
Z1​=2coshkB​TμB​,M=nμtanhkB​TμB​,χH​≃kB​Tμ0​nμ2​
(10)
when the field is weak and demagnetizing corrections are negligible. Stronger fields saturate the moment; interactions and conduction-electron Pauli paramagnetism can depart from this Curie law.
Ordinary diamagnetism comes from a field-induced change of orbital motion, including in closed-shell matter without permanent atomic moments. Its weak susceptibility is often nearly temperature independent away from a transition, and it contributes a background even when another magnetic response dominates. A nonuniform field tends to attract a paramagnetic specimen toward stronger field and repel a diamagnetic specimen in the weak linear regime. Calibrate the holder contribution and field gradient and measure a temperature/field series to distinguish induced response from retained magnetization. Ferromagnetic hysteresis and superconducting flux expulsion require different collective explanations.

The vorticity of a fluid velocity field is ω=∇×v. A vortex line is an integral curve tangent to that vector, not generally a particle path or streamline. Since ∇⋅ω=0, vorticity flux through successive cross-sections of a vortex tube is equal; a regular line cannot simply end inside the fluid. By Stokes's theorem, the tube strength is the circulation
Γ=∫S​ω⋅dA=∮∂S​v⋅dl.
(19)
For an inviscid barotropic fluid with conservative body force, Kelvin's circulation theorem conserves circulation around a material circuit. Under those assumptions vortex tubes move with the fluid. In three-dimensional incompressible ideal flow,
DtDω​=(ω⋅∇)v;
(20)
stretching a material tube raises its vorticity as its cross-section shrinks. In two-dimensional incompressible flow there is no such stretching, so scalar vorticity is materially conserved. Constant viscosity adds a diffusion term ν∇2ω; baroclinicity and boundaries can also generate vorticity.
An ideal line vortex has vϕ​=Γ/(2πr) and zero ordinary vorticity for r>0, with its circulation concentrated in the singular core. Circular trajectories therefore do not imply nonzero local vorticity everywhere. A finite physical core removes the velocity singularity.

The Reynolds number is Re=ρUL/η=UL/ν, comparing inertial stress ρU2 with viscous stress ηU/L. Nondimensionalizing an incompressible Newtonian flow with velocity U, length L and pressure ρU2 gives

At fixed positive pressure, the extrapolated term Cmp​ln(T/T0​) tends to −∞ as T→0+. That prediction is not physically realistic for an equilibrium sample. The zeroth law of thermodynamics states the transitivity of thermal equilibrium: if two systems each equilibrate thermally with a third, they equilibrate with one another. A reproducible thermometric property can therefore label their temperature. Its empirical numerical scale is not itself the complete thermodynamic content; reversible heat-engine relations establish an absolute temperature scale.
The first law of thermodynamics expresses energy conservation. For a closed system, dU=δQ−δW when δW is work done by the system. internal energy is a state function, while heat and work depend on the path. In reversible simple compressible motion, δW=pdV and dU=TdS−pdV; an open system additionally needs the energies carried by transferred matter. An ideal gas can expand freely with Q=W=0 and unchanged internal energy. The first law alone does not decide whether the reverse spontaneous process occurs.
The Second law of thermodynamics introduces entropy and restricts direction. For a reversible path, dS=δQrev​/T; for an irreversible process the entropy balance includes nonnegative production. The entropy of an isolated system cannot decrease. In a cycle, the Clausius inequality is ∮δQ/T≤0, using the appropriate boundary/reservoir temperatures. The Kelvin–Planck statement forbids a cyclic device whose sole effect is extracting heat from one reservoir and turning all of it into work; the Clausius statement forbids moving heat from cold to hot with no compensating effect. A refrigerator is allowed because it consumes work. A two-reservoir engine has η≤1−Tc​/Th​, with equality in the reversible limit. Work may be fully dissipated as heat without contradicting this one-way restriction.
The third law of thermodynamics constrains low-temperature entropy. An equilibrium perfect crystal with a nondegenerate ground state approaches zero entropy as T→0, fixing the conventional absolute entropy reference. Degenerate ground states or frozen nonequilibrium disorder require care when discussing residual entropy. Under regular equilibrium conditions the heat capacity must decrease sufficiently for ∫C/TdT to remain finite; a constant positive classical heat capacity cannot be extrapolated to zero temperature. The unattainability formulation states that absolute zero cannot be reached by a finite sequence of admissible cooling operations. These statements concern the limiting thermodynamic regime, rather than predicting that every substance stays in one classical phase during cooling.
The laws work together: conservation sets a device's energy budget, entropy production restricts the attainable budget, and constitutive equations give the properties of its working material. For fixed temperature and volume, Helmholtz free energy decreases toward equilibrium; at fixed temperature and pressure the corresponding potential is Gibbs free energy. Stable equilibrium, phase coexistence and Maxwell relations follow from these potentials with appropriate constraints. Calorimetry, mechanical-work measurements, thermometry and reversible-path comparisons test different parts of this framework. None of the laws by itself supplies a particular gas equation of state or microscopic heat capacity.
