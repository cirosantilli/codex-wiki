A1 OurBigBook.com
For the one-mole convention, b is the excluded molar volume: finite molecular size reduces the volume available for translational motion from V to V−b. It is an effective excluded volume, not simply the sum of the bare molecular volumes. a/V2 corrects for attractive intermolecular forces, which reduce the pressure exerted on the wall. The attraction per molecule scales with number density, and the number of affected molecules also scales with density, producing the density-squared correction. The Van der Waals equation consequently raises the measured pressure to the corresponding repulsive-gas value before multiplying by the available volume. Both corrections vanish relative to the ideal-gas terms at sufficiently low density.

A transmission line stores electric and magnetic energy along its length, so voltage and current depend on both position and time. A distributed circuit uses inductance L′ and capacitance C′ per length, together with resistance R′ and leakage conductance G′. Applying voltage and charge balance to a short segment gives the telegrapher's equations,
∂x​V=−R′I−L′∂t​I,∂x​I=−G′V−C′∂t​V.
(6)
For a lossless line, both variables obey wave equations with speed v=1/L′C′​ and characteristic impedance Z0​=L′/C′​. Forward and backward waves obey I+=V+/Z0​ and I−=−V−/Z0​. Matching a termination ZL​ gives the voltage reflection coefficient
ΓL​=ZL​+Z0​ZL​−Z0​​.​
(7)
A matched load absorbs without reflection; an open circuit has ΓL​=1, and a short has ΓL​=−1. Reflections can produce standing waves. A coaxial line confines the fields between conductors and, for a uniform dielectric TEM wave, has v=1/μϵ​. Finite loss attenuates waves; frequency-dependent losses or material properties can also distort pulses. A long line is a wave-carrying distributed system, so it cannot generally be replaced by one lumped capacitor.

Polarization density P is electric dipole moment per unit volume. Displacing positive and negative bound charges leaves the bulk nearly neutral locally but can produce uncompensated charge where polarization varies or terminates. For a dielectric occupying a volume with outward unit normal n,
ρb​=−∇⋅P,σb​=P⋅n.​
(8)

In a linear isotropic dielectric, P=ϵ0​χe​E and D=ϵ0​E+P=ϵE. Gauss's law becomes ∇⋅D=ρf​, separating free from bound charge. Across a static interface, tangential electric field is continuous, while the normal displacement jumps by the free surface charge. Polarization charges alter the internal field, as in the dielectric cylinder calculation. Molecular distortion and orientation contribute differently, and response can depend on temperature and frequency. c OurBigBook.com
Write magnetization as magnetic dipole moment per volume. In weak-field linear response, M=χH and B=μ0​(H+M).
Paramagnetism has positive susceptibility: permanent spin or orbital moments tend to align with a field against thermal disorder. Independent localized moments in the classical weak-field regime give χ∝1/T, the Curie law. Conduction-electron Pauli paramagnetism has a different origin and need not follow that temperature law.
Diamagnetism is the induced magnetic response opposing the applied field, giving negative susceptibility. Changes in orbital motion underlie this response; it exists even without permanent moments. It is often relatively weak and only weakly temperature dependent, although particular quantum systems have stronger or more complicated behaviour.
Ferromagnetism arises from interactions favouring parallel magnetic order. Below the Curie temperature a domain can have spontaneous magnetization even at zero applied field. A sample can nevertheless have nearly zero net moment if differently oriented domains cancel. Domain rearrangement and rotation produce a nonlinear magnetization curve, saturation, remanence and coercivity; hysteresis dissipates energy in a field cycle. Above the ordering temperature the spontaneous order disappears, and a Curie-Weiss susceptibility is a useful mean-field approximation. The signs of weak-field susceptibility distinguish para- and diamagnetism; spontaneous order and domain dynamics distinguish ferromagnetism from independent-moment alignment.

In microscopic SI form, Maxwell's equations couple charge density ρ and current density J to electric and magnetic fields:
∇⋅E=ϵ0​ρ​,∇⋅B=0,∇×E=−∂t​B,∇×B=μ0​J+μ0​ϵ0​∂t​E.
(11)
Gauss's electric law expresses electric flux from charge and reduces to the inverse-square Coulomb field for a point charge. Gauss's magnetic law gives zero net magnetic flux through every closed surface; magnetic field lines do not begin or end on magnetic monopoles in this theory.
Faraday's induction law says changing magnetic flux produces a circulating electric field. For a fixed loop, its circulation is minus the rate of change of enclosed magnetic flux, explaining the sign of induction and Lenz's law. For example, changing a primary coil's electric current changes the flux through a stationary secondary coil and induces a voltage. A moving wire also has the magnetic part of the Lorentz force on its charges; the fixed-loop field equation and the full motional-emf calculation must be distinguished.
The Ampère-Maxwell law relates magnetic circulation to conduction current and displacement current. The latter is essential around a charging capacitor: a surface cutting the lead and another passing through the gap must predict the same circulation. Taking the divergence gives
∇⋅J+∂t​ρ=0,
(12)
using Gauss's electric law. Charge conservation therefore fixes the consistency of the time-dependent equations. In matter, the equivalent macroscopic equations use D and H, free sources and constitutive relations. The Lorentz force density ρE+J×B describes exchange of momentum with charges.
In source-free vacuum, take the curl of Faraday's law and use ∇×(∇×E)=∇(∇⋅E)−∇2E. Both fields satisfy
∇2E=μ0​ϵ0​∂t2​E,∇2B=μ0​ϵ0​∂t2​B,c=μ0​ϵ0​​1​.​
(13)
For a plane wave, ω=ck, k⋅E=k⋅B=0 and B=k^×E/c. The fields are transverse, mutually perpendicular and in phase. Their energy density and energy flux are
u=2ϵ0​E2​+2μ0​B2​,S=μ0​E×B​,∂t​u+∇⋅S=−J⋅E.
(14)
This is Poynting's theorem: field energy is transported and can do work on matter. Electromagnetic radiation is a propagating, energy-carrying solution of the coupled electric and magnetic field equations.

Let a frame's origin have inertial acceleration a0​ and its axes rotate with angular velocity Ω(t). For position r and velocity v′ measured in that frame, Newton's law becomes
ma′=F−ma0​−2mΩ×v′−mΩ˙×r−mΩ×(Ω×r).
(15)
The extra terms are translational, Coriolis, Euler and centrifugal inertial forces. Centrifugal force points away from the rotation axis; Coriolis force deflects moving bodies sideways and does no instantaneous work on their relative motion, since it is perpendicular to v′. The Euler term appears when the rotation rate or axis changes.
For constant Ω, centrifugal force has potential Ucf​=−m∣Ω×r∣2/2. It can be combined with a conservative real potential to find rotating-frame equilibria. The Coriolis term remains velocity dependent. They are essential for trajectories on rotating apparatus and for large-scale terrestrial motions. Frame-dependent terms must be included consistently with the chosen coordinates.

ρ∂t2​u=(λ+μ)∇(∇⋅u)+μ∇2u.

cL​=ρλ+2μ​​,cT​=ρμ​​.​

For a simple compressible one-component equilibrium system, the fundamental thermodynamic relation is
dU=TdS−pdV+μdN.
(42)
The internal energy has natural variables (S,V,N): its derivatives give T, −p and chemical potential μ. The natural variables of a thermodynamic potential identify the independent arguments whose conjugates appear directly in a potential's differential. A Legendre transform exchanges a variable for its conjugate, giving potentials suited to different controlled conditions:
potentialdefinitionnatural variablesdifferential
internal energyUS,V,NdU=TdS−pdV+μdN
enthalpyH=U+pVS,p,NdH=TdS+Vdp+μdN
Helmholtz free energyF=U−TST,V,NdF=−SdT−pdV+μdN
Gibbs free energyG=U+pV−TST,p,NdG=−SdT+Vdp+μdN
For fixed composition and the appropriate closed-system constraints, equilibrium minimizes U at fixed S,V, H at fixed S,p, F at fixed T,V, or G at fixed T,p, with other allowable internal variations understood. Enthalpy is useful for constant-pressure heat exchange with only pressure–volume work. Helmholtz free energy is suited to fixed-temperature, fixed-volume systems, and Gibbs free energy to fixed-temperature, fixed-pressure phase or chemical equilibrium. Coexisting phases of one substance have equal chemical potentials.
Where a potential is twice continuously differentiable, equality of mixed derivatives gives Maxwell relations. At fixed N, the four usual forms are
(∂V∂T​)S​=−(∂S∂p​)V​,(∂p∂T​)S​=(∂S∂V​)p​,​
(43)
(∂V∂S​)T​=(∂T∂p​)V​,(∂p∂S​)T​=−(∂T∂V​)p​.​
(44)
The fixed subscripts and signs follow from the chosen natural variables. Derivatives through a nonsmooth phase boundary require separate phase descriptions rather than automatic use of the smooth single-phase formulas.
As an application, the Helmholtz relation turns pressure data into an isothermal entropy change. For an ideal gas, (∂p/∂T)V​=nR/V, giving
ΔST​=∫V1​V2​​(∂T∂p​)V​dV=nRlnV1​V2​​.​
(45)
Combining the same relation with dU=TdS−pdV yields (∂U/∂V)T​=T(∂p/∂T)V​−p, which vanishes for an ideal gas and reveals attractive-energy effects for a van der Waals gas. Thermodynamic potentials organize equilibrium constraints, while Maxwell relations convert difficult entropy derivatives into measurable response derivatives.
