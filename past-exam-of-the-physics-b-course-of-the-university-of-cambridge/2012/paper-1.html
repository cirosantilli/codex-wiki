In a charge-free region of homogeneous permittivity, Gauss's law and E=−∇V give Laplace's equation, ∇2V=0. Prescribed surface electric potentials specify a Dirichlet problem. Its Uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem permits replacing conductors by fictitious charges outside the physical solution region: a harmonic candidate with the same boundary conditions is the physical solution. For an exterior problem its behaviour at infinity must also be prescribed. For a proof, subtract two candidate potentials to obtain u with zero boundary values. Green's first identity gives ∫∣∇u∣2d3x=0, so u is constant and its boundary value fixes it to zero. Conditions on a normal electric field instead determine a potential only up to an additive constant when the usual compatibility condition holds.
A cylindrical Gaussian surface around an infinite line charge gives Er​=λ/(2πϵ0​r). The electric potential difference is therefore
V(r2​)−V(r1​)=−∫r1​r2​​Er​dr=2πϵ0​λ​lnr2​r1​​.
(9)
An isolated infinite line charge has no finite potential zero at infinity. For the opposite pair, however, the logarithmic growth terms cancel and one can choose V→0 there. Define rb2​=(x−b)2+y2 and rc2​=(x−c)2+y2. superposition gives
V(x,y)=2πϵ0​λ​lnrb​rc​​.
(10)
On x2+y2=bc,

(11)
Thus the circle, and its cylindrical extension, is an equipotential. This is an Apollonius circle: a constant ratio of distances gives a circle rather than a constant distance from either charge.
For the cylinder above the plane, call its centre height H>a and set s=H2−a2​. The cylinder-plane image charges are +λ at height s and −λ at height −s. Their distances are equal on the grounded plane, while their distance ratio is (H+s)/a on the circular surface. A Gaussian surface enclosing the physical cylinder confirms that its charge per unit length is indeed λ. Its voltage is

A normal mode is a motion in which all displacement coordinates oscillate at one angular frequency with fixed amplitude ratios. For small conservative oscillations, the symmetric stiffness and mass matrices give the generalized eigenvalue problem for small oscillations, Ku=ω2Mu. For positive mass and a stable equilibrium, its mass-orthogonal eigenvectors span the motion. A superposition of these modes, with phases and amplitudes fixed by initial displacements and velocities, gives the general solution; different modes need not share a phase.
Use upward end displacements. The spring compressions are x0​−x1​ and x0​−x2​, while the beam's centre rises by q=(x1​+x2​)/2. The potential energy is
V=2k​[(x0​−x1​)2+(x0​−x2​)2]+2mg​(x1​+x2​).
(30)
Static force balance gives 2kx0​=mg, cancelling the linear terms and leaving
V=21​k(x12​+x22​)+kx02​.​
(31)
For a uniform beam, its central moment of inertia in the vertical plane is ml2/12. To first order in the tilt, θ=(x2​−x1​)/l. Its translational and rotational kinetic energies are
T=8m​(x˙1​+x˙2​)2+24m​(x˙2​−x˙1​)2=6m​(x˙12​+x˙1​x˙2​+x˙22​).
(32)
Thus M=(m/6)(21​12​) and K=k1. The normal modes of a rigid beam on two end springs have eigenvectors
us​=(1,1),ωs​=2k/m​;ua​=(1,−1),ωa​=6k/m​.​
(33)
The first translates without tilt; the second rocks about a stationary centre. Ordinary frequencies are ωs​/(2π) and ωa​/(2π).
Assume the system had settled before the end load leaves, and put that load at end 1. Its extra static spring load is Mg at that end and zero at the other. Therefore x1​(0)=−Mg/k, x2​(0)=0, and both initial velocities vanish because no impulse is imparted. Decomposing into the two normal modes gives
x1​(t)=−2kMg​(cosωs​t+cosωa​t),x2​(t)=−2kMg​(cosωs​t−cosωa​t).
(34)
Only the symmetric mode contributes to the centre position:
zCM​(t)−zeq​=−2kMg​cos(m2k​​t).​
(35)
The datum zeq​ is the unloaded beam's equilibrium height; no absolute height is supplied. The rocking mode is present even though it cancels out of this coordinate. These results use small tilt, Mg/(kl)≪1, ideal light vertical springs and negligible damping.

The two conditions at x=0 give B+D=0 and −B+D=0, hence B=D=0. At L, addition and subtraction of X(L)=X′′(L)=0 give CsinhkL=0 and AsinkL=0. Nontrivial positive-frequency modes therefore have C=0 and kn​=nπ/L. Thus the simply supported beam normal modes are

Writing ω1​=π2EI/ρ​/L2, the resulting motion is

(55)
Only odd normal modes occur because the initial profile is symmetric about the midpoint, and their amplitudes decrease as n−3. The frequencies grow as n2, so the motion is dispersive and is not a single shape multiplied by one cosine. Nevertheless the ideal undamped solution is exactly periodic with period 2π/ω1​. At t=π/(2ω1​) all odd-mode cosines vanish, so the bar is instantaneously straight; at t=π/ω1​ it has the opposite initial deflection. The first mode dominates displacement, with third-mode amplitude only 1/27 of the first.
There is initial curvature incompatibility at a moment-free beam end: the prescribed parabola has yxx​(x,0)=2a, whereas a moment-free pivot requires zero curvature at an endpoint. Therefore the data do not define a classical solution with curvature continuous through the two end-time corners. They do define a finite-energy weak solution after release of the forces or end moments that prepared the initial shape. The sine expansion represents that solution: its displacement reproduces the initial parabola, each mode satisfies the released endpoint conditions, and ∑n​n4∣An​∣2<∞ gives finite bending energy. One must not differentiate the initial series twice at its endpoints and assign the interior parabola's curvature there. This qualification leaves the modal displacement prediction intact.
