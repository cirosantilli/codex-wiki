Capacity convention. The printed equation requires c to be volumetric heat capacity, in Jm−3K−1. If “specific heat capacity” means per unit mass instead, the storage term is ρm​c∂t​T and both denominators in the printed equation must contain ρm​c. This distinction is fixed by dimensions.
For a fixed material region V, conservation of thermal energy gives the heat balance with volumetric heat generation,
dtd​∫V​cTdV=−∮∂V​J⋅ndS+∫V​HdV.
(32)
Use the divergence theorem, J=−κ∇T and constant coefficients. Since the region is arbitrary,
c∂t​T=κ∇2T+H,∂t​T=cκ​∇2T+cH​.​
(33)
The heat source is power per volume, while κ is thermal conductivity rather than thermal diffusivity.
For uniform electrical conductivity and imposed current, current density is j=I/(πa2) and Joule heating is H=j2/σ. A long, steady, cylindrically symmetric wire therefore satisfies
s1​dsd​(sdsdT​)=−κH​.
(34)
Integration gives T′=−Hs/(2κ)+C/s. Regularity at s=0 makes C=0, and the surface condition fixes the remaining constant. Thus the Joule-heated cylindrical wire has
T(s)=T0​+4π2a4κσI2​(a2−s2).​
(35)
Here s denotes the printed radial coordinate ρ and avoids confusing it with mass density. The centre is hotter by I2/(4π2a2κσ).
For the air boundary, the internal flux at s=a is −κT′(a)=Ha/2. Equate this to α[T(a)−Tair​]. The Newton cooling of a Joule-heated wire gives
T(a)=Tair​+2αHa​=Tair​+2π2a3σαI2​.​
(36)
Multiplication by circumference 2πa verifies that surface loss per length equals I2/(πa2σ), the electrical power per length. The full air-cooled profile uses this surface temperature plus the same parabolic interior excess, assuming constant material properties and negligible end losses.
