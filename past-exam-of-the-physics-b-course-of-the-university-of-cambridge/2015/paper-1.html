Take the initial thrower and receiver positions to be (−a,0) and (a,0), with counterclockwise angular velocity ω. Interpret v as the launch speed relative to the thrower. The ball inherits the thrower's tangential velocity, so in an inertial frame its initial horizontal velocity is (v,−aω). The flight time across the diameter is tf​≃2a/v. During that time the receiver moves approximately +aωtf​ sideways while the ball moves −aωtf​. Thus the transverse miss on a rotating platform is
d≃2aωtf​=v4a2ω​.​
(2)
The ball passes behind the receiver in the sense of rotation. Equivalently, in the rotating frame its transverse Coriolis acceleration has magnitude 2ωv to leading order; integrating over tf​ gives d=21​(2ωv)tf2​. Terms neglected here are higher order in aω/v. The dynamic viscosity measures the transport of tangential momentum between adjacent layers. The kinetic estimates of gas transport coefficients give
η∼31​nmvλ,λ∼2​nσc​1​,v∝mkB​T​​,
(3)
where n is number density, m the molecular mass, λ the mean free path, and σc​ a collision cross section. At fixed temperature, raising the pressure increases the number of momentum carriers but shortens their mean free path by the same factor. Consequently the dynamic viscosity of a dilute gas is approximately independent of pressure.
For a temperature-independent collision cross section, the same estimate gives η∝mkB​T​/σc​, so η∝T1/2 in the hard-sphere model. Faster molecules carry momentum across layers more rapidly. Real intermolecular interactions make the effective collision cross section temperature-dependent, changing this simple exponent. The pressure-independence also fails when wall collisions, rather than molecule–molecule collisions, set the transport distance; the continuum estimate assumes a small Knudsen number.

The varying magnetic flux through the ring induces an electromotive force by Faraday's law of induction. Its direction follows Lenz's law. With solenoid current Is​, ring current Ir​, ring self-inductance Lr​, electrical resistance R, and mutual inductance M(z),
Lr​I˙r​+RIr​=−MI˙s​,Fz​=Is​Ir​dzdM​.

⟨Is​Ir​⟩=−2(R2+ω2Lr2​)I02​ω2MLr​​,⟨Fz​⟩=−2(R2+ω2Lr2​)I02​ω2MLr​M′​>0.​

In electrostatics, the Maxwell equations in matter are
∇⋅D=ρf​,∇×E=0,D=ϵ0​E+P.
(8)
Here ρf​ is free charge density and P is polarization density. For a homogeneous isotropic linear dielectric, the constitutive equation is D=ϵ0​ϵr​E. Integrating the first equation over a thin pillbox straddling an interface, with unit normal n from medium 1 to medium 2, gives n⋅(D2​−D1​)=σf​. Integrating the second around a vanishingly narrow rectangular loop gives n×(E2​−E1​)=0. These are the electrostatic interface boundary conditions. When there is no free surface electric charge, the normal electric displacement field and tangential electric field are continuous; the normal electric field generally is not.
Neglect end fringing field of a capacitor. In either axial segment of the coaxial cylindrical capacitor, a cylindrical Gaussian surface gives Dr​=λf​/(2πr), where λf​ is free electric charge per length. Integrating the radial electric field between the conductors gives
V=2πϵ0​ϵr​λf​​lnab​,C′=Vλf​​=ln(b/a)2πϵ0​ϵr​​.

C(h)=ln(b/a)2πϵ0​​[L−h+ϵh].​
(10)
Here the question's ϵ is relative permittivity, and air's relative permittivity is approximated by one.

The differential form of Faraday's law of induction is
∇×E=−∂t∂B​.
(16)
By Stokes theorem, for a stationary oriented circuit it becomes E=∮E⋅dℓ=−dΦB​/dt, where ΦB​=∫B⋅dS is magnetic flux and E is electromotive force. Circuit orientation and surface normal obey the right-hand rule. Lenz's law is the physical meaning of the minus sign: the induced current's magnetic field opposes the change in linked magnetic flux. For a moving circuit, the force per unit electric charge is E+v×B, and its line integral is the corresponding electromotive force. Motion through a static magnetic field can therefore induce a voltage even when ∂t​B=0.
Let θ be the wire's angle from the downward vertical, r=Lsinθ, and choose ω and B upward, as in the figure. The motional term along the wire has radial component Bωs at cylindrical radius s. In steady electrical equilibrium, integrating from the grounded axis to the bob gives
Vb​=∫0r​Bωsds=21​Bωr2,Q=CVb​=21​BCωr2.
(17)
The Lorentz force on this positive electric charge is radially outward. Its ratio to the required centripetal term is QB/(mω)≲CB2L2/(2m), so the stated heavy-bob condition makes its effect on the zero-field conical pendulum geometry negligible. With wire tension Tw​, Tw​cosθ=mg and Tw​sinθ=mω2r, giving cosθ=g/(ω2L). Hence the magnetically charged conical pendulum has
Q0​=21​BC(ωL2−ω3g2​).​

cosθ(E)=mω2Lmg−Q0​E​,rδr​=−sin2θcosθδ(cosθ)​=mω4r2Q0​Eg​=2mω3CBgE​.​

δs=−2mωCBLE​(1−3s02​),rδr​=2mω3CBgE​1−s02​1−3s02​​.​

That term is linear in E too; the heavy-bob assumption alone does not justify retaining Q0​E while dropping it. The two expressions refer to the two electrical approximations just specified. The expansion requires a small change relative to the initial radius and is nonuniform as that radius tends to zero.

Alternatively, isolate the charged bob briefly and measure its electric potential with a high-input-impedance electrometer, accounting for the instrument's extra capacitance and for the fact that isolation changes the electrical boundary condition.

The characteristic impedance of a lossless transmission line is the ratio of the voltage to the current amplitude of a single forward-travelling wave. If L and C denote inductance and capacitance per unit length, the lossless telegrapher equations give
∂z​V=−L∂t​I,∂z​I=−C∂t​V,Z=L/C​​,vp​=(LC)−1/2.
(22)
A backward wave has I−​=−V−​/Z when current is positive along +z. Thus characteristic impedance is not a resistor's electrical resistance: it describes travelling-wave amplitudes and determines impedance matching, power transfer and reflections. A semi-infinite line, or a line terminated by a matching load, presents that impedance at its input.
At a junction with no lumped branch, voltage and electric current are continuous:
Vi​+Vr​=Vt​,Z1​Vi​−Vr​​=Z2​Vt​​.
(23)
Eliminating Vt​ gives the voltage reflection coefficient
r=Vi​Vr​​=Z2​+Z1​Z2​−Z1​​.​
(24)
For real positive Z1​, incident and reflected waves occupy the same line, so their power ratio is ∣r∣2.

Choose the guide axis as z, with 0<x<a and 0<y<b. The electric field is along y, parallel to the walls' b dimension. Take a homogeneous lossless nonmagnetic dielectric with relative permittivity ϵ, ideal conducting walls, and time dependence e−iωt. The lowest transverse electric mode of a rectangular waveguide is

The associated normal magnetic field also vanishes at each wall, as verified below. The fundamental sine across the wider dimension minimizes the nonzero transverse wave number; TE00​ is not a physical propagating mode of a hollow simply connected guide.

Ey​=E0​sin(πx/a)e−κz,ℓ−2=κ2=π2(a21​−c24ϵf2​).​

aℓ​=π1−ϵ/4​1​(0<ϵ<4).​

For ϵ>4, β=(π/a)ϵ/4−1​ is real and the mode propagates without axial attenuation in this ideal lossless model. There is no real finite evanescent decay length to continue beyond cutoff. Here attenuation comes from geometric cutoff in a lossless region, with no conductivity and no dissipative power sink; exponential decay alone does not establish absorption.

Use positive normal stress for tension. On a small rectangular material element, τi​ is the normal force per unit area on the face perpendicular to axis i, and ei​=Δℓi​/ℓi​ is the corresponding normal strain. Young's modulus E is the ratio of axial normal stress to axial strain in uniaxial stress; Poisson's ratio σ is minus the transverse-to-axial strain ratio in the same test. Thus isotropic linear elasticity gives Ee1​=τ1​−στ2​−στ3​ and cyclic permutations. The stress symbols here follow the question; σ denotes Poisson's ratio, not a stress component.
For e2​=e3​=0, the two transverse compliance equations imply τ2​=τ3​=στ1​/(1−σ). Substitution into the first gives
Ee1​=1−σ(1+σ)(1−2σ)​τ1​,M=(1+σ)(1−2σ)E(1−σ)​.​
(51)
This uniaxial strain modulus is greater than the uniaxial-stress Young's modulus for the positive Poisson's ratio of interest, because the lateral constraints prevent free Poisson contraction.
For a small twist ϕ, the bonded faces' relative tangential displacement at radius r is rϕ. The layer's shear strain is rϕ/h, so the shear stress is Grϕ/h. Integrating the torque gives
TV​=−hGϕ​∫A​r2dA=−hGϕ​∫0a​2πr3dr=−2π​hGa4​ϕ.​
(52)
The minus sign denotes a restoring torque; the question quotes its magnitude. This uses the disk's polar second moment of area.
For tilt about H, choose y in the plate perpendicular to H. The vertical displacement of the bonded top face is yθ to first order, giving normal strain yθ/h. The thin layer is constrained laterally by bonding, so its leading normal traction is Myθ/h, not Eyθ/h. Integrating its moment about H gives

(53)
since the diametric second moment of area is πa4/4. This is the specified thin-layer approximation to the torsion and tilt stiffness of a bonded elastic disk. Relaxation near the free cylindrical edge is neglected; for a nearly incompressible layer it can limit quantitative accuracy unless the thinness is sufficient.

The coefficient η is dynamic viscosity, with units Pas. Faster layers transfer positive x momentum toward slower neighbours; the internal shear stress resists relative sliding.
