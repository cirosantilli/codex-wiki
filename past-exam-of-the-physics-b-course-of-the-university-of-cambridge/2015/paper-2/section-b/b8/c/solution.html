For positions measured from the chosen origin, the inertia tensor is
Iij​=∫ρ(r)(r2δij​−xi​xj​)d3r.​
(30)
It relates angular momentum and rotational kinetic energy to angular velocity:
L=Iω,Trot​=21​ω⋅Iω.
(31)
The real symmetric tensor has orthogonal principal axes, in which I=diag(I1​,I2​,I3​). Its eigenvalues are nonnegative moments of inertia. Rotation about a principal axis has L∥ω; a general rotation does not. The moment about a unit axis n is In​=n⋅In.
Changing from the centre of mass to a parallel origin displaced by a gives the tensor parallel-axis theorem,
IO​=ICM​+M(a21−aaT).​
(32)
For example, a uniform solid sphere has I=(2MR2/5)1, while a thin rod along x has principal moments 0,Ml2/12,Ml2/12 in the ideal zero-radius limit. The tensor is needed to describe off-axis rotation and torque-free precession; a single scalar inertia only suffices after an axis is specified.[ref]
