In electrostatics, the Maxwell equations in matter are
∇⋅D=ρf​,∇×E=0,D=ϵ0​E+P.
(8)
Here ρf​ is free charge density and P is polarization density. For a homogeneous isotropic linear dielectric, the constitutive equation is D=ϵ0​ϵr​E. Integrating the first equation over a thin pillbox straddling an interface, with unit normal n from medium 1 to medium 2, gives n⋅(D2​−D1​)=σf​. Integrating the second around a vanishingly narrow rectangular loop gives n×(E2​−E1​)=0. These are the electrostatic interface boundary conditions. When there is no free surface electric charge, the normal electric displacement field and tangential electric field are continuous; the normal electric field generally is not.
Neglect end fringing field of a capacitor. In either axial segment of the coaxial cylindrical capacitor, a cylindrical Gaussian surface gives Dr​=λf​/(2πr), where λf​ is free electric charge per length. Integrating the radial electric field between the conductors gives
V=2πϵ0​ϵr​λf​​lnab​,C′=Vλf​​=ln(b/a)2πϵ0​ϵr​​.
(9)
The liquid and air segments share the same conductor electric potential difference, so their capacitances add in parallel. Thus the partially filled coaxial capacitor has
C(h)=ln(b/a)2πϵ0​​[L−h+ϵh].​
(10)
Here the question's ϵ is relative permittivity, and air's relative permittivity is approximated by one.
Write x for the rise above the external level. The wide reservoir keeps that level approximately fixed. At constant applied voltage, one must include work done by the source: the appropriate electrical contribution to the mechanical potential is −CV2/2. The upward dielectric insertion force at fixed voltage is consequently
Fe​=21​V2dxdC​=ln(b/a)πϵ0​(ϵ−1)V2​.
(11)
The excess hydrostatic pressure ρgx acts over the annular area A=π(b2−a2). Alternatively its gravitational potential energy is ρgAx2/2, since liquid initially at the reservoir level must be raised through all heights from zero to x. Balancing the derivatives gives
x=Δh=ρg(b2−a2)ln(b/a)ϵ0​(ϵ−1)V2​.​
(12)
This assumes h+x<L, a communicating liquid reservoir, negligible capillarity, and a sufficiently long capacitor that end effects are small. Differentiating gives the dielectric-rise voltmeter's sensitivity at a bias V:
dVdx​=ρg(b2−a2)ln(b/a)2ϵ0​(ϵ−1)V​.​
(13)
To obtain its numerical value in μmV−1, multiply the right-hand side in SI units by 106. A convenient parametric scale, with β=b/a, is
dVdx​=1.805×10−3(β2−1)lnβϵ−1​(1 VV​)(ρ103 kgm−3​)(a1 mm​)2(g9.81 ms−2​) μmV−1.
(14)
For a concrete estimate, choose a=1mm, b=2mm, mass density ρ=103kgm−3, relative permittivity ϵ=2 and operating voltage V=100V. These are illustrative choices, not supplied apparatus data. They give
dVdx​≃0.087μmV−1,x≃4.3μm.​
(15)
Thus this millimetre-scale example requires fine optical displacement measurement for volt-level changes. The parametric formula states exactly how the estimate changes with the chosen geometry, liquid and bias.
The displacement is quadratic in voltage, cannot distinguish its sign, and has zero first-order sensitivity at zero bias. Smaller gaps and larger relative permittivity increase sensitivity, but capillarity, leakage, dielectric breakdown, temperature-dependent liquid properties, and finite available rise then limit performance. A nonconducting liquid avoids a sustained conduction load, although charging still draws electric charge and leakage is possible. A high voltage can give a readable displacement; a low-voltage instrument would require fine displacement measurement and careful calibration.
