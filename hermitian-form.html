Hermitian form (H(w,v)=H(v,w)​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra Sesquilinear form
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 9
A Hermitian form is a sesquilinear form on a complex vector space satisfying conjugate symmetry,
H(w,v)=H(v,w)​.
(10)
Hence q(v)=H(v,v) is a real number for every vector. In a basis, the matrix of a Hermitian form has Aij​=H(ei​,ej​) and obeys Aji​=Aij​​, equivalently A=A∗: it is a Hermitian matrix. The coordinate rule remains H(x,y)=xTAy​. Conjugate symmetry does not imply positivity.[ref]
The diagonal contains more information than might be expected. For any complex sesquilinear form B, put q(v)=B(v,v). Expanding four expressions gives the complex polarization identity in the linear-first convention:
B(u,v)=41​(q(u+v)−q(u−v)+iq(u+iv)−iq(u−iv)).
(11)
This is an algebraic identity, with no positivity assumption. If every q(v) is real, interchanging u,v preserves the first difference and negates the second: use q(αv)=∣α∣2q(v) for α=−1,i. The identity then gives B(v,u)=B(u,v)​. Thus a complex sesquilinear form is a Hermitian form if and only if all its diagonal values are real. Testing only the basis vectors is insufficient: the off-diagonal example in sesquilinear form has zero diagonal entries in its matrix but a nonreal value at (1,i).
Consider the indefinite Hermitian form
H−​(z,w)=z1​w1​​−z2​w2​​,q−​(z)=∣z1​∣2−∣z2​∣2.
(12)
Its values at e1​,e2​ are 1,−1. Moreover u=(1,1) and v=(1,−1) are nonzero isotropic vectors: q−​(u)=q−​(v)=0, yet H−​(u,v)=2. These vectors do not belong to the radical of a Hermitian form, and the usual Cauchy-Schwarz inequality would incorrectly require 4≤0. An indefinite Hermitian form records signed geometry rather than defining a norm by H(v,v)​.
There is a separate boundary case: H0​(z,w)=z1​w1​​ is a positive semidefinite Hermitian form, since H0​(z,z)=∣z1​∣2≥0, but H0​(e2​,e2​)=0. Its radical of a Hermitian form is the entire second coordinate line. Positive definiteness is exactly the additional condition in a Hermitian inner product. The three examples below show why these distinctions matter: on the real slice z=(x,y)∈R2⊂C2, q+​(z)=∣z1​∣2+2∣z2​∣2 has an ellipse as its unit level, q−​ has hyperbolas, and q0​ has two parallel lines. These are slices of the complex examples, not drawings of their full four-dimensional domains.
