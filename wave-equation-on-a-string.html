Wave equation on a string (ytt​=(T/μ)yxx​,c=T/μ​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Partial differential equation Wave equation
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 18
An ideal uniform, flexible string under constant tension T>0 has transverse displacement y(x,t) satisfying
μytt​=Tyxx​,c=μT​​,
(33)
where μ>0 is linear mass density, meaning mass per unit length of the straight reference string. The approximation assumes small slope ∣yx​∣≪1, transverse motion, negligible bending stiffness, negligible damping, and no distributed external force. Constant tension and uniform linear mass density make c constant. Some texts use ρ for this same linear mass density, giving c=T/ρ​; here ρ must have units kgm−1, rather than units of volume mass density. [ref]
The units provide a useful check: T/μ has units (kgms−2)/(kgm−1)=m2s−2. For T=100N and μ=0.010kgm−1, the wave speed is 100ms−1, so a pulse crosses a one-metre string in 0.010s. Quadrupling tension doubles wave speed; quadrupling linear mass density halves it. These square-root laws explain how material and loading set the time scale of the vibration.
For a length-L string with fixed endpoints, the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions are y(0,t)=y(L,t)=0. Its nonzero normal modes have
ϕn​(x)=sinLnπx​,ωn​=Lcnπ​,νn​=2πωn​​=2Lcn​,n=1,2,….
(34)
Thus cnπ/L is an angular frequency, in radians per second, whereas cn/(2L) is the frequency in hertz. The wavelength is 2L/n, and each node of a standing wave lies at x=jL/n for some j=0,…,n. For the numerical string above with L=1m, the first three frequencies are 50, 100 and 150Hz, with angular frequencies 100π, 200π and 300πs−1. [ref]
Each normal mode is a standing wave, but it also has a travelling wave description:
sin(kx)cos(ckt)=21​sin(k(x−ct))+21​sin(k(x+ct)).
(35)
At a fixed endpoint an incident displacement pulse reflects with reversed sign, allowing incident and reflected displacements to cancel at the endpoint. Periodic reflection for a fixed-end string implements both endpoints through odd, 2L-periodic extensions of the initial data. This is the bridge between moving pulses and the stationary Fourier sine basis. [ref]
A short derivation comes from Newton's second law. The transverse force on a small segment is approximately T[yx​(x+δx,t)−yx​(x,t)], and its mass is μδx. Dividing the force balance by δx and taking the limit gives μytt​=Tyxx​. Separation of variables then gives ϕ′′+k2ϕ=0; the fixed-end boundary conditions select k=nπ/L, while the temporal equation is a harmonic oscillator equation with ω=ck.
