Near-field diffraction laboratory experiment

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics Physical optics Fresnel diffraction
OurBigBook.com Words: 244
A documented experiment uses curved illumination rather than the uniform Fresnel slit propagation model. Laboratory Exercise No. 4: Diffraction, MIT 6.237, Fall 2024, printed pp. 5–6, gives a helium–neon laser at 632.8nm, a microscope objective optical lens, a circular aperture and a screen 5–40 cm from the object. Preparation includes comparing central brightness in Fresnel diffraction and Fraunhofer diffraction. A 40× objective produces a nonplanar incident wavefront; the opening is placed less than 1 cm from it. With objective-to-screen distance fixed, students move the opening, sketch patterns, and record positions of consecutive central nulls to estimate aperture size. The Fraunhofer diffraction pattern supplies a regime comparison. A separate spherical-obstacle task observes a bright centre inside the shadow. The handout supplies procedures, rather than measured distances or profiles.[ref]
The circular-opening test probes cancellation of complex amplitudes at the centre while the source wavefront curvature changes. To interpret it, include that curvature in the aperture field U0​ and use a circular aperture, rather than substituting the plane-illuminated Fresnel slit propagation profile. Useful additional controls are to retain the same laser and opening, centre the illumination, repeat position readings and check background with the beam blocked. If a camera replaces the visual screen readout, avoid saturation and calibrate its position scale. Alignment, imperfect aperture edges, illumination variation and limited contrast near a null constrain the inferred size. A bright central obstacle spot illustrates why the sharp shadows of geometrical optics can fail; it does not imply that every Fresnel diffraction pattern has a bright centre.
