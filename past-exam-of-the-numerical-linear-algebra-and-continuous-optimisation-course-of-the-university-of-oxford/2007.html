The outer product has entries (xyT)ij​=xi​yj​​. Every column is a multiple of x, and at least one column is nonzero because y=0. Its column space is therefore exactly span{x}, giving rank(xyT)=1​.
A Householder matrix is H=I−2vvT/(vTv), v=0. With P=vvT/(vTv) one has PT=P and P2=P, so HTH=(I−2P)2=I. Thus it is an orthogonal matrix and is its own inverse.
To express an arbitrary orthogonal Q as a product of these reflections, argue by induction on its dimension. Its first column q is a unit vector. If q=e1​, take v=q−e1​. Then vTq=1−q1​ and vTv=2(1−q1​), so the corresponding reflection satisfies Hq=q−v=e1​. If q=e1​, no reflection is required. The resulting orthogonal matrix has first column e1​; orthogonality of its columns then makes its first row e1T​ as well. It is consequently diag(1,Q′) with Q′ orthogonal. Apply the induction hypothesis to Q′ and embed its reflections in the full space. In dimension one the only possibilities are 1, the empty product, and −1, itself a reflection. Reversing the performed transformations proves the Householder factorization of an orthogonal matrix, with at most the dimension many reflections.

Direct solution usually uses a pivoted LU decomposition PA=LU: solve Lz=Pb by forward substitution, then Ux=z by back substitution. For a symmetric positive-definite matrix, the Cholesky decomposition is
A=CCT,​
(4)
with C lower triangular and positive diagonal. It exploits symmetry, requiring about n3/3 arithmetic operations rather than the leading 2n3/3 for general elimination.
If A has full column rank and its reduced QR decomposition is A=QR, QTQ=I, then
ATA=RTR.​
(5)
Choose the signs of Q,R so that R has positive diagonal. Then R is precisely the upper Cholesky factor of the normal-equation matrix ATA, or RT is its lower factor. This identifies the factors; in computation, obtaining R directly by QR decomposition avoids explicitly forming the normal equations, which square the condition number.

The matrix splitting produces Mx(k+1)=Nx(k)+b. If the iterates converge to z, passing to the limit yields Mz=Nz+b, hence Az=b. Thus any finite limit solves the system; when A is nonsingular it is the unique solution.
The iteration matrix is G=M−1N=I−M−1A. For M symmetric positive definite, its positive-definite square root gives
M1/2GM−1/2=I−M−1/2AM−1/2,​
(6)
which is symmetric when A is symmetric. Let θi​ be the eigenvalues of A=M−1/2AM−1/2. In its orthonormal eigenbasis the transformed error is multiplied by 1−θi​ at each step. Therefore convergence to the unique solution for every starting vector is equivalent to
∣1−θi​∣<1 for all i⟺0<θi​<2 for all i.​
(7)
Sufficiency follows because each scalar mode tends to zero. Necessity follows by starting with a nonzero error in any mode whose multiplier has modulus at least one. Equivalently the condition says A and 2M−A are positive definite​, by congruence of their quadratic forms.
Under the usual nonsingularity assumption, a symmetric A that is not positive definite has a negative quadratic direction. Its congruent A then has a negative eigenvalue, making an error multiplier 1−θi​>1. A starting error in that mode diverges, proving the final claim in that setting.
If singular matrices are permitted, the last claim needs qualification. A zero eigenvalue gives a persistent error mode, but the iterates may still converge to another solution of a compatible singular system. For example A=0, M=I, b=0 makes every iterate constant and already a solution, despite A not being positive definite. Thus literal nonconvergence cannot be deduced from a zero eigenvalue alone.

A multigrid method alternates local error damping with correction on coarser grids. For clarity write Ah​=h−2 times the five-point stencil and Ah​uh​=fh​, incorporating Dirichlet boundary data into the right side. One two-grid cycle proceeds as follows.
  • Apply a few smoothing sweeps, for example weighted Jacobi iteration with weight 4/5, to damp grid-scale oscillations in the error.
  • Form the fine-grid linear-system residual rh​=fh​−Ah​uh​ and restrict it to the grid of spacing 2h. An example is the full-weighting restriction stencil 161​(121​242​121​), centered at a coarse-grid point.
  • Solve the coarse error equation A2h​e2h​=Rrh​, with homogeneous error boundary data. The coarse-grid operator may be the rediscretized Poisson operator at spacing 2h, or the Galerkin coarse-grid operator A2h​=RAh​P with consistent transfer operators.
  • Prolong the correction to the fine grid, for example by bilinear interpolation, and update uh​←uh​+Pe2h​.
  • Apply a few further smoothing sweeps to damp oscillatory components introduced by interpolation.
The coarse solve is performed recursively, with a direct solve on the smallest grid, giving a V-cycle. Smoothing treats oscillations that are cheap for local relaxation to remove; coarse-grid correction treats errors that appear smooth on the fine grid. The scaling of the residual and coarse operator must agree if one instead uses the paper's unscaled stencil.

Every point in the affine search space is uniquely xk​=x0​+Vk​y, since the columns of Vk​ form a basis. Its linear-system residual is rk​=r0​−AVk​y, so minimizing the residual is exactly the linear least-squares problem miny​∥r0​−AVk​y∥2​.
Use the standard starting choice v1​=r0​/∥r0​∥2​ and an orthonormal extended basis Vk+1​. If AVk​=Vk+1​Lk​, then r0​=Vk+1​(∥r0​∥2​e1​) and orthonormality gives
∥r0​−AVk​y∥2​=​∥r0​∥2​e1​−Lk​y​2​.​
(12)
The starting-vector choice is needed for this particular e1​ representation; an arbitrary orthonormal basis need not have those initial coordinates.
The Arnoldi iteration constructs the basis. At column j, orthogonalize Avj​ against v1​,…,vj​, recording coefficients hij​=viT​Avj​; normalize the remaining vector with hj+1,j​ to get vj+1​. This makes Lk​ an upper Hessenberg matrix of size (k+1)×k. The resulting GMRES algorithm starts from x0​,r0​, grows this Arnoldi basis, solves the small least-squares problem, and sets xk​=x0​+Vk​y. Its small residual norm provides the stopping test. Incremental Givens rotations efficiently update the small QR decomposition instead of solving it anew. Zero initial residual means the system is already solved; Arnoldi breakdown gives an invariant subspace and, for nonsingular A, permits exact solution within it.

At the current point x, a trust-region method approximately minimizes a local quadratic model
m(s)=f(x)+g(x)Ts+21​sTBssubject to ∥s∥2​≤Δ,
(15)
where B is a symmetric Hessian approximation and Δ>0 is the trust-region radius. For a step with positive predicted decrease, compare the true and predicted reductions through
ρ=m(0)−m(s)f(x)−f(x+s)​.​
(16)
Choose fixed thresholds 0<η1​<η2​<1. If ρ<η1​, the step is unsuccessful: reject it and reduce the radius. If η1​≤ρ<η2​, accept the successful step and normally retain the radius. If ρ≥η2​, the step is very successful: accept it and permit an increase in radius, especially when the step reaches its boundary. The ratio tests whether the local model is accurate enough to justify moving. An approximate subproblem solver should deliver at least the model decrease achieved at the Cauchy point.

The sequential quadratic programming step minimizes a quadratic local objective model while enforcing the first-order constraint expansion c(xk​+s)≈ck​+Ak​s=0. When the iterate and multipliers are close to a regular solution and Bk​ approximates the Lagrangian Hessian, the resulting correction approximates a Newton step for the nonlinear KKT conditions.

Two reasons to prefer more advanced methods for smooth, high-accuracy optimization are:
  • Slow progress on ill-conditioned problems. On an elongated quadratic level set, successive negative gradients can zigzag across the narrow valley. Convergence can be only linear with a factor close to one, and line search cannot remove the underlying poor scaling.
  • Failure to retain curvature information. Each new step again uses only the current gradient. Newton's method and quasi-Newton methods exploit curvature to obtain substantially faster local convergence under suitable hypotheses, often compensating for their more expensive steps.
The question's blanket wording is too strong: the steepest descent method and related gradient algorithms are used in practice, especially when problem size, cheap gradients or simplicity make curvature-based alternatives costly. The objections describe limitations, not universal nonuse.

Write the constraint as c(x)=1−xTx≥0 and its multiplier as y≥0. The KKT conditions become
(A+yI)x=0,∥x∥2​≤1,y≥0,y(1−∥x∥22​)=0.​

If A is positive definite, A+yI is positive definite for every y≥0, so x=0, y=0​ is the only critical point.
For indefinite A, the origin remains a critical point, contrary to an unqualified reading of the question. Every nonzero critical point is an eigenvector with Ax=−yx​, so its eigenvalue is nonpositive. A negative-eigenvalue vector must lie on the unit sphere, with multiplier equal to the negative of that eigenvalue. A nonzero vector in kerA may have any norm at most one and has multiplier zero. Positive-eigenvalue directions do not satisfy the multiplier sign condition. These first-order critical points include saddles; the global minimum selects a unit vector in the lowest-eigenvalue eigenspace.

Along a fixed nonzero branch, ∥x(μ)∥22​=1+μ/λ1​→1 and the radial Hessian eigenvalue grows like 4λ12​/μ. The nonzero transverse eigenvalues 2(λi​−λ1​) stay fixed. If λ1​ is simple, the ratio of the largest to smallest absolute eigenvalue therefore tends to infinity. If it repeats, the Hessian is already singular because of the zero transverse directions; there is no finite spectral condition number to begin with. These conclusions concern the nonzero branches: at the stationary origin H=2(A+μI), and the same blowup is not implied.
Ill-conditioning does not invalidate barrier minimizers as approximations to the constrained solution. Let λmin​<0. At radius r, the smallest possible quadratic value is λmin​r2, so global barrier minimization reduces to minimizing λmin​t−μlog(1−t) for 0≤t<1. For 0<μ<−λmin​ its unique minimizing radius satisfies
r2=1+μ/λmin​,x(μ)=rvmin​,∥vmin​∥2​=1.​
(46)
Thus every limiting unit vector in that eigenspace solves the original problem, with objective λmin​. For μ≥−λmin​ the barrier minimizer is the origin instead. The increasing condition number concerns the numerical difficulty of small-parameter subproblems, which can call for scaling or a primal-dual interior-point method; it does not prevent the mathematical convergence just established. This is the quadratic-ball barrier eigenvalue branch.
