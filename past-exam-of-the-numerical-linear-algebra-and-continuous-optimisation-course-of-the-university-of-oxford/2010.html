An orthogonal projector sends x to the foot Px of its perpendicular onto the image of a linear map rangeP. The residual r=x−Px is orthogonal to the entire range, not just to the single vector Px. Indeed, for any range vector Pv,
(Pv)Tr=vTPT(I−P)x=vT(P−P2)x=0.
(1)
In particular (Px)T(x−Px)=0​. The orthogonal projection diagram illustrates the resulting right triangle.

The vector Euclidean norm is ∥x∥2​=(xTx)1/2=(i∑​xi2​)1/2​, the ordinary length from the origin. The induced matrix norm is
∥A∥2​=x=0sup​∥x∥2​∥Ax∥2​​=∥x∥2​=1max​∥Ax∥2​.​
(2)
Geometrically this is the largest stretch of a unit vector. A linear map sends the unit sphere to an ellipsoid, possibly degenerate; its largest semiaxis has length ∥A∥2​. Equivalently the matrix 2-norm is the largest singular value.

The nullspace is the vector subspace kerA={x∈Rn:Ax=0}​. A full real singular value decomposition is
A=UΣVT,U∈Rm×m,Σ∈Rm×n,V∈Rn×n,​
(4)
where U,V are orthogonal matrices and Σij​=0 for i=j, with Σjj​=σj​≥0 for 1≤j≤n. We may order σ1​≥⋯≥σn​≥0. The columns of V form an orthonormal basis of the input space Rn, and the columns of U form one of the output space Rm. The positive singular values number exactly the matrix rank.

Let r be the matrix rank, and partition the right singular vectors as V=(Vr​ V0​), with V0​∈Rn×(n−r) consisting of the columns associated with zero singular values. From Ax=0⟺ΣVTx=0, these columns form an orthonormal basis of the nullspace. Therefore its orthogonal projector is
PkerA​=V0​V0T​=In​−Vr​VrT​.​
(5)
It acts on the input space Rn. Symmetry and idempotence follow from V0T​V0​=In−r​. At full column rank it is zero, and at rank zero it is the identity.

At elimination column k, let r=m−k be the size of the remaining trailing block. Computing its multipliers costs r divisions; updating all r2 entries costs 2r2 floating-point operations, one multiplication and one subtraction per entry. Thus the dominant count is
∑r=1m−1​(2r2+r)=3m(m−1)(2m−1)​+2m(m−1)​=32​m3+O(m2)​.
(6)
Updating the right side and back substitution add only O(m2) floating-point operations. Pivot searches involve comparisons and row exchanges, without changing the leading arithmetic count. Hence Gaussian elimination with pivoting has the requested asymptotic cost.

Solution

b OurBigBook.com
An orthogonal similarity transformation is H=QTAQ with QTQ=I. An upper Hessenberg matrix has Hij​=0 whenever i>j+1; its first subdiagonal may be nonzero.
At step k=1,…,m−2, choose a Householder reflector Qk​=diag(Ik​,Q​k​) whose trailing block sends the current column segment in rows k+1,…,m to a multiple of its first coordinate. The left multiplication zeros positions (k+2,k),…,(m,k). Apply the matching right multiplication to retain similarity: A←QkT​AQk​. Since Qk​ acts only on rows and columns k+1,…,m, earlier Hessenberg zeros are preserved. Finally Q=Q1​⋯Qm−2​ gives H=QTAQ.
Each reflector is applied through a rank-one update, costing O(m2) rather than a full matrix multiplication. Across m−2 steps, the Hessenberg reduction costs O(m3).

Solution
Words: 122
c OurBigBook.com
Solving the m unrelated shifted systems by separate Gaussian elimination costs 32​m4+O(m3)​ floating-point operations.
Instead compute H=QTAQ once in O(m3) work. For each shift, solve
(H−λj​I)zj​=QTbj​,xj​=Qzj​.
(7)
The shift changes only the diagonal, so H−λj​I remains an upper Hessenberg matrix. In each elimination column only the entry immediately below the pivot needs removal. With partial pivoting the only possible nonzero candidate rows are the current row and the next, and each update touches O(m) entries. Factorization therefore costs O(m2) per shift; the triangular solve and both orthogonal-vector transformations also cost O(m2) each.
Thus m shifted solves plus the initial Hessenberg reduction cost O(m3)​ in total. Pivoting is still needed in general: nonsingularity excludes a singular system but does not by itself guarantee that every unpivoted elimination pivot is nonzero.

The off-diagonal Toeplitz matrix T=A−I is symmetric and has row and column absolute sums bounded by
r=2∑j=2m​∣tj​∣≤32​.
(8)
For an elementary Euclidean bound, apply the weighted Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in each row:
∣(Tx)i​∣2≤(∑j​∣Tij​∣)(∑j​∣Tij​∣∣xj​∣2).
(9)
Sum over i and use both absolute-sum bounds to get ∥Tx∥22​≤r2∥x∥22​. Therefore
∥T∥2​≤2j=2∑m​∣tj​∣≤32​.​
(10)
The entry t1​ is unused because the diagonal of A is fixed to one; including it only weakens the bound. This is the row-column bound for the matrix 2-norm.

An eigenvalue of A is a scalar λ for which Az=λz has a nonzero eigenvector. Allowing initially z∈Cm, symmetry of the real matrix gives A∗=A, so z∗Az is real. Since z∗z>0,
λ=z∗zz∗Az​∈R.​
(12)

f OurBigBook.com
The standard conjugate gradient convergence bound is
∥ek​∥A​≤2(κ​+1κ​−1​)k∥e0​∥A​.
(15)
Here κ≤5, so the factor in parentheses is at most q=(3−5​)/2≃0.381966. Since 2q25<10−10, 25 iterations suffice to reduce the energy-norm error by a factor exceeding 1010.
There are at most m/20 nonzero entries of t, and each contributes to at most two off-diagonals. Thus A−I has at most m2/10 nonzero entries. Initialize a matrix-vector product with the diagonal contribution p, which costs no arithmetic because the diagonal is one. Each off-diagonal term needs one multiplication and one addition, giving at most m2/5 floating-point operations per product.
A standard conjugate gradient step adds two inner products, three vector updates and two scalar divisions, at most 10m further operations. Starting from x0​=0 makes r0​=b and avoids an initial matrix-vector product; its squared norm needs 2m−1 operations. The total for 25 steps is at most
25(m2/5+10m)+2m−1=5m2+252m−1<8m2(m≥84).​
(16)
This supplies an explicit meaning of the paper's large-m qualification. The quoted conjugate gradient convergence bound is the usual exact-arithmetic result.

Solution

i OurBigBook.com
At an unconstrained local minimizer of a twice continuously differentiable objective function, the first-order optimality condition is ∇f(x∗​)=0​. The second-order necessary condition is dT∇2f(x∗​)d≥0 for every direction d, so the Hessian matrix is positive semidefinite.
For the Euclidean norm, the unit steepest descent direction is −g/∥g∥2​ when g=∇f(x)=0; the algorithm usually uses the unnormalized direction p=−g. It is a descent direction because gTp=−∥g∥22​<0​.
Two limitations of unpreconditioned steepest descent method in high-accuracy smooth optimization are its slow zigzagging on ill-conditioned valleys and its sensitivity to scaling of the coordinates. It uses no curvature information to correct those directions, so it can require many function and gradient evaluations compared with a suitable Newton or quasi-Newton method. These are reasons to prefer curvature-aware methods in that setting, rather than a claim that gradient-based methods are never useful.

The descent lemma follows by integrating the gradient along the line and using its Lipschitz continuity:
f(x+αp)≤f(x)+αg(x)Tp+2γ​α2∥p∥22​,α≥0.
(17)
This upper bound is at most the right side of the Armijo condition when α[(1−β)gTp+γα∥p∥22​/2]≤0. Since gTp<0, it holds for
0≤α≤αmax​=γ∥p∥22​2(1−β)(−gTp)​=γ∥p∥22​2(β−1)gTp​.​
(18)
The zero step gives equality; every positive step in this interval satisfies the prescribed sufficient-decrease test.

The quadratic model has gradient g(xk​)+Bk​p and positive-definite Hessian matrix Bk​. Its unique minimizer is therefore
pk​=−Bk−1​g(xk​).​
(19)
If g(xk​)=0, then pk​=0 and g(xk​)Tpk​=−pkT​Bk​pk​<0, proving that it is a descent direction.
Backtracking line search starts with a trial step and repeatedly reduces it until the Armijo condition holds. Part (ii) guarantees finite termination because sufficiently small steps are acceptable. The test guarantees a specified positive fraction of the decrease predicted by the linear model, namely f(xk​+αpk​)≤f(xk​)+αβg(xk​)Tpk​<f(xk​) for an accepted positive step. It does not require solving the line-minimization problem exactly.

Use the optimization Lagrangian L(x,y)=f(x)−yTc(x), with A(x)=Dc(x) and H(x,y)=∇xx2​L(x,y). Under the usual linear independence constraint qualification, a local minimizer has multipliers y∗​ satisfying the equality-constrained Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions
c(x∗​)=0,g(x∗​)−A(x∗​)Ty∗​=0.​
(25)
The second-order necessary condition is
dTH(x∗​,y∗​)d≥0for every d∈kerA(x∗​).​
(26)
Only feasible tangent directions enter this last test; the Hessian matrix need not be positive semidefinite on the entire ambient space.

ii

Words: 163 Articles: 1
5 OurBigBook.com
Solution
Words: 163
ii OurBigBook.com
With y=−c/μ, differentiation of the quadratic penalty method objective gives
∇Pμ​=g−ATy,∇2Pμ​=H(x,y)+μ−1ATA.​
(27)
The primal Newton system is therefore
(H+μ−1ATA)p=−g+ATy.​
(28)
For bounded multipliers and regular derivatives, H remains of ordinary size while μ−1ATA becomes large in directions normal to the constraints. If the feasible tangent space is nontrivial and retains ordinary-size curvature, this produces a large condition number as μ↓0.
Introduce w=Ap/μ. The same step is obtained from the symmetric augmented system
(HA​AT−μI​)(pw​)=(−g+ATy0​).​
(29)
Eliminating w recovers the primal system exactly. This matrix contains no explicit μ−1 entries and tends to a saddle-point matrix. Under full row rank of the limiting constraint Jacobian matrix and nonsingularity of the limiting reduced Hessian, that limiting matrix is nonsingular, so this formulation avoids the artificial penalty-induced separation of scales.
These conditioning statements require regularity beyond bounded y alone. A rank-deficient constraint Jacobian matrix or singular reduced Hessian can still make the augmented system ill-conditioned. If m=n and A is nonsingular, all directions may scale together in the primal system, so a large condition number is not automatic there either.

Solution
Words: 77
iii OurBigBook.com
Complete the square in the augmented Lagrangian method objective:
Lμ​(x,u)=f(x)+2μ1​∥c(x)−μu∥22​−2μ​∥u∥22​.​
(30)
For fixed u,μ, the last term is constant in x. Thus its minimization is the quadratic penalty method applied to the shifted constraint c(x)=μu.
Its practical advantage is that multiplier updates, typically u+=u−c(x)/μ, can drive the original constraint residual to zero while μ stays moderately sized. The plain quadratic penalty method generally needs μ very small to produce the same feasibility accuracy, exposing the conditioning problem in part (ii).

The linearized constraints may be inconsistent, for example when −c(xk​) is not in the image of a linear map defined by A(xk​). Even if feasible, the quadratic objective may have negative curvature along its feasible tangent space, making it unbounded below and leaving no local minimizer.
Fletcher's Sℓ1QP replaces the hard linearized constraints by an L1 exact penalty and bounds the step inside a trust region. One usual form is
∥s∥∞​≤Δk​min​ gkT​s+21​sTHk​s+ρk​∥ck​+Ak​s∥1​,ρk​>0.​
(31)
The zero step is always admissible. The trust region is compact, so a minimum exists even when the model Hessian matrix is indefinite; the constraint penalty measures infeasibility rather than making the subproblem impossible to solve. Adjusting its weight promotes feasibility, while the radius keeps the local model within a controlled neighbourhood. Introducing nonnegative positive- and negative-part slack variables converts the absolute values to an equivalent smooth quadratic program with box constraints.[ref]

v OurBigBook.com
Stationarity of the equality-constrained quadratic model gives gk​+Hk​sk​−AkT​yk+1​=0, while feasibility gives Ak​sk​=−ck​. A symmetric system linking the step and new multiplier is
(Hk​−Ak​​−AkT​0​)(sk​yk+1​​)=(−gk​ck​​).​
(32)
Let Z have columns forming a basis of kerAk​. The saddle-point matrix is nonsingular exactly when Ak​ has full row rank and ZTHk​Z is nonsingular. Full row rank is necessary because a nonzero vector in kerAkT​ would give a null vector of the block matrix. Under full row rank, solving a homogeneous system forces s=Zq and then ZTHk​Zq=0; this proves sufficiency and the converse. Positive definiteness of the reduced Hessian is sufficient for a constrained minimum but is stronger than the nonsingularity condition asked here.
A null-space method first finds sp​ with Ak​sp​=−ck​, then solves
ZTHk​Zq=−ZT(gk​+Hk​sp​),sk​=sp​+Zq,
(33)
and recovers yk+1​ from AkT​yk+1​=gk​+Hk​sk​. It needs the stated full row rank and nonsingular reduced matrix; Cholesky decomposition is available when the latter is positive definite.
A range-space method additionally assumes Hk​ is invertible. It solves the Schur complement equations
Ak​Hk−1​AkT​yk+1​=Ak​Hk−1​gk​−ck​,sk​=−Hk−1​gk​+Hk−1​AkT​yk+1​.
(34)
If Hk​ is positive definite and Ak​ has full row rank, the Schur matrix is positive definite and both solves can use Cholesky decomposition. An indefinite but invertible Hk​ instead requires a suitable indefinite factorization; the Schur matrix must remain nonsingular.

c(x∗​)≥0,y∗​≥0,C(x∗​)y∗​=0,g(x∗​)−A(x∗​)Ty∗​=0.​

At the current exact path point v(μ), stationarity is already zero and Cy=μe. Therefore Fμˉ​​(v(μ))=(0,(μ−μˉ​)e)T. Its Newton correction solves
J(v(μ))ΔvN​=(0(μˉ​−μ)e​).​

ΔvN​=(μˉ​−μ)v′(μ)​
