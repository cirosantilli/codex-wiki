Qubit (H≅C2)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Quantum mechanics Quantum system Quantum state
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 1
A qubit, or quantum bit, is a quantum system whose state space is a two-dimensional complex Hilbert space. Choose an orthonormal basis ∣0⟩,∣1⟩, called the computational basis. A pure state has a normalized representative ∣ψ⟩=α∣0⟩+β∣1⟩, where α,β are complex probability amplitudes and ∣α∣2+∣β∣2=1. General qubit states, including mixed states, require a density matrix. [ref].
The abstract qubit describes the chosen degree of freedom: for spin one-half, ∣0⟩,∣1⟩ can label spin up and down along a specified axis; for photon polarization in a fixed propagation mode, they can label horizontal and vertical polarization, ∣H⟩,∣V⟩. These implementations share the same two-dimensional linear algebra, but their preparation and measurement in quantum mechanics use different apparatus. A qubit therefore need not be an entire two-level particle. [ref].
The usefulness of a qubit lies in controlling quantum superpositions and their relative quantum phases, then converting them into outcome probabilities with quantum gates and an appropriate measurement. One ideal computational-basis measurement returns one label, zero or one. It does not reveal both probability amplitudes, their relative quantum phase, or all the bits of a larger register's expansion. Quantum state tomography instead estimates a qubit state using many repetitions of the preparation and suitable measurement settings. [ref].
An actual preparation and measurement example is Dederick and Beck's 2014 teaching-laboratory experiment. A 405-nm laser pumped two nonlinear crystals, producing signal and idler photons near 810 nm. In their non-entangled run the source produced ∣H⟩s​∣H⟩i​; a quarter-wave plate in the signal arm changed its photon polarization to ∣L⟩=(∣H⟩+i∣V⟩)/2​ in their convention. The plate supplies a relative quantum phase between polarization components. An idler detection selected the signal trials; signal detectors measured horizontal/vertical, diagonal, and circular polarization bases on separate runs. Before the polarization analyzer, a half-wave plate at 22.5 degrees selected the diagonal basis, and a quarter-wave plate at 45 degrees selected the circular basis. Coincidence counts NAB​,NAB′​ gave the conditional positive-outcome frequency NAB​/(NAB​+NAB′​). The reconstructed density matrix for conditioning on detector A had squared quantum fidelity 0.96 with ∣L⟩ and purity of a density operator 0.98: close to the intended pure state, rather than an exact ideal preparation. [ref].
The model diagram explains why those settings matter. With ∣0⟩=∣H⟩ and ∣1⟩=∣V⟩, the intended ∣L⟩ gives equal probabilities in both the horizontal/vertical computational basis and diagonal Hadamard basis, just as the mixed state I/2 does. In the circular basis ∣±i⟩=(∣0⟩±i∣1⟩)/2​, however, the ideal ∣L⟩=∣+i⟩ always gives the plus outcome, whereas I/2 still gives equal probabilities. These are deductions from the Born rule, not the paper's measured counts. Unequal detector efficiencies and finite samples must be accounted for when interpreting experimental frequencies; the ideal comparison assumes equally efficient detection.
