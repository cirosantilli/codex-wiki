Charles Darwin was neither the first nor the only pre-1859 advocate of changing species. The claim needs a second qualification: believing that species have histories is not the same as identifying natural selection, and neither entails that every lineage changes continuously at a constant rate. Several rival explanations were available before the publication of On the Origin of Species by Charles Darwin.
Jean-Baptiste Lamarck's 1809 programme is an unambiguous earlier example. In Zoological Philosophy by Jean-Baptiste Lamarck, forms which appear stable over a short interval can change through the accumulated effects of circumstances, altered habits, use and disuse, and transmission of acquired modifications. His separate account of increasing organizational complexity also mattered. This was a substantial causal programme, rather than a passing acknowledgment that individuals differ. It was not, however, an anticipation of every feature of Charles Darwin's explanation: an internally ordered tendency toward complexity and a response produced through an organism's activities differ from the sorting of heritable differences through unequal reproductive success.[ref]
Robert Chambers's anonymously published Vestiges of the Natural History of Creation by Robert Chambers supplied another public alternative in 1844. It placed the succession of living forms within a law-governed history extending from the formation of the heavens to human beings. Its treatment of development was speculative and did not contain the developed selection mechanism of On the Origin of Species by Charles Darwin. Nevertheless, its very existence rules out an intellectual landscape in which an isolated Charles Darwin first introduced the possibility of organic change to an otherwise uniformly fixist audience. The difference between a privately developed theory and a book circulating publicly is especially important here.[ref]
Alfred Russel Wallace provides the decisive counterexample even to the narrower claim of unique possession of natural selection. His 1858 essay argued that varieties could persist and depart progressively from their original type through differences in their capacity to survive under particular conditions. The materials jointly communicated to the Linnean Society on 1 July 1858 included his essay, extracts from Charles Darwin's earlier work and an account of his thinking in correspondence. The introductory communication explicitly presented the two as independent originators. This public event preceded the 1859 book and is stronger evidence than an attempt to infer influence from a loose resemblance between two later texts.[ref]
There were also resources and disagreements beyond professed transformists. Geology supplied evidence of a long history; palaeontology documented succession and extinction; geographical distribution raised questions about affinities between inhabitants of neighbouring regions. Yet a commitment to extinction did not logically entail common descent, and an appeal to slowly acting geological causes did not oblige an author to accept changing species. The historical question is which inferences particular investigators drew from shared evidence, not whether a predecessor can be recruited to a modern camp merely because one premise resembles a later one.
What distinguished the 1859 synthesis was the scale and integration of the argument. Variation, heredity, the multiplication of organisms and limited opportunities for reproduction made unequal success consequential across generations. Branching descent could then connect adaptation, classification, geographical distribution and the fossil record. The theory did not require a breeder intentionally choosing the direction of change. At the same time, the first edition discussed other possible causes of variation and acknowledged serious difficulties. It should not be retrospectively equipped with a later genetic account of heredity.[ref]
The appropriate judgment separates novelty of a general possibility, novelty of a mechanism and persuasiveness of a synthesis. Earlier and contemporary transformists defeat the first claim of solitary originality; the 1858 communication defeats the strongest version of the second. Neither fact erases Charles Darwin's achievement in constructing a sustained case. Equally, the premise of constant change is too crude: stable conditions, unequal rates and lineages persisting with little visible modification are compatible with a history of descent. Historical priority is best assessed by identifying the actual claim and evidence, rather than allocating one indivisible discovery called evolution.
