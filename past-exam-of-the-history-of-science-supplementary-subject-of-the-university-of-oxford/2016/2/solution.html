A mathematician in Galileo Galilei's world was a practitioner situated within a particular division of knowledge and work. The word could embrace demonstrative geometry and arithmetic, but also astronomy, optics, mechanics, surveying, calendar calculation and the design or use of scientific instruments. Such mixed mathematics treated features of natural or practical objects through mathematical relations. It was neither identical to a present-day university department nor confined to calculation without claims about nature.
The distinction from a natural philosopher was important. Within a traditional university hierarchy, natural philosophy investigated the causes and principles of physical change. Mathematics could establish a relation between abstract quantities with great certainty, while opponents doubted whether it captured the constitution of a real body. Showing what follows for an ideal inclined plane, for example, leaves a further question about whether a particular apparatus realizes the assumptions closely enough. That question is not an objection which can be answered merely by producing a longer geometrical proof. The relation of abstraction, measurement and physical explanation was itself contested.[ref]
There were also several routes to employment. A university teacher, an scientific instrument maker and someone serving a court or military patron might all perform mathematical work while possessing different income, status and audiences. Practical expertise in fortification, surveying or optics could bring rewards without establishing that its holder had authority over every question of natural philosophy. Conversely, a learned author could rely on the skills of people whose work was not credited equally in print. The important historical questions are what tasks were recognized, who paid for them, and whose testimony carried weight. A modern boundary between theoretical research and technical service would obscure these connections.
Galileo Galilei's career makes the contested boundary visible. The Sidereus Nuncius by Galileo Galilei communicated new observations through a manufactured telescope, successive drawings and an account intended to make unfamiliar objects credible. The work also addressed a courtly audience: naming the bodies accompanying Jupiter was part of presenting an achievement to a patron, not an incidental ornament added after a purely private discovery. The title and dedication situate mathematical work within relationships of employment and reputation.[ref]
His mathematical treatment of motion likewise pressed beyond the role of calculating celestial appearances. In Two New Sciences by Galileo Galilei, a definition of uniform acceleration leads to a geometrical result in which travelled distance varies as the square of elapsed time. An inclined plane stretches the time of descent, making a comparison more manageable. In modern notation the relationship is s=at2/2 for release from rest. The notation is ours; the historically significant combination is an idealized proof, a proposed physical regularity and a reported experimental test.[ref]
The label mathematician therefore cannot be read as a declaration that Galileo Galilei made no physical claims. Nor does his insistence on mathematical explanation prove that other occupations or disciplinary standards were irrelevant. His 1610 transition into Tuscan service, with the combined style of mathematician and philosopher, is evidence of the status being sought, rather than proof that the existing hierarchy had already disappeared. The case shows an investigator using scientific instruments, patronage and mathematical demonstration to enlarge the jurisdiction of his expertise. It also explains why a seemingly terminological distinction could become consequential in disputes over heliocentrism.[ref]
