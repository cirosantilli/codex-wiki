There is no convincing single birthplace of science unless the definition is narrowed in advance. Systematic astronomy and mathematics preceded classical Greece; Greek natural philosophy developed distinctive explanatory and demonstrative ambitions; early modern Europe transformed the relationship between mathematics, experiment and institutions. These are different historical achievements. Treating any one of them as the creation of everything now called science confuses a particular form of inquiry with inquiry's entire history.
A good test begins with surviving practices rather than a list of supposed founding geniuses. The Babylonian astronomical diary VAT 4956 records positions of the Moon and planets relative to named stars, together with other observations, for 568–567 BCE. Such a record requires trained observers, a conventional vocabulary and a means of accumulating observations over time. Its intellectual value is not annulled because astronomy was entangled with divination and royal administration. However, this particular diary should not be credited with everything achieved by later Babylonian predictive astronomy: an observational record is evidence of systematic recording, not by itself evidence of a particular predictive algorithm.[ref]
Egyptian mathematics provides a different kind of evidence. Problem 50 of the Rhind mathematical papyrus gives a procedure for the area of a circular field: remove one ninth of its diameter and square the remainder. Written in modern algebraic notation, the procedure is
A≈(98d​)2.
(1)
Comparing this with the modern circle formula A=πd2/4 gives the effective approximation π≈256/81. This is our reconstruction of the rule's accuracy, not a claim that its author used our symbol π or formulated an abstract theory of that constant. A worked numerical procedure is mathematical knowledge even when it is not presented as a Greek-style proof.[ref]
The case for Greece therefore needs a more specific claim. In Physics by Aristotle, nature is investigated through principles of motion and change, and explanation requires identifying different kinds of cause. His distinctions between material, formal, moving and final causes constitute an explicit programme of explanation, not merely a collection of useful recipes. Greek mathematics, exemplified by Euclid's Elements, made the organization of results into chains of proof unusually prominent. These traditions help explain why later European scholars gave Greek texts such authority. They do not establish that earlier or non-Greek practitioners lacked reasoning, nor that all Greek explanations were correct.[ref][ref]
The European sixteenth and seventeenth centuries nevertheless deserve attention as more than the recovery of ancient truths. Galileo Galilei's Two New Sciences by Galileo Galilei joins precise definitions of motion to geometrical demonstrations and reported tests. An inclined plane lengthens a descent sufficiently to compare its distance and time; a mathematical dependence becomes something an apparatus can discriminate. Francis Bacon's Novum Organum by Francis Bacon instead urges a disciplined reconstruction of inquiry through ordered experience and the correction of intellectual prejudices. Their programmes differ, but both show participants deliberately contesting how knowledge should be made. Neither text authorizes the claim that observation or reasoning had only just been invented.[ref][ref]
Institutional criteria also change the answer. If science means a modern profession with specialized laboratories, credentials and journals, even sixteenth-century Europe is too early for much of it. If it means any successful craft technique, the category becomes so broad that the first surviving written text cannot identify its beginning. If it means public, revisable claims about nature supported by disciplined observation and reasoning, several ancient traditions qualify, with different strengths and social settings. Each definition selects different evidence; none should be smuggled into a geographical verdict.
The strongest conclusion is consequently plural but discriminating. Greece was a major centre of explanatory natural philosophy and demonstrative mathematics; early modern Europe was a major site of their reorganization; neither was the unique beginning of systematic knowledge of nature. This conclusion recognizes real differences in standards of explanation without using proximity to present-day science as the sole measure of past achievement. It also leaves open the historical work of tracing transmission and transformation rather than imagining isolated civilizations independently possessing, or wholly lacking, a timeless scientific essence.
