Isaac Newton made the greater contribution if the criterion is the scope and explanatory integration of mathematical natural philosophy; Galileo Galilei has the stronger claim if the criterion is the formative transformation of how terrestrial motion could be investigated. The first criterion gives a defensible overall verdict for Isaac Newton, but a serious comparison must show what each changed rather than award credit according to who worked later.
Galileo Galilei's contribution was not simply to attach numbers to familiar events. In the Third Day of Two New Sciences by Galileo Galilei, he defines uniformly accelerated motion by equal additions of speed in equal intervals of time, then derives consequences. Starting from rest, the modern notation is v=at; the mean speed over the interval is at/2, so
s=21​at2.
(2)
Hence distances in successive equal intervals have the ratios 1:3:5:7:…. The novelty lies in making a precisely specified kind of motion the subject of mathematical demonstration, with observed falls assessed against its consequences. This is not the same as deriving the underlying physical cause of gravity.[ref]
The reported apparatus shows how such an idealization could be investigated. A smooth bronze ball descended in a straight grooved wooden channel, raised at one end. Water discharged through a small opening into a vessel during the descent; its collected mass served as a measure of elapsed time. Repeating runs over different portions of the channel compared the predicted squared dependence of time on distance. The inclined plane slowed the event, while repetition and weighing made a temporal comparison possible without a modern stopwatch. The published report is evidence of Galileo Galilei's experimental presentation, not a surviving raw-data archive from which every reported precision can now be reconstructed.[ref]
A necessary physical qualification is often missed. A ball in rolling without slipping is not a point particle sliding without friction. For a solid sphere on an ideal inclined plane, modern rigid body dynamics gives a=5gsinα/7, rather than gsinα. Both are constant accelerations, so both give s∝t2. The trial can therefore test the temporal form without directly establishing the free-fall coefficient. This distinction makes the experiment more intelligible: the evidence supports a specified relation under approximations, rather than every detail of a modern idealization.
The Fourth Day extends this approach to projectile motion. Combine uniform horizontal velocity u with constant downward acceleration g, taking downward displacement positive. Then x=ut and y=gt2/2. Eliminating time gives
y=2u2g​x2,​
(3)
a parabola. Actual projectiles experience air resistance, but the ideal curve organizes the effects that need separate treatment. Galileo Galilei thus gave a powerful example of how mathematical abstraction and physical observation could cooperate.[ref]
Isaac Newton went further by connecting kinds of motion through a general account of force. Newton's second law links the change of momentum to the impressed force; for constant mass, its modern differential expression is F=ma. Newton's third law makes interaction reciprocal, enabling a consistent analysis of interacting bodies. The Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica by Isaac Newton develops consequences of central forces and then asks which laws fit astronomical phenomena. Mathematical deduction now connects measurable orbits to an underlying force law, rather than just describing each trajectory separately.[ref]
A simple modern reconstruction shows the increase in explanatory reach. For a circular orbit of radius r and orbital period T about a much more massive central body, centripetal acceleration is 4π2r/T2. Kepler's third law, T2∝r3, therefore implies an acceleration proportional to r−2. This circular argument is illustrative; Isaac Newton's treatment of elliptical orbits is more general. With Newton's law of universal gravitation,

(4)
the two-body orbital period is tied to the semimajor axis a and both masses. The same theory connects the Moon's orbital acceleration with terrestrial gravity, and supports further investigations of tides, comets and departures from a simple two-body orbit.[ref]
This achievement depended on more than two individuals. Johannes Kepler's relations supplied crucial astronomical constraints, and both authors inherited geometry, observational records and technical practices. Nor should the Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica by Isaac Newton be described simply as the first application of symbolic calculus: its published arguments are largely geometrical. What distinguishes Isaac Newton is the demonstrative network uniting terrestrial and celestial problems, together with explicit idealizations whose failures generated further calculable questions.
On breadth, quantitative unification and the inference from motion to force, Isaac Newton's contribution was greater. Galileo Galilei's work supplied an exemplary way to mathematize terrestrial motion and test its idealized relations. Giving the former the overall verdict should therefore acknowledge the latter's distinct foundational achievement, not portray the Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica by Isaac Newton as a creation without predecessors.
