Gram-Schmidt process (uj​=vj​−∑i<j​⟨vj​,qi​⟩qi​,qj​=uj​/∥uj​∥)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra Inner product
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 8
The Gram-Schmidt process turns a finite ordered family of linearly independent vectors v1​,…,vk​ in an inner product space over the real numbers into an orthonormal basis q1​,…,qk​ of their linear span. It keeps every successive linear span, so the first j new vectors describe exactly the same directions as the first j inputs. Its idea is geometric: remove the orthogonal projection onto directions already found, then divide the remaining vector by its norm. This makes a complicated basis easier to use without changing the vector subspace it describes.[ref]
Start with u1​=v1​ and q1​=v1​/∥v1​∥. At step j, use the previously obtained orthonormal set to define
uj​=vj​−∑i=1j−1​⟨vj​,qi​⟩qi​,qj​=∥uj​∥uj​​,∥uj​∥=⟨uj​,uj​⟩​.
(25)
The sum is the orthogonal projection onto a finite-dimensional subspace Wj−1​=span(q1​,…,qj−1​), and uj​ is the orthogonal projection residual. Linear independence ensures uj​=0: otherwise vj​ would already be a linear combination of earlier inputs. If the input family is linearly dependent, use only the retained orthonormal vectors in the sum and omit an input whenever its remainder is the zero vector; do not divide by zero. The retained outputs still form an orthonormal basis of the original linear span, but their number can be smaller than k.
For a short verification, orthonormality makes every earlier inner product vanish:
⟨uj​,qℓ​⟩=⟨vj​,qℓ​⟩−∑i<j​⟨vj​,qi​⟩⟨qi​,qℓ​⟩=0(ℓ<j).
(26)
Also uj​ is vj​ minus a linear combination of earlier vectors, while vj​ is uj​ plus that same linear combination. These two inclusions, followed by division by a nonzero norm, give
span(q1​,…,qj​)=span(v1​,…,vj​).
(27)
The argument also works in an inner product space over the complex numbers with the existing inner product convention, linear in the first argument: the coefficients remain ⟨vj​,qi​⟩. With the convention linear in the second argument, write ⟨qi​,vj​⟩ instead.
For a first picture, take v1​=(1,1)T and v2​=(1,0)T in R2 with the ordinary dot product. Then
q1​=2​(1,1)T​,⟨v2​,q1​⟩q1​=21​(1,1)T,u2​=21​(1,−1)T,q2​=2​(1,−1)T​.
(28)
Subtracting the diagonal component leaves an orthogonal direction; dividing by its norm 1/2​ makes its length one. If the second input were (2,2)T instead, its orthogonal projection would be the entire input and u2​=0, revealing linear dependence.
For a full three-dimensional calculation, use the ordinary dot product and the input vectors
v1​=(1,1,0)T,v2​=(1,0,1)T,v3​=(0,1,1)T.
(29)
The first two steps give
q1​=2​(1,1,0)T​,⟨v2​,q1​⟩=2​1​,u2​=(1/2,−1/2,1)T,
(30)
∥u2​∥2=23​,q2​=6​(1,−1,2)T​.
(31)
At the third step, the inner products are ⟨v3​,q1​⟩=1/2​ and ⟨v3​,q2​⟩=1/6​. Consequently
u3​=(0,1,1)T−21​(1,1,0)T−61​(1,−1,2)T=(−32​,32​,32​)T,
(32)
∥u3​∥2=34​,q3​=3​(−1,1,1)T​.
(33)
The three numerator vectors have pairwise dot products 0 and squared Euclidean norms 2,6,3. Thus the displayed outputs are indeed an orthonormal basis, and the three nonzero remainders also confirm linear independence of the inputs. Keeping the removed coefficients produces the QR decomposition below.
An immediate reward is a reusable orthogonal projection: once an orthonormal basis of a vector subspace W is known, any vector b has
PW​b=∑i​⟨b,qi​⟩qi​.
(34)
For the linear span of just the first two inputs in the three-dimensional example, b=(0,0,1)T gives PW​b=(1/3,−1/3,2/3)T. Its orthogonal projection residual is (−1/3,1/3,1/3)T=q3​/3​, visibly in the missing orthogonal direction. This is the geometry behind the linear least-squares problem.
The formulas above describe exact arithmetic. With floating-point arithmetic, subtracting an orthogonal projection nearly equal to the input can leave a small remainder; dividing by its small norm can magnify existing roundoff error. The Classical Gram-Schmidt and Modified Gram-Schmidt arrangements agree in exact arithmetic but can give different rounded results. Exact linear dependence and a small computed remainder therefore require different judgments in a numerical implementation.[ref]
