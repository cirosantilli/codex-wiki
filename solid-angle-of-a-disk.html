Solid angle of a disk (Ω=2π(1−d/d2+a2​))

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Geometry and topology Euclidean geometry Sphere Solid angle
OurBigBook.com Words: 388
Consider a flat circular disk of radius a>0, with its center a perpendicular distance d>0 from the viewpoint O. The line from O to its center is normal to the disk. Its rim defines a right circular cone with tanθ=a/d and cosθ=d/d2+a2​. The solid angle of a circular cone immediately gives
Ω=2π(1−d2+a2​d​).​
(20)
For a=d, this is the same 45∘ half-opening example: about 1.8403 steradians. Both the radius and distance enter; doubling both leaves the solid angle unchanged.
A useful local version applies to a small surface patch of area dA, at distance r from O. If ψ is the angle between its unit normal and the line to O, its unsigned geometric contribution is
dΩ=r2∣cosψ∣dA​.
(21)
The numerator is the projected area. For a visible patch, perpendicular projection followed by radial projection onto a small piece of a sphere gives this formula. Integrating counts the directions once provided no direction encounters multiple counted patches. For an opaque detector, use its unoccluded face. At grazing view, the projected area tends to zero.
For this on-axis disk, a ring of radius ρ has r=d2+ρ2​ and ∣cosψ∣=d/r. Its polar coordinates gives dA=ρdρdϕ, so the same answer follows directly from the surface integral:
Ω=2π∫0a​(d2+ρ2)3/2dρ​dρ=2π(1−d2+a2​d​).
(22)
The changing distance and obliquity are both already accounted for; an extra detector-wide cosine factor would count the projection twice.
For d≫a, the Taylor series of (1+a2/d2)−1/2 gives
Ω=d2πa2​+O(d4a4​).
(23)
Thus the accepted fraction of an ideal point emitter's isotropic output is approximately A/(4πd2), where A=πa2. This is the geometry behind the inverse-square law for a small detector. At fixed a, however, taking d→0+ gives Ω→2π, so the accepted fraction approaches 1/2 rather than diverging.
For a chosen numerical example, let a=1cm and d=5cm. The exact solid angle is approximately 0.122015 steradians, giving an acceptance fraction 0.00970966. An ideal point emitter with isotropy and total radiant flux 1mW would therefore send about 9.71μW onto the disk. The distant-source approximation gives 10.0μW, about 3.0% high. This calculation assumes unobstructed propagation and counts incident radiant flux; photon absorption, detector response, and any optical attenuator require separate factors.
