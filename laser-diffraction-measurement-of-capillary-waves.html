Laser-diffraction measurement of capillary waves

 Home Physics Branches of physics Fluid mechanics Surface tension Capillary wave
OurBigBook.com Words: 469
Zhu, Miao, Xu and Cao's 2007 experiment drove a triangular metal frame electromagnetically with a sinusoidal generator. In a roughly 3cm-deep, temperature-regulated pool, it produced nearly plane capillary waves. A 632.8nm laser illuminated the surface obliquely; the moving ripples acted as a reflection diffraction grating. One split beam monitored laser stability, while a CCD recorded diffraction orders on a screen 6.76m away. An air-supported optical table reduced ambient vibration. [ref]
The frequency sweep was 100–290Hz in 10Hz steps, using distilled water and kerosene at 20∘C. For each setting they averaged at least three measurements of first-order spot separation. Known optical geometry and the grating equation yielded k=2π/λ; generator readings yielded ω=2πf. Agreement with the predicted log–log slope 3/2 tests the capillary law, and its intercept gives surface tension if mass density is known. The authors identify surface contamination as a major error source; noncontact optical detection reduces additional disturbance. [ref]
The optical measurement can be understood from the grating equation. Let λL​ be the laser wavelength, α the incident grazing angle above the mean surface, and βm​ the outgoing grazing angle of diffraction order m, with the sign of m chosen so that
λ(cosα−cosβm​)=mλL​.
(118)
Thus calibrated spot positions determine outgoing angles, which determine the ripple wavelength λ, independently of the mechanical dispersion relation. This optical geometry treats the surface as a reflection diffraction grating at the laser wavelength. [ref]
The inferred surface tension in the full deep-water model is
γ=ρk3ω2−gk​.
(119)
This formula makes useful experimental controls explicit. Measure mass density at the sample's temperature; check depth with kh; keep the driven wave amplitude small enough that ka≪1; and distinguish f from angular frequency ω. For the stipulated parameters, the 100–290Hz window corresponds to calculated wavelengths about 3.62–1.76mm and kh≃52–107 at h=30mm. Depth corrections are negligible here, but the gravity contribution is about 4.5%–1.1% of the capillary contribution. These are ideal predictions, not digitized results from the experiment.
Omitting gravity would infer γcap​=ρω2/k3=γ+ρg/k2, biasing surface tension upward. In the capillary limit, the differential estimate dγ/γ≃dρ/ρ+2dω/ω−3dk/k also shows why careful spot-spacing calibration matters. A clean surface and weak viscosity are modeling assumptions: appreciable damping, surfactant stresses or large wave amplitudes call for additional physics. The experiment tests the short-wave regime; its drive window does not establish the crossover or the long-wave surface gravity wave limit.
