Minkowski inequality (∥f+g∥p​≤∥f∥p​+∥g∥p​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Lp norm
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 4
For measurable functions f,g with values in the real numbers or complex numbers on the same measure space (X,A,μ), and 1≤p≤∞, membership in the same Lp space gives
f,g∈Lp(μ)⟹f+g∈Lp(μ),∥f+g∥p​≤∥f∥p​+∥g∥p​.
(63)
Thus adding two functions cannot make their combined Lp norm exceed the sum of their separate Lp norms. The measure need not be finite or a probability measure. The hypotheses require finite Lp norms; p=∞ uses the essential supremum of the absolute value, not an ordinary pointwise supremum. For complex numbers, the absolute value is the complex modulus. As usual, Lp spaces identify functions equal almost everywhere. [ref]
The Minkowski inequality makes the Lp norm a workable notion of size: it supplies the triangle inequality needed to turn dp​(f,g)=∥f−g∥p​ into a metric on Lp space. Applied to (f−h)+(h−g), it says that travelling through an intermediate function h cannot shorten the direct distance measured by this metric. Applied to two error functions, it gives a bound on the total error even when the errors reinforce one another; Minkowski inequality for error aggregation gives explicit examples.
The geometry is already visible in the Euclidean plane. Under counting measure on two coordinates, the closed unit balls for the L1 norm, Euclidean norm, and L-infinity norm are respectively ∣x∣+∣y∣≤1, x2+y2≤1, and max(∣x∣,∣y∣)≤1. Every one is a convex set: if u,v have Lp norm at most one, the Minkowski inequality and absolute homogeneity of a norm give ∥tu+(1−t)v∥p​≤t+(1−t)=1 for 0≤t≤1. The Euclidean norm has no flat boundary segments, whereas the other two norms do. This is the distinction captured by a strictly convex normed space and developed in equality in Minkowski inequality.
A short Hölder's inequality proof outline handles 1<p<∞. First ∣f+g∣p≤2p−1(∣f∣p+∣g∣p), so f+g has finite Lp norm before any division. With conjugate exponents p and q=p/(p−1), set h=∣f+g∣p−1; then ∥h∥q​=∥f+g∥pp−1​. The scalar triangle inequality followed by Hölder's inequality gives
∥f+g∥pp​≤∫(∣f∣+∣g∣)∣f+g∣p−1dμ≤(∥f∥p​+∥g∥p​)∥f+g∥pp−1​.
(64)
Divide if ∥f+g∥p​>0; otherwise the desired bound is immediate. For p=1, integrate ∣f+g∣≤∣f∣+∣g∣. For p=∞, the almost everywhere bounds ∣f∣≤∥f∥∞​ and ∣g∣≤∥g∥∞​ give the result by the essential supremum. The same scalar triangle inequality works for real numbers and complex numbers.
