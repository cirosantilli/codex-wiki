First prove the unlettered common-eigenvector assertion, in the stronger form that some nonzero vector is killed by every operator in W. This is the linear form of Engel's theorem.
Induct on dimW, with the zero algebra immediate. If ar=0, the operator ada on EndV is nilpotent: left and right multiplication commute, and every term in (La​−Ra​)2r−1 contains a power of a at least r. This argument works in every characteristic.
For a proper Lie subalgebra H<W, its action on W/H by brackets is therefore by nilpotent operators. The image of this action has dimension at most dimH<dimW, so induction gives a nonzero class w+H killed by H. Thus the normalizer NW​(H)={w:[w,H]⊆H} strictly contains H. Choose H maximal proper. Its normalizer must be W, so H is an ideal. Moreover W/H has dimension one, since otherwise any one-dimensional subalgebra of the quotient would lift to an intermediate proper subalgebra.
Induction applied to H acting on V makes V0​={v:Hv=0} nonzero. This space is W-invariant: for h∈H,x∈W,v∈V0​, h(xv)=x(hv)+[h,x]v=0. Choose x complementing H in W. Its restriction to the nonzero space V0​ is nilpotent and has a nonzero kernel. A vector in this kernel is killed by x and H, hence by all of W. This proves the preliminary assertion, with every common eigenvalue equal to zero.
Now apply it to the adjoint action of L on itself. If L=0, its common zero space is the center Z(L), which is therefore nonzero. Every induced adjoint map on L/Z(L) remains nilpotent. Induction on dimension makes that quotient nilpotent. A central extension of a nilpotent Lie algebra is nilpotent: if its lower central series vanishes after projection, the next term lies in the center and the following term is zero. Hence L is nilpotent. The zero algebra is immediate.

Every subalgebra of a nilpotent Lie algebra is nilpotent, since its lower central series is contained term by term in the ambient lower central series.
Conversely, if L were not nilpotent, part (a) would give x for which adx is not nilpotent. Over an algebraically closed field, such an endomorphism has a nonzero eigenvalue λ and eigenvector y. The vectors x,y are independent because [x,x]=0 while [x,y]=λy=0. Their span is a two-dimensional subalgebra, and its lower central series is Ky at every stage after the first. It is therefore not nilpotent, a contradiction. Thus
L nilpotent⟺every two-dimensional subalgebra is nilpotent.​

Polarizing [x,[x,z]]=0 gives
[x,[y,z]]+[y,[x,z]]=0.
(2)
Let T=[x,[y,z]]. Antisymmetry and this identity give [y,[z,x]]=T and [z,[x,y]]=T. The Jacobi identity therefore gives 3T=0. Since the characteristic is not three, T=0 for every triple. Consequently
[L,[L,L]]=0,L has nilpotency class at most two.​
(3)
No division by two was used, so the proof also applies in characteristic two with the usual alternating definition of a Lie bracket.

The Killing form is κL​(x,y)=Tr(adxady). Write X=adx, Y=ady, Z=adz. Since ad[x,y]=[X,Y], cyclicity of trace gives
κL​([x,y],z)=Tr((XY−YX)Z)=Tr(X(YZ−ZY))=κL​(x,[y,z]).
(4)
If x∈I⊥, a∈L and y∈I, invariance gives κL​([a,x],y)=κL​(x,[y,a])=0, because [y,a]∈I. Thus I⊥ is an ideal. For x,y∈I, the restrictions of their adjoint maps to I are the adjoint maps of I, and their induced maps on L/I vanish. Block traces therefore give κL​∣I×I​=κI​.
If A is an Abelian ideal, adaadx maps L into A and vanishes on A for a∈A. In a basis starting with A it has zero diagonal blocks, hence trace zero. Therefore every Abelian ideal lies in L⊥.
Cartan criterion for semisimplicity says that a finite-dimensional complex Lie algebra is semisimple exactly when its Killing form is nondegenerate. To see why nondegeneracy implies semisimplicity, any nonzero solvable ideal has a last nonzero derived term which is an Abelian ideal of L. The previous paragraph would put it in the radical of the Killing form, a contradiction.
If L is a semisimple ideal of M, the restriction κM​∣L​=κL​ is nondegenerate. Put J=L⊥κM​​. This is an ideal of M by the argument above, and nondegeneracy on L gives L∩J=0 and M=L+J. One way to check the latter is to restrict the pairing map M→L∗: its restriction to L is an isomorphism, so every vector of M can be adjusted by an element of L to lie in its kernel J. Since both are ideals, [L,J]⊆L∩J=0. Hence
M=L⊕J as a direct sum of ideals.​
(5)
Apply this to the supplied extension by the derivation. If δ=0, it is ad0. Otherwise D and J are one-dimensional, and projection J→D is an isomorphism. Choose its vector with D-component δ, writing it as j=δ−x for x∈L. Because [J,L]=0, for every l∈L one has 0=[j,l]=δ(l)−[x,l]. Thus
δ=adL​x.​
(6)
This also handles the sign in the extension bracket explicitly.

A base of a root system is a vector-space basis Δ of the ambient Euclidean space such that every root is an integer combination of its elements with all coefficients nonnegative or all nonpositive. The corresponding nonzero nonnegative combinations are the positive roots.
If distinct α,β∈Δ had positive inner product, the allowed root-difference fact would make α−β a root. Its simple-root coefficients have opposite signs, contradicting the definition. Hence distinct simple roots have nonpositive inner product.
For a positive root γ=∑nα​α which is not simple, the identity (γ,γ)=∑nα​(γ,α)>0 gives a simple root α with nα​>0 and (γ,α)>0. The allowed fact makes γ−α a root. Its coefficients remain nonnegative, and it is nonzero because γ is not simple. It has smaller height ∑nα​. Induction expresses it as a sum of simple roots with each partial sum a root; appending α gives the required expression for γ.
Finally, reflection in a simple root changes only that root's coefficient:
σα​(γ)=γ−(α,α)2(γ,α)​α.
(7)
For positive γ=α, at least one other simple coefficient is positive: in a reduced root system the only positive root on the line of α is α itself. That other coefficient is unchanged. The reflected vector is a root, so its coefficients cannot all be nonpositive and must all be nonnegative. Thus σα​ preserves all positive roots except α, which it sends to −α.

Use the column-coroot convention Cij​=2(αi​,αj​)/(αj​,αj​), consistent with the printed matrix and the stated shorter first root. It gives
∥β∥22(α,β)​=−1,∥α∥22(β,α)​=−3,∥β∥2=3∥α∥2.
(8)
The root string through β in the α direction contains β,β+α,β+2α,β+3α. Reflecting 3α+β in β gives 3α+2β. Together with α and their negatives these give
Φ=±{α, β, α+β, 2α+β, 3α+β, 3α+2β}.​
(9)
There are no further roots. Indeed the simple reflections act on coefficient pairs by (a,b)↦(−a+3b,b) and (a,b)↦(a,a−b); the displayed twelve-element set is closed under both. Every positive nonsimple root can be reflected to a positive root of smaller height: choose a simple root with positive inner product as in (a), and its positive integral Cartan pairing decreases height. Repetition reaches a simple root, so every root lies in an orbit already contained in the list. This is the G2 root system.

The Young diagram is [λ]={(i,j):1≤i≤k, 1≤j≤λi​}. The hook length of a box is
h(i,j)=λi​−j+λj′​−i+1,
(11)
counting the box itself, the boxes to its right in the same row, and the boxes below it in the same column. The hook-length formula is
fλ=∏(i,j)∈[λ]​h(i,j)n!​.​
(12)
The first implication in the equivalence is immediate: if every hook length is odd, no hook can have length two.

Assume no hook has length two. The last row has length one, since otherwise its penultimate box has hook length two. If two adjacent row lengths are equal, take the last two rows in the maximal block of that length; the last box in the upper of those two rows has hook length two. Thus all row lengths are distinct. If a gap λi​−λi+1​ were at least two, the penultimate box in row i would again have hook length two, since there would be no boxes below it. Therefore every successive gap equals one and the final part equals one, proving (2) implies (3).

The final condition makes λ=(k,k−1,…,1). Its column lengths are the same staircase, so
h(i,j)=2k−2i−2j+3,
(13)
which is odd. This proves (3) implies (1) and completes the equivalence. Such a partition exists exactly at the triangular numbers
n=k(k+1)/2(k≥1),λ=(k,k−1,…,1).​
(14)
The hook formula gives v2​(fλ)=v2​(n!)=a, since its denominator is odd. Thus 2a divides fλ. Conversely 2a∣fγ implies v2​(∏hγ​)=a−v2​(fγ)≤0. The product is an integer, so its valuation is zero and all its hooks are odd. By the equivalence γ is a staircase; its size uniquely determines its length, so γ=λ.
For the character assertion, every removable rim hook has length equal to some hook length of the diagram. Here is a direct justification. If a border strip λ/μ occupies rows i through j, connectedness and absence of a two-by-two block make adjacent strips of boxes overlap in exactly one column. Hence μl​=λl+1​−1 for i≤l<j, so its size is λi​−μj​+j−i. The column c=μj​+1 has height j, giving exactly hλ​(i,c)=λi​−μj​+j−i. Therefore every removable rim hook of the staircase has odd length.
A fixed-point-free permutation of power-of-two order has only even cycles. Apply the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule starting with any cycle. It would require removal of a rim hook of that even length, but none exists. The sum is empty, so
χλ(g)=0.​
(15)
Finally suppose γ has distinct parts and is not λ. It cannot be another staircase of the same size. Thus some gap between successive rows is at least two, or the last row has length at least two; in either case a horizontal rim domino is removable. No vertical rim domino is removable, since that would require two equal adjacent row lengths. Murnaghan-Nakayama on a transposition consequently gives a nonempty sum
χγ(τ)=∑removable horizontal dominoes​fγ∖domino>0,
(16)
all signs being positive and all tableau counts positive. Thus the character is nonzero at every transposition, proving the requested existence.

For a tableau t, let Ct​ be its column stabilizer and et​=∑σ∈Ct​​sgn(σ){σt} its polytabloid. The Specht module is the span of all such polytabloids inside the tabloid permutation module.
A tabloid with second row of size k is determined by that subset, so dimM(n−k,k)=(kn​). The two-row hook formula gives
dimS(n−k,k)=(n−k+1)!k!n!(n−2k+1)​=(kn​)−(k−1n​).
(17)
At k=3 this proves
dimM(n−3,3)=dimM(n−2,2)+dimS(n−3,3).​
(18)
By Maschke's theorem, characteristic-zero permutation modules are completely reducible, so their Specht submodules are summands. The nonzero sum of all three-subset tabloids is fixed by every permutation; its span is a copy of the trivial module S(n).

The four constructed submodules have the distinct shapes (n−3,3), (n−2,2), (n−1,1) and (n), so they are pairwise nonisomorphic irreducible characteristic-zero modules. Complete reducibility therefore makes their sum direct. The given decomposition of M(n−2,2) and the dimension identity in (a) show their dimensions sum to dimM(n−3,3). Their direct sum is consequently the whole module:

Use the standard invariant tabloid bilinear form, with the tabloid basis orthonormal. Each permutation acts by an orthogonal matrix, so ⟨gx,y⟩=⟨x,g−1y⟩. Inversion permutes Ct​ and preserves its signs. Therefore
⟨κt​x,y⟩=g∈Ct​∑​sgn(g)⟨x,g−1y⟩=⟨x,κt​y⟩.​
(22)
The James submodule theorem states that for any submodule U≤Mλ, either Sλ⊆U or U⊆(Sλ)⊥. Use the allowed fact κt​Mλ=Fet​. The coefficient of {t} in et​ is one, so the displayed adjoint identity gives κt​x=⟨x,et​⟩et​. If U is not orthogonal to Sλ, some x∈U and some tableau t have ⟨x,et​⟩=0. Hence et​∈U, and its permutation translates span Sλ. This proves the theorem.
Let R=Sλ∩(Sλ)⊥. Every proper submodule of Sλ lies in R by the theorem. If the restricted form is nonzero, R is proper, hence is the unique maximal submodule and
Dλ=Sλ/R is the unique simple quotient.​
(23)
In the stronger nondegenerate case specified in the question, R=0, so Sλ itself is simple. The nonzero-form version also covers the possibly degenerate case needed in the final calculation.
For shape (n−1,1), identify M with Fn with basis v1​,…,vn​. Its Specht module is the augmentation subspace S={∑ai​vi​:∑ai​=0}, spanned freely by vi​−vn​, 1≤i<n. Its Gram matrix is G=In−1​+Jn−1​, with diagonal entries two and off-diagonal entries one. Equivalently, S⊥ in M is the span of v1​+⋯+vn​. This vector belongs to S exactly when p∣n. Thus the restricted radical has dimension one if p∣n and zero otherwise, giving
dimD(n−1,1)={n−2,n−1,​p∣n,p∤n.​​
(24)
For n≥3 this quotient is nonzero and simple by the theorem. In the small exceptional case n=2,p=2, the canonical radical quotient is zero, as the same dimension formula states; it is then not a nonzero simple module. This qualification distinguishes the numerical formula from the nonzero-form hypothesis.
