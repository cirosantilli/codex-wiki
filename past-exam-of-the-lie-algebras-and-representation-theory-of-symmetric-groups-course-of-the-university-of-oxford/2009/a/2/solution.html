The Killing form is κL​(x,y)=Tr(adxady). Write X=adx, Y=ady, Z=adz. Since ad[x,y]=[X,Y], cyclicity of trace gives
κL​([x,y],z)=Tr((XY−YX)Z)=Tr(X(YZ−ZY))=κL​(x,[y,z]).
(4)
If x∈I⊥, a∈L and y∈I, invariance gives κL​([a,x],y)=κL​(x,[y,a])=0, because [y,a]∈I. Thus I⊥ is an ideal. For x,y∈I, the restrictions of their adjoint maps to I are the adjoint maps of I, and their induced maps on L/I vanish. Block traces therefore give κL​∣I×I​=κI​.
If A is an Abelian ideal, adaadx maps L into A and vanishes on A for a∈A. In a basis starting with A it has zero diagonal blocks, hence trace zero. Therefore every Abelian ideal lies in L⊥.
Cartan criterion for semisimplicity says that a finite-dimensional complex Lie algebra is semisimple exactly when its Killing form is nondegenerate. To see why nondegeneracy implies semisimplicity, any nonzero solvable ideal has a last nonzero derived term which is an Abelian ideal of L. The previous paragraph would put it in the radical of the Killing form, a contradiction.
If L is a semisimple ideal of M, the restriction κM​∣L​=κL​ is nondegenerate. Put J=L⊥κM​​. This is an ideal of M by the argument above, and nondegeneracy on L gives L∩J=0 and M=L+J. One way to check the latter is to restrict the pairing map M→L∗: its restriction to L is an isomorphism, so every vector of M can be adjusted by an element of L to lie in its kernel J. Since both are ideals, [L,J]⊆L∩J=0. Hence
M=L⊕J as a direct sum of ideals.​
(5)
Apply this to the supplied extension by the derivation. If δ=0, it is ad0. Otherwise D and J are one-dimensional, and projection J→D is an isomorphism. Choose its vector with D-component δ, writing it as j=δ−x for x∈L. Because [J,L]=0, for every l∈L one has 0=[j,l]=δ(l)−[x,l]. Thus
δ=adL​x.​
(6)
This also handles the sign in the extension bracket explicitly.
