We prove Lie's theorem in the required form by induction on dimL. If L=0, any nonzero vector works. Otherwise solvability gives [L,L]=L, so choose a codimension-one ideal K⊇[L,L] and x∈L∖K. By induction there are v=0 and a linear functional λ:K→C with av=λ(a)v for all a∈K.
Let W=span{v,xv,x2v,…}. Choose the cyclic basis v,xv,…,xr−1v up to its first linear dependence. The commutator identity axj=xaxj−1+[a,x]xj−1, with [a,x]∈K, proves inductively that
axjv≡λ(a)xjv(modspan{v,…,xj−1v}).
(1)
Hence W is invariant under K and x, and every a∈K has trace rλ(a) on W. In particular trW​([x,a])=0 implies rλ([x,a])=0. Characteristic zero gives λ([x,a])=0.
The simultaneous weight space Vλ​={w:aw=λ(a)w ∀a∈K} is nonzero and stable under x, because
a(xw)=x(aw)+[a,x]w=λ(a)xw.
(2)
Over C, the restriction of x to this nonzero finite-dimensional space has an eigenvector. That vector is an eigenvector of both x and every a∈K, hence of every element of L.

Let π:L→L/I be the quotient map. A full flag of ideals in L/I lifts by inverse images to a flag from I to L, with dimensions increasing by one. Prepending 0<I supplies the required full flag in L. Thus supersolubility lifts across a one-dimensional ideal.
Over C, a nonzero soluble L has a one-dimensional ideal: apply part (a) to its adjoint image acting on L. A common eigenvector spans an adjoint-invariant line, which is an ideal. The quotient is soluble and has smaller dimension, so induction and the lifting result give a full flag of ideals. Therefore a soluble complex Lie algebra is supersoluble.
Conversely this implication needs no field restriction. If L has a full flag, its first nonzero ideal I is one-dimensional and abelian, and L/I has a full flag. By induction L/I is soluble, so some term of the derived series of L lies in I; its next derived term is zero. Hence
L is supersoluble⟺L is soluble(L finite-dimensional over C).​
(3)

Let A be the two-dimensional affine Lie algebra with basis u,v and bracket [u,v]=v, and put
L=sl2​(C)⊕A.​
(4)
Both factors have zero centre, so Z(L)=0. The line Cv in the second factor is an ideal, making L PSS. But its quotient onto the nonabelian simple algebra sl2​ is not soluble. A quotient of a soluble algebra would be soluble, so L is not soluble.

If L is abelian and nonzero, L′=0<L. Otherwise choose x∈/Z=Z(L) such that Z+Fx is an ideal. For each a∈L write
[a,x]=λ(a)x+z(a),z(a)∈Z.
(5)
The coefficient λ(a) is uniquely defined and linear. The Jacobi identity gives
[[a,b],x]=λ(b)z(a)−λ(a)z(b)∈Z,
(6)
so λ([a,b])=0. If λ=0, then L′⊆kerλ<L.
If λ=0, the same equation says [[a,b],x]=0 for all a,b. Hence L′⊆CL​(x). Since x is not central, its centralizer is proper. Thus in both cases
L′<L.​
(7)
No algebraic closure or characteristic restriction was used.

Choose a basis (u,v) with U=Fu. The representation matrices are
ρ(h)=(λ(h)0​a(h)μ(h)​).
(8)
The one-dimensional modules U,V/U have characters λ,μ. If they coincide, these modules are isomorphic and the first alternative holds.
Otherwise choose h with δ(h)=0, where δ=λ−μ. Replacing v by v−a(h)u/δ(h) makes the upper-right entry of ρ(h) zero. The representation identity on commutators now gives, for every x∈H,
a([h,x])=δ(h)a(x).
(9)
Iteration yields a((adh)rx)=δ(h)ra(x). Nilpotence of H implies (adh)rx=0 for sufficiently large r, by its lower central series. Since δ(h)=0, it follows that a(x)=0 for all x. The new line W=Fv is therefore a submodule, and
V=U⊕W.​
(10)
The argument works over the stated arbitrary field, including positive characteristic.

The flag makes every composition factor one-dimensional, described by a character λ:H→F. The Jordan–Hölder theorem for modules for finite-length modules says that the multiset of simple composition factors is independent of the chosen composition series.
Here is a construction valid over every F. For h∈H, every generalized eigenspace of ρ(h) is an H-submodule. Indeed ad(h) is nilpotent on H, say of exponent c, and
(ρ(h)−aI)Nρ(x)=∑j=0N​(jN​)ρ((adh)jx)(ρ(h)−aI)N−j.
(11)
If v is killed by (ρ(h)−aI)N0​, take N≥N0​+c. Terms with j≥c vanish by adjoint nilpotence, and all other terms kill v. This proves invariance of the generalized eigenspace.
The flag also makes the characteristic polynomial of every ρ(h) split over F. Whenever two composition characters differ, choose an h on which they differ and split the module into the distinct generalized eigenspaces of ρ(h). Repeat within each summand until all its composition factors have the same character. Every nontrivial split lowers dimensions, so the process terminates. Finally combine summands carrying the same character. Jordan–Hölder ensures that the resulting distinct blocks have distinct factor types, giving
V=λ⨁​Wλ​,all composition factors of Wλ​ have character λ.​
(12)
This recursive method does not assume that a single element separates every character, which could fail over a finite field.

Writing matrices in 2×2 blocks, the defining relation gives
L={(AC​B−AT​):BT=−B, CT=−C}.
(13)
The trace is automatically zero. The relation is linear, and if as=−saT, bs=−sbT, then
[a,b]s=s(aTbT−bTaT)=−s[a,b]T.
(14)
Thus L is closed under the commutator and is a Lie subalgebra of sl4​(F).
Its diagonal subalgebra is
H={D(a,b)=diag(a,b,−a,−b):a,b∈F}.
(15)
Put ε1​(D(a,b))=a, ε2​(D(a,b))=b. Since [D,Eij​]=(Dii​−Djj​)Eij​, the required block decomposition is
L=​H⊕F(E12​−E43​)⊕F(E21​−E34​)⊕F(E14​−E23​)⊕F(E32​−E41​).​​
(16)
The four lines have respective characters ε1​−ε2​, −ε1​+ε2​, ε1​+ε2​, −ε1​−ε2​. They are distinct and nonzero because charF=2. The two-dimensional summand H has two trivial composition factors. These five submodules therefore have exactly the factor-grouping properties of part (b). Their dimensions sum to 6, the dimension obtained from four free entries in A and one each in the skew-symmetric blocks B,C.

A reduced crystallographic root system Φ is a finite spanning subset of a real Euclidean vector space, excluding zero, such that Φ∩Rα={±α}, each reflection sα​(v)=v−2(v,α)α/(α,α) preserves Φ, and all Cartan integers ⟨β,α⟩=2(β,α)/(α,α) are integers. A base is a vector-space basis Δ⊂Φ in which each root has integer coordinates either all nonnegative or all nonpositive. The Weyl group is generated by the root reflections.
For nonparallel roots put p=⟨β,α⟩ and q=⟨α,β⟩. Then pq=4cos2θ is an integer strictly below 4 and at least zero. Therefore
cosθ∈{0,±21​,±2​1​,±23​​}.​
(17)
If (α,β)>0, the positive integers p,q have product 1,2 or 3, so at least one is one. If p=1, reflection gives β−α∈Φ and hence its negative. If q=1, reflection directly gives α−β∈Φ. This proves the positive-inner-product assertion.
To prove that an α-string has no gaps, suppose consecutive occurring integer positions a<b in β+tα had b−a≥2. If (β+aα,α)<0, apply the previous result to β+aα and −α to obtain the missing root at a+1. Thus this inner product must be nonnegative. Similarly the absence of a root at b−1 forces (β+bα,α)≤0. But the latter inner product exceeds the former by (b−a)(α,α)>0, impossible. Thus every intermediate integer position occurs.
Write the complete finite string as β−rα,…,β+sα. Reflection maps position t to −p−t, so it interchanges the two endpoints. Hence
r−s=⟨β,α⟩.​
(18)
Here r,s are the maximal endpoint distances, not arbitrary shorter distances mentioned in the existence assertion.
Use the Cartan-matrix convention Cij​=⟨αi​,αj​⟩. The displayed matrix is realized by
α1​=e2​,α2​=e1​−e2​,
(19)
with the standard orthonormal coordinates. Closure under the two simple reflections gives
Φ={±e1​,±e2​,±(e1​−e2​),±(e1​+e2​)}.​
(20)
The positive roots are α1​,α2​,α1​+α2​,2α1​+α2​. Conversely the standard fact that every root is Weyl-conjugate to a simple root, or successive height reduction by simple reflections, shows these two reflection orbits exhaust the roots. This is the rank-two system B2​, also called C2​ after exchanging the length convention. With a transposed Cartan-matrix convention, interchange which simple-root label is short; the geometric system and Weyl group are unchanged.
The reflection in e2​ flips the second coordinate, while the reflection in e1​−e2​ swaps the two coordinates. These generate all eight signed permutation matrices in two coordinates. Thus
W(Φ)≅D8​,∣W(Φ)∣=8,​
(21)
where D8​ means the symmetry group of a square.

For a Young diagram cell (i,j), its hook length is one plus the number of cells to its right and below. The hook-length formula is
fλ=∏(i,j)∈λ​hij​n!​.
(22)
Applying it to the two shapes gives
f(n−2,1,1)=2(n−1)(n−2)​,f(n−2,2)=2n(n−3)​.​
(23)
The shapes here require n≥4.
A hook tabloid remembers its first-row set and the ordered singleton entries below it. The proposed map keeps that first-row set and merges the remaining entries into one unordered row. Thus it is well-defined on tabloids. Permuting entries before merging gives the same tabloid as merging before permuting, so it is a module homomorphism. Every target tabloid is reached by putting its second-row entries, in any order, into the singleton rows. Therefore it is surjective.
For r=2, the strictly dominating partitions of λ=(n−2,1,1) are exactly (n),(n−1,1),(n−2,2). Indeed dominance forces the first part to be at least n−2, and the possibilities for the remaining sum give precisely those shapes or λ itself.
In characteristic zero, Maschke's theorem makes the permutation module semisimple, and Young's rule gives one copy of Sλ and only strictly dominating additional shapes. The trivial multiplicity is one, since a transitive permutation module has a one-dimensional invariant space. For the standard representation S(n−1,1), Frobenius reciprocity identifies its multiplicity with the dimension of fixed vectors under the row stabilizer Sn−2​. In its realization {(z1​,…,zn​):∑zi​=0}, those fixed vectors have the form (c,…,c,a,b) with (n−2)c+a+b=0, a two-dimensional space. Thus its multiplicity is two.
Finally dimMλ=n(n−1). Subtracting the dimensions of one Sλ, one trivial module and two standard modules leaves n(n−3)/2, exactly one S(n−2,2). Therefore
M(n−2,1,1)≅S(n−2,1,1)⊕S(n)⊕2S(n−1,1)⊕S(n−2,2).​
(24)
The additional multiplicities are 1,2,1 respectively. If the printed weak summation index is read literally alongside its separate Sλ, then mλ,λ​=0; the total multiplicity of Sλ is one.

A tabloid is the row-equivalence class {t} of a tableau filled bijectively with 1,…,n: order inside each row is forgotten, but the rows remain distinguished. The permutation module Mλ has these tabloids as an F-basis, with Σn​ permuting their entries. Let Ct​ be the column stabilizer of t and define
κt​=∑σ∈Ct​​sgn(σ)σ,et​=κt​{t}.
(25)
The Specht module Sλ is the submodule spanned by all polytabloids et​.
If a module homomorphism ϕ:Mλ→Mμ is nonzero on Sλ, it is nonzero on some spanning et​. Equivariance gives 0=ϕ(et​)=κt​ϕ({t}), so κt​Mμ=0. The allowed criterion yields λ≥μ.
In characteristic zero, Maschke's theorem gives an equivariant projection Mλ→Sλ. If Sλ≅Sμ, compose that projection with the isomorphism and the inclusion into Mμ. The preceding argument gives λ≥μ. Reversing the roles gives μ≥λ, so antisymmetry of dominance gives
λ=μ.​
(26)
For a map defined only on a Specht module in arbitrary characteristic, dominance need not hold. Take F=F2​, n=2, λ=(1,1) and μ=(2). The first Specht module is the one-dimensional span of
e={1∣2}−{2∣1}={1∣2}+{2∣1}.
(27)
The transposition fixes e, so this is the trivial module. The one-row permutation module M(2) is also trivial and one-dimensional. Sending e to its single tabloid is a nonzero equivariant map, yet (1,1)≥(2). In this characteristic such a map need not extend to the surrounding permutation module; that is exactly where the earlier proof ceases to apply.

Let πλ be the permutation character of Mλ. Young's rule gives πλ=χλ+∑μ>λ​Kμλ​χμ, with integer Kostka multiplicities. This dominance-triangular system has diagonal entries one, so recursive subtraction expresses every irreducible character as an integer linear combination of permutation characters. The latter count fixed tabloids, proving that all χλ(g) are integers.
For a k-cycle, the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule reduces to
χλ(g)=∑ν⊆λ, ∣ν∣=n−kλ/ν a rim hook of length k​​(−1)ht(λ/ν)fν,
(28)
where height is the number of occupied rows minus one, and fν is the degree of the remaining Specht character at the identity. In particular a full n-cycle has character zero unless λ=(n−r,1r) is a hook, in which case the value is (−1)r.

There is more than one solution: the trivial representation λ=(6) has character 1 everywhere and the sign representation λ=(16) has character ±1 everywhere. Thus
λ=(6) and (16) both work.​
(29)
In fact they are the only ones. The full-cycle formula in the preceding discussion forces any candidate to be a hook (6−r,1r). For r=1, the standard character is the number of fixed points minus one, so it vanishes on cycle type (5,1); its sign twist r=4 does too. For r=2, the hook representation is the exterior square of the standard representation and has character 21​(χ(g)2−χ(g2)). At cycle type (4,1,1) both standard-character values are one, so this character is zero. Its sign twist r=3 also vanishes there. Only r=0,5 remain.

The full-cycle formula says that precisely the nonhook shapes have zero value on a 6-cycle. These are (4,2),(3,3),(3,2,1),(2,2,1,1),(2,2,2).
Use the standard two-row permutation-character rule π(6−r,r)=χ(6)+χ(5,1)+⋯+χ(6−r,r). A transposition has four fixed points, so χ(5,1)=3. It fixes (24​)+1=7 two-subsets, giving χ(4,2)=7−1−3=3. It fixes (34​)+(14​)=8 three-subsets, giving χ(3,3)=8−1−3−3=1.
Conjugating a partition tensors its character with the sign character. Hence the values on a transposition for the conjugate shapes are −3 and −1. The shape (3,2,1) is self-conjugate, so its character equals its own sign twist and is zero on every odd permutation. The complete candidate table is therefore:
ShapeValue on a transposition
(4,2)3
(3,3)1
(3,2,1)0
(2,2,1,1)−3
(2,2,2)−1
Thus the unique answer is
λ=(3,2,1).​
