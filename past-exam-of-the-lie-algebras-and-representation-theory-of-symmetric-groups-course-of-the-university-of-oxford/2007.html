We prove the common-zero-vector form of Engel's theorem by strong induction on the dimension of the acting Lie algebra of operators, allowing its action on a nonzero finite-dimensional space to have a kernel. The zero-dimensional case is immediate.
First, every proper Lie subalgebra H<L has a strictly larger normalizer of a Lie subalgebra. The action of H on L/H by h⋅(x+H)=[h,x]+H consists of nilpotent operators by part (a). Its image has dimension at most dimH<dimL. By induction it has a nonzero common-zero vector x+H, giving [H,x]⊆H with x∈/H. Hence NL​(H)>H.
Choose a maximal proper Lie subalgebra H. The preceding paragraph forces NL​(H)=L, so H is an ideal of a Lie algebra. Moreover L/H has dimension one: otherwise any nonzero one-dimensional subspace of L/H, which is a Lie subalgebra, would lift to an intermediate proper subalgebra. By induction the common kernel
W={v∈V:Hv=0}
(3)
is nonzero. It is L-invariant, since h(xv)=x(hv)+[h,x]v=0 for v∈W, h∈H, x∈L. Take x∈/H. Its restriction to W is nilpotent and so has a nonzero vector v in its kernel. As L=H+Kx, this vector is killed by every element of L:
Lv=0for some v=0.​
(4)
This completes the induction. The underlying space is understood to be nonzero, as required for the asserted vector to exist.

A Cartan subalgebra is a nilpotent Lie subalgebra equal to its normalizer of a Lie subalgebra. Write H=L0,x​ for a choice x∈A attaining the minimum dimension, and let h=dimH. For y∈A, the subalgebra H is invariant under ad(x+ty) because A⊆H. On L/H, the operator induced by adx is invertible: H is its full generalized zero eigenspace, and the finite-dimensional Fitting decomposition separates that from the invertible part.
The determinant of the operator induced by ad(x+ty) on L/H is a polynomial in t, nonzero at zero. Thus it is nonzero for all but finitely many t∈K. For these values, the generalized zero eigenspace L0,x+ty​ lies in H. Minimality of dimH among elements from A forces equality. Hence ad(x+ty)∣H​ is nilpotent for all but finitely many t.
Each nonleading coefficient of its characteristic polynomial is a polynomial in t. Since K is infinite, a polynomial vanishing at all but finitely many elements is identically zero. The coefficient of degree i in the matrix entries is homogeneous of degree i; its highest possible ti coefficient is the corresponding characteristic coefficient of ady∣H​. All these vanish, so that operator has characteristic polynomial Th and is nilpotent by the Cayley-Hamilton theorem. Thus
H⊆L0,y​(y∈A).​
(6)
For existence, choose an L0,x​=H of smallest dimension among all x∈L. Since x∈H, it also minimizes the same set with x restricted to A=H. Applying the proved statement makes every ady∣H​ nilpotent. Engel's theorem makes H nilpotent, and the allowed self-idealizer property makes it self-normalizing. Therefore it is a Cartan subalgebra, illustrating Cartan subalgebra existence from a minimal Engel subalgebra.
For the three-dimensional example, direct use of the brackets gives CL​(z)=Kz for every nonzero z: the equations [u,z]=0 are the vanishing of all two-by-two minors of the two coordinate vectors, so they are proportional. Every nonzero nilpotent Lie algebra has a nonzero center, as seen from its last nonzero lower-central term. A nilpotent subalgebra H with a central nonzero element z must therefore satisfy H⊆CL​(z)=Kz, and has dimension one. The zero subalgebra is not self-normalizing. Thus all Cartan subalgebras have dimension one. For example Ka is nilpotent and its normalizer is Ka, so such subalgebras do occur.

Use the standard split weight-space decomposition in characteristic zero, in particular the usual complex setting: L=⨁φ​Lφ​, with generalized weights for the nilpotent algebra H. The characteristic-zero condition prevents nonzero weight shifts from wrapping around; the unrestricted positive-characteristic version would need additional hypotheses.
For fixed h∈H, let D=adh. On a bracket of vectors from Lφ​,Lψ​, the derivation identity gives
(D−(φ+ψ)(h))[u,v]=[(D−φ(h))u,v]+[u,(D−ψ(h))v].
(7)
Iterating it gives the binomial expansion of these two commuting actions. At power 2n−1, each term kills at least one factor, because generalized weight vectors are killed by the nth power of the corresponding shifted operator. Therefore the bracket lies in the generalized eigenspace for (φ+ψ)(h) for every h. Stabilization of kernels by dimension n gives
[Lφ​,Lψ​]⊆Lφ+ψ​,​
(8)
where Lη​=0 when η is not a weight.
Nilpotence of H gives H⊆L0​. If H is a Cartan subalgebra and L0​/H=0, the action of H on this quotient consists of nilpotent operators. Engel's theorem supplies a nonzero coset v+H killed by H, giving [H,v]⊆H with v∈/H, a contradiction to self-normalization. Conversely, if L0​=H and v normalizes H, then [h,v]∈H for each h. Applying further powers of adh kills this vector because its restriction to H is nilpotent. Hence v∈L0​=H. This proves the equivalence.
The operator ade sends Lφ​ into Lφ+α​. Repetition follows a consecutive α-string; after r steps it must leave the weight set, because r is the largest number of weights in any such string. Thus (ade)r=0​ on every weight space and hence on L.
Finally put E=ade and F=adf. Expand (E+F)n as the sum of all words of length n, without commuting E and F. Acting on Lγ​, each word follows weights γ+iα+jβ with i+j increasing by one at each step. Linear independence of α,β in characteristic zero makes the successive weights distinct. A nonzero length-n path would require n+1 different weight spaces, but dimL=n. Every word therefore vanishes, giving
(ad(e+f))n=0.​
(9)

A reduced crystallographic root system in a real Euclidean space is a finite spanning subset Φ not containing zero, with Φ∩Rα={α,−α}, closed under sα​(v)=v−2(v,α)α/(α,α), and satisfying 2(β,α)/(α,α)∈Z for all roots. A base of a root system is a vector-space basis Δ⊆Φ such that every root has integer coefficients of one sign in that basis.
Choose h not orthogonal to any root and call roots with (h,α)>0 positive. Let Δ be the positive roots that cannot be written as a sum of two positive roots. Repeated decomposition terminates because each summand has strictly smaller positive h-value in a finite set. Thus every positive root is a nonnegative integer combination of Δ.
For distinct α,β∈Δ, (α,β)≤0: otherwise the allowed difference-root lemma would make either α−β or β−α positive, decomposing one of them. In a real linear relation among Δ, move negative coefficients to the other side, obtaining u=v as positive combinations with disjoint supports. Then ∥u∥2=(u,v)≤0, so u=0. But a nonempty positive combination has (h,u)>0, a contradiction. Thus the elements are linearly independent. They span every root and hence E, proving existence of a base of a root system. The difference-root lemma is used only for distinct roots; that qualification excludes the zero difference.
For the displayed set, there are eight coordinate roots, twenty-four roots with two nonzero unit entries, and sixteen half-coordinate roots: forty-eight in total. It spans R4, is reduced, and has squared root lengths one and two. Signed coordinate permutations preserve it. Reflections in coordinate roots are sign changes, and reflections in roots ei​±ej​ are signed coordinate permutations. Every half-coordinate root is obtained by a signed permutation from h=(1/2,1/2,1/2,1/2), so it remains to check its reflection
sh​(v)=v−2∑i​vi​​(1,1,1,1).
(10)
It sends ei​ to ei​−h, a half-coordinate root; it fixes ei​−ej​ and sends ei​+ej​ to the negative sum of the other two coordinate vectors. For a half-coordinate root, the sum of its four signs is 0,±2 or ±4; the formula sends it, respectively, to itself, a coordinate root, or its negative when parallel to h. Thus reflection closure holds. For a long root, the required Cartan integer is an integer dot product; for a coordinate root it is twice a half-integer; for h it is ∑i​βi​∈Z. Symmetry gives the remaining cases. Therefore this is the F4 root system.

A tabloid records the sets of entries in each row of a tableau. Let Ct​ be the subgroup permuting entries independently within each column. The polytabloid is
et​=∑π∈Ct​​sgn(π){πt},
(15)
and the Specht module Sλ is spanned by these vectors. A standard polytabloid is one whose tableau increases along rows and down columns.
Order tabloids by first sorting each row increasingly, then comparing the resulting row-reading words lexicographically. For a standard tableau t, every nonidentity column permutation produces a strictly larger word. In the first row affected by that permutation, each replacement comes from farther down the same column and is larger than the entry it replaces; all earlier rows remain unchanged. Sorting the affected row therefore increases its word. Also no nontrivial column permutation fixes its row sets, since a cell is the unique intersection of its row and column. Hence the least tabloid in et​ is {t} with coefficient one.
Distinct standard tableaux have distinct row tabloids. In a nonzero linear relation among their polytabloids, choose the least leading tabloid with a nonzero coefficient. None of the vectors with larger leading tabloids can contribute to it, so its coefficient cannot cancel. This contradiction proves linear independence of the standard polytabloids.

The hook length of a cell is one plus the number of cells to its right in the same row and below it in the same column. The hook-length formula is
fλ=∏c∈λ​h(c)n!​.

f(n−k,k)=k!(n−k+1)!n!(n−2k+1)​=(kn​)−(k−1n​)​.

First prove preservation of the row and column inequalities for distinct ordered labels. If b>cl​, row insertion appends it to the first row, and the inequalities remain valid. Otherwise let j be the first position with cj​>b. Replace cj​ by b and insert the bumped cj​ in the second row. Its first larger entry is some dk​ with k≤j, because dj​>cj​. Replace dk​ by cj​ and put the bumped dk​ in the new third-row cell.
The first-larger-entry choices preserve strict row order. At column k, if k<j the first-row entry is ck​<cj​, while if k=j it is the new b<cj​. At column j when k<j, the unchanged lower entry satisfies dj​>cj​>b. Every other old column keeps its previous inequalities. The new third-row entry is larger than the second-row first entry: for k=1 this is d1​>cj​, while for k>1 it follows from dk​>d1​. Thus the new shape (l,l,1) is a Young diagram with strictly increasing rows and columns. This proves the insertion assertion for the ordered-label convention.
There is a numbering qualification in the literal standard convention of part (a). If T uses exactly 1,…,2l, an arbitrary missing natural number b need not be 2l+1. For example, inserting six into rows (1,2);(3,4) produces (1,2,6);(3,4), which has the order inequalities but not the labels 1,…,5. Thus standardness with consecutive labels requires b=2l+1, or increasing relabelling afterward. The printed wording is retained in the private transcription; row insertion itself preserves the needed order structure.

The symmetric group acts on tabloids by permuting their entries; extending linearly gives the permutation module Mμ. Its dimension is
dimMμ=∏i​μi​!n!​.​
(26)
A tableau of type μ contains exactly μi​ copies of the label i. Fix a numbering of the λ-diagram's cells by 1,…,n. For a filling T, let the ith row set of an associated μ-tabloid be the set of cell numbers whose labels in T equal i. This is a bijection from type-μ fillings to μ-tabloids.
Define σT by moving labels along the numbered positions: its label at cell j is the old label at cell σ−1j. The associated row set is then the image under σ of the old row set. The simple modules are indexed by the p-regular partitions λ of n, meaning no part is repeated p or more times. Equip the tabloid permutation module with the bilinear form invariant under the symmetric group making its tabloid basis orthonormal. The construction is
Dλ=Sλ/(Sλ∩(Sλ)⊥).
(33)
The standard modular Specht theorem says this quotient is nonzero precisely for p-regular λ, is then simple, and these quotients give all simple modules.
To verify distinctness of labels, use the standard dominance triangularity theorem: if Dν occurs as a composition factor of Sλ, then ν≥λ; for a p-regular label the diagonal multiplicity is one. If Dλ≅Dμ, then Dλ occurs in Sμ, so λ≥μ, and the symmetric argument gives μ≥λ. Antisymmetry of the dominance order on partitions yields λ=μ​. These are the general representation-theoretic results allowed in this part.

The standard polytabloid basis is defined over the integers and remains a basis after reduction modulo three. Thus dimS(3,2)=f(3,2)=(25​)−(15​)=5. The given submodule has dimension four by part (d), so the quotient is one-dimensional and part (c) leaves only the trivial or sign action.
A transposition has ordinary trace one on S(3,2): the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule has only the horizontal bottom-row two-strip, leaving shape (3) with dimension one. Reduction of its integral action matrix preserves this trace in F. Its trace on D(4,1)=S(4,1) is 3−1=2, since the natural five-point permutation representation has three fixed coordinates and contains the trivial line. Therefore its trace on the one-dimensional quotient is 1−2=−1, distinct from one in characteristic three. This gives the characteristic-three sign quotient of the Specht module of shape three two. The quotient is the sign representation:
σ⋅v=sgn(σ)von S(3,2)/D(4,1).​
