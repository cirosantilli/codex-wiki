Second law of thermodynamics

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 14
For an isolated system, the second law requires ΔS≥0. More generally, include all affected surroundings to obtain
ΔStot​=ΔSsystem​+ΔSsurroundings​≥0.
(109)
Equality describes the reversible limit; irreversible macroscopic processes produce entropy. A closed system can lose entropy by exporting heat, so nondecrease does not apply to that system alone. The Clausius statement of the second law forbids a cyclic device whose sole effect is transferring heat from cold to hot. The Kelvin-Planck statement of the second law forbids a cyclic device whose sole effect is converting heat from one thermal reservoir completely into work. "Sole effect" matters: a refrigerator moves heat from cold to hot by consuming work. [ref]
For direct transfer of Q=1000J from a thermal reservoir at 600K to one at 300K, with no work extracted,
ΔStot​=−6001000​+3001000​=35​JK−1>0.
(110)
The reverse transfer alone would have negative total entropy change. Both directions conserve energy, but only the hot-to-cold direction is spontaneous. The finite-body example under entropy replaces these constant temperatures by changing ones.
Friction, free expansion and heat transfer across a finite temperature difference all introduce irreversibility. Moving arbitrarily slowly is insufficient if friction remains. A reversible thermodynamic process must permit restoration of both the system and surroundings. This gives engineers a useful ideal benchmark: irreversibility spends an opportunity to obtain work, even though energy itself is conserved. [ref]
