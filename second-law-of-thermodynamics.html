Second law of thermodynamics

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics
OurBigBook.com Words: 4k Articles: 27
For an isolated system, the second law requires ΔS≥0. More generally, include all affected surroundings to obtain
ΔStot​=ΔSsystem​+ΔSsurroundings​≥0.
(1)
Equality describes the reversible limit; irreversible macroscopic processes produce entropy. A closed system can lose entropy by exporting heat, so nondecrease does not apply to that system alone. The Clausius statement of the second law forbids a cyclic device whose sole effect is transferring heat from cold to hot. The Kelvin-Planck statement of the second law forbids a cyclic device whose sole effect is converting heat from one thermal reservoir completely into work. "Sole effect" matters: a refrigerator moves heat from cold to hot by consuming work. [ref]
For direct transfer of Q=1000J from a thermal reservoir at 600K to one at 300K, with no work extracted,
ΔStot​=−6001000​+3001000​=35​JK−1>0.
(2)
The reverse transfer alone would have negative total entropy change. Both directions conserve energy, but only the hot-to-cold direction is spontaneous. The finite-body example under entropy replaces these constant temperatures by changing ones.
Friction, free expansion and heat transfer across a finite temperature difference all introduce irreversibility. Moving arbitrarily slowly is insufficient if friction remains. A reversible thermodynamic process must permit restoration of both the system and surroundings. This gives engineers a useful ideal benchmark: irreversibility spends an opportunity to obtain work, even though energy itself is conserved. [ref]

Kelvin-Planck statement

Words: 51
Second law of thermodynamics OurBigBook.com
No cyclic device can have as its sole effect the extraction of heat from one reservoir and its complete conversion into work. If the reservoir loses heat Q>0 at temperature T, its entropy change is −Q/T while the cyclic device returns to its starting state. A sole-effect conversion would therefore reduce total entropy.

Thermodynamic arrow of time

Words: 92 Articles: 1
Second law of thermodynamics OurBigBook.com
The temporal orientation associated with the typical increase of macroscopic entropy away from suitable low-entropy conditions. It is a statistical claim about specified initial or boundary conditions and a macroscopic description, rather than a universal monotonicity theorem for every microscopic trajectory. Time reversal in classical mechanics pairs suitable increasing trajectories with decreasing ones, while fine-grained entropy is conserved under regular volume-preserving Hamiltonian flow.

Past hypothesis

Words: 37
Thermodynamic arrow of time OurBigBook.com
The hypothesis of a special low-entropy boundary condition in the past, used in statistical accounts of the thermodynamic arrow of time. A complete account must also state its probability measure over compatible microstates and the macroscopic description used to define entropy.

Entropy generation in finite-temperature heat transfer (dStot​=dQ(1/T−1/TR​))

Words: 97 Articles: 1
Second law of thermodynamics OurBigBook.com
When a system at instantaneous temperature T absorbs heat dQ from a reservoir at TR​, with no other entropy production in the system, its entropy increases by dQ/T while the reservoir changes by −dQ/TR​. The total is positive for spontaneous heat transfer across a finite temperature difference. A quasistatic mechanical path can therefore be thermally irreversible.

Entropy generation by mixing equal thermal masses (ΔS=2McP​ln2T1​T2​​T1​+T2​​)

Words: 45
Entropy generation in finite-temperature heat transfer OurBigBook.com
For equal masses M of the same liquid with constant specific heat cP​, adiabatic constant-pressure mixing gives final temperature Tf​=(T1​+T2​)/2 by enthalpy conservation. The entropy change is McP​ln(Tf​/T1​)+McP​ln(Tf​/T2​)=2McP​ln[(T1​+T2​)/(2T1​T2​​)]. The arithmetic-geometric mean inequality proves nonnegativity, with equality only for equal initial temperatures. There is no separate compositional mixing entropy for identical fluids.

Maximum work from two finite heat reservoirs (Wmax​)

Words: 33
Second law of thermodynamics OurBigBook.com
With constant heat capacities C1​,C2​, a reversible heat engine taking two bodies to a common temperature has Tf​=T1C1​/(C1​+C2​)​T2C2​/(C1​+C2​)​. The maximum work is C1​T1​+C2​T2​−(C1​+C2​)Tf​. heat isolation does not prohibit transferring work to a store included in the total isolated system.

Clausius inequality (∮δQ/T≤0)

Words: 73
Second law of thermodynamics OurBigBook.com
For a thermodynamic cycle, with heat positive into the system and each transfer divided by the absolute temperature of the relevant thermal boundary or reservoir, the cyclic integral is nonpositive. Equality holds for a reversible cycle. Closing an arbitrary process by a reversible return path yields ΔS≥∫δQ/T, because the reversible integral defines the entropy difference. The difference is the nonnegative entropy production; entropy transferred with heat need not itself be positive.

Reversible thermodynamic process (dS=δQrev​/T)

Words: 42
Second law of thermodynamics OurBigBook.com
A thermodynamically reversible process can be reversed without a net change of the system and surroundings. Its entropy change equals reversible heat transfer divided by temperature. A reversible adiabatic process conserves entropy; adiabaticity alone does not forbid internal entropy production in an irreversible evolution.

Clausius statement of the second law

Words: 248
Second law of thermodynamics OurBigBook.com
No cyclic device can have as its sole effect the transfer of heat from a colder thermal reservoir to a hotter thermal reservoir. The word “sole” includes the surroundings: the device must return to its starting state, and there must be no net supplied work, depletion of a stored resource, or accompanying change elsewhere. The statement excludes a self-running refrigerator, while allowing an ordinary refrigerator or heat pump supplied with work.[ref]
For a proposed transfer Q>0 from a fixed cold thermal reservoir at Tc​ to a fixed hot thermal reservoir at Th​>Tc​>0, with no other effect, the total entropy change would be
ΔStot​=Th​Q​−Tc​Q​<0.
(3)
The first law of thermodynamics permits this energy balance; the Second law of thermodynamics forbids the negative total entropy change. For Q=600J, Tc​=300K and Th​=600K, it is −1JK−1. Direct heat transfer in the opposite direction has positive total entropy change. A powered heat pump has a different budget: it supplies work and delivers more heat to the hot side than it extracts from the cold side.
A short equivalence argument links this restriction to the Kelvin-Planck statement of the second law. Couple a hypothetical device that moves 600J from cold to hot with no other effect to the heat engine illustrated above, which absorbs 1000J, rejects 600J, and delivers 400J. The cold-side transfers cancel; the combined cyclic device takes 400J from the hot thermal reservoir and delivers exactly 400J of work, violating the Kelvin-Planck statement of the second law. Matching transfers is an ideal composition argument, not a claim that such a device exists.[ref]

Kelvin-Planck statement of the second law

Words: 288
Second law of thermodynamics OurBigBook.com
No cyclic device can have as its sole effect the extraction of heat from one thermal reservoir and its complete conversion into net work. A putative device absorbing Qh​>0 and returning to its starting state with W=Qh​ and no other transfer would conserve energy, yet violate the Second law of thermodynamics. Rejecting heat to colder surroundings is an essential part of the ordinary two-thermal reservoir heat engine budget.[ref]
The cyclic and sole-effect qualifications matter. A single isothermal expansion of an ideal gas can absorb heat and deliver equal work while leaving its volume changed; restoring the initial state must also be counted. Likewise, a device discharging stored internal energy or consuming fuel has another effect. Converting supplied electrical work completely to heat through Joule heating reverses the proposed conversion and is allowed. The statement also allows cyclic devices that exchange heat with one thermal reservoir while consuming net work; it forbids positive net work output with that single heat source as the only external change.
Conversely, a hypothetical complete-conversion device could take 100J from the hot thermal reservoir and supply 100J of work to an ideal refrigerator that extracts 100J from the cold thermal reservoir and delivers 200J to the hot one. At 600K and 300K, that refrigerator is itself admissible: its reservoir entropy changes cancel. The composite would consume no external work and move a net 100J from cold to hot, violating the Clausius statement of the second law. Together with the construction there, this proves the equivalence of the two impossibility statements.[ref]
For the two fixed positive-temperature thermal reservoir hypotheses, Carnot's theorem gives the sharper restriction η≤1−Tc​/Th​<1. A reversible thermodynamic process reaches that bound in the ideal Carnot cycle; an ordinary device's smaller thermal efficiency reflects its entropy production and measurement boundary. An inefficient observed engine is compatible with the Second law of thermodynamics, rather than evidence that the bound has been experimentally determined.

Carnot's theorem (η≤1−Tc​/Th​)

Words: 3k Articles: 16
Second law of thermodynamics OurBigBook.com
No cyclic heat engine exchanging heat only with two fixed thermal reservoirs at positive absolute temperatures Th​>Tc​ can exceed the thermal efficiency of an engine whose cycle is a reversible thermodynamic process between them. All such reversible engines have the same thermal efficiency, regardless of their working substance. Let Qh​>0 be heat absorbed from the hot thermal reservoir and Qc​>0 the magnitude rejected to the cold one. Then
W=Qh​−Qc​,η=Qh​W​≤ηC​=1−Th​Tc​​.
(4)
The work convention is the one in the first law of thermodynamics: W>0 is net output by the engine. Use absolute thermodynamic temperatures, such as values in kelvin; ratios of readings in degree Celsius are invalid because their zero is shifted.
For Th​=600K, Tc​=300K and Qh​=1000J, the maximum thermal efficiency is 50%. A reversible engine delivers W=500J and rejects Qc​=500J. The thermal reservoirs have entropy changes −1000/600 and +500/300JK−1, which cancel. The engine's own entropy change is zero over its thermodynamic cycle.
A short derivation now becomes transparent: the Second law of thermodynamics gives −Qh​/Th​+Qc​/Tc​≥0, and the first law of thermodynamics gives W=Qh​−Qc​. Together they yield the bound and, more precisely,
W=Qh​(1−Th​Tc​​)−Tc​ΔStot​.
(5)
At fixed Qh​,Th​,Tc​, every extra unit of total entropy production reduces output by Tc​ units of energy. [ref]
Fixed thermal reservoirs are essential. Finite bodies cool and warm, so their initial temperature ratio cannot be used throughout an entire extraction process. As a computed contrast, the bodies with equal constant heat capacity in the entropy example would reach Tf​=600×300​=424.26K if a reversible engine extracted work until their temperatures matched. Zero total entropy change gives this geometric mean; the first law of thermodynamics then gives Wmax​=C(600+300−2Tf​)=5147.19J. Direct contact reaches 450K and extracts no work.
Carnot's 1824 book asked whether the useful output of machines driven by heat was fundamentally limited and whether changing the working substance could improve that limit. Its reversible comparisons are the historical setting of Carnot's theorem. Carnot used the then-current caloric picture, treating heat as conserved in its passage through an engine; the modern balance Qh​−Qc​=W should not be read back into that text.[ref]

Carnot cycle (W=(Th​−Tc​)ΔS)

Words: 3k Articles: 14
Carnot's theorem OurBigBook.com
A Carnot cycle realizes the reversible bound in Carnot's theorem. Its four steps for an ideal gas working substance are:
  • Reversible isothermal expansion at Th​, absorbing Qh​ and increasing entropy by ΔS.
  • Reversible adiabatic expansion, doing work while the temperature falls from Th​ to Tc​ and entropy stays fixed.
  • Reversible isothermal compression at Tc​, rejecting Qc​ and decreasing entropy by ΔS.
  • Reversible adiabatic compression, requiring work to restore Th​ at fixed entropy.
The temperature versus entropy plot in the Carnot's theorem figure is therefore a rectangle traversed clockwise. Because δQrev​=TdS, its width is ΔS=Qh​/Th​. For the illustrated engine, ΔS=5/3JK−1: the upper step absorbs 600×5/3=1000J, the lower step rejects 300×5/3=500J, and the enclosed area is the 500J net work. Geometry displays the thermal efficiency without solving the gas motion. The two vertical steps are isentropic processes, not constant-temperature steps. [ref] [ref]
Reversible heat transfer is a limit of vanishing temperature differences; ordinary finite-rate transfer introduces entropy production. The Carnot cycle is a performance benchmark rather than a guarantee of useful power. Running it backward gives the ideal operation of a refrigerator or heat pump, with work supplied to move heat toward the hotter thermal reservoir. [ref]

Isothermal process

Words: 2k Articles: 12
Carnot cycle OurBigBook.com
An isothermal process changes a thermodynamic system while keeping its temperature constant. It need not keep its pressure, volume or internal energy constant, and it does not generally have zero heat transfer. An adiabatic process specifies zero heat transfer instead. For example, a slowly expanding ideal gas can absorb heat from a heat bath and deliver the same amount of work while its temperature stays fixed.
For a fixed amount of substance and composition, internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on temperature. With Q>0 for heat entering and Wby​>0 for work delivered to the surroundings, the first law of thermodynamics therefore gives
ΔU=Q−Wby​=0,Q=Wby​.
(7)
This equality does not determine either transfer until the path is specified. Isothermal ideal-gas work gives the reversible value; an insulated free expansion has a different value even when its equilibrium endpoints have the same temperature. The closed system convention here excludes changes in bulk kinetic energy and external potential energy.
A conducting cylinder in a large, stirred heat bath approximates an isothermal process when the piston moves slowly enough for thermal conduction to keep up. A quasistatic process keeps the gas close to internal thermodynamic equilibrium; a reversible thermodynamic process additionally requires negligible friction and vanishing driving differences in pressure and temperature. Exact equality of the gas and heat bath temperatures gives no finite heat transfer rate through a finite thermal resistance. The reversible description is a limit of arbitrarily small differences and arbitrarily slow motion, illustrated by the finite-rate isothermal piston model.[ref]
For a real gas, internal energy at constant temperature can change with volume because molecular interaction potential energy changes. In one smooth thermodynamic phase of a fixed-composition simple compressible system,
(∂V∂U​)T,n​=T(∂T∂p​)V,n​−p.
(8)
Thus an isothermal process generally has Q=ΔU+Wby​, with no reason to set ΔU=0. The right-hand side vanishes for the ideal gas law, but not generally for another equation of state.[ref]

An isotherm is a curve of equilibrium states at fixed temperature, often drawn in the pressure-volume plane. For an ideal gas of fixed amount it obeys pV=constant. The curve is not itself a specification of the rate, reversibility or experimental mechanism by which the states are visited.
Isothermal ideal-gas work (Wby,rev​=nRTln(Vf​/Vi​))
Words: 215
Isothermal process OurBigBook.com
For a reversible thermodynamic process that changes the volume of a fixed amount of ideal gas at absolute temperature T, the work done by the gas is
Wby,rev​=∫Vi​Vf​​pdV=nRT∫Vi​Vf​​VdV​=nRTlnVi​Vf​​=nRTlnpf​pi​​.
(9)
Here n is the amount of substance, R the molar gas constant, p the pressure, and ln the natural logarithm. The ideal gas law supplies p=nRT/V; the integral is the signed area under the pressure-volume curve. Isothermal expansion gives positive work; isothermal compression gives negative work. A convention counting work done on the gas instead has Won​=−Wby​.[ref]
For a calculated example, let n=1mol, T=300K and Vi​=10L, Vf​=20L. The pressure falls from approximately 249.4 to 124.7kPa, and
Wby,rev​=Q=nRTln2≈1.729kJ,ΔU=0.
(10)
Traversing the same isothermal process backward changes both transfer signs: Wby,rev​=Q≈−1.729kJ. Neither direction changes internal energy of an ideal gas. These stipulated values illustrate the energy balance; they are not experimental measurements.
The shaded geometric area is positive in both panels; the direction of the integral supplies the sign of work. Knowing only the two equilibrium endpoints does not justify shading this curve for an irreversible process. For an idealized piston moving against a specified external resisting pressure, boundary work is Wby​=∫pext​dV. Substitution of the equilibrium gas pressure requires the appropriate reversible limit. In free expansion into vacuum, pext​=0 and this boundary work is zero.
Finite-rate isothermal piston model
Words: 226
Isothermal process OurBigBook.com
Why must a conducting piston move slowly? A simple model couples the first law of thermodynamics to Newton's law of cooling. Assume a fixed amount of ideal gas with uniform temperature T(t) and constant whole-sample heat capacity at constant volume CV​, a heat bath maintained at Tb​, negligible friction, and mechanically slow motion. Let G>0 be the effective thermal conductance, the reciprocal of the thermal resistance, in WK−1. Then
Q˙​=G(Tb​−T),p=VnRT​,CV​T˙=G(Tb​−T)−pV˙.
(11)
Positive heat transfer must offset expansion work to keep T near Tb​. A finite G and nonzero piston speed therefore require a temperature difference. This uniform-temperature model neglects spatial temperature gradients, sensor response, cylinder heat capacity and any change of G with piston position; it is an illustrative calculation, not a fit to the isothermal gas syringe experiment.
For a prescribed exponential volume change V=Vi​eλt, define τ=CV​/G, s=t/τ and ϵ=nRλ/G. The dimensionless temperature θ=T/Tb​ obeys a linear ordinary differential equation with initial value θ(0)=1:
dsdθ​=1−(1+ϵ)θ,θ(s)=1+ϵ1​+1+ϵϵ​e−(1+ϵ)s.
(12)
The plotted expansion uses stipulated CV​=(5/2)nR and stops at Vf​=2Vi​, at sf​=ln2/[(5/2)ϵ]. During expansion, the larger ϵ produces the larger temperature deficit; once the piston is held fixed, the deficit undergoes exponential decay as e−(s−sf​). Reducing ∣ϵ∣ makes the path more nearly an isothermal process, while increasing its duration. Reversing the piston with λ<0 produces a temperature excess and outward heat transfer; the small-∣ϵ∣ regime is the intended near-isothermal approximation. Mechanical slowness alone does not ensure sufficient heat transfer.
Boyle's law (pV=constant)
Words: 1k Articles: 4
Isothermal process OurBigBook.com
Boyle's law states that a fixed amount of substance of an ideal gas at constant absolute thermodynamic temperature has constant product of absolute pressure and total volume:
pV=C,p1​V1​=p2​V2​,p2​=p1​V2​V1​​.
(13)
Here p is measured relative to a vacuum, not relative to the surrounding atmospheric pressure. Halving the volume doubles the absolute pressure; doubling the volume halves it. A real gas approximately follows Boyle's law when it is sufficiently dilute and far from a phase transition.[ref]
For an original calculated example, suppose a sealed gas sample has p1​=100kPa and V1​=100cm3. After isothermal compression to V2​=50cm3,
p2​=(100kPa)50cm3100cm3​=200kPa.
(14)
Both states have C=10000kPacm3=10J, since 1kPacm3=10−3J. The volume ratio is dimensionless, so using the same volume units in numerator and denominator leaves the pressure in kilopascals. Although pV has units of energy, its value is not the compression work: isothermal ideal-gas work involves an integral along the path.
The blue curves show calculated ideal gas states, not measurements. The left panel is a branch of a hyperbola; the right panel plots exactly the same absolute pressures against 1/V. The dashed line uses gauge pressure with a stipulated constant atmospheric pressure of 100kPa. Its displaced intercept explains why absolute pressure in Boyle's law matters.
A short derivation starts from the ideal gas law, pV=nRT. Fixing the amount of substance n and absolute thermodynamic temperature T fixes C=nRT, where R is the molar gas constant. The microscopic picture in ideal gas pressure from wall collisions is equally appealing: at fixed temperature, the typical particle kinetic energy stays fixed, while compression increases the number density and wall momentum transfer per unit surface area and time. A larger absolute pressure follows without requiring hotter particles.
The constraints are essential. A leak changes the amount of substance. Rapid compression can raise the temperature before heat transfer restores thermal equilibrium, as in the isothermal gas syringe experiment. Molecular size and interactions in a dense real gas invalidate the ideal gas approximation; the Van der Waals equation describes one such correction. Equal endpoint temperatures allow an endpoint test of Boyle's law, but do not establish that every intervening state lies on an isothermal process.
For fixed mass m, mass density satisfies ρ=m/V=mp/C, so it is proportional to absolute pressure. In a dilute bubbly flow, Boyle's law allows bubble volume and its contribution to buoyancy to increase as hydrostatic pressure decreases, provided gas mass is retained, dissolution is negligible, and the temperature stays approximately fixed. Its usefulness is that a simple ratio predicts a substantial size change without tracking individual molecules.
Absolute pressure in Boyle's law
Words: 127
Boyle's law OurBigBook.com
With gauge pressure pg​ referenced to atmospheric pressure patm​, absolute pressure is p=pg​+patm​. Thus Boyle's law becomes
(pg,1​+patm,1​)V1​=(pg,2​+patm,2​)V2​.
(15)
Even a constant atmospheric pressure cannot be dropped from these sums. For the calculated 100kPa to 200kPa example, take patm​=100kPa. The initial and final gauge pressures are 0 and 100kPa; their products with the respective volume are 0 and 5000kPacm3, which are unequal. Zero gauge pressure means balance with the surroundings, not absence of gas or absence of wall momentum transfer.
For a frictionless piston of surface area A with its outer face exposed to atmospheric pressure, the additional inward force required for mechanical equilibrium is F=(p−patm​)A=pg​A. This is why a mechanical load can reveal a pressure difference even though the equation of state requires absolute pressure. With piston friction, the load alone no longer determines pg​A; a direct pressure sensor avoids that particular inference.
Linearized plot of Boyle's law (p=C(1/V))
Words: 124
Boyle's law OurBigBook.com
A linearized plot of Boyle's law uses x=1/V as its horizontal coordinate. Then absolute pressure satisfies p=Cx: the slope is C=nRT, and the ideal intercept is zero. In the figure, (V,p)=(100,100) and (50,200) in cm3 and kPa become (x,p)=(0.010,100) and (0.020,200) in cm−3 and kPa. Both give slope 10000kPacm3. The Vernier experiment includes this reciprocal-volume graph as its extension.[ref]
At constant atmospheric pressure, gauge pressure instead obeys pg​=Cx−patm​, with the same slope and a negative intercept. A nonzero fitted intercept in an intended absolute pressure plot can indicate sensor bias or an incorrect volume correction, but is not by itself a unique diagnosis. The origin is a mathematical extrapolation to infinite volume and zero absolute pressure, rather than an additional measured state.
Gas-syringe measurement of Boyle's law
Words: 472 Articles: 1
Boyle's law OurBigBook.com
Vernier's Boyle's Law: Pressure-Volume Relationship in Gases, Experiment 6 of Chemistry with Vernier, specifies a graduated 20mL gas syringe, a pressure sensor and LabQuest recording. Preparation sets the disconnected syringe to 10.0mL, then attaches it to the sensor valve, trapping air. At several plunger positions, the displayed pressure is allowed to stabilize before the paired volume is entered. The analysis tests whether pV remains approximately constant. It adds 0.8mL to each syringe reading for the sensor's internal volume; this correction belongs to that apparatus. The worksheet supplies a blank results table, not measured values.[ref]
The manufacturer's Gas Pressure Sensor User Manual identifies a membrane sensor referenced to a vacuum, hence an absolute pressure readout. Its calibration section uses the actual local atmospheric pressure for an ambient reference check, rather than a weather report corrected to sea level. Checking this baseline before sealing separates an offset error from compression physics.[ref]
Additional controls follow from the model. Keep the same sealed amount of substance, connections and ambient temperature; persistent pressure drift at held volume after thermal equilibrium suggests a leak or another uncontrolled change. Small steps and waiting allow compression heat to leave the gas; avoiding hand warming reduces another heat input. Compare settled readings when approaching a position from either direction. Plunger friction affects the applied force and position reproducibility, so record actual pressure and actual plunger position rather than predicting pressure from the load. These are proposed controls, not reported experimental outcomes.
Nearly constant pV across equilibrated states supports the fixed-temperature ideal gas approximation over the tested range. Stabilized pressure alone does not demonstrate uniform or constant temperature; the isothermal gas syringe experiment adds a temperature readout to distinguish a transient from its cooled endpoint. Finite sensor accuracy and uncertain total volume limit the test, and agreement does not establish ideal behavior at every pressure. Repeated return readings help distinguish reproducibility from a one-direction trend.
Gas-syringe dead-volume correction (V=Vread​+Vd​)
Words: 133
Gas-syringe measurement of Boyle's law OurBigBook.com
In a gas syringe, the graduated barrel reading Vread​ can omit a fixed connecting or sensor volume Vd​ occupied by the same sealed gas. For a common settled temperature, Boyle's law then requires
p1​(Vread,1​+Vd​)=p2​(Vread,2​+Vd​),p1​=CVread​​+CVd​​.
(16)
This second relation makes a graph of 1/p against Vread​ a straight line; its intercept divided by its slope estimates Vd​ under these assumptions. Unequal temperatures between the barrel and the connecting space require a different correction, analogous to the gas thermometer dead-space correction.
For an original hypothetical example, let Vd​=1cm3, Vread,1​=20cm3, p1​=100kPa, and Vread,2​=10cm3. The total volume falls from 21 to 11cm3, so p2​=100(21/11)kPa≈190.91kPa, rather than 200kPa. The omitted volume makes the total compression ratio smaller than the barrel-reading ratio. These stipulated values are independent of the manufacturer's apparatus correction and are not measurements.
Isothermal compression
Words: 437 Articles: 2
Isothermal process OurBigBook.com
An isothermal compression reduces the volume of a thermodynamic system at fixed temperature. For a fixed-composition ideal gas compressed reversibly from Vi​ to Vf​<Vi​, isothermal ideal-gas work gives
Wby,rev​=nRTln(Vf​/Vi​)<0,Q=Wby,rev​<0,ΔU=0.
(17)
The surroundings supply positive work Won​, and the gas rejects the same amount of heat to the heat bath. Compressing 1mol at 300K from 20 to 10L requires approximately 1.729kJ of work input and rejects 1.729kJ of heat. If that heat transfer cannot keep up, the temperature rises: the process departs from isothermal compression toward adiabatic compression. The finite-rate isothermal piston model explains the required small temperature excess.
Isothermal gas syringe experiment
Words: 356 Articles: 1
Isothermal compression OurBigBook.com
A gas syringe makes the distinction between a constant-temperature path and equal-temperature endpoints observable. The Ideal Gas Law Apparatus (TD-8596A), Instruction Manual 012-08420B by PASCO scientific specifies a TD-8596A gas syringe with a mechanical stop, an internal thermistor, an attached pressure sensor and data acquisition. Record the stop volume with the coupling disconnected, then set 40mL and seal the coupling. Record pressure and temperature, compress quickly to the stop near 20mL, and hold until both readouts settle. The manual gives roughly 30s for settling and about 0.5s for the thermistor response. Releasing the plunger gives a second transient and a final volume to record.[ref]
The manual describes a pressure overshoot and temperature rise during rapid compression, followed by cooling at the held volume; the settled pressure remains higher than initially. This illustrates approximately adiabatic compression followed by heat rejection, rather than an isothermal compression throughout. Use the settled initial and compressed readings to test Boyle's law, correcting both syringe readings by the unmarked tubing volume Vd​:
pi​(Vread,i​+Vd​)=pf​(Vread,f​+Vd​).
(18)
Use absolute pressure, not gauge pressure. A transient comparison of pV/T must pair simultaneous pressure and temperature readings: the sensor lag can separate their peaks.[ref]
For an additional test of the path, repeat with small compression steps, wait for stable temperature and pressure at every step, and repeat in reverse. Keep the sealed amount of substance, connecting tubing and ambient temperature fixed; prevent hand warming of the barrel and check for leaks by watching for continued pressure drift at held volume after thermal equilibrium. These are proposed controls, rather than reported measurements. Agreement of equilibrated points with Boyle's law supports the fixed-temperature ideal gas law approximation, but does not prove that the rapid segments between them were isothermal processes. A single local temperature sensor can miss temperature gradients; plunger friction can produce direction-dependent readings. The pressure and volume data estimate reversible work only insofar as the path approaches a reversible thermodynamic process; they do not directly measure heat. After motion stops, cooling at fixed volume has zero boundary work and Q=ΔU<0, even though the complete equal-endpoint-temperature ideal gas run has ΔU=0.

Isothermal expansion
Words: 195
Isothermal process OurBigBook.com
An isothermal expansion increases the volume of a thermodynamic system at fixed temperature. For a reversible ideal gas path, the gas delivers positive work and absorbs positive heat, with Q=Wby,rev​=nRTln(Vf​/Vi​) and ΔU=0. The heat bath supplies the expansion energy; without sufficient heat transfer, doing work instead lowers the temperature. This transfer is the hot step of a Carnot cycle, and it is why an expanding gas need not cool when its surroundings continuously replenish its internal energy.
An insulated free expansion into an evacuated compartment is different. In the idealization with no heat exchange, no external work and no container energy change, Q=Wby​=0. Since internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on temperature, its final equilibrium temperature equals its initial value. The transient need not have a uniform temperature or pressure, so equal endpoint temperatures do not make it a reversible isotherm. Do not apply the logarithmic isothermal ideal-gas work formula to this irreversible process. Its entropy still increases by ΔS=nRln(Vf​/Vi​); entropy change in free expansion evaluates this state function difference using a hypothetical reversible path.[ref]
For a real gas, even equal-temperature equilibrium endpoints need not have equal internal energy. An insulated free expansion can therefore change the temperature, while a controlled isothermal expansion must provide the heat needed for both its internal energy change and its delivered work. The internal energy at constant temperature article gives the Van der Waals equation example.[ref]
