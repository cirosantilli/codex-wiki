Bardeen-Brattain point-contact transistor experiment (1947)

 Home Physics Semiconductor Transistor Point-contact transistor
OurBigBook.com Words: 369
In December 1947, John Bardeen and Walter Houser Brattain obtained transistor action at Bell Telephone Laboratories. William Shockley proposed the distinct bipolar junction transistor within the following month.[ref]
Their 1948 paper describes ground, etched germanium, initially also anodized and rinsed. Surface contacts separated by 50–250μm share a broad base contact. Positive emitter biasing injects electron holes; negative collector biasing draws them toward the collector. An input signal changes emitter electric current, and a collector electrical load converts the resulting change into voltage.[ref]
Early electric field control experiments gave unexpectedly weak responses. John Bardeen proposed that surface states screened the interior. Surface charge could create a barrier and a hole-rich semiconductor inversion layer. Replacing an electrolyte control with a gold contact reversed the response sign, suggesting electron hole carrier injection. Closely spaced contacts made from a gold-coated wedge split with a razor then enabled power gain. The relative importance of surface and bulk transport depended on preparation and collector forming.[ref]
The 1948 authors found that collector-current increments could exceed emitter-current increments; their current gain depended on biasing.[ref] A terminal-current ratio alone cannot identify the microscopic path. Surface states, the local barrier, carrier injection and contact preparation prevent a single ideal modern bipolar junction transistor model from explaining every point-contact transistor.

Thus a local current gain α>1 requires an accompanying base-current change. The collector increment is not simply a count of injected electron holes, and it does not violate electric charge conservation.
For an experiment following the functional arrangement, first establish each contact's separate current-voltage characteristic relative to the base. Then hold collector biasing fixed, vary a small emitter signal, and compare the collector electrical load voltage with the input. A decisive power gain check measures both input electric current and voltage and the output power in a known electrical load; voltage enlargement alone is insufficient. These are proposed measurement controls, not additional claims about the 1947 laboratory record.
Repeating a setting tests drift; reversing or removing emitter biasing tests whether the response requires carrier injection. Contact motion, surface contamination, temperature, direct electrical coupling and noise can all affect the inference. A small-signal model describes measured local terminal response without deciding every microscopic mechanism.
