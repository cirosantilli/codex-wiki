Chemical potential and Helmholtz free energy (μm​=(∂F/∂n)T,V​)

Rigid compartments impose a different constraint: fixed volume rather than fixed pressure. With Helmholtz free energy F=U−TS,
dF=−SdT−pdV+∑i​μm,i​dni​,μm,i​=(∂ni​∂F​)T,V,nj=i​​.
(203)
This follows immediately by differentiating F=U−TS in the chemical potential differential above. It expresses the same chemical potential through the thermodynamic potential appropriate to fixed temperature and volume.
Consider a closed system consisting of two rigid compartments of equal volume V, maintained at a common fixed temperature T. A valve permits exchange of the same ideal gas; the partition stays in place. Initially nA​=9mol and nB​=3mol, with conserved total amount of substance ntot​=12mol. These are macroscopic moles, modeled continuously, not twelve individual particles. By the ideal gas law, pB​/pA​=nB​/nA​=1/3, so initially
μm,B​−μm,A​=−RTln3.
(204)
At 300K with the stipulated R, this is approximately −2740.311Jmol−1, favoring transfer from A to B.
At fixed T and equal V, integrating the ideal-gas chemical potential gives the total Helmholtz free energy, up to a term independent of the allocation,
Ftotal​=RT[nA​lnnref​nA​​+nB​lnnref​nB​​−ntot​]+C.
(205)
Here nref​ is a positive reference amount of substance; any omitted common term linear in nA​+nB​ is constant along this exchange. Let x=nA​/ntot​ be the dimensionless fraction in A. The relative Helmholtz free energy then takes the particularly simple form
ntot​RTFtotal​(x)−Ftotal​(1/2)​=xlnx+(1−x)ln(1−x)+ln2.
(206)
Its derivative along the allowed transfer and its second derivative are
dnA​dFtotal​​=μm,A​−μm,B​=RTln1−xx​,dnA2​d2Ftotal​​=RT(nA​1​+nB​1​)>0.
(207)
Thus the Helmholtz free energy is a strictly convex function of the allocation for positive amounts. Its unique minimum has x=1/2, or nA​=nB​=6mol, and equal chemical potentials. Initially its excess above this minimum is ntot​RT[43​ln(3/2)+41​ln(1/2)]≃3915.474J at 300K. Relaxation decreases the total Helmholtz free energy by this amount in the model; the final pressures are also equal because both compartments have the same volume.
The beautiful connection is that the direction of matter exchange and the slope of a free energy curve are the same calculation. The entropy gained by redistributing the ideal gas makes equal allocation favorable here. As with chemical equilibrium, a free energy minimum identifies the favored state, while transport properties determine how quickly it is reached. The fixed-temperature, fixed-pressure Gibbs free energy criterion is summarized in 16.4 Free Energy; the rigid experiment instead uses Helmholtz free energy.
