For real z>0, define
F(z)=∫0∞​1+t/ze−t​dt.
(5)
The integrand is positive and at most e−t, so 0<F(z)<1 and the improper integral converges. Substituting s=z+t also gives F(z)=zezE1​(z) in terms of the exponential integral; the original representation makes positivity and the large-z behavior transparent.

F(z)=SN​(z)+RN​(z),SN​(z)=∑k=0N−1​zk(−1)kk!​,

(8)
This remainder formula proves the asymptotic expansion at every fixed order: RN​=O(z−N)=o(z−N+1) as z→+∞. Its sign also gives alternating bounds: an odd N overestimates F(z), and an even N underestimates it.
At z=10, an independent evaluation of the integral gives F(10)≈0.9156333394. Three retained terms give
S3​(10)=1−0.1+0.02=0.92,∣F(10)−S3​(10)∣<0.006.
(9)
Five retained terms give S5​(10)=0.9164 with error bounded by 5!/105=0.0012. The actual errors are about 0.004367 and 0.000767, respectively. The error bound is conservative, but established without relying on the numerical integral evaluation.
Nevertheless, the formal infinite series diverges at every fixed z>0. The magnitudes Tk​=k!/zk satisfy Tk+1​/Tk​=(k+1)/z, so they eventually increase without bound. In particular, the terms fail the necessary zero-term condition for a convergent series. The finite remainder identity remains valid after convergence has failed; this is precisely what makes the example useful.
