Module theory

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Commutative algebra
OurBigBook.com Words: 12k Articles: 232
Module theory studies abelian groups with scalar multiplication by a ring. It extends linear algebra: a module over a field is a vector space, while ring scalars let the same language describe integer arithmetic and linear operators. The new phenomena are visible before any abstract classification: some nonzero scalars cannot be divided out, some modules have torsion elements of a module, and some have no basis of a module.
The distinction is useful because an integer relation such as 6m=0 carries information which disappears if one assumes all nonzero scalars have multiplicative inverses. A finitely generated module over a principal ideal domain can be separated into a finite free module and a torsion module; the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain unifies the classification of finitely generated abelian groups with the study of linear operators through polynomial rings. See Keith Conrad's Modules over a PID, Theorem 4.1 and the operator applications.
A historical anchor is Emmy Noether's 1921 paper [ref]. Its first section studies rings and ideals under the condition that every ideal is a finitely generated module over its ring, and connects this to the ascending chain condition; later sections develop primary decomposition. This is an early source for the structural viewpoint underlying Noetherian rings. [ref] makes those statements accessible in English.
