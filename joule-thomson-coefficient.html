Joule-Thomson coefficient (μ=(∂T/∂p)H​)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Enthalpy
OurBigBook.com Words: 846 Articles: 4
The Joule-Thomson coefficient is the local temperature response to changing pressure at fixed enthalpy and fixed amount and composition:
μJT​=(∂p∂T​)H​,[μJT​]=KPa−1.
(7)
It describes the equilibrium endpoints of a Joule-Thomson effect experiment meeting the steady-flow energy balance assumptions. With Δp=p2​−p1​<0, a sufficiently small drop gives ΔT≃μJT​Δp. Thus μJT​>0 means cooling, μJT​<0 warming, and μJT​=0 no first-order change. At zero coefficient, a finite drop can still change temperature. For a chosen illustrative local value μJT​=0.20Kbar−1 and Δp=−0.50bar, the linear prediction is ΔT=−0.10K; these are stipulated numbers, not measured gas properties.
For a fixed-composition simple compressible system, the measurable form is
μJT​=Cp​T(∂V/∂T)p​−V​=Cp​V​(Tα−1).
(8)
Here Cp​ is the whole sample's heat capacity at constant pressure and α its volumetric coefficient of thermal expansion; alternatively use molar volume and molar heat capacity consistently. An ideal gas has α=1/T, so μJT​=0. Its enthalpy depends only on temperature; positive heat capacity at constant pressure then makes any such finite throttle preserve temperature. A real gas can have either sign. For Cp​>0, compare Tα with one: the thermal expansion response decides the sign, not insulation alone.[ref]
A short derivation uses dH=TdS+Vdp and the Maxwell relation (∂S/∂p)T​=−(∂V/∂T)p​:
dH=Cp​dT+[V−T(∂T∂V​)p​]dp.
(9)
Setting dH=0 gives the coefficient. These are equilibrium state function identities, not a claim of a reversible thermodynamic process inside the throttle. For a finite drop, solve the endpoint enthalpy equality or integrate
T2​−T1​=∫p1​p2​​μJT​(T(p),p)dp
(10)
along the constant-enthalpy equilibrium curve. The coefficient can change sign during that integration, as the Van der Waals throttling model illustrates.
