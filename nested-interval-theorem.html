Nested interval theorem

 ... Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Real line Interval (mathematics) Nested intervals
OurBigBook.com Words: 233
If I1​⊇I2​⊇⋯ are nonempty closed intervals with finite endpoints, then at least one real number belongs to every member. If their lengths tend to zero, exactly one real number belongs to every member. The result turns an unlimited sequence of consistent enclosures into an existence guarantee.
Write In​=[an​,bn​]. Nesting makes the left endpoints nondecreasing and the right endpoints nonincreasing. Their bounds give finite supremum and infimum, with
⋂n=1∞​In​=[supn≥1​an​, infn≥1​bn​].
(57)
Indeed, every bn​ is an upper bound for all the aj​, so the least-upper-bound property gives supj​aj​≤bn​ for every n. This proves the displayed interval is nonempty. If bn​−an​→0, its two endpoints must coincide. This is the connection to the completeness of the real numbers discussed in Günther Hörmann and David Langer, Einführung in die Analysis, §§3.15–3.16: nested intervals and order completeness.
The distinction between existence and uniqueness is useful in computation. If an unknown point x∗​ lies in every [an​,bn​], the midpoint mn​=(an​+bn​)/2 satisfies
∣mn​−x∗​∣≤2bn​−an​​.
(58)
Thus the enclosure itself certifies an absolute error without knowing the answer. The bisection error bound is the special case where each update halves the width.
Closedness and a finite initial enclosure matter. The open intervals (0,1/n) have no common point, while the closed but unbounded intervals [n,∞) also have empty intersection. A picture of a few intervals cannot certify the intersection of an infinite family; the hypotheses of the nested interval theorem do that work.
