Biological cell

 Home Biology
OurBigBook.com Words: 26k Articles: 466
A biological cell is a membrane-bounded basic unit of living organization. In a cell-level experiment it can be an experimental unit, provided that treatment assignment and relevant interactions are handled at that level.
The shared architecture of cellular life combines a selective cell membrane, internal cytoplasm, inherited information in DNA, and ribosomes that assemble proteins. Metabolism supplies transformations of matter and energy needed for maintenance and growth. These components form a coupled system: a boundary retains useful molecules, enzymes organize chemical reactions, and inherited information supports rebuilding the machinery. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: prokaryotic cells.
A worked structural comparison: in a typical prokaryote, DNA occupies a region without a surrounding cell nucleus; in a typical nucleated eukaryote, much of the DNA lies inside a membrane-bounded cell nucleus. Both still have a cell membrane, cytoplasm and ribosomes. The extra boundary compartmentalizes processes; it does not create a different basic principle of living organization. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: eukaryotic cells.
Shared architecture describes cellular life and its developmental machinery, rather than requiring every specialized biological cell to retain every component. A mature mammalian red blood cell, for example, lacks a cell nucleus, although it develops from nucleated precursors. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: components of blood. This distinction makes cellular unity compatible with striking specialization.
