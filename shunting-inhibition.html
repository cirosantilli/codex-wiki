Shunting inhibition

 Home Biology Neuroscience Neuron Synapse Chemical synapse
OurBigBook.com Words: 223
Shunting inhibition is suppression of another input's effect through increased synaptic conductance: opening additional ion channels lowers effective electrical resistance, reducing the voltage response to a given added electric current. When the added reversal potential equals the resting membrane potential, there can be no voltage change at rest and still be inhibition of a simultaneous input. See 1.4 Limitations of the Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Model.
Use the passive synaptic potential model below with leak gL​=10nS, EL​=−65mV and capacitance C=200pF. A sustained inward test electric current of Iext​=100pA alone gives a steady voltage increase of Iext​/gL​=10mV, so V∞​=−55mV. Effective electrical resistance is 100MΩ and the time constant is 20ms.
Now hold a shunting conductance gi​=20nS open with Ei​=EL​=−65mV. With no test electric current, rest stays at −65mV. With the same 100pA test electric current, total conductance becomes 30nS, so the steady voltage increase is only 100/30≃3.333mV: V∞​≃−61.667mV. Effective electrical resistance is 33.333MΩ and the time constant is 200/30≃6.667ms. A faster response can therefore be smaller. At the original rest the shunting electric current is zero; at V=−55mV it is 20(−65+55)=−200pA, opposing the voltage excursion.
The factor of three reduction applies to this constant-electric current, constant-conductance example. A finite pulse, conductance-driven input or spatially extended dendrite need not have the same reduction. The interesting feature is conditional action: the shunt can leave a quiet neuron's voltage unchanged yet reshape its response when another input arrives. A model that represents all inhibition as a fixed outward electric current misses this behavior.
