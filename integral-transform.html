Mellin transform (M)

Words: 1k Articles: 11
Integral transform OurBigBook.com
The Mellin transform is an integral transform suited to changes of scale: replacing f(x) by f(ax) multiplies its transform by a−s for a>0. For a locally integrable function f:(0,∞)→C, define
Mf(s)=∫0∞​xs−1f(x)dx,s=σ+iτ,
(3)
where the improper integral converges. The complex number xs−1=exp((s−1)logx) uses the real natural logarithm because x>0. Its absolute value is xσ−1, so absolute convergence depends on the real part σ rather than the imaginary part τ. The Mellin fundamental strip records the open range of such real parts.[ref]
A familiar example is the exponential function f(x)=e−x:
M{e−x}(s)=Γ(s),Res>0.
(4)
This is exactly the Gamma integral. Near zero e−x tends to one, so absolute convergence requires σ>0; at infinity xre−x→0 for every real r, so there is no upper boundary. The Gamma function has a meromorphic continuation with simple poles at 0,−1,−2,…, but a finite continued value elsewhere in σ≤0 does not make the defining improper integral converge.[ref]
The Mellin transform of an interval indicator gives an elementary example with absolute convergence for every complex number s. The Mellin transform in logarithmic coordinates explains why changes of scale become translations, connecting the scaling property of the Mellin transform to the Fourier transform.

Fundamental strip of a Mellin transform (α<Res<β)

Words: 302
Mellin transform OurBigBook.com
The fundamental strip of a Mellin transform is the interior of the set of real parts σ for which
∫0∞​xσ−1∣f(x)∣dx<∞.
(7)
Viewed in the complex plane, it is a vertical strip α<Res<β; either bound may be infinite, and the interior may be empty. Both endpoints of the improper integral must be checked. Convergence on a boundary line, whether absolute convergence or conditional convergence, requires a separate test. The Mellin transform is holomorphic throughout a nonempty fundamental strip.[ref]
A practical sufficient test uses Big O notation: if f(x)=O(xp) near zero and f(x)=O(x−q) near infinity, with real p,q, then comparison with elementary improper integrals gives
∫01​xσ+p−1dx<∞ ⟺ σ>−p,∫1∞​xσ−q−1dx<∞ ⟺ σ<q.
(8)
Thus −p<σ<q guarantees absolute convergence. These upper bounds on ∣f∣ need not locate the maximal Mellin fundamental strip; faster actual decay can widen it. The strip property itself follows by bounding the weight at any intermediate σ by the sum of two weights already known to be integrable. On a closed narrower strip, neighboring integrable weights also dominate the natural logarithm factors introduced by differentiation under the integral sign, making the transform a holomorphic function.
An exact two-endpoint example is
f(x)={x2,x−3,​0<x≤1,x>1,​Mf(s)=s+21​+3−s1​,−2<Res<3.
(9)
Here both bounds are sharp: at σ=−2 the zero-end absolute convergence test contains ∫01​dx/x, and at σ=3 the infinity-end test contains ∫1∞​dx/x. The displayed rational function continues meromorphically outside the Mellin fundamental strip and has simple poles at −2 and 3. Its other finite continued values outside the strip are values of a meromorphic continuation, not of a convergent defining integral. More general endpoint information leads to meromorphic continuation of a Mellin transform from an asymptotic expansion.

Laplace transform (L)

Words: 2k Articles: 22
Integral transform OurBigBook.com
The unilateral Laplace transform of a function f:[0,∞)→C is the integral transform
F(s)=L{f}(s)=∫0∞​e−stf(t)dt,s=σ+iω∈C.
(17)
Here s is a complex number, σ is its real part, and i is the imaginary unit. The exponential function factor e−σt weights the history, while e−iωt probes its oscillations. The integral starts at zero, so it uses only the history from that starting time onward. This section concerns ordinary functions; their value at the single endpoint does not change the integral. See MIT 18.03SC, definition and elementary examples.
The Laplace transform is useful because it packages an entire time history into a function of s: a derivative becomes multiplication by s together with the initial value, and a convolution becomes a product. Thus a linear differential equation can become an algebraic equation that already remembers its initial value problem. The worked example below shows both descriptions of the same response.
If f is a piecewise continuous function and has exponential order a, then its defining integral is absolutely convergent for Res>a. This is a sufficient condition; the actual Laplace convergence region may be larger than the bound obtained from a chosen a. [ref].
The bound has a short explanation. For sufficiently large t, ∣f(t)∣≤Meat, so ∣e−stf(t)∣≤Me−(σ−a)t, whose tail integral is finite for σ>a. The finite initial interval is covered by piecewise continuity.
Three calculations make the convergence condition concrete. For the input f(t)=1, evaluate the finite-history integral first:
∫0T​e−stdt=s1−e−sT​(s=0),L{1}(s)=s1​(Res>0).
(18)
At s=0 the finite-history integral is T. For the exponential function input f(t)=e−2t, the same calculation replaces s by s+2, giving
L{e−2t}(s)=s+21​,Res>−2.
(19)
In particular, s=−1 is allowed for this decaying input: its weighted integral is ∫0∞​e−tdt=1. For the sine input, Euler's formula gives sint=(eit−e−it)/(2i); linearity then yields
L{sint}(s)=2i1​(s−i1​−s+i1​)=s2+11​,Res>0.
(20)
The Laplace convergence region is part of the answer. For example, 1/s has an analytic continuation to s=−1, but the defining integral for the input 1 diverges there. The sine calculation is also developed in MIT 18.03SC, further elementary transforms.
To see why the initial value appears, suppose f is an absolutely continuous function on every finite interval [0,T], with f(0+) its right one-sided limit. At the chosen s, assume e−stf(t) and e−stf′(t) are Lebesgue integrable functions and e−sTf(T)→0. The Laplace transform of a derivative is then
L{f′}(s)=sF(s)−f(0+).
(21)
For example, exponential order bounds on both f and f′ guarantee these conditions whenever Res exceeds both growth bounds. The initial value is the lower-end boundary contribution of integration by parts.
Consider the initial value problem
y′(t)+2y(t)=1,y(0)=0,t≥0.
(22)
Write Y=L{y}. The Laplace transform of a derivative and linearity give sY−0+2Y=1/s for Res>0. A partial fraction decomposition therefore gives
Y(s)=s(s+2)1​=21​(s1​−s+21​),y(t)=21−e−2t​.
(23)
We recognize the two elementary Laplace transforms already computed. Directly verify the result: its derivative is y′(t)=e−2t, so
y′(t)+2y(t)−1=e−2t+1−e−2t−1=0,y(0)=21−1​=0.
(24)
This verifies both the ordinary differential equation and the initial value, and shows that the resulting bounded function has the Laplace transform used above. The response rises from zero toward 1/2: at that value the input 1 balances the term 2y. The general method is described in MIT 18.03SC, solving initial value problems.
The same solution is a convolution. For functions defined on [0,∞), the unilateral convolution is
(f∗g)(t)=∫0t​f(τ)g(t−τ)dτ,t≥0.
(25)
The main convolution article integrates over the whole real line; applying a causal extension by zero to both functions makes its formula identical to this one. Nonzero contributions require both τ≥0 and t−τ≥0, hence 0≤τ≤t. If e−σtf(t) and e−σtg(t) are Lebesgue integrable functions on [0,∞), the convolution theorem for Laplace transforms states
L{f∗g}(s)=F(s)G(s),Res=σ.
(26)
For piecewise continuous functions of exponential order a and b, it holds throughout Res>max(a,b). The weighted absolute double integral factors into the two finite weighted absolute integrals; Fubini's theorem therefore permits exchanging the order, and setting u=t−τ separates the product. [ref].
For the linear time-invariant system y′+2y=f starting at zero, the convolution kernel is its impulse response h(t)=e−2t for t≥0, extended by zero for t<0. With the Heaviside step function input, f(t)=1 for t≥0, the accumulated response is
y(t)=(h∗f)(t)=∫0t​e−2(t−τ)dτ=21−e−2t​.
(27)
Thus Y(s)=(1/(s+2))(1/s) on Res>0 is both the algebraic solution and the Laplace transform of a finite-history convolution. Each past input contributes an exponential function that decays with its age t−τ, which makes the two descriptions easy to connect.
Finally, compare the Fourier transform convention g​(ω)=∫R​g(t)e−iωtdt. If f0​ is the causal extension by zero of f, define gσ​(t)=e−σtf0​(t). Whenever gσ​ is a Lebesgue integrable function,
F(σ+iω)=gσ​​(ω).
(28)
A vertical slice of the Laplace transform is therefore the Fourier transform of the zero-extended, weighted input. Positive σ damps its positive-time history. A finite window function instead gives gσ,T​(t)=e−σtf0​(t)1[0,T]​(t) and gσ,T​​(ω)=∫0T​e−(σ+iω)tf(t)dt; this approaches F(σ+iω) in the Laplace convergence region. Setting σ=0 directly requires an integrable zero-extended input: the input 1 fails that condition, even though its Laplace transform exists for every σ>0.
The history of the Laplace transform spans several stages. Schiff's historical summary notes that Laplace's 1812 treatise acknowledged earlier work on integral transforms and describes later development of the modern Laplace transform method. [ref].

A bounded function on [0,∞) has a Laplace transform satisfying the displayed bound, by integration of ∣y(t)e−pt∣. Consequently its Laplace transform cannot have a pole of order two or higher at p=0. This can eliminate the arbitrary constant introduced when multiplication by t turns an ordinary differential equation into a first-order differential equation for its Laplace transform.
For example, transforming ty′′+(t−1)y′−y=0 with y(0)=1 gives p(p+1)Y′+(3p+2)Y=2. An integrating factor p2(p+1) yields

The bound forces C=0, selecting y=e−t. Uniqueness of the Laplace transform

Words: 49
Laplace transform OurBigBook.com
If two piecewise continuous functions of exponential order on [0,∞) have the same unilateral Laplace transform for every sufficiently large positive real parameter, they agree at every common point of continuity and agree almost everywhere. For continuous functions, the equality holds everywhere. Values at an isolated discontinuity cannot be recovered from an ordinary integral transform.
