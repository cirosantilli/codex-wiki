Diagonalizable matrix (A=PDP−1)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra Operator theory Eigenvalue
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 18
A finite-dimensional linear operator is diagonalizable over a specified field when it has an eigenbasis. Equivalently, its matrix A admits
A=PDP−1,D=diag(λ1​,…,λn​),
(70)
where P is an invertible matrix whose columns are eigenvectors, and D is a diagonal matrix of the corresponding eigenvalues. This change of basis reveals a linear map as independent one-dimensional scalings. The matrix may look coupled in the original coordinates, but each coordinate in the eigenbasis evolves on its own.
For the symmetric matrix A=(21​12​) computed in eigenvector, take
P=(11​1−1​),P−1=21​(11​1−1​),D=(30​01​).
(71)
Then AP=PD, hence P−1AP=D. The orthonormal basis version is Q=P/2​, an orthogonal matrix satisfying QTAQ=D. Every real symmetric matrix has an orthonormal eigenbasis by the spectral theorem; a general diagonalizable matrix need not have orthogonal eigenvectors.
The immediate payoff is repeated application: adjacent factors P−1P cancel, giving An=PDnP−1. In this example,
An=21​(3n+13n−1​3n−13n+1​),An(10​)=21​(3n(11​)+(1−1​)).
(72)
The component in E3​ grows while the component in E1​ stays fixed. After division by its Euclidean norm, this particular sequence approaches (1,1)T/2​. This is the mechanism behind the power method; an initial vector entirely in E1​ would stay there and never find the dominant eigenvalue 3.
Two different obstructions deserve comparison. The planar rotation R in eigenvector is diagonalizable over C but not over R, because its eigenvalues are i and −i. By contrast, the shear mapping
S=(10​11​),(S−I)(xy​)=(y0​)
(73)
has only λ=1, of algebraic multiplicity two, and E1​=span{(1,0)T}, of geometric multiplicity one. There is no second independent eigenvector, even over C. This is a defective matrix, already a two-dimensional Jordan block. Its powers are
Sn=(10​n1​),Sn(01​)=(n1​).
(74)
Thus even though both eigenvalues are one, repeated application can produce growth. Eigenvalues alone miss the coupling recorded by a Jordan block. A repeated eigenvalue is harmless when it has enough independent eigenvectors, as I demonstrates. More generally, a matrix is diagonalizable exactly when its characteristic polynomial splits over the chosen field and every eigenvalue has equal geometric multiplicity and algebraic multiplicity. The proof outline is to join bases of the distinct eigenspaces; their direct sum fills the space exactly under these conditions.
A physical motivation is the separation of normal modes. Consider two equal masses m between three identical springs of stiffness k, with the outer ends fixed. By Hooke's law and Newton's second law, the displacement vector q=(q1​,q2​)T obeys
mq¨​=−kKq,K=(2−1​−12​).
(75)
The eigenvectors (1,1)T and (1,−1)T have eigenvalues 1 and 3: the masses move together in the first normal mode and oppositely in the second. Writing q=a(t)(1,1)T+b(t)(1,−1)T separates the coupled equations into two harmonic oscillators,
a¨+mk​a=0,b¨+m3k​b=0.
(76)
Their angular frequencies are k/m​ and 3k/m​; the eigenvalues give squared angular frequencies after multiplication by k/m. This is the beauty of diagonalization: finding the right directions turns interacting motions into independent patterns. For a physical treatment, see Yen-Jie Lee's "Coupled Oscillators, Normal Modes" (MIT 8.03SC, 2016), and the local article normal modes of two equal masses between three springs.[ref]
