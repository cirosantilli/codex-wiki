Complex force (Fx​−iFy​)

Words: 47
Force OurBigBook.com
In planar potential flow, encode a physical force (Fx​,Fy​) by Fx​−iFy​, using the same negative-imaginary-component convention as the complex velocity u−iv. The Blasius force theorem then expresses the force per unit span as a single contour integral. The encoding is a convention: the physical vertical component is the negative imaginary part of this complex number.

Force field (physics, F(x,t))

Words: 56
Force OurBigBook.com
A force field specifies a force vector as a function of position and, when necessary, time, for a specified test body. It is a vector field. A time-independent conservative vector field has F=−∇V, where V is the body's potential energy; its work along a path equals the decrease in V. Position-dependent forces can have nonzero curl, so a scalar potential need not exist.

Thrust

Words: 70
Force OurBigBook.com
A propulsive force exerted on a body by an engine or another momentum-transfer mechanism. In a vehicle moving through a fluid, its direction need not coincide with the vehicle's actual velocity, and a transverse component can steer the motion. The component along the velocity contributes mechanical power T⋅v; a purely transverse component changes direction without doing instantaneous work. Rocket thrust is one particular mechanism and includes momentum carried by expelled material.

Entropic force (FS​=T(∂S/∂x)T​)

Words: 91
Force OurBigBook.com
An entropic force is the part of a generalized force arising from the dependence of entropy on a coordinate. At fixed temperature, the system's force is −∂A/∂x=−∂U/∂x+T∂S/∂x, where A=U−TS is the Helmholtz free energy. If the internal energy is independent of the coordinate, the force is entirely entropic. For an ideal chain whose configurations become less numerous on extension, it acts toward shorter extension. The external tension holding the chain extended has the opposite sign. Thus the force depends on the statistical number of available configurations, even when the energy of each configuration is unchanged.

Apparent weight (N=m(g+a))

Words: 215 Articles: 1
Weight (physics) OurBigBook.com
Apparent weight measures support rather than the gravitational force itself. In an ideal scale model, it is the magnitude N of the normal force exerted on the body. A scale calibrated to display mass divides that support force by its calibration value of g.
Take upward as positive in a constant downward gravitational acceleration of magnitude g. If a body stays in contact with a horizontal scale and accelerates upward at a, Newton's second law gives N−mg=ma, hence N=m(g+a). At rest, N=mg. In common free fall, a=−g, so N=0 despite the nonzero gravitational weight mg. If the formula would require N<0, ordinary supporting contact cannot realize it: the body loses contact instead.

Weightlessness

Words: 106
Apparent weight OurBigBook.com
Weightlessness is the absence of supporting or constraining forces that would give a body an apparent weight. It does not require zero gravitational acceleration. A freely falling cabin and a small freely falling object inside share the same acceleration in a uniform field, so neither needs to support the other.
The circular orbit as continuous free fall gives a sustained example. Real spacecraft have small residual relative accelerations from tidal forces, atmospheric drag force and onboard disturbances; the ideal common-fall explanation does not assert that every point in an extended spacecraft experiences exactly the same Newtonian gravitational field. [ref]

Restoring force (F(x)=−kx)

Force OurBigBook.com
A restoring force acts toward an equilibrium when a body is displaced from it. In one dimension, taking displacement x=0 at equilibrium, this means xF(x)<0 for sufficiently small nonzero x. An ideal linear spring has F(x)=−kx with positive spring constant k, producing simple harmonic motion for a constant mass without damping. More general restoring forces need not be linear.

Static friction (∣fs​∣≤μs​N)

Words: 2k Articles: 6
Force OurBigBook.com
Static friction is the tangential contact force that maintains zero contact slip. “Static” describes the contact state: both surfaces can move together in the chosen inertial frame. In the ideal dry-contact model, the actual static friction adjusts to the equations of motion, subject to a bound, rather than always taking its maximum value.[ref]
∣fs​∣≤μs​N,N≥0,μs​≥0.
(2)
Here N is the magnitude of the normal force and μs​ the coefficient of static friction. Equality means limiting static friction; it is a threshold, not an instruction to replace every unknown contact force by μs​N.
For a stipulated example, place a nonrotating 2kg block initially at rest on a stationary horizontal surface. Take gravitational acceleration g=9.8m/s2 and coefficient of static friction μs​=0.40, with no other vertical force. The vertical force balance gives N=mg=19.6N, so the largest allowed static friction magnitude is 7.84N. Choose right as positive and apply a horizontal force F. Remaining at rest requires Newton's second law to give
F+fs​=0,fs​=−F,∣F∣≤7.84N.
(3)
At F=2N, the static friction is −2N; at F=6N, it is −6N. Both states have zero acceleration. At F=10N, balancing would require fs​=−10N, outside the bound, so this initially resting block cannot remain at rest. Its subsequent acceleration requires a kinetic friction law; setting fs​=−7.84N after contact slip begins would still use the wrong contact state. At exactly F=7.84N, the ideal inequality still permits rest at the threshold. These are model calculations, not measured material properties.
The toy model used in kinetic friction makes the contact-state distinction explicit: m=1kg, N=10N, μs​=0.5 and coefficient of kinetic friction μk​=0.3. On stationary ground, an initially resting box pushed right with 4N has fs​=−4N. At 6N it cannot remain at rest; once it slides right, fk​=−3N and a=3m/s2. At 5N, rest is allowed with fs​=−5N, whereas an already right-sliding box has fk​=−3N and a=2m/s2. The applied force alone does not specify the friction branch: contact slip and motion history matter.
For a flat contact with two tangential directions, the bound applies to a vector norm, not separately to each component:
∥fs​∥≤μs​N,fs,x2​+fs,y2​≤(μs​N)2.
(4)
For the stipulated 7.84N bound, balancing horizontal components 4N and 4N requires a force magnitude 32​N≃5.657N, which is allowed. Components 6N and 6N require 72​N≃8.485N, which is forbidden even though each component separately is below 7.84N. This circular bound assumes the same coefficient of static friction in every tangential direction. Near impending contact slip, the limiting static friction opposes the relative contact motion that would otherwise begin. Once actual contact slip exists, kinetic friction instead opposes its tangential relative velocity, which need not have the direction of the body's center of mass motion.
The normal force must also come from the equations of motion. On horizontal ground, an upward pull of magnitude P at angle α above horizontal gives, for a block remaining at rest with no other forces,
N=mg−Psinα,fs​=−Pcosα,∣Pcosα∣≤μs​(mg−Psinα).
(5)
The sine component unloads the contact and the cosine component demands tangential support. These equations require N>0; if the supporting contact opens, this dry-contact model cannot supply a negative normal force. For the stipulated 2kg block and α=30∘, the positive threshold is P=μs​mg/(cosα+μs​sinα)≃7.3544N, with N≃15.9228N and ∣fs​∣≃6.3691N. A downward pull increases N instead. An inclined plane gives another reason why N is not always mg.
To solve a new problem, impose zero contact slip, find the required static friction and normal force from the equations of motion, then check the bound. A demand inside it permits that contact state; a demand outside it rules the state out. For an ideal driven tire undergoing rolling without slipping on stationary ground, a forward static friction can accelerate the vehicle. Its role is to prevent contact slip, rather than to oppose all motion of the whole body.
Nor does the word “static” by itself make friction do zero work. The contact power delivered to a body is Ps​=fs​⋅vc​, a dot product with its material contact-point velocity in the chosen inertial frame. At an ideal contact undergoing rolling without slipping on stationary ground, vc​=0, so this power is zero. For a rigid body, adding the translational and rotational contributions gives fs​⋅VCM​+τCM​⋅ω=fs​⋅vc​, where τCM​ is the contact torque about the center of mass and ω its angular velocity. Counting only the first term omits the rotational contribution.
At a moving contact with zero contact slip, the two material contact points share a velocity vc​ that can be nonzero. For instance, a nonrotating block moving with an accelerating belt receives positive contact power fs​U when the belt's rightward velocity U and rightward static friction fs​ are positive. The belt receives −fs​U by Newton's third law. These contact contributions sum to zero in the ideal no-slip model, while the block's kinetic energy can increase. Zero contact slip and zero work on each body are different conditions.

Coefficient of static friction (μs​)

Words: 857 Articles: 2
Static friction OurBigBook.com
The coefficient of static friction is the nonnegative dimensionless quantity μs​=∣fs​∣max​/N, with N>0, in the ideal dry-contact model. It characterizes the pair of surfaces under specified conditions. It describes the largest supported tangential force, whereas the coefficient of kinetic friction describes sliding. It is an empirical approximation, not a universal material constant.[ref]
In the horizontal 2kg example, an observed state balancing a 2N pull would only establish μs​≥2/19.6≃0.10204 under the model assumptions. It would not establish μs​=0.10204: the actual static friction is below its limit. Identifying the coefficient needs an onset measurement, such as inclined-plane measurement of static friction, or the starting peak in a suitably controlled horizontal pull. The sustained sliding part of constant-speed measurement of kinetic friction measures a different coefficient.
In a simple grain model, the existing name granular friction coefficient represents resistance to downstream motion. That model excludes lift and detailed contact torques. Its coefficient should not automatically be identified with a bulk pile's angle of repose: individual contact geometry and collective rearrangement can matter.

Static friction on an inclined plane (tanθc​=μs​)

Words: 695 Articles: 1
Coefficient of static friction OurBigBook.com
An inclined plane tilted slowly from horizontal turns a coefficient of static friction into a critical angle. Assume a nonrotating block on a stationary, rigid, dry surface, with gravitational weight and the contact forces as its only forces. Take 0≤θ<π/2 to be the angle between the uphill surface direction and horizontal. Let et​ be a unit vector pointing uphill and en​ a perpendicular unit vector pointing outward from the surface. Resolving the gravitational weight gives
W=−mgsinθet​−mgcosθen​.
(6)
The angle between the downward vertical and the inward normal −en​ is also θ. Thus the inward component uses cosine and the downhill component uses sine. These are components of one force, not two additional forces.
Before downhill contact slip, zero acceleration and Newton's second law require
fs​=mgsinθ,N=mgcosθ.
(7)
The required static friction points uphill. Substituting into the bound and dividing by the positive mgcosθ gives
mgsinθ≤μs​mgcosθ⟺tanθ≤μs​.
(8)
The tangent function increases on this range of angles, so the last admissible resting angle is
θc​=arctanμs​,tanθc​=μs​.
(9)
The inverse tangent takes a dimensionless quantity to an angle. At equality the ideal model still permits a limiting resting state; increasing θ further excludes it. At a smaller angle, fs​/N=tanθ is the demand ratio, not the coefficient of static friction.[ref]
For the stipulated m=2kg, g=9.8m/s2 and μs​=0.40, the critical angle is approximately 21.8014∘. At θ=15∘, N≃18.932N and fs​≃5.073N, comfortably below the bound μs​N≃7.573N. At θ=25∘, maintaining rest would require fs​≃8.283N, but the bound is only 7.105N: rest is impossible. At θc​, N≃18.198N and fs​≃7.279N. Notice that the limiting tangential force here is smaller than the horizontal 7.84N limit because tilting reduces the normal force.
Cancellation of mass makes this relation useful: the ideal critical angle tests a contact coefficient without needing a calibrated force gauge. Doubling the block's mass doubles both demanded static friction and its bound, leaving θc​ unchanged if μs​ is unchanged. This is a testable model prediction. It need not survive a change in surface condition, a direction-dependent coefficient, appreciable deformation, or vibration.
The derivation also assumes continued contact and that sliding occurs before tipping or rolling without slipping. The resultant contact force must act within the supporting footprint so that the block can satisfy torque balance; a tall block may tip first. No extra tension, imposed tangential pull, or lift is included. In a slowly raised apparatus, quasistatic process means that motion of the board contributes negligible inertial forces while locating the threshold. Once downhill contact slip begins, a constant-velocity sliding angle would concern kinetic friction, not the onset of static friction.

Empirical testing of Newton's laws of motion

Words: 67
Newton's laws of motion OurBigBook.com
Testing requires independently constrained force laws, mass assignments, initial conditions and a common family of inertial frames. Defining the net force separately for every observation by F=ma cannot test Newton's second law. With interaction parameters fixed, the same equation predicts new trajectories. Likewise, zero net force implies uniform motion within the second-law framework, but the existence and empirical identification of its inertial frames are extra content. Pairwise Newton's third law adds a constraint not supplied by the individual second-law equations.

Newton's first law (Fnet​=0⟹v=v0​)

Words: 1k Articles: 3
Newton's laws of motion OurBigBook.com
In an inertial frame, a body with zero net external force remains at rest or moves with constant velocity. For a point mass of constant positive mass, write the net external force as the vector sum of all external forces on that body:
Fnet​=∑i​Fi​=0⟹a=dtdv​=0,v(t)=v0​,r(t)=r0​+v0​(t−t0​).
(10)
Here r0​ and v0​ are the position and velocity at time t0​. The zero net external force must hold throughout the interval, not merely at one instant. Both the magnitude and direction of the velocity stay fixed: constant speed along a curved trajectory still involves acceleration. Rest is the case v0​=0. For an extended closed system, the analogous statement concerns its center of mass; zero net external force need not prevent its parts from moving relative to one another.
The short mathematical argument is to use Newton's second law with m>0, then take the integral of dv/dt=0 and of dr/dt=v0​. This gives the two constants fixed by the initial position and velocity. The physical content also includes the existence of inertial frames in which these relations apply; an arbitrary accelerating reference frame need not show constant velocity for a body with zero actual net external force.
The useful question is therefore which forces change the motion, rather than which force keeps it going. A free-body diagram may show several nonzero forces whose vector sum is zero. Equal opposing forces on the same body can balance; a third-law interaction pair acts on different bodies and cannot be cancelled within that one body's diagram.
In 1687, Isaac Newton stated Law I in Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica: a body's rest or uniform straight motion persists unless impressed forces compel a change. The following paragraph identifies air resistance and gravity as influences on the trajectory of a thrown body. The vector equations above are a modern expression of the law, not a quotation of his notation.[ref]

Balanced forces and constant velocity (6N−6N=0)

Newton's first law OurBigBook.com
Consider a stipulated ideal cart modeled as a point mass of mass 2kg on a horizontal track in a laboratory inertial frame. Its initial position is x(0)=0 and its initial velocity is vx​(0)=1.5m/s to the right. Independent devices exert horizontal forces +6N and −6N on this same cart. Its upward support force balances its downward gravitational weight; no other forces contribute. Rotation and deformation are excluded from this model.
Fnet,x​=6−6=0N,ax​=2kg0N​=0m/s2,vx​(t)=1.5m/s,x(t)=(1.5m/s)t.
(11)
After 4s, the cart is at 6m, still moving right at 1.5m/s. Starting the same model at rest instead would leave it at rest. Force balance determines the zero acceleration, while the initial velocity determines whether the cart moves.

Newton's third law (FA on B​=−FB on A​)

Words: 1k Articles: 4
Newton's laws of motion OurBigBook.com
In the pair-interaction model of Newtonian mechanics, two bodies exert equal and opposite forces on each other:
FA on B​=−FB on A​.
(15)
Both vectors are expressed in the same inertial frame at the same time. The subscripts name the agent and the recipient: A acts on B, and B acts on A. These are forces on different bodies, so they cannot cancel in the net force on either body. Newton's second law still uses all the forces on the particular body whose acceleration is sought. Identifying this third-law interaction pair does not require the bodies to be isolated: additional external forces can act at the same time.
The distinction makes Newton's third law useful both for drawing a free-body diagram and for deriving momentum conservation. A body-by-body calculation retains one member of each third-law interaction pair; a calculation for a collection containing both bodies cancels that pair in the total momentum balance. Equal interaction forces consequently coexist with nonzero, generally unequal accelerations.
Isaac Newton stated the equality and opposition of mutual actions in Law III of his 1687 Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica. The explanation distinguishes equal opposite changes of momentum from changes of velocity, which are inversely proportional to the bodies' masses when other influences are absent. Corollary III then explains why mutual interactions preserve the signed total momentum. The equations here are modern notation for that distinction. [ref]
The instantaneous body-pair equation has a scope: use it for the mechanical interactions assumed by this Newtonian mechanics model. In electromagnetism, bodies can exchange momentum with an electromagnetic field; their instantaneous mutual forces need not sum to zero. Momentum conservation then accounts for electromagnetic field momentum as well as matter, and for momentum crossing the chosen boundary. This complication occurs in classical electromagnetism, without invoking quantum mechanics.[ref]
