Physics investigates matter, motion, and interactions by building mathematical models and testing their consequences against observations. A physical law supplies a relation; a model specifies the objects, conditions, and approximations used to apply it. Classical mechanics, electromagnetism, thermodynamics, and quantum mechanics organize different aspects of these investigations. Their usefulness comes from turning measurements into predictions that can be checked and refined.
Consider a simple pendulum: how does its oscillation period change when its length changes? For small amplitude, the ideal model gives T0​=2πℓ/g​, where g is gravitational acceleration. Taking g=9.81m/s2 as the model parameter, a one-metre pendulum has predicted period 2.0061s. Increasing its length to four metres gives 4.0121s: quadrupling the length doubles the period. The point mass cancels from the equation, so changing it alone does not change this model's answer.
The prediction identifies a mechanism and a way to challenge it. The small-angle approximation replaces sinθ by θ; the full ideal equation predicts a longer period at large amplitude. At 60∘, its period is about 1.07318T0​, already a 7.3% change. Measurements at such amplitudes can reveal the limit of the approximation, while appreciable drag requires a damped pendulum. These distinctions are developed in simple pendulum and dimensional analysis. The small-amplitude model is also derived in Ling, Sanny and Moebs, University Physics, pendulums.
Several different experiments can become one calculation. Nondimensionalization rewrites the pendulum equation using τ=tg/ℓ​, leaving d2θ/dτ2+sinθ=0. All lengths share that dimensionless equation; the amplitude remains a parameter. Similarly, conservation laws and symmetry reveal features that many models share. This is one source of the subject's beauty: a compact relation can organize a large range of phenomena.
The connection to evidence remains essential when a model is elegant. In his introductory lectures, Richard Feynman emphasizes testing proposed relations by experiment and using idealizations whose accuracy depends on the conditions. [ref] A discrepancy can motivate a better measurement, a better approximation, or a different physical model; the mathematical calculation and the empirical assessment are both part of the explanation.
