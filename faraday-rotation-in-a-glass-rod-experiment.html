Faraday rotation in a glass-rod experiment

 ... Branches of physics Electromagnetism Electromagnetic wave Plane electromagnetic wave Polarization of an electromagnetic wave Faraday effect
OurBigBook.com Words: 325
A William & Mary teaching experiment places a 10cm glass rod inside a solenoid. Align a polarized helium-neon laser through the rod's centre onto a photodiode after a rotatable polarimeter analyzer. First scan the analyzer from maximum to minimum transmission in 5∘ steps and record detector voltage, establishing the angular response. A function generator and electronic amplifier then drive sinusoidal electric current through the solenoid; a multimeter measures the electric current and an oscilloscope reads the photodiode. Trigger the oscilloscope from the generator, remove the large constant detector component by AC coupling, and average repeated traces. Convert the small synchronized voltage modulation to rotation using the calibrated angular slope. The manual fits rotation against magnetic flux density, using its stated coil calibration and converting measured root mean square current to peak current. This is a specified laboratory procedure, rather than measured results reproduced here.[ref]
To test the inference, add matched zero-electric current readings before and after each reversal, hold the analyzer fixed during paired readings, and compare equal positive and negative electric currents. A blocked beam tests electrical pickup in the detector channel. These are proposed controls. Joule heating scales as current squared, so reversing electric current at equal magnitude helps separate an odd rotation from even heating effects; it does not remove drift between readings. Record temperature and allow repeatable settling. Changes in transmission, mechanical motion, stressed-glass birefringence and detector pickup can imitate a signal. Synchronized averaging suppresses unsynchronized noise, but cannot reject an artifact synchronized to the drive.
Quantitative extraction of the Verdet constant also needs the integral of magnetic flux density through the rod: a finite solenoid can have a nonuniform axial magnetic field. Use its calibrated path integral, or justify a uniform-field approximation; do not silently multiply a central field by the whole rod length. A signed reversal or phase-sensitive readout tests the part odd in applied magnetic field. Its agreement with the simple rotation law supports the model under these controls, rather than proving that every change of transmitted light intensity is Faraday rotation.
