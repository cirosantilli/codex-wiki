X-ray spectral attenuation operator ((Ax​f)(E)=e−μ(E)xf(E))

For a fixed homogeneous absorber in a narrow-beam geometry, define the X-ray spectral attenuation operator on L1 space over a bounded positive energy interval. A physical input f(E)≥0 is an integrable function giving incident photon rate density. Let its linear attenuation coefficient μ(E)≥0 be a measurable function, with x≥0. The transmitted uncollided density is
(Ax​f)(E)=e−μ(E)xf(E),S(x)=∫w(E)(Ax​f)(E)dE.
(18)
Here w(E)≥0 is a fixed bounded detector response described by a measurable function; background has been subtracted. This is a positive linear operator on L1 space. Since 0≤e−μ(E)x≤1, it cannot increase the integrated absolute incident rate. Multiplication of exponentials proves Ax+y​=Ax​Ay​ for successive layers of the same material. Ignore secondary radiation entering the detector, saturation and changes to the source. Discrete spectral lines use the finite-sum version below instead of an ordinary rate density.

(19)
The selected 8.5keV line's fraction rises from 4/5=0.800 to 0.929974, but its absolute rate falls by about 33.0%. Filtering improves purity at the cost of signal. It multiplies existing components and cannot create a new emitted spectral line.
With just one integrated reading, S=Nα​+Nβ​, the two unknown line rates are underdetermined. If the source is unchanged between an empty-path reading and a filtered reading, the ideal inverse problem becomes
(S(0)S(2mm)​)=(1e−0.4​1e−1.6​)(Nα​Nβ​​)=(52.88318​).
(20)
The determinant e−1.6−e−0.4≃−0.468424 is nonzero, so these two ideal readings determine the two stipulated line rates uniquely. Identical coefficients would give zero determinant and destroy that separation. This small linear algebra example explains why absorber contrast supplies information, while also showing its limit: unknown scattered components or unknown detector weights add unknowns that two readings cannot determine.
