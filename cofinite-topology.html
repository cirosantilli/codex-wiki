Cofinite topology (τcof​={∅}∪{X∖F:F⊆X finite})

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Geometry and topology Topology
OurBigBook.com Words: 757 Articles: 3
The cofinite topology on a set X declares the empty set and all cofinite subsets to be open sets: a nonempty open set may exclude only a finite set of points. There is no assumed metric. On an infinite set, this gives a particularly small collection of open sets while still making every singleton a closed set. On a finite set, every subset has finite complement, so the cofinite topology is the discrete topology.[ref]
For an original example, use X=N>0​ and choose
U=X∖{2,5,8},V=X∖{1,3,7}.
(1)
Both are open sets, and their intersection is U∩V=X∖{1,2,3,5,7,8}. Thus 4,6,9,10,… are in both. Their finite exclusions cannot separate the infinite remainder. The finite intersection of any finite collection of nonempty open sets is again a nonempty open set, since its complement is a finite union of finite sets. An arbitrary nonempty union is open because its complement is contained in the finite complement of any one nonempty member.
The cofinite topology is useful for separating hypotheses that familiar metric spaces package together. An infinite cofinite topological space is a T1 space, because its singletons are closed, but is not a Hausdorff space, because two nonempty open sets cannot be disjoint sets. It is a hyperconnected space and hence a connected space. Moreover, every subset of a cofinite space is compact, and sequence convergence in the cofinite topology can have many limits. These are compatible properties, rather than contradictions: the usual uniqueness and closedness results use the Hausdorff space hypothesis.[ref]
