A 15M⊙​ star burns core hydrogen chiefly by the CNO cycle for about 107 years. It commonly evolves towards a red supergiant, though winds and mixing alter its track. Core helium burning lasts of order 106 years. Continued contraction then permits carbon, neon, oxygen and silicon burning, producing successively heavier nuclei up to an iron-group core. Neutrino cooling makes the advanced phases much shorter: carbon burning can last thousands of years, while the final burning stages fall to years or days, with substantial model dependence.
An iron-group core cannot gain energy by ordinary fusion into heavier nuclei. Electron captures, photodisintegration and loss of pressure support lead to stellar core collapse. A core-collapse supernova can eject the envelope and leave a neutron star; strong fallback or a failed explosion can instead produce a black hole. The massive-star endpoint involves core collapse, rather than the quiet carbon-oxygen white-dwarf cooling of the two lower-mass examples.
