Write Ω=−aGM⊙2​/R⊙​ with a structure factor a of order unity. Then the thermal radiation timescale is
tth​=L⊙​U​=2a​R⊙​L⊙​GM⊙2​​∼107–3×107yr.​
(216)
For a uniform sphere a=3/5, this is about 9.4×106 years. The conventional Kelvin-Helmholtz cooling time GM⊙2​/(R⊙​L⊙​) is about 3.1×107 years; a centrally concentrated solar model changes the coefficient. Both estimates are far shorter than the nuclear lifetime.
Ionization and dissociation can store compression energy without the full temperature rise of a monatomic gas, lowering the effective adiabatic response in partially ionized outer layers. Radiation pressure tends towards an adiabatic exponent 4/3. These effects, changing composition and degeneracy make the effective exponent position dependent. The local dynamical exponent is Γ1​=(∂logP/∂logρ)s​; it equals CP​/CV​ in the simple fixed-composition ideal-gas model, but need not do so for an ionizing mixture.
If an envelope has an effective exponent at or below 4/3, its pressure response is soft and sufficiently extensive such regions can promote radial pulsation, envelope instability or mass loss. In the ideal homologous global model, γ=4/3 is marginal and γ<4/3 is unstable to contraction or expansion. A low value confined to a thin outer layer does not by itself prove collapse of the entire star: global stability depends on the pressure-weighted structure. Its location and mass extent matter.
