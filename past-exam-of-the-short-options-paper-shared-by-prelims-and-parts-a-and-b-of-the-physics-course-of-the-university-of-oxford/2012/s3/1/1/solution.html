Black-body radiation and the photoelectric effect exposed failures of classical continuous-wave energy exchange. Classical equipartition predicted an unbounded short-wavelength thermal spectrum, the ultraviolet catastrophe. Quantized oscillator energies En​=nhν give the mean excitation energy hν/(ehν/(kB​T)−1) and suppress high-frequency modes, producing Planck's law. In the modern description, thermal radiation consists of photons with energy hν and Bose occupation numbers.
For the photoelectric effect, increasing intensity below a threshold frequency does not eject electrons, whereas the maximum electron kinetic energy grows with frequency above threshold. Absorption of one photon transfers hν to one electron, giving Kmax​=hν−W​ and threshold ν0​=W/h, where W is the work function. Intensity primarily changes the photon arrival rate and hence the photocurrent in the ordinary single-photon regime. Thus changing energy per quantum and changing the number of quanta have distinct consequences. Historically the thermal derivation first quantized matter's energy exchange; it should not be described as an already complete derivation of modern photon theory.
