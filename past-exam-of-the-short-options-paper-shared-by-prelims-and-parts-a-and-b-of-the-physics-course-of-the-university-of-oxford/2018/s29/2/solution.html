Ancient Martian valley networks, lake basins, deltas, rounded or transported sediments and water-altered minerals show that liquid water was once more widespread than the present cold, low-pressure surface permits. Such evidence motivates a denser early atmosphere and different greenhouse conditions. It does not uniquely establish a continuously warm global ocean: episodic warming, local hydrology and several greenhouse compositions are alternatives that must be tested.
Over time, volatile loss to space, declining interior activity and changing surface storage altered the atmosphere. Early intense solar ultraviolet radiation could drive escape of light species; ion pickup, sputtering and photochemical processes removed additional material. Reduced dynamo activity changed solar-wind interaction, while water and carbon-bearing material could also be sequestered in ice, adsorbed material or minerals. Impacts can both remove and deliver volatiles. The balance is not reducible to one process or to the simple assertion that a magnetic field perfectly retains an atmosphere.
Surface ages are estimated mainly by crater size-frequency statistics, calibrated through radiometric ages and an assumed impact history. More heavily cratered ancient highlands and younger, resurfaced volcanic plains demonstrate substantial age differences on Mars. Lunar sample calibration must be transferred cautiously; erosion, burial and secondary craters complicate counting. Martian meteorites and the pre-2018 Curiosity potassium-argon measurement add radiometric constraints, but a mineral age need not equal the age of its final sedimentary deposition.[ref]
Let Σ=Matm​ denote the printed atmospheric mass per area. An impactor's projected area intercepts a column mcol​=πRimp2​Σ. The requested model assigns energy mcol​Vimp2​/2 to atmospheric removal, and equates it to GMMars​MEsc​/RMars​. Thus
MEsc​=2GMMars​πRimp2​ΣVimp2​RMars​​.​
(176)
This uses a thin atmosphere, a uniform column, surface escape energy, complete useful coupling of that column-energy estimate, and negligible radiative or other losses. It is not a general consequence of the impactor's kinetic energy, which would contain the impactor mass instead of the column mass. A realistic shock/escape calculation needs a coupling model, impact geometry and atmospheric structure. The expression is evaluated as the stipulated approximate model.
At one bar, g≃3.71ms−2, so Σ=P/g≃2.69×104kgm−2 and the total atmospheric mass is 4πRMars2​Σ≃3.89×1018 kg. With a radius of 150 km for the stated diameter,
Matmosphere​MEsc​​=8GMMars​RMars​Rimp2​Vimp2​​≃4.38×10−3≃0.44%,​
(177)
corresponding to about 1.7×1016 kg. The pressure cancels from this fractional estimate. Confusing diameter with radius would overestimate it fourfold.
A single such impact therefore does not remove the bulk of the atmosphere in this model. Repeating an identical fractional loss would require roughly 525 such events for ninety-percent removal, before allowing for volatile delivery or geological evolution. Craters and basins show that bombardment was substantial, but their presence does not establish that the necessary net loss occurred. Heavy-isotope enrichment, including argon and hydrogen isotopes, demonstrates selective escape and constrains competing processes. MAVEN had already supplied evidence for substantial space loss by 2017.[ref] These signatures are not uniquely diagnostic of impact erosion, which can be relatively bulk-like; they should not be presented as proof of impact dominance.
