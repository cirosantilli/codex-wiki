American Big science emerged when expensive instruments and team-based research met unusually strong financial, industrial and political support. Its prehistory matters: the Second World War accelerated an existing development rather than creating large laboratories from nothing.
Ernest Lawrence's prewar cyclotron programme illustrates the instrumental pressure toward scale. Increasing accelerator size demanded magnets, power supplies, vacuum engineering, technical staff and coordinated fundraising. A laboratory could become an organization supporting shared machinery, rather than a professor and a few assistants using tabletop apparatus. Philanthropic and university support helped establish this model before sustained federal patronage expanded it.[ref]
The war then made coordinated scientific work a national priority. Radar and nuclear programmes demonstrated that physicists, engineers, industry and government could combine resources on a scale unavailable to ordinary academic departments. Wartime urgency reduced normal budget constraints and established contractual relationships, facilities and managerial experience. Nuclear weapons added a particularly powerful strategic justification for maintaining those capacities after the war, but should not be taken as the sole origin of large-scale research.
Postwar institutions converted emergency mobilization into durable support. National laboratories provided expensive shared facilities; defense agencies supported research and training; universities expanded; and national prestige became tied to scientific capability. Vannevar Bush's 1945 report argued for continued public support of basic research and scientific talent, while also defending investigator freedom. It was an influential programme for the government-university relationship, not a claim that all research should be centrally directed through enormous projects.[ref]
Cold War competition reinforced funding for accelerators, space research, computing and other large undertakings. The United States combined a large industrial economy, relatively intact postwar infrastructure and an expanded university system, making that commitment feasible at an especially high level. The dependence on federal budgets also brought secrecy, strategic priorities and vulnerability to political changes.
Big science did not make small experiments or individual theoretical work obsolete. Large facilities can answer otherwise inaccessible questions, but coordination costs, long lead times and concentration of resources can narrow the available research portfolio. The timing is best explained by the interaction of scientific instrument requirements, prewar organizational experiments, wartime mobilization and sustained postwar state patronage, rather than by an inevitable internal law that every science must simply become larger.
