Added nuclear heat initially raises pressure and causes expansion. The expansion makes gravitational energy less negative and can lower the temperature of the self-gravitating gas, reducing the temperature-sensitive fusion rate. Conversely, a small contraction can raise the core temperature and increase burning. This negative feedback explains stable main-sequence burning in an ordinary nondegenerate stellar core; a degenerate core need not respond the same way.
Before hydrogen ignition, a cloud loses energy by radiation and contracts. Its gravitational energy decreases; for the ideal-gas virial relation, roughly half the released binding energy heats the gas and half is radiated. Heating continues until nuclear reactions can balance the luminosity, explaining the Kelvin-Helmholtz phase of pre-main-sequence evolution.
The effective adiabatic response varies with ionization, molecular dissociation, radiation pressure and degeneracy. Partial ionization can absorb compression energy and lower the local first adiabatic exponent below 4/3, encouraging compressibility, convection and pulsational instability. A local outer layer with γ≤4/3 does not by itself prove global collapse: ordinary stars have ionization layers of that kind. The dynamical stability criterion concerns a suitable pressure-weighted global response and its coupling to the eigenmode; an extended star with an effectively global exponent below 4/3 can be dynamically unstable.
