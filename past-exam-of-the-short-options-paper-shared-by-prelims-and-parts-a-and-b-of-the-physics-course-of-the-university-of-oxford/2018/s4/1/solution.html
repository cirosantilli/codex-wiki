The Shockley-Queisser calculation treats a single-junction solar cell with a single band gap, in steady state, with one collected electron-hole pair per absorbed above-gap photon, an ideal absorption edge, thermalized carriers and radiative recombination as the limiting recombination process. The source spectrum, cell temperature, angular acceptance and optical concentration must be specified. Below-gap photons are transmitted; energy above the gap is lost during carrier thermalization; the cell must emit photons by detailed balance, producing a recombination current and an entropy-related voltage loss. Delivering power at finite current also puts the operating voltage below the open-circuit voltage. Nonradiative recombination, resistance and imperfect collection worsen a real cell but are not required for the ideal limit.
Three ways to change those assumptions are tandem solar cells, which divide the spectrum among gaps and reduce both transmission and thermalization losses; hot-carrier solar cells, which aims to collect excess carrier energy before thermalization; and carrier multiplication or spectral conversion, which aims to use a high-energy photon for more than one excitation or combine otherwise unusable sub-gap photons. These concepts have additional practical losses and do not automatically outperform an optimized conventional device. The usual unconcentrated single-junction optimum is approximately one third under a terrestrial solar spectrum, rather than a universal efficiency independent of illumination.
