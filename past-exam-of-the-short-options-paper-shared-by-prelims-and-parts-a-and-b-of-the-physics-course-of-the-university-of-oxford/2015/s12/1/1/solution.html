The protein primary structure is the ordered protein sequence along a polypeptide, from its N-terminus to its C-terminus. In cells, information in a DNA coding sequence is copied into messenger RNA by transcription. During translation, the ribosome reads successive three-base codons; transfer RNA molecules match these by their anticodons and supply the corresponding amino acids. The genetic code is degenerate: different codons can encode the same amino acid. A start establishes the reading frame and a stop terminates translation. Peptide bonds link the amino acids into the backbone, leaving chemically different side chains. Thus the sequence specifies the order of side-chain interactions, rather than a literal coordinate list for the folded molecule.
Protein secondary structure describes local backbone arrangements. An alpha helix is stabilized by a regular pattern of backbone hydrogen bonds, typically between the carbonyl of residue i and the amide of residue i+4. Beta sheets assemble extended strands through inter-strand hydrogen bonding, with parallel or antiparallel strand orientations. Turns and loops connect these elements; not every region belongs to a helix or sheet. The peptide bond is approximately planar, while backbone torsion angles provide conformational freedom subject to steric restrictions.
Protein tertiary structure is the complete three-dimensional organization of one chain, often arranged into protein domains. Protein quaternary structure describes the organization of multiple chains in an assembly. Soluble globular proteins often bury nonpolar side chains and expose polar ones; membrane proteins can instead expose hydrophobic surfaces to the surrounding lipid. Other stabilizing interactions include hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions, Van der Waals forces and sometimes covalent disulfide bonds. These competing contributions define active sites, binding pockets and mechanically useful structures; folded proteins are dynamic ensembles, not perfectly rigid objects.
The connection from sequence to structure is thermodynamic and kinetic. Folding usually decreases the chain's conformational entropy, but favorable interactions and changes in solvent entropy, including the hydrophobic effect, can lower the total Gibbs free energy. Under suitable conditions the native ensemble is favored over unfolded alternatives. A change in sequence can therefore change the balance of accessible conformations and alter folding or function. Reversible refolding of a denatured protein in an appropriate environment demonstrates that the sequence can contain the information needed for its native structure.[ref]
The environment is part of the specification: temperature, pH, ionic conditions, ligands and post-translational modifications matter. Molecular chaperones can prevent aggregation and help chains escape kinetic traps without providing a residue-by-residue structural template. Some sequences are intrinsically disordered proteins or disordered regions, and some require partners for a stable fold. Thus primary structure strongly constrains structure, but does not guarantee one unique rigid shape independent of conditions.
