Use number concentrations and write the electrochemical potential of a sodium ion as μ​=μ∘+kB​Tlnc+eψ. Define the inward sodium-motive force as available work per positive charge for transport from outside to inside. With Vm​=ψi​−ψo​,
ENa​=eμ​o​−μ​i​​=ekB​T​lnci​co​​−Vm​.​
(73)
Its negative is the signed inward free-energy cost per charge; stating this direction removes a common sign ambiguity.
Long-open sodium channels establish electrochemical equilibrium, kB​Tln(ci0​/co​)+eVm0​=0. This does not generally require equal concentrations: impermeant counterions can support a nonzero equilibrium membrane potential. Let n0​=ci0​V. For a large fixed-concentration external reservoir, after pumping n ions out, ci​(n)=(n0​−n)/V and Vm​(n)=Vm0​−ne/C. The sodium export cost from a capacitive vesicle for exporting another ion is
ΔGNa​(n)=kB​Tlnn0​−nco​V​−eVm0​+Cne2​=Cne2​+kB​Tlnn0​−nn0​​​.
(74)
The initial-equilibrium relation was used in the last step. The first term is the incremental electrical cost and the second is the change in chemical cost from their initial balanced values.
If the intended initial reference is uncharged with Vm0​=0 and ci0​=co​, then n0​=co​V and the requested form in the listed variables is
ΔGNa​(n)=Cne2​+kB​Tlnco​−n/Vco​​,0<n<co​V.​
(75)
That equal-concentration reference is an additional assumption. For example, ci0​=2co​ and Vm0​=−(kB​T/e)ln2 also give zero initial sodium flux, but a different ion inventory. Thus the open-channel condition alone cannot determine n0​ from co​V. If concentrations are molar rather than number densities, use the corresponding Avogadro constant conversion and RT for molar free energies. Treating the capacitor charge discretely gives a charging-energy increment (n+1/2)e2/C rather than the differential ne2/C; the distinction is negligible for the thousands of ions used next.
