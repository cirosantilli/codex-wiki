Conventional RF cavities accelerate beams with controlled phase, energy spread and transverse beam emittance. Their material surfaces limit sustainable gradients to order 10–100MVm−1, depending on technology and operating conditions. Mature RF systems already reached multi-GeV and much higher beam energies in 2015. The 2013 ILC baseline proposed 250GeV electrons colliding with 250GeV positrons, approximately 31.5MVm−1 superconducting cavities and a roughly 31km facility. These were design parameters, not an operating collider.[ref]
In plasma wakefield acceleration, an intense laser displaces plasma electrons and excites a longitudinal charge-density wave. A suitably injected electron bunch gains energy from that wave, with fields of order tens to hundreds of GVm−1. A concrete result available before this examination was 4.2GeV from a 9cm capillary, with 6% rms energy spread, 6pC bunch charge and 0.3mrad rms divergence. These values characterize that experiment, not every laser-plasma beam; divergence alone is not an emittance measurement.[ref]
A collider concept stages many separately driven plasma modules, with fresh laser pulses, interstage capture and refocusing, controlled injection, and a final collision focus. Dephasing and pump depletion limit a single stage. Although the accelerating sections could be much shorter than ILC linacs, a useful collider also requires high repetition rate and wall-plug efficiency, reproducible low energy spread, preserved normalized emittance, stable timing, high bunch charge and acceptable beam-beam effects. Positron acceleration is an additional challenge because an electron-optimized plasma wake need not focus positrons. Large gradient alone does not demonstrate the luminosity or beam quality of an RF collider.
