For the standard meaning of a canonical transformation, the new coordinates must retain Hamiltonian form for every Hamiltonian, with the same physical function expressed in the new coordinates. Let z=(q,p), Z=F(z)=(Q,P), S=DF and J=(0−I​I0​). With K(Z)=H(F−1(Z)), the chain rule gives
Z˙=SJST∇Z​K.

Agreement with Z˙=J∇Z​K for arbitrary K forces SJST=J. Its matrix entries are precisely the Poisson brackets of the new coordinates, so
{Qi​,Qj​}=0,{Pi​,Pj​}=0,{Qi​,Pj​}=δij​.​
(59)
The qualification for every Hamiltonian matters. Preserving one Hamiltonian flow does not imply canonicality. For example, with two coordinate pairs and H=(q12​+p12​)/2, the invertible change Q1​=q1​, P1​=p1​, Q2​=2q2​, P2​=p2​ preserves the equations and the same physical Hamiltonian, but {Q2​,P2​}=2. Thus a definition requiring only one chosen Hamiltonian flow is too weak to prove the claimed bracket identities. The universal definition supplies the missing condition.
