William Thomson had to reconcile a successful constraint on engine performance with evidence that mechanical work could be converted into heat. Count Rumford's cannon-boring observations challenged a finite store of caloric by showing sustained production of heat through mechanical action. James Prescott Joule made the connection quantitative through controlled comparisons of mechanical or electrical input and thermal output. Neither result alone supplied the general limit on converting heat back into useful work.
Carnot's reversible-engine reasoning supplied that second ingredient, but its conservation-of-caloric interpretation could not simply coexist unchanged with the mechanical equivalent of heat. The synthesis retains energy conservation while replacing caloric conservation: in a cycle, the heat received equals work produced plus heat rejected. A second principle restricts what transformations of that conserved energy are possible. In the Thomson formulation, a cyclic device cannot have as its sole result the extraction of heat from one reservoir and its complete conversion into work. A reversible engine between two temperatures sets the upper bound; an irreversible one performs worse.
This separation explains why the first law does not imply the second. An imagined perfect converter of heat from a single reservoir could conserve total energy and still violate the required directionality. Conversely, observing heat from friction does not prove that every reverse conversion can occur without additional change. Absolute thermodynamic temperature gives the reversible comparison an operational scale independent of any particular thermometer substance.
Thomson's contribution was a developing reconciliation, not the immediate acceptance of a finished package. Experimental work, revised theoretical commitments and collaboration and disagreement with contemporaries all mattered. The resulting laws harmonized work-heat equivalence with limits on cyclic conversion while preserving the useful core, rather than every premise, of Carnot's argument.[ref][ref]
