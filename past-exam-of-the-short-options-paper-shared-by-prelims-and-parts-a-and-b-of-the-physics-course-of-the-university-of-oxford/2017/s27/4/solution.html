Newton's first law says that an unforced body maintains uniform straight-line motion in an inertial frame. It can function partly as a criterion for identifying inertial frames, but that does not automatically empty it of empirical content. The substantive claim is that there is a common class of frames in which independently isolated bodies have this behavior, rather than needing a different accelerated frame for each trajectory.
Testing requires auxiliary judgments about forces and measurement. If every unexpected acceleration is redescribed as an unknown force, or if a new coordinate system is tailored to every body, the law becomes insulated by construction. In practice forces are investigated independently through interactions, shielding, environmental changes and comparison among bodies. Reducing drag and varying external influences allow extrapolation toward the ideal. Mutually consistent force-free trajectories and the success of the broader dynamical system then support the inertial-frame hypothesis.
A simple counter to the claim that any coordinate choice suffices is a rotating laboratory: free bodies generally have velocity-dependent Coriolis accelerations and cannot all be made uniformly rectilinear by calling that rotating frame inertial while retaining the same force account. Conversely, no finite experiment establishes complete isolation or exact uniform motion forever. The law is tested as part of a network of force laws, geometrical assumptions and clock/ruler procedures, and may have local rather than global validity in a gravitational theory.
The first law is therefore neither an independently observed slogan with no definitions nor a mere tautology. Its definition-like role fixes a useful framework, while its applicability and the consistency of that framework across systems are answerable to experience.[ref]
