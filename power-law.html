Power law (f(x)=Cxp)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 570 Articles: 3
A power law is a relation f(x)=Cxp, with fixed constants C and p. For positive x, any real number p is allowed. Its useful feature is a simple scaling rule:
f(λx)=λpf(x)(λ>0).
(3)
The same proportional change of input produces the same proportional change of output, independently of the starting value. The exponent controls that response; the coefficient fixes the magnitude.
For example, the area of a circle is A(r)=πr2. Doubling the radius multiplies its area by four, and tripling it multiplies the area by nine. The volume of a sphere is V(r)=4πr3/3: the same changes multiply its volume by eight and twenty-seven. The distinction explains why similarly shaped objects do not preserve their ratio of surface area to volume when their size changes.
When the output is positive, a logarithm turns the power law into a straight-line relation. Using positive reference values x0​ and f0​=f(x0​),
f0​f(x)​=(x0​x​)p,logf0​f(x)​=plogx0​x​.
(4)
These ratios are dimensionless quantities, so the formula also applies to physical measurements with units. Plotting both ratios on a logarithmic scale gives a line of slope p. Changing a unit changes the numerical coefficient, while the exponent stays the same. Positive p describes increasing values; negative p describes decreasing values; p=0 gives a constant. The zero coefficient is a degenerate case with no useful logarithmic slope.
Exact scaling is stronger than regular variation. The function x2logx has a doubling ratio tending to four, but its logarithmic correction keeps growing. The diagram contrasts it with the exact power law x2.
A power law for a probability density and a power law for a tail probability have different exponents. For a Pareto distribution with α>0 and lower endpoint one,
P(X>x)=x−α,pX​(x)=αx−α−1(x>1).
(5)
The tail probability has exponent −α; differentiating it and changing sign gives the probability density exponent −α−1. Confusing them changes the interpretation of a fitted exponent. The normalizing coefficient and lower endpoint also matter: a decreasing power law is not automatically a valid probability density over the entire positive real line.
The appearance of a line over a finite logarithmic range is evidence to investigate, rather than a proof of an exact power law or its mechanism. In their study of power-law distributions, Clauset, Shalizi and Newman combine maximum likelihood estimation, goodness-of-fit tests and comparisons with alternative models; their abstract explains why ordinary least-squares fitting can give misleading results for tail data. See Aaron Clauset, Cosma Rohilla Shalizi and M. E. J. Newman, Power-law distributions in empirical data, SIAM Review 51 (2009), 661–703. The scaling rule is mathematically exact when it defines a model; whether a dataset supports that model is a question of statistical inference.
