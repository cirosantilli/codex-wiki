An astronomical interferometer combines light from collectors separated by projected interferometric baseline B. For a distant, narrowband, spatially incoherent source, the Van Cittert-Zernike theorem gives normalized complex visibility

The resolving scale is λ/B, while the individual collector and beam combiner restrict the field of view. Delay lines compensate geometric path differences. To see broadband fringes, residual optical path length mismatch must lie within the coherence length of order λ2/Δλ; fringe tracking follows rapidly changing atmospheric piston in interferometry. Baseline changes and Earth-rotation aperture synthesis sample additional spatial frequencies.

Several baselines constrain binaries, stellar radii and circumstellar structure; orbital angular separations combined with radial velocities can give physical orbital scales. calibration stars test contrast losses, alongside wavelength, photometric imbalance, polarization and bandwidth controls. For three collectors the closure phase cancels telescope-based phase errors: ϕ12​+ϕ23​+ϕ31​ retains source asymmetry information. Sparse baseline coverage still leaves image/model ambiguities.
In stellar intensity interferometry, thermal-light correlations instead give g(2)(0)−1=∣g(1)∣2 for an ideal single-polarization resolved temporal mode. The Sirius experiment correlated separate detector outputs rather than optically combining the beams.[ref] Pure atmospheric phase shifts do not alter local intensities, but finite detector bandwidth dilutes the correlation and photon statistics limit sensitivity; the two-detector observable also loses the complex phase.
