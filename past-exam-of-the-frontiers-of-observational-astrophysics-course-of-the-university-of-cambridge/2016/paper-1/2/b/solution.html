Atmospheric refractive-index fluctuations impose a changing phase screen on the pupil. For a telescope much larger than the Fried parameter r0​, long-exposure resolution is approximately 0.98λ/r0​, rather than the diffraction scale λ/D. The scaling r0​∝λ6/5 makes the seeing width proportional to λ−1/5 for fixed turbulence. An atmospheric coherence time sets the exposure or correction cadence needed to freeze the phase, while the isoplanatic angle sets the useful angular separation from a reference source. These are atmospheric quantities, not improvements automatically supplied by a larger mirror.
First reduce avoidable turbulence: choose a good high site, control mirror and enclosure temperatures, ventilate the dome, and observe when the line of sight crosses less turbulent air. Guiding removes telescope drift, and a fast tip-tilt correction removes image motion. Slow active optics corrects mirror figure and alignment; it cannot by itself follow all atmospheric phase fluctuations. These measures improve the input wavefront but do not guarantee diffraction-limited performance.
In adaptive optics, a wavefront sensor measures the reference-star wavefront, a reconstructor estimates its aberration, and a fast control loop commands a deformable mirror and tip-tilt element. A Shack-Hartmann sensor obtains local slopes from the displacement of focused lenslet spots. The science beam receives the corresponding optical-path correction. Residual phase variance limits the Strehl ratio approximately as S≃e−σϕ2​ when the residual is small. Enough corrected spatial modes, adequate reference photons and a response faster than atmospheric changes are all needed. infrared correction is easier because a given path error has smaller phase in radians and the atmospheric coherence length is larger. A faint or distant guide star leaves larger errors and a core-plus-halo point spread function. The NACO manual documents these reference-star and anisoplanatic limitations.[ref]
A laser guide star supplies a wavefront reference in fields lacking a bright suitable star, but a finite-altitude beacon samples a cone rather than the full stellar cylinder. It also does not by itself supply the absolute celestial tip-tilt reference; a natural source is still required for that measurement. Multiple guide stars and conjugate correction planes can improve wider-field compensation, with greater hardware and reconstruction demands. The point spread function can vary across the field and with time, so reference stars and telemetry are needed to calibrate astrometry and photometry; a sharper image alone is not a guarantee of accurate science.[ref]
Short-exposure methods provide another route. Exposures shorter than the atmospheric coherence time freeze speckle patterns. speckle interferometry uses calibrated Fourier statistics of many frames to recover diffraction-scale information; phase information requires methods such as bispectrum reconstruction, not just averaging the blurred frames. lucky imaging selects the best short frames, registers them and combines them. It sacrifices photons through selection and can be read-noise limited for faint targets, so it is most effective with enough reference photons. Ordinary shift-and-add mainly corrects motion; it does not remove every high-order phase error. Deconvolution using a measured point spread function can recover supported structure but amplifies noise and cannot reliably invent spatial frequencies absent from the observations. Good-site engineering, fast wavefront correction and short-exposure reconstruction address different limitations and can be combined.
