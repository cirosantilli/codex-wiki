In a CCD, absorbed optical photons create photoelectrons collected in electrostatic wells beneath gate electrodes. During readout, clock voltages move the charge packets row by row into a serial register; serial clocks move them one pixel at a time to an output node. The node converts charge to a voltage, approximately ΔV=Q/C, which is amplified and digitized. pixel addresses are recovered from the row and serial-clock sequence. Resetting and sampling the output between packets establishes the baseline. Efficient transfer preserves charge; trapping or incomplete transfer produces loss and trailing. This is CCD clocked charge transfer, not independent wiring of an amplifier to every imaging pixel.[ref]
dark current is thermally generated charge accumulated even without illumination. If its mean rate is d electrons per pixel per second, a single exposure adds mean dt and Poisson variance dt. Cooling the detector and using low-defect material suppresses this generation. A dark calibration removes the reproducible mean pattern, but cannot remove the dark electrons' random shot noise in the science exposure. read noise comes from measuring the output packet and resetting the sense node, amplifiers and electronics; write its rms as r electrons per read. correlated double sampling reduces reset and correlated offsets by subtracting the baseline measurement from the signal measurement. Low-noise electronics and an appropriate slower readout reduce the remaining contribution. The CCD noise budget for M separate exposures combined over total time t contains independent dark and read contributions dt+Mr2, not (dt)2+M2r2. Long exposures reduce repeated read noise, but saturation, cosmic rays and tracking requirements constrain the exposure length. On-chip binning can reduce read-noise penalties for a fixed binned area at the cost of angular sampling.
For the source and sky calculation, define the count conversion factor K=AtΔν/(hν) and the source flux per pixel sν​=Fν​/Npix​. The ideal source and background electron counts are S=Ksν​ and B=Kbν​Ωpix​. These assume unit throughput and quantum efficiency, approximately constant quantities across the band and a well-determined mean sky level from many background pixels. Source and sky photons have independent Poisson fluctuations, so the variance after subtracting that mean sky is S+B. Subtracting a background removes its mean, not its photon noise. Therefore

A nonunit common efficiency replaces A by the effective collecting area; a nonflat spectrum requires the corresponding frequency integral. For example, estimating the sky from one equally exposed background pixel would add an extra B, whereas a large sky region makes that extra term small.

Reducing source flux by ten can therefore require nearly a hundred times the exposure once sky noise dominates. Increasing the diameter increases both source and sky photon rates as D2 for fixed angular pixels. In this ideal limit the required exposure decreases as D−2, whereas the stated telescope cost increases as D2.5: collecting area grows only as cost to the power 0.8. A larger ground telescope does not remove atmospheric photons or recover arbitrary wavefront information lost in a long seeing-limited exposure. Space observations avoid atmospheric airglow, weather and atmospheric turbulence, and offer stable high-resolution imaging and access to very faint structure. They still have zodiacal light, instrumental backgrounds and their own calibration limits. adaptive optics can provide excellent ground-based resolution in suitable fields, but its guide-star, wavelength and field-dependent limitations do not remove the need for space-based continuum imaging. The need is for complementary low-background, stable and high-resolution measurements; the cost exponent alone is not a proof that every imaging programme is cheaper in space.
For X rays, one fully absorbed photon produces many electron-hole pairs in silicon, rather than one optical photoelectron count. At moderate surface brightness, the probability of two photons landing in the same event island during one CCD frame is small. The charge packet or sum over neighbouring split-event pixels then supplies a pulse height for each photon, while its pixel position supplies the image coordinate. Calibrating pulse height against energy produces an event list from which a spectrum can be accumulated for any spatial region. photon pile-up at larger occupancies merges photon energies and corrupts both spectra and event grades, so fast enough frames and event-pattern screening are essential.[ref]
A measured silicon pair-creation energy w≃3.65eV gives Ne​=E/w≃2.74×103 electrons for a 10keV photon. Let F be the Fano factor of pair production, nr​ the number of independently read pixels in the event sum, and r the rms read noise per pixel. Then
σE​=wFwE​+nr​r2+σd2​+⋯​,ΔEFWHM​=22ln2​σE​.
(18)
The Fano-limited X-ray CCD energy resolution using the representative silicon value F≃0.12 gives an ideal Fano-limited ΔEFWHM​≃0.16keV at 10keV, or resolving power E/ΔE≃64. Finite read noise, charge loss and event summing broaden this; an order-of-magnitude practical estimate is a few tenths of a keV, but the particular detector's value is not determined by the given optical-pixel parameters. These w and F values are external detector properties, not supplied question data. Treating every pair as independent Poisson production instead would give 0.19keV rms, or 0.45keV FWHM; stating which width is meant avoids conflating those estimates. Radiation-induced charge-transfer inefficiency requires additional gain and spectral-response calibration.[ref]
