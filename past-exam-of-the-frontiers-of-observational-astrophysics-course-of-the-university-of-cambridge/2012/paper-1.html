The geometric path difference for a direction at zenith angle θ+α is bsin(θ+α). Equal cable lengths leave that difference unchanged; the inserted electronic delay cancels bsinθ for the tracking center. The residual delay is therefore
τ=cb​[sin(θ+α)−sinθ]≃cbαcosθ​.
(1)
The complex visibility of the unresolved source has phase 2πντ, giving exp(ikbαcosθ) in the chosen baseline ordering. Reversing the signal order complex-conjugates it without changing the measured visibility magnitude. Tracking the antennas geometrically does not itself provide this fringe stopping.
For a top-hat band centered at ν0​, let f=ν−ν0​. Averaging the residual fringe gives
Δν1​∫−Δν/2Δν/2​e2πi(ν0​+f)τdf=e2πiν0​τsinc(πΔντ),sincx=xsinx​.
(2)
After removing the central fringe phase, its envelope is exactly
sinc(cπΔνbαcosθ​).​
(3)
The full complex average retains the displayed central-frequency phase; the bare sinc in the question is the phase-referenced envelope or an average written in frequency-offset coordinates. This bandwidth decorrelation of a point source is cancellation between different frequency-dependent phases, not absorption of source radiation.

The center-to-center extent is 18m on each side and 182​=25.46m diagonally, while the shortest nonzero baseline is 6m. At λ=0.02m, characteristic scales are
θsyn​∼bmax​λ​=2.70′,θlarge​∼bmin​λ​=11.46′,θprimary​∼Dλ​=17.19′.​
(4)
These describe the finest synthesized scale, a largest recoverable scale estimate, and the individual-dish field of view, respectively. Along an array side the longest baseline is only 18m, giving 3.82′ rather than the diagonal estimate. Projection, weighting and sidelobes determine an actual synthesized beam.
There is no sharp universal largest-scale cutoff: the commonly used 0.6λ/bmin​ convention would give 6.88′. For an ideal uniformly illuminated circular dish, the primary-beam full width at half maximum is approximately 1.02λ/D=17.5′, while the first-null angular radius is 1.22λ/D=21.0′. These definitions should not be mixed. Uniform lattice spacing also produces grating sidelobes; the primary beam restricts the useful field but does not supply the missing zero-baseline flux.

Use the projected baseline along the source offset, bp​, and α=10′=2.909×10−3rad. The first bandwidth null occurs at Δν=c/(bp​α). A worst-case bp​=25.46m gives 4.05GHz. That is a zero of the response, not a good usable bandwidth. A quantitative maximum needs an allowed attenuation.
For example, requiring a response of at least 0.9 gives sincx=0.9 at x≃0.787. Hence
Δν10%​≃0.250bp​αc​≃1.01GHz​
(5)
for that worst projection. A strictly east-west offset in a sides-aligned square instead samples at most 18m, giving a 5.73GHz first null and a 1.43GHz ten-percent-loss bandwidth. The original two-antenna geometry supplies an additional cosθ projection; these examples use its maximum magnitude. Thus a bandwidth of order 1GHz is a reasonable conservative estimate with a stated small-loss criterion, rather than a unique geometry-free limit.
To use a much wider total band, channelize the signals before correlation or before irreversible frequency averaging. Correlate sufficiently narrow channels, track the reference delay and phase at each frequency, and combine frequency-aware visibilities or images. This preserves sensitivity while avoiding field-dependent averaging losses in radio interferometry. Merely adding a phase correction after a broad channel has decorrelated cannot recover the lost signal; nor can one tracking delay cancel every sky direction simultaneously.[ref]

The wavelength corresponds to about 15GHz, in the low-frequency decrement part of the thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect. Hot cluster electrons inverse-Compton scatter CMB photons. In the Rayleigh-Jeans limit,
TCMB​ΔT​≃−2y,y=∫ne​σT​me​c2kB​Te​​dl.
(6)
The signal probes integrated electron pressure. Short baselines give sensitivity to extended arcminute-scale cluster structure, while the primary field covers the cluster and surrounding sky. The array's several-arcminute resolution suits many such structures; it is not equally well matched to every cluster size.
Simultaneous cross-correlations reject uncorrelated receiver noise and provide spatial information; compact radio sources can be constrained with the relatively longer baselines and separated from extended structure. Broad frequency coverage helps sensitivity provided channel smearing is controlled. Correlated atmospheric emission, calibration error, primordial CMB fluctuations and radio-source contamination remain relevant. The lack of zero and very short spacings also means that total integrated cluster flux requires a model or complementary data. An interferometer is therefore useful for the spatially resolved decrement, rather than automatically measuring every cluster's full pressure integral.

An observed catalogue is the result of a population, an instrument and a selection rule. Its astronomical selection function can depend on flux, color, size, surface brightness, sky location, cadence and follow-up success. The relevant cut is often on noisy measured quantities. A model that selects on noiseless true flux instead can predict a different sample even when it uses the same nominal threshold.
Malmquist bias occurs because brighter members of a population are visible through a larger volume in a flux-limited survey. For a uniform Euclidean population, rmax​∝L​ and Vmax​∝L3/2. The selected luminosity distribution is therefore weighted toward large L. For an intrinsic Gaussian absolute-magnitude distribution with mean M0​ and variance σM2​, multiplying by 10−0.6M and completing the square gives the Gaussian absolute-magnitude Malmquist shift
⟨M⟩selected​=M0​−0.6ln(10)σM2​≃M0​−1.382σM2​.​
(7)
This assumes the stated simple population, a complete flux cut and no additional boundaries. Exactly identical noiseless standard candles have a distance truncation, not a luminosity spread to reweight. Scatter, extinction, cosmological volume and source evolution need their own model.
Lutz-Kelker bias concerns parallax errors, spatial population weighting and selected calibration samples. Uniform spatial density gives p(r)∝r2 locally, hence p(ϖ)∝ϖ−4 on the physically valid range. More distant objects can scatter into a measured-parallax cut. In addition, inversion r=1/ϖ is nonlinear and gives misleading estimates at low signal-to-noise; a negative measured parallax is possible even for a positive true distance. A posterior instead combines the parallax likelihood with a justified population prior. An unbounded uniform-volume prior is improper here; a finite population model or a proper distance prior for parallax inference is necessary.[ref]
Bayes' theorem makes the assumptions explicit. If η denotes population parameters, x latent properties and S the catalogue inclusion event, a selected-sample likelihood has the form
p(d∣η,S)=∫d′∈S​dd′∫p(d′∣x)p(x∣η)dx1S​(d)∫p(d∣x)p(x∣η)dx​.
(8)
This selection-normalized astronomical likelihood accounts for the model-dependent probability of entering the catalogue. For individual inference, selection information already determined by the fully conditioned measured data must not be multiplied in a second time. The sampling design, inferential target and actual conditioned information determine the appropriate expression.
Other effects include Eddington bias, where steep number counts and measurement scatter move more faint objects upward than bright objects downward, and surface-brightness selection that misses diffuse galaxies despite substantial total flux. Dust extinction affects both detectability and inferred luminosity. Variable-source detection depends on cadence and duration; spectroscopic follow-up can favor easy-to-measure redshifts or particular colors. Blends, calibration drift and redshift errors can mimic population changes.
Examples include magnitude-limited supernova samples used in distance-redshift fits, where luminosity scatter and follow-up must be modeled, and radial-velocity planet surveys, which favor large-amplitude, short-period systems and leave inclination degeneracies. Forward modeling through the survey pipeline, injection-and-recovery estimates of completeness, calibrated noise and properly modeled censored or nondetected observations address these effects. A universal correction or an unexamined inverse-volume weight does not replace an explicit generative and selection model.

Thermal bremsstrahlung arises from electron acceleration in ion Coulomb fields. For a thermal plasma its optically thin spectral emissivity has the dependence
jν​∝ne​∑i​Zi2​ni​Te−1/2​e−hν/(kB​Te​)gff​(ν,Te​).
(9)
The exponential high-energy slope helps constrain temperature in X-ray spectra, with composition, absorption and other emission components included. At radio frequencies with hν≪kB​Te​, the thin spectrum is much flatter and does not alone determine a unique temperature; the Gaunt factor supplies a weak frequency dependence.
For an approximately isothermal layer, radiative transfer gives Iν​=Bν​(Te​)(1−e−τν​). Radio free-free opacity scales approximately as τν​∝EMTe−3/2​ν−2gff​, where the hydrogen-plasma emission measure is EM=∫ne2​dl. A resolved optically thick brightness temperature approaches Te​; an unresolved filling factor lowers the observed value. The turnover between thin and thick regimes helps constrain emission measure.
An angular extent and distance give a physical size, and an assumed geometry supplies a line-of-sight depth. Combining that depth with emission measure estimates an rms density, ne,rms​∼EM/ℓ​. Clumping, filling factor, composition and unknown depth preserve a density-depth degeneracy of free-free radiation. These measurements constrain different combinations of temperature, density and size rather than independently fixing all three without assumptions.

The radial-velocity method measures repeated Doppler shifts of stellar spectral lines as a star responds to an orbiting companion. A stable high-resolution spectrograph and wavelength reference distinguish stellar motion from instrumental drift; many lines or a spectral template improve precision. A Keplerian fit yields period, eccentricity, phase and velocity semi-amplitude. For a companion mass mp​, stellar mass M∗​ and inclination i,
K=(P2πG​)1/3(M∗​+mp​)2/31−e2​mp​sini​.​
(10)
With a stellar-mass estimate and mp​≪M∗​, this measures approximately mp​sini, a minimum mass unless the inclination is independently constrained. A nearly face-on orbit can hide a massive companion. The method preferentially detects larger masses, shorter periods and favorable inclinations.
Starspots, activity, pulsations, rotation, blended spectra and sampling aliases can imitate or distort a periodic signal. Line-shape diagnostics, activity indicators, repeated observations and independent constraints help test a planetary explanation. Photon noise and spectral line width constrain precision even for a perfectly calibrated spectrograph. Multiple planets or incomplete orbital coverage require an adequate model and longer monitoring; a periodic centroid shift alone is not a proof of a particular planetary system.

A charge-coupled device converts absorbed photons into stored charge in semiconductor potential wells. An exposure accumulates a pixelated image; clocked electrodes transfer rows toward a serial register and output amplifier. Quantum efficiency, pixel sampling and optical throughput connect collected photons to the expected signal. In ordinary optical imaging it primarily counts photoelectrons rather than independently measuring every photon's energy.
The CCD noise budget combines source and sky shot noise, dark-current fluctuations and read noise. For an aperture containing n pixels, known background expectation Nb​, total dark expectation Nd​, source expectation Ns​ and per-pixel read noise r,
SNR=Ns​+Nb​+Nd​+nr2​Ns​​.
(11)
Estimating the background from a finite separate region adds its own uncertainty. Cooling reduces dark current; longer integration reduces the relative importance of one read but can encounter saturation, cosmic rays or changing conditions. Full well capacity, amplifier gain, linearity, blooming and charge-transfer efficiency limit useful detector dynamic range.
Bias frames establish the electronic offset; matched dark frames characterize time- and temperature-dependent dark signal. Flat fields calibrate pixel-to-pixel response and illumination, with appropriate attention to wavelength. Bad-pixel masks, repeated exposures and dithering help reject defects and cosmic rays. A calibrated PSF and astrometric solution are needed for reliable photometry and positions. High quantum efficiency and stable linear integration are advantages, but an uncalibrated count array is not yet an astronomical flux map.

Atmospheric refractive-index fluctuations distort the incoming wavefront. In a Kolmogorov model, the long-exposure seeing width is approximately 0.98λ/r0​, where the Fried parameter scales as r0​∝λ6/5. High-quality sites and longer wavelengths can improve the observing conditions, but do not eliminate the distorted phase. Short coherence times also require rapid correction or exposure.
Adaptive optics uses a guide source, wavefront sensor, deformable mirror and fast feedback loop. Tip-tilt correction removes image wander; higher-order actuators correct finer spatial distortions. Finite actuator spacing, sensor noise, delay and changing turbulence leave residual errors. A nearby natural guide star limits sky coverage, while a laser beacon supplies higher-order reference information over more directions. Conventional laser-guide-star systems still need independent image-motion information, commonly from a natural star.[ref] Anisoplanatism limits the corrected field; laser finite-altitude sampling and variable PSFs need their own calibration.
Lucky imaging selects favorable short exposures and aligns them, trading exposure efficiency and photon statistics for reduced blur. Speckle interferometry uses short-exposure spatial-frequency information and suitable phase/statistical reconstruction instead of simply averaging away the speckles. Both need adequate time resolution, detector performance and calibration; selection cannot restore information never recorded. Interferometric methods can also recover fine structure with phase control or closure information. These approaches correct or infer wavefront effects; they do not make clouds, absorption or every atmospheric amplitude fluctuation disappear.[ref]
