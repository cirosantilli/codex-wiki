Positive definiteness (q(v)>0(v=0))

 ... Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra Bilinear form Symmetric bilinear form Positive-definite bilinear form
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 4
The strict positivity of a quadratic form on every nonzero vector. A real symmetric matrix has positive definiteness exactly when its associated quadratic form does, making it a positive-definite matrix. A Hermitian form is positive definite when its value on (v,v) is real and strictly positive for every nonzero vector.
Think of a positive-definite quadratic form as an energy cost for a displacement: every direction costs something, and zero cost identifies the origin. For example, the real symmetric matrix
A=(31​13​),q(x,y)=3x2+2xy+3y2=3(x+3y​)2+38​y2
(8)
is a positive-definite matrix because the two squares can vanish together only at (0,0). The mixed term couples the coordinates, but cannot overwhelm their positive contributions: q(1,1)=8 and q(1,−1)=4. The graph is a bowl, and its positive level sets are ellipses with axes along the eigenvectors, rather than along the original coordinates.
The real and complex settings require different symmetry assumptions. For a real symmetric matrix use q(x)=xTAx with x∈Rn; for a complex Hermitian matrix use q(z)=z∗Az with z∈Cn, where z∗ is the conjugate transpose. The latter expression is real, so strict positivity is meaningful. A complex quadratic form such as z12​+z22​ has no such positivity: it vanishes at (1,i) and takes negative real values at (i,0). A Hermitian form uses complex conjugation, which changes this example to ∣z1​∣2+∣z2​∣2.
For a real matrix M without a symmetry hypothesis, its quadratic form depends only on its symmetric part of a matrix:
xTMx=xT2M+MT​x.
(9)
Positive eigenvalues of M alone do not establish positive definiteness of this quadratic form. For example, M=(10​41​) has both eigenvalues equal to 1, yet q(1,−1)=1−4+1=−2. The relevant test is positivity of the symmetric part of a matrix, not the spectrum of an arbitrary matrix.[ref]
