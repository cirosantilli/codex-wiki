Spherical trigonometry

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Geometry and topology Spherical geometry
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 2
Spherical trigonometry calculates the sides and interior angles of spherical triangles using trigonometric functions. Its sides must be shorter great circle arcs, which are geodesics on a round sphere. A curve at constant nonzero latitude does not qualify. The spherical law of sines and spherical law of cosines play the roles of the familiar flat-triangle rules, but account for the sphere's curvature.
Use uppercase A,B,C both for the vertices and for their interior angles, and lowercase a,b,c for the opposite angular sides. For example, a is the central angle subtended by the arc from B to C. On a sphere of radius R, its physical arc length is ℓa​=Ra when a is in radians; similarly ℓb​=Rb and ℓc​=Rc. Only on the unit sphere do the angular and physical side lengths have the same numerical values. See J. B. Tatum, Celestial Mechanics, chapter 3, section 3.5: great-circle sides and angular units.
One useful application of spherical trigonometry is finding great-circle distance from spherical coordinates. Write a unit vector as u(ϕ,λ)=(cosϕcosλ,cosϕsinλ,sinϕ), where ϕ is latitude and λ is the equatorial azimuth, or longitude, measured from a chosen reference direction. The dot product of two such unit vectors gives
cosδ=sinϕ1​sinϕ2​+cosϕ1​cosϕ2​cos(λ2​−λ1​),d=Rδ.
(4)
Here δ∈[0,π] is the central angle and d is the great-circle distance. The same identity follows from the spherical law of cosines by adding the north pole as the third vertex, with angular sides π/2−ϕ1​ and π/2−ϕ2​. This connects spherical geometry to position calculations in astronomy and to navigation on a spherical model of Earth. Choosing the surface model matters: Paula Neeley and Anthony Narkawicz, Map Projection Induced Variations in Locations of Polygon Geofence Edges, NASA/TM-2017-219675 (2017), sections 2-3 distinguishes spherical and ellipsoidal geodesics and gives this coordinate identity.
For a toy example, put two points at latitude 30∘, with equatorial azimuths −60∘ and 60∘. The great-circle distance and the arc length along constant latitude are
d=Rarccos(41​+43​cos120∘)=Rarccos(−1/8)≈1.696124R,ℓparallel​=Rcos30∘32π​≈1.813799R.
(5)
The great circle takes a shorter route by bending towards the pole in these spherical coordinates. To locate its highest latitude, the endpoint unit vectors are p=(3​/4,−3/4,1/2) and q=(3​/4,3/4,1/2). Their great circle lies in the plane through the origin with z=2x/3​. On its shorter arc, the maximum latitude occurs at y=0 and is arctan(2/3​)≈49.106605∘. Thus a geodesic can change latitude substantially even when its endpoints have equal latitude. These are lengths on an ideal sphere; the example illustrates the geometry rather than specifying an actual flight route.
Historically, spherical trigonometry was prominent in teaching as well as in astronomy. John Conway and Alex Ryba note its presence in early twentieth-century school textbooks in Remembering spherical trigonometry, The Mathematical Gazette 100(547), March 2016, pages 1-8, DOI 10.1017/mag.2016.3. Their introductory observation documents that teaching history; it does not identify a single inventor of the spherical law of sines or spherical law of cosines.
