Finite-band Cherenkov yield model

 ... Physics Branches of physics Electromagnetism Electromagnetic wave Cherenkov radiation Frank-Tamm formula
OurBigBook.com Words: 282
Choose an original transparent-band model, defined only for 400≤λ≤700nm:
n(λ)=1.28+0.04(λ500nm​)2.
(68)
This chosen refractive index is not a fit to water, glass or the historical liquids. It decreases from 1.3425 to approximately 1.300408, so the threshold 1/n increases across the band. For β=0.75, solving n(λ∗​)=1/β gives
λ∗​=5001/0.75−1.280.04​​nm≃433.013nm.
(69)
Only 400≤λ<λ∗​ emits. Faster particles with β=0.78 and 0.90 emit throughout this chosen band. The Cherenkov cone angle and the Frank-Tamm formula are both evaluated with the same n(λ).
For a 1cm track the chosen model predicts about 0.589, 27.40 and 142.85 emitted photons for β=0.75, 0.78 and 0.90, respectively. An expected count below one is meaningful over repeated equivalent tracks; it is not a fractional photon observed on one track. The near-threshold example illustrates why extending a constant-index approximation across an entire optical band can give the wrong emitting interval.
Finite bandwidth is essential. The 1/λ2 factor cannot be extrapolated down to arbitrarily small wavelength: the chosen material model stops at its stated band. Real optical dispersion changes the threshold and direction, and appreciable absorption requires a complex material response beyond the simple transparent-medium expression. A detected count must also include transport, collection geometry and the detector's wavelength-dependent response. Even a correct produced spectrum therefore does not, on its own, predict perceived colour or observed glow intensity.
