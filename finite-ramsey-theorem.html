Finite Ramsey theorem (∃n[n]⟶(m)kr​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Combinatorics Ramsey theory Ramsey's theorem
OurBigBook.com Words: 988 Articles: 4
Fix positive integers r,k,m with m≥r. There is an integer n≥m, depending only on r,k,m, such that every finite coloring
c:(r[n]​)⟶{1,…,k}
(5)
has a subset H⊆[n] of size m for which the restriction of a function c to (rH​) is a constant function. Here [n]={1,…,n}, and (rS​) denotes the collection of all r-element subsets of S, rather than its binomial coefficient cardinality. The notation [n]⟶(m)kr​ abbreviates this property. Thus H is a homogeneous set for a colouring; when r>1, the monochromatic set consists of its r-element subsets, not its individual elements. This is the finite assertion in Ramsey's original Theorem B, p. 267.
For r=1, this is the pigeonhole principle, with sharp threshold n=k(m−1)+1. For instance, every red-blue coloring of five objects has three of one color; four objects can split two and two. For r=2, k=2 and m=3, the sharp threshold is six, as shown by the Ramsey number for triangles. Coloring pairs makes this a problem about edges of a complete graph. For larger r, the analogous viewpoint uses a hypergraph.
The finite Ramsey theorem lets an existence argument discard most of a large finite set while retaining a prescribed amount of uniform structure. Its threshold applies to every coloring; one does not need an assumption that colors were assigned randomly. A diagonal compactness argument deduces finite existence from Ramsey's theorem, but does not by itself identify the smallest threshold. For pairs, a Ramsey number makes that optimization explicit.
