Malus's law (Iout​=Iin​cos2θ)

 Home Physics Wave Polarization (waves)
OurBigBook.com Words: 401 Articles: 1
For incident light with linear polarization, an ideal linear polarizer transmits
Iout​=Iin​cos2θ,
(4)
where I is light intensity and θ is the angle between the incident electric field's line of oscillation and the polarizer's pass axis. The input and output are compared in the same medium. The classical explanation is an orthogonal projection: for a unit vector u along the pass axis,
Eout​=(Ein​⋅u)u.
(5)
The dot product reduces the transmitted wave amplitude by cosθ. For a plane electromagnetic wave in the same medium, light intensity is proportional to squared electric field wave amplitude, giving Malus's law. For example, θ=60∘ transmits one quarter of the incident light intensity, although the transmitted wave amplitude is one half. [ref].
Start with light already in linear polarization along the horizontal 0∘ axis and let its light intensity be I0​. Two ideal absorbing linear polarizers with pass axes 0∘ and 90∘ give I0​cos20∘cos290∘=0. Insert a 45∘ polarizer between them: the first leaves I0​ unchanged, the middle transmits I0​/2 with linear polarization along 45∘, and the last transmits I0​/4 with linear polarization along 90∘. Each angle in Malus's law is relative to the preceding output, so the final step uses 90∘−45∘=45∘:
Iout​=I0​cos20∘cos245∘cos2(90∘−45∘)=4I0​​.
(6)
For these ideal absorbing polarizers, the rejected electric field component transfers energy to the filter. In the three-filter arrangement the middle absorbs the fraction I0​/2, and the last absorbs I0​/4, leaving I0​/4 transmitted. Conservation of energy holds at each step. Inserting the middle polarizer changes the linear polarization arriving at the last one; it does not create light. The effect illustrates why successive orthogonal projections cannot generally be replaced by a single comparison of the first and last axes.
The initially horizontal assumption matters. For unpolarized light of light intensity I0​, the first ideal linear polarizer transmits I0​/2: averaging cos2θ over uniformly distributed incident orientations gives 1/2. The same 0∘,45∘,90∘ sequence then transmits I0​/8. This extra first-stage factor does not apply to the already horizontally polarized input in the figure.
