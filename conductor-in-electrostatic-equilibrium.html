Conductor in electrostatic equilibrium

 Home Physics Branches of physics Electromagnetism Electrostatics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 9
A conductor in electrostatic equilibrium is an electrical conductor at rest whose mobile charge carriers have finished redistributing, with no maintained bulk electric current. In the ideal macroscopic model,
Emetal​=0,Φ=Vc​ on each connected conductor,ρmetal​=0.
(12)
A residual bulk electric field would drive further electric charge redistribution. The electric potential difference between any two points joined through the metal is −∫E⋅dl=0, so each connected piece is an equipotential. Gauss's law then gives zero net bulk charge density. This is a statement about net macroscopic electric charge, not the absence of positive atomic nuclei and negative electrons. Excess electric charge is represented by surface charge density; atomic surface structure is outside this continuum model. [ref].
The word inside must distinguish metal from a cavity in a conductor. A completely enclosed, charge-free cavity in a conductor has constant boundary electric potential Vc​. The constant solution of Laplace's equation and Uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem give Φ=Vc​ and E=0 throughout the cavity in a conductor, regardless of its shape or external electric charges. Grounding is unnecessary for this inward electrostatic shielding; an isolated enclosure can change its overall electric potential without producing an internal electric field. Zero enclosed net electric charge alone would only give zero electric flux. A positive and negative pair inside a cavity in a conductor can have zero total electric charge and a nonzero electric field. [ref].
If fixed electric charges inside a cavity in a conductor sum to q, a Gaussian surface lying entirely in the metal and enclosing that cavity in a conductor has zero electric flux. Thus the total induced inner-wall electric charge is −q. For one such cavity in a conductor and an isolated enclosure with total metal electric charge Qc​, the outer-wall electric charge is Qc​+q. In particular, an initially neutral isolated enclosure generally has an exterior electric field when q=0. For fixed internal sources, external changes only shift the constant electric potential inside an isolated enclosure, leaving the internal electric field unchanged. [ref].
A grounded enclosure fixes Vc​=0 and can acquire metal electric charge −q; with no exterior sources and Φ→0 at infinity, Uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem gives zero exterior electric field. With external sources present, the exterior electric field need not vanish, but it is independent of the internal sources at fixed grounded boundary electric potential. [ref].
Faraday's ice pail experiment makes the distinction between redistribution and transfer visible. In his letter of 4 February 1843, Faraday described an insulated pewter pail connected by wire to a gold-leaf electrometer, and a brass ball suspended on a long dry silk thread to reduce the hand's influence. He discharged the pail and charged the ball away from it. Lowering the ball without contact produced leaf divergence, which grew during entry and became approximately independent of further depth; withdrawing it collapsed the leaves. Contact with the bottom transferred electric charge, and the withdrawn ball was discharged. The readout was leaf divergence, not a direct map of the internal electric field. These observations distinguish electrostatic induction from contact transfer of electric charge. [ref].
For a modern version of Faraday's ice pail experiment, connect an insulated inner metal pail to the signal input of a high-input-impedance electrometer, with its reference lead and an outer shield grounded. Zero and momentarily ground the pail, then isolate the signal side. Lower a charged paddle deeply without touching and record the electric potential difference; withdraw it and check return to zero. Repeat with contact, then withdrawal: a residual reading indicates transferred electric charge. Reverse the paddle's sign and repeat. The electrometer reads volts; inferring electric charge requires the effective capacitance of pail, cable and input, so a stable reading at fixed geometry is a relative measure, not automatically a value in coulombs. Keep supports clean, nearby charged objects distant, and the operator grounded; humidity can cause leakage. [ref].
A complementary electrostatic shielding demonstration uses a charged hollow metal enclosure, a small metal sampling ball on an insulating handle, and an electrometer. Discharge the sampling ball between trials; touch it to the exterior, withdraw it, and transfer its collected electric charge to the electrometer. Repeat by touching the inner wall without touching the exterior during withdrawal. The exterior sample produces a response, whereas the inner sample gives a null within instrument sensitivity. This compares accessible surface responses rather than assuming a perfectly spherical enclosure. [ref]. Useful additional controls are an uncharged enclosure baseline, a repeat exterior sample to verify sensitivity, and repeated trials at different depths. An access hole, charged handle, leakage or disturbed surface charge density limits the inference; a null bounds detectable transfer, rather than proving an exactly zero electric field. An open pail's depth plateau tests good enclosure empirically. Neither demonstration establishes shielding of arbitrary time-dependent electromagnetic fields.
