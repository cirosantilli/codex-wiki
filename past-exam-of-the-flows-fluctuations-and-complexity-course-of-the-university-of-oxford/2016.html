An irrotational flow satisfies ∇×u=0. On a simply connected region, or locally on any small regular region, this gives a velocity potential ϕ with u=∇ϕ. The incompressibility condition is ∇⋅u=0, so
∇2ϕ=0.​
(1)
Thus the potential is a harmonic function. The locality qualification matters: a curl-free circulation around an excluded vortex core need not admit a globally single-valued potential. No such circulation is needed for the sphere flow below.

Put the stagnation point at the origin and the local tangent plane at z=0. A regular Taylor expansion has no linear term because u(0)=0. The impermeable tangent plane imposes ∂z​ϕ=0 at z=0 to leading order, excluding mixed quadratic terms xz,yz. For the illustrated axisymmetric approach, rotational symmetry in the tangent plane makes the x2 and y2 coefficients equal and removes xy. Finally the Laplace equation fixes the normal coefficient to minus twice the tangential one. Up to an irrelevant additive constant,
ϕ=W(x2+y2−2z2)+O(∣r∣3),u=(2Wx,2Wy,−4Wz)+O(∣r∣2).​
(2)
For incoming flow from z>0, W>0. This is a local leading form under the stated symmetry, not the exact universal potential near every possible wall stagnation point; a generic tangential strain can have unequal principal rates.
A streamline solves x˙=2Wx, y˙​=2Wy, z˙=−4Wz, hence x=Ae2Wt, y=Be2Wt, z=Ce−4Wt. Eliminating the parameter gives
arg(x+iy)=constant,z(x2+y2)=constant.​
(3)
Each streamline lies in a meridional plane. With cylindrical radius s=x2+y2​, its shape is z=C1​/s2. The symmetry axis and wall are limiting invariant streamlines. A particle exactly on the incoming axis approaches the stagnation point asymptotically.

Choose the positive z direction along the sphere's laboratory velocity vz^. In its rest frame the far-field fluid velocity is −vz^. Therefore the far-field potential is −vrcosθ. The impermeable sphere imposes ∂r​ϕ=0 at r=R. The monopole coefficient vanishes because the sphere is not a source or sink, and the dipole coefficient follows from the wall condition:
A1​=−v,B0​=0,B1​=−2vR3​.
(4)
Thus
ϕ=−v(r+2r2R3​)cosθ+C,r≥R.​
(5)
The regular exterior boundary value problem has no forced higher multipoles; uniqueness fixes their coefficients to zero. Differentiating gives
ur​=−v(1−r3R3​)cosθ,uθ​=v(1+2r3R3​)sinθ,uφ​=0.​
(6)
Here uθ​ is the polar velocity component. An equivalent Cartesian form is
u=−vz^+2r3vR3​[3cosθr^−z^].
(7)
Only no-penetration, not no-slip, is appropriate to this ideal inviscid solution. The sphere density ρ0​ does not enter the velocity field for an externally prescribed constant speed.[ref]

On the surface, ur​=0 and uθ​=(3v/2)sinθ. The only surface stagnation points are therefore
(0,0,R)and(0,0,−R).​
(8)
There are no other stagnation points in the exterior: away from the axis the polar component is nonzero, and on the axis the radial component vanishes only at r=R.
Near the front point put (X,Y,Z)=(x,y,z−R). Expanding the potential from solution gives
ϕ=−23vR​+4R3v​(X2+Y2−2Z2)+O(∣X∣3/R2),
(9)
so the linear velocity field is (3vX/(2R),3vY/(2R),−3vZ/R). This agrees with solution for W=3v/(4R).
At the rear point, using Z=z+R instead, the quadratic term has the opposite sign: W=−3v/(4R). Incoming axial flow and tangential outflow at the front become tangential inflow and axial outflow at the back. The local streamline geometry is unchanged while its direction reverses.

The flow is steady and irrotational, so the Bernoulli equation has the same constant throughout the connected exterior. Far from the sphere it equals v2/2+p∞​/ρ. On the surface the speed is 3v∣sinθ∣/2, giving
p(R,θ)=p∞​+2ρv2​(1−49​sin2θ).​
(10)
The maximum stagnation pressure is p∞​+ρv2/2 at both poles. At the equator, where the flow is fastest, the pressure is p∞​−5ρv2/8. The fore-aft symmetry gives zero integrated pressure drag in this inviscid model, the content of D'Alembert's paradox; it does not predict zero drag for a real viscous separated flow.

a OurBigBook.com
For an incompressible Newtonian fluid at low Reynolds number, the quasi-steady Stokes equations are
∇⋅u=0,−∇p+μ∇2u+ρg=0,μ=ρν.​
(11)
The first equation expresses local conservation of volume for constant density; the second balances pressure, viscous stresses and body force with negligible inertia. Convective inertia is negligible when Re=Uℓ/ν≪1 at the relevant flow scale. Quasi-steady flow also requires the viscous diffusion time ℓ2/ν to be short compared with the evolution time. Low convective Reynolds number alone does not justify neglecting a rapidly oscillating unsteady acceleration.
For a thin layer, slenderness further changes the comparison of longitudinal and transverse derivatives; these scalings, as well as low inertia, are needed for lubrication theory.

Let the cylinder radius be a, with h/a≪1 and slowly varying thickness. Arc length is s=aθ, with increasing θ following the upward-moving surface at the right-hand side. Gravity has tangential component −gcosθ. In the leading thin-film balance, normal viscous gradients dominate tangential ones, curvature corrections are small, and the longitudinal hydrostatic-pressure gradient is smaller by the aspect ratio. Neglect capillary-pressure gradients and imposed gas shear. The tangential Stokes balance is then
ν∂z2​u=gcosθ.​
(12)
These assumptions also require that inertia and curvature effects from rotation remain small on the thin-film scale; an arbitrary high-speed coating need not obey this equation.
No-slip at the solid wall and negligible shear stress at the free surface give
u(θ,0)=U,∂z​u∣z=h​=0.
(13)
Twice integrating the constant normal forcing determines the two integration constants:
u(θ,z)=U+νg​(2z2​−zh)cosθ.​
(14)
The profile is most strongly retarded by gravity on the ascending side. The surface velocity is U−gh2cosθ/(2ν), not generally equal to the wall speed.

Integrate the profile over the thickness:
Q(θ)=Uh−3νgcosθ​h3.​
(15)
This is a volume flux per unit axial length. In a steady layer with no evaporation, sources or axial variation, mass conservation gives ∂θ​Q=0 to leading lubrication order. Therefore Q is constant around the cylinder even though the local speed and thickness vary.
Assume U,Q>0 for the wall-entrained branch, and substitute h=(Q/U)H into the flux formula. After division by Q,
1=H−3νU3gQ2​cosθH3.
(16)
Consequently
H21​−H31​=αcosθ,α=3νU3gQ2​.​
(17)
The cubic correction measures gravitational drainage relative to transport by the rotating wall. The nondimensionalization would need modification for zero or oppositely directed mean flux.

Seek the branch continuous from a uniformly wall-advected film, H=1+αH1​+O(α2). Expanding either the flux equation or the left-hand side gives
H−αcosθH3=1+α(H1​−cosθ)+O(α2).
(18)
Equating this to one yields H1​=cosθ, hence
h(θ)=UQ​[1+αcosθ+O(α2)].​
(19)
The film is thicker on the ascending side, where a reduced mean fluid speed must be compensated by greater thickness to carry the same flux. It is thinner on the descending side, where gravity assists the wall motion. The approximation is uniformly small only while α≪1, not near the critical turning point discussed next.

Define A(H)=(H−1)/H3 for positive thickness. Its derivative is A′(H)=(3−2H)/H4, so its maximum is attained at H=3/2 and equals 4/27. At θ=0, the equation requires A(H)=α, proving the necessary condition α≤4/27.
It is also sufficient for a continuous finite periodic branch. On 0<H≤3/2, A increases monotonically from −∞ to 4/27, so every value αcosθ in the required range has exactly one root on this branch. It joins H=1 where cosθ=0. The second large-thickness root on the ascending side does not extend to a finite periodic branch through those points.
Thus the limiting maximum thickness occurs at the ascending point of the critical film:
αc​=274​,Hmax​=23​,hmax​=2U3Q​.​
(20)
Subcritical films satisfy a strict thickness bound there. Equivalently the largest carried flux and critical absolute thickness are
Qc​=32​gνU3​​,hc​=gνU​​.
(21)
The mechanism is a transport capacity limit: wall transport grows only as Uh, while opposing gravitational drainage grows as h3. At the critical point, adding thickness no longer increases the upward flux. Indeed ∂Q/∂h=U(3/H−2) on the steady branch and vanishes at H=3/2. Excess liquid cannot be maintained as this globally wall-entrained film and must redistribute or drain.
The algebra establishes existence and the limiting branch of the steady lubrication model. A full temporal free-surface stability result requires its time-dependent equation and, when relevant, pressure and capillary corrections; the sign of ∂Q/∂h alone is not a proof of growth of perturbations.

For r=0, the equilibrium points satisfy sinx=0, so xk​=kπ for every integer k. The derivative of the vector field is f′(xk​)=−cos(kπ)=−(−1)k. Thus
x=2kπ is attracting,x=(2k+1)π is repelling.​
(22)
Between an odd multiple and the next even multiple the sine is negative, so the flow points right; between that even multiple and the next odd multiple it points left. A compact phase-line sketch is
(2k−1)π⟶2kπ⟵(2k+1)π.
(23)
Every initial condition strictly between two adjacent repelling points approaches the even multiple between them. The repelling equilibria themselves remain fixed if initialized exactly there.

For r>0, the origin always exists. Its derivative r−1 makes it attracting for 0<r<1 and repelling for r>1. Nonzero equilibria solve
r=xsinx​,∣x∣≤r1​.
(24)
They occur in symmetric pairs. On the first positive lobe 0<x<π, the sinc function decreases from one to zero, giving one positive and one negative nonzero equilibrium for 0<r<1. Both are repelling, since f′=sinx/x−cosx>0 there. Near r=1,
x˙=(r−1)x+6x3​+O(x5),x±​≃±6(1−r)​.
(25)
The unstable branches meet the stable origin in a subcritical pitchfork bifurcation. At r=1 the origin is still nonlinearly repelling because the leading term is +x3/6. For 1<r≤2 it is the only equilibrium.
For each higher positive-sine lobe 2nπ<x<(2n+1)π, n≥1, the sinc function has a single positive maximum at an​, where
tanan​=an​,2nπ<an​<(2n+21​)π,rn​=an​sinan​​=1+an2​​1​.​
(26)
If 0<r<rn​, that lobe has two roots: the smaller positive root is attracting and the larger is repelling, because f′=−xd(sinx/x)/dx. Their negative counterparts have the same stability. At r=rn​ the pair on each side merges in a saddle-node bifurcation; the double equilibria are semistable. For r>rn​ that lobe contributes none. The first such threshold is about 0.128375.
For n≫1, put bn​=(2n+1/2)π; expanding near the sine maximum gives an​=bn​−bn−1​+O(bn−3​) and rn​≃1/bn​. Away from bifurcation values, the exact counting rule for 0<r<1 and its small-r estimate are
N(r)=3+4#{n≥1:r<rn​},N(r)=πr2​+O(1)(r→0+).​
(27)
At a saddle-node threshold, count each merged positive or negative equilibrium once rather than twice. Infinitely many thresholds accumulate at r=0, consistent with the infinitely many equilibria in solution.

Restrict the complexified equation to the imaginary axis by writing its coordinate as z=iy with real y. Transform the left-hand side as well: iy˙​=iry−isinhy. Thus the real equation along that axis is
y˙​=ry−sinhy.​
(28)
For r<1, sinhy/y>1 away from zero, so the origin is the only equilibrium point and is attracting. At r=1 the leading term is −y3/6, so it remains attracting with algebraic, rather than exponential, relaxation.
For r>1, the origin becomes repelling and a unique pair ±y∗​ appears, where r=sinhy∗​/y∗​. Each nonzero root is attracting, since r−coshy∗​<0. Close to the threshold,

The fixed-point conditions are y(y2−1)=0 and x=y2. They give (0,0) and (1,±1). The Jacobian matrix is
J=(01​3y2−1−2y​).
(30)
At (1,1) its eigenvalues are −1±3​; at (1,−1) they are 1±3​. Both equilibria are saddle equilibria. At the origin the eigenvalues are ±i. This linear calculation alone does not distinguish a nonlinear center from a weak focus.
To settle the origin and the global connections, set
a±​=21±3​​,F±​=x−1−a±​(y2−1).
(31)
Using 2a±2​−2a±​−1=0, direct differentiation gives F˙±​=−2a±​yF±​. Hence each parabola F±​=0 is invariant and, away from them, the following is a first integral:
I=∣F+​∣−a−​∣F−​∣a+​,dtdI​=0.​
(32)
The two parabolas intersect precisely at the saddle equilibria. Their arcs between y=−1 and y=1 enclose the lens
1+a+​(y2−1)<x<1+a−​(y2−1),∣y∣<1.
(33)
Inside it F+​>0, F−​<0. The origin is a critical point of logI with negative-definite Hessian −23​ times the identity. It is therefore a strict local maximum of this first integral, proving a nonlinear center equilibrium. The regular interior levels are closed periodic orbits, circulating anticlockwise. In fact the only critical point of I inside the lens is the origin, so these levels fill the lens up to its boundary.
On F+​=0, y˙​=(a+​−1)(y2−1), so the interior arc runs from the upper saddle equilibrium to the lower. On F−​=0 it runs from the lower to the upper. Together they form a heteroclinic cycle. Their outer branches supply the remaining stable and unstable separatrices.
For the full exterior portrait, the two invariant parabolas partition the rest of the plane into four regions. Where both F±​>0, y˙​>0 everywhere; where both are negative, y˙​<0. In the region above both saddle equilibria, y>1 and x˙>0; in the corresponding lower region, y<−1 and x˙<0. These exterior trajectories are unbounded and cannot cross the separatrices. The nullclines are y=0,±1 for x˙=0 and x=y2 for y˙​=0.

For constant positive reaction rates, the integrating factor for the RNA equation gives
R(t)=R∗​+[R(0)−R∗​]e−γR​t,R∗​=γR​kR​​,P∗​=γP​kP​R∗​​.​
(34)
For completeness, the protein transient when γP​=γR​ is
P(t)=P∗​+[P(0)−P∗​]e−γP​t+kP​[R(0)−R∗​]γP​−γR​e−γR​t−e−γP​t​.
(35)
At equal degradation rates, the last term becomes kP​[R(0)−R∗​]te−γP​t, its continuous limit.
One transcript per minute means kR​=1min−1 for a single active gene. A mean RNA lifetime of ten minutes means γR​=0.1min−1, not a ten-minute half-life. The steady mean is therefore ⟨R⟩=10​ RNA molecules per cell.
For the Fano factors below, R,P denote molecule numbers in a fixed cell. If they instead denote dimensional concentrations, the same deterministic equations hold after rescaling, but the conversion to molecule-number variance introduces the cell-volume factor; variance divided by concentration is not intrinsically a dimensionless count Fano factor.

Write the source's noise terms consistently as ηR​,ηP​. Subtract the steady mean to get δR˙=−γR​δR+ηR​. Use the Fourier convention f(t)=∫dωe−iωtf​(ω)/(2π). After initial transients decay,
δR(ω)=γR​−iωη​R​(ω)​.
(36)
Fourier transforming the stationary white-noise covariance gives
⟨η​R​(ω)η​R​(ω′)⟩=2πqR​δ(ω+ω′).
(37)
Therefore the RNA fluctuation power spectral density is SR​(ω)=qR​/(γR2​+ω2). Its integral is the equal-time variance:
⟨δR2⟩=∫−∞∞​2πdω​γR2​+ω2qR​​=2γR​qR​​.​
(38)
The positivity of γR​ ensures a stationary limit. The same calculation gives the autocovariance ⟨δR(t)δR(0)⟩=[qR​/(2γR​)]e−γR​∣t∣: the decay rate filters the white forcing into a finite correlation time. Here the covariance convention is qR​δ(t−t′); replacing it by 2DR​δ(t−t′) would identify qR​=2DR​.

The protein fluctuation equation gives δP=(kP​δR+η​P​)/(γP​−iω). With the stated independent noises,
SP​(ω)=γP2​+ω2qP​​+(γP2​+ω2)(γR2​+ω2)kP2​qR​​.
(39)
Partial fractions and the supplied integral give the general variance
⟨δP2⟩=2γP​qP​​+2γR​γP​(γR​+γP​)kP2​qR​​.​
(40)
The specific count-noise formula in the question additionally uses the usual steady birth-and-death noise strengths qR​=2γR​R∗​ and qP​=2γP​P∗​. Then P∗​=kP​R∗​/γP​ reduces it to P∗​[1+kP​/(γR​+γP​)]. Arbitrary unspecified qR​,qP​ do not by themselves imply this particular Fano factor.
The requested rewriting is
F=1+1+ϕb​,b=γR​kP​​,ϕ=γR​γP​​=τP​τR​​.​
(41)
Here b is the translational burst size, the expected number of proteins produced during one RNA's mean lifetime. The ratio ϕ compares removal rates, equivalently RNA to protein mean lifetimes. Poisson molecule-number statistics have variance equal to mean, hence F=1.
When RNA is much less stable than protein, ϕ≪1 and F≃1+b. At fixed degradation rates it rises linearly with the rate of biological translation, with exact slope 1/(γR​+γP​). The formal intercept at zero rate is the limit from positive protein mean; at exactly zero production both mean and variance vanish and their ratio is undefined. A larger Fano factor need not imply a larger relative variance varP/P∗2​, because the mean also increases.

Intrinsic gene-expression noise is variability from the random reaction events of the expression process itself, conditional on the cell's shared state: transcript production and removal, translation events and protein loss. It remains possible in nominally identical cells in the same environment and is particularly visible when molecule numbers are small.
Extrinsic gene-expression noise comes from fluctuations in shared cellular conditions, such as cell size, growth state, expression machinery abundance or environmental signals. It can correlate otherwise distinct expression processes. In a matched two-reporter measurement, reporter differences reveal an intrinsic component while correlated cell-to-cell changes reveal a shared component, after reporter-specific measurement noise is accounted for.[ref]
Intrinsic fluctuations can be beneficial when a population gains a diversity of temporary physiological states, or when a noisy threshold crossing helps a cell explore or select a state. They can be detrimental when an essential product transiently falls too low, when tightly coordinated components become imbalanced, or when a regulatory threshold is crossed at an inappropriate time. These are context-dependent effects: noise is not automatically adaptive, and increasing a Fano factor is not synonymous with improving a cellular function.
