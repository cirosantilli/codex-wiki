Negative binomial distribution

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Probability distribution Discrete probability distribution
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 5
The negative binomial distribution counts failures before a target number of successes in successive independent Bernoulli trials with constant success probability p. In this stopping model, r is a positive integer and 0<p≤1. On the failures-before-the-rth-success convention, the random variable X takes values 0,1,…; for 0<p<1, its probability mass function is
P(X=x)=(xx+r−1​)(1−p)xpr,x=0,1,….
(33)
The binomial coefficient counts possible arrangements before the final success, as explained by the negative binomial stopping argument. At p=1, every trial succeeds: X=0 with probability one, so its expected value and variance are both zero. This handles the endpoint without needing to interpret 00. At p=0, the target is never reached and there is no proper finite waiting-count law.
For a worked example, toss a fair coin until the second head, calling heads S and tails F. Thus r=2 and p=1/2. Exactly two failures means that the second success is on trial four. The complete stopped sequences are SFFS, FSFS and FFSS; each has probability (1/2)4=1/16, giving P(X=2)=3/16. The first three masses are
P(X=0)=41​,P(X=1)=41​,P(X=2)=163​.
(34)
Both 0 and 1 are modes, each with mass 1/4: there is no unique mode here. Later masses decrease, since P(X=x+1)/P(X=x)=(x+2)/(2x+2)<1 for x≥1.
The expected value and variance of the failures count are
EX=pr(1−p)​,Var(X)=p2r(1−p)​.
(35)
If instead N=X+r counts all trials, including the r successes, then N≥r and
EN=pr​,Var(N)=p2r(1−p)​.
(36)
For the fair-coin example, EX=2, Var(X)=4, and EN=4. Shifting the count changes its expected value but preserves its variance. When r=1, X has the failures-count geometric distribution, while N=X+1 has the all-trials geometric distribution. The two conventions and the moments are treated in Kyle Siegrist, The Negative Binomial Distribution, Random Services.
The negative binomial distribution is useful when a procedure runs until it collects a target number of successes: for example, obtaining r usable samples from independent attempts with a stable acceptance probability. A binomial distribution instead counts successes in a fixed number of trials. The attractive connection is that these two experimental questions can be answered from the same trial sequence, using the negative binomial stopping argument. For the failures count and 0<p<1, the positive mean μ=r(1−p)/p satisfies Var(X)=μ/p>μ, so the law allows overdispersion relative to a Poisson distribution. At p=1, both quantities vanish and the inequality is not strict. The separate Poisson-gamma mixture explains another way this count law arises; its noninteger shape parameters do not represent a literal target number of successes.
