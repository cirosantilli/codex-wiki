Likewise, the Gram matrix of A2​V is diag(1−λj​). Normalize its nonzero columns and complete at its zero columns in Rm−k, using m−k≥n. The resulting U2​ has orthonormal columns and A2​V=U2​Σ2​. Combining the identities gives

(8)
This is a form of the cosine-sine decomposition. Completing the zero columns is essential: neither block was assumed to have full column rank.

UTU=Σ−1VTATAVΣ−1=Σ−1Σ2Σ−1=In​.

ii OurBigBook.com
Let A=UΣVT. Interpret the diagonal of Σ as nonnegative computed singular values and sort them consistently. If signed diagonal entries are used instead, take their absolute values and absorb signs into the orthonormal columns. Because the two outer factors have the stated orthonormality, these sorted numbers are exactly σj​(A).
For completeness, the singular value perturbation bound follows from the Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem. If Bj−1​ is the truncated singular value decomposition of a matrix B, the triangle inequality gives
σj​(A)=minrankD≤j−1​∥A−D∥2​≤∥A−Bj−1​∥2​≤∥A−B∥2​+σj​(B).
(13)
Exchange A and B to get ∣σj​(A)−σj​(B)∣≤∥A−B∥2​. Applying this to B=A and the given backward error proves
∣σj​(A)−σj​∣≤ϵ(1≤j≤n)​.
(14)
Thus backward stability of the singular value decomposition gives an absolute forward error bound for every singular value, even a very small one.

iii OurBigBook.com
An error of order ϵ in a squared singular value can become an error of order ϵ​ after taking its square root. To keep A nonsingular as required, take 0<ϵ<1/2 and
A=diag(1,ϵ),V=I2​,Σ=diag(1,ϵ2+ϵ​).
(15)
Then ∥A∥2​=1, and the computed eigendecomposition has exactly the permitted backward error:
∥ATA−VΣ2VT∥2​=∥diag(0,−ϵ)∥2​=ϵ.
(16)
Nevertheless the second computed singular value has error
ϵ2+ϵ​−ϵ=ϵ​(1+ϵ​−ϵ​)∼ϵ​.
(17)
By the singular value perturbation bound, every product with these computed singular values and exactly orthonormal outer factors has spectral norm error at least this large. In particular, even the favorable choice U=I2​ gives
∥A−UΣVT∥2​=ϵ2+ϵ​−ϵ∼ϵ​​,
(18)
which has no uniform O(ϵ) bound in big O notation. Therefore backward stability for the eigendecomposition of the Gram matrix does not imply backward stability for the singular value decomposition.
There is a related loss of orthonormality in the raw formula: evaluating AVΣ−1 exactly for these perturbed factors gives diag(1,ϵ/ϵ2+ϵ​), whose second column has length tending to zero. Its product with ΣVT equals A, but its diagonal factors are consequently not a singular value decomposition. The paper's idealized orthonormality assumption lets us isolate the unavoidable singular value error even if this additional problem is repaired.

The orthogonal projection P=QQT gives
QM−A=Q(M−QTA)−(Im​−P)A.

The first summand has every column in the column space of Q, and the second has every column in its orthogonal complement. They therefore have zero Frobenius inner product. The Pythagorean identity, together with QTQ=Ir​, gives the stronger exact formula
∥QM−A∥F2​=∥M−QTA∥F2​+∥(Im​−P)A∥F2​.

Because r<n, choose a unit vector v in the null space of XT, so that vTX=0. Let J∈Rm×n have orthonormal columns, for example the first n columns of Im​, and choose a real number α>1. Set
A=J(In​+(α−1)vvT)​.
(29)
Its singular value along v is α, and its other singular values are 1. Thus σ1​(A)=α>1=σ2​(A). Meanwhile AX=JX. If X has full column rank, the column space of the thin QR decomposition is exactly range(JX), which is orthogonal to Jv. Hence PJv=0 for P=QQT, and
∥(Im​−P)A∥2​≥∥(Im​−P)Av∥2​=∥αJv∥2​=α.
(30)
Conversely, an orthogonal projection is a contraction in the Euclidean norm, so ∥(Im​−P)A∥2​≤∥A∥2​=α. Therefore
∥A−A∥2​=σ1​(A)=α>σ2​(A)=1​.
(31)
If X is rank deficient, complete the QR decomposition within J(v⊥): this space has dimension n−1≥r and contains all columns of JX, so the required orthonormal completion exists there. The same proof applies. For rank-deficient data the choice of unused columns of Q matters; the construction specifies a valid choice. If Q instead contains only a basis of range(AX), the conclusion holds without any completion. A fixed sampling matrix can thus miss the unique largest singular value completely.

Write ℓ=r/2; the requested expression requires the positive even integer r≥2. Split the thin singular value decomposition after ℓ columns:
A=Ua​Da​VaT​+Ub​Db​VbT​,Ωa​=VaT​X∈Rℓ×r,Ωb​=VbT​X∈R(n−ℓ)×r.
(32)
Here ∥Db​∥2​=σℓ+1​=∥A−Aℓ​∥2​. Each column of a Gaussian random matrix is an orthogonally invariant random vector: its probability density function is proportional to exp(−∥z∥22​/2), and an orthogonal matrix preserves both ∥z∥2​ and volume. Thus VTX, where V=[Va​,Vb​], again has independent and identically distributed standard normal entries. In particular both displayed blocks have the Gaussian distributions needed below.
First prove a deterministic error bound for a randomized range finder. Put P=QQT and suppose Ωa​ has full row rank. Its Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse is Ωa†​=ΩaT​(Ωa​ΩaT​)−1, so Ωa​Ωa†​=Iℓ​. The QR decomposition implies (Im​−P)AX=0, hence
(Im​−P)Ua​Da​Ωa​=−(Im​−P)Ub​Db​Ωb​.
(33)
Multiplication by Ωa†​ expresses the missed leading columns in terms of the trailing columns. Substitution into (Im​−P)A gives the exact identity
(Im​−P)A=(Im​−P)Ub​Db​(−Ωb​Ωa†​​In−ℓ​​)VT.
(34)
For any matrix F, the spectral norm of [−F I] is 1+∥F∥22​​, because its product with its transpose is FFT+I. Using submultiplicativity of the operator norm, the orthonormality of Ub​, and the contraction property of the orthogonal projection, we obtain
∥A−PA∥2​≤σℓ+1​1+∥Ωb​Ωa†​∥22​​​.
(35)
This estimate is deterministic and also covers rank-deficient A; if σℓ+1​=0, its right-hand side vanishes.
Now use the paper's permitted singular value estimates. Apply the lower estimate to the tall matrix ΩaT​ of size r×ℓ, rather than to its wide transpose. It gives
σmin​(Ωa​)≥r​−ℓ​=r​(1−1/2​)>0,∥Ωa†​∥2​≤r​−ℓ​1​.
(36)
The upper estimate for Ωb​, transposing if necessary, gives
∥Ωb​∥2​≤n−ℓ​+r​≤2n​.
(37)
Thus, with K=2/(1−1/2​),
∥Ωb​Ωa†​∥2​≤Kn/r​.
(38)
Since n/r>1, the deterministic estimate yields
∥A−A∥2​≤Crn​​∥A−Ar/2​∥2​​,C=1+K2​.
(39)
This proves the requested conclusion under the explicitly authorized estimates.
The probabilistic qualification is necessary. A Gaussian random matrix does not always have all its singular values between the two endpoints printed in the hint. Already a 1×1 example would require ∣Z∣≤2 for every standard normal random variable Z, which fails with positive probability. The proof above holds on the event where the two block estimates hold. In a probabilistic formulation one supplies estimates for that event, usually allowing a positive margin in both endpoints; no failure probability is specified by the paper.
Nor can the requested inequality with one universal C hold almost surely for every A. For example, take n=m=3, r=2, A=diag(L,1,1) with L>1, and a sampling matrix
X0​=​010​001​​.
(40)
At X0​, the sampled column space misses the first coordinate, so ∥A−A∥2​=L, whereas ∥A−A1​∥2​=1. Choose L>C3/2​. Because AX0​ has full column rank, its orthogonal projection depends continuously on X near X0​ through AX(XTATAX)−1XTAT. The strict violation persists in an open neighborhood of X0​. The joint Gaussian probability density function is positive there, so that neighborhood has positive probability. This identifies the missing qualification without weakening the conditional calculation.

For the small QR decomposition, each of the k Givens rotations eliminates one subdiagonal entry of the upper Hessenberg matrix. At column j, apply the rotation to two rows in columns j,…,k, costing O(k−j+1) arithmetic operations. Summing gives O(k2), and applying the rotations to the right-hand side costs O(k). Backward substitution in a triangular system costs O(k2).
Computing ∥b∥2​ costs O(n) and the final dense matrix multiplication Qk​yk​ costs O(nk). Thus the total, with the Arnoldi decomposition already supplied, is
O(k2+nk+n)=O(nk)​,s=1,t=1​.
(44)
Here 1≤k<n, since Qk+1​ has orthonormal columns in Rn. In the standard dense arithmetic model, forming the general matrix–vector product gives the sharp Θ(nk) cost. The pair above records that two-parameter bound; looser bounds such as O(n2) are also possible because k<n. Treating Hk​ as an unstructured dense matrix would unnecessarily replace its O(k2) factorization by an O(k3) computation.

The least-squares normal equations are
Hx=c,H=GTG,c=GTb​.
(52)
The full column rank of G gives zTHz=∥Gz∥22​>0 for nonzero z, so H is a symmetric positive-definite matrix. Apply the Conjugate gradient method to this linear system. To verify that its solution is the desired one, let x∗​ solve the displayed equations. Then GT(Gx∗​−b)=0, and for any x the Pythagorean identity gives
∥Gx−b∥22​=∥Gx∗​−b∥22​+∥G(x−x∗​)∥22​.
(53)
Thus x∗​ is the unique minimizer of the linear least-squares problem.
One can apply H without forming the Gram matrix: compute Hp=GT(Gp). More explicitly, initialize x0​, r0​=c−Hx0​, p0​=r0​; until the linear-system residual vanishes, use the Conjugate gradient method recurrence
αj​=pjT​Hpj​rjT​rj​​,xj+1​=xj​+αj​pj​,rj+1​=rj​−αj​Hpj​,
(54)
βj​=rjT​rj​rj+1T​rj+1​​,pj+1​=rj+1​+βj​pj​.
(55)
Each dense application of H this way costs O(mn) operations. The condition number satisfies κ2​(H)=κ2​(G)2, where κ2​(G)=σmax​(G)/σmin​(G); this is the conditioning cost of the least-squares normal equations.

The Pythagorean identity in part (iii) also identifies ∥ek​∥H2​ with the excess squared linear least-squares problem objective, which consequently decreases by a bound proportional to 4−k. For relative accuracy 0<ϵ<1 in the H-weighted norm, it is sufficient to take
k≥⌈log2​ϵ2​⌉.
(59)
Compute c=GTb once in O(mn) operations. Each iteration applies p↦GT(Gp) with two dense matrix multiplications and uses O(n) further arithmetic operations; when m=4n this costs O(n2). Hence the total cost is
O(n2logϵ2​)​.
(60)
Using finite termination of the conjugate gradient method in exact arithmetic refines this to O(n2min{n,⌈log2​(2/ϵ)⌉}). Explicitly constructing GTG first would add O(mn2)=O(n3) setup work and is unnecessary. These accuracy estimates concern relative error; an absolute tolerance replaces 2/ϵ by 2∥e0​∥H​/ϵ when ∥e0​∥H​>ϵ. If e0​=0, the initial guess already solves the problem.
As in part 2(b)(v), the Gaussian assumption alone does not make the printed singular value interval a certainty. For example, the event that every entry of G has absolute value below a sufficiently small positive number has positive probability and forces ∥G∥F​<n​, contradicting the asserted lower endpoint. The displayed rate is therefore conditional on the singular-value bounds authorized by the paper. The cost and convergence derivation are exact under that condition; an unconditional probabilistic statement would need an additional failure-probability estimate.
