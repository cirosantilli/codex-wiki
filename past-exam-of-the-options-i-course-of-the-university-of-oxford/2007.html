A1 OurBigBook.com
Burnside's lemma counts the orbits of a finite group action:
∣Ω/G∣=∣G∣1​∑g∈G​∣Fix(g)∣.
(1)
Identify each face with its opposite vertex. Every symmetry induces the same permutation on these two four-element sets, so a permutation with c cycles fixes 3c2c=6c colorings. The rotational symmetry group is A4​, with one identity, eight three-cycles, and three products of two disjoint transpositions. Their cycle counts are four, two and two. Thus the rotation count is
1264+8⋅62+3⋅62​=141​.
(2)
Allowing reflections gives the full tetrahedral group S4​. In addition there are six transpositions with three cycles and six four-cycles with one cycle. The count becomes
2464+6⋅63+3⋅62+8⋅62+6⋅6​=126​.
(3)
Equivalently, 30 of the 141 rotational classes form 15 mirror pairs, while 111 rotational classes are their own mirror class.
The product of fixed-coloring counts is an instance of the orbit count for simultaneous colorings.

B1 OurBigBook.com
The degree [K:F] is the dimension of K as a vector space over F. The tower theorem states that for intermediate fields F⊆E⊆K in a finite extension,
[K:F]=[K:E][E:F].
(4)
If the degree is prime, one factor must be one, forcing E=K or E=F; no strictly intermediate field exists.
Let K have 32 elements. Its prime subfield is F2​ and [K:F2​]=5. Every element outside that subfield has minimal polynomial of degree five, by the tower theorem. Also every member of K satisfies x32=x: nonzero elements form a group of order 31. Hence x32−x splits over K, with distinct roots because its derivative is −1=1 in characteristic two.
Every irreducible degree-five polynomial over F2​ divides x32−x. Indeed its quotient field has 32 elements, and its residue class of x satisfies that equation. Thus every such polynomial splits into five distinct roots in K. Conversely each of the 30 elements outside F2​ has one such minimal polynomial, and different minimal polynomials have disjoint root sets. Consequently the number is
30/5=6​.
(5)
There is no extra leading-coefficient factor, since every nonzero leading coefficient over F2​ is one.

Euler's theorem states that aϕ(m)≡1(modm) whenever gcd(a,m)=1, where ϕ(m) counts the invertible residue classes. For each prime-power divisor, the same theorem gives aϕ(piei​​)≡1(modpiei​​). Since the exponent defined in the source is a multiple of every one of these exponents, raising that congruence to the corresponding integer power gives
aλ(m)≡1(modpiei​​).
(6)
The prime powers are pairwise coprime, so their product divides aλ(m)−1, proving the congruence modulo m.
For the displayed composite integer,
λ(561)=lcm(2,10,16)=80,560=7⋅80.
(7)
Thus every integer coprime to 561 satisfies a560≡1(mod561)​. This is a Carmichael number. The source's prime-power least common multiple need not be the minimal universal exponent for all moduli; for powers of two its definition can exceed the usual Carmichael function. No minimality claim is needed here.

A dominated differentiation theorem is the following. Suppose ϕ(x,t) is measurable in x and differentiable in t on an open interval, is integrable for at least one parameter value, and its parameter derivative is dominated in absolute value by an integrable function independent of t. Then its integral is differentiable and
dtd​∫ϕ(x,t)dx=∫∂t​ϕ(x,t)dx.
(11)
Local versions require the domination only on a neighbourhood of each parameter. The proof bounds the difference quotients by the dominating function, using the fundamental theorem of calculus or the mean-value theorem, then applies dominated convergence. Apply the argument to real and imaginary parts for a complex integrand.
For fixed t, f(x)eitx is measurable and has absolute value ∣f(x)∣, so is integrable. Its derivative with respect to t is ixf(x)eitx, whose absolute value is the assumed integrable function ∣xf(x)∣. Hence
f​′(t)=∫R​ixf(x)eitxdx​.
(12)
Dominated convergence also shows this derivative is continuous, though only differentiability is required.

A metric space is sequentially compact if every sequence in it has a subsequence converging to a point of the same space.

Solution

Words: 120
E1 OurBigBook.com
The sequence xn​=1/(n+1) lies in the open interval and converges in the ambient real line to zero. Every subsequence also converges to zero, and uniqueness of limits prevents it converging to any point of the interval. Thus the interval is not sequentially compact.
For a sequence (xn​,yn​) in the product, sequential compactness of X supplies indices nj​ with xnj​​→x∈X. Apply sequential compactness of Y to the corresponding ynj​​, extracting a further subsequence ynjℓ​​​→y∈Y. The first coordinate still tends to x. For any ϵ>0, both coordinate distances are eventually less than ϵ/2, so their sum is less than ϵ. Hence the same subsequence converges in the given product metric to (x,y). This proves the product is sequentially compact.

Time independence conserves the energy E=T+mgacosθ. The two cyclic coordinates give
pψ​=C(ψ˙​+ϕ˙​cosθ)=Cn,pϕ​=Aϕ˙​sin2θ+Cncosθ=h.

These are respectively angular momentum along the symmetry axis and angular momentum along the fixed upward vertical; n is the axial spin rate. For u=cosθ and −1<u<1, eliminate ϕ˙​ using ϕ˙​=(h−Cnu)/[A(1−u2)]. The energy becomes
E=2A​θ˙2+2C​n2+mgau+2A(1−u2)(h−Cnu)2​.
(23)
Multiplying and rearranging gives
A2θ˙2=1−u2A(2E−Cn2−2mgau)(1−u2)−(h−Cnu)2​​.
(24)
To justify the double-root condition, use the effective potential
U(u)=2C​n2+mgau+2A(1−u2)(h−Cnu)2​.
(25)
Eliminating the cyclic coordinates by Routh reduction gives the remaining equation Aθ¨=−dU(cosθ)/dθ. A constant nonvertical angle therefore has U′(u0​)=0 and E=U(u0​). Since P(u)=2A(E−U(u))(1−u2), both P(u0​) and P′(u0​) vanish. This argument uses the second-order equation; merely differentiating the energy identity at a stationary angle would not establish the derivative condition.
Thus steady nonvertical precession gives a common root in (−1,1). The nonvertical qualification is implicit in the denominator used by the source. A top spinning exactly upright or inverted is also possible but corresponds to an endpoint u=±1, not an interior root; the unrestricted literal claim would need that exception.
The distinction is captured by the double-root criterion for steady precession of a symmetric top.

For steady flow the given equation gives ∇H=−ρω×u. Dotting with u yields u⋅∇H=0, so H is constant along each streamline. It can have different values on different streamlines. Dotting with ω also makes it constant along vortex lines where they are defined.

A frequentist confidence interval is a pair of data-dependent endpoints whose random interval covers the fixed parameter with probability at least 1−α under every allowed parameter value. In repeated uses of the same sampling procedure, at least the advertised fraction of intervals cover the true value. It is not a posterior probability assigned to the parameter after observing the data.
For a sample of size n from a Poisson law with mean μ, the sum S is Poisson with mean nμ. At observed s>0, define the lower and upper limits by
PnL​(S≥s)=α/2,PnU​(S≤s)=α/2.
(39)
At s=0 set L=0 and use the same upper equation. This inverts the two exact one-sided tail tests. Discreteness means the resulting central interval is generally conservative: its coverage is at least the nominal value, not identically equal to it.
Here one observes a four-year total of 39, so the lower endpoint satisfies ϕL​(38)=0.975 and the upper satisfies ϕU​(39)=0.025. Selecting the corresponding entries supplied in the source gives
[27.7, 53.3]​.
(40)
This interval concerns the expected four-year total, not an annual rate. The other two supplied numbers correspond to the wrong inclusive tail for one of these endpoints.

The power of a test at a parameter value is its probability of rejecting the null there. Its size is the supremum of this probability over the null parameter set. The Neyman-Pearson lemma states that for simple null and alternative densities f0​,f1​, a test rejecting where f1​>cf0​, accepting where f1​<cf0​, and randomizing as needed on equality to attain size α, has greatest power among tests of size at most α.
The specified function is nonnegative. Its integral is
θ+1θ2​{∫0∞​xe−θxdx+∫0∞​e−θxdx}=θ+1θ2​(θ−2+θ−1)=1.
(41)
For independent observations write S=∑i​Xi​. The likelihood ratio of the smaller alternative parameter to the null is
L(θ0​)L(θ1​)​={θ02​(θ1​+1)θ12​(θ0​+1)​}nexp{(θ0​−θ1​)S}.
(42)
It is strictly increasing in S. Thus the most powerful level-α test rejects for
S>cα​,Pθ0​​(S>cα​)=α​.
(43)
The continuous distribution permits this size without randomization for 0<α<1. Its threshold depends only on the null and the desired size, not on which θ1​<θ0​ was chosen. The same test is therefore uniformly most powerful against the entire one-sided alternative θ<θ0​.
