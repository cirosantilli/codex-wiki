The splitting field is the smallest subfield of C containing Q and all the roots of a polynomial under consideration. Equivalently it is generated over Q by those roots, and the polynomial splits there into linear factors.
Put ζ6​=eπi/3. All sixth roots of unity are powers of ζ6​, so the required splitting field is
Q(ζ6​)=Q(−3​)​.
(1)
Indeed ζ6​=(1+−3​)/2, and conversely −3​=2ζ6​−1. Its field extension degree is two.

B1 OurBigBook.com
A solvable group has a finite subnormal series from the identity to the group with abelian group factors. An equivalent definition uses the derived series: set G(0)=G and G(j+1)=[G(j),G(j)]; the series must reach the identity. For completeness, an abelian-factor series forces successive derived subgroups into its successive terms, while a terminating derived series itself supplies such a series.
If H≤G, induction gives H(j)≤G(j): every commutator of elements of H(j) is also a commutator in G(j). Thus termination for G implies termination for H, proving that every subgroup of a solvable group is solvable.
In S4​, use
{1}◃V4​◃A4​◃S4​,V4​={1,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)}.
(4)
The Klein four-group is invariant under conjugation in S4​; the other normality assertions follow from the alternating group being the kernel of the sign map. The quotient group factors are V4​,C3​,C2​, all abelian. Hence S4​ is solvable.
For the last example take A5​×C2​. Its subgroup {1}×C2​ is nontrivial, proper and normal, so the product is not a simple group. Projection onto A5​ is surjective, and every quotient of a solvable group is solvable, because a homomorphism carries each derived subgroup onto the derived subgroup of its image. But the nonabelian simple group A5​ has A5′​=A5​: its derived subgroup is nontrivial and normal. Thus the product is not solvable either. One standard justification of the simplicity used here is that the nonidentity conjugacy class sizes of A5​ are 15,20,12,12; no proper nontrivial union with the identity has size dividing 60, as a normal subgroup would require by Lagrange's theorem for finite groups.

m∗(E)=inf{∑j=1∞​∣Ij​∣:E⊆⋃j=1∞​Ij​, Ij​ open intervals}.

One version of dominated differentiation under the integral sign is the following. Let the parameter range be open. Suppose f(⋅,y) is integrable, ∂y​f(x,y) exists for almost every x, and near each parameter value its absolute value is bounded by an integrable function of x independent of y. Then
F′(y)=∫I​∂y​f(x,y)dx.
(14)
This follows by applying the dominated convergence theorem to difference quotients, bounded by that same integrable function through the mean value theorem.
On any compact parameter interval 0<y≤B, the original integrand is bounded by B/(1+x4), using ∣sin(x2y)∣≤Bx2. Its first and second parameter derivatives are
1+x4cos(x2y)​,−1+x4x2sin(x2y)​.
(15)
Their absolute values are bounded respectively by (1+x4)−1 and x2/(1+x4), both integrable on (0,∞). Applying the theorem twice proves that F is twice differentiable and gives
F′′(y)−F(y)=−∫0∞​x2sin(x2y)​dx.
(16)
This last integral is absolutely convergent: its integrand is bounded near zero and bounded by x−2 at infinity. Substituting t=xy​ yields
F′′(y)−F(y)=cy​​,c=−∫0∞​t2sin(t2)​dt.
(17)
The expression for c is finite and independent of y, which is all that is required.

Both ∅ and X satisfy the condition. For an arbitrary intersection of members, either every member contains a, in which case the intersection contains a, or one does not, in which case that member is countable and so is the intersection. The empty intersection is X.
For a finite union, either a member contains a, so the union does too, or all members omit a and are countable, so their finite union is countable. Thus the closed set axioms hold and define a topology. This is a Fortissimo space: every point other than a is isolated, while the open neighbourhoods of a have countable complement.

The family contains the empty set and the entire real line. The intersection of two proper nonempty members is
(a,∞)∩(b,∞)=(max(a,b),∞),

If m=minA, any open cover contains a member containing m. Such a member is either R or a ray (a,∞) with a<m. It therefore contains every element of A, so that single member is already a subcover.
Conversely suppose A is nonempty and has no smallest element. Then
{(a,∞):a∈A}
(19)
is an open cover of A: for any x∈A, the lack of a minimum gives an a∈A below x. A nonempty finite subcollection has union (a0​,∞), where a0​ is the smallest of its thresholds. It misses a0​∈A. The empty subcollection cannot cover nonempty A either. Hence there is no finite subcover, and A is not compact. Thus compactness is equivalent to having a smallest element.

Take a smooth variation u+εη with η vanishing on the boundary; compactly supported test functions suffice for the interior equation. At an extremum, the first variation is zero:
0=dεd​I[u+εη]​ε=0​=∫V​(Fu​η+Fux​​ηx​+Fuy​​ηy​+Fuz​​ηz​)dV.

∫V​(Fu​−∂x​Fux​​−∂y​Fuy​​−∂z​Fuz​​)ηdV=0.
(24)

This is the classical equation for sufficiently smooth admissible extrema; for weaker regularity the first-variation identity supplies its weak form.

For a planar incompressible flow, a stream function satisfies u=ψy​, v=−ψx​. A velocity potential satisfies u=ϕx​, v=ϕy​ where the flow is irrotational; such a potential exists locally, and globally on a simply connected nonsingular region. The irrotational flow condition is vx​−uy​=0. Where both exist, the Cauchy-Riemann equations make the complex potential w=ϕ+iψ analytic with w′=u−iv.
For the logarithmic potential, write z−z0​=reiθ. Then
ϕ=2πm​logr,ψ=2πm​θ,ur​=2πrm​,uθ​=0.
(38)
The outward volume flux per unit span across a circle is 2πrur​=m. Hence it is a line source of strength m. The potential may use local logarithm branches; its velocity is single-valued away from the source.
By superposition, the full flow has
w(z)=Uz+2πm​logz−2πm​log(z−1),w′(z)=U−2πz(z−1)m​.
(39)

z±​=21±1+2m/(πU)​​​.

Flow inside goes from source to sink; the external stream divides around this oval and tends to horizontal flow far away.
