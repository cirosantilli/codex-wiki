The degree of a field extension is the dimension of the larger field as a vector space over the smaller one:
∣K:F∣=dimF​K.​
(1)
The field extension is a finite field extension precisely when this dimension is finite, equivalently when finitely many elements of K span K over F. Finiteness here concerns the dimension, not the number of elements of the fields; for example, C has degree two over R although both fields are infinite.

Let [L:F]=n<∞. The intermediate field K is an F-linear linear subspace of L, so the finite-dimensional linear subspace theorem gives [K:F]≤n. To handle the other field extension, take an F-basis v1​,…,vn​ of L. Every ℓ∈L is a sum ∑fi​vi​ with fi​∈F⊆K, so the same list spans L as a K-vector space. It need not remain linearly independent, but spanning suffices to give [L:K]≤n. Hence both intermediate extensions are finite.

The tower law for field extensions states that, for F⊆K⊆L with both successive degrees finite,
[L:F]=[L:K][K:F].​
(2)
Indeed, if ui​ form an F-basis of K and vj​ form a K-basis of L, their products ui​vj​ form an F-basis of L: expansion gives spanning, and independence follows first over K and then over F.
Starting from a unit segment, every straightedge and compass construction introduces coordinates by solving linear or quadratic equations over the coordinates already available. Thus a constructible number belongs to a tower of field extensions whose successive degrees are one or two; the total degree is a power of two. Doubling the volume of a unit cube would require the edge length 32​. Its minimal polynomial over Q is x3−2, irreducible by Eisenstein's criterion at the prime number two. Thus [Q(32​):Q]=3. If that number lay in a construction field of degree 2r, the tower law would make three divide 2r, a contradiction. Therefore doubling the cube by straightedge and compass is impossible.

An isometry of the Euclidean plane is a map T:R2→R2 preserving all Euclidean distances, ∥T(x)−T(y)∥=∥x−y∥. Such a map of the whole plane is automatically bijective, and has the form T(x)=Rx+b with R an orthogonal matrix.
The classification is identity, translations, rotations about a point, reflections in a line, and glide reflections. Here a glide reflection is a reflection followed by a nonzero translation parallel to its mirror. The cases preserving orientation have detR=1 and are translations or rotations; the orientation-reversing cases have detR=−1 and are reflections or glide reflections. The identity is included by allowing a zero translation or rotation angle.

Euler's totient function is
φ(n)=#{1≤r≤n:gcd(r,n)=1},n≥1.
(5)
Euler's theorem states that gcd(a,n)=1 implies aφ(n)≡1(modn)​. For n>1, let r1​,…,rφ(n)​ be the invertible residue classes modulo n. Multiplication by a permutes these classes, since it has an inverse modulo n. Multiplying the resulting congruences gives
aφ(n)r1​⋯rφ(n)​≡r1​⋯rφ(n)​(modn).
(6)
Every factor is a unit, so the product can be cancelled, proving the theorem. The case n=1 is immediate. For distinct prime numbers p,q, removing multiples of either prime from 1,…,pq gives
φ(pq)=pq−q−p+1=(p−1)(q−1).​
(7)
For the RSA cryptosystem, choose distinct large primes p,q, put N=pq, and choose e coprime to φ(N). Compute d with ed≡1(modφ(N)). The public key is (N,e); the private exponent d is retained secretly. Represent a message by 0≤m<N, encrypt by c≡me(modN), and decrypt by m′≡cd(modN).
To prove correct decryption even for messages not coprime to N, write ed=1+k(p−1)(q−1). Modulo p, if p∣m then med≡m≡0; otherwise Fermat's little theorem gives med≡m. The same reasoning holds modulo q. The Chinese remainder theorem therefore gives med≡m(modN), and the chosen representative recovers m. Thus decryption works for every message residue, not merely the units.

For a nonnegative measurable function, the Lebesgue integral is defined through nonnegative simple functions:
∫R​fdx=sup{∫R​sdx:s simple, 0≤s≤f}.​
(8)
The value may be infinite. A real measurable g is Lebesgue integrable when ∫∣g∣dx<∞; then ∫g=∫g+−∫g−, where g+=max(g,0) and g−=max(−g,0).
If ∣g∣≤h, every simple function below ∣g∣ is also below h. Taking the defining suprema proves monotonicity of the Lebesgue integral:
0≤∫∣g∣dx≤∫hdx<∞.
(9)
Hence g is integrable, and both its positive and negative parts have finite integrals.
The oscillatory function k is measurable because it is continuous off the singleton {0} and has a specified value there. The elementary bounds ∣sinu∣≤min(1,∣u∣) give the integrable majorant
∣k(x)∣≤H(x)={1,∣x∣−3,​∣x∣≤1,∣x∣>1.​

Fubini's theorem states that an absolutely integrable measurable function on a product of two σ-finite measure spaces has integrable sections almost everywhere, the corresponding iterated integrals exist, and both orders equal its product integral. In particular, for f∈L1(R2),
∬f(x,y)dxdy=∫(∫f(x,y)dx)dy=∫(∫f(x,y)dy)dx.
(11)
To establish integrability of the nonnegative function here, first use Tonelli's theorem: for a nonnegative measurable function the product integral equals either iterated integral with infinity allowed. This avoids assuming the conclusion needed for Fubini's theorem.
If α≥−1, ∫0∞​(1+x+y)αdx=∞ for every fixed y. If α<−1, it is
∫0∞​(1+x+y)αdx=−α−1(1+y)α+1​.
(12)

∬(0,∞)2​(1+x+y)αdxdy=(−α−1)(−α−2)1​,f∈L1⟺α<−2.​
(13)

A basis of a topology T is a collection B⊆T such that every open set is a union of members of B. Equivalently, for each open U and every x∈U, there exists B∈B with x∈B⊆U. The empty union represents the empty set. In particular, the basis covers X, and any intersection of two basis elements is itself a union of basis elements.
