Derivative seminorms on a compact interval (pk​(f)=max0≤j≤k​∥f(j)∥∞​)

Here C∞([0,1]) means the real- or complex-valued functions continuous on [0,1], smooth on (0,1), with every interior derivative having a continuous extension to the endpoints; endpoint values of derivatives are these extensions. On this space of smooth functions on a compact interval, define
pk​(f)=max0≤j≤k​supt∈[0,1]​∣f(j)(t)∣,k=0,1,2,…,f(0)=f.
(10)
These finite-valued seminorms increase with k and separate points. In fact every pk​ is a norm, since it includes p0​(f)=∥f∥∞​. The terminology seminorm includes norms; it does not assert that every measurement has a nontrivial kernel of a seminorm. Smooth convergence means pk​(fn​−f)→0 for every fixed k, equivalently uniform convergence of every derivative separately. It does not demand one tolerance or one convergence index that works for all orders simultaneously. The maximum convention is equivalent to the derivative supremum norm convention using a finite sum, since pk​(f)≤∑j=0k​∥f(j)∥∞​≤(k+1)pk​(f).
For a worked example, f(t)=t3 gives p0​(f)=1, p1​(f)=3, p2​(f)=6, and pk​(f)=6 for k≥3. The linear functional ℓ(f)=f′′(1/2) obeys ∣ℓ(f)∣≤p2​(f) and is continuous. Similarly, differentiation is a continuous linear operator D:f↦f′ for smooth convergence, because pk​(Df)≤pk+1​(f).
The space of smooth functions on a compact interval is complete for this seminorm topology. A Cauchy sequence has uniform limits gj​ for its derivatives of each order. Passing to the limit in the fundamental theorem of calculus identity
fn(j)​(t)−fn(j)​(0)=∫0t​fn(j+1)​(s)ds
(11)
gives gj​(t)−gj​(0)=∫0t​gj+1​(s)ds, so gj′​=gj+1​ and g0​ belongs to the same space. This is the completeness of the smooth-function metric argument; the metric there and the increasing-pk​ metric above induce the same smooth topology and the same Cauchy sequences.
