Both in-plane orientations minimize the one-site energy. Since every adsorption site is occupied, the sites label independent choices even though the molecules are chemically identical. The ground state of the whole system therefore has

For the same atoms in a three-dimensional volume V, the one-atom translational partition function is qtrans(3)​=V/Λ3. Comparing the same N atoms at the same temperature cancels the common N! in their canonical partition functions. Therefore the molar adsorption confinement free energy, with the sign defined as adsorbed minus free, is
ΔFmtrans​=−RTlnqtrans(3)​qtrans(2)​​=RTlnσΛV​.​
(12)
The logarithm is dimensionless: V/σ and Λ are both lengths. For a macroscopic gas depth V/σ≫Λ, confinement raises the translational Helmholtz free energy. Equivalently, the translational state count is reduced by a factor σΛ/V per atom. The question supplies neither a volume nor an adsorption binding energy, so this answer is symbolic and describes the confinement cost, not a numerical net adsorption free energy.
For example, a binding energy D>0 per atom would supply a separate −NA​D to ΔFm​, together with any normal-motion internal contribution retained by a more detailed model. Adsorption can then be favourable despite the loss of free translational phase space. Merely calling the change a loss must not reverse the sign of the explicitly defined Fads​−Fgas​.

A planar rigid rotor has angular eigenfunctions eimϕ and single-valuedness imposes m=0,±1,±2,…. Its energy depends on m2, so if the level is labelled by J=∣m∣, then
J=0,1,2,…;g0​=1,gJ​=2 (J≥1).​
(13)
The two states at J>0 represent opposite directions of angular momentum. If the signed quantum number itself is called J, each signed state is counted once instead; one must not both sum over both signs and multiply by two again.
For an unrestricted three-dimensional linear rigid rotor, J=0,1,2,… has M=−J,…,J and state degeneracy 2J+1, with EJ​=ℏ2J(J+1)/(2I). Planar confinement removes one rotational coordinate and changes both the spectrum and degeneracy. These are the spatial counts before nuclear spin statistics. For homonuclear hydrogen, the allowed even/odd spatial states must also be paired with the appropriate parahydrogen or orthohydrogen nuclear states, as considered explicitly in A8.

For the free electron model, the Fermi energy is the highest occupied one-electron energy at zero temperature, measured relative to the bottom of the free-electron band. The occupied wavevectors fill a disk in two dimensions or a sphere in three dimensions. The radius is the Fermi wavevector kF​, with
EF​=2me​ℏ2kF2​​.​
(29)
Each allowed wavevector has two spin states. At nonzero temperature, the Fermi-Dirac distribution rounds the occupation boundary; the zero-temperature Fermi energy remains a useful density-defined scale and should not be defined as the thermal energy kB​T.

ii OurBigBook.com
Metallic atomic densities place very many electrons in a small volume. The Pauli exclusion principle prevents all these fermions occupying the lowest spatial state, so filling the available states produces a Fermi energy of order electronvolts. At room temperature, kB​T≃0.026 eV, much smaller than that scale. Equivalently, ordinary metals are at T≪TF​=EF​/kB​, where TF​ is the Fermi temperature. Their electrons are quantum degenerate even at ordinary laboratory temperatures.

Substitutional arsenic has one more valence electron than germanium, so it acts as a donor impurity. Thermal ionization of its shallow bound state supplies mobile conduction-band electrons and leaves fixed positive donor charge. The resulting n-type semiconductor can have ne​≫ni​ even when only a small fraction of host atoms are replaced. Its electrical conductivity then rises because the mobile carrier count increases; the opposite donor charge preserves macroscopic charge neutrality.
This argument assumes that the donors are substantially ionized at the measurement temperature and that the added impurity scattering does not overwhelm the carrier increase. The screened bound-donor picture and its departures from a simple hydrogenic model are described in institutional semiconductor spectroscopy notes.[ref]

In a weakly doped n-type semiconductor, the mobile electrons occupy states near the bottom of the conduction band. Their local band curvature is positive, so their band effective mass is positive and approximately constant sufficiently near that minimum. For the usual β<0 convention, expand about k=0:
Ek​≃α+2β−βa2k2,me∗​≃2∣β∣a2ℏ2​>0.​
(56)
Stronger overlap gives larger ∣β∣, greater curvature and a smaller mass within this model. Positive β simply places the minimum at the zone boundary and again gives a positive edge mass. A negative electron mass near a band maximum instead motivates the positive-mass electron hole description; it is not the usual conduction-edge situation for an n-type semiconductor. No numerical comparison with me​ follows without values of a and β.
