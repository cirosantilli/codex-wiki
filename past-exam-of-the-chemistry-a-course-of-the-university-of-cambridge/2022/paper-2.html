Use the ideal Langmuir adsorption model: identical independent sites, at most one atom per site, no lateral interactions, and no additional thermally active adsorbed degrees of freedom beyond the two spin states. Treat the atoms as indistinguishable and localized after adsorption; only site occupancy and spin choices distinguish configurations.
There are (NM​) ways to select N occupied sites. For any such selection, independent spin sums contribute zN. Thus
QN​=(NM​)zN.​
(4)
With qads​=Mz as defined in part (b), this is
QN​=qadsN​f(M,N),f(M,N)=N!(M−N)!MNM!​,0≤N≤M.​
(5)
Equivalently, starting from labelled particles gives M!/(M−N)! distinct assignments to different sites; division by N! removes particle relabellings. The M−N removes the site multiplicity already included in each full one-particle function.
If qads​ denotes the one-site internal factor instead, f(M,N)=(NM​) and QN​=(NM​)qadsN​. These are identical physical results, not different counting models. In particular, the complete-function convention gives f(M,1)=1 and hence Q1​=qads​.

For monatomic species, there are no molecular rotational or vibrational factors. Assume excited electronic states are negligible, so the internal partition sums are the ground-state degeneracies. A free electron has two spin states, giving
fe​=2(h22πme​kB​T​)3/2,fM​=gM​(h22πmM​kB​T​)3/2,
(20)

(21)

Nuclear-spin factors may be omitted when they cancel between the atom and ion; they must not be counted on only one side.

In the orthogonal nearest-neighbour tight-binding model,
αs​=⟨sj​∣H∣sj​⟩,βs​=⟨sj​∣H∣sj+1​⟩.
(42)
The on-site integral αs​ sets the band centre, while the hopping integral βs​ describes coupling between neighbouring orbitals. With a consistent phase convention, the bonding combination at the zone centre has βs​<0.
For p orbitals directed along the chain, use
Ep​(k)=αp​+2βp​cos(ka),βp​>0.​
(43)
Here every positive p lobe points in the same spatial direction. Neighbouring equal-sign coefficients then bring opposite-sign facing lobes together and make the zone-centre combination antibonding; the alternating zone-boundary combination is bonding. Thus the usual axial p hopping has the opposite sign to s hopping. Orbital phase and the sign of one-dimensional hopping must be treated consistently; replacing only αs​ by αp​ while silently keeping the same physical hopping sign would give the wrong band ordering.

In a nondegenerate semiconductor, the Fermi-Dirac distribution reduces near the conduction edge to f(E)≃e−(E−EF​)/kB​T. The corresponding dilute hole probability near the valence edge gives
n=Nc​e−(Ec​−EF​)/kB​T,p=Nv​e−(EF​−Ev​)/kB​T.

Arsenic has five valence electrons, one more than silicon. As a substitutional donor impurity, it supplies a weakly bound extra electron which is largely thermally released into the conduction band at room temperature, leaving an ionized positive donor.
Consequently the mobile electron density increases far above the intrinsic value, so the conductivity increases substantially even at ppm dopant levels. The crystal remains macroscopically neutral: fixed donor charge compensates the added mobile charge.

There are three useful temperature regimes.
At low temperature, dopant freeze-out keeps most donor electrons bound. Warming ionizes donors and increases conductivity through the rising mobile-electron concentration. For an uncompensated dilute donor system, negligible holes and spin degeneracy two, charge neutrality with partial donor ionization gives
ND​−nn2​=2Nc​​e−Eb​/kB​T.
(62)
Deep in freeze-out, n≪ND​, so
n≃2ND​Nc​​​e−Eb​/(2kB​T).
(63)
The activation scale is set by the shallow donor binding, rather than the whole band gap.
In the extrinsic regime, donors are largely ionized and n≃ND​ is nearly constant. The temperature dependence of conductivity then mainly follows the electron mobility. Increasing phonon scattering can make conductivity fall as temperature rises, even though donors remain ionized; impurity scattering can give a different mobility trend at lower temperature.
At sufficiently high temperature, thermally generated electron–hole pairs overwhelm the dopant population. The intrinsic, gap-activated increase in conductivity returns. The boundaries depend on donor concentration, compensation and scattering, so no one exponential describes the entire doped-sample curve.

Warming thermally ionizes neutral donors, depleting the occupied bound ground states from which these absorption lines start. Increased scattering can also broaden the lines. Once the donors are predominantly ionized, their bound-to-bound absorption becomes weak or disappears.
The inferred binding provides the natural thermal scale:

This is not a sharp onset: significant ionization can start below that scale because free carriers have a large translational density of states. The precise loss of line strength requires donor density and compensation, as in part (ii), rather than just equating one energy with kB​T.

Gallium has three valence electrons, one fewer than silicon, and acts as an acceptor impurity. A neutral acceptor can be described as a negatively charged centre binding an electron hole. Thermal release of these holes gives a p-type semiconductor.
At low temperature, the bound hole can absorb far-IR photons in transitions between acceptor levels. The simplest scalar hydrogenic acceptor state model predicts
En​=−n2RA∗​​,RA∗​=RH​ϵr2​mh∗​/me​​,
(70)
so one expects a discrete bound-hole absorption series followed by a photoionization continuum. In that idealization, the first lines would be at 3RA∗​/4, 8RA∗​/9 and 15RA∗​/16, with threshold RA∗​.
The hole effective mass is not supplied, so the electron mass 0.2me​ cannot be reused to give numerical Ga line positions. In a real valence band, multiple hole bands, anisotropic masses and spin-orbit effects can split and complicate the series; acceptor spectra are generally less accurately described by one scalar hydrogenic ladder.[ref]
