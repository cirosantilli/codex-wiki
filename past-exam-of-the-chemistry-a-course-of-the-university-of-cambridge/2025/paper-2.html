The internal energy is the population-weighted sum of the molecular energy levels:
U=∑i​ni​εi​=qN​∑i​εi​e−εi​/kT.
(1)
At fixed volume, assume the energy levels themselves are temperature-independent. Differentiating the molecular partition function gives
(∂T∂q​)V​=kT21​∑i​εi​e−εi​/kT,U=NkT2∂T∂logq​​.
(2)
Choosing the zero of energy changes U by the corresponding constant per molecule, so the ground-level convention must be consistent throughout.

Relative to the ground state, the quantum harmonic oscillator levels are vhcω. Put θv​=hcω/k. Summing the geometric series gives the vibrational partition function q=(1−e−θv​/T)−1. Therefore the vibrational internal energy is
U=eθv​/T−1Nkθv​​​=ehcω/kT−1Nhcω​.

ii OurBigBook.com
“Low” and “high” compare kT with the vibrational spacing hcω, not temperature with an arbitrary number of kelvin. For T≪θv​, occupation of excited levels is exponentially suppressed and
U∼Nkθv​e−θv​/T⟶0​.
(4)
This vanishing result uses the ground-relative energy zero. For T≫θv​, expand eθv​/T−1∼θv​/T to obtain
U∼NkT​.
(5)
This agrees with equipartition: the oscillator has one quadratic kinetic and one quadratic potential term, contributing kT/2 each. The corresponding limiting vibrational heat capacity is zero at low temperature and Nk at high temperature.

For identical, non-interacting molecules, the translational canonical partition function is QN​=qtransN​/N!. Stirling's approximation gives the Helmholtz free energy
A=−NkT[log(qtrans​/N)+1].
(12)
At fixed V, qtrans​∝T3/2, so differentiation gives S=Nk[log(qtrans​/N)+5/2]. For one mole at the standard-state molar volume,
Sm,trans∘​=R[log(NA​qm,trans∘​​)+25​]​.
(13)
Here the quoted molar translational quantity is per mole, and its ratio to NA​ is dimensionless. The derivative defining entropy is taken at fixed volume before inserting the standard-state volume; differentiating along a constant-pressure path would give the wrong additive constant.

A transition state is the configuration or dividing-surface region associated with passage between reactant and product basins. In the elementary saddle-point picture it is a first-order saddle of the potential energy surface: stable against small displacements in transverse directions but unstable along the reaction direction. Transition state theory counts forward crossings and, in its simplest form, neglects recrossing.

d OurBigBook.com
For T≪TF​, only electrons within an energy window of order kT around the Fermi energy can change occupation appreciably, because of the Pauli exclusion principle. The active fraction is of order kT/EF​=T/TF​. Giving each of these electrons energy of order kT estimates an excitation energy U−U0​∼Ne​kT2/TF​, and therefore
CV​∼TF​2Ne​kT​​.
(31)
Per mole of conduction electrons, Ne​k=R, which is the quoted 2RT/TF​. Per mole of metal atoms with z model conduction electrons each, the conduction-electron count in the molar electronic heat capacity instead gives 2zRT/TF​. The numerical factor is only approximate: the Sommerfeld expansion gives CV​=(π2/2)Ne​kT/TF​ for a three-dimensional free-electron gas.

Lattice vibrations contribute a phonon heat capacity. At temperatures well below the Debye temperature, its leading contribution is proportional to T3, whereas the electronic part is proportional to T:
CV,m​≃γT+βT3​.

For an intrinsic, nondegenerate semiconductor, charge neutrality and the Boltzmann approximation to the Fermi-Dirac distribution give ni​=NC​NV​​exp[−Eg​/(2kT)]. Assume the two polymorphs have equal effective-density-of-states prefactors and retain the quoted band gaps at the temperature being estimated. Their carrier-density ratio is then
ni,α​ni,β​​≃exp(2kB​T3.4−3.2​),
(38)
with kB​ expressed in eV per kelvin. At 77 K,
ni,β​/ni,α​≃3.51×106​.
(39)
Both absolute intrinsic densities are extremely small at this temperature; a large ratio does not imply that either material is a good intrinsic conductor. Defects or unintended dopants can dominate real “undoped” specimens.

Suitable substitutional donors include Si on a Ga site and O on an N site. The former replaces a group-III atom with a group-IV atom; the latter replaces a group-V atom with a group-VI atom. Each provides an extra valence electron relative to its host site. Si is a plausible substitutional cation-site dopant, and the compact oxygen atom is compatible with an anion site. Site compatibility matters: merely choosing an element with an extra electron without considering which sublattice it occupies is insufficient.

A standard substitutional acceptor choice is Mg on a Ga site: a group-II atom replaces group III, leaving one fewer valence electron. Its size is sufficiently compatible with the cation sublattice to make substitution plausible. Solution

Words: 159
c OurBigBook.com
At equilibrium, electrons initially diffuse from the n side and holes from the p side. Recombination leaves ionized donors and acceptors in a depletion region, producing an internal electric field and band bending. The Fermi level is constant across the equilibrium p-n junction. With p material on the left, both band edges lie higher on the p side, as in the left panel.
Forward bias, with the p side positive relative to n, reduces the injection barrier. Electrons and holes enter the junction region and can recombine radiatively, emitting photons with energy close to the band gap. Under bias, separate electron and hole quasi-Fermi levels replace a single equilibrium Fermi level.
Both GaN polymorphs have a direct band gap, so a conduction-band electron and valence-band hole can recombine without a large crystal-momentum change. Silicon and germanium have indirect band gaps, requiring phonon assistance for near-edge radiative recombination, which makes efficient band-edge light emission much harder. A direct gap enables the process; defects, carrier confinement and competing nonradiative channels still determine device efficiency.

Solution

Words: 118
e OurBigBook.com
GaN has a substantial band-gap advantage for high-temperature operation. Its gap is larger than those of GaAs and GaP, suppressing thermally generated intrinsic carriers through ni​∝e−Eg​/(2kT). A doped junction therefore retains its intended carrier imbalance and can have lower thermally generated leakage over a wider temperature range, other factors being equal. The wide gap also supports emission at higher photon energies.
This is a materials-based expectation, not a guarantee for every finished device. Dopant activation and compensation, defects, contact stability, heat extraction and nonradiative recombination can set the practical limit. Increasing temperature also reduces carrier mobility and can broaden or quench light emission. The relevant advantage is reduced intrinsic excitation; a comparison of operating temperatures needs those other device properties too.
