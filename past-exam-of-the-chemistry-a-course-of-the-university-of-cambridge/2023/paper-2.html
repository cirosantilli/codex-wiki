Let εs​ be the energy of one adsorbed atom, using the same energy reference as the gas-phase value ε0​. For equivalent, noninteracting adsorption sites, choosing the occupied positions gives
Ω(M,N1​)=(N1​M​)=N1​!(M−N1​)!M!​.
(1)
Every configuration has energy N1​εs​, so the canonical lattice-gas partition function is
ZN1​​=(N1​M​)e−βN1​εs​​,β=kB​T1​.
(2)
There is no further 1/N1​! factor: the combinatorial choice already counts indistinguishable adsorbates correctly. The Helmholtz free energy, using Stirling's approximation for a macroscopic number of occupied and empty sites, is
As​≃N1​εs​+kB​T[N1​lnN1​+(M−N1​)ln(M−N1​)−MlnM].
(3)
Hence the adsorbed-phase chemical potential at fixed T,M is
μs​=(∂N1​∂As​​)T,M​=εs​+kB​TlnM−N1​N1​​=εs​+kB​Tln1−θ1​θ1​​.
(4)
The logarithm comes from configurational entropy: adding an atom is less favorable when vacancies are scarce. In the gas, the stipulated expression can be written
μg​=ε0​+kB​TlnkB​TpΛ3​,Λ=2πmkB​T​h​,
(5)
where Λ is the thermal de Broglie wavelength. Equality of chemical potentials at chemical equilibrium gives
1−θ1​θ1​​=kB​TpΛ3​e(ε0​−εs​)/(kB​T)=bp.
(6)
Therefore
θ1​=1+bpbp​​,b=kB​TΛ3​e(ε0​−εs​)/(kB​T)​.
(7)
The binding energy Eb​=ε0​−εs​ is positive for favorable binding. The units of b are inverse pressure, making bp dimensionless. Choosing the adsorbed energy as zero simply sets εs​=0 and leaves the same physics; it must not remove the relative binding energy. This is the Langmuir isotherm, with low-pressure linear uptake and high-pressure monolayer saturation.

For the uniform-binding multilayer adsorption model, θj​ describes sites of exactly height j, not the fraction whose height is at least j. At equilibrium the upward and downward fluxes on each adjacent pair of heights balance:
ka​pθj−1​=kd​θj​,j≥1.

iii OurBigBook.com
The model allows unlimited column height without weakening the binding or reducing the upward rate as a column grows. As bp→1−, successive heights become nearly equally probable and the mean height diverges. At bp=1, the putative height weights cannot even be normalized; for bp>1 they increase with height and no finite stationary uptake exists.
This is a condensation-like failure of the unbounded-layer idealization, rather than a monolayer reaching its capacity. Finite gas supply, finite geometrical capacity, altered layer binding and interactions can modify or limit the actual uptake. The divergent expression is an equilibrium result only below its convergence threshold.

Choose the ground-state energy as zero and write x=ε/(kB​T). The molecular partition function must sum individual states, so the triply degenerate excited level contributes three Boltzmann factors:

For an ideal mixture, the Helmholtz free energy is a sum over species. At fixed T,V and all other populations, the molecular partition function qi​ does not depend on Ni​. Differentiating the contribution written using each species' own energy reference gives
∂Ni​∂​[−Ni​kB​Tlnqi​+kB​T(Ni​lnNi​−Ni​)]=−kB​Tlnqi​+kB​TlnNi​.
(25)
The desired additive energy term requires a consistent energy reference in molecular partition functions. Let ε0,i be the energy per molecule of species i in its reference state, measured relative to a common chemical energy zero, and let qi​ sum excitations above that reference. Then
qiabs​=e−ε0,i/(kB​T)qi​,Ai​=Ni​ε0,i−Ni​kB​Tlnqi​+kB​T(Ni​lnNi​−Ni​).
(26)
Therefore
μi​=(∂Ni​∂A​)T,V,Nj=i​​=ε0,i−kB​TlnNi​qi​​​.
(27)
One convenient choice is each species' rovibrational ground state, so the molecular reference includes its zero-point energy. A different consistent reference is possible, but the corresponding partition function must change. The offset cannot arise from differentiation of a formula that has already omitted it, and it must not be counted twice. These chemical potentials are per particle; molar expressions instead use the molar gas constant.

The density of states D(E) counts available one-electron states: D(E)dE is the number with energies in [E,E+dE]. It describes the spectrum, irrespective of whether those states contain electrons.
The density of occupied states additionally weights each state by the Fermi-Dirac distribution:

Equal constant densities of states in broad, symmetric bands give equal populations when the distances Ec​−μ and μ−Ev​ are equal.
This is a statement about the chemical potential, not an available electron state in the gap. Unequal band densities of states would shift the intrinsic Fermi level away from exact midgap; the equality follows from the symmetry assumed here.

Arsenic has five valence electrons, whereas silicon has four. A substitutional arsenic atom can therefore supply an extra electron after participating in the silicon bonding network. It acts as a shallow donor impurity, introducing a localized level ED​ just below the conduction band. Ionizing that donor costs much less energy than excitation across the full band gap.
At the lowest temperatures, donor electrons remain bound and the valence band is filled. Mobile-carrier populations are negligible: this is dopant freeze-out. Initial heating releases donor electrons into the conduction band, producing a rapid rise in conductivity governed by the shallow-donor energy scale. The positive ionized donor charge compensates the released electronic charge; the bulk need not acquire net charge.
At intermediate temperatures, nearly all donors are ionized but intrinsic electron–hole generation is still small. In an uncompensated sample,
n≃ND​,p≪n,σ≃eND​ue​,
(77)
where ND​ is donor number density. The supply of donor electrons is finite, so further heating cannot keep creating them. This is the extrinsic plateau under the approximation of weakly varying mobility, and its carrier density is much larger than that of the undoped sample at the same temperature. In real material, scattering changes mobility, so donor exhaustion alone predicts a carrier-density plateau rather than guaranteeing perfectly flat conductivity.
At higher temperatures, intrinsic generation becomes comparable to and then exceeds the donor contribution. Once ni​≫ND​, the populations approach n≃p≃ni​, and activation across the main gap causes the steeper rise. The relevant regimes are therefore freeze-out, donor exhaustion, and intrinsic excitation, not three successive types of dopant.

The ambient-temperature Fermi level is raised into the upper part of the gap, toward the conduction-band edge, relative to intrinsic silicon. The sample is an n-type semiconductor: ionized donors supply many more conduction electrons than the intrinsic population.
For a nondegenerate, substantially ionized sample with negligible acceptors, n≃ND​. With effective density of states Nc​, the conduction-band relation gives
n=Nc​e−(Ec​−μ)/(kB​T),μ≃Ec​−kB​TlnND​Nc​​​.
(78)
Because a few ppm normally gives ND​≪Nc​ at ambient temperature, the chemical potential remains below the conduction-band edge rather than entering it as in a strongly degenerate semiconductor. Its precise position requires the dopant density, density of states and degree of ionization; the broad qualitative shift is fixed by donor doping.
It is not necessary for μ to lie between the donor level and the conduction edge. In a substantially ionized nondegenerate sample, the chemical potential can lie below the shallow donor level, as in the illustrative diagram. Donor ionization is determined by the level's occupation relative to μ; a finite-temperature Fermi level is not the highest filled one-electron level of every individual configuration.
