The Lax-Milgram theorem states that a bounded bilinear form a on a real Hilbert space V, satisfying a(v,v)≥α∥v∥V2​ for some α>0, has for each F∈V∗ a unique u∈V with a(u,v)=F(v) for all v. Moreover ∥u∥V​≤∥F∥/α.
For a compact operator K on a Hilbert space, the Fredholm alternative says that I−λK has closed range, finite-dimensional kernel and cokernel of equal dimension, and
Ran(I−λK)=ker(I−λK∗)⊥(λ∈R).
(11)
Thus a zero kernel gives unique solvability for every right-hand side; a nonzero kernel gives solvability exactly under the adjoint orthogonality conditions, and then all solutions differ by a kernel vector.
Take V=H01​(Ω). Gårding's inequality supplies constants a>0, b≥0 with B[v,v]≥a∥v∥H12​−b∥v∥22​. Therefore for λ≤−b the shifted form Bλ​[u,v]=B[u,v]−λ(u,v)L2​ is coercive. It is bounded for every fixed λ, and v↦(f,v) is bounded on V. Lax-Milgram gives unique weak solvability for λ≤−γ, with γ=b. The inequality itself follows, for example, by absorbing the drift cross-term ∥b∥∞​∥∇v∥2​∥v∥2​ using Young's inequality and allowing a lower L2 correction for c.
For the compact inverse and adjoint of a coercive elliptic operator, under the additional zero-shift coercivity assumption, define Gg∈V by B[Gg,v]=(g,v) for all v∈V, and let K be this solution viewed in L2. The solution operator is linear and bounded into V, and the Rellich-Kondrachov compact embedding V↪L2 makes K compact. Rewriting the weak equation as B[u,v]=(f+λu,v) gives
(I−λK)u=Kf.​
(12)
Conversely an L2 solution of this operator equation lies in V, because it equals K(f+λu), and satisfies the weak differential equation.
For constant drift coefficients and zero Dirichlet traces, integration by parts gives ∫bi​Di​vu=−∫bi​vDi​u. Symmetry of the principal coefficients therefore makes B∗[u,v]=B[v,u] the unshifted weak form of the displayed formal adjoint. The shifted adjoint equation requires
B∗[u,v]−λ(u,v)=(f,v)(v∈V).​
(13)
The spectral shift in an adjoint weak formulation is essential: the printed sentence omitting it is false for nonzero λ. For instance take a smooth ball, L=−Δ+1, any nonzero u∈Cc∞​(Ω) and f=−Δu+u. The printed unshifted identity holds, but (L∗−λ)u=f−λu=f whenever λ=0. This counterexample also meets zero-shift coercivity and constant-drift assumptions. The corrected identity above is used for the remainder of the argument.
Let G∗​g∈V solve B∗[G∗​g,v]=(g,v), which exists by Lax-Milgram because the diagonal coercivity is unchanged. For p,q∈L2,
(Kp,q)=B[Gp,G∗​q]=(p,G∗​q).
(14)
Thus G∗​, viewed as an L2 operator, is exactly K∗. Applying this inverse to the corrected shifted adjoint equation gives
(I−λK∗)u=K∗f,​
(15)
and the converse follows from its range lying in V, just as before.
Finally let the original homogeneous equation have a nonzero solution. Its parameter is nonzero, since the unshifted problem is coercive. Fredholm gives solvability precisely when (Kf,w)=0 for every w∈ker(I−λK∗). Such a w satisfies K∗w=w/λ, so (Kf,w)=(f,w)/λ. Hence the necessary and sufficient condition is
∫Ω​fwdx=0for every w∈H01​(Ω) satisfying (L∗−λ)w=0 weakly.​
(16)
When this holds, the solutions are an affine translate of the original finite-dimensional homogeneous solution space. The compatibility space is the adjoint kernel; a drift term does not generally permit replacing it by the original kernel.
