The weak derivative condition is
∫Ω​u∂i​ϕdx=−∫Ω​vϕdx(ϕ∈Cc∞​(Ω)).​
(1)
Both integrals exist since u,v are locally integrable and the test function has compact support. Derivatives are defined up to almost-everywhere equality. The space W1,p(Ω) consists of u∈Lp(Ω) whose first weak derivatives Di​u all lie in Lp(Ω). One standard norm is
∥u∥1,p​=(∥u∥pp​+∑i=1n​∥Di​u∥pp​)1/p(p<∞),∥u∥1,∞​=max(∥u∥∞​,∥D1​u∥∞​,…,∥Dn​u∥∞​).
(2)
A sum in the last formula gives an equivalent norm. For restriction to E, extend any ϕ∈Cc∞​(E) by zero to Ω. Its support is compactly contained in E, so the extension is smooth. The identity over Ω then reduces exactly to the identity over E, proving locality of the weak derivative.
For the jump contribution to a piecewise weak derivative, integration by parts on the two sides of the joining point gives the distributional derivative
Du=2x1(0,1)​+1(1,2)​+(a−1)δ1​.
(3)
The coefficient is the right trace 1+a minus the left trace 2. If a locally integrable weak derivative existed, locality on each open side would identify it almost everywhere with the displayed regular part. Substituting that into the global test identity would force (a−1)ϕ(1)=0 for every test function, hence a=1. Equivalently a nonzero Dirac point mass cannot be represented by a locally integrable derivative.
For a=1 the point-mass term vanishes, and both the function and its piecewise derivative are bounded on the finite interval. Thus u∈W1,∞ and hence in every listed W1,p. Therefore membership holds exactly for a=1. Changing a single point value is irrelevant to Sobolev membership; it cannot remove mismatched one-sided traces when a=1.

Weak convergence uj​⇀u means F(uj​)→F(u) for every F∈X∗. Weak-star convergence Tj​⇀∗T means Tj​(x)→T(x) for every x∈X, viewing X as the predual. The Sequential Banach-Alaoglu theorem for a separable predual says that every norm-bounded sequence in X∗ has a weak-star convergent subsequence. Separability is what turns the bounded weak-star compactness statement into this sequential form.
For the weak-star limit of periodic rescalings, apply it with X=L1(R), which is separable, and X∗=L∞(R). The rescaled functions have uniform essential-supremum bound ∥θ∥∞​. Let q=θ−θˉ and F(t)=∫0t​q(s)ds. Since q is one-periodic with zero mean, F is a bounded one-periodic absolutely continuous primitive. Thus for every finite interval,
∫ab​[θ(jx)−θˉ],dx=jF(jb)−F(ja)​⟶0.
(4)
Every weak-star subsequential limit h therefore satisfies ∫ab​(h−θˉ)=0 for all a<b. To apply the supplied uniqueness fact without assuming global integrability, fix R>0 and set v=(h−θˉ)1[−R,R]​. This is in L1(R), and its integral over any interval is zero, since intersecting with [−R,R] again gives an interval. Hence v=0 almost everywhere. Taking integer R→∞ yields h=θˉ almost everywhere on the whole line.
Finally, if the full sequence did not converge weak-star to this constant, some g∈L1 and some subsequence would have its pairing with θ(jx)−θˉ bounded away from zero. Sequential compactness gives a further weak-star convergent subsequence, whose limit must be the constant just identified, a contradiction. Therefore
θ(j⋅)⇀∗θˉin L∞(R).​
(5)
This is averaging against integrable test functions, not a claim of pointwise or strong norm convergence.

For a coercive Neumann energy with a positive reaction term, let ν>0 be the ellipticity constant and A0​=maxi,j​∥aij​∥∞​. Cauchy-Schwarz gives
∣B[u,v]∣≤nA0​∥∇u∥2​∥∇v∥2​+∥c∥∞​∥u∥2​∥v∥2​≤C∥u∥H1​∥v∥H1​.
(6)
Bilinearity follows from the integral and weak-derivative linearity. Ellipticity and the strictly positive zeroth-order coefficient give
B[u,u]≥ν∥∇u∥22​+μ∥u∥22​≥κ∥u∥H12​,κ=min(ν,μ)>0.​
(7)
This controls constants as well as gradients, so no zero-mean constraint or Neumann compatibility condition is required. Symmetry of the coefficients makes B symmetric.
Write F(v)=∫Ω​fv. This is a bounded linear functional on H1, with ∣F(v)∣≤∥f∥2​∥v∥H1​. Consequently
I(v)≥2κ​∥v∥H12​−∥f∥2​∥v∥H1​⟶+∞
(8)
as the norm tends to infinity. The infimum is finite, and every minimizing sequence is bounded. The Hilbert space H1 is reflexive, so a minimizing subsequence converges weakly to some u∈H1.
To verify lower semicontinuity directly, put wj​=vj​−u. Boundedness of B makes B[u,wj​]→0 under weak convergence, and symmetry gives
B[vj​,vj​]=B[u,u]+2B[u,wj​]+B[wj​,wj​]≥B[u,u]+o(1).
(9)
Also F(vj​)→F(u). Therefore I(u)≤liminfI(vj​), so the absolute minimum is attained.
For every h∈H1 the real function t↦I(u+th) has a minimum at zero. Its derivative there is B[u,h]−F(h), yielding the required weak equation for every h. If two weak solutions exist, their difference z satisfies B[z,h]=0 for all h; choose h=z and use coercivity to obtain z=0. In fact the weak equation also gives the exact energy identity
I(v)−I(u)=21​B[v−u,v−u]≥2κ​∥v−u∥H12​.​
(10)
Thus the minimizer and weak solution are both unique. The natural boundary condition is encoded by testing in the whole H1 space, without needing pointwise conormal derivatives for rough coefficients.

The Lax-Milgram theorem states that a bounded bilinear form a on a real Hilbert space V, satisfying a(v,v)≥α∥v∥V2​ for some α>0, has for each F∈V∗ a unique u∈V with a(u,v)=F(v) for all v. Moreover ∥u∥V​≤∥F∥/α.
For a compact operator K on a Hilbert space, the Fredholm alternative says that I−λK has closed range, finite-dimensional kernel and cokernel of equal dimension, and
Ran(I−λK)=ker(I−λK∗)⊥(λ∈R).
(11)
Thus a zero kernel gives unique solvability for every right-hand side; a nonzero kernel gives solvability exactly under the adjoint orthogonality conditions, and then all solutions differ by a kernel vector.
Take V=H01​(Ω). Gårding's inequality supplies constants a>0, b≥0 with B[v,v]≥a∥v∥H12​−b∥v∥22​. Therefore for λ≤−b the shifted form Bλ​[u,v]=B[u,v]−λ(u,v)L2​ is coercive. It is bounded for every fixed λ, and v↦(f,v) is bounded on V. Lax-Milgram gives unique weak solvability for λ≤−γ, with γ=b. The inequality itself follows, for example, by absorbing the drift cross-term ∥b∥∞​∥∇v∥2​∥v∥2​ using Young's inequality and allowing a lower L2 correction for c.
For the compact inverse and adjoint of a coercive elliptic operator, under the additional zero-shift coercivity assumption, define Gg∈V by B[Gg,v]=(g,v) for all v∈V, and let K be this solution viewed in L2. The solution operator is linear and bounded into V, and the Rellich-Kondrachov compact embedding V↪L2 makes K compact. Rewriting the weak equation as B[u,v]=(f+λu,v) gives
(I−λK)u=Kf.​
(12)
Conversely an L2 solution of this operator equation lies in V, because it equals K(f+λu), and satisfies the weak differential equation.
For constant drift coefficients and zero Dirichlet traces, integration by parts gives ∫bi​Di​vu=−∫bi​vDi​u. Symmetry of the principal coefficients therefore makes B∗[u,v]=B[v,u] the unshifted weak form of the displayed formal adjoint. The shifted adjoint equation requires
B∗[u,v]−λ(u,v)=(f,v)(v∈V).​
(13)
The spectral shift in an adjoint weak formulation is essential: the printed sentence omitting it is false for nonzero λ. For instance take a smooth ball, L=−Δ+1, any nonzero u∈Cc∞​(Ω) and f=−Δu+u. The printed unshifted identity holds, but (L∗−λ)u=f−λu=f whenever λ=0. This counterexample also meets zero-shift coercivity and constant-drift assumptions. The corrected identity above is used for the remainder of the argument.
Let G∗​g∈V solve B∗[G∗​g,v]=(g,v), which exists by Lax-Milgram because the diagonal coercivity is unchanged. For p,q∈L2,
(Kp,q)=B[Gp,G∗​q]=(p,G∗​q).
(14)
Thus G∗​, viewed as an L2 operator, is exactly K∗. Applying this inverse to the corrected shifted adjoint equation gives
(I−λK∗)u=K∗f,​
(15)
and the converse follows from its range lying in V, just as before.
Finally let the original homogeneous equation have a nonzero solution. Its parameter is nonzero, since the unshifted problem is coercive. Fredholm gives solvability precisely when (Kf,w)=0 for every w∈ker(I−λK∗). Such a w satisfies K∗w=w/λ, so (Kf,w)=(f,w)/λ. Hence the necessary and sufficient condition is
∫Ω​fwdx=0for every w∈H01​(Ω) satisfying (L∗−λ)w=0 weakly.​
(16)
When this holds, the solutions are an affine translate of the original finite-dimensional homogeneous solution space. The compatibility space is the adjoint kernel; a drift term does not generally permit replacing it by the original kernel.

Banach's fixed-point theorem states that a map T:Y→Y on a nonempty complete metric space with d(Tx,Ty)≤qd(x,y) for a constant 0≤q<1 has a unique fixed point, approached from every starting point by iteration.
Choose x0​ and let xn+1​=Txn​. Induction gives d(xn+1​,xn​)≤qnd(x1​,x0​). For m>n, the triangle inequality and geometric series give
d(xm​,xn​)≤1−qqn​d(x1​,x0​).
(17)
Thus the sequence is Cauchy and converges by completeness to some x∗​. The contraction estimate implies continuity, so Tx∗​=limTxn​=limxn+1​=x∗​. If y∗​ were another fixed point, d(x∗​,y∗​)≤qd(x∗​,y∗​) would force equality of the points. Passing m→∞ also yields the quantitative error estimate
d(xn​,x∗​)≤1−qqn​d(x1​,x0​).​
(18)

A weak solution belongs to V=H01​(Ω) and satisfies
∫Ω​∇u⋅∇hdx=∫Ω​f(u)hdx(h∈V),​
(19)
with the nonlinear right-hand side defining a bounded functional on V. Under the stated global Lipschitz hypothesis this requirement is automatic: ∣f(s)∣≤∣f(0)∣+ϵ∣s∣ and boundedness of Ω give f(u)∈L2 whenever u∈V. Continuity of f alone, without growth control, would not guarantee this for an arbitrary Sobolev function.
For the semilinear Poisson contraction threshold, use the gradient norm on V, and let CP​ satisfy the Poincaré inequality ∥v∥2​≤CP​∥∇v∥2​. For g∈L2, Lax-Milgram gives the zero-Dirichlet Poisson solution Pg∈V. Testing with Pg yields ∥∇Pg∥2​≤CP​∥g∥2​. Define T(v)=P(f(v)), a self-map of the complete space V. For v,w∈V,
∥T(v)−T(w)∥V​≤CP​∥f(v)−f(w)∥2​≤ϵCP2​∥v−w∥V​.
(20)
Therefore ϵCP2​<1 is a sufficient condition for Banach's theorem to give a unique fixed point and hence a unique weak solution. There is no need to assume f(0)=0 or to restrict to a small ball: the Lipschitz bound makes the map defined on all of V. The same estimates give
∥∇u∥2​≤1−ϵCP2​CP​∣Ω∣1/2∣f(0)∣​.
(21)
With the optimal Poincaré constant, the threshold is ϵ<λ1​ for the First Dirichlet eigenvalue; the stated sufficient condition does not depend on knowing that optimal constant.

For a uniform contraction from a comparison function on a bounded set, the closed set M is complete and has diameter at most 2R. Suppose first R>0. The limit condition supplies r0​>0 and q0​<1 such that ψ(r)/r≤q0​ for 0<r≤r0​. On the remaining compact interval [r0​,2R], if nonempty, the continuous ratio ψ(r)/r attains a maximum q1​<1, since it is strictly below one at every point. Thus there is one q<1 with ψ(r)≤qr for every distance 0≤r≤2R arising in M.
Consequently d(Tx,Ty)≤qd(x,y) throughout M. Banach's theorem applies and gives a unique fixed point in M. No linear structure or compactness of the ball is used; the compact object in the argument is only the scalar distance interval. If the closed ball is the singleton at radius zero, its self-map fixes that point. The estimate on the ratio near zero is what prevents the compact-interval supremum argument from approaching one at its missing endpoint.

Brouwer's fixed-point theorem says that every continuous self-map of a nonempty closed Euclidean ball has a fixed point. It does not promise convergence of repeated application. For example f(x)=1−x with starting point x1​=0 gives the nonconvergent alternating sequence 0,1,0,1,…, although 1/2 is a fixed point.
For an invariant ball from sublinear growth, choose
R≥max{(2A)1/(1−α),2B}.
(22)
Then ARα≤R/2 and B≤R/2. For every ∣x∣≤R the given estimate yields ∣f(x)∣≤ARα+B≤R. Thus f restricts to a continuous self-map of the closed ball of this radius, and Brouwer gives a fixed point. The exponent α<1 ensures that the linear radius eventually dominates its sublinear growth; no differentiability or contraction estimate is needed.

One form of Schauder's theorem says that if M is a nonempty closed bounded convex subset of a Banach space and T:M→M is continuous with relatively compact image, then T has a fixed point.
For a Schauder solution of a small Hammerstein integral equation, take X=C([0,1]) with the supremum norm and M={u:∥u∥∞​≤R}. This set is nonempty, closed, bounded and convex. Put
FR​=max0≤y≤1, ∣z∣≤R​∣f(y,z)∣,G0​=maxx,y∈[0,1]​∣G(x,y)∣,μ0​=1+G0​FR​R​>0.
(23)
Both maxima are finite by continuity on compact sets. Define (Tμ​u)(x)=μ∫01​G(x,y)f(y,u(y))dy. Continuity and uniform continuity of the kernel give Tμ​u∈X, and for 0<μ≤μ0​,
∥Tμ​u∥∞​≤μG0​FR​≤R,
(24)
so it is a self-map. This choice of μ0​ also handles the case when either maximum is zero without division by zero.
If un​→u uniformly in M, uniform continuity of f on [0,1]×[−R,R] makes f(⋅,un​(⋅))→f(⋅,u(⋅)) uniformly. The integral estimate then proves continuity of Tμ​ in the supremum norm. Moreover
∣(Tμ​u)(x)−(Tμ​u)(x′)∣≤μFR​supy​∣G(x,y)−G(x′,y)∣,
(25)
which tends to zero with ∣x−x′∣ uniformly in u∈M. Thus the image is uniformly bounded and equicontinuous. Arzelà-Ascoli states that such a family on a compact interval has compact closure in the supremum norm. Hence Tμ​(M) is relatively compact, and Schauder gives a fixed point for each μ in the specified interval. Therefore a continuous solution with supremum norm at most R exists. The argument imposes no Lipschitz hypothesis on f and makes no uniqueness assertion.

Using the dual pairing, monotonicity means
⟨A(u)−A(v),u−v⟩≥0(u,v∈X).​
(26)
Strict monotonicity requires strict positivity whenever u=v. Coercivity with respect to zero, in the growth sense used for monotone-operator existence theory, means
∥v∥X​⟨A(v),v⟩​⟶+∞as ∥v∥X​→∞.​
(27)
This is a condition on all sequences escaping to infinity, not just each fixed direction with a bound depending on that direction. Strict monotonicity concerns pairs of distinct arguments; it does not by itself imply this uniform large-norm growth.

For the log-cosh energy on a Sobolev space, the elementary inequalities 0≤logcosht≤t2/2 follow by integrating ∣tanht∣≤∣t∣ from zero. Thus
0≤I(v)≤21​(∥∇v∥22​+∥v∥22​)<∞,
(28)
so the energy is well-defined on H1. For a fixed v, its proposed derivative is linear in the direction h and bounded, because ∣tanhv∣≤∣v∣ gives
∣∂h​I(v)∣≤∥∇v∥2​∥∇h∥2​+∥v∥2​∥h∥2​≤∥v∥H1​∥h∥H1​.
(29)
To justify differentiation, use (logcosht)′′=sech2t∈(0,1]. Taylor's integral remainder pointwise, combined with the exact quadratic gradient expansion, gives
0≤I(v+h)−I(v)−∫Ω​(∇v⋅∇h+tanh(v)h)dx≤21​∥h∥H12​.
(30)
Every term is integrable by the displayed bounds. The remainder is quadratic in the norm, so the functional is even Fréchet differentiable, and in particular Gâteaux differentiable with the required derivative. This also proves that A(v) belongs to the stated dual space.
For v,w∈H1,
⟨A(v)−A(w),v−w⟩=∥∇(v−w)∥22​+∫Ω​[tanhv−tanhw](v−w)dx.
(31)
Both terms are nonnegative, and the integrand in the second is strictly positive wherever v=w, since tanh is strictly increasing. Distinct Sobolev elements differ on a set of positive measure, so the expression is positive. Hence A is strictly monotone.
This gives strict monotonicity without coercivity on H1: it is not coercive on H1. For constant functions vt​=t with t→+∞, the norm is t∣Ω∣1/2 and
∥vt​∥H1​⟨A(vt​),vt​⟩​=∣Ω∣1/2tanht⟶∣Ω∣1/2,
(32)
which is finite. In contrast it is coercive on H01​: if ∥v∥2​≤CP​∥∇v∥2​, then
⟨A(v),v⟩=∥∇v∥22​+∫Ω​vtanhvdx≥1+CP2​∥v∥H12​​.
(33)
Dividing by the norm proves growth to infinity. On this smaller space the operator is understood by restricting its dual action to zero-trace test functions. Removing the constant directions by the boundary condition is what restores coercivity.

For the log-cosh obstacle variational inequality, the admissible set is convex: for any v∈M, u+t(v−u) remains above the obstacle and in H01​ for 0≤t≤1. Minimality and the right derivative at zero therefore give
∫Ω​∇u⋅∇(v−u)+tanh(u)(v−u)dx≥0(v∈M).​
(34)
The one-sided sign comes from admissibility, which permits motion toward every v∈M but does not permit an arbitrary negative variation at a contact point.
Now fix ϕ∈Cc∞​(D). Smoothness on D and continuity of the obstacle give a strictly positive minimum of u−g on the compact support of ϕ. Hence u+tϕ is admissible for both signs of sufficiently small t. Applying the inequality to both variations yields
∫D​∇u⋅∇ϕ+tanh(u)ϕdx=0.
(35)
Integration by parts gives −Δu+tanhu=0 distributionally on D, and smoothness makes it pointwise. Thus
−Δu+tanhu=0where u>g.​
(36)
On the contact set the variational inequality, rather than unrestricted stationarity, remains the governing statement. Convexity of the energy also makes the inequality sufficient for minimization; strict monotonicity implies at most one minimizer, although existence was already stipulated in this part.
