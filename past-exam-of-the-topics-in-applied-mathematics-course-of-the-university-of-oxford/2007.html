iv OurBigBook.com
At low density the per-generation growth factor is approximately α/β. The power γ controls how sharply crowding reduces recruitment or survival when resources become limiting; at high density F(u)∼αu1−γ. Larger γ therefore means a stronger overcompensation in a population model.
The flip threshold says that sufficiently strong density feedback can destabilize a constant positive biological population and create alternating generations, under the usual nondegeneracy conditions. A local loss of fixed-point stability alone does not establish chaos, nor does it prove every ecological system with stronger crowding must follow the same route.

At a differentiable fixed point, linear perturbations advance by the Jacobian matrix. A hyperbolic source fixed point of a map has both eigenvalues of modulus greater than one, so nearby perturbations expand under forward iteration of a map. Real signs can cause alternating directions and a complex pair can cause spiral expansion. Eigenvalues on the unit circle are excluded from this linear classification.

ii OurBigBook.com
A hyperbolic sink fixed point of a map has both Jacobian eigenvalues strictly inside the unit circle. Its iterates locally attract nearby initial states; complex fixed-point multipliers can make the approach spiral. Zero fixed-point multipliers are strongly contracting and can occur for noninvertible maps. A fixed-point multiplier of modulus one requires nonlinear or exact analysis.

A hyperbolic saddle fixed point of a map has one eigenvalue of modulus less than one and one greater than one. The corresponding stable and unstable directions persist locally as invariant sets or manifolds under the usual smoothness and invertibility assumptions. For a noninvertible map a zero stable fixed-point multiplier is possible, and the word saddle fixed point of a map describes forward contraction in one direction and expansion in the other; backward dynamics may not be unique.

On the invariant biological population interval 0≤x≤1, the fixed points are 0 and x∗​=1−1/μ when meaningful. The zero fixed-point multiplier is μ: extinction attracts for 0≤μ<1. At μ=1, the nonzero branch crosses the origin in the full real-line family, giving a transcritical bifurcation. The nonzero point lies in the biological population interval for μ>1 and has fixed-point multiplier 2−μ, so it is stable for 1<μ<3 and loses stability through −1 at μ=3.
Factoring F2(x)−x and removing the fixed points gives the two-cycle
x±​=2μμ+1±(μ−3)(μ+1)​​,μ>3.​

F′(x+​)F′(x−​)=−μ2+2μ+4.​

It lies between −1 and 1 exactly for 3<μ<1+6​. At the upper endpoint it crosses −1, creating the next period doubling. The two-cycle amplitude tends to zero like μ−3​ at its birth, so the first flip is supercritical. At the equality endpoints linear fixed-point multipliers alone do not establish asymptotic stability.
Period-doubling cascade universality concerns the continuation of this period-doubling cascade in smooth unimodal maps with a quadratic maximum. After suitable rescalings their repeated two-step maps approach the same limiting shape; successive parameter spacings have the Feigenbaum ratio approximately 4.6692 and the spatial rescaling has magnitude approximately 2.5029. These are asymptotic statements involving later doublings, not numbers that can be established from the first two thresholds alone. The accumulation near μ≃3.56995 is outside the first interval considered here. One should not claim chaos throughout the stable two-cycle interval.
Four useful dynamical properties to distinguish are sensitivity to initial conditions, topological transitivity or mixing, dense periodic points, and aperiodic orbits with persistent complex evolution. They concern the invariant set, not an assertion that every orbit must be aperiodic. A positive Lyapunov exponent for typical initial data is an additional quantitative diagnostic.
At μ=4, set x0​=sin2θ0​. The double-angle identity gives the exact solution
xn​=sin2(2nθ0​),θ0​=arcsinx0​​.​

T(y)={2y,2(1−y),​0≤y≤1/2,1/2≤y≤1.​

Every branch doubles interval length until it crosses a fold; the finite binary cylinders have length 2−n and map onto the whole interval after n steps. Thus every open interval eventually has an iterate covering the full interval: the map is topologically mixing and sensitive. Repeating a finite left/right branch word gives a periodic itinerary; arbitrarily long prescribed prefixes locate such periodic points in arbitrarily small cylinders, proving density. Nonperiodic binary itineraries yield uncountably many aperiodic orbits. Conjugacy transfers these properties to F4​, proving chaotic behaviour while retaining its dense special periodic orbits.
As a metric check, its invariant measure has density 1/[πx(1−x)​] and its typical Lyapunov exponent is log2>0. This is a typical-orbit assertion, not a claim about the derivative along every exceptional critical or preperiodic orbit.
