Let A=A0​ be maintained constant, and let DX​,DY​>0 be the diffusion coefficients. Xt​=DX​Xxx​−2k1​X2+k2​A0​X−k3​XY+k4​A0​Y,Yt​=DY​Yxx​−k3​XY−k4​A0​Y.​

A diffusion-driven instability means that the homogeneous reaction equilibrium is asymptotically stable against spatially uniform perturbations, but adding diffusion makes at least one admissible spatially nonuniform mode grow. Thus the eigenvalues of J must have negative real parts, while J−k2D has an eigenvalue with positive real part for some positive Neumann Laplacian eigenvalue k2 on the actual domain.

ϕnx​,ny​​=cos(nx​πx/Lx​)cos(ny​πy/Ly​),nx​,ny​∈{0,1,2,…}.

Domain geometry can select biological pattern dimension even when the same reaction parameters and diffusion coefficients are used. A sufficiently wide developing tissue can admit instabilities with variation in two spatial directions; nonlinear development may produce spots, labyrinths or other two-dimensional patterns. A thin strip suppresses transverse unstable modes, allowing primarily bands whose concentration varies along its long axis and is nearly uniform across its width.
This is a mechanism for shape-dependent pattern selection during growth: increasing tissue width can admit additional transverse modes without changing the local chemical interactions. Linear instability predicts initial wavelengths and permitted directions, not the eventual stable spot or stripe pattern; nonlinear saturation and boundary geometry determine that later outcome. Zero-flux boundaries also mean these modes arise through internal redistribution and reaction rather than externally prescribed chemical concentrations.

Let F(x,y)=(y,x−Ωy−x3−x2y). Every term changes sign when both variables change sign, so F(−x,−y)=−F(x,y). Consequently the derivative of (−x(t),−y(t)) equals F(−x(t),−y(t)), proving the stated rotational symmetry.
This is equivariance under a half-turn, not time reversibility. Reversibility of an autonomous differential equation requires a reverser R satisfying F(Rz)=−DR(z)F(z), so that Rz(−t) is a solution. The half-turn R=−I would require F(−z)=F(z), the opposite of what holds. The usual mechanical candidate R(x,y)=(x,−y) also fails when damping is present: its second equation would require (Ω+x2)y=−(Ω+x2)y identically. The symmetry relates two orbits with the same direction of time, and does not reverse their arrows.

First assume ω0​=0 and regard θ modulo 2π. The section θ=0 has return time T=2π/∣ω0​∣. Solving the resonantly forced oscillator from this section gives
x(t)=x0​cos(ω0​t)+ω0​y0​​sin(ω0​t)+2ω0​γ​tsin(ω0​t).
(26)
At t=T, the sine vanishes and the cosine is one, while differentiating the secular term adds γT/2 to y. Hence the Poincaré map is
P(x,y)=(x,y+γT/2).​
(27)
For γ>0, every iterate has yn​=y0​+nγT/2→∞. Thus every oscillator trajectory is unbounded, regardless of its initial amplitude. The zero-frequency case allowed by the printed parameter range is different. If ω0​=0, then θ=θ0​ is constant and x=x0​+y0​t+21​γcosθ0​t2. There is no phase-crossing Poincaré section. One explicit future itinerary is obtained by concatenating all binary words, ordered first by length and then lexicographically:
0100011011000001010011100101110111⋯.​
(33)
Extend it arbitrarily to negative indices, for example by zeros. Every finite binary block occurs in its forward itinerary, and indeed occurs arbitrarily far out as a subword of longer words. Its shift orbit is therefore dense in the full two-symbol shift space. It is not eventually periodic: arbitrarily long runs of zeros and ones occur, together with all other finite words.
The full shift has sensitive dependence because sequences agreeing on an arbitrarily large initial window can differ just beyond it, and shifting brings that disagreement into view. Thus this itinerary supplies a dense aperiodic orbit in the chaotic symbolic system assumed by the question. A merely nonrepeating sequence would not be enough: restricted aperiodic sequences can have much simpler, nonchaotic orbit closures. Nor does symbolic description alone assign a numerical Lyapunov exponent without information about the underlying map and its coding.

The Lorenz vector field has constant divergence ∇⋅F=−σ−1−b. Liouville formula for a fundamental matrix for the derivative of the flow therefore gives
detDΦt​=e−(σ+1+b)t,V(t)=V(0)e−(σ+1+b)t.​
(34)
This concerns transported three-dimensional phase volume, not the Euclidean distance between each pair of trajectories.
A quasiperiodic orbit with the usual smooth two-frequency torus closure would lie on an invariant embedded two-torus. That torus bounds a solid torus of positive finite volume in R3. Since the vector field is tangent to its boundary, its outward flux is zero, contradicting the divergence theorem, whose volume integral is −(σ+1+b)V<0. This negative-divergence obstruction to invariant tori excludes such quasiperiodic tori. The argument uses global negative divergence and the enclosed volume; saying only that an attracting torus has zero volume would not exclude it.
At an equilibrium the sum of eigenvalues is −(σ+1+b)<0, so they cannot all have positive real parts: no equilibrium is a fully repelling source. For a periodic orbit of period T, one Floquet multiplier equals one in the flow direction. The product of the other two is e−(σ+1+b)T<1, so they cannot both have modulus greater than one: no periodic orbit is fully repelling either. Saddle equilibria and saddle periodic orbits remain possible; contraction of volume does not imply contraction in every direction.

A useful operational definition of chaos for a deterministic flow is bounded, nonperiodic long-time dynamics on an invariant set with sensitive dependence on initial conditions: arbitrarily close starting states can separate by a fixed macroscopic amount. Boundedness distinguishes recurrent irregular dynamics from simple exponential escape; aperiodicity excludes a limit cycle; sensitivity distinguishes chaotic unpredictability from a regular quasiperiodic signal. These are properties of the asymptotic dynamics, not a short irregular transient. One precise topological convention, Devaney chaos on a compact invariant set, instead specifies topological transitivity, dense periodic orbits, and sensitive dependence. Transitivity expresses indecomposability of the recurrent motion, while dense periodic orbits mean every region contains unstable recurrent structure. The convention should be stated because these formulations are not identical in complete generality.
An attractor is an invariant set approached by trajectories from a neighbourhood or positive-measure basin, according to the adopted attractor convention. Calling it strange refers to irregular, typically fractal geometry rather than a smooth point, cycle or torus. Strangeness alone does not force chaos. Strange nonchaotic attractors provide counterexamples: geometrically irregular attracting sets can lack positive exponential separation. Primary examples appear in [ref]. This geometric convention does not assert that every such example occurs in the autonomous three-variable Lorenz family.
Two complementary numerical diagnostics are to estimate the largest Lyapunov exponent and to construct a return map. For the first, integrate the variational equation η˙​=DF(x(t))η alongside the Lorenz trajectory, repeatedly normalize η, and average the logarithms of the accumulated stretch factors after discarding a transient. A positive converged exponent suggests sensitive exponential separation; the full spectrum should include a zero flow exponent and have sum −(σ+1+b), providing checks. For the second, record transverse crossings of a Poincaré section or successive maxima of a state variable, then look for the stretching and folding of an aperiodic return set rather than one point or a finite cycle. Nearby-orbit separation or a broadband power spectrum can add evidence, but plots alone do not prove chaos. Repeat with smaller integration step, longer runs and several initial conditions to distinguish numerical error, long transients and coexistence of attractors from persistent dynamics.
