Newton's first law (Fnet​=0⟹v=v0​)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Classical mechanics Force Newton's laws of motion
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 3
In an inertial frame, a body with zero net external force remains at rest or moves with constant velocity. For a point mass of constant positive mass, write the net external force as the vector sum of all external forces on that body:
Fnet​=∑i​Fi​=0⟹a=dtdv​=0,v(t)=v0​,r(t)=r0​+v0​(t−t0​).
(10)
Here r0​ and v0​ are the position and velocity at time t0​. The zero net external force must hold throughout the interval, not merely at one instant. Both the magnitude and direction of the velocity stay fixed: constant speed along a curved trajectory still involves acceleration. Rest is the case v0​=0. For an extended closed system, the analogous statement concerns its center of mass; zero net external force need not prevent its parts from moving relative to one another.
The short mathematical argument is to use Newton's second law with m>0, then take the integral of dv/dt=0 and of dr/dt=v0​. This gives the two constants fixed by the initial position and velocity. The physical content also includes the existence of inertial frames in which these relations apply; an arbitrary accelerating reference frame need not show constant velocity for a body with zero actual net external force.
The useful question is therefore which forces change the motion, rather than which force keeps it going. A free-body diagram may show several nonzero forces whose vector sum is zero. Equal opposing forces on the same body can balance; a third-law interaction pair acts on different bodies and cannot be cancelled within that one body's diagram.
In 1687, Isaac Newton stated Law I in Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica: a body's rest or uniform straight motion persists unless impressed forces compel a change. The following paragraph identifies air resistance and gravity as influences on the trajectory of a thrown body. The vector equations above are a modern expression of the law, not a quotation of his notation.[ref]
