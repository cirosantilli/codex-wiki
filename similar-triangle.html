Similar triangle (△ABC∼△A′B′C′)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Geometry and topology Euclidean geometry Triangle
OurBigBook.com Words: 323
Two triangles are similar if a Euclidean similarity maps one onto the other. Their corresponding angles agree, and corresponding side lengths have a common positive ratio.
Specify the correspondence first: A matches A′, B matches B′ and C matches C′. For similar triangles, the common scale factor k>0 satisfies
∣AB∣∣A′B′∣​=∣BC∣∣B′C′∣​=∣CA∣∣C′A′∣​=k.
(24)
The ratios compare matching sides in the same order. A Euclidean similarity can also move, turn or reflect the triangle; its orientation on the page is irrelevant. In the Euclidean plane, matching just two interior angles is enough: their sums force the third to match, and equal corresponding angles force the side ratios to agree. This is the angle-angle (AA) criterion for similar triangles. Matching just one angle is insufficient.
Scale the right triangle with vertices (0,0), (4,0) and (0,3) by k=2. Its new vertices are (0,0), (8,0) and (0,6), and its side lengths are 6,8,10. The matching side ratios are 6/3=8/4=10/5=2. Both triangles have a right angle and the same two other interior angles, approximately 36.87∘ and 53.13∘. However, their areas are 3⋅4/2=6 and 6⋅8/2=24: doubling lengths multiplies area by four.
Generally, a Euclidean similarity multiplies both a base and its perpendicular height by k, so the area becomes 21​(kb)(kh)=k221​bh. This explains why an enlarged drawing needs four times as much surface when each length doubles. It also enables indirect measurement: under assumptions of level ground, vertical objects and parallel light rays, an object of height 3 casting a shadow of length 4 provides a right triangle. Another object's shadow of length 8 gives a similar triangle and height 3(8/4)=6. The shared angles justify the inference; visual resemblance alone does not.
Euclid's Elements VI, Definition 1 describes similarity using corresponding angles and proportional sides. VI.4 establishes the implication from matching angles to matching side ratios, while VI.19 states the squared relation between side ratios and areas. These are documented ancient formulations of the principles used above, without establishing a first discoverer.[ref][ref][ref]
