Metric embedding (f:(X,dX​)↪(Y,dY​))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Functional analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 33
A metric embedding represents the points of one metric space (X,dX​) inside another (Y,dY​) by an injective function f:X→Y. The geometric question is how faithfully the representation preserves pairwise distances. For example, binary strings can be treated as vectors so that tools from linear algebra become available, but the chosen norm determines what their separation means. A drawing of the points alone does not specify a metric embedding: both metrics matter.
An isometric embedding preserves every distance exactly. A metric embedding that is a bi-Lipschitz map allows multiplicative error while keeping its bi-Lipschitz distortion bounded over all pairs. A coarse embedding instead controls separation through possibly nonlinear functions of distance. These are different requirements: the Hamming cube as an L1 and L2 metric below preserves Hamming distance exactly in one representation and takes its square root in another.
The usefulness is that a complicated distance comparison becomes a calculation with familiar vectors and norms. This also helps assess a visualization: nearby points should not acquire arbitrarily large separations, and far-apart points should not collapse together. Matoušek's 2013 lecture notes describe how motivations from computer science, especially approximation algorithms, accelerated the study of metric embeddings from the 1990s onward. [ref]

Bi-Lipschitz distortion (dist(f)=b/a)

Words: 492 Articles: 1
Metric embedding OurBigBook.com
For an injective function between metric spaces with at least two source points, consider all distance ratios
rf​(x,y)=dX​(x,y)dY​(f(x),f(y))​,x=y.
(1)
If constants 0<a≤b<∞ satisfy
a≤rf​(x,y)≤b(x=y),
(2)
then f is a bi-Lipschitz map: both f and its inverse on f(X) are Lipschitz maps. The bound b/a compares the most expanded pair with the most contracted pair. To define the bi-Lipschitz distortion of this particular metric embedding, use the sharp bounds a=infx=y​rf​(x,y) and b=supx=y​rf​(x,y):
dist(f)=ab​=Lip(f)Lip(f−1)≥1.
(3)
Here the Lipschitz constant of f−1:f(X)→X is 1/a. If no positive finite bounds exist, the bi-Lipschitz distortion is infinite. For a finite source, every injective function has finite bi-Lipschitz distortion, but its size can depend on the source. Matoušek gives the equivalent definition using one scale factor and a multiplicative error bound. [ref]
Changing units is harmless: replacing dY​ by λdY​, with λ>0, multiplies both a and b by λ and leaves b/a unchanged. In a normed vector space this can be achieved by multiplying every output vector by λ. Thus bi-Lipschitz distortion one means that all distances change by the same factor; an isometric embedding additionally requires that factor to be one. A stretch factor with a specified normalization is a related quantity; when comparing sources, check which overall scale has been fixed.
Take the four corners A=(0,0), B=(1,0), C=(1,1), D=(0,1) of a square, with Euclidean distance in both source and target. For the linear map f(x,y)=(2x,y), the six unordered pairs exhaust the possibilities: the two vertical sides AD,BC have ratio 1, the two horizontal sides AB,CD have ratio 2, and the two diagonals AC,BD have ratio 5​/2​=5/2​. Consequently a=1, b=2, and dist(f)=2. In contrast, g(x,y)=(3x,3y), three times the identity function, gives ratio 3 for every pair and has bi-Lipschitz distortion 3/3=1.
The ratio chart makes the reason visible: a common multiplier moves every ratio together, while unequal coordinate scaling spreads the ratios apart. More generally, in a normed vector space, the ratio bounds give
b/a1​dX​(x,y)≤​bf(x)​−bf(y)​​≤dX​(x,y).
(4)
This normalization gives Lipschitz constant one and inverse Lipschitz constant b/a. It is only a change of scale, so it cannot improve the bi-Lipschitz distortion.
The best possible bi-Lipschitz distortion of a source into a specified target is a separate quantity:
cY​(X)=inff:X↪Y​dist(f).
(5)
The infimum runs over all injective functions, and need not be attained. The notation cp​(X) means the analogous infimum over metric embeddings into an Lp space. A calculation for one chosen f gives an upper bound for cY​(X); proving optimality also requires a lower bound for every possible f.

Stretch factor (t(f)=supx=y​dX​(x,y)/dY​(f(x),f(y)))

Words: 81
Bi-Lipschitz distortion OurBigBook.com
For an injective function that does not increase distances, one stretch-factor convention measures the largest compression that must be undone:
t(f)=supx=y​dY​(f(x),f(y))dX​(x,y)​=Lip(f−1).
(6)
If the sharp Lipschitz constant of f is one, this equals bi-Lipschitz distortion. Fixing the scale matters: uniform compression f(x)=x/2 has stretch factor two in this convention, but bi-Lipschitz distortion one. In a geometric spanner, the corresponding stretch factor is the largest graph-route distance divided by direct Euclidean distance; the graph-to-Euclidean identification does not increase distances. [ref].

Coarse embedding (ρ−​(dX​)≤dY​≤ρ+​(dX​))

Words: 174 Articles: 1
Metric embedding OurBigBook.com
A map f:X→Y between metric spaces is a coarse embedding when nondecreasing control functions ρ−​,ρ+​:[0,∞)→[0,∞) satisfy
ρ−​(dX​(x,y))≤dY​(f(x),f(y))≤ρ+​(dX​(x,y)),ρ−​(t)⟶∞.
(7)
The last condition means that distances tending to infinity in the source must also tend to infinity in the target. The upper control prevents bounded source distances from having unbounded image distances. Unlike a metric embedding as defined here, a coarse embedding need not be injective: its lower control may vanish at small distances. Finite bi-Lipschitz distortion implies a coarse embedding by taking ρ−​(t)=at and ρ+​(t)=bt, but nonlinear controls such as t​ allow much more compression.

Uniform coarse embedding of a family of metric spaces

Words: 87
Coarse embedding OurBigBook.com
A family (Xn​) uniformly coarsely embeds into Y when maps fn​:Xn​→Y admit the same two control functions ρ−​ and ρ+​ from the definition of a coarse embedding, independent of n. For a single finite metric space, the lower control can vanish up to its diameter and then grow without bound; this says little about its representation. For a family with unbounded diameters, common controls have real content: they cannot be chosen afresh after seeing each member's diameter. The Hamming cube as an L1 and L2 metric supplies explicit common controls, in contrast with the expander graph obstruction to uniform coarse embedding.

Hamming cube as an L1 and L2 metric (Hn​={0,1}n)

Words: 335
Metric embedding OurBigBook.com
Use the {0,1}n convention for the Hamming cube Hn​. Its elements are length-n binary strings, and their Hamming distance counts the differing coordinates:
k=dH​(x,y)=∑j=1n​∣xj​−yj​∣.
(8)
Send each binary string to the vector with the same coordinates, by ιn​:Hn​→Rn. Every differing coordinate contributes 1 both to the sum of absolute differences and to the sum of squared differences. Therefore the L1 norm and L2 norm give, respectively,
d1​(ιn​x,ιn​y)=k,d2​(ιn​x,ιn​y)=k​.
(9)
This short coordinate calculation proves that the L1 norm gives an isometric embedding, while Euclidean distance grows like the square root of the number of changed binary digits. For example, 000 and 101 differ twice: their Hamming distance and Manhattan distance are 2, but their Euclidean distance is 2​. Comparing binary strings in these two geometric representations answers different questions even though the points have the same coordinates.
For distinct pairs k ranges over every integer from 1 to n. The metric embedding into the L2 norm thus has sharp ratios
dH​(x,y)d2​(ιn​x,ιn​y)​=k​1​,a=n​1​,b=1,dist(ιn​)=n​.
(10)
Neighbors attain b and opposite corners attain a. This computes the bi-Lipschitz distortion of this coordinate map; it does not prove that every metric embedding into an L2 space has at least this bi-Lipschitz distortion. Uniformly multiplying the output coordinates changes a and b together and leaves n​ unchanged.
The important family distinction is that these coordinate maps have no dimension-independent bound on their bi-Lipschitz distortion into the L2 norm, yet they do give a uniform coarse embedding of a family of metric spaces. Pad each output vector with zero coordinates to view all maps in the single l2 sequence space, an L2 space for counting measure. Then the same functions
ρ−​(t)=ρ+​(t)=t​(t≥0)
(11)
work for every n, and ρ−​(t)→∞. Similarly, padding into the l1 sequence space gives common controls ρ−​(t)=ρ+​(t)=t and exact preservation of Hamming distance. These claims use unnormalized Hamming distance; replacing it by normalized Hamming distance changes the family of source metrics.
The geometry has a practical origin: Hamming's 1950 paper uses the number of differing positions between binary strings to study error-correcting codes. Changing one binary digit moves to a neighboring corner, so a question about corrupted messages becomes a question about separation in the Hamming cube. [ref]

Zig-zag product

Words: 165 Articles: 1
Expander graph OurBigBook.com
For a port-labeled D-regular graph G and a d-regular graph H on its D port labels, replace every vertex by a copy of H. An edge makes a step in H, crosses the corresponding edge of G, then makes another step in H. The result has ∣G∣D vertices and degree d2. Its normalized operator is BRB, where B=I⊗PH​ and R is the involutive edge rotation of G. Decomposing a mean-zero vector into cloud-constant and cloud-orthogonal parts proves the bound ρ(GzigzagH)≤ρ(G)+2ρ(H)+ρ(H)2.

Squaring and zig-zag iteration yields bounded-degree expanders

Choose D=d2 and a fixed d-regular graph H on D2 vertices with absolute nonconstant eigenvalues at most 1/10. Start with the loop-inclusive complete graph G0​ on D vertices and iterate Gt+1​=Gt2​zigzagH. The degree stays D, vertex counts are D2t+1, and ρt+1​≤ρt2​+21/100. Since ρ0​=0, all ρt​≤1/2. The spectral cut bound gives multiplicity-counted edge expansion at least D/4. Removing loops and merging parallel edges loses at most a factor D, producing simple expander graphs with maximum degree D and edge expansion at least 1/4.

Expander graph obstruction to uniform coarse embedding

Words: 63
Expander graph OurBigBook.com
An expander family does not uniformly coarsely embed into L1 or L2. Uniform upper control bounds every embedded edge, whereas the expander Poincare inequality bounds the average image distance. A fixed proportion of vertex pairs have graph distance tending to infinity, contradicting the lower control. The L2 case also follows from the isometric Gaussian embedding of a Hilbert space into L1.

Superreflexive Banach space

Words: 281 Articles: 5
Finite representability of a Banach space OurBigBook.com
A Banach space is superreflexive when every Banach space finitely representable in it is a reflexive Banach space. Equivalently, it admits an equivalent uniformly convex norm, or every one of its ultrapowers is reflexive.

Convex-block separation criterion for reflexivity

Words: 149 Articles: 2
Superreflexive Banach space OurBigBook.com
A Banach space X is reflexive exactly when, for every θ>0 and every sequence (xi​) in BX​, some convex combination of a finite initial segment lies within θ of a convex combination of the remaining tail. In a reflexive space both convex hulls approximate a common weak cluster point. The converse is the convex-block form of the James reflexivity criterion.
James's theorem

Convex-block separation criterion for reflexivity OurBigBook.com
James's theorem says that a Banach space is reflexive exactly when every continuous linear functional attains its supremum on the closed unit ball. Its separation proof also yields the equivalent convex-block criterion: nonreflexivity produces a bounded sequence whose initial and tail convex hulls remain uniformly separated.

For finite-dimensional subspaces E⊆X∗∗ and F⊆X∗, the principle of local reflexivity gives an almost-isometric map T:E→X that fixes E∩X and preserves the pairings with F. It lets finite-dimensional bidual separation data be realized inside X.

Uniform finite convex-block criterion for superreflexivity

Words: 58
Superreflexive Banach space OurBigBook.com
A Banach space X is superreflexive exactly when, for every θ>0, some N forces every sequence (xi​)i=1N​⊆BX​ to have two convex combinations, one before and one after a cut, at distance below θ. Failure produces a nonreflexive ultrapower; finite representability transfers a violating finite sequence back from any nonreflexive space finitely representable in X.

Diamond-graph characterization of superreflexivity

Words: 42
Superreflexive Banach space OurBigBook.com
A Banach space X is superreflexive exactly when the distortions cX​(Dn​) of the finite diamond graphs tend to infinity. A uniformly separated convex-block sequence recursively realizes all branches of Dn​ with bounded distortion, while uniform convexity forces quantitative collapse across repeated diamonds.

Fréchet embedding

Words: 74
Bourgain embedding theorem OurBigBook.com
Fix a point x0​ in a metric space X. The Fréchet embedding sends x to the bounded function a↦d(x,a)−d(x0​,a) in ℓ∞(X). The triangle inequality bounds each coordinate by d(x,x0​) and gives ∥fx​−fy​∥∞​≤d(x,y). Taking a=x gives equality, so the map is an isometric embedding. For a separable metric space a countable dense set of anchors suffices. Finite collections of distances to selected subsets instead give one-Lipschitz coordinates that need not be an exact Fréchet embedding.

Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma

Words: 1k Articles: 8
Metric embedding OurBigBook.com
For 0<ε<1 and any n points in a Hilbert space, there is a linear map into Euclidean dimension O(ε−2logn) that multiplies every pairwise distance by a factor between 1−ε and 1+ε.
The striking feature is that the target dimension depends on the number of points and the allowed error, independently of the ambient dimension. Fix a finite set S={x1​,…,xn​}⊂RD, with n≥2, and 0<ε<1. An equivalent sufficient statement uses squared Euclidean distances: a linear map T:RD→Rk exists, with k=O(ε−2logn), such that
(1−ε)∥xi​−xj​∥22​≤∥Txi​−Txj​∥22​≤(1+ε)∥xi​−xj​∥22​(1≤i<j≤n).
(19)
The finite-set theorem cited here supplies an explicit sufficient target dimension for this squared-distance formulation. [ref]
Taking square roots gives distance factors 1−ε​ and 1+ε​, which lie inside [1−ε,1+ε]. Conversely, bounds 1±ε on distances only give squared-distance bounds (1±ε)2; the same numerical tolerance should not be silently used for both. Under the squared-distance bounds, the bi-Lipschitz distortion is at most (1+ε)/(1−ε)​.
The Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma preserves this fixed finite set, rather than every vector in the ambient Euclidean space. A linear map with k<D has a nonzero kernel by the rank-nullity theorem, so some nonzero vectors collapse completely. For finitely many points in a real Hilbert space, apply the finite-dimensional argument on their linear span and extend by orthogonal projection onto that linear span. If the sufficient target dimension exceeds the ambient dimension, the identity map already preserves every Euclidean distance exactly, with zero padding if a larger target is requested.

Gaussian Johnson–Lindenstrauss transform (T(x)=k−1/2Gx)

Words: 770 Articles: 2
Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma OurBigBook.com
Draw a k×D random matrix G whose entries are independent random variables with the standard normal distribution, and set
T(x)=k​1​Gx.
(20)
The factor 1/k​ makes the expected value of the squared L2 norm equal to the original squared L2 norm. This Gaussian Johnson–Lindenstrauss transform is a linear map, generally not an orthogonal projection: its rows are not required to be orthogonal vectors. [ref]
For the fixed finite set S, let 0<δ<1 be an allowed failure probability, and define
c(ε)=4ε2​−6ε3​>0.
(21)
With log denoting the natural logarithm, a sufficient integer target dimension is
k≥⌈c(ε)log(n(n−1)/δ)​⌉.
(22)
Then one draw of this specified random matrix satisfies all the squared Euclidean distance bounds simultaneously with probability at least 1−δ. Since c(ε)≥ε2/12, the simpler sufficient bound k≥⌈12ε−2log(n(n−1)/δ)⌉ also works. Both are sufficient bounds for this argument; they do not claim the smallest possible target dimension.
This is a probability statement about a map drawn independently of the fixed finite set. It implies existence because the success probability is positive, but permits failed draws. The set cannot be chosen after inspecting the map with this same guarantee. In particular, no single draw preserves every possible finite set in the ambient Euclidean space when k<D.

Here is an original reproducible finite set with 40 points and 512 coordinates. Let e1​,…,e4​ be the first four vectors of the standard basis of R512. With point indices i=0,…,39, generate
xi​=2e1+⌊i/10⌋​+512​zi​​,zij​∼N(0,1) independently.
(23)
The four groups have different centers, and the standard normal random variables perturb all 512 coordinates. Generate the coordinates in row order using NumPy's PCG64 pseudorandom number generator with seed 20261013. Then keep this finite set fixed. A second PCG64 stream with seed 424242 generates a 32×512 matrix of independent random variables with the standard normal distribution; divide it by 32​ to obtain the Gaussian Johnson–Lindenstrauss transform.
For all 780 unordered pairs, compute the distance ratio rij​=∥Txi​−Txj​∥2​/∥xi​−xj​∥2​. In this draw, its minimum is approximately 0.733010 and its maximum 1.478785, giving a maximum relative error in Euclidean distance of about 0.478785. The squared-distance ratios rij2​ instead range from approximately 0.537304 to 2.186806: their maximum relative error is about 1.186806. Thus this particular draw fails a 20% distance tolerance. The Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma does not assert that every draw into 32 dimensions must succeed.
The two bands of original Euclidean distances distinguish pairs within a group from pairs between groups. Their spread after the linear map shows why checking an average distance is insufficient: the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma controls every pair. The dashed 20% distance band is an illustrative tolerance, not a confidence interval or a squared-distance band. The affine hull of 40 points has dimension at most 39; 512 is the ambient coordinate count, and no full-dimensionality assumption is required. For this draw the bi-Lipschitz distortion is approximately 1.478785/0.733010=2.017414.
Now keep the same points and draw 48 fresh Gaussian Johnson–Lindenstrauss transforms for each target dimension k∈{8,16,32,64,128}. NumPy's SeedSequence(314159).spawn(240) supplies distinct PCG64 streams, ordered first by target dimension, then by draw. Each stream generates a new k×512 random matrix, normalized by 1/k​; no map is a row prefix of another map. Each plotted value is
E(T)=maxi<j​​∥xi​−xj​∥2​∥Txi​−Txj​∥2​​−1​.
(24)
The observed median maximum relative errors in Euclidean distance are approximately 0.735, 0.520, 0.381, 0.267, and 0.184, respectively. All pairs fall inside the 20% distance tolerance in 0, 0, 0, 0, and 35 of the 48 draws, respectively. These are computations on one synthetic finite set, rather than optimal dimension bounds or measured physical data. The displayed quantiles describe the sampled maps; they do not certify a success probability for other sets. Even at 128 dimensions, 13 of the sampled maps fail this tolerance.
The practical benefit is that each point acquires fewer coordinates while its pairwise Euclidean distances can remain useful. The choice of target dimension balances compression, accuracy, and failure probability. For example, a conservative sufficient squared-distance tolerance ε=0.2 with n=40 and δ=0.05 gives k≥1195 by the explicit bound above. Here that exceeds the ambient dimension, so the identity map is better for a certified guarantee. The much smaller dimensions in the plots illustrate observed behavior without claiming that this sufficient bound is sharp.
