Velocity-addition formula

 Home Physics Branches of physics Special relativity Lorentz transformation
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 7
The velocity-addition formula translates a particle's velocity between two specified inertial frames. Let S′ move at signed velocity V along the x-axis of S, with parallel axes and ∣V∣<c, where c is the speed of light. Define ux​=dx/dt and ux′​=dx′/dt′ for the same particle's world line, using position and time measured within the corresponding inertial frame. With Lorentz factor γV​=(1−V2/c2)−1/2, the forward Lorentz boost gives
ux′​=1−Vux​/c2ux​−V​,uy′​=γV​(1−Vux​/c2)uy​​,uz′​=γV​(1−Vux​/c2)uz​​.
(30)
For collinear motion, write u=ux​ and u′=ux′​. The inverse velocity-addition formula is
u=1+Vu′/c2u′+V​.
(31)
This is the composition of the particle's velocity in S′ and that frame's velocity relative to S. The velocities must belong to this same chain of reference frames, with directions specified. Two arbitrary quoted speeds cannot be inserted blindly. Each velocity measurement uses distances and clocks in a single inertial frame, with Einstein synchronisation for separated clocks.
For example, let V=0.6c and let the particle travel at u′=0.6c in S′. Its velocity in S is
u=1+0.6×0.60.6c+0.6c​=1715​c≈0.88235294c.
(32)
The Galilean transformation would instead give u=u′+V=1.2c, which fails here. For light moving along the positive axis, u′=c gives u=c; u′=−c similarly gives u=−c. Negative directions also work for massive particles: at V=0.6c, u′=−0.6c gives u=0, while u′=−0.8c gives u=−5c/13. Thus “addition” means composition of signed velocities, rather than addition of their magnitudes.
A short derivation differentiates the Lorentz transformation: dx′=γV​(dx−Vdt) and dt′=γV​(dt−Vdx/c2). Dividing these differentials gives the longitudinal velocity-addition formula. The transverse formulas use dy′=dy and dz′=dz, but the same transformed time denominator; omitting it would ignore the relativity of simultaneity. Replacing V by −V gives the inverse. When both relative and particle velocities are small compared with the speed of light, the denominator is close to one and the nonrelativistic limit recovers the Galilean transformation.
The formula lets us compare particle motion in a moving apparatus and a laboratory without losing the invariant speed of light. Einstein's 1905 paper derives the coordinate change in §3 and the composition of velocities in §5, including the collinear formula, its light-speed case, and composition of parallel transformations.
