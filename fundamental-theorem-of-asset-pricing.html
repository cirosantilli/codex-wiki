In a finite market, absence of arbitrage is equivalent to the existence of an equivalent martingale measure. If that measure is unique, every contingent claim has a unique no-arbitrage price given by its discounted expectation.

Positive-density separation proof of the one-period asset-pricing theorem (no arbitrage⟺∃Q∼P:EQ​Y=0)

Words: 87
Fundamental theorem of asset pricing OurBigBook.com
Let Y be the discounted gains of finitely many financial assets on any probability space. Tilting the physical probability measure by a normalized multiple of (1+∥Y∥)−1 makes Y integrable without changing null events. The positive-weight expectation cone alternative then says either a bounded strictly positive weight makes the weighted gain expectation zero, or a deterministic risky holding gives an arbitrage. Normalizing the weight yields an equivalent martingale measure. Conversely, under such a measure a nonnegative terminal gain with zero initial price has zero expectation, and must vanish almost surely. This proves the one-period fundamental theorem of asset pricing without assuming physical integrability of asset gains.

Gaussian-damped martingale density construction (dQ/dP∝e−∣X∣2−h∗​⋅X)

Words: 100
Fundamental theorem of asset pricing OurBigBook.com
For a finite-dimensional discounted gain random vector X, absence of arbitrage means h⋅X≥0 almost surely only when h⋅X=0 almost surely. Let N={h:h⋅X=0 almost surely} and minimize F(h)=Ee−∣X∣2−h⋅X on the orthogonal complement N⊥. The Gaussian damping makes F finite and differentiable without physical integrability assumptions on X. Every nonzero direction in N⊥ has negative gains on an event of positive probability; compactness of directions proves coercivity. At a minimizer, differentiation gives E[Xe−∣X∣2−h∗​⋅X]=0. Normalizing the positive weight produces an equivalent martingale measure with integrable gains. This is a constructive one-period proof of the fundamental theorem of asset pricing even on an infinite probability space.

Positive state-price density alternative (A0​=E[νA1​],ν>0)

Words: 92
Fundamental theorem of asset pricing OurBigBook.com
For deterministic initial asset values A0​ and a finite-dimensional terminal random vector A1​, exactly one possibility holds: a portfolio has nonpositive initial cost and nonnegative payoff with strict gain at at least one date, or there is a strictly positive integrable state-price density pricing A1​ as displayed. Apply the strictly positive barycentre cone lemma. Outside the closed support cone, strict separation gives a negative-cost portfolio. On its relative boundary, a supporting functional gives a zero-cost portfolio with positive payoff on a set of positive probability. With a traded positive bank account, normalize its discounted density to obtain an equivalent martingale measure.

Complete market

Words: 404 Articles: 5
Fundamental theorem of asset pricing OurBigBook.com
A complete market is a financial market in which every contingent claim in the chosen class can be replicated by an admissible self-financing portfolio. In an arbitrage-free finite market, completeness is equivalent to uniqueness of the equivalent martingale measure.

Completeness and uniqueness of dominated martingale measures

Words: 133
Complete market OurBigBook.com
In an arbitrage-free one-period market with finitely many assets and an equivalent martingale measure, market completeness is equivalent to uniqueness of the dominated martingale measure. Completeness prices every indicator function, forcing two measures to agree on every event. Conversely, if the attainable payoff span has dimension k but is not the whole payoff space, choose k+1 disjoint positive-probability events. Their moment vectors against a basis of attainable payoffs are linearly dependent. A nonzero bounded linear combination h of their indicators consequently has zero pricing moments. Multiplying an existing equivalent density by 1+εh, for sufficiently small positive and negative ε, constructs distinct equivalent martingale measures. This proves the converse without assuming that the original space was finite. On a general probability space, completeness here means replication of all bounded claims, or equivalently all square-integrable claims when asset payoffs are square-integrable.

Complete markets have unique state-price densities

Words: 92
Complete market OurBigBook.com
In a one-period complete market with a strictly positive numéraire, at most one positive state-price density prices every traded asset. Suppose ρ,ρ′ do so. For each event A, replicate the contingent claim S1(0)​1A​. Linearity of the pricing equations gives
E[(ρ−ρ′)S1(0)​1A​]=0.
(1)
Both weighted densities are integrable because they price the numéraire. An integrable random variable whose integral over every event is zero vanishes almost surely: take the events where it is positive and negative. Therefore (ρ−ρ′)S1(0)​=0, and strict positivity of the numéraire gives ρ=ρ′. This argument avoids assuming that the unweighted densities themselves are integrable.

Complete two-state market

Words: 49
Complete market OurBigBook.com
Two assets with linearly independent payoff vectors across two positive-probability states span all terminal payoffs. The state-price equations have a unique solution. A positive solution gives the state-price density after division by physical state probabilities. Arrow state prices sum to the riskless discount factor, while normalized Arrow state prices are risk-neutral probabilities; these three quantities must not be confused.

Uniqueness of a one-period pricing density

Words: 46
Complete market OurBigBook.com
In a one-period complete market with deterministic initial holdings, two integrable positive pricing variables assigning the same prices to every traded asset are equal almost surely. Replicate indicator functions to obtain equality of their integrals on every event, then apply this to the event where one density exceeds the other.

With n assets and a trivial initial sigma-field, market completeness forces at most n positive-probability successors per current atom. Indicators of disjoint successor events are independent payoffs, whereas terminal holdings on a parent atom span at most n asset-price functions. Iterating yields at most nt atoms at time t.

European call option (CT​=(ST​−K)+)

Words: 2k Articles: 24
Fundamental theorem of asset pricing OurBigBook.com
A European call option gives its holder the right, without an obligation, to buy one unit of an underlying financial asset at the strike price K on the expiry date T. European describes exercise timing, not geography: exercise is allowed only at T. Here the underlying is one stock share, ST​ is its price at expiry, and the financial payoff is expressed as a cash amount. Physical exercise delivers a share against payment of K; immediate resale gives the same net amount under the assumed frictionless market.
The holder exercises only when buying at the strike price is advantageous. Writing x+=max(x,0) for the positive part, the terminal financial payoff is
CT​=(ST​−K)+={0,ST​−K,​ST​≤K,ST​>K.​
(3)
The European call option is a reusable building block for claim replication: its financial payoff is zero below the strike price and rises by one unit for each unit of stock price above it. The corner at K lets combinations of European call options construct other payoff shapes, as in static replication on a finite terminal support.

Zero-rate barrier-strike call hedge (B=K,Cout​=S−K)

Words: 82

For a down-and-out European call with barrier equal to strike, zero interest and no dividends, hold one share and borrow the strike until barrier hitting or maturity. At continuous hitting the share price is exactly the debt, so liquidating leaves zero; on survival the portfolio pays ST​−K. Hence the contract has value S−K and delta one before knock-out. Words: 57

For any fixed maturity, a European call option price is convex in its strike because K↦(s−K)+ is convex for every terminal stock value s. Specifically, for 0≤a≤1, (s−aK1​−(1−a)K2​)+≤a(s−K1​)++(1−a)(s−K2​)+. Multiplication by the positive discount factor and expectation under the pricing measure preserve this inequality. Finite-strike nonidentification of a pricing density ({C(Ki​)}i=1n​ ⇒ a unique pricing density)

Finitely many call prices impose finitely many payoff-moment constraints. They do not require a continuous terminal law or determine prices of arbitrary new claims. For example, S0​=1, terminal values 1/2 and 2+2​, and upper-state probability 3−22​ give C(1)=2​−1, matching the p=2 power curve. A payoff vanishing on these two states must cost zero, whereas integration against the power curve's strictly positive density can assign it a positive cost. A pricing density intended for arbitrary claims must be compatible with an equivalent law on the actual state space.

Put-call parity (C0​−P0​=S0​−Ke−rT)

Words: 743 Articles: 2
European call option OurBigBook.com
Put-call parity fixes the price difference between a European call option and a European put option on the same underlying stock, with the same strike price K and expiry T. Assume no dividends or other payments from the stock before or at expiry, a known constant continuously compounded interest rate r, and a frictionless market. Borrowing and lending are available at that same interest rate; short selling of the stock, European call option, European put option, and zero-coupon bond is permitted. Settlement is certain, and the buy-and-hold portfolios below may be held until T without interim funding restrictions or forced liquidation. These assumptions exclude transaction costs, taxes, borrowing fees, and execution or settlement risks from the arbitrage argument.
At expiry, the two positive parts cancel their corners:
CT​−PT​=(ST​−K)+−(K−ST​)+=ST​−K.
(4)
Thus a European call option and a short European put option together have the same terminal financial payoff as one stock share with a debt of K. A particularly readable claim replication uses two portfolios with nonnegative terminal amounts: portfolio A holds one European call option and a risk-free zero-coupon bond paying K at T; portfolio B holds one European put option and one stock share. The zero-coupon bond costs Ke−rT today, since the discount factor is e−rT. There are no intermediate cash flows. For every terminal stock price,
AT​=CT​+K=max(ST​,K)=PT​+ST​=BT​.
(5)
For the strike price K=100, even the following very different outcomes leave the two portfolios equal:
Terminal stock price ST​A: European call option + zero-coupon bondB: European put option + stock
800+100=10020+80=100
1000+100=1000+100=100
12020+100=1200+120=120
The law of one price forces A0​=B0​ in this setting: otherwise buy the cheaper portfolio and sell the more expensive portfolio using short selling. Their terminal obligations cancel in every scenario, while the initial price difference can grow in the bank account. Hence absence of arbitrage gives
C0​+Ke−rT=P0​+S0​,C0​−P0​=S0​−Ke−rT.
(6)
With a constant per-period interest rate i over N discrete periods, the same reasoning gives C0​−P0​=S0​−K(1+i)−N. This is a different compounding convention; i is not the continuously compounded interest rate r.
For an illustrative stock price S0​=100, strike price K=100, expiry one year away, interest rate r=0.05 per year, and given European call option price C0​=10, put-call parity requires
Ke−rT=100e−0.05≈95.12294245,P0​=10+100e−0.05−100≈5.12294245.
(7)
Both portfolios therefore cost approximately 105.12294245. The inputs specify no stock price dynamics: C0​=10 is supplied for illustration, not calculated from the Black-Scholes model. Put-call parity determines the European put option price conditional on that European call option price; it does not determine both prices by itself. At zero interest rate, the same S0​=K=100 and C0​=10 give the exact result P0​=10.
To compute both directions of arbitrage, keep all the preceding inputs except the quoted European put option price. If it is 6, portfolio B costs 106 while portfolio A costs 105.12294245. Buy A, sell B, and place the initial surplus 6−(10+100e−0.05−100)≈0.87705755 in the bank account. At expiry, A and the obligations from selling B cancel exactly, leaving 96e0.05−100≈0.92202525 in every scenario. If the European put option instead costs 4, buy B for 104 and sell A for 105.12294245; the initial surplus is approximately 1.12294245, growing to 100−94e0.05≈1.18051694. Both constructions start with zero external capital. They are model calculations under the stated frictionless market assumptions.
The beauty of put-call parity is that uncertain individual financial payoffs produce an exact relation between today's prices. No forecast of the terminal stock price and no probability distribution are needed. It provides a consistency check and lets claim replication exchange one contract for a combination of others. Dividends introduce extra cash flows, and American put options can be exercised early, so the displayed European equality cannot simply be transferred to those contracts. K. Foster's [ref] explain the matching portfolios; Jun Pan's [ref] discuss put-call parity and its dividend adjustment.
An original modern research source is Hans R. Stoll's [ref], The Journal of Finance 24(5), 801-824 (December 1969). Stoll studied the relation between contract prices and tested it against market data; this was a relation between prices rather than a formula fixing each price individually. Robert C. Merton's [ref], 28(1), 183-184, highlighted the importance of excluding premature exercise for an exact arbitrage equality. This distinction explains why the exercise assumption belongs in the statement of put-call parity.
