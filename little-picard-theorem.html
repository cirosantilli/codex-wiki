Little Picard theorem (#(C∖f(C))≤1)

 ... Area of mathematics Analysis Complex analysis Holomorphic function Entire function Picard theorem
OurBigBook.com Words: 800 Articles: 1
Every nonconstant entire function takes every complex value with at most one exception. Equivalently, its image is either C or C∖{a} for one complex number a. The hypothesis of being nonconstant matters: a constant function has only one value. The Little Picard theorem guarantees existence of preimages, without requiring infinitely many preimages for each attained value. [ref]
For a nonconstant polynomial p, the fundamental theorem of algebra applied to p(z)−w supplies a preimage for every w∈C. The complex exponential function has precisely one omitted value, zero, as the explicit formula for its preimages shows. In contrast, the complex sine function has no omitted value. To solve sinz=w, set u=eiz and rearrange to the quadratic equation
u2−2iwu−1=0.
(36)
Its roots have product −1, so they are nonzero. Every such root has a preimage under the complex exponential function, giving a solution for z. Adding 2πk supplies further solutions by the periodicity of the complex sine.
The force of the Little Picard theorem is that it applies to every nonconstant entire function, without an explicit formula. The Liouville theorem already gives a dense image of a nonconstant entire function, but a dense subset could still leave out many individual points. The Little Picard theorem limits these missing points to one. In particular, establishing two omitted values proves that an entire function is constant.
For a nonconstant meromorphic function on the complex plane, the corresponding bound is two omitted values in the Riemann sphere C∪{∞}, where poles take the value infinity. [ref] For example,
m(z)=1−ez1​
(37)
omits zero and one, and has poles at 2πik for k∈Z. Every other finite value w is attained by solving ez=1−1/w. For an entire function, infinity is already an omitted value in the Riemann sphere, leaving at most one additional finite omitted value.
Proof outline: use the version of Montel's theorem for two fixed finite omitted values. [ref] It makes the rescaled holomorphic functions gn​(ζ)=f(a+nζ) a normal family on the unit disc. The fixed value gn​(0)=f(a) gives pointwise anchoring of a holomorphic normal family, hence a subsequence with a finite holomorphic function as its locally uniform convergence limit. The Cauchy integral formula bounds its derivatives at zero, whereas gn′​(0)=nf′(a). Consequently f′(a)=0; arbitrary a makes f constant.
