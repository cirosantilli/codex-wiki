The numerical coordinates assigned to an event can be changed without changing the event. It does not follow that every relation between coordinate systems is devoid of physical content. A standard inertial frame includes a family of resting measuring standards and synchronized clocks. Its coordinates are normalized so that the Minkowski metric has the same standard form. The relation between two such descriptions therefore records properties of the standards and of spacetime, not just an arbitrary choice of names.
Write xμ=(ct,x,y,z) and use metric signature (−,+,+,+). A Lorentz transformation satisfies
x′μ=Λμν​xν,ΛTηΛ=η,η=diag(−1,1,1,1).
(9)
An origin translation can also be included without changing the Minkowski interval. The displayed condition preserves the interval between events, including the null cone and proper time along a timelike curve. Its physical significance lies in the claim that comparisons made with actual idealized rods and clocks obey this same metric structure.
A more general passive relabeling is allowed, but then the metric components must change with the labels. For example, let t′=2t and leave x,y,z fixed. In the new coordinates,
ds2=−4c2​dt′2+dx2+dy2+dz2.
(10)
Nothing physical has changed, but t′ no longer has the original clock-normalized meaning of t. Retaining the old metric components as well as the new labels would change the mathematical model. This distinction is part of the physical content of a passive Lorentz transformation: the transformation between standard inertial coordinate systems preserves a specified metric form, whereas arbitrary relabelings need not preserve that form.
For an operational example, a clock moving at velocity v between two events on its world line accumulates
Δτ=Δt1−v2/c2​
(11)
in the first frame. In its own rest frame the same result is simply the interval between its local readings. The equality is a physical agreement about one clock, derived using the preserved Minkowski interval. It is not a claim that the two coordinate-time differences are identical. Similarly, an observer measures a light angular frequency through ω=−kμ​uμ, where kμ is the wave four-vector and uμ the observer's four-velocity. Transforming both sets of components leaves this scalar unchanged. Comparing two different observers gives the physical relativistic Doppler effect rather than a disagreement over one observer's measurement.
There is a further distinction between passive transformations of the same situation and active changes to a situation. Re-expressing a particular experiment in different coordinates is always possible if all geometric fields are transformed consistently. Preparing a second, moving copy of the apparatus is a different physical arrangement. Its relation to the first is governed by a dynamical symmetry only if the relevant laws and background structure have the appropriate invariance. An active Lorentz transformation of a vector field makes the changed field configuration explicit.
This is why mere Lorentz covariance of notation must not be confused with the Lorentz invariance of the dynamics. A physically preferred timelike vector field can be transformed as a vector under passive relabeling while distinguishing particular states of motion. Holding that preferred field fixed can destroy the active boost symmetry of the matter dynamics. The covariant equation with a preferred timelike vector is a concrete example.
Coordinates are therefore labels, but the Lorentz transformation relates labels tied to shared physical standards and a definite metric structure. Its measurable consequences follow from that structure and the dynamical assumptions, not from the bare act of changing labels.[ref]
