There are two different issues: whether a law follows logically from the other laws, and whether an experiment can test it without assuming anything else. Newton's laws of motion have empirical content when combined with rules for identifying inertial frames, measuring mass and specifying forces. An experiment tests predictions of that combination. Calling every observed acceleration an otherwise unspecified force would remove the empirical constraint rather than verify Newton's second law. The distinction is central to the empirical testing of Newton's laws of motion.
Newton's first law contributes the claim that a common family of inertial frames exists: bodies with zero net external force have uniform straight motion in those frames. It is not enough to choose coordinates which straighten the trajectory of just one body; an admissible frame must accommodate many independent bodies and interactions. Once that frame and the ordinary constant-positive-mass form of Newton's second law are assumed,
F=mdtdv​=0⟹v(t)=v0​.
(1)
This conditional deduction does not establish the existence of the required frame, or independently identify the physical circumstances in which the net force vanishes. Defining an inertial frame and then counting a straight trajectory as proof of the definition would not be a test.
Nevertheless, the law of inertia has testable implications in a network of assumptions. Consider low-friction motion along an air track: determine positions at measured times, reduce the known residual influences, and ask whether the remaining acceleration approaches zero in the same laboratory frame for different bodies. Rotating the frame changes the description through Coriolis forces and centrifugal forces; this dependence must be accounted for. No finite experiment establishes that every unmeasured influence is absent, but varying the apparatus can discriminate an interaction from a frame effect. A failed prediction is evidence against some part of the package, not automatically the isolated verbal statement of Newton's first law.
Newton's second law is more than a definition if the force law and mass assignments constrain predictions before the new acceleration is measured. For instance, independently characterize a spring's reproducible extension response, specify the same interaction in several configurations, and compare the resulting accelerations for different bodies. If its strength and every mass are freely refitted to each outcome, agreement is empty. If they are fixed by an independently checked model and calibration, the law predicts held-out trajectories. Such a test remains conditional on the spring model, initial conditions and frame identification; this is an instance of the Duhem problem, not a reason to deny all empirical content.
Newton's third law supplies a different constraint. For two bodies exchanging forces, Newton's second law gives
dtd​(p1​+p2​)=F12​+F21​
(2)
when there is no net external force. Equal-and-opposite interaction forces imply conservation of momentum for the pair. The two second-law equations alone do not require that cancellation: assigning F12​=f and F21​=−2f is algebraically consistent with both second-law equations while violating the third law. This is a logical countermodel, not a proposed isolated physical interaction. Measuring paired impulses or checking the pair's total momentum therefore tests an additional constraint, conditional on the assumed absence of momentum exchange with anything else.
The system boundary matters. In electromagnetism, matter can exchange momentum with the electromagnetic field. Instantaneous mechanical forces between two particles need not satisfy the simple pairwise statement, although total matter-plus-field momentum is conserved under the appropriate closed-system conditions. Likewise, Noether theorem can derive conservation of momentum from a translationally invariant action; that uses an extra symmetry assumption and does not derive the third law from the second alone.
Thus the first law is not an additional differential consequence once the full second-law framework is fixed, and the third law is not a consequence of that framework. All three can contribute to empirical tests, but none is tested in total isolation from frame, interaction and measurement assumptions. This preserves the difference between logical dependence and experimental testability.[ref]
