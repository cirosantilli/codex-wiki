Alvarez hydrogen-bubble-chamber reconstruction and resonances

 Home Physics Particle detection Particle detector Bubble chamber Hydrogen bubble chamber
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 5
Luis Walter Alvarez's program extended the Glaser bubble-chamber experiment into large hydrogen bubble chambers and systematic analysis. His lecture credits John G. Wood, an accelerator technician, with early hydrogen tracks, and emphasizes technicians' responsibility for chamber development.[ref]
Alvarez's 1956 report describes a ten-inch hydrogen bubble chamber in a steady magnetic field, exposed to a 700MeV negative-pion beam for proton scattering. Liquid hydrogen was the target. Horizontal windows admitted illumination and stereo viewing; a shielded light source let bubble-scattered light reach the lenses. A temperature gradient initially restricted sensitivity near the top. Tilting and fast recompression improved uniformity.[ref]
The same report compares tracks without a magnetic field against straightness expectations, testing spurious curvature. Multiple Coulomb scattering remained a limitation. This separates an instrumental check from the physical momentum inferred with the field on.[ref]
The scientific opportunity is to turn a visible geometry into a test of an invisible intermediate state. The bubble-chamber pressure cycle prepares the sensitive target; bubble-chamber track readout supplies geometric measurements. Neither step alone identifies a particle resonance.
