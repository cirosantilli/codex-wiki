Put-call parity (C0​−P0​=S0​−Ke−rT)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Mathematical optimization Mathematical finance Fundamental theorem of asset pricing European call option
OurBigBook.com Words: 743 Articles: 2
Put-call parity fixes the price difference between a European call option and a European put option on the same underlying stock, with the same strike price K and expiry T. Assume no dividends or other payments from the stock before or at expiry, a known constant continuously compounded interest rate r, and a frictionless market. Borrowing and lending are available at that same interest rate; short selling of the stock, European call option, European put option, and zero-coupon bond is permitted. Settlement is certain, and the buy-and-hold portfolios below may be held until T without interim funding restrictions or forced liquidation. These assumptions exclude transaction costs, taxes, borrowing fees, and execution or settlement risks from the arbitrage argument.
At expiry, the two positive parts cancel their corners:
CT​−PT​=(ST​−K)+−(K−ST​)+=ST​−K.
(4)
Thus a European call option and a short European put option together have the same terminal financial payoff as one stock share with a debt of K. A particularly readable claim replication uses two portfolios with nonnegative terminal amounts: portfolio A holds one European call option and a risk-free zero-coupon bond paying K at T; portfolio B holds one European put option and one stock share. The zero-coupon bond costs Ke−rT today, since the discount factor is e−rT. There are no intermediate cash flows. For every terminal stock price,
AT​=CT​+K=max(ST​,K)=PT​+ST​=BT​.
(5)
For the strike price K=100, even the following very different outcomes leave the two portfolios equal:
Terminal stock price ST​A: European call option + zero-coupon bondB: European put option + stock
800+100=10020+80=100
1000+100=1000+100=100

The law of one price forces A0​=B0​ in this setting: otherwise buy the cheaper portfolio and sell the more expensive portfolio using short selling. Their terminal obligations cancel in every scenario, while the initial price difference can grow in the bank account. Hence absence of arbitrage gives
C0​+Ke−rT=P0​+S0​,C0​−P0​=S0​−Ke−rT.
(6)
With a constant per-period interest rate i over N discrete periods, the same reasoning gives C0​−P0​=S0​−K(1+i)−N. This is a different compounding convention; i is not the continuously compounded interest rate r.
For an illustrative stock price S0​=100, strike price K=100, expiry one year away, interest rate r=0.05 per year, and given European call option price C0​=10, put-call parity requires
Ke−rT=100e−0.05≈95.12294245,P0​=10+100e−0.05−100≈5.12294245.
(7)
Both portfolios therefore cost approximately 105.12294245. The inputs specify no stock price dynamics: C0​=10 is supplied for illustration, not calculated from the Black-Scholes model. Put-call parity determines the European put option price conditional on that European call option price; it does not determine both prices by itself. At zero interest rate, the same S0​=K=100 and C0​=10 give the exact result P0​=10.
To compute both directions of arbitrage, keep all the preceding inputs except the quoted European put option price. If it is 6, portfolio B costs 106 while portfolio A costs 105.12294245. Buy A, sell B, and place the initial surplus 6−(10+100e−0.05−100)≈0.87705755 in the bank account. At expiry, A and the obligations from selling B cancel exactly, leaving 96e0.05−100≈0.92202525 in every scenario. If the European put option instead costs 4, buy B for 104 and sell A for 105.12294245; the initial surplus is approximately 1.12294245, growing to 100−94e0.05≈1.18051694. Both constructions start with zero external capital. They are model calculations under the stated frictionless market assumptions.
The beauty of put-call parity is that uncertain individual financial payoffs produce an exact relation between today's prices. No forecast of the terminal stock price and no probability distribution are needed. It provides a consistency check and lets claim replication exchange one contract for a combination of others. Dividends introduce extra cash flows, and American put options can be exercised early, so the displayed European equality cannot simply be transferred to those contracts. K. Foster's [ref] explain the matching portfolios; Jun Pan's [ref] discuss put-call parity and its dividend adjustment.
An original modern research source is Hans R. Stoll's [ref], The Journal of Finance 24(5), 801-824 (December 1969). Stoll studied the relation between contract prices and tested it against market data; this was a relation between prices rather than a formula fixing each price individually. Robert C. Merton's [ref], 28(1), 183-184, highlighted the importance of excluding premature exercise for an exact arbitrage equality. This distinction explains why the exercise assumption belongs in the statement of put-call parity.
