a OurBigBook.com
Write R=er, u=4/3 and d=2/3. Since both moves have positive physical probability, the binomial options pricing model is free of arbitrage exactly when
32​<er<34​.​
(1)
If R≤d, borrowing the share price and buying one share gives terminal gain St​(u−R) or St​(d−R), both nonnegative and at least one positive. If R≥u, shorting one share and investing its proceeds gives the opposite comparison, with gain St​(R−u) or St​(R−d). Equality at either endpoint still permits arbitrage because the other outcome has positive probability.
Conversely, strict inequalities give a risk-neutral probability q=(R−d)/(u−d)∈(0,1), for which the discounted share is a martingale. Any zero-cost self-financing portfolio with nonnegative terminal value has zero discounted expectation under this equivalent positive-probability law and hence cannot have a strictly positive payoff in either state. Applying this argument conditionally at each node proves no-arbitrage over the tree.

Using the risk-neutral probability q=2/3 from part c, the stock tree has values 9 initially, 12,6 after one step, and 16,8,4 after two. The American put option has terminal financial payoffs 0,1,5. Work backwards, comparing the immediate exercise payoff with its discounted continuation value.
At S1​=12, continuation is (3/10)(2⋅0+1)=3/10, exceeding the zero exercise payoff. At S1​=6, continuation is (3/10)(2⋅1+5)=21/10, while exercise pays three. Therefore exercise at the down node S1​=6 and continue at S1​=12. The initial exercise payoff is zero and the continuation value is
P0​=103​(2⋅103​+3)=2527​=1.08.​
(7)
The Snell envelope gives this backwards maximum recursion. For comparison, restricting exercise to expiry would give 81/100, so early exercise genuinely adds value.

While the up-and-out digital put is alive, its pricing domain is 0<S<B, t<T. Its Black-Scholes equation, terminal financial payoff, and absorbing boundary condition are
Ht​+21​σ2S2HSS​+rSHS​−rHH(S,T)H(B,t)​=0,=1{S<K}​,=0,​0<S<B, t<T,0<S<B,t≤T.​​
(23)
For completeness, the natural limiting condition is limS↓0​H(S,t)=e−r(T−t), with 0≤H(S,t)≤e−r(T−t). A positive geometric Brownian motion cannot hit zero in finite time, and from a very small starting value the survival and below-strike terminal events have probability tending to one. If the barrier has already been hit, its value is zero regardless of the current stock price.
For K>B, the terminal payoff tends to one from inside at S=B, while an already hit barrier pays zero. Thus there is a terminal-boundary corner discontinuity; the terminal condition is imposed only for S<B, not as a requirement of continuous extension at (B,T).

Let DK​(S,t)=Pd​(S,t) and γ=1−2r/σ2. The Black-Scholes reflection symmetry gives another solution (S/B)γDK​(B2/S,t). Subtracting it enforces absorption:
H(S,t)=DK​(S,t)−(S/B)γDK​(B2/S,t),0<S<B,B>K.​

At S=B the terms agree. At expiry the reflected argument satisfies B2/S>B>K, so its digital-put payoff vanishes; the first term supplies the required terminal payoff. These facts and the bounded pricing solution's uniqueness prove the formula. The up-and-out digital put price is zero once knocked out.

If 0<B<K, every surviving terminal price is already below the strike, so this is a barrier digital put paying one at expiry exactly when the upper barrier has been avoided. To use the Black-Scholes reflection symmetry, cap the free digital-put strike at B, rather than continuing to use K:
H(S,t)=DB​(S,t)−(S/B)γDB​(B2/S,t),0<S<B<K.​
(25)
Indeed, DB​ has terminal payoff one throughout the alive region, while its reflected argument exceeds B and hence has zero terminal payoff. The barrier values again cancel. Using DK​ in both terms would generally leave an unwanted reflected terminal payoff near the barrier.
Writing b=r−σ2/2 makes the discounted survival probability explicit:
H(S,t)=e−rτ[Φ(στ​log(B/S)−bτ​)−(S/B)γΦ(στ​log(S/B)−bτ​)].​
(26)
The price is independent of K in this case. Both cases combine by replacing the free strike with min(K,B) in the up-and-out digital put formula.
