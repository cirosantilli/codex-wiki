Apply Itô's formula to f(u,s)=e−αus. Its second stock derivative is zero, and the two drift terms cancel, giving
d(e−αuSu​)=σe−αudWu​.
(1)
Integrating and multiplying by eαT solves the linear stochastic differential equation:
ST​=eα(T−t)St​+σ∫tT​eα(T−u)dWu​.​
(2)
The Itô integral has a deterministic integrand, so it is a centered Gaussian random variable independent of the current filtration Ft​. Its variance follows from the Itô isometry. With τ=T−t,
E[ST​∣Ft​]=eατSt​,var(ST​∣Ft​)=⎩⎨⎧​2ασ2​(e2ατ−1),σ2τ,​α=0,α=0.​​
(3)
The second line is also the limit as α→0. The normal law asserted here is conditional on Ft​; an arbitrary random initial value need not give an unconditional normal distribution.

Use a self-financing portfolio with Δt​ units of the traded asset and the remainder in the continuous-time bank account. If its value equals V(St​,t), its self-financing condition is
dV=Δt​dSt​+r(V−Δt​St​)dt.
(4)
Meanwhile Itô's formula gives
dV=(Vt​+αSt​VS​+21​σ2VSS​)dt+σVS​dWt​.
(5)
Matching the Brownian motion coefficients gives the delta hedge Δt​=VS​. Matching drifts then cancels the physical drift α, leaving the pricing equation for a linear asset with additive noise:
Vt​+21​σ2VSS​+rSVS​−rV=0,V(S,T)=Po​(S).​
(6)
The domain is the full real line: additive noise can take the asset through zero. The diffusion coefficient is σ, so the second-derivative term has no factor S2. As usual, the replication argument assumes suitable differentiability and an admissible trading strategy.

The differential operator in part b is the infinitesimal generator of the auxiliary diffusion process dSu​=rSu​du+σdWu​, with killing rate r. The Feynman-Kac formula therefore gives
V(S,t)=e−r(T−t)EQ[Po​(ST​)∣St​=S].​
(7)
Equivalently, Itô's formula makes e−r(u−t)V(Su​,u) a local martingale under these dynamics; under appropriate growth and integrability assumptions it is a martingale, whose terminal conditional expectation yields the same formula. The expectation uses the drift-r law, rather than the physical drift-α law in part a. This is risk-neutral pricing.

Apply part a with α=r. For τ>0, the transition density is the normal distribution with mean m=Serτ and positive variance v=σ2(e2rτ−1)/(2r). Integrating the financial payoff against this probability density function gives
V(S,t)=2πv​e−rτ​∫−∞∞​Po​(x)exp[−2v(x−Serτ)2​]dx,v=2rσ2​(e2rτ−1).​
(8)
Thus d∗​=(x−Serτ)/v​, as required. The variance is positive even if r<0, because numerator and denominator have the same sign. For r=0, replace m by S and v by σ2τ; at expiry use the terminal value directly. The integral presumes an integrable financial payoff.

For a positive starting value, Itô's formula applied to logSu​ gives dlogSu​=(μ−σ2/2)du+σdWu​. Hence the geometric Brownian motion is
ST​=Sexp[(μ−σ2/2)τ+σ(WT​−Wt​)].​
(9)
The future Brownian increment WT​−Wt​ is independent of Ft​ and has normal distribution N(0,τ). If Wv​=Wt+v​−Wt​, then W is a fresh Brownian motion and the increment is exactly Wτ​. This specifies the meaning of the paper's abbreviated Wτ​; the original process value WT−t​ is not generally that future increment. The stochastic exponential solution also verifies positivity for S>0.

The Black-Scholes reflection symmetry becomes a weighted reflection after taking a logarithm. Put x=log(S/B) and v(x,t)=V(Bex,t). The multivariable chain rule transforms the equation into
vt​+21​σ2vxx​+bvx​−rv=0,b=r−σ2/2.
(12)
For w(x,t)=eγxv(−x,t), its left-hand side, after removing the factor eγx, is
vt​+21​σ2vxx​−(σ2γ+b)vx​+(21​σ2γ2+bγ−r)v,
(13)
evaluated at (−x,t). Taking γ=−2b/σ2 makes the derivative coefficient b and the additional zeroth-order term zero. Thus
W(S,t)=(S/B)1−2r/σ2V(B2/S,t)​
(14)

Subtract the reflected solution from part c. On the alive side of the down-and-out barrier option,
VB​(S,t)=V(S,t)−(S/B)1−2r/σ2V(B2/S,t),S>B.​
(15)
Both terms satisfy the Black-Scholes equation, and at S=B they cancel. At expiry, S≥B gives B2/S≤B<K, so the reflected log-call option has zero terminal financial payoff; the first term gives the required payoff. With the usual growth condition, uniqueness for the killed pricing problem identifies the expression as the risk-neutral price. After the barrier has been hit the contract value is zero. The algebraic image expression on S<B is not its financial value.

a OurBigBook.com
A European call option has nonnegative financial payoff, so its price is nonnegative under no-arbitrage. A second arbitrage comparison buys one call and a zero-coupon bond paying K, and shorts one share. Its terminal value is (ST​−K)++K−ST​=(K−ST​)+≥0, so its initial cost cannot be negative. Consequently
Cte​≥max(St​−Ke−r(T−t),0).​
(16)
The holder of an American call option can choose to wait until expiry, so Cta​≥Cte​. Since r>0 and t<T, an in-the-money call has Cta​≥St​−Ke−r(T−t)>St​−K: immediate exercise sacrifices value. An out-of-the-money exercise pays zero and cannot improve the holder's position. This proves no early exercise of a call without dividends: waiting is always at least as good, and is strictly better for positive immediate exercise payoff. In a degenerate market with zero continuation value an out-of-the-money holder can be indifferent; the Black-Scholes model with positive volatility gives strictly positive continuation value before expiry.

b OurBigBook.com
For the perpetual American call option, substitute a power Sq into the stationary Black-Scholes equation with continuous stock dividends. The characteristic equation is
F(q)=21​σ2q(q−1)+(r−y)q−r=0.
(17)
Its two roots have negative product −2r/σ2, so one is negative and one positive. The condition at zero excludes the negative power. Since F(1)=−y<0 and F(q)→∞ as q→∞, the positive root satisfies p>1; explicitly,
p=σ2−(r−y−σ2/2)+(r−y−σ2/2)2+2rσ2​​.
(18)
Value matching gives C(S)=(S^−K)(S/S^)p. The smooth fit condition gives p(S^−K)/S^=1, hence
S^=p−1pK​,C(S)={(S^−K)(S/S^)p,S−K,​0≤S<S^,S≥S^.​​
(19)
As y↓0, the positive root tends to 1 from above, so p→1, S^→∞​. For fixed S, the value tends to S. In the dividend-free perpetual limit no finite threshold attains that supremum; this agrees with the finite-maturity no early exercise of a call without dividends.

For a fixed exercise threshold Sˉ>K, the continuation value solves the same stationary equation but is matched to Sˉ−K at that threshold. Boundedness at zero removes the negative power. Thus the fixed-threshold value of a perpetual call is
C(S)=(Sˉ−K)(S/Sˉ)p,0≤S≤Sˉ.​
(20)
It is also (Sˉ−K)ESQ​[e−rτSˉ​;τSˉ​<∞], where τSˉ​ is the first up-crossing stopping time. Differentiating while keeping the current S fixed gives
∂Sˉ∂C​=SpSˉ−p−1[pK−(p−1)Sˉ].
(21)
This is positive for K<Sˉ<S^=pK/(p−1), so increasing such an early threshold increases its continuation value. The comparison assumes S<Sˉ; a policy already exercised has no remaining continuation value to vary.
If Sˉ>S^, then
C′(Sˉ−)=p(1−SˉK​)>1.
(22)
Since C(Sˉ)=Sˉ−K, the derivative comparison gives C(S)<S−K for S just below Sˉ. Therefore quoting this policy value as the price of an unrestricted American call permits arbitrage: buy the option below its immediate exercise value and exercise it at once. A contract that actually commits its holder to the threshold is a different claim; its lower value is a suboptimal policy value, not by itself an arbitrage. The perpetual American call option optimizes over all exercise policies and remains above its exercise payoff.
