Use a self-financing portfolio which holds Δ=VS​ shares and V−SVS​ in the bank account, withdrawing the claim's cash flow Cdt. A share's total gain includes the continuous dividend yield, so its gain is dS+DSdt. Matching the claim's total gain gives
dV+Cdt=VS​(dS+DSdt)+r(V−SVS​)dt.
(1)
By the Itô formula,
dV=[Vt​+(μ−D)SVS​+21​σ2S2VSS​]dt+σSVS​dW.
(2)
The delta hedge cancels the Brownian motion term. Comparing the remaining drifts yields the Black-Scholes equation with claim dividends, with the stock's own dividend yield included:
Vt​+21​σ2S2VSS​+(r−D)SVS​−rV=−C(S,t),V(S,T)=S​.
(3)
This gain calculation accounts for rebalancing through the self-financing portfolio condition. The physical expected-return parameter μ cancels, as required by risk-neutral pricing.

For claim dividends proportional to the underlying stock, put V(S,t)=Sϕ(t). Then VS​=ϕ, VSS​=0 and Vt​=Sϕ′, so the partial differential equation reduces to the linear ordinary differential equation
ϕ′−Dϕ=−f(t),ϕ(T)=1.
(4)
The integrating factor e−Dt gives (e−Dtϕ)′=−e−Dtf(t). Integrating backwards from the terminal value,
V(S,t)=S[e−D(T−t)+∫tT​e−D(u−t)f(u)du]​.
(5)
The formula assumes the displayed integral is finite. Under the risk-neutral measure, Et​[Su​]=Se(r−D)(u−t). Discounting each cash payment at its own payment time gives the same result:
V=Et​[e−r(T−t)ST​+∫tT​e−r(u−t)f(u)Su​du].
(6)
The interest rate disappears because every payment is proportional to the stock price.

Setting f=0 in b gives
V(S,t)=Se−D(T−t)​.
(7)
This is a prepaid forward contract: the holder pays now and receives one share at T, with no interim dividend payments. Buying e−D(T−t) shares now and reinvesting their continuous dividend yield grows the shareholding to one at expiry. Thus the displayed initial cost replicates the terminal delivery. When D=0, holding one share already does so and V=S.

For the non-dividend-paying stock, a self-financing portfolio replicating a smooth European option has Δ=VS​ shares and V−SVS​ in the bank account. Its gain satisfies
dV=VS​dS+r(V−SVS​)dt.
(8)
The Itô formula gives dV=(Vt​+μSVS​+σ2S2VSS​/2)dt+σSVS​dW. Matching its diffusion and drift with the portfolio gains cancels μ, yielding
Vt​+21​σ2S2VSS​+rSVS​−rV=0​.
(9)
The financial payoff supplies the terminal value. This is the Black-Scholes equation; the associated risk-neutral measure replaces the physical stock drift by r.

Write τ=T−t>0 and gn​(s)=(K−s)+n​. The European power put formula is
Pn​(S,t)=e−rτE[(K−SY)+n​],Y=e(r−σ2/2)τ+στ​Z,Z∼N(0,1).
(10)
Here Y>0 has a lognormal distribution. Since gn​ is decreasing and is a convex function, its discounted expected value is also decreasing and convex as a function of S. For S>0 it is smooth and strictly positive: the normal distribution assigns positive probability to SY<K whatever the current positive spot price. Its endpoints are
Pn​(0,t)=Kne−rτ,S→∞lim​Pn​(S,t)=0​.
(11)
The dominated convergence theorem justifies the second limit, because 0≤(K−SY)+n​≤Kn. For a quantitative sketch, the binomial theorem and truncated lognormal moment give
Pn​(S,t)=e−rτ∑j=0n​(−1)j(jn​)Kn−jSjejrτ+j(j−1)σ2τ/2Φ(−dj​),dj​=στ​log(S/K)+[r+(j−21​)σ2]τ​,
(12)
where Φ is the standard normal distribution function. This finite sum is useful for drawing the curve; its positive expectation form makes its qualitative shape clearer.
The solid curves use K=1, r=0.05, σ=0.30 and τ=1; the dashed curves show their terminal financial payoffs for comparison. Each price remains positive at S=K and tends smoothly towards zero beyond it. At small S, discounting gives a price below the undiscounted financial payoff; European exercise is permitted only at expiry.

Assume r>0, put b=S⋆∈(0,K) and let τb​=inf{u≥0:Su​≤b}. In the continuation region S>b, the time-independent Black-Scholes equation is a Cauchy-Euler equation. Substituting V=Sm gives
21​σ2m(m−1)+rm−r=(m−1)(21​σ2m+r)=0.
(13)
Set γ=2r/σ2>0. The two powers are S and S−γ. The boundary condition V(S)→0 as S→∞ removes the growing solution; value matching at b sets the coefficient. Hence the fixed-trigger value is
Vb​(S)=(K−b)n(S/b)−γ,S>b​.
(14)
For 0≤S≤b the policy exercises immediately, giving Vb​(S)=(K−S)n. The quoted continuation expression alone does not apply below the exercise boundary. Equivalently, the discounted boundary-hitting representation gives ES​[e−rτb​;τb​<∞]=(S/b)−γ, which multiplies the exercise financial payoff.

For S>b, maximize the coefficient H(b)=bγ(K−b)n of S−γ. Its logarithmic derivative is
H(b)H′(b)​=bγ​−K−bn​.
(15)
It changes sign once, at b=γK/(γ+n). The perpetual power put option therefore has candidate value
b=2r+nσ22rK​,Vn​(S)={(K−S)n,(K−b)n(S/b)−2r/σ2,​0≤S≤b,S>b.​​
(16)
The same boundary follows from smooth fit, −γ(K−b)n/b=−n(K−b)n−1. To establish optimality among all stopping times, rather than only fixed barriers, we verify the candidate. Let L=rS∂S​+σ2S2∂SS​/2 be the infinitesimal generator. The maximum property of H gives Vn​≥gn​ everywhere, and (L−r)Vn​=0 for S>b. For S≤b, set y=S/(K−S)≤γ/n. Then
gn​(L−r)gn​​=21​σ2[n(n−1)y2−γny−γ]≤−r(1+y)<0.
(17)
Thus the discounted candidate is a nonnegative supermartingale. The smooth fit removes the derivative-jump local time of a semimartingale term in the Itô formula. The optional stopping theorem and localization bound every exercise policy's discounted expected value by Vn​. Immediate exercise attains it below b; the first hitting time of b attains it above b. The terminal remainder vanishes since 0≤e−ruVn​(Su​)≤Kne−ru→0. This proves the displayed value.
For n=1 it reduces to the ordinary perpetual put option. Larger n lowers the optimal trigger, because the more strongly curved financial payoff makes further downward moves more valuable. If r=0, under the risk-neutral measure the stock tends to zero almost surely, so the supremum is Kn, approached as the exercise time tends to infinity and not attained at a finite time when S>0. If r<0, the values at deterministic exercise times grow without bound; the perpetual value is infinite. These cases do not admit the positive-rate formula above.

Write τ=T−t and a=e−ατ. The stochastic integral in b has a deterministic integrand, so it is a Gaussian random variable independent of the information available at t. Its variance, by the Itô isometry, is
v=σ2e−2αT∫tT​e2αudu=2ασ2​(1−a2).
(22)
Thus xT​ conditionally has a normal distribution with expected value m=axt​+(1−a)μ​ and variance v. The moment-generating function of a normal distribution gives Et​[exT​]=em+v/2, so the conditional mean of a log-mean-reverting price is
F(t,T)=exp[e−α(T−t)logSt​+μ​(1−e−α(T−t))+4ασ2​(1−e−2α(T−t))]​.
(23)
The correction v/2 is essential: exponentiating the conditional mean of the logarithm alone would omit the dispersion of the lognormal distribution.

Keep the maturity T fixed and write F(t,T)=expL(t,xt​), where
L(t,x)=ax+(1−a)μ​+4ασ2​(1−a2),a=e−α(T−t).
(24)
As calendar time increases, ∂t​a=αa, so Lx​=a, Lxx​=0 and Lt​=αa(x−μ​)−σ2a2/2. Applying the Itô formula to L and then to its exponential gives
dL=−21​σ2a2dt+σadZt​,F(t,T)dF(t,T)​=σe−α(T−t)dZt​​.
(25)
The Itô formula's exponential correction cancels the displayed drift of L. The zero drift also follows from the tower property of conditional expectation: for t≤u≤T, Et​[F(u,T)]=Et​[ST​]=F(t,T). In particular F(T,T)=ST​, while the quote's instantaneous spot volatility approaches σ as expiry approaches.
This is a martingale under the same measure used in the stipulated conditional expectation. To interpret it as futures pricing, that measure must be a suitable pricing measure. For a non-dividend-paying traded stock at a deterministic interest rate, the absence of arbitrage instead gives a forward contract delivery price St​er(T−t). A physical-measure expectation under the given mean-reverting drift is not automatically that price; the question's expectation convention is sufficient for the calculation above, but not for this additional identification.
