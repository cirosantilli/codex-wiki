The portfolio value is Πt​=ϕt​St​+ψt​Bt​. The self-financing condition means that changes in holdings are financed by trading within the portfolio, with no external infusion or withdrawal. Thus
dΠt​=ϕt​dSt​+ψt​dBt​=(μϕt​St​+rψt​Bt​)dt+σϕt​dWt​.​
(1)
The holdings are predictable and satisfy the usual stochastic-integrability and admissibility conditions; without admissibility, doubling strategies would spoil the intended no-arbitrage interpretation.

ii OurBigBook.com
For V∈C1,2, Itô's formula gives
dV=(Vt​+μsVs​+2σ2​Vss​)dt+σVs​dW.
(2)
Match the Brownian coefficient with the self-financing portfolio by choosing the option delta ϕ=Vs​ and bond value ψB=V−sVs​. Equating drifts cancels the physical drift μ and gives
Vt​+rsVs​+2σ2​Vss​−rV=0,(t,s)∈[0,T)×R,V(T,s)=F(s).​
(3)
This is the pricing equation for a linear asset with additive noise. Its spatial domain is the whole real line, because the asset can be negative. In particular the diffusion term is σ2Vss​/2, not σ2s2Vss​/2. The derivation is delta hedging and does not impose a boundary condition at s=0.

Set θt​=(μ−r)St​/σ. The Girsanov theorem uses the density
ZT​=exp(−∫0T​θu​dWu​−21​∫0T​θu2​du),WtQ​=Wt​+∫0t​θu​du.
(4)
For these nonexplosive linear stochastic differential equations, the linear drift change of measure ensures that Z is a true martingale on each finite horizon. This matters: a stochastic exponential being a local martingale alone would not prove that its terminal expectation is one. Its strict positivity gives an equivalent risk-neutral measure Q, under which
dSt​=rSt​dt+σdWtQ​.​
(5)
The discounted asset is then a martingale. The Feynman-Kac formula, equivalently risk-neutral valuation of the replicable claim, gives
V(t,s)=e−rτEQ​[F(ST​)∣St​=s].​
(6)
Here the payoff must meet the usual integrability and replication assumptions; the bounded digital payoff below does.

The discounted stock over the remaining horizon satisfies
e−rτST​=s+σ∫tT​e−r(u−t)dWuQ​.
(7)
The Itô integral on the right is a centered normal random variable with variance aτ​=(1−e−2rτ)/(2r). Therefore Q(ST​≥0∣St​=s)=Φ(d), where d=s/(σaτ​​). The digital option price and its option delta are
V(t,s)=e−rτΦ(d),Δ(t,s)=σaτ​​e−rτϕ(d)​.​
(8)
The formula also shows V(t,0)=e−rτ/2 for every t<T. At maturity use the specified step payoff, including its chosen value one at zero; the terminal discontinuity is not removed by the earlier-time smoothing.

Since aτ​ does not depend on σ, differentiation of the digital option value gives its vega:
∂σ∂V​=−σe−rτ​dϕ(d).​
(9)
Thus vega is negative for s>0, zero at s=0, and positive for s<0. Increasing volatility spreads the conditional normal distribution around its mean: when the current asset value is positive this reduces the chance of ending positive, while for a negative starting value it increases that chance. At zero the distribution stays symmetric and the probability remains one half. A digital payoff need not have the positive vega familiar from a European call option.

a OurBigBook.com
A self-financing replicating portfolio for a terminal payoff F(ST​) has the same terminal value on every relevant path, with all earlier rebalancing funded internally. In a complete market its initial cost is unique under no-arbitrage: otherwise buy the cheaper payoff and sell the more expensive identical payoff.
In the Black-Scholes model, the portfolio holds Vs​ shares and bond value V−SVs​, where V solves the Black-Scholes equation with terminal data F. Itô's formula verifies the self-financing condition. Equivalently,
V(t,s)=e−rτEQ​[F(ST​)∣St​=s],dSt​=rSt​dt+σSt​dWtQ​.​
(10)
This relates claim replication, the Black-Scholes equation, and risk-neutral valuation.

b OurBigBook.com
Buy 1/F0T​ shares and take a short forward contract for 1/F0T​ shares at delivery price F0T​. The forward has zero initial value, and the combined terminal payoff is
F0T​ST​​+F0T​F0T​−ST​​=1.
(11)
Therefore the synthetic zero-coupon bond has initial price
P(0,T)=F0T​S0​​.​
(12)
Its implied continuously compounded maturity yield is rT​=T−1log(F0T​/S0​)>0.
To replicate a constant-rate risk-free asset throughout the interval, the market must support dynamically coherent discounting, not just this one terminal payment. A sufficient condition is that for all 0≤t≤u≤T the fair forward prices satisfy Ftu​=St​er(u−t) for the same deterministic r>0, with the required stock/forward positions tradable and no intervening payments. Then P(t,u)=St​/Ftu​=e−r(u−t), and a suitably scaled fixed-maturity zero-coupon bond grows deterministically as ert. A single initial forward quote does not establish these intermediate-time conditions; even a flat initial yield curve need not imply deterministic future rates.

c OurBigBook.com
Under the risk-neutral measure, write
ST​/s=exp((r−σ2/2)τ+στ​Z),Z∼N(0,1).
(13)
Complete the square in the normal density after multiplying by the power of ST​/s. The truncated lognormal moment gives, for K>0,
Vp​(t,s,K)=Mp​(τ)Φ(dp​),Mp​(τ)=e[pr+p(p−1)σ2/2]τ,dp​=στ​log(s/K)+[r+(p−1/2)σ2]τ​.​
(14)
All real powers have finite moments in this lognormal distribution. If K=0, the indicator is identically one and Vp​=Mp​. The function Vp​ is an undiscounted conditional moment; the option prices below additionally include the discount factor.

The logarithm of the geometric average is
logST​=logS0​+21​(r−σ2/2)T+Tσ​∫0T​WuQ​du.
(17)
By stochastic Fubini theorem, ∫0T​Wu​du=∫0T​(T−v)dWv​, a centered Gaussian random variable with variance T3/3. Therefore
log((ST​)p)∼N(m,v),m=p[logS0​+21​(r−σ2/2)T],v=3p2σ2T​.
(18)
The Geometric Asian option is consequently a call on this lognormal random variable. Completing the square as in part c gives
e−rT[em+v/2Φ(d1​)−KΦ(d2​)],d2​=v​m−logK​,d1​=d2​+v​.​
(19)
The averaging changes both the log-mean and log-variance; replacing ST​ by an ordinary stock price at a shorter maturity without these adjustments would give the wrong formula.

Solution

i OurBigBook.com
For a long position in the claim, hold −VS​ shares and bank value SVS​−V so that claim plus hedge has zero initial value. The Black-Scholes equation and Itô's formula show that the total position is self-financing and its Brownian and drift terms cancel. Equivalently, the positive replicating portfolio holds VS​ shares and bond value V−SVS​.
Under the usual growth, integrability and admissibility conditions, no-arbitrage forces the claim price to be V: a different price could be traded against the replicating portfolio. The qualification matters because an arbitrary formal PDE solution without a suitable financial class need not be a valid price.

Solution

ii OurBigBook.com
Let τB​=inf{u≥t:Su​≤B}. While the contract is alive, the down-and-out put pays (K−ST​)+1{τB​>T}​ at maturity; a prior touch has already knocked it out.
The truncated terminal payoff can be represented by the ordinary European-price combination
F(S,t):=C(S,t;K)−C(S,t;B)+(K−B)Cd​(S,t;B).
(20)
At maturity its three regions are
F(S,T)=(K−S)+1{S≥B}​.​
(21)
Indeed it is zero below B, equals K−S between B and K, and is zero above K. This supplies the terminal building block for reflection.
The zero-below-barrier property is a property of the terminal payoff. Before maturity, F(S,t) need not vanish for S<B, because the ordinary European contracts can still finish above B. The killed barrier option price, rather than this unrestricted European combination, is zero after a touch.

Words: 113 Articles: 1

Solution
Words: 113
iii OurBigBook.com
Put q=1−2r/σ2. The Black-Scholes reflection symmetry maps F to (S/B)qF(B2/S,t), which solves the same PDE. Their difference vanishes at S=B. For S>B at maturity the reflected argument is below B, where the terminal function in part ii is zero. Thus the boundary and terminal data of the alive down-and-out put are satisfied:
G(S,t)=F(S,t)−(BS​)qF(B2/S,t),S>B.​
(22)
The killed-domain Feynman-Kac formula identifies this solution with the discounted payoff conditional on survival. For S≤B, or after any prior barrier hit, the actual price is zero. The reflected difference is a formula on the alive domain S>B; extending that algebraic expression to S<B generally gives a nonzero, possibly negative function and is not a valid barrier-option price.

i OurBigBook.com
A down-and-out digital call with strike at the barrier pays one exactly when the barrier is avoided until expiry. Reflection therefore gives its alive price
Dout​(S,t)=Cd​(S,t;B)−(BS​)qCd​(B2/S,t;B),S>B.
(23)
The complementary contract pays one at expiry when the barrier has been hit. Its price is
H(t,S)=e−rτ−Dout​(S,t),S>B and no previous hit.​
(24)
This is a cash-at-expiry one-touch option, not cash paid at the first touch. After the barrier has already been hit, its price is e−rτ regardless of the current spot. In particular this branch applies immediately for S≤B. Current spot alone does not encode the full path history.

Solution

ii OurBigBook.com
If the barrier is reached, path continuity gives SτB​​=B and the early payment is K−B. If it is not reached, the terminal payment is exactly the down-and-out put payoff. When r=0, moving a fixed payment from the hitting time to expiry does not change its value. Hence the barrier-triggered put price is
EB​(S,t)=G(S,t)+(K−B)H(t,S),r=0.​
(25)
Here G,H are evaluated with zero interest rate. If the barrier has already been hit, the exploding put has been exercised and has no remaining value after its payment; the displayed replication concerns an initially alive contract and its cash flows.

iii

Words: 279 Articles: 1
b OurBigBook.com
Solution
Words: 279
iii OurBigBook.com
The barrier-triggered put exercises at the particular stopping time τB​∧T. The American put option optimizes over all allowed exercise times, so
EB​(S,t)≤A(S,t;K).​
(26)
It can be strictly smaller, but can never exceed the American value. Equality is possible if immediate exercise is already triggered and optimal; a fixed barrier is not generally the optimal finite-maturity exercise rule.
The comparison with the European put option can go in either direction. By the early exercise premium for a fixed barrier,
EB​(S,t)−P(S,t;K)=EQ​[e−r(τB​−t){K−B−P(B,τB​;K)}1{τB​≤T}​∣St​=S].
(27)
Thus an early payment gains interest but sacrifices remaining put time value; the expression inside braces need not have a fixed sign for arbitrary barriers.
For a concrete general construction take current spot S≥K, r>0, σ>0, and t<T. Set γ=2r/σ2 and b∞​=Kγ/(1+γ), the perpetual put option exercise boundary. For 0<B<b∞​, the perpetual value at B equals K−B and strictly dominates the finite-maturity European value. The premium formula and positive probability of hitting B therefore give EB​>P. Strictness follows from the perpetual verification argument: before a fixed positive expiry, its discounted value loses drift rK whenever the stock is below b∞​, with positive expected occupation time when started at B<b∞​.
Conversely, for B<K sufficiently close to K, every possible payment of the exploding put is at most K−B, whether paid at touch or on surviving to expiry. Hence 0≤EB​≤K−B→0, while P(S,t;K)>0. This gives EB​<P for such barriers, and also strict inequality below the American value.
These examples establish both possibilities in general, not a claim that every fixed initial state admits both. If S<b∞​ and only alive barriers B<S are allowed, all those barriers are in the same perpetual stopping region and the premium comparison is nonnegative.
