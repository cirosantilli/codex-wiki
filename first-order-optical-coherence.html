First-order optical coherence (g12(1)​(τ))

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics Physical optics Optical coherence
OurBigBook.com Words: 4k Articles: 21
Consider one selected polarization of waves, represented by complex fields E1​(t) and E2​(t) at two sampling positions. Suppose their second moments are finite and stationary: Jj​=⟨∣Ej​(t)∣2⟩ and the following uncentered wave-field correlation do not depend on the absolute time t. Define
Γ12​(τ)=⟨E1∗​(t)E2​(t+τ)⟩,g12(1)​(τ)=J1​J2​​Γ12​(τ)​,J1​,J2​>0.
(46)
The star denotes the complex conjugate; brackets denote an ensemble average. A long detector average estimates this quantity only if it adequately samples the fluctuations. The superscript 1 identifies a field correlation: it does not mean the autocorrelation of the measured light intensity. The one-position version is g(1)(τ)=⟨E∗(t)E(t+τ)⟩/⟨∣E∣2⟩, so g(1)(0)=1 for every nonzero field covered by this definition; this alone says nothing about its correlation at nonzero delay. [ref].
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives 0≤∣g12(1)​(τ)∣≤1. At a specified delay, magnitude one means E2​(t+τ)=aE1​(t) in mean square for a fixed nonzero complex a: the samples have a reproducible relation in both wave amplitude and wave phase. Magnitude zero means the averaged field cross term vanishes. It need not mean statistical independence. A deterministic phase difference changes the argument of g(1), not its complex modulus; a distorted but fixed wavefront can therefore remain fully coherent. [ref].
To see the observable, recombine the samples in the same detected spatial mode with matched wave polarizations. With a common positive conversion factor C, let Ij​=CJj​ be the single-arm light intensities reaching that detector. Adding E1​(t)+eiϕE2​(t+τ) gives
I(ϕ,τ)=I1​+I2​+2I1​I2​​Re[eiϕg12(1)​(τ)],V(τ)=I1​+I2​2I1​I2​​​∣g12(1)​(τ)∣.
(47)
This follows simply by expanding the squared complex modulus and averaging, then taking the maximum and minimum as ϕ spans a full cycle. The extra wave phase scan must leave I1​,I2​ and the magnitude of the correlation effectively unchanged. Thus interferometric visibility equals ∣g(1)∣ only for equal nonzero light intensities and ideal mode matching. For I2​/I1​=1/4, perfectly coherent beams have interferometric visibility 0.8; a measured value 0.4 would imply ∣g(1)∣=0.5 under these assumptions. Orthogonal linear polarizations, background light, unresolved interference fringes or vibrations can reduce measured contrast further. One absent arm gives zero contrast and makes the normalized two-sample correlation undefined.
