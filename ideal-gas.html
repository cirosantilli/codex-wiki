Ideal gas

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics
OurBigBook.com Words: 4k Articles: 25
An ideal gas is a model of a dilute gas whose particle centres move according to classical mechanics and occupy negligible volume compared with their container. Treating the particles as point masses removes the molecular-size correction; neglecting interactions between separated particles removes the intermolecular contribution to potential energy. The usual kinetic theory model retains brief elastic collisions with the walls and, optionally, with other particles: these transfer momentum while conserving the total kinetic energy of the colliding bodies. There is no appreciable interaction energy stored between collisions. These assumptions lead to the ideal gas law, PV=NkB​T. [ref] [ref]
The model's attraction is its universality: at the same temperature and number density, different classical ideal gases have the same pressure, even when their particle masses differ. In ideal gas pressure from wall collisions, heavier particles move more slowly at a given temperature, and the two effects cancel. For example, one mole of any ideal gas at 300K in 0.025m3 has pressure approximately 99.774kPa, as computed under the ideal gas law.
The ideal gas law describes the translational contribution to pressure; it does not specify all of the internal energy or the heat capacity. A monatomic gas has three classical translational degrees of freedom, whereas a gas of molecules can also store rotational kinetic energy and energy in vibrational normal modes. An equation of state alone therefore does not determine how much heat a chosen temperature increase requires. It also does not select a process: isothermal expansion and adiabatic expansion need different additional constraints. [ref]
A real gas departs from this approximation when particle size or interactions become important, especially at high number density or low temperature near a phase transition. The Van der Waals equation adds such corrections. Independently, at sufficiently low temperature or high number density, quantum statistics can become essential: even particles with negligible interactions need not obey the classical ideal gas law. The classical statistical assumption is the nondegenerate gas regime, which is distinct from neglecting interactions. [ref] [ref]

Classical ideal-gas entropy limits (Sm​=S0​−Rln(p/p0​)+Cmp​ln(T/T0​))

Words: 65
Ideal gas OurBigBook.com
For constant positive molar heat capacity, the ideal gas entropy grows without bound on isothermal dilution to unbounded volume, corresponding to p→0. This is a meaningful limiting configuration count, not an infinite entropy in a finite container. Extrapolation to T→0 at fixed positive pressure instead predicts negative infinite entropy. That extrapolation fails: phase changes, quantum statistics and temperature-dependent heat capacities prevent a real equilibrium substance from retaining the same classical formula down to zero temperature.

Isobaric ideal-gas internal-energy density (U/V=p/(γ−1))

Words: 67
Ideal gas OurBigBook.com
An ideal gas with constant heat capacity ratio γ has U=ncV,m​T up to a consistent thermal-energy reference, and cV,m​=R/(γ−1). Combining this with pV=nRT gives the displayed thermal internal-energy density. At fixed pressure it does not grow with temperature: the particle density falls as 1/T. A fixed-volume space exchanging gas with the atmosphere therefore differs from a sealed fixed-particle system. Composition changes, activated internal modes or a pressure change require a modified comparison.

Specific gas constant (R)

Words: 45
Ideal gas OurBigBook.com
The gas constant per unit mass for a specified composition. An ideal gas obeys p=ρRT. With dimensionless mean molecular weight μ relative to an atomic mass unit, R=kB​/(μmu​); the common astrophysical notation R∗/μ uses a modified mass-based constant rather than the molar gas constant without a mass conversion.

Two-dimensional ideal gas (q=A/Λ2)

Words: 65
Ideal gas OurBigBook.com
An ideal gas whose particles move in an area A with two independent translational momentum components. In its dilute classical limit the one-particle translational partition function is q=A/Λ2, where Λ is the thermal de Broglie wavelength, and ZN​=qN/N!. The molar gas constant is the product of the Avogadro constant and the Boltzmann constant. It converts the particle-number form of the ideal gas law, pV=NkB​T, into the amount-of-substance form pV=nRT, using N=nNA​ with n measured in moles.

Ideal gas law (PV=NkB​T=nmol​RT)

Words: 706 Articles: 1
Ideal gas OurBigBook.com
The ideal gas law is the equation of state relating the absolute pressure P, volume V, particle count N, and absolute thermodynamic temperature T of a classical ideal gas:
PV=NkB​T=nmol​RT,N=nmol​NA​,R=NA​kB​.
(2)
Here kB​ is the Boltzmann constant, NA​ is the Avogadro constant, R is the molar gas constant, and nmol​ is the amount of substance in moles. The distinct symbol nmol​ avoids confusing amount of substance with the number density n=N/V, measured in m−3: the same law can be written P=nkB​T. Use absolute pressure, relative to a vacuum, rather than an unconverted gauge pressure. [ref]
The exact SI definitions of the Avogadro constant and Boltzmann constant give
R=(6.02214076×1023)(1.380649×10−23)Jmol−1K−1=8.31446261815324Jmol−1K−1.
(3)
This numerical value is exact; the applicability of the ideal gas model to a real gas is an approximation. [ref] [ref]
For nmol​=1mol, T=300K, and V=0.025m3, direct substitution gives
RT=2494.338785445972Jmol−1,P=Vnmol​RT​=99773.55141783888Pa≈99.774kPa.
(4)
At fixed temperature and particle count, doubling the volume to 0.050m3 halves the pressure to approximately 49.887kPa: this is Boyle's law. At fixed volume and particle count, doubling the temperature from 300K to 600K doubles the pressure to approximately 199.547kPa. These ratios use kelvins, not readings in the degree Celsius scale: 300K is 26.85∘C, and 600K is 326.85∘C.
The curved slices show Boyle's law at three temperatures; the straight slice shows proportionality to absolute temperature at one fixed volume. They are relations among equilibrium states, rather than a prescribed process history. Compression could hold the temperature fixed through heat transfer, or raise it during adiabatic compression. To calculate a process's heat or work, supplement the ideal gas law with the process constraint, the first law of thermodynamics, and the relevant internal energy or heat capacity information. The graph's extrapolation toward T=0 is mathematical; it does not assert that a real gas remains classical or gaseous there.
For a stellar mixture with fixed mean molecular weight, the ideal gas law can be written P=RρT/μ, where ρ is mass density and R is the stellar gas constant. It assumes negligible interaction corrections and a nondegenerate gas; excitation or ionization can change the particle count and thermodynamic response.
In his 1834 memoir on the motive power of heat, Émile Clapeyron combined gas laws into a relation between pressure, volume, and temperature while analysing heat engines (printed page 164). The equation above uses modern SI notation and absolute thermodynamic temperature; it is not a transcription of his historical temperature convention. [ref]

Ideal gas pressure from wall collisions (P=Nm⟨vx2​⟩/V)

Words: 298
Ideal gas law OurBigBook.com
Consider a particle of mass m in a rigid rectangular box of length L along x and wall surface area A, so V=AL is the volume. Choose +x toward the right wall. For an incoming normal velocity vx​=v>0, an elastic collision reverses the normal momentum:
px,in​=mv,px,out​=−mv,Δpx,particle​=−2mv,Jx,wall​=+2mv.
(5)
The impulse delivered to the wall points outward, toward +x, by Newton's third law. The particle's opposite momentum change is not the wall's impulse. A tangential velocity component is unchanged by the ideal smooth-wall collision.
Ignoring intervening particle-particle collisions in this counting model, the particle travels a normal distance 2L before hitting the same wall again. Its hit rate is ∣vx​∣/(2L); multiplying by the impulse per hit gives the time-averaged normal force and its contribution to pressure:
F⊥,1​=(2m∣vx​∣)2L∣vx​∣​=Lmvx2​​,P1​=AF⊥,1​​=Vmvx2​​.
(6)
For example, stipulate m=4×10−26kg, ∣vx​∣=500ms−1, L=0.10m, and A=0.010m2. Then V=0.0010m3, the impulse per hit is 4×10−23Ns, the hit rate is 2500s−1, and P1​=10−17Pa. This is one particle's tiny time-averaged contribution to one wall's pressure, not an isotropic equilibrium gas by itself.
For N particles of equal mass, add their force contributions and define ⟨vx2​⟩=N−1∑i=1N​vx,i2​. In the gas's rest frame, isotropy gives ⟨vx2​⟩=⟨vy2​⟩=⟨vz2​⟩=⟨v2⟩/3, where v2=vx2​+vy2​+vz2​. Hence
P=VNm⟨vx2​⟩​=3VNm⟨v2⟩​,21​m⟨v2⟩=23​kB​T⟹PV=NkB​T.
(7)
The final equality uses the classical equipartition theorem. The kinetic pressure of an isotropic gas therefore recovers the ideal gas law from momentum transfers. Doubling the normal speed would double both the impulse per hit and the hit rate, giving four times the pressure contribution; pressure measures a mean squared speed. Interparticle elastic collisions redistribute velocities and help establish isotropy; the individual round-trip times need not remain 2L/∣vx​∣, but the equilibrium momentum flux result survives. [ref]

Monatomic gas (γ=5/3)

Words: 78
Ideal gas OurBigBook.com
A monatomic gas consists of individual atoms or ions rather than molecules. In the classical dilute ideal-gas regime, its three translational degrees of freedom give U=3NkB​T/2 by equipartition theorem. Thus CV​=3NkB​/2, while CP​−CV​=NkB​ follows by differentiating the ideal-gas enthalpy H=U+PV=5NkB​T/2. Consequently the adiabatic index is γ=CP​/CV​=5/3 and the specific internal energy is e=3P/(2ρ). The same translational result applies to a fully ionized mixture when the particle numbers are fixed. This excludes additional excitation, ionization and radiation contributions to the heat capacity.

Stellar gas constant (R=kB​/mu​)

In stellar ideal gas equations, the stellar gas constant R=kB​/mu​ converts a dimensionless mean molecular weight μ into the specific thermal relation P=RρT/μ. Here mu​ is the reference atomic mass unit, commonly approximated by the proton mass in simplified hydrogen-helium models. It has units of energy per mass per temperature. Heat capacity ratio (γ)

Words: 1k Articles: 2
Ideal gas OurBigBook.com
The heat capacity ratio compares heat capacity at constant pressure Cp​ with heat capacity at constant volume CV​ at the same equilibrium state and fixed composition:
γ=CV​Cp​​=CV,m​Cp,m​​=cV​cp​​.
(8)
Here Cp​,CV​ refer to the whole sample, in JK−1; molar heat capacities Cp,m​=Cp​/n and CV,m​=CV​/n are per mole; specific heat capacities cp​=Cp​/m and cV​=CV​/m are per unit mass. The amount of substance n or mass m cancels only when both capacities use the same normalization. Thus heat capacity ratio is dimensionless and independent of sample size. For a fixed-composition ideal gas with positive finite CV​, Mayer's relation gives
γ(T)=1+CV​(T)nR​=1+CV,m​(T)R​>1,
(9)
where R is the molar gas constant. More ways to store internal energy make a given temperature increase require more heat, bringing heat capacity ratio closer to unity. [ref]
The equipartition theorem predicts CV,m​=fR/2 when f independent quadratic terms in molecular energy are thermally active and their number stays fixed; consequently γ=1+2/f. A classical monatomic gas with no appreciable electronic excitation has f=3, giving CV,m​=3R/2 and γ=5/3. For an ideal gas of diatomic molecules with three translational and two rotational terms active, but its vibrational normal mode effectively frozen, f=5 gives CV,m​=5R/2 and γ=7/5. A fully classical harmonic vibration adds both kinetic energy and potential energy, giving f=7 and γ=9/7. These are model regimes, not universal constants of every gas: rotational freezing, electronic excitation, and changing composition invalidate the simple count.[ref]
The mechanical significance of heat capacity ratio follows from the first law of thermodynamics. For a fixed amount of ideal gas in a reversible adiabatic process, CV​(T)dT=−pdV. Combining this with the ideal gas law gives the local relation
(∂lnV∂lnp​)S​=−γ(T),∫T1​T2​​TCV​(T)​dT+nRlnV1​V2​​=0.
(10)
The partial derivative holds at fixed entropy and composition even when heat capacity ratio varies with temperature. The finite-path formulas pVγ=constant and TVγ−1=constant require the additional approximation of constant heat capacity ratio. A rapid compression also needs internal equilibration to approximate this path; rapid motion alone does not ensure reversibility. The same local response gives isentropic compressibility 1/(γp) and, for small sound disturbances in this ideal gas regime, speed of sound a=γp/ρ​, where ρ is mass density. Thus thermal storage controls mechanical stiffness, sound propagation and the measurements in Rüchardt experiment and Clément-Desormes experiment.

Rüchardt experiment

Words: 415
Heat capacity ratio OurBigBook.com
The Rüchardt experiment infers heat capacity ratio from the period of an oscillation of a piston supported by trapped gas. Use a vertical precision cylinder with a close-fitting movable piston, known moving mass M, face area A, trapped equilibrium volume V0​, and local atmospheric pressure patm​. With negligible static friction, force balance gives the absolute gas pressure p0​=patm​+Mg/A, where g is gravitational acceleration. Displace the piston slightly and release it: compression raises pressure, and expansion lowers it, producing a restoring force. The oscillation is a mechanical probe of thermal response, without separately measuring two heat inputs. [ref]
Let x be upward displacement from equilibrium, so V=V0​+Ax. For constant heat capacity ratio and a reversible adiabatic process, the net upward force, including the ambient load and weight, is
F(x)=Ap0​[(1+V0​Ax​)−γ−1]≃−V0​γp0​A2​x.
(11)
The linear approximation requires ∣Ax∣/V0​≪1. Applying Newton's second law gives simple harmonic motion with
ω02​=MV0​γp0​A2​,γ=p0​A2T24π2MV0​​,T=ω0​2π​.
(12)
Here ω0​ is the undamped angular frequency and T the undamped oscillation period, rather than temperature. Record repeated cycles, measure M,A,V0​,patm​ independently, and include all connected trapped volume. An illustrative model with M=0.020kg, A=2.0×10−4m2, V0​=0.010m3, patm​=100000Pa, g=9.81ms−2 and γ=1.4 gives p0​=100981Pa and T≃1.182s. These stipulated values are not measurements.
In Shearwood and Sloan's implementation, piston-mounted magnets induce an electromotive force in a stationary coil through Faraday's law of induction; an oscilloscope records the signal. They observed faster oscillations at smaller trapped volume values and stronger damping at small cylinder volume values. The signal tracks velocity locally, so its amplitude is not directly piston displacement. Calibration also identified inlet dead space beyond the graduated cylinder volume. [ref]
Repeat at smaller initial amplitudes to test the linear approximation, and compare several V0​ values. Heat transfer weakens the restoring force toward the isothermal process limit, biasing the inference toward 1. Check leakage through resting-piston drift and before-and-after trapped volume readings. For viscous damping with envelope e−t/τ, the measured angular frequency obeys ω02​=ωd2​+τ−2; this correction does not remove thermal leakage. Internal equilibration must be faster than an oscillation period, with heat transfer and leakage slow over a run. Large amplitude or sticking friction require a richer model. [ref]

Clément-Desormes experiment

Words: 389
Heat capacity ratio OurBigBook.com
The Clément-Desormes experiment infers heat capacity ratio from a rapid pressure reduction followed by reheating. Use a rigid bottle, pump or bellows, a quick release valve, and a U-tube pressure gauge connected to the bottle and atmosphere. First pump to a small excess pressure, close the inlet, and wait for thermal equilibrium with the room at T0​. Measure p1​>pa​, where pa​ is local absolute atmospheric pressure. Briefly open the release valve until the bottle reaches pa​, then close it promptly. The remaining gas is cooler than the room. As it warms back to T0​ at fixed bottle volume, its pressure rises to p3​, with pa​<p3​<p1​ in the ideal procedure. The rebound reveals cooling during release. [ref]
The bottle loses molecules while the valve is open: it is not a closed system whose geometrical volume expands. In the well-mixed, negligible-heat transfer discharge model with constant heat capacity ratio, the remaining gas obeys T2​/T0​=(pa​/p1​)(γ−1)/γ. Subsequent heating at fixed amount of substance and bottle volume gives p3​/pa​=T0​/T2​ by the ideal gas law. Eliminating the unmeasured T2​ yields
γ=ln(p1​/p3​)ln(p1​/pa​)​≃Δp1​−Δp3​Δp1​​=h1​−h3​h1​​,Δpi​=pi​−pa​=ρliquid​ghi​.
(13)
The logarithms use absolute pressure ratios. The approximation requires small gauge pressures Δpi​≪pa​; hi​ are signed height differences between the two liquid surfaces, and the final equality uses the same liquid in both readings with hydrostatic equilibrium. It is not an instruction to insert gauge readings into the logarithmic formula. For synthetic states generated with pa​=100000Pa, p1​=102000Pa and γ=1.4, the model gives p3​≃100567.39Pa. The logarithmic inference recovers 1.4; the small-pressure approximation gives approximately 1.3961.
Wait for a stable initial pressure after pumping and for a stable final pressure after closing. Vary the valve-open interval and initial excess pressure across repeated trials: closing too early leaves residual excess pressure, while leaving the valve open during warming lets more gas escape and suppresses the rebound. Test the seal for leaks, and read both liquid levels consistently. In a variant with incomplete release, measure the actual immediate post-release absolute pressure p2​ and replace the numerator by ln(p1​/p2​). The Zurich laboratory manual explains that liquid-column oscillations can delay this reading until appreciable reheating has occurred; its proposed backward extrapolation assumes an exponential thermal relaxation and must be checked against the recorded readings. These controls separate a thermal signal from valve timing, instrumental lag and leakage. [ref]

Nondegenerate gas

Words: 50
Ideal gas OurBigBook.com
A gas is nondegenerate when its occupation numbers are low enough that quantum statistics reduce to the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution. A common criterion is nλth3​≪1, where λth​ is the thermal de Broglie wavelength.
For an ideal gas, this is the classical statistical regime; nondegeneracy alone does not assert the absence of interactions.

Internal energy of an ideal gas

Words: 515
Ideal gas OurBigBook.com
For a fixed amount of substance and composition of classical ideal gas, internal energy depends only on temperature: U=U(T,n), with no independent volume dependence. This does not require constant heat capacity at constant volume. Between equilibrium endpoints,
dU=CV​(T)dT=nCV,m​(T)dT,U(T2​)−U(T1​)=n∫T1​T2​​CV,m​(T)dT.
(14)
Here CV​ is total heat capacity at constant volume, CV,m​ is molar heat capacity, and U/m for sample mass m is specific internal energy, whose change is ∫T1​T2​​cV​(T)dT. These endpoint relations apply even when the intervening process is not at fixed volume; they do not say that transferred heat always equals ΔU. With constant CV​, the safe statement is U(T)=U(T∗​)+CV​(T−T∗​). Writing U=CV​T additionally chooses the reference energy; it does not prove that a physical gas remains classical and ideal down to zero temperature. [ref]
A thermodynamic proof outline uses the first law of thermodynamics in equilibrium form, dU=TdS−pdV, and the Maxwell relation (∂S/∂V)T​=(∂p/∂T)V​:
(∂V∂U​)T​=T(∂T∂p​)V​−p=TVnR​−VnRT​=0.
(15)
The last step uses the ideal gas law and the molar gas constant R, with fixed composition and amount of substance. Microscopically, separated ideal gas particles store no intermolecular interaction energy; their translational kinetic energy and any internal molecular energy are determined by temperature. Negligible intermolecular interactions still allow a molecule to have internal vibrational potential energy.
An original analytical model makes the distinction between an ideal gas and constant heat capacity concrete. Assume classical translation, two fully active rotational terms, and one independent quantum harmonic oscillator per diatomic molecule, with fixed angular frequency ω. Define the vibrational temperature scale Θ=ℏω/kB​, using the reduced Planck constant ℏ=h/(2π) and Boltzmann constant kB​. Summing its thermal occupations gives
nRU(T)−U∗​​=25​T+eΘ/T−1Θ​,RCV,m​(T)​=25​+(1−e−x)2x2e−x​,x=TΘ​.
(16)
The constant U∗​ includes the chosen reference and zero-point energy, whose temperature derivative vanishes. When T≪Θ the vibrational contribution to heat capacity is exponentially small; when T≫Θ it tends to R per mole. This model assumes rotations remain active throughout the interval and excludes dissociation, electronic excitation and real gas corrections. Its low-T/Θ edge is not a model of rotational freezing or of an actual substance at absolute zero.[ref]
For this stipulated model, heating from T1​=0.2Θ to T2​=Θ gives ΔU/(nRΘ)≃2.57519. Holding the frozen-vibration value CV,m​=5R/2 throughout would give 2, underestimating the required internal energy increase by about 22.3% of the model's full value. The integral measures all thermal storage activated along the interval. At fixed volume with only heat input, this is also the supplied heat; at fixed pressure, expansion work must be added through Mayer's relation.
Joule's free-expansion experiment tests the independence from volume in a different way: an insulated rigid vessel allows gas into an evacuated compartment, so no external work is delivered and ideally no heat is exchanged. The first law of thermodynamics gives ΔU=0; positive CV​ then predicts equal initial and final equilibrium temperatures for the ideal gas. The apparatus, thermal-capacity controls and limitations of this inference are described in Joule's free-expansion experiment and temperature sensitivity of a free-expansion experiment. This endpoint test differs from the reversible adiabatic expansion of a loaded piston, which delivers work and lowers internal energy.

Mayer's relation

Words: 359
Ideal gas OurBigBook.com
Mayer's relation states that the difference between heat capacity at constant pressure and heat capacity at constant volume for a fixed-composition ideal gas is
Cp​(T)−CV​(T)=nR=NkB​.
(17)
Here both capacities are total heat capacities in JK−1, n is amount of substance, N is the number of particles, R is the molar gas constant and kB​ is the Boltzmann constant. In other normalizations the same statement is
Cp,m​(T)−CV,m​(T)=R,cp​(T)−cV​(T)=mnR​.
(18)
The first is a difference of molar heat capacities, in Jmol−1K−1; the second uses specific heat capacities, in Jkg−1K−1. Writing Cp​−CV​=R with total capacities would omit the sample's amount of substance. The relation holds pointwise even when the capacities depend on temperature. [ref]
The proof outline is short: enthalpy is H=U+pV=U+nRT, and internal energy of an ideal gas is independent of volume at fixed temperature. Differentiating at fixed composition and amount of substance therefore gives Cp​=dH/dT=dU/dT+nR=CV​+nR. It uses the ideal gas law, rather than assuming any particular number of molecular degrees of freedom. At fixed pressure, the additional heat supplies expansion work, pdV=nRdT. The two processes can have the same temperature endpoints and equal ΔU while requiring different heat inputs.
For one mole of the rigid-rotation diatomic ideal gas model, CV​=5R/2 and Cp​=7R/2. Heating by 10K with R≃8.31446Jmol−1K−1 gives QV​≃207.86J in a rigid sealed vessel and Qp​≃291.01J under a frictionless piston at fixed pressure. Both gain 207.86J of internal energy, while the piston delivers approximately 83.14J of work. This is a calculated model budget. If the capacities vary with temperature, the corresponding finite statement is still
Qp​−QV​=nR(T2​−T1​),QV​=n∫T1​T2​​CV,m​(T)dT,
(19)
under the fixed-pressure and fixed-volume heating assumptions in heat capacity. Their final volume values generally differ. Combining Mayer's relation with heat capacity ratio also gives CV​=nR/(γ−1) and Cp​=γnR/(γ−1) locally; constant values cannot be extrapolated across intervals in which γ(T) changes.
For a smooth stable single thermodynamic phase of a general simple compressible system, the broader Maxwell relation identity is
Cp​−CV​=T(∂T∂p​)V​(∂T∂V​)p​=κT​TVα2​,
(20)
where α is the coefficient of thermal expansion and κT​ the isothermal compressibility, with composition fixed throughout. Substituting the ideal gas law gives nR; a real gas need not do so. This distinction matters near a phase transition or when interactions appreciably change the equation of state. It explains why Mayer's relation is useful: thermal measurements under different constraints become linked to a mechanical response, while the difference retains a particularly simple value for an ideal gas.[ref]
