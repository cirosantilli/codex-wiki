Spatial homogeneity makes ρ independent of position and makes the two-particle number density depend only on the separation. Isotropy removes its directional dependence. Consequently
n2​(x,x⋆​,t)=ρ2g2​(∣x⋆​−x∣,t).
(15)
Here g2​ is a radial distribution function, with the finite-N normalization inherited from a; statistical independence is unnecessary. In particular, the translated pair in c has separation R for every s, so its s integral is simply ρ2g2​(R,t).
Isotropy of the random velocity c=v−u makes its second velocity moment proportional to the identity. Taking its trace fixes the proportionality constant:
θ=3ρ1​∫∣v−u∣2fd3v,Pij​=ρθδij​.​
(16)
Thus θ is the one-component random velocity variance; for unit mass in thermal equilibrium, θ=kB​T. A Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution is not required for the pressure tensor to be isotropic.
For the interaction contribution, the angular integral is ∫dΩRi​Rj​=(4π/3)δij​, by isotropy and its trace. With d3R=R2dRdΩ, c becomes
Pijϕ​=−32π​ρ2δij​∫0∞​R3ϕ′(R)g2​(R,t)dR.
(17)
Combining the two pressure tensors gives the virial pressure from pair correlations:
P+Pϕ=pI,p=ρθ−32π​ρ2∫0∞​R3ϕ′(R)g2​(R,t)dR.​
(18)
For a repulsive pair potential, the outward radial force is −ϕ′(R)≥0. Since g2​≥0, the second term in p is nonnegative, and positive when occupied separations feel nonzero repulsion. It expresses additional momentum transfer through interactions. The displayed integral assumes a differentiable pair potential and convergence; a hard core requires a contact formula or a limiting argument.
