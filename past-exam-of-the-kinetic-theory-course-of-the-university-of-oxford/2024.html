The long-wavelength limit in d is resonant heating. Most electrons screen the slowly varying spatial field. The noise nevertheless excites collective Langmuir waves, whose sharp temporal spectrum lies near ω=±ωpe​. Electrons with v≃ω/k remain in phase with a wave and exchange energy with it. A stable tail absorbs net wave energy through Landau damping. The heating is initially deposited in narrow tail regions of velocity space, even though the volume-integrated rate equals that in c.
The short-wavelength limit in c is stochastic heating in the terminology intended here. With ϵ≃1, the imposed electric field is essentially unscreened. Its temporally uncorrelated kicks cause a random walk of electron velocity, so ⟨(Δv)2⟩=2D0​Δt. Electrons throughout the thermal population contribute to the resulting kinetic energy increase; no narrow collective wave peak selects a small tail population.
Both mechanisms are driven by white noise, and the formal condition ω=kv enters the stationary quasilinear diffusion calculation in both cases. The physical distinction is the sharp collective resonance in d versus the broad direct random forcing in c, rather than the mere presence of randomness. In collisionless theory this heating is an increase of the averaged kinetic energy; subsequent collisions can establish thermal equilibrium.

For an integrable or approximately integrable background Hamiltonian, action-angle variables turn an otherwise complicated stellar orbit into J˙=0 and θ˙=Ω(J). This has two useful consequences. First, a phase-mixed equilibrium can be represented directly as f0​(J): the action variables label the unperturbed orbits, while angle variables describe position along them. Second, the angles are periodic, so Fourier series reduce unperturbed transport to multiplication by in⋅Ω and make orbital resonances explicit. The action variables also separate rapid orbital motion from slow secular changes. These advantages apply on regular orbital regions; a globally chaotic background need not admit global action-angle variables.
The transformation is canonical, preserving the phase space measure and the stated total mass M=∫fd3θd3J. The collisionless Vlasov equation is
∂t​f+{f,H}θ,J​=∂t​f+∂J∂H​⋅∂θ∂f​−∂θ∂H​⋅∂J∂f​=0.​
(40)
Here H is the single-star specific Hamiltonian; the normalization of f by mass changes neither the Poisson bracket nor the transport equation.

For analytic continuation, resolve velocity parallel to k: let u=k⋅v and F(u)=∫f0​(u,v⊥​)d2v⊥​. Then

For example, F(u)=Cexp[−1/(1−u2)] for ∣u∣<1 and zero elsewhere is smooth but not analytic at u=±1; its Cauchy integral cannot be holomorphically continued across those endpoints. The Landau contour gives the standard causal continuation on the domains where the equilibrium permits it, with possible singularities retained.

The waiting time is controlled by the slowest excited collective damping, not just an orbital or crossing time. If ωj​=ωrj​−iΓj​ with Γj​>0, then a modal amplitude is suppressed by e−Γj​t, and one needs tΓj​≫1 for every appreciably excited mode, together with decay of the phase-mixing remainder. Large transient prefactors can require additional time. Near marginal stability, a relevant Γj​ tends to zero and the required waiting time diverges; the stationary wake approximation is then not uniform in the stability parameter.[ref]
Qualification of stability. If stable means only non-growing, a persistent neutral mode invalidates the asserted asymptote. A concrete example is a homogeneous waterbag distribution with parallel marginal F(u)=ρ1∣u∣<V​/(2V). Its gravitational dielectric function is
ϵk​(ω)=1−k2V2−ω24πGρ​.
(61)
For k2V2>4πGρ, the roots ω=±k2V2−4πGρ​ are real, so the switch-on transients persist although that harmonic has no growing mode. Taking the smallest box wavenumber to satisfy the same inequality makes the whole waterbag model free of growing harmonics. The claimed late-time formula therefore requires damping of all excited modes and ϵk​(a)=0; exact forcing at an undamped root instead produces a secular response.
