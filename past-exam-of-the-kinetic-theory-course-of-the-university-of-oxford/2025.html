Normalize the symmetric N-particle phase-space distribution function DN​ to one and write zi​=(xi​,vi​), dzi​=d3xi​d3vi​. The number-normalized one- and two-particle distribution functions are
f(z1​,t)=N∫DN​dz2​⋯dzN​,f2​(z1​,z2​,t)=N(N−1)∫DN​dz3​⋯dzN​.
(1)
Thus ∫fdz=N and ∫f2​(z,z⋆​)dz⋆​=(N−1)f(z). These conventions absorb the otherwise explicit factor N−1 in the first BBGKY hierarchy equation.
Separate the Hamiltonian into kinetic energy, external potential energy, and pair potential terms. Their Hamilton's equations give
x˙i​=vi​,v˙i​=−∇U(xi​)−∑j=i​∇xi​​ϕ(∣xi​−xj​∣).
(2)
The sign follows directly from the stated Poisson bracket: v˙iα​={viα​,H}=−∂xiα​​H. The Hamiltonian Liouville equation is consequently
∂t​DN​+∑i​[vi​⋅∇xi​​DN​−(∇Ui​+∑j=i​∇xi​​ϕij​)⋅∇vi​​DN​]=0.
(3)

To obtain the Boltzmann equation, take a dilute gas with a short-range pair potential. Its interaction range is small compared with the mean free path and the spatial scale on which the phase-space distribution function changes. The binary collision approximation treats encounters as isolated elastic collisions; simultaneous encounters involving three or more particles can be neglected. The encounter duration is short compared with the collision relaxation time and macroscopic evolution time, and the external force changes negligibly during an encounter. This permits local, effectively instantaneous scattering at one position x.
The essential statistical closure is molecular chaos for the Boltzmann equation: the distribution of incoming pairs factorizes as ff⋆​. Outgoing pairs need not be independent. Follow each incoming pair through its two-body scattering, and use conservation of momentum and kinetic energy, together with time reversal and the invariant scattering measure, to express both gain and loss in the same variables. This gives
CB​[f](v)=∫d3v⋆​∫hemisphere​dΩB(g,θ)(f′f⋆′​−ff⋆​),g=∣v−v⋆​∣.
(6)
Here dΩ=sinθdθdφ; when the measure is written as dθdφ, its Jacobian is included in the Boltzmann scattering kernel. In a scattering-angle convention, that kernel contains relative speed times differential scattering cross-section. The hemisphere prevents double counting equivalent outcomes for indistinguishable particles; the precise factor is included in B. The primed velocities are connected to the unprimed ones by the reversible elastic collision map. This closure is appropriate for a dilute short-range gas, rather than a generic long-range interacting system with an important collective mean-field theory contribution.
The further replacement by the BGK model is a phenomenological relaxation closure. Replace the full nonlinear Boltzmann collision operator by relaxation towards a local Maxwellian, with a single, velocity-independent collision relaxation time τ>0:
CBGK​[f]=−τf−f(0)​,f(0)=(2πΘ)3/2ρ​exp(−2Θ∣v−u∣2​).
(7)
It models relaxation towards a local Maxwellian without resolving the detailed scattering. Treating τ as constant is an additional simplification; it is of the order of a mean time between effective collisions. Near a local Maxwellian, departures in the nonconserved velocity moments decay on this timescale.
Choose f(0) to match the collision invariants 1, v and ∣v∣2 of f. For unit particle mass, this means
ρ=∫fd3v,u=ρ−1∫vfd3v,Θ=3ρ1​∫∣v−u∣2fd3v.​
(8)
Here ρ is the mass density, u is the mean velocity, and Θ is the temperature in squared-velocity units, Θ=kB​T/m before setting m=1. In particular,
∫(1,v,∣v∣2)CBGK​[f],d3v=0.
(9)
Thus relaxation preserves local mass, momentum and kinetic energy rather than relaxing them to externally prescribed values.

Take k>0 initially; real solutions for k<0 follow by (k,ω)↦(−k,−ω). Put V=v0​+v2​−v1​. Approaching the real phase velocity from the upper half-plane, the Landau contour and the Sokhotski–Plemelj formula give the following Cauchy principal value decomposition
∫CL​​v−uf0′​(v)​dv=PV∫v−uf0′​(v)​dv+iπf0′​(u).
(22)
The real frequency condition ϵ=0 therefore requires f0′​(u)=0. This is possible in the interior of the velocity plateau and outside the occupied velocity range:
u<−v0​,v1​<u<v2​,u>V.​
(23)
These are candidate intervals, not yet solutions of the real dispersion relation. The endpoints have jumps in f0′​ and logarithmic singularities in the response; they are not finite-k undamped modes. At the cusp u=0, the slope is also discontinuous and the real response diverges, so it supplies no additional mode.
The physical condition is absence of net resonant energy exchange with particles moving at the wave's phase velocity. Outside the support there are no such particles. Within a velocity plateau, the equal populations on either side of the resonance cancel Landau damping.

(33)
These are Langmuir waves. The difference between n∗​ and the reference n0​ is only first order in δ/v0​, so it does not alter the requested leading-order result.
For the finite-phase velocity branch put u=v1​+δa, with 0<a<1. To leading order in δ/v0​ and δ/v1​, the real dispersion relation becomes
ln[v02​−v12​v12​​a1−a​]=K.
(34)
At long wavelength set K=0 to leading order. Then a=v12​/v02​, and
ωa​≈k[v1​+δv02​v12​​].​
(35)
For comparison, the exact k→0 phase velocity from the cubic equation in b is B/A; expanding it gives precisely v1​+δv12​/v02​+O(δ2/v0​) at fixed v1​/v0​.
A single Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution has a nonzero slope at every finite nonzero u and has no velocity plateau. The would-be electron acoustic wave is therefore subject to strong Landau damping, so there is no corresponding completely undamped acoustic branch in a single Maxwellian electron population with immobile ions. At its only stationary point u=0, a Maxwellian has ∫f0′​(v)/vdv<0, making the static dielectric function positive rather than zero. This statement does not exclude acoustic modes of suitably distinct electron populations or an ion-supported mode when ion dynamics is retained.

For a regular integrable Hamiltonian system, action-angle variables offer two related simplifications.
  • Unperturbed Hamilton's equations are J˙=0 and θ˙=Ω(J). Complicated stellar orbits become uniform motion on an invariant torus, and the unperturbed transport operator becomes Ω⋅∂θ​. A Fourier series in the angles then makes orbital resonances explicit through k⋅Ω.
  • A phase-mixed equilibrium galactic distribution function depends on the action variables rather than the fast angles, consistently with Jeans theorem. Its slow evolution, including quasilinear diffusion in action space, can therefore be described by an angle average in two action variables rather than by resolving every stellar orbit in position and velocity.
These advantages assume regular or approximately integrable mean motion; globally defined action-angle variables need not exist in chaotic regions.

The required time separation is τ≪tsec​, where tsec​ is the evolution timescale of f0​, together with tpeak​≫τ and an observation time well after the pulse. The first condition permits freezing f0​(J,t′) at its pre-pulse value; its neglected change would enter beyond the retained second order. The second makes the original initial time lie in a negligible tail of the Gaussian function, permitting replacement of the lower time limits by −∞. Short duration alone would not justify that replacement for a pulse centered at the initial time.
Integrate the continuity equation in c over the whole pulse. For fixed J, the time factor is the ordered Gaussian response integral
I(ω)=∫−∞∞​dt∫−∞t​dt′e−iω(t−t′)g(t)g(t′).
(54)
A common translation by tpeak​ removes the peak time. Pair the positive and negative azimuthal harmonics: (−m,−kR​) is paired with (m,kR​). Both kφ​ and k⋅∂J​f0​ change sign, so their product does not; ω−k​=−ωk​ makes the two time factors complex conjugates. Hence the pair contributes 2ReI.
For completeness, the real part of the integrand is symmetric under t↔t′. The triangular time domain therefore gives half of the full plane, and the Fourier transform of a Gaussian function gives
ReI(ω)=21​​∫−∞∞​g(t)e−iωtdt​2=πτ2e−ω2τ2.
(55)
This derives the supplied identity rather than requiring a separate time-ordering calculation.
Define

(56)
Using the paired harmonics and ΔF0​=−∂Jφ​​∫Q0​dt now yields
ΔF0​=4π2mτ2∂Jφ​∂​∑kR​​∫0∞​dJR​∣umkR​​∣2DmkR​​e−ωmkR​2​τ2.

For kR​=±1, the selected orbital resonances satisfy
m(Ωφ​−Ωp​)±ΩR​=0.
(61)
For near-circular stellar orbits, ΩR​ is the radial epicyclic frequency. Thus kR​=−1 is the inner Lindblad resonance, m(Ωφ​−Ωp​)=ΩR​, and kR​=+1 is the outer Lindblad resonance, m(Ωφ​−Ωp​)=−ΩR​.
The flux is driven by m∂Jφ​​f0​±∂JR​​f0​, and each resonant harmonic exchanges both angular momentum and radial action in the ratio
ΔJR​=mkR​​ΔJφ​.​
(62)
These terms can therefore produce galactic disk heating as well as angular momentum transport. In the usual absorbing response, stars losing angular momentum at the inner Lindblad resonance gain radial action, and stars gaining angular momentum at the outer Lindblad resonance also gain radial action. The actual direction of flux follows from the displayed derivatives of f0​; it is not fixed by the resonance condition alone. A finite pulse replaces exact Lindblad resonance selection by a band of detunings of width of order 1/τ.
