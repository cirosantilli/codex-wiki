Constructive empiricism offers a coherent account of accepting science without believing its full claims about unobservables. Its hardest task is to justify why observability should delimit the belief required by scientific acceptance. Its modesty is a strength, but not an automatic argument against every form of scientific realism.
For Bas van Fraassen, science aims at empirical adequacy. Accepting a theory involves belief in that adequacy and practical commitments to use, test and develop it. Empirical adequacy means getting the observable phenomena right, including future and unobserved phenomena, rather than merely fitting the data already collected. Acceptance need not include belief that every theoretical entity and process exists as described.[ref]
The position nevertheless interprets theoretical claims literally. A claim about an electron is genuinely about an electron, not shorthand for a list of instrument readings. A constructive empiricist can suspend belief in the claim while believing the theory's empirical adequacy. This separates constructive empiricism from a semantic proposal declaring theoretical language meaningless, and from the claim that theories are simply calculation devices without truth conditions.
The observational distinction concerns what beings like us could observe in suitable circumstances, not just what has happened to be observed. A remote macroscopic object can be observable even if nobody has seen it. Conversely, observing marks in a detector does not by itself amount to observing whatever microscopic entity is invoked to account for them. Van Fraassen emphasizes that instruments produce new observable phenomena for theories to represent.[ref]
This permits serious experimental work. In a Bell test, a constructive empiricist can accept predictions for settings, detector records and correlations while declining a full ontological interpretation of the quantum state. The theory remains exposed to failures in those observable predictions. Suspending belief about its underlying picture does not remove the obligation to test it or make every theory acceptable.
An attraction is economy of belief. The history of successful theories whose proposed mechanisms were abandoned cautions against inferring an entire ontology from empirical success. Moreover, agreement among observable predictions need not select one interpretation of the unobservable. Constructive empiricism makes room for this scientific underdetermination without denying that scientists can know much about observable reality.
The realist objects that the restriction is arbitrary. Ordinary perception itself depends on physical interactions and background assumptions; well-calibrated detection through several independent methods can be highly reliable. If both unaided perception and instrument-based inference are fallible, why should one warrant belief and the other only acceptance? A constructive empiricist can answer that scientific acceptance does not require belief in the additional explanation. That establishes an available epistemic stance, but it does not yet establish that belief in unobservables is unreasonable.
Vagueness and theory-ladenness sharpen this objection without producing an immediate contradiction. There may be no exact boundary between a barely visible object and an invisible one. Clear cases can remain despite uncertain margins. Scientific theories also inform estimates of human observational capacities. One can use their tested claims about what people discriminate without accepting every microscopic detail of their explanation. Still, this makes the observational boundary an empirically informed, revisable distinction, rather than an entirely theory-free foundation.
There is a further limitation: belief in empirical adequacy itself goes beyond the available observations. It includes what will happen in unperformed experiments. Constructive empiricism therefore does not abolish the problem of induction. Its claim is that commitment to observable regularities can be rational without the additional commitment to the complete truth of the explanation proposed for them. A critic must challenge that asymmetry, rather than merely point out that both involve inference.
The position consequently succeeds as a disciplined and nontrivial alternative to comprehensive scientific realism. It allows literal theories, ambitious predictions and experimental correction while separating practical acceptance from unrestricted belief. Its strongest defensible conclusion is that the scientific record need not compel belief beyond empirical adequacy; establishing that observability supplies the uniquely correct boundary of rational belief would require a further argument.
