Falsifiability is a relation between a claim, testing assumptions and possible outcomes that would conflict with it. It is not the fact that someone has criticized the claim, and not the same property as being false. It gives an important demand for empirical accountability: a theory that is compatible with every conceivable result cannot lose by an observational test. That demand motivates Popper's approach to the demarcation problem.[ref]
Simply possessing a falsifiable hypothesis is nevertheless insufficient to make an entire activity scientific. A precise prediction can be false, based on unreliable methods, or repeatedly insulated from contrary evidence by new excuses. A practice that makes one testable prediction while disregarding all its failures does not acquire sound scientific methodology merely because a sentence in its vocabulary could have been refuted. How claims are tested and revised matters.
Strict deductive refutability is also a difficult necessary condition for every legitimate scientific claim. A stochastic radioactive decay model may assign nonzero probability to every finite sequence of counts. No individual finite record then logically contradicts it. For example, a Poisson distribution with λ>0 gives every integer count k≥0 probability e−λλk/k!>0. A sufficiently extreme count can nevertheless give strong evidence against the specified rate. A probability model can be tested through sampling distributions and error-controlled procedures. Extremely unlikely outcomes can count strongly against it without being impossible. Replacing scientific testing by a requirement of zero model probability would misdescribe much of quantitative science.
A further difficulty is the use of auxiliary hypotheses. A highly specific physical prediction becomes a test of the intended account only when instrument behaviour and background conditions are sufficiently controlled. Otherwise failure can be redirected indefinitely. Conversely, designing and calibrating an instrument can be genuine scientific work without itself taking the form of a single dramatic conjecture awaiting refutation.
The plausible conclusion is that falsifiability, or a suitably statistical form of testability, is one important component of a scientific practice. It does not provide a self-sufficient binary label for whole areas of inquiry. Publicly specified commitments, reliable methods, independent scrutiny and responsiveness to evidence are also needed. This preserves the force of the demand that claims risk failure without treating a useful methodological principle as a complete definition of science.
