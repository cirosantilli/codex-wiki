Gaussian integral (∫−∞∞​e−x2dx=π​)

OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 17
The Gaussian integral gives the exact area under a Gaussian function. For a positive real number a,
I(a)=∫−∞∞​e−ax2dx=aπ​​.​
(19)
This is a finite improper integral over the whole real line. For example, I(1)=π​≈1.772454 and I(4)=π​/2≈0.886227. Increasing a from one to four narrows the curve and halves its area, while its height at zero stays one. The change of variables u=a​x explains the factor a−1/2.
The useful consequence is an exact normalization. For a>0 and any real center μ,
pa​(x)=πa​​e−a(x−μ)2,∫R​pa​(x)dx=1.
(20)
This is a probability density function for a normal distribution with expected value μ and variance 1/(2a). A narrower probability density function must be taller to keep total probability one. In the usual notation a=1/(2σ2), the prefactor is 1/(σ2π​), where σ is the standard deviation.
The same area calculation makes the one-dimensional heat kernel
K(x,t)=4πDt​1​e−x2/(4Dt),D>0,t>0,
(21)
integrate to one in x, with D the diffusion coefficient. A spreading Gaussian function can therefore preserve total mass while its height falls. This links the Gaussian integral with both the normal distribution and the diffusion equation.
Finite intervals lead to the error function, rather than this single constant:
erf(z)=π​2​∫0z​e−u2du,∫−RR​e−ax2dx=aπ​​erf(a​R),R≥0.
(22)
The complementary tail is described by the complementary error function. These definitions and their real-axis limits are given in the NIST reference. [ref]
The polar-coordinate proof of the Gaussian integral explains why π appears in an integral of a function defined on a line: squaring its area creates a rotationally symmetric integral on a plane. Substitution t=x2 also gives Γ(1/2)=2∫0∞​e−x2dx=π​, connecting the result with the gamma function.
