Measure (μ)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Measure theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 4k Articles: 52
On a measurable space (X,F), a measure is a function μ:F→[0,∞] with μ(∅)=0 and countable additivity: for pairwise disjoint measurable sets An​,
μ(⋃n=1∞​An​)=∑n=1∞​μ(An​).
(1)
Infinite size is permitted. Nonnegativity implies that if measurable sets satisfy A⊆B, then μ(A)≤μ(B): decompose B into A and B∖A. For overlapping measurable sets, use countable subadditivity rather than adding their full sizes twice.
For example, counting measure gives μ({a,b,c})=3, while a Dirac measure δa​ gives that same set size 1 if it contains a. Under Lebesgue measure, [0,1/4]∪[3/4,1] has size 1/4+1/4=1/2. These are all measures, but they answer different questions: how many objects, whether a distinguished object is present, or how much length is occupied.

Inner regular measure (μ(B)=supK⊆B, K compact​μ(K))

Words: 42
Measure OurBigBook.com
A measure is inner regular on its measurable sets if their measures are suprema of the measures of contained compact sets. Every Borel probability measure on a compact metric space is inner regular and has outer regularity. Combining the two properties allows approximation of an indicator function by a continuous function in the L1 norm.

p-adic measure (μ:C(G,I)→I)

Words: 305 Articles: 3
Measure OurBigBook.com
For a profinite group G and a complete discrete valuation ring I with a continuous Zp​-algebra structure, an I-valued measure is a bounded I-linear functional on continuous I-valued functions, with the uniform norm. Equivalently it assigns finitely additive values in I to clopen sets. Locally constant functions are uniformly dense, so integration extends uniquely from finite sums. Countable additivity in the usual real-measure sense is not imposed. The coefficient ring may have infinite residue field and need not be compact.

Mahler expansion of continuous p-adic functions (f(x)=∑n≥0​an​(nx​),an​→0)

Words: 135
p-adic measure OurBigBook.com
Let I be a complete discrete valuation ring with a continuous Zp​-algebra structure. Every continuous function on Zp​ with values in I has a unique uniformly convergent expansion in the binomial polynomials, with an​=Δnf(0). This is the coefficient-ring version of the Mahler theorem. To see that an​→0, approximate the function modulo any power of the maximal ideal by a function of period pk. In the expansion of (E−1)pk, all intermediate binomial coefficients are divisible by p, while (Epk−1) kills the periodic function. Iterating makes its differences vanish modulo the prescribed ideal power. Finite differences do not increase the uniform norm, so the same conclusion holds for the original function. Binomial inversion gives agreement at the nonnegative integers, and their density in the p-adic integers gives the expansion everywhere. This gives the dual description of p-adic measures by arbitrary bounded sequences of binomial moments, and hence the Amice transform.

Convolution of p-adic measures ((μ∗ν)(h)=∫∫h(xy)dμ(x)dν(y))

Words: 54
p-adic measure OurBigBook.com
On a profinite group, convolution is first defined for functions on a finite quotient by summing products of the two measures' coset coefficients, then extended by uniform approximation. Finite-quotient associativity proves associativity. For an abelian group it is commutative; the Dirac measure at the identity is the identity element. Under the measure realization of an Iwasawa algebra, it becomes group-algebra multiplication.

Stieltjes transform of a measure (gμ​(z)=∫R​(x−z)−1dμ(x))

For a finite positive measure on the real line, this convention for its Stieltjes transform is analytic off the real line and has positive imaginary part in the upper half-plane if the measure is nonzero. Some sources use (z−x)−1 instead, changing the sign. The transform of an empirical spectral measure equals the normalized trace of the Stieltjes matrix resolvent.

Outer regular measure (μ(E)=infU⊇E, U open​μ(U))

A measure on a topological space is outer regular on its measurable sets when the displayed open-cover identity holds. Lebesgue measure has this property. It allows a null set to be placed in arbitrarily small open neighborhoods, and a compact small set to receive a smooth cutoff of similarly small integral.

Complex measure (ν:F→C)

A complex measure is a countably additive complex-valued set function on a sigma-algebra. Its total variation norm of a measure is obtained by taking the supremum of sums ∑j​∣ν(Ej​)∣ over finite measurable partitions. For finite variation and absolute continuity of measures with respect to a sigma-finite positive measure, the Radon-Nikodym theorem supplies an integrable complex density.

Complete measure

Words: 71 Articles: 1
Measure OurBigBook.com
A measure is complete when every subset of each null set is a measurable set. Such a subset also has measure zero by monotonicity. The completion of a measure extends it to a complete measure by adjoining all subsets of measurable null sets to its sigma-algebra.

The completion of a measure space adjoins every subset of every measurable null set to its sigma-algebra. A statement that two events or σ-algebras agree modulo null sets becomes literal after passing to the corresponding measure-algebra completion.

Dirac measure (δx​)

Words: 647
Measure OurBigBook.com
On a measurable space (X,F), the Dirac measure at x∈X assigns unit mass according to whether a measurable set contains that point:
δx​(A)=1A​(x)={1,0,​x∈A,x∈/A,​A∈F.
(4)
Here 1A​ is the indicator function. This is a probability measure, since δx​(X)=1. For pairwise disjoint measurable sets An​, at most one contains x, so δx​(⋃n​An​)=∑n​δx​(An​): countable additivity reduces to a membership test. The definition works even when the singleton {x} is not a measurable set. [ref]
To assert that its support of a measure is exactly {x}, assume that X is a metric space and F is its Borel sigma-algebra. The singleton {x} is then a closed set and a Borel set, with δx​({x})=1. Every open neighbourhood of x has mass one; every other point y has an open ball of radius less than its distance from x, with mass zero. Thus suppδx​={x}. An abstract measurable space has no specified topology, so this topological conclusion is not part of the bare definition. Indeed, on the sigma-algebra {∅,X}, all Dirac measures coincide: the available measurable sets cannot distinguish points.
The Lebesgue integral against a Dirac measure is evaluation:
∫X​fdδx​=f(x).
(5)
This holds for every nonnegative measurable function, allowing an infinite value, and for real or complex measurable functions satisfying ∣f(x)∣<∞, which are precisely the integrable functions for δx​. For a nonnegative simple function, only the cell containing x contributes to its integral; increasing simple functions and the monotone convergence theorem extend the identity. Splitting into positive and negative parts, and then real and imaginary parts, gives the remaining cases. This is why a Dirac measure turns integration into a point query.
For a measurable function T:(X,F)→(Y,G), its pushforward measure satisfies
(T∗​δx​)(B)=δx​(T−1(B))=1B​(T(x)),T∗​δx​=δT(x)​.
(6)
Consequently ∫Y​gd(T∗​δx​)=g(T(x)) for nonnegative or integrable measurable functions g. For example, on the real numbers with their Borel sigma-algebra, T(t)=2t+1 sends δ1​ to δ3​, so integrating g(y)=y2 against that pushforward measure gives 9. [ref]
Finite weighted sums of Dirac measures give useful measures with finitely many point masses. For finite nonnegative weights wj​,
μ=∑j=1m​wj​δxj​​,μ(A)=∑j=1m​wj​1A​(xj​),∫fdμ=∑j=1m​wj​f(xj​)
(7)
for nonnegative measurable functions f, or whenever ∑j​wj​∣f(xj​)∣<∞, with zero-weight terms omitted. Repeated locations have their weights added. On the real numbers, this is the finite version of the measure underlying the left-continuous cumulative function of an atomic measure; a positive-weight singleton is an atom of a measure. Unit weights at the distinct points of a finite set recover counting measure on that set. [ref]
The same function can have sharply different integrals for different measures. On [0,2] with its Borel sigma-algebra, define
f(t)={5,t+1,​t=1,t=1.​∫[0,2]​fdδ1​=5,∫[0,2]​fdλ=4,∫[0,2]​fd#=∞.
(8)
Here λ is Lebesgue measure and # is counting measure. The singleton {1} is a Lebesgue null set, so its changed value leaves the Lebesgue integral equal to ∫02​(t+1)dt=4. For counting measure on the whole interval, f≥1 on an infinite set, forcing an infinite integral. On the smaller measure space D={0,1,2} with its power set and counting measure, the restricted function instead has integral 1+5+3=9.
This also explains the almost everywhere convention in Lp spaces: equality almost everywhere for δx​ requires equality at x, whereas equality almost everywhere for Lebesgue measure allows different values at that singleton. A point value therefore cannot be read unambiguously from an equivalence class in an Lp space formed using Lebesgue measure.
On the real numbers, a Dirac measure gives the Dirac delta distribution by ⟨δx​,φ⟩=∫φdδx​=φ(x) for every test function. The name Dirac delta function does not mean an ordinary function that is zero away from x and infinitely high at x. Such a nonnegative measurable function still has Lebesgue integral zero, because its nonzero values occur on a Lebesgue null set. More generally, no nonnegative density h with respect to Lebesgue measure can represent δx​: it would give δx​({x})=∫{x}​hdλ=0, contradicting unit mass. The measure and its associated distribution act on different kinds of inputs, namely measurable sets and test functions, but express the same point evaluation. [ref]

Atom (measure theory)

Words: 123 Articles: 2
Measure OurBigBook.com
An atom of a measure μ is a measurable set A of positive measure for which every measurable B⊆A has either μ(B)=0 or μ(B)=μ(A). A singleton of positive mass is an example. A transport map cannot split the mass of a singleton among several destinations.

Non-atomic measure

Words: 79 Articles: 1
Atom (measure theory) OurBigBook.com
A non-atomic measure contains no atom of a measure. For a finite Borel measure on a Polish space, this is equivalent to giving every singleton mass zero. For a probability measure on R, it is also equivalent to continuity of its cumulative distribution function.

Lyapunov convexity theorem

Words: 44
Non-atomic measure OurBigBook.com
The range of a finite-dimensional, finite, countably additive, atomless vector measure is compact and convex. In particular, an atomless measure restricted to a measurable set E can realize every mass between zero and μ(E). This allows exact balancing by small disjoint consumer cohorts in an exchange economy.

Counting measure

Words: 581 Articles: 1
Measure OurBigBook.com
Counting measure makes integration into summation by giving every measurable singleton unit weight. On any measurable space (X,F), it is the measure # defined by
#(A)={∣A∣,∞,​A is finite,A is infinite,​A∈F.
(9)
Thus #(∅)=0, a finite set has its usual cardinality, and every infinite set has the same value ∞ in the extended real number line. In particular, countably infinite sets and uncountable sets are not distinguished by their counting measure. Taking F to be the power set makes every subset measurable; restricting the same rule to a smaller sigma-algebra is also allowed. [ref]
Why is this a countably additive measure? For pairwise disjoint measurable sets An​,
#(⋃n=1∞​An​)=∑n=1∞​#(An​).
(10)
If the union is finite, only finitely many An​ are nonempty, and finite cardinality adds. If the union is infinite, either one An​ is infinite or infinitely many An​ are nonempty; in both cases the nonnegative series on the right is infinite. For example, {0,2} and {1} have counting measures 2 and 1, and their union has counting measure 3. The singletons {n} for positive natural numbers give the infinite case: #(N)=∑n=1∞​1=∞.
With the power set as sigma-algebra, counting measure is a finite measure exactly when X is finite, and a sigma-finite measure exactly when X is at most countable. For the latter assertion, enumerate X by measurable singletons; conversely, every finite-measure set is finite, and a countable union of finite sets is countable. Specifying the sigma-algebra matters: if X=N and F={∅,N}, counting measure is not a sigma-finite measure, since the only measurable finite-measure set is the empty set.
For a finite or countably infinite set X equipped with its power set, every nonnegative function is a measurable function, and
∫X​fd#=∑x∈X​f(x):=supF⊆X finite​∑x∈F​f(x).
(11)
The supremum allows an infinite result and avoids choosing an order for the terms. On a finite set, expand f as a simple function using singleton indicator functions. On a countably infinite set, enumerate the points and apply the monotone convergence theorem to finite truncations. Real or complex functions are integrable functions for counting measure exactly when ∑x∈X​∣f(x)∣<∞; then their integral equals the absolutely convergent sum. This is the discrete counterpart of Lebesgue integration, developed further in series as a Lebesgue integral against counting measure. [ref]
For example, f(n)=2−n on the positive natural numbers has counting measure integral one, by the geometric series. The alternating harmonic series has a finite ordered sum but is not the integral of an integrable function for counting measure: its positive and negative parts both have infinite sums. Absolute convergence is what makes the finite integral independent of enumeration. In particular, the Lp spaces for counting measure on N are the l-p sequence spaces, with their sums replacing continuous integrals.
Compare three measures on the same Borel sigma-algebra of the real numbers: counting measure #, Dirac measure δ1​, and Lebesgue measure λ.
A∅{0}{0,1,2}[0,2]Q∩[0,2]​#(A)013∞∞​δ1​(A)00111​λ(A)00020​​
(12)
The rational numbers in the last row form a countably infinite set; their Lebesgue measure is zero by countable additivity applied to singletons, even though they are dense in the interval.
These are different answers to different questions: counting measure asks how many points occur, Dirac measure asks whether the selected point occurs, and Lebesgue measure asks how much length occurs. A null set therefore depends on its measure. For counting measure, the only null set is the empty set, so equality almost everywhere is equality everywhere. For δ1​, a measurable set is a null set exactly when it misses 1; for Lebesgue measure, even the infinite set Q∩[0,2] is a null set. Integrating an indicator function recovers each table entry through ∫1A​dμ=μ(A). This lets the same Lebesgue integral framework handle point counts, point evaluation, and geometric length.

Support of a measure (suppμ)

Words: 134 Articles: 1
Borel measure OurBigBook.com
For a positive Borel measure on a topological space, the support consists of the points whose every open neighborhood has positive measure. For a finite regular complex measure use its variation measure. Regularity makes the complement of the support null: compact subsets of that open complement have finite covers by null neighborhoods. A regular probability measure on a compact Hausdorff space whose support is a single point is therefore a Dirac measure.

Support under a continuous map (supp(f∗​μ)=f(suppμ)​)

Words: 66
Support of a measure OurBigBook.com
For a Borel probability measure on a Polish space and a continuous map to another Polish space, the image measure has the displayed support. Every neighborhood of an image of a support point has an open inverse image of positive measure. Conversely, the closed image closure has full image measure because the original support has full measure. This transfers a support description for a driving rough path to the solution of a rough differential equation.

A Borel probability measure is a Borel measure with total mass one. On a compact metric space it is determined by its integrals against continuous functions. On that compact metric space, the family of Borel probability measures is compact for weak convergence of probability measures.

Continuous approximation of Borel indicators on a compact metric space (∥φ−1B​∥L1(μ)​<ϵ,0≤φ≤1)

Borel probability measure OurBigBook.com
For a Borel probability measure on a compact metric space, inner regularity and outer regularity give K⊆B⊆U with K compact, U open and μ(U∖K)<ϵ. The Urysohn lemma supplies a continuous function φ equal to one on K and zero outside U, with values in [0,1]. Its difference from the indicator function of B is supported in U∖K and bounded by one, proving the displayed estimate. This transfers identities established for continuous test functions to measurable sets.

Regular Borel measure

A finite positive Borel measure on a compact Hausdorff space is regular when its values on Borel sets can be approximated from inside by compact sets and from outside by open sets. For signed or complex measures require this property of the variation measure. These are the measures representing the continuous dual space of C(K) in the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem.

Radon measure

Words: 169 Articles: 1
Borel measure OurBigBook.com
A Radon measure is a Borel measure that is finite on compact sets and is inner regular, so the measure of a Borel set can be approximated from below by compact subsets. On a locally compact Hausdorff space one also usually includes outer regularity.

Regularity of finite Borel measures on Euclidean space (μ(A)=supK⊂A, K compact​μ(K))

Words: 125
Radon measure OurBigBook.com
Every finite positive Borel measure on Euclidean space is inner regular by compact sets and outer regular by open sets. For an open set U, the compact sets {x:∣x∣≤n, dist(x,Uc)≥1/n} increase to U, so continuity from below of a measure gives inner approximation. The empty complement is handled by compact balls. The class of Borel sets admitting both approximations is closed under complements: interchange inner and outer approximations, then truncate the inner closed set by a large compact ball, using finiteness of the measure. For a countable union, approximate its components from outside with summable errors, and approximate finitely many components from inside after making the remaining union's measure small. Thus this class is a sigma-algebra containing the open sets. In particular, a finite measure carried by a Borel null set has compact subsets of that carrier capturing arbitrarily nearly all its mass.

Measure space ((X,M,μ))

Words: 100
Measure OurBigBook.com
A measure space is a set X, a sigma-algebra M of its subsets, and a measure μ defined on M. Specifying only X leaves both the measurable questions and their weights undetermined.
For a concrete probability space, take X={a,b,c}, M its power set, and weights 1/2,1/3,1/6. Define μ(A) by adding the weights of the elements in A. Then
μ(X)=21​+31​+61​=1,μ({b,c})=21​.
(14)
A probability measure is exactly a measure normalized by μ(X)=1, usually written P(Ω)=1. An indicator function has expected value equal to its event's probability. For a different continuous example, [0,1] with the restricted Lebesgue measure is a probability space; the event [0,1/4] has probability 1/4.

Haar measure

Words: 728 Articles: 8
Measure OurBigBook.com
A left Haar measure on a locally compact group that is a Hausdorff space is a nonzero regular Borel measure, finite on compact sets, satisfying μ(gA)=μ(A) for every Borel set A. Such a measure exists and is unique up to a positive scalar. Right Haar measure instead satisfies μ(Ag)=μ(A); the two need not coincide. On a compact group, normalization gives total mass one and invariance on both sides. See Tornier, Haar Measures, Definition 2.1 and Theorem 2.2.
For a finite group, normalized Haar measure is uniform counting; for the circle group, it is normalized angle integration on the circle. These examples turn symmetry into a rule for averaging.

Haar measure from a left-invariant coframe (dμ=∣θ1∧⋯∧θd∣)

Words: 56
Haar measure OurBigBook.com
On a d-dimensional Lie group, take the coframe dual to a basis of left-invariant vector fields. Its top wedge is nonvanishing and invariant under left translations, so its absolute density defines a left Haar measure. If the frame has coordinate matrix A(x), the density is ∣detA(x)∣−1dx1⋯dxd. This makes explicit computation of Haar measure a determinant calculation.

Haar measure from normalized covering functionals (Iu​(f)=(f:u)/(h:u))

Words: 138
Haar measure OurBigBook.com
For nonzero nonnegative u,h∈Cc​(G), let (f:u) be the infimum of ∑j​aj​ over finite dominations f≤∑j​aj​u(xj−1​⋅) with aj​>0. Composition of covers gives (f:u)≤(f:h)(h:u), so Iu​(f) is uniformly bounded while Iu​(h)=1. It is invariant under left translation, homogeneous, monotone and subadditive. As the support of u shrinks to the identity, its additivity defect tends to zero: use the continuous proportions fi​/(f1​+f2​+δp), with a cutoff p positive on both supports, whose oscillations are small on every translate of that small support. In a locally compact sigma-compact metrizable group, diagonal convergence on a countable dense family, followed by uniform approximation on fixed compact supports, gives a positive invariant linear functional on all Cc​(G). The Riesz representation on compactly supported continuous functions turns it into Haar measure. Local compactness is essential for the compactly supported cutoffs and small-support test functions.

Compact-group invariant probability by finite averaging (ϕ(f)=ϕ(lg​f),ϕ(1)=1)

Words: 121
Haar measure OurBigBook.com
For a compact group G, positive normalized linear functionals on C(G) form a nonempty weak-star topology compact convex set. For finitely many translations, apply the Schauder-Tychonoff fixed-point theorem to the average of their induced affine dual maps. A fixed functional is stationary under this average. For each real continuous f, its orbit function h(g)=ϕ(lg​f) is continuous on the compact subgroup generated by those translations and satisfies a finite averaging identity. At a maximum, every summand must attain that same maximum; iteration and density of the positive-word semigroup force h to be constant. Thus the functional is fixed by each of the finitely many translations. Compactness and the finite intersection property now give a functional invariant under every translation. The Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem turns it into a normalized Haar measure, without assuming commutativity of the group.

Two minimal left covering nets for the same identity neighbourhood V can be matched bijectively so that paired translates intersect. Join f∈F to f′∈F′ when fV∩f′V=∅. Countable additivity

Words: 121 Articles: 2
Measure OurBigBook.com
For pairwise disjoint measurable sets En​, countable additivity means
μ(⋃n=1∞​En​)=∑n=1∞​μ(En​).
(17)
The infinite series is the limit of its nonnegative partial sums. This controls an entire countable union, not merely each finite stage. If q1​,q2​,… enumerate the rational numbers in [0,1], their finite collections have Lebesgue measure zero, and countable additivity gives their whole union Lebesgue measure zero. Their being a dense set does not change this calculation.
The axiom makes no assertion about an arbitrary uncountable set of disjoint pieces. In particular, [0,1] is the union of its singletons, each of Lebesgue measure zero, yet its Lebesgue measure is one. Extending additivity to all such unions would contradict interval length.
