Gaussian quadrature

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Numerical analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 881 Articles: 9
Gaussian quadrature is a quadrature rule that chooses both sample locations and coefficients to achieve unusually high polynomial exactness. For a finite interval [a,b] with a<b, assume the integration weight w is integrable and positive almost everywhere. Define
Iw​(f)=∫ab​f(x)w(x)dx,Qn​(f)=∑i=1n​Ai​f(xi​),n≥1.
(83)
Let pn​ be the monic orthogonal polynomial of polynomial degree n for the inner product ⟨u,v⟩w​=∫ab​u(x)v(x)w(x)dx: it satisfies ⟨pn​,q⟩w​=0 for every polynomial q of polynomial degree below n. The polynomial roots of pn​ are distinct and inside (a,b); take them as the nodes xi​. Integrate the Lagrange cardinal polynomials ℓi​ to obtain Ai​=Iw​(ℓi​). These coefficients are positive, and
Qn​(f)=Iw​(f)for every polynomial f with degf≤2n−1.
(84)
The integration weight w(x) and the discrete coefficients Ai​ play different roles: w specifies the integral being approximated, while Ai​ tell us how much each sample contributes.[ref]
A proof outline explains the gain over simply integrating an arbitrary polynomial interpolation. Use polynomial division to write f=pn​q+r, with degq≤n−1 and degr≤n−1. Orthogonality makes Iw​(pn​q)=0, and sampling at the polynomial roots of pn​ makes Qn​(pn​q)=0. The Lagrange cardinal polynomials recover r exactly, so both expressions agree. Also Ai​=Qn​(ℓi2​)=Iw​(ℓi2​)>0, since ℓi2​ has polynomial degree 2n−2. This is the mechanism behind positive weights of Gaussian quadrature.
For a concrete two-node example, set w=1 on [−1,1]. The Legendre polynomial P2​(x)=(3x2−1)/2 has polynomial roots x=±1/3​. Its monic polynomial version p2​=x2−1/3 has zero integral against both 1 and x, as required. Symmetry and exactness for the constant polynomial give A1​=A2​=1. Thus the Gauss–Legendre instance of Gaussian quadrature is
∫−11​f(x)dx ≈ f(−1/3​)+f(1/3​).
(85)
Direct evaluation on 1,x,x2,x3 gives 2,0,2/3,0, exactly their integrals. For the polynomial f(x)=1+x+x2+x3, two samples therefore give 8/3 exactly. But for x4 the rule gives 2/9, whereas the integral is 2/5: the error I1​−Q2​ is 8/45.
The ceiling 2n−1 is sharp for n distinct real nodes under the positive-weight assumptions. Their squared nodal polynomial ∏i​(x−xi​)2 has polynomial degree 2n, is zero at every sample, and has a strictly positive weighted integral. Thus no such quadrature rule integrates every polynomial of polynomial degree 2n exactly. Nor does Gaussian quadrature integrate every nonpolynomial function exactly: the same two-node example gives Q2​(∣x∣)=2/3​, while I1​(∣x∣)=1.
Gaussian quadrature is useful when evaluations are costly and a modest polynomial captures the integrand well. More precisely, for a continuous function f and any polynomial r of polynomial degree at most 2n−1, positivity and constant exactness give
∣Iw​(f)−Qn​(f)∣≤2Iw​(1)∥f−r∥∞​.
(86)
The uniform norm on [a,b] measures the approximation between samples. This bound connects quadrature rule accuracy to uniform approximation, rather than promising an error from sample count alone. Corners, sharp unresolved features and roundoff error require their own assessment; the absolute value function example already shows why polynomial exactness is a qualified guarantee.
