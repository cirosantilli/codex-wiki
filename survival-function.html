Survival function (S(t))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Survival analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 14
For a nonnegative survival time T, the survival function is
S(t)=P(T>t)=1−F(t),F(t)=P(T≤t),
(2)
where F is the cumulative distribution function. Thus S(t) is the probability that the chosen occurrence is still in the future at time t. We consistently use the strict inequality T>t: a probability mass at t has already left the survival function at t itself. With this convention S is a right-continuous function and a nonincreasing function taking values in [0,1]. If P(T=0)=0, then S(0)=1; if T is finite with probability one, then S(t)→0 as t→∞.
For S(a)>0 and b>a, conditional probability gives
P(T>b∣T>a)=S(a)S(b)​,P(a<T≤b∣T>a)=1−S(a)S(b)​.
(3)
For example, S(2)=0.8 and S(5)=0.6 mean that 0.6/0.8=0.75 of those still event-free at year two remain event-free through year five. Their intervening event probability is 0.25, whereas the original population's probability of an occurrence in (2,5] is 0.8−0.6=0.2. Conditioning changes the denominator.
The area under the survival function up to a horizon gives the restricted mean survival time. A positive terminal plateau in a plotted Kaplan–Meier estimator does not establish that the occurrence will never happen: terminal censoring and survival-mean identifiability explains why the unobserved tail remains undetermined.
