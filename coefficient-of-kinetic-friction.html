Coefficient of kinetic friction (μk​)

 ... Physics Branches of physics Classical mechanics Newtonian mechanics Friction Kinetic friction
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 3
The coefficient of kinetic friction is the dimensionless quantity μk​=∣fk​∣/N for sliding contact with positive normal force N in the ideal kinetic friction model. It characterizes the pair of contacting surfaces and their condition. A constant μk​ means the model neglects dependence on contact slip speed; unlike the coefficient of static friction, it specifies a sliding force magnitude rather than a no-slip upper bound.
The ratio compares the tangential kinetic friction magnitude with the perpendicular normal force:
μk​=N∣fk​∣​,N>0,∣w∣>0.
(3)
Here w is the tangential relative velocity defining contact slip. Both numerator and denominator have the same physical dimension, so the coefficient of kinetic friction has no units. It belongs to a contact pair under specified conditions, rather than to one material alone. The coefficient of static friction instead describes the threshold ∣fs​∣max​/N before contact slip; a pull required to start motion cannot be substituted for a sliding measurement.[ref]
As an original model example, stipulate μk​=0.30 and gravitational acceleration g=9.8m/s2. An already sliding, nonrotating block of total mass m=2kg is pulled horizontally across a stationary horizontal surface at constant velocity. With no other vertical force, Newton's second law gives N=mg=19.6N. Zero horizontal acceleration then requires a pulling force F=∣fk​∣=μk​mg=5.88N. Doubling the total mass to 4kg gives N=39.2N and F=11.76N. The force doubles while the coefficient of kinetic friction stays 0.30 by assumption. These numbers are model predictions, not observations or a calibration for a particular material pair.
A constant coefficient of kinetic friction is useful because one ratio can summarize many loads, as tested by load dependence of kinetic friction. It is an empirical approximation over a stated range of normal forces, speeds and surface conditions. It implies no universal independence from speed or apparent contact area: a change of face or sliding regime can change the result. Temperature, wear and lubrication are further conditions to report. Newton's second law still applies when the constant-coefficient approximation fails.[ref]
