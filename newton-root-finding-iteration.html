Newton root-finding iteration (tr+1​=tr​−F(tr​)/F′(tr​))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Numerical analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 610 Articles: 6
The Newton root-finding iteration seeks a zero of a function F by repeatedly solving its tangent line approximation. At a current guess tr​ with derivative F′(tr​)=0, set
0=F(tr​)+F′(tr​)(tr+1​−tr​),tr+1​=tr​−F′(tr​)F(tr​)​.
(2)
Geometrically, follow the tangent line to the horizontal axis, then return to the function graph for the next tangent line. Close to a suitable zero of a function, the error becomes dramatically smaller.[ref]
For a concrete square root calculation, take the polynomial F(t)=t2−2 and start at t0​=1. The Newton root-finding iteration becomes tr+1​=(tr​+2/tr​)/2, giving
r01234​tr​11.51.4166666666671.4142156862751.414213562375​∣tr​−2​∣4.14214×10−18.57864×10−22.45310×10−32.12390×10−61.59486×10−12​​
(3)
The errors use the unrounded iterates. After the first step, each iterate overestimates 2​, and the exact identity
tr+1​−2​=2tr​(tr​−2​)2​
(4)
exhibits quadratic convergence: once close, the number of correct decimal digits roughly doubles per step. This speed makes the Newton root-finding iteration useful when accurate derivatives are available.
Global convergence requires additional care. For the polynomial F(x)=x3−2x+2, starting at x0​=0 gives F(0)=2, F′(0)=−2, hence x1​=1. Then F(1)=1, F′(1)=1 gives x2​=0. The sequence repeats 0,1,0,1,… forever, although a real polynomial root lies in (−2,−1) by the intermediate value theorem. Neither point in the cycle is a zero of a function, and neither has zero derivative. A maintained sign-changing interval and fallback bisection method can protect a root solver from unsuitable Newton steps.
