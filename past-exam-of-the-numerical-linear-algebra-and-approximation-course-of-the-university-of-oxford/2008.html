A real square orthogonal matrix Q satisfies QTQ=QQT=I​. Equivalently its columns form an orthonormal basis. In particular it preserves the Euclidean norm, since ∥Qx∥22​=xTQTQx=∥x∥22​.

A Householder matrix has the form
H=I−2vTvvvT​,v=0.​
(1)
It reflects in the hyperplane perpendicular to v. Put P=vvT/(vTv), an orthogonal projector satisfying PT=P and P2=P. Then HTH=(I−2P)2=I−4P+4P2=I, proving that every Householder matrix is orthogonal.

If x=0, choose α=−sgn(x1​)∥x∥2​, with sgn(0)=1 for this choice, and put v=x−αe1​. This nonzero vector satisfies
vTx=∥x∥22​−αx1​,vTv=2(∥x∥22​−αx1​).
(2)
Thus the Householder matrix H=I−2vvT/(vTv) gives Hx=x−v=αe1​. Symmetry gives xTH=αe1T​​. If x=0, any Householder matrix works with α=0.
For an LQ decomposition, process rows j=1,…,min(m,n). At step j, postmultiply by an orthogonal block matrix Hj​=diag(Ij−1​,Hj​) that zeros entries to the right of the diagonal in the current row. If its row tail is already suitable, use the identity. Earlier processed rows have zero entries throughout this trailing block, so their zeros are preserved. Consequently L=AH1​⋯Hr​ has lij​=0 for i<j. Setting Q=HrT​⋯H1T​​ gives A=LQ​. The argument handles both rectangular shapes; L is lower trapezoidal when it is not square.

c OurBigBook.com
The singular value decomposition A=UΣVT gives
ATA=VΣ2VT.
(3)
Therefore σi​(A)=λi​(ATA)​​, counting multiplicities. These are the nonnegative square roots: zero singular values must be included if A is singular. Strict positivity requires full rank, which the question does not assume.
Write the lower bidiagonal matrix entries as Bii​=di​ and Bi+1,i​=ei​. Its columns i and j have disjoint supports if ∣i−j∣>1, so T=BTB has no entries outside the three central diagonals. More explicitly,
Tii​=di2​+ei2​ (i<n),Tnn​=dn2​,Ti,i+1​=Ti+1,i​=ei​di+1​.​
(4)
This proves both symmetry and the tridiagonal matrix structure.

d OurBigBook.com
A generic lower triangular matrix has a dense first column. The first left Householder transformation in its QR decomposition mixes those rows, usually creating entries throughout the rows to the right. Subsequent eliminations impose upper triangularity but no further systematic zeros; thus R is generically a full upper triangular matrix.
For Householder bidiagonalization use two-sided operations instead. At stage j, a right Householder transformation acting on columns j,…,n clears the current row j beyond its diagonal. Then a left Householder transformation acting on rows j+1,…,n clears column j below its first subdiagonal. The left operation leaves row j unchanged; both operations preserve the already completed lower-bidiagonal rows and columns. Continue for j=1,…,n−1, omitting a final unnecessary left operation. The result is
B=PAQ,P,Q orthogonal,B lower bidiagonal.​
(5)
It follows that BTB=QTATAQ. Hence the two symmetric matrices are orthogonally similar and have the same eigenvalues. By part (c), compute the eigenvalues of the symmetric tridiagonal matrix BTB and take their nonnegative square roots to obtain all singular values of A.

a OurBigBook.com
For a tridiagonal matrix with subdiagonal ai​, diagonal bi​ and superdiagonal ci​, banded Gaussian elimination introduces no fill beyond the original band. The Thomas algorithm uses
p1​=b1​,ℓi​=ai​/pi−1​,pi​=bi​−ℓi​ci−1​(i=2,…,n).
(6)
Update the right side by d1​=f1​, di​=fi​−ℓi​di−1​. Then back substitution gives
xn​=dn​/pn​,xi​=(di​−ci​xi+1​)/pi​(i=n−1,…,1).​
(7)
Each row requires only a fixed number of arithmetic operations, so both work and storage are O(n)​. The assumed absence of a need for pivoting includes nonzero elimination pivots pi​.

Write G=M−1N. Subtracting the exact equation Mx=Nx+b from the iteration gives eℓ+1​=Geℓ​, where eℓ​=x−x(ℓ). Hence eℓ​=Gℓe0​. Also
det(N−λM)=det(M)det(G−λI),
(8)
so the stated scalars are precisely the eigenvalues of the iteration matrix G.
The spectral radius criterion for matrix powers states that Gℓ→0 if and only if all its eigenvalues have modulus strictly less than one. Applying that theorem gives eℓ​→0 for every initial vector. Therefore x(ℓ)→A−1b​.

c OurBigBook.com
Order points along each j-line before moving to the next k-line. The splitting has
M=diag(Tn​,…,Tn​),Tn​=tridiag(−1,4,−1),
(9)
and N couples the two neighboring k-lines. Factor Tn​ once with the Thomas algorithm; each iteration then needs n tridiagonal solves and the neighboring-line right sides, costing O(n2)​ for n2 unknowns. Known boundary values are moved to the right side; the error eigenmodes have homogeneous boundary values.
The generalized eigenvalue problem is
Uj,k+1​+Uj,k−1​=λ(4Uj,k​−Uj+1,k​−Uj−1,k​),
(10)
with U=0 at the boundary indices. Put θr​=rπ/(n+1), θs​=sπ/(n+1). The sine addition formula gives Uj+1,k​+Uj−1,k​=2cosθr​Uj,k​ and the corresponding identity in k. Thus
λr,s​=4−2cosθr​2cosθs​​=2−cosθr​cosθs​​.​
(11)
These tensor-product sine vectors form a basis, so they give the whole spectrum. With c=cos(π/(n+1))∈[0,1),
ρ(M−1N)=2−cc​<1.​
(12)
The iteration therefore converges.
It is not a uniformly effective multigrid smoother. Take r=1 and s=n: the error is slowly varying along a line but highly oscillatory between lines, and its multiplier is −c/(2−c)→−1 as the grid is refined. It changes sign at each step while retaining almost its full amplitude. This line-Jacobi Fourier spectrum distinguishes convergence on each fixed grid from the high-frequency damping needed by the multigrid method.

Let ej​=x−x(j). The underlying Richardson iteration gives ej​=(I−A)je0​. The polynomials 1,(1−t),…,(1−t)k form a basis for polynomials of degree at most k. Thus write
pk​(t)=∑j=0k​cj​(1−t)j,∑j=0k​cj​=pk​(0)=1.
(13)
Define y(k)=∑j=0k​cj​x(j). Since the coefficients sum to one,
x−y(k)=j∑​cj​ej​=pk​(A)e0​.​
(14)
The spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices supplies an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. In that basis pk​(A) has diagonal entries pk​(λi​), so
∥x−y(k)∥2​≤imax​∣pk​(λi​)∣∥x−x(0)∥2​.​
(15)
The algebraic recombination is valid in exact arithmetic even if the original unaccelerated iterates themselves fail to converge.

b OurBigBook.com
Map [a,b] to [−1,1] by z(r)=(2r−a−b)/(b−a). Since a>0, the normalization point z(0)=−(a+b)/(b−a) lies outside that interval. The proposed Chebyshev polynomial ratio has degree k and equals one at zero. On the spectral interval its maximum absolute value is
∣Tk​(−(a+b)/(b−a))∣∣Tk​(1)∣​.​
(16)
The Chebyshev minimax polynomial with exterior normalization minimizes this maximum among degree-at-most-k polynomials with p(0)=1. One may quote the minimax property. Its reason is the alternating k+1 extremal values: a strictly better competitor would force their difference to have at least k roots in [a,b] and the additional root at zero outside it, impossible for degree at most k.
The paper uses a monic normalization of Tk​ for k≥1, rather than the usual Tk​(1)=1 convention. The common factor cancels in the ratio. With κ=b/a>1 and q=(κ​−1)/(κ​+1), the hyperbolic-cosine formula gives the error factor 2qk/(1+q2k)≤2qk​. This tends rapidly to zero when the spectral interval is well-conditioned. If a=b, use p(r)=1−r/a for exact one-step termination instead of the displayed interval map.

For [a,b]=[1,2], z(0)=−3 and coshθ=3 gives eθ=3+22​. The normalization factors cancel, so
∥x−y(k)∥2​≤(3+22​)k+(3−22​)k2​∥x−x(0)∥2​≤2(3−22​)k∥x−x(0)∥2​.​
(17)
If the eigenvalues are known to be exactly 1,3/2,2, use the more targeted annihilating polynomial
p3​(r)=(1−r)(1−2r/3)(1−r/2).​
(18)
It equals one at zero and vanishes on the entire spectrum, so the spectral theorem gives p3​(A)=0. Thus y(3)=x in exact arithmetic. Degree three is the least degree that can vanish at all three distinct eigenvalues; a particular initial error supported on fewer eigenspaces can terminate earlier.

The Krylov subspace is Kk​(A,b)=span{b,Ab,…,Ak−1b}​. If y∈Kk​, write y=q(A)b with degq≤k−1. Since b=Ax,
x−y=[I−Aq(A)]x=p(A)x,p(t)=1−tq(t),
(19)
which has degree at most k and p(0)=1. Conversely, if x−y=p(A)x with these properties, then q(t)=(1−p(t))/t is a polynomial of degree at most k−1 and
y=Aq(A)x=q(A)b∈Kk​(A,b).​
(20)
This is the polynomial description of the admissible errors in a Krylov subspace method.

For a symmetric positive-definite matrix, the conjugate gradient method chooses the unique point in x0​+Kk​(A,r0​) minimizing the energy norm of the error. Equivalently it minimizes 21​yTAy−bTy on that affine space. Here x0​=0 and r0​=b.
Choose the eigenbasis orthonormally, as the spectral theorem permits, and write x=∑i​αi​vi​. By part (a), every admissible error is p(A)x=∑i​αi​p(λi​)vi​, degp≤k, p(0)=1. Orthogonality then gives
∥p(A)x∥A2​=i∑​αi2​λi​[p(λi​)]2.​
(21)
Thus the implicit CG polynomial minimizes exactly the displayed weighted sum. Strictly, the printed hypothesis that the eigenvectors are individually normalized must be supplemented by mutual orthogonality when eigenvalues repeat. Otherwise cross terms within a repeated-eigenvalue eigenspace need not vanish. An orthonormal eigenbasis always supplies the intended formulation.
If at most ℓ eigenvectors contribute to x, a polynomial with one factor 1−t/λ for each distinct contributing eigenvalue is admissible by step ℓ and makes that error zero. CG minimizes a nonnegative quantity, so exact termination occurs in at most ℓ steps​.
For the last question, write x=αv1​+βv2​ with eigenvalues 1,L, L=106. The first polynomial has the form p1​(t)=1−ηt. Differentiating its weighted objective gives
η=α2+β2L3α2+β2L2​,p1​(1)=1−η.​
(22)
If the high-eigenvalue component is not exceptionally small, the L-weighted terms dominate and η≈1/L, so p1​(1)≈1−10−6≈1: the first step mainly removes the high-eigenvalue error. This is the spectral weighting of conjugate gradient error. The expectation is conditional on the coefficients, not guaranteed by the two eigenvalues alone; if β=0, the first step instead solves the low-eigenvalue component exactly.
