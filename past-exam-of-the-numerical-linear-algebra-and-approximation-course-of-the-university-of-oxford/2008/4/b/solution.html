For a symmetric positive-definite matrix, the conjugate gradient method chooses the unique point in x0​+Kk​(A,r0​) minimizing the energy norm of the error. Equivalently it minimizes 21​yTAy−bTy on that affine space. Here x0​=0 and r0​=b.
Choose the eigenbasis orthonormally, as the spectral theorem permits, and write x=∑i​αi​vi​. By part (a), every admissible error is p(A)x=∑i​αi​p(λi​)vi​, degp≤k, p(0)=1. Orthogonality then gives
∥p(A)x∥A2​=i∑​αi2​λi​[p(λi​)]2.​
(21)
Thus the implicit CG polynomial minimizes exactly the displayed weighted sum. Strictly, the printed hypothesis that the eigenvectors are individually normalized must be supplemented by mutual orthogonality when eigenvalues repeat. Otherwise cross terms within a repeated-eigenvalue eigenspace need not vanish. An orthonormal eigenbasis always supplies the intended formulation.
If at most ℓ eigenvectors contribute to x, a polynomial with one factor 1−t/λ for each distinct contributing eigenvalue is admissible by step ℓ and makes that error zero. CG minimizes a nonnegative quantity, so exact termination occurs in at most ℓ steps​.
For the last question, write x=αv1​+βv2​ with eigenvalues 1,L, L=106. The first polynomial has the form p1​(t)=1−ηt. Differentiating its weighted objective gives
η=α2+β2L3α2+β2L2​,p1​(1)=1−η.​
(22)
If the high-eigenvalue component is not exceptionally small, the L-weighted terms dominate and η≈1/L, so p1​(1)≈1−10−6≈1: the first step mainly removes the high-eigenvalue error. This is the spectral weighting of conjugate gradient error. The expectation is conditional on the coefficients, not guaranteed by the two eigenvalues alone; if β=0, the first step instead solves the low-eigenvalue component exactly.
