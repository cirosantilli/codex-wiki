a OurBigBook.com
Compact support of a measure gives finite second absolute moments for both probability measures. Since the source satisfies absolute continuity of measures with respect to Lebesgue measure, Brenier's theorem identifies the quadratic Kantorovich optimal transport problem with the Monge optimal transport problem:
d22​(μ,ν)​=π∈Γ(μ,ν)min​∫∣x−y∣2dπ(x,y)=S#​μ=νmin​∫∣x−S(x)∣2dμ(x)=∫∣x−T(x)∣2dμ(x).​​
(1)
Here S#​μ is the pushforward measure. The optimal transport plan is unique and equals (Id,T)#​μ. The optimal transport map is unique up to a μ-null set, with
T=∇uμ-almost everywhere,u convex.​
(2)
The gradient of this convex function is c-cyclically monotone for c(x,y)=∣x−y∣2 on a full-measure set. In particular, summing the two supporting inequalities for the convex function gives (T(x)−T(x′))⋅(x−x′)≥0 wherever both gradients exist. No regularity of the target beyond the stated probability measure assumptions is needed.

Write P=ωωT. Since PT=P and P2=P, the orthogonal reflection Rω​=I−2P satisfies RωT​Rω​=I and Rω−1​=Rω​. Thus, for every Borel set E, invariance under the orthogonal group gives
(Rω​#​μ)(E)=μ(Rω−1​E)=μ(E),Rω​#​μ=μ.​
(3)
The spherical average must use normalized surface measure without an additional area factor. Let dσ be the normalized surface measure on a sphere, and set
Aη(r)=∫Sd−1​η(rθ)dσ(θ).
(4)
The correct radial disintegration of an orthogonally invariant measure is
∫η(x)dμ(x)=∫[0,∞)​Aη(r)d(Φ#​μ)(r).​
(5)
Indeed, take normalized Haar measure H on the compact orthogonal group O(d). Invariance and Fubini's theorem give
∫ηdμ=∫∫O(d)​η(Qx)dH(Q)dμ(x).
(6)
Right invariance of Haar measure and transitivity of the orthogonal group on each sphere show that the inner average depends only on r=∣x∣. Averaging it once more over x=rθ, using invariance of surface measure on a sphere and Fubini's theorem, identifies its value as Aη(r). At x=0 the same identity reads η(0)=η(0). The definition of pushforward measure now proves the boxed formula. This argument also works for invariant probability measures with an atom of a measure at the origin.
For the printed normalization, choose a smooth cutoff function η equal to one on a ball containing the support of a measure μ. Its left integral is one, whereas the printed right integral is σd​. Thus the extra σd​ cannot be retained. Equivalently, with unnormalized area measure dS, the average is σd−1​∫η(rθ)dS(θ).
The reflection-averaging hint also fails in general dimension. For x=e1​,
∫Sd−1​Rω​e1​dσ(ω)=(1−d2​)e1​,∫Sd−1​ωdσ(ω)=0.
(7)
The first equality uses ∫ωi​ωj​dσ=δij​/d, which follows from coordinate reflections, coordinate permutations, and ∑i​ωi2​=1. Taking a test function equal to the first coordinate near the unit sphere disproves the hint when d=2. Averaging over the full orthogonal group, as above, proves the required corrected result without that hint.

Write dμ=f(x)dx, with f≥0. Applying polar coordinates and Fubini's theorem gives, for bounded nonnegative measurable h,
∫hdμ​=∫0∞​h(r)g(r)dr,g(r)=σd​rd−1∫Sd−1​f(rθ)dσ(θ)(r>0).
(10)
Also μ​({0})=μ({0})=0 by absolute continuity of measures. Extending g by zero on the negative half-line proves
μ​≪L1.​
(11)
Both radial pushforward measures have compact support of a measure, so Brenier's theorem on the line supplies a unique optimal transport plan and a transport map τ, unique μ​-almost everywhere, with
d22​(μ​,ν)​=s#​μ​=νmin​∫0∞​∣r−s(r)∣2dμ​(r)=∫0∞​∣r−τ(r)∣2dμ​(r).​​
(12)
In one dimension, the gradient of a convex function is a nondecreasing function. Nonnegativity holds μ​-almost everywhere because τ#​μ​=ν is carried by [0,∞). To choose a nonnegative, nondecreasing representative everywhere, let F,G be the radial cumulative distribution functions and use the monotone rearrangement
τ(r)=G−1(F(r)),G−1(u)=inf{s≥0:G(s)≥u}(0<u<1).​
(13)
At F(r)=0 set τ(r)=0, and at F(r)=1 use the largest point of the support of a measure ν. These choices preserve monotonicity and boundedness. Since F is continuous, F(R) has the uniform distribution on (0,1) when R has law μ​, and the quantile function G−1 sends this uniform distribution to ν. Thus this representative has the required pushforward measure, even if the target has atoms.

Set q=y−x and zt​=x+tq. The definition of pushforward measure gives the confinement energy V[μt​]=∫V(zt​)dΠ. The chain rule and differentiation under the integral sign, whenever justified, give
dtd​V[μt​]=∫∇V(zt​)⋅qdΠ,dt2d2​V[μt​]=∫qTD2V(zt​)qdΠ.​
(24)
For example, bounded Hessian matrix is sufficient: it gives ∣∇V(z)∣≤C(1+∣z∣), and the first two differentiated integrands are bounded by C′(1+∣x∣2+∣y∣2). The dominated convergence theorem applies because Π has finite second absolute moments. Compactly supported endpoints also suffice because all interpolating points lie in one compact set.
The printed growth assumption alone does not ensure a classical second derivative. Here is a counterexample within the stated assumptions. On the line take the bounded nonnegative smooth function
V(x)=1−cos(2πx3),pn​=cn−4(n≥1),c−1=∑n≥1​n−4,
(25)
and the probability measures μ=∑n​pn​δn−1/2​, ν=∑n​pn​δn+1/2​. Both have finite second absolute moments. Their translation transport plan is optimal: its cost is one, while every coupling has cost at least the square of its mean displacement, also one. At t0​=1/2, zt0​​=n, so F(t)=V[μt​] satisfies F(t0​)=0. Each summand is nonnegative, and the Fatou lemma gives
liminfh→0​h2F(t0​+h)+F(t0​−h)−2F(t0​)​≥∑n​pn​V′′(n)=36π2c∑n​1=∞.
(26)
Thus the requested classical identity requires an additional integrability assumption on the Hessian matrix; quadratic growth controls the energy but not its second derivative.
For a confinement potential V that is a λ-strongly convex function, the derivative formula, where valid, yields
dt2d2​V[μt​]≥λ∫∣q∣2dΠ=λD2.​
(27)
The underlying displacement convexity conclusion is valid under the printed assumptions without that extra differentiation hypothesis. Apply convexity of V(x)−λ∣x∣2/2 pointwise and integrate:
V[μt​]≤(1−t)V[μ]+tV[ν]−2λ​t(1−t)D2.​
(28)
The same argument on every subinterval makes t↦V[μt​]−λD2t2/2 convex, so the second derivative lower bound also holds in the sense of distributions.

Use an independent copy (x′,y′) of (x,y) under the product measure Π⊗Π. Write q=y−x, q′=y′−x′, and
rt​=zt​−zt′​=(1−t)(x−x′)+t(y−y′),a=q−q′.
(29)
Since the pushforward measure of Π⊗Π under (zt​,zt′​) is μt​⊗μt​, the interaction energy is
W[μt​]=21​∬W(rt​)dΠ(x,y)dΠ(x′,y′).
(30)
Two applications of the chain rule give
dt2d2​W[μt​]=21​∬(q−q′)TD2W(zt​−zt′​)(q−q′)dΠdΠ′.​
(31)
The factor 1/2 is retained. This classical identity requires differentiation under the integral sign to be justified, just as in part (b). Bounded Hessian matrix of W is sufficient, since ∣q−q′∣2≤2∣q∣2+2∣q′∣2 is integrable; compactly supported endpoints are another sufficient case. The printed quadratic-growth condition alone does not provide this domination.
The failure can occur for the interaction energy too. Take the even interaction potential W(x)=1−cos(2πx3) and replace the endpoint probability measures in part (b) by 21​δ0​+21​μ and 21​δ0​+21​ν. Keep the origin fixed and translate every other atom by one. This coupling is increasing on the line, hence is optimal: its assigned values extend to a nondecreasing transport map, which is the gradient of a convex function. Differences between translated atoms remain integers, so their mutual interaction vanishes. Only interactions with the origin contribute, giving W[μt​]=F(t)/4, with F from part (b). It therefore also lacks a finite classical second derivative at t=1/2.

Define the barycenter of a measure by mμ​=∫xdμ(x). Under Π, the mean displacement is qˉ​=mν​−mμ​, and optimality gives ∫∣q∣2dΠ=D2. Expanding the square under the product measure yields the variance identity for independent copies:
21​∬∣q−q′∣2dΠdΠ′=∫∣q∣2dΠ−​∫qdΠ​2=D2−∣mν​−mμ​∣2.
(32)
In the classical regime of part (c), the lower bound on the Hessian matrix of the interaction potential therefore gives
dt2d2​W[μt​]≥λ(D2−∣mν​−mμ​∣2).
(33)
For equal barycenters, this becomes
dt2d2​W[μt​]≥λD2.​
(34)
For the full stated class of probability measures, prove the displacement convexity inequality directly. Pointwise strong convexity of W gives
W((1−t)(x−x′)+t(y−y′))≤(1−t)W(x−x′)+tW(y−y′)−2λ​t(1−t)∣q−q′∣2.
(35)
Integrating, including the 1/2 in the interaction energy, and using the displayed variance identity for independent copies gives
W[μt​]≤(1−t)W[μ]+tW[ν]−2λ​t(1−t)(D2−∣mν​−mμ​∣2).
(36)
All terms are finite by quadratic growth and finite second absolute moments. Equal barycenters give the claimed λ-displacement convexity. Also mμt​​=(1−t)mμ​+tmν​, so this metric geodesic remains in the prescribed fixed-mean class. Applying the same argument to subintervals proves the second derivative bound in the sense of distributions even when a classical derivative has not been justified. The mean restriction is natural: translating all particles together leaves their pairwise differences and hence the interaction energy unchanged.

Symmetry here means W(−z)=W(z), so ∇W(0)=0 and K(0)=0. The bounded Hessian matrix and the fact that J is an orthogonal matrix give

Thus the particle system is X˙i=N−1∑j​K(Xi−Xj). On the configuration space with norm ∥X∥max​=maxi​∣Xi∣, its vector field has Lipschitz constant at most 2L:
∣Fi​(X)−Fi​(Y)∣≤NL​∑j​(∣Xi−Yi∣+∣Xj−Yj∣)≤2L∥X−Y∥max​.

The Picard-Lindelöf theorem gives local existence and uniqueness. Linear growth and the Gronwall inequality give ∥X(t)∥max​≤e2Lt∥X(0)∥max​, which rules out escape to infinity in finite time and extends the solution to every t≥0.

For a candidate curve ρ, define the velocity field by the convolution
bρ​(t,x)=K∗ρt​(x)=−∫J∇W(x−z)dρt​(z).
(45)
It is well defined with a finite first absolute moment, because ∣K(x−z)∣≤L(∣x∣+∣z∣). Equality of mixed partial derivatives gives
divK=∂x​Wy​−∂y​Wx​=0,divbρ​=0.
(46)
The Hessian matrix bound justifies moving a spatial derivative through this convolution. For smooth densities, the transport equation consequently becomes the continuity equation
∂t​ρ+div(bρ​ρ)=0.​
(47)
This form defines the equation for singular probability measures as well. With initial probability measure ρ0​, a weak solution is a curve in C([0,∞),P1​) such that every φ∈Cc1​([0,∞)×R2) satisfies
∫0∞​∫[∂t​φ(t,x)+bρ​(t,x)⋅∇φ(t,x)]dρt​(x)dt+∫φ(0,x)dρ0​(x)=0.​
(48)
Using smooth test functions gives the same definition by approximation on compact sets. Continuity in the Wasserstein distance bounds first absolute moments on compact time intervals, so the displayed integrals exist.
For the empirical measure, the chain rule and the particle equations give
dtd​∫φ(t,x)dμtN​(x)​=N1​i∑​[∂t​φ(t,Xi)+∇φ(t,Xi)⋅X˙i]=∫[∂t​φ+bμN​⋅∇φ]dμtN​.​
(49)
Integrating in time, with zero terminal contribution because φ has compact support, gives precisely the weak identity and the correct initial empirical measure. Thus μN is a global weak solution. The self-interaction term vanishes since K(0)=0.

A finite first moment suffices for the velocity, but may not suffice for the printed potential. For example, W(z)=∣z∣2/2 has all the stated properties. If μ is the law of (S,0) with density 2s−3 on s≥1, then ES=2 but ES2=∞, and (W∗μ)(x)=∞ for every x. Thus the literal definition of V needs a finite second absolute moment, or another hypothesis ensuring integrability of W.
For the specified class P1​, define the velocity fields directly by bμ​=K∗μ and bμ​​=K∗μ​. Equivalently, use the renormalized interaction potential
Vren​(t,x)=∫[W(x−z)−W(−z)]dμt​(z).
(50)
The bounded Hessian matrix and ∇W(0)=0 imply
∣W(x−z)−W(−z)∣≤L∣x∣∣z∣+2L​∣x∣2.
(51)
Thus Vren​ is finite, is twice continuously differentiable in space, and has ∇Vren​=∇W∗μ, by dominated differentiation under the integral sign. Its rotated negative gradient is exactly bμ​. Whenever W∗μ is finite, the two potentials differ only by an x-independent constant and give the same transport equation.
We check the hypotheses for characteristic representation of a measure-valued continuity equation. For every x,y,
∣bμ​(t,x)−bμ​(t,y)∣≤L∣x−y∣,∣bμ​(t,x)∣≤L(∣x∣+m1​(μt​)),m1​(μt​)=∫∣z∣dμt​(z).
(52)
For any coupling γ∈Γ(μt​,μ​t​), the Lipschitz condition on K also gives
∣bμ​(t,x)−bμ​​(t,x)∣​=​∫[K(x−z)−K(x−z)]dγ(z,z)​≤L∫∣z−z∣dγ(z,z).​
(53)
Taking the infimum over couplings proves the uniform bound
xsup​∣bμ​(t,x)−bμ​​(t,x)∣≤Ld1​(μt​,μ​t​).​
(54)
Applying the same estimate at two times of one curve proves time continuity of bμ​, uniformly in x. Also ∣m1​(μt​)−m1​(μs​)∣≤d1​(μt​,μs​), so m1​(μt​) is bounded on compact time intervals. Spatial partial derivatives of bμ​ are continuous and bounded by L, using dominated convergence in space and weak convergence of probability measures in time for the bounded continuous kernel DK. All corresponding facts hold for μ​.
The characteristic representation of a measure-valued continuity equation says that a continuous velocity field, continuously differentiable and globally Lipschitz in space and with locally uniform linear growth, has a global characteristic flow map Xμ​(t;s,a) solving
dtd​Xμ​(t;s,a)=bμ​(t,Xμ​(t;s,a)),Xμ​(s;s,a)=a.
(55)
For each f∈P1​, its pushforward measure is the unique weak solution in C([0,∞),P1​) of the associated continuity equation. The hypotheses have all been verified above, and the fields have zero divergence, so these are also the two required transport equations. Explicitly,
ρt​=Xμ​(t;0,⋅)#​f,ρ​t​=Xμ​​(t;0,⋅)#​f.​
(56)
For completeness, the Gronwall inequality gives
∣Xμ​(t;0,a)∣≤eLt∣a∣+L∫0t​eL(t−s)m1​(μs​)ds,
(57)
so these pushforward measures have finite first absolute moments. Integrating ∣Xμ​(t;0,a)−Xμ​(s;0,a)∣ against f proves continuity in the Wasserstein distance. The chain rule proves the weak identity. For uniqueness at a fixed terminal time t, use the backward test function φ(s,x)=ψ(Xμ​(t;s,x)), where ψ is continuously differentiable with compact support. It satisfies ∂s​φ+bμ​⋅∇φ=0 and has compact spatial support uniformly for 0≤s≤t, by the backward linear-growth bound. The weak identity with temporal cutoffs therefore gives ∫ψdρt​=∫ψ(Xμ​(t;0,a))df(a), identifying every weak solution with the displayed pushforward measure.
Finally compare the two characteristic curves with the same initial point a. Put Δt​=Xμ​(t;0,a)−Xμ​​(t;0,a). The spatial Lipschitz condition and the boxed velocity estimate give
dtd​∣Δt​∣≤L∣Δt​∣+Ld1​(μt​,μ​t​),Δ0​=0,
(58)
for almost every time; the integral form gives the same conclusion at zeros of Δ. The Gronwall inequality implies
∣Δt​∣≤L∫0t​eL(t−s)d1​(μs​,μ​s​)ds.
(59)
The pushforward measure (Xμ​(t;0,⋅),Xμ​​(t;0,⋅))#​f is a coupling of ρt​,ρ​t​. Integrating the last bound against the probability measure f proves
d1​(ρt​,ρ​t​)≤L∫0t​eL(t−s)d1​(μs​,μ​s​)ds.​
(60)
The same constant L depends only on the uniform bound for the Hessian matrix of W, and is independent of the prescribed curves and their common initial data.
