Automorphism (AutC​(X))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Morphism
OurBigBook.com Words: 528 Articles: 3
An automorphism is an isomorphism f:X→X: a structure-preserving self-map that can be undone by another structure-preserving self-map. Equivalently, it is an invertible endomorphism. Its inverse satisfies
f−1∘f=f∘f−1=1X​,
(6)
where 1X​ is the identity morphism. Both maps must be morphisms in the chosen category. For ordinary groups, rings, and vector spaces over a fixed field, a bijective morphism automatically has a compatible inverse. For topological spaces, an automorphism is a homeomorphism from the space to itself: a continuous bijection needs a continuous inverse as well. See Riehl, Category Theory in Context, Definition 1.1.10 and Example 1.1.11.
For example, T(x,y)=(x+y,y) is an automorphism of the vector space R2 over the real numbers, with inverse T−1(u,v)=(u−v,v). Both are linear maps. The linear endomorphism P(x,y)=(x,0) from endomorphism preserves addition and scalar multiplication too, but discards a coordinate and cannot be undone. Preserving the structure and preserving enough information for reversal are separate requirements.
The automorphisms of a fixed object in a locally small category form a group under composition in a category: the identity morphism is the identity element, and the inverse element of a composite reverses the order of its factors. This makes reversible internal changes calculable. For a group, the resulting automorphism group records which relabelings preserve all products; the examples in group automorphism show how restrictive that condition can be.
