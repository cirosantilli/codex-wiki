The paper uses Tμν​λ=Γνμ​λ−Γμν​λ, the negative of a common torsion tensor convention. Keep that sign throughout. Also, ∇μ​∇ν​φ denotes the covariant Hessian, so the second derivative acts on the lower index ν of the covector ∇ν​φ; it is not just a composition of two directional derivatives on a scalar.
Since the covariant derivative of a scalar field is its ordinary derivative,
∇μ​∇ν​φ=∂μ​∂ν​φ−Γμν​λ∂λ​φ.
(1)
Commuting the ordinary partial derivatives therefore gives
(∇μ​∇ν​−∇ν​∇μ​)φ=Tμν​λ∇λ​φ.​
(2)
For a vector field, write Dν​Xλ=∂ν​Xλ+Γνρ​λXρ. This is a tensor with one covariant and one contravariant index. Hence
∇μ​∇ν​Xλ=∂μ​(Dν​Xλ)+Γμσ​λDν​Xσ−Γμν​σDσ​Xλ.
(3)
Subtracting the expression with μ,ν exchanged, the first two terms combine to
(∂μ​Γνρ​λ−∂ν​Γμρ​λ+Γμσ​λΓνρ​σ−Γνσ​λΓμρ​σ)Xρ.
(4)
The coefficients of first derivatives of X in those two terms cancel. The last terms give (Γνμ​σ−Γμν​σ)Dσ​Xλ. Identifying the curvature coefficient yields
[∇μ​,∇ν​]Xλ=Rμνρ​λXρ+Tμν​ρ∇ρ​Xλ.​
(5)
Here the bracket denotes antisymmetrization of the tensorial second covariant derivative, consistently with the preceding calculation.
To check tensoriality directly, antisymmetrize the supplied transformation law of the connection coefficients in the two lower indices. The inhomogeneous term vanishes because mixed coordinate second derivatives are symmetric. The remaining transformation law is
Tνλ​μ=∂xμ′∂xμ​∂xν∂xν′​∂xλ∂xλ′​Tν′λ′​μ′,
(6)
which is precisely that of a (1,2) tensor. Intrinsically, with the paper's sign,
T(X,Y)=∇Y​X−∇X​Y+[X,Y].​
(7)
The derivative terms involving the components of X,Y cancel against their Lie bracket, leaving (Γνμ​λ−Γμν​λ)XμYν. This also shows that the expression is linear over smooth functions in each argument.
Finally take the torsion-free connection and the commuting parameter fields Y,Z along the geodesic family. Vanishing torsion gives ∇Y​Z=∇Z​Y, while affine geodesic parametrization gives ∇Y​Y=0. The curvature operator in the specified convention is
R(Y,Z)W=∇Y​∇Z​W−∇Z​∇Y​W−∇[Y,Z]​W.
(8)
Therefore
∇Y​∇Y​Z=∇Y​∇Z​Y=R(Y,Z)Y+∇Z​∇Y​Y+∇[Y,Z]​Y=R(Y,Z)Y.
(9)
In components this is the geodesic deviation equation
dτ2D2Zμ​=Rνλρ​μYνZλYρ.​
(10)
The connecting field represents infinitesimal separation within the family, so this is a linear equation for relative free-fall acceleration, rather than an exact coordinate difference for widely separated worldlines.

For this question use the Levi-Civita connection, its metric compatibility and the usual curvature symmetries. In the index ordering of Question 1, put Rμνρσ​=gσλ​Rμνρ​λ and Rνρ​=gμσRμνρσ​. Contract the second Bianchi identity in its derivative index μ and curvature index ρ. The curvature symmetries give
∇ρRνλρσ​=∇λ​Rνσ​−∇ν​Rλσ​.
(11)
Now contract λ,σ. The left-hand side becomes −∇ρRνρ​, while the right-hand side becomes ∇σRνσ​−∇ν​R. Thus
2∇μRμν​=∇ν​R.
(12)
Using ∇g=0 yields the contracted Bianchi identity
∇μGμν​=0,Gμν​=Rμν​−21​gμν​R.​
(13)
Taking the covariant divergence of the Einstein field equations, with constant G, then gives 8πG∇μTμν​=0. The matter equation is therefore consistent with the geometric identity rather than imposing an extra incompatible condition on the metric.
Stress-energy conservation expresses the local balance of energy and momentum, including their transport and the effects of the gravitational Levi-Civita connection. In a freely falling frame at a point it reduces there to the familiar ordinary divergence law. A global conserved energy requires additional geometric structure, such as a timelike Killing vector field; it does not follow in an arbitrary spacetime just by replacing a covariant derivative with an ordinary derivative.
For relativistic dust,
0=∇μ​(ρuμuν)=uν∇μ​(ρuμ)+ρuμ∇μ​uν.
(14)
With signature (+,−,−,−), uνuν​=1 implies uν​uμ∇μ​uν=0. Contracting gives ∇μ​(ρuμ)=0; where ρ>0, substitution then gives uμ∇μ​uν=0. Thus the continuity law combines rest-mass conservation and free geodesic motion for this matter model.
For the Newtonian limit, take c=1 and η=diag(1,−1,−1,−1), consistently with Question 3. Use the static weak field sourced by the time-independent, slowly moving matter, retaining first order in ϵ and leading order in the spatial speed. Write spatial gradients with ∂i=δij∂j​ here; these are Euclidean spatial indices in the Newtonian equation. The Levi-Civita connection formula gives
Γ00​i=−21​ηij∂j​(ϵh00​)=2ϵ​δij∂j​h00​.
(15)
The exact change from proper time to t in the spatial geodesic equation is
dt2d2xi​=−Γαβ​ivαvβ+Γαβ​0vαvβvi,v0=1.
(16)
Terms containing spatial velocities are negligible at leading order, leaving
dt2d2xi​=−∂iΦ,Φ=2ϵ​h00​.​
(17)
An additive constant in Φ is immaterial to this acceleration and is normally fixed by the asymptotic time normalization. With units restored, g00​=1+2Φphysical​/c2.
At the same order, staticity and the curvature definition give
R00​=∂λ​Γ00​λ−∂0​Γλ0​λ+O(ϵ2)=2ϵ​δij∂i​∂j​h00​=∇2Φ.
(18)
The trace of the four-dimensional Einstein equations is R=−8πGT, so their trace-reversed form is
Rμν​=8πG(Tμν​−21​gμν​T).
(19)
For nonrelativistic dust, u0≃1, ui≃0, so T00​≃ρ and T≃ρ. Keeping leading matter terms therefore gives R00​=4πGρ. Combining this with the geometric expression yields
∇2Φ=4πGρ.​
(20)
This trace reversal is essential: equating R00​ directly to 8πGρ would miss the factor of two. The approximation is the quasistatic Newtonian sector; arbitrary independently imposed gravitational radiation is not part of that sector.

Rotational symmetry allows the axes to be chosen so that any nonradial initial trajectory has those data: its conserved angular-momentum direction fixes an orbital plane through the centre. Radial trajectories can also be placed in an equatorial plane.
The coordinates t,ϕ are cyclic in L, so their canonical momenta are constant:
pt​=ft˙=E,pϕ​=−r2sin2θϕ˙​=−J.
(23)
The corresponding Killing vector fields are ∂t​ and ∂ϕ​. With the displayed signature, g(∂t​,x˙)=E and g(∂ϕ​,x˙)=−J, explaining the sign in the second canonical momentum. Thus E is conserved energy per unit rest mass for a massive particle parametrized by proper time, and J is its axial angular momentum per unit rest mass. For a photon, affine rescaling rescales both constants; after the tangent is normalized to the physical four-momentum they represent its energy at infinity and axial angular momentum. Their ratio b=J/E is the signed impact parameter.

Reversing the direction of motion gives an incoming critical ray asymptotically approaching the photon circle. These are separatrix trajectories, not evidence of stability.
The potential maximum and the outward-growing radial separation display the same instability in two complementary descriptions.
