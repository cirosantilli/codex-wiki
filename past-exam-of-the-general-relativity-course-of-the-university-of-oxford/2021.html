In general relativity, gravity is the geometry of spacetime, whose curvature is related to the stress-energy tensor, and freely falling test particles follow timelike geodesics of its metric tensor.

(1)

dτ2d2xμ​+Γνλμ​uνuλ=0,uμ=dτdxμ​,Γνλμ​=∂ξα∂xμ​∂xν∂xλ∂2ξα​.​

The last expression applies when the ξ coordinates are locally inertial at the event considered; one such chart need not remain inertial throughout a finite region.

Approximate Earth by an isolated, nonrotating, spherically symmetric body. By Birkhoff's theorem, the exterior vacuum metric tensor is the Schwarzschild metric. With x0=ct it is
ds2=−f(r)c2dt2+f(r)dr2​+r2(dθ2+sin2θdϕ2),f(r)=1−rc22GM​.​
(4)
Here r is the areal radius, θ and ϕ are spherical angles, t is Schwarzschild time normalized to the proper time of a static observer at infinity, M is Earth's mass, G the gravitational constant, and c the speed of light. The nonzero diagonal metric tensor components are g00​=−f, grr​=f−1, gθθ​=r2, gϕϕ​=r2sin2θ.
The apple is a test particle; neglect its effect on the metric tensor, air resistance, Earth's rotation and nonspherical structure, and external tidal forces. Near Earth's surface, GM/(RE​c2)≃7×10−10, h/RE​≪1, and the falling speed remains much smaller than c. Thus the Newtonian limit of this metric tensor is appropriate, while the exact radial geodesic is available as a check. A static observer under the tree has proper time T=f(RE​)​t, up to an additive constant. Such an observer is supported by the ground and has nonzero proper acceleration; the released apple instead has zero proper acceleration.

Displacement must be specified relative to an observer. In transverse-traceless gauge the free particles can have fixed coordinates, while their measured proper lengths oscillate. In a local, freely falling orthonormal frame, the geodesic deviation equation for an initial separation X=(X,Y,0) is, to first order in H,
ξ¨​i​=21​h¨ij​(t,0)Xj,ξ¨​z​=0.
(18)
Its general solution with physical displacement ξ(0)=0 and initial physical velocity V is
ξi​(t)=21​[hij​(t,0)−hij​(0,0)]Xj+[Vi​−21​h˙ij​(0,0)Xj]t.​
(19)
The source has been running indefinitely, so there is no wave-free initial state. Its initial conditions for an eternal monochromatic gravitational wave need more than positions to fix V. The usual bounded, periodic response selects Vi​=h˙ij​(0,0)Xj/2; if the particles instead start at physical rest at t=0, set Vi​=0 and retain the linear term. Both solve the same linear geodesic deviation problem. The distinction between fixed TT coordinates and measured separation is explained in [ref].
Choose β so the wave phase at the detector is Φ(t,0)=2ωt. For the bounded response the four initial placements then give
X(σa,0,0)(0,σa,0)​ξx​2σaH​(1−cos2ωt)−2σaH​sin2ωt​ξy​−2σaH​sin2ωt2σaH​(cos2ωt−1)​ξz​00​​(σ=±1).
(20)
These are displacements from the specified positions at t=0; positions are X+ξ. Equivalently, relative to mean positions the usual oscillatory displacement is ξosc​=hX/2 to first order. The response repeats after π/ω.
Both plus polarization and cross polarization occur with equal amplitude and a quarter-cycle phase difference. Their superposition is circular gravitational-wave polarization, with handedness reversed when the viewing direction or source rotation is reversed. The principal stretching axis turns through half the wave phase. There is no leading longitudinal motion.

i OurBigBook.com
Gravitational radiation admits polarization: general relativity has two transverse tensor modes, plus polarization and cross polarization, whose relative amplitudes and phase give linear, elliptical or circular gravitational-wave polarization. Their axes transform through twice a rotation of the transverse coordinate frame, unlike the vector polarization of electromagnetic radiation. Measuring these modes is physically plausible with several differently oriented detectors; a single detector generally measures only one projected combination.

Gravitational waves can exhibit interference. In linearized gravity, overlapping strains add, so coherent contributions can enhance or cancel the measured response. Independent astrophysical sources generally have unrelated phases and average to a stochastic background rather than stable fringes. More plausible coherent paths arise when gravitational lensing sends different parts of the same signal to the detector with different delays; fringes require sufficient overlap and coherence. The relevant quadratic radiative energy flux contains the cross terms even though the leading wave equation is linear.

In a local inertial frame in vacuum, gravitational waves travel at c in general relativity; there is no ordinary material index of refraction analogous to a transparent dielectric. Ordinary matter couples so weakly that laboratory prisms for gravitational waves are implausible. Propagation through a varying background gravitational field nevertheless changes ray directions and travel times, giving gravitational lensing and Shapiro time delay. In weak static coordinates ds2≃−(1+2ΦN​/c2)c2dt2+(1−2ΦN​/c2)dx2, the null condition gives an effective coordinate index of refraction neff​≃1−2ΦN​/c2. This useful description depends on coordinates and does not change the local propagation speed. Thus astronomical gravitational refraction is plausible, while a substantial everyday material effect is not.

Gravitational waves also exhibit diffraction: the geometrical optics ray description fails when a propagation structure or lens delay is not large compared with the wave's wavelength or period. For a compact lens of mass ML​, a useful dimensionless parameter is w=4GML​ωgw​/c3; w of order one signals potentially appreciable wave effects, with cosmological lens redshift supplying an additional factor 1+zL​ when needed. Astrophysical gravitational lensing can therefore produce diffraction and frequency-dependent amplification. Ordinary objects are poor opaque screens for gravitational waves, so everyday slit-and-screen demonstrations are implausible despite the large wavelengths, which are about 3000 km at 100 Hz.

On large scales the appropriate background is the spatially flat FLRW metric,
ds2=−c2dt2+R2(t)[dr2+r2dθ2+r2sin2θdϕ2].​
(34)
Here t is cosmic time, the proper time of a comoving observer; R(t) is the scale factor; and (r,θ,ϕ) are dimensionless comoving coordinates. With this convention R has units of length. A fixed comoving radial interval has instantaneous proper length R(t)dr. The cosmological principle assumes spatial homogeneity and cosmological isotropy on the scales where this background description applies.
The general FLRW metric instead has dr2/(1−kr2) in its radial part, with normalized k=+1,0,−1 describing positive, zero or negative spatial curvature of an FLRW universe. The best simple description takes k=0; observations constrain spatial curvature to be small rather than proving it identically zero. The late accelerated expansion is encoded by the time dependence of R, determined by the stress-energy tensor through the Friedmann equations, rather than by an additional term in the FLRW metric. Small-scale galaxies and other inhomogeneities require perturbations of this background.

dtdE​+PdtdV​=c2V[ρ˙​+3H(ρ+c2P​)]=0.
(38)

Conversely, expanding that first law of thermodynamics and dividing by c2V recovers the same equation. No heat or particle flux crosses the comoving boundary; interacting components separately need source terms, although the total still obeys this conservation equation.
There is a sign inconsistency in the supplied formula sheet worth isolating. Its Riemann curvature tensor and Ricci tensor conventions, together with its stated −+++ metric signature, give Gtt​=−3H2 for R(t)=eHt with constant H. Its vacuum Einstein field equations with +Λgμν​ then require H2=−Λc2/3, whereas its printed vacuum Friedmann equation gives H2=+Λc2/3. For example, H>0, ρ=P=0 and Λ=3H2/c2 satisfy the latter but leave the former's tt component equal to −6H2. Changing the geometric cosmological constant term to −Λgμν​ repairs this mismatch. The conservation derivation is unaffected; below take the geometric cosmological constant to be zero and include any vacuum energy as a fluid, so no ambiguous sign enters.

Write the equation of state as P=wρc2. Integrating the cosmological perfect-fluid continuity equation for constant w gives ρ∝R−3(1+w). With spatial flatness and the geometric cosmological constant set to zero, the Friedmann equation is H2=8πGρ/3.
In S1, w=−1 describes vacuum energy: Tμν​=−ρc2gμν​ and ρ is constant. Its negative pressure drives accelerated expansion, R¨/R=8πGρ/3=H12​, and the expanding solution is R∝eH1​t. This is the ideal de Sitter spacetime approximation to cosmic inflation. An exactly constant vacuum component cannot end its phase unaided; a phase transition or reheating mechanism is required for the assumed change to S2.
In S2, w=1/3 describes an isotropic bath of ultrarelativistic particles, such as photons, with negligible rest mass relative to their energy. The number per physical volume falls as R−3, while the energy per particle redshifts as R−1, so ρ∝R−4. This radiation-dominated universe has R∝(t−t∗​)1/2 on its expanding branch.
In S3, w=0 describes pressureless matter, or relativistic dust, with negligible random kinetic energy. Conserved particle number and approximately fixed rest energy give ρ∝R−3. A flat universe containing only this component is the Einstein-de Sitter universe, with R∝(t−t∗​)2/3. The constants t∗​ are integration offsets in the individual phases; they need not represent one common start time when phases are matched.

e OurBigBook.com
The actual large-scale expansion is well described by the Lambda-CDM model: radiation in cosmology, ordinary baryonic matter, cold dark matter and late-time dark energy coexist. A component becomes dominant through continuous changes in their density ratios, rather than every previous component disappearing at an instantaneous phase boundary. Matter pressure is small in its nonrelativistic regime, but radiation persists, and massive neutrinos interpolate between relativistic and nonrelativistic behavior.
There is also a late accelerated era, absent from a model ending permanently in S3. It is consistent with nearly constant vacuum energy, whose negative pressure eventually dominates because pressureless matter dilutes as R−3. The scale factor then ceases to follow the pure t2/3 Einstein-de Sitter universe law.
An early phase of cosmic inflation is a well-motivated model rather than a directly observed exact w=−1 phase. Successful cosmic inflation must end and transfer its energy to matter and radiation through reheating; realistic models generally have slowly varying H and w close to, rather than exactly equal to, −1. Extrapolating a strictly constant H to arbitrarily small R is not a measurement of the universe's past duration. Finally, spatial homogeneity and cosmological isotropy apply statistically on large scales: galaxies, gravitational waves and other perturbations are absent from the exact FLRW metric, and small spatial curvature remains an observational possibility.
