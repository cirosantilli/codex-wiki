Closed set (X∖F∈τ)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Geometry and topology Topology
OurBigBook.com Words: 547 Articles: 4
A subset F of a topological space (X,τ) is closed when its complement in that same ambient space is open: X∖F∈τ. For example, the closed interval [0,1] is closed in the usual real line because its complement is the open set (−∞,0)∪(1,∞). Always specify the ambient topological space: a subset can be closed in a subspace topology without being closed in the surrounding space. [ref]
Being closed is not the logical negation of being open. In the usual real line, all four combinations occur: (0,1) is open but not closed; [0,1] is closed but not open; the half-open interval [0,1) is neither open nor closed; and both the empty set and the whole real line are open and closed, hence clopen. Nor does being closed imply being a bounded set: [0,∞) is closed because its complement (−∞,0) is open, but it is not a bounded set. The symbol ∞ is not an extra point that this interval must contain.

In a metric space X, a subset F is closed exactly when every convergent sequence (xn​) in F whose limit of a sequence x lies in X has x∈F. For example, xn​=1−1/n lies in [0,1) for n≥1 and has limit of a sequence 1, which is missing; thus this half-open interval is not closed in the real line. This criterion is useful for keeping limiting constructions inside a prescribed set. [ref]
In every topological space, the general criterion is F=F, using closure; equivalently, F contains all its limit points. A closed set is always a sequentially closed set, but the converse can fail outside metric spaces. Arbitrary intersections and finite unions of closed sets are closed, by taking complements in the topology axioms.
