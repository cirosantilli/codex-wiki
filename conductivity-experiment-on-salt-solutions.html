Conductivity experiment on salt solutions

 Home Chemistry Chemical bond Ionic bonding Physical properties of salts
OurBigBook.com Words: 468
How can a clear chemical solution reveal otherwise invisible mobile ions? The documented Conductivity of Solutions by Harvard Natural Sciences Lecture Demonstrations demonstration uses metal electrodes enclosed in tubing, an electrical supply, and a lamp in the circuit. The protocol specifies about 150mL samples in labeled 250mL beakers and a separate deionized-water rinse station. The recorded readout is lamp brightness after the electrodes enter each sample; the simplified variant tests water before and after adding sodium chloride. These are apparatus and procedure specifications, not new measurements reported here.[ref]
The useful comparison is water, a sucrose chemical solution, and a sodium chloride chemical solution with the same probe geometry. A documented university account reports no visible light with water, a glowing lamp with dissolved sodium chloride, and a weak filament glow with its sucrose sample. The diagram summarizes the latter as little or no visible light; the precise detection threshold depends on the tester. Sucrose is a control for the idea that any dissolved material must conduct: its dispersed neutral molecules do not provide the abundant mobile ions supplied by the salt. This contrast is a documented teaching observation, not a numerical calibration of lamp brightness.[ref][ref]
Rinsing the electrodes between samples controls carryover of dissolved ions, and the water blank checks the solvent background. Repeating the blank after the salt sample would test whether that rinse was effective. Additional methodological controls are fixed immersed area, electrode separation, supply setting, temperature, and comparison at stated concentrations. These additional controls are proposed checks, not undocumented claims about the source demonstration.
The inference is that mobile electric charge carriers complete the circuit. In the chemical solution, an electric field drives positive ions along the field and negative ions against it; multiplying each drift velocity by its electric charge makes both contributions to conventional current point the same way. With an alternating supply the direction reverses each half-cycle. The external wires instead carry electric current through electrons. The lamp alone identifies neither the species nor an exact electrical conductivity.
Important limits remain. Water has a small intrinsic population of hydronium and hydroxide, so a dark lamp does not prove absence of ions. Changes in probe geometry affect measured electrical conductance; electrolysis, electrode interfaces, contamination, and heating can also affect a real circuit. The comparison supports a transport model but does not independently measure a coordination number, a partitioned partial atomic charge, or a lattice energy.
Nor does poor conduction by ordinary dry sodium chloride establish that all ionic crystals are electrical insulators. Crystallographic defects can allow transport, and solid electrolytes can have substantial ionic electrical conductivity without melting. The authored abstract of A lithium superionic conductor by Noriaki Kamaya and others reports room-temperature ionic conduction in a solid electrolyte; the full experimental article was not accessible here.[ref]
