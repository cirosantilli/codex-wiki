Finite-dimensional spectral theorem (A=∑j=1n​λj​ej​ej∗​)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra Operator theory Self-adjoint operator
OurBigBook.com Words: 573 Articles: 3
Every self-adjoint operator on a finite-dimensional real or complex inner product space has an orthonormal eigenbasis, and all its eigenvalues are real. In orthonormal coordinates, a real symmetric matrix has the form A=QΛQT, with Q an orthogonal matrix and Λ a real diagonal matrix. A complex Hermitian matrix instead has A=UΛU∗, with U a unitary matrix and U∗ its conjugate transpose. These are the self-adjoint cases of the spectral theorem; Axler gives the real and complex finite-dimensional statements in Section 7B.[ref]
An orthonormal eigenbasis turns a seemingly coupled linear map into independent scalar multiplications while preserving lengths and angles. If x=∑j​cj​ej​ and Aej​=λj​ej​, then
Ax=∑j​λj​cj​ej​,∥x∥2=∑j​∣cj​∣2,x∗Ax=∑j​λj​∣cj​∣2.
(15)
The last identity removes every cross term from the quadratic form. The eigenvalues tell us whether it is positive, negative, or mixed in sign, and the eigenvectors identify the corresponding directions. This connects positive-definite matrices, quadratic forms, and the Hermitian Rayleigh quotient range.
For a fully computed example, take
A=(21​12​),e+​=2​1​(11​),e−​=2​1​(−11​).
(16)
Direct multiplication gives Ae+​=3e+​ and Ae−​=e−​. Their dot product is zero and each has norm one. Thus
Q=2​1​(11​−11​),QTAQ=(30​01​).
(17)
Writing x=ce+​+de−​ changes xTAx=2x12​+2x1​x2​+2x22​ into 3c2+d2. Its level set xTAx=1 is therefore an ellipse with semiaxis lengths 1/3​ and 1, along e+​ and e−​. The coordinates change, but the ellipse does not: orthogonal diagonalization reveals its existing geometry.
For distinct eigenvalues, orthogonality follows at once from self-adjointness: λ⟨u,v⟩=⟨Au,v⟩=⟨u,Av⟩=μ⟨u,v⟩. Within a repeated eigenspace, choose an orthonormal basis; arbitrarily chosen eigenvectors of the same eigenvalue need not be orthogonal. One outline of the full argument finds an eigenvector, observes that its orthogonal complement is invariant, and repeats in that smaller space. Merely having real eigenvalues is weaker: (20​11​) has two real eigenvalues, but its eigenvectors (1,0)T and (−1,1)T are not orthogonal.
