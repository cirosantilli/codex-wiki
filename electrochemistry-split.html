Electrochemistry

 Home Chemistry
OurBigBook.com Words: 6k Articles: 57
Electrochemistry studies how chemical reactions, transport, and chemical equilibrium couple to electric charge and electric potential. In a cell, electrons travel through the external circuit, while mobile ions carry electric current through the electrolyte. At the electrodes, redox reactions connect these two forms of electric charge transport. This connection makes it possible both to obtain electrical work from a chemical reaction and to use electrical work to drive a chemical reaction.
An anode is an electrode where oxidation occurs; a cathode is an electrode where reduction occurs. These definitions refer to the chemical reactions, rather than universal positive or negative terminal signs. In a galvanic cell delivering electrical work, the anode is negative and the cathode positive. In an electrolytic cell driven by an external source, the anode is positive and the cathode negative. See Pingarrón et al., Terminology of electrochemical methods of analysis (IUPAC Recommendations 2019), §§3.1 and 3.4 and Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, §17.2: galvanic cells.
For a worked example, consider an electrolytic cell containing molten sodium chloride, with the anode connected to the positive terminal and the cathode to the negative terminal of an external source. At the cathode, sodium cations gain electrons; at the anode, chlorine anions lose electrons:
cathode, reduction:anode, oxidation:overall:​2Na++2e−⟶2Na,2Cl−⟶Cl2​+2e−,2Na++2Cl−⟶2Na+Cl2​.​
(1)
The first line has net electric charge zero on each side; the second has −2e on each side. Adding the lines cancels the transferred electrons and preserves both chemical atom counts and net electric charge. The sodium product is neutral, and the chlorine product is a diatomic molecule. These equations specify molten sodium chloride; aqueous chemical solutions introduce competing water reactions. See 17.7 Electrolysis (§17.7: electrolysis of molten sodium chloride).
Electrochemistry underlies battery charging and the manufacture of chemicals by electrolysis. Its particularly useful idea is that an electron inventory in a balanced chemical equation also accounts for transported electric charge. Transport alone is a different question from an electrode chemical reaction: an ion may carry electric current through the electrolyte without itself being consumed at an electrode.
Historically, Michael Faraday introduced the terms electrode, electrolyte, anion, cation, and ion in the terminology section of his 1834 paper, Experimental Researches in Electricity, Seventh Series by Michael Faraday (§§662–667). His §667 explicitly treats the direction assigned to electric current as a convention. His ion language predates the modern electron and atomic nucleus picture used in the charge-counting example above.
For an electrolyte near chemical equilibrium, a Poisson-Boltzmann equation can approximate the distribution of ions in an electric potential. Such an equilibrium model does not by itself describe a cell carrying sustained electric current.
