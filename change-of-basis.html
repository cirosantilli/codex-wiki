Change of basis

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra
OurBigBook.com Words: 399 Articles: 2
A change of basis rewrites a vector using a different ordered basis of the same vector space. The vector stays fixed; its coordinate vector changes because the measuring directions change. Both bases must describe every vector uniquely, so a change of basis is reversible.
Start with the Cartesian basis e1​=(1,0), e2​=(0,1), and choose the new basis vectors u=(1,1) and v=(1,−1). For the vector z=(2,0),
z=2e1​+0e2​=u+v,[z]old​=(20​),[z]new​=(11​).
(43)
The second coordinate vector says to travel once along u and once along v. Their vertical contributions cancel, leaving exactly the original horizontal arrow. The diagram shows two descriptions of that same arrow, with the same origin and endpoint.
Choosing a useful basis can reveal simple structure hidden by the old coordinate vectors. Here the new basis describes common and opposing contributions. For two equal measurements (5,5), the new coordinate vector is (5,0): only the common contribution is present. In larger examples, a suitable basis can concentrate important information into a few coefficients. Strang's lecture on change of basis develops this principle through applications: the reversible change of basis preserves information, while subsequently discarding small coefficients loses information.[ref]
