Rare-jump counterexample to a strong law with convergent first-moment averages (P(Xn​=1±n)=1/(2nlogn),Xn​→1 in L1,n−1∑i=2n+1​Xi​→ a finite limit a.s.)

Take independent random variables equal to 1+n and 1−n with probability 1/(2nlogn) each, and otherwise equal to one. Their first absolute deviations are 1/logn→0, so Xn​→1 in L1 convergence. The same triangle-inequality argument gives convergence in L1 of their arithmetic averages to one. For p>1, however, E∣Xn​−1∣p=np−1/logn→∞.
The exceptional jumps occur infinitely often almost surely by the Borel-Cantelli second lemma, since ∑1/(nlogn)=∞. If the arithmetic averages Sn​/n converged to any finite value along a sample path, then Xn+1​/n=Sn​/n−[(n−1)/n][Sn−1​/(n−1)] would tend to zero. At each exceptional jump its magnitude is at least one. Thus the averages have no finite almost sure limit. Independence and convergence of first-moment averages do not alone give a strong law of large numbers.
