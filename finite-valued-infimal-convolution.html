Finite-valued infimal convolution

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Mathematical optimization Convex optimization Convex analysis Infimal convolution
OurBigBook.com Words: 392 Articles: 1
If two nonnegative finite-valued convex functions are defined on all of a finite-dimensional space, their infimal convolution is finite, convex and continuous everywhere. Convex perturbation duality then gives strong duality and dual attainment at every argument. It does not require or imply attainment of the primal decomposition infimum.
More precisely, let f,g:Rn→[0,∞) be finite everywhere and write h=f□g. Nonnegativity supplies a lower bound, while the two trial splits (x,0) and (0,x) give
0≤h(x)≤min{f(x)+g(0), f(0)+g(x)}<∞.
(46)
Mixing nearly optimal splits proves convexity, and continuity of a convex function applies because the effective domain is the entire finite-dimensional vector space. These statements concern the optimized value, rather than existence of an optimal split. For the continuity theorem and dual attainment under the equality constraint u+v=x, see Bertsekas, Convex Optimization Theory: A Summary, Propositions 1.3.11 and 5.3.3(a). The equality has a feasible point in the whole space for every x, and the displayed bound makes its optimal value finite.
A finite value without any optimal split. On R, take the convex functions f(u)=eu and g(v)=0. Both are nonnegative and finite everywhere, but
(f□g)(x)=infu∈R​eu=0
(47)
for every x. The splits (u,v)=(−k,x+k) have costs e−k→0, yet the exponential function is strictly positive at every finite u. No split attains the infimum. The value function is even constant and differentiable; regularity of the value function does not prevent optimizing splits from escaping to infinity. For example, k=1,3,6 gives costs approximately 0.367879, 0.049787 and 0.002479.
A sufficient extra condition for attainment at a fixed x is that u↦f(u)+g(x−u) be a coercive function, meaning it tends to +∞ as ∥u∥→∞. Under the finite-valued assumptions it is a continuous function, so a sufficiently large closed ball contains a global minimum by the extreme value theorem. If this cost is also a strictly convex function, the optimal split is unique. The quadratic example above satisfies both conditions; the exponential function example does not satisfy coercivity.
Nonnegativity is sufficient, not necessary: finite lower bounds f≥A, g≥B would give h≥A+B by the same argument. Removing all lower-bound control can lose finiteness even for finite convex functions: f(u)=u and g(v)=−v give f(u)+g(x−u)=2u−x, hence h(x)=−∞. Nor does finiteness alone guarantee differentiability: choosing f=g=∣⋅∣ gives h(x)=∣x∣ by the triangle inequality and the split (x,0).
