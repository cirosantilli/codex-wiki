Qubit state (ρ=21​(I+r⋅σ))

 ... Physics Branches of physics Quantum mechanics Quantum system Quantum state Qubit
OurBigBook.com Words: 647
A qubit state specifies measurement probabilities, rather than a pre-existing answer to every possible measurement. For a pure state, normalized vectors differing by a global phase represent the same physical state:
∣ψ⟩∼eiγ∣ψ⟩,γ∈R.
(17)
Equivalently, the pure state is a ray: the one-dimensional complex linear subspace spanned by any nonzero representative. Dividing a nonzero vector by its norm fixes its length, but leaves the global phase free. The density operator is unchanged because (eiγ∣ψ⟩)(eiγ∣ψ⟩)†=∣ψ⟩⟨ψ∣. In contrast, multiplying only one nonzero component by eiϕ changes a relative quantum phase and can change measurement probabilities. [ref].
After choosing a global phase, every pure state of a qubit can be written
∣ψ(θ,ϕ)⟩=cos(θ/2)∣0⟩+eiϕsin(θ/2)∣1⟩,0≤θ≤π,0≤ϕ<2π.
(18)
At θ=0 or π, the parameter ϕ does not distinguish physical states: only one component survives. Elsewhere it is the relative quantum phase. The corresponding unit Bloch vector is r=(sinθcosϕ,sinθsinϕ,cosθ) on the existing Bloch sphere. Its angles describe a space of states; for a photon polarization qubit they are not the photon's position or direction of travel. [ref].
The Born rule in the computational basis gives P(0)=cos2(θ/2) and P(1)=sin2(θ/2). These probabilities discard ϕ. In the Hadamard basis ∣±⟩=(∣0⟩±∣1⟩)/2​, adding or subtracting the probability amplitudes instead gives
PX​(±)=21​∣α±β∣2=21​±Re(α∗β)=21​(1±sinθcosϕ).
(19)
This last equality follows by substituting the angular representative. In the basis ∣±i⟩=(∣0⟩±i∣1⟩)/2​, the same calculation gives
PY​(±)=21​∣α∓iβ∣2=21​±Im(α∗β)=21​(1±sinθsinϕ).
(20)
Thus both the real and imaginary parts of quantum coherence can be observed, using different measurement settings. For example, (∣0⟩+∣1⟩)/2​ and (∣0⟩−∣1⟩)/2​ both give P(0)=P(1)=1/2, but the first gives PX​(+)=1 and the second PX​(+)=0. A global phase could never cause this difference.
For a general qubit state, write its density matrix in the computational basis as
ρ=(pc∗​c1−p​),p∈R,c∈C,0≤p≤1,∣c∣2≤p(1−p).
(21)
These are exactly the Hermitian, unit-trace, positive semidefinite constraints. Positivity means every eigenvalue is nonnegative, not that every matrix entry is nonnegative. The general definition is given in density matrix. [ref].
For this two-by-two matrix, the explicit constraint follows from its determinant, detρ=p(1−p)−∣c∣2: with trace one, a nonnegative determinant and real eigenvalues rule out a negative eigenvalue. Its eigenvalues are
λ±​=21​(1±(2p−1)2+4∣c∣2​).
(22)
The pure states have ∣c∣2=p(1−p) and eigenvalues 1,0; the mixed states have strict inequality and two positive eigenvalues. For a normalized representative, p=∣α∣2 and c=αβ∗. A statistical preparation ρ=∑j​wj​∣ψj​⟩⟨ψj​∣, with wj​≥0 and ∑j​wj​=1, averages probabilities, rather than adding probability amplitudes. Such a preparation is mixed if the positive-weight vectors do not all represent the same ray. A reduced density matrix of an entangled pair can also be mixed even when the pair has a pure state. [ref].
For a concrete check, the pure state (3​∣0⟩+∣1⟩)/2 and the mixed state obtained by preparing ∣0⟩ with probability 3/4 and ∣1⟩ with probability 1/4 have
ρpure​=(3/43​/4​3​/41/4​),ρmix​=(3/40​01/4​).
(23)
Both have trace one and the same computational-basis measurement probabilities. Their eigenvalues are respectively (1,0) and (3/4,1/4); the respective values of their purity of a density operator Tr(ρ2) are 1 and 5/8. Their Hadamard basis plus probabilities are respectively 1/2+3​/4≈0.9330 and 1/2. By contrast, setting p=3/4,c=1/2 would give determinant −1/16 and eigenvalue (2−5​)/4<0: normalized diagonal entries alone do not make a valid qubit state.
The same constraints become especially beautiful in the Bloch ball representation. With the Pauli matrices σ=(X,Y,Z) and the identity operator I,
ρ=21​(I+rx​X+ry​Y+rz​Z),r=(2Rec,−2Imc,2p−1),∣r∣≤1.
(24)
The eigenvalues are (1±∣r∣)/2, so the Bloch sphere is precisely the pure state boundary, and I/2 is the maximally mixed centre. For each axis j, the Born rule gives Pj​(±)=(1±rj​)/2. This unifies a geometric picture, matrix positivity, and experimental frequencies in one description. For the equatorial family in the phase diagram, p=1/2,c=e−iϕ/2 and r=(cosϕ,sinϕ,0); replacing it by I/2 erases the transverse components and every fringe. See quantum state tomography for how independent ensembles measured along the three axes estimate these components. [ref].
