Thermal conductivity (k)

 ... Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Heat Thermal conduction Fourier's law
OurBigBook.com Words: 417 Articles: 5
Thermal conductivity is the material response coefficient in Fourier's law. For an isotropic material it is a scalar k, with units Wm−1K−1; larger positive k means a larger conductive heat flux for the same temperature gradient. It can depend on temperature and composition. Direction-dependent response in an anisotropic material is described by a thermal conductivity tensor. The two worked examples use stipulated positive constants, rather than measured values for named materials.
Thermal conductivity describes material response, whereas thermal resistance also includes geometry: for the steady bar, Rth​=L/(kA). Doubling k at fixed length, cross-sectional area and end temperatures halves thermal resistance and doubles the rate of heat transfer. Doubling length changes thermal resistance without changing the material's thermal conductivity. This distinction lets a designer improve cooling either by choosing a higher thermal conductivity or by shortening and widening the path. It also explains why the less conductive layer in thermal resistance takes the greater temperature drop.
Thermal diffusivity answers a different question: how rapidly a temperature disturbance spreads. Its usual model κ=k/(ρcp​) also involves mass density ρ and specific heat capacity cp​, so equal thermal conductivities need not imply equal rates of warming.
