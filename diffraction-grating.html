Diffraction grating

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics Physical optics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 16
A diffraction grating is a periodic optical structure whose repeated slits or grooves direct light into diffraction orders through wave interference. Its beauty is that many individually broad contributions cooperate to select narrow directions: the spacing determines where the principal peaks occur, while the number of illuminated elements determines how sharply they are selected. This makes a diffraction grating useful in a spectrometer, where separating wavelengths spatially lets us examine an optical spectrum. [ref].
Consider a transmission diffraction grating in air, illuminated at normal incidence by a plane wave. Let d be the centre-to-centre slit spacing, λ the wavelength in the surrounding medium, and θ the signed outgoing angle from the forward normal. In Fraunhofer diffraction, where observation directions specify the far-field pattern, adjacent contributions have path difference dsinθ and phase difference δ=2πdsinθ/λ. Their wave amplitudes reinforce whenever δ=2πm, giving dsinθ=mλ. This normal-incidence geometry should be kept distinct from the incident and reflected angle convention in the grating equation below. [ref].
Angular colour separation follows immediately. At fixed nonzero diffraction order, increasing the wavelength moves the peak farther from the normal. With d=2μm, first-order light at 450nm leaves at 13.003∘, whereas 650nm leaves at 18.966∘: a separation of 5.963∘. The zero diffraction order stays at 0∘ for both wavelengths. Thus grating dispersion comes from an orderly dependence of wave interference on wavelength, rather than an arbitrary assignment of colours to directions.

The apparent 0/0 at a principal peak has limiting value one. A short explanation uses the geometric series: equal wave phases add N equal complex amplitudes; a small directional change distributes those contributions around the complex plane and allows cancellation. With each slit's contribution held fixed, peak scalar wave intensity scales as N2. The normalized plots remove that height change to make the narrowing visible. Stable relative wave phases, or optical coherence, are essential; a sum of independent intensities would lose this array structure. [ref].
For finite slit width a, the ideal rectangular-slit model multiplies FN​ by a broad single-slit diffraction envelope:
I(0)I(θ)​=[usinu​]2FN​(θ),u=λπasinθ​.
(59)
The sinc function factor, extended continuously at u=0, controls the brightness available to the narrow peaks. For example, a=0.5μm gives envelope values about 0.811, 0.405, and 0.090 at diffraction orders 1, 2, and 3 in the 600nm example. If an envelope zero coincides with an allowed diffraction order, that peak disappears. A permitted direction therefore need not produce a visible bright spot. [ref].
Historically, Joseph von Fraunhofer used fine parallel wires stretched between threaded rods to make diffraction gratings. Kenyon's historical apparatus account describes both this construction and a later reconstruction with visible light. The example makes the governing idea tangible: regular spacing can turn a simple array of obstacles into an instrument for distinguishing wavelengths. [ref].
