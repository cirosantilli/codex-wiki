Surface tension (γ)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Fluid mechanics
OurBigBook.com Words: 5k Articles: 72
Surface tension γ is the tensile force per unit length across a line drawn in a liquid interface. For a clean interface with isotropy, it has the same value in every tangential direction: each side pulls the other along the surface, perpendicular to the line. A straight segment of length ℓ therefore transmits a force of magnitude γℓ from one interface. Here we assume constant γ>0, with fixed temperature and composition. The same coefficient measures the reversible work required to create surface area:
Fone interface​=γℓ,dEs​=γdA,[γ]=Nm−1=Jm−2.
(1)
The equality of units follows from 1J=1Nm; the equality of the mechanical coefficients follows from the moving-rim argument below. [ref] [ref]
For a concrete example, take an ideal flat soap film spanning a rectangular wire frame with a straight sliding rim of length ℓ. Pull the rim outwards in the plane of the film, perpendicular to the rim. Each of the film's two liquid–gas interfaces pulls the rim back towards the film with force γℓ, so the outward holding force is F=2γℓ. The two faces are two interfaces, each with its own surface tension. With the assumed toy value γ=0.072Nm−1 and ℓ=20mm=0.020m, the required force is 2.88mN; one interface would contribute 1.44mN. This value of γ is a stipulated model parameter, rather than a measured value for a particular soap solution.
Why does an interface resist being enlarged? Its molecules have a different environment from those in the bulk liquid; changing that environment has an energy cost. In the constant-surface tension model, reducing surface area lowers surface energy. This connects an easily measured rim force to the shape of a droplet, the Young–Laplace equation for its pressure, and the restoring mechanism of capillary waves. It is useful because one coefficient links forces, energy and geometry. [ref]
The moving-rim argument also explains the factor of two without memorizing a special formula. An outward displacement dx creates surface area ℓdx on each face, hence dAtotal​=2ℓdx. Slow pulling without viscous dissipation requires work Fdx=γdAtotal​, giving F=2γℓ. For one interface, the same argument gives F=γℓ. A surface tension gradient instead produces Marangoni stress; the constant-γ assumption excludes that additional effect.

Zero-contact-angle wall meniscus (z0​=2​ℓc​)

Words: 80
Meniscus (liquid) OurBigBook.com
A static liquid meniscus rising on a perfectly wetting vertical wall has dimensional height z0​=2​ℓc​, where ℓc​=σ/(ρg)​ is the capillary length. In capillary-length coordinates, write the interface x=h(z). The Young–Laplace equation gives hzz​/(1+hz2​)3/2=z and the first integral hz​/1+hz2​​=z2/2−1. The integration constant follows from the horizontal far bath, where z→0 and hz​→−∞. Zero contact angle means h=hz​=0 at the wall, so z0​=2​. Expanding and integrating there gives h∼(z−z0​)2/2​, the parabolic shape used to match an entrained film.

Hamaker estimate of liquid surface tension (γ≃AH​/(24πh02​))

Words: 79
Surface tension OurBigBook.com
For two liquid half-spaces, integrate the magnitude of a dispersion attraction per area AH​/(6πh3) from a molecular contact cutoff h0​ to infinity. The work per area is AH​/(12πh02​). Because cleavage creates two interfaces, each has the displayed surface tension in this approximation. The cutoff is essential: a continuum attraction cannot describe atomic overlap and short-range repulsion. This estimate neglects interfacial entropy, detailed density profiles and nonadditive interactions, and it cannot replace the metallic bonding or hydrogen bonds that dominate some liquids' cohesion.

Thermodynamics of a liquid film

Words: 113
Surface tension OurBigBook.com
For a film described by dU=TdS+γdA, with material content fixed, its Helmholtz free energy obeys dF=−SdT+γdA. Equality of mixed partial derivatives gives the Maxwell relation (∂S/∂A)T​=−(∂γ/∂T)A​. If γ depends only on temperature, write γ′=dγ/dT; then
dS=TCA​​dT−γ′dA,dU=CA​dT+(γ−Tγ′)dA.
(2)
Here CA​ is the heat capacity at constant area. For CA​>0 and γ′<0, reversible adiabatic stretching cools the film, since dT/dA=Tγ′/CA​<0, although positive mechanical input can increase its total internal energy. If γ=γ0​−βT with constant positive β and constant CA​, the isentropic process satisfies CA​ln(T/T∗​)+βA=constant, where T∗​ is an arbitrary fixed reference temperature. The work convention uses the total area conjugate to γ; a two-surface film described by projected area requires the corresponding factor of two consistently throughout.

Surface energy (Es​=γA)

Words: 260
Surface tension OurBigBook.com
For a liquid interface with constant surface tension γ, the mechanical surface energy is the energy associated with its surface area A:
Es​=γA+C,Es​(A2​)−Es​(A1​)=γ(A2​−A1​).
(3)
The additive constant C does not affect the work needed to change surface area. For several interfaces with the same surface tension, A means their total surface area; the two faces of a thin soap film must both be counted. [ref]
With assumed γ=0.072Nm−1, creating 1cm2=10−4m2 of one interface requires 7.2μJ of reversible work. In the sliding-rim example illustrated under surface tension, moving a 20mm rim outwards by 1mm creates 4.0×10−5m2 across the two faces and requires 2.88μJ. Independently, multiplying its 2.88mN holding force by the displacement gives the same work.
This makes the preference for a round isolated droplet precise. At fixed volume V, the three-dimensional isoperimetric inequality gives
A3≥36πV2,
(4)
with equality for a sphere. Thus a sphere minimizes surface energy among sufficiently regular closed shapes enclosing the same volume, when surface tension is uniform and other energy contributions are negligible. This is an application of the isoperimetric inequality, rather than a proof of it. Walls, wetting, and gravity can change the shape by adding constraints or other energy contributions. [ref]
The differential rule dEs​=γdA integrates directly to Es​=γA+C because surface tension is constant. At fixed liquid volume, decreasing surface area can drive viscous fluid flow; the released surface energy can supply viscous dissipation. This mechanical bookkeeping is useful for predicting which deformations can release energy. If surface tension depends on temperature or composition, distinguish this mechanical area-work relation from the complete interfacial thermodynamic free energy and internal energy.

A liquid spreads macroscopically over a solid with zero macroscopic equilibrium contact angle. In the simple interfacial-energy model the threshold is γSV​−γSL​=γLV​, or zero spreading coefficient; positive coefficient favors a spreading film. Extrapolating a concentration-dependent liquid-vapour surface tension to this threshold assumes the other interfacial energies remain fixed and no intervening adsorption or micellization behavior changes the trend.

Spreading coefficient (S=γSV​−γSL​−γLV​)

Cassie–Baxter state (cosθ∗​=fs​(1+cosθY​)−1)

Words: 67
Wetting OurBigBook.com
A Cassie–Baxter state is a composite wetting state in which a drop contacts solid asperities over area fraction fs​ and trapped gas over the remainder. An ideal area-weighted contact-angle model gives the displayed apparent angle from the smooth-solid Young's equation angle θY​. Reducing solid contact can strongly enhance apparent hydrophobicity. Such a state can be metastable and can collapse when liquid enters the texture; roughness alone does not guarantee it.

Contact line

The line where the interfaces of three phases meet, for example where a liquid film meets a solid and the surrounding gas. Continuum assumptions at this line can differ from the bulk lubrication approximation.

Contact-line pinning

Words: 69
Contact line OurBigBook.com
A contact line is pinned when its position remains fixed while the surrounding drop shape or contact angle changes. Surface heterogeneity or deposited material can sustain such a state over a range of angles. Evaporation from a pinned drop then requires compensating liquid motion and can generate outward solute transport associated with the coffee-ring effect. A model with a freely receding line and fixed contact angle has a different boundary constraint and need not predict the same flow direction.

Bond number (Bo=Δρgℓ2/γ)

Surface tension OurBigBook.com
The Bond number compares hydrostatic stress over a length ℓ with capillary stress. For a gas bubble of radius a and negligible density, Bo=ρga2/γ. Small Bond number is the usual sufficient criterion for capillarity to resist gravity-scale deformations. For the clean-bubble terminal speed, the capillary number is Ca=μU/γ=Bo/3.

Oil lens capillary balance (h2=2Γ/((ρw​−ρo​)g))

Words: 93
Surface tension OurBigBook.com
For a broad, thin oil lens below a flat ice-water interface, define the effective interfacial energy Γ=γio​+γow​−γiw​ from the relevant surface tensions. At fixed oil volume V=Ah, the energy is E=ΓV/h+21​(ρw​−ρo​)gVh. Setting its derivative to zero gives h2=2Γ/[(ρw​−ρo​)g] for Γ>0, and the positive second derivative proves a minimum. Contact angle (θc​)

Surface tension OurBigBook.com
The contact angle is measured through a liquid between its interface and a solid substrate at their contact line. The planar interfacial balance γSV​−γSL​=γLV​cosθ for the equilibrium contact angle through the liquid, with interfacial free energies defined per actual area. Roughness and retained air require additional area accounting.

Contact-angle hysteresis (θR​≤θ≤θA​)

Words: 58
Contact angle OurBigBook.com
A contact line can remain pinned while the observed contact angle changes between receding and advancing limits. Surface roughness and chemical heterogeneity can produce this hysteresis. Slowly adding or removing liquid from a sessile drop measures advancing and receding angles separately. An observed pinned angle therefore need not be the single equilibrium angle predicted by Young's equation for a perfectly homogeneous surface.

Young's equation (γSV​=γSL​+γLV​cosθ)

Words: 154 Articles: 2
Contact angle OurBigBook.com
For a smooth homogeneous solid in thermodynamic equilibrium with a liquid and vapor, Young's equation relates their three interfacial tensions to the contact angle measured through the liquid. A virtual increase dA of wetted area changes the interfacial free energy by (γSL​−γSV​+γLV​cosθ)dA. Setting this to zero proves the equation. Roughness, pinning and chemical heterogeneity can make an observed apparent angle differ from this ideal equilibrium value. The equation does not determine each solid–liquid surface energy from a contact angle alone, but two liquids on the same unchanged dry substrate allow the solid–vapor term to be eliminated by subtraction.

Young–Laplace equation (Δp=γκ)

Words: 583 Articles: 4
Surface tension OurBigBook.com
The Young–Laplace equation relates the pressure jump across a stationary interface to its curvature and surface tension. Choose a unit normal vector n pointing from the inside region to the outside region, and define κ=∇s​⋅n to be positive for a convex sphere. In mechanical equilibrium, with constant surface tension and no additional normal viscous stresses,
Δp=pin​−pout​=γκ=γ(k1​+k2​).
(10)
Here k1​,k2​ are the signed principal curvatures in this convention. Their sum is twice the corresponding signed mean curvature; stating the convention prevents a factor-of-two ambiguity. The Young–Laplace equation is a local normal stress balance for one interface. [ref]
A spherical liquid droplet of radius R in a gas has one interface, with k1​=k2​=1/R. An ideal thin soap bubble has two liquid–gas interfaces. Adding their pressure jumps gives
Δpdroplet​=R2γ​,Δpsoap bubble​≃R4γ​.
(11)
For the soap bubble, Δp now means the inner gas pressure minus the outer gas pressure. The second formula assumes equal surface tensions on both faces and film thickness negligible relative to R, so their radii can be identified. Retaining distinct radii gives 2γ/Rinner​+2γ/Router​. A gas bubble inside bulk liquid has only one interface and therefore uses 2γ/R. [ref]
With the assumed constant toy surface tension γ=0.072Nm−1, a droplet with R=1mm has excess pressure 144Pa; at R=0.5mm it has 288Pa. A thin soap bubble assigned the same γ and R=1mm has excess pressure 288Pa. These are differences from the surrounding pressure, rather than absolute pressures. Halving the radius doubles the capillary pressure; doubling the interface count also doubles it at fixed radius and surface tension.
The spherical case has a short work argument. A virtual increase in radius gives dV=4πR2dR and dA=8πRdR. At mechanical equilibrium, the net pressure work balances the increase in surface energy:
pressure workΔp4πR2dR​​=surface energy increaseγ8πRdR​​⟹Δp=R2γ​.
(12)

Laser-diffraction measurement of capillary waves

Words: 469
Capillary wave OurBigBook.com
Zhu, Miao, Xu and Cao's 2007 experiment drove a triangular metal frame electromagnetically with a sinusoidal generator. In a roughly 3cm-deep, temperature-regulated pool, it produced nearly plane capillary waves. A 632.8nm laser illuminated the surface obliquely; the moving ripples acted as a reflection diffraction grating. One split beam monitored laser stability, while a CCD recorded diffraction orders on a screen 6.76m away. An air-supported optical table reduced ambient vibration. [ref]
The frequency sweep was 100–290Hz in 10Hz steps, using distilled water and kerosene at 20∘C. For each setting they averaged at least three measurements of first-order spot separation. Known optical geometry and the grating equation yielded k=2π/λ; generator readings yielded ω=2πf. Agreement with the predicted log–log slope 3/2 tests the capillary law, and its intercept gives surface tension if mass density is known. The authors identify surface contamination as a major error source; noncontact optical detection reduces additional disturbance. [ref]
The optical measurement can be understood from the grating equation. Let λL​ be the laser wavelength, α the incident grazing angle above the mean surface, and βm​ the outgoing grazing angle of diffraction order m, with the sign of m chosen so that
λ(cosα−cosβm​)=mλL​.
(22)
Thus calibrated spot positions determine outgoing angles, which determine the ripple wavelength λ, independently of the mechanical dispersion relation. This optical geometry treats the surface as a reflection diffraction grating at the laser wavelength. [ref]

(23)

Depth corrections are negligible here, but the gravity contribution is about 4.5%–1.1% of the capillary contribution. These are ideal predictions, not digitized results from the experiment.
