Surface tension (γ)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Fluid mechanics
OurBigBook.com Words: 4k Articles: 39
Surface tension γ is the tensile force per unit length across a line drawn in a liquid interface. For a clean interface with isotropy, it has the same value in every tangential direction: each side pulls the other along the surface, perpendicular to the line. A straight segment of length ℓ therefore transmits a force of magnitude γℓ from one interface. Here we assume constant γ>0, with fixed temperature and composition. The same coefficient measures the reversible work required to create surface area:
Fone interface​=γℓ,dEs​=γdA,[γ]=Nm−1=Jm−2.
(99)
The equality of units follows from 1J=1Nm; the equality of the mechanical coefficients follows from the moving-rim argument below. [ref] [ref]
For a concrete example, take an ideal flat soap film spanning a rectangular wire frame with a straight sliding rim of length ℓ. Pull the rim outwards in the plane of the film, perpendicular to the rim. Each of the film's two liquid–gas interfaces pulls the rim back towards the film with force γℓ, so the outward holding force is F=2γℓ. The two faces are two interfaces, each with its own surface tension. With the assumed toy value γ=0.072Nm−1 and ℓ=20mm=0.020m, the required force is 2.88mN; one interface would contribute 1.44mN. This value of γ is a stipulated model parameter, rather than a measured value for a particular soap solution.
Why does an interface resist being enlarged? Its molecules have a different environment from those in the bulk liquid; changing that environment has an energy cost. In the constant-surface tension model, reducing surface area lowers surface energy. This connects an easily measured rim force to the shape of a droplet, the Young–Laplace equation for its pressure, and the restoring mechanism of capillary waves. It is useful because one coefficient links forces, energy and geometry. [ref]
The moving-rim argument also explains the factor of two without memorizing a special formula. An outward displacement dx creates surface area ℓdx on each face, hence dAtotal​=2ℓdx. Slow pulling without viscous dissipation requires work Fdx=γdAtotal​, giving F=2γℓ. For one interface, the same argument gives F=γℓ. A surface tension gradient instead produces Marangoni stress; the constant-γ assumption excludes that additional effect.
