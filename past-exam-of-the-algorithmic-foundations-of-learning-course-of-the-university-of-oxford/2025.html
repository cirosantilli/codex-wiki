Write ψ(s)=logEesX for the cumulant-generating function, and let D+​={s>0:ψ(s)<∞}. This set is nonempty. In fact, finiteness of the moment-generating function at some η>0 and −η gives
Eeη∣X∣≤EeηX+Ee−ηX<∞,
(1)
so X is an integrable random variable. Put m=EX.
For the upper tail, use the following one-sided convex conjugate and its strict generalized inverse of a nondecreasing function:
I(x)=sups∈D+​∪{0}​{sx−ψ(s)},ψX∗−1​(t):=inf{x∈R:I(x)>t},t>0.
(2)
Here ψ(0)=0, and I is nondecreasing because every slope in its defining supremum is nonnegative. For x≥m, negative s cannot increase the conjugate: ψ(s)≥sm gives sx−ψ(s)≤s(x−m)≤0, and s=0 already gives zero. Thus I agrees with the full Legendre transform of a cumulant-generating function on its upper-tail branch. Using a strict generalized inverse of a nondecreasing function handles constant random variables and bounded supports without assuming a strictly increasing, everywhere finite conjugate. Directly from the definition,
{x:I(x)>t}=⋃s∈D+​​(st+ψ(s)​,∞),ψX∗−1​(t)=s∈D+​inf​st+ψ(s)​.​
(3)
This threshold is finite: any fixed s∈D+​ gives a finite upper bound, while eu≥1+u gives Ees(X−m)≥1 and hence ψ(s)≥sm, providing the lower bound m.
We now derive the needed Chernoff bound. For s∈D+​ and any real x, the pointwise inequality esX≥esx1{X>x}​ implies
P(X>x)≤eψ(s)−sx.
(4)
Let q=ψX∗−1​(t). For every ε>0, the infimum formula supplies an s∈D+​ such that (t+ψ(s))/s<q+ε. Thus P(X>q+ε)≤e−t. As ε↓0, these events increase to {X>q}; continuity from below of a measure proves
P{X>ψX∗−1​(t)}≤e−t.​
(5)
The strict event and the strict generalized inverse of a nondecreasing function avoid any assumption that the optimizing s exists. For example, if X=m almost surely, the threshold is m for every t>0, and the probability is zero.

esMn​≤∑i=1n​esXi​,EesMn​≤nexp(2(1−cs)νs2​).
(6)

EMn​≤s1​logEesMn​≤slogn​+2(1−cs)νs​.
(7)
For n>1, put a=2logn/ν​ and choose s=a/(1+ca). This gives 1−cs=1/(1+ca) and therefore
slogn​+2(1−cs)νs​=alogn​+clogn+2νa​=2νlogn​+clogn.
(8)
For n=1, let s↓0 in the preceding upper bound, obtaining EX1​≤0. Consequently
E1≤i≤nmax​Xi​≤2νlogn​+clogn(n≥1).​
(9)
The proof uses only the individual moment-generating function bounds for Sub-Gamma random variables in the right tail; it requires no independence. Under the paper's literal one-sided definition, the negative part need not be integrable. Use closed balls with centres belonging to the space being covered. The covering number is
N(ε;R,ρ)=min{∣A∣:A⊆R, R⊆a∈A⋃​Bρ​(a,ε), ∣A∣<∞},​
(10)
with value ∞ if there is no finite metric net, and value zero for the empty space.
With this intrinsic-centre convention, the proposed monotonicity under subsets is false. Give R={−1,0,1} the usual distance, take S={−1,1}, and set ε=3/2. A ball centred at 0 covers R, so N(3/2;R,ρ)=1. The two points of S are distance 2 apart, so a ball centred in S contains only its centre, and N(3/2;S,ρ)=2. These values are also valid with open balls.
The obstruction is that the useful centre 0 disappears on passing to the subspace. If centres were allowed anywhere in a fixed ambient space, monotonicity would hold. With intrinsic centres, one instead has
N(2ε;S,ρ)≤N(ε;R,ρ).​
(11)
Indeed, discard covering balls missing S and choose one point of S from each remaining ball. The triangle inequality shows that every point of its intersection with S is within 2ε of the chosen point. This proves the replacement bound whenever the right side is finite; otherwise it is automatic.

We give a finite chaining argument, keeping the constants explicit. If δ=0, the metric space has just the point t0​ and both sides vanish. Suppose δ>0, and write
εk​=2−kδ,Nk​=N(εk​;T,d),k≥1.
(12)
Choose a smallest metric net Tk​⊆T of radius εk​, and maps πk​:T→Tk​ satisfying d(t,πk​(t))≤εk​. Set T0​={t0​} and π0​(t)=t0​. Since T is finite, choose K with εK​ smaller than the minimum distance between distinct points; then TK​=T and πK​(t)=t.
For each k, retain only the pairs actually occurring along these approximation paths:
Ek​={(πk​(t),πk−1​(t)):t∈T},mk​=∣Ek​∣.
(13)
The triangle inequality gives d(u,v)≤εk​+εk−1​=3εk​ for (u,v)∈Ek​; for k=1 the same argument uses d(t,t0​)≤δ=ε0​. Also
m1​≤N1​,mk​≤Nk​Nk−1​≤Nk2​ (k≥2),logmk​≤2logNk​.
(14)
Here only monotonicity in the radius for the same space T is used. No monotonicity under subsets is assumed.
For every pair in Ek​, the increment has the Sub-Gamma random variable in the right tail bound with common parameters 9νεk2​ and 3cεk​: for 0<s<(3cεk​)−1,
logEes(Xu​−Xv​)≤2(1−cd(u,v)s)νd(u,v)2s2​≤2(1−3cεk​s)9νεk2​s2​.
(15)
Identical pairs give the zero increment, which obeys the same bound. Both orientations have finite exponential moments, so all increments are integrable random variables. Differentiating their two upper bounds at zero also gives E(Xu​−Xv​)=0. Applying the maximum bound from part (b), including its mk​=1 case, gives
E(u,v)∈Ek​max​(Xu​−Xv​)​≤3εk​2νlogmk​​+3cεk​logmk​≤6ν​εk​logNk​​+6cεk​logNk​.​
(16)
The telescoping sum along each path is exact:
Xt​−Xt0​​=∑k=1K​(Xπk​(t)​−Xπk−1​(t)​).
(17)
Take the supremum, bound it by the sum of the maxima, and take expected values. This proves
Esupt∈T​(Xt​−Xt0​​)≤6ν​∑k=1K​εk​logNk​​+6c∑k=1K​εk​logNk​.
(18)
For either f(u)=logN(u;T,d)​ or f(u)=logN(u;T,d), the function is nonnegative and nonincreasing. Thus
∫εk+1​εk​​f(u)du≥2εk​​f(εk​),∑k=1K​εk​f(εk​)≤2∫0δ/2​f(u)du.
(19)
Combining the estimates yields the required metric entropy bound:
E[t∈Tsup​Xt​−Xt0​​]≤12ν​∫0δ/2​logN(u;T,d)​du+12c∫0δ/2​logN(u;T,d)du.​
(20)
In particular, C=12 and C′=12 work. The square-root and linear metric entropy terms arise respectively from the two terms in the sub-Gamma maximum bound. Because the chain ends exactly at T, no limiting sample-path regularity assumption is needed.

Use the single event
E=⋂t≥0​{θ⋆​∈Θt​},P(E)≥1−δ.
(28)
Fix a realization in E. The action space is nonempty and compact, since it is a closed subset of the finite-dimensional unit ball. The Lipschitz continuous reward function is continuous, so there is an optimal action x⋆​∈argmaxx∈X​μ(⟨x,θ⋆​⟩). Because the same reward function is used at every time, the pseudoregret is Rn​=∑t=1n​rt​, where
rt​=μ(⟨x⋆​,θ⋆​⟩)−μ(⟨Xt​,θ⋆​⟩)≥0.
(29)
The pair (x⋆​,θ⋆​) is feasible in the optimization defining (Xt​,θt​). This optimism in the face of uncertainty gives
μ(⟨x⋆​,θ⋆​⟩)≤μ(⟨Xt​,θt​⟩),rt​≤L∣⟨Xt​,θt​−θ⋆​⟩∣.
(30)
For any positive-definite matrix V, the ordinary Cauchy-Schwarz inequality applied to V−1/2x and V1/2z proves
∣⟨x,z⟩∣≤∣x∣V−1​∣z∣V​.
(31)
Both θt​ and θ⋆​ belong to the same confidence ellipsoid Θt−1​. The triangle inequality for the norm associated with this weighted Euclidean inner product therefore gives
∣θt​−θ⋆​∣Vt−1​​≤∣θt​−θt−1​∣Vt−1​​+∣θt−1​−θ⋆​∣Vt−1​​≤2βt−1​(δ),
(32)
and hence rt​≤2Lβt−1​(δ)∣Xt​∣Vt−1−1​​. Monotonicity of βt​(δ) and another Cauchy-Schwarz inequality now yield
Rn​​≤2Lβn​(δ)t=1∑n​∣Xt​∣Vt−1−1​​≤2Lβn​(δ)nt=1∑n​∣Xt​∣Vt−1−1​2​​​.​
(33)
This reasoning holds on E for every n simultaneously, proving the asserted probability. In combination with part (a), it also gives
Rn​≤2Lβn​(δ)2ndlog(1+dλn​)​.
(34)
Only the supplied simultaneous confidence set coverage and the optimistic choice are needed here. No claim that the displayed linear estimator is automatically a suitable estimator for a nonlinear link is required: its coverage was an explicit hypothesis.

Let Gt​=σ(X1​,…,Xt​). Under Pθ⋆​​, the true probability density function is positive and finite at every observation almost surely. Define the sequential likelihood ratio
M0​=1,Mt​=∏s=1t​pθ⋆​​(Xs​)pθs−1​​(Xs​)​.
(35)
A zero numerator sets the product to zero thereafter. Thus Mt​ is a well-defined nonnegative process, and Lt​(θ⋆​)=logMt​, with log0=−∞.
The next observation is independent of Gs−1​, while θs−1​ is measurable with respect to that sigma-algebra. Its conditional expectation is therefore
Eθ⋆​​[pθ⋆​​(Xs​)pθs−1​​(Xs​)​​Gs−1​]​=∫{pθ⋆​​>0}​pθs−1​​(x)dμ(x)≤1.​
(36)
Consequently E(Ms​∣Gs−1​)≤Ms−1​; induction also gives EMs​≤1. Thus (Mt​) is a nonnegative supermartingale for the filtration (Gt​). It need not be a martingale: an alternative distribution can put mass outside the support of the true distribution.
The maximal inequality for a nonnegative supermartingale, the corresponding form of the Ville inequality, gives
Pθ⋆​​{∃t≥0:θ⋆​∈/Θt​}​=Pθ⋆​​{∃t≥0:Lt​(θ⋆​)≥log(1/δ)}≤Pθ⋆​​{t≥0sup​Mt​≥1/δ}≤δEM0​=δ​.​
(37)
The strict inequality in the definition of the confidence set correctly becomes a non-strict crossing event.
There is a minor definitional omission in the source: for false candidates θ, the printed sum of log-likelihood ratios can involve 0/0 or both infinite signs. To define the entire confidence set without a positivity assumption, use the logarithm of the ratio of the two full products, ∏s​pθs−1​​(Xs​) and ∏s​pθ​(Xs​), taking 0/a=0 for a>0, a/0=+∞ for a>0, and, for example, 0/0=1. This is a measurable extension of the printed formula wherever that formula is defined. The choice at 0/0 cannot affect coverage of the truth, since its full denominator is positive almost surely. Alternatively, strict positivity of every density on the common support removes the omission.

The statement necessarily treats P0​,P1​ as probability measures, as required by the sampling model X∼PJ​. The word "measures" in the introduction must be read with this normalization. Use the paper's convention H2(P,Q)=21​∫(p​−q​)2dμ for the Squared Hellinger distance throughout this question.
A measurable binary hypothesis test ψ is determined by the measurable set A={x:ψ(x)=1}. Under the uniform prior on the two hypotheses, its error probability is
P{ψ(X)=J}=21​(P0​(A)+P1​(Ac))=21​(1−[P1​(A)−P0​(A)]).
(38)
Since the two measures have equal total mass,
Asup​[P1​(A)−P0​(A)]​=∫(p1​−p0​)+​dμ=21​∫∣p1​−p0​∣dμ=V(P0​,P1​).​
(39)
Indeed, the integral over any set is at most the integral of the positive part, and equality holds for A={p1​>p0​}. This is also the total variation distance. The likelihood ratio rule choosing hypothesis 1 precisely on that set therefore attains the infimum, proving
ψinf​P{ψ(X)=J}=21​[1−V(P0​,P1​)].​
(40)
Arbitrary choices on the tie set {p1​=p0​} leave the error unchanged. This is the exact link between total variation distance and optimal binary statistical hypothesis testing.

Use two translated uniform distributions, with parameters 0 and a=1/(2n). Their distance in the usual metric is a, so take the separation radius δ=a/2=1/(4n) and the loss function Φ(r)=r2. The supplied testing reduction, equivalently the Le Cam two-point lemma, and part (a)(i) imply
mn​≥δ221−V(U0n​,Uan​)​=8a2​[1−V(U0n​,Uan​)].
(44)
To see the reduction directly, turn any estimator into a binary hypothesis test by choosing the closer of 0 and a. If the test is wrong, the absolute estimation error is at least a/2. The worst quadratic risk is at least its average over these two parameters, giving precisely this bound.
Under Lebesgue measure on Rn, the two sample probability density functions are the indicators of the cubes [0,1]n and [a,a+1]n. Their intersection is [a,1]n, of volume (1−a)n. Therefore
1−V(U0n​,Uan​)=∫min(p0(n)​,pa(n)​)dx=(1−a)n≥1−na=21​,
(45)
where the inequality is Bernoulli's inequality. Consequently
mn​≥16a2​=64n21​,C=641​.​
(46)
This also fits the Hellinger distance approach: the two one-sample affinities give H2(U0​,Ua​)=a, while equal-height indicator densities imply V(U0n​,Uan​)=H2(U0n​,Uan​). Part (a)(ii) thus yields the same bound V≤na=1/2. The change of support produces Squared Hellinger distance proportional to a, explaining the parameter scale a≍n−1 and quadratic risk scale n−2.
