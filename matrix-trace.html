Matrix trace (trA=∑i=1n​aii​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 9
For an n-by-n square matrix A=(aij​) over a field, its matrix trace is the sum of its diagonal entries:
trA=∑i=1n​aii​.
(89)
For example, A=(21​11​) has trace 2+1=3. Adding corresponding diagonal entries immediately proves linearity:
tr(αA+βB)=αtrA+βtrB.
(90)
Thus matrix trace is a linear functional on the vector space of n-by-n matrices.[ref]
The surprising point is that the sum is intrinsic to a linear operator, even though individual diagonal entries depend on the basis. The cyclic property of the trace gives, for any invertible matrix P,
tr(P−1AP)=tr(APP−1)=trA.
(91)
Hence matrix similarity preserves trace. For the example above, choose the change-of-basis matrix P=(10​11​). Then
P−1AP=(11​12​):1+2=2+1=3.
(92)
The diagonal entries have changed but their sum has not. This makes trace useful for extracting a feature of the underlying linear operator without finding a preferred basis.
Over the complex numbers, or over the real numbers after allowing complex eigenvalues, the trace equals their sum with algebraic multiplicity:
trA=λ1​+⋯+λn​.
(93)
No diagonalizable matrix hypothesis is needed.[ref] To see why, use triangularization over an algebraically closed field: an upper triangular matrix similar to A has the eigenvalues on its diagonal, counted with algebraic multiplicity, because its characteristic polynomial is ∏i​(z−λi​). Similarity preserves trace, so summing that diagonal proves the formula.
For instance,
J=(20​12​),det(zI−J)=(z−2)2,trJ=2+2=4.
(94)
There is only one independent eigenvector, since (J−2I)(x,y)T=(y,0)T, so J is not a diagonalizable matrix. Nevertheless its repeated eigenvalue contributes twice. For our earlier A, the characteristic polynomial is z2−3z+1, its eigenvalues are (3+5​)/2 and (3−5​)/2, and their sum is 3. An inexpensive diagonal sum therefore gives spectral information before solving the characteristic polynomial.
The trace of a real matrix does not give that linear map's volume factor: that factor is the absolute value of its determinant. For example, 2I2​ has trace 4 and area factor 4, but 2I3​ has trace 6 and volume factor 8. Their occasional numerical agreement in two dimensions is a coincidence. The connection between trace and local volume change gives a geometric interpretation near the identity matrix, and describes the volume rate of a flow map generated by a linear system of differential equations.
