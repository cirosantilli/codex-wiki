By Vieta's formulas, the elementary symmetric polynomials in the roots give
c1​=−(α1​+α2​+α3​),c2​=α1​α2​+α2​α3​+α3​α1​,c3​=−α1​α2​α3​.
(1)
For each root, αj3​+c1​αj2​+c2​αj​+c3​=0. Multiplying by αjk​ and summing gives the power sum recurrence
sk+3​+c1​sk+2​+c2​sk+1​+c3​sk​=0(k≥0).​
(2)
Its initial values are s0​=3, s1​=−c1​ and s2​=c12​−2c2​. They are polynomials with integer coefficients in c1​,c2​,c3​. The recurrence uses only addition and multiplication by those coefficients, so induction proves the same for every sk​. This is the cubic case of Newton's identities.
For c1​=0, put α1​=u, α2​=v, α3​=−u−v. Then c2​=−(u2+uv+v2) and c3​=uv(u+v). Expanding the product of root differences gives
Δ​=(u−v)2(u+2v)2(2u+v)2=4(u2+uv+v2)3−27u2v2(u+v)2=−4c23​−27c32​​.​
(3)
This is the discriminant of a depressed cubic in depressed form, and the displayed identity also proves its expression in integer-coefficient symmetric polynomials.

An algebraic element is a separable algebraic element over K if its minimal polynomial of an algebraic element has distinct roots in a splitting field. A field extension is a separable extension if all its elements are separable algebraic elements.
The formal derivative is additive and satisfies D(Xm)=mXm−1. For monomials, D(XrXs)=(r+s)Xr+s−1=XrD(Xs)+XsD(Xr). Distributing over the finitely many coefficients proves the product rule D(fg)=fDg+gDf over any field. If f=(X−α)2h in an extension, the rule gives Df=2(X−α)h+(X−α)2Dh, so Df(α)=0, including in characteristic two. If f,Df were relatively prime in K[X], the Bézout's identity for polynomials would give uf+vDf=1, whose evaluation at α is impossible. Hence they are not relatively prime.
In characteristic zero, the formal derivative of a positive-degree irreducible polynomial m is nonzero and has smaller degree. Thus gcd(m,Dm)=1, since an irreducible polynomial has no proper nonconstant factor. By the previous argument, m has no repeated root. Every element of a finite field extension is algebraic, so the extension is a separable extension.
For K=Fp​(t), let αp=t. The characteristic-p identity gives Xp−t=(X−α)p, so K(α) is already a splitting field. It is a proper extension by the allowed no-root fact. More precisely, the minimal polynomial of an algebraic element α divides Xp−t and, over K(α), equals (X−α)r for some 1≤r≤p. If r<p, its next-to-leading coefficient is −rα∈K, forcing α∈K because r=0 in Fp​, a contradiction. Hence its minimal polynomial of an algebraic element is Xp−t, with its one root repeated p times. The extension is therefore inseparable, of degree p.
By contrast, every finite field extension L/Fp​ is a separable extension. If [L:Fp​]=n, then ∣L∣=pn. Lagrange's theorem in its nonzero multiplicative group gives apn=a for all a∈L. Thus every element is a root of Xpn−X, whose formal derivative is −1; it has no repeated roots. Every minimal polynomial of an algebraic element divides this separable polynomial, proving the assertion.

A finite straightedge and compass construction repeatedly draws the line through two available distinct points or the circle with an available center and an available distance as radius, and adjoins isolated intersections of two such lines or circles. A finite set is constructible when its points occur after finitely many such steps, starting with the specified points.
If the current coordinate field is F, intersections of two lines solve linear equations over F. A line and a circle lead to a quadratic equation over F. For two distinct circles, subtraction of their equations gives a line, reducing to the previous case. Thus each step adjoins coordinates in an extension of degree one or two. The tower law gives a finite tower of quadratic-or-trivial field extensions containing all output coordinates. If the final coordinate field contains K0​, then
[K:K0​]=2rfor some integer r≥0,​
(13)
because its degree divides the power-of-two degree of that tower.
Two implicit qualifications are necessary for this degree statement. The output is obtained in finitely many steps; the aggregate of infinitely many individually constructible points need not generate a finite extension. Also, unless the output retains the original coordinates, K need not contain K0​. In that case the degree statement applies to the compositum K0​K, rather than an undefined [K:K0​]. The heptagon application explicitly includes S0​, so neither issue obstructs it.
Here K0​=Q, and a vertex adjacent to (1,0) has first coordinate c=cos(2π/7), so Q(c)⊆K. Let ζ=e2πi/7. The cyclotomic polynomial 1+X+⋯+X6 is irreducible, giving [Q(ζ):Q]=6. Since ζ+ζ−1=2c, it satisfies X2−2cX+1 over Q(c). The element ζ is nonreal while Q(c)⊂R, so this relative degree is exactly two. By the tower law,
[Q(c):Q]=3.
(14)
If the regular regular heptagon were constructible, this degree would divide [K:Q]=2r, impossible. Therefore the regular heptagon is not constructible from the given unit segment.

For the n distinct complex embeddings σ1​,…,σn​ of the number field, define the discriminant of elements of a number field by
D(w1​,…,wn​)=det(σi​(wj​))2=det(TrK/Q​(wi​wj​)).
(15)
The equality follows by multiplying the transpose of the embedding matrix by itself. Each field trace is rational: it is also the trace of the rational matrix of multiplication by wi​wj​ on K. Thus the determinant lies in Q. If the wi​ are algebraic integers, the products and their conjugates are algebraic integers, and so are their sums. A rational algebraic integer is an integer, hence all trace entries and their determinant are integers.
Write uj​=∑i​aij​vi​. The inclusion of the generated abelian groups makes A=(aij​) an integer matrix. Its embedding matrix is the embedding matrix of the vi​ multiplied by A. Taking squared determinants gives
D(u1​,…,un​)=(detA)2D(v1​,…,vn​).​
(16)

Dedekind factorization theorem states that if K=Q(θ) with integral θ and p does not divide [OK​:Z[θ]], then a factorization f​=∏i​f​iei​​ of its minimal polynomial of an algebraic element into distinct monic irreducible polynomials over Fp​ gives
pOK​=∏i​Piei​​,Pi​=(p,fi​(θ)),N(Pi​)=pdegfi​.
(21)
The factors are distinct prime ideals; the residue degrees and ramification indices satisfy ∑i​ei​degfi​=[K:Q]. Unique prime ideal factorization holds in the ring of integers.
For a square-free integer d≡1(mod4), OK​=Z[−d​], so the index condition is automatic. If pi​∣d, the polynomial X2+d reduces to X2 modulo pi​. Hence
pi​OK​=Pi2​,Pi​=(pi​,−d​),N(Pi​)=pi​.
(22)
Therefore dOK​=(∏i​Pi​)2=(−d​)2. By unique prime ideal factorization, ∏i​Pi​=(−d​).
Let S be a nonempty proper subset and put m=∏i∈S​pi​. For d>1, 1<m<d and m is a square-free integer. If ∏i∈S​Pi​=(u+v−d​), its ideal norm gives
m=u2+dv2.
(23)
The inequality m<d forces v=0, leaving m=u2, impossible for a square-free integer greater than one. Thus every such subproduct is nonprincipal.
These are ramified prime classes for discriminant minus four d. The ideal classes [Pi​] have order dividing two. Their product is the identity, and the preceding proof rules out every other nonempty subset relation. The homomorphism F2k​→Cl(K) defined by these classes consequently has kernel exactly {0,(1,…,1)}. Its image is therefore
C2k−1​.​
(24)
The boundary case d=1 has k=0 and makes the printed exponent k−1 meaningless; its field is Q(i) with trivial ideal class group. The subgroup statement is understood for d>1, while the prime-factor and proper-subset statements are empty for d=1.

Minkowski convex body theorem states that a centrally symmetric convex measurable subset of Rn with volume greater than 2n times the covolume of a full Euclidean lattice contains a nonzero lattice point. The equivalent closed-boundary form follows by compactness for compact convex bodies.
The set St​ is centrally symmetric. It is convex because for 0≤λ≤1, the triangle inequality gives ∥λu+(1−λ)v∥1​≤λ∥u∥1​+(1−λ)∥v∥1​≤t. It is a diamond of area 2t2. For the embedded nonzero integral ideal I, the given covolume is 2N(I)d​. Hence Minkowski convex body theorem applies when
2t2>8N(I)d​,t2>4N(I)d​.
(25)
For the resulting nonzero α∈I, write x=σ1​(α), y=σ2​(α). Then
∣NormK/Q​(α)∣=∣xy∣≤4(∣x∣+∣y∣)2​≤4t2​.
(26)
Decrease t to 2N(I)d​​. A fixed slightly larger diamond contains only finitely many lattice points, so one nonzero α recurs along a sequence of such t values. Passing to the limit proves
∣NormK/Q​(α)∣≤N(I)d​.​
(27)
Given a target ideal class C, choose an integral ideal I in C−1. Since α∈I, the ideal J=(α)I−1 is integral, belongs to C, and has ideal norm
N(J)=N(I)∣Norm(α)∣​≤d​.
(28)
For K=Q(14​), every ideal class thus has an integral representative of norm at most 14​<4. The only possibilities are norms one, two or three. The prime two ramifies, giving the unique ideal of norm two P2​=(2,14​). The element 4+14​ has field norm 16−14=2, so (4+14​)=P2​, making it principal. The prime three is inert because X2−14≡X2+1(mod3) is an irreducible polynomial; its prime ideal has norm nine, so there is no ideal of norm three. Every possible small representative is principal, proving class number one for Q square root of fourteen. Therefore
h(Q(14​))=1.​

Work in OK​=Z[−d​], since d≡2(mod4). First x must be odd: if it were even, coprimality would make a odd, so x2+a2d≡2(mod4), which cannot be an odd power yp for p≥3. Thus y is odd too. Also no odd prime ℓ can divide both x and d. Such a prime does not divide a, and square-freeness of d gives vℓ​(x2+a2d)=1, whereas vℓ​(yp) is divisible by p. Hence gcd(x,d)=1.
Put α=x+a−d​. Suppose a prime ideal P divides both (α) and (α), and let ℓ be the rational prime below it. It then contains 2x and 2a−d​. The case ℓ=2 is impossible because N((α))=yp is odd. If ℓ is odd, the first inclusion gives ℓ∣x, and gcd(a,x)=1 makes a nonzero in the residue field. The second inclusion therefore gives −d​∈P, so ℓ∣d, contradicting the previous paragraph. Thus (α) and (α) are coprime ideals. In particular they generate the unit ideal, the precise meaning of coprimality here; no unique factorization of elements is assumed.
Their product is (y)p. Unique prime ideal factorization and disjoint prime support imply (α)=Ap for an integral ideal A. In the ideal class group, [A]p=1. Since p does not divide the class number, Lagrange's theorem forces [A]=1. Write A=(u+v−d​). Equality of the principal ideals gives α=ε(u+v−d​)p. The only units are ±1, since a unit satisfies u2+dv2=1 and d≥2. Both signs are pth powers for odd p, so they can be absorbed into the generator:
x+a−d​=(u+v−d​)p(u,v∈Z).​
