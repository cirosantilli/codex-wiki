An algebraic element is a separable algebraic element over K if its minimal polynomial of an algebraic element has distinct roots in a splitting field. A field extension is a separable extension if all its elements are separable algebraic elements.
The formal derivative is additive and satisfies D(Xm)=mXm−1. For monomials, D(XrXs)=(r+s)Xr+s−1=XrD(Xs)+XsD(Xr). Distributing over the finitely many coefficients proves the product rule D(fg)=fDg+gDf over any field. If f=(X−α)2h in an extension, the rule gives Df=2(X−α)h+(X−α)2Dh, so Df(α)=0, including in characteristic two. If f,Df were relatively prime in K[X], the Bézout's identity for polynomials would give uf+vDf=1, whose evaluation at α is impossible. Hence they are not relatively prime.
In characteristic zero, the formal derivative of a positive-degree irreducible polynomial m is nonzero and has smaller degree. Thus gcd(m,Dm)=1, since an irreducible polynomial has no proper nonconstant factor. By the previous argument, m has no repeated root. Every element of a finite field extension is algebraic, so the extension is a separable extension.
For K=Fp​(t), let αp=t. The characteristic-p identity gives Xp−t=(X−α)p, so K(α) is already a splitting field. It is a proper extension by the allowed no-root fact. More precisely, the minimal polynomial of an algebraic element α divides Xp−t and, over K(α), equals (X−α)r for some 1≤r≤p. If r<p, its next-to-leading coefficient is −rα∈K, forcing α∈K because r=0 in Fp​, a contradiction. Hence its minimal polynomial of an algebraic element is Xp−t, with its one root repeated p times. The extension is therefore inseparable, of degree p.
By contrast, every finite field extension L/Fp​ is a separable extension. If [L:Fp​]=n, then ∣L∣=pn. Lagrange's theorem in its nonzero multiplicative group gives apn=a for all a∈L. Thus every element is a root of Xpn−X, whose formal derivative is −1; it has no repeated roots. Every minimal polynomial of an algebraic element divides this separable polynomial, proving the assertion.
