A splitting field of a nonconstant polynomial f over a field K is an field extension L in which f factors into linear factors and which is generated over K by those roots. We prove existence and the divisibility simultaneously by induction on n=degf; constant nonzero polynomials have splitting field K.
If f is reducible, write f=gh with positive degrees r,s<n. By induction first split g, obtaining E/K of degree dividing r!, then split h over E, obtaining L/E of degree dividing s!. The tower law for field extensions gives [L:K]∣r!s!∣(r+s)!. All adjoined elements are roots of f, so L has the required minimality.
If f is irreducible, take E=K[x]/(f) and let α be the image of x. Then [E:K]=n and f=(x−α)h in E[x]. Apply induction to h, of degree n−1, to obtain [L:E]∣(n−1)!. Therefore
[L:K]=n[L:E]∣n!.​
(1)
This proof also covers inseparable polynomials; it does not incorrectly identify extension degree with automorphism count in that case.

For a splitting field L of f over K, define Gal(f)=AutK​(L). Each automorphism permutes the distinct roots, since it fixes the coefficients. An automorphism fixing every root fixes the field they generate, so this action is faithful. If there are r≤n distinct roots, it gives an embedding into Sr​, and hence into Sn​ by fixing n−r additional symbols.

KG={a∈K:σ(a)=a for every σ∈G}.
(10)

The Fundamental theorem of Galois theory says that for a finite Galois extension L/F with group G, the maps H↦LH and E↦Gal(L/E) are mutually inverse inclusion-reversing correspondences between subgroups and intermediate fields. They satisfy [L:LH]=∣H∣ and [LH:F]=[G:H]. An intermediate extension E/F is Galois exactly when its subgroup is normal, in which case its group is the quotient by that subgroup.
Let a=2​, b=3​, c=i. The real field Q(a,b) has degree 4: if 3​=u+v2​ with u,v∈Q, squaring forces uv=0 and either u2=3 or v2=3/2, both impossible. Adjoining the nonreal i doubles the degree. All three square-root signs can be changed independently, giving
G≅C23​,[L:Q]=8.​
(13)
Write σuvw​ for the automorphism sending (a,b,c) to ((−1)ua,(−1)vb,(−1)wc), where (u,v,w)∈F23​. Every nonzero vector generates an order-two subgroup, so there are 7 such subgroups. Their fixed fields are:
GeneratorFixed field
σ100​Q(3​,i)
σ010​Q(2​,i)
σ001​Q(2​,3​)
σ110​Q(6​,i)
σ101​Q(3​,−2​)
σ011​Q(2​,−3​)
σ111​Q(6​,−2​)
Each listed field has degree 4 and is fixed by the indicated sign change, which proves equality with the fixed field.
There are also 7 order-four subgroups: they are the kernels of the seven nonzero linear functionals on F23​. For e=(e1​,e2​,e3​)=0, define He​={σuvw​:e1​u+e2​v+e3​w=0}. Then
LHe​=Q(2e1​3e2​(−1)e3​​).
(14)
Thus the seven quadratic fields have radicands 2,3,−1,6,−2,−3,−6. Their square classes are distinct, so all are different. Finally the trivial subgroup fixes L, and all of G fixes Q. This accounts for every subgroup and intermediate field.

The Fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials states that every symmetric polynomial in n variables over a field is a polynomial in the elementary symmetric polynomials, uniquely so. For the three roots, Vieta's formulas give e1​=0, e2​=p, e3​=−q.
The squared product of root differences is symmetric. Expansion in elementary symmetric polynomials gives
(α−β)2(β−γ)2(γ−α)2​=e12​e22​−4e23​−4e13​e3​−27e32​+18e1​e2​e3​=−4p3−27q2.​​
(15)
One can verify the displayed identity simply by substituting the definitions of e1​,e2​,e3​ and expanding; it is the cubic polynomial discriminant.
If the cubic is irreducible, its roots are distinct in characteristic zero, so δ=0. Its Galois group is a transitive subgroup of S3​, hence either A3​ or S3​. A permutation of the roots multiplies δ by its sign. The fixed-field assertion of the Fundamental theorem of Galois theory therefore gives δ∈Q exactly when all group elements are even. Thus
Gal(f)=A3​ if δ∈Q,Gal(f)=S3​ otherwise.​
(16)
For example, f=x3−3x−1 has no rational root: neither 1 nor −1 is a root, and these are the only possibilities by the rational root theorem. Its discriminant is 108−27=81, so δ=±9∈Q and its group is A3​.

A finite solvable group is a group admitting a finite chain of normal subgroups with abelian successive quotients; equivalently its derived series eventually becomes trivial. The chain
S4​▹A4​▹V4​▹1
(17)
has quotients C2​,C3​,V4​, all abelian. Thus S4​ is solvable. Every subgroup of a solvable group is solvable, because intersecting the chain with the subgroup gives factors isomorphic to subgroups of the original abelian factors. A Galois group of a polynomial of degree at most 4 embeds in S4​ by its action on the roots, so it is solvable even if the polynomial is reducible or has repeated roots.
For the substitution, the characteristic-zero solvability by radicals theorem says that a polynomial has solvable Galois group if and only if its splitting field is contained in a finite tower formed by adjoining successive roots of elements of the preceding field. First place all roots α of f in such a tower. Adjoin a primitive mth root of unity and, for every nonzero α, an mth root βα​. These are radical adjunctions, and all roots of xm−α are βα​ζmj​. Zero roots require no adjunction. The resulting radical tower therefore contains the splitting field of f(xm). The theorem implies
Gal(f(xm)/Q) is solvable.​
(18)

An integral basis is a Z-basis of the ring of integers OK​; it consists of n algebraic integers and is also a Q-basis of K.
The lattice M has finite index h=[OK​:M]>1. Choose a prime p∣h. The finite abelian group OK​/M has an element of order p, represented by α∈OK​∖M. Thus
pα=∑j=1n​cj​wj​∈M.
(19)
If all cj​ were divisible by p, cancellation in the characteristic-zero field would put α in M, a contradiction. The discriminant-index formula for an integral lattice gives
Δ2(M)=h2Δ2(K),
(20)
where Δ2(K) is an integer. Hence p2∣Δ2(M), and the required integral element is α=p−1∑j​cj​wj​.

The Kummer-Dedekind theorem states the following. If K=Q(θ) with θ integral and p∤[OK​:Z[θ]], factor the minimal polynomial modulo p as fˉ​=∏i​gˉ​iei​​ into distinct monic irreducible factors. Then
pOK​=∏i​piei​​,pi​=(p,gi​(θ)),N(pi​)=pdeggi​.
(27)
The index restriction is essential.
For the proposed ideal relation, reflexivity uses α=β=1 and symmetry interchanges α,β. If (α)I1​=(β)I2​ and (γ)I2​=(δ)I3​, multiplication gives (αγ)I1​=(βδ)I3​. All multipliers remain nonzero. Thus it is an equivalence relation, namely equality of ideal classes.
Put s=−2​. The minimal polynomial is x2+2 and the given full power basis makes every prime eligible for the theorem. Modulo 3 its roots are ±1; modulo 5 and 7 it is irreducible; modulo 17 its roots are ±7. Consequently
(3)(5)(7)(17)​=(3,s−1)(3,s+1), is prime, is prime,=(17,s−7)(17,s+7).​​

The two inert ideals have norms 25 and 49; each split factor has norm equal to its rational prime.
