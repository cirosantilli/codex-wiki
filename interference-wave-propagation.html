Interference (wave propagation)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics Physical optics
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 6
Wave interference is the reinforcement or cancellation that occurs when overlapping wave amplitudes add by the superposition principle. For two monochromatic waves of the same frequency, mutual optical coherence means that their relative wave phase is stable during the observation. Equal wave phases give constructive interference; a half-cycle phase difference gives the destructive interference intensity minimum. Wave amplitudes add before their squares determine wave intensity. [ref].
Consider two overlapping beams in a homogeneous medium, with the same polarization of an electromagnetic wave and individual local time-averaged light intensities I1​,I2​. Their phase difference δ is measured in radians. In the scalar detection convention, the combined local time-averaged wave intensity is
I=I1​+I2​+2I1​I2​​cosδ.
(66)
The last term records wave interference; adding I1​+I2​ alone misses it. The formula describes irradiance when both fields propagate along the same direction, or when the same detector geometry factor is included in all three intensities. For crossing beams it describes the squared electric field response, proportional to scalar wave intensity; a specified component of the Poynting vector also requires the propagation geometry. The ideal crossing plane waves below make that distinction explicit.
For equal individual light intensities I1​=I2​=I0​>0, the cosine half-angle identity gives
I=2I0​(1+cosδ)=4I0​cos2(δ/2).
(67)
The constructive interference intensity is 4I0​ at δ=0 modulo 2π, and the destructive interference intensity is zero at δ=π modulo 2π. At phase quadrature, δ=π/2, it is 2I0​. With I1​=1 and I2​=0.25 in the same arbitrary wave intensity unit, the maximum is 2.25, the minimum is 0.25, and the phase quadrature value is 1.25: unequal wave amplitudes cannot cancel completely.
Both curves use the same wave intensity unit and a horizontal angle in radians. The dashed levels are the averages over a full phase difference cycle. Equal beams have interferometric visibility one; the unequal example has interferometric visibility 0.8.
A spatially varying phase difference turns the same law into interference fringes. Take two ideal plane electromagnetic waves with common wavelength λ, equal individual light intensities I0​, and propagation directions at angles +θ and −θ from the z axis in the xz plane, with 0<θ<π/2. Both have linear polarization along y, perpendicular to both propagation directions. Choose equal initial wave phases; their wavevectors and phase difference are
k±​=k(±sinθ,0,cosθ),k=λ2π​,ϕ±​=±kxsinθ+kzcosθ−ωt,δ=ϕ+​−ϕ−​=2kxsinθ.
(68)
Here k is the wavenumber and ω the angular frequency. The common z and time terms cancel, so the interference fringes stay at fixed x for every z. Their interference fringe spacing is
Λ=2ksinθ2π​=2sinθλ​,I0​I(x,z)​=4cos2(kxsinθ).
(69)
Set the length unit to λ, so that λ=1, and take θ=30∘=π/6: then Λ=1. The constructive interference intensity bands lie at integer x/λ; the destructive interference intensity bands lie at half-integer x/λ.
The grid covers −3≤x/λ≤3 and 0≤z/λ≤4; both axes are dimensionless lengths in wavelength units. The colour scale is I/I0​ from zero to four, using the same single-beam normalization as the equal-beam curve above. This is an ideal plane wave superposition, with no slits or point sources and no near-field approximation. For these y-polarized plane electromagnetic waves in vacuum, the longitudinal time-averaged Poynting vector obeys ⟨Sz​⟩=Icosθ; the plotted scalar I therefore differs from the irradiance through a plane normal to z by that common factor. This relation follows by adding the magnetic fields as well as the electric fields.
The bright peaks do not create energy. Averaging over one interference fringe spacing, or equivalently over one full phase difference cycle, gives
2π1​∫02π​I(δ)dδ=I1​+I2​,
(70)
because the integral of the cosine is zero, with sine as its antiderivative. Equal beams thus average to 2I0​, despite bright bands at 4I0​. For the crossing-wave geometry, the average longitudinal energy flux is correspondingly 2I0​cosθ. Bright and dark regions redistribute the incoming energy; conservation of energy concerns the full energy flux, rather than the height of one peak.
Wave interference is useful because phase differences convert small changes in optical path length into measurable changes of wave intensity, as in a Mach-Zehnder interferometer. Its beauty is that the same superposition principle produces both complete cancellation and fourfold brightening; adding more coherent contributions also produces the narrow peaks of a diffraction grating. Feynman's treatment connects this many-source pattern directly to addition of complex amplitudes. [ref].
A short proof outline uses local real fields Ej​(t)=Aj​cos(ωt+ϕj​) with parallel linear polarizations. Write I=C⟨(E1​+E2​)2⟩t​ and Ij​=CAj2​/2 for the same positive conversion factor C. Averaging over many wave periods while the phase difference stays fixed uses ⟨cos2(ωt+ϕj​)⟩t​=1/2 and ⟨cos(ωt+ϕ1​)cos(ωt+ϕ2​)⟩t​=cosδ/2, giving the stated law. Equivalently, add the complex amplitudes and take the squared complex modulus. [ref].
For different fixed linear polarizations in a common transverse plane, the cross term acquires the dot product of their real unit vectors, equal to cosα for relative angle α. Orthogonal linear polarizations remove the cross term in polarization-insensitive scalar detection; different orientations generally only reduce it. A common polarizer can select overlapping components and recover wave interference. Likewise, rapid random variation of the phase difference removes the observed cross term only when the detector's time average has ⟨eiδ(t)⟩t​=0. Slowly varying wave phases can instead move resolved interference fringes, and biased or incompletely sampled wave phases can leave residual contrast. Finite optical coherence therefore depends on the observation time as well as the fields.
