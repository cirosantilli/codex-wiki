Faraday effect

 ... Physics Branches of physics Electromagnetism Electromagnetic wave Plane electromagnetic wave Polarization of an electromagnetic wave
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 8
The Faraday effect is magnetic field-induced circular birefringence: opposite circular polarizations acquire different wave phases, rotating transmitted linear polarization. For a transparent medium with isotropy before applying the magnetic field, in the linear magnetic-response regime at fixed vacuum wavelength λ0​ and temperature T,
θ=V(λ0​,T)∫0L​B∥​(ℓ)dℓ.
(23)
Here V is the Verdet constant, L is the material path length, and B∥​ is the applied magnetic flux density component along the ray. This B is the external bias, distinct from the oscillating magnetic field of the light itself. Michael Faraday discovered the effect in 1845.[ref]
Fix the sign before using the formula. On the forward path choose a right-handed Cartesian coordinate system, propagation along +z, and measure θ from +x toward +y for the electric field's oscillation line. Positive θ is counterclockwise in the x-right, y-up end view seen from z>0, looking toward the source. Then B∥​=Bz​ and positive V means positive rotation for positive Bz​. This convention differs from the clockwise-positive convention in optical rotation. A linear polarization line is unchanged by 180∘; a measured orientation determines accumulated rotation only modulo π, unless continuity selects a branch.
For uniform B∥​, the formula reduces to θ=VBL. Its appeal is that a weak change of refractive index becomes a visible change of orientation. Magnetic reversal measurement of Faraday rotation turns this into an inference of V or of the magnetic field path integral. Faraday rotation on a round trip explains the directional behavior used by an optical isolator. In an astronomical cold plasma, the high-frequency limit instead leads to the rotation measure law θ=RMλ02​; that wavelength dependence is not a universal law for transparent solids.
