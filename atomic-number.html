Atomic number (Z)

 Home Chemistry Chemical substance Chemical element
OurBigBook.com Words: 371
The atomic number Z is the number of protons in an atomic nucleus, and fixes the chemical element. It is a count, with no units. An atomic nucleus with six protons is always carbon, regardless of how many neutrons accompany them or how many electrons surround it. See the IUPAC Gold Book, atomic number.
Three independent bookkeeping questions become easy once their symbols are kept separate. Let N count neutrons, ne​ count electrons, A be the mass number, and Q be the net electric charge. A proton carries electric charge +e and an electron carries −e, where e is the positive elementary charge; a neutron carries zero net electric charge. Hence
A=Z+N,N=A−Z,Q=(Z−ne​)e,ne​=Z−Q/e.
(13)
The reasoning is just counting: add protons and neutrons for A, and subtract negative electron contributions from positive proton contributions for Q. A neutral chemical atom has Q=0, so ne​=Z. For a positively charged ion, fewer electrons remain. Notation ZA​X gives the mass number at upper left and atomic number at lower left, with X standing for the chemical element; a right superscript such as + specifies the electric charge in units of e. See Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, atomic structure and symbolism.
For neutral carbon-12, 612​C, the atomic number gives six protons, the subtraction N=12−6=6 gives six neutrons, and neutrality gives six electrons. Neutral carbon-13, 613​C, instead has N=13−6=7 neutrons, with the same six protons and six electrons. Thus these two isotopes occupy the same carbon box in the periodic table.
For neutral sodium of mass number 23, 1123​Na, the counts are eleven protons, 23−11=12 neutrons, and eleven electrons. Removing one electron gives the ion 1123​Na+: Q/e=1, so ne​=11−1=10. Both its atomic number and its twelve neutrons stay unchanged. This is ionization, not conversion into the chemical element neon, even though a neutral neon chemical atom also has ten electrons.
This bookkeeping is useful whenever a symbol must be translated into a physical inventory. Mass number answers how many protons plus neutrons there are; electric charge answers the imbalance between protons and electrons; atomic number alone answers which chemical element it is. Keeping those questions distinct prevents an isotope or an ion from being mistaken for a different chemical element.
