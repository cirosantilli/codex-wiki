a OurBigBook.com
A prime ideal is a proper ideal I=R such that ab∈I implies a∈I or b∈I, for every a,b∈R.
Suppose first that I is prime. Since I is proper, 1+I=0+I in the quotient ring. If (a+I)(b+I)=0+I, then ab∈I, so primeness gives a+I=0+I or b+I=0+I. Thus the commutative quotient ring has a nonzero identity and no nonzero zero divisors, hence is an integral domain.
Conversely, if R/I is an integral domain, its identity is nonzero, so I=R. If ab∈I, then (a+I)(b+I)=0 in R/I. Absence of nonzero zero divisors implies a+I=0 or b+I=0, namely a∈I or b∈I. Therefore
I is prime ⟺ R/I is an integral domain.​
(1)
The properness condition matters: taking I=R would produce the zero ring, which is not an integral domain.

b OurBigBook.com
Suppose, for a contradiction, that the monic irreducible polynomials in the polynomial ring k[t] form a finite list p1​,…,pr​. The list is nonempty because t itself is monic and irreducible. Put
F(t)=p1​(t)⋯pr​(t)+1.
(2)
This has positive degree, so it is a nonzero nonunit. Every such polynomial over a field has an irreducible polynomial as a factor: repeatedly factor a reducible polynomial, with the positive degrees strictly decreasing until an irreducible factor is reached. Dividing that factor by its leading coefficient makes it monic; call it q.
By the assumed completeness of the list, q=pj​ for some j. But then pj​ divides both F and p1​⋯pr​, hence their difference 1. A positive-degree polynomial cannot divide 1. This contradiction proves there are infinitely many monic irreducible polynomials over every field. This is the polynomial version of Euclid's theorem; it does not require the field itself to be infinite.

Take a reduced fraction a/b∈R, with b=0. Since gcd(a,b)=1, Bézout's identity supplies integers u,v with ua+vb=1. As the subring R contains all integers,
b1​=uba​+v∈R.​
(3)
Thus a denominator appearing in a reduced fraction in R is automatically a unit of R.
Now let I∩Z=dZ. Because d∈I, the principal ideal dR is contained in I. For the reverse inclusion, take x∈I and write x=a/b in lowest terms. Multiplying by b∈R gives a=bx∈I∩Z=dZ, so a=dc for some integer c. The preceding result gives 1/b∈R, whence
x=ba​=d(bc​)∈dR.
(4)
Therefore
I=dR.​
(5)
This includes d=0: the same argument forces every x∈I to be zero. This is the ideals of subrings of the rational numbers property; every such ring is a principal ideal domain.

In this localization of a ring, a nonzero fraction a/b with odd b is a unit exactly when a is odd: its reciprocal then has odd denominator; if a is even, an inverse would equate an even integer with an odd one after clearing denominators. Consequently every nonzero element has a unique form
x=2ru,r≥0,u∈R×.
(6)
Here r is the exponent of 2 in the numerator of the reduced fraction, its nonnegative 2-adic valuation.
Every nonzero ideal has the form dR by part (c). Write the nonzero integer d=2rv with v odd; since v is a unit, dR=2rR. Hence the nonzero proper ideals are exactly
2rR={a/b∈R:2r∣a},r=1,2,3,….​
(7)
The zero ideal and R=20R complete the full list. These ideals are distinct: 2r does not belong to 2r+1R, since 1/2∈/R.
The reduction map a/b↦amod2 is a well-defined surjective ring homomorphism R→F2​ with kernel of a ring homomorphism 2R; odd denominators reduce to 1. Thus R/2R≅F2​ is a field, so 2R is a prime ideal and 2 is a prime element. Every nonzero nonunit is associated to 2r with r≥1. If r≥2, the factorization 2r=2⋅2r−1 is into two nonunits, so it is not an irreducible element and therefore not a prime element. We conclude
The sole prime element up to units is 2.​
(8)
The failed step in the argument of part (b) is the guarantee that the product plus one is a nonunit and hence has an irreducible factor. Here every prime element is an even fraction, so a product of prime elements plus 1 is an odd fraction, therefore a unit. Already 2+1=3 has no prime divisor in this ring. In k[t], positive degree prevented that failure.

For a nonzero integer-coefficient polynomial F, its polynomial content c(F) is the positive greatest common divisor of all its coefficients; one may set c(0)=0. A primitive polynomial has content one.
For f∈Q[t]∖{0}, choose a positive integer m clearing its denominators, so F=mf∈Z[t]. Put d=c(F)>0. Then
f=md​dF​,
(9)
where f1​=F/d is an integer-coefficient primitive polynomial. This proves existence with α=d/m>0.
For uniqueness, suppose αf1​=βg1​, where both integer-coefficient polynomials are primitive and α,β>0. Write α/β=a/b in lowest terms with a,b positive. Then g1​=(a/b)f1​ has integer coefficients. Coprimality forces b to divide every coefficient of f1​, hence b=1. Now g1​=af1​ has content a, so a=1. Therefore α=β and f1​=g1​.
Define the rational polynomial content by this unique positive multiplier. If f=αf1​ and g=βg1​, Gauss lemma for polynomials says that f1​g1​ is primitive. Thus
fg=(αβ)(f1​g1​),c(fg)=c(f)c(g).​
(10)
The uniqueness argument also shows that the construction is independent of the chosen denominator-clearing integer m.

Write a rational root of a polynomial as γ=a/b in lowest terms with b>0. Substituting into the monic integer-coefficient polynomial and multiplying by bd gives
ad+cd−1​ad−1b+cd−2​ad−2b2+⋯+c0​bd=0.
(11)
Every term other than ad is divisible by b, so b∣ad. But gcd(a,b)=1 implies gcd(ad,b)=1, and hence b=1. Thus
γ∈Z.​
(12)
This is the monic case of the rational root theorem. The monic polynomial assumption is essential because it removes any leading coefficient that could supply prime factors of the denominator.

Use the reduction criterion: a primitive integer-coefficient polynomial whose leading coefficient remains nonzero modulo a prime number p is irreducible over Q if its reduction is irreducible over Fp​. This follows from Gauss lemma for polynomials, since a nontrivial factorization over the integers would reduce to a nontrivial factorization of the same degrees. For a monic polynomial the factors may be taken monic, so neither degree can drop.
Modulo 2 the polynomial is f​=t4+t+1. Its values at 0 and 1 are both 1, so it has no linear polynomial factor. A reducible quartic polynomial without a linear polynomial factor must factor into two irreducible quadratic polynomials. The only monic irreducible quadratic polynomial over F2​ is q=t2+t+1: among monic quadratic polynomials a nonzero constant is necessary, and t2+1 has a root, while q has none.
But t2≡t+1(modq), so t3≡1 and t4≡t. Consequently
f​≡t+t+1=1(modq).
(13)
Thus q does not divide f​, excluding the remaining possible factorization. We obtain
t4+t+1 is irreducible over Q.​
(14)

Eisenstein's criterion states that an integer-coefficient polynomial is irreducible over Q if some prime number divides every nonleading coefficient, does not divide the leading coefficient, and its square does not divide the constant coefficient. Equivalently apply this to the primitive part when phrased for primitive polynomials.
Take p=2. Every nonleading coefficient of t5+4t+2 is divisible by 2 (including the zero coefficients), its leading coefficient is 1, and its constant coefficient 2 is not divisible by 4. Therefore
t5+4t+2 is irreducible over Q.​
(15)

A cubic polynomial over a field is reducible if and only if it has a root in that field: a nontrivial factorization of degree three has a linear polynomial factor, and conversely a root supplies one by the factor theorem.
The rational root theorem, or part (b), shows that a rational root of this monic polynomial must be an integer. Such an integer must divide the constant coefficient 4, so the only candidates are ±1,±2,±4. Direct evaluation gives
ah(a)​19​−1−1​220​−2−12​484​−4−76​​
(16)
None vanishes. Hence there is no rational root, proving
t3+4t+4 is irreducible over Q.​
(17)
The same no-root argument alone would not decide a quartic polynomial, which could factor as two quadratic polynomials; its sufficiency here uses degree three.

a OurBigBook.com
The structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain applies to a Euclidean domain because every Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain. In invariant-factor form, every finitely generated module M has a decomposition
M≅Rr⊕R/(d1​)⊕⋯⊕R/(ds​),d1​∣d2​∣⋯∣ds​,​
(18)
where r≥0, and each di​ is a nonzero nonunit. The rank of a module r is unique and the invariant factors of a finitely generated module di​ are unique up to multiplication by units. The zero module is allowed by taking r=s=0.
Every abelian group is a module over Z, and a finite abelian group is finitely generated. Since Z is a Euclidean domain, the theorem applies. A nonzero free module summand would be infinite, so a finite group has r=0. Choosing positive integer representatives gives the finite abelian classification
A≅Z/d1​Z⊕⋯⊕Z/ds​Z,2≤d1​∣⋯∣ds​.​
(19)
The ordered integers di​ are uniquely determined. Splitting each di​ into prime powers using the Chinese remainder theorem gives the equivalent elementary-divisor form as a direct sum of cyclic prime-power groups.

b OurBigBook.com
Use the Chinese remainder theorem to separate relatively prime factors:
Z/60Z≅Z/4Z⊕Z/3Z⊕Z/5Z,Z/21Z≅Z/3Z⊕Z/7Z.
(20)
These five cyclic prime-power summands are the elementary-divisor form. Leave one Z/3Z summand aside and combine the factors of orders 4,3,5,7, which are pairwise coprime. Their direct sum of modules is cyclic of order 420. The invariant-factor canonical form is therefore
A≅Z/3Z⊕Z/420Z,3∣420.​
(21)
Its group order is 3⋅420=1260=60⋅21, and its exponent of a finite group is 420=lcm(60,21), providing independent checks on the decomposition. Since its exponent is smaller than its order, A itself is not cyclic.

We prove the unique-subgroup criterion for a finite abelian group to be cyclic using the invariant-factor classification from part (a). If A is nontrivial, write
A≅Cd1​​⊕⋯⊕Cds​​,2≤d1​∣⋯∣ds​,
(22)
where Cm​ denotes a cyclic group of order m. If s≥2, choose a prime number p dividing d1​; then p also divides d2​. In the first cyclic factor, the element represented by d1​/p has order of a group element p, and the corresponding element in the second factor has order p as well.
The two elements supported in their separate coordinates generate distinct subgroups of order p: one has zero second coordinate, and the other has zero first coordinate. Both are subgroups of A, and p∣∣A∣. This contradicts the assumed uniqueness for that divisor. Hence s≤1, and
A is cyclic.​
(23)
If A is the trivial group, it is already cyclic, generated by its identity. Thus the argument covers every finite order.

d OurBigBook.com
The multiplicative group of a field is abelian, so its finite subgroup G is a finite abelian group. Let d∣∣G∣, and suppose H is a subgroup of order d. By Lagrange's theorem, the order of each h∈H divides d, hence hd=1. Thus H is contained in the set of roots in k of td−1.
This nonzero polynomial has degree d, so it has at most d roots. Since the d distinct elements of H are already roots, they comprise the whole root set. Any second subgroup K of order d would also consist of d roots of the same polynomial, forcing K=H. Therefore there is at most one subgroup of each order dividing ∣G∣.
Part (c) now applies to give
G is cyclic.​
(24)
The root bound remains valid in positive characteristic, even if the formal derivative of td−1 vanishes: the argument counts distinct roots, so no separability assumption is needed.
