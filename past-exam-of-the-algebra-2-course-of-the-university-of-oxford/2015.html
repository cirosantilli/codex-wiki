i OurBigBook.com
If a=ub with u a unit in a ring, then a lies in the principal ideal generated by b. This gives (a)⊆(b). Since u has a multiplicative inverse, b=u−1a, giving the reverse inclusion. Therefore
a=ub, u∈R×⟹(a)=(b).​
(1)
Thus associate elements always generate the same principal ideal; the associator ring condition asks whether equality of these ideals also forces the generators to differ by a unit.

Suppose R is an integral domain and (a)=(b). If a=0, then (b)=0, so b=0 and a=1⋅b.
Otherwise membership in the equal principal ideals supplies r,s∈R with a=rb and b=sa. Substituting gives a=rsa, or (1−rs)a=0. There are no nonzero zero divisors in an integral domain, and a=0, so rs=1. Hence r is a unit with inverse s, and a=rb makes a and b associate elements. We have proved
Every integral domain is an associator ring.​
(2)

The ideal correspondence theorem identifies the ideals of Z/4Z with the ideals of Z containing 4Z. The possible positive generators divide 4, giving exactly
{0},(2)={0,2},Z/4Z.​
(3)
The units are 1 and 3. The generators of the three displayed ideals are, respectively,
{0},{2},{1,3}.
(4)
Within the last pair, multiplication by the unit 3 exchanges the generators. The other two sets are singletons, whose members are associate elements to themselves by multiplication by 1. Thus equal principal ideals always have associate generators: this is an associator ring.
However, 2=0 and 22=0, so 2 is a nonzero zero divisor. Therefore
Z/4Z is an associator ring but not an integral domain.​
(5)

For two factors, the source's R1​⊕R2​ is the direct product of rings with componentwise operations. Its principal ideals satisfy
((a1​,a2​))=(a1​)×(a2​),
(6)
One inclusion follows by projection to the factors. For the reverse inclusion, multiplication by (1,0) and (0,1) puts (a1​,0) and (0,a2​) in the generated ideal; taking ideal combinations separately produces every pair in (a1​)×(a2​).
Suppose (a1​,a2​) and (b1​,b2​) generate the same principal ideal. Projecting to the factors gives (ai​)=(bi​) for i=1,2. Since each factor is an associator ring, there are units ui​∈Ri​ with ai​=ui​bi​. The pair (u1​,u2​) is a unit of the direct product of rings, with inverse (u1−1​,u2−1​), and
(a1​,a2​)=(u1​,u2​)(b1​,b2​).
(7)
Hence
R1​⊕R2​ is an associator ring.​
(8)
Repeating this argument proves the same assertion for every finite direct product of rings.

c OurBigBook.com
Put A=R/(pk). A principal ideal domain is a unique factorization domain, so every nonzero element of this quotient ring can be written
a=p​es,0≤e<k,p∤s.
(9)
Indeed, remove the largest power of the prime element p from a representative that is not divisible by pk. Since s and pk have greatest common divisor 1 up to a unit, Bézout's identity in the principal ideal domain gives xs+ypk=1. Thus s is a unit in A.
Consequently every nonzero principal ideal in A is generated by one of 1,p​,…,p​k−1. Include the zero ideal by assigning it exponent e=k. These ideals are distinct: their inverse images in R are (1),(p),…,(pk), and equality of two different powers would, by cancellation in the integral domain, make the nonunit p a unit.
Now suppose (a)=(b). If this is the zero ideal, both generators are zero and are associate elements. Otherwise their exponents agree, so write
a=p​es,b=p​et
(10)
with s,t both units. Then
a=(st−1)b,
(11)
and the multiplier is a unit. Thus
R/(pk) is an associator ring for every k≥1.​
(12)
The exponent detects the principal ideal, while its remaining factor is invertible; this is why zero divisors cause no obstruction here.

By the first isomorphism theorem for rings, the given surjection identifies S with R/kerϕ. Since R is a principal ideal domain, write kerϕ=(a).
If a=0, the quotient ring is R, an integral domain, so part (a)(ii) applies. If a is a unit, the quotient ring is the zero ring; its unique element is associate to itself by multiplication by its identity, so it too is an associator ring.
In the remaining case, unique factorization gives
a=εp1k1​​⋯prkr​​,
(13)
where ε is a unit, the pi​ are pairwise nonassociate prime elements, and ki​≥1. The principal ideals (piki​​) are pairwise comaximal: different prime factors have greatest common divisor 1, and Bézout's identity gives a linear combination equal to 1. The Chinese remainder theorem therefore gives a ring isomorphism
R/(a) ≅ ∏i=1r​R/(piki​​).
(14)
Every factor is an associator ring by part (c), and their finite direct product of rings is an associator ring by part (b). A ring isomorphism preserves units and principal ideals, so the property transfers to S. Hence
S is an associator ring.​
(15)

A Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain. The structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain therefore gives, for every finitely generated module M over R,
M≅Rr⊕R/(d1​)⊕⋯⊕R/(ds​),
(16)
where the di​ are nonzero nonunits and d1​∣d2​∣⋯∣ds​. The factors di​ are unique up to multiplication by units; r is uniquely determined.
The rank of a module is this integer r. Equivalently, with K the field of fractions of R,
rankR​M=dimK​(K⊗R​M)=r.​
(17)
The tensor product of modules kills each displayed torsion summand after extending scalars to K, while sending Rr to Kr, explaining the equality.
If M is a torsion-free module, no summand R/(di​) can occur: its nonzero element 1+(di​) is killed by the nonzero scalar di​. Hence s=0 and
M≅Rr,so M is a free module.​
(18)
This also covers M=0, with r=0.

Let x,y∈Q. Choose a positive integer D clearing their denominators and write x=m/D, y=n/D with m,n∈Z. If x and y are not both zero, then
nx−my=0
(19)
is a relation with integer coefficients not both zero. If both vanish, use 1⋅x+0⋅y=0. Thus every pair is linearly dependent over Z.
A submodule M⊆Q is a torsion-free module: if kz=0 for a nonzero integer k and a rational number z, then z=0. If M is also a finitely generated module, part (a), applied to the Euclidean domain Z, makes it a free module. Its rank cannot exceed one, since two different members of a basis of a module would be linearly independent, contradicting the relation just proved. Since M=0, its rank cannot be zero. Therefore
rankZ​M=1,M≅Z.​
(20)
The denominator-and-greatest common divisor construction in part (c) gives an explicit version of this result for a finitely generated subgroup of the rational additive group.

Suppose a finite set of rational numbers q1​,…,qm​ generated Q as a module over Z. A positive common denominator D makes every Dqi​ an integer. For every integer linear combination, therefore,
D∑i​ni​qi​∈Z.
(26)
The entire generated submodule is contained in (1/D)Z. But 1/(2D) is a rational number outside that set: 1/(2D)=k/D would require the noninteger k=1/2. This contradicts generation of all of Q. Consequently
Q is not finitely generated as a Z-module.​
(27)
Each finite set of generators imposes a common denominator; the full rational number system has no such bound.

For a nonzero polynomial f=∑i​ai​ti∈Z[t], its polynomial content is the positive greatest common divisor of its coefficients:
c(f)=gcd(a0​,a1​,…)>0.
(28)
For nonzero g∈Q[t], choose a positive integer n with ng∈Z[t] and define its rational polynomial content by
c(g)=nc(ng)​.​
(29)
To check independence of the denominator, write g=f/n=h/m with f,h∈Z[t] and n,m>0. Then mf=nh, while multiplication of all coefficients by a positive integer gives
mc(f)=c(mf)=c(nh)=nc(h).
(30)
Dividing by mn proves c(f)/n=c(h)/m, as required.
For multiplication, write gi​=fi​/ni​. The integer-coefficient content identity permitted in the source gives
c(g1​g2​)​=n1​n2​c(f1​f2​)​=n1​n2​c(f1​)c(f2​)​=c(g1​)c(g2​).​
(31)
Thus
c(g1​g2​)=c(g1​)c(g2​).​
(32)
We will also use the normalization g/c(g)∈Z[t], with content one: if g=f/n, then g/c(g)=f/c(f). It is a primitive polynomial. For a nonzero rational constant r, the same definition gives c(r)=∣r∣.

The set P contains zero. It is closed under negatives because −f∈P whenever f∈P. If f,g∈P and n,m>0, then
nf​+mg​=nmmf+ng​∈P,
(33)
since P is an ideal of Z[t]. Also, any q∈Q[t] can be written q=h/d with h∈Z[t] and d>0, giving
qnf​=dnhf​∈P.
(34)
This proves that P is an ideal of Q[t].
Clearly P⊆P∩Z[t], by taking denominator one. Conversely, if h∈P∩Z[t], then nh∈P for some positive integer n. The condition P∩Z={0} implies n∈/P. Since P is a prime ideal, nh∈P forces h∈P. Hence
P∩Z[t]=P.​
(35)
In particular, P is nonzero because it contains the nonzero ideal P, and it is proper: otherwise its intersection with Z[t] would contain 1. This is the concrete extension of an ideal and contraction of an ideal calculation for the localization that inverts nonzero integers.

The nonzero ideal P is principal because Q[t] is a principal ideal domain. Choose a nonzero generator h∈Q[t]. Part (a) gives
f=c(h)h​∈Z[t],c(f)=1.
(36)
The nonzero rational scalar c(h) is a unit in Q[t], so this rescaling does not change the generated principal ideal:
P=(f)Q[t]​,c(f)=1.​
(37)
Part (b) now places f in P, since f∈P∩Z[t]. Thus (f)Z[t]​⊆P.
For the reverse inclusion, let g∈P be nonzero. The displayed equality supplies q∈Q[t] with g=fq. Multiplicativity of rational polynomial content gives
c(g)=c(f)c(q)=c(q).
(38)
Since g has integer coefficients, c(g) is a positive integer. The normalization from part (a) gives q/c(q)∈Z[t], so multiplying it by the integer c(q)=c(g) proves q∈Z[t]. Consequently g∈(f)Z[t]​. The zero polynomial belongs as well, proving
P=(f)Z[t]​.​
(39)
This is the divisibility descent in Gauss lemma for polynomials: dividing an integer-coefficient polynomial by a primitive polynomial cannot introduce a denominator into an exact quotient. Here it yields the description of prime ideals of the integer polynomial ring above zero. The generator has positive polynomial degree, since a primitive constant would be a unit and would put 1 in P.

Take I=(2,t)⊆Z[t]. The ring homomorphism
θ:Z[t]⟶F2​,θ(g)=g(0)(mod2)
(40)
is surjective. Its kernel of a ring homomorphism is exactly I: a polynomial maps to zero precisely when its constant coefficient is even, in which case it has the form 2a+th(t) with a∈Z and h∈Z[t]. By the first isomorphism theorem for rings,
Z[t]/I≅F2​.
(41)
This quotient ring is a field, so I is a maximal ideal and in particular a prime ideal.
Suppose I=(h) were a principal ideal. Because 2∈I, the nonzero polynomial h divides the constant 2, forcing its polynomial degree to be zero: h=d for a nonzero integer d. But t∈I also makes d divide the coefficient 1 of t, so d=±1. This would give I=Z[t], contradicting the properness of the kernel of a ring homomorphism kerθ. Therefore
(2,t) is a prime ideal of Z[t] that is not principal.​
(42)
There is no conflict with part (c): I∩Z=2Z={0}. The zero-intersection hypothesis is exactly what excludes this example.
