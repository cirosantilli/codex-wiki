A nonzero contact speed or evaporative flux is singular in the strictly no-slip sharp-contact-line model with mobility proportional to h3. A finite nonzero limiting u would require hxxx​=3u/(Ch2), which is singular for a linearly vanishing height and generally incompatible with a prescribed finite slope at the exact line. Thus a dynamic finite-contact-angle condition needs microscopic regularization, such as slip or a precursor film. In the slow-drying limit a fixed contact angle can instead be used for the leading quasistatic outer profile; it is not an exact regular finite-rate contact-line solution.
At leading quasistatic order hxxx​=0, so the symmetric profile satisfying the imposed outer angle is
h(x,T)=2s(T)θ​(s(T)2−x2),T=ϵt.
(22)
Its cross-sectional volume is V=∫−ss​hdx=2θs2/3. The moving endpoints contribute no boundary-height term, and the end flux vanishes, giving Vt​=−2ϵs. Consequently
dTds​=−2θ3​,s(T)=s0​−2θ3T​,Tdry​=32θs0​​.​
(23)
These are leading slow-time results up to extinction; the approximation need not remain uniform at the final vanishing scale.
To recover the small velocity, differentiate the quasistatic profile using st​=−3ϵ/(2θ):
ht​=−43ϵ​(1+x2/s2),(hu)x​=4ϵ​(−1+3x2/s2).
(24)
Symmetry makes the flux zero at the centre, so hu=−ϵx(s2−x2)/(4s2). Division by the height gives
u=−2sθϵx​.​
(25)
The flow is inward on both sides, suggesting transport of suspended matter toward a central deposit rather than a perimeter ring. Coffee commonly leaves the opposite, ring-like pattern. A possible missing ingredient is contact-line pinning: holding the perimeter fixed makes evaporation require outward replenishment, and stronger evaporation near the edge reinforces that transport. Receding constant-angle and pinned contact lines therefore have qualitatively different deposit mechanisms.
For comparison, the same uniform-evaporation parabolic model with fixed s gives θt​=−3ϵ/s and u=ϵx/(sθ), an outward velocity. This comparison isolates the boundary assumption even before adding edge-enhanced evaporation.
The receding solution is the quasistatic evaporation of a receding parabolic drop.
