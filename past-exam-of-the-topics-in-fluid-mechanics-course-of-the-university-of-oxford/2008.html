The four dimensional equations express mass conservation, momentum balance with pressure, gravity and viscous stress, heat advection and conduction, and a linear thermal-expansion equation of state. For heating from below, suitable plate conditions are u=0 and T=T0​+ΔT at z=0, and u=0, T=T0​ at z=d. No slip and thermal insulation on the sidewall provide one suitable lateral completion; prescribed lateral temperatures would be another specified thermal setup.
Let κ=k/(ρ0​cp​) be thermal diffusivity and ν=μ/ρ0​ kinematic viscosity. Use d, d2/κ, κ/d and ΔT as length, time, velocity and temperature scales. Subtract the static reference pressure pH​ with ∇pH​=−ρ0​gk and scale its remainder by μκ/d2. Then
B=αΔT,Pr=ν/κ,Ra=νκgαΔTd3​.​
(1)
Density becomes ρ0​(1−BT). Substitution and division by the corresponding diffusion/viscous scales give
−BTt​+∇⋅[(1−BT)u]=0,Pr−1(1−BT)(ut​+u⋅∇u)=−∇p+RaTk+Δu,
(2)
(1−BT)(Tt​+u⋅∇T)=ΔT.
(3)
In the Boussinesq small-density-contrast limit retain buoyancy while neglecting the other B factors. At infinite Prandtl number, after any fast initial adjustment, the equations are
∇⋅u=0,0=−∇p+RaTk+Δu,Tt​+u⋅∇T=ΔT.​
(4)
The velocity is instantaneously constrained by a viscous elliptic balance; the temperature still evolves.
Take the divergence of the vector Poisson equation. Derivatives commute, and divergence of a curl is zero, so Δ(∇⋅A)=0. Its boundary value is zero, hence the maximum principle gives ∇⋅A=0 throughout D. The vector identity now gives curlcurlA=curlu, or
curl(curlA−u)=0.
(5)
The layer domain is simply connected because its horizontal section is simply connected. For a curl-free field on such a domain, define χ(x) as its line integral from a fixed base point to x. Contractibility of loops and Stokes theorem make the integral path independent, and differentiation gives its gradient equal to the field. Thus curlA−u=∇χ.
Incompressibility then gives Δχ=0, while the prescribed normal curl gives ∂n​χ=0. Green's identity yields
∫D​∣∇χ∣2dV=∫∂D​χ∂n​χdS−∫D​χΔχdV=0.
(6)
Hence χ is constant on the connected domain and curlA=u​. The extra condition A⋅n=0 belongs to the stated potential boundary problem; the reconstruction argument above uses the divergence and normal-curl conditions. On a multiply connected domain a curl-free field need not be a globally single-valued gradient.
Finally curl(Tk)=(Ty​,−Tx​,0). Taking the curl of the infinite-Prandtl momentum balance and using curlu=−ΔA gives
0=Ra(Ty​i−Tx​j)−Δ2A,∇4A+Ra(Tx​j−Ty​i)=0.​
(7)
The derivation is an instance of velocity reconstruction from a mixed-boundary vector Poisson problem.

Define χ1​ to be one in phase 1 and zero in phase 2, including its dependence on both transverse coordinates even though the averaged fields depend only on x,t. With no interphase mass transfer, its distributional phase-mass equation is ∂t​(ρχ1​)+∇⋅(ρvχ1​)=0. At a pipe cross section Σ of area A, set
α=A1​∫Σ​χ1​dA,ρ1​=Aα1​∫Σ​ρχ1​dA,u1​=Aαρ1​∫Σ​ρvx​χ1​dA​.​
(8)
Thus ρ1​ is an intrinsic phase density and u1​ is the density-weighted mean axial velocity. This weighting is necessary when density varies within the phase; it becomes the ordinary intrinsic mean for an incompressible phase of constant density.
Integrate over the cross section. The transverse divergence contributes no boundary mass flux at the impermeable pipe wall, and the material interface has no mass leakage. The section area is constant, so
∂t​(ρ1​α)+∂x​(ρ1​αu1​)=0.​
(9)
For phase 2 use χ2​=1−χ1​ and fraction 1−α, with the analogous intrinsic density and density-weighted velocity:
∂t​[ρ2​(1−α)]+∂x​[ρ2​(1−α)u2​]=0.​
(10)
The intrinsic quantities are defined where their phase fraction is nonzero. These are the intrinsic phase averages in a two-fluid pipe model.

Freeze a uniform state with 0<α<1, ρi​>0, and seek a finite characteristic speed c. Denote its perturbation amplitudes by a,v1​,v2​,π. The two phase-mass equations and two momentum equations give
(u1​−c)a+αv1​=0,−(u2​−c)a+(1−α)v2​=0,
(11)
ρ1​(u1​−c)v1​+π=0,ρ2​(u2​−c)v2​+π=0.
(12)
Eliminating the amplitudes gives
ρ1​(1−α)(u1​−c)2+ρ2​α(u2​−c)2=0.
(13)
With s2=ρ1​(1−α)/(ρ2​α)>0, factor the quadratic over the complex numbers to obtain
c±​=1±isu2​±isu1​​=1+s2u2​+s2u1​​±i1+s2s(u1​−u2​)​.​
(14)
For nonzero relative velocity the speeds are a nonreal conjugate pair. A Fourier perturbation eik(x−ct) on the growing branch has magnitude e∣k∣∣Imc∣t. Arbitrarily short waves therefore grow arbitrarily quickly, preventing a continuous dependence estimate in the usual finite-order Sobolev norms. This generic slip state is ill-posed as an initial value problem.
The pressure has no independent time-evolution equation; it enforces the incompressible volume constraint, so the request concerns the finite characteristic speeds rather than a freely propagating pressure mode. At u1​=u2​ the two speeds coincide and this particular nonreal-speed argument disappears; further degeneracy analysis is needed there, rather than calling coincident real speeds complex. The issue is complex characteristic speeds of a pressure-coupled two-fluid model.

For constant phase density, let u1​ be the intrinsic mean axial speed and define
D1​=Aαu12​∫Σ​χ1​vx2​dA​.​
(15)
The profile coefficient measures the momentum flux relative to a uniform velocity profile with the same mean. Cauchy-Schwarz gives D1​≥1 where the mean is nonzero, with equality for a uniform phase profile. At zero mean it is safer to retain the second moment itself rather than divide by u12​.
In the stated common-pressure, no-extra-force closure, the averaged conservative momentum equation is
ρ1​[∂t​(αu1​)+∂x​(αD1​u12​)]+αpx​=0.
(16)
For constant D1​, expand its derivatives and use αt​+u1​αx​+α(u1​)x​=0. The terms combine as
∂t​(αu1​)+∂x​(αD1​u12​)=α(u1​)t​+(2D1​−1)αu1​(u1​)x​+(D1​−1)u12​αx​.
(17)
Therefore
ρ1​{α(u1​)t​+(2D1​−1)αu1​(u1​)x​+(D1​−1)u12​αx​}+αpx​=0.​
(18)
This is the phase momentum profile coefficient correction. It accounts for the second moment of velocity, not merely a rescaling of the convective speed. A variable coefficient would also produce ρ1​αu12​(D1​)x​.

Stretch z=δZ and write f=δh, w=δW, with horizontal velocity and time left order one. The longitudinal viscous term is δ−2uZZ​, so set P=δ2p. For Reδ2≪1, the leading bulk equations are PZ​=0, uZZ​=Px​ and ux​+WZ​=0.
The normal-stress condition gives P=−δ3Chxx​ at leading order, and the tangential condition gives uZ​(h)=0. Hence
β=3,C=δ3C,P=−Chxx​.​
(19)
No slip at the bed, u(0)=0, yields u=Px​(Z2/2−hZ). Averaging through the depth gives
u=−3h2​Px​=3C​h2hxxx​.​
(20)
The kinematic condition becomes ht​+u(h)hx​−W(h)=−ϵ/δ at leading order. Integration of continuity from the impermeable bed then gives
ht​+(hu)x​=−ϵ,ϵ=ϵ/δ.​
(21)
A nonzero contact speed or evaporative flux is singular in the strictly no-slip sharp-contact-line model with mobility proportional to h3. A finite nonzero limiting u would require hxxx​=3u/(Ch2), which is singular for a linearly vanishing height and generally incompatible with a prescribed finite slope at the exact line. Thus a dynamic finite-contact-angle condition needs microscopic regularization, such as slip or a precursor film. In the slow-drying limit a fixed contact angle can instead be used for the leading quasistatic outer profile; it is not an exact regular finite-rate contact-line solution.
At leading quasistatic order hxxx​=0, so the symmetric profile satisfying the imposed outer angle is
h(x,T)=2s(T)θ​(s(T)2−x2),T=ϵt.
(22)
Its cross-sectional volume is V=∫−ss​hdx=2θs2/3. The moving endpoints contribute no boundary-height term, and the end flux vanishes, giving Vt​=−2ϵs. Consequently
dTds​=−2θ3​,s(T)=s0​−2θ3T​,Tdry​=32θs0​​.​
(23)
These are leading slow-time results up to extinction; the approximation need not remain uniform at the final vanishing scale.
To recover the small velocity, differentiate the quasistatic profile using st​=−3ϵ/(2θ):
ht​=−43ϵ​(1+x2/s2),(hu)x​=4ϵ​(−1+3x2/s2).
(24)
Symmetry makes the flux zero at the centre, so hu=−ϵx(s2−x2)/(4s2). Division by the height gives
u=−2sθϵx​.​
(25)
The flow is inward on both sides, suggesting transport of suspended matter toward a central deposit rather than a perimeter ring. Coffee commonly leaves the opposite, ring-like pattern. A possible missing ingredient is contact-line pinning: holding the perimeter fixed makes evaporation require outward replenishment, and stronger evaporation near the edge reinforces that transport. Receding constant-angle and pinned contact lines therefore have qualitatively different deposit mechanisms.
For comparison, the same uniform-evaporation parabolic model with fixed s gives θt​=−3ϵ/s and u=ϵx/(sθ), an outward velocity. This comparison isolates the boundary assumption even before adding edge-enhanced evaporation.
The receding solution is the quasistatic evaporation of a receding parabolic drop.
