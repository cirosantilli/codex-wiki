Greedy coloring

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Foundations of mathematics Graph theory Graph coloring
OurBigBook.com Words: 488 Articles: 3
For a finite simple graph, choose an order of its vertices and process them one at a time. Give each vertex the smallest positive colour label not already used by its coloured neighbours, and keep all earlier assignments. This is greedy coloring: each step makes a locally available choice. The resulting graph coloring is proper because, for each edge, its later endpoint avoids the colour of its earlier endpoint.[ref]
If the maximum degree of a graph is Δ(G), a vertex has at most Δ(G) previously coloured neighbours, forbidding at most that many distinct colours. At least one label among 1,…,Δ(G)+1 remains available. Consequently every order gives
χ(G)≤number of colours used by greedy colouring≤Δ(G)+1.
(2)
This is an upper bound, not a promise that greedy coloring attains the chromatic number.[ref]
Consider the path graph P4​ with edges 12,23,34. In the order 1,2,3,4, greedy coloring assigns the colours 1,2,1,2 respectively. In the order 1,4,2,3, it assigns colours 1,1,2,3 in processing order: vertices 1,4 both get colour 1, then vertex 2 gets colour 2, and finally vertex 3 sees colours 2 and 1 at its two neighbours and must take colour 3. Written in vertex order 1,2,3,4, this second colouring is 1,2,3,1.
The path graph has chromatic number two: the first assignment achieves two colours, and any edge rules out one. Its maximum degree of a graph is two, so three colours in the second assignment still satisfy the bound. The extra colour comes entirely from the processing order. The crown graph gives a larger family where a poor order can make greedy coloring use arbitrarily many colours although the chromatic number remains two.
