Coulomb potential energy (U(r)=kq1​q2​/r)

The Coulomb potential energy is the interaction potential energy of two point charges in vacuum. With separation r>0, electric charges q1​,q2​, k=1/(4πε0​) and zero potential energy at infinite separation,
U(r)=rkq1​q2​​.​
(178)
This is mutual interaction energy; it does not include the divergent self-energies of ideal point charges. Unlike electric potential, which has units of volts, U has SI units of joules. With charge 1 fixed and positive radial direction away from it, the conservative force in Coulomb's law gives
U(r)−U(∞)=−∫∞r​s2kq1​q2​​ds=rkq1​q2​​,Fr​=−drdU​=r2kq1​q2​​.
(179)
Thus the 1/r potential energy and the inverse-square law for force are two descriptions of the same interaction. [ref].
For q1​=+2nC, q2​=−3nC and r=0.20m, using the stipulated approximation k=8.99×109Nm2/C2,
U=0.20(8.99×109)(2×10−9)(−3×10−9)​J=−2.697×10−7J.
(180)
At r=0.40m, U=−1.3485×10−7J. Separating the pair from 0.20 to 0.40m therefore requires external work ΔU=+1.3485×10−7J if the initial and final kinetic energies agree; the electric force does −1.3485×10−7J of work. Separating them all the way to infinity requires +2.697×10−7J. The negative initial potential energy means that the pair lies below the chosen separated reference; it does not mean a negative kinetic energy.
Like-signed electric charges have positive U, which decreases as they separate, so their electric force is repulsive. Opposite-signed electric charges have negative U, which decreases as they approach, so their electric force is attractive. This attractive interaction supplies the Coulomb potential used in the hydrogen atom; determining its stationary quantum states also requires quantum mechanics. [ref]. Dividing U by a nonzero test electric charge q2​ gives the source's electric potential Φ1​=kq1​/r. More generally, the electrostatic potential energy of point charges sums each unordered charge pair once. This energy description is useful because work between two separations follows from endpoint values, without integrating a complicated trajectory.
