Gaussian Poisson summation identity (S(a)=a−1/2S(1/a),a>0)

OurBigBook.com Words: 460 Articles: 1
Take the Gaussian function fa​(x)=e−πax2 with a>0. Every derivative is a polynomial times the same Gaussian function, so fa​ is a Schwartz function. The Fourier transform of a Gaussian gives, in the cycles convention,
f​a​(ξ)=a−1/2e−πξ2/a.
(12)
Thus the Poisson summation formula gives the exact identity
S(a):=∑n∈Z​e−πan2=a​1​∑k∈Z​e−πk2/a=a​1​S(1/a),a>0.
(13)
The Gaussian Fourier transform is given in scaled form in Exercise 41 of Terence Tao's 245C, Notes 2: The Fourier transform.[ref] The identity is also the imaginary-axis transformation of the Jacobi theta function. At a=1 the Gaussian function is its own Fourier transform; at reciprocal values of a its spatial and spectral widths exchange.
For a short derivation of the Gaussian Fourier transform, write H(ξ)=f​a​(ξ). Differentiation under the integral and integration by parts, using fa′​(x)=−2πaxfa​(x), give
H′(ξ)=−2πi∫R​xfa​(x)e−2πiξxdx=−a2πξ​H(ξ).
(14)
The Gaussian integral gives H(0)=a−1/2, so this ordinary differential equation yields the claimed formula. The decay of the Gaussian function justifies these steps and removes the boundary term.[ref]
For a=0.04, the physical Gaussian function has width parameter 1/a​=5, while its Fourier transform has width parameter a​=0.2. Many physical samples matter, but the dual zero sample already gives
S(0.04)=5(1+2e−25π+2e−100π+⋯),0<S(0.04)−5<7.774×10−34.
(15)
For a=25, use the physical side instead:
S(25)=1+2e−25π+2e−100π+⋯,0<S(25)−1<1.555×10−34.
(16)
These are bounds on the full positive tails, obtained below, rather than estimates based only on the first omitted term. Ordinary floating-point arithmetic rounds these sums to 5 and 1; the exact real numbers are slightly larger. A Gaussian sum truncation bound lets us choose the cheaper side and certify its truncation error before evaluating it.
