Cartesian basis (E=(e1​,…,en​))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra
OurBigBook.com Words: 768 Articles: 3
A Cartesian basis is an ordered basis of unit vectors along the mutually perpendicular axes of a Cartesian coordinate system. It is an orthonormal basis of a real Euclidean space: ei​⋅ej​=δij​, with δij​ the Kronecker delta. In the plane, the familiar standard basis is e1​=(1,0)T, e2​=(0,1)T; the vector p=(2,1)T is 2e1​+e2​. A rotated pair of perpendicular unit vectors is also a Cartesian basis, even though its basis vectors have different standard basis entries.
The advantage is that coordinates can be measured separately by dot products, and the squared norm is the sum of their squares:
p=∑i​(p⋅ei​)ei​,[p]E​=(p⋅e1​,…,p⋅en​)T,∥p∥2=∑i​(p⋅ei​)2.
(1)
These are the real Euclidean space instances of the orthonormal basis expansion.[ref]
An orthogonal basis only requires perpendicular basis vectors; their lengths need not be one. For example, (2,0)T,(0,3)T are orthogonal, but p=(2,1)T has coordinates (1,1/3)T, while its dot products with them are 4,3. For an orthogonal basis (ai​), the coordinate along ai​ is (p⋅ai​)/(ai​⋅ai​). Dividing each nonzero basis vector by its norm makes it a unit vector without changing its direction. Unit length alone does not imply orthogonality: (1,0)T and (1,1)T/2​ are both unit vectors but have dot product 1/2​.
The examples below show why these distinctions matter: rotating a Cartesian basis changes the coordinate vector while keeping lengths easy to compute; using nonperpendicular basis vectors still gives unique coordinates, but requires the dual basis or a matrix inverse to extract them.
