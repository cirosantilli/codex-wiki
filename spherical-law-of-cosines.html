Spherical law of cosines (cosa=cosbcosc+sinbsinccosA)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Geometry and topology Spherical geometry Spherical trigonometry
OurBigBook.com Words: 402
For a nondegenerate convex minor spherical triangle, the spherical law of cosines relates two angular sides b,c, their included interior angle A, and the opposite angular side a:
cosa=cosbcosc+sinbsinccosA.
(8)
Relabelling the vertices gives the corresponding formulas for b,B and c,C. As with the spherical law of sines, physical side lengths must first be divided by the sphere's radius. The spherical law of cosines determines a∈(0,π) uniquely from b,c,A when the data describe such a spherical triangle. It also recovers each interior angle from three sides, for example
cosA=sinbsinccosa−cosbcosc​.
(9)
Using the cosine and the range 0<A<π selects a unique angle, avoiding the supplementary-angle ambiguity of an inverse sine calculation.
To derive the spherical law of cosines, let u,v,w be the vertex unit vectors at A,B,C, so u⋅v=cosc, u⋅w=cosb and v⋅w=cosa. The outgoing unit tangent vectors at u are tB​=(v−coscu)/sinc and tC​=(w−cosbu)/sinb. Their dot product is both cosA and (cosa−cosbcosc)/(sinbsinc), proving the identity. This gives a geometric explanation: spherical trigonometry compares angles in the tangent plane using vectors in the surrounding Euclidean space.
For a worked example on the unit sphere, take the vertices
A=(0,0,1),B=(3​/2,0,1/2),C=(0,3​/2,1/2).
(10)
At A, the outgoing unit tangent vectors are (1,0,0) and (0,1,0), so the interior angle is a right angle. The vertex dot products give b=c=π/3=60∘. The spherical law of cosines then reduces to the right-angle identity cosa=cosbcosc, giving
cosa=41​,a=arccos(1/4)≈1.318116 radians=75.522488∘.
(11)
To find B, use the relabelled spherical law of cosines:
cosB=sinasinccosb−cosacosc​=(15​/4)(3​/2)1/2−1/8​=5​1​,B=C≈63.434949∘.
(12)
The physical side lengths on a sphere of radius R are ℓa​≈1.318116R and ℓb​=ℓc​=πR/3≈1.047198R. For example, on a toy sphere with R=10 metres they are approximately 13.18116, 10.47198 and 10.47198 metres; the interior angles stay the same. Applying the planar Pythagorean theorem directly to the angular sides would instead give 602+602​≈84.852814∘. That flat right triangle calculation does not give the great-circle distance between B and C.
The familiar law of cosines reappears as a local approximation. With a,b,c all small in radians, substitute the small-angle approximations sinx≈x and cosx≈1−x2/2 into the spherical law of cosines and keep terms through second order:
1−2a2​≈1−2b2+c2​+bccosA,a2≈b2+c2−2bccosA.
(13)
Multiplication by R2 gives the planar law of cosines for the physical side lengths. For a fixed small surface triangle on a progressively larger sphere, the angular sides shrink and this approximation improves. Thus the curved and flat formulas fit together: the Pythagorean theorem is recovered locally when A is a right angle.
