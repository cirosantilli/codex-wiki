Faraday rotation on a round trip (R(θ)R(θ)=R(2θ))

 ... Branches of physics Electromagnetism Electromagnetic wave Plane electromagnetic wave Polarization of an electromagnetic wave Faraday effect
OurBigBook.com Words: 268
Faraday rotation exhibits optical nonreciprocity: with the bias magnetic field held fixed, a retracing beam gains another rotation in the same laboratory sense. Natural optical activity in a reciprocal medium instead unwinds on retracing the path.[ref]
To reconcile this with the signed B∥​ formula, retain the laboratory x,y,z axes for comparing physical lines. For the backward ray choose a right-handed ray basis x′=x, y′=−y, z′=−z. Its magnetic flux density component is B∥′​=−Bz​, so its local rotation increment is −θ. But positive y′ is negative laboratory y: the backward increment is therefore +θ in the laboratory orientation plot. Reversing the ray reverses both the longitudinal component and the basis used for its signed angle. Reversing only one creates the false prediction of cancellation.
In fixed laboratory transverse components, define the rotation matrix
R(θ)=(cosθsinθ​−sinθcosθ​).
(27)
For an ideal mirror at normal incidence that contributes the same complex amplitude factor r to both transverse components, the round-trip map is rR(θ)R(θ)=rR(2θ). The common mirror factor does not change the oscillation line. A reciprocal natural rotator instead gives rR(−α)R(α)=rI. This comparison assumes a retraced straight path and no additional polarization-changing elements.
For example, a chosen single-pass 30∘ Faraday rotation produces a returned line at 60∘ to the original laboratory x axis. A chosen reciprocal 30∘ natural rotator returns it to 0∘. The same addition makes an ideal optical isolator possible: polarizers at 0∘ and 45∘ surround a 45∘ Faraday rotator. Forward light follows 0∘→45∘ and passes. Returning light selected at 45∘ reaches 90∘ at the entrance polarizer and is rejected by Malus's law. Real transmission loss and incomplete extinction limit the isolation.
