Sliding and cavities. Regelation is melting under increased pressure and refreezing when the pressure is released. For ice near its melting point, the Clapeyron equation gives a lower melting temperature at greater pressure. Against an obstacle, ice melts on the high-pressure upstream face, the water passes around it, and it refreezes on the lower-pressure downstream face. Transport of the released latent heat back towards the melting face sustains the process. Together with viscous deformation around obstacles, this permits basal sliding; regelation is especially effective for small obstacles, for which heat conduction distances are short.
Subglacial cavitation occurs where the contact pressure predicted behind an obstacle falls below the water pressure: the ice separates from the bed and a water-filled cavity opens. The effective pressure in subglacial drainage is
N=pi​−pw​,​
(10)
the ice overburden pressure minus water pressure. Smaller N facilitates larger cavities and reduces the resisting ice–bed contact. The exponent n comes from Glen's flow law, with strain rate proportional to the nth power of deviatoric stress, commonly n≃3. Its homogeneity makes Nn the natural velocity scale, yielding τ/N=f(u/Nn) after including the fixed bed and ice-rheology scales.
Without cavities, material creep gives a drag law with f(x)∝x1/n. Cavities lower the resistance relative to that extrapolation. On a bounded-slope bed the drag is bounded in proportion to N; depending on bed geometry, the curve may level off or pass through a maximum and weaken as contact diminishes. The illustrated weakening curve is a qualitative possibility, not a universal formula for all beds.
Channels versus distributed drainage. In Röthlisberger channel drainage, frictional dissipation of flowing water supplies heat to melt the channel walls, while material creep of ice closes them under effective pressure. An area balance has the schematic form
S˙ch​=ρi​Lf​QΨ​−ANnSch​,
(11)
where Ψ is hydraulic-potential drop per length, Lf​ is latent heat per mass, and the positive constant A incorporates ice material creep and geometry. Water-flow resistance relates the flux to channel size and potential gradient. Balancing melting against closure produces the stated increasing relation NR​(Q).
A channel gaining flux can maintain lower water pressure, since larger N means smaller pw​. It draws still more water from neighboring routes, melts wider, and causes competing small conduits to lose flux and close. This positive feedback concentrates flow into an arterial network.
A linked cavity drainage system consists of water-filled lee-side cavities connected by narrow passages. Sliding over bed obstacles opens cavities; ice material creep tends to close them. The given decreasing relation NK​(Q) means that carrying more water requires higher water pressure. A route gaining flux then has a higher pressure and diverts water towards neighboring routes. This negative redistribution feedback supports many connected routes and a distributed system.
