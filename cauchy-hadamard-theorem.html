Cauchy-Hadamard theorem (R−1=limsupn→∞​∣an​∣1/n)

 ... Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Sequence and series Power series Radius of convergence
OurBigBook.com Words: 217
For the power series ∑n≥0​an​(z−c)n, the Cauchy-Hadamard theorem gives
R1​=limsupn→∞​∣an​∣1/n,
(69)
with R=∞ when the limit superior is zero, and R=0 when it is infinite. Away from the centre, the root test explains the formula: the limit superior of the term roots at displacement z−c is ∣z−c∣/R, interpreted as zero for R=∞ and infinite for R=0. Values below one give absolute convergence; values above one imply that the terms fail to tend to zero. A value of one leaves the boundary question open. At the centre the series converges regardless of R.
For a2n​=1 and a2n+1​=0, the coefficient roots alternate between one and zero, so their ordinary limit does not exist. Their limit superior is nevertheless one, giving R=1 for ∑n≥0​z2n=1/(1−z2) inside the unit disk. A ratio of consecutive coefficients would be undefined at the zero coefficients; the root-limsup formula handles the gap directly.
In the first part of his 1892 study of functions given by Taylor series, Jacques Hadamard introduced an upper-limit formulation for sequences and used coefficient roots to determine the radius of convergence. Sections 4–6 separate finite positive, zero, and infinite root-growth cases; the later parts study singularities and continuation. See Hadamard, Essai sur l'étude des fonctions données par leur développement de Taylor, Journal de mathématiques pures et appliquées, fourth series 8 (1892), pp. 107–109. This original text explains the coefficient-growth question without implying that the study of power series began there.
