Solution
Words: 95
i OurBigBook.com
A linear operator T:X→Y is a compact operator if the image of the closed unit ball has compact closure in Y. Equivalently, every bounded sequence (xn​) in X has a subsequence whose images converge in the norm of Y. This implies boundedness of T.
The Schauder theorem for compact operators says that for a bounded linear operator between Banach spaces,
T:X→Y compact⟺T∗:Y∗→X∗ compact,​
(1)
where the dual map of bounded linear operator is T∗f=f∘T. The forward implication also holds for normed spaces; completeness of the target gives the usual converse. The compactness definition requires the image subsequence's limit to belong to Y, so that completeness qualification matters when stating the theorem beyond Banach spaces.

Let An​ be finite-rank bounded operators with ∥T−An​∥→0 in operator norm. Fix ϵ>0 and choose n with ∥T−An​∥<ϵ/3. The set An​(BX​) is bounded in a finite-dimensional subspace, hence has a finite ϵ/3-net y1​,…,ym​.
For each x∈BX​, some j satisfies
∥Tx−yj​∥≤∥(T−An​)x∥+∥An​x−yj​∥<2ϵ/3.
(2)
Thus T(BX​) is totally bounded. Its closure is totally bounded and, as a closed subset of the Banach space Y, complete. A complete totally bounded metric space is compact. Consequently T is compact.
The operator-norm limit is bounded, since ∥T∥≤∥An​∥+∥T−An​∥ for any sufficiently good approximation. This proves directly that an approximable operator into a Banach space is compact; no assertion that every compact operator on an arbitrary Banach space is approximable is needed.

Let QN​ be truncation to the first N coordinates in ℓ1. For x∈ℓ1, boundedness of T and absolute convergence give
(T−TQN​)x=∑n>N​xn​Ten​,∥(T−TQN​)x∥1​≤supn>N​∥Ten​∥1​∥x∥1​.
(3)
Applying the difference to each en​ with n>N gives the reverse bound on the operator norm. Hence
∥T−TQN​∥=n>Nsup​∥Ten​∥1​.​
(4)
The stated condition makes this tend to zero. Each TQN​ has finite rank, so part (a)(ii) proves compactness. Thus the condition is sufficient on ℓ1.
For failure of necessity, set Tx=(∑n≥1​xn​)e1​. The sum defines a bounded linear functional on ℓ1, so T is a bounded rank-one operator and therefore compact. But Ten​=e1​ for every n, so ∥Ten​∥1​=1. Thus compactness need not make the basis images tend to zero on ℓ1.

Let 1<p<∞ and q=p/(p−1). The dual of ℓp is ℓq. For every a∈ℓq, ⟨a,en​⟩=an​→0, so en​⇀0 weakly. A compact operator sends a weakly convergent sequence to a norm-convergent sequence with the corresponding limit. Indeed, any failure of ∥Ten​∥→0 would give a subsequence bounded away from zero, with a further norm-convergent subsequence by compactness; weak continuity of T forces that norm limit to be zero, a contradiction. The condition is therefore necessary.
For failure of sufficiency, partition the positive integers into consecutive blocks
Bk​={k(k−1)/2+1,…,k(k+1)/2},∣Bk​∣=k,
(5)
and define the block averaging compactness counterexample
(Tx)k​=k−1/qj∈Bk​∑​xj​.​
(6)
By Hölder's inequality, ∣(Tx)k​∣p≤∑j∈Bk​​∣xj​∣p. Summing over disjoint blocks shows ∥Tx∥p​≤∥x∥p​, so T is bounded. If j∈Bk​, then Tej​=k−1/qek​; as j→∞, also k→∞, so ∥Tej​∥p​→0.
However, the vectors x(k)=k−1/p1Bk​​ have norm one and satisfy Tx(k)=ek​. Distinct output vectors have distance 21/p, so this bounded sequence of inputs has no norm-convergent image subsequence. Thus T is not compact.
To connect with the hint, define (Uy)j​=k−1/qyk​ for j∈Bk​. Then U:ℓq→ℓq is an isometry, and the dual pairing calculation gives T=U∗. The vectors Uek​ have disjoint supports and norm one. The direct estimates above establish the counterexample without relying only on the hint.

The weak topology σ(X,X∗) is the coarsest topology on X making every continuous linear functional f∈X∗ continuous. A basic neighborhood of x0​ is specified by finitely many inequalities ∣fj​(x−x0​)∣<ϵ.
The weak-star topology σ(X∗,X) is the coarsest topology on X∗ making every evaluation f↦f(x), for x∈X, continuous. Its basic neighborhoods use finitely many such evaluations. Nets converge in these topologies exactly when all the corresponding scalar evaluations converge.
Let JX​:X→X∗∗ be the canonical isometric embedding, JX​x(f)=f(x). Goldstine theorem states
JX​(BX​)​σ(X∗∗,X∗)=BX∗∗​.​
(7)
Here the balls are closed unit balls. Thus any bidual unit vector can be approximated in finitely many dual evaluations by unit vectors from X; this does not claim norm density or sequential density in complete generality.

ii OurBigBook.com
The weak topology is coarser than the norm topology, so norm convergence implies weak convergence, and
C∥⋅∥⊆Cweak.
(8)
For the reverse inclusion, let x∈/K=C∥⋅∥. The set K is closed and convex. The Hahn-Banach separation theorem supplies f∈X∗ and a∈R such that f(x)>a≥supy∈K​f(y). The set {z:f(z)>a} is weakly open, contains x and misses C. Hence x is outside the weak closure as well. If C is empty the equality is immediate. Therefore
Cweak=C∥⋅∥for every convex C.​
(9)
The separating functional explains the role of convexity: an arbitrary norm-closed subset need not be weakly closed.

A reflexive Banach space is one for which JX​:X→X∗∗ is onto. If X is reflexive, every θ∈X∗∗ has the form θ(f)=f(x) for some x∈X. Thus the functionals defining σ(X∗,X∗∗) and σ(X∗,X) are precisely the same, proving equality of the topologies.
Conversely, assume those topologies coincide. For any θ∈X∗∗, the linear functional f↦θ(f) on X∗ is then weak-star continuous. Its continuity at zero gives finitely many x1​,…,xm​∈X and ϵ>0 such that
∣f(xj​)∣<ϵ for all j⟹∣θ(f)∣<1.
(10)
If all these evaluations are zero, arbitrary scalar multiples of f still satisfy the inequalities, forcing θ(f)=0. Hence θ factors through the finite-dimensional evaluation map f↦(f(x1​),…,f(xm​)). A linear functional on its image extends to Rm, so for suitable scalars aj​,
θ(f)=∑j=1m​aj​f(xj​)=f(∑j=1m​aj​xj​).
(11)
Thus θ lies in JX​(X). Since it was arbitrary, X is reflexive. We have proved
X reflexive⟺σ(X∗,X)=σ(X∗,X∗∗).​
(12)

The domain carries σ(Z∗∗,Z∗), and the target carries σ(X∗,X). For each x∈X⊆Z∗,
(Rθ)(x)=θ(x),
(13)
which is one of the defining weak-star continuous evaluations on Z∗∗. Thus R is weak-star continuous.
Restriction cannot increase a functional's norm, so R(BZ∗∗​)⊆BX∗​. Conversely, any f∈BX∗​ is a bounded linear functional on the subspace X of Z∗. The Hahn-Banach theorem extends it to a functional θ∈(Z∗)∗=Z∗∗ with the same norm. Then Rθ=f and ∥θ∥≤1. Consequently
R(BZ∗∗​)=BX∗​.​
(14)
This proves exact surjectivity on closed balls, not just density of the image.

v OurBigBook.com
Put T=R∘JZ​:Z→X∗. Then (Tz)(x)=x(z) and ∥Tz∥≤∥z∥. We must prove T onto.
Take f∈BX∗​. Part (iv) supplies θ∈BZ∗∗​ with Rθ=f. Goldstine's theorem gives a net zα​∈BZ​ with JZ​zα​→θ weak-star. By continuity of R, Tzα​→f in σ(X∗,X). Reflexivity of X makes this the weak topology on X∗ by part (iii). Thus BX∗​ is contained in the weak closure of T(BZ​).
The reverse containment holds because T(BZ​)⊆BX∗​, and the latter ball is weakly closed: it is the intersection of the closed evaluation constraints ∣g(x)∣≤1 for x∈BX​. The set T(BZ​) is convex, so part (ii), now on the Banach space X∗, gives
T(BZ​)​∥⋅∥=BX∗​.​
(15)
Density of the ball gives exact surjectivity by a convergent correction argument. For nonzero f∈X∗, choose z1​ with ∥z1​∥≤∥f∥ and ∥f−Tz1​∥≤∥f∥/2. Inductively approximate the current residual rn−1​ by Tzn​ with ∥zn​∥≤∥rn−1​∥ and ∥rn−1​−Tzn​∥≤∥rn−1​∥/2. If a residual is zero, stop.
The residual norms are bounded by 2−n∥f∥, while ∑n​∥zn​∥≤2∥f∥. Completeness of Z gives z=∑n​zn​∈Z, and continuity of T gives Tz=f. For f=0 use z=0. Therefore
∀f∈X∗ ∃z∈Z:x(z)=f(x) for every x∈X.​
(16)
This is evaluation onto the dual of a reflexive dual subspace. The correction argument is needed: a dense image alone would not imply surjectivity.

A biorthogonal system is a family (xi​,fi​)i∈I​ with xi​∈X, fi​∈X∗, satisfying
fi​(xj​)=δij​(i,j∈I).​
(17)
Thus each continuous coordinate functional takes value one on its own vector and zero on all others. The vectors need not be orthogonal in any inner product, and totality of their linear span is an additional condition.

Solution

Words: 138
ii OurBigBook.com
Fix i and let Mi​=span​{xj​:j=i}. The hypothesis gives Mi​=X. Moreover xi​∈/Mi​, since otherwise the closed span of all the vectors would be contained in Mi​. Hence di​=dist(xi​,Mi​)>0.
On Mi​+Rxi​, define ϕi​(m+txi​)=t. It is well-defined because xi​∈/Mi​. For t=0,
∥m+txi​∥=∣t∣∥xi​+m/t∥≥∣t∣di​,
(18)
so ∣ϕi​(m+txi​)∣≤∥m+txi​∥/di​; the inequality also holds for t=0. The Hahn-Banach theorem extends ϕi​ without increasing its norm to xi∗​∈X∗. This gives xi∗​(xj​)=δij​.
If two continuous functionals have these values, their difference vanishes on the linear span of every xj​, which is dense in X. Continuity then makes the difference zero everywhere. Thus each functional is unique, proving the required unique biorthogonal system. In fact its norm is ∥xi∗​∥=1/di​, by taking vectors in Mi​ approaching the distance infimum. This is the coordinate-functional construction for a minimal total family in a normed space.

Define the finite-rank operator
Pn​x=i=1∑n​xi∗​(x)xi​.​
(19)
It is linear and bounded, with ∥Pn​∥≤∑i=1n​∥xi∗​∥∥xi​∥. Biorthogonality gives exactly the two required values on xj​. It also gives
Pn2​x=∑i=1n​∑j=1n​xj∗​(x)xi∗​(xj​)xi​=Pn​x.
(20)
Thus Pn​ is a bounded projection onto span{x1​,…,xn​}, with kernel ⋂i=1n​kerxi∗​. Its action on the dense span determines it uniquely among bounded linear operators. These are the partial-sum projections; no uniform bound in n has yet been assumed.

iv OurBigBook.com
Assume first that ∥Pn​∥≤M for all n. Given x∈X and ϵ>0, choose a finite linear combination y of the xi​ with ∥x−y∥<ϵ/(M+1). For all sufficiently large n, Pn​y=y. Therefore
∥Pn​x−x∥≤∥Pn​(x−y)∥+∥y−x∥≤(M+1)∥x−y∥<ϵ.
(21)
Thus Pn​x→x, and the formula in part (iii) yields the norm-convergent expansion
x=i=1∑∞​xi∗​(x)xi​.​
(22)
This proves the stated existence of coefficients.
Conversely, suppose every x has some norm-convergent expansion x=∑i​αi​xi​. Applying the continuous functional xj∗​ to its partial sums and passing to the limit gives αj​=xj∗​(x). Hence those partial sums are precisely Pn​x, so Pn​x→x for every x. In particular supn​∥Pn​x∥<∞ for each fixed x. The Uniform boundedness principle, using completeness of X, now gives
nsup​∥Pn​∥<∞.​
(23)
This proves the reverse implication. The coefficients are automatically unique, so the equivalence is the uniform partial-sum criterion for a Schauder basis for the given minimal total sequence. Its projection bound is the basis constant.
