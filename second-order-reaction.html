Second-order reaction

 ... Chemistry Chemical reaction Chemical kinetics Reaction rate Rate law Order of reaction
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 1
A second-order reaction has overall order of reaction two: a rate law may depend on [A]2, or on [A][B] with partial orders of reaction one and one. Doubling all relevant reactant concentrations multiplies the reaction rate by four at fixed conditions. This experimentally determined dependence does not establish molecularity or a unique reaction mechanism. For an elementary reaction obeying the law of mass action, molecularity and overall order of reaction agree; a net chemical equation alone cannot establish that the conversion is elementary.[ref]
For the single-concentration model, explicitly choose the disappearance rate convention
−dtd[A]​=kobs​[A]2,[A](0)=[A]0​>0,kobs​>0.
(140)
Here kobs​ is the coefficient for loss of A, held fixed at the specified temperature and other conditions. Its subscript labels the chosen observed disappearance rate; it does not imply a pseudo-first-order reaction. Assume a uniformly mixed closed system, fixed volume, and no appreciable reverse or competing chemical reactions. The integrated rate law is
[A](t)1​=[A]0​1​+kobs​t​,[A](t)=1+kobs​[A]0​t[A]0​​.
(141)
The reciprocal-concentration plot is a straight line with slope kobs​ and intercept 1/[A]0​. A brief derivation uses the chain rule: d(1/[A])/dt=−[A]−2d[A]/dt=kobs​, then applies the initial condition. This is a hyperbolic decay, rather than the exponential decay of a first-order reaction.[ref]
As an original prescribed example, choose [A]0​=0.020molL−1 and kobs​=0.50Lmol−1s−1. At t=100s, the reciprocal-concentration calculation is 50+50=100Lmol−1, giving [A]=0.010molL−1. The initial disappearance rate is 0.50(0.020)2=2.0×10−4molL−1s−1; after 100s it is 5.0×10−5molL−1s−1. Halving the concentration quarters the disappearance rate.
Dimensional analysis checks every formula: kobs​[A]2 has units molL−1s−1, kobs​t has units Lmol−1, and kobs​[A]0​t is a dimensionless quantity. Changing concentration units changes the numerical rate constant: the same example is [A]0​=20molm−3, kobs​=5.0×10−4m3mol−1s−1, giving [A](100s)=10molm−3.
For an elementary reaction 2A⟶P, instead define the stoichiometrically normalized reaction rate r=k[A]2. Two A entities disappear per P formed, so
r=−21​dtd[A]​=dtd[P]​=k[A]2,−dtd[A]​=2k[A]2,kobs​=2k.
(142)
Consequently the reciprocal-concentration slope is 2k, not k. In the prescribed example, the normalized rate constant would be k=0.25Lmol−1s−1; initially r=1.0×10−4molL−1s−1 while the disappearance rate is twice that. With [P](0)=0, stoichiometry gives [P](100s)=([A]0​−[A])/2=0.0050molL−1. Both rate constants have the same dimensions, so a units check alone cannot catch their factor-of-two difference. The symbol r denotes the same normalization as v in reaction rate.
The half-life, meaning the time to reach half a specified starting concentration, follows by setting [A]=[A]0​/2:
t1/2​=kobs​[A]0​1​​.
(143)
For r=k[A]2 in 2A⟶P, this becomes t1/2​=1/(2k[A]0​). Its units are seconds because kobs​[A]0​ has units s−1. Doubling the starting concentration halves the first half-life; a quoted half-life needs its starting concentration. This differs from a first-order reaction, whose half-life is independent of starting concentration.[ref]
Within the prescribed run, [A] reaches 0.010, 0.0050, and 0.0025molL−1 at 100, 300, and 700s. The successive halving intervals are 100, 200, and 400s: each begins at a smaller concentration. More generally, reaching fraction f of the initial concentration, with 0<f<1, requires t=(f−1−1)/(kobs​[A]0​), so 90% consumption takes nine initial half-lives, rather than a fixed sequence of equal waiting times.
A straight reciprocal-concentration plot tests this particular single-concentration rate equation. It does not uniquely identify a reaction mechanism, nor does curvature rule out every second-order reaction: two independently changing reactant concentrations require their own integrated rate law. At low concentration, a small measurement error δ[A] becomes approximately −δ[A]/[A]2 in the reciprocal, magnifying late-time errors. Use the line as a diagnostic; a nonlinear regression of the original concentrations can retain their measurement scale and explicit error model.
