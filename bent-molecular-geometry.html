Bent molecular geometry

 Home Chemistry Molecule Molecular geometry
OurBigBook.com Words: 192
In bent molecular geometry, two neighbours and their central chemical atom form a noncollinear triangle, with an interior bond angle strictly between 0∘ and 180∘. Water, H2​O, is the standard example. The NIST CCCBDB experimental water entry, internal coordinates, citing Hoy and Bunker (1979) gives the equilibrium H–O–H bond angle as 104.4776∘ with quoted uncertainty 0.0019∘, usually rounded to 104.5∘.
The oxygen in water has two bonding electron domains and two lone pairs. Its ideal electron pair geometry is tetrahedral, while the three atomic nuclei give bent molecular geometry. Three points always lie in a plane; this does not make the four-domain scaffold planar. Nor does the word bent by itself determine an electron domain count: the same atomic shape can arise from other domain arrangements.
In an illustrative model with two equal bond-dipole contributions of magnitude p separated by bond angle θ, their sum of vectors points along the bisector and has magnitude 2pcos(θ/2). At 180∘ it vanishes; at 104.4776∘ it is approximately 1.2247p. This ratio is a geometric calculation, not a prediction of water's measured electric dipole moment. The entire electron density, including contributions represented by lone pairs, matters. Water is a polar molecule; chemical polarity helps explain its interactions with other polar molecules. See McMurry, Organic Chemistry, §2.2: water, lone-pair contributions, and polarity.
