Use the normalized local truncation error
Tn​=hy(xn+1​)−y(xn​)​−θy′(xn+1​)−(1−θ)y′(xn​).
(1)
The Taylor theorem expansions about xn​ are
hy(xn​+h)−y(xn​)​=y′(xn​)+2h​y′′(xn​)+6h2​y′′′(ξn​),y′(xn​+h)=y′(xn​)+hy′′(xn​)+2h2​y′′′(ηn​).
(2)
Substitution gives exactly
Tn​=2h​(1−2θ)y′′(xn​)+6h2​[y′′′(ξn​)−3θy′′′(ηn​)]​.
(3)
Thus the theta method has order two exactly for θ=1/2, and otherwise order one, as a statement about the method on a sufficiently smooth class of problems. The leading term vanishes at one half, and a function with nonzero third derivative shows that the next term does not vanish universally. As usual, a uniform order estimate assumes bounded relevant derivatives; mere existence of y′′′ on the open interval gives the stated pointwise remainders but not, by itself, a uniform bound. Special exact solutions can of course have zero truncation error even for a first-order method.

Put en​=y(xn​)−yn​. Subtracting the numerical update from the exact update with its local truncation error gives
en+1​=en​+hθ[f(xn+1​,y(xn+1​))−f(xn+1​,yn+1​)]+h(1−θ)[f(xn​,y(xn​))−f(xn​,yn​)]+hTn​.
(4)
The Lipschitz condition in the solution variable and the triangle inequality imply
(1−Lθh)∣en+1​∣≤[1+L(1−θ)h]∣en​∣+h∣Tn​∣.​
(5)
The assumed positive denominator permits division. With K=[1+L(1−θ)h]/(1−Lθh) and e0​=0, repeated substitution of this Lipschitz one-step error bound yields
∣en​∣≤1−Lθhh​∑j=0n−1​Kn−1−j∣Tj​∣≤1−Lθhh​K−1Kn−1​0≤j<nmax​∣Tj​∣.​
(6)
Here K>1 because L,h>0, so the displayed geometric series quotient is well defined. For implicit steps, the same condition makes the scalar update map a contraction mapping, ensuring a unique numerical next value.

On the Dahlquist test equation, set z=hλ. The theta method has stability function
R(z)=1−θz1+(1−θ)z​,yn​=R(z)n.
(7)
With the decaying-solution convention, its region of absolute stability is ∣R(z)∣<1. Writing z=x+iy, squaring numerator and denominator gives
Sθ​={z:2x+(1−2θ)∣z∣2<0}.​
(8)
For 0≤θ<1/2 this is the open disk with centre −1/(1−2θ) and radius 1/(1−2θ). At θ=1/2 it is the open left half-plane. For θ>1/2 it is the exterior of the closed disk with centre 1/(2θ−1) and radius 1/(2θ−1). The pole z=1/θ, when present, lies outside the stability region.
Every z with Rez<0 satisfies the inequality exactly for θ≥1/2. If θ<1/2, sufficiently large negative real z fails it. Therefore
The method is A-stable exactly when 1/2≤θ≤1.​
(9)
If absolute stability is instead defined by bounded amplification, the boundary ∣R∣=1 is included; this convention does not change the A-stability conclusion for the open left half-plane.

In the normalization used by the supplied coefficient expansion, the local truncation error of the linear multistep method is
Tn​=h∑j=0k​βj​∑j=0k​αj​y(xn+j​)−h∑j=0k​βj​y′(xn+j​)​.​
(10)
The nonzero sum in the denominator is an explicit hypothesis. Consistency of a numerical method requires Tn​→0 uniformly on a fixed finite time interval for every sufficiently smooth exact solution as h→0. Equivalently, the two polynomial identities are
ρ(1)=0,ρ′(1)=σ(1),ρ(ζ)=j∑​αj​ζj,σ(ζ)=j∑​βj​ζj.​
(11)
These follow by inserting constant and affine functions, or by setting the first two Taylor coefficients C0​,C1​ to zero.

Zero-stability means that perturbations of the starting values of the zero-step recurrence remain bounded by a fixed multiple of their initial size, uniformly as the number of steps tends to infinity on a fixed time interval. In particular solutions of ∑j=0k​αj​en+j​=0 obey maxn≤N​∣en​∣≤Cmaxj<k​∣ej​∣ with C independent of N.
The equivalent root condition for a multistep method is
Every root of ρ(ζ) has ∣ζ∣≤1, and roots with ∣ζ∣=1 are simple.​
(12)
A root outside the unit circle gives exponential growth of a recurrence relation mode; a repeated root on it gives polynomial growth. Repeated roots strictly inside it are harmless because their polynomial factor is dominated by decay.

The first characteristic polynomial factors as
ρ(ζ)=ζ3+αζ2−αζ−1=(ζ−1)[ζ2+(α+1)ζ+1].
(13)
The two quadratic roots have product one. Both can lie in the closed unit disk only if both have modulus one, requiring ∣α+1∣≤2. At α=−3 the root one is triple and β=0; at α=1 the root minus one is double. Both fail the root condition for a multistep method. In the intervening open interval the two quadratic roots are distinct conjugate unit-modulus roots, different from one. Thus
−3<α<1is exactly the zero-stability range.​
(14)
Using 2β=α+3, the supplied expansion gives
C0​=C1​=C2​=0,C3​=129−α​,C4​=89−α​,C5​=240231−23α​.
(15)
Throughout the zero-stable range, C3​=0, so every such method has order two. There is a unique higher-order choice:
α=9,β=6.​
(16)
Here C3​=C4​=0 and C5​=1/10=0, giving exactly order four, with normalized leading truncation error h4y(5)/120. But the extra roots are −5±26​, one of modulus greater than one. By the Dahlquist equivalence theorem this consistent but non-zero-stable method is not convergent for arbitrary consistent starting values.

Let δxx​Uij​=(Ui+1,j​−2Uij​+Ui−1,j​)/(Δx)2, and define δyy​ analogously. The Crank-Nicolson diffusion scheme is
ΔtUijm+1​−Uijm​​=21​(δxx​+δyy​)(Uijm+1​+Uijm​),1≤i,j≤N−1.​

Write s=μx​+μy​. The update can be rearranged as
(1+s)Uijm+1​−2μx​​(Ui+1,jm+1​+Ui−1,jm+1​)−2μy​​(Ui,j+1m+1​+Ui,j−1m+1​)=​(1−s)Uijm​+2μx​​(Ui+1,jm​+Ui−1,jm​)+2μy​​(Ui,j+1m​+Ui,j−1m​).​
(18)
When s≤1, the right side is a convex combination of old values: its coefficients are nonnegative and sum to one. Assume all old values lie in [Umin​,Umax​]. A new maximum v>Umax​ is interior because boundary values are zero and Umax​≥0. At its node the left side is at least (1+s)v−sv=v, while the right side is at most Umax​, a contradiction. A new minimum v<Umin​ similarly makes the left side at most v and the right side at least Umin​, again a contradiction.
The initial values satisfy the bounds by construction; mathematical induction proves
Umin​≤Uijm​≤Umax​at every node and time level.​
(19)
The new-time matrix is a strictly diagonally dominant matrix with positive diagonal and nonpositive off-diagonal entries, so the linear update is uniquely solvable. This Crank-Nicolson maximum principle under a step restriction concerns preservation of value bounds, which is a stronger requirement than scalar A-stability.

For nonnegative initial data, Umin​=0. The proved Crank-Nicolson maximum principle under a step restriction immediately gives Uijm​≥0​ for all indices. For nonpositive initial data, Umax​=0 and the same bound gives Uijm​≤0​. Equivalently, apply positivity to −U, since the finite difference method is linear and has homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. Both conclusions use μx​+μy​≤1.

For the second-order boundary value problem, introduce an unknown initial slope s and solve the initial value problem u′=v, v′=g(x,u,v) with u(a)=α, v(a)=s. Its endpoint mismatch is F(s)=u(b;s)−β. The shooting method seeks a root F(s)=0; in general the dependence of the flow on s is nonlinear. A sign-changing bracket for a continuous F allows the bisection method, provided the intervening initial value solutions exist up to b.
For Newton's method, solve the parameter sensitivity equation alongside the trajectory. With z=∂s​u and w=∂s​v,
z′=w,w′=gu​z+gu′​w,z(a)=0,w(a)=1.
(20)
Then F′(s)=z(b) and the update is snew​=s−F(s)/z(b)​ whenever z(b)=0.
For the third-order problem, shooting from a leaves both u′(a) and u′′(a) unknown, requiring two shooting parameters to satisfy two right-endpoint conditions. Shooting backwards from b already knows u(b)=β and u′(b)=γ; only u′′(b) is unknown, with the single residual u(a)−α. Backward shooting reduces the nonlinear solve from two scalar unknowns to one. This parameter-count advantage does not by itself guarantee numerical stability of backward integration.

The four-point midpoint third derivative uses the four values at xj−1​,xj​,xj+1​,xj+2​, centred at xj+1/2​. The Taylor theorem about that midpoint gives
h3u(xj+2​)−3u(xj+1​)+3u(xj​)−u(xj−1​)​=u′′′(xj+1/2​)+8h2​u(5)(xj+1/2​)+O(h4).
(21)
Thus, for n≥2, take the n−1 interior equations
Uj+2​−3Uj+1​+3Uj​−Uj−1​=h3f(xj+1/2​),j=1,…,n−1.​
(22)
Impose the two value conditions exactly, U0​=α, Un+1​=β, and approximate the remaining condition with the second-order backward finite difference:
2h3Un+1​−4Un​+Un−1​​=γ.​
(23)
This gives n equations for U1​,…,Un​. The endpoint derivative formula equals u′(b)−h2u′′′(b)/3+O(h3), so its residual tends to zero along with those of the four-point equations. Every stencil lies within [a,b]; no unprescribed ghost value is introduced. The construction is therefore a consistent finite difference method for all three boundary conditions.

The five-point equations for the Poisson equation are
(Lh​U)jk​:=h24Ujk​−Uj+1,k​−Uj−1,k​−Uj,k+1​−Uj,k−1​​=f(xj​,yk​),1≤j,k≤n,​
(24)
with prescribed boundary values. If Lh​ϕ≤0, an interior maximal value is at most the average of its four neighbours. Since no neighbour exceeds the maximum, equality forces all four to equal it. Repeating along adjacent nodes propagates that value to the boundary. Thus the discrete maximum principle gives maxϕ≤max∂Ωh​​ϕ, in particular the usual positive-maximum bound with zero included.
For an O(h2) error estimate assume the exact solution has bounded continuous fourth partial derivatives up to the boundary. The local truncation error τ=Lh​u−f then satisfies ∥τ∥∞​≤Ch2 by the Taylor theorem. Put e=u∣Ωh​​−U, so Lh​e=τ and e=0 on boundary nodes. The Discrete Poisson barrier on a square
B(x,y)=4x(1−x)+y(1−y)​
(25)
satisfies Lh​B=1 exactly, B≥0 on the grid boundary, and B≤1/8. Set ε=∥τ∥∞​. Then Lh​(±e−εB)≤0, with nonpositive boundary values. Applying the discrete maximum principle twice gives
∣ejk​∣≤εB(xj​,yk​)≤81​∥τ∥∞​=O(h2).​
(26)
For nonzero smooth Dirichlet boundary data g, impose U=g at the boundary nodes. The error still vanishes there, so the same argument and order apply, under the same fourth-derivative regularity of the exact solution. Smooth boundary values alone do not remove every possible corner-regularity issue.

Replace Lh​ by Lh​=Lh​+I​. At a positive interior maximum v>0, the neighbour average is no larger than v, so (Lh​ϕ)jk​≥v>0. Thus Lh​ϕ≤0 cannot have such an interior maximum. The positive-maximum form of the discrete maximum principle therefore remains valid:
maxϕ≤max(0,max∂Ωh​​ϕ).
(27)
With e=u∣Ωh​​−U the error equation is Lh​e=τ, where τ=O(h2) under the preceding regularity assumptions. Since Lh​B=1+B≥1 for the same Discrete Poisson barrier on a square, the comparison functions ±e−∥τ∥∞​B prove
∣ejk​∣≤81​∥τ∥∞​=O(h2).​
(28)
There is no loss of uniqueness: apply the positive-maximum argument to both signs of a homogeneous discrete solution. The continuous weak formulation also has the coercive bilinear form ∫Ω​(∣∇v∣2+v2), so the added positive reaction term strengthens its positive energy.

Let x∗​=A−1b, ek​=xk​−x∗​ and G=M−1N. Subtract the exact fixed-point equation to obtain ek​=Gke0​. Hence convergence for every initial vector is equivalent to Gk→0. If this holds, it holds after complexification; applying it to a complex eigenvector gives λkv→0, so ∣λ∣<1. Conversely, the complex Jordan normal form expresses each power as a finite sum of terms (rk​)λk−r from its blocks. Every term tends to zero when all eigenvalues have modulus below one. Thus
Convergence for every starting vector⟺ρ(G)<1.​
(29)
The convergence criterion for a stationary iteration needs complex triangularization or Jordan normal form. The paper's assertion that every real matrix is similar to a real upper-triangular matrix is false when nonreal eigenvalues occur; complex triangularization supplies the intended argument. In the Gauss-Seidel method, with A=D+L+U split into diagonal, strictly lower and strictly upper parts, M=D+L, N=−U​.
For n≥4, eliminating the ghost values gives the symmetric pentadiagonal matrix
A=​7−410⋮​−46−41⋮​1−46−4⋱​01−46⋱​⋯⋯⋱⋱⋱​​,Ann​=7, Ajj​=6 (2≤j≤n−1),Aj,j+1​=−4,Aj,j+2​=1.​
(30)
All other off-diagonal entries vanish. The first two equations are
7U1​−4U2​+U3​=h4f1​,−4U1​+6U2​−4U3​+U4​=h4f2​,
(31)
and the last two are
Un−3​−4Un−2​+6Un−1​−4Un​=h4fn−1​,Un−2​−4Un−1​+7Un​=h4fn​.
(32)
For n=3 the matrix is ​7−41​−46−4​1−47​​, for n=2 it is (7−4​−47​), and for n=1 it is (8) because both ghost contributions meet the same unknown. The diagonal entries are positive in every case, so the lower triangular matrix D+L is invertible and the Gauss-Seidel iteration is well defined.
An eigenvalue λ of G=−(D+L)−1U makes H(λ)=λ(D+L)+U singular. Let ℓi​ and ri​ be the sums of absolute lower and upper entries in row i. The row's diagonal-dominance margin is
∣λ∣di​−∣λ∣ℓi​−ri​.
(33)
Interior full rows have di​=6, ℓi​=ri​=5, giving margin ∣λ∣−5. Boundary rows have larger margins; for instance the first gives 7∣λ∣−5, and the second gives at least 2∣λ∣−5. Therefore ∣λ∣>5 would make H(λ) a strictly diagonally dominant matrix, hence nonsingular, a contradiction.
At ∣λ∣=5 it is weakly diagonally dominant with strict boundary rows. Its nonzero nearest-neighbour entries make it an irreducibly diagonally dominant matrix. The nonsingularity theorem for that class again contradicts singularity. For the small matrices above all rows are already strict at this value. Hence
Every iteration eigenvalue has ∣λ∣<5; equality is impossible.​
(34)
There is also a stronger conclusion. For T=tridiag(−1,2,−1),
A=T2+2e1​e1T​+2en​enT​,
(35)
including the n=1 overlap. Since T is positive definite, A is a symmetric positive-definite matrix. The Gauss-Seidel convergence from diagonal dominance and positive definiteness theorem consequently gives the stronger bound ρ(G)<1.
