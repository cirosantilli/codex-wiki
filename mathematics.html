Mathematics studies patterns in quantity, structure, space, and change by giving objects precise mathematical definitions and examining what follows from them. Arithmetic starts with operations on numbers; algebra organizes relations and transformations; geometry and topology studies shapes and the structures preserved by maps; analysis studies limits and continuous change. These fields connect because the same structure can appear in different forms.
A small example shows the value of changing viewpoint. A point moving around the unit circle has coordinates x=cosθ, y=sinθ. Regard the plane as complex numbers and the same point is z=eiθ, by Euler's formula. Adding two angles now corresponds to multiplying their complex exponentials: eiαeiβ=ei(α+β). A geometric rotation has become an algebraic operation.
The connection reaches calculus too. Differentiating twice gives x′′+x=0 and y′′+y=0, so the two coordinates solve the harmonic oscillator equation. At angle θ=π/3, z=1/2+i3​/2; one point supplies an exact cosine, an exact sine, and the state of an oscillation. The Pythagorean trigonometric identity records that the modulus stays one. Seeing these as descriptions of one object makes each formula easier to remember and reuse.
This is why a useful mathematical explanation provides both a reliable mathematical proof and a way to see what the result is doing. In his 1994 essay, William Thurston discusses mathematical progress in terms of advancing understanding, and uses the many interpretations of a derivative to illustrate this aim. [ref] A diagram, a computed example, and a concise argument can make different parts of the same idea accessible.
Applications ask us to select a mathematical model of a physical or other system. Its assumptions determine which conclusions transfer to that system. Within mathematics, the same practice of making assumptions explicit lets a result about groups, vector spaces, or probability distributions apply to many concrete examples. The beauty is the combination of precision and reuse: an argument can reveal more than the particular case that first suggested it.

The Chinese remainder theorem says that remainders modulo pairwise coprime positive integers can be specified independently. For m1​,…,mr​ pairwise coprime and M=m1​⋯mr​, every prescribed list a1​,…,ar​ determines one congruence class modulo M satisfying x≡aj​(modmj​) for all j. It determines a class, rather than one unrestricted integer: adding a multiple of M preserves every remainder.
For example, combine x≡2(mod3) and x≡3(mod5). Write x=2+3k from the first condition. The second becomes 3k≡1(mod5). Since three has modular inverse two modulo five, k≡2(mod5), giving x≡8(mod15). The integers 8, 23 and −7 all work. The fifteen possibilities modulo fifteen correspond to the fifteen pairs in a three-by-five grid.
The theorem also preserves arithmetic: reducing an addition or a multiplication modulo each mj​ gives the same tuple as performing that operation on the corresponding congruence classes. In the language of quotient rings, reduction is a ring isomorphism
Z/MZ[x]M​​⟶j=1∏r​Z/mj​Z,⟼([x]m1​​,…,[x]mr​​).​
(1)
One large residue is thus an exact encoding of several smaller residues. This is useful for splitting arithmetic into smaller calculations and then reconstructing the result; MIT's modular arithmetic notes develop both the reconstruction and the product representation. [ref] For a concrete multiplication, 7 corresponds to (1,2) and 8 to (2,3) modulo (3,5). Their componentwise product is (2,1), which reconstructs to 11(mod15), agreeing with 56≡11(mod15).
A constructive formula explains the independence. For mj​>1, put Mj​=M/mj​ and choose uj​ with Mj​uj​≡1(modmj​), using a modular multiplicative inverse. Then ej​=Mj​uj​ is one modulo mj​ and zero modulo every other mi​. Therefore
x≡∑j=1r​aj​ej​(modM)
(2)
has the desired remainders. A modulus equal to one imposes no restriction and can be omitted. Uniqueness follows because a difference divisible by every mj​ is divisible by their product when they are pairwise coprime. This short construction gives a reusable algorithm as well as an existence statement.
The coprime hypothesis explains why every cell in the left grid is attainable. With integer moduli six and eight, a pair must agree modulo their greatest common divisor, two. A mathematical model represents selected features of a system with mathematical objects and relations so that its behavior can be analyzed or predicted. Choosing the representation is part of the work: specify the quantities tracked, the equations or probability distributions connecting them, and the conditions under which those connections are expected to apply.
