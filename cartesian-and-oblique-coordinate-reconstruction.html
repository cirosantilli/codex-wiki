Cartesian and oblique coordinate reconstruction (c=B−1p,BTp=Gc)

Keep the same origin and the same vector p=(2,1)T, but use the nonperpendicular basis vectors b1​=(1,0)T and b2​=(1,1)T. They form a basis because the column matrix B has nonzero determinant:
B=[b1​ b2​]=(10​11​),B−1=(10​−11​),[p]B​=B−1p=(11​).
(5)
The reconstruction is p=b1​+b2​. Its coordinates are read along lines parallel to the other axis, producing a parallelogram. These lines need not be perpendicular to the axis being measured. In contrast, the dot products are p⋅b1​=2 and p⋅b2​=3; treating those as coordinates would reconstruct 2b1​+3b2​=(5,3)T, a different vector.
The dual basis supplies the right measuring rules for any basis.[ref] Here these coordinate functionals are b1∗​(x,y)=x−y and b2∗​(x,y)=y, the rows of B−1. They give b1∗​(p)=b2∗​(p)=1. Equivalently, their representing vectors d1​=(1,−1)T, d2​=(0,1)T satisfy di​⋅bj​=δij​ and extract the coordinates as di​⋅p. These are Riesz dual basis in an inner product space representatives, rather than the original basis vectors.
The Gram matrix records how the nonperpendicular directions interact:
G=BTB=(11​12​),BTp=G[p]B​=(23​),∥p∥2=[p]BT​G[p]B​=5.
(6)
For any real basis represented by a square invertible matrix B, writing p=Bc gives these identities immediately. One can recover the coordinates either as c=B−1p or by solving Gc=BTp. In this example, if c=(a,b)T, the squared norm is a2+2ab+2b2, not a2+b2. Oblique coordinates remain useful when natural directions are not perpendicular, but the Gram matrix must accompany them when computing lengths or dot products.
