Antibody

 Home Biology Protein
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 32
An antibody is a protein that recognizes particular molecular targets through binding. Antibodies participate in immunity; laboratory assays such as Western blots exploit their selective binding to detect chosen proteins.
An IgG gives a concrete architecture: two immunoglobulin heavy chains and two immunoglobulin light chains, joined by disulfide bonds. Each arm ends in a paratope assembled from an antibody variable region of each kind of chain. The two paratopes recognize the same epitope in this conventional IgG; the flexible hinge allows different arm separations. The antibody constant regions form the remaining portions. This is an illustrative IgG, rather than the architecture of every antibody format. [ref]
Three questions must be kept separate. Antibody specificity asks which antigens are recognized. Antibody affinity describes one paratope–epitope interaction. Avidity describes the combined attachment when several contacts can cooperate. A selective antibody can have modest antibody affinity, and a monoclonal antibody can still show cross-reactivity. For an assay, the practical question is whether the observed readout identifies the intended antigen under the tested conditions; Western blot antibody specificity controls make that question concrete.
