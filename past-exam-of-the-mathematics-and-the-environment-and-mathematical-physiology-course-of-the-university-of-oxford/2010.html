i OurBigBook.com
The heat capacity per unit horizontal area is C=ρcp​d: mass density times specific heat capacity times atmospheric depth. Thus CT˙ is the rate of stored thermal energy per unit area. The solar constant Q is the incident radiative flux on a surface normal to sunlight; the ratio of the Earth's intercepting disk area to its spherical area is 1/4. The absorbed fraction is 1−a, where albedo a is the reflected fraction. The outgoing radiative flux is σγT4, a Stefan–Boltzmann law with effective radiation factor γ incorporating the simplified greenhouse effect. Every term therefore has units of power per area.
The albedo curve decreases smoothly, passes through (T0​,1/2), and approaches 1/4 at high temperature. At T=0 it is (2+tanhμ)/4, close to 3/4 when μ is large. Its derivative is
a0′​(T)=−T04​μT3​sech2[μ(T4/T04​−1)]≤0.
(1)
An ice-covered planet reflects more sunlight than exposed land or ocean. Warming removes reflective ice, lowers albedo, and increases absorbed radiative flux: this is ice-albedo feedback. The parameter μ controls the sharpness of this transition; the formula is an idealized global response, rather than a local melting law.

Set x=T4/T04​−1. Since σγT04​=Q/8, the steady state energy balance is 2(1−a0​)=1+x. Substitution gives
tanh(μx)=2x.​
(2)
The two sides have slopes μ and 2 at the origin. For x>0, tanh(μx) is strictly concave, its initial slope is μ, and it remains below 1. Consequently no positive root exists for μ≤2, while for μ>2 there is exactly one positive root x+​∈(0,1/2). Oddness supplies the corresponding negative root. At μ=2 the only root is still zero. Hence multiple steady temperatures occur precisely when μ>μc​=2.
The largest root corresponds monotonically to the largest positive absolute temperature:
T+​=T0​(1+x+​)1/4,a+​=21−x+​​.​
(3)
For large μ, the positive root tends to 1/2, so a+​≃1/4 and T+​/T0​≃(3/2)1/4. The negative branch has larger albedo and lower temperature.

(16)

λ=−(1+ik)[3δ+ik(F2−1)]mk2​.​
(17)

(18)

The figure shows these two phase relations. They identify the bedforms but do not by themselves establish growth. Resolve gravity into components gsinα down the bed and −gcosα normal to it. For slow incompressible flow, inertia is negligible and Cauchy momentum equation reduces to the divergence of the stress tensor plus the gravitational force density being zero. Write the dominant shear component as τ=σxz​ and the leading normal stresses as −p.
The shallow-ice approximation assumes a small thickness-to-length ratio. Vertical variation of the shear is retained in the along-bed equation, while longitudinal deviatoric-stress gradients and the shear contribution to the normal equation are higher order. The leading balances are consequently
−px​+τz​+ρgsinα=0,−pz​−ρgcosα=0.​
(20)
The second is a hydrostatic pressure balance; the first balances gravitational driving and the surface-induced pressure gradient against vertical shear. This approximation requires shallow geometry and sufficiently slow flow, not a Newtonian constitutive equation for ice.

A typical net ice accumulation curve decreases downstream: snow gain at high elevation, zero at the equilibrium line, and negative values in the ice ablation zone. In a steady glacier with zero volume flux at its head, Q(x)=∫0x​a(X)dX. If its terminus also has zero volume flux, a natural glacier length L is the positive root of ∫0L​a(X)dX=0, with the integral positive between the endpoints. Such a root requires a suitable given accumulation profile; it is not guaranteed by dimensional analysis alone.
Let a0​ be a representative accumulation rate and K=A(ρgsinα)n. Choose

On a characteristic emitted by the boundary x=0, the invariant is zero and H=H0​(x). The travel time of the one emitted at time zero is

Glacier characteristic admissibility gives a substantive domain restriction. Factoring the invariant gives A(x)−A(ξ)=(x−ξ)(1−x−ξ). A positive forward branch requires

(30)
Equivalently it is the image of this parameter domain under (ξ,x)↦(x,t(ξ,x)), restricted further to regions where that map is single-valued. Characteristics cannot start with positive thickness growth at ξ>1/2, where the prescribed initial rate Ht​=1−2ξ is negative. This is why the printed piecewise expression is a formal characteristic construction, not a global nonnegative solution from a bare bed. For example for x>1/2, putting Lξ​=1−2ξ gives

(31)
Both factors increase with ξ there, so no positive initial-data branch exists at that x for 0<t<f(x), contrary to a literal global reading of the first case. Compression can also make characteristics intersect; entropy solutions then require shock waves.
One can nevertheless give the exact zero-thickness endpoint locus of these formal characteristics. Let K=f(1)=(n+2)−pB(q,q). At x=1−ξ the change of variable z=ξ+(1−2ξ)y gives
tend​(ξ)=K(1−2ξ)−n/(n+2),xend​(t)=21​[1+(tK​)(n+2)/n],t≥K.​
(32)
This is the formal snout locus inferred from the given characteristic family; it retreats from 1 towards 1/2. It is not justified as the physical terminus of an accumulating glacier formed from zero initial ice. In particular it says nothing about a positive advancing snout at t<K. A physical free boundary problem must specify no ablation on a bare bed, maintain H≥0, and select an entropy solution or retain the small slope regularization. Those additional conditions are absent from the printed first-order construction. Thus a unique global physical snout trajectory cannot be claimed from that construction alone. The steady endpoint remains x=1. The degenerate n=0 limit would instead have the same endpoint time K for every initial characteristic, so the inverted formula presumes n>0.
