The depth h is free-surface height η minus bed elevation s. The first differential equation conserves water volume. Momentum balances local and advective acceleration against the free-surface pressure gradient, mean gravitational bed-slope forcing and the quadratic drag fu2/h.
The concentration c is suspended sediment mass per water volume. Multiplication of its material rate by depth gives the sediment change per bed area. Settling at speed vs​ removes mass at rate vs​c, while entrainment supplies vs​E(u); increasing equilibrium concentration E(u) represents stronger erosive capacity at greater speed. The same exchange changes the bed mass per area, whose solid volume fraction is 1−ϕ and grain density is ρs​. Hence net suspended gain equals −ρs​(1−ϕ)st​: erosion lowers the bed and deposition raises it.
One derives these balances by depth integration with hydrostatic pressure and a sectionally averaged velocity, followed by closures for drag and sediment entrainment/settling. The model neglects additional bedload transport and uses the dilute-suspension approximation to keep the water-volume balance independent of sediment volume.

Glacier sliding is motion of the ice base relative to its bed, distinct from internal ice deformation. Near a melting bed, basal water, pressure-melting/refreezing around obstacles and creep past bed roughness can permit motion; a cold frozen bed can suppress it. A simple sliding law is increasing in basal traction, for example ub​∝τbm​ on the positive-traction branch, with resistance also affected in richer models by roughness and basal water pressure.
Slow ice motion has negligible inertial acceleration. In a long shallow glacier, leading vertical stress is hydrostatic and longitudinal stress gradients are neglected relative to transverse shear gradients. Resolving gravity downslope and normally yields the two stated balances. This approximates a two-dimensional slab/valley centreline; lateral drag, rapidly varying geometry and strong longitudinal-stress regions need additional terms.
With a stress-free surface,
p=ρgcosα(h−z),τ=ρgsinα[1−cotαhx​](h−z).​
(23)
Put K=ρgsinα and D=1−cotαhx​, assuming positive driving shear for the stated power law. Integrating from the bed gives
u(z)=ub​+n+1A(KD)n​[hn+1−(h−z)n+1],q=hub​+n+2A(KD)nhn+2​.​
(24)
The surface speed is us​=ub​+A(KD)nhn+1/(n+1). Mass conservation, with a measured as ice-equivalent thickness accumulation, is ht​+qx​=a​.
Choose h=Hh^, x=lx^, ub​=Uu^b​ and t=(l/U)t^, with U=(KH/R)m. Scale accumulation with HU/l. The dimensionless basal law and parameters are
ub​=hm(1−μhx​)m,μ=lHcotα​,δ=UAKnHn+1​=ARmKn−mHn+1−m.​
(25)
The resulting flux is exactly hub​+δ(1−μhx​)nhn+2/(n+2).
When internal deformation is small and slope corrections are uniformly small, ub​≃hm and
ht​+(hm+1)x​=a.​
(26)
For a steady accumulation profile and prescribed upstream flux qin​, h∗​(x)=[qin​+∫0x​a(ξ)dξ]1/(m+1)​. For constant a and zero upstream flux this is (ax)1/(m+1) on the region of positive flux.
A small thickness disturbance ζ satisfies ζt​+[c(x)ζ]x​=0 with c=(m+1)h∗m​. Hence it travels down-glacier along dx/dt=c>0, with amplitude changing as dζ/dt=−c′ζ. Since surface speed is us​≃ub​=h∗m​ in this sliding-dominated limit,
c=(m+1)us​.​
(27)
The thickness wave therefore outruns individual ice particles. This is the sliding-dominated glacier kinematic wave.

α=dg​+ϵ,γch​=−(1+ϵμ)dg​.​
(46)
Here γch​ is the coefficient called γ in the source's characteristic equation; it is not the proliferating-cell death hazard. Any characteristic root with positive real part makes the equilibrium linearly unstable. A nonzero imaginary crossing satisfies α+γch​cosω=0 and ω−γch​sinω=0; these are its Hopf conditions, subject to nondegeneracy.
On slow time T=ϵt^, the unit delay becomes u1​=u(T−ϵ)=u−ϵuT​+O(ϵ2). Taylor expansion gives g(u1​)−g(u)=−ϵg′(u)uT​+O(ϵ2). Dividing the equation by ϵ therefore yields
dTdu​≃1+g′(u)μg(u)−u​.​
(47)
This approximation requires slow smooth variation and a denominator bounded away from zero; a vanishing denominator marks failure of this slow reduction. On the fast time t^=O(1), omit only the explicit order-ϵ terms, retaining the finite delay:
dt^du​≃g(u(t^−1))−g(u(t^)).​
(48)
Replacing the delayed argument by the present value on that fast scale would incorrectly erase the dynamics. These are the slow and fast limits of a delayed stem-cell model.
