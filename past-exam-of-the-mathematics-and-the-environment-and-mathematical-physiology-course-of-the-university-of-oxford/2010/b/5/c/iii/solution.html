Let c↑​(w) denote the increasing-front speed from the preceding solution. Reversing that heteroclinic reverses its speed, so a decreasing back has speed c↓​(w)=−c↑​(w). A rigidly travelling pulse has one speed throughout, requiring
c=c↑​(0)=c↓​(w0​),c↑​(w0​)=−c↑​(0).​
(51)
This is the correct use of the integral formula: compare the oppositely oriented transitions, including their signs, rather than equating two increasing-front speeds.
For completeness, write f(v)−w=−(v−r1​)(v−r2​)(v−r3​), r1​<r2​<r3​. The explicit increasing frozen-recovery cubic front is
v(X)=r1​+1+exp[−(r3​−r1​)X/2​]r3​−r1​​,c↑​(w)=2​r1​+r3​−2r2​​.
(52)
Substitution, using vX​=(v−r1​)(r3​−v)/2​, verifies the equation. Since r1​+r2​+r3​=1+a, speed matching gives
c↑​(0)=2​1−2a​,r2​(w0​)=32−a​,w0​=f(32−a​)=272(2−a)(1−2a)(1+a)​.​
(53)
For 0<a<1/2 this selects the appropriate positive back level within the cubic's three-root range. The high branch connects B to C slowly, and the low branch supplies recovery after D.
The stated positive-speed, rising-front orientation imposes a<1/2 at leading order. The printed range 0<a<1 by itself does not guarantee that pulse construction: at a=1/2 the leading front is stationary, while for a>1/2 the calculated increasing-front speed is negative. Existence and stability of a full finite-ϵ pulse also require the slow connections; speed matching is its leading necessary construction, rather than a proof for every parameter in the printed range.
