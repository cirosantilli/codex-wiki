For Röthlisberger channel drainage, S is the water-filled cross-sectional area of the subglacial channel, Q its volume discharge, m the mass of ice melted per unit channel length per unit time, and N=pi​−pw​ the effective pressure, namely ice overburden pressure minus water pressure. The first balance converts melt mass to area growth through division by ice density ρi​, and subtracts material creep closure KSN3. The cubic pressure dependence comes from the adopted ice-flow law; K includes its material and geometric factors.
The second relation balances the downslope hydraulic driving gradient ρw​gsinα against the adopted frictional pressure loss. It is an instantaneous discharge-area law, giving Q=(sinα/f)1/2S4/3 for positive downstream flow. The final balance equates latent heat mL needed to melt the channel walls with the gravitational power dissipated per unit length, ρw​gsinαQ. It assumes that this dissipated power is available for melting rather than substantial sensible heating or other energy losses. The model thus couples melt opening, pressure-driven material creep closure and hydraulic discharge.

For quasistatic bed stability with suspended sediment, in the leading approximation, (hu)x​=0 and ηx​=0. For spatially varying perturbations at fixed unit water flux and fixed surface level, write u=1+U, h=1+H, c=1+C, s=s∗​+S1​. The constraints give H=−U and S1​=−H=U. Linearizing the suspended and bed equations yields

The cytoplasmic concentration c gains a constant influx r from outside the cell and loses calcium through a first-order extrusion term kc. The pump flux J+​ moves calcium from cytoplasm into the sarcoplasmic reticulum, saturating with cytoplasmic concentration through a Hill function with scale K1​, maximal rate V1​ and exponent n. The release flux J−​ returns stored calcium to the cytoplasm. A complete classification of criticality requires a nonlinear coefficient, not merely the trace calculation. At leading singular order the thresholds approach the folds determined by f=0 and fv​−γfu​=0, equivalently V′=−1/γ.
Periodic solutions are also supported globally in the positive quadrant. The axes point inward for positive input, v′≤(β−δv)/ϵ bounds v, and the u equation then bounds u by a linear restoring term plus a bounded source. A large rectangle can thus be chosen forward invariant. For an initial point distinct from the repelling unique equilibrium, its omega-limit set cannot contain that equilibrium or a boundary equilibrium. The Poincaré-Bendixson theorem supplies a periodic orbit. In the strong separation regime it follows the two attracting branches and jumps between them, a relaxation oscillation.
The interval requires the maximum of fv​−γfu​ along the critical manifold to exceed ϵ; the listed scale hierarchy alone is not a substitute for that test for arbitrary remaining constants. In a relaxation cycle, cytoplasmic calcium rises slowly on the left attracting branch while total calcium is accumulated, then rises rapidly during store discharge along a constant-w fast path to the right branch. It subsequently decreases on the right slow branch as extrusion dominates input, and finally drops rapidly during store uptake back to the left branch. In delayed carbon-dioxide ventilation feedback, P is the effective carbon-dioxide partial pressure of the lumped body compartment. Its stored carbon-dioxide amount is proportional to P, with effective capacitance K, so KdP/dT is its accumulation rate. Metabolism produces carbon dioxide at rate M. Ventilation removes it at a rate proportional to partial pressure times ventilation, PV˙, with gas-conversion constants absorbed into the model coefficients. The equation is therefore a one-compartment carbon-dioxide balance: production minus respiratory removal equals accumulation. It idealizes mixing and treats metabolic production as constant over the time scale of interest.

Chemoreceptors respond to carbon-dioxide levels, but feedback is delayed by blood transport to sensing sites, neural processing and the response of respiratory muscles. A fixed positive delay τ approximates their combined effect. The ventilation at the present instant therefore depends on the earlier pressure, V˙(t)=V(P(t−τ)), not just on P(t). This delayed negative feedback can overcorrect after the pressure has already changed, providing a mechanism for oscillatory breathing. The delay equation requires a pressure history over an interval of length τ, rather than a single initial value.

The positive-part law V(P)=G[P−P0​]+​ is a threshold-linear approximation to the ventilatory response. Below the apnoeic threshold P0​, this model assigns zero ventilation; above it, each pressure increment produces a proportional ventilation increase, with gain G>0. Taking the positive part ensures a nonnegative ventilation rate rather than the unphysical negative rate that unrestricted linear extrapolation would give. It is a local idealization of the response, not a claim that actual physiology lacks other respiratory drives or saturates nowhere. In the delayed model this response is evaluated at the delayed pressure.

For real λ≥0, draw y=λ+β and y=−γe−λ. The first is nonnegative and increasing, while the second is negative and approaches zero from below. They cannot intersect on the nonnegative real axis. At zero the characteristic function equals β+γ>0, so a stationary zero-root crossing is excluded as well.
