In the non-scattering grey atmosphere approximation, I is the frequency-integrated specific intensity along a ray, s is distance along that ray, ρ is the mass density, and κ is the mass absorption coefficient. The local thermodynamic equilibrium source is the frequency-integrated Planck function B=σSB​T4/π. Across a thin element ds, absorption removes κρIds and thermal emission adds κρBds. Their balance gives dI/ds=−κρ(I−B).
Take the upward vertical as the polar axis, put μ=cosθ, and define downward optical depth by
τ(z)=∫z∞​κ(z′)ρ(z′)dz′.​
(1)
Along a ray, dz/ds=μ and dτ/ds=−κρμ. The chain rule therefore gives μIτ​=I−B​. In a plane-parallel, azimuthally symmetric field, the solid angle element is dω=dφdμ, so
J(τ)=21​∫−11​I(τ,μ)dμ.​
(2)
Local radiative equilibrium requires zero net radiative heating of the material: absorption 4πκρJ balances emission 4πκρB. Where the absorption coefficient is nonzero this gives B=J, and substitution gives the required angular radiative transfer equation. It does not require the specific intensity itself to be isotropic.
At the top of an atmosphere with no external incoming long-wave radiation, impose I(0,μ)=0(−1≤μ<0)​. These are inward directions; prescribing the same zero value for outward directions would remove the emergent radiation as well. The upward radiative flux and its derivative are
Φ=2π∫−11​μIdμ,dτdΦ​=2π∫−11​(I−B)dμ=4π(J−B)=0.
(3)
Thus Φ is constant with height​: local radiative equilibrium expresses energy conservation through successive atmospheric layers.

The first Saint-Venant equation conserves water volume. The second balances material acceleration against downslope gravity, bed friction and the free-surface pressure gradient. With length scale L, depth scale H, slope S and velocity scale U, the conventional gravity-friction scaling has ε=H/(SL) and Froude number F=U/gH​. The pressure-gradient/downslope-gravity ratio is ε, and the inertia/downslope-gravity ratio is εF2.
The uniform steady solution obeys u2=h and hu=Q, hence u0​=Q1/3, h0​=Q2/3​. In the constant-flux river momentum relaxation reduction, substitute h=Q/u and X=1−s. Since us​=−uX​ and hs​=QuX​/u2, the resulting quasilinear partial differential equation is
ut​+c(u)uX​=εF21−u3/Q​,c(u)=F2u2Q​−u.​
(4)
A downstream boundary at X=0 supplies data into X>0 precisely when c(u1​)>0, equivalent to u1​<Q1/3/F2/3​. Although water flows out there, the relevant characteristic curves carry information upstream in this subcritical regime.
Along a characteristic curve, dX/dt=c(u) and du/dt=(1−u3/Q)/(εF2). Positive u relaxes toward u0​; near u0​, its displacement decays at rate 3/(εF2u0​). Boundary-generated deviations therefore become small after a time of order ε and, while the characteristic speed stays bounded away from zero, within a distance of order ε. The outer state is the initial uniform solution. A jump between initial and boundary values at their corner can produce a weak solution or a thin initial adjustment; a globally smooth corner solution requires compatible data.
For the moving boundary put Y=X−vt. The characteristic speed relative to it is c(u)−v, while the outer speed is
vb​=c(u0​)=Q1/3(F−2−1).​
(5)
For the intended tidal slowing 0<u1​≤u0​, c is decreasing in u and hence c(u)≥vb​ throughout the relaxation. Thus v<vb​ leaves an incoming family and an attached thin boundary layer. The diagram shows numerical characteristics of this scalar reduction, with a slower boundary velocity. When v>vb​, the tidal boundary overtakes the unperturbed characteristic family; an attached smooth relaxation layer is no longer appropriate, and a tidal bore or shock wave can arise in a fuller river model.
There are two qualifications to the printed reduction. First, without the physical slowing assumption, v<vb​ alone need not make the boundary incoming: for u1​>u0​, also require v<c(u1​). Second, imposing hu=Q exactly everywhere in the original continuity equation gives ht​=0, and consequently ut​=0. The time-dependent characteristics above solve the requested momentum-only approximation, rather than the complete Saint-Venant equation system with an exactly constant flux.

Bedload transport carries sediment by rolling, sliding and short hops near the bed. Suspended sediment is held in the water column by turbulent mixing. The competition between particle settling velocity and the turbulent vertical velocity scale chiefly determines which description applies; particle size and density influence that settling velocity.
Scale the depths and bed/free-surface elevations by d, both velocities by U, distance by L, and time by T. A choice producing exactly the requested coefficients is
U=fgSd​​,L=fρw​ρs​a​,T=aUdL​.​
(6)
The sediment continuity equation then has unit coefficients, while both material-acceleration time derivatives acquire the factor L/(UT)=a/d. Dividing water momentum by gd/L and sediment momentum by U2/L gives
ε=da​,F2=gdU2​=fS​,δ=dSL​=ρw​ρs​​εF2,κ=Ufb​L​.​
(7)
For example d=1m, a=10−3m, S=10−3, f=10−2 and ρs​/ρw​=2.65 give ε=10−3 and δ=2.65×10−4. These are illustrative values: small effective mobile-layer thickness and small slope explain the two small parameters.
Set ε=δ=0 at leading order. Water continuity gives hu=q, with spatially constant flux; take a prescribed constant q. Water momentum integrates to η+F2u2/2=C. Since s=η−h,
s(u)=C−2F2u2​−uq​.​
(8)
The bedload transport model with sediment inertia has remaining equations s(u)t​+vx​=0 and vvx​=(u−v)2−κv.
Choose a uniform state of this reduced model with h0​=u0​=q=1, constant s0​=C−F2/2−1, and
v0​=22+κ−κ2+4κ​​∈(0,1).​
(9)
It satisfies (1−v0​)2=κv0​. For small perturbations U,V of the two velocities, put a1​=2(1−v0​)>0 and b1​=a1​+κ>0. Linearization gives
(1−F2)Ut​+Vx​=0,v0​Vx​=a1​U−b1​V.
(10)
For a Fourier mode eikx+λt this yields
λ=−(1−F2)(b1​+ikv0​)ika1​​,Reλ=−(1−F2)(b12​+k2v02​)a1​v0​k2​.​
(11)
Every nonzero mode decays for F<1 and grows for F>1. The spatially uniform mode is neutral, and F=1 is a singular threshold where the neglected terms matter. In a general uniform state the controlling sign is q/u02​−F2u0​: equivalently the actual mean-flow Froude number is less or greater than one. The displayed F=1 criterion uses the stated unit-depth, unit-velocity normalization of the reduced steady state.

Basal sliding allows ice to pass over bed obstacles by enhanced deformation and regelation: pressure-induced melting on the upstream side and refreezing downstream transfer ice around small obstacles. Cavitation opens water-filled gaps on the downstream side. Their size and the remaining ice-bed contact depend on effective pressure in subglacial drainage N=pice​−pwater​ as well as basal shear stress τ. At fixed τ, reducing N generally enlarges cavities and reduces resistance, giving a sliding law u(τ,N) with uN​<0 in this regime.
A linked cavity drainage system connects these cavities through passages along the bed. Under the prescribed decreasing relation N(Q), the chain rule gives uQ​=uN​N′(Q)>0​. This conclusion uses that hydraulic relation; it need not apply to a different drainage regime.
With t=ετ, the ice continuity equation becomes Hτ​+ε(uH)x​=εs′(x). For bounded spatial gradients over τ=O(1), H is independent of the fast seasonal time to leading order. Meanwhile S′(Q)=εκ gives St​=κQτ​, so
κQτ​+Qx​=M(τ).​
(12)
Let J(τ)=∫0τ​M(r)dr. Direct differentiation proves that
Q(x,τ)=κJ(τ)−J(τ−κx)​​
(13)
satisfies the equation and Q(0,τ)=0. This is the boundary-generated solution, or a periodic-forcing solution with the forcing continued into its past. An independently prescribed initial profile would also need matching in the region whose characteristic curves have not yet reached the upstream boundary.
If M>0, J is invertible and rearranging gives x=[τ−J−1(J(τ)−κQ)]/κ. For M=1−δcosτ with ∣δ∣<1,
J(τ)=τ−δsinτ,J−1(θ)=θ+δsinθ+O(δ2).​
(14)
Substitution, followed by the sine-difference identity, gives
x=Q+κ2δ​sin2κQ​cos(τ−2κQ​)+O(δ2).​
(15)
For fixed Q and nonzero first-order amplitude, the extrema occur at τ=κQ/2+nπ+O(δ). Since x=Q+O(δ), the seasonal phase lag in linked cavity drainage gives the stated phase relation τ≈κx/2(modπ)​. Maxima and minima alternate; when sin(κQ/2)=0, the first-order approximation is flat and does not locate individual extrema.

Write concentrations as E,S,X,Y,Ei​,P in this calculation. The law of mass action assigns the forward bimolecular rate k1​ES and the first-order rates k−1​X,k2​X,k3​Y,k4​Y. Each species gains the rates of reactions producing it and loses those consuming it:
E˙X˙E˙i​​=−k1​ES+k−1​X+k3​Y,=k1​ES−(k−1​+k2​)X,=k4​Y,​S˙Y˙P˙​=−k1​ES+k−1​X,=k2​X−(k3​+k4​)Y,=k3​Y.​​
(16)
Adding the equations for all enzyme-bearing species cancels every reaction rate, so E+X+Y+Ei​=e0​​. This is conservation of the total enzyme-derived material, including the inactivated form. It does not assert conservation of active enzyme: mechanism-based inhibition transfers some of that conserved material irreversibly into Ei​.

For quasi-steady enzyme kinetics at low substrate saturation, the quasi-steady-state approximation is controlled here by ε=e0​/(s0​+Km​), rather than by e0​/s0​ alone. Even with e0​≈s0​, taking Km​≫s0​ makes ε≪1 and σ=s0​/Km​=O(ε).
After the initial fast layer T=O(ε), the third equation gives
y∼(1+σ)(1+ρ)ψσx​=O(ε).
(19)
Since ϕ=O(ε), the last equation gives (ei​)T​=(ϕ/ε)y=O(ε). Starting from ei​(0)=0, this implies ei​=O(ε) on bounded intervals of the slow time. The quasi-steady second equation then gives x=s+O(ε). In the first equation,
sT​=−s+1+ρρ​s+O(ε)=−1+ρs​+O(ε).
(20)
Matching to the initial substrate value s(0)=1 therefore yields s(T)∼e−T/(1+ρ)​. The relation x∼s is an outer approximation: it does not replace the initial layer needed to reconcile x(0)=0 with s(0)=1. Likewise the smallness of accumulated inactivated enzyme has been justified for bounded slow times, not arbitrarily long times.

For the travelling wave coordinate ξ=x+st, the chain rule gives
s(U′+γV′)=μ−U+εU′′,εsV′=f(U,V).​
(21)
Fast-slow matching for a calcium pulse joins two fast transitions, B→C and D→A, joined by slow portions C→D and A→B. Write the nullcline as V=g(U). The resting point has UB​=μ, VB​=g(μ) in the leading matched description.
Put ξ=εX. Multiplying the first equation by ε gives UXX​=s(UX​+γVX​)+ε(U−μ). At leading order, integrate and use UX​→0, (U,V)→(UB​,VB​) at the start of the front. Together with the second equation this gives
UX​=s[U−UB​+γ(V−VB​)],sVX​=f(U,V).​
(22)
The fast equilibria are intersections of V=g(U) and V=VB​−(U−UB​)/γ. As the slope −1/γ changes, there may be one or three simple intersections, with two distinct intersections at a tangency. At a simple point of the nullcline, fU​=−fV​g′, and the sign convention above/below it gives fV​<0. The Jacobian matrix of the fast system has
detJ=fV​−γfU​=fV​(1+γg′),trJ=s+fV​/s.
(23)
For the usual three-intersection configuration, the two outer points have 1+γg′>0, hence negative determinant and one positive and one negative eigenvalue: they are saddle points. The middle point has positive determinant; it is linearly stable if s2<−fV​ and unstable if s2>−fV​. Zero trace is a possible oscillatory stability threshold, with a nonlinear classification requiring further information about f.
To select the front speed, follow the appropriate branch of the unstable manifold of B and vary s until it meets the stable manifold of C. This is a shooting method for a heteroclinic orbit. Follow the high-U slow branch from C to D; the falling front obeys the same fast equations with the integration constant anchored at D. Choose D so that its descending saddle connection reaches a low-U point A at that same speed. The remaining slow branch returns toward the resting point, completing the matched spike. The construction requires the indicated connections; a nullcline sketch alone does not specify their numerical speed or prove their existence for every possible kinetic function f.
On the slow portions, f(U,V)=0 at leading order, so V=g(U) and V′=g′(U)U′. Neglecting the small diffusion term yields
s(1+γg′(U))U′=μ−U.​
(24)
On the outer rising branches the coefficient on the left is positive. Recovery from C toward D requires decreasing U, hence μ<UD​; the low branch from A back toward rest requires increasing U, hence UA​<μ. Therefore UA​<μ<UD​​. These inequalities distinguish the recovery branches from a trajectory that would move away from the required matching points.

Systole is ventricular contraction and ejection; diastole includes ventricular relaxation and filling. Let Va​,Vv​,VLV​ denote arterial, venous and left-ventricular blood volumes. Let Q+ flow through the aortic valve into the arteries, Qc​ through the peripheral circulation, and Q− through the mitral valve into the ventricle. Conservation of volume gives
V˙a​=Q+−Qc​,V˙v​=Qc​−Q−,V˙LV​=Q−−Q+.​
(25)
The same Qc​ leaves arteries and enters veins because the capillary compartment has no changing stored volume in this approximation. Adding the equations conserves total modeled blood volume.
Use constant vascular compliances Ca​,Cv​, with V˙a​=Ca​p˙​a​ and V˙v​=Cv​p˙​v​, and ventricular elastance pLV​=ELV​(t)(VLV​−V0​). The hydraulic resistances and ideal one-way valves give
Qc​=Rc​pa​−pv​​,Q+=Ra​[pLV​−pa​]+​,Q−=Rv​[pv​−pLV​]+​,
(26)
where [z]+=max(z,0). Substitution into the conservation equations gives the two stated arterial/venous pressure equations. Since VLV​−V0​=pLV​/ELV​, differentiating this identity gives the third equation. The positive parts encode valve closure against a reverse pressure difference.
During isovolumetric contraction both valves are closed. Let V+ be the end-diastolic ventricular volume, V0​ its unstressed reference volume, and pa+​ arterial pressure before ejection. The rapidly raised elastance generates Es​(V+−V0​), so the aortic valve opens if Es​(V+−V0​)>pa+​​.
During ejection, Ra​/Rc​≪1 makes the open-valve pressure drop small on the peripheral drainage time scale, so pLV​≈pa​. The mitral valve is closed, and adding arterial and ventricular volume balances eliminates Q+. With pv​≪pa​ this gives
Rc​Ca​p˙​a​+Rc​dtd(pa​/ELV​)​≈−pa​.
(27)
For approximately constant systolic elastance, set Cs​=1/Es​ and reset time to the start of the slow ejection phase. Then
pa​(t)≈p^​a​exp[−Rc​(Ca​+Cs​)t​].​
(28)
Here p^​a​ is the matched pressure at that start. If peripheral outflow is negligible during the preceding rapid artery-ventricle pressure equilibration, conservation gives the more explicit p^​a​=[Ca​pa+​+V+−V0​]/(Ca​+Cs​).
After aortic closure the ventricular volume is fixed at the end-systolic value V−. Relaxation decreases ELV​ until the mitral valve opens at pLV​=pv​, hence ELV​∼pv​/(V−−V0​)​, with pv​ the venous filling pressure at opening. During filling the aortic valve is closed, so Rc​Ca​p˙​a​=−(pa​−pv​)≈−pa​. With pa−​ the arterial pressure at the start of filling and time reset there,
pa​(t)≈pa−​e−t/(Rc​Ca​).​
(29)
The change from Ca​+Cs​ to Ca​ is physical: the ejecting ventricle shares the arterial pressure during systole, whereas a closed aortic valve disconnects it during filling.

In this delayed carbon-dioxide ventilation feedback model, p is a representative carbon-dioxide partial pressure, K an effective storage capacitance, M>0 metabolic production, and pV removal by pulmonary ventilation. The delay represents transport and sensing between the gas-exchange compartment and the ventilatory response. The Hill equation response rises sigmoidally with pressure, reaches half its maximum α at p=θ, and saturates at α; n>1 permits a steep threshold-like response.
For positive parameters, pV(p) increases strictly from zero to infinity because its derivative is V+pV′>0. Its graph therefore intersects the horizontal line M exactly once: M=p∗V(p∗),p∗>0​. The figure shows this argument and the first imaginary-root crossing.
For a small perturbation z=p−p∗, linearization gives
z˙=−Az(t)−Bz(t−τ),A=V(p∗)/K,B=p∗V′(p∗)/K.
(30)
The sigmoidal delayed ventilation threshold follows from the characteristic equation of a delay differential equation, λ+A+Be−λτ=0. At an imaginary root λ=iω, separate real and imaginary parts:
A+Bcos(ωτ)=0,ω−Bsin(ωτ)=0.
(31)
For B>A>0, the first positive crossing has
ωc​=B2−A2​,τc​=B2−A2​arccos(−A/B)​.​
(32)
At zero delay the scalar equation is stable. No root can cross the imaginary axis before this first crossing, and zero itself is not a root. To check the crossing direction, implicit differentiation gives
λ′=−1+τ(λ+A)λ(λ+A)​,Reλ′(τc​)=(1+Aτc​)2+ωc2​τc2​ωc2​​>0.​
(33)
All subsequent imaginary crossings have the same direction. Thus the equilibrium is linearly stable below τc​ and unstable above it. This establishes the oscillatory linear threshold; the full nonlinear Hopf bifurcation additionally requires the usual nondegeneracy conditions. If B≤A, no finite imaginary-root crossing occurs and the equilibrium is stable for every finite delay. For the stated response, B/A=nθn/(θn+(p∗)n), showing explicitly when the gain can exceed instantaneous damping.
If A≪B, then ωc​≈B and ωc​τc​≈π/2. Consequently the small-amplitude period near onset is 2π/ωc​≈4τc​​. The comparison is to the critical delay near the first instability, rather than an arbitrary delay far beyond onset. A longer circulatory transit time can move the physiological system across this delayed-feedback threshold, providing a mechanism for periodic breathing associated with impaired cardiac output.
