Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution (s=kB​T/m​)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Quantum ideal-gas statistics
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 6
The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution describes the random velocities and speeds of particles in a classical ideal gas at thermal equilibrium. Individual particles keep changing velocity through collisions, while the equilibrium probability distribution stays fixed. For one species of particle mass m at positive temperature T, the characteristic scale is
s=mkB​T​​,
(54)
where kB​ is the Boltzmann constant. A signed velocity component follows a normal distribution; the nonnegative magnitude follows the Maxwell speed distribution. Their peaks differ because a speed measurement groups together all directions of motion.
Use the gas rest frame, three spatial dimensions, and nonrelativistic particles, with kB​T≪mc2 where c is the speed of light. Assume a dilute gas whose interactions between encounters can be neglected in the ideal gas approximation and whose translational motion is classical. A sufficient condition for negligible quantum exchange effects is nλ3≪1, where n is the number density and λ the thermal de Broglie wavelength. Diluteness alone does not guarantee thermal equilibrium; the gas must have equilibrated. These are the assumptions behind the classical Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution. [ref] [ref]
The connection to Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics is the Boltzmann factor for translational kinetic energy, Ekin​=m∣v∣2/2. Normalizing e−Ekin​/(kB​T) over velocity gives a probability density; an occupation factor e(μ−E)/(kB​T) instead specifies a mean occupation, with chemical potential μ. Their normalizations serve different purposes. [ref]
This connects microscopic motion to measurements: a gas thermometer uses the ideal gas law, collisions and the mean free path govern transport, and measurements of spectral lines use thermal Doppler broadening to infer temperature. Each probes a different aspect of the same thermal motion.
In 1860, James Clerk Maxwell presented the component and speed laws in Part I, Proposition IV of Illustrations of the Dynamical Theory of Gases, pp. 22–23. His argument combined independence of the three velocity components with isotropy, and he explicitly compared the component law with the normal distribution of observational errors. Independence is an assumption of that argument; perpendicular coordinate axes alone do not establish it. [ref]
