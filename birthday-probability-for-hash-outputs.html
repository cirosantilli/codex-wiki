Birthday probability for hash outputs (pM​(n)=1−∏j=0n−1​(1−j/M))

How soon should a hash collision appear? Specify the probability distribution of the outputs first. Evaluate n distinct keys and assume their hash values Y1​,…,Yn​ are independent random variables, each with the uniform distribution on a finite set of M≥1 possible outputs. The randomness may come from an idealized randomly chosen hash function; a fixed hash function and a fixed list of keys have a definite outcome. Sampling the same key twice creates a repeated hash value without creating a hash collision, so distinctness of the queried keys matters.
This is the existing birthday problem, with hash values in place of birthdays. Let pM​(n) be the probability of at least one equal pair. The exact formula is
pM​(n)=⎩⎨⎧​1−j=0∏n−1​(1−Mj​),1,​0≤n≤M,n>M.​
(15)
The empty product is 1, so pM​(0)=pM​(1)=0. Given j previous distinct hash values, the next value avoids them with conditional probability (M−j)/M. Multiply these conditional chances and subtract from 1. Equivalently, for n≤M, exactly M(M−1)⋯(M−n+1) of the Mn equally likely ordered outcomes have no repeated value. This derivation also explains why independence of all outputs, rather than merely uniform individual values, is required.[ref]
For M=256, the exact hash collision probabilities are p256​(19)≈0.495742, p256​(20)≈0.533167, and p256​(32)≈0.867643. Thus only 20 distinct queries already exceed a one-half chance of some pair matching. The striking feature is the number of opportunities: n queries supply (2n​) pairs. By linearity of expectation, the expected value of the number of equal pairs is λ=(2n​)/M. This expectation counts pairs, not the probability of at least one pair; three equal outputs contribute three pairs in one outcome. The union bound gives pM​(n)≤min(1,λ).
Using the natural logarithm expansion log(1−u)=−u+O(u2) in the exact product gives
pM​(n)≈1−exp(−2Mn(n−1)​).
(16)
For n≤M/2, the omitted correction in the logarithm of the no-collision probability is O(n3/M2). Thus the approximation is controlled when n3/M2 is small; in particular it becomes accurate at the birthday scale n proportional to M​ as M increases. The inequality 1−u≤e−u also shows that the displayed approximation is a lower bound for pM​(n) when n≤M. Setting its exponent to log2 gives the approximate half-probability sample size n≈2Mlog2​ for large M.
For comparison, under the same independent uniform model the probability of hitting one previously fixed digest t in n queries is
qM​(n)=1−(1−M1​)n.
(17)
Each query supplies one chance to hit t, instead of a growing number of possible pairs. For M=256, q256​(20)≈0.075293, and the first integer n with q256​(n)≥1/2 is 178. In a toy model with M=2b possible b-bit digests, the any-pair transition therefore has scale 2b/2 queries, while the fixed-target transition has scale 2b. This explains the birthday-search distinction between collision resistance and preimage resistance.[ref]
The assumptions are essential. Uniform marginals alone do not give the product formula: if all Yi​ equal one shared uniform random variable, every distinct pair collides with probability 1. A universal hash family bounds each pair's hash collision probability, but that condition alone does not supply joint independence of every output. Nor does a fixed output length establish collision resistance or preimage resistance: the toy h(k)=kmod16 has h(k)=h(k+16), making deliberate hash collisions immediate. The curves describe the specified model and do not establish the security of a concrete cryptographic hash function.
