Goldstone theorem

 Home Physics Branches of physics Quantum field theory Spontaneous symmetry breaking
OurBigBook.com Words: 637 Articles: 6
In a local quantum field theory obeying special relativity, with a positive physical state space, a conserved continuous internal global symmetry in field theory and a selected Lorentz-invariant ground state, a broken generator requires a massless Goldstone boson. For G→H, there are dimG−dimH symmetry-required Goldstone bosons in this setting; additional accidental massless excitations are possible. Jeffrey Goldstone's institutional biography identifies his work with Abdus Salam and Steven Weinberg on the zero-mass theorem.[ref]
The Goldstone directions in the scalar mass matrix give a short classical proof at a smooth minimum. The quantum argument uses a conserved current jμ and a scalar local operator O whose expectation changes under the broken generator. The Ward identity for the current–local operator correlation function then has a nonzero contact term. After a Fourier transform, its divergence remains nonzero as four-momentum tends to zero. Lorentz invariance forces the vector–scalar correlator to have the form pμF(p2), so F must have a pole at p2=0. The spectral representation associates that pole with a physical zero-mass excitation. A properly regulated spatial integral of the conserved current implements the broken generator; a well-defined global charge acting on the broken ground state cannot simply be assumed.
The hypotheses explain the boundaries of the result. A discrete symmetry has no infinitesimal generator and need not give a Goldstone boson. A gauge redundancy does not supply the physical global generator assumed here. In nonrelativistic matter the type-B Goldstone boson shows why one cannot universally count one independent mode per broken generator. Long-wavelength fluctuations can also prevent an assumed broken state from existing, as in the Mermin-Wagner theorem.
