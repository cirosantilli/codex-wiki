Selective biochemical reaction pathways

 Home Chemistry Biochemistry
OurBigBook.com Words: 333
An enzyme accelerates particular chemical reactions, with catalytic selectivity arising from recognition and from the relative ease of the subsequent chemistry. Strong binding alone does not guarantee fast conversion. Nor does catalysis change the chemical equilibrium for an unchanged net chemical reaction. These distinctions explain how enzymes can direct the flow of material without creating an extra source of free energy.[ref]
For an original model of competing routes, let a substrate S become either a desired reaction product P or another reaction product Q:
SkP​​P,SkQ​​Q.

The labels represent hypothetical chemical species with the same elemental composition, not molecular structures. Assume fixed volume, irreversible first-order reactions, constant effective rate constants, and initially 100μmol of S with neither reaction product present. The model approximates a limited operating regime; real enzyme kinetics can include saturation and reverse reactions.
The reaction-rate equations give dnS​/dt=−(kP​+kQ​)nS​, dnP​/dt=kP​nS​ and dnQ​/dt=kQ​nS​, where n denotes amount of substance. Consequently,
nS​=n0​e−(kP​+kQ​)t,nP​=kP​+kQ​kP​​(n0​−nS​),nQ​=kP​+kQ​kQ​​(n0​−nS​).
(7)
With kP​=0.08 and kQ​=0.02min−1, the eventual split is 80μmol of P and 20μmol of Q. At ten minutes, the calculated amounts are approximately 36.8, 50.6 and 12.6μmol for S, P and Q. Selectively increasing kP​ fourfold changes the eventual desired fraction to 0.32/0.34=16/17≈94.1%. Increasing both rate constants fourfold instead preserves the 80% split and only shortens the conversion time. Speed and selectivity answer different questions.
The bookkeeping matters independently of that model. In ideal alcoholic fermentation, complete conversion of 3mmol of glucose yields 6mmol each of ethanol and carbon dioxide. Of the initial 18mmol of carbon chemical atoms, 12 remain in ethanol and 6 enter carbon dioxide. This is a chosen material-balance example using the net stoichiometry explained under Saccharomyces cerevisiae; it gives neither an observed yield nor the mechanism of the individual steps. Equal amounts of the two reaction products also do not mean equal masses.
