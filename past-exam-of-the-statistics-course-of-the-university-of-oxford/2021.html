A likelihood-ratio test rejects for small λ. The derivative of the natural logarithm of its variable factor is
dtd​{nlogt+mlog(1−t)}=t(1−t)n−(n+m)t​.
(16)
Thus tn(1−t)m increases up to n/(n+m) and decreases thereafter, tending to zero at both endpoints. Every nontrivial lower level set of λ therefore consists of two tails. For a prescribed test size 0<α<1, the exact critical region is
T<k1​orT>k2​​,0<k1​<n+mn​<k2​<1.
(17)
Writing Fn,m​ for the cumulative distribution function of the beta distribution with parameters (n,m), the required two equations are
k1n​(1−k1​)mFn,m​(k1​)+1−Fn,m​(k2​)​=k2n​(1−k2​)m,=α.​​
(18)
The first makes both cutoffs have the same likelihood ratio; the second calibrates the null hypothesis rejection probability. Equivalently, the second left-hand side is
B(n,m)1​{∫0k1​​tn−1(1−t)m−1dt+∫k2​1​tn−1(1−t)m−1dt}.
(19)
As the common likelihood ratio threshold increases from zero to one, this probability increases continuously and strictly from zero to one; hence the equations determine unique cutoffs. The two tail probabilities need not each equal α/2 when n=m. Exact calibration holds for every common rate because the given null distribution of T is independent of that rate. Boundary equalities have zero probability.

False. A test size constrains the probability of rejection conditional on the null hypothesis being true:
PH0​​(reject H0​)=α
(20)
for an exact size-α test of a simple hypothesis. It does not assign a posterior probability to that statistical hypothesis after observing nonrejection. In the frequentist interpretation, the statistical parameter is fixed and the data are random. Let S=∑i​Xi​, which has a binomial distribution with parameters (n,p). For 0<p1​<1/2, the alternative-to-null likelihood ratio of the independent Bernoulli trials is
R(S)=(1/2)np1S​(1−p1​)n−S​=[2(1−p1​)]n(1−p1​p1​​)S.
(22)
Since p1​/(1−p1​)<1, this is strictly decreasing in S. A threshold on large R is therefore a lower-tail likelihood-ratio test, and a threshold between R(2) and R(1) gives the stated critical region S<2. The Neyman-Pearson lemma also proves it is a most powerful test at its attained test size.
Using the binomial probability mass, its test size and statistical power are respectively
P1/2​(S<2)=2n1+n​​,Pp1​​(S<2)=(1−p1​)n+np1​(1−p1​)n−1​.
(23)
The associated power function is π(p)=(1−p)n+np(1−p)n−1. These formulas hold for positive integer n; when n=1 the test always rejects and has size one. If the boundary value p1​=0 is admitted, all alternative probability is at S=0; the same test still has statistical power one and can be represented using a threshold with ties in the likelihood ratio.

Condition on the total of 400 complaints. Assume each complaint independently has the same probability p of being received on Monday; in particular, repeated complaints from one incident must not create material dependence. The Monday count S then has a binomial distribution with parameters (400,p). Test H0​:p=0.2 against H1​:p>0.2, rejecting for large S as suggested by the monotone likelihood ratio.
Under the null hypothesis, E0​S=80 and Var0​(S)=400(0.2)(0.8)=64. Both expected successes and expected failures are large, so the central limit theorem gives a suitable normal approximation:
Z=8S−80​ ∼˙ N(0,1),zobs​=8100−80​=2.5​.
(27)
Using the paper's supplied rounded table, the upper-tail p-value is approximately
papprox​=1−Φ(2.5)≈0.005​.
(28)
A more accurate evaluation of the standard normal distribution cumulative distribution function gives about 0.0062. For example, choose significance level 0.05 before examining the data; the supplied upper 5% standard normal quantile is approximately 1.6, and 2.5>1.6. Reject H0​ at 5%, and also at 1%, using this approximation. The observed binomial proportion is 100/400=0.25, providing evidence that the Monday probability exceeds 0.2 under the sampling assumptions.
If applying a continuity correction, approximate P0​(S≥100) by 1−Φ((99.5−80)/8)=1−Φ(2.4375)≈0.0074; this gives the same 5% and 1% conclusions. The uncorrected value 0.005 is approximate, so a decision exactly at the 0.5% boundary is not warranted by the supplied rounded table.

The prior mean 1/(β+1) decreases as β increases. More strongly, its cumulative distribution function and upper-tail probability are
Fβ​(t)=1−(1−t)β,Pβ​(θ>t)=(1−t)β,0<t<1.

dβdλ​=(N+β+1)2N​>0​,dβdθ1​​=−(N+β+1)2S+1​<0​.

Thus the prior weight increases and the posterior mean decreases. Their limits are λ→1 and θ1​→0. Both the changing prior mean and the increasing weight contribute to the change in the posterior mean.

Put M0​=α+x0​β. As a linear combination of independent normal random variables under the posterior distribution,
M0​∣y∼N(m0​,v0​),m0​=mα​+x0​mβ​,v0​=vα​+x02​vβ​.
(47)

[mα​+x0​mβ​−Φ−1(0.975)vα​+x02​vβ​​,mα​+x0​mβ​+Φ−1(0.975)vα​+x02​vβ​​]​.

The normal distribution is symmetric and its density strictly decreases with distance from its mean, so this probability is uniquely maximized when the midpoint is m0​. The target is the experiment's mean at x0​. A posterior predictive distribution for a new noisy observation would instead have variance v0​+σ2; that extra observation variance does not belong in the requested interval.

An unknown variance requires a joint prior distribution for (α,β,σ2). The analysis is not determined until a prior for the unknown variance, and its relation to the coefficients, is specified. A maximum-likelihood estimate substituted for σ2 does not by itself account for its posterior uncertainty.
For example, retain the independent coefficient prior distributions from part (b) and independently assign s=σ2 an inverse-gamma distribution with shape a0​>0 and scale b0​>0, whose probability density function is proportional to s−a0​−1e−b0​/s. Then the joint posterior density is proportional to
s−a0​−1−n/2exp{−sb0​+21​∑i​(yi​−α−βxi​)2​−2τ02​(α−α0​)2​−2τ12​(β−β0​)2​}.
(49)
Conditional on s, the two coefficient posterior distributions are still the independent normal distributions in part (b), with s substituted for σ2. The other full conditional distribution is
s∣α,β,y∼InvGamma(a0​+2n​,b0​+21​∑i​(yi​−α−βxi​)2).
(50)
The marginal coefficient posterior distribution requires marginalization over s; it is generally nonnormal and the two coefficients need not remain independent. In particular, the posterior cumulative distribution function of the requested mean becomes
P(M0​≤t∣y)=∫0∞​Φ(vα​(s)+x02​vβ​(s)​t−mα​(s)−x0​mβ​(s)​)π(s∣y)ds.
(51)
Its shortest 95% credible interval must be found from this marginal posterior distribution, for example by numerical integration or posterior simulation. This is normal regression with independent coefficient and variance priors.
A different, convenient choice is a normal-inverse-gamma prior, in which the conditional coefficient prior covariance matrix is proportional to σ2. That choice gives a multivariate Student's t-distribution after marginalization, so the interval uses its univariate Student's t-distribution quantiles. It changes the coefficient prior specification; a Student interval does not follow automatically from retaining the fixed-τ0​,τ1​ priors in part (b).
