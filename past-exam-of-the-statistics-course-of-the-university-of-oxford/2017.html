For a Bernoulli distribution with fixed 0<p<1, the mean and variance are p and p(1−p). The odds transformation g(x)=x/(1−x) has derivative g′(x)=(1−x)−2. Applying the delta method from part (ii),
1−XX​∼˙N(1−pp​,n(1−p)3p​).​
(8)
More precisely, n​{X/(1−X)−p/(1−p)} converges to a normal distribution of mean zero and variance p/(1−p)3, after defining the statistic arbitrarily on X=1. That exceptional event has probability pn→0, so this extension does not change the limit. The unextended ratio is undefined there and has no finite exact variance if interpreted as infinite. At p=0 it is zero almost surely; at p=1 the denominator is always zero. This is the delta method for Bernoulli odds.

The Neyman-Pearson lemma concerns a simple hypothesis H0​:θ=θ0​ against another simple hypothesis H1​:θ=θ1​. For a continuous statistical sample, put fj​(x)=∏i​f(xi​;θj​) for the joint probability density functions, j=0,1. A randomized statistical test is a measurable ϕ(x)∈[0,1], the conditional probability of rejection after observing x. Its test size is E0​ϕ and its statistical power is E1​ϕ.
Choose k≥0 and boundary randomization so that
ϕ∗​(x)=⎩⎨⎧​1,0,γ(x)∈[0,1],​f1​(x)>kf0​(x),f1​(x)<kf0​(x),f1​(x)=kf0​(x),​E0​ϕ∗​=α.
(23)
Then ϕ∗​ is most powerful among all tests of size at most α. Thus one rejects for sufficiently large alternative-to-null likelihood ratio. If the equality set has zero probability under H0​, the test needs no randomization. Continuous observations alone do not guarantee that their likelihood ratio has a continuous probability distribution.
For the proof, let ϕ be any competing test with E0​ϕ≤α. At every x,
(ϕ∗​(x)−ϕ(x))(f1​(x)−kf0​(x))≥0.
(24)
Indeed, on f1​>kf0​ the first factor is 1−ϕ≥0; on f1​<kf0​ it is −ϕ≤0; and on equality the product is zero. Integration gives
E1​ϕ∗​−E1​ϕ≥k(E0​ϕ∗​−E0​ϕ)≥0,
(25)
which proves the claimed optimality. Moreover, when k>0, equality of statistical power forces equality of test size and forces ϕ=ϕ∗​ almost everywhere where f1​=kf0​: otherwise the nonnegative integrand would have positive integral. This proves the usual necessity assertion as well, up to equality sets and sets of zero probability under both hypotheses.

θ0​2S​∼χ2n2​.

L(θ,μ)=θ−nμ−mexp(−A/θ−B/μ).

For a specified posterior probability 1−α, an interval I=(θ1​,θ2​) is a highest posterior density interval if
∫θ1​θ2​​π(θ∣x)dθ=1−α
(39)
and every included point has posterior density at least as large as every excluded point, apart from boundary and null-set choices. Equivalently, I is a level set {θ:π(θ∣x)>k}, with possible choices on its boundary, whose posterior probability is 1−α.
This selects the most densely supported values of the statistical parameter. When the relevant level set is one interval and has no plateau of positive probability, it is the shortest credible interval of that posterior probability. With several separated peaks, the corresponding highest density region can instead be disconnected, so an interval need not exist. The definition is in the stated parameter coordinate: posterior density ordering can change under a nonlinear change of parameter.

Put A=a+s>1 and B=b+n−s>1 as in part (a), and let FA,B​ be the cumulative distribution function of the beta distribution Beta(A,B). The two endpoint equations are
FA,B​(θ2​)−FA,B​(θ1​)θ1A−1​(1−θ1​)B−1​=0.95,=θ2A−1​(1−θ2​)B−1.​​

This is the beta highest posterior density endpoint characterization.

I(θ)=Eθ​[{∂θ​logf(X∣θ)}2]=∑x​f(x∣θ){∂θ​f(x∣θ)}2​.

The Jeffreys prior is invariant under smooth one-to-one reparameterization. This means that applying the rule directly in a new parameter η=h(θ) gives the same prior distribution as first constructing the rule for θ and then transforming its prior density by the change-of-variable formula.
More explicitly, for a differentiable invertible change of parameter with nonzero derivative,
Iη​(η)=Iθ​(θ)(dηdθ​)2,πJ,η​(η)∝Iη​(η)​=Iθ​(θ)​​dηdθ​​.
(45)
Thus πJ,η​(η)dη and πJ,θ​(θ)dθ represent the same measure, up to the normalization constant. Invariance concerns the measure: the numerical values of the prior densities need not be equal in the two coordinates. The same change of variables then makes the posterior distributions agree. This is the reparameterization invariance of Jeffreys prior.
