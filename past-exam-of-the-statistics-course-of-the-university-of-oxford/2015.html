The given probability density function is that of a beta distribution with parameters r and n−r+1. We can derive the required moments directly rather than assuming the beta moment formulas. For an integer j≥0, the Euler beta function gives
E[U(r)j​]​=(r−1)!(n−r)!n!​∫01​ur+j−1(1−u)n−rdu=(r−1)!(n+j)!n!(r+j−1)!​.​
(1)
Here B(a,b)=(a−1)!(b−1)!/(a+b−1)! for positive integers a,b; this identity also follows by repeated integration by parts. Taking j=1,2 yields
E[U(r)​]=n+1r​,E[U(r)2​]=(n+1)(n+2)r(r+1)​.
(2)
Subtract the square of the first moment from the second to obtain the variance:
E[U(r)​]=n+1r​,Var(U(r)​)=(n+1)2(n+2)r(n−r+1)​.​
(3)
These are exact finite-sample identities for each uniform order statistic.

Continuity and strict increase make the quantile function F−1 well-defined on (0,1). The endpoints of the uniform distribution occur with probability zero and do not affect the construction. For every real x,
Pr(Xi​≤x)​=Pr(F−1(Ui​)≤x)=Pr(Ui​≤F(x))=F(x).​
(5)
Thus each Xi​ has cumulative distribution function F. Applying a fixed measurable function separately to independent random variables preserves their independence; explicitly, joint lower-tail probabilities factor as
Pr(X1​≤x1​,…,Xn​≤xn​)=∏i=1n​F(xi​).
(6)
Consequently
X1​,…,Xn​ are independent and identically distributed with CDF F.​
(7)
This is inverse transform sampling. Since F−1 preserves order, it also sends the central order statistic to the central order statistic, justifying MX​=F−1(MU​).

First establish the limiting law needed by the delta method. For fixed t, put un​=1/2+t/n​ and let Nn​ count how many of the Ui​ are at most un​. Then Nn​ has a binomial distribution with expectation n/2+tn​ and variance n/4−t2. The event MU​≤un​ is exactly Nn​≥(n+1)/2. Its standardized threshold tends to −2t, so the central limit theorem for these binomial counts gives
Pr(n​(MU​−1/2)≤t)⟶Φ(2t).
(8)
Here Φ is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution. Equivalently,
n​(MU​−1/2)d​N(0,1/4).
(9)
Use the usual local regularity for the delta method: f is continuous at the population median θ and f(θ)>0. The inverse function theorem, applied at F(θ)=1/2, gives
dud​F−1(u)​u=1/2​=f(θ)1​.
(10)
Therefore
n​(MX​−θ)d​N(0,4f(θ)21​).​
(11)
The asymptotic normality gives the requested leading variance approximation
avar(MX​)=4nf(θ)21​.​
(12)
Here the variance parameter for the rescaled limiting law is 1/(4f(θ)2), while the source states the corresponding 1/n approximation for the unscaled estimator. Strict increase of F alone does not force f(θ)>0; that local condition is needed for this form of the asymptotic distribution of a sample median.

For the normal distribution, the density at its centre is f(θ)=1/(σ2π​). The sample mean has exact variance σ2/n, whereas part (b) gives the sample median the leading variance πσ2/(2n). Thus the requested asymptotic relative efficiency is
ARE(MX​,X)=π2​≈0.637for the normal model.​
(13)
At a fixed confidence level, a confidence interval based on the sample mean has a leading half-width smaller by the factor 2/π​. With known σ, X±z1−α/2​σ/n​ also has exact normal coverage. Prefer the sample mean under the normal model.
For the Laplace distribution, symmetry puts both the mean and median at θ. Its density there is 1/(2σ), and
Var(Xi​)=σ1​∫0∞​y2e−y/σdy=2σ2.
(14)
Consequently the sample mean has variance 2σ2/n, while the sample median has leading variance σ2/n. Hence
ARE(MX​,X)=2for the Laplace model.​
(15)
A large-sample confidence interval based on the sample median has leading half-width smaller by 1/2​; with known scale it is MX​±z1−α/2​σ/n​. Prefer the sample median under the Laplace model. Consistent estimation of an unknown scale preserves these leading comparisons. The reason for the reversal is quantitative: the median's precision depends on the central density f(θ), while the sample mean's precision depends on the whole population variance.

Let L(ϑ;x) be the likelihood function, with full parameter space Θ and null hypothesis ϑ∈Θ0​⊆Θ. The generalized likelihood-ratio test compares the best null fit to the best unrestricted fit:
R(x)=supϑ∈Θ​L(ϑ;x)supϑ∈Θ0​​L(ϑ;x)​,Λ(x)=−2logR(x).
(16)
The likelihood ratio R lies between zero and one, and small values count against the null hypothesis; equivalently, large values of the likelihood-ratio test statistic Λ count against it.
Compute restricted and unrestricted maximum-likelihood estimates, evaluate their log-likelihoods, and form Λ=2(ℓ(ϑ)−ℓ(ϑ0​)). Under the regular conditions of Wilks theorem, the null limit is
Λd​χd2​,d=dimΘ−dimΘ0​.
(17)
These statistical degrees of freedom count independent parameter restrictions. The regularity includes a locally identifiable smooth model, nonsingular Fisher information, and an interior null value; parameter-dependent support can invalidate the result.
For a chosen significance level α, obtain the (1−α) quantile qd,1−α​ of the chi-squared distribution and use
reject H0​ if Λobs​>qd,1−α​.​
(18)
Equivalently, compare the asymptotic p-value Pr(χd2​≥Λobs​) to α. This is a large-sample calibration; part (c) requires direct treatment because its support depends on a parameter.

The count vector has a multinomial distribution, with likelihood function
L(π)=∏i​ni​!n!​∏i=1k​πini​​,∑i​πi​=1.
(19)
Maximize its log-likelihood using a Lagrange multiplier for the sum constraint. The equations ni​/πi​=λ and ∑i​πi​=1 give λ=n and πi​=ni​/n. Since all counts are positive and the log-likelihood is strictly concave on the interior of the probability simplex, this is the global maximum-likelihood estimate.
Under the uniform null hypothesis, each category has expected count
ei​=nπi,0​=kn​.​
(20)
The common multinomial coefficient cancels from the likelihood ratio:
R=∏i​(ni​/n)ni​k−n​=∏i=1k​(ni​ei​​)ni​.
(21)
Taking minus twice its logarithm gives exactly
Λ=2i=1∑k​ni​log(ei​ni​​).​
(22)
There are k−1 free category probabilities, because their sum is one, and the null fixes all of them. Thus its regular asymptotic chi-squared distribution has k−1 statistical degrees of freedom.

For four categories the number of statistical degrees of freedom is 4−1=3. The supplied 5% critical value is therefore q3​=7.82, and 7.3<7.82. Hence
Do not reject equal category probabilities at the 5% level.​
(23)
The approximate p-value is Pr(χ32​≥7.3)≈0.063. This conclusion assumes the large-sample multinomial approximation is adequate, in particular sufficiently large expected category counts. It means there is insufficient evidence against the uniform null hypothesis, rather than establishing that the probabilities are equal.

Write a=x(1)​=mini​xi​, T=∑i​xi​ and x=T/n. For nondegenerate data from the shifted exponential distribution, the likelihood function is
L(θ,β)=β−nexp[−βT−nθ​]1{0≤θ≤a}​.
(24)
For fixed feasible θ, differentiating its log-likelihood with respect to β gives
−βn​+β2T−nθ​=0,β​(θ)=x−θ.
(25)
The derivative changes from positive to negative there. Substituting gives the profile likelihood e−n(x−θ)−n, which increases with θ. Therefore the unrestricted maximum-likelihood estimates are
θ=a,β​=x−a,
(26)
while under the null hypothesis θ=0 the estimate is β​=x. Their likelihood ratio is
R=(x−a)−ne−nx−ne−n​=(1−xa​)n.
(27)
Since the likelihood-ratio test rejects for small R, the requested form is obtained with
g(x1​,…,xn​)=xx(1)​​=∑i​xi​nmini​xi​​,C={x:(1−g(x))n≤k}.​
(28)
The formulas assume x>a, which holds almost surely for a continuous sample with n≥2; coincident observations give an unbounded unrestricted likelihood and can be treated by its supremum.
An exact calibration is available and also explains why Wilks theorem should not be applied here. Under the null, the memoryless property makes A=nX(1)​ and D=∑i​(Xi​−X(1)​) independent gamma distributions with common scale β and shapes 1 and n−1. By beta sampling by gamma ratios,
1−g=A+DD​∼Beta(n−1,1).
(29)
Consequently, for 0≤k≤1,
Pr0​(R≤k)=k(n−1)/n,kα​=αn/(n−1).​
(30)
This yields an exact test size α, independent of the unknown scale. The support endpoint and boundary null make this a likelihood-ratio test for an exponential support endpoint, with a different calibration from a regular one-parameter test.

Let p0​=Pr(θ∈Θ0​) and p1​=1−p0​, assuming both are positive. The prior odds are α=p0​/p1​. Given data x, the posterior odds are
O01​(x)=Pr(H1​∣x)Pr(H0​∣x)​.
(31)
With conditional joint likelihood L(θ;x)=∏i​f(xi​∣θ), define the hypothesis-conditional marginal densities
m0​(x)=p0​1​∫Θ0​​L(θ;x)π(dθ),m1​(x)=p1​1​∫Θ∖Θ0​​L(θ;x)π(dθ).
(32)
The Bayes factor for H0​ relative to H1​ is B=m0​(x)/m1​(x). The normalizations p0​,p1​ matter: the prior distribution within each hypothesis must have total probability one. Bayes' theorem now gives
O01​(x)=p1​m1​(x)p0​m0​(x)​=αB.
(33)
Converting posterior odds to posterior probability therefore gives
Pr(H0​∣x)=1+αBαB​.​

Take σ,τ>0 to be the positive standard deviations. Multiply the normal likelihood function by the normal prior density. Terms independent of θ can be absorbed into the normalizing constant:
π(θ∣x)∝exp[−2σ21​∑i​(xi​−θ)2−2τ2θ2​].
(35)
The identity ∑i​(xi​−θ)2=∑i​(xi​−x)2+n(θ−x)2 makes the quadratic term explicit. Completing the square gives
π(θ∣x)∝exp[−2vn​(θ−μn​)2​],
(36)
where
vn​=(σ2n​+τ21​)−1=σ2+nτ2σ2τ2​,μn​=σ2+nτ2nτ2​x.​
(37)
Thus θ∣x∼N(μn​,vn​). This is normal-normal conjugacy: prior and data precisions add, and the posterior mean shrinks the sample mean towards the prior mean zero.

The symmetric continuous prior distribution gives Pr(θ<0)=Pr(θ≥0)=1/2, so the prior odds are one. Therefore the Bayes factor equals the posterior odds. Standardizing the normal posterior distribution, put
z=vn​​μn​​=σσ2+nτ2​nτx​.
