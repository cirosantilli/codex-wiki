Write I1​(θ) for the Fisher information in one observation:
I1​(θ)=Eθ​[{∂θ∂​logf(X;θ)}2]=−Eθ​[∂θ2∂2​logf(X;θ)].
(1)

At the conventional 5% significance level, Student 1's p-value 0.72 gives no rejection of equal mean wear, while Student 2's p-value 0.009 gives rejection of equal mean wear. The latter also rejects at the 1% significance level. More generally, compare each p-value with the prespecified level α; no decision threshold was fixed in the data supplied.
Failure to reject does not establish equality, and a p-value is not the posterior probability that the null hypothesis is true. These two-sided p-values alone also do not identify which material has larger mean wear.

c OurBigBook.com
Solution
Words: 142
iii OurBigBook.com
Prefer Student 2's paired analysis, because the observational units are matched pairs: two shoes exposed to the same child's activity. A paired t-test uses Yi​=Ai​−Bi​, so shared child-to-child variation is removed before estimating the standard error. The independent units are the ten pairs, giving 9 statistical degrees of freedom under the model of independent normally distributed pair differences.
The role of covariance is explicit:
Var(Ai​−Bi​)=Var(Ai​)+Var(Bi​)−2Cov(Ai​,Bi​).
(13)
With common marginal variance σ2 and within-pair correlation coefficient ρ, the true variance of the average difference is 2σ2(1−ρ)/10. The independent-groups calculation ignores the covariance term and uses 2σ2/10. Positive within-pair association can therefore make the paired standard error much smaller, explaining how the reported p-values can differ without arithmetic mistakes. The preference follows from the paired design, not from selecting the smaller p-value. Marginal normality alone need not imply normally distributed differences; exact paired inference uses the normal-difference assumption, for example justified by a joint multivariate normal distribution.

For two simple statistical hypotheses with joint probability density functions g0​ and g1​, fix a significance level α. A randomized statistical test φ(x) is the conditional probability of rejection given the data. Choose k≥0 and, if necessary, randomization on the equality set so that
φ∗​(x)=⎩⎨⎧​1,0,γ(x)∈[0,1],​g1​(x)>kg0​(x),g1​(x)<kg0​(x),g1​(x)=kg0​(x),​E0​φ∗​=α.
(14)
The Neyman-Pearson lemma says that this is a most powerful test among all tests with test size at most α:
E1​φ≤E1​φ∗​wheneverE0​φ≤α.​
(15)
Thus the optimal ordering rejects where the alternative-to-null likelihood ratio is largest. On null-impossible observations with g1​>0, the rejection probability is one. Randomization is dispensable whenever an ordinary threshold already attains the required test size.

For a random sample, write gj​(x)=∏i=1n​f(xi​;θj​), j=0,1, for its joint probability density functions. Let φ be any competing statistical test of level at most α. Pointwise,
(φ∗​−φ)(g1​−kg0​)≥0.
(16)
Indeed, where g1​>kg0​, φ∗​=1≥φ; where g1​<kg0​, φ∗​=0≤φ, so both factors have the same sign. On the equality set the product is zero, regardless of randomization. Integrate over the joint sample space using its ordinary volume measure:
0​≤∫(φ∗​−φ)(g1​−kg0​)dx=E1​φ∗​−E1​φ−k(E0​φ∗​−E0​φ).​
(17)
Since k≥0 and E0​φ∗​=α≥E0​φ,
E1​φ∗​−E1​φ≥k(α−E0​φ)≥0.​
(18)
This proves maximal power. A continuous sample distribution does not itself guarantee that the equality set of the likelihood ratio has zero probability; allowing randomization there makes the proof valid in that case too.

Put T=∑i​Xi​. For interior parameters 0<θ0​<θ1​<1, the alternative-to-null likelihood ratio is
L(θ0​)L(θ1​)​=(1−θ0​1−θ1​​)n(θ0​(1−θ1​)θ1​(1−θ0​)​)T.
(19)
The second base is greater than one, so this is strictly increasing in T. The Neyman-Pearson lemma therefore gives an upper-tail rejection rule. Under the null hypothesis, T∼Bin(n,θ0​), and an ordinary conservative threshold can be chosen as
C={x:T(x)≥cα​},Prθ0​​(T≥cα​)≤α.
(20)
There is an important discrete qualification: an integer cutoff need not have test size exactly α. To obtain exact size, choose an integer b with Pr0​(T>b)≤α≤Pr0​(T≥b), reject certainly when T>b, and at T=b reject with probability
γ=Pr0​(T=b)α−Pr0​(T>b)​.​
(21)
This randomized statistical test is a most powerful test at exact size α; when a binomial upper tail equals α, no randomization is needed and the stated region is literal. Otherwise the unrandomized upper-tail region has size at most α, and the exact-size result includes the boundary randomization. Endpoint parameter cases retain the same upper-tail ordering by taking limits, with ties resolved on the null-supported outcomes.

Given observed data x, a credible interval [L(x),U(x)] has posterior probability 1−α:
Pr{L(x)≤θ≤U(x)∣x}=1−α.​
(26)
For a continuous posterior distribution with probability density function π(θ∣x), this is ∫L(x)U(x)​π(θ∣x)dθ=1−α. The probability is conditional on the observed data and concerns the statistical parameter under its posterior distribution; it does not assert frequentist coverage probability.

ii

Words: 125 Articles: 1
a OurBigBook.com
Solution
Words: 125
ii OurBigBook.com
An equal-tailed credible interval allocates equal posterior probability to its two excluded tails:
Pr(θ<L∣x)=Pr(θ>U∣x)=α/2.
(27)
For a continuous strictly increasing posterior cumulative distribution function F, the endpoints are F−1(α/2) and F−1(1−α/2).
A highest posterior density interval instead includes the parameter values of greatest posterior density: there is a cutoff c such that the retained region is a superlevel set of π(θ∣x), of posterior mass 1−α. Every interior retained value has density at least as large as every excluded value, apart from possible ties. For a unimodal continuous density this is an interval; when both endpoints lie inside the support they have equal posterior density. For a multimodal posterior distribution, a highest-density region may be a union of intervals, rather than one interval. Equal tail probabilities and highest density are different criteria.

Use A=a+∑i​xi​ and B=b+n as above. The future observation is independent of the old observations conditional on θ, so the posterior predictive probability is obtained by integrating its Poisson distribution against the posterior distribution of θ:
Pr(Xn+1​=k∣x)​=∫0∞​k!e−θθk​Γ(A)BA​θA−1e−Bθdθ=Γ(A)k!BA​∫0∞​θA+k−1e−(B+1)θdθ=Γ(A)k!Γ(A+k)​(B+1B​)A(B+11​)k,k=0,1,….​​
(37)
The last integral is the normalizing gamma function integral, equal to Γ(A+k)/(B+1)A+k.
