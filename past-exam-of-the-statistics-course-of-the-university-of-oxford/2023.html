Sort the observations into order statistics x(1)​≤⋯≤x(n)​. Choose plotting positions pi​=(i−1/2)/n and calculate the standard normal quantiles zi​=Φ−1(pi​). The normal Q-Q plot consists of the pairs (zi​,x(i)​).
If the normal distribution model is appropriate, the points should lie approximately on a straight line with positive slope, because its population quantile function is
Q(p)=μ+σΦ−1(p).
(1)
The intercept is approximately μ and the slope approximately σ; neither needs to be known in advance. The reference need not be the identity line. One justification is the probability integral transform: for an independent normal statistical sample, Φ((X(i)​−μ)/σ) are uniform order statistics, whose typical values are near their plotting positions. The equality is approximate for a finite sample, particularly at the extremes. Systematic curvature, unusually heavy tails, or isolated extreme departures argue against the proposed distributional shape. A straight marginal Q-Q plot does not itself establish independence of observations.

The derivative of the log ratio is 21​(n/q−1), so λ(q) increases from zero to one on (0,n) and decreases to zero on (n,∞). Rejecting for a sufficiently small ratio therefore gives two tails with 0<q1​<n<q2​.

Fn−1​(q1​)+1−Fn−1​(q2​)=α,nlog(q2​/q1​)=q2​−q1​​.

For a critical region C, the size of a statistical test of the simple null hypothesis P0​ is α=P0​(X∈C), the Type I error probability. Its statistical power against the simple alternative hypothesis P1​ is P1​(X∈C), the probability of correctly rejecting that null. ii OurBigBook.com
The Neyman-Pearson lemma states that a test which rejects where the joint likelihood ratio L1​/L0​ is largest is a most powerful test of its size: its power is at least that of any test with no larger size. More precisely, choose a threshold k and reject if L1​/L0​>k, accept if it is smaller, and if necessary randomize on equality to attain the specified size.
For an independent and identically distributed statistical sample, take
Yi​=logf1​(Xi​)f0​(Xi​)​.
(12)
The Yi​ are independent and identically distributed random variables under either simple hypothesis, and the log joint ratio is their sum. Thus the nonrandomized threshold form can be written
i=1∑n​Yi​≤c​
(13)
with c=−logk, using either strict or non-strict inequality when the boundary has probability zero. The usual finite-log expression assumes positive common support; zero densities can instead be handled by the extended likelihood ratio on the relevant supports. Continuous observations alone do not ensure a continuous likelihood-ratio statistic: ties may require the boundary randomization in the lemma. This qualification preserves the exact-size formulation.

Solution

ii OurBigBook.com
An equal-tailed credible interval for a continuous posterior distribution has probability α/2 below its lower endpoint and α/2 above its upper endpoint. If F is the posterior cumulative distribution function, the endpoints are F−1(α/2) and F−1(1−α/2).
A highest posterior density interval is a credible interval consisting of points of highest posterior density: every included point has density at least as high as every excluded point, apart from null-set and boundary choices. It is a posterior highest density region when that region is an interval. For a continuous unimodal density with two interior endpoints, their densities agree. A mode at a support boundary can instead force one endpoint to that boundary; a multimodal posterior can have a disconnected highest-density region rather than one interval. Equal tails and highest density need not give the same interval.

The posterior mean is a convex combination of the prior mean and the usual Poisson maximum-likelihood estimate:
E(θ∣x)=λ+nr+s​=λ+nλ​λr​+λ+nn​xˉ​.
