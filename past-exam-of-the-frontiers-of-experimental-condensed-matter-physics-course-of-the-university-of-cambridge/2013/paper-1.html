X-ray diffraction arises predominantly from electromagnetic scattering by the electronic charge distribution. For elastic scattering, contributions from different unit cells interfere constructively at a reciprocal lattice vector, while the unit-cell structure factor weights the different atoms and their positions. The atomic X-ray scattering factor generally decreases with increasing momentum transfer because an extended electron cloud scatters out of phase with itself. Heavy atoms often scatter more strongly, making a light atom difficult to locate beside a heavy one; near an absorption edge, anomalous dispersion changes the contrast and can provide additional chemical sensitivity.
In neutron diffraction, the strong interaction with atomic nuclei supplies a short-range nuclear scattering amplitude. Its coherent scattering length depends on isotope and does not vary monotonically with atomic number; isotope substitution can therefore give useful contrast, and some light elements can be prominent. A neutron's magnetic moment also couples to magnetic moments in a specimen, so magnetic diffraction measures ordered spin structures. Absorption and incoherent scattering depend strongly on isotope as well. The nuclear scattering length is approximately independent of momentum transfer in the usual thermal-neutron regime, whereas magnetic scattering has a form factor reflecting the electronic magnetization distribution.
Electron diffraction probes the electrostatic potential of both nuclei and electrons. The electron's charge makes scattering strong, allowing thin specimens or surfaces to be studied with intense signals. Strong interaction also makes multiple, dynamical scattering more important, so electron diffraction intensities often cannot be interpreted as a simple single-scattering structure factor squared. Transmission geometry needs electron-transparent material; reflected low-energy electrons provide a surface-sensitive geometry. All three methods use phase-sensitive interference to reveal periodic structure, but their scattering interactions, penetration, sample requirements and contrast differ.

A scanning tunneling microscope can modify an adsorbate's potential landscape as well as image it. First locate the atom with a weakly perturbing tunnel-current setpoint. Bringing the tip closer strengthens tip–atom coupling. In lateral atomic manipulation, moving the tip parallel to the surface can pull or push the atom between adsorption sites; reducing the coupling afterwards leaves it at the chosen location. A change in tunneling current during the motion can signal a jump, and a subsequent low-current image checks the final arrangement.
In vertical atomic manipulation, a suitable tip interaction or voltage pulse transfers an atom between substrate and tip. The tip is repositioned and the atom is deposited elsewhere by changing that interaction again. Electric fields and inelastic tunneling excitation can also change adsorption, induce hopping or modify bonds. The useful mechanism depends on the atom, substrate, tip and bias: there is no universal pulse amplitude that works for every system. Low temperature suppresses thermal diffusion and ultrahigh vacuum helps preserve a clean, stable surface.
A motivating demonstration is IBM in atoms. Don Eigler and Erhard Schweizer used a low-temperature STM to position individual xenon atoms on a nickel surface, constructing a chosen atomic pattern. The 1990 paper reported operation at about 4K with atomic positioning precision. This demonstrates controlled rearrangement of surface adsorbates, rather than making the image alone prove that every STM feature is an isolated atom.[ref]

In inelastic scattering, the probe transfers energy as well as momentum to the specimen. A neutron, X-ray or electron can create or annihilate a phonon, while electronic or magnetic excitations provide other channels. Measuring the incident and outgoing energies resolves the transfer ℏω; their wavevectors determine momentum transfer ℏQ. In a crystal, phonon momentum is conserved modulo a reciprocal lattice vector. Resolved spectra therefore probe excitation dispersions and lifetimes, rather than only average atomic positions.
Thermal diffuse scattering arises from thermally populated lattice displacements and their correlations. The equilibrium lattice produces sharp Bragg peaks, but displacements redistribute part of the scattering into diffuse intensity. To one-phonon order a mode contribution contains a displacement projection and thermal occupation factors of the form

Stokes and anti-Stokes energy-resolved components carry nB​+1 and nB​ respectively; the displayed combination describes their summed scale. At high temperature, nB​≃kB​T/(ℏω), so soft modes and their polarization strongly influence the pattern. Zero-point motion also contributes at low temperature. An energy-integrating diffraction image can record this inelastic intensity as a diffuse background without resolving its small energy transfers. Static disorder gives additional diffuse scattering, so diffuse intensity alone does not establish a thermal mechanism.

In near-edge X-ray absorption fine structure, a photon promotes a core electron into an unoccupied molecular or solid-state level. Near an oxygen K-edge this is principally an O 1s excitation. Bound or resonant final states, orbital symmetry, local bonding and multiple scattering shape the edge. A monochromatic tunable X-ray beam can measure transmission through a thin specimen, or fluorescence or electron yield as an absorption proxy. In scanning transmission X-ray microscopy, a focused spot is scanned while transmitted intensity is recorded; energy stacks across an edge distinguish regions with different chemistry even when their thicknesses are similar. A zone plate or another suitable X-ray optic sets spatial resolution, while energy resolution and reference spectra control chemical discrimination.
The dipole transition amplitude contains e⋅r, so the incident electric-field direction selects final-orbital orientations. For an approximately free diatomic molecule with axis n, the axial σ∗ transition has strength proportional to ∣e⋅n∣2. Summing the two perpendicular π∗ directions gives strength proportional to 1−∣e⋅n∣2, with separate radial matrix-element factors for the two resonance families. This NEXAFS orientation dependence of a diatomic molecule explains the complementary polarization behavior; the molecular axis is not itself the direction of a π∗ orbital.
In the supplied geometry, normal incidence has electric field parallel to the surface, whereas near-grazing incidence with the illustrated polarization has a large surface-normal field component. The single-layer curves show a stronger π∗ resonance and much weaker σ∗ structure in the latter geometry. This is consistent with oxygen axes lying largely parallel to the surface: a normal electric field is perpendicular to those axes and excites π∗ efficiently, while suppressing the axial σ∗ transition. For flat molecules with random in-plane azimuths, the ideal axial factor is 1/2 for a tangential field and zero for a normal field.
The multilayer curves instead have a strong π∗ peak at normal incidence and substantially stronger relative σ∗ response at grazing incidence. Their axes therefore have a substantial surface-normal component, consistent with the proposed tilted, more upright packing. The distinction supports a different orientation distribution in the multilayer and monolayer, rather than one isotropically oriented molecular population in both.
For illustration, a molecular polar tilt β and random azimuth give ⟨∣e⋅n∣2⟩=cos2βcos2α+21​sin2βsin2α, where α is the electric-field angle to the surface normal. The original diagram plots these ideal factors for two chosen tilts; it is not a reconstruction or fit of the supplied spectra. Quantitative tilt extraction would additionally need intensity normalization, polarization purity, azimuthal distribution and substrate-induced changes of the resonances. The spectra alone do not determine unique bond lengths or every adsorption site.

Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy probes occupied valence states through photoemitted electron energies. Angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy adds emission direction and therefore parallel crystal momentum, revealing occupied band dispersions and Fermi contours. For a clean periodic surface, k∥​=2me​Ekin​​sinθ/ℏ modulo a surface reciprocal-lattice vector. A two-dimensional surface band has no intrinsic perpendicular dispersion; varying photon energy helps distinguish it from bulk bands, while allowing for matrix-element changes. A simple surface-state example is a parabolic band E(k∥​)=E0​+ℏ2k∥2​/(2m∗​).
Scanning tunneling spectroscopy measures local differential conductance. With a suitable featureless tip and weak tunneling, dI/dV samples the local density of states, giving occupied and unoccupied features on opposite bias sides. It provides spatial localization, defects and standing-wave patterns, while ordinary spectroscopy at one point does not directly give the complete momentum-resolved band structure. For the parabolic two-dimensional band, the ideal density of states has a step at its onset, while spatial interference can supply information about allowed wavevectors. Inverse photoemission spectroscopy accesses unoccupied states by injecting electrons and measuring emitted photons. Optical absorption and electron energy loss spectroscopy instead probe allowed excitation energies and collective modes, with their own selection rules and momentum ranges. These methods supply complementary information; none automatically returns the full interacting band structure without matrix elements, resolution and surface effects.
For the Pt core-level spectra, electrons suffering inelastic losses emerge with reduced kinetic energy and appear at larger inferred binding energies. Loss tails and secondary-electron contributions therefore generate the background, principally on the high-binding-energy side of a core peak. The two clean-surface components correspond to different environments of the same Pt core-level branch. The stated values imply a surface core-level shift
EBsurface​−EBbulk​=70.5−70.9=−0.4eV.​

Reduced coordination at a surface narrows the d band. In a simple almost-filled-band picture, maintaining its occupation moves the band center upward relative to the Fermi level; associated local-potential changes can move core levels upward too, reducing their binding energy. This is a plausible initial-state explanation of the negative shift. Final-state screening of the core hole also matters, so the measured shift alone does not uniquely determine a local charge or separate these mechanisms.
After adsorption, Pt atoms directly bonded to CO have a different local potential, hybridization and core-hole screening. In carbon monoxide adsorption on platinum, carbon-end donation and metal-to-molecule back-donation change the Pt–CO bond and hence the Pt core binding energy. The additional weak high-binding-energy component is naturally assigned to these chemically modified surface Pt atoms. Its small weight is compatible with only a minority of surface sites being occupied; a quantitative coverage estimate requires fitted areas and appropriate attenuation assumptions.
The new component is centered at approximately 71.9–72.0eV in the supplied plot. Relative to bare surface Pt at 70.5eV, the core-level chemical shift is therefore

Atomic force microscopy senses tip–sample interactions through a compliant cantilever. Contact deflection, oscillation amplitude, phase or resonance frequency can be converted into a feedback signal. Chemical bonding and short-range repulsion, van der Waals forces, electrostatic forces and, where relevant, magnetic or capillary interactions produce contrast. Apparent height at a fixed force or frequency setpoint can therefore contain interaction contrast as well as geometry. Lateral resolution depends on the effective tip apex and interaction range; finite tip radius broadens features. Vertical sensitivity also depends on thermal and detector noise, cantilever calibration, vibration, drift and feedback bandwidth. Strongly localized short-range forces favor atomic resolution, whereas extended interactions average over more of the tip and surface.

In a scanning electron microscope, a focused electron beam scans the surface and a detector records an emitted signal at each beam position. Low-energy secondary electrons escape from near the surface; changes in local slope, edges and detector-facing geometry give strong secondary-electron topographic contrast. Backscattered electrons result from elastic deflection of primary electrons and generally have a larger yield for higher atomic number. Their backscattered-electron atomic-number contrast therefore helps separate compositional regions. Detector geometry, crystal orientation and topography can also affect this signal, so brightness is not a unique elemental identification.
An environmental scanning electron microscope admits gas around the specimen while pressure-limiting apertures and differential pumping preserve the electron-optical column's vacuum. Electron collisions ionize the gas. Positive ions can neutralize negative charging of a poorly conducting specimen, enabling observation without a conductive coating. A gaseous secondary electron detector uses gas amplification to collect secondary-electron signals under these conditions. Gas pressure, path length and scattering must be controlled because they also affect the probe and background.[ref]
Finally, acquire an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy spectrum at each pixel to add elemental specificity. The beam creates inner-shell vacancies, and characteristic X-rays emitted as they fill identify chemical elements. Selecting fitted characteristic-line intensities yields elemental maps that can be compared with the electron image. Peak overlap, detector response, absorption and the X-ray-generating interaction volume limit identification, quantification and spatial resolution. This gives chemical-element contrast; resolving different bonds or oxidation states generally needs more detailed spectral information than one broad elemental map.
