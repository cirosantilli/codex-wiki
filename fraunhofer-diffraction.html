Fraunhofer diffraction

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics Physical optics
OurBigBook.com Words: 883 Articles: 1
Fraunhofer diffraction is the far-field approximation for diffraction: contributions from an aperture reach a selected observation direction with approximately linear path-dependent wave phases. Adding their complex amplitudes gives a Fourier transform of the field across the aperture. It also describes the appropriate rear focal plane of an ideal optical lens under coherent plane-wave illumination. The diffraction grating examples use this regime. [ref]; [ref].
For a slit of width a, wavelength λ and screen distance L, sufficient conditions near the forward direction are
L≫a,L≫λa2​,λLa2​≪1.
(72)
The last two statements are equivalent: the important comparison is with a2/λ, rather than just with the slit width. For a finite rectangular aperture, check the corresponding condition for both transverse dimensions. The single-slit diffraction calculation treats the transverse cut associated with width a. [ref].
A short explanation is that the distance from slit coordinate x to an observation point at distance R and angle θ is
r(x)=R2−2Rxsinθ+x2​=R−xsinθ+2Rx2cos2θ​+O(a3/R2).
(73)
The linear term supplies the phase difference; the quadratic term supplies a wave phase error of order a2/(λR), since the wavenumber is 2π/λ. Near the forward direction R≈L, so the stated condition makes this error small. This is why a distant screen permits the simple single-slit diffraction formula. [ref].
