Bijection

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Foundations of mathematics Set theory Function
OurBigBook.com Words: 141
A bijection f:X→Y is a function that is both injective and surjective: each element of its codomain is the output of exactly one element of its domain of a function. The two requirements exclude unused targets and merged inputs respectively. The finite example f(A)=1, f(B)=2, f(C)=3 is a bijection from {A,B,C} to {1,2,3}, whereas adding an unused target 4 makes that same rule fail surjectivity.
A bijection gives an inverse function by reversing every pairing. Conversely, a two-sided inverse function implies a bijection, as the proof under inverse function shows. This is useful for transporting structures without losing or identifying elements. It also defines equality of cardinality: two sets have equal cardinality exactly when a bijection pairs them. An infinite example is n↦2n from the integers to the even integers, with inverse m↦m/2 on that specified codomain. A proper subset can therefore have the same cardinality as an infinite set. [ref].
