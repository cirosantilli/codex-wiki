A useful hippocampal episodic memory model assigns complementary computations to competitive learning, recurrent autoassociation and feedforward pattern association. The event must bind its participants, location and context, so that a partial cue later reinstates the appropriate conjunction. Merely classifying inputs or remembering a location is insufficient to explain an episodic memory.
A concrete pathway begins with distributed cortical input through the entorhinal cortex. The dentate gyrus expands and sparsifies this input: strong synaptic competition means that only a small fraction of granule cells respond strongly. A winning unit or winning sparse population strengthens its active afferents through constrained Hebbian learning. Similar episodes can then recruit less-overlapping outputs, providing pattern separation before storage. Separating two nearly identical episodes is useful because a recurrent memory otherwise tends to merge their cues. The desired separation must preserve relevant conjunctions; indiscriminately randomizing the input would make later retrieval impossible.
The sparse code recruits a hippocampal CA3 ensemble. Rapid, associatively induced long-term potentiation is a candidate substrate for storing the conjunction, with its induction conditions and persistence requiring experimental constraints. Its recurrent collateral synapses allow coactive elements of the event to become associated through Hebbian learning. One idealized centred storage rule is

where a is mean coding activity and ξμ is an event pattern. With inhibitory control maintaining sparsity, recurrent retrieval iterates r←ϕ(Wr+c−θ) from cue c. A sufficiently informative partial cue can move activity into the stored event's basin of attraction; recurrent reinstatement supplies missing components. This is pattern completion. A Hopfield network makes the principle precise in a symmetric idealization with an energy landscape, but biological CA3 connectivity need not obey the exact symmetry or update assumptions of asynchronous Hopfield energy descent. Interference, spurious states and an incorrect initial cue can all produce erroneous completion.
The hippocampal CA1 and cortical return pathways provide heteroassociative memory. During the event, CA3 activity ξμ is paired with a cortical or CA1 target zμ, giving an illustrative mapping A=∑μ​zμ(ξμ)T. When CA3 completes the event, Aξ​ reinstates its associated output representation. Thus competitive learning distinguishes inputs, autoassociation restores a stored conjunction, and pattern association translates the retrieved conjunction into content usable elsewhere. These are separable operations even if a real circuit performs them concurrently. CA1 also receives direct entorhinal information, so it cannot be treated as a passive relay without testing the contribution of that second input.
There is mechanistically relevant evidence beyond anatomical resemblance. In adult mice, CA3-restricted NMDA receptor deletion spared ordinary acquisition and retrieval of water-maze reference memory with the full cue set, but impaired retrieval when only some cues remained. CA1 place-related activity also deteriorated under partial-cue conditions. Full-cue performance is an important control against interpreting the deficit as a general inability to swim, see cues or express a learned response. This fits the prediction that recurrent associative plasticity is particularly important for pattern completion. Nevertheless, the deletion affects receptor-dependent processing throughout the targeted CA3 cells, and does not isolate only recurrent collateral plasticity or the exact storage rule.[ref]
A separate delayed matching-to-place experiment found impaired rapid acquisition of novel platform locations after CA3-restricted NMDA receptor deletion, with previously experienced locations relatively spared. That extends the interpretation from partial-cue recall to rapid storage of new experiences; it also cautions against assigning CA3 an exclusively retrieval-related role.[ref]
Simultaneous dentate gyrus and hippocampal CA3 recordings offer a more direct input–output comparison. In behaving rats, conflict between local and global spatial reference frames disrupted dentate population patterns, while CA3 maintained a more coherent representation related to the stored map. Comparing the two populations under the same manipulation supports the complementary pattern separation and pattern completion computations more specifically than finding different firing rates in different areas. It still does not prove that a particular recalled human episode corresponds to one fixed CA3 attractor.[ref]
The evidence supports the computational division of labour, but the simplest implementation is incomplete. The same region can separate strongly changed inputs and complete moderately degraded ones; separation and completion are operating regimes, not immutable labels for every trial. Fast storage must coexist with protection of old memories, and recurrent completion must not overwrite a new but similar event. Novelty, inhibition and synaptic plasticity regulation are therefore functional requirements, rather than arbitrary additions. Experimental interventions can affect acquisition, retrieval, exploration and sensory input differently, so their timing and behavioural controls matter.
An unordered fixed-point memory also omits the temporal structure of an episode. The same people in the same place at different times need contextual or sequential representations; an attractor for a bag of features does not distinguish reversed event order. Rehearsal and longer-term cortical learning may change the dependence on the hippocampus. The theory should consequently predict partial-cue success, lure errors, interference and pathway-specific deficits across time, while acknowledging that neither a spatial task nor a correlation in population activity establishes the whole of episodic memory. The combined architecture supplies an explicit storage-and-retrieval mechanism, with experimentally supported components and clear remaining requirements.
