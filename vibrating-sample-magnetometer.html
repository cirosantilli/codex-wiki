Vibrating-sample magnetometer

 Home Physics Branches of physics Electromagnetism Bound charge and bound current Magnetization
OurBigBook.com Words: 274
A vibrating-sample magnetometer measures a specimen's magnetic dipole moment through the induced electromotive force as it moves relative to stationary pickup coils. Dividing that magnetic dipole moment by the specimen's independently determined volume gives its average magnetization.
In Simon Foner's 1959 apparatus, the specimen was weighed and glued to a mount. A loudspeaker drive moved it perpendicular to an applied uniform magnetic field between magnet poles; stationary coils detected the induced electromotive force. A reference magnet on the same vibrating assembly generated a second coil electromotive force. Adjusting the reference voltage divider to an electrical null compared the specimen signal with this reference. The measurement calibration substituted a magnetically saturated pure nickel sphere of known magnetic dipole moment; a specimen-removed reading corrected the mount's contribution. The calibrated signal inferred the specimen's magnetic dipole moment, not its local magnetization map. Measurement uncertainty in the standard, positioning, finite specimen size, coil noise, and mechanical coupling limited the inference.[ref]
The readout follows Faraday's law of induction. In a small-displacement model with constant projected magnetic dipole moment m, let the pickup-coil magnetic flux linkage be Φ(z)=g(z)m and the specimen motion be z(t)=z0​+acosωt. Here g incorporates coil geometry and turn count, a is the displacement amplitude, and ω is angular frequency. To first order in displacement, with g′(z0​) the derivative at the mean position,
V(t)=−dtdΦ​≃mg′(z0​)aωsinωt.

This is an original idealized readout relation: a larger induced electromotive force can mean larger magnetic dipole moment, greater motion, or a changed coil position. A synchronously moving reference allows common motion factors to cancel in the ratio of induced electromotive forces. The measurement calibration and specimen-removed control are therefore integral to the measurement. A single amplitude reading cannot by itself distinguish microscopic alignment from a change in individual magnetic dipole moments.
