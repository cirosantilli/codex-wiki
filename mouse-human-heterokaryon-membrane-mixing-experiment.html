Mouse-human heterokaryon membrane-mixing experiment

 Home Biology Biological cell Cell membrane Membrane protein Lateral mobility of membrane proteins
OurBigBook.com Words: 249
In 1970 Larry Frye and Michael Edidin fused mouse C11D and human VA-2 cell lines with Sendai virus, forming heterokaryons. Suspensions were maintained in a shaking water bath at 37∘C. Removed aliquots were stained by indirect immunofluorescence: antibodies against parental surface antigens, then differently fluorescent secondary antibodies. A Leitz Ortholux fluorescence microscope used a mercury lamp and color-selecting filters. Green mouse and red human surface patterns became intermixed.[ref]
Same-species fusions tested antibody specificity; stable hybrid lines supplied a two-color control. Inhibitors of biological translation did not prevent mixing; lowering temperature inhibited it. These observations supported lateral redistribution over rapid synthesis. The authors could not conclusively exclude passage through cytoplasm and return. The readout compared populations, not trajectories of a continuously observed individual biological cell, and did not establish unrestricted motion of every membrane protein.[ref]
The decisive design idea is to begin with spatially distinguishable material. A uniformly labeled surface can look unchanged while its components move. Bringing together differently identifiable parental surfaces creates an initial pattern whose loss reveals redistribution; antibody specificity makes that loss interpretable. Membrane topology asks which side a part faces, whereas this experiment asks where it can move along that side.
