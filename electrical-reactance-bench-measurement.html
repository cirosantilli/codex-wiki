Electrical reactance bench measurement (X=∣Z∣sinϕ)

 ... Physics Branches of physics Electromagnetism Electrical circuit Electrical impedance Electrical reactance
OurBigBook.com Words: 789
The motivating observation is a pair of sinusoids on an oscilloscope: changing frequency changes both their relative amplitudes and their phase difference. Gary Tuttle's EE 201 laboratory uses resistor voltage as the current-phase reference, compares predictions with readings at several frequencies, and explicitly asks students to measure component values and account for coil electrical resistance. The following bench arrangement applies that method to a series RLC circuit; its component values and illustrated traces are our own toy example. [ref].
Connect a low-voltage sine generator to a series chain ending in a known sensing resistor Rs​ at the common return. Use a resistor whose unwanted inductance is negligible over the chosen frequency range. An oscilloscope displays voltage against time: channel A reads the loaded voltage across the whole chain, and channel B reads the voltage vs​ across Rs​. Both channel ground connections and the generator return meet at that same node. Ordinary bench oscilloscope grounds are electrically common; attaching one to an intermediate circuit node would change the circuit. A separate floating component voltage needs a suitable differential measurement, rather than moving a ground clip. [ref].
After the free transient decays, record frequency f, period T=1/f, both voltage amplitudes, and matched rising zero-crossing times tA​,tB​. Check that the traces are sinusoidal. Define Δt=tB​−tA​ on the branch ∣Δt∣<T/2. With negligible probe loading and negligible sensing-resistor electrical reactance, vs​=Rs​i, so channel B is the electric current reference, and in the reactance phase convention
Irms​=Rs​Vs,rms​​,∣Zchain​∣=Irms​VA,rms​​,ϕ=T2πΔt​,Xchain​=∣Zchain​∣sinϕ,Rchain​=∣Zchain​∣cosϕ.
(10)
A later current crossing gives Δt>0, hence positive electrical reactance; an earlier crossing gives negative electrical reactance. Use the same amplitude convention for both channels. An amplitude-only ratio recovers ∣Z∣, so it cannot distinguish the two signs: timing supplies the missing information. The formulas follow from Z=∣Z∣eiϕ and the chosen terminal orientation.
Begin with a known resistor replacing the reactive chain: its expected phase difference is approximately zero, testing channel polarity and relative delay. Measure the same signal with both channels to establish the instrumental delay baseline, then exchange probes to check that a phase offset does not belong to one channel. Next test an inductor and a capacitor separately with Rs​: after subtracting Rs​ from the inferred real part, expect approximately X/f=2πL and fX=−1/(2πC), respectively. Finally combine L,C,R,Rs​ and sweep through the predicted resonance. These are proposed controls and consequences of the model, not reported observations.
For the toy series RLC circuit, the prediction at 500Hz is X=−286.894Ω, ∣Z∣=307.259Ω, ϕ=−69.02∘, and Vs,rms​=32.55mV. At 5000Hz, X=+282.328Ω and ϕ=+68.71∘. Near 1591.55Hz the model predicts zero phase difference and Vs,rms​=90.91mV. Keep actual loaded VA,rms​ fixed or normalize by it at each frequency; the generator's display alone does not establish that voltage. The 10Ω sensing resistor is part of the 110Ω total and therefore changes the predicted peak electric current.
Real components add unintended electrical impedance: coil wire has electrical resistance, nearby turns add capacitance, and capacitor leads add inductance. These effects can produce a component's own resonance, beyond which the ideal ωL or −1/(ωC) approximation fails. Work in a range where that approximation is adequate, measure coil wire electrical resistance, and compare the inferred Rchain​ with the sensing resistor, intended resistor, and component losses. A measured nonzero real part is evidence of loss in the assembly, not negative or positive electrical reactance becoming heat. [ref]; [ref].
The probes also add electrical resistance and capacitance to the measured nodes; their loading becomes more important as frequency increases. Use compensated probes with known input electrical impedance and short ground connections, and repeat with a smaller drive voltage to test whether the inferred X depends on amplitude. [ref]. Record actual voltage and phase readings separately from calculated predictions, with component tolerances and timing uncertainty. A phase error δϕ alone produces approximately δX=∣Z∣cosϕδϕ, so near zero electrical reactance, a small delay can imitate a small residual X. Agreement within stated uncertainties tests the model; the smooth toy curves establish no experimental accuracy.
