Intestinal nucleus transfer experiment

 ... Organism Multicellular organism Developmental integration Cellular differentiation Cellular reprogramming Somatic cell nuclear transfer
OurBigBook.com Words: 477
Can a specialized donor still supply information for other cell types? The 1962 The Developmental Capacity of Nuclei taken from Intestinal Epithelium Cells of Feeding Tadpoles by J. B. Gurdon used mid-intestinal cells of feeding Xenopus laevis tadpoles. Donors were recognized by columnar shape, basal cell nuclei and a striated absorptive border. Dissociation used Versene solution; this made individual donors accessible. The readout was subsequent development, including normal feeding tadpoles, rather than just division or survival. [ref]
Apparatus and intervention. A microscope with ultraviolet illumination directed light at the surface region containing the unfertilized ovum's chromosomes, inactivating the recipient nuclear contribution. Irradiation also softened the surrounding jelly so a glass micropipette could enter. The pipette delivered donor nuclear material into recipient cytoplasm. In the technique's validation, fertilizing irradiated eggs produced haploid embryos, supporting destruction of the maternal nuclear contribution. This is inactivation, not a diagram of surgically removing an intact cell nucleus. [ref]
Controls ask different questions. Donor cell nuclei carried a marker absent from recipients; post-blastula embryos carried that marker. The marker distinguished diploid donors with one nucleolus per cell nucleus from the usual two-nucleolus recipient background, rather than mistaking one nucleolus for proof of a haploid state. [ref] Interspersed transfers of blastula or later embryonic cell nuclei checked variable recipient quality. A positive embryonic-donor control thus tests the preparation, while the marker tests donor origin. Abnormal development remained common. Neither control establishes universal transfer competence. [ref]
Inference and limits. A successful marked donor demonstrates that specialization had not irreversibly removed all information required to build other cell types. Failure is less decisive: transfer damage, recipient condition and incomplete cellular reprogramming can also prevent development. The marker identifies nuclear origin; it is not a complete DNA sequence comparison. The experiment tests donor nuclear potential in an altered cytoplasm, rather than normal donor cell fate, a universal capacity of every donor, or development from a mature mammalian red blood cell. The Nobel account also describes serial transfers through embryonic intermediates; these must not be silently combined with direct transfers into one success rate. [ref]
