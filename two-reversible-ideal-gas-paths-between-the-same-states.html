Two reversible ideal-gas paths between the same states

OurBigBook.com Words: 591
Take a closed system consisting of one mole of a classical monatomic gas, idealized as an ideal gas with constant heat capacity at constant volume CV​=3nR/2. Stipulate amount of substance n=1mol and molar gas constant R=8.314Jmol−1K−1 for this calculation. Set initial temperature T0​=300K and volume V0​=0.010m3, and define E0​=nRT0​=2.4942kJ and p0​=E0​/V0​=249.42kPa. The specified equilibrium endpoints are
A:(V,T,p)=(V0​,T0​,p0​),B:(V,T,p)=(3V0​,2T0​,2p0​/3).
(9)
Thus B has volume 0.030m3, temperature 600K and pressure 166.28kPa. The fixed material inventory and ideal gas law make these complete endpoint specifications for this model. All numbers below are calculated values, rather than measurements of a particular gas.
Use a frictionless piston, ignore bulk mechanical energy changes, and allow only pressure-volume work. Each step is the limit of a reversible thermodynamic process: adjust loads by infinitesimal pressure differences, and supply heat through infinitesimal temperature differences. Heating with changing temperature requires a succession of matching thermal reservoirs; contact with a single hotter heat bath would give an irreversible finite difference. On a fixed-volume leg the piston is locked. These assumptions justify using the actual equilibrium path's pressure in
Wby,rev​=∫path​pdV.
(10)
An endpoint chord is not the path; substituting a guessed straight line would compute a different thermodynamic process.[ref]
The upper route heats at fixed volume from A to C=(V0​,2T0​,2p0​), then undergoes isothermal expansion at 2T0​ to B. The lower route undergoes isothermal expansion at T0​ from A to D=(3V0​,T0​,p0​/3), then heats at fixed volume to B. The tuples use the same (V,T,p) ordering as the endpoints. Vertical legs do zero expansion work, and the two curved legs have different pressures at every shared intermediate volume.
The internal energy of an ideal gas gives the same endpoint difference for both routes:
ΔU=23​nR(2T0​−T0​)=23​E0​=3.7413kJ.
(11)
On either fixed-volume heating leg, Wby​=0 and Q=3E0​/2. On each isothermal expansion, ΔU=0, so the first law of thermodynamics gives Q=Wby​. The ideal gas law and the integral of 1/V then give
Wby,upper​Wby,lower​​=∫V0​3V0​​V2E0​​dV=2E0​ln3,=∫V0​3V0​​VE0​​dV=E0​ln3,​Qupper​Qlower​​=23​E0​+2E0​ln3,=23​E0​+E0​ln3.​
(12)
Here ln is the natural logarithm. Rounded to 0.001kJ, the upper route has (Q,Wby​,ΔU)=(9.222,5.480,3.741)kJ and the lower route has (6.481,2.740,3.741)kJ. Subtracting separately rounded entries can differ by 0.001kJ; the unrounded balances are exact in the stipulated model.
The shaded area between the curves explains the entire difference:
Wby,upper​−Wby,lower​=Qupper​−Qlower​=E0​ln3≈2.740159kJ.
(13)
Following the upper route forward and the lower route backward makes a thermodynamic cycle with this positive net work and matching net heat. Its internal energy change is zero. The two open routes themselves are reversible, so their different work outputs should not be mistaken for a loss caused by entropy production: they receive different heat transfers at different temperatures.
Two other state functions provide independent endpoint checks. With enthalpy H=5nRT/2 in this model,
ΔH=25​E0​=6.2355kJ,ΔS=23​nRln2+nRln3≈17.7781JK−1.
(14)
To obtain the entropy change along either route, integrate CV​dT/T on its heating leg, giving CV​ln2, and nRdV/V on its isothermal expansion, giving nRln3. Reordering the legs changes their heat totals but leaves the sum of their entropy changes unchanged. In particular, neither route's total Q divided by its final temperature equals ΔS, and neither total Q equals ΔH: both routes include an isothermal expansion, rather than a single fixed-pressure heating step.
