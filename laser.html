Laser

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics
OurBigBook.com Words: 8k Articles: 65
A laser generates amplified light through stimulated emission. Its name abbreviates “light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation”. Lasers supply the intense beams used in optical tweezers; optical coherence also makes them useful for wave interference measurements. [ref].
In the usual material picture, a gain medium stores energy supplied by pumping, for example by incident light or an electric current. Stimulated emission transfers that stored energy to a selected optical normal mode, while competing photon absorption removes light. Producing net optical gain requires a suitable population distribution, such as population inversion in the simple model below.
A familiar laser oscillator uses mirrors to return light through the gain medium. This optical feedback favours resonant normal modes; a partly transmitting mirror lets useful power escape. Feedback and mode selection have different implementations, including ring arrangements: every laser need not resemble the same two-mirror device. The laser threshold example treats just that device. [ref] [ref].
The useful combination is concentrated power and controlled optical normal modes. It gives optical tweezers a localized source of force and wave interference measurements a source with useful optical coherence. A laser can nevertheless emit several frequencies; “laser” alone does not guarantee a single frequency. [ref].
An original experimental landmark is T. H. Maiman's 1960 report of stimulated optical radiation from optically pumped ruby at about 694.3nm. His paper describes both the pumping path and the observed emission, connecting stored material energy with amplified light. [ref].

Three-level laser

Words: 1k Articles: 6
Laser OurBigBook.com
A three-level laser pumps emitters out of their ground state into a higher pump state, which rapidly relaxes into the upper lasing state. The lower lasing state is the ground state itself. Photon absorption on the lasing transition therefore competes with stimulated emission until enough emitters have been removed from the ground state. This is the ground state depletion problem implicit in population inversion.
The model below treats nondegenerate lasing energy levels with equal absorption and emission cross-sections. Its inversion condition is N2​>N1​. Real materials require the relevant sublevel populations, degeneracies and transition strengths; the simple half-population condition should not be imposed on every optical transition. Extra levels let the pump and the emitted light act on different transitions, avoiding the steady two-level saturation limit discussed in population inversion.

Three-level ground-state inversion threshold (Rτ21​>1)

Words: 83
Three-level laser OurBigBook.com
In a three-level laser whose lower lasing level is the ground state, fast relaxation from a pumped higher level feeds a long-lived upper lasing level. With equal lasing-level degeneracies, negligible pump-level population and pump rate R per ground-state emitter, the no-light stationary rate equation gives N2​/N1​=Rτ21​. Positive population inversion therefore requires more than half the active emitters to leave the ground state. This is a transparency threshold; optical resonator loss imposes an additional positive-gain requirement. Degenerate sublevels require population comparisons weighted by their statistical weights and transition strengths.

Maiman ruby laser experiment

Words: 270
Ruby laser OurBigBook.com
Theodore Harold Maiman and Irnee J. D'Haenens obtained laser action in May 1960. Their arrangement put a ruby rod inside a helical xenon flashlamp. Increasing excitation produced a marked intensity threshold, with subsequent measurements showing narrowing of the ruby emission line.[ref] The 1960 letter describes silvered parallel crystal faces and compares low- and high-excitation spectra.[ref]
The controls answer different questions. Increased brightness establishes neither optical feedback nor narrow-band emission by itself: ordinary fluorescence can also brighten with pumping. Comparing spectra at lower and higher excitation, retaining the same optical collection geometry, tests a change in the emission process. Spectrometer resolution, response saturation and heating can alter an apparent line profile; a bright spot alone cannot distinguish those effects. These are interpretation requirements, rather than additional measurements attributed to the short original letter.
Several experimental models were made. The Smithsonian's surviving head has uncertain priority, and its installed crystal came from a later experiment.[ref] The functional diagram therefore describes the optical mechanism rather than claiming to reconstruct the first surviving unit.

Three-level laser rate equation (u˙=r(1−u)−u−C(2u−1)s)

Words: 698 Articles: 2
Three-level laser OurBigBook.com
Consider an original reduced model with a fixed number N of active emitters. Let u=N2​/N be the upper lasing-state fraction, 1−u=N1​/N the ground-state fraction, and s=n/N the number of cavity photons per emitter. Assume the pump state empties rapidly, so its population can be neglected. Measure time by x=t/τ, where τ is the upper state's spontaneous lifetime. Let r=Wτ be the effective pump rate, C>0 the dimensionless stimulated-coupling coefficient, K>0 the photon-loss rate in these units, and 0≤β≤1 the fraction of spontaneous decays feeding this optical normal mode. The rate equations are
dxdu​=r(1−u)−u−C(2u−1)s,dxds​=[C(2u−1)−K]s+βu.
(1)
Each term has a physical role. Pumping transfers emitters out of the ground state; spontaneous emission removes upper-state population. The net stimulated term includes emission from fraction u and absorption by fraction 1−u, giving their difference 2u−1. A stimulated transition removes one stored excitation and creates one cavity photon, or does the reverse. The factor β accounts for spontaneous emission into the selected mode without pretending that all spontaneous emission enters it.
For negligible cavity light, the upper-state equation is linear. A constant pump applied to u(0)=0 gives
u(x)=r+1r​(1−e−(r+1)x),u∞​=r+1r​.
(2)
Thus this model first permits population inversion at r>1. Positive optical gain is still insufficient for an oscillator: the gain must exceed the complete laser threshold, including photon loss. The small-signal photon growth coefficient changes sign when
C(2u−1)=K,uth​=21+K/C​,rth​=C−KC+K​(C>K).​
(3)
If C≤K, even complete inversion cannot exceed the assumed loss in this model. For chosen C=100 and K=20, the threshold population is uth​=0.6 and the pump threshold is rth​=1.5. At r=2, the unsaturated population first reaches 0.6 at x=(log10)/3≃0.7675. These numbers are stipulated examples, not ruby material parameters.

Laser gain clamping

Three-level laser rate equation OurBigBook.com
In the steady limit with nonzero cavity light and negligible spontaneous seeding, the photon equation requires C(2u−1)=K. The upper-state fraction is therefore fixed near uth​ while increased pumping principally increases the photon population. Substituting into the population equation gives
u=uth​,s=Kr(1−uth​)−uth​​(r>rth​).
(4)
For C=100, K=20 and r=3, this predicts u=0.6 and s=0.03. The inversion does not rise indefinitely above threshold. A finite β supplies seed photons and smooths the ideal onset; with β=0 and exactly s(0)=0, the deterministic equations remain on the zero-photon solution even when that solution is unstable. This distinguishes instability of an unseeded state from actual startup of a noisy optical field.

Stored excitation in a pulsed laser

The cavity field initially remains small while pumping fills the upper state. Once sufficient gain develops, the photon population grows and depletes the stored inversion. Continuing pump action can refill it, giving relaxation oscillations in this simple model. This is not a fit to Maiman's recorded output.

Words: 40
Laser OurBigBook.com
Laser detuning is the difference between a laser angular frequency and a specified atomic resonance angular frequency. Red detuning has δ<0; blue detuning has δ>0. The sign matters in laser cooling: a moving atom can see a red-detuned laser closer to resonance because of the Doppler effect.

Stimulated emission

Words: 581 Articles: 4
Laser OurBigBook.com
Stimulated emission is emission induced by incident light, in contrast with the unprompted decay in spontaneous emission. It supplies the amplification mechanism of a laser. [ref].
For an emitter with upper and lower energy values E2​>E1​, the resonant photon has frequency ν satisfying
ΔE=E2​−E1​=hν,
(8)
where h is the Planck constant. In the elementary photon picture, an incident photon stimulates an emitter in the upper quantum state to emit an additional photon into the same optical normal mode, with the same frequency, propagation direction and photon polarization. The emitter supplies the extra energy; the stimulating photon is not consumed. This builds the existing field and its optical coherence. An emitter in the lower quantum state can instead undergo photon absorption, so stimulated emission alone does not establish net optical gain.
For example, a 2.0eV gap corresponds to ν≈4.8360×1014Hz and a vacuum wavelength of about 620nm. One ideal stimulated emission event converts one incoming photon into two outgoing photons in the selected optical normal mode, adding 2.0eV of light energy while lowering the emitter's energy by that amount.

Population inversion (N2​>N1​)

Words: 304 Articles: 1
Stimulated emission OurBigBook.com
A population inversion puts more emitters in the upper than in the lower quantum state of a transition in the nondegenerate two-level model. Let N2​,N1​ be their number densities. Equal stimulated-transition strengths make the emission rate proportional to N2​ and the competing photon absorption rate proportional to N1​, so net optical gain is proportional to N2​−N1​. For N2​=6×1020m−3 and N1​=4×1020m−3, the difference is positive; swapping them makes the medium absorb. Equal populations give zero net stimulated contribution. With degenerate levels, the relevant comparison uses populations per statistical weight of an atomic level, rather than simply N2​>N1​.
Why pump through extra levels? Ordinary steady resonant pumping of a nondegenerate two-level system drives both photon absorption and stimulated emission. With equal induced rate W and upper-state decay rate Γ, its elementary population equation is
N˙2​=W(N1​−N2​)−ΓN2​,N1​N2​​=W+ΓW​≤1
(9)
at a stationary population. Increasing this pump saturates towards N2​=N1​; it cannot sustain population inversion in this model. This is a steady rate-equation conclusion, not a universal prohibition on all driven two-level dynamics or all possible laser gain mechanisms. [ref].
In a familiar three-level path, pumping excites a higher level which relaxes into the upper lasing level; the lower lasing level is the ground state, so substantial ground-state depletion is needed. A four-level path also provides rapid emptying of the lower lasing level into a separate ground state. Suitable lifetimes then allow accumulation above and depletion below the lasing transition. Pumping still supplies the energy: extra levels provide a route to population inversion, not free energy. [ref].

Laser threshold (gth​)

Words: 367 Articles: 2
Optical gain OurBigBook.com
In a simple two-mirror oscillator, laser threshold is the small-signal optical gain at which a resonant optical normal mode exactly replaces its round-trip intensity loss. Assume constant intensity coefficients g and α throughout a one-way distance L, and mirror intensity reflectivities 0<R1​,R2​≤1. One round trip has multiplier
M=R1​R2​exp(2(g−α)L),In+1​=MIn​.
(11)
Two traversals give the distance 2L; multiplying the two mirror factors completes the bookkeeping. Setting M=1 and taking a logarithm gives
gth​=α−2Lln(R1​R2​)​.
(12)
The reflectivities here are intensity fractions, not field-amplitude reflectivities. A shorter gain region or nonuniform loss requires replacing the path exponent by the appropriate path integral. The balance also presumes a permitted resonant optical normal mode. [ref].
For L=0.50m, R1​=1, R2​=0.90 and α=0.10m−1, the threshold is gth​≈0.2053605m−1. At g=0.15m−1, M≈0.946144: a small circulating signal decays even though the material has positive optical gain. At g=0.30m−1, M≈1.099262: the signal initially grows. Raising passive loss or lowering mirror reflectivity raises laser threshold.
For M>1, the fixed-gain recurrence predicts startup amplification, not infinite actual output. Growing light intensity depletes the available population inversion, reducing optical gain through saturation. In the simplest steady oscillator, the saturated round-trip gain balances loss and pumping supplies the outgoing power and dissipated energy. The small-signal model locates the onset; it does not determine the final output power. [ref].

Optical frequency comb (fn​=nfr​+f0​)

Words: 1k Articles: 9
Laser OurBigBook.com
An optical frequency comb has evenly spaced optical spectral lines, or teeth. A mode-locked laser supplies short optical pulses at repetition frequency fr​, the tooth spacing. The carrier-envelope offset frequency f0​ positions the whole ruler:
fn​=nfr​+f0​,n∈Z,0≤f0​<fr​.
(13)
Only teeth within the emitted optical spectrum are present; the extrapolated intercept need not be an emitted tooth.[ref]
The achievement associated with John L. Hall and Theodor W. Hänsch is a practical bridge between optical frequencies and countable electronic frequencies. Instead of constructing a separate chain of lasers for each optical measurement, a broad comb supplies many linked reference teeth. Stable lasers, short optical pulses and strong nonlinear optics in optical fibers came together around 1999–2000.[ref]

Mode locking

Words: 337 Articles: 3
Optical frequency comb OurBigBook.com
Mode locking fixes relative phases between longitudinal normal modes of a laser, allowing short optical pulses. The same optical spectrum with wandering relative phases would not supply that stable pulse train.[ref]
Here is an original finite-mode model, with the convention that a positive frequency has time dependence e2πift:
E(t)=∑n=2440​an​e2πi(nfr​+f0​)t,an​=∑j=2440​e−(j−32)2/18e−(n−32)2/18​.
(14)
These field coefficients follow a Gaussian function and have aligned phases at t=0. Choose f0​=fr​/4 and write T=1/fr​. Since e2πin=1 for every integer n,
E(t+T)=e2πif0​TE(t)=iE(t),∣E(t+T)∣2=∣E(t)∣2.
(15)
The pulse light intensity repeats every T although the field needs four periods to repeat. At the pulse centers, E(0)=1, E(T)=i, E(2T)=−1 and E(3T)=−i. A detector of light intensity alone therefore cannot distinguish this offset from zero. This model has far fewer teeth than a practical optical comb; its normalized time and frequency scales are chosen to make the carrier oscillations visible.
A short proof of the converse uses the Fourier series. If E(t+T)=eiΔϕE(t), then E(t)e−iΔϕt/T is periodic with period T. Its Fourier series has frequencies n/T; restoring the removed factor shifts every tooth by Δϕ/(2πT). A finite observation time broadens the spectral peaks; it does not change this ideal infinite-train relation.

Chirped pulse amplification

Words: 1k Articles: 4
Laser OurBigBook.com
Chirped pulse amplification increases an optical pulse's energy while keeping its peak power lower during amplification: stretch the pulse, amplify it, then compress it. A chirp is a changing instantaneous frequency. Stretching separates the arrival times of different frequencies; pulse compression brings their contributions back together. The gain medium supplies energy, whereas the compressor concentrates the surviving energy into a shorter time.
Donna Strickland and Gérard Mourou demonstrated chirped pulse amplification at Rochester in 1985. Their experiment used an optical fiber, a regenerative amplifier and a diffraction grating compressor.[ref]
The useful idea is to arrange where the large peak power occurs. Lower light intensity in the amplifier reduces unwanted self-focusing and self-phase modulation; the compressed output can still drive strong nonlinear optics. Stretching does not remove every damage mechanism, and compression does not manufacture energy.

Attosecond physics

Words: 2k Articles: 10
Laser OurBigBook.com
Attosecond physics resolves changes on the scale of 10−18 seconds, especially electron dynamics. Its central trick is to convert a short optical event into an experimentally accessible energy or phase change. A broad optical spectrum provides the ingredients for a short optical pulse; their spectral phases determine when those ingredients add constructively. High harmonic generation supplies the optical spectrum, while RABBITT and attosecond streaking supply complementary timing measurements. XUV denotes extreme ultraviolet radiation.
There are two separate timing problems: characterize the incident optical pulse, then infer the response of the target. A photoelectron measurement mixes the incident spectral phase, the target's transition response and the probing electric field. Recovering one requires constraints on the others. These methods measure distributions of photoelectron energies over repeated shots, rather than photographing a moving atomic orbital.

High harmonic generation

Words: 372 Articles: 1
Attosecond physics OurBigBook.com
High harmonic generation converts an intense driving electric field into radiation at high integer multiples of its frequency. High harmonic generation in a gas is a process in nonlinear optics: an electron can leave a neutral target by tunnel ionization, gain kinetic energy in the driving electric field, and return to radiate upon recombination. This three-step picture is an approximate account of a probability amplitude for emission in quantum mechanics, not a measured path of one electron.[ref]
Why odd multiples? In an ideal centrosymmetric gas driven by a single frequency, assume the radiating electric dipole moment satisfies d(t+T/2)=−d(t), where T is the driving period. Its Fourier series coefficient obeys dq​=−(−1)qdq​, so every even coefficient vanishes. A finite envelope, an oriented target or an additional driving frequency can invalidate that symmetry argument. Producing many odd components also does not prove that their spectral phases form a short optical pulse.
The 1988 report involving Anne L'Huillier observed odd multiples of a 1064nm driving wavelength in rare gases, reaching order 33 in argon. The unexpectedly slow fall of yield beyond the fifth order supplied a broad spectral plateau. A spectral observation alone did not establish the relative spectral phases needed for an attosecond pulse train.[ref]
