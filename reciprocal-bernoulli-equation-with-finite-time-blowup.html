Reciprocal Bernoulli equation with finite-time blowup (y(x)=e−x/(C−x))

Consider the original analytic initial value problem
y′+y=exy2,y(0)=a.
(100)
Here x and y are dimensionless variables in a stipulated mathematical model. The trajectories below are exact solutions of a differential equation, with no measured data or empirical parameter fit. For a=0, the Bernoulli power substitution z=1/y gives
z′−z=−ex,(e−xz)′=−1,z(x)=ex(C−x),y(x)=C−xe−x​,C=a1​.
(101)
The integrating factor is e−x. Direct differentiation gives y′=−y+exy2, independently checking the transformation. The linear z exists for every real x, while the nonlinear solution of a differential equation uses only the interval containing 0 where z=0:
initial valuea>0a<0a=0​maximal interval(−∞,1/a)(1/a,∞)R​forward behaviory→+∞ as x↑1/ay→0 from below as x→∞y≡0.​
(102)
For a<0, y′>0, so its absolute value decreases toward zero. The lost zero solution of a differential equation is recovered by substitution into the original equation. Since f(x,y)=−y+exy2 is locally Lipschitz in y everywhere, the Picard-Lindelöf theorem ensures uniqueness through every finite point, including y=0. Thus a nonzero trajectory never meets or crosses zero at a finite x.
For a=1/2, the exact solution of a differential equation is y=e−x/(2−x) on (−∞,2). It first decreases, reaches a local minimum y(1)=e−1, then increases without bound. At the endpoint,
limx↑2​(2−x)y(x)=e−2.
(103)
The finite-time blowup comes from a simple zero of z at 2, where z′(2)=−e2, rather than from a singular coefficient in the first-order linear differential equation. The same algebraic expression for y is negative on (2,∞), but that is a separate solution of a differential equation on a disjoint interval; infinity supplies no finite joining value. For a=−1, y=−e−x/(1+x) on (−1,∞), giving a distinct decaying branch whose finite blowup is instead in the past.
The direction field also explains the positive turning point. Its nullclines are y=0 and y=e−x, because
y′=y(exy−1).
(104)
Above the positive nullcline, y′>0; between it and zero, y′<0; below zero, y′>0. The moving nullcline y=e−x is not a solution of a differential equation: its derivative is −e−x, whereas the prescribed slope there is zero. The a=1/2 trajectory crosses it at x=1, exactly its local minimum.
