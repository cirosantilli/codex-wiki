Nucleic acid

 Home Chemistry Polymer
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 13
A nucleic acid is a polymer built from linked nucleotides. The two principal kinds are DNA and RNA. Each nucleotide contributes a sugar, a nucleobase, and phosphate to the chain; the nucleobase is only the letter-bearing component, not the entire nucleotide. DNA uses deoxyribose, whereas RNA uses ribose. A chain of six nucleotides is therefore not six free nucleobases placed beside one another. See NHGRI: nucleic acids.
The chemical scaffold and the variable lettering do different jobs. Covalent bonds maintain the sugar–phosphate backbone, while the order of attached nucleobases specifies the nucleic acid sequence. A phosphodiester bond links the 3′ position of one sugar to the 5′ position of the next through phosphate. These position numbers refer to carbon chemical atoms in the sugar; they define a strand's direction even when it bends or forms a circle. See Brown, Genomes, 2nd edition: the human genome.
This division is useful: the backbone supplies a connected physical record, and base pairing allows its letters to recognize partners. DNA replication copies that record, and transcription uses a DNA template to make RNA. RNA can also fold into functional shapes through base pairing within one chain; a nucleic acid sequence specifies chemical order, not a requirement for a straight shape. The beauty is that the same kind of polymer can carry a message and form a structure that acts on messages.
