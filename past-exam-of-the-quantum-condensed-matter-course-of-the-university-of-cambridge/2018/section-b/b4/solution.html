Energy and momentum measurement. In angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy, monochromatic photons illuminate a clean surface in vacuum, and an electron energy analyzer measures the outgoing kinetic energies and emission directions. Measuring both gives the occupied electronic band structure, rather than only a momentum-integrated distribution of removal energies.
Choose a common energy reference for initial and final states. Let Ei​ be the initial electron energy, EF​ the Fermi energy, and Evac​=EF​+ϕ the vacuum level immediately outside the sample. The binding energy is EB​=EF​−Ei​≥0. For a direct emission event without additional losses, conservation of energy gives
Ef​=Ei​+ℏω=Evac​+Ekin​,Ekin​=ℏω−ϕ−EB​.​
(35)
Here the work function ϕ is the energy from the Fermi level to the local vacuum. The initial and final energies are total single-electron energies on the same scale, while Ekin​ is measured above vacuum. The diagram illustrates each interval independently.
Neglecting ultraviolet photon momentum, photoemission momentum conversion gives
k∥​=ℏ2me​Ekin​​​sinθ,​
(36)
where θ is measured from the surface normal. Parallel crystal momentum is conserved modulo a surface reciprocal lattice vector; the surface breaks perpendicular translation symmetry. A three-dimensional sample therefore generally needs a final-state model to infer perpendicular momentum. The stated two-dimensional sheet avoids that ambiguity. The kinetic energy used in this formula is the sample-vacuum value; if the analyzer and sample have different work functions, their contact potential must be included when converting the analyzer's reading, as in photoelectron binding energy reference.
Spectral information. Define ϵ=Ei​−EF​=−EB​ and the electron spectral function by A(k,ϵ)=−ImGR(k,ϵ)/π. In the sudden approximation, the basic photoemission intensity and spectral function relation is
I(k,ϵ)=C∣M(k,ℏω,polarization)∣2f(ϵ)A(k,ϵ),​
(37)
before instrumental broadening and background are included. Here f is the Fermi-Dirac distribution, and M is the transition matrix element. The electron removal spectral function contains the occupied part of the spectrum. In a noninteracting band it has a delta peak at the band energy; interactions shift and broaden peaks and can add incoherent weight. Thus peak trajectories reveal dispersion, while line shapes can constrain lifetimes after resolution effects are accounted for. Dividing by known matrix and occupation factors can estimate A where those factors are appreciable; intensity alone is not identical to A, and a missing peak can reflect a matrix-element suppression. States well above EF​ are not accessible to ordinary low-temperature photoemission.
Two independent band-structure probes. First, the de Haas-van Alphen effect measures oscillatory magnetization as Landau levels cross the Fermi level when a magnetic field changes. The inverse-field frequency is
F=2πeℏ​Sext​,Δ(1/B)=F1​,
(38)
where Sext​ is an extremal Fermi surface cross-sectional area perpendicular to the field. Changing field orientation maps its shape. This reveals the momentum-space geometry of occupied bands; the related Shubnikov-de Haas effect measures oscillatory resistance.
Second, absorption or reflectance spectroscopy detects interband transitions. Photon energies at absorption edges and peaks constrain the band gap and interband energy differences. For a direct band gap, negligible photon momentum selects approximately vertical transitions in the band diagram, weighted by the joint density of states and transition matrix elements. An indirect transition needs a phonon to carry the extra crystal momentum. These measurements provide evidence beyond a free-electron parabola, although an optical spectrum alone does not reconstruct every band's dispersion.
Reading the graphite spectra. For each direction, follow a moving peak through successive angles and convert each peak's own kinetic energy to k∥​; angle is not itself proportional to momentum over this wide range. The prominent dispersing valence feature starts at roughly 7–9eV binding energy near Γ and moves towards lower binding energy as k∥​ increases. Along ΓK it approaches the Fermi level at the corner, then turns back to larger binding energy on continuing beyond the corner. Along ΓM it reaches a finite minimum binding energy of roughly 3–4eV near the edge midpoint and turns back; it does not reach the Fermi level there. Additional deeper peaks describe other occupied valence branches, with visibility varying across the spectra.
A useful qualitative description is the upper occupied π band of the nearest-neighbour model of graphene: Eπ​=−t∣f(k)∣. It rises from −3t at Γ to zero at K, but only to −t at M. The schematic curve in the figure captures those two different behaviours. It is not a full multi-orbital assignment or a quantitative fit to graphite, whose deeper valence features are also visible in the supplied spectra.
Angle needed to reach the corner. The corner's parallel wavevector is
kK​=3a4π​=3(0.246nm)4π​=17.03nm−1=1.703A˚−1.
(39)
Use the near-zero binding energy of the upper valence band at that point. With the sample work function interpreted as the stated 3.8eV separation below vacuum,
Ekin​(K)≃19.5−3.8=15.7eV,ℏ2me​Ekin​​​=20.30nm−1.
(40)
Hence
θK​≃sin−1(20.3017.03​)=57.0∘.​
(41)
The energy and angle readings in the PDF are approximate. If a peak at the corner were instead assigned a small binding energy EB​, the estimate becomes
sinθK​=2me​(15.7eV−EB​)​ℏkK​​.
(42)
For EB​=0–1eV this gives about 57–60∘, consistent with the near-Fermi feature in that angular range. Substituting the full photon energy for the kinetic energy would omit the work-function cost and underestimate the required angle.
