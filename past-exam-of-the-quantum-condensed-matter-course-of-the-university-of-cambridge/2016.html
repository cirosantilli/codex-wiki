In the three-dimensional Debye model, the lattice has 3N normal modes for N atoms. Replacing their dispersion by a linear acoustic dispersion up to a cutoff gives the Debye heat capacity
Cm​(T)=9NkB​(θD​T​)3∫0θD​/T​(ex−1)2x4ex​dx.
(1)
At low temperature the upper limit can be replaced by infinity. Since the integral then equals 4π4/15,
Cm​(T)∼512π4​NkB​(θD​T​)3(T≪θD​).​
(2)
At high temperature each harmonic mode contributes kB​ by the equipartition theorem, giving the Dulong-Petit law
Cm​(T)⟶3NkB​(T≫θD​).​
(3)
For one mole replace NkB​ by R. The curve starts with zero slope, rises as T3, and levels off toward the classical limit. This is the lattice contribution; a metal's electronic heat capacity is a separate low-temperature term.
The Debye wavevector follows by counting three polarizations inside the cutoff sphere:
3(2π)3V​34πkD3​​=3N,kD​=(6π2n)1/3.
(4)
With the supplied speed of sound treated as the effective Debye speed, ωD​=vkD​ and
θD​=kB​ℏv​(6π2n)1/3≃kB​ℏ(3600)​(6π2×5.9×1028)1/3≃4.17×102K.​
(5)
Here kD​≃1.52×1010m−1. Real crystals have different longitudinal and transverse sound speeds; the one-speed estimate uses the stated value, rather than inferring those unprovided branches.

For nondegenerate electrons in a parabolic conduction band,
n(T)=Nc​(T)exp[−kB​TΔ(T)​],Δ(T)=Ec​(T)−μ(T),Nc​(T)∝T3/2
(9)
when the density-of-states effective mass and valley multiplicity are fixed. The intrinsic carrier extrapolation across a crossover must therefore specify how the activation energy changes with temperature.
The usual intended estimate treats the quoted 0.9eV as a constant intrinsic activation energy between the two temperatures, and identifies the crossover by ni​(Tc​)≃next​. Then
ni​(T1​)ni​(Tc​)​=(T1​Tc​​)3/2exp[−kB​0.9eV​(Tc​1​−T1​1​)].
(10)
For T1​=350K, Tc​=280K and kB​=8.6173×10−5eVK−1, the exponent is −7.46 and the prefactor is 0.83/2=0.7155. Hence
next​≃(2.3×1016)(0.7155)e−7.46≃9.5×1012cm−3under the constant-activation approximation.​
(11)
Equivalently, a fixed-gap intrinsic model with Eg​/2=0.9eV gives this result. A crossover is gradual: if next​ denotes the net ionized donor density and ni​(Tc​)=next​, charge neutrality gives the actual electron population n(Tc​)=(1+5​)next​/2, rather than exactly next​.
There are two limits to interpreting the numerical estimate literally. First, the printed data specify Ec​−μ only at 350K, without specifying Eg​ or the valence density of states. They do not mathematically determine Δ(280K): without the intended activation assumption the answer remains
next​≃n(350)(350280​)3/2exp[−kB​(280)Δ(280)​+kB​(350)0.9eV​].
(12)
Second, using the original PDF's 0.9eV and density implies Nc​(350)≃2.1×1029cm−3, extraordinarily large for a conventional semiconductor. This confirms that the numerical data should not be treated as a realistic material determination. No unprinted decimal-point correction has been assumed.

A junction field-effect transistor has a conducting channel between source and drain, with gate regions forming reverse-biased p-n junctions to that channel. In an n-channel device, a more negative gate-source electric potential difference increases the reverse bias and widens the depletion region. The conducting cross-section shrinks, reducing the drain electric current. The gate ideally draws only a small leakage electric current, giving a high input resistance; the source-drain supply provides the controlled output power. Carrier motion in a p-channel device reverses the relevant polarities.
At small positive VDS​, the channel conducts approximately as a resistor. As the potential rises from source to drain, the local gate-channel reverse bias increases toward the drain. The depletion layers are therefore thicker there. For a symmetric n channel of half-thickness a, uniformly doped at density ND​, the one-sided depletion approximation gives
w(y)≃eND​2εs​​[Vbi​+V(y)−VGS​]​,
(13)
where w is the depletion width from each side and Vbi​ the built-in junction potential. The neutral channel closes locally when w=a. Define the positive geometrical depletion electric potential difference UP​=eND​a2/(2εs​). The approximate drain-end pinch-off condition is
VDS,sat​≃UP​−Vbi​+VGS​.​
(14)
More negative gate bias makes this saturation occur at a smaller drain electric potential difference.
Pinch-off in a junction field-effect transistor does not mean that the drain electric current vanishes. Electrons arriving through the remaining neutral channel are swept across the high-field depleted region into the drain. Further increases in VDS​ mostly fall across that region, so the electric current changes only weakly: this is the saturation regime used for amplification. Channel shortening gives a finite output slope in real devices. Excessive reverse electric potential difference eventually causes breakdown, beyond the normal saturation model.
A sufficiently negative gate electric potential difference can instead deplete the entire channel from the source end and produce cutoff. In the simplified geometry its threshold is VGS,off​≃Vbi​−UP​<0. A commonly used approximate transfer law in saturation is
ID​≃IDSS​(1−VGS,off​VGS​​)2,VGS,off​≤VGS​≤0.
(15)
Here IDSS​ is the saturated drain electric current at zero gate-source bias. The square law is a useful device approximation, not an exact result for arbitrary channel profiles. It connects field control of depletion to a measurable transfer characteristic while keeping drain-end pinch-off distinct from complete gate cutoff.

Direct exchange is a quantum-mechanical spin-dependent energy arising from the Coulomb interaction and the required antisymmetry of a two-electron state. It requires spatial overlap of the relevant orbitals. It is not the classical magnetic dipole force between two small magnets, and it can be much stronger than that force.
For an explicit fixed-orbital illustration, take orthonormal spatial orbitals a,b. Orthonormality means ⟨a∣b⟩=0; it does not require their spatial densities to occupy disjoint regions. The normalized spatial states are
Ψ±​(1,2)=2​a(1)b(2)±b(1)a(2)​.
(16)
The symmetric spatial state combines with the antisymmetric spin-one-half singlet state, and the antisymmetric spatial state combines with a symmetric spin-one-half triplet state, so that the complete fermionic state is antisymmetric.
For Coulomb potential V12​=e2/(4πε∣r1​−r2​∣), distinguish the direct electrostatic integral and exchange integral:
D=∫d3r1​d3r2​∣a(1)∣2V12​∣b(2)∣2,
(17)
K=∫d3r1​d3r2​a∗(1)b(1)V12​b∗(2)a(2).
(18)
The Coulomb exchange splitting of two orbitals gives
ES​=ϵa​+ϵb​+D+K,ET​=ϵa​+ϵb​+D−K,ES​−ET​=2K.​
(19)
The repulsive Coulomb potential makes K≥0 in this model. Thus the triplet is favored when the orbitals have nonzero exchange overlap. Its antisymmetric spatial wavefunction suppresses close electron encounters and reduces their repulsion. Since the Coulomb interaction contains no explicit spin operator, the spin dependence enters through the allowed spatial symmetry.
For dimensionless spin operators si​=σi​/2, whose pair eigenvalues are 1/4 in the triplet and −3/4 in the singlet, the same splitting is represented by
Hex​=C−2Ks1​⋅s2​.​
(20)
This explicitly states the sign convention: positive K here favors parallel spins. A convention H=Js1​⋅s2​ would instead have J=−2K.
In a crystal, direct exchange therefore depends sensitively on orbital extent, overlap and interatomic spacing. It often decreases rapidly as localized orbitals separate. The fixed-orbital example does not prove that every material must be ferromagnetic: orbital rearrangement and competing kinetic or virtual-hopping processes alter the singlet-triplet balance. In particular, antiferromagnetic superexchange through an intermediate ligand is a different mechanism and should not be confused with direct overlap exchange.

A magnetic field quantizes a charged carrier's cyclotron motion into Landau levels. For an isotropic parabolic band, neglecting spin splitting,
εℓ,kz​​=ℏωc​(ℓ+21​)+2m∗ℏ2kz2​​,ωc​=m∗eB​,ℓ=0,1,2,….
(21)
The degeneracy of each orbital level per unit perpendicular area is eB/h per spin. Increasing the field changes both the level spacing and the number of states in each level. Successive levels cross the Fermi energy, producing an oscillatory density of states and hence quantum oscillations in thermodynamic and transport quantities.
For a general closed semiclassical orbit, Onsager quantization relates its wavevector-space area to field:
Ak​(εℓ​)=ℏ2πeB​(ℓ+γ).
(22)
Setting this area to an extremal Fermi-surface cross-section gives successive inverse-field crossings separated by
Δ(B1​)=ℏAext​2πe​,F=2πeℏAext​​.​
(23)
A typical oscillatory contribution is proportional to cos(2πF/B+φ). The oscillations are periodic in 1/B, not in B. In three dimensions, extremal cross-sections dominate because contributions from other longitudinal momenta cancel by phase averaging. Rotating the field therefore maps the shape of the Fermi surface.
Oscillations in magnetization are the de Haas-van Alphen effect; oscillations in longitudinal electrical resistivity are the Shubnikov-de Haas effect. They are most readily observed in clean samples at low temperature. The thermal damping factor is
RT​=sinhXX​,X=ℏωc​2π2kB​T​,
(24)
and a common first-harmonic disorder factor is RD​=exp[−π/(ωc​τq​)]. Thus the Landau spacing must be sufficiently large compared with thermal broadening and disorder broadening, approximately kB​T≲ℏωc​ and ωc​τq​≳1, with the displayed factors giving the quantitative suppression.
The temperature dependence measures the cyclotron effective mass, and the field-dependent damping probes the quantum lifetime τq​. That lifetime need not equal the momentum relaxation time controlling electrical conductivity: small-angle scattering can broaden Landau levels while relaxing electric current inefficiently. Spin splitting can generate further modulation or resolved spin structure. Oscillations are not the same as the ordinary smooth magnetoresistance or cyclotron resonance; they probe quantized orbital state counting near the Fermi energy.

mass as band curvature. In a periodic potential, a weakly forced electron is usefully described by a wave packet confined to one energy band. Its group velocity is
vi​=ℏ1​∂ki​∂εn​​,ℏk˙j​=Fj​.
(25)
Differentiating the velocity gives
v˙i​=∑j​(m∗−1)ij​Fj​,(m∗−1)ij​=ℏ21​∂ki​∂kj​∂2εn​​.​
(26)
This effective-mass tensor describes the band response, not a change of the electron's bare rest mass. Along a principal direction, a steep upward curvature gives a small positive mass and a flatter curvature a larger one. Near a band maximum the electron curvature and effective mass are negative; the corresponding missing-electron description uses positive electron hole masses. Anisotropy produces distinct principal masses, and strong nonparabolicity makes them momentum dependent. The single-band semiclassical description assumes fields weak and slowly varying enough to avoid substantial interband transitions.
Lattice symmetry and Bloch's theorem. Use the active spatial translation operator Ta​ψ(r)=ψ(r−a). A lattice-periodic scalar potential satisfies V(r+a)=V(r), while the kinetic term is translation invariant. Therefore [H,Ta​]=0. The lattice translations also commute with one another, so they can be diagonalized simultaneously with H, including within a degenerate energy subspace.
For a common eigenstate, write Ta​ψ=t(a)ψ. Unitarity gives ∣t(a)∣=1, and the group law gives t(a+b)=t(a)t(b). On primitive Bravais vectors aj​, choose t(aj​)=e−ik⋅aj​. Every lattice vector is an integer combination of these generators, hence
Ta​ψnk​=e−ik⋅aψnk​,ψnk​(r+a)=eik⋅aψnk​(r).
(27)
The vector k is defined modulo a reciprocal lattice vector. The band index labels the different energy eigenstates with the same translation character. Define unk​(r)=e−ik⋅rψnk​(r). The phase factors cancel under a lattice translation, proving Bloch's theorem in its usual form:
ψnk​(r)=eik⋅runk​(r),unk​(r+a)=unk​(r).​
(28)
This is the Translation-character proof of Bloch theorem; the commutator guarantees a basis of such states, even when an arbitrary superposition of different translation characters is not itself a translation eigenstate.
Periodic Hamiltonian. Acting once with momentum gives p(eik⋅ru)=eik⋅r(p+ℏk)u. Applying it again and cancelling the common plane-wave factor yields
H(k)unk​=[2m(p+ℏk)2​+V(r)]unk​=εn​(k)unk​.​
(29)
The Bloch Hamiltonian acts on lattice-periodic functions in a unit cell. Expanding its square separates a zero-momentum Hamiltonian, a term linear in k and an identity term quadratic in k.
Expansion about the zone centre. Let ∣n⟩=un0​, En​=εn​(0), and normalize all these states using a unit-cell inner product. For the real scalar Hamiltonian in the question, a nondegenerate zone-centre eigenfunction can be chosen real. Then
⟨n∣p∣n⟩=−2iℏ​∫cell​∇(un02​)d3r=0,
(30)
because opposite unit-cell faces cancel. Equivalently, time-reversal symmetry forbids a nonzero electric current in this nondegenerate spinless state. This explains why no linear energy term appears; it should not simply be omitted without a symmetry or extremum argument.
The first-order energy shift is therefore ℏ2k2/(2m). In the second-order sum the quadratic term is proportional to the identity and has no off-diagonal matrix elements. Only the linear momentum vertex contributes. Second-order nondegenerate perturbation theory gives the momentum matrix-element expansion of band energy
εn​(k)=En​+2mℏ2k2​+m2ℏ2​l=n∑​En​−El​∣⟨n∣k⋅p∣l⟩∣2​+O(∣k∣4).​
(31)
Hermitian operator makes the two reciprocal matrix elements complex conjugates. All other periodic eigenstates at k=0 enter the sum. The remainder is even because time-reversal symmetry gives εn​(k)=εn​(−k) near this isolated state. At a degeneracy the denominators signal the need for degenerate perturbation theory instead.
mass ratio and the printed formula. Write pnli​=⟨n∣pi​∣l⟩. Differentiating the quadratic energy yields
(m∗−1)ij​=mδij​​+m22​l=n∑​En​−El​Re(pnli​plnj​)​.​
(32)
For a specified unit direction e, the resulting scalar mass ratio is
me∗​m​=1+m2​l=n∑​En​−El​∣⟨n∣e⋅p∣l⟩∣2​.​
(33)
In an isotropic three-dimensional band, averaging equivalent directions gives
m∗m​=1+3m2​l=n∑​En​−El​∣⟨n∣p∣l⟩∣2​.​
(34)
Coupling to lower bands contributes positively to inverse mass; coupling to higher bands contributes negatively. Their signs and matrix elements determine the actual curvature.
The original PDF prints m∗=ℏ2[∇k2​εn​]−1. Taken literally as a three-dimensional Laplacian, this misses a factor of three for an isotropic scalar mass. The correct identities are
ℏ2∇k2​εn​​=tr(m∗−1),m∗=∇k2​εn​3ℏ2​in three isotropic dimensions.
(35)
The printed expression would return m/3 even for the free dispersion ℏ2k2/(2m). It is correct in one dimension, or if its second derivative is interpreted directionally. For completeness, its literal three-dimensional trace ratio would instead be 3+(2/m)∑l=n​∣⟨n∣p∣l⟩∣2/(En​−El​); that is the trace of the dimensionless inverse-mass tensor, not the usual scalar m/m∗.

Relaxation-time model. The Drude model treats mobile charge carriers as independent particles accelerated by the Lorentz force between scattering events. Collisions randomize the drift momentum toward local equilibrium and are represented by a uniform, field-independent momentum relaxation time. For the present calculation the carrier density, isotropic mass and relaxation time are fixed, the fields are uniform and stationary, and quantum orbital quantization and intercarrier correlations are neglected. The steady drift velocity obeys
0=q(E+v×B)−τm​v.
(36)
Here n is the number of carriers of that species per unit volume, q their signed charge, m>0 their inertial or isotropic band mass, τ the momentum relaxation time, and σ0​ their single-species electrical conductivity at zero field.
One-carrier tensor. With B=Bz^ and J=nqv, solving for the electric field gives
E=nq2τm​J−nq1​J×B.
(37)
Since (J×B)x​=BJy​ and (J×B)y​=−BJx​,
Ex​=nq2τm​Jx​−nqB​Jy​,Ey​=nqB​Jx​+nq2τm​Jy​.
(38)
Thus the resistivity tensor, defined by E=ρJ, is
ρq​=(σ0−1​B/(nq)​−B/(nq)σ0−1​​),σ0​=mnq2τ​.​
(39)
Its off-diagonal signs follow from the stated axes and signed q. For zero transverse electric current, Ey​/Jx​=B/(nq) is the Hall electrical resistivity; reversing charge reverses it. The single-species longitudinal electrical resistivity is independent of B in this ideal isotropic model.
Adding electrons and electron holes. Let both species have density n and charges q=±e, with e>0. The stipulated equal zero-field electrical conductivity means equal positive charge carrier mobility μ=σ0​/(ne), even if their masses and momentum relaxation times separately differ. Put r=1/σ0​ and h=B/(ne). Inverting each electrical resistivity matrix gives
σ+​=r2+h21​(r−h​hr​),σ−​=r2+h21​(rh​−hr​).
(40)
Both species experience the same electric field, so their electric current densities add. Therefore it is their conductivity tensors, rather than their resistivity tensors, that must be summed. The Hall parts cancel and
σtot​=1+(μB)22σ0​​I2​,ρtot​=2σ0​1+(μB)2​I2​.​
(41)
This is compensated two-carrier magnetoresistance. Although the net Hall field vanishes, each carrier species is bent in its own direction; maintaining the total longitudinal electric current requires an increasing longitudinal field. Equal carrier numbers alone do not ensure zero Hall response if charge carrier mobilities differ; both equalities given here are used.
Large-field limit and quantum oscillations. Expanding the exact diagonal entry,
ρxx​=ρyy​=2σ0​1​+2n2e2σ0​B2​.
(42)
For μB≫1,
ρxx​∼ρyy​∼2n2e2σ0​B2​,B≫σ0​ne​=eτtr​m​.​
(43)
Here the last equality applies to either species with its own mass and transport time; the stipulated charge carrier mobilities give the same threshold. This is the classical strong-field condition ωc​τtr​≫1: carriers turn substantially before their drift momentum relaxes.
Observable quantum oscillations additionally require sufficiently sharp Landau levels and a sufficiently cold Fermi-Dirac distribution, approximately
ωc​τq​≳1,ℏωc​≳kB​T.
(44)
When τq​≃τtr​, the disorder field scale is comparable to the onset scale for large classical magnetoresistance. The thermal condition can demand a much larger field, and when τq​≪τtr​ even the disorder criterion is stronger. The quantitative damping factors in Q2(c) show that these are crossover scales rather than sharp visibility boundaries. A large smooth B2 magnetoresistance therefore does not itself demonstrate Landau level; it can exist while thermal smearing suppresses oscillations. Conversely, the asymptotic condition ωc​τtr​≫1 is stronger than merely reaching unity, so there is no universal ordering of the two experimental onset fields without temperatures and lifetime information.
Scaling function. The material's actual zero-field longitudinal electrical resistivity is ρtot​(0)=1/(2σ0​). Dividing the exact answer by this value gives
ρxx​(0)ρxx​(B)​=1+(neBσ0​​)2=F(Bσ0​),F(x)=1+n2e2x2​.​
(45)
The assumption that n stays constant is essential to eliminating further temperature-dependent parameters. Bσ0​ is a dimensionful scaling variable; Bσ0​/(ne)=μB is its dimensionless version. The corresponding fractional increase is [ρ(B)−ρ(0)]/ρ(0)=(μB)2.
What the supplied data test. In the original PDF's left panel, the field dependence is strongly temperature dependent. In the right panel, plotting the fractional magnetoresistance against the squared rescaled field makes the displayed 125, 150, 175, 200 and 225 K curves collapse approximately onto one straight line, over the field intervals where they overlap. This supports the proposed one-parameter scaling and, on that range, the quadratic field law. The 100 K curve does not collapse: it bends away and has a different slope in the rescaled plot. The indicated scaling is therefore supported for the displayed 125–225 K data, not for the entire displayed temperature range. The graph alone does not locate a sharp transition between 100 and 125 K or establish behavior above 225 K.
This empirical collapse is Kohler's rule. It is consistent with a common temperature-dependent charge carrier mobility or relaxation scale and a fixed carrier population; it is not a unique proof of exact compensation or of one microscopic scattering mechanism. A change in relative scattering times, carrier population or Fermi-surface structure could spoil the collapse, and this figure alone does not select the cause of the 100 K departure.
There is a factor-of-two normalization convention in the printed text. The σ0​ introduced for each species gives ρtot​(0)=1/(2σ0​), whereas the final paragraph calls 1/σ0​ “ρ0​.” If ρ0​ denotes the experimentally measured zero-field electrical resistivity, the consistent slope is
ρ0​ρ(B)−ρ0​​=4n2e21​(ρ0​B​)2,ρ0​=ρtot​(0).
(46)
If one instead keeps the literal single-species definition ρs​=1/σ0​, the same model has [ρ(B)−ρs​]/ρs​=−1/2+[B/ρs​]2/(2n2e2). The choice rescales and offsets the axes by temperature-independent constants, so it does not change which temperature curves collapse, but it must be resolved before inferring a carrier density from the slope.
