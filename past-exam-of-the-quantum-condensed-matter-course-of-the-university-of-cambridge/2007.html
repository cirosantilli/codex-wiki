a OurBigBook.com
Long anisometric molecules cannot pack independently. In the Onsager excluded-volume mechanism, two slender rods of length L and diameter D exclude a relative-position volume approximately 2L2D∣sinγ∣, where γ is their mutual angle. Aligning rods reduces this excluded volume, increasing the translational entropy available at a fixed number density. It simultaneously reduces orientational entropy. At sufficiently high concentration the positional gain wins, producing a nematic liquid crystal even for purely repulsive rods.
The nematic director is apolar: n and −n describe the same orientation. The molecules retain liquid-like positional disorder, in contrast to a smectic phase with layered order. A typical dimensionless concentration controlling the slender-rod competition is cL2D, although finite aspect ratio, flexibility and attractions change the transition. The mechanism illustrates that ordering can increase total entropy: the lost orientational freedom is compensated by less mutual obstruction.

At a micrometre scale, viscous forces normally dominate inertia. As an illustrative Newtonian estimate, L=1μm, U=10μms−1, ρ=103kgm−3 and η=10−3Pas give a Reynolds number ρUL/η=10−5. The surrounding flow is approximately a Stokes flow, satisfying −∇p+η∇2u=0 and ∇⋅u=0.
A body of radius 0.5μm translated at that speed in an unbounded Newtonian liquid would need Stokes drag 6πηaU≃9.4×10−14N. It stops almost immediately when propulsion stops. The scallop theorem rules out propulsion by a single reciprocal stroke in this ideal regime; a rotating helix or a nonreciprocal cycle of shapes can propel it. Brownian motion also randomizes position and orientation: Stokes–Einstein relation scales as a−1 and rotational diffusion as a−3, so retaining heading becomes harder as size decreases.
The Newtonian calculation is a scale estimate, not a complete model of blood. Vessel walls alter drag, the imposed flow advects the body, and nearby red blood cells make the medium heterogeneous at this scale. Non-Newtonian fluid effects and deformable obstacles can matter; steering must be assessed against those effects rather than inertial coasting.

A lipid bilayer is fluid within its plane, so a bare equilibrated bilayer has no static in-plane shear modulus. Stretching its area is nevertheless expensive, whereas bending is much softer. The Helfrich membrane energy expresses this distinction through
F=∫[2κ​(2H−C0​)2+κˉK+σ]dA,
(5)
with membrane bending rigidity κ, mean curvature H, Gaussian curvature K, spontaneous curvature C0​ and membrane tension σ. Area dilation additionally costs approximately KA​(ΔA)2/(2A0​). For a closed surface of fixed topology, the Gauss-Bonnet theorem makes the integrated Gaussian-curvature term constant.
These energies control vesicle shapes, budding and the ease of forming membrane tubes. Asymmetric leaflet composition or bound proteins can change the preferred curvature. Real cell membranes can also be coupled to a cytoskeleton, which supplies shear elasticity absent from a free lipid bilayer. Thus a red-cell shape cannot generally be inferred from the bare bilayer model alone.
Two useful measurements probe different terms. Optical tracking of thermal membrane flickering measures fluctuation amplitudes; for small height modes, a normalization with hq​=A−1∫he−iq⋅xd2x gives ⟨∣hq​∣2⟩=kB​T/[A(κq4+σq2)]. Micropipette aspiration controls pressure while measuring the membrane drawn into a narrow pipette, linking apparent area change to tension and, at larger strain, area dilation. The observables test the elastic coefficients rather than merely showing a membrane image.

In diffusion-controlled aggregation, particles meet through Brownian motion and stick with probability close to one on encounter. Diffusion-limited cluster aggregation allows already formed clusters to diffuse and join; diffusion-limited aggregation in its usual single-seed model instead adds diffusing individual particles to one growing cluster. First-contact sticking shields inner regions from later arrivals, favouring open branched structures. A reaction-limited cluster aggregation model has a small sticking probability and permits many failed encounters before attachment.
For two dilute spherical species, the Smoluchowski diffusion-controlled encounter rate is k12​=4π(D1​+D2​)(a1​+a2​). In relative coordinates, solve the steady diffusion equation with an absorbing surface at R=a1​+a2​ and concentration c∞​ at infinity: c(r)=c∞​(1−R/r). The inward diffusive flux is 4π(D1​+D2​)Rc∞​, proving the rate coefficient. Interaction barriers and non-spherical aggregates require changes to this ideal encounter calculation.
Cluster growth can be tested by microscopy and light scattering. A mass fractal dimension df​ relates particle number to cluster size through N∝Rdf​; a scattering regime between the monomer and cluster length scales can reveal that scaling. The measured morphology depends on whether whole clusters move, on the sticking rule, and on possible bond rearrangement.

In a dilute solution, solvent-mediated hydrodynamic interactions couple distant chain segments. The Zimm model describes the resulting cooperative dynamics: an intermediate-time segment mean-square displacement scales as t2/3, while whole-chain translation has DZ​∼kB​T/(ηs​R). Once internal modes relax, each tagged segment follows the chain's ordinary long-time diffusion, with mean-square displacement asymptotic to 6DZ​t.
In a concentrated solution or polymer melt, hydrodynamic coupling is screened. The unentangled Rouse model has an intermediate t1/2 segment regime and whole-chain diffusivity proportional to N−1. Long chains with polymer entanglement are additionally confined by neighbouring chains: reptation gives a slower intermediate t1/4 regime and an asymptotic chain diffusivity scaling approximately as N−2. The time windows depend on segment, entanglement and terminal relaxation times, so these powers do not all describe the same time interval.
After sufficiently long times both dilute and concentrated chains undergo ordinary translational diffusion. The main long-time difference is the coefficient and its chain-length dependence, with entanglement strongly reducing mobility when the chains are long enough; concentration alone does not guarantee an entangled regime.

Creating a liquid surface changes the molecular environment and costs interfacial free energy. In the dispersion-only cleavage model, the attractive Van der Waals force per area has magnitude A/(6πh3). Separating two contacting halves from a molecular cutoff h0​ to infinity takes work per area
AW​=∫h0​∞​6πh3AH​​dh=12πh02​AH​​.
(12)
Here AH​ denotes the Hamaker constant, to distinguish it from an area. Cleavage creates two free surfaces, giving the Hamaker estimate of liquid surface tension
γ≃24πh02​AH​​.​
(13)
The continuum attraction diverges at zero separation and neglects molecular impenetrability and short-range repulsion. The cutoff is therefore an effective contact separation of molecular size, commonly of order 0.1–0.3nm, not a limit that can be taken to zero.
An experimental limitation is the strong temperature dependence of surface tension. For water in equilibrium with its vapour, the recommended experimental values decrease from 71.98mNm−1 at 25∘C to 58.92mNm−1 at 100∘C, and the fitted tension vanishes at the critical point. A fixed pairwise cleavage energy misses interfacial entropy, changing density and many-body correlations.[ref] The dispersion-only model is unsuitable when cohesion is dominated by hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions or metallic bonding; liquid metals are a clear example of the latter failure.

The mobile electrons in a metal screen an incident optical field. In the weak-damping Drude model, ε(ω)≃ε∞​−ωp2​/ω2. Below the screened plasma frequency, the real dielectric response is negative, the wave is evanescent inside the metal, and a clean surface is highly reflective. Above the plasma edge, the response can become positive and propagating waves are allowed, explaining the qualitative transmission window at sufficiently high, often ultraviolet, frequencies.
This is a free-carrier argument, not a statement that every metal is transparent throughout the ultraviolet. Interband absorption, damping and sample thickness can still produce strong absorption above the plasma edge.

Angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy maps occupied energy bands: ultraviolet or x-ray photons eject electrons, and an energy- and angle-resolving analyzer measures Ekin​ and emission direction. The photoelectric effect gives the binding energy EB​=hν−Φ−Ekin​; the parallel momentum follows from k∥​=2mEkin​​sinθ/ℏ, up to surface reciprocal vectors. Clean surfaces and controlled photon energy are needed, and an occupied-state measurement alone does not map an empty conduction band.
Scanning tunneling spectroscopy instead brings a conducting tip close to a surface and modulates the bias while recording the differential tunnel current. At low temperature with a smooth tip density of states and tunnelling matrix element, dI/dV probes the local density of states near energy EF​+eV. The signal is thermally broadened and depends on tunnelling selection, so it is not an unqualified map of the whole bulk band structure.
Bulk probes complement these surface measurements. In the de Haas-van Alphen effect, a low-temperature magnetization or torque measurement versus magnetic field gives quantum-oscillation frequencies F=ℏAext​/(2πe), measuring extremal Fermi surface sections; temperature damping gives a cyclotron effective mass. The linear electronic heat capacity gives the density of low-energy excitations at the Fermi level. Optical absorption measures transition thresholds and matrix-element-weighted joint densities, rather than a unique full dispersion by itself.

In a heavy fermion material, interactions of narrow, often f-derived states with conduction electrons produce low-energy quasiparticles with very large thermodynamic effective mass. A coherent Kondo lattice can form a narrow low-energy band, so the large mass is an interaction-renormalized response rather than a larger bare electron mass.
Two concrete experimental signatures are an enormous linear electronic heat capacity coefficient and a strongly enhanced quadratic low-temperature electrical resistivity. Calorimetry separates C=γT+βT3 by plotting C/T against T2; the intercept measures the electronic coefficient rather than a lattice contribution. Four-terminal transport tests the Fermi liquid law ρ=ρ0​+AT2. The low-temperature calorimetry and resistivity of CeAl3 found γ=1.62Jmol−1K−2 and A=35μΩcmK−2 below 0.2K, making both signatures quantitative.[ref]
An enhanced nearly temperature-independent spin susceptibility and a large quantum-oscillation cyclotron effective mass provide additional tests. Not every heavy-fermion compound has the same ordered ground state; magnetism or superconductivity can intervene before a simple normal Fermi-liquid limit is reached.

A partly filled one-dimensional energy band is especially sensitive to a lattice modulation at 2kF​. At half filling, 2kF​=π/a, so the Peierls distortion alternates the bond lengths and doubles the primitive unit cell. Mixing states at the Fermi points opens a band gap, lowering the energy of occupied states. In the ideal weak-coupling, zero-temperature chain, the electronic gain behaves as −u2ln(1/∣u∣) while the elastic cost is proportional to u2, favouring a small distortion. The dimerized tight-binding chain gives an explicit band calculation.
The structural signature is extra diffraction intensity at the modulation wavevector; optical absorption can detect the gap and transport can detect loss of metallic conduction. A dynamic precursor is a Kohn anomaly, with phonon softening near 2kF​. Inelastic neutron scattering of TTF-TCNQ, using a deuterated sample over 60–300K, found an anomaly near 2kF​ developing below about 150K as the 54K transition was approached. This probes a lattice mode coupled to the electronic instability rather than inferring distortion from resistance alone.[ref]
Away from half filling, the modulation need not simply double the lattice. Finite-temperature ordering in real quasi-one-dimensional solids also involves interchain coupling and fluctuations; the ideal isolated-chain argument alone does not establish a finite-temperature transition.

A direct band gap has its conduction minimum and valence maximum at the same crystal momentum. A photon can then connect the band-edge states with negligible momentum change. An indirect band gap requires an additional phonon to supply or remove momentum, making radiative recombination much less probable. Direct-gap materials such as gallium arsenide or gallium nitride are therefore usually better for a light-emitting diode than silicon.
For the graded junction, let q=∣e∣>0, εs​=ε0​εr​, and use electrostatic potential φ with Ex​=−φ′. Fully ionized dopants have charge density ρ=q(ND​−NA​)=qαx, so Poisson's equation is φ′′=−qαx/εs​. The paper's displayed plus sign corresponds to using the negative potential V=−φ, or a signed electron charge in that formula. The positive barrier magnitude and charge magnitude must be used consistently in the width below.
The intended compensation point is x=0, where ND​=NA​ and the net doping changes sign. A locally neutral model with comparable electron and hole mobilities has its lowest carrier conductivity near there. The depletion approximation neglects mobile-carrier conductivity throughout the layer, so it does not by itself locate an exact conductivity minimum; unequal mobilities or asymmetric carrier profiles can shift it.
Let the depletion edges be x=±d. With zero exterior field, integrating Ex′​=ρ/εs​ and imposing Ex​(±d)=0 gives the linearly graded p-n junction field
Ex​(x)=2εs​qα​(x2−d2),∣Ex​∣max​=2εs​qαd2​ at x=0.​

Their separation is the local band gap, and the equilibrium electrochemical potential is flat. In the orthogonal tight-binding approximation, choose localized orthonormal orbitals ∣j⟩ with on-site matrix element E0​ and real nearest-neighbour hopping integral t. The Bloch sum ∣k⟩=N−1/2∑j​eikja∣j⟩ is an eigenstate because each neighbour shift contributes a phase e±ika. Thus
E(k)=E0​+t(eika+e−ika)=E0​+2tcoska.​
(25)
The simple-cubic nearest-neighbour tight-binding band has six neighbours, giving E(k)=E0​+2t[cos(kx​a)+cos(ky​a)+cos(kz​a)]. The cosine sum ranges from −3 to 3, so the positive bandwidth is W=12∣t∣. The stated hopping formula is negative when E1​>0, so the printed 12t must be read as a bandwidth magnitude.

The formula grows at both very small and very large s; the small-separation divergence is a mathematical extrapolation into a regime where localized tight binding itself becomes unreliable.
