In a weak applied field, magnetic susceptibility relates magnetization to magnetic field intensity, M=χH. Its sign, temperature dependence and nonlinear field dependence distinguish several mechanisms.
Diamagnetism is the orbital response opposing an applied magnetic field, with χ<0. Closed-shell atoms and filled bands generally give a weak, nearly temperature-independent response. In a simple free electron metal, Landau diamagnetism contributes χL​=−χP​/3; the factor is specific to the isotropic parabolic-band model and is not universal in crystals.
Paramagnetism gives χ>0. Localized magnetic moments align statistically with the field against thermal disorder. For independent moments of angular momentum J,
χ=3kB​Tμ0​n(gμB​)2J(J+1)​,
(17)
which is the Curie law. Exchange interactions often lead approximately to the Curie–Weiss law, χ=C/(T−Θ), sufficiently far above any ordering temperature. The sign of Θ suggests predominantly parallel or antiparallel exchange, though it is not a complete classification. Conduction electrons instead exhibit Pauli paramagnetism:
χP​=μ0​μB2​g(EF​),
(18)
for g≃2 and a spin-summed density of states per unit volume. Only states close to the Fermi energy change occupation, making this response nearly temperature independent when kB​T≪EF​.
Ferromagnetism has spontaneous parallel magnetic order below a Curie temperature. Exchange interaction, not simply classical dipole attraction, provides the dominant alignment mechanism. In the convention H=−Jex​∑⟨ij⟩​Si​⋅Sj​, positive Jex​ favours alignment. Magnetic domains can make a ferromagnet's total magnetization vanish before an external field aligns its domains. Domain motion and anisotropy produce saturation, hysteresis, remanent magnetization and a reverse field measured by magnetic coercivity.
Antiferromagnetism has oppositely ordered sublattices below a Néel temperature; equal opposing moments give zero net magnetization while retaining a nonzero staggered order parameter. A susceptibility anomaly and magnetic neutron diffraction reveal this order even when bulk magnetization vanishes. Ferrimagnetism also has opposing sublattices but unequal contributions, giving a nonzero spontaneous net moment. Above their ordering temperatures both can become paramagnetic. Measurements with a magnetometer as a function of field and temperature, supplemented by magnetic diffraction, therefore distinguishes a compensated ordered material from an ordinary paramagnet.
