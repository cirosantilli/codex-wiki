This is the Joule-Thomson coefficient, μJT​=(∂T/∂P)H​, describing a temperature change during an isenthalpic pressure change. Here H is enthalpy, CP​=(∂H/∂T)P​ is the heat capacity at constant pressure, and V is volume, all for the same chosen amount of substance. Starting with dH=TdS+VdP and using the Gibbs free-energy Maxwell relation (∂S/∂P)T​=−(∂V/∂T)P​ gives
dH=CP​dT+[V−T(∂T∂V​)P​]dP.
(7)
Setting dH=0 proves
μJT​=CP​1​[T(∂T∂V​)P​−V].​
(8)
In a steady insulated throttle or porous plug, negligible kinetic- and potential-energy changes make the upstream and downstream enthalpies equal. A positive coefficient gives cooling on a pressure decrease; a negative one gives warming. For an ideal gas, V∝T at fixed P, so the coefficient is zero. Real-gas interactions permit cooling and an inversion curve, making the effect useful in gas refrigeration and liquefaction.
The measurement of the Joule-Thomson effect with a porous plug compares pressures and thermometers on the two sides of an insulated flow restriction. Suppressing external heat exchange and separating the readings from high-speed jets are part of making the enthalpy argument applicable; an arbitrary adiabatic expansion need not be isenthalpic.
