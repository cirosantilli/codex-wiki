Put z=eβμ, with β=1/(kB​T). For a single quantum state of energy ϵ, dilute Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics gives lnΞϵ​≃ze−βϵ. For bosons this is the leading expansion of −ln(1−ze−βϵ); the occupation must be small for that expansion. Thus the grand potential is
Φ(ϵ)=−kB​TlnΞϵ​≃−kB​Te−β(ϵ−μ).​
(23)
With the ground-state energy set to zero, a dilute classical gas requires eβμ≪1, giving negative μ. This sign is relative to the chosen energy zero. For an ideal Bose gas, μ approaches the ground energy from below at condensation, so it approaches zero with this convention.
The printed degeneracy formula requires nx​,ny​,nz​=0,1,2,…, rather than strictly positive integers. With zero-point energy omitted these are the appropriate oscillator quantum numbers. The stars and bars count of three nonnegative integers summing to n gives
dn​=(2n+2​)=n!2!(n+2)!​.​
(24)
There is one ground state at n=0. If the integers were strictly positive, the count would instead be (2n−1​) for n≥3, inconsistent with both the printed formula and its energy reference.
For large n, dn​≃n2/2. Consecutive energy shells are separated by ℏω, giving the continuum density of states
g(E)≃2ℏωn2​=2(ℏω)3E2​.​
(25)
Use this when kB​T≫ℏω, and integrate the single-state grand potential over energy:
Φtot​≃−2(ℏω)3kB​Teβμ​∫0∞​E2e−βEdE=−(ℏω)3(kB​T)4​eβμ,
(26)
because the integral is 2/β3. Particle number follows by differentiating at fixed temperature and trap frequency:
N=−∂μ∂Φ​=(ℏωkB​T​)3eβμ,μ=kB​Tln[N(kB​Tℏω​)3].​
(27)
These are the high-temperature expressions for the classical grand potential in a harmonic trap.
Extrapolating to μ=0 gives the requested transition scale
Tc​∼kB​ℏω​N1/3.​
(28)
It is an order-one estimate because Maxwell-Boltzmann occupations cease to be accurate near condensation. The Bose-Einstein distribution gives the more precise ideal-trap result Nexmax​=ζ(3)(kB​T/ℏω)3, and hence Tc​=(ℏω/kB​)[N/ζ(3)]1/3, about 0.94 times the estimate. Excess particles populate the ground state after the excited-state capacity saturates: Bose condensation in a three-dimensional harmonic trap.
The observation of Bose-Einstein condensation in rubidium in 1995 implemented laser precooling followed by radio-frequency evaporation from a magnetic trap. Imaging the released cloud revealed a narrow anisotropic component within a broader thermal population. This provides an experimental connection to the population-counting argument; the measured expansion also reflects interactions and imaging resolution, not just the ideal-trap density of states.[ref]
