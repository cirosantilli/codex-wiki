Use the colloidal probe technique: attach a sphere of known radius to the cantilever of an atomic force microscope and approach a chemically matched planar surface in the same liquid as the suspension. A laser reflected from the cantilever measures its deflection d; calibration of the spring constant kc​ converts this to force, F=kc​d. The surface separation is reconstructed from piezo motion after correcting for cantilever bending and defining the contact position. Slow approach reduces hydrodynamic drag; residual surface charge, roughness and hydration must be controlled when identifying a dispersion force.
The Derjaguin approximation accounts for the geometry. With interaction free energy per area W(h) between flat plates, F=2πReff​W(h), where Reff​=a for a sphere and plane but a/2 for equal spheres. Since WvdW​=−AH​/(12πh2), the sphere-plane force is −AH​a/(6h2); divide it by two to infer the equal-sphere result in the same near-contact approximation. Attractive force gradients can produce a jump to contact, limiting the directly accessible approach curve.
An actual demonstration attached a silica sphere of radius 3.5μm to an AFM force sensor and measured its interaction with a plane in sodium chloride solutions. Its double-layer contribution and short-distance deviations show why charge and surface conditions must be measured rather than simply presumed absent.[ref]
