a OurBigBook.com
A liquid crystal flows like a liquid while retaining some molecular order. In a nematic liquid crystal, elongated molecules have long-range orientational order but no ordinary positional lattice. The nematic director n specifies the preferred axis, with n and −n equivalent for head-tail-symmetric molecules. The nematic order parameter S=⟨(3cos2θ−1)/2⟩ distinguishes an isotropic distribution, S=0, from perfect parallel alignment, S=1. A smectic phase additionally has a periodic layered density modulation, while molecules remain mobile within layers.
The broken rotational symmetry allows birefringence and distinct elastic costs for nematic splay, nematic twist and nematic bend. Under crossed polarizers, rotating a thin aligned sample changes the transmitted intensity because its two optical polarization components acquire different phases. Heating through a nematic-to-isotropic transition removes that orientational birefringence. An electric field can rotate the director if the dielectric anisotropy of a nematic favors alignment, explaining the optical switching principle of a liquid-crystal cell. Molecular shape, temperature, concentration and surface anchoring determine which ordered phase appears.

The restoring force in ideal rubber elasticity is mainly entropic: stretching restricts the conformations of network strands. A Gaussian chain of Ns​ statistical segments of length b has extension free energy 3kB​TR2/(2Ns​b2), so its small-extension spring constant is proportional to T. For an affine network with active-strand number density νs​, the shear modulus is approximately G=νs​kB​T. Increasing the cross-link density normally raises G and shortens the strands; polymer entanglement can add effective constraints. Dangling ends and loops do not contribute like elastically active bridges, while junction fluctuations reduce the affine-network estimate.
For incompressible uniaxial stretching by ratio λ, the nominal tensile stress is G(λ−λ−2) in the ideal Gaussian-network model. Finite contour length causes stronger stiffening as strands approach full extension; swelling dilutes the active strands and changes their reference conformations. A direct demonstration is to hang a load from a stretched rubber strip and warm it gently: an entropy-dominated network tends to contract under that fixed load. This temperature dependence contrasts with a model based solely on stretching bond energies. The simple formula assumes equilibrium, adequate cross-linking and temperatures away from glass transitions or degradation.

DLVO theory combines the attractive Van der Waals force with repulsion between overlapping electrical double layers. A charged colloid attracts counterions; their electrostatic and osmotic response screens its field over the Debye length. Adding salt generally shortens this range and can lower the repulsive aggregation barrier, allowing particles to reach the strongly bound primary minimum. A shallow secondary minimum can instead give weak reversible association. Whether Brownian encounters cause rapid aggregation depends on barrier heights relative to kB​T, not just on the sign of the force at one separation.
Adsorbed or grafted polymer layers can provide steric stabilization: overlap costs chain entropy and increases the local osmotic pressure. Nonadsorbing smaller particles or polymers can create a depletion force, because bringing large surfaces together increases the volume accessible to the smaller species. Polymer bridging can be attractive when one chain binds more than one particle. A useful comparison experiment varies salt concentration while monitoring scattered light or aggregation, then repeats with a sterically protected suspension. Charge, solvent quality, polymer coverage and particle size determine which interaction dominates; none is a universal constant of all colloids.

For a microscopic swimmer, the Reynolds number Re=ρUL/η is usually small. Taking L=2 μm, U=20 μms−1 and water-like properties gives Re≃4×10−5. The Stokes equations then balance viscous stress and pressure almost instantaneously, so a swimmer cannot coast appreciably when its propulsive stroke stops.
The scallop theorem rules out a reciprocal opening-and-closing stroke in an otherwise unchanged Newtonian environment: reversing the sequence of shapes reverses the motion, and changing the stroke speed does not prevent cancellation. Organisms therefore use nonreciprocal motion. A sperm-like bending wave travels along a flagellum; a bacterium can rotate a helical flagellar bundle. The unequal parallel and perpendicular drag of a slender filament converts those motions into a net thrust. A freely suspended swimmer is force-free and torque-free overall, so body drag balances thrust and the body counter-rotates against the rotating bundle. Tracking a body and its flagellum together can reveal these opposite rotations. The helical microswimmer with a spherical head makes that force and torque balance quantitative.

Passive microrheology uses thermal bead motion as a probe of mechanical compliance. The fluctuation-dissipation theorem relates equilibrium displacement fluctuations to the same linear response measured by a small applied force. The stated thermal stress scale has units of energy per bead volume; dividing the mean-square displacement by a2 provides a dimensionless effective strain. With that prescribed analogy,
J(t)=kB​T/(πa3)⟨Δr2(t)⟩/a2​,⟨Δr2(t)⟩=πakB​T​J(t).​
(10)
The displacement is relative to the starting position, and the variance here is the sum over three spatial coordinates. For a Newtonian fluid, substitution gives ⟨Δr2⟩=kB​Tt/(πηa)=6Dt, exactly the Stokes–Einstein relation D=kB​T/(6πηa).
The continuum interpretation assumes a dilute no-slip spherical probe, thermal equilibrium, linear response and a homogeneous incompressible medium on the bead scale. The stated stress estimate is an effective scale for the analogy; a literal instantaneous stress variance would also require a microscopic or temporal averaging prescription.

Put N=Nx​Ny​Nz​ primitive cells in a periodic box. In each reciprocal-space direction, the allowed Bloch wavevectors are spaced by 2π/Li​, giving Ni​ independent values in the first Brillouin zone. There are consequently N wavevectors per band and, including two electron spin states at each wavevector,
2N states per band in the whole crystal,2 states per atom.​
(24)
The total and per-atom counts are different; a factor N cannot remain in the per-atom count for this monatomic lattice.

The tight-binding approximation starts with localized atomic orbitals. Their overlap allows hopping, broadening each atomic energy into a band. For example, a single orbital on a simple cubic lattice with nearest-neighbor hopping gives E(k)=E0​−2t[cos(kx​a)+cos(ky​a)+cos(kz​a)]. Small overlap means a narrow band; orbital symmetry and hopping signs determine its extrema and dispersion.
The nearly-free electron model starts instead with free-electron parabolas, folded into the Brillouin zone, and treats the periodic crystal potential as a perturbation. Near a Bragg plane, states differing by a reciprocal vector become degenerate and mix, opening a gap approximately 2∣VG​∣. Away from such degeneracies, the energy remains close to the free-electron dispersion. These are complementary limits of the same Bloch-band framework, not different counts of electronic states. Both can give metals or insulators depending on filling and band overlap, but their natural basis functions and useful expansion parameters differ.

Thomas-Fermi screening treats the carrier density locally as that of a uniform electron gas whose chemical potential is shifted by the slowly varying electrostatic potential. Linearizing gives δn=(∂n/∂μ)eφ for electrons. The resulting screened Poisson equation has inverse screening length
ks2​=εe2​∂μ∂n​.

The Hubbard model combines band motion and local repulsion:
H=−t∑⟨ij⟩,σ​(ciσ†​cjσ​+h.c.)+U∑i​ni↑​ni↓​.
(27)
Hopping favors itinerant states, while positive U penalizes simultaneous opposite-spin occupation on one site. The Stoner criterion for instability of the unpolarized metal is Ug↑​(EF​)>1 when g↑​ is the density of states per site, per energy, for one spin. The ordered mean-field state has exchange-split spin bands and a spontaneous magnetization, with the kinetic and interaction costs balancing at equilibrium. This is a model of itinerant ferromagnetism, not a guarantee that every repulsive Hubbard system becomes ferromagnetic. Filling, lattice structure and competing order matter: near half filling on a bipartite lattice, antiferromagnetism and a Mott insulator can be favored instead. The approximation also omits important spin fluctuations and strong-correlation effects.

In a two-dimensional electron system under a perpendicular magnetic field, orbital energies form Landau levels with spacing ℏωc​. Each resolved spin level has eBA/h states in area A. At integer filling, the integer quantum Hall effect has Hall conductance σxy​=νe2/h in magnitude and vanishing longitudinal conductance in the ideal plateau regime; inversion of the conductivity tensor gives Hall resistance h/(νe2).
A Hall-bar experiment cools the sample, passes a small current and records longitudinal and transverse voltages while sweeping the field. The Hall resistance develops plateaus while the longitudinal resistance develops minima. Disorder broadens the Landau levels and localizes many bulk states, allowing the chemical potential to move through a range of carrier density without changing the number of conducting edge channels. Extended states near a Landau-level center allow transitions between plateaus. Spin splitting determines which integer sequence is resolved. The robustness comes from the quantum state and its edge transport, rather than the Hall bar having a specially calibrated geometric width.

For photon absorption or emission, electron energy changes by ±ℏω and crystal wavevector by ±q modulo a reciprocal lattice vector. Optical photon momentum is normally tiny on the Brillouin zone scale, so interband optical transitions are approximately vertical on an energy-wavevector diagram. A direct-gap semiconductor can therefore absorb a photon between valence and conduction states near the same wavevector; an indirect transition usually needs an additional phonon or another source of momentum. Occupation and optical matrix elements impose further restrictions.
Within one ideal parabolic band of a clean metal, such a small photon momentum cannot normally supply the required finite energy difference. Intraband optical absorption then relies on momentum relaxation by impurities, phonons or boundaries, as in the finite-scattering-time Drude response. Interband transitions can still occur. For an acoustic phonon with ωq​≃vs​q, energy and crystal momentum conservation require Ek′​−Ek​=±ℏvs​q and k′−k=±q+G. The sound speed is usually much smaller than the relevant electron velocity in a metal, so scattering can change the electron's direction substantially while changing its energy only slightly. It is approximately elastic for many transport calculations, though its finite energy transfer is essential for cooling.
The middle panel gives an allowed backscattering example with a small energy loss. In a degenerate metal, the Pauli exclusion principle blocks occupied final states, restricting low-temperature scattering to a thermal neighborhood of the Fermi surface. Phonon occupations also shrink on cooling. A normal process has G=0; an Umklapp process transfers reciprocal-lattice momentum. In semiconductors, accessible valleys and their separation add further momentum constraints.

An optical phonon often has approximately constant energy ℏω0​ over the relevant wavevector range. Its absorption or emission therefore moves the electron between energy surfaces separated by this finite amount, with the phonon supplying the associated crystal momentum modulo a reciprocal vector. The last panel shows emission between two points on a parabolic conduction band.
Emission from a band with a lower edge requires at least ℏω0​ of available kinetic energy and an unoccupied final state. For nondegenerate hot carriers this produces a threshold and can lead to rapid energy loss in successive phonon emissions. Absorption is weighted by the Bose-Einstein distribution nB​(ω0​), while emission has the factor nB​+1; absorption is strongly suppressed at kB​T≪ℏω0​. In a metal, Pauli blocking also prevents an electron near the Fermi energy from simply dropping into the occupied Fermi sea.

Let α=ℏ2/(2m∗), so the initial conduction-electron kinetic energy is E1​=αk12​. In the normal momentum-conserving process with the final hole fixed at zero wavevector, the two final conduction electrons obey k1​=k2​+k3​. Their total kinetic energy satisfies
α(k22​+k32​)=2E1​​+2α​∣k2​−k3​∣2≥2E1​​.
(28)
Energy conservation for impact ionization is E1​=Eg​+α(k22​+k32​). Consequently E1​≥Eg​+E1​/2, giving
E1,min​=2Eg​.​
(29)
The bound is attained when k2​=k3​=k1​/2; opposite spins permit the two electrons to share that wavevector. Fixing the hole momentum is essential: a mobile final hole gives a different threshold. This is the threshold for impact ionization with a hole at zero momentum.
The two hot electrons subsequently redistribute energy by electron-electron collisions and transfer it to the lattice by electron-phonon scattering. Optical-phonon emission can provide large discrete losses while the electrons are hot; acoustic phonons dominate later small energy exchanges. Electron-electron collisions alone conserve the electron system's total energy and do not cool it into the lattice. The electron-hole pair eventually recombines radiatively or through nonradiative channels, such as defect-assisted recombination or Auger recombination. Recombination is distinct from the preceding intraband cooling.

A Sommerfeld expansion gives electronic energy above its zero-temperature value proportional to g(EF​)(kB​T)2, hence Ce​=γT.
