Why degenerate perturbation theory is needed. If several unperturbed states have equal or nearly equal energy, a perturbation can mix them at leading order. In that subspace, diagonalize the perturbation matrix rather than using nondegenerate formulas with small energy denominators. The resulting eigenvectors identify the combinations selected by the coupling, and the eigenvalues give the leading splitting. Other remote states can then be included perturbatively.
For the linear combination of atomic orbitals description of molecular hydrogen, equivalent atomic 1s states on the two nuclei supply the nearly degenerate starting functions. In an orthonormal two-state model, equal diagonal energy Ea​ and real hopping t give Ea​±t and symmetric/antisymmetric combinations. For actual overlapping atomic functions with overlap S and Hamiltonian coupling h, one instead solves a generalized eigenvalue problem:
Eb​=1+SEa​+h​,Eab​=1−SEa​−h​,ψb,ab​=2(1±S)​ϕA​±ϕB​​.
(27)
The bonding state has enhanced density between the nuclei and the antibonding state a separating node. Two opposite-spin electrons occupying the bonding molecular orbital describe the simplest closed-shell hydrogen-molecule bond. Electron-electron interaction and nuclear repulsion are needed to calculate a realistic total binding energy; the two-state splitting alone is not the full molecular energy.
In the nearly-free electron model, the analogous degenerate states are plane waves ∣k⟩ and ∣k−G⟩ at a Bragg scattering. A Fourier coefficient UG​ of the periodic potential couples them. At exact degeneracy EB​, diagonalizing their two-by-two matrix gives
E±​=EB​+U0​±∣UG​∣,Egap​=2∣UG​∣.​
(28)
The associated standing waves place electronic density differently relative to the ionic potential. These are the same gap-opening combinations illustrated in Q1(b). The labels of the two starting basis states survive as mixed orbital or plane-wave character, rather than independent crossing eigenstates.
Origin of broad and narrow bands. Spatially extended s/p-derived orbitals have substantial overlap and therefore large hopping amplitudes and broad bands. More localized d orbitals overlap less strongly and produce narrower bands. A multiorbital tight-binding calculation assigns the on-site orbital energies and hopping matrices, forms Bloch sums, and diagonalizes the resulting matrix at each wavevector. Different hopping ranges and orbital symmetry determine the bandwidths and allowed interband coupling. The two-band description retains one broad combination and one nearly flat d-derived combination; a real transition metal contains several d orbitals and more complicated dispersions.
Hybridized eigenvalues and eigenstates. Let ∣ψk​⟩=a∣ϕk​⟩+b∣χk​⟩, in an orthonormal basis. Hermitian operator fixes the reverse coupling to be V∗. Projection onto the two components gives
(E1​(k)V∗​VEd​​)(ab​)=E(ab​),(E1​−E)(Ed​−E)−∣V∣2=0.
(29)
Solving this quadratic yields the band hybridization dispersion
E±​(k)=2E1​(k)+Ed​​±[2E1​(k)−Ed​​]2+∣V∣2​.​
(30)
For nonzero V, one eigenvector representation is (a,b)∝(V,E−E1​), followed by normalization. Thus the wavefunctions exchange broad-band and d-band character through the avoided crossing; the levels are equal mixtures at the uncoupled crossing.
At V=0, the parabola crosses the horizontal level at k=±k0​, with k0​=2mEd​​/ℏ. For small nonzero ∣V∣, the curves avoid crossing with minimum direct separation 2∣V∣. The lower branch follows the parabola below the crossing and flattens toward Ed​ above it; the upper does the opposite. The lower branch satisfies E−​<Ed​ everywhere and tends to Ed​ from below as k→∞, while the upper branch always lies above Ed​. This is the perfectly flat-band continuum approximation, with zone-boundary intersections excluded as requested.
Fixed particle number and the larger Fermi sphere. Denote the uncoupled broad-band Fermi radius by k0​ to distinguish it from the new one K. With two spin states, the broad-band number density at V=0 is k03​/(3π2). The stipulated equal population in the flat sector makes the total density twice that value. At zero temperature and any nonzero hybridization, all occupied states fit into the monotonic lower branch below Ed​, while the upper branch is empty. Therefore
3π2K3​=23π2k03​​,K=21/3k0​.​
(31)
This is the stated factor increase. It uses fixed total particle number, equal spin counting, an exactly flat unhybridized band and enough available states below any Brillouin-zone restriction. In generic pairs of dispersive bands both hybridized pockets can remain occupied; then Fermi-volume conservation in two hybridized electron bands fixes a sum of volumes rather than this one radius. The exactly flat band is degenerate at V=0, so its initial occupation must be specified, as it is here.

vF(1)​vF​​=k0​K​Δ(K)2∣V∣2​+O(Ed4​∣V∣4​),

so the flat-band hybridization Fermi-velocity suppression is
vF(1)​vF​​≃(22/3−1)221/3​Ed2​∣V∣2​,A≃3.65.​

The reference velocity is that of the original broad-band Fermi sphere, not the broad-band slope at the larger K; omitting the radius change loses the factor 21/3.
The low-temperature qualification becomes more stringent as V→0: the new chemical potential approaches Ed​ with Ed​−μ≃∣V∣2/Δ(K), and the relevant thermal energy must be small compared with this hybridization scale. At fixed nonzero temperature the singular flat-band redistribution is not automatically resolved for arbitrarily small coupling. A small but finite narrow-band dispersion can also change the occupation topology.
Density of states and magnetism. For an isotropic three-dimensional branch with spin degeneracy two,
Vg(EF​)​=π2∣dE−​/dk∣K​K2​=π2ℏvF​K2​.
(35)
A small Fermi velocity therefore produces a large density of states. The localized, partially filled d-derived bands in iron, cobalt and nickel place many states close to the Fermi level; broad s/p-derived conduction bands in copper, silver and gold coexist with filled d bands lying below it. This explains the substantially larger Fermi-level density of states of the former group within the band picture. An exact factor of ten requires the actual band parameters and is not fixed by the two-band toy model.
A high density of states lowers the kinetic-energy cost of spin polarization. A repulsive exchange interaction can then satisfy the Stoner criterion and stabilize ferromagnetism. With a single-spin density of states gs​ and an on-site interaction normalization, the criterion is Ugs​(EF​)>1; with spin-summed g=2gs​, it is Ug(EF​)>2. The large d-state contribution favors this instability in Fe, Co and Ni. The noble metals do not satisfy the corresponding instability condition in the same ordinary state; this does not imply that they have no weak magnetic response whatsoever.
