For state counting in a Bloch band, take a primitive cubic lattice of spacing a and impose periodic boundary conditions on lengths Li​=Ni​a. The allowed wavevectors are ki​=2πli​/Li​. The first Brillouin zone has side 2π/a, so it contains Ni​ distinct values in each direction and hence Nx​Ny​Nz​=N wavevectors. Each orbital-derived energy band has one state per wavevector per spin orientation. Therefore
states per band in the whole crystal=2N,states per atom per band=2.​
(1)
The phrase “per atom” attached to 2N in the PDF is a counting error: 2N is the total, not the per-atom count.
By the Pauli exclusion principle, a monovalent crystal supplies N valence electrons and half-fills a spin-degenerate band; its Fermi level lies within allowed states, giving a metal in the independent-electron picture. Two electrons per atom can fill one band. If a band gap separates it from all empty bands, the crystal is a band insulator. An even valence count only permits insulation: overlapping bands can still produce a metal. Conversely, electron interactions can produce a Mott insulator, and symmetry breaking can permit insulation outside this simple spin-degenerate electronic band structure criterion. The relevant count in a crystal with a basis is per primitive unit cell.

In a simple metal, conduction electrons can move collectively in response to an incident electromagnetic wave. The Drude model gives, with time dependence e−iωt,
ϵr​(ω)=1−ω(ω+iγ)ωp2​​,ωp2​=ϵ0​m∗ne2​.
(2)
In the low-loss range γ≪ω<ωp​, the relative permittivity is approximately 1−ωp2​/ω2<0. The wavevector inside is predominantly imaginary, so the field is evanescent and penetrates only a short distance. Surface currents reradiate the field; little energy enters a good conductor. The normal-incidence Fresnel equations give
R=​ϵr​​+1ϵr​​−1​​2≃1(ω<ωp​, γ small).​
(3)
Many ordinary metals have their plasma frequency in the ultraviolet, placing visible light below this reflection edge. For ω>ωp​ the free-carrier contribution permits propagation; at ω≫ωp​, ϵr​→1 and R∼ωp4​/(16ω4)→0. The electrons' inertial response becomes too small to screen the rapidly oscillating field.
Ultraviolet transparency is a simple-metal model prediction, not a universal property of real metals. Electronic transitions between bands and bound-electron absorption can remain strong, shift the plasma edge and prevent transparency; transmission also depends on thickness and attenuation. The robust explanation is the change from a reflective negative-permittivity range to a possible propagating range above the plasma edge.

Let adjacent equal masses m be separated by a, with alternating strong and weak spring constants K and κ, K≫κ>0. The pattern repeats after 2a, even though the masses are identical. Thus the first Brillouin zone is
−2aπ​≤q≤2aπ​.​
(4)
Choose the strong bond inside each two-atom primitive unit cell. With displacement amplitudes (u,v)ei(2naq−ωt), the normal mode equations are
mω2(uv​)=(K+κ−K−κe2iqa​−K−κe−2iqaK+κ​)(uv​).
(5)
The alternating-spring chain dispersion relation is consequently
ω±2​(q)=mK+κ±(K−κ)2+4Kκcos2(qa)​​.​
(6)
The lower acoustic phonon branch has in-phase motion within the strongly bound pair and ω−​(0)=0. Its small-q slope is the speed of sound,
cs​=am(K+κ)2Kκ​​≃am2κ​​.
(7)
The upper optical phonon branch has opposite motion within each pair, with ω+​(0)=2(K+κ)/m​. Here “optical” names the gapped branch; equal neutral masses need not couple to light through an electric dipole.
In the strong-bond limit of the alternating-spring chain, the pairs act almost as rigid dimers in the low-frequency branch, while their internal stretch gives a high, nearly flat branch:
ω−2​≃m2κ​sin2(qa),ω+2​≃m2K​+m2κ​cos2(qa).
(8)
At the zone edge the two frequencies are 2κ/m​ and 2K/m​, separated by a large forbidden frequency interval. Neighbouring cells are out of phase there. Both branches have horizontal tangents at the edge.

For a Hamiltonian with a lattice-periodic potential V(r+R)=V(r), its simultaneous energy eigenstates and lattice-translation eigenstates can be chosen in the Bloch wave form
ψnk​(r)=eik⋅runk​(r),unk​(r+R)=unk​(r).​
(9)

Use one orthonormalized localized atomic orbital ∣j⟩ at each position ja. In the nearest-neighbour tight-binding model, translation invariance gives the on-site energy and real hopping integral
E0​=⟨j∣H∣j⟩,t=⟨j∣H∣j+1⟩=⟨j+1∣H∣j⟩.
(10)
The Bloch state is
∣k⟩=N​1​∑j​eikja∣j⟩.
(11)
Since H∣j⟩=E0​∣j⟩+t∣j−1⟩+t∣j+1⟩, shifting the two summation indices gives
H∣k⟩=[E0​+teika+te−ika]∣k⟩,E(k)=E0​+2tcos(ka).​
(12)
The hopping integral is an energy, and its sign sets whether k=0 is a minimum or maximum. E0​ is the diagonal matrix element in the crystal Hamiltonian, which can differ from an isolated-atom energy.
The orthogonal tight-binding approximation matters. If raw neighbouring orbitals retain a nonzero orbital overlap s in their normalization, projection instead gives a generalized eigenvalue problem and the nonorthogonal nearest-neighbour tight-binding band E(k)=[E0​+2hcos(ka)]/[1+2scos(ka)]. The stated cosine band follows after orthonormalization and nearest-neighbour truncation, or after neglecting that overlap metric while retaining the hopping it motivates.

A site in a primitive cubic lattice has six nearest neighbours, at ±ax^, ±ay^​ and ±az^. Repeating the Bloch state calculation with equal hopping integrals t along all six bonds gives
E(k)=E0​+2t[cos(kx​a)+cos(ky​a)+cos(kz​a)].​
(13)
The cosine sum ranges from −3 to 3 within the first Brillouin zone. Therefore
Emin​=E0​−6∣t∣,Emax​=E0​+6∣t∣,W=Emax​−Emin​=12∣t∣.​
(14)
For t<0 the minimum is at the zone centre; for t>0 the maximum is there.

For a degenerate Fermi gas, the Pauli exclusion principle restricts thermally changed occupations to an energy shell of width kB​T around the Fermi energy. The number of active electrons is of order g(EF​)kB​T∼NkB​T/EF​; each carries an excitation energy of order kB​T. Thus the thermal energy grows as N(kB​T)2/EF​ and the electronic heat capacity is proportional to T.
More precisely, the fixed-particle-number Sommerfeld expansion gives
U(T)−U(0)=6π2​g(EF​)(kB​T)2+O(T4),Ce​=3π2​g(EF​)kB2​T+O(T3).
(21)
Here g is the total spin-summed density of states for the sample. For a three-dimensional parabolic band, g(EF​)=3N/(2EF​), yielding
Ce​=AEF​NkB2​T​,A=2π2​,kB​T≪EF​.​
(22)
The linear law concerns the electronic contribution; the low-temperature lattice heat capacity usually adds a T3 term.

Let n=N/V and ce​=Ce​/V be the carrier density and electronic heat capacity per unit volume. In isotropic kinetic theory, the angular average ⟨vx2​⟩=vF2​/3 gives the electronic thermal conductivity
Ke​=31​ce​vF​ℓQ​=31​ce​vF2​τQ​,
(23)
where ℓQ​=vF​τQ​ is the mean free path associated with the heat-current relaxation time. With vF2​=2EF​/m∗,
Ke​=32A​m∗nkB2​TτQ​​.
(24)
The Drude model gives the electrical conductivity σ=ne2τσ​/m∗, where τσ​ is the momentum relaxation time. Therefore
σTKe​​=32A​(ekB​​)2τσ​τQ​​.
(25)
When the same approximately elastic collisions relax heat and charge currents, τQ​=τσ​. Using A=π2/2 gives the Wiedemann-Franz law
Ke​=L0​σT,L0​=3π2​(ekB​​)2≃2.44×10−8WΩK−2.​
(26)
L0​ is the Lorenz number. Degenerate electrons move at the Fermi velocity, not the classical thermal velocity; using classical equipartition would give the wrong coefficient.

The Wiedemann-Franz law applies to a normal degenerate metal when the same itinerant electrons carry heat and charge, scattering is approximately elastic over the thermal shell, and the transport probability varies slowly across the energy interval kB​T around the Fermi level. The comparison uses the electronic thermal conductivity Ke​, with any lattice contribution removed.
It works particularly well as T→0 when elastic impurity or defect scattering dominates. It also often holds approximately for ΘD​≪T≪TF​, where thermally populated phonons act as a nearly elastic classical scattering field. Room temperature need not lie above the Debye temperature for every metal.
At intermediate temperatures, inelastic small-angle electron-phonon scattering can relax heat much more efficiently than charge. Then τQ​=τσ​ and Ke​/(σT) can be smaller than L0​. Strong energy dependence of scattering, additional neutral heat carriers, or a transition out of the normal metallic state also invalidates the simple derivation. An observed deviation alone does not identify which assumption failed.

For one conduction electron per atom, let na​ be the atom density. State counting in a Bloch band gives the Fermi wavevector kF​=(3π2na​)1/3. The Debye model preserves the three vibrational modes per atom by a spherical Debye wavevector cutoff qD​=(6π2na​)1/3, so
qD​=21/3kF​≃1.26kF​.​
(27)
Both wavevectors are of order the inverse atomic spacing. The numerical factor uses the spherical Debye approximation; a real zone boundary and the largest-frequency mode depend on direction and branch. Because qD​<2kF​, short-wavelength acoustic phonons can scatter Fermi surface electrons through substantial angles. For nearly elastic scattering within a spherical Fermi surface,
q=2kF​sin(θ/2),1−cosθ=2kF2​q2​.
(28)
Near room temperature, when a substantial fraction of the phonon spectrum is occupied, electron-phonon scattering is often the main source of electrical resistance. For T≫ΘD​, the phonon population and scattering rate are approximately proportional to T, giving ρ∝T, σ∝T−1 and roughly constant Ke​ when the Wiedemann-Franz law holds.
For small-angle electron-phonon transport at low temperature, thermally active acoustic modes instead have
qT​∼ℏcs​kB​T​=qD​ΘD​T​≪kF​.
(29)
They predominantly scatter forward. The factor 1−cosθ makes such collisions inefficient at relaxing an electrical current, while a phonon energy of order kB​T can still substantially change an electron's thermal excitation. Heat-current relaxation therefore need not share the charge-current relaxation time. In the clean three-dimensional acoustic-phonon model this gives τQ−1​∝T3 but τσ−1​∝T5, so the phonon-limited electrical resistivity scales as T5 and Ke​∝T−2. These laws describe the regime before residual elastic scattering takes over; at sufficiently low temperature impurities give nearly constant residual resistivity and Ke​∝T, restoring the usual Lorenz number.[ref]
Phonons also transport heat themselves, with Kph​≃cph​cs​ℓph​/3. At low temperature their population decreases and their phonon mean free path can become boundary- or defect-limited. At higher temperature Umklapp scattering can relax the phonon heat current. Such lattice conduction is an additional contribution, not the quantity constrained by the electronic Wiedemann-Franz law.

b OurBigBook.com
At equilibrium, majority electrons undergo diffusion from n to p, while majority electron holes diffuse from p to n. Their departure exposes positive donors on the n side and negative acceptors on the p side. The resulting electric field points from n to p and opposes further diffusion. Equilibrium is reached when the chemical potential is constant across the junction. The built-in potential is this electrostatic adjustment, not a discontinuity of the material's band gap.
Since electronic band energies shift by −eV(x), both the conduction band and valence band undergo band bending down from p to n by eVb​ at zero applied bias. Their separation remains the same gap. In the nondegenerate thermal equilibrium limit the built-in voltage can also be expressed using neutral-edge densities as eVb​=kB​Tlog(nn​/np​)=kB​Tlog(nn​pp​/ni2​).
Take the n-contact potential and its electrochemical potential as reference. Applying Vext​ to the p side changes the total depletion-layer drop to
U=Vb​−Vext​,Ec,p​−Ec,n​=Ev,p​−Ev,n​=eU.​
(32)
Thus Vext​<0 is reverse bias: it increases the barrier and widens the depletion region. The bands have horizontal tangents at the neutral edges in this approximation and a smooth cubic bend within the linearly graded layer.
A biased junction is not in global thermal equilibrium. Its contact electron electrochemical potentials obey
μp​−μn​=−eVext​.​
(33)
Under reverse bias the p-contact level is higher by e∣Vext​∣. A single horizontal equilibrium chemical potential cannot describe this state; electron and hole quasi-Fermi levels describe the nonequilibrium populations. The sketch marks the contact levels. Detailed spatial quasi-Fermi profiles require transport and recombination information beyond the electrostatic doping profile.

At equilibrium the diffusion currents and drift currents cancel separately for each carrier species. Electron diffusion is from n to p, while the electric field drives electrons from p to n; hole diffusion is from p to n, while hole drift is from n to p. The net current is zero, although these opposing microscopic fluxes are present. In conventional-current signs, both majority diffusion contributions point p to n and the balancing drift contributions point n to p.
Forward bias, Vext​>0, reduces the barrier and enhances majority-carrier diffusion and minority carrier injection. The ideal low-injection Shockley diode equation gives
J=Js​[eeVext​/(kB​T)−1],​
(34)
where positive current is p to n. For reverse bias, majority diffusion is suppressed and the field sweeps thermally supplied minority carriers across the junction, giving approximately J=−Js​. This saturation is limited by carrier supply rather than a current proportional to the field. Depletion-layer generation, recombination and eventual diode reverse breakdown modify the ideal result.
