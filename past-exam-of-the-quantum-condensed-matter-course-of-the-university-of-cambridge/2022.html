The drift velocity is the small ensemble-average velocity acquired by charge carriers in an applied electric field. It is distinct from their much larger random thermal or Fermi velocity. For conduction electrons of charge −e, the isotropic Drude model gives
vd​=−m∗eτ​E=−μe​E,​
(1)
where τ is the momentum relaxation time.
The positive electron mobility μe​=∣vd​∣/∣E∣ measures the ease of this drift in linear response. In the same approximation,
μe​=m∗eτ​,σ=neμe​.​
(2)
Its units are m2V−1s−1; in an anisotropic crystal the mobility is a tensor.
The band effective mass describes the acceleration implied by the curvature of the dispersion relation. The group velocity is v=ℏ−1∇k​E, while ℏk˙=−eE. Thus
(m∗−1)ij​=ℏ21​∂ki​∂kj​∂2E​.​
(3)
For an isotropic parabolic minimum this reduces to E=Ec​+ℏ2k2/(2m∗) and m∗v˙=−eE. It incorporates the periodic crystal potential and need not equal the bare electron mass. Negative electronic curvature near a band maximum is conveniently described by positive-mass electron holes.

Work at zero temperature and fixed total carrier density. Write n↑​=n/2+δ and n↓​=n/2−δ. Let gs​(EF​) be the single-spin density of states per unit volume in the unpolarised state. Since dEs​/dns​=EFs​ and dEFs​/dns​=1/gs​, the linear kinetic-energy shifts cancel and the quadratic cost is
ΔEkin​=gs​(EF​)δ2​+O(δ4).
(4)
The repulsive interaction instead decreases under spin polarization:
Eint​=U(4n2​−δ2),ΔE=[gs​(EF​)1​−U]δ2+O(δ4).
(5)
Hence the Stoner criterion for an opposite-spin contact repulsion is
Uc​=gs​(EF​)1​=gtot​(EF​)2​,U>Uc​ destabilises the unpolarised state.​
(6)
The two forms differ only in whether the stated density of states counts one spin or both. For the three-dimensional free electron model, gtot​(EF​)=3n/(2EF​), so
Uc​=3n4EF​​.​
(7)
Equivalently, for fractional polarisation ζ=(n↑​−n↓​)/n, the kinetic-energy density is 103​nEF​[(1+ζ)5/3+(1−ζ)5/3]; its quadratic coefficient is nEF​/3, while the interaction contributes −Un2/4. With number densities, U has units of energy times volume and the displayed interaction is an energy density; multiplying by the sample volume does not change the threshold.

An angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy intensity map locates occupied electronic band structure through its bright dispersing ridges. For a nearly isotropic graphene cone,
E±​(q)=ED​±ℏvF​∣q∣,vF​=ℏ1​​dkdE​​.​
(8)
Fit straight lines to the reasonably linear portions of the ridges, avoiding the broad central crossing, and extrapolate them to locate the Dirac point energy ED​. The PDF places the crossing near ky​=0 and plotted energy roughly 1.4–1.5eV. On a lower branch, a representative change from approximately (∣ky​∣,E)=(0.10A˚−1,0.9eV) towards (0,1.5eV) gives a slope near 6eVA˚. Allowing for the ridge width gives about 5–7eVA˚, hence
vF​≃(0.8–1.1)×106ms−1,approximately 106ms−1.​
(9)
Here 1eVA˚/ℏ=1.52×105ms−1.
To determine the carrier Fermi energy, identify the Fermi level from a calibrated photoemission cutoff, then measure its separation from the Dirac point:
EFcone​=∣EF,plot​−ED​∣=ℏvF​kF​,pF​=ℏkF​.​
(10)
The separation of the two Fermi crossings gives 2kF​ for a cut through the cone centre. This determines the doping energy relative to the Dirac point; the absolute plotted energy origin is not itself a Fermi energy.
The reproduced panel has no marked Fermi level or stated energy-zero calibration. Its upper ridges end near 1.9eV, but that alone does not prove that the endpoint is a Fermi cutoff. If it is the intended cutoff, the estimate is EFcone​≃0.4–0.5eV and kF​∼0.06–0.08A˚−1. If instead the plotted numbers are binding energies measured from an unstated Fermi zero, the inferred separation would be roughly 1.4–1.5eV. The supplied figure securely supports the slope estimate, but it does not distinguish these energy-reference conventions; an unconditional numerical Fermi energy requires the missing calibration.

Take q=−e as the signed electron charge. The Lorentz oscillator model is a driven damped bound charge:
mu¨+mγu˙+mω02​u=qE(t).​
(11)
The first term is inertia, the second is a phenomenological frictional loss of coherent motion to other degrees of freedom, and the third is the linear restoring force from binding to the atom. The driving term is the electric force. The natural angular frequency ω0​ represents the electronic transition; this classical oscillator describes its near-resonant linear response. The friction convention is mγu˙, so no factor two is hidden in γ.

Set D=(ω02​−ω2)2+γ2ω2. The real part and imaginary part of the complex permittivity are
ϵr′​=1+Dωp2​(ω02​−ω2)​,ϵr′′​=Dωp2​γω​.​
(15)

For a nonmagnetic medium write its complex refractive index as N=nr​+iκ, with N2=ϵr​ and optical extinction coefficient κ. In a transparent region with κ≪nr​ and ϵr′​>0,
nr​≃ϵr′​​,κ≃2ϵr′​​ϵr′′​​,αabs​=c2ωκ​.​
(16)
The real part controls phase velocity and optical dispersion; the loss part controls wave absorption and the cycle-averaged dissipated power density ωϵ0​ϵr′′​∣E0​∣2/2. Weak damping gives a narrow line but does not make wave absorption weak at its centre. The transparent-medium approximation applies away from the line.

For independent electronic transitions, add the electric dipole moment responses:
ϵr​(ω)=1+j∑​ωj2​−ω2−iγj​ωSj​​,Sj​>0.​
(17)
The positive constants Sj​ include electron density and transition atomic oscillator strength. Each term adds an absorption peak to ϵr′′​ and a dispersive positive-to-negative contribution to ϵr′​−1, as in the middle row of the figure.
Between well-separated absorption lines, where the medium is transparent, the refractive index generally increases with frequency: dnr​/dω>0, or dnr​/dλ<0. This is normal dispersion. Near a resonance the real response turns down with increasing frequency, producing an interval of anomalous dispersion, dnr​/dω<0, accompanied by appreciable absorption. The absorptive and dispersive parts are linked by the Kramers-Kronig relations; the steep variation is not independent of the loss spectrum.

An unbound conduction electron has no local restoring force, so set ω0​=0 while retaining inertia and momentum relaxation time τ=1/γ. This is the Drude model:
ϵr​(ω)=1−ω(ω+iγ)ωp2​​.​
(18)
Separating the response gives
ϵr′​=1−ω2+γ2ωp2​​,ϵr′′​=ω(ω2+γ2)ωp2​γ​.​
(19)
For a good metal, γ≪ωp​. The real part is strongly negative at low frequency, crosses zero at ωp2​−γ2​≃ωp​, and approaches one from below. The imaginary part diverges as ωp2​/(γω) at low frequency, reflecting the finite dc electrical conductivity σ0​=ϵ0​ωp2​/γ, then decays as ω−3. The low-frequency imaginary response describes conduction loss, not a finite-frequency bound-electron absorption line. Core-electron and interband responses can add a background in a real metal.

At normal incidence from vacuum, the Fresnel equations give the reflectance
R(ω)=​N(ω)+1N(ω)−1​​2,N2=ϵr​.​
(20)
A good metal has R close to one below its plasma frequency. At very low frequency the electromagnetic field penetrates only a skin depth; the local Drude limit gives 1−R≃8ϵ0​ω/σ0​​. In the collisionless negative-permittivity range the transmitted field is evanescent and reflection remains strong. Near the plasma edge the reflectance drops sharply; above it ϵr′​>0 and propagating waves become possible. In the ideal free-electron high-frequency limit,
R∼16ω4ωp4​​⟶0.​
(21)
Finite damping rounds the plasma edge, and interband absorption changes the detailed response of actual metals.

Choose one atomic orbital ϕA​ and ϕB​ on the two atoms and approximate a molecular orbital by the linear combination of atomic orbitals
ψ=cA​ϕA​+cB​ϕB​.
(22)
Projecting the Schrödinger equation onto the two orbitals gives a generalized eigenvalue problem Hc=ESc, where Hij​=⟨ϕi​∣H^∣ϕj​⟩ and Sij​=⟨ϕi​∣ϕj​⟩. In an orthonormal basis the matrix is
H=(ϵA​t∗​tϵB​​),E±​=2ϵA​+ϵB​​±4(ϵA​−ϵB​)2​+∣t∣2​.
(23)
The coupling mixes the atomic states and produces a lower bonding molecular orbital and upper antibonding molecular orbital. For equivalent real orbitals with overlap s the normalized combinations are (ϕA​±ϕB​)/2(1±s)​; the precise energy ordering follows from the Hamiltonian matrix elements. This restricted orbital basis is an approximation to the molecule's wavefunction, rather than a claim that exact isolated-atom orbitals remain unchanged in the molecule.

At nonzero band hybridization, the crossing becomes an avoided crossing. The separation at kc​ is 2α. If α≪Efl​, appreciable mixing occurs only where ∣Ak2−Efl​∣≲α; away from that region each branch retains nearly its original orbital character. For α∼Efl​, the avoided crossing is broad and both branches are substantially reshaped, as shown in the figure.
Use E−​ for the lower branch and E+​ for the upper one. For α>0,
E−​(k)<Efl​<E+​(k),mink​E+​(k)=E+​(0)=2Efl​+Efl2​+4α2​​.
(25)
The lower branch increases towards Efl​ from below. Thus an indirect gap exists in the exactly flat-band model. In the unbounded-parabola limit its lower bound is
Δind​=2Efl2​+4α2​−Efl​​≃Efl​α2​(α≪Efl​).​
(26)
A finite Brillouin zone gives a finite maximum of E−​ below Efl​ and hence a slightly larger indirect gap. This hybridization gap differs from the local direct gap 2α.
The material can therefore be a band insulator when the lower band is full and the upper band empty. With two spin-degenerate orbitals per unit cell, the required filling is
2 electrons per unit cell, i.e. half of the combined four-state capacity.​
(27)
With spin degeneracy removed, replace two by one for each independent spin sector. Other fillings are metallic in this noninteracting model.

Let the common uncoupled Fermi wavevector be k0​. Equal carrier densities imply equal Fermi surface volumes, and hence equal radii for the spherical spin-degenerate electron pockets. The conserved total density is
ntot​=3π22k03​​.
(32)
If hybridisation leaves only one occupied electron-like band, state counting gives
kF​=21/3k0​,pF​=21/3p0​,p0​ΔpF​​=21/3−1≃0.260.​
(33)
This is the familiar single-large-Fermi-surface answer. It requires the other hybridised band to be empty.
For finite heavy-band mass, that conclusion does not follow from an arbitrary nonzero coupling. At sufficiently small α, the upper band still has an occupied pocket, so there are two Fermi momenta, not one. The exact fixed-density statement is Fermi-volume conservation in two hybridized electron bands:
kF,−3​+kF,+3​=2k03​.​
(34)
For an explicit weak-coupling check, write Ep​=Ak2, Eh​=C+Bk2, with 0<B≪A, and enforce the uncoupled equality at k0​: C=(A−B)k02​, μ0​=Ak02​. Put μ=μ0​+δμ and y=kF2​−k02​. The Fermi-surface equation is
(δμ−Ay)(δμ−By)−α2=0.
(35)
At fixed density δμ=O(α2), giving
kF,∓​=k0​±2k0​AB​α​+O(α2).​
(36)
For arbitrary coupling, put q±​=kF,∓2​. The roots of the same characteristic equation, before discarding unoccupied pockets, are
q±​=2AB(A+B)μ−AC±[(A−B)μ−AC]2+4ABα2​​.
(37)
Choose the chemical potential so that [max(q+​,0)]3/2+[max(q−​,0)]3/2=2k03​. This specifies the Fermi momentum of each pocket for any supplied curvatures and coupling.
The transition to one occupied pocket occurs at μ=E+​(0). Set q∗​=22/3k02​; at the transition the two squared radii are q∗​ and zero. Their sum and product give
μc​=A+BA(C+Bq∗​)​,αc2​=μc​(μc​−C)=(A+B)2AB(C+Bq∗​)(Aq∗​−C)​.​
(38)
For 0<α<αc​ there are two pockets; for α≥αc​ the only nonzero radius is 21/3k0​. The outer radius grows continuously and the inner one shrinks. The nearly flat band limit can make depopulation occur at a small coupling, but the mass ratio or a single-surface assumption is needed to turn the question into a unique finite-coupling numerical change.

Ordinary doped semiconductor devices need a regime where their donor impurities or acceptor impurities supply mobile charge carriers while intrinsic thermal excitation remains sufficiently small.
At low temperature, donor impurities or acceptor impurities may no longer be ionized. Dopant freeze-out reduces the carrier density, raises electrical resistance and changes the intended carrier injection and p-n junction response. At high temperature the intrinsic carrier concentration grows as
ni​∝T3/2e−Eg​/(2kB​T)​
(45)
when band effective masses and band gap are treated as constant. Once it becomes comparable with the net doping density, it overwhelms the intended electron-hole imbalance; reverse leakage and dissipation increase and useful p/n control is lost.
The usual operating window lies between freeze-out and intrinsic conduction, with mobility and thermal management placing further practical limits. This is a statement about conventional doped-device operation, not a fundamental prohibition on engineered cryogenic or wide-bandgap devices.

An ideal diffusion-dominated p-n junction obeys the Shockley diode equation
I(V,T)=Is​(T)[eeV/(ηkB​T)−1],​
(46)
with diode ideality factor η=1 in the simplest model. Forward bias gives exponential current growth; reverse bias gives a nearly constant −Is​ until diode reverse breakdown, outside this ideal expression.
For a long abrupt p-n junction in low carrier injection, the diode saturation current is
Is​=eAni2​(Lp​ND​Dp​​+Ln​NA​Dn​​)=C(T)ni2​.​
(47)
Here A is area, ND​,NA​ are ionized dopant densities, and D,L are minority-carrier diffusion coefficients and diffusion lengths in the appropriate regions. At each temperature, extract Is​ either from the reverse-saturation plateau below breakdown or from a forward exponential fit using its measured diode ideality factor.
Extracting a semiconductor band gap from diode saturation current requires correcting for the transport prefactor:
logC(T)T3Is​(T)​=constant−kB​TEg​​.​
(48)
A plot against 1/T has slope −Eg​/kB​ when the gap and effective masses are nearly temperature-independent. Alternatively divide the measured Is​ by a known C(T) to recover the full ni2​(T). The prefactor cannot generally be assumed constant over an arbitrarily broad range. Junction-generation or recombination-dominated current can scale as ni​ instead, so the diffusion regime should be identified from the current-voltage behaviour.

A current-carrying sample in a transverse magnetic field develops a transverse electric field because the Lorentz force deflects its charge carriers. In steady state this Hall voltage balances the transverse deflection. With Hall coefficient RH​=Ey​/(jx​Bz​), one isotropic electron band gives RH​=−1/(ne) and one hole band gives RH​=+1/(pe).
When electrons and electron holes both conduct, the weak-field two-carrier result is
RH​=e(pμh​+nμe​)2pμh2​−nμe2​​,σ=e(nμe​+pμh​).​
(49)
The two-carrier Hall coefficient weights each species by mobility squared. Its sign changes when
pμh2​=nμe2​.​
(50)
Temperature changes dopant ionization, intrinsic electron-hole generation, and the two charge carrier mobilities, so it can move the system across this condition. For example, a p-type semiconductor can be hole-dominated in its extrinsic regime but become negative in its intrinsic regime if n≃p and μe​>μh​. A sign change is possible, not mandatory: in many n-type semiconductors electrons dominate at both temperatures. The density majority alone does not determine the Hall sign in a two-carrier material.
