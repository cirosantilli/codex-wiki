Creating a liquid surface changes the molecular environment and costs interfacial free energy. In the dispersion-only cleavage model, the attractive Van der Waals force per area has magnitude A/(6πh3). Separating two contacting halves from a molecular cutoff h0​ to infinity takes work per area
AW​=∫h0​∞​6πh3AH​​dh=12πh02​AH​​.
(12)
Here AH​ denotes the Hamaker constant, to distinguish it from an area. Cleavage creates two free surfaces, giving the Hamaker estimate of liquid surface tension
γ≃24πh02​AH​​.​
(13)
The continuum attraction diverges at zero separation and neglects molecular impenetrability and short-range repulsion. The cutoff is therefore an effective contact separation of molecular size, commonly of order 0.1–0.3nm, not a limit that can be taken to zero.
An experimental limitation is the strong temperature dependence of surface tension. For water in equilibrium with its vapour, the recommended experimental values decrease from 71.98mNm−1 at 25∘C to 58.92mNm−1 at 100∘C, and the fitted tension vanishes at the critical point. A fixed pairwise cleavage energy misses interfacial entropy, changing density and many-body correlations.[ref] The dispersion-only model is unsuitable when cohesion is dominated by hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions or metallic bonding; liquid metals are a clear example of the latter failure.
