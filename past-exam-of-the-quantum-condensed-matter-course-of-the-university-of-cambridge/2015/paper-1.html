In the isotropic parabolic-band approximation implied by the single supplied curvature, the hole mass from a valence-band maximum is
mh∗​=∣E′′(0)∣ℏ2​=4.56×10−31kg≃0.500me​.
(1)
The hole has charge +e, so its cyclotron angular frequency is
ωc​=mh∗​eB​=3.52×1011rads−1.​
(2)
If “frequency” means cycles per second, fc​=ωc​/(2π)=5.59×1010Hz. The curvature unit is Jm2, so ℏ2/E′′ has mass units. The external constants used are ℏ=1.054571800×10−34Js, e=1.6021766208×10−19C and me​=9.10938356×10−31kg.[ref] A genuinely anisotropic band would require the cyclotron mass for the orbit plane, not a single arbitrary directional curvature.

Let the atomic spacing be a and define the nearest-neighbour matrix elements tα​=⟨j,pα​∣H∣j+1,pα​⟩, using the same translated orbital convention on every site. Then
Eα​(k)=ϵα​+2tα​cos(ka),−π/a≤k≤π/a.
(3)
For the opposite sigma and pi hopping signs in a p-orbital chain, consider pz​ first: neighbouring lobes above the chain have the same sign, so the in-phase Bloch sum is bonding. Thus tπ​<0 and
Epz​​=ϵz​−2∣tπ​∣cos(ka):minimum at k=0.​
(4)
For px​, the lobes facing one another have opposite signs for equal site coefficients. Bonding therefore needs alternating coefficients, giving tσ​>0 and
Epx​​=ϵx​+2∣tσ​∣cos(ka):minimum at k=±π/a.​
(5)
End-on σ overlap is normally stronger than side-on π overlap, so the px​ band is wider, with bandwidths 4∣tσ​∣ and 4∣tπ​∣. The curve orientations require a stated hopping-sign convention; a Hamiltonian written with an explicit minus sign in front of the hopping uses correspondingly opposite parameter signs.[ref]

A hydrogenic donor has the effective Bohr radius of a hydrogenic donor
aB∗​=ϵr​m∗me​​a0​=80a0​=4.23nm,​
(6)
using the external a0​=0.52917721067×10−10m.[ref] Increasing donor density causes donor states to overlap and screens their binding, allowing a metallic impurity system rather than isolated frozen-out donors.
Using the standard approximate Mott criterion, nD1/3​aB∗​≃0.25, gives
nD,c​∼(aB∗​0.25​)3≃2.1×1023m−3=2.1×1017cm−3.​
(7)
The factor 0.25 is an externally documented empirical criterion, not supplied exam data or an exact universal threshold.[ref] An order-of-magnitude overlap argument without that prefactor would give (aB∗​)−3≃1.3×1025m−3. Compensation, disorder and multivalley anisotropy affect a real material's transition; this estimate uses the scalar mass and permittivity actually supplied, without substituting other germanium parameters.

a OurBigBook.com
Heavy fermion materials are correlated metals whose low-energy quasiparticles have exceptionally large effective masses. Partly localized f-electron moments hybridize with broader conduction states. The Kondo effect and coherence below the Kondo-lattice coherence temperature produce a narrow low-energy band, so a small energy change corresponds to a large momentum-space state density.
For the electronic heat capacity of a heavy Fermi liquid, the resulting density of states gives a large low-temperature electronic specific-heat coefficient,
Cel​=γT,γ=3π2kB2​​g(EF​),​
(8)
and often an enhanced magnetic susceptibility. Here g includes both spins and must be expressed per sample, mole or volume consistently with γ. A coherent Fermi-liquid regime often has ρ=ρ0​+AT2 with an enhanced coefficient A. Above coherence, scattering by local moments can instead give a Kondo-like resistivity upturn.
The competition between moment screening and intersite magnetic interactions can yield a heavy paramagnetic metal, magnetic order, quantum-critical behaviour or unconventional superconductivity. Ce- and U-based compounds supply familiar examples. “Heavy” describes the emergent electronic quasiparticles, not the ionic mass; the simple heavy-band picture need not survive at a quantum critical point.[ref]

The Hubbard model combines hopping with a local repulsion,
H=−t∑⟨ij⟩,σ​(ciσ†​cjσ​+h.c.)+U∑i​ni↑​ni↓​,U>0.
(9)
Hopping favours a broad itinerant band; the interaction penalizes opposite-spin double occupancy. For uniform Stoner decoupling of the Hubbard interaction, let s=n↑​−n↓​ be the spin imbalance per site. The interaction is U(n2−s2)/4, so polarization lowers it by Us2/4, at the expense of kinetic energy.
If D(EF​) is the noninteracting density of states per spin per site, small polarization costs s2/[4D(EF​)]. Therefore
ΔE=4s2​[D(EF​)−1−U]+O(s4),UD(EF​)>1​
(10)
is the mean-field ferromagnetic instability criterion. The exchange splitting is Δ=Us, and the paramagnetic response is enhanced as χ=χ0​/[1−UD(EF​)]. A density of states including both spins changes the written factor in the criterion.
The model explains how itinerant bands can polarize without imposing pre-existing localized atomic spins. It is not a claim that every large-U metal becomes ferromagnetic: near half filling the Hubbard model can instead form a Mott insulator with antiferromagnetic superexchange of scale 4t2/U. Band filling, bandwidth and fluctuations matter.[ref] The interaction-energy and kinetic-cost expansion above is the stated mean-field derivation of the enhancement.[ref]

For an electron wavepacket in one isolated band under a uniform electric field,
ℏk˙=−eE,v(k)=ℏ1​dkdϵ​.
(11)
Because ϵ(k+2π/a)=ϵ(k), acceleration through successive Brillouin zones makes the velocity periodic rather than increasing indefinitely. For ϵ=ϵ0​−2Jcos(ka),
ωB​=ℏe∣E∣a​,TB​=e∣E∣ah​,v(t)=ℏ2Ja​sin(k0​a−ωB​t)​
(12)
for a positive field along the chosen axis. Integration gives
x(t)−x(0)=eE2J​[cos(k0​a−ωB​t)−cos(k0​a)].
(13)
The excursion amplitude of a Bloch-oscillating wavepacket is 2∣J∣/(e∣E∣) about its mean and peak-to-peak extent 4∣J∣/(e∣E∣). The turning of velocity corresponds semiclassically to Bragg reflection near a zone boundary.
Coherent Bloch oscillations require scattering/dephasing times long compared with TB​ and a field weak enough to avoid substantial interband tunnelling. Ordinary metals usually scatter too rapidly; semiconductor superlattices and cold atoms in periodic optical potentials provide cleaner realizations. A completely filled band has canceling velocities, so its net current does not oscillate simply because individual wavepackets can.[ref]

For a spin-degenerate band, each unit cell contributes two available states. An even electron count per cell can fill an integer number of bands; a band insulator additionally needs a global gap between the highest occupied and lowest unoccupied bands. Even filling alone is not sufficient when bands overlap.
For a free two-dimensional gas with spin degeneracy two,
n=(2π)22πkF2​​,kF​=2πn​.​
(14)
The reciprocal vectors of the periodic potential are Qx^ and Qy^​. Its first Brillouin zone is the square −Q/2≤kx​,ky​≤Q/2, of side Q and area Q2; the real-space cell has side a=2π/Q.
At zero potential, the extended-zone Fermi surface is the circle of radius kF​. Since 2​<α<2, it crosses every square face but misses the corners. For Fermi-surface reconstruction in a weak square potential, weak positive potential opens gaps at the Bragg crossings: the second band has electron pockets at the two inequivalent edge centres X,Y, while unfilled regions of the first band form a hole pocket at the zone corner M. Opposite edges are identified: four edge half-pockets are two electron pockets, and four corner pieces are one hole pocket.
For the circular-cap carrier count in a square Brillouin zone, put R=kF​ and s=Q/(2R)=α/2. Each circular cap outside a square face has area
C=R2[arccoss−s1−s2​].
(15)
No two such caps overlap in the specified interval. The total second-band electron area is Ae​=4C. The first-band hole area is the square area minus the occupied central-circle area, Ah​=Q2−πR2+4C. Including spin,
nne​​=π4​[arccos(α/2)−(α/2)1−α2/4​],nnh​​=πα2​−1+nne​​.​
(16)
Thus at α=3​,
ne​=(32​−π3​​)n≃0.11534n,nh​=(π3−3​​−31​)n≃0.07027n.​
(17)
The carrier densities are the electron- and hole-pocket counts, not the total electron density. The carrier compensation and even band filling in a square lattice follows because their difference obeys ne​−nh​=n−Q2/(2π2), so compensation occurs at α=π​. These area formulas are the V0​→0+ limit, before finite-gap reconstruction changes the pocket shapes.[ref]
The filling per real-space cell is ν=na2=2π/α2. In the given interval, the only even integer filling is ν=2, again requiring α=π​. At any other fixed filling a full gap cannot yield an ordinary spin-degenerate band insulator in this unchanged periodicity. In particular, the α=3​ system retains excess electron carriers even after its holes disappear.
A rough gap threshold comes from comparing different symmetry directions, rather than only a direct zone-face gap. Define EQ​=ℏ2Q2/(2m). The zone-face two-plane-wave gap for a cosine potential follows because each cosine couples plane waves by −V0​/2, giving the one-dimensional zone-edge levels EQ​/4∓V0​/2. The four-plane-wave splitting at a square-zone corner is the sum of two such pairs. The potential is separable in x,y, so to first order
E1,max​(M)≃EQ​/2−V0​,E2,min​(X,Y)≃EQ​/4+V0​/2.
(18)
The indirect gap of a separable square cosine potential is consequently
Δind​≃23​V0​−41​EQ​,V0,min​∼6EQ​​=12mℏ2Q2​.​
(19)
At the compensated filling, this is V0,min​∼(π/6)EF(0)​, with EF(0)​=ℏ2kF2​/(2m). An insulator needs both the even filling and a positive indirect gap. At the touching threshold the gap is zero; a positive direct gap at every Bragg crossing is not by itself sufficient. The estimate neglects higher-plane-wave corrections and is not an exact critical amplitude.

Assume three-dimensional parabolic band edges, with density-of-states masses mc​,mh​ and one valley per edge, each with spin degeneracy two. Their effective densities of states are
Nc​=2(2πℏ2mc​kB​T​)3/2,Nv​=2(2πℏ2mh​kB​T​)3/2.
(20)
For the semiconductor mass-action law, integrating the square-root density of states against the Boltzmann tail gives n=Nc​e(μ−Ec​)/(kB​T) and p=Nv​e(Ev​−μ)/(kB​T). Multiplication cancels the chemical potential, giving
np=A(kB​T)3e−Eg​/(kB​T),A=(2πℏ2)34(mc​mh​)3/2​.​
(21)
If there are additional valley degeneracies, multiply by their product; anisotropic bands use the appropriate density-of-states masses. In the later equal-mass model, A=4m3/(2πℏ2)3. No numerical mass or valley multiplicity has been invented.
When μ=Ec​=0, the conduction-edge occupation is exactly 1/2, so the nondegenerate approximation for electrons fails at every positive temperature. Define the Normalized complete Fermi-Dirac integral
F1/2​(η)=π​2​∫0∞​1+ex−ηx​​dx,f0​=F1/2​(0)=(1−2−1/2)ζ(3/2)≃0.765147.
(22)
For the carrier densities with chemical potential pinned to a band edge, with the two specified equal masses, put N(T)=2[mkB​T/(2πℏ2)]3/2. Direct Fermi-Dirac integration then gives
n(T)=N(T)f0​,p(T)=N(T)F1/2​[−Eg​/(kB​T)].​
(23)
Thus n∝T3/2 exactly within the parabolic model. At low temperature, p≃N(T)e−Eg​/(kB​T) is thermally activated; at kB​T≫Eg​, p/n→1 from below. Both vanish at zero temperature and increase at fixed chemical potential. The other states that pin μ can supply the compensating charge; one must not additionally impose n=p.
For the Pauli reduction of the pinned-edge mass-action product, the exact product relative to the classical law is
N(T)2e−Eg​/(kB​T)np​=f0​eEg​/(kB​T)F1/2​[−Eg​/(kB​T)]<1.
(24)
It approaches f0​ at low temperature and f02​≃0.58545 in the formal high-temperature limit. The low-temperature activation factor is unchanged but its prefactor is reduced; the classical mass-action formula overestimates the product. The high-temperature limit here refers to the given unbounded parabolic bands, not a claim that real semiconductor band structures remain unchanged at arbitrarily high temperature.[ref]
Finally, for a fixed positive μ, the zero-temperature Fermi sphere has radius kμ​=2mμ​/ℏ, so
n(0)=3π2kμ3​​=3π21​(ℏ22mμ​)3/2,n(T)=N(T)F1/2​[μ/(kB​T)].​
(25)
The low-temperature expansion at fixed μ gives the fixed-chemical-potential Sommerfeld density increase:
n(T)=n(0)[1+8π2​(μkB​T​)2+O(μkB​T​)4].​
(26)
The monotonic density at fixed chemical potential in a square-root density of states initially rises quadratically from n(0). For kB​T≫μ, its leading behaviour crosses to f0​N(T)∝T3/2. The fixed chemical potential is what allows the particle number to change.
